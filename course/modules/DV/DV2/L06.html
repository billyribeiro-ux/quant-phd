Risk-Neutral Densities & Breeden–Litzenberger Extraction

In their landmark 1978 contribution, Douglas Breeden and Robert Litzenberger demonstrated that European option prices serve as generators of state prices: the second derivative of discounted call price with respect to strike is exactly proportional to the risk-neutral probability density of the underlying asset at expiration. This profound non-parametric result bridges market quotes and probability theory without requiring any structural model specification. By extracting the risk-neutral density, quantitative researchers reveal market-implied disaster risk, tail asymmetry, and the pricing kernel, enabling model-independent valuation and deep insights into market sentiment.

  • Introduces: risk-neutral density
  • Prerequisites: volatility smile and skew, butterfly arbitrage, SVI parameterization

1. The Breeden–Litzenberger Theorem

Under the risk-neutral pricing framework, the value of a European call option with strike \(K\) and expiration \(T\) is the discounted expected payoff under measure \(\mathbb{Q}\):

\[ C(K, T) = D(T) \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[(S_T - K)^+\right] = D(T) \int_K^\infty (S_T - K) q_T(S_T) \mathrm{d}S_T \]

where \(D(T) = e^{-r\tau}\) is the deterministic risk-free discount factor and \(q_T(S_T)\) is the risk-neutral probability density function of terminal spot price \(S_T\).

Definition 6.1 (Risk-Neutral Density). The risk-neutral density \(q_T(S_T)\) is the unique probability density function under equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) that prices all European contingent claims maturing at time \(T\) via discounted expectation.

Theorem 6.1 (Breeden–Litzenberger Identity). If European call option prices \(C(K, T)\) are twice continuously differentiable with respect to strike \(K\), the risk-neutral density \(q_T(K)\) is given by:

\[ q_T(K) = \frac{1}{D(T)} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}(K, T) \]

Proof. Differentiating the integral representation of \(C(K, T)\) with respect to strike \(K\) using the Leibniz integral rule:

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = D(T) \frac{\partial}{\partial K} \int_K^\infty (S_T - K) q_T(S_T) \mathrm{d}S_T = -D(T) \int_K^\infty q_T(S_T) \mathrm{d}S_T = -D(T)(1 - Q_T(K)) \]

where \(Q_T(K) = \mathbb{Q}(S_T \le K)\) is the cumulative distribution function under \(\mathbb{Q}\). Differentiating once more with respect to strike \(K\):

\[ \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = D(T) \frac{\partial}{\partial K}\left(Q_T(K) - 1\right) = D(T) q_T(K) \]

Dividing by \(D(T) > 0\) yields \(q_T(K) = \frac{1}{D(T)}\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\). ■

This result was first established by Breeden & Litzenberger (1978). It shows that options market prices directly reveal the state prices of Arrow–Debreu securities.

2. Analytical Density from Total Implied Variance

Rather than performing numerical double differentiation on discrete option prices, modern desks differentiate a fitted continuous total variance curve \(w(k) = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau)\tau\) in forward log-moneyness \(k = \ln(K/F)\) Gatheral (2006).

Theorem 6.2 (Analytical Risk-Neutral Density in Log-Moneyness). The risk-neutral probability density of forward log-moneyness \(k\) is given analytically by:

\[ p(k) = \frac{g(k)}{\sqrt{2\pi w(k)}} \exp\left(-\frac{d_2(k)^2}{2}\right) \]

where \(d_2(k) = -\frac{k}{\sqrt{w(k)}} - \frac{\sqrt{w(k)}}{2}\), and \(g(k)\) is the Gatheral–Jacquier density factor:

\[ g(k) = \left(1 - \frac{k w'(k)}{2 w(k)}\right)^2 - \frac{w'(k)^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{w(k)} + \frac{1}{4}\right) + \frac{w''(k)}{2} \]

The risk-neutral density in spot price coordinates \(q_T(K)\) satisfies \(q_T(K) = \frac{p(k)}{K}\).

Proof. From Theorem 2.1 in Lesson 2, the undiscounted call second derivative satisfies \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = \frac{n(d_1)}{K \sqrt{w(k)}} g(k)\). By Theorem 6.1, \(q_T(K) = \frac{1}{D(T)}\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\). Converting between \(d_1\) and \(d_2\): \(n(d_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-d_1^2/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(d_2 + \sqrt{w})^2/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-d_2^2/2} e^{-d_2\sqrt{w} - w/2}\). Substituting \(d_2 = -k/\sqrt{w} - \sqrt{w}/2\): \(-d_2\sqrt{w} - w/2 = k + w/2 - w/2 = k\). Therefore, \(n(d_1) = n(d_2) e^k = n(d_2) \frac{K}{F}\). Substituting into the second derivative: \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = \frac{n(d_2)}{F \sqrt{w(k)}} g(k)\). Setting \(p(k) = K q_T(K) = \frac{K}{D(T)}\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\) (normalized by forward price) yields \(p(k) = \frac{g(k)}{\sqrt{2\pi w(k)}} \exp\left(-\frac{d_2(k)^2}{2}\right)\). ■

When the implied volatility surface is flat (\(w(k) \equiv w_0\)), \(w'(k) = 0\) and \(w''(k) = 0\), so \(g(k) = 1\). The density collapses to the classical lognormal Gaussian density: \(p_0(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi w_0}} \exp\left(-\frac{(k + w_0/2)^2}{2w_0}\right)\). The density factor \(g(k)\) explicitly modulates the baseline Gaussian distribution to match market fat tails and skewness.

"""Lab 1 · Risk-Neutral Densities & Breeden-Litzenberger Extraction.

Extracts the risk-neutral probability density q(S_T) from European call prices
using the Breeden-Litzenberger identity q(K) = exp(r*tau) * d^2 C / dK^2.
Compares numerical finite-difference extraction with analytical SVI density p(k)
and verifies unit integral and martingale expectation E^Q[S_T] = F.
All data is SIMULATED (seed 42).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def bs_call_price(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> float:
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return max(s - k, 0.0)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * math.sqrt(tau))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * math.sqrt(tau)
    return s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)


def svi_derivs(k: float, a: float, b: float, rho: float, m: float, sigma: float) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    diff = k - m
    disc = math.sqrt(diff**2 + sigma**2)
    w = a + b * (rho * diff + disc)
    w_p = b * (rho + diff / disc)
    w_pp = b * (sigma**2) / (disc**3)
    return w, w_p, w_pp


def g_factor(k: float, a: float, b: float, rho: float, m: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    w, w_p, w_pp = svi_derivs(k, a, b, rho, m, sigma)
    term1 = (1.0 - (k * w_p) / (2.0 * w)) ** 2
    term2 = (w_p**2 / 4.0) * (1.0 / w + 0.25)
    term3 = 0.5 * w_pp
    return term1 - term2 + term3


def svi_rnd_density(k: float, a: float, b: float, rho: float, m: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    """Analytical risk-neutral density in log-moneyness p(k)."""
    w, _, _ = svi_derivs(k, a, b, rho, m, sigma)
    g = g_factor(k, a, b, rho, m, sigma)
    d2 = -k / math.sqrt(w) - 0.5 * math.sqrt(w)
    return (g / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi * w)) * math.exp(-0.5 * d2**2)


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    rate = 0.03
    tau = 0.50
    fwd = spot * math.exp(rate * tau)

    # Benchmark SVI slice parameters (SIMULATED)
    params = {"a": 0.030, "b": 0.120, "rho": -0.35, "m": 0.015, "sigma": 0.080}

    # Extract density across strike grid
    strikes = [round(fwd * math.exp(-0.40 + 0.05 * i), 2) for i in range(17)]  # k in [-0.4, 0.4]

    print("Strike (K) | Log-Moneyness (k) | Breeden-Litzenberger q(K) | Analytical SVI q(K)")
    print("-" * 80)

    bl_densities: list[float] = []
    svi_densities: list[float] = []

    for k_strike in strikes:
        k_log = math.log(k_strike / fwd)

        # Numerical Breeden-Litzenberger via central finite difference of call prices
        h = 0.50
        k_up = k_strike + h
        k_down = k_strike - h
        w_up, _, _ = svi_derivs(math.log(k_up / fwd), **params)
        w_down, _, _ = svi_derivs(math.log(k_down / fwd), **params)
        w_mid, _, _ = svi_derivs(k_log, **params)

        c_up = bs_call_price(spot, k_up, rate, math.sqrt(w_up / tau), tau)
        c_down = bs_call_price(spot, k_down, rate, math.sqrt(w_down / tau), tau)
        c_mid = bs_call_price(spot, k_strike, rate, math.sqrt(w_mid / tau), tau)

        # q(K) = exp(r * tau) * (C(K+h) - 2C(K) + C(K-h)) / h^2
        q_bl = math.exp(rate * tau) * (c_up - 2.0 * c_mid + c_down) / (h**2)

        # Analytical SVI density transformed to strike: q(K) = p(k) / K
        p_log = svi_rnd_density(k_log, **params)
        q_svi = p_log / k_strike

        bl_densities.append(q_bl)
        svi_densities.append(q_svi)

        print(f"{k_strike:10.2f} | {k_log:17.4f} | {q_bl:25.6f} | {q_svi:20.6f}")

    # Dense numerical integration over k in [-2.5, 2.5]
    n_steps = 5000
    step = 5.0 / n_steps
    int_mass = 0.0
    int_exp_k = 0.0
    int_k = 0.0

    for j in range(n_steps):
        k_val = -2.5 + (j + 0.5) * step
        p_val = svi_rnd_density(k_val, **params)
        int_mass += p_val * step
        int_exp_k += math.exp(k_val) * p_val * step
        int_k += k_val * p_val * step

    mean_s_t = fwd * int_exp_k

    print("-" * 80)
    print(f"Total Probability Mass Integral:        {int_mass:.6f} (Theoretical: 1.000000)")
    print(f"Martingale Asset Expectation E^Q[S_T]:  {mean_s_t:.4f} (Forward F = {fwd:.4f})")
    print(f"Risk-Neutral Mean Log-Return E^Q[k]:    {int_k:.6f} (Negative due to Jensen's inequality)")
    print(
        "Numerical vs Analytical BL max diff < 1e-4:",
        max(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(bl_densities, svi_densities, strict=True)) < 1e-4,
    )
    print("Martingale Pricing Identity Verified:   ", abs(mean_s_t - fwd) < 1e-3)

Where the canon breaks

Extracting the risk-neutral density from market quotes reveals severe breakdowns of textbook assumptions:

  • Discreteness and Quote Noise: In exchange-traded markets, option strikes are spaced at discrete intervals (\$5 or \$10). Approximating \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\) via raw second differences produces jagged, oscillating, or negative probability spikes. Robust density extraction requires fitting a continuous, arbitrage-free parametric model (like SVI) before differentiating.
  • Wing Extrapolation Sensitivity: Liquid option strikes rarely extend beyond \(\pm 2\) standard deviations. The behavior of the extracted density in the extreme tails depends heavily on the extrapolation model chosen. Unconstrained splines can generate negative densities, while ad-hoc polynomial fits create artificial multimodal lobes.
  • Measure Distortion: The risk-neutral density \(q(S_T)\) differs systematically from the real-world physical density \(p(S_T)\). The ratio \(\frac{q(S_T)}{p(S_T)} = \lambda m(S_T)\) is the stochastic discount factor (pricing kernel). The risk-neutral distribution assigns much higher probability to severe market crashes than actually occurs physically, reflecting economic risk aversion.

Misconceptions

  • The Breeden–Litzenberger risk-neutral density represents the real-world probability of where the stock will trade at expiration.

    The risk-neutral density incorporates both physical transition probabilities and market risk preferences (the pricing kernel). It prices contingent claims but does not describe the physical data-generating distribution \(\mathbb{P}\).

  • Applying the Breeden–Litzenberger formula requires assuming that asset prices follow geometric Brownian motion.

    Breeden–Litzenberger is completely model-free. It relies solely on absence of static arbitrage and European option payoff structure; it holds whether asset dynamics are diffusions, jump processes, or discrete chains.

  • An option strike with zero open interest has zero risk-neutral probability density.

    Risk-neutral density is a continuous property of the underlying asset's terminal distribution. A strike with zero traded volume still has a positive Arrow–Debreu state price determined by the no-arbitrage continuum of surrounding options.

Practice problems

At strike \(K = 100\) and \(\tau = 1\), the discount factor is \(D(1) = 0.95\). The call option prices around strike 100 with strike spacing \(\Delta K = 2\) are \(C(98) = 11.20\), \(C(100) = 9.80\), and \(C(102) = 8.44\). Estimate the risk-neutral density \(q_T(100)\) using centered second differences.

Worked Solution

Compute the numerical second derivative: \(\frac{\Delta^2 C}{\Delta K^2} = \frac{C(98) - 2C(100) + C(102)}{(\Delta K)^2} = \frac{11.20 - 2(9.80) + 8.44}{2^2} = \frac{11.20 - 19.60 + 8.44}{4} = \frac{0.040}{4} = 0.0100\). Applying the Breeden–Litzenberger identity: \(q_T(100) = \frac{1}{D(T)}\frac{\Delta^2 C}{\Delta K^2} = \frac{0.0100}{0.95} \approx 0.010526\).

For an ATM option (\(k = 0\)), total variance is \(w(0) = 0.040\), and the Gatheral–Jacquier factor is \(g(0) = 1.0\). Calculate the analytical risk-neutral density \(p(0)\) in log-moneyness coordinates.

Worked Solution

At \(k = 0\), \(d_2(0) = -\frac{0}{\sqrt{0.040}} - \frac{\sqrt{0.040}}{2} = -\frac{0.20}{2} = -0.100\). Evaluate the exponential: \(\exp(-d_2^2/2) = \exp(-(-0.10)^2 / 2) = \exp(-0.01 / 2) = \exp(-0.005) \approx 0.995012\). The denominator is \(\sqrt{2\pi w(0)} = \sqrt{2\pi \times 0.040} = \sqrt{0.251327} \approx 0.501326\). Therefore, \(p(0) = \frac{1.0 \times 0.995012}{0.501326} \approx 1.98476\).

If the underlying asset forward price is \(F = 100\), convert the log-moneyness density value \(p(0) = 1.98476\) from Problem 2 into the spot price risk-neutral density \(q_T(100)\).

Worked Solution

By Theorem 6.2, the relationship between spot price density and log-moneyness density is \(q_T(K) = \frac{p(k)}{K}\). At \(k = 0\), \(K = F = 100\). Therefore, \(q_T(100) = \frac{p(0)}{100} = \frac{1.98476}{100} = 0.0198476\).

Prove using the Dirac delta generalized function that the second derivative of European call payoff \((S_T - K)^+\) with respect to strike \(K\) equals \(\delta(S_T - K)\).

Worked Solution

Write the call payoff as \((S_T - K)^+ = (S_T - K)\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T \ge K\}}\). Differentiating with respect to strike \(K\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial K}\left[(S_T - K)\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T \ge K\}}\right] = -1 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T \ge K\}} + (S_T - K)(-\delta(S_T - K))\). Because \(x \delta(x) = 0\), the second term vanishes, leaving \(\frac{\partial}{\partial K}(S_T - K)^+ = -\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T \ge K\}}\). Differentiating a second time with respect to \(K\): \(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial K^2}(S_T - K)^+ = -\frac{\partial}{\partial K}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T \ge K\}} = -(-\delta(S_T - K)) = \delta(S_T - K)\). Taking expectations under \(\mathbb{Q}\): \(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial K^2}\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[(S_T - K)^+] = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\delta(S_T - K)] = q_T(K)\).

Prove that any valid risk-neutral density \(q_T(S_T)\) extracted from European call prices must satisfy the martingale mean condition: \(\int_0^\infty S_T q_T(S_T)\mathrm{d}S_T = F_T\).

Worked Solution

Using integration by parts on \(\int_0^\infty S_T q_T(S_T)\mathrm{d}S_T\): let \(u = S_T\) and \(\mathrm{d}v = q_T(S_T)\mathrm{d}S_T\), so \(\mathrm{d}u = \mathrm{d}S_T\) and \(v = -(1 - Q_T(S_T))\). Then \(\int_0^\infty S_T q_T(S_T)\mathrm{d}S_T = \left[-S_T (1 - Q_T(S_T))\right]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty (1 - Q_T(S_T))\mathrm{d}S_T\). Assuming \(S_T(1 - Q_T(S_T)) \to 0\) as \(S_T \to \infty\), the boundary terms evaluate to 0. Recall from Theorem 6.1 that \(1 - Q_T(K) = -\frac{1}{D(T)}\frac{\partial C}{\partial K}\). Integrating: \(\int_0^\infty \left(-\frac{1}{D(T)}\frac{\partial C}{\partial K}\right)\mathrm{d}K = -\frac{1}{D(T)} [C(\infty) - C(0)]\). Since \(C(\infty) = 0\) and \(C(0) = D(T)F_T\), this evaluates to \(-\frac{1}{D(T)}[0 - D(T)F_T] = F_T\).

Show that the total integral of the analytical log-moneyness density \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty p(k)\mathrm{d}k\) equals 1 if and only if \(\lim_{k \to \infty} d_1(k) = -\infty\) and \(\lim_{k \to -\infty} d_1(k) = +\infty\).

Worked Solution

From the proof of Theorem 6.2, \(p(k) = -\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}k} N(d_2(k) + \sqrt{w(k)}) = -\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}k} N(d_1(k))\). Integrating across the real line: \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty p(k)\mathrm{d}k = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left(-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}k} N(d_1(k))\right)\mathrm{d}k = -\left[N(d_1(+\infty)) - N(d_1(-\infty))\right]\). For this integral to equal 1, we must have \(N(d_1(-\infty)) = 1\) (which requires \(d_1(-\infty) = +\infty\)) and \(N(d_1(+\infty)) = 0\) (which requires \(d_1(+\infty) = -\infty\)). Hence the integral evaluates to \(-(0 - 1) = 1\).

Comparing an empirical S&P 500 risk-neutral density extracted via Breeden–Litzenberger to a lognormal density with the same ATM volatility: what structural differences appear in the left wing (downside strikes) and right wing (upside strikes)?

Worked Solution

The empirical risk-neutral density exhibits substantial negative skewness and leptokurtosis: the left wing contains significantly higher probability mass (often 2 to 5 times greater than lognormal at \(k \le -0.2\)), reflecting market crash fear. In contrast, the right wing has slightly lower density than lognormal, indicating compressed upside expectations and creating a mode shifted slightly right of the mean.

A trader attempts to price a digital call option paying \$1 if \(S_T \ge 105\) using call prices. Market prices are \(C(104) = 4.30\) and \(C(106) = 3.50\) with \(r = 0\). Estimate the digital option price and the implied risk-neutral probability \(\mathbb{Q}(S_T \ge 105)\).

Worked Solution

The terminal payoff of a digital call is \(\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T \ge K\}}\). By static replication, a digital call can be approximated by a tight bull spread: \(\text{Digital}(K) \approx -\frac{C(K + \Delta K) - C(K - \Delta K)}{2\Delta K}\). With \(K = 105\) and \(\Delta K = 1\): \(\text{Digital}(105) \approx -\frac{3.50 - 4.30}{2} = -\frac{-0.80}{2} = \$0.40\). With \(r = 0\) (\(D(T) = 1\)), the digital price directly equals the risk-neutral probability: \(\mathbb{Q}(S_T \ge 105) = 0.40\) (40%).

How does the variance risk premium manifest when comparing the physical density \(p_{\mathbb{P}}(S_T)\) (estimated from historical realized returns) to the risk-neutral density \(q_{\mathbb{Q}}(S_T)\) (extracted from option prices)?

Worked Solution

The risk-neutral density \(q_{\mathbb{Q}}\) has a wider standard deviation (higher implied variance) and substantially fatter left tails than the physical density \(p_{\mathbb{P}}\). This divergence reflects the pricing kernel \(m(S_T) = \frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{q_{\mathbb{Q}}(S_T)}{p_{\mathbb{P}}(S_T)}\), which is monotonically decreasing in \(S_T\), indicating that investors value \$1 of consumption during market crashes much more highly than during market rallies.

How does the Jackwerth (2000) non-parametric method recover the empirical pricing kernel \(m(S_T)\) using Breeden–Litzenberger risk-neutral densities and historical return distributions?

Worked Solution

Jackwerth estimates the risk-neutral density \(q(S_T)\) by solving a constrained optimization that matches option prices while minimizing a curvature penalty \(\int (q''(S))^2 \mathrm{d}S\). The physical density \(p(S_T)\) is estimated via kernel density estimation on historical returns. The pricing kernel is extracted as \(m(S_T) = \frac{q(S_T)}{p(S_T)}\). Post-1987 empirical studies reveal the 'pricing kernel puzzle': \(m(S_T)\) is locally increasing in the region around ATM, contradicting standard risk-averse utility models.

Describe the mixture of lognormals method for parameterizing the risk-neutral density \(q_T(K) = \sum_{j=1}^M \lambda_j \phi_{\mathrm{LN}}(K; \mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\) and explain how option prices are evaluated in closed form.

Worked Solution

A mixture of \(M\) lognormal densities expresses the terminal distribution as a convex combination (\(\sum \lambda_j = 1, \lambda_j \ge 0\)) of lognormal distributions with distinct drifts \(\mu_j\) and volatilities \(\sigma_j\). Because the Black–Scholes formula is linear in the terminal density, the market call price is a simple linear combination of standard Black–Scholes formulas: \(C(K) = \sum_{j=1}^M \lambda_j C_{\mathrm{BS}}(F_j, K, \tau, \sigma_j)\). This parameterization guarantees non-negative densities and closed-form derivatives while flexibly capturing skewness and kurtosis.

Explain how minimum relative entropy (Kullback–Leibler divergence) can be used to construct the closest risk-neutral density to a prior distribution that exactly satisfies market option constraints.

Worked Solution

Given a prior physical density \(p(x)\), the minimum relative entropy problem solves \(\min_q \int q(x) \ln\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\mathrm{d}x\) subject to \(\int q(x)\mathrm{d}x = 1\) and \(\int (x - K_i)^+ q(x)\mathrm{d}x = \tilde{C}_i\). By the calculus of variations, the optimal density takes the Gibbs form \(q^*(x) = \frac{p(x) \exp\left(-\sum_i \mu_i (x - K_i)^+\right)}{\int p(u) \exp\left(-\sum_i \mu_i (u - K_i)^+\right)\mathrm{d}u}\), where Lagrange multipliers \(\mu_i\) are computed by convex optimization, ensuring non-negativity and maximum statistical parsimony.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What does the Breeden–Litzenberger theorem state regarding European call option prices?

    Options

    Breeden and Litzenberger showed that \(q_T(K) = \frac{1}{D(T)}\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\).

  2. What financial instrument does an infinitesimal butterfly spread synthesize?

    Options

    A butterfly spread in the limit \(\Delta K \to 0\) delivers a Dirac delta spike payoff, which is an Arrow–Debreu security.

  3. If the discount factor is \(D(T) = 1.0\) and the second derivative of call price is \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = 0.010\), what is the risk-neutral density \(q_T(K)\)?

    \(q_T(K) = \frac{1}{1.0} \times 0.010 = 0.0100\).

  4. How does the Gatheral–Jacquier factor \(g(k)\) modify the baseline Gaussian density in the analytical RND formula?

    Options

    \(g(k)\) modulates the Gaussian kernel to accommodate empirical smiles, skew, and heavy tails.

  5. What is the value of the total integrated risk-neutral probability \(\int_0^\infty q_T(S_T)\mathrm{d}S_T\) across all possible positive asset prices?

    As a probability density function, the total area under \(q_T(S_T)\) must equal 1.0 identically.

  6. Why does the risk-neutral density \(q(S_T)\) differ from the physical probability density \(p(S_T)\)?

    Options

    The change of measure from \(\mathbb{P}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\) weights outcomes by the marginal utility of consumption (the pricing kernel).

  7. What does the first derivative of call price with respect to strike, \(-\frac{\partial C}{\partial K}\), represent?

    Options

    \(-\frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = D(T)(1 - Q_T(K)) = D(T)\mathbb{Q}(S_T \ge K)\), which is the discounted probability of finishing in-the-money.

  8. If an underlying asset has forward price \(F_T = 100.0\), what is the expected value of terminal price under the risk-neutral density: \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[S_T]\)?

    Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), discounted asset prices are martingales, so \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[S_T] = F_T = 100.0\).

  9. What dangerous artifact occurs when applying Breeden–Litzenberger directly to discrete raw market prices without smoothing?

    Options

    Microstructure bounce in raw option quotes is amplified heavily by division by \((\Delta K)^2\), producing spurious negative probabilities.
