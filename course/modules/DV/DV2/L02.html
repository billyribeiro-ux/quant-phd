Strike Arbitrage & Butterfly Spreads: The Non-Negative Density Condition

In a rational financial market, static payoff dominance establishes inviolable boundary conditions on option prices across strikes. A butterfly spread composed of three strikes yields a payoff that is non-negative everywhere and strictly positive in a central corridor. Absence of arbitrage mandates that its market cost must be non-negative. In continuous strike space, this convexity requirement corresponds to the non-negativity of the risk-neutral probability density. Smooth implied volatility curves that violate convexity generate negative state prices, allowing market participants to extract costless arbitrage. Understanding butterfly arbitrage is critical to building mathematically sound volatility surfaces.

  • Introduces: butterfly arbitrage
  • Prerequisites: volatility smile and skew, arbitrage opportunity, put-call parity

1. No-Arbitrage Strike Bounds and Vertical Spreads

Consider European call options written on an underlying asset \(S\) with expiration \(\tau = T - t\) and strikes \(K_1 < K_2\). Let \(D(\tau) = e^{-r\tau}\) be the deterministic discount factor. The terminal payoffs satisfy:

\[ 0 \le (S_T - K_1)^+ - (S_T - K_2)^+ \le K_2 - K_1 \]

By static replication, a bull call spread (long call at \(K_1\), short call at \(K_2\)) must have a price bounded strictly between zero and the discounted strike difference:

\[ 0 \le C(K_1, \tau) - C(K_2, \tau) \le D(\tau)(K_2 - K_1) \]

Dividing by \(K_2 - K_1\) and taking the limit \(K_2 \to K_1\) establishes the slope bound for European call prices:

\[ -D(\tau) \le \frac{\partial C}{\partial K} \le 0 \]

2. Butterfly Spreads and Strike Convexity

A long butterfly spread consists of buying one call at strike \(K - \Delta K\), buying one call at strike \(K + \Delta K\), and selling two calls at the central strike \(K\). Its terminal payoff at maturity \(T\) is:

\[ \Pi_{\mathrm{BF}}(S_T) = (S_T - (K - \Delta K))^+ - 2(S_T - K)^+ + (S_T - (K + \Delta K))^+ \]

Because \(\Pi_{\mathrm{BF}}(S_T) \ge 0\) for all \(S_T \ge 0\) with strict positivity on the interval \((K - \Delta K, K + \Delta K)\), absence of arbitrage dictates that its initial value must be strictly positive.

Definition 2.1 (Butterfly Arbitrage). A set of European option prices contains butterfly arbitrage (or strike arbitrage) if there exist strikes \(K_1 < K_2 < K_3\) and weights \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) with \(K_2 = (1 - \lambda)K_1 + \lambda K_3\) such that:

\[ C(K_2, \tau) > (1 - \lambda) C(K_1, \tau) + \lambda C(K_3, \tau) \]

In smooth strike space, butterfly arbitrage exists if and only if the call price function is not convex in strike: \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} < 0\).

Dividing the butterfly spread cost by \((\Delta K)^2\) and letting \(\Delta K \to 0\) yields the continuous convexity condition:

\[ \lim_{\Delta K \to 0} \frac{C(K - \Delta K, \tau) - 2C(K, \tau) + C(K + \Delta K, \tau)}{(\Delta K)^2} = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}(K, \tau) \ge 0 \]

3. Total Variance Convexity: The Gatheral–Jacquier Factor \(g(k)\)

In market practice, option surfaces are parameterized by total implied variance \(w(k) = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau)\tau\) as a function of forward log-moneyness \(k = \ln(K/F)\). Differentiating Black–Scholes call price with respect to strike \(K\) when total variance depends on \(k\) yields a fundamental condition established by Gatheral (2006) and Gatheral & Jacquier (2013).

Theorem 2.1 (Absence of Butterfly Arbitrage in Total Variance). A smooth total implied variance slice \(k \mapsto w(k)\) at expiration \(\tau\) is free of butterfly arbitrage if and only if:

\[ g(k) \ge 0 \quad \forall k \in \mathbb{R} \]

and \(\lim_{k \to +\infty} d_1(k) = -\infty\), where the density factor \(g(k)\) is defined by:

\[ g(k) = \left(1 - \frac{k w'(k)}{2 w(k)}\right)^2 - \frac{w'(k)^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{w(k)} + \frac{1}{4}\right) + \frac{w''(k)}{2} \]

Proof. Express the undiscounted call price as \(C(k) = F N(d_1(k)) - K N(d_2(k))\). Since \(K = F e^k\), \(\frac{\partial k}{\partial K} = \frac{1}{K}\). Differentiating \(C\) with respect to \(K\):

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = F n(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial K} - N(d_2) - K n(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial K} \]

Using the Black–Scholes identity \(F n(d_1) = K n(d_2)\) and \(d_1 - d_2 = \sqrt{w(k)}\), the derivative terms simplify to:

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = -N(d_2) + F n(d_1) \left(\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial K} - \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial K}\right) = -N(d_2) \]

Differentiating once more with respect to \(K\) yields the second derivative:

\[ \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = -n(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial K} = -n(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial k} \frac{1}{K} \]

Recalling \(d_2(k) = -\frac{k}{\sqrt{w(k)}} - \frac{\sqrt{w(k)}}{2}\), differentiate \(d_2\) with respect to \(k\):

\[ \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial k} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{w(k)}} + \frac{k w'(k)}{2 w(k)^{3/2}} - \frac{w'(k)}{4\sqrt{w(k)}} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{w(k)}} \left(1 - \frac{k w'(k)}{2 w(k)} + \frac{w'(k)}{4}\right) \]

Substituting into the full expression and using \(n(d_2) = n(d_1) e^{k}\) gives after algebraic rearrangement:

\[ \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = \frac{n(d_1)}{K \sqrt{w(k)}} g(k) \]

Because \(n(d_1) > 0\), \(K > 0\), and \(\sqrt{w(k)} > 0\), the sign of \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\) is identical to the sign of \(g(k)\). Therefore, \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} \ge 0\) if and only if \(g(k) \ge 0\) for all \(k\). ■

Lab 1 · Verification of Butterfly Arbitrage and Density Factor Non-Negativity

Hypothesis: In an ill-behaved implied volatility curve where the downside skew is excessively steep, the Gatheral–Jacquier density factor \(g(k)\) will become negative (\(g(k) < 0\)), producing negative butterfly spread prices and enabling costless arbitrage.

"""Lab 1 · Strike Arbitrage & Butterfly Spreads: The Non-Negative Density Condition.

Evaluates European call prices across a strike grid, tests call price convexity
C(K - dK) - 2*C(K) + C(K + dK) >= 0, and calculates the Gatheral-Jacquier density
factor g(k). Demonstrates how a non-convexity violation creates butterfly arbitrage.
All data is SIMULATED (seed 42).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def svi_derivs(k: float, a: float, b: float, rho: float, m: float, sigma: float) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    diff = k - m
    disc = math.sqrt(diff**2 + sigma**2)
    w = a + b * (rho * diff + disc)
    w_p = b * (rho + diff / disc)
    w_pp = b * (sigma**2) / (disc**3)
    return w, w_p, w_pp


def g_factor(k: float, a: float, b: float, rho: float, m: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    w, w_p, w_pp = svi_derivs(k, a, b, rho, m, sigma)
    term1 = (1.0 - (k * w_p) / (2.0 * w)) ** 2
    term2 = (w_p**2 / 4.0) * (1.0 / w + 0.25)
    term3 = 0.5 * w_pp
    return term1 - term2 + term3


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def bs_call_price(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> float:
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return max(s - k, 0.0)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * math.sqrt(tau))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * math.sqrt(tau)
    return s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    rate = 0.03
    tau = 0.50
    fwd = spot * math.exp(rate * tau)

    # Slice 1: Arbitrage-free valid benchmark
    p_valid = {"a": 0.035, "b": 0.12, "rho": -0.30, "m": 0.02, "sigma": 0.10}

    # Slice 2: Pathological parameters inducing negative density (butterfly arbitrage)
    p_bad = {"a": 0.045, "b": 0.75, "rho": -0.65, "m": 0.0, "sigma": 0.015}

    k_evals = [-0.4, -0.2, -0.1, 0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4]

    print("--- Benchmark Arbitrage-Free Slice ---")
    min_g_valid = float("inf")
    for k in k_evals:
        g = g_factor(k, **p_valid)
        if g < min_g_valid:
            min_g_valid = g
        w, _, _ = svi_derivs(k, **p_valid)
        print(f"k = {k:5.2f} | w(k) = {w:.5f} | Density Factor g(k) = {g:.5f}")

    print(f"Minimum density factor g(k) on valid slice: {min_g_valid:.5f}")
    print("Valid slice is butterfly arbitrage-free:", min_g_valid >= 0.0)

    print("\n--- Pathological Slice with Butterfly Arbitrage ---")
    min_g_bad = float("inf")
    k_min_bad = 0.0
    for k in k_evals:
        g = g_factor(k, **p_bad)
        if g < min_g_bad:
            min_g_bad = g
            k_min_bad = k
        w, _, _ = svi_derivs(k, **p_bad)
        print(f"k = {k:5.2f} | w(k) = {w:.5f} | Density Factor g(k) = {g:.5f}")

    print(f"Minimum density factor on pathological slice: {min_g_bad:.5f} at k = {k_min_bad:.2f}")
    print("Butterfly Arbitrage Detected (Density Factor < 0):", min_g_bad < 0.0)

    # Numerical butterfly spread test around negative density point
    k_center = k_min_bad
    dk = 0.02
    k1, k2, k3 = k_center - dk, k_center, k_center + dk
    strike1, strike2, strike3 = fwd * math.exp(k1), fwd * math.exp(k2), fwd * math.exp(k3)

    w1, _, _ = svi_derivs(k1, **p_bad)
    w2, _, _ = svi_derivs(k2, **p_bad)
    w3, _, _ = svi_derivs(k3, **p_bad)

    c1 = bs_call_price(spot, strike1, rate, math.sqrt(w1 / tau), tau)
    c2 = bs_call_price(spot, strike2, rate, math.sqrt(w2 / tau), tau)
    c3 = bs_call_price(spot, strike3, rate, math.sqrt(w3 / tau), tau)

    fly_cost = c1 - 2.0 * c2 + c3
    print(f"\nConstructing Butterfly Spread at Strikes [{strike1:.2f}, {strike2:.2f}, {strike3:.2f}]:")
    print(f"Call Prices: C1 = {c1:.4f}, C2 = {c2:.4f}, C3 = {c3:.4f}")
    print(f"Butterfly Spread Net Cost: {fly_cost:.6f}")
    print("Negative Butterfly Cost (Arbitrage Opportunity):", fly_cost < 0.0)


main()

Where the canon breaks

Quantitative models frequently fail butterfly arbitrage tests when market data is interpolated using standard numerical techniques:

  • High-Order Polynomial Fits: Fitting implied volatility across strikes with 3rd, 4th, or 5th-order polynomials almost always produces severe oscillations (Runge's phenomenon) in the wings. Even if the fitted volatility appears plausible to the eye, its second derivative \(w''(k)\) oscillates violently, causing \(g(k) < 0\) and negative butterfly costs.
  • Cubic Spline Inflection Points: Natural cubic splines enforce \(\sigma''(K) = 0\) at outer boundary knots. This forces the implied volatility curve to become linear at the boundaries, which frequently violates Lee's asymptotic bounds and induces negative density near the extrapolation boundary.
  • Independent Strike Slicing: Calibrating each maturity slice independently ignores calendar relationships and often produces subtle localized butterfly violations in illiquid strikes.

Misconceptions

  • Any smooth, monotonically downward-sloping implied volatility smile is guaranteed to be free of butterfly arbitrage.

    Even a strictly monotonic smile can violate butterfly arbitrage if its first derivative \(|w'(k)|\) is too steep. If the slope exceeds the bound \(w'(k)^2 > 4 w(k) / (1 + w(k)/4)\), \(g(k)\) turns negative, yielding negative butterfly prices.

  • Butterfly arbitrage can only be detected by checking prices on discrete traded strikes.

    The Gatheral–Jacquier factor \(g(k)\) provides an exact analytical condition across the entire continuous strike continuum. Discrete strike checks can easily miss localized negative density violations between strikes.

  • Negative butterfly spreads are harmless in practice because bid-ask transaction costs prevent real-world arbitrage.

    Even if wide bid-ask spreads prevent immediate physical execution, a surface with \(g(k) < 0\) produces negative probabilities in risk-neutral pricing engines and imaginary numbers in local volatility denominators, crashing exotic pricing PDEs.

Practice problems

Three European calls at strikes \(K_1 = 90\), \(K_2 = 100\), and \(K_3 = 110\) with \(\tau = 1\) trade at market prices \(C_1 = 15.00\), \(C_2 = 10.50\), and \(C_3 = 5.00\). Calculate the cost of the butterfly spread and determine whether butterfly arbitrage exists.

Worked Solution

The butterfly spread consists of long 1 call at \(K_1\), short 2 calls at \(K_2\), and long 1 call at \(K_3\). The net initial cost is \(\Pi = C_1 - 2 C_2 + C_3 = 15.00 - 2(10.50) + 5.00 = 15.00 - 21.00 + 5.00 = -1.00\). Because \(\Pi = -1.00 < 0\), the butterfly costs negative money (an immediate cash inflow of \$1.00) while yielding a terminal payoff \(\Pi_T \ge 0\). This represents direct butterfly arbitrage.

At ATM forward log-moneyness \(k = 0\), an implied variance slice has total variance \(w(0) = 0.040\), slope \(w'(0) = -0.080\), and curvature \(w''(0) = 0.200\). Compute the Gatheral–Jacquier density factor \(g(0)\).

Worked Solution

At \(k = 0\), the first term is \(\left(1 - \frac{0 \cdot w'(0)}{2 w(0)}\right)^2 = (1 - 0)^2 = 1.0\). The second term is \(-\frac{w'(0)^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{w(0)} + \frac{1}{4}\right) = -\frac{(-0.080)^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{0.040} + 0.25\right) = -\frac{0.0064}{4}(25 + 0.25) = -0.0016 \times 25.25 = -0.0404\). The third term is \(\frac{w''(0)}{2} = \frac{0.200}{2} = 0.100\). Summing the three terms: \(g(0) = 1.0 - 0.0404 + 0.100 = 1.0596 > 0\), so no butterfly arbitrage occurs at ATM.

Suppose at strike \(K = 100\), \(C(95) = 12.00\), \(C(100) = 8.00\), and \(C(105) = 4.20\). Compute the numerical second derivative \(\frac{\Delta^2 C}{\Delta K^2}\) with \(\Delta K = 5\).

Worked Solution

The finite-difference second derivative is \(\frac{C(95) - 2C(100) + C(105)}{(\Delta K)^2} = \frac{12.00 - 2(8.00) + 4.20}{5^2} = \frac{12.00 - 16.00 + 4.20}{25} = \frac{0.20}{25} = 0.0080\). Because \(\frac{\Delta^2 C}{\Delta K^2} = 0.0080 > 0\), the call prices are convex around strike 100.

Prove that for European call prices, the first strike derivative satisfies \(0 \le -\frac{\partial C}{\partial K} \le D(\tau)\) under no-arbitrage.

Worked Solution

For any two strikes \(K_1 < K_2\), terminal call payoffs satisfy \(0 \le (S_T - K_1)^+ - (S_T - K_2)^+ \le K_2 - K_1\). By no-arbitrage (risk-neutral pricing with discount factor \(D(\tau)\)), \(0 \le C(K_1) - C(K_2) \le D(\tau)(K_2 - K_1)\). Dividing through by \(K_2 - K_1 > 0\) yields \(0 \le \frac{C(K_1) - C(K_2)}{K_2 - K_1} \le D(\tau)\). Taking the limit as \(K_2 \to K_1\) gives \(0 \le -\frac{\partial C}{\partial K} \le D(\tau)\).

Prove that for a flat implied volatility surface \(w(k) \equiv w_0 > 0\), the Gatheral–Jacquier factor \(g(k)\) is identically 1 for all \(k \in \mathbb{R}\).

Worked Solution

For a flat surface, \(w'(k) = 0\) and \(w''(k) = 0\) for all \(k\). Evaluating \(g(k)\): \(\left(1 - \frac{k(0)}{2 w_0}\right)^2 - \frac{0^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{w_0} + \frac{1}{4}\right) + \frac{0}{2} = 1^2 - 0 + 0 = 1\). Hence \(g(k) \equiv 1 > 0\), confirming that the flat Black–Scholes volatility model is unconditionally free of butterfly arbitrage.

Show that if \(g(k) < 0\) at some point \(k^*\), the risk-neutral probability density of the underlying asset at that strike is strictly negative.

Worked Solution

By the Breeden–Litzenberger theorem, the risk-neutral density is \(q_T(K) = \frac{1}{D(\tau)}\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\). In total variance coordinates, \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = \frac{n(d_1)}{K \sqrt{w(k)}} g(k)\). Because the Gaussian density \(n(d_1) > 0\), strike \(K > 0\), and volatility \(\sqrt{w(k)} > 0\), the prefactor \(\frac{n(d_1)}{K \sqrt{w(k)} D(\tau)}\) is strictly positive. Consequently, \(q_T(K^*) = \text{positive prefactor} \times g(k^*) < 0\). A negative probability density violates Kolmogorov probability axioms and represents direct arbitrage.

In an S&P 500 option chain, market makers quote a bid of \$1.20 and an ask of \$1.40 for a 90-strike put, a bid of \$2.00 and ask of \$2.20 for a 95-strike put, and a bid of \$3.00 and ask of \$3.20 for a 100-strike put. Can an arbitrageur capture a riskless cash profit through a butterfly spread after paying the ask on long legs and receiving the bid on short legs?

Worked Solution

To execute a long butterfly, the trader buys the 90-strike put at ask (\$1.40), buys the 100-strike put at ask (\$3.20), and sells 2 of the 95-strike puts at bid (\$2.00 each). The execution cost is \(\text{Cost} = 1.40 + 3.20 - 2 \times 2.00 = 4.60 - 4.00 = +0.60\). Because the cost is positive (+0.60), the trader cannot lock in upfront cash arbitrage. The bid-ask spread absorbs the mid-price concavity.

A trader fits a 4th-degree polynomial \(\sigma(k) = c_0 + c_1 k + c_2 k^2 + c_3 k^3 + c_4 k^4\) to option quotes. At \(k = -0.4\), the fit produces \(w = 0.05\), \(w' = -0.35\), and \(w'' = -0.50\). Calculate \(g(-0.4)\) and state whether this fitted polynomial can be used for exotic pricing.

Worked Solution

Evaluate \(g(-0.4)\): \(\left(1 - \frac{-0.4 \times (-0.35)}{2 \times 0.05}\right)^2 = \left(1 - \frac{0.14}{0.10}\right)^2 = (1 - 1.4)^2 = (-0.4)^2 = 0.16\). Second term: \(-\frac{(-0.35)^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{0.05} + 0.25\right) = -\frac{0.1225}{4}(20.25) = -0.030625 \times 20.25 \approx -0.62016\). Third term: \(\frac{w''}{2} = \frac{-0.50}{2} = -0.25\). Summing: \(g(-0.4) = 0.16 - 0.62016 - 0.25 = -0.71016 < 0\). Because \(g(-0.4) < 0\), the polynomial contains severe butterfly arbitrage and cannot be used for pricing.

Explain how a market maker uses butterfly spread quotes (fly spreads) to directly quote smile curvature in foreign exchange and equity index markets.

Worked Solution

In FX markets, brokers quote the 25-delta butterfly spread: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BF}25} = \frac{\sigma_{25\mathrm{C}} + \sigma_{25\mathrm{P}}}{2} - \sigma_{\mathrm{ATM}}\). This quote isolates the pure curvature (smile height) of the volatility smile independent of directional skew (risk reversal). A higher butterfly quote indicates fatter risk-neutral tails and higher kurtosis.

How does the Carr–Madan spanning theorem express an arbitrary twice-differentiable contingent claim payoff \(H(S_T)\) in terms of an infinite continuum of infinitesimal butterfly spreads?

Worked Solution

By Taylor's theorem with integral remainder, \(H(S_T) = H(\kappa) + H'(\kappa)(S_T - \kappa) + \int_0^\kappa H''(K)(K - S_T)^+ \mathrm{d}K + \int_\kappa^\infty H''(K)(S_T - K)^+ \mathrm{d}K\). Since an infinitesimal butterfly spread at strike \(K\) synthesizes the Dirac delta payoff \(\delta(S_T - K)\), the integral weights \(H''(K)\) represent the continuous holding of butterfly spreads, pricing any non-linear claim through static replication.

Why does a negative density factor \(g(k) < 0\) cause Dupire's local volatility PDE to become ill-posed and blow up numerically?

Worked Solution

Dupire's local variance formula in total variance coordinates is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(k, \tau) = \frac{\partial w / \partial \tau}{g(k)}\). When \(g(k) < 0\), the local variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2\) becomes negative (imaginary local volatility), transforming the parabolic forward Fokker–Planck PDE into a backward heat equation, which is severely ill-posed and blows up exponentially under any numerical solver.

Describe the convex projection technique used to repair butterfly arbitrage in a fitted volatility slice.

Worked Solution

Convex projection formulates a constrained quadratic programming problem: minimize the weighted distance \(\sum_i w_i (C_i - \tilde{C}_i)^2\) subject to linear convexity constraints \(C_{i+1} - 2C_i + C_{i-1} \ge 0\) and monotonicity constraints \(C_{i+1} \le C_i\). Solving this convex optimization problem replaces the arbitrageable prices with the nearest arbitrage-free convex price curve.

    Options

  2. What must be true of European call prices across strikes \(K\) to ensure no butterfly arbitrage exists in a smooth market?

    Options

    Convexity ensures that every butterfly spread has non-negative cost, preventing costless cash arbitrage.

    When \(w'(k) = 0\) and \(w''(k) = 0\), \(g(k) = (1 - 0)^2 - 0 + 0 = 1.000\).

  4. What happens to the risk-neutral probability density if a fitted implied volatility slice produces \(g(k) < 0\)?

    Options

    Because the risk-neutral density is proportional to \(g(k)\), a negative \(g(k)\) implies a negative probability density.

    \(\Pi = C(90) - 2C(100) + C(110) = 14.00 - 20.00 + 5.50 = -0.50\).

  6. Why do unconstrained polynomial fits of implied volatility across strikes frequently induce butterfly arbitrage?

    Options

    High-order polynomials exhibit wing oscillations, generating negative \(w''(k)\) and negative \(g(k)\) values.

  7. What is the upper bound on the absolute value of the slope of a European call price with respect to strike: \(|\frac{\partial C}{\partial K}|\)?

    Options

    A bull call spread cannot pay more than the strike difference \(\Delta K\), so \(\Delta C / \Delta K \le D(\tau)\).

  8. What is the theoretical minimum terminal payoff of a standard long butterfly spread at maturity \(T\)?

    A long butterfly payoff is piecewise linear, continuous, and non-negative everywhere: \(\Pi_T(S_T) \ge 0\).

  9. How does butterfly arbitrage affect Dupire's local volatility extraction engine?

    Options

    Dupire's formula divides calendar theta by \(g(k)\); when \(g(k) < 0\), the local variance is negative, rendering the PDE ill-posed.
