Local Volatility: Dupire's PDE and Forward Equations

In 1994, Bruno Dupire solved a fundamental problem in quantitative finance: how to construct a continuous one-dimensional diffusion process that perfectly recovers the entire two-dimensional continuum of market option prices. By differentiating the Fokker–Planck forward equation, Dupire established that instantaneous volatility is uniquely determined by the ratio of option calendar theta to strike gamma. Local volatility provides a complete market model that prices exotic derivatives consistently with vanilla options. At the money, local volatility displays an iconic geometric signature: its strike slope is exactly twice that of implied volatility.

  • Introduces: local volatility
  • Prerequisites: volatility smile and skew, butterfly arbitrage, calendar arbitrage, Itô's lemma

1. The Dupire Forward Equation and Local Diffusion

In standard Black–Scholes theory, volatility is a constant parameter. In contrast, Bruno Dupire (1994), "Pricing with a Smile", Risk, posited that the underlying asset follows a one-dimensional Markovian diffusion where instantaneous volatility is a deterministic function of both spot price and calendar time:

\[ \mathrm{d}S_t = (r_t - q_t) S_t \mathrm{d}t + \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(S_t, t) S_t \mathrm{d}W_t^\mathbb{Q} \]

where \(W_t^\mathbb{Q}\) is a standard Brownian motion under risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(r_t\) is the risk-free rate, and \(q_t\) is the continuous dividend yield.

Definition 5.1 (Local Volatility). Local volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(S, t)\) is the state-dependent diffusion coefficient of a one-dimensional Markovian asset price process chosen such that the model matches the market prices of all European vanilla options across all strikes and maturities.

Let \(p(S_T, T \mid S_t, t)\) denote the transition probability density of \(S_T\) given \(S_t\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\). The evolution of \(p\) with respect to terminal variables \((S_T, T)\) is governed by the Fokker–Planck (Kolmogorov forward) PDE:

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial T} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial S_T}\left[(r_T - q_T) S_T p\right] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial S_T^2}\left[\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(S_T, T) S_T^2 p\right] \]

Theorem 5.1 (Dupire's Local Volatility Formula). Given a continuum of European call prices \(C(K, T)\) twice-differentiable in strike \(K\) and differentiable in expiration \(T\), the local variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(K, T)\) is uniquely given by:

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(K, T) = \frac{\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} + q_T C + (r_T - q_T) K \frac{\partial C}{\partial K}}{\frac{1}{2} K^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}} \]

Proof. The discounted call price is \(C(K, T) = D(T) \int_K^\infty (S_T - K) p(S_T, T) \mathrm{d}S_T\), where \(D(T) = \exp(-\int_t^T r_u \mathrm{d}u)\). Differentiating \(C\) with respect to strike \(K\):

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = -D(T) \int_K^\infty p(S_T, T) \mathrm{d}S_T, \quad \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = D(T) p(K, T) \]

Next, differentiate undiscounted call price \(\bar{C} = e^{\int_t^T r_u \mathrm{d}u} C\) with respect to \(T\):

\[ \frac{\partial \bar{C}}{\partial T} = \int_K^\infty (S_T - K) \frac{\partial p}{\partial T} \mathrm{d}S_T \]

Substitute the forward Fokker–Planck equation for \(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\) into the integral and integrate by parts twice. The first integral evaluates to \(-(r_T - q_T) K \int_K^\infty p \mathrm{d}S_T = (r_T - q_T) K \frac{\partial \bar{C}}{\partial K}\). The second integral evaluates to \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(K, T) K^2 p(K, T) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(K, T) K^2 \frac{\partial^2 \bar{C}}{\partial K^2}\). Converting back to discounted call price \(C\) yields the result. ■

2. Local Volatility in Total Variance Coordinates

In market implementations, evaluating Dupire's formula via call price derivatives is numerically unstable because \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\) approaches zero in the wings, leading to zero-over-zero indeterminacy. Transforming to forward log-moneyness \(k = \ln(K/F_T)\) and total implied variance \(w(k, \tau) = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau)\tau\) yields the elegant Gatheral representation Gatheral (2006):

Theorem 5.2 (Dupire's Formula in Total Variance). In total implied variance coordinates, local variance is the ratio of calendar derivative to the Gatheral–Jacquier density factor:

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(k, \tau) = \frac{\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau}(k, \tau)}{g(k, \tau)} \]

where \(g(k, \tau) = \left(1 - \frac{k w'(k)}{2 w(k)}\right)^2 - \frac{w'(k)^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{w(k)} + \frac{1}{4}\right) + \frac{w''(k)}{2}\).

This formulation reveals the profound connection between arbitrage freedom and local volatility existence:

  • Absence of Calendar Arbitrage: Ensures the numerator is non-negative: \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau} \ge 0\).
  • Absence of Butterfly Arbitrage: Ensures the denominator is strictly positive: \(g(k) > 0\).

Thus, a volatility surface admits a strictly positive, well-defined local volatility diffusion if and only if it is simultaneously free of calendar and butterfly arbitrage!

3. The ATM "Twice the Slope" Rule

A celebrated empirical property discovered by Derman, Kani, and Dupire links the slope of local volatility to the slope of implied volatility near the money Gatheral (2006).

Theorem 5.3 (The 2x Rule of Thumb). At short maturities (\(\tau \to 0\)), the slope of local volatility with respect to log-moneyness at the money (\(k = 0\)) is exactly twice the slope of implied volatility:

\[ \left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} \approx 2 \left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} \]

Proof Sketch. Under a local volatility diffusion, the implied volatility over a short horizon \(\tau\) is approximately the harmonic average of local volatility along the expected asset path: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K, \tau) \approx \frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^\tau \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{E}[S_u], u)\mathrm{d}u\). Expanding \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(S)\) in a Taylor series around \(S_0\): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(S) \approx \sigma_0 + \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}' \cdot (S - S_0)\). For a call struck at \(K\), conditioning on terminal arrival at \(K\) implies the average asset level along the path is roughly \(\frac{S_0 + K}{2}\). Therefore, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K) \approx \sigma_0 + \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}' \cdot \frac{K - S_0}{2}\). Differentiating with respect to \(K\) yields \(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial K} \approx \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\partial K}\). Inverting gives \(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\partial K} \approx 2 \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial K}\). ■

Hypothesis: Local volatility extracted via Dupire's formula will produce an ATM log-moneyness skew slope that is approximately twice the empirical implied volatility skew slope (\(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\partial k} / \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k} \approx 2.0\)).

"""Lab 1 · Local Volatility: Dupire's Equation and Surface Extraction.

Extracts Dupire local volatility sigma_loc(k, tau) from total variance w(k, tau)
and Gatheral-Jacquier density factor g(k, tau). Verifies the rule of thumb that
local volatility skew is approximately twice the implied volatility skew at ATM.
All data is SIMULATED (seed 42).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def svi_derivs(k: float, a: float, b: float, rho: float, m: float, sigma: float) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    diff = k - m
    disc = math.sqrt(diff**2 + sigma**2)
    w = a + b * (rho * diff + disc)
    w_p = b * (rho + diff / disc)
    w_pp = b * (sigma**2) / (disc**3)
    return w, w_p, w_pp


def g_factor(k: float, a: float, b: float, rho: float, m: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    w, w_p, w_pp = svi_derivs(k, a, b, rho, m, sigma)
    term1 = (1.0 - (k * w_p) / (2.0 * w)) ** 2
    term2 = (w_p**2 / 4.0) * (1.0 / w + 0.25)
    term3 = 0.5 * w_pp
    return term1 - term2 + term3


def main() -> None:
    t1 = 0.40
    t2 = 0.60
    dt = t2 - t1
    t_mid = 0.50

    # Calibrated SVI parameters at t1 and t2 (SIMULATED)
    p1 = {"a": 0.024, "b": 0.100, "rho": -0.35, "m": 0.008, "sigma": 0.070}
    p2 = {"a": 0.036, "b": 0.125, "rho": -0.35, "m": 0.012, "sigma": 0.085}

    # Interpolated parameters at t_mid
    p_mid = {
        "a": 0.5 * (p1["a"] + p2["a"]),
        "b": 0.5 * (p1["b"] + p2["b"]),
        "rho": -0.35,
        "m": 0.5 * (p1["m"] + p2["m"]),
        "sigma": 0.5 * (p1["sigma"] + p2["sigma"]),
    }

    k_grid = [-0.40, -0.20, -0.10, 0.0, 0.10, 0.20, 0.40]

    print("Log-Moneyness (k) | Implied Vol (sigma_BS) | Local Vol (sigma_loc) | Density Factor g(k)")
    print("-" * 85)

    bs_vols: list[float] = []
    loc_vols: list[float] = []

    for k in k_grid:
        w1, _, _ = svi_derivs(k, **p1)
        w2, _, _ = svi_derivs(k, **p2)
        dw_dt = (w2 - w1) / dt

        g = g_factor(k, **p_mid)
        w_mid, _, _ = svi_derivs(k, **p_mid)
        sigma_bs = math.sqrt(w_mid / t_mid)

        loc_var = dw_dt / g
        sigma_loc = math.sqrt(max(0.0, loc_var))

        bs_vols.append(sigma_bs)
        loc_vols.append(sigma_loc)

        print(f"{k:17.2f} | {sigma_bs:22.4f} | {sigma_loc:21.4f} | {g:19.4f}")

    # Slope comparison around ATM (k in [-0.10, 0.10])
    idx_minus = k_grid.index(-0.10)
    idx_plus = k_grid.index(0.10)
    dk = k_grid[idx_plus] - k_grid[idx_minus]

    bs_skew = (bs_vols[idx_plus] - bs_vols[idx_minus]) / dk
    loc_skew = (loc_vols[idx_plus] - loc_vols[idx_minus]) / dk
    ratio = loc_skew / bs_skew if bs_skew != 0.0 else 0.0

    print("-" * 85)
    print(f"ATM Implied Volatility Skew (d sigma_BS / dk):  {bs_skew:.4f}")
    print(f"ATM Local Volatility Skew   (d sigma_loc / dk): {loc_skew:.4f}")
    print(f"Empirical Ratio (Local Skew / Implied Skew):     {ratio:.2f}")
    print("Dupire Smile Exaggeration Factor in [1.5, 2.5]:  ", 1.5 <= ratio <= 2.5)
    print("Local Volatility Exists & Positive Everywhere:  ", all(v > 0.0 for v in loc_vols))

Where the canon breaks

Although local volatility is mathematically complete and exact for vanilla replication, it fails fundamentally as a dynamic model of market reality:

  • Forward Smile Flattening: As time advances in a pure local volatility model, the risk-neutral distribution conditional on future spot levels becomes increasingly normal due to central limit effects. Consequently, local volatility models predict that forward smiles flatten rapidly (\(\sim 1/\sqrt{\tau}\)), whereas actual market smiles remain persistently steep across forward horizons.
  • Inverse Dynamics ("Sticky Strike" Fallacy): When the underlying asset price drops, a local volatility model assumes the volatility at each fixed nominal strike remains invariant. In reality, market smiles tend to follow "sticky delta", shifting horizontally with spot movements. Pure local volatility models systematically mis-hedge delta and vega for barrier and cliquet options.
  • Stochastic Local Volatility (SLV) as the Synthesis: Modern trading desks combine local volatility with stochastic volatility: \(\mathrm{d}S_t = r S_t \mathrm{d}t + \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(S_t, t) \sqrt{v_t} S_t \mathrm{d}W_t\). The stochastic variance \(v_t\) preserves realistic forward smile dynamics and volatility-of-volatility, while the leverage function \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}\) calibrates vanilla quotes.

Misconceptions

  • Local volatility represents the market's forecast of what implied volatility will be in the future.

    Local volatility is an instantaneous conditional diffusion coefficient: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(K, T) = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[v_T \mid S_T = K]\). It is a mathematical calibration construct, not a forward expectation of Black–Scholes volatility.

  • A calibrated local volatility model correctly prices exotic options such as barrier and forward-starting options.

    Calibrating to vanilla options guarantees only that marginal one-dimensional distributions \(S_T\) match the market. Path-dependent exotics depend on multi-dimensional joint distributions \((S_{t_1}, \dots, S_{t_n})\), which local volatility distorts through artificial forward smile flattening.

  • Local volatility can be computed accurately by applying standard finite-difference formulas directly to market strike quotes.

    Market quotes are spaced coarsely and contaminated by bid-ask noise. Differentiating discrete quotes twice with respect to strike amplifies noise catastrophically, creating spurious negative densities and unstable local volatilities. A smooth arbitrage-free parameterization like SVI must precede differentiation.

Practice problems

At ATM forward log-moneyness \(k = 0\) and \(\tau = 0.5\), the calendar derivative is \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau} = 0.040\) and the Gatheral–Jacquier factor is \(g(0) = 1.050\). Calculate the local variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2\) and the instantaneous local volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}\).

Worked Solution

Using Theorem 5.2, local variance is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(0, 0.5) = \frac{\partial w / \partial \tau}{g(0)} = \frac{0.040}{1.050} \approx 0.038095\). Taking the positive square root: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}} = \sqrt{0.038095} \approx 0.19518\) or 19.52%.

An equity index has an ATM Black–Scholes implied volatility slope of \(\left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} = -0.320\). Using the 2x rule of thumb, estimate the local volatility skew slope at the money.

Worked Solution

According to Theorem 5.3, the ATM local volatility slope is approximately twice the implied volatility slope: \(\left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} \approx 2 \times (-0.320) = -0.640\). The local volatility skew is twice as steep as the market implied volatility smile at the money.

At strike \(K = 100\) and maturity \(T = 1\), market call prices yield \(\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} = 2.50\), \(C = 10.00\), \(\frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = -0.40\), and \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = 0.0080\). Interest rates and dividends are zero (\(r = q = 0\)). Compute Dupire local variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(100, 1)\).

Worked Solution

Under \(r = q = 0\), Dupire's formula simplifies to \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2 = \frac{\partial C / \partial T}{\frac{1}{2} K^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}}\). The numerator is 2.50. The denominator is \(\frac{1}{2} (100)^2 (0.0080) = \frac{1}{2}(10000)(0.0080) = 5000 \times 0.0080 = 40.0\). Dividing numerator by denominator: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2 = \frac{2.50}{40.0} = 0.0625\). Local volatility is \(\sqrt{0.0625} = 0.250\) (25%).

Prove that for a flat implied volatility surface \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K, T) \equiv \sigma_0 > 0\), Dupire's formula yields a constant local volatility equal to \(\sigma_0\).

Worked Solution

For a flat surface, total variance is \(w(k, \tau) = \sigma_0^2 \tau\). Differentiating with respect to \(\tau\): \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau} = \sigma_0^2\). Since \(w\) does not depend on \(k\), \(w'(k) = 0\) and \(w''(k) = 0\). The Gatheral–Jacquier factor evaluates to \(g(k) = (1 - 0)^2 - 0 + 0 = 1\). Substituting into Theorem 5.2: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(k, \tau) = \frac{\sigma_0^2}{1} = \sigma_0^2\). Taking the square root gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}} \equiv \sigma_0\) everywhere, proving consistency with Black–Scholes.

Prove that if a calibrated surface contains calendar arbitrage at \((k^*, \tau^*)\) with \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau} < 0\), local variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2\) is strictly negative (assuming absence of butterfly arbitrage).

Worked Solution

Under absence of butterfly arbitrage, the density factor satisfies \(g(k^*, \tau^*) > 0\). By Theorem 5.2, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(k^*, \tau^*) = \frac{\partial w / \partial \tau}{g(k^*, \tau^*)}\). Because the numerator \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau} < 0\) is strictly negative and the denominator is strictly positive, the quotient is strictly negative: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2 < 0\). Local volatility is therefore purely imaginary, demonstrating that calendar arbitrage destroys the physical validity of local diffusion models.

Show that Dupire local variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(K, T)\) equals the risk-neutral conditional expectation of instantaneous stochastic variance: \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[v_T \mid S_T = K]\).

Worked Solution

By Gyöngy's (1986) mimicking theorem, if a continuous semi-martingale \(S_t\) satisfies \(\mathrm{d}S_t = \mu_t \mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{v_t} S_t \mathrm{d}W_t\), there exists a one-dimensional Markovian diffusion \(\mathrm{d}X_t = \mu(X_t, t)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(X_t, t)X_t \mathrm{d}W_t^*\) such that \(X_t\) and \(S_t\) share identical one-dimensional marginal probability distributions for all \(t \ge 0\), where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(K, T) = \mathbb{E}[v_T \mid S_T = K]\). Because European option prices depend solely on marginal distributions \(S_T\), Dupire's local variance is exactly the conditional expectation of instantaneous variance given terminal spot arrival at strike \(K\).

An algorithmic options trader extracts local volatility across strikes for 1-month SPX options. At strike 90% moneyness, implied vol is 24%, but extracted local vol is 38%. At strike 110% moneyness, implied vol is 16%, but local vol is 11%. Explain why local volatility wings are more extreme than implied volatility wings.

Worked Solution

Implied volatility represents an integrated, time-averaged expected volatility over the entire path \([0, \tau]\). In contrast, local volatility measures the instantaneous volatility conditioned on arriving at that specific strike. Because an asset starting ATM only reaches the 90% strike after experiencing negative drift and elevated volatility along the path, the conditional instantaneous volatility required at that boundary must be significantly higher than the average, resulting in an amplified, steeper local volatility smile.

A quant desk attempts to compute local volatility by applying central differences to raw exchange strike data with \$5 strike spacing and 30-day maturity spacing. What numerical pathology will occur?

Worked Solution

Raw exchange quotes contain microstructure noise (bid-ask bounce). Second-order central differences \(\frac{C_{i+1} - 2C_i + C_{i-1}}{\Delta K^2}\) divide tiny price fluctuations by \((\Delta K)^2 = 25\), amplifying quote noise by a factor of hundreds. This causes the estimated denominator to oscillate violently between large positive and negative numbers, producing negative local variances and numerical blow-up in pricing engines.

Why do reverse barrier options (e.g. up-and-out calls) price significantly cheaper under local volatility models than under stochastic volatility models calibrated to the same vanilla surface?

Worked Solution

In equity local volatility models, local volatility declines as spot rises (the negative skew). As the stock approaches the upper barrier, local volatility drops, meaning the stock has less diffusion variance to cross the barrier, but if it stays near the barrier, the call value is depressed. More critically, stochastic volatility allows sudden volatility spikes independent of spot level, enabling the stock to punch through the barrier with higher probability, leading to higher knock-out frequencies and different option values.

Derive why pure local volatility models predict that the forward smile skew decays as \(\tau^{-1/2}\), and explain why this contradicts market prices of forward-starting options.

Worked Solution

In a diffusion \(\mathrm{d}S_t = \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(S_t, t)S_t \mathrm{d}W_t\), as time horizon \(T_2 - T_1\) increases, the transition density of log returns converges toward Gaussianity by the central limit theorem for mixing diffusions. Consequently, higher cumulants (skewness and kurtosis) decay at rates \(T_2^{-1/2}\) and \(T_2^{-1}\). Market forward smiles, however, remain persistently skewed because market participants anticipate that future volatility will remain stochastic with jump risk, causing pure local volatility models to underprice forward-starting skew.

In Stochastic Local Volatility (SLV) models, the asset price follows \(\mathrm{d}S_t = r S_t \mathrm{d}t + L(S_t, t)\sqrt{v_t} S_t \mathrm{d}W_t^S\). Formulate the non-linear PDE or particle method used to calibrate the leverage function \(L(S, t)\).

Worked Solution

By Gyöngy's theorem, matching the vanilla market surface requires \(L^2(S, t)\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[v_t \mid S_t = S] = \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(S, t)\). Solving for the leverage function gives \(L(S, t) = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(S, t)}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[v_t \mid S_t = S]}}\). Because the conditional expectation depends on the unknown transition density of the joint system \((S_t, v_t)\), calibration requires either solving a 2D forward Fokker–Planck PDE or simulating an interacting particle system (particle method) where kernel density regression estimates the conditional expectation at each time step.

How does Tikhonov regularization stabilize the inverse problem of calibrating Dupire local volatility directly from discrete, noisy option prices?

Worked Solution

Tikhonov regularization defines the objective \(\min_{\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}} \sum_i w_i (C_{\mathrm{model}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}) - C_{\mathrm{market}})^2 + \alpha \int \int \left(\|\nabla \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}\|^2 + \beta \|\Delta \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}\|^2\right)\mathrm{d}S \mathrm{d}t\). The penalty term suppresses high-frequency spatial oscillations and curvature spikes in \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}\), transforming an ill-posed inverse problem into a well-conditioned optimization that guarantees a smooth, non-oscillating local volatility surface.
