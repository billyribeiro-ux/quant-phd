Volatility Smile & Skew: The Death of Black–Scholes Flat Volatility

Modern option pricing began with the constant-volatility model of Black, Scholes, and Merton. If asset returns were truly geometric Brownian motion, inverting market prices across strikes and maturities would yield a constant parameter. Instead, option markets post-1987 exhibit persistent, strike-dependent implied volatility curves. Equity markets trade with a steep negative skew where out-of-the-money puts command large premia, while foreign exchange markets display symmetric smiles. Understanding the geometry and economic origins of this surface is the prerequisite for all quantitative derivatives modeling.

  • Introduces: volatility smile and skew
  • Prerequisites: Black–Scholes–Merton PDE, dynamic replication, put-call parity

1. The Black–Scholes Inversion and Empirical Smile

Let \(S_t\) denote the spot price of an underlying asset, \(F_{t,T} = S_t e^{(r - q)\tau}\) its forward price for expiration \(T\), and \(\tau = T - t\) the time to maturity. The Black–Scholes European call pricing function Black & Scholes (1973) maps forward price \(F\), strike \(K\), tenor \(\tau\), risk-free discount factor \(D(\tau) = e^{-r\tau}\), and volatility \(\sigma\) to the option premium:

\[ \begin{aligned} C_{\mathrm{BS}}(F, K, \tau, \sigma) &= D(\tau) \left[F N(d_1) - K N(d_2)\right] \\ d_1 &= \frac{\ln(F/K) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} \\ d_2 &= d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau} \end{aligned} \]

Because Black–Scholes vega \(\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial C_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial \sigma} = D(\tau) F \sqrt{\tau} n(d_1) > 0\) is strictly positive for all \(\tau > 0\) and \(K > 0\), the map \(\sigma \mapsto C_{\mathrm{BS}}\) is strictly monotonic. By the inverse function theorem, for any observed non-arbitrage market call price \(C_{\mathrm{mkt}} \in (D(\tau)\max(F - K, 0), D(\tau)F)\), there exists a unique implied volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}\) satisfying:

\[ C_{\mathrm{BS}}(F, K, \tau, \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}) = C_{\mathrm{mkt}}(K, \tau) \]

Definition 1.1 (Volatility Smile and Skew). The volatility smile and skew is the empirical relationship showing that the Black–Scholes implied volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K, \tau)\) varies systematically with strike price \(K\) and expiry \(\tau\), directly violating the Black–Scholes hypothesis of a constant diffusion coefficient.

To compare options across assets and expiries, strikes are parameterized by forward log-moneyness:

\[ k = \ln\left(\frac{K}{F_{t,T}}\right) \]

At-the-money (ATM) corresponds to \(k = 0\) (\(K = F_{t,T}\)). Out-of-the-money (OTM) puts have \(k < 0\), and OTM calls have \(k > 0\). In equity index markets, the empirical curve \(k \mapsto \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(k, \tau)\) is monotonically decreasing over traded strikes, a pattern termed the equity volatility skew or volatility smirk Gatheral (2006).

2. The 1987 Structural Break and Economic Drivers

Prior to the October 19, 1987 market crash, empirical implied volatility surfaces across US equities were essentially flat. Option traders priced vanilla contracts with near-constant volatility. Following the crash, in which the S&P 500 plunged 20.5% in a single trading session, market participants instituted a structural repricing of downside protection Gatheral (2006).

Theorem 1.1 (Put-Call Implied Volatility Equivalence). Under European put-call parity with deterministic discount factor \(D(\tau)\), the Black–Scholes implied volatility of a European call option equals the Black–Scholes implied volatility of a European put option at the identical strike \(K\) and expiration \(\tau\):

\[ \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^{\mathrm{call}}(K, \tau) = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^{\mathrm{put}}(K, \tau) \]

Proof. European put-call parity enforces \(C_{\mathrm{mkt}}(K, \tau) - P_{\mathrm{mkt}}(K, \tau) = D(\tau)(F - K)\). By definition of the Black–Scholes pricing formula, \(C_{\mathrm{BS}}(F, K, \tau, \sigma) - P_{\mathrm{BS}}(F, K, \tau, \sigma) = D(\tau)(F - K)\) holds identically for any choice of \(\sigma\). Let \(\sigma^* = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^{\mathrm{call}}(K, \tau)\), so that \(C_{\mathrm{BS}}(F, K, \tau, \sigma^*) = C_{\mathrm{mkt}}(K, \tau)\). Substituting into market put-call parity yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\mathrm{mkt}}(K, \tau) &= C_{\mathrm{mkt}}(K, \tau) - D(\tau)(F - K) \\ &= C_{\mathrm{BS}}(F, K, \tau, \sigma^*) - D(\tau)(F - K) \\ &= P_{\mathrm{BS}}(F, K, \tau, \sigma^*) \end{aligned} \]

Since the Black–Scholes put pricing function is strictly monotonic in volatility (\(\partial P_{\mathrm{BS}}/\partial \sigma = \mathcal{V} > 0\)), \(\sigma^*\) is the unique implied volatility for the put option. Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^{\mathrm{put}}(K, \tau) = \sigma^* = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^{\mathrm{call}}(K, \tau)\). ■

Two primary economic explanations govern the downward slope \(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k} < 0\):

  1. The Leverage Hypothesis: As a firm's equity value declines, its debt-to-equity ratio increases, making the residual equity structurally riskier and raising future equity volatility. However, empirical calibrations indicate the financial leverage effect accounts for less than one-third of the observed equity index skew magnitude.
  2. Crash Risk Aversion and the Variance Risk Premium: Institutional investors hold long equity portfolios and purchase OTM index puts as disaster insurance. Market makers shorting these puts demand a substantial risk premium, inflating OTM put prices and depressing implied risk-neutral return distributions relative to physical expectations.

3. Total Implied Variance Coordinates

In quantitative modeling, working directly with annualized implied volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K, \tau)\) introduces cumbersome square-root factors \(\sqrt{\tau}\). Following Gatheral (2006), we define total implied variance:

\[ w(k, \tau) = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau) \, \tau \]

The dimensionless quantity \(w(k, \tau)\) measures the cumulative variance of log returns over the option horizon. In total variance coordinates, the Black–Scholes \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) variables simplify to:

\[ d_1(k) = -\frac{k}{\sqrt{w(k)}} + \frac{\sqrt{w(k)}}{2}, \quad d_2(k) = d_1(k) - \sqrt{w(k)} = -\frac{k}{\sqrt{w(k)}} - \frac{\sqrt{w(k)}}{2} \]

Total variance transforms the non-linear surface into a geometry where calendar monotonicity and strike convexity take transparent analytical expressions.

Evaluates market option prices across strikes K, inverts the Black-Scholes formula
to extract implied volatilities sigma_BS(K), and converts them to total implied
variance w(k) = sigma_BS(K)^2 * tau as a function of log-moneyness k = ln(K / F).
All market data is SIMULATED (seed 42).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def bs_call_price(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> float:
    """Analytical Black-Scholes European call price."""
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return max(s - k, 0.0)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * math.sqrt(tau))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * math.sqrt(tau)
    return s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)


def bs_call_vega(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> float:
    """Analytical Black-Scholes call vega."""
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return 0.0
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * math.sqrt(tau))
    phi = math.exp(-0.5 * d1**2) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)
    return s * math.sqrt(tau) * phi


def implied_volatility(price: float, s: float, k: float, r: float, tau: float, is_call: bool = True) -> float:
    """Invert Black-Scholes price to find implied volatility using Newton-Raphson."""
    sigma = 0.25
    for _ in range(50):
        c = bs_call_price(s, k, r, sigma, tau)
        diff = c - price
        if abs(diff) < 1e-10:
            return sigma
        vega = bs_call_vega(s, k, r, sigma, tau)
        if vega < 1e-12:
            break
        sigma -= diff / vega
        if sigma <= 1e-4:
            sigma = 1e-4
    return sigma


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    rate = 0.03
    tau = 0.50
    fwd = spot * math.exp(rate * tau)

    # Simulated market quotes reflecting negative equity skew (SIMULATED)
    strikes = [80.0, 85.0, 90.0, 95.0, 100.0, 105.0, 110.0, 115.0, 120.0]
    market_vols = [0.285, 0.264, 0.245, 0.228, 0.212, 0.199, 0.188, 0.180, 0.174]

    print("Strike (K) | Log-Moneyness (k) | Market Call Price | Implied Vol (sigma) | Total Variance (w)")
    print("-" * 85)

    extracted_vols: list[float] = []
    total_vars: list[float] = []

    for k_val, vol_true in zip(strikes, market_vols, strict=True):
        c_market = bs_call_price(spot, k_val, rate, vol_true, tau)
        sigma_imp = implied_volatility(c_market, spot, k_val, rate, tau)
        k_log = math.log(k_val / fwd)
        w_val = sigma_imp**2 * tau

        extracted_vols.append(sigma_imp)
        total_vars.append(w_val)
        print(f"{k_val:10.1f} | {k_log:17.4f} | {c_market:17.4f} | {sigma_imp:19.4f} | {w_val:18.5f}")

    atm_vol = extracted_vols[4]
    otm_put_vol = extracted_vols[0]
    otm_call_vol = extracted_vols[-1]

    skew_slope = (otm_call_vol - otm_put_vol) / (math.log(120.0 / fwd) - math.log(80.0 / fwd))

    print("-" * 85)
    print(f"At-The-Money Implied Volatility (K=100.0): {atm_vol:.4f}")
    print(f"Downside Wing Volatility (K=80.0):         {otm_put_vol:.4f}")
    print(f"Upside Wing Volatility (K=120.0):          {otm_call_vol:.4f}")
    print(f"Empirical Smile Skew Slope (d_sigma / dk): {skew_slope:.4f}")
    print("Negative Skew Detected (Downside Vol > Upside Vol):", otm_put_vol > otm_call_vol)
    print("Black-Scholes Flat Volatility Hypothesis Violated:  True")

Where the canon breaks

The standard Black–Scholes model assumes asset log-returns are normally distributed with stationary quadratic variation. In actual markets, this assumption fails in three catastrophic dimensions:

  • Fat Tails and Extreme Jumps: Empirical asset returns exhibit leptokurtosis and discontinuous jumps. A 5-standard-deviation daily move is impossible under a Gaussian density (occurring once every 14,000 years), yet occurs once every few years in equity markets.
  • Stochastic and Clustering Volatility: Volatility is not constant; it exhibits mean-reversion, intraday clustering, and strong negative correlation with asset returns (the leverage/asymmetry effect).
  • Inconsistent Greek Sensitivities: Applying Black–Scholes delta \(\Delta_{\mathrm{BS}} = N(d_1)\) to hedge an option in the presence of a dynamic skew generates unhedged skew exposure. If volatility moves with the underlying asset price, the true minimum-variance hedge ratio is \(\Delta^* = \Delta_{\mathrm{BS}} + \mathcal{V} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial S}\). Ignoring skew risk leads to systematic hedging slippage.

Misconceptions

  • Implied volatility represents the market's unbiased statistical forecast of future realized physical volatility.

    Implied volatility is a risk-neutral pricing quote under measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), incorporating variance risk premia and crash hedging demand. In equity index markets, implied volatility exceeds realized volatility by 2 to 4 percentage points on average.

  • A European call option and a European put option at the same strike and expiration can trade at different implied volatilities if call demand differs from put demand.

    Put-call parity guarantees that European call and put options at the same strike and maturity must have identical Black–Scholes implied volatilities. Any discrepancy creates an immediate synthetic cash arbitrage.

  • The equity volatility skew is entirely driven by corporate debt-to-equity leverage ratios.

    Empirical studies demonstrate that corporate debt leverage explains less than one-third of index skew slope. The primary driver is risk aversion to discontinuous market crashes and institutional downside put demand.

Practice problems

An equity index forward price is \(F = 100\). A 6-month European call option (\(\tau = 0.5\)) with strike \(K = 95\) trades at a total implied variance of \(w = 0.0450\). Calculate the forward log-moneyness \(k\) and the annualized implied volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}\).

Worked Solution

First, compute forward log-moneyness using the definition \(k = \ln(K/F) = \ln(95/100) = \ln(0.95) \approx -0.051293\). Next, recall the relationship between total implied variance and annualized volatility: \(w = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2 \tau\). Solving for annualized volatility gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}} = \sqrt{w / \tau} = \sqrt{0.0450 / 0.5} = \sqrt{0.0900} = 0.3000\) or 30.00%.

Given an ATM option with \(\tau = 0.25\) years, \(F = 50\), \(K = 50\), and risk-free rate \(r = 0.04\). The ATM implied volatility is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}} = 0.20\). Compute the normalized Black–Scholes variables \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) and the total variance \(w\).

Worked Solution

For an ATM option, \(k = \ln(50/50) = 0\). Total variance is \(w = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2 \tau = 0.20^2 \times 0.25 = 0.04 \times 0.25 = 0.0100\). The square root of total variance is \(\sqrt{w} = \sqrt{0.0100} = 0.100\). Using the total variance formulation, \(d_1 = -\frac{k}{\sqrt{w}} + \frac{\sqrt{w}}{2} = 0 + \frac{0.100}{2} = 0.050\), and \(d_2 = d_1 - \sqrt{w} = 0.050 - 0.100 = -0.050\).

Suppose at strike \(K_1 = 90\) (\(k_1 = -0.1054\)), \(\sigma_1 = 0.25\), and at strike \(K_2 = 110\) (\(k_2 = 0.0953\)), \(\sigma_2 = 0.19\) for a 1-year expiration (\(\tau = 1.0\)). Compute the empirical finite-difference skew slope \(\frac{\Delta \sigma}{\Delta k}\).

Worked Solution

The difference in implied volatility is \(\Delta \sigma = \sigma_2 - \sigma_1 = 0.19 - 0.25 = -0.06\). The difference in forward log-moneyness is \(\Delta k = k_2 - k_1 = 0.0953 - (-0.1054) = 0.2007\). The empirical skew slope is therefore \(\frac{\Delta \sigma}{\Delta k} = \frac{-0.06}{0.2007} \approx -0.29895\), confirming a downward-sloping equity smirk of roughly -29.9% per unit of log-moneyness.

Prove that Black–Scholes European call vega \(\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial C_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial \sigma} = D(\tau) F \sqrt{\tau} n(d_1)\) is strictly positive for all \(\sigma > 0\), \(\tau > 0\), and \(F, K > 0\).

Worked Solution

Differentiating \(C_{\mathrm{BS}} = D(\tau)[F N(d_1) - K N(d_2)]\) with respect to \(\sigma\): \(\frac{\partial C_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial \sigma} = D(\tau) [F n(d_1)\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} - K n(d_2)\frac{\partial d_2}{\partial \sigma}]\). Note that \(\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} = -\frac{\ln(F/K)}{\sigma^2\sqrt{\tau}} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\tau}\) and \(\frac{\partial d_2}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} - \sqrt{\tau}\). Also, the identity \(F n(d_1) = K n(d_2)\) holds because \(\ln(F n(d_1) / (K n(d_2))) = \ln(F/K) - \frac{1}{2}(d_1^2 - d_2^2) = \ln(F/K) - \frac{1}{2}(d_1 - d_2)(d_1 + d_2) = \ln(F/K) - \frac{1}{2}(\sigma\sqrt{\tau})(2\ln(F/K)/(\sigma\sqrt{\tau})) = 0\). Factoring out \(F n(d_1)\) gives \(\frac{\partial C_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial \sigma} = D(\tau) F n(d_1) [\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} - \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial \sigma}] = D(\tau) F \sqrt{\tau} n(d_1)\). Since \(D(\tau) > 0\), \(F > 0\), \(\sqrt{\tau} > 0\), and the standard normal density \(n(d_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-d_1^2/2} > 0\), vega is strictly positive.

Prove that as \(\sigma \to \infty\), the Black–Scholes call price converges to the discounted forward price \(D(\tau)F\), and as \(\sigma \to 0\), it converges to intrinsic value \(D(\tau)\max(F - K, 0)\).

Worked Solution

As \(\sigma \to \infty\), \(d_1 = \frac{\ln(F/K)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau} \to +\infty\) and \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau} = \frac{\ln(F/K)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} - \frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau} \to -\infty\). Therefore, \(N(d_1) \to 1\) and \(N(d_2) \to 0\), so \(C_{\mathrm{BS}} \to D(\tau)[F(1) - K(0)] = D(\tau)F\). Conversely, as \(\sigma \to 0^+\), if \(F > K\), \(\ln(F/K) > 0\), so \(d_1, d_2 \to +\infty\), yielding \(C_{\mathrm{BS}} \to D(\tau)(F - K)\). If \(F < K\), \(\ln(F/K) < 0\), so \(d_1, d_2 \to -\infty\), yielding \(C_{\mathrm{BS}} \to 0\). If \(F = K\), \(d_1 \to 0^+\) and \(d_2 \to 0^-\), so \(C_{\mathrm{BS}} \to D(\tau)F(0.5 - 0.5) = 0\). In all cases, \(\lim_{\sigma \to 0} C_{\mathrm{BS}} = D(\tau)\max(F - K, 0)\).

Using the implicit function theorem, derive the formula for the minimum-variance hedge ratio \(\Delta^* = \frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}S}\) when implied volatility depends on the spot price: \(\sigma = \sigma(S, t)\).

Worked Solution

Let the market option price be represented as \(C(S, K, \tau) = C_{\mathrm{BS}}(S, K, \tau, \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(S, K, \tau))\). Applying the total derivative with respect to spot price \(S\): \(\Delta^* = \frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}S} = \frac{\partial C_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial S} + \frac{\partial C_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial \sigma} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial S}\). Recognizing \(\frac{\partial C_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial S} = \Delta_{\mathrm{BS}}\) and \(\frac{\partial C_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial \sigma} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}}\), we obtain \(\Delta^* = \Delta_{\mathrm{BS}} + \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial S}\). Because \(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial S} = -\frac{K}{S^2} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k} < 0\) for typical downward-sloping equity smiles, the skew adjustment reduces the call delta below its pure Black–Scholes value.

In an S&P 500 options chain, 1-month (\(\tau = 1/12\)) 90% moneyness puts trade at \(\sigma = 0.28\), while ATM options trade at \(\sigma = 0.18\). At 1-year maturity (\(\tau = 1.0\)), the 90% put trades at \(\sigma = 0.22\) while the ATM option trades at \(\sigma = 0.19\). Describe how the skew slope changes across maturity.

Worked Solution

At \(\tau = 1/12\), \(\Delta \sigma / \Delta k \approx (0.18 - 0.28) / (0 - \ln(0.90)) = -0.10 / 0.10536 = -0.949\). At \(\tau = 1.0\), \(\Delta \sigma / \Delta k \approx (0.19 - 0.22) / (0.10536) = -0.03 / 0.10536 = -0.285\). The empirical skew slope is substantially steeper at short maturities and flattens out at longer maturities, typically decaying at a rate roughly proportional to \(1/\sqrt{\tau}\) or \(\tau^{\alpha-1/2}\) as predicted by central limit theorem aggregation of return shocks.

Suppose realized volatility over a 30-day period was 14.5% annualized, while the 30-day ATM implied volatility at the start of the period was 18.2%. Compute the realized variance risk premium in volatility points and describe who profits from this spread.

Worked Solution

The volatility spread is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{IV}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{RV}} = 18.2\% - 14.5\% = +3.7\%\) (or in variance terms, \(0.182^2 - 0.145^2 = 0.03312 - 0.02102 = +0.01210\)). A systematic seller of index options (or variance swap seller) captures this positive variance risk premium, earning steady carry in exchange for bearing the tail risk of catastrophic market drawdowns.

Calculate total implied variance \(w(k)\) across three strikes \(k \in \{-0.1, 0, 0.1\}\) for \(\tau = 0.25\) when \(\sigma(-0.1) = 0.24\), \(\sigma(0) = 0.20\), and \(\sigma(0.1) = 0.18\). Is \(w(k)\) convex in \(k\)?

Worked Solution

At \(k = -0.1\), \(w(-0.1) = 0.24^2 \times 0.25 = 0.0576 \times 0.25 = 0.01440\). At \(k = 0\), \(w(0) = 0.20^2 \times 0.25 = 0.0400 \times 0.25 = 0.01000\). At \(k = 0.1\), \(w(0.1) = 0.18^2 \times 0.25 = 0.0324 \times 0.25 = 0.00810\). Check the second difference: \(\Delta^2 w = w(0.1) - 2w(0) + w(-0.1) = 0.00810 - 2(0.01000) + 0.01440 = 0.00810 - 0.02000 + 0.01440 = +0.00250 > 0\). The positive second difference confirms that total variance is locally convex across these strikes.

Under the Heston (1993) stochastic volatility model, show intuitively why a negative correlation \(\rho < 0\) between spot returns and volatility innovations generates a downward-sloping implied volatility skew.

Worked Solution

When \(\rho = \operatorname{Corr}(\mathrm{d}W_S, \mathrm{d}W_v) < 0\), negative asset price shocks are systematically accompanied by instantaneous increases in volatility. This creates a fat left tail in the risk-neutral distribution of log-returns, elevating the probability of deep out-of-the-money put payoffs relative to a lognormal density. To match these elevated market put prices, the Black–Scholes inversion must impute higher implied volatility to lower strikes, generating the characteristic downward-sloping equity skew.

At ultra-short expirations (\(\tau \to 0\)), standard diffusive stochastic volatility models predict that the ATM skew slope \(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\) tends to a finite constant \(\frac{\rho \eta}{2}\). In contrast, high-frequency empirical market data shows the skew explodes as \(\tau^{-H}\) with \(H \approx 0.1\). What does this imply for volatility modeling?

Worked Solution

This empirical power-law explosion in short-term skew contradicts smooth Brownian diffusion models (where Hurst parameter \(H = 1/2\)). It provides decisive empirical evidence for rough fractional volatility models, where log-volatility behaves as a fractional Brownian motion with \(H \in (0, 0.5)\), typically \(H \approx 0.1\), capturing the rough, non-Markovian nature of order flow and market dynamics.

Why does a deterministic local volatility model \(\mathrm{d}S_t = \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(S_t, t)S_t \mathrm{d}W_t\) calibrated to today's smile predict that tomorrow's smile moves counter to spot price movements (the sticky-strike vs sticky-delta dilemma)?

Worked Solution

In a pure local volatility model, the volatility function \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(S, t)\) is fixed in price space. If spot price \(S\) drops, the stock moves into a higher \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}\) region, which means the model's smile is 'sticky strike' rather than 'sticky delta'. In reality, when spot drops, the entire implied volatility curve shifts rightward in strike space (implied vol increases at every fixed delta), exposing the dynamic misspecification of static local volatility surfaces.

    Options

    Prior to the 1987 crash, equity option implied volatility surfaces were essentially flat. Post-crash, institutional demand for downside tail-risk hedges caused a permanent upward re-pricing of OTM puts.

  3. If the underlying forward price is \(F = 100\) and the strike price is \(K = 95.1229\), calculate the forward log-moneyness \(k = \ln(K/F)\).

    \(k = \ln(95.1229 / 100) = \ln(0.951229) \approx -0.0500\).

  4. Why must European call and European put options with identical strike \(K\) and expiration \(\tau\) share identical Black–Scholes implied volatilities?

    Options

    Because \(C_{\mathrm{mkt}} - P_{\mathrm{mkt}} = C_{\mathrm{BS}}(\sigma) - P_{\mathrm{BS}}(\sigma) = D(\tau)(F - K)\), the volatility equating \(C_{\mathrm{BS}}\) to \(C_{\mathrm{mkt}}\) must simultaneously equate \(P_{\mathrm{BS}}\) to \(P_{\mathrm{mkt}}\).

  5. An option has 3 months to maturity (\(\tau = 0.25\) years) and trades at an implied volatility of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}} = 0.40\) (40%). What is its total implied variance \(w\)?

    \(w = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2 \tau = 0.40^2 \times 0.25 = 0.16 \times 0.25 = 0.0400\).

  6. How does total implied variance \(w(k, \tau)\) scale with time to maturity \(\tau\) under the classical Black–Scholes model?

    Options

    Under constant volatility \(\sigma\), cumulative variance grows in direct linear proportion to elapsed time \(\tau\).

  7. What does a positive variance risk premium (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{IV}} > \sigma_{\mathrm{RV}}\)) indicate about market participant preferences?

    Options

    A positive variance risk premium reflects risk aversion: buyers pay more than fair expected variance to secure insurance against catastrophic market drops.

  8. If a 1-year option (\(\tau = 1.0\)) has a total variance \(w = 0.0625\), what is its annualized implied volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}\)?

    \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}} = \sqrt{w / \tau} = \sqrt{0.0625 / 1.0} = 0.2500\) (25%).

  9. Why does the standard Black–Scholes delta fail to provide a variance-minimizing hedge in the presence of an active equity skew?

    Options

    When implied volatility correlates with underlying asset price changes, the total derivative of the option price includes a vega-skew cross term that must be hedged.
