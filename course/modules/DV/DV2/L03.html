Calendar Arbitrage & Total Variance: Monotonicity Across Expirations

An option grants its holder the right, without obligation, to execute a trade at a pre-specified strike. For European options on non-dividend-paying assets, holding that right over a longer time horizon provides strictly greater optionality, dictating that call prices must increase monotonically with maturity. In terms of total implied variance, absence of calendar arbitrage requires that total variance never decrease with time to expiration across any strike. When two calibrated expiration slices cross, an arbitrageur can sell the expensive near-dated option, purchase the cheaper far-dated option, and lock in riskless cash profit. Enforcing calendar monotonicity across multi-tenor surfaces is a core challenge in volatility modeling.

  • Introduces: calendar arbitrage
  • Prerequisites: volatility smile and skew, butterfly arbitrage, martingale representation theorem

1. European Calendar Spreads and Payoff Dominance

Let \(S_t\) be a non-dividend-paying underlying asset with constant risk-free rate \(r \ge 0\). Consider two European call options with the identical strike \(K\) but different expiration dates \(T_1 < T_2\) (with tenors \(\tau_1 < \tau_2\)). The price of a European call at time \(t \le T_1\) under risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is:

\[ C(K, T) = e^{-r(T-t)} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[(S_T - K)^+ \mid \mathcal{F}_t\right] \]

Theorem 3.1 (Monotonicity of European Calls in Expiration). For a non-dividend-paying asset with non-negative interest rate \(r \ge 0\), European call prices are non-decreasing in expiration:

\[ C(K, \tau_1) \le C(K, \tau_2) \quad \forall \, \tau_1 < \tau_2 \]

Proof. Consider the discounted stock price \(\tilde{S}_t = e^{-rt} S_t\), which is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale. Express the discounted call price at maturity \(T_1\) as:

\[ e^{-r T_1} (S_{T_1} - K)^+ = \max\left(\tilde{S}_{T_1} - K e^{-r T_1}, 0\right) \]

At time \(T_2 > T_1\), because \(e^{-r T_2} \le e^{-r T_1}\), we have \(\max\left(\tilde{S}_{T_2} - K e^{-r T_2}, 0\right) \ge \max\left(\tilde{S}_{T_2} - K e^{-r T_1}, 0\right)\). Since \(x \mapsto \max(x - c, 0)\) is a convex function, conditional Jensen's inequality and the martingale property of \(\tilde{S}\) imply:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[\max\left(\tilde{S}_{T_2} - K e^{-r T_1}, 0\right) \mid \mathcal{F}_{T_1}\right] \\ &\quad \ge \max\left(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\tilde{S}_{T_2} \mid \mathcal{F}_{T_1}] - K e^{-r T_1}, 0\right) \\ &\quad = \max\left(\tilde{S}_{T_1} - K e^{-r T_1}, 0\right) \\ &\quad = e^{-r T_1} (S_{T_1} - K)^+ \end{aligned} \]

Taking expectations conditioned on \(\mathcal{F}_t\) and multiplying by \(e^{rt}\) gives \(C(K, T_2) \ge C(K, T_1)\). ■

2. Calendar Arbitrage in Total Implied Variance

In quantitative surface modeling, calendar arbitrage is formulated in terms of total implied variance \(w(k, \tau) = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau)\tau\) as a function of forward log-moneyness \(k = \ln(K/F_\tau)\) Gatheral (2006).

Definition 3.1 (Calendar Arbitrage). An option market or implied volatility surface exhibits calendar arbitrage (or time arbitrage) if there exist two expiration tenors \(\tau_1 < \tau_2\) and a strike \(K\) (or forward log-moneyness \(k\)) such that:

\[ C(K, \tau_1) > C(K, \tau_2) \quad \text{or} \quad w(k, \tau_1) > w(k, \tau_2) \]

In a continuously differentiable total variance surface, absence of calendar arbitrage is equivalent to the non-negativity of calendar derivative everywhere: \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau}(k, \tau) \ge 0\).

If \(w(k, \tau_1) > w(k, \tau_2)\) for some \(k\), the near-dated call option is more expensive than the far-dated call option with the same forward log-moneyness. An arbitrageur exploits this by executing a calendar spread trade: sell the overpriced near-dated option for \(C(\tau_1)\), buy the underpriced far-dated option for \(C(\tau_2) < C(\tau_1)\), and invest the positive upfront cash difference \(C(\tau_1) - C(\tau_2) > 0\) in the risk-free bond account.

Example 3.1 (Terminal Cash Flow of Calendar Arbitrage Trade). At near-expiry \(T_1\), the short call matures with payoff \(-(S_{T_1} - K)^+\). The trader exercises or sells the long far-dated call at its prevailing market price \(C_{T_1}(K, T_2)\). Because \(C_{T_1}(K, T_2) \ge (S_{T_1} - K)^+\) by intrinsic value dominance, the liquidation value of the long position is guaranteed to cover the short payoff: \(C_{T_1}(K, T_2) - (S_{T_1} - K)^+ \ge 0\). The initial cash profit is locked in with zero probability of negative future cash flows.

3. Inverted Volatility Term Structures vs Total Variance Monotonicity

A crucial conceptual distinction exists between annualized Black–Scholes implied volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(k, \tau)\) and total implied variance \(w(k, \tau)\). During market stress or earnings announcements, annualized implied volatility frequently displays an inverted term structure: short-term volatility spikes above long-term volatility (\(\sigma_1 > \sigma_2\)).

Theorem 3.2 (Validity of Inverted Implied Volatility). An inverted implied volatility term structure \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(k, \tau_1) > \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(k, \tau_2)\) for \(\tau_1 < \tau_2\) is completely free of calendar arbitrage if and only if:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau_2) \, \tau_2 &\ge \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau_1) \, \tau_1 \\ \iff \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(k, \tau_2)}{\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(k, \tau_1)} &\ge \sqrt{\frac{\tau_1}{\tau_2}} \end{aligned} \]

Proof. Calendar monotonicity requires \(w(k, \tau_2) \ge w(k, \tau_1)\). Substituting \(w(k, \tau) = \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau)\tau\) yields \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau_2)\tau_2 \ge \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau_1)\tau_1\). Dividing by \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau_1)\tau_2 > 0\) gives \(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau_2)}{\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}^2(k, \tau_1)} \ge \frac{\tau_1}{\tau_2}\). Taking square roots yields the condition. Thus, annualized volatility can decrease with maturity, provided it decays slower than \(\tau^{-1/2}\). ■

The increment of total variance between two tenors defines the forward implied total variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} w_{\mathrm{fwd}}(k; \tau_1, \tau_2) &= w(k, \tau_2) - w(k, \tau_1) \\ &= \sigma_{\mathrm{fwd}}^2(k; \tau_1, \tau_2)(\tau_2 - \tau_1) \end{aligned} \]

Absence of calendar arbitrage is mathematically equivalent to the requirement that forward implied variance is non-negative everywhere: \(w_{\mathrm{fwd}}(k; \tau_1, \tau_2) \ge 0\) for all \(k\).

Hypothesis: When two independent SVI slices cross in total variance (\(w(k, \tau_1) > w(k, \tau_2)\) at downside moneyness), a calendar spread trade captures immediate positive upfront cash with guaranteed non-negative terminal payoff.

"""Lab 1 · Calendar Arbitrage & Total Variance: Monotonicity Across Expirations.

Evaluates total implied variance w(k, tau) across multiple maturities tau_1 < tau_2,
tests the monotonicity condition dw / dtau >= 0, and demonstrates calendar spread
arbitrage when term structure slices cross.
All data is SIMULATED (seed 42).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def svi_val(k: float, a: float, b: float, rho: float, m: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    diff = k - m
    disc = math.sqrt(diff**2 + sigma**2)
    return a + b * (rho * diff + disc)


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def bs_call_price(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> float:
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return max(s - k, 0.0)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * math.sqrt(tau))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * math.sqrt(tau)
    return s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    rate = 0.0  # Forward measure / zero interest rate setting
    t1 = 0.25
    t2 = 0.75

    # Valid calibrated slices with strictly increasing total variance
    p1 = {"a": 0.015, "b": 0.08, "rho": -0.32, "m": 0.005, "sigma": 0.06}
    p2_valid = {"a": 0.040, "b": 0.12, "rho": -0.32, "m": 0.015, "sigma": 0.09}

    # Crossed slice with lower total variance at later maturity (calendar arbitrage)
    p2_crossed = {"a": 0.010, "b": 0.07, "rho": -0.32, "m": 0.015, "sigma": 0.07}

    k_grid = [-0.4, -0.2, 0.0, 0.2, 0.4]

    print("--- Monotonic Term Structure (No Calendar Arbitrage) ---")
    min_diff_valid = float("inf")
    for k in k_grid:
        w1 = svi_val(k, **p1)
        w2 = svi_val(k, **p2_valid)
        diff = w2 - w1
        if diff < min_diff_valid:
            min_diff_valid = diff
        print(f"k = {k:5.2f} | w(T1=0.25) = {w1:.5f} | w(T2=0.75) = {w2:.5f} | Spread (w2 - w1) = {diff:.5f}")

    print(f"Minimum variance spread on valid surface: {min_diff_valid:.5f}")
    print("Valid surface is calendar arbitrage-free:", min_diff_valid >= 0.0)

    print("\n--- Crossing Term Structure (Calendar Arbitrage Detected) ---")
    min_diff_cross = float("inf")
    k_worst = 0.0
    for k in k_grid:
        w1 = svi_val(k, **p1)
        w2_c = svi_val(k, **p2_crossed)
        diff = w2_c - w1
        if diff < min_diff_cross:
            min_diff_cross = diff
            k_worst = k
        print(f"k = {k:5.2f} | w(T1=0.25) = {w1:.5f} | w(T2=0.75) = {w2_c:.5f} | Spread (w2 - w1) = {diff:.5f}")

    print(f"Minimum variance spread on crossed surface: {min_diff_cross:.5f} at k = {k_worst:.2f}")
    print("Calendar Arbitrage Detected (w2 < w1):", min_diff_cross < 0.0)

    # Financial manifestation: Calendar spread trade (buy longer, sell shorter)
    k_target = k_worst
    strike = spot * math.exp(k_target)

    w1_val = svi_val(k_target, **p1)
    w2_val = svi_val(k_target, **p2_crossed)
    vol1 = math.sqrt(w1_val / t1)
    vol2 = math.sqrt(w2_val / t2)

    c1 = bs_call_price(spot, strike, rate, vol1, t1)
    c2 = bs_call_price(spot, strike, rate, vol2, t2)

    spread_cost = c2 - c1
    print(f"\nExecuting Calendar Spread (Buy T2={t2}, Sell T1={t1}) at Strike K = {strike:.2f}:")
    print(f"Near Call Price C(T1={t1}):  {c1:.4f} (Vol = {vol1:.4f})")
    print(f"Far Call Price C(T2={t2}):   {c2:.4f} (Vol = {vol2:.4f})")
    print(f"Net Calendar Spread Price:  {spread_cost:.4f}")
    print("Negative Cost for Longer Maturity Option (Arbitrage):", spread_cost < 0.0)


main()

Where the canon breaks

Multi-expiry volatility surface calibration breaks down when slices are parameterized in isolation:

  • Slice-by-Slice SVI Fitting: Calibrating raw SVI parameters independently for each tenor \(\tau_i\) guarantees that each slice is free of butterfly arbitrage if \(g_i(k) \ge 0\). However, independent optimizations virtually always cause total variance curves to cross at extreme wings where liquidity is sparse, introducing severe calendar arbitrage.
  • Surface SVI (SSVI) as the Remedy: To guarantee absence of calendar arbitrage by design, Gatheral & Jacquier (2013) developed Surface SVI, parameterizing total variance in terms of ATM variance \(\theta = w(0, \tau)\) and a scaling function \(\varphi(\theta)\). Calendar monotonicity is guaranteed if \(\theta \mapsto \theta \varphi(\theta)\) is non-decreasing.
  • Dividend-Paying Equities: For stocks with large discrete cash dividends, European call prices can legitimately decrease across calendar dates that span an ex-dividend date, since dividends are captured by the stock holder rather than the call holder.

Misconceptions

  • An inverted implied volatility term structure (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(\tau_1) > \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(\tau_2)\) for \(\tau_1 < \tau_2\)) indicates calendar arbitrage.

    Annualized implied volatility routinely inverts during market stress. Calendar arbitrage depends strictly on total implied variance \(w = \sigma^2 \tau\). As long as \(\sigma_2^2 \tau_2 \ge \sigma_1^2 \tau_1\), total variance increases with time and no arbitrage exists.

  • Calibrating each expiration slice with zero butterfly arbitrage ensures the entire 2D surface is arbitrage-free.

    Slices can be perfectly convex in strike yet cross each other across calendar time. Calendar arbitrage is an independent dimension of no-arbitrage that must be explicitly constrained across expiries.

  • Calendar spreads can be held to the far expiration \(T_2\) without managing risk at near expiration \(T_1\).

    At near expiration \(T_1\), the short option position expires or is assigned. To capture the arbitrage, the trader must liquidate or roll the far-dated long option at \(T_1\); holding past \(T_1\) exposes the trader to unhedged directional market risk.

Practice problems

An option trader observes 1-month (\(\tau_1 = 1/12\)) implied volatility of \(\sigma_1 = 0.30\) and 3-month (\(\tau_2 = 0.25\)) implied volatility of \(\sigma_2 = 0.20\) at the money. Compute total implied variance for both tenors and determine if calendar arbitrage is present.

Worked Solution

Compute total variance for tenor 1: \(w_1 = \sigma_1^2 \tau_1 = 0.30^2 \times (1/12) = 0.09 \times 0.083333 = 0.00750\). Compute total variance for tenor 2: \(w_2 = \sigma_2^2 \tau_2 = 0.20^2 \times 0.25 = 0.04 \times 0.25 = 0.01000\). Comparing: \(w_2 = 0.01000 > w_1 = 0.00750\). Because total variance increases with maturity (\(w_2 > w_1\)), there is no calendar arbitrage despite the inverted annualized volatility.

At a wing strike \(K = 70\) with \(F = 100\), a calibrated surface yields total variances \(w(k, 0.25) = 0.065\) and \(w(k, 0.50) = 0.055\). Calculate the forward implied total variance \(w_{\mathrm{fwd}}\) between these two tenors.

Worked Solution

The forward implied total variance is defined as \(w_{\mathrm{fwd}} = w(k, \tau_2) - w(k, \tau_1) = 0.055 - 0.065 = -0.010\). Because forward variance is negative (\(-0.010 < 0\)), calendar arbitrage exists: the far-dated option has less total variance than the near-dated option at this strike.

A 1-month call option trades at \$4.50 and a 2-month call option on the same non-dividend stock at the same strike trades at \$4.10. Interest rates are zero (\(r = 0\)). Detail the exact trades executed at time 0 and the net cash flow.

Worked Solution

At time 0, sell 1 share of the 1-month call at \$4.50 and buy 1 share of the 2-month call at \$4.10. The initial net cash flow is \(\text{Cash}_0 = +4.50 - 4.10 = +\$0.40\). This \$0.40 is retained as riskless upfront profit. At month 1, the short call requires payoff \(-(S_{T_1} - K)^+\), which is covered by selling the 2-month call for its intrinsic value \(C_{T_1} \ge (S_{T_1} - K)^+\).

Prove that if total variance \(w(k, \tau)\) is strictly decreasing in \(\tau\) over an interval \((\tau_1, \tau_2)\), the Dupire local variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(k, \tau)\) must be strictly negative on that interval (assuming no butterfly arbitrage).

Worked Solution

Recall Dupire's local variance formula in total variance coordinates: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(k, \tau) = \frac{\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau}(k, \tau)}{g(k, \tau)}\). In the absence of butterfly arbitrage, the Gatheral–Jacquier factor satisfies \(g(k, \tau) > 0\). If total variance is strictly decreasing in \(\tau\), then \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau}(k, \tau) < 0\). Consequently, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2 = \frac{\text{negative}}{\text{positive}} < 0\). Local variance is negative, rendering instantaneous local volatility imaginary.

Prove that for an asset paying a continuous dividend yield \(q > 0\), European call prices are not guaranteed to be monotonic in calendar maturity if \(q\) is sufficiently large.

Worked Solution

Under continuous dividends, the risk-neutral forward price is \(F_\tau = S_0 e^{(r - q)\tau}\). If \(q > r\), the forward price decreases exponentially with maturity: \(\lim_{\tau \to \infty} F_\tau = 0\). For a deep in-the-money call with strike \(K > 0\), as \(\tau \to \infty\), the discounted expected payoff converges to zero: \(\lim_{\tau \to \infty} C(K, \tau) \le \lim_{\tau \to \infty} e^{-r\tau} F_\tau = 0\). Meanwhile, for short tenors \(\tau_1 > 0\), \(C(K, \tau_1) \approx S_0 - K > 0\). Therefore, \(C(K, \tau_2) < C(K, \tau_1)\) for large \(\tau_2\), demonstrating non-monotonicity driven by dividend cash outflows.

Let \(k = \ln(K/F_\tau)\) be forward log-moneyness. Prove that if the forward curve is upward sloping (\(r > q\)), a fixed strike \(K\) corresponds to decreasing forward log-moneyness as maturity increases.

Worked Solution

The forward price is \(F_\tau = S_0 e^{(r-q)\tau}\). Taking the logarithm of \(K / F_\tau\): \(k(\tau) = \ln(K / S_0) - (r - q)\tau\). Differentiating with respect to \(\tau\): \(\frac{\partial k}{\partial \tau} = -(r - q)\). When \(r > q\), \(\frac{\partial k}{\partial \tau} < 0\). Thus, for a fixed nominal strike \(K\), forward log-moneyness shifts steadily to the left (more negative) as maturity increases.

During an earnings announcement for an equity stock scheduled in 3 days, 1-week implied volatility is 85% (\(\tau_1 = 7/365\)), while 1-month implied volatility is 35% (\(\tau_2 = 30/365\)). Calculate both total variances. Does an annualized volatility collapse of 50 points constitute calendar arbitrage?

Worked Solution

Compute 1-week total variance: \(w_1 = 0.85^2 \times (7 / 365) = 0.7225 \times 0.019178 = 0.013856\). Compute 1-month total variance: \(w_2 = 0.35^2 \times (30 / 365) = 0.1225 \times 0.082192 = 0.010068\). Comparing: \(w_1 = 0.013856 > w_2 = 0.010068\). Total variance actually declines over time (\(w_1 > w_2\)). In this case, the volatility collapse is so steep that it does violate calendar monotonicity in forward total variance, creating calendar arbitrage unless stock borrowing constraints prevent physical shorting.

In an S&P 500 options surface, SVI parameters for \(\tau_1 = 0.1\) are \((a_1, b_1, \rho_1, m_1, \sigma_1) = (0.01, 0.10, -0.6, 0.0, 0.1)\) and for \(\tau_2 = 0.5\) are \((a_2, b_2, \rho_2, m_2, \sigma_2) = (0.04, 0.08, -0.6, 0.0, 0.1)\). Find the asymptotic left wing slopes \(\lim_{k \to -\infty} \frac{\partial w}{\partial k}\) for both slices and verify whether the slices will cross at large negative \(k\).

Worked Solution

The asymptotic left wing slope of raw SVI is \(\beta_L = b(1 - \rho)\). For slice 1: \(\beta_{L1} = 0.10(1 - (-0.6)) = 0.10(1.6) = 0.160\). For slice 2: \(\beta_{L2} = 0.08(1 - (-0.6)) = 0.08(1.6) = 0.128\). Because \(\beta_{L1} = 0.160 > \beta_{L2} = 0.128\), slice 1 grows faster than slice 2 as \(k \to -\infty\). Even though slice 2 starts higher at ATM (\(w_2(0) > w_1(0)\)), the steeper slope of slice 1 guarantees that slice 1 will cross above slice 2 at sufficiently negative \(k\), creating calendar arbitrage in the deep downside wing.

How do derivatives desks resolve calendar arbitrage crossing between liquid front-month contracts and illiquid back-month contracts?

Worked Solution

Desks typically anchor calibration to the most liquid expiries and fit the back months using penalized spline smoothing or constrained parametric models such as Surface SVI (SSVI). If crossing occurs, the optimizer enforces linear inequality constraints \(w(k_j, \tau_{i+1}) \ge w(k_j, \tau_i) + \epsilon\) across a dense grid of strikes, shifting the illiquid back-month slice upward to eliminate arbitrage.

In the Surface SVI (SSVI) formulation of Gatheral & Jacquier (2013), total variance is parameterized via ATM variance \(\theta = w(0, \tau)\) and skew function \(\varphi(\theta)\). State the necessary and sufficient condition on the function \(\varphi(\theta)\) to guarantee absence of calendar arbitrage for all \(\theta > 0\).

Worked Solution

As proven in Gatheral & Jacquier (2013, Theorem 4.1), absence of calendar arbitrage is guaranteed if and only if \(\partial_\theta w(k, \theta) \ge 0\) for all \(k\). This holds if and only if \(\theta \mapsto \theta \varphi(\theta)\) is a non-decreasing function and \(\theta \mapsto \varphi(\theta)\) is non-increasing. A standard choice meeting this condition is the power-law parameterization \(\varphi(\theta) = \frac{\eta}{\theta^\gamma(1 + \theta)^{1-\gamma}}\) with \(\gamma \in (0, 0.5]\).

Under a multi-factor Heston stochastic volatility model, can forward instantaneous variance \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[v_t \mid \mathcal{F}_0]\) ever become negative? What ensures calendar monotonicity in standard affine jump diffusions?

Worked Solution

In the Heston model, the instantaneous variance process \(v_t\) follows a CIR diffusion \(\mathrm{d}v_t = \kappa(\bar{v} - v_t)\mathrm{d}t + \xi \sqrt{v_t}\mathrm{d}W_t\). Under Feller's condition \(2\kappa\bar{v} > \xi^2\), the boundary at zero is inaccessible and \(v_t > 0\) almost surely. The expectation \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[v_t] = \bar{v} + (v_0 - \bar{v})e^{-\kappa t} > 0\) is strictly positive. Because total variance is an integrated expectation of non-negative variance, \(w(T) = \int_0^T \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[v_t]\mathrm{d}t\), its derivative \(\partial w / \partial T = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[v_T] > 0\) is strictly positive, ensuring calendar monotonicity by construction.

Why does calibrating local volatility to a surface with discrete calendar arbitrage cause the finite-difference solver for pricing exotics to produce negative option prices?

Worked Solution

When calendar arbitrage is present, \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau} < 0\), which makes Dupire local variance \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2 < 0\). In a finite-difference grid, the diffusion coefficient matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) loses its M-matrix property (off-diagonal elements change sign). This violates the maximum principle of parabolic PDEs, allowing numerical errors to generate spurious oscillations that produce negative option values and probability mass exceeding 1.

    Options

    Calendar monotonicity requires that total cumulative variance \(w = \sigma^2 \tau\) never decreases with time.

  2. If total variance for a 6-month option is lower than for a 3-month option at the same forward log-moneyness, what trade generates riskless profit?

    Options

    The near option is overpriced relative to the far option; selling near and buying far locks in net cash upfront with zero chance of negative net payoff.

  3. An option with \(\tau = 0.083333\) (1 month) has implied volatility \(\sigma = 0.30\). What is its total implied variance \(w\)?

    \(w = 0.30^2 \times 0.083333 = 0.09 \times 0.083333 = 0.00750\).

  4. Can annualized implied volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}\) legitimately decrease across maturities without creating calendar arbitrage?

    Options

    Annualized volatility can decline as long as it decays slower than \(\tau^{-1/2}\), preserving non-negative forward total variance.

  5. If \(w(k, 0.25) = 0.0100\) and \(w(k, 0.083333) = 0.0075\), compute the forward implied total variance \(w_{\mathrm{fwd}} = w(k, 0.25) - w(k, 0.083333)\).

    \(w_{\mathrm{fwd}} = 0.0100 - 0.0075 = 0.0025\).

  6. Why does independent slice-by-slice SVI calibration frequently introduce calendar arbitrage?

    Options

    Without cross-tenor regularization, near-dated wings can easily have steeper asymptotic slopes than far-dated wings, crossing at extreme strikes.

  7. Under what circumstance can a European call option price legally decrease with expiration on a dividend-paying asset?

    Options

    A dividend payment detaches from the stock price, lowering forward value and potentially offsetting the time value of additional optionality.

  8. What is the minimum permissible value of forward implied total variance \(w_{\mathrm{fwd}} = w(k, \tau_2) - w(k, \tau_1)\) for \(\tau_2 > \tau_1\) under no-arbitrage?

    Forward total variance must be non-negative: \(w_{\mathrm{fwd}} \ge 0\).

  9. How does Surface SVI (SSVI) eliminate calendar arbitrage across maturities?

    Options

    SSVI formulates total variance such that \(\partial_\theta w(k, \theta) \ge 0\) is satisfied analytically for all strikes and tenors.
