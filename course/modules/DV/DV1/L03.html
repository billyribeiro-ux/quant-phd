Analytical Solutions & Measure Transformation

The Black–Scholes formula is often presented as a mysterious formula of normal distributions. In truth, it reflects two deep mathematical dualities: the solution of a parabolic heat equation via Gaussian convolution, and the Feynman–Kac conditional expectation under equivalent martingale measures. Deriving the formula exposes how discounting transforms under change of numeraire: the term \(\Phi(d_2)\) represents the risk-neutral probability of expiring in-the-money under the money-market measure, while \(\Phi(d_1)\) represents the probability under the share measure where the stock itself serves as numeraire.

  • Uses: dynamic replication, Black–Scholes–Merton PDE
  • Prerequisites: Black–Scholes formula, put–call parity, Girsanov's theorem, change of numeraire, risk-neutral probability

1. Transforming the BSM PDE to the Heat Equation

In Lesson 2, the no-arbitrage price \(V(t, S)\) was shown to satisfy the Black–Scholes–Merton PDE:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - r V = 0, \quad (t, S) \in [0, T) \times (0, \infty) \]

with terminal boundary condition \(V(T, S) = \max(S - K, 0)\). To solve this variable-coefficient PDE analytically, introduce logarithmic spatial coordinates, reverse time to forward time to maturity \(\tau = T - t\), and factor out discounting:

\[ x = \ln\left(\frac{S}{K}\right), \quad \tau = T - t, \quad u(\tau, x) = \frac{1}{K} e^{r\tau} V(T - \tau, K e^x) \]

As proved in Problem P2 of Lesson 2, the function \(u(\tau, x)\) satisfies the constant-coefficient convection-diffusion equation:

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \left(r - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2\right) \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \]

To remove the convective first-derivative drift term, make the moving frame transformation:

\[ \xi = x + \left(r - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2\right) \tau, \quad w(\tau, \xi) = u\left(\tau, \xi - \left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\tau\right) \]

A direct chain rule calculation establishes that \(w(\tau, \xi)\) satisfies the canonical one-dimensional heat equation:

\[ \frac{\partial w}{\partial \tau} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 w}{\partial \xi^2}, \quad (\tau, \xi) \in (0, T] \times \mathbb{R} \]

with initial condition at \(\tau = 0\) given by the normalized call payoff: \(w(0, \xi) = \max(e^\xi - 1, 0)\).

2. The Feynman–Kac Representation & Risk-Neutral Expectation

By the classical Green's function for the heat equation, the solution \(w(\tau, \xi)\) is the spatial convolution of the initial condition with the Gaussian heat kernel \(\Gamma(\tau, \xi) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2 \tau}} \exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{2\sigma^2 \tau}\right)\). In the probabilistic framework of SC3, this convolution is identical to the Feynman–Kac expectation under the equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Theorem 3.1 · Risk-Neutral Pricing Representation

Let \(\mathbb{Q}\) denote the equivalent martingale measure (risk-neutral measure) associated with the money-market numeraire \(B_t = e^{rt}\). The no-arbitrage price of any European contingent claim \(h(S_T)\) is the discounted conditional expectation:

\[ V(t, S) = e^{-r(T-t)} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[h(S_T) \mid S_t = S] \]

where under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the underlying stock price follows the geometric Brownian motion:

\[ S_T = S_t \exp\left(\left(r - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2\right)(T-t) + \sigma \sqrt{T-t} \, Z\right), \quad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \]

Proof

By Girsanov's theorem (SC3), under the Radon–Nikodym density process with market price of risk \(\lambda = (\mu - r)/\sigma\), the process \(W_t^\mathbb{Q} = W_t + \int_0^t \lambda \mathrm{d}u\) is a standard Brownian motion under \(\mathbb{Q}\). The stock price SDE under \(\mathbb{P}\), \(\mathrm{d}S_t = \mu S_t \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t \mathrm{d}W_t\), transforms under \(\mathbb{Q}\) into:

\[ \mathrm{d}S_t = r S_t \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t \mathrm{d}W_t^\mathbb{Q} \]

Applying Itô's formula to \(\tilde{S}_t = e^{-rt} S_t\) confirms \(\mathrm{d}\tilde{S}_t = \sigma \tilde{S}_t \mathrm{d}W_t^\mathbb{Q}\), so \(\tilde{S}_t\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale. As proved in Lesson 1, the discounted replicating portfolio \(\tilde{V}_t\) is also a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale with terminal condition \(\tilde{V}_T = e^{-rT} h(S_T)\). By the martingale property:

\[ \tilde{V}_t = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\tilde{V}_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \implies V(t, S_t) = e^{-r(T-t)} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[h(S_T) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \]

Evaluating the lognormal distribution of \(S_T \mid S_t\) completes the proof.

3. The Black–Scholes Analytical Formula

Evaluating the conditional expectation for the European call payoff \(h(S_T) = \max(S_T - K, 0)\) yields the celebrated Black–Scholes formula:

\[ C(S, K, r, \sigma, \tau) = e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[(S_T - K) \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}}] = e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[S_T \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}}] - K e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}}] \]

The second expectation is the risk-neutral probability of expiring in-the-money:

\[ \mathbb{Q}(S_T > K) = \mathbb{Q}\left(\ln\left(\frac{S_t}{K}\right) + \left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\tau + \sigma\sqrt{\tau} Z > 0\right) = \Phi(d_2) \]

where \(\Phi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and:

\[ d_2 = \frac{\ln(S / K) + (r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma \sqrt{\tau}} \]

The first expectation involves the asset-weighted payoff \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[S_T \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}}]\). By completing the square in the Gaussian integral:

\[ e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[S_T \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}}] = S \, \Phi(d_1), \quad d_1 = d_2 + \sigma \sqrt{\tau} = \frac{\ln(S / K) + (r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma \sqrt{\tau}} \]

Definition 3.1 · Black–Scholes Call and Put Formulas

For spot price \(S\), strike \(K\), risk-free rate \(r\), volatility \(\sigma\), and time to expiration \(\tau = T - t\), the European call price \(C\) and put price \(P\) are given analytically by:

\[ \begin{aligned} C(S, K, r, \sigma, \tau) &= S \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(d_2) \\ P(S, K, r, \sigma, \tau) &= K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(-d_2) - S \Phi(-d_1) \end{aligned} \]

where:

\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(S / K) + (r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma \sqrt{\tau}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{\tau} \]
Theorem 3.2 · Dual-Measure Numeraire Decomposition

By the change of numeraire theorem (SC3; Geman, El Karoui & Rochet 1995), the Black–Scholes formula decomposes into probabilities under two distinct equivalent martingale measures:

\[ C(S, K, r, \sigma, \tau) = S \cdot \mathbb{Q}^S(S_T > K) - K e^{-r\tau} \cdot \mathbb{Q}^B(S_T > K) \]

where \(\mathbb{Q}^B \equiv \mathbb{Q}\) is the risk-neutral measure with money-market numeraire \(B_t\), and \(\mathbb{Q}^S\) is the share measure with stock numeraire \(S_t\). Specifically, \(\Phi(d_1) = \mathbb{Q}^S(S_T > K)\) and \(\Phi(d_2) = \mathbb{Q}^B(S_T > K)\).

This dual-measure perspective illuminates the economic structure: \(\Phi(d_2)\) is the probability that the option will be exercised in the risk-neutral world, while \(\Phi(d_1)\) is the delta \(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S}\), which represents the expected stock value received upon exercise normalized by the current stock price under the share numeraire.

Lab 1 · Analytical Black–Scholes formula vs risk-neutral Monte Carlo expectation

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the discounted expectation of the European call payoff \(\exp(-rT)\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\max(S_T - K, 0)]\) matches the closed-form Black–Scholes price \(6.387430\) within two standard errors of a 200,000-path antithetic Monte Carlo simulation. Put–call parity \(C - P = S - K \exp(-rT)\) holds to machine precision.

"""Lab 1 · Analytical Black-Scholes formula vs risk-neutral Monte Carlo expectation.

Verifies the risk-neutral expectation pricing representation:
    C(S, K, r, sigma, T) = exp(-r T) E_Q[(S_T - K)^+]
    P(S, K, r, sigma, T) = exp(-r T) E_Q[(K - S_T)^+]
and exact put-call parity C - P = S - K exp(-r T).
Compares the closed-form Black-Scholes price against a 200,000-path Monte Carlo simulation
under Q with antithetic variates, verifying agreement within 2 standard errors.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def bs_prices(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, t: float) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Analytical European call and put prices."""
    sq_t = math.sqrt(t)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * t) / (sigma * sq_t)
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sq_t
    df = math.exp(-r * t)

    call = s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * df * norm_cdf(d2)
    put = k * df * norm_cdf(-d2) - s * norm_cdf(-d1)
    return call, put


def mc_risk_neutral_prices(
    s: float,
    k: float,
    r: float,
    sigma: float,
    t: float,
    n_pairs: int,
    seed: int,
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Monte Carlo pricing under Q using antithetic variates.

    Returns:
        (call_mc, call_se, put_mc, put_se)
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    df = math.exp(-r * t)
    drift = (r - 0.5 * sigma**2) * t
    vol = sigma * math.sqrt(t)

    call_sum = 0.0
    call_sq = 0.0
    put_sum = 0.0
    put_sq = 0.0

    for _ in range(n_pairs):
        z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        s1 = s * math.exp(drift + vol * z)
        s2 = s * math.exp(drift - vol * z)

        c1 = max(s1 - k, 0.0)
        c2 = max(s2 - k, 0.0)
        c_pair = 0.5 * df * (c1 + c2)
        call_sum += c_pair
        call_sq += c_pair * c_pair

        p1 = max(k - s1, 0.0)
        p2 = max(k - s2, 0.0)
        p_pair = 0.5 * df * (p1 + p2)
        put_sum += p_pair
        put_sq += p_pair * p_pair

    call_mc = call_sum / n_pairs
    call_var = (call_sq - n_pairs * call_mc**2) / (n_pairs - 1)
    call_se = math.sqrt(max(call_var, 0.0) / n_pairs)

    put_mc = put_sum / n_pairs
    put_var = (put_sq - n_pairs * put_mc**2) / (n_pairs - 1)
    put_se = math.sqrt(max(put_var, 0.0) / n_pairs)

    return call_mc, call_se, put_mc, put_se


def main() -> None:
    # Contract and market parameters (SIMULATED)
    s = 100.0
    k = 105.0
    r = 0.05
    sigma = 0.20
    t = 0.75
    n_pairs = 100000
    seed = 12345

    call_exact, put_exact = bs_prices(s, k, r, sigma, t)
    c_mc, c_se, p_mc, p_se = mc_risk_neutral_prices(s, k, r, sigma, t, n_pairs, seed)

    # Put-call parity checks
    df = math.exp(-r * t)
    parity_lhs = call_exact - put_exact
    parity_rhs = s - k * df
    parity_diff = abs(parity_lhs - parity_rhs)

    c_diff = abs(c_mc - call_exact)
    p_diff = abs(p_mc - put_exact)

    print("Risk-neutral expectation pricing under Q (SIMULATED):")
    print(f"Call: Exact = {call_exact:.6f} | MC = {c_mc:.6f} (SE = {c_se:.6f}) | Diff = {c_diff:.6f}")
    print(f"Put:  Exact = {put_exact:.6f} | MC = {p_mc:.6f} (SE = {p_se:.6f}) | Diff = {p_diff:.6f}")
    print(f"Put-call parity check: LHS={parity_lhs:.6f}, RHS={parity_rhs:.6f}, Diff={parity_diff:.2e}")
    print("Call within 2*SE:", c_diff <= 2.0 * c_se)
    print("Put within 2*SE:", p_diff <= 2.0 * p_se)
    print("Put-call parity holds to machine precision:", parity_diff < 1e-12)

Where the canon breaks

The analytical Black–Scholes formula is the most widely quoted equation in financial history, but its underlying lognormal distribution fails to capture crucial empirical realities:

  • Fat tails and crash risk: Real asset return distributions exhibit excess kurtosis and negative skewness (Lesson 4, M4). Log-returns have heavier tails than the normal distribution, causing the Black–Scholes formula to severely underprice deep out-of-the-money puts (disaster insurance).
  • The post-1987 volatility smile: Prior to the October 1987 crash, implied volatilities across strikes were relatively flat. Following the crash, option markets permanently instituted a steep volatility skew and smile (Hull 2021), proving that no single volatility parameter \(\sigma\) can price all strikes simultaneously.
  • Stochastic interest rates: For long-dated options (such as LEAPS or swaptions), assuming a constant interest rate \(r\) produces significant pricing bias, requiring forward measures and term-structure models (SC3, FI4).

Misconceptions

  1. \(\Phi(d_1)\) is the probability that the call option expires in-the-money.

    Reality: The probability of the option expiring in-the-money under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is \(\Phi(d_2)\), not \(\Phi(d_1)\). The quantity \(\Phi(d_1)\) is the option's delta \(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S}\), which equals the in-the-money probability under the share measure \(\mathbb{Q}^S\), where the stock price itself is the numeraire.

  2. Put–call parity relies on the assumption of geometric Brownian motion and constant volatility.

    Reality: Put–call parity \(C - P = S - K e^{-rT}\) is a model-free static arbitrage relation established by Stoll (1969). It holds for any European options regardless of the underlying stochastic process, whether volatility is constant, local, or stochastic, and whether returns contain jumps.

  3. A higher expected stock return \(\mu\) increases the call option price.

    Reality: The physical drift \(\mu\) does not enter the Black–Scholes formula. If an analyst believes a stock will appreciate by 50% per year, the no-arbitrage call option price remains completely unchanged. Any attempt to price options using \(\mu\) creates immediate static and dynamic arbitrage opportunities.

Problem set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Calculate the Black–Scholes European call price for a stock with current price \(S = \$100\), strike \(K = \$100\), risk-free rate \(r = 0.05\), volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), and maturity \(\tau = 1.0\) year. Given \(\Phi(0.35) = 0.63683\) and \(\Phi(0.15) = 0.55962\), show each intermediate calculation step.

Solution

Compute \(d_1\) and \(d_2\): \[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(100/100) + (0.05 + 0.5 \times 0.04) \times 1.0}{0.20 \times 1.0} = \frac{0 + 0.07}{0.20} = 0.35 \] \[ d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{\tau} = 0.35 - 0.20 = 0.15 \] Discount factor: \(e^{-r\tau} = e^{-0.05} \approx 0.951229\). Substitute into the call formula: \[ C = 100 \times \Phi(0.35) - 100 \times 0.951229 \times \Phi(0.15) = 100 \times 0.63683 - 95.1229 \times 0.55962 = 63.683 - 53.233 = \$10.45 \] The call price is \(\$10.45\).

Using put–call parity and the call price from Problem M1 (\(C = \$10.45\)), calculate the European put option price with identical strike \(K = \$100\) and maturity \(\tau = 1.0\).

Solution

By put–call parity: \(C - P = S - K e^{-r\tau}\). Rearranging for the put price: \[ P = C - S + K e^{-r\tau} = 10.45 - 100 + 100 \times e^{-0.05} = 10.45 - 100 + 95.1229 = \$5.5729 \approx \$5.57 \] The corresponding put option price is \(\$5.57\).

For an at-the-money forward option where \(S = K e^{-r\tau}\), show that \(d_1 = \frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\) and \(d_2 = -\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\), and write the resulting simplified call option price.

Solution

If \(S = K e^{-r\tau}\), then \(\ln(S/K) = -r\tau\). Substituting into \(d_1\): \[ d_1 = \frac{-r\tau + (r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau} \] Then \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau} = -\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\). Because \(\Phi(-d_2) = \Phi(d_1)\) by symmetry of standard normal distribution, the call price simplifies to: \[ C = S \Phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\right) - S \Phi\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\right) = S \left[2 \Phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\right) - 1\right] \] This expresses the at-the-money forward option purely in terms of spot and normalized volatility.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove analytically that the Black–Scholes call and put formulas identically satisfy put–call parity \(C - P = S - K e^{-r\tau}\) for all parameter combinations.

Solution

Subtracting the Black–Scholes put formula from the call formula: \[ C - P = [S \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(d_2)] - [K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(-d_2) - S \Phi(-d_1)] \] Regroup terms multiplying \(S\) and \(K e^{-r\tau}\): \[ C - P = S [\Phi(d_1) + \Phi(-d_1)] - K e^{-r\tau} [\Phi(d_2) + \Phi(-d_2)] \] By symmetry of the standard normal distribution, \(\Phi(x) + \Phi(-x) = 1\) for all real \(x\). Therefore: \[ \Phi(d_1) + \Phi(-d_1) = 1, \quad \text{and} \quad \Phi(d_2) + \Phi(-d_2) = 1 \] Substituting these into the expression yields: \[ C - P = S (1) - K e^{-r\tau} (1) = S - K e^{-r\tau} \] This holds identically without approximation.

Prove that \(\lim_{\sigma \to 0} C(S, K, r, \sigma, \tau) = \max(S - K e^{-r\tau}, 0)\), and interpret this result economically.

Solution

Consider the limits of \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) as \(\sigma \to 0^+\): \[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(S / (K e^{-r\tau}))}{\sigma \sqrt{\tau}} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau} \] Case 1: \(S > K e^{-r\tau}\). Then \(\ln(S / (K e^{-r\tau})) > 0\). As \(\sigma \to 0^+\), the numerator is strictly positive, so \(d_1 \to +\infty\) and \(d_2 \to +\infty\). Thus \(\Phi(d_1) \to 1\) and \(\Phi(d_2) \to 1\). The call price becomes \(C \to S (1) - K e^{-r\tau} (1) = S - K e^{-r\tau}\). Case 2: \(S < K e^{-r\tau}\). Then \(\ln(S / (K e^{-r\tau})) < 0\), so \(d_1 \to -\infty\) and \(d_2 \to -\infty\). Thus \(\Phi(d_1) \to 0\) and \(\Phi(d_2) \to 0\), so \(C \to 0\). Combining both cases: \(\lim_{\sigma \to 0} C = \max(S - K e^{-r\tau}, 0)\). Economically, when volatility collapses to zero, the stock grows deterministically at rate \(r\) to \(S_T = S e^{r\tau}\). The discounted payoff \(\max(S e^{r\tau} - K, 0) e^{-r\tau} = \max(S - K e^{-r\tau}, 0)\) has no uncertainty, so the option price equals its intrinsic discounted value.

Prove the fundamental density identity: \(S \phi(d_1) = K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2)\), where \(\phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}\).

Solution

Compute the ratio \(\frac{\phi(d_1)}{\phi(d_2)}\): \[ \frac{\phi(d_1)}{\phi(d_2)} = \frac{e^{-d_1^2 / 2}}{e^{-d_2^2 / 2}} = \exp\left(-\frac{d_1^2 - d_2^2}{2}\right) \] Using the identity \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\), we factor \(d_1^2 - d_2^2 = (d_1 - d_2)(d_1 + d_2) = \sigma\sqrt{\tau}(2d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau})\). Substituting the formula for \(d_1\): \[ 2d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau} = \frac{2\ln(S/K) + 2(r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau - \sigma^2\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} = \frac{2\ln(S/K) + 2r\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} \] Therefore: \[ \frac{d_1^2 - d_2^2}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma\sqrt{\tau} \left(\frac{2\ln(S/K) + 2r\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{S}{K}\right) + r\tau \] Taking the exponential: \[ \frac{\phi(d_1)}{\phi(d_2)} = \exp\left(-\ln\left(\frac{S}{K}\right) - r\tau\right) = \frac{K}{S} e^{-r\tau} \] Multiplying both sides by \(S \phi(d_2)\) yields \(S \phi(d_1) = K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2)\), completing the proof.

Tier 3 · Empirical

A trader in the FX options market observes EUR/USD trading at \(S = 1.1000\) with domestic USD interest rate \(r_d = 0.04\) and foreign EUR interest rate \(r_f = 0.03\). How does the Black–Scholes formula adapt to foreign exchange (Garman–Kohlhagen 1983), and what is the effective forward exchange rate \(F\) for \(\tau = 0.5\)?

Solution

In foreign currency options (Garman & Kohlhagen 1983), holding foreign currency earns continuous foreign interest \(r_f\), acting as a dividend yield \(q = r_f\). The spot term in the call formula is discounted by \(e^{-r_f \tau}\): \(C = S e^{-r_f \tau} \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r_d \tau} \Phi(d_2)\). The forward exchange rate is \(F = S \exp((r_d - r_f)\tau) = 1.1000 \times \exp((0.04 - 0.03) \times 0.5) = 1.1000 \times \exp(0.005) \approx 1.1000 \times 1.0050125 = 1.10551\). The formula can be written as \(C = e^{-r_d \tau} [F \Phi(d_1) - K \Phi(d_2)]\) with \(d_1 = \frac{\ln(F/K) + 0.5\sigma^2\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}\).

In an algorithmic market-making system, why is the numerical evaluation of \(\Phi(x)\) and \(\Phi^{-1}(p)\) a critical performance bottleneck, and how do rational approximations (such as Hart's or Abramowitz & Stegun's algorithm) achieve sub-microsecond latency?

Solution

Option pricing and Greek risk calculations in high-frequency market-making involve millions of evaluations of \(\Phi(x)\) per second. Direct numerical integration of the Gaussian error function \(\operatorname{erf}(x)\) is computationally prohibitive. Production systems employ rational Chebyshev polynomial approximations (such as Abramowitz & Stegun 7.1.26 or Hart 6825), which compute \(\Phi(x)\) as a ratio of high-order polynomials \(P(x)/Q(x)\). These rational functions map directly onto SIMD vector floating-point registers (AVX-512), evaluating in fewer than 10 CPU cycles with absolute precision better than \(1.5 \times 10^{-7}\).

An empirical options trader notices that near-the-money 1-month European options on a large-cap equity have implied volatility \(\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}} = 0.22\), while historical 30-day realized volatility was \(0.17\). Provide an economic explanation for this positive variance risk premium.

Solution

The gap between implied and realized volatility reflects the variance risk premium (Carr & Wu 2008). Institutional investors structurally demand out-of-the-money put options to hedge systemic market drawdown risk. Because market makers who sell these options bear non-diversifiable downside jump risk and inventory risk, they demand a premium over objective expected volatility. Consequently, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}} > \sigma_{\mathrm{realized}}\) serves as an insurance premium paid by option buyers to liquidity providers.

Tier 4 · Research

Contrast the Black–Scholes formula derivation by transformation to the heat equation with the Fourier transform approach of Carr & Madan (1999). What computational advantages does the Fourier approach offer for non-Gaussian asset returns?

Solution

The Black–Scholes PDE heat-equation approach requires an analytical Gaussian density, which is unavailable for jump-diffusions (Merton 1976) or affine stochastic volatility models (Heston 1993). Carr & Madan (1999, Journal of Computational Finance) developed a Fourier inversion framework that evaluates option prices directly from the characteristic function \(\psi(u) = \mathbb{E}[e^{i u \ln S_T}]\). By damping the call payoff with \(e^{-\alpha k}\) to ensure \(L^1\) square-integrability, the Fourier transform of the option price is expressed in closed form in terms of \(\psi\). The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) then computes option prices across an entire grid of thousands of strikes simultaneously in \(O(N \log N)\) operations, revolutionizing surface calibration for Lévy and jump models.

Explain the Breeden–Litzenberger (1978) theorem, which relates the second derivative of the Black–Scholes call price with respect to strike \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\) to the risk-neutral density \(q(S_T)\).

Solution

Breeden & Litzenberger (1978, Journal of Business) proved that European call prices reveal the complete risk-neutral probability density of the underlying asset without imposing parametric diffusion assumptions. Differentiating the pricing integral \(C(K) = e^{-rT} \int_K^\infty (S_T - K) q(S_T) \mathrm{d}S_T\) with respect to strike \(K\): \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = -e^{-rT} \int_K^\infty q(S_T) \mathrm{d}S_T = -e^{-rT} \mathbb{Q}(S_T > K) \] Differentiating a second time by Leibniz's rule yields: \[ \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = e^{-rT} q(K) \implies q(K) = e^{rT} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} \] The risk-neutral density at strike \(K\) is proportional to the curvature of the call pricing function with respect to strike, which can be synthesized by a butterfly spread.

How does Merton's (1973) continuous-dividend formula reconcile with Margrabe's (1978) exchange option formula? Prove that Black–Scholes is a special case of Margrabe's formula.

Solution

Margrabe (1978) priced the option to exchange asset 2 for asset 1, with payoff \(\max(S_1(T) - S_2(T), 0)\). The closed form is \(V_0 = S_1 e^{-q_1 T} \Phi(d_1) - S_2 e^{-q_2 T} \Phi(d_2)\), with relative volatility \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2}\). If asset 1 is a stock with dividend yield \(q_1 = q\) and volatility \(\sigma_1 = \sigma\), and asset 2 is a cash bond paying \(K\) at \(T\) so \(S_2(t) = K e^{-r(T-t)}\) with volatility \(\sigma_2 = 0\) and yield \(q_2 = r\), then \(\sigma_M = \sigma\), \(S_1(0) = S\), and \(S_2(0) = K e^{-rT}\). Substituting these gives \(V_0 = S e^{-qT} \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-rT} \Phi(d_2)\), which is identical to the Black–Scholes–Merton formula with dividends.

Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Black–Scholes call option formula \(C = S \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(d_2)\), what is the probabilistic interpretation of \(\Phi(d_2)\)?

    Options

    \(\Phi(d_2)\) is the expectation of the indicator \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T > K\}}]\), which represents the risk-neutral probability of finishing in-the-money.

  2. What does \(\Phi(d_1)\) represent in the dual-measure numeraire framework of Geman, El Karoui & Rochet (1995)?

    Options

    Using the underlying stock as numeraire, Geman et al. (1995) proved that \(\Phi(d_1) = \mathbb{Q}^S(S_T > K)\).

  3. What is the sum of \(\Phi(d_1) + \Phi(-d_1)\) for any real value of \(d_1\)?

    By symmetry of the standard normal distribution around zero, \(\Phi(x) + \Phi(-x) = 1\) for all real \(x\).

  4. Why does put–call parity hold independently of the Black–Scholes model assumptions?

    Options

    A portfolio of long call, short put, short stock, and long bond has identical zero cash flows at expiration across all states of nature, enforcing parity by static no-arbitrage.

  5. What is the limit of European call option price \(C(S, K, r, \sigma, \tau)\) as volatility \(\sigma \to 0\) when \(S < K e^{-r\tau}\)?

    When \(S < K e^{-r\tau}\) and \(\sigma \to 0\), the stock grows deterministically to \(S e^{r\tau} < K\), expiring strictly out-of-the-money with value 0.

  6. In the Garman–Kohlhagen FX option formula, what replaces the stock dividend yield \(q\)?

    Options

    Holding foreign currency earns interest at the foreign rate \(r_f\), which functions identically to a continuous dividend yield.

  7. How does the Breeden–Litzenberger theorem recover the risk-neutral density \(q(K)\) from option prices?

    Options

    Breeden & Litzenberger (1978) proved that \(e^{rT} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\) yields the exact risk-neutral probability density function.

  8. For an at-the-money forward option where \(S = K e^{-r\tau}\) and \(\sigma\sqrt{\tau} \to 0\), what does the option delta \(\Phi(d_1)\) approach?

    When \(S = K e^{-r\tau}\), \(d_1 = \frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau} \to 0\). Then \(\Phi(0) = 0.5\).

  9. What algebraic relationship links \(S \phi(d_1)\) and \(K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2)\)?

    Options

    As proved in Problem P3, completing the square demonstrates that \(S \phi(d_1) = K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2)\) holds identically.

  10. Why does the variance risk premium imply that implied volatility is typically higher than realized volatility?

    Options

    Market makers demand compensation for market crash and volatility gap risk, pricing options at an implied volatility that exceeds realized volatility on average.
