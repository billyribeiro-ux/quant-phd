Delta-Hedging Mechanics & Portfolio Dynamics

In trading rooms, delta hedging is not an abstract mathematical theorem; it is an active daily engineering process. When an option desk sells derivatives to institutional clients, it does not warehouse directional market risk. Instead, the desk dynamically trades the underlying stock and manages a collateralized cash account to neutralize first-order market moves. Understanding the exact accounting equations of dynamic rebalancing, borrowing costs, and cash flow timing reveals how theoretical continuous-time replication translates into operational desk risk management.

  • Introduces: delta hedging
  • Uses: option Greeks, Black–Scholes–Merton PDE
  • Prerequisites: geometric Brownian motion

1. The Mechanics of Delta Neutrality

Definition 5.1 · Delta Hedging

Delta hedging is an active risk-management strategy in which an options position is continuously or periodically paired with an offsetting quantity of the underlying asset to achieve a net portfolio delta of zero:

\[ \Delta_{\mathrm{portfolio}} = \frac{\partial V_{\mathrm{book}}}{\partial S} = 0 \]

For a short European call position of \(N_{\mathrm{opt}}\) contracts, the trader maintains a long position of \(\Delta_t \cdot N_{\mathrm{opt}}\) shares of stock, where \(\Delta_t = \Phi(d_1)\) is the analytical option Greek delta.

Consider a market maker who sells one European call option at time \(t = 0\) for the market premium \(C_0 = C(S_0, K, r, \sigma, T)\). The trader establishes a delta-neutral book by buying \(\Delta_0\) shares of the underlying stock at spot price \(S_0\). The purchase of stock is financed by the option premium received, with any shortfall borrowed from (or excess deposited into) the cash account:

\[ B_0 = C_0 - \Delta_0 S_0 \]

Because \(\Delta_0 = \Phi(d_1) \in (0, 1)\) and \(C_0 < S_0\), the cash balance \(B_0\) is typically negative, representing net borrowing from the money-market account.

2. Discrete Cash Account Financing and Rebalancing

Let the trading horizon \([0, T]\) be partitioned into \(N\) discrete rebalancing intervals of length \(\Delta t = T / N\), with dates \(t_0 = 0 < t_1 < \dots < t_N = T\). Over each step \([t_{k-1}, t_k]\):

  1. Interest accrual: The cash account accumulates interest continuously at the risk-free rate \(r\): \[ B_{t_k}^- = B_{t_{k-1}} \exp(r \Delta t) \]
  2. Stock price update: The stock price moves from \(S_{t_{k-1}}\) to \(S_{t_k}\).
  3. Delta recalculation: The trader computes the updated delta for remaining maturity \(\tau_k = T - t_k\): \[ \Delta_k = \frac{\partial C}{\partial S}(t_k, S_{t_k}) = \Phi(d_1(t_k, S_{t_k})) \]
  4. Rebalancing transaction: To adjust stock inventory from \(\Delta_{k-1}\) to \(\Delta_k\), the trader executes an order for \(\Delta_k - \Delta_{k-1}\) shares at the prevailing spot price \(S_{t_k}\). The trade cash flow \(-(\Delta_k - \Delta_{k-1}) S_{t_k}\) is debited to the cash account: \[ B_{t_k} = B_{t_k}^- - (\Delta_k - \Delta_{k-1}) S_{t_k} = B_{t_{k-1}} e^{r \Delta t} - (\Delta_k - \Delta_{k-1}) S_{t_k} \]

At expiration \(t_N = T\), the option matures with payoff \(h(S_T) = \max(S_T - K, 0)\). The trader liquidates the final stock holding \(\Delta_{N-1} S_T\), adds the final cash balance \(B_N = B_{t_{N-1}} e^{r\Delta t}\), and settles the option liability. The net terminal tracking P&L of the delta-hedged short option is:

\[ \Pi_T = \Delta_{N-1} S_T + B_N - \max(S_T - K, 0) \]

3. Variance Reduction and Risk Neutralization

Theorem 5.1 · Unbiasedness and Variance Reduction of Delta Hedging

Under continuous-time geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility \(\sigma\) and risk-free rate \(r\):

  1. The terminal delta-hedging tracking error has zero expectation: \(\mathbb{E}[\Pi_T] = 0\).
  2. In discrete time with interval \(\Delta t\), the variance of the delta-hedged portfolio satisfies: \[ \operatorname{Var}(\Pi_T) \propto \Delta t \cdot \operatorname{Var}(\text{Unhedged Option}) \] achieving greater than 90% variance reduction for typical daily or weekly rebalancing frequencies.

Proof

From Lesson 1, the discounted portfolio value \(\tilde{V}_t = e^{-rt} V_t\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale under the self-financing condition: \(\tilde{V}_T = V_0 + \int_0^T \Delta_t \mathrm{d}\tilde{S}_t\). Since \(V_0 = C_0\), \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\tilde{V}_T] = C_0\). By Theorem 3.1, the expected discounted option payoff is \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rT} h(S_T)] = C_0\). Hence, \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\Pi_T] = 0\). Under physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\), Girsanov's theorem preserves unbiasedness up to drift covariance of order \(O(\Delta t)\). The variance reduction follows from eliminating the \(O(1)\) directional diffusion risk \(\sigma S_t \mathrm{d}W_t\), leaving only residual discrete convexity errors of order \(O(\Delta t)\).

Lab 1 · Delta hedging simulation: dynamic rebalancing of a short European option

Simulating a delta-hedged short European call option across 2,000 paths with weekly rebalancing (\(N = 50\)) yields an unbiased mean P&L statistically indistinguishable from zero (\(|\mathrm{Mean}| \le 2 \times \mathrm{SE}\)) and achieves greater than 90% variance reduction relative to an unhedged short call.

"""Lab 1 · Delta hedging simulation: dynamic rebalancing of a short European option.

Simulates the discrete delta-hedging of a short European call option position across 2,000
paths under geometric Brownian motion. Tracks the underlying stock, the hedging share position,
the cash borrowing/lending account, and the terminal tracking error:
    E = Delta_{N-1} S_T + B_N - max(S_T - K, 0).
Demonstrates that delta hedging reduces portfolio variance by over 90% relative to the unhedged
short option, while remaining unbiased (mean tracking error statistically indistinguishable from 0).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def bs_call_price(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> float:
    """Analytical European call option price."""
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return max(s - k, 0.0)
    sq_tau = math.sqrt(tau)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * sq_tau)
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sq_tau
    return s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)


def bs_call_delta(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> float:
    """Analytical European call delta."""
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return 1.0 if s > k else 0.0
    sq_tau = math.sqrt(tau)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * sq_tau)
    return norm_cdf(d1)


def simulate_hedged_portfolio(
    s0: float,
    k: float,
    r: float,
    sigma: float,
    maturity: float,
    n_steps: int,
    n_paths: int,
    seed: int,
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Run discrete delta hedging simulation for short call option.

    Returns:
        (mean_hedged_pnl, std_hedged_pnl, mean_unhedged_pnl, std_unhedged_pnl)
    """
    dt = maturity / n_steps
    drift = (r - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt
    vol = sigma * math.sqrt(dt)
    df_step = math.exp(r * dt)

    rng = random.Random(seed)
    c0 = bs_call_price(s0, k, r, sigma, maturity)

    hedged_pnls: list[float] = []
    unhedged_pnls: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(n_paths):
        s = s0
        delta = bs_call_delta(s, k, r, sigma, maturity)
        # Sell 1 call, buy delta shares of stock, financed by cash
        cash = c0 - delta * s

        for step in range(1, n_steps):
            z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            s_next = s * math.exp(drift + vol * z)
            cash = cash * df_step

            tau_rem = max(maturity - step * dt, 1e-12)
            delta_new = bs_call_delta(s_next, k, r, sigma, tau_rem)

            # Rebalance stock holdings: buy (delta_new - delta) shares at s_next
            cash -= (delta_new - delta) * s_next
            delta = delta_new
            s = s_next

        # Final step to maturity
        z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        s_t = s * math.exp(drift + vol * z)
        cash = cash * df_step

        payoff = max(s_t - k, 0.0)
        # Hedged terminal P&L: stock proceeds + cash account - option settlement
        terminal_pnl = delta * s_t + cash - payoff
        hedged_pnls.append(terminal_pnl)

        # Unhedged short call P&L (premium compounded to T minus payoff)
        unhedged_pnl = c0 * math.exp(r * maturity) - payoff
        unhedged_pnls.append(unhedged_pnl)

    mean_h = sum(hedged_pnls) / n_paths
    std_h = math.sqrt(sum((x - mean_h) ** 2 for x in hedged_pnls) / (n_paths - 1))

    mean_u = sum(unhedged_pnls) / n_paths
    std_u = math.sqrt(sum((x - mean_u) ** 2 for x in unhedged_pnls) / (n_paths - 1))

    return mean_h, std_h, mean_u, std_u


def main() -> None:
    # Simulation parameters (SIMULATED)
    s0 = 100.0
    k = 100.0
    r = 0.05
    sigma = 0.20
    maturity = 0.5
    n_steps = 50  # Weekly rebalancing over 6 months
    n_paths = 2000
    seed = 42

    mean_h, std_h, mean_u, std_u = simulate_hedged_portfolio(s0, k, r, sigma, maturity, n_steps, n_paths, seed)

    se_mean_h = std_h / math.sqrt(n_paths)
    var_reduction = (1.0 - (std_h**2) / (std_u**2)) * 100.0

    print("Delta-Hedged vs Unhedged Short Call Performance (SIMULATED):")
    print(f"Hedged Short Call:   Mean P&L = {mean_h:8.4f} (SE = {se_mean_h:6.4f}) | Std = {std_h:7.4f}")
    print(f"Unhedged Short Call: Mean P&L = {mean_u:8.4f}                   | Std = {std_u:7.4f}")
    print(f"Variance reduction via delta hedging: {var_reduction:.2f}%")
    print("Mean hedged P&L is unbiased within 2*SE:", abs(mean_h) <= 2.0 * se_mean_h)
    print("Variance reduction exceeds 90%:", var_reduction > 90.0)


main()

Where the canon breaks

While delta hedging dramatically compresses portfolio variance, practical implementation exposes desks to substantial secondary risks:

  • Gamma risk on large moves: A delta hedge is instantaneous. If the market gaps down 10% on an unexpected earnings announcement or geopolitical crisis, the delta changes before the desk can rebalance. The desk suffers second-order losses of \(\frac{1}{2} \Gamma (\Delta S)^2\).
  • Execution slippage and liquidity black holes: Rebalancing requires buying stock when the market rises and selling when it falls (for short calls, \(\Gamma < 0\)). During market panics, liquidity evaporates; the desk's selling pressure further depresses prices, incurring massive execution slippage.
  • Pinning and gamma explosion: For at-the-money options near expiration, delta snaps between 0 and 1, forcing massive frantic rebalancing orders that generate catastrophic transaction costs.

Misconceptions

  1. Delta hedging guarantees that the trader will make a profit.

    Reality: Delta hedging is a risk-neutralization technique, not an alpha generation strategy. In a frictionless market where realized volatility matches implied volatility, expected profit is exactly zero. In the presence of transaction costs, discrete delta hedging suffers guaranteed negative drag unless the option was sold above its fair implied volatility.

  2. A trader should rebalance the delta hedge continuously on every market tick.

    Reality: Continuous rebalancing in the presence of bid-ask spreads and exchange fees generates infinite transaction costs: \(\sum c S_t |\Delta_{t_k} - \Delta_{t_{k-1}}| \to \infty\) as \(\Delta t \to 0\). Real desks optimize a trade-off between discrete tracking variance (which shrinks with \(\Delta t\)) and transaction costs (which explode with \(1/\sqrt{\Delta t}\)), adopting threshold rebalancing bands.

  3. Once an option book is delta-hedged, the trader can ignore implied volatility changes.

    Reality: Delta hedging neutralizes only spot price moves \(\Delta S\). The book remains fully exposed to changes in implied volatility through Vega \(\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial \sigma}\). If implied volatility spikes, a short option position suffers immediate mark-to-market losses regardless of delta neutrality.

Problem set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A market maker sells 10,000 European call options on a stock currently trading at \(S_0 = \$50\). The call delta is \(\Delta_0 = 0.40\) and the option premium is \(C_0 = \$3.50\). Compute: (a) the number of shares purchased to delta-hedge; (b) the total stock cost; (c) the initial cash balance \(B_0\); and (d) the cash balance after 30 days (\(\Delta t = 30/365\)) at \(r = 0.06\) if no rebalancing occurs.

Solution

(a) To hedge 10,000 short calls, the desk buys \(10,000 \times 0.40 = 4,000\) shares. (b) Total stock cost is \(4,000 \times \$50 = \$200,000\). (c) Premium received is \(10,000 \times \$3.50 = \$35,000\). The initial cash account balance is \(B_0 = \$35,000 - \$200,000 = -\$165,000\) (borrowed). (d) After 30 days (\(\Delta t = 30/365 \approx 0.08219\) yr), the borrowed cash accumulates interest at \(r = 0.06\): \[ B_{30} = -\$165,000 \times \exp(0.06 \times 0.08219) = -\$165,000 \times \exp(0.0049315) \approx -\$165,815.48 \] Interest expense is \(\$815.48\).

Continuing from Problem M1, after 30 days the stock price rises to \(S_1 = \$53\), and the option delta increases to \(\Delta_1 = 0.52\). Compute the cash outlay required to rebalance the stock holding to maintain delta neutrality, and determine the new cash balance \(B_1\).

Solution

The required stock holding is \(10,000 \times 0.52 = 5,200\) shares. The desk currently holds 4,000 shares, so it must purchase \(5,200 - 4,000 = 1,200\) additional shares at \(S_1 = \$53\). The cash outlay for the trade is \(1,200 \times \$53 = \$63,600\). Subtracting this trade from the cash account gives the new balance: \[ B_1 = B_{30} - \$63,600 = -\$165,815.48 - \$63,600 = -\$229,415.48 \] Total borrowing has increased to \(\$229,415.48\).

A trader delta-hedges a long European put option with \(\Delta_{\mathrm{put}} = -0.35\). Explain what stock position must be established, and state whether the initial cash account is positive or negative.

Solution

For a long put option with delta \(\Delta_{\mathrm{put}} = -0.35\), the option loses \(\$0.35\) for every \(\$1.00\) drop in stock. To achieve delta neutrality, the trader must establish a long stock position of \(+0.35\) shares per option contract. The total cash required is the purchase of the put premium \(P_0\) plus the purchase of the stock: \(0.35 S_0\). Therefore, the cash account balance is \(B_0 = -P_0 - 0.35 S_0 < 0\), which is strictly negative (representing total borrowed funds).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for a short call position delta-hedged with stock, the portfolio convexity is strictly negative (short Gamma), meaning that the portfolio suffers losses on both large upward and large downward stock moves over a discrete interval \(\Delta t\).

Solution

Let the portfolio value be \(\Pi(S) = -C(S) + \Delta_0 S + B\), where \(\Delta_0 = C'(S_0)\). Consider a Taylor expansion of \(\Pi(S)\) around \(S_0\): \[ \Pi(S) = [-C(S_0) + \Delta_0 S_0 + B] + (-C'(S_0) + \Delta_0)(S - S_0) - \frac{1}{2} C''(S_0) (S - S_0)^2 + O((S - S_0)^3) \] Since \(\Delta_0 = C'(S_0)\), the linear term \((-C'(S_0) + \Delta_0)(S - S_0) = 0\). The second-order term is \(-\frac{1}{2} \Gamma_0 (\Delta S)^2\). For any standard European call option, Gamma \(\Gamma_0 = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S_0 \sigma \sqrt{\tau}} > 0\). Therefore, the second derivative of the hedged portfolio is \(\Pi''(S_0) = -\Gamma_0 < 0\). Because \((\Delta S)^2 \ge 0\), the term \(-\frac{1}{2} \Gamma_0 (\Delta S)^2 \le 0\) for both positive and negative moves \(\Delta S\). The short call delta hedge is strictly concave, incurring losses on any large move in either direction.

Prove that the discrete-time cash rebalancing equation \(B_{t_k} = B_{t_{k-1}} e^{r \Delta t} - (\Delta_k - \Delta_{k-1}) S_{t_k}\) preserves the discrete self-financing property \(V_{t_k} - V_{t_{k-1}} = \Delta_{k-1} (S_{t_k} - S_{t_{k-1}}) + B_{t_{k-1}} (e^{r\Delta t} - 1)\).

Solution

The total portfolio value before rebalancing at \(t_k\) is \(V_{t_k}^- = \Delta_{k-1} S_{t_k} + B_{t_{k-1}} e^{r\Delta t}\). The value at \(t_{k-1}\) was \(V_{t_{k-1}} = \Delta_{k-1} S_{t_{k-1}} + B_{t_{k-1}}\). Subtracting gives: \[ V_{t_k}^- - V_{t_{k-1}} = \Delta_{k-1} (S_{t_k} - S_{t_{k-1}}) + B_{t_{k-1}} (e^{r\Delta t} - 1) \] Now rebalance stock holdings to \(\Delta_k\) and adjust cash by \(-(\Delta_k - \Delta_{k-1}) S_{t_k}\). The post-rebalance portfolio value is: \[ V_{t_k} = \Delta_k S_{t_k} + B_{t_k} = \Delta_k S_{t_k} + [B_{t_{k-1}} e^{r\Delta t} - (\Delta_k - \Delta_{k-1}) S_{t_k}] = \Delta_{k-1} S_{t_k} + B_{t_{k-1}} e^{r\Delta t} = V_{t_k}^- \] Because \(V_{t_k} = V_{t_k}^-\), rebalancing consumes exactly zero net capital, proving discrete self-financing.

Prove that in continuous time, the total variation of the delta hedge \(\int_0^T |\mathrm{d}\Delta_t|\) is infinite almost surely under geometric Brownian motion, proving that exact continuous replication requires infinite turnover.

Solution

By Itô's lemma, the differential of \(\Delta_t = \Phi(d_1(t, S_t))\) is an Itô diffusion: \[ \mathrm{d}\Delta_t = \mu_\Delta(t, S_t) \mathrm{d}t + \Gamma_t \sigma S_t \mathrm{d}W_t \] where \(\Gamma_t = \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial S} > 0\). The quadratic variation of the delta process is: \[ [\Delta, \Delta]_T = \int_0^T (\Gamma_t \sigma S_t)^2 \mathrm{d}t \] Since \(\Gamma_t \sigma S_t > 0\) almost surely on \([0, T)\), \([\Delta, \Delta]_T > 0\) strictly. In real analysis and stochastic calculus, any continuous stochastic process with strictly positive quadratic variation \([\Delta, \Delta]_T > 0\) must have infinite first-order total variation: \(\int_0^T |\mathrm{d}\Delta_t| = \sup_{\mathcal{P}} \sum |\Delta_{t_k} - \Delta_{t_{k-1}}| = \infty\) almost surely. Thus continuous delta hedging requires infinite trading turnover.

Tier 3 · Empirical

An options desk executes daily delta hedging at 4:00 PM market close. A stock closes at \(\$100\) on Monday (\(\Delta = 0.50\)). On Tuesday, positive earnings are announced at 8:00 AM, causing the stock to gap up and open at \(\$108\) (\(\Delta = 0.70\)). Calculate the overnight tracking loss per 10,000 short call options resulting from the unhedged gap.

Solution

Overnight, the desk held 5,000 shares to hedge 10,000 calls. The stock gained \(\$8.00\), so the stock holding gained \(5,000 \times \$8 = +\$40,000\). Approximate the call option's price increase using average delta: \(\bar{\Delta} = \frac{0.50 + 0.70}{2} = 0.60\). The 10,000 short calls lost approximately \(10,000 \times 0.60 \times \$8 = -\$48,000\). The net overnight gap tracking loss is \(\$40,000 - \$48,000 = -\$8,000\). The gap occurred outside trading hours when continuous rebalancing was impossible.

In an empirical backtest of daily delta hedging, why does an option desk experience higher tracking errors during high-volatility regimes even when the option is hedged using the exact realized volatility?

Solution

Discrete hedging error over each interval \(\Delta t\) is governed by the second-order Taylor term \(\frac{1}{2} \Gamma_t (\Delta S_t)^2\). Under geometric Brownian motion, \(\mathbb{E}[(\Delta S_t)^2] \approx \sigma^2 S_t^2 \Delta t\), and its variance scales with \(\sigma^4 S_t^4 (\Delta t)^2\). When volatility \(\sigma\) doubles from 15% to 30%, the magnitude of squared daily price moves increases by a factor of 4, quadrupling the dispersion of daily convexity slippage. Even if the hedge uses the exact true volatility, discrete sampling error is proportional to \(\sigma^2\).

Explain the operational risk known as "gamma scalping" employed by option market makers. How does a long gamma desk generate cash flows from underlying price oscillations?

Solution

A trader long European options holds positive Gamma (\(\Gamma > 0\)). When the stock rises, delta increases, so the trader sells stock at high prices to restore delta neutrality. When the stock falls, delta decreases, so the trader buys stock at low prices. Over repeated market oscillations, this dynamic forces the trader to systematically "sell high and buy low", harvesting cash flows that offset the negative Theta time decay of the long options. If realized volatility exceeds implied volatility, cumulative gamma scalping cash flows exceed total theta paid.

Tier 4 · Research

Analyze the band-hedging framework of Whalley & Wilmott (1997) for optimal delta hedging with proportional transaction costs. How does the width of the no-trade band \([\Delta - \delta, \Delta + \delta]\) scale with transaction cost \(c\) and Gamma?

Solution

Whalley & Wilmott (1997, Mathematical Finance) formulated optimal hedging under proportional transaction costs \(c\) as an asymptotic stochastic control problem. Rather than rebalancing at fixed time intervals, the trader maintains a no-transaction band around the Black–Scholes delta \([\Delta - \delta, \Delta + \delta]\). Rebalancing trades occur only when the actual stock holding breaches the boundary. By asymptotic expansion in the small parameter \(c \to 0\), the optimal half-width of the no-trade band scales as: \[ \delta(t, S) = \left(\frac{3 c S \Gamma^2}{2 \gamma}\right)^{1/3} \] where \(\gamma\) is the absolute risk-aversion parameter. The no-trade bandwidth scales with the cube root of transaction costs \(c^{1/3}\) and with \(\Gamma^{2/3}\), widening in volatile regions to balance transaction costs against tracking variance.

Examine Baltussen, van Bekkum & van der Grient (2021) regarding institutional hedging demand and market intraday momentum. How does market-maker delta hedging amplify market trends into the close?

Solution

Baltussen et al. (2021, Journal of Financial Economics) demonstrated that index option market makers are structurally short index options (short Gamma). When markets trend upward during the day, market makers become increasingly short delta, forcing them to buy large volumes of index futures into the 4:00 PM close to rebalance. Conversely, when markets fall, market makers are forced to sell heavily into the close. This non-discretionary rebalancing order flow amplifies intraday momentum, explaining why market returns in the final 30 minutes of the trading day exhibit strong positive autocorrelation with earlier-day returns.

How does prime brokerage margin mechanics impact dynamic replication? Formulate the funding liquidity constraint when borrowing capacity is limited by exchange margin requirements (e.g. Reg T or portfolio margin).

Solution

Classical replication assumes unconstrained borrowing at rate \(r\). In practice, prime brokers impose initial margin \(m_I\) and maintenance margin \(m_M\) on both short options and stock inventory. The trader must pledge liquid unencumbered cash collateral \(C_t \ge m_I(S_t, \Delta_t, V_t)\). If a sudden market gap increases margin requirements, the trader may face a margin call. If the trader cannot post cash, the prime broker forces premature liquidation of the replicating stock at distressed prices, truncating the replication strategy and converting a paper tracking error into irreversible capital impairment (Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009).

Retrieval quiz

  1. What does delta neutrality eliminate from an option portfolio?

    Options

    Delta hedging sets \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = 0\), cancelling linear first-order exposure to infinitesimal spot moves while leaving higher-order risks intact.

  2. To delta-hedge a short position of 1,000 European call options with \(\Delta = 0.60\), how many shares of stock must the trader buy?

    Total delta is \(1,000 \times 0.60 = 600\) shares.

  3. In a short call delta hedge, why is the initial cash account balance \(B_0 = C_0 - \Delta_0 S_0\) typically negative?

    Options

    Since \(C_0 = S_0 \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-rT} \Phi(d_2)\) and \(\Delta_0 = \Phi(d_1)\), \(C_0 - \Delta_0 S_0 = -K e^{-rT}\Phi(d_2) < 0\), so borrowing is always required.

  4. Why does a short call delta-hedged position lose money on large overnight market gaps in either direction?

    Options

    Shorting an option gives negative Gamma. Taylor expansion shows the portfolio suffers second-order losses of \(-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(\Delta S)^2\) on both positive and negative moves.

  5. In a frictionless continuous market, what is the expected terminal tracking error \(\mathbb{E}[\Pi_T]\) of a delta-hedged book?

    In frictionless continuous time, delta hedging is an exact replication, so the expected tracking error is zero.

  6. What trading action does a long gamma desk perform during gamma scalping when the stock price rises?

    Options

    When the stock rises, long gamma causes call delta to increase. To neutralize the excess positive delta, the trader sells shares of stock.

  7. Why is continuous delta hedging impossible in real financial markets with bid-ask spreads?

    Options

    Because Brownian motion has infinite first-order variation, continuous rebalancing under proportional trading costs incurs infinite cumulative fees.

  8. What approximate percentage of variance does weekly delta hedging eliminate relative to an unhedged short call?

    Delta hedging typically eliminates 90% to 99% of total unhedged option variance.

  9. How does market-maker delta hedging amplify market momentum into the close according to Baltussen et al. (2021)?

    Options

    Short gamma requires buying stock when the market goes up and selling when it drops, magnifying market trends into the 4:00 PM close.

  10. In the Whalley & Wilmott (1997) model, how does the optimal delta no-trade bandwidth scale with transaction cost \(c\)?

    Options

    Asymptotic expansion proves that the optimal rebalancing band scales as \((3 c S \Gamma^2 / 2\gamma)^{1/3} \propto c^{1/3}\).
