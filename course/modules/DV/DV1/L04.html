Option Greeks: Sensitivities & Invariants

An option position is not a static bet; it is a multi-dimensional surface of risk exposures. In quantitative trading, risk management and portfolio optimization operate by decomposing an option's sensitivity into partial derivatives with respect to spot price, volatility, passage of time, and interest rates. Known universally as the Greeks, these sensitivities dictate hedging ratios, margin requirements, and P&L attribution. Understanding their mathematical structure—and the deep algebraic identities linking Gamma to Vega and Theta—is essential for managing real-world trading desks.

  • Introduces: option Greeks
  • Uses: Black–Scholes–Merton PDE
  • Prerequisites: Black–Scholes formula, partial derivative

1. Definition of the Option Greeks

Definition 4.1 · Option Greeks

The option Greeks are the partial derivatives of the option pricing function \(V(t, S; K, r, \sigma)\) with respect to its state variables and parameters:

  • Delta (\(\Delta\)): First-order directional sensitivity to the underlying asset price: \(\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\).
  • Gamma (\(\Gamma\)): Second-order curvature sensitivity to the underlying asset price: \(\Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\).
  • Vega (\(\mathcal{V}\)): First-order sensitivity to implied volatility: \(\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial \sigma}\).
  • Theta (\(\Theta\)): Sensitivity to the passage of calendar time: \(\Theta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau}\), where \(\tau = T - t\).
  • Rho (\(\rho\)): Sensitivity to the risk-free interest rate: \(\rho = \frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\).

In addition to these primary first- and second-order Greeks, trading desks track cross-derivatives: Vanna (\(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S \partial \sigma}\), sensitivity of delta to volatility) and Volga / Vomma (\(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \sigma^2}\), convexity of vega with respect to volatility).

2. Analytical Black–Scholes Greek Formulas

Recall the European call pricing formula \(C = S \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(d_2)\), where:

\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau} \]

and \(\phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}\). From Lesson 3, we have the fundamental algebraic identity: \(S \phi(d_1) = K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2)\).

Theorem 4.1 · Closed-Form European Call and Put Greeks

For European calls (\(C\)) and puts (\(P\)) under constant \(r\) and \(\sigma\), the Greeks are given analytically by:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta_{\mathrm{call}} &= \Phi(d_1), \quad \Delta_{\mathrm{put}} = \Phi(d_1) - 1 = -\Phi(-d_1) \\ \Gamma_{\mathrm{call}} &= \Gamma_{\mathrm{put}} = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{\tau}} \\ \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{call}} &= \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{put}} = S \sqrt{\tau} \, \phi(d_1) \\ \Theta_{\mathrm{call}} &= -\frac{S \phi(d_1) \sigma}{2\sqrt{\tau}} - r K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(d_2) \\ \Theta_{\mathrm{put}} &= -\frac{S \phi(d_1) \sigma}{2\sqrt{\tau}} + r K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(-d_2) \\ \rho_{\mathrm{call}} &= K \tau e^{-r\tau} \Phi(d_2), \quad \rho_{\mathrm{put}} = -K \tau e^{-r\tau} \Phi(-d_2) \end{aligned} \]

Proof (Delta and Gamma)

Differentiating \(C = S \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(d_2)\) with respect to \(S\):

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} = \Phi(d_1) + S \phi(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} - K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial S} \]

Since \(d_1 - d_2 = \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\) is independent of \(S\), \(\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} = \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial S} = \frac{1}{S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}\). Substituting this and using the density identity \(S \phi(d_1) = K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2)\):

\[ S \phi(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} - K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial S} = [S \phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2)] \frac{1}{S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} = 0 \]

Therefore, the chain rule derivatives through \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) cancel out identically, leaving \(\Delta_{\mathrm{call}} = \Phi(d_1)\). Differentiating \(\Delta_{\mathrm{call}}\) with respect to \(S\) immediately yields \(\Gamma = \phi(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{\tau}}\).

3. The Fundamental Gamma–Vega Identity

A remarkable algebraic symmetry connects the second spatial derivative (Gamma) and the volatility sensitivity (Vega). Comparing the expressions:

\[ \Gamma = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{\tau}}, \quad \mathcal{V} = S \sqrt{\tau} \, \phi(d_1) \]
Theorem 4.2 · The Gamma–Vega Identity

For any European option under Black–Scholes–Merton dynamics, Vega and Gamma satisfy the exact deterministic relation:

\[ \mathcal{V} = \sigma S^2 \tau \, \Gamma \]

Proof

Multiply \(\Gamma\) by \(\sigma S^2 \tau\):

\[ \sigma S^2 \tau \, \Gamma = \sigma S^2 \tau \left(\frac{\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{\tau}}\right) = \frac{\sigma S^2 \tau}{\sigma S \sqrt{\tau}} \phi(d_1) = S \sqrt{\tau} \, \phi(d_1) = \mathcal{V} \]

The identity holds identically for both calls and puts across all parameter values.

This identity has profound practical implications for derivatives risk management: an option position cannot have high Vega without having high Gamma. A trader who seeks to harvest volatility risk premiums by selling Vega inevitably exposes the portfolio to extreme local curvature risk (Gamma), creating severe exposure to large discontinuous market gaps.

4. Numerical Verification via Finite Differences

In quantitative production systems, complex derivatives pricing engines lack closed-form Greek formulas. Desks rely on symmetric second-order central finite differences:

\[ \Delta \approx \frac{V(S + h) - V(S - h)}{2h}, \quad \Gamma \approx \frac{V(S + h) - 2V(S) + V(S - h)}{h^2} \]

where the step size \(h = 10^{-4} S\) balances truncation error \(O(h^2)\) against roundoff cancellation.

Lab 1 · Analytical option Greeks vs central finite-difference approximations

Analytical Black–Scholes formulas for Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta, and Rho match second-order central finite differences with relative error strictly below \(10^{-5}\). The algebraic identity \(\mathcal{V} = \sigma S^2 \tau \Gamma\) holds to machine precision.

"""Lab 1 · Analytical option Greeks vs central finite-difference approximations.

Computes analytical formulas for Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta (calendar time derivative d_t V),
and Rho for European call and put options. Compares each analytical Greek against second-order
central finite differences, verifying that relative errors remain strictly below 1e-5.
Also verifies the fundamental identity connecting Gamma and Vega:
    Vega = sigma S^2 tau Gamma.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def norm_pdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal probability density function."""
    return math.exp(-0.5 * x * x) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)


def bs_price(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float, is_call: bool = True) -> float:
    """Compute European call or put price."""
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return max(s - k, 0.0) if is_call else max(k - s, 0.0)
    sq_tau = math.sqrt(tau)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * sq_tau)
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sq_tau
    df = math.exp(-r * tau)
    if is_call:
        return s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * df * norm_cdf(d2)
    return k * df * norm_cdf(-d2) - s * norm_cdf(-d1)


def analytical_greeks(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float, is_call: bool = True) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Compute analytical Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta (calendar time d_t V), and Rho."""
    sq_tau = math.sqrt(tau)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * sq_tau)
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sq_tau
    df = math.exp(-r * tau)
    pdf_d1 = norm_pdf(d1)

    gamma = pdf_d1 / (s * sigma * sq_tau)
    vega = s * sq_tau * pdf_d1

    if is_call:
        delta = norm_cdf(d1)
        theta = -(s * pdf_d1 * sigma) / (2.0 * sq_tau) - r * k * df * norm_cdf(d2)
        rho = k * tau * df * norm_cdf(d2)
    else:
        delta = norm_cdf(d1) - 1.0
        theta = -(s * pdf_d1 * sigma) / (2.0 * sq_tau) + r * k * df * norm_cdf(-d2)
        rho = -k * tau * df * norm_cdf(-d2)

    return {"delta": delta, "gamma": gamma, "vega": vega, "theta": theta, "rho": rho}


def finite_difference_greeks(
    s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float, is_call: bool = True
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Compute central finite-difference approximations for all Greeks."""
    h_s = 1e-4 * s
    h_v = 1e-5
    h_r = 1e-5
    h_t = 1e-5

    v0 = bs_price(s, k, r, sigma, tau, is_call)
    v_s_up = bs_price(s + h_s, k, r, sigma, tau, is_call)
    v_s_down = bs_price(s - h_s, k, r, sigma, tau, is_call)
    delta_fd = (v_s_up - v_s_down) / (2.0 * h_s)
    gamma_fd = (v_s_up - 2.0 * v0 + v_s_down) / (h_s**2)

    v_v_up = bs_price(s, k, r, sigma + h_v, tau, is_call)
    v_v_down = bs_price(s, k, r, sigma - h_v, tau, is_call)
    vega_fd = (v_v_up - v_v_down) / (2.0 * h_v)

    # Note: tau = T - t, so d_t V = -d_tau V
    v_t_up = bs_price(s, k, r, sigma, tau + h_t, is_call)
    v_t_down = bs_price(s, k, r, sigma, tau - h_t, is_call)
    theta_fd = -(v_t_up - v_t_down) / (2.0 * h_t)

    v_r_up = bs_price(s, k, r + h_r, sigma, tau, is_call)
    v_r_down = bs_price(s, k, r - h_r, sigma, tau, is_call)
    rho_fd = (v_r_up - v_r_down) / (2.0 * h_r)

    return {"delta": delta_fd, "gamma": gamma_fd, "vega": vega_fd, "theta": theta_fd, "rho": rho_fd}


def main() -> None:
    # Contract parameters (SIMULATED)
    s = 100.0
    k = 100.0
    r = 0.05
    sigma = 0.20
    tau = 0.50

    call_exact = analytical_greeks(s, k, r, sigma, tau, is_call=True)
    call_fd = finite_difference_greeks(s, k, r, sigma, tau, is_call=True)

    print("European Call Greeks Comparison (SIMULATED):")
    all_pass = True
    for g in ["delta", "gamma", "vega", "theta", "rho"]:
        exact = call_exact[g]
        fd = call_fd[g]
        rel_err = abs(exact - fd) / (abs(exact) + 1e-12)
        passed = rel_err < 1e-5
        if not passed:
            all_pass = False
        print(
            f"{g.capitalize():6s} | Analytical = {exact:11.6f} | FD = {fd:11.6f} | "
            f"RelErr = {rel_err:.2e} | Pass: {passed}"
        )

    # Gamma-Vega identity check: Vega = sigma * S^2 * tau * Gamma
    gamma_vega_lhs = call_exact["vega"]
    gamma_vega_rhs = sigma * (s**2) * tau * call_exact["gamma"]
    identity_diff = abs(gamma_vega_lhs - gamma_vega_rhs)
    print(
        f"Gamma-Vega identity check: Vega = {gamma_vega_lhs:.6f}, "
        f"sigma*S^2*tau*Gamma = {gamma_vega_rhs:.6f}, Diff = {identity_diff:.2e}"
    )
    print("Gamma-Vega identity holds exactly:", identity_diff < 1e-12)
    print("All Greek finite differences within 1e-5 tolerance:", all_pass)

Where the canon breaks

Black–Scholes Greeks assume constant volatility, leading to severe hedging distortions in real markets:

  • Sticky strike vs sticky delta: In markets with a volatility skew, a change in stock price shifts the implied volatility. Black–Scholes delta assumes \(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}S} = 0\). The true market delta is \(\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}S} = \Delta_{\mathrm{BS}} + \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}}{\partial S}\). Hedging with plain Black–Scholes delta results in systematic under- or over-hedging.
  • Pin risk at expiration: As \(\tau \to 0\) for an at-the-money option, Gamma explodes toward infinity: \(\Gamma \propto \tau^{-1/2} \to \infty\). Delta oscillates violently between 0 and 1 over infinitesimal stock moves, rendering delta hedging practically impossible during the final expiration hours.
  • Jump risk destroys delta neutrality: A delta-neutral portfolio (\(\Delta = 0\)) is protected only against infinitesimal diffusion increments \(\mathrm{d}W_t\). If the stock jumps discretely by \(\Delta S\), Taylor expansion shows the portfolio suffers unexpected loss \(\frac{1}{2} \Gamma (\Delta S)^2\).

Misconceptions

  1. Vega is a true derivative of the option price with respect to a state variable.

    Reality: In the Black–Scholes–Merton model, volatility \(\sigma\) is a constant parameter, not a stochastic state variable. Mathematically, Vega \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial \sigma}\) is a comparative statics derivative with respect to a model parameter. In stochastic volatility models (Heston 1993, DV3), volatility becomes a true state variable with its own diffusion increment.

  2. A delta-neutral portfolio has zero risk.

    Reality: Delta neutrality (\(\Delta = 0\)) eliminates only linear directional exposure to infinitesimal stock price changes. The portfolio remains exposed to Gamma (curvature risk on large moves), Vega (implied volatility shifts), Theta (time decay), and higher-order risks.

  3. Long option positions always have negative Theta because time decay erodes value.

    Reality: While long European call options and most put options have negative Theta (\(\Theta < 0\)), deep in-the-money European put options on high-interest-rate assets have positive Theta (\(\Theta > 0\)). This occurs because the time value of receiving the strike \(K\) sooner exceeds the optionality value, causing the option price to appreciate as expiration approaches.

Problem set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Calculate the analytical Delta, Gamma, and Vega for a European call option with \(S = \$120\), \(K = \$120\), \(r = 0.05\), \(\sigma = 0.25\), and \(\tau = 0.5\) years. Given \(\Phi(0.2285) = 0.5904\) and \(\phi(0.2285) = 0.3887\).

Solution

Compute \(d_1\): \[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(120/120) + (0.05 + 0.5 \times 0.0625) \times 0.5}{0.25 \times \sqrt{0.5}} = \frac{0 + 0.08125 \times 0.5}{0.25 \times 0.707107} = \frac{0.040625}{0.176777} \approx 0.2298 \approx 0.2285 \] Using the given values: Call Delta is \(\Delta = \Phi(d_1) = 0.5904\). Gamma is: \[ \Gamma = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{\tau}} = \frac{0.3887}{120 \times 0.25 \times 0.707107} = \frac{0.3887}{21.2132} \approx 0.01832 \] Vega is: \[ \mathcal{V} = S \sqrt{\tau} \, \phi(d_1) = 120 \times 0.707107 \times 0.3887 = 84.8528 \times 0.3887 \approx 32.982 \] The option has Delta \(0.5904\), Gamma \(0.01832\), and Vega \(32.98\).

A trader is short 5,000 European call options with \(\Delta = 0.55\), \(\Gamma = 0.025\), and \(\mathcal{V} = 28.0\). Calculate the number of shares required to make the portfolio delta-neutral, and state the net Gamma and Vega of the hedged book.

Solution

For 5,000 short call options, total portfolio delta is \(-5000 \times 0.55 = -2750\) shares. To make the portfolio delta-neutral, the trader must purchase \(+2,750\) shares of the underlying stock. Since shares have zero Gamma and zero Vega, the net portfolio Gamma remains \(-5000 \times 0.025 = -125.0\) (short gamma), and net portfolio Vega remains \(-5000 \times 28.0 = -140,000\) (short vega). Delta hedging leaves the book completely exposed to second-order moves and implied volatility spikes.

Using the Gamma–Vega identity \(\mathcal{V} = \sigma S^2 \tau \Gamma\), determine the Gamma of an option trading at \(S = \$50\) with volatility \(\sigma = 0.30\) and maturity \(\tau = 0.25\) years, given that its Vega is \(\mathcal{V} = 15.0\).

Solution

From the identity \(\mathcal{V} = \sigma S^2 \tau \Gamma\), solve for \(\Gamma\): \[ \Gamma = \frac{\mathcal{V}}{\sigma S^2 \tau} = \frac{15.0}{0.30 \times (50)^2 \times 0.25} = \frac{15.0}{0.30 \times 2500 \times 0.25} = \frac{15.0}{187.5} = 0.08 \] The option's Gamma is exactly \(0.08\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that European call Gamma reaches its global maximum with respect to strike \(K\) when \(K = S \exp((r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau)\), which corresponds exactly to \(d_1 = 0\).

Solution

The call Gamma is \(\Gamma = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{\tau}} = \frac{1}{S \sigma \sqrt{2\pi \tau}} \exp(-d_1^2 / 2)\). Since \(S, \sigma, \tau > 0\) are fixed, \(\Gamma\) is proportional to \(\exp(-d_1^2 / 2)\). The exponential \(\exp(-x^2/2)\) achieves its unique global maximum when its exponent is zero, which occurs if and only if \(d_1 = 0\). Setting \(d_1 = 0\): \[ \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} = 0 \iff \ln(S/K) + (r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau = 0 \] Solving for \(K\): \[ \ln(K/S) = (r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau \iff K = S \exp\left(\left(r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\tau\right) \] Thus Gamma peaks at this slightly in-the-money strike.

Prove that Vanna satisfies the dual representation: \(\frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{V}}{\partial S} = -\frac{\phi(d_1) d_2}{\sigma}\).

Solution

By Schwarz's theorem on mixed partial derivatives, for any \(C^2\) function, \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S \partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma}\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S}\right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial S}\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma}\right)\). First, differentiate Delta \(\Delta = \Phi(d_1)\) with respect to \(\sigma\): \[ \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma} = \phi(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} \] From \(d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + r\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\): \[ \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} = -\frac{\ln(S/K) + r\tau}{\sigma^2\sqrt{\tau}} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\tau} = -\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\frac{\ln(S/K) + r\tau - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}\right) = -\frac{d_2}{\sigma} \] Therefore, \(\frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma} = -\frac{\phi(d_1) d_2}{\sigma}\). Second, differentiate Vega \(\mathcal{V} = S\sqrt{\tau}\phi(d_1)\) with respect to \(S\): \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{V}}{\partial S} = \sqrt{\tau} \phi(d_1) + S\sqrt{\tau} \phi'(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} = \sqrt{\tau}\phi(d_1) - S\sqrt{\tau} d_1 \phi(d_1) \frac{1}{S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} = \phi(d_1)\left[\sqrt{\tau} - \frac{d_1}{\sigma}\right] = -\frac{\phi(d_1) d_2}{\sigma} \] Both expressions match identically.

Prove that the BSM PDE can be written purely in terms of the Greeks as \(\Theta + r S \Delta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma - r V = 0\), and deduce that for a delta-neutral portfolio (\(\Delta = 0\)), Theta and Gamma must have opposite signs whenever \(r V \approx 0\).

Solution

The BSM PDE is \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - r V = 0\). Substituting the Greek definitions \(\Theta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\), \(\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\), and \(\Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\) gives directly: \[ \Theta + r S \Delta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma - r V = 0 \] For a delta-neutral portfolio with \(\Delta = 0\) and negligible interest financing \(r V \approx 0\): \[ \Theta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma = 0 \iff \Theta = -\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma \] Since \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 > 0\), \(\Theta\) and \(\Gamma\) must have strictly opposite signs. Long gamma (\(\Gamma > 0\)) guarantees negative time decay (\(\Theta < 0\), "paying theta to rent gamma"), while short gamma (\(\Gamma < 0\)) collects positive theta (\(\Theta > 0\)).

Tier 3 · Empirical

An options desk runs a trading book with net Delta = \(\$0\), net Gamma = \(-\$50,000\) per 1% move, and net Vega = \(-\$120,000\) per 1 vol point. The underlying index suddenly drops by 4% overnight while 30-day implied volatility surges by 5 percentage points. Calculate the estimated overnight P&L from Gamma and Vega.

Solution

The portfolio is delta-neutral. The second-order Taylor P&L from the 4% move (\(\Delta S = -4\) percentage points) under Gamma is: \[ \text{P\&L}_{\Gamma} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Gamma} \times (\Delta S)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times (-50000) \times (4)^2 = -25000 \times 16 = -\$400,000 \] The P&L from the 5-point volatility surge (\(\Delta \sigma = +5\)) under Vega is: \[ \text{P\&L}_{\mathcal{V}} = \text{Vega} \times \Delta \sigma = (-120000) \times 5 = -\$600,000 \] Total estimated overnight loss is \(-400,000 - 600,000 = -\$1,000,000\). Short Gamma and short Vega amplify losses during market selloffs.

Explain the practical phenomenon of "volatility clustering on option expiration dates" documented by Ni, Pearson & Poteshman (2005), and relate it to the hedging behavior of market makers with large open interest.

Solution

Ni, Pearson & Poteshman (2005, Journal of Financial Economics) showed that underlying stock prices cluster near strike prices on option expiration dates. When market makers are net long options near expiration, they possess positive Gamma (\(\Gamma > 0\)). As the stock rises, their delta increases, requiring them to sell stock to rebalance; as the stock falls, they buy stock. This dynamic delta-hedging feedback exerts a stabilizing, mean-reverting force that pulls the underlying price toward the strike, dampening realized volatility into the close.

In high-frequency automated market making, why do market makers prefer to delta-hedge using liquid index futures rather than the underlying basket of individual constituent stocks?

Solution

Delta hedging an index option book with constituent equities incurs massive execution friction: trading 500 individual stocks triggers exchange ticket fees, odd-lot slippage, and non-synchronous execution latency. Liquid index futures (such as E-mini S&P 500 futures) trade with sub-tick effective bid-ask spreads, zero short-borrow borrowing friction, and nearly instantaneous execution, dramatically minimizing hedging transaction costs and execution tracking error.

Tier 4 · Research

How does local volatility \(\sigma(t, S)\) modify the Black–Scholes delta? Formulate the exact local-volatility delta and explain why it explains the empirical failure of standard BSM delta hedging.

Solution

In local volatility models (Dupire 1994, DV2), the option price is \(V(t, S) = V_{\mathrm{BS}}(t, S, \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}(t, S))\). Differentiating with respect to spot \(S\) yields the total market delta: \[ \Delta_{\mathrm{LV}} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}}{\partial S} = \Delta_{\mathrm{BS}} + \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}}{\partial S} \] In equity markets, the implied volatility skew is downward sloping (\(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}}{\partial S} < 0\)). Because \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}} > 0\), the correction term \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}}{\partial S}\) is strictly negative. Consequently, the true local-volatility delta is strictly lower than the standard Black–Scholes delta (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{LV}} < \Delta_{\mathrm{BS}}\)). A trader using standard Black–Scholes delta will over-hedge long calls and under-hedge puts.

Analyze the behavior of Gamma and Vega in the rough volatility framework of Gatheral, Jaisson & Rosenbaum (2018), where volatility exhibits fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter \(H \approx 0.1\).

Solution

Gatheral, Jaisson & Rosenbaum (2018, Quantitative Finance) demonstrated that historical and implied volatility exhibits rough trajectories with Hurst exponent \(H \approx 0.1 < 0.5\), violating semimartingale and Markovian diffusion properties. Under rough volatility, the short-end at-the-money skew scales as \(\tau^{H - 1/2} \approx \tau^{-0.4}\) rather than remaining bounded. Consequently, short-dated Gamma and Vega exhibit intense singular concentration near maturity, producing non-standard Greek hedging ratios that require fractional calculus representations rather than classical parabolic PDEs.

Investigate the "Gamma trap" during extreme short squeezes (such as the GameStop phenomenon of January 2021). How does retail call option buying force dealer delta-hedging to create a positive feedback loop?

Solution

When retail investors aggressively purchase short-dated out-of-the-money call options, market makers take the short call position. The market makers are short Gamma (\(\Gamma < 0\)). As the underlying stock price rises toward the strike, call delta increases rapidly (\(\frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial S} = \Gamma > 0\)). To remain delta-neutral, market makers must purchase more shares of stock as the price goes up. This mandatory buying pressure drives the stock higher, which increases delta further, forcing additional stock purchases. This recursive positive feedback loop accelerates upward price surges until retail call buying exhausts or margin limits intervene.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the delta of a European call option under Black–Scholes?

    Options

    By differentiating the call formula with respect to \(S\), the chain-rule derivatives cancel out, leaving \(\Delta = \Phi(d_1)\).

  2. What is the difference between call delta and put delta: \(\Delta_{\mathrm{call}} - \Delta_{\mathrm{put}}\)?

    Since \(\Delta_{\mathrm{call}} = \Phi(d_1)\) and \(\Delta_{\mathrm{put}} = \Phi(d_1) - 1\), their difference is exactly 1.

  3. Which mathematical formula represents the exact algebraic link between Vega and Gamma?

    Options

    As proved in Theorem 4.2, multiplying \(\Gamma\) by \(\sigma S^2 \tau\) gives \(\mathcal{V} = S\sqrt{\tau}\phi(d_1)\).

  4. For a delta-neutral portfolio with negligible interest financing, what is the relationship between Theta and Gamma?

    Options

    Substituting \(\Delta = 0\) into the BSM PDE shows that Theta and Gamma trade off inversely: positive gamma costs negative theta.

  5. For an out-of-the-money option as expiration approaches (\(\tau \to 0\)), what does option Gamma approach?

    For \(S \ne K\), \(|d_1| \to \infty\) as \(\tau \to 0\), causing \(\phi(d_1) \to 0\) faster than \(\sqrt{\tau}\), so Gamma collapses to 0.

  6. Why does deep in-the-money European put option have positive Theta (\(\Theta > 0\)) under positive interest rates?

    Options

    A deep ITM put approaches \(K e^{-r\tau} - S\). As \(\tau\) decreases, \(K e^{-r\tau}\) increases toward \(K\), increasing the put's value as time passes.

  7. What does Vanna measure?

    Options

    Vanna is the mixed partial derivative with respect to spot price and volatility: \(\frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{V}}{\partial S}\).

  8. What is the approximate Delta of an at-the-money forward European call option?

    At-the-money forward has \(d_1 \approx 0\), so \(\Delta = \Phi(0) = 0.5\).

  9. How does local volatility skew (\(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}}{\partial S} < 0\)) affect equity call delta?

    Options

    Because \(\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}S} = \Delta_{\mathrm{BS}} + \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}} \frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}}{\partial S}\), a negative skew reduces the effective hedging delta.

  10. What Greek measures the curvature of an option's vega with respect to volatility?

    Options

    Volga / Vomma is \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{V}}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \sigma^2}\), measuring volatility convexity.
