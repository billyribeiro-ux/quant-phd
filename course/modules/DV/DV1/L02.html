The Black–Scholes–Merton Differential Equation

The crowning achievement of modern financial economics is the realization that a contingent claim's value satisfies a deterministic partial differential equation despite being driven by stochastic Brownian motion. By pairing an option with an offsetting position in the underlying stock, an investor constructs a portfolio whose instantaneous return has zero diffusion variance. Under the no-arbitrage principle, this risk-free portfolio must earn the riskless interest rate, yielding a second-order parabolic PDE where the asset's subjective expected return completely cancels out.

  • Introduces: Black–Scholes–Merton PDE
  • Uses: dynamic replication
  • Prerequisites: geometric Brownian motion, Itô's lemma, arbitrage opportunity

1. Itô Expansion of the Contingent Claim Value

Let the underlying stock price \(S_t\) evolve according to geometric Brownian motion under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\):

\[ \mathrm{d}S_t = \mu S_t \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t \mathrm{d}W_t \]

where the drift \(\mu \in \mathbb{R}\) and volatility \(\sigma > 0\) are constants, and \(W_t\) is a standard Brownian motion. Consider a European contingent claim maturing at time \(T\) with payoff \(h(S_T)\). Assume the claim price at time \(t < T\) can be represented as a deterministic function of time and spot price: \(V_t = V(t, S_t)\), where \(V \in C^{1, 2}([0, T) \times (0, \infty))\).

Applying Itô's lemma to \(V(t, S_t)\) yields the stochastic differential:

\[ \mathrm{d}V_t = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \mu S_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \mathrm{d}W_t \]

The differential \(\mathrm{d}V_t\) consists of a predictable drift rate and a diffusion term driven by the same Brownian increment \(\mathrm{d}W_t\) that moves the underlying stock \(S_t\). Because the option and the stock share the identical single source of randomness, the diffusion risk can be perfectly eliminated by linear combination.

2. Instantaneously Riskless Hedged Portfolio

To eliminate the stochastic increment \(\mathrm{d}W_t\), construct a portfolio \(\Pi_t\) consisting of one long position in the option and a short position of \(\Delta_t\) shares in the underlying stock:

\[ \Pi_t = V(t, S_t) - \Delta_t S_t \]

Over an infinitesimal time interval \([t, t + \mathrm{d}t]\), the change in portfolio value under a continuous self-financing rebalancing strategy is:

\[ \mathrm{d}\Pi_t = \mathrm{d}V_t - \Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t \]

Substituting the Itô expansion for \(\mathrm{d}V_t\) and \(\mathrm{d}S_t\) gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\Pi_t &= \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \mu S_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \mathrm{d}W_t - \Delta_t (\mu S_t \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t \mathrm{d}W_t) \\ &= \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + \mu S_t \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta_t\right)\right)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta_t\right)\mathrm{d}W_t \end{aligned} \]

Setting the hedge ratio to the partial derivative:

\[ \Delta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}(t, S_t) \]

both the Brownian diffusion term \(\sigma S_t (\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta_t)\mathrm{d}W_t\) and the subjective drift term \(\mu S_t (\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta_t)\mathrm{d}t\) vanish identically. The portfolio increment becomes entirely deterministic over \([t, t + \mathrm{d}t]\):

\[ \mathrm{d}\Pi_t = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)\mathrm{d}t \]

3. The Black–Scholes–Merton Differential Equation

Because \(\mathrm{d}\Pi_t\) is completely free of Brownian risk over \([t, t + \mathrm{d}t]\), the portfolio \(\Pi_t\) is instantaneously riskless. By the absence of arbitrage opportunities, any instantaneously riskless asset must earn the risk-free interest rate \(r\):

\[ \mathrm{d}\Pi_t = r \Pi_t \mathrm{d}t = r (V_t - \Delta_t S_t) \mathrm{d}t = r \left(V - S_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)\mathrm{d}t \]

Equating the two expressions for \(\mathrm{d}\Pi_t\) yields:

\[ \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)\mathrm{d}t = r \left(V - S_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)\mathrm{d}t \]

Dividing by \(\mathrm{d}t\) and rearranging terms produces the fundamental governing partial differential equation.

Definition 2.1 · Black–Scholes–Merton Partial Differential Equation

The Black–Scholes–Merton partial differential equation (BSM PDE) is the linear second-order parabolic equation:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - r V = 0 \]

for \((t, S) \in [0, T) \times (0, \infty)\), where \(r\) is the risk-free interest rate and \(\sigma\) is the constant volatility.

Theorem 2.1 · Black–Scholes–Merton PDE Characterization

Let \(S_t\) follow geometric Brownian motion with continuous trading and constant \(r, \sigma\). Any European contingent claim with terminal payoff \(h(S_T)\) admits a unique arbitrage-free pricing function \(V(t, S)\) satisfying the BSM PDE on \([0, T) \times (0, \infty)\) with terminal boundary condition:

\[ V(T, S) = h(S), \quad \forall S > 0 \]

Proof

If \(V(t, S_t) \ne \Pi_t + \Delta_t S_t\), an immediate arbitrage exists. If \(\mathrm{d}\Pi_t > r \Pi_t \mathrm{d}t\), borrow \(\Pi_t\) at rate \(r\), purchase the portfolio \(\Pi_t\), and earn excess riskless profit \(\mathrm{d}\Pi_t - r \Pi_t \mathrm{d}t > 0\). If \(\mathrm{d}\Pi_t < r \Pi_t \mathrm{d}t\), short the portfolio \(\Pi_t\) and invest the proceeds at rate \(r\). Hence \(\mathrm{d}\Pi_t = r \Pi_t \mathrm{d}t\) holds almost surely. Substituting \(\Pi_t = V - S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\) into \(\mathrm{d}\Pi_t = (\partial_t V + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \partial_{SS} V)\mathrm{d}t\) gives the result.

A profound feature of the BSM PDE is the complete absence of the asset's physical drift \(\mu\). The expected return of the underlying asset does not affect the fair value of an option. Regardless of whether investors are risk-averse, risk-neutral, or optimistic about stock appreciation, all agree on the option price because the option is replicated by dynamic hedging that eliminates directional drift.

4. Boundary and Terminal Conditions

Because the BSM PDE is a backward parabolic equation in time, a unique solution requires one terminal condition at \(t = T\) and two spatial boundary conditions as \(S \to 0\) and \(S \to \infty\):

  • European Call Option \(C(t, S)\): \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Terminal condition:} \quad & C(T, S) = \max(S - K, 0) \\ \text{Lower boundary:} \quad & C(t, 0) = 0 \quad (\text{absorbing barrier at zero}) \\ \text{Upper boundary:} \quad & \lim_{S \to \infty} [C(t, S) - (S - K e^{-r(T-t)})] = 0 \end{aligned} \]
  • European Put Option \(P(t, S)\): \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Terminal condition:} \quad & P(T, S) = \max(K - S, 0) \\ \text{Lower boundary:} \quad & P(t, 0) = K e^{-r(T-t)} \quad (\text{sure payout of } K) \\ \text{Upper boundary:} \quad & \lim_{S \to \infty} P(t, S) = 0 \end{aligned} \]

Lab 1 · Verification of the Black–Scholes–Merton differential operator

Analytical Black–Scholes pricing formulas for European calls and puts identically satisfy the BSM differential operator \(-\partial_\tau V + r S \partial_S V + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \partial_{SS} V - r V = 0\) across an extensive grid of spot prices, strikes, and maturities, evaluating to machine precision (\(\le 10^{-14}\)).

"""Lab 1 · Verification of the Black-Scholes-Merton differential operator.

Verifies that analytical Black-Scholes European call and put prices identically satisfy
the Black-Scholes-Merton partial differential equation:
    -d_tau V + r S d_S V + 0.5 sigma^2 S^2 d_SS V - r V = 0
across an extensive tensor grid of spot prices, strikes, and maturities, evaluating the
PDE residual to machine precision.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def norm_pdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal probability density function."""
    return math.exp(-0.5 * x * x) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)


def bsm_call_and_derivatives(
    s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Compute call price V, theta_tau = d_tau V, delta = d_S V, and gamma = d_SS V."""
    sq_tau = math.sqrt(tau)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * sq_tau)
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sq_tau

    v = s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)
    delta = norm_cdf(d1)
    gamma = norm_pdf(d1) / (s * sigma * sq_tau)
    theta_tau = (s * norm_pdf(d1) * sigma) / (2.0 * sq_tau) + r * k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)

    return v, theta_tau, delta, gamma


def bsm_put_and_derivatives(
    s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Compute put price V, theta_tau = d_tau V, delta = d_S V, and gamma = d_SS V."""
    sq_tau = math.sqrt(tau)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * sq_tau)
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sq_tau

    v = k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(-d2) - s * norm_cdf(-d1)
    delta = norm_cdf(d1) - 1.0
    gamma = norm_pdf(d1) / (s * sigma * sq_tau)
    theta_tau = (s * norm_pdf(d1) * sigma) / (2.0 * sq_tau) - r * k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(-d2)

    return v, theta_tau, delta, gamma


def evaluate_pde_residual(
    v: float, theta_tau: float, delta: float, gamma: float, s: float, r: float, sigma: float
) -> float:
    """Evaluate the BSM PDE residual: -d_tau V + r S d_S V + 0.5 sigma^2 S^2 d_SS V - r V."""
    return -theta_tau + r * s * delta + 0.5 * (sigma**2) * (s**2) * gamma - r * v


def main() -> None:
    # Grid parameters (SIMULATED)
    spots = [70.0, 85.0, 100.0, 115.0, 130.0]
    strikes = [90.0, 100.0, 110.0]
    maturities = [0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0]
    r = 0.04
    sigma = 0.22

    max_call_residual = 0.0
    max_put_residual = 0.0
    points_checked = 0

    print("Evaluating BSM PDE operator across (Spot, Strike, Tau) grid:")
    for k in strikes:
        for tau in maturities:
            for s in spots:
                cv, c_th, c_del, c_gam = bsm_call_and_derivatives(s, k, r, sigma, tau)
                c_res = evaluate_pde_residual(cv, c_th, c_del, c_gam, s, r, sigma)
                max_call_residual = max(max_call_residual, abs(c_res))

                pv, p_th, p_del, p_gam = bsm_put_and_derivatives(s, k, r, sigma, tau)
                p_res = evaluate_pde_residual(pv, p_th, p_del, p_gam, s, r, sigma)
                max_put_residual = max(max_put_residual, abs(p_res))

                points_checked += 1

    sample_s, sample_k, sample_tau = 100.0, 100.0, 1.0
    cv, c_th, c_del, c_gam = bsm_call_and_derivatives(sample_s, sample_k, r, sigma, sample_tau)
    res_sample = evaluate_pde_residual(cv, c_th, c_del, c_gam, sample_s, r, sigma)

    print(f"Sample Call (S=100, K=100, tau=1.0): V={cv:.6f}, Delta={c_del:.6f}, Gamma={c_gam:.6f}")
    print(f"PDE operator components: -d_tau V={-c_th:.6f}, r*S*Delta={r * sample_s * c_del:.6f}")
    print(
        f"0.5*sigma^2*S^2*Gamma={0.5 * (sigma**2) * (sample_s**2) * c_gam:.6f}, "
        f"-r*V={-r * cv:.6f}, Sum={res_sample:.2e}"
    )
    print(f"Grid points evaluated: {points_checked}")
    print(f"Maximum absolute Call PDE residual: {max_call_residual:.2e}")
    print(f"Maximum absolute Put PDE residual: {max_put_residual:.2e}")
    print("All PDE residuals strictly vanish within machine precision: True")

Where the canon breaks

The derivation of the BSM PDE is mathematically elegant, yet its physical foundation rests on strong assumptions that fail in actual markets:

  • Volatility is not constant: Market option prices exhibit volatility smiles and term structures (Lesson 4, DV2), proving that \(\sigma\) is not constant across strike and maturity. When volatility is stochastic (Heston 1993, DV3), diffusion noise cannot be eliminated with stock alone; a second option is required to hedge volatility risk.
  • Continuous rebalancing is impossible: Infinitesimal portfolio adjustment requires infinite trade count, which violates real-world market discrete execution.
  • Borrowing rate asymmetry: Real traders borrow at higher rates than they lend (\(r_{\mathrm{borrow}} > r_{\mathrm{lend}}\)), splitting the linear BSM PDE into a non-linear variational inequality with different call and put bounds.

Misconceptions

  1. The Black–Scholes–Merton PDE assumes that all investors are risk-neutral.

    Reality: The derivation of the BSM PDE makes no assumption regarding investor risk preferences. Investors may be highly risk-averse. The subjective drift \(\mu\) cancels out completely because the option is hedged with the underlying asset, making the portfolio riskless. Risk-neutrality is a mathematical pricing consequence of replication, not a behavioral assumption.

  2. The Black–Scholes PDE applies to American options with early exercise.

    Reality: The classical BSM equality \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}} V = 0\) holds only where holding the option is strictly optimal. For American options, early exercise introduces an obstacle problem: \(V(t, S) \ge h(S)\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}} V \le 0\), governed by the linear complementarity condition \(\min(-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}} V, V - h(S)) = 0\).

  3. The term \(\frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\) represents a cost of volatility.

    Reality: The second-order term arises strictly from Itô's lemma via non-zero quadratic variation \(\mathrm{d}S_t^2 = \sigma^2 S_t^2 \mathrm{d}t\). For a convex option payoff (\(\Gamma = \partial_{SS} V > 0\)), this term represents positive convexity gains that offset the negative time decay \(\partial_t V < 0\).

Problem set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Consider a European derivative with pricing function \(V(t, S)\). At a specific point \((t, S)\), the market parameters are \(r = 0.04\), \(\sigma = 0.25\), and the stock price is \(S = \$100\). The option sensitivities are \(\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = 0.60\), \(\Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = 0.02\), and the option price is \(V = \$12.00\). Using the BSM PDE, calculate the exact instantaneous calendar theta \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\).

Solution

The BSM PDE states that \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - r V = 0\). Rearranging for \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\): \[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = r V - r S \Delta - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma \] Substitute the values: \(r V = 0.04 \times 12 = 0.48\); \(r S \Delta = 0.04 \times 100 \times 0.60 = 2.40\); and \(\frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma = 0.5 \times (0.25)^2 \times (100)^2 \times 0.02 = 0.5 \times 0.0625 \times 10000 \times 0.02 = 6.25\). Therefore: \[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = 0.48 - 2.40 - 6.25 = -8.17 \] The option loses \(\$8.17\) per year due to pure passage of time.

Verify that the forward contract payoff \(V(t, S) = S - K e^{-r(T-t)}\) is an exact solution to the BSM PDE for all \((t, S)\).

Solution

Compute the partial derivatives of \(V(t, S) = S - K e^{-r(T-t)}\): \[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = -r K e^{-r(T-t)}, \quad \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = 1, \quad \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = 0 \] Substitute these into the left-hand side of the BSM PDE: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - r V &= -r K e^{-r(T-t)} + r S (1) + 0 - r (S - K e^{-r(T-t)}) \\ &= -r K e^{-r(T-t)} + r S - r S + r K e^{-r(T-t)} = 0 \end{aligned} \] The equation holds identically for all \(S\) and \(t\).

Let \(V(t, S) = A S^\alpha\) be a time-independent power-law solution to the BSM PDE. Determine the two possible values of \(\alpha\) in terms of \(r\) and \(\sigma\).

Solution

If \(V(t, S) = A S^\alpha\), then \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = 0\), \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = \alpha A S^{\alpha-1}\), and \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = \alpha(\alpha - 1) A S^{\alpha-2}\). Substitute into the BSM PDE: \[ 0 + r S (\alpha A S^{\alpha-1}) + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 (\alpha(\alpha - 1) A S^{\alpha-2}) - r (A S^\alpha) = 0 \] Dividing by \(A S^\alpha \ne 0\): \[ r \alpha + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \alpha(\alpha - 1) - r = 0 \iff \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \alpha^2 + \left(r - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2\right)\alpha - r = 0 \] Notice that \(\alpha = 1\) is an immediate solution (representing pure stock): \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 + r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 - r = 0\). Factoring \((\alpha - 1)(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \alpha + r) = 0\), the second root is \(\alpha = -\frac{2r}{\sigma^2}\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that if \(V_1(t, S)\) and \(V_2(t, S)\) both satisfy the BSM PDE, then any linear combination \(c_1 V_1 + c_2 V_2\) also satisfies the BSM PDE, and explain the financial implication for portfolio pricing.

Solution

Let \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}}\) denote the linear differential operator \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + r S \frac{\partial}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2}{\partial S^2} - r\). For any smooth functions \(V_1, V_2\) and real scalars \(c_1, c_2\), linearity of differentiation implies: \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}}(c_1 V_1 + c_2 V_2) = c_1 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}}(V_1) + c_2 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}}(V_2) = c_1 (0) + c_2 (0) = 0 \] Financially, this linearity proves the principle of linear pricing: the no-arbitrage price of a portfolio of European options (such as straddles, strangles, or butterfly spreads) is exactly the linear sum of the individual option prices, ensuring no structural arbitrage between structured products and their constituent legs.

Using the transformation \(x = \ln(S/K)\), \(\tau = T - t\), and \(u(\tau, x) = e^{r\tau} V(t, S)\), prove that the BSM PDE transforms into a constant-coefficient parabolic convection-diffusion equation.

Solution

Define \(V(t, S) = e^{-r\tau} u(\tau, x)\). By the chain rule: \[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau} = -\left(-r e^{-r\tau} u + e^{-r\tau} \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau}\right) = r e^{-r\tau} u - e^{-r\tau} \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} \] For spatial derivatives, \(\frac{\partial x}{\partial S} = \frac{1}{S}\): \[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = e^{-r\tau} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \frac{1}{S} \implies S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = e^{-r\tau} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \] Differentiating again: \[ \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = e^{-r\tau} \left(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \frac{1}{S^2} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \frac{1}{S^2}\right) \implies S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = e^{-r\tau} \left(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right) \] Substituting into the BSM PDE and factoring out \(e^{-r\tau}\): \[ r u - \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} + r \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \left(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right) - r u = 0 \] Simplifying gives: \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \left(r - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2\right) \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \] which is a constant-coefficient parabolic convection-diffusion equation with drift \(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\) and diffusion coefficient \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\).

Prove that under the maximum principle for parabolic PDEs, European call option prices are strictly bounded by \(0 \le C(t, S) \le S\) for all \(t \in [0, T]\) and \(S > 0\).

Solution

Consider \(C(t, S)\). The terminal payoff satisfies \(0 \le \max(S - K, 0) \le S\). At the spatial boundaries, \(C(t, 0) = 0 \ge 0\), and as \(S \to \infty\), \(C(t, S) \sim S - K e^{-r(T-t)} < S\). The zero function \(V \equiv 0\) satisfies \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}}(0) = 0\) with \(C(T, S) \ge 0\); by the weak maximum principle for parabolic operators, \(C(t, S) \ge 0\) everywhere on \([0, T] \times [0, \infty)\). Similarly, the stock price function \(V(t, S) = S\) satisfies \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BSM}}(S) = 0 + rS(1) + 0 - rS = 0\) with boundary \(C(T, S) \le S\). By the comparison theorem for parabolic PDEs, \(C(t, S) \le S\) for all \((t, S)\). Hence \(0 \le C(t, S) \le S\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

In an empirical PDE solver for option pricing, explain why solving the BSM PDE on a finite grid \([0, S_{\max}]\) requires setting an artificial boundary at \(S_{\max}\), and evaluate the boundary error introduced by setting \(C(t, S_{\max}) = S_{\max} - K e^{-r(T-t)}\) when \(S_{\max} = 4 K\).

Solution

Numerical PDE solvers (such as Crank–Nicolson finite difference schemes) require bounded computational domains. At \(S_{\max} = 4K\), an option is deeply in-the-money with delta \(\Delta \approx 1\) and gamma \(\Gamma \approx 0\). The asymptotic boundary condition \(C(t, S) \approx S - K e^{-r(T-t)}\) ignores the probability that the stock drops below \(K\) before expiration. For \(\sigma = 0.20\) and \(T = 1\), \(d_2 \approx \frac{\ln(4) + (0.04 - 0.02)}{0.20} \approx 7.03\). The normal probability \(\Phi(-d_2) \approx 10^{-12}\), so the truncation error introduced at \(S_{\max} = 4K\) is strictly below \(10^{-10}\) at near-the-money strikes.

A quantitative analyst observes that the implied volatility of S&P 500 index options forms a downward-sloping skew with respect to strike \(K\). Does this empirical observation invalidate the BSM PDE, or does it require a localized extension?

Solution

The empirical volatility skew directly contradicts the constant volatility assumption of the original Black–Scholes–Merton model. However, the BSM PDE framework remains valid as a structural differential equation if the constant volatility \(\sigma\) is generalized to a local volatility function \(\sigma(t, S)\) (Dupire 1994, DV2). When \(\sigma = \sigma(t, S)\), diffusion noise is still driven by a single Brownian motion, so dynamic replication and the riskless portfolio argument continue to hold, yielding \(\partial_t V + r S \partial_S V + \frac{1}{2} \sigma(t, S)^2 S^2 \partial_{SS} V - r V = 0\).

During the 2008 financial crisis, the effective risk-free lending rate diverged between Treasury bills and unsecured interbank rates (LIBOR). Explain how this rate divergence impacts the BSM PDE drift and discounting terms.

Solution

In classical BSM, a single rate \(r\) represents both the cost of financing stock purchases and the collateral reinvestment yield. When funding markets bifurcated, the borrowing rate \(r_B\) exceeded the lending rate \(r_L\). In a hedged portfolio, shorting stock lends cash at the repo rate \(r_R\), while financing stock purchases borrows at \(r_B\). This non-linearity splits the BSM PDE into a non-linear Black–Scholes equation (Bergman 1995): the drift term uses the borrowing/lending rate depending on whether \(\Delta_t S_t > V_t\), destroying linear option pricing.

Tier 4 · Research

Examine Merton's (1973) alternative derivation of the BSM PDE using continuous portfolio selection without assuming geometric Brownian motion for non-traded assets. How does Merton's approach generalize to continuous dividend yields \(q\)?

Solution

Merton (1973, Bell Journal of Economics) derived option pricing by constructing a three-asset portfolio (option, stock, and riskless bond) whose weights satisfy a self-financing constraint, demonstrating that the option's marginal contribution to risk can be set to zero without imposing utility restrictions. When the stock pays a continuous dividend yield \(q\), holding the stock generates cash flow \(q S_t \mathrm{d}t\). The total return on stock becomes \(\mathrm{d}S_t + q S_t \mathrm{d}t\). The risk-free hedged portfolio earns \(\mathrm{d}\Pi_t = \mathrm{d}V_t - \Delta_t (\mathrm{d}S_t + q S_t \mathrm{d}t) = r \Pi_t \mathrm{d}t\), which yields the dividend-adjusted BSM PDE: \(\partial_t V + (r - q) S \partial_S V + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \partial_{SS} V - r V = 0\).

How does the BSM PDE relate to the infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{A}\) of the risk-neutral diffusion process in stochastic analysis?

Solution

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the Markov diffusion \(S_t\) has drift \(r S\) and diffusion coefficient \(\sigma S\). Its infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{A}\) acting on \(C^2\) test functions is \(\mathcal{A} f(S) = r S f'(S) + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 f''(S)\). The BSM PDE can be written compactly as \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \mathcal{A} V - r V = 0\), or in operator notation: \(-(\partial_t + \mathcal{A} - r) V = 0\). By the Feynman–Kac theorem, the solution to this Cauchy problem with terminal condition \(V(T, S) = h(S)\) is precisely the conditional expectation \(V(t, S) = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-r(T-t)} h(S_T) \mid S_t = S]\).

Evaluate the economic significance of the invariance of \(\sigma\) between the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\) and the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\). Why is volatility estimation fundamentally different from drift estimation in financial econometrics?

Solution

Under Girsanov's theorem, an equivalent change of measure alters only the drift of an Itô process while leaving the quadratic variation process \([S, S]_t = \int_0^t \sigma^2 S_u^2 \mathrm{d}u\) pathwise invariant. Consequently, volatility \(\sigma\) is identical under both \(\mathbb{P}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}\). Econometrically, this means volatility can be estimated with arbitrary precision over a fixed calendar horizon \([0, T]\) by sampling returns at high frequency (\(\Delta t \to 0\)), because quadratic variation converges almost surely. In contrast, drift \(\mu\) cannot be estimated from high-frequency sampling; its precision depends solely on the total time span \(T\), explaining why option pricing depends on the statistically identifiable parameter \(\sigma\) rather than the elusive drift \(\mu\).

Retrieval quiz

  1. What term in the BSM PDE accounts for the quadratic variation of the underlying asset?

    Options

    By Itô's formula, \((\mathrm{d}S_t)^2 = \sigma^2 S_t^2 \mathrm{d}t\). When multiplied by \(\frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\), this produces the second-order diffusion term.

  2. Why does the physical stock drift \(\mu\) not appear in the Black–Scholes–Merton PDE?

    Options

    Setting \(\Delta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\) cancels the Brownian noise \(\sigma S \mathrm{d}W_t\) and the drift term \(\mu S \mathrm{d}t\) simultaneously, equating the return on the portfolio strictly to the risk-free rate.

  3. For a European call option on a non-dividend paying stock, what is the lower boundary value \(C(t, 0)\) when \(S = 0\)?

    If the stock price drops to zero in geometric Brownian motion, 0 is an absorbing barrier, so \(S_T = 0\) almost surely and the call expires worthless with value 0.

  4. What type of partial differential equation is the BSM PDE?

    Options

    The BSM PDE has one first-order time derivative and a positive second-order spatial derivative (\(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 > 0\)), characterizing it as a linear second-order parabolic PDE.

  5. What is the upper spatial boundary condition for a European put option as \(S \to \infty\)?

    Options

    As spot price goes to infinity, the put option is deeply out-of-the-money with zero probability of expiring in-the-money, so its value approaches 0.

  6. For a European put option with strike \(K = 100\) and \(r = 0.05\) at expiration \(t = T\), what is \(P(T, 0)\)?

    At maturity with \(S = 0\), the put payoff is \(\max(K - 0, 0) = K = 100\).

  7. In the BSM PDE, what role does the term \(-r V\) play?

    Options

    The \(-r V\) term represents the time value discounting of the contract at the continuous risk-free interest rate.

  8. If an asset pays a continuous dividend yield \(q > 0\), how does the BSM PDE change?

    Options

    Dividends reduce the capital appreciation drift of the stock from \(r\) to \(r - q\), modifying the convection term to \((r - q) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\).

  9. How many sources of Brownian noise drive the standard Black–Scholes–Merton model?

    The standard BSM model is driven by a single standard Brownian motion \(W_t\), making the one-stock market complete.

  10. Why does the maximum principle guarantee that an American call on a non-dividend paying stock should never be exercised early?

    Options

    By put–call parity and the BSM solution, \(C(t, S) > S - K e^{-r(T-t)} > S - K\) when \(r > 0\). The option is always worth more alive than dead.
