Continuous-Time Trading & Dynamic Replication

Modern derivative pricing rests on the insight that an option is not a speculative bet on the future, but a redundant contract whose cash flows can be synthesized exactly by dynamic trading. In discrete time, multi-period replication requires branching trees; in continuous time, stochastic calculus provides an exact self-financing condition where infinitesimal portfolio rebalancing eliminates directional diffusion risk without requiring external cash injections. Understanding dynamic replication transforms option pricing from subjective risk preference modeling into rigorous mathematical arbitrage bounds.

  • Introduces: dynamic replication
  • Prerequisites: arbitrage opportunity, continuous-time martingale, Itô integral, Itô's lemma, geometric Brownian motion

1. Continuous-Time Trading Strategies

Fix a filtered probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \mathbb{P})\) satisfying the usual conditions of completeness and right-continuity. Consider a financial market consisting of two primary traded assets available for continuous investment over the finite time horizon \([0, T]\):

  1. A riskless money-market account (bond numeraire) \(B_t\) satisfying the deterministic differential equation: \[ \mathrm{d}B_t = r_t B_t \mathrm{d}t, \quad B_0 = 1, \quad B_t = \exp\left(\int_0^t r_u \mathrm{d}u\right) \] where \(r_t\) is an adapted, bounded instantaneous risk-free interest rate process. For notational clarity, unless stated otherwise, \(r_t \equiv r\) is taken to be constant.
  2. A risky stock \(S_t\) whose price dynamics follow an Itô diffusion: \[ \mathrm{d}S_t = \mu_t S_t \mathrm{d}t + \sigma_t S_t \mathrm{d}W_t \] where \(W_t\) is a standard one-dimensional \(\mathbb{P}\)-Brownian motion, \(\mu_t\) is the adapted drift rate, and \(\sigma_t > 0\) is the strictly positive adapted volatility process.

Definition 1.1 · Trading Strategy

A trading strategy (or dynamic portfolio) is a bivariate stochastic process \(\phi = (\Delta_t, \eta_t)_{t \in [0, T]}\) where:

  • \(\Delta_t\) represents the number of shares of the risky asset \(S_t\) held at time \(t\);
  • \(\eta_t\) represents the number of units of the riskless bond \(B_t\) held at time \(t\).

Both \(\Delta_t\) and \(\eta_t\) are assumed to be \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-predictable processes satisfying the integrability conditions \(\int_0^T \Delta_t^2 \sigma_t^2 S_t^2 \mathrm{d}t < \infty\) almost surely and \(\int_0^T |\eta_t| B_t r_t \mathrm{d}t < \infty\) almost surely.

The total liquidated accounting value of the portfolio at any time \(t \in [0, T]\) is given by the linear combination:

\[ V_t(\phi) = \Delta_t S_t + \eta_t B_t \]

2. The Self-Financing Condition in Itô Calculus

In discrete time with trading dates \(0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_N = T\), a portfolio is self-financing if between dates \(t_{k-1}\) and \(t_k\), the change in portfolio value arises solely from capital gains and interest accrual on existing holdings, without external capital injections or withdrawals:

\[ V_{t_k} - V_{t_{k-1}} = \Delta_{t_{k-1}} (S_{t_k} - S_{t_{k-1}}) + \eta_{t_{k-1}} (B_{t_k} - B_{t_{k-1}}) \]

Taking the continuous-time limit requires careful formulation within stochastic calculus. Naive differentiation of \(V_t = \Delta_t S_t + \eta_t B_t\) would yield \(\mathrm{d}V_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t + S_t \mathrm{d}\Delta_t + \mathrm{d}\langle \Delta, S \rangle_t + \eta_t \mathrm{d}B_t + B_t \mathrm{d}\eta_t\). The self-financing principle dictates that the terms representing portfolio rebalancing trades, \(S_t \mathrm{d}\Delta_t + \mathrm{d}\langle \Delta, S \rangle_t + B_t \mathrm{d}\eta_t\), must identically sum to zero at all times.

Definition 1.2 · Self-Financing Portfolio

A trading strategy \(\phi = (\Delta_t, \eta_t)_{t \in [0, T]}\) is self-financing if its total value process \(V_t = \Delta_t S_t + \eta_t B_t\) satisfies the stochastic differential equation:

\[ \mathrm{d}V_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t + \eta_t \mathrm{d}B_t \]

Equivalently, in integral form:

\[ V_t = V_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_u \mathrm{d}S_u + \int_0^t \eta_u \mathrm{d}B_u = V_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_u \mathrm{d}S_u + \int_0^t r_u (V_u - \Delta_u S_u) \mathrm{d}u \]

Notice that the bond holding \(\eta_t\) can be eliminated by substituting \(\eta_t B_t = V_t - \Delta_t S_t\). Hence, a continuous-time self-financing strategy is completely determined by its initial wealth \(V_0\) and its stock allocation process \(\Delta_t\):

\[ \mathrm{d}V_t = r V_t \mathrm{d}t + \Delta_t (\mathrm{d}S_t - r S_t \mathrm{d}t) \]

Defining the discounted processes \(\tilde{S}_t = e^{-rt} S_t\) and \(\tilde{V}_t = e^{-rt} V_t\), Itô's product rule demonstrates that discounting absorbs the risk-free rate:

\[ \mathrm{d}\tilde{V}_t = \mathrm{d}(e^{-rt} V_t) = -r e^{-rt} V_t \mathrm{d}t + e^{-rt} \mathrm{d}V_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}\tilde{S}_t \]

Under any equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) where \(\tilde{S}_t\) is a martingale, the discounted portfolio value \(\tilde{V}_t\) is automatically a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-local martingale.

3. Dynamic Replication of Contingent Claims

A European contingent claim with maturity \(T\) is an \(\mathcal{F}_T\)-measurable random variable \(\mathcal{X} \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_T, \mathbb{P})\). Examples include the European call payoff \(\mathcal{X} = \max(S_T - K, 0)\) and European put payoff \(\mathcal{X} = \max(K - S_T, 0)\).

Definition 1.3 · Dynamic Replication

Dynamic replication is the construction of an admissible self-financing trading strategy \(\phi = (\Delta_t, \eta_t)_{t \in [0, T]}\) whose terminal portfolio value matches the contingent claim almost surely:

\[ V_T(\phi) = \mathcal{X}, \quad \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.} \]

The strategy \(\phi\) is called a replicating strategy (or synthetic hedge) for \(\mathcal{X}\), and \(V_0(\phi)\) is its unique no-arbitrage price at \(t = 0\).

Theorem 1.1 · Law of One Price and Replication Uniqueness

If a contingent claim \(\mathcal{X}\) admits a dynamic replicating strategy \(\phi = (\Delta_t, \eta_t)\) such that \(V_T(\phi) = \mathcal{X}\), then in the absence of arbitrage opportunities, the market price of \(\mathcal{X}\) at any time \(t \in [0, T]\) must equal \(V_t(\phi)\).

Proof

Suppose the market price \(C_t\) of claim \(\mathcal{X}\) at time \(t\) differs from \(V_t(\phi)\). We proceed by construction of an arbitrage portfolio.

Case 1: \(C_t > V_t(\phi)\). At time \(t\), sell one unit of the contingent claim for \(C_t\) and establish the replicating portfolio \(\phi\) at cost \(V_t(\phi)\). Invest the positive difference \(C_t - V_t(\phi) > 0\) in the riskless cash account. At maturity \(T\), the cash account has grown to \((C_t - V_t(\phi)) e^{r(T-t)} > 0\). The replicating portfolio delivers \(V_T(\phi)\), which by definition exactly covers the obligation \(\mathcal{X}\). The net terminal cash flow is \((C_t - V_t(\phi)) e^{r(T-t)} > 0\) with zero probability of loss, constituting an arbitrage opportunity.

Case 2: \(C_t < V_t(\phi)\). At time \(t\), buy the contingent claim for \(C_t\) and short the replicating strategy \(\phi\), generating cash \(V_t(\phi)\). Deposit \(V_t(\phi) - C_t > 0\) in the riskless bond. At maturity \(T\), the payoff \(\mathcal{X}\) received from the claim settles the liability \(V_T(\phi) = \mathcal{X}\). The remaining cash grows to \((V_t(\phi) - C_t) e^{r(T-t)} > 0\) with certainty.

Both cases generate riskless positive wealth from zero net initial investment. Hence, no-arbitrage requires \(C_t = V_t(\phi)\) almost surely.

In the seminal contributions of Black & Scholes (1973) and Merton (1973), the existence of such a replicating strategy was demonstrated by constructing an instantaneously risk-free hedged portfolio. In modern mathematical finance, market completeness follows from the martingale representation theorem (SC3), which ensures that every square-integrable \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale can be represented as a stochastic integral against the Brownian motion driving the underlying asset.

Lab 1 · Verification of discrete self-financing dynamic replication convergence

Along a fixed underlying simulated asset trajectory generated on a fine grid (\(N_{\mathrm{fine}} = 1000\)), we evaluate self-financing replication portfolios across rebalancing frequencies \(N \in \{10, 50, 200, 1000\}\). As rebalancing frequency increases along the identical realized trajectory, the replication error \(|V_T - \text{Payoff}|\) converges toward zero.

"""Lab 1 · Continuous-time self-financing portfolio and dynamic replication.

Verifies the discrete approximation of a continuous-time self-financing replicating portfolio
for a European call option. Along a fixed underlying simulated asset path generated at a fine grid
(N_fine = 1000), we evaluate rebalancing frequencies N in {10, 50, 250, 1000}. As rebalancing
frequency increases along the identical realized trajectory, the replication error |V_T - Payoff|
vanishes.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def bs_call_price(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> float:
    """Black-Scholes European call option analytical price."""
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return max(s - k, 0.0)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * math.sqrt(tau))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * math.sqrt(tau)
    return s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)


def bs_call_delta(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> float:
    """Black-Scholes European call delta."""
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        return 1.0 if s > k else 0.0
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * tau) / (sigma * math.sqrt(tau))
    return norm_cdf(d1)


def generate_fine_path(
    s0: float,
    r: float,
    sigma: float,
    maturity: float,
    n_fine: int,
    seed: int,
) -> list[float]:
    """Generate a single fine-grid Geometric Brownian motion path."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    dt = maturity / n_fine
    drift = (r - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt
    vol = sigma * math.sqrt(dt)

    path = [s0]
    s = s0
    for _ in range(n_fine):
        z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        s = s * math.exp(drift + vol * z)
        path.append(s)
    return path


def replicate_on_path(
    path: list[float],
    k: float,
    r: float,
    sigma: float,
    maturity: float,
    n_rebalance: int,
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Simulate self-financing replication on a subsampled trajectory."""
    n_fine = len(path) - 1
    stride = n_fine // n_rebalance
    dt = maturity / n_rebalance

    s0 = path[0]
    v0 = bs_call_price(s0, k, r, sigma, maturity)
    delta = bs_call_delta(s0, k, r, sigma, maturity)
    cash = v0 - delta * s0

    for step in range(1, n_rebalance + 1):
        fine_idx = step * stride
        s_next = path[fine_idx]

        # Cash accrues interest
        cash = cash * math.exp(r * dt)

        # Portfolio value before rebalancing
        portfolio_val = delta * s_next + cash

        # New hedge ratio for remaining time
        tau_remaining = max(maturity - step * dt, 1e-12)
        delta_new = bs_call_delta(s_next, k, r, sigma, tau_remaining)

        # Self-financing rebalance
        cash = portfolio_val - delta_new * s_next
        delta = delta_new

    s_t = path[-1]
    payoff = max(s_t - k, 0.0)
    terminal_portfolio = delta * s_t + cash
    return s_t, payoff, terminal_portfolio


def main() -> None:
    # Market parameters (SIMULATED)
    s0 = 100.0
    k = 100.0
    r = 0.05
    sigma = 0.20
    maturity = 1.0
    seed = 42
    n_fine = 1000

    path = generate_fine_path(s0, r, sigma, maturity, n_fine, seed)
    s_t = path[-1]
    payoff = max(s_t - k, 0.0)

    print(f"Underlying terminal price S_T = {s_t:.4f}, European call payoff = {payoff:.4f}")
    print("Replication convergence along fixed simulated path:")
    for n in [10, 50, 200, 1000]:
        _, _, v_t = replicate_on_path(path, k, r, sigma, maturity, n)
        err = abs(v_t - payoff)
        print(f"N = {n:4d} | Portfolio V_T = {v_t:8.4f} | Absolute Error = {err:8.4f}")

Where the canon breaks

Continuous-time dynamic replication provides the theoretical bedrock of modern derivatives pricing, yet its mathematical assumptions break down under empirical market conditions:

  • Continuous trading is physically impossible: Continuous rebalancing implies trading with infinite variation of order flow. In real markets, trading occurs on discrete millisecond clocks and order books. As shown in Lesson 6, discrete rebalancing introduces inescapable residual hedging risk of order \(O(\sqrt{\Delta t})\).
  • Transaction costs explode under continuous rebalancing: In the presence of proportional bid-ask spreads or exchange fees, the total turnover of a continuously rebalanced delta hedge is proportional to total variation of Brownian motion, which is infinite: \(\int_0^T |\mathrm{d}\Delta_t| = \infty\). As analyzed in Lesson 8, transaction costs render exact continuous replication economically infeasible.
  • Discontinuous jumps violate market completeness: If the underlying asset exhibits discontinuous price jumps (Poisson jumps or Lévy processes), a single stock and cash account cannot span all state transitions. The market becomes incomplete, and unique arbitrage-free dynamic replication ceases to exist.
  • Borrowing and short-selling constraints: Dynamic replication assumes unconstrained borrowing at the risk-free rate \(r\) and frictionless short selling of stock. Real prime brokerage introduces borrowing spreads, margin requirements, short locates, and hard-to-borrow fees.

Misconceptions

  1. Dynamic replication means an option trader must predict whether the stock will rise or fall.

    Reality: Dynamic replication is strictly non-directional. The hedge ratio \(\Delta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\) depends only on the current stock price, time to maturity, interest rate, and volatility. The physical drift \(\mu\) of the stock does not enter the replication formula or the option price, because the replicating portfolio holds shares to cancel instantaneous directional exposure regardless of drift direction.

  2. A self-financing portfolio cannot incur losses because no money is added or removed after time 0.

    Reality: "Self-financing" means that changes in total portfolio value are governed entirely by market price movements and interest accrual without external capital deposits or withdrawals: \(\mathrm{d}V_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t + \eta_t \mathrm{d}B_t\). If the underlying stock drops while holding positive shares \(\Delta_t > 0\), the portfolio value \(V_t\) declines. Self-financing defines an accounting flow constraint, not a capital guarantee.

  3. Static replication and dynamic replication are identical concepts.

    Reality: Static replication establishes a portfolio of buy-and-hold contracts at time 0 that requires no subsequent rebalancing before expiration (for example, replicating a forward contract with stock and zero-coupon bond, or Carr–Madan variance swap replication using a continuum of static OTM options). Dynamic replication requires continuous adjustment of asset holdings over the life of the trade. If rebalancing ceases, dynamic replication immediately fails.

Problem set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A trader holds a portfolio of 500 shares of stock currently trading at \(\$100\) and has borrowed \(\$30,000\) from the cash account at a constant interest rate of \(r = 0.04\). The portfolio has just been rebalanced to hedge 1,000 European call options. Calculate the initial portfolio value \(V_0\) and determine the cash balance \(B_{\Delta t}\) after one week (\(\Delta t = 1/52\) year) if no trading occurs during that week.

Solution

The initial portfolio value is \(V_0 = \Delta_0 S_0 + B_0 = 500 \times 100 + (-30000) = 50000 - 30000 = \$20000\). The cash account accumulates interest continuously at rate \(r = 0.04\). After one week (\(\Delta t = 1/52 \approx 0.01923\) year), the borrowed cash balance becomes \(B_{\Delta t} = B_0 \exp(r \Delta t) = -30000 \times \exp(0.04 / 52) = -30000 \times 1.0007695 = -\$30023.09\). The interest cost incurred over the week is \(\$23.09\).

A self-financing portfolio holds \(\Delta_t = 0.65\) shares of stock trading at \(S_t = \$80\) and cash \(B_t = -\$32\). The stock price moves infinitesimally by \(\mathrm{d}S_t = +\$1.50\). The risk-free rate is \(r = 0.05\) over a time interval \(\mathrm{d}t = 0.01\) year. Compute the exact change in portfolio value \(\mathrm{d}V_t\).

Solution

By the self-financing differential condition, \(\mathrm{d}V_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t + r B_t \mathrm{d}t\). Substituting the given values: \(\Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t = 0.65 \times 1.50 = \$0.975\). The interest on the borrowed cash is \(r B_t \mathrm{d}t = 0.05 \times (-32) \times 0.01 = -\$0.016\). Therefore, the net change in portfolio value is \(\mathrm{d}V_t = 0.975 - 0.016 = \$0.959\).

Let \(V_t\) be a self-financing replicating portfolio for a claim \(\mathcal{X}\). Express the number of units of bond numeraire \(\eta_t\) as an explicit function of \(V_t\), the stock holding \(\Delta_t\), stock price \(S_t\), and interest rate \(r\).

Solution

The total portfolio value definition is \(V_t = \Delta_t S_t + \eta_t B_t\), where \(B_t = \exp(rt)\). Rearranging this linear accounting identity directly gives \(\eta_t B_t = V_t - \Delta_t S_t\). Dividing by the bond price \(B_t\) yields \(\eta_t = (V_t - \Delta_t S_t) e^{-rt}\). Hence, the quantity of bond units held is simply the discounted cash position required to finance the difference between total portfolio wealth and stock inventory.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that if a trading strategy \(\phi = (\Delta_t, \eta_t)\) is self-financing, then the discounted portfolio value process \(\tilde{V}_t = e^{-rt} V_t\) satisfies \(\mathrm{d}\tilde{V}_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}\tilde{S}_t\), where \(\tilde{S}_t = e^{-rt} S_t\).

Solution

By Itô's product formula applied to the scalar product of the deterministic function \(f(t) = e^{-rt}\) and the Itô process \(V_t\): \[ \mathrm{d}\tilde{V}_t = \mathrm{d}(e^{-rt} V_t) = -r e^{-rt} V_t \mathrm{d}t + e^{-rt} \mathrm{d}V_t + \mathrm{d}\langle e^{-rt}, V \rangle_t \] Since \(e^{-rt}\) is of finite variation, the quadratic covariation vanishes: \(\mathrm{d}\langle e^{-rt}, V \rangle_t = 0\). Using the self-financing condition \(\mathrm{d}V_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t + \eta_t \mathrm{d}B_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t + r (V_t - \Delta_t S_t) \mathrm{d}t\), we substitute: \[ \mathrm{d}\tilde{V}_t = -r e^{-rt} V_t \mathrm{d}t + e^{-rt} [\Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t + r V_t \mathrm{d}t - r \Delta_t S_t \mathrm{d}t] = \Delta_t [e^{-rt} \mathrm{d}S_t - r e^{-rt} S_t \mathrm{d}t] \] Now apply Itô's formula to the discounted stock price \(\tilde{S}_t = e^{-rt} S_t\): \(\mathrm{d}\tilde{S}_t = -r e^{-rt} S_t \mathrm{d}t + e^{-rt} \mathrm{d}S_t\). Substituting this into the brackets yields \(\mathrm{d}\tilde{V}_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}\tilde{S}_t\), completing the proof.

Prove that under any equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) where \(\tilde{S}_t\) is a martingale, every admissible self-financing portfolio \(\tilde{V}_t\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale, and therefore satisfies the no-arbitrage pricing representation \(V_0 = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rT} V_T]\).

Solution

From Problem P1, the discounted portfolio satisfies \(\tilde{V}_t = V_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_u \mathrm{d}\tilde{S}_u\). Under the equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the discounted stock price \(\tilde{S}_t\) is an Itô diffusion with zero drift: \(\mathrm{d}\tilde{S}_t = \sigma_t \tilde{S}_t \mathrm{d}W_t^\mathbb{Q}\). Hence, \(\tilde{V}_t = V_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_u \sigma_u \tilde{S}_u \mathrm{d}W_u^\mathbb{Q}\) is a continuous local martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\). For an admissible strategy, the admissibility condition implies that \(\tilde{V}_t\) is bounded from below, making it a true \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale. By the martingale property: \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\tilde{V}_T \mid \mathcal{F}_0] = \tilde{V}_0 = V_0\). Substituting \(\tilde{V}_T = e^{-rT} V_T\) gives \(V_0 = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rT} V_T]\).

Prove that dynamic replication is incompatible with doubling strategies (suicide strategies) when trading strategies are restricted to admissible classes where \(\tilde{V}_t \ge -M\) almost surely for some constant \(M < \infty\).

Solution

A classic Harrison–Kreps doubling strategy in continuous time operates by betting at rapidly increasing leverage as \(t \to T\), generating a sure profit of 1 from zero initial wealth. However, the portfolio value along the losing trajectory reaches arbitrarily large negative numbers: \(\inf_{t \in [0, T)} V_t = -\infty\). By imposing the admissibility constraint that the discounted wealth process is uniformly bounded from below by \(-M\), Fatou's lemma applies to the local martingale \(\tilde{V}_t + M \ge 0\), establishing that \(\tilde{V}_t + M\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-supermartingale. Consequently, \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\tilde{V}_T] \le \tilde{V}_0 = 0\). A strategy with \(V_T \ge 0\) almost surely must therefore satisfy \(V_T = 0\) almost surely, ruling out arbitrage doubling strategies.

Tier 3 · Empirical

An empirical options desk runs a discrete replicating strategy for 100,000 European call options with \(\Delta = 0.50\). Over the trading day, the stock price moves from \(\$150.00\) to \(\$153.00\), and the delta increases to \(0.55\). If the desk rebalances once at end-of-day versus twice intraday (when stock is \(\$151.50\) and delta is \(0.525\)), calculate the difference in stock purchase cash flows.

Solution

For 100,000 options, the delta in shares increases from \(50,000\) to \(55,000\) shares (+5,000 shares purchased). Under once-daily rebalancing at end-of-day, the desk buys 5,000 shares at \(\$153.00\), spending \(5000 \times 153.00 = \$765,000\). Under twice-daily rebalancing, the desk buys 2,500 shares at \(\$151.50\) spending \(2500 \times 151.50 = \$378,750\), and then buys 2,500 shares at \(\$153.00\) spending \(2500 \times 153.00 = \$382,500\). Total twice-daily expenditure is \(378,750 + 382,500 = \$761,250\). The intraday rebalancing saves \(\$765,000 - \$761,250 = \$3,750\) in acquisition cost due to buying earlier on the upward path.

Explain how bid-ask bounce in underlying stock quotes creates artificial turnover in an empirical automated delta-hedging algorithm, and describe how setting a minimum delta rebalancing threshold \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{rebal}}\) mitigates this cost.

Solution

At high frequencies, order flow fluctuates between trades at the bid and ask price without changes in fundamental valuation. If a hedging engine updates delta continuously on every tick, bid-ask bounce induces artificial oscillation in the computed delta \(\Delta_t\). The engine continually buys on ask upticks and sells on bid downticks, accumulating execution slippage and round-trip spread losses. Implementing a deadband \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{rebal}}\) (trading only when \(|\Delta_{\mathrm{current}} - \Delta_{\mathrm{held}}| > \epsilon_{\mathrm{rebal}}\)) filters out high-frequency bid-ask oscillations, substantially reducing trade count and cumulative transaction costs at the expense of a bounded tracking error.

In an empirical backtest of dynamic call replication over 252 trading days, the tracking error at maturity is \(\$1.20\) per contract on an option with initial price \(\$8.50\). State three distinct real-world market imperfections that could generate this replication discrepancy.

Solution

Three key real-world imperfections: (1) Discrete-time rebalancing: market rebalancing occurred daily rather than continuously, incurring convexity tracking error proportional to realized daily Gamma and squared returns; (2) Volatility mismatch: the delta hedge was executed using an estimated or implied volatility that differed from the actual pathwise realized volatility over the year; (3) Financing and execution friction: borrowing and lending rates diverged due to prime brokerage spreads, and stock execution incurred exchange fees and bid-ask spreads.

Tier 4 · Research

Contrast the continuous-time dynamic replication framework of Black–Scholes–Merton with the deep hedging approach of Buehler et al. (2019) under convex risk measures and market frictions.

Solution

The classical Black–Scholes–Merton dynamic replication framework assumes complete markets, frictionless continuous trading, and geometric Brownian motion, where no-arbitrage guarantees a unique hedging strategy that eliminates risk entirely. In contrast, Buehler et al. (2019, Quantitative Finance) formulate deep hedging as a machine learning optimization problem under convex risk measures (such as Conditional Value-at-Risk or entropic utility) in incomplete markets. Deep hedging parameterizes the trading policy \(\Delta_t = F_\theta(t, S_t, \dots)\) via deep neural networks, directly optimizing the trade-off between hedging error and transaction costs on discrete historical or simulated paths without requiring market completeness or analytical tractability.

How does market illiquidity and permanent price impact alter the self-financing equation? Formulate the nonlinear extension of \(\mathrm{d}V_t\) when purchasing \(\mathrm{d}\Delta_t\) shares shifts the execution price by \(\lambda \mathrm{d}\Delta_t\).

Solution

In the presence of permanent linear price impact, executing a trade of \(\mathrm{d}\Delta_t\) shares shifts the effective market price to \(S_t + \lambda \mathrm{d}\Delta_t\). The execution cost of rebalancing is \((S_t + \lambda \mathrm{d}\Delta_t)\mathrm{d}\Delta_t = S_t \mathrm{d}\Delta_t + \lambda (\mathrm{d}\Delta_t)^2\). When \(\Delta_t\) is an Itô diffusion \(\mathrm{d}\Delta_t = a_t \mathrm{d}t + b_t \mathrm{d}W_t\), the quadratic variation of the trading strategy \((\mathrm{d}\Delta_t)^2 = b_t^2 \mathrm{d}t\) generates an unavoidable finite-rate liquidity drain: \(\mathrm{d}V_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t + r(V_t - \Delta_t S_t)\mathrm{d}t - \lambda b_t^2 \mathrm{d}t\). This non-linear feedback transforms the linear BSM equation into a fully non-linear Black–Scholes PDE with price impact (as in Almgren 2003 or Schönbucher & Wilmott 2000).

Evaluate the economic debate over whether options are truly redundant assets. If dynamic replication is feasible, why do options markets exhibit massive independent trading volume and open interest?

Solution

In frictionless theory, options are strictly redundant because their payoffs can be synthesized dynamically. In real financial markets, options trade actively for three fundamental reasons: (1) Incomplete markets and jump risk: continuous replication fails during market crashes, so options provide non-linear insurance against unhedgeable discontinuous tail events; (2) Friction efficiency: managing a static option position avoids the prohibitive transaction costs, operational monitoring, and margin calls of continuous rebalancing; (3) Volatility trading and information revelation: option surfaces allow investors to trade variance and volatility risk premiums directly without delta exposure, revealing market expectations of future turbulence.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What does the continuous-time self-financing condition \(\mathrm{d}V_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}S_t + \eta_t \mathrm{d}B_t\) signify?

    Options

    A self-financing strategy requires that changes in portfolio wealth come entirely from capital gains and interest on existing holdings, meaning no cash is deposited or withdrawn.

  2. Why does discounting by \(e^{-rt}\) simplify the self-financing equation to \(\mathrm{d}\tilde{V}_t = \Delta_t \mathrm{d}\tilde{S}_t\)?

    Options

    Discounting by the money market numeraire removes the drift due to interest accrual, equating changes in discounted portfolio value directly to the integral of delta against discounted stock increments.

  3. In a complete, frictionless continuous-time market, what is the theoretical replication tracking error \(V_T - \mathcal{X}\) at maturity?

    In frictionless continuous time, dynamic replication is exact: the terminal portfolio value matches the contingent claim payoff almost surely, resulting in zero tracking error.

  4. What prevents an investor from generating riskless profits via doubling strategies in continuous time?

    Options

    Standard arbitrage theory requires strategies to be admissible (\(V_t \ge -M\)), which rules out suicidal doubling strategies that require unbounded borrowing.

  5. If the market price of an option is \(C_t > V_t(\phi)\) where \(\phi\) replicates the option, what arbitrage trade exploits this mispricing?

    Options

    Selling the overpriced option and synthesizing it cheaply with the replicating portfolio locks in the positive difference \(C_t - V_t > 0\) as riskless profit.

  6. If a portfolio has \(\$50\) in stock and \(-\$30\) in cash, what is its total liquidated value \(V_t\)?

    Total liquidated accounting value is simply stock value plus cash: \(50 + (-30) = \$20\).

  7. Why does dynamic replication fail in the presence of discontinuous jump diffusions?

    Options

    Jumps introduce multiple degrees of freedom in price transitions that cannot be cancelled by adjusting a single stock allocation, creating market incompleteness.

  8. In Carr–Madan static replication, what financial instrument serves as the numeraire for terminal discounting?

    The zero-coupon bond maturing at \(T\) with face value 1 serves as the static discounting numeraire.

  9. Under the self-financing condition, what must be true of the trading turnover terms \(S_t \mathrm{d}\Delta_t + \mathrm{d}\langle \Delta, S \rangle_t + B_t \mathrm{d}\eta_t\)?

    Options

    The self-financing property requires that rebalancing trades incur zero net cash inflow or outflow, so the trade terms sum to zero identically.
