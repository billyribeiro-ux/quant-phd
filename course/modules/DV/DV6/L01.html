Path-Dependent Options: Barriers, Rebates & Analytical Symmetries

Vanilla European options price payoffs contingent solely on the terminal asset price. Barrier options introduce path-dependency by activating or extinguishing payoffs upon touching a price boundary. Understanding their mathematical structure unlocks exact reflection symmetries, reveals discrete monitoring biases, and exposes the severe gamma instabilities that challenge quantitative option desks.

  • Introduces: barrier-option
  • Prerequisites: black-scholes-formula, put-call-parity, delta-hedging, option-greeks

1. Barrier Option Payoffs, In-Out Parity & Classification

A standard European option depends strictly on terminal spot \(S_T\). In contrast, path-dependent contracts introduce stopping times associated with the first passage of the underlying asset through an agreed boundary.

Definition 1.1 (Barrier Option). A barrier option is an option contract whose terminal payoff is conditional upon whether the underlying asset price path \(\{S_t\}_{0 \le t \le T}\) breaches a predetermined barrier level \(H > 0\) during the option's lifetime. The contract is classified by direction (up or down) and activation mechanism (knock-in or knock-out):

  • Down-and-out: Extinguished if \(\min_{0 \le t \le T} S_t \le H\) for \(H < S_0\).
  • Down-and-in: Activated only if \(\min_{0 \le t \le T} S_t \le H\) for \(H < S_0\).
  • Up-and-out: Extinguished if \(\max_{0 \le t \le T} S_t \ge H\) for \(H > S_0\).
  • Up-and-in: Activated only if \(\max_{0 \le t \le T} S_t \ge H\) for \(H > S_0\).

When a knock-out contract hits its barrier, it terminates either with zero payoff or by paying a predetermined contractual cash rebate \(R\) upon breach or at maturity \(T\).

Theorem 1.1 (In-Out Barrier Parity). Let \(V_{\text{in}}\) and \(V_{\text{out}}\) denote the market prices of an otherwise identical knock-in and knock-out option with zero rebate, identical strike \(K\), barrier \(H\), and expiration \(T\). Then on every sample path \(\omega\), and hence under any no-arbitrage pricing model:

\[ V_{\text{in}} + V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{vanilla}} \]

Proof. Let \(\tau_H = \inf\{t \ge 0 : S_t = H\}\) be the first hitting time of the barrier \(H\). For any sample path \(\omega \in \Omega\), exactly one of two mutually exclusive events occurs: either \(\tau_H(\omega) \le T\) or \(\tau_H(\omega) > T\). If \(\tau_H(\omega) \le T\), the knock-in option pays \(g(S_T(\omega))\) and the knock-out option pays 0. If \(\tau_H(\omega) > T\), the knock-in option pays 0 and the knock-out option pays \(g(S_T(\omega))\). Therefore, for every path \(\omega\):

\[ V_{\text{in}}(\omega) + V_{\text{out}}(\omega) = g(S_T(\omega)) \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_H \le T\}} + g(S_T(\omega)) \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_H > T\}} = g(S_T(\omega)) \]

Taking the conditional expectation under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) discounted by \(e^{-r T}\) establishes the exact equality \(V_{\text{in}} + V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{vanilla}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.1 (In-Out Parity in Action). Consider a 1-year European call on a stock with \(S_0 = 100\), strike \(K = 100\), risk-free rate \(r = 4\%\), dividend yield \(q = 1\%\), and volatility \(\sigma = 25\%\). The standard Black–Scholes European call trades at \(\$11.2356\). For a lower barrier \(H = 85\), an analytical down-and-out call prices at \(\$10.0388\). By in-out parity, the price of an identical down-and-in call is directly determined without separate numerical integration as \(C_{\text{di}} = 11.2356 - 10.0388 = \$1.1968\).

2. Reflection Principle & Merton's Closed-Form Solutions

Under the geometric Brownian motion assumptions of Black and Scholes (1973), Merton (1973) derived the first closed-form analytical solution for a down-and-out barrier call using the method of images.

Theorem 1.2 (Merton's Down-and-Out Call Closed Form). Let \(S_t\) follow a geometric diffusion \(dS_t / S_t = (r - q) dt + \sigma dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\). For a down-and-out European call with strike \(K\) and continuous barrier \(H\) satisfying \(S_0 > H\) and \(K \ge H\), the no-arbitrage price is given by:

\[ C_{\text{do}}(S_0, K, H, T) = C_{\text{BS}}(S_0, K, T) - \left(\frac{S_0}{H}\right)^{2 \mu} C_{\text{BS}}\left(\frac{H^2}{S_0}, K, T\right) \]

where \(C_{\text{BS}}(S, K, T)\) is the standard Black–Scholes call formula with dividend yield \(q\), and the drift scaling exponent is:

\[ \mu = \frac{r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2}{\sigma^2} \]

Proof sketch. Let \(x = \ln(S / H)\). Under the risk-neutral measure, \(x_t\) is an arithmetic Brownian motion with drift \(\alpha = r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\) and diffusion coefficient \(\sigma\), starting at \(x_0 = \ln(S_0 / H) > 0\). The barrier condition corresponds to absorption at \(x = 0\). By Girsanov's theorem, we remove the drift \(\alpha\) via change of measure: \(d\mathbb{Q}_0 / d\mathbb{Q} = \exp(-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma} W_T - \frac{\alpha^2}{2\sigma^2} T) = (S_T / S_0)^{-\alpha/\sigma^2} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^2 T / \sigma^2}\). Under \(\mathbb{Q}_0\), \(x_t\) is a driftless Brownian motion. By the classical reflection principle, the transition density of driftless Brownian motion absorbed at 0 is \(p(t, x, y) - p(t, x, -y)\), where \(p(t, x, y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2 t}} e^{-(y-x)^2 / (2\sigma^2 t)}\). Transforming back to the original measure introduces the power-law scaling factor \((S_0 / H)^{2\mu}\) evaluated at the geometric reflection point \(S' = H^2 / S_0\). Integrating the payoff \((S_T - K)^+\) against this absorbed density yields the stated difference of Black–Scholes call formulas. \(\blacksquare\)

As documented by Hull (2021), the method of images extends to all eight basic types of barrier options (down-and-out, down-and-in, up-and-out, up-and-in, for both calls and puts) and accommodates arbitrary strike relationships \(K < H\).

3. Discrete Monitoring Bias & The Broadie–Glasserman–Kou Shift

In exchange-traded and OTC derivative contracts, barriers are rarely monitored continuously. Instead, contracts specify discrete monitoring: daily closing fixings, hourly snapshots, or weekly reviews.

Theorem 1.3 (Broadie–Glasserman–Kou Continuity Correction). Let \(V_{\text{discrete}}(H, \Delta t)\) denote the price of a barrier option with barrier \(H\) observed at discrete intervals of length \(\Delta t\). As \(\Delta t \to 0\), the discrete price equals the continuous barrier option price evaluated at a shifted effective barrier \(H_{\text{eff}}\):

\[ H_{\text{eff}} = H \exp\left( \pm \beta \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} \right) + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t) \]

where the sign is negative for lower barriers (\(-\)) and positive for upper barriers (\(+\)), and the universal boundary constant \(\beta\) is defined via the Riemann zeta function:

\[ \beta = -\frac{\zeta(1/2)}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.5826 \]

Because discrete monitoring misses intra-interval excursions that cross and re-cross the barrier, a knock-out option under discrete monitoring has a strictly lower knock-out probability than under continuous monitoring. Consequently, discrete knock-out options are strictly more valuable than continuous knock-out options.

"""Lab 1 · Path-Dependent Options: Analytical Barrier Pricing & Discrete Monitoring Bias.

Implements Merton (1973) analytical down-and-out call option pricing, verifies
in-out parity (C_di + C_do = C_vanilla), and demonstrates the Broadie, Glasserman
& Kou (1997) continuity correction for discrete barrier monitoring on seed 42 (SIMULATED).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Cumulative distribution function of standard normal distribution."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def bs_call_price(s: float, k: float, t: float, r: float, q: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    """Standard Black-Scholes-Merton European call option price."""
    if t <= 0.0:
        return max(0.0, s - k)
    if sigma <= 0.0:
        return max(0.0, s * math.exp(-q * t) - k * math.exp(-r * t))
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r - q + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * t) / (sigma * math.sqrt(t))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * math.sqrt(t)
    return s * math.exp(-q * t) * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * t) * norm_cdf(d2)


def down_and_out_call_price(
    s: float,
    k: float,
    h: float,
    t: float,
    r: float,
    q: float,
    sigma: float,
) -> float:
    """Analytical continuous down-and-out call price for strike K >= barrier H (Merton 1973)."""
    if s <= h:
        return 0.0
    if k < h:
        raise ValueError("This analytical formulation requires strike K >= barrier H")
    vanilla_call = bs_call_price(s, k, t, r, q, sigma)
    # Power exponent for reflection transformation
    mu = (r - q - 0.5 * sigma * sigma) / (sigma * sigma)
    power = 2.0 * mu
    reflected_spot = (h * h) / s
    reflected_call = bs_call_price(reflected_spot, k, t, r, q, sigma)
    down_and_in_call = ((s / h) ** power) * reflected_call
    down_and_out_call = vanilla_call - down_and_in_call
    return float(max(0.0, float(down_and_out_call)))


def simulate_discrete_barrier_call(
    s0: float,
    k: float,
    h: float,
    t: float,
    r: float,
    q: float,
    sigma: float,
    n_steps: int,
    n_paths: int,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Monte Carlo simulation of discrete-monitoring down-and-out call."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = t / float(n_steps)
    sqrt_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
    drift = (r - q - 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * dt

    z = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, n_steps))
    log_increments = drift + sigma * sqrt_dt * z
    log_paths = np.empty((n_paths, n_steps + 1), dtype=np.float64)
    log_paths[:, 0] = math.log(s0)
    np.cumsum(log_increments, axis=1, out=log_paths[:, 1:])
    log_paths[:, 1:] += log_paths[:, 0:1]

    paths = np.exp(log_paths)
    # Discrete monitoring: breach occurs if spot at any monitoring grid step <= H
    min_spots = np.min(paths, axis=1)
    survived = min_spots > h
    terminal_spots = paths[:, -1]
    payoffs = np.where(survived, np.maximum(terminal_spots - k, 0.0), 0.0)

    df = math.exp(-r * t)
    discounted_payoffs = df * payoffs
    mc_price = float(np.mean(discounted_payoffs))
    mc_se = float(np.std(discounted_payoffs, ddof=1) / math.sqrt(n_paths))
    return mc_price, mc_se


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    strike = 100.0
    barrier = 85.0
    maturity = 1.0
    rate = 0.04
    div_yield = 0.01
    vol = 0.25

    # 1. Closed-form continuous barrier pricing
    c_vanilla = bs_call_price(spot, strike, maturity, rate, div_yield, vol)
    c_do_cont = down_and_out_call_price(spot, strike, barrier, maturity, rate, div_yield, vol)
    c_di_cont = c_vanilla - c_do_cont

    # 2. Broadie-Glasserman-Kou (1997) shift for daily monitoring (252 steps)
    n_daily = 252
    dt_daily = maturity / float(n_daily)
    # Shift parameter beta = -zeta(1/2) / sqrt(2*pi) ~= 0.5826
    beta_bgk = 0.5826
    h_shifted = barrier * math.exp(-beta_bgk * vol * math.sqrt(dt_daily))
    c_do_bgk = down_and_out_call_price(spot, strike, h_shifted, maturity, rate, div_yield, vol)

    # 3. Monte Carlo discrete daily monitoring (100,000 paths)
    n_paths = 100000
    mc_do, mc_se = simulate_discrete_barrier_call(
        spot, strike, barrier, maturity, rate, div_yield, vol, n_steps=n_daily, n_paths=n_paths, seed=42
    )

    print("=== Lab 1: Barrier Option Pricing & Discrete Monitoring Bias ===")
    print(f"Vanilla European Call Price:       {c_vanilla:.4f}")
    print(f"Continuous Down-and-Out Call:      {c_do_cont:.4f}")
    print(f"Continuous Down-and-In Call:       {c_di_cont:.4f}")
    print(f"In-Out Parity Check (DO + DI):     {c_do_cont + c_di_cont:.4f}")
    print(f"Discrete Daily MC DO Price:        {mc_do:.4f} (SE: {mc_se:.4f})")
    print(f"BGK Shifted Analytical DO Price:   {c_do_bgk:.4f}")
    print(f"Continuous vs Discrete Gap:        {mc_do - c_do_cont:.4f}")
    print(f"BGK Shifted vs Discrete MC Error:  {abs(mc_do - c_do_bgk):.4f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical Black–Scholes analytical barrier theory assumes continuous price diffusions, infinite market liquidity, and static volatility surfaces. In live trading, this canon breaks down catastrophically across three structural dimensions:

  • Pin Risk & Gamma Discontinuity: As spot approaches the knock-out barrier near expiration (\(S \to H, t \to T\)), the delta \(\Delta = \partial V / \partial S\) jumps discontinuously from its in-the-money value to zero. Consequently, gamma \(\Gamma = \partial^2 V / \partial S^2\) and vanna \(\partial \Delta / \partial \sigma\) explode towards infinity. A delta-hedging dealer cannot rebalance inventory fast enough, suffering massive slippage and unhedged gap losses.
  • Discontinuous Price Gaps & Jump Risk: Under jump diffusions or overnight market gaps, the stock price can jump directly across the barrier without ever touching \(H\). For a knock-out option with a rebate paid upon touching, the rebate trigger is ambiguous, and the option is extinguished without the smooth delta transition predicted by continuous models.
  • Volatility Smile Inconsistency: Merton's closed form assumes a flat volatility \(\sigma\). But in market reality, the volatility smile dictates that different strikes have different implied volatilities. Attempting to price barriers by simply plugging in the Black–Scholes implied volatility of strike \(K\) violates no-arbitrage, as local volatility dynamics (Dupire) govern the continuous probability of hitting the barrier.

Misconceptions

  1. Delta-hedging a barrier option becomes progressively easier and less risky as the option approaches expiration.

    Why it is wrong: For an in-the-money barrier option near expiration, delta-hedging becomes nearly impossible if spot is near the barrier. The contractual discontinuity creates an infinite gamma spike (\(\Gamma \to \infty\)), meaning that infinitesimal spot moves require immense share rebalancing, generating catastrophic pin risk and execution slippage.

  2. Discrete monitoring only causes negligible pricing differences (<0.1%) compared to continuous monitoring.

    Why it is wrong: As shown in Lab 1 and proven by Broadie, Glasserman & Kou (1997), daily monitoring on a 1-year option creates a pricing discrepancy of over 2% of the option value. For high-volatility assets or shorter maturities, discrete monitoring bias can exceed 10% of the total option premium.

  3. In-out parity holds only under the continuous Black–Scholes model.

    Why it is wrong: In-out parity is completely model-free. Because \(V_{\text{in}} + V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{vanilla}}\) holds pathwise for every sample trajectory \(\omega\), it must hold under any arbitrage-free asset pricing model, including jump-diffusions, rough volatility, and discrete non-parametric processes.

Problem Set

Mechanics

A non-dividend-paying stock trades at \(S_0 = \$100\) with risk-free rate \(r = 5\%\), volatility \(\sigma = 20\%\), and maturity \(T = 1.0\) year. A vanilla European call with strike \(K = 100\) trades at \(\$10.45\). An identical down-and-out call with barrier \(H = 80\) trades at \(\$9.85\). What is the no-arbitrage price of an otherwise identical down-and-in call?

Solution

By in-out parity (Theorem 1.1), \(V_{\text{in}} + V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{vanilla}}\). Substituting the given values: \(C_{\text{di}} + 9.85 = 10.45 \implies C_{\text{di}} = 10.45 - 9.85 = \$0.60\). Any deviation from \(\$0.60\) would admit a model-free arbitrage by longing the underpriced combination and shorting the vanilla call.

Calculate the Broadie, Glasserman & Kou (1997) effective barrier \(H_{\text{eff}}\) for a down-and-out call with nominal barrier \(H = 90.0\), asset volatility \(\sigma = 30\%\), and daily monitoring with \(\Delta t = 1/252\).

Solution

Under Theorem 1.3, the effective lower barrier is \(H_{\text{eff}} = H \exp(-\beta \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t})\) with \(\beta \approx 0.5826\). We compute: \(\Delta t = 1/252 \approx 0.003968\), \(\sqrt{\Delta t} \approx 0.06300\). The exponential argument is \(-\beta \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} = -0.5826 \times 0.30 \times 0.06300 \approx -0.01101\). Therefore, \(H_{\text{eff}} = 90.0 \times \exp(-0.01101) = 90.0 \times 0.98905 = 89.014\). The effective barrier is shifted down by approximately \(0.986\) points.

Consider a stock trading at \(S_0 = 100\). For a lower barrier \(H = 80\), what is the reflected image spot price \(S'\) in Merton's reflection formula?

Solution

In Merton's method of images (Theorem 1.2), the reflected image point is defined as \(S' = H^2 / S_0\). With \(H = 80\) and \(S_0 = 100\): \(S' = (80)^2 / 100 = 6400 / 100 = 64.0\). The auxiliary Black–Scholes option is evaluated at an initial spot price of \(\$64.00\).

Proof

Prove that for an up-and-out call option with strike \(K\) and barrier \(H\), the option price must be identically zero if the strike \(K \ge H\).

Solution

For a call option to have a positive terminal payoff \((S_T - K)^+ > 0\), the terminal price must satisfy \(S_T > K\). However, if \(K \ge H\), then \(S_T > K\) implies \(S_T > H\). Because the sample paths of asset prices are continuous (or must cross \(H\) to exceed \(H\)), any path where \(S_T > H\) must have breached the barrier \(H\) at some stopping time \(\tau_H \le T\). Upon touching \(H\), the up-and-out call is extinguished, paying 0. Therefore, the payoff is zero on every possible path, and the no-arbitrage price is identically zero.

Prove that an up-and-in call with barrier \(H\) and strike \(K \ge H\) has the exact same price and payoff as a standard vanilla European call with strike \(K\).

Solution

By in-out parity, \(C_{\text{ui}} + C_{\text{uo}} = C_{\text{vanilla}}\). In Problem P1, we proved that when \(K \ge H\), \(C_{\text{uo}} \equiv 0\). Substituting this into in-out parity yields \(C_{\text{ui}} + 0 = C_{\text{vanilla}} \implies C_{\text{ui}} = C_{\text{vanilla}}\). Intuitively, any path that finishes in the money (\(S_T > K \ge H\)) must have crossed the barrier \(H\), so the knock-in condition is guaranteed to be satisfied whenever the call finishes in the money.

Show that the Black–Scholes PDE boundary condition for a down-and-out barrier option with zero rebate is \(V(H, t) = 0\) for all \(t \in [0, T]\).

Solution

By contract definition, if the asset price reaches the barrier \(S_t = H\) at any time \(t \le T\), the option immediately deactivates with zero rebate. Because the contract ceases to exist with zero terminal value upon touching the barrier, the continuation value at the boundary must equal the immediate liquidation payout: \(V(H, t) = 0\). In PDE terms, this establishes a Dirichlet boundary condition on the spatial domain \((H, \infty)\).

Empirical

In an empirical study of FX barrier options, an analyst observes that pricing discrete daily barrier options using continuous analytical formulas results in systematic mispricing. Why do structured product desks that sell discrete barrier options using continuous pricing lose money on average?

Solution

Continuous barrier formulas assume continuous monitoring, which overestimates the knock-out probability. Consequently, continuous formulas price knock-out options too cheaply. A desk that sells discrete knock-out options at continuous prices is selling underpriced options. When paths cross the barrier intra-day but recover before the daily close, the option survives, forcing the desk to pay out claims it had mathematically priced as knocked out.

Using the results from Lab 1, compute the relative pricing error \((C_{\text{discrete}} - C_{\text{continuous}}) / C_{\text{continuous}}\) of a down-and-out call under daily monitoring. Explain why institutional trading desks mandate the Broadie–Glasserman–Kou shift.

Solution

From Lab 1, \(C_{\text{continuous}} = 10.0388\) and \(C_{\text{discrete}} = 10.2660\). The absolute pricing gap is \(+0.2272\) USD, representing a relative pricing error of \(0.2272 / 10.0388 \approx 2.26\%\). On an institutional notional of \(\$100\) million, this pricing bias corresponds to over \(\$2.2\) million in mispriced risk. Institutional desks mandate the BGK shift because it eliminates this bias instantaneously in analytical pricer engines without requiring computationally expensive Monte Carlo simulations.

Explain how a sharp decrease in underlying asset implied volatility impacts the price of a down-and-out call when the spot price is close to the barrier.

Solution

While vanilla call options have strictly positive vega (\(\mathcal{V} > 0\)), a barrier option near its barrier can exhibit negative vega (\(\mathcal{V} < 0\)). When spot is near the barrier, higher volatility drastically increases the probability of hitting the barrier and knocking out. A decrease in implied volatility reduces the knock-out probability, allowing the option to survive and increasing its market value.

Research

How does local volatility skew (Gatheral 2006) resolve the smile-inconsistency problem in barrier option pricing compared to Black–Scholes?

Solution

As analyzed by Gatheral (2006, Ch. 7), Dupire local volatility \(\sigma_{\text{loc}}(S, t)\) defines a state-dependent diffusion coefficient that recovers the entire market European option surface. In a market with a downward volatility skew, local volatility is elevated at low spot levels. Consequently, as the underlying declines toward a lower barrier, local volatility increases, producing higher barrier-crossing probabilities and lower down-and-out option prices than a constant-volatility Black–Scholes model calibrated to the strike's implied volatility.

What is semi-static barrier replication (Carr & Chou 2002), and why is it preferred over pure dynamic delta-hedging on exotic trading desks?

Solution

Semi-static replication synthesizes the boundary condition of a barrier option using a static portfolio of vanilla European options with strikes along the barrier. By matching the boundary values across all times \(t \le T\), the portfolio replicates the barrier option without requiring continuous rebalancing. If the barrier is touched, the vanilla portfolio is unwound. This eliminates the extreme pin risk, infinite gamma spikes, and transaction cost bleed associated with continuous delta-hedging near the barrier.

Formulate the discrete barrier hitting probability across a single Monte Carlo step \([t, t+\Delta t]\) using the Brownian bridge construction (Glasserman 2003).

Solution

Following Glasserman (2003, p. 364), given simulated endpoint values \(S_t\) and \(S_{t+\Delta t}\) that both lie strictly above a lower barrier \(H\), the maximum excursion toward \(H\) during the interval follows a Brownian bridge. The conditional probability that the path touched or crossed \(H\) intra-step is given in closed form by \(\mathbb{P}(\tau_H \in [t, t+\Delta t] \mid S_t, S_{t+\Delta t}) = \exp\left( - \frac{2 \ln(S_t / H) \ln(S_{t+\Delta t} / H)}{\sigma^2 \Delta t} \right)\). Incorporating this conditional probability allows Monte Carlo pricers to correct for intra-step barrier breaches without infinitesimal time discretization.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the exact mathematical relationship between a knock-in option and an otherwise identical knock-out option?

    Options

    By pathwise decomposition, the barrier is either hit or not hit. The payoffs sum to the vanilla payoff on every path, yielding \(V_{\text{in}} + V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{vanilla}}\).

  2. Under the Merton (1973) reflection formula for a down-and-out call, at what transformed spot price is the reflected Black–Scholes call evaluated?

    Options

    The geometric reflection of \(\ln(S_0 / H)\) across 0 in log space corresponds to \(-\ln(S_0 / H) = \ln(H / S_0)\), which maps to price \(S' = H^2 / S_0\).

  3. Why are discretely monitored knock-out options strictly more valuable than continuously monitored knock-out options?

    Options

    Paths that cross and re-cross the barrier between observation dates survive under discrete monitoring but are extinguished under continuous monitoring, increasing survival probability and option price.

  4. In the Broadie, Glasserman & Kou (1997) shift \(H_{\text{eff}} = H \exp(\pm \beta \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t})\), what is the universal constant \(\beta\)?

    Options

    The constant \(\beta\) arises from the boundary behavior of Gaussian random walks and equals \(-\zeta(1/2)/\sqrt{2\pi} \approx 0.5826\).

  5. What happens to the gamma (\(\Gamma\)) of a down-and-out call as spot approaches the barrier level from above near expiration?

    Options

    Because delta drops discontinuously from a positive value to zero at the barrier, the derivative of delta (gamma) spikes towards infinity, creating extreme pin risk.

  6. If strike \(K \ge H\) for an up-and-out call, what is the option price?

    Options

    Any path finishing in the money (\(S_T > K \ge H\)) must have touched \(H\) prior to maturity, extinguishing the option. Thus the payout is always zero.

  7. Why can the vega (\(\mathcal{V}\)) of a down-and-out call turn negative when spot is near the barrier?

    Options

    Near the barrier, the risk of knock-out dominates the convex upside of the option. Increasing volatility makes knock-out more likely, lowering the option's value (\(\mathcal{V} < 0\)).

  8. What is a primary advantage of semi-static barrier replication (Carr & Chou 2002) over dynamic delta hedging?

    Options

    Semi-static replication matches option payoffs along the barrier boundary with vanilla options, avoiding explosive dynamic rebalancing when spot is near the barrier.
