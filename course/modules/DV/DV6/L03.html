Structured Products: Autocallables, Phoenix Notes & Knock-Out Boundaries

Structured retail products package exotic derivatives into yield-enhancement debt securities. Autocallable notes represent the dominant global volume in equity-linked issuance, paying above-market conditional coupons in exchange for short downside tail risk. Pricing these instruments requires decomposing complex early-termination conditions under Dupire local volatility surfaces, exposing how downside skew alters barrier breach probabilities and expected contract tenors.

  • Introduces: autocallable
  • Prerequisites: barrier-option, local-volatility, volatility-smile, option-payoff

1. Architecture of Autocallable Notes & Payoff Mechanics

Structured retail notes emerged from the appetite of wealth management and institutional investors for enhanced coupon income in low-interest-rate environments. Rather than purchasing pure fixed income, the investor sells out-of-the-money downside optionality to fund contingent coupon enhancements.

Definition 3.1 (Autocallable Note). An autocallable note (or automatically callable structured product) is a capital-at-risk structured debt security linked to one or more underlying reference assets \(\{S_t\}\) with scheduled observation dates \(\{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_M = T\}\). If the underlying asset price meets or exceeds a predetermined autocall barrier \(A_k\) on observation date \(t_k\), the note automatically terminates early, repaying 100% of principal notional \(N\) plus accrued coupons. If not autocalled prior to maturity \(T\), terminal principal redemption depends on whether a downside knock-in barrier \(B < S_0\) was breached.

The standard contemporary structure is the Phoenix Autocallable Note, which features two independent barrier levels: a coupon barrier \(C_b\) (typically 70% to 85% of \(S_0\)) and a downside knock-in protection barrier \(B\) (typically 60% to 70% of \(S_0\)).

In addition, Phoenix notes standardly incorporate a memory coupon feature: if the underlying settles below the coupon barrier on date \(t_k\), the coupon is not paid, but is stored in memory. If spot recovers above \(C_b\) on any subsequent observation date \(t_j > t_k\), all unpaid historical coupons are paid out in full.

Example 3.1 (Phoenix Note Payoff Schedule). Consider a 2-year semi-annual Phoenix note linked to the Euro Stoxx 50 (\(S_0 = 4000\)) with notional \(N = \$1000\), coupon rate \(c = 3\%\) per semi-annual (6% p.a.), autocall barrier \(A_k = 100\% \times S_0 = 4000\), coupon barrier \(C_b = 80\% \times S_0 = 3200\), and European maturity knock-in barrier \(B = 65\% \times S_0 = 2600\):

  • At \(t_1 = 0.5\) yr: If \(S_{0.5} = 4100 \ge 4000\), the note autocalls: investor receives \(\$1000 + \$30 = \$1030\), and the contract terminates immediately.
  • If \(S_{0.5} = 3500\): \(3200 \le S_{0.5} < 4000\). No autocall. Coupon \(\$30\) is paid. The note continues to \(t_2\).
  • If \(S_{0.5} = 3000\): \(S_{0.5} < 3200\). No autocall. No coupon is paid; \(\$30\) is remembered. The note continues to \(t_2\).
  • At \(t_2 = 1.0\) yr: If \(S_{1.0} = 3300 \ge 3200\), the investor receives current coupon \(\$30\) plus remembered coupon \(\$30\) (total \(\$60\)).
  • At maturity \(t_4 = 2.0\) yr: If never autocalled, redemption is: \(\$1000\) if \(S_2 \ge 2600\); or \(\$1000 \times (S_2 / 4000)\) if \(S_2 < 2600\), exposing the investor to full downside equity losses from initial spot.

2. Payoff Decomposition & Embedded Exotic Optionality

To value and risk-manage an autocallable note, the exotic derivatives trading desk decomposes the structured liability into elementary linear and exotic contracts.

Theorem 3.1 (Phoenix Autocallable Payoff Decomposition). Neglecting issuer default risk, the market value of a Phoenix autocallable note decomposes into three primary balance sheet components:

\[ V_{\text{autocall}}(t) = P_{\text{bond}}(t) + V_{\text{coupon-strip}}(t) - V_{\text{downside-put}}(t) \]

where:

  1. \(P_{\text{bond}}(t)\) is a variable-tenor zero-coupon bond paying notional \(N\) at the stochastic stopping time \(\tau^* = \min(\{t_k : S_{t_k} \ge A_k\} \cup \{T\})\).
  2. \(V_{\text{coupon-strip}}(t)\) is a sequence of conditional memory digital options paying \(c \cdot N\) conditional on \(\{S_{t_k} \ge C_b\}\) while the note remains active.
  3. \(V_{\text{downside-put}}(t)\) is a European or American down-and-in put option with strike \(S_0\) and barrier \(B\), active only if the note is not autocalled prior to \(T\): \[ \text{Payoff}_{\text{put}} = N \left( 1 - \frac{S_T}{S_0} \right)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\{\min_{0 \le t \le T} S_t \le B\}} \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau^* = T, S_T < A_M\}} \]

Proof. Consider any realization \(\omega \in \Omega\). If the underlying price satisfies the autocall condition at date \(t_k < T\), the investor receives \(N\) plus accrued coupons. The cash flow at \(t_k\) is exactly \(N + \text{Coupons}\). If the note survives to maturity \(T\) without early redemption, the terminal payout is:

\[ V_T = \text{Final Coupon} + \begin{cases} N & \text{if } S_T \ge A_M \text{ or } \min_{0 \le t \le T} S_t > B \\ N \frac{S_T}{S_0} & \text{if } S_T < A_M \text{ and } \min_{0 \le t \le T} S_t \le B \end{cases} \]

Notice that in the breach state (\(S_T < A_M\) and \(\min S_t \le B\)), the redemption can be written identically as \(N \frac{S_T}{S_0} = N - N(1 - \frac{S_T}{S_0}) = N - \frac{N}{S_0}(S_0 - S_T)^+\). Therefore, on every path, the principal redemption equals full notional \(N\) minus the payout of a down-and-in put option. Discounting all cash flows under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) establishes the decomposition. \(\blacksquare\)

Because the investor is short the down-and-in put, the investor bears substantial negative skewness and tail risk. The premium earned from this short put position is what subsidizes the attractive above-market coupon rate.

3. Valuation Under Local Volatility: Skew & Barrier Knock-In Dynamics

A constant Black–Scholes volatility model cannot price autocallable notes correctly. As documented by Gatheral (2006), barrier hitting probabilities are acutely sensitive to the local volatility surface \(\sigma_{\text{loc}}(S, t)\).

In equity index markets, the implied volatility surface exhibits persistent negative skew (\(\partial \sigma_{\text{impl}} / \partial K < 0\)). Under Dupire's continuous local volatility model:

\[ dS_t = (r - q) S_t\,dt + \sigma_{\text{loc}}(S_t, t) S_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}} \]

As the underlying spot price declines towards the downside barrier \(B\), local volatility \(\sigma_{\text{loc}}(S_t, t)\) rises steeply. This state-dependent volatility escalation has two dramatic pricing consequences:

  • Higher Barrier Breach Frequency: The probability of breaching the knock-in barrier \(B\) is substantially higher under local volatility than under flat Black–Scholes volatility.
  • Lower Fair Note Value: Because the embedded short put option is more likely to knock in, its value increases, which strictly lowers the fair value of the autocallable note (or requires the issuer to reduce the offered coupon).

Lab 1 · Autocallable valuation: Flat volatility vs. local volatility skew

Adding realistic downside skew (skew=0.16) increases the barrier breach probability to 9.16% (+0.79%), reducing note fair value by -$2.61 USD.

"""Lab 3 · Structured Products: Autocallable Phoenix Note Valuation under Local Volatility Skew.

Evaluates a multi-period Phoenix autocallable note with memory coupons and a down-and-in
barrier by Monte Carlo under constant Black-Scholes volatility versus a parametric
Dupire local volatility surface with realistic downside skew on seed 42 (SIMULATED).
Demonstrates the pricing and risk divergence driven by volatility skew.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def eval_local_vol(
    spot: float,
    strike: float,
    time: float,
    base_vol: float,
    skew: float,
    curvature: float,
) -> float:
    """Parametric local volatility surface sigma(S, t)."""
    m = math.log(spot / strike)
    term = 1.0 / math.sqrt(1.0 + 0.5 * time)
    vol = base_vol * term * (1.0 - skew * math.tanh(m) + curvature * (m**2))
    return float(max(0.01, min(2.0, vol)))


def simulate_autocallable(
    spot: float,
    strike: float,
    rate: float,
    div_yield: float,
    notional: float,
    obs_dates: list[float],
    autocall_levels: list[float],
    coupon_rate: float,
    coupon_barrier: float,
    knock_in_barrier: float,
    base_vol: float,
    skew: float,
    curvature: float,
    n_paths: int = 50000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate autocallable under parametric local volatility."""
    n_obs = len(obs_dates)
    maturity = obs_dates[-1]
    sub_steps_per_obs = 10
    total_steps = n_obs * sub_steps_per_obs
    dt = maturity / float(total_steps)
    sqrt_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
    obs_indices = [(i + 1) * sub_steps_per_obs for i in range(n_obs)]

    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    z = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, total_steps))
    paths = np.empty((n_paths, total_steps + 1), dtype=np.float64)
    paths[:, 0] = spot
    drift_coeff = rate - div_yield

    for step in range(total_steps):
        s_curr = paths[:, step]
        t_curr = step * dt
        m = np.log(s_curr / strike)
        term = 1.0 / np.sqrt(1.0 + 0.5 * t_curr)
        vol = base_vol * term * (1.0 - skew * np.tanh(m) + curvature * (m**2))
        vol = np.clip(vol, 0.01, 2.0)
        paths[:, step + 1] = s_curr * np.exp((drift_coeff - 0.5 * (vol**2)) * dt + vol * sqrt_dt * z[:, step])

    min_spot = np.min(paths, axis=1)
    knocked_in = min_spot <= knock_in_barrier

    alive = np.ones(n_paths, dtype=bool)
    discounted_coupons = np.zeros(n_paths, dtype=np.float64)
    discounted_redemption = np.zeros(n_paths, dtype=np.float64)
    autocall_counts = np.zeros(n_obs, dtype=np.float64)
    unpaid_coupons = np.zeros(n_paths, dtype=np.float64)

    for obs_idx in range(n_obs):
        step_idx = obs_indices[obs_idx]
        t_obs = obs_dates[obs_idx]
        df = math.exp(-rate * t_obs)
        s_obs = paths[:, step_idx]
        autocall_trigger = autocall_levels[obs_idx]

        active = alive
        unpaid_coupons[active] += coupon_rate * notional
        coupon_paid_mask = active & (s_obs >= coupon_barrier)
        discounted_coupons[coupon_paid_mask] += df * unpaid_coupons[coupon_paid_mask]
        unpaid_coupons[coupon_paid_mask] = 0.0

        if obs_idx < n_obs - 1:
            autocall_mask = active & (s_obs >= autocall_trigger)
            discounted_redemption[autocall_mask] += df * notional
            autocall_counts[obs_idx] += float(np.sum(autocall_mask))
            alive[autocall_mask] = False
        else:
            autocall_mask = active & (s_obs >= autocall_trigger)
            discounted_redemption[autocall_mask] += df * notional
            autocall_counts[obs_idx] += float(np.sum(autocall_mask))

            non_autocall_active = active & (~autocall_mask)
            safe_mask = non_autocall_active & (~knocked_in)
            discounted_redemption[safe_mask] += df * notional

            loss_mask = non_autocall_active & knocked_in
            discounted_redemption[loss_mask] += df * notional * (s_obs[loss_mask] / strike)
            alive[:] = False

    coupon_pv = float(np.mean(discounted_coupons))
    redemption_pv = float(np.mean(discounted_redemption))
    total_price = coupon_pv + redemption_pv
    prob_autocall_total = float(np.sum(autocall_counts) / n_paths)
    prob_knock_in = float(np.mean(knocked_in))

    return {
        "price": total_price,
        "coupon_pv": coupon_pv,
        "redemption_pv": redemption_pv,
        "autocall_prob": prob_autocall_total,
        "knock_in_prob": prob_knock_in,
    }

Where the Canon Breaks

While local volatility captures static vanilla smile pricing, autocallable risk management fails under several real-world market breakdowns:

  • Worst-Of Multi-Asset Correlation Breakdown: Most institutional autocallables are issued on a basket of 3 or 4 stocks or indices ('worst-of' autocallable), where redemption depends on the worst-performing asset \(\min_i (S_T^i / S_0^i)\). During market stress, pairwise equity correlations spike towards 1, while correlation skew distorts multi-asset local volatility, destroying independent marginal calibrations.
  • Forward Smile Dynamics & Flawed Hedging: Pure local volatility assumes the volatility surface moves deterministically with spot (the 'sticky local vol' assumption). In reality, implied volatility smiles exhibit stochastic shifts. As shown by Gatheral (2006), local volatility models predict that forward smiles flatten as spot moves, whereas empirical forward smiles remain steeply skewed, leading to severe vega and vanna mishedges.
  • Gap-at-Default & Overnight Jumps: If a single-stock underlying experiences catastrophic corporate earnings releases, fraud, or bankruptcy overnight, the stock price gaps down through the barrier instantaneously. Delta hedging cannot be executed, and the dealer absorbs the full gap loss without earning dynamic gamma gains.

Misconceptions

  1. An autocallable note is a conservative income investment suitable for risk-averse bond investors.

    Why it is wrong: Autocallables are equity derivatives packaged as debt. The investor has zero upside participation beyond the coupon, but retains asymmetric 100% downside equity risk once the knock-in barrier is breached. In major market crashes, investors suffer severe capital impairment identical to an unleveraged equity investment.

  2. In a multi-asset 'worst-of' autocallable note, adding more stocks provides diversification that protects the investor.

    Why it is wrong: The 'worst-of' payoff condition is an extreme value selector \(\min(S^1/S_0^1, \dots, S^m/S_0^m)\). Adding more assets strictly increases the probability that at least one constituent breaches the downside barrier, strictly increasing risk and lowering capital safety for the investor.

  3. The duration of an autocallable note is fixed by its stated final maturity date.

    Why it is wrong: The duration of an autocallable is stochastic and highly path-dependent. In a bull market, early autocall redemptions collapse duration to 6 months; in a severe bear market where autocall triggers are out of reach, duration extends out to the full multi-year maturity. This negative convexity is known as extension risk.

Problem Set

Mechanics

A 1-year quarterly Phoenix autocallable note with notional \(N = \$1000\) offers a quarterly coupon of \(2.5\%\). The memory coupon feature is active with a coupon barrier at \(80\%\). On the four observation dates, the underlying spot settles at: \(t_1: 75\%\), \(t_2: 78\%\), \(t_3: 82\%\), and \(t_4: 105\%\) (autocall barrier is \(100\%\)). Calculate the cash flow received by the investor at each observation date.

Solution

At \(t_1\) (\(75\% < 80\%\)): No coupon is paid; unpaid balance = \(\$25\). At \(t_2\) (\(78\% < 80\%\)): No coupon is paid; unpaid balance = \(\$25 + \$25 = \$50\). At \(t_3\) (\(82\% \ge 80\%\)): Spot exceeds coupon barrier. Investor receives current coupon \(\$25\) plus accrued memory coupons \(\$50\), for a total payment of \(\$75\); unpaid balance resets to \(\$0\). At \(t_4\) (\(105\% \ge 100\%\)): Spot exceeds autocall barrier. Investor receives principal \(\$1000\) plus current coupon \(\$25\), for a total payment of \(\$1025\). Total cash flows received: \(t_1: \$0\), \(t_2: \$0\), \(t_3: \$75\), \(t_4: \$1025\).

An investor buys an autocallable note linked to Apple stock at \(S_0 = \$150\). The note has a continuous knock-in barrier at \(65\%\) (\(\$97.50\)). During the 2-year tenor, Apple stock drops to an intraday low of \(\$95.00\) in month 14, but rallies to settle at \(\$135.00\) at final maturity without ever triggering an autocall. If the notional is \(\$1000\), what is the principal redemption at maturity?

Solution

Because the barrier is continuous and \(\min S_t = \$95.00 < \$97.50\), the knock-in barrier was breached. At maturity, the note did not autocall (\(S_T = \$135 < \$150\)). Therefore, the breach penalty applies: redemption is \(N \times (S_T / S_0) = \$1000 \times (135 / 150) = \$1000 \times 0.90 = \$900.00\). The investor loses \(10\%\) of principal despite the stock finishing above the barrier at maturity.

Explain the difference in principal redemption between a European maturity-only knock-in barrier and an American continuous knock-in barrier for the scenario in Problem M2.

Solution

Under a European maturity-only knock-in barrier, the barrier status is evaluated strictly at final expiration \(T\). In Problem M2, at \(T = 2\) years, \(S_T = \$135 > \$97.50\). Under a European barrier, the barrier was never breached at maturity, so the investor receives 100% principal redemption (\(\$1000\)). Under an American continuous barrier, the intraday breach at month 14 is permanently registered, reducing redemption to \(\$900\). European barriers offer strictly higher capital safety to investors.

Proof

Prove that for an otherwise identical autocallable structure, lowering the autocall trigger schedule (e.g. step-down autocalls: \(100\%, 95\%, 90\%\)) strictly shortens the expected product tenor and reduces the expected total coupon payout.

Solution

Let \(\tau_A = \min\{t_k : S_{t_k} \ge A_k\}\) denote the autocall stopping time. If the trigger schedule \(A_k'\) satisfies \(A_k' \le A_k\) for all \(k\), then on every sample path \(\omega\), \(\{S_{t_k}(\omega) \ge A_k\} \implies \{S_{t_k}(\omega) \ge A_k'\}\). Therefore, \(\tau_A'(\omega) \le \tau_A(\omega)\) pathwise. Taking expectations under any probability measure: \(\mathbb{E}[\tau_A'] \le \mathbb{E}[\tau_A]\), proving that the expected tenor is strictly shorter. Because the contract terminates earlier or at the same time on every path, the number of coupon-eligible periods is weakly less on all paths, strictly reducing the expected total coupon cash flows.

Prove that the fair coupon rate of an autocallable note is strictly increasing in the underlying asset's dividend yield \(q\).

Solution

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the drift of the underlying asset is \(r - q\). Increasing the dividend yield \(q\) reduces the forward price \(F(t) = S_0 e^{(r-q)t}\). A lower forward price has two effects: (1) it decreases the probability of hitting upside autocall barriers, extending duration, and (2) it shifts the terminal asset distribution downwards, substantially increasing the probability of breaching the downside knock-in barrier. An increased probability of knock-in raises the value of the embedded short put option sold by the investor. Because the investor sells a more valuable put, the issuer must pay a higher coupon rate to restore the note's par value at inception.

Show that the delta of an autocallable note is strictly positive for spot levels in the vicinity of the initial spot price \(S_0\).

Solution

By Theorem 3.1, \(V_{\text{note}} = P_{\text{bond}} + V_{\text{coupon-strip}} - V_{\text{downside-put}}\). Differentiating with respect to spot \(S\): \(\Delta_{\text{note}} = \Delta_{\text{bond}} + \Delta_{\text{coupon-strip}} - \Delta_{\text{put}}\). When spot increases, the probability of early redemption increases (bringing forward principal repayment), so \(\Delta_{\text{bond}} \ge 0\). The digital coupon options have positive delta near moneyness, so \(\Delta_{\text{coupon-strip}} \ge 0\). Finally, the embedded put has negative delta (\(\Delta_{\text{put}} \le 0\)), so \(-\Delta_{\text{put}} \ge 0\). Summing these terms establishes that \(\Delta_{\text{note}} > 0\). The investor is economically long the underlying asset.

Empirical

In 2015 and 2020, investment banks in South Korea suffered massive losses hedging ELS (Equity-Linked Securities) autocallables on the Euro Stoxx 50 and Hang Seng China Enterprises Index (HSCEI). Explain the structural hedging feedback loop that caused these dealer losses during market crashes.

Solution

South Korean retail investors held over \(\$40\) billion in autocallables with knock-in barriers clustered around 60% of spot. Dealers who sold these notes were long puts near the money, giving them positive gamma during calm markets. However, as the HSCEI and Euro Stoxx plummeted toward the 60% barrier, dealer gamma flipped from positive to violently negative. To maintain delta neutrality, dealers were forced to dump billions of dollars of index futures as the market fell. When markets breached the barrier, delta stabilized, forcing dealers to buy back hedges at the bottom. This 'sell-low, buy-high' dynamic cost global dealer desks an estimated \(\$1.5\) billion in 2015.

Using the results from Lab 1, calculate the increase in annual coupon rate an issuer must offer to compensate an investor for downside volatility skew (skew = 0.16) versus flat Black–Scholes volatility over a 2-year note.

Solution

From Lab 1, the value deficit caused by downside skew is \(\$2.61\) per \(\$1000\) notional on a note with expected tenor of \(\sim 1.07\) years. To restore par value (\(\$1000\)), the present value of coupons must increase by \(\$2.61\). With an expected tenor of \(\sim 1.07\) years and 4 semi-annual observation dates, the annualized coupon spread is approximately \(\Delta c \approx \frac{\$2.61}{\$1000 \times 1.07} \approx 0.00244\) (or \(24.4\) bps p.a.). Issuers must offer higher headline coupons to clear structured products in high-skew markets.

Why do structured product desks issue autocallables heavily on single stocks with high implied volatility (e.g. Tesla, Nvidia) rather than broad market indices during retail distribution campaigns?

Solution

Single stocks with high implied volatility (e.g. \(\sigma = 40\%\)–\(60\%\)) generate immense option premium from the embedded short knock-in put. Selling this rich downside volatility allows issuers to market headline coupon rates of 12% to 20% p.a., which retail investors perceive as high yield. However, the high single-stock volatility dramatically increases the probability of breaching the knock-in barrier, transferring substantial uncompensated tail risk to unsophisticated retail buyers.

Research

Why does a pure local volatility model underestimate the price of an autocallable note relative to a Stochastic Local Volatility (SLV) model (Gatheral 2006)?

Solution

As proved by Gatheral (2006, Ch. 8), pure local volatility implies that forward volatility smiles flatten as spot moves into the wings ('sticky local vol'). In contrast, stochastic local volatility combines a Dupire local vol skeleton with an independent stochastic variance process (e.g. Heston), preserving steep forward volatility skew. Because forward skew remains steep under SLV, the conditional probability of subsequent knock-in remains elevated even after the market drops, increasing the true market value of the downside put and reducing the note's fair price relative to local volatility.

Formulate the optimal semi-static hedging problem for an autocallable knock-in barrier using a portfolio of vanilla European puts.

Solution

To eliminate explosive dynamic gamma risk near the barrier \(B\), the desk solves a constrained quadratic programming problem: \(\min_{w_i} \int_0^T \left( V_{\text{autocall}}(B, t) - \sum_{i=1}^K w_i P(B, K_i, t) \right)^2 dt\). The weights \(w_i\) on vanilla puts with strikes \(K_i\) are chosen to match the autocallable continuation value along the spatial boundary \(S = B\) across all times \(t \le T\). If spot touches \(B\), the vanilla put portfolio is unwound to fund the client's downside settlement, immunizing the desk against localized gamma pin risk.

Analyze the impact of jump-to-default risk in single-stock autocallable notes. How does incorporating a Poisson jump process modify the PDE valuation boundary conditions?

Solution

Under a jump-to-default model with hazard rate \(\lambda(S_t, t)\), the asset price jumps to 0 with probability \(\lambda dt\). If default occurs, spot drops instantaneously to 0, breaching any knock-in barrier \(B > 0\) and destroying the principal recovery. The pricing PDE acquires an integral jump-to-default term: \(\partial_t V + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \partial_{SS} V + (r-q+\lambda) S \partial_S V - (r+\lambda) V + \lambda V_{\text{default}} = 0\), where \(V_{\text{default}} = 0\) (or recovery value). Jump-to-default risk imposes a hard floor on the fair coupon, as investors face total capital loss without the opportunity for dynamic delta hedging.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What embedded derivative position generates the funding for high coupon payments in an autocallable note?

    Options

    The investor sells a down-and-in put option (retaining full downside risk if the barrier is breached), and the premium earned from this short put subsidizes the above-market coupon.

  2. How does the memory coupon feature operate in a Phoenix autocallable note?

    Options

    The memory feature retains a ledger of missed coupons during downturns, paying the full backlog if the underlying asset subsequently recovers above the coupon threshold.

  3. Why does negative volatility skew in local volatility models decrease the fair value of an autocallable note?

    Options

    Under negative skew, as spot falls, local volatility surges, making downward barrier crossing significantly more likely than under flat volatility. This increases the value of the short put, lowering note value.

  4. In a 'worst-of' multi-asset autocallable linked to three equities, what happens to the investor's risk as correlation between the equities decreases?

    Options

    Because redemption depends on \(\min_i(S^i / S_0^i)\), lower correlation widens the dispersion of the minimum. It becomes much more likely that at least one stock crashes through the barrier.

  5. What is 'duration extension risk' in an autocallable note?

    Options

    Autocallables exhibit negative convexity: in bull markets they redeem early (short duration), but in bear markets where the investor wants liquidity, autocalls fail and the note extends to full maturity.

  6. How does an American continuous knock-in barrier differ from a European maturity-only knock-in barrier?

    Options

    American continuous monitoring activates upon the first touching time \(\tau_B \le T\), whereas European monitoring activates only if the terminal price \(S_T \le B\).

  7. Why does an increase in underlying dividend yield increase the fair coupon rate of an autocallable note?

    Options

    Dividends reduce forward prices under \(\mathbb{Q}\), which shifts the path distribution toward the downside barrier. The client's short put becomes more valuable, generating more premium to fund coupons.

  8. What catastrophic event occurred in 2015 when global equity derivatives desks hedged South Korean ELS autocallables on the HSCEI?

    Options

    Concentrated knock-in barriers flipped dealer gamma into severe negative territory, forcing algorithmic rebalancing to sell futures as spot fell, exacerbating the selloff and causing massive trading losses.
