Averaging & Extremum Payoffs: Asians & Lookbacks

Path-dependent derivatives extend beyond single-barrier threshold events to continuous trajectories. Asian options replace terminal prices with path averages, suppressing volatility and manipulation risk in illiquid commodities and foreign exchange. Lookback options deliver extreme payoffs pegged to running extrema, creating high-convexity exposure with substantial vega. Mastering their mathematical distributions provides essential machinery for exotic structuring and numerical variance reduction.

  • Introduces: asian-option, lookback-option
  • Prerequisites: barrier-option, black-scholes-formula, option-greeks

1. Asian Options: Arithmetic vs. Geometric Averages

In commodity, crude oil, and foreign exchange markets, market participants frequently face delivery schedules spread across an entire month or quarter rather than a single terminal settlement date. A standard European option exposes the hedger to acute expiration date manipulation or sudden localized price spikes.

Definition 2.1 (Asian Option). An Asian option (or average-rate option) is a path-dependent option contract whose payoff depends on the historical average of the underlying asset price over a predetermined observation schedule \(\{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n\} \subset [0, T]\). The option is classified by strike type and averaging method:

  • Fixed Strike: Payoff is \((A_T - K)^+\) for a call and \((K - A_T)^+\) for a put.
  • Floating Strike: Payoff is \((S_T - A_T)^+\) for a call and \((A_T - S_T)^+\) for a put.
  • Arithmetic Average: \(A_T = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n S_{t_i}\) (discrete) or \(A_T = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T S_u\,du\) (continuous).
  • Geometric Average: \(G_T = \left(\prod_{i=1}^n S_{t_i}\right)^{1/n} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \ln S_{t_i}\right)\).

Because the sum of log-normal random variables does not follow a log-normal distribution, arithmetic Asian options do not admit an analytical closed-form density under Black–Scholes diffusions. In contrast, the geometric average \(G_T\) is an exponential of a sum of normal random variables, making \(G_T\) strictly log-normal.

Theorem 2.1 (Jensen's Inequality Ordering for Asian Options). For identical parameters, strikes, and observation schedules, the arithmetic Asian average dominates the geometric Asian average on every path (\(A_T \ge G_T\)). Furthermore, both Asian options are strictly cheaper than an otherwise identical vanilla European option:

\[ V_{\text{geom Asian}} \le V_{\text{arith Asian}} \le V_{\text{European}} \]

Proof. The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality (a direct consequence of the concavity of the natural logarithm via Jensen's inequality) guarantees that for any positive sequence \(S_{t_1}, \dots, S_{t_n} > 0\):

\[ \ln\left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n S_{t_i} \right) \ge \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \ln S_{t_i} \implies A_T \ge G_T \]

Since the call payoff \(f(x) = (x - K)^+\) is non-decreasing, \((A_T - K)^+ \ge (G_T - K)^+\) pathwise. Taking discounted risk-neutral expectations establishes \(C_{\text{geom}} \le C_{\text{arith}}\). To establish the second inequality, note that \(A_T = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n S_{t_i}\). By the strict convexity of the call payoff \(f(x) = (x - K)^+\):

\[ \left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n S_{t_i} - K \right)^+ = \left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (S_{t_i} - K) \right)^+ \le \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (S_{t_i} - K)^+ \]

Taking conditional expectations under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the expected value of \((S_{t_i} - K)^+\) grows over time under risk-neutral discounting due to volatility accumulation. Therefore, \(\mathbb{E}[(A_T - K)^+] < \mathbb{E}[(S_T - K)^+]\), completing the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

2. Kemna–Vorst Geometric Solution & Control Variate Architecture

Recognizing the exact log-normality of the geometric average, Kemna & Vorst (1990) derived an analytical pricing formula for geometric Asian options and introduced its use as an optimal control variate for arithmetic Asian simulation.

Theorem 2.2 (Kemna–Vorst Analytical Geometric Asian Pricing). Under geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t / S_t = (r - q) dt + \sigma dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\) with \(n\) discrete observation dates \(t_i = i \frac{T}{n}\), the log geometric average \(\ln G_T\) is normally distributed \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_G, \sigma_G^2)\) with parameters:

\[ \mu_G = \ln S_0 + \left(r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) T \frac{n + 1}{2n}, \quad \sigma_G^2 = \sigma^2 T \frac{(n + 1)(2n + 1)}{6 n^2} \]

The exact no-arbitrage price of a geometric Asian call is:

\[ C_{\text{geom}} = e^{-r T} \left[ e^{\mu_G + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_G^2} \Phi(d_1) - K \Phi(d_2) \right] \]

where \(d_2 = \frac{\mu_G - \ln K}{\sigma_G}\) and \(d_1 = d_2 + \sigma_G\).

Proof. Expanding the log geometric average: \(\ln G_T = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \ln S_{t_i}\). Since \(\ln S_{t_i} = \ln S_0 + (r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2) t_i + \sigma W_{t_i}\), the expected value is:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\ln G_T] = \ln S_0 + \left(r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) \frac{T}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n i = \ln S_0 + \left(r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) T \frac{n + 1}{2n} = \mu_G \]

For the variance, \(\mathrm{Cov}(W_{t_i}, W_{t_j}) = \min(t_i, t_j) = \frac{T}{n} \min(i, j)\). Summing across all pairs \((i, j)\):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \min(i, j) = \sum_{k=1}^n k^2 + 2 \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} i = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} \]

Dividing by \(n^2\) and multiplying by \(\sigma^2 \frac{T}{n}\) yields \(\sigma_G^2 = \sigma^2 T \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6 n^2}\). Because \(G_T = \exp(\ln G_T)\) is log-normal, integrating \((G_T - K)^+\) against the Gaussian density of \(\ln G_T\) and discounting by \(e^{-r T}\) produces the stated Black–Scholes-style formula. \(\blacksquare\)

In Monte Carlo simulation, the arithmetic Asian payoff \(X = e^{-r T}(A_T - K)^+\) and geometric Asian payoff \(Y = e^{-r T}(G_T - K)^+\) share identical Gaussian innovations. Following Glasserman (2003), the controlled estimator is:

\[ X_{\text{cv}} = X - c^* \left( Y - \mathbb{E}[Y] \right), \quad c^* = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{\mathrm{Var}(Y)} \]

Because \(\mathrm{Corr}(X, Y) > 0.99\), the variance of the controlled estimator \(\mathrm{Var}(X_{\text{cv}}) = \mathrm{Var}(X)(1 - \rho_{X, Y}^2)\) is reduced by more than 99% compared to crude Monte Carlo.

3. Lookback Options & Extremum Distributions

While Asian options average price paths to reduce volatility, lookback options maximize volatility exposure by granting the holder the right to buy at the lowest historical price or sell at the highest historical price.

Definition 2.2 (Lookback Option). A lookback option is an option contract whose payoff depends on the optimal extremum (maximum or minimum) of the underlying asset price over the contract tenor \([0, T]\). The contract is structured as floating strike or fixed strike:

  • Floating-strike lookback call: Payoff is \(S_T - S_{\min}\), where \(S_{\min} = \min_{0 \le t \le T} S_t\).
  • Floating-strike lookback put: Payoff is \(S_{\max} - S_T\), where \(S_{\max} = \max_{0 \le t \le T} S_t\).
  • Fixed-strike lookback call: Payoff is \((S_{\max} - K)^+\).
  • Fixed-strike lookback put: Payoff is \((K - S_{\min})^+\).

Theorem 2.3 (Goldman, Sosin & Gatto Analytical Floating Lookback Call). Under continuous geometric Brownian motion with cost-of-carry \(b = r - q \ne 0\), the analytical price of a floating-strike lookback call with initial minimum \(S_{\min} \le S_0\) is given by:

\[ C_{\text{lookback}} = S_0 e^{-q T} \Phi(a_1) - S_{\min} e^{-r T} \Phi(a_2) + S_0 e^{-r T} \frac{\sigma^2}{2b} \left[ e^{b T} \Phi(a_1) - \left(\frac{S_0}{S_{\min}}\right)^{-2b/\sigma^2} \Phi(-a_3) \right] \]

where \(a_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0 / S_{\min}) + (b + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}}\), \(a_2 = a_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T}\), and \(a_3 = a_1 + \frac{2b}{\sigma^2} \sigma \sqrt{T}\).

"""Lab 2 · Averaging & Extremum Payoffs: Kemna-Vorst Asian Control Variates & Lookback Pricing.

Implements the Kemna & Vorst (1990) closed-form geometric Asian option pricer,
demonstrates its effectiveness as a high-precision control variate for arithmetic
Asian Monte Carlo simulation, and evaluates Goldman, Sosin & Gatto (1979)
continuous floating-strike lookbacks on seed 42 (SIMULATED).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Cumulative distribution function of standard normal distribution."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def geometric_asian_call_analytical(
    s0: float,
    k: float,
    t: float,
    r: float,
    q: float,
    sigma: float,
    n_obs: int,
) -> float:
    """Analytical Kemna & Vorst (1990) geometric Asian call price under discrete observation."""
    # Discrete observation times t_i = i * (T / n)
    mu = r - q - 0.5 * sigma * sigma
    mean_g = math.log(s0) + mu * t * (n_obs + 1.0) / (2.0 * n_obs)
    var_g = (sigma * sigma) * t * ((n_obs + 1.0) * (2.0 * n_obs + 1.0)) / (6.0 * n_obs * n_obs)
    std_g = math.sqrt(var_g)

    d2 = (mean_g - math.log(k)) / std_g
    d1 = d2 + std_g

    df = math.exp(-r * t)
    price = df * (math.exp(mean_g + 0.5 * var_g) * norm_cdf(d1) - k * norm_cdf(d2))
    return float(max(0.0, price))


def lookback_floating_call_analytical(
    s0: float,
    s_min: float,
    t: float,
    r: float,
    q: float,
    sigma: float,
) -> float:
    """Analytical Goldman, Sosin & Gatto (1979) floating-strike lookback call (S_T - S_min)."""
    if s_min > s0:
        raise ValueError("Minimum observed spot cannot exceed current spot")
    if t <= 0.0 or sigma <= 0.0:
        return max(0.0, s0 - s_min)

    b = r - q
    a1 = (math.log(s0 / s_min) + (b + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * t) / (sigma * math.sqrt(t))
    a2 = a1 - sigma * math.sqrt(t)

    if abs(b) < 1e-7:
        # Limit as b -> 0
        term1 = s0 * norm_cdf(a1) - s_min * norm_cdf(a2)
        term2 = (
            s0
            * sigma
            * math.sqrt(t)
            * (norm_cdf(a1) * (a1) + (1.0 / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)) * math.exp(-0.5 * a1 * a1))
        )
        return float(term1 + term2)

    power = -2.0 * b / (sigma * sigma)
    a3 = a1 + power * sigma * math.sqrt(t)

    term1 = s0 * math.exp(-q * t) * norm_cdf(a1)
    term2 = s_min * math.exp(-r * t) * norm_cdf(a2)
    term3 = (
        s0
        * math.exp(-r * t)
        * (sigma * sigma / (2.0 * b))
        * (math.exp(b * t) * norm_cdf(a1) - ((s0 / s_min) ** power) * norm_cdf(-a3))
    )
    return float(term1 - term2 + term3)


def simulate_asian_options(
    s0: float,
    k: float,
    t: float,
    r: float,
    q: float,
    sigma: float,
    n_obs: int,
    n_paths: int,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Monte Carlo simulation of arithmetic Asian with geometric control variate."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = t / float(n_obs)
    sqrt_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
    drift = (r - q - 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * dt

    z = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, n_obs))
    log_increments = drift + sigma * sqrt_dt * z
    log_paths = np.empty((n_paths, n_obs + 1), dtype=np.float64)
    log_paths[:, 0] = math.log(s0)
    np.cumsum(log_increments, axis=1, out=log_paths[:, 1:])
    log_paths[:, 1:] += log_paths[:, 0:1]

    # Exclude t=0 from averaging (standard average of discrete fixings t_1 ... t_n)
    fixings = np.exp(log_paths[:, 1:])
    arithmetic_avg = np.mean(fixings, axis=1)
    geometric_avg = np.exp(np.mean(log_paths[:, 1:], axis=1))

    df = math.exp(-r * t)
    payoffs_arith = df * np.maximum(arithmetic_avg - k, 0.0)
    payoffs_geom = df * np.maximum(geometric_avg - k, 0.0)

    # Standard crude Monte Carlo
    price_arith_crude = float(np.mean(payoffs_arith))
    var_crude = float(np.var(payoffs_arith, ddof=1))
    se_crude = math.sqrt(var_crude / n_paths)

    # Analytical geometric price
    exact_geom = geometric_asian_call_analytical(s0, k, t, r, q, sigma, n_obs)

    # Optimal control variate coefficient c* = Cov(X, Y) / Var(Y)
    cov_matrix = np.cov(payoffs_arith, payoffs_geom, ddof=1)
    cov_xy = cov_matrix[0, 1]
    var_y = cov_matrix[1, 1]
    c_star = cov_xy / var_y if var_y > 0.0 else 0.0

    # Controlled estimator
    payoffs_controlled = payoffs_arith - c_star * (payoffs_geom - exact_geom)
    price_arith_cv = float(np.mean(payoffs_controlled))
    var_cv = float(np.var(payoffs_controlled, ddof=1))
    se_cv = math.sqrt(var_cv / n_paths)
    var_reduction = (1.0 - var_cv / var_crude) * 100.0 if var_crude > 0.0 else 0.0

    return {
        "crude_price": price_arith_crude,
        "crude_se": se_crude,
        "cv_price": price_arith_cv,
        "cv_se": se_cv,
        "exact_geom": exact_geom,
        "c_star": float(c_star),
        "variance_reduction_pct": var_reduction,
    }


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    strike = 100.0
    maturity = 1.0
    rate = 0.05
    div_yield = 0.02
    vol = 0.20
    n_obs = 12  # Monthly fixings

    # 1. Asian option evaluation
    asian_res = simulate_asian_options(
        spot, strike, maturity, rate, div_yield, vol, n_obs=n_obs, n_paths=50000, seed=42
    )

    # 2. Lookback option evaluation
    lb_floating = lookback_floating_call_analytical(spot, spot, maturity, rate, div_yield, vol)

    print("=== Lab 2: Asian & Lookback Option Valuation ===")
    print(f"Exact Geometric Asian Call:        {asian_res['exact_geom']:.4f}")
    print(f"Arithmetic Asian (Crude MC):       {asian_res['crude_price']:.4f} (SE: {asian_res['crude_se']:.4f})")
    print(f"Arithmetic Asian (Control Variate):{asian_res['cv_price']:.4f} (SE: {asian_res['cv_se']:.4f})")
    print(f"Optimal CV Coefficient (c*):       {asian_res['c_star']:.4f}")
    print(f"Variance Reduction Achieved:       {asian_res['variance_reduction_pct']:.2f}%")
    print(f"Floating-Strike Lookback Call:     {lb_floating:.4f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

While analytical formulas for geometric Asians and continuous lookbacks provide elegant benchmarks, practical trading desks confront severe model breakdowns in real markets:

  • Fixing Window Manipulation: Asian options settle on official exchange closing fixes. In illiquid underlying markets, traders with large directional positions can exert predatory order pressure during the final 5 minutes of each fixing date, shifting the average price at low transaction cost.
  • Continuous vs. Discrete Lookback Discrepancy: Continuous lookback formulas assume continuous observation of the minimum or maximum. In reality, contracts monitor high/low prints or daily closes. Continuous formulas overestimate the expected extremum, overpricing discrete lookback options by 5% to 15% (Broadie, Glasserman & Kou 1999).
  • Curse of Dimensionality in PDE Solvers: Pricing an arithmetic Asian option by PDE requires introducing an auxiliary state variable \(I_t = \int_0^t S_u du\). This transforms a 1D parabolic PDE into a degenerate 2D PDE with zero diffusion in the \(I\) direction, creating convective instabilities and severe numerical dissipation.

Misconceptions

  1. An arithmetic Asian option can be priced accurately by simply substituting the geometric average's adjusted volatility into the Black–Scholes formula.

    Why it is wrong: The arithmetic average is strictly greater than the geometric average by Jensen's inequality (\(A_T \ge G_T\)). Relying on geometric parameters systematically underprices arithmetic Asian calls by 3% to 6%, exposing options desks to persistent arbitrage losses.

  2. A floating-strike lookback call is always in the money and therefore carries zero downside risk for the seller.

    Why it is wrong: The payoff \(S_T - S_{\min}\) is always non-negative, meaning the option holder always exercises. However, for the option seller, the payoff can be immense if the asset rallies after setting a low minimum, generating severe unhedged losses if dynamic delta-hedging cannot be executed continuously.

  3. Asian options have higher vega than vanilla European options because they depend on the volatility of the entire path.

    Why it is wrong: Averaging path prices dampens the variance of the terminal payoff (\(\mathrm{Var}(A_T) \approx \frac{1}{3}\mathrm{Var}(S_T)\) in continuous time). Because option sensitivity to volatility scales with the standard deviation of the terminal distribution, Asian options have strictly lower vega than vanilla options.

Problem Set

Mechanics

Calculate the geometric volatility \(\sigma_G\) and expected mean \(\mu_G\) for a geometric Asian option with initial spot \(S_0 = 100\), asset volatility \(\sigma = 30\%\), risk-free rate \(r = 6\%\), dividend yield \(q = 1\%\), maturity \(T = 1.0\) year, and \(n = 4\) quarterly observation dates.

Solution

Under Theorem 2.2, with \(n = 4\): \(\frac{n+1}{2n} = \frac{5}{8} = 0.625\), and \(\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6 n^2} = \frac{5 \times 9}{6 \times 16} = \frac{45}{96} \approx 0.46875\). Drift coefficient: \(r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 = 0.06 - 0.01 - 0.5(0.09) = 0.05 - 0.045 = 0.005\). The expected mean is \(\mu_G = \ln(100) + 0.005 \times 1.0 \times 0.625 = 4.60517 + 0.003125 = 4.608295\). The variance is \(\sigma_G^2 = (0.09) \times 1.0 \times 0.46875 = 0.0421875\), so \(\sigma_G = \sqrt{0.0421875} \approx 0.205396\) (approximately \(20.54\%\)).

In continuous time (\(n \to \infty\)), what fraction of the underlying asset volatility \(\sigma\) represents the continuous geometric Asian volatility \(\sigma_G\)?

Solution

Taking the limit of \(\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6 n^2}\) as \(n \to \infty\): \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2n^2 + 3n + 1}{6 n^2} = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}\). Therefore, \(\sigma_G^2 = \frac{1}{3} \sigma^2 T\), which implies that \(\sigma_G = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.57735 \times \sigma\). Continuous averaging reduces volatility by approximately \(42.26\%\).

A trader holds a portfolio consisting of a long arithmetic Asian call with strike \(K\) and a short geometric Asian call with identical strike \(K\). What is the minimum possible settlement value of this spread at maturity \(T\)?

Solution

The spread payoff is \((A_T - K)^+ - (G_T - K)^+\). By the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality, \(A_T \ge G_T\) holds pathwise. Since \(f(x) = (x - K)^+\) is weakly monotonic, \((A_T - K)^+ \ge (G_T - K)^+\) on every single path. Therefore, the spread payoff is always non-negative: \(\min(\text{Payoff}) = 0.0\). The spread is a model-free non-negative claim.

Proof

Prove that a floating-strike lookback put option \(S_{\max} - S_T\) can be statically decomposed into a fixed-strike lookback call and terminal forward positions under put-call parity.

Solution

Consider the identity \(S_{\max} - S_T = (S_{\max} - K) - (S_T - K)\). If we choose strike \(K\) such that \(K \le \min(S_t)\), then \(S_{\max} \ge K\) holds with probability 1, so \((S_{\max} - K)^+ = S_{\max} - K\). Therefore, \(S_{\max} - S_T = (S_{\max} - K)^+ - S_T + K\). Taking discounted expectations under \(\mathbb{Q}\): \(P_{\text{lookback}}^{\text{float}} = C_{\text{lookback}}^{\text{fixed}}(K) - S_0 e^{-q T} + K e^{-r T}\). This proves that floating-strike lookbacks and fixed-strike lookbacks are linked via an exact generalized put-call parity identity.

Prove that the optimal control variate coefficient \(c^* = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}\) minimizes the variance of the controlled Monte Carlo estimator \(X_{\text{cv}} = X - c(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])\).

Solution

The variance of \(X_{\text{cv}}\) is \(\mathrm{Var}(X - c(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])) = \mathrm{Var}(X) - 2 c \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) + c^2 \mathrm{Var}(Y)\). To minimize this quadratic function of \(c\), we differentiate with respect to \(c\) and set the derivative to zero: \(\frac{d}{dc}\mathrm{Var}(X_{\text{cv}}) = -2 \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) + 2 c \mathrm{Var}(Y) = 0 \implies c^* = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}\). The second derivative is \(2 \mathrm{Var}(Y) > 0\), confirming a global minimum. Substituting \(c^*\) back yields \(\mathrm{Var}(X_{\text{cv}}^*) = \mathrm{Var}(X) [1 - \rho_{X, Y}^2]\).

Show that the delta of a continuous arithmetic Asian call with respect to the running average \(A_t = \frac{1}{t}\int_0^t S_u du\) is strictly positive when the option is near maturity.

Solution

At time \(t < T\), the final average is \(A_T = \frac{t}{T} A_t + \frac{1}{T}\int_t^T S_u du\). As \(t \to T\), \(\frac{t}{T} \to 1\), so \(A_T \to A_t\). Differentiating the discounted expectation \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{-r(T-t)}(A_T - K)^+ \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) with respect to \(A_t\) yields \(e^{-r(T-t)} \frac{t}{T} \mathbb{Q}(A_T > K \mid \mathcal{F}_t)\). Because \(\frac{t}{T} > 0\) and the probability of finishing in the money is non-negative, the sensitivity \(\partial V / \partial A_t\) is strictly positive whenever there is non-zero probability of in-the-money settlement.

Empirical

In crude oil derivatives (e.g. Brent and WTI futures options), why do commercial airlines and refiners systematically trade Asian options rather than European vanilla options?

Solution

Airlines consume jet fuel continuously throughout the month, and refiners purchase crude barrels daily. A vanilla European option hedges only the price on a single expiration day, leaving the firm exposed to basis risk between daily cash purchases and the expiration settlement price. Asian options match the cash-flow consumption profile of industrial consumers, hedging average monthly procurement costs while costing substantially less premium due to volatility dampening.

Using the results from Lab 1, evaluate the computational efficiency gain of using the Kemna–Vorst control variate versus crude Monte Carlo simulation for arithmetic Asian calls.

Solution

From Lab 1, the crude Monte Carlo standard error is \(0.0362\), whereas the control variate standard error is \(0.0010\), achieving a variance reduction of \(99.92\%\). Because Monte Carlo standard error converges at rate \(\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})\), achieving the control variate accuracy of \(0.0010\) with crude simulation would require \((0.0362 / 0.0010)^2 \approx (36.2)^2 \approx 1310\) times more sample paths. The control variate yields an over 1300-fold computational speedup.

Explain why structured equity desks that sell lookback put options face severe discrete monitoring execution risks when hedging with daily closing prices.

Solution

In market crashes or sudden intraday gap rallies, high/low intraday extremes diverge substantially from closing prices. If the contract defines \(S_{\max}\) using intraday highs while the dealer delta-hedges only on daily closing prints, the dealer suffers unhedged basis slippage: the client exercises against the peak intraday price, while the dealer's rebalancing hedge captured only the lower closing level.

Research

How does Curran's (1994) conditioning method price arithmetic Asian options by conditioning on the geometric average?

Solution

Curran (1994) decomposes the arithmetic expectation by conditioning on the geometric average: \(\mathbb{E}[(A_T - K)^+] = \mathbb{E}[(A_T - K) \mathbf{1}_{G_T \ge \hat{K}}] + \mathbb{E}[(A_T - K)^+ \mathbf{1}_{G_T < \hat{K}}]\), where \(\hat{K}\) is chosen such that \(\mathbb{E}[A_T \mid G_T = \hat{K}] = K\). For \(G_T \ge \hat{K}\), \(A_T \ge K\) with near certainty, allowing the first term to be integrated analytically using conditional Gaussian expectations. The second term is a tiny residual tail probability that can be approximated by one-dimensional numerical quadrature, yielding near-exact analytical arithmetic Asian prices without Monte Carlo.

Analyze the Broadie, Glasserman & Kou (1999) discrete monitoring adjustment for lookback options. Why is the discrete lookback price related to the continuous price by a multiplicative factor?

Solution

Broadie, Glasserman & Kou (1999) show that discrete monitoring on grid \(\Delta t\) systematically underestimates continuous extrema because random walks fail to reach their continuous supremum. The discrete maximum relates to the continuous maximum by \(S_{\max}^{\text{discrete}} \approx S_{\max}^{\text{cont}} \exp(-\beta \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t})\) with \(\beta \approx 0.5826\). Consequently, a floating-strike lookback call is priced by adjusting the initial extreme values by the multiplicative factor \(\exp(\pm \beta \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t})\), providing a first-order accurate correction for discrete fixings.

How does local volatility skew (Gatheral 2006) impact Asian options relative to European vanilla options?

Solution

As analyzed by Gatheral (2006, Ch. 7), Asian option payoffs depend on integrated local volatility along the path \(\int_0^T \sigma_{\text{loc}}(S_u, u) du\). In the presence of negative skew, when spot drops, local volatility increases, but because earlier path points contribute to the average, the effective moneyness of an Asian option moves more sluggishly than terminal spot. Consequently, the implied volatility smile of an Asian option is substantially flatter than the European vanilla smile at the same maturity.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. Why does an arithmetic Asian call option price strictly below an otherwise identical vanilla European call option?

    Options

    Averaging the price path over time reduces the dispersion of the final settlement price. Since option payoffs are convex, Jensen's inequality guarantees that lower variance reduces the expected payout.

  2. Under the Black–Scholes model, why does a geometric Asian option have an exact analytical closed form?

    Options

    The geometric average is \(\exp(\frac{1}{n} \sum \ln S_{t_i})\). Because the sum of Gaussians is Gaussian, the geometric average is log-normal, allowing closed-form integration.

  3. In continuous time, what is the effective volatility of a geometric Asian option \(\sigma_G\) relative to underlying volatility \(\sigma\)?

    Options

    Integrating the Brownian covariance kernel \(\int_0^T \int_0^T \min(u, v) du dv = T^3 / 3\) yields \(\sigma_G^2 = \sigma^2 T / 3\), so \(\sigma_G = \sigma / \sqrt{3}\).

  4. How does a floating-strike lookback call payoff differ from a fixed-strike lookback call payoff?

    Options

    A floating-strike call allows the buyer to purchase at the historical minimum (\(S_T - S_{\min}\)), while a fixed-strike call pays the excess of the historical maximum over a predetermined strike \(K\).

  5. What optimal control variate coefficient \(c^*\) achieves maximum variance reduction when simulating arithmetic Asian options with geometric Asian control variates?

    Options

    Minimizing \(\mathrm{Var}(X - c(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y]))\) yields the standard OLS regression coefficient \(c^* = \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) / \mathrm{Var}(Y)\).

  6. Why are lookback options substantially more expensive than vanilla European options?

    Options

    A lookback option guarantees execution at the absolute extremum (minimum or maximum) of the path, eliminating market timing risk and commanding a high premium.

  7. How does discrete observation affect the market value of a floating-strike lookback call relative to its continuous theoretical price?

    Options

    Under discrete observation, the sampled minimum is higher than the continuous minimum (\(S_{\min}^{\text{discrete}} \ge S_{\min}^{\text{cont}}\)), reducing the payoff \(S_T - S_{\min}\) and lowering option value.

  8. What structural feature of Asian options protects commodity consumers against market manipulation on expiration day?

    Options

    Because the settlement price is an average of \(n\) dates, moving the settlement price significantly requires manipulating the price across many days, making predatory manipulation economically prohibitive.
