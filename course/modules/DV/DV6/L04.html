Dealer Gamma Positioning, Vanna/Volga & Pinning Mechanics

Derivative pricing assumes market makers can delta-hedge continuously without impacting underlying prices. In modern equity markets, the immense scale of retail option trading and structured note issuance means dealer hedging flows actively drive market liquidity and volatility. Measuring aggregate dealer gamma exposure reveals why markets pin to major strike prices on expiration days and how negative gamma cliffs trigger severe market dislocations.

  • Introduces: dealer-gamma-exposure
  • Prerequisites: autocallable, delta-hedging, option-greeks

1. Dealer Positioning Aggregation & Aggregate Gamma Exposure

In modern financial markets, options are intermediated by market-making dealer desks (e.g. Citadel Securities, Susquehanna, Jane Street, and global investment banks). Dealers provide liquidity by taking the opposite side of client order flow, dynamically delta-hedging their net inventory in the underlying cash or futures market.

When clients buy options (e.g. retail put buying for crash insurance), dealers sell options, inheriting short gamma positions. Conversely, when clients sell options (e.g. yield-harvesting covered call overwriting, cash-secured put selling, or autocallable structured note issuance), dealers buy options, inheriting long gamma positions.

Definition 4.1 (Dealer Gamma Exposure). Dealer gamma exposure (commonly denoted \(\mathrm{DGE}\) or \(\mathrm{GEX}\)) is the aggregate second-order dollar delta sensitivity of dealer option inventories across all active strikes and maturities with respect to a change in the underlying asset spot price:

\[ \mathrm{GEX}(S) = \sum_{i=1}^K \Gamma_{\text{dealer}, i}(S) \cdot S \cdot \Delta S_{\text{ref}} \cdot \text{Multiplier} \cdot \mathrm{OI}_i^{\text{dealer}} \]

Conventionally quoted in USD of delta rebalancing per 1% move in spot (\(\Delta S_{\text{ref}} = 0.01 S\)):

\[ \mathrm{GEX}_{\$1\%}(S) = 0.01 S^2 \sum_{i=1}^K \Gamma_{\text{dealer}, i}(S) \cdot \text{Multiplier} \cdot \mathrm{OI}_i^{\text{dealer}} \]

The sign of aggregate dealer gamma determines the market's endogenous volatility regime:

  • Positive Dealer Gamma (\(\mathrm{GEX} > 0\)): When spot rises, dealer delta increases; dealers must sell underlying shares to restore delta neutrality. When spot falls, dealer delta decreases; dealers must buy shares. This continuous buying of dips and selling of rallies provides counter-cyclical liquidity, dampening realized market volatility.
  • Negative Dealer Gamma (\(\mathrm{GEX} < 0\)): When spot rises, dealers must buy shares to cover short delta. When spot falls, dealers must sell shares to cover long delta. This pro-cyclical rebalancing amplifies market trends, creating momentum surges, market liquidity vacuums, and sharp volatility spikes.

2. Expiration Strike Pinning Mechanics

A striking empirical regular documented by Ni, Pearson & Poteshman (2005) is that stock prices cluster abnormally at active strike prices on option expiration dates.

Theorem 4.1 (Ni–Pearson–Poteshman Expiration Pinning Mechanics). Let open interest at strike \(K\) be heavily concentrated in long dealer gamma positions (\(\Gamma_{\text{dealer}} > 0\)). As time to maturity \(t \to T\), option gamma concentrates into a Dirac delta spike centered at \(K\). In a market with finite depth \(\Lambda > 0\), dynamic delta hedging of this long gamma inventory creates an endogenous restoring force towards \(K\):

\[ dS_t^{\text{net}} = dS_t^{\text{exogenous}} - \frac{\Gamma_{\text{dealer}}(S_t, t)}{\Lambda} dS_t^{\text{exogenous}} = \frac{1}{1 + \Gamma_{\text{dealer}}(S_t, t) / \Lambda} dS_t^{\text{exogenous}} \]

Consequently, local price volatility vanishes in the neighborhood of \(K\), causing underlying closing prices to pin to the strike on expiration day.

Proof sketch. Let exogenous non-option order flow generate an unconstrained price innovation \(dS_t^{\text{exo}} = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t\). If the price moves by \(dS_t\), the dealer's delta changes by \(d\Delta_{\text{dealer}} = \Gamma_{\text{dealer}} dS_t\). To maintain delta neutrality, the dealer submits a rebalancing order of \(-d\Delta_{\text{dealer}} = -\Gamma_{\text{dealer}} dS_t\) shares. In a Kyle-type linear price impact model with market depth parameter \(\Lambda\) (shares per dollar price change), the net price change is the sum of exogenous impact and dealer hedging impact: \(dS_t = dS_t^{\text{exo}} - \frac{\Gamma_{\text{dealer}}}{\Lambda} dS_t\). Collecting terms: \(dS_t (1 + \frac{\Gamma_{\text{dealer}}}{\Lambda}) = dS_t^{\text{exo}} \implies dS_t = \frac{dS_t^{\text{exo}}}{1 + \Gamma_{\text{dealer}} / \Lambda}\). As \(t \to T\), the Black–Scholes gamma \(\Gamma = \frac{e^{-q(T-t)} \phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{T-t}}\) diverges to \(+\infty\) at \(S = K\). For any spot near \(K\), \(\Gamma_{\text{dealer}} / \Lambda \to \infty\), forcing \(dS_t \to 0\). Away from \(K\), \(\Gamma \to 0\), so the asset moves freely. Thus, the asset can move freely towards \(K\), but upon entering the strike's high-gamma basin of attraction, price volatility is suppressed, trapping ('pinning') the stock at \(K\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 4.1 (Pinning Feedback Magnitude). Consider a stock at \(S_0 = 100\) with 25,000 net long contracts at the 100 strike on expiration Friday (\(T - t = 1\) day). The total dealer delta sensitivity is \(\Gamma_{\text{dealer}} \approx 150{,}000\) shares per dollar. If the limit order book depth is \(\Lambda = 100{,}000\) shares per dollar, the feedback dampening factor is \(\frac{1}{1 + 150{,}000 / 100{,}000} = \frac{1}{2.5} = 0.40\). Exogenous shocks are dampened by \(60\%\), pulling the closing price distribution into a high-density cluster at \(\$100.00\).

3. Structured Product Barrier Cliffs & Negative Gamma Inversion

In retail structured equity markets, billions of dollars of Phoenix autocallable notes share identical downside knock-in barriers (e.g. 60%–70% of initial index levels). This institutional concentration creates an extreme non-linear hazard: the negative gamma cliff.

When the index trades near its highs (e.g. \(S \approx S_0\)), the dealer who sold the autocallable note is long downside puts (since the client sold the put), providing positive gamma. Dealers buy dips and stabilize the market.

However, as the index falls toward the knock-in barrier \(B\), the probability of barrier breach surges. Because the barrier payoff contains a discontinuous step or steep delta transition from 0 to \(1/S_0\), the client's gamma turns positive, which forces the dealer's gamma to flip from positive to violently negative.

"""Lab 4 · Dealer Gamma Positioning, Vanna/Volga & Expiration Pinning Mechanics.

Aggregates dealer gamma exposure (GEX) across option strikes and structured product
barriers, and simulates the Ni, Pearson & Poteshman (2005) expiration strike pinning
dynamic driven by delta rebalancing feedback on seed 42 (SIMULATED).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def norm_pdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Probability density function of standard normal distribution."""
    return (1.0 / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)) * math.exp(-0.5 * x * x)


def bs_gamma(s: float, k: float, t: float, r: float, q: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    """Option gamma d^2V / dS^2 under Black-Scholes."""
    if t <= 0.0 or sigma <= 0.0 or s <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r - q + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * t) / (sigma * math.sqrt(t))
    return float(math.exp(-q * t) * norm_pdf(d1) / (s * sigma * math.sqrt(t)))


def calculate_dealer_gex(
    spot: float,
    strikes: list[float],
    dealer_net_contracts: list[float],
    t: float,
    r: float,
    q: float,
    sigma: float,
) -> float:
    """Calculate aggregate dealer gamma exposure (GEX in USD per 1% spot move)."""
    total_gex = 0.0
    for k, contracts in zip(strikes, dealer_net_contracts, strict=True):
        gamma_k = bs_gamma(spot, k, t, r, q, sigma)
        # 1 contract = 100 shares. GEX_USD_1pct = Gamma * Spot * (0.01 * Spot) * 100 * Contracts
        gex_k = gamma_k * (spot**2) * 0.01 * 100.0 * contracts
        total_gex += gex_k
    return total_gex


def simulate_expiration_pinning(
    s0: float,
    pin_strike: float,
    dealer_gamma_shares: float,
    market_depth: float,
    sigma: float,
    t_hours: float = 6.5,
    n_steps: int = 390,  # 1-minute steps over trading day
    n_sims: int = 5000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate stock price paths into expiration with and without dealer hedging feedback.

    Following Ni, Pearson & Poteshman (2005):
    Under long dealer gamma, dealer delta is Delta(S).
    If price moves dS, dealer rebalances -Gamma * dS shares.
    Market impact of dealer flow is: dS_hedging = -(Dealer_Gamma / Market_Depth) * dS_exogenous.
    Effective price return: dS_net = dS_exogenous / (1 + Dealer_Gamma / Market_Depth).
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = (t_hours / (252.0 * 6.5)) / float(n_steps)
    sqrt_dt = math.sqrt(dt)

    z = rng.standard_normal((n_sims, n_steps))

    # Without dealer hedging feedback (unpinned exogenous GBM)
    s_unpinned = np.empty((n_sims, n_steps + 1))
    s_unpinned[:, 0] = s0

    # With dealer hedging feedback (pinned)
    s_pinned = np.empty((n_sims, n_steps + 1))
    s_pinned[:, 0] = s0

    for step in range(n_steps):
        # Exogenous innovation
        dw = z[:, step] * sqrt_dt
        exo_shock = sigma * dw

        # Unpinned step
        s_u = s_unpinned[:, step]
        s_unpinned[:, step + 1] = s_u * (1.0 + exo_shock)

        # Pinned step: feedback strength depends on gamma near strike
        s_p = s_pinned[:, step]
        # Local dealer gamma concentrated near strike (bell curve profile around pin_strike)
        gamma_local = dealer_gamma_shares * np.exp(-0.5 * ((s_p - pin_strike) / 1.0) ** 2)
        feedback_dampening = 1.0 / (1.0 + gamma_local / market_depth)
        net_shock = exo_shock * feedback_dampening
        s_pinned[:, step + 1] = s_p * (1.0 + net_shock)

    terminal_unpinned = s_unpinned[:, -1]
    terminal_pinned = s_pinned[:, -1]

    # Fraction of paths closing within 0.25 USD of pin strike
    cluster_unpinned = float(np.mean(np.abs(terminal_unpinned - pin_strike) <= 0.25))
    cluster_pinned = float(np.mean(np.abs(terminal_pinned - pin_strike) <= 0.25))
    vol_unpinned = float(np.std(terminal_unpinned - s0))
    vol_pinned = float(np.std(terminal_pinned - s0))

    return {
        "cluster_unpinned": cluster_unpinned,
        "cluster_pinned": cluster_pinned,
        "terminal_vol_unpinned": vol_unpinned,
        "terminal_vol_pinned": vol_pinned,
    }


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    pin_strike = 100.0
    strikes = [90.0, 95.0, 100.0, 105.0, 110.0]
    # Positive indicates dealer long gamma (investors bought puts / sold covered calls)
    net_contracts = [5000.0, 12000.0, 25000.0, 15000.0, 4000.0]
    t = 1.0 / 252.0  # 1 day to expiration
    rate = 0.04
    div_yield = 0.01
    vol = 0.20

    # 1. Dealer Gamma Exposure (GEX) profile
    gex_at_spot = calculate_dealer_gex(spot, strikes, net_contracts, t, rate, div_yield, vol)
    gex_down = calculate_dealer_gex(spot * 0.98, strikes, net_contracts, t, rate, div_yield, vol)
    gex_up = calculate_dealer_gex(spot * 1.02, strikes, net_contracts, t, rate, div_yield, vol)

    # 2. Expiration pinning simulation
    dealer_gamma_shares = 150000.0
    market_depth = 100000.0
    sim_res = simulate_expiration_pinning(
        spot, pin_strike, dealer_gamma_shares, market_depth, vol, n_sims=10000, seed=42
    )

    print("=== Lab 4: Dealer Gamma Exposure (GEX) & Expiration Pinning ===")
    print(f"Dealer Net GEX at Spot ($100):     ${gex_at_spot:,.2f} per 1% move")
    print(f"Dealer Net GEX at Spot ($98):      ${gex_down:,.2f} per 1% move")
    print(f"Dealer Net GEX at Spot ($102):     ${gex_up:,.2f} per 1% move")
    print("\nExpiration Day Pinning Results (Ni, Pearson & Poteshman 2005):")
    print(f"  Unpinned Strike Clustering:      {sim_res['cluster_unpinned'] * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Dealer-Pinned Strike Clustering: {sim_res['cluster_pinned'] * 100.0:.2f}%")
    surge_pct = (sim_res["cluster_pinned"] - sim_res["cluster_unpinned"]) * 100.0
    print(f"  Clustering Probability Surge:    +{surge_pct:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Unpinned Realized Volatility:    {sim_res['terminal_vol_unpinned']:.4f}")
    print(f"  Dealer Pinned Realized Vol:      {sim_res['terminal_vol_pinned']:.4f}")


main()

4. Cross Greeks: Vanna, Volga & Volatility-Induced Flow Dynamics

In addition to spot gamma, exotic derivative books carry large cross-sensitivities between spot price and implied volatility:

  • Vanna (\(\partial^2 V / \partial S \partial \sigma = \partial \Delta / \partial \sigma = \partial \mathcal{V} / \partial S\)): Measures how option delta shifts when implied volatility changes. For out-of-the-money options, vanna is large. In market panics where spot crashes and volatility explodes (the leverage effect), negative vanna forces dealers to sell additional underlying shares even if spot does not move further.
  • Volga or Vomma (\(\partial^2 V / \partial \sigma^2 = \partial \mathcal{V} / \partial \sigma\)): Measures the convexity of option value with respect to volatility. Out-of-the-money options have high volga. When volatility surges, vega increases rapidly, forcing desks to re-hedge volatility exposure at escalating costs.

Under the Whalley & Wilmott (1997) framework, transaction costs and gamma exposure dictate optimal non-trading bands: attempting to hedge vanna and gamma continuously without regard to market bid-ask spreads drains trading desks through bid-ask friction bleed.

Where the Canon Breaks

Classical Black–Scholes delta hedging assumes infinite market depth, zero transaction costs, and perfectly continuous execution. In live market trading, these assumptions break down under dealer gamma crowding:

  • Crowded Hedging & Liquidity Cascades: When multiple algorithmic dealer desks hold identical short gamma positions (e.g. near autocallable knock-in barriers), their delta-hedging orders arrive simultaneously. If market depth is thin, the collective selling exceeds available bids, triggering a liquidity black hole and a discontinuous price gap.
  • Transaction Costs & Non-Trading Bands: Under Whalley & Wilmott (1997), bid-ask spreads make continuous rebalancing impossible. Dealers must allow delta to drift within an optimal bandwidth of width \(\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1/3})\). When spot moves rapidly through the barrier, the band is breached violently, forcing panic rebalancing.
  • Multi-Asset Cross-Gamma Breakdown: In multi-asset structured notes (e.g. worst-of baskets), cross-gamma \(\partial^2 V / \partial S_i \partial S_j\) couples the hedging of separate stocks. A price move in stock A requires rebalancing shares of stock B, transmitting shocks across non-fundamental equity pairs.

Misconceptions

  1. Option expiration pinning is caused by criminal market manipulation where hedge funds collude to force stocks onto strike prices.

    Why it is wrong: As proven by Ni, Pearson & Poteshman (2005), pinning is an endogenous, fully automated consequence of standard risk-neutral delta rebalancing by market makers. Long gamma positions naturally push prices toward the strike without any discretionary or collusive intent.

  2. A market maker whose book is delta-neutral carries zero market risk.

    Why it is wrong: Delta neutrality is purely instantaneous (\(\partial V / \partial S = 0\)). The desk retains second-order spot risk (gamma), volatility risk (vega), time decay (theta), cross-volatility risk (vanna), and vol-convexity risk (volga). A sudden market gap or volatility shock inflicts immense P&L swings on a delta-neutral book.

  3. Structured product issuers maintain positive gamma across all market levels because they are long downside options.

    Why it is wrong: While issuers are long puts when spot is high, the exotic barrier condition inverts the Greek signs. As spot drops into the immediate vicinity of the knock-in barrier, dealer gamma collapses into extreme negative territory, transforming issuers into destabilizing sellers.

Problem Set

Mechanics

A market maker is net short 10,000 contracts of European call options on a stock trading at \(S = \$50\). Each contract is for 100 shares. The Black–Scholes gamma of each option is \(\Gamma = 0.040\). Calculate the dealer's total dollar Gamma Exposure (\(\mathrm{GEX}_{\$1\%}\)) for a 1% move in spot.

Solution

Because the dealer is short the options, the dealer's gamma per share is \(-0.040\). The total number of option shares is \(10{,}000 \times 100 = 1{,}000{,}000\) shares. Under Definition 4.1, \(\mathrm{GEX}_{\$1\%} = 0.01 \times S^2 \times \Gamma_{\text{dealer}} \times \text{Shares}\). Substituting: \(\mathrm{GEX}_{\$1\%} = 0.01 \times (50)^2 \times (-0.040) \times 1{,}000{,}000 = 0.01 \times 2500 \times (-40{,}000) = 25 \times (-40{,}000) = -\$1{,}000{,}000\). The dealer must sell \(\$1{,}000{,}000\) worth of stock (20,000 shares) per 1% decline in spot, or buy \(\$1{,}000{,}000\) worth of stock per 1% rise.

If the market depth is \(\Lambda = 200{,}000\) shares per dollar price change and the dealer's net gamma position is \(\Gamma_{\text{dealer}} = +300{,}000\) shares per dollar, calculate the price dampening feedback factor \(\frac{1}{1 + \Gamma / \Lambda}\).

Solution

Under Theorem 4.1, the effective net price change relative to exogenous shocks is \(\frac{1}{1 + \Gamma_{\text{dealer}} / \Lambda}\). Substituting the given parameters: \(\frac{\Gamma_{\text{dealer}}}{\Lambda} = \frac{300{,}000}{200{,}000} = 1.50\). The dampening factor is \(\frac{1}{1 + 1.50} = \frac{1}{2.50} = 0.40\). Net price innovations are compressed to \(40\%\) of their exogenous magnitude, reducing realized volatility by \(60\%\).

A trader evaluates an out-of-the-money put option with strike \(K = 90\) on a stock trading at \(S = 100\). The option has delta \(\Delta = -0.15\) and vanna \(\frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma} = -0.80\). If implied volatility increases by \(\Delta \sigma = +5\%\) while spot remains unchanged, calculate the new option delta.

Solution

Using the first-order Taylor expansion for delta: \(\Delta_{\text{new}} \approx \Delta_{\text{old}} + \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma} \Delta \sigma\). With \(\Delta_{\text{old}} = -0.15\), vanna \(= -0.80\), and \(\Delta \sigma = 0.05\): \(\Delta_{\text{new}} \approx -0.15 + (-0.80 \times 0.05) = -0.15 - 0.040 = -0.190\). The option delta becomes more negative (moving from \(-0.15\) to \(-0.19\)), forcing a delta-hedging dealer who sold the put to buy more underlying stock as volatility rises.

Proof

Prove that for an option satisfying the Black–Scholes–Merton PDE, vanna can be expressed symmetrically as \(\frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{V}}{\partial S}\) and derive its analytical formula under constant volatility.

Solution

By Schwarz's theorem on the equality of mixed partial derivatives, if \(V(S, \sigma)\) is twice continuously differentiable, \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S \partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right) = \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma}\), and \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \sigma \partial S} = \frac{\partial}{\partial S}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial \sigma}\right) = \frac{\partial \mathcal{V}}{\partial S}\). Since \(\mathcal{V} = S e^{-q T} \phi(d_1) \sqrt{T}\), we differentiate with respect to \(S\): \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{V}}{\partial S} = e^{-q T} \phi(d_1) \sqrt{T} + S e^{-q T} \phi'(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} \sqrt{T}\). Since \(\phi'(d_1) = -d_1 \phi(d_1)\) and \(\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} = \frac{1}{S \sigma \sqrt{T}}\), we obtain \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{V}}{\partial S} = -e^{-q T} \phi(d_1) \frac{d_2}{\sigma}\). This confirms that vanna changes sign at \(d_2 = 0\).

Prove that when aggregate dealer gamma is negative (\(\Gamma_{\text{dealer}} < 0\)) and its magnitude exceeds market depth (\(|\Gamma_{\text{dealer}}| > \Lambda\)), the price dynamics become unstable and discontinuous.

Solution

Under Theorem 4.1, the net price change is \(dS_t = \frac{dS_t^{\text{exo}}}{1 + \Gamma_{\text{dealer}} / \Lambda}\). When \(\Gamma_{\text{dealer}} < 0\), let \(\gamma = |\Gamma_{\text{dealer}}| / \Lambda > 0\), so \(1 + \Gamma_{\text{dealer}} / \Lambda = 1 - \gamma\). As \(\gamma \to 1^-\), the multiplier \(\frac{1}{1 - \gamma} \to +\infty\), meaning that an infinitesimal exogenous sell order triggers an infinitely large net price collapse. If \(\gamma > 1\), the denominator turns negative, implying that a sell order would cause a price rise under continuous linear clearing—a physical impossibility that triggers an instantaneous market freeze, order book void, and discontinuous downward price jump until new non-linear liquidity is found.

Show that the maximum gamma of a European option under Black–Scholes occurs at strike \(K = S \exp((r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T)\) and scales as \(\mathcal{O}(1 / \sqrt{T})\) as \(T \to 0\).

Solution

The Black–Scholes gamma is \(\Gamma = \frac{e^{-q T} \phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{T}}\). The standard Gaussian density \(\phi(d_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-d_1^2 / 2}\) achieves its global maximum when \(d_1 = 0\). Setting \(d_1 = 0\): \(\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T = 0 \implies K = S \exp((r - q + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T)\). At this peak, \(\phi(0) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}\), so \(\Gamma_{\max} = \frac{e^{-q T}}{S \sigma \sqrt{2\pi T}} = \mathcal{O}(1 / \sqrt{T})\). As expiration approaches (\(T \to 0\)), \(\Gamma_{\max} \to +\infty\), proving that gamma concentrates into an infinite spike at the at-the-money strike.

Empirical

Cite the empirical sample, methodology, and primary quantitative finding of Ni, Pearson & Poteshman (2005) regarding expiration strike pinning.

Solution

Ni, Pearson & Poteshman (2005) examined all US exchange-listed equity options from 1996 through 2002 across thousands of underlying stocks on expiration dates versus non-expiration days. Using non-parametric kernel regressions and chi-squared tests of price clustering, they found that on expiration Fridays, stock prices close exactly at an active option strike with a frequency that exceeds non-expiration control days by an economically and statistically significant margin (\(p < 0.001\)). The clustering effect was shown to be heavily concentrated in stocks with high dealer open interest and high gamma.

Explain how the massive growth of 0DTE (zero days-to-expiration) S&P 500 index options since 2022 has altered the intraday dealer gamma profile.

Solution

0DTE options represent over 45% of total SPX option volume. Because \(T \le 1\) day, 0DTE options possess extreme localized gamma. Research

How does Whalley & Wilmott's (1997) asymptotic model establish optimal delta hedging corridors under proportional transaction costs?

Solution

Whalley & Wilmott (1997) formulate the option hedging problem with proportional transaction costs \(\epsilon\) and risk aversion \(\gamma_{\text{risk}}\) as a free-boundary Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman variational inequality. By asymptotic expansion in powers of \(\epsilon^{1/3}\), they prove that the optimal hedging policy is a non-trading band centered at the Black–Scholes delta \(\Delta_{\text{BS}}\) with half-width \(w = \left(\frac{3 \epsilon S \Gamma^2}{2 \gamma_{\text{risk}}}\right)^{1/3}\). The trader trades only when portfolio delta touches the boundary of the band, rebalancing just enough to stay inside, balancing hedging tracking error against transaction friction.

What is the 'volatility clustering reflection' hypothesis, and how does dealer gamma explain the asymmetric volatility leverage effect in equity markets?

Solution

In equity indices, customer positioning is structurally net long out-of-the-money puts (downside crash insurance) and net short out-of-the-money calls (covered call overwriting). This asymmetry means that as spot rises, dealers remain net long gamma (stabilizing the market and dampening volatility). However, as spot crashes, index levels penetrate deep out-of-the-money customer puts and structured product knock-in barriers, flipping aggregate dealer positioning to net short gamma. The resulting pro-cyclical rebalancing magnifies downward selloffs, generating the well-known asymmetric volatility leverage effect endogenously from derivatives positioning.

Formulate the multi-asset cross-gamma feedback equation for an equity index options market maker holding single-stock and index options simultaneously.

Solution

Let an index \(I = \sum_{i=1}^M w_i S_i\). An option desk holding index options and single-stock options has cross-gamma \(\Gamma_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S_i \partial S_j} = \Gamma_{\text{index}} w_i w_j + \delta_{ij} \Gamma_{\text{stock}, i}\). When stock \(i\) moves by \(dS_i\), the dealer must rebalance shares not only in stock \(i\) but across all other stocks \(j \ne i\): \(d\Delta_j = \Gamma_{ij} dS_i = \Gamma_{\text{index}} w_i w_j dS_i\). This cross-hedging flow creates artificial co-movement, transmitting idiosyncratic price shocks from single mega-cap stocks into systematic index-wide liquidation pressure.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What market condition causes option expiration strike pinning according to Ni, Pearson & Poteshman (2005)?

    Options

    Long dealer gamma requires market makers to buy when the stock declines and sell when the stock rallies. As expiration approaches, this stabilizing restoring force traps the stock at the strike.

  2. When aggregate Dealer Gamma Exposure (\(\mathrm{GEX}\)) is negative, how do dealer delta-hedging flows affect realized market volatility?

    Options

    Short gamma forces dealers to rebalance pro-cyclically: selling as prices fall and buying as prices rise, accelerating market trends and expanding volatility.

  3. What does the cross Greek 'vanna' (\(\partial^2 V / \partial S \partial \sigma\)) measure?

    Options

    By Schwarz's theorem, vanna is the mixed partial derivative \(\partial (\partial V / \partial S) / \partial \sigma = \partial (\partial V / \partial \sigma) / \partial S\), linking delta changes to volatility shocks.

  4. Why does dealer gamma flip from positive to negative as an index approaches an autocallable knock-in barrier?

    Options

    Near the knock-in barrier, the client's downside put delta jumps from 0 to \(\approx 1/S_0\), creating a large positive gamma spike for the client. Because the dealer is short the note, the dealer inherits severe negative gamma.

  5. Under Whalley & Wilmott (1997), how does the optimal non-trading delta bandwidth scale with proportional transaction cost \(\epsilon\)?

    Options

    The asymptotic expansion of the HJB equation under proportional transaction costs yields an optimal delta tolerance corridor of width \(\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1/3})\).

  6. What happens to maximum European option gamma as time to expiration \(T \to 0\)?

    Options

    From \(\Gamma = \frac{e^{-q T}\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{T}}\), evaluating at ATM (\(d_1 = 0\)) shows that peak gamma explodes as \(1/\sqrt{T}\) as \(T \to 0\).

  7. Why does retail covered call overwriting contribute to positive dealer gamma in calm market regimes?

    Options

    When retail or institutional overwriting funds sell calls to harvest yield, market makers buy the calls. Long calls provide positive gamma, dampening market volatility.

  8. What is volga (or vomma)?

    Options

    Volga (vomma) measures the convexity of an option with respect to volatility (\(\partial \mathcal{V} / \partial \sigma = \partial^2 V / \partial \sigma^2\)).
