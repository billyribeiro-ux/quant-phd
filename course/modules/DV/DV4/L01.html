Trinomial Trees & Explicit Finite Difference Equivalence

Discrete recombining binomial trees exhibit high-frequency saw-tooth oscillations when the strike price falls between adjacent terminal lattice nodes. Adding a horizontal continuation branch creates a trinomial lattice that matches both drift and variance while providing an extra degree of freedom to match higher moments or guarantee non-negative transition probabilities. This lesson demonstrates that trinomial lattices and explicit finite difference schemes are not separate pricing methodologies, but mathematically identical algebraic operators applied to discrete log-price grids.

  • Introduces: trinomial tree
  • Prerequisites: Black–Scholes–Merton PDE, explicit finite differences, numerical stability

1. Geometry of the Recombining Trinomial Lattice

Consider the risk-neutral geometric Brownian motion diffusion for an underlying asset price \(S_t\) under measure \(\mathbb{Q}\):

\[ \mathrm{d}S_t = (r - q) S_t \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t \mathrm{d}W_t^\mathbb{Q} \]

where \(r\) is the continuously compounded risk-free rate, \(q\) is the continuous dividend yield, and \(\sigma > 0\) is volatility. Setting \(x_t = \ln(S_t)\), Itô's lemma gives the constant-coefficient drift diffusion:

\[ \mathrm{d}x_t = \left(r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) \mathrm{d}t + \sigma \mathrm{d}W_t^\mathbb{Q} = \nu \mathrm{d}t + \sigma \mathrm{d}W_t^\mathbb{Q} \]

where \(\nu \equiv r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\). Over a discrete time increment \(\Delta t = T / N\), the log-price increment \(\Delta x = x_{t+\Delta t} - x_t\) has exact mean \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta x] = \nu \Delta t\) and variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\Delta x) = \sigma^2 \Delta t\).

Definition 1.1 · Trinomial Tree

A trinomial tree is a discrete-time, discrete-state Markov chain approximation to an underlying asset price diffusion on a uniform log-price grid with spatial step \(\Delta x > 0\) and time step \(\Delta t > 0\). From node \((n, j)\) with log-price \(x_{n,j} = x_0 + j \Delta x\), the process transitions to exactly three child nodes at time step \(n+1\):

  1. An up-branch to \((n+1, j+1)\) with log-price \(x_{n,j} + \Delta x\) and transition probability \(p_u\);
  2. A middle branch to \((n+1, j)\) with log-price \(x_{n,j}\) and transition probability \(p_m\);
  3. A down-branch to \((n+1, j-1)\) with log-price \(x_{n,j} - \Delta x\) and transition probability \(p_d\).

The tree recombines because an up-step followed by a down-step returns to the identical log-price node as two middle steps: \((x + \Delta x) - \Delta x = x + 0\).

At time step \(n\), the lattice contains exactly \(2n + 1\) distinct nodes indexed by \(j \in \{-n, -n+1, \dots, n-1, n\}\). The total number of nodes in an \(N\)-step trinomial tree is \((N+1)^2\), scaling as \(O(N^2)\), in contrast to non-recombining trees that scale exponentially as \(3^N\).

2. Moment Matching and Probability Derivation

Following Boyle (1977), the transition probabilities \((p_u, p_m, p_d)\) are determined by equating the discrete lattice moments to the true continuous-time moments over interval \(\Delta t\):

  1. Total probability axiom: \[ p_u + p_m + p_d = 1 \]
  2. First moment (drift matching): \[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta x] = p_u (\Delta x) + p_m (0) + p_d (-\Delta x) = (p_u - p_d) \Delta x = \nu \Delta t \]
  3. Second moment (variance matching): \[ \mathbb{E}[(\Delta x)^2] = p_u (\Delta x)^2 + p_m (0)^2 + p_d (-\Delta x)^2 = (p_u + p_d) \Delta x^2 = \sigma^2 \Delta t + (\nu \Delta t)^2 \]
Theorem 1.1 · Boyle Trinomial Probabilities

The unique transition probabilities satisfying total probability, drift matching, and variance matching on a uniform spatial grid \(\Delta x\) are:

\[ p_u = \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2}{2 \Delta x^2} + \frac{\nu \Delta t}{2 \Delta x} \] \[ p_d = \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2}{2 \Delta x^2} - \frac{\nu \Delta t}{2 \Delta x} \] \[ p_m = 1 - (p_u + p_d) = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2}{\Delta x^2} \]

Proof

Subtracting the drift equation from the second moment equation yields:

\[ p_u + p_d = \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2}{\Delta x^2} \]

From total probability, \(p_m = 1 - (p_u + p_d)\), directly confirming the formula for \(p_m\). Adding and subtracting the first moment relation \((p_u - p_d) = \frac{\nu \Delta t}{\Delta x}\) isolates \(p_u\) and \(p_d\):

\[ 2 p_u = (p_u + p_d) + (p_u - p_d) = \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2}{\Delta x^2} + \frac{\nu \Delta t}{\Delta x} \] \[ 2 p_d = (p_u + p_d) - (p_u - p_d) = \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2}{\Delta x^2} - \frac{\nu \Delta t}{\Delta x} \]

Dividing by 2 produces the stated expressions.

3. Optimal Mesh Spacing and Numerical Stability

A valid probability distribution requires \(0 < p_u < 1\), \(0 < p_m < 1\), and \(0 < p_d < 1\). The middle probability \(p_m = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2}{\Delta x^2}\) remains positive if and only if:

\[ \Delta x > \sqrt{\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2} \]

Choosing \(\Delta x\) too small drives \(p_m < 0\), creating catastrophic negative probabilities and numerical divergence. Conversely, setting \(\Delta x\) too large drives \(p_u\) and \(p_d\) to zero, degenerating the lattice.

Theorem 1.2 · The \(\sqrt{3}\) Spatial Rule

Setting the spatial step to:

\[ \Delta x = \sigma \sqrt{3 \Delta t} \]

minimizes fourth-order truncation error, matches the kurtosis of the continuous Gaussian distribution, and guarantees positive probabilities whenever \(\Delta t < \frac{4\sigma^2}{3\nu^2}\).

Proof

Substitute \(\Delta x^2 = 3 \sigma^2 \Delta t\) into the probability formulas:

\[ p_m = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2}{3 \sigma^2 \Delta t} = \frac{2}{3} - \frac{\nu^2 \Delta t}{3 \sigma^2} \] \[ p_u = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{\nu^2 \Delta t}{6 \sigma^2} + \frac{\nu \sqrt{\Delta t}}{2 \sigma \sqrt{3}} \] \[ p_d = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{\nu^2 \Delta t}{6 \sigma^2} - \frac{\nu \sqrt{\Delta t}}{2 \sigma \sqrt{3}} \]

As \(\Delta t \to 0\), the probabilities approach the symmetric limits \(p_m \to 2/3\) and \(p_u, p_d \to 1/6\). The central probability \(p_m > 0\) requires \(\Delta t < \frac{2\sigma^2}{\nu^2}\), and \(p_d > 0\) requires \(\frac{\nu \sqrt{\Delta t}}{2 \sigma \sqrt{3}} < \frac{1}{6} + O(\Delta t)\), which holds for \(\Delta t < \frac{4 \sigma^2}{3 \nu^2}\).

4. Exact Algebraic Equivalence to Explicit Finite Differences

The continuous-time pricing function \(V(t, x)\) satisfies the log-spot Black–Scholes PDE:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \nu \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} - r V = 0 \]

Let \(V_j^n \approx V(n \Delta t, x_0 + j \Delta x)\). Approximating the derivatives using forward time differences and central spatial differences gives:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \approx \frac{V_j^{n+1} - V_j^n}{\Delta t} \] \[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} \approx \frac{V_{j+1}^{n+1} - V_{j-1}^{n+1}}{2 \Delta x} \] \[ \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} \approx \frac{V_{j+1}^{n+1} - 2 V_j^{n+1} + V_{j-1}^{n+1}}{\Delta x^2} \]

Substituting into the PDE and discounting back from step \(n+1\) to step \(n\):

\[ V_j^n = e^{-r \Delta t} \left[ \left(\frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t}{2 \Delta x^2} + \frac{\nu \Delta t}{2 \Delta x}\right) V_{j+1}^{n+1} + \left(1 - \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t}{\Delta x^2}\right) V_j^{n+1} + \left(\frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t}{2 \Delta x^2} - \frac{\nu \Delta t}{2 \Delta x}\right) V_{j-1}^{n+1} \right] \]

Comparing the coefficients of \(V_{j+1}^{n+1}\), \(V_j^{n+1}\), and \(V_{j-1}^{n+1}\) with \(p_u\), \(p_m\), and \(p_d\) reveals that backward induction on the trinomial tree is algebraically identical to the explicit finite difference discretization of the parabolic PDE up to \(O(\Delta t^2)\) drift terms.

Lab 1 · Trinomial lattice convergence and stability conditions

We construct a recombining trinomial tree pricer on SIMULATED market parameters, verify that moment matching holds across step counts N in {25, 50, 100, 200}, and confirm linear O(Delta t) error convergence toward the analytical Black–Scholes benchmark.

"""Lab 1 (L01): Trinomial lattice convergence and stability conditions.

SIMULATED market parameters under geometric Brownian motion.
Prices a European call option across tree depths N in {25, 50, 100, 200},
verifies transition probability positivity, and calculates empirical convergence to BSM.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def bsm_call_price(spot: float, strike: float, rate: float, vol: float, maturity: float) -> float:
    sqrt_t = math.sqrt(maturity)
    d1 = (math.log(spot / strike) + (rate + 0.5 * vol * vol) * maturity) / (vol * sqrt_t)
    d2 = d1 - vol * sqrt_t
    cdf_d1 = 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(d1 / math.sqrt(2.0)))
    cdf_d2 = 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(d2 / math.sqrt(2.0)))
    return float(spot * cdf_d1 - strike * math.exp(-rate * maturity) * cdf_d2)


def trinomial_call(spot: float, strike: float, rate: float, vol: float, maturity: float, n_steps: int) -> float:
    dt = maturity / n_steps
    dx = vol * math.sqrt(3.0 * dt)
    u = math.exp(dx)
    1.0 / u
    df = math.exp(-rate * dt)
    nu = (rate - 0.5 * vol * vol) * dt

    pu = 1.0 / 6.0 + 0.5 * (nu * nu / (dx * dx) + nu / dx)
    pm = 2.0 / 3.0 - (nu * nu / (dx * dx))
    pd = 1.0 / 6.0 + 0.5 * (nu * nu / (dx * dx) - nu / dx)

    # Assert stability: probabilities strictly within (0, 1)
    if not (0.0 < pu < 1.0 and 0.0 < pm < 1.0 and 0.0 < pd < 1.0):
        raise ValueError(f"Stability violated: pu={pu:.4f}, pm={pm:.4f}, pd={pd:.4f}")

    indices = np.arange(-n_steps, n_steps + 1)
    s_terminal = spot * np.exp(indices * dx)
    v = np.maximum(s_terminal - strike, 0.0)

    for k in range(n_steps - 1, -1, -1):
        v = df * (pu * v[2 : 2 * k + 3] + pm * v[1 : 2 * k + 2] + pd * v[0 : 2 * k + 1])

    return float(v[0])


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    strike = 100.0
    rate = 0.05
    vol = 0.20
    maturity = 1.0

    bsm_ref = bsm_call_price(spot, strike, rate, vol, maturity)
    print(f"Analytical BSM benchmark: {bsm_ref:.6f}")

    steps = [25, 50, 100, 200]
    prices: list[float] = []
    errors: list[float] = []

    for n in steps:
        p = trinomial_call(spot, strike, rate, vol, maturity, n)
        err = abs(p - bsm_ref)
        prices.append(p)
        errors.append(err)
        print(f"N = {n:3d} | Price = {p:.6f} | Error = {err:.6f}")

    # Empirical convergence order estimate
    log_steps = [math.log(n) for n in steps]
    log_errs = [math.log(e) for e in errors]
    slope = -(log_errs[-1] - log_errs[0]) / (log_steps[-1] - log_steps[0])
    print(f"Empirical convergence order: {slope:.2f}")


main()

N =  25 | Price = 10.374792 | Error = 0.075792
N =  50 | Price = 10.412879 | Error = 0.037705
N = 100 | Price = 10.431777 | Error = 0.018807
N = 200 | Price = 10.441191 | Error = 0.009392
Empirical convergence order: 1.00

The empirical convergence order is 1.00, demonstrating that the standard trinomial tree converges linearly in time step Delta t, eliminating the saw-tooth parity oscillations typical of binomial models.

Where the canon breaks

Standard textbook presentations assert that trinomial trees converge monotonically to continuous-time prices. In production quantitative trading, this claim fails in two critical regimes:

  1. Barrier and Touch Contracts: When pricing barrier options, if the barrier level does not align with a discrete lattice layer \(j \Delta x\), the effective barrier location shifts by up to \(\Delta x / 2\). This causes spatial discretization errors of order \(O(\sqrt{\Delta t})\) that dominate the second-order interior accuracy. Adaptive mesh methods are required to place a grid line directly on the barrier.
  2. Extreme Volatility Regimes: For high-drift or short-maturity underlyings where \(\nu^2 \Delta t / \sigma^2\) becomes large, the down-probability \(p_d\) becomes negative unless \(\Delta x\) is expanded. Expanding \(\Delta x\) coarsens spatial resolution, worsening discrete hedging Greek estimates.

Misconceptions

  1. Trinomial trees always converge faster than binomial trees without tuning spatial mesh spacing.

    Reality: If the spatial mesh spacing is set to \(\Delta x = \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}\) instead of \(\sigma \sqrt{3 \Delta t}\), the middle probability \(p_m\) drops to zero, reducing the trinomial tree to a two-branch binomial tree. Without setting \(\Delta x = \sigma \sqrt{3 \Delta t}\), fourth-moment (kurtosis) matching is lost, and the asymptotic pricing error remains first-order \(O(\Delta t)\) rather than achieving minimized discretization error.

  2. Trinomial trees are unconditionally stable regardless of the time step size \(\Delta t\).

    Reality: Because backward induction on a trinomial tree is mathematically isomorphic to an explicit finite difference scheme, stability is strictly conditional on the CFL-type constraint \(\Delta t \le \frac{\Delta x^2}{\sigma^2}\). If \(\Delta t\) is chosen too large relative to \(\Delta x\), transition probabilities \(p_u\) or \(p_d\) become negative, violating the maximum principle and producing divergent spurious oscillations.

  3. Lattice methods can be seamlessly applied to high-dimensional basket options with ten or more underlying assets.

    Reality: Recombining trinomial trees suffer from an exponential curse of dimensionality. For \(d\) correlated underlying assets, the branching factor at each time step is \(3^d\) and the state space grows as \(O(N^d)\). While highly efficient in one or two dimensions, lattice methods become computationally intractable beyond three dimensions, where Monte Carlo and sparse-grid PDE techniques dominate.

Problem set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A trader sets up a trinomial tree for a stock with \(S_0 = 100\), \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(r = 0.05\), \(q = 0\), and maturity \(T = 1\) year with \(N = 100\) steps. Using the standard Hull–White rule \(\Delta x = \sigma \sqrt{3 \Delta t}\), calculate \(\Delta t\), \(\Delta x\), and the three transition probabilities \(p_u, p_m, p_d\).

Solution

With \(N = 100\) steps over \(T = 1.0\), the time increment is \(\Delta t = 1.0 / 100 = 0.01\) year. The spatial grid spacing is \(\Delta x = 0.20 \times \sqrt{3 \times 0.01} = 0.20 \times \sqrt{0.03} = 0.20 \times 0.173205 = 0.034641\). The drift in log space is \(\nu = r - q - 0.5 \sigma^2 = 0.05 - 0.02 = 0.03\). Computing \(\nu \Delta t = 0.03 \times 0.01 = 0.0003\) and \(\Delta x^2 = 3 \times 0.04 \times 0.01 = 0.0012\). Thus, \(p_m = 1 - (\sigma^2 \Delta t + \nu^2 \Delta t^2) / \Delta x^2 = 1 - (0.0004 + 0.00000009) / 0.0012 \approx 1 - 0.3334 = 0.6666\). The up-probability is \(p_u = 0.5 \times (0.3334) + 0.0003 / (2 \times 0.034641) = 0.1667 + 0.0043 = 0.1710\). The down-probability is \(p_d = 0.1667 - 0.0043 = 0.1624\). All three probabilities sum to 1.0000 and lie strictly in \((0, 1)\).

Compute the total number of distinct log-price nodes across all time steps in a recombining trinomial tree with \(N = 50\) steps, and compare this to the total number of paths in a non-recombining 3-branch tree of the same depth.

Solution

At time step \(n \in \{0, 1, \dots, N\}\), the recombining trinomial tree has \(2n + 1\) nodes. The total node count across all steps is the arithmetic series sum \(\sum_{n=0}^N (2n + 1) = (N + 1)^2\). For \(N = 50\), the total number of nodes is \(51^2 = 2,601\) nodes. In contrast, a non-recombining ternary tree branches threefold at every step, yielding \(3^{50} \approx 7.18 \times 10^{23}\) distinct leaf nodes at expiration. Recombination reduces the computational complexity from an impossible \(O(3^N)\) to a tractable \(O(N^2)\).

Suppose a practitioner accidentally sets the spatial grid spacing to \(\Delta x = \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}\) in Boyle's trinomial tree formulas. Compute the resulting middle probability \(p_m\) when \(\nu = 0\), and explain why this causes the model to collapse to a standard binomial tree.

Solution

Under \(\nu = 0\), Boyle's formula for the middle probability is \(p_m = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t}{\Delta x^2}\). Substituting \(\Delta x^2 = \sigma^2 \Delta t\) yields \(p_m = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t}{\sigma^2 \Delta t} = 1 - 1 = 0\). The middle branch has zero probability, leaving \(p_u = 0.5\) and \(p_d = 0.5\). The three-branch structure completely vanishes, degenerating into the symmetric Cox–Ross–Rubinstein binomial lattice without a horizontal continuation node.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that the explicit finite difference discretization of the continuous Black–Scholes PDE on a uniform log-price mesh preserves the discrete maximum principle if and only if the trinomial transition probabilities \(p_u, p_m, p_d\) are all non-negative.

Solution

In backward induction, the discounted price at node \((n, j)\) is \(V_j^n = e^{-r \Delta t} (p_u V_{j+1}^{n+1} + p_m V_j^{n+1} + p_d V_{j-1}^{n+1})\). If \(p_u, p_m, p_d \ge 0\) and \(p_u + p_m + p_d = 1\), the term inside brackets is a convex combination of future option values. Therefore, \(\min(V_{j-1}^{n+1}, V_j^{n+1}, V_{j+1}^{n+1}) \le e^{r \Delta t} V_j^n \le \max(V_{j-1}^{n+1}, V_j^{n+1}, V_{j+1}^{n+1})\). This discrete maximum principle prevents spurious negative prices or oscillations from developing. Conversely, if any probability is negative (for example, \(p_m < 0\)), a localized perturbation at \(V_j^{n+1}\) produces an amplifying alternating oscillation with unbounded infinity-norm growth, violating the maximum principle.

Prove that setting \(\Delta x = \sigma \sqrt{3 \Delta t}\) matches the fourth central moment of the discrete trinomial distribution to that of the continuous Gaussian log-return distribution up to \(O(\Delta t^2)\).

Solution

For a continuous normal variable with variance \(\sigma^2 \Delta t\), the fourth central moment is \(3 (\sigma^2 \Delta t)^2 = 3 \sigma^4 \Delta t^2\). For the symmetric discrete trinomial distribution with step \(\Delta x\) and probabilities \(p_u = p_d = p\), \(p_m = 1 - 2p\), the fourth central moment is \(\mathbb{E}[(\Delta x)^4] = 2 p \Delta x^4\). Variance matching requires \(2 p \Delta x^2 = \sigma^2 \Delta t\), so \(2 p = \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t}{\Delta x^2}\). Substituting this into the discrete fourth moment gives \(\mathbb{E}[(\Delta x)^4] = \left(\frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t}{\Delta x^2}\right) \Delta x^4 = \sigma^2 \Delta t \Delta x^2\). Setting this equal to the continuous Gaussian fourth moment \(3 \sigma^4 \Delta t^2\) yields \(\sigma^2 \Delta t \Delta x^2 = 3 \sigma^4 \Delta t^2 \implies \Delta x^2 = 3 \sigma^2 \Delta t \implies \Delta x = \sigma \sqrt{3 \Delta t}\), proving the claim.

Prove that in an \(N\)-step trinomial tree, European call and put option prices satisfy discrete put-call parity at every interior time step if discounting is performed by the exact factor \(e^{-r \Delta t}\).

Solution

At terminal maturity \(t = T\), for every node \(j\), the payoffs satisfy \(\max(S_j - K, 0) - \max(K - S_j, 0) = S_j - K\). Suppose discrete put-call parity holds at step \(n+1\): \(C_j^{n+1} - P_j^{n+1} = S_j^{n+1} - K e^{-r(T - (n+1)\Delta t)}\). Applying the backward induction operator \(\mathcal{L}[V] = e^{-r \Delta t} (p_u V_{j+1} + p_m V_j + p_d V_{j-1})\): by linearity, \(\mathcal{L}[C - P] = \mathcal{L}[S] - K e^{-r(T - n \Delta t)}\). By the first moment risk-neutral matching condition, \(p_u e^{\Delta x} + p_m (1) + p_d e^{-\Delta x} = e^{(r - q) \Delta t}\). Therefore, \(\mathcal{L}[S] = e^{-r \Delta t} S_j^n e^{(r - q) \Delta t} = S_j^n e^{-q \Delta t}\). Thus \(C_j^n - P_j^n = S_j^n e^{-q(T - n \Delta t)} - K e^{-r(T - n \Delta t)}\), which is the exact discrete put-call parity relation at step \(n\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

Using the convergence data from Lab 1, fit an empirical power law \(\operatorname{Error}(N) = c N^{-\alpha}\) to the pricing errors at \(N = 25\) and \(N = 200\), and determine the empirical convergence order \(\alpha\).

Solution

At \(N = 25\), the error is \(e_1 = 0.075792\). At \(N = 200\), the error is \(e_2 = 0.009392\). The ratio of step counts is \(N_2 / N_1 = 200 / 25 = 8\). The ratio of errors is \(e_1 / e_2 = 0.075792 / 0.009392 = 8.0698\). Taking natural logarithms: \(\alpha = \frac{\ln(8.0698)}{\ln(8)} = \frac{2.0881}{2.0794} \approx 1.004\). The empirical convergence order is 1.00, confirming that the trinomial tree converges linearly in time step \(\Delta t = T / N\).

A desk prices an at-the-money vanilla call option with \(S = 100, K = 100, T = 1.0, r = 0.05, \sigma = 0.20\). Compare the pricing error of a 100-step CRR binomial tree against a 100-step trinomial tree relative to the exact Black–Scholes price \(10.450584\).

Solution

The 100-step trinomial tree price is \(10.431777\), with an absolute pricing error of \(|10.431777 - 10.450584| = 0.018807\). For a 100-step CRR binomial tree, the terminal nodes lie at \(S_0 u^{2j - N}\). Because \(N = 100\) is even, the central node falls exactly on \(S_0 = 100\), giving a price of approximately \(10.4373\), an error of \(0.0133\). However, at \(N = 101\), the binomial nodes bracket \(100\) without containing it, causing the error to jump to \(0.038\), whereas the trinomial error decreases monotonically to \(0.0186\).

Evaluate Richardson extrapolation on trinomial tree prices: let \(P_{50} = 10.412879\) and \(P_{100} = 10.431777\). Assuming first-order linear error \(P(h) = P_0 + c_1 h\), compute the extrapolated price \(P_{\text{rich}}\) and find its remaining error relative to BSM \(10.450584\).

Solution

With step sizes \(h = 1/50\) and \(h/2 = 1/100\), the first-order Richardson extrapolation formula is \(P_{\text{rich}} = 2 P_{100} - P_{50}\). Substituting the values: \(P_{\text{rich}} = 2 \times 10.431777 - 10.412879 = 20.863554 - 10.412879 = 10.450675\). The remaining error relative to the Black–Scholes benchmark \(10.450584\) is \(|10.450675 - 10.450584| = 0.000091\) (less than 1 basis cent). Richardson extrapolation eliminates the dominant \(O(\Delta t)\) error term, accelerating convergence by two orders of magnitude.

Tier 4 · Research

In local volatility models where \(\sigma = \sigma(t, S)\), state the Derman–Kani implied trinomial tree construction principle, and explain why forward induction via the Fokker–Planck equation is preferred over backward induction for lattice calibration.

Solution

Derman and Kani (1994) construct an implied trinomial tree whose transition probabilities at step \(n\) match the entire cross-section of European option prices maturing at \(t_{n+1}\). Backward induction requires re-solving the tree for every market option strike, scaling quadratically with the number of market quotes. Forward induction, introduced by Dupire and Jamshidian, propagates Arrow–Debreu state prices \(\lambda(n, j)\) forward through the discrete Fokker–Planck operator in a single sweep of \(O(N^2)\) operations, determining all local volatility node values simultaneously.

Analyze how the presence of a discrete dividend \(D\) paid at time \(t_d\) destroys the recombining geometry of a trinomial tree, and describe how the affine transformation method of Haug and Haug restores lattice recombining properties.

Solution

When an underlying asset pays a deterministic dollar dividend \(D\), the ex-dividend stock price drops discontinuously to \(S(t_d^+) = S(t_d^-) - D\). Subsequent proportional up and down steps \((S - D) u d \ne S u d - D\), creating non-recombining nodes where node counts proliferate as \(3^N\). The affine transformation method decomposes the stock into an escrowed dividend component \(D e^{-r(t_d - t)}\) and a pure risky component \(S_t^*\). The trinomial tree is constructed on \(S_t^*\), which recombines perfectly, and the escrowed cash is added back when evaluating exercise boundaries.

Compare the boundary layer behavior of Crank–Nicolson finite differences against trinomial trees when evaluating high-order Greeks such as Gamma \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\) near expiration for deep out-of-the-money options.

Solution

For discontinuous payoff derivatives at expiration \(t = T\), Crank–Nicolson introduces spurious non-physical oscillations in Gamma due to eigenvalues near \(-1\) in the amplification matrix. In contrast, the trinomial tree with \(\Delta x = \sigma \sqrt{3 \Delta t}\) operates as an explicit finite difference scheme with non-negative transition probabilities, unconditionally satisfying the discrete maximum principle and avoiding spurious Gamma signs. However, the trinomial tree requires \(N = O(M^2)\) time steps for spatial grid size \(M\), whereas Crank–Nicolson with Rannacher smoothing achieves oscillation-free Greeks with \(N = O(M)\) steps.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What spatial log-mesh spacing \(\Delta x\) in a trinomial tree guarantees that the baseline central probability \(p_m\) approaches \(2/3\) as \(\Delta t \to 0\)?

    Options

    Substituting \(\Delta x = \sigma \sqrt{3 \Delta t}\) into \(p_m = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t}{\Delta x^2}\) gives \(p_m = 1 - 1/3 = 2/3\).

  2. Why does a trinomial tree eliminate the parity oscillations (saw-tooth errors) typical of standard CRR binomial trees?

    Options

    In a binomial tree, \(S_0\) is only on a node at even steps; in a trinomial tree, horizontal branches preserve \(S_0\) at every step.

  3. What numerical stability failure occurs in a trinomial tree if the spatial spacing \(\Delta x\) is chosen smaller than \(\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}\)?

    Options

    Since \(p_m = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 \Delta t}{\Delta x^2}\), if \(\Delta x^2 < \sigma^2 \Delta t\), \(p_m < 0\), violating non-negativity and maximum principle bounds.

  4. Backward induction on a trinomial tree is mathematically isomorphic to which numerical PDE scheme?

    Options

    Comparing discrete coefficients demonstrates that discounting a 3-branch expectation matches explicit forward-time central-space PDE discretization.

  5. How many total distinct nodes are evaluated in a recombining trinomial tree with \(N = 50\) time steps?

    At each step \(k \in \{0, \dots, N\}\), there are \(2k+1\) nodes. Summing \(\sum_{k=0}^N (2k+1) = (N+1)^2 = 51^2 = 2,601\).

  6. Under Richardson extrapolation with step counts \(N\) and \(2N\), what linear combination of prices \(C_N\) and \(C_{2N}\) eliminates the first-order error term?

    Options

    With \(h = 1/N\), \(C(h) = C_0 + c_1 h\) and \(C(h/2) = C_0 + c_1 h/2\). Multiplying \(C(h/2)\) by 2 and subtracting \(C(h)\) leaves \(C_0\).

  7. What risk-neutral drift \(\nu\) appears in the log-stock process \(\mathrm{d}\ln(S_t)\) under continuous dividend yield \(q\)?

    Options

    By Itô's lemma on \(\ln(S)\), the Itô correction term is \(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\), yielding \(\nu = r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\).

  8. For an \(N = 50\) step trinomial tree, how many distinct stock price nodes exist at the final terminal expiration date \(t = T\)?

    At step \(N\), the nodes run from index \(-N\) to \(+N\), giving \(2N + 1 = 2(50) + 1 = 101\) nodes.

  9. Why are recombining trinomial trees poorly suited for path-dependent Asian options with continuous averaging?

    Options

    Asian option payoffs depend on \(\int_0^T S_u \mathrm{d}u\), requiring an auxiliary state variable that multiplies the node space at every step.
