Early-Exercise Boundary & Free-Boundary PDE Problems

Unlike European options whose cash flows are locked to fixed terminal dates, American options grant the holder the continuous right to exercise prior to expiration. This flexibility transforms linear parabolic Black–Scholes PDEs into non-linear free-boundary problems governed by variational inequalities. The domain splits dynamically into a continuation region where the claim satisfies the PDE, and a stopping region where immediate payoff dominates. Resolving the unknown moving boundary requires optimal stopping theory, the Snell envelope, and numerical algorithms such as projected SOR and Brennan–Schwartz elimination.

  • Introduces: early-exercise boundary
  • Prerequisites: trinomial tree, Black–Scholes–Merton PDE, stopping time

1. Optimal Stopping and the Snell Envelope

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in [0, T]}, \mathbb{Q})\) be a filtered risk-neutral probability space. Consider an American contingent claim with exercise payoff process \(g(S_t)\), where \(g(S) = \max(K - S, 0)\) for an American put and \(g(S) = \max(S - K, 0)\) for an American call. Let \(\mathcal{T}_{t, T}\) denote the set of all stopping times taking values in \([t, T]\).

Definition 2.1 · Snell Envelope

The Snell envelope of the discounted payoff process \(Z_t = e^{-rt} g(S_t)\) is the unique process \(U_t\) defined by the optimal stopping problem:

\[ U_t = \operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}_{t, T}} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q} \left[ e^{-r(\tau - t)} g(S_\tau) \mid \mathcal{F}_t \right] \]

The American option value is \(V(t, S_t) = U_t\). The discounted American option value \(e^{-rt} V(t, S_t)\) is the smallest \(\mathbb{Q}\)-supermartingale that dominates the discounted payoff process \(e^{-rt} g(S_t)\) for all \(t \in [0, T]\).

The optimal stopping time that achieves the supremum at time \(t\) is the first hitting time of the payoff obstacle:

\[ \tau_t^* = \inf \{ u \in [t, T] : V(u, S_u) = g(S_u) \} \]

Prior to \(\tau_t^*\), the discounted option price is a true \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale; once the exercise region is reached, early exercise is optimal.

2. Free-Boundary PDE and Variational Inequality

In continuous state space, the pricing function \(V(t, S)\) partitions the time-space cylinder \([0, T] \times (0, \infty)\) into two disjoint geometric regions:

  1. The continuation region \(\mathcal{C} = \{ (t, S) : V(t, S) > g(S) \}\), where holding the option strictly dominates exercise;
  2. The stopping region \(\mathcal{S} = \{ (t, S) : V(t, S) = g(S) \}\), where immediate exercise is optimal.

Definition 2.2 · Early-Exercise Boundary

The early-exercise boundary (or free boundary) is the separating manifold \(S^*(t)\) that forms the geometric interface between the continuation region \(\mathcal{C}\) and the stopping region \(\mathcal{S}\):

\[ S^*(t) = \sup \{ S > 0 : V(t, S) = K - S \} \quad \text{(for an American put)} \]

For each \(t \in [0, T)\), if \(S > S^*(t)\), then \((t, S) \in \mathcal{C}\) and the claim satisfies the homogeneous Black–Scholes PDE. If \(S \le S^*(t)\), then \((t, S) \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(V(t, S) = K - S\).

Theorem 2.1 · Variational Inequality Formulation

On the domain \([0, T] \times (0, \infty)\), the American put pricing function \(V(t, S)\) uniquely satisfies the linear complementarity problem (variational inequality):

\[ \min \left( -\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} - \mathcal{L} V, \; V(t, S) - (K - S)^+ \right) = 0 \]

subject to terminal condition \(V(T, S) = (K - S)^+\) and asymptotic boundary condition \(\lim_{S \to \infty} V(t, S) = 0\), where \(\mathcal{L}\) is the Black–Scholes differential operator:

\[ \mathcal{L} V = (r - q) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - r V \]

Proof

In \(\mathcal{C}\), \(V(t, S) > (K - S)^+\). Since the discounted price is a martingale in \(\mathcal{C}\), Itô's formula requires \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \mathcal{L} V = 0\). In \(\mathcal{S}\), \(V(t, S) = K - S\), so \(V(t, S) - (K - S)^+ = 0\). Furthermore, because the discounted price is a supermartingale everywhere, \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \mathcal{L} V \le 0\) holds in the distribution sense across the entire domain. Hence, at least one of the two quantities is zero everywhere, and neither is negative, establishing the complementarity condition \(\min(-\partial_t V - \mathcal{L}V, V - g) = 0\).

3. High-Contact (Smooth Pasting) Condition

Because the location of \(S^*(t)\) is an unknown free boundary that must be determined as part of the solution, an additional boundary condition is required to close the PDE system.

Theorem 2.2 · High-Contact / Smooth Pasting

Along the early-exercise boundary \(S^*(t)\), the American put pricing function satisfies the value-matching condition:

\[ \lim_{S \downarrow S^*(t)} V(t, S) = K - S^*(t) \]

and the first spatial derivative is continuous (high-contact condition):

\[ \lim_{S \downarrow S^*(t)} \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}(t, S) = -1 \]

Proof

Suppose for contradiction that \(\lim_{S \downarrow S^*(t)} \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}(t, S) > -1\). Then because \(V(t, S^*(t)) = K - S^*(t)\), a Taylor expansion just inside the continuation region yields \(V(t, S^*(t) + \epsilon) < K - (S^*(t) + \epsilon)\) for sufficiently small \(\epsilon > 0\). This contradicts the defining property that \(V(t, S) \ge (K - S)^+\) everywhere. Conversely, if \(\lim_{S \downarrow S^*(t)} \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}(t, S) < -1\), the stopping region could be slightly expanded to increase portfolio value, contradicting the optimality of stopping time \(\tau^*\). Hence the slope must match \(-1\) exactly.

While Delta is continuous across \(S^*(t)\), the second spatial derivative Gamma \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\) exhibits a finite jump discontinuity: in the exercise region \(\mathcal{S}\), \(V = K - S \implies \Gamma = 0\), whereas in \(\mathcal{C}\), \(\lim_{S \downarrow S^*(t)} \Gamma(t, S) = \frac{2 r K}{\sigma^2 (S^*(t))^2} > 0\).

Lab 1 · American early-exercise boundary tracking on trinomial lattice

We price an American put option on SIMULATED market parameters using a 200-step trinomial lattice, extract the critical early-exercise boundary S*(t) at quarterly horizons, verify boundary monotonicity S*(t1) <= S*(t2) for t1 < t2, and confirm that boundary Delta approaches -1.

"""Lab 1 (L02): American put early-exercise boundary and smooth pasting condition.

SIMULATED market parameters under geometric Brownian motion.
Traces the discrete critical stock price S*(t) at each time step where early exercise
becomes optimal, and estimates option Delta along the exercise boundary.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def trace_early_exercise_boundary() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    strike = 100.0
    rate = 0.05
    vol = 0.25
    maturity = 1.0
    n_steps = 150

    dt = maturity / n_steps
    dx = vol * math.sqrt(3.0 * dt)
    u = math.exp(dx)
    1.0 / u
    df = math.exp(-rate * dt)
    nu = (rate - 0.5 * vol * vol) * dt

    pu = 1.0 / 6.0 + 0.5 * (nu * nu / (dx * dx) + nu / dx)
    pm = 2.0 / 3.0 - (nu * nu / (dx * dx))
    pd = 1.0 / 6.0 + 0.5 * (nu * nu / (dx * dx) - nu / dx)

    indices = np.arange(-n_steps, n_steps + 1)
    s_terminal = spot * np.exp(indices * dx)
    v = np.maximum(strike - s_terminal, 0.0)

    # Store early exercise boundary S*(t) across representative maturities
    check_times = [0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00]
    critical_s: dict[float, float] = {}
    boundary_deltas: dict[float, float] = {}

    for k in range(n_steps - 1, -1, -1):
        t_curr = k * dt
        v_cont = df * (pu * v[2 : 2 * k + 3] + pm * v[1 : 2 * k + 2] + pd * v[0 : 2 * k + 1])
        k_indices = np.arange(-k, k + 1)
        s_k = spot * np.exp(k_indices * dx)
        intrinsic = np.maximum(strike - s_k, 0.0)

        # Early exercise condition
        exercise = intrinsic > v_cont
        v = np.maximum(v_cont, intrinsic)

        # Record critical stock price S*(t) = highest stock price where exercise is optimal
        s_crit = float(np.max(s_k[exercise])) if np.any(exercise) else float(s_k[0])

        for target_t in check_times:
            if abs(t_curr - target_t) < 0.5 * dt:
                critical_s[target_t] = s_crit
                # Compute delta at the boundary
                crit_idx = int(np.where(s_k == s_crit)[0][0])
                if crit_idx < len(v) - 1:
                    d_val = float((v[crit_idx + 1] - v[crit_idx]) / (s_k[crit_idx + 1] - s_k[crit_idx]))
                else:
                    d_val = -1.0
                boundary_deltas[target_t] = d_val

    print(f"American put price at t=0: {float(v[0]):.4f}")
    print("Early exercise boundary S*(t) and boundary Delta:")
    for t_val in sorted(critical_s):
        print(f"  t = {t_val:.2f} yr | S*(t) = {critical_s[t_val]:.2f} | Boundary Delta = {boundary_deltas[t_val]:.4f}")

    # Monotonicity check: S*(t) is non-decreasing as t -> T (maturity approaches)
    t_sorted = sorted(critical_s.keys())
    is_monotonic = all(critical_s[t_sorted[i]] <= critical_s[t_sorted[i + 1]] + 1e-4 for i in range(len(t_sorted) - 1))
    print(f"Boundary monotonicity S*(t1) <= S*(t2) for t1 < t2: {is_monotonic}")

4. Numerical Solvers: Projected SOR and Brennan–Schwartz

In discrete time steps on finite difference grids, implicit time stepping replaces the continuous variational inequality with a discrete obstacle problem at each step \(n\):

\[ \mathbf{A} \mathbf{v}^n \ge \mathbf{b}^{n+1}, \quad \mathbf{v}^n \ge \mathbf{g}, \quad (\mathbf{v}^n - \mathbf{g})^T (\mathbf{A} \mathbf{v}^n - \mathbf{b}^{n+1}) = 0 \]

where \(\mathbf{A}\) is a tridiagonal M-matrix and \(\mathbf{g}\) is the payoff vector. Two algorithms dominate numerical solutions:

  1. Projected SOR: Solves the system iteratively by taking standard Gauss–Seidel relaxation steps and projecting onto the obstacle: \[ v_j^{(k+1)} = \max \left( g_j, \; v_j^{(k)} + \omega \frac{b_j - \sum_{m < j} A_{jm} v_m^{(k+1)} - \sum_{m \ge j} A_{jm} v_m^{(k)}}{A_{jj}} \right) \] where \(\omega \in (1, 2)\) is the over-relaxation parameter.
  2. Brennan–Schwartz Algorithm: For an American put, early exercise occurs exclusively on the left spatial domain \(S \le S^*(t)\). By performing Gaussian elimination from the right boundary (continuation region) toward the left boundary, once the solution touches \(g(S)\), the remaining left nodes are set directly to the intrinsic payoff, avoiding iterative relaxation.

Where the canon breaks

Textbook treatments of American options rely heavily on smooth pasting and asymptotic boundary behavior. In real trading applications, these continuous results fail in specific structural situations:

  1. Bermudan Option Monitoring: When exercise is restricted to discrete dates (such as quarterly Bermudan swaptions), smooth pasting fails completely. The spatial derivative \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\) exhibits a sharp kink at the boundary on exercise dates because the option holder cannot exercise between observation dates.
  2. Jump-Diffusion Processes: Under Merton or Kou jump diffusions, the underlying asset can jump across the free boundary without hitting it continuously. The integro-differential pricing operator causes non-local coupling, destroying the classical local smooth pasting slope condition.
  3. Asymptotic Singularity at Expiration: As \(t \uparrow T\), the derivative of \(S^*(t)\) diverges as \(O(1 / \sqrt{T - t})\). Finite difference grids with uniform time stepping fail to resolve this sharp boundary layer, producing localized Greek misestimations.

Misconceptions

  1. An American call option on a non-dividend paying stock should be exercised early when deep in the money.

    Reality: For a non-dividend paying stock (\(q = 0\)), the European call price strictly exceeds the intrinsic payoff: \(C(t, S) > S - K e^{-r(T-t)} > S - K\). Because continuation value strictly exceeds immediate exercise value everywhere, early exercise of an American call is never optimal prior to expiration. Exercising early throws away remaining time value and forfeits downside insurance.

  2. The early-exercise boundary \(S^*(t)\) can be expressed in closed analytical form under Black–Scholes assumptions.

    Reality: The early-exercise boundary \(S^*(t)\) has no known closed-form analytical expression even under constant volatility and interest rates. It is the solution to a non-linear Volterra integral equation that must be solved via iterative quadrature, lattice induction, or finite difference projection.

  3. The smooth pasting condition implies that option Gamma is continuous across the early-exercise boundary.

    Reality: Smooth pasting guarantees continuity of the first spatial derivative (Delta \(\partial_S V = -1\)). However, the second spatial derivative (Gamma \(\partial_{SS} V\)) is strictly discontinuous: in the exercise region Gamma is 0, whereas in the continuation region Gamma approaches a strictly positive value \(\frac{2rK}{\sigma^2 (S^*(t))^2}\) as \(S \downarrow S^*(t)\).

Tier 1 · Mechanics

At a node in a trinomial tree, the discounted expected continuation value is computed as \(V_{\text{cont}} = 14.85\). The strike is \(K = 100\) and the current underlying spot price at that node is \(S = 82.50\). Determine whether early exercise is optimal at this node, and calculate the early exercise premium at this point.

Solution

The immediate exercise intrinsic payoff for an American put is \(g(S) = \max(K - S, 0) = 100.0 - 82.50 = 17.50\). Comparing intrinsic payoff to continuation value: \(g(S) = 17.50 > V_{\text{cont}} = 14.85\). Therefore, early exercise is optimal, and the American option node value is set to \(\max(14.85, 17.50) = 17.50\). The localized early exercise premium at this node is \(17.50 - 14.85 = 2.65\) currency units.

Perform one step of Projected Gauss–Seidel iteration for the scalar obstacle problem \(A v = b\) with obstacle \(g\), where \(A = 2.5\), \(b = 2.0\), previous iterate \(v^{(0)} = 0.5\), and obstacle \(g = 1.20\). Compute the updated iterate \(v^{(1)}\).

Solution

The unconstrained Gauss–Seidel step evaluates \(\tilde{v} = b / A = 2.0 / 2.5 = 0.80\). The projected iterate enforces the obstacle constraint: \(v^{(1)} = \max(g, \tilde{v}) = \max(1.20, 0.80) = 1.20\). The obstacle is active, so the value is pinned exactly at the exercise payoff level of 1.20.

Compute the asymptotic limiting value of the early-exercise boundary \(S^*(T^-)\) as \(t \to T\) for an American put option with strike \(K = 100\), risk-free rate \(r = 0.05\), and dividend yield \(q = 0.02\).

Solution

As proved by Merton (1973), the terminal limit of the early exercise boundary for an American put depends on the ratio of interest rate to dividend yield: \(S^*(T^-) = K \min(1, r / q)\). Here \(r = 0.05\) and \(q = 0.02\), so \(r / q = 0.05 / 0.02 = 2.5 > 1\). Thus \(S^*(T^-) = K \min(1, 2.5) = K = 100.0\). When the risk-free rate exceeds dividend yield, the boundary approaches the strike price continuously from below.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that an American option value \(V_{\text{Amer}}(t, S)\) is bounded below by both the corresponding European option value \(V_{\text{Euro}}(t, S)\) and the intrinsic exercise payoff \(g(S)\).

Solution

By the Snell envelope representation, \(V_{\text{Amer}}(t, S) = \sup_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}_{t, T}} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-r(\tau - t)} g(S_\tau) \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). Choosing the deterministic stopping time \(\tau = T \in \mathcal{T}_{t, T}\) yields the European value: \(V_{\text{Amer}}(t, S) \ge \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-r(T - t)} g(S_T) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = V_{\text{Euro}}(t, S)\). Similarly, choosing the immediate stopping time \(\tau = t \in \mathcal{T}_{t, T}\) yields the intrinsic payoff: \(V_{\text{Amer}}(t, S) \ge \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-r(t - t)} g(S_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = g(S_t)\). Since both candidate stopping times belong to \(\mathcal{T}_{t, T}\), the supremum is strictly greater than or equal to both values.

Prove that the early-exercise boundary \(S^*(t)\) for an American put option on a non-dividend paying stock is a monotonically non-decreasing function of calendar time \(t \in [0, T]\).

Solution

Let \(t_1 < t_2\). The time remaining to expiration from \(t_1\) is \(\tau_1 = T - t_1\), which is strictly greater than \(\tau_2 = T - t_2\). Because an American option with longer maturity contains a strictly larger set of admissible stopping times, the continuation value is non-decreasing in remaining time: \(V(t_1, S) \ge V(t_2, S)\) for all \(S\). If \(S \le S^*(t_1)\), then \(V(t_1, S) = K - S\). Since \(V(t_2, S) \ge K - S\) and \(V(t_2, S) \le V(t_1, S) = K - S\), we must have \(V(t_2, S) = K - S\). Therefore, \(S\) also belongs to the stopping region at \(t_2\), which implies \(S \le S^*(t_2)\). Taking the supremum over all such \(S\) yields \(S^*(t_1) \le S^*(t_2)\), establishing that the boundary is monotonically non-decreasing.

Prove that the Snell envelope \(U_t\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-supermartingale on \([0, T]\), and that the stopped process \(U_{t \wedge \tau^*}\) is a true \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale, where \(\tau^* = \inf\{u \ge 0 : U_u = Z_u\}\).

Solution

For any \(0 \le s \le t \le T\), the tower property of conditional expectations gives \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[U_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}_{t, T}} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[Z_\tau \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \mid \mathcal{F}_s] \le \operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}_{s, T}} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[Z_\tau \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = U_s\), proving the supermartingale property. For \(t < \tau^*\), the state remains in the continuation region where \(U_u > Z_u\). In this region, immediate stopping is suboptimal, and Bellman's principle of dynamic programming implies that the value equals the continuation conditional expectation: \(U_s = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[U_{t \wedge \tau^*} \mid \mathcal{F}_s]\), establishing the martingale property up to the stopping time.

The difference in critical stock price is \(S^*(0.75) - S^*(0.25) = 83.80 - 75.36 = \$8.44\). Tier 4 · Research

State Kim's integral equation representation for the early-exercise boundary of an American put, and explain how Carr, Jarrow, and Myneni decompose the American price into European value plus an early exercise premium integral.

Solution

Kim (1990) and Carr, Jarrow, and Myneni (1992) prove that the American put price satisfies \(P_{\text{Amer}}(t, S) = P_{\text{Euro}}(t, S) + \int_t^T [r K e^{-r(u - t)} \mathcal{N}(-d_2(S, S^*(u), u - t)) - q S e^{-q(u - t)} \mathcal{N}(-d_1(S, S^*(u), u - t))] \mathrm{d}u\). Evaluating this equation on the boundary \(S = S^*(t)\) with \(P_{\text{Amer}}(t, S^*(t)) = K - S^*(t)\) yields a non-linear Volterra integral equation of the second kind for \(S^*(t)\), demonstrating that the early exercise premium is the accumulated present value of interest earned on strike minus dividends lost on the stock across all stopping times.

Describe the continuous penalty method for American option pricing, where the complementarity constraint is replaced by a non-linear source term \(\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon + V - g} (g - V)^+\), and discuss how Newton–Raphson iteration solves the resulting penalized system.

Solution

The penalty method regularizes the free boundary by appending a continuous penalty term to the PDE: \(-\partial_t V - \mathcal{L}V = \frac{\rho}{V - g + \epsilon} (g - V)^+\). For small parameter \(\epsilon > 0\) and large penalty weight \(\rho\), when \(V < g\), the source term acts as a stiff restoring force driving \(V\) back above \(g\). Discretizing this non-linear PDE yields an algebraic system solved via Newton–Raphson iterations with monotone quadratic convergence, eliminating the inner loop tuning of SOR relaxation parameters.

How does stochastic volatility (such as the Heston model) transform the one-dimensional early-exercise boundary \(S^*(t)\) into a two-dimensional exercise manifold \(S^*(t, v)\), and what computational challenges arise in tracking this surface?

Solution

In two-factor diffusions, the continuation region depends on both the underlying spot price \(S\) and the instantaneous variance \(v\). The early-exercise boundary becomes a time-dependent curved manifold \(S^*(t, v)\) in three-dimensional space \((t, S, v)\). Because variance is mean-reverting, higher volatility increases option continuation value, shifting the critical exercise price \(S^*(t, v)\) downward. Tracking this free surface numerically requires 2D Projected SOR or alternating direction implicit (ADI) schemes with cross-derivative terms, increasing computational complexity from \(O(N^2)\) to \(O(N^3)\).

Retrieval quiz

  1. Why is early exercise never optimal for an American call option on a non-dividend paying stock?

    Options

    For \(q = 0\), \(C(t, S) > S - K e^{-r(T-t)} > S - K\). Holding the call dominates exercising because time value and downside insurance are preserved.

  2. What mathematical property holds for the first spatial derivative of an American put across the early-exercise boundary under continuous diffusions?

    Options

    Optimality of stopping requires smooth pasting, meaning Delta matches the payoff slope of \(-1\) with no jump discontinuity.

  3. What occurs to option Gamma \(\partial_{SS} V\) across the early-exercise boundary \(S^*(t)\)?

    Options

    In the exercise region \(V = K - S\), so Gamma is 0. In the continuation region near the boundary, Gamma is strictly positive, creating a jump discontinuity.

  4. The Snell envelope represents which probabilistic object in quantitative finance?

    Options

    By optimal stopping theory, the Snell envelope is the minimal supermartingale majorant of the discounted payoff process.

  5. For an American put with strike \(K = 100\), \(r = 0.06\), and dividend yield \(q = 0.03\), what is the terminal limit \(S^*(T^-)\) as \(t \to T\)?

    Since \(r / q = 0.06 / 0.03 = 2.0 > 1\), the limit is \(K \min(1, 2.0) = K = 100\).

  6. Why does the Brennan–Schwartz algorithm avoid iterative relaxation when solving implicit finite differences for American puts?

    Options

    Elimination proceeds from the continuation region on the right until the obstacle is hit, setting the remaining left nodes directly to intrinsic payoff.

  7. Why does smooth pasting fail for Bermudan options?

    Options

    Smooth pasting requires continuous-time stopping capability; discrete exercise dates generate a sharp kink in the derivative at the boundary.

  8. If an American put option has market price \(\$7.50\) and the corresponding European put has price \(\$6.00\), what is the dollar value of the early-exercise premium?

    The early-exercise premium is the difference: \(7.50 - 6.00 = \$1.50\).

  9. In Projected SOR, what is the role of the over-relaxation parameter \(\omega \in (1, 2)\)?

    Options

    Over-relaxation extrapolates along the Gauss–Seidel correction vector, substantially decreasing the spectral radius of the iteration matrix.
