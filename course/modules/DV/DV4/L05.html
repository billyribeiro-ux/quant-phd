The COS Method (Fourier-Cosine Series Expansion)

While Fast Fourier Transform algorithms evaluate option prices across strike grids efficiently, they suffer from reciprocal grid spacing rigidity and algebraic convergence limited by discrete quadrature. In their seminal work, Fang & Oosterlee (2008) introduced the Fourier-cosine (COS) expansion, which replaces transform quadrature with orthogonal cosine series projections on truncated support. For diffusions with smooth probability densities, the COS method exhibits exponential convergence, achieving double-precision machine accuracy with as few as 64 expansion terms without requiring artificial damping parameters.

  • Introduces: COS method
  • Prerequisites: Carr–Madan FFT, characteristic function, Black–Scholes–Merton PDE

1. Fourier-Cosine Density Expansion

Let \(x = \ln(S_0 / K)\) and \(y = \ln(S_T / K)\). The European option price is the discounted risk-neutral expectation \(V(t, x) = e^{-rT} \int_{-\infty}^\infty v(y) f(y \mid x) \mathrm{d}y\), where \(v(y) = K(e^y - 1)^+\) for a call and \(f(y \mid x)\) is the transitional density.

Because the transitional density decays rapidly in the tails, we truncate the infinite integration domain to a bounded interval \([a, b] \subset \mathbb{R}\). On \([a, b]\), the conditional density is expanded into a Fourier-cosine series:

\[ f(y \mid x) \approx \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} {}' A_k(x) \cos\left( k \pi \frac{y - a}{b - a} \right) \]

where the prime \(\sum{}'\) denotes that the first term (\(k = 0\)) is multiplied by \(\frac{1}{2}\). The Fourier-cosine expansion coefficients are given by the projection integral:

\[ A_k(x) = \frac{2}{b - a} \int_a^b f(y \mid x) \cos\left( k \pi \frac{y - a}{b - a} \right) \mathrm{d}y \]

Definition 5.1 · The COS Method

The COS method (Fang & Oosterlee 2008) is a numerical pricing methodology that approximates option values by expanding the transitional probability density in a Fourier-cosine series, obtaining the expansion coefficients \(A_k(x)\) directly from the characteristic function \(\phi(u \mid x)\) and evaluating the inner product against analytically integrated payoff coefficients \(V_k\).

2. Linking Cosine Coefficients to Characteristic Functions

By Euler's formula, \(\cos(\theta) = \operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta})\). Using the definition of the characteristic function \(\phi(u \mid x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{i u y} f(y \mid x) \mathrm{d}y\), the cosine coefficients can be extracted directly without knowing the density in closed form.

Theorem 5.1 · Analytical Cosine Coefficients

For any characteristic function \(\phi(u \mid x)\) with truncated support \([a, b]\), the Fourier-cosine coefficients are given by:

\[ A_k(x) \approx F_k(x) \equiv \frac{2}{b - a} \operatorname{Re} \left\{ \phi\left( \frac{k \pi}{b - a} \mathrel{\Big|} x \right) \exp\left( -i \frac{k \pi a}{b - a} \right) \right\} \]

The approximation error \(|A_k(x) - F_k(x)|\) is bounded by the tail probability mass \(\int_{\mathbb{R} \setminus [a, b]} f(y \mid x) \mathrm{d}y\).

Proof

Writing cosine in complex exponential form:

\[ A_k(x) = \frac{2}{b - a} \operatorname{Re} \left\{ \int_a^b \exp\left( i k \pi \frac{y - a}{b - a} \right) f(y \mid x) \mathrm{d}y \right\} = \frac{2}{b - a} \operatorname{Re} \left\{ \exp\left( -i \frac{k \pi a}{b - a} \right) \int_a^b \exp\left( i \frac{k \pi}{b - a} y \right) f(y \mid x) \mathrm{d}y \right\} \]

Extending the integral from \([a, b]\) to \((-\infty, \infty)\) replaces the integral by \(\phi\left(\frac{k \pi}{b - a} \mid x\right)\). The truncation error is:

\[ \left| \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{i \frac{k\pi}{b-a} y} f(y \mid x) \mathrm{d}y - \int_a^b e^{i \frac{k\pi}{b-a} y} f(y \mid x) \mathrm{d}y \right| \le \int_{\mathbb{R} \setminus [a, b]} |e^{i \frac{k\pi}{b-a} y}| f(y \mid x) \mathrm{d}y = \int_{\mathbb{R} \setminus [a, b]} f(y \mid x) \mathrm{d}y \]

Since \([a, b]\) is chosen wide enough that the tail probability is below machine epsilon, \(F_k(x)\) approximates \(A_k(x)\) with exponential accuracy.

3. Closed-Form Payoff Coefficients and Valuation Formula

Substituting the Fourier-cosine series into the risk-neutral valuation integral and interchanging summation and integration yields:

\[ V(t, x) \approx e^{-rT} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} {}' F_k(x) V_k, \quad \text{where} \quad V_k = \int_a^b v(y) \cos\left( k \pi \frac{y - a}{b - a} \right) \mathrm{d}y \]

For European call options with payoff \(v(y) = K(e^y - 1)^+\), the payoff is non-zero only for \(y > 0\). The payoff coefficients are computed analytically via two elementary trigonometric integrals:

\[ \chi_k(c, d) \equiv \int_c^d e^y \cos\left( k \pi \frac{y - a}{b - a} \right) \mathrm{d}y \] \[ \psi_k(c, d) \equiv \int_c^d \cos\left( k \pi \frac{y - a}{b - a} \right) \mathrm{d}y \]

Integrating by parts gives the explicit formulas for \(\omega_k = \frac{k \pi}{b - a}\):

\[ \chi_k(c, d) = \frac{1}{1 + \omega_k^2} \left[ e^d (\cos(\omega_k(d - a)) + \omega_k \sin(\omega_k(d - a))) - e^c (\cos(\omega_k(c - a)) + \omega_k \sin(\omega_k(c - a))) \right] \] \[ \psi_k(c, d) = \begin{cases} d - c & \text{if } k = 0 \\ \frac{1}{\omega_k} \left[ \sin(\omega_k(d - a)) - \sin(\omega_k(c - a)) \right] & \text{if } k > 0 \end{cases} \]

For a call option, the integration limits are \(c = 0\) and \(d = b\), giving \(V_k = \frac{2}{b - a} K (\chi_k(0, b) - \psi_k(0, b))\).

Theorem 5.2 · Exponential Convergence of the COS Method

If the transitional density \(f(y \mid x) \in C^\infty([a, b])\) and all its derivatives decay exponentially in the tails, the truncation error satisfies:

\[ |V(t, x) - V_N(t, x)| \le C \exp(-r N) \]

for strictly positive constants \(C, r > 0\).

Lab 1 · Exponential convergence of the COS method

We implement the Fang & Oosterlee (2008) COS method for European option pricing on SIMULATED market parameters, expanding the risk-neutral transition density into a Fourier-cosine series across truncation orders N in {8, 16, 32, 64, 128}, and demonstrate exponential error convergence toward the analytical Black–Scholes price.

"""Lab 1 (L05): The Fang-Oosterlee COS method and exponential convergence verification.

SIMULATED market parameters under geometric Brownian motion.
Prices European options using Fourier-cosine series expansions, sweeps expansion order N
from 8 to 128, and measures exponential convergence rates toward analytical machine precision.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def bsm_call_analytic(spot: float, strike: float, rate: float, vol: float, maturity: float) -> float:
    sqrt_t = math.sqrt(maturity)
    d1 = (math.log(spot / strike) + (rate + 0.5 * vol * vol) * maturity) / (vol * sqrt_t)
    d2 = d1 - vol * sqrt_t
    cdf_d1 = 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(d1 / math.sqrt(2.0)))
    cdf_d2 = 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(d2 / math.sqrt(2.0)))
    return float(spot * cdf_d1 - strike * math.exp(-rate * maturity) * cdf_d2)


def cos_method_call_price(
    spot: float,
    strike: float,
    rate: float,
    vol: float,
    maturity: float,
    n_terms: int = 64,
    l_param: float = 10.0,
) -> float:
    """Evaluate European call price using Fang-Oosterlee (2008) Fourier-cosine series."""
    # Cumulants for truncation interval [a, b]
    c1 = (rate - 0.5 * vol * vol) * maturity
    c2 = vol * vol * maturity
    c4 = 0.0

    a = c1 - l_param * math.sqrt(c2 + math.sqrt(c4))
    b = c1 + l_param * math.sqrt(c2 + math.sqrt(c4))

    k = np.arange(n_terms)
    omega = k * math.pi / (b - a)

    # Characteristic function of x = ln(S_T / K) - ln(S_0 / K)
    # phi(u) = exp(i * u * c1 - 0.5 * c2 * u^2)
    phi_val = np.exp(1j * omega * c1 - 0.5 * c2 * omega * omega)

    # Cosine expansion coefficients of density
    cf_term = np.real(phi_val * np.exp(-1j * omega * a))
    f_k = (2.0 / (b - a)) * cf_term

    # Analytical integration of call payoff: g(y) = K (exp(x + x0) - 1)
    x0 = math.log(spot / strike)
    # Truncated integral of cos(omega * (y - a)) against (exp(y + x0) - 1) over [0, b - x0]
    d = b - x0
    c_lim = max(0.0, -x0)

    # Chi and Psi functions
    # Chi_k = int_{c}^{d} exp(y) cos(omega * (y - a)) dy
    denom = 1.0 + omega * omega
    cos_d = np.cos(omega * (d - a))
    sin_d = np.sin(omega * (d - a))
    cos_c = np.cos(omega * (c_lim - a))
    sin_c = np.sin(omega * (c_lim - a))

    chi_k = (math.exp(d) * (cos_d + omega * sin_d) - math.exp(c_lim) * (cos_c + omega * sin_c)) / denom

    # Psi_k = int_{c}^{d} cos(omega * (y - a)) dy
    psi_k = np.zeros(n_terms)
    psi_k[0] = d - c_lim
    psi_k[1:] = (sin_d[1:] - sin_c[1:]) / omega[1:]

    # Payoff expansion coefficients V_k
    v_k = (2.0 / (b - a)) * strike * (math.exp(x0) * chi_k - psi_k)

    # Option price summation (term 0 carries factor 0.5)
    terms = f_k * v_k
    terms[0] *= 0.5
    price = math.exp(-rate * maturity) * 0.5 * (b - a) * float(np.sum(terms))

    return max(0.0, price)


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    strike = 100.0
    rate = 0.05
    vol = 0.20
    maturity = 1.0

    bsm_ref = bsm_call_analytic(spot, strike, rate, vol, maturity)
    print(f"Analytical BSM benchmark: {bsm_ref:.12f}")

    n_values = [8, 16, 32, 64, 128]
    print("\nCOS method convergence across expansion order N:")
    for n in n_values:
        p = cos_method_call_price(spot, strike, rate, vol, maturity, n_terms=n)
        err = abs(p - bsm_ref)
        print(f"  N = {n:3d} | Price = {p:.12f} | Absolute Error = {err:.2e}")

Where the canon breaks

The stellar exponential convergence of the COS method is predicated on smooth densities on well-chosen intervals. In quantitative operations, failure modes include:

  1. Ultra-Short Expirations (\(T < 0.005\) yr): As \(T \to 0\), the transition density degenerates into a singular Dirac delta distribution \(\delta(y - x)\). The cosine coefficients no longer decay exponentially with \(k\), causing high-frequency Gibbs-like ringing unless \(N\) is scaled into the thousands.
  2. Cumulant Truncation Window Sizing: Fang & Oosterlee determine \([a, b]\) via cumulants: \([c_1 - L \sqrt{c_2 + \sqrt{c_4}}, c_1 + L \sqrt{c_2 + \sqrt{c_4}}]\). If \(L < 8\), tail mass is truncated prematurely, establishing an error floor. If \(L > 16\), spatial resolution is diluted, requiring larger \(N\).
  3. Non-Smooth Payoffs and Barriers: For digital options, knock-out barriers, or discontinuous payoffs, the series coefficients \(V_k\) exhibit first-order algebraic decay \(O(1/k)\). The global convergence rate degrades from exponential \(O(e^{-c N})\) to algebraic \(O(1/N)\).

Misconceptions

  1. The COS method requires an artificial damping parameter \(\alpha\) like Carr–Madan FFT.

    Reality: The COS method operates on a truncated finite spatial interval \([a, b]\). Because the integration domain is compact, the option payoff is naturally bounded and square-integrable. No damping factor \(e^{\alpha k}\) is needed, eliminating parameter tuning and moment explosion hazards.

  2. The COS method converges at the same algebraic \(O(1/N)\) rate as discrete FFT quadrature.

    Reality: Because the Fourier-cosine series approximates smooth probability densities possessing infinitely many continuous derivatives, the truncation error decays exponentially: \(|\text{Error}| \le C e^{-r N}\). In practice, \(N = 64\) to \(128\) terms achieves machine double precision (\(10^{-14}\)), compared to thousands of points required by FFT.

  3. The truncation interval \([a, b]\) can be set arbitrarily wide without computational penalty.

    Reality: Setting \([a, b]\) excessively wide forces the fundamental frequency step \(\Delta \omega = \frac{\pi}{b - a}\) to become extremely small. To capture the relevant features of the characteristic function, the number of terms \(N\) must be increased proportionally, slowing evaluation.

Problem set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Calculate the truncation interval \([a, b]\) for an underlying asset with drift \(c_1 = 0.03\), variance \(c_2 = 0.04\), fourth cumulant \(c_4 = 0.0016\), and scaling parameter \(L = 10\).

Solution

Compute the spread term: \(\sqrt{c_2 + \sqrt{c_4}} = \sqrt{0.04 + \sqrt{0.0016}} = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.04} = \sqrt{0.08} \approx 0.282843\). Multiplying by \(L = 10\) gives \(10 \times 0.282843 = 2.82843\). The interval bounds are \(a = c_1 - 2.82843 = 0.03 - 2.82843 = -2.79843\) and \(b = c_1 + 2.82843 = 0.03 + 2.82843 = 2.85843\). The interval width is \(b - a = 5.65686\).

Evaluate the elementary cosine integral \(\psi_k(c, d) = \int_c^d \cos\left(k \pi \frac{y - a}{b - a}\right) \mathrm{d}y\) for \(k = 0\) with \(c = 0\) and \(d = 3\).

Solution

For \(k = 0\), \(\cos(0) = 1\) everywhere. The integral simplifies to the length of the integration interval: \(\psi_0(c, d) = \int_c^d 1 \mathrm{d}y = d - c\). Substituting \(c = 0\) and \(d = 3\) yields \(\psi_0(0, 3) = 3 - 0 = 3.0\).

A trader evaluates European option Delta using the COS method. How is \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S_0}\) evaluated analytically from the COS series representation without numerical finite difference bumping?

Solution

In the COS method, the spot price \(S_0\) enters the pricing formula exclusively through the log-moneyness state variable \(x = \ln(S_0 / K)\) in the characteristic function phase factor \(\exp\left(i \frac{k \pi}{b - a} (x - a)\right)\). Differentiating with respect to \(S_0\): \(\frac{\partial x}{\partial S_0} = \frac{1}{S_0}\). The derivative of the phase factor is \(\frac{\partial}{\partial S_0} \exp\left(i \omega_k (x - a)\right) = \frac{i \omega_k}{S_0} \exp\left(i \omega_k (x - a)\right)\). Substituting this into the COS summation evaluates Delta directly in closed form in the same \(O(N)\) sweep.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that the Fourier-cosine coefficients \(A_k\) of an even function on \([-b, b]\) match the standard Fourier series exponential coefficients \(\hat{f}_k\) up to a scaling factor of 2.

Solution

Let \(f(y)\) be an even function on \([-b, b]\), so \(f(-y) = f(y)\). The standard complex Fourier coefficient is \(\hat{f}_k = \frac{1}{2b} \int_{-b}^b f(y) e^{-i \frac{k\pi y}{b}} \mathrm{d}y\). Expanding \(e^{-i \theta} = \cos(\theta) - i \sin(\theta)\): because \(f(y)\) is even and \(\sin\) is odd, the imaginary part vanishes. The real part is even, so \(\hat{f}_k = \frac{1}{2b} \times 2 \int_0^b f(y) \cos\left(\frac{k\pi y}{b}\right) \mathrm{d}y = \frac{1}{b} \int_0^b f(y) \cos\left(\frac{k\pi y}{b}\right) \mathrm{d}y\). The Fourier-cosine expansion coefficient on \([0, b]\) is defined as \(A_k = \frac{2}{b} \int_0^b f(y) \cos\left(\frac{k\pi y}{b}\right) \mathrm{d}y\). Therefore, \(A_k = 2 \hat{f}_k\), establishing exact equivalence.

Prove that if the transitional probability density \(f(y)\) has \(m\) continuous bounded derivatives on \([a, b]\), then its Fourier-cosine coefficients decay asymptotically as \(|A_k| = O(k^{-(m+1)})\).

Solution

Consider \(A_k = \frac{2}{b - a} \int_a^b f(y) \cos\left(\omega_k (y - a)\right) \mathrm{d}y\) with \(\omega_k = \frac{k \pi}{b - a}\). Integrating by parts \(m+1\) times: at each step, differentiation of \(f^{(j)}(y)\) is accompanied by division by \(\omega_k\): \(\int_a^b f^{(j)}(y) \cos(\omega_k(y - a)) \mathrm{d}y = \left[ \frac{f^{(j)}(y) \sin(\omega_k(y - a))}{\omega_k} \right]_a^b - \frac{1}{\omega_k} \int_a^b f^{(j+1)}(y) \sin(\omega_k(y - a)) \mathrm{d}y\). Because \(f^{(m+1)}(y)\) is bounded by assumption, repeated integration produces a leading denominator of \(\omega_k^{m+1} \propto k^{m+1}\). Therefore, \(|A_k| \le C / k^{m+1} = O(k^{-(m+1)})\).

Prove that for an analytic characteristic function \(\phi(u)\) in a horizontal strip around the real axis, the COS pricing error decays exponentially: \(|V - V_N| \le C e^{-r N}\).

Solution

If \(\phi(u)\) is analytic in a strip \(|\operatorname{Im}(u)| < \rho\), then the transitional density \(f(y)\) admits an analytic continuation into a Bernstein ellipse with foci at \(a\) and \(b\) and semi-axis sum \(\rho^* > 1\). By classical approximation theory (Trefethen 2013, Theorem 8.2), Chebyshev and Fourier-cosine series projections of functions analytic on a Bernstein ellipse satisfy \(\max_{y \in [a, b]} |f(y) - f_N(y)| \le \frac{2 M}{\rho^* - 1} (\rho^*)^{-N}\). Setting \(r = \ln(\rho^*) > 0\), the error is bounded by \(C e^{-r N}\). Integrating against the bounded payoff coefficients \(V_k\) preserves the exponential decay, completing the proof.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Using the Lab 1 output for Lesson 5, observe that the error drops from \(5.38 \times 10^{-2}\) at \(N = 16\) to \(1.37 \times 10^{-6}\) at \(N = 32\), and then to \(1.07 \times 10^{-14}\) at \(N = 64\). Estimate the exponential decay parameter \(r\) in \(\text{Error}(N) \approx C e^{-r N}\) between \(N = 16\) and \(N = 32\).

Solution

At \(N_1 = 16\), \(e_1 = 5.38 \times 10^{-2}\). At \(N_2 = 32\), \(e_2 = 1.37 \times 10^{-6}\). The ratio is \(e_1 / e_2 = 5.38 \times 10^{-2} / (1.37 \times 10^{-6}) \approx 39,270\). Taking the natural logarithm: \(\ln(e_1 / e_2) = \ln(39270) \approx 10.578\). Since \(\Delta N = 32 - 16 = 16\), the decay constant is \(r = 10.578 / 16 \approx 0.661\). Every 16 additional terms reduce the error by more than 4 orders of magnitude.

A high-frequency trading desk compares the computational runtime of pricing 1,000 European options using Carr–Madan FFT (\(N = 4096\)) versus the COS method (\(N = 64\)). If a single trigonometric evaluation takes 10 nanoseconds, estimate the CPU operations ratio between the two methods.

Solution

The COS method evaluates \(N = 64\) terms in a single vector dot product, requiring \(64\) operations per option. Carr–Madan FFT evaluates \(N \log_2 N = 4096 \times 12 = 49,152\) operations. The operations ratio is \(49,152 / 64 = 768\). The COS method requires more than 700 times fewer floating-point operations while delivering higher numerical precision.

Suppose an analyst accidentally sets the cumulant truncation parameter to \(L = 3\) instead of \(L = 10\) for a 1-year option with \(\sigma = 0.20\). Explain why increasing \(N\) from \(64\) to \(1024\) fails to improve pricing accuracy beyond \(0.003\).

Solution

For \(L = 3\), the truncation interval covers only \(\pm 3\) standard deviations, excluding approximately \(0.27\%\) of the probability mass in the Gaussian tails. The truncation error is bounded below by this excluded tail mass regardless of how many expansion terms \(N\) are computed. Increasing \(N\) refines the interior series representation of the truncated density, but cannot restore the missing probability mass outside \([a, b]\), stalling accuracy at an irreducible error floor.

Tier 4 · Research

Describe the 2D COS method developed by Ruijter & Oosterlee (2012) for pricing two-asset basket and spread options, and explain how the bivariate characteristic function is incorporated into a double Fourier-cosine series.

Solution

Ruijter & Oosterlee (2012) extend the COS method to two dimensions by expanding the bivariate transitional density \(f(y_1, y_2)\) on a rectangle \([a_1, b_1] \times [a_2, b_2]\) using a double cosine basis \(\cos\left(k_1 \pi \frac{y_1 - a_1}{b_1 - a_1}\right) \cos\left(k_2 \pi \frac{y_2 - a_2}{b_2 - a_2}\right)\). The 2D cosine coefficients \(A_{k_1, k_2}\) are extracted from the bivariate characteristic function \(\phi(u_1, u_2)\) using a linear combination of four evaluations at \((\pm u_1, \pm u_2)\). Payoff coefficients for spread options \((S_1 - S_2 - K)^+\) are integrated analytically along the non-rectangular rotated boundary, achieving spectral convergence in \(O(N_1 N_2)\) time.

How does the Bermudan COS method (B-COS) evaluate backward dynamic programming across monitoring dates, and why does the intermediate convolution structure admit fast Toeplitz/Hankel matrix-vector multiplications?

Solution

Between Bermudan exercise dates \(t_m\) and \(t_{m+1}\), the option continuation value is the risk-neutral expectation of the value function at \(t_{m+1}\). Expanding both the density and continuation function into Fourier-cosine series transforms the conditional expectation integral into a convolution of cosine basis functions. This continuous convolution reduces to an algebraic matrix-vector multiplication \(\mathbf{M} \mathbf{v}\), where \(\mathbf{M}\) splits into the sum of a Toeplitz matrix and a Hankel matrix. These structured matrices are embedded into circulant matrices and multiplied by vectors using FFTs in \(O(N \log N)\) operations per time step.

Analyze why the COS method encounters algebraic convergence degradation when pricing digital cash-or-nothing call options, and discuss how Lanczos sigma filtering or polynomial smoothing mitigates Gibbs oscillations.

Solution

A digital option payoff has a jump discontinuity at the strike price: \(v(y) = \mathbf{1}_{y > 0}\). The Fourier-cosine coefficients of a step function decay only as \(O(1/k)\), exciting Gibbs ringing oscillations of magnitude \(\approx 9\%\) near the discontinuity. Lanczos \(\sigma\)-filtering multiplies the \(k\)-th series coefficient by the sinc factor \(\operatorname{sinc}(k \pi / N) = \frac{\sin(k \pi / N)}{k \pi / N}\), which damps high-frequency Fourier modes and smoothly attenuates overshoot, restoring rapid convergence without biasing the option price.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What convergence rate does the COS method achieve for European option pricing under smooth probability densities like Black–Scholes?

    Options

    Because the transitional density is infinitely differentiable, its Fourier-cosine coefficients decay faster than any polynomial, yielding exponential convergence.

  2. How does the COS method avoid the damping parameter tuning required by Carr–Madan FFT?

    Options

    Truncating the support to \([a, b]\) keeps the payoff bounded, eliminating the need for an artificial exponential damping factor.

  3. How are the cosine series coefficients \(A_k\) obtained in the COS method?

    Options

    By Euler's formula, the cosine expansion coefficients are given by the real part of the characteristic function times a phase shift factor.

  4. In Lab 1, what minimum expansion order \(N\) was sufficient to achieve machine double precision (absolute error \(\approx 10^{-14}\))?

    The COS method reaches machine precision at \(N = 64\), where error hits \(1.07 \times 10^{-14}\).

  5. What rule is used by Fang & Oosterlee to set the truncation interval \([a, b]\)?

    Options

    Cumulants \(c_1\) (mean), \(c_2\) (variance), and \(c_4\) (kurtosis) ensure that the truncation interval captures virtually all probability mass.

  6. Why does the COS method evaluate European option payoff coefficients \(V_k\) analytically rather than numerically?

    Options

    Integrals of exponential times trigonometric functions can be integrated by parts explicitly, eliminating quadrature errors in the payoff projection.

  7. What is the value of the elementary sine difference in \(\psi_k(c, d)\) when \(k = 0\)?

    For \(k = 0\), \(\cos(0) = 1\), so \(\psi_0(c, d) = d - c\). The sine term formula applies only for \(k > 0\).

  8. How does the COS method extract option Delta \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\) in closed form?

    Options

    Because \(S_0\) enters through \(x\) in the exponential term, differentiation is analytical, yielding exact Greeks without finite differences.

  9. What happens to the COS method convergence rate when pricing contracts with discontinuous payoffs (like digital options)?

    Options

    Discontinuous step payoffs introduce slow \(O(1/k)\) decay in the Fourier coefficients, inducing Gibbs ringing and algebraic convergence.
