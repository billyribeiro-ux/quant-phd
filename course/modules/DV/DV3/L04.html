Rough Volatility: Foundations and Pricing

All classical stochastic volatility diffusions predict that the at-the-money implied volatility skew flattens to a finite constant as option maturity approaches zero. In stark contrast, empirical index options exhibit an explosive power-law skew \(T^{-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha \approx 0.4\). Rough volatility resolves this fundamental anomaly by driving log-variance with fractional Brownian motion possessing Hurst parameter \(H \approx 0.1 < 1/2\). Because the paths of variance are rougher than Brownian motion, the model reproduces singular short-dated skews without artificial price jumps.

  • Introduces: rough-volatility
  • Prerequisites: forward-variance model, Heston model, Brownian motion

1. Fractional Brownian Motion and Path Regularity

In classical continuous-time finance, noise drivers are assumed to be standard Brownian motions \(W_t\), whose sample paths are almost surely Hölder continuous of order \(\gamma < 1/2\) and whose increments over non-overlapping intervals are independent. Fractional Brownian motion generalizes this framework by introducing memory and non-diffusive scaling.

Definition 4.1 (Fractional Brownian Motion). A continuous, centered Gaussian process \(W_t^H = (W_t^H)_{t \ge 0}\) is a fractional Brownian motion (fBm) with Hurst parameter \(H \in (0, 1)\) if its covariance function satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[W_t^H W_s^H\right] &= \frac{1}{2}\bigl(t^{2H} + s^{2H} \\ &\quad - |t - s|^{2H}\bigr) \end{aligned} \]

for all \(s, t \ge 0\). When \(H = 1/2\), the covariance reduces to \(\min(s, t)\), recovering standard Brownian motion.

Fractional Brownian motion satisfies the self-similarity property \(W_{a t}^H \stackrel{d}{=} a^H W_t^H\) for any scale factor \(a > 0\). Increments over an interval of length \(\Delta t\) satisfy:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\left|W_{t+\Delta t}^H - W_t^H\right|^2\right] &= \Delta t^{2H} \end{aligned} \]

By the Kolmogorov–Chentsov continuity theorem, the sample paths of \(W_t^H\) are almost surely Hölder continuous of order \(\gamma\) for every \(\gamma < H\). The parameter space divides into three distinct regimes:

  • Persistent / Long Memory (\(H > 1/2\)): Increments exhibit positive auto-correlation \(\sum_k \mathrm{Cov}(\Delta W_0^H, \Delta W_k^H) = \infty\). Paths are smoother than standard Brownian motion (\(\gamma > 1/2\)).
  • Diffusive (\(H = 1/2\)): Standard Brownian motion with independent increments and Markovian dynamics.
  • Rough / Anti-Persistent (\(H < 1/2\)): Increments have negative auto-correlation. Paths are jagged and strictly rougher than Brownian motion (\(\gamma < 1/2\)).

Theorem 4.1 (Failure of Semimartingale Property). For any \(H \in (0, 1) \setminus \{1/2\}\), the fractional Brownian motion \(W_t^H\) is not a semimartingale. Consequently, classical Itô stochastic calculus does not apply to integrals with respect to \(d W_t^H\).

Proof sketch. Let \(\Pi_n = \{0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T\}\) be a partition with mesh \(|\Pi_n| \to 0\). The \(p\)-variation is defined as \(V_p(W^H) = \lim_{|\Pi_n| \to 0} \sum_{i=1}^n |W_{t_i}^H - W_{t_{i-1}}^H|^p\). By self-similarity, the expected sum is \(\sum \Delta t_i^{p H} = T \Delta t^{p H - 1}\). For \(p = 2\): if \(H < 1/2\), \(2H - 1 < 0\), so the quadratic variation \(V_2(W^H) = \infty\); if \(H > 1/2\), \(2H - 1 > 0\), so \(V_2(W^H) = 0\). A continuous semimartingale must possess finite, non-zero quadratic variation. Thus \(W_t^H\) is a semimartingale if and only if \(H = 1/2\). \(\blacksquare\)

2. The Rough Bergomi (rBergomi) Model

In their seminal contribution, Bayer, Friz & Gatheral (2016) and Gatheral, Jaisson & Rosenbaum (2018) established that volatility is rough under both the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\) and the risk-neutral pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Definition 4.2 (Rough Volatility Model). A rough volatility model is any continuous-time asset pricing model in which instantaneous variance \(v_t\) is driven by a fractional process or Volterra integral with Hurst parameter \(H < 1/2\), endowing the log-variance trajectory with sample path Hölder regularity strictly less than that of standard Brownian motion (\(\gamma < 1/2\)).

The continuous-time rough Bergomi (rBergomi) model specifies the risk-neutral forward variance dynamics via the Mandelbrot–Van Ness fractional Riemann–Liouville kernel:

\[ \begin{aligned} \xi_t(T) &= \xi_0(T) \exp\biggl(\eta \sqrt{2H} \\ &\quad \times \int_0^t (T - s)^{H - 1/2}\,dW_s \\ &\quad - \frac{\eta^2}{2}\bigl(T^{2H} - (T - t)^{2H}\bigr)\biggr) \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(T = t\) gives the instantaneous spot variance \(v_t = \xi_t(t)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} v_t &= \xi_0(t) \exp\biggl(\eta \sqrt{2H} \tilde{W}_t^H \\ &\quad - \frac{\eta^2}{2} t^{2H}\biggr) \end{aligned} \]

where \(\tilde{W}_t^H = \int_0^t (t - s)^{H - 1/2}\,dW_s\) is the Riemann–Liouville fractional Brownian motion of the second kind. The underlying asset price satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS_t}{S_t} &= r\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^S \end{aligned} \]

with cross-correlation \(d\langle W^S, W \rangle_t = \rho\,dt\). Note that although \(v_t\) is not a semimartingale, the discounted stock price \(S_t e^{-r t}\) is a true local martingale with respect to its filtration, guaranteeing the complete absence of classical arbitrage in vanilla options.

3. Short-Time Skew Explosion and Power-Law Scaling

The mathematical triumph of rough volatility is its ability to replicate the power-law explosion in short-dated ATMI skew without introducing discontinuous jumps in asset prices.

Theorem 4.2 (rBergomi ATMI Skew Asymptotics). In the rough Bergomi model with Hurst parameter \(H \in (0, 1/2)\), vol-of-vol \(\eta > 0\), and correlation \(\rho \in [-1, 0]\), the at-the-money implied volatility skew \(\psi(T) = \left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K, T)}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0}\) satisfies the leading-order short-time asymptotic behavior:

\[ \begin{aligned} \psi(T) &\sim \frac{\rho \eta}{2(H + 1/2)(H + 3/2)} \\ &\quad \times T^{H - 1/2} \end{aligned} \]

as maturity \(T \to 0\).

Proof sketch. By the Gatheral (2006) leading-order formula for small-time implied volatility skew in forward-variance models:

\[ \begin{aligned} \psi(T) &\approx \frac{1}{4 T \sqrt{\xi_0 T}} \int_0^T \int_s^T \\ &\quad \mathrm{Cov}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\frac{dS_s}{S_s}, \frac{d\xi_u(u)}{\xi_0(u)}\right)du \end{aligned} \]

Substituting the rBergomi forward variance driver \(d\xi_u(u) / \xi_0(u) \sim \eta \sqrt{2H}(u - s)^{H - 1/2}dW_s\) and the correlation \(d\langle W^S, W \rangle_s = \rho ds\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\int_s^T (u - s)^{H - 1/2}\,du \\ &= \frac{(T - s)^{H + 1/2}}{H + 1/2} \end{aligned} \]

Integrating with respect to \(s \in [0, T]\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\int_0^T \frac{(T - s)^{H + 1/2}}{H + 1/2}\,ds \\ &= \frac{T^{H + 3/2}}{(H + 1/2)(H + 3/2)} \end{aligned} \]

Dividing by the denominator \(4 T \sqrt{\xi_0 T} = 4 \sqrt{\xi_0} T^{3/2}\), and normalizing by \(\sqrt{\xi_0}\) produces the skew scaling:

\[ \begin{aligned} \psi(T) &\propto \frac{T^{H + 3/2}}{T^{3/2}} \\ &= T^{H - 1/2} \quad \blacksquare \end{aligned} \]

For \(H \approx 0.10\), the exponent is \(H - 1/2 \approx -0.40\), which exactly matches the empirical market power law \(\psi(T) \sim T^{-0.40}\) across S&P 500, Euro Stoxx 50, and FX option surfaces.

Fractional Brownian Motion Simulation and Skew Explosion

Hypothesis: Fractional Brownian motion with \(H = 0.10\) exhibits rougher increment paths than standard Brownian motion (\(H = 0.50\)), and its leading-order rBergomi ATMI skew explodes as \(T \to 0\) with power law \(T^{-0.40}\), contrasting sharply with the flat \(O(1)\) diffusive limit.

"""Lab 4 · Rough Fractional Dynamics & ATMI Skew Explosion.

This lab simulates fractional Brownian motion via exact Cholesky factorization of the
covariance matrix for Hurst parameters H=0.10 (rough) and H=0.50 (diffusive) on seed 0 (SIMULATED),
computes pathwise regularity properties, and demonstrates the rBergomi power-law
explosion of short-dated ATMI skew psi(T) ~ T^(H - 1/2).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def simulate_fbm_cholesky(n: int, h: float, seed: int) -> list[float]:
    """Simulate fractional Brownian motion on [0, 1] using exact Cholesky decomposition."""
    cov: list[list[float]] = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    dt = 1.0 / n
    for i in range(n):
        ti = (i + 1) * dt
        for j in range(i + 1):
            tj = (j + 1) * dt
            diff = abs(ti - tj)
            val = 0.5 * (ti ** (2.0 * h) + tj ** (2.0 * h) - diff ** (2.0 * h))
            cov[i][j] = val
            cov[j][i] = val

    l_mat: list[list[float]] = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1):
            s_sum = sum(l_mat[i][k] * l_mat[j][k] for k in range(j))
            if i == j:
                diag = cov[i][i] - s_sum
                l_mat[i][j] = math.sqrt(max(1e-12, diag))
            else:
                l_mat[i][j] = (cov[i][j] - s_sum) / l_mat[j][j]

    rng = random.Random(seed)
    z = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n)]
    fbm: list[float] = [0.0] * n
    for i in range(n):
        fbm[i] = sum(l_mat[i][j] * z[j] for j in range(i + 1))
    return fbm


def rbergomi_skew(t: float, h: float, eta: float, rho: float) -> float:
    """Theoretical leading-order rBergomi ATMI skew."""
    if t <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    denom = 2.0 * (h + 0.5) * (h + 1.5)
    coef = (rho * eta) / denom
    res = coef * (t ** (h - 0.5))
    return float(res)


def main() -> None:
    print("=== Rough Volatility: fBm Simulation & Skew Explosion ===")
    n_pts = 100
    seed = 0

    print(f"Simulating fBm paths (N={n_pts} steps on [0, 1], seed={seed}, SIMULATED):")
    fbm_rough = simulate_fbm_cholesky(n_pts, h=0.10, seed=seed)
    fbm_brown = simulate_fbm_cholesky(n_pts, h=0.50, seed=seed)

    inc_rough = [fbm_rough[i + 1] - fbm_rough[i] for i in range(n_pts - 1)]
    inc_brown = [fbm_brown[i + 1] - fbm_brown[i] for i in range(n_pts - 1)]

    var_r = sum(x * x for x in inc_rough) / len(inc_rough)
    var_b = sum(x * x for x in inc_brown) / len(inc_brown)

    dt_r = (1.0 / n_pts) ** 0.10
    dt_b = (1.0 / n_pts) ** 0.50
    print("\nIncrement Properties:")
    print(f"  H=0.10 (Rough):      Increment Std Dev = {math.sqrt(var_r):.5f} (scaling dt^H = {dt_r:.5f})")
    print(f"  H=0.50 (Diffusive):  Increment Std Dev = {math.sqrt(var_b):.5f} (scaling dt^H = {dt_b:.5f})")

    print("\nSample Path Values along [0, 1]:")
    print(" Time t | W_t^(H=0.10) (Rough) | W_t^(H=0.50) (Standard BM) ")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------")
    checkpoints = [10, 25, 50, 75, 99]
    for cp in checkpoints:
        t_val = (cp + 1) / n_pts
        print(f"  {t_val:4.2f}  |       {fbm_rough[cp]:8.4f}       |          {fbm_brown[cp]:8.4f} ")

    eta = 1.90
    rho = -0.70
    maturities = [0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 1.00]

    print(f"\nATMI Skew Term Structure (eta={eta}, rho={rho}):")
    print(" Maturity T (yr) | Skew H=0.10 (Rough T^-0.4) | Skew H=0.50 (Diffusive T^0) ")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    for mat in maturities:
        sk_rough = rbergomi_skew(mat, h=0.10, eta=eta, rho=rho)
        sk_diff = rbergomi_skew(mat, h=0.50, eta=eta, rho=rho)
        print(f"     {mat:6.3f}      |           {sk_rough:8.4f}          |            {sk_diff:8.4f} ")

    print("\nConclusion: At T = 0.005 (approx 1 trading day):")
    print(f"  Rough skew explodes to {rbergomi_skew(0.005, 0.10, eta, rho):.4f}")
    print(f"  Diffusive skew remains flat at {rbergomi_skew(0.005, 0.50, eta, rho):.4f}")
    print("This power-law divergence matches empirical equity index options without artificial jumps.")


main()

Where the canon breaks

The transition to rough volatility represents a foundational rupture with 50 years of continuous financial modeling:

  • Abandonment of the Semimartingale Hypothesis for Volatility: Since Bachelier (1900) and Black & Scholes (1973), financial models assumed that all underlying state variables are semimartingales driven by Brownian motion. Rough volatility proves that while prices must remain semimartingales to prevent arbitrage, volatility itself is not a traded asset and is strictly non-semimartingale.
  • Collapse of Classical PDE Methods: Because the fractional Volterra kernel \((t - s)^{H - 1/2}\) does not factorize, rough volatility processes are fundamentally non-Markovian. There is no infinitesimal generator and no low-dimensional partial differential equation (such as the 2D Heston PDE). Option pricing requires Monte Carlo simulations, infinite-dimensional lifts, or neural network surrogates.
  • Singularity at Zero Maturity: Classical perturbation expansions (like Hagan et al. SABR) expand around smooth diffusive limits and break down completely when confronted with the \(T^{H - 1/2}\) singular power-law behavior of rough volatility.

Misconceptions

  • Because rough volatility is not a semimartingale, option prices admit arbitrage opportunities.

    Arbitrage theorems (such as Delbaen & Schachermayer's Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing) require the tradable asset price \(S_t\) to be a semimartingale. In rough volatility models, the discounted stock price \(S_t e^{-r t} = S_0 \mathcal{E}\left(\int_0^t \sqrt{v_s}\,dW_s^S\right)\) is a true continuous local martingale driven by standard Brownian motion \(W_t^S\). Instantaneous variance \(v_t\) is not directly traded, so its non-semimartingale nature does not generate arbitrage in the physical or option markets.

  • Rough volatility implies long memory and persistent volatility clustering.

    In classical time-series econometrics (e.g. FIGARCH), fractional processes were studied with \(H > 1/2\), which implies long memory and positive autocorrelation. In contrast, rough volatility operates in the anti-persistent regime \(H \in (0, 1/2)\), typically \(H \approx 0.1\). Here, increments are negatively autocorrelated, and trajectories are intensely jagged and rough, rather than smooth and persistent.

  • Option pricing under rough volatility can be solved with classical 2D finite difference PDEs.

    Because fractional Brownian motion does not satisfy the semigroup property, the pair \((S_t, v_t)\) is not Markovian. No two-dimensional parabolic PDE exists for option pricing. Pricing in rough volatility models must be carried out using Monte Carlo (e.g. the hybrid scheme of Bennedsen et al.), rough path expansions, or affine Volterra ODE systems (for rough Heston).

Problem set

Mechanics

Given Hurst parameter \(H = 0.12\), vol-of-vol \(\eta = 2.10\), and correlation \(\rho = -0.75\), compute the rBergomi ATMI skew for maturities \(T_1 = 0.01\) years (approx 2.5 trading days) and \(T_2 = 0.25\) years (3 months).

Worked solution

Compute the denominator constant:

\[ \begin{aligned} &2(H + 0.5)(H + 1.5) \\ &= 2(0.12 + 0.5)(0.12 + 1.5) \\ &= 2 \times 0.62 \times 1.62 = 2.0088 \end{aligned} \]

Compute the coefficient:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\rho \eta}{2(H + 0.5)(H + 1.5)} \\ &= \frac{-0.75 \times 2.10}{2.0088} \\ &= \frac{-1.575}{2.0088} \approx -0.78405 \end{aligned} \]

The power-law exponent is \(H - 0.5 = 0.12 - 0.50 = -0.38\).

For \(T_1 = 0.01\): \((0.01)^{-0.38} = (100)^{0.38} \approx 5.7544\). Skew is \(-0.78405 \times 5.7544 \approx -4.5117\).

For \(T_2 = 0.25\): \((0.25)^{-0.38} = (4)^{0.38} \approx 1.6935\). Skew is \(-0.78405 \times 1.6935 \approx -1.3278\).

Compute the theoretical covariance \(\mathbb{E}[W_t^H W_s^H]\) for fractional Brownian motion with \(H = 0.10\) at times \(t = 0.25\) and \(s = 1.0\).

Worked solution

Using the fBm covariance formula \(\frac{1}{2}(t^{2H} + s^{2H} - |t - s|^{2H})\) with \(2H = 0.20\):

\[ \begin{aligned} t^{2H} &= (0.25)^{0.20} \approx 0.75786 \\ s^{2H} &= (1.00)^{0.20} = 1.00000 \\ |t - s| &= 1.0 - 0.25 = 0.75 \\ |t - s|^{2H} &= (0.75)^{0.20} \approx 0.94406 \end{aligned} \]

Substitute into the covariance expression:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}[W_{0.25}^H W_1^H] \\ &= \frac{1}{2}(0.75786 + 1.00000 \\ &\quad - 0.94406) \\ &= \frac{1}{2}(0.81380) = 0.40690 \end{aligned} \]

Calculate the auto-correlation of increments of fractional Brownian motion \(\mathrm{Corr}(W_1^H, W_2^H - W_1^H)\) for \(H = 0.10\) and compare it with the standard Brownian motion case \(H = 0.50\).

Worked solution

The covariance between unit increments \([0, 1]\) and \([1, 2]\) is \(\frac{1}{2}(2^{2H} - 2)\).

For \(H = 0.50\): \(2^{1.0} - 2 = 0\), so increments are uncorrelated.

For \(H = 0.10\): \(2^{0.20} \approx 1.14870\). Covariance is \(\frac{1}{2}(1.14870 - 2) = -0.42565\).

Since \(\operatorname{Var}(W_1^H) = 1^{0.20} = 1\), the correlation is exactly \(-0.42565\).

The negative correlation confirms the strong anti-persistence and roughness of fractional Brownian motion when \(H < 0.5\).

Proof

Prove that the Mandelbrot–Van Ness representation for fractional Brownian motion,

\[ \begin{aligned} W_t^H &= \frac{1}{\Gamma(H + 1/2)} \\ &\quad \times \biggl[\int_{-\infty}^0 \bigl((t - s)^{H - 1/2} \\ &\qquad\quad - (-s)^{H - 1/2}\bigr)dW_s \\ &\quad + \int_0^t (t - s)^{H - 1/2}\,dW_s\biggr] \end{aligned} \]

produces a process with stationary increments.

Worked solution

Consider the increment \(W_{t+h}^H - W_t^H\). From the representation:

\[ \begin{aligned} &W_{t+h}^H - W_t^H \\ &= \frac{1}{\Gamma(H + 1/2)} \\ &\quad \times \int_{-\infty}^{t+h} \Bigl[(t + h - s)^{H - 1/2} \\ &\qquad - \mathbf{1}_{\{s < t\}}(t - s)^{H - 1/2}\Bigr]dW_s \end{aligned} \]

Perform the change of integration variable \(u = s - t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &W_{t+h}^H - W_t^H \\ &= \frac{1}{\Gamma(H + 1/2)} \\ &\quad \times \int_{-\infty}^h \Bigl[(h - u)^{H - 1/2} \\ &\qquad - \mathbf{1}_{\{u < 0\}}(-u)^{H - 1/2}\Bigr]dW_{u+t} \end{aligned} \]

Because Brownian motion \(W\) has stationary increments, the time-shifted Brownian motion \(\tilde{W}_u = W_{u+t} - W_t\) has identical law to \(W_u\). Thus the distribution of \(W_{t+h}^H - W_t^H\) is invariant under shifts in \(t\) and depends solely on lag \(h\), proving increment stationarity. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that for any \(H \in (0, 1/2)\), the Riemann–Liouville fractional kernel \(K(t) = t^{H - 1/2}\) belongs to \(L^2([0, T])\), but fails to belong to \(L^p([0, T])\) for \(p \ge \frac{1}{1/2 - H}\).

Worked solution

Consider \(\int_0^T |K(t)|^p\,dt = \int_0^T t^{p(H - 1/2)}\,dt\). The integral converges at the origin if and only if the power exponent satisfies \(p(H - 1/2) > -1\).

For \(p = 2\): \(2(H - 1/2) = 2H - 1\). Since \(H > 0\), \(2H - 1 > -1\), so \(\int_0^T t^{2H - 1}dt = \frac{T^{2H}}{2H} < \infty\). Thus \(K \in L^2([0, T])\).

For general \(p\), convergence requires \(p(1/2 - H) < 1 \iff p < \frac{1}{1/2 - H}\). When \(p \ge \frac{1}{1/2 - H}\), \(p(H - 1/2) \le -1\), and the integral diverges logarithmically or as a power at \(t = 0\), so \(K \notin L^p([0, T])\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that the discounted stock price \(S_t e^{-r t}\) in the rough Bergomi model is a true martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\), despite the variance process \(v_t\) being non-semimartingale.

Worked solution

Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(S_t e^{-r t} = S_0 \mathcal{E}\left(\int_0^t \sqrt{v_s}\,dW_s^S\right)\). Decompose \(W_s^S = \rho W_s + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} W_s^\perp\) where \(W^\perp\) is independent of \(W\). Conditioning on the entire path of the variance driver \(\mathcal{F}_\infty^W = \sigma(W_s, s \ge 0)\), the variance path \(s \mapsto v_s\) is completely deterministic and strictly positive.

The conditional process \(M_t = S_t e^{-r t}\) given \(\mathcal{F}_\infty^W\) is a geometric Brownian motion with time-dependent deterministic volatility. Since \(\int_0^T v_s\,ds < \infty\) almost surely (as \(\mathbb{E}[\int_0^T v_s ds] = \int_0^T \xi_0(s)ds < \infty\)), the conditional expectation satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[S_T e^{-r T} \mid \mathcal{F}_\infty^W] = S_0\). By the tower property of conditional expectation, \(\mathbb{E}[S_T e^{-r T}] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[S_T e^{-r T} \mid \mathcal{F}_\infty^W]] = S_0\), proving that \(S_t e^{-r t}\) is a true martingale. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Empirical S&P 500 option skews at maturities \(T = 1\) week (0.0192 yr) and \(T = 1\) month (0.0833 yr) are measured as \(\psi_1 = -3.20\) and \(\psi_2 = -1.80\). Estimate the implied Hurst parameter \(H\) assuming power-law scaling \(\psi(T) \propto T^{H - 1/2}\).

Worked solution

Under the power-law hypothesis, \(\frac{\psi_1}{\psi_2} = \left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right)^{H - 1/2}\). Taking natural logarithms:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln(-3.20 / -1.80) \\ &= (H - 0.5)\ln(0.0192 / 0.0833) \\ \ln(1.7778) &= (H - 0.5)\ln(0.23049) \\ 0.57536 &= (H - 0.5)(-1.4675) \\ H - 0.5 &= \frac{0.57536}{-1.4675} \approx -0.39207 \\ H &= 0.5 - 0.39207 \approx 0.1079 \end{aligned} \]

The estimated Hurst parameter is \(H \approx 0.11\), in precise agreement with the empirical findings of Gatheral et al. (2018) and Bayer et al. (2016).

In a rough Bergomi model with \(H = 0.10\), \(\rho = -0.70\), and \(\eta = 1.90\), compare the predicted ATMI skew at \(T = 1\) day (0.004 yr) against a standard Heston model calibrated to the 1-month skew (\(\psi = -1.80\)).

Worked solution

In the rBergomi model, the skew at \(T = 0.004\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\psi_{\mathrm{rough}}(0.004) \\ &= \frac{-0.70 \times 1.90}{2(0.60)(1.60)} \times (0.004)^{-0.40} \\ &= \frac{-1.33}{1.92} \times (250)^{0.40} \\ &= -0.6927 \times 9.1028 \approx -6.305 \end{aligned} \]

In the standard Heston model, as shown in Lesson 1, the short-time skew converges to a finite constant \(\frac{\rho \xi}{2\sqrt{v_0}} \approx -1.80\). Thus, at the 1-day horizon, the rough model predicts a skew more than 3.5 times steeper than the diffusive Heston model (\(-6.31\) vs \(-1.80\)), matching the steep skew observed in daily-expiring 0DTE options.

Why do standard jump-diffusion models (e.g. Merton or Bates) produce an apparent skew explosion as \(T \to 0\), and how does the rough volatility explanation differ from the jump explanation?

Worked solution

In jump models, the presence of Poisson jumps in the underlying price generates out-of-the-money option prices that scale linearly with maturity: \(C(K, T) \sim \lambda T (S_0 e^J - K)^+\). Inverting the Black–Scholes formula produces an implied volatility that scales as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{BS}} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\), creating an apparent skew explosion \(\psi(T) \sim T^{-1/2}\).

However, jump models suffer from two decisive empirical flaws: (1) they predict that as \(T \to 0\), the implied volatility of deep out-of-the-money puts flattens to a horizontal line across strikes, whereas empirical surfaces maintain smooth, downward-sloping curvature; (2) high-frequency realized volatility time series show continuous rough trajectories rather than piecewise-constant jumps. Rough volatility models match both the continuous path regularity of realized volatility and the entire implied volatility smile curvature across all tenors without jumps.

Research

Explain why the exact Cholesky simulation of fractional Brownian motion has computational complexity \(\mathcal{O}(N^3)\), and describe how the Hybrid Scheme of Bennedsen, Lunde & Pakkanen (2017) reduces this complexity to \(\mathcal{O}(N \log N)\).

Worked solution

Exact Cholesky simulation requires constructing the full \(N \times N\) covariance matrix \(\Sigma_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}(t_i^{2H} + t_j^{2H} - |t_i - t_j|^{2H})\) and factoring it as \(\Sigma = L L^\top\), which requires \(\frac{1}{3}N^3\) floating point operations. For \(N = 10,000\) steps across many Monte Carlo paths, this is computationally prohibitive.

The Hybrid Scheme decomposes the Volterra integral \(\int_0^t (t - s)^{H - 1/2}dW_s\) into two parts: a local singular part over the most recent \(\kappa\) intervals \([t - \kappa \Delta t, t]\) simulated exactly, and a smooth non-singular convolution part over \([0, t - \kappa \Delta t]\). Because the non-singular part is a discrete convolution with power-law weights, it can be evaluated simultaneously across all time steps using Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT). This reduces the total simulation cost from \(\mathcal{O}(N^3)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(N \log N)\).

What is the 'Rough Heston' model of El Euch & Rosenbaum (2019), and how does it preserve semi-analytical tractability despite the non-Markovian nature of rough volatility?

Worked solution

The Rough Heston model replaces the standard CIR variance SDE with a fractional Volterra integral equation:

\[ \begin{aligned} v_t &= v_0 + \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}\lambda(\theta - v_s)\,ds \\ &\quad + \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}\nu\sqrt{v_s}\,dW_s \end{aligned} \]

with \(\alpha = H + 1/2 \in (1/2, 1)\).

Although the variance process is non-Markovian, El Euch & Rosenbaum proved that the model remains affine: the characteristic function of the log-price satisfies an affine representation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[e^{i u \ln S_T}\right] &= \exp\biggl(g(T, u) \\ &\quad + \int_0^T f(T - s, u)v_s\,ds\biggr) \end{aligned} \]

where the deterministic coefficient functions satisfy a fractional Riccati equation involving Caputo fractional derivatives \(\mathcal{D}^\alpha h(t)\). While not admitting closed-form solutions in terms of elementary functions, these fractional Riccati ODEs can be solved in milliseconds using predictor-corrector schemes, enabling fast calibration without Monte Carlo.

Analyze how the microstructural foundation of rough volatility is derived from Hawkes self-exciting point processes with heavy-tailed kernels.

Worked solution

Jaisson & Rosenbaum (2015, 2016) demonstrated that rough fractional dynamics emerge as the macroscopic scaling limit of high-frequency order flows. Modeling order arrivals as nearly-unstable self-exciting Hawkes processes with power-law memory kernels \(\phi(t) \sim c t^{-(1 + \alpha)}\) with \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\), they proved that when the spectral radius of the branching ratio approaches unity (reflecting market maker inventory replenishment and high order cancellation rates), the normalized integrated intensity process converges weakly in law to a fractional Volterra process with Hurst parameter \(H = \alpha - 1/2\). For financial order books with empirically estimated \(\alpha \approx 0.6\), the limiting Hurst parameter is \(H \approx 0.1\), providing a rigorous microstructural justification for why volatility is rough.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What distinguishes fractional Brownian motion with \(H < 1/2\) from standard Brownian motion (\(H = 1/2\))?

    Options
    When \(H < 1/2\), fBm increments exhibit negative auto-correlation, sample paths have Hölder regularity \(\gamma < H < 1/2\), and quadratic variation is infinite.

  2. Why does the failure of the semimartingale property for \(v_t\) NOT introduce arbitrage into the rough Bergomi model?

    Options
    Arbitrage theorems require tradable assets to be semimartingales. In rBergomi, stock prices are semimartingales; non-semimartingale roughness is confined to unobserved variance.

  3. What is the asymptotic scaling of the ATMI skew \(\psi(T)\) as \(T \to 0\) in the rough Bergomi model?

    Options
    By Theorem 4.2, integrating the singular kernel \((T - s)^{H - 1/2}\) yields ATMI skew scaling exactly as \(T^{H - 1/2}\).

  4. What empirical market value is typically estimated for the Hurst parameter \(H\) of equity volatility?

    Options
    Gatheral et al. (2018) consistently find \(H \in [0.08, 0.14]\) across thousands of equity indices and individual stocks.

  5. Why cannot classical two-dimensional finite-difference PDEs price options under rough volatility?

    Options
    Because the Volterra kernel \((t - s)^{H - 1/2}\) does not factorize, the system is non-Markovian and possesses no finite-dimensional infinitesimal generator.

  6. What is the kernel used in the rough Bergomi forward variance specification?

    Options
    The rBergomi model replaces the exponential kernel with the singular Riemann–Liouville kernel \((T - s)^{H - 1/2}\).

  7. How does the Hybrid Scheme of Bennedsen et al. accelerate rough volatility simulation?

    Options
    The Hybrid Scheme approximates the non-singular memory tail as a discrete convolution evaluated with FFTs in \(\mathcal{O}(N \log N)\) operations.

  8. What microstructural market mechanism generates rough fractional volatility in the macroscopic scaling limit?

    Options
    Jaisson & Rosenbaum (2015, 2016) proved that nearly-unstable Hawkes processes with power-law kernels scale asymptotically to rough fractional diffusions.

  9. What does the Hurst parameter \(H = 0.50\) correspond to in the fractional Brownian motion family?

    Options
    When \(H = 1/2\), the covariance \(\frac{1}{2}(t^{2H} + s^{2H} - |t - s|^{2H})\) reduces to \(\min(t, s)\), recovering standard Brownian motion.

  10. How does rough volatility explain the skew of ultra-short dated options (such as 0DTE options)?

    Options
    For \(H \approx 0.10\), \(T^{H - 1/2}\) naturally diverges as \(T \to 0\), matching the steep empirical skew of 0DTE options without requiring jump components.
