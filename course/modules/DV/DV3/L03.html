Forward-Variance and Bergomi-Type Models

Standard spot-variance diffusions like Heston or SABR treat instantaneous variance as a scalar state variable, forcing variance swap curves to conform to rigid parametric shapes. Forward-variance models bypass this limitation by taking the entire curve of conditional forward variances as the modeling primitive, automatically matching market variance swap quotes by construction—in exact analogy to Heath–Jarrow–Morton models in fixed income. However, continuous multi-factor Bergomi models encounter the joint SPX/VIX smile calibration puzzle and cannot match the power-law explosion in short-dated equity skews.

  • Introduces: forward-variance-model
  • Prerequisites: Heston model, volatility smile, risk-neutral density

1. The Forward-Variance Framework

In standard stochastic volatility diffusions, the variance swap term structure is an endogenous model output determined by mean reversion \(\kappa\) and long-term variance \(\theta\). As a result, fitting the term structure of variance swaps requires continuous recalibration of parameters across maturities, which introduces time inconsistency and calendar spread arbitrage.

To eliminate this drawback, Gatheral (2006) and Bergomi (2005) introduced the continuous-time forward-variance model, which models the family of forward variance curves \(\xi_t(T) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[v_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) directly under the risk-neutral pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Definition 3.1 (Forward Variance Process). Let \(v_t\) denote the instantaneous variance of the underlying log-price under pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), such that \(d\langle \ln S \rangle_t = v_t\,dt\). For any maturity \(T \ge t\), the instantaneous forward variance \(\xi_t(T)\) observed at time \(t\) is defined as:

\[ \begin{aligned} \xi_t(T) &\equiv \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[v_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t\right] \end{aligned} \]

By the tower property of conditional expectation, for any fixed \(T\), the process \(t \mapsto \xi_t(T)\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale on \(t \in [0, T]\), terminating at the spot variance \(\xi_T(T) = v_T\).

The fair strike of a variance swap spanning \([0, T]\), denoted \(\mathrm{VS}(0, T)\), equals the risk-neutral expectation of annualized integrated variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{VS}(0, T) &= \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T v_u\,du\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \xi_0(u)\,du \end{aligned} \]

Differentiating with respect to maturity \(T\) yields the exact relationship between the initial forward variance curve and variance swap quotes:

\[ \begin{aligned} \xi_0(T) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left(T \cdot \mathrm{VS}(0, T)\right) \end{aligned} \]

2. The Bergomi \(n\)-Factor Model Dynamics

To guarantee that instantaneous variance \(\xi_t(T)\) remains strictly positive for all horizons, Bergomi (2005) introduced the exponential forward-variance specification. Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the forward variance curve obeys:

\[ \begin{aligned} \xi_t(T) &= \xi_0(T)\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \omega_i X_t^i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n \omega_i^2 \mathrm{Var}(X_t^i)\right) \end{aligned} \]

where each \(X_t^i\) is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) driver starting at \(X_0^i = 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} dX_t^i &= -\kappa_i X_t^i\,dt + dW_t^i \end{aligned} \]

Integrating the OU equation gives \(X_t^i = \int_0^t e^{-\kappa_i(t - s)}dW_s^i\), so that \(\mathrm{Var}(X_t^i) = \frac{1 - e^{-2\kappa_i t}}{2\kappa_i}\). In differential form, the forward variance martingale satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\xi_t(T)}{\xi_t(T)} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \omega_i e^{-\kappa_i(T - t)}\,dW_t^i \end{aligned} \]

The underlying asset price \(S_t\) follows the continuous diffusion:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dS_t}{S_t} &= r\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^S, \quad v_t = \xi_t(t) \end{aligned} \]

with Brownian cross-correlations \(d\langle W^S, W^i \rangle_t = \rho_i\,dt\) and \(d\langle W^i, W^j \rangle_t = \delta_{ij}\,dt\).

3. The Joint SPX/VIX Smile Calibration Puzzle

A crucial test of any stochastic volatility model is the simultaneous pricing of S&P 500 index options (SPX) and options on the Cboe Volatility Index (VIX). In forward variance models, the VIX index at time \(T\) over forward window \(\Delta = 30/365\) is explicitly defined by:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{VIX}_T &= 100 \times \sqrt{\frac{1}{\Delta}\int_T^{T+\Delta} \xi_T(u)\,du} \end{aligned} \]

By Jensen's inequality, since \(x \mapsto \sqrt{x}\) is strictly concave:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathrm{VIX}_T] < \sqrt{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\mathrm{VIX}_T^2\right]} = \sqrt{\frac{100^2}{\Delta}\int_T^{T+\Delta}\xi_0(u)\,du} \end{aligned} \]

Consequently, VIX futures prices strictly trade below the square root of the forward variance swap strike. The difference \(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{VIX}_T^2]} - \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{VIX}_T]\) reflects the variance of the forward variance curve, directly governed by the volatility-of-volatility parameters \(\omega_i\).

However, when calibrating standard continuous diffusive forward variance models (e.g. 2-factor Bergomi) to empirical market data, an irreconcilable tension emerges: fitting the steepness of the short-dated SPX implied volatility skew requires high correlation \(\rho \approx -0.8\) and moderate vol-of-vol, but fitting the upward-sloping, upward-curving smile of short-dated VIX call options requires extremely large vol-of-vol \(\omega \ge 3.0\), which in turn severely overprices SPX out-of-the-money put wings and produces excessive smile curvature.

4. Diffusive Short-Time Skew Asymptotics

Why do standard continuous stochastic volatility and forward variance models fail to match market index skews as maturity \(T \to 0\)? We now establish the fundamental diffusive short-time skew bound.

Theorem 3.1 (Diffusive Short-Time Skew Limit). In any continuous \(n\)-factor Bergomi model with exponential kernels \(K_i(t) = \omega_i e^{-\kappa_i t}\), the at-the-money implied (ATMI) volatility skew satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}(K, T)}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \rho_i \omega_i \int_0^T (T - s)e^{-\kappa_i(T - s)}\,ds}{4 T \sqrt{T \cdot \int_0^T \xi_0(u)\,du}} + O(T) \end{aligned} \]

Evaluating the integral for small \(T\) via Taylor expansion \(e^{-\kappa_i u} = 1 - \kappa_i u + O(u^2)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^T (T - s)e^{-\kappa_i(T - s)}\,ds &= \frac{1}{2}T^2 - \frac{\kappa_i}{6}T^3 + O(T^4) \end{aligned} \]

Substituting into the skew expression yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{T \to 0}\left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \rho_i \omega_i}{8\sqrt{\xi_0(0)}} = O(1) \end{aligned} \]

In contrast to the \(O(1)\) finite limit of diffusive models, empirical equity index options exhibit a power-law skew explosion Gatheral, Jaisson & Rosenbaum (2018):

\[ \begin{aligned} \left|\left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0}\right| &\sim C \cdot T^{-\alpha}, \quad \alpha \approx 0.4 \end{aligned} \]

No continuous finite-factor diffusion can reproduce this singular explosion without letting \(\omega \to \infty\), which destroys stability at longer maturities. This structural failure provides the core physical motivation for rough volatility.

Forward Variance Curve Extraction and Bergomi Skew

Hypothesis: Multi-factor exponential Bergomi dynamics calibrate exactly to variance swap term structures, but produce a strictly bounded \(O(1)\) ATMI skew as maturity \(T \to 0\), failing to capture empirical power-law skew explosion.

"""Lab 3 · Bergomi Forward Variance Dynamics & Skew Term Structure.

This lab implements the forward variance curve extraction from variance swap quotes,
simulates 2-factor Bergomi forward variance dynamics on seed 0 (SIMULATED),
computes instantaneous spot variance v(t) = xi_t(t), and derives the ATMI skew
term structure, showing that exponential kernels yield a finite, bounded limit as T -> 0.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def extract_forward_variance_curve(
    tenors: list[float],
    var_swap_vols: list[float],
) -> list[float]:
    """Extract piecewise-constant forward variance curve xi_0(t) from variance swap term structure."""
    xi_0: list[float] = []
    prev_t = 0.0
    prev_total_var = 0.0
    for t_i, vol_i in zip(tenors, var_swap_vols, strict=True):
        total_var = (vol_i**2) * t_i
        dt = t_i - prev_t
        fwd_var = (total_var - prev_total_var) / dt
        xi_0.append(fwd_var)
        prev_t = t_i
        prev_total_var = total_var
    return xi_0


def evaluate_fwd_curve(t: float, tenors: list[float], xi_values: list[float]) -> float:
    """Evaluate piecewise constant forward variance curve at time t."""
    for tenor, xi in zip(tenors, xi_values, strict=True):
        if t <= tenor:
            return xi
    return xi_values[-1]


def bergomi_skew_formula(
    t: float,
    xi_0: float,
    omega1: float,
    k1: float,
    rho1: float,
    omega2: float,
    k2: float,
    rho2: float,
) -> float:
    """Analytical leading-order ATMI skew in a 2-factor Bergomi model."""
    if t <= 0.0 or xi_0 <= 0.0:
        return 0.0

    def kernel_integral(omega: float, k: float, rho: float) -> float:
        integ = 0.5 * t * t if k <= 1e-6 else (k * t - 1.0 + math.exp(-k * t)) / (k * k)
        return rho * omega * integ

    term1 = kernel_integral(omega1, k1, rho1)
    term2 = kernel_integral(omega2, k2, rho2)
    return (term1 + term2) / (4.0 * t * math.sqrt(xi_0 * t))


def main() -> None:
    print("=== Bergomi Forward-Variance Dynamics & Skew Analysis ===")

    # Tenors and market variance swap strike volatilities (SIMULATED)
    tenors = [0.25, 0.50, 1.00, 2.00]
    vs_vols = [0.20, 0.21, 0.22, 0.23]

    xi_pieces = extract_forward_variance_curve(tenors, vs_vols)
    print("Forward Variance Curve Extraction from Variance Swaps:")
    print(" Tenor Range (yr) | VS Vol (%) | Instantaneous Fwd Variance xi_0(t) | Fwd Vol (%)")
    print("---------------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    prev_t = 0.0
    for t_i, vol_i, xi_i in zip(tenors, vs_vols, xi_pieces, strict=True):
        fwd_vol = math.sqrt(xi_i) * 100.0
        v_pct = vol_i * 100.0
        print(
            f"  [{prev_t:4.2f}, {t_i:4.2f}]     |    {v_pct:4.1f}%   |  "
            f"           {xi_i:10.6f}           |   {fwd_vol:5.2f}%"
        )
        prev_t = t_i

    # 2-Factor Bergomi parameters: fast mean-reverting factor + slow factor
    omega1, k1, rho1 = 2.50, 8.0, -0.65  # Fast factor (short-dated smile)
    omega2, k2, rho2 = 0.80, 0.5, -0.40  # Slow factor (long-dated smile)
    xi_ref = xi_pieces[0]

    hl_fast = math.log(2.0) / k1 * 12.0
    hl_slow = math.log(2.0) / k2
    print("\n2-Factor Bergomi Parameters:")
    print(f" Factor 1 (Fast): omega1={omega1}, k1={k1}, rho1={rho1} (half-life: {hl_fast:.1f} months)")
    print(f" Factor 2 (Slow): omega2={omega2}, k2={k2}, rho2={rho2} (half-life: {hl_slow:.1f} years)")

    # Simulate path of spot variance v(t) = xi_t(t) on seed 0
    rng = random.Random(0)
    n_steps = 100
    t_end = 1.0
    dt = t_end / n_steps
    x1, x2 = 0.0, 0.0
    v_path: list[float] = []

    for step in range(n_steps):
        t_curr = step * dt
        xi_t0 = evaluate_fwd_curve(t_curr, tenors, xi_pieces)
        var_x1 = (1.0 - math.exp(-2.0 * k1 * t_curr)) / (2.0 * k1) if k1 > 0 else t_curr
        var_x2 = (1.0 - math.exp(-2.0 * k2 * t_curr)) / (2.0 * k2) if k2 > 0 else t_curr
        convexity = 0.5 * (omega1 * omega1 * var_x1 + omega2 * omega2 * var_x2)
        v_t = xi_t0 * math.exp(omega1 * x1 + omega2 * x2 - convexity)
        v_path.append(v_t)

        dw1 = rng.gauss(0.0, math.sqrt(dt))
        dw2 = rng.gauss(0.0, math.sqrt(dt))
        x1 += -k1 * x1 * dt + dw1
        x2 += -k2 * x2 * dt + dw2

    print("\nSimulated Spot Variance v(t) over 1 Year (100 steps, seed 0):")
    print(f"  v(0) = {v_path[0]:.5f} (vol: {math.sqrt(v_path[0]) * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"  v(0.25) = {v_path[25]:.5f} (vol: {math.sqrt(v_path[25]) * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"  v(0.50) = {v_path[50]:.5f} (vol: {math.sqrt(v_path[50]) * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"  v(1.00) = {v_path[-1]:.5f} (vol: {math.sqrt(v_path[-1]) * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"  Path Range: [{min(v_path):.5f}, {max(v_path):.5f}]")

    print("\nTerm Structure of ATMI Skew under 2-Factor Bergomi:")
    print(" Maturity T (yr) | ATMI Skew (d sigma / d ln K) ")
    print("-------------------------------------------------")
    test_maturities = [0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 1.00, 2.00]
    for mat in test_maturities:
        skew = bergomi_skew_formula(mat, xi_ref, omega1, k1, rho1, omega2, k2, rho2)
        print(f"      {mat:4.2f}       |            {skew:8.4f} ")

    lim_skew_0 = (rho1 * omega1 + rho2 * omega2) / (8.0 * math.sqrt(xi_ref))
    print(f"\nTheoretical Limit as T -> 0: {lim_skew_0:.4f}")
    print("Key Result: Even multi-factor exponential kernels produce a finite, flat skew")
    print("as T -> 0, proving that standard diffusion cannot capture market short-dated skew explosion.")


main()

Where the canon breaks

The primary breakdown of classical forward-variance modeling lies in the diffusive bound on short-dated skew and the joint SPX/VIX calibration conflict:

  • Finite Diffusive Skew Limit: Because the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck factors \(X_t^i\) are continuous semimartingales driven by standard Brownian motion, the integrated covariance scales as \(O(T^2)\). The resulting ATMI skew converges to a finite constant limit \(\frac{\sum \rho_i \omega_i}{8\sqrt{\xi_0}} = O(1)\). It is mathematically impossible for any finite-factor continuous diffusion to replicate the empirical power-law explosion \(T^{-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha \approx 0.4\).
  • Joint SPX/VIX smile calibration conflict: Calibrating to the steep upward-sloping implied volatility smile of short-dated VIX call options requires setting vol-of-vol \(\omega \ge 3.0\). However, imposing \(\omega \ge 3.0\) on the SPX smile generates excessive put wing curvature and severely overprices out-of-the-money SPX puts.
  • Non-Markovianity Trade-off: To break the \(O(1)\) skew barrier, one must replace the exponential decay kernel \(e^{-\kappa(T-t)}\) with a singular fractional kernel \((T-t)^{H-1/2}\) with \(H < 1/2\). Doing so destroys the Markovian property of the state variables, requiring infinite-dimensional forward curve tracking or rough path integrations.

Misconceptions

  • Forward variance models require iterative numerical calibration to match market variance swap prices.

    In classical spot-variance models, fitting the initial term structure requires numerical optimization over model parameters. In forward variance models, the initial forward variance curve \(\xi_0(T) = \frac{\partial}{\partial T}(T \cdot \mathrm{VS}(0, T))\) is an exact functional input. By construction, the model automatically prices every vanilla variance swap with zero calibration error, exactly as the Heath–Jarrow–Morton framework matches the initial yield curve.

  • Adding more exponential factors enables diffusive models to reproduce short-term skew explosion.

    Any linear combination of exponential Ornstein–Uhlenbeck factors \(\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i e^{-\kappa_i(T - t)}\) has an analytic kernel that is smooth and non-singular at the origin (\(K(0) < \infty\)). As proven in Theorem 3.1, the resulting ATMI skew limit is always bounded by \(\frac{1}{8\sqrt{\xi_0}}\sum \rho_i \omega_i < \infty\). Generating a true power-law skew explosion \(T^{-\alpha}\) requires a singular kernel where \(K(0) = \infty\), which is mathematically impossible within continuous Brownian semimartingale diffusions.

  • VIX futures prices equal the square root of the forward variance swap strike.

    The VIX index is the square root of a 30-day integrated variance. Because the square-root function is strictly concave, Jensen's inequality guarantees that \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathrm{VIX}_T] < \sqrt{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathrm{VIX}_T^2]}\). The gap between the forward variance swap vol \(\sqrt{\mathrm{VS}(T, T+\Delta)}\) and the VIX futures price is the convexity adjustment. Approximating VIX futures directly by forward variance swap quotes creates systematic upward bias.

Problem set

Mechanics

Suppose the 6-month and 1-year variance swap strike volatilities are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{VS}}(0, 0.5) = 18.0\%\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{VS}}(0, 1.0) = 22.0\%\). Compute the instantaneous forward variance \(\xi_0(t)\) on the interval \(t \in (0.5, 1.0]\) and its equivalent forward annualized volatility.

Worked solution

Total variance at \(T_1 = 0.5\) is \(w_1 = (0.18)^2 \times 0.5 = 0.0324 \times 0.5 = 0.0162\).

Total variance at \(T_2 = 1.0\) is \(w_2 = (0.22)^2 \times 1.0 = 0.0484\).

The forward variance on \((0.5, 1.0]\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \xi_0(0.5, 1.0) &= \frac{w_2 - w_1}{T_2 - T_1} \\ &= \frac{0.0484 - 0.0162}{0.5} \\ &= \frac{0.0322}{0.5} = 0.0644 \end{aligned} \]

The equivalent forward volatility is \(\sqrt{0.0644} \approx 0.25377 = 25.38\%\).

Verify that for a 1-factor Bergomi model \(\xi_t(T) = \xi_0(T)\exp\left(\omega X_t - \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 \mathrm{Var}(X_t)\right)\) with \(dX_t = -\kappa X_t\,dt + dW_t\), the drift of \(\xi_t(T)\) vanishes under \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Worked solution

Consider the forward-shifted driver \(X_t^T = \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(T-s)}dW_s\). By Itô's formula, \(dX_t^T = e^{-\kappa(T-t)}dW_t\). The deterministic variance is \(\mathrm{Var}(X_t^T) = \int_0^t e^{-2\kappa(T-s)}ds\), so \(\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Var}(X_t^T) = e^{-2\kappa(T-t)}\).

Let \(Y_t = \omega X_t^T - \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 \mathrm{Var}(X_t^T)\). Then \(dY_t = \omega e^{-\kappa(T-t)}dW_t - \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 e^{-2\kappa(T-t)}dt\).

By Itô's formula applied to \(\xi_t(T) = \xi_0(T)e^{Y_t}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} d\xi_t(T) &= \xi_t(T)\left[dY_t + \frac{1}{2}(dY_t)^2\right] \\ &= \xi_t(T)\left[\omega e^{-\kappa(T-t)}dW_t - \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 e^{-2\kappa(T-t)}dt + \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 e^{-2\kappa(T-t)}dt\right] \\ &= \xi_t(T)\omega e^{-\kappa(T-t)}dW_t \end{aligned} \]

The drift is identically zero, confirming that \(\xi_t(T)\) is a true \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale for each fixed maturity \(T\).

A 2-factor Bergomi model has mean-reversion rates \(\kappa_1 = 12.0\) and \(\kappa_2 = 0.40\). Compute the half-lives of both variance shock factors in days and months.

Worked solution

The half-life \(t_{1/2}\) satisfies \(e^{-\kappa t_{1/2}} = 0.5 \implies t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\kappa}\).

For factor 1: \(t_{1/2}^{(1)} = \frac{0.69315}{12.0} \approx 0.05776\) years \(= 0.05776 \times 365 \approx 21.08\) calendar days (or \(\approx 0.69\) months).

For factor 2: \(t_{1/2}^{(2)} = \frac{0.69315}{0.40} \approx 1.7329\) years \(\approx 20.8\) months.

This confirms that Factor 1 models high-frequency spot volatility shocks, while Factor 2 governs persistent regime shifts in the overall variance curve.

Proof

Formally prove that under any risk-neutral pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the price of a VIX futures contract maturing at \(T\) satisfies \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathrm{VIX}_T] \le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathrm{VIX}_T^2]}\), and state the exact condition under which strict inequality holds.

Worked solution

Let \(X = \mathrm{VIX}_T^2\). By definition, \(\mathrm{VIX}_T = \sqrt{X}\). The square-root function \(g(x) = \sqrt{x}\) is twice differentiable on \((0, \infty)\) with \(g''(x) = -\frac{1}{4}x^{-3/2} < 0\), so \(g\) is strictly concave.

By Jensen's inequality for concave functions:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[g(X)\right] &\le g\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X]\right) \\ \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\mathrm{VIX}_T\right] &\le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\mathrm{VIX}_T^2\right]} \end{aligned} \]

Strict inequality holds if and only if \(X\) is not almost surely constant under \(\mathbb{Q}\), which is true whenever the volatility-of-volatility \(\omega > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that in a 1-factor Bergomi model, \(\lim_{T \to 0}\left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} = \frac{\rho \omega}{8\sqrt{\xi_0(0)}}\).

Worked solution

From Theorem 3.1, the leading-order ATMI skew is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} &= \frac{\rho \omega \int_0^T (T - s)e^{-\kappa(T-s)}ds}{4 T \sqrt{T \cdot \int_0^T \xi_0(u)du}} \end{aligned} \]

Let \(u = T - s\). The integral in the numerator is \(\int_0^T u e^{-\kappa u}du = \frac{1}{\kappa^2}\left[1 - e^{-\kappa T}(1 + \kappa T)\right]\). Using Taylor expansion \(e^{-\kappa T} = 1 - \kappa T + \frac{1}{2}\kappa^2 T^2 - \frac{1}{6}\kappa^3 T^3 + O(T^4)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 - e^{-\kappa T}(1 + \kappa T) &= 1 - \left(1 - \kappa T + \frac{1}{2}\kappa^2 T^2 - \frac{1}{6}\kappa^3 T^3\right)(1 + \kappa T) + O(T^4) \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\kappa^2 T^2 - \frac{1}{3}\kappa^3 T^3 + O(T^4) \end{aligned} \]

Dividing by \(\kappa^2\), the numerator integral is \(\frac{1}{2}T^2 - \frac{1}{3}\kappa T^3 + O(T^4)\).

For the denominator, \(\int_0^T \xi_0(u)du = \xi_0(0)T + O(T^2)\), so \(\sqrt{T \int_0^T \xi_0(u)du} = T\sqrt{\xi_0(0)} + O(T^2)\). The denominator is \(4 T \cdot T\sqrt{\xi_0(0)} = 4 T^2 \sqrt{\xi_0(0)}\).

Taking the limit as \(T \to 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{T \to 0}\left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} &= \frac{\rho \omega \left(\frac{1}{2}T^2\right)}{4 T^2 \sqrt{\xi_0(0)}} = \frac{\rho \omega}{8\sqrt{\xi_0(0)}} \quad \blacksquare \end{aligned} \]

Prove that any forward variance curve \(\xi_t(T)\) must satisfy \(\xi_t(T) \ge 0\) for all \(T \ge t\) almost surely to prevent calendar arbitrage in vanilla variance swaps.

Worked solution

Suppose on the contrary that there exists an interval \([T_1, T_2]\) with \(T_2 > T_1 \ge t\) such that \(\mathbb{Q}(\xi_t(u) < 0 \text{ for } u \in [T_1, T_2]) > 0\).

Then the fair forward variance swap strike over \([T_1, T_2]\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{FVS}_t(T_1, T_2) &= \frac{1}{T_2 - T_1}\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\xi_t(u)\,du < 0 \end{aligned} \]

An arbitrageur can enter a long forward variance swap at a negative strike \(K < 0\). At maturity \(T_2\), the payoff is \(\frac{1}{T_2 - T_1}\int_{T_1}^{T_2} v_u\,du - K\). Since instantaneous variance \(v_u = \left(\frac{d\langle \ln S \rangle_u}{du}\right) \ge 0\) by non-negativity of quadratic variation, the realized variance is strictly non-negative. Hence the terminal payoff is at least \(-K > 0\) with probability 1, generating an arbitrage opportunity with strictly positive payout and zero initial capital. Thus \(\xi_t(T) \ge 0\) is a necessary condition for no-arbitrage. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

A 1-factor Bergomi model is calibrated to the 1-month SPX ATMI skew (\(\partial_k \sigma \approx -1.2\)) with spot variance \(\xi_0 = 0.04\) (\(\sigma_0 = 20\%\)) and maximum correlation \(\rho = -0.9\). Compute the required vol-of-vol \(\omega\), and contrast it with the value \(\omega \approx 3.5\) required to fit the ATM 1-month VIX option implied volatility.

Worked solution

Using the short-time skew formula \(\partial_k \sigma \approx \frac{\rho \omega}{8\sqrt{\xi_0}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} -1.2 &= \frac{-0.9 \cdot \omega}{8 \times 0.20} = \frac{-0.9 \cdot \omega}{1.60} \\ \omega &= \frac{1.2 \times 1.60}{0.9} = \frac{1.92}{0.9} \approx 2.133 \end{aligned} \]

Fitting the SPX skew requires \(\omega \approx 2.13\). However, VIX call option implied volatilities for 1-month options regularly trade at 80% to 120%, which requires \(\omega \ge 3.5\). If \(\omega = 3.5\) is used in the 1-factor Bergomi model, the implied SPX skew becomes \(\frac{-0.9 \times 3.5}{1.60} \approx -1.97\), severely overestimating the SPX skew and generating excessive deep-OTM put prices. This empirical discrepancy demonstrates the fundamental limitation of 1-factor diffusive models.

Using the 2-factor Bergomi parameters from Lab 3 (\(\omega_1 = 2.50, \kappa_1 = 8.0, \rho_1 = -0.65\), \(\omega_2 = 0.80, \kappa_2 = 0.5, \rho_2 = -0.40\), \(\xi_0 = 0.04\)), compute the ATMI skew for \(T = 0.05\) (approx 2.5 weeks) and \(T = 1.0\) year.

Worked solution

For Factor 1 at \(T = 0.05\): \(\kappa_1 T = 8.0 \times 0.05 = 0.40\). \(\int_0^T (T-s)e^{-\kappa_1(T-s)}ds = \frac{0.40 - 1 + e^{-0.40}}{64} = \frac{0.40 - 1 + 0.67032}{64} = \frac{0.07032}{64} \approx 0.0010988\).

For Factor 2 at \(T = 0.05\): \(\kappa_2 T = 0.5 \times 0.05 = 0.025\). \(\int_0^T (T-s)e^{-\kappa_2(T-s)}ds \approx \frac{1}{2}(0.05)^2 = 0.00125\).

The numerator sum: \((-0.65 \times 2.50 \times 0.0010988) + (-0.40 \times 0.80 \times 0.00125) = -0.0017855 - 0.0004000 = -0.0021855\).

The denominator: \(4 \times 0.05 \times \sqrt{0.04 \times 0.05} = 0.20 \times \sqrt{0.002} = 0.20 \times 0.044721 \approx 0.0089443\).

Ratio: \(\frac{-0.0021855}{0.0089443} \approx -0.2443\).

At \(T = 1.0\): \(\kappa_1 T = 8.0\), \(\int_0^1 (1-s)e^{-8(1-s)}ds = \frac{8 - 1 + e^{-8}}{64} = \frac{7.0003}{64} \approx 0.10938\).

\(\kappa_2 T = 0.5\), \(\int_0^1 (1-s)e^{-0.5(1-s)}ds = \frac{0.5 - 1 + e^{-0.5}}{0.25} = \frac{-0.5 + 0.60653}{0.25} = \frac{0.10653}{0.25} \approx 0.42612\).

Numerator: \((-0.65 \times 2.50 \times 0.10938) + (-0.40 \times 0.80 \times 0.42612) = -0.17774 - 0.13636 = -0.31410\).

Denominator: \(4 \times 1.0 \times \sqrt{0.04 \times 1.0} = 4 \times 0.20 = 0.80\).

Ratio: \(\frac{-0.31410}{0.80} \approx -0.3926\).

A market quotes a 3-month variance swap at \(\mathrm{VS} = 0.04\) (20% strike vol) and the variance of 3-month realized variance is estimated as \(\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{RV}) = 0.0004\). Estimate the expected percentage difference between \(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{RV}]}\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{\mathrm{RV}}]\) using a second-order Taylor expansion.

Worked solution

Let \(X = \mathrm{RV}\) with \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[X] = 0.04\) and \(\sigma_X^2 = \mathrm{Var}(X) = 0.0004\).

Expanding \(g(x) = \sqrt{x}\) around \(\mu\):

\[ \begin{aligned} g(X) &\approx g(\mu) + g'(\mu)(X - \mu) + \frac{1}{2}g''(\mu)(X - \mu)^2 \\ \mathbb{E}[\sqrt{X}] &\approx \sqrt{\mu} - \frac{1}{8}\mu^{-3/2}\sigma_X^2 \end{aligned} \]

Substituting \(\mu = 0.04\) and \(\sigma_X^2 = 0.0004\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{\mu} &= 0.20 \\ \mu^{-3/2} &= (0.04)^{-1.5} = \frac{1}{(0.2)^3} = \frac{1}{0.008} = 125 \\ \frac{1}{8} \times 125 \times 0.0004 &= 15.625 \times 0.0004 = 0.00625 \\ \mathbb{E}[\sqrt{X}] &\approx 0.20 - 0.00625 = 0.19375 \text{ (19.375\%)} \end{aligned} \]

The percentage convexity downward bias is \(\frac{0.00625}{0.20} = 3.125\%\). This explains why implied volatilities derived from VIX futures trade below the corresponding variance swap strike volatility.

Research

Explain why the instantaneous spot variance \(v_t = \xi_t(t)\) in an \(n\)-factor Bergomi model is a Markovian diffusion in the state variables \((X_t^1, \dots, X_t^n)\), whereas replacing the exponential kernel \(e^{-\kappa(T-t)}\) with a power-law kernel \((T - t)^{H - 1/2}\) renders \(v_t\) inherently non-Markovian.

Worked solution

In the exponential case, the kernel factorizes: \(e^{-\kappa(T - s)} = e^{-\kappa(T - t)} \cdot e^{-\kappa(t - s)}\). Consequently, the past path of Brownian motion enters \(\xi_t(T)\) solely through the scalar state variable \(X_t = \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t - s)}dW_s\). The infinitesimal generator of \(X_t\) depends only on the current value \(X_t\), satisfying the semigroup property of Markov processes.

In contrast, a power-law kernel \(K(T - s) = (T - s)^{H - 1/2}\) does not factorize: \((T - s)^{H - 1/2} \neq f(T - t)g(t - s)\). Knowledge of the current spot variance \(v_t\) is insufficient to determine the future distribution of \(v_{t+u}\); one requires the entire continuous trajectory of \(W_s\) for all \(s \in [0, t]\). This breaks Markovianity, necessitating infinite-dimensional state representations or Volterra integral representations.

Contrast the forward variance dynamics generated by Dupire's local volatility model \(\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(t, S)\) with the Bergomi forward variance framework. Specifically, analyze how the forward variance curve responds to a shock in spot price \(S_t\) under both models.

Worked solution

In Dupire's local volatility model, instantaneous variance is a deterministic function of spot and time: \(v_t = \sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(t, S_t)^2\). The forward variance is \(\xi_t(T) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(T, S_T)^2 \mid S_t]\). Because \(S_T \mid S_t\) is driven by the single Brownian motion of the stock price, forward variance innovations are 100% correlated with stock price returns (\(\rho = \pm 1\)). Furthermore, local volatility predicts that when spot rises, the implied volatility surface moves in the opposite direction of the smile slope (the "sticky local vol" property), which counterfactually predicts that the skew flattens during crashes.

In the Bergomi model, forward variance dynamics possess independent Brownian drivers \(W_t^i\) with imperfect cross-correlation \(\rho_i \in (-1, 0)\). This allows the forward variance curve to fluctuate independently of spot moves, matching empirical conditional skew dynamics and the observed sticky-delta smile behavior.

Formulate the theoretical transition from the Bergomi model to the rough Bergomi (rBergomi) model of Bayer, Friz & Gatheral (2016). What mathematical modification converts the bounded \(O(1)\) short-time skew limit into the empirical \(T^{H - 1/2}\) explosion?

Worked solution

The standard Bergomi model specifies forward variance innovations via the exponential kernel \(K(T - t) = \sum_i \omega_i e^{-\kappa_i(T - t)}\). Since \(K(0) = \sum \omega_i < \infty\), the kernel is bounded at zero, guaranteeing that the integrated variance covariance scales as \(O(T^2)\), yielding skew \(\lim_{T \to 0} \partial_k \sigma = O(1)\).

Bayer, Friz & Gatheral (2016) replace the exponential kernel with the singular fractional Volterra kernel \(K(T - t) = \eta \sqrt{2H} (T - t)^{H - 1/2}\) for \(H \in (0, 1/2)\). As \(T - t \to 0\), \(K(T - t) \to \infty\). Integrating this singular kernel against \(T - s\) scales as \(\int_0^T (T - s)(T - s)^{H - 1/2}ds \propto T^{H + 3/2}\). Dividing by the denominator \(T \sqrt{T \cdot T} = T^2\) produces an ATMI skew scaling exactly as \(\frac{T^{H + 3/2}}{T^2} = T^{H - 1/2}\). For \(H \approx 0.1\), \(H - 1/2 = -0.4\), analytically reproducing the market's power-law skew explosion.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the instantaneous forward variance \(\xi_t(T)\) defined as under the pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\)?

    Options
    By Definition 3.1, forward variance is the conditional expectation under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) of spot variance \(v_T\) given the filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t\).

  2. How is the initial forward variance curve \(\xi_0(T)\) extracted from the variance swap term structure \(\mathrm{VS}(0, T)\)?

    Options
    Since \(\mathrm{VS}(0, T) = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \xi_0(u)\,du\), total variance is \(T \cdot \mathrm{VS}(0, T)\), whose derivative with respect to \(T\) yields instantaneous forward variance \(\xi_0(T)\).

  3. Why does the Bergomi model formulate forward variance as an exponential martingale \(\xi_t(T) = \xi_0(T)\mathcal{E}(\cdot)\)?

    Options
    The stochastic exponential of Gaussian OU drivers ensures that \(\xi_t(T) > 0\) for all horizons with probability 1 while guaranteeing zero risk-neutral drift.

  4. In a continuous \(n\)-factor Bergomi model with exponential kernels, what is the asymptotic behavior of the ATMI skew as \(T \to 0\)?

    Options
    As proven in Theorem 3.1, smooth exponential kernels yield bounded \(O(1)\) skew as \(T \to 0\) because standard Brownian increments scale as \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\).

  5. What empirical feature of index options directly contradicts the short-time behavior of standard continuous diffusive models?

    Options
    Gatheral et al. (2018) documented that market skews scale as \(T^{-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha \approx 0.4\), exploding as \(T \to 0\) rather than flattening to an \(O(1)\) constant.

  6. Why does the VIX futures price trade below the square root of the forward variance swap \(\sqrt{\mathrm{VS}(T, T+\Delta)}\)?

    Options
    Since \(x \mapsto \sqrt{x}\) is strictly concave, \(\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{X}] < \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X]}\) for any non-deterministic positive random variable \(X\).

  7. What is the role of the fast factor (\(\kappa_1 \approx 8.0\)) in a 2-factor Bergomi model?

    Options
    A mean reversion of \(\kappa_1 = 8.0\) yields a half-life of \(\approx 1.0\) month, allowing the model to fit short-dated skew without blowing up long-term volatility.

  8. What is the "joint SPX/VIX calibration puzzle" in continuous forward variance modeling?

    Options
    VIX options require \(\omega \ge 3.0\) to match steep VIX implied vol smiles, but using \(\omega \ge 3.0\) generates unphysically high SPX put wing prices.

  9. How does the forward variance framework compare to the Heath–Jarrow–Morton (HJM) framework in fixed income?

    Options
    Both HJM (for instantaneous forward rates) and Bergomi (for instantaneous forward variances) treat the forward curve as state variables to automatically fit the initial term structure.

  10. What structural modification is required to generate a true power-law skew explosion \(T^{H - 1/2}\) in forward-variance models?

    Options
    A singular power-law kernel with \(H < 1/2\) diverges at zero (\(K(0) = \infty\)), enabling the integrated covariance to scale as \(T^{H + 1/2}\) and the skew as \(T^{H - 1/2}\).
