The Heston Stochastic Volatility Model

Black–Scholes constant volatility fails to reproduce market smiles, while Dupire local volatility predicts counter-empirical forward smile flattening. Stochastic volatility treats variance as an unobserved state variable. Heston's (1993) affine square-root diffusion yields semi-analytical option prices via Fourier inversion, making it the benchmark structural model for equity and FX derivatives desks.

  • Introduces: heston-model
  • Prerequisites: volatility smile, BSM PDE, characteristic function, Girsanov theorem

1. The Heston SDE and Feller Boundary Condition

In the framework of Heston (1993), the spot asset price \(S_t\) and its instantaneous variance \(v_t\) follow a coupled two-dimensional system of stochastic differential equations under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} dS_t &= \mu S_t\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,S_t\,dW_t^S \\ dv_t &= \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^v \\ d\langle W^S, W^v\rangle_t &= \rho\,dt \end{aligned} \]

where \(\mu \in \R\) is the drift, \(\kappa > 0\) is the rate of mean reversion, \(\theta > 0\) is the long-term variance level, \(\xi > 0\) is the volatility of variance ('vol-of-vol'), and \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\) is the instantaneous correlation between asset returns and variance innovations.

Definition 1.1 (Heston Stochastic Volatility Model). The Heston stochastic-volatility model is the continuous-time bivariate diffusion \((S_t, v_t)_{t \ge 0}\) where variance \(v_t\) follows a mean-reverting square-root process (Cox, Ingersoll & Ross 1985) correlated with asset log-returns, admitting closed-form characteristic functions for the log-price.

Theorem 1.1 (Feller Boundary Classification). Let \(v_t\) follow the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross diffusion \(dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^v\) with \(\kappa, \theta, \xi > 0\) and \(v_0 > 0\). If the parameter condition Cox, Ingersoll & Ross (1985):

\[ 2\kappa\theta \ge \xi^2 \]

holds, the boundary \(v = 0\) is strictly inaccessible, and \(v_t > 0\) almost surely for all \(t > 0\). If \(2\kappa\theta < \xi^2\), the boundary \(v=0\) is regular reflecting, and \(v_t\) touches zero almost surely in finite time while remaining non-negative.

Proof sketch. Construct the scale function \(p(x)\) and speed measure \(m(dx)\) of the one-dimensional diffusion on \((0, \infty)\). The scale density is \(p'(x) = \exp\left(-\int \frac{2\kappa(\theta - y)}{\xi^2 y}\,dy\right) = x^{-2\kappa\theta/\xi^2} e^{2\kappa x/\xi^2}\). Integrating near zero, \(p(0^+) = -\infty\) if and only if \(\frac{2\kappa\theta}{\xi^2} \ge 1\), which is equivalent to \(2\kappa\theta \ge \xi^2\). By Feller's test of boundary conditions, \(p(0^+) = -\infty\) implies the origin cannot be reached from the interior in finite time with positive probability. When \(2\kappa\theta < \xi^2\), \(p(0^+) > -\infty\) and the speed integral \(\int_0^\epsilon m((0, y))\,dp(y) < \infty\), establishing that zero is an attainable, instantaneously reflecting boundary. \(\blacksquare\)

2. Risk-Neutral Dynamics and the Valuation PDE

Because variance \(v_t\) is not a traded asset, the market is incomplete, and the equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is not unique. By Girsanov's theorem, parameterizing the market price of volatility risk as \(\lambda(S, v, t) = \lambda v_t\) preserves the affine structure of the drift:

\[ \begin{aligned} dS_t &= r S_t\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,S_t\,dW_t^{S,\mathbb{Q}} \\ dv_t &= \kappa^*(\theta^* - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^{v,\mathbb{Q}} \\ d\langle W^{S,\mathbb{Q}}, W^{v,\mathbb{Q}}\rangle_t &= \rho\,dt \end{aligned} \]

where \(\kappa^* = \kappa + \lambda\) and \(\theta^* = \frac{\kappa\theta}{\kappa + \lambda}\). In derivative pricing practice, the model is calibrated directly under \(\mathbb{Q}\), so we write \((\kappa, \theta)\) directly for the risk-neutral parameters.

By the Feynman–Kac theorem, the price of any European contingent claim \(V(S, v, t)\) satisfies the Heston partial differential equation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} &+ r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \kappa(\theta - v)\frac{\partial V}{\partial v} \\ &+ \frac{1}{2} v S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + \rho\xi v S \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S \partial v} \\ &+ \frac{1}{2}\xi^2 v \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial v^2} - r V = 0 \end{aligned} \]

subject to terminal condition \(V(S, v, T) = \Phi(S_T)\).

3. Affine Characteristic Function & The Little Heston Trap

Transforming to forward log-moneyness \(x_t = \ln(S_t / S_0) - r t\), the conditional characteristic function of the terminal log-price \(\phi(u; \tau) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{i u x_T} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) with \(\tau = T - t\) has an affine representation:

\[ \phi(u; \tau) = \exp\left(C(\tau, u) + D(\tau, u) v_t + i u x_t\right) \]

Substituting into the PDE reduces the problem to a system of coupled Riccati ordinary differential equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dD}{d\tau} &= -\frac{1}{2}u^2 - \frac{i u}{2} + (i\rho\xi u - \kappa)D + \frac{1}{2}\xi^2 D^2 \\ \frac{dC}{d\tau} &= \kappa\theta D(\tau, u) \end{aligned} \]

with initial conditions \(C(0, u) = 0\) and \(D(0, u) = 0\).

Remark 1.1 (The Little Heston Trap). In the original solution of Heston (1993), the Riccati solution was expressed in terms of \(g = \frac{\beta - d}{\beta + d}\) using the positive root. For long maturities \(T\) or high vol-of-vol \(\xi\), the complex path crosses the branch cut of the complex logarithm, resulting in discontinuous jumps of \(2\pi i\) and catastrophic mispricings. Albrecher et al. (2007) and Gatheral (2006) proved that factoring the solution with:

\[ \begin{aligned} d &= \sqrt{(\kappa - i\rho\xi u)^2 + \xi^2(u^2 + iu)} \\ g &= \frac{\kappa - i\rho\xi u - d}{\kappa - i\rho\xi u + d} \end{aligned} \]

and writing \(C(\tau, u) = \frac{\kappa\theta}{\xi^2}\left[(\kappa - i\rho\xi u - d)\tau - 2\ln\left(\frac{1 - g e^{-d\tau}}{1 - g}\right)\right]\) guarantees that \(|g e^{-d\tau}| < 1\), ensuring the argument of the complex logarithm stays strictly in the right half-plane \(\operatorname{Re}(z) > 0\), completely eliminating branch-cut discontinuity.

4. Semi-Analytical Option Pricing via Fourier Inversion

Using the damped Fourier transform method of Carr & Madan (1999), the price of a European call option with strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\) is given by:

\[ C(S_0, K, T) = \frac{e^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}} k}}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \operatorname{Re}\left(e^{-i v k}\psi(v)\right)\,dv \]

where \(k = \ln(K)\), \(\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}} > 0\) (typically \(\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}} = 1.5\)) is the damping factor ensuring square-integrability, and:

\[ \psi(v) = \frac{e^{-r T}\phi\left(v - (\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}} + 1)i; T\right)}{\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}}^2 + \alpha_{\mathrm{cm}} - v^2 + i(2\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}} + 1)v} \]

Implement Carr–Madan Fourier inversion with the stable Little Heston Trap branch formulation, verify the Feller condition, price European calls across strikes and maturities on seed 0 (SIMULATED), and evaluate the short-time ATMI skew asymptote.

"""Lab 1 · Heston Characteristic Function & Fourier Option Pricing.

This lab implements the semi-analytical Heston (1993) Fourier inversion formula
with the Albrecher et al. (2007) stable branch formulation ('Little Heston Trap' fix),
computes European call prices across strikes and maturities on seed 0 (SIMULATED),
inverts Black-Scholes implied volatilities, and demonstrates that the short-time
ATMI skew converges to the finite diffusive limit rho * xi / (2 * sqrt(v0)).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import cmath
import math


def heston_cf(
    u: complex,
    t: float,
    s0: float,
    r: float,
    v0: float,
    kappa: float,
    theta: float,
    xi: float,
    rho: float,
) -> complex:
    """Characteristic function of log(S_T) under risk-neutral measure with stable branch."""
    beta = kappa - 1j * rho * xi * u
    d = cmath.sqrt(beta * beta + xi * xi * (u * u + 1j * u))
    g = (beta - d) / (beta + d)
    exp_neg_dt = cmath.exp(-d * t)

    # Little Heston Trap fix: use ln((1 - g*exp(-d*t)) / (1 - g))
    c_val = (kappa * theta / (xi * xi)) * ((beta - d) * t - 2.0 * cmath.log((1.0 - g * exp_neg_dt) / (1.0 - g)))
    d_val = ((beta - d) / (xi * xi)) * (1.0 - exp_neg_dt) / (1.0 - g * exp_neg_dt)

    return cmath.exp(c_val + d_val * v0 + 1j * u * (math.log(s0) + r * t))


def heston_call_price(
    s0: float,
    k: float,
    t: float,
    r: float,
    v0: float,
    kappa: float,
    theta: float,
    xi: float,
    rho: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute European call price using Carr-Madan Fourier inversion."""
    alpha_cm = 1.5
    log_k = math.log(k)

    def psi(v: float) -> complex:
        u = v - (alpha_cm + 1.0) * 1j
        cf = heston_cf(u, t, s0, r, v0, kappa, theta, xi, rho)
        denom = (alpha_cm * alpha_cm + alpha_cm - v * v) + 1j * (2.0 * alpha_cm + 1.0) * v
        return math.exp(-r * t) * cf / denom

    # Midpoint integration rule
    n_steps = 1000
    v_max = 100.0
    dv = v_max / n_steps
    integral = 0.0
    for i in range(n_steps):
        v = (i + 0.5) * dv
        integrand = (cmath.exp(-1j * v * log_k) * psi(v)).real
        integral += integrand * dv

    call = math.exp(-alpha_cm * log_k) / math.pi * integral
    intrinsic = max(0.0, s0 - k * math.exp(-r * t))
    return max(intrinsic, call)


def bs_call_price(s0: float, k: float, t: float, r: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    """Black-Scholes call price."""
    if t <= 0.0 or sigma <= 0.0:
        return max(0.0, s0 - k * math.exp(-r * t))
    d1 = (math.log(s0 / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * t) / (sigma * math.sqrt(t))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * math.sqrt(t)

    def n_cdf(x: float) -> float:
        return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))

    return s0 * n_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * t) * n_cdf(d2)


def bs_implied_vol(s0: float, k: float, t: float, r: float, price: float) -> float:
    """Invert Black-Scholes implied volatility via bisection."""
    intrinsic = max(0.0, s0 - k * math.exp(-r * t))
    if price <= intrinsic:
        return 1e-4
    low, high = 1e-4, 5.0
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = 0.5 * (low + high)
        p_mid = bs_call_price(s0, k, t, r, mid)
        if p_mid < price:
            low = mid
        else:
            high = mid
    return 0.5 * (low + high)

    print(f"Model Parameters: S0={s0}, r={r}, v0={v0}, kappa={kappa}, theta={theta}, xi={xi}, rho={rho}")

        iv_025 = bs_implied_vol(s0, k, 0.25, r, heston_call_price(s0, k, 0.25, r, v0, kappa, theta, xi, rho))
        iv_100 = bs_implied_vol(s0, k, 1.00, r, heston_call_price(s0, k, 1.00, r, v0, kappa, theta, xi, rho))
        print(f" {k:6.1f} | {iv_005:8.4f} | {iv_025:8.4f} | {iv_100:8.4f} ")

    print("\nATMI Skew Analysis (d(sigma_BS)/d(log(K/F)) at ATM):")
    theoretical_limit = (rho * xi) / (2.0 * math.sqrt(v0))
    print(f"Theoretical Short-Time Limit (rho * xi / (2 * sqrt(v0))): {theoretical_limit:.4f}")

    dk = 0.01
    for t_val in [0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 1.00]:
        fwd = s0 * math.exp(r * t_val)
        k_down = fwd * math.exp(-dk)
        k_up = fwd * math.exp(dk)
        p_down = heston_call_price(s0, k_down, t_val, r, v0, kappa, theta, xi, rho)
        p_up = heston_call_price(s0, k_up, t_val, r, v0, kappa, theta, xi, rho)
        iv_down = bs_implied_vol(s0, k_down, t_val, r, p_down)
        iv_up = bs_implied_vol(s0, k_up, t_val, r, p_up)
        skew = (iv_up - iv_down) / (2.0 * dk)
        print(f"  T = {t_val:4.2f} yr | ATMI Skew = {skew:7.4f}")

Where the canon breaks

The fundamental breakdown of the Heston model is its inability to generate the steep short-dated volatility skew observed in equity index option markets. In empirical equity options (SPX), the at-the-money implied volatility skew blows up as expiration approaches zero according to a power law:

\[ \left|\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} \sim C T^{-\alpha}, \quad \alpha \approx 0.4 \]

In the Heston model, because variance is driven by a standard Brownian motion \(W_t^v\), Gatheral (2006) proved that the short-time ATMI skew converges to a finite constant:

\[ \lim_{T \to 0} \left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} = \frac{\rho \xi}{2\sqrt{v_0}} = O(1) \]

To match a steep short-term market skew of \(-2.5\) at \(T = 1\) week, the Heston model requires setting vol-of-vol \(\xi > 2.5\). However, an enormous \(\xi\) catastrophically violates the Feller condition \(2\kappa\theta \ge \xi^2\), drives the variance process to touch zero persistently, and generates unphysically fat wings for 1-year and 2-year maturities, destroying the simultaneous fit across tenors.

Misconceptions

  • Violating the Feller condition \(2\kappa\theta \ge \xi^2\) creates mathematical arbitrage or breaks the Heston model.

    The Heston SDE remains mathematically well-posed even when \(2\kappa\theta < \xi^2\). Under Feller violation, the origin \(v=0\) is an instantaneously reflecting boundary rather than an entrance boundary; the process touches zero and immediately reflects back into the positive half-line. The discounted stock price \(S_t\) remains a true martingale, and option prices remain arbitrage-free. However, Feller violation requires care in Euler discretizations to prevent negative variance square roots.

  • The original Heston (1993) closed-form solution is numerically valid for all maturities and strikes.

    The original algebraic expression for \(C(\tau, u)\) uses the branch cut of the complex logarithm. For maturities exceeding 1 year or high vol-of-vol, numerical integration traverses branch discontinuities, producing negative prices or numerical spikes ('The Little Heston Trap'). Modern libraries universally use the Albrecher et al. (2007) formulation.

  • Stochastic volatility models can replicate any market implied volatility skew at short maturities if vol-of-vol is tuned high enough.

    Standard diffusive stochastic volatility models are fundamentally constrained by Brownian scaling: the standard deviation of variance over horizon \(\Delta t\) scales as \(O(\sqrt{\Delta t})\). Consequently, the ATMI skew is bounded as \(T \to 0\). Tuning \(\xi\) to match ultra-short skew over-inflates longer-dated smiles, creating an irreparable term structure distortion.

Problem set

Mechanics

A Heston model has parameters \(\kappa = 1.50\), \(\theta = 0.04\), and \(\xi = 0.40\). Evaluate the Feller ratio \(\frac{2\kappa\theta}{\xi^2}\). Is the boundary \(v=0\) accessible or inaccessible?

Worked solution

Compute the numerator and denominator:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2\kappa\theta &= 2 \times 1.50 \times 0.04 = 0.12 \\ \xi^2 &= 0.40^2 = 0.16 \end{aligned} \]

The Feller ratio is \(\frac{0.12}{0.16} = 0.75 < 1.0\). Because \(2\kappa\theta < \xi^2\), the Feller condition is violated, and the boundary \(v = 0\) is regular reflecting (accessible in finite time).

Given initial variance \(v_0 = 0.04\), correlation \(\rho = -0.65\), and vol-of-vol \(\xi = 0.35\), calculate the theoretical short-time limit of the ATMI implied volatility skew \(\lim_{T \to 0} \left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0}\).

Worked solution

Apply Gatheral's short-time skew formula for diffusive stochastic volatility:

\[ \lim_{T \to 0} \left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} = \frac{\rho \xi}{2\sqrt{v_0}} \]

Substitute the parameters:

\[ \frac{-0.65 \times 0.35}{2\sqrt{0.04}} = \frac{-0.2275}{2 \times 0.20} = \frac{-0.2275}{0.40} = -0.56875 \]

The short-time skew limit is \(-0.5688\).

Compute the unconditional long-term expectation and variance of the variance process \(v_t\) under CIR dynamics with \(\kappa = 2.0\), \(\theta = 0.04\), and \(\xi = 0.30\).

Worked solution

For the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross diffusion, the stationary distribution is a gamma distribution \(\Gamma(\alpha, \beta)\) with shape \(\alpha = \frac{2\kappa\theta}{\xi^2}\) and rate \(\beta = \frac{2\kappa}{\xi^2}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[v_\infty] &= \theta = 0.04 \\ \operatorname{Var}(v_\infty) &= \frac{\theta \xi^2}{2\kappa} = \frac{0.04 \times 0.30^2}{2 \times 2.0} = \frac{0.0036}{4.0} = 0.0009 \end{aligned} \]

The stationary standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.0009} = 0.03\).

Proof

Prove that in the Heston model, when correlation \(\rho = 0\), the implied volatility smile is symmetric around the forward price to leading order.

Worked solution

When \(\rho = 0\), the stock price and variance drivers are independent: \(d\langle W^S, W^v\rangle_t = 0\). Conditioning on the realization of the integrated variance path \(I_T = \int_0^T v_s\,ds\), the log-return \(x_T = \ln(S_T / S_0) - r T\) is conditionally Gaussian with mean \(-\frac{1}{2}I_T\) and variance \(I_T\). By Romano–Touzi (1997), the call price is the expectation of Black–Scholes call prices over the distribution of \(I_T\):

\[ C(K) = \mathbb{E}\left[C_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(S_0, K, T, \sqrt{I_T / T}\right)\right] \]

Because the Black–Scholes call pricing formula with respect to log-moneyness \(k = \ln(K/F)\) satisfies the symmetry property \(C_{\mathrm{BS}}(k) - C_{\mathrm{BS}}(-k) = S_0 (1 - e^k)\), every order of the derivative with respect to \(k\) at \(k=0\) of odd order vanishes except the delta. In particular, \(\left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} = 0\), establishing that the smile is symmetric around \(k=0\) with zero skew. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that the discounted stock price \(S_t e^{-r t}\) is a true martingale under the Heston model for any \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\) provided the CIR parameters satisfy \(\kappa > 0\) and \(\theta > 0\).

Worked solution

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the discounted stock price \(Z_t = S_t e^{-r t}\) is a continuous local martingale given by the stochastic exponential \(Z_t = Z_0 \mathcal{E}\left(\int_0^t \sqrt{v_s}\,dW_s^{S,\mathbb{Q}}\right)\). By Novikov's or Kazamaki's criterion, \(Z_t\) is a true martingale if \(\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_0^T v_s\,ds\right)\right] < \infty\). For the CIR process, the moment generating function of integrated variance \(\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda \int_0^T v_s\,ds)]\) is finite for all \(\lambda < \frac{\kappa^2}{2\xi^2}\). For general correlation \(\rho\), Andersen & Piterbarg (2007) proved that the affine structure guarantees \(\mathbb{E}[S_T] = S_0 e^{r T}\) whenever the Riccati ODE for \(D(\tau, -i)\) does not explode in \([0, T]\). Evaluating the Riccati equation at \(u = -i\):

\[ \frac{dD}{d\tau} = -\frac{1}{2}(-i)^2 - \frac{i(-i)}{2} + (i\rho\xi(-i) - \kappa)D + \frac{1}{2}\xi^2 D^2 = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} + (\rho\xi - \kappa)D + \frac{1}{2}\xi^2 D^2 \]

Since the constant term is zero, \(D(\tau, -i) \equiv 0\) and \(C(\tau, -i) \equiv 0\) is the unique solution for all \(\tau \ge 0\). Thus \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\ln(S_T / S_0) - r T}] = \phi(-i; T) = e^0 = 1\), confirming that \(\mathbb{E}[S_T] = S_0 e^{r T}\) and \(Z_t\) is a true martingale. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that as vol-of-vol \(\xi \to 0\), the Heston European call price converges to the Black–Scholes price with constant volatility \(\sigma = \sqrt{\theta + (v_0 - \theta)\frac{1 - e^{-\kappa T}}{\kappa T}}\).

Worked solution

As \(\xi \to 0\), the variance process becomes deterministic: \(dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt\), whose analytical solution is \(v_t = \theta + (v_0 - \theta)e^{-\kappa t}\). The integrated variance over \([0, T]\) is:

\[ \int_0^T v_t\,dt = \theta T + (v_0 - \theta)\frac{1 - e^{-\kappa T}}{\kappa} \]

The root-mean-square average volatility is \(\bar{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T v_t\,dt} = \sqrt{\theta + (v_0 - \theta)\frac{1 - e^{-\kappa T}}{\kappa T}}\). Because the variance path is deterministic, the asset price \(S_t\) follows geometric Brownian motion with time-dependent deterministic volatility \(\sigma(t) = \sqrt{v_t}\). By standard Black–Scholes theory with time-varying deterministic volatility, the terminal price distribution is lognormal with total variance \(\bar{\sigma}^2 T\), exactly matching the Black–Scholes formula with volatility \(\bar{\sigma}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Explain why calibrating the Heston model to a single cross-section of SPX options typically yields a correlation parameter \(\rho \in [-0.85, -0.65]\) and discuss the economic intuition behind this 'leverage effect'.

Worked solution

In equity index markets, the implied volatility smile is strongly negatively skewed: out-of-the-money put options trade at a large volatility premium relative to out-of-the-money calls. In the Heston model, the slope of the smile is directly controlled by the spot-variance correlation \(\rho\). A negative \(\rho\) means that when asset prices drop (\(dW^S < 0\)), variance spikes (\(dW^v > 0\)), fattening the left tail of the risk-neutral return distribution and generating a steep negative skew. Calibrations to SPX options consistently estimate \(\rho \in [-0.85, -0.65]\). Economically, this reflects both the financial leverage effect (as equity values drop, debt-to-equity ratios rise, increasing equity risk) and the volatility feedback / risk premium effect (investors demand higher expected returns when market uncertainty spikes, depressing current stock prices).

Suppose an options market maker calibrates Heston parameters independently on each day of a calm month versus a crisis month. Describe the empirical stability of \(\kappa\) and \(\xi\) across time.

Worked solution

Empirical studies (e.g., Bakshi, Cao & Chen 1997) demonstrate that Heston calibrated parameters exhibit significant day-to-day instability. While instantaneous variance \(v_0\) moves closely with market implied volatility, the structural parameters \(\kappa\) (mean reversion speed) and \(\xi\) (vol-of-vol) fluctuate dramatically across trading days. In calm regimes, \(\kappa\) is estimated small (\(\approx 1.0\)) and \(\xi \approx 0.30\). During market shocks, fitted \(\xi\) spikes to \(1.5\)–\(3.0\) to accommodate steep short-dated skew, and \(\kappa\) jumps to \(5.0\)–\(10.0\) to pull long-term variance back down. This parameter instability proves that the Heston model is an effective daily interpolation tool rather than a truly invariant physical data-generating process.

In foreign exchange option markets (e.g., EUR/USD), the calibrated Heston correlation \(\rho\) is typically close to zero, but vol-of-vol \(\xi\) is large. Contrast the resulting smile shape with equity markets.

Worked solution

In FX markets, both currencies are sovereign fiat assets, so neither direction represents a structural 'crash' analogous to an equity collapse. Consequently, the spot-vol correlation \(\rho \approx 0\). With \(\rho \approx 0\), the linear skew term vanishes, but a non-zero vol-of-vol \(\xi > 0\) generates excess kurtosis (fat tails) on both the upside and downside. This produces a symmetric 'U-shaped' volatility smile where both out-of-the-money calls and puts command premia over at-the-money options (quoted via 25-delta risk reversals near zero and positive butterfly spreads). In contrast, equity markets display an asymmetric smirk with deep left-wing skew.

Research

Analyze how the COS method of Fang & Oosterlee (2009) compares to Carr–Madan FFT for Heston option pricing in terms of computational complexity and truncation error.

Worked solution

Carr–Madan FFT evaluates option prices across an entire grid of strikes simultaneously in \(O(N \log N)\) operations using the Fast Fourier Transform, but requires tuning the damping factor \(\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}}\) and suffers from fixed grid spacing \(\Delta k \cdot \Delta v = \frac{2\pi}{N}\), forcing a trade-off between strike resolution and integration domain. The Fourier-cosine (COS) method of Fang & Oosterlee (2009) expands the risk-neutral density in a Fourier-cosine series on a truncated interval \([a, b]\). Because cosine coefficients of call payoffs have simple analytical closed forms, the COS method achieves exponential (spectral) convergence for smooth densities: with only \(N = 64\) to \(128\) terms, truncation error drops below \(10^{-8}\). The COS method does not require damping parameters and prices single strikes in \(O(N)\) operations, making it the preferred algorithm for calibration routines.

Evaluate the 'Bates (1996) jump-diffusion' extension of Heston, which adds lognormal Poisson jumps to the stock price. Why does adding jumps resolve the short-dated skew puzzle?

Worked solution

Bates (1996) adds compound Poisson jumps \(J_t\) with intensity \(\lambda_J\) and log-jump distribution \(\ln(1 + J) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_J, \sigma_J^2)\) to the Heston stock price SDE. Because the jump process is independent of the diffusion, the characteristic function factors multiplicatively: \(\phi_{\mathrm{Bates}}(u) = \phi_{\mathrm{Heston}}(u) \cdot \phi_{\mathrm{Merton}}(u)\). At ultra-short maturities \(T \to 0\), the diffusion skew is \(O(1)\), but the jump component generates an \(O(1/T)\) skew contribution in total variance coordinates, producing a steep \(O(1/\sqrt{T})\) implied volatility skew. This decouples short-dated smile calibration from long-dated vol-of-vol, allowing moderate \(\xi\) to fit long tenors while jumps capture short-dated crash risk.

Discuss why continuous-time affine stochastic volatility models are structurally incapable of capturing the empirical scaling law of ATM volatility skew without jumping to fractional or rough processes.

Worked solution

In all Markovian diffusion models where variance \(v_t\) is an Itô semimartingale driven by Brownian motion, the infinitesimal generator is a local differential operator. By Malliavin calculus (Alòs, León & Vives 2007), the ATMI skew is proportional to the integrated covariance between the stock price and instantaneous variance: \(\left.\frac{\partial \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}}{\partial k}\right|_{k=0} = \frac{1}{T \sigma_{\mathrm{BS}}} \int_0^T \operatorname{Cov}(S_t, v_t)\,dt\). Because Brownian increments over \([0, t]\) have covariance scaling linearly with \(t\), the time integral divided by \(T\) converges to a finite constant \(\frac{\rho\xi}{2\sqrt{v_0}}\) as \(T \to 0\). To obtain a singular power-law explosion \(T^{H - 1/2}\) with \(H < 1/2\), the volatility kernel must be non-Markovian and weakly singular at the origin, necessitating fractional Brownian drivers.

Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Heston model \(dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^v\), what is the economic interpretation of the parameter \(\kappa\)?

    Options

    \(\kappa\) governs the rate at which expected variance reverts to its long-term attractor \(\theta\); the half-life of variance shocks is \(\ln(2)/\kappa\).

  2. What boundary behavior occurs at \(v=0\) when the Feller condition \(2\kappa\theta \ge \xi^2\) is violated?

    Options

    When \(2\kappa\theta < \xi^2\), Feller's boundary test shows that zero is an attainable, regular reflecting boundary. The process touches zero but immediately returns to \((0, \infty)\).

  3. What causes the numerical instability known as the 'Little Heston Trap' in the original 1993 Heston formulation?

    Options

    In Heston's original formulation, the expression for \(C(\tau, u)\) involved \(\ln(1 - g e^{d\tau})\). As \(\tau\) grows, the argument encircles the origin, jumping across the branch cut of the complex logarithm by \(2\pi i\).

  4. In Carr–Madan (1999) option pricing, why is the damping factor \(\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}} > 0\) introduced into the modified call payoff \(c_T(k) = e^{\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}} k} C(k)\)?

    Options

    As \(k \to -\infty\), the undiscounted call price \(C(k) \to S_0\), which is not integrable in \(L^1(\R)\) or \(L^2(\R)\). Multiplying by \(e^{\alpha_{\mathrm{cm}} k}\) forces the payoff to decay to zero as \(k \to -\infty\), making the Fourier transform well-defined.

  5. What is the theoretical short-time limit of the ATMI implied volatility skew in the Heston model as \(T \to 0\)?

    Options

    Because Heston variance is driven by standard Brownian motion, the short-time skew converges to the finite constant \(\frac{\rho\xi}{2\sqrt{v_0}}\), failing to replicate the empirical power-law explosion.

  6. If the spot-variance correlation \(\rho\) is set to zero in the Heston model, what happens to the implied volatility smile?

    Options

    When \(\rho = 0\), asset returns are conditionally Gaussian given the integrated variance, resulting in an implied volatility curve that is symmetric around forward moneyness with zero skew.

  7. What role does the parameter \(\xi\) (vol-of-vol) play in determining the geometry of the Heston implied volatility surface?

    Options

    Vol-of-vol \(\xi\) dictates the variance of the variance process, generating excess kurtosis in the log-price distribution and controlling the curvature (smile depth and wing elevation).

  8. How does the Heston model handle the market price of volatility risk when transforming from \(\mathbb{P}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\)?

    Options

    Heston assumes \(\lambda(S, v, t) = \lambda v_t\), translating the drift from \(\kappa(\theta - v_t)\) to \((\kappa + \lambda)(\frac{\kappa\theta}{\kappa + \lambda} - v_t)\), which preserves the affine square-root structure.

  9. In the stationary distribution of the Heston CIR variance process, what family of probability distributions does \(v_\infty\) follow?

    Options

    The stationary solution of the forward Kolmogorov (Fokker–Planck) equation for the CIR process is a gamma distribution with shape \(\alpha = \frac{2\kappa\theta}{\xi^2}\) and rate \(\beta = \frac{2\kappa}{\xi^2}\).

  10. Why does calibrating the Heston model to fit short-dated equity index options lead to model failure across longer maturities?

    Options

    Because diffusive skew is bounded, fitting steep short skew forces \(\xi\) to unphysically large values (> 2.0). That large \(\xi\) propagates into long tenors, generating smile wings that are far wider than market quotes.
