Delta-Hedged Option Returns

In classic Black-Scholes theory, delta-hedged option positions are riskless and earn the money-market rate. In real financial markets, delta-hedged options earn deeply negative average returns, with out-of-the-money put hedges losing upwards of ten to twenty percent per month. This phenomenon is governed by the gamma-theta identity: delta-hedged profits depend strictly on the spread between realized and implied variance. Because implied volatility systematically exceeds realized volatility to compensate for unhedgeable market crash risk, delta hedgers bleed capital to theta decay. Decomposing delta-hedged returns across moneyness illuminates how options price non-linear systematic risk factors that linear stock-and-bond portfolios cannot span.

1. The Delta-Hedged Portfolio & Gamma-Theta Attribution

A delta-hedged option position is designed to insulate the option holder from first-order linear movements in the underlying price, thereby isolating the performance of second-order volatility and time-decay dynamics.

Definition 4.1 (Delta-Hedged Option Returns). Consider an option with market value \(V(S_t, t)\) on an underlying asset \(S_t\). The delta-hedged option returns refer to the cumulative rate of return on a self-financing portfolio \(\Pi_t\) formed by going long one option contract, shorting \(\Delta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\) shares of the underlying asset, and financing the residual cash balance in the risk-free money-market account:

\[ \Pi_t = V_t - \Delta_t S_t \]

Over a rebalancing increment \(dt\), the instantaneous dollar P&L of the self-financing portfolio is given by:

\[ d\Pi_t = dV_t - \Delta_t dS_t - r(V_t - \Delta_t S_t) dt \]

where \(r\) is the continuously compounded risk-free rate.

Theorem 4.1 (Continuous Gamma-Theta P&L Attribution). Suppose the underlying asset follows a continuous diffusion process with instantaneous physical volatility \(\sigma_{R, t}\):

\[ \frac{dS_t}{S_t} = \mu_t dt + \sigma_{R, t} dW_t \]

Suppose the option is priced in the market using implied volatility \(\sigma_{I, t}\) under the Black-Scholes formula, and delta is chosen as the Black-Scholes delta \(\Delta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\). Then the instantaneous self-financing hedged dollar P&L satisfies the exact identity:

\[ d\Pi_t = \frac{1}{2} S_t^2 \Gamma_t \left( \left(\frac{dS_t}{S_t}\right)^2 - \sigma_{I, t}^2 dt \right) = \frac{1}{2} S_t^2 \Gamma_t \left( \sigma_{R, t}^2 - \sigma_{I, t}^2 \right) dt + \frac{1}{2} S_t^2 \Gamma_t \sigma_{R, t}^2 (dW_t^2 - dt) \]

where \(\Gamma_t = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} > 0\) is the option gamma.

Proof. By Itô's lemma, the differential of the option price \(V(S_t, t)\) under the true underlying process is:

\[ dV_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} dt + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} dS_t + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} (dS_t)^2 = \Theta_t dt + \Delta_t dS_t + \frac{1}{2} \Gamma_t S_t^2 \sigma_{R, t}^2 dt \]

Meanwhile, the market implied volatility \(\sigma_{I, t}\) is by definition the parameter that satisfies the Black-Scholes partial differential equation for the observed option price:

\[ \Theta_t + r S_t \Delta_t + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_{I, t}^2 S_t^2 \Gamma_t = r V_t \]

Rearranging the Black-Scholes PDE yields an explicit expression for theta (time decay):

\[ \Theta_t = r(V_t - \Delta_t S_t) - \frac{1}{2} \sigma_{I, t}^2 S_t^2 \Gamma_t \]

Substitute this expression for \(\Theta_t\) into the Itô expansion of \(dV_t\):

\[ dV_t = \left[ r(V_t - \Delta_t S_t) - \frac{1}{2} \sigma_{I, t}^2 S_t^2 \Gamma_t \right] dt + \Delta_t dS_t + \frac{1}{2} S_t^2 \Gamma_t (dS_t)^2 \]

Now evaluate the self-financing hedged portfolio increment \(d\Pi_t = dV_t - \Delta_t dS_t - r(V_t - \Delta_t S_t) dt\):

\[ d\Pi_t = \left[ r(V_t - \Delta_t S_t) - \frac{1}{2} \sigma_{I, t}^2 S_t^2 \Gamma_t \right] dt + \Delta_t dS_t + \frac{1}{2} S_t^2 \Gamma_t \left(\frac{dS_t}{S_t}\right)^2 - \Delta_t dS_t - r(V_t - \Delta_t S_t) dt \]

The financing cost \(r(V_t - \Delta_t S_t) dt\) and the first-order underlying price change \(\Delta_t dS_t\) cancel identically, leaving:

\[ d\Pi_t = \frac{1}{2} S_t^2 \Gamma_t \left[ \left(\frac{dS_t}{S_t}\right)^2 - \sigma_{I, t}^2 dt \right] \]

Taking expectations under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\) where \(\mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left(\frac{dS_t}{S_t}\right)^2\right] = \sigma_{R, t}^2 dt\), we obtain:

\[ \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[d\Pi_t] = \frac{1}{2} S_t^2 \Gamma_t \left( \sigma_{R, t}^2 - \sigma_{I, t}^2 \right) dt \]

This completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 4.1 (Economic Intuition of Gamma vs. Theta). For any long vanilla option (call or put), convexity is positive (\(\Gamma > 0\)) and time decay is negative (\(\Theta < 0\)). The delta hedger incurs a guaranteed theta decay bill equal to \(\frac{1}{2} S^2 \Gamma \sigma_I^2 dt\). In return, every time the stock fluctuates, the long gamma position captures rebalancing gains equal to \(\frac{1}{2} S^2 \Gamma (dS/S)^2\). If the stock moves by more than the implied volatility hurdle (\(\sigma_R > \sigma_I\)), the rebalancing gains exceed theta decay and the hedger profits. If the stock realizes less volatility than priced (\(\sigma_R < \sigma_I\)), theta decay dominates and the hedger loses capital.

2. Why Delta-Hedged Options Systematically Lose Money

In Lesson 03, we established that equity index markets exhibit a persistent, statistically positive variance risk premium: \(\mathrm{VRP}_t = \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}}[RV] - \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[RV] > 0\). Theorem 4.1 immediately unveils the structural link between the variance risk premium and delta-hedged option returns.

Theorem 4.2 (Expected Delta-Hedged Option Drift Under Positive VRP). If the variance risk premium is strictly positive (\(\sigma_{I, t}^2 > \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[\sigma_{R, t}^2]\)), then the physical expected excess return of any long delta-hedged vanilla option position is strictly negative:

\[ \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[d\Pi_t] = -\frac{1}{2} S_t^2 \Gamma_t \cdot \mathrm{VRP}_t \cdot dt < 0 \]

Expressed as a percentage of initial option capital \(V_t\), the expected instantaneous return is:

\[ \frac{\mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[d\Pi_t]}{V_t} = -\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{S_t^2 \Gamma_t}{V_t} \right) \cdot \mathrm{VRP}_t \cdot dt < 0 \]

Because \(\frac{S^2 \Gamma}{V}\) represents the elasticity of vega with respect to option price, short-dated out-of-the-money options experience massive percentage losses when delta hedged, as their tiny capital base is eroded rapidly by the variance risk premium wedge.

3. The Cross-Section of Delta-Hedged Option Returns

In their landmark empirical investigation, Coval & Shumway (2001) studied the cross-section of S&P 500 index options over multiple decades and uncovered striking return patterns that utterly contradict the standard Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM).

Theorem 4.3 (Coval & Shumway 2001 Cross-Sectional Option Return Stylized Facts). Across the moneyness cross-section of S&P 500 index options:

  1. Negative Zero-Beta Straddle Returns: S&P 500 zero-beta at-the-money straddles earn an average excess return of approximately \(-3.1\%\) per month (\(t\)-statistic \(< -4.0\)), despite having zero market beta.
  2. Moneyness Monotonicity: Delta-hedged call and put returns decrease monotonically as the strike price moves further out-of-the-money on the downside. Out-of-the-money puts exhibit devastating delta-hedged returns averaging \(-10\%\) to \(-15\%\) per month.
  3. CAPM Failure: The CAPM beta cannot explain the cross-section of option returns. S&P 500 options price an additional, highly rewarded negative market risk factor corresponding to crash and aggregate volatility innovations.

Why do out-of-the-money puts experience much more negative delta-hedged returns than at-the-money or in-the-money options? In the pricing kernel decomposition of asset returns:

\[ \mathbb{E}[R_i] - R_f = -\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(M, R_i)}{\mathbb{E}[M]} \]

The marginal utility of wealth \(M\) spikes whenever the market suffers catastrophic drops. Out-of-the-money puts have positive payoffs concentrated exactly in these disastrous states of the world. Because OTM puts provide insurance against the states where consumption is most painful, investors are willing to pay enormous risk premia for them—rendering their expected physical returns steeply negative.

4. Discrete Hedging Error and Transaction Costs

Theorem 4.1 establishes perfect continuous replication under continuous diffusion. In realistic trading environments, market makers face two fundamental physical frictions: discrete rebalancing intervals \(\Delta t\) and bid-ask transaction costs.

Theorem 4.4 (Discrete Rehedging Variance). When a delta hedge is updated discretely at intervals \(\Delta t = T/N\), the total accumulated tracking error variance over \([0, T]\) does not converge to zero with stock volatility, but satisfies the asymptotic limit:

\[ \mathrm{Var}\left( \Pi_T - \Pi_0 \right) = \frac{1}{2} \Delta t \int_0^T S_t^4 \Gamma_t^2 \sigma_{R, t}^4 dt + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3/2}) \]

Consequently, the standard deviation of discrete hedging error shrinks at rate \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\Delta t})\).

To reduce discrete hedging error, a trader might wish to rebalance every minute (\(\Delta t \to 0\)). However, doing so triggers catastrophic transaction costs. Under proportional bid-ask transaction costs \(\kappa = \frac{S_{\text{ask}} - S_{\text{bid}}}{2S}\), Leland (1985) demonstrated that discrete hedging costs can be modeled by an effective adjusted volatility:

\[ \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2 = \sigma^2 \pm \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \frac{\kappa}{\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}} \]

where the sign is positive for option sellers and negative for option buyers. As \(\Delta t \to 0\), transaction costs explode at rate \(1/\sqrt{\Delta t}\), enforcing an optimal intermediate rebalancing threshold that balances gamma risk against bid-ask slippage.

"""Lab 4 · Delta-Hedged Option Returns & Gamma-Theta PnL Attribution.

This lab simulates daily delta-hedging of long European call and put options
across moneyness (seed 99, SIMULATED), verifies the continuous gamma-theta
identity dPi = 0.5 * S^2 * Gamma * (sigma_R^2 - sigma_I^2) dt, and illustrates
the Coval & Shumway (2001) empirical cross-section of delta-hedged returns.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def norm_pdf(x: float) -> float:
    return math.exp(-0.5 * x * x) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)


def bs_call_and_greeks(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, t: float) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Returns (price, delta, gamma) for a European call."""
    if t <= 1e-12 or sigma <= 1e-12:
        return max(0.0, s - k * math.exp(-r * t)), (1.0 if s > k else 0.0), 0.0
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * t) / (sigma * math.sqrt(t))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * math.sqrt(t)
    price = s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * t) * norm_cdf(d2)
    delta = norm_cdf(d1)
    gamma = norm_pdf(d1) / (s * sigma * math.sqrt(t))
    return price, delta, gamma


def bs_put_and_greeks(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, t: float) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Returns (price, delta, gamma) for a European put."""
    if t <= 1e-12 or sigma <= 1e-12:
        return max(0.0, k * math.exp(-r * t) - s), (-1.0 if k > s else 0.0), 0.0
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * t) / (sigma * math.sqrt(t))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * math.sqrt(t)
    price = k * math.exp(-r * t) * norm_cdf(-d2) - s * norm_cdf(-d1)
    delta = norm_cdf(d1) - 1.0
    gamma = norm_pdf(d1) / (s * sigma * math.sqrt(t))
    return price, delta, gamma


def main() -> None:
    print("=== Coval & Shumway (2001) Delta-Hedged Option Returns & Greeks PnL ===")
    rng = random.Random(99)

    s0 = 100.0
    r = 0.02
    sigma_implied = 0.22  # 22% implied volatility
    sigma_realized = 0.16  # 16% realized volatility
    n_days = 21  # 1 month
    dt = 1.0 / 252.0
    t_initial = n_days * dt

    print(f"Contract: T=21 days ({t_initial:.4f} yr), r={r:.4f}")
    print(f"Pricing Vol (IV)={sigma_implied:.4f}, Realized Vol (RV)={sigma_realized:.4f}")

    # Generate underlying price path
    daily_vol = sigma_realized * math.sqrt(dt)
    spot_path: list[float] = [s0]
    curr_s = s0
    for _ in range(n_days):
        z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        curr_s *= math.exp((r - 0.5 * daily_vol * daily_vol) * dt + daily_vol * z)
        spot_path.append(curr_s)

    # Moneyness strikes
    options = [
        ("OTM Put (90K)", 90.0, "put"),
        ("ATM Put (100K)", 100.0, "put"),
        ("ATM Call (100K)", 100.0, "call"),
        ("OTM Call (110K)", 110.0, "call"),
    ]

    print("\nDelta-Hedged Performance over 21 Days (IV > RV => Negative Long PnL):")
    print(" Option Type    | Initial Prem | Total PnL | Gamma-Theta Pred | Return on Prem ")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    for label, strike, opt_type in options:
        # Initial option price and delta
        if opt_type == "call":
            p0, d0, _ = bs_call_and_greeks(s0, strike, r, sigma_implied, t_initial)
        else:
            p0, d0, _ = bs_put_and_greeks(s0, strike, r, sigma_implied, t_initial)

        # Self-financing delta hedge: long option (+P0), short delta shares (-d0 * S0)
        cash = -p0 + d0 * s0
        delta = d0
        gamma_theta_accum = 0.0

        for step in range(n_days):
            t_rem = t_initial - step * dt
            s_curr = spot_path[step]
            s_next = spot_path[step + 1]

            if opt_type == "call":
                _, _, g_curr = bs_call_and_greeks(s_curr, strike, r, sigma_implied, t_rem)
                t_next = max(1e-6, t_initial - (step + 1) * dt)
                _, d_next, _ = bs_call_and_greeks(s_next, strike, r, sigma_implied, t_next)
            else:
                _, _, g_curr = bs_put_and_greeks(s_curr, strike, r, sigma_implied, t_rem)
                t_next = max(1e-6, t_initial - (step + 1) * dt)
                _, d_next, _ = bs_put_and_greeks(s_next, strike, r, sigma_implied, t_next)

            # Daily realized squared return
            ret_sq = (math.log(s_next / s_curr)) ** 2
            # Analytical gamma-theta increment: 0.5 * Gamma * S^2 * (ret_sq - iv^2 * dt)
            gamma_theta_accum += 0.5 * g_curr * (s_curr**2) * (ret_sq - (sigma_implied**2) * dt)

            # Cash interest
            cash *= math.exp(r * dt)
            # Rebalance stock: short (d_next - delta) more shares
            cash += (d_next - delta) * s_next
            delta = d_next

        # Terminal option payoff
        s_final = spot_path[-1]
        terminal_payoff = max(0.0, s_final - strike) if opt_type == "call" else max(0.0, strike - s_final)
        # Close out stock hedge and collect cash
        final_pnl = terminal_payoff - delta * s_final + cash
        ret_on_prem = (final_pnl / p0) * 100.0

        row_str = (
            f" {label:14s} |    {p0:9.4f} | {final_pnl:9.4f} |"
            f"         {gamma_theta_accum:8.4f} |       {ret_on_prem:7.2f}% "
        )
        print(row_str)


main()

6. Canonical Breaks: Where Textbook Derivatives Break Down

Canon Break 4.1: The Fallacy of Costless Risk-Neutral Replication. Textbook quantitative finance teaches that options are strictly redundant derivative claims that can be cloned risk-free by dynamically trading the underlying stock. In reality, market frictions (discrete trading, bid-ask spreads, transaction costs) and market incompleteness (stochastic volatility and discrete price jumps) make perfect dynamic replication impossible. Options are non-redundant economic securities that complete incomplete asset markets. The negative expected returns of delta-hedged options are not market inefficiencies, but the equilibrium market prices paid to shed unhedgeable jump and crash risk.

Canon Break 4.2: Black-Scholes Delta Hedging Under Non-Flat Implied Volatility. Hedging an option using the standard Black-Scholes delta \(\frac{\partial V^{BS}}{\partial S}\) ignores the fact that implied volatility itself changes when the underlying price moves (the "sticky strike" vs. "sticky delta" phenomenon). When the market has a downward-sloping volatility smile (\(\frac{\partial \sigma_I}{\partial S} < 0\)), a drop in stock price causes implied volatility to spike, generating additional losses for short vega positions that Black-Scholes delta completely fails to hedge. Robust delta hedging requires the minimum-variance delta: \(\Delta_{\text{MV}} = \Delta_{BS} + \mathcal{V}_{BS} \frac{d\sigma_I}{dS}\).

7. Misconceptions

  • Delta hedging completely removes directional market risk.

    Why it is wrong: Delta hedging eliminates directional exposure only locally for infinitesimal price increments \(dS_t \to 0\). For discrete price jumps (\(\Delta S \gg 0\)), second-order gamma and higher-order convexities dominate. If the underlying gaps down overnight by 10%, a delta-hedged short put position experiences severe catastrophic losses because delta changes instantaneously as the underlying plunges, leaving the hedge severely under-protected.

  • If an option's implied volatility is higher than historical volatility, selling and delta hedging it is an arbitrage.

    Why it is wrong: Selling an option with \(\sigma_I > \sigma_{\text{hist}}\) is not an arbitrage; it is a leveraged short volatility trade that carries unhedged jump risk and discrete tracking error. In market crashes, realized volatility over the contract life can surge to multiples of \(\sigma_I\), resulting in catastrophic losses. The expected positive return is an insurance risk premium, not a risk-free profit.

  • Delta-hedging more frequently always improves strategy performance.

    Why it is wrong: While continuous rebalancing theoretically drives discrete tracking error to zero (\(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\Delta t})\)), in practice bid-ask spreads and transaction costs explode at rate \(\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\Delta t})\) as trading frequency increases. At ultra-high frequencies, slippage obliterates all strategy returns.

8. Problem Set

Mechanics

A trader holds a long delta-hedged position on an ATM index call option with underlying price \(S = \$4,000\), implied volatility \(\sigma_I = 20\%\), and gamma \(\Gamma = 0.0015\). Over one trading day (\(\Delta t = 1/252\)), the stock moves by \(1.8\%\) (daily realized return \(\epsilon = 0.018\)). Compute the theoretical gamma-theta P&L of the hedged position over this day.

Solution

From Theorem 4.1, the instantaneous dollar P&L increment is:

\[ \Delta \Pi = \frac{1}{2} S^2 \Gamma \left[ \left(\frac{\Delta S}{S}\right)^2 - \sigma_I^2 \Delta t \right] \]

Calculate the terms step by step:

\[ \frac{1}{2} S^2 \Gamma = 0.5 \times (4000)^2 \times 0.0015 = 0.5 \times 16{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0015 = 12{,}000 \] \[ \left(\frac{\Delta S}{S}\right)^2 = (0.018)^2 = 0.000324 \] \[ \sigma_I^2 \Delta t = (0.20)^2 \times \frac{1}{252} = 0.04 \times 0.00396825 \approx 0.00015873 \] \[ \Delta \Pi = 12{,}000 \times (0.000324 - 0.00015873) = 12{,}000 \times 0.00016527 = +\$1.983 \]

Because the realized daily variance (\(0.000324\)) exceeded the daily theta hurdle (\(0.000159\)), the long gamma position produced a net profit of \(\$1.98\).

For an option with current implied volatility \(\sigma_I = 25\%\), determine the minimum absolute daily price return \(|\frac{\Delta S}{S}|\) required for a delta-hedged long option position to break even on a single trading day (\(\Delta t = 1/252\)).

Solution

Setting \(\Delta \Pi = 0\) in the gamma-theta equation requires:

\[ \left(\frac{\Delta S}{S}\right)^2 = \sigma_I^2 \Delta t \implies \left|\frac{\Delta S}{S}\right| = \sigma_I \sqrt{\Delta t} \] \[ \left|\frac{\Delta S}{S}\right| = 0.25 \times \sqrt{\frac{1}{252}} = 0.25 \times 0.062994 = 0.015748 = 1.575\% \]

The underlying asset must move by at least \(\pm 1.575\%\) during the trading day for gamma gains to offset theta decay.

An out-of-the-money put with strike \(K = 90\) and underlying \(S = 100\) trades at price \(P = \$0.50\), with delta \(\Delta = -0.08\) and gamma \(\Gamma = 0.012\). The annualized variance risk premium is \(\mathrm{VRP} = \sigma_I^2 - \mathbb{E}[\sigma_R^2] = 0.03\). Calculate the expected daily percentage return on initial option premium of the delta-hedged position.

Solution

Using Theorem 4.2 for daily increment \(\Delta t = 1/252\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta \Pi] = -\frac{1}{2} S^2 \Gamma \cdot \mathrm{VRP} \cdot \Delta t = -0.5 \times (100)^2 \times 0.012 \times 0.03 \times \frac{1}{252} \] \[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta \Pi] = -0.5 \times 10000 \times 0.012 \times 0.03 / 252 = -1.80 / 252 = -\$0.007143 \]

Expressed as a percentage of initial option capital \(P = \$0.50\):

\[ \frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta \Pi]}{P} = \frac{-\$0.007143}{\$0.50} = -0.014286 = -1.43\% \text{ per day} \]

Compounded over a 21-day monthly horizon, the expected loss on initial option premium exceeds \(-26\%\).

Proof

Prove that for European options under Black-Scholes assumptions, an at-the-money forward straddle (\(K = S e^{r T}\)) has zero market beta under the CAPM when hedged by market weights, and show that its expected excess return under positive VRP is strictly negative.

Solution

Let \(C\) and \(P\) denote call and put prices with strike \(K = F = S e^{rT}\). By put-call parity:

\[ C - P = S - K e^{-rT} = S - S = 0 \implies C = P \]

The Black-Scholes deltas satisfy \(\Delta_C = \mathcal{N}(d_1)\) and \(\Delta_P = \mathcal{N}(d_1) - 1\). When \(K = S e^{rT}\), \(d_1 = \frac{1}{2} \sigma \sqrt{T}\). For short maturities, \(\Delta_C \approx 0.50\) and \(\Delta_P \approx -0.50\).

A long straddle consists of long one call and long one put: \(V_{\text{straddle}} = C + P\). Its delta is \(\Delta_{\text{straddle}} = \Delta_C + \Delta_P = 2\mathcal{N}(d_1) - 1 \approx 0\). Under the CAPM, \(\beta_{\text{straddle}} = \frac{S \Delta_{\text{straddle}}}{V_{\text{straddle}}} \approx 0\).

Because the straddle gamma is \(\Gamma_{\text{straddle}} = \Gamma_C + \Gamma_P = 2\Gamma_C > 0\), Theorem 4.2 implies:

\[ \mathbb{E}[d\Pi_{\text{straddle}}] = -\frac{1}{2} S^2 (2\Gamma_C) \cdot \mathrm{VRP} \cdot dt < 0 \]

Thus, even though the straddle has zero market beta, its expected excess return is strictly negative under positive VRP, violating the CAPM. \(\blacksquare\)

Consider discrete delta hedging with transaction cost \(\kappa\) per share traded. In discrete interval \(\Delta t\), the number of shares rebalanced is \(\Delta(\Delta_t) \approx \Gamma \Delta S\). Prove that expected transaction costs per unit time can be absorbed into the Black-Scholes PDE by modifying volatility to \(\sigma_{\text{eff}}^2 = \sigma^2 + \sqrt{2/\pi} \frac{\kappa}{\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}}\) for short positions.

Solution

Let \(\kappa\) be the proportional transaction cost. The cash outlay for rehedging shares over \(\Delta t\) is \(\kappa S |\Delta \Delta_t| = \kappa S \Gamma |\Delta S|\).

Assuming \(\Delta S = S \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} Z\) where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), the expected transaction cost over \(\Delta t\) is:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{TC}] = \kappa S^2 \Gamma \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} \mathbb{E}[|Z|] \]

For standard normal \(Z\), \(\mathbb{E}[|Z|] = \sqrt{2/\pi}\). Therefore:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{TC}] = \kappa S^2 \Gamma \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} = \frac{1}{2} S^2 \Gamma \left( \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \frac{2 \kappa \sigma}{\sqrt{\Delta t}} \right) \Delta t \]

Adding this cost to the continuous Black-Scholes hedging equation \(\Theta + rS\Delta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma - \frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{TC}]}{\Delta t} = rV\) gives:

\[ \Theta + rS\Delta + \frac{1}{2} S^2 \Gamma \left( \sigma^2 - \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \frac{2\kappa \sigma}{\sqrt{\Delta t}} \right) = rV \]

For option sellers (who must sell low and buy high to hedge convex liability), transaction costs increase the effective volatility hurdle by \(\sqrt{2/\pi} \frac{2\kappa}{\sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that under geometric Brownian motion, the discrete hedging error \(\epsilon_k = \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} (S_t - S_{t_k}) d\Delta_t\) over step \(\Delta t\) has variance scaling as \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)\), and that accumulated variance over \(N = T/\Delta t\) steps scales as \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\).

Solution

Over a small step \(\Delta t\), \(\Delta_t - \Delta_{t_k} \approx \Gamma_{t_k} (S_t - S_{t_k})\). The discrete tracking error is:

\[ \epsilon_k \approx \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} \Gamma_{t_k} (S_t - S_{t_k}) dS_t \]

By Itô isometry, the variance of this stochastic integral is:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_k) = \Gamma_{t_k}^2 \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} \mathbb{E}[(S_t - S_{t_k})^2] \sigma^2 S_t^2 dt \approx \Gamma_{t_k}^2 S_{t_k}^4 \sigma^4 \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} (t - t_k) dt = \frac{1}{2} \Gamma_{t_k}^2 S_{t_k}^4 \sigma^4 (\Delta t)^2 \]

Summing across \(N = T/\Delta t\) approximately independent increments:

\[ \mathrm{Var}\left( \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \epsilon_k \right) \approx N \times \frac{1}{2} \Gamma^2 S^4 \sigma^4 (\Delta t)^2 = \frac{T}{\Delta t} \times \frac{1}{2} \Gamma^2 S^4 \sigma^4 (\Delta t)^2 = \frac{1}{2} T \Gamma^2 S^4 \sigma^4 \Delta t = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t) \]

Taking the square root reveals that discrete hedging standard deviation shrinks at rate \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\Delta t})\). \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

In the Coval & Shumway (2001) empirical dataset, compare the monthly delta-hedged returns of 5% OTM puts (\(K/S = 0.95\)) versus 5% ITM puts (\(K/S = 1.05\)). Explain why the OTM put exhibits a more negative return despite having a smaller absolute dollar delta.

Solution

In empirical equity index data (Coval & Shumway 2001, Table 4), 5% OTM puts have average delta-hedged returns of approximately \(-9.5\%\) per month, whereas 5% ITM puts have average returns of approximately \(-2.1\%\) per month.

The mathematical driver is the leverage ratio \(\frac{S^2 \Gamma}{V}\). An OTM put has a low dollar premium \(V\), but high relative sensitivity to volatility shocks and tail crashes. Because crash risk is heavily priced into OTM put implied volatilities (the volatility skew), the spread \(\sigma_I^2 - \mathbb{E}[\sigma_R^2]\) is largest for low-strike options. When divided by the tiny initial premium \(V\), the percentage drain on capital is magnified by a factor of 4 to 5 compared to deep in-the-money options.

A trader delta-hedges an index option book under the assumption that implied volatility is fixed ("sticky strike", \(\frac{d\sigma_I}{dS} = 0\)). During an equity market sell-off of 3%, market implied volatility surges by 4 percentage points (\(\frac{d\sigma_I}{dS} = -1.33\)). If the book has vega \(\mathcal{V} = +\$200{,}000\) per vol point, compute the unhedged P&L error caused by relying on Black-Scholes delta rather than minimum-variance delta.

Solution

The total derivative of the option price with respect to underlying stock is:

\[ \frac{dV}{dS} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial \sigma_I} \frac{d\sigma_I}{dS} = \Delta_{BS} + \mathcal{V} \frac{d\sigma_I}{dS} \]

Under the "sticky strike" assumption, the trader hedges only \(\Delta_{BS}\), leaving an unhedged exposure to the co-movement between price and volatility: \(\Delta_{\text{unhedged}} = \mathcal{V} \frac{d\sigma_I}{dS}\).

For a volatility increase of \(+4\) percentage points, the vega profit is:

\[ \Delta P\&L_{\text{vega}} = \mathcal{V} \times \Delta \sigma_I = \$200{,}000 \times (+4.0) = +\$800{,}000 \]

If the trader was short the option book, the unhedged loss from this volatility spike would be \(-\$800{,}000\), which was completely unaccounted for by the Black-Scholes delta hedge.

Using the Leland formula, consider a portfolio with \(\sigma = 20\%\), stock price \(S = 100\), and transaction cost \(\kappa = 10\text{ bps} = 0.0010\). Compare the effective volatility adjustment for daily rebalancing (\(\Delta t = 1/252\)) versus hourly rebalancing (\(\Delta t = 1/(252 \times 6.5)\)).

Solution

The Leland volatility spread adjustment is \(\Delta \sigma^2 \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \frac{2\kappa \sigma}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}\).

For daily rebalancing (\(\Delta t = 1/252 = 0.003968\), \(\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.06299\)):

\[ \Delta \sigma^2_{\text{daily}} = 0.7979 \times \frac{2 \times 0.0010 \times 0.20}{0.06299} = 0.7979 \times \frac{0.00040}{0.06299} = 0.005067 \] \[ \sigma_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{0.20^2 + 0.005067} = \sqrt{0.045067} \approx 21.23\% \quad (+1.23\% \text{ vol drag}) \]

For hourly rebalancing (\(\Delta t = 1/(252 \times 6.5) = 0.0006105\), \(\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.02471\)):

\[ \Delta \sigma^2_{\text{hourly}} = 0.7979 \times \frac{0.00040}{0.02471} = 0.012916 \] \[ \sigma_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{0.04000 + 0.012916} = \sqrt{0.052916} \approx 23.00\% \quad (+3.00\% \text{ vol drag}) \]

Rebalancing hourly increases effective transaction friction by nearly 2.5 times, heavily penalizing short-term volatility arbitrageurs.

Research

Under a Merton (1976) jump-diffusion model where stock price jumps discretely by percentage \(Y - 1\) with Poisson intensity \(\lambda\), prove that a continuous delta hedge cannot eliminate risk, and derive the residual jump exposure.

Solution

Let \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t + S_{t^-}(Y-1) dN_t\). The change in option price across a discrete jump is \(V(S_{t^-} Y) - V(S_{t^-})\).

The delta-hedged portfolio change across the jump is:

\[ \Delta \Pi_{\text{jump}} = [V(S_{t^-} Y) - V(S_{t^-})] - \Delta_t S_{t^-} (Y - 1) \]

By Taylor expansion with Lagrange remainder:

\[ V(SY) - V(S) = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} S(Y - 1) + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}(\xi) S^2 (Y - 1)^2 \]

Substituting into the portfolio change yields:

\[ \Delta \Pi_{\text{jump}} = \frac{1}{2} S^2 (Y - 1)^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}(\xi) \neq 0 \]

Because the jump size \(Y\) is random and continuous, no single linear underlying hedge can zero out the jump risk across all possible jump magnitudes. Markets are incomplete, and options carry non-zero jump risk premia that cannot be eliminated by dynamic delta hedging. \(\blacksquare\)

How does the empirical fact that OTM puts have deeply negative delta-hedged returns constrain the shape of the stochastic discount factor \(M(R_m)\) as a function of market returns? Explain the pricing kernel puzzle.

Solution

Under standard power utility, the pricing kernel is \(M = (R_m)^{-\gamma}\), which is monotonically decreasing and convex. Coval & Shumway (2001) demonstrate that while monotonic power utility can qualitatively predict negative delta-hedged put returns, the empirical magnitude of OTM put losses requires implausibly high risk aversion (\(\gamma > 30\)).

Furthermore, empirical cross-sectional studies (e.g. Bakshi, Kapadia & Madan 2003) show that the empirical pricing kernel projected onto market returns is U-shaped: it declines steeply for negative market returns, but flattens or rises slightly for large positive market returns. This violates standard monotonic expected utility and indicates that options reflect separate risk premia for volatility innovations and market jump intensity.

Formulate the optimization problem for the minimum-variance delta hedge \(\Delta^* = \arg\min_{\Delta} \mathrm{Var}(dV - \Delta dS)\) in a local volatility model where \(d\sigma_I = \alpha dS + \beta dW^\perp\), and derive \(\Delta^*\).

Solution

Let \(dV = \Delta_{BS} dS + \mathcal{V} d\sigma_I\). Substitute \(d\sigma_I = \alpha dS + \beta dW^\perp\):

\[ dV = (\Delta_{BS} + \alpha \mathcal{V}) dS + \beta \mathcal{V} dW^\perp \]

The hedged variance is:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(dV - \Delta dS) = \mathrm{Var}\left( (\Delta_{BS} + \alpha \mathcal{V} - \Delta) dS + \beta \mathcal{V} dW^\perp \right) \] \[ = (\Delta_{BS} + \alpha \mathcal{V} - \Delta)^2 \mathrm{Var}(dS) + \beta^2 \mathcal{V}^2 \mathrm{Var}(dW^\perp) \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\Delta\) and setting to zero:

\[ -2(\Delta_{BS} + \alpha \mathcal{V} - \Delta) \mathrm{Var}(dS) = 0 \implies \Delta^* = \Delta_{BS} + \alpha \mathcal{V} \]

Since \(\alpha = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(dS, d\sigma_I)}{\mathrm{Var}(dS)} = \frac{\partial \sigma_I}{\partial S}\), the minimum-variance delta is exactly the Black-Scholes delta plus the vega-weighted slope of the volatility smile. \(\blacksquare\)

10. Retrieval Practice Quiz

  1. In the continuous gamma-theta P&L equation \(d\Pi = \frac{1}{2} S^2 \Gamma [(dS/S)^2 - \sigma_I^2 dt]\), what happens to a delta-hedged long option position when realized volatility equals implied volatility (\(\sigma_R = \sigma_I\))?

    Options

    When \(\sigma_R = \sigma_I\), the realized convexity gains from gamma rebalancing exactly match the theta decay bill, yielding an expected excess return of zero over the risk-free rate.

  2. Why do long delta-hedged equity index options systematically lose money on average?

    Options

    The positive variance risk premium (\(\sigma_I > \mathbb{E}[\sigma_R]\)) means that option buyers pay a higher implied variance than what is realized on average, causing continuous net losses to theta decay.

  3. According to Coval & Shumway (2001), what is the average monthly return on a zero-beta S&P 500 straddle?

    Options

    Coval & Shumway (2001) find that zero-beta ATM straddles lose an average of roughly \(-3.1\%\) per month with high statistical significance, demonstrating that options price non-linear volatility risk not captured by the CAPM.

  4. Across the moneyness cross-section, which options suffer the steepest negative percentage returns when delta hedged?

    Options

    OTM puts have the highest vega leverage relative to premium and carry high crash insurance premia (steep implied volatility skew), resulting in devastating monthly delta-hedged losses (\(-10\%\) to \(-15\%\)).

  5. How does the standard deviation of discrete delta-hedging error scale with the rebalancing interval \(\Delta t\)?

    Options

    The total accumulated tracking error variance scales as \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\), which means the tracking error standard deviation shrinks at rate \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\Delta t})\).

  6. Under the Leland (1985) model with transaction cost \(\kappa\), what happens to trading costs as rebalancing interval \(\Delta t \to 0\)?

    Options

    Total turnover explodes as \(\Delta t \to 0\), causing total transaction cost drag to scale as \(1/\sqrt{\Delta t}\), rendering continuous replication physically impossible.

  7. What is the "minimum-variance delta" (\(\Delta_{\text{MV}}\)) when implied volatility co-moves negatively with stock price (\(\partial \sigma_I / \partial S < 0\))?

    Options

    When the smile slopes downward (\(\partial \sigma_I / \partial S < 0\)), a drop in stock price increases implied volatility, adding positive vega P&L; the minimum-variance delta adjusts Black-Scholes delta downward to hedge this co-movement.

  8. Why can continuous delta hedging not eliminate risk under a jump-diffusion process?

    Options

    Option payoffs across a jump of random magnitude \(Y\) are non-linear; a single stock share can only hedge one degree of freedom, leaving jump convexity unhedged and markets incomplete.
