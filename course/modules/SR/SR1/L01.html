The Diamond–Dybvig Architecture & Liquidity Insurance

  • Uses: financial intermediary from FC1, maturity transformation from BK1, expected utility and CRRA utility from E3, random variable from M4
  • Introduces: Diamond–Dybvig model
  • Ttheorem (deductive mathematical result)
  • Aempirically replicated finding
  • Bpublished empirical finding, not fully settled
  • Cworking paper, practitioner research or single sample
  • Dpractitioner lore
  • Xrefuted claim

Households face uncertain future liquidity needs, but productive investments require long-term capital commitments. If individuals invest in isolation, early liquidity shocks force the premature liquidation of long assets at severe discounts. A financial intermediary resolves this tension by pooling idiosyncratic liquidity risks. By offering demand deposit contracts that permit early withdrawal on demand, banks provide optimal consumption insurance, raising social welfare above autarky. This lesson establishes the mathematical architecture of Diamond and Dybvig (1983), proving how deposit contracts implement the social planner's first-best allocation.

1The Economic Environment and Technology

Consider an economy with three dates \(t \in \{0, 1, 2\}\) and a single homogeneous good used for consumption and investment. The economy is populated by a continuum of ex-ante identical risk-averse agents with total mass normalized to 1. Each agent is endowed with 1 unit of the good at date 0 and zero endowments at dates 1 and 2.

Definition 1.1 (Diamond–Dybvig model)

The Diamond–Dybvig model is a three-date general equilibrium framework of financial intermediation where risk-averse agents face uninsurable idiosyncratic preference shocks over consumption timing, and a financial intermediary provides liquidity insurance through demand deposit contracts backed by illiquid productive assets .

Assumption 1.2 (preference shocks and technologies)

The economic environment satisfies the following conditions:

  1. Preferences: At date 1, each consumer privately observes an idiosyncratic preference shock modeled as a random variable. With probability \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\), the agent is impatient (type 1) and derives utility only from date 1 consumption: \(U(c_1, c_2) = u(c_1)\). With probability \(1 - \lambda\), the agent is patient (type 2) and derives utility from consumption at either date: \(U(c_1, c_2) = u(c_1 + c_2)\). Because patient agents can always costlessly store goods from date 1 to date 2, a patient consumer consumes at date 2: \(u(c_2)\).
  2. Utility function: The period utility function \(u(c)\) is strictly increasing, strictly concave (\(u'(c) > 0, u''(c) < 0\)), twice continuously differentiable on \((0, \infty)\), and satisfies the Inada conditions \(\lim_{c \to 0} u'(c) = \infty\) and \(\lim_{c \to \infty} u'(c) = 0\). Preferences exhibit CRRA utility with relative risk aversion coefficient strictly exceeding unity: \(-c u''(c) / u'(c) > 1\).
  3. Production technologies: Two constant-returns-to-scale assets exist:
    • Liquid storage: One unit invested at date \(t\) yields 1 unit of consumption at date \(t+1\).
    • Illiquid long asset: One unit invested at date 0 yields a gross mature return \(R_2 > 1\) at date 2. If liquidated prematurely at date 1, it yields a salvage liquidation return \(L < 1\) (where \(0 < L < 1\)).

2Autarky versus the Social Planner Allocation

Under autarky, each agent manages their own portfolio without trading with others. At date 0, the isolated agent divides their 1 unit endowment between liquid storage \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) and the illiquid long asset \(1 - \alpha\). If the agent turns out to be impatient at date 1, they consume all liquid storage plus whatever they can obtain by salvaging the long asset: \(c_1(\alpha) = \alpha + (1 - \alpha) L\). If patient, they let the long asset mature to date 2: \(c_2(\alpha) = \alpha + (1 - \alpha) R_2\). Because \(L < 1\), liquidating the long asset at date 1 incurs an irreversible salvage penalty.

Proposition 1.3 (autarky consumption bounds)

Under Assumption 1.2, under autarky, early consumption is strictly bounded from above by unity: \(c_1(\alpha) \le 1\) for all feasible portfolios \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\). An isolated individual cannot achieve \(c_1 > 1\).

Proof

For any \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\), early consumption is \(c_1(\alpha) = \alpha + (1 - \alpha) L = L + \alpha(1 - L)\). Since \(L < 1\), the coefficient \(1 - L\) is strictly positive, so \(c_1(\alpha)\) is strictly increasing in \(\alpha\). The maximum occurs at \(\alpha = 1\), giving \(c_1(1) = 1 + 0 = 1\). Therefore, for any \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\), \(c_1(\alpha) \le 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Now consider a benevolent social planner who observes aggregate types. By the law of large numbers, the aggregate fraction of impatient consumers is identically equal to \(\lambda\). The planner pools all endowments at date 0, invests an amount \(I \in [0, 1]\) in liquid storage and \(1 - I\) in the illiquid long asset, and allocates consumption \(c_1\) to each impatient consumer at date 1 and \(c_2\) to each patient consumer at date 2. To avoid deadweight salvage penalties, the planner never liquidates long assets at date 1, funding date 1 consumption entirely from liquid storage: \(I = \lambda c_1\). At date 2, the remaining long investment matures, funding late consumption: \((1 - I) R_2 = (1 - \lambda) c_2\).

Theorem 1.4 (optimal liquidity insurance)

Under Assumption 1.2, the social planner's first-best consumption allocation \((c_1^*, c_2^*)\) satisfies the resource constraint:

\[ \lambda c_1^* + \frac{1 - \lambda}{R_2} c_2^* = 1 \]

and the marginal rate of substitution condition:

\[ u'(c_1^*) = R_2 u'(c_2^*) \]

Because relative risk aversion strictly exceeds unity (\(-c u''(c)/u'(c) > 1\)), the first-best allocation provides liquidity insurance, satisfying:

\[ 1 < c_1^* < c_2^* < R_2 \]

Expected utility under the first-best allocation strictly exceeds expected utility under autarky.

Proof

The planner solves the constrained optimization problem:

\[ \max_{c_1, c_2} \lambda u(c_1) + (1 - \lambda) u(c_2) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \lambda c_1 + \frac{1 - \lambda}{R_2} c_2 \le 1 \]

Formulating the Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}(c_1, c_2, \mu) = \lambda u(c_1) + (1 - \lambda) u(c_2) + \mu \left(1 - \lambda c_1 - \frac{1 - \lambda}{R_2} c_2\right)\), the first-order conditions with respect to \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) yield \(\lambda u'(c_1^*) = \mu \lambda\) and \((1 - \lambda) u'(c_2^*) = \mu \frac{1 - \lambda}{R_2}\). Eliminating multiplier \(\mu\) yields \(u'(c_1^*) = R_2 u'(c_2^*)\). Because \(R_2 > 1\) and \(u'(c)\) is strictly decreasing, \(u'(c_1^*) > u'(c_2^*)\), which implies \(c_1^* < c_2^*\).

To prove \(c_1^* > 1\), define the function \(g(c) = c \cdot u'(c)\). Differentiating yields \(g'(c) = u'(c) + c u''(c) = u'(c) \left(1 - \frac{-c u''(c)}{u'(c)}\right)\). Because relative risk aversion strictly exceeds 1 by Assumption 1.2, \(1 - (-c u''(c)/u'(c)) < 0\), which implies \(g'(c) < 0\); the function \(c u'(c)\) is strictly decreasing. From the resource constraint, if \(c_1^* = 1\), then \(c_2^* = R_2\). Evaluating the ratio at \((1, R_2)\) gives \(\frac{u'(1)}{u'(R_2)} = \frac{1 \cdot u'(1)}{(1/R_2) \cdot R_2 u'(R_2)} = \frac{g(1)}{(1/R_2) g(R_2)} > R_2\) because \(g(1) > g(R_2)\). Therefore, at \((1, R_2)\), \(u'(1) > R_2 u'(R_2)\). To restore equality \(u'(c_1^*) = R_2 u'(c_2^*)\), the planner must increase \(c_1\) above 1 and decrease \(c_2\) below \(R_2\). Hence, \(1 < c_1^* < c_2^* < R_2\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.5 (closed-form CRRA allocation)

Let \(\lambda = 1/2\), \(R_2 = 4\), and period utility exhibit CRRA with \(\gamma = 2\): \(u(c) = 1 - 1/c\), so \(u'(c) = c^{-2}\). The optimality condition is \((c_1^*)^{-2} = 4 (c_2^*)^{-2}\), which implies \(c_2^* = 2 c_1^*\). Substituting into the resource constraint:

\[ \frac{1}{2} c_1^* + \frac{1/2}{4} (2 c_1^*) = \frac{1}{2} c_1^* + \frac{1}{4} c_1^* = \frac{3}{4} c_1^* = 1 \implies c_1^* = \frac{4}{3} \]

and \(c_2^* = 2 \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{8}{3}\). Early consumers receive \(4/3 \approx 1.3333 > 1\), and late consumers receive \(8/3 \approx 2.6667 < 4\). Expected utility is \(\frac{1}{2}(1 - 3/4) + \frac{1}{2}(1 - 3/8) = \frac{1}{8} + \frac{5}{16} = \frac{7}{16} = 0.4375\), strictly exceeding autarky with 100% liquid storage (\(u(1) = 0\)).

3The Demand Deposit Contract and Sequential Service

In a decentralized market economy, the social planner cannot directly observe individual preference shocks. Depositor types are private information. Diamond and Dybvig demonstrate that a commercial bank can decentralized and implement the first-best allocation by offering a standard demand deposit contract.

At date 0, the bank accepts 1 unit deposits from all agents and invests an amount \(I^* = \lambda c_1^*\) in liquid storage and \(1 - I^*\) in the productive long asset. The contract stipulates that depositors may withdraw on demand at date 1 or date 2:

  • Any depositor who withdraws at date 1 receives a fixed promised payout \(r_1 = c_1^*\).
  • Depositors who do not withdraw at date 1 share equally in the remaining liquidation and mature asset value at date 2.
Definition 1.6 (sequential service constraint)

The sequential service constraint requires that a bank must satisfy withdrawal requests sequentially in the order depositors arrive. The bank must pay each withdrawing depositor the promised contractual amount \(r_1\) until its available liquid reserves and liquidation proceeds are completely exhausted, without conditioning current payouts on future withdrawal volumes.

Remark 1.7 (the origin of maturity transformation)

By issuing demandable liabilities paying \(r_1 = c_1^* > 1\) while holding long assets that yield \(R_2 > 1\) at date 2, the bank performs maturity transformation. When only impatient depositors withdraw at date 1, liquid reserves \(\lambda c_1^*\) exactly clear the market, and the bank delivers first-best liquidity insurance without premature asset liquidation.

Lab 1 · Diamond–Dybvig Liquidity Insurance and First-Best Contract

Verifying in exact fractions that the first-best allocation satisfies c1* = 4/3 > 1 and c2* = 8/3 < 4, delivering an expected utility of 7/16 that strictly dominates autarky.

"""Lab SR1-L01-1: Diamond-Dybvig Liquidity Insurance and First-Best Contract.

Verifies the first-best risk pooling allocation against autarky using exact Fraction
arithmetic. Demonstrates that demand deposits provide optimal liquidity insurance
by transferring consumption from late patient consumers to early impatient consumers.
"""

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    # Model parameters
    lambda_early = Fraction(1, 2)  # Probability of impatient type
    one_minus_lambda = Fraction(1, 2)  # Probability of patient type
    r_mature = Fraction(4, 1)  # Long-term asset payoff at t=2
    l_salvage = Fraction(1, 2)  # Illiquid salvage return at t=1

    # First-best social planner problem under CRRA utility u(c) = 1 - 1/c (gamma = 2):
    # u'(c) = c^(-2). Optimality condition: u'(c1*) = r_mature * u'(c2*)
    # c1^(-2) = 4 * c2^(-2) ==> c2* = 2 * c1*
    # Resource constraint: lambda * c1* + (1 - lambda) * c2* / r_mature = 1
    # (1/2) * c1* + (1/2) * (2 * c1*) / 4 = 1 ==> (3/4) * c1* = 1 ==> c1* = 4/3
    c1_star = Fraction(4, 3)
    c2_star = Fraction(8, 3)

    # Expected utility calculation: u(c) = 1 - 1/c
    def utility(c: Fraction) -> Fraction:
        return Fraction(1, 1) - Fraction(1, 1) / c

    eu_first_best = lambda_early * utility(c1_star) + one_minus_lambda * utility(c2_star)

    # Autarky benchmark: isolated agent chooses liquid storage alpha in [0, 1]
    # c1(alpha) = alpha + (1 - alpha) * l_salvage = (1 + alpha) / 2
    # c2(alpha) = alpha + (1 - alpha) * r_mature = 4 - 3 * alpha
    # For alpha = 1 (full liquid storage): c1 = 1, c2 = 1
    eu_autarky_liquid = lambda_early * utility(Fraction(1, 1)) + one_minus_lambda * utility(Fraction(1, 1))
    # For alpha = 0 (full illiquid investment): c1 = 1/2, c2 = 4
    eu_autarky_illiquid = lambda_early * utility(l_salvage) + one_minus_lambda * utility(r_mature)

    # Bank demand deposit implementation
    liquid_buffer = lambda_early * c1_star
    long_asset = Fraction(1, 1) - liquid_buffer
    c2_bank = (long_asset * r_mature) / one_minus_lambda

    print(f"First-Best Early Consumption c1*: {c1_star} ({float(c1_star):.4f})")
    print(f"First-Best Late Consumption c2*: {c2_star} ({float(c2_star):.4f})")
    print(f"Bank Liquid Storage Buffer: {liquid_buffer} ({float(liquid_buffer):.4f})")
    print(f"Bank Illiquid Long Investment: {long_asset} ({float(long_asset):.4f})")
    print(f"Delivered Date 2 Payoff per Patient Depositor: {c2_bank} ({float(c2_bank):.4f})")
    print(f"Expected Utility (First-Best Contract): {eu_first_best} ({float(eu_first_best):.6f})")
    print(f"Expected Utility (Autarky 100% Liquid): {eu_autarky_liquid} ({float(eu_autarky_liquid):.6f})")
    print(f"Expected Utility (Autarky 100% Illiquid): {eu_autarky_illiquid} ({float(eu_autarky_illiquid):.6f})")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical Diamond–Dybvig liquidity insurance theorem relies on specific structural assumptions that break in real-world financial systems:

  • Deterministic aggregate liquidity shock: The model assumes the aggregate fraction of impatient consumers \(\lambda\) is known with certainty. In reality, aggregate liquidity demand fluctuates stochastically with macroeconomic cycles. If the realized fraction of early withdrawals exceeds \(\lambda\), the bank's liquid reserves are exhausted, forcing asset liquidations even without a panic.
  • Binary unobservable types: In practice, liquidity needs are continuous and correlated across households and corporations, rather than independent binary states.
  • Complete absence of secondary markets: Diamond and Dybvig assume asset markets are entirely closed at date 1. Jacklin (1987) shows that if competitive secondary asset markets exist at date 1, individuals can trade equity shares, altering the bank's ability to cross-subsidize early withdrawers without run risk.
  • No fundamental asset risk: The model assumes the long-term investment yields a guaranteed return \(R_2\) at date 2. In modern banking, credit risk and interest rate shocks introduce fundamental solvency uncertainty.

Misconceptions

  1. “Diamond–Dybvig bank runs occur because the bank made imprudent or negative-yielding investments.”

    In the classical Diamond–Dybvig model, the bank's investment technology is fully solvent and highly productive (\(R_2 > 1\)). Runs do not originate from bad loans or negative asset shocks. Rather, runs represent a pure strategic coordination failure driven by the sequential service rule and maturity mismatch.

  2. “Under autarky, an individual can always achieve optimal liquidity insurance by saving cash.”

    Proposition 1.3 proves that under autarky, early consumption is strictly bounded by 1. Because an isolated individual cannot pool idiosyncratic preference risks with others, they must bear the full cost of self-insurance, sacrificing long-term returns to hold cash.

  3. “A demand deposit contract is inefficient because it pays early withdrawers more than their initial deposit.”

    Paying \(r_1 = c_1^* > 1\) is precisely what makes the contract socially optimal. Risk-averse households demand consumption smoothing across states. Transferring purchasing power from late patient consumers to early impatient consumers maximizes ex-ante expected utility under CRRA preferences with relative risk aversion \(\gamma > 1\).

Mechanics

M1 · Social planner first-best allocation calculation

Let \(\lambda = 1/3\), long asset mature return \(R_2 = 3\), and utility \(u(c) = 1 - 1/c\) (\(\gamma = 2\)). Compute the exact first-best consumption levels \(c_1^*\) and \(c_2^*\).

Solution

The optimality condition is \(u'(c_1^*) = R_2 u'(c_2^*)\), which gives \((c_1^*)^{-2} = 3 (c_2^*)^{-2} \implies c_2^* = \sqrt{3} c_1^*\). Substituting into the resource constraint \(\frac{1}{3} c_1^* + \frac{2/3}{3} c_2^* = 1 \implies \frac{1}{3} c_1^* + \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{9} c_1^* = 1\). Factoring out \(c_1^*\) yields \(c_1^* \left(\frac{3 + 2\sqrt{3}}{9}\right) = 1 \implies c_1^* = \frac{9}{3 + 2\sqrt{3}} \approx 1.3923\). Then \(c_2^* = \sqrt{3} c_1^* \approx 2.4116\). Both satisfy \(1 < c_1^* < c_2^* < 3\).

M2 · Bank liquid reserve sizing

A bank manages $120 million in deposits under the parameters of Example 1.5 (\(\lambda = 1/2\), \(c_1^* = 4/3\)). How much capital must the bank hold in liquid storage, and how much must it invest in the long asset?

Solution

Liquid storage must cover early withdrawals of impatient depositors: \(I^* = \lambda \cdot c_1^* \cdot \text{Deposits} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{4}{3} \times \$120\text{M} = \frac{2}{3} \times \$120\text{M} = \$80\text{M}\). The remaining endowment is invested in the long asset: \(1 - I^* = \frac{1}{3} \times \$120\text{M} = \$40\text{M}\). At date 2, the long asset matures to \(\$40\text{M} \times 4 = \$160\text{M}\), which provides \(\frac{\$160\text{M}}{60\text{M patient agents}} = \$2.6667 = c_2^*\) per patient depositor.

M3 · Autarky expected utility under log utility

Let \(u(c) = \ln(c)\), \(\lambda = 0.5\), \(R_2 = 2\), and \(L = 0.5\). If an isolated individual invests \(\alpha = 0.5\) in liquid storage and 0.5 in the long asset, compute their expected utility.

Solution

For \(\alpha = 0.5\), early consumption is \(c_1 = 0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75\). Late consumption is \(c_2 = 0.5 + 0.5 \times 2 = 1.50\). Expected utility is \(\E[u] = 0.5 \ln(0.75) + 0.5 \ln(1.50) = 0.5(\ln(0.75) + \ln(1.50)) = 0.5 \ln(0.75 \times 1.50) = 0.5 \ln(1.125) \approx 0.5 \times 0.11778 = 0.05889\).

Proof

P1 · Inadequacy of autarky for liquidity insurance

Prove that if an economy consists solely of isolated agents with no credit markets, no agent can achieve an early consumption level \(c_1 > 1\) regardless of portfolio weights \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\).

Solution

Each agent has 1 unit of endowment at date 0 allocated across storage \(\alpha\) and illiquid asset \(1 - \alpha\). Liquidating storage at date 1 yields \(\alpha \times 1 = \alpha\). Liquidating the long asset at date 1 yields salvage return \((1 - \alpha) L\). Thus total early consumption is \(c_1(\alpha) = \alpha + (1 - \alpha)L = L + \alpha(1 - L)\). Since \(L < 1\), the slope with respect to \(\alpha\) is \(1 - L > 0\). The maximum occurs at \(\alpha = 1\), where \(c_1(1) = 1\). Therefore \(c_1(\alpha) \le 1\) for all \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\). Liquidity insurance (\(c_1 > 1\)) requires risk-pooling.

P2 · Strict necessity of risk aversion exceeding unity

Prove that if consumers are risk-neutral (\(u(c) = c\)), the social planner allocation satisfies \(c_1^* = 0\) and \(c_2^* = R_2 / (1 - \lambda)\), providing zero early liquidity insurance.

Solution

For \(u(c) = c\), marginal utility is constant: \(u'(c) = 1\). The objective function is \(\lambda c_1 + (1 - \lambda) c_2\). From the resource constraint, \(c_2 = \frac{R_2}{1 - \lambda}(1 - \lambda c_1)\). Substituting gives \(\lambda c_1 + R_2(1 - \lambda c_1) = R_2 + \lambda c_1 (1 - R_2)\). Because \(R_2 > 1\), the coefficient \(\lambda (1 - R_2) < 0\). The objective is strictly decreasing in \(c_1\). The corner solution is \(c_1^* = 0\), yielding \(c_2^* = R_2 / (1 - \lambda)\). Risk aversion exceeding unity is strictly necessary for \(c_1^* > 1\).

P3 · Zero premature liquidation under planner optimality

Prove that the social planner will never choose to liquidate any positive amount of the long asset at date 1 under Assumption 1.2.

Solution

Suppose the planner liquidates an amount \(\Delta > 0\) of long assets at date 1, obtaining \(L \Delta\) in early consumption. Storing that same resource as liquid storage at date 0 would yield \(\Delta\) at date 1. Because \(L < 1\), early liquidation yields strictly less date 1 consumption than direct storage: \(L \Delta < \Delta\). Simultaneously, it destroys \(\Delta R_2\) of date 2 consumption. Because liquid storage weakly dominates early liquidation in date 1 yield without sacrificing late returns, premature liquidation is strictly Pareto-dominated. The optimal policy sets liquidation to zero.

Empirical

E1 · First-best utility gain in Lab 1

Using the numerical outputs from Lab 1, compute the percentage gain in expected utility achieved by the first-best deposit contract relative to 100% autarky liquid storage.

Solution

Under 100% liquid storage, \(c_1 = 1\) and \(c_2 = 1\), yielding \(u(1) = 1 - 1/1 = 0.0000\). The first-best contract achieves expected utility \(\E[u] = 7/16 = 0.4375\). The absolute utility gain is \(0.4375 - 0.0000 = 0.4375\). In certainty-equivalent consumption terms, \(1 - 1/c_{ce} = 0.4375 \implies 1/c_{ce} = 0.5625 \implies c_{ce} = 1.7778\), representing a 77.78% increase in effective consumption.

E2 · Liquid reserve exhaustion under aggregate demand shock

Suppose a bank sizes its liquid buffer to \(\lambda = 0.50\) with \(c_1^* = 4/3\). If an unexpected macroeconomic shock causes the actual fraction of early withdrawers to rise to \(\lambda' = 0.70\), compute the cash shortfall as a percentage of total initial deposits.

Solution

The bank holds liquid reserves of \(I^* = \lambda c_1^* = 0.50 \times (4/3) = 2/3 \approx 66.67\%\) of deposits. Early withdrawal demand is \(\lambda' c_1^* = 0.70 \times (4/3) = 14/15 \approx 93.33\%\) of deposits. The cash shortfall is \(14/15 - 2/3 = 14/15 - 10/15 = 4/15 \approx 26.67\%\) of initial deposits. The bank is forced to liquidate long assets to meet early claims.

E3 · Premature liquidation loss calculation

Continuing from Problem E2, if the bank must raise the 26.67% cash shortfall by selling long assets with salvage value \(L = 0.50\), compute the book value of long assets that must be liquidated.

Solution

To raise cash \(C = 4/15\), the bank must sell long assets \(A_{\text{sold}} = C / L = (4/15) / 0.50 = 8/15 \approx 53.33\%\) of initial deposit assets. However, the bank initially only held \(1 - I^* = 1/3 = 5/15 \approx 33.33\%\) in long assets! Liquidating all remaining long assets yields only \(5/15 \times 0.50 = 2.5/15 \approx 16.67\%\) cash. Total cash generated is \(66.67\% + 16.67\% = 83.33\% < 93.33\%\). The bank fails at date 1.

Research

R1 · Jacklin's critique of deposit contracts

Why does Charles Jacklin (1987) argue that the Diamond–Dybvig liquidity insurance allocation cannot survive if agents are permitted to trade dividend-paying equity shares in secondary financial markets?

Solution

Jacklin proves that if competitive secondary equity markets exist at date 1, patient depositors can arbitrage the bank deposit contract. If \(c_1^* > 1\), patient depositors withdraw early at date 1, take the cash payout \(c_1^*\), and purchase equity claims in the secondary market at fire-sale discounts. This unconstrained market trading breaks the bank's ability to cross-subsidize impatient consumers, collapsing the demand deposit contract unless secondary asset trading is legally restricted.

R2 · Observable versus unobservable preference shocks

Suppose a medical or legal registry could verify whether an agent is truly impatient at date 1. How would perfect verifiable state observation affect the vulnerability of banks to panic runs?

Solution

If liquidity needs were publicly verifiable, the bank could make contractual payouts strictly contingent on verified type: impatient agents receive \(c_1^*\), while patient agents are legally prohibited from withdrawing early at date 1. With state-contingent contracts, patient agents cannot execute early withdrawals, completely eliminating the strategic run equilibrium. Bank runs exist precisely because preference shocks are private information.

R3 · Aggregate liquidity risk and Allen–Gale buffers

How do Allen and Gale (2000) extend Diamond–Dybvig to accommodate stochastic aggregate liquidity demand, and why does this require state-contingent deposit payouts?

Solution

Allen and Gale introduce a stochastic distribution over \(\lambda\). If \(\lambda\) is random, fixed non-contingent deposit promises \(r_1 = c_1^*\) cause inefficient insolvency in high-liquidity-demand states. Allen and Gale show that the constrained-optimal contract must be state-contingent: early deposit payouts must adjust downward when aggregate withdrawal demand is high, sharing the aggregate liquidity risk between early and late consumers.

    \(c_1^* = 4/3 \approx 1.3333\) (Example 1.5, Lab 1).

  4. In Lab 1, what is the delivered date 2 consumption \(c_2^*\) for patient depositors?

    \(c_2^* = 8/3 \approx 2.6667\) (Example 1.5, Lab 1).

  5. What does the sequential service constraint require of a commercial bank?

    Options

    Sequential service mandates a first-come, first-served payout rule where the bank pays par until all available resources are exhausted (Definition 1.6).

  6. Under autarky without financial intermediation, what is the maximum possible early consumption \(c_1\) an agent can obtain?

    Under autarky, \(c_1(\alpha) = \alpha + (1-\alpha)L \le 1\); the maximum is achieved by holding 100% liquid storage (\(\alpha = 1\)), yielding exactly 1.0 (Proposition 1.3).

  7. Why does premature liquidation of the long asset reduce social welfare?

    Options

    Prematurely salvaging long assets at date 1 yields \(L < 1\), destroying wealth relative to liquid storage and eliminating the mature payoff \(R_2\).

    \(\E[u] = 7/16 = 0.437500\) (Lab 1, Example 1.5).
