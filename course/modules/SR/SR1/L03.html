Information, Solvency & Uninsured Deposit Runs

Government deposit insurance guarantees small retail balances, but commercial banks increasingly rely on large corporate deposits exceeding statutory insurance limits. Unlike insured depositors who never run, uninsured depositors bear immediate credit losses if a bank defaults. When rising interest rates or credit defaults impair a bank's asset portfolio, uninsured depositors monitor bank solvency metrics. Even minor fundamental impairments can trigger rapid collective withdrawals. This lesson derives the fundamental run threshold \(\theta^*\) under mark-to-market asset losses and models the structural dynamics of the 2023 regional banking panic.

1Incomplete Insurance and Liability Segmentation

In real-world financial architectures, government deposit insurance is bounded by statutory ceilings. Under Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) regulations, retail deposits are insured up to $250,000 per depositor per bank. Deposits exceeding this limit are completely uninsured and sit as general unsecured senior liabilities on the bank's balance sheet.

Consider a bank with total deposits normalized to 1 unit. Bank liabilities are segmented into:

  • Insured deposits (\(1 - \mu\)): Fully guaranteed by the sovereign insurer. Insured depositors never participate in a bank run, as their nominal claims are backed by government credit.
  • Uninsured deposits (\(\mu \in [0, 1]\)): Unguaranteed commercial balances held by corporations, venture capital startups, and high-net-worth individuals. Uninsured depositors absorb first losses upon bank liquidation.
Definition 3.1 (uninsured-depositor run)

An uninsured-depositor run is a coordinated withdrawal of bank deposits initiated by uninsured creditors who observe or infer negative signals regarding the bank's fundamental asset quality, seeking par redemption under sequential service before asset losses exhaust bank solvency .

2Asset Fundamental Shocks and the Run Threshold

On the asset side, the bank holds two asset classes against its 1 unit of deposits:

  1. A liquid cash and reserve buffer \(\ell \in (0, 1)\).
  2. An illiquid long asset book \(1 - \ell\) (such as long-term fixed-rate Treasuries, mortgage-backed securities, or commercial loans).

The long asset has a terminal payoff at date 2 that depends on a public fundamental state \(\theta \in [0, 1]\), where \(\theta\) represents asset quality or mark-to-market recovery value per unit of book assets. If liquidated prematurely at date 1 under fire-sale distress, the long asset recovers only \(\kappa \in (0, 1]\) of its fundamental value.

At date 1, uninsured depositors observe fundamental state \(\theta\). If all uninsured depositors run, total withdrawal demand is \(\mu\). The bank first expends its liquid buffer \(\ell\). If \(\mu \le \ell\), the liquid buffer covers all uninsured withdrawals without liquidating long assets. If \(\mu > \ell\), the bank must sell long assets to cover the cash shortfall \(\mu - \ell\). Total cash proceeds available from liquidating long assets at date 1 are \(\kappa \cdot \theta \cdot (1 - \ell)\).

Theorem 3.2 (fundamental run threshold)

Suppose a bank has uninsured deposit share \(\mu \in [0, 1]\), liquid reserve buffer \(\ell \in (0, 1)\), and asset liquidation recovery \(\kappa \in (0, 1]\). Uninsured depositors observe fundamental asset quality \(\theta \in [0, 1]\). Under sequential service, a run of uninsured depositors is triggered whenever the fundamental falls below the critical threshold \(\theta^*(\mu, \kappa, \ell)\):

\[ \theta^*(\mu, \kappa, \ell) = \begin{cases} 0.0 & \text{if } \mu \le \ell \\ \min\left(1.0, \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa (1 - \ell)}\right) & \text{if } \mu > \ell \end{cases} \]

If \(\theta \ge \theta^*\), available liquidity and liquidation proceeds suffice to cover uninsured demands, and no run occurs. If \(\theta < \theta^*\), available resources fail to cover uninsured claims, and all uninsured depositors run.

Proof

If \(\mu \le \ell\), the bank's cash reserves \(\ell\) exceed all uninsured claims \(\mu\). Even if every uninsured depositor demands redemption, the bank pays par entirely out of cash without selling long assets. Uninsured depositors know their claims are fully backstopped by cash, so running is weakly dominated; \(\theta^* = 0.0\).

If \(\mu > \ell\), cash reserves are insufficient to meet a full run. The bank must liquidate long assets. To satisfy withdrawal demand \(\mu\), the bank requires total date 1 cash: \(\ell + \kappa \cdot \theta \cdot (1 - \ell) \ge \mu\). Rearranging this solvency condition:

\[ \kappa \cdot \theta \cdot (1 - \ell) \ge \mu - \ell \iff \theta \ge \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa (1 - \ell)} \]

Define the raw threshold \(\theta_{\text{raw}} = \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa (1 - \ell)}\). If \(\theta < \theta_{\text{raw}}\), total liquidation proceeds fall strictly short of \(\mu\). Under sequential service, depositors arriving late in the withdrawal queue receive zero. A patient uninsured depositor who waits receives zero at date 2 because the bank is liquidated into bankruptcy. An uninsured depositor who runs receives par with probability \(\frac{\ell + \kappa \theta (1 - \ell)}{\mu} > 0\). Running is a strict best response for every uninsured depositor. Therefore, a run occurs for all \(\theta < \theta^*\). Capping \(\theta^*\) at 1.0 establishes the theorem. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.3 (comparative statics of uninsured run vulnerability)

On the interior domain where \(0 < \theta^* < 1\), the fundamental run threshold satisfies:

  1. \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \mu} = \frac{1}{\kappa (1 - \ell)} > 0\) (strictly increasing in uninsured deposit share).
  2. \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \kappa} = -\frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa^2 (1 - \ell)} < 0\) (strictly decreasing in liquidation recovery).
  3. \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \ell} = -\frac{1 - \mu}{\kappa (1 - \ell)^2} < 0\) (strictly decreasing in liquid reserve buffer).
Proof

Differentiating \(\theta^* = \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa(1 - \ell)}\) directly:

1. With respect to \(\mu\): \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \mu} = \frac{1}{\kappa (1 - \ell)}\). Since \(\kappa > 0\) and \(\ell < 1\), the denominator is strictly positive, establishing \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \mu} > 0\).

2. With respect to \(\kappa\): \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \kappa} = -\frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa^2 (1 - \ell)}\). Since \(\mu > \ell\), the numerator is strictly positive, establishing \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \kappa} < 0\).

3. With respect to \(\ell\): rewrite \(\theta^* = \frac{1}{\kappa} \left(\frac{\mu - 1 + 1 - \ell}{1 - \ell}\right) = \frac{1}{\kappa} \left(1 - \frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \ell}\right)\). Differentiating with respect to \(\ell\): \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \ell} = -\frac{1}{\kappa} \frac{\partial}{\partial \ell}\left(\frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \ell}\right) = -\frac{1}{\kappa} \frac{(1 - \mu)}{(1 - \ell)^2} = -\frac{1 - \mu}{\kappa (1 - \ell)^2} < 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.4 (retail bank versus uninsured regional bank)

Consider a liquid buffer \(\ell = 0.20\) and liquidation recovery \(\kappa = 0.70\).

  • Retail Bank (\(\mu = 0.20\)): Uninsured deposit share is 20%. Because \(\mu \le \ell\), cash reserves fully cover uninsured deposits. The run threshold is \(\theta^* = 0.0000\). The bank is completely immune to uninsured depositor runs.
  • Regional Bank (\(\mu = 0.80\)): Uninsured deposit share is 80%. The run threshold is \(\theta^* = \frac{0.80 - 0.20}{0.70 \times (1 - 0.20)} = \frac{0.60}{0.56} = 1.0714 \implies \theta^* = 1.0000\). Even under perfect asset quality (\(\theta = 1.0\)), the bank cannot cover uninsured claims if liquidation discounts exist, making it acutely fragile.

Lab 1 · Uninsured Depositor Run Thresholds and Solvency Limits

Verifying on a grid of uninsured shares mu in [0.20, 0.90] and liquidation values kappa in [0.50, 0.90] that theta* rises monotonically from 0.00 to 1.00 as uninsured share increases.

"""Lab SR1-L03-1: Uninsured Depositor Run Thresholds and Solvency Limits.

Calculates the exact fundamental run threshold theta*(mu, kappa, ell) across a grid
of uninsured deposit shares and asset liquidation recovery rates. Demonstrates that
high uninsured deposit concentration dramatically raises bank run vulnerability,
reducing tolerance for asset mark-to-market declines.
"""

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    # Model parameters
    ell = Fraction(2, 10)  # Liquid reserve buffer = 0.20
    kappas = [Fraction(5, 10), Fraction(7, 10), Fraction(9, 10)]
    mus = [Fraction(2, 10), Fraction(4, 10), Fraction(6, 10), Fraction(8, 10), Fraction(9, 10)]

    print(f"Bank Liquid Reserve Buffer ell = {ell} ({float(ell):.2f})")
    print("Run Threshold theta*(mu, kappa, ell) Grid:")
    header = "mu \\ kappa  | " + " | ".join(f"kappa={float(k):.2f}" for k in kappas)
    print(header)
    print("-" * len(header))

    for mu in mus:
        row = []
        for k in kappas:
            if mu <= ell:
                th = Fraction(0, 1)
            else:
                raw = (mu - ell) / (k * (Fraction(1, 1) - ell))
                th = min(Fraction(1, 1), raw)
            row.append(f"{float(th):10.4f}")
        mu_str = f"mu={float(mu):.2f}"
        print(f"{mu_str:11} | " + " | ".join(row))

    print("\nCase Analysis:")
    # Retail bank profile: mu = 0.20 <= ell = 0.20
    print("1. Retail Bank (mu = 0.20): Liquid buffer covers all uninsured deposits. Threshold = 0.0000 (Immune).")
    # Regional bank profile (SVB-like): mu = 0.90, kappa = 0.70
    raw_svb = (Fraction(9, 10) - ell) / (Fraction(7, 10) * (Fraction(1, 1) - ell))
    th_svb = min(Fraction(1, 1), raw_svb)
    print(
        f"2. Regional Bank (mu = 0.90, kappa = 0.70): Run threshold = {th_svb} ({float(th_svb):.4f}). "
        f"Bank suffers a run whenever asset recovery drops below {float(th_svb) * 100:.2f}%."
    )

3The 2023 Regional Banking Episode

The theoretical framework of uninsured depositor runs directly explains the March 2023 collapse of Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) and Signature Bank. Between 2020 and 2021, rapid inflow of venture capital deposits expanded SVB's balance sheet. Rather than holding short-term reserves, the bank invested deposits into long-duration fixed-rate U.S. Treasuries and agency mortgage-backed securities (MBS).

When the Federal Reserve raised policy interest rates by over 450 basis points across 2022–2023, the economic value of equity was severely impaired by duration mismatch. Mark-to-market losses on Hold-to-Maturity (HTM) and Available-for-Sale (AFS) portfolios exceeded the bank's total regulatory common equity tier 1 capital .

Crucially, SVB's liability structure exhibited extreme uninsured concentration: over 90% of total deposits exceeded the $250,000 FDIC insurance limit . On March 9, 2023, when SVB announced a $1.8 billion realized loss on securities sales to meet outflows, venture capital funds advised portfolio companies to withdraw funds. Accelerated by mobile banking and digital communication channels, depositors initiated $42 billion in withdrawal requests in a single business day, exhausting all available liquid reserves and triggering immediate regulatory seizure by the FDIC .

Where the Canon Breaks

The static run threshold model abstracts from key dynamic mechanisms observed in modern banking panics:

  • Digital withdrawal velocity: Classical models assume physical withdrawal queues. Modern electronic payment rails (Fedwire, ACH, mobile apps) allow depositors to transfer billions within seconds, compressing bank survival horizons from weeks to hours.
  • Social media communication cascades: Depositor beliefs are formed not through independent signal extraction, but via social media networks (such as X/Twitter and group messaging chats), generating rapid information cascades.
  • Systemic regulatory intervention: In March 2023, the U.S. Treasury, Federal Reserve, and FDIC invoked the Systemic Risk Exception, guaranteeing all uninsured deposits at SVB and Signature Bank, and created the Bank Term Funding Program (BTFP) to lend at par against collateral. Expectations of ex-post bailouts alter ex-ante monitoring incentives.
  • Accounting classification distortions: Accounting rules permit banks to record securities under Hold-to-Maturity (HTM) without reflecting mark-to-market losses in regulatory CET1 capital, concealing true economic insolvency from casual observers.

Misconceptions

  1. “The 2023 regional bank failures were caused entirely by credit defaults on risky tech startup loans.”

    Silicon Valley Bank held very low non-performing credit loan rates. The primary impairment was interest rate risk and duration mismatch on pristine, default-free U.S. Treasury and agency securities, whose market values collapsed when benchmark rates rose.

  2. “A bank with 10% regulatory capital is always protected against deposit runs.”

    Regulatory capital measures book equity. If mark-to-market unrealized losses on bond portfolios exceed book capital, economic equity is negative. Furthermore, if uninsured deposits exceed 80% of liabilities, even a solvent bank can be forced into insolvency by fire-sale liquidation discounts.

  3. “Uninsured depositors are patient institutional investors who rarely execute panic runs.”

    Uninsured corporate treasurers face fiduciary obligations to protect operational payroll cash. Because they bear first-loss exposure upon failure without earning equity upside, their optimal risk management rule is to withdraw immediately at the first sign of distress.

Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Analytical run threshold evaluation

A regional bank has uninsured deposit share \(\mu = 0.75\), liquid reserve buffer \(\ell = 0.25\), and liquidation recovery rate \(\kappa = 0.80\). Compute the exact run threshold \(\theta^*\) as a fraction and decimal.

Solution

Because \(\mu = 0.75 > \ell = 0.25\), we evaluate \(\theta^* = \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa (1 - \ell)} = \frac{0.75 - 0.25}{0.80 \times (1.0 - 0.25)} = \frac{0.50}{0.80 \times 0.75} = \frac{0.50}{0.60} = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.8333\). If asset fundamental recovery falls below 83.33%, uninsured depositors will execute an insolvency run.

M2 · Required buffer calibration for target safety

Under \(\mu = 0.75\) and \(\kappa = 0.80\), the bank regulator demands that the run threshold be lowered to \(\theta_{\text{target}} = 0.50\). Calculate the required liquid reserve buffer \(\ell^*\).

Solution

Using the calibration formula \(\ell^* = \frac{\mu - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}}}{1 - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}}} = \frac{0.75 - 0.80 \times 0.50}{1.0 - 0.80 \times 0.50} = \frac{0.75 - 0.40}{1.0 - 0.40} = \frac{0.35}{0.60} = \frac{7}{12} \approx 0.5833\). The bank must increase its liquid reserve buffer from 25.00% to 58.33% of total deposits.

M3 · Mark-to-market duration loss computation

A bank holds $100 billion of 10-year Treasury bonds with a modified duration of 8.5 years. If benchmark Treasury yields increase by 200 basis points (+0.02), compute the mark-to-market loss in billions of dollars.

Solution

Under the first-order duration approximation, \(\Delta P / P \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \cdot \Delta y = -8.5 \times (+0.02) = -0.170 = -17.0\%\). The total dollar mark-to-market loss on the bond portfolio is \(\$100\text{B} \times (-0.170) = -\$17.0\) billion. If initial common equity was $15 billion, the bank's economic capital is completely wiped out.

Proof

P1 · Monotonicity of run threshold with respect to uninsured share

Prove that for any bank operating in the interior solvency regime (\(0 < \theta^* < 1\)), the derivative \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \mu}\) is strictly positive, and prove that increasing \(\mu\) strictly expands the set of run-inducing fundamental states.

Solution

In the interior regime, \(\theta^*(\mu, \kappa, \ell) = \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa(1 - \ell)}\). Differentiating with respect to \(\mu\) yields \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \mu} = \frac{1}{\kappa (1 - \ell)}\). Since \(\kappa \in (0, 1]\) and \(\ell \in (0, 1)\), the denominator \(\kappa (1 - \ell)\) is strictly positive, so \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \mu} > 0\). The run region is the set of fundamental states \(\Theta_{\text{run}} = \{\theta \in [0, 1] : \theta < \theta^*\} = [0, \theta^*)\). Because \(\theta^*(\mu)\) is strictly increasing in \(\mu\), if \(\mu_1 < \mu_2\), then \(\theta^*(\mu_1) < \theta^*(\mu_2)\), which implies \([0, \theta^*(\mu_1)) \subset [0, \theta^*(\mu_2))\). The run set strictly expands.

P2 · Analytical derivation of the buffer calibration formula

Prove that for any achievable target threshold \(\theta_{\text{target}} \in (0, \min(1, \mu/\kappa))\), the unique liquid buffer satisfying \(\theta^*(\ell^*) = \theta_{\text{target}}\) is given by \(\ell^* = \frac{\mu - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}}}{1 - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}}}\).

Solution

The threshold equation is \(\theta_{\text{target}} = \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa (1 - \ell)}\). Multiplying both sides by the denominator: \(\kappa \theta_{\text{target}} (1 - \ell) = \mu - \ell \implies \kappa \theta_{\text{target}} - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}} \ell = \mu - \ell\). Collecting terms in \(\ell\): \(\ell - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}} \ell = \mu - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}} \implies \ell (1 - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}}) = \mu - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}}\). Dividing by \(1 - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}}\) (which is strictly positive since \(\kappa \le 1\) and \(\theta_{\text{target}} < 1\)) yields \(\ell^* = \frac{\mu - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}}}{1 - \kappa \theta_{\text{target}}}\). Since \(\theta^*(\ell)\) is strictly decreasing in \(\ell\), the solution is unique.

P3 · Attainable range boundary conditions

Prove that as the liquid buffer \(\ell \to 0^+\), the run threshold converges to \(\min(1, \mu / \kappa)\), and as \(\ell \to \mu^-\), the run threshold converges to 0.

Solution

Taking the limit as \(\ell \to 0^+\): \(\lim_{\ell \to 0^+} \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa (1 - \ell)} = \frac{\mu - 0}{\kappa(1 - 0)} = \frac{\mu}{\kappa}\). Taking into account the upper bound capping at 1.0, \(\lim_{\ell \to 0^+} \theta^* = \min(1, \mu/\kappa)\). Taking the limit as \(\ell \to \mu^-\): \(\lim_{\ell \to \mu^-} \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa(1 - \ell)} = \frac{0}{\kappa(1 - \mu)} = 0\). Because \(\theta^*(\ell)\) is continuous and strictly decreasing on \((0, \mu)\), by the intermediate value theorem, the image of \((0, \mu)\) under \(\theta^*\) is the open interval \((0, \min(1, \mu/\kappa))\). Any target outside this interval is unattainable.

Empirical

E1 · Grid threshold bounds in Lab 1

From Lab 1, state the run threshold for a regional bank with \(\mu = 0.90\) when liquidation recovery is \(\kappa = 0.90\). What percentage of fundamental asset value must be preserved to avoid a run?

Solution

Lab 1 prints: for \(\mu = 0.90\) and \(\kappa = 0.90\) with \(\ell = 0.20\), \(\theta^* = 0.9722\). This means that to avoid a run, the bank's fundamental asset value must remain above 97.22% of par. A mark-to-market loss of merely \(1.0000 - 0.9722 = 0.0278 = 2.78\%\) is sufficient to breach solvency and trigger a full run.

E2 · SVB deposit outflow velocity

According to the Federal Reserve review (2023), Silicon Valley Bank experienced $42 billion in deposit withdrawal requests on March 9, 2023, and had an additional $100 billion queued for withdrawal on March 10. Express these requests as a percentage of SVB's total $175 billion deposit base.

Solution

The single-day withdrawal on March 9 was \(\$42\text{B} / \$175\text{B} \approx 24.00\%\) of total deposits. The combined two-day withdrawal demand was \((\$42\text{B} + \$100\text{B}) / \$175\text{B} = \$142\text{B} / \$175\text{B} \approx 81.14\%\) of total deposits. This represents the fastest deposit run in U.S. commercial banking history.

E3 · Aggregate mark-to-market losses in the U.S. banking system

Jiang et al. (2024) estimate aggregate unrealized mark-to-market losses on U.S. commercial bank asset portfolios in 2022–2023. If the U.S. banking system held $2.2 trillion in total tangible common equity, explain why $1.7 trillion in mark-to-market securities losses created widespread fragility.

Solution

Unrealized mark-to-market losses of $1.7 trillion represented approximately 77% of total tangible common equity ($2.2 trillion) across the U.S. banking system. If marked to market, hundreds of banks had negative economic equity. Jiang et al. demonstrate that if only half of uninsured depositors had withdrawn, 186 U.S. commercial banks would have become completely insolvent.

Research

R1 · Deposit franchise value as an interest rate hedge

How does the deposit franchise (Drechsler, Savov, and Schnabl 2021) mitigate mark-to-market losses on assets during monetary tightening, and why did this hedge fail at Silicon Valley Bank?

Solution

Retail deposits pay low, sticky interest rates that adjust sluggishly to rising market rates (low deposit beta). This rate wedge creates a valuable intangible asset—the deposit franchise—whose present value increases as interest rates rise, offsetting asset duration losses. However, this hedge relies on deposit stickiness. At SVB, deposits were predominantly large, rate-sensitive venture capital balances with near-instantaneous flight velocity. When uninsured deposits ran, the franchise value vanished, leaving only asset losses.

R2 · Systemic Risk Exception and the too-big-to-fail boundary

Evaluate the trade-offs involved in the FDIC invoking the Systemic Risk Exception to fully guarantee all uninsured deposits at SVB and Signature Bank in March 2023.

Solution

The short-term benefit was halting immediate contagion across other regional banks (such as First Republic and Western Alliance), preventing widespread deposit flight. The long-term cost is severe moral hazard: guaranteeing uninsured depositors at regional banks effectively eliminates market discipline by corporate creditors, expands the unpriced government safety net, and blurs the statutory boundary established by the $250,000 FDIC cap.

R3 · Central bank lender of last resort at par

How did the Federal Reserve's Bank Term Funding Program (BTFP) modify classical Bagehot lender-of-last-resort principles, and what are its market implications?

Solution

Bagehot's classical dictum dictates lending freely to solvent banks against good collateral at a penalty rate and at market value with a haircut. The BTFP modified this by lending against eligible collateral valued at par (100% of face value) rather than market value, with zero haircut. This effectively subsidized underwater banks by absorbing the duration-induced market discount, preventing forced fire sales but muting market price signals.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What distinguishes an uninsured-depositor run from a classical Diamond–Dybvig retail bank run?

    Options

    Uninsured depositors hold balances exceeding statutory insurance limits, making them sensitive to solvency signals and asset mark-to-market losses (Definition 3.1).

  2. If \(\mu = 0.75, \ell = 0.25\), and \(\kappa = 0.80\), what is the exact run threshold \(\theta^* = \frac{\mu - \ell}{\kappa(1 - \ell)}\)?

    \(\theta^* = (0.75 - 0.25) / (0.80 \times 0.75) = 0.50 / 0.60 = 5/6 \approx 0.8333\) (Problem M1).

  3. Under \(\mu = 0.75\) and \(\kappa = 0.80\), what calibrated liquid buffer \(\ell^* = \frac{\mu - \kappa \theta}{1 - \kappa \theta}\) achieves a target threshold \(\theta_{\text{target}} = 0.50\)?

    \(\ell^* = (0.75 - 0.80 \times 0.50) / (1 - 0.80 \times 0.50) = 0.35 / 0.60 = 7/12 \approx 0.5833\) (Problem M2).

  4. Why does a higher uninsured deposit share \(\mu\) strictly increase the bank's run threshold \(\theta^*\)?

    Options

    \(\frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial \mu} = \frac{1}{\kappa(1-\ell)} > 0\): larger uninsured withdrawal demands require higher liquidation proceeds, raising the solvency threshold (Proposition 3.3).

  5. If a bank holds liquid buffer \(\ell = 0.30\) and uninsured deposit share \(\mu = 0.20\), what is the theoretical run threshold \(\theta^*\)?

    Because \(\mu \le \ell\) (0.20 <= 0.30), liquid cash covers all uninsured claims without selling long assets; \(\theta^* = 0.0000\) (Theorem 3.2).

  6. What primary balance sheet factor caused Silicon Valley Bank's capital impairment in 2022–2023?

    Options

    SVB held long-duration fixed-rate securities whose market value dropped by billions when benchmark interest rates rose rapidly (Jiang et al. 2024).

  7. In Lab 1, what is the exact run threshold for \(\mu = 0.90, \kappa = 0.90\), and \(\ell = 0.20\)?

    \(\theta^* = (0.90 - 0.20) / (0.90 \times 0.80) = 0.70 / 0.72 = 35/36 \approx 0.9722\) (Lab 1).

  8. Why does Hold-to-Maturity (HTM) accounting obscure bank run vulnerability?

    Options

    HTM accounting does not reflect mark-to-market declines in book equity, concealing underlying solvency erosion until assets are liquidated.
