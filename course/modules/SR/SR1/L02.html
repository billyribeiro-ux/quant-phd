Demand deposit contracts provide liquidity insurance when depositors coordinate on waiting. However, funding illiquid assets with demandable debt creates an inherent strategic fragility. Because sequential service pays depositors first-come, first-served until assets are exhausted, a patient depositor's optimal decision depends on their belief about peer withdrawals. If a depositor anticipates that other patient depositors will withdraw, running becomes a self-fulfilling best response. This lesson formalizes the withdrawal game, proves the multiplicity of pure-strategy Nash equilibria, and establishes how government deposit insurance eliminates bank runs.

1The Withdrawal Game at Date 1

Consider the Diamond–Dybvig model established in L01. At date 0, all agents deposit 1 unit of endowment with the bank. The bank offers a demand deposit contract promising \(r_1 = c_1^* > 1\) at date 1 or a pro-rata share of remaining mature assets at date 2. The bank invests \(I^* = \lambda c_1^*\) in liquid storage and \(1 - I^*\) in the illiquid long asset. The long asset yields \(R_2 > 1\) at date 2, but early liquidation at date 1 recovers only salvage value \(L < 1\) per unit.

At date 1, each consumer privately observes their preference shock. Impatient depositors derive utility only from date 1 consumption and strictly dominate by withdrawing immediately. Patient depositors face an interactive game: they must choose between two actions:

  • Wait (\(W\)): Leave the deposit with the bank until date 2.
  • Run (\(R\)): Demand immediate withdrawal of \(r_1\) at date 1.
Definition 2.1 (bank run)

A bank run is an equilibrium coordination failure in which patient depositors demand immediate early redemption of demand deposit claims at date 1, driven by the belief that other depositors will withdraw, forcing the premature liquidation of illiquid assets and resulting in bank insolvency .

Assumption 2.2 (sequential service and liquidation technology)

The date 1 withdrawal game satisfies the following rules:

  1. Sequential service: Depositors arrive at the bank in random order. The bank must service withdrawal demands at the promised contractual value \(r_1 = c_1^*\) until total cash resources are exhausted.
  2. Liquidation order: The bank first expends its liquid storage reserves \(I^* = \lambda c_1^*\). If withdrawal demands exceed \(I^*\), the bank liquidates its long asset at salvage recovery value \(L \in (0, 1)\) until either all withdrawal requests are satisfied or all long assets are liquidated.
  3. Insolvency payout: If total liquidation proceeds are exhausted before all withdrawing depositors are paid, depositors arriving after funds are exhausted receive 0. Depositors who waited receive 0 at date 2.

2Equilibrium Multiplicity in the Withdrawal Game

Let \(f \in [\lambda, 1]\) denote the total fraction of depositors who choose to withdraw at date 1. Since all \(\lambda\) impatient depositors withdraw, \(f = \lambda + (1 - \lambda) x\), where \(x \in [0, 1]\) is the fraction of patient depositors choosing to run.

If \(f = \lambda\) (\(x = 0\), all patient depositors wait), the bank expends its liquid buffer \(I^* = \lambda c_1^*\) exactly. Zero long assets are salvaged. At date 2, the long investment matures to \((1 - I^*) R_2\), which is divided equally among the \(1 - \lambda\) patient depositors, delivering \(c_2^* = \frac{(1 - I^*) R_2}{1 - \lambda}\). By Theorem 1.4, \(c_2^* > c_1^*\).

Now suppose all patient depositors run: \(f = 1\) (\(x = 1\)). Total withdrawal demand is \(1 \times c_1^* = c_1^* > 1\). The bank exhausts its liquid reserves \(I^* = \lambda c_1^*\) and liquidates all \(1 - I^*\) long assets, raising salvage cash \((1 - I^*) L\). Total maximum cash generated at date 1 is:

\[ C_{\max} = I^* + (1 - I^*) L = \lambda c_1^* + (1 - \lambda c_1^*) L \]

Because \(L < 1\) and \(c_1^* > 1\), total cash \(C_{\max} < c_1^*\). The bank cannot satisfy all depositors. Payouts cease when the fraction \(\bar{f} = C_{\max} / c_1^* < 1\) of depositors has been serviced.

Theorem 2.3 (equilibrium multiplicity in Diamond–Dybvig)

Under Assumption 2.2, the simultaneous withdrawal game between patient depositors admits at least two pure-strategy Nash equilibria:

  1. The efficient equilibrium (no run): Every patient depositor chooses to Wait (\(x = 0\)). Payouts are \((c_1^*, c_2^*)\), and the allocation achieves the social planner's first-best welfare.
  2. The bank run equilibrium (panic): Every patient depositor chooses to Run (\(x = 1\)). The bank is liquidated prematurely, and all patient depositors receive an expected payout strictly less than 1.
Proof

Consider an arbitrary patient depositor \(i\) forming beliefs about the withdrawal fraction of other patient depositors.

Case 1: Depositor \(i\) believes all other patient depositors will Wait (\(x = 0\)). Total withdrawal demand is \(\lambda c_1^* = I^*\). If depositor \(i\) Waits, they receive \(c_2^*\) at date 2. If depositor \(i\) deviates to Run, they receive \(c_1^*\) at date 1. Because \(u'(c_1^*) = R_2 u'(c_2^*)\) and \(R_2 > 1\) under concave utility implies \(c_2^* > c_1^*\), waiting yields strictly higher utility: \(u(c_2^*) > u(c_1^*)\). Thus, Waiting is a strict best response. The strategy profile where all patient depositors Wait is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium.

Case 2: Depositor \(i\) believes all other patient depositors will Run (\(x = 1\)). Total withdrawal demand is \(c_1^* > C_{\max}\). The bank liquidates all assets at date 1 and is dissolved. If depositor \(i\) Waits, the bank has zero assets remaining at date 2, yielding a payout of 0 and utility \(u(0)\). If depositor \(i\) Runs, they enter the random queue. With probability \(\bar{f} = C_{\max} / c_1^*\), they arrive before reserves are exhausted and receive \(c_1^*\); with probability \(1 - \bar{f}\), they arrive after exhaustion and receive 0. Their expected utility from running is \(\bar{f} u(c_1^*) + (1 - \bar{f}) u(0)\). Because \(\bar{f} > 0\) and \(c_1^* > 0\), this expected utility strictly exceeds \(u(0)\). Therefore, Running is a strict best response. The strategy profile where all patient depositors Run is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.4 (payoff matrix for a 3-depositor bank)

Consider \(N = 3\) depositors with 1 unit endowments, liquid buffer \(= 2\), long asset \(= 1\), salvage value \(L = 1/2\), mature return \(R_2 = 4\), and contractual payout \(r_1 = 4/3\). Depositor 1 is impatient and always withdraws. Depositors 2 and 3 are patient, choosing between Wait (\(W\)) and Run (\(R\)).

  • Profile \((W, W)\): Only Depositor 1 withdraws, requiring cash \(4/3 \le 2\). Leftover cash is \(2/3\). Long asset matures to \(1 \times 4 = 4\). Date 2 resources are \(2/3 + 4 = 14/3\). Split equally, Depositors 2 and 3 each receive \(\frac{14/3}{2} = \frac{7}{3} \approx 2.333 > 4/3\). Payoffs: \((7/3, 7/3)\).
  • Profile \((R, W)\): Depositors 1 and 2 withdraw. Demand is \(8/3 > 2\). The bank liquidates its entire long asset, obtaining \(1 \times (1/2) = 1/2\) in cash. Total cash is \(2 + 1/2 = 5/2\). The first arrival in the random queue receives \(4/3 = 8/6\); the second receives the residual \(5/2 - 4/3 = 7/6\). Expected payoff for running Depositor 2 is \(\frac{1}{2}(4/3) + \frac{1}{2}(7/6) = \frac{15}{12} = \frac{5}{4} = 1.25\). Waiting Depositor 3 receives 0. Payoffs: \((5/4, 0)\).
  • Profile \((R, R)\): All 3 depositors run. Total cash is \(5/2\). Arrivals receive \(4/3\), \(7/6\), and 0. Symmetric expected payoff is \(\frac{4/3 + 7/6 + 0}{3} = \frac{5/2}{3} = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833\). Payoffs: \((5/6, 5/6)\).

If Depositor 3 Waits, Depositor 2 prefers \(W\) (\(7/3 > 5/4\)). If Depositor 3 Runs, Depositor 2 prefers \(R\) (\(5/6 > 0\)). Both \((W, W)\) and \((R, R)\) are pure Nash equilibria.

Lab 1 · Sequential Service, Equilibrium Multiplicity, and Bank Runs

Verifying the 3-depositor sequential service payoff matrix in exact fractions, confirming that (Wait, Wait) and (Run, Run) are both Nash equilibria, and proving that deposit insurance renders waiting strictly dominant.

"""Lab SR1-L02-1: Sequential Service, Equilibrium Multiplicity, and Bank Runs.

Calculates exact expected payoffs for patient depositors under sequential service.
Demonstrates that both the good equilibrium (Wait, Wait) and the bank run equilibrium
(Run, Run) are pure-strategy Nash equilibria. Verifies that government deposit
insurance renders waiting strictly dominant, eliminating the bank run equilibrium.
"""

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    # N = 3 depositors, each depositing 1 unit. Total deposits = 3.
    # Bank assets: Liquid buffer = 2, Long asset = 1.
    # Long asset mature return = 4, salvage value upon early liquidation = 1/2.
    # Deposit contract promises r1 = 4/3 at t=1.
    # Depositor 1 is impatient (always withdraws at t=1).
    # Depositors 2 and 3 are patient, choosing Action in {Wait, Run}.

    # Profile payoffs for (Depositor 2, Depositor 3):
    # 1. (Wait, Wait): Only Depositor 1 withdraws.
    # Cash needed = 4/3 <= 2. Remaining cash = 2/3. Long asset matures to 4.
    # Total date 2 resources = 2/3 + 4 = 14/3. Split equally: 7/3 each.
    payoff_ww = (Fraction(7, 3), Fraction(7, 3))

    # 2. (Run, Wait): Depositors 1 and 2 withdraw at t=1.
    # Cash demand = 8/3 > 2. Bank liquidates entire long asset yielding 1/2 cash.
    # Total cash = 2 + 1/2 = 5/2.
    # Arrivals uniformly random: first gets 4/3, second gets 5/2 - 4/3 = 7/6.
    # Expected payoff for running Depositor 2: (1/2)*(4/3) + (1/2)*(7/6) = 15/12 = 5/4.
    # Depositor 3 (waiting): 0 (bank has zero assets remaining).
    payoff_rw = (Fraction(5, 4), Fraction(0, 1))
    payoff_wr = (Fraction(0, 1), Fraction(5, 4))

    # 3. (Run, Run): All 3 depositors withdraw at t=1.
    # Total available cash = 5/2.
    # Random arrival queue: 1st gets 4/3, 2nd gets 7/6, 3rd gets 0.
    # Symmetric expected payoff: (4/3 + 7/6 + 0) / 3 = (15/6) / 3 = 5/6.
    payoff_rr = (Fraction(5, 6), Fraction(5, 6))

    print(
        f"Profile (Wait, Wait): P2 = {payoff_ww[0]} ({float(payoff_ww[0]):.4f}), "
        f"P3 = {payoff_ww[1]} ({float(payoff_ww[1]):.4f})"
    )
    print(
        f"Profile (Run, Wait):  P2 = {payoff_rw[0]} ({float(payoff_rw[0]):.4f}), "
        f"P3 = {payoff_rw[1]} ({float(payoff_rw[1]):.4f})"
    )
    print(
        f"Profile (Wait, Run):  P2 = {payoff_wr[0]} ({float(payoff_wr[0]):.4f}), "
        f"P3 = {payoff_wr[1]} ({float(payoff_wr[1]):.4f})"
    )
    print(
        f"Profile (Run, Run):   P2 = {payoff_rr[0]} ({float(payoff_rr[0]):.4f}), "
        f"P3 = {payoff_rr[1]} ({float(payoff_rr[1]):.4f})"
    )

    # Best response analysis:
    # If P3 plays Wait: P2 payoff for Wait is 7/3, for Run is 5/4. 7/3 > 5/4 ==> Wait is BR.
    br_p3_wait = "Wait" if payoff_ww[0] > payoff_rw[0] else "Run"
    # If P3 plays Run: P2 payoff for Wait is 0, for Run is 5/6. 5/6 > 0 ==> Run is BR.
    br_p3_run = "Run" if payoff_rr[0] > payoff_wr[0] else "Wait"

    print(f"P2 Best Response to P3=Wait: {br_p3_wait} (Payoff {payoff_ww[0]} > {payoff_rw[0]})")
    print(f"P2 Best Response to P3=Run:  {br_p3_run} (Payoff {payoff_rr[0]} > {payoff_wr[0]})")
    print("Multiplicity Confirmed: (Wait, Wait) and (Run, Run) are both Nash equilibria.")

    # Government Deposit Insurance Guarantee:
    # Guarantee guarantees full promised payoff r1 = 4/3 at date 2 even if bank liquidates.
    # Waiting payoff under run becomes 4/3 instead of 0.
    di_guarantee = Fraction(4, 3)
    p2_wait_with_di = di_guarantee
    print(
        f"With Deposit Insurance: P2 payoff for Wait when P3=Run becomes "
        f"{p2_wait_with_di} ({float(p2_wait_with_di):.4f})"
    )
    print(f"Comparison: Guaranteed Wait payoff {p2_wait_with_di} > Run payoff {payoff_rr[0]}")
    print("Run equilibrium eliminated: Waiting is strictly dominant for patient depositors.")

3Policy Mechanisms: Suspension of Convertibility and Deposit Insurance

Because bank runs destroy productive investments through premature liquidation, economists and regulators have analyzed institutional mechanisms to eliminate the run equilibrium.

Proposition 2.5 (suspension of convertibility)

Suppose the bank announces a contract clause that suspends all cash withdrawals once the cumulative withdrawal volume reaches exactly \(\hat{f} = \lambda\). Under Assumption 2.2, if \(\lambda\) is known with certainty, suspension of convertibility eliminates the bank run equilibrium while preserving the first-best allocation.

Proof

Under suspension at \(\hat{f} = \lambda\), the bank pays out at most \(\lambda c_1^* = I^*\) at date 1. Because exactly \(I^*\) is paid out, zero long assets are liquidated prematurely. At date 2, all long assets mature to deliver \((1 - I^*) R_2\). Any patient depositor who attempts to run at date 1 can only be serviced if they arrive ahead of impatient depositors. If they arrive after \(\lambda\) depositors have withdrawn, their redemption request is deferred to date 2, where they receive \(c_2^*\). Since deferred depositors receive \(c_2^* > c_1^*\), patient depositors have no incentive to incur the risk of early arrival, and running is weakly dominated by waiting. \(\blacksquare\)

While suspension of convertibility succeeds when \(\lambda\) is deterministic, it fails if aggregate liquidity demand is stochastic. If the realized fraction of truly impatient households is \(\lambda' > \lambda\), suspension traps truly needy depositors, generating severe deadweight welfare losses.

Theorem 2.6 (government deposit insurance)

Suppose the government establishes a deposit insurance guarantee financed by taxation of mature wealth. The insurer guarantees that any depositor who waits until date 2 will receive a payout at least equal to \(c_2^*\). Under this guarantee, waiting is a strictly dominant strategy for all patient depositors, uniquely selecting the efficient equilibrium at zero fiscal cost in equilibrium.

Proof

Let the government commit to levy taxes on date 2 returns to fund any shortfall if early liquidations impair the bank. For any patient depositor \(i\), regardless of whether other depositors wait or run, the guaranteed payoff from waiting until date 2 is \(c_2^*\) (or at least \(c_1^*\)). Since \(c_2^* > c_1^*\), waiting yields strictly higher utility than running (\(u(c_2^*) > u(c_1^*)\)) for every possible withdrawal fraction \(f \in [\lambda, 1]\). Waiting becomes a strictly dominant strategy. Because every patient depositor waits, the bank run never occurs, liquid reserves \(\lambda c_1^*\) perfectly satisfy impatient demand, the bank never defaults, and zero government taxes are levied in equilibrium. \(\blacksquare\)

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical theory of self-fulfilling runs and deposit insurance abstracts from critical empirical frictions:

  • Sunspot coordination versus fundamental insolvency: Diamond and Dybvig treat bank runs as sunspots—pure coordination failures uncorrelated with economic fundamentals. Gorton (1988) and Calomiris and Mason (2003) show empirically that historical panics typically occurred during business cycle peaks when depositor expectations of asset losses rose.
  • Moral hazard of underpriced insurance: In Theorem 2.6, deposit insurance operates at zero fiscal cost. In real banking systems, full insurance subsidizes bank risk-taking, encouraging excessive leverage and speculative loan origination.
  • Sovereign fiscal capacity constraints: The proof assumes the government possesses unlimited, credible taxation capacity. In emerging markets or during sovereign debt crises, depositors recognize that sovereign guarantees are non-credible, triggering twin banking-sovereign crises.
  • Uninsured liability concentration: Modern commercial banks fund balance sheets with large uninsured corporate deposits that exceed statutory guarantee ceilings, rendering classical full-coverage insurance proofs inapplicable.

Misconceptions

  1. “A bank run is an irrational psychological mania driven by herd hysteria.”

    Game theory establishes that running on an illiquid bank is fully rational. If a patient depositor correctly anticipates that other depositors are withdrawing, waiting guarantees a payout of zero under sequential service. Running maximizes individual expected utility given the actions of others.

  2. “Suspension of convertibility is universally superior to deposit insurance because it requires no taxpayer funds.”

    Suspension of convertibility requires exact knowledge of the aggregate liquidity shock \(\lambda\). If aggregate liquidity shocks are stochastic, setting a rigid suspension ceiling penalizes genuinely impatient depositors, denying them emergency liquidity when they need it most.

  3. “Deposit insurance eliminates bank run vulnerability for all commercial banks.”

    Deposit insurance eliminates runs only for accounts that fall below statutory insurance caps ($250,000 in the U.S.). Institutions funded predominantly by uninsured corporate deposits, certificates of deposit, or wholesale repo remain exposed to strategic run panics.

Mechanics

M1 · Maximum cash realization under asset liquidation

A bank has 1,000 depositors with $1,000 deposits each ($1,000,000 total). Liquid reserves are $200,000, and long assets are $800,000. If the salvage recovery rate is \(L = 0.60\) and promised early return is \(r_1 = \$1,200\), calculate the maximum number of depositors the bank can satisfy at date 1.

Solution

Total cash the bank can generate is liquid reserves plus liquidated long assets: \(C_{\max} = \$200,000 + 0.60 \times \$800,000 = \$200,000 + \$480,000 = \$680,000\). The maximum number of withdrawing depositors serviced at $1,200 each is \(\lfloor \$680,000 / \$1,200 \rfloor = \lfloor 566.67 \rfloor = 566\) depositors. The remaining 434 depositors receive $0.

M2 · Expected payout in a 3-depositor panic run

In Example 2.4, verify the symmetric expected payoff of \(5/6\) when all three depositors run. Show the calculation across all six arrival orderings.

Solution

Total cash is \(5/2\). Promised payout is \(4/3 = 8/6\). The first arrival receives \(8/6\). Remaining cash is \(5/2 - 4/3 = 15/6 - 8/6 = 7/6\). The second arrival receives \(7/6\). The third arrival receives 0. Each depositor is equally likely to arrive in position 1, 2, or 3 (probability 1/3 each). Expected payout is \(\frac{1}{3}(8/6) + \frac{1}{3}(7/6) + \frac{1}{3}(0) = \frac{15/6}{3} = \frac{5/2}{3} = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.8333\).

M3 · Early liquidation threshold for bank solvency

Let liquid buffer be \(I^* = 0.30\), long asset be 0.70 with salvage value \(L = 0.50\), and contractual payout be \(r_1 = 1.20\). What fraction of early withdrawals \(f\) exhausts liquid reserves, and at what fraction is the bank entirely insolvent?

Solution

Liquid reserves are exhausted when \(f \cdot r_1 = I^* \implies f \times 1.20 = 0.30 \implies f = 0.30 / 1.20 = 0.25\) (25% of depositors). Total possible cash is \(C_{\max} = 0.30 + 0.50 \times 0.70 = 0.30 + 0.35 = 0.65\). The bank is entirely insolvent when withdrawal demands exceed 0.65: \(f_{\text{insolvent}} = 0.65 / 1.20 \approx 0.5417\) (54.17% of depositors).

P1 · Strict dominance under government deposit insurance

Prove that under a credible government guarantee ensuring late payout \(c_2^*\) at date 2, waiting is a strictly dominant strategy for patient depositors for any arbitrary withdrawal fraction \(f \in [\lambda, 1]\).

Solution

Let \(f\) be any arbitrary fraction of depositors withdrawing at date 1. If a patient depositor runs, they receive \(c_1^*\) if serviced or 0 if unserviced. Thus, the maximum possible payoff from running is \(c_1^*\). Under the government guarantee, if the depositor waits until date 2, the sovereign guarantees payment of \(c_2^*\). By Theorem 1.4, \(c_2^* > c_1^*\). Because \(u(c)\) is strictly increasing, \(u(c_2^*) > u(c_1^*)\) holds in all states. The payoff from waiting strictly exceeds the payoff from running for every realization of \(f\). Waiting is strictly dominant.

P2 · Failure of suspension under aggregate uncertainty

Suppose aggregate liquidity demand \(\tilde{\lambda}\) is uniformly distributed on \([0.20, 0.60]\). Prove that a fixed suspension threshold \(\hat{f} = 0.40\) results in a positive probability of rationing genuinely impatient depositors.

Solution

Impatient depositors have no utility for date 2 consumption (\(U = u(c_1)\)). If realized liquidity demand \(\lambda > 0.40\), a fraction \(\lambda - 0.40\) of impatient depositors are barred from withdrawing at date 1. Their redemptions are suspended until date 2, where their consumption utility is identically zero. The probability of this rationing occurring is \(\Prob(\tilde{\lambda} > 0.40) = \frac{0.60 - 0.40}{0.60 - 0.20} = \frac{0.20}{0.40} = 0.50\). Suspension introduces severe welfare losses under aggregate uncertainty.

P3 · Pareto ranking of the two equilibria

Prove that the efficient equilibrium strictly Pareto dominates the bank run equilibrium in Theorem 2.3.

Solution

In the efficient equilibrium, impatient consumers receive \(c_1^*\) and patient consumers receive \(c_2^*\). In the bank run equilibrium, all long assets are liquidated prematurely at salvage value \(L < 1\). Total aggregate consumption available is \(C_{\max} = \lambda c_1^* + (1 - \lambda c_1^*) L < 1\). Because \(C_{\max} < 1\), average per-capita consumption is strictly less than the initial 1 unit endowment. Furthermore, impatient and patient consumers receive an expected payout of \(C_{\max} / c_1^* \cdot c_1^* = C_{\max} < 1 < c_1^* < c_2^*\). Both types strictly prefer the efficient equilibrium. The efficient equilibrium strictly Pareto dominates the run equilibrium.

E2 · Historical bank run incidence prior to FDIC

Between 1930 and 1933 in the United States, over 9,000 commercial banks suspended operations during the Great Depression. Explain how the establishment of the FDIC in 1934 affected bank failure rates.

Solution

Following the enactment of the Banking Act of 1933 and the operational debut of the FDIC on January 1, 1934, bank suspensions fell from over 4,000 in 1933 to fewer than 50 in 1934. By insuring retail deposits up to the statutory limit, the FDIC made waiting strictly dominant for insured depositors, eliminating panic runs across covered commercial banks.

Research

R1 · Sunspots versus fundamentals in banking panics

How does Gary Gorton's (1988) empirical analysis of U.S. National Banking Era panics contrast with the pure sunspot interpretation of Diamond and Dybvig (1983)?

Solution

Gorton examines bank panics from 1863 to 1914 and finds that panics were not random sunspot coordination events. Instead, panics occurred systematically when leading economic indicators—specifically the liabilities of failed commercial businesses—crossed a threshold. Depositors ran because they correctly anticipated that asset defaults would impair bank solvency, supporting a fundamental information-based theory rather than pure sunspots.

R2 · Global games and unique threshold equilibrium

How do Goldstein and Pauzner (2005) apply global games techniques to Diamond–Dybvig, and how does this resolve the problem of equilibrium multiplicity?

Solution

Goldstein and Pauzner replace common knowledge of bank fundamentals with dispersed private information: each depositor receives a private noisy signal of asset quality. Under strategic complementarities and slight private information noise, global games techniques eliminate equilibrium multiplicity, selecting a unique threshold \(\theta^*\) such that all depositors run if the fundamental falls below \(\theta^*\) and wait if above.

R3 · Deposit insurance pricing and risk-taking incentives

Why do flat-rate deposit insurance premiums create moral hazard in commercial banking books, and how does risk-based capital regulation address this friction?

Solution

When insurance premiums are invariant to asset risk, bank equity holders face asymmetric payoff structures: they retain upside equity gains while the government insurer absorbs downside insolvency losses. This call-option structure incentivizes banks to originate high-volatility, risky credit assets. Basel III risk-weighted capital requirements constrain this moral hazard by requiring higher equity buffers for riskier assets.

    If aggregate liquidity demand fluctuates, a rigid suspension threshold freezes genuinely impatient depositors, causing deadweight welfare losses.

  7. In Lab 1, what is the payoff to waiting Depositor 3 when Depositor 2 chooses to Run?

    0.0, because the bank's assets are completely liquidated at date 1 to service early withdrawals.

  8. Why does the efficient equilibrium Pareto dominate the bank run equilibrium?

    Options

    A bank run liquidates long assets prematurely at salvage value \(L < 1\), destroying wealth and leaving all agents worse off (Problem P3).
