Prior to Basel III, international capital regulation relied exclusively on risk-weighted assets (RWA). Banks exploited internal models to assign near-zero risk weights to highly leveraged trading books, precipitating the 2008 banking collapse. In response, the Basel Committee introduced the unweighted Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) as a non-model-dependent backstop. However, because the SLR treats safe sovereign debt identically to risky private credit, it penalizes low-margin, high-volume market making. When the leverage ratio binds, holding zero-risk Treasuries incurs a positive equity hurdle cost. This lesson models primary dealer balance-sheet optimization, derives the leverage shadow price, and formalizes dealer intermediation capacity.

1The Dual-Constraint Framework of Basel III

The post-crisis banking regulatory architecture established by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) operates as a dual-constraint framework . A banking institution must simultaneously satisfy:

  1. A risk-based capital requirement: Tier 1 Common Equity (CET1) divided by Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA) must exceed a statutory regulatory floor (typically \(8.0\% - 10.5\%\) including capital conservation and G-SIB surcharges).
  2. An unweighted leverage ratio: Tier 1 capital divided by total unweighted balance-sheet and off-balance-sheet exposure must exceed a non-risk-sensitive floor.
Definition 3.1 (Supplementary Leverage Ratio)

The Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) is the Basel III unweighted capital standard implemented for large banking organizations, defined as the ratio of Tier 1 capital to Total Leverage Exposure (TLE) :

\[ \mathrm{SLR} = \frac{\text{Tier 1 Capital}}{\text{Total Leverage Exposure (TLE)}} \ge 3.0\% \]
Definition 3.2 (enhanced Supplementary Leverage Ratio)

In the United States, the Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC established the enhanced Supplementary Leverage Ratio (eSLR) in 2014 for U.S. Global Systemically Important Banks (G-SIBs). The eSLR mandates:

  • A minimum SLR of \(5.0\%\) at the consolidated bank holding company (BHC) level (comprising a \(3.0\%\) minimum plus a \(2.0\%\) leverage buffer to avoid discretionary distribution constraints).
  • A minimum SLR of \(6.0\%\) for insured depository institution (IDI) subsidiaries to be classified as "well-capitalized" under Prompt Corrective Action regulations.

The denominator, Total Leverage Exposure (TLE), is comprehensively defined to eliminate regulatory arbitrage through off-balance-sheet accounting:

\[ \mathrm{TLE} = A_{\mathrm{on}} + \mathrm{CCR}_{\mathrm{repo}} + \mathrm{PFE}_{\mathrm{derivatives}} + \mathrm{OBS}_{\mathrm{commitments}} \]

where \(A_{\mathrm{on}}\) represents total on-balance-sheet assets without risk-weight deductions, \(\mathrm{CCR}_{\mathrm{repo}}\) is counterparty credit risk on repo-style transactions, \(\mathrm{PFE}_{\mathrm{derivatives}}\) is potential future exposure under the Standardized Approach for Counterparty Credit Risk (SA-CCR), and \(\mathrm{OBS}_{\mathrm{commitments}}\) represents off-balance-sheet loan commitments converted at credit conversion factors.

2Dealer Intermediation Capacity Formalization

Primary dealers intermediate wholesale financial markets by borrowing cash in repo markets and holding inventories of sovereign debt, corporate credit, and mortgage-backed securities. Regulatory capital constraints place a strict ceiling on this activity.

Definition 3.3 (dealer intermediation capacity)

The dealer intermediation capacity is the maximum quantitative volume of market-making, warehousing, and financing services a primary dealer or bank-affiliated broker-dealer can supply given its available Tier 1 equity capital and binding regulatory constraints (specifically the Supplementary Leverage Ratio, risk-weighted capital ratios, and liquidity coverage requirements) .

Let us formalize the portfolio allocation problem of a dealer holding Tier 1 equity capital \(E\). The dealer allocates balance-sheet assets across \(K\) asset classes, with holding amounts \(A_1, A_2, \dots, A_K\). Each asset class \(k\) yields expected return \(r_k\), carries regulatory risk weight \(w_k \ge 0\), and incurs wholesale debt funding cost \(r_D\). Total debt issued is \(D = \sum_{k=1}^K A_k - E\).

The dealer solves the profit-maximization problem:

\[ \max_{\{A_k\}_{k=1}^K} \sum_{k=1}^K (r_k - r_D) A_k + r_D E - \frac{1}{2} \phi \Var(\Pi) \]

subject to two regulatory constraints:

  1. Risk-Weighted Capital Constraint: \[ \sum_{k=1}^K w_k A_k \le \frac{E}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min}} \quad \iff \quad E - \mathrm{CAR}_{\min} \sum_{k=1}^K w_k A_k \ge 0 \quad (\text{multiplier } \lambda_{\mathrm{RWA}}) \]
  2. Supplementary Leverage Ratio Constraint: \[ \sum_{k=1}^K A_k \le \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} \quad \iff \quad E - \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \sum_{k=1}^K A_k \ge 0 \quad (\text{multiplier } \lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}}) \]
Theorem 3.4 (asset hurdle rate under dual regulatory constraints)

In the dealer constrained optimization problem, the marginal equilibrium return on asset class \(k\) required to justify balance-sheet expansion (the hurdle rate) satisfies the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) first-order condition:

\[ r_k - r_D = \lambda_{\mathrm{RWA}} \cdot \mathrm{CAR}_{\min} \cdot w_k + \lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} \cdot \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} + \phi \Cov(R_k, \Pi) \]

where \(\lambda_{\mathrm{RWA}} \ge 0\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} \ge 0\) are the non-negative Lagrange multipliers on the risk-weighted and leverage constraints, respectively.

Proof

Formulate the Lagrangian function \(\mathcal{L}(A_1, \dots, A_K, \lambda_{\mathrm{RWA}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}})\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} &= \sum_{k=1}^K (r_k - r_D) A_k + r_D E - \frac{1}{2} \phi \Var(\Pi) \\ &\quad + \lambda_{\mathrm{RWA}} \left( \frac{E}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min}} - \sum_{k=1}^K w_k A_k \right) + \lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} \left( \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} - \sum_{k=1}^K A_k \right) \end{aligned} \]

Differentiating with respect to asset allocation \(A_k\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial A_k} = (r_k - r_D) - \phi \Cov(R_k, \Pi) - \lambda_{\mathrm{RWA}} w_k - \lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} = 0 \]

Under statutory scaling where the constraint is written as capital consumed, multiplying through by the regulatory capital requirement coefficients yields the stated condition. Complementary slackness requires \(\lambda_{\mathrm{RWA}} \left(\frac{E}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min}} - \sum w_k A_k\right) = 0\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} \left(\frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} - \sum A_k\right) = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

3The Leverage Shadow Cost on Safe Assets

Theorem 3.4 reveals the central distortion created by unweighted leverage regulation in sovereign and money markets.

Consider a riskless asset, such as a U.S. Treasury bill or central bank reserve balance. Under Basel III risk-weighted rules, sovereign debt carries a regulatory risk weight of zero: \(w_{\mathrm{UST}} = 0\). Furthermore, because the asset is risk-free, \(\Cov(R_{\mathrm{UST}}, \Pi) = 0\).

Theorem 3.5 (the Treasury hurdle wedge)

Under the dual-constraint framework, if the Supplementary Leverage Ratio binds on the dealer (\(\lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} > 0\)), zero-risk-weight Treasury securities (\(w_{\mathrm{UST}} = 0\)) carry a strictly positive regulatory hurdle spread above the dealer's wholesale borrowing cost:

\[ r_{\mathrm{UST}} - r_D = \lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} \cdot \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} > 0 \]

At the dealer's cost of Tier 1 equity capital \(r_E\), the minimum required net carry spread on sovereign debt is \(\Delta r_{\mathrm{UST}} = \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \cdot r_E\). If the risk-weighted capital ratio binds instead (\(\lambda_{\mathrm{RWA}} > 0\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} = 0\)), the hurdle spread is identically zero.

Proof

Substituting \(w_{\mathrm{UST}} = 0\) and \(\Cov(R_{\mathrm{UST}}, \Pi) = 0\) into Theorem 3.4 yields:

\[ r_{\mathrm{UST}} - r_D = \lambda_{\mathrm{RWA}} \cdot \mathrm{CAR}_{\min} \cdot (0) + \lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} \cdot \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} = \lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} \cdot \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \]

When the SLR binds, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} > 0\). Each \$1 of additional Treasury assets expands TLE by \$1, requiring the dealer to allocate \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}\) dollars of equity capital to the position. If the opportunity cost of equity is \(r_E\), the economic cost of funding the position is \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \cdot r_E\). Thus, \(r_{\mathrm{UST}} - r_D = \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \cdot r_E > 0\). Conversely, when RWA binds and SLR is slack, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}} = 0\), yielding \(r_{\mathrm{UST}} - r_D = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.6 (quantifying the SLR hurdle spread)

Consider a U.S. G-SIB primary dealer facing an enhanced SLR requirement of \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min} = 5.0\%\). The dealer's annual cost of equity capital is \(r_E = 12.0\%\). If the SLR binds, holding \$100 of Treasury securities requires \$5.00 of Tier 1 equity. The required return on equity funds is \(0.05 \times 0.12 = 0.0060 = 60\text{ basis points}\). Therefore, the dealer cannot intermediate Treasuries at a break-even carry unless the asset yield exceeds its wholesale funding rate by at least 60 basis points!

"""Lab SR4-L03-1: Basel III Capital and Supplementary Leverage Ratio Optimization.

SIMULATED. Formulates and solves the primary dealer balance-sheet constrained
optimization problem under joint risk-weighted capital (RWA) and unweighted
Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) requirements (Basel Committee, 2014;
Adrian & Shin, 2010). Quantifies dealer intermediation capacity and the shadow
cost of leverage on zero-risk-weight Treasury securities.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_dealer_capacity(
    tier1_equity: float,
    slr_min: float,
    cet1_min: float,
    corp_weight: float,
    mbs_weight: float,
    treasury_weight: float,
    portfolio_mix: tuple[float, float, float],
    equity_cost_of_capital: float,
) -> dict[str, float | str | bool]:
    """Calculate dealer intermediation capacity and shadow costs under dual regulatory constraints.

    portfolio_mix: (share_corp, share_mbs, share_treasury) summing to 1.0.
    """
    share_c, share_m, share_t = portfolio_mix
    if abs(share_c + share_m + share_t - 1.0) > 1e-6:
        raise ValueError("portfolio shares must sum to 1.0")

    # 1. Capacity limit under unweighted SLR: TLE <= Tier1 / slr_min
    max_assets_slr = tier1_equity / slr_min

    # 2. Capacity limit under risk-weighted capital: RWA <= Tier1 / cet1_min
    # where RWA = Assets * (share_c * w_c + share_m * w_m + share_t * w_t)
    effective_risk_weight = share_c * corp_weight + share_m * mbs_weight + share_t * treasury_weight
    max_assets_rwa = tier1_equity / (cet1_min * effective_risk_weight) if effective_risk_weight > 0 else float("inf")

    # Binding constraint
    slr_binds = max_assets_slr <= max_assets_rwa
    binding_capacity = min(max_assets_slr, max_assets_rwa)
    binding_constraint = "SLR (Supplementary Leverage Ratio)" if slr_binds else "RWA (Risk-Weighted Capital)"

    # Shadow cost of leverage on zero-risk-weight Treasuries:
    # If SLR binds, expanding Treasury holdings by $1 requires holding $slr_min of Tier 1 equity.
    # At cost of equity r_e, the required hurdle spread is slr_min * r_e.
    # If RWA binds and Treasury risk weight is 0%, the hurdle spread is 0 bps!
    treasury_hurdle_bps = (slr_min * equity_cost_of_capital * 10000.0) if slr_binds else 0.0

    return {
        "tier1_equity": tier1_equity,
        "max_assets_slr": max_assets_slr,
        "max_assets_rwa": max_assets_rwa,
        "binding_capacity": binding_capacity,
        "binding_constraint": binding_constraint,
        "effective_risk_weight": effective_risk_weight,
        "slr_binds": slr_binds,
        "treasury_hurdle_bps": treasury_hurdle_bps,
    }


def main() -> None:
    tier1_equity = 50.0  # $50 billion Tier 1 Capital
    slr_min = 0.05  # 5.0% enhanced SLR for U.S. G-SIBs
    cet1_min = 0.10  # 10.0% CET1 / Tier 1 risk-based capital target
    cost_of_equity = 0.12  # 12.0% annual cost of equity capital

    # Asset risk weights:
    w_corp = 1.00  # 100% risk weight on corporate loans/bonds
    w_mbs = 0.20  # 20% risk weight on agency MBS
    w_ust = 0.00  # 0% risk weight on U.S. Treasuries / central bank reserves

    # Regime 1: High-risk traditional banking mix (Corp 50%, MBS 30%, Treasuries 20%)
    res_risky = solve_dealer_capacity(
        tier1_equity=tier1_equity,
        slr_min=slr_min,
        cet1_min=cet1_min,
        corp_weight=w_corp,
        mbs_weight=w_mbs,
        treasury_weight=w_ust,

4Balance-Sheet Elasticity and Market Structure

Prior to Basel III, primary dealer balance sheets were highly elastic. As documented by Adrian and Shin (2010), investment bank dealers expanded leverage during booms and contracted during downturns, behaving as procyclical liquidity providers .

Under a hard unweighted leverage cap, balance-sheet elasticity is extinguished:

Definition 3.7 (balance-sheet elasticity)

Balance-sheet elasticity is the responsiveness of dealer total assets to changes in market intermediation demand or arbitrage spreads: \(\epsilon_A = \frac{\partial \ln A}{\partial \ln (r - r_D)}\). Under a strictly binding SLR constraint, \(\epsilon_A = 0\): the dealer cannot expand its balance sheet to intermediate customer order flow regardless of how wide the arbitrage spread becomes, unless it raises additional equity capital or sheds non-Treasury assets.

Where the Canon Breaks

Classical corporate finance and banking theory rests on the Modigliani–Miller theorem: the composition of liabilities and the division between debt and equity should not distort real investment decisions or relative asset prices. Under the Basel III dual-constraint regime, Modigliani–Miller fails completely.

Because the SLR is unweighted, it introduces an artificial tax on balance-sheet size rather than balance-sheet risk. A bank holding \$1 billion of riskless central bank reserves or overnight reverse repo consumes the exact same \$50 million of Tier 1 equity under the eSLR as a bank holding \$1 billion of subprime corporate debt. Consequently, bank holding companies are systematically incentivized to reject low-margin sovereign intermediation and reserve deposits, pushing cash into non-bank shadow channels and degrading sovereign debt market depth.

Misconceptions

  • Holding U.S. Treasury securities consumes zero bank equity capital because Treasuries carry a 0% Basel risk weight.

    While Treasuries carry a 0% risk weight under the standardized risk-based framework, they are included at 100% of par value in the denominator of the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (Total Leverage Exposure). Under the 5% U.S. eSLR, holding \$100 of Treasuries consumes \$5.00 of Tier 1 equity capital .

  • The leverage ratio is redundant if risk-weighted capital ratios are calibrated conservatively.

    Risk-weighted models rely on historical default and loss correlations that broke down catastrophically in 2008. The unweighted leverage ratio provides an essential non-model-dependent backstop against model error, tail correlation spikes, and institutional gaming of internal rating systems.

  • Bank equity capital is a pool of idle cash locked in a vault that banks cannot invest.

    Equity is an accounting claim on the liability and net-worth side of the balance sheet, not an asset. Every dollar of equity is fully invested in operating assets (loans, securities, reserves). Capital requirements dictate how assets are funded, not what assets can be purchased.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A bank holding company holds \$45 billion in Tier 1 capital. Calculate its maximum allowable Total Leverage Exposure (TLE) under the 5.0% U.S. enhanced Supplementary Leverage Ratio (eSLR).

Solution

Under the eSLR, the leverage ratio constraint requires:

\[ \mathrm{TLE} \le \frac{\text{Tier 1 Capital}}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} = \frac{45}{0.05} = 900.0 \text{ billion dollars} \]

The bank's total balance-sheet and off-balance-sheet leverage exposure cannot exceed \$900.0 billion.

A bank has \$40 billion in Tier 1 capital. It holds a portfolio with an effective risk weight of 40%. The minimum CET1 risk-based capital ratio is 10.0%, and the minimum SLR is 5.0%. Determine whether the RWA constraint or the SLR constraint is binding on total balance-sheet size.

Solution

We compute the asset capacity ceilings under each constraint:

\[ \text{Max Assets (SLR)} = \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} = \frac{40}{0.05} = 800.0 \text{ billion dollars} \] \[ \text{Max Assets (RWA)} = \frac{E}{\mathrm{CET1}_{\min} \times \bar{w}} = \frac{40}{0.10 \times 0.40} = \frac{40}{0.04} = 1000.0 \text{ billion dollars} \]

Because \$800.0 billion is strictly less than \$1000.0 billion, the unweighted SLR constraint is the binding constraint on the bank's balance-sheet capacity.

A primary dealer faces a binding 6.0% IDI SLR requirement. The dealer's annual cost of Tier 1 equity capital is 11.0%. Calculate the required hurdle spread on zero-risk Treasury securities in basis points.

Solution

From Theorem 3.5, when the SLR binds, the required hurdle spread is:

\[ \Delta r_{\mathrm{UST}} = \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \times r_E = 0.06 \times 0.11 = 0.0066 = 66.0 \text{ basis points} \]

The dealer requires a 66.0 bps net spread to cover its regulatory equity cost on sovereign intermediation.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for any portfolio of assets with effective risk weight \(\bar{w} = \sum_k w_k \frac{A_k}{\sum_j A_j}\), the Supplementary Leverage Ratio binds if and only if \(\bar{w} < \frac{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min}}\).

Solution

Total asset capacity under the SLR constraint is \(A_{\mathrm{SLR}} = \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}}\). Total asset capacity under the risk-weighted capital constraint is \(A_{\mathrm{RWA}} = \frac{E}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min} \cdot \bar{w}}\). The SLR constraint is strictly binding if and only if \(A_{\mathrm{SLR}} < A_{\mathrm{RWA}}\):

\[ \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} < \frac{E}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min} \cdot \bar{w}} \]

Dividing both sides by \(E > 0\) and cross-multiplying by positive constants \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}\) and \(\mathrm{CAR}_{\min} \cdot \bar{w}\) yields:

\[ \mathrm{CAR}_{\min} \cdot \bar{w} < \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \iff \bar{w} < \frac{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min}} \]

Thus, the SLR binds if and only if the portfolio's average risk weight falls below the critical ratio \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min} / \mathrm{CAR}_{\min}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that if an asset carries risk weight \(w_k = \frac{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min}}\), its regulatory capital cost is invariant to whether the SLR or RWA constraint binds.

Solution

When the RWA constraint binds, the marginal capital consumed per dollar of asset \(k\) is \(\mathrm{CAR}_{\min} \cdot w_k\). Substituting \(w_k = \frac{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min}}\):

\[ \text{Capital Charge (RWA)} = \mathrm{CAR}_{\min} \times \frac{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}}{\mathrm{CAR}_{\min}} = \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \]

When the SLR constraint binds, the marginal capital consumed per dollar of any asset is identically \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}\). Since both charges equal \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}\), the capital cost of asset \(k\) is invariant to the binding regulatory regime. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that excluding central bank reserves from the denominator of the SLR increases maximum dealer balance-sheet capacity by exactly the amount of excluded reserves, holding Tier 1 capital constant.

Solution

Under the standard SLR, total assets \(A = A_{\mathrm{other}} + R \le \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}}\), so non-reserve capacity is \(A_{\mathrm{other}} \le \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} - R\). When reserves \(R\) are excluded from TLE, the constraint becomes \(\mathrm{TLE} = A_{\mathrm{other}} \le \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}}\). Total permissible assets are now \(A_{\mathrm{new}} = A_{\mathrm{other}} + R = \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} + R\). The change in maximum total balance-sheet size is:

\[ \Delta A_{\max} = A_{\mathrm{new}} - \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} = \left(\frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} + R\right) - \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}} = R \]

Maximum capacity increases by exactly \(R\). \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

In 2019, the aggregate Tier 1 capital of the eight U.S. G-SIB holding companies was approximately \$900 billion. Under the 5.0% eSLR, calculate the aggregate industry balance-sheet capacity ceiling in trillions of dollars.

Solution

The aggregate capacity ceiling is:

\[ \mathrm{TLE}_{\max} = \frac{900}{0.05} = 18{,}000 \text{ billion dollars} = 18.0 \text{ trillion dollars} \]

Aggregate U.S. G-SIB leverage exposure was constrained to a maximum of \$18.0 trillion.

Following the Federal Reserve's quantitative easing operations in early 2020, total bank reserves expanded by \$1.5 trillion. If the banking system faced an eSLR of 5.0%, calculate how many billions of dollars in Tier 1 equity capital were absorbed purely by holding the additional reserves before the April 2020 exemption rule was enacted.

Solution

Under the unweighted SLR, each dollar of reserve expansion consumes \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}\) of Tier 1 capital:

\[ \Delta E = \Delta R \times \mathrm{SLR}_{\min} = 1500 \times 0.05 = 75.0 \text{ billion dollars} \]

The expansion of central bank reserves absorbed \$75.0 billion in equity capital, severely crowding out dealer balance-sheet capacity for intermediating private credit and Treasuries.

An econometrician tests whether primary dealer bid-ask spreads in Treasury repo widen when the SLR constraint tightens. Regressing the Treasury general collateral repo spread against dealer leverage headroom yields \(\Delta \mathrm{Spread}_{\mathrm{bps}} = 2.4 - 0.035 \times \text{Headroom}_{\$B}\). If dealer headroom falls from \$80 billion to \$10 billion, calculate the predicted repo spread change.

Solution

The change in dealer leverage headroom is \(\Delta \text{Headroom} = 10 - 80 = -70\) billion dollars. The predicted spread change is:

\[ \Delta \mathrm{Spread} = -0.035 \times (-70) = +2.45 \text{ basis points} \]

As dealer leverage headroom contracts by \$70 billion, repo market intermediation spreads widen by 2.45 basis points.

Tier 4 · Research

Analyze how the Supplementary Leverage Ratio distorts the transmission of central bank monetary policy during quantitative easing (QE). What friction arises when reserve creation expands bank balance sheets?

Solution

When the central bank executes QE, it purchases securities from non-bank investors and credits the reserve accounts of commercial banks. This mechanically inflates commercial bank balance sheets: bank assets (reserves) and liabilities (customer deposits) expand dollar-for-dollar. Because reserves are included in Total Leverage Exposure, QE forces banks closer to their regulatory SLR ceilings without increasing their Tier 1 equity. As the SLR binds, banks disincentivize deposit inflows (lowering deposit rates or levying fees) and curtail market-making intermediation in repo and Treasury markets, creating an endogenous policy friction where monetary expansion paradoxically degrades market liquidity.

Evaluate the interaction between the Supplementary Leverage Ratio and the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR). Under what conditions does the SLR penalize a bank for holding High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA)?

Solution

The Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) requires banks to hold an unencumbered buffer of High-Quality Liquid Assets (Level 1 HQLA: central bank reserves and sovereign debt) sufficient to withstand a 30-day stressed cash outflow. While the LCR mandates a large safe-asset buffer, the SLR penalizes the total size of that buffer by imposing an unweighted 5% capital charge on TLE. Consequently, banks face contradictory regulatory mandates: the liquidity regulation forces them to hold reserves and Treasuries, while the leverage regulation penalizes them for holding the required safe assets.

Assess proposed structural reforms to recalibrate the enhanced SLR for U.S. G-SIBs, such as replacing the fixed 2.0% buffer with a dynamically calibrated surcharge tied to a bank's G-SIB systemic footprint. What are the economic merits of this approach?

Solution

Under the 2014 rule, the 2.0% eSLR buffer was calibrated as a static add-on, making the leverage ratio persistently tighter than risk-based capital for institutions specializing in custody, clearing, and sovereign market making. A dynamic recalibration—scaling the leverage buffer to 50% of the bank's risk-based G-SIB surcharge—restores the original Basel III architectural intent: ensuring that the leverage ratio serves as a backstop against unexpected tail risk rather than the everyday binding constraint governing marginal safe-asset transactions.

  1. What is the statutory minimum enhanced Supplementary Leverage Ratio (eSLR) requirement for U.S. Global Systemically Important Bank (G-SIB) holding companies?

  2. Why does the Supplementary Leverage Ratio bind aggressively on banks specializing in wholesale market making and custody services?

    Options

    When a bank's average risk weight falls below the ratio of leverage to risk-based requirements (\(\bar{w} < \mathrm{SLR} / \mathrm{CAR}\)), the unweighted leverage cap binds first (Theorem 3.4, Theorem 3.5).

  3. If the regulatory SLR requirement is 5.0%, what is the maximum balance-sheet leverage multiplier (Total Assets / Tier 1 Capital)?

    \(1.0 / 0.05 = 20.0\times\).

  4. What is the economic consequence of a binding SLR on the pricing of zero-risk-weight U.S. Treasury securities?

    Options

    As proven in Theorem 3.5, holding an extra dollar of Treasuries consumes Tier 1 equity, creating a hurdle spread \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \cdot r_E\).

  5. A dealer has an effective portfolio risk weight of \(\bar{w} = 0.50\). If the minimum risk-based CAR is 10.0% and the minimum SLR is 5.0%, what is the critical threshold ratio \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min} / \mathrm{CAR}_{\min}\) expressed as a percentage?

    \((0.05 / 0.10) \times 100 = 50.0\%\).

  6. Which of the following exposures is INCLUDED in Total Leverage Exposure (TLE) under Basel III?

    Options

    Total Leverage Exposure includes all on-balance-sheet assets without risk weights, repo exposures, and derivative exposures (Definition 3.1).

  7. A bank dealer has an annual cost of Tier 1 equity capital of 12.0% and faces a binding 5.0% SLR constraint. Calculate the equity hurdle rate on sovereign debt in basis points: \(0.05 \times 0.12 \times 10{,}000\).

    \(0.05 \times 0.12 \times 10{,}000 = 60.0\) basis points.

  8. How does quantitative easing (QE) by the central bank interact with commercial banks' Supplementary Leverage Ratios?

    Options

    QE expands bank reserve holdings, increasing the TLE denominator and causing the SLR constraint to bind more tightly.

  9. A bank holds \$60 billion in Tier 1 equity. If regulators set the minimum SLR to 5.0%, calculate the maximum total balance-sheet capacity in billions of dollars.

    \(60.0 / 0.05 = 1200.0\) billion dollars.

  10. What does balance-sheet elasticity refer to in primary dealer market microstructure?

    Options

    Balance-sheet elasticity measures how readily dealers can expand balance-sheet capacity in response to customer selling and arbitrage spreads (Definition 3.7).
