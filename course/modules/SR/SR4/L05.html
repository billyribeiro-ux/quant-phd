Modern financial rulemaking requires more than qualitative legal drafting; it demands rigorous empirical cost-benefit quantification under statutory mandates and administrative law standards. A regulatory intervention designed to enhance individual bank solvency can unintentionally degrade aggregate market liquidity, raise sovereign borrowing costs, and induce regulatory arbitrage across shadow banking channels. Regulatory Impact Analysis (RIA) provides the microeconomic and econometric methodology to model policy counterfactuals, quantify direct compliance expenses against systemic risk mitigation, and evaluate welfare trade-offs. This lesson establishes the quantitative framework of financial RIA, evaluating major post-2020 structural reforms to the Supplementary Leverage Ratio and Treasury market clearing.

1Principles and Methodology of Financial RIA

In the United States, administrative law (governed by the Administrative Procedure Act, executive orders, and judicial rulings such as Business Roundtable v. SEC) requires economic regulatory agencies to conduct formal economic analysis prior to promulgating major financial rules.

Definition 5.1 (regulatory-impact analysis)

The regulatory-impact analysis (RIA) is a formal quantitative and qualitative evaluative methodology assessing the incremental net economic benefit of a proposed or enacted financial regulation by defining a clear policy baseline, constructing counterfactual structural models, quantifying direct compliance and funding costs against systemic risk reduction benefits, and evaluating unintended market distortions or structural capacity constraints .

A rigorous financial RIA proceeds through four sequential methodological stages:

  1. Problem Identification and Market Failure: Articulating the precise externality, information asymmetry, or systemic coordination failure that private contracting cannot resolve (e.g. fire-sale externalities or unmodeled leverage tail risk).
  2. Counterfactual Baseline Setting: Defining the state of the financial system in the absence of the proposed rule, accounting for existing complementary regulations (e.g. Basel III risk-weighted capital, the Volcker Rule, and central clearing).
  3. Behavioral Responses and Equilibrium Adjustments: Modeling how regulated banks and unconstrained non-bank market participants adjust portfolio allocations, capital structures, and trading strategies in response to regulatory incentives.
  4. Net Social Welfare Quantification: Measuring the net societal gain \(\Delta W = \Delta B - \Delta C\), explicitly balancing reduced probability of systemic crises against market liquidity costs and sovereign financing friction.

2The Solvency versus Liquidity Trade-Off

The fundamental structural tension in wholesale market regulation is the trade-off between banking sector solvency and secondary market liquidity .

Increasing the stringency of the unweighted Supplementary Leverage Ratio (\(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}\)) produces two opposing macroeconomic effects:

  • Prudential Benefit (Solvency): Raising \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}\) forces banks to hold more Tier 1 equity per dollar of total assets, reducing the annualized probability of bank default \(p_d(\mathrm{SLR})\) and lowering the expected societal cost of catastrophic banking collapses and government bailouts: \[ B_{\mathrm{solvency}}(\mathrm{SLR}) = [p_d(\text{Baseline}) - p_d(\mathrm{SLR})] \cdot L_{\mathrm{bailout}} \] where \(L_{\mathrm{bailout}}\) is the deadweight loss of a systemic financial crisis.
  • Microstructural Cost (Liquidity & Debt Issuance): Because the SLR treats sovereign debt identically to risky private credit, raising \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min}\) constrains primary dealer balance-sheet capacity \(C_T(\mathrm{SLR}) = \frac{E}{\mathrm{SLR}} - A_{\mathrm{other}}\). This widens secondary Treasury bid-ask spreads \(s(C_T)\) and raises the sovereign debt issuance concession \(y_{\mathrm{concession}}(C_T)\), imposing deadweight costs on investors and taxpayers: \[ C_{\mathrm{market}}(\mathrm{SLR}) = V_{\mathrm{secondary}} \cdot [s(\mathrm{SLR}) - s_0] + D_{\mathrm{new}} \cdot [y_{\mathrm{concession}}(\mathrm{SLR}) - y_0] \]
Theorem 5.2 (optimal regulatory leverage ratio calibration)

Let the social welfare function be \(W(\mathrm{SLR}) = B_{\mathrm{solvency}}(\mathrm{SLR}) - C_{\mathrm{market}}(\mathrm{SLR})\), where \(p_d(\mathrm{SLR})\) is strictly decreasing and convex in \(\mathrm{SLR}\) (\(p_d' < 0, p_d'' > 0\)), and market illiquidity costs \(C_{\mathrm{market}}(\mathrm{SLR})\) are strictly increasing and convex in \(\mathrm{SLR}\) (\(C_{\mathrm{market}}' > 0, C_{\mathrm{market}}'' > 0\)). The socially optimal leverage ratio \(\mathrm{SLR}^*\) satisfies the first-order condition:

\[ -p_d'(\mathrm{SLR}^*) \cdot L_{\mathrm{bailout}} = V_{\mathrm{secondary}} \cdot \frac{\partial s}{\partial \mathrm{SLR}} + D_{\mathrm{new}} \cdot \frac{\partial y_{\mathrm{concession}}}{\partial \mathrm{SLR}} \]

At the optimum, the marginal reduction in expected systemic crisis costs exactly equals the marginal increase in wholesale transaction costs and sovereign debt service.

Proof

Differentiating \(W(\mathrm{SLR}) = -p_d(\mathrm{SLR}) L_{\mathrm{bailout}} - C_{\mathrm{market}}(\mathrm{SLR}) + \text{constant}\) with respect to \(\mathrm{SLR}\):

\[ \frac{\partial W}{\partial \mathrm{SLR}} = -p_d'(\mathrm{SLR}) L_{\mathrm{bailout}} - \left( V_{\mathrm{secondary}} \frac{\partial s}{\partial \mathrm{SLR}} + D_{\mathrm{new}} \frac{\partial y_{\mathrm{concession}}}{\partial \mathrm{SLR}} \right) = 0 \]

Rearranging yields the condition. Because \(W''(\mathrm{SLR}) = -p_d''(\mathrm{SLR}) L_{\mathrm{bailout}} - C_{\mathrm{market}}''(\mathrm{SLR}) < 0\), the objective function is strictly concave, ensuring that the critical point \(\mathrm{SLR}^*\) is the unique global welfare maximum. \(\blacksquare\)

3Evaluating Post-2020 Structural Market Reforms

Following the market dislocations of March 2020, financial regulatory agencies initiated two landmark structural market reforms to resolve the dealer balance-sheet capacity bottleneck.

1. SEC Mandatory Treasury Central Clearing (2023)

In December 2023, the Securities and Exchange Commission adopted final rules amending the standards for covered clearing agencies for U.S. Treasury securities. The rule mandates that clearing agencies require their direct participants to submit for clearing all secondary market transactions in U.S. Treasury securities and all repurchase and reverse repurchase agreements collateralized by U.S. Treasuries.

Under RIA evaluation, the primary economic benefit is balance-sheet netting: novating repo and reverse repo trades to the Fixed Income Clearing Corporation (FICC) allows dealers to net offsetting obligations with different counterparties, collapsing on-balance-sheet Total Leverage Exposure and unlocking hundreds of billions of dollars in intermediation headroom without altering capital requirements.

2. Federal Reserve Enhanced SLR Recalibration (2025)

In 2025, the Federal Reserve Board, OCC, and FDIC issued final rules modifying the enhanced Supplementary Leverage Ratio framework for U.S. G-SIBs . The reform replaces the static 2.0% holding company leverage buffer with a dynamic buffer calibrated to 50% of the firm's risk-based G-SIB surcharge, while recalibrating the insured depository institution (IDI) requirement from 6.0% down to 4.0% - 4.5%.

This RIA-backed recalibration restores the leverage ratio to its intended status as a non-binding backstop for routine sovereign transactions, while maintaining high equity buffers for high-risk lending books.

"""Lab SR4-L05-1: Quantitative Regulatory-Impact Analysis Framework.

SIMULATED. Implements a quantitative Regulatory Impact Analysis (RIA) model
evaluating post-2020 reforms to the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) and
dealer intermediation capacity (Federal Reserve Board, 2020, 2025; Basel
Committee, 2014). Solves for social welfare trade-offs between market liquidity
benefits, sovereign borrowing cost savings, and incremental systemic risk.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def evaluate_regulatory_regime(
    regime_name: str,
    dealer_equity: float,
    slr_threshold: float,
    treasury_exemption: bool,
    annual_treasury_volume: float,
    public_debt_issuance: float,
    default_prob_annual: float,
    systemic_bailout_cost: float,
) -> dict[str, float | str]:
    """Calculate market liquidity gains, sovereign savings, and net annual welfare."""
    if dealer_equity <= 0 or slr_threshold <= 0:
        raise ValueError("equity and threshold must be positive")

    # Dealer intermediation capacity for Treasuries ($ billions)
    # If exempt, Treasuries do not consume SLR capacity; capacity is bounded by risk capital (~$1,200B)
    if treasury_exemption:
        treasury_capacity = 1200.0
        effective_spread_bps = 0.20  # Tight bid-ask spread
        issuance_spread_bps = 1.00  # Low sovereign yield concession
    else:
        # Bounded by (Equity / SLR) - Non-Treasury assets ($800B)
        max_total = dealer_equity / slr_threshold
        treasury_capacity = max(100.0, max_total - 800.0)
        # Tighter capacity implies higher intermediation spread
        capacity_factor = 400.0 / treasury_capacity
        effective_spread_bps = 0.20 * capacity_factor
        issuance_spread_bps = 1.00 * capacity_factor

    # 1. Market Liquidity Annual Savings ($ billions):
    # Reduction in transaction costs = Volume * (Spread_baseline - Spread_regime)
    # Baseline spread: 0.80 bps
    spread_reduction_bps = max(0.0, 0.80 - effective_spread_bps)
    liquidity_benefit = annual_treasury_volume * (spread_reduction_bps / 10000.0)

    # 2. Sovereign Debt Service Annual Savings ($ billions):
    # Concession reduction on annual new issuance
    # Baseline issuance concession: 4.0 bps
    issuance_savings_bps = max(0.0, 4.00 - issuance_spread_bps)
    sovereign_benefit = public_debt_issuance * (issuance_savings_bps / 10000.0)

    # 3. Expected Annual Systemic Risk Cost ($ billions):
    # Expected annual loss = default_probability * systemic_bailout_cost
    expected_risk_cost = default_prob_annual * systemic_bailout_cost

    # Net Social Benefit ($ billions / year)
    net_welfare = liquidity_benefit + sovereign_benefit - expected_risk_cost

    return {
        "regime_name": regime_name,
        "treasury_capacity": treasury_capacity,
        "bid_ask_spread_bps": effective_spread_bps,
        "liquidity_benefit": liquidity_benefit,
        "sovereign_benefit": sovereign_benefit,
        "expected_risk_cost": expected_risk_cost,
        "net_welfare": net_welfare,
    }


def main() -> None:
    equity = 60.0  # $60B Tier 1 capital
    annual_volume = 150000.0  # $150,000B ($150T) annual secondary Treasury trading volume
    debt_issuance = 4000.0  # $4,000B ($4T) annual gross Treasury issuance
    bailout_cost = 500.0  # $500B social cost of G-SIB systemic distress

    # Regime 1: Baseline Status Quo (Strict 5.0% SLR, No Exemption)
    # Default prob: 0.10% (10 bps/year)
    reg_baseline = evaluate_regulatory_regime(
        regime_name="1. Status Quo (5.0% SLR, No Exemption)",
        dealer_equity=equity,

        treasury_exemption=False,
        annual_treasury_volume=annual_volume,
        public_debt_issuance=debt_issuance,

    reg_exempt = evaluate_regulatory_regime(

        slr_threshold=0.05,

        annual_treasury_volume=annual_volume,
        public_debt_issuance=debt_issuance,

    # Regime 3: Calibrated Buffer Reform (3.5% Base SLR + 1.0% G-SIB Surcharge, Partial Relief)

        treasury_exemption=False,

        print(f"  Annual Secondary Liquidity Benefit: ${float(reg['liquidity_benefit']):.2f}B")
        print(f"  Annual Sovereign Borrowing Savings: ${float(reg['sovereign_benefit']):.2f}B")

4The Lucas Critique in Regulatory Architecture

A central challenge in conducting financial regulatory impact analysis is the Lucas Critique: econometric relationships estimated under one regulatory regime cannot be treated as structural invariants when predicting behavior under a new regime.

When capital and leverage regulations alter the relative profitability of bank market making, financial activity does not simply contract; it endogenously migrates to unregulated or lightly regulated non-bank financial intermediaries (NBFIs), including private credit funds, principal trading firms (PTFs), and offshore hedge funds. An RIA that treats non-bank participation as exogenous will severely miscalculate both market liquidity resilience and systemic financial stability.

Where the Canon Breaks

The traditional administrative canon of cost-benefit analysis assumes that regulatory costs can be neatly separated into direct accounting compliance expenses (legal fees, reporting systems, capital costs) and social benefits (avoided crises). In interconnected financial networks, this separation is an illusion.

Every dollar of equity capital mandated by an unweighted leverage constraint creates non-linear general equilibrium spillover effects: it alters wholesale repo hair-cuts, widens sovereign debt issuance concessions, shifts arbitrage trades into shadow non-banks, and increases central bank balance-sheet dependency. A static cost-benefit model that ignores endogenous balance-sheet reallocation and network contagion will systematically fail to predict the true market footprint of financial regulation.

Misconceptions

  • Regulatory impact analysis is merely an ex-post political document written to justify decisions already made.

    Under modern administrative law and judicial review standards, federal courts routinely strike down major financial regulations if an agency fails to articulate a rigorous, empirical, and falsifiable economic cost-benefit analysis supported by data.

  • Financial regulations that increase bank capital requirements are always socially beneficial because they make banks safer.

    Higher capital standards create positive macroprudential benefits, but if unweighted leverage ratios are calibrated excessively tight, they impose massive deadweight costs on sovereign debt markets, widen secondary trading spreads, and increase the interest expense borne by taxpayers on public debt .

  • Exempting central bank reserves from the leverage ratio gives banks a free subsidy with zero systemic cost.

    Exempting reserves and sovereign debt lowers the effective equity buffer supporting bank balance sheets. If banks expand overall leverage without equity backstops, unexpected asset losses or liquidity shocks can deplete solvency buffers, increasing the probability of banking failure and emergency public bailouts.

A proposed market reform reduces secondary Treasury bid-ask spreads by 0.35 basis points across an annual secondary trading volume of \$140 trillion. Calculate the annual transaction cost savings for market participants in billions of dollars.

Solution

Annual transaction cost savings are calculated as:

\[ \text{Savings} = \text{Volume} \times \left(\frac{\Delta \text{Spread}}{10{,}000}\right) = 140{,}000 \times \left(\frac{0.35}{10{,}000}\right) = 140{,}000 \times 0.000035 = 4.90 \text{ billion dollars} \]

Market participants save \$4.90 billion annually in execution costs.

The U.S. government issues \$3.5 trillion in gross marketable Treasury debt annually. An enhanced SLR recalibration reduces the primary issuance yield concession by 1.20 basis points. Calculate the annual debt service savings for the sovereign treasury in billions of dollars.

Solution

Annual sovereign debt service savings are calculated as:

\[ \text{Sovereign Savings} = 3{,}500 \times \left(\frac{1.20}{10{,}000}\right) = 3{,}500 \times 0.000120 = 0.420 \text{ billion dollars (\$420 million)} \]

Taxpayers save \$420 million annually in sovereign debt financing costs.

A regulatory reform changes the annual default probability of a G-SIB from 0.08% to 0.12%. If the systemic social loss of a G-SIB failure is estimated at \$600 billion, calculate the change in expected annual systemic bailout cost in billions of dollars.

Solution

The change in annual default probability is \(\Delta p_d = 0.0012 - 0.0008 = +0.0004\). The change in expected annual bailout cost is:

\[ \Delta \text{Cost} = \Delta p_d \times L_{\mathrm{bailout}} = 0.0004 \times 600.0 = 0.240 \text{ billion dollars (\$240 million)} \]

Expected systemic risk costs increase by \$240 million per year.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that if net social welfare \(W(\theta) = B(\theta) - C(\theta)\) is strictly concave in regulatory stringency parameter \(\theta\) on compact interval \([\underline{\theta}, \bar{\theta}]\), and the marginal benefit at the boundary satisfies \(B'(\underline{\theta}) > C'(\underline{\theta})\) and \(B'(\bar{\theta}) < C'(\bar{\theta})\), then there exists a unique interior optimum \(\theta^* \in (\underline{\theta}, \bar{\theta})\) where marginal benefit strictly equals marginal cost.

Solution

Define the marginal net welfare function \(f(\theta) = W'(\theta) = B'(\theta) - C'(\theta)\). By assumption, \(W(\theta)\) is twice continuously differentiable and strictly concave, which implies that \(W''(\theta) = f'(\theta) < 0\) for all \(\theta \in [\underline{\theta}, \bar{\theta}]\); that is, \(f(\theta)\) is strictly decreasing on the interval. Evaluating at the boundaries, \(f(\underline{\theta}) = B'(\underline{\theta}) - C'(\underline{\theta}) > 0\) and \(f(\bar{\theta}) = B'(\bar{\theta}) - C'(\bar{\theta}) < 0\). By the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists at least one \(\theta^* \in (\underline{\theta}, \bar{\theta})\) such that \(f(\theta^*) = 0\), which means \(B'(\theta^*) = C'(\theta^*)\). Because \(f(\theta)\) is strictly decreasing, this root is unique. Since \(W''(\theta^*) < 0\), \(\theta^*\) is the unique global maximum of social welfare. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that under mandatory central clearing of Treasury repos, multilateral netting weakly compresses on-balance-sheet Total Leverage Exposure (TLE) relative to bilateral settlement.

Solution

Under bilateral settlement, a dealer holding repo borrowing \(R_{ij}\) with counterparty \(j\) and reverse repo lending \(RR_{ik}\) with counterparty \(k \neq j\) must record both positions on its balance sheet without netting (since close-out netting applies only pairwise with the identical legal entity). Total leverage exposure is \(\mathrm{TLE}_{\mathrm{bilat}} = \sum_j R_{ij} + \sum_k RR_{ik}\). Under mandatory central clearing through a covered clearinghouse (such as FICC), all repos and reverse repos are novated to the CCP as the common counterparty. Under GAAP ASC 210-20 (FIN 41), qualifying repo and reverse repo transactions with the same counterparty maturing on the same date can be netted into a single net asset or liability. Total leverage exposure becomes \(\mathrm{TLE}_{\mathrm{CCP}} = \max\left(0, \sum_j R_{ij} - \sum_k RR_{ik}\right)\). By the triangle inequality, \(\mathrm{TLE}_{\mathrm{CCP}} \le \mathrm{TLE}_{\mathrm{bilat}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that if an unweighted leverage ratio binds on safe assets, a reduction in the leverage requirement from \(\mathrm{SLR}_1\) to \(\mathrm{SLR}_2 < \mathrm{SLR}_1\) expands dealer Treasury capacity with elasticity strictly exceeding unity if non-Treasury assets \(A_{\mathrm{other}} > 0\).

Solution

Dealer Treasury capacity is \(C(s) = \frac{E}{s} - A_{\mathrm{other}}\), where \(s\) is the SLR ratio. The derivative with respect to \(s\) is \(\frac{\partial C}{\partial s} = -\frac{E}{s^2}\). The elasticity of capacity with respect to the leverage ratio \(s\) is:

\[ \epsilon = \frac{\partial C}{\partial s} \cdot \frac{s}{C(s)} = \left(-\frac{E}{s^2}\right) \cdot \frac{s}{\frac{E}{s} - A_{\mathrm{other}}} = -\frac{\frac{E}{s}}{\frac{E}{s} - A_{\mathrm{other}}} \]

Let total capacity be \(A_{\mathrm{tot}} = \frac{E}{s}\). Then \(\epsilon = -\frac{A_{\mathrm{tot}}}{A_{\mathrm{tot}} - A_{\mathrm{other}}}\). Because non-Treasury assets \(A_{\mathrm{other}} > 0\), the denominator \(A_{\mathrm{tot}} - A_{\mathrm{other}} < A_{\mathrm{tot}}\). Therefore, the magnitude of the elasticity satisfies \(|\epsilon| = \frac{A_{\mathrm{tot}}}{A_{\mathrm{tot}} - A_{\mathrm{other}}} > 1\). Thus, dealer Treasury intermediation capacity is strictly elastic (\(|\epsilon| > 1\)) with respect to regulatory leverage recalibration. \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

In an RIA evaluating the 2025 eSLR recalibration, secondary liquidity benefits are estimated at \$5.2 billion/year, debt service savings at \$1.4 billion/year, and incremental systemic risk costs at \$0.8 billion/year. Direct legal and accounting compliance costs across all G-SIBs are \$0.3 billion/year. Calculate the net annual societal welfare benefit in billions of dollars.

Net annual social welfare is the sum of benefits minus costs:

The proposed eSLR reform yields a net positive economic welfare contribution of \$5.50 billion annually.

An empirical study of FICC Treasury repo clearing estimates that mandatory central clearing increases bilateral netting efficiency from 45% to 75% across \$4.0 trillion in gross repo transactions. Calculate the net reduction in on-balance-sheet leverage exposure across the dealer sector in trillions of dollars.

Solution

Under bilateral netting (45% efficiency), netted exposure is:

\[ E_{\mathrm{bilat}} = 4.0 \times (1 - 0.45) = 4.0 \times 0.55 = 2.20 \text{ trillion dollars} \]

Under central clearing (75% efficiency), netted exposure is:

\[ E_{\mathrm{CCP}} = 4.0 \times (1 - 0.75) = 4.0 \times 0.25 = 1.00 \text{ trillion dollars} \]

The net balance-sheet exposure compression across the dealer sector is:

\[ \Delta \mathrm{TLE} = 2.20 - 1.00 = 1.20 \text{ trillion dollars} \]

Mandatory clearing compresses \$1.20 trillion in balance-sheet exposure, creating substantial leverage headroom.

Econometric estimation reveals that the arc elasticity of primary dealer Treasury capacity with respect to the regulatory SLR requirement between 5.0% and 4.0% is -2.25. If regulators lower the SLR requirement by 10% (e.g. from 5.0% to 4.5%), predict the percentage increase in primary dealer Treasury capacity.

Solution

Using the definition of elasticity \(\% \Delta C = \epsilon \times \% \Delta \mathrm{SLR}\):

\[ \% \Delta C = (-2.25) \times (-10.0\%) = +22.5\% \]

A 10% reduction in the regulatory leverage requirement expands dealer Treasury capacity by 22.5%.

Tier 4 · Research

Formulate a structural model of regulatory arbitrage under the SLR. How does a binding leverage ratio on banks induce the migration of repo financing into non-bank sponsored repo facilities (such as FICC Sponsored Repo)?

Solution

Under standard bilateral repo, a bank acting as cash borrower or lender must record the gross repo receivable or payable in its Total Leverage Exposure, incurring an equity charge \(\mathrm{SLR}_{\min} \cdot r_E\). To avoid this capital cost, banks utilize FICC Sponsored Repo: a bank sponsors a non-bank client (such as a money market fund or hedge fund) into the CCP. The sponsored client transacts directly with the CCP, while the sponsoring bank provides an off-balance-sheet guarantee. Because the CCP contract is cleared and nets against other FICC positions, the sponsoring bank reduces its on-balance-sheet leverage footprint while continuing to earn intermediation fees. However, this shifts systemic liquidity risk into the clearinghouse and non-bank repo channels.

Evaluate the macroeconomic debate over 'central bank footprint': if the Federal Reserve must continually intervene with emergency purchases and facilities (such as the SRF and emergency SLR exemptions) whenever the Treasury market faces stress, does this constitute regulatory failure in the design of Basel III?

Solution

The repeated need for central bank emergency intervention reveals an architectural inconsistency in post-2008 regulatory design. Basel III was calibrated under the premise that banks would become self-sufficient private shock absorbers. However, unweighted leverage ratios simultaneously stripped dealers of the balance-sheet elasticity required to warehouse sovereign debt during panic liquidations. Consequently, the central bank was forced to step in as the buyer and dealer of last resort, expanding the public sector's footprint in wholesale price formation and creating structural moral hazard.

Design an optimal regulatory framework that resolves the tension between bank leverage discipline and sovereign debt market liquidity. Compare a leverage ratio that excludes central bank reserves against an approach that dynamically adjusts the leverage buffer over the business cycle.

Solution

An optimal regulatory framework combines structural exclusion with countercyclical calibration. Excluding central bank reserves from TLE eliminates the mechanical friction where monetary policy expansion (QE) crowds out private bank lending. For sovereign debt, rather than a full permanent exemption (which invites excessive unhedged duration risk), the optimal rule establishes a countercyclical leverage buffer: during macro stress regimes, the leverage buffer is automatically lowered, permitting primary dealers to expand balance sheets and absorb customer selling without regulatory breach, and then gradually restored during tranquil expansions.
