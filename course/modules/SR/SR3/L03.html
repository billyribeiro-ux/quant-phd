Percentage risk metrics like CoVaR and MES determine how sensitive a bank's equity is to stress, but they cannot answer the central question facing macroprudential regulators: in a catastrophic systemic crisis, how many billions of dollars of capital would the financial system need to avoid collapse? Brownlees and Engle (2017) resolve this by combining dynamic market tail expectations (LRMES) with accounting balance-sheet debt and prudential capital requirements to create SRISK. By tracking dollar capital deficits, SRISK identifies systemically important financial institutions (SIFIs) and aggregates economy-wide capital shortfalls in real time.

1Macroprudential Solvency and Capital Shortfall Theory

Banking regulations such as Basel III and Federal Reserve Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review (CCAR) require institutions to maintain a minimum ratio of equity capital to total assets. When an asset shock hits, equity absorbs the first loss while nominal debt obligations remain fixed .

Let \(W_{i,t}\) denote the market value of equity of institution \(i\) at time \(t\), and let \(D_{i,t}\) denote the book value of its total debt liabilities. The institution's quasi-market assets are:

\[ A_{i,t} = D_{i,t} + W_{i,t} \]

and its quasi-market leverage ratio is \(\text{Lev}_{i,t} = \frac{A_{i,t}}{W_{i,t}} = 1 + \frac{D_{i,t}}{W_{i,t}}\).

Regulators mandate that equity capital must constitute at least fraction \(k\) of total assets, where \(k\) is the prudential capital fraction (typically \(k = 0.08\) or 8% in the United States, and \(k = 0.055\) or 5.5% under European regulatory standards) . Under baseline operating conditions, the institution requires capital \(k A_{i,t} = k(D_{i,t} + W_{i,t})\). The institution is adequately capitalized if \(W_{i,t} \ge k A_{i,t}\), or equivalently if its market leverage satisfies \(\text{Lev}_{i,t} \le \frac{1}{k}\).

2The Brownlees & Engle (2017) SRISK Formulation

Brownlees and Engle (2017) analyze what happens to the institution's balance sheet during a systemic market crisis, defined as a multi-month period over which aggregate equity markets decline by fraction \(d\) (standard benchmark \(d = 0.40\) or 40% over 6 months). During this crisis, the institution's equity declines by its Long-Run Marginal Expected Shortfall: \(\text{LRMES}_{i,t}\).

Definition 2.1 (Post-Crisis Balance Sheet and SRISK)

Assuming book debt \(D_{i,t}\) cannot be renegotiated or defaulted upon during the crisis horizon, the post-crisis market value of equity and post-crisis total assets are:

\[ W_{i,t+h}^{\text{crisis}} = \left(1 - \text{LRMES}_{i,t}\right) W_{i,t} \] \[ A_{i,t+h}^{\text{crisis}} = D_{i,t} + W_{i,t+h}^{\text{crisis}} = D_{i,t} + \left(1 - \text{LRMES}_{i,t}\right) W_{i,t} \]

The post-crisis capital requirement is \(k A_{i,t+h}^{\text{crisis}}\). The conditional capital shortfall \(\text{CS}_{i,t}\) is the dollar deficiency between required capital and post-crisis equity :

\[ \text{CS}_{i,t} = k A_{i,t+h}^{\text{crisis}} - W_{i,t+h}^{\text{crisis}} = k D_{i,t} - (1 - k)\left(1 - \text{LRMES}_{i,t}\right) W_{i,t} \]

Because an institution with excess capital (a capital surplus, \(\text{CS}_{i,t} < 0\)) cannot be legally coerced to bailout failing competitors, individual SRISK truncates the shortfall at zero :

\[ \text{SRISK}_{i,t} = \max\left(0, \; \text{CS}_{i,t}\right) = \max\left(0, \; k D_{i,t} - (1 - k)\left(1 - \text{LRMES}_{i,t}\right) W_{i,t}\right) \]
Remark 2.2 (Units and Dimensions of SRISK)

Unlike percentage risk metrics (\(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) in bps, \(\text{MES}\) in %), \(\text{SRISK}\) is denominated in monetary units (billions of USD or EUR). It represents the exact recapitalization required by an institution to restore its capital ratio to \(k\) following a systemic collapse.

3Aggregate Systemic SRISK and Percentage Contribution Index

A crucial property of SRISK is that it provides a natural, non-arbitrary aggregation across the entire financial sector.

Definition 2.3 (Aggregate Systemic SRISK and Contribution Index)

The aggregate systemic capital shortfall of the financial system, denoted \(\text{SRISK}_{\text{sys}, t}\), is the total dollar deficit summed across all undercapitalized institutions :

\[ \text{SRISK}_{\text{sys}, t} = \sum_{i=1}^N \text{SRISK}_{i,t} = \sum_{i=1}^N \max\left(0, \; \text{CS}_{i,t}\right) \]

The systemic risk contribution share of institution \(i\), denoted \(\text{SRISK}\%_{i,t}\), measures the fraction of aggregate systemic deficit attributable to firm \(i\):

\[ \text{SRISK}\%_{i,t} = \begin{cases} \frac{\text{SRISK}_{i,t}}{\text{SRISK}_{\text{sys}, t}} & \text{if } \text{SRISK}_{\text{sys}, t} > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } \text{SRISK}_{\text{sys}, t} = 0 \end{cases} \]

By construction, \(\sum_{i=1}^N \text{SRISK}\%_{i,t} = 1\) (or 100%) whenever the system faces an aggregate shortfall.

4Comparative Statics and Sensitivity Decompositions

The analytical structure of SRISK permits explicit closed-form comparative statics, revealing how individual balance-sheet components drive systemic vulnerability.

Theorem 2.4 (SRISK Sensitivities)

For an institution operating in capital shortfall (\(\text{CS}_{i,t} > 0\)), the partial derivatives of \(\text{SRISK}_{i,t}\) with respect to book debt, equity capitalization, tail risk, and the regulatory benchmark satisfy :

\[ \frac{\partial \text{SRISK}_{i,t}}{\partial D_{i,t}} = k > 0 \] \[ \frac{\partial \text{SRISK}_{i,t}}{\partial W_{i,t}} = -(1 - k)\left(1 - \text{LRMES}_{i,t}\right) < 0 \] \[ \frac{\partial \text{SRISK}_{i,t}}{\partial \text{LRMES}_{i,t}} = (1 - k) W_{i,t} > 0 \] \[ \frac{\partial \text{SRISK}_{i,t}}{\partial k} = D_{i,t} + \left(1 - \text{LRMES}_{i,t}\right) W_{i,t} = A_{i,t+h}^{\text{crisis}} > 0 \]
Proof

When \(\text{CS}_{i,t} > 0\), \(\text{SRISK}_{i,t} = k D_{i,t} - (1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}_{i,t}) W_{i,t}\). Differentiating directly:

  1. With respect to \(D_{i,t}\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial D} [k D - (1-k)(1-\text{LRMES})W] = k\). Each additional dollar of debt requires exactly \(k\) dollars of post-crisis equity buffer.
  2. With respect to \(W_{i,t}\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial W} [k D - (1-k)(1-\text{LRMES})W] = -(1-k)(1-\text{LRMES})\). Each additional dollar of pre-crisis equity delivers \((1 - \text{LRMES})\) dollars of surviving post-crisis equity, reducing required capital injection by \((1-k)(1-\text{LRMES})\).
  3. With respect to \(\text{LRMES}_{i,t}\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \text{LRMES}} [-(1-k)(1-\text{LRMES})W] = (1-k)W\). A marginal increase in tail crisis loss reduces surviving equity by \(W\), expanding the shortfall by \((1-k)W\).
  4. With respect to \(k\): \(\frac{\partial}{\partial k} [k D - (1-k)W^{\text{crisis}}] = D + W^{\text{crisis}} = A^{\text{crisis}}\), which equals post-crisis total assets.

This completes the proof.

Example 2.5 (Decomposing SRISK Drivers)

Consider Megabank Alpha with \(W = \$150\text{B}\), \(D = \$1{,}850\text{B}\), \(\text{LRMES} = 0.52\), and \(k = 0.08\):

\[ k D = 0.08 \times 1850 = \$148.00\text{B} \] \[ (1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) W = 0.92 \times (1 - 0.52) \times 150 = 0.92 \times 0.48 \times 150 = \$66.24\text{B} \] \[ \text{SRISK} = 148.00 - 66.24 = \$81.76\text{ billion} \]

If Megabank Alpha issues \(\$10\text{B}\) in new debt, its SRISK increases by \(\Delta\text{SRISK} = k \times \$10\text{B} = 0.08 \times \$10\text{B} = \$0.80\text{ billion}\), independent of firm size or LRMES.

"""Lab SR3-L03-1: SRISK and Systemic Capital Shortfall Index.

SIMULATED: calculates individual conditional capital shortfalls, non-negative SRISK,
aggregate systemic shortfall indices, and systemic risk percentage contributions
following Brownlees and Engle (2017).
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def compute_srisk(w: float, d: float, lrmes: float, k: float) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Calculate gross capital shortfall and non-negative SRISK."""
    cs = k * d - (1.0 - k) * (1.0 - lrmes) * w
    srisk = max(0.0, cs)
    return cs, srisk

    firms = [
        {"name": "Megabank Alpha", "W": 150.0, "D": 1850.0, "LRMES": 0.52},
        {"name": "Broker Beta", "W": 45.0, "D": 720.0, "LRMES": 0.60},
        {"name": "Regional Gamma", "W": 80.0, "D": 600.0, "LRMES": 0.38},
        {"name": "Insurer Delta", "W": 110.0, "D": 450.0, "LRMES": 0.25},
    ]

    k_prudential = 0.08  # 8% regulatory capital requirement

    print("=== SRISK Conditional Capital Shortfall Index (Brownlees & Engle 2017) ===")
    print(f"Prudential Capital Ratio (k): {k_prudential * 100:.1f}%")
    print("-" * 75)
    print(f"{'Institution':<18} {'Equity (W)':<12} {'Debt (D)':<12} {'LRMES':<10} {'CS ($B)':<10} {'SRISK ($B)':<10}")
    print("-" * 75)

    cs_list: list[float] = []
    srisk_list: list[float] = []

    for f in firms:
        w_val = float(str(f["W"]))
        d_val = float(str(f["D"]))

        name_val = str(f["name"])
        cs, srisk = compute_srisk(w_val, d_val, lrmes_val, k_prudential)
        cs_list.append(cs)

        print(f"{name_val:<18} ${w_val:<11.1f} ${d_val:<11.1f} {lrmes_val * 100:<9.1f}% ${cs:<9.2f} ${srisk:<9.2f}")

    total_srisk = sum(srisk_list)

    print(f"Total Aggregate SRISK (SRISK_sys): ${total_srisk:.2f}B")
    print("=" * 75)

        name_val = str(f["name"])

        print(f"{name_val:<18} {leverage:<24.2f} {srisk_pct:<24.2f}%")

    print("Comparative Statics Verification:")
    # Sensitivity of Megabank Alpha SRISK to +$10B debt: dSRISK/dD = k = 0.08
    _cs_d_shock, srisk_d_shock = compute_srisk(150.0, 1860.0, 0.52, k_prudential)
    delta_srisk = srisk_d_shock - srisk_list[0]
    print(f"Megabank Alpha +$10B Debt Shock: dSRISK = ${delta_srisk:.2f}B (Expected k*10 = $0.80B)")

Where the canon breaks

While SRISK is widely deployed by central banks and the NYU Stern V-Lab, it possesses notable structural and empirical limitations:

  1. Zero Cross-Subsidization Assumption: Truncating \(\text{SRISK}_i = \max(0, \text{CS}_i)\) assumes healthy banks with capital surpluses contribute zero to stabilizing distressed peers. While legally true under private ownership, during major crises healthy banks often acquire distressed peers under government facilitation (e.g., JPMorgan acquiring Bear Stearns and Washington Mutual), effectively deploying private capital surplus to absorb systemic losses.
  2. Static Debt Invariance: The assumption that book debt \(D\) remains unchanged during a 6-month crisis ignores debt roll-off, wholesale repo runs, and haircuts. When short-term repo lenders refuse to roll over credit, the bank's debt contracts rapidly through forced liquidations, altering both \(D\) and asset prices.
  3. Arbitrary Prudential Ratio \(k\): The index is highly sensitive to the choice of benchmark \(k\). Changing \(k\) from 8% to 5.5% (US vs European standards) can alter aggregate systemic SRISK by hundreds of billions of dollars, shifting the apparent systemic risk epicenter between jurisdictions.

Misconceptions

  • A commercial bank with a large capital surplus offsets the capital shortfalls of failing banks in aggregate SRISK.

    Aggregate SRISK is the sum of positive shortfalls: \(\sum_i \max(0, \text{CS}_i)\). An institution with \(\text{CS}_j = -\$40\text{B}\) (surplus) does not reduce the \(\$80\text{B}\) deficit of an insolvent peer. Private shareholders of a solvent bank cannot be forced to recapitalize a competitor. Hence, capital surpluses do not net out systemic shortfalls.

  • An institution with extreme balance-sheet leverage is automatically guaranteed to have a massive SRISK deficit.

    SRISK requires both high leverage and substantial tail risk (\(\text{LRMES}\)). A non-bank financial intermediary or specialized utility trust with high leverage but zero equity correlation with the market (\(\beta \approx 0 \implies \text{LRMES} \approx 0\)) retains its pre-crisis equity value during a market crash. If its baseline equity exceeds \(k D / (1 - k)\), its SRISK remains zero despite elevated leverage.

  • SRISK is purely a stock market metric that ignores the institution's balance sheet and liability structure.

    While \(\text{LRMES}\) is estimated from market returns, the SRISK formula is dominated by book liabilities \(k D\). For a mega-bank with \(\$2\text{ trillion}\) in assets and \(\$1.85\text{ trillion}\) in debt, book liabilities represent the primary driver of required dollar capital, making SRISK a true hybrid of market equity expectations and accounting debt.

Problems

Problem M.1: A commercial bank has equity market capitalization \(W = \$50\text{B}\), book debt liabilities \(D = \$650\text{B}\), estimated \(\text{LRMES} = 0.45\), and regulatory prudential capital ratio \(k = 0.08\). Calculate its gross capital shortfall \(\text{CS}\) and its non-negative \(\text{SRISK}\).

Worked solution

Applying the Brownlees & Engle formula:

\[ \text{CS} = k D - (1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) W \] \[ k D = 0.08 \times \$650\text{B} = \$52.00\text{B} \] \[ (1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) W = (1 - 0.08) \times (1 - 0.45) \times \$50\text{B} = 0.92 \times 0.55 \times 50 = \$25.30\text{B} \] \[ \text{CS} = 52.00 - 25.30 = \$26.70\text{ billion} \] \[ \text{SRISK} = \max(0, \; 26.70) = \$26.70\text{ billion} \]

The bank has an estimated conditional capital shortfall of \(\$26.70\text{ billion}\).

Problem M.2: A regional bank has equity \(W = \$120\text{B}\), book debt \(D = \$400\text{B}\), \(\text{LRMES} = 0.30\), and \(k = 0.08\). Compute \(\text{CS}\) and \(\text{SRISK}\).

Worked solution

Calculate required capital and post-crisis surviving equity:

\[ k D = 0.08 \times \$400\text{B} = \$32.00\text{B} \] \[ (1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) W = 0.92 \times 0.70 \times \$120\text{B} = \$77.28\text{B} \] \[ \text{CS} = 32.00 - 77.28 = -\$45.28\text{ billion} \] \[ \text{SRISK} = \max(0, \; -\$45.28\text{B}) = \$0.00\text{ billion} \]

The bank holds a capital surplus of \(\$45.28\text{B}\), so its SRISK is zero.

Problem M.3: In a three-firm economy, Bank 1 has \(\text{SRISK} = \$60\text{B}\), Bank 2 has \(\text{SRISK} = \$30\text{B}\), and Bank 3 has \(\text{SRISK} = \$10\text{B}\). Calculate aggregate systemic \(\text{SRISK}_{\text{sys}}\) and the percentage contribution of Bank 1.

Worked solution

Sum across positive SRISK values:

\[ \text{SRISK}_{\text{sys}} = \$60\text{B} + \$30\text{B} + \$10\text{B} = \$100.00\text{ billion} \]

Contribution share of Bank 1:

\[ \text{SRISK}\%_1 = \frac{\$60\text{B}}{\$100\text{B}} = 0.60 = 60.0\% \]

Bank 1 accounts for 60% of total financial sector systemic capital deficit.

Problem P.1: Prove that an institution is in capital shortfall (\(\text{CS} > 0\)) if and only if its post-crisis quasi-market leverage ratio exceeds the reciprocal of the prudential capital ratio: \(\text{Lev}^{\text{crisis}} > \frac{1}{k}\).

Worked solution

By definition, post-crisis equity is \(W^{\text{crisis}} = (1 - \text{LRMES})W\) and post-crisis total assets are \(A^{\text{crisis}} = D + W^{\text{crisis}}\). The post-crisis quasi-market leverage ratio is:

\[ \text{Lev}^{\text{crisis}} = \frac{A^{\text{crisis}}}{W^{\text{crisis}}} = \frac{D + W^{\text{crisis}}}{W^{\text{crisis}}} = 1 + \frac{D}{W^{\text{crisis}}} \]

The capital shortfall condition is \(\text{CS} > 0\):

\[ k A^{\text{crisis}} - W^{\text{crisis}} > 0 \iff k A^{\text{crisis}} > W^{\text{crisis}} \iff \frac{A^{\text{crisis}}}{W^{\text{crisis}}} > \frac{1}{k} \] \[ \iff \text{Lev}^{\text{crisis}} > \frac{1}{k} \]

For example, with \(k = 0.08\), \(\frac{1}{k} = 12.5\). The institution enters capital shortfall if and only if its post-crisis leverage ratio exceeds 12.5.

Problem P.2: Prove that for any institution with positive debt \(D > 0\), \(\text{SRISK}\) is a strictly convex function of \(\text{LRMES}\) over \([0, 1]\).

Worked solution

Let \(g(\text{LRMES}) = k D - (1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) W\). The function \(g\) is linear (affine) in \(\text{LRMES}\) with slope \((1 - k)W > 0\). The function \(\text{SRISK}(\text{LRMES}) = \max(0, g(\text{LRMES}))\) is the pointwise maximum of two convex (affine) functions: the constant function 0 and \(g(\text{LRMES})\). Since the maximum of convex functions is convex, \(\text{SRISK}\) is convex on \([0, 1]\).

Problem P.3: Prove that an equity injection of \(\Delta W > 0\) reduces \(\text{SRISK}\) by less than \(\Delta W\) whenever \(\text{LRMES} > 0\) and \(k < 1\).

Worked solution

From Theorem 2.4, the sensitivity of SRISK to equity is:

\[ \frac{\partial \text{SRISK}}{\partial W} = -(1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) \]

Since \(k \in (0, 1)\), \(1 - k < 1\). Since \(\text{LRMES} \in (0, 1)\), \(1 - \text{LRMES} < 1\). The product of two numbers strictly between 0 and 1 is strictly less than 1:

\[ (1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) < 1 \]

Therefore, \(\left|\frac{\partial \text{SRISK}}{\partial W}\right| < 1\). An injection of \(\$1.00\) in equity pre-crisis reduces crisis shortfall by only \((1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) < \$1.00\), because part of the new equity is eroded during the market crash (\(\text{LRMES}\)) and part is absorbed by the required prudential buffer on the newly created assets (\(k\)).

Problem E.1: Using the Lab 1 data, Megabank Alpha has \(\text{SRISK} = \$81.76\text{B}\) (65.32% share) and Broker Beta has \(\text{SRISK} = \$41.04\text{B}\) (32.79% share). Explain why Broker Beta's SRISK is nearly half of Megabank Alpha's despite Broker Beta having less than half the equity and debt of Megabank Alpha.

Worked solution

Broker Beta has significantly higher quasi-leverage (\(A/W = 17.00\) vs \(13.33\) for Megabank Alpha) and higher tail crisis risk (\(\text{LRMES} = 60.0\%\) vs \(52.0\%\)). Because Broker Beta's equity erodes by 60% during a crisis, its post-crisis equity buffer is reduced to just \(40\% \times \$45\text{B} = \$18\text{B}\) against \(\$720\text{B}\) in nominal debt, generating massive capital shortfall relative to its balance-sheet size.

Problem E.2: In the European banking system, why did European banks frequently rank higher in SRISK than US banks during 2011–2015 despite having lower reported regulatory risk-weighted asset (RWA) densities?

Worked solution

European banks made heavy use of internal ratings-based (IRB) risk models that assigned zero or negligible risk weights to European sovereign bonds and residential mortgages, inflating reported Tier 1 ratios. However, SRISK utilizes total unweighted balance-sheet liabilities \(D\) and market equity valuations \(W\). Because European banks maintained massive unweighted leverage (assets often exceeding 30 to 40 times market equity) and depressed market valuations during the sovereign debt crisis, SRISK captured their extreme unweighted fragility.

Problem E.3: During a financial panic, a dealer bank's market equity capitalization drops by 30% while its book liabilities remain constant at \(\$800\text{B}\). If its pre-crash equity was \(\$80\text{B}\), \(\text{LRMES} = 0.50\), and \(k = 0.08\), calculate the change in its SRISK.

Worked solution

Pre-crash: \(W = \$80\text{B}\).

\[ \text{SRISK}_0 = 0.08 \times 800 - 0.92 \times (1 - 0.50) \times 80 = 64.0 - 0.92 \times 0.50 \times 80 = 64.0 - 36.8 = \$27.20\text{B} \]

Post-crash: \(W_1 = 0.70 \times \$80\text{B} = \$56\text{B}\).

\[ \text{SRISK}_1 = 64.0 - 0.92 \times 0.50 \times 56 = 64.0 - 25.76 = \$38.24\text{B} \]

Change: \(\Delta\text{SRISK} = \$38.24\text{B} - \$27.20\text{B} = +\$11.04\text{ billion}\). The 30% drop in market equity expands required recapitalization by over \(\$11\text{ billion}\).

Problem R.1: Propose an optimal macroprudential capital surcharge rule (G-SIB surcharge) based dynamically on an institution's real-time SRISK contribution \(\text{SRISK}\%_{i,t}\).

Worked solution

Regulators can mandate a time-varying common equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital surcharge: \(\text{Surcharge}_{i,t} = \bar{\lambda} \cdot \left(\text{SRISK}_{i,t} / \text{Assets}_{i,t}\right)\) or \(\text{Surcharge}_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{SRISK}\%_{i,t}\). When an institution expands wholesale borrowing or experiences widening market tail risk, its SRISK share rises, automatically increasing its minimum equity requirement. This forces the bank to internalize the systemic externality, incentivizing deleveraging or equity retention during credit booms.

Problem R.2: Contrast the regulatory incentives created by the Basel III leverage ratio (unweighted Tier 1 over total exposure) against the Brownlees & Engle SRISK metric.

Worked solution

The Basel III leverage ratio treats all balance-sheet assets identically, regardless of correlation with aggregate macroeconomic shocks or risk profiles. In contrast, SRISK scales debt requirements by \((1 - \text{LRMES})W\), which directly incorporates forward-looking market estimates of systemic tail risk. A bank holding liquid Treasury securities with negative market beta will exhibit low LRMES and low SRISK, whereas a bank holding illiquid real estate loans with high systemic beta will face massive SRISK even if both have identical accounting leverage ratios.

Problem R.3: How does endogenous fire-sale liquidation invalidate the assumption that book liabilities \(D\) remain fixed during a multi-month systemic crisis?

Worked solution

When an institution experiences a capital shortfall, counterparties in repo and commercial paper markets impose margin calls or refuse roll-overs. The bank is forced to liquidate assets into an illiquid market, realizing fire-sale losses that depress both equity and asset values further (SR1 liquidity spirals). While debt may decrease as loans are repaid, equity is destroyed faster due to fire-sale price discounts, causing the true post-crisis capital shortfall to exceed the static SRISK formula.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What does the Brownlees and Engle (2017) \(\text{SRISK}\) metric measure?

    Options

    SRISK evaluates the dollar amount of equity capital a firm would need to raise during a crisis to restore its equity-to-asset ratio to regulatory requirement \(k\) (Brownlees & Engle 2017).

  2. Calculate \(\text{SRISK}\) for a firm with \(k = 0.08\), book debt \(D = \$900\text{B}\), equity capitalization \(W = \$100\text{B}\), and \(\text{LRMES} = 0.40\).

    \(k D = 0.08 \times 900 = 72.0\). \((1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) W = 0.92 \times 0.60 \times 100 = 55.2\). \(\text{SRISK} = \max(0, 72.0 - 55.2) = 16.8\).

  3. Why are institutions with negative capital shortfalls (\(\text{CS}_i < 0\)) assigned \(\text{SRISK}_i = 0\) instead of a negative number that reduces aggregate systemic risk?

    Options

    Private capital surpluses cannot be transferred to cover competitor deficits without bankruptcy or voluntary acquisition, so surpluses do not net out systemic deficits.

  4. What is the exact theoretical partial derivative of \(\text{SRISK}\) with respect to book debt liabilities \(D\) when the institution is in capital shortfall (\(\text{CS} > 0\)) under prudential ratio \(k = 0.08\)?

    \(\frac{\partial \text{SRISK}}{\partial D} = k = 0.08\) (Theorem 2.4).

  5. What happens to an institution's \(\text{SRISK}\) if its equity capitalization increases by \(\$10\text{B}\) while debt and LRMES remain unchanged (\(k = 0.08, \text{LRMES} = 0.50\))?

    Options

    \(\frac{\partial \text{SRISK}}{\partial W} = -(1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) = -0.92 \times 0.50 = -0.46\). A \(\$10\text{B}\) increase in equity reduces shortfall by \(\$4.6\text{B}\).

  6. If a commercial bank has debt \(D = \$300\text{B}\), equity \(W = \$100\text{B}\), \(k = 0.08\), and \(\text{LRMES} = 0.20\), calculate its \(\text{SRISK}\) in billions of dollars.

    \(k D = 0.08 \times 300 = 24.0\). \((1 - k)(1 - \text{LRMES}) W = 0.92 \times 0.80 \times 100 = 73.6\). \(\text{CS} = 24.0 - 73.6 = -49.6\). \(\text{SRISK} = \max(0, -49.6) = 0.00\).

  7. How does the NYU Stern V-Lab utilize SRISK to monitor systemic risk across international jurisdictions?

    Options

    V-Lab uses daily econometric models (T-GARCH and DCC) on global equity returns coupled with quarterly accounting debt to publish real-time SRISK rankings.

  8. Under what condition can an institution with quasi-market leverage of 15.0 have an SRISK of zero under \(k = 0.08\)?

    Options

    Since pre-crisis leverage is 15.0, post-crisis leverage \(\text{Lev}^{\text{crisis}} \ge 15.0\). Since \(1/k = 1/0.08 = 12.5 < 15.0\), post-crisis leverage is guaranteed to exceed 12.5, yielding \(\text{CS} > 0\) (Problem P.1).
