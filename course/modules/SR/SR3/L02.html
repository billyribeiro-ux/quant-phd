When an individual bank fails in isolation, private bankruptcy resolution absorbs its loss. But when a bank becomes undercapitalized during an aggregate systemic crisis, it liquidates assets into illiquid markets, freezes interbank credit, and imposes severe externalities on the real economy. Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon, and Richardson (2017) formalize this externality through Marginal Expected Shortfall (MES): an institution's expected equity loss conditional on the aggregate financial system crashing. MES bridges welfare economics with quantitative tail risk, providing the mathematical foundation for systemic capital surcharges and multi-month crisis projections.

1Welfare Foundations and Systemic Expected Shortfall

Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon, and Richardson (2017) develop a formal equilibrium model where financial institutions fund risky investments using equity \(w_i\) and guaranteed debt \(d_i\). When aggregate intermediary capital falls below a critical prudential threshold, the financial sector cannot intermediate credit, imposing a real output externality of \(\Phi(z)\) where \(z\) is the aggregate capital shortfall .

Definition 2.1 (Systemic Expected Shortfall and Systemic Tax)

Let \(w_i\) be the current equity of bank \(i\), \(R_i\) its gross return, and \(w_i R_i\) its end-of-period capital. Let \(a_i\) be its prudential capital requirement. The institution experiences a capital shortfall whenever \(w_i R_i < a_i\). The aggregate capital shortfall of the financial system is:

\[ S_{\text{sys}} = \sum_{i=1}^N \max(0, \; a_i - w_i R_i) \]

A systemic crisis occurs when aggregate capital shortfall exceeds systemic capacity \(\bar{S}\), denoted by the event \(\mathbb{S} = \{S_{\text{sys}} \ge \bar{S}\}\). The Marginal expected shortfall (MES) of bank \(i\) is the conditional expectation of its equity return (or loss) given a systemic crisis :

\[ \text{MES}_{\alpha}^i = -\E\left[ R_i \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] \]

Under a Pigouvian systemic tax regime, each bank is assessed a regulatory levy equal to its expected contribution to the aggregate crisis externality :

\[ \tau_i = \E\left[ \frac{\partial \Phi(S_{\text{sys}})}{\partial (w_i R_i)} \;\middle|\; \mathbb{S} \right] = \bar{\phi} \cdot \Prob(\mathbb{S}) \cdot \left[ a_i - w_i \left(1 - \text{MES}^i\right) \right] \]
Remark 2.2 (Sign Conventions in MES Literature)

In Acharya et al. (2017), returns \(R_i\) are defined such that a loss is a negative percentage (e.g., \(-5.5\%\)). However, risk metrics are customarily quoted as positive losses. In this text, we define \(\text{MES}\) as a positive number representing expected percentage loss:

\[ \text{MES}_\alpha^i = -\E[R_i \mid R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}] \]

Thus, if the bank's stock drops by an average of \(5.52\%\) when the market drops into its worst 5% tail, \(\text{MES}_{0.05}^i = +0.0552\) (or \(5.52\%\)).

2Non-Parametric MES Estimation

Unlike quantile regression CoVaR which conditions system returns on the institution's distress, MES conditions the institution's return on aggregate market distress. This makes empirical estimation straightforward via non-parametric tail conditioning over historical trading days.

Theorem 2.3 (Consistency of Non-Parametric Sample MES)

Let \(\{(R_{i,t}, R_{\text{sys},t})\}_{t=1}^T\) be a stationary ergodic return process with continuous joint distribution \(F(r_i, r_{\text{sys}})\). Let \(\widehat{\text{VaR}}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\) be the empirical system quantile, and let the crisis index set be:

\[ \mathcal{T}_\alpha = \left\{t \le T : R_{\text{sys},t} \le \widehat{\text{VaR}}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\right\} \]

with cardinality \(|\mathcal{T}_\alpha| = \lfloor T \alpha \rfloor\). The non-parametric sample estimator:

\[ \widehat{\text{MES}}_\alpha^i = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_\alpha|} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_\alpha} R_{i,t} \]

converges almost surely to \(\text{MES}_\alpha^i\) as \(T \to \infty\).

Proof

By the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem and the continuity of the marginal distribution \(F_{\text{sys}}\), the sample quantile \(\widehat{\text{VaR}}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} = F_{\text{sys}, T}^{-1}(\alpha)\) converges almost surely to the true population quantile \(\text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} = F_{\text{sys}}^{-1}(\alpha)\). The indicator process \(\indic_{\{R_{\text{sys},t} \le \widehat{\text{VaR}}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\}}\) converges point-wise almost surely to \(\indic_{\{R_{\text{sys},t} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\}}\). By the ergodic theorem for stationary processes with finite first moments:

\[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T R_{i,t} \indic_{\{R_{\text{sys},t} \le \widehat{\text{VaR}}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\}} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \E\left[ R_i \indic_{\{R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\}} \right] \]

Similarly, the sample size of crisis days satisfies:

\[ \frac{|\mathcal{T}_\alpha|}{T} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \indic_{\{R_{\text{sys},t} \le \widehat{\text{VaR}}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\}} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \Prob(R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}) = \alpha \]

Taking the ratio:

\[ \widehat{\text{MES}}_\alpha^i = -\frac{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T R_{i,t}\indic_{\{R_{\text{sys},t} \le \widehat{\text{VaR}}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\}}}{\frac{1}{T}|\mathcal{T}_\alpha|} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} -\frac{\E\left[R_i \indic_{\{R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\}}\right]}{\Prob(R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}})} = -\E\left[R_i \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\right] \]

which completes the proof.

3Tail Dependence and the Beta Approximation

How does MES relate to standard CAPM market beta \(\beta_i = \Cov(R_i, R_{\text{sys}}) / \Var(R_{\text{sys}})\)? Acharya et al. (2017) demonstrate that under elliptical distributions (such as bivariate Gaussian), MES is strictly proportional to market beta multiplied by the system's Expected Shortfall .

Theorem 2.4 (MES Under Bivariate Normality)

Suppose \((R_i, R_{\text{sys}})\) follow a joint bivariate normal distribution with means \(\mu_i, \mu_{\text{sys}}\), variances \(\sigma_i^2, \sigma_{\text{sys}}^2\), and correlation \(\rho\). Then the Marginal Expected Shortfall satisfies:

\[ \text{MES}_\alpha^i = -\mu_i + \beta_i \left( \text{ES}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} - \mu_{\text{sys}} \right) \]

where \(\beta_i = \rho \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_{\text{sys}}}\) and:

\[ \text{ES}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} = -\E[R_{\text{sys}} \mid R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}] = -\mu_{\text{sys}} + \sigma_{\text{sys}} \frac{\phi(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha))}{\alpha} \]
Proof

Under bivariate normality, the linear regression decomposition holds exactly without conditional heteroskedasticity:

\[ R_i = \mu_i + \beta_i (R_{\text{sys}} - \mu_{\text{sys}}) + \varepsilon_i \]

where \(\E[\varepsilon_i \mid R_{\text{sys}}] = 0\). Taking the conditional expectation given the market crisis event \(\{R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\}\):

\[ \E\left[ R_i \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] = \mu_i + \beta_i \left( \E\left[ R_{\text{sys}} \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] - \mu_{\text{sys}} \right) + \E\left[ \varepsilon_i \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] \]

Because \(\varepsilon_i\) is independent of \(R_{\text{sys}}\), the residual conditional expectation vanishes. Recognizing that:

\[ \E\left[ R_{\text{sys}} \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] = -\text{ES}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \]

evaluating the conditional expectation yields:

\[ \E\left[ R_i \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] = \mu_i + \beta_i \left( -\text{ES}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} - \mu_{\text{sys}} \right) \]

Multiplying by \(-1\) yields:

\[ \text{MES}_\alpha^i = -\mu_i + \beta_i (\text{ES}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} + \mu_{\text{sys}}) \]

which confirms the theorem.

Remark 2.5 (Non-Linearity and Extreme Tail Dependence)

While the linear beta relation holds under normality, empirical asset returns display strong asymmetric tail dependence. In stress periods, financial institutions experience co-crashes far in excess of Gaussian predictions, causing empirical \(\widehat{\text{MES}}\) for highly levered institutions to systematically exceed \(\beta_i \text{ES}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\) .

4Long-Run MES (LRMES) Multi-Month Crisis Projection

Daily MES measures what happens on a single day when the market experiences a 5% tail shock. However, financial crises are extended drawdowns that unfold over months (e.g., September 2008 to March 2009). Regulators requiring capital surcharges must estimate the institution's expected equity drop over a cumulative systemic crisis.

Definition 2.6 (Long-Run Marginal Expected Shortfall)

Let \(d \in (0, 1)\) denote the cumulative market crash over a 6-month crisis horizon (standard benchmark \(d = 0.40\) or 40% aggregate market decline). The Long-Run Marginal Expected Shortfall (LRMES) is the projected percentage decline in the institution's equity value over the 6-month crisis period :

\[ \text{LRMES}_i = 1 - \exp\left( \beta_i \ln(1 - d) \right) = 1 - (1 - d)^{\beta_i} \]

Alternatively, Brownlees and Engle (2017) and Acharya et al. (2017) establish the daily non-parametric rule-of-thumb approximation:

\[ \text{LRMES}_i \approx 1 - \exp\left( -18 \times \text{MES}_\alpha^i \right) \]

where the coefficient 18 compounds daily 5% crisis losses over approximately 60 trading days of systemic market turmoil.

Example 2.7 (Dealer Bank vs Commercial Bank LRMES)

Consider two institutions during a 40% market collapse (\(d = 0.40\)):

  • Dealer Bank A: Market beta \(\beta_A = 1.568\). Using the power formulation: \[ \text{LRMES}_A = 1 - (1 - 0.40)^{1.568} = 1 - (0.60)^{1.568} = 1 - 0.4489 = 0.5511 \quad (55.11\%) \]
  • Commercial Bank B: Market beta \(\beta_B = 0.989\). Using the power formulation: \[ \text{LRMES}_B = 1 - (1 - 0.40)^{0.989} = 1 - (0.60)^{0.989} = 1 - 0.6033 = 0.3967 \quad (39.67\%) \]

The high-beta dealer bank loses over 55% of its equity capital during the 6-month crisis, drastically eroding its solvency buffer.

Lab 1 · Marginal Expected Shortfall and LRMES Estimation

Estimating non-parametric MES across simulated heavy-tailed institutional returns reveals monotonic scaling with market beta, and projecting multi-month LRMES demonstrates that high-beta dealer equity drops by more than 55% during a 40% market crisis.

"""Lab SR3-L02-1: Marginal Expected Shortfall (MES) and Long-Run MES (LRMES).

SIMULATED: generates heavy-tailed market returns and cross-sectional institutional
returns to compute non-parametric MES and multi-month projected LRMES following
Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon, and Richardson (2017).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(202)
    t_obs = 600  # 600 trading days (~2.4 years)
    alpha = 0.05  # 5% tail crisis threshold
    crisis_drop = 0.40  # 40% aggregate market crash horizon

    # Leptokurtic market returns R_m via Student-t(4) innovations
    scale = np.sqrt(rng.chisquare(df=4, size=t_obs) / 4.0)
    market_noise = rng.normal(0.0, 0.012, size=t_obs) / np.maximum(scale, 1e-4)
    r_market = 0.0003 + market_noise

    # Three financial institution return series:
    # Firm A: High Beta Dealer (beta ~ 1.6)
    # Firm B: Unit Beta Commercial Bank (beta ~ 1.0)
    # Firm C: Low Beta Insurer (beta ~ 0.5)
    betas = [1.60, 1.00, 0.50]
    names = ["Dealer Bank A", "Commercial Bank B", "Insurer C"]
    idio_vols = [0.015, 0.010, 0.008]

    r_firms: list[np.ndarray] = []
    for b, iv in zip(betas, idio_vols, strict=True):
        idio = rng.normal(0.0, iv, size=t_obs)
        r_firms.append(b * r_market + idio)

    # 1. Empirical Market VaR at alpha = 0.05
    var_market = float(np.quantile(r_market, alpha))
    crisis_days = r_market <= var_market
    n_crisis = int(np.sum(crisis_days))

    print("=== Marginal Expected Shortfall (Acharya et al. 2017) ===")
    print(f"Sample periods (T): {t_obs}")
    print(f"Market VaR(0.05):   {var_market:.4f} (-{abs(var_market) * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"Crisis observations: {n_crisis} days ({n_crisis / t_obs * 100:.1f}%)")
    print("-" * 65)
    print(f"{'Institution':<20} {'Sample Beta':<12} {'MES (5%)':<12} {'LRMES (40%)':<12}")
    print("-" * 65)

    var_m_sample = float(np.var(r_market, ddof=1))

    for name, r_i in zip(names, r_firms, strict=True):
        # Sample beta
        cov_im = float(np.cov(r_i, r_market, ddof=1)[0, 1])
        b_est = cov_im / var_m_sample

        # Non-parametric MES: average loss on market crisis days
        mes_val = float(-np.mean(r_i[crisis_days]))

        # Dynamic LRMES: 1 - (1 - d)^beta
        lrmes_val = float(1.0 - (1.0 - crisis_drop) ** b_est)

        print(f"{name:<20} {b_est:<12.3f} {mes_val * 100:<11.2f}% {lrmes_val * 100:<11.2f}%")

    print("-" * 65)
    print("Interpretation: High-beta dealer equity suffers over 55% loss in a 40% crisis,")
    print("generating severe capital shortfall externalities under systemic market strain.")


main()

Where the canon breaks

While MES provides a rigorous welfare foundation for systemic risk taxation, several key empirical and econometric vulnerabilities emerge in practice:

  1. Look-Back Inefficiency: Unconditional sample MES relies on historical market crashes. During prolonged tranquil bull markets, historical sample quantiles reflect low volatility, causing estimated MES to remain artificially low right up to the onset of a crisis.
  2. Beta Assumption Breakdown: The parametric LRMES formula \(\text{LRMES} = 1 - (1 - d)^\beta\) presumes a constant equity beta across the entire distribution. In real market crashes, liquidity dry-ups and margin spirals cause betas to jump discontinuously, meaning historical OLS beta underestimates extreme tail drawdowns.
  3. Equity-Only Focus: MES measures equity price drops but does not directly incorporate the institution's balance-sheet debt, short-term wholesale funding reliance, or nominal size. A boutique hedge fund may have an MES of 70% while posing zero threat to the banking system, whereas a trillion-dollar repo dealer with an MES of 30% can crash global markets.

Misconceptions

  • Marginal Expected Shortfall measures the risk of a financial institution becoming insolvent on its own.

    MES specifically conditions on aggregate system distress: \(\E[R_i \mid R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha]\). An institution could have severe idiosyncratic operational losses (e.g., Rogue trader loss) that bankrupt the firm alone; such losses appear in its standalone Expected Shortfall but do not contribute to MES unless they coincide with a systemic market collapse.

  • Holding a diversified cross-section of financial institutions eliminates aggregate systemic risk.

    While diversification eliminates idiosyncratic firm risk under independent shocks, systemic risk arises from common macro factors, fire-sale externalities, and funding interconnectedness. If all institutions share exposure to an aggregate shock (e.g., Subprime MBS or sovereign bond crashes), their equity tail losses occur simultaneously, preventing diversification from mitigating aggregate MES.

  • MES and CoVaR measure the exact same quantity because both condition on a 5% tail event.

    MES conditions the institution on the market (\(R_i \mid R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}}\)), measuring systemic vulnerability. \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) conditions the market on the institution (\(R_{\text{sys}} \mid R_i = \text{VaR}_\alpha^i\)), measuring outward systemic contribution. Because conditional distributions are not symmetric, an institution with high MES may have modest \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\), and vice versa.

Problems

Problem M.1: In a sample of \(T = 1{,}000\) trading days, the 5% worst market return days number exactly \(N_{\text{crisis}} = 50\). On these 50 days, Bank X experienced a total cumulative return of \(-2.85\) (sum of simple daily returns). Calculate Bank X's non-parametric sample \(\widehat{\text{MES}}_{0.05}^X\) expressed as a positive percentage loss.

Worked solution

Applying the sample average formula:

\[ \widehat{\text{MES}}_{0.05}^X = -\frac{1}{N_{\text{crisis}}} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_{0.05}} R_{X,t} = -\frac{-2.85}{50} = +0.0570 = 5.70\% \]

Bank X loses an average of \(5.70\%\) per day when the broader market is in its 5% tail.

Problem M.2: An investment bank has an estimated equity beta of \(\beta = 1.40\). Under a 6-month crisis scenario with aggregate market decline \(d = 0.40\), compute its Long-Run Marginal Expected Shortfall \(\text{LRMES} = 1 - (1 - d)^\beta\).

Worked solution

Evaluate the power expression:

\[ \text{LRMES} = 1 - (1 - 0.40)^{1.40} = 1 - (0.60)^{1.40} \] \[ \ln(0.60) \approx -0.5108256 \implies 1.40 \times (-0.5108256) \approx -0.7151558 \] \[ (0.60)^{1.40} = \exp(-0.7151558) \approx 0.489115 \] \[ \text{LRMES} = 1 - 0.489115 = 0.510885 \approx 51.09\% \]

The bank is projected to lose \(51.09\%\) of its equity value over the 6-month crisis.

Problem M.3: Using the daily approximation rule:

\[ \text{LRMES} \approx 1 - \exp(-18 \times \text{MES}) \]

calculate the projected 6-month equity drop for a commercial bank whose daily \(\text{MES}_{0.05} = 0.035\) (3.5%).

Worked solution

Evaluate the exponential approximation:

\[ \text{LRMES} \approx 1 - \exp(-18 \times 0.035) = 1 - \exp(-0.630) \] \[ \exp(-0.630) \approx 0.53259 \] \[ \text{LRMES} \approx 1 - 0.53259 = 0.46741 \approx 46.74\% \]

The daily 3.5% crisis loss compounds into an estimated 46.74% cumulative equity erosion.

Problem P.1: Prove that under the CAPM relation \(R_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_m + \varepsilon_i\) with zero-mean idiosyncratic risk \(\E[\varepsilon_i \mid R_m] = 0\), the Marginal Expected Shortfall is linear in market Expected Shortfall:

\[ \text{MES}_q^i = -\alpha_i + \beta_i \text{ES}_q^m \]
Worked solution

By definition of MES:

\[ \text{MES}_q^i = -\E\left[ R_i \;\middle|\; R_m \le \text{VaR}_q^m \right] \]

Substitute the linear factor model decomposition:

\[ \text{MES}_q^i = -\E\left[ \alpha_i + \beta_i R_m + \varepsilon_i \;\middle|\; R_m \le \text{VaR}_q^m \right] \]

Using linearity of conditional expectation:

\[ \text{MES}_q^i = -\alpha_i - \beta_i \E\left[ R_m \;\middle|\; R_m \le \text{VaR}_q^m \right] - \E\left[ \varepsilon_i \;\middle|\; R_m \le \text{VaR}_q^m \right] \]

Since \(\E[\varepsilon_i \mid R_m] = 0\), the law of iterated expectations implies:

\[ \E\left[ \varepsilon_i \;\middle|\; R_m \le \text{VaR}_q^m \right] = 0 \]

Recognizing the market expected shortfall \(\text{ES}_q^m = -\E[R_m \mid R_m \le \text{VaR}_q^m]\):

\[ \text{MES}_q^i = -\alpha_i + \beta_i \text{ES}_q^m \]

which establishes exact linearity.

Problem P.2: Prove that an equal-weighted portfolio of \(N\) institutions \(R_p = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N R_i\) satisfies the aggregation property: \(\text{MES}_\alpha^p = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \text{MES}_\alpha^i\).

Worked solution

Evaluating portfolio MES:

\[ \text{MES}_\alpha^p = -\E\left[ R_p \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] = -\E\left[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N R_i \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] \]

By linearity of the conditional expectation operator:

\[ \text{MES}_\alpha^p = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \E\left[ R_i \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left( -\E\left[ R_i \;\middle|\; R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{sys}} \right] \right) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \text{MES}_\alpha^i \]

Unlike Value-at-Risk, Marginal Expected Shortfall aggregates linearly across institutions, meaning diversification across the banking sector does not reduce aggregate systemic risk exposure when systemic conditioning is identical.

Problem P.3: Prove that the multi-period crisis formula:

\[ \text{LRMES} = 1 - (1 - d)^\beta \]

is the continuous-compounding limit of log returns under the systematic relation:

\[ \ln(1 + R_i) = \beta \ln(1 + R_s) \]
Worked solution

Let \(r_i = \ln(1 + R_i)\) and \(r_s = \ln(1 + R_s)\) denote cumulative log returns over the crisis horizon. Under the linear log-return model \(r_i = \beta r_s\). In a crisis where the market declines by fraction \(d\), the market gross return is \(1 + R_s = 1 - d\), so:

\[ r_s = \ln(1 - d) \]

The bank's cumulative log return is then:

\[ r_i = \beta \ln(1 - d) = \ln\left( (1 - d)^\beta \right) \]

Converting log return back to cumulative gross return \(1 + R_i\):

\[ 1 + R_i = \exp(r_i) = \exp\left( \ln\left((1 - d)^\beta\right) \right) = (1 - d)^\beta \]

The institution's percentage equity loss (LRMES) is defined as \(-\text{Return} = 1 - (1 + R_i)\):

\[ \text{LRMES} = 1 - (1 - d)^\beta \]

which completes the proof.

Problem E.1: In Lab 1, Dealer Bank A has an estimated beta of \(1.568\) and an MES of \(5.52\%\), while Commercial Bank B has beta \(0.989\) and MES \(3.51\%\). Compare their ratio of MES against their ratio of betas and explain the empirical consistency.

Worked solution

Ratio of betas: \(\frac{1.568}{0.989} \approx 1.585\).

Ratio of MES values: \(\frac{5.52\%}{3.51\%} \approx 1.573\).

The ratios match to within \(1\%\). Under symmetric tail innovation distributions where market expected shortfall drives conditional expectations, MES scales nearly in direct proportion to equity beta, confirming the theoretical prediction of Theorem 2.4.

Problem E.2: In the 2007–2009 financial crisis, Acharya et al. (2017) show that pre-crisis MES measured during June 2006 – June 2007 predicted bank equity losses during July 2007 – December 2008 far better than regulatory Tier 1 capital ratios. Why did Tier 1 capital fail to predict crisis performance?

Worked solution

Regulatory Tier 1 ratios are backward-looking accounting ratios based on regulatory risk-weighted assets (RWA). Banks optimized their portfolios by holding assets with low regulatory risk weights (such as AAA-rated private-label CDO tranches carrying zero or near-zero risk weights) while offloading assets through off-balance-sheet structured investment vehicles (SIVs). MES, being derived from daily market equity prices, captured forward-looking investor perceptions of hidden tail risk, liquidity exposure, and wholesale repo funding reliance that static accounting ratios missed entirely.

Problem E.3: Suppose a life insurer has market equity of \(\$40\text{B}\) and \(\text{MES}_{0.05} = 2.23\%\), while a commercial bank has market equity of \(\$40\text{B}\) and \(\text{MES}_{0.05} = 5.52\%\). If a single-day 5% market crash occurs, calculate the expected dollar equity loss for each institution.

Worked solution

For the life insurer: \(\text{Loss} = \$0.89\text{B}\) (\(\$892\text{M}\), from \(\$40\text{B} \times 0.0223\)).

For the commercial bank: \(\text{Loss} = \$2.21\text{B}\) (from \(\$40\text{B} \times 0.0552\)).

The commercial bank's equity erodes by \(\$2.21\text{B}\), nearly 2.5 times more than the insurer, reflecting higher leverage and tail sensitivity to aggregate asset market liquidations.

Problem R.1: How can regulators design a dynamic systemic risk insurance fund (or mandatory contingent capital conversion) where insurance premiums are tied directly to an institution's MES?

Worked solution

Under the Acharya et al. (2017) Pigouvian tax model, each bank pays an annual systemic insurance premium \(\Pi_i = \tau \cdot \text{LRMES}_i \cdot \text{Assets}_i\). Alternatively, the regulator mandates contingent convertible bonds (CoCos) that automatically convert from debt to common equity whenever market-wide systemic distress triggers and the bank's equity drops by its predicted LRMES threshold. This internalizes the fire-sale externality: banks with higher systematic tail risk face higher funding costs unless they build greater equity buffers.

Problem R.2: Why does conditioning on market distress in MES (\(R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha\)) make MES less sensitive to reverse causality compared to conditioning on single-firm distress in \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\)?

Worked solution

The aggregate market index \(R_{\text{sys}}\) aggregates thousands of securities across the entire financial economy. Except for the very largest mega-institutions, an individual bank's idiosyncratic failure has negligible direct mechanical weight in the aggregate market return. Thus, conditioning on a market crash identifies the bank's exposure to aggregate distress rather than reflecting a single bank driving the market. In contrast, in \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\), conditioning on firm distress can reflect omitted macro variables simultaneously depressing both the bank and the sector.

Problem R.3: How does the Brownlees and Engle (2017) Dynamic MES (DCC-GARCH) framework improve upon the static sample MES of Acharya et al. (2017)?

Worked solution

Static sample MES averages historical returns unconditionally across a trailing window, resulting in slow reaction to regime changes. Dynamic MES decomposes returns into time-varying volatility via T-GARCH and time-varying correlation via Dynamic Conditional Correlation (DCC):

\[ \text{MES}_{i,t} = \sigma_{i,t} \rho_{i,t} \E\left[z_{s,t} \mid z_{s,t} \le c\right] + \sigma_{i,t} \sqrt{1 - \rho_{i,t}^2} \E\left[z_{i,t} \mid z_{s,t} \le c\right] \]

This allows daily updates reflecting current implied volatility, asymmetric volatility response (leverage effect), and time-varying tail correlation before a crisis breaks out.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What does Marginal Expected Shortfall (\(\text{MES}_\alpha^i\)) measure for financial institution \(i\)?

    Options

    MES is defined as the expected equity loss conditional on systemic market distress: \(-\E[R_i \mid R_{\text{sys}} \le \text{VaR}_\alpha]\) (Acharya et al. 2017).

  2. In the welfare foundation of Acharya et al. (2017), why does a bank's capital shortfall impose a negative externality only during systemic crises?

    Options

    In tranquil periods, an isolated bank's failure is absorbed by healthy competitors. In a systemic crisis, all intermediaries face capital constraints, leading to fire sales, credit freezing, and output contraction.

  3. If the market experienced 25 crisis days in a historical window, and Bank Z's daily returns on those days summed to \(-1.20\) (or \(-120\%\)), calculate Bank Z's non-parametric sample \(\text{MES}\) expressed as a positive percentage.

    \(\text{MES} = -(-1.20) / 25 = 1.20 / 25 = 0.048 = 4.80\%\).

  4. Under bivariate normality with zero mean returns, what is the exact mathematical relationship between institution MES and market Expected Shortfall (\(\text{ES}_{\text{sys}}\))?

    Options

    Under bivariate normality, linear regression holds without heteroskedasticity, yielding \(\text{MES}^i = \beta_i \text{ES}_{\text{sys}}\) (Theorem 2.4).

  5. Compute the Long-Run MES percentage for an institution with beta \(\beta = 1.40\) under a 6-month crisis of \(d = 40\%\).

    \(\text{LRMES} = 1 - (1 - 0.40)^{1.40} = 1 - (0.60)^{1.40} \approx 1 - 0.4891 = 0.5109 = 51.09\%\).

  6. Why does portfolio diversification fail to reduce aggregate systemic risk when evaluated through Marginal Expected Shortfall?

    Options

    By linearity of expectations, the MES of a portfolio is the weighted average of individual firm MES values. Common factor exposure cannot be diversified away.

  7. Using the daily approximation \(\text{LRMES} \approx 1 - \exp(-18 \times \text{MES})\), what is the estimated cumulative LRMES percentage for a bank with daily \(\text{MES} = 0.0305\) (3.05%)?

    \(18 \times 0.0305 = 0.549\). \(\exp(-0.549) \approx 0.5775\). \(\text{LRMES} \approx 1 - 0.5775 = 0.4225 \approx 42.3\%\).

  8. What is a major conceptual difference between an institution's Marginal Expected Shortfall and its \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\)?

    Options

    MES measures systemic vulnerability (exposure to an aggregate crash), while \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) measures systemic contribution (spillover to the system when the bank fails).

  9. Why did empirical equity MES prior to 2007 predict 2007–2009 financial crisis losses more accurately than regulatory Tier 1 capital ratios?

    Options

    Regulatory risk weights allowed banks to hold massive off-balance-sheet and AAA subprime exposures with zero capital charges, whereas market prices priced in systemic tail vulnerability (Acharya et al. 2017).
