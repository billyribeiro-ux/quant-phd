Modern financial intermediaries do not operate as isolated silos; they are joined by trillions of dollars of bilateral repo obligations, derivatives contracts, and unsecured interbank loans. When an institution experiences asset insolvency, its failure cascades through these contractual linkages to downstream counterparties. Eisenberg and Noe (2001) establish the foundational lattice-theoretic clearing architecture that computes simultaneous default payments. Acemoglu, Ozdaglar, and Tahbaz-Salehi (2015) prove a profound phase-transition theorem: dense interbank connectivity enhances financial stability under moderate shocks by sharing losses, but shifts discontinuously into a contagion propagation engine under catastrophic shocks.

1Interbank Networks and Bilateral Liabilities

Consider a financial system comprising \(N\) financial institutions indexed by \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). Interbank interconnectedness is characterized by nominal debt contracts between institutions.

Definition 1.1 (Interbank Network and Relative Liabilities)

Let \(L_{ij} \ge 0\) denote the nominal liability that institution \(i\) owes to institution \(j\), with \(L_{ii} = 0\) (no self-obligations). The total nominal interbank obligation of institution \(i\) is:

\[ \bar{p}_i = \sum_{j=1}^N L_{ij} \]

The relative liability matrix \(\Pi \in \R^{N \times N}\) is defined by row-normalizing positive liabilities :

\[ \Pi_{ij} = \begin{cases} \frac{L_{ij}}{\bar{p}_i} & \text{if } \bar{p}_i > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } \bar{p}_i = 0 \end{cases} \]

By construction, \(\sum_{j=1}^N \Pi_{ij} = 1\) for all banks with positive obligations, making \(\Pi\) a substochastic transition matrix. Here, \(\Pi_{ij}\) represents the fraction of institution \(i\)'s total available payments that must be paid to creditor \(j\).

In addition to interbank obligations, each institution holds an external asset portfolio \(z_i \ge 0\) (e.g., loans to non-financial firms, mortgage-backed securities, Treasury holdings) and senior outside liabilities \(b_i \ge 0\) (e.g., retail demand deposits or taxes that take legal precedence over interbank claims) . The net outside equity buffer of institution \(i\) prior to interbank claims is \(z_i - b_i\).

2Eisenberg–Noe Clearing Payments and the Picard Contraction

When an institution's incoming cash flows are insufficient to pay its nominal obligations \(\bar{p}_i\) in full, it defaults and enters resolution. Bankruptcy law enforces two universal principles: limited liability (an equity holder cannot be forced to pay more than the firm's total available assets) and priority of debt (absolute priority) with pari passu equal treatment of all interbank claimants.

Definition 1.2 (Clearing Payment Vector and Contagion)

Let \(p = (p_1, \dots, p_N)^\top \in [0, \bar{p}]\) denote the vector of actual interbank payments made by all institutions, where \(0 \le p_i \le \bar{p}_i\). Total incoming payments received by institution \(i\) from all debtor institutions \(j\) equal:

\[ \text{Inflows}_i = \sum_{j=1}^N \Pi_{ji} p_j = (\Pi^\top p)_i \]

Under limited liability and absolute priority, institution \(i\) pays its full obligation \(\bar{p}_i\) if its total available resources \(z_i + (\Pi^\top p)_i - b_i \ge \bar{p}_i\). If total resources are strictly less than \(\bar{p}_i\), the institution defaults and distributes all remaining net assets to its interbank claimants, subject to non-negativity. A clearing payment vector \(p^*\) is a fixed point of the Eisenberg–Noe operator :

\[ p_i^* = \min\left(\bar{p}_i, \; \max\left(0, \; z_i + \sum_{j=1}^N \Pi_{ji} p_j^* - b_i\right)\right) \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\} \]

Network contagion is the process by which an initial shortfall or contractual default at institution \(i\) reduces payments \(p_i^* < \bar{p}_i\), propagating credit losses through \(\Pi_{ij}\) that trigger secondary default cascades across counterparties.

Theorem 1.3 (Existence, Uniqueness, and Monotone Picard Convergence)

Let the Eisenberg–Noe operator \(\Phi: [0, \bar{p}] \to [0, \bar{p}]\) be defined component-wise by \(\Phi_i(p) = \min(\bar{p}_i, \max(0, z_i + (\Pi^\top p)_i - b_i))\). Then:

  1. The operator \(\Phi\) is monotone increasing on the complete lattice \([0, \bar{p}]\): if \(p \le q\) component-wise, then \(\Phi(p) \le \Phi(q)\).
  2. By Tarski's fixed-point theorem, the set of fixed points is a non-empty complete lattice with a greatest clearing vector \(p_{\max}^*\) and a least clearing vector \(p_{\min}^*\). Under standard regularity (every node with surplus can route to outside sinks or regular surplus networks), the clearing vector is unique: \(p^* = p_{\max}^* = p_{\min}^*\).
  3. The Picard iteration sequence \(p^{(0)} = \bar{p}\), \(p^{(k+1)} = \Phi(p^{(k)})\) converges monotonically downward to \(p^*\) in at most \(N\) structural default rounds.
Proof

1. Monotonicity: Let \(p, q \in [0, \bar{p}]\) with \(p \le q\) component-wise. Since \(\Pi_{ji} \ge 0\), it follows that \((\Pi^\top p)_i \le (\Pi^\top q)_i\) for each \(i\). Adding constant \(z_i - b_i\) preserves inequality: \(z_i + (\Pi^\top p)_i - b_i \le z_i + (\Pi^\top q)_i - b_i\). Since \(\max(0, \cdot)\) and \(\min(\bar{p}_i, \cdot)\) are non-decreasing functions, taking compositions preserves the ordering, so \(\Phi_i(p) \le \Phi_i(q)\) for all \(i\).

2. Lattice fixed point: The hyper-rectangle \([0, \bar{p}] \subset \R^N\) is a compact complete lattice under the component-wise partial order. Monotonicity of \(\Phi\) implies by Tarski's fixed-point theorem that \(\Phi\) possesses a greatest fixed point \(p_{\max}^* = \sup\{p : p \le \Phi(p)\}\).

3. Monotone convergence: Start from \(p^{(0)} = \bar{p}\). Since \(\Phi(p) \le \bar{p}\) by definition, \(p^{(1)} = \Phi(p^{(0)}) \le p^{(0)}\). Applying monotonicity recursively yields the descending chain \(\bar{p} = p^{(0)} \ge p^{(1)} \ge p^{(2)} \ge \dots \ge p^*\). Because each institution's status (solvent vs default) can switch from solvent to default at most once, and the state space of default combinations contains at most \(N\) switches, the sequence terminates at the fixed point in at most \(N\) rounds.

3The Acemoglu, Ozdaglar & Tahbaz-Salehi (2015) Phase Transition Theorem

Does a more interconnected, diversified interbank financial network enhance or reduce systemic stability? Acemoglu, Ozdaglar, and Tahbaz-Salehi (2015) provide the definitive mathematical answer by proving that the relationship is non-monotonic and exhibits a sharp phase transition.

Theorem 1.4 (Phase Transition of Financial Networks, Acemoglu et al. 2015)

Consider an interbank network of \(N\) institutions, each with outside senior debt \(b\), nominal interbank liabilities \(\bar{p}\), and baseline outside assets \(z > b\). Each solvent institution possesses an outside equity buffer of \(\Delta = z - b > 0\). Suppose institution 0 experiences a negative asset shock of magnitude \(\varepsilon > 0\), leaving its liquid assets at \(z_0 = z - \varepsilon\).

Define two benchmark topologies: a dense (complete) network where liabilities are evenly distributed across all \(N-1\) peers (\(\Pi_{ij} = \frac{1}{N-1}\) for \(i \ne j\)), and a sparse (ring) network where each institution owes its entire liability to a single counterparty (\(\Pi_{i, i+1} = 1\)).

Then there exists a critical threshold \(\varepsilon^* = (N - 1)\Delta\) such that :

  1. Regime 1 (Moderate Shocks, \(\varepsilon \le \varepsilon^*\)): The complete network experiences strictly fewer defaults than the ring network. In the complete network, the shock is diversified across \(N-1\) peers, with each absorbing \(\frac{\varepsilon}{N-1} \le \Delta\). Thus, zero secondary default cascades occur (only Bank 0 fails). In the ring network, Bank 1 absorbs the entire shock \(\varepsilon > \Delta\), triggering cascade failures.
  2. Regime 2 (Catastrophic Shocks, \(\varepsilon > \varepsilon^*\)): The complete network experiences strictly more defaults (widespread systemic collapse). Each peer is allocated a loss of \(\frac{\varepsilon}{N-1} > \Delta\), which exceeds its individual equity buffer, causing all \(N\) institutions to fail simultaneously.
Proof Sketch

In the complete network, Bank 0 defaults with a payment shortfall of \(\text{Shortfall}_0 = \min(\bar{p}, \varepsilon - \Delta)\). When \(\varepsilon > \Delta\), Bank 0 pays \(p_0 = \bar{p} - (\varepsilon - \Delta)\). This shortfall is divided equally among the \(N-1\) counterparties, each receiving an interbank loss of \(\delta_j = \frac{\varepsilon - \Delta}{N-1}\). Counterparty \(j\) remains solvent if and only if its equity buffer exceeds its loss: \(\Delta \ge \delta_j = \frac{\varepsilon - \Delta}{N-1} \iff (N - 1)\Delta \ge \varepsilon - \Delta \iff \varepsilon \le N \Delta\). Thus, for moderate shocks (\(\varepsilon \le N\Delta\)), all \(N-1\) peers absorb the loss without defaulting.

Conversely, when \(\varepsilon > N\Delta\), the per-node loss exceeds the buffer: \(\delta_j > \Delta\). Every counterparty defaults simultaneously, wiping out the entire financial system. In contrast, in a sparse ring network, the shock propagates sequentially along the circle. While Bank 1 defaults, downstream banks that are further away along the ring remain insulated if senior outside assets and partial recovery dampen the cascade, preventing total system-wide collapse under catastrophic shocks.

Example 1.5 (Moderate vs Catastrophic Shocks in a 4-Bank Network)

Let \(N = 4\), \(\bar{p} = 40.0\), outside assets \(z = 20.0\), and senior debt \(b = 14.0\). Each bank has an outside equity buffer of \(\Delta = 20 - 14 = 6.0\). The critical threshold is \(\varepsilon^* = (4 - 1) \times 6.0 = 18.0\).

  • Scenario 1 (Moderate Shock): Outside assets of Bank 0 drop to \(z_0 = 5.0\) (\(\varepsilon = 15.0 \le 18.0\)). Shortfall is \(9.0\). In the complete network, each peer absorbs \(9.0 / 3 = 3.0 < 6.0\). Total defaults = 1/4 (only Bank 0 fails). In the ring network, Bank 1 absorbs all 9.0 (\(9.0 > 6.0\)), triggering cascade default (2/4 defaults).
  • Scenario 2 (Catastrophic Shock): Outside assets of Bank 0 drop to 0 and senior debt is 22 (\(\text{shortfall} = 22.0 > 18.0\)). In the complete network, each peer absorbs \(22.0 / 3 = 7.33 > 6.0\). Every bank defaults (4/4 defaults). Interconnectedness transforms from a shock absorber into a systemic contagion channel.

Lab 1 · Eisenberg-Noe Clearing and Acemoglu Phase Transition

Simulating Eisenberg-Noe clearing across complete versus ring network topologies demonstrates that complete networks minimize defaults under moderate shocks (1 vs 2 defaults) but trigger systemic collapse (4 of 4 defaults) under catastrophic shocks exceeding aggregate peer equity buffers.

"""Lab SR3-L04-1: Interbank Network Contagion and Clearing Dynamics.

SIMULATED: solves the Eisenberg-Noe clearing vector and verifies the Acemoglu,
Ozdaglar, and Tahbaz-Salehi (2015) phase transition from risk diversification
to systemic fragility under moderate versus catastrophic asset shocks.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def solve_clearing(
    l_mat: np.ndarray,
    z_vec: np.ndarray,
    b_vec: np.ndarray,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, int]:
    """Solve Eisenberg-Noe clearing payments via Picard fixed-point iteration."""
    p_bar = np.sum(l_mat, axis=1)
    pi_mat = np.zeros_like(l_mat)
    has_liab = p_bar > 0
    pi_mat[has_liab] = l_mat[has_liab] / p_bar[has_liab, None]

    p = p_bar.copy()
    rounds = 0
    for _ in range(500):
        rounds += 1
        # Inflows: Pi.T @ p
        p_next = np.minimum(p_bar, np.maximum(0.0, z_vec + pi_mat.T @ p - b_vec))
        if np.max(np.abs(p_next - p)) < 1e-10:
            p = p_next
            break
        p = p_next

    defaults = (p < p_bar - 1e-7).astype(int)
    return p, defaults, rounds


def main() -> None:
    # 4-bank financial network
    n = 4
    total_liab = 40.0  # Each bank owes 40 in nominal interbank liabilities

    # 1. Complete Network: liabilities evenly distributed across counterparties
    l_complete = np.full((n, n), total_liab / (n - 1))
    np.fill_diagonal(l_complete, 0.0)

    # 2. Ring Network: bank i borrows entirely from bank (i+1)%n
    l_ring = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i in range(n):
        l_ring[i, (i + 1) % n] = total_liab

    # Baseline asset endowments: outside assets z=20, outside senior debt b=14
    # Net outside equity buffer = 20 - 14 = 6 per bank
    z_baseline = np.full(n, 20.0)
    b_baseline = np.full(n, 14.0)

    # Scenario 1: Moderate shock on Bank 0 (outside assets drop from 20 to 5)
    # Bank 0 shortfall = 9.
    # In ring: Bank 1 absorbs all 9 (exceeds buffer 6 -> Bank 1 defaults).
    # In complete: Counterparties share 9/3 = 3 each (below buffer 6 -> 0 cascade defaults).
    z_mod = z_baseline.copy()
    z_mod[0] = 5.0
    _p_comp_mod, def_comp_mod, _ = solve_clearing(l_complete, z_mod, b_baseline)
    _p_ring_mod, def_ring_mod, _ = solve_clearing(l_ring, z_mod, b_baseline)

    # Scenario 2: Catastrophic shock on Bank 0 (assets wiped to 0, senior debt=22)
    # Shortfall = 22. In complete: each receives 22/3 = 7.33 loss (exceeds buffer 6 -> ALL fail).
    z_cat = z_baseline.copy()
    z_cat[0] = 0.0
    b_cat = b_baseline.copy()
    b_cat[0] = 22.0
    p_comp_cat, def_comp_cat, _ = solve_clearing(l_complete, z_cat, b_cat)
    _p_ring_cat, def_ring_cat, _ = solve_clearing(l_ring, z_cat, b_cat)

    print("=== Interbank Network Contagion (Acemoglu, Ozdaglar & Tahbaz-Salehi 2015) ===")
    print(f"Network size: N={n} banks. Nominal obligations per bank: p_bar={total_liab}")
    print("Net outside equity buffer per solvent bank: z - b = 6.0")
    print("-" * 75)
    print("Scenario 1: Moderate Negative Shock on Bank 0 (Shortfall = 9.0)")
    print(f"  Complete Network: Defaults = {int(np.sum(def_comp_mod))}/{n} (Default vector: {def_comp_mod})")
    print(f"  Ring Network:     Defaults = {int(np.sum(def_ring_mod))}/{n} (Default vector: {def_ring_mod})")
    print("  Outcome: Complete network diversifies shock across 3 peers (3.0 < 6.0).")
    print("           Ring network concentrates shock on Bank 1 (9.0 > 6.0), triggering cascade.")
    print("-" * 75)
    print("Scenario 2: Catastrophic Shock on Bank 0 (Shortfall = 22.0)")
    print(f"  Complete Network: Defaults = {int(np.sum(def_comp_cat))}/{n} (Default vector: {def_comp_cat})")
    print(f"  Ring Network:     Defaults = {int(np.sum(def_ring_cat))}/{n} (Default vector: {def_ring_cat})")
    print("  Outcome: Complete network propagates loss to ALL institutions (7.33 > 6.0).")
    print(f"  Clearing payments: Complete p*={p_comp_cat.round(2)}")
    print("-" * 75)
    print("Theorem 1 Phase Transition Verified:")
    print("Diversified interbank connections stabilize the system under moderate shocks,")
    print("but act as an unavoidable transmission mechanism during catastrophic shocks.")


main()

The numerical simulation confirms Theorem 1.4: under a moderate shock (shortfall 9.0), the complete network shares losses across 3 peers (3.0 per bank < 6.0 buffer), containing defaults to 1/4, while the ring network concentrates loss on Bank 1, causing 2/4 defaults. Where the canon breaks

While the Eisenberg–Noe and Acemoglu et al. models form the bedrock of financial network theory, several critical empirical realities depart from the idealized assumptions:

  1. Exogenous Liquidation Value: The Eisenberg–Noe model treats external asset values \(z_i\) as static exogenous constants. In reality, defaulting institutions are forced to liquidate assets into illiquid secondary markets, generating endogenous fire sales and liquidity spirals that depress \(z_j\) for all surviving peers holding similar asset classes.
  2. Information Opacity and Preemptive Runs: In actual banking panics, bilateral liabilities \(\Pi_{ij}\) are not publicly disclosed. Counterparties suspecting insolvency cannot distinguish between solvent and insolvent peers, triggering indiscriminate wholesale funding freezes (as observed with Lehman Brothers in September 2008).
  3. Bankruptcy Delays and Friction: Eisenberg–Noe assumes frictionless, instantaneous netting and distribution of assets. In practice, Chapter 11 bankruptcy filings freeze collateral for months or years, creating immediate cash-flow insolvencies for solvent institutions awaiting recovery payments.

Misconceptions

  • Maximizing interbank network interconnectedness and diversification always enhances financial system stability.

    Diversification stabilizes the network only when individual shocks are smaller than counterparty equity buffers. Under catastrophic shocks (such as the subprime mortgage collapse), dense interconnectedness acts as a transmission highway, distributing catastrophic losses across every institution and triggering system-wide collapse (Theorem 1.4).

  • Network contagion occurs only when downstream banks have zero equity capital.

    Contagion occurs whenever the incoming payment shortfall exceeds the downstream bank's equity buffer. Even a bank with substantial positive capital will default if its counterparty exposure shortfall exceeds its equity: \(\text{Shortfall} > z_i - b_i\).

  • The final clearing payment vector depends on the sequence or order in which bank defaults are declared.

    By Tarski's fixed-point theorem and monotonicity, the Eisenberg–Noe clearing payment vector is uniquely determined by fundamental asset endowments and the liability structure. The sequence in which defaults are processed in time does not alter the terminal equilibrium clearing payment vector \(p^*\).

Problems

Problem M.1: In a 3-bank network, Bank 0 owes \(L_{01} = 30\) and \(L_{02} = 10\). Compute Bank 0's total nominal obligations \(\bar{p}_0\) and its relative liability vector \(\Pi_{0} = (\Pi_{01}, \Pi_{02})\).

Worked solution

Total nominal liabilities:

\[ \bar{p}_0 = L_{01} + L_{02} = 30 + 10 = 40 \]

Relative liabilities:

\[ \Pi_{01} = \frac{L_{01}}{\bar{p}_0} = \frac{30}{40} = 0.75 \] \[ \Pi_{02} = \frac{L_{02}}{\bar{p}_0} = \frac{10}{40} = 0.25 \]

Bank 1 receives 75% and Bank 2 receives 25% of any clearing payment made by Bank 0.

Problem M.2: Bank 1 promised \(\bar{p}_1 = 60.0\). Its external assets are \(z_1 = 15.0\), senior liabilities \(b_1 = 10.0\), and incoming interbank payments from solvent peers equal \(35.0\). Calculate Bank 1's actual clearing payment \(p_1^* = \min(\bar{p}_1, \max(0, z_1 + \text{Inflows} - b_1))\).

Worked solution

Evaluate available net resources:

\[ \text{Resources} = z_1 + \text{Inflows} - b_1 = 15.0 + 35.0 - 10.0 = 40.0 \]

Apply the Eisenberg-Noe operator:

\[ p_1^* = \min(60.0, \; \max(0, 40.0)) = \min(60.0, 40.0) = 40.0 \]

Bank 1 defaults and pays \(40.0\), generating a shortfall of \(\bar{p}_1 - p_1^* = 20.0\).

Problem M.3: In a 5-bank complete network with outside equity buffer \(\Delta = 5.0\) per bank, calculate the Acemoglu et al. critical shock threshold \(\varepsilon^* = (N - 1)\Delta\).

Worked solution

Substitute \(N = 5\) and \(\Delta = 5.0\):

\[ \varepsilon^* = (5 - 1) \times 5.0 = 4 \times 5.0 = 20.0 \]

Any idiosyncratic asset shock on Bank 0 less than or equal to 20.0 is absorbed without systemic cascade defaults; shocks exceeding 20.0 trigger systemic failure.

Problem P.1: Prove that the Eisenberg–Noe operator \(\Phi(p)\) preserves the priority of senior outside debt \(b_i\) over junior interbank debt \(\bar{p}_i\).

Worked solution

Consider the operator component: \(\Phi_i(p) = \min(\bar{p}_i, \max(0, z_i + (\Pi^\top p)_i - b_i))\). If total asset inflows \(z_i + (\Pi^\top p)_i \le b_i\), then \(z_i + (\Pi^\top p)_i - b_i \le 0\), which yields \(\max(0, z_i + (\Pi^\top p)_i - b_i) = 0\), and therefore \(\Phi_i(p) = 0\). Interbank creditors receive zero payment unless outside senior debt \(b_i\) is satisfied in full. Thus, senior debt holds strict absolute priority over interbank obligations.

Problem P.2: Prove that total equity value in the financial network \(E(p^*) = \sum_{i=1}^N e_i^*\) equals the total net outside asset surplus minus interbank deadweight bankruptcy liquidation losses.

Worked solution

For each bank \(i\), net equity is \(e_i^* = \max(0, z_i + (\Pi^\top p^*)_i - b_i - \bar{p}_i)\). Summing across all institutions:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^N e_i^* + \sum_{i=1}^N p_i^* = \sum_{i=1}^N \max(0, z_i + (\Pi^\top p^*)_i - b_i) \]

Since \(\sum_{i=1}^N (\Pi^\top p^*)_i = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N \Pi_{ji} p_j^* = \sum_{j=1}^N p_j^* \sum_{i=1}^N \Pi_{ji} = \sum_{j=1}^N p_j^*\), interbank payments are purely internal transfers between nodes. Consequently, internal payments cancel out in the aggregate summation, establishing that aggregate net worth equals net outside assets \(\sum (z_i - b_i)\) minus unrecovered default shortfalls.

Problem P.3: Prove that if an institution \(i\) is solvent in the clearing vector (\(p_i^* = \bar{p}_i\)), its downstream counterparties experience zero payment shortfall from node \(i\).

Worked solution

The payment made by institution \(i\) to counterparty \(j\) is \(p_{ij}^* = \Pi_{ij} p_i^*\). When \(p_i^* = \bar{p}_i\), \(p_{ij}^* = \Pi_{ij} \bar{p}_i = \frac{L_{ij}}{\bar{p}_i} \bar{p}_i = L_{ij}\). The counterparty receives the exact nominal amount promised in the contract, meaning counterparty \(j\) suffers zero credit loss from node \(i\).

Problem E.1: In the Lab 1 simulation under Scenario 1 (moderate shock of shortfall 9.0), why did the complete network have 1 default while the ring network had 2 defaults?

Worked solution

In the complete network, Bank 0's debt is owed equally to 3 peers (\(\Pi_{0j} = 1/3\)). The shortfall of 9.0 was dispersed as \(9.0 / 3 = 3.0\) per bank. Since each solvent bank held an equity buffer of 6.0, \(3.0 < 6.0\), so all 3 peers absorbed the loss without defaulting. In the ring network, Bank 0 owed its entire debt to Bank 1 (\(\Pi_{01} = 1.0\)). Bank 1 absorbed the entire 9.0 shortfall, which exceeded its 6.0 buffer, forcing Bank 1 into secondary cascade default.

Problem E.2: Explain how central counterparty (CCP) multilateral clearing in OTC derivatives markets mimics a complete network topology, and describe the systemic failure risk identified by Acemoglu et al. (2015).

Worked solution

A CCP acts as the buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, replacing bilateral contracts with centralized novation and mutualized default funds. This effectively creates a complete risk-sharing structure where clearing members share the default losses of any member. While this stabilizes the market against moderate member insolvencies, Acemoglu et al. show that if a mega-dealer experiences catastrophic losses that exhaust its initial margin and default fund contributions, the CCP waterfall cascades losses across all clearing members, threatening aggregate market failure.

Problem E.3: In the 2008 failure of Lehman Brothers, why did money market mutual funds (such as the Reserve Primary Fund) "break the buck", and how does this illustrate Eisenberg-Noe senior outside debt priority?

Worked solution

The Reserve Primary Fund held \(\$785\text{ million}\) in Lehman Brothers commercial paper. When Lehman entered Chapter 11 bankruptcy, commercial paper (an unsecured interbank/wholesale claim) was subordinated to secured claims and administrative costs. With expected recoveries plunging, the fund wrote down Lehman assets, causing its NAV to drop to \(\$0.97\) per share ("breaking the buck"), which sparked an immediate run across the \(\$3\text{ trillion}\) prime money market fund sector.

Problem R.1: How can central banks use the Eisenberg-Noe clearing model to design an optimal, cost-minimizing targeted emergency lender-of-last-resort (LOLR) intervention?

Worked solution

The central bank solves a constrained optimization problem: \(\min_{c \in \R_+^N} \mathbf{1}^\top c\) subject to \(p^*(z + c) = \bar{p}\) (zero cascade defaults). Because banks with high network centrality or dense outbound liabilities transmit losses to multiple systemic counterparties, injecting targeted liquidity \(c_i\) into a single central node (e.g., AIG or Bear Stearns) halts cascades at a fraction of the cost required to recapitalize all downstream peripheral institutions.

Problem R.2: Formulate the coupled fixed-point problem where Eisenberg-Noe clearing payments and endogenous fire-sale asset prices must be solved simultaneously.

Worked solution

Let \(q\) denote the vector of asset prices, and let \(z(q) = z_{\text{cash}} + H q\) be the mark-to-market value of illiquid asset holdings \(H\). When banks default (\(p_i^* < \bar{p}_i\)), they liquidate assets \(\ell_i = f(\bar{p}_i - p_i^*)\). Total asset sales equal \(Q = \sum_i \ell_i\). Asset prices satisfy an inverse demand function \(q = P(Q)\) where \(P'(Q) < 0\). The coupled equilibrium is a joint fixed point \((p^*, q^*)\) satisfying \(p^* = \Phi(p^*, z(q^*))\) and \(q^* = P(\sum_i \ell_i(p^*))\). This creates multiple equilibria and catastrophic cliff-edge liquidity crashes.

Problem R.3: How does core-periphery (tiered) network structure in actual interbank fed funds markets alter contagion resilience relative to symmetric complete networks?

Worked solution

Empirical interbank markets exhibit a core-periphery architecture: a dozen money-center dealer banks form a densely connected core, while hundreds of regional banks form an unconnected periphery that trades exclusively with the core. This architecture isolates peripheral shocks from cascading to other peripheral banks. However, it creates concentrated systemic vulnerability: if a core money-center bank fails, it simultaneously severs funding to the entire periphery, making core-periphery structures more fragile to targeted core shocks than uniform complete networks.

Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Eisenberg–Noe (2001) framework, how is the relative liability matrix \(\Pi_{ij}\) defined?

    Options

    \(\Pi_{ij}\) row-normalizes bilateral liabilities such that \(\sum_j \Pi_{ij} = 1\), allocating payments proportionately across counterparties (Definition 1.1).

  2. In a 5-node Eisenberg-Noe network where Node 0 promised \(\bar{p}_0 = 50.0\), outside senior debt \(b_0 = 15.0\), asset shock leaves liquid assets \(z_0 = 0.0\), and incoming interbank payments from solvent peers equal \(39.2\), compute Node 0's actual clearing payment \(p_0^* = \min(\bar{p}_0, \max(0, z_0 + \text{Inflows} - b_0))\).

    \(p_0^* = \min(50.0, \max(0, 0.0 + 39.2 - 15.0)) = \min(50.0, 24.2) = 24.2\).

  3. What is the central finding of the Acemoglu, Ozdaglar & Tahbaz-Salehi (2015) financial network phase transition theorem?

    Options

    Dense connectivity diversifies small-to-moderate shocks across multiple buffers, but acts as a transmission highway that wipes out all counterparties under large shocks (Theorem 1.4).

  4. Why does the Picard iteration algorithm for the Eisenberg–Noe operator \(\Phi(p)\) terminate in at most \(N\) rounds?

    Options

    Starting from \(p^{(0)} = \bar{p}\), payments decrease monotonically. Since a node that enters default never returns to solvency during the iteration, there are at most \(N\) default transitions (Theorem 1.3).

  5. In a 4-bank complete network where each solvent bank has an outside equity buffer of \(\Delta = z - b = 6.0\), calculate the Acemoglu critical shock threshold \(\varepsilon^* = (N - 1)\Delta\).

    \(\varepsilon^* = (4 - 1) \times 6.0 = 3 \times 6.0 = 18.0\).

  6. What legal bankruptcy principle ensures that an insolvent institution's interbank creditors share proportionately in remaining assets rather than racing for full repayment?

    Options

    Pari passu requires that all creditors of the same class receive pro-rata distributions proportional to their claims \(\Pi_{ij}\).

  7. Under what network topology is an idiosyncratic shock concentrated entirely on a single adjacent counterparty?

    Options

    In a ring network, each node borrows from exactly one creditor, transmitting 100% of any default shortfall to that single counterparty.

  8. How does endogenous fire-sale price feedback alter the Eisenberg–Noe network clearing outcome?

    Options

    Liquidations depress market prices, which erodes the asset values of all institutions holding common assets, creating coupled contractual and fire-sale spirals.
