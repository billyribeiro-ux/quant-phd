Geanakoplos Collateral Equilibrium Mechanics

  • Prerequisites: procyclical leverage (L01), limits to arbitrage (AP6)
  • Introduces: collateral equilibrium
  • Core Sources: Geanakoplos (2010)
  • Ttheorem (deductive mathematical result)
  • Aempirically replicated finding
  • Bpublished empirical finding, not fully settled
  • Cworking paper, practitioner research or single sample
  • Dpractitioner lore
  • Xrefuted claim

Arrow–Debreu general equilibrium theory assumes that promises are kept costlessly or enforced by infinite penal sanctions. In modern financial markets, promises are backed by collateral. When borrowers lack personal liability, the collateral backing a loan determines how much credit can be extended. This lesson formalizes the collateral equilibrium framework of Geanakoplos (2010). In this framework, heterogeneous beliefs create natural buyers and lenders, and equilibrium determines not only asset prices and interest rates, but also endogenous borrowing margins (haircuts) and the identity of the marginal buyer.

1The Collateral Contract Space and Limited Enforcement

Consider an economy where economic agents trade collateralized loan contracts. In the absence of reputational penalties or personal bankruptcy recourse, a borrower who promises to repay \(j\) dollars at date 1 secured by 1 unit of collateral asset \(C\) will choose to default whenever the liquidation value of the collateral \(V_s\) in state \(s\) falls below the promised payment \(j\).

Consequently, the actual delivery to the lender in state \(s\) is given by the min operator:

\[ \text{Delivery}(j, C, s) = \min(j, V_s) \]

A collateralized loan contract is thus defined by the pair \((j, C)\), where \(j\) is the face promise and \(C\) is the collateral requirement.

Definition 2.1 (Collateral equilibrium)

A collateral equilibrium is a collection of asset prices, collateralized loan contract prices, consumption allocations, and portfolio holdings such that:

  1. All agents maximize expected utility subject to budget constraints and physical collateral constraints (each unit of contract \((j, C)\) issued must be secured by holding at least \(C\) units of physical collateral).
  2. Markets clear for all physical assets and for all actively traded collateral contracts.
  3. Contracts that are not traded are priced such that no agent strictly desires to issue or purchase them (no arbitrage in inactive contract space).

2Two-State Economy and the No-Default Loan Condition

Consider a two-date economy \(t \in \{0, 1\}\) with a single risky asset available in total supply \(Q = 1\). At date 1, the economy realizes one of two states \(s \in \{U, D\}\), where \(U\) denotes the up state and \(D\) denotes the down state. The asset generates payoffs:

\[ V_U > V_D > 0 \]

Lenders can also invest in a storage technology or risk-free bond delivering gross riskless return \(R_f \ge 1.0\).

Proposition 2.2 (Safe Debt Dominance)

In a two-state collateralized economy with risk-neutral agents and complete contract spaces, the contract that maximizes borrower leverage without default promises exactly the down-state payoff \(j = V_D\). The maximal safe loan amount per unit of collateral is:

\[ L = \frac{V_D}{R_f} \]
Proof of Proposition 2.2

If a borrower promises \(j \le V_D\), delivery in state \(D\) is \(\min(j, V_D) = j\), and delivery in state \(U\) is \(\min(j, V_U) = j\). The loan is riskless. By risk-neutral pricing at gross rate \(R_f\), the market value of the loan at date 0 is \(L = j / R_f\). To maximize debt capacity per unit of collateral without default, the borrower sets \(j = V_D\), yielding \(L = V_D / R_f\). Any promise \(j > V_D\) incurs default in state \(D\), requiring risk discounting. Geanakoplos (2010, Section 2) shows that when optimists have strong comparative advantage in holding the upside, safe debt \(j = V_D\) strictly dominates risky debt. \(\blacksquare\)

3Heterogeneous Beliefs and the Marginal Buyer Theorem

Following Geanakoplos (2010), suppose the population of investors is indexed by \(h \in [0, 1]\). All agents are risk-neutral, but they hold heterogeneous subjective beliefs about the probability of the up state \(U\). Specifically, agent \(h\) believes that state \(U\) occurs with probability:

\[ p^h = h \]

Thus, agents with \(h \approx 1\) are extreme optimists, while agents with \(h \approx 0\) are extreme pessimists. Assume wealth is uniformly distributed across the continuum, such that the total wealth of agents in any interval \([a, b]\) is \(b - a\), and total aggregate wealth is \(\int_0^1 1 \, dh = 1\).

Theorem 2.3 (Geanakoplos Marginal Buyer Determination)

In the two-state collateral equilibrium with asset supply \(Q = 1\), risk-neutral beliefs \(p^h = h\), safe borrowing \(L = V_D / R_f\), and uniform wealth density 1, there exists a unique marginal buyer cutoff \(h^* \in (0, 1)\) such that:

  1. Optimists with \(h \in [h^*, 1]\) invest their entire wealth in the leveraged asset, borrowing \(L = V_D / R_f\) per unit of asset.
  2. Pessimists with \(h \in [0, h^*)\) lend their wealth to optimists at gross rate \(R_f\) or hold cash.
  3. The marginal buyer cutoff \(h^*\) is given in closed form by: \[ h^* = \frac{R_f}{R_f + V_U - V_D} \]
  4. The unique equilibrium asset price \(P\) is: \[ P = \frac{V_D}{R_f} + (1 - h^*) = \frac{V_D}{R_f} + \frac{V_U - V_D}{R_f + V_U - V_D} \]
Proof of Theorem 2.3

Let \(P\) be the equilibrium asset price. A buyer holding 1 unit of the asset borrows \(L = V_D / R_f\). The required equity down payment (margin) per unit of asset is:

\[ m = P - L = P - \frac{V_D}{R_f} \]

At date 1, the asset payoff net of debt repayment is:

  • In state \(U\): \(V_U - L R_f = V_U - V_D\).
  • In state \(D\): \(V_D - L R_f = V_D - V_D = 0\).

For agent \(h\), the expected return on equity from investing in the leveraged asset is:

\[ \E_h[\text{Return on Equity}] = \frac{h (V_U - V_D) + (1 - h)(0)}{m} = \frac{h (V_U - V_D)}{P - V_D / R_f} \]

Agent \(h\) strictly prefers buying the leveraged asset over lending at \(R_f\) if and only if \(\E_h[\text{ROE}] > R_f\), which is equivalent to \(h > h^*\), where \(h^*\) satisfies the marginal buyer indifference condition:

\[ \frac{h^* (V_U - V_D)}{P - V_D / R_f} = R_f \implies P - \frac{V_D}{R_f} = \frac{h^* (V_U - V_D)}{R_f} \]

Next, consider asset market clearing. The total wealth of optimists \(h \in [h^*, 1]\) is \(\int_{h^*}^1 1 \, dh = 1 - h^*\). Each unit of asset requires cash equity \(m = P - V_D / R_f\). Hence, total asset demand from optimists is \(\frac{1 - h^*}{P - V_D / R_f}\). Equating demand to supply \(Q = 1\):

\[ \frac{1 - h^*}{P - V_D / R_f} = 1 \implies P - \frac{V_D}{R_f} = 1 - h^* \]

Equating the indifference condition to the market-clearing condition:

\[ \frac{h^* (V_U - V_D)}{R_f} = 1 - h^* \implies h^* (V_U - V_D) = R_f (1 - h^*) = R_f - R_f h^* \] \[ h^* [R_f + V_U - V_D] = R_f \implies h^* = \frac{R_f}{R_f + V_U - V_D} \]

Substituting \(1 - h^* = \frac{V_U - V_D}{R_f + V_U - V_D}\) back into \(P = V_D / R_f + (1 - h^*)\) yields:

\[ P = \frac{V_D}{R_f} + \frac{V_U - V_D}{R_f + V_U - V_D} \]

This establishes existence, uniqueness, and the closed forms. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.4 (Representative Equilibrium Calculation)

Let \(V_U = 1.20\), \(V_D = 0.20\), and \(R_f = 1.00\). The spread is \(V_U - V_D = 1.00\).

  • Marginal buyer: \(h^* = \frac{1.00}{1.00 + 1.00} = 0.500000\).
  • Safe loan: \(L = V_D / R_f = 0.20 / 1.00 = \$0.200000\).
  • Asset price: \(P = 0.20 + (1 - 0.50) = \$0.700000\).
  • Required margin: \(m = P - L = 0.70 - 0.20 = \$0.500000\).
  • Leverage ratio: \(\lambda = P / m = 0.70 / 0.50 = 1.4000\text{x}\).
  • Haircut: \(\text{Haircut} = m / P = 0.50 / 0.70 \approx 71.43\%\).

In contrast, if borrowing were prohibited (zero leverage, \(\theta = 0\)), market clearing requires \(P_{\text{unlev}} = 1 - h^*_{\text{unlev}}\) and \(h^*_{\text{unlev}}(1.20) + (1 - h^*_{\text{unlev}})(0.20) = P_{\text{unlev}}\), yielding \(P_{\text{unlev}} = \$0.600000\). Collateralized borrowing creates a leverage collateral premium of \(\$0.100000\) (+16.67%).

4Credit Clearing via Haircuts Versus Interest Rates

Geanakoplos emphasizes that in collateral equilibrium, the primary mechanism clearing the credit market is the haircut (margin requirement), not the interest rate.

In standard neoclassical credit markets, excess demand for loans drives the interest rate higher. In collateralized markets, raising the promised interest rate beyond \(R_f\) induces default in bad states, destroying the safety of the debt. Because lenders care about recovery in default states, credit rationing occurs along the margin dimension: lenders adjust the haircut \(m\) to balance loan demand with the physical risk of the collateral.

Lab 1 · Geanakoplos Collateral Equilibrium Mechanics

Solving the two-state collateral equilibrium analytically verifies that marginal buyer expected return on equity equals the risk-free rate to machine precision (residual < 1e-7) and that market clearing holds exactly.

"""Lab SR2-L02-1: Geanakoplos Collateral Equilibrium Mechanics.

Solves the two-state Geanakoplos (2010) collateral equilibrium with heterogeneous
optimism and collateralized borrowing. Verifies the marginal buyer indifference condition
and market-clearing identity to machine precision.
"""


def solve_collateral_equilibrium(
    v_u: float,
    v_d: float,
    rf: float = 1.0,
    loan_ratio: float = 1.0,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Analytical solver for the 2-state Geanakoplos collateral equilibrium."""
    loan = (loan_ratio * v_d) / rf
    num = rf - (1.0 - loan_ratio) * v_d
    den = rf + (v_u - v_d)
    h_star = min(1.0, max(0.0, num / den))
    price = loan + (1.0 - h_star)
    margin = price - loan
    leverage = price / margin if margin > 0.0 else 1.0
    haircut = margin / price if price > 0.0 else 0.0

    return {
        "price": price,
        "marginal_buyer": h_star,
        "loan": loan,
        "margin": margin,
        "leverage": leverage,
        "haircut": haircut,
    }


def main() -> None:
    # Representative instance (SIMULATED)
    vu = 1.20  # Up-state asset payoff
    vd = 0.20  # Down-state asset payoff
    rf = 1.00  # Gross risk-free rate
    theta = 1.00  # Full non-default borrowing (L = V_D)

    res = solve_collateral_equilibrium(vu, vd, rf, theta)
    p = res["price"]
    h_star = res["marginal_buyer"]
    loan = res["loan"]
    margin = res["margin"]
    lev = res["leverage"]
    hc = res["haircut"]

    print("=== GEANAKOPLOS COLLATERAL EQUILIBRIUM (SIMULATED) ===")
    print(f"Payoffs: V_U = ${vu:.2f}, V_D = ${vd:.2f} | Gross R_f = {rf:.2f}")
    print(f"Equilibrium Price P: ${p:.6f}")
    print(f"Marginal Buyer Cutoff h*: {h_star:.6f} (Optimists [h*, 1] buy asset)")
    print(f"Collateralized Loan per Unit: ${loan:.6f}")
    print(f"Required Margin (Down Payment): ${margin:.6f}")
    print(f"Equilibrium Leverage: {lev:.4f}x | Haircut: {hc * 100:.2f}%")

    # Invariant Verification 1: Marginal buyer expected return on equity == R_f
    expected_equity_payoff = (1.0 - theta) * vd + h_star * (vu - vd)
    return_on_equity = expected_equity_payoff / margin
    residual_indifference = abs(return_on_equity - rf)
    print("\n--- Invariant Verification ---")
    print(f"Marginal Buyer Return on Equity: {return_on_equity:.8f}")
    print(f"Indifference Residual |ROE - R_f|: {residual_indifference:.2e} <= 1e-7")

    # Invariant Verification 2: Market clearing
    optimist_wealth = 1.0 - h_star
    total_asset_demand = optimist_wealth / margin
    residual_market_clearing = abs(total_asset_demand - 1.0)
    print(f"Total Asset Demand: {total_asset_demand:.8f} (Supply = 1.0)")
    print(f"Market Clearing Residual |Demand - 1.0|: {residual_market_clearing:.2e} <= 1e-7")

    # Unleveraged benchmark: if no borrowing is permitted (theta = 0)
    res_unleveraged = solve_collateral_equilibrium(vu, vd, rf, loan_ratio=0.0)
    p_unlev = res_unleveraged["price"]
    h_unlev = res_unleveraged["marginal_buyer"]
    print(f"\nUnleveraged Benchmark (Zero Borrowing): Price = ${p_unlev:.6f}, h* = {h_unlev:.6f}")
    print(f"Leverage Collateral Premium: ${p - p_unlev:.6f} (+{(p - p_unlev) / p_unlev * 100:.2f}%)")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Canonical asset pricing models (such as CCAPM or representative-agent models) imply that asset prices reflect average or representative expectations. The Geanakoplos collateral equilibrium breaks this canon completely: asset prices are set by the marginal buyer, who is significantly more optimistic than the population mean. When leverage is high, a tiny group of extreme optimists can purchase the entire asset supply, driving asset prices far above the consensus fundamental valuation. Furthermore, when margin shocks occur, prices crash not because fundamentals changed, but because the identity of the marginal buyer shifts downward into pessimistic agents.

Misconceptions

  1. “The interest rate is the sole clearing price of credit in financial markets.”

    In collateralized lending, the loan-to-value ratio (haircut) is an independent and often dominant market-clearing variable. Lenders restrict credit during crises by increasing margin requirements rather than merely raising interest rates (Geanakoplos 2010).

  2. “Asset prices in competitive markets reflect the average belief of all participants.”

    In collateral equilibrium, optimists concentrate their wealth into leveraged asset holdings. The equilibrium price is determined by the marginal buyer \(h^*\), which is substantially higher than the mean belief \(\bar{h} = 0.5\).

  3. “Requiring 100% collateral backing eliminates all asset price volatility.”

    Collateral eliminates credit default risk for the lender, but it does not eliminate asset price volatility. If downside payoff expectations decline or haircuts widen, the forced deleveraging of borrowers generates severe secondary price crashes.

Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Equilibrium price under asymmetric payoffs

Consider an asset with \(V_U = 1.60\), \(V_D = 0.40\), and gross riskless rate \(R_f = 1.00\). Calculate the marginal buyer cutoff \(h^*\), the safe loan \(L\), equilibrium price \(P\), required margin \(m\), and equilibrium leverage \(\lambda\).

Solution

The spread is \(V_U - V_D = 1.20\). Marginal buyer cutoff is \(h^* = \frac{1.00}{1.00 + 1.20} = \frac{1}{2.20} \approx 0.454545\). Safe loan is \(L = 0.40 / 1.00 = \$0.40\). Price is \(P = L + (1 - h^*) = 0.40 + (1 - 0.454545) = \$0.945455\). Margin is \(m = P - L = \$0.545455\). Leverage is \(\lambda = P / m = 0.945455 / 0.545455 \approx 1.7333\text{x}\).

M2 · Impact of an interest rate hike

Using the parameters from Example 2.4 (\(V_U = 1.20, V_D = 0.20\)), suppose the central bank increases the risk-free rate from \(R_f = 1.00\) to \(R_f = 1.05\). Calculate the new marginal buyer cutoff \(h^*\) and asset price \(P\).

Solution

With \(R_f = 1.05\): \(h^* = \frac{1.05}{1.05 + 1.00} = \frac{1.05}{2.05} \approx 0.512195\). Safe loan is \(L = 0.20 / 1.05 \approx \$0.190476\). Price is \(P = L + (1 - h^*) = 0.190476 + (1 - 0.512195) = 0.190476 + 0.487805 = \$0.678281\). The rate hike lowers price from $0.700000 to $0.678281 (-3.10%).

M3 · Partial collateral borrowing constraint

Suppose lenders require an extra safety margin, lending only 80% of worst-case payoff: \(\theta = 0.80\). For \(V_U = 1.20, V_D = 0.20, R_f = 1.00\), calculate the resulting equilibrium price and leverage.

Solution

Safe loan is \(L = 0.80 \times 0.20 / 1.00 = \$0.160000\). The numerator of \(h^*\) is \(R_f - (1 - \theta) V_D = 1.00 - 0.20 \times 0.20 = 0.96\). Denominator is \(1.00 + 1.00 = 2.00\). Thus, \(h^* = 0.96 / 2.00 = 0.480000\). Price is \(P = L + (1 - h^*) = 0.16 + 0.52 = \$0.680000\). Margin is \(m = 0.68 - 0.16 = \$0.52\). Leverage is \(\lambda = 0.68 / 0.52 \approx 1.3077\text{x}\).

Proof

P1 · Monotonicity of equilibrium price with respect to downside payoff

Prove that in the Geanakoplos two-state collateral equilibrium, \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V_D} > 0\), establishing that improved downside guarantees expand asset prices through the collateral channel.

Solution

From Theorem 2.3 with \(R_f = 1\), \(P = V_D + \frac{V_U - V_D}{1 + V_U - V_D}\). Let \(x \equiv V_U - V_D\). Then \(P = V_D + \frac{x}{1 + x} = V_D + 1 - \frac{1}{1 + x}\). Differentiating with respect to \(V_D\): noting \(\frac{\partial x}{\partial V_D} = -1\), we have \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V_D} = 1 + \left(-\frac{1}{(1+x)^2} \cdot (-1)\right) = 1 - \frac{1}{(1+x)^2}\). Since \(x = V_U - V_D > 0\), \(1 + x > 1\), which implies \((1+x)^2 > 1\), so \(\frac{1}{(1+x)^2} < 1\). Thus, \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V_D} = 1 - \frac{1}{(1+x)^2} > 0\). This proves the proposition.

P2 · Leverage is strictly increasing in downside payoff

Prove that equilibrium leverage \(\lambda = P / m\) is strictly increasing in \(V_D\) for any fixed \(V_U > V_D\).

Solution

Recall \(m = P - L = 1 - h^* = \frac{x}{1 + x}\), where \(x = V_U - V_D\). Note that \(m\) depends on \(V_D\) only through \(x\). As \(V_D\) increases, \(x\) decreases, so \(m = \frac{x}{1+x}\) strictly decreases. Meanwhile, \(P\) strictly increases in \(V_D\) (from P1). Since the numerator \(P\) increases and the denominator \(m\) decreases, leverage \(\lambda = P / m\) is strictly increasing in \(V_D\).

P3 · Pessimist consumption in collateral equilibrium

Prove that all pessimists \(h \in [0, h^*)\) achieve higher expected utility by lending at rate \(R_f\) than by holding equity in the risky asset.

Solution

For any agent \(h\), the expected return on equity is \(\text{ROE}(h) = \frac{h (V_U - V_D)}{m}\). By definition of the marginal buyer \(h^*\), \(\text{ROE}(h^*) = R_f\). Because \(V_U - V_D > 0\) and \(m > 0\), \(\text{ROE}(h)\) is strictly increasing in \(h\). For any \(h < h^*\), \(\text{ROE}(h) < \text{ROE}(h^*) = R_f\). Therefore, lending at \(R_f\) strictly dominates investing in equity for all \(h < h^*\).

Empirical

E1 · The CDS-bond basis as a collateral haircut indicator

Explain why a negative CDS-bond basis (bond yield spread exceeding the CDS spread) during the 2008 crisis reflects collateral margin constraints rather than credit default risk.

Solution

In a frictionless market, purchasing a corporate bond and buying CDS protection creates a riskless synthetic asset yielding the risk-free rate, so Bond Spread = CDS Spread. In 2008, repo haircuts on corporate bonds widened dramatically, requiring cash equity down payments. Arbitrageurs could not fund the trade without tying up scarce capital. The negative CDS-bond basis represented the shadow price of intermediary balance sheet capacity and collateral haircuts.

E2 · Subprime mortgage margin expansion

According to Gorton and Metrick (2012), how did repo haircuts on subprime AAA-rated tranches evolve from early 2007 to late 2008, and what were the consequences for shadow bank solvency?

Solution

Haircuts on AAA-rated subprime tranches were 0% to 2% in early 2007, permitting leverage of 50x to 100x. By late 2008, haircuts escalated to 45%–50%, reducing maximum leverage to ~2x. Shadow banks funding long-term mortgage assets through overnight repo faced instant liquidity insolvency: unable to meet multi-billion-dollar margin calls, they were forced into liquidation regardless of fundamental credit quality.

E3 · Sovereign debt collateral haircuts in the European crisis

How did clearinghouse margin hikes on Greek, Irish, and Portuguese sovereign debt in 2010–2011 trigger self-fulfilling price spirals in peripheral European bond markets?

Solution

LCH.Clearnet and other central counterparties raised initial margin haircuts on peripheral sovereign debt as sovereign bond yields rose. Banks holding peripheral bonds were required to post cash margin. To obtain cash, banks sold the underlying sovereign bonds, driving bond prices down and yields up. This yield increase triggered further mechanical margin hikes by clearinghouses, generating a collateral-driven sovereign debt spiral.

Research

R1 · Generalizing belief distributions beyond uniform

How does the Geanakoplos marginal buyer condition generalize when investor optimism is distributed according to a continuous cumulative distribution function \(F(h)\) on \([0, 1]\)?

Solution

With distribution \(F(h)\), the aggregate wealth of optimists above \(h^*\) is \(\int_{h^*}^1 w(h) dF(h) = 1 - F(h^*)\) under normalized wealth. The market clearing condition becomes \(\frac{1 - F(h^*)}{P - L} = Q\). Combining with the indifference condition \(P - L = \frac{h^*(V_U - V_D)}{R_f}\) yields the equilibrium condition \(1 - F(h^*) = Q \frac{h^*(V_U - V_D)}{R_f}\). When \(F(h)\) is skewed toward optimists, \(h^*\) rises, compressing the optimist buyer cohort while driving price higher.

R2 · Endogenous default and tranche securitization

How do Fostel and Geanakoplos (2008) show that tranche securitization (senior AAA vs junior equity tranches) emerges endogenously to maximize collateral borrowing capacity?

Solution

Fostel and Geanakoplos prove that when an asset has multiple payoff states, issuing a single non-contingent bond leaves collateral borrowing capacity unexploited. Securitizing the asset into senior safe tranches and junior risky tranches allows lenders to fund the senior tranche with zero haircut, while leveraged optimists buy the junior tranche with minimal down payment. Securitization thereby endogenously maximizes credit capacity and asset prices.

R3 · Central bank lending facilities as collateral interventions

Evaluate the Federal Reserve's Term Asset-Backed Securities Loan Facility (TALF) through the lens of Geanakoplos collateral equilibrium.

Solution

In 2008, private haircuts on securitized paper jumped to 50%, collapsing asset prices. Rather than cutting interest rates further (which had reached zero), the Federal Reserve created TALF to lend non-recourse cash against asset-backed securities at low, fixed haircuts (e.g., 10%–15%). By directly setting the collateral haircut, the central bank bypassed impaired private intermediaries, restored leverage capacity, and successfully stabilized secondary asset prices.

    Leverage permits optimists to control more units of asset per dollar of wealth, creating an endogenous leverage collateral premium.

    The unleveraged price is $0.600000 (Lab 1, Example 2.4).
