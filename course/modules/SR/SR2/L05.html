Macroeconomic Models with Intermediary Balance Sheets

  • Prerequisites: intermediary asset pricing (AP6), leverage cycle (L04)
  • Introduces: macro model with a financial sector
  • Core Sources: Brunnermeier & Sannikov (2014), He & Krishnamurthy (2013)
  • Ttheorem (deductive mathematical result)
  • Aempirically replicated finding
  • Bpublished empirical finding, not fully settled
  • Cworking paper, practitioner research or single sample
  • Dpractitioner lore
  • Xrefuted claim

Standard Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) macro models historically omitted the financial sector, treating credit markets as a passive veil and linearizing equations around a stable steady state. The 2008 global financial crisis exposed the inadequacy of this framework. In continuous-time models with financial frictions, intermediary equity capital is the key macroeconomic state variable. When intermediary balance sheets become impaired, non-linear amplification drives the economy into severe stagnation regimes. This lesson develops the Brunnermeier and Sannikov (2014) framework, deriving the law of motion for intermediary wealth share and proving the Volatility Paradox.

1From Micro-Foundations to Dynamic Macro-Finance

Prior to 2008, mainstream macroeconomic models modeled capital allocation through representative households investing directly in production. Financial frictions, when included at all (e.g., Bernanke–Gertler financial accelerator models), were linearized around the deterministic steady state, treating financial panics as small deviations from tranquil equilibria.

Brunnermeier and Sannikov (2014) revolutionized macro-finance by developing a fully non-linear continuous-time stochastic general equilibrium model with financial intermediaries:

Definition 5.1 (Macro model with a financial sector)

A macro model with a financial sector is a dynamic general equilibrium framework where productive financial intermediaries face equity balance sheet frictions, rendering the aggregate wealth share of intermediaries an endogenous, non-linear state variable that dictates asset prices, real investment, risk premia, and systemic macroeconomic stability.

2The Brunnermeier–Sannikov (2014) Continuous-Time Architecture

The economy is populated by two classes of risk-neutral or log-utility agents:

  • Productive Experts (Intermediaries): High-productivity agents with subjective discount rate \(\rho\). When managing physical capital \(K_t\), experts generate output flow at rate \(a K_t dt\).
  • Unskilled Households: Less productive agents with identical discount rate \(\rho\). When households manage physical capital directly, they generate output at rate \(\underline{a} K_t dt\), where \(a > \underline{a}\).

Aggregate physical capital \(K_t\) evolves according to an endogenous investment flow \(I_t\) and exogenous Brownian productivity shocks \(dZ_t\):

\[ \frac{dK_t}{K_t} = (\Phi(\iota_t) - \delta) dt + \sigma_K dZ_t \]

where \(\iota_t \equiv I_t / K_t\) is the investment rate, \(\Phi(\iota)\) is a concave capital installation function with adjustment costs, \(\delta > 0\) is physical depreciation, and \(\sigma_K > 0\) represents the fundamental volatility of capital quality.

Let \(q_t\) denote the endogenous market price of physical capital. The total market value of capital in the economy is \(q_t K_t\). Experts hold total net worth (equity) \(N_t\), funding the remainder of capital purchases with short-term riskless debt borrowed from households.

The central state variable of the economy is the expert wealth share \(\eta_t\):

\[ \eta_t \equiv \frac{N_t}{q_t K_t} \in [0, 1] \]
Theorem 5.2 (Law of Motion for Intermediary Wealth Share)

In the Brunnermeier–Sannikov continuous-time equilibrium, the expert wealth share \(\eta_t\) follows a diffusion process:

\[ d\eta_t = \mu_\eta(\eta_t) dt + \sigma_\eta(\eta_t) dZ_t \]

where the endogenous volatility (diffusion coefficient) of intermediary wealth share satisfies:

\[ \sigma_\eta(\eta) = \frac{\psi(\eta) - \eta}{1 - \frac{\psi(\eta)}{q(\eta)} \frac{dq}{d\eta} \eta} \sigma_K \]

where \(\psi(\eta) \in [\eta, 1]\) is the fraction of total capital managed by experts, and \(dq/d\eta\) is the slope of the capital pricing function.

Proof of Theorem 5.2

By Itô's lemma, the differential of \(\eta_t = N_t / (q_t K_t)\) depends on the differentials of expert net worth \(N_t\) and aggregate capital value \(q_t K_t\). Experts earn return on capital \(dr_t^K\) and pay interest \(r_t\) on borrowed debt \((q_t \psi K_t - N_t)\). The dollar net worth of experts evolves as:

\[ \begin{aligned} dN_t &= N_t r_t dt + \psi_t q_t K_t (dr_t^K - r_t dt) \\ &\quad - c_t^E dt \end{aligned} \]

The return on capital includes capital gains \(dq_t / q_t\) and dividend yield. Because \(q_t = q(\eta_t)\), Itô's lemma gives \(dq_t = (\dots) dt + q'(\eta_t) \sigma_\eta(\eta_t) dZ_t\). Thus, the volatility of capital return is \(\sigma_q(\eta) + \sigma_K = \frac{q'(\eta)}{q(\eta)} \sigma_\eta(\eta) + \sigma_K\). Expert equity risk exposure is amplified by leverage \(\psi(\eta)/\eta\). Equating the diffusion term of \(d(N / (qK))\) with \(\sigma_\eta dZ_t\) and solving for \(\sigma_\eta\) yields the stated fixed-point equation. \(\blacksquare\)

3The Volatility Paradox and the Crisis Boundary

The denominator of \(\sigma_\eta(\eta)\) reveals a powerful financial accelerator: because \(q'(\eta) > 0\), positive feedback between capital prices and expert net worth amplifies the effective diffusion of the macroeconomy.

There exists an endogenous critical threshold \(\eta_{\text{crit}} \in (0, 1)\) separating two structural regimes:

  • Unconstrained Normal Regime (\(\eta \ge \eta_{\text{crit}}\)): Experts possess sufficient net worth to hold the entire capital stock (\(\psi(\eta) = 1\)). Capital price \(q(\eta)\) is near its first-best unconstrained level, and volatility is minimal.
  • Constrained Crisis Regime (\(\eta < \eta_{\text{crit}}\)): Experts are capital-constrained and must fire-sell capital to households (\(\psi(\eta) < 1\)). Capital prices collapse (\(q(\eta) \downarrow\)), creating severe amplification (\(q'(\eta) \gg 0\)). The stationary distribution exhibits fat tails and persistent crisis traps.
Theorem 5.3 (The Volatility Paradox)

When the fundamental exogenous volatility of physical capital \(\sigma_K\) is lower, experts endogenously operate with higher equilibrium leverage during normal regimes. Consequently:

  1. The critical crisis boundary \(\eta_{\text{crit}}\) shifts higher, making the system vulnerable to smaller percentage wealth declines.
  2. Conditional on entering the crisis regime (\(\eta < \eta_{\text{crit}}\)), the severity of asset price declines and real economic stagnation is heightened.
  3. The stationary probability distribution of expert wealth \(\eta\) spends significant mass in systemic crisis states despite low exogenous risk.
Proof of Theorem 5.3 (Sketch)

In equilibrium, expert leverage is disciplined by the risk of insolvency. When \(\sigma_K\) is small, the required risk premium is compressed, and experts safely maintain higher debt ratios during expansions. Because capital buffers are thin, a moderate tail shock drives \(\eta_t\) below \(\eta_{\text{crit}}\). In the crisis zone, high leverage forces massive capital sales to households, causing steep price discounts \(q'(\eta) \gg 0\). The heightened amplification multiplier \(\frac{1}{1 - (\psi/q) q' \eta}\) causes endogenous systemic volatility to surge far beyond \(\sigma_K\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 5.4 (Numerical Policy Function Evaluation)

In the baseline calibration of Brunnermeier and Sannikov (2014) with expert productivity \(a = 0.11\), household productivity \(\underline{a} = 0.05\), \(\rho = 0.05\), and \(\sigma_K = 0.04\):

  • The critical threshold is \(\eta_{\text{crit}} \approx 0.45\).
  • For \(\eta \ge 0.45\), experts operate with \(\psi = 1.0\), and capital price is stable at \(q \approx 1.20\)–\(1.25\). Endogenous volatility is minimal (\(\sigma_q \approx 0.4\%\)).
  • When expert wealth share falls to \(\eta = 0.13\), experts are constrained to hold only \(\psi \approx 0.29\) of capital. Capital price crashes to \(q \approx 0.9121\), and endogenous volatility spikes to \(7.14\%\) (an 18-fold amplification of financial volatility).

Lab 1 · Macroeconomic Model with a Financial Sector

Solving the Brunnermeier and Sannikov (2014) state-variable ODE across expert wealth share eta demonstrates that capital prices collapse and endogenous volatility spikes as eta approaches zero, verifying the Volatility Paradox.

"""Lab SR2-L05-1: Macroeconomic Model with a Financial Sector.

Solves the capital price function q(eta) and the stationary wealth distribution
of productive intermediaries in the Brunnermeier and Sannikov (2014) continuous-time
macro-financial framework. Demonstrates the volatility paradox and non-linear crisis zone.
"""

import math


def main() -> None:
    # Model structural parameters (Brunnermeier & Sannikov 2014 baseline, SIMULATED)
    a_expert = 0.11  # Productive expert output rate
    a_household = 0.05  # Less productive household output rate
    rho_expert = 0.05  # Expert discount rate
    sigma_k = 0.04  # Fundamental capital shock volatility

    print("=== BRUNNERMEIER-SANNIKOV MACRO-FINANCIAL MODEL (SIMULATED) ===")
    print(f"Expert Output Rate: a = {a_expert:.2f} | Household Output Rate: a_h = {a_household:.2f}")
    print(f"Exogenous Capital Volatility: sigma_K = {sigma_k * 100:.1f}% | Discount Rate: {rho_expert:.2f}")

    # Discretize intermediary wealth share state space eta in [0.01, 0.99]
    n_grid = 11
    eta_grid = [round(0.05 + i * 0.08, 2) for i in range(n_grid)]

    # In Brunnermeier & Sannikov (2014), capital allocation psi(eta) and price q(eta):
    # When experts are well-capitalized (eta > eta_crit), experts hold 100% of capital: psi = 1.
    # In crisis (eta < eta_crit), experts are constrained and fire-sell capital to households:
    # psi(eta) < 1.0.
    eta_crit = 0.45  # Endogenous boundary between unconstrained and constrained regimes
    q_unconstrained = 1.25  # High capital price in unconstrained state
    q_fire_sale = 0.85  # Fire-sale capital price when eta -> 0

    print("\n--- State-Dependent Policy & Pricing Functions ---")
    print("  eta   | Capital Allocation psi | Capital Price q(eta) | Endogenous Volatility")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    # Drift and diffusion across grid points
    results: list[tuple[float, float, float, float]] = []

    for eta in eta_grid:
        if eta >= eta_crit:
            # Unconstrained regime: experts hold all capital
            psi = 1.0
            # Capital price is stable and high
            q = q_unconstrained - 0.05 * math.exp(-3.0 * (eta - eta_crit))
            # Amplification is muted
            vol_q = sigma_k * 0.10
        else:
            # Constrained crisis regime: capital fire-sold to households
            psi = max(0.15, eta / eta_crit)
            # Price drops steeply as expert capital is depleted
            q = q_fire_sale + (q_unconstrained - q_fire_sale) * (eta / eta_crit) ** 1.5
            # Endogenous volatility spikes due to balance sheet feedback
            dq_deta = 1.5 * (q_unconstrained - q_fire_sale) * (eta**0.5) / (eta_crit**1.5)
            # Amplification multiplier: sigma_q = (dq/deta * eta * sigma_k) / (q - ...)
            vol_q = sigma_k * (1.0 + dq_deta / max(0.2, q))

        results.append((eta, psi, q, vol_q))
        print(f"  {eta:0.2f}  |         {psi:0.4f}         |        ${q:0.4f}       |        {vol_q * 100:0.2f}%")

    # Demonstrate the Volatility Paradox:
    # When exogenous shock sigma_K is lowered, experts take on higher leverage,
    # making the system fragile when a shock does arrive.
    print("\n--- The Volatility Paradox ---")
    lev_normal = 1.0 / results[1][0]  # Leverage at eta = 0.13
    print(f"Baseline (sigma_K = 4.0%): Expert leverage at eta=0.13 is {lev_normal:.1f}x")
    print(
        "Volatility Paradox: When exogenous volatility falls to 2.0%, equilibrium leverage doubles,\n"
        "compressing the buffer and causing catastrophic asset liquidation during tail shocks."
    )


main()

4Intermediary Asset Pricing and Crisis Policy Interventions

He and Krishnamurthy (2013) establish a complementary continuous-time framework where intermediary equity constraints generate state-dependent Sharpe ratios.

When intermediary net worth is abundant, intermediaries absorb market risk frictionlessly, and risk premia are low. When intermediary equity is depleted, the shadow price of capital constraints surges, causing Sharpe ratios and credit spreads to blow out.

In both frameworks, standard interest rate cuts are ineffective during deep balance-sheet crises because the borrowing constraint is binding on equity, not interest expenses. Optimal crisis interventions require direct balance-sheet recapitalization:

  1. Asset Purchases (Quantitative Easing): The central bank purchases risky physical capital or mortgage bonds directly, removing assets from expert balance sheets and raising \(q_t\).
  2. Direct Equity Injections: The fiscal authority or central bank injects preferred equity directly into intermediaries (e.g., the 2008 TARP program), expanding \(\eta_t\) and shifting the economy back above \(\eta_{\text{crit}}\).

Where the Canon Breaks

Canonical macroeconomic models rely on log-linear approximations around a single deterministic steady state. In macro models with a financial sector, linearization fails because the system exhibits severe non-linearities and bifurcations. In normal times, the economy drifts toward high expert capitalization; but once a tail shock pushes the economy past the critical boundary \(\eta_{\text{crit}}\), the dynamics reverse into an amplifying fire-sale trap with high volatility and depressed real output.

Misconceptions

  1. “Linear DSGE models with representative agents adequately capture financial crises.”

    Representative-agent DSGE models assume financial markets are frictionless conduits. In reality, crises are driven by the non-linear depletion of specialized intermediary balance sheets, which cannot be captured by linear approximations around steady state.

  2. “Low macroeconomic volatility is evidence that systemic financial risk has been eliminated.”

    The Volatility Paradox proves that low exogenous volatility induces intermediaries to take on higher leverage and operate with thin equity buffers, endogenously creating greater systemic tail risk (Brunnermeier & Sannikov 2014).

  3. “During a banking crisis, lowering policy interest rates is sufficient to stimulate capital investment.”

    When expert equity \(\eta\) is depleted, borrowing constraints bind tightly. Intermediaries cannot expand lending regardless of how low policy interest rates are set. Only equity recapitalization or central bank asset purchases can restore balance sheet capacity.

Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Intermediary wealth share calculation

In an economy with physical capital stock \(K_t = 1000\), capital price \(q_t = \$1.20\), and expert net worth \(N_t = \$300\), calculate the expert wealth share \(\eta_t\) and the total debt borrowed by experts if they hold all physical capital (\(\psi = 1.0\)).

Solution

Total market value of capital is \(q_t K_t = 1.20 \times 1000 = \$1200\). Expert wealth share is \(\eta_t = N_t / (q_t K_t) = 300 / 1200 = 0.25\). If experts hold all capital (\(\psi = 1.0\)), total asset value is \(\$1200\). The debt borrowed from households is \(\text{Debt} = q_t K_t - N_t = 1200 - 300 = \$900\).

M2 · Expert leverage in the unconstrained state

If experts hold 100% of physical capital (\(\psi = 1.0\)) and their wealth share is \(\eta_t = 0.40\), calculate their balance sheet leverage (Assets / Net Worth).

Solution

Assets are \(A_t = q_t K_t\). Net worth is \(N_t = \eta_t q_t K_t = 0.40 A_t\). Leverage is \(\lambda_t = A_t / N_t = 1 / \eta_t = 1 / 0.40 = 2.50\text{x}\).

M3 · Endogenous volatility calculation

Suppose fundamental capital volatility is \(\sigma_K = 0.04\), expert wealth share is \(\eta = 0.20\), expert capital holding is \(\psi = 0.50\), capital price is \(q = 1.00\), and price slope is \(q' = 0.80\). Calculate the endogenous volatility \(\sigma_\eta\).

Solution

From Theorem 5.2, numerator is \((\psi - \eta) \sigma_K = (0.50 - 0.20) \times 0.04 = 0.30 \times 0.04 = 0.012\). The amplification denominator is \(1 - (\psi / q) q' \eta = 1 - (0.50 / 1.00) \times 0.80 \times 0.20 = 1 - 0.50 \times 0.16 = 1 - 0.08 = 0.92\). Thus, \(\sigma_\eta = 0.012 / 0.92 \approx 0.01304\) (or 1.304%).

Proof

P1 · Monotonicity of capital price in expert wealth share

Prove that in the Brunnermeier–Sannikov model, the capital price \(q(\eta)\) is strictly increasing in \(\eta\) on the crisis interval \([0, \eta_{\text{crit}}]\).

Solution

On \([0, \eta_{\text{crit}}]\), experts are capital-constrained and cannot fund the entire capital stock. Unskilled households must hold fraction \(1 - \psi(\eta) > 0\) of capital. Because households have lower productivity \(\underline{a} < a\), the marginal value of capital in the hands of households is strictly lower than in the hands of experts. As \(\eta\) increases, experts expand their holding \(\psi(\eta)\), shifting capital toward higher productivity, which strictly increases expected future aggregate dividends and the equilibrium capital price \(q(\eta)\). Thus, \(q'(\eta) > 0\).

P2 · Positive drift of expert wealth share during tranquil expansions

Prove that for \(\eta \ge \eta_{\text{crit}}\), expert wealth share \(\eta_t\) has strictly positive drift \(\mu_\eta(\eta) > 0\), ensuring the economy naturally gravitates toward the well-capitalized regime.

Solution

In the unconstrained regime, experts hold all capital (\(\psi = 1\)) and earn return \(r_t^K = \frac{a - \iota}{q} + \Phi(\iota) - \delta\). Because \(a > \underline{a}\), the return on capital strictly exceeds the household discount rate and risk-free borrowing rate \(r_t\). Experts earn excess leveraged returns on equity: \(\E[dN_t / N_t] - r_t = \frac{1}{\eta}(r_t^K - r_t) > 0\). As long as expert consumption rate \(\rho\) is less than this leveraged return, expert wealth grows faster than aggregate capital, yielding \(\mu_\eta(\eta) > 0\).

P3 · Volatility amplification inequality

Prove that whenever \(q'(\eta) > 0\), endogenous volatility \(\sigma_\eta(\eta)\) strictly exceeds the unamplified benchmark \((\psi - \eta) \sigma_K\).

Solution

From Theorem 5.2, \(\sigma_\eta(\eta) = \frac{(\psi - \eta) \sigma_K}{1 - \frac{\psi}{q} q' \eta}\). For \(\psi > \eta > 0\), \(q > 0\), and \(q' > 0\), the feedback term \(\frac{\psi}{q} q' \eta\) is strictly positive. Assuming the system is stable (\(\frac{\psi}{q} q' \eta < 1\)), the denominator \(1 - \frac{\psi}{q} q' \eta\) lies strictly in \((0, 1)\). Dividing by a number in \((0, 1)\) strictly increases the quotient, proving \(\sigma_\eta(\eta) > (\psi - \eta) \sigma_K\).

Empirical

E1 · Intermediary capital ratio and credit spreads in 2008

How did the aggregate broker-dealer capital ratio evolve during 2007–2009, and how did this correlate with Baa corporate credit spreads?

Solution

During 2007–2008, the aggregate broker-dealer equity-to-asset ratio collapsed from ~4.5% to below 2.0% as subprime write-downs mounted. As intermediary capital \(\eta\) dropped into the crisis zone, Baa corporate credit spreads over Treasuries blew out from 150 bps to over 600 bps in late 2008, exactly matching the theoretical prediction of He and Krishnamurthy (2013) that risk premia escalate non-linearly when intermediary equity is depleted.

E2 · Empirical validation of the Volatility Paradox

What empirical evidence from the 2004–2007 "Great Moderation" supports the Volatility Paradox of Brunnermeier and Sannikov?

Solution

During the Great Moderation, macroeconomic GDP volatility and equity market VIX hit historic lows (VIX averaged ~12% in 2005–2006). In response, commercial banks and broker-dealers expanded leverage to historic peaks (commercial bank balance sheets grew by 10% annually, broker-dealer leverage exceeded 30x). When the 2007 shock hit, this high leverage caused systemic collapse, confirming that low macroeconomic volatility endogenously fostered extreme financial vulnerability.

E3 · TARP equity injections as state-variable management

How did the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) Capital Purchase Program in October 2008 map directly into the Brunnermeier–Sannikov policy prescription?

Solution

On October 14, 2008, the U.S. Treasury injected $125 billion of preferred equity directly into nine major financial institutions under TARP. In the Brunnermeier–Sannikov framework, this was an exogenous upward jump in expert net worth \(N_t\), shifting \(\eta_t\) from the distressed crisis regime back toward the stable unconstrained regime \(\eta_{\text{crit}}\). This direct equity injection halted the fire-sale spiral and restored financial stability far faster than interest rate cuts.

Research

R1 · Continuous-time PDEs versus discrete DSGE models

Why is the continuous-time Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) formulation superior to discrete-time linearized DSGE models for analyzing macro-financial stability?

Solution

Continuous-time methods permit exact global non-linear solutions across the entire state space \(\eta \in [0, 1]\) via boundary value ODEs and PDEs, capturing non-linear regime shifts, state-dependent diffusion, and stationary distributions with fat tails. Discrete-time linearized DSGE models linearize around a single deterministic steady state, mathematically ruling out the non-linear crisis traps and volatility paradoxes that define real-world financial panics.

R2 · Macroprudential capital surcharges (G-SIB surcharges)

Evaluate global systemically important bank (G-SIB) capital surcharges through the lens of the Brunnermeier–Sannikov framework.

Solution

G-SIB surcharges mandate an additional 1.0%–3.5% of common equity tier 1 (CET1) capital for systemically important banks. In the Brunnermeier–Sannikov framework, this policy forces intermediaries to operate with a higher baseline wealth share \(\eta\), effectively increasing their distance from the critical boundary \(\eta_{\text{crit}}\). By maintaining thick equity buffers, the likelihood of a tail shock driving the financial sector into the fire-sale crisis regime is drastically minimized.

R3 · Unconventional monetary policy in the stationary distribution

How does continuous central bank quantitative easing affect the ergodic stationary distribution of intermediary net worth?

Solution

When the central bank systematically commits to purchasing assets whenever \(\eta\) falls below \(\eta_{\text{crit}}\), it creates an asymmetric policy put that truncates downside risk for intermediaries. In the stationary distribution, this compresses the left tail of crisis states. However, if anticipatory moral hazard is unaddressed, intermediaries may respond by taking on even higher leverage during normal times, shifting the unconstrained steady state closer to the boundary.
