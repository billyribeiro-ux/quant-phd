Procyclical Leverage & Mark-to-Market Accounting

  • Prerequisites: maturity transformation (BK1), fire sale (SR1), intermediary asset pricing (AP6)
  • Introduces: procyclical leverage
  • Ttheorem (deductive mathematical result)
  • Aempirically replicated finding
  • Bpublished empirical finding, not fully settled
  • Cworking paper, practitioner research or single sample
  • Dpractitioner lore
  • Xrefuted claim

In classical macroeconomics, financial intermediaries are viewed as passive conduits channeling savings into productive capital. However, empirical evidence reveals that security broker-dealers actively manage their balance sheets to maintain target leverage ratios or Value-at-Risk boundaries. When asset prices rise, mark-to-market accounting generates surplus equity capital. Rather than holding this surplus, intermediaries issue new short-term collateralized debt to purchase additional securities, driving asset prices higher and compressing risk premia. In downturns, this feedback loop reverses violently: falling prices erode equity, forcing immediate asset liquidations that transmit systemic distress across the entire financial system.

1Balance Sheet Management Across Institution Types

Consider the fundamental balance sheet accounting identity for any economic entity at date \(t\):

\[ A_t = D_t + E_t \]

where \(A_t\) represents total mark-to-market assets, \(D_t\) denotes total debt liabilities, and \(E_t\) is net worth (equity capital). Leverage \(\lambda_t\) is defined as the ratio of total assets to equity:

\[ \lambda_t \equiv \frac{A_t}{E_t} = \frac{A_t}{A_t - D_t} \]

Adrian and Shin (2010) show that empirical leverage management differs fundamentally across three institutional categories:

  • Households and non-financial corporations: Liabilities \(D_t\) are sticky in the short run (e.g., fixed mortgages or corporate bonds). When housing or corporate asset values \(A_t\) rise by \(\Delta A > 0\), equity absorbs the full gain: \(\Delta E = \Delta A\). Consequently, leverage \(\lambda_t = (A_0 + \Delta A) / (E_0 + \Delta A)\) declines. Household leverage is countercyclical.
  • Commercial banks: Regulatory capital requirements (such as Basel capital ratios) impose minimum equity-to-asset ratios. Commercial banks target a relatively constant leverage ratio over the credit cycle, expanding debt in proportion to equity gains.
  • Security broker-dealers and shadow banks: Wall Street investment banks (including the historical major broker-dealers Bear Stearns, Lehman Brothers, Merrill Lynch, Morgan Stanley, and Goldman Sachs) actively adjust debt and assets, resulting in leverage that expands alongside total asset size.
Definition 1.1 (Procyclical leverage)

Procyclical leverage is the property of financial intermediary balance sheet management whereby leverage \(\lambda_t \equiv A_t / E_t\) is positively correlated with total asset growth \(\Delta A_t / A_t\), such that intermediaries actively expand debt and leverage during asset price booms and contract leverage during downturns.

In quarterly regressions of asset growth on leverage growth for U.S. broker-dealers over 1990–2008, Adrian and Shin find a regression slope of 1.41 (t-statistic = 4.79), whereas the slope for commercial banks is close to zero (confirming constant leverage targeting).

2Mark-to-Market Accounting and Asset Purchase Multipliers

To analyze the transmission mechanism of mark-to-market accounting, consider an intermediary operating at an initial target leverage \(\lambda^* = A_0 / E_0\). Suppose asset prices increase exogenously by \(\Delta A > 0\) per unit of initial assets.

Theorem 1.2 (Adrian–Shin Balance Sheet Expansion Identity)

Suppose an intermediary holds assets \(A_0\) funded by debt \(D_0\) and equity \(E_0\), with initial leverage \(\lambda^* = A_0 / E_0\). If an asset price gain increases balance-sheet value by \(\Delta A > 0\), and the intermediary restores its target leverage \(\lambda^*\), the total value of newly purchased assets \(\Delta A_{\text{new}}\) and newly issued debt \(\Delta D\) satisfy:

\[ \Delta A_{\text{new}} = (\lambda^* - 1) \Delta A, \quad \Delta D = (\lambda^* - 1) \Delta A \]

The total expanded balance-sheet asset size is \(A_1 = A_0 + \lambda^* \Delta A\).

Proof of Theorem 1.2

Under mark-to-market accounting, short-term debt liabilities \(D_0\) do not revalue. Therefore, the change in equity capital absorbs the entire asset price gain:

\[ E_1 = E_0 + \Delta A \]

If the intermediary maintains its target leverage ratio \(\lambda^*\), its target asset size must satisfy:

\[ A_1 = \lambda^* E_1 = \lambda^* (E_0 + \Delta A) = \lambda^* E_0 + \lambda^* \Delta A = A_0 + \lambda^* \Delta A \]

The total balance sheet before new asset purchases was \(A_0 + \Delta A\). Hence, the required purchases of additional assets \(\Delta A_{\text{new}}\) are:

\[ \Delta A_{\text{new}} = A_1 - (A_0 + \Delta A) = (A_0 + \lambda^* \Delta A) - (A_0 + \Delta A) = (\lambda^* - 1) \Delta A \]

From the balance sheet identity \(A_1 = D_1 + E_1\), the new total debt is:

\[ D_1 = A_1 - E_1 = (A_0 + \lambda^* \Delta A) - (E_0 + \Delta A) = (A_0 - E_0) + (\lambda^* - 1) \Delta A = D_0 + (\lambda^* - 1) \Delta A \]

Thus, new debt issued is \(\Delta D = (\lambda^* - 1) \Delta A\). This completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.3 (Numerical Balance Sheet Expansion)

Consider a broker-dealer with \(A_0 = \$100\text{M}\), \(E_0 = \$10\text{M}\), \(D_0 = \$90\text{M}\), and target leverage \(\lambda^* = 10\). Suppose asset values appreciate by 1%, so \(\Delta A = \$1\text{M}\).

  • Immediate mark-to-market equity: \(E_1 = \$10\text{M} + \$1\text{M} = \$11\text{M}\).
  • Unadjusted leverage drops: \(\lambda_{\text{unadjusted}} = \$101\text{M} / \$11\text{M} = 9.1818\).
  • Target asset size: \(A_1 = 10 \times \$11\text{M} = \$110\text{M}\).
  • New asset purchases: \(\Delta A_{\text{new}} = \$110\text{M} - \$101\text{M} = \$9\text{M}\).
  • New debt issued: \(\Delta D = \$99\text{M} - \$90\text{M} = \$9\text{M}\).

A $1M equity gain generates $9M of net asset buying pressure in the market. In reverse, a 1% price decline (\(\Delta A = -\$1\text{M}\)) forces \(\$9\text{M}\) of forced asset sales to restore target leverage.

3Value-at-Risk Constraints and Endogenous Volatility

Why do intermediaries target higher leverage during booms rather than keeping leverage constant? Adrian and Shin (2010) show that economic capital allocation based on Value-at-Risk (VaR) endogenously dictates procyclical leverage.

Theorem 1.4 (VaR-Constrained Leverage)

Let an intermediary hold assets \(A_t\) with per-period return standard deviation \(\sigma_t\). Under a Value-at-Risk capital rule at confidence level \(1 - \alpha\), regulatory capital must cover extreme losses over a horizon: \(\text{VaR}_t = Z_{1-\alpha} \sigma_t A_t\), where \(Z_{1-\alpha}\) is the standard normal critical value. If the bank holds economic equity \(E_t = \text{VaR}_t\), permissible leverage satisfies:

\[ \lambda_t = \frac{A_t}{E_t} = \frac{1}{Z_{1-\alpha} \sigma_t} \]

Consequently, leverage is inversely proportional to measured asset volatility \(\sigma_t\):

\[ \frac{\partial \lambda_t}{\partial \sigma_t} = -\frac{1}{Z_{1-\alpha} \sigma_t^2} < 0 \]
Proof of Theorem 1.4

The Value-at-Risk at confidence \(1 - \alpha\) represents the maximal dollar loss that occurs with probability at most \(\alpha\). Assuming returns \(r_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma_t^2)\), dollar losses are bounded by \(Z_{1-\alpha} \sigma_t A_t\). Setting equity equal to capital requirement \(E_t = Z_{1-\alpha} \sigma_t A_t\), dividing both sides by \(E_t\) yields \(1 = Z_{1-\alpha} \sigma_t \lambda_t\), from which \(\lambda_t = 1 / (Z_{1-\alpha} \sigma_t)\). Differentiating with respect to \(\sigma_t\) yields \(-1 / (Z_{1-\alpha} \sigma_t^2) < 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Because measured financial market volatility is strongly countercyclical (low during expansions and high during crises), the VaR constraint mechanically generates procyclical leverage:

  1. During tranquil economic expansions, volatility \(\sigma_t\) falls, relaxing the VaR constraint and permitting intermediaries to lever up.
  2. When volatility spikes during market panics, \(\sigma_t\) surges, forcing immediate deleveraging and asset liquidations even if accounting losses have not yet materialized.

Adrian, Etula, and Muir (2014) demonstrate that shocks to the leverage of security broker-dealers price the cross-section of equity and bond returns, confirming that broker-dealer balance sheet capacity is the marginal pricing kernel of financial markets.

Lab 1 · Procyclical Leverage Simulation

Simulating balance sheet adjustments under countercyclical, constant, and procyclical VaR leverage rules demonstrates that broker-dealers amplify asset price shocks through procyclical asset purchases in booms and severe fire-sale liquidations in downturns.

"""Lab SR2-L01-1: Procyclical Leverage and Mark-to-Market Accounting.

Simulates intermediary balance sheet adjustments under countercyclical (household),
constant (commercial bank), and procyclical VaR (broker-dealer) leverage rules
following Adrian and Shin (2010).
"""


def main() -> None:
    # Initial balance sheet (SIMULATED broker-dealer)
    a0 = 100.0  # Initial assets
    e0 = 10.0  # Initial equity
    d0 = a0 - e0  # Initial debt = 90.0
    lev0 = a0 / e0  # Initial leverage = 10.0

    print("=== INITIAL BALANCE SHEET (SIMULATED) ===")
    print(f"Assets: ${a0:.2f}M | Debt: ${d0:.2f}M | Equity: ${e0:.2f}M | Leverage: {lev0:.2f}x")

    # Scenario 1: Asset appreciation (+1% shock, +$1.0M)
    delta_a_price = 1.0
    e1 = e0 + delta_a_price  # Equity absorbs the full mark-to-market gain = 11.0
    a1_unadjusted = a0 + delta_a_price  # 101.0

    # Rule A: Passive debt (countercyclical leverage, typical household/corporate)
    lev_passive = a1_unadjusted / e1
    print("\n--- Boom (+1% Asset Price Shock): Passive Debt ---")
    print(f"Assets: ${a1_unadjusted:.2f}M | Equity: ${e1:.2f}M | Leverage: {lev_passive:.4f}x (fell from {lev0:.2f}x)")

    # Rule B: Constant leverage targeting (L* = 10.0, typical commercial bank)
    target_lev_b = 10.0
    a1_target_b = target_lev_b * e1  # 110.0
    purchases_b = a1_target_b - a1_unadjusted  # 9.0
    d1_b = a1_target_b - e1  # 99.0
    print("\n--- Boom (+1% Asset Price Shock): Constant Leverage (L*=10.0) ---")
    print(
        f"Target Assets: ${a1_target_b:.2f}M | Additional Debt: ${d1_b - d0:.2f}M | "
        f"Asset Purchases: ${purchases_b:.2f}M"
    )

    # Rule C: Procyclical VaR leverage (broker-dealer; volatility falls from 2.0% to 1.5%)
    alpha_z = 5.0
    vol_boom = 0.015
    target_lev_c = 1.0 / (alpha_z * vol_boom)  # 1 / 0.075 = 13.3333
    a1_target_c = target_lev_c * e1  # 146.6667
    purchases_c = a1_target_c - a1_unadjusted  # 45.6667
    d1_c = a1_target_c - e1  # 135.6667
    print("\n--- Boom (+1% Asset Price Shock): Procyclical VaR Leverage ---")
    print(
        f"Target Leverage: {target_lev_c:.4f}x | Target Assets: ${a1_target_c:.2f}M | "
        f"Additional Debt: ${d1_c - d0:.2f}M | Asset Purchases: ${purchases_c:.2f}M"
    )

    # Scenario 2: Asset downturn (-1% shock, -$1.0M) and volatility spike to 3.0%
    delta_a_down = -1.0
    e2 = e0 + delta_a_down  # Equity falls to 9.0
    a2_unadjusted = a0 + delta_a_down  # 99.0
    vol_bust = 0.03
    target_lev_bust = 1.0 / (alpha_z * vol_bust)  # 1 / 0.15 = 6.6667
    a2_target_bust = target_lev_bust * e2  # 60.0
    forced_sales = a2_unadjusted - a2_target_bust  # 99.0 - 60.0 = 39.0
    d2_bust = a2_target_bust - e2  # 51.0
    debt_repaid = d0 - d2_bust  # 90.0 - 51.0 = 39.0

    print("\n--- Downturn (-1% Asset Price Shock & Volatility Spike to 3.0%): Procyclical VaR ---")
    print(
        f"Target Leverage: {target_lev_bust:.4f}x | Target Assets: ${a2_target_bust:.2f}M | "
        f"Forced Fire Sales: ${forced_sales:.2f}M | Debt Runoff: ${debt_repaid:.2f}M"
    )


main()

Under a passive debt rule, leverage drops to 9.18x following a positive price shock. Under constant leverage targeting, the bank purchases $9M of assets. Under procyclical VaR targeting where volatility drops from 2.0% to 1.5%, leverage expands to 13.33x, driving $45.67M of new asset purchases. In a downturn accompanied by a volatility spike to 3.0%, the firm must liquidate $39.0M of assets (39% of initial balance sheet), transmitting severe fire-sale externalities.

Where the Canon Breaks

Traditional macroeconomic models built on the Modigliani–Miller theorem assume that financial structure is irrelevant: firms and intermediaries can always raise equity or issue debt at the risk-free rate plus a frictionless risk premium. In reality, modern financial intermediaries rely heavily on short-term collateralized wholesale funding (such as repurchase agreements). When market volatility increases, the canonical assumption of constant credit supply breaks down: haircuts widen endogenously, VaR constraints bind simultaneously across all dealers, and liquidity evaporates exactly when it is most needed.

Misconceptions

  1. “High leverage during credit booms is driven by debt inertia and sticky liabilities.”

    Non-financial corporate leverage is countercyclical because debt is sticky. In contrast, financial intermediary balance sheet expansion is completely active: broker-dealers actively take on massive short-term repo debt to purchase securities as their net worth expands (Adrian & Shin 2010).

  2. “Commercial banks and broker-dealers manage balance sheet leverage identically.”

    Commercial banks face strict regulatory capital requirements tied to risk-weighted assets, targeting relatively constant leverage. Broker-dealers historically managed capital against internal Value-at-Risk limits, generating extreme procyclical leverage swings.

  3. “Mark-to-market accounting is always stabilizing because it reflects true economic values.”

    While mark-to-market accounting provides transparency, it creates powerful systemic feedback loops: asset gains generate artificial equity that fuels further debt-fueled purchases, while temporary price declines trigger immediate forced sales that depress prices further.

Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Balance sheet adjustment under constant leverage

A broker-dealer holds \(A_0 = \$200\text{M}\) of assets funded by \(E_0 = \$20\text{M}\) of equity and \(D_0 = \$180\text{M}\) of debt, with target leverage \(\lambda^* = 10\). If an asset price gain increases asset value by 2% (\(\Delta A = \$4\text{M}\)), calculate the new equity, target asset size, and new debt issued.

Solution

Under mark-to-market accounting, equity absorbs the full gain: \(E_1 = \$20\text{M} + \$4\text{M} = \$24\text{M}\). Target asset size is \(A_1 = \lambda^* E_1 = 10 \times \$24\text{M} = \$240\text{M}\). The pre-adjustment balance sheet is \(A_0 + \Delta A = \$204\text{M}\). Hence, new asset purchases are \(\Delta A_{\text{new}} = \$240\text{M} - \$204\text{M} = \$36\text{M}\). New debt issued is \(\Delta D = \$240\text{M} - \$24\text{M} - \$180\text{M} = \$36\text{M}\).

M2 · Value-at-Risk leverage calculation

An intermediary allocates economic capital such that \(E_t \ge 2.33 \cdot \sigma_t \cdot A_t\) (corresponding to a 99% one-tailed VaR). If measured volatility \(\sigma_t\) drops from 3.0% to 1.5% during an economic expansion, calculate the initial and expanded permissible leverage ratios.

Solution

Permissible leverage is \(\lambda_t = 1 / (2.33 \cdot \sigma_t)\). For \(\sigma_0 = 0.03\): \(\lambda_0 = 1 / (2.33 \times 0.03) = 1 / 0.0699 \approx 14.306x\). For \(\sigma_1 = 0.015\): \(\lambda_1 = 1 / (2.33 \times 0.015) = 1 / 0.03495 \approx 28.612x\). Permissible leverage exactly doubles when volatility is halved.

M3 · Forced liquidation in a downturn

A leveraged fund with \(A_0 = \$500\text{M}\), \(E_0 = \$25\text{M}\), and \(\lambda_0 = 20\text{x}\) experiences a 2% asset price decline (\(\Delta A = -\$10\text{M}\)). If the fund is forced to restore its initial leverage of 20x, calculate the dollar volume of assets it must liquidate.

Solution

Post-shock equity is \(E_1 = \$25\text{M} - \$10\text{M} = \$15\text{M}\). Target asset size is \(A_1 = 20 \times \$15\text{M} = \$300\text{M}\). The post-shock asset size before liquidation is \(\$500\text{M} - \$10\text{M} = \$490\text{M}\). The required forced asset liquidation is \(\Delta A_{\text{liquidate}} = \$490\text{M} - \$300\text{M} = \$190\text{M}\).

Proof

P1 · General balance sheet purchase multiplier

Prove that if an intermediary targets a leverage function \(\lambda(A) = c A^\gamma\) with \(\gamma > 0\), a small percentage asset price change \(da / A\) induces an asset purchase multiplier strictly exceeding unity.

Solution

From \(E = A / \lambda(A) = A^{1-\gamma} / c\), taking total differentials yields \(dE = \frac{1-\gamma}{c} A^{-\gamma} dA\). Under mark-to-market accounting, \(dE = dA_{\text{price}}\). When \(\gamma > 0\), \(\lambda\) increases with asset size, which implies \(dA / A > dE / E\). Expanding total assets requires active debt issuance \(dD > 0\), ensuring that total asset expansion strictly exceeds the initial price shock.

P2 · Countercyclical leverage for passive debt

Prove that if an economic entity keeps debt liabilities strictly constant (\(D_t = D\)), its leverage \(\lambda_t\) is a strictly decreasing function of asset value \(A_t\) for all \(A_t > D\).

Solution

With constant debt \(D\), equity is \(E_t = A_t - D\). Leverage is \(\lambda(A_t) = \frac{A_t}{A_t - D}\). Taking the first derivative with respect to \(A_t\): \(\frac{d\lambda}{dA_t} = \frac{(A_t - D)(1) - A_t(1)}{(A_t - D)^2} = -\frac{D}{(A_t - D)^2}\). Since \(D > 0\) and \((A_t - D)^2 > 0\), \(\frac{d\lambda}{dA_t} < 0\) everywhere on \(A_t > D\). Thus, leverage is strictly countercyclical.

P3 · Volatility elasticity of VaR leverage

Prove that the elasticity of permissible leverage with respect to measured volatility under a VaR capital rule is identically equal to \(-1\).

Solution

Under \(\lambda = \frac{1}{Z \sigma}\), the elasticity is defined as \(\epsilon_{\lambda, \sigma} \equiv \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial \sigma} \frac{\sigma}{\lambda}\). Differentiating yields \(\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial \sigma} = -\frac{1}{Z \sigma^2}\). Substituting into the elasticity formula gives \(\epsilon_{\lambda, \sigma} = \left(-\frac{1}{Z \sigma^2}\right) \frac{\sigma}{1 / (Z \sigma)} = -\frac{\sigma}{Z \sigma^2} \cdot Z \sigma = -1\). The elasticity is identically \(-1\).

Empirical

E1 · Adrian–Shin regression specification

Explain the regression specification used by Adrian and Shin (2010, Table 1) to test procyclical leverage, and interpret why a regression slope greater than 1 implies procyclical leverage.

Solution

Adrian and Shin estimate \(\Delta \ln(\text{Assets}_t) = \alpha + \beta \Delta \ln(\text{Leverage}_t) + \varepsilon_t\) (or equivalently regressing leverage growth on asset growth). Since \(\text{Assets} = \text{Leverage} \times \text{Equity}\), \(\Delta \ln A = \Delta \ln \lambda + \Delta \ln E\). If leverage is passive (countercyclical), \(\Delta \ln \lambda\) and \(\Delta \ln A\) have negative covariance, so \(\beta < 0\). If leverage is constant, \(\Delta \ln \lambda = 0\). A positive slope \(\beta > 1\) demonstrates that leverage expands even faster than equity during balance sheet expansions, proving procyclicality.

E2 · Commercial banks versus broker-dealers in the 2008 crisis

Why did U.S. commercial bank leverage remain relatively flat between 2000 and 2007 while the leverage of the top five independent investment banks expanded to over 30x?

Solution

Commercial banks were constrained by regulatory Basel I/II leverage caps and Prompt Corrective Action minimums. Broker-dealers were regulated under the SEC's 2004 Consolidated Supervised Entities (CSE) program, which permitted them to use internal VaR models to determine capital requirements. In the low-volatility environment of 2004–2007, internal VaR models indicated negligible risk, allowing investment banks to expand leverage from ~20x to ~33x.

E3 · Repo haircut dynamics as leverage shocks

How does a sudden increase in collateral haircuts in the bilateral repo market directly constrain broker-dealer leverage?

Solution

If a security carries a repo haircut of \(h\), an institution can borrow \(1 - h\) per dollar of collateral. Maximum leverage is \(\lambda_{\max} = 1 / h\). When haircuts on non-agency mortgage collateral jumped from 2% in early 2007 to 50% in late 2008, maximum permissible leverage collapsed from 50x to 2x, forcing immediate balance sheet contractions regardless of accounting solvency.

Research

R1 · Intermediary leverage as an asset pricing factor

How do Adrian, Etula, and Muir (2014) construct their intermediary leverage factor, and why does it price the cross-section of value, momentum, and bond portfolios?

Solution

Adrian, Etula, and Muir (2014) measure the seasonally adjusted broker-dealer leverage growth from the Federal Reserve's Flow of Funds. Because broker-dealers are marginal investors across multiple asset classes, their balance sheet capacity dictates aggregate risk tolerance. When broker-dealer leverage is high, intermediary marginal utility of wealth is low; when leverage collapses, marginal utility spikes. The single broker-dealer leverage factor successfully prices the cross-section of 25 Fama–French portfolios, momentum portfolios, and Treasury bonds with high \(R^2\).

R2 · Endogenous risk and the volatility paradox

Explain why a regulatory policy that successfully dampens high-frequency market volatility might endogenously create greater systemic risk in a financial system governed by VaR capital constraints.

Solution

When market volatility is suppressed, VaR constraints relax across the financial sector, inducing intermediaries to accumulate massive leverage and concentrated asset positions. While high-frequency volatility appears minimal, systemic fragility compounds endogenously. When an unexpected shock eventually breaches risk limits, the resulting deleveraging is catastrophic. This phenomenon is known as the Volatility Paradox.

R3 · Macroprudential countercyclical leverage caps

How can countercyclical capital buffers (CCyB) or leverage ratio floors counteract the procyclicality documented by Adrian and Shin?

Solution

Under countercyclical capital regulation, regulators increase required capital buffers (or lower permissible leverage caps) during macroeconomic credit expansions. By forcing intermediaries to hold higher equity capital during booms, the policy prevents balance sheets from expanding excessively when measured volatility is artificially low. In downturns, regulators release the buffer, permitting intermediaries to absorb losses without triggering forced fire-sale cascades.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What distinguishes the leverage management of security broker-dealers from that of non-financial corporations?

    Options

    Adrian and Shin (2010) establish that broker-dealer leverage is procyclical while non-financial corporate leverage is countercyclical.

  2. If a firm maintains a constant leverage target of \(L^* = 10\), how many dollars of new assets does it purchase following a $1 mark-to-market equity gain?

    \(\Delta A_{\text{new}} = (L^* - 1) \Delta A = (10 - 1) \times 1 = \$9\) (Theorem 1.2).

  3. Under a Value-at-Risk capital constraint, what is the mathematical relationship between permissible leverage \(\lambda\) and asset volatility \(\sigma\)?

    Options

    From Theorem 1.4, \(\lambda = 1 / (Z \sigma)\), so leverage is strictly inversely proportional to volatility.

  4. In Lab 1, if \(\alpha Z = 5.0\) and volatility drops to \(\sigma = 0.015\) during an economic boom, what is the target VaR leverage ratio?

    \(L^* = 1 / (5.0 \times 0.015) = 1 / 0.075 = 13.3333\) (Lab 1).

  5. In Lab 1, if volatility spikes to \(\sigma = 0.030\) during a crisis, what is the compressed target VaR leverage ratio?

    \(L^* = 1 / (5.0 \times 0.030) = 1 / 0.15 = 6.6667\) (Lab 1).

  6. Why does mark-to-market accounting amplify fire-sale externalities during downturns?

    Options

    Mark-to-market accounting requires immediate recognition of asset price declines, directly reducing equity and forcing asset liquidations to restore leverage targets.

  7. In Lab 1, what dollar volume of assets ($M) must the broker-dealer fire-sell when asset prices fall by 1% and volatility spikes to 3%?

    Unadjusted assets fall to $99M and equity to $9M. Target assets are \(6.6667 \times \$9\text{M} = \$60\text{M}\). Forced sales equal \(\$99\text{M} - \$60\text{M} = \$39.0\text{M}\) (Lab 1).

  8. What empirical finding does Adrian, Etula, and Muir (2014) report regarding broker-dealer leverage?

    Options

    Adrian, Etula, and Muir (2014) demonstrate that broker-dealer leverage growth prices cross-sectional returns with high empirical explanatory power.
