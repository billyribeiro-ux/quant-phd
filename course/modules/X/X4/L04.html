Stablecoin Mechanics, Reserve Backing & Peg Run Dynamics

Stablecoins serve as the transactional monetary base of the digital asset ecosystem, promising instantaneous par redemption at one US dollar. However, because issuers invest fiat reserves into interest-bearing Treasuries and uninsured commercial bank deposits, their balance sheets replicate the classic vulnerabilities of shadow banking: liquidity and maturity transformation without deposit insurance or central bank discount window access. When interest rate shocks induce mark-to-market duration losses or banking partners fail, first-come, first-served redemptions trigger strategic runs. Quantitative risk professionals must model multi-tier asset liquidation waterfalls and fire-sale discounts to compute the precise threshold where secondary market pegs break.

1Stablecoin Architecture and Backing Taxonomy

Stablecoins are cryptographic tokens designed to maintain a fixed exchange rate (typically 1:1) against a sovereign fiat currency. The digital asset ecosystem features three primary structural designs:

  1. Fiat-Collateralized (Centralized): Tokens (e.g. USDC, USDT) issued by centralized entities against reserves of cash, short-term US Treasury bills, repurchase agreements, and commercial bank deposits. Primary authorized participants can mint and redeem tokens at $1.00 par directly with the issuer.
  2. Crypto-Overcollateralized (Decentralized): Tokens (e.g. MakerDAO/Sky DAI) issued against surplus cryptographic collateral locked in on-chain smart contract vaults. Overcollateralization ratios (e.g. 150%) protect the peg against collateral volatility via automated Dutch auction liquidations.
  3. Algorithmic Unbacked (Endogenous Seigniorage): Tokens (e.g. Terra/UST) maintaining stability via an algorithmic mint-and-burn arbitrage mechanism paired with an endogenous sister token (e.g. LUNA). Lacking exogenous collateral, these designs are subject to catastrophic death spirals when market capitalization collapses.

In this lesson and project, our focus is the systemic risk engine of the macro ecosystem: fiat-backed stablecoins operating maturity and liquidity transformation.

2Microeconomics of Runs: Diamond–Dybvig & Gorton–Metrick

The fragility of fiat-backed stablecoins mirrors the microeconomic foundations of classic bank runs (Diamond & Dybvig 1983) and shadow banking repo runs (Gorton & Metrick 2012).

Definition 2.1 · Stablecoin Reserve Run
A stablecoin reserve run is a coordination game among token holders under a sequential service constraint, wherein rational agents race to redeem tokens at par ($1.00) before reserve assets are depleted by mark-to-market duration losses, counterparty defaults, or fire-sale liquidation haircuts. Let \(A_t\) denote the marked-to-market liquidation value of reserves and \(D_t\) denote outstanding token liabilities. If \(A_t < D_t\), the remaining net asset value per token strictly decays with cumulative redemptions \(R\): \[\begin{aligned} \text{NAV}(R) &= \frac{A_t - \mathcal{L}(R)}{D_t - R}, \end{aligned}\] where \(\mathcal{L}(R) \ge R\) is the gross reserve cash consumed to satisfy redemption volume \(R\) under asset liquidation haircuts.

Because early redeemers receive $1.00 while late redeemers receive \(\text{NAV}(R) < \$1.00\), redemption exhibits strategic complementarity: each agent's incentive to redeem increases with the expected redemption volume of other agents.

3Maturity Mismatch and Monetary Tightening Duration Losses

To generate revenue, stablecoin issuers invest customer reserves into yield-bearing debt instruments. Holding term Treasury notes (e.g. 1-to-3-year maturities) generates substantial yield during stable rate environments but exposes the issuer to interest rate risk. When central banks implement aggressive monetary tightening, reserve balance sheets experience sharp mark-to-market duration losses (Jiang, Matvos, Piskorski & Seru 2024).

Theorem 3.1 · Dilution of Residual NAV Under Underwater Reserves
Suppose initial marked-to-market reserve assets \(A_0\) are strictly less than nominal liabilities \(D_0\) (i.e. initial \(\text{NAV}_0 = \frac{A_0}{D_0} < 1\)). If redeemers are paid par ($1.00) from liquid reserves without haircuts (\(\mathcal{L}(R) = R\)), the residual NAV is strictly decreasing in cumulative redemptions \(R\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{d\,\text{NAV}(R)}{dR} &= \frac{A_0 - D_0}{(D_0 - R)^2} < 0 \quad \forall R \in [0, A_0). \end{aligned}\]

Proof
The residual NAV after paying \(R\) dollars of redemptions at par is: \[\begin{aligned} \text{NAV}(R) &= \frac{A_0 - R}{D_0 - R}. \end{aligned}\] Differentiating with respect to \(R\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{d\,\text{NAV}(R)}{dR} &= \frac{(-1)(D_0 - R) - (A_0 - R)(-1)}{(D_0 - R)^2} = \frac{-D_0 + R + A_0 - R}{(D_0 - R)^2} = \frac{A_0 - D_0}{(D_0 - R)^2}. \end{aligned}\] Since \(A_0 < D_0\) by assumption, the numerator \(A_0 - D_0 < 0\). The denominator \((D_0 - R)^2 > 0\) for all \(R < D_0\). Hence, \(\frac{d\,\text{NAV}(R)}{dR} < 0\), proving that every redemption at par strictly dilutes the backing of remaining token holders.

4Reserve Liquidation Waterfalls and Fire-Sale Discounts

When mass redemptions occur, an issuer must liquidate reserve assets in order of increasing liquidation friction, forming a priority waterfall:

  1. Tier 1 — Unencumbered Cash: Haircut \(h_1 = 0.0\%\), duration \(D_1 = 0.0\). Immediately available.
  2. Tier 2 — Overnight Reverse Repo: Haircut \(h_2 = 0.5\%\), duration \(D_2 = 0.0\). Liquid overnight government collateral.
  3. Tier 3 — Short-Dated Treasury Bills: 1-to-3 month maturities, duration \(D_3 \approx 0.25\) years, haircut \(h_3 = 1.0\%\).
  4. Tier 4 — Term Treasury Notes: 2-to-3 year maturities, duration \(D_4 \approx 2.60\) years, subject to mark-to-market rate shocks \(\Delta y\) and secondary market haircut \(h_4 = 3.5\%\).
  5. Tier 5 — Uninsured Commercial Bank Deposits: Subject to bank counterparty haircuts \(h_5 \in [5\%, 15\%]\) during banking system distress (as seen in the March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank run).

As redemptions progress into less liquid tiers, fire-sale discounts (Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009) accelerate asset depletion, precipitating secondary market peg breaks.

5Computational Lab: Simulating Reserve Waterfalls and Peg Breaks

The following laboratory script simulates the reserve portfolio of a $10B stablecoin under a +150 bps monetary tightening shock and traces the liquidation waterfall to determine the exact peg-break threshold \(R^*\).

Lab 1 · Stablecoin reserve run dynamics and peg break simulation

Simulating a 5-tier liquidation waterfall on a $10B stablecoin reserve under a +150 bps interest rate shock will confirm that: (1) mark-to-market duration losses and liquidation haircuts cause residual NAV to decay monotonically; (2) binary search identifies a unique critical run size R* exceeding $1,000M where NAV drops below $0.995.

"""Lab X4-L04-1: Stablecoin Reserve Run Dynamics & Peg Break Simulation.

SIMULATED reserve portfolio of a $10B fiat-backed stablecoin under interest rate shocks
and multi-tier liquidation waterfalls following Diamond-Dybvig (1983) and Jiang et al. (2024).
Calculates mark-to-market duration losses, cash buffer exhaustion, fire-sale haircuts,
and the critical cumulative redemption run size R* that breaks the peg.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def simulate_waterfall(
    total_liabilities: float,
    assets: dict[str, dict[str, float]],
    rate_shock_bps: float,
    redemption_amount: float,
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Execute reserve liquidation waterfall.

    Returns (remaining_tokens, remaining_mv, nav).
    """
    dy = rate_shock_bps / 10000.0
    tiers = ("cash", "overnight_repo", "tbills_short", "treasury_notes", "bank_deposits")

    # Step 1: Mark-to-market valuation after interest rate shock
    current_mv: dict[str, float] = {}
    haircuts: dict[str, float] = {}
    for t in tiers:
        par = assets[t]["par"]
        dur = assets[t]["duration"]
        h = assets[t]["haircut"]
        current_mv[t] = par * max(0.0, 1.0 - dur * dy)
        haircuts[t] = h

    # Step 2: Liquidate assets to satisfy redemption_amount
    rem_r = redemption_amount
    for t in tiers:
        if rem_r <= 0.0:
            break
        mv = current_mv[t]
        h = haircuts[t]
        avail_cash = mv * (1.0 - h)

        if rem_r <= avail_cash:
            mv_used = rem_r / (1.0 - h)
            current_mv[t] -= mv_used
            rem_r = 0.0
        else:
            current_mv[t] = 0.0
            rem_r -= avail_cash

    rem_tokens = max(0.0, total_liabilities - redemption_amount)
    rem_mv = sum(current_mv.values())

    if rem_r > 1e-9:
        nav = 0.0
    elif rem_tokens <= 1e-9:
        nav = 1.0
    else:
        nav = rem_mv / rem_tokens

    return rem_tokens, rem_mv, nav


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab X4-L04-1 | Stablecoin Reserve Run Dynamics & Peg Break")
    print("----------------------------------------------------------")

    liab = 10000.0  # $10 Billion liabilities
    # Assets include 0.8% ($80M) initial equity / reserve capital surplus
    assets = {
        "cash": {"par": 1880.0, "duration": 0.0, "haircut": 0.0},
        "overnight_repo": {"par": 2500.0, "duration": 0.0, "haircut": 0.005},
        "tbills_short": {"par": 3200.0, "duration": 0.25, "haircut": 0.010},
        "treasury_notes": {"par": 1500.0, "duration": 2.60, "haircut": 0.035},
        "bank_deposits": {"par": 1000.0, "duration": 0.0, "haircut": 0.080},
    }

    rate_shock = 150.0  # +150 bps rate shock
    peg_threshold = 0.995

    # Initial state (0 redemptions)
    _, init_mv, init_nav = simulate_waterfall(liab, assets, rate_shock, 0.0)
    print(f"Total Liabilities:             ${liab:.1f}M")
    print(f"Post-Shock Initial Market Val: ${init_mv:.2f}M")
    print(f"Initial Reserve Backing (NAV): ${init_nav:.5f}")

    # Trace liquidation waterfall across redemption levels
    redemptions_to_test = [0.0, 1000.0, 2000.0, 3500.0, 5000.0, 7000.0]
    print("\nLiquidation Waterfall Progression:")
    print("  Redemptions ($M) | Remaining Tokens | Remaining MV ($M) | Post-Run NAV")
    print("  ----------------------------------------------------------------------")
    for r in redemptions_to_test:
        tokens, mv, nav = simulate_waterfall(liab, assets, rate_shock, r)
        print(f"  {r:16.1f} | {tokens:16.1f} | {mv:17.2f} | ${nav:.5f}")

    # Binary search for exact critical run size R* that breaks the peg (NAV < 0.995)
    low = 0.0
    high = liab
    for _ in range(50):
        mid = (low + high) / 2.0
        _, _, nav_mid = simulate_waterfall(liab, assets, rate_shock, mid)
        if nav_mid < peg_threshold:
            high = mid
        else:
            low = mid

    r_star = (low + high) / 2.0
    _, _, nav_at_break = simulate_waterfall(liab, assets, rate_shock, r_star)

    print(f"\nPeg Break Analysis (Threshold NAV < ${peg_threshold:.3f}):")
    print(f"  Critical Run Size R*:     ${r_star:.2f}M")
    print(f"  Critical Run Percentage:  {r_star / liab * 100.0:.2f}% of total supply")
    print(f"  NAV at Critical Point:    ${nav_at_break:.5f}")

    # Verification invariants
    assert init_nav >= peg_threshold
    assert r_star > 1000.0
    print("\nRun dynamics verified: first-mover redemption advantage leads to monotonic NAV decline.")


main()

Where the canon breaks

Stablecoins violate foundational assumptions of commercial banking and asset management:

  1. Absence of a Lender of Last Resort (LLR): Traditional commercial banks survive liquidity runs via Federal Reserve Discount Window borrowing against pledged collateral. Stablecoin issuers lack discount window access and cannot borrow from central banks during liquidity spirals.
  2. Absence of Government Deposit Insurance: Retail bank depositors are insured up to $250,000 by the FDIC, eliminating the incentive to run for the vast majority of retail accounts. Stablecoins offer zero insurance; all holders face full downside tail risk.
  3. Constant NAV Illusion: Stablecoin issuers quote a fixed $1.00 redemption price regardless of mark-to-market asset declines. In traditional money market funds, floating NAV rules (Rule 2a-7) force immediate mark-to-market reflection, whereas stablecoins maintain par until secondary market arbitrage forces a sudden de-peg.

Misconceptions

  • A stablecoin with 100% reserve backing in government securities is completely immune to runs.

    Even if an issuer holds 100% backing in US Treasuries, term duration creates mark-to-market deficits during rate-hiking cycles. If a 3-year Treasury portfolio suffers a 5% rate-induced mark-to-market loss and holders redeem 50% of supply at par, the remaining assets fall short of remaining liabilities, driving the residual NAV below $1.00.

  • Redeeming at par ($1.00) under sequential service is fair to all token holders.

    Under sequential service, first redeemers exit at $1.00 without penalty, consuming the issuer's highest-quality liquid cash. Late redeemers are left holding illiquid, discounted, or impaired assets, suffering concentrated losses. Sequential service creates a rational run panic.

  • Algorithmic seigniorage stablecoins can maintain their peg through automated mint-and-burn arbitrage.

    Endogenous seigniorage tokens (such as Terra/UST) rely on the market value of their secondary governance token. When the peg breaks, arbitrageurs mint trillions of governance tokens to sell, crashing the governance token's price to zero in a hyperinflationary death spiral.

Mechanics

Problem M1 · Mark-to-Market Duration Loss on Reserve Assets

A stablecoin reserve holds $2.0B par value of 2-year Treasury notes with modified duration \(\mathcal{D}^* = 1.90\) years. If the 2-year Treasury yield jumps by 200 basis points (+0.0200), calculate the post-shock market value of the notes.

Worked solution

The first-order percentage price change is: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\Delta P}{P} &\approx -\mathcal{D}^* \Delta y = -1.90 \times 0.0200 = -0.0380 = -3.80\%. \end{aligned}\] The post-shock market value is: \[\begin{aligned} \text{MV} &= \$2,000\text{M} \times (1 - 0.0380) = \$2,000\text{M} \times 0.9620 = \$1,924.0\text{M}. \end{aligned}\] The interest rate shock generates a $76.0M mark-to-market loss.

Problem M2 · Liquidation Haircut Cash Consumption

An issuer must satisfy a $300M redemption request. The only available assets are term Treasury notes carrying a 4.0% liquidation haircut. How much market value of notes must be liquidated, and what is the dollar haircut loss?

Worked solution

Under a haircut \(h = 0.04\), the net cash generated per dollar of market value liquidated is \(1 - h = 0.96\). To generate cash \(R = \$300\text{M}\): \[\begin{aligned} \text{MV}_{\text{used}} &= \frac{R}{1 - h} = \frac{\$300\text{M}}{0.96} = \$312.5\text{M}. \end{aligned}\] The haircut loss absorbed by the reserve is: \[\begin{aligned} \text{Loss} &= \text{MV}_{\text{used}} - R = \$312.5\text{M} - \$300.0\text{M} = \$12.5\text{M}. \end{aligned}\] The reserve balance sheet loses $312.5M in assets to retire $300M in liabilities.

Problem M3 · Residual NAV Calculation After Redemptions

A stablecoin has $5,000M in token liabilities backed by $4,900M in reserve assets (initial \(\text{NAV} = 0.98\)). If $1,000M in redemptions are satisfied at $1.00 par without haircuts, what is the remaining token supply and residual NAV?

Worked solution

Remaining token liabilities: \[\begin{aligned} D_{\text{rem}} &= \$5,000\text{M} - \$1,000\text{M} = \$4,000\text{M}. \end{aligned}\] Remaining reserve assets: \[\begin{aligned} A_{\text{rem}} &= \$4,900\text{M} - \$1,000\text{M} = \$3,900\text{M}. \end{aligned}\] Residual NAV: \[\begin{aligned} \text{NAV}_{\text{rem}} &= \frac{\$3,900\text{M}}{\$4,000\text{M}} = 0.9750. \end{aligned}\] Paying early redeemers at par reduced residual backing from $0.9800 to $0.9750 per token.

Proof

Problem P1 · Proof of Strict Monotonic Decay of Residual NAV Under Haircuts

Let \(A(R) = A_0 - \frac{R}{1 - h}\) and \(D(R) = D_0 - R\), where \(h \in [0, 1)\) is the liquidation haircut. Prove that if \(\text{NAV}_0 = \frac{A_0}{D_0} < \frac{1}{1 - h}\), then \(\text{NAV}(R) = \frac{A(R)}{D(R)}\) is strictly decreasing in \(R\).

Worked solution

Write the NAV function: \[\begin{aligned} \text{NAV}(R) &= \frac{A_0 - \frac{R}{1 - h}}{D_0 - R}. \end{aligned}\] Differentiating with respect to \(R\) using the quotient rule: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{d\,\text{NAV}}{dR} &= \frac{-\frac{1}{1-h}(D_0 - R) - \left( A_0 - \frac{R}{1-h} \right)(-1)}{(D_0 - R)^2} \\ &= \frac{-\frac{D_0}{1-h} + \frac{R}{1-h} + A_0 - \frac{R}{1-h}}{(D_0 - R)^2} \\ &= \frac{A_0 - \frac{D_0}{1-h}}{(D_0 - R)^2}. \end{aligned}\] The derivative is negative if and only if: \[\begin{aligned} A_0 - \frac{D_0}{1 - h} < 0 \iff \frac{A_0}{D_0} < \frac{1}{1 - h}. \end{aligned}\] Since \(h \ge 0\), \(\frac{1}{1-h} \ge 1\). If initial \(\text{NAV}_0 \le 1.0\), the condition \(\text{NAV}_0 < \frac{1}{1-h}\) is strictly satisfied for any \(h > 0\), guaranteeing that residual NAV decays monotonically with redemptions.

Problem P2 · Proof of Unique Peg Break Threshold via Intermediate Value Theorem

Let \(\text{NAV}(R)\) be a continuous, strictly decreasing function on \([0, R_{\max}]\) with \(\text{NAV}(0) > \theta\) and \(\text{NAV}(R_{\max}) < \theta\). Prove that there exists a unique critical run size \(R^* \in (0, R_{\max})\) such that \(\text{NAV}(R^*) = \theta\).

Worked solution

Existence: Define the function \(g(R) = \text{NAV}(R) - \theta\). Since \(\text{NAV}(R)\) is continuous on \([0, R_{\max}]\), \(g(R)\) is also continuous. We are given \(g(0) = \text{NAV}(0) - \theta > 0\) and \(g(R_{\max}) = \text{NAV}(R_{\max}) - \theta < 0\). By the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT), there exists at least one \(R^* \in (0, R_{\max})\) such that \(g(R^*) = 0\), i.e., \(\text{NAV}(R^*) = \theta\). Uniqueness: Assume there exist two points \(R_1^* < R_2^*\) such that \(\text{NAV}(R_1^*) = \text{NAV}(R_2^*) = \theta\). Because \(\text{NAV}(R)\) is strictly decreasing on \([0, R_{\max}]\), \(R_1^* < R_2^*\) implies \(\text{NAV}(R_1^*) > \text{NAV}(R_2^*)\), which contradicts \(\text{NAV}(R_1^*) = \text{NAV}(R_2^*)\). Hence, the root \(R^*\) is unique, guaranteeing convergence of binary search.

Problem P3 · Strategic Complementarity and Global Game Coordination

In a two-depositor run game, each depositor decides whether to Run (\(a_i = 1\)) or Hold (\(a_i = 0\)). If both hold, each receives payoff \(1 + r\). If one runs, the runner receives 1 and the holder receives \(\max(0, A_0 - 1)\). If both run, each receives \(\frac{A_0}{2}\). Prove that if \(A_0 < 2\), the game exhibits strategic complementarity.

Worked solution

Strategic complementarity requires that the incremental payoff from running, \(\Delta u_i(a_{-i}) = u_i(1, a_{-i}) - u_i(0, a_{-i})\), is non-decreasing in the opponent's action \(a_{-i}\). Case 1: Opponent holds (\(a_{-i} = 0\)): \[\begin{aligned} u_i(1, 0) &= 1, \quad u_i(0, 0) = 1 + r \implies \Delta u_i(0) = 1 - (1 + r) = -r. \end{aligned}\] Case 2: Opponent runs (\(a_{-i} = 1\)): \[\begin{aligned} u_i(1, 1) &= \frac{A_0}{2}, \quad u_i(0, 1) = \max(0, A_0 - 1). \end{aligned}\] Incremental payoff: \[\begin{aligned} \Delta u_i(1) &= \frac{A_0}{2} - \max(0, A_0 - 1). \end{aligned}\] If \(1 \le A_0 < 2\), \(\max(0, A_0 - 1) = A_0 - 1\). Then: \[\begin{aligned} \Delta u_i(1) &= \frac{A_0}{2} - (A_0 - 1) = 1 - \frac{A_0}{2} > 0. \end{aligned}\] Since \(-r < 0 < 1 - \frac{A_0}{2}\), \(\Delta u_i(1) > \Delta u_i(0)\). The incentive to run is strictly higher when the other depositor runs, proving strategic complementarity and multiple Nash equilibria (Diamond-Dybvig 1983).

Empirical

Problem E1 · The March 2023 USDC De-Peg Event

In March 2023, Circle revealed that $3.3B of its $40B USDC reserve backing was held as uninsured cash deposits at Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) when SVB failed. USDC traded down to $0.87 on secondary DEXs. Explain why the secondary market discount far exceeded the fundamental percentage loss (\(\frac{\$3.3\text{B}}{\$40\text{B}} = 8.25\%\)).

Worked solution

Key economic factors amplifying the discount beyond the 8.25% fundamental loss: (1) Uncertainty over recovery: On Saturday March 11, depositors did not yet know whether the FDIC would bail out uninsured depositors or impose a 20–40% haircut; (2) Liquidity freeze over the weekend: Primary redemption with Circle was closed until Monday morning, meaning secondary DEX liquidity pools (Curve 3pool) absorbed all panic selling; (3) First-mover advantage: Traders feared that if Circle honored Monday redemptions at $1.00 on a first-come, first-served basis, late redeemers would absorb the entire SVB shortfall; (4) Leveraged liquidations: DAI and DeFi protocols using USDC as collateral triggered automated de-leveraging cascades.

Problem E2 · Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) Calibration for Stablecoins

The Basel III Liquidity Coverage Ratio requires: \(\text{LCR} = \frac{\text{HQLA}}{\text{Net 30-Day Outflows}} \ge 100\%\). For a $20B stablecoin where 40% of supply is held by institutional arbitrageurs with estimated 100% 30-day run propensity, calculate the required Level 1 High-Quality Liquid Assets.

Worked solution

Institutional outflow volume: \[\begin{aligned} \text{Outflows}_{\text{inst}} &= 40\% \times \$20\text{B} \times 100\% = \$8.0\text{B}. \end{aligned}\] Assuming remaining 60% retail supply has a conservative 25% 30-day run rate: \[\begin{aligned} \text{Outflows}_{\text{retail}} &= 60\% \times \$20\text{B} \times 25\% = \$3.0\text{B}. \end{aligned}\] Total net 30-day outflows: \(\$8.0\text{B} + \$3.0\text{B} = \$11.0\text{B}\). To maintain \(\text{LCR} \ge 100\%\), the issuer must hold at least $11.0B in Level 1 HQLA (unencumbered cash and overnight reverse repo), representing 55% of total balance sheet assets.

Problem E3 · DEX Secondary Market Arbitrage Mechanics

If a stablecoin trades at $0.985 on Curve DEX while the issuer continues to process redemptions at $1.00 par with a $5,000 transaction wire fee, what is the minimum redemption size for an arbitrageur to achieve a net 1.0% return?

Worked solution

Let \(N\) be the number of tokens purchased on DEX at $0.985 each. Total purchase cost: \(C = 0.985 N\). Gross redemption revenue: \(1.000 N\). Net profit after fee: \[\begin{aligned} \Pi(N) &= 1.000 N - 0.985 N - \$5,000 = 0.015 N - \$5,000. \end{aligned}\] Target net return is 1.0% on capital invested: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{0.015 N - 5,000}{0.985 N} &\ge 0.010 \implies 0.015 N - 5,000 \ge 0.00985 N \\ &\implies 0.00515 N \ge 5,000 \implies N \ge \frac{5,000}{0.00515} \approx 970,874 \text{ tokens}. \end{aligned}\] The arbitrageur requires a trade size of at least ~$971,000 to overcome the fixed wire fee and achieve a 1.0% net margin.

Research

Problem R1 · Dynamic Liquidity Surcharges on Redemptions

To prevent the dilution of residual NAV, propose a dynamic redemption fee schedule \(\phi(R)\) such that residual NAV remains exactly constant at \(\text{NAV}_0\) for all redemption amounts \(R\).

Worked solution

To keep \(\text{NAV}(R) = \text{NAV}_0 = \frac{A_0}{D_0}\), each redeemed token must remove exactly \(\text{NAV}_0\) in gross reserve assets rather than $1.00 par. If redemptions consume assets with liquidation haircut \(h\), liquidating \(\text{MV}\) yields cash \((1 - h)\text{MV}\). To ensure remaining assets equal \(\text{NAV}_0 (D_0 - R)\): \[\begin{aligned} A_{\text{rem}} &= A_0 - \frac{\text{Cash Paid}}{1 - h} = \text{NAV}_0 (D_0 - R) = A_0 - \text{NAV}_0 R. \end{aligned}\] Hence: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\text{Cash Paid}}{1 - h} &= \text{NAV}_0 R \implies \text{Cash Paid} = (1 - h) \text{NAV}_0 R. \end{aligned}\] The dynamic redemption fee per dollar redeemed is: \[\begin{aligned} \phi(R) &= 1 - (1 - h) \text{NAV}_0 = 1 - \text{NAV}_0 + h \text{NAV}_0. \end{aligned}\] Charging fee \(\phi(R)\) forces redeemers to internalize both the existing reserve deficit \((1 - \text{NAV}_0)\) and the liquidation haircut \(h \text{NAV}_0\), eliminating the first-mover advantage and preventing runs.

Problem R2 · Contagion Across DeFi Collateral Protocols

How does a 3% stablecoin de-peg trigger automated systemic liquidation cascades across money market protocols such as Aave and Compound?

Worked solution

Transmission mechanism: (1) Stablecoin de-pegs to $0.97. Oracle feeds (e.g. Chainlink) update secondary market price. (2) Borrowers who deposited volatile crypto (ETH, BTC) to borrow stablecoins see their loan-to-value ratios decline favorably; however, users who deposited the de-pegging stablecoin as collateral see their collateral valuation drop by 3%. (3) Health factors \(\text{HF} = \frac{\sum \text{Collateral} \times \text{LT}}{\text{Borrow}}\) fall below 1.0, triggering permissionless liquidator bots. (4) Liquidator bots seize collateral at a 5–10% liquidation bonus and dump it on decentralized exchanges, depressing spot market prices across collateral assets and spreading liquidation contagion throughout DeFi.

Problem R3 · Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) as a Threat or Complement

Evaluate the competitive equilibrium between interest-bearing wholesale CBDCs and private fiat-backed stablecoins under regulatory reserve segregation mandates.

Worked solution

Competitive equilibrium analysis: (1) Disintermediation risk: If central banks issue a retail CBDC that pays the risk-free rate, risk-averse depositors will abandon non-yielding private stablecoins, causing systemic stablecoin runoffs. (2) Synthetic CBDC model: If central banks permit stablecoin issuers to hold 100% of reserves directly at the central bank master account earning the Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB) rate, stablecoins become "synthetic CBDCs." (3) Credit and liquidity implications: This eliminates duration and counterparty bank run risks entirely (\(h = 0, \mathcal{D}^* = 0\)), but drains deposits from the commercial banking system, shrinking private credit creation.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What microeconomic feature creates the "first-mover advantage" in stablecoin redemptions?

    Options

    Under sequential service, the issuer pays out par ($1.00) until liquid reserves are exhausted, transferring mark-to-market losses onto remaining holders.

  2. According to Jiang et al. (2024), what was the primary driver of balance sheet fragility during the 2022–2023 rate hiking cycle?

    Options

    Monetary tightening caused steep bond price declines, creating massive unrecognized mark-to-market duration losses on reserve portfolios.

  3. A reserve portfolio holds $1,000M par in Treasury notes with modified duration \(\mathcal{D}^* = 2.5\) years. If yields jump by 150 basis points (+0.0150), calculate the dollar duration loss in millions of dollars.

    \(\text{Loss} = \$1,000\text{M} \times 2.5 \times 0.0150 = \$37.5\text{M} \approx \$38\text{M}\).

  4. In a multi-tier liquidation waterfall, why does an issuer liquidate cash and overnight repo before term Treasury notes?

    Options

    Liquidating zero-haircut assets preserves capital surplus for as long as possible before fire-sale discounts are incurred.

  5. What was the primary vulnerability of algorithmic stablecoins like Terra/UST during run episodes?

    Options

    Lacking external independent assets, algorithmic stablecoins collapse into a death spiral when arbitrageurs sell minted governance tokens into illiquid markets.

  6. If satisfying a $300M redemption requires liquidating an asset with a 4.0% haircut, how many millions of dollars are lost purely to the liquidation haircut?

    \(\text{MV}_{\text{used}} = 300 / (1 - 0.04) = 312.5\text{M}\). Haircut loss \(= 312.5 - 300 = \$12.5\text{M}\).

  7. Why does a floating NAV money market fund face lower run incentives than a stablecoin with par redemption?

    Options

    Under floating NAV, exiting investors receive the marked-down share price, eliminating the transfer of losses onto remaining holders.

  8. How does Brunnermeier & Pedersen's (2009) framework explain liquidity spirals during stablecoin reserve runs?

    Options

    Loss spirals and margin spirals mutually reinforce: asset fire sales reduce market liquidity, which triggers higher haircuts and accelerates run dynamics.
