Cross-Sectional Crypto Risk Factors & Spanning

As the digital asset market expanded beyond Bitcoin into thousands of tradable tokens, cross-sectional asset pricing became necessary to distinguish genuine risk premia from idiosyncratic noise. In equities, the classic Fama–French and Carhart models rely on book-to-market, annual momentum, and profitability. In digital assets, corporate balance sheets and earnings do not exist. In their landmark study, Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022) establish that the cross-section of cryptocurrency returns is parsimoniously priced by a three-factor model: cryptocurrency market (CMKT), size (CSMB), and ultra-short weekly momentum (CMOM). Understanding this factor architecture allows quantitative researchers to perform rigorous factor spanning, detect data snooping, and attribute alpha across alternative cryptocurrency investment strategies.

1The Cross-Section of Digital Assets and Common Factor Structure

In classical asset pricing (F2), cross-sectional variation in expected excess returns is explained by exposure to systematic risk factors (Fama & French 1993). When applied to cryptocurrencies, asset characteristics differ fundamentally: tokens lack book values, debt covenants, and cash flow yields. To identify systematic drivers across the cross-section, Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022) examine a comprehensive universe of over 1,800 digital assets trading between 2014 and 2020.

Definition 1.1 · Cross-Sectional Crypto Risk Factors
Crypto-asset risk and return factors denote systematic long-short portfolio return series constructed from cross-sectional characteristic sorts across cryptocurrencies. The three benchmark factors established by Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022) are:

  1. Cryptocurrency Market (CMKT): The value-weighted excess return of the aggregate cryptocurrency market over the risk-free rate: \[\begin{aligned} \text{CMKT}_t &= \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} w_{i, t-1} r_{i, t} - r_{f, t}, \quad w_{i, t-1} = \frac{\text{MCap}_{i, t-1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_t} \text{MCap}_{j, t-1}}. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Cryptocurrency Size (CSMB — Small Minus Big): The return spread between small-market-capitalization and large-market-capitalization coin portfolios: \[\begin{aligned} \text{CSMB}_t &= \frac{1}{3} (R_{\text{Small, LowMom}} + R_{\text{Small, MedMom}} + R_{\text{Small, HighMom}}) - \frac{1}{3} (R_{\text{Big, LowMom}} + R_{\text{Big, MedMom}} + R_{\text{Big, HighMom}}). \end{aligned}\]
  3. Cryptocurrency Momentum (CMOM — High Minus Low): The return spread between high past return (winners) and low past return (losers) sorted over past 3-week cumulative performance: \[\begin{aligned} \text{CMOM}_t &= \frac{1}{2} (R_{\text{Small, HighMom}} + R_{\text{Big, HighMom}}) - \frac{1}{2} (R_{\text{Small, LowMom}} + R_{\text{Big, LowMom}}). \end{aligned}\]

Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022) report that the weekly average return of the value-weighted market factor CMKT is 1.62% (annualized ~84%), the CSMB size premium is 1.34% per week, and the CMOM momentum factor generates 1.25% per week. All three factors command statistically significant risk premia with \(t\)-statistics exceeding 2.30.

2Cross-Sectional Asset Pricing: The Fama–MacBeth Methodology

To test whether candidate factors explain the cross-section of digital asset returns, researchers use the classic two-pass regression methodology of Fama & MacBeth (1973).

Theorem 2.1 · Two-Pass Factor Pricing Representation
Under linear Arbitrage Pricing Theory (arbitrage pricing theory), the expected excess return of asset \(i\) satisfies: \[\begin{aligned} \E[r_i - r_f] &= \beta_{i, \text{CMKT}} \lambda_{\text{CMKT}} + \beta_{i, \text{CSMB}} \lambda_{\text{CSMB}} + \beta_{i, \text{CMOM}} \lambda_{\text{CMOM}}, \end{aligned}\] where \(\beta_{i, k}\) represents the asset's marginal risk loading and \(\lambda_k\) is the cross-sectional factor risk premium.

Proof
The two-pass estimation proceeds as follows:
Pass 1 (Time-Series): For each asset \(i = 1, \dots, N\), estimate factor betas via time-series OLS over \(T\) weeks: \[\begin{aligned} r_{i, t} - r_{f, t} &= \alpha_i + \beta_{i, \text{CMKT}} \text{CMKT}_t + \beta_{i, \text{CSMB}} \text{CSMB}_t + \beta_{i, \text{CMOM}} \text{CMOM}_t + \epsilon_{i, t}. \end{aligned}\] Pass 2 (Cross-Sectional): At each week \(t = 1, \dots, T\), run a cross-sectional regression of realized excess returns on the estimated Pass 1 betas: \[\begin{aligned} r_{i, t} - r_{f, t} &= \gamma_{0, t} + \hat{\beta}_{i, \text{CMKT}} \lambda_{\text{CMKT}, t} + \hat{\beta}_{i, \text{CSMB}} \lambda_{\text{CSMB}, t} + \hat{\beta}_{i, \text{CMOM}} \lambda_{\text{CMOM}, t} + \eta_{i, t}. \end{aligned}\] The unconditional factor risk premia are the sample time-series averages: \[\begin{aligned} \hat{\lambda}_k &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \lambda_{k, t}, \quad \widehat{\Var}(\hat{\lambda}_k) = \frac{1}{T(T-1)} \sum_{t=1}^T (\lambda_{k, t} - \hat{\lambda}_k)^2. \end{aligned}\] Under asymptotic stability and stationary innovations, \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\lambda}_k - \lambda_k) \dto \Normal(0, \sigma_{\lambda}^2)\), establishing the estimator.

In finite samples, using estimated betas \(\hat{\beta}\) from Pass 1 introduces errors-in-variables (EIV). Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022) apply the Shanken (1992) correction factor \((1 + \hat{\lambda}' \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda})\) to adjust standard errors, confirming that all three factor premia remain statistically significant at the 5% level.

3Factor Spanning Regressions & Anomaly Subsumption

A central challenge in empirical asset pricing is the proliferation of candidate factors (the factor zoo). To determine whether a proposed anomaly (such as network transaction volume, price-to-user ratio, token age, or volatility) constitutes a distinct source of systematic risk, researchers execute spanning regressions (Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu 2022).

Definition 1.2 · Factor Spanning Regression
Let \(F_t^{\text{candidate}}\) be the excess return of a proposed alternative factor portfolio. The spanning regression against the three benchmark crypto factors is: \[\begin{aligned} F_t^{\text{candidate}} &= \alpha_{\text{span}} + \beta_1 \text{CMKT}_t + \beta_2 \text{CSMB}_t + \beta_3 \text{CMOM}_t + \nu_t. \end{aligned}\] The candidate factor is defined as spanned (redundant) if the intercept satisfies: \[\begin{aligned} H_0: \alpha_{\text{span}} = 0. \end{aligned}\] If \(H_0\) is not rejected, the candidate anomaly's average return is entirely accounted for by its covariance with CMKT, CSMB, and CMOM.

Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022) test 14 prominent cryptocurrency anomalies—including Network Value to Transactions (NVT), Price-to-User ratio, liquidity/turnover, idiosyncratic volatility, and maximum daily return—and establish that all 14 candidate anomalies yield spanning alphas statistically indistinguishable from zero (\(|t| < 1.85\)). The three-factor model successfully spans the entire known cross-section.

Lab 1 · Three-factor cryptocurrency asset pricing and factor spanning

Constructing CMKT, CSMB, and CMOM factors on a simulated panel of 20 cryptocurrencies will verify that: (1) Fama-MacBeth two-pass regressions estimate positive risk premia across all three factors; (2) a candidate network anomaly (NVT factor) yields a spanning alpha statistically indistinguishable from zero (|t| < 1.96), confirming factor redundancy.

"""Lab X4-L02-1: Cross-Sectional Crypto Risk Factors (CMKT, CSMB, CMOM) and Spanning Regressions.

SIMULATED weekly panel of 20 cryptocurrencies over 104 weeks.
Constructs cryptocurrency market (CMKT), size (CSMB), and momentum (CMOM) factors
following Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022, JF), runs Fama-MacBeth risk premia estimation,
and tests factor spanning on a candidate network factor.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def regress_ols(y: list[float], x_matrix: list[list[float]]) -> tuple[list[float], list[float], float]:
    """Multiple OLS regression: y = X * beta + eps (X includes intercept as column 0).

    Returns (betas, t_stats, r2).
    """
    n = len(y)
    p = len(x_matrix[0])

    # X^T * X
    xtx = [[sum(x_matrix[i][j] * x_matrix[i][k] for i in range(n)) for k in range(p)] for j in range(p)]
    # X^T * y
    xty = [sum(x_matrix[i][j] * y[i] for i in range(n)) for j in range(p)]

    # Solve via Gaussian elimination
    a = [row[:] for row in xtx]
    b = xty[:]
    for i in range(p):
        pivot = a[i][i]
        for j in range(i + 1, p):
            factor = a[j][i] / pivot
            for k in range(i, p):
                a[j][k] -= factor * a[i][k]
            b[j] -= factor * b[i]

    beta = [0.0] * p
    for i in range(p - 1, -1, -1):
        s = sum(a[i][j] * beta[j] for j in range(i + 1, p))
        beta[i] = (b[i] - s) / a[i][i]

    # Residuals & variance
    y_pred = [sum(x_matrix[i][j] * beta[j] for j in range(p)) for i in range(n)]
    residuals = [yi - ypi for yi, ypi in zip(y, y_pred, strict=True)]
    sse = sum(e**2 for e in residuals)
    mean_y = sum(y) / n
    sst = sum((yi - mean_y) ** 2 for yi in y)
    r2 = 1.0 - (sse / sst) if sst > 0 else 0.0

    sigma2 = sse / (n - p) if n > p else 0.0

    # Invert xtx for standard errors
    inv_xtx = [[1.0 if j == k else 0.0 for k in range(p)] for j in range(p)]
    a_inv = [row[:] for row in xtx]
    for i in range(p):
        piv = a_inv[i][i]
        for k in range(p):
            a_inv[i][k] /= piv
            inv_xtx[i][k] /= piv
        for j in range(p):
            if j != i:
                f = a_inv[j][i]
                for k in range(p):
                    a_inv[j][k] -= f * a_inv[i][k]
                    inv_xtx[j][k] -= f * inv_xtx[i][k]

    t_stats = [beta[j] / math.sqrt(max(1e-12, sigma2 * inv_xtx[j][j])) for j in range(p)]
    return beta, t_stats, r2


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab X4-L02-1 | Three-Factor Cryptocurrency Asset Pricing & Spanning")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------")

    rng = random.Random(101)
    n_assets = 20
    n_weeks = 104

    # Generate market capitalization, historical returns, and factor loadings
    # Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022): CMKT (mkt excess), CSMB (size), CMOM (momentum)
    cmkt: list[float] = [rng.gauss(0.008, 0.065) for _ in range(n_weeks)]
    csmb: list[float] = [rng.gauss(0.005, 0.040) for _ in range(n_weeks)]
    cmom: list[float] = [rng.gauss(0.007, 0.045) for _ in range(n_weeks)]

    # Candidate network adoption anomaly factor (NVT factor)
    # Constructed to have beta on CMKT and CSMB, but zero true abnormal alpha
    nvt_factor: list[float] = [0.0005 + 0.35 * cmkt[t] + 0.40 * csmb[t] + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.020) for t in range(n_weeks)]

    # Asset return panel
    asset_returns: list[list[float]] = []  # shape: (n_assets, n_weeks)
    for i in range(n_assets):
        b_mkt = 0.70 + 0.05 * i
        b_smb = 1.20 - 0.10 * i
        b_mom = -0.40 + 0.05 * i
        ret_series: list[float] = []
        for t in range(n_weeks):
            idio = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.05)
            r = b_mkt * cmkt[t] + b_smb * csmb[t] + b_mom * cmom[t] + idio
            ret_series.append(r)
        asset_returns.append(ret_series)

    # First pass: Time-series regressions to estimate factor betas
    betas_mkt: list[float] = []
    betas_smb: list[float] = []
    betas_mom: list[float] = []

    x_mat_ts = [[1.0, cmkt[t], csmb[t], cmom[t]] for t in range(n_weeks)]
    for i in range(n_assets):
        b, _, _ = regress_ols(asset_returns[i], x_mat_ts)
        betas_mkt.append(b[1])
        betas_smb.append(b[2])
        betas_mom.append(b[3])

    # Second pass: Cross-sectional Fama-MacBeth regressions at each week t
    lambda_mkt_series: list[float] = []
    lambda_smb_series: list[float] = []
    lambda_mom_series: list[float] = []

    for t in range(n_weeks):
        y_cs = [asset_returns[i][t] for i in range(n_assets)]
        x_cs = [[1.0, betas_mkt[i], betas_smb[i], betas_mom[i]] for i in range(n_assets)]
        gammas, _, _ = regress_ols(y_cs, x_cs)
        lambda_mkt_series.append(gammas[1])
        lambda_smb_series.append(gammas[2])
        lambda_mom_series.append(gammas[3])

    # Average Fama-MacBeth risk premia and t-stats
    def mean_and_t(series: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float]:
        m = sum(series) / len(series)
        var = sum((s - m) ** 2 for s in series) / (len(series) - 1)
        se = math.sqrt(var / len(series))
        return m, m / se

    prem_mkt, t_mkt = mean_and_t(lambda_mkt_series)
    prem_smb, t_smb = mean_and_t(lambda_smb_series)
    prem_mom, t_mom = mean_and_t(lambda_mom_series)

    print("Fama-MacBeth Cross-Sectional Risk Premia (Weekly):")
    print(f"  CMKT Risk Premium: {prem_mkt:.4f} (t-stat = {t_mkt:.2f})")
    print(f"  CSMB Risk Premium: {prem_smb:.4f} (t-stat = {t_smb:.2f})")
    print(f"  CMOM Risk Premium: {prem_mom:.4f} (t-stat = {t_mom:.2f})")

    # Spanning Regression: Test whether NVT factor alpha is subsumed by 3-factor model
    # NVT_t = alpha + beta_mkt * CMKT_t + beta_smb * CSMB_t + beta_mom * CMOM_t + eps_t
    b_span, t_span, r2_span = regress_ols(nvt_factor, x_mat_ts)
    alpha_span = b_span[0]
    t_alpha_span = t_span[0]

    print("\nFactor Spanning Regression for Candidate Network (NVT) Factor:")
    print(f"  Spanning Alpha:   {alpha_span:.5f} (t-stat = {t_alpha_span:.2f})")
    print(f"  Beta on CMKT:     {b_span[1]:.4f} (t-stat = {t_span[1]:.2f})")
    print(f"  Beta on CSMB:     {b_span[2]:.4f} (t-stat = {t_span[2]:.2f})")
    print(f"  Beta on CMOM:     {b_span[3]:.4f} (t-stat = {t_span[3]:.2f})")
    print(f"  R-squared:        {r2_span:.4f}")

    # Spanned test: alpha is statistically indistinguishable from zero (|t| < 1.96)
    is_spanned = abs(t_alpha_span) < 1.96
    print(f"Candidate factor spanned by Liu-Tsyvinski-Wu 3-factor model: {is_spanned}")


main()

Where the canon breaks

The cross-sectional asset pricing of cryptocurrencies diverges sharply from equity market canon:

  1. Absence of the Value Factor (HML): In equity markets, the Fama–French HML factor (value premium) is central. Cryptocurrencies have no balance sheet book value or replacement cost of capital. Attempts to construct synthetic crypto value factors (e.g. Price-to-Hash or NVT ratios) fail spanning tests (Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu 2022).
  2. Momentum Horizon Discrepancy: In equities, Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) and Carhart (1997) define momentum over 12-month lookback windows (\(t-12\) to \(t-2\)). In digital assets, 12-month momentum is economically uninformative due to rapid token lifecycles and technological obsolescence; momentum operates almost exclusively over 1-to-4-week horizons.
  3. Extreme Factor Volatility: Cryptocurrency factor returns exhibit annualized volatilities between 40% and 80%, exceeding the volatility of individual common stocks. Factor investing in crypto requires dynamic leverage and strict downside risk budgeting.

Misconceptions

  • The Fama–French three-factor model for equities (Mkt, SMB, HML) directly prices the cross-section of digital assets.

    Traditional equity factor models fail completely in digital assets. Regressing cryptocurrency cross-sections on equity SMB, HML, or Carhart MOM produces \(R^2\) values below 2% and statistically insignificant betas (Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu 2022). Crypto requires internal, domain-specific factor construction.

  • Small-cap cryptocurrencies outperform large-cap tokens solely due to higher technological innovation.

    The CSMB size premium reflects compensation for severe illiquidity, exchange listing risk, and developer abandonment. When microcap tokens are excluded or trading friction costs are applied, the net realizable size premium declines substantially.

  • Network Value to Transactions (NVT) ratio operates as an unspanned fundamental anomaly generating independent alpha.

    Spanning regressions in Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022) prove that NVT anomaly portfolios carry a spanning alpha of zero when regressed on CMKT, CSMB, and CMOM. High NVT is economically equivalent to a combination of large-cap and low-momentum exposures.

Problem set

Mechanics

Problem M1 · Value-Weighted Market Factor Return

A universe consists of three tokens: Token A (market cap $60B, weekly return +5%), Token B (market cap $30B, weekly return -2%), and Token C (market cap $10B, weekly return +10%). Calculate the value-weighted cryptocurrency market factor return CMKT assuming a zero risk-free rate.

Worked solution

Total universe market capitalization is: \[\begin{aligned} \text{Total MCap} &= 60 + 30 + 10 = 100 \text{ billion USD}. \end{aligned}\] The portfolio weights are: \[\begin{aligned} w_A = \frac{60}{100} = 0.60, \quad w_B = \frac{30}{100} = 0.30, \quad w_C = \frac{10}{100} = 0.10. \end{aligned}\] The value-weighted market factor return is: \[\begin{aligned} \text{CMKT} &= w_A r_A + w_B r_B + w_C r_C - r_f \\ &= 0.60(0.05) + 0.30(-0.02) + 0.10(0.10) - 0.0 \\ &= 0.030 - 0.006 + 0.010 = 0.0340 \text{ (or 3.40\%)}. \end{aligned}\] The market return is 3.40% per week.

Problem M2 · Expected Return via APT Model

According to the Liu–Tsyvinski–Wu three-factor model, factor risk premia are \(\lambda_{\text{CMKT}} = 0.015\) (1.5%/week), \(\lambda_{\text{CSMB}} = 0.012\) (1.2%/week), and \(\lambda_{\text{CMOM}} = 0.010\) (1.0%/week). A newly listed token has estimated betas \(\beta_{\text{CMKT}} = 1.20\), \(\beta_{\text{CSMB}} = 0.80\), and \(\beta_{\text{CMOM}} = -0.50\). Calculate its expected weekly excess return.

Worked solution

By the Arbitrage Pricing Theory pricing equation: \[\begin{aligned} \E[r_i - r_f] &= \beta_{i, \text{CMKT}} \lambda_{\text{CMKT}} + \beta_{i, \text{CSMB}} \lambda_{\text{CSMB}} + \beta_{i, \text{CMOM}} \lambda_{\text{CMOM}} \\ &= 1.20(0.015) + 0.80(0.012) + (-0.50)(0.010) \\ &= 0.0180 + 0.0096 - 0.0050 = 0.0226 \text{ (or 2.26\% per week)}. \end{aligned}\] The token's expected weekly excess return is 2.26%.

Problem M3 · Spanning Regression Alpha t-Statistic

In a factor spanning regression with \(T = 156\) weekly observations, an alternative anomaly factor produces an estimated spanning alpha of \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.0018\) (18 bps/week) with standard error \(\text{SE}(\hat{\alpha}) = 0.0012\). Conduct a two-sided hypothesis test of factor redundancy at the 5% significance level.

Worked solution

The \(t\)-statistic for the null hypothesis \(H_0: \alpha = 0\) is: \[\begin{aligned} t &= \frac{\hat{\alpha}}{\text{SE}(\hat{\alpha})} = \frac{0.0018}{0.0012} = 1.50. \end{aligned}\] For a two-sided test at \(\alpha = 0.05\) with 152 degrees of freedom, the critical value is \(t_{\text{crit}} = 1.96\). Because \(|t| = 1.50 < 1.96\), we fail to reject the null hypothesis of zero alpha. The candidate anomaly is spanned by the benchmark model and provides no statistically significant abnormal return.

Proof

Problem P1 · Proof of Spanning Equivalence to Maximum Sharpe Ratio

Prove Gibbons, Ross & Shanken (1989) spanning equivalence: a candidate factor \(f\) is spanned by benchmark factors \(K\) (\(\alpha = 0\) in spanning regression) if and only if adding \(f\) to \(K\) does not increase the maximum achievable squared Sharpe ratio: \(\SR_{K+f}^2 = \SR_K^2\).

Worked solution

Let \(f_t\) be the candidate factor and \(K_t\) be the vector of benchmark factors. Project \(f\) onto \(K\): \[\begin{aligned} f_t &= \alpha + \beta' K_t + e_t, \quad \E[e_t] = 0, \quad \Cov(K_t, e_t) = 0. \end{aligned}\] The variance of \(e_t\) is \(\sigma_e^2 = \Var(e_t)\). By the algebraic decomposition of optimal portfolios (GRS 1989): \[\begin{aligned} \SR_{K+f}^2 &= \SR_K^2 + \frac{\alpha^2}{\sigma_e^2}. \end{aligned}\] Because \(\sigma_e^2 > 0\), the increment \(\frac{\alpha^2}{\sigma_e^2} \ge 0\). Therefore, \(\SR_{K+f}^2 = \SR_K^2 \iff \frac{\alpha^2}{\sigma_e^2} = 0 \iff \alpha = 0\). Hence, spanning is mathematically equivalent to zero Sharpe ratio expansion.

Problem P2 · Derivation of the Shanken (1992) Correction

In Fama–MacBeth cross-sectional regressions, Pass 1 estimates betas with estimation error \(\hat{\beta}_i = \beta_i + \epsilon_i\). Prove that the true asymptotic variance of the risk premium estimator \(\hat{\lambda}\) requires the multiplicative inflation factor \((1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda)\).

Worked solution

In Pass 2, the cross-sectional regression at time \(t\) is \(R_t = \hat{\beta} \lambda_t + \eta_t = (\beta + e) \lambda_t + \eta_t\). The OLS estimator is \(\hat{\lambda}_t = (\hat{\beta}' \hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta}' R_t\). Expanding around true parameters: \[\begin{aligned} \hat{\lambda}_t - \lambda &= (f_t - \bar{f}) + (\beta' \beta)^{-1} \beta' e (\lambda - f_t) + (\beta' \beta)^{-1} \beta' \eta_t. \end{aligned}\] Taking the time-series variance of the sample average \(\bar{\lambda} = \frac{1}{T} \sum \hat{\lambda}_t\): The first term contributes \(\frac{1}{T} \Sigma_f\). The second term introduces cross-sectional measurement error from \(\hat{\beta}\). Shanken (1992) demonstrates that under conditional independence, the covariance between estimation error in \(\beta\) and factor realization \(f_t\) inflates the residual variance by the squared Sharpe ratio of the factors: \[\begin{aligned} \text{AsyVar}(\sqrt{T}(\bar{\lambda} - \lambda)) &= \Sigma_f + (1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda) (\beta' \Sigma_\eta^{-1} \beta)^{-1}. \end{aligned}\] The term \(\lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda = \SR_f^2\) represents the squared Sharpe ratio of the benchmark factors, proving the Shanken correction.

Problem P3 · Size vs. Momentum Orthogonality in Independent Sorts

Prove that a \(2 \times 3\) bivariate independent portfolio sort on Size and Momentum asymptotically orthogonalizes the constructed factor returns CSMB and CMOM when characteristic distributions are independent.

Worked solution

Consider the factor definitions: \[\begin{aligned} \text{CSMB} &= \frac{1}{3} (R_{S, L} + R_{S, M} + R_{S, H}) - \frac{1}{3} (R_{B, L} + R_{B, M} + R_{B, H}), \\ \text{CMOM} &= \frac{1}{2} (R_{S, H} + R_{B, H}) - \frac{1}{2} (R_{S, L} + R_{B, L}). \end{aligned}\] Notice that CSMB takes an equal average across all three momentum tiers (Low, Medium, High). Consequently, any common variation in returns driven purely by momentum cancels out: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \text{CSMB}}{\partial R_{\text{Mom}}} &= \frac{1}{3}(1 + 1 + 1) - \frac{1}{3}(1 + 1 + 1) = 1 - 1 = 0. \end{aligned}\] Similarly, CMOM takes an equal average across both size tiers (Small and Big): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \text{CMOM}}{\partial R_{\text{Size}}} &= \frac{1}{2}(1 + 1) - \frac{1}{2}(1 + 1) = 1 - 1 = 0. \end{aligned}\] Because each factor is balanced across the sorting bins of the other, their covariance \(\Cov(\text{CSMB}, \text{CMOM})\) is asymptotically zero under independent marginals, proving structural orthogonality.

Empirical

Problem E1 · Analyzing the Liu–Tsyvinski–Wu (2022) Market Factor

In Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu (2022, Table 3), the value-weighted cryptocurrency market factor CMKT has a weekly mean of 1.62% and a weekly standard deviation of 10.95%. Compute its annualized mean return, annualized volatility, and annualized Sharpe ratio (52 weeks/year, zero risk-free rate).

Worked solution

Annualized expected return: \[\begin{aligned} \mu_{\text{ann}} &= 1.62\% \times 52 = 84.24\% \text{ per year}. \end{aligned}\] Annualized volatility: \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{ann}} &= 10.95\% \times \sqrt{52} \approx 10.95\% \times 7.2111 \approx 78.96\% \text{ per year}. \end{aligned}\] Annualized Sharpe ratio: \[\begin{aligned} \SR_{\text{ann}} &= \frac{\mu_{\text{ann}}}{\sigma_{\text{ann}}} = \frac{84.24\%}{78.96\%} \approx 1.067. \end{aligned}\] The CMKT factor achieved a Sharpe ratio of 1.07, substantially higher than typical historical equity index Sharpe ratios (~0.40–0.50), reflecting compensation for systemic adoption and crash risk.

Problem E2 · Economic Interpretation of the Size Factor in Crypto

Why does the small-cap cryptocurrency factor CSMB generate a massive weekly premium of 1.34% (nearly 70% annualized), far exceeding the equity SMB premium (~2% annualized)?

Worked solution

The enormous CSMB premium reflects distinct crypto-market hazards: (1) Extreme illiquidity: microcap digital assets have bid-ask spreads often exceeding 200–500 bps and negligible market depth; (2) Delisting and abandonment risk: a substantial fraction of small tokens fail completely or are delisted by centralized exchanges within 12 months; (3) Retail speculative mania: microcaps attract lottery-seeking retail flows during market expansions; (4) Limits to arbitrage: shorting microcap crypto assets is functionally impossible due to lack of borrow markets, preventing arbitrageurs from correcting overpricing.

Problem E3 · Factor Spanning Across Bull vs. Bear Cycles

Explain why factor spanning regressions might hold during market bull expansions but break down during severe liquidity drawdowns.

Worked solution

During speculative bull regimes, cross-sectional returns are dominated by market beta and momentum, aligning with linear factor models. However, during systemic liquidity crises (e.g. November 2022 FTX collapse), cross-asset correlations surge toward 1.0, market depth evaporates, and stablecoins or token-specific liquidation spirals occur. Linear spanning models break down because assets experience nonlinear margin call liquidations and flight-to-quality dynamics not captured by unconditional static betas.

Research

Problem R1 · Data Snooping and the P-Hacking Hurdle in Crypto

Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) recommend raising the \(t\)-statistic hurdle for factor discovery in equities from 2.0 to 3.0. Should the statistical significance threshold be higher or lower in cryptocurrency asset pricing?

Worked solution

The statistical hurdle in cryptocurrency asset pricing should be strictly higher (e.g. \(t > 3.5\) or \(4.0\)) for several reasons: (1) Shorter time series: crypto data spans only ~10 years compared to 60+ years in CRSP/Compustat, reducing effective statistical power and increasing susceptibility to regime-specific anomalies; (2) Massive multiple testing: thousands of pseudonymous quantitative researchers and algorithms test millions of technical and on-chain indicators; (3) Extreme non-normality: heavy tails and positive skewness inflate empirical \(t\)-statistics under standard Gaussian asymptotic formulas; (4) Survivorship bias: coin tracking websites often exclude thousands of dead or delisted coins.

Problem R2 · On-Chain Governance and Token Risk Premia

How can differences in on-chain token governance (e.g. centralized founder keys vs. decentralized governance DAOs) be modeled as a cross-sectional risk factor?

Worked solution

To construct a Governance Risk Factor (Governance Minus Autocracy — GMA): (1) Quantify governance centralization using metrics such as Gini coefficient of token voting power, time-lock delay on contract upgrades, and multi-signature threshold requirements; (2) Form long-short portfolios sorting tokens into decentralized versus centralized governance tiers; (3) Test whether tokens with high centralization command a positive risk premium (compensating for rug-pull and regulatory enforcement risk) or trade at a governance discount; (4) Run spanning regressions against CMKT, CSMB, and CMOM to determine if governance risk represents an independent priced risk factor.

Problem R3 · Microstructure Liquidity Haircuts on Crypto MOM

Design an empirical protocol to test whether the CMOM momentum factor remains profitable after accounting for realistic trading fees and bid-ask spreads.

Worked solution

Protocol: (1) Collect tick-level order book snapshots from top centralized exchanges (Binance, Coinbase) across the rebalancing dates; (2) Measure effective bid-ask spread and compute market impact using the Kyle (1985) lambda or square-root law of price impact for a target AUM (e.g. $10M); (3) Incorporate exchange fee schedules (e.g. 10 bps taker fee per turn); (4) Calculate portfolio turnover per weekly rebalance (typically 30–50% for weekly CMOM); (5) Deduct total round-trip friction costs from gross returns: \(R_{\text{net}, t} = R_{\text{gross}, t} - \text{Turnover}_t \times (2 \times \text{Fee} + \text{Spread}_t + \text{Impact}_t)\); (6) Re-estimate Fama–MacBeth risk premia on net returns to establish whether net alpha survives.

  4. What is the null hypothesis in a factor spanning regression \(F_t^{\text{cand}} = \alpha + \beta' F_t^{\text{bench}} + \epsilon_t\)?

    Options

  6. What econometric correction did Shanken (1992) formulate for two-pass Fama–MacBeth regressions?

    Options

    The Shanken correction accounts for the fact that generated regressors (\(\hat{\beta}\)) from Pass 1 contain estimation noise, inflating Pass 2 standard errors.

    Options

  9. In bivariate independent portfolio sorts (e.g. 2 size x 3 momentum), why is independent sorting preferred over sequential conditional sorting?

    Options

    Independent sorts ensure that the size factor averages equally across momentum tiers, neutralizing momentum exposure in the size factor.

  10. According to Gibbons, Ross & Shanken (1989), what happens to the maximum squared Sharpe ratio when a candidate factor with non-zero spanning alpha is added to a factor model?

    Options

    By GRS (1989), \(\SR_{\text{new}}^2 = \SR_{\text{old}}^2 + \alpha^2 / \sigma_e^2\). Any factor with non-zero alpha expands the mean-variance frontier.
