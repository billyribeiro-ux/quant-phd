Purchasing Power Parity

Purchasing power parity anchors international macroeconomics by asserting that goods across borders must equalize in price when denominated in a common currency. If PPP held continuously, nominal exchange rate swings would merely offset inflation differentials, neutralizing real exchange rate risk. In reality, real exchange rates exhibit massive volatility and sluggish mean reversion, requiring 3 to 5 years to cut misalignments in half. Quantifying this persistence is essential for long-horizon corporate hedging, cross-border asset allocation, and macroeconomic equilibrium modeling.

1The Law of One Price and Absolute PPP

The foundational microeconomic building block of international parity conditions is the Law of One Price (LOOP). In an integrated, competitive market without transport costs or trade barriers, an identical tradable good \(i\) must sell for the same price across different national locations when converted to a common currency:

\[\begin{aligned} P_{i,t} &= S_t P^*_{i,t}, \end{aligned}\]

where \(P_{i,t}\) denotes the domestic price, \(P^*_{i,t}\) is the foreign price, and \(S_t\) is the nominal spot exchange rate expressed as domestic currency units per foreign currency unit. Taking natural logarithms yields:

\[\begin{aligned} s_t &= p_{i,t} - p^*_{i,t}, \end{aligned}\]

with lower-case letters indicating natural logs.

Definition 5.1 · Purchasing Power Parity
Purchasing power parity (PPP) is the macroeconomic hypothesis that aggregate price levels equalize across countries when denominated in a common currency, such that the nominal exchange rate equals the ratio of domestic to foreign aggregate price indexes: \[\begin{aligned} S_t &= \frac{P_t}{P^*_t} \iff s_t = p_t - p^*_t. \end{aligned}\]

Absolute PPP requires that the real purchasing power of currency be identical everywhere: a single unit of domestic currency converts to \(1/S_t\) units of foreign currency, purchasing an identical consumption bundle. In practice, absolute PPP fails severely because consumer price indexes (CPI) reflect different expenditure weights, contain non-traded goods (housing, local services), and are published as arbitrary base-year index numbers rather than absolute currency cost levels.

2Relative PPP and Real Exchange Rates

Because baseline price levels vary due to statistical definitions and transportation costs, economists formulate Relative Purchasing Power Parity. Relative PPP requires only that the rate of nominal exchange rate depreciation matches the differential between domestic and foreign inflation rates:

\[\begin{aligned} \Delta s_t &= \pi_t - \pi^*_t, \end{aligned}\]

where \(\pi_t = p_t - p_{t-1}\) and \(\pi^*_t = p^*_t - p^*_{t-1}\). Deviations from relative PPP correspond to changes in the log real exchange rate \(q_t\):

\[\begin{aligned} q_t &= s_t + p^*_t - p_t, \\ \Delta q_t &= \Delta s_t - (\pi_t - \pi^*_t). \end{aligned}\]

Under continuous PPP, \(q_t\) would remain constant over time (\(\Delta q_t = 0\)). When \(q_t > 0\), foreign goods are relatively more expensive than domestic goods, signifying that the domestic currency is real-depreciated (undervalued relative to PPP). Conversely, \(q_t < 0\) signifies a real-appreciated domestic currency.

3The Balassa-Samuelson Productivity Effect

Systematic, permanent departures from absolute and relative PPP between developing and developed nations are explained by the Balassa-Samuelson effect. Assume both economies produce traded (\(T\)) and non-traded (\(N\)) goods using linear labor technologies \(Y_T = A_T L_T\) and \(Y_N = A_N L_N\). Competitive profit maximization requires wages to equal marginal labor revenue:

\[\begin{aligned} W &= P_T A_T = P_N A_N. \end{aligned}\]

Domestic labor mobility forces wages to equalize across domestic sectors. Thus, the relative price of non-traded goods within each country is dictated strictly by relative labor productivity:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{P_N}{P_T} &= \frac{A_T}{A_N} \iff p_N - p_T = a_T - a_N. \end{aligned}\]

Assuming the Law of One Price holds for traded goods (\(p_T = s + p^*_T\)), the log aggregate price differential satisfies:

\[\begin{aligned} p - (s + p^*) &= (1 - \gamma) \left[ (a_T - a^*_T) - (a_N - a^*_N) \right], \end{aligned}\]

where \(\gamma \in (0,1)\) is the expenditure share of traded goods. Developing economies experiencing rapid industrial productivity growth in traded sectors (\(a_T \gg a^*_T\)) experience rising non-traded wages and service prices, causing long-term secular real exchange rate appreciation without losing international export competitiveness.

4Persistence and the PPP Half-Life

In empirical macroeconomics, convergence toward purchasing power parity is modeled as an autoregressive mean-reverting process on the log real exchange rate:

\[\begin{aligned} q_t &= \alpha + \rho q_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \quad |\rho| < 1. \end{aligned}\]

Definition 5.2 · Half-Life of PPP Deviations
The half-life of PPP deviations, denoted \(H\), is the expected time required for a unit shock to the real exchange rate \(\epsilon_t\) to decay by 50% toward its unconditional mean: \[\begin{aligned} \rho^H &= 0.5 \implies H = \frac{\ln(0.5)}{\ln(\rho)}. \end{aligned}\] For monthly data with autoregressive parameter \(\rho\), the half-life expressed in years is: \[\begin{aligned} H_{\text{years}} &= \frac{\ln(0.5)}{12 \ln(\rho)}. \end{aligned}\]

In his landmark survey, Rogoff (1996) as analyzed in Obstfeld & Rogoff (1996) synthesized dozens of empirical studies and established the consensus estimate: the half-life of real exchange rate deviations among floating currencies ranges from 3 to 5 years (implying a monthly autoregressive parameter \(\rho \approx 0.982\) to \(0.988\)).

Lab 5 · Purchasing power parity and half-life estimation

Simulating 30 years of monthly real exchange rate dynamics under an AR(1) process with true \(\rho = 0.985\) (\(H \approx 3.82\) years) will confirm: (1) raw OLS suffers from Kendall downward bias; (2) small-sample bias correction restores true persistence; and (3) the resulting half-life falls precisely within Rogoff's 3–5 year empirical consensus range.

"""Lab X1-L05-1: Purchasing power parity and half-life of real exchange rate deviations.

Estimates AR(1) persistence and half-life of deviations from purchasing power parity,
applying Kendall's small-sample bias correction to confirm the 3-5 year consensus (Rogoff 1996).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def estimate_ppp_half_life(
    seed: int = 16,
    n_months: int = 360,
    true_monthly_rho: float = 0.985,  # implies half-life of ~45.8 months (~3.8 years)
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate real exchange rate deviations and estimate persistence and half-life.

    Real exchange rate: q_t = s_t + p_t^* - p_t.
    AR(1) specification: q_t = alpha + rho * q_{t-1} + e_t.
    Half-life H = ln(0.5) / ln(rho).
    Kendall small-sample bias correction: rho_unbiased = rho_hat + (1 + 3*rho_hat) / T.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    # Generate AR(1) real exchange rate process
    q: list[float] = [0.0]
    for _ in range(1, n_months):
        q_next = true_monthly_rho * q[-1] + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.02)
        q.append(q_next)

    # OLS estimation of q_t on q_{t-1}
    y = q[1:]
    x = q[:-1]
    t_obs = len(y)

    mean_x = sum(x) / t_obs
    mean_y = sum(y) / t_obs

    var_x = sum((xi - mean_x) ** 2 for xi in x)
    cov_xy = sum((xi - mean_x) * (yi - mean_y) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True))

    rho_hat = cov_xy / var_x
    alpha_hat = mean_y - rho_hat * mean_x

    # Kendall small-sample bias correction for AR(1)
    bias = -(1.0 + 3.0 * rho_hat) / float(t_obs)
    rho_corrected = rho_hat - bias

    # Half-life in months
    half_life_raw_months = math.log(0.5) / math.log(rho_hat) if rho_hat < 1.0 else float("inf")
    half_life_corrected_months = math.log(0.5) / math.log(rho_corrected) if rho_corrected < 1.0 else float("inf")

    # True half-life in years
    true_hl_years = (math.log(0.5) / math.log(true_monthly_rho)) / 12.0

    return {
        "true_monthly_rho": true_monthly_rho,
        "true_half_life_years": round(true_hl_years, 2),
        "rho_hat": round(rho_hat, 4),
        "alpha_hat": round(alpha_hat, 6),
        "half_life_raw_years": round(half_life_raw_months / 12.0, 2),
        "rho_corrected": round(rho_corrected, 4),
        "half_life_corrected_years": round(half_life_corrected_months / 12.0, 2),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab X1-L05-1 | Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) Half-Life Estimation")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------------")

    res = estimate_ppp_half_life(seed=16, n_months=360, true_monthly_rho=0.985)

    print("Data Generating Process (Monthly Frequency, T=360 months / 30 years):")
    print(f"  True AR(1) Persistence (rho):      {res['true_monthly_rho']:.4f}")
    print(f"  True Half-Life (years):           {res['true_half_life_years']:>6.2f} years")
    print("\nOLS Estimation Results:")
    print(f"  Estimated rho (uncorrected):      {res['rho_hat']:>6.4f}")
    print(f"  Raw Half-Life Estimate:           {res['half_life_raw_years']:>6.2f} years")
    print("\nKendall Small-Sample Bias Correction:")
    print(f"  Bias-Corrected rho:               {res['rho_corrected']:>6.4f}")
    print(f"  Bias-Corrected Half-Life:         {res['half_life_corrected_years']:>6.2f} years")
    print("\nConsensus Comparison (Rogoff 1996 consensus: 3 to 5 years):")
    in_consensus = 3.0 <= res["half_life_corrected_years"] <= 5.0
    print(f"  Matches 3-5 Year Consensus Range: {in_consensus}")


main()

5Where the Canon Breaks: The PPP Puzzle and Inaction Bands

The "Purchasing Power Parity Puzzle" (Obstfeld & Rogoff 1996) arises from the stark conflict between nominal exchange rate volatility and real exchange rate persistence:

  • Extreme Short-Run Volatility: Floating nominal exchange rates display annualized volatility exceeding 10–15%, moving almost entirely in response to financial asset shocks and monetary policy surprises (Dornbusch 1976).
  • Glacial Convergence Speed: Real exchange rate misalignments persist for 3 to 5 years, far too long to be explained solely by nominal wage and price stickiness (which typically clears within 1 to 2 years).

Physical arbitrage in goods markets faces significant transport costs, tariffs, and distribution hurdles. These frictions create a non-linear "band of inaction" \([-\tau, \tau]\): inside the band, goods arbitrage is unprofitable, and the real exchange rate drifts like a random walk; mean reversion operates only when deviations breach the threshold band.

6Misconceptions

  1. When national CPI indexes are equalized, absolute purchasing power parity holds exactly.

    Why it is wrong: National CPI series are index numbers calibrated to an arbitrary baseline year (such as 2015 = 100); they do not reflect the actual currency expenditure required to buy identical market baskets. Even if both CPI indexes equal 100 today, absolute price levels across countries can differ by an order of magnitude.

  2. Sluggish real exchange rate reversion contradicts market efficiency.

    Why it is wrong: Physical goods markets are inherently subject to transport costs, tariffs, shipping delays, and sticky multi-period retail contracts. Financial markets clear immediately, but physical goods arbitrage cannot instantaneously eliminate price disparities, creating an inaction band where real exchange rates move freely.

  3. Standard OLS estimates of autoregressive persistence in real exchange rates are unbiased in 20-to-30 year samples.

    Why it is wrong: In finite samples of length \(T\), OLS estimates of AR(1) coefficients near unity suffer from substantial Kendall downward bias (\(\E[\hat{\rho} - \rho] \approx -(1 + 3\rho)/T\)). Uncorrected OLS severely underestimates persistence, leading researchers to conclude half-lives are under 2 years when true persistence is 4 to 5 years.

7Practice Problems

Mechanics

1. A monthly regression of the log real exchange rate on its lagged value yields an estimated autoregressive parameter \(\hat{\rho} = 0.980\). Compute the estimated half-life of real exchange rate deviations in months and in years.

Solution

The half-life formula is \(H = \ln(0.5) / \ln(\hat{\rho})\). Substituting \(\hat{\rho} = 0.980\), we have \(\ln(0.5) \approx -0.693147\) and \(\ln(0.980) \approx -0.020203\). Thus, \(H_{\text{months}} = -0.693147 / -0.020203 \approx 34.31\) months. Converting to years yields \(H_{\text{years}} = 34.31 / 12 \approx 2.86\) years.

2. Suppose the domestic CPI index is \(P = 120\), foreign CPI index is \(P^* = 100\), and the nominal exchange rate is \(S = 1.30\) domestic units per foreign unit. If both indexes equaled 100 when absolute PPP held, calculate the log real exchange rate \(q\) and state whether the domestic currency is overvalued or undervalued.

Solution

The log real exchange rate is \(q = \ln(S) + \ln(P^*) - \ln(P) = \ln(1.30) + \ln(100) - \ln(120)\). Numerically, \(\ln(1.30) \approx 0.26236\), \(\ln(100/120) = \ln(0.83333) \approx -0.18232\). Thus, \(q \approx 0.26236 - 0.18232 = +0.08004\) (or \(+8.00\%\)). Because \(q > 0\), foreign goods are more expensive than domestic goods, meaning the domestic currency is real-depreciated (undervalued relative to PPP).

3. Over a sample of \(T = 240\) monthly observations, the raw OLS estimate of real exchange rate persistence is \(\hat{\rho} = 0.970\). Using Kendall's formula \(\text{Bias} \approx -(1 + 3\hat{\rho})/T\), compute the bias-corrected autoregressive coefficient \(\hat{\rho}_{\text{corrected}}\) and the resulting half-life in years.

Solution

Kendall's bias is \(\text{Bias} \approx -(1 + 3(0.970)) / 240 = -(1 + 2.91) / 240 = -3.91 / 240 \approx -0.01629\). The bias-corrected coefficient is \(\hat{\rho}_{\text{corrected}} = 0.970 - (-0.01629) \approx 0.98629\). The raw half-life was \(\ln(0.5) / (12 \cdot \ln(0.970)) \approx -0.693147 / (12 \cdot (-0.030459)) \approx 1.896\) years. The bias-corrected half-life is \(\ln(0.5) / (12 \cdot \ln(0.98629)) \approx -0.693147 / (12 \cdot (-0.013795)) \approx 4.187\) years, shifting the estimate cleanly into the Rogoff consensus window.

Proof & Derivations

1. Prove that for an AR(1) real exchange rate process \(q_t = \alpha + \rho q_{t-1} + \epsilon_t\) with \(|\rho| < 1\), the impulse response function at horizon \(k\) is given by \(\partial q_{t+k} / \partial \epsilon_t = \rho^k\), and derive the closed-form expression for the half-life \(H\).

Solution

By repeated backward substitution, \(q_{t+k} = \alpha \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \rho^j + \rho^k q_t + \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \rho^j \epsilon_{t+k-j}\). Since \(q_t = \alpha + \rho q_{t-1} + \epsilon_t\), we have \(\partial q_t / \partial \epsilon_t = 1\). Differentiating \(q_{t+k}\) with respect to \(\epsilon_t\) yields \(\partial q_{t+k} / \partial \epsilon_t = \rho^k \cdot (\partial q_t / \partial \epsilon_t) = \rho^k\). The half-life \(H\) is defined as the elapsed time at which the impulse response decays to 0.5 of its initial unit value: \(\rho^H = 0.5 \implies H \ln(\rho) = \ln(0.5) \implies H = \ln(0.5) / \ln(\rho)\). Since \(0 < \rho < 1\), \(\ln(\rho) < 0\) and \(\ln(0.5) < 0\), ensuring \(H > 0\).

2. In the Balassa-Samuelson model, assume production functions are linear in labor: \(Y_T = A_T L_T\) and \(Y_N = A_N L_N\). Perfect domestic labor mobility equates wages across sectors (\(W = P_T A_T = P_N A_N\)). If the law of one price holds for traded goods (\(P_T = S P^*_T\)) and foreign production functions are similarly defined with \(A^*_T, A^*_N\), prove that the relative price ratio \(P / (S P^*)\) increases when domestic traded sector productivity growth outpaces foreign traded sector productivity growth.

Solution

In logs, \(p_N = p_T + a_T - a_N\) and \(p^*_N = p^*_T + a^*_T - a^*_N\). Let aggregate price indexes be geometric averages with weight \(\gamma\) on traded goods: \(p = \gamma p_T + (1 - \gamma) p_N = p_T + (1 - \gamma)(a_T - a_N)\). Similarly, \(p^* = p^*_T + (1 - \gamma)(a^*_T - a^*_N)\). By LOOP for traded goods, \(s = p_T - p^*_T\). Substituting into the log real exchange rate \(q = s + p^* - p\): \(q = (p_T - p^*_T) + [p^*_T + (1 - \gamma)(a^*_T - a^*_N)] - [p_T + (1 - \gamma)(a_T - a_N)] = -(1 - \gamma)[(a_T - a^*_T) - (a_N - a^*_N)]\). Therefore, if domestic traded productivity growth exceeds foreign traded productivity growth (\(a_T - a^*_T > a_N - a^*_N\)), \(q\) decreases (a real appreciation, \(P / (S P^*)\) rises).

3. Show that if trade costs introduce a proportional friction \(\tau \in (0, 1)\) such that arbitrage occurs only when \(|s_t - (p_t - p^*_t)| > \tau\), the real exchange rate follows a threshold autoregressive (TAR) process that behaves like a random walk inside the band \([-\tau, \tau]\) and mean-reverts only outside the band.

Solution

Define \(q_t = s_t + p^*_t - p_t\). An arbitrageur faces transaction, shipping, and tariff costs \(\tau\). For any price disparity inside the band \(|q_t| \le \tau\), expected trading profit \(\Pi = |q_t| - \tau \le 0\), so no arbitrage occurs and goods markets remain uncoupled. Consequently, \(q_t = q_{t-1} + \epsilon_t\) (a driftless unit-root process) within the corridor. Outside the band, when \(|q_t| > \tau\), arbitrage yields strictly positive profit, driving international order flow and price pressure that forces \(q_t\) back toward the band edges: \(\Delta q_t = -\kappa \text{sgn}(q_{t-1})(|q_{t-1}| - \tau) + \epsilon_t\) for \(|q_{t-1}| > \tau\) with \(\kappa > 0\). This establishes non-linear mean reversion with an inaction band.

Empirical Analysis

1. Why do unit root tests (e.g., Augmented Dickey-Fuller) frequently fail to reject the null hypothesis of a unit root in bilateral real exchange rates over 20-30 year post-Bretton Woods samples, despite PPP holding over centuries of data?

Solution

The power of standard Dickey-Fuller tests against highly persistent alternatives (\(\rho \approx 0.985\) monthly) is notoriously low in samples spanning only 20 to 30 years (240 to 360 observations). Over 30 years, an AR(1) process with a 4-year half-life completes fewer than 8 half-lives of reversion, providing insufficient independent cycles to distinguish a root of 0.985 from 1.000 at standard significance levels. Long-horizon panel datasets covering 100 to 200 years of data achieve substantially greater statistical power and consistently reject the unit root null.

2. Explain how the choice between headline CPI and core CPI impacts empirical estimates of real exchange rate persistence and volatility.

Solution

Headline CPI includes highly volatile components such as food and energy commodities, which are heavily traded internationally and subject to sharp supply shocks. Using headline CPI can introduce high-frequency transitory noise into \(p_t - p^*_t\), slightly lowering the measured persistence of \(q_t\). Core CPI removes food and energy, focusing on sticky non-traded services and manufactured goods; real exchange rates constructed with core CPI exhibit even higher persistence and smoother trajectories.

3. In panel unit root tests of purchasing power parity (such as Levin-Lin-Chu or Im-Pesaran-Shin), what critical assumption regarding cross-sectional dependence must be handled to prevent spurious rejection of the non-stationary null?

Solution

Bilateral real exchange rates defined against a common numeraire currency (e.g., the US dollar) share strong cross-sectional correlation driven by global dollar cycles and common macroeconomic shocks. Standard first-generation panel unit root tests assume cross-sectional independence; violating this assumption leads to massive size distortions and severe over-rejection of the unit root null. Second-generation panel unit root tests (such as Pesaran's CADF) explicitly account for cross-sectional factor dependence by augmenting regressions with cross-sectional averages.

Research & Extensions

1. Formulate how currency valuation models utilized by institutional macro hedge funds translate real exchange rate deviations into trade signals, and why they pair PPP with carry and momentum factors.

Solution

Institutional macro funds compute valuation z-scores \(z_{i,t} = (q_{i,t} - \bar{q}_{i}) / \sigma_q\), going long undervalued currencies (\(z > 0\)) and short overvalued ones (\(z < 0\)). However, because PPP has a 3-5 year half-life, a standalone valuation strategy experiences prolonged drawdowns when misalignments persist or expand. By combining PPP valuation with short-horizon momentum (which rides ongoing trend deviations) and carry (which collects positive yield while waiting for mean reversion), the multi-factor portfolio achieves negative correlation among alpha streams and superior Sharpe ratios.

2. Discuss how the Dornbusch (1976) exchange rate overshooting model reconciles short-run nominal exchange rate volatility with long-run PPP equilibrium.

Solution

Dornbusch (1976) assumes that financial asset markets clear instantaneously under uncovered interest parity, whereas goods markets adjust with price stickiness. Following an unanticipated domestic monetary expansion, the domestic interest rate falls to clear money markets. Under UIP, for investors to hold lower-yielding domestic assets, the domestic currency must be expected to appreciate. To appreciate in the future while depreciating in the long run to its new PPP level, the spot exchange rate must immediately overshoot its long-run equilibrium value. As sticky goods prices slowly rise over subsequent years, the exchange rate appreciates back toward its long-run PPP level.

3. Why do tradable goods prices across online retail platforms (e.g., the Billion Prices Project) still exhibit price stickiness and border effects despite minimal physical search costs?

Solution

Empirical studies using web-scraped micro-price data reveal that online prices still exhibit substantial nominal stickiness and national segmentation. Firms practice active market segmentation (pricing to market) reflecting country-specific demand elasticities, local sales taxes, shipping and return logistics, local distribution and advertising overheads, and currency hedging frictions. Consequently, even frictionless digital catalog pricing fails to eliminate international real exchange rate disparities across national borders.
