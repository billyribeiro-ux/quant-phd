The Forward Premium Anomaly

The forward premium anomaly is one of the most resilient empirical paradoxes in asset pricing. In efficient markets with risk neutrality, the forward discount should act as an unbiased predictor of subsequent exchange rate depreciation, yielding a regression slope of unity. In historical data, the slope is not merely different from 1; it is overwhelmingly negative across developed currency pairs. Eugene Fama proved that this negative slope imposes severe mathematical restrictions on asset pricing models: the variance of time-varying currency risk premia must exceed the variance of expected currency depreciation, and the two components must be negatively correlated. This lesson unpacks the Fama decomposition, evaluates structural resolutions, and analyzes the limits of currency risk premia.

1The Forward Premium Anomaly

In wholesale currency markets, an investor hedging a foreign asset locks in the forward exchange rate \(F_t\). Under Covered Interest Parity, the forward premium \(f_t - s_t\) equals the interest rate differential \((i_t - i_t^*)k\). If Uncovered Interest Parity holds under rational expectations, the forward premium should equal the market's expected currency depreciation \(\E_t[s_{t+k} - s_t]\).

Definition 4.1 · Forward-Premium Anomaly
The forward-premium anomaly (established as the forward-premium puzzle in E8) denotes the empirical rejection of forward rate unbiasedness in the regression introduced by Fama (1984): \[\begin{aligned} s_{t+k} - s_t &= \alpha + \beta (f_t - s_t) \\ &\quad + \epsilon_{t+k}. \end{aligned}\] While theoretical forward rate unbiasedness requires \(\beta = 1.0\), empirical estimates across developed currency pairs are consistently negative (\(\hat{\beta} < 0\)).

A negative slope (\(\hat{\beta} < 0\)) implies that when a foreign currency trades at a forward discount (\(f_t - s_t < 0\), corresponding to a higher domestic interest rate \(i_t > i_t^*\)), the domestic currency tends to appreciate rather than depreciate over the subsequent period. This systematic predictability directly violates the joint hypothesis of market efficiency and risk neutrality.

2The Fama (1984) Variance Decomposition

In his seminal paper, Fama (1984) demonstrated that a negative regression slope \(\beta < 0\) cannot be dismissed as random noise. Instead, it imposes strict necessary conditions on the second moments of expected depreciation and currency risk premia.

Let \(q_t = \E_t[s_{t+1} - s_t]\) denote expected currency depreciation, and let \(p_t = f_t - \E_t[s_{t+1}]\) denote the expected currency risk premium. The forward premium decomposes additively: \(f_t - s_t = q_t + p_t\).

Theorem 4.1 · Fama's Necessary Conditions for \(\beta < 0\)
In the Fama regression, under rational expectations, the population regression coefficient \(\beta\) is: \[\begin{aligned} \beta &= \frac{\Var(q_t) + \Cov(q_t, p_t)}{\Var(f_t - s_t)}, \\ \Var(f_t - s_t) &= \Var(q_t) + \Var(p_t) \\ &\quad + 2\Cov(q_t, p_t). \end{aligned}\] Consequently, \(\beta < 0\) if and only if: \[\begin{aligned} \Cov(q_t, p_t) < -\Var(q_t) < 0, \end{aligned}\] which requires as a necessary condition that: \[\begin{aligned} \Var(p_t) > \Var(q_t). \end{aligned}\]

Proof
Under rational expectations, realized exchange rate change is \(s_{t+1} - s_t = q_t + \eta_{t+1}\), where \(\E_t[\eta_{t+1}] = 0\) and \(\Cov(\eta_{t+1}, f_t - s_t) = 0\). The population OLS regression slope is: \[\begin{aligned} \beta &= \frac{\Cov(s_{t+1} - s_t, f_t - s_t)}{\Var(f_t - s_t)}. \end{aligned}\] Substitute \(s_{t+1} - s_t = q_t + \eta_{t+1}\) and \(f_t - s_t = q_t + p_t\): \[\begin{aligned} \Cov(s_{t+1} - s_t, f_t - s_t) &= \Cov(q_t, q_t + p_t) \\ &= \Var(q_t) + \Cov(q_t, p_t). \end{aligned}\] The variance of the denominator is: \[\begin{aligned} \Var(f_t - s_t) &= \Var(q_t + p_t) \\ &= \Var(q_t) + \Var(p_t) \\ &\quad + 2\Cov(q_t, p_t). \end{aligned}\] Because \(\Var(f_t - s_t) > 0\), \(\beta < 0\) requires the numerator to be strictly negative: \[\begin{aligned} \Var(q_t) + \Cov(q_t, p_t) < 0 \\ &\implies \Cov(q_t, p_t) < -\Var(q_t). \end{aligned}\] Since \(\Var(q_t) > 0\), this implies \(\Cov(q_t, p_t) < 0\). By Cauchy–Schwarz: \[\begin{aligned} (\Var(q_t))^2 < (\Cov(q_t, p_t))^2 \\ &\le \Var(q_t) \Var(p_t) \\ &\implies \Var(p_t) > \Var(q_t). \end{aligned}\] The variance of the time-varying risk premium must strictly exceed the variance of expected currency depreciation, completing the proof.

Lab 4 · Forward premium anomaly and Fama variance decomposition

Simulating expected depreciation and time-varying risk premia under Fama's conditions (\(\Var(p) > \Var(q)\) and \(\Cov(q, p) < 0\)) will demonstrate that: (1) both necessary conditions hold; (2) the theoretical and empirical Fama regression slopes are negative (\(\beta \approx -0.75\)); (3) violating either condition forces \(\beta \ge 0\).

"""Lab X1-L04-1: Forward premium anomaly and Fama variance decomposition.

Estimates the Fama (1984) regression on simulated forward premia and verifies
Fama's necessary conditions for a negative beta: Var(p_t) > Var(E[Delta s]) and Cov(E[Delta s], p_t) < 0.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def fama_decomposition(
    seed: int = 101,
    n_obs: int = 240,
    var_expected_ds: float = 0.0001,
    var_risk_premium: float = 0.0004,
    corr_ed_p: float = -0.75,
) -> dict[str, float | bool]:
    """Simulate expected depreciation and time-varying risk premium to test Fama conditions.

    Forward premium: fp_t = f_t - s_t = E_t[Delta s_{t+1}] + p_t
    Fama beta = Cov(Delta s_{t+1}, fp_t) / Var(fp_t)
              = [Var(E[Delta s]) + Cov(E[Delta s], p_t)] / Var(fp_t)
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    # Covariance between expected depreciation and risk premium
    cov_ed_p = corr_ed_p * math.sqrt(var_expected_ds * var_risk_premium)

    # Variance of the forward premium fp = E[Delta s] + p
    var_fp = var_expected_ds + var_risk_premium + 2.0 * cov_ed_p

    # Theoretical Fama beta:
    beta_theoretical = (var_expected_ds + cov_ed_p) / var_fp

    # Simulate series
    sd_e = math.sqrt(var_expected_ds)
    sd_p = math.sqrt(var_risk_premium)

    fp_series: list[float] = []
    ds_series: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(n_obs):
        # Bivariate normal draw with correlation corr_ed_p
        z1 = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        z2 = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        e_ds = sd_e * z1
        p_t = sd_p * (corr_ed_p * z1 + math.sqrt(1.0 - corr_ed_p**2) * z2)

        # Forward premium
        fp = e_ds + p_t
        # Realized exchange rate change = E_t[Delta s] + rational expectations error
        realized_ds = e_ds + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.02)

        fp_series.append(fp)
        ds_series.append(realized_ds)

    # Sample regression of realized Delta s on forward premium
    mean_fp = sum(fp_series) / n_obs
    mean_ds = sum(ds_series) / n_obs

    sample_var_fp = sum((x - mean_fp) ** 2 for x in fp_series)
    sample_cov = sum((x - mean_fp) * (y - mean_ds) for x, y in zip(fp_series, ds_series, strict=True))
    beta_empirical = sample_cov / sample_var_fp

    cond1 = var_risk_premium > var_expected_ds
    cond2 = cov_ed_p < 0.0
    both_hold = cond1 and cond2

    return {
        "beta_theoretical": round(beta_theoretical, 4),
        "beta_empirical": round(beta_empirical, 4),
        "var_expected_ds": round(var_expected_ds, 6),
        "var_risk_premium": round(var_risk_premium, 6),
        "cov_ed_p": round(cov_ed_p, 6),
        "fama_condition_1": cond1,
        "fama_condition_2": cond2,
        "both_conditions_hold": both_hold,
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab X1-L04-1 | Forward Premium Anomaly & Fama (1984) Decomposition")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------")

    res = fama_decomposition(
        seed=101,
        n_obs=240,
        var_expected_ds=0.0001,
        var_risk_premium=0.0004,
        corr_ed_p=-0.75,
    )

    print("Variance & Covariance Components:")
    print(f"  Var(E_t[Delta s_{{t+1}}]):       {res['var_expected_ds']:.6f}")
    print(f"  Var(p_t) (Risk Premium):       {res['var_risk_premium']:.6f}")
    print(f"  Cov(E_t[Delta s_{{t+1}}], p_t): {res['cov_ed_p']:.6f}")
    print("\nFama Necessary Conditions for Negative Beta (beta < 0):")
    print(f"  Condition 1: Var(p_t) > Var(E_t[Delta s]):     {res['fama_condition_1']}")
    print(f"  Condition 2: Cov(E_t[Delta s], p_t) < 0:        {res['fama_condition_2']}")
    print(f"  Both Conditions Hold:                           {res['both_conditions_hold']}")
    print("\nRegression Slope Coefficient:")
    print(f"  Theoretical Fama Beta:                         {res['beta_theoretical']:>8.4f}")
    print(f"  Empirical Estimated Beta:                      {res['beta_empirical']:>8.4f}")


main()

Standard macroeconomic models that assume constant or small risk premia are mathematically incapable of generating a negative Fama beta.

3Macroeconomic Explanations of the Anomaly

Economists have proposed three primary structural frameworks to generate the volatile, negatively correlated risk premia required by Theorem 4.1:

  • Cross-Sectional Currency Risk Premia: Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan (2011) demonstrate that currency excess returns are driven by two common risk factors: a level dollar factor and a slope "carry" factor (HML-FX). High-interest currencies systematically depreciate during global liquidity crashes, exposing investors to global volatility risk.
  • Rare Disasters and Peso Problems: Burnside, Eichenbaum, Kleshchelski & Rebelo (2010) show that currency carry trades generate large steady returns punctuated by rare catastrophic drawdowns. If rational investors price crash risk that occurs infrequently in historical samples, econometric regressions find \(\hat{\beta} < 0\).
  • Intermediary Constraints and Limits to Arbitrage: Capital constraints on global dealer balance sheets prevent arbitrageurs from taking the massive leveraged positions required to eliminate the forward premium wedge, allowing risk premia to fluctuate with market volatility.

4Where the Canon Breaks: Emerging Markets vs Developed Currencies

The forward premium anomaly is primarily a developed market phenomenon. In emerging market economies (such as Turkey, Argentina, and Brazil), inflation differentials are large and monetary policy volatility is extreme. In these economies, the fundamental inflation channel dominates risk premium fluctuations, and empirical Fama regressions typically yield positive slope coefficients (\(\hat{\beta} > 0\)), confirming that high inflation currencies do indeed depreciate rapidly (Obstfeld & Rogoff 1996).

5Misconceptions

  1. A negative Fama regression slope proves that foreign exchange markets are inefficient.

    Why it is wrong: Market efficiency implies that excess returns cannot be earned without bearing risk. If high-interest currencies compensate investors for systematic crash risk or illiquidity during global market downturns, the negative slope is fully consistent with rational equilibrium asset pricing.

  2. Fama's decomposition shows that expected exchange rate depreciation is more volatile than the currency risk premium.

    Why it is wrong: Theorem 4.1 mathematically proves the exact opposite: for \(\beta < 0\), the variance of the risk premium \(\Var(p_t)\) must strictly exceed the variance of expected depreciation \(\Var(q_t)\).

  3. The forward premium anomaly guarantees profits every month for currency carry traders.

    Why it is wrong: While carry trades have positive average returns over long samples, monthly carry returns exhibit high volatility and sharp negative skewness. Severe unwinds (such as the 2008 Lehman collapse or the August 2024 yen unwind) inflict massive losses in days.

6Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 4.1: In a currency market, \(\Var(q_t) = 0.0002\) and \(\Var(p_t) = 0.0008\). If the correlation between expected depreciation \(q_t\) and risk premium \(p_t\) is \(\rho = -0.85\), calculate the covariance \(\Cov(q_t, p_t)\), the variance of the forward premium \(\Var(f_t - s_t)\), and the theoretical Fama slope \(\beta\).

Solution

The covariance is \(\Cov(q, p) = \rho \sigma_q \sigma_p = -0.85 \times 0.0004 = -0.00034\). The forward premium variance is: \[\begin{aligned} \Var(f - s) &= \Var(q) + \Var(p) + 2\Cov(q, p) \\ &= 0.0002 + 0.0008 - 0.00068 \\ &= 0.00032. \end{aligned}\] The theoretical Fama slope is: \[\begin{aligned} \beta &= \frac{\Var(q) + \Cov(q, p)}{\Var(f - s)} \\ &= \frac{0.0002 - 0.00034}{0.00032} \\ &= -0.4375. \end{aligned}\]

Problem 4.2: Suppose a researcher estimates the Fama regression on quarterly data and finds \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.002\), \(\hat{\beta} = -1.10\), and \(R^2 = 0.03\). If the 3-month forward premium on EUR/USD is \(-0.015\) (-1.5%), what is the expected realized exchange rate change \(\Delta s_{t+1}\)?

Solution

The predicted currency change is: \[\begin{aligned} \Delta s_{t+1} &= \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} (f_t - s_t) \\ &= 0.002 + (-1.10)(-0.015) \\ &= +0.0185. \end{aligned}\] This corresponds to a +1.85% appreciation. Under forward rate unbiasedness, the expected change would have been -1.50%. The model predicts an exchange rate change in the opposite direction of the forward discount.

Problem 4.3: In the Fama regression, if \(\Var(q_t) = 0.0005\) and \(\Cov(q_t, p_t) = -0.0002\), can the regression slope \(\beta\) be negative? Explain using Theorem 4.1.

Solution

By Theorem 4.1, \(\beta < 0\) if and only if \(\Cov(q_t, p_t) < -\Var(q_t)\). Here, \(-\Var(q_t) = -0.0005\). Because \(\Cov(q_t, p_t) = -0.0002 > -0.0005\), the numerator \(\Var(q_t) + \Cov(q_t, p_t) = 0.0005 - 0.0002 = +0.0003 > 0\). Therefore, the regression slope \(\beta\) cannot be negative; it must be strictly positive.

Proof

Problem 4.4: Prove that if currency risk premia are constant over time (\(p_t = \bar{p}\)), the population slope coefficient in the Fama regression must equal exactly unity (\(\beta = 1.0\)).

Solution

If \(p_t = \bar{p}\) is constant, then \(\Var(p_t) = 0\) and \(\Cov(q_t, p_t) = 0\). Substituting into the Fama formula from Theorem 4.1 yields: \[\begin{aligned} \beta &= \frac{\Var(q_t) + 0}{\Var(q_t) + 0 + 2(0)} \\ &= \frac{\Var(q_t)}{\Var(q_t)} = 1.0. \end{aligned}\] Constant risk premia are mathematically incapable of generating deviations from \(\beta = 1.0\); time-varying risk premia are strictly necessary.

Problem 4.5: Prove that if \(\beta < 0\), the correlation between expected depreciation \(q_t\) and the risk premium \(p_t\) must be strictly negative: \(\Corr(q_t, p_t) < -\sqrt{\frac{\Var(q_t)}{\Var(p_t)}} < 0\).

Solution

By Theorem 4.1, \(\beta < 0\) requires \(\Cov(q_t, p_t) < -\Var(q_t)\). Dividing both sides by standard deviations \(\sigma_q \sigma_p\) gives: \[\begin{aligned} \Corr(q_t, p_t) &= \frac{\Cov(q_t, p_t)}{\sigma_q \sigma_p} \\ &< -\frac{\sigma_q}{\sigma_p} = -\sqrt{\frac{\Var(q_t)}{\Var(p_t)}}. \end{aligned}\] Because variances are strictly positive, the upper bound is strictly negative, proving that \(\Corr(q_t, p_t) < 0\).

Problem 4.6: Show that the regression of the forward contract payoff \(f_t - s_{t+1}\) on the forward premium \(f_t - s_t\) has slope coefficient \(\beta_{\text{excess}} = 1 - \beta\), where \(\beta\) is the standard Fama slope.

Solution

The excess return from forward foreign currency exposure is \(f_t - s_{t+1} = (f_t - s_t) - (s_{t+1} - s_t)\). Regressing this payoff on \(f_t - s_t\), the slope is: \[\begin{aligned} \beta_{\text{excess}} &= \frac{\Var(f_t - s_t)}{\Var(f_t - s_t)} \\ &\quad - \frac{\Cov(s_{t+1} - s_t, f_t - s_t)}{\Var(f_t - s_t)} \\ &= 1 - \beta. \end{aligned}\] When \(\beta < 0\), \(\beta_{\text{excess}} > 1\), confirming that the forward premium predicts forward excess returns with a slope exceeding unity.

Empirical

Problem 4.7: In Burnside et al. (2010), the authors find that hedging out-of-the-money crash risk using currency options reduces the Sharpe ratio of the carry trade. What does this reveal about the source of carry profits?

Solution

Because buying out-of-the-money put options to protect against crash risk is expensive, hedged carry strategies experience significant performance degradation. This confirms that currency carry trade excess returns do not represent pure alpha; rather, they reflect compensation for selling disaster insurance to the market, which is consumed when paying option premia to eliminate tail crash exposure.

Problem 4.8: In the Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan (2011) two-factor model, how does the HML-FX factor correlate with global equity market volatility (VIX)?

Solution

The HML-FX carry factor exhibits strong, statistically significant negative correlation with innovations in global market volatility (VIX). During market crashes and liquidity contractions, investors flee high-yielding emerging and commodity currencies to seek refuge in funding currencies (USD, JPY, CHF), causing severe drawdowns in the HML-FX portfolio precisely when the marginal utility of wealth is highest.

Problem 4.9: Why does the forward premium anomaly disappear or reverse (\(\beta > 0\)) during periods of hyperinflation or in high-inflation emerging markets?

Solution

Under high inflation, nominal interest rate differentials are dominated by massive expected inflation differentials rather than time-varying risk premia (\(\Var(q_t) \gg \Var(p_t)\)). By Theorem 4.1, when \(\Var(q_t)\) dwarfs \(\Var(p_t)\), the condition \(\Var(p_t) > \Var(q_t)\) fails, and \(\beta\) converges toward its theoretical rational expectations value of +1.0.

Research

Problem 4.10: How does Verdelhan's (2017) demonstration of a common global dollar factor challenge bilateral macroeconomic models of the forward premium anomaly?

Solution

Verdelhan (2017) shows that a single common dollar factor accounts for over 50% of the variation in bilateral exchange rates of developed currencies against the US dollar. Bilateral models that focus solely on country-specific monetary shocks or bilateral trade imbalances fail to capture this synchronized global co-movement, indicating that the forward premium anomaly is fundamentally driven by global dollar risk premia rather than idiosyncratic bilateral fundamentals.

Problem 4.11: Evaluate how survey data on professional exchange rate forecasts (e.g. Consensus Forecasts) inform the debate between time-varying risk premia and expectational errors in explaining \(\beta < 0\).

Solution

Studies examining survey expectations find that professional forecasters expect high-interest-rate currencies to appreciate, contradicting theoretical UIP. This reveals that \(\beta < 0\) cannot be explained solely by rational risk premia with unbiased expectations; systematic expectational errors and extrapolative beliefs play a substantial role in driving forward premium dynamics.

Problem 4.12: What are the implications of the forward premium anomaly for corporate currency hedging policies in multinational non-financial corporations?

Solution

If forward rates were unbiased predictors of future spot rates, hedging would merely reduce variance without affecting expected cash flows. Because \(\beta < 0\), systematic forward hedging of foreign revenues from high-interest currencies locks in a forward discount, incurring a persistent negative expected return. Multinationals must balance the reduction in balance-sheet volatility against the cash cost of hedging against the forward premium anomaly.

8Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the theoretical value of \(\beta\) in the Fama regression under the joint null of forward rate unbiasedness and rational expectations?

    Under unbiasedness, \(f_t - s_t = \E_t[\Delta s_{t+1}]\), implying an exact regression slope of \(\beta = 1.0\).

  2. What is Fama's primary mathematical conclusion regarding the variance of currency risk premia when \(\beta < 0\)?

    Options

    Theorem 4.1 proves that \(\beta < 0\) requires \(\Var(p_t) > \Var(q_t)\); the risk premium must fluctuate more than expected depreciation.

  3. What must be the sign of the covariance between expected depreciation \(q_t\) and the risk premium \(p_t\) for \(\beta < 0\)?

    Options

    For the numerator \(\Var(q_t) + \Cov(q_t, p_t)\) to be negative, the covariance must be strictly negative and larger in magnitude than \(\Var(q_t)\).

  4. How does Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan (2011) explain the currency carry trade excess return?

    Options

    Carry trade returns reflect compensation for global risk: high-yielding currencies crash during times of market distress and volatility spikes.

  5. Why does the forward premium anomaly disappear in emerging markets experiencing high inflation?

    Options

    When \(\Var(q_t) \gg \Var(p_t)\), Fama's necessary condition fails, restoring positive regression slopes (\(\beta > 0\)).

  6. If the Fama regression slope is \(\beta = -1.10\), what is the slope coefficient \(\beta_{\text{excess}}\) in the regression of excess forward returns \(f_t - s_{t+1}\) on the forward premium \(f_t - s_t\)?

    As proved in Problem 4.6, \(\beta_{\text{excess}} = 1 - \beta = 1 - (-1.10) = 2.10\).

  7. What is Burnside et al.'s (2010) "peso problem" explanation of the forward premium puzzle?

    Options

    Peso problems occur when rational investors demand a risk premium for rare disaster events that are unobserved in the econometric sample.

  8. If risk premia are constant over time, what must be the value of \(\beta\) in the Fama regression?

    Options

    If risk premia are constant, \(\Var(p_t) = 0\) and \(\Cov(q_t, p_t) = 0\), forcing \(\beta = \Var(q_t)/\Var(q_t) = 1.0\).
