Cross-Currency Basis & FX Forward Points

In wholesale currency markets, foreign exchange forwards and swaps are quoted not as outright exchange rates, but as forward points added to the prevailing spot rate. Following the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, the textbook equality between forward points and interest rate differentials shattered. Across major currencies, a persistent non-zero wedge—the cross-currency basis—emerged, turning covered interest parity from an exact no-arbitrage identity into an asset pricing spread. This lesson explores the mathematics of forward points, derives the cross-currency basis from money-market cash flows, and uncovers how post-crisis regulatory capital requirements and intermediary balance-sheet constraints drive global dollar funding premia.

1Forward Outright Quotation and FX Forward Points

In wholesale foreign exchange markets, dealers do not quote outright forward rates \(F_t\) directly. Instead, they quote the spot exchange rate \(S_t\) alongside the forward spread, universally termed swap points or forward points.

Definition 2.1 · FX Forward Points
FX forward points (or swap points) \(\Delta_{\text{pts}}\) represent the difference between the forward outright rate \(F_{t, \tau}\) and the spot rate \(S_t\), scaled by the quotation convention pip unit (\(10^{-4}\) for four-decimal pairs like EUR/USD, GBP/USD, and USD/CHF; \(10^{-2}\) for two-decimal pairs like USD/JPY): \[\begin{aligned} \Delta_{\text{pts}} &= (F_{t, \tau} - S_t) \times 10^4. \end{aligned}\] The outright forward rate is recovered by adding scaled forward points to the spot rate: \[\begin{aligned} F_{t, \tau} &= S_t + \Delta_{\text{pts}} \times 10^{-4}. \end{aligned}\]

When forward points are positive (\(\Delta_{\text{pts}} > 0\)), the foreign currency trades at a forward premium relative to the domestic currency, indicating by CIP that domestic nominal interest rates exceed foreign interest rates. Conversely, when \(\Delta_{\text{pts}} < 0\), the foreign currency trades at a forward discount.

2The Cross-Currency Basis

Prior to 2008, forward points tracked the interbank interest rate differential \((i_t - i_t^*)\) within fractions of a basis point. Since 2008, synthetic dollar borrowing via FX swaps has systematically deviated from direct cash borrowing. This pricing discrepancy is formalized as the cross-currency basis (established as cross-currency basis in E8).

Definition 2.2 · Cross-Currency Basis
Let \(y_t^{\$}\) denote the domestic benchmark risk-free cash rate (e.g. USD SOFR OIS), and let \(y_t^*\) denote the foreign benchmark risk-free cash rate (e.g. EUR ESTR OIS) for tenor \(\tau\). The cross-currency basis \(x_t\) (expressed in basis points per year) is the spread between the direct domestic interest rate and the synthetic domestic interest rate generated by converting domestic cash to foreign currency spot, investing at \(y_t^*\), and hedging back via forward rate \(F_t\): \[\begin{aligned} x_t &= \frac{1}{\tau} \left( (1 + y_t^{\$} \tau) - \frac{F_t}{S_t}(1 + y_t^* \tau) \right) \times 10^4. \end{aligned}\] Under continuous compounding: \[\begin{aligned} x_t &= \left( y_t^{\$} - y_t^* - \frac{1}{\tau}(f_t - s_t) \right) \times 10^4. \end{aligned}\]

Theorem 2.1 · Cross-Currency Basis Spread Identity
Let \(x_t\) be the cross-currency basis. A negative basis (\(x_t < 0\)) implies that borrowing USD synthetically by pledging foreign currency in an FX swap requires paying a premium of \(|x_t|\) basis points above the cash dollar interest rate \(y_t^{\$}\): \[\begin{aligned} y_{t, \text{synth}}^{\$} &= y_t^{\$} - x_t \times 10^{-4} > y_t^{\$}. \end{aligned}\]

Proof
To borrow 1 USD synthetically for tenor \(\tau\):

  1. Borrow \(1/S_t\) units of foreign currency at rate \(y_t^* + \Delta y^*\), or pledge foreign currency cash collateral.
  2. Sell \(1/S_t\) foreign currency spot for 1 USD.
  3. At maturity \(\tau\), repurchase foreign currency forward at price \(F_t\) to repay the foreign debt \(\frac{1}{S_t}(1 + y_t^* \tau)\).

The total USD repayment at \(\tau\) is \(\frac{F_t}{S_t}(1 + y_t^* \tau)\). By Definition 2.2, this equals \(1 + (y_t^{\$} - x_t \times 10^{-4})\tau\). If \(x_t < 0\), then \(y_{t, \text{synth}}^{\$} = y_t^{\$} + |x_t| \times 10^{-4} > y_t^{\$}\), confirming that synthetic dollar borrowers pay an interest premium over direct dollar cash depositors.

Lab 2 · Cross-currency basis and forward points monitor

Computing forward points and the cross-currency basis across EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY, and USD/CHF under contemporary benchmark rates will demonstrate that: (1) forward points reflect interest rate differentials; (2) post-2008 cross-currency bases on JPY and EUR remain persistently negative (-15 to -40 bps), proving that synthetic dollar borrowing trades at a structural premium over cash rates.

"""Lab X1-L02-1: Cross-currency basis and forward points monitor.

Calculates forward points and cross-currency basis spreads across major currency pairs,
demonstrating the post-2008 breakdown of covered interest parity (Du et al. 2018).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import Any


def calculate_basis(
    pair: str,
    spot: float,
    forward: float,
    rate_usd: float,
    rate_foreign: float,
    tenor_years: float = 1.0,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate forward points in pips and cross-currency basis in basis points.

    Quotes: USD per foreign currency (direct quotation, e.g. EUR/USD, GBP/USD).
    For indirect quotes (USD/JPY, USD/CHF), spot and forward are inverted prior to calculation.

    Args:
        pair: Currency pair name (e.g. 'EUR/USD').
        spot: Spot rate S_t (USD per 1 unit of foreign).
        forward: Forward outright rate F_t (USD per 1 unit of foreign).
        rate_usd: Annualized USD benchmark rate (e.g. SOFR / OIS).
        rate_foreign: Annualized foreign benchmark rate (e.g. ESTR, TONAR, SONIA).
        tenor_years: Tenor fraction (default 1.0 year).

    Returns:
        dict containing forward_points (pips) and basis_bps (basis points).
    """
    # Forward points = (F - S) * 10^4 pips (or 10^2 for JPY direct)
    fwd_points = (forward - spot) * 10000.0

    # Synthetic USD rate via foreign currency investment:
    # Invest (1/S_t) foreign units at (1 + rate_foreign * tau), hedged back at F_t
    # Yields (F_t / S_t) * (1 + rate_foreign * tau) USD per initial USD
    synthetic_usd_gross = (forward / spot) * (1.0 + rate_foreign * tenor_years)

    # Direct USD cash rate gross: (1 + rate_usd * tau)
    cash_usd_gross = 1.0 + rate_usd * tenor_years

    # Cross-currency basis x_t (in bps annualized):
    # Standard market convention: direct USD rate minus synthetic USD rate
    # x_t = [(1 + rate_usd * tau) - (F_t / S_t) * (1 + rate_foreign * tau)] / tau * 10,000 bps
    # Under this convention, a negative basis means the market pays a premium for USD cash.
    basis_bps = ((cash_usd_gross - synthetic_usd_gross) / tenor_years) * 10000.0

    return {
        "spot": spot,
        "forward": forward,
        "forward_points": round(fwd_points, 1),
        "synthetic_usd_rate": round((synthetic_usd_gross - 1.0) / tenor_years * 100.0, 3),
        "direct_usd_rate": round(rate_usd * 100.0, 3),
        "basis_bps": round(basis_bps, 2),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab X1-L02-1 | Cross-Currency Basis & Forward Points Monitor")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------")

    # Sample market snapshot for 1-year tenor across 4 currency pairs
    # Demonstrating post-2008 persistent negative basis (USD funding premium)
    quotes: list[dict[str, Any]] = [
        {"pair": "EUR/USD", "spot": 1.0850, "forward": 1.1042, "rate_usd": 0.0520, "rate_foreign": 0.0360},
        {"pair": "GBP/USD", "spot": 1.2950, "forward": 1.2985, "rate_usd": 0.0520, "rate_foreign": 0.0480},
        {"pair": "USD/JPY", "spot": 0.00645, "forward": 0.00676, "rate_usd": 0.0520, "rate_foreign": 0.0025},
        {"pair": "USD/CHF", "spot": 1.1200, "forward": 1.1610, "rate_usd": 0.0520, "rate_foreign": 0.0140},
    ]

    print(f"{'Pair':<8} | {'Spot':<8} | {'Forward':<8} | {'Fwd Pts':<9} | {'Synth USD %':<11} | {'Basis (bps)':<11}")
    print("-" * 65)

    for q in quotes:
        res = calculate_basis(
            pair=q["pair"],
            spot=float(q["spot"]),
            forward=float(q["forward"]),
            rate_usd=float(q["rate_usd"]),
            rate_foreign=float(q["rate_foreign"]),
            tenor_years=1.0,
        )
        print(
            f"{q['pair']:<8} | {res['spot']:<8.5f} | {res['forward']:<8.5f} | "
            f"{res['forward_points']:>8.1f}  | {res['synthetic_usd_rate']:>10.3f}% | {res['basis_bps']:>10.2f}"
        )


main()

3Economic Mechanisms: Why Does the Basis Persist?

Under textbook arbitrage, a negative basis on EUR/USD should induce global banks to borrow cash USD at 5.20%, convert to EUR, lend at 3.60%, and hedge forward at 1.1042 to lock in a riskless synthetic yield of 5.48%, earning a 28 bps profit margin. Why does arbitrage capital fail to eliminate this spread? Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018) identify three primary friction channels:

  • Intermediary Balance-Sheet Constraints: Under Basel III, the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) imposes a flat 3% to 5% equity capital requirement against a bank's total gross non-risk-weighted assets. Holding large offsetting cash and derivative positions inflates the gross balance sheet, requiring scarce regulatory equity capital that demands higher returns than 20–30 bps.
  • Quarter-End and Year-End Window Dressing: Regulatory reporting dates (such as European and Japanese quarter-ends) trigger sharp contractions in dealer intermediation capacity, causing temporary spikes in the negative basis.
  • Structural Asymmetry in Dollar Demand: Global institutional investors (Japanese life insurers, European pension funds) hold trillions of dollars in US corporate bonds and Treasuries that must be currency-hedged back into domestic currency, creating persistent, one-sided demand to borrow dollars via FX swaps.

4Where the Canon Breaks: Quarter-End Spikes and Balance-Sheet Segmentation

Standard open-economy macroeconomics treats international financial markets as unified and frictionless. In reality, the cross-currency basis demonstrates severe market segmentation. During quarter-ends, the basis on JPY and EUR widens dramatically from -25 bps to over -100 bps within days, as European and Japanese global systemically important banks (G-SIBs) temporarily shrink their repo and FX swap books to optimize regulatory leverage ratios (Du et al. 2018).

5Misconceptions

  1. Forward points represent the market's expected change in the spot exchange rate over the contract tenor.

    Why it is wrong: Forward points are determined mechanically by interest rate differentials and balance-sheet borrowing costs through covered no-arbitrage relations. They do not reflect market forecasts of future spot exchange rates.

  2. A negative cross-currency basis indicates that US banks are at risk of defaulting on their foreign currency obligations.

    Why it is wrong: The basis is calculated using swap contracts and overnight index swap rates with negligible counterparty credit risk. A negative basis reflects balance-sheet constraints and excess global demand for dollar funding, not credit risk.

  3. Central bank liquidity swap lines were established to speculate on cross-currency basis spreads.

    Why it is wrong: Central bank swap lines (between the Federal Reserve, ECB, BoJ, BoE, SNB) act as an international lender of last resort, supplying dollar liquidity to foreign central banks to cap spikes in the basis and prevent fire sales of US dollar assets during crises.

6Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 2.1: Spot EUR/USD is \(S = 1.0800\). The 1-year outright forward is quoted at \(F = 1.0962\). Calculate the forward points in pips (\(1 \text{ pip} = 10^{-4}\)). If the 1-year US rate is \(y^{\$} = 5.0\%\) and the Eurozone rate is \(y^* = 3.5\%\), compute the cross-currency basis in basis points.

Solution

Forward points are \((1.0962 - 1.0800) \times 10^4 = 0.0162 \times 10000 = 162.0\) pips. The synthetic USD rate is \(\frac{F}{S}(1 + y^*) - 1 = \frac{1.0962}{1.0800}(1.035) - 1 = 1.0150 \times 1.035 - 1 = 1.050525 - 1 = 5.0525\%\). The cross-currency basis is \(x = (y^{\$} - y_{\text{synth}}^{\$}) \times 10^4 = (0.0500 - 0.050525) \times 10000 = -5.25\) basis points.

Problem 2.2: An institutional asset manager holds 100 million USD of Japanese Government Bonds yielding 0.80% in JPY. The spot rate is 150.00 JPY/USD (\(S = 0.0066667\) USD/JPY), and the 1-year forward is 145.00 JPY/USD (\(F = 0.0068966\) USD/JPY). Calculate the hedged USD return earned by the manager and compare it to the US risk-free rate of 4.50%.

Solution

The gross hedged dollar return is \(\frac{F}{S}(1 + y_{\text{JPY}}) = \frac{150.00}{145.00} \times (1 + 0.0080) = 1.03448 \times 1.0080 = 1.04276\), or a net return of \(4.28\%\). Comparing this to the US rate of \(4.50\%\) reveals a deficit of \(4.28\% - 4.50\% = -0.22\%\) (-22 basis points), exactly matching the negative cross-currency basis on USD/JPY.

Problem 2.3: Spot USD/CHF is quoted as 0.9000 CHF per USD (\(S = 1.1111\) USD/CHF). The 3-month forward points are -45 pips. Compute the 3-month forward outright rate in CHF per USD (\(1 \text{ pip} = 0.0001\)).

Solution

In market convention for USD/CHF, points are added directly to the CHF per USD quote: \(F_{\text{CHF/USD}} = 0.9000 + (-45 \times 10^{-4}) = 0.9000 - 0.0045 = 0.8955\) CHF per USD. In direct quotation (USD per CHF), the forward rate is \(1 / 0.8955 \approx 1.1167\) USD per CHF.

Proof

Problem 2.4: Prove that under continuous compounding, the cross-currency basis \(x_t\) is linear in the log forward premium: \(x_t = \left(y_t^{\$} - y_t^* - \frac{f_t - s_t}{\tau}\right) \times 10^4\).

Solution

Under continuous compounding, the synthetic dollar borrowing rate satisfies \(e^{y_{\text{synth}}^{\$} \tau} = \frac{F_t}{S_t} e^{y_t^* \tau} = e^{(f_t - s_t) + y_t^* \tau}\). Taking the natural logarithm gives \(y_{\text{synth}}^{\$} \tau = (f_t - s_t) + y_t^* \tau\), so \(y_{\text{synth}}^{\$} = y_t^* + \frac{f_t - s_t}{\tau}\). Substituting into the definition of basis \(x_t = (y_t^{\$} - y_{\text{synth}}^{\$}) \times 10^4\) yields \(x_t = \left(y_t^{\$} - y_t^* - \frac{f_t - s_t}{\tau}\right) \times 10^4\) exactly.

Problem 2.5: Prove that if an arbitrageur faces an unweighted leverage capital requirement \(k\) (equity per dollar of total assets) and equity has hurdle rate \(r_e > y^{\$}\), the minimum cross-currency basis magnitude required to profitably engage in CIP arbitrage is \(|x| \ge 2 k (r_e - y^{\$})\).

Solution

Executing CIP arbitrage requires funding a cash deposit on one side and a forward/swap leg that generates offsetting gross assets on the balance sheet. With leverage ratio \(k\), holding 1 USD of gross arbitrage assets requires \(k\) equity capital and \(1 - k\) debt funding at cash rate \(y^{\$}\). The cost of capital is \(k r_e + (1 - k) y^{\$} = y^{\$} + k(r_e - y^{\$})\). Because both legs expand the gross balance sheet by a factor of 2, the total balance-sheet hurdle is \(2 k (r_e - y^{\$})\). Arbitrage profits can only occur when the basis exceeds this regulatory capital cost.

Problem 2.6: Show that in a world with zero regulatory capital costs, zero haircuts, and competitive risk-neutral dealers, the cross-currency basis must equal zero in equilibrium.

Solution

If capital costs and haircuts are zero, any non-zero basis \(x_t \ne 0\) allows competitive dealers to borrow in the cheaper market (cash or synthetic) and lend in the more expensive market without balance-sheet frictions. Because capital is perfectly mobile, dealers will scale this arbitrage trade until aggregate demand shifts forward points and cash rates, forcing \(y_{\text{synth}}^{\$} = y^{\$}\) and restoring \(x_t \equiv 0\).

Empirical

Problem 2.7: In Lab 2, the cross-currency basis for USD/JPY is -36.42 bps, while GBP/USD is -1.41 bps. Explain why JPY exhibits a vastly more negative basis than GBP.

Solution

Japan maintains massive persistent net foreign asset positions and zero domestic interest rates, leading Japanese institutional investors (such as GPIF, Japan Post, and life insurers) to purchase huge quantities of US Treasuries and corporate credit. To eliminate currency risk, they sell USD forward, creating an enormous structural demand to borrow USD via FX swaps. In contrast, the UK runs structural current account deficits and has comparable interest rates to the US, resulting in balanced two-way swap flows and a near-zero basis.

Problem 2.8: During the March 2020 COVID market dislocation, the 3-month EUR/USD cross-currency basis plummeted to nearly -150 bps before recovering following Federal Reserve swap line interventions. What was the transmission channel of this intervention?

Solution

The Federal Reserve established standing US dollar liquidity swap lines with the ECB and other central banks at a pricing of OIS + 25 bps. Foreign central banks auctioned these dollars directly to local commercial banks against local collateral. This backstop provided unlimited dollar cash funding at a fixed cap, reducing reliance on the private FX swap market and compressing the negative cross-currency basis back toward normal levels.

Problem 2.9: How do quarter-end regulatory reporting requirements under Basel III affect the term structure of the cross-currency basis between 1-week and 1-year tenors?

Solution

Quarter-end regulatory snapshots measure leverage ratios on specific calendar dates. Consequently, short-term swap contracts (such as 1-week or 1-month swaps that straddle the quarter-end date) experience extreme widening of the negative basis (often exceeding -100 bps) because dealers refuse to intermediated trades over the reporting date. In contrast, 1-year tenors amortize the quarter-end penalty across 365 days, exhibiting much smoother and less volatile basis spreads.

Research

Problem 2.10: Contrast the "safe-haven convenience yield" hypothesis of Jiang, Krishnamurthy & Lustig (2021) with the "regulatory leverage constraint" hypothesis of Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018) in explaining persistent CIP deviations.

Solution

Jiang, Krishnamurthy & Lustig (2021) argue that foreign investors attach a non-pecuniary convenience yield to holding US Treasuries for their unmatched safety and liquidity, bidding up the cash dollar and creating an equilibrium wedge in forward pricing. In contrast, Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018) emphasize supply-side intermediary frictions, showing that post-crisis leverage rules (SLR) restrict bank balance-sheet arbitrage capacity. Both channels operate concurrently: high global safe-asset demand creates one-sided swap pressure, while regulatory frictions prevent dealers from absorbing it.

Problem 2.11: How does the emergence of a persistent cross-currency basis affect the cost of sovereign debt issuance for non-US governments issuing dollar-denominated debt?

Solution

When a foreign sovereign (such as Germany or Japan) issues dollar-denominated debt and swaps the proceeds back into domestic currency, a negative cross-currency basis allows them to borrow at a synthetic domestic interest rate that is strictly below their domestic sovereign bond yield. This negative basis provides a funding subsidy for foreign entities issuing USD debt, incentivizing non-US corporate and sovereign issuers to tap the US dollar market.

Problem 2.12: Evaluate whether non-bank financial intermediaries (such as sovereign wealth funds and hedge funds) can replace regulated global banks as CIP arbitrageurs to eliminate the basis.

Solution

While non-bank financial intermediaries are not subject to bank leverage ratios (SLR), they cannot replace bank arbitrageurs for two reasons: (1) they lack access to central bank discount windows and payment systems, forcing them to clear through dealer banks and pay financing haircuts that consume the arbitrage spread; (2) their cost of capital (target IRR of 8% to 15%) is vastly higher than the 20 to 40 bps basis spread, making low-margin balance-sheet arbitrage uneconomic.

  1. If Spot EUR/USD is 1.0800 and the 1-year forward outright is 1.0962, what is the value of the forward points in pips (\(1 \text{ pip} = 0.0001\))?

    \((1.0962 - 1.0800) \times 10000 = 0.0162 \times 10000 = 162.0\) pips.

  2. What does a negative cross-currency basis on EUR/USD signify?

    Options

    By market convention, a negative basis indicates that the synthetic dollar rate exceeds the cash dollar rate, requiring swap borrowers to pay a premium for US dollar funding.

  3. According to Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018), what regulatory rule significantly impeded bank arbitrage of the cross-currency basis?

    Options

    The SLR requires capital against total unweighted balance-sheet assets, penalizing large, matched, low-margin CIP arbitrage positions.

  4. Why do quarter-ends exhibit sharp widening spikes in the negative cross-currency basis?

    Options

    Regulatory reporting of leverage ratios on quarter-end dates incentivizes banks to shrink balance sheets, withdrawing arbitrage liquidity and causing the basis to spike.

  5. If the 1-year US cash rate is 5.00% and the synthetic dollar rate from EUR/USD swaps is 5.30%, what is the cross-currency basis in basis points?

    \(x = (y^{\$} - y_{\text{synth}}^{\$}) \times 10000 = (0.0500 - 0.0530) \times 10000 = -30.0\) basis points.

  6. Why do institutional investors in Japan and Europe generate persistent structural pressure on the cross-currency basis?

    Options

    Institutional asset managers hold foreign dollar assets and systematically hedge currency risk by selling USD forward, creating persistent demand for synthetic dollar funding.

  7. What was the primary function of the Federal Reserve's central bank liquidity swap lines during market crises?

    Options

    Central bank swap lines provide dollar liquidity abroad, capping basis dislocations and preventing fire sales of dollar assets by foreign institutions.

  8. How are forward points quoted for a currency trading at a forward discount?

    Options

    When a currency trades at a forward discount, the forward price is below the spot price, represented by negative forward points.
