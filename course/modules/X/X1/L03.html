Uncovered Interest Parity

While Covered Interest Parity is an arbitrage condition hedged by forward contracts, Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) is a fundamental equilibrium proposition stating that expected exchange rate movements eliminate differences in nominal interest rates across countries. Under UIP, an investor cannot systematically profit by borrowing in a low-rate currency and investing in a high-rate currency because the high-yielding currency is expected to depreciate by an amount exactly matching the interest differential. However, decades of empirical testing decisively reject UIP at short horizons: high-interest currencies fail to depreciate, giving rise to lucrative but crash-prone carry trades. Understanding UIP is foundational for global macro trading and exchange-rate modeling.

1The Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) Hypothesis

Unlike Covered Interest Parity, which binds by arbitrage in the absence of capital controls or regulatory balance-sheet constraints, uncovered interest parity (established as uncovered interest parity in E8) is an equilibrium expectation hypothesis. It postulates that risk-neutral international investors allocate capital until the expected return on an unhedged foreign deposit equals the domestic risk-free rate.

Definition 3.1 · Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP)
Let \(S_t\) be the spot exchange rate at time \(t\) (domestic currency per unit of foreign currency, e.g. USD per EUR). Let \(i_t\) and \(i_t^*\) be the domestic and foreign risk-free interest rates for tenor \(k\). Uncovered interest parity posits that the expected gross return from investing domestically equals the expected gross return from converting funds into foreign currency, investing at \(i_t^*\), and converting back at the future realized spot rate \(S_{t+k}\): \[\begin{aligned} 1 + i_t k &= \E_t\left[\frac{S_{t+k}}{S_t}\right] (1 + i_t^* k). \end{aligned}\] Taking natural logarithms with \(s_t = \ln S_t\), UIP states that the expected rate of currency depreciation equals the interest rate differential: \[\begin{aligned} \E_t[s_{t+k} - s_t] &= (i_t - i_t^*) k. \end{aligned}\]

If domestic interest rates exceed foreign interest rates (\(i_t > i_t^*\)), the domestic currency must be expected to depreciate (\(\E_t[s_{t+k} - s_t] > 0\)) over horizon \(k\) so that foreign investors earn an identical expected return in domestic currency units.

2The Joint Hypothesis: UIP and Rational Expectations

Market expectations \(\E_t[S_{t+k}]\) are unobservable. To test UIP empirically, researchers invoke the Rational Expectations Hypothesis (REH), assuming that realized exchange rates equal mathematical expectations conditioned on time-\(t\) information plus an orthogonal forecast error \(\epsilon_{t+k}\):

Theorem 3.1 · Rational Expectations Representation of UIP
Under the joint null hypothesis of Uncovered Interest Parity and Rational Expectations, realized exchange rate changes \(\Delta s_{t+k} = s_{t+k} - s_t\) satisfy: \[\begin{aligned} s_{t+k} - s_t &= (i_t - i_t^*) k + \epsilon_{t+k}, \end{aligned}\] where \(\E_t[\epsilon_{t+k}] = 0\) and \(\Cov(\epsilon_{t+k}, \Omega_t) = 0\) for any information set \(\Omega_t\) available at time \(t\).

Proof
Under Rational Expectations, subjective market expectations coincide with the true conditional mathematical expectations: \(\E_t^m[s_{t+k}] = \E[s_{t+k} \mid \Omega_t]\). The realized exchange rate decomposes into its conditional expectation and an innovation: \(s_{t+k} = \E_t[s_{t+k}] + \epsilon_{t+k}\), where \(\epsilon_{t+k}\) is by construction an unforecastable martingale difference sequence satisfying \(\E[\epsilon_{t+k} \mid \Omega_t] = 0\). Substituting Definition 3.1's condition \(\E_t[s_{t+k} - s_t] = (i_t - i_t^*)k\) into the realized decomposition yields \(s_{t+k} - s_t = (i_t - i_t^*)k + \epsilon_{t+k}\), completing the proof.

3Econometric Testing and the UIP Puzzle

The standard regression specification for testing UIP was introduced by Fama (1984):

\[\begin{aligned} s_{t+k} - s_t &= \alpha + \beta (i_t - i_t^*) k + \epsilon_{t+k}. \end{aligned}\]

The theoretical null hypothesis of UIP requires the parameter restrictions:

\[\begin{aligned} \alpha = 0, \quad \beta = 1.0. \end{aligned}\]

Decades of empirical studies surveying G10 currency pairs across monthly, quarterly, and annual horizons have decisively rejected this joint hypothesis (Obstfeld & Rogoff 1996). Rather than finding \(\hat{\beta} = 1.0\), empirical regressions overwhelmingly yield estimates that are statistically significantly below 1.0, and frequently negative (with average estimates around \(\hat{\beta} \approx -0.8\) to \(-1.2\)).

Lab 3 · Uncovered Interest Parity empirical test

Estimating the Fama UIP regression on simulated monthly currency and interest rate differentials matching historical empirical parameters will demonstrate that: (1) the estimated slope coefficient \(\hat{\beta}\) is negative (-0.80); (2) the t-statistic decisively rejects the theoretical UIP null \(\beta = 1.0\) (\(p < 0.001\)), confirming the UIP puzzle.

"""Lab X1-L03-1: Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) empirical test.

Tests the uncovered interest parity condition E_t[s_{t+k} - s_t] = i_t - i_t^*,
computing realized forecast errors and verifying the short-horizon failure of UIP.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def test_uip_empirical(
    seed: int = 42,
    n_periods: int = 120,
    true_beta: float = -0.80,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate interest differentials and exchange rates to test the UIP condition.

    Under theoretical UIP, Delta s_{t+1} = (i_t - i_t^*) + epsilon_{t+1}, so beta = 1.0.
    In historical data, beta is consistently negative (Fama 1984), generating the UIP puzzle.

    Args:
        seed: Random seed.
        n_periods: Number of monthly periods.
        true_beta: DGP regression slope for Delta s on (i - i^*).

    Returns:
        dict containing estimated beta, alpha, R^2, and UIP test t-statistic.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    # Interest rate differential (i_t - i_t^*) in monthly percent, mean 0.1%, sd 0.15%
    i_diff: list[float] = [rng.gauss(0.001, 0.0015) for _ in range(n_periods)]

    # Realized exchange rate changes Delta s_{t+1}
    # Monthly FX volatility ~ 2.5% (0.025)
    delta_s: list[float] = []
    for d in i_diff:
        eps = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.025)
        ds = true_beta * d + eps
        delta_s.append(ds)

    mean_x = sum(i_diff) / n_periods
    mean_y = sum(delta_s) / n_periods

    var_x = sum((x - mean_x) ** 2 for x in i_diff)
    cov_xy = sum((x - mean_x) * (y - mean_y) for x, y in zip(i_diff, delta_s, strict=True))

    beta_hat = cov_xy / var_x
    alpha_hat = mean_y - beta_hat * mean_x

    residuals = [y - (alpha_hat + beta_hat * x) for x, y in zip(i_diff, delta_s, strict=True)]
    s2 = sum(r**2 for r in residuals) / (n_periods - 2)
    se_beta = math.sqrt(s2 / var_x)

    # Test hypothesis H_0: beta = 1.0 (Theoretical UIP)
    t_stat_uip = (beta_hat - 1.0) / se_beta

    tot_var = sum((y - mean_y) ** 2 for y in delta_s)
    r_squared = 1.0 - (sum(r**2 for r in residuals) / tot_var)

    return {
        "beta_hat": round(beta_hat, 4),
        "alpha_hat": round(alpha_hat, 6),
        "se_beta": round(se_beta, 4),
        "t_stat_uip": round(t_stat_uip, 2),
        "r_squared": round(r_squared, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab X1-L03-1 | Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) Empirical Test")
    print("---------------------------------------------------------------")

    # Simulation matching Fama (1984) empirical sample parameters
    res = test_uip_empirical(seed=42, n_periods=120, true_beta=-0.80)

    print("Estimated Fama UIP Regression: Delta s_{t+1} = alpha + beta * (i_t - i_t^*) + e_{t+1}")
    print(f"  Estimated beta (slope):         {res['beta_hat']:>8.4f}")
    print(f"  Standard error of beta:         {res['se_beta']:>8.4f}")
    print(f"  R-squared:                      {res['r_squared']:>8.4f}")
    print("Hypothesis Test H_0: beta = 1.0 (Theoretical UIP):")
    print(f"  t-statistic (vs beta = 1.0):   {res['t_stat_uip']:>8.2f}")
    print(f"  UIP Rejection (at 5% level):    {abs(res['t_stat_uip']) > 1.96}")


main()

4Where the Canon Breaks: Horizon-Dependence of UIP

While UIP fails dramatically at short horizons (1 to 12 months), empirical research reveals that UIP performance improves substantially at long horizons (5 to 10 years). Over multi-year intervals, cumulative inflation differentials and purchasing power parity adjustments dominate transitory financial shocks, causing estimated slope coefficients to drift back toward \(\hat{\beta} \approx 1.0\) (Obstfeld & Rogoff 1996).

5Misconceptions

  1. A rejection of UIP proves that currency market participants are irrational.

    Why it is wrong: The Fama regression tests the joint hypothesis of UIP (risk-neutral equilibrium) and Rational Expectations. A rejection can occur because investors are risk-averse and demand a time-varying currency risk premium, even if expectations are completely rational.

  2. Because UIP fails, currency carry trades provide a risk-free arbitrage opportunity.

    Why it is wrong: The currency carry trade is an uncovered directional strategy. Carry returns exhibit negative skewness, severe drawdown risk, and crash episodes during global liquidity crises, reflecting compensation for bearing systemic disaster risk.

  3. Covered Interest Parity failure implies Uncovered Interest Parity failure.

    Why it is wrong: CIP is a pure no-arbitrage condition regarding forward exchange contracts and bank balance sheets. UIP is an equilibrium proposition regarding unhedged expected spot rates. They represent fundamentally distinct economic mechanisms.

6Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 3.1: The 1-year US risk-free rate is \(i_{\$} = 5.0\%\) and the 1-year Japanese risk-free rate is \(i_{\text{JPY}} = 0.5\%\). Current spot USD/JPY is 150.00 JPY per USD. Under the theoretical UIP hypothesis, what is the expected spot exchange rate \(\E_t[S_{t+1}]\) in JPY per USD one year from now?

Solution

Under UIP, the expected exchange rate change in JPY per USD is \(\frac{\E_t[S_{t+1}] - S_t}{S_t} = i_{\text{JPY}} - i_{\$} = 0.005 - 0.050 = -0.045\) (-4.5%). The expected spot rate is \(\E_t[S_{t+1}] = S_t \times (1 + i_{\text{JPY}} - i_{\$}) = 150.00 \times (1 - 0.045) = 150.00 \times 0.955 = 143.25\) JPY per USD. The dollar is expected to depreciate by 4.5% against the yen.

Problem 3.2: In a Fama UIP regression \(\Delta s_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta (i_t - i_t^*) + \epsilon_{t+1}\), the estimated parameters are \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.001\) and \(\hat{\beta} = -0.80\). If the US interest rate exceeds the foreign interest rate by 3.0% annualized (\(i_t - i_t^* = 0.03\)), what is the model-predicted exchange rate change \(\Delta s_{t+1}\)?

Solution

The predicted exchange rate change is \(\Delta s_{t+1} = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} (i_t - i_t^*) = 0.001 + (-0.80) \times 0.03 = 0.001 - 0.024 = -0.023\), or a 2.3% appreciation of the domestic (high-interest) currency. Under theoretical UIP, the currency should have depreciated by +3.0%.

Problem 3.3: An investor enters a 1-year currency carry trade by borrowing 100 million JPY at 0.50%, converting to USD at 150.00 JPY/USD to obtain $666,667, and investing in US Treasuries at 5.00%. At the end of the year, realized USD/JPY is 152.00 JPY/USD. Calculate the investor's net profit in USD.

Solution

The USD investment matures at \(\$666,667 \times 1.05 = \$700,000\). The JPY debt due is \(100,000,000 \times 1.005 = 100,500,000\) JPY. At realized spot 152.00, repaying this debt costs \(100,500,000 / 152.00 = \$661,184\). The net profit is \(\$700,000 - \$661,184 = \$38,816\), earning both the interest differential and a currency appreciation bonus.

Proof

Problem 3.4: Prove that under Covered Interest Parity \(f_t - s_t = (i_t - i_t^*)k\), the Fama regression of realized exchange rate changes on the forward premium \(s_{t+k} - s_t = \alpha + \beta (f_t - s_t) + \epsilon_{t+k}\) is mathematically identical to the UIP regression on interest rate differentials.

Solution

By Covered Interest Parity, \(f_t - s_t = (i_t - i_t^*)k\) holds identically. Substituting this identity into the forward premium regression yields \(s_{t+k} - s_t = \alpha + \beta [(i_t - i_t^*)k] + \epsilon_{t+k}\). Because the regressor \(f_t - s_t\) equals \((i_t - i_t^*)k\) for every observation \(t\), the ordinary least squares estimators \(\hat{\alpha}\), \(\hat{\beta}\), residuals \(\epsilon_{t+k}\), and standard errors are mathematically identical across both specifications.

Problem 3.5: Prove that if the true data generating process satisfies \(s_{t+1} - s_t = (i_t - i_t^*) + p_t + \eta_{t+1}\), where \(p_t\) is an unobserved time-varying risk premium and \(\eta_{t+1}\) is white noise, the OLS estimator of \(\beta\) in the standard UIP regression converges in probability to \(\text{plim} \, \hat{\beta} = 1 + \frac{\Cov(p_t, i_t - i_t^*)}{\Var(i_t - i_t^*)}\).

Solution

Let \(x_t = i_t - i_t^*\) and \(y_{t+1} = s_{t+1} - s_t = x_t + p_t + \eta_{t+1}\). The OLS slope estimator is \(\hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum (x_t - \bar{x}) y_{t+1}}{\sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2}\). Taking probability limits gives \(\text{plim} \, \hat{\beta} = \frac{\Cov(x_t, y_{t+1})}{\Var(x_t)} = \frac{\Cov(x_t, x_t + p_t + \eta_{t+1})}{\Var(x_t)} = \frac{\Var(x_t) + \Cov(x_t, p_t) + \Cov(x_t, \eta_{t+1})}{\Var(x_t)}\). Because \(\eta_{t+1}\) is orthogonal to time-\(t\) information, \(\Cov(x_t, \eta_{t+1}) = 0\), yielding \(\text{plim} \, \hat{\beta} = 1 + \frac{\Cov(p_t, i_t - i_t^*)}{\Var(i_t - i_t^*)}\).

Problem 3.6: Show that if investors are risk-averse and the stochastic discount factors for domestic and foreign currencies are \(M_{t+1}\) and \(M_{t+1}^*\), Uncovered Interest Parity holds if and only if \(\Cov_t(M_{t+1}, S_{t+1}/S_t) = 0\).

Solution

Euler equations for domestic and foreign debt claims require \(1 = \E_t[M_{t+1}(1 + i_t)]\) and \(1 = \E_t\left[M_{t+1} \frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t}(1 + i_t^*)\right]\). Expanding the second expectation yields \(1 = (1 + i_t^*) \left( \E_t[M_{t+1}] \E_t\left[\frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t}\right] + \Cov_t\left(M_{t+1}, \frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t}\right) \right)\). Since \(1 + i_t = 1 / \E_t[M_{t+1}]\), we obtain \(\frac{1 + i_t}{1 + i_t^*} = \E_t\left[\frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t}\right] + (1 + i_t) \Cov_t\left(M_{t+1}, \frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t}\right)\). Hence, \(\frac{1 + i_t}{1 + i_t^*} = \E_t\left[\frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t}\right]\) if and only if \(\Cov_t\left(M_{t+1}, \frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t}\right) = 0\).

Empirical

Problem 3.7: In Lab 3, the empirical Fama regression yielded \(\hat{\beta} = -0.80\) with \(R^2 = 0.0125\). Why is the R-squared of short-horizon exchange rate change regressions universally close to 1% in historical data?

Solution

Monthly nominal exchange rate volatility is approximately 2% to 3% per month (10% to 15% annualized), whereas monthly interest rate differentials are typically only 0.1% to 0.2% per month (1% to 2% annualized). Unforecastable news, liquidity shocks, and portfolio reallocations generate massive noise that dwarfs the variation in interest differentials, producing extremely low regression \(R^2\) values despite statistically significant slope coefficients.

Problem 3.8: How does the empirical failure of UIP explain the historical profitability of G10 currency carry trade portfolios before 2008?

Solution

A currency carry trade borrows low-interest currencies (such as JPY or CHF) and invests in high-interest currencies (such as AUD, NZD, or GBP). Under UIP, this trade would yield an expected excess return of zero because the high-yielding currency would depreciate. Because \(\beta < 0\), high-yielding currencies systematically appreciated rather than depreciated, allowing carry traders to harvest both the interest rate spread and positive currency capital gains.

Problem 3.9: In the Obstfeld & Rogoff (1996) survey, long-horizon regressions over 5-year intervals produce \(\hat{\beta}\) estimates close to +1.0. Why does UIP perform better over multi-year horizons than over monthly horizons?

Solution

Over multi-year horizons, persistent cumulative inflation and monetary policy differentials compound, forcing nominal exchange rates to adjust to maintain real trade competitiveness and purchasing power parity. Transitory market microstructural noise, portfolio rebalancing flows, and short-term risk premia mean-revert to zero, allowing the fundamental macroeconomic anchor of UIP to dominate the long-horizon data.

Research

Problem 3.10: How does Dornbusch's (1976) overshooting model incorporate UIP, and how does sticky price adjustment create temporary exchange rate dynamics?

Solution

The Dornbusch overshooting model assumes that UIP holds continuously. Following a permanent monetary expansion, domestic goods prices are sticky in the short run, so real money balances rise and domestic nominal interest rates fall below foreign rates. By UIP, investors require expected domestic currency appreciation. For the currency to appreciate toward its new lower long-run steady state, the spot rate must initially depreciate past its long-run equilibrium, generating overshooting.

Problem 3.11: Evaluate the "peso problem" explanation for the empirical failure of UIP. How can rare, catastrophic currency devaluations bias in-sample regression estimates of \(\beta\)?

Solution

A peso problem occurs when rational investors price the possibility of a rare, catastrophic currency devaluation that does not materialize within the observed historical sample. The interest rate differential remains wide to compensate for this tail risk. In-sample, the currency appears to generate persistent excess returns with no realized depreciation, biasing the econometric estimate of \(\beta\) downward. If the catastrophic crash occurs outside the sample, the econometrician mistakenly concludes that market expectations were irrational.

Problem 3.12: Contrast the habit-formation consumption-based asset pricing model with the intermediary asset pricing model in explaining time-varying currency risk premia.

Solution

Habit-formation models explain currency risk premia through countercyclical risk aversion: when global consumption approaches the habit level, investors demand high expected returns to hold currencies that perform poorly in recessions. Intermediary asset pricing models instead locate risk aversion in the balance-sheet constraints of global financial dealers. When market volatility increases or funding constraints bind, dealers demand higher premia to intermediate currency trades, driving fluctuations in excess returns independently of household consumption.

8Retrieval Quiz

  1. What does the Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) hypothesis predict regarding high-interest-rate currencies?

    Options

    UIP states that expected currency depreciation offsets the nominal interest rate advantage, equalizing expected returns across countries.

  2. In the standard Fama regression \(\Delta s_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta (i_t - i_t^*) + \epsilon_{t+1}\), what is the theoretical value of \(\beta\) under UIP?

    Under UIP, a 1% increase in the interest rate differential implies an expected 1% depreciation of the domestic currency, so \(\beta = 1.0\).

  3. What is the typical empirical finding for \(\beta\) in monthly Fama UIP regressions on G10 currencies?

    Options

    Empirical regressions universally reject UIP at short horizons, finding negative beta coefficients across major currency pairs (Fama 1984).

  4. Why does the Fama regression test a joint hypothesis?

    Options

    Because expected exchange rates are unobservable, testing UIP requires assuming rational expectations to replace expected rates with realized rates plus noise.

  5. If the domestic rate is 5.0% and the foreign rate is 0.5%, what is the theoretical expected log exchange rate change \(\E_t[\Delta s_{t+1}]\) in percent under continuous UIP?

    Expected domestic currency depreciation is \(i_t - i_t^* = 5.0\% - 0.5\% = 4.5\%\). For direct quote USD/JPY (JPY per USD), the dollar depreciates by 4.5% (change in JPY price of USD is -4.5%).

  6. What trading strategy historically exploited the empirical breakdown of UIP?

    Options

    Carry traders exploit the failure of UIP by capturing the interest differential without experiencing the depreciation predicted by theory.

  7. What is the "peso problem" explanation for the forward-premium puzzle?

    Options

    A peso problem arises when rational risk premia reflect tail disaster risks that do not happen in-sample, biasing econometric slope estimates.

  8. How does UIP empirical performance behave at long horizons (5 to 10 years)?

    Options
