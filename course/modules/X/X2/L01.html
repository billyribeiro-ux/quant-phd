The Currency Carry Trade & the Forward Discount Puzzle

The classical theory of international finance posits that exchange rates adjust to equalize expected returns across currencies. In reality, this uncovered interest parity condition fails systematically across major global markets. Currencies with high short-term interest rates appreciate on average rather than depreciating, creating substantial profits for the currency carry trade. Understanding the forward discount puzzle and the empirical mechanics of the carry trade is essential for global fixed income portfolio management, sovereign risk analysis, and macro hedge fund strategy design.

1Currency Carry Trade Mechanics & Excess Returns

In foreign exchange markets, an investor can take unhedged currency risk by funding positions in currencies with low nominal yields and investing the proceeds in currencies offering elevated interest rates. This investment strategy represents one of the most prominent quantitative trading styles in macro finance.

Definition 1.1 · Currency Carry Trade
The currency carry trade is an investment strategy that borrows in currencies with low short-term nominal interest rates (funding currencies) and invests in currencies with high short-term nominal interest rates (target or investment currencies) without taking forward cover against foreign exchange risk.

Let \(S_t\) denote the spot exchange rate at time \(t\) expressed as units of domestic currency (USD) per unit of foreign currency, such that an increase in \(S_t\) represents foreign currency appreciation. Let \(F_t\) denote the forward exchange rate contracted at time \(t\) for delivery at \(t+1\). Let \(i_t\) be the domestic risk-free rate and \(i_t^*\) be the foreign risk-free rate over the investment horizon.

Under covered interest parity (CIP), the forward discount \(f_t - s_t \equiv \ln F_t - \ln S_t\) equals the interest rate differential: \[\begin{aligned} f_t - s_t &\approx i_t - i_t^*. \end{aligned}\] When an investor buys foreign currency at spot rate \(S_t\), deposits it at foreign rate \(i_t^*\), and converts proceeds back to domestic currency at future spot rate \(S_{t+1}\), the gross nominal domestic return is \((1 + i_t^*) \frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t}\). Subtracting the domestic risk-free gross return \(1 + i_t\) yields the currency excess return \(R^e_{t+1}\): \[\begin{aligned} R^e_{t+1} &\equiv \frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t}(1 + i_t^*) - (1 + i_t). \end{aligned}\] In continuous log return notation with \(s_t = \ln S_t\) and \(f_t = \ln F_t\), the log currency excess return \(rx_{t+1}\) is defined as: \[\begin{aligned} rx_{t+1} &\equiv \Delta s_{t+1} - (f_t - s_t) = s_{t+1} - f_t \approx \Delta s_{t+1} + (i_t^* - i_t). \end{aligned}\] Here, \(\Delta s_{t+1} \equiv s_{t+1} - s_t\) is the log change in the spot exchange rate (currency appreciation), and \(i_t^* - i_t\) is the interest rate yield spread (the carry).

Remark 1.1 · Generalized Carry Interpretation
Following Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen & Vrugt (2018), the carry on any financial instrument is defined as its expected return assuming market prices and economic states remain unchanged. In currency markets, setting \(\Delta s_{t+1} = 0\) produces the carry \(C_t = i_t^* - i_t \approx s_t - f_t\). If spot exchange rates were unforecastable martingales (\(\E_t[\Delta s_{t+1}] = 0\)), the expected excess return would equal the interest differential.

2The Forward Discount Puzzle & Fama Decompositions

The classical macroeconomic benchmark is uncovered interest parity (UIP), which asserts that high-interest currencies must depreciate systematically by an amount exactly equal to the interest yield advantage, leaving expected excess returns across currencies identically equal to zero.

Theorem 1.1 · Fama (1984) Regression Specification & Risk Premium Decomposition
Consider the projection of spot exchange rate depreciation on the forward premium: \[\begin{aligned} \Delta s_{t+1} &= \alpha + \beta (f_t - s_t) + \varepsilon_{t+1}. \end{aligned}\] Under Uncovered Interest Parity and rational expectations (\(\E_t[\varepsilon_{t+1}] = 0\)), \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\beta = 1\). Furthermore, decomposing the forward discount into expected depreciation \(q_t \equiv \E_t[\Delta s_{t+1}]\) and a time-varying foreign exchange risk premium \(p_t \equiv f_t - \E_t[s_{t+1}]\): \[\begin{aligned} f_t - s_t &= p_t + q_t, \end{aligned}\] the population OLS regression slope coefficient \(\beta\) is given by: \[\begin{aligned} \beta &= \frac{\Cov(\Delta s_{t+1}, f_t - s_t)}{\Var(f_t - s_t)} = \frac{\Cov(q_t, p_t + q_t)}{\Var(p_t + q_t)} = \frac{\Var(q_t) + \Cov(p_t, q_t)}{\Var(p_t) + \Var(q_t) + 2\Cov(p_t, q_t)}. \end{aligned}\] A negative slope coefficient (\(\beta < 0\)) requires two necessary conditions:

  1. \(\Cov(p_t, q_t) < -\Var(q_t) < 0\) (negative covariance between the risk premium and expected depreciation).
  2. \(\Var(p_t) > \Var(q_t)\) (the variance of the currency risk premium strictly exceeds the variance of expected currency depreciation).

Proof
Under rational expectations, realized spot depreciation is \(\Delta s_{t+1} = q_t + \eta_{t+1}\), where \(\E_t[\eta_{t+1}] = 0\) and \(\Cov(\eta_{t+1}, f_t - s_t) = 0\). The covariance in the numerator of \(\beta\) evaluates to: \[\begin{aligned} \Cov(\Delta s_{t+1}, f_t - s_t) &= \Cov(q_t + \eta_{t+1}, p_t + q_t) = \Cov(q_t, p_t + q_t) = \Var(q_t) + \Cov(p_t, q_t). \end{aligned}\] The variance in the denominator is: \[\begin{aligned} \Var(f_t - s_t) &= \Var(p_t + q_t) = \Var(p_t) + \Var(q_t) + 2\Cov(p_t, q_t). \end{aligned}\] Since \(\Var(f_t - s_t) > 0\), \(\beta < 0\) if and only if the numerator is negative: \[\begin{aligned} \Var(q_t) + \Cov(p_t, q_t) &< 0 \iff \Cov(p_t, q_t) < -\Var(q_t). \end{aligned}\] Because \(\Var(q_t) \ge 0\), this directly implies \(\Cov(p_t, q_t) < 0\). Finally, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality: \[\begin{aligned} (\Cov(p_t, q_t))^2 &\le \Var(p_t) \Var(q_t). \end{aligned}\] Squaring \(\Cov(p_t, q_t) < -\Var(q_t)\) yields \((\Cov(p_t, q_t))^2 > (\Var(q_t))^2\), which requires \(\Var(p_t) \Var(q_t) > (\Var(q_t))^2\), and dividing by \(\Var(q_t) > 0\) establishes \(\Var(p_t) > \Var(q_t)\).

In empirical studies across developed economies (Fama 1984; Burnside et al. 2011), the estimated regression slope \(\hat{\beta}\) is consistently negative, averaging approximately \(-0.8\) across G10 currency pairs. This anomaly is known as the forward premium puzzle.

Lab 1 · Currency carry trade backtest & UIP simulation

Simulating a 60-month multi-currency carry strategy across 4 currencies with persistent interest rate differentials under empirical UIP breakdown will produce: (1) an annualized excess return exceeding 8% per annum; (2) an annualized Sharpe ratio above 1.0; and (3) an empirical Fama regression beta strictly less than zero.

"""Lab X2-L01-1: Currency Carry Trade Backtest & UIP Breakdown Simulator.

Simulates a multi-currency carry strategy across funding and target currencies,
computing realized excess returns, annualized Sharpe ratios, and Fama regression beta.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def simulate_carry_trade(
    n_months: int = 60,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate a multi-currency carry trade portfolio over n_months.

    Args:
        n_months: Sample length in months.
        seed: Random seed for reproducibility.

    Returns:
        Summary metrics dictionary including annualized mean return, volatility,
        Sharpe ratio, and UIP regression beta.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    # 4 currencies: 2 funding (low rate), 2 target (high rate)
    # Interest rate differentials (i_j^* - i_USD) annualized
    spreads = [-0.030, -0.020, 0.035, 0.050]
    n_cur = len(spreads)

    # Initial log spot rates
    log_spots = [0.0] * n_cur

    carry_excess_returns: list[float] = []
    depreciation_series: list[float] = []
    interest_diff_series: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(n_months):
        # Monthly interest rate differential per month
        monthly_diffs = [s / 12.0 for s in spreads]

        # Exchange rate innovations: UIP failure implies spot changes do not
        # offset interest differentials in sample (random walk with noise)
        delta_spots: list[float] = []
        for j in range(n_cur):
            # Spot innovation: small noise, beta_UIP approx -0.5
            u = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.025)
            # Spot depreciation: beta * (i_USD - i_j^*) = -beta * diff
            # With beta = -0.4, high rate currency slightly appreciates!
            ds = -0.4 * (-monthly_diffs[j]) + u
            delta_spots.append(ds)
            log_spots[j] += ds

        # Log currency excess return: rx_{t+1, j} = Delta s_{t+1, j} + (i_j^* - i_USD)/12
        rx_j = [delta_spots[j] + monthly_diffs[j] for j in range(n_cur)]

        # Carry strategy: long top 2 target currencies, short bottom 2 funding currencies
        # Target: indices 2, 3; Funding: indices 0, 1
        long_ret = 0.5 * (rx_j[2] + rx_j[3])
        short_ret = 0.5 * (rx_j[0] + rx_j[1])
        carry_ret = long_ret - short_ret
        carry_excess_returns.append(carry_ret)

        for j in range(n_cur):
            depreciation_series.append(delta_spots[j])
            interest_diff_series.append(-monthly_diffs[j])  # (i_USD - i_j^*)

    # Compute annualized portfolio metrics
    t_len = len(carry_excess_returns)
    mean_monthly = sum(carry_excess_returns) / t_len
    var_monthly = sum((r - mean_monthly) ** 2 for r in carry_excess_returns) / (t_len - 1)
    std_monthly = math.sqrt(var_monthly)

    ann_mean = mean_monthly * 12.0
    ann_std = std_monthly * math.sqrt(12.0)
    ann_sr = ann_mean / ann_std if ann_std > 1e-8 else 0.0

    # Fama UIP regression: Delta s_{t+1} = alpha + beta * (f_t - s_t) + eps
    # where (f_t - s_t) approx (i_USD - i_j^*)
    mean_x = sum(interest_diff_series) / len(interest_diff_series)
    mean_y = sum(depreciation_series) / len(depreciation_series)
    cov_xy = sum((x - mean_x) * (y - mean_y) for x, y in zip(interest_diff_series, depreciation_series, strict=True))
    var_x = sum((x - mean_x) ** 2 for x in interest_diff_series)
    fama_beta = cov_xy / var_x if var_x > 1e-12 else 0.0

    return {
        "n_months": float(n_months),
        "ann_mean": round(ann_mean, 4),
        "ann_std": round(ann_std, 4),
        "sharpe_ratio": round(ann_sr, 4),
        "fama_beta": round(fama_beta, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = simulate_carry_trade(n_months=60, seed=42)
    print("=== Currency Carry Trade & UIP Simulation ===")
    print(f"Sample length: {int(res['n_months'])} months")
    print(f"Annualized mean excess return: {res['ann_mean'] * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Annualized volatility: {res['ann_std'] * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Annualized Sharpe ratio: {res['sharpe_ratio']:.3f}")
    print(f"Fama UIP regression beta: {res['fama_beta']:.3f}")


main()

3Where the Canon Breaks

The neoclassical canonical model of foreign exchange pricing relies on two pillars: rational expectations and risk-neutral no-arbitrage. Under this canon, Uncovered Interest Parity must hold, leaving currency carry excess returns identically zero on average. The canon breaks along several dimensions:

  • Empirical Reversal: In historical data across G10 and emerging market currencies, high-interest currencies frequently appreciate over 1-to-12-month horizons rather than depreciating, turning the UIP condition completely upside down.
  • Sharpe Ratio Asymmetry: Unhedged currency carry portfolios historically generated annualized Sharpe ratios between 0.6 and 1.0, exceeding broad equity market benchmarks, despite requiring zero initial capital.
  • Limits to Arbitrage: The apparent "free lunch" of the carry trade cannot be readily eliminated by cross-border arbitrageurs due to leverage constraints, institutional balance sheet limits, and devastating drawdown risks during liquidity freezes.

4Misconceptions

  • The currency carry trade represents pure riskless arbitrage ("picking up free money").

    Unlike covered interest parity (CIP), the carry trade is completely unhedged against foreign exchange rate fluctuations. While it generates positive yield spread in calm market periods, carry positions are exposed to severe left-tail exchange rate devaluations and liquidity freezes (Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen 2008).

  • The negative Fama regression slope \(\beta < 0\) implies that international financial markets are necessarily inefficient or irrational.

    As demonstrated by Fama (1984) and modern asset pricing theory (Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan 2011), a negative beta is entirely consistent with rational equilibrium pricing if the foreign exchange risk premium is time-varying, negatively correlated with expected spot depreciation, and possesses greater volatility than expected depreciation.

  • Carry trade excess returns disappear once transaction costs and bid-ask spreads are accounted for.

    Because interest rate differentials are highly persistent across countries, carry portfolios require infrequent turnover (rebalancing only when relative interest rate rankings change). Burnside, Eichenbaum, Kleshchelski & Rebelo (2011) prove that bid-ask spreads reduce annualized carry returns by only 100–150 basis points, leaving substantial net positive Sharpe ratios intact.

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 1.1 · Log Excess Return Calculation
A US-based macro trader funds a 1-month carry trade in Japanese Yen (JPY) and invests in Australian Dollars (AUD). At \(t=0\), spot rates are \(S_{0}^{\mathrm{JPY}} = 0.0090\) USD/JPY and \(S_{0}^{\mathrm{AUD}} = 0.7000\) USD/AUD. The annualized 1-month interest rates are \(i_0^{\mathrm{JPY}} = -0.5\%\), \(i_0^{\mathrm{USD}} = 5.0\%\), and \(i_0^{\mathrm{AUD}} = 4.0\%\). At \(t=1\) month, spot rates become \(S_{1}^{\mathrm{JPY}} = 0.0089\) USD/JPY and \(S_{1}^{\mathrm{AUD}} = 0.7070\) USD/AUD. Calculate the monthly log excess returns for the AUD investment leg and the JPY funding leg, and compute the net carry trade excess return.

Worked Solution

For each currency \(j\), the log excess return over the US dollar is \(rx_{t+1, j} = \Delta s_{t+1, j} + (i_j^* - i_{\mathrm{USD}}) \tau\), where \(\tau = 1/12\). For AUD: \(\Delta s^{\mathrm{AUD}} = \ln(0.7070 / 0.7000) = \ln(1.0100) \approx 0.009950\). The interest yield spread is \((0.040 - 0.050) / 12 = -0.010 / 12 \approx -0.000833\). Thus, \(rx^{\mathrm{AUD}} = 0.009950 - 0.000833 = 0.009117\) (or \(+0.912\%\)). For JPY: \(\Delta s^{\mathrm{JPY}} = \ln(0.0089 / 0.0090) = \ln(0.988889) \approx -0.011173\). The interest yield spread is \((-0.005 - 0.050) / 12 = -0.055 / 12 \approx -0.004583\). Thus, \(rx^{\mathrm{JPY}} = -0.011173 - 0.004583 = -0.015756\) (or \(-1.576\%\)). The net carry trade goes long AUD and short JPY: \(rx^{\mathrm{carry}} = rx^{\mathrm{AUD}} - rx^{\mathrm{JPY}} = 0.009117 - (-0.015756) = 0.024873\) (or \(+2.487\%\) in 1 month). The positive return reflects both AUD appreciation and JPY depreciation, in addition to the interest differential.

Problem 1.2 · Forward Discount and Interest Rate Spread
Under covered interest parity, the 1-year US risk-free rate is \(i = 5.25\%\) and the 1-year UK risk-free rate is \(i^* = 4.25\%\). The spot rate is \(S = 1.3000\) USD per GBP. Calculate the 1-year outright forward rate \(F\), the forward discount \(fd = \ln(F/S)\), and verify the CIP approximation \(fd \approx i - i^*\).

Worked Solution

Under CIP, the forward rate is given by \(F = S \frac{1 + i}{1 + i^*} = 1.3000 \times \frac{1 + 0.0525}{1 + 0.0425} = 1.3000 \times \frac{1.0525}{1.0425} \approx 1.31247\) USD/GBP. The exact continuous log forward discount is: \(fd = \ln(F / S) = \ln(1.31247 / 1.3000) = \ln(1.009592) \approx 0.009547\) (or \(+95.47\) basis points). The linear approximation is: \(i - i^* = 0.0525 - 0.0425 = 0.010000\) (or \(+100.00\) basis points). The discrepancy between the exact log discount and linear approximation is \(100 - 95.47 = 4.53\) basis points, driven by the higher-order terms in the Taylor expansion \(\ln((1+i)/(1+i^*)) \approx (i - i^*) - \frac{1}{2}(i^2 - (i^*)^2)\).

Problem 1.3 · Annualized Sharpe Ratio Computation
A systematic currency carry strategy evaluated over 120 monthly observations achieves a sample mean monthly excess return of \(\bar{rx} = 0.0055\) (55 basis points per month) and a sample monthly standard deviation of \(s = 0.0210\). Compute the annualized mean return, annualized volatility, and annualized Sharpe ratio of the strategy.

Worked Solution

With monthly observations, annualization scales the mean return by 12 and the standard deviation by \(\sqrt{12}\): Annualized mean return: \(\bar{rx}_{\mathrm{ann}} = \bar{rx} \times 12 = 0.0055 \times 12 = 0.0660\) (or \(6.60\%\)). Annualized standard deviation: \(s_{\mathrm{ann}} = s \times \sqrt{12} = 0.0210 \times 3.46410 \approx 0.072746\) (or \(7.27\%\)). Annualized Sharpe ratio: \(\mathrm{SR}_{\mathrm{ann}} = \frac{\bar{rx}_{\mathrm{ann}}}{s_{\mathrm{ann}}} = \frac{0.0660}{0.072746} \approx 0.9073\) (or \(\frac{\bar{rx}}{s} \times \sqrt{12} = \frac{0.0055}{0.0210} \times \sqrt{12} \approx 0.2619 \times 3.4641 \approx 0.9073\)). The annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.91 is substantially higher than the historical equity market Sharpe ratio (typically 0.4 to 0.5), illustrating the magnitude of the forward premium anomaly.

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 1.4 · Proof of the Fama Beta Lower Bound
Prove that if the expected rate of currency depreciation \(q_t\) is constant over time (\(\Var(q_t) = 0\)), then the theoretical Fama regression slope coefficient \(\beta\) must equal exactly zero, regardless of the variance of the foreign exchange risk premium \(\Var(p_t) > 0\).

Worked Solution

Recall the Fama (1984) slope formula from Theorem 1.1: \[\beta = \frac{\Var(q_t) + \Cov(p_t, q_t)}{\Var(p_t) + \Var(q_t) + 2\Cov(p_t, q_t)}.\] If expected depreciation \(q_t = \E_t[\Delta s_{t+1}]\) is constant over time, its variance is zero: \(\Var(q_t) = 0\). By the properties of covariance, any constant random variable has zero covariance with all other random variables: \(\Cov(p_t, q_t) = 0\). Substituting \(\Var(q_t) = 0\) and \(\Cov(p_t, q_t) = 0\) into the slope formula yields: \[\beta = \frac{0 + 0}{\Var(p_t) + 0 + 0} = \frac{0}{\Var(p_t)} = 0.\] Therefore, if exchange rates follow a driftless random walk in expectations, the forward premium regression slope is zero. For the slope to be strictly negative (\(\beta < 0\)), expected exchange rate depreciation \(q_t\) must be time-varying (\(\Var(q_t) > 0\)) and must covary negatively with the currency risk premium \(p_t\).

Problem 1.5 · Implied Ratio of Variances
Let \(\rho = \Corr(p_t, q_t)\) be the correlation between the risk premium \(p_t\) and expected depreciation \(q_t\). Prove that \(\beta < 0\) requires the standard deviation ratio \(\sigma(p_t) / \sigma(q_t) > 1 / |\rho| \ge 1\).

Worked Solution

By Theorem 1.1, \(\beta < 0\) requires the numerator of \(\beta\) to be negative: \(\Var(q_t) + \Cov(p_t, q_t) < 0\). Expressing covariance in terms of correlation and standard deviations: \(\Cov(p_t, q_t) = \rho \sigma(p_t) \sigma(q_t)\). Substituting into the inequality: \(\sigma^2(q_t) + \rho \sigma(p_t) \sigma(q_t) < 0\). Dividing both sides by \(\sigma^2(q_t) > 0\): \(1 + \rho \frac{\sigma(p_t)}{\sigma(q_t)} < 0 \iff \rho \frac{\sigma(p_t)}{\sigma(q_t)} < -1\). Since standard deviations are positive, this requires \(\rho < 0\). Letting \(\rho = -|\rho|\): \(-|\rho| \frac{\sigma(p_t)}{\sigma(q_t)} < -1 \iff |\rho| \frac{\sigma(p_t)}{\sigma(q_t)} > 1 \iff \frac{\sigma(p_t)}{\sigma(q_t)} > \frac{1}{|\rho|}\). Since \(|\rho| \le 1\), it follows that \(1/|\rho| \ge 1\). Thus, \(\sigma(p_t) > \sigma(q_t)\), proving that the currency risk premium must strictly dominate expected exchange rate depreciation in volatility.

Problem 1.6 · Fama Beta Under Regressors with Measurement Noise
Suppose the true forward discount is \(fd_t^* = f_t^* - s_t^*\), but the econometrician observes \(fd_t = fd_t^* + \xi_t\), where \(\xi_t \sim \Normal(0, \sigma_\xi^2)\) is classical independent measurement error. Prove that measurement error causes attenuation bias toward zero, and state whether measurement error alone can explain a negative \(\hat{\beta} < 0\) when the true \(\beta^* = 1\).

Worked Solution

Let \(\Delta s_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta^* fd_t^* + \varepsilon_{t+1}\). The OLS estimator using observed \(fd_t = fd_t^* + \xi_t\) converges in probability to: \[\operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta} = \frac{\Cov(\Delta s_{t+1}, fd_t^* + \xi_t)}{\Var(fd_t^* + \xi_t)} = \frac{\Cov(\Delta s_{t+1}, fd_t^*)}{\Var(fd_t^*) + \Var(\xi_t)} = \beta^* \left( \frac{\Var(fd_t^*)}{\Var(fd_t^*) + \sigma_\xi^2} \right).\] Let \(\theta = \frac{\Var(fd_t^*)}{\Var(fd_t^*) + \sigma_\xi^2} \in (0, 1)\) be the reliability ratio. If the true parameter is \(\beta^* = 1\) (UIP holds), then: \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta} = 1 \cdot \theta \in (0, 1)\). Attenuation bias can shrink the estimated slope from 1.0 down toward 0, but because \(\theta > 0\), it can never flip the sign to negative (\(\hat{\beta} < 0\)). Therefore, classical measurement error cannot explain the forward discount puzzle.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 1.7 · Empirical Fama Regression Estimation
Using a monthly dataset of 120 observations for GBP/USD, the sample statistics are: \(\Var(f_t - s_t) = 0.000100\), \(\Var(\Delta s_{t+1}) = 0.000625\), and \(\Cov(\Delta s_{t+1}, f_t - s_t) = -0.000085\). Calculate the estimated Fama regression slope \(\hat{\beta}\), its standard error assuming homoskedastic i.i.d. residuals, and conduct a t-test of the UIP null hypothesis \(H_0: \beta = 1\).

Worked Solution

1. Slope estimate: \(\hat{\beta} = \frac{\Cov(\Delta s_{t+1}, f_t - s_t)}{\Var(f_t - s_t)} = \frac{-0.000085}{0.000100} = -0.850\). 2. Residual variance: \(\hat{\sigma}_\varepsilon^2 = \Var(\Delta s) - \hat{\beta}^2 \Var(f - s) = 0.000625 - (-0.850)^2 \times 0.000100 = 0.000625 - 0.7225 \times 0.000100 = 0.000625 - 0.00007225 = 0.00055275\). 3. Standard error: \(\mathrm{se}(\hat{\beta}) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{\sigma}_\varepsilon^2}{(N - 2) \Var(f - s)}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.00055275}{118 \times 0.000100}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.00055275}{0.011800}} = \sqrt{0.046843} \approx 0.2164\). 4. Test of UIP null \(H_0: \beta = 1\): \(t = \frac{\hat{\beta} - 1}{\mathrm{se}(\hat{\beta})} = \frac{-0.850 - 1.000}{0.2164} = \frac{-1.850}{0.2164} \approx -8.55\). Since \(|t| = 8.55 \gg 1.96\), the UIP null hypothesis is rejected at the \(p < 0.001\) significance level, confirming strong statistical evidence for the forward discount puzzle.

Problem 1.8 · G10 Carry Portfolio Construction
A quantitative currency fund ranks 10 G10 currencies monthly by their forward discount \(f_t - s_t\) and allocates equal weights to the top 2 highest carry currencies (long leg) and bottom 2 lowest carry currencies (short leg). Over a 10-year sample, the annualized mean returns of the four selected currency legs are: Long leg \(+7.50\%\) (volatility \(10.20\%\)), Short leg \(-1.80\%\) (volatility \(9.80\%\)), and the correlation between the long and short legs is \(\rho = 0.45\). Compute the annualized mean return, annualized volatility, and Sharpe ratio of the zero-cost carry portfolio.

Worked Solution

Let \(R_L\) and \(R_S\) denote the returns of the long and short legs: 1. Carry portfolio mean return: \(\mu_{\mathrm{carry}} = \mu_L - \mu_S = 7.50\% - (-1.80\%) = 9.30\%\) per annum. 2. Carry portfolio variance: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{carry}}^2 = \sigma_L^2 + \sigma_S^2 - 2 \rho \sigma_L \sigma_S = (0.1020)^2 + (0.0980)^2 - 2 \times 0.45 \times 0.1020 \times 0.0980\). \(\sigma_L^2 = 0.010404\), \(\sigma_S^2 = 0.009604\), \(2 \times 0.45 \times 0.1020 \times 0.0980 = 0.0089964\). \(\sigma_{\mathrm{carry}}^2 = 0.010404 + 0.009604 - 0.0089964 = 0.0110116\). Annualized volatility: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{carry}} = \sqrt{0.0110116} \approx 0.104936\) (or \(10.49\%\)). 3. Annualized Sharpe ratio: \(\mathrm{SR} = \frac{\mu_{\mathrm{carry}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{carry}}} = \frac{0.0930}{0.104936} \approx 0.886\). The portfolio achieves an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.886, driven by both the positive interest rate spread and favorable currency co-movements.

Problem 1.9 · Transaction Cost Impact on Breakeven Carry
A currency carry portfolio rebalances monthly, experiencing a one-way portfolio turnover of 25% per month (0.25 fraction of total notional traded each month). The gross annualized carry return is \(6.00\%\) with an annualized volatility of \(8.00\%\). If the average effective round-trip bid-ask transaction cost is 4 basis points (0.0004), compute the net annualized return, net Sharpe ratio, and the breakeven round-trip transaction cost that would completely eliminate carry profitability.

Worked Solution

1. Annual turnover: \(\text{Annual Turnover} = 12 \times 0.25 = 3.0\times\) portfolio notional traded per year. 2. Annual transaction cost drag: \(\text{Cost Drag} = 3.0 \times 0.0004 = 0.0012\) (or \(0.12\%\) per year, or 12 basis points). 3. Net return and Sharpe ratio: \(\mu_{\mathrm{net}} = 6.00\% - 0.12\% = 5.88\%\). \(\mathrm{SR}_{\mathrm{net}} = \frac{0.0588}{0.0800} = 0.735\) (compared to gross Sharpe 0.750). 4. Breakeven transaction cost: Setting \(\mu_{\mathrm{net}} = \mu_{\mathrm{gross}} - \text{Annual Turnover} \times C_{\mathrm{be}} = 0\): \(C_{\mathrm{be}} = \frac{\mu_{\mathrm{gross}}}{\text{Annual Turnover}} = \frac{0.0600}{3.0} = 0.0200\) (or 200 basis points per round-trip trade). Since institutional G10 round-trip spreads are typically 2 to 5 basis points, transaction costs reduce gross carry profitability by only a minor fraction.

Problem 1.10 · Carry Horizon Dependence & Long-Horizon UIP Convergence
Empirical studies (e.g. Chinn & Meredith 2004) find that while Fama regression slope coefficients \(\hat{\beta}\) are negative at 1-month to 12-month horizons, they become positive and approach \(\beta \approx 1.0\) at horizons of 5 to 10 years. Formulate an economic explanation for this term-structure variation in UIP failure based on inflation differentials, price stickiness, and central bank monetary policy reaction functions.

Worked Solution

At short horizons (1 to 12 months), nominal goods prices are sticky, meaning nominal interest rate changes induce real interest rate differentials. Central banks increase short rates to tighten monetary policy, which induces capital inflows from international yield seekers. In the presence of limits to arbitrage and persistent liquidity demand, the target currency appreciates over the short run (\(\beta < 0\)). Over long horizons (5 to 10 years), price stickiness dissolves, and purchasing power parity (PPP) asserts itself. Countries with persistently higher nominal interest rates have higher long-run inflation rates (the Fisher effect). Over multi-year horizons, relative PPP forces the high-inflation/high-nominal-rate currency to depreciate by approximately the inflation differential (\(\Delta s_{t+k} \approx \pi_t - \pi_t^* \approx i_t - i_t^*\)). Consequently, the long-run regression coefficient \(\beta\) converges toward 1.0.

Problem 1.11 · Consumption CAPM Failure and Currency Return Puzzles
Under the standard consumption-based capital asset pricing model (CCAPM) with power utility, the stochastic discount factor is \(M_{t+1} = \beta_u (C_{t+1} / C_t)^{-\gamma}\). Derive the currency risk premium under log-normality, explain why standard CCAPM fails to account for empirical carry trade Sharpe ratios with plausible risk aversion coefficients (\(\gamma \le 5\)), and evaluate what value of \(\gamma\) would be required.

Worked Solution

Under complete markets and log-normality, no-arbitrage implies \(\Delta s_{t+1} = m_{t+1}^* - m_{t+1}\). The log risk premium is \(\E_t[rx_{t+1}] + \frac{1}{2}\Var_t(rx_{t+1}) = -\Cov_t(m_{t+1}, rx_{t+1})\). With power utility, \(m_{t+1} = \ln \beta_u - \gamma \Delta c_{t+1}\), yielding: \(\E_t[rx_{t+1}] \approx \gamma \Cov_t(\Delta c_{t+1}, rx_{t+1})\). In macro data, the standard deviation of aggregate consumption growth is small (\(\sigma(\Delta c) \approx 1.5\%\)), and the correlation between domestic consumption growth and carry trade returns is low (\(|\rho| \le 0.15\)). Given an empirical carry volatility \(\sigma(rx) \approx 10\%\): \(\Cov_t(\Delta c_{t+1}, rx_{t+1}) \le 0.15 \times 0.015 \times 0.10 = 0.000225\). To generate an annualized carry risk premium of \(\E[rx] \approx 6\%\) (0.060), the implied risk aversion coefficient must be: \(\gamma \ge \frac{0.060}{0.000225} \approx 267\). A risk aversion of \(\gamma > 250\) is economically implausible, constituting the currency analog of the Mehra–Prescott equity premium puzzle.

Problem 1.12 · Monetary Policy Shocks vs. Endogenous Risk Premia
Critique the hypothesis that carry trade profitability is driven purely by predictable systematic central bank interest rate smoothing. If central bank policy rate hikes are predictable, why does rational speculation by currency traders fail to eliminate the forward discount anomaly instantly at the announcement date?

Worked Solution

If central bank rate smoothing were the sole driver in frictionless markets, forward exchange rates would immediately incorporate the entire expected future interest rate path, equating expected returns. The persistence of the anomaly requires two frictions: (1) intermediary capital constraints and risk-bearing capacity (Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009; He & Krishnamurthy 2013), whereby specialized FX dealers require compensation for holding foreign currency inventory; and (2) delayed information processing and slow-moving capital (Duffie 2010), where institutional asset managers reallocate capital across global fixed income markets with multi-quarter lags. Speculators who recognize the predictability face capital limits, margin constraints, and non-trivial drawdown risks, preventing instantaneous market clearing.

7Retrieval Quiz

  1. What position does a currency carry trade take?

    Options

    The carry trade goes long high-rate target currencies and borrows in low-rate funding currencies to capture the interest differential.

  2. What does Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) predict regarding exchange rate movements?

    Options

    UIP states that expected currency depreciation offsets the interest yield spread: \(\E_t[\Delta s_{t+1}] = i_t - i_t^*\).

  3. In the Fama (1984) regression \(\Delta s_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta (f_t - s_t) + \varepsilon_{t+1}\), what is the empirical finding across major G10 currencies?

    Options

    Empirically, \(\beta\) is systematically negative, meaning high-interest currencies tend to appreciate rather than depreciate.

  4. According to Fama's decomposition, what is a necessary condition for the regression slope \(\beta\) to be negative?

    Options

    Fama proved that \(\beta < 0\) requires \(\Var(p_t) > \Var(q_t)\) and \(\Cov(p_t, q_t) < -\Var(q_t)\).

  5. A carry portfolio earns an annualized mean excess return of \(6.40\%\) with an annualized standard deviation of \(8.00\%\). What is its annualized Sharpe ratio?

    \(\mathrm{SR} = 6.40\% / 8.00\% = 0.80\).

  6. How does portfolio rebalancing frequency affect currency carry profitability?

    Options

    Interest rate differentials persist over months or years, keeping turnover low (approx 0.6x–0.8x annually) and transaction cost drag modest.

  7. What happens to the Fama regression slope \(\beta\) at long multi-year horizons (5 to 10 years)?

    Options

    At long horizons, goods price stickiness resolves and purchasing power parity forces high-inflation/high-rate currencies to depreciate, restoring \(\beta \approx 1\).

  8. In a Fama regression, \(\Var(f_t - s_t) = 0.00020\) and \(\Cov(\Delta s_{t+1}, f_t - s_t) = -0.00010\). What is the estimated OLS slope \(\hat{\beta}\)?

    \(\hat{\beta} = \frac{-0.00010}{0.00020} = -0.50\).
