Common Risk Factors in Currency Markets: Dollar & HML-FX

Does a low-dimensional factor model price the cross-section of currency returns, analogously to the Fama-French factors in equities? In their seminal work, Lustig, Roussanov, and Verdelhan (2011) demonstrate that two simple portfolio factors—the Dollar factor and HML-FX—explain the vast majority of cross-sectional variation in currency returns. By sorting currencies into portfolios based on their forward discounts, they show that currency risk premia arise from differential exposure to systematic global shocks rather than idiosyncratic currency anomalies. This lesson derives the affine pricing kernel decomposition, establishes the economic interpretation of currency factor loadings, and presents empirical two-pass cross-sectional regressions.

1Portfolio Sorting & Factor Construction

In equity asset pricing, sorting individual stocks into portfolios based on firm characteristics (such as market equity and book-to-market) diversifies idiosyncratic firm risk and reveals systematic factor pricing. Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan (2011) imported this portfolio sorting paradigm into international finance.

At the end of each period \(t\), all available currencies \(j = 1, \dots, N_t\) are ranked by their forward discount \(f_{t}^j - s_{t}^j \approx i_t - i_{t}^{j*}\) and allocated into \(K\) portfolios (typically quintiles, \(K=5\)):

  • Portfolio 1: Currencies with the smallest forward discounts (highest foreign interest rates \(i^{j*}\)).
  • Portfolio 5: Currencies with the largest forward discounts (lowest foreign interest rates \(i^{j*}\)).
(When quoted as foreign currency units per USD, Portfolio 5 contains high-yield investment targets and Portfolio 1 contains low-yield funding currencies.)

Definition 4.1 · Dollar Factor
The Dollar factor (\(DOL_t\)) is the equally-weighted average excess return across all currency portfolios against the domestic currency (typically the US dollar): \[\begin{aligned} DOL_t &\equiv \frac{1}{K} \sum_{p=1}^K rx_t^p. \end{aligned}\] It represents a level factor capturing broad US dollar appreciation or depreciation against the global basket.

Definition 4.2 · HML-FX Factor
The HML-FX (High-Minus-Low FX) carry factor is a zero-cost slope portfolio defined as the return difference between the highest interest rate portfolio and the lowest interest rate portfolio: \[\begin{aligned} HML_{\mathrm{FX}, t} &\equiv rx_t^K - rx_t^1. \end{aligned}\] Because both legs are long foreign currencies against the dollar with equal weights, \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) is dollar-neutral and captures pure cross-sectional carry risk.

2Affine Stochastic Discount Factor Decomposition

To provide a macroeconomic foundation for why two factors price currency cross-sections, Lustig, Roussanov, and Verdelhan specify a multi-country affine pricing kernel model. Let \(m_{t+1}\) denote the log stochastic discount factor (SDF) of the domestic country (US) and \(m_{t+1}^j\) denote the log SDF of foreign country \(j\).

In complete or no-arbitrage markets, the log change in the bilateral spot exchange rate satisfies: \[\begin{aligned} \Delta s_{t+1}^j &= m_{t+1}^j - m_{t+1}. \end{aligned}\] Each country's log pricing kernel is decomposed into a country-specific idiosyncratic component and a common global factor: \[\begin{aligned} -m_{t+1}^j &= \alpha + \chi z_t^j + \tau z_t^w + \sqrt{\gamma z_t^j} u_{t+1}^j + \sqrt{\delta z_t^w} u_{t+1}^w, \end{aligned}\] where \(z_t^j\) is the country-specific state variable, \(z_t^w\) is the global state variable, and \(u_{t+1}^j, u_{t+1}^w \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) are mutually orthogonal structural shocks.

Theorem 4.1 · Currency Pricing Kernel Decomposition & Factor Loadings
Suppose domestic and foreign pricing kernels share a common global innovation \(u_{t+1}^w\) with heterogeneous country loadings \(\delta_j\). Then:

  1. The Dollar factor \(DOL_{t+1}\) loads primarily on domestic-specific shocks \(u_{t+1}^{US}\), acting as a level factor with identical loadings \(\beta_{DOL}^p \approx 1.0\) across all currency portfolios;
  2. The \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) carry factor differences out the domestic shock and isolates differential exposure to global innovations \(u_{t+1}^w\);
  3. In the two-factor linear asset pricing model: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[rx_{t+1}^p] &= \beta_{DOL}^p \lambda_{DOL} + \beta_{HML}^p \lambda_{HML}, \end{aligned}\] the cross-sectional loadings \(\beta_{HML}^p\) increase monotonically from negative values for low-yield currencies (\(\beta_{HML}^1 < 0\)) to positive values for high-yield currencies (\(\beta_{HML}^K > 0\)).

Proof. The bilateral log excess return of currency \(j\) against the domestic currency (US) over a single period is: \[\begin{aligned} rx_{t+1}^j &= \Delta s_{t+1}^j + i_t^j - i_t^{US} = (m_{t+1}^j - m_{t+1}^{US}) + (i_t^j - i_t^{US}). \end{aligned}\] Substituting the affine pricing kernels: \[\begin{aligned} rx_{t+1}^j &= \mathbb{E}_t[rx_{t+1}^j] + \sqrt{\gamma z_t^{US}} u_{t+1}^{US} - \sqrt{\gamma z_t^j} u_{t+1}^j + (\sqrt{\delta_j} - \sqrt{\delta_{US}})\sqrt{z_t^w} u_{t+1}^w. \end{aligned}\] Taking the equal-weighted average across a large panel of \(N\) foreign currencies: \[\begin{aligned} DOL_{t+1} &\equiv \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N rx_{t+1}^j = \mathbb{E}_t[DOL_{t+1}] + \sqrt{\gamma z_t^{US}} u_{t+1}^{US} + \mathcal{O}_p\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right). \end{aligned}\] Because idiosyncratic shocks \(\frac{1}{N} \sum u_{t+1}^j \to 0\) by the Law of Large Numbers, the Dollar factor captures the domestic US kernel shock \(\sqrt{\gamma z_t^{US}} u_{t+1}^{US}\). For any portfolio \(p\), regressing on \(DOL_{t+1}\) yields: \[\begin{aligned} \beta_{DOL}^p &= \frac{\Cov(rx_{t+1}^p, DOL_{t+1})}{\Var(DOL_{t+1})} \approx \frac{\Cov(\sqrt{\gamma} u^{US}, \sqrt{\gamma} u^{US})}{\Var(\sqrt{\gamma} u^{US})} = 1.0. \end{aligned}\] Now consider the long-short carry spread \(HML_{t+1} = rx_{t+1}^K - rx_{t+1}^1\). Subtracting cancels out the domestic shock \(\sqrt{\gamma z_t^{US}} u_{t+1}^{US}\): \[\begin{aligned} HML_{t+1} &= \mathbb{E}_t[HML_{t+1}] + (\sqrt{\delta_K} - \sqrt{\delta_1})\sqrt{z_t^w} u_{t+1}^w + \text{idiosyncratic terms}. \end{aligned}\] Currencies sorted into Portfolio \(K\) have high interest rates precisely because their marginal utility is countercyclical to global shocks (\(\delta_K > \delta_{US}\)), while low-interest funding currencies in Portfolio 1 have safe-haven properties (\(\delta_1 < \delta_{US}\)). Regressing portfolio returns on \(HML_{t+1}\) yields: \[\begin{aligned} \beta_{HML}^p &= \frac{\sqrt{\delta_p} - \bar{\delta}}{\sqrt{\delta_K} - \sqrt{\delta_1}}, \end{aligned}\] which increases monotonically from \(\beta_{HML}^1 \approx -0.5\) to \(\beta_{HML}^K \approx +0.5\). In the cross-sectional regression, this monotonic spread in \(\beta_{HML}\) prices the cross-section of returns with \(\lambda_{HML} = \mathbb{E}[HML] > 0\). End of proof.∎

Lab 1 · Lustig-Roussanov-Verdelhan factor decomposition backtest

Sorting a simulated multi-currency panel into 5 forward-discount portfolios will produce: (1) monotonic mean excess returns from P1 (-5.13%) to P5 (+4.24%); (2) identical Dollar factor betas (\(\beta_{\mathrm{DOL}} \approx 1.0\)) across all portfolios; (3) monotonically increasing HML-FX betas from -0.52 to +0.48; and (4) time-series \(R^2\) exceeding 95% across all portfolios.

"""Lab X2-L04-1: Lustig-Roussanov-Verdelhan Dollar & HML-FX Decomposition.

Replicates cross-sectional currency portfolio sorting, Dollar level factor,
and HML-FX slope factor regressions (Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan 2011).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from typing import Any


def simulate_factor_decomposition(
    n_months: int = 120,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """Sort currencies into 5 quintiles and decompose into Level and Slope factors.

    Args:
        n_months: Sample length in months.
        seed: Random seed.

    Returns:
        Dictionary containing portfolio returns, factor returns, and regression results.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n_portfolios = 5

    # Base interest differentials (funding vs target)

    portfolio_returns: list[list[float]] = []

    # Monthly common dollar factor and slope shock
    for _ in range(n_months):
        dol_shock = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.025)
        hml_shock = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.018)

        # Monthly return per portfolio k in 0..4
        month_p: list[float] = []
        for k in range(n_portfolios):
            # Monotonic carry yield from -3% to +5% annualized
            carry_yield = (-0.03 + k * 0.02) / 12.0
            # Level factor beta = 1.0; Slope factor beta increases from -0.5 to +0.5
            beta_slope = -0.5 + k * 0.25
            idio = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.006)
            ret = carry_yield + 1.0 * dol_shock + beta_slope * hml_shock + idio
            month_p.append(ret)
        portfolio_returns.append(month_p)

    # Build factors:
    # DOL = mean of all 5 portfolios
    # HML = Portfolio 5 minus Portfolio 1
    dol_factor: list[float] = []
    hml_factor: list[float] = []

    for t in range(n_months):
        row = portfolio_returns[t]
        dol_val = sum(row) / n_portfolios
        hml_val = row[-1] - row[0]
        dol_factor.append(dol_val)
        hml_factor.append(hml_val)

    # Regress each portfolio k on constant, DOL, and HML
    # Design matrix: [1, DOL, HML]
    reg_results: list[dict[str, float]] = []

    # Helper bivariate OLS
    for k in range(n_portfolios):
        y = [portfolio_returns[t][k] for t in range(n_months)]
        x1 = dol_factor
        x2 = hml_factor

        # Means
        my = sum(y) / n_months
        m1 = sum(x1) / n_months
        m2 = sum(x2) / n_months

        # Centered moments
        s11 = sum((x - m1) ** 2 for x in x1)
        s22 = sum((x - m2) ** 2 for x in x2)
        s12 = sum((x1[t] - m1) * (x2[t] - m2) for t in range(n_months))
        sy1 = sum((y[t] - my) * (x1[t] - m1) for t in range(n_months))
        sy2 = sum((y[t] - my) * (x2[t] - m2) for t in range(n_months))

        det = s11 * s22 - s12 * s12
        b1 = (s22 * sy1 - s12 * sy2) / det
        b2 = (s11 * sy2 - s12 * sy1) / det
        alpha = my - b1 * m1 - b2 * m2

        fitted = [alpha + b1 * x1[t] + b2 * x2[t] for t in range(n_months)]
        ss_tot = sum((y[t] - my) ** 2 for t in range(n_months))
        ss_res = sum((y[t] - fitted[t]) ** 2 for t in range(n_months))
        r2 = 1.0 - (ss_res / ss_tot)

        reg_results.append(
            {
                "portfolio": k + 1,
                "ann_mean": round(my * 12.0 * 100, 2),
                "beta_dol": round(b1, 3),
                "beta_hml": round(b2, 3),
                "r_squared": round(r2, 3),
            }
        )

    return {
        "n_months": n_months,
        "ann_mean_dol": round(sum(dol_factor) / n_months * 12.0 * 100, 2),
        "ann_mean_hml": round(sum(hml_factor) / n_months * 12.0 * 100, 2),
        "portfolios": reg_results,
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = simulate_factor_decomposition(n_months=120, seed=42)
    print("=== Lustig-Roussanov-Verdelhan Factor Decomposition ===")
    print(f"Sample: {res['n_months']} months | DOL Mean: {res['ann_mean_dol']}% | HML Mean: {res['ann_mean_hml']}%")
    print("Port | Mean Return | Beta (DOL) | Beta (HML) | R^2")
    print("--------------------------------------------------")
    for p in res["portfolios"]:
        ret_s = f"{p['ann_mean']:>9.2f}%"
        b_dol = f"{p['beta_dol']:>10.3f}"
        b_hml = f"{p['beta_hml']:>10.3f}"
        r2_s = f"{p['r_squared']:>5.3f}"
        print(f"P{p['portfolio']:<4}| {ret_s} | {b_dol} | {b_hml} | {r2_s}")


main()

3Two-Pass Fama-MacBeth Regressions

The standard empirical technique to verify that \(DOL\) and \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) price currency returns is the two-pass Fama-MacBeth (1973) cross-sectional methodology:

  1. Stage 1 (Time-Series OLS): For each portfolio \(p = 1, \dots, K\), estimate factor loadings: \[\begin{aligned} rx_t^p &= \alpha^p + \beta_{DOL}^p DOL_t + \beta_{HML}^p HML_{\mathrm{FX}, t} + \varepsilon_t^p. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Stage 2 (Cross-Sectional Regression): Regress sample average portfolio returns on estimated betas: \[\begin{aligned} \bar{rx}^p &= \lambda_0 + \lambda_{DOL} \hat{\beta}_{DOL}^p + \lambda_{HML} \hat{\beta}_{HML}^p + e^p. \end{aligned}\]

In empirical evaluations (Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan 2011; Verdelhan 2017):

  • The estimated cross-sectional price of carry risk \(\lambda_{HML}\) matches the sample mean return of \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) (\(\approx 5.5\%\) per annum for developed countries, \(\approx 9.0\%\) when emerging markets are included).
  • The cross-sectional \(R^2\) consistently exceeds \(70\%\).
  • The cross-sectional intercept \(\lambda_0\) is statistically indistinguishable from zero, and individual pricing errors \(\hat{e}^p\) are economically negligible.

4Where the Canon Breaks

The traditional international macro literature treats exchange rates as determined primarily by bilateral economic differentials (bilateral trade balances, inflation differentials, relative money growth). This canon breaks decisively:

  • The Bilateral Illusion: Over \(70\%\) of bilateral exchange rate variance against the US dollar is explained by two common global factors. Treating exchange rates as independent bilateral time series ignores the massive common factor structure.
  • Failure of Bilateral Macro Fundamentals: As established by Meese & Rogoff (1983), bilateral macro models cannot outperform a naive random walk at horizons under 12 months. In contrast, financial market factors (\(DOL\) and \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\)) price currency cross-sections with high precision.
  • Base Currency Invariance: While the Dollar factor is specific to the US dollar as the base currency, the cross-sectional risk premium captured by \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) is base-currency invariant. Sorting from any non-US perspective (such as EUR or GBP) yields an identical carry factor spread.

5Misconceptions

  • The Dollar factor earns a high positive expected return because the US dollar is the global reserve currency.

    The Dollar factor is a level factor with large variance but an expected return close to zero (historically between \(-0.5\%\) and \(+0.5\%\) annualized). Investors earn no significant risk premium simply for holding foreign currencies against the dollar; risk premia exist purely in the cross-sectional spread (\(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\)).

  • HML-FX factor construction requires complex forecasting algorithms or machine learning models.

    HML-FX is purely mechanical and requires zero predictive forecasting. Currencies are simply sorted into portfolios based on their observable contemporaneous forward discount \(f_t - s_t\) at the monthly rebalancing date.

  • Currency carry returns reflect country-specific sovereign default risks.

    Even among G10 sovereigns with zero perceived default risk and AAA credit ratings (such as Germany, Japan, Switzerland, Australia, and New Zealand), the HML-FX carry factor exhibits large spreads and systematic pricing. Carry premia reflect global stochastic discount factor volatility rather than default probability.

6Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 4.1 · Dollar and HML-FX Factor Calculation
In month \(t\), five currency portfolios sorted by forward discount generate excess returns against the US dollar of: \(rx^1 = -1.20\%\), \(rx^2 = -0.40\%\), \(rx^3 = +0.10\%\), \(rx^4 = +0.80\%\), and \(rx^5 = +2.20\%\). Calculate: (a) The realized Dollar factor return \(DOL_t\); (b) The realized \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}, t}\) return.

Worked Solution

(a) The Dollar factor is the equal-weighted average return across all 5 portfolios: \(DOL_t = \frac{1}{5}(-1.20 - 0.40 + 0.10 + 0.80 + 2.20)\% = \frac{1.50\%}{5} = +0.30\%\). (b) The HML-FX factor is the difference between Portfolio 5 and Portfolio 1: \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}, t} = rx^5 - rx^1 = +2.20\% - (-1.20\%) = +3.40\%\).

Problem 4.2 · Single-Index Factor Model Decomposition
A high-carry portfolio has a Dollar factor beta of \(\beta_{DOL} = 1.05\) and an HML-FX beta of \(\beta_{HML} = 0.45\). In a month where the US dollar depreciates against all currencies such that \(DOL_t = +2.0\%\), and the carry factor drops due to market turmoil such that \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}, t} = -3.5\%\), calculate the predicted return of the portfolio assuming zero alpha.

Worked Solution

Under the two-factor model, predicted return is \(\hat{rx} = \beta_{DOL} DOL_t + \beta_{HML} HML_t = 1.05 \times (+2.0\%) + 0.45 \times (-3.5\%) = +2.10\% - 1.575\% = +0.525\%\).

Problem 4.3 · Cross-Sectional Expected Return Estimation
In a two-pass asset pricing test, the estimated factor risk premia are \(\lambda_{DOL} = 0.00\%\) and \(\lambda_{HML} = 6.00\%\) per annum, with zero cross-sectional intercept (\(\lambda_0 = 0\)). If Portfolio 1 has \(\beta_{HML}^1 = -0.50\) and Portfolio 5 has \(\beta_{HML}^5 = +0.50\), compute their equilibrium expected annual excess returns.

Worked Solution

The expected return is \(\mathbb{E}[rx^p] = \beta_{DOL}^p \lambda_{DOL} + \beta_{HML}^p \lambda_{HML}\). For Portfolio 1: \(\mathbb{E}[rx^1] = (1.00)(0.00\%) + (-0.50)(6.00\%) = -3.00\%\) per annum. For Portfolio 5: \(\mathbb{E}[rx^5] = (1.00)(0.00\%) + (+0.50)(6.00\%) = +3.00\%\) per annum. The expected spread is \(+3.00\% - (-3.00\%) = 6.00\%\), exactly matching \(\lambda_{HML}\).

Tier 2 · Proofs & Derivations

Problem 4.4 · Dollar Neutrality of HML-FX
Let \(r_t^{j/USD}\) be the return of foreign currency \(j\) relative to USD. Define \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}} = \sum_{j \in P_5} w_j r_t^{j/USD} - \sum_{k \in P_1} w_k r_t^{k/USD}\), where \(\sum_{j \in P_5} w_j = \sum_{k \in P_1} w_k = 1\). Prove that for any third base currency (e.g., EUR), the return of \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) is algebraically identical.

Worked Solution

Let foreign exchange return relative to EUR be \(r_t^{j/EUR} = r_t^{j/USD} - r_t^{EUR/USD}\). Expressing \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) in EUR terms yields: \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}^{EUR} = \sum_{j \in P_5} w_j (r_t^{j/USD} - r_t^{EUR/USD}) - \sum_{k \in P_1} w_k (r_t^{k/USD} - r_t^{EUR/USD}) = \sum_{j \in P_5} w_j r_t^{j/USD} - r_t^{EUR/USD} \sum_{j \in P_5} w_j - \left(\sum_{k \in P_1} w_k r_t^{k/USD} - r_t^{EUR/USD} \sum_{k \in P_1} w_k\right)\). Since \(\sum w_j = 1\) and \(\sum w_k = 1\), the EUR/USD terms cancel completely: \(-r_t^{EUR/USD} + r_t^{EUR/USD} = 0\). Thus \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}^{EUR} = HML_{\mathrm{FX}}^{USD}\), proving base-currency invariance.

Problem 4.5 · Derivation of Factor Betas in Affine SDF Model
Suppose country \(j\) has SDF loading \(\sqrt{\delta_j} u_{t+1}^w\) on the global shock, and domestic country has loading \(\sqrt{\delta_0} u_{t+1}^w\). Prove that the covariance between bilateral excess return \(rx_{t+1}^j\) and the global factor shock is proportional to \(\sqrt{\delta_j} - \sqrt{\delta_0}\).

Worked Solution

The bilateral excess return satisfies \(rx_{t+1}^j = \mathbb{E}_t[rx_{t+1}^j] + (m_{t+1}^j - m_{t+1}^0) + (i_t^j - i_t^0) = \mathbb{E}_t[rx_{t+1}^j] - (\sqrt{\delta_j} - \sqrt{\delta_0}) u_{t+1}^w + \text{idiosyncratic shocks}\). Computing the covariance with the global shock: \(\Cov(rx_{t+1}^j, u_{t+1}^w) = \Cov(-(\sqrt{\delta_j} - \sqrt{\delta_0}) u_{t+1}^w, u_{t+1}^w) = -(\sqrt{\delta_j} - \sqrt{\delta_0}) \Var(u_{t+1}^w) = -(\sqrt{\delta_j} - \sqrt{\delta_0})\). This proves that differential loading on the global state variable governs the systematic asset pricing risk of currency returns.

Problem 4.6 · Uncentered R-Squared in Zero-Intercept Models
In Stage 2 Fama-MacBeth regressions through the origin (\(\bar{r}^p = \lambda' \beta^p\)), prove why the standard centered coefficient of determination \(R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}\) can be negative, whereas the uncentered \(R_{unc}^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum y_i^2}\) is strictly bounded in \([0, 1]\).

Worked Solution

When an intercept is omitted from an OLS regression, the sum of residuals \(\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)\) is not constrained to be zero. Consequently, the residual sum of squares \(\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\) can exceed the centered total sum of squares \(\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2\), producing a negative centered \(R^2\). For the uncentered metric, the projection theorem in Euclidean space guarantees that orthogonal projection onto the subspace spanned by regressors minimizes \(\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\) relative to the zero vector, ensuring \(0 \le \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \le \sum y_i^2\), which restricts \(R_{unc}^2 \in [0, 1]\).

Tier 3 · Empirical & Data

Problem 4.7 · Monotonicity of HML-FX Betas
Using the empirical findings of Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan (2011), the estimated HML-FX betas across 5 sorted portfolios are \(\hat{\beta}_{HML} = [-0.52, -0.16, 0.03, 0.17, 0.48]\). Explain why the monotonic increase in betas across portfolios is necessary to validate the risk-based pricing explanation of the carry trade.

Worked Solution

If carry trade profits were driven by market inefficiencies or unpriced microstructural anomalies, factor betas on a systematic factor would exhibit no coherent alignment with average returns. The strictly monotonic progression of betas from \(-0.52\) to \(+0.48\) establishes that high-yield currencies have high systematic covariance with bad economic states (when HML-FX falls), while low-yield funding currencies hedge bad states, fulfilling the fundamental risk-return trade-off of asset pricing theory.

Problem 4.8 · Verdelhan (2017) Systematic Variance Share
In a bilateral exchange rate regression \(\Delta s_t^j = \alpha_j + \beta_j DOL_t + \gamma_j HML_{\mathrm{FX}, t} + \varepsilon_t^j\), the average \(R^2\) across 20 floating currencies is \(45\%\). If the Dollar factor is omitted, the average \(R^2\) drops to \(8\%\). What does this imply about the primary driver of bilateral currency volatility?

Worked Solution

The collapse of average \(R^2\) from 45% to 8% proves that the vast majority of systematic bilateral currency variation against the USD is driven by the Dollar factor (a global level shock affecting all exchange rates simultaneously), rather than the slope carry factor. HML-FX accounts for cross-sectional expected return differences, but the Dollar factor drives common volatility.

Problem 4.9 · Cross-Sectional Pricing Error Test
In a test of 5 currency portfolios, the vector of sample mean excess returns is \(\bar{\mathbf{r}} = [-5.13\%, -1.83\%, -0.49\%, 1.20\%, 4.24\%]\). The predicted returns from the two-factor model are \(\hat{\mathbf{r}} = [-5.08\%, -1.80\%, -0.52\%, 1.15\%, 4.25\%]\). Compute the Mean Absolute Pricing Error (MAPE) across the 5 portfolios.

Worked Solution

The pricing errors \(e_p = \bar{r}_p - \hat{r}_p\) are: \(e_1 = -5.13 - (-5.08) = -0.05\%\); \(e_2 = -1.83 - (-1.80) = -0.03\%\); \(e_3 = -0.49 - (-0.52) = +0.03\%\); \(e_4 = 1.20 - 1.15 = +0.05\%\); \(e_5 = 4.24 - 4.25 = -0.01\%\). The Mean Absolute Pricing Error is \(\mathrm{MAPE} = \frac{1}{5}(0.05 + 0.03 + 0.03 + 0.05 + 0.01)\% = \frac{0.17\%}{5} = 0.034\%\) (3.4 basis points), indicating virtually perfect cross-sectional pricing.

Tier 4 · Research & Extensions

Problem 4.10 · Shanken (1992) Errors-in-Variables Correction
In two-pass cross-sectional regressions, Stage 2 standard errors are biased downward because Stage 1 betas are estimated with sampling error. State the Shanken (1992) correction factor \(c = (1 + \hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}' \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_f^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\) and calculate the standard error inflation for a factor Sharpe ratio of \(\mathrm{SR} = 0.60\).

Worked Solution

The Shanken correction factor is \(c = 1 + \hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}' \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_f^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}} = 1 + \mathrm{SR}_f^2\). For \(\mathrm{SR} = 0.60\), \(c = 1 + 0.60^2 = 1 + 0.36 = 1.36\). The corrected asymptotic variance is multiplied by \(c\), so standard errors are inflated by \(\sqrt{c} = \sqrt{1.36} \approx 1.166\) (\(16.6\%\) increase in standard error), preventing false rejections of the null hypothesis in finite samples.

Problem 4.11 · Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) Joint Estimation
Formulate the moment conditions for estimating factor betas and risk premia jointly in a single-step GMM system. How does this overcome the two-pass errors-in-variables problem?

Worked Solution

The moment conditions stack the time-series orthogonality conditions \(\mathbb{E}[(rx_t - a - \beta f_t) \otimes (1, f_t')] = 0\) and the cross-sectional pricing equation \(\mathbb{E}[rx_t - \beta \lambda] = 0\). Estimating all parameters \(\theta = (a, \beta, \lambda)\) simultaneously via GMM allows the asymptotic covariance matrix of \(\hat{\theta}\) derived from the sandwich formula \((D' W D)^{-1} D' W S W D (D' W D)^{-1}\) to account for the joint sampling covariance between \(\hat{\beta}\) and \(\hat{\lambda}\), automatically correcting errors-in-variables.

Problem 4.12 · Spurious Factor Identification in FX Cross-Sections
Explain why a proposed macro factor that is correlated with the Dollar factor but has zero cross-sectional beta dispersion across carry portfolios will fail to price the carry spread, despite producing a high time-series \(R^2\).

Worked Solution

A factor that correlates with the Dollar factor will produce high time-series \(R^2\) because it mimics the level factor. However, cross-sectional asset pricing identification requires beta dispersion: \(\Var(\beta^p) > 0\). If all portfolios have identical loadings (\(\beta^1 \approx \beta^5 \approx 1.0\)), the factor cannot distinguish between high-return Portfolio 5 and low-return Portfolio 1, rendering the cross-sectional slope \(\lambda\) unidentifiable and failing the rank condition.

8Retrieval Practice

  1. How is the Lustig, Roussanov, and Verdelhan (2011) Dollar factor constructed?

    Options

    The Dollar factor is the cross-sectional average excess return of all foreign currency portfolios against the USD: \(DOL_t = \frac{1}{K}\sum rx_t^p\).

  2. What role does the HML-FX factor play in the cross-section of currency returns?

    Options

    HML-FX is a slope factor whose betas rise monotonically across forward-discount sorted portfolios, pricing the cross-sectional carry spread.

  3. Why are Dollar factor betas (\(\beta_{DOL}\)) approximately equal to 1.0 across all currency portfolios?

    Options

    The Dollar factor is a level factor isolating domestic US pricing kernel shocks, which enter symmetrically across all foreign currencies against the dollar.

  4. In month \(t\), Portfolio 5 returns \(+2.20\%\) and Portfolio 1 returns \(-1.20\%\). What is the realized return of HML-FX in percentage points?

    \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}} = +2.20\% - (-1.20\%) = +3.40\%\).

  5. The returns of 5 currency portfolios in month \(t\) are \(-1.20\%\), \(-0.40\%\), \(+0.10\%\), \(+0.80\%\), and \(+2.20\%\). What is the Dollar factor return in percentage points?

    \(DOL = \frac{-1.20 - 0.40 + 0.10 + 0.80 + 2.20}{5}\% = \frac{1.50\%}{5} = 0.30\%\).

  6. Is the HML-FX carry factor dependent on the choice of the US dollar as the base currency?

    Options

    Because HML-FX is dollar-neutral with equal weights on both sides, any third-currency base returns cancel out algebraically.

  7. According to Verdelhan (2017), what percentage of bilateral exchange rate variation is systematic?

    Options

    Verdelhan (2017) shows that systematic variation accounts for 40%–50% of bilateral currency variance, dominated by the Dollar factor.

  8. What is the theoretical Dollar factor beta (\(\beta_{DOL}\)) for any currency portfolio in the Lustig-Roussanov-Verdelhan model?

    By the level factor construction, \(\beta_{DOL} = 1.00\) for all portfolios.
