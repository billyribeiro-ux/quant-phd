Carry Crash Risk & Asymmetric Liquidity Spirals

The currency carry trade generates attractive Sharpe ratios in quiet periods but suffers severe left-tail skewness and catastrophic drawdowns. During liquidity crunches and spikes in global risk aversion, funding currencies appreciate violently while target currencies collapse. This lesson examines the economic mechanisms driving currency crashes: the interaction of funding constraints, margin requirements, and fire-sale spirals formalized by Brunnermeier, Nagel, and Pedersen (2008). We analyze crash episodes, model asymmetric liquidity spirals, measure higher return moments, and derive how sudden flight-to-safety dynamics unwind leveraged carry portfolios.

1The Asymmetry of Currency Carry Returns

A classic empirical stylized fact of the carry trade is that returns are characterized by “going up by the stairs and coming down by the elevator.” While carry positions accumulate positive interest differentials gradually during periods of tranquil market conditions, they frequently experience violent, abrupt drawdowns over horizons of days or weeks.

Definition 2.1 · Carry Crash Risk
Carry crash risk is the susceptibility of currency carry trade strategies to sudden, severe, and negatively skewed drawdowns caused by the coordinated unwinding of leveraged positions when financial intermediaries face binding funding liquidity constraints or market volatility surges.

In standard asset pricing models with frictionless markets and Gaussian fundamentals, excess returns would be conditionally symmetric. However, across historical samples spanning both G10 currencies and emerging markets, currency carry portfolios exhibit substantial negative sample skewness (\(\gamma_3 < -0.70\)) and pronounced excess kurtosis (\(\gamma_4 > 4.0\)).

Consider the excess return of a target currency \(j\) over a funding currency \(k\): \[\begin{aligned} rx_{t+1}^{j/k} &= \Delta s_{t+1}^{j/k} + (i_{t}^j - i_{t}^k). \end{aligned}\] While the carry yield \(i_{t}^j - i_{t}^k\) is deterministic and strictly positive over period \(t \to t+1\), the spot depreciation \(\Delta s_{t+1}^{j/k}\) can become intensely negative when investors simultaneously liquidate target currency assets to repay funding currency liabilities.

2Theoretical Framework: Loss Spirals and Margin Spirals

To explain why carry trade drawdowns are so fast and severe, Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen (2008) and Brunnermeier & Pedersen (2008) developed a model of endogenous liquidity spirals connecting market liquidity with funding liquidity.

Consider speculators who hold a carry trade position of size \(x_t\) in the target currency funded by borrowing in the funding currency. Speculators possess equity capital \(W_t\). Financiers enforce a margin requirement \(m_t \in (0, 1)\) per unit of position, imposing the leverage constraint: \[\begin{aligned} x_t &\le \frac{W_t}{m_t}. \end{aligned}\] When an adverse fundamental shock hits the target currency, two distinct feedback loops ignite:

  1. The Loss Spiral: A negative shock to the target currency reduces net worth \(W_{t+1} < W_t\). Because leverage is constrained by \(x_{t+1} \le W_{t+1} / m\), speculators must sell \(\Delta x = (W_t - W_{t+1})/m\) units of the target currency. With finite market depth \(\Lambda\), this forced liquidation depresses the target currency further.
  2. The Margin Spiral: As currency volatility increases during the crash, prime brokers raise margin requirements from \(m_t\) to \(m_{t+1} > m_t\). Higher margin requirements force immediate deleveraging even if capital were intact, compounding fire-sale pressure.

Theorem 2.1 · Asymmetric Deleveraging & Endogenous Negative Skewness
Suppose speculators enter period \(t\) with equity capital \(W_t\), target position \(x_t = W_t / m\), and face a linear price impact curve \(S_{t+1} - S_t = \varepsilon_{t+1} - \lambda \cdot \max(0, x_t - W_{t+1}/m)\), where \(\lambda > 0\) represents the market illiquidity coefficient and \(\varepsilon_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) is a symmetric fundamental shock. Then the net return distribution of the carry trade is strictly negatively skewed: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[(rx_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}[rx_{t+1}])^3] &< 0. \end{aligned}\] Furthermore, the downside amplification factor strictly exceeds the upside gain factor.

Proof. Let gross fundamental return without forced liquidations be \(r_0 \equiv \varepsilon_{t+1}\). If \(\varepsilon_{t+1} \ge 0\), capital increases (\(W_{t+1} = W_t + x_t \varepsilon_{t+1} \ge W_t\)). The margin constraint \(x_t \le W_{t+1} / m\) is slack, so forced liquidations are zero: \(\max(0, x_t - W_{t+1}/m) = 0\). The net realized return on the position is simply \(r_{net} = \varepsilon_{t+1}\).

Conversely, if \(\varepsilon_{t+1} < 0\), initial net worth falls to \(W_{t+1}^{gross} = W_t + x_t \varepsilon_{t+1}\). The allowable position drops to \(W_{t+1}^{gross} / m = x_t + \frac{x_t}{m} \varepsilon_{t+1} < x_t\). To restore compliance, the trader must liquidate: \[\begin{aligned} \Delta x_{sell} &= x_t - \frac{W_{t+1}^{gross}}{m} = -\frac{x_t}{m} \varepsilon_{t+1} > 0. \end{aligned}\] Selling \(\Delta x_{sell}\) into an order book with illiquidity parameter \(\lambda\) induces an additional price decline \(-\lambda \Delta x_{sell} = \frac{\lambda x_t}{m} \varepsilon_{t+1}\). Including this secondary price impact, the realized return becomes: \[\begin{aligned} r_{net}(\varepsilon_{t+1}) &= \begin{cases} \varepsilon_{t+1}, & \text{if } \varepsilon_{t+1} \ge 0, \\ \varepsilon_{t+1} \left(1 + \frac{\lambda x_t}{m}\right), & \text{if } \varepsilon_{t+1} < 0. \end{cases} \end{aligned}\] Let \(k \equiv 1 + \frac{\lambda x_t}{m} > 1\). The transformation \(r_{net}(\varepsilon)\) is piecewise linear with slope \(1\) for \(\varepsilon \ge 0\) and slope \(k > 1\) for \(\varepsilon < 0\). By convexity on the left and linearity on the right, for any symmetric density \(f(\varepsilon) = f(-\varepsilon)\), the third central moment satisfies: \[\begin{aligned} \int_{-\infty}^\infty (r_{net}(\varepsilon) - \bar{r})^3 f(\varepsilon) \, d\varepsilon &= \int_{0}^\infty (\varepsilon - \bar{r})^3 f(\varepsilon) \, d\varepsilon + \int_{0}^\infty (-k\varepsilon - \bar{r})^3 f(\varepsilon) \, d\varepsilon \\ &= -(k^3 - 1) \int_0^\infty \varepsilon^3 f(\varepsilon) \, d\varepsilon - 3 \bar{r} (k^2 - 1)\int_0^\infty \varepsilon^2 f(\varepsilon) \, d\varepsilon + \mathcal{O}(\bar{r}^2). \end{aligned}\] Since \(k > 1\) and \(\bar{r} = \frac{1-k}{2} \mathbb{E}[|\varepsilon|] < 0\), every leading term is strictly negative. Therefore, the return distribution exhibits strict negative skewness \(\gamma_3 < 0\). End of proof.∎

Lab 1 · Carry crash risk & liquidity spiral simulation

Simulating carry trade returns under margin constraints and feedback unwinding will generate negative skewness (\(\gamma_3 < -0.5\)) and excess kurtosis (\(\gamma_4 > 3.0\)) from symmetric Gaussian fundamental shocks.

"""Lab X2-L02-1: Carry Crash Risk & Asymmetric Liquidity Spiral Simulator.

Simulates endogenous margin constraints, forced unwinding, and negative skewness
in currency carry trades (Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen 2008).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def simulate_liquidity_spiral(
    n_days: int = 500,
    seed: int = 101,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate carry trade returns under margin constraints and feedback unwinding.

    Args:
        n_days: Number of simulated trading days.
        seed: Random seed.

    Returns:
        Summary metrics including mean return, volatility, skewness, and kurtosis.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    daily_carry_yield = 0.0003  # approx 7.5% annualized interest rate spread
    capital = 1.0  # trader net worth
    max_leverage = 5.0
    position = capital * max_leverage  # target size
    market_depth = 40.0  # liquidity parameter

    returns: list[float] = []
    vol = 0.005

    for _ in range(n_days):
        # Stochastic volatility clustering
        vol = max(0.003, min(0.025, 0.94 * vol + 0.06 * 0.005 + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.001)))
        fund_shock = rng.gauss(0.0, vol)
        gross_ret = daily_carry_yield + fund_shock

        # Mark-to-market capital update
        capital_change = position * gross_ret
        capital = max(0.05, capital + capital_change)

        # Margin constraint: max_position = capital * max_leverage
        max_pos = capital * max_leverage
        unwind_flow = 0.0

        if position > max_pos:
            # Margin call: forced deleveraging
            unwind_flow = position - max_pos
            position = max_pos
        else:
            # Re-leveraging towards target
            position = min(max_pos, position + 0.1 * (max_pos - position))

        # Price impact from fire sales
        price_impact = -unwind_flow / market_depth
        net_ret = gross_ret + price_impact
        returns.append(net_ret)

    # Calculate sample moments
    n = len(returns)
    mean_ret = sum(returns) / n
    var_ret = sum((r - mean_ret) ** 2 for r in returns) / (n - 1)
    std_ret = math.sqrt(var_ret)

    # Sample skewness (gamma_3) and kurtosis (gamma_4)
    m3 = sum((r - mean_ret) ** 3 for r in returns) / n
    m4 = sum((r - mean_ret) ** 4 for r in returns) / n
    skewness = m3 / (std_ret**3)
    kurtosis = m4 / (std_ret**4)

    return {
        "n_days": float(n_days),
        "mean_daily_ret": round(mean_ret, 5),
        "ann_vol": round(std_ret * math.sqrt(252), 4),
        "skewness": round(skewness, 4),
        "kurtosis": round(kurtosis, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = simulate_liquidity_spiral(n_days=500, seed=101)
    print("=== Carry Crash Risk & Liquidity Spiral Simulation ===")
    print(f"Sample length: {int(res['n_days'])} days")
    print(f"Daily mean return: {res['mean_daily_ret'] * 100:.3f}%")
    print(f"Annualized volatility: {res['ann_vol'] * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Sample skewness (gamma_3): {res['skewness']:.3f}")
    print(f"Sample kurtosis (gamma_4): {res['kurtosis']:.3f}")


main()

Sample length: 500 days
Daily mean return: -0.081%
Annualized volatility: 14.92%
Sample skewness (gamma_3): -0.757
Sample kurtosis (gamma_4): 4.403

The simulation demonstrates that asymmetric margin constraints truncate right-tail gains while amplifying left-tail losses through forced deleveraging fire sales, driving realized skewness to -0.757 and kurtosis to 4.403.

3Where the Canon Breaks

The neoclassical canonical model of foreign exchange pricing relies on two pillars: rational expectations and risk-neutral no-arbitrage. Under this canon, Uncovered Interest Parity must hold, leaving currency carry excess returns identically zero on average. The canon breaks along several dimensions:

  • The Free Lunch Sharpe Fallacy: Conventional wealth management presentations often display the carry trade's historical Sharpe ratio of 0.80 as pure unpriced “alpha.” This is an artifact of applying mean-variance statistics to a severely non-Gaussian, negatively skewed return distribution. Carry returns represent fair compensation for catastrophic left-tail jump risk.
  • Liquidity Black Holes: In textbook models, liquidity is continuous and friction-free. In reality, during carry crashes, foreign exchange liquidity vanishes discontinuously: bid-ask spreads explode, dealer quotes widen by orders of magnitude, and electronic limit order books empty out.
  • Cross-Market Contagion: Shocks originating in non-FX markets (such as the 1998 Russian debt default or the 2008 Lehman bankruptcy) trigger instantaneous liquidations of currency carry portfolios as global financial intermediaries scramble to protect consolidated balance sheet leverage.

4Misconceptions

  • Carry trade crashes are triggered solely by macroeconomic interest rate cuts in target countries.

    During acute liquidity crises, carry collapses unfold over hours or days before monetary authorities can hold emergency meetings. The primary catalyst is an exogenous shock to intermediary funding liquidity or credit spreads, which forces margin liquidations and fire sales independent of contemporaneous central bank rate changes (Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen 2008).

  • Mechanical stop-loss orders insulate leveraged carry strategies from severe drawdowns.

    During a market-wide liquidity spiral, market depth collapses and prices gap through order books. Stop-loss orders become market orders that execute with catastrophic slippage far below the stop trigger, converting planned risk control into realized fire-sale losses.

  • Foreign exchange volatility is symmetric between target and funding currencies.

    Standard Black-Scholes currency options assume geometric Brownian motion with symmetric volatility. In empirical FX markets, implied and realized volatility spikes during depreciations for target currencies (AUD, NZD), but spikes during rapid appreciations for funding currencies (JPY, CHF), reflecting flight-to-safety dynamics.

5Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 2.1 · Loss Spiral Deleveraging Calculation
A macro fund has \(\$10\) million in equity capital and establishes a \(5\times\) leveraged long position in AUD/JPY (\(\$50\) million total notional). The prime broker enforces a strict \(20\%\) margin requirement (\(m = 0.20\)). Overnight, AUD/JPY drops by \(4.0\%\). Calculate: (a) The fund's new equity capital \(W_1\); (b) The fund's maximum allowable position under the margin constraint; (c) The required liquidation volume to restore margin compliance.

Worked Solution

Initial position is \(x_0 = \$50\) million and initial equity is \(W_0 = \$10\) million. The overnight return is \(r = -0.04\), causing a capital loss of \(\Delta W = \$50 \times (-0.04) = -\$2.0\) million. (a) New equity is \(W_1 = W_0 + \Delta W = \$10.0 - \$2.0 = \$8.0\) million. The existing position value drops to \(\$50 \times (1 - 0.04) = \$48.0\) million. (b) With margin requirement \(m = 0.20\), maximum allowable position is \(x_{\max} = W_1 / m = \$8.0 / 0.20 = \$40.0\) million. (c) The fund must liquidate \(\Delta x = \$48.0 - \$40.0 = \$8.0\) million in AUD/JPY to regain compliance.

Problem 2.2 · Risk Reversal Valuation
For 3-month NZD/USD options, the 25-delta call implied volatility is \(10.8\%\) and the 25-delta put implied volatility is \(13.4\%\). Calculate the 25-delta risk reversal \(RR_{25}\) and explain the market sentiment implied by this quote.

Worked Solution

The risk reversal is calculated as \(RR_{25} = \sigma_{call}^{25\Delta} - \sigma_{put}^{25\Delta} = 10.8\% - 13.4\% = -2.60\%\). The negative risk reversal of \(-2.60\%\) indicates that traders are willing to pay a 260 basis point volatility premium for downside puts relative to upside calls, demonstrating pronounced market fear of a sudden depreciation of the New Zealand dollar.

Problem 2.3 · Cornish-Fisher Value-at-Risk Expansion
A monthly carry strategy has mean return \(\mu = 0.60\%\), standard deviation \(\sigma = 2.50\%\), skewness \(\gamma_3 = -0.90\), and excess kurtosis \(\gamma_4 = 2.10\). Compute the \(99\%\) 1-month Value-at-Risk using: (a) The standard Gaussian approximation (\(z_{0.99} = 2.326\)); (b) The Cornish-Fisher expansion incorporating skewness and kurtosis.

Worked Solution

(a) Under Gaussian normality: \(\text{VaR}_{0.99}^{\mathrm{Gauss}} = -(\mu - z_{0.99}\sigma) = -(0.0060 - 2.326 \times 0.0250) = 5.215\%\). (b) The Cornish-Fisher quantile adjustment for the 1st percentile (\(z = -2.326\)) gives \(w_{0.01} \approx -2.985\). The resulting Cornish-Fisher VaR is \(-(\mu + w_{0.01}\sigma) = -(0.0060 - 2.985 \times 0.0250) = 6.86\%\), showing an increase of 165 basis points over Gaussian Value-at-Risk.

Tier 2 · Proofs & Derivations

Problem 2.4 · Derivation of the Loss Spiral Multiplier
Suppose aggregate carry traders manage equity \(W_t\) with constant leverage \(L\), holding position \(X_t = L W_t\). The price impact of aggregate net selling \(Q\) on currency spot price \(P\) is \(\Delta P = -\lambda Q\). If an initial fundamental shock \(\delta\) reduces the asset value by \(\delta X_t\), derive the total equilibrium price decline \(\Delta P_{eq}\) accounting for the infinite feedback geometric series of fire sales.

Worked Solution

The initial price drop is \(\Delta P_0 = \delta\). This causes an initial equity loss of \(\Delta W_1 = X_t \Delta P_0 = L W_t \delta\). To restore leverage \(L\), traders must reduce their position by \(\Delta X_1 = L \Delta W_1 = L^2 W_t \delta\). This forced liquidation induces a second-round price decline of \(\Delta P_1 = -\lambda \Delta X_1 = -\lambda L^2 W_t \delta\). The sequence of price declines forms a geometric series: \(\Delta P_{eq} = \delta + \lambda L^2 W_t \delta + (\lambda L^2 W_t)^2 \delta + \dots = \frac{\delta}{1 - \lambda L^2 W_t}\), provided \(\lambda L^2 W_t < 1\). If \(\lambda L^2 W_t \ge 1\), the market undergoes an explosive liquidity collapse.

Problem 2.5 · Skewness from Piecewise Linear Payoffs
Let \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). Define the asymmetric payoff \(Y = X\) if \(X \ge 0\), and \(Y = c X\) if \(X < 0\), with \(c > 1\). Prove analytically that \(\mathbb{E}[(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])^3] < 0\).

Worked Solution

Let \(\phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}\). The mean is \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[Y] = \int_0^\infty x \phi(x) dx + c \int_{-\infty}^0 x \phi(x) dx = \frac{1-c}{\sqrt{2\pi}} < 0\). The uncentered third moment is \(\mathbb{E}[Y^3] = \int_0^\infty x^3 \phi(x) dx + c^3 \int_{-\infty}^0 x^3 \phi(x) dx = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}(1 - c^3)\). Since \(c > 1\), \(1 - c^3 < 0\), so \(\mathbb{E}[Y^3] < 0\). Expanding the central moment \(\mu_3 = \mathbb{E}[Y^3] - 3\mu \mathbb{E}[Y^2] + 2\mu^3\), direct algebraic evaluation confirms that the negative leading term \(\sqrt{2/\pi}(1-c^3)\) strictly dominates the mean adjustment terms, establishing that \(\mu_3 < 0\).

Problem 2.6 · Endogenous Margin Escalation
Suppose prime brokers set margin requirements according to a Value-at-Risk rule: \(m_t = k \sigma_t\), where \(\sigma_t\) is conditional daily volatility and \(k > 0\). If realized volatility responds to market-wide selling flow \(Q_t\) according to \(\sigma_t = \sigma_0 + \alpha Q_t\), derive the equilibrium margin \(m_t\) as a function of forced selling volume.

Worked Solution

Substituting the volatility reaction function into the broker margin rule gives \(m_t(Q_t) = k (\sigma_0 + \alpha Q_t) = k \sigma_0 + k \alpha Q_t\). If traders face an equity shock \(\Delta W < 0\), their required selling volume is \(Q_t = \frac{W_t}{m_t} - \frac{W_t + \Delta W}{m_t}\). Because \(m_t\) increases with \(Q_t\), the margin constraint tightens non-linearly, requiring solving the quadratic relation \(Q_t (k \sigma_0 + k \alpha Q_t) = -\Delta W\), showing that margin requirements jump sharply during liquidations.

Tier 3 · Empirical & Data

Problem 2.7 · The October 1998 USD/JPY Crash
On October 6, 1998, USD/JPY closed at 135.50. On October 8, 1998, it reached an intraday low of 111.50. (a) Compute the logarithmic return of the USD over this 2-day period. (b) If USD/JPY daily volatility in 1998 prior to October was \(12.0\%\) annualized, calculate the standard deviation multiple of this 2-day move.

Worked Solution

(a) The 2-day logarithmic return is \(r = \ln(111.50 / 135.50) = \ln(0.82288) = -0.1949\), or \(-19.49\%\). (b) Annualized volatility of \(12\%\) implies a daily standard deviation of \(\sigma_{daily} = 0.12 / \sqrt{252} = 0.00756\) (\(0.756\%\)). Over a 2-day window, the standard deviation is \(\sigma_{2d} = \sqrt{2} \times 0.00756 = 0.01069\) (\(1.069\%\)). The z-score of the move is \(z = -0.1949 / 0.01069 = -18.23\). Under a Gaussian distribution, an 18-sigma event has probability virtually zero, demonstrating the extreme non-normality of carry unwinds.

Problem 2.8 · Option Implied vs Realized Skewness
In a panel of 10 currencies over 15 years, the cross-sectional correlation between portfolio forward discount \(f_t - s_t\) and sample realized skewness is \(-0.68\). Explain how this empirical finding supports the hypothesis that carry trades face crash risk rather than continuous diffusive volatility.

Worked Solution

The strongly negative correlation (\(-0.68\)) confirms that high-interest rate target currencies (which have the most negative forward discounts \(f_t - s_t < 0\)) systematically experience the most severe negative return skewness. In contrast, low-rate funding currencies exhibit positive skewness. This empirical asymmetry proves that carry returns are not generated by symmetric diffusion but are vulnerable to rare, large negative jumps.

Problem 2.9 · Safe Haven Currency Betas during VIX Spikes
During months when the VIX increases by more than 10 points, the average return of the Japanese yen is \(+3.8\%\) and the Swiss franc is \(+2.9\%\), while the Australian dollar returns \(-6.4\%\). Estimate the conditional drawdown of a long AUD / short JPY carry portfolio during such volatility spikes.

Worked Solution

The return of the long AUD / short JPY carry portfolio is \(r_{AUD/JPY} \approx r_{AUD} - r_{JPY}\). Substituting the conditional returns yields \(r_{AUD/JPY} \approx -6.4\% - (+3.8\%) = -10.2\%\) in a single month. This double-whammy (target currency falling while funding currency surges) illustrates why carry trades suffer severe left-tail drawdowns when global risk aversion spikes.

Tier 4 · Research & Extensions

Problem 2.10 · Dynamic Leverage Sizing under Tail Constraints
A quantitative fund mandates that the 99% 1-day Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR / Expected Shortfall) must not exceed 2.0% of portfolio equity. In quiet regimes, carry return standard deviation is 0.40% daily with normal tails. In turbulent regimes (which occur with 5% probability), daily standard deviation jumps to 1.80% with mean -0.50%. Formulate the optimal leverage policy across regimes.

Worked Solution

Under the quiet regime, \(\text{CVaR}_{0.99}^{daily} = \sigma \frac{\phi(z_{0.99})}{0.01} = 0.40\% \times \frac{0.0266}{0.01} = 1.064\%\). With leverage \(L_q\), portfolio \(\text{CVaR} = L_q \times 1.064\% \le 2.0\%\), allowing leverage \(L_q \le 1.88\times\). In the turbulent regime, \(\text{CVaR}_{0.99}^{turb} \approx -(-0.50\%) + 1.80\% \times 2.66 = 5.29\%\). Setting \(L_t \times 5.29\% \le 2.0\%\) requires \(L_t \le 0.38\times\). To avoid forced liquidations when regimes switch, the fund must pre-emptively maintain leverage closer to 0.4x or hold large unencumbered cash buffers.

Problem 2.11 · Cross-Asset Contagion: FX and Credit Default Swaps
Explain why carry unwinds coincide with sharp widenings in sovereign and corporate CDS spreads. What is the economic channel connecting bank balance sheet capacity with foreign exchange basis and credit risk?

Worked Solution

Global financial intermediaries (dealers and hedge funds) participate simultaneously as market makers in FX swap markets and underwriters of credit protection in CDS markets. When asset market shocks erode dealer equity capital or regulatory leverage ratios bind (e.g., Basel III supplementary leverage ratio), intermediaries shrink their aggregate balance sheet. This contraction reduces funding liquidity in FX swaps and increases credit risk premia simultaneously, causing carry collapses and CDS spreads to spike in unison.

Problem 2.12 · Extreme Value Theory (EVT) Tail Index Estimation
Using the Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) for excess losses over a high threshold \(u\), the tail distribution is \(F_u(y) = 1 - (1 + \xi y / \beta)^{-1/\xi}\). For carry trades, empirical estimates find the shape parameter \(\xi \approx 0.25\). Prove that all moments of order \(k \ge 1/\xi = 4\) are infinite, and explain the financial implication for risk models relying on sample kurtosis.

Worked Solution

For a Pareto-type tail with index \(\xi > 0\), the uncentered moment \(\mathbb{E}[Y^k] = \int_0^\infty y^k dF_u(y) \propto \int^\infty y^k y^{-(1/\xi + 1)} dy = \int^\infty y^{k - 1/\xi - 1} dy\). This integral converges if and only if \(k - 1/\xi - 1 < -1\), which simplifies to \(k < 1/\xi\). With \(\xi = 0.25\), \(1/\xi = 4\). Therefore, moments of order \(k \ge 4\) (including the 4th moment used in kurtosis) do not exist in population. Sample kurtosis will fail to converge and will fluctuate wildly as sample size grows, invalidating traditional kurtosis-based risk metrics.

7Retrieval Practice

  1. What empirical characteristic distinguishes carry trade returns from standard Gaussian distributions?

    Options

    Carry trade returns are characterized by steady positive yields in calm times punctuated by sharp negative crashes, producing negative skewness and fat tails.

  2. According to Brunnermeier, Nagel, and Pedersen (2008), what mechanism generates the "loss spiral"?

    Options

    In a loss spiral, falling prices deplete speculator equity, forcing position liquidations that create further downward price pressure in illiquid markets.

  3. What defines the "margin spiral"?

    Options

    The margin spiral occurs when rising volatility induces lenders to raise margin haircut requirements, forcing traders to liquidate assets even if capital were intact.

  4. How is a 25-delta Risk Reversal defined?

    Options

    \(RR_{25} = \sigma_{call}^{25\Delta} - \sigma_{put}^{25\Delta}\). A negative value indicates that puts trade at a premium to calls.

  5. If the 25-delta call implied volatility is \(10.8\%\) and the 25-delta put implied volatility is \(13.4\%\), what is the 25-delta risk reversal in percentage points?

    \(RR_{25} = 10.8\% - 13.4\% = -2.60\%\).

  6. During the October 1998 LTCM crisis, what occurred in the USD/JPY currency pair?

    Options

    USD/JPY plunged from 136 to 115 within two days as hedge funds rushed to cover massive short yen funding positions.

  7. Why are standard stop-loss orders often ineffective at protecting against carry crashes?

    Options

    During a liquidity crash, bid-ask spreads widen dramatically and bids disappear, causing stop orders to execute with severe slippage.

  8. A currency pair drops from 135.50 to 111.50. What is the logarithmic return in percent: \(100 \times \ln(111.50 / 135.50)\)?

    \(100 \times \ln(111.50 / 135.50) = 100 \times (-0.1949) = -19.49\%\).
