The Peso Problem & Rare Disaster Models

Can the high historical Sharpe ratios of currency carry trades be explained by rare, catastrophic disasters that simply did not materialize in historical samples? This fundamental question lies at the heart of the peso problem hypothesis in empirical asset pricing. First identified in the Mexican peso market of the 1970s, peso problems describe how low-probability disaster regimes distort finite-sample estimators of expected returns and risk. This lesson formalizes rare disaster models, quantifies small-sample Sharpe ratio biases, and evaluates the landmark empirical test of Burnside, Eichenbaum, Kleshchelski, and Rebelo (2011), demonstrating why peso problems alone cannot resolve the carry trade puzzle.

1Origins & Formal Definition of the Peso Problem

In the early 1970s, the Mexican peso was pegged to the US dollar at \(12.50\) pesos per dollar. Despite this fixed exchange rate peg, Mexican short-term interest rates persistently exceeded US Treasury bill rates by several hundred basis points. Economists observed that the forward Mexican peso traded at a massive discount relative to the spot rate, appearing to offer a riskless arbitrage to anyone borrowing in dollars and investing in Mexican peso deposits.

Milton Friedman famously pointed out that investors rationally anticipated a large, discrete devaluation of the pegged peso. Month after month, as long as the Mexican government defended the peg, the devaluation did not materialize. Investors earned elevated returns in sample, making the strategy appear to produce persistent “abnormal” profits. In August 1976, the peg collapsed, and the peso devalued by nearly \(50\%\) overnight, inflicting crushing losses on carry positions.

Definition 3.1 · Peso Problem
A peso problem is an econometric phenomenon in which rational economic agents price in the probability of an infrequent, large structural shift or catastrophic disaster state, but because that disaster state occurs rarely or fails to materialize within a finite sample, empirical sample averages provide biased and misleading estimates of population expected returns and risk.

Under a peso problem, forward exchange rates incorporate rational disaster expectations: \[\begin{aligned} F_t &= \mathbb{E}_t[S_{t+1}] = (1 - p_t) \mathbb{E}_t[S_{t+1} \mid \text{Normal}] + p_t \mathbb{E}_t[S_{t+1} \mid \text{Disaster}], \end{aligned}\] where \(p_t \in (0, 1)\) is the conditional probability of a currency collapse. In a sample of length \(T\) where no disaster occurs, the econometrician observes only returns conditional on the normal regime. The observed sample mean \(\hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \Delta s_{t+1}\) converges to \(\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1} \mid \text{Normal}]\), not the unconditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}]\), producing an apparent violation of market efficiency.

2Mathematical Framework of Rare Disaster Pricing

Consider a two-state Markov regime-switching model for foreign exchange returns. Let \(D_t \in \{0, 1\}\) denote the regime indicator, where \(D_t = 0\) denotes the normal tranquil regime and \(D_t = 1\) represents a catastrophic currency collapse occurring with independent probability \(p \in (0, 1)\).

The conditional return of a long target currency position is: \[\begin{aligned} R_{t+1} &= \begin{cases} R_N + \varepsilon_{t+1}, & \text{with probability } 1 - p \quad (D_{t+1} = 0), \\ -L + \eta_{t+1}, & \text{with probability } p \quad (D_{t+1} = 1), \end{cases} \end{aligned}\] where \(R_N > 0\) is the normal regime yield, \(L > 0\) is the discrete disaster loss, and \(\varepsilon_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_N^2)\), \(\eta_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_D^2)\) are zero-mean idiosyncratic shocks.

Theorem 3.1 · Small-Sample Sharpe Ratio Bias under Rare Disasters
Suppose an asset's true unconditional expected excess return is \(\mu = (1 - p) R_N - p L\) and its unconditional variance is \(\sigma^2 = (1 - p) \sigma_N^2 + p \sigma_D^2 + p(1 - p)(R_N + L)^2\). In any finite sample of length \(T\) that does not contain a disaster realization (which occurs with probability \((1 - p)^T\)):

  1. The sample mean excess return \(\hat{\mu}_N\) is upward biased by \(\Delta \mu = p(R_N + L) > 0\);
  2. The sample variance \(\hat{\sigma}_N^2\) is downward biased relative to population variance;
  3. The conditional sample Sharpe ratio strictly exceeds the true unconditional population Sharpe ratio: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{SR}} \mid \text{No Disaster}] &> \mathrm{SR}_{\text{true}}. \end{aligned}\]

Proof. The unconditional mean excess return is: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[R] &= (1 - p) R_N - p L = R_N - p(R_N + L). \end{aligned}\] In a sample path of length \(T\) conditioned on no disasters (\(D_t = 0\) for all \(t = 1, \dots, T\)): \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu} \mid \text{No Disaster}] &= \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T (R_N + \varepsilon_t)\right] = R_N. \end{aligned}\] Subtracting the unconditional mean yields the exact bias: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu} \mid \text{No Disaster}] - \mathbb{E}[R] &= R_N - [R_N - p(R_N + L)] = p(R_N + L) > 0. \end{aligned}\] For the variance, by the law of total variance: \[\begin{aligned} \Var(R) &= \mathbb{E}[\Var(R \mid D)] + \Var(\mathbb{E}[R \mid D]) \\ &= (1 - p)\sigma_N^2 + p\sigma_D^2 + p(1 - p)(R_N - (-L))^2 \\ &= (1 - p)\sigma_N^2 + p\sigma_D^2 + p(1 - p)(R_N + L)^2. \end{aligned}\] Conditioned on no disaster, the sample variance converges to \(\sigma_N^2\). Assuming \(\sigma_D^2 \ge \sigma_N^2\) (or simply recognizing that \(p(1-p)(R_N + L)^2 > 0\)), we have \(\Var(R) > \sigma_N^2\). Finally, the sample Sharpe ratio without disasters satisfies: \[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{SR}_{\text{sample}} &\approx \frac{R_N}{\sigma_N} > \frac{R_N - p(R_N + L)}{\sqrt{(1-p)\sigma_N^2 + p\sigma_D^2 + p(1-p)(R_N + L)^2}} = \mathrm{SR}_{\text{true}}. \end{aligned}\] Both an inflated numerator and a deflated denominator strictly inflate the observed Sharpe ratio. End of proof.∎

Lab 1 · Peso problem small-sample bias simulator

Simulating 1,000 Monte Carlo paths of length 120 months with a rare disaster probability of 1.5% per annum will demonstrate: (1) over 80% of paths experience zero disasters; (2) paths without disasters produce sample Sharpe ratios upward biased by at least 15% above the true population Sharpe ratio; and (3) disaster paths suffer catastrophic drawdowns.

"""Lab X2-L03-1: Peso Problem Econometric Bias Simulator.

Quantifies small-sample upward bias in carry trade Sharpe ratios when rare catastrophic
devaluations do not materialize in finite samples (Burnside et al. 2011).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def simulate_peso_bias(
    n_simulations: int = 1000,
    n_months: int = 120,
    seed: int = 2024,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate carry returns under rare disaster regime and measure small-sample bias.

    Args:
        n_simulations: Number of Monte Carlo paths.
        n_months: Sample length in months per path (10 years).
        seed: Random seed.

    Returns:
        Dictionary of population, benign-sample, and disaster-sample Sharpe ratios.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    # Model parameters:
    # Monthly interest spread (carry yield) = +6.0% annualized / 12 = 0.005
    monthly_carry = 0.005
    # Normal monthly exchange rate volatility = 10% annualized / sqrt(12) = 0.02887
    normal_sd = 0.02887
    # Rare disaster: probability per month p_disaster = 0.015 / 12 = 0.00125
    p_disaster = 0.015 / 12.0
    # Disaster loss: -35% devaluation
    disaster_jump = -0.35

    # Theoretical unconditional moments:
    # E[rx] = monthly_carry + p_disaster * disaster_jump
    pop_mean_monthly = monthly_carry + p_disaster * disaster_jump
    pop_var_monthly = (1.0 - p_disaster) * (normal_sd**2) + p_disaster * (1.0 - p_disaster) * (disaster_jump**2)
    pop_std_monthly = math.sqrt(pop_var_monthly)
    pop_ann_sr = (pop_mean_monthly * 12.0) / (pop_std_monthly * math.sqrt(12.0))

    benign_sharpes: list[float] = []
    disaster_sharpes: list[float] = []
    paths_with_disaster = 0

    for _ in range(n_simulations):
        has_disaster = False
        path_returns: list[float] = []

        for _ in range(n_months):
            is_jump = rng.random() < p_disaster
            if is_jump:
                has_disaster = True
                ret = monthly_carry + disaster_jump + rng.gauss(0.0, normal_sd)
            else:
                ret = monthly_carry + rng.gauss(0.0, normal_sd)
            path_returns.append(ret)

        # Path metrics
        m = sum(path_returns) / n_months
        v = sum((r - m) ** 2 for r in path_returns) / (n_months - 1)
        s = math.sqrt(v)
        path_sr = (m * 12.0) / (s * math.sqrt(12.0)) if s > 1e-8 else 0.0

        if has_disaster:
            paths_with_disaster += 1
            disaster_sharpes.append(path_sr)
        else:
            benign_sharpes.append(path_sr)

    mean_benign_sr = sum(benign_sharpes) / len(benign_sharpes) if benign_sharpes else 0.0
    mean_disaster_sr = sum(disaster_sharpes) / len(disaster_sharpes) if disaster_sharpes else 0.0
    bias_ratio = mean_benign_sr / pop_ann_sr if pop_ann_sr > 0 else 0.0

    return {
        "n_simulations": float(n_simulations),
        "n_months": float(n_months),
        "disaster_prob_pct": round(paths_with_disaster / n_simulations * 100, 2),
        "pop_sharpe_ratio": round(pop_ann_sr, 4),
        "benign_sharpe_ratio": round(mean_benign_sr, 4),
        "disaster_sharpe_ratio": round(mean_disaster_sr, 4),
        "bias_ratio": round(bias_ratio, 3),
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = simulate_peso_bias(n_simulations=1000, n_months=120, seed=2024)
    print("=== Peso Problem Small-Sample Bias Simulator ===")
    print(f"Monte Carlo replications: {int(res['n_simulations'])}, Path horizon: {int(res['n_months'])} months")
    print(f"Paths experiencing rare disaster: {res['disaster_prob_pct']:.1f}%")
    print(f"True unconditional population Sharpe ratio: {res['pop_sharpe_ratio']:.3f}")
    print(f"Sample Sharpe ratio in benign paths (no disaster): {res['benign_sharpe_ratio']:.3f}")
    print(f"Sample Sharpe ratio in disaster paths: {res['disaster_sharpe_ratio']:.3f}")
    print(f"Small-sample upward bias factor: {res['bias_ratio']:.2f}x")


main()
=== Peso Problem Small-Sample Bias Simulator ===
Monte Carlo replications: 1000, Path horizon: 120 months
Paths experiencing rare disaster: 14.8%
True unconditional population Sharpe ratio: 0.503
Sample Sharpe ratio in benign paths (no disaster): 0.601
Sample Sharpe ratio in disaster paths: 0.184
Small-sample upward bias factor: 1.19x

The Monte Carlo simulation confirms that in benign paths (which represent 85.2% of all 10-year realizations), the observed sample Sharpe ratio is 0.601, compared to the true unconditional population Sharpe ratio of 0.503—an upward bias factor of 1.19x. This quantitatively proves the magnitude of peso problem distortions in finite historical datasets.

3The Burnside et al. (2011) Empirical Test

If the carry trade's positive Sharpe ratio is entirely an artifact of unobserved peso problem disasters, how can researchers test the hypothesis empirically? Burnside, Eichenbaum, Kleshchelski & Rebelo (2011) devised an ingenious test using options markets.

Burnside et al. construct a hedged carry trade:

  1. Enter a standard long target / short funding carry position.
  2. Purchase an out-of-the-money put option on the target currency (protecting against severe depreciation).
  3. Sell an out-of-the-money call option on the target currency to finance the put premium.
Because the downside put truncates all potential crash losses below the strike price \(K_{put}\), the hedged carry trade is immune to catastrophic tail risk. If peso problems explain carry returns, then buying crash insurance in the options market should fully absorb the carry premium, reducing the hedged carry Sharpe ratio to zero.

Remarkably, Burnside et al. (2011) find that:

  • The hedged carry trade still earns an annualized Sharpe ratio between \(0.40\) and \(0.60\) across G10 currencies.
  • The options required to hedge crash risk are not sufficiently expensive to eliminate carry profitability.
  • Therefore, peso problems alone cannot explain the currency carry trade; a substantial portion of the excess return reflects compensation for non-disaster systematic risk factors.

4Where the Canon Breaks

The neoclassical canon often treats historical time-series averages as unbiased ergodic estimators of forward-looking expectations. In foreign exchange markets, this assumption fails fundamentally:

  • The Sample Length Illusion: Researchers frequently assume that 30 or 40 years of monthly data (360–480 observations) is large enough for the Law of Large Numbers to ensure convergence. For rare events with return periods of 50 to 100 years (such as sovereign defaults, world wars, or global reserve currency regime shifts), even four decades of data represents a small sample.
  • Disaster Risk Under-Insurance: Economic theory suggests that if investors demand huge risk premia for disaster risk, out-of-the-money crash puts should trade at steep premiums. Burnside et al. (2011) show that market-implied put prices are insufficient to eliminate carry returns, contradicting pure disaster explanations.
  • Regime Non-Stationarity: Exchange rate regimes change endogenously. Fixed pegs, crawling bands, and floating currencies transition dynamically, meaning that past non-occurrences of devaluation do not imply stationary disaster probabilities.

5Misconceptions

  • The peso problem proves that carry trade profitability is purely an illusion and carries no true economic value.

    A peso problem demonstrates that sample averages can be upward biased, but it does not mean the carry trade is unprofitable. As shown by Burnside et al. (2011), even fully crash-hedged portfolios earn statistically significant positive returns with Sharpe ratios around 0.50, proving that true risk premia exist beyond disaster risk.

  • A 40-year backtest without a catastrophic crash guarantees that a currency strategy is immune to disaster risk.

    If a disaster has an annual probability of 1.5%, the probability of observing zero disasters in a 40-year window is \((1 - 0.015)^{40} \approx 54.6\%\). More than half of all 40-year sample paths will experience zero crashes, creating a false sense of security while disaster risk remains fully active.

  • The peso problem applies only to emerging market pegged currencies like the 1976 Mexican peso.

    Developed floating currencies are also subject to peso problems. For example, the Swiss franc (EUR/CHF) floor removal in January 2015, the UK Brexit vote in June 2016, and the 1998 USD/JPY collapse represent rare structural events that distorted sample estimators across major G10 markets.

6Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 3.1 · Disaster Probability from Forward Discount
Suppose a foreign currency has spot rate \(S_t = 1.00\) USD. In the normal regime, the expected spot rate at \(t+1\) is \(1.00\) USD. If a disaster occurs, the exchange rate devalues to \(0.60\) USD. The 1-year forward rate is \(F_t = 0.94\) USD. Under risk-neutral pricing, calculate the implied probability \(p\) of a disaster occurring over the year.

Worked Solution

Under risk neutrality, the forward rate equals the expected future spot rate: \(F_t = (1 - p) S_{\mathrm{normal}} + p S_{\mathrm{disaster}}\). Substituting the values: \(0.94 = (1 - p)(1.00) + p(0.60) = 1.00 - 0.40 p\). Solving for \(p\) gives \(0.40 p = 1.00 - 0.94 = 0.06\), which yields \(p = 0.06 / 0.40 = 0.15\) (\(15\%\) annual probability of disaster).

Problem 3.2 · Probability of Zero Disasters over Multi-Decade Sample
A sovereign debt or currency crisis has an annual occurrence probability of \(p = 0.02\). Calculate the probability that an econometrician observes zero crises over: (a) A 10-year sample (\(T = 10\)); (b) A 30-year sample (\(T = 30\)); (c) A 50-year sample (\(T = 50\)).

Worked Solution

The probability of zero disasters in \(T\) independent years is \(P(N = 0) = (1 - p)^T = (0.98)^T\). (a) For \(T = 10\): \((0.98)^{10} \approx 0.817\) (\(81.7\%\)). (b) For \(T = 30\): \((0.98)^{30} \approx 0.545\) (\(54.5\%\)). (c) For \(T = 50\): \((0.98)^{50} \approx 0.364\) (\(36.4\%\)). Even after half a century, there is more than a one-in-three chance that the disaster never occurs in sample.

Problem 3.3 · Sharpe Ratio Inflation Calculation
A carry strategy earns \(R_N = 8.0\%\) annual excess return in normal years with volatility \(\sigma_N = 10.0\%\). Rare crashes occur with probability \(p = 0.02\), inflicting a loss of \(L = 40.0\%\) with crash volatility \(\sigma_D = 25.0\%\). Compute: (a) The sample Sharpe ratio observed in a sample with no crashes; (b) The true population unconditional mean return and unconditional Sharpe ratio.

Worked Solution

(a) In a crash-free sample: \(\mathrm{SR}_{\mathrm{sample}} = R_N / \sigma_N = 8.0\% / 10.0\% = 0.800\). (b) The true unconditional mean is \(\mathbb{E}[R] = (1 - 0.02)(8.0\%) - 0.02(40.0\%) = 7.84\% - 0.80\% = 7.04\%\). Unconditional variance is \(\Var(R) = (0.98)(0.10^2) + (0.02)(0.25^2) + (0.02)(0.98)(0.08 - (-0.40))^2 = 0.0098 + 0.00125 + 0.0196 \times 0.2304 = 0.01105 + 0.004516 = 0.015566\). Thus \(\sigma_{\mathrm{pop}} = \sqrt{0.015566} \approx 0.12476\) (\(12.48\%\)). The true population Sharpe ratio is \(\mathrm{SR}_{\mathrm{true}} = 7.04\% / 12.48\% = 0.564\). The crash-free sample Sharpe ratio is inflated by \((0.800 - 0.564) / 0.564 = 41.8\%\).

Tier 2 · Proofs & Derivations

Problem 3.4 · Asymptotic Distribution of Sample Mean under Rare Events
Let \(R_t = R_N + \varepsilon_t\) with probability \(1 - p\), and \(R_t = -L + \eta_t\) with probability \(p\), where \(p = c / T\) shrinks as sample size \(T \to \infty\). Derive the asymptotic limiting distribution of the standardized sample mean \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\mu}_T - \mathbb{E}[R])\).

Worked Solution

When rare disaster probability scales as \(p_T = c/T\), the number of disasters \(K_T \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(T, c/T)\) converges in distribution to a Poisson random variable \(K \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(c)\). The sample sum is \(S_T = \sum_{t=1}^T R_t = \sum_{i=1}^{T - K_T} (R_N + \varepsilon_i) + \sum_{j=1}^{K_T} (-L + \eta_j)\). Normalizing yields \(\hat{\mu}_T = R_N - \frac{K_T}{T}(R_N + L) + \bar{\varepsilon}\). Consequently, the limiting distribution is a compound Poisson-Gaussian mixture rather than a standard Gaussian distribution, proving that standard central limit theorems fail under rare-disaster asymptotics.

Problem 3.5 · Crash Hedging Option Cost Bounds
Consider an asset with normal return \(R_N\) and crash return \(-L\). A put option paying \(\max(0, K - S_1)\) is purchased at premium \(C_P\). Prove that if the option market prices crash risk fairly under a risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) where \(\mathbb{Q}(\text{Disaster}) = q > p\), the hedged carry trade Sharpe ratio must satisfy \(\mathrm{SR}_{\mathrm{hedged}} \le \frac{R_N - q L}{\sigma_N}\).

Worked Solution

The cost of the crash put with strike \(K\) that covers the entire disaster loss \(L\) is \(C_P = e^{-r} \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\max(0, K - S_1)] \ge e^{-r} q L\). Under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\), the expected payout received from the put during disaster is \(p L\). The net expected return of the hedged position is \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[R_{\mathrm{hedged}}] = R_N - (C_P e^r - p L) \le R_N - (q L - p L) = R_N - (q - p)L\). Since risk aversion implies \(q > p\), the cost of hedging exceeds the actuarial value of losses by \((q - p)L\). Dividing by normal volatility \(\sigma_N\) establishes the upper bound on the hedged Sharpe ratio.

Problem 3.6 · Maximum Likelihood Estimation of Disaster Probability
In a sample of \(T\) independent periods, \(k\) disasters are observed. The likelihood function is \(L(p) = p^k (1 - p)^{T - k}\). (a) Show that the maximum likelihood estimator is \(\hat{p} = k / T\). (b) If \(k = 0\), calculate the upper bound of the 95% one-sided confidence interval for \(p\) using the Rule of Three.

Worked Solution

(a) The log-likelihood is \(\ln L(p) = k \ln p + (T - k)\ln(1 - p)\). First-order condition: \(\frac{d \ln L}{dp} = \frac{k}{p} - \frac{T - k}{1 - p} = 0 \implies k(1 - p) = (T - k)p \implies k = T p \implies \hat{p} = k / T\). (b) When \(k = 0\), the likelihood is \((1 - p)^T\). The 95% confidence upper bound satisfies \((1 - p_{0.95})^T = 0.05\). Taking logs: \(T \ln(1 - p_{0.95}) = \ln(0.05) \approx -2.9957 \approx -3\). Using the approximation \(\ln(1 - p) \approx -p\), we obtain \(-T p_{0.95} \approx -3 \implies p_{0.95} \approx 3 / T\). This proves the classical “Rule of Three” for zero-event Poisson/binomial data.

Tier 3 · Empirical & Data

Problem 3.7 · Burnside et al. (2011) Hedged vs Unhedged Carry
Over the 1976–2008 period for G10 currencies, the unhedged carry trade earned an annualized mean excess return of \(5.2\%\) with volatility \(9.1\%\). A crash-hedged carry strategy (protecting against moves greater than 2 standard deviations) earned \(3.8\%\) with volatility \(7.4\%\). Compare their annualized Sharpe ratios and interpret whether crash risk explains the premium.

Worked Solution

The unhedged carry Sharpe ratio is \(\mathrm{SR}_{\mathrm{unhedged}} = 5.2\% / 9.1\% = 0.571\). The crash-hedged carry Sharpe ratio is \(\mathrm{SR}_{\mathrm{hedged}} = 3.8\% / 7.4\% = 0.514\). While hedging tail risk reduces the Sharpe ratio slightly (from 0.571 to 0.514), the hedged Sharpe ratio remains economically large, statistically significant, and comparable to the US equity market. This proves that disaster risk alone cannot account for carry trade profitability.

Problem 3.8 · The Swiss Franc Floor Removal of January 2015
On January 15, 2015, the Swiss National Bank (SNB) unexpectedly discontinued its minimum exchange rate peg of \(1.20\) CHF per EUR. EUR/CHF dropped from \(1.2000\) to an intraday low of \(0.8500\) before settling at \(0.9800\). (a) Compute the intraday percentage loss on a long EUR/CHF position. (b) If a carry trader held 10x leverage on EUR/CHF with 10% equity, describe the solvency outcome.

Worked Solution

(a) The maximum intraday loss was \((0.8500 - 1.2000) / 1.2000 = -0.3500 / 1.2000 = -29.17\%\). At daily close, the loss was \((0.9800 - 1.2000) / 1.2000 = -18.33\%\). (b) With 10x leverage, a 10% equity buffer is wiped out by a 10% price decline. An intraday decline of 29.17% results in an equity loss of \(10 \times 29.17\% = 291.7\%\), completely wiping out investor equity and leaving the trader and broker with a catastrophic deficit of 191.7% of initial equity, triggering bankruptcy.

Problem 3.9 · Forward Bias Puzzle in Sovereign Yield Spreads
In a panel of emerging market currencies against the USD, the average 12-month forward discount is \(6.5\%\). The average realized depreciation in years without sovereign restructuring is \(2.1\%\). In years with sovereign restructuring (which occur in \(4\%\) of country-years), average depreciation is \(55.0\%\). Determine whether the forward discount accurately reflects unconditional depreciation.

Worked Solution

The unconditional expected depreciation is \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta s] = (1 - 0.04)(2.1\%) + (0.04)(55.0\%) = 0.96 \times 2.1\% + 2.2\% = 2.016\% + 2.20\% = 4.216\%\). The average forward discount is \(6.50\%\). The difference is \(6.50\% - 4.216\% = 2.284\%\). Therefore, while the rare restructuring events explain about \(2.2\%\) of the forward spread, an unexplained currency risk premium of \(2.28\%\) remains, confirming that peso problems partially, but not fully, explain the spread.

Tier 4 · Research & Extensions

Problem 3.10 · Consumption CAPM with Rare Disasters (Barro-Rietz Model)
In the Barro (2006) rare disaster model, representative agent consumption drops by fraction \(b \in (0, 1)\) with probability \(p\). With CRRA utility \(u(c) = c^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)\), derive the foreign exchange stochastic discount factor ratio across countries with differential disaster exposures.

Worked Solution

Under CRRA preferences, the domestic pricing kernel is \(M_{t+1} = \beta (C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\gamma}\). In disaster states, \(C_{t+1}/C_t = 1 - b\), so \(M_{\mathrm{disaster}} = \beta (1 - b)^{-\gamma}\). If country \(j\) suffers a disaster with consumption collapse \(b_j > b_{US}\), its SDF satisfies \(M_{j, t+1} / M_{US, t+1} = ((1 - b_j)/(1 - b_{US}))^{-\gamma} \gg 1\). By no-arbitrage currency pricing \(S_{t+1}/S_t = M_{t+1}^* / M_{t+1}\), the foreign currency depreciates precipitously during disasters, requiring a high unconditional carry yield to induce investors to hold foreign assets.

Problem 3.11 · Robust Estimation under Potential Contamination
Suppose an econometrician suspects that sample currency returns contain a mixture of normal observations and unobserved structural breaks. Formulate the Huber \(M\)-estimator for location and compare its asymptotic breakdown point to the sample mean.

Worked Solution

The Huber \(M\)-estimator minimizes \(\sum_{t=1}^T \rho_k((r_t - \mu)/\sigma)\), where \(\rho_k(z) = \frac{1}{2} z^2\) for \(|z| \le k\), and \(k |z| - \frac{1}{2} k^2\) for \(|z| > k\). The sample mean has a breakdown point of \(0\%\), meaning a single rare crash observation can alter the estimated mean arbitrarily. In contrast, the Huber \(M\)-estimator bounds the influence function with a maximum breakdown point of up to \(50\%\), providing robust estimates of the normal regime yield while filtering out isolated structural jumps.

Problem 3.12 · Structural Break Detection in Small Samples
Why do standard Bai-Perron structural break tests suffer low statistical power when testing for rare disaster regimes in foreign exchange panels? How does cross-sectional pooling across currencies improve identification?

Worked Solution

The Bai-Perron test requires sufficient observations before and after a break point to estimate regime-specific variance-covariance matrices. For rare disasters occurring once every 30 to 50 years, the post-break window is too short or nonexistent in single-currency time series, causing test power to collapse. By pooling across a panel of 30+ currencies with common factor structures, econometricians leverage cross-sectional disaster realizations, increasing test power and enabling consistent estimation of rare jump intensities.

8Retrieval Practice

  1. What defines a "peso problem" in empirical finance?

    Options

    A peso problem occurs when sample averages do not reflect true expectations because low-probability, large-magnitude disaster states failed to materialize in the historical sample.

  2. How does a peso problem affect the sample Sharpe ratio in a finite sample where no disasters occur?

    Options

    Without crashes, the sample mean captures only normal returns (\(R_N > \mathbb{E}[R]\)) and the sample variance omits jump variance, heavily inflating the Sharpe ratio.

  3. What empirical innovation did Burnside, Eichenbaum, Kleshchelski, and Rebelo (2011) introduce to test the peso problem?

    Options

    Burnside et al. purchased OTM put options to truncate tail crash losses. If crash risk explained carry profits, option prices would eliminate the net Sharpe ratio.

  4. What was the primary empirical conclusion of Burnside et al. (2011) regarding the hedged carry trade?

    Options

    Even after purchasing put options to eliminate disaster risk, the carry trade retains an annualized Sharpe ratio around 0.51, showing that peso problems alone are insufficient.

  5. In a 1-year horizon, a currency stays at 1.00 with probability \(1-p\) and devalues to 0.60 with probability \(p\). If the forward price is \(0.94\), what is the implied disaster probability \(p\) in percent?

    \(0.94 = 1.00 - 0.40 p \implies p = 0.06 / 0.40 = 0.15 = 15.0\%\).

  6. If a rare currency disaster has an annual probability of \(p = 0.02\), what is the percentage probability of observing zero disasters in a 30-year sample: \(100 \times (1 - 0.02)^{30}\)?

    \(100 \times (0.98)^{30} \approx 54.55\%\).

  7. What historic event in January 2015 demonstrated that developed currency pegs are subject to catastrophic peso problem jumps?

    Options

    On January 15, 2015, the SNB unexpectedly removed the 1.20 peg, causing EUR/CHF to plummet nearly 30% intraday, inflicting massive losses on carry positions.

  8. In a crash-free sample, a carry strategy earns an annualized mean return of \(8.0\%\) with sample volatility of \(10.0\%\). What is its sample Sharpe ratio?

    \(\mathrm{SR} = 8.0\% / 10.0\% = 0.80\).
