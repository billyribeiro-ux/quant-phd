Global FX Volatility Risk

Why do high-interest currencies offer a persistent positive excess return over low-interest currencies? Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling, and Schrimpf (2012) resolve this fundamental puzzle by establishing that currency carry returns are compensation for global FX volatility risk. By aggregating daily intraday exchange rate returns into a proxy for global foreign exchange volatility and extracting innovations via an AR(1) filter, they show that high-rate currencies suffer large negative returns precisely when global FX volatility surges. Low-rate funding currencies, by contrast, act as volatility hedges. This lesson details the construction of global volatility proxies, derives cross-sectional beta pricing, and explores the pricing kernel implications of volatility risk.

1Constructing the Global FX Volatility Proxy

In standard financial theory, risk premia exist because assets perform poorly in bad economic states—states where the marginal utility of consumption is elevated. In international currency markets, periods of surging aggregate volatility represent classic bad states characterized by heightened uncertainty, financial distress, and funding liquidity freezes.

Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf (2012) construct a daily global realized volatility index using a broad cross-section of bilateral exchange rates. For each trading day \(\tau\) in month \(t\), the aggregate FX volatility is computed as the cross-sectional average of absolute daily returns across all available currencies \(j = 1, \dots, N_\tau\): \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\tau}^{\mathrm{FX}} &= \frac{1}{N_\tau} \sum_{j=1}^{N_\tau} |r_{\tau, j}|. \end{aligned}\] The monthly global FX volatility is then the average daily volatility during month \(t\): \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{t}^{\mathrm{FX}} &= \frac{1}{D_t} \sum_{\tau=1}^{D_t} \sigma_{\tau}^{\mathrm{FX}}, \end{aligned}\] where \(D_t\) is the number of trading days in month \(t\).

Definition 5.1 · Global FX Volatility Risk
Global FX volatility risk is the systematic risk stemming from unanticipated innovations in aggregate foreign exchange market volatility, which prices the cross-section of currency returns because high-yielding investment currencies depreciate sharply during volatility spikes while low-yielding funding currencies appreciate as safe-haven hedges.

Because volatility levels \(\sigma_t^{\mathrm{FX}}\) are persistent and largely predictable, asset pricing theory requires isolating the unanticipated component. Menkhoff et al. estimate an autoregressive AR(1) model: \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_t^{\mathrm{FX}} &= \alpha + \rho \sigma_{t-1}^{\mathrm{FX}} + u_t^{\mathrm{vol}}, \end{aligned}\] where \(u_t^{\mathrm{vol}}\) represents the unanticipated innovation in global FX volatility.

2Cross-Sectional Pricing of Volatility Innovations

To determine whether global volatility risk explains carry returns, consider the two-factor model consisting of the Dollar factor (\(DOL_t\)) and the volatility innovation factor (\(\Delta \text{Vol}_t \equiv u_t^{\mathrm{vol}}\)).

In the first-stage time-series regression for each carry portfolio \(p = 1, \dots, K\): \[\begin{aligned} rx_t^p &= \alpha^p + \beta_{DOL}^p DOL_t + \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^p \Delta \text{Vol}_t + \varepsilon_t^p. \end{aligned}\] The empirical factor loadings exhibit a striking pattern:

  • Funding Currencies (Portfolio 1): Have positive volatility betas (\(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^1 > 0\)). When global FX volatility spikes (\(u_t^{\mathrm{vol}} > 0\)), funding currencies appreciate, delivering positive excess returns. They act as natural disaster hedges.
  • Target Currencies (Portfolio 5): Have large negative volatility betas (\(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^5 \ll 0\)). When global FX volatility surges, target currencies suffer severe devaluations. They have intense exposure to volatility shocks.

Theorem 5.1 · Volatility Beta Dispersion & The Negative Price of Risk
In a no-arbitrage economy where the representative investor is risk averse with strictly convex marginal utility of wealth, the market price of global FX volatility risk \(\lambda_{\mathrm{vol}}\) is strictly negative: \[\begin{aligned} \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} &< 0. \end{aligned}\] Consequently, the expected excess return contributed by volatility risk, \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^p \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}}\), is strictly positive for high-yield currencies (\(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^p < 0\)) and strictly negative for funding currencies (\(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^p > 0\)), generating the carry trade risk premium.

Proof. By standard stochastic discount factor theory, for any factor \(f_{t+1}\) with zero mean innovation \(\mathbb{E}_t[f_{t+1}] = 0\), the cross-sectional risk price is given by: \[\begin{aligned} \lambda_f &= -\frac{\Cov(M_{t+1}, f_{t+1})}{\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}]}, \end{aligned}\] where \(M_{t+1}\) is the pricing kernel. In periods of unexpected global FX volatility spikes (\(f_{t+1} = u_{t+1}^{\mathrm{vol}} > 0\)), economic uncertainty escalates and investment opportunities deteriorate. Risk-averse agents value wealth more highly in volatile states, implying that marginal utility of wealth covaries positively with volatility innovations: \(\Cov(M_{t+1}, u_{t+1}^{\mathrm{vol}}) > 0\).

Since \(\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}] > 0\), it follows immediately that: \[\begin{aligned} \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} &= -\frac{\Cov(M_{t+1}, u_{t+1}^{\mathrm{vol}})}{\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}]} < 0. \end{aligned}\] Now consider the cross-sectional expected excess return: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[rx^p] &= \beta_{DOL}^p \lambda_{DOL} + \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^p \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}}. \end{aligned}\] For high-carry target currencies (Portfolio 5), returns are negatively exposed to volatility shocks: \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^5 < 0\). The risk compensation is the product of two negative quantities: \[\begin{aligned} \text{Risk Premium}_5 &= \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^5 \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} = (-) \times (-) > 0. \end{aligned}\] For low-carry funding currencies (Portfolio 1), returns provide positive payoffs during volatility spikes: \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^1 > 0\). The required return is: \[\begin{aligned} \text{Risk Premium}_1 &= \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^1 \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} = (+) \times (-) < 0. \end{aligned}\] Investors accept a lower (even negative) average return on funding currencies because they provide valuable insurance against global volatility shocks. The expected spread is: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[rx^5] - \mathbb{E}[rx^1] &= (\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^5 - \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^1) \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} > 0, \end{aligned}\] which explains the carry premium as fair compensation for bearing volatility risk. End of proof.∎

Lab 1 · Menkhoff et al. (2012) global FX volatility risk pricing

Estimating the Menkhoff et al. two-pass asset pricing model on 5 carry portfolios will demonstrate: (1) positive volatility beta for Portfolio 1 (\(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^1 > 0\)); (2) negative volatility beta for Portfolio 5 (\(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^5 < 0\)); (3) a strictly negative price of volatility risk (\(\lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} < 0\)); and (4) cross-sectional \(R^2\) exceeding 80%.

"""Lab X2-L05-1: Global FX Volatility Innovation Factor & Cross-Sectional Pricing.

Implements the Menkhoff et al. (2012) global foreign exchange volatility proxy,
AR(1) innovation series, portfolio beta estimation, and Fama-MacBeth risk price tests.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from typing import Any


def simulate_volatility_pricing(
    n_months: int = 120,
    seed: int = 777,
) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """Simulate carry portfolios, global FX volatility innovations, and pricing tests.

    Args:
        n_months: Number of months.
        seed: Random seed.

    Returns:
        Dictionary of volatility betas, Fama-MacBeth lambda estimates, and R-squared.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n_portfolios = 5

    # Simulate monthly global FX volatility as AR(1) process
    # Vol_t = c + rho * Vol_{t-1} + u_t
    c_vol = 0.002
    rho_vol = 0.65
    vol_series: list[float] = [0.008]
    innovations: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(n_months):
        u = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.002)
        v_next = c_vol + rho_vol * vol_series[-1] + u
        v_next = max(0.002, v_next)
        vol_series.append(v_next)
        innovations.append(u)

    # Monthly dollar factor
    dol_series = [rng.gauss(0.0, 0.02) for _ in range(n_months)]

    # Simulate 5 carry portfolios:
    # High carry portfolios have negative sensitivity to volatility shocks
    portfolio_returns: list[list[float]] = []
    # Volatility betas: P1 is positive (safe haven), P5 is negative (crash prone)
    true_vol_betas = [0.8, 0.3, -0.1, -0.6, -1.2]

    for t in range(n_months):
        u_t = innovations[t]
        dol_t = dol_series[t]
        month_row: list[float] = []
        for k in range(n_portfolios):
            # Base carry spread: -3% for P1 to +5% for P5
            base_yield = (-0.03 + k * 0.02) / 12.0
            ret = base_yield + 1.0 * dol_t + true_vol_betas[k] * u_t + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.005)
            month_row.append(ret)
        portfolio_returns.append(month_row)

    # Pass 1: Time-series regressions of each portfolio on [1, DOL, VOL_INNOV]
    betas_dol: list[float] = []
    betas_vol: list[float] = []
    mean_excess_returns: list[float] = []

    for k in range(n_portfolios):
        y = [portfolio_returns[t][k] for t in range(n_months)]
        x1 = dol_series
        x2 = innovations

        my = sum(y) / n_months
        m1 = sum(x1) / n_months
        m2 = sum(x2) / n_months

        s11 = sum((x - m1) ** 2 for x in x1)
        s22 = sum((x - m2) ** 2 for x in x2)
        s12 = sum((x1[t] - m1) * (x2[t] - m2) for t in range(n_months))
        sy1 = sum((y[t] - my) * (x1[t] - m1) for t in range(n_months))
        sy2 = sum((y[t] - my) * (x2[t] - m2) for t in range(n_months))

        det = s11 * s22 - s12 * s12
        b1 = (s22 * sy1 - s12 * sy2) / det
        b2 = (s11 * sy2 - s12 * sy1) / det

        betas_dol.append(round(b1, 3))
        betas_vol.append(round(b2, 3))
        mean_excess_returns.append(my)

    # Pass 2: Cross-sectional regression of mean excess returns on betas:
    # bar{R}_k = lambda_DOL * beta_{k, DOL} + lambda_VOL * beta_{k, VOL}
    # Solve 2x2 normal equations: (B'B) lambda = B' bar{R}
    b11 = sum(b1**2 for b1 in betas_dol)
    b22 = sum(b2**2 for b2 in betas_vol)
    b12 = sum(b1 * b2 for b1, b2 in zip(betas_dol, betas_vol, strict=True))

    br1 = sum(b1 * r for b1, r in zip(betas_dol, mean_excess_returns, strict=True))
    br2 = sum(b2 * r for b2, r in zip(betas_vol, mean_excess_returns, strict=True))

    det_b = b11 * b22 - b12 * b12
    lam_dol = (b22 * br1 - b12 * br2) / det_b
    lam_vol = (b11 * br2 - b12 * br1) / det_b

    fitted_means = [lam_dol * b1 + lam_vol * b2 for b1, b2 in zip(betas_dol, betas_vol, strict=True)]
    ss_tot = sum((r - (sum(mean_excess_returns) / n_portfolios)) ** 2 for r in mean_excess_returns)
    ss_res = sum((r - f) ** 2 for r, f in zip(mean_excess_returns, fitted_means, strict=True))
    cs_r2 = 1.0 - (ss_res / ss_tot)

    return {
        "n_months": n_months,
        "betas_dol": betas_dol,
        "betas_vol": betas_vol,
        "mean_excess_returns_ann": [round(r * 12.0 * 100, 2) for r in mean_excess_returns],
        "lambda_dol_ann": round(lam_dol * 12.0 * 100, 2),
        "lambda_vol_monthly": round(lam_vol, 5),
        "cross_sectional_r2": round(cs_r2, 3),
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = simulate_volatility_pricing(n_months=120, seed=777)
    print("=== Global FX Volatility Risk Pricing (Menkhoff et al. 2012) ===")
    print(f"Sample: {res['n_months']} months")
    print("Port | Mean Return | Beta (DOL) | Beta (Vol) ")
    print("---------------------------------------------")
    for k in range(5):
        ret_s = f"{res['mean_excess_returns_ann'][k]:>9.2f}%"
        b_dol = f"{res['betas_dol'][k]:>10.3f}"
        b_vol = f"{res['betas_vol'][k]:>10.3f}"
        print(f"P{k + 1:<4}| {ret_s} | {b_dol} | {b_vol}")
    print("---------------------------------------------")
    print(f"Cross-Sectional R^2: {res['cross_sectional_r2']:.3f}")
    print(f"Price of Volatility Risk lambda_VOL: {res['lambda_vol_monthly']} (negative, compensation for vol risk)")


main()

3Does Volatility Risk Subsume HML-FX?

A central finding of Menkhoff et al. (2012) is that when global FX volatility innovations are included in asset pricing tests alongside the Dollar factor, the ad-hoc portfolio factor \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) is substantially subsumed.

While \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) is a constructed trading strategy (sorting on forward discounts), global FX volatility innovations are a macroeconomic state variable measuring real-time aggregate uncertainty across all currency markets. Showing that \(\Delta \text{Vol}_t\) prices the cross-section of carry portfolios anchors the carry trade within macroeconomic asset pricing: carry returns are not an unpriced microstructural anomaly, but an equilibrium return on a volatility risk factor.

4Where the Canon Breaks

Neoclassical finance often models asset volatility as a nuisance parameter or a stationary second moment. In modern empirical FX pricing, this canon breaks decisively:

  • Volatility as a Priced First-Order Risk: Aggregate FX volatility is not merely a dispersion parameter; its innovations constitute a priced state variable with a market price of risk that directly determines the cross-section of expected returns.
  • Negative Expected Returns on Safe Havens: Neoclassical models predict positive risk premia for holding foreign currencies. In reality, safe-haven funding currencies (JPY, CHF) systematically deliver negative excess returns over long horizons because investors are willing to pay an insurance premium to hedge volatility shocks.
  • FX Volatility vs Equity VIX: While financial commentary frequently equates currency market stress with the equity VIX, Menkhoff et al. show that currency-specific volatility innovations price the FX cross-section significantly better than equity VIX, achieving higher cross-sectional \(R^2\) and smaller pricing errors.

5Misconceptions

  • Volatility risk pricing uses historical volatility levels rather than unanticipated innovations.

    Because volatility is highly persistent (with monthly AR(1) coefficients often exceeding 0.70), levels of volatility are predictable and do not represent unexpected shocks. Rational asset pricing requires isolating unanticipated innovations \(u_t^{\mathrm{vol}} = \sigma_t^{\mathrm{FX}} - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\sigma_t^{\mathrm{FX}}]\) to measure systematic risk.

  • A negative market price of volatility risk (\(\lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} < 0\)) implies that investors do not care about volatility.

    In asset pricing, a negative price of risk means that assets positively correlated with the factor provide hedging benefits. Because investors dislike volatility, assets that pay off during volatility spikes (safe havens) have positive betas and require lower expected returns, while assets that lose value during volatility spikes (carry targets) have negative betas and demand positive premia.

  • Global FX volatility risk pricing requires high-frequency tick data that is unavailable to academic researchers.

    Menkhoff et al. (2012) prove that simple daily absolute returns \(|r_{\tau, j}|\) averaged across a standard panel of 30 to 40 currencies provide an exceptionally robust proxy for global FX volatility that closely matches intraday high-frequency measures.

6Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 5.1 · AR(1) Volatility Innovation Extraction
A monthly global FX volatility series follows the AR(1) model: \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_t = 0.0020 + 0.70 \sigma_{t-1} + u_t. \end{aligned}\] In month \(t-1\), realized volatility was \(\sigma_{t-1} = 0.0080\). In month \(t\), realized volatility surges to \(\sigma_t = 0.0125\). Calculate: (a) The expected volatility \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\sigma_t]\); (b) The unanticipated volatility innovation \(u_t\).

Worked Solution

(a) Expected volatility is \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\sigma_t] = 0.0020 + 0.70(0.0080) = 0.0020 + 0.0056 = 0.0076\) (\(0.76\%\)). (b) The unanticipated volatility innovation is the residual: \(u_t = \sigma_t - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\sigma_t] = 0.0125 - 0.0076 = +0.0049\) (\(+49\) basis points).

Problem 5.2 · Volatility Risk Premium Calculation
The estimated market price of global FX volatility risk is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} = -0.0030\) per month. Portfolio 1 (funding currencies) has \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^1 = +0.75\), and Portfolio 5 (target currencies) has \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^5 = -1.50\). Calculate the monthly risk premium contributed by volatility risk for both portfolios and compute the annualized carry spread.

Worked Solution

For Portfolio 1: \(\text{RP}_1 = \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^1 \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} = (+0.75) \times (-0.0030) = -0.00225\) (\(-0.225\%\) monthly, or \(-2.70\%\) annualized). For Portfolio 5: \(\text{RP}_5 = \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^5 \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} = (-1.50) \times (-0.0030) = +0.00450\) (\(+0.450\%\) monthly, or \(+5.40\%\) annualized). The annualized spread is \(\text{RP}_5 - \text{RP}_1 = 5.40\% - (-2.70\%) = +8.10\%\) per annum.

Problem 5.3 · Daily Realized Volatility Proxy Calculation
On a given day, four exchange rate absolute returns against the US dollar are: EUR: \(0.40\%\), JPY: \(0.60\%\), GBP: \(0.50\%\), AUD: \(0.90\%\). Compute the aggregate daily global FX volatility proxy \(\sigma_\tau^{\mathrm{FX}}\).

Worked Solution

The daily proxy is the equal-weighted average of absolute returns: \(\sigma_\tau^{\mathrm{FX}} = \frac{1}{4}(0.40\% + 0.60\% + 0.50\% + 0.90\%) = \frac{2.40\%}{4} = 0.60\%\).

Tier 2 · Proofs & Derivations

Problem 5.4 · Negative Risk Price from Marginal Utility of Wealth
Let the stochastic discount factor be \(M = e^{-\rho - \gamma \Delta c}\), where \(\Delta c\) is log consumption growth. Suppose consumption growth is negatively affected by global volatility shocks: \(\Delta c = \mu_c - \delta u^{\mathrm{vol}} + \varepsilon_c\), with \(\delta > 0\). Prove that the price of volatility risk \(\lambda_{\mathrm{vol}}\) is strictly negative.

Worked Solution

Taking a first-order Taylor expansion of \(M\) around mean consumption: \(M \approx \mathbb{E}[M] - \gamma \mathbb{E}[M] (\Delta c - \mu_c)\). Substituting \(\Delta c - \mu_c = -\delta u^{\mathrm{vol}} + \varepsilon_c\): \(M \approx \mathbb{E}[M] + \gamma \delta \mathbb{E}[M] u^{\mathrm{vol}} - \gamma \mathbb{E}[M] \varepsilon_c\). The covariance between \(M\) and \(u^{\mathrm{vol}}\) is \(\Cov(M, u^{\mathrm{vol}}) = \gamma \delta \mathbb{E}[M] \Var(u^{\mathrm{vol}}) > 0\), since \(\gamma, \delta > 0\). The price of risk is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} = -\frac{\Cov(M, u^{\mathrm{vol}})}{\mathbb{E}[M]} = -\gamma \delta \Var(u^{\mathrm{vol}}) < 0\), proving the result.

Problem 5.5 · Equivalence between Two-Pass OLS and Cross-Sectional Projection
Prove that when the Dollar factor has identical loadings \(\beta_{DOL}^p = 1.0\) for all \(p\), a cross-sectional regression without an intercept of portfolio returns on \((\beta_{DOL}, \beta_{\mathrm{vol}})\) yields an estimate \(\hat{\lambda}_{DOL} = \bar{DOL}\) and \(\hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{vol}} = \frac{\Cov_c(\bar{\mathbf{r}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}})}{\Var_c(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}})}\), where \(\Cov_c\) denotes the cross-sectional covariance.

Worked Solution

Let \(\mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{1}, \boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}}]\). The OLS coefficient vector is \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}} = (\mathbf{X}' \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}' \bar{\mathbf{r}}\). Because \(\mathbf{1}' \boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}} = K \bar{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}}\), by the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, projecting out the constant vector \(\mathbf{1}\) from both sides transforms \(\bar{\mathbf{r}}\) into cross-sectional deviations \(\bar{\mathbf{r}} - \bar{r}\mathbf{1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}}\) into \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}} - \bar{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}}\mathbf{1}\). Regressing centered returns on centered betas yields \(\hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{vol}} = \frac{\sum (\bar{r}_p - \bar{r})(\beta_p - \bar{\beta})}{\sum (\beta_p - \bar{\beta})^2} = \frac{\Cov_c(\bar{\mathbf{r}}, \boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}})}{\Var_c(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}})}\). The intercept loading \(\hat{\lambda}_{DOL}\) then absorbs the grand mean \(\bar{r} - \hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{vol}} \bar{\beta}_{\mathrm{vol}} = \bar{DOL}\).

Problem 5.6 · Asymptotic Variance of AR(1) Volatility Innovations
For a stationary AR(1) process \(\sigma_t = \alpha + \rho \sigma_{t-1} + u_t\) with \(| \rho | < 1\) and \(u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2)\), prove that the variance of the innovation is \(\sigma_u^2 = \sigma^2 (1 - \rho^2)\), where \(\sigma^2\) is the unconditional variance of volatility levels.

Worked Solution

Taking the variance of both sides of \(\sigma_t = \alpha + \rho \sigma_{t-1} + u_t\): \(\Var(\sigma_t) = \Var(\rho \sigma_{t-1} + u_t) = \rho^2 \Var(\sigma_{t-1}) + \Var(u_t) + 2\rho \Cov(\sigma_{t-1}, u_t)\). Since \(u_t\) is independent of past observations \(\sigma_{t-1}\), \(\Cov(\sigma_{t-1}, u_t) = 0\). In stationarity, \(\Var(\sigma_t) = \Var(\sigma_{t-1}) = \sigma^2\). Thus \(\sigma^2 = \rho^2 \sigma^2 + \sigma_u^2 \implies \sigma_u^2 = \sigma^2 (1 - \rho^2)\).

Tier 3 · Empirical & Data

Problem 5.7 · Safe Haven Currencies during Volatility Spikes
Using data from Menkhoff et al. (2012), the Swiss franc (CHF) and Japanese yen (JPY) have average volatility betas of \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{CHF}} = +0.82\) and \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{JPY}} = +0.94\). The South African rand (ZAR) has \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{ZAR}} = -1.85\). Describe the performance of a carry trade funded in JPY and invested in ZAR when global FX volatility surges by 3 standard deviations.

Worked Solution

The spread beta of the ZAR/JPY carry trade is \(\beta_{\mathrm{spread}} = \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{ZAR}} - \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^{\mathrm{JPY}} = -1.85 - (+0.94) = -2.79\). If a 3-standard-deviation volatility shock hits (\(u^{\mathrm{vol}} = +3 \sigma_u\)), the predicted return of the position is \(-2.79 \times 3 \sigma_u \approx -8.37 \sigma_u\), representing a catastrophic simultaneous collapse of the investment currency and surge in the funding currency.

Problem 5.8 · Menkhoff et al. Cross-Sectional Fit Comparison
In a comparison of cross-sectional asset pricing models across 5 currency portfolios, the CAPM (using the Dollar factor) yields \(R^2 = 12\%\). The Lustig et al. model (\(DOL + HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\)) yields \(R^2 = 91\%\). The Menkhoff et al. model (\(DOL + \Delta \text{Vol}\)) yields \(R^2 = 92\%\). Explain what this demonstrates regarding the economic nature of \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\).

Worked Solution

The near-identical cross-sectional explanatory power (\(91\%\) vs \(92\%\)) demonstrates that the empirical carry factor \(HML_{\mathrm{FX}}\) is fundamentally a proxy for global foreign exchange volatility risk. High-interest currencies earn high expected returns not because of currency-specific anomalies, but because their cash flows are vulnerable to aggregate volatility spikes.

Problem 5.9 · Realized Volatility vs Equity VIX Regressions
Regressing monthly global FX volatility innovations \(\Delta \sigma_t^{\mathrm{FX}}\) on monthly equity VIX changes \(\Delta \text{VIX}_t\) yields a slope coefficient of \(0.45\) and an \(R^2\) of \(38\%\). What does the \(62\%\) of unexplained variance imply about currency-specific risk?

Worked Solution

The \(62\%\) unexplained residual variance proves that foreign exchange markets experience unique, currency-specific liquidity and volatility shocks that are not captured by equity market implied volatility. Intermediary balance sheet constraints in foreign exchange dealing desks create currency volatility dynamics distinct from US stock market sentiment.

Tier 4 · Research & Extensions

Problem 5.10 · Intermediary Capital Ratio Pricing Kernel
Following He, Kelly & Manconi (2017), the pricing kernel is governed by primary dealer capital ratios: \(M_{t+1} = 1 - b \Delta \eta_{t+1}\), where \(\eta_t\) is dealer equity capital divided by assets. Explain how dealer capital erosion mechanically produces spikes in global FX volatility innovations.

Worked Solution

Primary dealers serve as marginal market makers in global FX swap and forward markets. When negative shocks deplete dealer equity capital, regulatory leverage constraints (e.g. Basel III leverage ratios) bind. Dealers widen bid-ask spreads and pull liquidity from electronic order books. In thinner markets, order flow shocks generate amplified price impacts, mechanically causing aggregate realized foreign exchange volatility to surge.

Problem 5.11 · Higher-Order Volatility Moments: Realized Skewness Factors
Beyond volatility innovations, consider an aggregate realized skewness factor \(SKEW_t^{\mathrm{FX}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum r_{\tau, j}^3 / \sigma_\tau^3\). Formulate a three-factor pricing model \((DOL, \text{Vol}, SKEW)\) and hypothesize the expected sign of the price of skewness risk \(\lambda_{skew}\).

Worked Solution

The pricing equation is \(\mathbb{E}[rx^p] = \beta_{DOL}^p \lambda_{DOL} + \beta_{\mathrm{vol}}^p \lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} + \beta_{skew}^p \lambda_{skew}\). Investors exhibit skewness preference (liking positive asymmetry and disliking negative crash risk), meaning marginal utility is high when aggregate skewness becomes intensely negative. Consequently, the price of skewness risk is positive (\(\lambda_{skew} > 0\)): assets that load positively on skewness hedge tail risk, requiring lower expected returns.

Problem 5.12 · Volatility Timing Strategies in FX Carry Portfolios
Design an algorithmic volatility-timing overlay that scales carry portfolio leverage as \(w_t = \min\left(L_{\max}, \frac{\sigma^*}{\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{\mathrm{FX}}}\right)\). Evaluate whether this rule reduces drawdown severity without degrading the unconditional Sharpe ratio.

Worked Solution

Volatility targeting scales position size inversely with conditional volatility \(\hat{\sigma}_t^{\mathrm{FX}}\). Because volatility is clustered and persistent, spikes in volatility reliably predict elevated crash probability. Deleveraging during high-volatility regimes cuts off left-tail drawdowns while maintaining maximum leverage during tranquil regimes, reducing portfolio kurtosis and expanding the realized Sharpe ratio by 15%–25%.

8Retrieval Practice

  1. How do Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling, and Schrimpf (2012) construct their global FX volatility proxy?

    Options

    Menkhoff et al. average absolute daily returns across currencies to construct monthly realized volatility, then filter via AR(1) to isolate innovations.

  2. What is the sign of the market price of global FX volatility risk (\(\lambda_{\mathrm{vol}}\))?

    Options

    Because investors dislike volatility and marginal utility rises when volatility spikes, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{vol}}\) is negative.

  3. Why do high-interest target currencies earn a positive risk premium in the Menkhoff et al. framework?

    Options

    High-rate currencies depreciate during volatility spikes (negative beta), yielding a positive risk compensation when multiplied by the negative price of risk.

  4. What characterizes funding currencies (such as JPY and CHF) during global FX volatility spikes?

    Options

    Funding currencies appreciate when volatility shocks hit, providing valuable portfolio insurance (safe havens) and leading investors to accept lower expected returns.

  5. An AR(1) volatility model has equation \(\sigma_t = 0.0020 + 0.70 \sigma_{t-1}\). If \(\sigma_{t-1} = 0.0080\), what is the expected volatility \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\sigma_t]\) in percent?

    \(100 \times [0.0020 + 0.70(0.0080)] = 100 \times [0.0020 + 0.0056] = 0.76\%\).

  6. If the monthly volatility price of risk is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{vol}} = -0.0030\), Portfolio 1 has \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}} = +0.75\), and Portfolio 5 has \(\beta_{\mathrm{vol}} = -1.50\), what is the annualized carry spread in percent: \(12 \times [(-1.50 - 0.75) \times (-0.0030)] \times 100\)?

    \(12 \times [-2.25 \times (-0.0030)] \times 100 = 12 \times 0.00675 \times 100 = 8.10\%\).

  7. What happens to the explanatory power of HML-FX when global FX volatility innovations are included in asset pricing regressions?

    Options

    Menkhoff et al. (2012) show that volatility risk innovations account for the cross-sectional return spread, subsuming the ad-hoc HML-FX portfolio factor.

  8. On a single day, four currency absolute returns against USD are \(0.40\%\), \(0.60\%\), \(0.50\%\), and \(0.90\%\). What is the daily global FX volatility proxy in percent?

    \(\sigma = \frac{0.40 + 0.60 + 0.50 + 0.90}{4}\% = 0.60\%\).
