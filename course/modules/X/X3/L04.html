Roll Yield, Term Structure Decompositions, and Strategic Returns

An investment in a commodity futures index differs fundamentally from a buy-and-hold equity investment. Because futures contracts have finite lifespans, long exposure must be maintained by continuously rolling expiring front-month contracts into distant tenors. Building on backwardation and contango, this lesson formalizes the 3-way return decomposition into spot price change, roll yield, and collateral yield. We analyze the empirical findings of Erb & Harvey (2006) and Gorton & Rouwenhorst (2006), and dissect the structural roll decay that decimates retail commodity exchange-traded products during sustained contango.

Retail and institutional investors frequently assume that buying a commodity ETF tracks the spot price of the underlying physical commodity. Over multi-year horizons, this assumption is disastrously wrong. A commodity whose spot price rises by 50% over a decade can deliver a -80% cumulative total return to a front-month futures strategy if the forward curve spends that decade in contango. The roll yield is not a secondary transaction cost; it is the single most powerful driver of long-term commodity performance, explaining over 80% of the cross-sectional return variance across commodity sectors. Mastering roll mechanics is essential for constructing profitable multi-asset carry strategies and avoiding structural wealth destruction.

1. The Tripartite Anatomy of Collateralized Futures Returns

Consider a fully collateralized futures investment in commodity \(i\). To avoid leverage and default risk, an investor who purchases a futures contract with notional value \(F_t\) deposits \(F_t\) in cash collateral earning the short-term risk-free rate \(r_{f, t}\) (U.S. Treasury bills).

Definition 4.1 · Return Decomposition The continuous total return \(R_{\text{total}, t+1}\) of a fully collateralized commodity futures investment over period \([t, t+1]\) decomposes into three distinct components:

\[R_{\text{total}, t+1} = R_{\text{spot}, t+1} + R_{\text{roll}, t+1} + R_{\text{collateral}, t+1}\]
  1. Spot Return (\(R_{\text{spot}}\)): The percentage change in the unobservable or immediate spot price of the physical commodity: \(R_{\text{spot}, t+1} = \frac{S_{t+1} - S_t}{S_t}\).
  2. Roll Yield (\(R_{\text{roll}}\)): The return generated by the convergence of the futures price toward the spot price as maturity approaches, or the basis spread earned when replacing an expiring contract with a deferred contract: \(\text{Roll Yield} = \frac{F(t, T_1) - F(t, T_2)}{F(t, T_1)}\).
  3. Collateral Yield (\(R_{\text{collateral}}\)): The risk-free interest earned on the 100% cash collateral backing the futures position: \(R_{\text{collateral}, t+1} = r_{f, t}\).

Definition 4.2 · Roll Yield The Roll yield is the portion of a commodity futures return attributable to the slope and shape of the futures term structure. For a continuous forward curve \(F(t, T)\), the instantaneous roll yield equals the negative slope of the log forward curve with respect to maturity:

\[\text{Roll Yield}(t) = -\frac{\partial \ln F(t, T)}{\partial T} = \delta(t) - r(t) - c(t)\]

In backwardation (\(\delta > r + c\)), the roll yield is strictly positive. In contango (\(r + c > \delta\)), the roll yield is strictly negative.

2. Roll Mechanics: Contango Bleed vs. Backwardation Tailwinds

How does the roll yield materialize in practical portfolio execution? Commodity indices (such as the S&P GSCI or Bloomberg Commodity Index BCOM) follow mechanical rolling rules. For example, during the 5th through 9th business days of every month, the index rolls 20% of its position each day from the front-month contract \(T_1\) to the second-month contract \(T_2\).

Example 4.1 · Rolling in Contango vs. Backwardation

  1. Contango (Selling Low, Buying High): Suppose crude oil is in contango. The front-month contract expires at \(F_1 = \$70.00\), while the second-month contract trades at \(F_2 = \$72.00\). The fund sells \(F_1\) for \$70.00 and buys \(F_2\) for \$72.00. The fund now holds \(70/72 = 0.9722\) contracts per initial contract. Over the subsequent month, if the spot price remains unchanged at \$70.00, \(F_2\) converges downward from \$72.00 to \$70.00. The fund experiences a loss of \(\$70.00 - \$72.00 = -\$2.00\) per contract (-2.78% roll decay), even though the physical spot price did not move.
  2. Backwardation (Selling High, Buying Low): Suppose crude oil is in backwardation: \(F_1 = \$80.00\) and \(F_2 = \$76.00\). The fund sells \(F_1\) for \$80.00 and buys \(F_2\) for \$76.00, purchasing \(80/76 = 1.0526\) contracts. As \(F_2\) converges upward toward the spot price of \$80.00 over the month, the fund gains +\$4.00 per contract (+5.26% roll return) with zero change in spot prices.

Theorem 4.1 · Continuous Roll Decay Identity Under a stationary forward curve \(F(t, T) = S_t e^{\psi (T - t)}\) where spot price \(S_t\) follows geometric Brownian motion with drift \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\), the cumulative continuous return \(V_t\) of a fully collateralized rolling futures strategy satisfies:

\[\frac{dV_t}{V_t} = (\mu - \psi + r_f) dt + \sigma dW_t\]

where \(-\psi = \delta - r - c\) is the instantaneous roll yield. Over horizon \(T\), the total wealth multiple is:

\[\frac{V_T}{V_0} = \left(\frac{S_T}{S_0}\right) \exp\left( (\delta - c) T \right)\]

Proof At each instant \(t\), the investor holds a front contract maturing at \(t + \Delta t\). By the theory of storage, \(\ln F(t, t + \Delta t) = \ln S_t + \psi \Delta t\). Over time increment \(dt\), the contract's time-to-maturity declines by \(dt\), generating price change \(d \ln F = d \ln S_t - \psi dt\). By Ito's Lemma, \(\frac{dF}{F} = \frac{dS}{S} - \psi dt\). Adding the risk-free collateral return \(r_f dt\) on 100% margin yields \(\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{dS}{S} - \psi dt + r_f dt\). Because \(\psi = r_f + c - \delta\), the net drift is \(\frac{dS}{S} - (r_f + c - \delta) dt + r_f dt = \frac{dS}{S} + (\delta - c) dt\). Integrating from 0 to \(T\) yields \(\ln(V_T / V_0) = \ln(S_T / S_0) + (\delta - c) T\), which exponentiates to the stated wealth multiple. \(\blacksquare\)

3. Empirical Evidence: Erb & Harvey (2006) and Gorton & Rouwenhorst (2006)

In two landmark empirical papers published in the Financial Analysts Journal in 2006, researchers conducted the first exhaustive multi-decade studies of commodity futures returns:

  1. Erb & Harvey (2006) "The Strategic and Tactical Value of Commodity Futures": Investigating 33 years of data (1969–2003), Erb & Harvey (2006) demonstrated that:
    • The historical average annualized excess return of individual commodity futures was close to zero (mean excess return across all commodities was -0.4% p.a.).
    • Individual commodity returns are largely uncorrelated with one another (average pairwise correlation ~0.10).
    • Roll yield dominates returns: Cross-sectional variation in roll yield explained 82% of the cross-sectional variation in commodity futures returns (Table 2). Backwardated commodities delivered substantial positive excess returns, while contango commodities experienced severe losses.
  2. Gorton & Rouwenhorst (2006) "Facts and Fantasies about Commodity Futures": Analyzing a fully collateralized, equal-weighted commodity index from 1959 to 2004, Gorton & Rouwenhorst (2006) proved that:
    • An equal-weighted commodity futures index historically generated an annualized equity-like return (+10.5% vs +11.2% for the S&P 500) with lower annualized volatility (12.1% vs 14.8%).
    • Commodity futures returns were negatively correlated with equity and bond returns over 1-year to 5-year horizons.
    • Commodity returns correlated positively with unexpected inflation (+0.45 correlation), serving as an effective inflation hedge.

Lab 1 · Commodity Futures Return Decomposition

Simulating a 10-year (120-month) investment in two commodities with identical spot drift (+2% p.a.) but contrasting curve shapes (+6% backwardation vs -8% contango) will show that roll yield drives over 80% of wealth divergence, resulting in a 4x terminal wealth ratio.

"""Lab X3-L04-1: Commodity Futures Return Decomposition: Spot, Roll, and Collateral Yield.

Replicates the Erb & Harvey (2006) and Gorton & Rouwenhorst (2006) return decomposition,
demonstrating that roll yield drives the long-term divergence between backwardation and contango.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def simulate_futures_decomposition(
    n_months: int = 120,
    annual_spot_drift: float = 0.02,
    annual_roll_yield: float = 0.06,
    annual_collateral_rate: float = 0.03,
    volatility: float = 0.18,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate a 10-year monthly fully collateralized commodity futures investment."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    dt = 1.0 / 12.0
    monthly_roll = annual_roll_yield * dt
    monthly_collateral = annual_collateral_rate * dt
    monthly_spot_drift = annual_spot_drift * dt
    monthly_vol = volatility * math.sqrt(dt)

    spot_wealth = 100.0
    total_wealth = 100.0

    cumulative_spot_ret = 0.0
    cumulative_roll_ret = 0.0
    cumulative_collateral_ret = 0.0

    for _ in range(n_months):
        r_spot = monthly_spot_drift + monthly_vol * rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        r_roll = monthly_roll
        r_collateral = monthly_collateral

        r_total = r_spot + r_roll + r_collateral

        spot_wealth *= 1.0 + r_spot
        total_wealth *= 1.0 + r_total

        cumulative_spot_ret += r_spot
        cumulative_roll_ret += r_roll
        cumulative_collateral_ret += r_collateral

    annualized_total_return = (total_wealth / 100.0) ** (12.0 / n_months) - 1.0
    annualized_spot_return = (spot_wealth / 100.0) ** (12.0 / n_months) - 1.0

    return {
        "final_wealth": round(total_wealth, 2),
        "spot_wealth": round(spot_wealth, 2),
        "ann_total_return": round(annualized_total_return * 100.0, 2),
        "ann_spot_return": round(annualized_spot_return * 100.0, 2),
        "cum_spot_ret": round(cumulative_spot_ret * 100.0, 2),
        "cum_roll_ret": round(cumulative_roll_ret * 100.0, 2),
        "cum_collateral_ret": round(cumulative_collateral_ret * 100.0, 2),
    }

Where the Canon Breaks

Classical index methodology assumes that rolling front-month contracts represents a friction-free strategy. In practice, this mechanical protocol breaks down:

  1. The "Roll Trap" and Pre-Roll Front-Running: Because major commodity indices follow public, fixed roll schedules (e.g. 5th to 9th business day of the month), proprietary high-frequency traders and quantitative hedge funds front-run index rebalancing. Predatory traders short front contracts and buy second-month contracts days before the scheduled index roll, widening contango and forcing passive index investors to buy at the widest possible spread.
  2. Capacity constraints and contract position limits: Under CFTC position limit rules, a single fund or ETF cannot hold more than a specified percentage of open interest in prompt contracts (e.g. 25% of deliverable supply). In April 2020, the United States Oil Fund (USO) grew so large that it held over 25% of the front-month WTI contract. Regulatory caps forced USO to abandon its front-month mandate and distribute holdings across contracts expiring 12 months forward, altering the fund's risk profile during the crisis.
  3. Negative roll convexity: During super-contango episodes, the front spread widens non-linearly. If contango deepens from -2% per month to -15% per month, the compounding roll decay accelerates exponentially, completely wiping out investor capital in a matter of weeks.

Misconceptions

  • When a fund rolls a futures contract in contango, it incurs an immediate cash loss on the day of the roll transaction.

    The roll transaction itself is an exchange of equal mark-to-market values: selling $70 worth of front contracts and purchasing $70 worth of second-month contracts involves zero cash loss. The loss occurs over the subsequent month as the purchased higher-priced contract decays downward toward spot as expiration nears.

  • Buying a commodity ETF provides exposure to the spot price of physical commodities.

    Commodity ETFs hold derivative futures contracts, not physical inventory in warehouses. The investor's return equals spot return plus roll yield plus collateral yield. In contango markets (such as natural gas or crude oil post-2014), negative roll yield can cause the ETF to decline by over 80% while spot prices rise.

  • Passive long-only commodity futures investment provides an automatic equity risk premium in all market regimes.

    Erb & Harvey (2006) showed that unhedged, passive buy-and-hold commodity futures had an average excess return near zero. Generating consistent positive risk premia requires dynamic cross-sectional factor sorting on roll yield (carry) or momentum.

Problem set

Mechanics Problems

An investor allocates \$1,000,000 to a fully collateralized 1-year crude oil futures strategy. Over the year, the spot price of crude oil increases by +10.0%. The average roll yield over the year is -8.0% (due to contango), and the 1-year Treasury bill collateral rate is +4.5%. Compute the total dollar return and the percentage return on the collateralized portfolio.

Solution

Under the return decomposition formula:

\[R_{\text{total}} \approx R_{\text{spot}} + R_{\text{roll}} + R_{\text{collateral}} = 0.10 - 0.08 + 0.045 = 0.065 \quad (6.50\%)\]

Under continuous compounding (\(R_{\text{total}} = (1 + R_{\text{spot}}) e^{(R_{\text{roll}} + R_{\text{collateral}})} - 1\)):

\[1.10 \times e^{-0.08 + 0.045} = 1.10 \times e^{-0.035} \approx 1.10 \times 0.965605 = 1.06216 \quad (+6.22\%)\]

Total dollar profit: \(\$1,000,000 \times 0.0622 = \$62,165\). Despite a 10% spot gain, contango consumed 8% of return.

A natural gas fund rolls its contract from March to April. The March contract is sold at \(F_{\text{Mar}} = \$2.40\) per MMBtu, and the April contract is purchased at \(F_{\text{Apr}} = \$2.70\) per MMBtu. Compute the 1-month roll yield. If the fund started with 1,000,000 MMBtu of March contracts, how many MMBtu of April contracts does it acquire?

Solution

The roll yield is:

\[\text{Roll Yield} = \frac{F_{\text{Mar}} - F_{\text{Apr}}}{F_{\text{Mar}}} = \frac{2.40 - 2.70}{2.40} = \frac{-0.30}{2.40} = -0.1250 \quad (-12.50\%)\]

The total proceeds from selling March contracts are \(1,000,000 \times \$2.40 = \$2,400,000\). The fund purchases April contracts at \$2.70:

\[\text{April Contracts} = \frac{\$2,400,000}{\$2.70} \approx 888,889 \text{ MMBtu}\]

The fund's physical contract volume shrinks by 11.11% in a single monthly roll.

In a cross-section of 10 commodity futures, 5 contracts are in backwardation with an average roll yield of +5.0% p.a., and 5 contracts are in contango with an average roll yield of -6.0% p.a. A long-short carry factor strategy goes long the top 5 and short the bottom 5. What is the expected annual roll yield of the dollar-neutral carry portfolio?

Solution

The long leg captures the backwardated roll yield: \(+5.0\%\). The short leg is short contango contracts, earning the negative of the negative roll yield: \(-(-6.0\%) = +6.0\%\). The net expected roll yield of the spread portfolio is:

\[\text{Net Roll Spread} = 5.0\% - (-6.0\%) = 5.0\% + 6.0\% = +11.0\% \text{ p.a.}\]

The strategy harvests an 11.0% annual carry premium before financing and trading costs.

Proof Problems

Prove that for an unhedged long futures roll strategy, the cumulative return over \(N\) rolling periods of length \(\Delta t\) equals the product of periodic price ratios: \(\frac{V_N}{V_0} = \prod_{k=1}^N \left( \frac{F(t_k, T_k)}{F(t_{k-1}, T_k)} \right)\). Show how this decouples from \(\frac{S_N}{S_0}\).

Solution

At time \(t_{k-1}\), the investor purchases contract maturing at \(T_k\) at price \(F(t_{k-1}, T_k)\). At time \(t_k\), the investor liquidates at price \(F(t_k, T_k)\). The periodic return is \(1 + R_k = \frac{F(t_k, T_k)}{F(t_{k-1}, T_k)}\). Compounding across \(N\) periods:

\[\frac{V_N}{V_0} = \prod_{k=1}^N \frac{F(t_k, T_k)}{F(t_{k-1}, T_k)} = \left( \prod_{k=1}^N \frac{S_{t_k}}{F(t_{k-1}, T_k)} \right) = \left(\frac{S_N}{S_0}\right) \prod_{k=1}^N \frac{S_{t_{k-1}}}{F(t_{k-1}, T_k)}\]

where the last equality assumes contract convergence \(F(t_k, T_k) = S_{t_k}\). The term \(\prod_{k=1}^N \frac{S_{t_{k-1}}}{F(t_{k-1}, T_k)} = \exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^N \psi_k \Delta t\right)\) is the cumulative roll return factor. If \(\psi_k > 0\) (contango), this term decays exponentially to zero as \(N \to \infty\), decoupling \(V_N\) completely from spot price \(S_N\).

Prove that if an investor replaces a 1-month roll protocol with a 12-month roll protocol (holding the 1-year contract and rolling annually), the annualized roll decay is lower under a concave forward curve than under a linear forward curve.

Solution

Let the forward curve be \(F(T)\). For a concave forward curve in contango, \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T} > 0\) and \(\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T^2} < 0\). The prompt slope \(\left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right|_{T=0}\) is strictly greater than the average slope over 1 year: \(\frac{F(1) - F(0)}{1} < \left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right|_{T=0}\). A 1-month rolling strategy repeatedly rolls at the steepest part of the curve near \(T=0\), incurring continuous roll drag \(\left.\frac{\partial \ln F}{\partial T}\right|_{T=0}\). A 12-month rolling strategy locks in the average slope \(\ln F(1) - \ln F(0)\), which by concavity is strictly smaller. Hence, rolling longer-dated contracts reduces roll decay in concave contango markets.

Prove that an equal-weighted portfolio of \(M\) uncorrelated commodity futures contracts with identical variance \(\sigma^2\) and zero individual expected excess return achieves a strictly positive geometric growth rate due to diversification rebalancing return (volatility pumping).

Solution

Let each asset have log-normal price dynamics: \(d \ln S_i = -\frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 dt + \sigma dW_i\), so arithmetic return is \(\E[dS_i/S_i] = 0\), but geometric drift is \(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\). For an equal-weighted rebalanced portfolio \(V = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M S_i\), portfolio return is \(\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \frac{dS_i}{S_i} = \frac{\sigma}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M dW_i\). The portfolio variance is \(\sigma_p^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{M}\). The geometric growth rate of the rebalanced portfolio is:

\[g_p = \E\left[\frac{dV}{V}\right] - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2 = 0 - \frac{\sigma^2}{2M}\]

Compared to holding a single asset with growth rate \(g_i = -\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\), the rebalanced portfolio captures a net diversification return of:

\[\Delta g = g_p - g_i = \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \left(1 - \frac{1}{M}\right) > 0\]

For large \(M\), this rebalancing dividend approaches \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 > 0\) (Erb & Harvey 2006).

Empirical Problems

The United States Natural Gas Fund (UNG) tracks front-month Henry Hub natural gas futures. Between 2007 and 2020, spot natural gas prices fell by approximately -70% due to the shale gas fracking revolution. Over the same period, UNG shares declined by over -99.8%. Quantify the role of cumulative negative roll yield in this massive underperformance.

Solution

Natural gas spent the vast majority of 2007–2020 in persistent contango, with monthly roll decay averaging -1.5% to -2.5% per month (-18% to -26% annualized). Over 13 years (156 months), a -2.0% monthly roll drag compounds to \((1 - 0.02)^{156} \approx 0.0425\) (a -95.7% loss from roll yield alone). Multiplying the spot decline of 0.30 by the roll factor 0.0425 yields a terminal wealth of \(0.30 \times 0.0425 \approx 0.0127\) (-98.7% total decline). The compounding roll decay converted a painful spot decline into near-total capital wipeout.

In an empirical test of the S&P GSCI (which is ~70% energy and historically weighted toward backwardated prompt crude) versus the Bloomberg Commodity Index BCOM (which caps energy at 33% and holds higher agriculture/metals weights), what explains the dramatic outperformance of GSCI during the 1990s and its underperformance post-2008?

Solution

During the 1990s and early 2000s, global crude oil production capacity was tight, keeping WTI and Brent in persistent backwardation. The heavily energy-weighted S&P GSCI captured compounding positive roll yields (+5% to +10% p.a.). Post-2008, the US shale revolution flooded crude markets with supply, while natural gas entered perpetual contango. As energy forward curves shifted from backwardation to contango, GSCI suffered severe negative roll drag, allowing the more diversified BCOM index to outperform.

Examine the rolling protocol of the "Dynamic Roll" commodity index variants (e.g. S&P GSCI Dynamic Roll). How do these enhanced index rules choose contract tenors to minimize contango drag and maximize backwardation yield?

Solution

Instead of mechanically rolling into the next prompt contract, dynamic roll indices evaluate the entire forward curve out to 24 months. In backwardation, the index rolls into prompt contracts to capture maximum convergence tailwinds. In contango, the algorithm scans forward tenors to find the point of minimum annualized carry: \(\min_{T} \frac{F(T) - F(T_{\text{prev}})}{T - T_{\text{prev}}}\). By rolling into flatter, longer-dated tenors, the index avoids the severe prompt roll decay near expiration.

Research Problems

Design an optimal execution algorithm for an institutional commodity desk managing a $5 billion passive commodity index tracker. How can execution during the 5-day roll window be optimized using VWAP, TWAP, and cross-contract calendar spread liquidity to minimize market impact and front-running losses?

Solution

Rather than executing discrete leg orders (selling prompt and buying second month separately), the desk trades the calendar spread contract directly as a single exchange-cleared synthetic order: \(\text{Spread} = F_1 - F_2\). This eliminates legging risk. To defeat front-running algorithms, the desk introduces randomized execution time windows around the scheduled roll days and utilizes volume-weighted average price (VWAP) matching against dark physical merchant inventory crossing networks.

Investigate the cross-asset spillover between the commodity roll yield factor and equity value/growth factors. Why do energy-producing equity stocks correlate strongly with backwardated commodity futures?

Solution

When physical commodity curves are in backwardation, spot prices sit at a premium to future contracts, indicating high current cash flows and depleted inventories. Energy exploration and production (E&P) companies generate peak free cash flow today rather than distant terminal growth, aligning them empirically with the Value factor (high book-to-market, low duration). In contango, spot prices are depressed and capital expenditure is curtailed, depressing E&P earnings.

Critique the carbon footprint of collateralized commodity futures investing. If a futures investor holds cash collateral in Treasury bills and never takes physical delivery of oil, does the financial investment bear Scope 1 or Scope 3 carbon emission liabilities?

Solution

Under GHG Protocol Corporate Standard, holding a cash-settled futures contract confers no physical ownership of physical assets; Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions belong to the physical driller and refinery. However, under the Partnership for Carbon Accounting Financials (PCAF), financed emissions include derivative investments that facilitate commercial enterprise financing. By providing risk-transfer capacity that lowers producers' cost of capital, futures speculation indirectly facilitates physical extraction, generating active debate over Scope 3 financed emissions accounting.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What are the three components of a fully collateralized commodity futures investment return?

    Options

    A fully collateralized futures investment earns the percentage change in spot price, the roll yield from forward curve convergence, and the interest on cash collateral (Gorton & Rouwenhorst 2006).

  2. In their empirical study, what did Erb & Harvey (2006) discover regarding the historical excess returns of individual commodity futures?

    Options

    Erb & Harvey (2006) showed individual commodity excess returns averaged near zero, but roll yield explained over 80% of the cross-sectional return dispersion between backwardated and contango assets.

  3. If the expiring front-month contract trades at \(F_1 = \$50.00\) and the second-month contract trades at \(F_2 = \$52.00\), what is the discrete roll yield in percent (\((F_1 - F_2)/F_1\))?

    \(\text{Roll Yield} = (50 - 52) / 50 = -2 / 50 = -0.04\) (-4.0%).

  4. Why does a long investor in a contango futures market suffer from "contango bleed"?

    Options

    In contango, distant contracts trade at a premium. Purchasing higher-priced contracts that subsequently converge downward to spot imposes continuous negative roll yield.

  5. Over a 1-year period, a commodity experiences spot return \(R_{\text{spot}} = +5.0\%\), roll yield \(R_{\text{roll}} = -2.0\%\), and collateral yield \(R_{\text{collateral}} = +4.5\%\). What is the total collateralized return in percent?

    \(R_{\text{total}} = 5.0 - 2.0 + 4.5 = 7.5\%\).

  6. What did Gorton & Rouwenhorst (2006) demonstrate about the correlation between commodity futures and equities over long horizons?

    Options

    Gorton & Rouwenhorst (2006) proved that diversified collateralized commodity futures provided strong equity diversification and positive inflation sensitivity.

  7. In backwardation, if \(F_1 = \$60.00\) and \(F_2 = \$57.00\), what is the discrete roll yield in percent (\((F_1 - F_2)/F_1\))?

    \(\text{Roll Yield} = (60 - 57) / 60 = 3 / 60 = +0.05\) (+5.0%).

  8. Why did the United States Oil Fund (USO) restructure its investment holdings in April 2020 away from front-month WTI futures?

    Options

    USO held over 25% of front-month open interest during the negative oil crash; regulatory limits and extreme contango forced it to spread contracts across tenors out to 12 months.
