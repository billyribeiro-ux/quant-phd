Theory of Storage & Inventory Dynamics

Physical commodity derivatives diverge from pure financial instruments because commodities must be extracted, transported, and stored subject to a physical non-negativity constraint on aggregate inventories. This lesson develops the microeconomic theory of storage formulated by Kaldor, Working, and Brennan. We define the convenience yield as an embedded timing option on inventory stockouts, derive the fundamental cash-and-carry bounds under inventory scarcity, and construct the non-linear supply-of-storage curve governing forward basis spreads.

Physical commodities cannot be manufactured from financial credit when physical stockpiles reach exhaustion. In financial assets, cash-and-carry and reverse cash-and-carry arbitrage enforce an exact no-arbitrage forward price. In commodities, reverse cash-and-carry fails whenever aggregate inventories approach zero because market participants cannot short-sell non-existent physical barrels or bushels. The convenience yield quantifies the economic value of holding physical inventory during periods of acute scarcity. Understanding this embedded real option is essential for pricing commodity curves, managing delivery risk, and avoiding catastrophic basis dislocations during physical inventory crunches.

1. Physical Storage Frictions and Asymmetric Arbitrage

In standard financial asset pricing, the forward price \(F_{t, T}\) for an asset with spot price \(S_t\) under constant continuously compounded risk-free rate \(r\) and zero storage costs satisfies the strict cost-of-carry equality cost-of-carry forward price:

\[F_{t, T} = S_t e^{r (T - t)}\]

If \(F_{t, T} > S_t e^{r (T - t)}\), an arbitrageur borrows cash, purchases the spot asset, and sells the forward contract, locking in a cash-and-carry profit. If \(F_{t, T} < S_t e^{r (T - t)}\), an arbitrageur short-sells the spot asset, invests the proceeds at risk-free rate \(r\), and buys the forward contract, locking in a reverse cash-and-carry profit.

In physical commodities, this bilateral symmetry collapses due to two structural frictions documented by Kaldor (1939) and analyzed in derivatives textbooks by Hull (2021):

  1. Physical carrying costs: Holding inventory incurs continuous warehousing fees, physical degradation, leakage, insurance, and handling charges, denoted by annualized continuous cost \(c > 0\).
  2. Non-negativity of aggregate physical inventory: Total physical inventory \(I_t \ge 0\) across all market participants cannot fall below zero. While an individual trader can borrow a financial share from a custodian, the aggregate economy cannot borrow non-existent crude oil or wheat from the future.

Definition 1.1 · Theory of Storage The Theory of storage states that the relationship between the contemporaneous spot price \(S_t\) and forward price \(F_{t, T}\) of a storable commodity is determined by the net cost of carrying physical inventory from \(t\) to \(T\):

\[F_{t, T} = S_t e^{(r + c - \delta)(T - t)}\]

where \(r\) is the continuously compounded risk-free financing rate, \(c\) is the marginal physical storage and insurance cost per unit time (expressed as a fraction of spot price), and \(\delta \ge 0\) is the marginal convenience yield earned by holders of physical stock.

Definition 1.2 · Commodity Convenience Yield The Commodity convenience yield \(\delta(I_t)\) is the flow of implicit economic benefits that accrues to the owner of physical commodity inventory but not to the owner of a forward contract. Economically, \(\delta(I_t)\) represents the embedded real option value of having immediate physical possession of raw materials to maintain continuous production, meet unexpected localized demand surges, or avoid plant shutdown during supply disruptions when inventories are scarce.

2. Convenience Yield as an Embedded Real Option

Why should holding physical inventory yield positive economic utility beyond its spot value? As first formalized by Kaldor (1939), physical inventory embeds an operational timing option. Consider an industrial consumer (an oil refinery or copper smelter) requiring commodity input to keep operations running.

Example 1.1 · The Refinery Stockout Option An oil refinery processing 100,000 barrels per day faces pipeline delivery uncertainty. If the refinery runs out of crude stock, the catalytic cracking unit must be shut down, costing $5,000,000 in recommissioning charges and contract default penalties. A forward contract delivering in 30 days provides no protection against an unpredicted pipeline rupture tomorrow afternoon. Physical inventory on site provides an insurance buffer whose marginal shadow value equals the expected shutdown cost avoided.

Following this operational option framework, let \(V(I_t)\) denote the total operational benefit of aggregate inventory \(I_t\). The marginal convenience yield is the derivative:

\[\delta(I_t) = \frac{\partial V(I_t)}{\partial I_t}\]

Theorem 1.1 · Properties of the Convenience Yield Function The marginal convenience yield \(\delta(I): (0, \infty) \to (0, \infty)\) satisfies three structural conditions:

  1. Monotonic depletion: \(\frac{\partial \delta(I)}{\partial I} < 0\). As inventory increases, the marginal probability of a stockout approaches zero, diminishing the marginal value of additional physical reserves.
  2. Stockout divergence: \(\lim_{I \to 0^+} \delta(I) = +\infty\). When physical inventory is exhausted, the shadow price of immediate physical delivery diverges to infinity.
  3. Satiation asymptote: \(\lim_{I \to \infty} \delta(I) = 0\). When inventory is abundant, stockout risk is zero, and physical inventory provides no operational convenience over forward delivery.

Proof Let the probability of stockout over horizon \(\Delta t\) given current inventory \(I\) be \(p(I) = \Prob(D > I)\), where \(D\) is cumulative demand with continuous strictly positive probability density function \(f(D)\) on \([0, \infty)\). The operational benefit of inventory is \(V(I) = K \int_0^I f(u) \, du - \int_0^I u f(u) \, du\), where \(K\) is the fixed cost of production interruption. Differentiating with respect to \(I\) by Leibniz's rule yields \(\delta(I) = V'(I) = (K - I) f(I)\). For a standard exponential or Pareto distribution of extreme demand spikes with right tail \(\bar{F}(I) \propto I^{-\alpha}\), the marginal shadow value satisfies \(\delta(I) \approx \gamma / (I + \epsilon)\) with \(\gamma, \epsilon > 0\). Differentiating gives \(\delta'(I) = -\gamma / (I + \epsilon)^2 < 0\), establishing monotonicity. Taking limits confirms \(\delta(I) \to \infty\) as \(I \to 0\) when \(\epsilon \to 0\), and \(\delta(I) \to 0\) as \(I \to \infty\). \(\blacksquare\)

3. The Supply-of-Storage Curve and Arbitrage Asymmetry

We now combine marginal storage costs \(c(I)\) and marginal convenience yields \(\delta(I)\) to formalize the net cost of carry \(\psi(I) = r + c(I) - \delta(I)\). The forward basis spread is defined as:

\[\text{Spread}(T) = \frac{F_{t, T} - S_t}{S_t} = e^{\psi(I)(T - t)} - 1 \approx \psi(I)(T - t)\]

Theorem 1.2 · The Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage Upper Bound (Full Carry) In a competitive storage market, the forward price cannot exceed the physical full carrying cost:

\[F_{t, T} \le S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\]

If \(F_{t, T} > S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\), a pure arbitrage opportunity exists regardless of the convenience yield.

Proof Assume \(F_{t, T} > S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\). At time \(t\), an arbitrageur executes the cash-and-carry strategy: (i) borrows \(S_t\) cash at risk-free rate \(r\); (ii) purchases 1 unit of physical commodity at spot price \(S_t\); (iii) pays physical storage cost \(c\) continuously until \(T\); (iv) sells 1 forward contract expiring at \(T\) at price \(F_{t, T}\). At time \(T\), the total accumulated debt owed to the bank is \(S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\). The arbitrageur delivers the physical commodity into the forward contract, receiving \(F_{t, T}\). The net riskless cash payoff at \(T\) is \(F_{t, T} - S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)} > 0\). Because this strategy requires zero net initial investment and carries zero risk of default, competitive arbitrage forces \(F_{t, T} \le S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.1 · Why the Reverse Arbitrage Lower Bound Fails If \(F_{t, T} < S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\), the theoretical reverse cash-and-carry strategy would require short-selling physical spot inventory and investing the cash. But when aggregate inventory \(I_t\) is near zero, non-owners cannot borrow physical commodity stock. Furthermore, owners of physical stock refuse to sell their units because their marginal convenience yield exceeds the interest earned on cash: \(\delta(I_t) > r + c\). Consequently, the forward price can fall substantially below the spot price without triggering arbitrage correction.

This dynamic produces the classic supply-of-storage curve. At high inventory levels, the curve flattens at the full carry upper bound (\(F - S \approx S(r + c)\)). At low inventory levels, the curve drops steeply into negative territory as convenience yields surge.

Lab 1 · Kaldor-Working-Brennan Supply-of-Storage Curve

Simulating marginal convenience yield and storage cost across inventory levels from I=2.0 to I=100.0 will verify the non-linear transition from deep backwardation (negative carry) to full carry contango.

"""Lab X3-L01-1: Kaldor-Working-Brennan Theory of Storage and Supply-of-Storage Curve.

Simulates the non-linear relationship between aggregate physical inventory levels,
marginal convenience yields, physical storage costs, and the forward basis spread.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def marginal_convenience_yield(inventory: float, gamma: float = 1.0, epsilon: float = 2.0) -> float:
    """Compute marginal convenience yield delta(I) as embedded stockout option value.

    delta(I) = gamma / (I + epsilon)
    """
    return gamma / (inventory + epsilon)


def marginal_storage_cost(inventory: float, c0: float = 0.02, c1: float = 0.0004) -> float:
    """Compute physical marginal warehouse and degradation cost c(I) = c0 + c1 * I."""
    return c0 + c1 * inventory


def net_cost_of_carry(inventory: float, risk_free_rate: float = 0.04) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Compute (delta, c, psi) where psi = r + c - delta is net cost of carry."""
    delta = marginal_convenience_yield(inventory)
    cost = marginal_storage_cost(inventory)
    psi = risk_free_rate + cost - delta
    return delta, cost, psi


def forward_price(spot: float, net_carry: float, tenor_years: float = 0.5) -> float:
    """Compute forward price F = S * exp(psi * T)."""
    return spot * math.exp(net_carry * tenor_years)


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab X3-L01-1 | Kaldor-Working-Brennan Theory of Storage & Supply-of-Storage")
    print("-" * 75)
    hdr = (
        f"{'Inventory':>10} {'Conv Yield':>12} {'Storage Cost':>14} "
        f"{'Net Carry':>12} {'6M Forward':>12} {'Basis %':>10}"
    )
    print(hdr)
    print("-" * 75)

    spot_price = 80.0  # $80 per barrel / unit
    tenor = 0.5  # 6 months (0.5 year)
    inventory_levels = [2.0, 5.0, 10.0, 20.0, 50.0, 100.0]

    for inv in inventory_levels:
        delta, cost, psi = net_cost_of_carry(inv, risk_free_rate=0.04)
        f_price = forward_price(spot_price, psi, tenor)
        basis_pct = ((f_price - spot_price) / spot_price) * 100.0

        print(f"{inv:10.1f} {delta:12.4f} {cost:14.4f} {psi:12.4f} {f_price:12.2f} {basis_pct:9.2f}%")

    print("-" * 75)
    delta_low, _, psi_low = net_cost_of_carry(2.0)
    delta_high, _, psi_high = net_cost_of_carry(100.0)
    print(f"Low inventory (I=2.0):   delta={delta_low:.4f} > r+c => Backwardation (psi={psi_low:.4f})")
    print(f"High inventory (I=100.0): delta={delta_high:.4f} < r+c => Contango      (psi={psi_high:.4f})")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Classical storage theory assumes a single frictionless spot price and homogeneous storage facilities. In real-world physical commodity markets, the canon breaks across three operational dimensions:

  1. Location basis and pipeline congestion: Physical storage is tied to specific geographic hubs (such as Cushing, Oklahoma for WTI crude oil, or Henry Hub, Louisiana for natural gas). When regional transport infrastructure is congested, local inventories cannot satisfy distant demand. On April 20, 2020, Cushing storage reached operational capacity (over 80% tank capacity with all remaining space leased), causing the May 2020 WTI contract to settle at -$37.63/bbl because holders of expiring long contracts had no access to physical storage.
  2. Quality grading and delivery differentials: Futures contracts specify standard deliverable grades (e.g. sulfur content and API gravity for crude; moisture and test weight for corn). Substandard physical lots trade at severe discounts that cannot be cured by financial arbitrage.
  3. Storage capacity non-linearities: Physical storage costs do not grow linearly with inventory. As storage approaches terminal capacity, the marginal cost of storage accelerates vertically as traders lease floating offshore supertankers (VLCCs) at exorbitant day rates ($100,000+/day).

Misconceptions

  • Convenience yield is an unobservable accounting plug inserted into formulas to force the cost-of-carry equality to balance.

    The convenience yield represents the structural shadow price of an active non-negativity constraint on inventory (Kaldor 1939). It is the embedded real option value of immediate operational availability during supply shocks, directly observable in the spread between spot and forward prices across varying inventory levels.

  • Commodity futures prices must always trade at a premium to spot prices because physical storage and insurance costs are strictly positive.

    While physical storage costs \(c > 0\) increase the forward price, the convenience yield \(\delta\) exerts an offsetting downward force on forward prices. Whenever inventories are depleted, \(\delta > r + c\), driving the forward price below the spot price into backwardation.

  • A sudden increase in physical storage costs will always cause forward prices to rise relative to spot prices.

    If higher storage costs induce inventory liquidation, the reduction in physical stockpiles increases the probability of future stockout, driving up the marginal convenience yield \(\delta(I)\). If \(\delta(I)\) rises by more than the storage cost increase \(\Delta c\), net carry decreases, causing the forward basis to fall rather than rise.

Problem set

Mechanics Problems

A storable metal trades at spot price \(S_0 = \$2,500\) per metric ton. The continuously compounded risk-free rate is \(r = 5.0\%\), physical storage and insurance costs are \(c = 3.0\%\) p.a., and the annual convenience yield is \(\delta = 1.0\%\). Compute the theoretical 6-month forward price \(F_{0, 0.5}\) and the forward basis spread \((F - S) / S\).

Solution

The net cost of carry is \(\psi = r + c - \delta = 0.05 + 0.03 - 0.01 = 0.07\) (7.0% p.a.). Over \(T = 0.5\) years, the theoretical forward price is:

\[F_{0, 0.5} = S_0 e^{\psi T} = 2,500 \times e^{0.07 \times 0.5} = 2,500 \times e^{0.035} \approx 2,500 \times 1.03562 = 2,589.05\]

The forward basis spread as a percentage of spot is:

\[\frac{F_{0, 0.5} - S_0}{S_0} = \frac{2,589.05 - 2,500.00}{2,500.00} = +3.562\%\]

The market trades in contango with an annualized carry spread of 7.12%.

During an acute supply squeeze in crude oil, the spot price is \(S_0 = \$90.00\) per barrel, the 3-month forward price is \(F_{0, 0.25} = \$84.00\) per barrel, the risk-free rate is \(r = 4.0\%\), and storage costs are \(c = 2.0\%\). Determine the annualized implied convenience yield \(\delta\).

Solution

Under the theory of storage, \(F = S e^{(r + c - \delta)T}\). Taking natural logarithms on both sides:

\[\ln(F / S) = (r + c - \delta) T\] \[\delta = r + c - \frac{1}{T} \ln\left(\frac{F}{S}\right)\]

Substituting \(S = 90.00\), \(F = 84.00\), \(T = 0.25\), \(r = 0.04\), and \(c = 0.02\):

\[\ln(84 / 90) = \ln(0.93333) \approx -0.06899\] \[\delta = 0.04 + 0.02 - \frac{-0.06899}{0.25} = 0.06 + 0.27597 = 0.33597 \approx 33.60\%\]

The implied convenience yield is 33.60% annualized, indicating extreme physical scarcity.

Suppose a warehouse holds 40,000 bushels of grain. The marginal convenience yield function is \(\delta(I) = 1.20 / (I + 5.0)\), where \(I\) is measured in tens of thousands of bushels. Marginal physical storage cost is \(c(I) = 0.02 + 0.005 I\). For \(I = 4.0\) (40,000 bushels) and risk-free rate \(r = 0.03\), calculate the net cost of carry \(\psi\) and determine whether the market is in backwardation or contango.

Solution

Evaluate \(\delta(4.0)\) and \(c(4.0)\):

\[\delta(4.0) = \frac{1.20}{4.0 + 5.0} = \frac{1.20}{9.0} \approx 0.1333 \quad (13.33\%)\] \[c(4.0) = 0.02 + 0.005(4.0) = 0.02 + 0.02 = 0.0400 \quad (4.00\%)\]

Net cost of carry \(\psi = r + c - \delta\):

\[\psi = 0.03 + 0.04 - 0.1333 = -0.0633 \quad (-6.33\%)\]

Because \(\psi < 0\), the marginal convenience yield exceeds total carrying costs (\(\delta > r + c\)), and the forward curve is in backwardation.

Proof Problems

Prove that if reverse cash-and-carry were unrestricted without inventory constraints, the commodity forward price would be uniquely fixed at full carry \(F_{t, T} = S_t e^{(r + c)T}\), implying that convenience yield must be identically zero.

Solution

Suppose an investor can short-sell physical commodity inventory with zero friction, receiving immediate cash proceeds \(S_t\) and paying continuous borrowing fee \(c_b\). Under frictionless shorting, the short-seller invests cash \(S_t\) at risk-free rate \(r\) and pays storage \(c\) on behalf of the lender (or avoids paying \(c\)). If \(F_{t, T} < S_t e^{(r + c)T}\), the arbitrageur shorts 1 unit of spot, receives \(S_t\), invests at \(r\), avoids paying storage \(c\), and buys 1 forward at \(F_{t, T}\). At maturity \(T\), the investment grows to \(S_t e^{(r + c)T}\), which is used to pay \(F_{t, T}\) to purchase the commodity and return it to the lender. The riskless profit is \(S_t e^{(r + c)T} - F_{t, T} > 0\). Combined with Theorem 1.2 (cash-and-carry upper bound), no-arbitrage requires \(F_{t, T} = S_t e^{(r + c)T}\). Therefore, a non-zero convenience yield \(\delta > 0\) requires the failure of reverse cash-and-carry, which arises directly from the non-negativity constraint on aggregate physical inventory \(I \ge 0\).

Prove that under a strictly convex storage cost function \(C(I)\) with \(C'(I) > 0, C''(I) > 0\) and a strictly concave inventory utility function \(U(I)\) with \(U'(I) > 0, U''(I) < 0\), the net price of storage \(\psi(I) = r + C'(I) - U'(I)\) is a strictly monotonically increasing function of inventory level \(I\).

Solution

Define the net cost of carry function as \(\psi(I) = r + C'(I) - U'(I)\), where \(r\) is constant. Differentiating \(\psi(I)\) with respect to \(I\):

\[\frac{d \psi(I)}{d I} = C''(I) - U''(I)\]

By assumption, storage costs are strictly convex, so \(C''(I) > 0\). Operational inventory utility is strictly concave, so \(U''(I) < 0\), which implies \(-U''(I) > 0\). Therefore:

\[\frac{d \psi(I)}{d I} = C''(I) + (-U''(I)) > 0\]

Because the first derivative is strictly positive for all \(I > 0\), the net price of carry \(\psi(I)\) is strictly monotonically increasing in aggregate inventory \(I\). This guarantees that forward basis spreads rise monotonically with inventory.

Let \(F(t, T)\) be a differentiable forward curve across maturity \(T\). Prove that the instantaneous forward convenience yield \(\delta(t, T)\) satisfies \(\delta(t, T) = r(t, T) + c(t, T) - \frac{\partial \ln F(t, T)}{\partial T}\).

Solution

Under continuous compounding, the forward curve satisfies \(\ln F(t, T) = \ln S_t + \int_t^T (r(t, u) + c(t, u) - \delta(t, u)) \, du\). Differentiating both sides with respect to contract maturity \(T\) by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

\[\frac{\partial \ln F(t, T)}{\partial T} = r(t, T) + c(t, T) - \delta(t, T)\]

Rearranging this equality isolates the instantaneous convenience yield:

\[\delta(t, T) = r(t, T) + c(t, T) - \frac{\partial \ln F(t, T)}{\partial T}\]

This proves that the instantaneous convenience yield equals the carrying cost minus the slope of the log forward curve.

Empirical Problems

In an empirical dataset of 60 monthly observations of US commercial crude oil inventories (million barrels) and the 1-month to 12-month WTI futures basis \((F_{12} - F_1)/F_1\), an OLS regression yields: \(\text{Basis}_t = -0.18 + 0.00045 \times \text{Inv}_t\) with \(R^2 = 0.64\) and \(t\)-statistic on inventory of \(+10.2\). Interpret the economic meaning of the positive slope coefficient in terms of the Kaldor-Working-Brennan supply-of-storage model.

Solution

The positive slope coefficient (\(\hat{\beta} = +0.00045\), \(t = 10.2\)) confirms the fundamental prediction of the theory of storage (Kaldor 1939). When inventory levels are low, convenience yields are elevated, causing the forward basis to be negative (backwardation). For each 100-million-barrel increase in commercial crude inventories, the 1-to-12-month forward basis increases by \(0.00045 \times 100 = +4.5\%\), shifting the curve toward contango as the marginal convenience yield decays toward zero.

During the COVID-19 demand collapse in April 2020, Cushing crude storage reached 60 million barrels against a practical working capacity of 65 million barrels. Spot WTI fell to -$37.63/bbl while the 1-month forward traded at +$15.00/bbl. Quantify the effective monthly storage cost implied by this basis and explain why the cash-and-carry arbitrage broke down.

Solution

The 1-month basis was \(F_1 - S_0 = \$15.00 - (-\$37.63) = +\$52.63\) per barrel for 1 month of storage. In theory, an arbitrageur could buy crude at -\$37.63 (getting paid to take oil) and sell forward at \$15.00, collecting \$52.63 risk-free. However, this arbitrage requires available physical tank space at Cushing. Because all storage capacity was 100% committed, arbitrageurs could not take physical delivery. The shadow price of storage became infinite at the physical boundary, disconnecting paper futures from spot.

In corn markets, pre-harvest inventories in August reach annual lows, whereas post-harvest inventories in November reach annual peaks. An empirical study finds that August 1-month basis spreads average -6.5% (backwardation) while November basis spreads average +4.2% (contango). Connect this seasonal shift to the non-negativity constraint on inventory.

Solution

Agricultural commodities follow an annual production cycle. In August, old-crop stockpiles are depleted prior to harvest. The non-negativity constraint binds: market participants cannot borrow from the unharvested new crop in the fields to satisfy August consumption. Consequently, spot prices trade at a high premium (\(\delta\) spikes), creating backwardation. In November, harvest supply surges into warehouses, driving \(\delta \to 0\) and pushing the curve into contango bounded by the full physical cost of carry.

Research Problems

Critique the classical assumption that convenience yield is purely a function of physical inventory \(\delta(I)\). How do financial market frictions—such as dealer balance-sheet constraints, repo haircuts, and margin requirements—introduce financial drivers into the convenience yield?

Solution

While Kaldor and Working modeled \(\delta(I)\) as a function of physical stocks alone, modern quantitative finance shows that physical commodity inventories are financed through bank repo lines and commodity trade finance facilities (Gorton & Rouwenhorst 2006). When financial dealers face post-2008 regulatory leverage constraints (Basel III Supplementary Leverage Ratio) or collateral liquidity crunches, the shadow cost of financing physical inventory surges. Even if physical warehouses have spare capacity, arbitrageurs cannot expand cash-and-carry positions, causing convenience yields to reflect intermediary funding liquidity alongside physical stockouts.

Formulate an econometric framework to test whether convenience yield exhibits state-dependent asymmetric volatility during inventory drawdowns versus inventory build-ups.

Solution

Specify a threshold autoregressive (TAR) or Markov-switching model for implied convenience yield \(\delta_t\):

\[\Delta \delta_t = \alpha + \beta_1 \Delta I_t \indic_{\{I_t < I^*\}} + \beta_2 \Delta I_t \indic_{\{I_t \ge I^*\}} + \epsilon_t\]

where \(I^*\) is a critical inventory threshold. The theory of storage predicts that \(|\beta_1| \gg |\beta_2|\): when inventories fall below \(I^*\), marginal convenience yield explodes non-linearly (\(\beta_1 < 0\) large in magnitude). Above \(I^*\), inventory additions have negligible impact on convenience yield (\(\beta_2 \approx 0\)), confirming the asymmetric curvature of the supply of storage.

Analyze how the transition toward renewable energy and decarbonization alters the convenience yield dynamics of fossil fuels versus critical battery metals (lithium, cobalt, nickel).

Solution

In traditional hydrocarbons (crude oil), capital discipline and long-run peak demand expectations reduce investment in new extraction and long-term storage infrastructure. This structural underinvestment lowers baseline global buffer stocks, making convenience yields more volatile and prone to acute geopolitical spikes. In critical transition metals, long development lead times (7–10 years for new mines) combine with rapid demand shocks from battery manufacturing. Because refined battery chemicals degrade over time, physical storage capacity is constrained, producing persistent convenience yield surges and structural backwardation during EV demand ramp-ups.

    Options

    Net carry \(\psi = r + c - \delta = 0.05 + 0.02 - 0.02 = 0.05\). The forward price is \(F = 100 \times e^{0.05 \times 1.0} = 100 \times 1.05127 \approx 105.13\).

  4. If the same commodity experiences a severe supply disruption such that convenience yield surges to \(\delta = 20.0\%\) while \(r = 5.0\%\) and \(c = 2.0\%\), what is the new 1-year forward price?

    Net carry \(\psi = 0.05 + 0.02 - 0.20 = -0.13\). The forward price is \(F = 100 \times e^{-0.13 \times 1.0} = 100 \times 0.878095 \approx 87.81\).

  5. What is the "full carry" upper bound on a commodity futures contract?

    Options

    If the forward price exceeds \(S e^{(r + c)T}\), traders can borrow cash, buy physical spot, pay storage, and sell forward, locking in riskless profit (Theorem 1.2).

  6. As aggregate physical inventory \(I \to \infty\), what happens to the marginal convenience yield \(\delta(I)\)?

    Options

    When inventories are abundant, stockout risk is negligible, eliminating the operational option value of holding physical stock (\(\delta \to 0\)).

  7. If the 6-month forward basis \((F - S)/S = -6.0\%\) under continuous compounding over \(T = 0.5\) years, what is the annualized net cost of carry \(\psi\) in percent (e.g. -12.37)?

    \(\psi = \ln(1 - 0.06) / 0.5 = \ln(0.94) / 0.5 = -0.061875 / 0.5 = -0.12375 \approx -12.37\%\).

  8. What primary operational bottleneck caused the May 2020 WTI crude oil futures contract to trade at negative prices (-$37.63/bbl)?

    Options

    Storage exhaustion at Cushing meant physical delivery could not be accommodated, forcing long contract holders to pay exorbitant premiums to exit positions before physical settlement.
