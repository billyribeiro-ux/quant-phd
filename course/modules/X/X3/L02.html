Curve Regimes: Backwardation, Contango, and Normal Backwardation

The forward curve of a commodity displays complex structural term structures that fluctuate between upward-sloping and downward-sloping regimes. Building upon the theory of storage and the commodity convenience yield established in Lesson 1, this lesson analyzes the mechanics of contango, backwardation, and super-contango. We rigorously untangle the classical distinction between observable market backwardation and Keynes's normal backwardation, and examine how physical storage capacity constraints govern the cross-section of commodity forward curves.

The shape of a commodity's futures curve dictates the returns of hedgers, producers, and quantitative commodity trading strategies. Confusing observable forward curve slope with expected spot price trajectory is one of the most pervasive fallacies in derivatives trading. A backwardated forward curve does not imply that market participants expect spot prices to decline; nor does a contango curve forecast rising prices. Mastering the economic mechanics separating carry costs from expected risk premia enables quantitative researchers to construct systematic curve-carry strategies, hedge storage exposure, and detect impending delivery bottlenecks before physical settlement crises occur.

1. Term Structure Taxonomy: Contango, Backwardation, and Super-Contango

Let \(F(t, T)\) denote the futures price quoted at time \(t\) for delivery of a physical commodity at maturity \(T > t\). The forward curve at time \(t\) is the function \(T \mapsto F(t, T)\) for \(T \ge t\), with \(F(t, t) = S_t\) denoting the spot price.

Definition 2.1 · Backwardation and Contango The shape of the commodity futures term structure is classified into Backwardation and contango regimes based on the sign of the forward curve slope:

  1. Contango: The futures curve is upward-sloping: \(\frac{\partial F(t, T)}{\partial T} > 0\). For any two maturities \(T_1 < T_2\), \(F(t, T_1) < F(t, T_2)\), and distant futures trade at a premium to the spot price: \(F(t, T) > S_t\). In terms of the cost of carry, net carrying cost is positive: \(r + c > \delta\).
  2. Backwardation: The futures curve is downward-sloping: \(\frac{\partial F(t, T)}{\partial T} < 0\). For any two maturities \(T_1 < T_2\), \(F(t, T_1) > F(t, T_2)\), and distant futures trade at a discount to the spot price: \(F(t, T) < S_t\). Net carrying cost is negative: \(\delta > r + c\).
  3. Super-Contango: An extreme regime wherein the spot price collapses relative to prompt futures due to physical storage exhaustion at the delivery hub. The prompt slope approaches or exceeds the physical cost of emergency storage (e.g. leasing floating offshore tankers), with annualized carry spreads exceeding 30% to 50%.

Example 2.1 · Cross-Commodity Term Structure Regimes

  1. Precious Metals (Gold, Silver): Gold is almost never in backwardation. Aggregate physical stocks (central bank vaults, private bullion reserves) represent decades of annual mine production (\(I / D \gg 10\)). Stockout risk is zero, so the convenience yield is negligible (\(\delta \approx 0\)). Consequently, the gold futures curve trades in persistent contango at full carry: \(F(t, T) = S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\).
  2. Crude Oil (WTI, Brent): Global crude oil consumption is approximately 100 million barrels per day, while global commercial storage buffers represent only 50 to 60 days of consumption. Supply disruptions or unexpected demand surges rapidly deplete inventories, driving convenience yield \(\delta\) well above carrying costs \(r + c\) and flipping the curve into steep backwardation.
  3. Agricultural Commodities (Wheat, Corn): Grains exhibit dramatic seasonal regime shifts. Prior to the autumn harvest, old-crop inventories reach annual lows and trade in backwardation; immediately following harvest, warehouse inflows flood storage and flip the curve into contango.

2. Keynesian Normal Backwardation vs. Price of Storage

The term backwardation was introduced by John Maynard Keynes in A Treatise on Money (1930) under his Theory of Normal Backwardation. However, modern financial economics makes a sharp distinction between observable forward curve slope and Keynes's risk premium hypothesis.

Theorem 2.1 · Observable Backwardation vs. Normal Backwardation Let \(\E_t[S_T]\) denote the market's subjective expectation at time \(t\) of the spot price at maturity \(T\). The following definitions are conceptually distinct:

  1. Market Backwardation (Observable): \(F(t, T) < S_t\). This is a purely observable cross-sectional relationship on the trading screen at time \(t\).
  2. Normal Backwardation (Keynes's Hypothesis): \(F(t, T) < \E_t[S_T]\). This is an unobservable asset pricing condition stating that the futures price is a downward-biased estimate of the expected future spot price, embedding an expected risk premium \(\pi_t = \E_t[S_T] - F(t, T) > 0\) earned by long speculators.

Proof Decompose the difference between the futures price and the spot price: \[F(t, T) - S_t = (F(t, T) - \E_t[S_T]) + (\E_t[S_T] - S_t)\] Define the expected risk premium as \(\pi(t, T) = \E_t[S_T] - F(t, T)\), and the expected spot price appreciation as \(g(t, T) = \E_t[S_T] - S_t\). Then: \[F(t, T) - S_t = -\pi(t, T) + g(t, T)\] Normal backwardation implies \(\pi(t, T) > 0\). However, observable market backwardation requires \(F(t, T) - S_t < 0\), which occurs if and only if \(g(t, T) < \pi(t, T)\). If the expected spot price rises sufficiently fast (\(g(t, T) > \pi(t, T) > 0\)), the market forward curve can trade in observable contango (\(F(t, T) > S_t\)) even while satisfying Keynesian normal backwardation (\(F(t, T) < \E_t[S_T]\)). Conversely, if the spot price is expected to fall sharply (\(g(t, T) < 0\)), market backwardation can occur even if \(\pi(t, T) \le 0\). Hence the two concepts are logically independent. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.1 · Working's (1949) Critique of Keynes Holbrook Working argued that Keynes's insurance theory overemphasized risk premia and neglected the economics of physical inventories. Working proved that backwardation is primarily determined by current inventory scarcity through the supply-of-storage curve, rather than by hedger risk aversion alone. While Keynes viewed backwardation as the natural equilibrium of all commodity futures markets, empirical data reveals that many commodities spend prolonged decades in contango.

3. Forward Curve Term Structure Fitting

To quantify the slope of the forward curve, researchers fit smooth parametric curves to listed contract tenors. Let \(T_1, T_2, \dots, T_M\) denote contract maturities (typically 1 to 12 months). The annualized continuous curve slope between maturities \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) is:

\[\text{Slope}(T_1, T_2) = \frac{\ln F(t, T_2) - \ln F(t, T_1)}{T_2 - T_1}\]

Under the theory of storage, this slope directly reflects the average net carry over the horizon:

\[\text{Slope}(T_1, T_2) = r + c - \bar{\delta}(T_1, T_2)\]

When the curve is in backwardation, \(\text{Slope} < 0\), implying \(\bar{\delta} > r + c\). In contango, \(\text{Slope} > 0\), bounded above by the physical full carry cost \(r + c\).

Lab 1 · Commodity Curve Regimes & Keynes Normal Backwardation

Fitting multi-tenor forward curves across contango, backwardation, and super-contango regimes will demonstrate that backwardated curves imply annualized convenience yields exceeding 20%, while super-contango reflects severe storage bottlenecks with positive slopes exceeding 40%.

"""Lab X3-L02-1: Commodity Futures Term Structure: Backwardation, Contango, and Super-Contango.

Models multi-tenor forward curves across storage regimes, calculates annualized
curve slopes, implied convenience yields, and quantifies Keynesian normal backwardation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def generate_curve(spot: float, net_carry: float, tenors_months: list[int]) -> list[float]:
    """Generate futures prices across tenors: F(T) = S * exp(psi * T/12)."""
    return [round(spot * math.exp(net_carry * (t / 12.0)), 2) for t in tenors_months]


def annualized_slope(f1: float, f12: float, dt_years: float = 11.0 / 12.0) -> float:
    """Compute annualized log slope: (ln(F_12) - ln(F_1)) / dt."""
    return (math.log(f12) - math.log(f1)) / dt_years


def implied_convenience_yield(f1: float, f12: float, r: float, c: float, dt_years: float = 11.0 / 12.0) -> float:
    """Compute implied annualized convenience yield delta = r + c - slope."""
    slope = annualized_slope(f1, f12, dt_years)
    return (r + c) - slope


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab X3-L02-1 | Commodity Curve Regimes & Keynes Normal Backwardation")
    print("-" * 75)

    spot = 80.0
    r = 0.04  # 4% risk-free financing
    c = 0.03  # 3% physical storage cost
    tenors = [1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12]

    # Regimes:
    # 1. Contango (ample inventory: delta = 0.01 < r+c = 0.07 => psi = +0.06)
    # 2. Backwardation (tight inventory: delta = 0.22 > r+c = 0.07 => psi = -0.15)
    # 3. Super-contango (storage collapse / bottleneck: psi = +0.40)
    regimes = {
        "Contango (High Inventory)": 0.06,
        "Backwardation (Tight Supply)": -0.15,
        "Super-Contango (Storage Bottleneck)": 0.40,
    }

    print(f"{'Regime':<35} {'1M':>6} {'3M':>6} {'6M':>6} {'12M':>6} {'Slope p.a.':>11} {'Impl Delta':>11}")
    print("-" * 75)

    for name, psi in regimes.items():
        curve = generate_curve(spot, psi, tenors)
        f1 = curve[0]
        f3 = curve[2]
        f6 = curve[3]
        f12 = curve[5]
        slope = annualized_slope(f1, f12)
        delta = implied_convenience_yield(f1, f12, r, c)
        print(f"{name:<35} {f1:6.2f} {f3:6.2f} {f6:6.2f} {f12:6.2f} {slope * 100:10.2f}% {delta * 100:10.2f}%")

    print("-" * 75)
    print("Keynes (1930) Normal Backwardation vs Price of Storage:")
    # Suppose expected future spot E[S_T] = $80.00 across all regimes (martingale spot)
    exp_spot = 80.0
    for name, psi in regimes.items():
        curve = generate_curve(spot, psi, tenors)
        f12 = curve[5]
        risk_premium = exp_spot - f12
        is_normal_back = f12 < exp_spot
        is_market_back = f12 < spot
        short_name = name.split()[0]
        print(
            f"  {short_name:<16}: F_12M=${f12:6.2f} | E[S_12M]-F_12M = ${risk_premium:6.2f} "
            f"| Normal Back: {is_normal_back!s:<5} | Market Back: {is_market_back!s:<5}"
        )


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Classical commodity models treat the forward curve as a deterministic function of uniform carry. In practical trading, this assumption breaks down:

  1. Curve kinks and refinery turnaround seasonality: The forward curve is rarely monotonic. In crude oil, March and October contracts often trade at discounts relative to adjacent months due to planned seasonal refinery maintenance (turnarounds) which temporarily depresses crude input demand.
  2. Delivery squeeze and squeezability index: As contract expiration approaches, financial speculators who cannot take physical delivery must roll or close their positions. If physical storage at the delivery hub is tightly controlled by dominant physical merchants, prompt futures can trade at irrational spikes or catastrophic discounts that disconnect from long-dated fundamentals.
  3. Financialization and curve flattening: As documented by Tang & Xiong (2012), the massive influx of passive index funds into front-month futures post-2004 exerted mechanical downward pressure on front contracts and upward pressure on second-month contracts during predetermined roll windows, distorting market-implied convenience yields.

Misconceptions

  • Backwardation and normal backwardation are interchangeable terms for the same market condition.

    Market backwardation is an observable spot-futures price relationship (\(F < S\)). Normal backwardation is an unobservable asset pricing condition stating that the futures price sits below the expected future spot price (\(F < \E[S_T]\)), embedding an expected speculative risk premium.

  • An upward-sloping futures curve (contango) proves that the market expects commodity spot prices to increase over the coming year.

    The forward curve slope reflects current physical carrying costs, financing interest, and marginal convenience yields, not the subjective expectations hypothesis. A commodity in full-carry contango can easily experience falling spot prices if unexpected demand weakens.

  • Commodity producers are always short hedgers, ensuring that commodity curves are permanently in normal backwardation.

    Industrial consumers (airlines hedging jet fuel, utilities hedging natural gas) are natural long hedgers. When consumer hedging demand exceeds producer hedging supply, the net hedging pressure reverses, causing futures to trade at a premium to expected spot prices.

Mechanics Problems

The 1-month WTI crude futures contract trades at \(F_1 = \$78.50\) and the 7-month contract trades at \(F_7 = \$73.20\). Compute the annualized continuous curve slope between month 1 and month 7 (\(\Delta t = 0.5\) years). If financing is \(r = 4.5\%\) and storage is \(c = 2.5\%\), determine the average annualized convenience yield \(\bar{\delta}\).

Solution

The annualized forward curve slope is:

\[\text{Slope} = \frac{\ln(F_7) - \ln(F_1)}{T_7 - T_1} = \frac{\ln(73.20 / 78.50)}{0.5} = \frac{\ln(0.93248)}{0.5} = \frac{-0.06994}{0.5} = -0.13988 \quad (-13.99\%)\]

Under the theory of storage, \(\text{Slope} = r + c - \bar{\delta}\). Rearranging for convenience yield:

\[\bar{\delta} = r + c - \text{Slope} = 0.045 + 0.025 - (-0.13988) = 0.070 + 0.13988 = 0.20988 \approx 20.99\%\]

The forward curve is in backwardation with an implied convenience yield of 20.99%.

A gold bullion market has spot price \(S_0 = \$2,000\) per troy ounce. Financing is \(r = 5.0\%\) and vault storage and insurance cost \(c = 0.8\%\) p.a. Assuming convenience yield is zero (\(\delta = 0\)), calculate the 1-year full-carry forward price \(F_{0, 1}\) and the 1-month forward price \(F_{0, 1/12}\).

Solution

The continuous carrying cost is \(r + c = 0.050 + 0.008 = 0.058\) (5.8% p.a.).

For \(T = 1.0\) year:

\[F_{0, 1} = 2,000 \times e^{0.058 \times 1.0} = 2,000 \times 1.05972 = \$2,119.44\]

For \(T = 1/12 \approx 0.08333\) years:

\[F_{0, 1/12} = 2,000 \times e^{0.058 / 12} = 2,000 \times e^{0.004833} \approx 2,000 \times 1.004845 = \$2,009.69\]

The curve trades in continuous contango with 1-month basis of +\$9.69 and 12-month basis of +\$119.44.

Suppose the current spot price of copper is \(S_0 = \$8,500\) per metric ton. An econometrician's survey reveals consensus expected spot price in 6 months is \(\E_0[S_{0.5}] = \$8,700\). The 6-month futures price trades at \(F_{0, 0.5} = \$8,400\). Is the market in observable backwardation? Is it in Keynesian normal backwardation? Compute the expected risk premium.

Solution

Check observable backwardation:

\[F_{0, 0.5} - S_0 = \$8,400 - \$8,500 = -\$100 < 0\]

Because \(F < S\), the market is in observable market backwardation.

Check Keynesian normal backwardation:

\[F_{0, 0.5} - \E_0[S_{0.5}] = \$8,400 - \$8,700 = -\$300 < 0\]

Because \(F < \E_0[S_{0.5}]\), the market satisfies Keynesian normal backwardation. The expected risk premium earned by long speculators is \(\pi = \E_0[S_{0.5}] - F_{0, 0.5} = \$8,700 - \$8,400 = +\$300\) per ton (+3.57% over 6 months).

Proof Problems

Prove that if the physical full-carry upper bound \(F(t, T) \le S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\) is violated, an arbitrageur can construct a self-financing trading strategy with zero initial cash outlay, zero probability of negative cash flow, and strictly positive terminal cash flow.

Solution

Assume \(F(t, T) > S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\). At time \(t\):

  1. Borrow cash amount \(S_t\) at risk-free rate \(r\). Cash flow: \(+S_t\).
  2. Purchase 1 unit of physical commodity at spot price \(S_t\). Cash flow: \(-S_t\). Net initial cash flow is exactly zero.
  3. Continuously borrow cash to pay physical storage and insurance cost \(c\). The total accumulated debt at maturity \(T\) is \(S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\).
  4. Enter into a short forward contract to deliver 1 unit of commodity at \(T\) for contractual price \(F(t, T)\). Initial cash flow: 0.

At time \(T\):

  1. Deliver the physical commodity held in storage into the forward contract, receiving \(+F(t, T)\).
  2. Repay the accumulated bank loan of \(S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)}\).

Net terminal payoff: \(\Pi_T = F(t, T) - S_t e^{(r + c)(T - t)} > 0\). The strategy requires zero net initial capital and generates strictly positive profit with certainty, violating no-arbitrage.

Prove that under the expectations hypothesis of futures pricing (\(F(t, T) = \E_t[S_T]\)), the forward curve is upward-sloping (\(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T} > 0\)) if and only if the commodity spot price is expected to appreciate over time (\(\frac{\partial \E_t[S_T]}{\partial T} > 0\)). Contrast this with the theory of storage.

Solution

Under the expectations hypothesis, \(F(t, T) = \E_t[S_T]\). Differentiating with respect to maturity \(T\):

\[\frac{\partial F(t, T)}{\partial T} = \frac{\partial \E_t[S_T]}{\partial T}\]

Thus, \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T} > 0 \iff \frac{\partial \E_t[S_T]}{\partial T} > 0\). Forward slope maps one-to-one to expected spot drift. Under the theory of storage, however, \(F(t, T) = S_t e^{\int_t^T (r + c - \delta(u)) du}\). Differentiating gives \(\frac{\partial F(t, T)}{\partial T} = (r + c - \delta(T)) F(t, T)\). Here, forward slope is determined entirely by current financing costs, storage fees, and marginal convenience yield. Even if \(\E_t[S_T]\) is constant or falling, the forward curve can be upward-sloping whenever \(r + c > \delta\), proving that the expectations hypothesis fails in storable physical commodities.

Let \(\delta(I)\) be a convex, monotonically decreasing convenience yield function. Prove that an increase in inventory uncertainty \(\Var(I)\) (a mean-preserving spread in inventory) increases the expected convenience yield \(\E[\delta(I)]\) by Jensen's Inequality.

Solution

By Theorem 1.1, the marginal convenience yield satisfies \(\delta'(I) < 0\) and \(\delta''(I) > 0\), establishing strict convexity on \((0, \infty)\). Let \(I\) be a random variable with mean \(\bar{I} = \E[I]\). By Jensen's Inequality, for any strictly convex function \(g(x)\), \(\E[g(x)] > g(\E[x])\). Applying this to \(\delta(I)\):

\[\E[\delta(I)] > \delta(\bar{I})\]

Now consider a mean-preserving spread that shifts distribution \(F_1(I)\) to a more dispersed distribution \(F_2(I)\) with equal mean \(\bar{I}\). By the Rothschild-Stiglitz theorem, if \(g(x)\) is strictly convex, \(\int g(I) dF_2(I) > \int g(I) dF_1(I)\). Therefore, heightened inventory uncertainty raises the expected convenience yield, depressing forward prices and steepening backwardation.

Empirical Problems

In an empirical study of 20 commodity futures markets between 1990 and 2020, Erb & Harvey (2006) find that backwardated commodities generated an annualized excess return of +12.4%, while contango commodities generated an annualized return of -5.2%. Connect this finding to Keynesian normal backwardation and curve roll mechanics.

Solution

When a contract trades in backwardation (\(F < S\)), the futures price sits at a discount. As maturity approaches, futures prices converge upward to the higher spot price, delivering a positive roll yield to long investors. If Keynes's normal backwardation holds, long investors also collect a positive expected risk premium for absorbing commercial inventory risk. In contango, the roll yield is negative: long investors buy higher-priced distant contracts and sell lower-priced expiring contracts, suffering chronic roll decay that overwhelms spot price gains.

During the 2008 oil price run-up to $147/bbl, WTI crude traded in steep backwardation. However, by December 2008, spot prices had collapsed to $35/bbl and the curve flipped into deep contango with a 1-to-12-month spread of +$18.00/bbl. What physical inventory dynamic explains this violent 3,000-basis-point curve inversion?

Solution

In mid-2008, surging global demand depleted spare production capacity and commercial stockpiles, driving convenience yield \(\delta \gg r + c\) and creating backwardation. During the fourth quarter of 2008, the global financial crisis caused a catastrophic demand contraction. Unconsumed crude production rapidly flooded storage tanks at Cushing, Oklahoma. As inventories surged toward capacity, convenience yield collapsed to zero (\(\delta \to 0\)), and the forward curve flipped to full-carry contango (\(F - S \approx S(r + c)\)), incentivizing traders to store oil in commercial tanks and floating tankers.

Evaluate the term structure of Henry Hub natural gas futures. Why does natural gas exhibit dramatic seasonal hump-shaped curve structures (spiking in January/February and collapsing in April) rather than a monotonic slope?

Solution

Natural gas demand is heavily seasonal due to winter space heating across North America. Subterranean salt caverns and depleted gas field storage have strict physical injection and withdrawal rate limits. In winter (January/February), physical withdrawal capacity binds, causing spot and prompt winter futures to spike (high convenience yield). In spring (April), heating demand terminates ("shoulder season"), causing prompt prices to collapse while autumn contracts trade at a premium to reflect the cost of refilling storage caverns throughout summer.

Research Problems

Formulate a continuous-time two-factor Gibson-Schwartz (1990) model where the spot price \(S_t\) and the instantaneous convenience yield \(\delta_t\) follow coupled stochastic processes. Derive the partial differential equation (PDE) for the commodity forward price \(F(S, \delta, t, T)\).

Solution

Assume risk-neutral dynamics:

\[\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = (r - \delta_t) dt + \sigma_1 dW_{1, t}^{\Prob}\] \[d\delta_t = \kappa(\bar{\delta} - \delta_t) dt + \sigma_2 dW_{2, t}^{\Prob}\]

with \(\Cov(dW_{1, t}, dW_{2, t}) = \rho dt\). By the Feynman-Kac theorem, because the forward price is a martingale under the risk-neutral measure (\(F(S, \delta, t, T) = \E_t^{\Prob}[S_T]\)), \(F\) satisfies the PDE:

\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial t} + (r - \delta) S \frac{\partial F}{\partial S} + \kappa(\bar{\delta} - \delta) \frac{\partial F}{\partial \delta} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_1^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial S^2} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_2^2 \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial \delta^2} + \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2 S \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial S \partial \delta} = 0\]

subject to terminal condition \(F(S, \delta, T, T) = S\). This PDE admits an affine closed-form solution: \(F(S, \delta, t, T) = S e^{A(T - t) - B(T - t)\delta}\), demonstrating how stochastic convenience yield drives forward curve curvature.

Propose an empirical strategy using high-frequency satellite imaging of crude oil floating roof storage tanks to test whether satellite-derived inventory changes lead or lag forward curve slope shifts.

Solution

Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) satellites measure the shadow cast by floating roof tanks at major global terminals (Cushing, Rotterdam, Qingdao) with weekly frequency. Construct a weekly panel of global storage fullness \(I_t\). Specify a vector autoregression (VAR) or Granger causality test between weekly inventory innovations \(\Delta I_t\) and changes in the 1-to-12-month forward curve slope \(\Delta \text{Slope}_t\). If financial markets are informationally efficient, futures curve slopes adjust contemporaneously to satellite passes, while inventory changes Granger-cause subsequent spot price adjustments.

Critique the viability of retail commodity exchange-traded funds (ETFs) such as the United States Oil Fund (USO) that invest exclusively in front-month futures. How does persistent contango systematically erode investor capital?

Solution

A front-month commodity ETF must sell its expiring contract and purchase the second-month contract every month during the roll window. When the curve is in persistent contango, the second contract trades at a premium (\(F_2 > F_1\)). The ETF sells low and buys high each month. Over time, the purchased contract decays toward the lower spot price. As documented by Erb & Harvey (2006), this cumulative negative roll yield imposes a persistent return drag of -10% to -20% p.a., destroying buy-and-hold wealth even if spot oil prices remain flat.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What defines an observable futures curve in backwardation?

    Options

    Backwardation is characterized by \(\partial F / \partial T < 0\), where forward prices decrease with maturity due to high convenience yields exceeding carry costs.

  2. How does Keynesian normal backwardation differ from observable market backwardation?

    Options

    Keynes hypothesized that futures prices trade at a discount to expected future spot prices to compensate risk-bearing speculators with an expected risk premium.

  3. If the 1-month futures price is \(F_1 = \$80.00\) and the 13-month futures price is \(F_{13} = \$72.00\), what is the annualized continuous curve slope in percent (\(\Delta t = 1.0\) year, e.g. -10.54)?

    \(\text{Slope} = \ln(72 / 80) / 1.0 = \ln(0.90) \approx -0.10536 \approx -10.54\%\).

  4. Why does the gold futures curve almost always trade in contango rather than backwardation?

    Options

    Because total gold inventory represents decades of annual production, stockout probability is zero, eliminating convenience yield and forcing the curve to full carry contango.

  5. If financing cost is \(r = 4.0\%\), storage cost is \(c = 2.0\%\), and the forward curve slope is \(-15.0\%\) p.a., what is the implied annualized convenience yield in percent?

    \(\delta = r + c - \text{Slope} = 0.04 + 0.02 - (-0.15) = 0.06 + 0.15 = 0.21\) (21.0%).

  6. What was Holbrook Working's (1949) primary critique of Keynes's normal backwardation theory?

    Options

    Working demonstrated that forward curve slopes are driven by inventory availability through the supply-of-storage curve, rather than permanent commercial hedging pressure alone.

  7. If the current spot price is \(S = \$100\), the 1-year futures price is \(F = \$95\), and the expected spot price in 1 year is \(\E[S_1] = \$100\), what is the expected speculator risk premium in dollars (\(\E[S_1] - F\))?

    \(\pi = \E[S_1] - F = \$100 - \$95 = \$5.00\).

  8. What describes the phenomenon of "super-contango"?

    Options

    Super-contango occurs when regional storage tanks are full, causing prompt prices to plunge to cover the exorbitant marginal costs of emergency storage (e.g. leasing floating tankers).
