Commodities

Commodity markets represent the intersection of physical supply constraints, real storage friction, and financial risk transfer. Unlike financial assets, physical commodities cannot be created by book entry; they must be extracted, transported, insured, and stored under strict non-negativity of physical inventories. Across five intensive lessons, this module establishes the microeconomic and macroeconomic foundations of commodity derivatives pricing. We formulate the Kaldor-Working-Brennan theory of storage and the embedded stockout option value of the convenience yield (Lesson 1); analyze term structure regimes across backwardation, contango, and Keynesian normal backwardation (Lesson 2); quantify commercial hedging pressure and speculative risk premia using CFTC reporting (Lesson 3); decompose collateralized futures returns into spot price drift, roll yield, and collateral return following Erb & Harvey (2006) (Lesson 4); and investigate post-2004 index financialization alongside Kilian's (2009) structural VAR decomposition of crude oil shocks (Lesson 5). The module concludes with a 25-hour capstone project re-estimating a structural VAR to isolate supply, aggregate demand, and precautionary demand shocks.

  • Prerequisites: FC2 Derivatives Foundations and F3 Multi-Asset Factors.
  • Unlocks: Capstone Macro & Commodity Trading Modules.
  • Project: Oil-shock decomposition: an autograded structural VAR engine (oil_var.py) evaluating contemporaneous identification, impulse response functions, and variance decompositions, accompanied by an analytical research memo (report.md).
  • Data: Synthetic deterministic instances and embedded monthly macroeconomic series.

Objectives

By the end of X3 you can:

  1. Formulate the Kaldor-Working-Brennan theory of storage, quantifying marginal convenience yield, physical storage costs, and the non-negativity constraint on inventory (Lesson 1).
  2. Differentiate futures curve regimes—contango, backwardation, and super-contango—and evaluate Keynesian normal backwardation versus Working's price of storage (Lesson 2).
  3. Measure commercial hedging pressure using CFTC Commitments of Traders data and estimate futures risk premia following Bessembinder (1992) and de Roon et al. (2000) (Lesson 3).
  4. Decompose collateralized commodity futures returns into spot return, roll yield, and collateral yield, and demonstrate how roll yield drives long-term returns following Erb & Harvey (2006) (Lesson 4).
  5. Analyze the post-2004 financialization of commodities, institutional index flows, and cross-market correlation surges following Tang & Xiong (2012) (Lesson 5).
  6. Estimate a structural vector autoregression (SVAR) identifying oil supply, global aggregate demand, and precautionary demand shocks following Kilian (2009) (Lesson 5).
  7. Execute an end-to-end structural oil-shock decomposition across multiple historical episodes, defending identification against hostile critique (Project).

Placement diagnostic

Eight questions: six on prerequisite concepts from FC2 and F3, and two skill checks marked with data-skill testing skills taught in rungs 1 and 2 of the project ladder. The mastery bar is 80 percent (at least 7 correct of 8). To skip rungs 1 and 2, you must reach the mastery bar and answer both skill checks correctly.

  1. Under the classical financial cost-of-carry formula (FC2), if the spot price is \(S = 100\), the risk-free rate is \(r = 5.0\%\), storage cost is \(c = 2.0\%\), and there is no convenience yield (\(\delta = 0\)), what is the 1-year forward price \(F\) under continuous compounding?

    Options

    By the financial cost of carry with positive physical storage cost and zero convenience yield, \(F = S e^{(r + c)T} = 100 e^{0.07} \approx 107.2508\).

  2. Why does the reverse cash-and-carry arbitrage (short physical spot, long forward) break down in physical commodity markets (FC2)?

    Options

    Reverse cash-and-carry requires borrowing physical inventory to sell short. When aggregate inventory approaches zero (stockout), no physical units are available to borrow, causing the lower cost-of-carry bound to fail.

  3. In a futures margin account (FC2), if an investor holds 1 contract of crude oil (1,000 barrels) with an initial margin of $6,000 and maintenance margin of $4,500, what maximum drop in price per barrel ($) will trigger a margin call?

    The cushion before a margin call is \(\$6,000 - \$4,500 = \$1,500\). For 1,000 barrels, this corresponds to \(\$1,500 / 1,000 = \$1.50\) per barrel.

  4. In multi-asset factor investing (F3), how is the commodity carry factor typically defined across the cross-section of futures contracts?

    Options

    The carry factor ranks assets by their implied roll yield or basis slope \((S - F)/F\), taking long positions in high-carry (backwardated) contracts and short positions in low-carry (contango) contracts.

  5. In time-series momentum (F3), an investor evaluates a 12-month lookback return \(\text{TSMOM}_{t} = \text{sign}(r_{t-12, t})\). If a contract experienced positive returns in 9 out of the past 12 individual months with an annualized excess return of +12.0%, what is the annualized trend signal?

    The 12-month lookback return signal is positive (+1.0 position), capturing the +12.0% cumulative trend.

  6. What was the primary econometric critique of early pooled time-series momentum regressions identified in F3?

    Options

    Pooled panel regressions conflate static cross-sectional dispersion in average returns with true time-series predictability; de-meaning each asset removes this upward bias.

  7. Skill check (Rung 1 · Project): In Kilian's (2009) structural VAR, global crude oil production is ordered first, global real economic activity second, and real oil price third. Under recursive Cholesky identification, what is the exact contemporaneous impact \(P_{0, 1}\) of a global aggregate demand shock on world crude oil production within the same month?

    Under the recursive Cholesky zero restriction, crude oil production does not respond contemporaneously to demand shocks within the month (\(P_{0, 1} = 0.0\)), reflecting extraction and transportation delays.

  8. Skill check (Rung 2 · Project): In a 3-variable structural VAR, the forecast error variance of the real oil price at horizon \(H = 12\) months has cumulative shock contributions \(\text{MSE}_{\text{supply}} = 0.008\), \(\text{MSE}_{\text{demand}} = 0.062\), and \(\text{MSE}_{\text{spec}} = 0.020\). What is the sum of the normalized FEVD shares across all three structural shocks (in percent)?

    By construction, the normalized forecast error variance decomposition shares sum to exactly 1.0 (or 100.0%) across all orthogonal structural shocks.

Project ladder

The module concludes with a 25-hour capstone project: Oil-Shock Decomposition. The four-rung ladder structure guides you from guided execution to independent structural modeling:

  1. Rung 1 · Worked example

    Trace the complete estimation and decomposition of a 3-variable Kilian VAR(2) on seed 42, calculating Cholesky impact matrices and 24-month IRFs.

  2. Rung 2 · Completion problem

    Complete the core linear algebra algorithms for Cholesky factorization, structural shock extraction, and FEVD normalization.

  3. Rung 3 · Independent project

    Author the complete Python solver oil_var.py evaluated across 50 seeds, and author the analytical research memo report.md evaluated by rubric.

  4. Rung 4 · Stretch

    Extend the model to sign-restriction identification, narrative shocks, or 4-variable global inventory specifications.
