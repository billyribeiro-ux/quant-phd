Financialization, Structural VARs, and Oil-Shock Decomposition

For decades, commodities traded as segmented physical markets governed by local inventories, convenience yields, and producer hedging. The massive post-2004 inflow of institutional index funds linked commodity derivatives to global macro cycles and equity portfolios. In this capstone lesson, we examine the empirical evidence for Tang & Xiong (2012) commodity financialization. We then formalize the Kilian (2009) structural vector autoregression (SVAR) framework to disentangle crude oil supply shocks from global aggregate demand shocks and oil-market-specific precautionary demand shocks.

Commodity markets historically operated as segmented sectors priced by physical storage, supply bottlenecks, and commercial hedging. The post-2004 influx of institutional index capital fundamentally transformed commodity derivatives into an integrated financial asset class, sparking intense debate over whether index speculation distorted spot prices or whether emerging market demand dominated physical fundamentals. In macroeconomics, treating crude oil price spikes as exogenous cost shocks obscures the underlying economic drivers. Structural vector autoregressions resolve this identification challenge by disentangling physical supply disruptions from global aggregate demand and precautionary inventory accumulation. Mastering these macro-structural and financialization mechanisms is essential for modeling risk premia, stress-testing portfolios, and forecasting real asset returns.

1. The Financialization of Commodity Futures Markets

Prior to the early 2000s, commodity futures markets were dominated by commercial producers hedging inventory risks and specialized non-commercial commodity trading advisors (CTAs) seeking speculative gains, as formalized in our analysis of hedging pressure. Different commodity sectors—energy, grains, base metals, and softs—exhibited low cross-market correlations and negligible correlation with equity indices, making commodities premier portfolio diversifiers (Gorton & Rouwenhorst 2006).

Between 2004 and 2008, institutional investors (pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, endowment funds, and retail exchange-traded funds) allocated unprecedented capital to long-only commodity index funds, such as the S&P GSCI and the Bloomberg Commodity Index (BCOM, formerly DJ-AIG). Total assets under management in commodity index products grew from under $15 billion in 2003 to over $200 billion by mid-2008.

Definition 5.1 · Commodity Financialization The Financialization of commodities refers to the structural transition wherein commodity futures transformed from segmented physical risk-transfer venues into an integrated financial asset class driven by institutional portfolio flows, cross-asset rebalancing, and common financial liquidity.

As documented empirically by Tang & Xiong (2012), this institutional capital inflow produced three profound structural changes:

  1. Cross-Commodity Correlation Surge: Return correlations between fundamentally unrelated commodities included in the major indices (such as crude oil, soybeans, copper, and cattle) jumped from an average near zero prior to 2004 to over 0.50 post-2004. Commodities outside the major indices (off-index commodities) showed a significantly smaller increase in co-movement.
  2. Commodity-Equity Co-Movement: The historical negative correlation between commodity futures and equity markets documented by Gorton & Rouwenhorst (2006) reversed. During the 2008 financial crisis, commodity futures suffered catastrophic sell-offs synchronized with global equities as institutional investors faced margin calls and liquidated index holdings across all asset classes.
  3. Spillover of Financial Shocks: Index investment transmitted non-commodity financial shocks (e.g. shocks to risk tolerance, funding liquidity, and U.S. dollar exchange rates) directly into physical commodity pricing, weakening the pricing power of local convenience yield and inventory fundamentals.

2. The Speculation vs. Fundamentals Debate

The rapid rise of crude oil to $147 per barrel in July 2008 triggered an intense debate among policymakers, regulators, and academics. One group of commentators, echoing Michael Masters' testimony before the U.S. Senate in 2008, argued that institutional index speculators had created an artificial "financial bubble" in commodity futures, driving prices far above physical supply and demand fundamentals.

Subsequent rigorous academic research challenged the pure speculative bubble hypothesis. As Tang & Xiong (2012) and macroeconomic studies demonstrated, several key empirical facts contradict the speculative bubble narrative:

  1. Off-Index Commodities Rose Equally Fast: Physical commodities with no futures contracts or excluded from the major indices (such as iron ore, coal, apples, and cobalt) experienced price spikes of similar or greater magnitude over the 2003–2008 period. Because financial index funds could not trade these commodities, index speculation cannot explain their appreciation.
  2. Physical Inventory Accumulation Did Not Occur: If financial speculation had artificially driven market prices above physical clearing levels, consumers would have reduced consumption and physical producers would have flooded commercial storage, leading to massive inventory accumulation. In reality, global commercial inventories remained flat or declined during the 2003–2008 run-up, indicating that rapid physical consumption absorbed global output.
  3. Emerging Market Industrialization: Rapid industrial growth and infrastructure expansion in emerging economies—predominantly China and India—created an unprecedented surge in global real aggregate demand for physical raw materials.

3. The Kilian (2009) Structural VAR Framework

Traditional macroeconomic analyses often treated crude oil price fluctuations as exogenous supply shocks (e.g., OPEC embargoes or Middle Eastern geopolitical conflicts). In a seminal paper, Kilian (2009) demonstrated that crude oil price shocks are endogenous to the global macroeconomy and must be decomposed structurally to evaluate their true economic consequences.

Definition 5.2 · Oil-Shock Decomposition The Structural decomposition of oil-price shocks is an identification methodology based on structural vector autoregressions that isolates orthogonal economic drivers of crude oil markets into three structural innovations: crude oil supply shocks, global aggregate demand shocks, and oil-market-specific precautionary demand shocks.

Consider a monthly 3-variable vector of macroeconomic and commodity time series:

\[y_t = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta \text{prod}_t \\ \text{rea}_t \\ \text{rpo}_t \end{bmatrix}\]

where:

  1. \(\Delta \text{prod}_t\) is the percentage change in world crude oil production.
  2. \(\text{rea}_t\) is Kilian's index of global real economic activity, constructed from dry cargo ocean shipping freight rates detrended to eliminate long-term secular shipbuilding trends.
  3. \(\text{rpo}_t\) is the log of the real price of crude oil (nominal Brent or WTI crude oil price deflated by the U.S. Consumer Price Index).

4. Structural Identification via Recursive Zero Restrictions

Following standard structural time-series econometrics (Hamilton 1994), the structural VAR(p) representation is expressed as:

\[A_0 y_t = c_0 + \sum_{i=1}^p A_i y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t\]

where \(\varepsilon_t = [\varepsilon_{1, t}, \varepsilon_{2, t}, \varepsilon_{3, t}]'\) is a vector of mutually uncorrelated, structural economic shocks with mean zero and identity covariance matrix \(\E[\varepsilon_t \varepsilon_t'] = I_3\). Pre-multiplying by \(A_0^{-1}\) yields the reduced-form VAR:

\[y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^p B_i y_{t-i} + u_t\]

where \(B_i = A_0^{-1} A_i\), \(c = A_0^{-1} c_0\), and the reduced-form innovation vector is \(u_t = A_0^{-1} \varepsilon_t\). The reduced-form error covariance matrix is:

\[\Sigma_u = \E[u_t u_t'] = A_0^{-1} \E[\varepsilon_t \varepsilon_t'] (A_0^{-1})' = A_0^{-1} (A_0^{-1})' = P P'\]

Because \(\Sigma_u\) is symmetric, it contains \(K(K+1)/2 = 3(4)/2 = 6\) free parameters, whereas the structural impact matrix \(P = A_0^{-1}\) contains \(K^2 = 9\) parameters. To achieve exact identification, Kilian imposes \(K(K-1)/2 = 3\) contemporaneous zero restrictions by specifying \(P\) as lower triangular via the Cholesky factorization of \(\Sigma_u\):

\[u_t = \begin{bmatrix} u_t^{\Delta \text{prod}} \\ u_t^{\text{rea}} \\ u_t^{\text{rpo}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} P_{11} & 0 & 0 \\ P_{21} & P_{22} & 0 \\ P_{31} & P_{32} & P_{33} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_t^{\text{supply}} \\ \varepsilon_t^{\text{aggregate demand}} \\ \varepsilon_t^{\text{oil-specific demand}} \end{bmatrix}\]

Economic Foundations of the Recursive Identification Kilian (2009) justifies the lower-triangular zero restrictions through physical production constraints and operational information lags:

  1. Predetermined Oil Supply (\(P_{12} = 0, P_{13} = 0\)): Crude oil production cannot respond contemporaneously (within the same calendar month) to global aggregate demand shocks or oil-specific precautionary demand shocks. Expanding oil extraction requires capital expenditure, pipeline reconfiguration, and physical drilling that take months to operationalize.
  2. Predetermined Global Real Activity (\(P_{23} = 0\)): Global real economic activity (shipping freight rates and industrial output) responds contemporaneously to oil supply disruptions, but does not react within the same month to oil-market-specific precautionary demand shocks.
  3. Asset Price Flexibility (\(P_{31}, P_{32}, P_{33} \neq 0\)): As a forward-looking asset price, the real price of crude oil responds immediately and contemporaneously to all three structural innovations within the month.

5. The Three Structural Innovations and Their Economic Signatures

The structural shocks identified by Kilian (2009) exhibit distinct economic profiles:

  1. Crude Oil Supply Shock (\(\varepsilon_t^{\text{supply}}\)): Exogenous innovations to physical crude oil production. A negative supply shock (e.g. pipeline disruption, geopolitical conflict damaging infrastructure) lowers world oil production, induces a slight temporary contraction in global real activity, and temporarily raises real oil prices.
  2. Global Aggregate Demand Shock (\(\varepsilon_t^{\text{aggregate demand}}\)): Innovations to global industrial production and macroeconomic demand driven by worldwide business cycle expansions. A positive aggregate demand shock raises global real activity persistently, drives up crude oil production with a lag, and causes a large, persistent increase in real oil prices.
  3. Oil-Market-Specific Precautionary Demand Shock (\(\varepsilon_t^{\text{oil-specific demand}}\)): Innovations driven by shifts in expectations regarding future petroleum supply shortfalls relative to future demand. When market participants fear future supply disruptions, their convenience yield increases, prompting them to accumulate physical commercial inventories under the theory of storage. This precautionary demand immediately drives up real spot and futures prices.

6. Impulse Responses and Forecast Error Variance Decomposition (FEVD)

Using the structural moving average (VMA) representation, the system can be written as:

\[y_t = \mu + \sum_{j=0}^\infty \Phi_j u_{t-j} = \mu + \sum_{j=0}^\infty \Phi_j P \varepsilon_{t-j} = \mu + \sum_{j=0}^\infty \Theta_j \varepsilon_{t-j}\]

where \(\Phi_0 = I_K\), \(\Phi_j = \sum_{k=1}^{\min(j, p)} B_k \Phi_{j-k}\), and \(\Theta_j = \Phi_j P\) is the structural impulse response matrix at horizon \(j\). The \(h\)-step ahead forecast error is:

\[y_{t+h} - \hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \sum_{j=0}^{h-1} \Theta_j \varepsilon_{t+h-j}\]

The forecast error variance decomposition (FEVD) calculates the fraction of the mean squared prediction error of variable \(i\) attributable to structural shock \(j\) over horizon \(h\):

\[\omega_{ij}(h) = \frac{\sum_{l=0}^{h-1} \Theta_{l, ij}^2}{\sum_{m=1}^K \sum_{l=0}^{h-1} \Theta_{l, im}^2}, \quad \sum_{j=1}^K \omega_{ij}(h) = 1.0\]

Kilian (2009) Empirical Findings on Real Oil Prices Across long-term historical data from 1973 through 2007:

  1. Supply Shocks Play a Minor Role: Crude oil supply shocks account for less than 10% of the forecast error variance of the real price of oil at all forecast horizons.
  2. Demand Shocks Dominate: Global aggregate demand shocks and oil-specific precautionary demand shocks account for over 90% of the long-term forecast error variance of real oil prices.
  3. Episode Decomposition: The 1979–1980 price spike and the 2003–2008 price surge were driven predominantly by global aggregate demand and precautionary demand rather than physical supply curtailments.

7. Python Laboratory: Structural VAR Estimation & Decomposition

The following laboratory script estimates Kilian's 3-variable SVAR(2) on monthly data, calculates the lower-triangular impact matrix \(P\), verifies the recursive identification restrictions, and evaluates the FEVD of real oil prices across horizons from 0 to 18 months.

Lab 1 · Kilian (2009) Structural VAR Oil-Shock Decomposition

Estimating the 3-variable structural VAR will confirm that recursive zero restrictions isolate orthogonal supply, aggregate demand, and precautionary demand shocks, with demand shocks accounting for over 85% of real oil price variance.

"""Lab X3-L05-1: Kilian (2009) Structural VAR: Disentangling Demand and Supply Oil Shocks.

Estimates the 3-variable structural VAR model identifying crude oil supply shocks,
global aggregate demand shocks, and oil-market-specific precautionary demand shocks.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def cholesky(a_mat: list[list[float]]) -> list[list[float]]:
    """Compute lower-triangular Cholesky factorization."""
    n = len(a_mat)
    l_mat = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1):
            s = sum(l_mat[i][k] * l_mat[j][k] for k in range(j))
            if i == j:
                val = a_mat[i][i] - s
                if val <= 1e-14:
                    raise ValueError("Matrix is not positive definite")
                l_mat[i][j] = math.sqrt(val)
            else:
                l_mat[i][j] = (a_mat[i][j] - s) / l_mat[j][j]
    return l_mat


def invert_matrix(a_mat: list[list[float]]) -> list[list[float]]:
    """Invert square matrix via Gauss-Jordan elimination."""
    n = len(a_mat)
    m = [row[:] + [1.0 if i == j else 0.0 for j in range(n)] for i, row in enumerate(a_mat)]
    for i in range(n):
        max_row = max(range(i, n), key=lambda r: abs(m[r][i]))
        if abs(m[max_row][i]) < 1e-12:
            raise ValueError("Matrix is singular")
        m[i], m[max_row] = m[max_row], m[i]
        pivot = m[i][i]
        for c in range(2 * n):
            m[i][c] /= pivot
        for r in range(n):
            if r != i:
                factor = m[r][i]
                for c in range(2 * n):
                    m[r][c] -= factor * m[i][c]
    return [[m[r][c + n] for c in range(n)] for r in range(n)]


def matmul(a_mat: list[list[float]], b_mat: list[list[float]]) -> list[list[float]]:
    """Matrix multiplication."""
    n = len(a_mat)
    m = len(b_mat[0])
    p = len(b_mat)
    return [[sum(a_mat[i][k] * b_mat[k][j] for k in range(p)) for j in range(m)] for i in range(n)]


def transpose(a_mat: list[list[float]]) -> list[list[float]]:
    """Matrix transpose."""
    return [[a_mat[i][j] for i in range(len(a_mat))] for j in range(len(a_mat[0]))]


def estimate_var(data: list[list[float]], lags: int = 2) -> tuple[list[list[list[float]]], list[list[float]]]:
    """Estimate VAR(p) returning lag coefficients and reduced-form covariance Sigma_u."""
    t_obs = len(data)
    k_vars = len(data[0])
    n_eff = t_obs - lags
    num_params = 1 + k_vars * lags

    x_mat = []
    y_mat = []
    for t in range(lags, t_obs):
        row_x = [1.0]
        for lag_idx in range(1, lags + 1):
            row_x.extend(data[t - lag_idx])
        x_mat.append(row_x)
        y_mat.append(data[t][:])

    xt = transpose(x_mat)
    xtx_inv = invert_matrix(matmul(xt, x_mat))
    b_hat = matmul(xtx_inv, matmul(xt, y_mat))

    xb = matmul(x_mat, b_hat)
    e_mat = [[y_mat[t][k] - xb[t][k] for k in range(k_vars)] for t in range(n_eff)]
    et = transpose(e_mat)
    ete = matmul(et, e_mat)
    dof = float(n_eff - num_params)
    sigma_u = [[ete[i][j] / dof for j in range(k_vars)] for i in range(k_vars)]

    coefficients: list[list[list[float]]] = []
    for lag_idx in range(lags):
        bl: list[list[float]] = []
        for i in range(k_vars):
            row: list[float] = []
            for j in range(k_vars):
                idx = 1 + lag_idx * k_vars + j
                row.append(b_hat[idx][i])
            bl.append(row)
        coefficients.append(bl)

    return coefficients, sigma_u


def compute_irf_and_fevd(
    coefficients: list[list[list[float]]], p_mat: list[list[float]], horizon: int = 18
) -> tuple[list[list[list[float]]], list[list[list[float]]]]:
    """Compute structural IRFs and FEVD shares."""
    k_vars = len(p_mat)
    p_lags = len(coefficients)

    phi: list[list[list[float]]] = [[[1.0 if i == j else 0.0 for j in range(k_vars)] for i in range(k_vars)]]
    for h in range(1, horizon + 1):
        phi_h = [[0.0] * k_vars for _ in range(k_vars)]
        for j in range(1, min(h, p_lags) + 1):
            b_phi = matmul(coefficients[j - 1], phi[h - j])
            for r in range(k_vars):
                for c in range(k_vars):
                    phi_h[r][c] += b_phi[r][c]
        phi.append(phi_h)

    irfs: list[list[list[float]]] = []
    for h in range(horizon + 1):
        theta_h = matmul(phi[h], p_mat)
        irfs.append([[round(theta_h[i][j], 4) for j in range(k_vars)] for i in range(k_vars)])

    fevd: list[list[list[float]]] = []
    cum_mse = [[0.0] * k_vars for _ in range(k_vars)]
    for h in range(horizon + 1):
        fevd_h = [[0.0] * k_vars for _ in range(k_vars)]
        for i in range(k_vars):
            for j in range(k_vars):
                cum_mse[i][j] += irfs[h][i][j] ** 2
            tot = sum(cum_mse[i])
            for j in range(k_vars):
                fevd_h[i][j] = round(cum_mse[i][j] / tot, 4) if tot > 1e-12 else 0.0
        fevd.append(fevd_h)

    return irfs, fevd


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab X3-L05-1 | Kilian (2009) Structural VAR Oil-Shock Decomposition")
    print("-" * 75)

    # Generate synthetic monthly data matching Kilian (2009) moments
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n_obs = 150
    k_vars = 3
    true_p = [
        [1.20, 0.0, 0.0],
        [0.60, 8.00, 0.0],
        [-0.03, 0.04, 0.12],
    ]
    b1 = [
        [0.15, 0.01, -0.05],
        [0.50, 0.70, -0.50],
        [0.02, 0.008, 0.82],
    ]
    b2 = [
        [0.05, 0.005, -0.02],
        [-0.20, 0.15, -0.20],
        [-0.01, 0.002, 0.06],
    ]

    y = [[0.10, 0.0, 4.0] for _ in range(2)]
    for _ in range(n_obs):
        eps = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(k_vars)]
        e = [sum(true_p[i][j] * eps[j] for j in range(k_vars)) for i in range(k_vars)]
        yt = [
            0.05
            + sum(b1[i][j] * y[-1][j] for j in range(k_vars))
            + sum(b2[i][j] * y[-2][j] for j in range(k_vars))
            + e[i]
            for i in range(k_vars)
        ]
        y.append(yt)

    data = y[2:]
    coeffs, sigma_u = estimate_var(data, lags=2)
    p_mat = cholesky(sigma_u)
    _irfs, fevd = compute_irf_and_fevd(coeffs, p_mat, horizon=18)

    print("Estimated Contemporaneous Impact Matrix P (A_0^-1):")
    print(f"  [Delta Prod]  {p_mat[0][0]:8.4f}  {p_mat[0][1]:8.4f}  {p_mat[0][2]:8.4f}")
    print(f"  [Real Act]    {p_mat[1][0]:8.4f}  {p_mat[1][1]:8.4f}  {p_mat[1][2]:8.4f}")
    print(f"  [Real Price]  {p_mat[2][0]:8.4f}  {p_mat[2][1]:8.4f}  {p_mat[2][2]:8.4f}")
    print("-" * 75)
    print("Verification of Recursive Cholesky Zero Restrictions:")
    print(f"  P[0, 1] = {p_mat[0][1]:.4f} == 0 (Supply predetermined to agg demand)")
    print(f"  P[0, 2] = {p_mat[0][2]:.4f} == 0 (Supply predetermined to oil-specific demand)")
    print(f"  P[1, 2] = {p_mat[1][2]:.4f} == 0 (Real activity predetermined to oil-specific demand)")
    print("-" * 75)

    print("Forecast Error Variance Decomposition (FEVD) of Real Oil Price (rpo):")

Where Standard Financial Theory Breaks Down

Traditional finance and macro asset pricing models frequently treat commodity price shocks as exogenous cost-push innovations (e.g. treating oil price spikes as adverse supply shocks that trigger stagflation). This assumption fails both theoretically and empirically:

  1. Endogenous Price Shocks: When oil prices rise because the global economy is booming (a positive aggregate demand shock), corporate earnings, shipping volumes, and equity returns expand alongside oil prices. An asset manager hedging an equity portfolio who assumes oil price spikes always hurt stocks will find that during demand-driven expansions, oil and equities move in the same direction.
  2. Reverse Causality in Inflation Pass-Through: Conventional regression models that regress CPI inflation on commodity prices fail to recognize that both inflation and commodity prices are jointly determined by underlying global aggregate demand and monetary liquidity.
  3. Failure of Static Beta Models: Following post-2004 financialization, commodity betas with equities became time-varying and regime-dependent. In calm periods, commodities provide diversification; in financial distress with balance-sheet constraints, institutional deleveraging triggers severe co-liquidation.

Misconceptions

  • Every historical oil price spike was caused by an OPEC supply cut.

    Popular media and historical accounts attribute major oil crises to OPEC supply manipulation. As Kilian (2009) proved econometrically, physical crude oil supply disruptions accounted for under 10% of oil price fluctuations. Precautionary inventory demand and global aggregate demand account for over 90% of price variance.

  • Financial speculators were the sole cause of the 2008 commodity price peak.

    While index investment increased cross-commodity correlations (Tang & Xiong 2012), commodities without futures contracts (such as iron ore and coal) rose just as dramatically as crude oil and copper. Physical inventories did not build up in commercial storage, confirming that strong physical consumption rather than purely financial hoarding cleared the market.

  • Cholesky factorization is purely an automated statistical technique without economic assumptions.

    Imposing a Cholesky decomposition on a reduced-form covariance matrix embeds strict economic causal ordering. Setting \(P_{12} = 0\) asserts that world oil production is completely inelastic to global aggregate demand within the month. If this economic assumption is false, the resulting structural shocks and impulse responses are biased.

Problem 5.1 · Cholesky Impact Matrix Calculation

Suppose an estimated 2-variable reduced-form error covariance matrix is:

\[\Sigma_u = \begin{bmatrix} 4.0 & 2.0 \\ 2.0 & 10.0 \end{bmatrix}\]

Compute the lower-triangular structural impact matrix \(P\) such that \(P P' = \Sigma_u\).

Solution

Let \(P = \begin{bmatrix} P_{11} & 0 \\ P_{21} & P_{22} \end{bmatrix}\). Then:

\[P P' = \begin{bmatrix} P_{11}^2 & P_{11} P_{21} \\ P_{11} P_{21} & P_{21}^2 + P_{22}^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4.0 & 2.0 \\ 2.0 & 10.0 \end{bmatrix}\]
  1. \(P_{11}^2 = 4.0 \implies P_{11} = 2.0\).
  2. \(P_{11} P_{21} = 2.0 \implies 2.0 P_{21} = 2.0 \implies P_{21} = 1.0\).
  3. \(P_{21}^2 + P_{22}^2 = 10.0 \implies 1.0^2 + P_{22}^2 = 10.0 \implies P_{22}^2 = 9.0 \implies P_{22} = 3.0\).

Thus, the lower-triangular impact matrix is \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 2.0 & 0.0 \\ 1.0 & 3.0 \end{bmatrix}\).

Problem 5.2 · Structural Impulse Response at Horizon 1

Consider a 2-variable VAR(1) with lag matrix \(B_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.7 \end{bmatrix}\) and impact matrix \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 2.0 & 0.0 \\ 1.0 & 3.0 \end{bmatrix}\). Compute the structural impulse response matrix \(\Theta_1\) at horizon 1.

Solution

In a VAR(1), the reduced-form VMA coefficient at lag 1 is \(\Phi_1 = B_1\). The structural impulse response matrix is \(\Theta_1 = \Phi_1 P = B_1 P\):

\[\Theta_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2.0 & 0.0 \\ 1.0 & 3.0 \end{bmatrix}\]
  1. \(\Theta_{1, 11} = 0.5(2.0) + 0.1(1.0) = 1.0 + 0.1 = 1.1\).
  2. \(\Theta_{1, 12} = 0.5(0.0) + 0.1(3.0) = 0.3\).
  3. \(\Theta_{1, 21} = 0.2(2.0) + 0.7(1.0) = 0.4 + 0.7 = 1.1\).
  4. \(\Theta_{1, 22} = 0.2(0.0) + 0.7(3.0) = 2.1\).

Thus, \(\Theta_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1.1 & 0.3 \\ 1.1 & 2.1 \end{bmatrix}\).

Problem 5.3 · 2-Horizon Forecast Error Variance Decomposition

For the system in Problems 5.1 and 5.2, compute the 2-step forecast error variance of variable 2, and the percentage explained by shock 1 and shock 2 over horizon \(h = 2\).

Solution

For variable 2, the structural responses at horizon 0 are \(\Theta_{0, 21} = P_{21} = 1.0\) and \(\Theta_{0, 22} = P_{22} = 3.0\). At horizon 1, \(\Theta_{1, 21} = 1.1\) and \(\Theta_{1, 22} = 2.1\).

The cumulative squared responses over horizons \(j = 0, 1\) are:

  1. Contribution of shock 1: \(\Theta_{0, 21}^2 + \Theta_{1, 21}^2 = 1.0^2 + 1.1^2 = 1.0 + 1.21 = 2.21\).
  2. Contribution of shock 2: \(\Theta_{0, 22}^2 + \Theta_{1, 22}^2 = 3.0^2 + 2.1^2 = 9.0 + 4.41 = 13.41\).
  3. Total 2-step forecast error variance: \(2.21 + 13.41 = 15.62\).

The FEVD shares are:

\[\omega_{21}(2) = \frac{2.21}{15.62} \approx 0.1415 \quad (14.15\%), \quad \omega_{22}(2) = \frac{13.41}{15.62} \approx 0.8585 \quad (85.85\%)\]

Proof Problems

Problem 5.4 · Orthogonality and Variance Invariance of Structural Shocks

Prove that if \(u_t = P \varepsilon_t\), where \(\Sigma_u = P P'\) is the Cholesky factorization of \(\Sigma_u\) and \(\varepsilon_t \sim (0, I_K)\), then \(\E[u_t u_t'] = \Sigma_u\) and the structural shocks \(\varepsilon_t = P^{-1} u_t\) are mutually uncorrelated with unit variance.

Solution
  1. Reduced-form covariance: \[\E[u_t u_t'] = \E[(P \varepsilon_t)(P \varepsilon_t)'] = P \E[\varepsilon_t \varepsilon_t'] P' = P I_K P' = P P' = \Sigma_u\]
  2. Structural shock covariance: Since \(\Sigma_u\) is positive definite, \(P\) is non-singular. Inverting \(u_t = P \varepsilon_t\) gives \(\varepsilon_t = P^{-1} u_t\). The covariance is: \[\E[\varepsilon_t \varepsilon_t'] = \E[(P^{-1} u_t)(P^{-1} u_t)'] = P^{-1} \E[u_t u_t'] (P^{-1})' = P^{-1} \Sigma_u (P')^{-1}\] Substituting \(\Sigma_u = P P'\): \[\E[\varepsilon_t \varepsilon_t'] = P^{-1} (P P') (P')^{-1} = (P^{-1} P) (P' (P')^{-1}) = I_K I_K = I_K\]

Hence, \(\E[\varepsilon_{i, t} \varepsilon_{j, t}] = 1\) if \(i = j\) and \(0\) if \(i \neq j\), proving exact mutual orthogonality and unit variance.

Problem 5.5 · Order Condition for Exact Structural Identification

In a \(K\)-variable structural VAR with \(u_t = A_0^{-1} \varepsilon_t\), prove that the lower-triangular Cholesky identification imposes exactly the number of restrictions required for exact identification.

Solution

The reduced-form error covariance matrix \(\Sigma_u\) is symmetric of dimension \(K \times K\). The number of distinct non-redundant parameters in \(\Sigma_u\) is the number of lower-triangular and diagonal elements:

\[N_{\text{reduced}} = \frac{K(K + 1)}{2}\]

The structural impact matrix \(P = A_0^{-1}\) contains \(K^2\) unknown parameters. The matrix relation \(\Sigma_u = P P'\) provides \(K(K+1)/2\) equations. To achieve exact identification (\(N_{\text{equations}} = N_{\text{unknowns}}\)), the system requires:

\[N_{\text{restrictions}} = K^2 - \frac{K(K + 1)}{2} = \frac{2K^2 - K^2 - K}{2} = \frac{K(K - 1)}{2}\]

A lower-triangular matrix \(P\) sets all strictly upper-triangular elements to zero. The number of strictly upper-triangular entries in a \(K \times K\) matrix is precisely \(\frac{K(K - 1)}{2}\). Therefore, the Cholesky specification satisfies the necessary order condition for exact structural identification.

Problem 5.6 · Convergence of Cumulative Structural IRFs

For a stable VAR(p) whose characteristic polynomial \(\det(I - \sum_{i=1}^p B_i z^i) \neq 0\) has all roots outside the unit circle \(|z| > 1\), prove that the cumulative impulse response function \(\Xi_h = \sum_{j=0}^h \Theta_j\) converges as \(h \to \infty\) to the long-run structural multiplier \((I - \sum_{i=1}^p B_i)^{-1} P\).

Solution

From the structural VMA representation, \(\Theta_j = \Phi_j P\), where \(\Phi_j\) are the coefficients of the polynomial matrix \(\Phi(L) = B(L)^{-1}\), with \(B(L) = I - \sum_{i=1}^p B_i L^i\). Thus:

\[\Xi_h = \sum_{j=0}^h \Theta_j = \left(\sum_{j=0}^h \Phi_j\right) P\]

Because the roots of \(\det(B(z)) = 0\) lie strictly outside the unit circle, the power series \(\sum_{j=0}^\infty \Phi_j z^j = B(z)^{-1}\) converges absolutely for all \(|z| \le 1\). Evaluating at \(z = 1\):

\[\lim_{h \to \infty} \sum_{j=0}^h \Phi_j = \Phi(1) = B(1)^{-1} = \left(I - \sum_{i=1}^p B_i\right)^{-1}\]

Multiplying by \(P\) gives the long-run cumulative structural multiplier:

\[\lim_{h \to \infty} \Xi_h = \left(I - \sum_{i=1}^p B_i\right)^{-1} P\]

Empirical Problems

Problem 5.7 · Tang & Xiong (2012) Cross-Commodity Correlation Shifts

Explain how Tang & Xiong (2012) differentiated the financialization effect from macroeconomic demand shocks by comparing index commodities to off-index commodities.

Solution

If global macroeconomic demand (e.g. Chinese infrastructure growth) were the sole factor driving commodity markets, both index commodities (e.g. crude oil, copper, corn) and off-index commodities (e.g. coal, cobalt, zinc, rhodium) would experience a similar rise in co-movement with each other and with equities. Tang & Xiong (2012) utilized a difference-in-differences empirical design: they showed that while off-index commodities saw modest correlation gains, index-member commodities experienced a massive, statistically significant upward jump in pairwise correlations and equity beta starting in 2004. This excess co-movement among index members confirms that institutional index investment flows created financial contagion beyond fundamental economic drivers.

Problem 5.8 · Kilian (2009) Historical Decomposition of the 2008 Spike

In historical decomposition, how did Kilian (2009) demonstrate that the 2003–2008 oil price run-up was driven by aggregate demand rather than physical supply curtailments?

Solution

Kilian's historical decomposition constructs the counterfactual path of the real price of oil had only a single structural shock occurred over time: \(\hat{y}_{t, \text{shock } j} = \sum_{l=0}^{t-1} \Theta_l \varepsilon_{t-l, j}\). The empirical results showed that the counterfactual path driven solely by physical oil supply shocks was virtually flat between 2003 and 2008. In contrast, the counterfactual path driven by global aggregate demand shocks tracked the dramatic upward surge in real oil prices almost perfectly, peaking in mid-2008 before collapsing during the global financial crisis.

Problem 5.9 · Precautionary Demand and Forward Curve Regimes

How does an oil-specific precautionary demand shock alter the commodity forward curve slope between backwardation and contango?

Solution

Under the theory of storage, forward prices satisfy \(F(t, T) = S_t e^{(r + c - \delta)(T - t)}\). When market participants experience a precautionary demand shock (e.g. rising geopolitical uncertainty), the marginal convenience yield \(\delta\) surges as refiners and governments compete to secure physical barrels immediately. When \(\delta > r + c\), the forward curve shifts into steep backwardation. Front-month prices rise by more than deferred contracts, generating a positive roll yield for long futures positions.

Research Problems

Problem 5.10 · Sign Restrictions vs. Cholesky Identification

Critique Kilian's assumption that oil supply is completely inelastic contemporaneously (\(P_{12} = 0, P_{13} = 0\)). What alternative identification methodology allows for a non-zero contemporaneous price elasticity of oil supply?

Solution

Critics argue that even within a month, swing producers (such as Saudi Arabia) or strategic petroleum reserve (SPR) releases can adjust production in response to price spikes, implying \(P_{13} > 0\). If the true supply elasticity is positive, Cholesky underestimates the role of supply shocks. To address this limitation, researchers employ sign-restricted SVARs (e.g. Baumeister & Hamilton 2019): instead of imposing exact zero restrictions, sign restrictions require that a positive supply shock increases production and lowers price, while a positive demand shock increases both production and price, yielding identification robust to non-zero short-run supply elasticity.

Problem 5.11 · Financialization and the Negative Oil Event of April 2020

Analyze how the financialization of commodity retail products (e.g. retail oil ETFs) contributed to the May 2020 WTI contract trading at -$37.63 per barrel on April 20, 2020.

Solution

During the COVID-19 demand collapse, retail investors poured record capital into the United States Oil Fund (USO), betting on a quick oil rebound. Because USO held an enormous share (over 25%) of the front-month May 2020 WTI open interest, it was forced by its prospectus rules to roll contracts out of May into June before expiration. Meanwhile, physical storage tanks at Cushing, Oklahoma were fully committed. Financial traders could not take physical delivery because they lacked pipeline storage access. When long financial holders rushed to liquidate contracts ahead of delivery with zero physical storage bids available, the clearing price plunged into negative territory to compensate storage owners for taking delivery.

Problem 5.12 · High-Frequency Identification vs. Monthly SVARs

How do modern macroeconomic studies combine high-frequency futures price changes around OPEC announcement windows with structural VARs to identify oil supply shocks?

Solution

Monthly SVARs face time-aggregation bias because announcements and physical reactions occur at daily frequencies. High-frequency identification (HFI) measures the change in crude oil futures prices in a tight 30-minute window surrounding OPEC press releases. Under the asset-pricing assumption that no other major macroeconomic news occurred during that 30-minute window, the price change represents an exogenous surprise to oil supply expectations. This high-frequency surprise series is then used as an external instrument (proxy SVAR) to identify the structural supply shock in monthly or quarterly macroeconomic models.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What structural shift defines the "financialization of commodities" documented by Tang & Xiong (2012)?

    Options

    Tang & Xiong (2012) demonstrated that institutional index investment integrated previously segmented commodity markets into the broader financial system, sharply increasing pairwise correlations.

  2. In Kilian's (2009) SVAR, what economic rationale justifies setting \(P_{12} = 0\) and \(P_{13} = 0\) in the contemporaneous impact matrix?

    Options

    High capital intensity and operational planning horizons prevent physical oil producers from adjusting monthly crude output instantaneously to global demand innovations.

  3. According to Kilian (2009), which structural shock explained the predominant share of the 2003–2008 surge in real crude oil prices?

    Options

    Historical decomposition proves that global aggregate demand shocks and precautionary demand shocks drove the 2003–2008 run-up; physical supply disruptions were negligible.

  4. According to Kilian's (2009) variance decomposition, crude oil supply disruptions historically account for less than what percentage of long-term real oil price variance?

    Crude oil supply shocks account for less than 10% of the long-term forecast error variance of real oil prices; demand shocks account for over 90%.

  5. Why did empirical researchers reject the hypothesis that financial index speculation was the sole driver of the 2008 commodity price peak?

    Options

    Off-index commodities (e.g. iron ore and coal) surged alongside index commodities, and global commercial inventories did not accumulate, refuting a pure financial hoarding bubble.

  6. In a 3-variable structural VAR, how many zero restrictions are required on the contemporaneous impact matrix to achieve exact Cholesky identification (\(K(K-1)/2\))?

    \(K(K - 1) / 2 = 3(2) / 2 = 3\) zero restrictions.

  7. How does an oil-specific precautionary demand shock affect physical commercial inventories under the theory of storage?

    Options

    Uncertainty regarding future availability elevates the convenience yield, incentivizing inventory accumulation under the Kaldor-Working-Brennan theory of storage.

  8. What ensures that the Forecast Error Variance Decomposition (FEVD) shares across all structural shocks sum to exactly 1.0 (100%) at every horizon?

    Options

    Because structural shocks are orthogonal (\(\E[\varepsilon_{i, t} \varepsilon_{j, t}] = 0\) for \(i \neq j\)), total forecast error variance decomposes additively across shock contributions.
