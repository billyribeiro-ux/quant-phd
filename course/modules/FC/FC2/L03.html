A futures contract (Definition 1.1) is a forward contract whose gains and losses are paid every day, and a swap is an exchange of interest payments whose fixed rate must be computed. Both claims need a precise statement. A futures position's cash flows arrive day by day, and under A8′ (the margin account earns nothing) their total is fixed by the prices alone. A swap's fixed rate is the one that gives the swap zero value at the start, and it is computed from discount factors. This lesson proves both results. The lab checks the margining identity on a seeded SIMULATED price path, in integer cents, and computes the fixed rate that gives a swap zero value exactly.

1Futures contracts and daily settlement

Definition 1.1 (futures contract)

A futures contract is a standardized agreement to buy or sell an asset at a price fixed when the contract is traded, with delivery at a stated date. It is traded on an exchange, where a clearing house stands between the two parties. The contract differs from the forward contract of Lesson 2 in one respect that matters here: gains and losses are settled every day through the clearing house, not only at expiry.

Definition 1.2 (futures margining)

Let \(F_i\) be the futures price at the close of day \(i\), for \(i = 0, 1, \dots, n\), where \(F_0\) is the price at which the position was taken and \(F_n\) is the settlement price at expiry. The variation margin to the long holder on day \(i\), per unit, is \(\Delta_i = F_i - F_{i-1}\). A short holder receives \(-\Delta_i\). Crediting or debiting these amounts each day is marking to market.

Assumption A8′ (the margin account earns nothing)

The margin account earns no interest, and the only cash flows of the position are the daily variation margins of Definition 1.2. The primed label distinguishes it from A8, the single-curve assumption of Section 3.

Proposition 1.3 (the margining identity)

Assume A8′. For a long futures position taken at \(F_0\), let the account balance after day \(i\) be \(B_i\), with \(B_0 = 0\) and \(B_i = B_{i-1} + \Delta_i\). Then \(B_i = F_i - F_0\) for every \(i\), and in particular the sum of the daily variation margins over days 1 to \(n\) equals \(F_n - F_0\).

Proof

Write \(\Delta_i = F_i - F_{i-1}\) and sum over the days. Then \(B_i = \sum_{k=1}^{i} \Delta_k = \sum_{k=1}^{i} (F_k - F_{k-1})\). In this sum each intermediate \(F_k\), for \(1 \le k \le i - 1\), is added once as the end of term \(k\) and subtracted once as the start of term \(k + 1\), so the sum telescopes to \(F_i - F_0\). Setting \(i = n\) gives the second claim. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.4 (what the identity uses)

Proposition 1.3 holds for any sequence of prices, because it is an identity about sums and needs no model of how prices move. It uses A8′, and it does not use the relation between \(F_n\) and the spot price at expiry. Margin requirements that the clearing house sets, such as initial or maintenance margin, are not modelled here, and the identity says nothing about whether the account is large enough to meet them.

Remark 1.5 (the forward contract is not the benchmark for this identity)

A forward contract of Lesson 2 pays once, at expiry. A long futures position pays its variation margins day by day, and by Proposition 1.3 their sum is \(F_n - F_0\). This lesson does not compare the two totals and does not derive any relation between the futures price and the forward price of Lesson 2. Proposition 1.3 does not need one.

2Why the timing of margin matters

Suppose the futures price falls below the entry price for some days and then recovers. Proposition 1.3 gives the balance \(B_i = F_i - F_0\) on each of those days, which is negative, while the balance at expiry can still be positive. The balance is the cumulative mark to market of the position, and a holder who must meet a loss in cash faces the path of that balance, not its final value. Lab 3, Part A, prints four loss days and the final balance of 84 cents, and it checks the balance against \(F_i - F_0\) on every day.

3Interest-rate swaps and the par rate

A swap exchanges two streams of payments on a notional amount, which is not itself exchanged. The stream here is a fixed rate against a floating rate, with payments at dates \(1, \dots, n\) and one year between dates. Per unit of notional the lesson works with unit amounts, and the notional in dollars is scaled at the end. The discount factor \(\mathrm{DF}_i\) is the present value at date 0 of one dollar paid at date \(i\), and \(\mathrm{DF}_0 = 1\). The zero rate \(z_i\) to date \(i\) is the annual rate that gives \(\mathrm{DF}_i = (1 + z_i)^{-i}\).

Definition 3.1 (interest-rate swap)

An interest-rate swap with fixed rate \(R\) and \(n\) annual periods has a fixed leg that pays \(R\) per unit of notional at each date \(i = 1, \dots, n\). Its floating leg pays, at each date \(i\), the floating coupon \(L_i\) for period \(i\), which is set at date \(i - 1\). The payer of the fixed leg pays \(R\) and receives \(L_i\) at each date.

Assumption A8 (single curve)

One set of discount factors both discounts the payments and sets the floating coupons, and every discount factor is strictly positive, \(\mathrm{DF}_i > 0\) for \(i = 1, \dots, n\). The floating coupon for period \(i\) is the rate \(L_i\) with \(1 + L_i = \mathrm{DF}_{i-1} / \mathrm{DF}_i\). The lesson does not treat separate curves, one for discounting and one for setting the coupons, and it does not discuss the spreads between them. The positivity is used in Propositions 3.3 and 3.5. Without it Proposition 3.3 fails: with \(n = 2\), \(\mathrm{DF}_1 = 1/2\) and \(\mathrm{DF}_2 = -1/2\) satisfy the coupon relation, the discount factors sum to zero, and the equation of Proposition 3.3 reads \(0 = 3/2\).

Proposition 3.2 (the floating leg is worth par)

Assume A8. The present value at date 0 of the floating leg, per unit of notional, is \(1 - \mathrm{DF}_n\).

Proof

The coupon \(L_i\) is paid at date \(i\), so its present value is \(\mathrm{DF}_i L_i = \mathrm{DF}_i (\mathrm{DF}_{i-1}/\mathrm{DF}_i - 1) = \mathrm{DF}_{i-1} - \mathrm{DF}_i\). Summing over \(i = 1, \dots, n\),

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\mathrm{DF}_{i-1} - \mathrm{DF}_i) = \mathrm{DF}_0 - \mathrm{DF}_n = 1 - \mathrm{DF}_n, \]

because the sum telescopes and \(\mathrm{DF}_0 = 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.3 (the par rate)

Assume A8. The fixed rate \(R_{\mathrm{par}}\) at which the swap has zero value at date 0 is called the par rate. It is the unique solution of the equation that equates the fixed leg and the floating leg, and it is

\[ R_{\mathrm{par}} = \frac{1 - \mathrm{DF}_n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{DF}_i}. \]

The sum \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{DF}_i\) in the denominator is the present value of a unit amount paid at each of the times \(1, \ldots, n\) under the schedule's discount factors. It is the schedule's annuity, written here in the discount factors \(\mathrm{DF}_i\) of this lesson.

Proof

The fixed leg pays \(R\) at each date \(i\), so its present value is \(R \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{DF}_i\). The floating leg is worth \(1 - \mathrm{DF}_n\) by Proposition 3.2. The swap has zero value when the two legs are equal, that is, when \(R \sum_{i} \mathrm{DF}_i = 1 - \mathrm{DF}_n\). By A8 each \(\mathrm{DF}_i\) is strictly positive, so the sum \(\sum_i \mathrm{DF}_i\) is strictly positive, and this equation has exactly one solution, which is the stated \(R_{\mathrm{par}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.4 (a flat curve)

Suppose \(\mathrm{DF}_i = (1 + \rho)^{-i}\) for a constant annual rate \(\rho \ne 0\). Put \(x = 1/(1 + \rho)\), so \(1 - x = \rho/(1 + \rho)\) and \(x/(1 - x) = 1/\rho\). The geometric-series formula (concept geometric-series, module M1) gives \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x^i = x(1 - x^n)/(1 - x) = (1 - x^n)/\rho\). Hence \(\rho \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{DF}_i = 1 - \mathrm{DF}_n\), and \(R_{\mathrm{par}} = \rho\). At \(\rho = 0\) every discount factor is one, so \(R_{\mathrm{par}} = (1 - 1)/n = 0 = \rho\) directly. On a flat curve the par rate is the flat rate.

Proposition 3.5 (value to the fixed payer)

Assume A8. For a fixed rate \(K\), the value to the fixed payer, per unit of notional, is \((R_{\mathrm{par}} - K)\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{DF}_i\). It is positive when \(K < R_{\mathrm{par}}\), zero when \(K = R_{\mathrm{par}}\), and negative when \(K > R_{\mathrm{par}}\).

Proof

The payer receives the floating leg, worth \(1 - \mathrm{DF}_n\), and pays the fixed leg, worth \(K \sum_i \mathrm{DF}_i\). The value is \(1 - \mathrm{DF}_n - K \sum_i \mathrm{DF}_i\). By Proposition 3.3, \(1 - \mathrm{DF}_n = R_{\mathrm{par}} \sum_i \mathrm{DF}_i\), so the value is \((R_{\mathrm{par}} - K)\sum_i \mathrm{DF}_i\). The sum is positive by A8, so the sign of the value is the sign of \(R_{\mathrm{par}} - K\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.6 (the day count is a stated convention)

Each period is taken to be one year, and no day-count convention is used. A day count changes the accrual factor that multiplies each rate. With accrual factors other than one the formulas change, and this lesson does not derive them.

Lab 3 · Margining a SIMULATED futures path, and the par rate of a swap

The daily margins sum to the settlement minus the initial price, and the account balance equals the futures price minus the initial price on every day, both exactly. Several days show a loss. Part B uses typed-in annual zero rates from 3 to 5 percent, and the par rate is 4.9026 percent, which lies within the range of the zero rates and equals none of them. """FC2 lab L03: daily margining of a SIMULATED futures path, and the par rate of a swap from discount factors.

Part A is SIMULATED: the futures price path is generated from a seeded random number generator and describes no
contract or market. Prices are integer cents: each daily change is an integer number of cents, so the margin flows
and the account balance are exact integers. The margin account is assumed to earn no interest (assumption A8-prime of
the lesson).

Part B is exact: discount factors and the par rate are fractions.Fraction values, and the swap's notional is in
integer cents. Its input annual zero rates are typed in, and the discount factors are computed from them.
Money rounding is half up, to the nearest cent.

Standard library only; no network. The random generator is seeded, so every run prints the same path.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
from fractions import Fraction

SEED = 20261028
DAYS = 10
START_CENTS = 5000
MOVE_LOW_CENTS = -40
MOVE_HIGH_CENTS = 40
NOTIONAL_CENTS = 100_000_000
ZERO_RATES = [Fraction(3, 100), Fraction(7, 200), Fraction(4, 100), Fraction(9, 200), Fraction(5, 100)]


def simulate_path(seed: int, days: int, start_cents: int) -> list[int]:
    """Futures price in integer cents at the close of each day, starting at start_cents on day 0."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    path = [start_cents]
    for _ in range(days):
        path.append(path[-1] + rng.randint(MOVE_LOW_CENTS, MOVE_HIGH_CENTS))
    return path


def variation_margin(path: list[int]) -> list[int]:
    """Daily variation-margin flow to the long holder, in cents: the change in the futures price each day."""
    return [path[i] - path[i - 1] for i in range(1, len(path))]


def half_up(x: Fraction) -> int:
    """Round a fraction to the nearest integer; a value exactly halfway is rounded toward plus infinity."""
    return math.floor(x + Fraction(1, 2))


def discount_factors(zero_rates: list[Fraction]) -> list[Fraction]:
    """DF_0 = 1 and DF_i = (1 + z_i)^(-i) for annual payment i, where z_i is the zero rate to date i."""
    dfs = [Fraction(1)]
    for i, z in enumerate(zero_rates, start=1):
        dfs.append(Fraction(1) / (1 + z) ** i)
    return dfs


def par_rate(dfs: list[Fraction]) -> Fraction:
    """Fixed rate per unit period that makes the fixed leg equal the floating leg: (1 - DF_N) / sum of DF_i."""
    n = len(dfs) - 1
    return (1 - dfs[n]) / sum(dfs[1:], Fraction(0))


def main() -> None:
    path = simulate_path(SEED, DAYS, START_CENTS)
    margins = variation_margin(path)
    print("A. SIMULATED futures path, integer cents, day 0 to day 10")
    print("futures price by day:", " ".join(str(p) for p in path))
    print("daily variation margin to the long holder, in cents:", " ".join(str(m) for m in margins))
    balance = 0
    balances = []
    for m in margins:
        balance += m
        balances.append(balance)
    print("margin account balance after day 10, in cents:", balances[-1])
    print("settlement minus initial price, in cents:", path[-1] - path[0])
    print("sum of daily margin equals settlement minus initial price:", sum(margins) == path[-1] - path[0])
    print(
        "balance equals the futures price minus the initial price on every day:",
        all(balances[i] == path[i + 1] - path[0] for i in range(DAYS)),
    )
    print("days with a loss to the long holder:", sum(1 for m in margins if m < 0))

    print()
    print("B. Interest-rate swap, exact: notional 1,000,000 dollars, annual payments, typed-in zero rates")
    dfs = discount_factors(ZERO_RATES)
    print("discount factors DF_1 to DF_5, to six places:", " ".join(f"{float(d):.6f}" for d in dfs[1:]))
    annuity = sum(dfs[1:], Fraction(0))
    fixed_leg = par_rate(dfs) * annuity
    floating_leg = sum((dfs[i - 1] - dfs[i] for i in range(1, len(dfs))), Fraction(0))
    print("floating leg telescopes to 1 minus DF_5:", floating_leg == 1 - dfs[-1])
    rate = par_rate(dfs)
    print(f"par rate, to six places: {float(rate):.6f}")
    print("fixed leg at the par rate equals the floating leg exactly:", fixed_leg == floating_leg)
    payment = half_up(rate * NOTIONAL_CENTS)
    print("fixed payment per year at the par rate, in cents:", payment)
    struck = rate + Fraction(1, 100)
    value_to_payer = half_up((floating_leg - struck * annuity) * NOTIONAL_CENTS)
    print("value to the fixed payer of a swap struck 1 percentage point higher, in cents:", value_to_payer)
    print(
        "that value is minus one percent times the annuity times the notional, in cents:",
        half_up(-Fraction(1, 100) * annuity * NOTIONAL_CENTS),
    )


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The identities are exact, and each one rests on a convention that the reader must check.

  • Margin is not remunerated. If the margin account earned interest, the flows would compound, and Proposition 1.3 would no longer give \(B_i = F_i - F_0\). Problem P3 computes the balance in that case.
  • Margin requirements are not modelled. Initial and maintenance margin, and what happens when an account falls short, are outside this lesson. The identity holds under A8′, which admits no cash flow other than the daily variation margins, so it does not cover any payment that a requirement adds.
  • One curve. A8 uses one set of discount factors for both discounting and projection. With separate curves the coupon term for period \(i\) is in general not \(\mathrm{DF}_{i-1} - \mathrm{DF}_i\), and Problem R1 shows that the single-curve assumption is needed already for one period.
  • No futures-forward relation. This lesson does not derive the relation between a futures price and the forward price of Lesson 2. Proposition 1.3 does not require it, and the claim is not made here.
  • Unit accruals. Each period is one year, and the day count is not modelled (Remark 3.6).

5Misconceptions

  1. “A futures position and a forward with the same price at expiry have the same cash flows.”

    They can have the same total at expiry, but the timing differs. The futures position's cash flows are its daily variation margins, whose sum is \(F_n - F_0\) by Proposition 1.3. A forward pays once, at expiry, by Definition 1.1 of Lesson 2.

  2. “The futures buyer pays the futures price up front.”

    In the model of this lesson the position costs nothing to enter, and the only cash flows are the daily variation margins of Definition 1.2. The collateral that a clearing house requires is outside the model (Remark 1.4), so the lesson makes no claim about it.

  3. “The par rate of a swap is the market's interest rate.”

    \(R_{\mathrm{par}}\) is computed from all the discount factors, by Proposition 3.3. In Lab 3, Part B, the par rate is 4.9026 percent, which is none of the typed-in zero rates of 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5 and 5 percent. It is the ratio of Proposition 3.3, which uses all five discount factors, and it is not one of the input zero rates.

Mechanics

M1 · Margin on a short path

A long holder takes a futures position at 100 dollars. The prices at the close of days 1, 2 and 3 are 103, 99 and 101 dollars. Find the variation margin on each day, the balance after day 2, and the balance after day 3.

Solution

The margins are \(103 - 100 = 3\), \(99 - 103 = -4\) and \(101 - 99 = 2\) dollars. The balance after day 2 is \(3 - 4 = -1\) dollar, which is \(99 - 100\), as Proposition 1.3 requires. The balance after day 3 is \(-1 + 2 = 1\) dollar, which is \(101 - 100\).

M2 · The par rate on a flat curve

The annual zero rate is 6 percent for every maturity. Find the par rate of a three-year swap, and the discount factor \(\mathrm{DF}_3\) to six places.

Solution

By Example 3.4 the par rate on a flat curve is the flat rate, so \(R_{\mathrm{par}} = 0.06\), or 6 percent. The discount factor is \(\mathrm{DF}_3 = 1.06^{-3} = 1/1.191016 = 0.839619\) to six places.

M3 · Value of a swap struck below par

On a flat 6 percent curve with three annual periods, a payer of fixed pays 5 percent on a notional of 100 dollars. Find the value to the payer to the cent, using Proposition 3.5.

Solution

The sum of discount factors is \(1/1.06 + 1/1.1236 + 1/1.191016 = 2.6730119\), computed from the unrounded factors. Each factor is shown to six places, as 0.943396, 0.889996 and 0.839619, and those displayed digits sum to 2.673011. The par rate is 0.06, so the value per unit of notional is \((0.06 - 0.05) \times 2.6730119 = 0.026730119\). On a notional of 100 dollars it is \(2.6730119\) dollars, which is 2.67 dollars to the cent. The value is positive, because the payer's fixed rate is below par.

Proof

P1 · The floating leg for two periods

Write out the present value of the floating leg for \(n = 2\) under A8, and show that it equals \(1 - \mathrm{DF}_2\) without using the general telescoping argument.

Solution

The first coupon is \(L_1\), paid at date 1, with present value \(\mathrm{DF}_1 L_1 = \mathrm{DF}_1(1/\mathrm{DF}_1 - 1) = 1 - \mathrm{DF}_1\), using \(\mathrm{DF}_0 = 1\). The second coupon is \(L_2\), paid at date 2, with \(1 + L_2 = \mathrm{DF}_1/\mathrm{DF}_2\), so its present value is \(\mathrm{DF}_2 L_2 = \mathrm{DF}_1 - \mathrm{DF}_2\). The sum is \((1 - \mathrm{DF}_1) + (\mathrm{DF}_1 - \mathrm{DF}_2) = 1 - \mathrm{DF}_2\), with the middle term cancelled.

P2 · The par rate and the swap value at another rate

Using Proposition 3.5, show that the value to the fixed payer is zero at \(K = R_{\mathrm{par}}\), and that the value at \(K = R_{\mathrm{par}} + h\) is \(-h\sum_i \mathrm{DF}_i\) for any \(h\).

Solution

Proposition 3.5 gives the value \((R_{\mathrm{par}} - K)\sum_i \mathrm{DF}_i\). At \(K = R_{\mathrm{par}}\) this is zero. At \(K = R_{\mathrm{par}} + h\) it is \((R_{\mathrm{par}} - R_{\mathrm{par}} - h)\sum_i \mathrm{DF}_i = -h\sum_i \mathrm{DF}_i\), which is negative for \(h > 0\) and positive for \(h < 0\). Lab 3, Part B, checks the case \(h = 0.01\) with the printed value of minus 4,415,468 cents on a notional of 100,000,000 cents.

P3 · The balance when margin earns interest

Suppose the margin account earns a constant daily rate \(\eta > -1\), so that \(B_i = (1 + \eta) B_{i-1} + \Delta_i\) with \(B_0 = 0\). Show that \(B_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta_i (1 + \eta)^{n-i}\), and that, for \(n \ge 2\), \(B_n = F_n - F_0\) for every price path only when \(\eta = 0\).

Solution

By induction: \(B_1 = \Delta_1\), and if \(B_{k-1} = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \Delta_i (1+\eta)^{k-1-i}\), then \(B_k = (1 + \eta) B_{k-1} + \Delta_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \Delta_i (1 + \eta)^{k-i}\). Take the path with \(\Delta_1 = 1\) and \(\Delta_i = 0\) for \(i \ge 2\), so \(F_n - F_0 = 1\). Then \(B_n = (1 + \eta)^{n-1}\). Since \(1 + \eta > 0\) and \(n - 1 \ge 1\), this equals 1 only when \(1 + \eta = 1\), that is, \(\eta = 0\). So the identity of Proposition 1.3 needs A8′.

Research

R1 · Why one curve is needed already for one period

Let the floating coupon be set from a projection factor \(G_1\) by \(1 + L_1 = 1/G_1\), with \(G_0 = 1\), and let the discount factor be \(\mathrm{DF}_1\). Show that the present value of the one-period floating leg equals \(1 - \mathrm{DF}_1\) if and only if \(G_1 = \mathrm{DF}_1\).

Solution

The coupon \(L_1\) is paid at date 1, so its present value is \(\mathrm{DF}_1 L_1 = \mathrm{DF}_1 (1/G_1 - 1) = \mathrm{DF}_1/G_1 - \mathrm{DF}_1\). This equals \(1 - \mathrm{DF}_1\) exactly when \(\mathrm{DF}_1/G_1 = 1\), that is, when \(G_1 = \mathrm{DF}_1\). So A8 cannot be dropped in Proposition 3.2, even with one period.

R2 · The par rate as a weighted average

Under A8 with \(n = 2\), write the one-period rate \(f_1 = 1/\mathrm{DF}_1 - 1\) and the forward rate \(f_2 = \mathrm{DF}_1/\mathrm{DF}_2 - 1\). Show that \(R_{\mathrm{par}}\) is a weighted average of \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) with positive weights that sum to one.

Solution

Since \(\mathrm{DF}_1 f_1 = 1 - \mathrm{DF}_1\) and \(\mathrm{DF}_2 f_2 = \mathrm{DF}_1 - \mathrm{DF}_2\), their sum is \(1 - \mathrm{DF}_2\). Proposition 3.3 then gives \(R_{\mathrm{par}} = (\mathrm{DF}_1 f_1 + \mathrm{DF}_2 f_2)/(\mathrm{DF}_1 + \mathrm{DF}_2)\). The weights \(w_1 = \mathrm{DF}_1/(\mathrm{DF}_1 + \mathrm{DF}_2)\) and \(w_2 = \mathrm{DF}_2/(\mathrm{DF}_1 + \mathrm{DF}_2)\) are positive when the discount factors are positive, and \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\). So \(R_{\mathrm{par}} = w_1 f_1 + w_2 f_2\). The value lies between \(f_1\) and \(f_2\), and it is strictly between them when \(f_1 \ne f_2\).

R3 · A non-flat two-period curve

Take \(\mathrm{DF}_1 = 1/1.03\) and a forward rate \(f_2 = 5\) percent, so \(\mathrm{DF}_2 = \mathrm{DF}_1/1.05\). Find \(R_{\mathrm{par}}\) to four places, and show that it equals neither the one-period rate of 3 percent nor the forward rate of 5 percent.

Solution

\(\mathrm{DF}_1 = 0.970874\) and \(\mathrm{DF}_2 = 0.924642\). Then \(R_{\mathrm{par}} = (1 - 0.924642)/(0.970874 + 0.924642) = 0.075358/1.895516 = 0.039756\), which is 3.9756 percent to four places. The one-period rate is 3 percent and the forward rate is 5 percent, and \(R_{\mathrm{par}}\) lies strictly between them, as Problem R2 shows. It is not 3 percent and not 5 percent. The factors are not those of a flat curve: a flat 3 percent curve would give \(\mathrm{DF}_2 = 1.03^{-2} = 0.942596\), not 0.924642.
