Arbitrage and the No-Arbitrage Principle

  • Uses: no concept from another lesson or module; the lesson states every assumption it uses
  • Introduces: arbitrage opportunity
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or convention
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A derivative price is a claim that prices are consistent with one another. The test is arbitrage: a portfolio that costs nothing or less today and cannot lose later. This lesson defines an arbitrage opportunity in a finite one-period market, states the no-arbitrage principle as a hypothesis, and proves two consequences: the law of one price, and the rule that, under no arbitrage, a portfolio which pays at least as much in every state costs at least as much today. It then gives a criterion, positive state prices (Definition 3.1), that rules arbitrage out. The lab finds arbitrage in a mispriced two-asset market and finds none in a consistent one within its search box.

1Setting and the definition

Fix two dates, 0 (today) and 1. At date 1 exactly one of \(m\) states occurs, and no other. There are \(n\) traded assets. Asset \(j\) costs \(p_j\) at date 0, and at date 1 it pays \(X_{sj}\) in state \(s\). The vector \(p\) of prices and the matrix \(X\) with rows indexed by states and columns by assets describe the market. A portfolio is a vector \(\theta\) of holdings, one entry per asset. A negative entry is a short position: the owner has sold an asset it does not hold. The portfolio costs \(p \cdot \theta = \sum_j p_j \theta_j\) at date 0 and pays \((X\theta)_s = \sum_j X_{sj}\theta_j\) in state \(s\) at date 1. Prices and payoffs are in the units of the model, and this lesson reports no money amount in cents.

Definition 1.1 (arbitrage opportunity)

A portfolio \(\theta\) is an arbitrage opportunity when all three hold: its cost is at most zero, \(p \cdot \theta \le 0\); its payoff is at least zero in every state, \((X\theta)_s \ge 0\) for every \(s\); and it is not free of profit, meaning that either \(p \cdot \theta < 0\) or \((X\theta)_s > 0\) for some state \(s\).

The second and third conditions say that the portfolio never loses, and that it gains either today or in some state. A zero-cost portfolio with zero payoff in every state is not an arbitrage opportunity, because it gains nothing. A portfolio that pays money today and has a zero payoff is an arbitrage opportunity, because its cost is strictly negative.

Assumption A3 (no arbitrage)

No portfolio is an arbitrage opportunity in the market under study. Assumptions A1 and A2, which concern income and frictions, are defined in Lesson 2, Section 1, and first used in Lesson 2, Section 2 (Proposition 2.2). This lesson uses A3 alone.

A3 is a no-arbitrage principle: a hypothesis about a market, not a fact that this lesson establishes. A price set can violate it, and Lab 1, Part B, constructs one. The proofs below state where they assume A3.

2Dominance and the law of one price

Proposition 2.1 (law of one price)

Assume A3. If portfolios \(a\) and \(b\) have the same payoff in every state, \(X a = X b\), then they have the same cost, \(p \cdot a = p \cdot b\).

Proof

Let \(\theta = a - b\). Then \(X\theta = Xa - Xb = 0\), so the payoff of \(\theta\) is at least zero in every state. Suppose \(p \cdot \theta < 0\). Then \(\theta\) meets all three conditions of Definition 1.1, so it is an arbitrage opportunity, which A3 excludes. Hence \(p \cdot \theta \ge 0\). Applying the same argument to \(-\theta = b - a\), which also has zero payoff, gives \(p \cdot (b - a) \ge 0\). Together the two inequalities give \(p \cdot a = p \cdot b\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.2 (payoff dominance)

Assume A3. If \((Xa)_s \ge (Xb)_s\) for every state \(s\), then \(p \cdot a \ge p \cdot b\). If in addition \((Xa)_s > (Xb)_s\) for every state \(s\), then \(p \cdot a > p \cdot b\).

Proof

Let \(\theta = a - b\), so \((X\theta)_s = (Xa)_s - (Xb)_s \ge 0\) for every \(s\). Suppose \(p \cdot \theta < 0\). Then \(\theta\) is an arbitrage opportunity by Definition 1.1, which A3 excludes. Hence \(p \cdot a - p \cdot b = p \cdot \theta \ge 0\). For the strict part, suppose \(p \cdot \theta = 0\). Every state has \((X\theta)_s > 0\), so the third condition of Definition 1.1 holds, and \(\theta\) is again an arbitrage opportunity. Hence \(p \cdot \theta > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.3 (what the two results use)

Both results use A3 and nothing else: no probabilities, no preferences and no particular model of prices. The number of states is finite, and each proof is a single argument that holds for every pair of portfolios in the market. Proposition 2.2 says nothing about portfolios whose payoffs are ordered in some states but not in others. For such pairs the cost order is not determined by these results.

Example 2.4 (a portfolio that pays more and costs more)

Let state \(u\) pay the bond 1 and the stock 3, and state \(d\) pay the bond 1 and the stock 1, with prices 9/10 for the bond and 1 for the stock. The portfolio \(a = (0, 2)\), two stocks, costs 2 and pays \((6, 2)\). The portfolio \(b = (1, 1)\), one bond and one stock, costs \(19/10\) and pays \((4, 2)\). Here \(a\) pays at least as much as \(b\) in both states, so Proposition 2.2 requires \(p \cdot a \ge p \cdot b\), and \(2 \ge 19/10\) holds. The example checks the statement; it does not prove it, because the proof is the argument above.

3Positive state prices exclude arbitrage

Definition 3.1 (strictly positive state prices)

A vector \(\psi = (\psi_1, \dots, \psi_m)\) with \(\psi_s > 0\) for every state \(s\) is a set of state prices for the market when \(p_j = \sum_{s} \psi_s X_{sj}\) for every asset \(j\). In matrix form, \(p = X^\top \psi\).

Proposition 3.2 (positive state prices exclude arbitrage)

If the market has a set of strictly positive state prices, then it has no arbitrage opportunity. A3 is not assumed.

Proof

Let \(\theta\) be a portfolio with \(p \cdot \theta \le 0\) and \((X\theta)_s \ge 0\) for every \(s\). Using \(p_j = \sum_s \psi_s X_{sj}\),

\[ p \cdot \theta = \sum_j \theta_j \sum_s \psi_s X_{sj} = \sum_s \psi_s (X\theta)_s. \]

Each term \(\psi_s (X\theta)_s\) is at least zero, because \(\psi_s > 0\) and \((X\theta)_s \ge 0\). So \(p \cdot \theta \ge 0\), and the assumption \(p \cdot \theta \le 0\) forces \(p \cdot \theta = 0\). Then every term is zero, and because \(\psi_s > 0\), \((X\theta)_s = 0\) for every \(s\). The portfolio has zero cost and zero payoff in every state, so it fails the third condition of Definition 1.1. It is not an arbitrage opportunity. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.3 (the converse is not claimed)

Proposition 3.2 runs one way. This lesson does not claim that a market without arbitrage has strictly positive state prices. In the market of Example 3.4 they are unique, because the two state-price equations have a nonzero determinant, \(-2\). The lesson does not state uniqueness for other markets, and the lab checks state prices only for the two markets it prints.

Example 3.4 (a consistent market)

Take the market of Example 2.4 with the stock priced at \(q\) and the bond at 9/10. The state-price equations are \(\psi_u + \psi_d = 9/10\) for the bond and \(3\psi_u + \psi_d = q\) for the stock. Subtracting the first from the second gives \(2\psi_u = q - 9/10\), so \(\psi_u = (q - 9/10)/2\), and then \(\psi_d = 9/10 - \psi_u = (27/10 - q)/2\). At \(q = 1\) this gives \(\psi_u = 1/20\) and \(\psi_d = 17/20\). Both are strictly positive, so by Proposition 3.2 this market has no arbitrage opportunity, for any holdings whatever. Lab 1, Part A, prints the same two state prices.

Proposition 3.5 (the range of consistent stock prices)

In the market of Example 3.4, with the bond priced at 9/10, the market has no arbitrage opportunity if and only if \(9/10 < q < 27/10\).

Proof

If. Let \(9/10 < q < 27/10\). Then \(\psi_u = (q - 9/10)/2 > 0\) and \(\psi_d = (27/10 - q)/2 > 0\), so Proposition 3.2 applies.

Only if. Suppose \(q \le 9/10\). The portfolio \(\theta = (-1, 1)\), one short bond and one stock, has cost \(-9/10 + q \le 0\) and payoff \((3 - 1, 1 - 1) = (2, 0)\). The payoff is at least zero in both states and is strictly positive in state \(u\), so \(\theta\) is an arbitrage opportunity. Suppose instead \(q \ge 27/10\). The portfolio \(\theta = (3, -1)\), three bonds and one short stock, has cost \(27/10 - q \le 0\) and payoff \((3 - 3, 3 - 1) = (0, 2)\), which is an arbitrage opportunity by the same argument. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.6 (the boundaries are arbitrage)

At \(q = 9/10\) and at \(q = 27/10\) the market admits arbitrage, although the state-price formulas give zero at those points. The boundary portfolios of the proof have zero cost, so they are arbitrage only because their payoff is strictly positive in one state. Definition 1.1 includes that case, and the open interval in Proposition 3.5 follows from it.

Lab 1 · Arbitrage in typed-in price sets

Predicted before running. Part A, the market with stock price 1, has no arbitrage portfolio with holdings in the search box, and its state prices are 1/20 and 17/20, both positive, which reproduce both prices exactly. Part B, with stock price 1/2, has arbitrage portfolios in the box, including the zero-cost portfolio of 9 stocks and minus 5 bonds, whose payoff is 22 in state \(u\) and 4 in state \(d\). """FC2 lab L01: arbitrage in a two-date, two-state price set, in exact arithmetic.

The two price sets are typed in for this lab. Holdings are integers searched over a finite box, so a count of zero
means zero portfolios in that box; the algebra in the lesson, not the box, decides the general claims. Prices and
payoffs are fractions.Fraction, so every printed value is exact. Standard library only; no network; no randomness.

Conventions: a portfolio is a pair (bond units, stock units). Its cost is the sum of holdings times prices at date 0.
Its payoff in a state is the sum of holdings times that asset's payoff in that state, at date 1. An arbitrage
opportunity is a portfolio whose cost is at most zero, whose payoff is at least zero in both states, and which is
not free of profit: its cost is strictly negative or its payoff is strictly positive in some state.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product

BOX = range(-10, 11)
SMALL = range(-2, 3)

Holding = tuple[int, int]


def payoff_of(holding: Holding, payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[Fraction]:
    """Payoff at date 1 in each state: payoffs[state][asset] times holding[asset], summed over assets."""
    return [sum((holding[j] * row[j] for j in range(len(row))), Fraction(0)) for row in payoffs]


def cost_of(holding: Holding, prices: list[Fraction]) -> Fraction:
    """Cost at date 0: prices[asset] times holding[asset], summed over assets."""
    return sum((holding[j] * prices[j] for j in range(len(prices))), Fraction(0))


def is_arbitrage(holding: Holding, prices: list[Fraction], payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> bool:
    """True when the cost is at most zero, the payoff is at least zero in every state, and the portfolio is not
    free of profit: its cost is strictly negative or its payoff is strictly positive in some state."""
    cost = cost_of(holding, prices)
    pay = payoff_of(holding, payoffs)
    if cost > 0 or any(x < 0 for x in pay):
        return False
    return cost < 0 or any(x > 0 for x in pay)


def arbitrages_in_box(prices: list[Fraction], payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[Holding]:
    """Every arbitrage portfolio with holdings in BOX, in lexicographic order."""
    return [h for h in product(BOX, BOX) if is_arbitrage(h, prices, payoffs)]


def state_prices(prices: list[Fraction], payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Solve psi_u * X[u][j] + psi_d * X[d][j] = price_j for j = 0, 1 (Cramer's rule on the 2 by 2 system)."""
    a, b = payoffs[0][0], payoffs[1][0]
    c, d = payoffs[0][1], payoffs[1][1]
    det = a * d - b * c
    psi_u = (prices[0] * d - b * prices[1]) / det
    psi_d = (a * prices[1] - c * prices[0]) / det
    return psi_u, psi_d


def dominance_violations(prices: list[Fraction], payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """Over holdings in SMALL: count pairs (a, b) with payoff(a) >= payoff(b) in both states, and the number of
    those pairs for which cost(a) < cost(b)."""
    holdings = list(product(SMALL, SMALL))
    dominating = 0
    violating = 0
    for a in holdings:
        pa = payoff_of(a, payoffs)
        for b in holdings:
            pb = payoff_of(b, payoffs)
            if all(x >= y for x, y in zip(pa, pb, strict=True)):
                dominating += 1
                if cost_of(a, prices) < cost_of(b, prices):
                    violating += 1
    return dominating, violating


def main() -> None:
    # Rows are states (u, d); columns are the bond and the stock, each paying the amount shown at date 1.
    payoffs = [[Fraction(1), Fraction(3)], [Fraction(1), Fraction(1)]]
    consistent = [Fraction(9, 10), Fraction(1)]
    mispriced = [Fraction(9, 10), Fraction(1, 2)]

    print("A. Consistent prices: bond 9/10, stock 1; state u pays bond 1 and stock 3; state d pays bond 1 and stock 1")
    print("arbitrage portfolios with holdings in the box:", len(arbitrages_in_box(consistent, payoffs)))
    psi_u, psi_d = state_prices(consistent, payoffs)
    print("state prices (state u, state d):", psi_u, psi_d)
    print("both state prices strictly positive:", psi_u > 0 and psi_d > 0)
    reprices = all(psi_u * payoffs[0][j] + psi_d * payoffs[1][j] == consistent[j] for j in range(2))
    print("state prices reproduce both prices exactly:", reprices)
    dom, bad = dominance_violations(consistent, payoffs)
    print("holdings pairs in SMALL with payoff(a) at least payoff(b) in both states:", dom)
    print("of those, pairs whose cost(a) is below cost(b):", bad)

    print()
    print("B. Mispriced stock: bond 9/10, stock 1/2, same payoffs")
    found = arbitrages_in_box(mispriced, payoffs)
    print("arbitrage portfolios with holdings in the box:", len(found))
    zero_cost = [
        h for h in product(BOX, BOX) if cost_of(h, mispriced) == 0 and all(x > 0 for x in payoff_of(h, payoffs))
    ]
    holding = zero_cost[0]
    print("first zero-cost portfolio with payoff strictly positive in both states, holdings (bond, stock):", holding)
    print("cost at date 0:", cost_of(holding, mispriced))
    pay = payoff_of(holding, payoffs)
    print("payoff in state u:", pay[0])
    print("payoff in state d:", pay[1])
    psi_u_b, psi_d_b = state_prices(mispriced, payoffs)
    print("state prices (state u, state d):", psi_u_b, psi_d_b)
    print("one state price is negative:", psi_u_b < 0 or psi_d_b < 0)
    value_by_states = psi_u_b * pay[0] + psi_d_b * pay[1]
    print("state prices applied to that payoff equal its cost:", value_by_states == cost_of(holding, mispriced))

    print("holdings pairs in SMALL with payoff(a) at least payoff(b) in both states:", dom_b)
    print("of those, pairs whose cost(a) is below cost(b):", bad_b)

4Where the canon breaks

The results are exact inside their hypotheses. Four places where the reader must act:

  • A3 is a hypothesis. Definition 1.1 has no transaction costs, no borrowing limit and no short-sale limit. A price set that shows an arbitrage under the definition may not yield a profit once those frictions are priced. Lesson 2 adds A2, which removes the frictions by assumption, and states what it removes.
  • Positive state prices are sufficient, not shown necessary. Proposition 3.2 is one direction. Remark 3.3 says that the converse is not claimed here.
  • One period. The market has one trading date and one payoff date. The definition does not cover trades at intermediate dates, so a multi-period claim needs its own proof.
  • Equal payoffs, not similar ones. Proposition 2.1 needs equal payoffs state by state, so it does not apply to assets whose payoffs differ, even slightly. Proposition 2.2 applies when one payoff is at least the other in every state.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Any trade that makes money in some state is an arbitrage.”

    Definition 1.1 requires the cost to be at most zero and the payoff to be at least zero in every state. Take the stock at 1 and the bond at 9/10 from Example 3.4 and hold one stock while shorting two bonds. The cost is \(1 - 2 \cdot 9/10 = -4/5\), so money comes in today, and the payoff is \((3 - 2, 1 - 2) = (1, -1)\). The portfolio gains in state \(u\) and loses 1 in state \(d\). It is not an arbitrage opportunity, because it can lose.

  2. “State prices are probabilities, so they add up to one.”

    In the market of Example 3.4 at stock price 1, the state prices are \(1/20\) and \(17/20\), which add to \(9/10\), the price of the bond that pays 1 in every state. They are not probabilities: they sum to 9/10, not to one, and this lesson assigns no probabilities to the states. Problem P3 makes the sum explicit.

  3. “No arbitrage is a fact that real markets obey.”

    A3 is a hypothesis. Lab 1, Part B, prices the stock at 1/2 with the same payoffs as Part A. That price set has arbitrage portfolios in the search box, including a zero-cost portfolio that pays 22 and 4 in the two states. A market that has such prices is not consistent with A3.

Mechanics

M1 · Testing a portfolio against Definition 1.1

In the market of Example 3.4, with the stock at 1, test the portfolio of 2 bonds and minus 1 stock against Definition 1.1.

Solution

The cost is \(2 \cdot 9/10 - 1 = 4/5\), which is positive, so the first condition fails. The payoff is \((2 - 3, 2 - 1) = (-1, 1)\), which is negative in state \(u\), so the second condition also fails. The portfolio is not an arbitrage opportunity on either count.

M2 · State prices for a cheaper bond

Find the state prices of the market with the bond at 4/5 and the stock at 1, with the payoffs of Example 3.4. Say whether Proposition 3.2 rules out arbitrage.

Solution

The equations are \(\psi_u + \psi_d = 4/5\) and \(3\psi_u + \psi_d = 1\). Subtracting gives \(2\psi_u = 1/5\), so \(\psi_u = 1/10\), and then \(\psi_d = 4/5 - 1/10 = 7/10\). Both are strictly positive, so Proposition 3.2 excludes arbitrage in this market.

M3 · Zero-cost portfolios of the mispriced market

In the market with the bond at 9/10 and the stock at 1/2, find every holding \((x, y)\) of bonds and stocks with zero cost, and say for which of them the payoff is strictly positive in both states.

Solution

Zero cost requires \(9x/10 + y/2 = 0\), so \(y = -9x/5\). Writing \(x = -5t\) gives \(y = 9t\), so the zero-cost portfolios are the multiples \(t(-5, 9)\) for real \(t\). Their payoff is \(t(22, 4)\), which is strictly positive in both states exactly when \(t > 0\). Lab 1, Part B, prints the case \(t = 1\).

Proof

P1 · The law of one price by a second route

Assume A3. Prove Proposition 2.1 using only the fact that the portfolio \(a - b\) has zero payoff, without referring to the sign of \(p \cdot \theta\) in the proof above.

Solution

Let \(\theta = a - b\), so \(X\theta = 0\). Suppose \(p \cdot \theta < 0\). Its payoff is zero, which is at least zero in every state, and its cost is strictly negative. That is an arbitrage opportunity, which A3 excludes. So \(p \cdot \theta \ge 0\). The same argument with \(-\theta\) gives \(-p \cdot \theta \ge 0\). Hence \(p \cdot \theta = 0\), which is the claim.

P2 · Strict dominance gives a strict price order

Assume A3 and suppose \((Xa)_s > (Xb)_s\) in every state. Prove that \(p \cdot a > p \cdot b\), citing the proof of Proposition 2.2.

Solution

Proposition 2.2 gives \(p \cdot a \ge p \cdot b\). Suppose equality holds. Then \(\theta = a - b\) has \(p \cdot \theta = 0\) and \((X\theta)_s > 0\) in every state, so it satisfies the third condition of Definition 1.1 and is an arbitrage opportunity. A3 excludes that, so the equality fails and \(p \cdot a > p \cdot b\).

P3 · State prices are not probabilities

In the market of Example 3.4 with \(q = 1\), show that the state prices sum to the bond's price, and that they are therefore not probabilities in the sense of summing to one.

Solution

The state prices are \(\psi_u = 1/20\) and \(\psi_d = 17/20\). Their sum is \(18/20 = 9/10\), which is the price of the bond. The bond pays 1 in every state, so its price is the sum of the state prices, and that sum is 9/10, not 1. A probability would sum to one. Here the sum is the price of the bond, which pays a sure dollar at date 1, and that price is 9/10.

Empirical

E1 · The zero-cost portfolio in Lab 1

Using only the values printed in Lab 1, Part B, state the holding, its cost, and its payoff in each state of the zero-cost portfolio that the lab finds.

Solution

The printed holding is \((-5, 9)\): minus 5 bonds and 9 stocks. The printed cost is 0. The printed payoffs are 22 in state \(u\) and 4 in state \(d\). The portfolio costs nothing, pays 22 and 4 in the two states, and so is an arbitrage opportunity under Definition 1.1.

E2 · Reading the arbitrage count in Lab 1, Part B

Lab 1, Part B, prints 30 arbitrage portfolios with holdings in the box. Does that number say how many arbitrage portfolios the market has?

Solution

No. Scaling an arbitrage opportunity by any positive real number keeps the cost at most zero, the payoff at least zero, and the profit strict, so the market has infinitely many arbitrage portfolios. The count 30 counts only the integer holdings from minus 10 to 10 of the two assets. It is a fact about that box.

E3 · What the zero count in Part A does and does not show

Lab 1, Part A, prints zero arbitrage portfolios in the box. Explain which result in this lesson shows that the market has no arbitrage opportunity at all, and what the box count contributes.

Solution

Proposition 3.2 shows it, using the state prices that Part A prints, 1/20 and 17/20, which are both positive and reproduce both prices. The box count of zero is consistent with that result, and it is a check on the arithmetic. The box alone would prove nothing, because it covers finitely many holdings.

Research

R1 · A middle state does not change the range

Add a third state \(w\) in which the bond pays 1 and the stock pays 2, so the stock pays 3, 2 and 1 in states \(u\), \(w\) and \(d\). Keep the bond at 9/10. Show that the market has no arbitrage opportunity if \(9/10 < q < 27/10\), and that it has one if \(q \le 9/10\) or \(q \ge 27/10\).

Solution

Write \(\psi_w = \varepsilon\). The bond equation gives \(\psi_u + \psi_d = 9/10 - \varepsilon\), and the stock equation gives \(3\psi_u + \psi_d = q - 2\varepsilon\). Subtracting, \(2\psi_u = q - 9/10 - \varepsilon\), so \(\psi_u = (q - 9/10 - \varepsilon)/2\). Then \(\psi_d = 9/10 - \varepsilon - \psi_u = (27/10 - q - \varepsilon)/2\). All three are strictly positive exactly when \(0 < \varepsilon < \min(q - 9/10,\ 27/10 - q)\). That interval is nonempty exactly when \(9/10 < q < 27/10\), so for such \(q\) pick \(\varepsilon\) inside it. Proposition 3.2 then gives no arbitrage. For \(q \le 9/10\), the portfolio \((-1, 1)\) has cost \(q - 9/10 \le 0\) and payoff \((2, 1, 0)\) in states \(u, w, d\). For \(q \ge 27/10\), the portfolio \((3, -1)\) has cost \(27/10 - q \le 0\) and payoff \((0, 1, 2)\). Each is an arbitrage opportunity by Definition 1.1.

R2 · Scaling an arbitrage

Show that if an arbitrage opportunity \(\theta\) exists, then \(k\theta\) is an arbitrage opportunity for every real \(k > 0\), and explain why the existence of arbitrage then implies unbounded profit.

Solution

Cost and payoff are linear in \(\theta\). For \(k > 0\), \(p \cdot (k\theta) = k(p \cdot \theta) \le 0\) and \((X k\theta)_s = k (X\theta)_s \ge 0\). The strict condition is preserved, because \(k(p \cdot \theta) < 0\) when \(p \cdot \theta < 0\), and \(k(X\theta)_s > 0\) when \((X\theta)_s > 0\). So \(k\theta\) is an arbitrage opportunity. Its payoff in a state where \(\theta\) pays a positive amount grows without bound as \(k\) grows, so the profit is unbounded. This does not contradict Proposition 2.2, which compares two portfolios and says nothing about the size of a profit.

R3 · Shorting the bond is forbidden

Suppose the bond cannot be sold short: the holding of the bond must be at least zero, while the stock may be held short or long. The bond is at 9/10 and the stock at \(q\), with the payoffs of Example 3.4. Show that the market then has no arbitrage opportunity if and only if \(0 < q < 27/10\).

Solution

Above the range. For \(q \ge 27/10\), the portfolio \((3, -1)\) has a non-negative bond holding, cost \(27/10 - q \le 0\), and payoff \((0, 2)\), so it is an arbitrage opportunity. Inside the range. For \(9/10 < q < 27/10\) the state prices of Example 3.4 are strictly positive, and Proposition 3.2 excludes arbitrage with no restriction on holdings, so in particular with the restriction. Between 0 and 9/10. Let \(0 < q \le 9/10\) and suppose \(\theta = (x, y)\), with \(x \ge 0\), is an arbitrage opportunity. If \(y \ge 0\), the cost \(9x/10 + qy\) is at least zero, so the cost condition forces \(x = y = 0\), which is not an arbitrage. If \(y < 0\), the state-\(d\) payoff \(x + y\) is non-negative, so \(x \ge |y|\). The cost is then \(9x/10 - q|y| \ge (9/10 - q)|y| \ge 0\). The cost condition therefore forces both inequalities to be equalities, which needs \(q = 9/10\) and \(x = |y|\). Then the state-\(u\) payoff is \(x + 3y = |y| - 3|y| = -2|y| < 0\), which is excluded. So no arbitrage. At or below 0. For \(q \le 0\), the portfolio \((0, 1)\) has cost \(q \le 0\) and payoff \((3, 1)\), which is non-negative, and it is an arbitrage opportunity. The holding of bonds is zero, which the restriction allows.
