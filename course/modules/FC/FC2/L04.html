Option Payoffs and American Versus European Exercise

  • Uses: present value, continuous compounding
  • Introduces: call and put payoffs; American exercise
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

An option's payoff on exercise is a function of the spot price on that date. This lesson fixes the call and put payoffs, separates American exercise (at any date up to expiry) from European exercise (at expiry only), and proves two bounds on the price of an American option: it is worth at least its intrinsic value (Definition 2.1 of Lesson 4), and, at date 0, at least the price of the European option. Lesson 5 uses the payoff identity to derive put–call parity, and shows that an American call on a stock with no income, at a non-negative rate, has the same value as the European call at date 0. Lab 1 checks the payoffs and one SIMULATED path.

1Standing assumptions and the payoffs at expiry

The assumptions below hold throughout Lessons 4 and 5. Each result names the ones it uses.

Standing assumptions (A1 to A5)
  • A1 (no income). The underlying pays no dividend, coupon or storage cost over the life of the contract.
  • A2 (no frictions). There are no transaction costs and no bid–ask spread. The underlying and the options can be sold short, and cash can be lent and borrowed at the riskless rate.
  • A3 (no arbitrage). Let a portfolio have initial value \(V_0 \le 0\) and a payoff \(X\) that is non-negative in every state. Then \(V_0 = 0\) and \(X = 0\) in every state. This is a hypothesis about markets, used in the proofs, not a fact about them.
  • A4 (constant rate). The riskless rate \(r\) is constant over the life of the contract and compounded continuously. The discount factor from date \(t\) to date \(\tau\) is \(e^{-r(\tau - t)}\).
  • A5 (European exercise). A European option may be exercised only at its expiry \(\tau\).
Definition 1.1 (call and put payoffs)

Let \(S_t\) be the spot price of the underlying at date \(t\), let \(K > 0\) be the strike, and write \((x)^+ = \max(x, 0)\). A call option with strike \(K\) and expiry \(\tau\) pays \((S_\tau - K)^+\) at \(\tau\). A put option with the same strike and expiry pays \((K - S_\tau)^+\). The call is in the money at a date when \(S_t > K\), and the put when \(S_t < K\). The functions \(s \mapsto (s - K)^+\) and \(s \mapsto (K - s)^+\) are the call and put payoffs.

Holding an option costs a premium, paid at date 0. The profit at expiry of a call bought for premium \(c\) is \((S_\tau - K)^+ - c\). This lesson ignores interest on the premium. That is a convention, stated here so that every profit in Lab 1 is computed the same way, and it is not a result. The break-even spot is the expiry spot at which the profit is zero.

Lemma 1.2 (payoff identity)

For every real \(s\) and every \(K\), \((s - K)^+ - (K - s)^+ = s - K\).

Proof

If \(s \ge K\), the second term is zero and the first term is \(s - K\). If \(s < K\), the first term is zero and the second term is \(K - s\), so the difference is \(-(K - s) = s - K\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.3 (what the identity says)

A long call and a short put with the same strike and expiry pay \(S_\tau - K\) at expiry, which is the payoff of a long forward on the underlying. Lesson 5 uses this to derive put–call parity. The identity concerns payoffs only. It does not say what the two options cost.

2American and European exercise

Definition 2.1 (American and European exercise)

Exercise is cash-settled: the holder receives a payment and no share changes hands. The American option with strike \(K\) and expiry \(\tau\) may be exercised at any date \(t\) in \([0, \tau]\) that its holder chooses, and the payment on exercise at \(t\) is \(h(S_t)\), where \(h(s) = (s - K)^+\) for a call and \(h(s) = (K - s)^+\) for a put. A European option with the same strike and expiry is exercised only at \(\tau\), as A5 states. The intrinsic value at date \(t\) is \(h(S_t)\), the payment the holder would receive by exercising at \(t\).

Proposition 2.2 (intrinsic value bound)

Under A2 and A3, the price \(V_A(t)\) of an American option at date \(t\) is at least its intrinsic value: \(V_A(t) \ge h(S_t)\).

Proof

Suppose \(V_A(t) < h(S_t)\). At date \(t\), buy the option for \(V_A(t)\) and exercise it at once, receiving \(h(S_t)\). Both cash flows occur at \(t\), so the position has initial value zero and a payoff \(h(S_t) - V_A(t)\) at \(t\), which is strictly positive. Its payoff is non-negative in every state, and A3 requires that payoff to be zero, a contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.3 (an American option is worth at least its European counterpart)

Under A2, A3 and A5, for an American option and a European option with the same type, strike and expiry, the prices at date 0 satisfy \(V_A(0) \ge V_E(0)\).

Proof

Suppose \(V_A(0) < V_E(0)\). At date 0 buy the American option for \(V_A(0)\) and sell the European option for \(V_E(0)\). The initial value of this portfolio is \(V_A(0) - V_E(0) < 0\). Keep the American option until \(\tau\), and exercise it at \(\tau\) if and only if it is in the money. It is never exercised before \(\tau\). Its payoff at \(\tau\) is \(h(S_\tau)\), which is the amount the short European option pays. The portfolio's payoff is therefore zero in every state. Its initial value is negative and its payoff is non-negative, which A3 excludes. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.4 (what the inequality does not say)

Proposition 2.3 is an inequality between prices. It does not say which date an American holder should choose, and it does not say that the inequality is strict. Lesson 5 proves equality at date 0 for a call on an underlying with no income and a non-negative rate (Theorem 4.1 there). The proof of Proposition 2.3 uses no date other than 0 and \(\tau\), and it does not use A1 or A4.

Example 2.5 (one SIMULATED path, hindsight)

Let the strike be 100 USD, the expiry \(\tau = 4\) years, and let the one-year discount factor be \(e^{-r} = 9/10\), so that \(r = \ln(10/9)\), about 0.10536 per year (A4). Take the SIMULATED path \(S_0, \dots, S_4 = 100, 120, 90, 80, 110\) USD. The call's intrinsic value at dates 0 to 4 is 0, 20, 0, 0 and 10 USD. Holding the call to expiry gives 10 USD at \(\tau\), which is \(10(9/10)^4 = 6.561\) USD at date 0, or 6.56 USD to the cent. Exercising at date 1 gives 20 USD at date 1, which is \(20 \cdot 9/10 = 18\) USD at date 0. On this path, then, exercising at date 1 is worth more than holding to expiry. The put's intrinsic value at dates 0 to 4 is 0, 0, 10, 20 and 0 USD. Its best date on this path is date 3, worth \(20(9/10)^3 = 14.58\) USD at date 0, and holding it to expiry is worth 0.

Remark 2.6 (the example is hindsight, not a rule)

The best dates in Example 2.5 are known only after the path is observed. The comparison also leaves out the choice to sell the option at date 1 for its market value, which is at least 20 USD by Proposition 2.2. The example shows which dates are best on one path. It does not show what a holder should do at date 1. Lesson 5 proves the date-0 equality of Theorem 4.1 and states in its Remark 4.2 that the theorem gives no rule for later dates.

Lab 1 · Payoffs at expiry, and exercise against holding on one SIMULATED path

Before running, the expected results are: the call payoff is zero at every expiry spot up to 100 USD and rises one for one above it, so its profit is negative for spots below 108 USD and positive above; the put payoff falls one for one up to 100 USD and is zero above it, with break-even at 93 USD; on the path of Example 2.5, the call's best exercise date is 1 and the put's is 3, and holding the call to expiry is worth 6.56 USD at date 0. Part 2 discounts by the one-year factor 9/10, which is \(e^{-r}\) for \(r = \ln(10/9)\) (A4). The lab draws no random numbers.

"""FC2 L04 lab 1: call and put payoffs at expiry, and an exercise comparison on one SIMULATED price path.

SIMULATED: the price path is a fixed list written in this file. It is not market data.
Units: prices, the strike and the premiums are integer cents; dates are years, with t = 0 first.
Part 1 prints the expiry payoff and the profit (payoff minus premium, with no interest on the premium) of one call
and one put on a grid of expiry prices. Part 2 compares, on the single path, the intrinsic value of exercising at
each date with the value at that date of holding the same option to expiry. The value at date t of holding discounts
the expiry payoff by DISCOUNT for each remaining year (assumption A4). DISCOUNT = 9/10 is exactly e^(-r) for the rate
r = ln(10/9), about 10.54 per cent per year, so every discount factor in Part 2 is an exact rational number.
Part 2 is a comparison with hindsight on one path: it is not a decision rule, and it leaves out the choice to sell
the option before expiry. Lesson 5 gives no rule for that choice at a later date (its Remark 4.2).
The lab draws no random numbers.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

STRIKE_CENTS = 10_000  # 100 USD
CALL_PREMIUM_CENTS = 800
PUT_PREMIUM_CENTS = 700
DISCOUNT = Fraction(9, 10)  # one-year discount factor e^(-r) for r = ln(10/9) (assumption A4), exact
PATH_CENTS: tuple[int, ...] = (10_000, 12_000, 9_000, 8_000, 11_000)  # spot at t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 years
GRID_CENTS: tuple[int, ...] = tuple(range(0, 20_001, 2_500))  # expiry prices 0 to 200 USD in steps of 25 USD


def call_payoff(spot_cents: int, strike_cents: int = STRIKE_CENTS) -> int:
    """Payoff of a call at expiry in cents: max(spot - strike, 0)."""
    return max(spot_cents - strike_cents, 0)


def put_payoff(spot_cents: int, strike_cents: int = STRIKE_CENTS) -> int:
    """Payoff of a put at expiry in cents: max(strike - spot, 0)."""
    return max(strike_cents - spot_cents, 0)


def usd(cents: Fraction | int) -> str:
    """Exact cents shown as dollars with two decimals, rounded by Python's fixed-point rule; no thousands separator."""
    return f"{float(cents) / 100:.2f}"


def part_one() -> None:
    """Payoff and profit of the call and the put on the expiry grid, plus the two break-even prices."""
    print(
        f"strike {STRIKE_CENTS} cents; call premium {CALL_PREMIUM_CENTS} cents; put premium {PUT_PREMIUM_CENTS} cents"
    )
    print("expiry spot (USD)  call payoff  call profit  put payoff  put profit")
    for spot in GRID_CENTS:
        c = call_payoff(spot)
        p = put_payoff(spot)
        print(
            f"{spot / 100:>17.2f}  {usd(c):>12}  {usd(c - CALL_PREMIUM_CENTS):>11}  "
            f"{usd(p):>10}  {usd(p - PUT_PREMIUM_CENTS):>10}"
        )
    print(f"call break-even spot, strike plus premium (USD): {usd(STRIKE_CENTS + CALL_PREMIUM_CENTS)}")
    print(f"put break-even spot, strike minus premium (USD): {usd(STRIKE_CENTS - PUT_PREMIUM_CENTS)}")


def part_two() -> None:
    """Intrinsic value of exercising at each date, and the value at each date of holding to expiry, on the one path."""
    expiry = len(PATH_CENTS) - 1
    spot_expiry = PATH_CENTS[expiry]
    print(f"path (USD) at t = 0..{expiry}: " + ", ".join(usd(s) for s in PATH_CENTS))
    print("date  spot  call exercise  call hold (value at t)  put exercise  put hold (value at t)")
    call_pv_exercise: list[Fraction] = []
    put_pv_exercise: list[Fraction] = []
    for t, spot in enumerate(PATH_CENTS):
        discount = DISCOUNT ** (expiry - t)
        call_hold = call_payoff(spot_expiry) * discount
        put_hold = put_payoff(spot_expiry) * discount
        call_now = call_payoff(spot)
        put_now = put_payoff(spot)
        print(
            f"{t:>4}  {usd(spot):>5}  {usd(call_now):>13}  {usd(call_hold):>22}  "
            f"{usd(put_now):>12}  {usd(put_hold):>21}"
        )
        call_pv_exercise.append(call_now * DISCOUNT**t)
        put_pv_exercise.append(put_now * DISCOUNT**t)
    best_call = max(range(len(PATH_CENTS)), key=lambda t: call_pv_exercise[t])
    best_put = max(range(len(PATH_CENTS)), key=lambda t: put_pv_exercise[t])
    call_best_pv = usd(call_pv_exercise[best_call])
    put_best_pv = usd(put_pv_exercise[best_put])
    print(f"hindsight best exercise date for the call on this path: t = {best_call}, PV at 0 {call_best_pv}")
    print(f"hindsight best exercise date for the put on this path: t = {best_put}, PV at 0 {put_best_pv}")
    held_call_pv0 = call_payoff(spot_expiry) * DISCOUNT**expiry
    print(f"call held to expiry, PV at 0: {usd(held_call_pv0)}")


def main() -> None:
    part_one()
    part_two()


main()

3Where the canon breaks

The comparisons in Example 2.5 and Lab 1 use the payoff of holding to expiry. A holder who exercises early gives up the option's remaining value, and a holder who sells gets its market price. Proposition 2.2 bounds that price from below by the intrinsic value. Lesson 5 proves bounds for European prices and, at date 0, an equality for an American call with no income and a non-negative rate (Theorem 4.1 there). Neither gives the American price in general.

Proposition 2.2 uses A2 and A3, and Proposition 2.3 uses the same two together with A5. Neither uses A1, so both hold whether or not the underlying pays income. The lesson makes no claim about early exercise of an American put or call in that case.

Remark 2.4 states the limit of Proposition 2.3. The inequality holds for both calls and puts, and the lesson shows only that the American option is worth at least the European one. It does not show that the two are equal for puts, and it does not show that they differ.

4Misconceptions

  1. “An American option is always worth more than its European counterpart.”

    Proposition 2.3 proves only \(V_A(0) \ge V_E(0)\). Lesson 5 (Theorem 4.1) proves equality at date 0 for a call on an underlying with no income and a non-negative rate, so the date-0 early-exercise premium, \(V_A(0) - V_E(0)\), is zero in that case. This lesson does not claim that the inequality is strict for any put or call.

  2. “A call that is in the money should be exercised at once.”

    Exercise gives the intrinsic value \(S_t - K\). Selling the option, or holding it, gives its market value, and by Proposition 2.2 that value is at least \(S_t - K\). Exercising at once is therefore never better than selling at the market price. Lesson 5 does not settle whether holding beats selling at a date after 0: it proves only the date-0 equality of Theorem 4.1 and gives no rule for later dates (its Remark 4.2).

  3. “The payoff at expiry is the profit.”

    The profit subtracts the premium. In Lab 1 the call pays 25.00 USD at a spot of 125 USD, and its profit is 17.00 USD, because the premium is 8.00 USD. The break-even spot of the call is 108 USD, not 100 USD.

  4. “The best exercise date on a path is a trading rule.”

    Example 2.5 picks the best date after the path is known. A rule has to choose the date from the prices observed so far, and a rule that exercises on the path's best date needs the future price. Remark 2.6 explains what the example leaves out.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · The payoff and profit of a call

A call has strike 50 USD and premium 4 USD. At expiry the spot is 62 USD. Find the payoff, the profit under the convention of this lesson, and the break-even spot.

Solution

The payoff is \((62 - 50)^+ = 12\) USD. The profit is \(12 - 4 = 8\) USD, with no interest on the premium. The break-even spot is the strike plus the premium, \(50 + 4 = 54\) USD, because the profit is zero there and negative below it.

M2 · The break-even of a put

A put has strike 80 USD and premium 3 USD. Find the break-even spot at expiry, and the profit when the spot at expiry is 60 USD.

Solution

The break-even is the strike minus the premium, \(80 - 3 = 77\) USD. At a spot of 60 USD the payoff is \((80 - 60)^+ = 20\) USD, and the profit is \(20 - 3 = 17\) USD.

M3 · Intrinsic values on the Example 2.5 path

For the path \(100, 120, 90, 80, 110\) USD of Example 2.5, list the call's intrinsic value at each date, and the dates at which exercise gives a positive payment.

Solution

With strike 100 USD, the call's intrinsic values are \((S_t - 100)^+\), which are 0, 20, 0, 0 and 10 USD. Exercise gives a positive payment at date 1, worth 20 USD, and at date 4, worth 10 USD. At date 4 the expiry is reached, so exercise there is the same as holding.

Proof

P1 · The call payoff is non-decreasing

Show that \(s \mapsto (s - K)^+\) is zero for \(s \le K\) and non-decreasing on the whole real line.

Solution

For \(s \le K\), \(s - K \le 0\), so \((s - K)^+ = 0\). For \(s_1 \le s_2\), the function \(x \mapsto \max(x, 0)\) is non-decreasing, and \(s_1 - K \le s_2 - K\), so \((s_1 - K)^+ \le (s_2 - K)^+\). The payoff is therefore non-decreasing, and it is constant at zero for \(s \le K\).

P2 · The intrinsic bound for a put

Under A2 and A3, show that the price of an American put at date \(t\) is at least \((K - S_t)^+\).

Solution

Suppose the put's price \(V(t)\) is less than \((K - S_t)^+\). At date \(t\), buy the put for \(V(t)\) and exercise it at once, receiving \((K - S_t)^+\). The two cash flows occur at \(t\), so the position has zero initial value and a payoff \((K - S_t)^+ - V(t) > 0\) at \(t\). This is non-negative in every state and strictly positive, and A3 requires it to be zero. The contradiction shows \(V(t) \ge (K - S_t)^+\). The argument of Proposition 2.2 with \(h(s) = (K - s)^+\) is the same.

P3 · A chord inequality for the put payoff

Show that \(s \mapsto (K - s)^+\) satisfies \(f(\lambda s_1 + (1 - \lambda)s_2) \le \lambda f(s_1) + (1 - \lambda)f(s_2)\) for all \(s_1, s_2\) on the real line and all \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\), using only that \(f\) is the maximum of two affine functions.

Solution

Write \(f(s) = \max(K - s, 0)\), the maximum of the two affine functions \(a_1(s) = K - s\) and \(a_2(s) = 0\). Fix \(s_1\), \(s_2\) and \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\), and let \(s = \lambda s_1 + (1 - \lambda)s_2\). Each affine piece satisfies \(a_i(s) = \lambda a_i(s_1) + (1 - \lambda)a_i(s_2)\). Since \(a_i \le f\) everywhere, \(a_i(s_1) \le f(s_1)\) and \(a_i(s_2) \le f(s_2)\), so \(a_i(s) \le \lambda f(s_1) + (1 - \lambda)f(s_2)\) for \(i = 1, 2\). The left side is \(f(s) = \max(a_1(s), a_2(s))\), so \(f(s) \le \lambda f(s_1) + (1 - \lambda)f(s_2)\). This is the inequality to be shown.

Empirical

E1 · Exercise against holding on the lab path

In the Part 2 table of Lab 1, on which dates is exercising the call worth more than holding it to expiry, and on which dates is it worth less? Use the printed values.

Solution

The printed call exercise values are 0.00, 20.00, 0.00, 0.00 and 10.00 USD. The printed call hold values are 6.56, 7.29, 8.10, 9.00 and 10.00 USD. Exercise is worth more at date 1 (20.00 against 7.29). It is worth less at dates 0, 2 and 3. At date 4 the two are equal at 10.00 USD, as they must be at expiry.

E2 · Changing the last spot in a copy

In a copy of Lab 1, change the last spot on the path from 110 USD to 90 USD and keep the rest. State the holding value at date 0 of the call and of the put, and the put's best exercise date and its value at date 0.

Solution

At expiry the call pays \((90 - 100)^+ = 0\), so holding it is worth 0.00 USD. The put pays \(100 - 90 = 10\) USD at expiry, which is \(10(9/10)^4 = 6.561\) USD at date 0, or 6.56 USD to the cent. The put's intrinsic values on the path are 0, 0, 10, 20 and 10 USD, so its best date is still 3, worth \(20(9/10)^3 = 14.58\) USD at date 0. The call's best date is still 1, worth 18.00 USD.

E3 · Early exercise of the put on the path

On the Example 2.5 path, is exercising the put at date 2 worth more at date 0 than holding it to expiry? Give both values.

Solution

The put's intrinsic value at date 2 is \(100 - 90 = 10\) USD, worth \(10(9/10)^2 = 8.10\) USD at date 0. Holding the put to expiry pays \((100 - 110)^+ = 0\) at \(\tau = 4\). So on this path exercising at date 2 is worth more, 8.10 USD against 0.00 USD. This is a fact about one path, as Remark 2.6 says.

Research

R1 · The expiry spot at which holding and exercising tie

On the Example 2.5 path, the spot at date 1 is 120 USD. Find the spot \(S_\tau\) at expiry at which the call held to expiry, valued at date 1, equals the exercise value of 20 USD at date 1. Use the discount factor of A4 and the call's payoff.

Solution

Holding to expiry, valued at date 1, gives \((S_\tau - 100)^+ (9/10)^{4 - 1}\). Setting this equal to 20 USD, with \(S_\tau > 100\), gives \(S_\tau - 100 = 20(10/9)^3 = 20000/729\), so \(S_\tau = 100 + 20000/729 = 127.43484\) USD to five decimals. For an expiry spot above this value, holding is worth more at date 1 than exercising, and for a lower value exercising is worth more. The calculation uses only the payoff and the discount factor, and it leaves out the sale of the option, as Remark 2.6 says.

R2 · Monotonicity of the holding value

For a call with strike \(K\), let \(H(s) = (s - K)^+ e^{-r(\tau - t)}\) be the value at date \(t\) of holding it to expiry, as a function of the expiry spot \(s\). Show that \(H\) is non-negative and non-decreasing in \(s\).

Solution

The discount factor \(e^{-r(\tau - t)}\) is positive, so \(H\) has the sign of \((s - K)^+\), which is non-negative. By P1, \((s - K)^+\) is non-decreasing, and multiplying by a positive constant keeps it non-decreasing. So \(H\) is non-negative and non-decreasing in \(s\).

R3 · A path on which holding beats every earlier exercise

Construct a path of spot prices at dates 0 to 4 with strike 100 USD on which the call held to expiry, discounted to date 0 by the one-year factor 9/10 of A4, is worth more than the discounted intrinsic value of exercising at any date before expiry. Say what the construction does and does not show.

Solution

Take \(S_0, \dots, S_4 = 100, 90, 95, 110, 150\) USD. The call's intrinsic values at dates 0 to 3 are 0, 0, 0 and 10 USD. The best of these, exercising at date 3, is worth \(10(9/10)^3 = 7.29\) USD at date 0, with the one-year factor 9/10 of A4. Holding to expiry pays \(150 - 100 = 50\) USD at date 4, worth \(50(9/10)^4 = 32.805\) USD at date 0. The hold value is larger, so the path has the required property. The path shows that exercising early can be worse than holding on this path. It does not show that holding is the better decision in general, because Remark 2.6 applies.

7Retrieval quiz

Answer from memory, then check. Finishing the quiz adds this lesson's cards to your review deck.

  1. A call has strike 100 USD. At expiry the spot is 125 USD. What is its payoff?

    Options

    The payoff of a call is \((S - K)^+ = (125 - 100)^+ = 25\) USD (Definition 1.1).

  2. The same call was bought for a premium of 8 USD. What is its profit at expiry, in USD, under this lesson's convention?

    \(25 - 8 = 17\) USD. The profit subtracts the premium and ignores interest on it (Lab 1, Part 1).

  3. Which relation between an American option and the European option with the same terms does Proposition 2.3 prove?

    Options

    The American holder can exercise at expiry as the European holder can, and the proof shows that a lower American price admits an arbitrage. The proof gives only the inequality (Remark 2.4).

  4. A put has strike 100 USD, and the spot is 80 USD. What is its intrinsic value, in USD?

    \((K - S)^+ = (100 - 80)^+ = 20\) USD, which is the payment the holder receives on exercise (Definition 2.1).

  5. Under A5, when may a European option be exercised?

    Options

    A5 restricts a European option to exercise at expiry. The American option of Definition 2.1 has the wider right.

  6. A put has strike 80 USD and premium 3 USD. What is its break-even spot at expiry, in USD?

    The profit is zero when \(80 - S - 3 = 0\), so \(S = 77\) USD (Problem M2).

  7. On the Example 2.5 path, what is the value at date 0 of holding the call to expiry, in USD to two decimals?

    The payoff at expiry is \(110 - 100 = 10\) USD, and \(10(9/10)^4 = 6.561\) USD, which is 6.56 USD to two decimals (Example 2.5; Lab 1, Part 2).

  8. On the Example 2.5 path, which date is the best exercise date for the call, in hindsight?

    Options

    Exercising at date 1 gives 20 USD, worth \(20 \cdot 9/10 = 18.00\) USD at date 0, which is the largest discounted intrinsic value on the path (Lab 1, Part 2).

  9. What does the payoff identity of Lemma 1.2 say?

    Options

    Lemma 1.2 states \((s - K)^+ - (K - s)^+ = s - K\). The first term is the call's payoff and the second the put's. Their difference is the payoff of a forward (Remark 1.3).
