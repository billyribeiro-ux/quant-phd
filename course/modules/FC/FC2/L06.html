One- and multi-period binomial pricing

  • Uses: arbitrage opportunity, call payoff, expectation, discount factor
  • Introduces: binomial option pricing (defined in §1), risk-neutral probability (defined in §2)
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A call option is worth what a portfolio of the stock and a riskless bond costs when the portfolio pays the same amount as the option. In a one-period binomial model, the stock can end at one of two prices, and that replication fixes the price today without any forecast of the stock's expected return. The same step, applied at each node, prices a tree of many periods. Section 2 names the probabilities that appear in the price, and Section 3 states the backward induction. Lab 1 computes a one-period and a two-period price in exact fractions and checks each against the replication. Lesson 7 takes the number of periods to a limit.

1The one-period model and its replicating portfolio

The lesson uses these assumptions from the FC2 numbering. A1: no dividends, storage costs or other income on the stock over the period. A2: no transaction costs, no short-sale constraint, and lending and borrowing at the riskless return below, without limit. A3: no arbitrage opportunity, as defined in Lesson 1. Equivalently, a portfolio whose cost today is at most zero and whose payoff is non-negative in every state has cost zero and payoff zero in every state. A5: the claim is paid at the end of the period only. A6: the binomial condition, stated in Definition 1.2. A3 is a hypothesis about the model, not a fact about markets. The term arbitrage opportunity is defined in Lesson 1.

Definition 1.1 (Gross riskless return)

Let \(r\) be the riskless rate over one period. The gross riskless return is \(R_f = 1 + r\). A sure payoff of 1 at the end of the period is worth \(1/R_f\) today. This lesson uses \(R_f\) for the gross return and \(r\) for the rate, and no other letter for either.

Definition 1.2 (One-period binomial model)

Binomial option pricing values a claim by replication when the stock moves between two values over each period. The stock is worth \(S_0 > 0\) today and is worth \(uS_0\) or \(dS_0\) at the end of the period, where \(u\) and \(d\) are constants. Assumption A6 is \(0 < d < R_f < u\). A claim pays \(V_1(u) = \max(uS_0 - K, 0)\) if the stock goes up and \(V_1(d) = \max(dS_0 - K, 0)\) if it goes down. This is the call payoff of Lesson 4, with strike \(K\) and expiry at the end of the period.

Definition 1.3 (Replicating portfolio, binomial)

A replicating portfolio holds \(\Delta\) shares and a bond position \(B\), where \(B > 0\) is cash lent and \(B < 0\) is cash borrowed, repaid with the gross return \(R_f\). It satisfies

\[ \Delta\, uS_0 + R_f B = V_1(u), \qquad \Delta\, dS_0 + R_f B = V_1(d). \]

Its cost today is \(V_0 = \Delta S_0 + B\).

Theorem 1.4 (One-period pricing)

Under A1, A2, A3, A5 and A6, with \(q = (R_f - d)/(u - d)\):

(a) there is exactly one replicating portfolio; (b) its cost is \(V_0 = \big(q\,V_1(u) + (1 - q)\,V_1(d)\big)/R_f\), and \(0 < q < 1\); (c) if the claim trades at a price \(\pi \ne V_0\) today, then A2 and A3 are violated, because a zero-cost portfolio with a strictly positive payoff in every state exists.

Proof

(a) Subtract the two equations of Definition 1.3: \(\Delta S_0 (u - d) = V_1(u) - V_1(d)\). Since \(u \ne d\) and \(S_0 > 0\), \(\Delta = (V_1(u) - V_1(d))/((u - d)S_0)\) is unique. Then \(R_f B = V_1(u) - u\Delta S_0\) is unique too. Both equations hold for these values, by construction.

(b) Write \(\Delta S_0 = (V_1(u) - V_1(d))/(u - d)\) and \(R_f B = V_1(u) - u\Delta S_0 = (uV_1(d) - dV_1(u))/(u - d)\). Then

\[ V_0 = \frac{V_1(u) - V_1(d)}{u - d} + \frac{uV_1(d) - dV_1(u)}{(u - d)R_f} = \frac{(R_f - d)\,V_1(u) + (u - R_f)\,V_1(d)}{(u - d)R_f}. \]

Because \(u - d > 0\), \(q = (R_f - d)/(u - d) > 0\) since \(R_f > d\), and \(1 - q = (u - R_f)/(u - d) > 0\) since \(u > R_f\). Hence \(0 < q < 1\) and \(V_0 = (qV_1(u) + (1 - q)V_1(d))/R_f\).

(c) Suppose \(\pi > V_0\). Sell the claim for \(\pi\), buy the replicating portfolio for \(V_0\), and lend the surplus \(\pi - V_0\). The cash flow today is zero. At the end of the period the claim is paid, so the position receives \(-V_1\) from the sold claim, the replicating portfolio pays \(V_1\) by (a), and the lent surplus pays \((\pi - V_0)R_f\). The total is \((\pi - V_0)R_f > 0\) in both states. If \(\pi < V_0\), reverse every position, and the total is \((V_0 - \pi)R_f > 0\) in both states. Either way a zero-cost portfolio has a strictly positive payoff, which A3 excludes. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.5 (One-period call, exact numbers)

Let \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 100\), \(u = 3/2\), \(d = 1/2\) and \(R_f = 5/4\). Then \(S_1 \in \{150, 50\}\), so \(V_1(u) = 50\) and \(V_1(d) = 0\). The replicating shares are \(\Delta = 50/(1 \cdot 100) = 1/2\), and the bond position is \(B = (u \cdot 0 - d \cdot 50)/((u - d)R_f) = -25/(5/4) = -20\). The cost is \(V_0 = 50/2 - 20 = 30\). Also \(q = (5/4 - 1/2)/(3/2 - 1/2) = 3/4\), and \((3/4 \cdot 50)/(5/4) = 30\), as Theorem 1.4 (b) requires. The value is 3000 cents. The riskless return \(R_f = 5/4\) is a model input chosen for exact arithmetic, a return of 25 percent per period, and it is not a market rate.

Example 1.6 (A mispriced call in the model of Example 1.5)

Let the call trade at \(\pi = 35\), above \(V_0 = 30\). Sell the call for 35, buy \(\Delta = 1/2\) share for 50, and borrow 20. The cash flow today is \(35 - 50 + 20 = 5\), which is lent at \(R_f\), so it pays \(5 \cdot 5/4 = 25/4\) at the end of the period. At the end of the period, the sold call costs \(-50\) if the stock goes up and 0 if it goes down. The shares and the loan give \(\tfrac12 \cdot 150 - 25 = 50\) in the up state and \(\tfrac12 \cdot 50 - 25 = 0\) in the down state. The total is \(25/4\) in both states. This is the portfolio that Theorem 1.4 (c) describes.

Remark 1.7 (The real-world probability is not in the price)

The formula in Theorem 1.4 (b) uses \(u\), \(d\), \(R_f\) and the two payoffs. It does not use the probability that the stock goes up. Two models with the same \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\) and different probabilities of an up move give the same \(V_0\), because the replication of Theorem 1.4 (a) uses no probability at all.

2The risk-neutral probability

Definition 2.1 (Risk-neutral probability, binomial)

The risk-neutral probability of an up move in the one-period binomial model is \(q = (R_f - d)/(u - d)\), and \(\mathbb{Q}(\text{up}) = q\), \(\mathbb{Q}(\text{down}) = 1 - q\). Expectations under \(\mathbb{Q}\) are written \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\).

Remark. In the binomial model, \(q\) is the weight under which the discounted stock has the same expected value next period as today. The stock's expected next-period value is \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_1] = q\,uS_0 + (1 - q)\,dS_0\), and this equals \(R_f S_0\) exactly when \(q = (R_f - d)/(u - d)\). This is an identity of the formula, not a theorem about markets. The weight \(q\) is not the probability that the stock rises: in Example 1.5, \(q = 3/4\), and the model contains no real-world probability at all. The equivalent martingale measure of a general model is a different object, defined in AP1, and this lesson does not use it.

Theorem 2.2 (Discounted expectation)

Under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.4, \(V_0 = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[V_1]/R_f\).

Proof

By definition \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[V_1] = q\,V_1(u) + (1 - q)\,V_1(d)\). Theorem 1.4 (b) states \(V_0 = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[V_1]/R_f\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Multi-period pricing by backward induction

Definition 3.1 (Recombining tree)

Let \(N \ge 1\) be the number of periods. The node \((n, k)\) is the stock price after \(n\) periods and \(k\) up moves, with \(S(n,k) = S_0 u^k d^{n - k}\). An up move then a down move and a down move then an up move reach the same node, so the tree recombines: it has \(n + 1\) nodes at time \(n\), not \(2^n\). Assumption A6 applies at every node \(n < N\) with the same \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\). This is assumption A6′.

Definition 3.2 (Backward induction)

Let \(f\) be the payoff at expiry, so the claim pays \(f(S(N,k))\) at node \((N, k)\). Define \(V(N,k) = f(S(N,k))\) and, for \(n < N\),

\[ V(n,k) = \frac{q\,V(n+1,\,k+1) + (1 - q)\,V(n+1,\,k)}{R_f}. \]
Theorem 3.3 (Multi-period pricing)

Under A1, A2, A3, A5, A6 and A6′, for \(0 \le n \le N\) the arbitrage-free price of the claim at node \((n,k)\) is \(V(n,k)\). In particular

\[ V(0,0) = R_f^{-N} \sum_{k=0}^{N} \binom{N}{k}\, q^{k} (1 - q)^{N - k}\, f\!\left(S_0 u^k d^{N-k}\right). \]
Proof

Price first. Induct backward on \(n\). At \(n = N\), the claim is paid and has no later cash flow (A5), so its price at \((N,k)\) is \(f(S(N,k)) = V(N,k)\). Suppose the price at each node \((n+1, j)\) is \(V(n+1, j)\). The one-step subtree from \((n,k)\) reaches \((n+1,k)\) and \((n+1,k+1)\), and A6 holds on it. Theorem 1.4 applied at \((n,k)\) with \(V_1(u) = V(n+1,k+1)\) and \(V_1(d) = V(n+1,k)\) gives the replicating cost \(V(n,k)\), and any other price admits the arbitrage of Theorem 1.4 (c). So the price at \((n,k)\) is \(V(n,k)\).

Closed form. Claim: for \(0 \le n \le N\),

\[ V(n,k) = R_f^{-(N-n)} \sum_{i=0}^{N-n} \binom{N-n}{i}\, q^{i} (1 - q)^{N-n-i}\, f\!\left(S(n,k)\,u^{i} d^{N-n-i}\right). \]

At \(n = N\) both sides equal \(f(S(N,k))\). Assume the claim at \(n+1\), write \(m = N - n - 1\), and note \(S(n+1,k+1) = uS(n,k)\) and \(S(n+1,k) = dS(n,k)\). Substituting into Definition 3.2, the up term has the factor \(q\) and the index \(j = i - 1\), and the down term has the factor \(1 - q\) and the index \(j = i\). Their coefficients on \(q^i(1-q)^{m+1-i} f(S(n,k)u^i d^{m+1-i})\) are \(\binom{m}{i-1}\) and \(\binom{m}{i}\). Pascal's rule \(\binom{m}{i-1} + \binom{m}{i} = \binom{m+1}{i}\) gives the sum, and the factor \(R_f^{-1} R_f^{-m} = R_f^{-(m+1)}\) gives the claim at \(n\). Setting \(n = 0\) and \(k = 0\) gives the displayed formula. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.4 (Two periods, the parameters of Example 1.5)

Use \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 100\), \(u = 3/2\), \(d = 1/2\), \(R_f = 5/4\) and a call. The expiry prices are \(S(2,2) = 225\), \(S(2,1) = 75\) and \(S(2,0) = 25\), with payoffs 125, 0 and 0. At \((1,1)\), \(S = 150\), and \(V(1,1) = (\tfrac34 \cdot 125)/\tfrac54 = 75\). At \((1,0)\), \(S = 50\), and \(V(1,0) = 0\). Then \(V(0,0) = (\tfrac34 \cdot 75)/\tfrac54 = 45\). The closed form gives \(R_f^{-2}\,\tfrac{9}{16}\cdot 125 = \tfrac{16}{25}\cdot\tfrac{9}{16}\cdot 125 = 45\). The price is 4500 cents.

Lab 1 · Replication, risk-neutral pricing and a two-period tree in exact arithmetic

Stated before running: with spot 100, strike 100, up factor 3/2, down factor 1/2 and gross return 5/4 per period, the one-period price by replication equals the discounted risk-neutral expectation, 30 (3000 cents). The two-period price by backward induction equals the closed-form path sum, 45 (4500 cents). A self-financing strategy constructed from the one-period replications reproduces each node value and each payoff at expiry. Expected result: the lines of output show these equalities, and each check prints True. All inputs are model values; no data are read.

"""Lab FC2-L06-1: one-period replication, risk-neutral pricing and a two-step binomial tree.

Every number is exact. Shares, bond positions and prices are Fraction values. Prices
are printed in integer cents (half up, which changes nothing here because every price
is already a whole number of cents). Shares and bond positions are printed as exact
fractions in currency units, because they are positions, not prices. The parameters are model
inputs chosen for exact arithmetic, not market data and not a forecast: spot 100
currency units, strike 100, up factor 3/2, down factor 1/2, and gross riskless return
5/4 per period. The claim is a European call paid at expiry, with payoff
max(S - K, 0). No input is random and no data file is read.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction

SPOT = Fraction(100)
STRIKE = Fraction(100)
UP = Fraction(3, 2)  # gross move on an up step
DOWN = Fraction(1, 2)  # gross move on a down step
RETURN = Fraction(5, 4)  # gross riskless return per period, R_f = 1 + r


def cents(amount: Fraction) -> int:
    """Integer cents of a currency amount, rounded half up (0.5 cent goes up)."""
    return math.floor(amount * 100 + Fraction(1, 2))


def payoff(price: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Payoff at expiry of the European call with the strike above, in currency units."""
    return max(price - STRIKE, Fraction(0))


def replicate(
    up_value: Fraction, down_value: Fraction, spot: Fraction, up: Fraction, down: Fraction
) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Shares and bond position that give the two next-date values, from one node.

    The bond position is the cash lent (positive) or borrowed (negative) at the node, repaid at
    RETURN per period. The two equations are solved exactly; up differs from down, so the solution is unique.
    """
    shares = (up_value - down_value) / ((up - down) * spot)
    bond = (up * down_value - down * up_value) / ((up - down) * RETURN)
    return shares, bond


def main() -> None:
    """Price one period two ways, price two periods by backward induction, and check the replication."""
    q = (RETURN - DOWN) / (UP - DOWN)
    print(f"parameters: spot={SPOT} strike={STRIKE} up={UP} down={DOWN} gross return per period={RETURN}")
    print(f"risk-neutral up probability q=(R-d)/(u-d) = {q}")

    print("one period")
    up_value, down_value = payoff(SPOT * UP), payoff(SPOT * DOWN)
    shares, bond = replicate(up_value, down_value, SPOT, UP, DOWN)
    price = shares * SPOT + bond
    print(f"  payoff after an up move = {up_value}; after a down move = {down_value}")
    print(f"  replicating shares = {shares}; bond position = {bond}")
    print(f"  replicating cost V0 = {price} = {cents(price)} cents")
    expected_value = (q * up_value + (1 - q) * down_value) / RETURN
    print(f"  discounted risk-neutral expectation = {expected_value} = {cents(expected_value)} cents")
    replicated_up = shares * SPOT * UP + bond * RETURN
    replicated_down = shares * SPOT * DOWN + bond * RETURN
    one_period_ok = replicated_up == up_value and replicated_down == down_value and price == expected_value
    print(f"  portfolio matches the payoff in both states and equals the expectation: {one_period_ok}")

    print("two periods")
    uu, ud, dd = SPOT * UP * UP, SPOT * UP * DOWN, SPOT * DOWN * DOWN
    terminal = {"uu": payoff(uu), "ud": payoff(ud), "dd": payoff(dd)}
    print(f"  stock at expiry: uu={uu} ud={ud} dd={dd}")
    print(f"  payoff at expiry: uu={terminal['uu']} ud={terminal['ud']} dd={terminal['dd']}")
    # Backward induction: each node's value is the risk-neutral discounted expectation of its two successors.
    value_after_up = (q * terminal["uu"] + (1 - q) * terminal["ud"]) / RETURN
    value_after_down = (q * terminal["ud"] + (1 - q) * terminal["dd"]) / RETURN
    value_today = (q * value_after_up + (1 - q) * value_after_down) / RETURN
    print(f"  backward induction: value after an up move = {value_after_up}; after a down move = {value_after_down}")
    print(f"  backward induction: V0 = {value_today} = {cents(value_today)} cents")

    # Closed form: discount the q-weighted sum over the paths of the tree.
    closed = (
        sum(math.comb(2, k) * q**k * (1 - q) ** (2 - k) * payoff(SPOT * UP**k * DOWN ** (2 - k)) for k in range(3))
        / RETURN**2
    )
    print(f"  closed form over paths: V0 = {closed} = {cents(closed)} cents")

    # Direct replication: hold the time-0 portfolio, rebalance at the node reached, and check the payoff.
    shares0, bond0 = replicate(value_after_up, value_after_down, SPOT, UP, DOWN)
    wealth_up = shares0 * SPOT * UP + bond0 * RETURN
    wealth_down = shares0 * SPOT * DOWN + bond0 * RETURN
    print(f"  time-0 holdings: shares {shares0}, bond {bond0}, cost {shares0 * SPOT + bond0}")
    print(
        f"  wealth after an up move = {wealth_up} (value_after_up {value_after_up}); after a down move = {wealth_down}"
    )
    shares_u, bond_u = replicate(terminal["uu"], terminal["ud"], SPOT * UP, UP, DOWN)
    shares_d, bond_d = replicate(terminal["ud"], terminal["dd"], SPOT * DOWN, UP, DOWN)
    print(f"  holdings after an up move: shares {shares_u}, bond {bond_u}")
    print(f"  holdings after a down move: shares {shares_d}, bond {bond_d}")
    # (path, terminal wealth of the strategy, payoff of the claim on that path)
    paths = [
        ("uu", shares_u * uu + bond_u * RETURN, terminal["uu"]),
        ("ud", shares_u * ud + bond_u * RETURN, terminal["ud"]),
        ("du", shares_d * ud + bond_d * RETURN, terminal["ud"]),
        ("dd", shares_d * dd + bond_d * RETURN, terminal["dd"]),
    ]
    for label, wealth_end, target in paths:
        print(f"  path {label}: terminal wealth {wealth_end} = payoff {target}: {wealth_end == target}")
    self_financing = all(wealth_end == target for _, wealth_end, target in paths)
    matches_induction = wealth_up == value_after_up and wealth_down == value_after_down
    reproduced = self_financing and matches_induction
    print(f"  self-financing strategy reproduces every node value and every payoff: {reproduced}")

    assert one_period_ok
    assert value_today == closed == Fraction(45)
    assert self_financing and matches_induction

The checks test the algebra of Theorems 1.4, 2.2 and 3.3 with these inputs, and the code that implements them. They say nothing about any market. Shares and bond positions are printed as exact fractions in currency units, and a negative bond position is borrowing. The lab keeps u, d and R_f fixed at the values of Example 1.5, so it does not test other choices of them. The self-financing path check is the direct replication: it shows that the portfolio constructed from the time-0 holdings and the rebalanced holdings pays the claim on each of the four paths, which the backward induction predicts.

4Where the canon breaks

The model has two possible next-date prices. With three possible next prices and only a stock and a bond to trade, two unknowns \(\Delta\) and \(B\) must satisfy three equations. A payoff that is not reproduced by the stock and the bond then has no replicating portfolio. For example, let the next prices be 150, 100 and 50, with payoffs 1, 0 and 0. The last two equations give \(\Delta \cdot 50 = 0\), so \(\Delta = 0\) and \(R_f B = 0\), and the first equation then reads \(0 = 1\). Theorem 1.4 does not apply to such a claim, and this lesson states no pricing rule for it.

Assumption A6′ holds \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\) fixed across nodes. The lesson takes these three numbers as given and states no rule for choosing them from data. Assumption A2 excludes transaction costs and short-sale constraints. Theorem 1.4 uses A2 for the bond position in (a) and (b) and for the arbitrage argument in (c). The lesson states nothing about prices when A2 fails.

The claims here are paid at expiry only (A5). An option that may be exercised before expiry is not valued in this lesson. Early exercise in a tree is a separate question, and Lesson 4 defines the terms for it.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A higher probability of an up move raises the price of the call.”

    Theorem 1.4 (b) gives \(V_0\) from \(u\), \(d\), \(R_f\) and the two payoffs only, and Remark 1.7 states that the real-world probability does not enter. Changing the probability of an up move changes no input of the formula, so the price in Example 1.5 stays at 30 whatever that probability is.

  2. “The risk-neutral probability \(q\) is the probability that the stock goes up.”

    In Example 1.5, \(q = 3/4\), and the stock's up factor is \(u = 3/2\). The weight \(q\) is the number that makes the discounted expected payoff equal the replicating cost (Theorem 2.2). It is a weight on the two payoffs, and the model contains no statement of how likely an up move is in the world.

  3. “The price of a call on a tree is the call's payoff at the expected final stock price, discounted.”

    In Example 3.4, \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S(2,\cdot)] = 156.25 = R_f^2 S_0\), so the discounted expected final price is \(S_0 = 100\), as Definition 2.1 requires. The payoff at that expected price is \(\max(156.25 - 100, 0) = 56.25\), and discounting it gives \(56.25 \cdot 16/25 = 36\). The correct price is 45, which is the discounted expectation of the payoff (Theorem 3.3). The expected payoff is larger than the payoff at the expected price, because the call payoff is convex in the price: before discounting, 70.3125 against 56.25.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · One period with \(u = 2\)

Let \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 100\), \(u = 2\), \(d = 1/2\) and \(R_f = 5/4\). Find \(q\) and the cost \(V_0\) of the call.

Solution

\(q = (5/4 - 1/2)/(2 - 1/2) = (3/4)/(3/2) = 1/2\). The payoffs are \(V_1(u) = 200 - 100 = 100\) and \(V_1(d) = 0\). Then \(V_0 = (\tfrac12 \cdot 100)/(5/4) = 40\), which is 4000 cents.

M2 · The replicating portfolio for M1

For the call in M1, give \(\Delta\) and \(B\), and check that \(\Delta S_0 + B = 40\).

Solution

\(\Delta = (100 - 0)/((2 - 1/2) \cdot 100) = 100/150 = 2/3\). \(B = (2 \cdot 0 - \tfrac12 \cdot 100)/((2 - \tfrac12)\cdot\tfrac54) = -50/(15/8) = -80/3\). Then \(\Delta S_0 + B = 200/3 - 80/3 = 40\), as Theorem 1.4 (b) requires.

M3 · Two periods with the M1 parameters

Use the parameters of M1 for a two-period tree and a call with strike 100. Give the expiry prices, the payoffs, the value after one up move and the price \(V(0,0)\).

Solution

Expiry prices are \(S(2,2) = 400\), \(S(2,1) = 100\) and \(S(2,0) = 25\), with payoffs 300, 0 and 0. With \(q = 1/2\), \(V(1,1) = (\tfrac12 \cdot 300)/(5/4) = 120\), and \(V(1,0) = 0\). Then \(V(0,0) = (\tfrac12 \cdot 120)/(5/4) = 48\), which is 4800 cents.

Proof

P1 · The risk-neutral weight is a probability

Under A6, show that \(0 < q < 1\) and that \(q\) and \(1 - q\) sum to 1.

Solution

By definition \(q + (1 - q) = 1\). Since \(d < R_f\), \(R_f - d > 0\), so \(q > 0\). Since \(R_f < u\), \(u - R_f > 0\), and \(1 - q = (u - R_f)/(u - d) > 0\) because \(u - d > 0\). So \(0 < q < 1\).

P2 · The replicating portfolio is unique

Show that Theorem 1.4 (a) holds without using the values of \(V_1(u)\) and \(V_1(d)\).

Solution

Subtracting the two equations of Definition 1.3 eliminates \(B\) and gives \(\Delta S_0 (u - d) = V_1(u) - V_1(d)\), a linear equation in \(\Delta\) with coefficient \(S_0(u - d)\). This coefficient is nonzero because \(S_0 > 0\) and \(u > d\), so \(\Delta\) is unique. The first equation then fixes \(R_f B\), and since \(R_f > 0\), \(B\) is unique.

P3 · An arbitrage at a higher price

Write the arbitrage of Theorem 1.4 (c) for a claim that trades at \(\pi > V_0\), and show that its payoff is \((\pi - V_0)R_f\) in both states.

Solution

Sell the claim for \(\pi\), buy \(\Delta\) shares and \(B\) bonds at cost \(V_0\), and lend \(\pi - V_0\). Today's cash is \(\pi - V_0 - (\pi - V_0) = 0\). At expiry the sold claim costs \(-V_1\), the replicating portfolio pays \(V_1\) in each state by Definition 1.3, and the lent amount pays \((\pi - V_0)R_f\). The sum is \((\pi - V_0)R_f\) in both states, which is positive.

E2 · Predict the scaling

Predict the one-period price if the spot and the strike are both doubled to 200, with \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\) unchanged. Then check the prediction in the lab's one-period formula by hand.

Solution

The payoffs double, because \(\max(uS_0 - K, 0)\) is positive homogeneous in \(S_0\) and \(K\). Theorem 1.4 (b) is linear in the payoffs, so \(V_0\) doubles to 60, or 6000 cents. By hand: \(q = 3/4\), \(V_1(u) = \max(300 - 200, 0) = 100\), and \((\tfrac34 \cdot 100)/(\tfrac54) = 60\).

E3 · Settle a quoted price

A dealer quotes the one-period call of Example 1.5 at 28. Using Theorem 1.4, say whether the quote admits an arbitrage, and describe the portfolio that exploits it.

Solution

The quote is below \(V_0 = 30\), so Theorem 1.4 (c) gives an arbitrage. Buy the call for 28. Short the replicating portfolio: sell \(1/2\) share for 50, and lend 20, which reverses the borrowing of Example 1.5. The short sale brings in \(50 - 20 = 30\). Together with the purchase, the cash flow today is \(-28 + 30 = 2\), and the surplus 2 is lent. Today's net cash is \(-28 + 30 - 2 = 0\). At expiry the call pays 50 in the up state and 0 in the down state. The short shares cost \(-\tfrac12 \cdot 150 = -75\) or \(-\tfrac12 \cdot 50 = -25\). The lent 20 returns \(20 \cdot 5/4 = 25\), and the lent 2 returns \(2 \cdot 5/4 = 5/2\). The totals are \(50 - 75 + 25 + 5/2 = 5/2\) in the up state and \(0 - 25 + 25 + 5/2 = 5/2\) in the down state, a strictly positive payoff at zero cost.

Research

R1 · A payoff with no replication

In the three-price example of Section 4, find a claim whose payoff the stock and the bond do replicate, and state what the example shows about the replication condition.

Solution

Take the claim with payoffs 50, 0 and \(-50\) at the next prices 150, 100 and 50. Hold \(\Delta = 1\) share and \(R_f B = -100\), so the bond position is \(B = -80\). The stock pays 150, 100 and 50 at the three prices, and the bond repays \(-100\) in every state, so the portfolio pays \(50\), \(0\) and \(-50\), exactly the claim. The claim is therefore replicated. The payoff (1, 0, 0) of Section 4 is not replicated: a combination \(a(150, 100, 50) + b(1, 1, 1)\) of the stock and the bond payoffs equals \((1, 0, 0)\) only if \(a = 0\) and \(b = 0\) from the last two entries, which then give \(0 = 1\) in the first. Replication is a property of the payoff vector, not of the number of states alone.

R2 · A zero rate

Take the parameters of Example 1.5 with \(R_f = 1\). Compute \(q\) and \(V_0\), and say what the zero-rate case removes from the replicating portfolio.

Solution

\(q = (1 - 1/2)/(3/2 - 1/2) = 1/2\). Then \(V_0 = (\tfrac12 \cdot 50)/1 = 25\). With \(R_f = 1\), the bond position is \(B = (u \cdot 0 - d \cdot 50)/((u - d) \cdot 1) = -25\), so 25 is borrowed and repaid with no interest. The zero rate removes the discounting from the bond position, and the replication of Theorem 1.4 (a) is unchanged.

R3 · The evidence a tree needs

State what evidence would show whether a one-period tree with fixed \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\) matches the prices of a traded option. Name one input that the model does not have. Make no claim about any market.

Solution

The test needs traded option prices at several strikes and expiries, the stock prices they refer to, and the riskless return over each period. It also needs a rule for choosing \(u\) and \(d\) from data. The model has no such rule, and it has one pair \(u\), \(d\) for the whole period. A tree that matches one option's price is not thereby shown to match another. This lesson cites no source for these inputs, so it makes no claim about any market.
