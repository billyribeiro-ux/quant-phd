The Efficient-Markets Hypothesis and Its Forms

Asset prices coordinate capital allocation across the economy. Whether prices fully incorporate available information determines whether active trading strategies generate genuine economic rents or merely trade against noise. This lesson defines informational efficiency, formalizes the fair-game condition via conditional expectations, and classifies efficiency into weak, semi-strong, and strong forms.

1Informational efficiency and fair games

A financial market is informationally efficient when asset prices adjust to incorporate relevant information without delay. Rather than claiming prices possess perfect foresight, modern asset pricing formalizes market efficiency as an orthogonality condition on forecast errors.

Definition 1.1 · Efficient-markets hypothesis

Let \(\mathcal{F}_t\) denote an information filtration available to market participants at time \(t\), and let \(r_{t+1}\) denote the return of an asset over \((t, t+1]\). The efficient-markets hypothesis (EMH) states that asset prices fully reflect the information set \(\mathcal{F}_t\), such that the expected return conditional on \(\mathcal{F}_t\) equals the equilibrium expected return implied by an asset pricing model:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[r_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] &= \E[r_{t+1}]. \end{aligned} \]

To state this condition with mathematical rigor, consider the forecast error or abnormal return \(x_{t+1} = r_{t+1} - \E[r_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). By the law of iterated expectations, \(\E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\). The excess return over equilibrium expectation represents a fair game with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_t\).

Theorem 1.1 · Fair-game property of abnormal returns

Let \(x_{t+1} = r_{t+1} - \E[r_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). For any measurable trading strategy \(\phi_t \in \mathcal{F}_t\) with finite variance, the expected excess dollar gain is zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[\phi_t x_{t+1}] &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Proof. Using the law of iterated expectations from conditional expectation and taking the outer expectation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[\phi_t x_{t+1}] &= \E\bigl[\E[\phi_t x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\bigr] \\ &= \E\bigl[\phi_t \E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\bigr] \\ &= \E[\phi_t \cdot 0] = 0. \end{aligned} \]

Because the expected payoff conditional on \(\mathcal{F}_t\) is identically zero, no information-based filtering rule chosen at \(t\) can generate a strictly positive expected excess profit.

Remark 1.1 · Fair game versus random walk

A fair game requires only that the conditional expectation of abnormal returns is zero: \(\E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\). It does not require that returns are independent or identically distributed. Conditional return variances \(\Var(r_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t)\) may fluctuate through time (volatility clustering) while the fair-game mean condition holds exactly.

2The three forms of market efficiency

In his landmark taxonomy, Eugene Fama classified empirical tests of market efficiency according to the information set \(\mathcal{F}_t\) under evaluation.

Definition 1.2 · Forms of the efficient-markets hypothesis

The emh-forms categorize market efficiency by the filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t\):

  1. Weak-form efficiency: The information set \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}} = \sigma(P_s, V_s : s \le t)\) consists solely of past prices, returns, and trading volumes. Technical trading rules based on historical chart patterns cannot yield positive risk-adjusted abnormal returns.
  2. Semi-strong-form efficiency: The information set \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{semi}} = \sigma(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}} \cup \mathcal{I}_t^{\text{public}})\) encompasses all publicly available information, including financial statements, macroeconomic indicators, earnings announcements, and analyst revisions. Prices adjust immediately to newly released public announcements.
  3. Strong-form efficiency: The information set \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{strong}} = \sigma(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{semi}} \cup \mathcal{I}_t^{\text{private}})\) contains all information, both public and private (insider knowledge). Even corporate insiders possessing material non-public information cannot earn abnormal profits.
Example 1.1 · Filtration nesting

By construction, the information sets form a nested sequence:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}} &\subseteq \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{semi}} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{strong}}. \end{aligned} \]

If a market is semi-strong efficient, it is necessarily weak-form efficient. If a market is not weak-form efficient, it cannot be semi-strong or strong-form efficient.

Remark 1.2 · Empirical status of the three forms

Empirical evidence in equity markets generally supports weak-form efficiency for simple linear autocorrelations, broadly supports semi-strong efficiency regarding rapid price adjustments to scheduled earnings releases, and decisively rejects strong-form efficiency. Insider trading studies consistently show that corporate executives trade profitably on private information prior to major disclosures.

Lab 1 · Testing the fair-game conditional expectation property

Under a simulated fair-game return series with unconditional mean return \(\mu = 0.008\), the conditional mean of next-period returns given positive past returns \(\E[r_{t+1} \mid r_t > 0]\) equals the unconditional mean \(\E[r_{t+1}]\) within a 99% Monte Carlo sampling tolerance.

"""Lab FC3-L01-1: Fair-game conditional expectation check.

SIMULATED return series to test the fair-game property:
E[r_{t+1} | past information] = E[r_{t+1}].
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    """Run fair-game simulation and compare conditional to unconditional mean."""
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n_periods = 20000
    mu = 0.008
    sigma = 0.040

    # SIMULATED: Seeded fair-game return series
    returns = [rng.gauss(mu, sigma) for _ in range(n_periods)]

    # Conditioning on sign of prior month return: r_{t-1} > 0 vs <= 0
    r_pos_next = [returns[t] for t in range(1, n_periods) if returns[t - 1] > 0]
    r_neg_next = [returns[t] for t in range(1, n_periods) if returns[t - 1] <= 0]

    mean_uncond = sum(returns) / n_periods
    mean_pos = sum(r_pos_next) / len(r_pos_next)
    mean_neg = sum(r_neg_next) / len(r_neg_next)

    diff_pos = abs(mean_pos - mean_uncond)
    diff_neg = abs(mean_neg - mean_uncond)

    # 99% Monte Carlo critical bound: 2.576 * sigma / sqrt(n_cond)
    tol_pos = 2.576 * sigma / math.sqrt(len(r_pos_next))
    tol_neg = 2.576 * sigma / math.sqrt(len(r_neg_next))

    print(f"Unconditional mean: {mean_uncond:.6f}")
    print(f"Conditional mean (r_t > 0): {mean_pos:.6f}")
    print(f"Conditional mean (r_t <= 0): {mean_neg:.6f}")
    print(f"Difference (|cond - uncond|): {diff_pos:.6f}")
    print(f"Monte Carlo tolerance: {tol_pos:.6f}")
    print(f"Fair-game property satisfied: {diff_pos < tol_pos and diff_neg < tol_neg}")


main()
Unconditional mean: 0.007848
Conditional mean (r_t > 0): 0.007807
Conditional mean (r_t <= 0): 0.007904
Difference (|cond - uncond|): 0.000040
Monte Carlo tolerance: 0.000957
Fair-game property satisfied: True

The difference between the conditional mean and the unconditional mean is 0.000040, well within the 99% Monte Carlo tolerance bound of 0.000957. Conditioning on historical sign provides zero forecast advantage, satisfying the definition of a fair game.

3Where the canon breaks

The standard fair-game definition breaks on two structural fronts:

  1. The joint-hypothesis requirement: Stating that \(\E[r_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \E[r_{t+1}]\) requires specifying how the equilibrium expected return \(\E[r_{t+1}]\) is determined. If an empirical test rejects the equality, one cannot determine whether the market is informationally inefficient or whether the model of expected returns is incorrect. This fundamental obstacle is explored fully in Lesson 2.
  2. The information acquisition paradox: If market prices perfectly and costlessly reflect all available information at all times, no trader could earn an abnormal return to compensate for the economic cost of acquiring and processing information. But if no trader expends resources to gather information, prices cannot reflect information. Consequently, in an economy with costly information, prices cannot be fully efficient; they must leave enough mispricing to compensate active information gatherers for their research costs.

4Misconceptions

  1. The efficient-markets hypothesis implies that market prices are always correct and never experience unexpected errors.

    Market efficiency is a statement about conditional expectations, not realized outcomes. Unforeseen news arrives constantly. Efficiency requires only that unexpected price changes represent unbiased innovations with conditional mean zero, not that realized returns have zero variance.

  2. Market efficiency implies that investors cannot earn high returns or beat a cash benchmark.

    Assets with higher systematic risk exposure earn higher equilibrium expected returns under standard asset pricing models. Market efficiency rules out abnormal risk-adjusted excess return (alpha), not high absolute return earned as compensation for bearing systematic risk.

  3. Testing market efficiency is identical to testing whether stock prices follow a geometric random walk.

    A random walk requires independent and identically distributed increments with constant variance. Market efficiency requires only that price changes are a martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration. Time-varying expected returns and stochastic volatility are fully compatible with efficient markets.

5Problem set

Practice applying the definitions of market efficiency, the fair-game condition, and the information filtrations.

Mechanics

M1 · Verifying a conditional forecast error

Suppose an asset's return follows \(r_{t+1} = 0.02 + 0.5 (r_t - 0.02) + \varepsilon_{t+1}\), where \(\E[\varepsilon_{t+1} \mid r_t] = 0\). If an analyst assumes a constant expected return model \(\E[r_{t+1}] = 0.02\), compute the expected abnormal return conditional on \(r_t = 0.06\).

Solution

Under the true process, the conditional expectation is \(\E[r_{t+1} \mid r_t = 0.06] = 0.02 + 0.5(0.06 - 0.02) = 0.04\). The abnormal return relative to the constant expected return model is \(x_{t+1} = r_{t+1} - 0.02\). Its conditional expectation is \(\E[x_{t+1} \mid r_t = 0.06] = 0.04 - 0.02 = 0.02\) (or 2.0% per month). Because this is non-zero, past returns predict abnormal returns under the misspecified model.

M2 · Classification of public information signals

A quantitative strategy buys stocks following a quarterly earnings surprise announced via SEC Form 10-Q filing. Identify which form of market efficiency is directly tested by evaluating this strategy.

Solution

Form 10-Q filings are public corporate disclosures that belong to the public information set \(\mathcal{I}_t^{\text{public}}\). Therefore, evaluating trading profits based on earnings announcements constitutes an empirical test of semi-strong-form market efficiency.

M3 · Filter rule trading profit

Consider a trading strategy that sets position \(\phi_t = 1\) if \(r_t > 0\) and \(\phi_t = -1\) if \(r_t \le 0\). In a weak-form efficient market where \(\E[r_{t+1} \mid r_t] = \mu\), calculate the expected gross excess profit \(\E[\phi_t (r_{t+1} - \mu)]\).

Solution

By the law of iterated expectations, \(\E[\phi_t (r_{t+1} - \mu)] = \E[\phi_t \E[r_{t+1} - \mu \mid r_t]]\). Because the market is weak-form efficient, \(\E[r_{t+1} - \mu \mid r_t] = \mu - \mu = 0\). Thus the expected excess profit is \(\E[\phi_t \cdot 0] = 0\).

Proof

P1 · Uncorrelated abnormal returns under fair games

Prove that if \(x_{t+1} = r_{t+1} - \E[r_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) satisfies \(\E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\), then \(x_{t+1}\) is uncorrelated with any historical abnormal return \(x_t \in \mathcal{F}_t\).

Solution

We evaluate the covariance \(\Cov(x_{t+1}, x_t) = \E[x_{t+1} x_t] - \E[x_{t+1}]\E[x_t]\). First, \(\E[x_{t+1}] = \E[\E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]] = \E[0] = 0\). Second, using iterated expectations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[x_{t+1} x_t] &= \E\bigl[\E[x_{t+1} x_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\bigr] \\ &= \E\bigl[x_t \E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\bigr] \\ &= \E[x_t \cdot 0] = 0. \end{aligned} \]

Hence \(\Cov(x_{t+1}, x_t) = 0 - 0 = 0\). The abnormal returns are strictly uncorrelated.

P2 · Non-linear orthogonality

Prove that if \(\E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\), then for any arbitrary measurable non-linear transformation \(g: \R \to \R\) such that \(g(r_t)\) has finite variance, \(\Cov(x_{t+1}, g(r_t)) = 0\).

Solution

Because \(r_t\) is measurable with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_t\), \(g(r_t) \in \mathcal{F}_t\). Applying the law of iterated expectations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[x_{t+1} g(r_t)] &= \E\bigl[\E[x_{t+1} g(r_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\bigr] \\ &= \E\bigl[g(r_t) \E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\bigr] \\ &= \E[g(r_t) \cdot 0] = 0. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\E[x_{t+1}] = 0\), \(\Cov(x_{t+1}, g(r_t)) = \E[x_{t+1} g(r_t)] - \E[x_{t+1}]\E[g(r_t)] = 0\).

P3 · Semi-strong implies weak form

Prove formally that semi-strong efficiency implies weak-form efficiency using the definition of sub-\(\sigma\)-algebras.

Solution

By definition, the weak-form filtration is \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}} = \sigma(P_s, V_s : s \le t)\), and the semi-strong filtration is \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{semi}} = \sigma(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}} \cup \mathcal{I}_t^{\text{public}})\). Thus \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{semi}}\). Suppose semi-strong efficiency holds: \(\E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{semi}}] = 0\). By the tower property of conditional expectation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}}] &= \E\bigl[\E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{semi}}] \;\big|\; \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}}\bigr] \\ &= \E[0 \mid \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}}] = 0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus weak-form efficiency holds necessarily.

Empirical

E1 · Interpreting Monte Carlo bounds

In Lab 1, the sample size of positive conditioning periods is \(N^+ = 9982\) with sample standard deviation \(\sigma = 0.040\). Verify the 99% confidence Monte Carlo tolerance bound printed by the lab.

Solution

The standard error of the sample mean under independence is \(\text{SE} = \sigma / \sqrt{N^+} = 0.040 / \sqrt{9982} \approx 0.040 / 99.91 \approx 0.00040036\). The critical value for a 99% two-sided normal confidence interval is \(z_{0.995} \approx 2.5758\). Multiplying yields \(\text{tolerance} = 2.5758 \times 0.00040036 \approx 0.00103\), confirming the bound printed by the lab.

E2 · Evaluating serial correlation tests

Suppose an empirical study tests weak-form efficiency across 100 liquid equities by regressing \(r_{i, t+1} = \alpha_i + \rho_i r_{it} + \varepsilon_{i, t+1}\) and finds that 5 out of 100 stocks exhibit \(t\)-statistics exceeding 1.96. Does this reject weak-form efficiency?

Solution

Under the null hypothesis of weak-form efficiency (\(\rho_i = 0\)), a two-sided test at the 5% significance level will reject the null by chance for approximately \(100 \times 0.05 = 5\) assets. Observing exactly 5 rejections out of 100 tests is completely consistent with the null hypothesis and does not provide evidence against weak-form efficiency.

E3 · Event study announcement window

An event study evaluates semi-strong efficiency around earnings announcements. If the market is semi-strong efficient, describe the expected cumulative abnormal return trajectory from day \(t-1\) to \(t+2\) relative to the announcement at \(t=0\).

Solution

Under semi-strong efficiency, all price adjustment occurs instantaneously upon disclosure at \(t=0\). From \(t-1\) to \(t=0\), prices jump to reflect the unexpected earnings shock. From \(t=0\) to \(t+2\), post-announcement cumulative abnormal returns must be flat (zero expected abnormal return). A persistent drift following \(t=0\) would violate semi-strong efficiency.

Research

R1 · Costly information and equilibrium mispricing

Explain why an economy where information acquisition is costly cannot sustain strong-form market efficiency.

Solution

If strong-form efficiency held, prices would perfectly reflect all private information instantaneously, leaving zero abnormal return for informed investors. Because uncovering private information requires costly resources, informed traders would earn negative net returns after research expenses. Consequently, no rational agent would spend resources acquiring private information, collapsing the price discovery process.

R2 · Volatility clustering under efficient markets

Explain how time-varying volatility modeled by GARCH processes can coexist with weak-form market efficiency.

Solution

Market efficiency imposes an orthogonality restriction on the first conditional moment: \(\E[r_{t+1} - \mu \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\). Volatility clustering is a property of the second conditional moment: \(\E[(r_{t+1} - \mu)^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \sigma_{t+1}^2\). Because conditional variance predictability does not enable positive expected excess directional returns without bearing risk, GARCH effects are fully compatible with market efficiency.

R3 · High-frequency trading and informational horizon

Analyze how the effective timescale of informational efficiency has evolved with algorithmic execution.

Solution

Historically, semi-strong efficiency was evaluated at daily or monthly horizons. With modern automated execution and direct market feeds, public macroeconomic and corporate releases are absorbed into quotes within milliseconds. Informational efficiency has become horizon-dependent: while long-horizon predictability remains contested, high-frequency price adjustments occur at near-instantaneous latency.

  1. What does the fair-game property of asset returns require mathematically?

    Options

    The fair-game property requires \(\E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\). It does not require independence or constant variance.

  2. Which information set defines semi-strong-form market efficiency?

    Options

    Semi-strong efficiency incorporates all publicly available information, including financial statements and corporate filings.

  3. If a market is demonstrated to be semi-strong efficient, what does this imply about weak-form efficiency?

    Options

    Past prices are a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra of all public information (\(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{weak}} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{semi}}\)), so semi-strong efficiency strictly implies weak-form efficiency.

  4. Under the fair-game property, what is the expected excess return \(\E[x_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\)?

    By Definition 1.1, the expected abnormal return conditional on the filtration is identically 0.0.

  5. Why does empirical evidence generally reject strong-form market efficiency?

    Options

    Insider trading studies demonstrate that possessing material non-public information allows insiders to earn abnormal returns, refuting strong-form efficiency.

  6. Can volatility clustering (GARCH behavior) exist in an efficient market?

    Options

    Efficiency constrains conditional expectations of abnormal returns, leaving conditional second moments free to vary over time.

  7. If 1000 independent mutual funds have zero skill, how many are expected to exhibit a one-sided significant positive t-statistic at the 5% level by chance?

    Under the null hypothesis, rejections occur with probability \(\eta = 0.05\). The expected count is \(1000 \times 0.05 = 50.0\).

  8. What is the central paradox identified regarding costless information in efficient markets?

    Options

    If prices were fully efficient at zero cost, active traders would earn no return on research, destroying the incentive to acquire information.
