The Joint-Hypothesis Problem and the Arithmetic of Active Management

Evaluating fund performance requires benchmark models, but any apparent abnormal return might reflect benchmark error rather than manager skill. Furthermore, before considering statistical noise, market clearing imposes strict accounting identities on aggregate returns. This lesson formalizes the joint-hypothesis dilemma and proves Sharpe's arithmetic of active management.

1The joint-hypothesis problem

In Lesson 1, the efficient-markets hypothesis was formalized by stating that abnormal returns have conditional expectation zero. However, defining an abnormal return requires a model of normal expected returns.

Definition 2.1 · Joint-hypothesis problem

Any empirical test of market efficiency evaluates the composite hypothesis \(H_0: \text{EMH} \cap \text{Model}\), where \(\text{Model}\) specifies the equilibrium expected return \(\E[r_{it} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\). The joint-hypothesis problem states that an empirical rejection of \(H_0\) cannot distinguish whether the market is informationally inefficient or whether the benchmark asset pricing model is misspecified.

In standard empirical practice, risk-adjusted performance is evaluated using Jensen's alpha relative to a linear factor model such as the CAPM:

\[ \begin{aligned} r_{it} - r_{ft} &= \alpha_i + \beta_i (r_{mt} - r_{ft}) + \varepsilon_{it}. \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 2.1 · Omitted factor distortion

Assume the true data-generating process follows a two-factor model \(r_{it} - r_{ft} = \beta_{i1} f_{1t} + \beta_{i2} f_{2t} + u_{it}\) with \(\alpha_i^{\text{true}} = 0\), \(\E[u_{it} \mid f_{1t}, f_{2t}] = 0\), and factor risk premia \(\E[f_{1t}] = \mu_1, \E[f_{2t}] = \mu_2\). If an analyst estimates a single-factor regression on \(f_{1t}\), the estimated intercept is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_i^{\text{apparent}} &= \beta_{i2} \left( \mu_2 - \frac{\Cov(f_{1t}, f_{2t})}{\Var(f_{1t})} \mu_1 \right). \end{aligned} \]

Proof. Taking expectations of the single-factor regression specification \(r_{it} - r_{ft} = a_i + b_i f_{1t} + \varepsilon_{it}\), the population slope is \(b_i = \Cov(r_i, f_1) / \Var(f_1) = \beta_{i1} + \beta_{i2} \Cov(f_1, f_2) / \Var(f_1)\). The population intercept is:

\[ \begin{aligned} a_i &= \E[r_{it} - r_{ft}] - b_i \E[f_{1t}] \\ &= \beta_{i1} \mu_1 + \beta_{i2} \mu_2 - \left(\beta_{i1} + \beta_{i2} \frac{\Cov(f_1, f_2)}{\Var(f_1)}\right) \mu_1 \\ &= \beta_{i2} \left( \mu_2 - \frac{\Cov(f_1, f_2)}{\Var(f_1)} \mu_1 \right). \end{aligned} \]

Whenever \(\beta_{i2} \ne 0\) and the term in parentheses is non-zero, the estimated alpha is strictly non-zero despite the true asset possessing zero abnormal rent.

Remark 2.1 · Empirical consequence of the dilemma

Eugene Fama emphasized that because expected returns cannot be observed directly without a model, market efficiency is not a standalone refutable empirical proposition. Any anomaly can always be interpreted either as proof of market irrationality or as an omitted risk factor.

2The arithmetic of active management

Beyond factor model specification, macroeconomic market clearing dictates aggregate portfolio returns. In 1991, William Sharpe established that active management is an arithmetic negative-sum game after costs.

Definition 2.2 · Active and passive management

Consider a market consisting of \(M\) risky assets with market capitalization vector \(V = (V_1, \dots, V_M)^\top\) and aggregate market wealth \(W = \sum_{j=1}^M V_j\). The market portfolio weights are \(w_m = V / W\). Investors are divided into two mutually exclusive, exhaustive categories:

  1. Passive investors: Hold each asset \(j\) in exact proportion to its market capitalization weight \(w_{mj} = V_j / W\).
  2. Active investors: Any investor whose portfolio weights differ from \(w_m\).

The arithmetic of active management refers to the market-clearing accounting identity linking passive, active, and market returns.

Theorem 2.2 · Sharpe's arithmetic of active management

Let passive investors hold fraction \(\theta \in (0, 1)\) of total market wealth. Under assumptions A1 (market clearing) and A2 (exhaustiveness):

  1. Before costs: The value-weighted average gross return of active investors equals the gross return of the market, which equals the gross return of passive investors: \[ \begin{aligned} R_{\text{active}}^{\text{gross}} &= R_{\text{passive}}^{\text{gross}} = R_m. \end{aligned} \]
  2. After costs: Let active management incur average expense ratio \(c_{\text{active}}\) and passive management incur \(c_{\text{passive}}\). If \(c_{\text{active}} > c_{\text{passive}}\), the value-weighted active return is strictly less than the passive return: \[ \begin{aligned} R_{\text{passive}}^{\text{net}} - R_{\text{active}}^{\text{net}} &= c_{\text{active}} - c_{\text{passive}} > 0. \end{aligned} \]

Proof. The total dollar holding in asset \(j\) across all investors is \(V_j\). Passive investors hold \(\theta V_j\) in asset \(j\). By market clearing (assumption A1), active investors must hold the remaining dollars:

\[ \begin{aligned} H_{\text{active}, j} &= V_j - \theta V_j = (1 - \theta) V_j. \end{aligned} \]

Total wealth held by all active investors is \(\sum_j H_{\text{active}, j} = (1 - \theta) W\). The value-weighted gross return of active management across all \(M\) assets with returns \(r_j\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} R_{\text{active}}^{\text{gross}} &= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^M H_{\text{active}, j} r_j}{(1 - \theta) W} \\ &= \frac{(1 - \theta) \sum_{j=1}^M V_j r_j}{(1 - \theta) W} \\ &= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^M V_j r_j}{W} = R_m. \end{aligned} \]

Since passive investors hold market weights by definition, \(R_{\text{passive}}^{\text{gross}} = R_m\). Subtracting operating expenses and trading costs yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} R_{\text{active}}^{\text{net}} &= R_m - c_{\text{active}}, \\ R_{\text{passive}}^{\text{net}} &= R_m - c_{\text{passive}}. \end{aligned} \]

Subtracting the active net return from the passive net return gives \(R_{\text{passive}}^{\text{net}} - R_{\text{active}}^{\text{net}} = c_{\text{active}} - c_{\text{passive}}\), which is strictly positive whenever active fees exceed passive fees.

Remark 2.2 · Active management as a zero-sum game before costs

Before costs, active management is a zero-sum game against other active managers: every dollar of outperformance earned by an active manager holding an overweight position in a winning stock is matched by an equal dollar of underperformance borne by another active manager holding an underweight position. After costs, active management is a negative-sum game in aggregate.

Lab 1 · Sharpe's arithmetic of active management, simulated

Under exact rational arithmetic (fractions.Fraction) on a simulated four-asset universe with 35% passive ownership, the value-weighted gross return of active investors equals the passive gross return identically (0.050000), and the net underperformance of the active group exactly equals the expense gap (0.011000, or 110 bps).

"""Lab FC3-L02-1: Sharpe's arithmetic of active management, simulated.

SIMULATED exact Fraction arithmetic on a market universe verifying that
before costs the active group matches the passive group, and after costs
underperforms by exactly the expense gap.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    """Demonstrate Sharpe (1991) arithmetic of active management exactly."""
    # SIMULATED: Market of 4 assets with market capitalizations ($M)
    caps = [Fraction(150), Fraction(250), Fraction(350), Fraction(250)]
    total_wealth = sum(caps)  # 1000
    market_weights = [c / total_wealth for c in caps]

    # Gross asset returns for the period
    asset_returns = [Fraction(14, 100), Fraction(9, 100), Fraction(4, 100), Fraction(-3, 100)]

    # Market portfolio return
    r_market = sum(w * r for w, r in zip(market_weights, asset_returns, strict=True))

    # Passive investors hold 35% of total wealth in index proportions
    theta = Fraction(35, 100)
    passive_wealth = theta * total_wealth
    active_wealth = (1 - theta) * total_wealth

    # Passive holdings: exactly market weights
    passive_holdings = [theta * c for c in caps]
    r_passive_gross = sum(h * r for h, r in zip(passive_holdings, asset_returns, strict=True)) / passive_wealth

    # Active holdings: market clearing implies Active = Total - Passive = (1 - theta) * Total
    active_holdings = [caps[j] - passive_holdings[j] for j in range(len(caps))]
    r_active_gross = sum(h * r for h, r in zip(active_holdings, asset_returns, strict=True)) / active_wealth

    # Cost schedules: passive 10 bps (0.10%), active 120 bps (1.20%)
    cost_passive = Fraction(1, 1000)
    cost_active = Fraction(12, 1000)

    r_passive_net = r_passive_gross - cost_passive
    r_active_net = r_active_gross - cost_active
    net_shortfall = r_passive_net - r_active_net
    cost_diff = cost_active - cost_passive

    print(f"Market return: {float(r_market):.6f} ({r_market})")
    print(f"Passive gross return: {float(r_passive_gross):.6f} ({r_passive_gross})")
    print(f"Active gross return: {float(r_active_gross):.6f} ({r_active_gross})")
    print(f"Gross difference (active - passive): {float(r_active_gross - r_passive_gross):.6f}")
    print(f"Passive net return: {float(r_passive_net):.6f} ({r_passive_net})")
    print(f"Active net return: {float(r_active_net):.6f} ({r_active_net})")
    print(f"Net shortfall (passive - active): {float(net_shortfall):.6f} ({net_shortfall})")
    print(f"Cost difference (c_act - c_pass): {float(cost_diff):.6f} ({cost_diff})")
    print(f"Arithmetic identity verified: {net_shortfall == cost_diff}")


main()

Active gross return: 0.050000 (1/20)
Gross difference (active - passive): 0.000000
Passive net return: 0.049000 (49/1000)
Active net return: 0.038000 (19/500)

The identity holds exactly: gross active return minus gross passive return is 0.000000. After deducting 10 bps for passive indexers and 120 bps for active managers, the net shortfall is exactly 110 bps (11/1000). Active underperformance in aggregate is an accounting necessity, not a statistical artifact.

3Where the canon breaks

Sharpe's arithmetic is an indisputable macroeconomic theorem, but its application to empirical finance has specific boundaries:

  1. Equal-weighted versus value-weighted performance: Sharpe's theorem applies strictly to value-weighted aggregate dollars. An empirical count of active funds (equal-weighted average) can in principle have more than half of funds beating the benchmark if outperformance is concentrated among small funds, while large funds underperform.
  2. Cross-sectional heterogeneity: The aggregate arithmetic does not imply that every active manager underperforms. A skilled minority can earn persistent positive net alpha if less skilled active investors and retail noise traders suffer losses exceeding aggregate fees.
  3. Cash drag and benchmark mismatch: Active funds maintain transactional cash buffers to manage investor redemptions. In bull markets, cash drag automatically depresses gross active returns relative to fully invested market indices, whereas in bear markets cash dampens drawdowns.

4Misconceptions

  1. Because active management is a zero-sum game before costs, approximately 50 percent of active mutual funds will beat the market index after costs.

    Active management is zero-sum in aggregate dollars before costs, but strictly negative-sum after management fees and turnover costs. Furthermore, fund size skewness and fee distributions typically result in 70% to 85% of active mutual funds underperforming broad passive index benchmarks over multi-year horizons.

  2. A statistically significant non-zero alpha in a mutual fund regression proves that the manager has skill and that the market is inefficient.

    The joint-hypothesis problem demonstrates that a significant alpha can arise from an omitted risk factor (e.g., size, value, momentum) that the manager systematically tilts toward, rather than genuine stock-picking skill.

  3. Sharpe's arithmetic only applies if markets are informationally efficient.

    Sharpe's arithmetic relies purely on market clearing and accounting identities. It requires zero assumptions about market efficiency, investor rationality, price formation, or normal distributions.

Mechanics

M1 · Computing net active underperformance

A mutual fund sector manages $800 billion with an average expense ratio of 1.15% and annual trading turnover costs of 0.35%. Passive index funds charge 0.08% with negligible turnover costs. Calculate the annual dollar penalty borne by active investors in aggregate.

Solution

Total active costs are \(c_{\text{active}} = 1.15\% + 0.35\% = 1.50\%\). Total passive costs are \(c_{\text{passive}} = 0.08\%\). The net cost gap is \(1.50\% - 0.08\% = 1.42\%\). Multiplying by the active asset base of $800 billion yields an annual aggregate dollar penalty of \(\$800 \times 10^9 \times 0.0142 = \$11.36\text{ billion}\).

M2 · Omitted factor alpha calculation

Suppose the true model is a two-factor model where \(\mu_1 = 0.06, \mu_2 = 0.04\), \(\Var(f_1) = 0.04\), and \(\Cov(f_1, f_2) = 0.01\). A fund has true loadings \(\beta_1 = 1.0, \beta_2 = 0.8\), and true alpha \(\alpha = 0\). Compute the apparent alpha when regressed on factor 1 alone.

Solution

Using Theorem 2.1, the omitted factor distortion is \(\alpha_i^{\text{apparent}} = \beta_{i2} (\mu_2 - \frac{\Cov(f_1, f_2)}{\Var(f_1)} \mu_1)\). The regression coefficient of \(f_2\) on \(f_1\) is \(0.01 / 0.04 = 0.25\). Thus \(\alpha_i^{\text{apparent}} = 0.8 \times (0.04 - 0.25 \times 0.06) = 0.8 \times (0.04 - 0.015) = 0.8 \times 0.025 = 0.020\) (or 2.0% per year).

M3 · Portfolio weights under market clearing

In a 2-stock market with total capitalizations \(V_1 = \$600\text{M}\) and \(V_2 = \$400\text{M}\), passive indexers hold 40% of all wealth. Active Manager A manages $200M holding $150M in Stock 1 and $50M in Stock 2. Find the aggregate dollar holdings of all remaining active managers.

Solution

Total market wealth is \(W = \$1000\text{M}\). Passive investors hold \(40\% \times 600 = \$240\text{M}\) in Stock 1 and \(40\% \times 400 = \$160\text{M}\) in Stock 2. Total active holdings must equal \(V_j - H_{\text{passive}, j}\): Stock 1 total active is \(\$600\text{M} - \$240\text{M} = \$360\text{M}\); Stock 2 total active is \(\$400\text{M} - \$160\text{M} = \$240\text{M}\). Since Manager A holds $150M of Stock 1 and $50M of Stock 2, remaining active managers hold \(\$360\text{M} - \$150\text{M} = \$210\text{M}\) of Stock 1 and \(\$240\text{M} - \$50\text{M} = \$190\text{M}\) of Stock 2.

Proof

P1 · Zero-sum active excess dollar returns

Prove that the dollar-weighted excess return of active management relative to the market portfolio across all active dollars is identically zero before costs.

Solution

Let active investor \(k\) hold dollar amount \(A_{kj}\) in asset \(j\), with total active wealth \(A = \sum_k \sum_j A_{kj} = (1 - \theta) W\). Market clearing requires \(\sum_k A_{kj} = (1 - \theta) V_j\). The aggregate active excess dollar return over the market return \(R_m = \sum_j V_j r_j / W\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k} A_k (R_k - R_m) &= \sum_{k} \sum_{j} A_{kj} r_j - A R_m \\ &= \sum_{j} \left(\sum_k A_{kj}\right) r_j - (1 - \theta) W \frac{\sum_j V_j r_j}{W} \\ &= (1 - \theta) \sum_j V_j r_j - (1 - \theta) \sum_j V_j r_j = 0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus active excess dollar gains and losses sum to exactly zero before costs.

P2 · Orthogonal factor decomposition

Prove that if factor 2 is orthogonal to factor 1 (\(\Cov(f_1, f_2) = 0\)), the omitted factor bias in the single-factor intercept equals \(\beta_{i2} \E[f_2]\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.1, the apparent intercept is \(\alpha_i^{\text{apparent}} = \beta_{i2} (\mu_2 - \frac{\Cov(f_1, f_2)}{\Var(f_1)} \mu_1)\). Setting \(\Cov(f_1, f_2) = 0\), the fraction vanishes, leaving \(\alpha_i^{\text{apparent}} = \beta_{i2} \mu_2 = \beta_{i2} \E[f_2]\). The intercept is biased whenever the omitted factor has a non-zero risk premium.

P3 · Independence of Sharpe's arithmetic from market efficiency

Prove that Sharpe's arithmetic does not require prices to equal fundamental values or information sets to be martingales.

Solution

The proof of Theorem 2.2 uses only two algebraic facts: (1) \(V_j = H_{\text{passive}, j} + H_{\text{active}, j}\) by physical market clearing, and (2) \(H_{\text{passive}, j} = \theta V_j\) by the definition of an index fund. No expectations, probabilities, price formation mechanisms, or informational filtrations are invoked. Hence the arithmetic holds in both efficient and wildly irrational markets.

Empirical

E1 · Mutual fund aggregate performance

In their comprehensive mutual fund study, Fama and French (2010) found that before costs, the average mutual fund alpha was close to zero, while after costs, net alphas were negative by approximately the fund expense ratio. Explain how this confirms Sharpe's arithmetic.

Solution

Fama and French observed that gross fund returns roughly match factor benchmarks (gross alpha \(\approx 0\)), while net returns underperform benchmarks by approximately 80 to 100 basis points annually, matching typical mutual fund expense ratios. This directly confirms Sharpe's deduction that active managers as a whole capture market returns before fees and deliver market returns minus expenses after fees.

E2 · Equal-weighting vs value-weighting divergence

Explain why an equal-weighted average of mutual fund alphas might be negative even before costs if smaller funds trade with higher market impact.

Solution

Sharpe's arithmetic guarantees that value-weighted active gross returns equal the market return. Smaller funds represent a tiny fraction of aggregate active dollars. If small funds incur severe market impact or hold micro-cap stocks with high bid-ask drag, their equal-weighted alpha can be significantly negative while dollar-weighted gross returns remain balanced.

E3 · Roll's critique as a joint hypothesis

Relate Richard Roll's 1977 critique of the CAPM to the joint-hypothesis problem.

Solution

Roll demonstrated that tests of the CAPM are tests of whether the proxy market portfolio is mean-variance efficient. Because the true market portfolio includes all wealth (real estate, human capital, foreign assets), any empirical proxy is incomplete. Thus, an apparent CAPM alpha could merely indicate that the S&P 500 is not the true market portfolio, exemplifying the joint-hypothesis problem.

Research

R1 · Berk-Green equilibrium and Sharpe's arithmetic

How does the Berk and Green (2004) model of rational mutual fund flows reconcile managerial skill with zero average net alpha?

Solution

Berk and Green assume skilled managers face decreasing returns to scale in deploying capital. Rational investors chase positive alpha, allocating capital to skilled funds until each fund's assets under management expand to the point where scale diseconomies and management fees erode net alpha to zero. Thus, skilled managers capture all economic rents via fee income, leaving net returns to investors equal to benchmark returns.

R2 · The growth of passive indexing and market efficiency

Evaluate the argument that as passive investing expands toward 90% of total market equity, informational market efficiency must degrade.

Solution

Sharpe's arithmetic shows that active managers set marginal prices regardless of whether \(\theta = 0.10\) or \(\theta = 0.90\). If passive share grows, fewer active dollars compete for price discovery, potentially increasing mispricing. However, this higher mispricing increases expected abnormal returns for remaining active managers, establishing an equilibrium active share where information gathering costs balance available mispricing.

R3 · Multifactor benchmark models as joint-hypothesis shields

Discuss why the asset pricing literature continually introduces additional factors (e.g., Fama-French 3-factor, 5-factor) when return anomalies are discovered.

Solution

When an empirical anomaly produces non-zero alpha under an existing benchmark, researchers can preserve market efficiency by defining a new factor tracking that anomaly. By expanding the pricing kernel to absorb the anomaly into the expected return model, the unexplained alpha is reduced to zero, directly manifesting the joint-hypothesis dilemma.
