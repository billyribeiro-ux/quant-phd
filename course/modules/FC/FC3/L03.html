Luck Versus Skill in Fund Performance

In a cross-section of thousands of active mutual funds, some managers inevitably achieve stellar track records by sheer good fortune. An allocator who mistakes random chance for genuine investment acumen will award mandates to lucky gamblers who mean-revert. This lesson develops the statistical framework to separate luck from skill, formalizes false-positive benchmarks, and analyzes bootstrap null distributions.

1The cross-section of fund performance under zero skill

Consider an institutional universe of \(N\) active funds, each observed over \(T\) months. For each fund \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), performance relative to a benchmark factor is evaluated via an OLS time-series regression:

\[ \begin{aligned} r_{it} - r_{ft} &= \alpha_i + \beta_i (r_{mt} - r_{ft}) + \varepsilon_{it}. \end{aligned} \]

The estimated intercept \(\hat\alpha_i\) and its t-statistic \(\hat t_i = \hat\alpha_i / \widehat{\text{se}}(\hat\alpha_i)\) form the empirical cross-section of performance.

Definition 3.1 · Luck versus skill

The problem of luck versus skill is the statistical challenge of determining whether the extreme right tail of observed fund performance exceeds what would be generated purely by sampling variability under the global null hypothesis of zero managerial skill:

\[ \begin{aligned} H_0: \alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \cdots = \alpha_N = 0. \end{aligned} \]

A fund exhibits genuine skill only if its performance cannot be explained by luck under an appropriate multi-fund critical threshold.

Theorem 3.1 · Expected false discoveries under zero skill

Assume the global null \(H_0: \alpha_i = 0\) for all \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). Let each fund's \(t\)-statistic satisfy the asymptotic normal distribution approximation \(\hat t_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). If an analyst conducts a one-sided hypothesis test with significance level \(\eta \in (0, 1)\) using critical value \(c = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \eta)\), the expected count of funds flagged as skilled is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[N_{\text{flagged}}] &= N \eta. \end{aligned} \]

Proof. Let \(I_i = \mathbf{1}_{\{\hat t_i > c\}}\) be the indicator that fund \(i\) is flagged. By Definition 3.1 under the null hypothesis, \(\Prob(\hat t_i > c) = \eta\). The total count of flagged funds is \(N_{\text{flagged}} = \sum_{i=1}^N I_i\). By the linearity of expectation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[N_{\text{flagged}}] &= \E\left[\sum_{i=1}^N I_i\right] = \sum_{i=1}^N \E[I_i] = \sum_{i=1}^N \Prob(\hat t_i > c) = N \eta. \end{aligned} \]

Notice that this result holds by linearity of expectation regardless of cross-fund correlation in fund returns.

Example 3.1 · The scale of naive false discoveries

In a universe of \(N = 2000\) mutual funds tested at the standard 5% significance level (\(\eta = 0.05, c \approx 1.645\)), the expected number of funds appearing skilled by chance is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[N_{\text{flagged}}] &= 2000 \times 0.05 = 100\text{ funds}. \end{aligned} \]

An allocator who observes 100 funds with \(t > 1.645\) has observed zero evidence of skill; that count is precisely the mathematical expectation of pure luck.

2Bootstrap null distributions and false discovery rates

Because fund returns share common industry exposures and macroeconomic shocks, fund residuals \(\varepsilon_{it}\) are cross-sectionally correlated. Furthermore, return distributions exhibit skewness and fat tails. To account for non-normality and cross-sectional dependence without imposing parametric assumptions, researchers employ the bootstrap.

Definition 3.2 · Fama–French residual bootstrap under the null

The Fama and French (2010) bootstrap constructs the empirical distribution of fund \(t\)-statistics under the null of zero skill through the following algorithm:

  1. Estimate OLS slopes \(\hat\beta_i\) and residuals \(e_{it} = r_{it} - \hat\alpha_i - \hat\beta_i f_t\) for each fund \(i\).
  2. For replication \(k = 1, \dots, B\), draw a shared sequence of time indices \(\tau_1, \dots, \tau_T\) uniformly with replacement from \(\{1, \dots, T\}\).
  3. Construct pseudo-returns imposing zero alpha: \(r_{it}^{*(k)} = \hat\beta_i f_t + e_{i, \tau_t}\).
  4. Estimate the pseudo \(t\)-statistic \(\hat t_i^{*(k)}\) for each fund by regressing \(r_{i}^{*(k)}\) on \(f\).
  5. Sort the cross-sectional \(t\)-statistics and record distribution quantiles across the \(B\) replications.
Remark 3.1 · Why residuals must be resampled

Resampling raw fund returns directly preserves the fund's sample alpha, failing to impose the null hypothesis. Generating returns from \(r_{it}^* = \hat\beta_i f_t + e_{i, \tau_t}\) sets the true population intercept to zero by construction while preserving the fund's factor loading, residual variance, and contemporaneous cross-fund residual correlation.

Theorem 3.2 · False discovery decomposition

In a cross-section of \(N\) funds containing \(N_0\) zero-skill funds and \(N_1\) truly skilled funds (\(N = N_0 + N_1\)), let a selection rule flag funds exceeding threshold \(c\). Let \(F_0(c) = \Prob(\hat t_i > c \mid \alpha_i = 0)\) be the null false positive rate. The expected proportion of false discoveries among flagged funds is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E\left[\frac{N_{0, \text{flagged}}}{N_{\text{flagged}}}\right] &\approx \frac{N_0 F_0(c)}{N_{\text{flagged}}}. \end{aligned} \]
Remark 3.2 · Empirical findings on mutual fund skill

Fama and French (2010) found that after accounting for luck via the bootstrap, the aggregate distribution of mutual fund alphas is roughly consistent with zero skill across the entire universe, with only the top 1% to 2% of funds showing positive net alpha beyond luck. Applying a false discovery rate approach, Barras, Scaillet, and Wermers (2010) estimated that approximately 75% of funds are zero-alpha, 24% are negative-alpha, and only 0.6% exhibit genuine positive net alpha. Conversely, Andrikogiannopoulou and Papakonstantinou (2019) demonstrated that standard FDR estimators suffer from low power in typical fund panels, and a Bayesian deconvolution suggests a larger share of skilled funds exists than classical FDR models report.

Lab 1 · Luck versus skill in fund performance

Across 10 independent random seeds simulating N=1000 zero-skill funds with 60 monthly observations each, the number of funds with one-sided t-statistics exceeding the 5% critical value (c = 1.644854) will fluctuate around the theoretical expectation of N * eta = 50.0.

"""Lab FC3-L03-1: Luck versus skill in fund performance.

SIMULATED universe of N=1000 zero-skill funds, testing the number of funds
exhibiting significant positive t-statistics by chance alone across seeds 0 to 9.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def normal_quantile(p: float, tol: float = 1e-12) -> float:
    """Compute normal quantile via bisection on math.erf."""
    low, high = -10.0, 10.0
    target = 2.0 * p - 1.0
    while high - low > tol:
        mid = (low + high) / 2.0
        val = math.erf(mid / math.sqrt(2.0))
        if val < target:
            low = mid
        else:
            high = mid
    return (low + high) / 2.0


def main() -> None:
    """Evaluate false positive counts across 10 random seeds."""
    n_funds = 1000
    n_months = 60
    level = 0.05
    crit = normal_quantile(1.0 - level)
    expected_count = n_funds * level

    print(f"Universe: N={n_funds} funds, T={n_months} months, zero true alpha (SIMULATED)")
    print(f"One-sided level: {level:.2f}, Normal critical value: {crit:.6f}")
    print(f"Expected false positives under null: {expected_count:.1f}")
    print("Seed | False Positives | Excess Over Expected")
    print("-----+-----------------+---------------------")

    counts: list[int] = []
    for seed in range(10):
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        factor = [rng.gauss(0.007, 0.045) for _ in range(n_months)]
        mean_f = sum(factor) / n_months
        s_ff = sum((f - mean_f) ** 2 for f in factor)

        cnt = 0
        for _ in range(n_funds):
            beta = 0.8 + 0.4 * rng.random()
            # True alpha is zero: r_it = beta * f_t + noise
            r = [beta * factor[t] + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.020) for t in range(n_months)]
            mean_r = sum(r) / n_months
            s_fr = sum((factor[t] - mean_f) * (r[t] - mean_r) for t in range(n_months))
            b = s_fr / s_ff
            a = mean_r - b * mean_f
            rss = sum((r[t] - (a + b * factor[t])) ** 2 for t in range(n_months))
            s2 = rss / (n_months - 2)
            se_a = math.sqrt(s2 * (1.0 / n_months + (mean_f**2) / s_ff))
            t_stat = a / se_a
            if t_stat > crit:
                cnt += 1
        counts.append(cnt)
        print(f"{seed:4d} | {cnt:15d} | {cnt - expected_count:+19.1f}")

    avg_cnt = sum(counts) / len(counts)
    print("-----+-----------------+---------------------")
    print(f"Mean false positives across 10 seeds: {avg_cnt:.2f} (expected {expected_count:.1f})")

3Where the canon breaks

Evaluating fund skill through the cross-sectional bootstrap faces distinct practical and econometric challenges:

  1. Survivorship and delisting bias: Commercial mutual fund databases frequently suffer from survivorship bias if funds that liquidated or merged due to poor performance are expunged from historical records. Conditioning a sample only on funds that survived for a full 5- or 10-year window artificially truncates the left tail of poor performers, biasing the apparent cross-sectional alpha upward.
  2. Statistical power in short track records: With monthly data, standard errors of annual alpha estimates are approximately \(\sigma_\varepsilon / \sqrt{T}\). For a fund with 2% monthly tracking error (\(\sigma_\varepsilon \approx 0.02\)) over 5 years (\(T = 60\)), the standard error of monthly alpha is \(0.02 / \sqrt{60} \approx 0.00258\) (3.1% annualized). Detecting an annual alpha of 2.0% with 80% statistical power requires over 15 years of uninterrupted track record.
  3. Non-stationarity of manager skill: Standard bootstrap frameworks assume alpha and factor loadings remain constant over the entire sample period. In reality, portfolio managers change, fund size scales up inducing diminishing returns, and market structures evolve.

4Misconceptions

  1. A mutual fund with a t-statistic of +2.2 over 5 years has proven beyond reasonable doubt that its manager is skilled.

    In isolation, a single test rejects the null at \(p \approx 0.03\). But in a search over 2,000 mutual funds, approximately 28 zero-skill funds will achieve \(t > 2.2\) purely by luck. Single-hypothesis thresholds are invalid when evaluating managers selected from large universes.

  2. Bootstrapping mutual fund skill is accomplished by randomly shuffling the monthly returns of each fund independently.

    Shuffling fund returns independently destroys cross-sectional correlation caused by common macroeconomic factors. Furthermore, shuffling raw returns retains the fund's sample mean return. A valid bootstrap must resample residuals with shared month draws while explicitly imposing the null hypothesis of zero alpha.

  3. If the average mutual fund alpha after fees is negative, there are no skilled managers in the active mutual fund industry.

    An industry-wide average net alpha of \(-1.0\%\) is consistent with a heterogeneous distribution where 80% of funds have negative net alpha, 18% have zero net alpha, and 2% possess genuine positive net skill. Aggregate underperformance does not preclude the existence of a small skilled minority.

5Problem set

Master false discovery calculations, bootstrap algorithm mechanics, and multiple-testing interpretations in fund performance.

Mechanics

M1 · Calculating expected false discoveries

An institutional consultant screens an equity universe of \(N = 800\) mutual funds. She selects funds whose 3-year \(t\)-statistic exceeds the 97.5% normal critical threshold (\(c = 1.96\)). Assuming all 800 funds possess zero true alpha, calculate the expected number of funds selected.

Solution

Under the null hypothesis of zero skill, each fund's \(t\)-statistic exceeds 1.96 with probability \(\eta = 0.025\). By Theorem 3.1, the expected number of false discoveries is \(\E[N_{\text{flagged}}] = N \eta = 800 \times 0.025 = 20\text{ funds}\). The consultant should expect to find 20 false stars purely by luck.

M2 · Standard error of alpha

A mutual fund is evaluated over \(T = 60\) months. The residual standard deviation is \(s = 0.022\) (2.2% per month). The market factor has monthly mean \(\bar f = 0.008\) and sum of squared deviations \(S_{ff} = 0.120\). Compute the standard error of the monthly alpha estimate.

Solution

The standard error formula is \(\text{se}(\hat\alpha) = s \sqrt{\frac{1}{T} + \frac{\bar f^2}{S_{ff}}}\). Substituting the values: \(\frac{1}{60} \approx 0.016667\); \(\frac{0.008^2}{0.120} = \frac{0.000064}{0.120} \approx 0.000533\). The sum is \(0.016667 + 0.000533 = 0.017200\). Taking the square root gives \(\sqrt{0.017200} \approx 0.131149\). Multiplying by \(s = 0.022\) yields \(\text{se}(\hat\alpha) = 0.022 \times 0.131149 \approx 0.002885\) (or 0.2885% per month, about 3.46% annualized).

M3 · False discovery proportion calculation

In a universe of 500 funds, an empirical test at threshold \(c = 2.0\) flags 25 funds. A bootstrap under the null indicates that zero-skill funds exceed \(c = 2.0\) with probability \(F_0(2.0) = 0.0228\). Assuming 90% of all funds have zero skill (\(N_0 = 450\)), estimate the false discovery proportion among the 25 flagged funds.

Solution

The expected number of false discoveries from the zero-skill population is \(E[\text{FD}] = N_0 F_0(c) = 450 \times 0.0228 = 10.26\text{ funds}\). The expected false discovery proportion among the 25 flagged funds is \(\text{FDP} \approx 10.26 / 25 \approx 0.4104\) (or 41.0%). More than four out of every ten flagged funds are lucky pretenders.

Proof

P1 · Shared time draws preserve cross-sectional covariance

Let fund residuals be \(e_{it}, e_{jt}\) with sample covariance \(S_{ij} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T e_{it} e_{jt}\). Prove that drawing shared time indices \(\tau_1, \dots, \tau_T\) uniformly with replacement preserves the expected sample covariance across bootstrap replications.

Solution

For any month \(s \in \{1, \dots, T\}\), the resampled residuals are \(e_{i, \tau_s}\) and \(e_{j, \tau_s}\), where \(\tau_s\) is drawn with equal probability \(1/T\) from \(\{1, \dots, T\}\). The expected cross-product is \(\E[e_{i, \tau_s} e_{j, \tau_s}] = \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{1}{T} e_{it} e_{jt} = S_{ij}\). By linearity of expectation, the expected bootstrap sample covariance \(\E[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^T e_{i, \tau_s} e_{j, \tau_s}] = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^T S_{ij} = S_{ij}\). Thus the shared time draw exactly preserves contemporaneous cross-sectional covariance.

P2 · Zero expected alpha under residual bootstrap

Prove that constructing pseudo-returns as \(r_{it}^* = \hat\beta_i f_t + e_{i, \tau_t}\) guarantees that the population intercept in a regression on \(f_t\) is zero when \(\sum_{t=1}^T e_{it} = 0\).

Solution

In OLS, the sample residuals sum to zero: \(\sum_{t=1}^T e_{it} = 0\), so \(\E[e_{i, \tau_t}] = 0\). The expectation of \(r_{it}^*\) conditional on the factor realization \(f_t\) is \(\E[r_{it}^* \mid f_t] = \hat\beta_i f_t + \E[e_{i, \tau_t}] = \hat\beta_i f_t + 0 = \hat\beta_i f_t\). Because the conditional expectation has no constant term, the true population intercept under the bootstrap DGP is identically zero.

P3 · Monotonicity of the max-t threshold in significance level

Prove that the bootstrap critical threshold \(c(\eta) = \text{Quantile}(1 - \eta)\) of the cross-sectional maximum \(t\)-statistic is non-increasing in the significance level \(\eta\).

Solution

Let \(G(m) = \Prob(M^* \le m)\) be the cumulative distribution function of the cross-sectional maximum \(M^*\). By definition, \(G\) is a non-decreasing function. The quantile function \(Q(p) = \inf\{m : G(m) \ge p\}\) is non-decreasing in \(p\). For \(\eta_1 < \eta_2\), we have \(1 - \eta_1 > 1 - \eta_2\), which implies \(Q(1 - \eta_1) \ge Q(1 - \eta_2)\). Thus \(c(\eta_1) \ge c(\eta_2)\), proving that the critical hurdle decreases as the allowable error rate increases.

Empirical

E1 · Interpreting Carhart (1997) persistence findings

Carhart (1997) documented that mutual fund performance persistence is largely explained by momentum and expenses, with only the bottom decile exhibiting strong persistence. Interpret this result in the context of the luck versus skill debate.

Solution

Carhart showed that top-performing mutual funds do not repeat their outperformance once returns are adjusted for the 4-factor model including momentum. Apparent winning streaks reflect momentum exposure rather than persistent stock-picking skill. Conversely, the worst-performing funds persist in underperforming because high expense ratios and excessive turnover drag down returns year after year.

E2 · Power of single-fund tests

A fund manager has a true annual alpha of +2.0% (\(\alpha_{\text{monthly}} \approx 0.001667\)) with monthly residual volatility \(\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.025\). Calculate the probability that a 5-year (\(T=60\)) \(t\)-test rejects the null \(\alpha \le 0\) at the 5% level (\(z_{\text{crit}} = 1.645\)).

Solution

The standard error of alpha is \(\text{se} \approx 0.025 / \sqrt{60} \approx 0.003227\). The non-centrality parameter is \(\delta = \alpha / \text{se} = 0.001667 / 0.003227 \approx 0.5166\). Under the alternative, the \(t\)-statistic follows \(\mathcal{N}(\delta, 1)\). The power is \(\Prob(Z + 0.5166 > 1.645) = \Prob(Z > 1.1284) \approx 1 - 0.8704 = 0.1296\) (or 13.0%). There is an 87% chance that this genuinely skilled manager will fail to reach statistical significance over 5 years.

E3 · Fama-French (2010) luck comparison

In Table 2 of Fama and French (2010), describe how the simulated distribution of mutual fund \(t(\alpha)\) compares with the actual distribution in the extreme tails.

Solution

Fama and French found that for net returns, the actual distribution of \(t(\alpha)\) was shifted significantly to the left of the simulated zero-alpha distribution, confirming that high fees cause widespread underperformance. In the extreme right tail (99th percentile), the actual net \(t\)-statistic was only slightly higher than the simulated luck distribution, indicating that very few managers possess enough skill to overcome their fees.

Research

R1 · Bayesian deconvolution versus frequentist false discovery

Compare the Bayesian deconvolution approach of Andrikogiannopoulou and Papakonstantinou (2019) with the frequentist false discovery approach of Barras, Scaillet, and Wermers (2010).

Solution

Barras et al. classify funds using a three-point discrete mixture (negative, zero, positive alpha) using empirical \(p\)-value histograms. However, in noisy samples with short horizons, tests have low power, causing skilled funds to have insignificant \(p\)-values that get lumped into the zero-skill group. Andrikogiannopoulou and Papakonstantinou use Bayesian deconvolution to model a continuous distribution of true alphas, proving that accounting for measurement noise reveals substantial latent skill that standard FDR estimators miss.

R2 · Incubation bias in fund performance databases

Explain how mutual fund incubation strategies create upward bias in reported returns and distort cross-sectional luck evaluations.

Solution

Fund families often launch dozens of small private incubator funds with diverse strategies. After several years, the family publicly registers and advertises only the few funds that achieved exceptional performance by luck, while quietly liquidating the unsuccessful funds without reporting their track records. This selective backfill bias severely inflates the historical returns of newly listed mutual funds.

R3 · Scale diseconomies as an equalizer

Discuss the equilibrium implication of scale diseconomies for long-term fund performance studies.

Solution

As proved by Berk and Green (2004), when skilled managers experience increasing assets under management, market impact and liquidity constraints erode gross alpha. In equilibrium, net alpha converges to zero across all funds regardless of manager skill. Consequently, long-term cross-sectional tests of net returns will conclude that skill is absent even in an economy where skilled managers generate billions in gross economic value.

7Retrieval quiz

Answer from memory. Explanations appear upon submission.

  1. Under the global null hypothesis of zero managerial skill, how many funds in a universe of 500 are expected to achieve t > 1.645 (one-sided 5% level)?

    Options

    By Theorem 3.1, expected false positives equal \(N \eta = 500 \times 0.05 = 25\).

  2. Why must a valid skill bootstrap resample residuals rather than raw returns?

    Options

    To generate the distribution of performance under the null, the null \(\alpha_i = 0\) must be imposed by construction by resampling residuals.

  3. How does the Fama-French bootstrap preserve contemporaneous cross-sectional correlation across funds?

    Options

    Drawing the same month \(\tau_t\) for all funds simultaneously preserves the empirical cross-fund covariance matrix.

  4. In a universe of 1000 funds tested at the 1% significance level (\(\eta = 0.01\)), what is the expected number of false discoveries under zero skill?

    \(N \eta = 1000 \times 0.01 = 10.0\).

  5. What did Carhart (1997) find regarding the persistence of mutual fund performance?

    Options

    Carhart demonstrated that four-factor risk adjustments (specifically momentum) eliminate persistence among top performers, while poor performance persists due to fees.

  6. What does the false discovery rate (FDR) represent in mutual fund evaluation?

    Options

    FDR measures the expected ratio of false positives to total rejected hypotheses (flagged funds).

  7. Why do standard errors of fund alphas remain large even after 5 years of monthly data?

    Options

    Idiosyncratic fund risk of 15% to 20% annualized yields standard errors of several percentage points over 60 months, making true 2% alphas difficult to detect.

  8. How does survivorship bias distort mutual fund performance studies?

    Options

    Failed funds with large negative alphas disappear from uncorrected databases, artificially shifting the apparent performance distribution to the right.
