A financial forecasting model is only as reliable as the economic moat preserving the firm's return on capital. Without structural barriers to entry or pricing power, abnormal returns on invested capital erode toward the cost of capital through competitive entry. This lesson formalizes industry competitive analysis, linking the five-forces framework to microeconomic market structures and concentration metrics.

1The Five-Forces Framework

Definition 1.1 (Five-Forces Analysis)

Five-forces analysis is an analytical framework that decomposes the structural determinants of industry profitability into five external competitive pressures: (1) the threat of new entrants, (2) the bargaining power of buyers, (3) the bargaining power of suppliers, (4) the threat of substitute products or services, and (5) the intensity of rivalry among existing competitors.

The collective strength of these five forces determines the long-run average return on invested capital across an industry:

  1. Threat of New Entrants: Constrained by economies of scale, capital requirements, customer switching costs, regulatory licensing, and intellectual property. High entry barriers preserve economic profits by preventing excess capacity.
  2. Bargaining Power of Buyers: Elevated when buyer concentration is high, purchase volumes represent substantial portions of supplier revenue, and buyer switching costs are low. Powerful buyers force price reductions and demand costly service enhancements.
  3. Bargaining Power of Suppliers: Elevated when input suppliers are concentrated, inputs have few substitutes, or supplier products create high switching costs. Powerful suppliers capture corporate profits by inflating input prices.
  4. Threat of Substitutes: Driven by the cross-price elasticity of demand between industry products and outside alternatives. High substitution limits the ceiling on industry pricing power.
  5. Rivalry Among Existing Competitors: Governed by industry concentration, fixed-to-variable cost ratios, exit barriers, and product differentiation.

2Microeconomic Foundations: Cournot, Bertrand, and Concentration

The five-forces framework connects directly to classical models of imperfect competition introduced in E1:

  • Quantity Competition (Cournot competition): When firms choose production capacities, the industry price-cost margin (Lerner index) is directly proportional to market concentration.
  • Price Competition (Bertrand competition): With undifferentiated products and constant marginal costs, two competing firms suffice to drive economic profits to zero (\(P = MC\)). Product differentiation and customer switching costs are required to sustain positive margins.
Definition 2.1 (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index)

For an industry of \(n\) firms with fractional market shares \(s_1, s_2, \dots, s_n\) satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^n s_i = 1\) and \(s_i \ge 0\), the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (\(\mathrm{HHI}\)) is the sum of squared market shares:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{HHI} = \sum_{i=1}^n s_i^2 \end{aligned} \]

In standard regulatory practice (e.g. U.S. Department of Justice merger guidelines), shares are expressed in percentage points (0 to 100), scaling the index from 0 to 10,000 points.

Proposition 2.1 (Cournot Industry Margin and HHI)

In an \(n\)-firm industry with homogeneous goods, linear inverse demand \(P(Q)\), price elasticity of demand \(\eta = - \frac{P}{Q P'(Q)}\), and firm marginal costs \(c_i\), the market-share weighted industry Lerner index equals the HHI divided by market elasticity:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n s_i \frac{P - c_i}{P} = \frac{\mathrm{HHI}}{\eta} \end{aligned} \]
Proof

Each Cournot firm \(i\) maximizes profit \(\pi_i(q_i) = P(Q)q_i - C_i(q_i)\) taking rival quantities as given. The first-order condition is:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(Q) + P'(Q)q_i - c_i = 0 \end{aligned} \]

Dividing by \(P(Q)\) and noting that \(q_i / Q = s_i\) and \(\eta = -P / (Q P')\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{P - c_i}{P} = -\frac{P'(Q) Q}{P} \frac{q_i}{Q} = \frac{s_i}{\eta} \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying each firm's Lerner margin by its market share \(s_i\) and summing across all \(n\) firms:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n s_i \frac{P - c_i}{P} = \sum_{i=1}^n s_i \left(\frac{s_i}{\eta}\right) = \frac{1}{\eta} \sum_{i=1}^n s_i^2 = \frac{\mathrm{HHI}}{\eta} \end{aligned} \]

which establishes the result. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Herfindahl-Hirschman index across market structures

Calculates exact fractional and 10,000-point integer HHI for three illustrative market structures: dominant firm monopoly (70/15/10/5), concentrated oligopoly (35/25/20/10/10), and fragmented competitive (10 equal firms), verifying that concentration decreases monotonically.

"""Lab FC4-L02-1: Herfindahl-Hirschman index (HHI) on industry market shares."""

from fractions import Fraction


def compute_hhi(shares: list[Fraction]) -> tuple[Fraction, int]:
    """Compute exact HHI in fraction [0, 1] and conventional integer points [0, 10000]."""
    assert sum(shares, Fraction(0)) == Fraction(1), "Market shares must sum to 1"
    hhi_frac = sum((s**2 for s in shares), Fraction(0))
    # Conventional index uses percentage shares squared: sum((100 * s)^2) = 10000 * sum(s^2)
    hhi_points = int(10000 * hhi_frac)
    return hhi_frac, hhi_points


def main() -> None:
    # Three illustrative market structures (SIMULATED)
    industries = {
        "Dominant firm monopoly": [Fraction(70, 100), Fraction(15, 100), Fraction(10, 100), Fraction(5, 100)],
        "Concentrated oligopoly": [
            Fraction(35, 100),
            Fraction(25, 100),
            Fraction(20, 100),
            Fraction(10, 100),
            Fraction(10, 100),
        ],
        "Fragmented competitive (10 equal firms)": [Fraction(1, 10)] * 10,
    }

    print("SIMULATED Industry Structure and Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)")
    print("-" * 75)
    print(f"{'Industry Structure':<40} {'Firms':>6} {'HHI (Exact)':>14} {'HHI (Points)':>12}")
    print("-" * 75)

    for name, shares in industries.items():
        hhi_frac, hhi_pts = compute_hhi(shares)
        print(f"{name:<40} {len(shares):>6} {hhi_frac!s:>14} {hhi_pts:>12d}")

    print("-" * 75)
    print("Exact HHI properties verified: concentration decreases strictly across the three panels.")


main()

The dominant firm structure generates an HHI of 5250 points, indicating extreme concentration; the 5-firm oligopoly produces 2450 points (moderate concentration); and the 10-firm symmetric market produces 1000 points. Lower HHI corresponds to lower aggregate pricing power under Cournot competition.

3Where the canon breaks

Static five-forces analysis suffers from significant analytical boundaries:

  • Market Boundary Ambiguity: The HHI and five-forces ratings depend critically on how the relevant market is demarcated. Defining an enterprise as a "railroad" versus a "transportation logistics provider" produces wildly divergent concentration scores.
  • Platform Ecosystems and Network Effects: In two-sided digital markets, high rivalry in one segment may be offset by two-sided positive feedback loops (cross-side network externalities) where a dominant firm sustains 80% operating margins despite zero physical entry barriers.
  • Endogenous Industry Structure: High initial profits induce innovation that creates substitute products outside the traditional industry classification, rendering historical five-forces assessments obsolete.

4Misconceptions

  • A high market share in an industry automatically guarantees high profitability and economic moats.

    Market share without entry barriers invites aggressive price competition. If products are undifferentiated and capital is mobile, a firm with 60% market share under Bertrand price competition may earn zero economic profit.

  • High industry growth rate is a competitive force that protects company profitability.

    Rapid market growth frequently attracts heavy capital influx, creating massive industry overcapacity. Without barriers to entry, high growth rates intensify rivalry and depress long-run returns on invested capital.

  • The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index is an empirical discovery that proves market competition.

    The HHI is a mathematical concentration index defined over market shares. Whether higher HHI leads to higher pricing power depends entirely on entry barriers, product differentiation, and the mode of competition (Cournot vs Bertrand).

5Problem set

M1 · HHI calculation for symmetric firms

An industry consists of \(N\) identical firms, each holding equal market share \(1/N\). Compute the HHI in exact fraction and in conventional points for \(N = 2, 4, 10, 20\).

Solution

For \(N\) equal firms, each has share \(s_i = 1/N\). The exact HHI is \(\sum_{i=1}^N (1/N)^2 = N \times (1/N^2) = 1/N\). In conventional points (\(10{,}000 \times \mathrm{HHI}\)): for \(N = 2\), \(\mathrm{HHI} = 1/2\) (5000 points); for \(N = 4\), \(\mathrm{HHI} = 1/4\) (2500 points); for \(N = 10\), \(\mathrm{HHI} = 1/10\) (1000 points); for \(N = 20\), \(\mathrm{HHI} = 1/20\) (500 points).

M2 · Impact of merger on HHI

An industry has five firms with market shares of 30%, 25%, 20%, 15%, and 10%. Firms 4 and 5 merge to form a combined firm with 25% share. Calculate the change in HHI (\(\Delta\mathrm{HHI}\)) resulting from the merger.

Solution

Pre-merger HHI is \(30^2 + 25^2 + 20^2 + 15^2 + 10^2 = 900 + 625 + 400 + 225 + 100 = 2250\) points. Post-merger, the shares are 30%, 25%, 25%, and 20%. Post-merger HHI is \(30^2 + 25^2 + 25^2 + 20^2 = 900 + 625 + 625 + 400 = 2550\) points. The change is \(\Delta\mathrm{HHI} = 2550 - 2250 = +300\) points. Notice this directly equals \(2 \times s_4 \times s_5 = 2 \times 15 \times 10 = 300\).

M3 · Cournot industry Lerner margin

An industry operates under Cournot competition with market demand elasticity \(\eta = 2.0\). The industry HHI is 0.25 (2500 points). Calculate the market-share weighted average Lerner markup \((P - c)/P\).

Solution

Applying Proposition 2.1, the share-weighted industry Lerner index is \(\mathrm{HHI} / \eta = 0.25 / 2.0 = 0.125\), or 12.5 percent. On average across the industry, price exceeds marginal cost by 12.5 percent of the price.

P1 · Minimum HHI for n firms

Prove that for any industry with \(n\) firms, the HHI satisfies the lower bound \(\mathrm{HHI} \ge 1/n\), with equality if and only if all \(n\) firms hold identical market shares \(s_i = 1/n\).

Solution

Consider the variance of market shares across the \(n\) firms: \(\sigma_s^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (s_i - \bar{s})^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n s_i^2 - \bar{s}^2\). Since \(\sum s_i = 1\), the mean share is \(\bar{s} = 1/n\). Thus \(\frac{1}{n} \mathrm{HHI} - (1/n)^2 = \sigma_s^2 \ge 0\). Multiplying by \(n\) gives \(\mathrm{HHI} \ge 1/n\). Equality holds if and only if \(\sigma_s^2 = 0\), which requires \(s_i = \bar{s} = 1/n\) for every firm \(i\).

P2 · Merger delta identity

Prove that when any two firms with market shares \(s_j\) and \(s_k\) merge, holding all other firms' market shares constant, the change in HHI is exactly \(\Delta\mathrm{HHI} = 2 s_j s_k\).

Solution

The pre-merger contribution of firms \(j\) and \(k\) to HHI is \(s_j^2 + s_k^2\). The post-merger combined entity has market share \(s_{jk} = s_j + s_k\). Its contribution to post-merger HHI is \((s_j + s_k)^2 = s_j^2 + 2 s_j s_k + s_k^2\). The shares of all other firms remain unchanged. Thus \(\Delta\mathrm{HHI} = (s_j^2 + 2 s_j s_k + s_k^2) - (s_j^2 + s_k^2) = 2 s_j s_k\). \(\blacksquare\)

P3 · Relationship between 4-firm concentration ratio and HHI

Let \(\mathrm{CR}_4 = \sum_{i=1}^4 s_i\) be the four-firm concentration ratio, with firms sorted such that \(s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \dots \ge s_n\). Prove that \(\mathrm{HHI} \ge \frac{(\mathrm{CR}_4)^2}{4}\).

Solution

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to the vectors \((s_1, s_2, s_3, s_4)\) and \((1, 1, 1, 1)\): \((\sum_{i=1}^4 s_i \cdot 1)^2 \le (\sum_{i=1}^4 s_i^2)(\sum_{i=1}^4 1^2) = 4 \sum_{i=1}^4 s_i^2\). Hence \(\sum_{i=1}^4 s_i^2 \ge \frac{(\mathrm{CR}_4)^2}{4}\). Since \(\mathrm{HHI} = \sum_{i=1}^4 s_i^2 + \sum_{i=5}^n s_i^2 \ge \sum_{i=1}^4 s_i^2\), it follows immediately that \(\mathrm{HHI} \ge \frac{(\mathrm{CR}_4)^2}{4}\).

E1 · DOJ merger screening thresholds

The U.S. Department of Justice classifies markets as unconcentrated (HHI < 1500), moderately concentrated (1500 to 2500), and highly concentrated (HHI > 2500). Mergers producing \(\Delta\mathrm{HHI} > 100\) in moderately concentrated or \(\Delta\mathrm{HHI} > 200\) in highly concentrated markets trigger antitrust scrutiny. Evaluate an industry with shares 35%, 30%, 15%, 10%, 10% where the third and fourth firms propose to merge.

Solution

Pre-merger HHI is \(35^2 + 30^2 + 15^2 + 10^2 + 10^2 = 1225 + 900 + 225 + 100 + 100 = 2550\) points (highly concentrated). The merger of firm 3 (15%) and firm 4 (10%) yields \(\Delta\mathrm{HHI} = 2 \times 15 \times 10 = 300\) points. Because the market is already highly concentrated (\(2550 > 2500\)) and \(\Delta\mathrm{HHI} = 300 > 200\), the merger creates a strong regulatory presumption of enhanced market power under antitrust guidelines.

E2 · Cross-industry returns on invested capital

Historical cross-sectional return data indicates that pharmaceutical and software sectors historically sustain 20–25% ROIC, while commercial airlines and retail sustain 5–8% ROIC over multi-decade horizons. Explain this dispersion using the five forces.

Solution

Software and pharmaceuticals benefit from immense entry barriers (patents, massive R&D scale, enterprise software switching costs) and minimal buyer power, allowing price premiums above marginal cost. Airlines and commodity retail face zero product differentiation, intense price rivalry (Bertrand dynamics), high fixed costs with low marginal costs encouraging price wars, and powerful commodity buyers, driving long-run returns to the cost of capital.

E3 · Switching costs and equity valuation

Enterprise SaaS businesses often trade at significant valuation multiples over on-premise software. Explain how high customer switching costs impact the cash flow forecasting assumptions in equity valuation.

Solution

High switching costs (retraining employees, data migration risk, workflow integration) create recurring revenue moats. In valuation modeling, this justifies lower customer churn rates, higher terminal growth longevity, and sustained gross margins, which dramatically elevates the present value of distant cash flows relative to cyclical firms.

R1 · Demsetz vs Bain market concentration hypothesis

Joe Bain (1951) hypothesized that high concentration causes collusion and monopoly profits. Harold Demsetz (1973) argued that superior firm efficiency causes both higher concentration and higher profits. How can an equity analyst distinguish these two mechanisms?

Solution

To distinguish the mechanisms, examine the relationship between firm size and profit margins within concentrated industries. Under the collusion hypothesis (Bain), high concentration elevates prices for all firms in the industry, including small fringe producers. Under the efficiency hypothesis (Demsetz), only the large low-cost producers achieve high profit margins, while small marginal firms earn normal returns. If small firms earn high returns, collusion is supported.

R2 · Contestable markets theory

Baumol, Panzar, and Willig (1982) show that in a perfectly contestable market (zero sunk entry and exit costs), even a natural monopoly is forced to charge competitive prices (\(P = AC\)). Explain how sunk costs determine the threat of entry.

Solution

Fixed costs that are recoverable upon exit (e.g. general aircraft leases) do not deter entry because an entrant can execute "hit-and-run" entry: enter if price exceeds average cost, capture profits, and exit costlessly if incumbents cut prices. Sunk costs (unrecoverable specialized plant, custom software, irreversible brand advertising) create an asymmetry: an entrant risks total loss if incumbents respond aggressively, creating a credible barrier to entry.

R3 · Complementary goods and platform value

Brandenburger and Nalebuff (1996) extend five-forces analysis to include "complementors" (goods that increase the value of the primary product). How does complement availability alter pricing power?

Solution

When complementors are competitive and provide cheap, high-quality complements (e.g., third-party smartphone apps for an operating system, or developer tools for a database), the consumer's total willingness to pay for the core platform expands. The platform owner can extract higher economic rents without expanding internal capital investment, enhancing long-term cash flow generation.

  1. Which force in the five-forces framework is driven by economies of scale and capital requirements?

    Options

    Economies of scale and large upfront capital requirements represent barriers to entry.

  2. What is the HHI in conventional points for an industry composed of exactly four symmetric firms?

    Each firm has 25% share. \(\mathrm{HHI} = 4 \times 25^2 = 4 \times 625 = 2500\) points.

  3. Under Cournot quantity competition with linear demand, how does an increase in industry HHI affect the average price-cost markup?

    Options

    By Proposition 2.1, the average Lerner index is \(\mathrm{HHI}/\eta\), which increases linearly with HHI.

  4. In an industry, Firm A (share 20%) merges with Firm B (share 10%). By how many points does the conventional HHI increase?

    \(\Delta\mathrm{HHI} = 2 \times s_A \times s_B = 2 \times 20 \times 10 = 400\) points.

  5. Why does high industry growth rate not necessarily protect corporate profit margins?

    Options

    Without entry barriers, high growth draws entrants that create industry overcapacity, eroding margins.

  6. An industry has 10 identical firms. What is its exact fractional HHI?

    For 10 symmetric firms, \(\mathrm{HHI} = 1/N = 1/10 = 0.10\).

  7. What distinguishes the threat of substitutes from internal industry rivalry?

    Options

    Substitutes perform the same function for customers but originate outside the industry definition (e.g. rail vs airlines).

  8. What is the theoretical maximum HHI in conventional points, corresponding to a pure monopoly?

    For a pure monopoly, share is 100%. \(\mathrm{HHI} = 100^2 = 10{,}000\) points.

  9. Under Bertrand competition with homogeneous products and constant marginal costs, what is the equilibrium market price?

    Options

    Bertrand price competition with homogeneous goods drives price down to marginal cost (\(P = MC\)).

  10. What happens to supplier bargaining power if a company can easily switch raw material sources without cost?

    Options

    Low switching costs and commodity supplier inputs eliminate supplier pricing power.
