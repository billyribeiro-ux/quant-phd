The Gordon Growth Model and the Spread r − g

  • Uses: dividend-discount-model, M1/CF1 foundations (growing perpetuity, geometric series)
  • Introduces: gordon-growth-model
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or convention
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

The Gordon growth model is the foundational closed-form valuation formula in corporate finance and equity research. By compressing an infinite stream of growing dividends into a single ratio \(D_1 / (r - g)\), it provides the engine used for terminal value capitalization across all discounted cash flow methodologies. Understanding the mathematical sensitivity of this formula as the spread \(r - g\) narrows toward zero is essential for risk management and model discipline.

1Derivation of the Constant Growth Perpetuity

Definition 1.1 (Gordon Growth Model)

The Gordon growth model expresses the intrinsic equity value \(P_0\) of a corporation whose dividends grow at a constant annual compound rate \(g\) indefinitely as:

\[ \begin{aligned} P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r - g} \end{aligned} \]

where \(D_1\) is the expected next-period dividend, \(r\) is the cost of equity, and \(g\) is the perpetual dividend growth rate satisfying \(r > g\).

Proposition 1.1 (Growing Perpetuity Closed Form)

Assume dividends satisfy \(D_t = D_1 (1 + g)^{t-1}\) for all integers \(t \ge 1\), with constant discount rate \(r\) and growth rate \(g\). If and only if \(r > g\) and \(r > -1\), the infinite discounted dividend sum converges to the finite closed form:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{t=1}^\infty \frac{D_t}{(1 + r)^t} = \frac{D_1}{r - g} \end{aligned} \]
Proof

Substitute the dividend trajectory \(D_t = D_1 (1 + g)^{t-1}\) into the infinite discounted sum:

\[ \begin{aligned} P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^\infty \frac{D_1 (1 + g)^{t-1}}{(1 + r)^t} = \frac{D_1}{1 + g} \sum_{t=1}^\infty \left(\frac{1 + g}{1 + r}\right)^t \end{aligned} \]

Define the common ratio \(x = \frac{1 + g}{1 + r}\). The condition \(r > g\) and \(r > -1\) implies \(1 + r > 1 + g > 0\), so \(0 < x < 1\). By the summation formula for an infinite geometric series:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{t=1}^\infty x^t = \frac{x}{1 - x} = \frac{\frac{1 + g}{1 + r}}{1 - \frac{1 + g}{1 + r}} = \frac{1 + g}{(1 + r) - (1 + g)} = \frac{1 + g}{r - g} \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying by the pre-factor \(\frac{D_1}{1 + g}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P_0 = \frac{D_1}{1 + g} \cdot \frac{1 + g}{r - g} = \frac{D_1}{r - g} \end{aligned} \]

If \(r \le g\), the common ratio satisfies \(x \ge 1\), and the partial sums diverge to \(+\infty\). \(\blacksquare\)

2The Sustainable Growth Relation

In corporate financial policy, dividend growth cannot be postulated arbitrarily; it is generated by retained earnings reinvested into operations. Let \(b\) denote the retention ratio (the fraction of net income retained by the firm):

\[ \begin{aligned} b = 1 - \frac{D_t}{NI_t} \end{aligned} \]

where \(1 - b\) is the dividend payout ratio. If the firm issues no new equity and earns a constant return on book equity \(\mathrm{ROE} = NI_t / B_{t-1}\), retained earnings \(\Delta B_t = b \cdot NI_t\) expand the equity capital base:

\[ \begin{aligned} g = \frac{\Delta B_t}{B_{t-1}} = \frac{b \cdot NI_t}{B_{t-1}} = b \cdot \mathrm{ROE} \end{aligned} \]
Proposition 2.1 (Sustainable Growth Identity)

Under clean surplus without external equity issuance or leverage changes, sustainable dividend growth equals the product of the earnings retention ratio and the return on beginning equity: \(g = b \cdot \mathrm{ROE}\).

3Sensitivity Analysis as the Spread r − g Shrinks

The mathematical behavior of \(P_0 = D_1 / (r - g)\) is dominated by the spread denominator \(\Delta = r - g\). The first and second partial derivatives with respect to the spread are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial P_0}{\partial \Delta} = -\frac{D_1}{\Delta^2} < 0, \quad \frac{\partial^2 P_0}{\partial \Delta^2} = \frac{2 D_1}{\Delta^3} > 0 \end{aligned} \]

As the spread \(\Delta \to 0^+\), the stock price is hyper-sensitive to estimation error: small adjustments in cost of capital or terminal growth generate massive percentage swings in implied valuation.

Lab 1 · Gordon-growth sensitivity: value as (r − g) shrinks

Evaluates closed-form intrinsic equity value P_0 for fixed next-period dividend D_1 = $2.00 as the spread r - g shrinks from 5.0% down to 0.01% (1 basis point), demonstrating non-linear price explosion.

"""Lab FC4-L04-1: Gordon-growth sensitivity: value as (r - g) shrinks (Curriculum Lab)."""

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    # Closed-form Gordon growth model: P_0 = D_1 / (r - g)
    # Fixed expected next dividend D_1 = $2.00
    d1 = Fraction(2, 1)

    # Grid of spreads r - g shrinking toward zero
    spreads = [
        Fraction(50, 1000),  # 5.0%
        Fraction(40, 1000),  # 4.0%
        Fraction(30, 1000),  # 3.0%
        Fraction(20, 1000),  # 2.0%
        Fraction(10, 1000),  # 1.0%
        Fraction(5, 1000),  # 0.5%
        Fraction(25, 10000),  # 0.25%
        Fraction(10, 10000),  # 0.10%
        Fraction(5, 10000),  # 0.05%
        Fraction(1, 10000),  # 0.01%
    ]

    print("Gordon Growth Sensitivity: Implied Equity Value P_0 as Spread (r - g) Shrinks")
    print(f"Fixed next-period dividend D_1 = ${float(d1):.2f}")
    print("-" * 68)
    print(f"{'Spread (r - g)':>16} {'Spread (pct)':>14} {'P_0 (Exact)':>18} {'P_0 (Decimal USD)':>16}")
    print("-" * 68)

    prev_p = Fraction(0)
    for spread in spreads:
        p0 = d1 / spread
        spread_pct = float(spread) * 100
        print(f"{spread!s:>16} {spread_pct:>13.2f}% {p0!s:>18} {float(p0):>16.2f}")
        assert p0 > prev_p, "Value must increase monotonically as spread shrinks"
        prev_p = p0

    print("-" * 68)
    print("Nonlinear explosion verified: as (r - g) -> 0+, implied value P_0 -> +infinity.")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The idealized assumptions of the Gordon growth model fail under macroeconomic and corporate reality:

  • Transversality and Macroeconomic Feasibility: A corporation whose dividends grow perpetually at rate \(g\) exceeding the aggregate macroeconomic growth rate (\(g > g_{\text{GDP}}\)) would eventually consume the entire gross domestic product of the global economy, violating economic feasibility.
  • Non-constant Competitive Dynamics: Excess economic returns (\(\mathrm{ROE} > r\)) attract competitive entry, eroding profit margins and forcing \(g\) downward over time. Multistage models (two-stage or three-stage H-models) are required to capture competitive convergence.
  • Negative or Non-dividend Payers: If \(D_1 = 0\), the closed form yields \(P_0 = 0\) regardless of underlying earnings power, rendering the pure formula unusable for non-distributing growth firms.

5Misconceptions

  • A firm can achieve any desired growth rate by simply retaining 100 percent of its earnings.

    Retaining earnings only generates growth if there are productive physical investment opportunities that earn an adequate return on equity (\(\mathrm{ROE}\)). Retaining cash and holding it in zero-return reserves produces \(g = 0\), while investing in negative-NPV projects actively destroys value.

  • If the dividend growth rate exceeds the cost of equity (g > r), the stock price is simply negative.

    The mathematical condition \(r > g\) is required for convergence of the geometric series. If \(g \ge r\), the infinite series diverges to \(+\infty\), not a negative value. A negative price would represent an arbitrage violation under limited liability.

  • Gordon growth can be applied using historical dividend growth as the perpetual growth rate g.

    Historical growth over a 3-year or 5-year cycle often reflects cyclical recovery or business expansion phases (e.g. 15% annual growth). Setting perpetual \(g = 15\%\) with cost of capital \(r = 9\%\) violates the convergence condition \(r > g\) and is macroeconomically impossible.

6Problem set

M1 · Gordon growth valuation

A utility company just paid an annual dividend of \$3.00 per share (\(D_0\)). Dividends are expected to grow at a constant rate of 3 percent per year indefinitely. If the cost of equity is 8 percent, what is the stock intrinsic value \(P_0\)?

Solution

First compute expected next-period dividend \(D_1 = D_0 \times (1 + g) = 3.00 \times 1.03 = 3.09\) dollars. The discount rate spread is \(r - g = 0.08 - 0.03 = 0.05\). Applying the Gordon formula: \(P_0 = D_1 / (r - g) = 3.09 / 0.05 = \$61.80\) per share.

M2 · Sustainable growth computation

A corporation generates a return on equity of 16 percent and maintains a dividend payout ratio of 40 percent. What is its sustainable growth rate \(g\)?

Solution

The retention ratio is \(b = 1 - \text{payout ratio} = 1 - 0.40 = 0.60\). Applying the sustainable growth relation: \(g = b \times \mathrm{ROE} = 0.60 \times 0.16 = 0.096\), or 9.6 percent per year.

M3 · Implied cost of equity

A mature consumer staple company trades at \$50.00 per share with an expected next dividend of \$2.00 per share and constant perpetual dividend growth of 4 percent. What is the market-implied cost of equity?

Solution

Rearranging the Gordon growth model: \(P_0 = D_1 / (r - g) \implies r - g = D_1 / P_0 \implies r = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g\). Substituting the given numbers: \(r = \frac{2.00}{50.00} + 0.04 = 0.04 + 0.04 = 0.08\), or 8.0 percent per year.

P1 · Convexity of price in the growth rate

Prove that under the Gordon growth model with \(r > g\), the stock price \(P_0(g)\) is a strictly convex function of the growth rate \(g\).

Solution

Let \(P_0(g) = \frac{D_1(g)}{r - g} = \frac{D_0(1 + g)}{r - g}\). Differentiating with respect to \(g\): \(\frac{\partial P_0}{\partial g} = \frac{D_0(r - g) - D_0(1 + g)(-1)}{(r - g)^2} = \frac{D_0(r + 1)}{(r - g)^2}\). Differentiating a second time: \(\frac{\partial^2 P_0}{\partial g^2} = D_0(r + 1) \frac{\partial}{\partial g}(r - g)^{-2} = D_0(r + 1)(-2)(r - g)^{-3}(-1) = \frac{2 D_0 (1 + r)}{(r - g)^3}\). Since \(D_0 > 0\), \(1 + r > 0\) (Assumption A1), and \(r > g\), \((r - g)^3 > 0\). Thus \(\frac{\partial^2 P_0}{\partial g^2} > 0\), proving strict convexity.

P2 · Value of growth opportunities (PVGO)

Decompose the Gordon share price into no-growth earnings value plus the present value of growth opportunities (PVGO), proving that \(\mathrm{PVGO} > 0\) if and only if \(\mathrm{ROE} > r\).

Solution

If the firm pays out 100% of earnings (\(b = 0\)), dividends equal earnings \(E_1\) and growth is zero; the no-growth value is \(P_{\text{no growth}} = E_1 / r\). In the Gordon model, \(D_1 = E_1(1 - b)\) and \(g = b \cdot \mathrm{ROE}\). Thus \(P_0 = \frac{E_1(1 - b)}{r - b \cdot \mathrm{ROE}}\). Define \(\mathrm{PVGO} = P_0 - \frac{E_1}{r} = \frac{E_1(1 - b)}{r - b \cdot \mathrm{ROE}} - \frac{E_1}{r} = \frac{E_1 [r(1 - b) - (r - b \cdot \mathrm{ROE})]}{r(r - b \cdot \mathrm{ROE})} = \frac{E_1 \cdot b(\mathrm{ROE} - r)}{r(r - g)}\). Since \(E_1 > 0\), \(b > 0\), \(r > 0\), and \(r > g\), \(\mathrm{PVGO} > 0 \iff \mathrm{ROE} > r\).

P3 · Convergence bound for multi-period geometric series

Let \(S_N = \sum_{t=1}^N \frac{D_1(1+g)^{t-1}}{(1+r)^t}\). Prove that the truncation error \(R_N = P_0 - S_N\) satisfies \(R_N = P_0 \left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^N\).

Solution

The finite sum of the geometric series with ratio \(x = \frac{1+g}{1+r}\) is \(S_N = \frac{D_1}{1+g} \sum_{t=1}^N x^t = \frac{D_1}{1+g} \frac{x(1 - x^N)}{1 - x}\). From Proposition 1.1, the infinite sum is \(P_0 = \frac{D_1}{1+g} \frac{x}{1 - x}\). Subtracting: \(R_N = P_0 - S_N = \frac{D_1}{1+g} \frac{x}{1 - x} [1 - (1 - x^N)] = P_0 x^N = P_0 \left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^N\). Since \(0 < x < 1\), \(\lim_{N \to \infty} R_N = 0\).

E1 · Long-run GDP growth bound on terminal g

U.S. nominal GDP growth averaged approximately 5.5% over the 1950–2020 period (roughly 3.0% real growth plus 2.5% inflation). Why do institutional valuation models typically cap terminal growth \(g\) at or below long-run GDP growth?

Solution

If a firm's cash flows grow at a rate \(g > g_{\text{GDP}}\) indefinitely, the firm's revenues would eventually exceed the aggregate output of the entire economy. Furthermore, competitive forces and industry maturation inevitably pull high corporate growth rates down toward or below aggregate economic growth over multi-decade horizons.

E2 · Equity market implied risk premium

Fama and French (2002) estimate the expected equity risk premium using the Gordon growth model on the S&P 500 index: \(r = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g\). Explain why this forward-looking estimate was substantially lower than the historical average realized return in the late 1990s.

Solution

Historical average realized equity returns were elevated in the late 1990s due to massive unanticipated capital gains as market valuation multiples expanded. However, the S&P 500 dividend yield fell to roughly 1.5% while long-run real dividend growth averaged 1.5% to 2.0%, implying an expected return of only 3.5% to 4.0% above inflation, demonstrating that historical average returns overstated the forward-looking discount rate.

E3 · Terminal value sensitivity in research reports

Sell-side initiation reports frequently report a DCF valuation sensitivity table over discount rates (\(r \pm 100\) bps) and terminal growth (\(g \pm 50\) bps). Using the findings of Lab 1, explain why research analysts provide this table.

Solution

Because terminal value represents 70% to 90% of total DCF value, small shifts in \(r\) or \(g\) alter the spread \(\Delta = r - g\) in the Gordon terminal denominator, producing large valuation swings. Providing a sensitivity grid communicates the model's fundamental parameter uncertainty to institutional investors.

R1 · Stochastic dividend growth and Gordon model adjustments

Suppose the dividend growth rate is lognormally distributed: \(\ln(D_{t+1}/D_t) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_g, \sigma_g^2)\). How does Jensen's inequality modify the expected growth rate in the Gordon model?

Solution

Because the exponential function is convex, the expected gross dividend growth rate is \(\E[D_{t+1}/D_t] = \exp(\mu_g + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_g^2)\). The arithmetic expected growth rate is \(g = \exp(\mu_g + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_g^2) - 1\). Volatility in dividend growth (\(\sigma_g^2\)) increases the arithmetic expected dividend growth rate through the convexity adjustment \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma_g^2\).

R2 · Two-stage H-model for competitive convergence

Fuller and Hsia (1984) formulate the H-model where initial growth \(g_a\) decays linearly over half-life \(H\) to long-run perpetual growth \(g_n\): \(P_0 = \frac{D_0}{r - g_n} [ (1 + g_n) + H(g_a - g_n) ]\). Derive this result from the two-stage integral.

Solution

The total dividend stream can be decomposed into two components: a perpetual stream growing at base rate \(g_n\) starting at \(D_0(1+g_n)\), which yields \(\frac{D_0(1+g_n)}{r - g_n}\), and an abnormal excess growth triangle of duration \(2H\) with peak excess rate \(g_a - g_n\). The area of the excess growth triangle is \(\frac{1}{2} \times 2H \times (g_a - g_n) = H(g_a - g_n)\). Capitalizing this excess growth at the perpetual capitalization factor \(\frac{D_0}{r - g_n}\) yields the H-model closed form.

R3 · Inflation pass-through and nominal vs real Gordon growth

Modigliani and Cohn (1979) hypothesized that stock market investors suffer from inflation illusion, discounting nominal cash flows at nominal rates while failing to adjust growth \(g\) for inflation pass-through. Evaluate this hypothesis in terms of the spread \(r - g\).

Solution

If firms can pass inflation through to product prices, nominal dividend growth increases with inflation: \(g_{\text{nom}} \approx g_{\text{real}} + \pi\). If nominal cost of equity also rises with inflation: \(r_{\text{nom}} \approx r_{\text{real}} + \pi\). The valuation spread \(\Delta = r_{\text{nom}} - g_{\text{nom}} = r_{\text{real}} - g_{\text{real}}\) remains invariant to inflation. If investors raise the discount rate for inflation but mistakenly keep growth \(g\) fixed at historical real levels, they artificially widen the spread, severely undervaluing equities during inflationary regimes.

  1. What mathematical condition is required for the Gordon growth model geometric series to converge to a finite price?

    Options

    If \(r \le g\), the common ratio \((1+g)/(1+r) \ge 1\) and the series diverges to infinity.

  2. A company will pay a dividend of 2.00 dollars next year (\(D_1\)). If the cost of equity is 9 percent and perpetual growth is 5 percent, what is the stock price in dollars?

    \(P_0 = D_1 / (r - g) = 2.00 / (0.09 - 0.05) = 2.00 / 0.04 = 50.00\) dollars.

  3. What formula defines the sustainable growth rate \(g\) under clean surplus without new equity issuance?

    Options

    Sustainable growth is the retention ratio \(b\) multiplied by the return on equity \(\mathrm{ROE}\).

  4. If a firm has a retention ratio of 50 percent and a return on equity of 12 percent, what is its sustainable growth rate in percent?

    \(g = 0.50 \times 12\% = 6.0\%\).

  5. What happens to the implied stock price in the Gordon growth model as the spread \(r - g\) approaches zero from above?

    Options

    As the denominator \((r - g) \to 0^+\), the quotient \(D_1 / (r - g)\) diverges to \(+\infty\).

  6. If \(D_1 = 3\) dollars, \(r = 8\%\), and \(g = 5\%\), what is \(P_0\) in dollars?

    \(P_0 = 3.00 / (0.08 - 0.05) = 3.00 / 0.03 = 100.00\) dollars.

  7. Under what condition is the present value of growth opportunities (PVGO) strictly positive?

    Options

    Growth creates value if and only if reinvested capital earns a return exceeding the cost of capital.

  8. A stock trades at 40 dollars with expected dividend of 2 dollars next year. If the market expects perpetual growth of 3 percent, what is the implied cost of equity in percent?

    \(r = D_1 / P_0 + g = 2.00 / 40.00 + 0.03 = 0.05 + 0.03 = 0.08 = 8.0\%\).

  9. Why can perpetual growth rate \(g\) not exceed long-run nominal GDP growth in corporate terminal valuation?

    Options

    Compounding faster than the aggregate economy forever violates macroeconomic feasibility.

  10. What does the H-model approximate in multistage dividend valuation?

    Options

    The Fuller-Hsia H-model models a linear decay in growth from initial rate \(g_a\) to long-term rate \(g_n\).
