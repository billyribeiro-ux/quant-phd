Scenario-Weighted Price Targets

A single deterministic valuation point estimate masks operating uncertainty and macro risk. Sell-side research reports synthesize forward expectations across discrete operational scenarios: base case, bull case, and bear case. Constructing a scenario-weighted price target requires rigorous probability weighting, exact rational expectation arithmetic, and strict commercial rounding rules to prevent numerical ambiguity in financial market execution.

1Definition and Rational Expectation Mechanics

A price target in professional equity analysis represents the probability-weighted expected value of intrinsic equity under a set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive business scenarios.

Definition 1.1 (Scenario-Weighted Price Target)

A scenario-weighted price target is the mathematical expectation of per-share equity value across a finite set of discrete states of nature \(\mathcal{S}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[V] = \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} p_s v_s \end{aligned} \]

subject to the strict probability axioms:

\[ \begin{aligned} p_s \ge 0 \quad \forall s \in \mathcal{S}, \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} p_s = 1 \end{aligned} \]

where each \(v_s \in \mathbb{N}_0\) represents the modeled per-share intrinsic valuation in integer cents under scenario \(s\), and \(p_s \in [0, 1]\) is its rational probability weight.

In standard sell-side practice, the scenario set \(\mathcal{S}\) consists of three primary operating branches:

  1. Base Case (Typical Weight 50%): Reflects consensus operating expectations, mid-cycle margins, and baseline macroeconomic growth.
  2. Bull Case (Typical Weight 25%): Captures upside operational outperformance, market share gains, premium pricing power, and valuation multiple expansion.
  3. Bear Case (Typical Weight 25%): Encapsulates downside regulatory disruption, competitive price erosion, margin compression, and multiple contraction.
Proposition 1.1 (Linearity of the Valuation Target)

The scenario-weighted target operator \(\E[V]\) is a linear functional on the space of scenario valuations: for any scalars \(\alpha, \beta \in \R\) and scenario vectors \(u, v\), \(\E[\alpha u + \beta v] = \alpha \E[u] + \beta \E[v]\).

Proof

By direct expansion using the finite sum: \(\E[\alpha u + \beta v] = \sum_{s} p_s (\alpha u_s + \beta v_s) = \sum_{s} (\alpha p_s u_s + \beta p_s v_s) = \alpha \sum_{s} p_s u_s + \beta \sum_{s} p_s v_s = \alpha \E[u] + \beta \E[v]\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Commercial Rounding: Half-Up vs Banker's Rounding

Financial contracts and published equity research express stock price targets in whole cents (or whole currency units). The exact expected value \(\E[V]\) is a rational number of cents (\(\text{Fraction}\)), requiring a deterministic rounding rule.

Definition 2.1 (Half-Up Cent Rounding Rule)

The commercial half-up rounding rule maps an exact rational value \(x \in \mathbb{Q}\) to the unique nearest integer, resolving an exact half-integer tie (\(x = k + 1/2\) for integer \(k\)) by rounding strictly upwards to \(k + 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{round}_{\text{half-up}}(x) = \left\lfloor x + \frac{1}{2} \right\rfloor \end{aligned} \]

In integer arithmetic with rational fraction \(x = \frac{N}{D}\) where \(D > 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{round}_{\text{half-up}}\left(\frac{N}{D}\right) = \left\lfloor \frac{2N + D}{2D} \right\rfloor = (2N + D) // (2D) \end{aligned} \]
Numerical Trap: Python Built-In Rounding

Standard Python round(x) implements IEEE 754 round-half-to-even (banker's rounding). Under banker's rounding, a half-way tie rounds to the nearest even integer: round(2.5) == 2 while round(3.5) == 4. In commercial equity research, this creates inconsistent price targets (e.g. 5002.5 cents rounding down to 5002 cents rather than up to 5003 cents). Valuation engines must explicitly use floor division on exact fractions: (2 * num + den) // (2 * den).

3Empirical Investment Value of Analyst Recommendations

Do sell-side price targets and recommendations convey genuine information to capital markets, or do they merely reflect marketing momentum? In a seminal empirical investigation, Womack (1996) analyzed the investment value of brokerage analysts' buy and sell recommendations.

Empirical Regularity 3.1 (Recommendation Drift and Asymmetry)

Womack (1996) showed that equity analyst recommendations generate significant price reactions and persistent post-recommendation drift. Crucially, the return reaction is highly asymmetric: additions to "Sell" lists produce larger and more persistent negative abnormal returns (-4.7% initial mean reaction and prolonged multi-month post-recommendation drift) than additions to "Buy" lists (+3.0% initial reaction, fading rapidly).

This asymmetric reaction arises because sell recommendations are rare (typically under 10% of total sell-side recommendations) due to investment banking client relationships and management access conflicts. When an analyst issues a sell recommendation, it carries a substantially stronger informational signal than a standard buy rating.

Lab 1 · Scenario-weighted price target and half-up cent rounding

Calculates scenario-weighted price target across Bull, Base, and Bear scenarios in exact Fraction cents, and demonstrates the exact half-cent tie where commercial half-up rounding yields 5003 cents while Python round() yields 5002 cents.

"""Lab FC4-L09-1: Scenario-weighted price target and half-up cent rounding."""

from fractions import Fraction


def compute_target_cents(scenarios: list[tuple[Fraction, int]]) -> tuple[Fraction, int, int]:
    """Compute exact expected value in cents, half-up rounded integer cents, and round-to-even cents."""
    tot_p = sum((p for p, _ in scenarios), Fraction(0))
    assert tot_p == Fraction(1), "Probabilities must sum to 1"
    exact_cents = sum((p * v for p, v in scenarios), Fraction(0))

    # Half-up rounding: floor(x + 1/2)
    num = 2 * exact_cents.numerator + exact_cents.denominator
    den = 2 * exact_cents.denominator
    half_up = num // den

    # Python built-in round (half-to-even) for comparison
    round_even = round(float(exact_cents))
    return exact_cents, half_up, round_even

    # Case B: Exact half-cent tie (Bull: 50% @ $50.03 [5003c], Bear: 50% @ $50.02 [5002c])
    # Expected value: 5002.5 cents -> half-up gives 5003 cents ($50.03), round-to-even gives 5002 cents ($50.02)
    case_a = [
        (Fraction(25, 100), 6500),

4Where the canon breaks

Despite widespread use, scenario-weighted price targets carry structural limitations:

  • Heuristic Probability Calibration: In practice, analysts rarely estimate scenario probabilities from econometric transition kernels. The conventional 25/50/25 weighting is an arbitrary rule of thumb rather than an empirical probability distribution.
  • Fat Tails and Extreme Disruption: Three discrete scenarios cannot capture the continuous, fat-tailed risk profile of corporate bankruptcy, macro currency devaluations, or disruptive technological displacement.
  • Incentive Distortion: Sell-side targets are frequently reverse-engineered to justify pre-existing buy ratings or investment banking pitches rather than honest expected values.

5Misconceptions

  • A price target is a contractual promise that the stock will trade at that exact price within 12 months.

    Correction: A price target is a mathematical expectation \(\E[V]\) under subjective scenario weights. It does not imply that market price will equal that target on any specific day, as subsequent macro and company news continuously shifts the distribution.

  • Python built-in round() is safe for financial price target calculations.

    Correction: Python uses round-half-to-even. A valuation expectation of $50.025 (5002.5 cents) rounds to 5002 cents under Python round(), but rounds to 5003 cents under commercial half-up rules. Exact rational fraction arithmetic with integer floor division must be used.

  • Scenario probabilities can sum to approximately 1.0 (e.g. 0.999 or 1.001) without consequence.

    Correction: Probability measures must sum to exactly 1: \(\sum p_s = 1\). Tolerating a floating point sum introduces systematic scaling distortion into the expected value operator.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Standard 3-scenario price target

An equity analyst establishes three scenarios for a stock: Bull case of \$80.00 (probability 0.20), Base case of \$55.00 (probability 0.60), and Bear case of \$30.00 (probability 0.20). Compute the exact scenario-weighted price target in integer cents.

Solution

Valuations in cents are 8000, 5500, and 3000. Expected cents: \(\E[V] = 0.20 \times 8000 + 0.60 \times 5500 + 0.20 \times 3000 = 1600 + 3300 + 600 = 5500\) cents (\$55.00). The target is exactly 5500 cents.

M2 · Half-up rounding on fractional expectation

A stock has two scenarios: 50% probability of \$24.15 (2415 cents) and 50% probability of \$24.16 (2416 cents). Compute the exact expected cents and the half-up rounded price target.

Solution

Exact expected value is \(\frac{2415 + 2416}{2} = \frac{4831}{2} = 2415.5\) cents. Applying half-up rounding: \(\lfloor 2415.5 + 0.5 \rfloor = \lfloor 2416.0 \rfloor = 2416\) cents (\$24.16). Under Python round-to-even, it would round to 2416 as well because 2416 is even.

M3 · Exact half-cent tie with odd lower integer

A stock has two scenarios: 50% probability of \$30.02 (3002 cents) and 50% probability of \$30.03 (3003 cents). Compute the half-up target and compare with Python built-in round().

Solution

Expected value is \(3002.5\) cents. Commercial half-up rounds strictly up to 3003 cents (\$30.03). Python built-in round(3002.5) rounds to the nearest even integer, which is 3002 cents (\$30.02). The commercial target is 3003 cents.

Proof

P1 · Bounds on scenario-weighted targets

Prove that for any scenario valuation vector \(v\) with \(v_{\min} = \min_{s} v_s\) and \(v_{\max} = \max_{s} v_s\), the scenario-weighted expected value satisfies \(v_{\min} \le \E[V] \le v_{\max}\).

Solution

Since \(p_s \ge 0\) and \(\sum_s p_s = 1\), \(\E[V] = \sum_s p_s v_s \ge \sum_s p_s v_{\min} = v_{\min} \sum_s p_s = v_{\min}\). Similarly, \(\E[V] = \sum_s p_s v_s \le \sum_s p_s v_{\max} = v_{\max} \sum_s p_s = v_{\max}\). The expected target is strictly bounded within the convex hull of the scenario values.

P2 · Half-up rounding exact integer formula

Prove that for any rational number \(x = \frac{N}{D}\) with positive integer denominator \(D > 0\), \(\lfloor x + 1/2 \rfloor = \lfloor \frac{2N + D}{2D} \rfloor\).

Solution

Write \(x + 1/2 = \frac{N}{D} + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2N + D}{2D}\). Taking the floor of both sides immediately gives \(\lfloor x + 1/2 \rfloor = \lfloor \frac{2N + D}{2D} \rfloor\). In integer arithmetic, this evaluates as the integer division \((2N + D) // (2D)\), proving correctness without floating point errors.

P3 · Monotonicity of target with respect to scenario probabilities

Consider two scenarios \(v_1 < v_2\) with probabilities \(p_1\) and \(p_2 = 1 - p_1\). Prove that \(\E[V]\) is strictly decreasing in \(p_1\).

Solution

Express the expectation as \(\E[V] = p_1 v_1 + (1 - p_1) v_2 = v_2 - p_1 (v_2 - v_1)\). Taking the derivative with respect to \(p_1\): \(\frac{\partial \E[V]}{\partial p_1} = -(v_2 - v_1)\). Since \(v_2 > v_1\), \(v_2 - v_1 > 0\), so the derivative is strictly negative \(-(v_2 - v_1) < 0\). Increasing the probability weight on the lower scenario strictly depresses the price target.

Empirical

E1 · Case A target verification (Lab 1)

Using the Case A parameters in Lab 1 (Bull: 25% @ 6500c, Base: 50% @ 5000c, Bear: 25% @ 3500c), compute the contribution of each scenario to the expected value.

Solution

Bull contribution is \(0.25 \times 6500 = 1625\) cents. Base contribution is \(0.50 \times 5000 = 2500\) cents. Bear contribution is \(0.25 \times 3500 = 875\) cents. Summing: \(1625 + 2500 + 875 = 5000\) cents (\$50.00), exactly matching Lab 1.

E2 · Womack recommendation drift calculation

According to Womack (1996), additions to "Sell" lists experience an initial mean price reaction of -4.7% and a subsequent 6-month drift of -9.1%. If a stock trades at \$100.00 prior to a sell rating, compute the cumulative implied price after 6 months.

Solution

Initial reaction produces price \(100 \times (1 - 0.047) = \$95.30\). Subsequent post-recommendation drift reduces price by an additional 9.1%: \(95.30 \times (1 - 0.091) = 95.30 \times 0.909 = \$86.63\). The cumulative price decline is \(100 - 86.63 = \$13.37\) (-13.37%).

E3 · Skewed scenario distribution

An analyst models a biotechnology company with binary FDA approval: 70% probability of rejection (\$10.00 value) and 30% probability of approval (\$120.00 value). Compute the scenario target in cents.

Solution

Values are 1000 cents and 12000 cents. Expected value: \(\E[V] = 0.70 \times 1000 + 0.30 \times 12000 = 700 + 3600 = 4300\) cents (\$43.00). Notice that the expected value is far above the downside floor of \$10 but far below the upside target of \$120.

Research

R1 · Information content of buy vs sell recommendations

Why did Womack (1996) document that sell recommendations produce significantly more persistent negative abnormal returns than buy recommendations produce positive returns?

Solution

Sell-side analysts face career concerns, commercial pressures, and investment banking underwriting conflicts that strongly discourage issuing sell ratings. Consequently, sell recommendations represent rare, high-conviction signals of material negative fundamental deterioration that overcome institutional reluctance, triggering significant institutional selling pressure and persistent downward drift.

R2 · Expected value vs modal price targets

Explain the economic distinction between a price target defined as the mathematical expectation \(\E[V]\) versus a price target defined as the most likely modal scenario (Base case).

Solution

The modal scenario (Base case) identifies the single most probable outcome, discarding all information regarding upside option value and catastrophic downside tail risk. The mathematical expectation \(\E[V]\) incorporates the full probability distribution, appropriately penalizing intrinsic value for asymmetric downside default risk and rewarding skewness.

R3 · Continuous density discretization error

Suppose intrinsic valuation follows a continuous lognormal distribution. What errors arise when approximating this continuous distribution with a 3-point discrete scenario distribution (Bull, Base, Bear)?

Solution

A 3-point discrete distribution matches at most the first two moments (mean and variance) or three moments (skewness) of a continuous distribution. It fails to match higher-order kurtosis and tail risk, underestimating the probability of extreme tail losses (black swan events) and distorting derivative hedging claims built upon the equity valuation.
