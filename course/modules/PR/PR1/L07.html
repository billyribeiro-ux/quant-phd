Turnover, Costs and Out-of-Sample Evaluation

Theoretical asset allocators frequently collapse in production when confronted with friction and realistic execution. A backtest that ignores transaction costs or unintentionally conditions on future prices creates a dangerous illusion of profitability. Evaluating allocators out of sample requires strict temporal separation between estimation windows and realization periods, rigorous accounting for portfolio turnover frictions, and unyielding discipline against look-ahead bias.

1Rolling-Window Protocol and Temporal Discipline

A realistic backtest simulates the exact sequence of information arrivals and execution decisions faced by a quantitative portfolio manager. Let \(r_t \in \R^N\) denote the vector of asset simple returns realized across period \(t \in \{1, \dots, H\}\).

Definition 7.1 (Rolling Window and Out-of-Sample Protocol)

In a rolling-window framework with lookback window length \(T\) across a total history of \(H\) periods, there are \(K = H - T\) sequential rebalancing dates indexed by \(k \in \{0, \dots, K-1\}\). At decision date \(t = T + k\):

  • The allocator observes strictly past historical returns in the estimation window \([r_{t-T}, r_{t-T+1}, \dots, r_{t-1}]\).
  • The allocator computes the target portfolio weight vector \(w_t \in \R^N\) subject to \(\iota^\top w_t = 1\) and \(w_t \ge 0\).
  • The portfolio is held across period \(t\), realizing gross and net returns evaluated out-of-sample against the newly revealed return vector \(r_t\).
Definition 7.2 (Look-Ahead Bias)

Look-ahead bias occurs whenever the allocation decision \(w_t\) conditions on information, prices, corporate actions, or return statistics realized at or after period \(t\). Even a subtle temporal contamination—such as including \(r_t\) in the estimation window or scaling weights by full-sample standard deviations—severely biases out-of-sample performance metrics upward.

2Turnover and Proportional Transaction Costs

Reallocating portfolio capital from a prior holding \(w_{t-1}\) to a new target \(w_t\) requires transacting in underlying market assets, incurring brokerage fees, exchange charges, and bid-ask spread frictions.

Definition 7.3 (Turnover and Net Return)

Let \(w_{t-1} \in \R^N\) be the prior target weight vector (with initial baseline \(w_0 = \iota / N\)) and \(w_t \in \R^N\) be the newly rebalanced target vector. The portfolio turnover (measured as one-way turnover) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_t = \sum_{i=1}^N |w_{t,i} - w_{t-1,i}| \end{aligned} \]

Given a constant proportional transaction cost rate \(c > 0\) per unit of one-way turnover, the transaction cost incurred at rebalancing date \(t\) is \(C_t = c \, \tau_t\). The resulting net return realized across period \(t\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} R_t^{\mathrm{gross}} = w_t^\top r_t = \sum_{i=1}^N w_{t,i} r_{t,i} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} R_t^{\mathrm{net}} = R_t^{\mathrm{gross}} - c \, \tau_t \end{aligned} \]

For example, a cost rate of \(c = 0.001\) corresponds to 10 basis points per unit of one-way turnover. If an allocator turns over 20% of the portfolio (\(\tau_t = 0.20\)), the friction is \(c \tau_t = 0.001 \times 0.20 = 0.0002\) (2 basis points per period).

3Per-Period Performance Metrics

To assess out-of-sample risk-adjusted performance without introducing annualization artifacts or assumptions regarding compounding periods, we adopt the standard per-period convention.

Definition 7.4 (Per-Period Out-of-Sample Sharpe Ratio)

Across the \(K = H - T\) out-of-sample evaluation periods, let \(\bar{R}^{\mathrm{net}}\) denote the sample mean net return and \(\hat{\sigma}(R^{\mathrm{net}})\) denote the unbiased sample standard deviation computed with divisor \(K - 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{R}^{\mathrm{net}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K R_k^{\mathrm{net}} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\sigma}(R^{\mathrm{net}}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K - 1} \sum_{k=1}^K (R_k^{\mathrm{net}} - \bar{R}^{\mathrm{net}})^2} \end{aligned} \]

The per-period out-of-sample Sharpe ratio is defined as:

\[ \begin{aligned} \SR_{\mathrm{net}} = \frac{\bar{R}^{\mathrm{net}}}{\hat{\sigma}(R^{\mathrm{net}})} \end{aligned} \]

No annualization factor (\(\sqrt{12}\) or \(\sqrt{252}\)) is applied; the ratio measures excess performance per unit of per-period standard deviation.

4Inline Lab: Look-Ahead Bias vs Honest Backtesting

The following experiment simulates a rolling backtest over \(H = 120\) periods with window \(T = 60\) across \(N = 4\) assets under proportional costs of 10 basis points (\(c = 0.001\)). It contrasts an honest backtest against a contaminated variant where the estimation window includes the contemporaneous return \(r_t\).

Lab 7 · Rolling backtest timing and look-ahead bias

Contaminating the estimation window with contemporaneous return r_t artificially inflates the out-of-sample net Sharpe ratio by creating a false information advantage.

"""Lab 7: Out-of-sample backtesting: turnover, costs, and look-ahead bias.

SIMULATED: synthetic return series generated under seed 7.
Compares a strictly honest rolling-window backtest against an intentionally
contaminated look-ahead variant that peeks at period t return during allocation.
Demonstrates how look-ahead bias artificially inflates the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(7)
    n_assets = 4
    h_periods = 120
    t_window = 60
    cost_rate = 0.001  # 10 basis points per unit of one-way turnover

    returns: list[list[float]] = [[rng.gauss(0.006, 0.04) for _ in range(n_assets)] for _ in range(h_periods)]

    def backtest(lookahead: bool) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
        k_dates = h_periods - t_window
        w_prev = [1.0 / n_assets] * n_assets
        net_returns: list[float] = []
        turnovers: list[float] = []

        for k in range(k_dates):
            t = t_window + k
            win = returns[t - t_window + 1 : t + 1] if lookahead else returns[t - t_window : t]

            mu_hat = [sum(win[s][i] for s in range(t_window)) / t_window for i in range(n_assets)]
            pos = [max(m, 0.0) for m in mu_hat]
            sum_pos = sum(pos)
            w = [p / sum_pos for p in pos] if sum_pos > 0 else [1.0 / n_assets] * n_assets

            turnover = sum(abs(w[i] - w_prev[i]) for i in range(n_assets))
            cost = cost_rate * turnover
            gross = sum(w[i] * returns[t][i] for i in range(n_assets))
            net = gross - cost

            net_returns.append(net)
            turnovers.append(turnover)
            w_prev = w

        mean_net = sum(net_returns) / k_dates
        var_net = sum((r - mean_net) ** 2 for r in net_returns) / (k_dates - 1)
        sr = mean_net / math.sqrt(var_net)
        mean_turnover = sum(turnovers) / k_dates
        return sr, mean_net, mean_turnover

    sr_honest, ret_honest, to_honest = backtest(lookahead=False)
    sr_lookahead, ret_lookahead, to_lookahead = backtest(lookahead=True)

    print(f"Honest backtest:    Net SR={sr_honest:.4f}, Net Ret={ret_honest:.4f}, TO={to_honest:.4f}")
    print(f"Lookahead backtest: Net SR={sr_lookahead:.4f}, Net Ret={ret_lookahead:.4f}, TO={to_lookahead:.4f}")
    print(f"Artificial Sharpe inflation: +{(sr_lookahead - sr_honest):.4f}")


main()
Honest backtest:    Net SR=0.2631, Net Ret=0.0064, TO=0.0986
Lookahead backtest: Net SR=0.3850, Net Ret=0.0091, TO=0.0975
Artificial Sharpe inflation: +0.1219

Under strict temporal separation, the honest backtest achieves a per-period Net Sharpe ratio of 0.2631, with a mean net return of 0.0064 and average one-way turnover of 0.0986. Contaminating the window by peeking ahead one period shifts the window from \([t-60, t-1]\) to \([t-59, t]\). This look-ahead leak inflates the realized net Sharpe ratio to 0.3850 and mean return to 0.0091—an artificial Sharpe inflation of +0.1219—illustrating how subtle timing bugs create illusory alpha in quantitative strategies.

5Where the canon breaks

While the rolling-window protocol provides a rigorous testing framework, several simplifying assumptions depart from industrial execution realities:

  • No Holding Drift: The model computes turnover against the previous target weights \(w_{t-1}\). In live trading, price movements during period \(t-1\) cause asset weights to passively drift: \(w_{t-1, i}^{\mathrm{drift}} = w_{t-1, i}(1 + r_{t-1, i}) / (1 + R_{t-1})\). Ignoring drift understates rebalancing turnover.
  • Linear Proportional Costs: Real-world market microstructure features bid-ask half-spreads, fixed ticket fees, and non-linear market impact where trading costs scale with trade size relative to average daily volume (e.g. square-root impact law).
  • Stationary Window Length: Fixing \(T = 60\) is an arbitrary design choice. Market regimes shift between high-volatility turbulence and calm expansion; static window lengths fail to adaptively optimize the bias-variance tradeoff across changing regimes.

6Misconceptions

  1. A strategy with a high gross Sharpe ratio in backtesting will automatically succeed in production.

    High gross returns often come from hyperactive trading in noisy signals. When realistic transaction costs are subtracted from gross profits, excessive turnover can completely erode alpha, turning an apparently lucrative strategy into a net loss.

  2. Using the entire dataset to compute asset volatilities before running a rolling backtest is harmless if expected returns are estimated rolling.

    Full-sample volatility scaling introduces severe look-ahead bias. Volatility estimates embed future market crash information, allowing the optimizer to artfully downweight risky assets decades before the stress regime actually occurs.

  3. One-way turnover is always calculated by dividing the sum of absolute weight changes by two.

    Conventions vary across institutions. Some practitioners define turnover as two-way turnover (\(\sum_i |\Delta w_i|\)) while others divide by two to represent one-way purchases. Precise operational documentation must state whether turnover is one-way or two-way to avoid 50% errors in cost estimation.

7Problem set

M1 · One-way turnover calculation

At date \(t-1\), a portfolio holds weights \(w_{t-1} = (0.4, 0.4, 0.2)\). At date \(t\), the allocator rebalances to target weights \(w_t = (0.2, 0.5, 0.3)\). Calculate the one-way turnover \(\tau_t\).

Solution

The absolute weight changes are: \(|0.2 - 0.4| = 0.2\), \(|0.5 - 0.4| = 0.1\), and \(|0.3 - 0.2| = 0.1\). Summing the absolute differences yields \(\tau_t = 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.40\) (40% one-way turnover).

M2 · Net return after transaction costs

In period \(t\), a portfolio realizes a gross return \(R_t^{\mathrm{gross}} = 0.015\) (1.50%). The rebalancing turnover was \(\tau_t = 0.35\) under a proportional cost rate \(c = 0.0015\) (15 bps). Compute the net return \(R_t^{\mathrm{net}}\).

Solution

The total transaction cost incurred is \(C_t = c \, \tau_t = 0.0015 \times 0.35 = 0.000525\) (5.25 bps). The net return is \(R_t^{\mathrm{net}} = 0.015 - 0.000525 = 0.014475\) (1.4475%).

M3 · Per-period Sharpe ratio calculation

Across \(K = 60\) evaluation dates, a strategy achieves a mean net return of \(\bar{R}^{\mathrm{net}} = 0.008\) and sample standard deviation \(\hat{\sigma} = 0.032\). Calculate the per-period Sharpe ratio.

Solution

Using the per-period convention \(\SR = \bar{R}^{\mathrm{net}} / \hat{\sigma}\), we compute \(\SR = 0.008 / 0.032 = 0.2500\).

P1 · Turnover upper bound

Prove that for any two long-only portfolios \(w_{t-1} \ge 0\) and \(w_t \ge 0\) satisfying the budget constraint \(\iota^\top w = 1\), the one-way turnover \(\tau_t = \sum_{i=1}^N |w_{t,i} - w_{t-1,i}|\) cannot exceed 2.0.

Solution

By the triangle inequality, \(|w_{t,i} - w_{t-1,i}| \le |w_{t,i}| + |w_{t-1,i}| = w_{t,i} + w_{t-1,i}\). Summing over all \(i\) gives \(\sum_{i=1}^N |w_{t,i} - w_{t-1,i}| \le \sum_{i=1}^N w_{t,i} + \sum_{i=1}^N w_{t-1,i} = 1 + 1 = 2\). The maximum occurs when shifting 100% of wealth from one disjoint asset basket to another.

P2 · Buy-and-hold turnover neutrality

Show that a pure buy-and-hold strategy that never changes target weights (\(w_t = w_{t-1}\) for all \(t > 0\)) incurs zero transaction costs under the un-drifted convention.

Solution

When \(w_t = w_{t-1}\), \(w_{t,i} - w_{t-1,i} = 0\) for every asset \(i\). The one-way turnover evaluates to \(\tau_t = \sum_{i=1}^N 0 = 0\). Consequently, \(C_t = c \times 0 = 0\), yielding zero transaction cost deductions.

P3 · Impact of cost on mean return

Prove that subtracting a constant proportional cost \(c \tau_t\) reduces the sample mean net return by exactly \(c \bar{\tau}\), where \(\bar{\tau} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \tau_k\).

Solution

By linearity of the sample mean: \(\bar{R}^{\mathrm{net}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K (R_k^{\mathrm{gross}} - c \tau_k) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K R_k^{\mathrm{gross}} - c \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \tau_k = \bar{R}^{\mathrm{gross}} - c \bar{\tau}\).

E1 · Diagnosing look-ahead bias

In an empirical study, an allocator demonstrates a Sharpe ratio of 1.45 under monthly rebalancing. When shifted to execute trades at the open of \(t+1\) rather than the close of \(t\), the Sharpe ratio drops to 0.32. Explain the likely source of this discrepancy.

Solution

The strategy suffered from look-ahead bias: it calculated allocation weights using closing prices of date \(t\) and assumed it could execute trades at those same closing prices simultaneously, capturing non-tradable returns.

E2 · Turnover penalty in optimization

Describe how a portfolio optimizer can be modified to penalize turnover directly in its objective function.

Solution

The practitioner augments the objective function with a linear turnover penalty term: \(\min_w \frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top S w - \hat{\mu}^\top w + \lambda \sum_{i=1}^N |w_i - w_{0, i}|\), which can be converted to a quadratic program via slack variables.

E3 · Window length trade-off

Discuss the empirical trade-offs of choosing a very short lookback window (\(T = 12\) months) versus a long lookback window (\(T = 120\) months) in a rolling equity backtest.

Solution

A short window (\(T = 12\)) adapts rapidly to structural shifts and market regimes but suffers from extreme estimation variance and high turnover. A long window (\(T = 120\)) minimizes sampling variance but incorporates stale data from obsolete economic regimes.

R1 · Modeling price drift between rebalances

Formulate the exact mathematical expression for drifted asset weights \(w_{t-1}^{\mathrm{drift}}\) at the start of period \(t\) prior to rebalancing, and compare it to the target holding \(w_{t-1}\).

Solution

During period \(t-1\), wealth in asset \(i\) compounds by \(1 + r_{t-1, i}\). The drifted weight is \(w_{t-1, i}^{\mathrm{drift}} = \frac{w_{t-1, i}(1 + r_{t-1, i})}{1 + \sum_j w_{t-1, j} r_{t-1, j}}\). If outperforming assets appreciate faster than underperforming assets, \(w^{\mathrm{drift}} \neq w_{t-1}\), creating additional turnover to re-establish targets.

R2 · Non-linear market impact

Why do quantitative institutional managers use square-root market impact models rather than simple linear proportional cost models?

Solution

Empirical market microstructure shows that large block orders deplete order book depth non-linearly. Price impact scales with the square root of order size normalized by average daily volume, meaning institutional strategies face accelerating execution penalties as capital scales.

R3 · Deflating Sharpe ratios for multiple testing

How does evaluating dozens of allocator variations across the same rolling backtest window invalidate standard asymptotic standard error formulas for the Sharpe ratio?

Solution

Testing multiple hyperparameter configurations on identical data induces data-snooping bias. The maximum Sharpe ratio across trials follows an extreme value distribution, requiring statistical corrections such as White's Reality Check or Bailey-López de Prado deflated Sharpe ratios.

9Retrieval quiz

  1. In a rolling-window backtest with window length \(T = 60\) at rebalancing date \(t = 60\), which historical returns may be included in the estimation window without look-ahead bias?

    Options

    At date \(t = 60\), the return across period 60 has not yet occurred. The estimation window must strictly terminate at period \(t-1 = 59\).

  2. What is the theoretical maximum one-way turnover \(\tau_t = \sum_{i=1}^N |w_{t,i} - w_{t-1,i}|\) for a long-only, budget-constrained portfolio?

    Options

    If 100% of wealth is liquidated from basket A (\(\Delta w = -1\)) and invested in basket B (\(\Delta w = +1\)), the one-way turnover reaches \(|-1| + |+1| = 2.0\).

  3. If the proportional transaction cost rate is \(c = 0.001\), how many basis points of transaction cost are incurred per unit of one-way turnover?

    Options

    One basis point is 0.01% or 0.0001. Therefore, \(c = 0.0010 = 10 \times 0.0001 = 10\) basis points.

  4. Why does look-ahead bias in backtesting artificially inflate the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio?

    Options

    Conditioning on future prices allows the optimizer to overweight assets that are already known to rise, creating artificial, non-reproducible profits.

  5. What convention is used for computing the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio in this module?

    Options

    Per the specification, performance is evaluated per period using the unbiased sample standard deviation with divisor \(K - 1\).

  6. In Lab 7, what is the effect on the net Sharpe ratio when look-ahead bias is introduced?

    Options

    The lab shows that including the contemporaneous period return artificially lifts the net Sharpe ratio by +0.1219 from 0.2631 to 0.3850.

  7. What happens to portfolio weights between rebalancing dates in actual physical markets?

    Options

    Asset price movements alter individual asset values, causing portfolio weights to passively drift away from the previous target.

  8. If a portfolio rebalances from \(w_0 = (0.5, 0.5)\) to \(w_1 = (0.3, 0.7)\), what is the one-way turnover \(\tau_1\)?

    \(|0.3 - 0.5| + |0.7 - 0.5| = 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.40\).
