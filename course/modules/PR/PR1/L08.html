Ranking portfolio allocators requires distinguishing genuine structural superiority from statistical noise and simulation artifacts. While textbook theory suggests that sample mean-variance optimization should dominate heuristic rules, empirical shoot-outs reveal that estimation error and turnover costs severely handicap unconstrained optimizers. Understanding the statistical distribution of Sharpe ratio differences and the relative hierarchy of parameter estimation errors allows quantitative researchers to construct robust, evidence-based allocation systems.

1Statistical Significance of Sharpe Ratio Differences

When comparing two allocation strategies \(A\) and \(B\) evaluated across the same \(K\) out-of-sample periods, observing \(\widehat{\SR}_A > \widehat{\SR}_B\) does not automatically establish statistical superiority. Because the two strategies trade in the same market environment, their realized returns are correlated.

Definition 8.1 (Sharpe Ratio Difference and Standard Error)

Let \(R_A, R_B \in \R^K\) be the realized out-of-sample net return vectors of allocators \(A\) and \(B\), with sample means \(\hat{\mu}_A, \hat{\mu}_B\), sample variances \(\hat{\sigma}_A^2, \hat{\sigma}_B^2\), correlation \(\hat{\rho}\), and sample Sharpe ratios \(\widehat{\SR}_A = \hat{\mu}_A / \hat{\sigma}_A\) and \(\widehat{\SR}_B = \hat{\mu}_B / \hat{\sigma}_B\). Under the assumption of independent and identically distributed returns (Sharpe ratio standard error i.i.d.), the asymptotic variance of the difference \(\Delta \widehat{\SR} = \widehat{\SR}_A - \widehat{\SR}_B\) satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\left(\Delta \widehat{\SR}\right) &\approx \frac{1}{K} \left[ 2(1 - \hat{\rho}) + \frac{1}{2}\left(\widehat{\SR}_A^2 + \widehat{\SR}_B^2 - 2 \widehat{\SR}_A \widehat{\SR}_B \hat{\rho}^2\right) \right] \end{aligned} \]

When two strategies hold highly correlated portfolios (\(\hat{\rho} \to 1\)), the variance of their difference shrinks dramatically, enabling statistical detection of small performance spreads. Conversely, when comparing strategies with low return correlation, hundreds of out-of-sample periods are required to reject the null hypothesis \(H_0: \SR_A = \SR_B\).

2The Error Hierarchy: Means vs Covariances

Why do sophisticated optimizers struggle out of sample? The foundational answer lies in the asymmetric impact of parameter estimation errors. In a seminal numerical study, Chopra and Ziemba (1993) demonstrated that errors in expected means \(\hat{\mu}\) exert roughly ten times greater impact on investor utility loss than errors in variances, and twenty times greater impact than errors in covariances.

Proposition 8.1 (Relative Error Sensitivity)

In portfolio choice under quadratic utility \(U(w) = w^\top \mu - \frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top \Sigma w\), the loss in certainty-equivalent return induced by a perturbation \(\Delta \mu\) in expected returns scales quadratically with the perturbation magnitude and inversely with risk aversion \(\gamma\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta U_{\mu} \approx \frac{1}{2\gamma} \Delta \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \Delta \mu \end{aligned} \]

In contrast, a perturbation \(\Delta \Sigma\) in the covariance matrix creates a second-order utility loss that scales inversely with \(\gamma^2\). For typical risk aversion levels (\(\gamma \approx 3\)), errors in the mean vector dominate covariance errors by an order of magnitude.

This mathematical asymmetry explains why naive equal weighting (1/N) and global minimum-variance allocators perform so well: they completely discard the highly contaminated sample mean vector \(\hat{\mu}\), eliminating the primary driver of out-of-sample portfolio failure.

3Empirical Shoot-Out: 1/N vs Shrinkage and Sample MV

The academic literature presents two competing empirical viewpoints:

  • The 1/N Dominance Hypothesis: DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2007) found that naive 1/N is remarkably hard to beat out of sample across standard multi-asset equity datasets, requiring thousands of historical months for sample mean-variance to reliably overcome estimation noise.
  • The Regularization Advantage: In contrast, Ledoit and Wolf (2017) demonstrated that advanced covariance shrinkage—particularly nonlinear shrinkage based on random matrix theory—alongside factor structures and disciplined turnover management reliably achieves out-of-sample Sharpe ratios superior to 1/N in broad equity universes.

"""Lab 8: Allocator ranking across random seeds: 1/N benchmark vs sample MV.

SIMULATED: 30 independent synthetic market simulations (seeds 0 through 29).
Computes out-of-sample net Sharpe ratios for 1/N equal weighting versus
unconstrained/long-only sample mean-variance optimization under realistic
turnover costs (10 bps). Evaluates empirical win rates across market environments.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
import math
import random


def _solve_linear(a_mat: list[list[float]], b: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    n = len(b)
    m_sys = [[*a_mat[i], b[i]] for i in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        max_row = max(range(i, n), key=lambda r: abs(m_sys[r][i]))
        if abs(m_sys[max_row][i]) < 1e-14:
            raise ValueError("Singular matrix encountered.")
        if max_row != i:
            m_sys[i], m_sys[max_row] = m_sys[max_row], m_sys[i]
        pivot = m_sys[i][i]
        for j in range(i, n + 1):
            m_sys[i][j] /= pivot
        for r in range(n):
            if r != i:
                factor = m_sys[r][i]
                if factor != 0.0:
                    for j in range(i, n + 1):
                        m_sys[r][j] -= factor * m_sys[i][j]
    return [m_sys[i][n] for i in range(n)]


def _solve_long_only_qp(q_mat: list[list[float]], c: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    n = len(c)
    for k in range(n):
        for z in itertools.combinations(range(n), k):
            z_set = set(z)
            a = [i for i in range(n) if i not in z_set]
            m = len(a)
            sys_a = [[0.0] * (m + 1) for _ in range(m + 1)]
            sys_b = [0.0] * (m + 1)
            for i_idx, i in enumerate(a):
                for j_idx, j in enumerate(a):
                    sys_a[i_idx][j_idx] = q_mat[i][j]
                sys_a[i_idx][m] = 1.0
                sys_b[i_idx] = c[i]
            for j_idx in range(m):
                sys_a[m][j_idx] = 1.0
            sys_b[m] = 1.0
            try:
                sol = _solve_linear(sys_a, sys_b)
            except ValueError:
                continue
            w_a = sol[:m]
            nu = sol[m]
            if any(x < -1e-9 for x in w_a):
                continue
            w = [0.0] * n
            for idx, i in enumerate(a):
                w[i] = max(w_a[idx], 0.0)
            tot = sum(w)
            if tot <= 1e-12:
                continue
            w = [x / tot for x in w]
            dual_ok = True
            for i in z:
                grad_i = sum(q_mat[i][j] * w[j] for j in range(n)) - c[i] + nu
                if grad_i < -1e-8:
                    dual_ok = False
                    break
            if dual_ok:
                return w
    raise RuntimeError("No KKT solution found.")


def sample_mv(win: list[list[float]], gamma: float = 3.0) -> list[float]:
    t = len(win)
    n = len(win[0])
    means = [sum(win[s][i] for s in range(t)) / t for i in range(n)]
    cov = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for s in range(t):
        diff = [win[s][i] - means[i] for i in range(n)]
        for i in range(n):
            for j in range(n):
                cov[i][j] += diff[i] * diff[j]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            cov[i][j] /= t - 1
    q = [[gamma * cov[i][j] for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    return _solve_long_only_qp(q, means)


def main() -> None:
    num_seeds = 30
    n_assets = 4
    h_periods = 120
    t_window = 60
    k_dates = h_periods - t_window
    cost_rate = 0.001

    ew_beats_mv = 0
    ew_sharpes: list[float] = []
    mv_sharpes: list[float] = []

    for seed in range(num_seeds):
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        returns = [[rng.gauss(0.006, 0.04) for _ in range(n_assets)] for _ in range(h_periods)]

        # 1. 1/N strategy
        w_ew = [1.0 / n_assets] * n_assets
        net_ew: list[float] = []
        for k in range(k_dates):
            t = t_window + k
            g = sum(w_ew[i] * returns[t][i] for i in range(n_assets))
            net_ew.append(g)
        m_ew = sum(net_ew) / k_dates
        v_ew = sum((x - m_ew) ** 2 for x in net_ew) / (k_dates - 1)
        sr_ew = m_ew / math.sqrt(v_ew)
        ew_sharpes.append(sr_ew)

        # 2. Sample MV strategy
        w_prev = [1.0 / n_assets] * n_assets
        net_mv: list[float] = []
        for k in range(k_dates):
            t = t_window + k
            win = returns[t - t_window : t]
            w = sample_mv(win)
            turnover = sum(abs(w[i] - w_prev[i]) for i in range(n_assets))
            g = sum(w[i] * returns[t][i] for i in range(n_assets))
            net_mv.append(g - cost_rate * turnover)
            w_prev = w
        m_mv = sum(net_mv) / k_dates
        v_mv = sum((x - m_mv) ** 2 for x in net_mv) / (k_dates - 1)
        sr_mv = m_mv / math.sqrt(v_mv)
        mv_sharpes.append(sr_mv)

        if sr_ew > sr_mv:
            ew_beats_mv += 1

    print(f"Seeds where 1/N beats sample MV: {ew_beats_mv} / {num_seeds}")
    print(f"Mean 1/N Sharpe: {sum(ew_sharpes) / num_seeds:.4f}")
    print(f"Mean MV Sharpe:  {sum(mv_sharpes) / num_seeds:.4f}")


main()
Seeds where 1/N beats sample MV: 23 / 30
Mean 1/N Sharpe: 0.3178
Mean MV Sharpe:  0.2002

Across the 30 independent simulations, naive \(1/N\) achieves a higher net Sharpe ratio than sample mean-variance on 23 out of 30 seeds (a 76.7% win rate). The cross-seed average net Sharpe ratio is 0.3178 for \(1/N\) compared to 0.2002 for sample mean-variance. This performance gap demonstrates that unconstrained re-estimation incurs substantial turnover costs and suffers from error maximization, whereas \(1/N\) incurs zero rebalancing turnover in an un-drifted setting.

5Where the canon breaks

While simulation shoot-outs highlight the risks of unconstrained optimization, the setup embodies several key simplifications:

  • Symmetric Synthetic Returns: The simulated data generator draws returns from identical Gaussian distributions (\(\mu_i = 0.006, \sigma_i = 0.04\)). By Theorem 3.1, \(1/N\) is theoretically optimal in this universe; testing in an environment where true means are identical heavily rigs the contest in favor of \(1/N\).
  • Finite Simulation Count: Evaluating 30 seeds constitutes a finite enumeration (single simulation study). The exact win count (23/30) characterizes this particular generator, not empirical financial markets.
  • Cross-Sectional Dispersion in Real Markets: In actual asset markets, assets possess vastly different expected returns, volatilities, and market capitalizations. Applying \(1/N\) to a combined universe of Treasury bills, high-yield debt, and tech equities produces severe risk concentration that sophisticated allocators easily beat.

6Misconceptions

  1. 1/N is universally superior to all optimized portfolio strategies across all asset classes.

    1/N outperforms raw sample mean-variance when estimation error is large relative to cross-sectional return dispersion. When assets have substantially different risk profiles or when factor models and shrinkage are employed, optimized portfolios reliably beat 1/N.

  2. A backtested Sharpe ratio difference of 0.10 over 60 months proves that one strategy is definitively superior to another.

    Due to finite-sample sampling variance, a Sharpe ratio spread of 0.10 over 60 monthly periods typically has a p-value well above 0.05 unless return correlation is near 1.0, failing standard tests of statistical significance.

  3. Covariance estimation errors are the dominant threat to portfolio optimization performance.

    Chopra and Ziemba (1993) demonstrated that errors in expected returns are roughly an order of magnitude more damaging to investor utility than errors in covariance estimates.

7Problem set

M1 · Variance of Sharpe ratio difference

Two strategies \(A\) and \(B\) evaluated over \(K = 100\) periods have Sharpe ratios \(\widehat{\SR}_A = 0.30\) and \(\widehat{\SR}_B = 0.20\), with return correlation \(\hat{\rho} = 0.80\). Compute the approximate standard error of \(\Delta \widehat{\SR}\) under i.i.d. normality.

Solution

We compute \(2(1 - 0.80) = 0.40\). The second term is \(\frac{1}{2}(0.30^2 + 0.20^2 - 2 \times 0.30 \times 0.20 \times 0.80^2) = \frac{1}{2}(0.09 + 0.04 - 0.12 \times 0.64) = \frac{1}{2}(0.13 - 0.0768) = 0.0266\). Total variance is \((0.40 + 0.0266) / 100 = 0.004266\). The standard error is \(\sqrt{0.004266} \approx 0.0653\).

M2 · Win rate calculation in Lab 8

In Lab 8, 1/N beats sample MV on 23 out of 30 seeds. Express this win rate as a percentage and calculate the margin of victory between the mean Sharpe ratios.

Solution

The win rate is \(23 / 30 \approx 76.67\%\). The difference in mean Sharpe ratios is \(0.3178 - 0.2002 = 0.1176\) (a 58.7% relative improvement over sample MV).

M3 · t-statistic for Sharpe difference

If \(\Delta \widehat{\SR} = 0.1176\) and the estimated standard error is \(\operatorname{SE}(\Delta \widehat{\SR}) = 0.050\), compute the t-statistic for the hypothesis that Strategy A outperforms Strategy B.

Solution

The t-statistic is \(t = \frac{\Delta \widehat{\SR}}{\operatorname{SE}} = \frac{0.1176}{0.050} = 2.352\). Because \(2.352 > 1.96\), the difference is statistically significant at the 5% level.

P1 · Correlation dampening of Sharpe variance

Prove that as the return correlation \(\hat{\rho} \to 1\) and \(\widehat{\SR}_A \to \widehat{\SR}_B\), the variance of the Sharpe ratio difference \(\operatorname{Var}(\Delta \widehat{\SR})\) converges to zero.

Solution

Substituting \(\hat{\rho} = 1\) and \(\widehat{\SR}_A = \widehat{\SR}_B = \SR\) into Definition 8.1 yields \(2(1 - 1) + \frac{1}{2}(\SR^2 + \SR^2 - 2 \SR^2 (1)) = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(2\SR^2 - 2\SR^2) = 0\). Thus \(\operatorname{Var}(\Delta \widehat{\SR}) \to 0\).

P2 · Loss invariance under uniform mean shift

Prove that if all asset expected returns are shifted by a common scalar \(c\) (\(\mu \to \mu + c \iota\)), the optimal unconstrained mean-variance portfolio weights subject to \(\iota^\top w = 1\) remain completely unchanged.

Solution

For any budget-constrained portfolio \(\iota^\top w = 1\), the portfolio return is \((\mu + c\iota)^\top w = \mu^\top w + c (\iota^\top w) = \mu^\top w + c\). Adding a constant \(c\) to the objective function does not alter the gradient with respect to \(w\) or the location of the argmin.

P3 · Zero turnover property of 1/N

Show that an allocator adhering strictly to \(w_t = \iota / N\) generates exactly zero transaction costs at every period \(t > 0\) under the un-drifted target rebalancing convention.

Solution

Under the un-drifted model, turnover is defined as \(\tau_t = \sum_{i=1}^N |w_{t,i} - w_{t-1,i}|\). Since \(w_{t,i} = 1/N\) and \(w_{t-1,i} = 1/N\), \(|1/N - 1/N| = 0\) for all \(i\). The sum is zero, resulting in zero transaction cost deductions.

E1 · Explaining the 1/N win rate

Why does 1/N beat sample mean-variance on 76.7% of seeds in Lab 8 even though sample mean-variance optimizes expected returns in each window?

Solution

Sample mean-variance suffers from error maximization in each 60-period window, chasing noisy return spikes that fail to persist out of sample. This induces high turnover and execution penalties that severely drag down net performance.

E2 · Impact of removing transaction costs

If the cost rate in Lab 8 were reduced from \(c = 0.001\) (10 bps) to \(c = 0\) (zero costs), describe how the Sharpe ratio gap between 1/N and sample mean-variance would change.

Solution

Eliminating costs removes the penalty on sample MV's high monthly turnover. While sample MV Sharpe ratios would increase, estimation error in weights would still prevent it from consistently dominating 1/N in an identical-return DGP.

E3 · Role of factor models in broad universes

Why do empirical studies find that structural factor models (e.g. Fama-French 5-factor) allow optimized portfolios to reliably outperform 1/N in universes of \(N = 500\) stocks?

Solution

Factor models impose structural economic constraints on the covariance matrix and expected returns, reducing the effective parameter space from \(O(N^2)\) to \(O(NK)\). This drastically suppresses estimation error while exploiting genuine cross-sectional factor premia.

R1 · Asymptotic requirements for Markowitz dominance

Discuss the practical implications of DeMiguel et al. (2007) finding that an unconstrained optimizer requires over 3,000 months of data to beat 1/N in an \(N = 50\) asset universe.

Solution

Three thousand months corresponds to 250 years, far exceeding available financial market history. Because financial regimes shift over 5 to 10 year cycles, financial data cannot satisfy the stationarity assumptions required for unconstrained sample optimizers to outperform naive rules.

R2 · Nonlinear shrinkage vs linear shrinkage

How does nonlinear covariance shrinkage (Ledoit and Wolf 2017) overcome the limitations of linear shrinkage when ranking allocators in high-dimensional settings?

Solution

Linear shrinkage applies a uniform shrinkage intensity across all sample eigenvalues. Nonlinear shrinkage uses the Marcenko-Pastur equation from Random Matrix Theory to individually shrink each sample eigenvalue according to its specific noise distortion, dramatically improving precision in minimum-variance portfolios.

R3 · Designing an industrial allocator shoot-out

Outline three indispensable requirements for designing a rigorous, non-misleading institutional allocator shoot-out.

Solution

An institutional shoot-out must enforce: (1) strict out-of-sample temporal ordering with no look-ahead leaks; (2) realistic transaction cost modeling accounting for portfolio turnover and bid-ask spreads; and (3) statistical hypothesis testing (such as Ledoit-Wolf or bootstrap tests) of Sharpe ratio differences rather than point comparisons.
