Optimizer Error Maximization

Standard optimization algorithms treat statistical estimates as exact mathematical truths. Because optimizers search for extreme values, they systematically over-allocate to assets whose expected returns are over-estimated by sample noise and whose risks are under-estimated. The resulting portfolios maximize estimation error rather than investment performance.

1The Mechanics of Error Maximization

In mean-variance optimization, the optimal portfolio weight vector \(\hat{w}\) solves an optimization program parameterized by sample statistics \(\hat{\mu}\) and \(S\). When statistical noise perturbs these estimates away from the true population parameters \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\), the optimizer exhibits a systematic mathematical pathology first articulated by Michaud (1989).

Definition 2.1 (Optimizer Error Maximization)

Optimizer error maximization is the property of mathematical optimizers whereby the search for an objective extremum systematically selects and amplifies estimation errors. Assets with large positive sampling errors in expected return (\(\hat{\mu}_i \gg \mu_i\)) or downward errors in estimated variance (\(S_{ii} \ll \Sigma_{ii}\)) receive the largest long allocations, while assets with negative sampling errors are aggressively shorted or pruned to zero.

Consider an unconstrained universe of \(N\) uncorrelated assets with identical true expected returns \(\mu_i = \bar{\mu}\) and identical true variances \(\sigma_i^2 = \sigma^2\). Under the true distribution, the unique optimal portfolio is the equal-weight allocation \(w_i^* = 1/N\). However, in any finite sample of size \(T\), the sample means \(\hat{\mu}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{\mu}, \sigma^2 / T)\) diverge due to Gaussian sampling variation.

Theorem 2.1 (Expected Weight Spread Under Identical Assets)

Assumption A1 (Identical Uncorrelated Assets): Let \(\mu = \bar{\mu} \iota\) and \(\Sigma = \sigma^2 I_N\) with known variance \(\sigma^2 > 0\). Let sample means \(\hat{\mu} \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{\mu} \iota, \frac{\sigma^2}{T} I_N)\) be independently estimated over \(T\) observations.

Under Assumption A1, the unconstrained mean-variance weight vector \(\hat{w}\) has expected value \(\E[\hat{w}] = \frac{1}{N} \iota\) and covariance matrix

\[ \begin{aligned} \Cov(\hat{w}) = \frac{1}{\gamma^2 \sigma^2 T} \left( I_N - \frac{1}{N} \iota \iota^\top \right) \end{aligned} \]

Furthermore, the expected variance of the sample weights is strictly positive and scales inversely with sample size \(T\): \(\Var(\hat{w}_i) = \frac{N - 1}{N \gamma^2 \sigma^2 T}\).

Proof

From Theorem 1.1, when \(\Sigma = \sigma^2 I_N\), \(\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} I_N\) and \(\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota = \frac{N}{\sigma^2}\). The weight formula simplifies to

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{w} = \frac{1}{N} \iota + \frac{1}{\gamma \sigma^2} \left( I_N - \frac{1}{N} \iota \iota^\top \right) \hat{\mu} \end{aligned} \]

Taking expectations, \(\E[\hat{w}] = \frac{1}{N} \iota\) because \((I_N - \frac{1}{N} \iota \iota^\top) \bar{\mu} \iota = \bar{\mu} (\iota - \iota) = 0\). The covariance matrix of \(\hat{w}\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \Cov(\hat{w}) = \frac{1}{\gamma^2 \sigma^4} \left( I_N - \frac{1}{N} \iota \iota^\top \right) \Cov(\hat{\mu}) \left( I_N - \frac{1}{N} \iota \iota^\top \right) \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\Cov(\hat{\mu}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{T} I_N\) and the projection matrix \(P = I_N - \frac{1}{N} \iota \iota^\top\) is idempotent (\(P^2 = P\)), we obtain \(\Cov(\hat{w}) = \frac{1}{\gamma^2 \sigma^2 T} P\). The diagonal entries of \(P\) are \(1 - 1/N = (N - 1)/N\), yielding the stated variance.

2The Resampling Heuristic

To combat error maximization, Michaud proposed the resampled efficient frontier. Instead of solving a single mean-variance optimization on point estimates \((\hat{\mu}, S)\), the procedure simulates \(M\) synthetic return panels of size \(T\) drawn from \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}, S)\). For each resample \(m = 1, \dots, M\), the optimizer computes the optimal weights \(\hat{w}^{(m)}\), and the final resampled portfolio averages these weights across simulations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{w}_{\text{resampled}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \hat{w}^{(m)} \end{aligned} \]
Remark 2.1 (Statistical Critique of Resampling)

While resampled efficiency stabilizes portfolio allocations and prevents extreme corner solutions, academic research demonstrates that resampling is fundamentally an ad-hoc shrinkage heuristic toward the equal-weight portfolio. It lacks an axiomatic Bayesian decision-theoretic foundation because it averages across simulated sample paths rather than integrating over the posterior distribution of unknown parameters.

3Numerical Lab: Resampling Instability

The following inline lab (curriculum lab) demonstrates optimizer instability by drawing 200 synthetic resamples of length \(T = 60\) from a known four-asset Gaussian model. It compares the unconstrained mean-variance weights of the true parameters against the mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum of the optimizer weights across resamples.

Lab 2 · Optimizer instability under resampling

SIMULATED: Draws 200 synthetic resamples (T = 60) from a 4-asset model to reveal extreme weight dispersion around true targets driven by error maximization.

"""Lab 2: Optimizer instability under resampling (curriculum lab).

SIMULATED: seeded synthetic resamples from a four-asset Gaussian model.
Demonstrates error maximization: small sampling fluctuations in estimated
means and covariances produce extreme spreads and instability in the
resulting unconstrained mean-variance portfolio weights.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def _solve_linear(a_mat: list[list[float]], b: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    n = len(b)
    m_sys = [[*a_mat[i], b[i]] for i in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        max_row = max(range(i, n), key=lambda r: abs(m_sys[r][i]))
        if max_row != i:
            m_sys[i], m_sys[max_row] = m_sys[max_row], m_sys[i]
        pivot = m_sys[i][i]
        for j in range(i, n + 1):
            m_sys[i][j] /= pivot
        for r in range(n):
            if r != i:
                factor = m_sys[r][i]
                if factor != 0.0:
                    for j in range(i, n + 1):
                        m_sys[r][j] -= factor * m_sys[i][j]
    return [m_sys[i][n] for i in range(n)]


def unconstrained_mv(sigma: list[list[float]], mu: list[float], gamma: float) -> list[float]:
    """Compute unconstrained mean-variance weights satisfying iota' w = 1."""
    n = len(mu)
    sys_a = [[0.0] * (n + 1) for _ in range(n + 1)]
    sys_b = [0.0] * (n + 1)
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            sys_a[i][j] = gamma * sigma[i][j]
        sys_a[i][n] = 1.0
        sys_b[i] = mu[i]
    for j in range(n):
        sys_a[n][j] = 1.0
    sys_b[n] = 1.0

    sol = _solve_linear(sys_a, sys_b)
    return sol[:n]


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    mu_true = [0.08, 0.07, 0.05, 0.03]
    sigma_true = [
        [0.0400, 0.0100, 0.0050, 0.0020],
        [0.0100, 0.0350, 0.0080, 0.0040],
        [0.0050, 0.0080, 0.0250, 0.0060],
        [0.0020, 0.0040, 0.0060, 0.0150],
    ]
    gamma = 2.5
    w_true = unconstrained_mv(sigma_true, mu_true, gamma)

    # Cholesky decomposition of Sigma_true
    chol = [[0.0] * 4 for _ in range(4)]
    for i in range(4):
        for j in range(i + 1):
            s = sum(chol[i][k] * chol[j][k] for k in range(j))
            if i == j:
                chol[i][j] = math.sqrt(sigma_true[i][i] - s)
            else:
                chol[i][j] = (sigma_true[i][j] - s) / chol[j][j]

    m_sims = 200
    t_window = 60
    resampled_weights: list[list[float]] = []

    for _ in range(m_sims):
        window: list[list[float]] = []
        for _ in range(t_window):
            z = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(4)]
            r = [mu_true[i] + sum(chol[i][k] * z[k] for k in range(4)) for i in range(4)]
            window.append(r)

        # Estimate sample mean and sample covariance
        mu_hat = [sum(window[t][i] for t in range(t_window)) / t_window for i in range(4)]
        s_mat = [[0.0] * 4 for _ in range(4)]
        for t in range(t_window):
            for i in range(4):
                for j in range(4):
                    s_mat[i][j] += (window[t][i] - mu_hat[i]) * (window[t][j] - mu_hat[j])
        for i in range(4):
            for j in range(4):
                s_mat[i][j] /= t_window - 1

        w_hat = unconstrained_mv(s_mat, mu_hat, gamma)
        resampled_weights.append(w_hat)

    print("True weights:", [round(x, 4) for x in w_true])
    for i in range(4):
        vals = [w[i] for w in resampled_weights]
        mean_val = sum(vals) / m_sims
        std_val = math.sqrt(sum((x - mean_val) ** 2 for x in vals) / (m_sims - 1))
        print(f"Asset {i}: mean={mean_val:.4f}, sd={std_val:.4f}, min={min(vals):.4f}, max={max(vals):.4f}")

The true optimal weights are \(w^* = (0.4549, 0.3614, 0.2444, -0.0607)\). While the resampled means \((0.4651, 0.3789, 0.2582, -0.1023)\) approximate the true targets, the sampling dispersion is staggering. Asset 0 fluctuates between \(-0.3068\) and \(+1.7777\); Asset 3 swings from \(-1.3821\) to \(+0.9653\). Standard deviations exceed 0.31 to 0.44 across all assets. 4Where the canon breaks

Error maximization undermines practical mean-variance optimization across several dimensions:

  • The Winner's Curse: Assets allocated the largest weights in sample mean-variance portfolios are statistically guaranteed to experience downward mean reversion out of sample, as extreme sample means reflect positive noise.
  • Eigenvalue Inversion: Small sample eigenvalues of \(S\) correspond to noise portfolios with low in-sample variance. The optimizer levers up these noise directions, leading to massive realized volatility when out-of-sample covariance shifts.
  • Instability of Resampling: Resampling smooths weights but introduces an arbitrary simulation artifact: the resulting allocation depends on the chosen number of resamples \(M\) and the rank-ordering convention used across points on the frontier.

5Misconceptions

  1. An optimizer selects assets because their true underlying fundamentals are superior.

    An optimizer selects assets based strictly on numerical inputs. In a universe of 50 assets with identical true returns, the optimizer will place 100% of wealth into the 5 assets that happened to experience the most positive sampling noise during the estimation window.

  2. Resampled efficiency represents an axiomatic Bayesian portfolio construction methodology.

    Michaud's resampling heuristic averages optimizer solutions across bootstrap draws, but it does not evaluate expected utility under a Bayesian predictive distribution. Scherer (2002) showed that resampling is an ad-hoc shrinkage method dominated by formal Bayesian shrinkage estimators.

  3. Long-only constraints prevent error maximization from degrading performance.

    While constraints prevent short-sale leverage, error maximization still forces all available capital into the handful of assets with the most upward-biased sample returns, causing severe portfolio concentration and zero-weight allocations for viable diversifying assets.

6Problem set

M1 · Weight variance scaling

Under the conditions of Theorem 2.1 with \(N = 5\) assets, \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(\gamma = 3\), and estimation window \(T = 60\), calculate the exact theoretical standard deviation of an asset's sample weight \(\text{SD}(\hat{w}_i)\).

Solution

By Theorem 2.1, \(\Var(\hat{w}_i) = \frac{N - 1}{N \gamma^2 \sigma^2 T}\). Substituting the parameters: \(N - 1 = 4\), \(N = 5\), \(\gamma^2 = 9\), \(\sigma^2 = 0.04\), \(T = 60\). The denominator is \(5 \times 9 \times 0.04 \times 60 = 5 \times 9 \times 2.4 = 108\). Thus \(\Var(\hat{w}_i) = 4 / 108 = 1 / 27 \approx 0.037037\). Taking the square root gives \(\text{SD}(\hat{w}_i) = \sqrt{1 / 27} = 1 / (3 \sqrt{3}) \approx 0.19245\) (19.25% weight standard deviation).

M2 · Required window length for weight stability

In the setup of Problem M1, how large must the estimation window \(T\) be to reduce the theoretical standard deviation of the asset weights to \(\text{SD}(\hat{w}_i) \le 0.05\) (5%)?

Solution

We require \(\Var(\hat{w}_i) \le 0.05^2 = 0.0025 = 1 / 400\). Using the formula: \(\frac{4}{1.8 T} \le \frac{1}{400} \implies \frac{400 \times 4}{1.8} \le T \implies T \ge \frac{1600}{1.8} \approx 888.89\). The required estimation window is at least 889 monthly observations (over 74 years of stationary data).

M3 · Trace of weight covariance

Calculate the total trace of the weight covariance matrix \(\tr(\Cov(\hat{w}))\) for an \(N\)-asset universe under the assumptions of Theorem 2.1.

Solution

\(\Cov(\hat{w}) = \frac{1}{\gamma^2 \sigma^2 T} P\), where \(P = I_N - \frac{1}{N} \iota \iota^\top\). The trace of the identity is \(N\), and \(\tr(\iota \iota^\top) = \iota^\top \iota = N\). Therefore, \(\tr(P) = N - \frac{1}{N}(N) = N - 1\). The total trace of the weight covariance matrix is \(\tr(\Cov(\hat{w})) = \frac{N - 1}{\gamma^2 \sigma^2 T}\).

P1 · Covariance between weights

Under Assumption A1 of Theorem 2.1, prove that the covariance between the sample weights of any two distinct assets \(i \ne j\) is strictly negative and equals \(\Cov(\hat{w}_i, \hat{w}_j) = -\frac{1}{N \gamma^2 \sigma^2 T}\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.1, \(\Cov(\hat{w}) = \frac{1}{\gamma^2 \sigma^2 T} (I_N - \frac{1}{N} \iota \iota^\top)\). For off-diagonal entries \(i \ne j\), the \((i, j)\) entry of \(I_N\) is 0, while the \((i, j)\) entry of \(\frac{1}{N} \iota \iota^\top\) is \(\frac{1}{N}\). Thus the \((i, j)\) covariance is \(\Cov(\hat{w}_i, \hat{w}_j) = \frac{1}{\gamma^2 \sigma^2 T} (0 - \frac{1}{N}) = -\frac{1}{N \gamma^2 \sigma^2 T}\). This negative covariance reflects the budget constraint: an upward error in asset \(i\) forces offsetting downward allocations across the remaining assets.

P2 · Zero-sum budget constraint preservation

Prove that \(\sum_{i=1}^N \Cov(\hat{w}_i, \hat{w}_j) = 0\) for every asset \(j \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), and explain its structural significance.

Solution

Summing across row \(j\) of \(\Cov(\hat{w})\): \(\sum_{i=1}^N \Cov(\hat{w}_i, \hat{w}_j) = \Var(\hat{w}_j) + \sum_{i \ne j} \Cov(\hat{w}_i, \hat{w}_j) = \frac{N - 1}{N \gamma^2 \sigma^2 T} + (N - 1) \left( -\frac{1}{N \gamma^2 \sigma^2 T} \right) = \frac{N - 1}{N \gamma^2 \sigma^2 T} - \frac{N - 1}{N \gamma^2 \sigma^2 T} = 0\). This holds because \(\sum_i w_i = 1\) is an exact deterministic constraint, so \(\Cov(\sum_i \hat{w}_i, \hat{w}_j) = \Cov(1, \hat{w}_j) = 0\).

P3 · Resampling convergence to the prior

Prove that as the number of bootstrap resamples \(M \to \infty\) with Gaussian draws centered at \((\hat{\mu}, S)\), the sample average \(\bar{w}_{\text{resampled}}\) converges almost surely to the plug-in optimizer solution for unconstrained quadratic programs.

Solution

For an unconstrained mean-variance program with fixed covariance \(S\), the weight mapping \(w(\tilde{\mu}) = w_{\text{GMV}} + \frac{1}{\gamma} M \tilde{\mu}\) is an affine linear operator of the resampled return vector \(\tilde{\mu}\). Under parametric Gaussian resampling, \(\tilde{\mu} \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}, S / T)\). Taking expectations: \(\E[w(\tilde{\mu})] = w_{\text{GMV}} + \frac{1}{\gamma} M \E[\tilde{\mu}] = w_{\text{GMV}} + \frac{1}{\gamma} M \hat{\mu} = w(\hat{\mu})\). By the strong law of large numbers, \(\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M w(\tilde{\mu}^{(m)}) \xrightarrow{a.s.} \E[w(\tilde{\mu})] = w(\hat{\mu})\). Thus, for unconstrained linear-quadratic problems with fixed covariance, resampling converges exactly back to the original error-maximizing plug-in weights.

E1 · Extreme value order statistics

Let \(Z_1, \dots, Z_N\) be i.i.d. standard normal random variables representing estimation noise across \(N = 50\) assets. Estimate the expected maximum error \(\E[\max_i Z_i]\) and explain why the optimizer concentrates capital in this asset.

Solution

By extreme value theory for Gaussian variables, the expected maximum satisfies \(\E[\max_{1 \le i \le N} Z_i] \approx \sqrt{2 \ln N} - \frac{\ln(\ln N) + \ln(4\pi)}{2 \sqrt{2 \ln N}}\). For \(N = 50\), \(\sqrt{2 \ln 50} = \sqrt{2 \times 3.912} = \sqrt{7.824} \approx 2.797\). The expected maximum is approximately 2.25 standard errors above the true mean. Because the optimizer sorts assets by sample return, it selects this \(+2.25\sigma\) outlier as its highest-conviction holding.

E2 · Noise portfolio leverage

An optimizer forms a portfolio that exploits an estimated correlation of \(\hat{\rho}_{12} = 0.98\) between two assets with equal sample variance \(s^2 = 0.04\). Compute the variance of the spread portfolio \(w = (1, -1)^\top\) and contrast it with true correlation \(\rho_{12} = 0.90\).

Solution

The estimated variance of the spread portfolio is \(\hat{\sigma}_{\text{spread}}^2 = s^2 + s^2 - 2 \hat{\rho}_{12} s^2 = 2(0.04)(1 - 0.98) = 0.08(0.02) = 0.0016\) (\(\hat{\sigma} = 0.04\)). Under the true correlation \(\rho_{12} = 0.90\), the true variance is \(\sigma_{\text{spread}}^2 = 2(0.04)(1 - 0.90) = 0.08(0.10) = 0.0080\) (\(\sigma = 0.0894\)). The true risk is \(\sqrt{0.0080 / 0.0016} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24\) times higher than estimated in-sample.

E3 · Out-of-sample turnover drag

In Lab 2, the standard deviation of weights across resamples is approximately 0.35 per asset. If an asset manager rebalances monthly and the weights randomly drift by their sampling error standard deviation, estimate the annual turnover.

Solution

With 4 assets each experiencing expected monthly absolute adjustments of \(\E[|\Delta w_i|] \approx \sqrt{2/\pi} \sigma_w \approx 0.7979(0.35) \approx 0.28\), the total monthly one-way turnover is \(\tau \approx 4 \times 0.28 = 1.12\) (112% monthly turnover). Over 12 months, annual turnover exceeds 1,300%, creating an insurmountable transaction fee drag.

R1 · Bayesian versus resampled frontiers

Contrast Michaud's resampled frontier with the Bayesian predictive distribution of Zellner and Chetty (1965). Why is Bayesian updating decision-theoretically superior?

Solution

Zellner and Chetty (1965) maximize expected utility under the predictive density \(p(r_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{D}_T) = \int p(r_{t+1} \mid \theta) p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}_T) d\theta\). Parameter uncertainty inflates the predictive covariance: \(\Var(r_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{D}_T) = \Sigma + \Var(\hat{\mu} \mid \mathcal{D}_T) = \Sigma (1 + 1/T)\). This rigorously downweights risky assets according to parameter uncertainty. In contrast, Michaud averages nonlinear optimization outputs across parameter draws, violating von Neumann-Morgenstern expected utility axioms.

R2 · Random matrix theory and eigenvalue clipping

How does the Marcenko-Pastur distribution from Random Matrix Theory explain optimizer error maximization in large asset universes?

Solution

For an empirical covariance matrix computed from pure Gaussian noise with ratio \(c = N / T\), Marcenko-Pastur proves that sample eigenvalues disperse across the interval \([(1 - \sqrt{c})^2, (1 + \sqrt{c})^2]\). When \(c \approx 1\), the smallest sample eigenvalue approaches zero. The optimizer mistakenly treats the corresponding eigenvector as a riskless arbitrage opportunity, allocating extreme leverage to pure noise.

R3 · Regularization duality

Show that adding an \(L_2\) norm penalty \(\frac{\lambda}{2} \|w\|_2^2\) to the mean-variance objective is mathematically equivalent to optimizing on a ridge-shrunk covariance matrix \(\Sigma + \frac{\lambda}{\gamma} I_N\).

Solution

The penalized objective is \(\min_{w: \iota^\top w = 1} \frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top \Sigma w - \mu^\top w + \frac{\lambda}{2} w^\top w\). Factoring out \(\frac{\gamma}{2}\), the quadratic terms combine as \(\frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top (\Sigma + \frac{\lambda}{\gamma} I_N) w - \mu^\top w\). This is formally identical to the standard mean-variance problem where the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) is replaced by the Tikhonov-regularized matrix \(\Sigma_\lambda = \Sigma + (\lambda / \gamma) I_N\), proving exact mathematical duality.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. Why did Michaud (1989) characterize the Markowitz mean-variance algorithm as an 'error maximizer'?

    Options

    By solving for maximum risk-adjusted return, the optimizer treats upward statistical noise as genuine alpha, creating extreme weights in mis-estimated assets.

  2. Under Theorem 2.1, how does the theoretical variance of an asset's sample weight \(\Var(\hat{w}_i)\) scale with the sample window length \(T\)?

    Options

    Theorem 2.1 proves \(\Var(\hat{w}_i) = \frac{N - 1}{N \gamma^2 \sigma^2 T}\), which is proportional to \(1 / T\).

  3. For \(N = 5\) assets with \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(\gamma = 3\), and \(T = 60\), what is the theoretical standard deviation of an asset's sample weight (expressed as a decimal)?

    \(\text{SD}(\hat{w}_i) = \sqrt{4 / (5 \times 9 \times 0.04 \times 60)} = \sqrt{4 / 108} = \sqrt{1/27} \approx 0.1925\).

  4. What does Michaud's resampled frontier heuristic actually compute?

    Options

    Resampling draws \(M\) synthetic samples from \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}, S)\) and averages the resulting weight vectors \(\bar{w} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \hat{w}^{(m)}\).

  5. Why does parametric resampling fail to provide a formal decision-theoretic improvement in unconstrained problems with fixed covariance?

    Options

    By linearity of expectation, \(\E[w(\tilde{\mu})] = w(\E[\tilde{\mu}]) = w(\hat{\mu})\). Without non-linear constraints, resampling averages back to the identical point estimate.

  6. What is the off-diagonal covariance between the sample weights of any two distinct assets \(i \ne j\) in Theorem 2.1?

    Options

    Because \(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i = 1\), increasing the weight in one asset forces an offsetting reduction in others, inducing negative covariance among weights.

  7. What phenomenon explains why the best-performing in-sample assets systematically underperform out of sample?

    Options

    Assets selected for extreme sample performance have large positive sampling errors that mean-revert to zero in subsequent periods.

  8. If the largest eigenvalue of a sample covariance matrix is 0.16 and the smallest is 0.001, what is its condition number \(\kappa(S)\)?

    \(\kappa(S) = \lambda_{\max} / \lambda_{\min} = 0.16 / 0.001 = 160\).
