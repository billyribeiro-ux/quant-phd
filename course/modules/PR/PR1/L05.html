Standard mean-variance optimization produces extreme, unstable portfolios when supplied with raw historical return estimates. The Black-Litterman model resolves this pathology by anchoring portfolio construction in a market equilibrium prior obtained via reverse optimization. By combining this neutral benchmark with investor views through a rigorous Bayesian updating formula, the model generates stable, intuitive expected returns that gracefully tilt away from market weights in direct proportion to view confidence.

1Equilibrium Prior via Reverse Optimization

In classical mean-variance optimization, investors face an acute information deficit: estimating \(N\) expected returns requires vast statistical samples that the non-stationary market cannot provide. To establish a sensible baseline, Black and Litterman (1992) introduced reverse optimization: rather than asking what portfolio weights optimize a set of estimated means, one asks what expected return vector would make the observed market capitalization portfolio optimal.

Definition 5.1 (Implied Equilibrium Excess Returns)

Let \(w_{\mathrm{mkt}} \in \R^N\) denote the vector of market capitalization weights satisfying \(\iota^\top w_{\mathrm{mkt}} = 1\), \(S \in \R^{N \times N}\) be the asset covariance matrix, and \(\gamma > 0\) represent the market-wide aggregate risk aversion coefficient. The implied equilibrium excess return vector \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}} \in \R^N\) is defined by reverse optimization of the unconstrained quadratic program:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} = \gamma S w_{\mathrm{mkt}} \end{aligned} \]

The vector \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\) acts as an equilibrium neutral prior. If an investor holds no private views, using \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\) inside an unconstrained mean-variance optimizer recovers the market portfolio \(w_{\mathrm{mkt}}\) exactly: \(w^* = \frac{1}{\gamma} S^{-1} \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} = w_{\mathrm{mkt}}\). This eliminates the arbitrary corner solutions that plague raw sample means.

2Investor Views and Observation Structure

Investors possess private insights or quantitative forecasts regarding only a small subset of assets or relative spreads. The Black–Litterman model formalizes these forecasts as a linear system of \(K\) views with additive Gaussian uncertainty.

Definition 5.2 (View Matrix and Error Structure)

A set of \(K\) investor views is specified by the linear observation model:

\[ \begin{aligned} q = P \mu + \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Omega) \end{aligned} \]

where \(P \in \R^{K \times N}\) is the pick matrix, \(q \in \R^K\) is the view vector, and \(\Omega \in \R^{K \times K}\) is a diagonal covariance matrix representing view uncertainty.

  • Relative views: Each row of \(P\) sums to zero (\(P_k \iota = 0\)), expressing an expected return spread between two assets or sectors (for instance, Asset A outperforming Asset B by 2%).
  • Absolute views: The row entries sum to one (\(P_k \iota = 1\)), expressing a nominal performance expectation for an asset or basket.
  • Diagonal view uncertainty: \(\Omega = \diag(\omega_1, \dots, \omega_K)\), where \(\omega_k > 0\) reflects the variance of the forecast error on view \(k\).

3Bayesian Updating: Precision and GLS Forms

The core mathematical engine of Black-Litterman is conjugate Bayesian inference on a Gaussian mean vector with a known covariance structure.

Theorem 5.1 (Black–Litterman Posterior Mean in Precision Form)

Assumptions:

  • A1 (Gaussian Prior): The true unobserved expected return vector \(\mu \in \R^N\) is distributed according to \(\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}, \tau S)\), where \(\tau > 0\) is a scalar scaling prior uncertainty and \(S \in \R^{N \times N}\) is positive definite.
  • A2 (Gaussian Views): The investor provides \(K \ge 1\) linear views \(q = P \mu + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Omega)\), where \(\Omega \in \R^{K \times K}\) is diagonal and strictly positive definite.
  • A3 (Known Covariance): The covariance matrix \(S\) is treated as fixed and known.

Conclusion:

The conditional posterior distribution of \(\mu\) given views \(q\) is multivariate normal \(\mu \mid q \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}}, \Sigma_{\mathrm{BL}})\), where the posterior mean in precision form is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{\mathrm{BL}} = \left[ (\tau S)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P \right]^{-1} \left[ (\tau S)^{-1} \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} q \right] \end{aligned} \]

Furthermore, because \((\tau S)^{-1}\) is strictly positive definite and \(P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\) is positive semidefinite, the combined precision matrix \(M = (\tau S)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\) is strictly positive definite and always invertible, even when \(K < N\) and \(P\) is rank-deficient.

Proof of Theorem 5.1

Under conjugate Gaussian updating, the prior density is \(p(\mu) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mu - \mu_{\mathrm{eq}})^\top (\tau S)^{-1} (\mu - \mu_{\mathrm{eq}})\right)\) and the conditional likelihood is \(p(q \mid \mu) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(q - P\mu)^\top \Omega^{-1} (q - P\mu)\right)\). Multiplying prior and likelihood yields the posterior kernel \(\exp(-J(\mu)/2)\), where:

\[ \begin{aligned} J(\mu) &= (\mu - \mu_{\mathrm{eq}})^\top (\tau S)^{-1} (\mu - \mu_{\mathrm{eq}}) \\ &\quad + (q - P\mu)^\top \Omega^{-1} (q - P\mu) \end{aligned} \]

Expanding and grouping quadratic and linear terms in \(\mu\):

\[ \begin{aligned} J(\mu) &= \mu^\top \left[ (\tau S)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P \right] \mu \\ &\quad - 2 \mu^\top \left[ (\tau S)^{-1} \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} q \right] + C \end{aligned} \]

where \(C\) is independent of \(\mu\). Completing the square identifies the posterior precision \(M = (\tau S)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\) and the posterior mean \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}} = M^{-1} \left[ (\tau S)^{-1} \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} q \right]\). Since \(\tau > 0\) and \(S \succ 0\), \((\tau S)^{-1} \succ 0\). For any non-zero \(x \in \R^N\), \(x^\top M x = x^\top (\tau S)^{-1} x + (Px)^\top \Omega^{-1} (Px) > 0\), establishing invertibility of \(M\) without requiring \(P\) to have full rank.

Proposition 5.1 (Generalized Least Squares / Woodbury Form)

Applying the Woodbury matrix inversion identity to the precision form yields the algebraically equivalent Theil / GLS representation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{\mathrm{BL}} = \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} + \tau S P^\top \left( P \tau S P^\top + \Omega \right)^{-1} \left( q - P \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} \right) \end{aligned} \]

This formulation requires inverting only a \(K \times K\) matrix \((P \tau S P^\top + \Omega)\) rather than an \(N \times N\) matrix, making it computationally advantageous when the number of views \(K\) is much smaller than the asset universe \(N\).

Remark 5.1 (Allocator Implementation)

In portfolio allocation, the practitioner solves the standard long-only mean-variance quadratic program using the Black-Litterman posterior mean \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}}\) as the linear objective coefficient and the sample (or shrunk) covariance matrix \(S\) as the quadratic risk metric:

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{w} \quad \frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top S w - \mu_{\mathrm{BL}}^\top w \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \iota^\top w = 1, \; w \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

4Inline Lab: Precision Form vs Woodbury Form

The following experiment verifies the algebraic equivalence between the precision-form posterior mean and the Woodbury generalized least squares formulation on an illustrative two-asset investment universe in exact Fraction arithmetic.

Lab 5 · Equivalence of precision and Woodbury Black-Litterman formulations

Under exact rational arithmetic, the posterior expected return vector computed via the N x N precision form matches the vector computed via the K x K Woodbury formulation identically, with both forms shifting expected returns toward the active investor view.

"""Lab 5: Black-Litterman model: precision form vs generalized least squares form.

Illustrative inputs in exact Fraction arithmetic.
Computes the Black-Litterman posterior expected return vector using both the
precision representation and the Theil / Woodbury generalized least squares
representation, verifying algebraic equivalence to machine precision.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    # 2 assets, 1 relative view
    s_cov = [
        [Fraction(4, 100), Fraction(1, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(9, 100)],
    ]
    w_mkt = [Fraction(6, 10), Fraction(4, 10)]
    gamma = Fraction(3)
    tau = Fraction(5, 100)  # tau = 0.05

    # 1. Equilibrium risk premiums: mu_eq = gamma * S * w_mkt
    mu_eq = [gamma * sum(s_cov[i][j] * w_mkt[j] for j in range(2)) for i in range(2)]

    # Investor view: Asset 0 will outperform Asset 1 by 2% (0.02)
    # P = [1, -1], q = [0.02], Omega = [0.0001]
    p_mat = [[Fraction(1), Fraction(-1)]]
    q_vec = [Fraction(2, 100)]
    omega_val = Fraction(1, 10000)

    # Invert S
    det_s = s_cov[0][0] * s_cov[1][1] - s_cov[0][1] * s_cov[1][0]
    s_inv = [
        [s_cov[1][1] / det_s, -s_cov[0][1] / det_s],
        [-s_cov[1][0] / det_s, s_cov[0][0] / det_s],
    ]

    # 2. Precision form:
    # M = (tau S)^-1 + P' Omega^-1 P
    # v = (tau S)^-1 mu_eq + P' Omega^-1 q
    tau_s_inv = [[s_inv[i][j] / tau for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]
    p_omega_inv_p = [[p_mat[0][i] * p_mat[0][j] / omega_val for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]
    m_mat = [[tau_s_inv[i][j] + p_omega_inv_p[i][j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]

    v_prior = [sum(tau_s_inv[i][j] * mu_eq[j] for j in range(2)) for i in range(2)]
    v_view = [p_mat[0][i] * q_vec[0] / omega_val for i in range(2)]
    v_vec = [v_prior[i] + v_view[i] for i in range(2)]

    det_m = m_mat[0][0] * m_mat[1][1] - m_mat[0][1] * m_mat[1][0]
    mu_bl_prec = [
        (m_mat[1][1] * v_vec[0] - m_mat[0][1] * v_vec[1]) / det_m,
        (-m_mat[1][0] * v_vec[0] + m_mat[0][0] * v_vec[1]) / det_m,
    ]

    # 3. Generalized least squares / Woodbury form:
    # mu_bl = mu_eq + tau S P' (P tau S P' + Omega)^-1 (q - P mu_eq)
    tau_s = [[tau * s_cov[i][j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]
    p_tau_s_pt = sum(p_mat[0][i] * sum(tau_s[i][j] * p_mat[0][j] for j in range(2)) for i in range(2))
    denom = p_tau_s_pt + omega_val
    p_mu_eq = sum(p_mat[0][i] * mu_eq[i] for i in range(2))
    innov = q_vec[0] - p_mu_eq
    gain = [sum(tau_s[i][j] * p_mat[0][j] for j in range(2)) / denom for i in range(2)]
    mu_bl_alt = [mu_eq[i] + gain[i] * innov for i in range(2)]

    print(
        "Prior equilibrium mean mu_eq:",
        [round(float(x), 6) for x in mu_eq],
    )
    print(
        "Precision-form posterior mean:",
        [round(float(x), 6) for x in mu_bl_prec],
    )
    print(
        "Woodbury-form posterior mean:",
        [round(float(x), 6) for x in mu_bl_alt],
    )
    print("Exact algebraic equivalence verified:", mu_bl_prec == mu_bl_alt)


main()
Prior equilibrium mean mu_eq: [0.084, 0.126]
Precision-form posterior mean: [0.100607, 0.081714]
Woodbury-form posterior mean: [0.100607, 0.081714]
Exact algebraic equivalence verified: True

The market equilibrium prior yields \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}} = [0.084, 0.126]\). The investor expresses a single relative view (\(K = 1\)) that Asset 0 will outperform Asset 1 by 2% (\(q = [0.02]\)) with confidence variance \(\Omega = [0.0001]\). Both the precision representation and the Woodbury representation evaluate to identical posterior expected return vectors: \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}} \approx [0.100607, 0.081714]\). The expected return on Asset 0 is lifted from 0.084 to 0.100607 while Asset 1 drops from 0.126 to 0.081714, directly reflecting the relative view without introducing unconstrained extreme weights.

5Where the canon breaks

While Black-Litterman provides a major structural improvement over unconstrained mean-variance optimization, several critical assumptions limit its real-world performance:

  • Gaussian Normality Assumption: The conjugate update relies strictly on multivariate normality of both asset returns and forecast errors. Heavy-tailed asset returns and non-linear payoff structures violate these conjugate conditions.
  • Heuristic Calibration of \(\tau\) and \(\Omega\): Economic theory provides little direct guidance for choosing the scalar \(\tau\). Practitioners routinely choose arbitrary heuristics (e.g. \(\tau \in [0.01, 0.1]\)), and diagonalizing \(\Omega\) ignores correlations among multi-asset analyst views.
  • Conditioning on Fixed Sample Covariance: The model assumes the true covariance \(S\) is known with certainty (Assumption A3). In practice, \(S\) is itself estimated with substantial finite-sample noise, propagating estimation error into the equilibrium prior \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}} = \gamma S w_{\mathrm{mkt}}\).

6Misconceptions

  1. The Black-Litterman model replaces the mean-variance optimization framework.

    Black-Litterman is an input generation technique, not an alternative optimizer. It generates a regularized vector of expected returns \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}}\) via Bayesian updating, which is then fed directly into standard mean-variance quadratic programming.

  2. The view matrix \(P\) must be full rank (\(K = N\)) to invert the posterior equations.

    The prior precision matrix \((\tau S)^{-1}\) is strictly positive definite. Therefore, the posterior precision \((\tau S)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\) is invertible for any number of views \(K \ge 0\), including underdetermined systems where \(K \ll N\).

  3. Reverse optimization reveals the objective, true future expected returns of capital market assets.

    Reverse optimization computes the expected returns that would make the market capitalization portfolio mean-variance optimal under the CAPM equilibrium. It serves as a neutral starting anchor to discipline forecasts, not an infallible forecast of future cash flows.

7Problem set

M1 · Equilibrium return calculation

An economy has two assets with covariance \(S = \begin{pmatrix} 0.04 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.09 \end{pmatrix}\), market weights \(w_{\mathrm{mkt}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix}\), and risk aversion \(\gamma = 2\). Compute the equilibrium prior excess return vector \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

Solution

Using the reverse optimization formula \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}} = \gamma S w_{\mathrm{mkt}}\), we compute \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}, 1} = 2 \times 0.04 \times 0.5 = 0.04\) (4.0%) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}, 2} = 2 \times 0.09 \times 0.5 = 0.09\) (9.0%). Thus \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.04 \\ 0.09 \end{pmatrix}\).

M2 · View specification matrix

Suppose an investor expresses a view that Asset 1 will yield an excess return of 6%, while holding no view on Asset 2. Express the pick matrix \(P\) and view vector \(q\) for this single view.

Solution

The single absolute view on Asset 1 corresponds to a \(1 \times 2\) row vector \(P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and scalar view magnitude \(q = \begin{pmatrix} 0.06 \end{pmatrix}\).

M3 · Innovation variance denominator

If the prior uncertainty scalar is set to \(\tau = 0.05\) and the view error variance is \(\omega = 0.002\), compute the scalar innovation denominator \(P (\tau S) P^\top + \Omega\) for the single-asset view where \(S_{11} = 0.04\).

Solution

We evaluate \(P (\tau S) P^\top = \tau S_{11} = 0.05 \times 0.04 = 0.002\). Adding \(\Omega = 0.002\) gives the total variance \(0.002 + 0.002 = 0.004\).

P1 · Zero views convergence

Prove that if an investor expresses zero views (\(K = 0\) or \(P = \mathbf{0}\)), the Black-Litterman posterior mean \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}}\) simplifies exactly to the equilibrium prior \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

Solution

In the precision form, setting \(P = \mathbf{0}\) leaves \(M = (\tau S)^{-1} + \mathbf{0} = (\tau S)^{-1}\). The linear vector becomes \((\tau S)^{-1} \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} + \mathbf{0}\). Premultiplying by \(M^{-1} = \tau S\) yields \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}} = (\tau S) (\tau S)^{-1} \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} = \mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

P2 · Positive definiteness of posterior precision

Show that the posterior precision matrix \(M = (\tau S)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\) is symmetric and positive definite whenever \(\tau > 0\), \(S \succ 0\), and \(\Omega \succ 0\).

Solution

Symmetry follows because \((\tau S)^{-1}\) is symmetric and \((P^\top \Omega^{-1} P)^\top = P^\top \Omega^{-\top} P = P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\). For any \(x \neq 0\), \(x^\top (\tau S)^{-1} x > 0\) by positive definiteness of \(S\), and \(x^\top P^\top \Omega^{-1} P x = (Px)^\top \Omega^{-1} (Px) \ge 0\). The sum of a positive definite matrix and a positive semidefinite matrix is strictly positive definite.

P3 · Infinite view uncertainty limit

Prove that when view variance approaches infinity (\(\Omega \to \infty\)), the Woodbury form converges to \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}} \to \mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

Solution

In the Woodbury representation, the update term is \(\tau S P^\top (P \tau S P^\top + \Omega)^{-1} (q - P \mu_{\mathrm{eq}})\). As \(\Omega \to \infty\), the inverse \((P \tau S P^\top + \Omega)^{-1} \to 0\). Consequently, the entire adjustment term vanishes, leaving \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}} = \mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

E1 · Turnover reduction mechanism

In a backtest of a 5-asset equity universe, an analyst sets \(\tau = 0.02\) and introduces a bullish view on Technology stocks. Explain why the resulting Black-Litterman portfolio weights exhibit significantly lower monthly turnover than unconstrained sample mean-variance weights.

Solution

Black-Litterman anchors returns in stable equilibrium market weights \(w_{\mathrm{mkt}}\). The Bayesian update acts as shrinkage toward consensus, dampening estimation noise and preventing wild portfolio reallocations between sample estimation windows.

E2 · Calibration of view uncertainty

Describe how an empirical practitioner can calibrate the diagonal view error covariance \(\Omega\) using historical tracking error of analyst recommendations.

Solution

Practitioners calculate the sample variance of past forecast errors \(e_k = q_{k, t} - P_k r_{t}\) over an out-of-sample calibration window. Setting \(\Omega_{kk} = \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(e_k)\) aligns view uncertainty with realized historical forecasting accuracy.

E3 · Avoiding look-ahead bias in backtests

When running a rolling backtest with Black-Litterman, explain what information may be used to compute \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}, t}\) without creating look-ahead bias.

Solution

The practitioner must use market capitalization weights \(w_{\mathrm{mkt}, t-1}\) observed at the close of period \(t-1\) and sample covariance \(S_{t-1}\) estimated strictly using returns prior to period \(t\), preventing contamination from future returns.

R1 · Covariance shrinkage integration

How does incorporating Ledoit-Wolf covariance shrinkage into the Black-Litterman prior covariance \(S\) alter the stability of the implied equilibrium returns \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\)?

Solution

Using a shrunk covariance \(\Sigma_\delta\) conditions the eigenvalues of \(S\), preventing extreme eigenvalues from excessively distorting \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}} = \gamma \Sigma_\delta w_{\mathrm{mkt}}\) and producing more balanced, realistic equilibrium expected return baselines.

R2 · Tau parameter sensitivity

Evaluate the theoretical consequences of setting \(\tau = 1\) versus \(\tau = 0.01\) in macroeconomic asset allocation.

Solution

Setting \(\tau = 1\) assigns equal weight to prior parameter uncertainty and sample return variance, allowing active views to dominate portfolio allocations. Setting \(\tau = 0.01\) reflects high confidence in market equilibrium, tightly constraining portfolio weights near market cap weights.

R3 · Correlated view errors

Discuss the limitations of assuming that investor forecast errors \(\varepsilon\) have a diagonal covariance matrix \(\Omega\).

Solution

A diagonal \(\Omega\) assumes forecast errors across different views are statistically independent. If an analyst expresses multiple views based on common macroeconomic factors, their errors will be correlated, leading to double-counting and overconfident positioning.

9Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the primary operational role of reverse optimization in the Black-Litterman model?

    Options

    Reverse optimization calculates the vector of expected excess returns that makes the observed market portfolio mean-variance optimal, serving as an equilibrium starting prior.

  2. If an investor expresses a view that Asset A will outperform Asset B by 3%, what is the sum of the elements in that row of the pick matrix \(P\)?

    Options

    Relative views compare the performance of two assets or baskets, meaning the positive and negative weights in that row of \(P\) sum to zero.

  3. Why is the combined posterior precision matrix \(M = (\tau S)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\) invertible even when the number of views \(K\) is much smaller than the number of assets \(N\)?

    Options

    Since \(S\) is positive definite and \(\tau > 0\), the prior precision \((\tau S)^{-1}\) is strictly positive definite. Adding a positive semidefinite matrix \(P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\) preserves strict positive definiteness.

  4. What happens to the Black-Litterman posterior mean \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}}\) as the investor's view uncertainty approaches infinity (\(\Omega \to \infty\))?

    Options

    As view uncertainty grows infinitely large, \(\Omega^{-1} \to 0\), causing the view information to vanish and the posterior mean to revert entirely to the market equilibrium prior \(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

  5. What is the dimension of the matrix that must be inverted in the Woodbury / GLS formulation of the Black-Litterman model?

    Options

    The Woodbury form requires inverting the innovation covariance \((P \tau S P^\top + \Omega)\), which has dimension \(K \times K\), where \(K\) is the number of views.

  6. In the Black-Litterman model, what does the scalar hyperparameter \(\tau\) represent?

    Options

    The parameter \(\tau\) scales the covariance of the prior distribution \(\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}, \tau S)\), parameterizing prior uncertainty about the true mean vector.

  7. If an unconstrained investor has zero active views (\(K = 0\)) and optimizes using \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}} = \mu_{\mathrm{eq}}\) and risk aversion \(\gamma\), what portfolio weights are obtained?

    Options

    By construction of reverse optimization, \(w^* = \frac{1}{\gamma} S^{-1} \mu_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{1}{\gamma} S^{-1} (\gamma S w_{\mathrm{mkt}}) = w_{\mathrm{mkt}}\).

  8. Which of the following is an accurate description of how the Black-Litterman posterior mean \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}}\) is used in practical allocation?

    Options

    The vector \(\mu_{\mathrm{BL}}\) enters the linear term of the quadratic programming objective \(\frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top S w - \mu_{\mathrm{BL}}^\top w\) subject to portfolio constraints.
