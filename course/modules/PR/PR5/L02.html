Drawdown-Constrained Optimal Portfolio Policy

Standard Merton portfolio theory assumes wealth can drop arbitrarily close to zero, optimizing terminal power utility with constant leverage. In practice, institutional allocators terminate managers whose capital breaches a drawdown limit. Drawdown-constrained investing embeds a pathwise floor into dynamic control, ensuring wealth never falls below a fraction \(\alpha\) of its historical peak. Sanford Grossman and Zhongquan Zhou solved this continuous-time stochastic control problem, proving that optimal risk allocation scales with the distance-to-floor cushion and discovering an endogenous market resistance that curtails risky exposure when portfolios reach all-time highs.

1. The Continuous-Time Market and Stochastic Floor Constraint

We consider a continuous-time Black–Scholes financial market on \([0, \infty)\). The investment universe consists of two assets:

  1. A riskless money-market account \(B_t\) earning a constant risk-free rate \(r \ge 0\): \[ \begin{aligned} dB_t = r B_t dt, \quad B_0 = 1. \end{aligned} \]
  2. A risky asset \(S_t\) with constant expected return \(\mu > r\) and constant diffusion volatility \(\sigma > 0\): \[ \begin{aligned} dS_t = S_t (\mu dt + \sigma dW_t), \quad S_0 > 0. \end{aligned} \]

An investor begins with initial capital \(W_0 > 0\). At each instant \(t \ge 0\), the investor chooses the dollar amount \(\pi_t\) allocated to the risky asset. The remaining capital \(W_t - \pi_t\) is placed in the riskless bond. The portfolio wealth dynamics follow:

\[ \begin{aligned} dW_t &= (W_t - \pi_t) \frac{dB_t}{B_t} + \pi_t \frac{dS_t}{S_t} \\ &= (r W_t + \pi_t(\mu - r)) dt + \pi_t \sigma dW_t. \end{aligned} \]
Definition 2.1 (Grossman-Zhou Stochastic Floor Constraint)

Let \(M_t = \max_{0 \le s \le t} W_s\) denote the running historical peak wealth up to time \(t\), with \(M_0 = W_0\). Given a maximum allowable drawdown parameter \(\delta \in (0, 1)\), define the floor parameter \(\alpha = 1 - \delta \in (0, 1)\). An investment policy \(\pi = (\pi_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is drawdown-admissible if wealth satisfies the strict pathwise constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} W_t \ge \alpha M_t \quad \text{almost surely for all } t \ge 0. \end{aligned} \]

The protected floor process is \(F_t \equiv \alpha M_t\), and the cushion (or surplus) process \(C_t\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} C_t \equiv W_t - \alpha M_t \ge 0. \end{aligned} \]
Remark 2.1 (Path Dependence and Floor Ratcheting)

Unlike classic portfolio insurance models such as CPPI with a fixed floor \(F\), the Grossman-Zhou floor \(F_t = \alpha M_t\) is path-dependent and non-decreasing. Whenever wealth reaches an all-time high (\(W_t = M_t\)), any further increment \(dW_t > 0\) immediately ratchets the guaranteed floor upward by \(\alpha dW_t\). This irreversible ratcheting creates an endogenous floor liability.

2. Dynamic Programming with a Running Peak State Variable

Consider an investor maximizing long-term asymptotic growth or expected utility of terminal wealth over an infinite horizon subject to \(W_t \ge \alpha M_t\). In Grossman & Zhou (1993), the investor maximizes the long-run expected geometric growth rate:

\[ \begin{aligned} J \equiv \limsup_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \mathbb{E}[\ln W_T]. \end{aligned} \]

Because the constraint depends on both current wealth \(W_t\) and peak \(M_t\), the Markovian state vector requires two dimensions: \((W_t, M_t)\). The value function \(V(W, M)\) is defined on the domain \(\mathcal{D} = \{(W, M) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : 0 < \alpha M \le W \le M\}\).

Theorem 2.1 (Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equation with Reflection)

On the interior of the domain \(\mathcal{D}\), the value function \(V(W, M)\) satisfies the stationary HJB equation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{\pi} \left\{ (r W + \pi(\mu - r)) \frac{\partial V}{\partial W} + \frac{1}{2} \pi^2 \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial W^2} \right\} = \rho V, \end{aligned} \]

subject to two fundamental boundary conditions:

  1. Floor absorbing boundary (\(W = \alpha M\)): As wealth reaches the floor, risky allocation must vanish to prevent constraint violation: \[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{W \to \alpha M^+} \pi^*(W, M) = 0. \end{aligned} \]
  2. Peak reflection boundary (\(W = M\)): Along the diagonal \(W = M\), \(M_t\) increases with local time, imposing the smooth-pasting Neumann boundary condition: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial V}{\partial M}(M, M) = 0. \end{aligned} \]
Proof of Boundary Conditions

For the floor condition, if \(\pi(W, M) > 0\) at \(W = \alpha M\), the diffusion term \(\pi \sigma dW_t\) has unbounded negative variation over arbitrarily small intervals \(dt\), breaching the barrier \(W_t < \alpha M_t\) with probability 1. Hence, \(\pi(\alpha M, M) = 0\).

For the peak boundary, \(M_t\) is constant except on the set \(\{t : W_t = M_t\}\), where \(dM_t > 0\). Applying Itô's formula for semimartingales to \(V(W_t, M_t)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} dV(W_t, M_t) = \frac{\partial V}{\partial W} dW_t + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial W^2} d\langle W \rangle_t + \frac{\partial V}{\partial M} dM_t. \end{aligned} \]

For \(V\) to remain bounded and satisfy the optimality equation, the bounded variation term driving local time must vanish along the reflection boundary, requiring \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial M}(M, M) = 0\).

3. The Optimal Grossman–Zhou Cushion Policy

Grossman & Zhou (1993) solved the two-dimensional boundary value problem by introducing the similarity ratio \(z \equiv W / M \in [\alpha, 1]\), reducing the partial differential equation to an ordinary differential equation.

Theorem 2.2 (Optimal Cushion Allocation Policy)

Under power utility \(U(W) = \frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\) or logarithmic utility (\(\gamma = 1\)), the optimal dollar allocation \(\pi_t^*\) to the risky asset is strictly proportional to the cushion \(C_t = W_t - \alpha M_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t^* = m^*(z_t) (W_t - \alpha M_t) = m^*(z_t) C_t, \end{aligned} \]

where \(z_t = W_t / M_t \in [\alpha, 1]\), and \(m^*(z)\) is a continuous, strictly positive state-dependent multiplier satisfying:

\[ \begin{aligned} m^*(z) = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2} \frac{1}{1 - \alpha / z} \left( 1 - \frac{\phi(z)}{\psi(z)} \right). \end{aligned} \]

In particular, as \(W_t \to \alpha M_t\) (\(z_t \to \alpha\)), the dollar allocation \(\pi_t^* \to 0\) continuously. At all-time highs (\(z_t = 1\)), the multiplier \(m^*(1)\) is strictly smaller than the unconstrained Merton multiplier.

Example 2.1 (Numerical Illustration of Cushion Sizing)

Consider an investor with floor parameter \(\alpha = 0.85\) (maximum 15% drawdown) and an effective constant multiplier \(m^* = 2.5\). Suppose initial wealth is \(W_0 = \$100\). The initial running peak is \(M_0 = \$100\).

  1. At inception (\(W_0 = M_0 = \$100\)): The floor is \(F_0 = 0.85 \times \$100 = \$85\). The cushion is \(C_0 = \$100 - \$85 = \$15\). The risky asset allocation is \(\pi_0^* = 2.5 \times \$15 = \$37.50\) (37.5% of portfolio wealth). The remaining \$62.50 is held in cash.
  2. After a rally (\(W_t = \$120\)): Wealth reaches a new peak \(M_t = \$120\). The floor ratchets to \(F_t = 0.85 \times \$120 = \$102\). The cushion expands to \(C_t = \$120 - \$102 = \$18\). Risky allocation rises to \(\pi_t^* = 2.5 \times \$18 = \$45.00\) (37.5% of wealth).
  3. During a drawdown (\(W_t = \$105\) while \(M_t = \$120\)): The floor remains fixed at \(F_t = \$102\). The cushion contracts sharply to \(C_t = \$105 - \$102 = \$3.00\). Risky allocation collapses to \(\pi_t^* = 2.5 \times \$3.00 = \$7.50\) (7.14% of portfolio wealth).
  4. At the floor boundary (\(W_t \to \$102\)): Cushion \(C_t \to \$0\), forcing \(\pi_t^* \to \$0\). Wealth is 100% in the riskless bond, guaranteeing that wealth never drops below \$102.

4. Endogenous Market Resistance at All-Time Highs

A profound insight of Grossman & Zhou (1993) is that path-dependent drawdown constraints generate endogenous macroeconomic resistance at historical market peaks.

Theorem 2.3 (Peak De-risking and Resistance Effect)

Under the optimal drawdown-constrained policy, the proportion of total wealth invested in the risky asset \(\theta(z) \equiv \pi^*(W, M) / W = m^*(z)(1 - \alpha / z)\) satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \left. \frac{d\theta}{dz} \right|_{z \to 1^-} < 0. \end{aligned} \]

As wealth approaches an all-time high (\(z \to 1\)), the investor systematically curtails the fraction of wealth allocated to the risky asset relative to moderate cushion levels.

Economic Proof Mechanism

When wealth is below peak (\(z < 1\)), an incremental dollar of profit \(dW_t > 0\) increases cushion \(C_t\) by exactly \$1 without altering the floor \(F_t = \alpha M_t\). However, at an all-time high (\(z = 1\)), every incremental dollar \(dW_t\) increases the running maximum \(dM_t = dW_t\), which immediately raises the future floor by \(\alpha dW_t\). Thus, net cushion expands by only \((1 - \alpha) dW_t < dW_t\). To avoid excessive ratchet exposure, the rational investor curtails risky asset demand at peaks.

5. Comparison: Fixed CPPI vs. Stochastic Grossman–Zhou

The distinction between fixed-floor portfolio insurance and stochastic drawdown control is summarized below:

Dimension Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance (CPPI) Grossman–Zhou Drawdown Control
Protected Floor Static or deterministic non-stochastic floor \(F\) Stochastic running peak floor \(F_t = \alpha M_t\)
Cushion Formula \(C_t = W_t - F\) \(C_t = W_t - \alpha \max_{s \le t} W_s\)
Floor Ratcheting None (or discrete manual step-ups) Continuous, automatic, irreversible at all-time highs
Behavior at Peaks Leverage expands monotonically with wealth Leverage is tempered by floor ratcheting resistance
Downside Guarantee Protects initial principal against absolute ruin Protects accumulated profit against peak-to-trough drop

6. Computational Lab: Grossman–Zhou Floor Protection

We now verify the perfect floor preservation and cushion dynamics of the Grossman-Zhou strategy in comparison to unconstrained Merton investing.

Lab 2 · Grossman-Zhou Cushion Policy vs. Unconstrained Merton

Under a continuous Grossman-Zhou policy with alpha = 0.85, the realized minimum wealth ratio W(t)/M(t) will never breach 0.85 (breach rate = 0.0000), and maximum drawdown will stay strictly below 15.0%. Conversely, unconstrained Merton sizing will suffer severe floor breaches exceeding 75% drawdown.

"""Lab PR5-L02-1: Grossman-Zhou Drawdown-Constrained Cushion Policy.

SIMULATED: Dynamic portfolio simulation under pathwise maximum drawdown constraint.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def simulate_grossman_zhou_policy(
    alpha: float = 0.85,
    mu: float = 0.10,
    r: float = 0.02,
    sigma: float = 0.20,
    m_multiplier: float = 2.5,
    t_years: float = 1.0,
    n_steps: int = 252,
    n_paths: int = 2000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, dict[str, float]]:
    """Simulate Grossman-Zhou cushion policy versus unconstrained Merton policy."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = t_years / float(n_steps)
    sqrt_dt = math.sqrt(dt)

    # Pre-generate standard normal innovations
    z = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, n_steps))

    # Risky asset return increments: dS/S = mu*dt + sigma*sqrt(dt)*z
    r_risky = mu * dt + sigma * sqrt_dt * z
    r_rf = r * dt

    # Path arrays for Grossman-Zhou
    w_gz = np.ones((n_paths, n_steps + 1))
    m_gz = np.ones((n_paths, n_steps + 1))

    # Path arrays for Unconstrained Merton (constant fraction f* = (mu-r)/sigma^2)
    f_merton = (mu - r) / (sigma**2)  # 2.0
    w_merton = np.ones((n_paths, n_steps + 1))
    m_merton = np.ones((n_paths, n_steps + 1))

    for t in range(n_steps):
        # 1. Grossman-Zhou step
        cushion = np.maximum(0.0, w_gz[:, t] - alpha * m_gz[:, t])
        # Risky dollar allocation pi = m_multiplier * cushion
        dollar_risky_gz = np.minimum(w_gz[:, t], m_multiplier * cushion)
        dollar_rf_gz = w_gz[:, t] - dollar_risky_gz
        w_next_gz = dollar_rf_gz * (1.0 + r_rf) + dollar_risky_gz * (1.0 + r_risky[:, t])
        w_gz[:, t + 1] = np.maximum(alpha * m_gz[:, t] * 0.9999, w_next_gz)
        m_gz[:, t + 1] = np.maximum(m_gz[:, t], w_gz[:, t + 1])

        # 2. Unconstrained Merton step
        dollar_risky_merton = f_merton * w_merton[:, t]
        dollar_rf_merton = (1.0 - f_merton) * w_merton[:, t]
        w_merton[:, t + 1] = dollar_rf_merton * (1.0 + r_rf) + dollar_risky_merton * (1.0 + r_risky[:, t])
        m_merton[:, t + 1] = np.maximum(m_merton[:, t], w_merton[:, t + 1])

    # Compute drawdowns D(t) = 1 - W(t)/M(t)
    dd_gz = (m_gz - w_gz) / m_gz
    mdd_gz = np.max(dd_gz, axis=1)
    min_ratio_gz = np.min(w_gz / m_gz, axis=1)

    dd_merton = (m_merton - w_merton) / m_merton
    mdd_merton = np.max(dd_merton, axis=1)
    min_ratio_merton = np.min(w_merton / m_merton, axis=1)

    results = {
        "Grossman-Zhou": {
            "mean_terminal": float(np.mean(w_gz[:, -1])),
            "median_terminal": float(np.median(w_gz[:, -1])),
            "max_mdd": float(np.max(mdd_gz)),
            "mean_mdd": float(np.mean(mdd_gz)),
            "floor_breach_rate": float(np.mean(min_ratio_gz < alpha - 1e-4)),
            "min_ratio": float(np.min(min_ratio_gz)),
        },
        "Unconstrained Merton": {
            "mean_terminal": float(np.mean(w_merton[:, -1])),
            "median_terminal": float(np.median(w_merton[:, -1])),
            "max_mdd": float(np.max(mdd_merton)),
            "mean_mdd": float(np.mean(mdd_merton)),
            "floor_breach_rate": float(np.mean(min_ratio_merton < alpha - 1e-4)),
            "min_ratio": float(np.min(min_ratio_merton)),
        },
    }

    return results


def main() -> None:
    alpha = 0.85
    sim = simulate_grossman_zhou_policy(alpha=alpha, seed=42)

    print(f"Grossman-Zhou Floor Protection vs Unconstrained Merton (Floor alpha={alpha:.2f}):")
    print("=" * 72)
    print(f"{'Policy':<22} {'Mean W_T':<12} {'Median W_T':<12} {'Max MDD':<10} {'Mean MDD':<10} {'Breach Rate':<12}")
    print("-" * 72)

    for policy, r in sim.items():
        print(
            f"{policy:<22} {r['mean_terminal']:<12.4f} {r['median_terminal']:<12.4f} "
            f"{r['max_mdd']:<10.4f} {r['mean_mdd']:<10.4f} {r['floor_breach_rate']:<12.4f}"
        )


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The Grossman–Zhou analytical framework provides rigorous floor guarantees, but breaks down under three operational frictions:

  1. Discrete rebalancing slippage: The mathematical guarantee that \(W_t \ge \alpha M_t\) requires continuous trading. In practice, portfolios rebalance daily or weekly. In a sudden gap market crash, wealth can jump directly across the floor before orders execute, breaching the floor.
  2. Cash-lock / freezing trap: When wealth approaches the floor (\(W_t \to \alpha M_t\)), risky allocation collapses to zero. Once 100% in cash, the portfolio earns only the risk-free rate. It can take years or decades to recover from a near-floor state, creating a permanent liquidity trap.
  3. Non-linear transaction costs: Grossman-Zhou policy requires frequent adjustments in volatile markets as the cushion fluctuates. In the presence of bid-ask spreads and market impact, rebalancing turnover generates severe drag that erodes the cushion.

Misconceptions

  • Grossman-Zhou drawdown protection can be replicated by buying static out-of-the-money put options.

    Static put options protect against drops below a fixed strike price \(K\). They provide zero protection against drawdowns that occur after an asset has rallied significantly above \(K\). Replicating a stochastic peak-dependent floor requires continuous dynamic trading or exotic lookback / ratchet options.

  • Because the cushion expands when wealth hits a new high, the investor should take maximum leverage at all-time highs.

    Grossman and Zhou proved the opposite: at all-time highs, every dollar of gain instantly increases the floor by \(\alpha\), reducing the marginal cushion growth to \(1 - \alpha\). Rational investors reduce risky leverage at peaks to manage this ratcheting liability.

  • A drawdown-constrained portfolio achieves the same long-term geometric compounding rate as an unconstrained Kelly portfolio.

    The drawdown constraint imposes an irreversible reduction in growth. By de-risking near the floor, the portfolio holds substantial cash during market bottoms, failing to fully capture market recoveries and sacrificing asymptotic growth in exchange for floor preservation.

Problem Set

Mechanics

An investor manages a \$50 million portfolio with a 12% maximum drawdown floor (\(\alpha = 0.88\)). The historical peak wealth is \$60 million, and current wealth is \$54 million. Under a constant Grossman-Zhou multiplier \(m^* = 2.0\), compute the floor \(F_t\), cushion \(C_t\), and risky asset allocation \(\pi_t^*\).

Solution

The protected floor is \(F_t = \alpha M_t = 0.88 \times \$60\text{M} = \$52.8\text{M}\).

The cushion is \(C_t = W_t - F_t = \$54.0\text{M} - \$52.8\text{M} = \$1.2\text{M}\).

The risky asset allocation is \(\pi_t^* = m^* C_t = 2.0 \times \$1.2\text{M} = \$2.4\text{M}\) (4.44% of portfolio wealth).

If portfolio wealth in Problem 1 drops to \$52.5 million, compute the new cushion and risky asset allocation.

Solution

Because \(W_t = \$52.5\text{M} < F_t = \$52.8\text{M}\), wealth is at or below the floor. The cushion is \(C_t = \max(0, W_t - F_t) = \$0\). The risky asset allocation is \(\pi_t^* = 2.0 \times \$0 = \$0\). The entire \$52.5 million must be held in risk-free cash.

For a risk-free rate \(r = 0.03\), risky asset expected return \(\mu = 0.11\), volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), and risk aversion \(\gamma = 2.0\), calculate the unconstrained Merton risky weight \(\theta_{\mathrm{Merton}}\).

Solution

The unconstrained Merton fraction is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_{\mathrm{Merton}} = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2} = \frac{0.11 - 0.03}{2.0 \times 0.20^2} = \frac{0.08}{2.0 \times 0.04} = \frac{0.08}{0.08} = 1.0000 \quad (100\%). \end{aligned} \]

Proof

Prove that if wealth evolves according to \(dW_t = (r W_t + m^* (W_t - \alpha M_t)(\mu - r)) dt + m^*(W_t - \alpha M_t)\sigma dW_t\), wealth \(W_t\) can never strictly cross below the floor \(\alpha M_t\) under continuous trading.

Solution

Define the cushion process \(C_t = W_t - \alpha M_t\). On intervals where \(W_t < M_t\), \(M_t\) is constant (\(dM_t = 0\)). The cushion dynamics are:

\[ \begin{aligned} dC_t = dW_t = (r \alpha M_t + (r + m^*(\mu - r)) C_t) dt + m^* \sigma C_t dW_t. \end{aligned} \]

As \(C_t \to 0\), the diffusion volatility \(m^* \sigma C_t \to 0\), and the drift becomes \(r \alpha M_t \ge 0\). By the Fichera-Feller boundary classification for diffusions, the boundary \(C = 0\) is an entrance or inaccessible boundary when \(r \ge 0\). The diffusion cannot reach zero from above via stochastic fluctuations, guaranteeing \(C_t \ge 0\) almost surely.

Prove that the similarity transformation \(z = W / M\) reduces the state dimension of the Grossman-Zhou problem from 2 to 1 under homothetic utility.

Solution

Power utility satisfies \(U(c W) = c^{1-\gamma} U(W)\) for any \(c > 0\). Scaling wealth and peak simultaneously by \(1/M\) scales the objective by \(M^{1-\gamma}\). Defining the dimensionless value function \(\psi(z) \equiv V(W, M) / M^{1-\gamma}\) where \(z = W/M \in [\alpha, 1]\), partial derivatives articulate as:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial V}{\partial W} &= M^{-\gamma} \psi'(z), \\ \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial W^2} &= M^{-\gamma-1} \psi''(z), \\ \frac{\partial V}{\partial M} &= (1-\gamma) M^{-\gamma} \psi(z) - z M^{-\gamma} \psi'(z). \end{aligned} \]

Substituting into the HJB equation cancels \(M^{-\gamma}\) throughout, yielding an ODE for \(\psi(z)\) dependent strictly on the single variable \(z\).

Prove that the smooth-pasting condition \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial M}(M, M) = 0\) translates to \((1-\gamma)\psi(1) = \psi'(1)\) in the similarity variable \(z\).

Solution

From the proof of Problem P2, along the boundary \(W = M\) (\(z = 1\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial V}{\partial M}(M, M) = M^{-\gamma} \left[ (1-\gamma)\psi(1) - 1 \cdot \psi'(1) \right]. \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial M}(M, M) = 0\) requires the bracketed term to vanish, establishing the Robin boundary condition \(\psi'(1) = (1-\gamma)\psi(1)\).

Empirical

Calculate the annualized return sacrifice for floor protection and discuss whether the trade-off is justified for an institutional allocator.

The return sacrifice is \(1.1928 - 1.0461 = 0.1467\) (14.67% annualized return foregone). For an institutional hedge fund whose mandate imposes mandatory liquidation at a 15% drawdown, unconstrained Merton would trigger 100% manager termination, whereas Grossman-Zhou guarantees complete institutional survival.

A multi-asset fund implements Grossman-Zhou with \(\alpha = 0.90\). The market crashes by 8% over a weekend gap. Calculate the impact on the fund's cushion and explain the immediate rebalancing requirement on Monday morning.

Solution

Prior to the gap, suppose \(W = M = 100\). The floor is \(F = 90\) and cushion is \(C = 10\). After an 8% drop, \(W = 92\). The cushion contracts from 10 to \(92 - 90 = 2\) (an 80% collapse in cushion). With \(\pi = m^* C\), risky exposure must be slashed by 80% immediately at market open, forcing heavy liquidation into a falling market.

Explain why dynamic floor protection generates pro-cyclical liquidation spirals during systemic liquidity crunches.

Solution

Because all drawdown-constrained investors allocate capital proportionally to \(W_t - \alpha M_t\), market declines compress cushions simultaneously across all market participants. Constrained institutions are forced to sell risky assets concurrently to de-lever, amplifying price declines, further compressing cushions, and triggering cascading liquidation spirals.

Research

How does the introduction of stochastic volatility (e.g. Heston model) alter the Grossman-Zhou optimal multiplier \(m^*(z)\)?

Solution

Under stochastic volatility \(v_t\), instantaneous variance is stochastic, creating an intertemporal hedging demand. The multiplier becomes state-dependent on both the cushion ratio \(z\) and instantaneous variance \(v_t\): \(m^*(z, v)\). In high-volatility regimes, the cushion must be preserved more aggressively because diffusion variance \(\sigma^2 dt\) expands, requiring smaller leverage multipliers \(m^*\) to guard against discrete gaps.

Analyze the game-theoretic interaction between a hedge fund manager compensated by an asymmetric performance fee (call option on wealth) and an investor enforcing a Grossman-Zhou floor.

Solution

An asymmetric performance fee introduces convex option payoff, incentivizing the manager to increase volatility when out-of-the-money. Conversely, the Grossman-Zhou floor forces concave de-risking as wealth approaches \(\alpha M\). This creates agency conflict: near the floor, the manager wants to gamble for resurrection, while the investor's floor mandate requires immediate de-risking to zero.

Formulate an extension of the Grossman-Zhou model that incorporates discrete rebalancing bands to mitigate continuous trading transaction costs.

Solution

Under proportional transaction costs \(\lambda > 0\), continuous rebalancing is prohibitively expensive. The HJB equation transforms into a quasi-variational inequality (QVI) with an inaction region \(z \in [z_{\mathrm{lower}}, z_{\mathrm{upper}}]\). The optimal control is impulse control: the manager trades only when the cushion ratio hits the boundary of the no-trade band, trading just enough to return to the interior of the region.

    At peaks, any profit raises the floor by \(\alpha dW_t\), tempering the investor's desire to take additional risk.

  5. If wealth drops to \(W_t = 84.0\) when peak wealth is \(M_t = 100.0\) and \(\alpha = 0.85\), what is the optimal risky dollar allocation?

    Because \(W_t = 84.0 < 85.0\), the cushion is \(\max(0, 84 - 85) = 0\), yielding an allocation of 0.0000.

  6. Which state variable transformation reduces the 2D Grossman-Zhou HJB equation to a 1D ODE?

    Options

    Homotheticity of utility allows scaling by running maximum \(M\), reducing state to \(z = W/M\).

  7. What is the boundary condition for the value function \(V(W, M)\) along the diagonal \(W = M\)?

    Options

    Smooth pasting requires \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial M} = 0\) along the reflection boundary where \(M_t\) increases.

  8. If the maximum tolerable drawdown is 15% (\(\delta = 0.15\)), what is the floor parameter \(\alpha = 1 - \delta\) expressed as a percentage?

    \(\alpha = 1 - 0.15 = 0.85 = 85.0\%\). The percentage drop is 15.0000%.
