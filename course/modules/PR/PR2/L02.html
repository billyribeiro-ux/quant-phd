While mean-variance optimization produces mathematically optimal portfolios in sample, it relies on unobservable expected returns and inverts poorly conditioned sample covariance matrices. The Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) or risk parity portfolio removes expected return estimation entirely by demanding that every asset contribute equally to total portfolio volatility. Formulated as a strictly convex logarithmic barrier optimization problem, risk parity guarantees a unique global solution without corner holdings or short positions, providing a robust, all-weather asset allocation benchmark.

1The Equal Risk Contribution Problem

Building on the risk budgeting framework developed in Lesson 1, consider a universe of \(N \ge 2\) assets with positive definite covariance matrix \(\Sigma \in \R^{N \times N}\). Recall that for a weight vector \(w = (w_1, \dots, w_N)^\top \in \R^N\) with \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}\), the total component risk contribution of asset \(i\) is \(\mathrm{TRC}_i(w) = w_i (\Sigma w)_i / \sigma_p\), and its percent risk contribution is \(\mathrm{PRC}_i(w) = w_i (\Sigma w)_i / \sigma_p^2\).

Definition 2.1 (Equal Risk Contribution and Risk Parity)

A portfolio \(w^* \in \R^N\) with strictly positive weights \(w_i^* > 0\) and full investment constraint \(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i^* = 1\) is an Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) or risk parity portfolio if every asset contributes an equal share to total portfolio volatility:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{TRC}_i(w^*) &= \mathrm{TRC}_j(w^*) \quad \forall i, j \in \{1, \dots, N\} \end{aligned} \]

Equivalently, \(\mathrm{PRC}_i(w^*) = \frac{1}{N}\), or in algebraic terms: \(w_i^* (\Sigma w^*)_i = w_j^* (\Sigma w^*)_j\) for all \(i, j\).

Directly solving the non-linear algebraic system \(w_i (\Sigma w)_i = w_j (\Sigma w)_j\) subject to \(\sum w_i = 1\) and \(w \ge 0\) is non-trivial because the system involves quadratic cross-terms. Maillard, Roncalli & Teïletche (2010) resolved this challenge by reformulating the problem as an unconstrained, strictly convex logarithmic barrier optimization.

Theorem 2.1 (Convex Log-Barrier Formulation of Risk Parity)

Assumption A1 (Positive Definite Covariance): The covariance matrix \(\Sigma \in \R^{N \times N}\) is symmetric and strictly positive definite.

Assumption A2 (Strictly Positive Budgets): The risk budget vector \(b = (b_1, \dots, b_N)^\top\) satisfies \(b_i > 0\) for all \(i\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^N b_i = 1\) (with \(b_i = 1/N\) for risk parity).

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, the unconstrained optimization problem

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{y \in \R_{++}^N} \quad f(y) = \frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma y - \sum_{i=1}^N b_i \ln(y_i) \end{aligned} \]

is strictly convex on the positive orthant \(\R_{++}^N = \{y \in \R^N : y_i > 0\}\), possesses a unique global minimizer \(y^* \in \R_{++}^N\), and the normalized weight vector

\[ \begin{aligned} w^* = \frac{y^*}{\sum_{i=1}^N y_i^*} \end{aligned} \]

is the unique portfolio satisfying \(\mathrm{PRC}_i(w^*) = b_i\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i^* = 1\).

Proof

The gradient of \(f(y)\) with respect to \(y\) is \(\nabla f(y) = \Sigma y - b \oslash y\), where \((b \oslash y)_i = b_i / y_i\). The Hessian matrix is

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla^2 f(y) &= \Sigma + \diag\left( \frac{b_1}{y_1^2}, \dots, \frac{b_N}{y_N^2} \right) \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\Sigma \succ 0\) by Assumption A1 and \(b_i / y_i^2 > 0\) for all \(y \in \R_{++}^N\), \(\nabla^2 f(y)\) is the sum of a positive definite matrix and a strictly positive diagonal matrix, hence strictly positive definite everywhere on \(\R_{++}^N\). Strict positive definiteness of the Hessian guarantees that \(f(y)\) is strictly convex with at most one stationary point.

Furthermore, as \(y_i \to 0^+\), \(-\ln(y_i) \to +\infty\), and as \(\|y\| \to +\infty\), \(\frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma y \to +\infty\). Thus \(f(y)\) is coercive on \(\R_{++}^N\) with level sets that are compact and bounded away from the boundary, ensuring a unique global minimizer \(y^*\) satisfying the first-order condition \(\nabla f(y^*) = 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (\Sigma y^*)_i - \frac{b_i}{y_i^*} = 0 \implies y_i^* (\Sigma y^*)_i = b_i \end{aligned} \]

Letting \(c = \sum_{j=1}^N y_j^* > 0\) and \(w^* = y^* / c\), we substitute \(y^* = c w^*\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (c w_i^*) [\Sigma (c w^*)]_i &= c^2 w_i^* (\Sigma w^*)_i = b_i \end{aligned} \]

Summing across all \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) gives \(c^2 w^{*\top} \Sigma w^* = \sum_{i=1}^N b_i = 1\), so \(c^2 \sigma_p^2(w^*) = 1\). Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{PRC}_i(w^*) &= \frac{w_i^* (\Sigma w^*)_i}{\sigma_p^2(w^*)} = \frac{b_i / c^2}{1 / c^2} = b_i \end{aligned} \]

For \(b_i = 1/N\), this yields \(\mathrm{PRC}_i(w^*) = 1/N\), proving that \(w^*\) is the unique risk parity portfolio.

2Newton-Raphson Optimization & Numerical Properties

Because \(f(y)\) is twice continuously differentiable and strongly convex on \(\R_{++}^N\), Newton-Raphson iteration with backtracking line search exhibits quadratic local convergence. At iteration \(k\), the Newton step \(\Delta y^{(k)}\) solves the linear system:

\[ \begin{aligned} \left[ \Sigma + \diag\left( \frac{b}{(y^{(k)})^2} \right) \right] \Delta y^{(k)} &= -\left( \Sigma y^{(k)} - \frac{b}{y^{(k)}} \right) \end{aligned} \]

Backtracking Armijo line search ensures strictly positive iterates \(y^{(k)} + \alpha \Delta y^{(k)} > 0\) and guarantees monotonic descent in \(f(y)\). In practice, convergence to machine precision is typically achieved in 5 to 10 iterations.

Theorem 2.2 (Analytical Solution for Uniform Pairwise Correlation)

Assumption A1: All asset variances are positive \(\sigma_i^2 > 0\), and all off-diagonal correlation coefficients are identical: \(\rho_{ij} = \rho\) for all \(i \ne j\) with \(-1/(N-1) < \rho < 1\).

Assumption A2: The equal risk contribution budget \(b_i = 1/N\) is enforced.

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, if \(\rho = 0\), the risk parity portfolio has analytical weights inversely proportional to asset volatility:

\[ \begin{aligned} w_i^* &= \frac{1/\sigma_i}{\sum_{j=1}^N (1/\sigma_j)} \end{aligned} \]

When \(\rho > 0\), the risk parity weights remain strictly positive and lie between 1/N and the inverse-volatility allocation.

Proof

When \(\rho = 0\), \(\Sigma\) is a diagonal matrix with \(\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i^2\). The risk parity condition \(w_i (\Sigma w)_i = w_j (\Sigma w)_j\) reduces to \(w_i (w_i \sigma_i^2) = w_j (w_j \sigma_j^2)\), or \((w_i \sigma_i)^2 = (w_j \sigma_j)^2\). Since weights and volatilities are strictly positive, \(w_i \sigma_i = w_j \sigma_j = K\) for some constant \(K > 0\). Thus \(w_i = K / \sigma_i\). Normalizing by \(\sum_{j=1}^N w_j = 1\) yields \(w_i^* = (1/\sigma_i) / \sum_{j=1}^N (1/\sigma_j)\), completing the proof.

Lab 2 · Equal Risk Contribution Numerical Solver

Implements the Maillard-Roncalli-Teiletche log-barrier Newton solver on a 3-asset multi-asset covariance matrix. Demonstrates quadratic convergence in under 10 iterations, verifies exact equality of percent risk contributions PRC_i = 1/3, and compares ERC weights against the inverse-volatility heuristic.

"""Lab 2: Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) numerical solver via log-barrier Newton method.

Demonstrates the Maillard-Roncalli-Teiletche (2010) log-barrier formulation:
min_y  0.5 * y.T @ Sigma @ y - sum_i (1/n) * ln(y_i)
with normalized weights w = y / sum(y), achieving exact equal risk contributions.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    # 3-asset covariance matrix: Equities (18%), Bonds (6%), Commodities (15%)
    cov = np.array(
        [
            [0.0324, 0.0020, 0.0050],
            [0.0020, 0.0036, -0.0010],
            [0.0050, -0.0010, 0.0225],
        ],
        dtype=np.float64,
    )
    n = 3
    b = np.full(n, 1.0 / n, dtype=np.float64)

    # Initial guess: inverse volatility
    diag_std = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov))
    inv_vol_w = (1.0 / diag_std) / np.sum(1.0 / diag_std)

    y = 1.0 / diag_std
    iterations = 0

    for i in range(1, 100):
        grad = cov @ y - b / y
        if float(np.max(np.abs(grad))) < 1e-10:
            iterations = i
            break
        hessian = cov + np.diag(b / (y**2))
        delta_y = np.linalg.solve(hessian, -grad)

        step = 1.0
        while np.any(y + step * delta_y <= 0):
            step *= 0.5
        y = y + step * delta_y

    w_erc = y / np.sum(y)
    port_vol = float(np.sqrt(w_erc @ cov @ w_erc))
    mrc = (cov @ w_erc) / port_vol
    trc = w_erc * mrc
    prc = trc / port_vol

    print(f"Newton Iterations to Convergence: {iterations}")
    print(f"Inverse-Vol Weights: {[round(float(x), 4) for x in inv_vol_w]}")
    print(f"ERC Weights: {[round(float(x), 4) for x in w_erc]}")
    print(f"ERC Portfolio Vol: {port_vol:.6f}")
    print(f"ERC Component TRC: {[round(float(x), 6) for x in trc]}")
    print(f"ERC Percent PRC: {[round(float(x), 6) for x in prc]}")
    print(f"Max PRC deviation from 1/N: {float(np.max(np.abs(prc - 1.0 / n))):.2e}")


main()

ERC Weights: [0.1691, 0.5935, 0.2374]
ERC Portfolio Vol: 0.063117

3Where the canon breaks

Equal Risk Contribution allocates risk, not capital or return. By deliberately ignoring expected returns, risk parity avoids the catastrophic estimation error magnification of sample Markowitz optimization. However, this agnosticism comes with a structural vulnerability: if an asset class offers a permanently depressed or negative Sharpe ratio (such as commodities during long secular bear markets or cash during negative real interest rate regimes), risk parity forces the portfolio to maintain a full, constant risk allocation to that loser. Furthermore, when applied to bond-heavy universes without leverage, risk parity portfolios produce very low expected returns, forcing institutional investors to employ borrowing leverage to achieve target return hurdles.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Risk parity means dividing capital equally among assets (1/N).”

    1/N equalizes capital, whereas risk parity equalizes risk contributions. In a stock/bond portfolio, 1/N equal capital weighting gives ~90% of total risk to equities, whereas risk parity allocates ~75-80% of capital to bonds so that both assets contribute exactly 50% to portfolio volatility.

  2. “Risk parity is equivalent to inverse-volatility weighting.”

    Inverse-volatility weighting \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\) ignores off-diagonal pairwise correlations \(\rho_{ij}\). As demonstrated in Lab 2, when correlations are non-zero, inverse-volatility weights fail to equalize risk contributions.

  3. “Risk parity portfolios are always mean-variance efficient.”

    Risk parity coincides with the Markowitz tangency portfolio if and only if all assets have identical Sharpe ratios and equal pairwise correlations. In realistic market environments with heterogeneous Sharpe ratios, risk parity deliberately trades off mathematical optimality for robust diversification.

M1 · Analytical ERC Weights for Two Assets

For two uncorrelated assets (\(\rho = 0\)) with volatilities \(\sigma_1 = 0.08\) and \(\sigma_2 = 0.24\), compute the exact analytical Equal Risk Contribution weights \(w_1^*\) and \(w_2^*\) and verify that \(\mathrm{PRC}_1 = \mathrm{PRC}_2 = 0.50\).

Solution

Because \(\rho = 0\), Theorem 2.2 applies: \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\). We have \(1/\sigma_1 = 1 / 0.08 = 12.5\) and \(1/\sigma_2 = 1 / 0.24 = 4.1667\). The sum is \(12.5 + 4.1667 = 16.6667\). Thus \(w_1^* = 12.5 / 16.6667 = 0.75\) (75%), and \(w_2^* = 4.1667 / 16.6667 = 0.25\) (25%). Verifying risk contributions: \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.75^2(0.08^2) + 0.25^2(0.24^2) = 0.5625(0.0064) + 0.0625(0.0576) = 0.0036 + 0.0036 = 0.0072\). Component variance contributions are \(w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 = 0.0036\) and \(w_2^2 \sigma_2^2 = 0.0036\). Therefore \(\mathrm{PRC}_1 = 0.0036 / 0.0072 = 0.50\) and \(\mathrm{PRC}_2 = 0.0036 / 0.0072 = 0.50\), exactly equal.

M2 · Gradient Evaluation at Inverse-Vol Weights

For a 2-asset universe with covariance \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 0.01 & 0.005 \\ 0.005 & 0.04 \end{pmatrix}\), evaluate the log-barrier gradient \(\nabla f(y) = \Sigma y - (0.5 \oslash y)\) at the inverse-volatility starting point \(y = (1/\sigma_1, 1/\sigma_2) = (10, 5)^\top\). Does the inverse-volatility point satisfy the first-order condition?

Solution

We evaluate \(\Sigma y\): \((\Sigma y)_1 = 0.01(10) + 0.005(5) = 0.10 + 0.025 = 0.125\); \((\Sigma y)_2 = 0.005(10) + 0.04(5) = 0.05 + 0.20 = 0.250\). The log-barrier term is \(b \oslash y = (0.5 / 10, 0.5 / 5)^\top = (0.05, 0.10)^\top\). The gradient is \(\nabla f(y) = (0.125 - 0.05, 0.250 - 0.10)^\top = (0.075, 0.150)^\top \ne (0, 0)^\top\). Because the gradient is non-zero, the inverse-volatility starting point does not satisfy the first-order condition, reflecting the distortion introduced by the non-zero covariance \(\Sigma_{12} = 0.005\).

M3 · Volatility Comparison: Risk Parity vs 1/N

Using the covariance matrix from M2, calculate the portfolio volatility of the 1/N equal capital allocation \(w = (0.5, 0.5)^\top\) versus the analytical ERC allocation \(w_{\text{ERC}} = (0.75, 0.25)^\top\). Which portfolio has lower total risk?

Solution

For 1/N: \(\sigma_{1/N}^2 = 0.5^2(0.01) + 0.5^2(0.04) + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.005) = 0.0025 + 0.0100 + 0.0025 = 0.0150\), so \(\sigma_{1/N} = \sqrt{0.0150} \approx 0.12247\) (12.25%). For the ERC portfolio: \(\sigma_{\text{ERC}}^2 = 0.75^2(0.01) + 0.25^2(0.04) + 2(0.75)(0.25)(0.005) = 0.5625(0.01) + 0.0625(0.04) + 0.375(0.005) = 0.005625 + 0.0025 + 0.001875 = 0.01000\), so \(\sigma_{\text{ERC}} = \sqrt{0.0100} = 0.1000\) (10.00%). The risk parity portfolio achieves 2.25 percentage points lower total volatility because it shifts capital into the lower-risk asset.

P1 · Strict Convexity of Log-Barrier Objective

Prove that the Hessian \(\nabla^2 f(y) = \Sigma + \diag(b / y^2)\) is strictly positive definite for all \(y \in \R_{++}^N\) whenever \(\Sigma \succ 0\) and \(b_i > 0\).

Solution

Let \(v \in \R^N\) with \(v \ne 0\). We evaluate the quadratic form \(v^\top \nabla^2 f(y) v = v^\top \Sigma v + v^\top \diag(b / y^2) v\). Since \(\Sigma\) is symmetric and positive definite (\(\Sigma \succ 0\)), \(v^\top \Sigma v > 0\) for all \(v \ne 0\). The second term is \(\sum_{i=1}^N (b_i / y_i^2) v_i^2\). Because \(b_i > 0\) and \(y_i > 0\), each coefficient \(b_i / y_i^2 > 0\), so \(\sum_{i=1}^N (b_i / y_i^2) v_i^2 \ge 0\) (and strictly positive if \(v_i \ne 0\)). Therefore, \(v^\top \nabla^2 f(y) v \ge v^\top \Sigma v > 0\). Since the quadratic form is strictly positive for all non-zero vectors \(v\), \(\nabla^2 f(y) \succ 0\) on all of \(\R_{++}^N\), proving strict convexity.

P2 · Existence of Coercive Minimizer

Prove that \(f(y) = \frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma y - \sum_{i=1}^N b_i \ln(y_i)\) is coercive on \(\R_{++}^N\), establishing the existence of a minimizer.

Solution

Let \(\lambda_{\min} > 0\) be the smallest eigenvalue of \(\Sigma\). Then \(\frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma y \ge \frac{1}{2} \lambda_{\min} \|y\|_2^2\). For any sequence \(\{y^{(k)}\} \subset \R_{++}^N\), consider two boundary cases: (1) As \(\|y^{(k)}\|_2 \to +\infty\), the quadratic term \(\frac{1}{2} \lambda_{\min} \|y^{(k)}\|^2\) dominates the logarithmic term \(\sum b_i \ln(y_i^{(k)})\), driving \(f(y^{(k)}) \to +\infty\). (2) If any component \(y_i^{(k)} \to 0^+\), \(-\ln(y_i^{(k)}) \to +\infty\), while the remaining terms are bounded below because \(y_j > 0\) and \(\frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma y \ge 0\). Thus \(f(y^{(k)}) \to +\infty\) as \(y\) approaches the boundary \(\partial \R_{++}^N\). Consequently, all sub-level sets \(\{y \in \R_{++}^N : f(y) \le c\}\) are compact, non-empty, and strictly interior to \(\R_{++}^N\). By the Weierstrass extreme value theorem, \(f(y)\) attains its infimum at an interior point \(y^* \in \R_{++}^N\).

P3 · Scale Invariance of Normalized Weights

Let \(y^*(\Sigma)\) denote the unique minimizer of \(f(y; \Sigma) = \frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma y - \sum b_i \ln y_i\). Prove that for any \(\alpha > 0\), \(y^*(\alpha \Sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}} y^*(\Sigma)\), and conclude that the normalized weights \(w^*(\alpha \Sigma) = w^*(\Sigma)\).

Solution

The first-order condition for \(f(y; \alpha \Sigma)\) is \(\alpha \Sigma y - b \oslash y = 0\). Dividing by \(\alpha\) yields \(\Sigma y - \frac{b}{\alpha y} = 0\). Let \(z = \sqrt{\alpha} y\), so \(y = z / \sqrt{\alpha}\). Substituting gives \(\Sigma (z / \sqrt{\alpha}) - \frac{b}{\alpha (z / \sqrt{\alpha})} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}} [\Sigma z - b \oslash z] = 0\). Thus \(z\) satisfies \(\Sigma z - b \oslash z = 0\), which is the exact first-order condition defining \(y^*(\Sigma)\). By uniqueness, \(z = y^*(\Sigma)\), which implies \(y^*(\alpha \Sigma) = y^*(\Sigma) / \sqrt{\alpha}\). When normalizing weights, \(w^*(\alpha \Sigma) = \frac{y^*(\alpha \Sigma)}{\sum_i y_i^*(\alpha \Sigma)} = \frac{y^*(\Sigma) / \sqrt{\alpha}}{\sum_i y_i^*(\Sigma) / \sqrt{\alpha}} = \frac{y^*(\Sigma)}{\sum_i y_i^*(\Sigma)} = w^*(\Sigma)\), proving exact scale invariance.

Empirical

E1 · Equal Risk Contribution Across Three Global Assets

Using the covariance matrix from Lab 2 (Equities: 18%, Bonds: 6%, Commodities: 15%), explain why the ERC portfolio allocates 59.35% of capital to bonds, 16.91% to equities, and 23.74% to commodities, and compare the resulting portfolio volatility (6.31%) to a 60/40 equity/bond blend (11.30%).

Solution

Because equities have triple the volatility of bonds (\(18\% / 6\% = 3\)), equalizing risk contributions requires bonds to hold approximately 3 to 4 times the capital weight of equities. Commodities, with volatility 15%, occupy an intermediate weight (23.74%). By avoiding the massive equity concentration of the 60/40 portfolio, total portfolio volatility is reduced from 11.30% to 6.31% (a 44% volatility reduction), creating a far more stable returns distribution that does not depend on equity market regimes.

E2 · Asymmetric Risk Budgeting for a Pension Fund

A pension fund mandates a risk budget of 50% to fixed income, 30% to equities, and 20% to real assets (commodities/real estate). Using the covariance from Lab 2, how does shifting the risk budget from ERC \((1/3, 1/3, 1/3)\) to \((0.30, 0.50, 0.20)\) affect bond capital allocation?

Solution

Under ERC, fixed income receives 33.3% of the risk budget and holds 59.35% of capital. Increasing the fixed income risk budget from 33.3% to 50.0% while decreasing equity risk from 33.3% to 30.0% and commodities from 33.3% to 20.0% pushes even more capital into fixed income. The solver increases the bond capital weight above 70%, reducing equity capital toward 12-14%. The pension fund achieves liability-matching duration stability while still participating in equity and inflation upside.

E3 · Rebalancing Turnover in Risk Parity Strategies

During a market rally where equities appreciate by 25% while bonds return 0%, calculate the rebalancing trade required for an unleveraged ERC portfolio that started with weights \(w_e = 0.17\) and \(w_b = 0.83\) to restore target weights (assuming covariance remains constant).

Solution

Starting portfolio value is 1.00 ($0.17 equity, $0.83 bonds). After the equity rally, equity value is \(0.17 \times 1.25 = 0.2125\), bond value is 0.83, and total portfolio value is \(0.2125 + 0.83 = 1.0425\). End-of-period weights before rebalancing are \(w_e' = 0.2125 / 1.0425 \approx 0.2038\) (20.38%) and \(w_b' = 0.83 / 1.0425 \approx 0.7962\) (79.62%). To restore target weights \((0.17, 0.83)\), the manager must sell \(0.2038 - 0.1700 = 0.0338\) (3.38% of portfolio value) of equities and buy 3.38% of bonds. Risk parity rebalancing systematically harvests the rebalancing premium by trimming outperforming, high-volatility assets and accumulating underperforming, low-volatility assets.

R1 · Cyclical Coordinate Descent for Risk Parity

Spinu (2013) developed a cyclical coordinate descent algorithm for solving the risk parity log-barrier problem without inverting the Hessian matrix. Formulate the one-dimensional coordinate update for variable \(y_i\) holding \(y_{-i}\) fixed, and explain why this algorithm is particularly attractive for large-scale universes (\(N > 1000\)).

Solution

For asset \(i\), holding \(y_{-i}\) constant, the first-order condition \(\Sigma_{ii} y_i + \sum_{j \ne i} \Sigma_{ij} y_j - b_i / y_i = 0\) is a quadratic in \(y_i\): \(\Sigma_{ii} y_i^2 + (\sum_{j \ne i} \Sigma_{ij} y_j) y_i - b_i = 0\). Let \(a = \Sigma_{ii} > 0\), \(c_i = \sum_{j \ne i} \Sigma_{ij} y_j\), and \(b = -b_i < 0\). The unique positive root is \(y_i^* = \frac{-c_i + \sqrt{c_i^2 + 4 \Sigma_{ii} b_i}}{2 \Sigma_{ii}}\). For large universes (\(N > 1000\)), Newton's method requires solving an \(N \times N\) linear system with complexity \(\mathcal{O}(N^3)\) per iteration, whereas cyclical coordinate descent updates each coordinate in \(\mathcal{O}(N)\) operations, requiring \(\mathcal{O}(N^2)\) per full cycle without matrix inversion or memory overhead.

R2 · Risk Parity with Expected Return Shrinkage

How can the pure risk parity framework be unified with Black-Litterman or mean-variance views? Formulate a combined optimization objective that minimizes divergence from equal risk contributions while penalizing deviations from a Bayesian expected return tangency portfolio.

Solution

A unified objective balances risk parity tracking against mean-variance utility: \(\min_{w \in \Delta} \sum_{i=1}^N [\mathrm{PRC}_i(w) - b_i]^2 - \kappa [w^\top \mu_{\text{BL}} - \frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top \Sigma w]\), where \(\kappa \ge 0\) controls the trade-off. When \(\kappa = 0\), the solver produces pure risk parity; as \(\kappa \to \infty\), it converges to the Black-Litterman mean-variance tangency allocation. Roncalli (2013) terms this 'risk-budgeting with active views', enabling fundamental asset managers to express alpha convictions while imposing hard upper bounds on component risk contributions.

R3 · Inflation Shock Fragility in Unleveraged Risk Parity

During the 2022 global inflation shock, both equities and sovereign bonds suffered severe simultaneous drawdowns (stock-bond correlation turned sharply positive). Analyze how this structural regime shift affected risk parity funds and why multi-asset risk parity requires commodity and inflation-linked bond exposures to maintain resilience.

Solution

Traditional risk parity relies heavily on the negative stock-bond correlation observed from 2000 to 2020 (the disinflationary moderation regime). When inflation surges unexpectedly, central banks hike interest rates aggressively, depressing bond prices (duration shock) while simultaneously compressing equity valuation multiples. In 2022, a pure equity-bond risk parity fund suffered large drawdowns because both components declined in tandem. Robust risk parity frameworks (such as Bridgewater's All Weather) mitigate this fragility by budgeting equal risk across four economic quadrants: rising/falling growth and rising/falling inflation, allocating substantial risk to inflation-linked debt (TIPS) and physical commodities that surge during stagflationary regimes.

  1. What condition defines the Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) portfolio for a universe of \(N\) assets?

    Options

    ERC requires that every asset's percent risk contribution equals \(1/N\), so each asset contributes identically to total portfolio risk.

  2. What objective function is minimized in the Maillard-Roncalli-Teiletche convex formulation of risk parity?

    Options

    The logarithmic barrier formulation \(\frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma y - \sum b_i \ln(y_i)\) provides a strictly convex unconstrained objective on the positive orthant.

  3. In Lab 2, how many Newton-Raphson iterations were required to converge to machine precision (\(< 10^{-10}\))?

    The Newton solver converged in exactly 6 iterations due to quadratic local convergence.

  4. Under what conditions is the Equal Risk Contribution portfolio identical to inverse-volatility weighting \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\)?

    Options

    Inverse-volatility ignores off-diagonal covariance; it equals risk parity if and only if correlations are zero or equal.

  5. Why does the risk parity log-barrier formulation guarantee that all portfolio weights \(w_i^*\) are strictly positive?

    Options

    The log barrier creates an infinite penalty as any coordinate approaches zero, guaranteeing strictly positive solutions.

  6. Where does the risk parity portfolio sit relative to the Markowitz minimum-variance portfolio (GMV) and the naive 1/N allocation?

    Options

    Risk parity diversifies risk exposure, lying between 1/N (equal capital) and GMV (minimum variance), avoiding corner allocations.

  7. In a 3-asset risk parity portfolio, what is the exact percent risk contribution \(\mathrm{PRC}_i\) of each asset (to 4 decimal places)?

    \(1 / 3 \approx 0.33333 \approx 0.3333\).

  8. What is a major limitation of risk parity portfolios in a low-interest-rate or inflationary regime?

    Options

    Because bonds have low volatility, risk parity allocates 60-80% of capital to fixed income, causing poor unleveraged returns during rate hikes.
