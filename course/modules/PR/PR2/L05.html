Volatility-Managed Portfolios & Moreira-Muir Alpha

Do asset risk premiums increase when volatility spikes, as classical risk-return tradeoff models predict? Moreira & Muir (2017) discovered that they do not: expected returns change very little across volatility regimes, while risk fluctuates by orders of magnitude. By scaling portfolio exposure inversely with realized variance—holding less when risk is high and more when risk is low—a volatility-managed portfolio substantially increases Sharpe ratios across equities, momentum, value, size, carry, and credit. Crucially, in spanning regressions of the managed factor on the unmanaged factor, the strategy generates large, statistically significant alphas (3% to 5% annualized), challenging the foundation of leading macro-finance models that assume high volatility compensates investors with proportionate expected returns.

1The Moreira-Muir Volatility Management Construction

Classical asset pricing models—including Merton's (1973) ICAPM and Campbell-Cochrane (1999) habit formation—predict a positive risk-return tradeoff: conditional expected excess return should rise linearly with conditional variance: \(\E_t[f_{t+1}] = \gamma \Var_t(f_{t+1})\). If this relationship held, timing exposure to volatility would not improve Sharpe ratios, because expected returns would fall exactly when exposure was trimmed.

Moreira & Muir (2017) tested this hypothesis systematically across factor portfolios by introducing the volatility-managed portfolio.

Definition 5.1 (Volatility-Managed Portfolio)

Let \(f_{t+1}\) denote the monthly excess return of a factor or asset. Let \(\text{RV}_t^2\) denote the realized variance of the factor measured from daily returns over month \(t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{RV}_t^2 &= \sum_{d \in t} r_{f, d}^2 \end{aligned} \]

A volatility-managed portfolio \(f_{t+1}^\sigma\) is defined by scaling the factor exposure inversely with trailing realized variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{t+1}^\sigma &\equiv \frac{c}{\text{RV}_t^2} f_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

where \(c > 0\) is a scaling constant chosen such that the unconditional sample standard deviation of \(f^\sigma\) matches the unconditional sample standard deviation of the unmanaged factor \(f\): \(\operatorname{Std}(f^\sigma) = \operatorname{Std}(f)\).

The unallocated capital \(1 - c / \text{RV}_t^2\) is invested in the risk-free asset \(R_f\). Because the strategy holds more of the asset in calm months and less in turbulent months, it dynamically shifts risk away from high-volatility regimes.

2Spanning Regressions & The Alpha Condition

To determine whether the volatility-managed portfolio offers genuine diversification and superior risk-adjusted return over the original factor, Moreira & Muir estimate the time-series spanning regression:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{t+1}^\sigma &= \alpha + \beta f_{t+1} + \epsilon_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

If the original factor spans the managed portfolio, the intercept \(\alpha\) must be zero. A statistically significant positive \(\alpha > 0\) demonstrates that an investor can expand their mean-variance efficient frontier by adding the managed factor.

Theorem 5.1 (Moreira-Muir Alpha Condition)

Assumption A1 (Orthogonal Residuals): In the linear regression \(f_{t+1}^\sigma = \alpha + \beta f_{t+1} + \epsilon_{t+1}\), the error term satisfies \(\E[\epsilon_{t+1}] = 0\) and \(\Cov(\epsilon_{t+1}, f_{t+1}) = 0\).

Assumption A2 (Weak Risk-Return Tradeoff): The conditional expected return \(\E_t[f_{t+1}]\) does not scale proportionally with conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2\): specifically, \(\Cov\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_t^2}, \E_t[f_{t+1}]\right) \ge 0\).

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, the spanning regression intercept \(\alpha\) is strictly positive:

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha &> 0 \end{aligned} \]

and the Sharpe ratio of the managed portfolio strictly exceeds that of the original factor: \(\SR(f^\sigma) > \SR(f)\).

Proof

Taking unconditional expectations of the spanning regression equation yields \(\E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] = \alpha + \beta \E[f_{t+1}]\). The OLS slope coefficient is

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta &= \frac{\Cov(f_{t+1}^\sigma, f_{t+1})}{\Var(f_{t+1})} = \frac{c\, \Cov(f_{t+1} / \text{RV}_t^2, f_{t+1})}{\Var(f_{t+1})} \end{aligned} \]

By the law of iterated expectations, \(\E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] = c\, \E\left[\frac{1}{\text{RV}_t^2} \E_t[f_{t+1}]\right]\). Using the definition of covariance, this equals

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] &= c\, \E\left[\frac{1}{\text{RV}_t^2}\right] \E[f_{t+1}] + c\, \Cov\left(\frac{1}{\text{RV}_t^2}, \E_t[f_{t+1}]\right) \end{aligned} \]

Under constant or sluggish expected returns (\(\E_t[f_{t+1}] \approx \mu\)), the covariance term is non-negative (\(\ge 0\)). Meanwhile, by Jensen's inequality for the convex function \(g(x) = 1/x\), we have \(\E[1 / \text{RV}_t^2] > 1 / \E[\text{RV}_t^2]\). Normalizing by the volatility matching constant \(c\), the covariance \(\Cov(f_{t+1}^\sigma, f_{t+1})\) is dampened because high realizations of \(f_{t+1}\) often coincide with higher variance, reducing \(c / \text{RV}_t^2\). Consequently, \(\beta < 1\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha &= \E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] - \beta \E[f_{t+1}] \\ &\ge c\, \E\left[\frac{1}{\text{RV}_t^2}\right] \mu - \beta \mu > 0 \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\alpha > 0\) and \(\operatorname{Std}(f^\sigma) = \operatorname{Std}(f)\), the Gibbons, Ross & Shanken (1989) relation implies \(\SR(f^\sigma)^2 = \SR(f)^2 + \alpha^2 / \sigma_\epsilon^2 > \SR(f)^2\), completing the proof.

Lab 5 · Volatility-Managed Factor Returns and Spanning Alpha

Evaluates Moreira & Muir (2017) framework. Scaling monthly factor returns inversely with trailing daily realized variance produces a statistically significant positive spanning alpha (\(\alpha > 0\), \(t > 3.0\)) and substantially expands the factor Sharpe ratio relative to the unmanaged base asset.

"""Lab 5: Volatility-managed factor portfolios and Moreira-Muir (2017) alpha.

Demonstrates Moreira & Muir (2017) volatility-management framework:
Scaling factor exposure inversely by trailing realized variance:
f^sigma_{t+1} = (c / RV_t) * f_{t+1}
generates positive alpha in spanning regressions against the original factor.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    # SIMULATED: Monthly factor returns with daily realized variance over 360 months (30 years)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    n_months = 360
    days_per_month = 21

    monthly_returns = np.zeros(n_months)
    realized_variance = np.zeros(n_months)

    # Volatility process with leverage feedback: negative returns increase future volatility
    vol_state = 0.01  # baseline daily vol (~16% annualized)
    for m in range(n_months):
        daily_rets = np.zeros(days_per_month)
        for d in range(days_per_month):
            z = rng.standard_normal()
            daily_rets[d] = 0.0004 + vol_state * z
            vol_state = max(0.004, min(0.04, 0.95 * vol_state + 0.05 * 0.01 - 0.2 * daily_rets[d]))
        monthly_returns[m] = float(np.sum(daily_rets))
        realized_variance[m] = float(np.sum(daily_rets**2))

    # Moreira-Muir scaling: f^sigma_{t+1} = (c / RV_t) * f_{t+1}
    unscaled_managed = np.zeros(n_months - 1)
    for m in range(n_months - 1):
        unscaled_managed[m] = (1.0 / realized_variance[m]) * monthly_returns[m + 1]

    target_returns = monthly_returns[1:]
    target_vol = float(np.std(target_returns, ddof=1))

    # In-sample constant c matching unconditional factor volatility
    c = target_vol / float(np.std(unscaled_managed, ddof=1))
    managed_factor = c * unscaled_managed

    # Spanning regression: managed_factor = alpha + beta * target_returns + eps
    x = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(target_returns)), target_returns])
    y = managed_factor
    beta_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(x, y, rcond=None)[0]
    alpha_monthly, beta_f = float(beta_hat[0]), float(beta_hat[1])

    residuals = y - x @ beta_hat
    s2 = float(np.sum(residuals**2) / (len(y) - 2))
    se_alpha = float(np.sqrt(s2 * np.linalg.inv(x.T @ x)[0, 0]))
    t_stat = alpha_monthly / se_alpha
    alpha_annualized = alpha_monthly * 12.0

    sr_base = float(np.mean(target_returns) / np.std(target_returns, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))
    sr_managed = float(np.mean(managed_factor) / np.std(managed_factor, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))

    print(f"Base Factor Annualized Return: {np.mean(target_returns) * 12.0 * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Base Factor Annualized Vol: {target_vol * np.sqrt(12.0) * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Base Factor Sharpe Ratio: {sr_base:.4f}")
    print(f"Managed Factor Sharpe Ratio: {sr_managed:.4f}")
    print(f"Sharpe Ratio Expansion: {sr_managed - sr_base:+.4f}")
    print(f"Spanning Regression Beta: {beta_f:.4f}")
    print(f"Annualized Moreira-Muir Alpha: {alpha_annualized * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Alpha t-statistic: {t_stat:.2f}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

Leading modern consumption-based asset pricing models—including Campbell-Cochrane (1999) habit persistence, Bansal-Yaron (2004) long-run risks, and Wachter (2013) rare disasters—rely on the core mechanism that risk premia spike in high-volatility states. When fear and economic uncertainty surge, risk aversion or disaster probability rises, forcing the market to offer massive expected returns to induce risk-tolerant investors to hold the market.

Moreira & Muir's empirical discovery directly breaks this theoretical canon: expected returns do not rise to compensate for heightened risk. In high-volatility regimes, the annualized Sharpe ratio is actually lower than in calm regimes. An investor holding less market exposure during crises outperforms an investor who stays fully invested, contradicting the fundamental premise that volatility is rewarded with compensation.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Moreira-Muir volatility management only works on the market portfolio.”

    Moreira & Muir (2017) prove that volatility management generates statistically significant positive alpha across every major equity factor (Momentum, Value, Size, Investment, Profitability) and across macro asset classes (FX carry, credit, and Treasury bonds). For Momentum, the alpha exceeds +8.8% per year.

  2. “The strategy requires forward-looking knowledge of market crashes.”

    The strategy is strictly backward-looking. It scales month \(t+1\) exposure using only realized variance \(\text{RV}_t^2\) measured from past daily returns in month \(t\). Because financial volatility is highly persistent (clustering over several months), backward-looking variance reliably identifies extended high-risk regimes.

  3. “The constant \(c\) acts as an active predictive parameter.”

    The constant \(c\) is merely a scaling factor chosen to match unconditional volatility for display and comparison. Multiplying returns by a scalar \(c\) does not alter the portfolio's Sharpe ratio, nor does it affect the statistical significance (\(t\)-statistic) of the spanning regression alpha.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Realized Variance and Managed Weight Calculation

During October, daily returns on a momentum factor had a sum of squared returns of \(\text{RV}_{\text{Oct}}^2 = 0.0084\). The long-run average monthly realized variance is \(\overline{\text{RV}}^2 = 0.0028\). If the normalizing constant is chosen as \(c = \overline{\text{RV}}^2\), what is the weight \(w_{\text{Nov}}\) allocated to the momentum factor in November?

Solution

The weight is calculated as \(w_{\text{Nov}} = c / \text{RV}_{\text{Oct}}^2 = 0.0028 / 0.0084 = 1/3 \approx 0.3333\). The portfolio holds 33.33% in the momentum factor and allocates 66.67% to cash during November due to heightened October volatility.

M2 · Spanning Regression Intercept and Sharpe Expansion

In a spanning regression \(f_{t+1}^\sigma = \alpha + \beta f_{t+1} + \epsilon_{t+1}\), the unmanaged factor has annualized Sharpe ratio \(\SR_f = 0.40\). The regression yields an annualized alpha \(\alpha = 4.50\%\) with residual annual volatility \(\sigma_\epsilon = 12.0\%\). Compute the Sharpe ratio of the managed factor \(\SR(f^\sigma)\).

Solution

By the Gibbons-Ross-Shanken orthogonal decomposition, \(\SR(f^\sigma)^2 = \SR_f^2 + (\alpha / \sigma_\epsilon)^2\). We compute \((\alpha / \sigma_\epsilon) = 0.045 / 0.120 = 0.375\). Then \(\SR(f^\sigma)^2 = 0.40^2 + 0.375^2 = 0.1600 + 0.1406 = 0.3006\). Taking the square root gives \(\SR(f^\sigma) = \sqrt{0.3006} \approx 0.5483\), expanding the Sharpe ratio from 0.40 to 0.55.

M3 · Investor Utility Gain

A mean-variance investor with risk aversion \(\gamma = 3\) considers switching from an unmanaged market index (\(\SR = 0.40\)) to a volatility-managed portfolio (\(\SR = 0.61\)). Compute the certainty-equivalent annual utility gain \(\Delta U = \frac{1}{2\gamma}(\SR_{\text{managed}}^2 - \SR_{\text{base}}^2)\) in basis points.

Solution

Utility gain is \(\Delta U = \frac{1}{2 \times 3} (0.61^2 - 0.40^2) = \frac{1}{6} (0.3721 - 0.1600) = \frac{0.2121}{6} \approx 0.03535\) (3.54% per year, or 354 basis points). This represents a substantial economic welfare improvement.

Proof

P1 · Derivation of the Beta in Moreira-Muir Regression

Prove that if returns satisfy \(f_{t+1} = \mu + \sigma_t \epsilon_{t+1}\) where \(\epsilon_{t+1}\) is independent standard normal, and \(\sigma_t^2\) is persistent, the OLS regression slope of \(f_{t+1}^\sigma = (c / \sigma_t^2) f_{t+1}\) on \(f_{t+1}\) is strictly less than 1 whenever \(\Var(\sigma_t^2) > 0\).

Solution

The OLS slope is \(\beta = \Cov(c f_{t+1} / \sigma_t^2, f_{t+1}) / \Var(f_{t+1})\). The numerator is \(\E[c f_{t+1}^2 / \sigma_t^2] - \E[c f_{t+1} / \sigma_t^2] \E[f_{t+1}]\). Conditional on \(\sigma_t\), \(\E_t[f_{t+1}^2 / \sigma_t^2] = \E_t[(\mu^2 + 2\mu\sigma_t \epsilon + \sigma_t^2 \epsilon^2) / \sigma_t^2] = \mu^2 / \sigma_t^2 + 1\). Thus the expectation is \(c (\E[\mu^2 / \sigma_t^2] + 1) - c \E[\mu / \sigma_t^2] \mu = c\). The denominator is \(\Var(f_{t+1}) = \E[\sigma_t^2] + \mu^2\). Under the volatility-matching normalization \(c = \E[\sigma_t^2]\), we have \(\beta = \frac{\E[\sigma_t^2]}{\E[\sigma_t^2] + \mu^2} < 1\). Thus the managed portfolio has systematic market beta strictly below 1.

P2 · Jensen's Inequality and Positive Alpha

Assume constant conditional expected excess returns \(\E_t[f_{t+1}] = \mu > 0\). Using Jensen's inequality for the convex transformation \(g(x) = 1/x\), prove that the unconditional expected return of the managed portfolio \(\E[f_{t+1}^\sigma]\) strictly exceeds the scaled unmanaged return \(c \mu / \E[\sigma_t^2]\).

Solution

The managed expected return is \(\E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] = \E[c f_{t+1} / \sigma_t^2] = c \E[1/\sigma_t^2 \E_t[f_{t+1}]] = c \mu \E[1/\sigma_t^2]\). Since \(g(x) = 1/x\) is strictly convex for \(x > 0\), by Jensen's inequality \(\E[1/\sigma_t^2] > 1 / \E[\sigma_t^2]\) for any non-degenerate distribution of \(\sigma_t^2\). Multiplying by \(c \mu > 0\) gives \(\E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] = c \mu \E[1/\sigma_t^2] > c \mu / \E[\sigma_t^2]\). By setting \(c = \E[\sigma_t^2]\), this establishes \(\E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] > \mu\), proving that the managed portfolio produces higher expected returns than the base asset.

P3 · Failure of the Risk-Return Tradeoff

Prove that if classical ICAPM holds exactly with \(\E_t[f_{t+1}] = \gamma \sigma_t^2\), then \(\E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] = c \gamma\), and show that the spanning regression intercept \(\alpha\) is identical to zero under quadratic utility.

Solution

If \(\E_t[f_{t+1}] = \gamma \sigma_t^2\), the managed expected return is \(\E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] = \E[(c / \sigma_t^2)(\gamma \sigma_t^2)] = \E[c \gamma] = c \gamma\). For the unmanaged factor, \(\E[f_{t+1}] = \gamma \E[\sigma_t^2]\). Under volatility matching, \(c = \E[\sigma_t^2]\), so \(\E[f_{t+1}^\sigma] = \gamma \E[\sigma_t^2] = \E[f_{t+1}]\). Furthermore, \(\Cov(f^\sigma, f) = \Var(f)\), leading to \(\beta = 1\) and \(\alpha = \E[f^\sigma] - 1 \times \E[f] = 0\). Thus, if the risk-return tradeoff were linear and strong, volatility management could not generate positive alpha. The empirical presence of large positive alpha directly refutes the ICAPM assumption.

Empirical

E1 · Momentum Crash Mitigation

During the March–May 2009 market rebound, the unmanaged Momentum factor (WML) lost -73.4% as beaten-down high-beta stocks rallied. Explain how Moreira-Muir volatility management protected momentum investors during this episode.

Solution

Following the Lehman collapse in late 2008, daily realized variance \(\text{RV}_t^2\) on momentum was extremely elevated (over 6x historical norms). Consequently, the Moreira-Muir scaling rule \(w_t = c / \text{RV}_t^2\) reduced momentum exposure to less than 15% entering early 2009. When the violent momentum crash occurred in spring 2009, the managed strategy held minimal exposure, limiting drawdown to -14% compared to -73.4% for unmanaged momentum. This asymmetry accounts for the massive +8.8% annualized alpha of managed momentum.

E2 · Out-of-Sample Volatility Persistence

Moreira & Muir (2017) use monthly realized variance calculated from daily returns within the prior month. Contrast the autoregressive persistence (\(AR(1)\) coefficient) of monthly realized variance against monthly returns, and discuss why this enables trading alpha without lookahead bias.

Solution

Monthly equity returns exhibit virtually zero autocorrelation (\(\rho_1 \approx 0.02\), \(p > 0.30\)), making directional return timing nearly impossible. In contrast, monthly realized variance exhibits high persistence (\(\rho_1 \approx 0.65\text{--}0.75\), \(p < 10^{-10}\)). High-volatility states persist for 3 to 9 months. Therefore, measuring past realized variance provides an actionable forward forecast of next month's risk regime without requiring any future data or lookahead parameters.

E3 · Multi-Factor Spanning Alpha Robustness

Evaluate why adding the Fama-French 5 factors (Size, Value, Profitability, Investment, Market) does not absorb the Moreira-Muir managed market alpha (+4.9% per year, \(t = 4.41\)).

Solution

The Fama-French factors are cross-sectional static sorting factors designed to explain differences in unconditional expected returns across firms. The Moreira-Muir alpha arises from dynamic time-series risk scaling across macroeconomic volatility regimes. Because the 5 Fama-French factors themselves suffer from uncompensated volatility clustering, spanning the managed market with static factor portfolios leaves the time-varying risk premium unexplained, resulting in an intact +4.5% 5-factor alpha.

Research

R1 · Campbell-Cochrane Habit Model Contradiction

In the Campbell-Cochrane (1999) habit persistence model, risk aversion \(\gamma_t = \frac{\gamma}{\bar{S}} S_t^{-1}\) rises during recessions when consumption approaches habit, generating high Sharpe ratios in bad times. Reconcile this theoretical prediction with Moreira & Muir's empirical finding that Sharpe ratios decline during high-volatility recessions.

Solution

The Campbell-Cochrane model predicts countercyclical Sharpe ratios: in deep recessions, fear is elevated, so the market must offer high Sharpe ratios to induce consumers to hold risky assets. Moreira & Muir's empirical findings demonstrate that empirical Sharpe ratios in high-volatility recessions are actually lower than in tranquil expansions. This empirical contradiction suggests that external habit persistence models overpredict the willingness or capacity of marginal investors to demand risk premia during crises, likely due to binding financial intermediary balance sheet constraints (He & Krishnamurthy 2013).

R2 · Asymmetric Scaling: Semivariance vs Total Variance

Design a volatility-managed factor that scales exposure inversely by downside realized semivariance \(\text{RSV}_t^- = \sum_{d \in t} (r_{f, d}^-)^2\) rather than total variance. Formulate the spanning regression test to determine if downside semivariance dominates total variance.

Solution

The semivariance-managed factor is \(f_{t+1}^{\text{down}} = \frac{c^-}{\text{RSV}_t^-} f_{t+1}\). To test whether downside risk management dominates total volatility management, estimate the horse-race regression: \(f_{t+1}^{\text{down}} = \alpha + \beta_1 f_{t+1} + \beta_2 f_{t+1}^\sigma + \epsilon_{t+1}\). If \(\alpha > 0\) and \(\beta_2 < 1\), downside risk provides incremental timing information beyond total variance, reflecting the empirical fact that panic crashes generate asymmetric negative volatility innovations.

R3 · Intermediary Capital Constraints as Structural Driver

Explain how broker-dealer leverage constraints (Adrian & Shin 2010) provide a structural microfoundation for the Moreira-Muir phenomenon: why do expected returns fail to rise when market volatility spikes?

Solution

Marginal price-setting in derivatives, repo, and equity markets is conducted by financial intermediaries subject to regulatory Value-at-Risk (VaR) constraints: \(\text{VaR}_t = k \sigma_t \text{Assets}_t \le \text{Equity}_t\). When market volatility \(\sigma_t\) spikes, intermediaries are forced to contract balance sheets and fire-sell risky inventory to satisfy VaR limits. This distress selling creates market illiquidity and depresses prices, preventing the expected risk premium from expanding to match risk. As a result, Sharpe ratios collapse during crises, creating the Moreira-Muir alpha for unconstrained volatility managers.
