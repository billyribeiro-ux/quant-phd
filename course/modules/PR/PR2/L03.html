Leverage Aversion, Borrowing Constraints & The Beta Anomaly

Why do low-risk assets offer higher Sharpe ratios than high-risk assets, violating classical CAPM predictions? Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen (2012) showed that the root cause is leverage aversion: institutional and retail investors face borrowing constraints that prevent them from levering up the tangency portfolio. Constrained investors overweight high-beta assets to achieve higher expected returns, bidding up their prices and compressing their Sharpe ratios. Consequently, unleveraged risk parity achieves superior risk-adjusted returns, and applying moderate leverage to risk parity produces significant positive alpha over the market.

1Microeconomics of Borrowing Constraints

Under the classical Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), all investors hold combinations of the risk-free asset and the unique mean-variance tangency portfolio (two-fund separation). An investor seeking higher expected return simply levers the tangency portfolio by borrowing at the risk-free rate \(R_f\). In equilibrium, the security market line has slope equal to the market risk premium and an intercept of zero.

In practice, the vast majority of market participants cannot borrow at the risk-free rate. Mutual funds, pension plans, private wealth clients, and sovereign wealth funds operate under explicit regulatory mandates or internal investment policy statements that prohibit or severely restrict margin leverage. Retail investors face borrowing rates substantially above institutional rates.

Definition 3.1 (Leverage Aversion)

Leverage aversion is the structural friction arising from legal prohibitions, institutional mandates, margin requirements, or behavioral preferences that constrain investors from borrowing at the risk-free rate to lever up low-risk assets, forcing them to satisfy high expected return targets by overweighting high-beta or high-volatility assets.

Theorem 3.1 (The Flatter Security Market Line under Borrowing Constraints)

Assumption A1 (Constrained Investors): A fraction \(\omega \in (0, 1]\) of aggregate wealth is held by investors subject to the borrowing constraint \(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i \le 1\) and \(w_i \ge 0\).

Assumption A2 (Mean-Variance Preferences): All investors have quadratic preferences over portfolio mean and variance with risk aversion parameter \(\gamma > 0\).

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, the equilibrium expected excess return on asset \(i\) satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[r_i] - R_f &= \alpha_0 + \beta_i (\E[r_m] - R_f - \alpha_0) \end{aligned} \]

where \(\alpha_0 = \omega \psi > 0\) is the shadow price (Lagrange multiplier) of the borrowing constraint. Consequently, the Security Market Line is flatter than predicted by CAPM: low-beta assets earn positive alpha (\(\alpha_i > 0\)), and high-beta assets earn negative alpha (\(\alpha_i < 0\)).

Proof

Unconstrained investors maximize \(w^\top \mu - \frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top \Sigma w - R_f (\iota^\top w - 1)\), leading to the standard first-order condition \(\mu - R_f \iota = \gamma \Sigma w\). For constrained investors, the Lagrangian is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(w, \psi) &= w^\top \mu - \frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top \Sigma w - \psi (\iota^\top w - 1) \end{aligned} \]

where \(\psi > 0\) is the multiplier on the binding leverage constraint \(\iota^\top w \le 1\). The first-order condition is \(\mu - \psi \iota = \gamma \Sigma w^c\), or \(\mu = (\gamma \Sigma w^c) + \psi \iota\).

Market clearing requires that aggregate market capitalization equals wealth-weighted holdings: \(w_m = (1 - \omega) w^u + \omega w^c\). Multiplying by \(\gamma \Sigma\) and aggregating yields

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma \Sigma w_m &= (1 - \omega)(\mu - R_f \iota) + \omega(\mu - \psi \iota) \\ &= \mu - [R_f + \omega (\psi - R_f)] \iota \end{aligned} \]

Letting \(R_z = R_f + \omega (\psi - R_f) > R_f\) denote the zero-beta rate (Black 1972), we have \(\mu - R_z \iota = \gamma \Sigma w_m\). For the market portfolio, \(\mu_m - R_z = \gamma \sigma_m^2\). For any asset \(i\), \((\Sigma w_m)_i = \Cov(r_i, r_m) = \beta_i \sigma_m^2\). Dividing the asset equation by the market equation gives \(\E[r_i] - R_z = \beta_i (\E[r_m] - R_z)\). Rearranging in terms of the risk-free rate yields

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[r_i] - R_f &= (R_z - R_f)(1 - \beta_i) + \beta_i (\E[r_m] - R_f) \end{aligned} \]

Since \(R_z > R_f\), setting \(\alpha_0 = R_z - R_f > 0\) completes the proof. Assets with \(\beta_i < 1\) earn positive alpha \(\alpha_i = \alpha_0 (1 - \beta_i) > 0\), and assets with \(\beta_i > 1\) earn negative alpha \(\alpha_i = \alpha_0 (1 - \beta_i) < 0\).

2The Leveraged Risk Parity Sharpe Advantage

Because leverage aversion inflates high-beta asset prices and depresses their Sharpe ratios, low-risk assets (such as Treasury bonds and low-beta equities) exhibit systematically higher Sharpe ratios than equities. However, because low-risk assets have low volatility, an unleveraged investment generates modest absolute returns that cannot satisfy institutional return targets (such as 7% nominal pension hurdles).

Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen (2012) demonstrated that resolving this dilemma requires applying moderate leverage to the high-Sharpe risk parity portfolio:

Theorem 3.2 (Leveraged Risk Parity Net Sharpe Ratio Breakeven)

Assumption A1: The unleveraged risk parity portfolio has volatility \(\sigma_{\text{RP}}\), expected excess return \(\mu_{\text{RP}}\), and Sharpe ratio \(\SR_{\text{RP}} = \mu_{\text{RP}} / \sigma_{\text{RP}}\).

Assumption A2: The market portfolio has volatility \(\sigma_m > \sigma_{\text{RP}}\) and Sharpe ratio \(\SR_m = \mu_m / \sigma_m < \SR_{\text{RP}}\).

Assumption A3: The investor borrows capital at a spread \(c_b > 0\) over the risk-free rate: \(R_{\text{borrow}} = R_f + c_b\).

Under Assumptions A1–A3, levering risk parity to match market volatility \(L = \sigma_m / \sigma_{\text{RP}} > 1\) produces a net excess return \(r_{\text{net}} = L \mu_{\text{RP}} - (L - 1) c_b\). The leveraged portfolio strictly outperforms the market in expected return and net Sharpe ratio if and only if the borrowing spread satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} c_b &< (\SR_{\text{RP}} - \SR_m) \frac{\sigma_m}{L - 1} = \frac{\SR_{\text{RP}} - \SR_m}{1 - (\sigma_{\text{RP}}/\sigma_m)} \sigma_{\text{RP}} \end{aligned} \]
Proof

The net excess return of the leveraged risk parity portfolio is \(\mu_{\text{net}} = L \mu_{\text{RP}} - (L - 1) c_b\). The net Sharpe ratio at volatility \(\sigma_{\text{net}} = L \sigma_{\text{RP}} = \sigma_m\) is \(\SR_{\text{net}} = \mu_{\text{net}} / \sigma_m = \frac{L \mu_{\text{RP}} - (L - 1) c_b}{\sigma_m} = \SR_{\text{RP}} - \frac{(L - 1) c_b}{\sigma_m}\). Setting the requirement \(\SR_{\text{net}} > \SR_m\) yields \(\SR_{\text{RP}} - \frac{(L - 1) c_b}{\sigma_m} > \SR_m\). Rearranging gives \(c_b < (\SR_{\text{RP}} - \SR_m) \frac{\sigma_m}{L - 1}\), completing the derivation.

Lab 3 · Leverage Aversion and the Risk Parity Sharpe Advantage

Evaluates Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen (2012) framework. Verifies that an unleveraged risk parity portfolio achieves a higher Sharpe ratio than a 60/40 market benchmark, and that levering risk parity by 1.67x to match equity volatility produces higher net returns and superior Sharpe ratio (+0.14 net Sharpe premium) after paying a 100 bps borrowing spread.

"""Lab 3: Leverage aversion and borrowing constraints: The risk-parity Sharpe advantage.

Demonstrates the theoretical mechanism of Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen (2012):
When investors face borrowing constraints (leverage aversion), high-beta assets
are bid up and have lower Sharpe ratios, while low-risk assets offer higher Sharpe ratios.
Leveraging the balanced risk parity portfolio beats the equity/market portfolio.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    # Asset 1: Low-risk/bonds (vol = 6%, SR = 0.70, exp return = 4.2% over rf)
    # Asset 2: High-risk/equities (vol = 18%, SR = 0.40, exp return = 7.2% over rf)
    # Correlation rho = 0.15, borrow cost spread c_b = 1.0% (100 bps over rf)
    vol1 = 0.06
    vol2 = 0.18
    sr1 = 0.70
    sr2 = 0.40
    rho = 0.15
    borrow_spread = 0.010

    mu1 = sr1 * vol1
    mu2 = sr2 * vol2
    cov12 = rho * vol1 * vol2
    cov = np.array([[vol1**2, cov12], [cov12, vol2**2]], dtype=np.float64)

    # 1. Market portfolio (equity heavy: 60/40 dollar weights)
    w_mkt = np.array([0.40, 0.60], dtype=np.float64)
    mu_mkt = float(w_mkt @ np.array([mu1, mu2]))
    vol_mkt = float(np.sqrt(w_mkt @ cov @ w_mkt))
    sr_mkt = mu_mkt / vol_mkt

    # 2. Unleveraged Risk Parity (ERC weights)
    # For 2 assets: w1 * (cov @ w)_1 = w2 * (cov @ w)_2
    # w_1 / w_2 = vol2 / vol1 when rho = 0
    w_rp_raw = np.array([1.0 / vol1, 1.0 / vol2], dtype=np.float64)
    w_rp = w_rp_raw / np.sum(w_rp_raw)
    mu_rp = float(w_rp @ np.array([mu1, mu2]))
    vol_rp = float(np.sqrt(w_rp @ cov @ w_rp))
    sr_rp = mu_rp / vol_rp

    # 3. Leveraged Risk Parity scaled to match Market Volatility
    leverage = vol_mkt / vol_rp
    w_lev_rp = leverage * w_rp
    gross_ret_lev = float(w_lev_rp @ np.array([mu1, mu2]))
    borrow_cost = (leverage - 1.0) * borrow_spread
    net_ret_lev = gross_ret_lev - borrow_cost
    vol_lev = float(np.sqrt(w_lev_rp @ cov @ w_lev_rp))
    sr_lev_net = net_ret_lev / vol_lev

    print(f"Asset 1 (Bonds) SR: {sr1:.2f}, Asset 2 (Equities) SR: {sr2:.2f}")
    print(f"Market (60/40) Return: {mu_mkt * 100:.2f}%, Vol: {vol_mkt * 100:.2f}%, SR: {sr_mkt:.4f}")
    print(f"Unleveraged RP Return: {mu_rp * 100:.2f}%, Vol: {vol_rp * 100:.2f}%, SR: {sr_rp:.4f}")
    print(f"Required Leverage for Vol Match: {leverage:.2f}x")
    print(f"Leveraged RP Gross Return: {gross_ret_lev * 100:.2f}%, Borrow Drag: {borrow_cost * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Leveraged RP Net Return: {net_ret_lev * 100:.2f}%, Vol: {vol_lev * 100:.2f}%, Net SR: {sr_lev_net:.4f}")
    print(f"Net Sharpe Premium over Market: {sr_lev_net - sr_mkt:+.4f}")


main()
Asset 1 (Bonds) SR: 0.70, Asset 2 (Equities) SR: 0.40
Market (60/40) Return: 6.00%, Vol: 11.41%, SR: 0.5259
Unleveraged RP Return: 4.95%, Vol: 6.82%, SR: 0.7253
Required Leverage for Vol Match: 1.67x
Leveraged RP Gross Return: 8.28%, Borrow Drag: 0.67%
Leveraged RP Net Return: 7.60%, Vol: 11.41%, Net SR: 0.6664
Net Sharpe Premium over Market: +0.1406

The empirical calculation confirms that unleveraged risk parity achieves a Sharpe ratio of 0.7253 compared to 0.5259 for the 60/40 benchmark. When levered 1.67x to match market volatility (11.41%), leveraged risk parity generates 7.60% net excess return versus 6.00% for the market, maintaining a +0.1406 net Sharpe premium after deducting 67 bps of borrowing drag.

3Where the canon breaks

The leveraged risk parity thesis assumes that funding liquidity is permanently accessible and that borrowing costs remain modest and stable. In financial crises, however, repo haircuts expand abruptly, margin calls force rapid liquidation of levered bond holdings, and prime brokers withdraw leverage (Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009). Furthermore, when macroeconomic shocks are stagflationary (as in 1973–1974 and 2022), the stock-bond correlation shifts from negative to strongly positive. In such regimes, levering up fixed income doubles portfolio losses rather than diversifying them, triggering severe drawdowns for leveraged risk parity funds.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Higher systematic risk must always command a higher Sharpe ratio in equilibrium.”

    Classical CAPM asserts that all assets offer the same Sharpe ratio when scaled by correlation to the market. But because borrowing-constrained investors disproportionately bid up high-beta assets, empirical low-beta assets command significantly higher Sharpe ratios than high-beta assets across global equities, Treasuries, and credit markets.

  2. “Applying leverage to a portfolio always increases its risk above unleveraged equities.”

    Risk depends on the volatility of the underlying asset mix. Levering a well-diversified multi-asset risk parity portfolio with 6% volatility by 2.0x produces a portfolio with 12% volatility—substantially less risky than the unleveraged S&P 500 (~18% volatility), with shallower tail drawdowns.

  3. “Financing costs and margin spreads eliminate the entire risk parity Sharpe advantage.”

    As demonstrated in Theorem 3.2 and Lab 3, the borrowing spread threshold required to erase the Sharpe advantage is between 250 and 400 basis points over the risk-free rate. Institutional repo rates and futures implied financing costs typically trade within 20 to 50 basis points of OIS/SOFR, leaving the net alpha robust.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Borrowing Spread Breakeven Calculation

An unleveraged risk parity portfolio has volatility \(\sigma_{\text{RP}} = 7\%\) and Sharpe ratio \(\SR_{\text{RP}} = 0.75\). The equity market benchmark has volatility \(\sigma_m = 16\%\) and Sharpe ratio \(\SR_m = 0.40\). Calculate the required leverage \(L\) to match market volatility and determine the maximum borrowing spread \(c_b^*\) above which the leveraged risk parity portfolio underperforms the market.

Solution

Required leverage is \(L = \sigma_m / \sigma_{\text{RP}} = 0.16 / 0.07 \approx 2.2857\)x. The Sharpe difference is \(\SR_{\text{RP}} - \SR_m = 0.75 - 0.40 = 0.35\). By Theorem 3.2, the breakeven borrowing spread is \(c_b^* = (\SR_{\text{RP}} - \SR_m) \frac{\sigma_m}{L - 1} = 0.35 \times \frac{0.16}{2.2857 - 1.0} = 0.35 \times \frac{0.16}{1.2857} = 0.35 \times 0.12444 \approx 0.04356\) (4.36% or 436 basis points). The leveraged strategy outperforms equities as long as the borrowing spread over cash is below 4.36%.

M2 · Alpha of Low-Beta Asset under Flattened SML

Under Theorem 3.1, suppose the zero-beta excess return is \(\alpha_0 = 3.0\%\) and the market excess return is \(\E[r_m] - R_f = 7.0\%\). Calculate the expected return and CAPM alpha \(\alpha_i\) for an asset with \(\beta = 0.50\) and an asset with \(\beta = 1.50\).

Solution

By Theorem 3.1, \(\E[r_i] - R_f = \alpha_0 (1 - \beta_i) + \beta_i (\E[r_m] - R_f)\). For \(\beta = 0.50\): \(\E[r_{0.5}] - R_f = 0.03(1 - 0.5) + 0.5(0.07) = 0.015 + 0.035 = 0.050\) (5.0%). Standard CAPM predicts \(\beta (\E[r_m] - R_f) = 0.5(0.07) = 0.035\) (3.5%). The alpha is \(\alpha_{0.5} = 5.0\% - 3.5\% = +1.5\%\). For \(\beta = 1.50\): \(\E[r_{1.5}] - R_f = 0.03(1 - 1.5) + 1.5(0.07) = -0.015 + 0.105 = 0.090\) (9.0%). Standard CAPM predicts \(1.5(0.07) = 10.5\%\). The alpha is \(\alpha_{1.5} = 9.0\% - 10.5\% = -1.5\%\). Low-beta assets earn positive alpha; high-beta assets earn negative alpha.

M3 · Net Return of 2x Leveraged Risk Parity

A risk parity fund applies \(L = 2.0\) leverage to a portfolio with expected gross excess return \(\mu_{\text{RP}} = 5.5\%\) and volatility \(\sigma_{\text{RP}} = 7.5\%\). If the risk-free rate is \(R_f = 4.0\%\) and the fund borrows at \(R_{\text{borrow}} = 4.8\%\), compute the gross excess return, borrowing drag, net excess return, and net Sharpe ratio.

Solution

The borrowing spread is \(c_b = 4.8\% - 4.0\% = 0.8\%\) (80 bps). Gross excess return is \(L \mu_{\text{RP}} = 2.0 \times 0.055 = 0.110\) (11.0%). Borrowing drag is \((L - 1) c_b = 1.0 \times 0.008 = 0.008\) (0.8%). Net excess return is \(\mu_{\text{net}} = 11.0\% - 0.8\% = 10.2\%\). Total volatility is \(L \sigma_{\text{RP}} = 2.0 \times 7.5\% = 15.0\%\). Net Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_{\text{net}} = 0.102 / 0.150 = 0.6800\).

P1 · Derivation of the Zero-Beta Rate

In a market with constrained investors whose Lagrange multipliers on the budget constraint average \(\psi\), prove that the zero-beta rate \(R_z\) satisfies \(R_z = R_f + \omega (\psi - R_f)\) and show that the slope of the Security Market Line with respect to beta is strictly less than the market risk premium.

Solution

Aggregating the first-order conditions \(\mu - R_f \iota = \gamma \Sigma w^u\) for unconstrained wealth \((1-\omega)\) and \(\mu - \psi \iota = \gamma \Sigma w^c\) for constrained wealth \(\omega\) yields \(\gamma \Sigma w_m = \mu - [(1-\omega)R_f + \omega \psi] \iota\). Defining the zero-beta rate \(R_z \equiv (1-\omega)R_f + \omega \psi = R_f + \omega (\psi - R_f)\), we have \(\mu - R_z \iota = \gamma \Sigma w_m\). For any asset \(i\), \(\E[r_i] - R_z = \beta_i (\E[r_m] - R_z)\). Expressing relative to \(R_f\): \(\E[r_i] - R_f = (R_z - R_f)(1 - \beta_i) + \beta_i (\E[r_m] - R_f)\). The derivative with respect to \(\beta_i\) is \(\frac{\partial \E[r_i]}{\partial \beta_i} = (\E[r_m] - R_f) - (R_z - R_f)\). Since \(\psi > R_f\) when leverage constraints bind, \(R_z - R_f = \omega (\psi - R_f) > 0\). Thus the slope is \((\E[r_m] - R_f) - \omega (\psi - R_f) < \E[r_m] - R_f\), proving that the empirical Security Market Line is strictly flatter than the CAPM prediction.

P2 · Monotonicity of Sharpe Degradation with Borrowing Costs

Prove that the net Sharpe ratio of a leveraged portfolio \(\SR_{\text{net}}(L) = \SR_0 - \frac{L-1}{L} \frac{c_b}{\sigma_0}\) is strictly decreasing in leverage \(L\) for any positive borrowing spread \(c_b > 0\), and find its asymptotic limit as \(L \to \infty\).

Solution

The net excess return is \(\mu_{\text{net}} = L \mu_0 - (L - 1) c_b\), and volatility is \(\sigma_{\text{net}} = L \sigma_0\). Thus \(\SR_{\text{net}}(L) = \frac{L \mu_0 - (L - 1) c_b}{L \sigma_0} = \frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0} - \frac{L - 1}{L} \frac{c_b}{\sigma_0} = \SR_0 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{L}\right) \frac{c_b}{\sigma_0}\). Differentiating with respect to \(L\): \(\frac{d \SR_{\text{net}}}{dL} = -\frac{1}{L^2} \frac{c_b}{\sigma_0}\). Since \(c_b > 0\), \(\sigma_0 > 0\), and \(L \ge 1\), this derivative is strictly negative (\(< 0\)) for all \(L \ge 1\), proving strict monotonicity. As \(L \to \infty\), \(1 - 1/L \to 1\), so \(\lim_{L \to \infty} \SR_{\text{net}}(L) = \SR_0 - \frac{c_b}{\sigma_0}\).

P3 · Optimality of Risk Parity under Homogeneous Sharpe Ratios

Prove that if all assets have identical Sharpe ratios \(\SR_i = \SR\) and equal pairwise correlation \(\rho_{ij} = \rho > 0\), the Equal Risk Contribution portfolio is mathematically identical to the Markowitz tangency portfolio.

Solution

Under equal Sharpe ratios \(\mu_i = \SR \sigma_i\). The Markowitz tangency portfolio solves \(\Sigma w \propto \mu\), which requires \((\Sigma w)_i \propto \sigma_i\). When \(\rho_{ij} = \rho\), let \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\). Then \((\Sigma w)_i = w_i \sigma_i^2 + \sum_{j \ne i} \rho \sigma_i \sigma_j w_j = \sigma_i [w_i \sigma_i + \rho \sum_{j \ne i} w_j \sigma_j]\). Since \(w_j \propto 1/\sigma_j\), \(w_j \sigma_j = K\) is constant for all \(j\). Thus \((\Sigma w)_i = \sigma_i [K + \rho (N - 1) K] = K [1 + \rho (N - 1)] \sigma_i \propto \sigma_i \propto \mu_i\). Therefore, \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\) satisfies the Markowitz tangency first-order condition \(\Sigma w \propto \mu\). By Theorem 2.2, \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\) also satisfies the Equal Risk Contribution condition \(w_i (\Sigma w)_i = K^2 [1 + \rho (N - 1)]\), which is constant for all \(i\). Thus, the risk parity portfolio coincides with the tangency portfolio.

Empirical

E1 · Historical Sharpe Ratios across Asset Classes

Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen (2012, Table 1) report that over the 1926–2010 period, US Treasuries generated an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.74, while US Equities generated a Sharpe ratio of 0.41. Discuss how this empirical disparity supports the leverage aversion hypothesis.

Solution

Under the classical frictionless CAPM, all assets and efficient portfolios should lie along the capital market line with identical risk-adjusted returns. The dramatic 33 basis point Sharpe ratio advantage of US Treasuries (0.74 vs 0.41) directly confirms the leverage aversion hypothesis. Because benchmark-constrained and leverage-restricted institutions (pension funds, insurance companies) cannot lever up Treasuries to achieve their return targets, they bid up equity prices, compressing the equity risk premium and lowering the realized equity Sharpe ratio. Unleveraged Treasuries remain structurally under-demanded relative to their risk, sustaining an abnormally high Sharpe ratio.

E2 · Margin Call Liquidity Spiral Dynamics

During March 2020, Treasury market liquidity deteriorated and prime brokers raised repo haircuts from 1% to 5%. Explain how a 5x increase in repo haircuts forces a leveraged risk parity fund holding $100M of Treasuries financed at 10x leverage to de-lever, and trace the feedback loop on asset prices.

Solution

At a 1% haircut, $100M of Treasuries requires $1M of equity capital ($99M borrowed). If the haircut is raised to 5%, the fund must post $5M of equity capital. If the fund has only $1M of capital, it can support only \(\$1\text{M} / 0.05 = \$20\text{M}\) of assets, forcing the immediate liquidation of $80M of Treasuries. As multiple leveraged funds dump Treasuries simultaneously to satisfy margin calls, market bid-ask spreads blow out and Treasury yields spike, inflicting capital losses that further erode the funds' equity capital. This self-reinforcing fire-sale spiral was the primary driver of the March 2020 Treasury market dislocation, requiring Federal Reserve intervention via massive open-market purchases.

E3 · Breakeven Financing Analysis for an Institutional Fund

A multi-asset manager can implement risk parity using physical cash securities financed via prime brokerage repo at SOFR + 45 bps, or using Treasury futures and equity index futures at an implied roll cost of SOFR + 20 bps. Evaluate which implementation vehicle provides greater Sharpe margin against the theoretical breakeven threshold of 350 bps.

Solution

Both implementation mechanisms easily clear the 350 bps breakeven threshold. Physical cash borrowing via repo incurs a spread of 45 bps, leaving a net alpha cushion of \(350 - 45 = 305\) bps. Futures implementation incurs only 20 bps of financing drag, leaving an even larger cushion of \(350 - 20 = 330\) bps. Furthermore, exchange-traded futures eliminate bilateral counterparty risk and avoid balance sheet charges under Basel III leverage ratio rules (supplementary leverage ratio, SLR), making futures the dominant vehicle for institutional risk parity implementations.

Research

R1 · Betting Against Beta (BAB) Factor Construction

Frazzini & Pedersen (2014) construct the Betting Against Beta (BAB) factor by going long leveraged low-beta assets and short high-beta assets. Formulate the zero-beta dollar-neutral BAB portfolio weights \(w_{\text{BAB}} = \frac{1}{\beta_L} w_L - \frac{1}{\beta_H} w_H\) and prove that the portfolio has zero market beta.

Solution

Let \(w_L\) and \(w_H\) denote portfolios of low-beta and high-beta assets with market betas \(\beta_L = w_L^\top \beta < 1\) and \(\beta_H = w_H^\top \beta > 1\). To create a zero-beta market-neutral spread, the portfolios are rescaled inversely by their betas: \(w_{\text{BAB}} = \frac{1}{\beta_L} w_L - \frac{1}{\beta_H} w_H\). The market beta of the spread is \(\beta_{\text{BAB}} = w_{\text{BAB}}^\top \beta = \frac{1}{\beta_L} (w_L^\top \beta) - \frac{1}{\beta_H} (w_H^\top \beta) = \frac{\beta_L}{\beta_L} - \frac{\beta_H}{\beta_H} = 1 - 1 = 0\). The long leg holds \(\frac{1}{\beta_L} > 1\) leverage in low-beta assets, and the short leg holds \(\frac{1}{\beta_H} < 1\) in high-beta assets, capturing the alpha spread while hedging systematic market risk.

R2 · Basel III Leverage Ratio Impact on Dealer Intermediation

Post-2008 regulatory reforms introduced the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR), which penalizes banks for total unweighted asset size including cash and Treasuries. Analyze how the SLR increases repo borrowing costs for leveraged risk parity funds during quarter-end reporting periods.

Solution

Under the Basel III Tier 1 leverage ratio, bank holding companies must maintain capital against all on-balance-sheet assets regardless of risk weighting. Financing high-volume, low-margin Treasury repo expands a dealer's balance sheet without generating proportionate return on equity. At quarter-end reporting dates, global systemically important banks (G-SIBs) actively contract their balance sheets to report higher leverage ratios, shrinking repo lending capacity. Consequently, repo borrowing spreads spike substantially at quarter-ends (window dressing), increasing leverage costs for risk parity and hedge funds reliant on short-term dealer financing.

R3 · Macroeconomic Regimes and Risk Parity Tail Risk

Design a dynamic regime-switching risk parity framework that detects shifts between disinflationary growth regimes (\(\rho_{\text{stock, bond}} < 0\)) and stagflationary regimes (\(\rho_{\text{stock, bond}} > 0\)), and specify how leverage should be throttled as correlations turn positive.

Solution

A regime-switching framework uses a hidden Markov model or trailing 60-day realized correlation estimator \(\hat{\rho}_t = \Corr(r_{\text{equity}}, r_{\text{bond}})\). When \(\hat{\rho}_t < 0\), the diversification benefit is strong, and leverage is set to target institutional volatility (\(L_t \approx 1.5 - 2.0\)x). When \(\hat{\rho}_t\) breaches a threshold (e.g. \(\hat{\rho}_t > +0.20\)), the regime probability shifts to stagflation, and leverage is throttled down: \(L_t = \max(1.0, L_{\text{base}} \times [1 - \hat{\rho}_t])\), while capital is reallocated toward physical commodities, energy futures, and short-duration TIPS. Throttling leverage when correlation turns positive prevents catastrophic dual-asset drawdowns.

7Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the primary microeconomic cause of leverage aversion?

    Options

    Leverage aversion stems from structural constraints and institutional prohibitions against borrowing.

  2. How does leverage aversion affect the empirical Security Market Line (SML)?

    Options

    Constrained investors bid up high-beta assets and neglect low-beta assets, flattening the SML.

  3. In Lab 3, what leverage factor was required to scale the unleveraged risk parity portfolio (volatility 6.82%) to match market volatility (11.41%)?

  4. What is the critical borrowing spread condition required for leveraged risk parity to outperform the market?

    Options

    By Theorem 3.2, net Sharpe ratio exceeds market Sharpe ratio as long as the borrowing spread is below the threshold.

  5. Why do US Treasuries historically exhibit a higher Sharpe ratio than US equities?

    Options

    Leverage aversion suppresses demand for low-volatility assets, sustaining an abnormally high Sharpe ratio.

  6. In Lab 3, what was the net Sharpe ratio premium of leveraged risk parity over the market benchmark (to 2 decimal places)?
