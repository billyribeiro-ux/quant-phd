Are volatility-managed portfolios a genuine free lunch, or an artifact of in-sample look-ahead bias and frictionless backtesting? Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan (2020) demonstrated that when the scaling parameter is estimated strictly in real time using out-of-sample expanding windows and realistic transaction costs are deducted, Moreira-Muir alphas are dramatically attenuated. For most cross-sectional anomalies, out-of-sample utility gains to mean-variance investors vanish. However, for momentum and multi-asset index futures where execution costs are low and crash risk is severe, volatility management retains significant economic value. Mastering this critique is essential for designing robust, implementable risk management overlays that survive real-world execution.

1The Out-of-Sample Critique: Look-Ahead Bias & Drift

In Moreira & Muir's (2017) foundational paper, the volatility-scaling constant \(c\) is computed across the entire historical sample: \(c = \operatorname{Std}(f_{1:T}) / \operatorname{Std}(f_{1:T} / \text{RV}_{0:T-1}^2)\). In a live quantitative strategy, an investor at month \(t\) cannot know the unconditional standard deviation of returns over the future decades \([t+1, T]\).

Definition 6.1 (Out-of-Sample Volatility Management Critique)

The out-of-sample critique of volatility management (Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan 2020) states that the statistical significance and economic gains of volatility-managed factor portfolios are substantially reduced when:

  1. The scaling constant \(c_t\) is estimated recursively using strictly past data: \(c_t = \widehat{\operatorname{Std}}(f_{1:t}) / \widehat{\operatorname{Std}}(f_{1:t} / \text{RV}_{0:t-1}^2)\);
  2. Turnover transaction costs \(\kappa |\Delta w_t|\) are deducted from monthly strategy rebalancing;
  3. Investor welfare is evaluated using out-of-sample certainty-equivalent returns rather than in-sample spanning regressions.
Theorem 6.1 (Alpha Attenuation Under OOS Estimation & Turnover Drag)

Assumption A1 (Recursive Estimation): The scaling constant \(c_t\) is estimated using an expanding window of historical observations with burn-in \(T_0 \ge 120\) months.

Assumption A2 (Turnover Cost Friction): Trading incur linear transaction cost \(\kappa > 0\) per unit of dollar turnover: \(\text{TO}_t = |w_t - w_{t-1}(1 + R_t)|\).

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, the net out-of-sample spanning alpha satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_{\text{net}}^{\text{oos}} &< \alpha_{\text{gross}}^{\text{is}} \end{aligned} \]

Specifically, out-of-sample parameter estimation error in \(c_t\) introduces excess tracking variance, while average monthly turnover of 30% to 50% imposes an annual performance drag of \(\Delta \alpha_{\text{cost}} \approx 12 \times \kappa \times \overline{\text{TO}}\).

Proof

In-sample, \(c\) is a deterministic constant that perfectly minimizes sample residual variance. In real time, the recursive estimator \(\hat{c}_t = c + \eta_t\) incorporates estimation noise \(\eta_t\) with variance \(\mathcal{O}(1/t)\). The real-time weight is \(w_t = \hat{c}_t / \text{RV}_t^2 = (c + \eta_t) / \text{RV}_t^2 = w_t^* + \eta_t / \text{RV}_t^2\). The realized managed return is

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{t+1}^{\text{oos}} &= w_t f_{t+1} = w_t^* f_{t+1} + \frac{\eta_t}{\text{RV}_t^2} f_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

The second term adds orthogonal noise with zero mean but positive variance: \(\Var(f^{\text{oos}}) > \Var(f^*)\). This increases the standard error of the spanning intercept. Furthermore, the net return deducts transaction cost \(\kappa \text{TO}_t\). Taking expectations,

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[f_{t+1}^{\text{net}}] &= \E[f_{t+1}^{\text{oos}}] - \kappa\, \E[\text{TO}_t] \end{aligned} \]

Because the spanning alpha is \(\alpha = \E[f_{t+1}] - \beta \E[f_{t+1}^{\text{base}}]\), deducting the positive constant \(\kappa \overline{\text{TO}} > 0\) directly reduces the net alpha by \(12 \times \kappa \times \overline{\text{TO}}\). For average monthly turnover of 40% and a conservative transaction cost of 10 bps, the annual drag is \(12 \times 0.0010 \times 0.40 = 0.0048\) (48 bps). For high-cost anomaly factors with 30 bps costs, the drag exceeds 140 bps, extinguishing the statistical significance of \(\alpha\).

Lab 6 · The Out-of-Sample Volatility Management Critique

Evaluates Cederburg et al. (2020) framework. Estimating the scaling constant c strictly out-of-sample with recursive expanding windows and deducting a 10 bps transaction cost on monthly turnover attenuates the annualized alpha and reduces statistical significance relative to the in-sample Moreira-Muir benchmark.

"""Lab 6: The Out-of-Sample Volatility Management Critique (Cederburg et al. 2020).

Demonstrates the critique of Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan (2020):
When the scaling constant c is estimated strictly out-of-sample using expanding windows
(eliminating look-ahead bias) and rebalancing transaction costs are deducted,
the volatility-management alpha is substantially attenuated.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    # SIMULATED: Multi-decade factor return and realized variance history (480 months / 40 years)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    n_months = 480
    days_per_month = 21
    cost_rate = 0.0010  # 10 bps transaction cost on turnover

    monthly_returns = np.zeros(n_months)
    realized_variance = np.zeros(n_months)

    vol_state = 0.01
    for m in range(n_months):
        daily_rets = np.zeros(days_per_month)
        for d in range(days_per_month):
            z = rng.standard_normal()
            daily_rets[d] = 0.0004 + vol_state * z
            vol_state = max(0.004, min(0.04, 0.95 * vol_state + 0.05 * 0.01 - 0.2 * daily_rets[d]))
        monthly_returns[m] = float(np.sum(daily_rets))
        realized_variance[m] = float(np.sum(daily_rets**2))

    # Evaluation window: burn-in 120 months (10 years) for out-of-sample estimation
    burn_in = 120
    eval_len = n_months - 1 - burn_in

    # 1. Full In-Sample Scaling (Moreira-Muir lookahead benchmark)
    unscaled_all = (1.0 / realized_variance[:-1]) * monthly_returns[1:]
    target_all = monthly_returns[1:]
    c_in_sample = float(np.std(target_all, ddof=1) / np.std(unscaled_all, ddof=1))

    # Evaluate over the evaluation window [burn_in:]
    managed_is = c_in_sample * unscaled_all[burn_in:]
    target_eval = target_all[burn_in:]

    # 2. Strict Out-of-Sample Expanding Window Estimation (Cederburg et al.)
    managed_oos = np.zeros(eval_len)
    w_oos = np.zeros(eval_len)
    w_is = (c_in_sample / realized_variance[:-1])[burn_in:]

    for idx, t in enumerate(range(burn_in, n_months - 1)):
        # Estimate c strictly using past data up to month t-1
        past_target = monthly_returns[1 : t + 1]
        past_unscaled = (1.0 / realized_variance[:t]) * monthly_returns[1 : t + 1]
        c_t = float(np.std(past_target, ddof=1) / np.std(past_unscaled, ddof=1))
        # Weight for month t+1 chosen at month t
        w_t = c_t / realized_variance[t]
        w_oos[idx] = w_t
        managed_oos[idx] = w_t * monthly_returns[t + 1]

    # 3. Transaction Costs Deduction
    # Monthly turnover: |w_t - w_{t-1} * (1 + r_t)|
    turnover_is = np.zeros(eval_len)
    turnover_oos = np.zeros(eval_len)
    for i in range(1, eval_len):
        turnover_is[i] = abs(w_is[i] - w_is[i - 1] * (1.0 + target_eval[i - 1]))
        turnover_oos[i] = abs(w_oos[i] - w_oos[i - 1] * (1.0 + target_eval[i - 1]))

    managed_oos_net = managed_oos - cost_rate * turnover_oos

    # Spanning regressions: strategy = alpha + beta * target_eval
    def run_regression(y: np.ndarray, x_in: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
        x = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x_in)), x_in])
        b_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(x, y, rcond=None)[0]
        alpha = float(b_hat[0])
        res = y - x @ b_hat
        s2 = float(np.sum(res**2) / (len(y) - 2))
        se = float(np.sqrt(s2 * np.linalg.inv(x.T @ x)[0, 0]))
        return alpha * 12.0, alpha / se, float(b_hat[1])

    alpha_is, t_is, _beta_is = run_regression(managed_is, target_eval)
    alpha_oos, t_oos, _beta_oos = run_regression(managed_oos, target_eval)
    alpha_oos_net, t_oos_net, _beta_oos_net = run_regression(managed_oos_net, target_eval)

    sr_base = float(np.mean(target_eval) / np.std(target_eval, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))
    sr_is = float(np.mean(managed_is) / np.std(managed_is, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))
    sr_oos = float(np.mean(managed_oos) / np.std(managed_oos, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))
    sr_oos_net = float(np.mean(managed_oos_net) / np.std(managed_oos_net, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))

    print(f"Base Factor Sharpe: {sr_base:.4f}")
    print(f"In-Sample Managed Alpha: {alpha_is * 100:.2f}%, t-stat: {t_is:.2f}, SR: {sr_is:.4f}")
    print(f"Out-of-Sample Managed Alpha (Gross): {alpha_oos * 100:.2f}%, t-stat: {t_oos:.2f}, SR: {sr_oos:.4f}")
    print(
        f"Out-of-Sample Managed Alpha (Net 10bps): {alpha_oos_net * 100:.2f}%, "
        f"t-stat: {t_oos_net:.2f}, SR: {sr_oos_net:.4f}"
    )
    print(f"Average Monthly Turnover (OOS): {float(np.mean(turnover_oos[1:])) * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Alpha Attenuation (IS to Net OOS): {(alpha_is - alpha_oos_net) * 100:.2f}% percentage points")


main()

3Debate Brief: Are Volatility-Managed Portfolios a Free Lunch?

1. Resolution

Resolved: Volatility-managed portfolios generate genuine, implementable economic alpha that delivers superior out-of-sample risk-adjusted returns to long-term investors after accounting for real-world transaction costs and parameter uncertainty.

2. Strongest Case for Volatility Management

Proponents (Moreira & Muir 2017, Harvey et al. 2018) emphasize that volatility clustering is an overwhelming structural feature of financial markets. Because the risk-return tradeoff is empirically weak, taking constant dollar risk during crises destroys wealth via variance drag. By cutting exposure when realized variance spikes, systematic volatility targeting compresses return kurtosis by 30% to 50% and truncates maximum drawdowns by 20% to 40% across equities, Treasuries, commodities, and currencies (Harvey et al. 2018). Crucially, in modern institutional execution, volatility targeting is implemented via liquid futures contracts (e.g., E-mini S&P, 10Y Note futures) where bid-ask spreads are under 1 basis point, making turnover costs negligible and preserving the documented Sharpe ratio expansion.

3. Strongest Case Against Volatility Management (The Cederburg Critique)

Critics (Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan 2020) demonstrate that the headline Moreira-Muir alphas are heavily flattered by in-sample look-ahead bias and frictionless paper trading (Cederburg et al. 2020). When the scaling constant \(c\) is estimated out-of-sample in real time, parameter drift increases tracking variance. For physical cash equity anomalies (Value, Size, Profitability, Investment), monthly turnover averages 40% to 60%, and real-world trading costs of 10 to 30 bps completely consume the alpha. Furthermore, mean-variance utility gains out-of-sample are statistically indistinguishable from zero for all factors except Momentum. Therefore, volatility management is not a general asset pricing free lunch, but rather a narrow tail-risk tool that fails in many implementations.

5. Pragmatic Synthesis

The academic debate resolves into an engineering distinction between asset selection and execution vehicle:

  • Where Vol Management Fails: Physical cash equity anomaly factor portfolios (Value, Size, Quality) where turnover is high, shorting costs are steep, and borrow availability is restricted. In these domains, Cederburg et al.'s critique holds decisively.
  • Where Vol Management Succeeds: Broad macro index allocations (equities, bonds, commodities) executed via ultra-liquid exchange-traded futures, and Momentum strategies where crash avoidance is indispensable. When paired with buffer bands (\(\pm 5\%\)) to control turnover, volatility management delivers robust tail-risk protection.

4Where the canon breaks

Academic asset pricing often treats trading strategies as costless mathematical abstractions, evaluating performance using in-sample spanning regressions and full-sample normalization constants. Cederburg et al. (2020) shattered this academic complacency by establishing that real-world frictions—out-of-sample parameter estimation, recursive updating, and transaction cost drag—can erase theoretical anomalies.

A strategy with a +4% in-sample paper alpha can easily deliver zero or negative net alpha in live trading if monthly turnover exceeds 30% and execution slippage is ignored. Quantitative portfolio managers must never deploy risk-scaling algorithms without rigorous out-of-sample cross-validation and explicit friction accounting.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The Cederburg critique proves volatility targeting is completely useless.”

    The Cederburg critique specifically refutes the claim that volatility management is a universal source of alpha across all equity anomalies. It does not refute the proven tail-risk truncation, kurtosis reduction, and drawdown mitigation achieved by volatility targeting on macro asset classes and index futures (Harvey et al. 2018).

  2. “Transaction costs can be eliminated by using daily volatility targeting instead of monthly.”

    Daily volatility targeting increases rebalancing frequency, which dramatically multiplies annualized turnover and transaction costs unless buffered. Weekly or monthly rebalancing with a \(\pm 5\%\) to \(\pm 10\%\) buffer band strikes the optimal balance between tracking conditional volatility and suppressing transaction friction.

  3. “Momentum volatility management is also completely debunked by Cederburg et al.”

    Even under Cederburg et al.'s strict out-of-sample evaluation and transaction cost deductions, Momentum retains a large and statistically significant net alpha (> +5.0% p.a., \(t > 3.0\)). The extreme asymmetry of momentum crashes makes volatility management uniquely valuable for trend and momentum factors.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Transaction Cost Drag Calculation

A volatility-managed portfolio generates an average monthly turnover of \(\overline{\text{TO}} = 35\%\). If the strategy executes physical equities with a one-way transaction cost of \(\kappa = 12\) basis points, calculate the annualized performance drag in percent.

Solution

Monthly transaction drag is \(\text{Cost}_{\text{month}} = \kappa \times \overline{\text{TO}} = 0.0012 \times 0.35 = 0.00042\) (4.2 bps per month). Annualizing across 12 months: \(\text{Cost}_{\text{annual}} = 12 \times 4.2 \text{ bps} = 50.4\) basis points (0.504% per year). If the gross alpha was 1.20%, transaction costs erase 42% of the alpha.

M2 · Expanding Window Recursive Scaling Constant

Over a 10-year burn-in period (120 months), the standard deviation of monthly returns was \(\hat{\sigma}_f = 4.20\%\), and the standard deviation of unscaled managed returns \((f_{t+1}/\text{RV}_t^2)\) was \(\hat{\sigma}_u = 1.40\). At month 121, realized variance is \(\text{RV}_{120}^2 = 0.0030\). Compute the recursive constant \(\hat{c}_{120}\) and the prescribed factor weight \(w_{121}\).

Solution

The recursive constant is \(\hat{c}_{120} = \hat{\sigma}_f / \hat{\sigma}_u = 0.0420 / 1.40 = 0.0300\). The weight for month 121 is \(w_{121} = \hat{c}_{120} / \text{RV}_{120}^2 = 0.0300 / 0.0030 = 10.0\). (With practical leverage capping, this weight would be clipped to the maximum allowed limit, e.g., 2.0x).

M3 · Breakeven Execution Cost

An out-of-sample volatility-managed Value strategy achieves a gross annual alpha of \(\alpha_{\text{gross}} = +1.10\%\) with an annual turnover of \(480\%\). Determine the maximum one-way transaction cost \(\kappa^*\) above which the strategy delivers negative net alpha.

Solution

Net alpha is \(\alpha_{\text{net}} = \alpha_{\text{gross}} - \kappa \times \text{TO}_{\text{annual}}\). Setting \(\alpha_{\text{net}} = 0\): \(\kappa^* = \alpha_{\text{gross}} / \text{TO}_{\text{annual}} = 0.0110 / 4.80 \approx 0.00229\) (22.9 basis points). Any transaction cost exceeding 23 bps renders the strategy unprofitable.

Proof

P1 · Estimation Variance of Expanding Window Multiplier

Let \(\hat{c}_t = c (1 + \delta_t)\) where \(\delta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\delta^2 / t)\) is estimation error in the recursive scaling constant. Prove that the out-of-sample managed return variance exceeds the in-sample managed return variance by \(\frac{\sigma_\delta^2}{t} \E[w^{*2} f^2]\), demonstrating excess tracking dispersion.

Solution

The out-of-sample return is \(f_{t+1}^{\text{oos}} = \hat{c}_t \frac{f_{t+1}}{\text{RV}_t^2} = c(1 + \delta_t) \frac{f_{t+1}}{\text{RV}_t^2} = f_{t+1}^* + \delta_t f_{t+1}^*\). Because estimation error \(\delta_t\) is determined by past data up to \(t\) and is independent of future return innovation \(\epsilon_{t+1}\), \(\E[\delta_t f_{t+1}^*] = 0\). The variance is \(\Var(f_{t+1}^{\text{oos}}) = \Var(f_{t+1}^*) + \Var(\delta_t f_{t+1}^*) + 2 \Cov(f_{t+1}^*, \delta_t f_{t+1}^*)\). The covariance term is zero by independence. Thus \(\Var(f_{t+1}^{\text{oos}}) = \Var(f_{t+1}^*) + \E[\delta_t^2] \E[(f_{t+1}^*)^2] = \Var(f_{t+1}^*) + \frac{\sigma_\delta^2}{t} \E[(f_{t+1}^*)^2]\). Since \(\sigma_\delta^2 > 0\), the out-of-sample return has strictly higher variance, lowering its Sharpe ratio.

P2 · Bound on Turnover Under Inverse-Variance Scaling

Prove that for the scaling rule \(w_t = c / \sigma_t^2\), the expected turnover \(\E[|\Delta w_t|] \ge c \frac{|\E[\Delta \sigma_t^2]|}{\E[\sigma_t^2 \sigma_{t-1}^2]}\), establishing that turnover is directly driven by the high volatility-of-volatility in financial markets.

Solution

The weight difference is \(\Delta w_t = w_t - w_{t-1} = \frac{c}{\sigma_t^2} - \frac{c}{\sigma_{t-1}^2} = c \frac{\sigma_{t-1}^2 - \sigma_t^2}{\sigma_t^2 \sigma_{t-1}^2} = -c \frac{\Delta \sigma_t^2}{\sigma_t^2 \sigma_{t-1}^2}\). Taking absolute values, \(|\Delta w_t| = c \frac{|\Delta \sigma_t^2|}{\sigma_t^2 \sigma_{t-1}^2}\). By Jensen's inequality and properties of convex norms, \(\E[|\Delta w_t|] \ge c \frac{|\E[\Delta \sigma_t^2]|}{\E[\sigma_t^2 \sigma_{t-1}^2]}\). Therefore, turnover is bounded from below by the volatility innovations of the underlying process. High volatility-of-volatility inevitably creates high strategy turnover.

P3 · Asymptotic Equivalence of Buffer Bands and Inaction Regions

Prove that applying a band \([w_{t-1}(1-\delta), w_{t-1}(1+\delta)]\) to a continuous stochastic control problem with proportional transaction costs produces an optimal inaction region with boundary \(\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{1/3})\), referencing Dixit (1991) and Shreve & Soner (1994).

Solution

In continuous time, let portfolio deviation be \(x_t = w_t - w_t^*\). The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation inside the continuation region satisfies \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma_x^2 V''(x) - \lambda x^2 = 0\), which yields a quadratic penalty. At the trading boundaries \(\pm \bar{x}\), the smooth pasting condition requires \(V'(\bar{x}) = \kappa\) and \(V''(\bar{x}) = 0\). Taylor expanding \(V'(x)\) around 0 gives \(V'(x) \approx V'''(0) x^3 / 6\). Setting \(V'(\bar{x}) = \kappa\) implies \(\bar{x}^3 \propto \kappa \implies \bar{x} = C \kappa^{1/3}\). This proves that the optimal buffer width scales with the cube root of proportional transaction cost \(\kappa^{1/3}\), confirming that a small buffer band drastically suppresses turnover without material tracking error.

Empirical

E1 · Momentum Exemption in Cederburg et al.

Explain why Momentum survives the Cederburg critique with a statistically significant net alpha (> +5.0% p.a.), while Value and Size fail completely.

Solution

Momentum suffers from rare, catastrophic left-tail crashes (e.g., 1932, 2009) where the factor loses 70%+ in a matter of months. Because momentum crashes are preceded by massive volatility spikes, volatility management de-levers momentum almost completely before the worst losses hit. The gross alpha for momentum (+8.8%) is large enough that even after losing 2.6% to out-of-sample estimation noise and 1.0% to turnover costs, the remaining +5.2% net alpha easily surpasses hurdle rates. Value and Size, by contrast, offer small gross alphas (+2% to +3%) that are easily wiped out by turnover drag.

E2 · Futures vs Physical Equity Implementation

Compare the round-trip transaction costs of implementing volatility targeting on the S&P 500 using E-mini futures (tick size 0.25 pt, bid-ask 1 tick, clearing fee $0.25/contract) versus physical stock basket replication (500 stocks, market impact, SEC fees).

Solution

E-mini S&P futures trade at $50 \times \text{index}$. At index 5,000, one contract is $250,000. Bid-ask spread of 0.25 index points is $12.50. Exchange/brokerage fees are ~$2.50 per contract. Total one-way cost is \(\$15.00 / \$250,000 = 0.00006\) (0.6 basis points). Physical stock replication across 500 names involves odd lots, crossing bid-ask spreads averaging 3–8 bps, and exchange fees, totaling 5 to 10 bps. Futures execution is over 10x cheaper, ensuring volatility targeting retains virtually all its gross performance.

E3 · Out-of-Sample Failure of Value Factor Timing

Discuss the empirical findings of Asness (2016) and Cederburg et al. (2020) regarding factor timing on HML. Why does timing Value exposure with realized volatility fail to expand investor utility out-of-sample?

Solution

The Value premium (HML) has long drawdown regimes that do not coincide cleanly with aggregate equity volatility spikes. Often, Value underperforms during tranquil tech bubbles (such as 1998–2000 and 2017–2020) when realized volatility is low. Scaling into Value during calm regimes resulted in overexposure during Value drawdowns, while scaling out during crises missed rapid value rebounds. Combined with turnover friction, out-of-sample utility gains are statistically indistinguishable from zero.

Research

R1 · Bayesian Learning for Real-Time Parameter Scaling

Formulate a Bayesian hierarchical model for the scaling parameter \(c_t\) with prior \(c_0 \sim \mathcal{G}(a_0, b_0)\) updated by monthly realized variance observations. Show how shrinkage prevents wild parameter drift during the initial burn-in period.

Solution

Let precision \(\tau = 1/c\) follow a conjugate Gamma prior \(\tau \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)\). At month \(t\), observing realized variance and return pairs \(\{(\text{RV}_s^2, f_{s+1})\}_{s=1}^t\), the posterior update is \(\tau_t \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(a_0 + t/2, b_0 + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{s=1}^t (\dots))\). The posterior mean is \(\hat{c}_t = \frac{b_0 + \text{data}}{a_0 + t/2} = \omega_t c_{\text{prior}} + (1 - \omega_t) c_{\text{sample}}\) where \(\omega_t = \frac{a_0}{a_0 + t/2}\). For small \(t\) (early in the sample), \(\omega_t \approx 1\), anchoring the parameter to the informative prior and preventing the catastrophic sample variance drift documented by Cederburg et al.

R2 · Volatility Targeting vs Moreira-Muir Variance Scaling

Contrast the mathematical sensitivity of volatility targeting \(w_t = \sigma^* / \sigma_t\) versus Moreira-Muir scaling \(w_t = c / \sigma_t^2\). Prove that Moreira-Muir scaling exhibits twice the turnover elasticity with respect to volatility shocks, explaining its higher trading friction.

Solution

Consider the derivative of weight with respect to volatility \(\sigma_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Vol Targeting: } & \frac{dw}{d\sigma} = -\frac{\sigma^*}{\sigma_t^2} \implies \frac{dw / w}{d\sigma / \sigma} = -1 \\ \text{Moreira-Muir: } & \frac{dw}{d\sigma} = -\frac{2c}{\sigma_t^3} \implies \frac{dw / w}{d\sigma / \sigma} = -2 \end{aligned} \]

The elasticity of exposure to volatility changes is \(-1\) for volatility targeting and \(-2\) for Moreira-Muir variance scaling. A doubling of volatility cuts exposure by 50% under volatility targeting, but by 75% under variance scaling. Consequently, Moreira-Muir generates approximately twice the turnover of linear volatility targeting, making it substantially more vulnerable to transaction cost friction.

R3 · Execution via Variance Swaps and Volatility Futures

Propose an implementation architecture where portfolio exposure is dynamically hedged using VIX futures or equity variance swaps rather than rebalancing the physical underlying assets. Formulate the hedge ratio to neutralize excess variance without incurring cash turnover.

Solution

Instead of trading the physical portfolio of equities, maintain static 100% long exposure and overlay a short variance swap or long VIX futures position. To match target variance \((\sigma^*)^2\), enter a short variance swap with notional \(N_{\text{var}} = \hat{\sigma}_t^2 - (\sigma^*)^2\) when \(\hat{\sigma}_t > \sigma^*\). The variance swap payoff \([(\sigma^*)^2 - \text{RV}_{t+1}^2]\) offsets portfolio drawdown during volatility spikes. Because variance swaps are cash-settled contracts with zero principal turnover, physical portfolio transaction costs are completely eliminated, resolving the Cederburg critique.
