Traditional asset allocation targets capital proportions, such as the ubiquitous 60/40 equity/bond blend. However, because asset volatilities differ by multiples, capital allocations create extreme imbalances in actual risk. In a 60/40 portfolio, equities typically generate over 85% of total portfolio variance, exposing the investor to catastrophic equity market drawdowns despite nominal diversification. Risk budgeting resolves this fundamental flaw by decomposing portfolio volatility through Euler's homogeneous function theorem, allowing asset managers to allocate risk explicitly across asset classes.

1Euler Decomposition of Portfolio Volatility

Consider a portfolio of \(N \ge 2\) risky assets with weights \(w = (w_1, \dots, w_N)^\top \in \R^N\) and symmetric positive definite covariance matrix \(\Sigma \in \R^{N \times N}\). Portfolio variance is \(\sigma_p^2(w) = w^\top \Sigma w\), and portfolio standard deviation (volatility) is \(\sigma_p(w) = \sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}\).

To understand how each asset contributes to overall portfolio risk, we examine the mathematical homogeneity of the volatility function. A function \(f: \R^N \to \R\) is linearly homogeneous (homogeneous of degree 1) if for any scalar \(c > 0\), \(f(c w) = c f(w)\). Standard deviation satisfies \(\sigma_p(c w) = \sqrt{(c w)^\top \Sigma (c w)} = c \sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w} = c \sigma_p(w)\).

Theorem 1.1 (Euler's Homogeneous Function Theorem on Volatility)

Assumption A1 (Positive Definite Covariance): The covariance matrix \(\Sigma \in \R^{N \times N}\) is symmetric and strictly positive definite.

Assumption A2 (Non-Zero Portfolio): The weight vector \(w\) satisfies \(w^\top \Sigma w > 0\).

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, portfolio volatility \(\sigma_p(w)\) admits an exact additive decomposition without residual:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_p(w) &= \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w_i} \end{aligned} \]
Proof

By Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions of degree \(k = 1\), differentiating the identity \(\sigma_p(c w) = c \sigma_p(w)\) with respect to \(c\) at \(c = 1\) yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dc} \sigma_p(cw) \Big|_{c=1} &= \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial (cw_i)} \frac{\partial (cw_i)}{\partial c} \Big|_{c=1} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w_i} \end{aligned} \]

Evaluating the right-hand side of \(\frac{d}{dc} [c \sigma_p(w)] = \sigma_p(w)\) at \(c = 1\) yields \(\sigma_p(w) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w_i}\), completing the proof.

Definition 1.1 (Marginal, Component, and Percent Risk Contributions)

Let \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}\) be the portfolio volatility under Assumptions A1 and A2.

  1. Marginal Risk Contribution (MRC): The partial derivative of portfolio volatility with respect to asset weight \(w_i\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{MRC}_i \equiv \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w_i} = \frac{(\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma_p} = \frac{\Cov(r_i, r_p)}{\sigma_p} \end{aligned} \]
  2. Total Risk Contribution (TRC): The component risk contribution of asset \(i\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{TRC}_i \equiv w_i \cdot \mathrm{MRC}_i = \frac{w_i (\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma_p} \end{aligned} \] satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^N \mathrm{TRC}_i = \sigma_p\).
  3. Percent Risk Contribution (PRC): The normalized share of total portfolio volatility contributed by asset \(i\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{PRC}_i \equiv \frac{\mathrm{TRC}_i}{\sigma_p} = \frac{w_i (\Sigma w)_i}{w^\top \Sigma w} \end{aligned} \] satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^N \mathrm{PRC}_i = 1.0\).
  4. Risk Budgeting: The portfolio construction policy specifying a non-negative target risk vector \(b = (b_1, \dots, b_N)^\top\) with \(b_i > 0\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^N b_i = 1\), requiring \(\mathrm{PRC}_i(w) = b_i\) for all \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\).
Remark 1.1 (Negative Marginal Risk Contribution and Safe-Haven Hedging)

While portfolio weights \(w_i\) may be strictly positive, \(\mathrm{MRC}_i = (\Sigma w)_i / \sigma_p\) can be negative if asset \(i\) exhibits strong negative covariance with the remaining assets such that \(\sum_{j=1}^N \Sigma_{ij} w_j < 0\). For such an asset, expanding its portfolio weight reduces overall portfolio volatility (\(\partial \sigma_p / \partial w_i < 0\)), reflecting its role as a volatility hedge.

2The Equity Risk Concentration Paradox

The standard multi-asset portfolio held by institutional endowments, pension funds, and wealth managers allocates 60% of capital to equities and 40% to fixed income. Consider an equity asset with annualized volatility \(\sigma_e = 18\%\) and a bond index with \(\sigma_b = 6\%\), with correlation \(\rho = 0.10\).

Example 1.1 (Risk Concentration in a 60/40 Portfolio)

Evaluating the 60/40 allocation \(w = (0.60, 0.40)^\top\) with covariance matrix

\[ \begin{aligned} \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 0.0324 & 0.00108 \\ 0.00108 & 0.0036 \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

The portfolio variance is \(w^\top \Sigma w = 0.6^2(0.0324) + 0.4^2(0.0036) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.00108) = 0.012758\), so \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.012758} \approx 11.29\%\). The total risk contributions are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{TRC}_e &= \frac{0.60 \times [0.60(0.0324) + 0.40(0.00108)]}{0.11295} \approx 0.1057 \\ \mathrm{TRC}_b &= \frac{0.40 \times [0.60(0.00108) + 0.40(0.0036)]}{0.11295} \approx 0.0074 \end{aligned} \]

The percent risk contributions are \(\mathrm{PRC}_e = 0.1057 / 0.11295 = 93.5\%\) and \(\mathrm{PRC}_b = 0.0074 / 0.11295 = 6.5\%\). Despite holding 40% in bonds, over 93% of the portfolio's total volatility is driven entirely by equity market fluctuations.

Lab 1 · Euler Risk Decomposition and Marginal Risk Contributions

Verifies Euler's homogeneous function theorem on a 3-asset multi-asset portfolio (Equities, Bonds, Commodities). Demonstrates that component risk contributions TRC_i sum exactly to portfolio volatility sigma_p, and shows the severe equity risk concentration of a nominal 50/40/10 capital allocation.

"""Lab 1: Euler risk decomposition and marginal risk contributions.

Demonstrates Euler's homogeneous function theorem on portfolio volatility:
sigma_p = sum(w_i * MRC_i) = sum(TRC_i), and sum(PRC_i) = 1.0.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    # 3-asset covariance matrix: Equities (18%), Bonds (6%), Commodities (15%)
    cov = np.array(
        [
            [0.0324, 0.0020, 0.0050],
            [0.0020, 0.0036, -0.0010],
            [0.0050, -0.0010, 0.0225],
        ],
        dtype=np.float64,
    )

    # Traditional 60/40 balanced asset allocation with commodities (50% eq, 40% fi, 10% com)
    weights = np.array([0.50, 0.40, 0.10], dtype=np.float64)

    port_var = float(weights @ cov @ weights)
    port_vol = float(np.sqrt(port_var))

    # Marginal risk contribution: d(sigma_p)/dw_i = (Sigma @ w)_i / sigma_p
    mrc = (cov @ weights) / port_vol

    # Total (component) risk contribution: TRC_i = w_i * MRC_i
    trc = weights * mrc

    # Percent risk contribution: PRC_i = TRC_i / sigma_p
    prc = trc / port_vol

    print(f"Portfolio Volatility: {port_vol:.6f}")
    print(f"Weights: {[round(float(x), 4) for x in weights]}")
    print(f"MRC: {[round(float(x), 6) for x in mrc]}")
    print(f"TRC: {[round(float(x), 6) for x in trc]}")
    print(f"PRC: {[round(float(x), 6) for x in prc]}")
    print(f"Euler sum(TRC): {float(np.sum(trc)):.6f}")
    print(f"Sum PRC: {float(np.sum(prc)):.6f}")
    print(f"Equity Risk Share: {prc[0] * 100:.2f}%")


main()

The empirical calculation demonstrates that while equities represent 50% of invested capital, they generate 86.45% of total portfolio volatility. The component risk contributions sum exactly to total portfolio volatility (0.100603), verifying Euler's theorem to floating-point precision.

3Where the canon breaks

Euler's risk decomposition assumes that the underlying risk metric is linearly homogeneous and continuously differentiable with respect to asset weights. When portfolios hold nonlinear derivatives (options, structured credit, mortgage-backed securities with prepayment options), portfolio volatility ceases to capture tail risk. For non-differentiable or tail-dependent risk measures such as historical Value-at-Risk (VaR), marginal risk contributions can become ill-defined or unstable. Furthermore, in severe market crises, empirical correlations between equities and commodities spike dramatically toward unity, breaking historical covariance estimates and concentrating risk even further.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A 60/40 capital allocation provides balanced risk exposure between stocks and bonds.”

    Because equities exhibit 2.5 to 3.5 times the volatility of bonds, risk contribution scales quadratically with asset standard deviation. In a 60/40 portfolio, equities generate between 85% and 94% of total variance, leaving the portfolio almost entirely unhedged against equity bear markets as documented by Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen (2012).

  2. “Risk budgeting is identical to inverse-volatility weighting.”

    Inverse-volatility weighting \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\) ignores off-diagonal pairwise correlations \(\rho_{ij}\). It achieves equal risk contributions if and only if all pairwise correlations are zero or uniform. When correlations vary across asset pairs, inverse-volatility allocations diverge materially from true risk budgets.

  3. “Marginal risk contributions must always be strictly positive for non-negative asset weights.”

    An asset that is negatively correlated with the rest of the portfolio (such as long-duration Treasuries or gold during flight-to-safety episodes) can have a negative marginal risk contribution \((\Sigma w)_i < 0\). Increasing the weight of such an asset lowers overall portfolio risk.

M1 · Two-Asset Marginal Risk Calculation

Consider a 2-asset universe with \(\sigma_1 = 0.10\), \(\sigma_2 = 0.20\), and correlation \(\rho = 0.25\). Calculate the portfolio volatility \(\sigma_p\), marginal risk contributions \(\mathrm{MRC}_1\) and \(\mathrm{MRC}_2\), and percent risk contributions \(\mathrm{PRC}_1\) and \(\mathrm{PRC}_2\) for an equal capital weighting \(w = (0.5, 0.5)^\top\).

Solution

The covariance matrix is \(\Sigma_{11} = 0.0100\), \(\Sigma_{22} = 0.0400\), and \(\Sigma_{12} = 0.25 \times 0.10 \times 0.20 = 0.0050\). Portfolio variance is \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.5^2(0.01) + 0.5^2(0.04) + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.005) = 0.0025 + 0.0100 + 0.0025 = 0.0150\). Volatility is \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0150} \approx 0.12247\). The marginal risk contributions are \(\mathrm{MRC}_1 = (0.5 \times 0.01 + 0.5 \times 0.005) / 0.12247 = 0.0075 / 0.12247 \approx 0.06124\), and \(\mathrm{MRC}_2 = (0.5 \times 0.005 + 0.5 \times 0.04) / 0.12247 = 0.0225 / 0.12247 \approx 0.18371\). Total risk contributions are \(\mathrm{TRC}_1 = 0.5 \times 0.06124 = 0.03062\) and \(\mathrm{TRC}_2 = 0.5 \times 0.18371 = 0.09186\). Sum: \(\mathrm{TRC}_1 + \mathrm{TRC}_2 = 0.12248 \approx \sigma_p\). Percent contributions: \(\mathrm{PRC}_1 = 0.03062 / 0.12247 = 25.0\%\) and \(\mathrm{PRC}_2 = 0.09186 / 0.12247 = 75.0\%\).

M2 · Euler Verification on Three Assets

A portfolio holds weights \(w = (0.30, 0.40, 0.30)^\top\) in assets with covariance matrix diagonal \((0.04, 0.09, 0.16)\) and all pairwise correlations \(\rho = 0\). Compute \(\mathrm{TRC}_i\) for all three assets and verify that they sum to \(\sigma_p\).

Solution

With zero correlations, \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.3^2(0.04) + 0.4^2(0.09) + 0.3^2(0.16) = 0.0036 + 0.0144 + 0.0144 = 0.0324\), so \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0324} = 0.1800\). The marginal risk contributions are \(\mathrm{MRC}_i = w_i \sigma_i^2 / \sigma_p\): \(\mathrm{MRC}_1 = 0.3(0.04) / 0.18 = 0.012 / 0.18 = 0.0667\); \(\mathrm{MRC}_2 = 0.4(0.09) / 0.18 = 0.036 / 0.18 = 0.2000\); \(\mathrm{MRC}_3 = 0.3(0.16) / 0.18 = 0.048 / 0.18 = 0.2667\). Component contributions: \(\mathrm{TRC}_1 = 0.3 \times 0.0667 = 0.0200\); \(\mathrm{TRC}_2 = 0.4 \times 0.2000 = 0.0800\); \(\mathrm{TRC}_3 = 0.3 \times 0.2667 = 0.0800\). Sum of TRC: \(0.0200 + 0.0800 + 0.0800 = 0.1800 = \sigma_p\), exactly verifying Euler's theorem.

M3 · Hedge Asset with Negative Marginal Risk

An equity portfolio with variance 0.04 (\(\sigma_p = 0.20\)) considers adding a safe-haven asset with variance 0.01 and correlation \(\rho = -0.50\) with equities. If the current portfolio weight in the hedge is \(w_h = 0.10\) and in equities is \(w_e = 0.90\), calculate \(\mathrm{MRC}_h\) and determine if increasing the hedge lowers portfolio risk.

Solution

Covariance between equities and the hedge is \(\Sigma_{eh} = -0.50 \times 0.20 \times 0.10 = -0.0100\). Portfolio variance is \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.9^2(0.04) + 0.1^2(0.01) + 2(0.9)(0.1)(-0.01) = 0.0324 + 0.0001 - 0.0018 = 0.0307\), so \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0307} \approx 0.1752\). Marginal risk contribution of the hedge is \(\mathrm{MRC}_h = [w_h \Sigma_{hh} + w_e \Sigma_{eh}] / \sigma_p = [0.1(0.01) + 0.9(-0.01)] / 0.1752 = [0.001 - 0.009] / 0.1752 = -0.008 / 0.1752 \approx -0.04565\). Because \(\mathrm{MRC}_h < 0\), increasing the weight in the hedge reduces total portfolio volatility.

P1 · General Homogeneity of Degree k

Prove Euler's theorem for an arbitrary differentiable function \(f: \R^N \to \R\) that is homogeneous of degree \(k\), and deduce that for portfolio variance \(\sigma_p^2(w)\) (which has \(k=2\)), \(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i \frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial w_i} = 2 \sigma_p^2\).

Solution

By definition, \(f(c w) = c^k f(w)\) for all \(c > 0\). Differentiating both sides with respect to \(c\) using the multivariate chain rule gives \(\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{\partial f(cw)}{\partial (cw_i)} \frac{\partial (cw_i)}{\partial c} = k c^{k-1} f(w)\). Simplifying the left-hand derivative gives \(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i \frac{\partial f(cw)}{\partial (cw_i)} = k c^{k-1} f(w)\). Evaluating at \(c = 1\) yields \(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i \frac{\partial f(w)}{\partial w_i} = k f(w)\). For portfolio variance \(\sigma_p^2(w) = w^\top \Sigma w\), \(f(c w) = c^2 w^\top \Sigma w = c^2 \sigma_p^2(w)\), so \(k = 2\). Hence \(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i \frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial w_i} = 2 \sigma_p^2(w)\).

P2 · Equivalence of Volatility and Variance Percent Risk Contributions

Prove that the percent risk contribution defined via volatility \(\mathrm{PRC}_i^{(\sigma)} = \frac{w_i (\partial \sigma_p / \partial w_i)}{\sigma_p}\) is mathematically identical to the percent risk contribution defined via variance \(\mathrm{PRC}_i^{(\sigma^2)} = \frac{w_i (\partial \sigma_p^2 / \partial w_i)}{2 \sigma_p^2}\).

Solution

Since \(\sigma_p = (\sigma_p^2)^{1/2}\), by the chain rule we have \(\frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w_i} = \frac{1}{2 (\sigma_p^2)^{1/2}} \frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial w_i} = \frac{1}{2 \sigma_p} \frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial w_i}\). Substituting this into the volatility percent risk contribution gives \(\mathrm{PRC}_i^{(\sigma)} = \frac{w_i (\partial \sigma_p / \partial w_i)}{\sigma_p} = \frac{w_i \left( \frac{1}{2 \sigma_p} \frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial w_i} \right)}{\sigma_p} = \frac{w_i \frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial w_i}}{2 \sigma_p^2} = \mathrm{PRC}_i^{(\sigma^2)}\). Therefore, volatility-based and variance-based percent risk contributions are identical.

P3 · Invariance to Matrix Rescaling

Prove that multiplying the covariance matrix by a positive scalar \(\alpha > 0\) leaves the percent risk contributions \(\mathrm{PRC}_i\) unchanged for any fixed weight vector \(w\).

Solution

Let \(\widetilde{\Sigma} = \alpha \Sigma\). The new portfolio variance is \(\widetilde{\sigma}_p^2 = w^\top \widetilde{\Sigma} w = \alpha (w^\top \Sigma w) = \alpha \sigma_p^2\). The new marginal risk contribution is \(\widetilde{\mathrm{MRC}}_i = \frac{(\widetilde{\Sigma} w)_i}{\widetilde{\sigma}_p} = \frac{\alpha (\Sigma w)_i}{\sqrt{\alpha} \sigma_p} = \sqrt{\alpha} \mathrm{MRC}_i\). The new percent risk contribution is \(\widetilde{\mathrm{PRC}}_i = \frac{w_i \widetilde{\mathrm{MRC}}_i}{\widetilde{\sigma}_p} = \frac{w_i (\sqrt{\alpha} \mathrm{MRC}_i)}{\sqrt{\alpha} \sigma_p} = \frac{w_i \mathrm{MRC}_i}{\sigma_p} = \mathrm{PRC}_i\). The scalar \(\alpha\) cancels completely, proving exact scale invariance.

Empirical

E1 · Historical 60/40 Equity Risk Share

Using historical US equity volatility of 16% and 10-year Treasury volatility of 6% with correlation 0.05, calculate the exact equity percent risk contribution in a 60/40 portfolio and explain why historical drawdowns in 60/40 portfolios trace equity bear markets.

Solution

Equity variance is \(0.16^2 = 0.0256\); bond variance is \(0.06^2 = 0.0036\); covariance is \(0.05 \times 0.16 \times 0.06 = 0.00048\). Portfolio variance is \(0.6^2(0.0256) + 0.4^2(0.0036) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.00048) = 0.009216 + 0.000576 + 0.0002304 = 0.0100224\). Total volatility is \(\sigma_p \approx 0.10011\) (10.01%). Equity TRC is \(0.60 \times [0.60(0.0256) + 0.40(0.00048)] / 0.10011 = 0.60 \times [0.01536 + 0.000192] / 0.10011 = 0.009331 / 0.10011 \approx 0.09321\). Equity percent risk contribution is \(0.09321 / 0.10011 \approx 93.1\%\). Because over 93% of portfolio volatility arises from equities, drawdown paths mirror equity bear markets almost dollar-for-dollar.

E2 · Commodity Risk Concentration in a 3-Asset Fund

An endowment invests 50% in equities (\(\sigma_e = 18\%\)), 30% in bonds (\(\sigma_b = 6\%\)), and 20% in commodities (\(\sigma_c = 24\%\)). Assume pairwise correlations \(\rho_{eb} = 0.1\), \(\rho_{ec} = 0.4\), and \(\rho_{bc} = -0.1\). Compute the percent risk contribution of each asset class.

Solution

Covariances: \(\Sigma_{ee} = 0.0324\), \(\Sigma_{bb} = 0.0036\), \(\Sigma_{cc} = 0.0576\), \(\Sigma_{eb} = 0.00108\), \(\Sigma_{ec} = 0.01728\), \(\Sigma_{bc} = -0.00144\). Portfolio variance: \(w^\top \Sigma w = 0.5^2(0.0324) + 0.3^2(0.0036) + 0.2^2(0.0576) + 2(0.5)(0.3)(0.00108) + 2(0.5)(0.2)(0.01728) + 2(0.3)(0.2)(-0.00144) = 0.00810 + 0.000324 + 0.002304 + 0.000324 + 0.003456 - 0.000173 = 0.014335\). Volatility \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.014335} \approx 0.11973\). Evaluating \(\Sigma w\): for equities: \(0.5(0.0324) + 0.3(0.00108) + 0.2(0.01728) = 0.01620 + 0.000324 + 0.003456 = 0.01998\); for bonds: \(0.5(0.00108) + 0.3(0.0036) + 0.2(-0.00144) = 0.00054 + 0.00108 - 0.000288 = 0.001332\); for commodities: \(0.5(0.01728) + 0.3(-0.00144) + 0.2(0.0576) = 0.00864 - 0.000432 + 0.01152 = 0.019728\). Component contributions: \(\mathrm{TRC}_e = 0.5(0.01998) / 0.11973 = 0.00999 / 0.11973 = 0.08344\); \(\mathrm{TRC}_b = 0.3(0.001332) / 0.11973 = 0.00040 / 0.11973 = 0.00334\); \(\mathrm{TRC}_c = 0.2(0.019728) / 0.11973 = 0.003946 / 0.11973 = 0.03295\). Percent risk contributions: \(\mathrm{PRC}_e = 0.08344 / 0.11973 \approx 69.7\%\), \(\mathrm{PRC}_b = 0.00334 / 0.11973 \approx 2.8\%\), \(\mathrm{PRC}_c = 0.03295 / 0.11973 \approx 27.5\%\). Equities and commodities together drive 97.2% of total risk.

E3 · Impact of Correlation Spikes on Risk Contributions

Suppose equity and commodity correlations jump from \(\rho = 0.20\) to \(\rho = 0.80\) during an inflation shock. For a 50/50 equity/commodity portfolio with \(\sigma_e = 0.18\) and \(\sigma_c = 0.22\), calculate the increase in total portfolio volatility and determine whether the risk shares shift.

Solution

Under \(\rho = 0.20\), \(\Sigma_{12} = 0.20 \times 0.18 \times 0.22 = 0.00792\). Variance is \(0.5^2(0.18^2 + 0.22^2) + 2(0.5^2)(0.00792) = 0.25(0.0324 + 0.0484) + 0.5(0.00792) = 0.0202 + 0.00396 = 0.02416\), so \(\sigma_p \approx 0.15543\). Under \(\rho = 0.80\), \(\Sigma_{12} = 0.80 \times 0.18 \times 0.22 = 0.03168\). Variance is \(0.0202 + 0.5(0.03168) = 0.03604\), so \(\sigma_p \approx 0.18984\). Volatility jumps by 3.44 percentage points (+22.1%). Risk shares: \(\mathrm{PRC}_e = [0.5(0.5 \times 0.0324 + 0.5 \times \Sigma_{12})] / \sigma_p^2 = 0.25(0.0324 + \Sigma_{12}) / \sigma_p^2\). For \(\rho = 0.20\), \(\mathrm{PRC}_e = 0.25(0.04032) / 0.02416 = 0.01008 / 0.02416 \approx 41.7\%\), \(\mathrm{PRC}_c \approx 58.3\%\). For \(\rho = 0.80\), \(\mathrm{PRC}_e = 0.25(0.06408) / 0.03604 = 0.01602 / 0.03604 \approx 44.45\%\), \(\mathrm{PRC}_c \approx 55.55\%\). The correlation surge significantly increases portfolio risk and modestly shifts risk shares toward equality.

Research

R1 · Generalization to Expected Shortfall Risk Budgeting

Expected Shortfall (\(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha\)) is a coherent risk measure that is linearly homogeneous in portfolio weights. How would Euler's theorem apply to decompose Expected Shortfall, and what mathematical difficulties arise when estimating marginal Expected Shortfall empirically?

Solution

Because \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(w)\) is linearly homogeneous (\(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(c w) = c \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(w)\) for \(c > 0\)), Euler's theorem states \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(w) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \frac{\partial \mathrm{ES}_\alpha}{\partial w_i}\). Tasche (1999) proved that \(\frac{\partial \mathrm{ES}_\alpha}{\partial w_i} = -\E[r_i \mid r_p \le -\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(r_p)]\), known as Marginal Expected Shortfall (MES). In practice, estimating MES is challenging because it conditions on extreme tail events where sample observations are scarce, creating substantial estimation variance and susceptibility to outliers unless heavy-tailed extreme value theory (EVT) or kernel smoothing is employed.

R2 · Risk Budgeting under Transaction Costs

Suppose an institutional portfolio manager enforces continuous risk budgeting \(\mathrm{PRC}_i(w_t) = b_i\). When asset covariances drift over time, maintaining exact risk budgets requires continuous rebalancing. Formulate the dynamic optimization problem that balances tracking error to risk budgets against quadratic transaction costs.

Solution

Let \(w_t\) be current weights and \(\Delta w_t = w_{t+1} - w_t\) be the rebalancing trade vector. The dynamic cost objective penalizes risk budget tracking errors and turnover frictions: \(\min_{\Delta w_t} \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i [\mathrm{PRC}_i(w_t + \Delta w_t) - b_i]^2 + c_1 \|\Delta w_t\|_1 + \Delta w_t^\top \Gamma \Delta w_t\) subject to \(\iota^\top \Delta w_t = 0\), where \(c_1\) captures linear bid-ask spread costs and \(\Gamma\) models quadratic market impact. Because the first term is nonlinear and non-convex in weights, solving this requires sequential quadratic programming (SQP) or augmented Lagrangian methods.

R3 · Asymmetric Correlation Regimes and Factor Risk Budgets

Instead of budgeting risk across individual asset instruments \(w\), institutional investors increasingly budget risk across underlying macroeconomic factors \(f = (f_{\text{growth}}, f_{\text{inflation}}, f_{\text{liquidity}})\). Formulate factor risk budgeting under a linear factor model \(r = B f + \epsilon\) and explain how factor risk budgeting avoids illusory instrument-level diversification.

Solution

Under \(r = B f + \epsilon\), portfolio return is \(r_p = w^\top B f + w^\top \epsilon = \beta_p^\top f + \epsilon_p\), where \(\beta_p = B^\top w\). Portfolio variance decomposes into factor variance \(\beta_p^\top \Sigma_f \beta_p\) and idiosyncratic variance \(w^\top D w\). Factor risk contribution of factor \(k\) is \(\mathrm{TRC}_k^f = \beta_{p,k} (\Sigma_f \beta_p)_k / \sigma_p\). Budgeting risk at the factor level ensures that nominally diverse instruments (such as emerging market debt, high-yield bonds, and equities) that all load heavily on the global equity growth factor do not inadvertently dominate the portfolio's total risk budget.

7Retrieval quiz

  1. Why does Euler's theorem apply directly to portfolio standard deviation \(\sigma_p(w)\)?

    Options

    \(\sigma_p(c w) = c \sigma_p(w)\) for any \(c > 0\). Linear homogeneity is the exact condition required for Euler's theorem.

  2. What is the algebraic expression for the marginal risk contribution \(\mathrm{MRC}_i = \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w_i}\)?

    Options

    \(\frac{\partial}{\partial w_i} \sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w} = \frac{1}{2 \sigma_p} \cdot 2 (\Sigma w)_i = (\Sigma w)_i / \sigma_p\).

  3. What is the sum of percent risk contributions \(\sum_{i=1}^N \mathrm{PRC}_i\) across all assets in any valid portfolio?

    By Euler's theorem, \(\sum \mathrm{TRC}_i = \sigma_p\), so \(\sum \mathrm{PRC}_i = \sum (\mathrm{TRC}_i / \sigma_p) = 1.0\).

  4. In a traditional 60/40 equity/bond portfolio, approximately what proportion of total portfolio volatility risk is driven by the equity component?

    Options

    Because equity volatility is ~3 times bond volatility, equity variance contribution is overwhelmingly dominant at ~90%.

  5. Under what condition can an asset's marginal risk contribution \(\mathrm{MRC}_i\) be negative?

    Options

    If \((\Sigma w)_i = \Cov(r_i, r_p) < 0\), increasing the asset's weight dampens overall portfolio volatility.

  6. If the covariance matrix is multiplied by 4 (\(\Sigma \to 4 \Sigma\)), what happens to each asset's percent risk contribution \(\mathrm{PRC}_i\)?

    Options

    \(\mathrm{PRC}_i\) is homogeneous of degree zero with respect to the covariance matrix, so scalar multiplication cancels out.

  7. What is the difference between inverse-volatility weighting and risk budgeting?

    Options

    Inverse-volatility assumes zero or uniform correlations, whereas risk budgeting accounts for full off-diagonal covariances.

  8. In Lab 1, for the 3-asset multi-asset portfolio with weights (0.5, 0.4, 0.1), what was the exact total portfolio volatility \(\sigma_p\) (to 4 decimal places)?

    From Lab 1 output, \(\sigma_p = 0.100603 \approx 0.1006\).
