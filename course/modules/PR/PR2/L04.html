Volatility Targeting & Time-Varying Exposure

  • Uses: risk budgeting, risk parity, leverage aversion

  • Bpublished, not settled

Unmanaged equity, commodity, and bond portfolios exhibit time-varying volatility clustering, fat tails, and catastrophic crash drawdowns. Volatility targeting dynamically scales asset allocation inversely with conditional risk forecasts, maintaining constant dollar risk over time. Harvey, Hoyle, Russell, Sargaison, Sepp & Sinclair (2018) proved across four decades of multi-asset data that volatility targeting systematically compresses return kurtosis, thins fat crash tails, and truncates maximum drawdowns by 20% to 40% without compromising long-run compounded returns. Because negative equity returns cluster in high-volatility regimes, scaling out of assets when volatility spikes acts as an endogenous tail-risk hedge, fundamentally resolving the static portfolio vulnerability to market crises.

1The Volatility Targeting Mechanism & Scaling Rules

A static portfolio maintains constant asset weights \(w\) regardless of market volatility. In reality, asset returns exhibit persistent volatility clustering: quiet regimes with annualized volatility below 10% alternate with crisis regimes where annualized volatility exceeds 50%. Consequently, a static portfolio takes massive dollar risk during crises and negligible dollar risk during tranquil expansions.

Definition 4.1 (Volatility Targeting)

Volatility targeting is the systematic risk management policy of dynamically adjusting portfolio exposure to maintain a constant conditional annualized volatility \(\sigma^* > 0\). Given an ex-ante volatility forecast \(\hat{\sigma}_t\) at time \(t\), the portfolio weight allocated to the underlying risky asset is

\[ \begin{aligned} w_t &= \operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\sigma^*}{\hat{\sigma}_t}, w_{\min}, w_{\max}\right) \end{aligned} \]

where \(w_{\min} \ge 0\) and \(w_{\max} < \infty\) define leverage boundaries. The residual capital \(1 - w_t\) is invested in cash earning \(R_{f, t}\) (if \(w_t < 1\)) or financed via margin borrowing at \(R_{f, t} + c_b\) (if \(w_t > 1\)).

The gross return of the managed strategy over period \(t+1\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} R_{p, t+1} &= w_t R_{t+1} + (1 - w_t) R_{f, t+1} - \kappa |w_t - w_{t-1}| \end{aligned} \]

where \(\kappa\) denotes proportional rebalancing transaction costs.

2Forecasting Conditional Volatility & Tail Compression

Systematic managers forecast conditional volatility \(\hat{\sigma}_t\) using trailing realized metrics such as rolling standard deviations or Exponentially Weighted Moving Averages (EWMA):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\sigma}_{t, \text{ewma}}^2 &= (1 - \lambda) 252\, r_{t-1}^2 + \lambda \hat{\sigma}_{t-1, \text{ewma}}^2 \end{aligned} \]

with RiskMetrics decay parameter \(\lambda = 0.94\).

Theorem 4.1 (Tail Compression & Drawdown Truncation)

Assumption A1 (Persistent Volatility Clustering): Asset returns follow a continuous stochastic volatility diffusion \(dS_t = \mu_t S_t dt + \sigma_t S_t dW_t\) where \(\sigma_t^2\) is mean-reverting and persistent.

Assumption A2 (Leverage Effect): Shocks to prices and volatility are negatively correlated: \(\Corr(dW_t, dW_t^v) < 0\).

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, setting portfolio exposure \(w_t = \sigma^* / \sigma_t\) renders instantaneous portfolio volatility constant: \(\sigma_p = \sigma^*\). Consequently, the unconditional excess kurtosis of portfolio returns is compressed relative to the unmanaged asset, and maximum drawdown during volatility spikes is truncated by factor \(\sigma^* / \sigma_t\).

Proof

Applying Ito's lemma to portfolio value \(V_t\) under weight \(w_t = \sigma^* / \sigma_t\) gives diffusion term \(w_t \sigma_t dW_t = (\sigma^* / \sigma_t) \sigma_t dW_t = \sigma^* dW_t\). Because unmanaged returns are a continuous scale mixture of normal distributions with time-varying variance \(\sigma_t^2\), by the law of total cumulance the unmanaged fourth moment satisfies \(\E[(\sigma_t \epsilon)^4] / (\E[\sigma_t^2 \epsilon^2])^2 = 3 \E[\sigma_t^4] / (\E[\sigma_t^2])^2 \ge 3\), with strict inequality whenever \(\Var(\sigma_t^2) > 0\). Volatility targeting stabilizes conditional variance, eliminating scale-mixture kurtosis and collapsing the tails toward normality. When negative return shocks coincide with volatility spikes (\(\Corr < 0\)), \(w_t\) scales down, attenuating cumulative drawdown.

Lab 4 · Tail-Risk Compression and Volatility Targeting

Evaluates Harvey et al. (2018) framework. Scaling exposure inversely with conditional volatility forecast compresses sample excess kurtosis by over 50% and truncates maximum drawdown relative to the unmanaged asset under GARCH(1,1) volatility clustering and fat-tailed shocks.

"""Lab 4: Volatility targeting and tail-risk compression.

Demonstrates Harvey et al. (2018) empirical mechanics:
Scaling portfolio weight inversely with conditional volatility forecast:
w_t = sigma_target / sigma_hat_t
compresses return kurtosis, thins fat tails, and mitigates maximum drawdown.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    # SIMULATED: Volatility clustering with crisis tail events over 2,000 trading days (~8 years)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(101)
    n_days = 2000
    target_vol_ann = 0.12
    dt = 1.0 / 252.0
    target_vol_daily = target_vol_ann * np.sqrt(dt)

    omega = 0.000005
    alpha = 0.12
    beta = 0.85
    mu_daily = 0.08 * dt  # 8% annualized drift

    daily_returns = np.zeros(n_days)
    sigma2_t = np.zeros(n_days)
    sigma2_t[0] = omega / (1.0 - alpha - beta)

    for t in range(n_days):
        eps = rng.standard_normal()
        if rng.random() < 0.01:
            eps *= 3.0  # tail shock
        sigma_t = np.sqrt(sigma2_t[t])
        daily_returns[t] = mu_daily + sigma_t * eps
        if t + 1 < n_days:
            sigma2_t[t + 1] = omega + alpha * (daily_returns[t] - mu_daily) ** 2 + beta * sigma2_t[t]

    # Forecast conditional volatility using trailing 21-day realized volatility
    trailing_vol = np.zeros(n_days)
    for t in range(21, n_days):
        trailing_vol[t] = float(np.std(daily_returns[t - 21 : t], ddof=1))
    trailing_vol[:21] = np.sqrt(sigma2_t[0])

    # Volatility targeted portfolio weights: w_t = target_vol_daily / trailing_vol_t
    w_target = np.clip(target_vol_daily / trailing_vol, 0.2, 3.0)

    # Strategy returns (lagged by 1 day to prevent lookahead)
    strat_returns = w_target[21:-1] * daily_returns[22:]
    unmanaged_returns = daily_returns[22:]

    def stats(r: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
        ann_ret = float(np.mean(r) * 252.0)
        ann_vol = float(np.std(r, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(252.0))
        sr = ann_ret / ann_vol
        centered = r - np.mean(r)
        kurt = float(np.mean(centered**4) / (np.var(r) ** 2) - 3.0)
        cum = np.cumprod(1.0 + r)
        peak = np.maximum.accumulate(cum)
        mdd = float(np.max((peak - cum) / peak))
        return ann_vol, sr, kurt, mdd

    u_vol, u_sr, u_kurt, u_mdd = stats(unmanaged_returns)
    m_vol, m_sr, m_kurt, m_mdd = stats(strat_returns)

    print(f"Unmanaged Realized Vol: {u_vol * 100:.2f}%, Vol-Targeted Realized Vol: {m_vol * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Unmanaged Sharpe Ratio: {u_sr:.4f}, Vol-Targeted Sharpe Ratio: {m_sr:.4f}")
    print(f"Unmanaged Excess Kurtosis: {u_kurt:.2f}, Vol-Targeted Excess Kurtosis: {m_kurt:.2f}")
    print(f"Unmanaged Max Drawdown: {u_mdd * 100:.2f}%, Vol-Targeted Max Drawdown: {m_mdd * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Kurtosis Reduction: {(1.0 - m_kurt / u_kurt) * 100:.1f}%")
    print(f"Drawdown Reduction: {(u_mdd - m_mdd) * 100:.2f}% percentage points")


main()

Unmanaged Sharpe Ratio: 0.0336, Vol-Targeted Sharpe Ratio: 0.2095
Unmanaged Excess Kurtosis: 5.64, Vol-Targeted Excess Kurtosis: 0.44
Unmanaged Max Drawdown: 57.40%, Vol-Targeted Max Drawdown: 25.79%

3Where the canon breaks

Classical Markowitz (1952) modern portfolio theory assumes returns are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) over time with a constant covariance matrix \(\Sigma\). Under this static assumption, optimal asset allocations are constant buy-and-hold weights. In real financial markets, however, volatility clusters aggressively across regimes. Holding static weights through volatility explosions forces investors to absorb massive dollar risk during severe market selloffs, causing destructive variance drag \(g \approx \mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\). Volatility targeting shatters the static-weight canon by demonstrating that dynamic risk scaling dominates buy-and-hold investing across all liquid asset classes.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Volatility targeting is market timing and requires predicting price returns.”

    Directional market timing forecasts the first moment (\(\hat{\mu}_t\)), which has near-zero autocorrelation. Volatility targeting forecasts the second moment (\(\hat{\sigma}_t\)), which has strong persistence (autoregressive \(R^2 > 0.40\)). Volatility targeting conditions purely on risk, not on return direction.

  2. “Volatility targeting doubles the annualized Sharpe ratio of equity portfolios.”

    Harvey et al. (2018) prove that the Sharpe ratio gain from volatility targeting in equities is modest (+0.05 to +0.15). The dominant economic benefit is tail risk compression: eliminating excess kurtosis, truncating fat left tails, and slashing maximum drawdowns by 20% to 40%.

  3. “Volatility targeting eliminates all crash risk and prevents flash crashes.”

    Volatility targeting updates positions based on trailing realized risk. Instantaneous price jumps, overnight gaps, or intraday flash crashes occur before trailing estimators can adjust, leaving the portfolio exposed to initial gap risk. Volatility targeting protects against prolonged bear markets rather than instantaneous discontinuous jumps.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Target Weight Under Leverage Bounds

A systematic fund targets \(\sigma^* = 10\%\) annualized volatility on an asset with leverage bounds \([w_{\min}, w_{\max}] = [0.20, 1.50]\). If trailing 21-day realized volatility is currently \(\hat{\sigma}_t = 16\%\), compute the risky asset weight \(w_t\) and the cash weight.

Solution

The raw weight is \(w_t^{\text{raw}} = \sigma^* / \hat{\sigma}_t = 0.10 / 0.16 = 0.625\). Because \(0.20 \le 0.625 \le 1.50\), the weight is unconstrained: \(w_t = 0.625\) (62.5% in risky asset). The unallocated fraction \(1 - w_t = 1 - 0.625 = 0.375\) (37.5%) is invested in cash.

M2 · Binding Leverage Cap Under Calm Regimes

An equity index experiences low volatility with \(\hat{\sigma}_t = 6\%\). The target volatility is \(\sigma^* = 12\%\), and the leverage cap is \(w_{\max} = 1.60\). Determine the allocated weight and margin financing if the risk-free rate is 3% and borrowing spread is 80 bps.

Solution

The raw weight is \(0.12 / 0.06 = 2.0\). The leverage cap binds, restricting exposure to \(w_t = 1.60\). The investor borrows 60% leverage at margin rate \(R_f + c_b = 3.0\% + 0.8\% = 3.8\%\), yielding portfolio return \(R_p = 1.60 R - 0.60 \times 3.8\%\).

M3 · EWMA Volatility Update

Using EWMA parameter \(\lambda = 0.94\), yesterday's annualized volatility forecast was \(\hat{\sigma}_{t-1} = 15\%\). Today's daily return is \(r_t = -3.5\%\). Compute the updated annualized volatility forecast \(\hat{\sigma}_t\) (assuming 252 trading days).

Solution

Daily variance update is \(\sigma_{t, \text{daily}}^2 = 0.94(0.15^2 / 252) + 0.06(-0.035)^2 = 0.94(8.9286 \times 10^{-5}) + 0.06(0.001225) = 1.5743 \times 10^{-4}\). Multiplying by 252 gives annualized variance \(\hat{\sigma}_t^2 = 0.039672\). Taking square root: \(\hat{\sigma}_t = \sqrt{0.039672} \approx 0.1992\) (19.92%).

Proof

P1 · Constant Diffusion Under Continuous Volatility Targeting

Let risky asset price follow \(dS_t / S_t = \mu_t dt + \sigma_t dW_t\). Under continuous rebalancing with weight \(w_t = \sigma^* / \sigma_t\), prove that the instantaneous variance of the portfolio value \(V_t\) is constant and equal to \((\sigma^*)^2\).

Solution

The wealth dynamics satisfy \(dV_t / V_t = w_t (dS_t / S_t) + (1 - w_t) R_f dt = [w_t \mu_t + (1 - w_t) R_f] dt + w_t \sigma_t dW_t\). Substituting \(w_t = \sigma^* / \sigma_t\), the diffusion term simplifies to \((\sigma^* / \sigma_t) \sigma_t dW_t = \sigma^* dW_t\). The quadratic variation over interval \([t, t+\Delta]\) is \(\int_t^{t+\Delta} (\sigma^*)^2 ds = (\sigma^*)^2 \Delta\), proving that the instantaneous diffusion coefficient is strictly constant and independent of \(\sigma_t\).

P2 · Scale Mixtures of Normals and Kurtosis Inflation

Prove that if return \(r = \sigma \epsilon\) where \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) is independent of positive random variable \(\sigma\), the unconditional excess kurtosis \(\operatorname{Kurt}(r) - 3 = 3 \frac{\Var(\sigma^2)}{(\E[\sigma^2])^2} \ge 0\), establishing that time-varying volatility generates fat tails.

Solution

By independence, \(\E[r^2] = \E[\sigma^2 \epsilon^2] = \E[\sigma^2] \E[\epsilon^2] = \E[\sigma^2]\). Similarly, \(\E[r^4] = \E[\sigma^4 \epsilon^4] = \E[\sigma^4] \E[\epsilon^4] = 3 \E[\sigma^4]\). Kurtosis is \(\frac{\E[r^4]}{(\E[r^2])^2} = 3 \frac{\E[\sigma^4]}{(\E[\sigma^2])^2}\). Substituting \(\E[\sigma^4] = \Var(\sigma^2) + (\E[\sigma^2])^2\), we obtain \(\frac{\E[r^4]}{(\E[r^2])^2} = 3 \left(1 + \frac{\Var(\sigma^2)}{(\E[\sigma^2])^2}\right) = 3 + 3 \frac{\Var(\sigma^2)}{(\E[\sigma^2])^2}\). Thus excess kurtosis is strictly proportional to the variance of volatility, vanishing if and only if \(\sigma\) is deterministic.

P3 · Continuous-Time Kelly Growth Maximization

Show that the log-wealth drift under continuous volatility targeting \(w_t = \sigma^* / \sigma_t\) is \(\E[d \ln V_t / dt] = \frac{\sigma^* \mu}{\sigma_t} - \frac{1}{2}(\sigma^*)^2\), and show that choosing \(\sigma^* = \mu / \sigma_t\) recovers the optimal Kelly leverage.

Solution

By Ito's lemma on \(\ln V_t\), \(d \ln V_t = \frac{dV_t}{V_t} - \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dV_t}{V_t}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\sigma^* \mu}{\sigma_t} - \frac{1}{2}(\sigma^*)^2\right) dt + \sigma^* dW_t\). Differentiating the expected growth rate with respect to \(\sigma^*\) gives \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma^*} = \frac{\mu}{\sigma_t} - \sigma^* = 0\). Setting the derivative to zero yields \(\sigma^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma_t}\). Substituting into the weight formula gives \(w_t = \frac{\sigma^*}{\sigma_t} = \frac{\mu}{\sigma_t^2}\), exactly matching the continuous-time Kelly portfolio fraction.

Empirical

E1 · Replicated Drawdown Compression Across Asset Classes

Harvey et al. (2018, Table 2) report that volatility targeting reduced the maximum drawdown of US Equities from -84.5% to -62.1%, and Global Equities from -53.7% to -34.8%. Explain the structural mechanism driving this multi-asset drawdown reduction.

Solution

Financial asset drawdowns cluster in periods of elevated conditional volatility (the leverage effect and volatility feedback). When market turbulence begins, trailing volatility forecasts spike rapidly, triggering systematic reduction in portfolio exposure \(w_t = \sigma^* / \hat{\sigma}_t\). During prolonged multi-month downturns (such as 1929–1932 or 2007–2009), the portfolio operates at 30% to 50% normal exposure, directly absorbing only a fraction of the bottom half of the crash, truncating cumulative peak-to-trough losses.

E2 · Buffer Band Friction Reduction

An institutional fund compares daily rebalancing against a \(\pm 5\%\) buffer band on a volatility-targeted portfolio. If daily rebalancing generates 420% annualized one-way turnover with 5 bps trading costs, evaluate how the buffer band improves net Sharpe ratio.

Solution

Daily rebalancing costs \(4.20 \times 0.0005 = 0.0021\) (21 bps drag). Introducing a \(\pm 5\%\) buffer band suppresses micro-adjustments from noise, cutting annual turnover by approximately 55% to 190%. The transaction cost drops to \(1.90 \times 0.0005 = 0.00095\) (9.5 bps drag), saving 11.5 bps annually with negligible tracking error relative to pure daily targeting.

E3 · Variance Drag in Two-Regime Markets

In a 2-year market cycle, Year 1 has \(+20\%\) return with \(10\%\) volatility, while Year 2 has \(-30\%\) return with \(40\%\) volatility. Compare the compound 2-year return of a static buy-and-hold portfolio against a 10% volatility-targeted portfolio.

Solution

Buy-and-hold terminal wealth is \((1 + 0.20)(1 - 0.30) = 1.20 \times 0.70 = 0.840\) (a \(-16.0\%\) loss, annualized \(-8.35\%\)). The vol-targeted portfolio holds \(w = 10/10 = 1.0\) in Year 1 (return \(+20\%\)) and \(w = 10/40 = 0.25\) in Year 2 (return \(0.25 \times (-30\%) = -7.5\%\)). Terminal wealth is \((1 + 0.20)(1 - 0.075) = 1.20 \times 0.925 = 1.110\) (a \(+11.0\%\) gain, annualized \(+5.35\%\)). The dynamic strategy prevents catastrophic variance drag.

Research

R1 · Merton Jump Diffusion and Tail Bounds

Suppose returns follow a Merton jump diffusion with Poisson arrival intensity \(\lambda_J\). Prove that volatility targeting cannot drive return excess kurtosis below \(\lambda_J \E[J^4] / (\sigma^*)^4\), identifying the fundamental limit of trailing volatility filters.

Solution

The total return over interval \(\Delta t\) decomposes into diffusion \(\sigma^* \Delta W\) and compound Poisson jump \(w_{t-\Delta} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\Delta t}} J_k\). The fourth cumulant of the jump component is \(\E[w^4] \lambda_J \Delta t \E[J^4]\). Normalizing by variance \((\sigma^*)^4 (\Delta t)^2\), the excess kurtosis remains strictly bounded away from zero by the jump arrival process. Because jumps occur discontinuously without warning, trailing volatility filters cannot pre-adjust exposure prior to the arrival of Poisson shocks.

R2 · Overlay Volatility Targeting on Risk Parity

Prove that applying an aggregate volatility targeting scalar \(m_t = \sigma^* / \hat{\sigma}_{\text{RP}, t}\) to an Equal Risk Contribution portfolio preserves exact equal risk contributions across all assets while stabilizing total portfolio variance at \((\sigma^*)^2\).

Solution

Let \(w_{\text{RP}}\) be the ERC weight vector satisfying \(w_i (\Sigma w)_i = \frac{1}{N} \sigma_{\text{RP}}^2\). Scaled weights are \(\tilde{w} = m_t w_{\text{RP}}\). Total variance is \(\tilde{w}^\top \Sigma \tilde{w} = m_t^2 (w_{\text{RP}}^\top \Sigma w_{\text{RP}}) = m_t^2 \sigma_{\text{RP}}^2 = (\sigma^*)^2\). Individual risk contribution is \(\tilde{w}_i (\Sigma \tilde{w})_i = (m_t w_i)(m_t (\Sigma w)_i) = m_t^2 [w_i (\Sigma w)_i] = m_t^2 (\frac{1}{N} \sigma_{\text{RP}}^2) = \frac{1}{N} (\sigma^*)^2\). Proportional risk contributions remain exactly equal at \(1/N\).

R3 · Parkinson High-Low vs Close-to-Close Efficiency

Under geometric Brownian motion with continuous price monitoring, Parkinson (1980) proved that the high-low variance estimator has variance \(\approx 0.40 / K\) compared to \(2.0 / K\) for close-to-close returns. Explain how using Parkinson volatility enhances target weight responsiveness.

Solution

Because the Parkinson estimator utilizes the intraday price range \(\ln(H_t / L_t)\), it captures extreme intraday variance without needing high-frequency tick data. With 5x higher statistical efficiency, an 8-day Parkinson window achieves the same estimation precision as a 40-day close-to-close window. This allows systematic portfolios to detect volatility regime shifts five times faster, adjusting weights before multi-day drawdown runs gather momentum.
