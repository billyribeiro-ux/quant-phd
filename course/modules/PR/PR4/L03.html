Backtesting Risk Measures: Kupiec POF Test

  • Uses: value-at-risk, likelihood ratio tests
  • Introduces: kupiec-test
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A quantitative risk model is only as credible as its out-of-sample predictive performance. Backtesting evaluates whether realized trading losses exceed forecasted Value-at-Risk at the theoretical rate. Kupiec (1995) established the industry standard Proportion of Failures likelihood ratio test, transforming exception counts into formal statistical hypothesis decisions. This framework directly governs the Basel Committee's traffic light system, determining statutory bank capital multipliers and institutional trading authority.

1The Binomial Exception Process

Consider a sequence of daily portfolio losses \(L_t\) and corresponding 1-day Value-at-Risk forecasts \(\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha, t}\) for trading days \(t = 1, \dots, T\). An exception (or hit/violation) occurs whenever the realized loss strictly exceeds the forecasted VaR threshold:

\[ \begin{aligned} I_t = \mathbf{1}_{\{L_t > \mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha, t}\}} = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } L_t > \mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha, t} \\ 0, & \text{if } L_t \le \mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha, t} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]

Under the null hypothesis that the risk measurement model is accurately calibrated at confidence level \(\alpha\), the probability of an exception on any given day is \(p = 1 - \alpha\). Assuming exceptions are independent across time, the total number of exceptions \(X = \sum_{t=1}^T I_t\) follows a binomial distribution:

\[ \begin{aligned} X \sim \text{Binomial}(T, p), \quad \Prob(X = x) = \binom{T}{x} p^x (1 - p)^{T - x} \end{aligned} \]

For a standard regulatory sample of \(T = 250\) trading days (approximately one calendar year) at confidence \(\alpha = 0.99\) (\(p = 0.01\)), the expected number of exceptions is \(\E[X] = T p = 250 \times 0.01 = 2.5\).

2Kupiec (1995) Proportion of Failures (POF) Likelihood Ratio Test

Definition 2.1 (Kupiec Test)

The Kupiec test (or Proportion of Failures test) is an unconditional coverage likelihood ratio test of the null hypothesis \(H_0: p = 1 - \alpha\) against the two-sided alternative \(H_1: p \ne 1 - \alpha\).

Theorem 2.1 (Kupiec POF Likelihood Ratio Statistic)

Let \(x\) denote the observed number of exceptions over \(T\) trading periods. The sample exception rate is \(\hat{p} = x / T\). The likelihood ratio test statistic is:

\[ \begin{aligned} LR_{\text{POF}} = -2 \ln\left( \frac{L(p)}{L(\hat{p})} \right) = -2 \ln\left( \frac{(1 - p)^{T - x} p^x}{(1 - \hat{p})^{T - x} \hat{p}^x} \right) \end{aligned} \]

Under \(H_0\), as \(T \to \infty\), \(LR_{\text{POF}}\) converges in distribution to a chi-squared distribution with 1 degree of freedom:

\[ \begin{aligned} LR_{\text{POF}} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(1) \end{aligned} \]
Proof

The likelihood function under the Bernoulli parameter \(\theta\) is \(L(\theta) = \binom{T}{x} \theta^x (1 - \theta)^{T - x}\). Under \(H_0\), \(\theta = p\). The unconstrained maximum likelihood estimator is \(\hat{\theta} = \hat{p} = x / T\). Forming the ratio of likelihoods:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Lambda = \frac{L(p)}{L(\hat{p})} = \frac{\binom{T}{x} p^x (1 - p)^{T - x}}{\binom{T}{x} \hat{p}^x (1 - \hat{p})^{T - x}} = \frac{p^x (1 - p)^{T - x}}{\hat{p}^x (1 - \hat{p})^{T - x}} \end{aligned} \]

Taking the natural logarithm and multiplying by \(-2\) yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} LR_{\text{POF}} = -2 \left[ x \ln\left(\frac{p}{\hat{p}}\right) + (T - x) \ln\left(\frac{1 - p}{1 - \hat{p}}\right) \right] \end{aligned} \]

By Wilks' theorem, since the null hypothesis restricts one free parameter (\(p\)), \(-2 \ln \Lambda \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(1)\) as \(T \to \infty\) as established by Kupiec (1995).

At a standard 5% significance level (\(\alpha_{\text{test}} = 0.05\)), the critical value of \(\chi^2(1)\) is \(3.841\). If \(LR_{\text{POF}} > 3.841\) (or equivalently, \(p\text{-value} < 0.05\)), the null hypothesis is rejected, indicating that the risk model systematically mis-estimates tail loss frequency.

3Basel Accord Traffic Light Framework and Statistical Power

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (1996) operationalized the Kupiec framework into a three-tiered supervisory traffic light system based on \(T = 250\) trading days at \(\alpha = 0.99\):

  • Green Zone (0 to 4 exceptions): The risk model is deemed statistically acceptable. The regulatory market risk capital multiplier remains at the baseline minimum \(k = 3.0\).
  • Yellow Zone (5 to 9 exceptions): The model raises supervisory concern. The capital multiplier increases progressively from \(k = 3.40\) (at 5 exceptions) to \(k = 3.85\) (at 9 exceptions), and the bank must justify why exceptions occurred.
  • Red Zone (10 or more exceptions): The model is formally rejected. The supervisor imposes the maximum penalty multiplier \(k = 4.0\) or revokes the bank's internal models license entirely.

Lab 3 · Kupiec POF Likelihood Ratio Backtesting and Basel Zones

Evaluating exception counts from 1 to 11 on a 250-day 99% VaR model will show that models with 1 to 4 exceptions pass cleanly in the Green zone, while 8 or more exceptions generate statistically significant rejections (p < 0.05), escalating into the Yellow and Red regulatory penalty zones.

"""Lab 3: Backtesting Risk Models via the Kupiec POF Test and Basel Traffic Lights.

Demonstrates unconditional coverage testing under Kupiec (1995) across
Basel capital compliance zones (Green, Yellow, Red) for a 99% 1-day VaR.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def kupiec_pof_test(exceptions: int, total_obs: int = 250, alpha: float = 0.99) -> tuple[float, float, str]:
    """Compute Kupiec POF Likelihood Ratio statistic and Basel traffic light."""
    p = 1.0 - alpha
    x = exceptions
    t = total_obs

    if x == 0:
        lr = -2.0 * t * math.log(1.0 - p)
    elif x == t:
        lr = -2.0 * t * math.log(p)
    else:
        p_hat = x / t
        lr = -2.0 * (
            (t - x) * math.log(1.0 - p) + x * math.log(p) - (t - x) * math.log(1.0 - p_hat) - x * math.log(p_hat)
        )

    lr = max(0.0, float(lr))
    # Chi-squared survival function with 1 degree of freedom
    p_val = float(math.erfc(math.sqrt(lr / 2.0)))

    # Basel Traffic Light zones for T = 250 at 99% confidence
    if x <= 4:
        zone = "Green (Accept Model, Multiplier k=3.0)"
    elif x <= 9:
        zone = "Yellow (Supervisory Discretion, Multiplier 3.0-3.85)"
    else:
        zone = "Red (Automatic Rejection, Multiplier k=4.0)"

    return lr, p_val, zone


def main() -> None:
    t_obs = 250
    alpha = 0.99
    print(f"Backtest Horizon: {t_obs} days | Confidence Level: {alpha * 100:.1f}%")
    print(f"Expected Violations: {t_obs * (1.0 - alpha):.1f}")
    print("-" * 65)

    test_exceptions = [1, 3, 5, 8, 11]

    for x in test_exceptions:
        lr, p_val, zone = kupiec_pof_test(x, total_obs=t_obs, alpha=alpha)
        status = "Reject H0 (p < 0.05)" if p_val < 0.05 else "Fail to Reject H0"
        print(f"Violations x={x:2d} | LR_POF={lr:7.4f} | p-value={p_val:6.4f} | {status}")
        print(f"  Regulatory Zone: {zone}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The fundamental limitation of the Kupiec POF test is its low statistical power in finite samples. Over \(T = 250\) trading days at 99% confidence, an inaccurate risk model whose true failure rate is double the nominal level (\(p = 0.02\), producing 5 exceptions) will only be rejected with probability approximately 10.8% at the 5% significance level as calculated by Kupiec (1995). A risk model must experience severe under-prediction (producing 7 or more exceptions) before the test achieves moderate rejection power. Consequently, a flawed model can operate undetected for multiple consecutive years.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Observing zero exceptions in 250 trading days proves that a 99% VaR model is highly accurate.”

    Zero exceptions over 250 days yields \(\hat{p} = 0.0\). While regulators do not penalize zero exceptions, statistically this indicates conservative over-estimation of risk. The Kupiec test evaluates both tails: a risk model with zero exceptions over 1,000 days is statistically rejected as excessively conservative, forcing the institution to hold unproductive idle capital.

  2. “The Kupiec test evaluates whether exceptions occur in clusters during market stress.”

    The Kupiec test is strictly an unconditional test: it aggregates the total count of exceptions \(X = \sum I_t\) and completely ignores their chronological order. Five exceptions occurring on five consecutive crash days produce the identical Kupiec statistic as five exceptions spread uniformly across the year.

  3. “A p-value of 0.04 in a Kupiec backtest proves the bank's risk model is flawed.”

    Under the null hypothesis of an accurate model, Type I error dictates that a false rejection occurs 5% of the time by pure chance. When an institution monitors hundreds of trading desks simultaneously, multiple hypothesis testing guarantees that several well-calibrated desks will fail backtests purely by random sampling noise.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Kupiec POF Calculation for Five Exceptions

A 99% 1-day VaR model is backtested over \(T = 250\) trading days and produces \(x = 5\) exceptions. Calculate \(\hat{p}\), the likelihood ratio statistic \(LR_{\text{POF}}\), and state whether the model is rejected at the 5% significance level (\(\chi_{0.05}^2(1) = 3.841\)).

Solution

Here \(T = 250\), \(p = 0.01\), and \(x = 5\). The empirical failure rate is \(\hat{p} = 5 / 250 = 0.020\). Evaluating the log likelihoods: \(L(p) = (0.99)^{245} (0.01)^5 \implies \ln L(p) = 245 \ln(0.99) + 5 \ln(0.01) = 245(-0.010050) + 5(-4.605170) = -2.4623 - 23.0259 = -25.4882\). Unconstrained: \(\ln L(\hat{p}) = 245 \ln(0.98) + 5 \ln(0.02) = 245(-0.020203) + 5(-3.912023) = -4.9497 - 19.5601 = -24.5098\). The LR statistic is \(LR_{\text{POF}} = -2 (-25.4882 - (-24.5098)) = -2(-0.9784) = 1.9568\). Since \(1.9568 < 3.841\) (\(p\text{-value} = 0.1619 > 0.05\)), the model is not rejected.

M2 · Critical Exception Thresholds

For \(T = 250\) days and \(p = 0.01\), determine the exact integer cutoff for \(x\) at which the Kupiec test first rejects the null hypothesis at the 5% level.

Solution

For \(x = 6\), \(\hat{p} = 6/250 = 0.024\), \(LR_{\text{POF}} \approx 3.555 < 3.841\) (\(p = 0.0594\)). For \(x = 7\), \(\hat{p} = 7/250 = 0.028\), \(LR_{\text{POF}} \approx 5.487 > 3.841\) (\(p = 0.0192\)). For the lower tail, for \(x = 0\), \(LR_{\text{POF}} = -2(250 \ln(0.99)) = -2(250 \times -0.010050) = 5.025 > 3.841\) (\(p = 0.0250\)). Thus, the non-rejection region at 5% is \(x \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\). Rejection occurs for \(x = 0\) and \(x \ge 7\).

M3 · Exact Binomial Probability under H0

Compute the exact probability under \(H_0: p = 0.01\) of observing 4 or fewer exceptions in \(T = 250\) trading days using the binomial distribution.

Solution

Using the binomial PMF \(P(X = k) = \binom{250}{k} (0.01)^k (0.99)^{250-k}\): \(P(X = 0) = 0.99^{250} \approx 0.081059\); \(P(X = 1) = 250(0.01)(0.99)^{249} \approx 0.204694\); \(P(X = 2) = \frac{250 \times 249}{2} (0.01)^2 (0.99)^{248} \approx 0.257404\); \(P(X = 3) = \frac{250 \times 249 \times 248}{6} (0.01)^3 (0.99)^{247} \approx 0.214930\); \(P(X = 4) \approx 0.133742\). Summing gives \(\Prob(X \le 4) = 0.081059 + 0.204694 + 0.257404 + 0.214930 + 0.133742 \approx 0.891829\) (89.18%).

Proof

P1 · Limiting Behavior for Zero Exceptions

Prove that as the observed number of exceptions \(x \to 0\), the unconstrained likelihood term \((1 - \hat{p})^{T-x} \hat{p}^x\) approaches 1, and deduce the closed form for \(LR_{\text{POF}}\) when \(x = 0\).

Solution

When \(x = 0\), \(\hat{p} = 0 / T = 0\). The term \((1 - \hat{p})^{T - x} = (1 - 0)^T = 1\). For \(\hat{p}^x = 0^0\), consider the limit \(\lim_{x \to 0^+} x \ln(x / T) = \lim_{x \to 0^+} (x \ln x - x \ln T) = 0\). Exponentiating yields \(\lim_{\hat{p} \to 0^+} \hat{p}^x = e^0 = 1\). Therefore, the unconstrained likelihood evaluated at \(\hat{p} = 0\) is \(L(0) = 1.0\). Substituting into the likelihood ratio formula: \(LR_{\text{POF}} = -2 \ln\left( \frac{(1 - p)^T p^0}{1.0} \right) = -2 \ln((1 - p)^T) = -2 T \ln(1 - p)\).

P2 · Concavity and Uniqueness of the MLE

Prove that the log-likelihood function \(\ell(\theta) = (T - x) \ln(1 - \theta) + x \ln(\theta)\) is strictly concave on \((0, 1)\) for \(0 < x < T\), and establish that \(\hat{\theta} = x / T\) is the unique global maximum.

Solution

Compute the first derivative: \(\ell'(\theta) = \frac{x}{\theta} - \frac{T - x}{1 - \theta}\). Setting \(\ell'(\theta) = 0\) yields \(\frac{x}{\theta} = \frac{T - x}{1 - \theta} \implies x(1 - \theta) = (T - x)\theta \implies x = T \theta \implies \theta = x / T\). Compute the second derivative: \(\ell''(\theta) = -\frac{x}{\theta^2} - \frac{T - x}{(1 - \theta)^2}\). For \(0 < x < T\) and \(\theta \in (0, 1)\), both terms are strictly negative, so \(\ell''(\theta) < 0\) for all \(\theta \in (0, 1)\). Strict negativity of the second derivative proves strict concavity, establishing \(\hat{\theta} = x / T\) as the unique global maximizer.

P3 · Score Test Relationship

Derive the Rao score (Lagrange multiplier) test statistic for \(H_0: \theta = p\) in the binomial model and show that it equals the Pearson chi-squared statistic \((x - T p)^2 / [T p (1 - p)]\).

Solution

The score function is \(S(\theta) = \ell'(\theta) = \frac{x}{\theta} - \frac{T - x}{1 - \theta} = \frac{x - T \theta}{\theta(1 - \theta)}\). The Fisher information is \(I(\theta) = -\E[\ell''(\theta)] = \frac{\E[x]}{\theta^2} + \frac{T - \E[x]}{(1 - \theta)^2} = \frac{T \theta}{\theta^2} + \frac{T(1 - \theta)}{(1 - \theta)^2} = \frac{T}{\theta(1 - \theta)}\). The Rao score statistic evaluates the score and information at the null value \(\theta = p\): \(LM = \frac{S(p)^2}{I(p)} = \frac{\left( \frac{x - T p}{p(1 - p)} \right)^2}{\frac{T}{p(1 - p)}} = \frac{(x - T p)^2}{T p (1 - p)}\). This is algebraically identical to the classic Pearson chi-squared goodness-of-fit statistic for a binomial proportion.

Empirical

E1 · Type II Error and Power Analysis

Suppose an underlying risk model has an actual failure rate of \(p_{\text{true}} = 0.03\) instead of the nominal \(p_0 = 0.01\) over \(T = 250\) days. Using the rejection rule \(x \ge 7\), compute the statistical power \(1 - \beta\) of the Kupiec test.

Solution

Under the alternative \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(250, 0.03)\), the expected number of exceptions is \(\mu = 250 \times 0.03 = 7.5\), with variance \(\sigma^2 = 250 \times 0.03 \times 0.97 = 7.275\) (\(\sigma \approx 2.697\)). Power is \(\Prob(X \ge 7 \mid p = 0.03) = 1 - \Prob(X \le 6)\). Summing exact binomial probabilities for \(k = 0, \dots, 6\) with \(p = 0.03\): \(\Prob(X \le 6) \approx 0.3804\). Therefore, the power of the test is \(1 - 0.3804 = 0.6196\) (62.0%). Even with a 300% surge in failure rate, the test has a 38% probability of failing to reject the inaccurate model.

E2 · Capital Charge Impact of Traffic Lights

A bank's internal models book has a 10-day 99% VaR of $20,000,000. Calculate its statutory market risk capital charge in the Green zone (\(k = 3.0\)), at 7 exceptions in the Yellow zone (\(k = 3.65\)), and in the Red zone (\(k = 4.0\)).

Solution

Under Basel rules, market risk capital is \(\text{Capital} = k \times \mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}^{10\text{-day}}\). In the Green zone: \(\text{Capital} = 3.0 \times \$20{,}000{,}000 = \$60{,}000{,}000\). In the Yellow zone with 7 exceptions: \(\text{Capital} = 3.65 \times \$20{,}000{,}000 = \$73{,}000{,}000\). In the Red zone: \(\text{Capital} = 4.0 \times \$20{,}000{,}000 = \$80{,}000{,}000\). Entering the Red zone imposes an immediate $20,000,000 (+33.3%) capital penalty on the institution.

E3 · Horizon Sensitivity of Backtest Rejection Rates

Why do regulators prefer a 250-day window over a 1,000-day window for supervisory backtesting, despite the lower statistical power of the 250-day test?

Solution

While a 1,000-day window provides far greater statistical power to detect small mis-calibrations, it has substantial regime lag: a model that worked well in three calm years but failed catastrophically during the last 6 months would still pass a 1,000-day backtest. Regulators enforce a 250-day window to maintain responsiveness to current market regime shifts and prompt swift intervention when risk models degrade.

Research

R1 · Multiple Hypothesis Testing Across Desks

An investment bank monitors 100 independent trading desks, each with an accurately calibrated 99% VaR model over 250 days. If the regulator flags any desk that produces 7 or more exceptions, what is the probability that at least one innocent desk is falsely penalized?

Solution

Under \(H_0: p = 0.01\), the probability that a single desk produces \(\ge 7\) exceptions is \(\alpha_1 = \Prob(X \ge 7) \approx 0.01374\) (1.374%). Assuming the 100 desks are independent, the probability that no desk produces \(\ge 7\) exceptions is \((1 - \alpha_1)^{100} = (1 - 0.01374)^{100} \approx (0.98626)^{100} \approx 0.2505\) (25.05%). The probability that at least one innocent desk is falsely flagged is \(1 - 0.2505 = 0.7495\) (74.95%). Multiple hypothesis adjustments (such as Bonferroni or False Discovery Rate control) are critical to avoid spurious regulatory penalties.

R2 · Non-Nested Alternative Formulations

How does the Kupiec test perform when the true data-generating process exhibits stochastic volatility, and why does unconditional backtesting fail to detect time-varying conditional coverage?

Solution

When volatility is stochastic, large innovations occur in temporal clusters. A model with static volatility will experience zero exceptions during low-volatility regimes and numerous consecutive exceptions during volatility spikes. Over an annual window, the total count may exactly equal the expected 2.5 exceptions, causing the Kupiec test to report a perfect pass despite the model failing catastrophically to forecast conditional risk on each day of market turbulence.

R3 · Regulatory Gaming and Window Exploitation

Describe how a trading desk can strategically time portfolio risk to pass the 250-day Kupiec test after experiencing an early run of exceptions.

Solution

If a desk experiences 4 exceptions within the first two months of a rolling 250-day period, the risk manager recognizes that a 5th exception will trigger Yellow zone capital penalties. The desk can artificially de-risk (slashing leverage or hedging via liquid index futures) for the remaining 10 months, virtually guaranteeing zero additional exceptions. The desk thereby stays in the Green zone while suppressing genuine risk-taking and masking the model's true conditional vulnerability.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. Under the null hypothesis of an accurate 99% VaR model, what is the distribution of the Kupiec likelihood ratio statistic as sample size \(T \to \infty\)?

    Options

    By Wilks' theorem, since the null hypothesis fixes one parameter (\(p = 1 - \alpha\)), the test statistic converges to \(\chi^2(1)\).

  2. In a standard regulatory trading year of \(T = 250\) days, what is the expected number of exceptions for an accurate 99% 1-day VaR model?

    \(\E[X] = T(1 - \alpha) = 250 \times 0.01 = 2.5\).

  3. What is the critical value for the Kupiec POF test at the 5% significance level?

    Options

    The 95th percentile of the \(\chi^2(1)\) distribution is 3.841.

  4. In the Basel Committee traffic light system for 99% 1-day VaR over 250 days, how many exceptions place a model into the Red zone?

    Options

    Under Basel rules, 0-4 exceptions is Green, 5-9 exceptions is Yellow, and 10+ exceptions triggers automatic Red zone rejection.

  5. What is the baseline market risk capital multiplier \(k\) in the Basel Green zone?

    The statutory minimum market risk capital multiplier is \(k = 3.0\).

  6. What is the primary statistical weakness of the Kupiec POF test in finite samples (\(T = 250\))?

    Options

    As Kupiec (1995) documented, with only 250 observations, the test has only ~10.8% power to reject a model that fails twice as often as claimed.

  7. Why does the Kupiec POF test fail to detect exception clustering?

    Options

    The Kupiec test is unconditional: it sums the binary indicators without conditioning on prior-day exceptions or volatility states.

  8. When the sample exception rate \(\hat{p}\) exactly equals the nominal failure probability \(p\), what is the value of \(LR_{\text{POF}}\)?

    When \(\hat{p} = p\), \(L(p) = L(\hat{p})\), so \(\ln(L(p) / L(\hat{p})) = \ln(1) = 0\), yielding \(LR_{\text{POF}} = 0.0\).
