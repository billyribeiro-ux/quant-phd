Value-at-Risk emerged in the 1990s as the universal benchmark for trading desk risk and regulatory capital. By summarizing complex portfolio risks into a single monetary threshold, VaR enabled systematic capital allocation. However, VaR suffers from a fatal mathematical flaw: it is not subadditive. Merging two portfolios can produce a joint VaR strictly greater than the sum of their individual VaRs, penalizing diversification and encouraging regulatory arbitrage.

1Mathematical Definition of Value-at-Risk

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \Prob)\) be a probability space. Consider a portfolio with value \(V_t\) at time \(t\) and \(V_{t+1}\) at time \(t+1\). In quantitative risk management, risk is formulated in terms of the loss random variable:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{t+1} = -(V_{t+1} - V_t) \end{aligned} \]

where positive values of \(L_{t+1}\) denote financial losses and negative values represent gains. Let \(F_L(l) = \Prob(L \le l)\) denote the cumulative distribution function of portfolio loss.

Definition 1.1 (Value-at-Risk)

Given a confidence level \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\), the Value-at-Risk at level \(\alpha\) is the generalized lower quantile of the loss distribution:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L) = q_\alpha(L) = \inf\{l \in \R : F_L(l) \ge \alpha\} \end{aligned} \]

When the loss cumulative distribution function \(F_L\) is continuous and strictly increasing, Value-at-Risk is the exact inverse: \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L) = F_L^{-1}(\alpha)\). In regulatory market risk standards under the Basel framework, typical parameters are \(\alpha = 0.99\) over a 10-day holding horizon or \(\alpha = 0.95\) for daily internal monitoring.

2Estimation Paradigms: Parametric Gaussian vs Historical Simulation

Two primary methodologies dominate industry estimation of Value-at-Risk:

Theorem 1.1 (Parametric Gaussian VaR)

Assume portfolio loss follows a normal distribution \(L \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\). Then for any confidence level \(\alpha \in (0.5, 1)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{norm}}(L) = \mu + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(\alpha) \end{aligned} \]

where \(\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)\) denotes the standard normal quantile function.

Proof

Standardizing the loss variable gives \(Z = (L - \mu) / \sigma \sim \Normal(0, 1)\). By definition of the quantile:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Prob(L \le \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha) = \Prob\left(Z \le \frac{\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha - \mu}{\sigma}\right) = \Phi\left(\frac{\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha - \mu}{\sigma}\right) = \alpha \end{aligned} \]

Applying the strictly monotonic inverse \(\Phi^{-1}\) to both sides yields \((\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha - \mu) / \sigma = \Phi^{-1}(\alpha)\). Solving for \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha\) completes the proof.

In contrast, Historical Simulation VaR makes no parametric distributional assumption. Given a sample of historical losses \(\{l_1, l_2, \dots, l_T\}\), empirical VaR is computed as the sample quantile:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha^{\text{hist}} = l_{(\lceil T \alpha \rceil)} \end{aligned} \]

where \(l_{(1)} \le l_{(2)} \le \dots \le l_{(T)}\) are the order statistics.

3The Failure of Subadditivity (Artzner et al. 1999)

An axiomatic property of any sound risk measure is subadditivity: the risk of a combined portfolio must not exceed the sum of the individual risks. A risk measure \(\rho\) is subadditive if for all loss random variables \(L_1, L_2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho(L_1 + L_2) \le \rho(L_1) + \rho(L_2) \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 1.2 (Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath 1999: Non-Subadditivity of VaR)

Value-at-Risk is not subadditive. There exist probability distributions and portfolios for which:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_1 + L_2) > \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_1) + \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_2) \end{aligned} \]
Proof (Constructive Counterexample)

Consider two independent corporate loans or short digital option positions, each with notional principal $100. Each loan defaults independently with probability \(p = 0.008\) (0.8%), losing the entire $100 notional, and repays with probability \(1 - p = 0.992\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Prob(L_1 = 100) = 0.008, \quad \Prob(L_1 = 0) = 0.992 \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \Prob(L_2 = 100) = 0.008, \quad \Prob(L_2 = 0) = 0.992 \end{aligned} \]

At confidence level \(\alpha = 0.99\), since \(\Prob(L_1 \le 0) = 0.992 > 0.99\), we have \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}(L_1) = 0\). Identically, \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}(L_2) = 0\). The sum of individual VaRs is \(0 + 0 = 0\).

Now consider the combined portfolio \(L = L_1 + L_2\). Because defaults are independent:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Prob(L = 0) = \Prob(L_1 = 0) \Prob(L_2 = 0) = 0.992^2 = 0.984064 \end{aligned} \]

The probability of zero loss is \(0.984064 < 0.99\). Consequently:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Prob(L \ge 100) = 1 - 0.984064 = 0.015936 > 0.01 \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, the 99% quantile of combined loss is \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}(L_1 + L_2) = 100\). We obtain:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}(L_1 + L_2) = 100 > \mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}(L_1) + \mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}(L_2) = 0 \end{aligned} \]

which strictly violates subadditivity as established by Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath (1999).

Lab 1 · Empirical VaR and Non-Subadditivity Verification

Simulation of continuous Gaussian returns and independent digital credit defaults will verify that continuous VaR scales monotonically with confidence, while discrete default portfolios produce strict non-subadditivity: VaR(L1 + L2) = 100 > VaR(L1) + VaR(L2) = 0.

"""Lab 1: Empirical Value-at-Risk and Non-Subadditivity Proof.

Demonstrates Value-at-Risk computation on empirical loss distributions
and proves the failure of subadditivity: VaR(L1 + L2) > VaR(L1) + VaR(L2).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    # Set seed for exact deterministic reproduction
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

    # 1. Continuous financial asset loss distribution (Gaussian vs heavy-tailed)
    n_days = 1000
    daily_vol = 0.015
    losses_normal = rng.normal(loc=0.0005, scale=daily_vol, size=n_days)

    alpha_95 = 0.95
    alpha_99 = 0.99

    var_norm_95 = float(np.quantile(losses_normal, alpha_95, method="linear"))
    var_norm_99 = float(np.quantile(losses_normal, alpha_99, method="linear"))

    print(f"Continuous 95% Historical VaR: {var_norm_95:.6f}")
    print(f"Continuous 99% Historical VaR: {var_norm_99:.6f}")

    # 2. Canonical Artzner et al. (1999) Non-Subadditivity Counterexample
    # Two independent credit or digital option positions, each with default probability p = 0.8%
    # and default loss of 100 currency units.
    p_default = 0.008
    notional = 100.0
    n_sims = 100_000

    l1 = (rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, size=n_sims) < p_default) * notional
    l2 = (rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, size=n_sims) < p_default) * notional
    l_combined = l1 + l2

    var_l1_99 = float(np.quantile(l1, alpha_99, method="linear"))
    var_l2_99 = float(np.quantile(l2, alpha_99, method="linear"))
    var_port_99 = float(np.quantile(l_combined, alpha_99, method="linear"))

    print(f"Stand-alone VaR_0.99(L1): {var_l1_99:.2f}")
    print(f"Stand-alone VaR_0.99(L2): {var_l2_99:.2f}")
    print(f"Sum of Stand-alone VaRs: {var_l1_99 + var_l2_99:.2f}")
    print(f"Portfolio VaR_0.99(L1 + L2): {var_port_99:.2f}")
    print(f"VaR Subadditivity Holds: {var_port_99 <= (var_l1_99 + var_l2_99)}")


main()
Continuous 95% Historical VaR: 0.024744
Continuous 99% Historical VaR: 0.032640
Stand-alone VaR_0.99(L1): 0.00
Stand-alone VaR_0.99(L2): 0.00
Sum of Stand-alone VaRs: 0.00
Portfolio VaR_0.99(L1 + L2): 100.00
VaR Subadditivity Holds: False

The empirical output validates Theorem 1.2: individual 99% VaRs are zero, yet their combined portfolio VaR is 100. This numerical result proves that Value-at-Risk can penalize diversification.

4Where the canon breaks

When return distributions are jointly elliptical (such as multivariate normal or Student-t distributions), Value-at-Risk satisfies subadditivity. However, in real financial markets, trading portfolios hold nonlinear derivative payoffs (such as short out-of-the-money options, barrier options, and credit default swaps). For these asymmetric, fat-tailed instruments, VaR routinely violates subadditivity. In credit crises, a bank dividing its book across two subsidiaries can artificially lower its reported aggregate regulatory capital by taking advantage of VaR's non-subadditivity, creating dangerous systemic hidden leverage as demonstrated by McNeil, Frey & Embrechts (2015).

5Misconceptions

  1. “Value-at-Risk represents the maximum possible loss that a portfolio can experience.”

    VaR is merely a quantile threshold, not a ceiling. By definition, in \((1 - \alpha)\) of holding periods (e.g., 2.5 days per trading year for 99% VaR), losses exceed VaR. VaR provides zero information about the magnitude of losses in the tail beyond the quantile threshold.

  2. “Diversification always reduces Value-at-Risk.”

    Because VaR is not subadditive on non-elliptical or skewed distributions, combining uncorrelated or independent risks can strictly increase VaR relative to holding concentrated positions, as proved in Theorem 1.2.

  3. “Parametric Gaussian VaR and Historical Simulation VaR converge to identical estimates as sample size grows.”

    If the true underlying data-generating process has fat tails (leptokurtosis) or negative skewness, the parametric Gaussian VaR will permanently underestimate extreme quantiles, regardless of sample size \(T\), because the normal distribution tail decays at rate \(\exp(-x^2 / 2)\).

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Gaussian VaR Calculation

A portfolio has a current market value of $50,000,000. Daily arithmetic returns are normally distributed with mean \(\mu = 0.0004\) and daily standard deviation \(\sigma = 0.012\). Compute the 1-day 99% Value-at-Risk in percentage terms and in dollar terms.

Solution

Under normality, the loss variable \(L = -R\) has mean \(\mu_L = -\mu = -0.0004\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_L = \sigma = 0.012\). The 99th percentile of standard normal is \(\Phi^{-1}(0.99) \approx 2.32635\). Percentage VaR is \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99} = -0.0004 + 0.012 \times 2.32635 = -0.0004 + 0.027916 = 0.027516\) (2.7516%). Dollar VaR is \(\$50{,}000{,}000 \times 0.027516 = \$1{,}375{,}810\).

M2 · Historical Simulation Quantile

A risk analyst observes 1,000 daily percentage returns. The 15 worst daily losses (in percent) sorted from largest to smallest are: 7.2%, 6.5%, 5.8%, 5.1%, 4.6%, 4.2%, 3.9%, 3.7%, 3.5%, 3.3%, 3.1%, 2.9%, 2.7%, 2.5%, 2.4%. Compute the empirical 99% Historical VaR.

Solution

For \(T = 1{,}000\) observations and \(\alpha = 0.99\), the quantile index is \(k = \lceil 1000 \times 0.99 \rceil = 990\). The number of observations exceeding the quantile is \(1000 - 990 = 10\). The 10th worst loss is 3.3%. Thus, the empirical 99% Historical VaR is 3.3%.

M3 · Horizon Scaling with Square-Root-of-Time

If a bank reports a 1-day 99% VaR of $4,000,000, calculate the 10-day 99% VaR under the assumption of independent and identically distributed Gaussian daily returns.

Solution

Under the assumption that daily returns are i.i.d. normal with mean zero, multi-day variance scales linearly with time: \(\sigma_{10} = \sqrt{10} \sigma_1\). Therefore, \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}^{10\text{-day}} = \sqrt{10} \times \mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}^{1\text{-day}} \approx 3.16228 \times \$4{,}000{,}000 = \$12{,}649{,}111\).

Proof

P1 · Monotonicity of Quantiles in Confidence Level

Prove that for any loss random variable \(L\), the function \(\alpha \mapsto \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\) is non-decreasing on \((0, 1)\).

Solution

Let \(\alpha_1 < \alpha_2 \in (0, 1)\). By definition, \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L) = \inf\{l \in \R : F_L(l) \ge \alpha\}\). Define the set \(S(\alpha) = \{l \in \R : F_L(l) \ge \alpha\}\). Since \(\alpha_1 < \alpha_2\), if \(F_L(l) \ge \alpha_2\) then \(F_L(l) \ge \alpha_1\). Hence \(S(\alpha_2) \subseteq S(\alpha_1)\). The infimum of a subset is greater than or equal to the infimum of the superset: \(\inf S(\alpha_2) \ge \inf S(\alpha_1)\). Thus \(\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha_2}(L) \ge \mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha_1}(L)\).

P2 · Positive Homogeneity of Value-at-Risk

Prove that for any constant scalar \(c > 0\) and loss random variable \(L\), \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(c L) = c \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\).

Solution

For \(c > 0\), the cumulative distribution function of \(c L\) is \(F_{cL}(l) = \Prob(c L \le l) = \Prob(L \le l / c) = F_L(l / c)\). Therefore, \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(c L) = \inf\{l : F_L(l / c) \ge \alpha\}\). Setting \(y = l / c\), we have \(l = c y\). As \(y\) ranges over \(\{y : F_L(y) \ge \alpha\}\), \(\inf\{c y : F_L(y) \ge \alpha\} = c \inf\{y : F_L(y) \ge \alpha\} = c \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\).

P3 · Subadditivity under Multivariate Normal Distribution

Prove that if losses \((L_1, L_2)^\top\) follow a bivariate normal distribution, then \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_1 + L_2) \le \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_1) + \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_2)\) for any \(\alpha \ge 0.5\).

Solution

Let \(L_1 \sim \Normal(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\) and \(L_2 \sim \Normal(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)\) with correlation \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\). Then \(L_1 + L_2 \sim \Normal(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_p^2)\) where \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + 2 \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2}\). By the triangle inequality, \(\sigma_p \le \sigma_1 + \sigma_2\). For \(\alpha \ge 0.5\), \(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha) \ge 0\). Thus: \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_1 + L_2) = \mu_1 + \mu_2 + \sigma_p \Phi^{-1}(\alpha) \le \mu_1 + \mu_2 + (\sigma_1 + \sigma_2) \Phi^{-1}(\alpha) = \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_1) + \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_2)\).

Empirical

E1 · Fat-Tailed Asset VaR Underestimation

An equity portfolio's daily loss follows a Student-t distribution with \(\nu = 4\) degrees of freedom, mean 0, and variance 0.0004 (\(\sigma = 0.02\)). Compare the true 99% VaR with the Gaussian VaR calculated using the sample standard deviation.

Solution

For standard Student-t with \(\nu = 4\), variance is \(\nu / (\nu - 2) = 4 / 2 = 2.0\). To match \(\sigma = 0.02\), the scale factor is \(s = 0.02 \times \sqrt{2 / 4} = 0.02 \times 0.7071 = 0.014142\). The 99th percentile of standard \(t_4\) is \(t_4^{-1}(0.99) \approx 3.74695\). True VaR is \(0.014142 \times 3.74695 \approx 0.05299\) (5.30%). In contrast, Gaussian VaR is \(0.02 \times 2.32635 \approx 0.04653\) (4.65%). The Gaussian model underestimates the 99% VaR by 12.2%.

E2 · Non-Subadditivity on Short Option Portfolios

A trader writes 1,000 independent out-of-the-money put options on two separate stocks. Each option has a 0.6% probability of expiring in the money with a $50,000 loss, and 99.4% probability of expiring worthless. Compute the individual and joint 99% VaR.

Solution

For each put position, \(\Prob(L_i = 50{,}000) = 0.006\) and \(\Prob(L_i = 0) = 0.994\). Since \(\Prob(L_i \le 0) = 0.994 > 0.99\), \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}(L_1) = \mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}(L_2) = \$0\). For the combined portfolio, \(\Prob(L = 0) = 0.994^2 \approx 0.988036 < 0.99\). The probability of at least one option expiring in the money is \(1 - 0.988036 = 0.011964 > 0.01\). Therefore, \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}(L_1 + L_2) = \$50{,}000 > \$0 + \$0\), exhibiting non-subadditivity.

E3 · Effect of Window Length on Historical VaR

Explain why using a 250-day window for Historical VaR leads to discrete jumps (ghost effects) when an extreme market crash exits the sample window.

Solution

In a rolling 250-day window, each historical observation carries equal weight \(1/250 = 0.4\%\). When a major crash occurs, it immediately enters the window and elevates the 99% VaR. However, exactly 251 trading days later, that observation abruptly exits the window, causing an instantaneous downward jump in reported VaR even if current market conditions are unchanged. This artifact is known as the ghost feature of unweighted historical simulation.

Research

R1 · Regulatory Arbitrage via Non-Subadditivity

How can a financial conglomerate exploit the failure of subadditivity of Value-at-Risk to minimize its regulatory capital requirements across decentralized subsidiaries?

Solution

If capital rules enforce \(\sum \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L_i)\) at the subsidiary level rather than \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(\sum L_i)\) on the consolidated entity, a firm can split concentrated asymmetric risks (such as credit default swap tranches or short volatility exposures) across separate corporate subsidiaries. If each subsidiary's probability of default is just below \(1 - \alpha\), each subsidiary reports \(\mathrm{VaR} = 0\), legally reducing aggregate capital reserves to zero while leaving the parent firm exposed to catastrophic consolidated ruin.

R2 · Coherence Requirements for Portfolio Optimization

Why does minimizing Value-at-Risk in portfolio optimization lead to non-convex optimization problems with multiple local minima?

Solution

Because VaR is not subadditive and not convex with respect to asset weights, the objective function \(w \mapsto \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(w^\top r)\) contains non-convex contours, flat plateaus, and local minima. Optimization algorithms based on gradient descent or quadratic programming fail to find the global risk minimum, frequently locking into portfolios with extreme hidden tail risk.

R3 · Transition to Expected Shortfall

Synthesize the arguments presented by Artzner et al. (1999) that led international banking regulators to abandon Value-at-Risk in favor of Expected Shortfall in the Basel III FRTB framework.

Solution

Artzner et al. (1999) demonstrated that VaR fails the subadditivity axiom, prevents coherent capital aggregation, fails to reflect tail severity beyond the quantile, and creates perverse incentives for risk managers to sell deep out-of-the-money insurance. Expected Shortfall restores subadditivity, satisfies convexity, and incorporates the full conditional expectation of tail losses, eliminating these regulatory blind spots.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the formal definition of Value-at-Risk at confidence level \(\alpha\)?

    Options

    \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L) = \inf\{l : F_L(l) \ge \alpha\}\), which is the generalized lower quantile of the loss distribution.

  2. Why does Value-at-Risk fail to satisfy the subadditivity axiom of coherent risk measures?

    Options

    As proved by Artzner et al. (1999), combining independent binary credit risks or short options can cause tail probability to exceed the threshold, making joint VaR exceed individual VaRs.

  3. What is the value of \(\Phi^{-1}(0.99)\) used in Gaussian 99% VaR calculations to three decimal places?

    \(\Phi^{-1}(0.99) \approx 2.32635 \approx 2.326\).

  4. Under what class of distributions is Value-at-Risk guaranteed to be subadditive?

    Options

    Elliptical symmetry ensures that portfolio variance determines quantiles, allowing the triangle inequality on standard deviations to preserve subadditivity.

  5. What is the "ghost effect" in rolling Historical Simulation VaR?

    Options

    When an unweighted historical window moves past a crisis date, that extreme loss suddenly drops out, causing an artificial discontinuous drop in VaR.

  6. In Lab 1, for the combined portfolio of two independent digital options each with default probability 0.8% and notional 100, what was the portfolio VaR at \(\alpha = 0.99\)?

    Because \(\Prob(L_1 + L_2 = 0) = 0.992^2 \approx 0.9841 < 0.99\), the combined 99% VaR jumps to 100.0.

  7. How does Historical Simulation VaR handle fat tails compared to Parametric Gaussian VaR?

    Options

    Historical simulation is non-parametric and reads directly from the empirical order statistics, preserving skewness and excess kurtosis present in the sample.

  8. What does positive homogeneity of a risk measure require?

    Options

    Positive homogeneity states that scaling position size by \(c > 0\) scales portfolio risk by the exact same proportion \(c\).
