Expected Shortfall & Axiomatic Risk Coherence

The failure of Value-at-Risk to satisfy subadditivity prompted Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath (1999) to establish an axiomatic foundation for risk measurement. A risk measure is coherent if it satisfies translation invariance, subadditivity, positive homogeneity, and monotonicity. Expected Shortfall fulfills all four axioms while capturing tail loss severity beyond the quantile. Furthermore, Rockafellar & Uryasev (2000) showed that Expected Shortfall can be optimized via convex programming, making it the mathematical cornerstone of modern portfolio risk management.

1Axioms of Coherent Risk Measures

Let \(\mathcal{X}\) denote a linear space of real-valued loss random variables on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \Prob)\). A mapping \(\rho: \mathcal{X} \to \R\) is a risk measure, where \(\rho(L)\) represents the minimum capital requirement needed to make loss \(L\) acceptable to regulators or risk managers.

Definition 2.1 (Coherent Risk Measure, Artzner et al. 1999)

A risk measure \(\rho: \mathcal{X} \to \R\) is a coherent risk measure if it satisfies the following four axioms for all \(L, L_1, L_2 \in \mathcal{X}\):

  1. Translation Invariance: For every deterministic cash constant \(c \in \R\): \[ \begin{aligned} \rho(L + c) = \rho(L) + c \end{aligned} \] Adding a certain loss of \(c\) increases the capital requirement by exactly \(c\).
  2. Subadditivity: \[ \begin{aligned} \rho(L_1 + L_2) \le \rho(L_1) + \rho(L_2) \end{aligned} \] Mergers and diversification never increase aggregate risk.
  3. Positive Homogeneity: For every scalar \(\lambda > 0\): \[ \begin{aligned} \rho(\lambda L) = \lambda \rho(L) \end{aligned} \] Scaling position size by \(\lambda\) scales risk proportionally.
  4. Monotonicity: If \(L_1 \le L_2\) almost surely (\(\Prob(L_1 \le L_2) = 1\)): \[ \begin{aligned} \rho(L_1) \le \rho(L_2) \end{aligned} \] A portfolio that produces lower losses in every possible state carries lower risk.
Remark 2.1 (Convexity of Coherent Risk Measures)

Subadditivity and positive homogeneity together imply convexity. For any \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho(\lambda L_1 + (1 - \lambda) L_2) \le \lambda \rho(L_1) + (1 - \lambda) \rho(L_2) \end{aligned} \]

Convexity guarantees that diversification is weakly encouraged and prevents the emergence of spurious local minima during risk optimization.

2Expected Shortfall Formulation and Subadditivity

Definition 2.2 (Expected Shortfall)

Given confidence level \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\), the Expected Shortfall (also termed Conditional VaR or Tail VaR) is the integral average of Value-at-Risk across tail probabilities:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \int_\alpha^1 \mathrm{VaR}_u(L) \, du \end{aligned} \]

When the loss variable \(L\) has a continuous cumulative distribution function \(F_L\), Expected Shortfall equals the conditional expectation of losses strictly exceeding Value-at-Risk:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) = \E[L \mid L > \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)] \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 2.1 (Coherence of Expected Shortfall)

For any confidence level \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\), Expected Shortfall \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha\) is a coherent risk measure satisfying Translation Invariance, Subadditivity, Positive Homogeneity, and Monotonicity.

Proof (Dual Representation Proof Sketch)

By the dual representation theorem for coherent risk measures (Artzner et al. 1999), a risk measure \(\rho\) is coherent if and only if it can be represented as the supremum of expectations over a closed convex set of probability density scenario measures \(\mathcal{P}_\alpha\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho(L) = \sup_{Q \in \mathcal{P}_\alpha} \E^Q[L] \end{aligned} \]

For Expected Shortfall, \(\mathcal{P}_\alpha = \left\{ Q \ll \Prob : \frac{dQ}{d\Prob} \le \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \text{ a.s.} \right\}\). Because the supremum of linear functionals preserves subadditivity, for any \(L_1, L_2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{Q \in \mathcal{P}_\alpha} \E^Q[L_1 + L_2] \le \sup_{Q \in \mathcal{P}_\alpha} \E^Q[L_1] + \sup_{Q \in \mathcal{P}_\alpha} \E^Q[L_2] \end{aligned} \]

which establishes \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L_1 + L_2) \le \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L_1) + \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L_2)\). Positive homogeneity, translation invariance, and monotonicity follow immediately from expectation properties as proved by Artzner et al. (1999).

3Rockafellar & Uryasev (2000) Variational Formulation

A central challenge in quantitative finance was that computing Expected Shortfall directly required integrating conditional tail distributions. Rockafellar & Uryasev (2000) resolved this by introducing a convex variational objective:

Theorem 2.2 (Rockafellar & Uryasev Optimization Principle)

Define the auxiliary convex loss function \(F_\alpha: \R \to \R\) by:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_\alpha(v) = v + \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \E\left[\max(0, L - v)\right] \end{aligned} \]

Then \(F_\alpha(v)\) is continuous, convex, and differentiable almost everywhere. Furthermore:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) = \min_{v \in \R} F_\alpha(v) \end{aligned} \]

and the optimal minimizing threshold is exactly Value-at-Risk: \(v^* = \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\).

Lab 2 · Expected Shortfall Coherence and Variational Optimization

Expected Shortfall will satisfy subadditivity on the Artzner credit portfolio where VaR failed, and the Rockafellar-Uryasev variational objective F_alpha(v) will achieve its global minimum at VaR with minimum value exactly equal to Expected Shortfall.

"""Lab 2: Expected Shortfall, Coherence Axioms, and Rockafellar-Uryasev Optimization.

Demonstrates the subadditivity of Expected Shortfall on the Artzner portfolio
and validates the Rockafellar-Uryasev (2000) variational formulation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

    # 1. Coherence on Discrete Credit Portfolio (Artzner et al. 1999 setup)
    p_default = 0.008
    notional = 100.0
    n_sims = 100_000
    alpha = 0.99

    l1 = (rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, size=n_sims) < p_default) * notional
    l2 = (rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, size=n_sims) < p_default) * notional
    l_combined = l1 + l2

    def compute_es(loss_arr: np.ndarray, a: float) -> float:
        var = float(np.quantile(loss_arr, a, method="linear"))
        tail = loss_arr[loss_arr > var]
        return float(np.mean(tail)) if len(tail) > 0 else var

    es_l1 = compute_es(l1, alpha)
    es_l2 = compute_es(l2, alpha)
    es_port = compute_es(l_combined, alpha)

    print(f"Stand-alone ES_0.99(L1): {es_l1:.2f}")
    print(f"Stand-alone ES_0.99(L2): {es_l2:.2f}")
    print(f"Sum of Stand-alone ES: {es_l1 + es_l2:.2f}")
    print(f"Portfolio ES_0.99(L1 + L2): {es_port:.2f}")
    print(f"ES Subadditivity Holds: {es_port <= (es_l1 + es_l2)}")

    # 2. Rockafellar-Uryasev (2000) Convex Variational Objective on Gaussian losses
    losses_norm = rng.normal(loc=0.001, scale=0.02, size=50_000)
    var_empirical = float(np.quantile(losses_norm, alpha, method="linear"))
    es_empirical = float(np.mean(losses_norm[losses_norm > var_empirical]))

    # Evaluate F_alpha(v) = v + (1 / (1 - alpha)) * E[max(0, L - v)]
    candidate_vs = np.linspace(var_empirical * 0.7, var_empirical * 1.3, 100)
    f_vals = [float(v + (1.0 / (1.0 - alpha)) * np.mean(np.maximum(0.0, losses_norm - v))) for v in candidate_vs]

    min_idx = int(np.argmin(f_vals))
    v_opt = candidate_vs[min_idx]
    f_min = f_vals[min_idx]

    print(f"Empirical VaR_0.99: {var_empirical:.6f}")
    print(f"Rockafellar-Uryasev Argmin v*: {v_opt:.6f}")
    print(f"Empirical ES_0.99: {es_empirical:.6f}")
    print(f"Rockafellar-Uryasev Min F(v*): {f_min:.6f}")
    print(f"RU Optimization Discrepancy: {abs(f_min - es_empirical):.6f}")


main()

Stand-alone ES_0.99(L2): 100.00
Sum of Stand-alone ES: 200.00
Portfolio ES_0.99(L1 + L2): 200.00
ES Subadditivity Holds: True

4Where the canon breaks

Despite its superior mathematical properties, Expected Shortfall suffers from a critical statistical weakness: it is not standalone elicitable (Gneiting 2011). A risk measure is elicitable if it minimizes the expected value of a scoring function, enabling competitive point forecast evaluation. While VaR is elicitable (scoring via the quantile check function), ES alone possesses no strictly consistent scoring function. Fissler & Ziegel (2016) proved that ES is elicitable only jointly with VaR as a two-dimensional vector \((\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha, \mathrm{ES}_\alpha)\). Consequently, backtesting Expected Shortfall requires joint statistical hypothesis testing rather than simple binomial exception counting.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Expected Shortfall is simply Value-at-Risk evaluated at a slightly higher confidence level.”

    Evaluating VaR at a higher confidence level (e.g., 99.5% instead of 99%) still measures a single quantile and ignores the shape of the distribution beyond that quantile. If a portfolio has unbounded or extreme tail losses (such as short deep out-of-the-money options), VaR at 99.5% remains blind to the catastrophic losses beyond 99.5%, whereas Expected Shortfall integrates the entire tail.

  2. “Expected Shortfall is impossible to backtest because it is not elicitable.”

    Non-elicitability does not mean a measure cannot be backtested. While standalone scoring functions do not exist, cumulative violation size, standardized tail residuals (McNeil & Frey 2000), and joint Fissler-Ziegel scoring allow rigorous backtesting of Expected Shortfall models.

  3. “Subadditivity implies that any merger between two risky financial institutions is always beneficial.”

    Subadditivity guarantees only that mathematical capital requirements aggregate super-additively. In practice, operational complexity, correlated liquidity runs, and contagion can generate systemic risks not captured by static linear portfolio summation.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Gaussian Expected Shortfall Closed Form

For a portfolio with normally distributed daily loss \(L \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 0.015\), compute the 1-day 99% Value-at-Risk and 99% Expected Shortfall using the Gaussian analytical formula.

Solution

For standard normal distribution at \(\alpha = 0.99\), the quantile is \(z = \Phi^{-1}(0.99) \approx 2.32635\). The standard normal density at this point is \(\phi(z) = (1 / \sqrt{2\pi}) \exp(-2.32635^2 / 2) \approx 0.026652\). The tail ratio is \(\phi(z) / (1 - \alpha) = 0.026652 / 0.01 \approx 2.6652\). Therefore: \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99} = 0 + 0.015 \times 2.32635 \approx 0.034895\) (3.490%), and \(\mathrm{ES}_{0.99} = 0 + 0.015 \times 2.6652 \approx 0.039978\) (3.998%).

M2 · Discrete Loss Expected Shortfall

A discrete loss distribution has five equally likely outcomes: $10, $25, $40, $70, and $150. Calculate the 80% Value-at-Risk and 80% Expected Shortfall.

Solution

Each outcome has probability \(p_i = 0.20\). The cumulative probabilities are 0.20 ($10), 0.40 ($25), 0.60 ($40), 0.80 ($70), and 1.00 ($150). The 80% quantile is \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.80} = \$70\). The only loss strictly exceeding $70 is $150. Thus, \(\mathrm{ES}_{0.80} = \E[L \mid L > \$70] = \$150\). Alternatively, using the integral definition: \(\mathrm{ES}_{0.80} = \frac{1}{0.20} \int_{0.8}^{1.0} \mathrm{VaR}_u \, du = \frac{1}{0.20} (0.20 \times \$150) = \$150\).

M3 · Uniform Distribution Expected Shortfall

Suppose losses follow a uniform distribution \(L \sim \text{Uniform}(0, B)\). Derive the exact analytical formulas for \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\) and \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L)\) as functions of \(B\) and \(\alpha\).

Solution

For \(L \sim \text{Uniform}(0, B)\), \(F_L(l) = l / B\) for \(l \in [0, B]\). The quantile is \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L) = \alpha B\). Conditional on \(L > \alpha B\), \(L\) is uniformly distributed on \([\alpha B, B]\). The expected value of a uniform distribution is its midpoint: \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) = \frac{\alpha B + B}{2} = \frac{1 + \alpha}{2} B\). For example, at \(\alpha = 0.90\), \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.90} = 0.90 B\) and \(\mathrm{ES}_{0.90} = 0.95 B\).

Proof

P1 · ES Strictly Exceeds VaR

Prove that for any continuous loss random variable \(L\) with non-degenerate tail variance, \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) > \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\) for all \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\).

Solution

By definition, \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) = \E[L \mid L > \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)] = \int_{\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha}^\infty l \frac{f_L(l)}{1 - \alpha} \, dl\). Since the loss distribution is continuous and non-degenerate on \(( \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha, \infty )\), for all \(l > \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha\) we have \(l - \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha > 0\). Integrating gives: \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) - \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L) = \int_{\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha}^\infty (l - \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha) \frac{f_L(l)}{1 - \alpha} \, dl > 0\). Hence \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) > \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\).

P2 · First-Order Optimality of Rockafellar-Uryasev Function

Prove that the derivative of the Rockafellar-Uryasev objective function \(F_\alpha(v) = v + \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \E[\max(0, L - v)]\) with respect to \(v\) is zero if and only if \(F_L(v) = \alpha\), assuming \(F_L\) is continuous.

Solution

Write \(F_\alpha(v) = v + \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \int_v^\infty (l - v) f_L(l) \, dl\). Applying Leibniz's rule to differentiate with respect to \(v\): \(\frac{d F_\alpha}{dv} = 1 + \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \left[ - (v - v) f_L(v) + \int_v^\infty (-1) f_L(l) \, dl \right] = 1 - \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \Prob(L > v) = 1 - \frac{1 - F_L(v)}{1 - \alpha}\). Setting \(\frac{d F_\alpha}{dv} = 0\) gives \(1 = \frac{1 - F_L(v)}{1 - \alpha} \iff 1 - \alpha = 1 - F_L(v) \iff F_L(v) = \alpha\). Since \(F_L(v) = \alpha\) defines the quantile, the unique minimizer is \(v^* = \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\).

P3 · Translation Invariance of Expected Shortfall

Prove that Expected Shortfall satisfies the Translation Invariance axiom: \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L + c) = \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) + c\) for any deterministic cash amount \(c \in \R\).

Solution

For \(c \in \R\), \(\mathrm{VaR}_u(L + c) = \mathrm{VaR}_u(L) + c\) for all \(u \in (0, 1)\) by definition of quantiles. Substituting this into the Expected Shortfall integral definition: \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L + c) = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \int_\alpha^1 \mathrm{VaR}_u(L + c) \, du = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \int_\alpha^1 (\mathrm{VaR}_u(L) + c) \, du = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \int_\alpha^1 \mathrm{VaR}_u(L) \, du + \frac{c}{1 - \alpha} \int_\alpha^1 1 \, du = \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) + c \frac{1 - \alpha}{1 - \alpha} = \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) + c\).

Empirical

E1 · Student-t Tail Discrepancy

Using Student-t losses with \(\nu = 4\) degrees of freedom and standard deviation \(\sigma = 0.02\), compare the ratio of \(\mathrm{ES}_{0.99} / \mathrm{VaR}_{0.99}\) under Student-t versus under the normal distribution.

Solution

Under normality, \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99} = 2.32635 \sigma\) and \(\mathrm{ES}_{0.99} = 2.6652 \sigma\), giving ratio \(\mathrm{ES} / \mathrm{VaR} = 2.6652 / 2.32635 \approx 1.1457\) (+14.6%). Under Student-t with \(\nu = 4\), the scale is \(s = \sigma \sqrt{2 / 4} \approx 0.014142\). \(t_4^{-1}(0.99) \approx 3.74695\), so \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.99} \approx 0.05299\). The analytical ES for \(t_4\) is \(\mathrm{ES}_{0.99} \approx 0.07185\). The ratio is \(0.07185 / 0.05299 \approx 1.3559\) (+35.6%). Fat tails expand Expected Shortfall relative to VaR by more than double the Gaussian spread.

E2 · Convex Optimization of Portfolio ES

Formulate the portfolio allocation problem that minimizes 99% Expected Shortfall subject to a required target return \(\mu_{\text{target}}\) as a linear programming problem using sample scenarios.

Solution

Given \(S\) historical scenarios of asset returns \(r_s \in \R^N\), portfolio loss in scenario \(s\) is \(l_s(w) = -w^\top r_s\). By the Rockafellar-Uryasev formulation, \(\min_w \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(w) \iff \min_{w, v, u_s} v + \frac{1}{(1 - \alpha) S} \sum_{s=1}^S u_s\) subject to: \(u_s \ge -w^\top r_s - v\), \(u_s \ge 0\) for \(s = 1, \dots, S\), \(w^\top \bar{r} \ge \mu_{\text{target}}\), and \(\mathbf{1}^\top w = 1\). This is a standard linear program (LP) that can be solved efficiently for thousands of assets and scenarios without local minima.

E3 · Impact of Outliers on Historical ES vs VaR

Suppose a portfolio data set has one extreme outlier loss equal to 10 times the 99% VaR. How does this single outlier affect 99% Historical VaR versus 99% Historical ES in a sample of \(T = 500\) days?

Solution

For \(T = 500\) and \(\alpha = 0.99\), exactly \(\lceil 500 \times 0.01 \rceil = 5\) observations exceed the 99% quantile. Value-at-Risk is determined exclusively by the 5th worst loss; replacing the worst loss with an extreme 10x outlier has zero effect on VaR. In contrast, Expected Shortfall is the average of the 5 worst losses: \(\mathrm{ES} = \frac{1}{5} (l_{(1)} + l_{(2)} + l_{(3)} + l_{(4)} + l_{(5)})\). The 10x outlier dramatically increases the mean of the 5 tail losses, increasing reported ES by roughly 180%, accurately signaling severe tail exposure.

Research

R1 · Fissler-Ziegel Joint Elicitability

Explain the mathematical significance of Fissler & Ziegel (2016) proving that \((\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha, \mathrm{ES}_\alpha)\) is jointly elicitable for risk management practice.

Solution

Gneiting (2011) proved that Expected Shortfall alone lacks a strictly consistent scoring function, creating skepticism about whether banks could ever statistically backtest or rank rival ES forecasting models. Fissler & Ziegel (2016) proved that the vector \((\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha, \mathrm{ES}_\alpha)\) is jointly elicitable under a family of bivariate scoring functions \(S(v_1, v_2, y)\). This breakthrough established the mathematical validity of comparative backtesting, allowing regulators and model validation teams to evaluate and compare competing internal Expected Shortfall models.

R2 · Spectral Risk Measures as Coherent Generalizations

How does the class of spectral risk measures generalize Expected Shortfall, and what conditions on the risk aversion spectrum \(\phi(u)\) preserve coherence?

Solution

Acerbi (2002) defined spectral risk measures as weighted integrals of quantiles: \(\rho_\phi(L) = \int_0^1 \mathrm{VaR}_u(L) \phi(u) \, du\). Expected Shortfall is the special case where the spectrum is a step function: \(\phi(u) = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \indic_{\{u \ge \alpha\}}\). Acerbi proved that \(\rho_\phi\) is a coherent risk measure if and only if \(\phi\) satisfies: (1) non-negativity \(\phi(u) \ge 0\), (2) normalization \(\int_0^1 \phi(u) \, du = 1\), and (3) non-decreasing risk aversion \(\phi'(u) \ge 0\), assigning weakly greater weight to worse losses.

R3 · Optimal Capital Allocation via Euler's Theorem on ES

Show that because Expected Shortfall is homogeneous of degree 1 in portfolio weights \(w\), Euler's homogeneous function theorem applies to allocate risk capital across business units.

Solution

Because \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(\lambda w) = \lambda \mathrm{ES}_\alpha(w)\) for \(\lambda > 0\), Euler's theorem guarantees \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(w) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \frac{\partial \mathrm{ES}_\alpha}{\partial w_i}\). Tasche (1999) proved that under mild smoothness assumptions, \(\frac{\partial \mathrm{ES}_\alpha}{\partial w_i} = \E[r_i \mid r_p \le -\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(r_p)]\). This quantity, Marginal Expected Shortfall (MES), decomposes total firm-wide capital into additive desk contributions without residual, providing an economically sound foundation for performance attribution and capital charges.
