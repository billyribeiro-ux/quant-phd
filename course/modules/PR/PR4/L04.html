Independence & Conditional Coverage: Christoffersen Framework

  • Uses: kupiec-test, volatility clustering
  • Introduces: christoffersen-test
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Unconditional backtests such as the Kupiec POF test count total exceptions but remain blind to their timing. Because market volatility exhibits strong temporal clustering, an inaccurate risk model can experience a cluster of consecutive catastrophic exceptions during market stress while still passing unconditional tests across the annual horizon. Christoffersen (1998) resolved this blind spot by formulating a joint likelihood ratio framework that simultaneously tests unconditional coverage and serial independence via first-order Markov chains.

1The Clustering Failure of Unconditional Backtests

Recall the binary violation indicator sequence \(I_t = \mathbf{1}_{\{L_t > \mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha, t}\}}\). For a risk measurement model to be conditionally valid with respect to the information filtration \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\), the exception process must satisfy:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[I_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \Prob(L_t > \mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha, t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = 1 - \alpha \end{aligned} \]

This conditional coverage hypothesis imposes two distinct mathematical requirements:

  1. Unconditional Coverage: The unconditional probability of an exception must equal the nominal target \(\Prob(I_t = 1) = 1 - \alpha\).
  2. Independence: The indicator random variables \(I_t\) must be independent and identically distributed across time: \(\Cov(I_t, I_{t-k}) = 0\) for all lags \(k \ge 1\).

If a risk model fails to update its volatility forecasts dynamically during market shocks, exceptions will occur in consecutive runs. While the total number of exceptions across 250 days may match the expected 2.5, clustered exceptions threaten institutional solvency because losses deplete capital reserves before the firm can de-risk.

2First-Order Markov Chain Representation

Christoffersen (1998) modeled the dependence structure of binary exceptions as a two-state, first-order Markov chain. Let the transition probability of moving from state \(i \in \{0, 1\}\) at time \(t-1\) to state \(j \in \{0, 1\}\) at time \(t\) be denoted by \(\pi_{ij} = \Prob(I_t = j \mid I_{t-1} = i)\). The transition probability matrix is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pi = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \pi_{01} & \pi_{01} \\ 1 - \pi_{11} & \pi_{11} \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\pi_{01}\) is the probability of an exception following a non-exception day, and \(\pi_{11}\) is the probability of an exception following an exception day. Let \(n_{ij}\) count the number of observed transitions from state \(i\) to state \(j\) in the sample of \(T\) trading days. The likelihood function under this first-order Markov alternative is:

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\Pi) = (1 - \pi_{01})^{n_{00}} \pi_{01}^{n_{01}} (1 - \pi_{11})^{n_{10}} \pi_{11}^{n_{11}} \end{aligned} \]

The unconstrained maximum likelihood estimators for the transition probabilities are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\pi}_{01} = \frac{n_{01}}{n_{00} + n_{01}}, \quad \hat{\pi}_{11} = \frac{n_{11}}{n_{10} + n_{11}} \end{aligned} \]

3The Christoffersen (1998) Likelihood Ratio Tests

Definition 2.1 (Christoffersen Test Framework)

The Christoffersen test decomposes conditional coverage into two independent likelihood ratio components:

  1. Independence Test: Tests \(H_0^{\text{ind}}: \pi_{01} = \pi_{11} = \pi\) against \(H_1^{\text{ind}}: \pi_{01} \ne \pi_{11}\).
  2. Conditional Coverage Test: Tests \(H_0^{\text{cc}}: \pi_{01} = \pi_{11} = 1 - \alpha\) against the general Markov alternative.
Theorem 2.1 (Christoffersen Independence and Conditional Coverage Statistics)

Under the null hypothesis of serial independence, the pooled exception rate across all observed transitions is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\pi} = \frac{n_{01} + n_{11}}{n_{00} + n_{01} + n_{10} + n_{11}} \end{aligned} \]

The independence likelihood ratio test statistic is:

\[ \begin{aligned} LR_{\text{ind}} = -2 \ln\left( \frac{L(\hat{\Pi}_0)}{L(\hat{\Pi})} \right) = -2 \ln\left( \frac{(1 - \hat{\pi})^{n_{00} + n_{10}} \hat{\pi}^{n_{01} + n_{11}}}{(1 - \hat{\pi}_{01})^{n_{00}} \hat{\pi}_{01}^{n_{01}} (1 - \hat{\pi}_{11})^{n_{10}} \hat{\pi}_{11}^{n_{11}}} \right) \end{aligned} \]

Under \(H_0^{\text{ind}}\), \(LR_{\text{ind}} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(1)\). Furthermore, the joint conditional coverage statistic satisfies the exact additive decomposition:

\[ \begin{aligned} LR_{\text{cc}} = LR_{\text{uc}} + LR_{\text{ind}} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(2) \end{aligned} \]

where \(LR_{\text{uc}}\) is the Kupiec unconditional coverage statistic.

Proof

Under \(H_0^{\text{ind}}\), the transition probabilities are identical regardless of the prior state: \(\pi_{01} = \pi_{11} = \pi\). The restricted likelihood is \(L(\hat{\Pi}_0) = (1 - \hat{\pi})^{n_{00} + n_{10}} \hat{\pi}^{n_{01} + n_{11}}\). The unrestricted likelihood \(L(\hat{\Pi})\) estimates two free parameters \((\pi_{01}, \pi_{11})\). Forming \(-2 \ln(L(\hat{\Pi}_0) / L(\hat{\Pi}))\) restricts 1 degree of freedom, so by Wilks' theorem, \(LR_{\text{ind}} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(1)\).

For the joint test of conditional coverage \(H_0^{\text{cc}}: \pi_{01} = \pi_{11} = p = 1 - \alpha\), the restricted likelihood is \(L(p) = (1 - p)^{n_{00} + n_{10}} p^{n_{01} + n_{11}}\). We can factor the joint likelihood ratio:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{L(p)}{L(\hat{\Pi})} = \frac{L(p)}{L(\hat{\Pi}_0)} \times \frac{L(\hat{\Pi}_0)}{L(\hat{\Pi})} \end{aligned} \]

Taking \(-2 \ln\) of both sides gives \(LR_{\text{cc}} = LR_{\text{uc}} + LR_{\text{ind}}\). Because the restrictions on unconditional frequency and Markov transitions are asymptotically orthogonal, the sum of two independent \(\chi^2(1)\) variables converges to \(\chi^2(2)\) as proved by Christoffersen (1998).

Lab 4 · Christoffersen Independence and Conditional Coverage Testing

Testing two 500-day violation series with identical 1% violation counts (5 hits) will demonstrate that Kupiec POF produces identical statistics (LR_uc = 0.0), whereas Christoffersen's independence test passes the spaced series (LR_ind = 0.10, p = 0.75) and decisively rejects the clustered series (LR_ind = 36.57, p < 1e-8).

"""Lab 4: Christoffersen Independence and Conditional Coverage Backtest.

Demonstrates that unconditional coverage (Kupiec) fails to detect exception
clustering, while Christoffersen (1998) likelihood ratio tests reveal volatility clustering.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from typing import Any

import numpy as np


def christoffersen_framework(
    hits: np.ndarray[Any, Any],
    alpha: float = 0.99,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Execute Kupiec POF and Christoffersen independence and conditional coverage tests."""
    arr = np.asarray(hits, dtype=np.int64)
    t = len(arr)
    x = int(np.sum(arr))
    p = 1.0 - alpha
    p_hat = x / t

    # 1. Unconditional Coverage (Kupiec 1995)
    if x == 0:
        lr_uc = -2.0 * t * math.log(1.0 - p)
    elif x == t:
        lr_uc = -2.0 * t * math.log(p)
    else:
        lr_uc = -2.0 * (
            (t - x) * math.log(1.0 - p) + x * math.log(p) - (t - x) * math.log(1.0 - p_hat) - x * math.log(p_hat)
        )
    lr_uc = max(0.0, float(lr_uc))
    p_uc = float(math.erfc(math.sqrt(lr_uc / 2.0)))

    # 2. Independence of Exceptions (Christoffersen 1998)
    h_prev = arr[:-1]
    h_next = arr[1:]
    n00 = int(np.sum((h_prev == 0) & (h_next == 0)))
    n01 = int(np.sum((h_prev == 0) & (h_next == 1)))
    n10 = int(np.sum((h_prev == 1) & (h_next == 0)))
    n11 = int(np.sum((h_prev == 1) & (h_next == 1)))

    pi01 = n01 / (n00 + n01) if (n00 + n01) > 0 else 0.0
    pi11 = n11 / (n10 + n11) if (n10 + n11) > 0 else 0.0
    pi = (n01 + n11) / (n00 + n01 + n10 + n11) if (n00 + n01 + n10 + n11) > 0 else 0.0

    def safe_log(v: float) -> float:
        return math.log(v) if v > 1e-12 else 0.0

    log_l0 = (n00 + n10) * safe_log(1.0 - pi) + (n01 + n11) * safe_log(pi)
    log_l1 = n00 * safe_log(1.0 - pi01) + n01 * safe_log(pi01) + n10 * safe_log(1.0 - pi11) + n11 * safe_log(pi11)

    lr_ind = max(0.0, -2.0 * (log_l0 - log_l1))
    p_ind = float(math.erfc(math.sqrt(lr_ind / 2.0)))

    # 3. Joint Conditional Coverage
    lr_cc = lr_uc + lr_ind
    p_cc = float(math.exp(-lr_cc / 2.0))

    return {
        "n00": float(n00),
        "n01": float(n01),
        "n10": float(n10),
        "n11": float(n11),
        "pi01": pi01,
        "pi11": pi11,
        "lr_uc": lr_uc,
        "p_uc": p_uc,
        "lr_ind": lr_ind,
        "p_ind": p_ind,
        "lr_cc": lr_cc,
        "p_cc": p_cc,
    }


def main() -> None:
    t_days = 500
    alpha = 0.99
    expected_hits = int(t_days * (1.0 - alpha))  # 5 hits

    # Sequence A: Evenly spaced (iid-like) violations
    hits_spread = np.zeros(t_days, dtype=np.int64)
    hits_spread[[50, 150, 250, 350, 450]] = 1

    # Sequence B: Clustered violations during a volatility regime shock
    hits_clustered = np.zeros(t_days, dtype=np.int64)
    hits_clustered[100:105] = 1

    res_a = christoffersen_framework(hits_spread, alpha)
    res_b = christoffersen_framework(hits_clustered, alpha)

    print(f"Total Observations: {t_days} | Nominal 99% Expected Exceptions: {expected_hits}")
    print("=" * 70)
    print("Sequence A: Independent Spaced Exceptions")
    n00_a, n01_a = int(res_a["n00"]), int(res_a["n01"])
    n10_a, n11_a = int(res_a["n10"]), int(res_a["n11"])
    print(f"  Transitions: n00={n00_a}, n01={n01_a}, n10={n10_a}, n11={n11_a}")
    print(f"  pi01={res_a['pi01']:.4f}, pi11={res_a['pi11']:.4f}")
    print(f"  LR_uc  = {res_a['lr_uc']:.4f} (p={res_a['p_uc']:.4f})")
    print(f"  LR_ind = {res_a['lr_ind']:.4f} (p={res_a['p_ind']:.4f})")
    print(f"  LR_cc  = {res_a['lr_cc']:.4f} (p={res_a['p_cc']:.4f})")
    print("=" * 70)
    print("Sequence B: Clustered Contagion Exceptions")
    n00_b, n01_b = int(res_b["n00"]), int(res_b["n01"])
    n10_b, n11_b = int(res_b["n10"]), int(res_b["n11"])
    print(f"  Transitions: n00={n00_b}, n01={n01_b}, n10={n10_b}, n11={n11_b}")
    print(f"  pi01={res_b['pi01']:.4f}, pi11={res_b['pi11']:.4f}")
    print(f"  LR_uc  = {res_b['lr_uc']:.4f} (p={res_b['p_uc']:.4f})")
    print(f"  LR_ind = {res_b['lr_ind']:.4f} (p={res_b['p_ind']:.4e})")
    print(f"  LR_cc  = {res_b['lr_cc']:.4f} (p={res_b['p_cc']:.4e})")

4Where the canon breaks

The Christoffersen first-order Markov framework tests only lag-1 serial dependence. In financial markets, volatility shocks often generate exception clustering with multi-day dispersion (such as exceptions occurring every 2 to 4 days during an extended liquidity crisis). If exceptions are separated by a single non-exception day (\(I_t = 1, I_{t+1} = 0, I_{t+2} = 1\)), \(n_{11} = 0\), causing the first-order Markov test to miss the clustering entirely. To capture higher-order and continuous time dynamics, Christoffersen & Pelletier (2004) introduced duration-based tests modeling the time elapsed between consecutive exceptions via Weibull or exponential hazard rates as discussed by McNeil, Frey & Embrechts (2015).

5Misconceptions

  1. “Passing the Kupiec POF test guarantees that a risk model's exceptions are independent over time.”

    The Kupiec test evaluates only unconditional frequency. A risk model that produces 5 exceptions on 5 consecutive days during an autumn market flash crash and zero exceptions for the rest of the year will pass the Kupiec test with a p-value near 1.0, despite exhibiting catastrophic violation clustering.

  2. “The conditional coverage test LR_cc can reject a model only if the independence test LR_ind rejects it.”

    Because \(LR_{\text{cc}} = LR_{\text{uc}} + LR_{\text{ind}}\), a model can be rejected by the joint test even if neither component alone reaches the individual 5% critical value of 3.841. If \(LR_{\text{uc}} = 3.2\) and \(LR_{\text{ind}} = 3.2\), neither rejects on \(\chi^2(1)\), but their sum \(LR_{\text{cc}} = 6.4\) exceeds the \(\chi^2(2)\) critical value of 5.991, rejecting joint conditional coverage.

  3. “If pi11 is zero in a backtest, the risk model is guaranteed to have independent exceptions.”

    A value of \(\pi_{11} = 0\) simply means no two exceptions occurred on immediately adjacent days. Clustering can occur at lags of 2, 3, or 5 days, which first-order transition matrices cannot detect.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Transition Matrix Estimation

A backtest of 500 trading days records the following transition counts: \(n_{00} = 485\), \(n_{01} = 5\), \(n_{10} = 5\), \(n_{11} = 4\). Calculate the maximum likelihood estimates \(\hat{\pi}_{01}\), \(\hat{\pi}_{11}\), and the pooled exception probability \(\hat{\pi}\).

Solution

The transition estimates are: \(\hat{\pi}_{01} = \frac{n_{01}}{n_{00} + n_{01}} = \frac{5}{485 + 5} = \frac{5}{490} \approx 0.010204\) (1.020%). \(\hat{\pi}_{11} = \frac{n_{11}}{n_{10} + n_{11}} = \frac{4}{5 + 4} = \frac{4}{9} \approx 0.444444\) (44.44%). The pooled rate is \(\hat{\pi} = \frac{n_{01} + n_{11}}{n_{00} + n_{01} + n_{10} + n_{11}} = \frac{5 + 4}{485 + 5 + 5 + 4} = \frac{9}{499} \approx 0.018036\) (1.804%).

M2 · Christoffersen Independence Test Statistic

Using the transition counts from problem M1 (\(n_{00}=485, n_{01}=5, n_{10}=5, n_{11}=4\)), calculate the independence test statistic \(LR_{\text{ind}}\) and test at the 5% level (\(\chi_{0.05}^2(1) = 3.841\)).

Solution

Under \(H_0\), \(\ln L_0 = (n_{00} + n_{10}) \ln(1 - \hat{\pi}) + (n_{01} + n_{11}) \ln(\hat{\pi}) = 490 \ln(0.981964) + 9 \ln(0.018036) = 490(-0.018200) + 9(-4.01538) = -8.9180 - 36.1384 = -45.0564\). Under \(H_1\), \(\ln L_1 = 485 \ln(1 - \hat{\pi}_{01}) + 5 \ln(\hat{\pi}_{01}) + 5 \ln(1 - \hat{\pi}_{11}) + 4 \ln(\hat{\pi}_{11}) = 485 \ln(0.989796) + 5 \ln(0.010204) + 5 \ln(0.555556) + 4 \ln(0.444444) = 485(-0.010256) + 5(-4.58497) + 5(-0.58779) + 4(-0.81093) = -4.9742 - 22.9248 - 2.9389 - 3.2437 = -34.0816\). The test statistic is \(LR_{\text{ind}} = -2 (-45.0564 - (-34.0816)) = -2(-10.9748) = 21.9496\). Because \(21.9496 > 3.841\) (\(p < 10^{-5}\)), independence is decisively rejected.

M3 · Joint Conditional Coverage Decision

Suppose a 99% VaR model produces \(LR_{\text{uc}} = 2.80\) and \(LR_{\text{ind}} = 3.50\). Determine the joint statistic \(LR_{\text{cc}}\), state its degrees of freedom, and evaluate whether the model passes conditional coverage at the 5% level (\(\chi_{0.05}^2(2) = 5.991\)).

Solution

By the additive property of Christoffersen statistics, \(LR_{\text{cc}} = LR_{\text{uc}} + LR_{\text{ind}} = 2.80 + 3.50 = 6.30\). Under the null hypothesis of accurate conditional coverage, \(LR_{\text{cc}} \sim \chi^2(2)\). Because \(LR_{\text{cc}} = 6.30 > 5.991\), the null hypothesis of accurate conditional coverage is rejected at the 5% level, even though neither individual test rejected at the 5% level (\(2.80 < 3.841\) and \(3.50 < 3.841\)).

Proof

P1 · Invariance under Independent Bernoulli

Prove that if the sequence \(I_t\) is generated by an independent Bernoulli process with parameter \(p\), then \(\E[\hat{\pi}_{01}] = \E[\hat{\pi}_{11}] = p\).

Solution

For an independent Bernoulli process, \(\Prob(I_t = 1 \mid I_{t-1} = i) = \Prob(I_t = 1) = p\) for all \(i \in \{0, 1\}\). Conditional on observing \(n_{00} + n_{01} > 0\), the transition count \(n_{01}\) is the sum of independent Bernoulli(\(p\)) trials over all non-exception days, so \(n_{01} \mid (n_{00} + n_{01}) \sim \text{Binomial}(n_{00} + n_{01}, p)\). Therefore, \(\E[\hat{\pi}_{01} \mid n_{00} + n_{01}] = \frac{(n_{00} + n_{01}) p}{n_{00} + n_{01}} = p\). By the law of total expectation, \(\E[\hat{\pi}_{01}] = p\). Symmetrically, \(n_{11} \mid (n_{10} + n_{11}) \sim \text{Binomial}(n_{10} + n_{11}, p)\), so \(\E[\hat{\pi}_{11}] = p\).

P2 · Chi-Squared Survival Closed Form for 2 Degrees of Freedom

Prove that for a random variable \(Y \sim \chi^2(2)\), the survival function has the exact closed form \(\Prob(Y > y) = \exp(-y / 2)\) for all \(y \ge 0\).

Solution

The probability density function of \(\chi^2(k)\) with \(k = 2\) degrees of freedom is \(f(y) = \frac{1}{2^{k/2} \Gamma(k/2)} y^{k/2 - 1} e^{-y/2} = \frac{1}{2^1 \Gamma(1)} y^0 e^{-y/2} = \frac{1}{2} e^{-y/2}\) for \(y \ge 0\). This is the exponential distribution with rate parameter \(\lambda = 1/2\). The survival function is the integral of the PDF from \(y\) to \(\infty\): \(\Prob(Y > y) = \int_y^\infty \frac{1}{2} e^{-u/2} \, du = \left[ -e^{-u/2} \right]_y^\infty = 0 - (-e^{-y/2}) = \exp(-y / 2)\).

P3 · Asymptotic Orthogonality of Unconditional and Independence Tests

Prove that the gradient of the log-likelihood evaluated at the null hypothesis \(\pi_{01} = \pi_{11} = p\) has an expected outer product (information matrix) that is block diagonal with respect to the unconditional mean parameter and the transition difference parameter.

Solution

Reparameterize the Markov chain in terms of the unconditional mean \(\theta_1 = p\) and the persistence gap \(\theta_2 = \pi_{11} - \pi_{01}\). Under \(H_0\), \(\theta_2 = 0\). The stationary probabilities are \(\Prob(I_t = 0) = 1 - p\) and \(\Prob(I_t = 1) = p\). Differentiating the log-likelihood with respect to \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) and taking expectations yields \(\E\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ln L}{\partial \theta_1 \partial \theta_2}\right] = 0\) because the expectation of the product of cross-derivatives vanishes under the null hypothesis of independence. Block diagonality of the Fisher information matrix guarantees that the likelihood ratio statistics \(LR_{\text{uc}}\) and \(LR_{\text{ind}}\) are asymptotically independent.

Empirical

E1 · GARCH Filtering to Eliminate Exception Clustering

Explain how replacing an unconditional historical simulation VaR model with a GARCH(1,1) filtered historical simulation model eliminates exception clustering during volatile regimes.

Solution

Unconditional historical VaR assumes constant volatility, so when market volatility surges, forecasted VaR remains flat, causing numerous consecutive losses to breach the threshold. A GARCH(1,1) filter estimates conditional volatility \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2\) daily. When a large loss occurs, GARCH immediately scales up \(\sigma_{t+1}\), elevating the next day's VaR. This adaptive scaling absorbs volatility spikes, maintaining a constant conditional failure probability \(\Prob(L_t > \mathrm{VaR}_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = 0.01\) and eliminating violation clustering.

E2 · Duration Between Exceptions Analysis

In a 500-day sample, 5 exceptions occur on days 40, 41, 42, 43, and 44. Calculate the empirical durations \(D_i = t_i - t_{i-1}\) between consecutive exceptions and compare them with the expected duration under i.i.d. 99% VaR.

Solution

The durations between the consecutive exceptions are \(D_2 = 41 - 40 = 1\), \(D_3 = 42 - 41 = 1\), \(D_4 = 43 - 42 = 1\), and \(D_5 = 44 - 43 = 1\). Under the null hypothesis of an accurate i.i.d. 99% VaR model, the time between exceptions follows a geometric distribution with mean \(\E[D] = 1 / p = 1 / 0.01 = 100\) trading days. Observing four consecutive durations of 1 day is extraordinarily improbable (\(0.01^4 = 10^{-8}\)), providing definitive empirical evidence of severe model breakdown.

E3 · Impact of Execution Lags on Independence

Why does a 1-day implementation lag in updating risk model parameters induce artificial exception clustering in empirical backtests?

Solution

If risk engine systems calculate end-of-day risk batch parameters with a 1-day or 2-day delay, the risk model will trade under yesterday's outdated volatility estimate on the morning of a major market crash. The first exception occurs on day 1. Because the risk model has not yet updated, it remains undersized on day 2, causing a second exception. This operational latency induces artificial serial correlation in the exception series, causing well-specified theoretical models to fail the Christoffersen test due to IT infrastructure lags.

Research

R1 · Duration-Based Backtesting (Christoffersen & Pelletier 2004)

How does the Christoffersen & Pelletier (2004) continuous duration framework overcome the limitations of discrete Markov transition tests?

Solution

Discrete Markov tests examine only transitions between adjacent days (\(I_t\) and \(I_{t-1}\)). If exceptions cluster loosely (e.g., occurring on Monday, Thursday, and the following Tuesday), first-order transition counts record \(n_{11} = 0\), failing to reject independence. Christoffersen & Pelletier (2004) model the continuous duration \(D_i\) between consecutive violations using a Weibull hazard function \(h(D) = a b (a D)^{b-1}\). Under \(H_0\) of independent i.i.d. exceptions, the duration distribution must be memoryless (exponential distribution with \(b = 1\)). Testing \(b = 1\) against \(b \ne 1\) detects clustering at all temporal horizons without choosing arbitrary lag windows.

R2 · Dynamic Quantile Regression (Engle & Manganelli 2004)

Describe the CAViaR (Conditional Autoregressive Value at Risk) test and explain how its Dynamic Quantile (DQ) test generalizes Christoffersen conditional coverage.

Solution

Engle & Manganelli (2004) defined the standardized hit variable \(Hit_t = I_t - (1 - \alpha)\). Under conditional coverage, \(Hit_t\) must be orthogonal to any lagged information variable, including lagged hits, lagged VaR levels, and lagged returns: \(\E[Hit_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\). The Dynamic Quantile (DQ) test runs an artificial regression \(Hit_t = X_t \beta + u_t\) where \(X_t = [1, Hit_{t-1}, \dots, Hit_{t-q}, \mathrm{VaR}_t]\) and tests \(H_0: \beta = 0\). The resulting Wald test statistic \(\hat{\beta}^\top (X^\top X) \hat{\beta} / [p(1-p)] \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(q+2)\), testing independence simultaneously across multiple lags and conditioning variables.

R3 · Regulatory Policy Implications of Conditional Failure

Why did the Basel Committee historically hesitate to incorporate Christoffersen independence tests into statutory bank capital penalties despite their statistical superiority?

Solution

Regulators hesitated for two reasons: (1) Operational simplicity: the Kupiec test depends only on an integer exception count, creating transparent rules that can be audited by bank examiners without econometric software. (2) Finite-sample noise: with only 250 daily observations, the cell count \(n_{11}\) is frequently 0 or 1, making asymptotic chi-squared approximations vulnerable to small-sample distortion. However, in supervisory internal reviews and FRTB model validation, conditional coverage tests are universally required as qualitative diagnostic standards.

  1. What does the Christoffersen (1998) independence test specifically evaluate?

    Options

    The test evaluates \(H_0: \pi_{01} = \pi_{11}\), testing whether exception occurrences form a first-order Markov chain versus independent Bernoulli draws.

  2. What is the asymptotic distribution of the joint conditional coverage statistic \(LR_{\text{cc}}\)?

    Options

    \(LR_{\text{cc}} = LR_{\text{uc}} + LR_{\text{ind}}\). The sum of two asymptotically orthogonal \(\chi^2(1)\) statistics is distributed as \(\chi^2(2)\).

  3. What is the critical value for the joint conditional coverage test \(LR_{\text{cc}}\) at the 5% significance level to three decimal places?

    The 95th percentile of the \(\chi^2(2)\) distribution is approximately 5.991.

  4. If a backtest yields \(n_{00} = 240, n_{01} = 5, n_{10} = 5, n_{11} = 0\), what is \(\hat{\pi}_{11}\)?

    Options

    \(\hat{\pi}_{11} = n_{11} / (n_{10} + n_{11}) = 0 / (5 + 0) = 0.0\).

  5. Why can a risk model fail conditional coverage (\(LR_{\text{cc}}\)) even if it passes both \(LR_{\text{uc}}\) and \(LR_{\text{ind}}\) individually?

    Options

    If \(LR_{\text{uc}} = 3.2 < 3.841\) and \(LR_{\text{ind}} = 3.2 < 3.841\), neither rejects individually, but their sum \(6.4 > 5.991\) rejects the joint test.

  6. In Lab 4, what was the value of \(LR_{\text{ind}}\) for Sequence B with clustered exceptions to two decimal places?

    From Lab 4, \(LR_{\text{ind}} \approx 36.5743 \approx 36.57\).

  7. What continuous distribution models the durations between exceptions under the null hypothesis of independence?

    Options

    In continuous time, independent arrivals follow a Poisson process, whose inter-arrival times are exponentially distributed.

  8. What is the primary cause of empirical exception clustering in trading books?

    Options

    When models do not dynamically scale VaR during volatility clusters, multiple consecutive losses breach the static risk threshold.
