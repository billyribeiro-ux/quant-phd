Human Capital, Labour Income & Lifecycle Asset Allocation

For most households, financial wealth is dwarfed by human capital: the discounted present value of expected future labor earnings. Because safe labor earnings function as an implicit position in non-tradable risk-free bonds, total wealth portfolio balance requires young investors to maintain heavily equity-biased financial assets. Over the working career, as human capital is exhausted and converted into liquid financial wealth, the ratio of implicit bonds to financial assets monotonically declines. Dynamic lifecycle allocation provides the mathematical foundation for optimal portfolio glide paths while formalizing wage cyclicality, background income risk, and borrowing frictions.

1. Total Wealth & Human Capital Valuation

Traditional mean-variance analysis and pure financial Merton models assume that the investor's balance sheet consists entirely of tradable financial assets \(W_t\). In reality, for a worker entering the labor force at age 25, liquid financial wealth represents less than 5% of their total economic resources. The overwhelming majority of their wealth resides in human capital \(H_t\), defined as the present discounted value of future labor income, as analyzed by Campbell & Viceira (2002) and Campbell (2006).

Definition 5.1 (Total Economic Wealth & Human Capital)

Let \(W_t\) denote liquid marketable financial wealth at time \(t\), and let \(Y_s\) denote the continuous rate of labor income at date \(s \ge t\). Under an arbitrage-free pricing kernel (stochastic discount factor) \(\{M_s\}_{s \ge 0}\), the investor's human capital \(H_t\) is the expected present value of labor earnings up to retirement date \(T_R\):

\[ H_t \equiv \E_t \left[ \int_t^{T_R} \frac{M_s}{M_t} Y_s \, ds \right]. \]

Total economic wealth \(W_t^{\mathrm{tot}}\) is the sum of tradable financial wealth and non-tradable human capital:

\[ W_t^{\mathrm{tot}} = W_t + H_t. \]

If labor earnings \(Y_t\) are deterministic or perfectly uncorrelated with financial market innovations, human capital is discounted at the risk-free rate \(r\):

\[ H_t = \int_t^{T_R} e^{-r(s-t)} Y_s \, ds. \]

Because labor contracts cannot be sold or capitalized on an exchange due to legal prohibitions against indentured servitude and extreme moral hazard, human capital is non-tradable. An investor cannot directly short their own human capital or liquidate future wages today. Consequently, all risk management and asset allocation adjustments must be executed exclusively through the liquid financial portfolio \(W_t\).

2. The Bodie-Merton-Samuelson Lifecycle Rule

The interaction between non-tradable human capital and financial portfolio choice was resolved in continuous time by Bodie, Merton, and Samuelson (1992). Consider an investor with constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) \(\gamma\) maximizing expected utility of terminal wealth at retirement \(T_R\), facing an equity market with expected excess return \(\mu - r\) and volatility \(\sigma\).

Theorem 5.1 (Bodie-Merton-Samuelson Portfolio Rule)

Let total wealth be \(W_t^{\mathrm{tot}} = W_t + H_t\). Under constant investment opportunities and CRRA utility \(U(W) = \frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\), the optimal dollar equity allocation on total wealth is determined by the classic Merton fraction:

\[ \alpha^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}. \]

Suppose human capital \(H_t\) exhibits an effective equity exposure (beta) \(\beta_H \equiv \frac{\Cov(dY/Y, dS/S)}{\Var(dS/S)}\). Then the optimal dollar allocation to equities in the liquid financial portfolio \(S_t \equiv \alpha_t^F W_t\) satisfies:

\[ S_t = \alpha^* W_t^{\mathrm{tot}} - \beta_H H_t = \alpha^* (W_t + H_t) - \beta_H H_t. \]

Dividing by liquid financial wealth \(W_t\) yields the optimal financial equity share:

\[ \alpha_t^F = \alpha^* + (\alpha^* - \beta_H) \frac{H_t}{W_t}. \]
Proof of Theorem 5.1

By value function homogeneity under CRRA utility and constant investment opportunities, the investor seeks an aggregate equity exposure equal to \(\alpha^*\) times total economic wealth \(W_t^{\mathrm{tot}}\). The aggregate dollar exposure to equity risk across both balance sheet components is:

\[ \text{Total Equity Exposure} = S_t + \beta_H H_t. \]

Setting this equal to the optimal total wealth target \(\alpha^* W_t^{\mathrm{tot}} = \alpha^* (W_t + H_t)\):

\[ S_t + \beta_H H_t = \alpha^* W_t + \alpha^* H_t \implies S_t = \alpha^* W_t + (\alpha^* - \beta_H) H_t. \]

Dividing both sides by liquid financial wealth \(W_t\) gives:

\[ \alpha_t^F \equiv \frac{S_t}{W_t} = \alpha^* + (\alpha^* - \beta_H) \frac{H_t}{W_t}. \]

This completes the proof.

Corollary 5.1 (The Riskless Human Capital Glide Path)

If human capital is riskless or uncorrelated with equity returns (\(\beta_H = 0\)), labor income behaves as an implicit holding of risk-free government bonds. The optimal financial equity weight simplifies to:

\[ \alpha_t^F = \alpha^* \left(1 + \frac{H_t}{W_t}\right). \]

Because \(H_t / W_t\) decreases monotonically from large values (\(> 10\)) at career entry to 0 at retirement, \(\alpha_t^F\) strictly declines over the investor's working lifetime toward the Merton benchmark \(\alpha^*\).

This result provides the rigorous economic justification for age-dependent asset allocation: young investors should allocate aggressive percentages of financial wealth to equities not because of fallacious time diversification, but because their implicit asset portfolio is overwhelmingly dominated by safe bonds.

3. Wage Cyclicality, Background Risk & Borrowing Constraints

The idealized formula \(\alpha_t^F = \alpha^* (1 + H_t/W_t)\) implies that a 25-year-old with \(\alpha^* = 0.40\) and \(H_t/W_t = 15\) should hold an equity allocation of \(\alpha_t^F = 0.40 \times 16 = 6.40\) (640% equity, financed by 540% leverage). In practice, two decisive economic forces alter this conclusion:

A. Wage Cyclicality and Human Capital Beta (\(\beta_H\))

Not all labor income is bond-like. If a worker's industry is highly cyclical (e.g. investment banking, construction, venture capital, executive compensation with equity grants), labor earnings covary strongly with equity returns (\(\beta_H > 0\)). As proven in Theorem 5.1:

  • If \(\beta_H < \alpha^*\): Labor is safer than the target portfolio. The worker overweights financial equities (\(\alpha_t^F > \alpha^*\)), gliding down over time.
  • If \(\beta_H = \alpha^*\): Labor income exactly mimics the target equity-bond mix. The financial portfolio weight is horizon-invariant: \(\alpha_t^F = \alpha^*\).
  • If \(\beta_H > \alpha^*\): Labor income is more equity-like than the investor's target total wealth allocation. Because the worker already holds excessive equity risk in their implicit career wealth, the optimal financial portfolio must underweight equities: \(\alpha_t^F < \alpha^*\). In this regime, the financial equity fraction glides upward toward \(\alpha^*\) as retirement approaches!

B. Borrowing Constraints and Leverage Caps

Unsecured borrowing against future wages is legally unenforceable. Retail financial intermediaries impose strict margin requirements preventing retail investors from taking 400% equity leverage. Under a leverage cap \(\bar{\alpha} = 1.0\) (no margin borrowing) and no-short-sales constraint (\(\alpha_t^F \ge 0\)), the constrained policy is:

\[ \alpha_t^{F, \mathrm{constrained}} = \min\left(1.0, \max\left(0.0, \alpha^* + (\alpha^* - \beta_H) \frac{H_t}{W_t}\right)\right). \]

Under realistic calibrations, young workers are heavily constrained at \(\alpha_t^F = 1.0\) (100% equity). The constraint typically binds for the first 15–20 years of the working career, creating a flat plateau at 100% equity before the downward glide begins once financial wealth accumulates sufficiently.

Lab 1 · Lifecycle Allocation & Human Capital Depletion

Simulating 10,000 career paths from age 25 to 65 will show that safe human capital induces young workers to hold maximum equity (100%), with equity allocations gliding down toward Merton's 38.58% fraction as human capital depletes, whereas cyclical human capital (beta_H=0.50) depresses early-career equity exposure.

"""Lab PR6-L05-1: Human Capital and Lifecycle Asset Allocation.

SIMULATED: Lifecycle simulation of human capital depletion, financial wealth accumulation,
and optimal equity glide paths under riskless vs cyclical human capital.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def simulate_lifecycle_allocation(
    start_age: int = 25,
    retire_age: int = 65,
    initial_wage: float = 50000.0,
    initial_wealth: float = 20000.0,
    savings_rate: float = 0.15,
    r_discount: float = 0.03,
    equity_risk_premium: float = 0.05,
    equity_vol: float = 0.18,
    gamma: float = 4.0,
    beta_h_cyclical: float = 0.50,
    leverage_cap: float = 1.0,
    n_paths: int = 5000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[int, dict[str, float]]:
    """Simulate career wage paths, human capital valuation, and financial portfolio equity weights."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    years = retire_age - start_age

    # Base target equity weight on total wealth (Merton fraction)
    alpha_star = equity_risk_premium / (gamma * equity_vol**2)

    # Wage trajectory parameters: humped shape peaking around age 50
    # Peak at t = 25 (age 50): g_1 = 0.04, g_2 = 0.0008 -> d(log_w)/dt = 0.04 - 0.0016 t = 0 at t=25
    deterministic_wages = np.zeros(years + 1)
    for i in range(years + 1):
        t = float(i)
        deterministic_wages[i] = initial_wage * math.exp(0.04 * t - 0.0008 * t**2)

    # Human capital at each age: discounted sum of future wages to retirement
    human_capital = np.zeros(years + 1)
    for i in range(years + 1):
        rem_years = (years + 1) - i
        discounts = (1.0 + r_discount) ** (-np.arange(rem_years))
        human_capital[i] = float(np.sum(deterministic_wages[i:] * discounts))

    # Simulate financial wealth paths under riskless human capital policy
    wealth_riskless = np.zeros((n_paths, years + 1))
    wealth_cyclical = np.zeros((n_paths, years + 1))
    wealth_riskless[:, 0] = initial_wealth
    wealth_cyclical[:, 0] = initial_wealth

    alpha_f_riskless = np.zeros(years + 1)
    alpha_f_cyclical = np.zeros(years + 1)

    for i in range(years):
        age = start_age + i
        w_r = np.maximum(wealth_riskless[:, i], 1000.0)
        w_c = np.maximum(wealth_cyclical[:, i], 1000.0)

        # Average optimal financial allocation at this age
        ratio_r = human_capital[i] / np.mean(w_r)
        ratio_c = human_capital[i] / np.mean(w_c)

        # Riskless human capital: alpha_F = alpha* * (1 + H/W)
        uncap_r = alpha_star * (1.0 + ratio_r)
        alpha_f_riskless[i] = min(leverage_cap, max(0.0, uncap_r))

        # Cyclical human capital: alpha_F = alpha* + (alpha* - beta_H) * (H/W)
        uncap_c = alpha_star + (alpha_star - beta_h_cyclical) * ratio_c
        alpha_f_cyclical[i] = min(leverage_cap, max(0.0, uncap_c))

        # Step forward 1 year
        z = rng.standard_normal(n_paths)
        r_eq = r_discount + equity_risk_premium + equity_vol * z
        r_port_r = (1.0 - alpha_f_riskless[i]) * r_discount + alpha_f_riskless[i] * r_eq
        r_port_c = (1.0 - alpha_f_cyclical[i]) * r_discount + alpha_f_cyclical[i] * r_eq

        # Add savings
        savings = savings_rate * deterministic_wages[i]
        wealth_riskless[:, i + 1] = wealth_riskless[:, i] * (1.0 + r_port_r) + savings
        wealth_cyclical[:, i + 1] = wealth_cyclical[:, i] * (1.0 + r_port_c) + savings

    # At retirement (age 65), H = 0, so alpha_F = alpha*
    alpha_f_riskless[-1] = min(leverage_cap, alpha_star)
    alpha_f_cyclical[-1] = min(leverage_cap, alpha_star)

    results: dict[int, dict[str, float]] = {}
    sampled_ages = [25, 35, 45, 55, 65]
    for age in sampled_ages:
        idx = age - start_age
        results[age] = {
            "wage": deterministic_wages[idx],
            "human_capital": human_capital[idx],
            "wealth_riskless": float(np.mean(wealth_riskless[:, idx])),
            "alpha_riskless": alpha_f_riskless[idx],
            "alpha_cyclical": alpha_f_cyclical[idx],
        }

    return results


def main() -> None:
    res = simulate_lifecycle_allocation(
        start_age=25,
        retire_age=65,
        initial_wage=50000.0,
        initial_wealth=20000.0,
        savings_rate=0.15,
        r_discount=0.03,
        equity_risk_premium=0.05,
        equity_vol=0.18,
        gamma=4.0,
        beta_h_cyclical=0.50,
        leverage_cap=1.0,
        n_paths=10000,
        seed=42,
    )

    # Base target Merton weight
    alpha_merton = 0.05 / (4.0 * 0.18**2)

    print("Human Capital, Labour Income & Lifecycle Portfolio Allocation:")
    print(f"Target Total Wealth Equity Fraction (alpha*): {alpha_merton:.4f} (38.58%)")
    print("=" * 90)
    print(
        f"{'Age':<6} {'Annual Wage ($)':<16} {'Human Capital ($)':<18} "
        f"{'Financial W ($)':<16} {'Alpha (Safe HC)':<16} {'Alpha (Cyclical)':<16}"
    )
    print("-" * 90)

    for age in [25, 35, 45, 55, 65]:
        d = res[age]
        print(
            f"{age:<6d} {d['wage']:<16.1f} {d['human_capital']:<18.1f} "
            f"{d['wealth_riskless']:<16.1f} {d['alpha_riskless']:<16.4f} {d['alpha_cyclical']:<16.4f}"
        )

    print("=" * 90)
    print("Core Theoretical Findings (Bodie, Merton & Samuelson 1992):")
    print("1. Safe Human Capital: Young investors hold maximum equity (100%) because implicit wealth is bond-like.")
    print("2. Human Capital Depletion: As age increases and H/W declines, optimal equity glides down toward alpha*.")
    print("3. Cyclical Labour: When labour has high equity beta (beta_H > alpha*), young workers hold LESS equity.")

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical Bodie-Merton-Samuelson model operates under frictionless assumptions that break down across several empirical dimensions documented in institutional household finance:

  • Non-Tradability & Illiquidity of Human Capital: Because future wages cannot be pledged as collateral for non-mortgage loans (Campbell 2006), households cannot borrow against human capital to smooth adverse market shocks. When equity markets collapse, a young investor cannot draw down human capital to rebalance into cheap equities, forcing suboptimal portfolio freezes.
  • Uninsurable Idiosyncratic Background Risk: Real-world labor earnings contain large uninsurable idiosyncratic shocks (e.g. chronic disability, sectoral layoffs, firm failure). Campbell & Viceira (2002) show that background income risk acts as an effective increase in risk aversion, driving households to accumulate precautionary buffer-stock liquid cash reserves and depressing risky equity participation.
  • Endogenous Labor Supply & Flexible Retirement: Bodie, Merton & Samuelson (1992) prove that if an individual can adjust labor hours or postpone retirement date in response to portfolio losses, the option to work acts as an embedded put option on financial wealth. Flexible labor supply substantially increases risk tolerance, rationalizing higher equity exposure than rigid-retirement models predict.

Misconceptions

  1. "Young investors should hold equities because they have decades to recover from market losses."

    This is the fallacious time diversification argument refuted by Samuelson. Under i.i.d. returns and financial-wealth-only utility, the optimal allocation is completely invariant to horizon. The true mathematical justification for high equity allocation in youth is human capital balance sheet hedging: young investors hold vast implicit bond positions in future earnings, so 100% financial equity represents a small, prudent fraction of total economic wealth.

  2. "Labor income always justifies an aggressive equity tilt in liquid portfolios."

    Labor income only acts as a bond substitute if its equity beta \(\beta_H\) is substantially below the target portfolio weight \(\alpha^*\). For professionals in financial services, private equity, technology startups, or cyclical commodities, \(\beta_H\) frequently exceeds \(\alpha^*\). For these individuals, holding high equity exposure in retirement accounts compounds their career vulnerability, violating basic risk management.

  3. "All individuals at age 35 should hold the identical target-date equity fraction."

    Commercial target-date fund glide paths are one-size-fits-all approximations. Two 35-year-olds with identical current financial wealth may have wildly different optimal allocations: a tenured government civil servant with guaranteed index-linked pension benefits (\(\beta_H \approx 0\), high \(H/W\)) should hold 100% equity, whereas a commission-based mortgage broker (\(\beta_H > 1\), volatile \(H/W\)) should hold conservative, bond-heavy financial portfolios.

Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

An investor with CRRA parameter \(\gamma = 5.0\) faces equity market excess return \(\mu - r = 0.06\) and volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\). At age 30, financial wealth is \(W = \$100,000\) and human capital is \(H = \$900,000\). Labor earnings are completely uncorrelated with equity markets (\(\beta_H = 0\)). Compute the optimal Merton equity share of total wealth \(\alpha^*\), the unconstrained financial equity share \(\alpha_t^F\), and the dollar equity holding under a leverage constraint \(\alpha_t^F \le 1.0\).

Solution

1. The Merton total wealth equity weight is \(\alpha^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2} = \frac{0.06}{5.0 \times 0.04} = \frac{0.06}{0.20} = 0.30\) (30%).

2. The ratio of human capital to financial wealth is \(H/W = 900,000 / 100,000 = 9\). With \(\beta_H = 0\), the unconstrained financial equity weight is \(\alpha^F = \alpha^* (1 + H/W) = 0.30 \times (1 + 9) = 3.00\) (300% equity exposure, requiring 200% financial leverage).

3. Under the constraint \(\alpha_t^F \le 1.0\), the financial equity allocation is clamped at \(\alpha_t^{F, \mathrm{constrained}} = 1.00\). The dollar equity holding is \(1.00 \times \$100,000 = \$100,000\). Total economic wealth is \(\$1,000,000\), so effective total equity exposure is \(\$100,000 / \$1,000,000 = 10\%\), significantly below the unconstrained target of 30%.

Consider a hedge fund analyst with \(\gamma = 3.0\), market parameters \(\mu - r = 0.06\) and \(\sigma = 0.20\). Their annual bonus and compensation give their human capital an equity beta of \(\beta_H = 0.80\). At age 28, \(W = \$150,000\) and \(H = \$1,350,000\). Calculate the target total wealth equity fraction \(\alpha^*\) and the optimal financial equity weight \(\alpha^F\).

Solution

1. The target total wealth equity fraction is \(\alpha^* = \frac{0.06}{3.0 \times 0.04} = \frac{0.06}{0.12} = 0.50\) (50%).

2. The wealth ratio is \(H/W = 1,350,000 / 150,000 = 9\). Applying Theorem 5.1: \(\alpha^F = \alpha^* + (\alpha^* - \beta_H) \frac{H}{W} = 0.50 + (0.50 - 0.80) \times 9 = 0.50 - 0.30 \times 9 = 0.50 - 2.70 = -2.20\).

3. Because \(\beta_H = 0.80 > \alpha^* = 0.50\), the analyst's human capital already contains vastly more equity exposure than their overall risk tolerance permits. Total equity exposure embedded in human capital is \(\beta_H H = 0.80 \times \$1,350,000 = \$1,080,000\), whereas their target equity exposure for total wealth \(W^{\mathrm{tot}} = \$1,500,000\) is only \(\alpha^* W^{\mathrm{tot}} = 0.50 \times \$1,500,000 = \$750,000\). To offset the excess $330,000 of equity risk in their job, the analyst would ideally short equities by $330,000 (a weight of \(-2.20\)). Under a no-short-sales constraint, they hold 0% equity.

A worker starts at age \(t = 25\) with starting annual wage \(Y_0 = \$60,000\), growing at constant annual rate \(g = 0.02\). The risk-free rate is \(r = 0.04\). Retirement occurs at \(T_R = 65\) (40 working years). Assuming wage payments are received continuously and are riskless, compute the exact valuation of human capital \(H_{25}\).

Solution

Under continuous discounting and wage growth, \(Y_s = Y_0 e^{g(s-t)}\). The present value is given by: \(H_t = \int_t^{T_R} e^{-r(s-t)} Y_0 e^{g(s-t)} ds = Y_0 \int_0^{T_R - t} e^{-(r-g)\tau} d\tau = Y_0 \frac{1 - e^{-(r-g)(T_R - t)}}{r - g}\). Here \(Y_0 = 60,000\), \(r - g = 0.04 - 0.02 = 0.02\), and \(T_R - t = 40\). Thus, \(H_{25} = 60,000 \times \frac{1 - e^{-0.02 \times 40}}{0.02} = 60,000 \times \frac{1 - e^{-0.80}}{0.02}\). Since \(e^{-0.80} \approx 0.44933\), the annuity factor is \(\frac{1 - 0.44933}{0.02} = \frac{0.55067}{0.02} = 27.5336\). Multiplying by $60,000 gives \(H_{25} = 60,000 \times 27.5336 \approx \$1,652,014\).

Proof Tier

Prove that the optimal financial equity share \(\alpha_t^F\) is strictly independent of the human capital to financial wealth ratio \(H_t/W_t\) if and only if the human capital equity beta equals the target Merton fraction: \(\beta_H = \alpha^*\).

Solution

From Theorem 5.1, the financial equity allocation is \(\alpha_t^F = \alpha^* + (\alpha^* - \beta_H) \frac{H_t}{W_t}\). Taking the partial derivative with respect to the wealth ratio \(\theta_t \equiv H_t / W_t\): \(\frac{\partial \alpha_t^F}{\partial \theta_t} = \alpha^* - \beta_H\). For \(\alpha_t^F\) to be strictly independent of \(\theta_t\), we require \(\frac{\partial \alpha_t^F}{\partial \theta_t} \equiv 0\). This holds if and only if \(\alpha^* - \beta_H = 0 \iff \beta_H = \alpha^*\). In that case, \(\alpha_t^F \equiv \alpha^*\) for all \(t \in [0, T_R]\), so the optimal financial portfolio is completely invariant to age and human capital depletion.

Let retirement occur at date \(T_R\). Prove that as \(t \to T_R\), regardless of wage process volatility, correlation \(\rho_{Y,S}\), or earnings level \(Y_t\), the financial equity allocation satisfies \(\lim_{t \to T_R} \alpha_t^F = \alpha^*\), provided financial wealth remains strictly positive (\(W_{T_R} > 0\)).

Solution

Human capital is defined as \(H_t = \E_t \left[ \int_t^{T_R} \frac{M_s}{M_t} Y_s ds \right]\). As \(t \to T_R\), the integration interval \([t, T_R]\) shrinks to zero length. Under mild regularity conditions on earnings (finite expectation), \(\lim_{t \to T_R} H_t = 0\). Given \(W_{T_R} > 0\), the ratio satisfies \(\lim_{t \to T_R} \frac{H_t}{W_t} = \frac{0}{W_{T_R}} = 0\). Substituting into the lifecycle rule: \(\lim_{t \to T_R} \alpha_t^F = \lim_{t \to T_R} \left[ \alpha^* + (\alpha^* - \beta_H) \frac{H_t}{W_t} \right] = \alpha^* + (\alpha^* - \beta_H) \times 0 = \alpha^*\). Thus, at retirement, the optimal financial portfolio collapses exactly to the standard Merton myopic weight.

Let financial wealth evolve according to \(dW_t = [W_t (r + \alpha_t^F (\mu - r)) + s Y_t] dt + \alpha_t^F W_t \sigma dW_{S,t}\), where \(s \in (0, 1)\) is the constant savings rate. Derive the expected financial wealth growth rate \(\frac{1}{dt} \E_t\left[\frac{dW_t}{W_t}\right]\) under the optimal unconstrained safe human capital policy \(\alpha_t^F = \alpha^*(1 + H_t/W_t)\).

Solution

Taking conditional expectations: \(\E_t[dW_t] = [W_t (r + \alpha_t^F (\mu - r)) + s Y_t] dt\). Dividing by \(W_t\): \(\frac{1}{dt} \E_t\left[\frac{dW_t}{W_t}\right] = r + \alpha_t^F (\mu - r) + s \frac{Y_t}{W_t}\). Substituting \(\alpha_t^F = \alpha^* \left(1 + \frac{H_t}{W_t}\right)\) and recalling \(\alpha^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\): \(\frac{1}{dt} \E_t\left[\frac{dW_t}{W_t}\right] = r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{\gamma \sigma^2} \left(1 + \frac{H_t}{W_t}\right) + s \frac{Y_t}{W_t}\). Early in life, financial wealth growth is heavily propelled by both the savings injection \(s Y_t / W_t\) and the leveraged equity risk premium generated by implicit human capital.

Empirical Tier

Empirical household survey data analyzed by Campbell (2006) demonstrates that over 40% of young US households hold zero public equities, directly contradicting the theoretical prediction of \(\alpha_t^F \ge 1.0\). Identify three institutional and behavioral mechanisms that explain this empirical participation puzzle.

Solution

The failure of young households to participate in equity markets is driven by three primary frictions: First, fixed entry and information costs: opening and managing a brokerage account requires cognitive effort and transaction fees that exceed the small dollar benefits on modest initial wealth. Second, uncollateralized debt commitments: young households frequently hold high-cost credit card or student debt with guaranteed interest rates of 15%–20%, making debt repayment mathematically superior to equity investment. Third, severe background labor risk and liquidity needs: young households require liquid cash buffer stocks to insure against job loss, housing moves, and medical emergencies without penalty.

Using US panel data, researchers estimate the wage beta \(\beta_H\) against the S&P 500 across sectors. Public school teachers exhibit \(\hat{\beta}_H \approx 0.05\), while Wall Street investment bankers exhibit \(\hat{\beta}_H \approx 0.75\). Contrast their predicted financial equity glide paths under a benchmark \(\alpha^* = 0.40\).

Solution

For the public school teacher, \(\beta_H = 0.05 < \alpha^* = 0.40\). The coefficient on human capital is \(\alpha^* - \beta_H = 0.35 > 0\). The teacher should hold a standard downward-sloping glide path: heavily equity-tilted (100% constrained) when young, declining smoothly toward 40% at retirement. For the investment banker, \(\beta_H = 0.75 > \alpha^* = 0.40\). The coefficient on human capital is \(\alpha^* - \beta_H = -0.35 < 0\). When young, the banker's large human capital already exposes them to massive equity-like market downturns. The banker's optimal financial equity weight begins near 0% (or bonds/cash) and glides upward toward 40% as retirement approaches.

Homeownership introduces a major illiquid asset financed by a long-term mortgage. How does a leveraged residential real estate purchase at age 32 alter the household's effective financial asset allocation glide path?

Solution

Purchasing a home introduces an illiquid asset with property-market risk and an enormous short bond position represented by mortgage debt. Fixed mortgage payment obligations act as a negative cash flow stream, effectively reducing the net bond-like quality of human capital. Furthermore, mortgage debt consumes all available borrowing capacity, leaving the household highly leveraged in illiquid real estate. As shown by Campbell (2006), newly mortgaged households drastically cut liquid equity exposure to build precautionary liquidity reserves, explaining the observed dip in equity share in early-30s cohorts.

Research Tier

Formulate the Bellman equation for a worker who chooses both consumption \(C_t\), financial equity weight \(\alpha_t^F\), and labor effort \(L_t \in [0, \bar{L}]\) with disutility of labor \(v(L_t) = \theta \frac{L_t^{1+\eta}}{1+\eta}\). Explain why the flexibility to adjust \(L_t\) acts as an embedded financial put option.

Solution

The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation is \(\rho J = \max_{C, \alpha^F, L} \left\{ \frac{C^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} - \theta \frac{L^{1+\eta}}{1+\eta} + J_W [W(r + \alpha^F(\mu - r)) + w L - C] + \frac{1}{2} J_{WW} (\alpha^F W \sigma)^2 \right\}\). When financial portfolio returns suffer a severe negative shock, financial wealth \(W\) plummets, causing marginal utility of wealth \(J_W\) to spike. From the first-order condition \(\theta L^\eta = w J_W\), the worker optimally increases labor effort \(L_t\) (or delays retirement). This ability to generate extra income exactly when market returns are disastrous creates a payoff structure equivalent to holding a protective put option on the investment portfolio, significantly raising effective risk-bearing capacity.

How does incorporating stochastic mortality hazard \(\lambda_t\) (Yaari 1965) into the stochastic discount factor alter the effective valuation of human capital and the optimal demand for life insurance versus annuities?

Solution

With hazard rate of death \(\lambda_t\), the survival probability to date \(s\) is \(S(s|t) = \exp(-\int_t^s \lambda_u du)\). Human capital conditional on survival is discounted at the augmented discount rate \(r + \lambda_t\): \(H_t = \int_t^{T_R} e^{-\int_t^s (r + \lambda_u) du} Y_s ds\). Because death extinguishes future labor earnings before retirement, an uninsured worker exposes surviving dependents to catastrophic loss, creating high demand for term life insurance equal to \(H_t\). Conversely, post-retirement, longevity risk (outliving assets) creates demand for life annuities that pay a mortality credit \(\lambda_t\), converting volatile financial wealth into guaranteed lifetime income.

Synthesize the results of Lesson 3 (predictability hedging demand) and Lesson 5 (human capital). Write down the combined formula for optimal financial equity share \(\alpha_t^F\) when dividend yields predict equity returns and the investor possesses safe human capital \(H_t\).

Solution

Let total wealth be \(W_t^{\mathrm{tot}} = W_t + H_t\). As established in Lesson 3, return predictability generates an optimal total wealth equity exposure composed of myopic demand plus intertemporal hedging demand: \(\alpha_t^{*, \mathrm{tot}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}}^* + \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}}^*\). When human capital is safe (\(\beta_H = 0\)), total dollar equity demand is \(\alpha_t^{*, \mathrm{tot}} (W_t + H_t)\). Dividing by financial wealth \(W_t\) gives: \(\alpha_t^F = (\alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}}^* + \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}}^*) \left(1 + \frac{H_t}{W_t}\right)\). Thus, the presence of safe human capital amplifies both the myopic allocation and the intertemporal hedging demand by the factor \((1 + H_t/W_t)\). Long-horizon young investors hold massive equity positions that exploit both mean reversion and labor-income bond buffering.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. Why does a young investor with riskless labor income optimally allocate a larger share of liquid financial wealth to equities than an older retiree?

    Options

    Under the Bodie-Merton-Samuelson theorem, safe labor income is an implicit risk-free asset. With \(H/W \gg 1\), holding 100% of liquid assets in equities amounts to a small, conservative fraction of total balance sheet wealth \(W^{\mathrm{tot}} = W + H\). Option 2 invokes the fallacious time diversification myth refuted by Samuelson.

  2. In the Bodie-Merton-Samuelson lifecycle rule \(\alpha_t^F = \alpha^* + (\alpha^* - \beta_H) \frac{H_t}{W_t}\), what happens if the worker's labor beta satisfies \(\beta_H = \alpha^*\)?

    Options

    When \(\beta_H = \alpha^*\), the term \((\alpha^* - \beta_H) = 0\), causing \(\alpha_t^F \equiv \alpha^*\) regardless of the human capital to financial wealth ratio \(H_t/W_t\). The financial portfolio is completely invariant to age.

  3. Why cannot an investor directly hedge their human capital by selling a forward contract on their future personal earnings?

    Options

    Human capital is non-pledgeable and non-tradable due to severe moral hazard (an individual who sells claims to future wages has no incentive to work) and legal prohibitions against indentured servitude.

  4. How does the presence of a binding borrowing constraint (\(\alpha_t^F \le 1.0\)) alter the theoretical lifecycle equity glide path for a young professional with safe earnings?

    Options

    The unconstrained rule calls for \(\alpha_t^F > 2.0\) (leverage). The borrowing constraint clamps the allocation to \(\min(1.0, \alpha_t^F) = 1.0\), creating a 100% plateau across early decades until \(H_t/W_t\) drops sufficiently.

  5. If an entrepreneur's labor income is strongly procyclical (\(\beta_H = 1.20\)) and their target Merton weight is \(\alpha^* = 0.40\), what is the optimal direction of their financial equity glide path as they approach retirement?

    Options

    Because \(\beta_H = 1.20 > \alpha^* = 0.40\), \((\alpha^* - \beta_H) = -0.80 < 0\). Early in life, the entrepreneur's vast human capital already overexposes them to equity risk, requiring a depressed financial equity weight (clamped at 0%). As retirement approaches and \(H_t \to 0\), the weight rises upward toward \(\alpha^* = 0.40\).

  6. In Lab 1, what is the exact theoretical Merton target equity fraction \(\alpha^*\) on total wealth for equity premium 0.05, volatility 0.18, and CRRA parameter \(\gamma = 4.0\)?

    \(\alpha^* = \frac{0.05}{4.0 \times 0.18^2} = \frac{0.05}{0.1296} \approx 0.3858\) (38.58%).

  7. At the exact moment of retirement date \(T_R\), what is the dollar value of remaining human capital \(H_{T_R}\)?

    At retirement, future career working years are zero, so \(H_{T_R} = 0.0000\).

  8. Which empirical household finance stylized fact documented by Campbell (2006) poses the greatest challenge to frictionless lifecycle allocation models?

    Options

    The equity non-participation puzzle: despite theoretical models mandating \(\ge 100\%\) equity exposure for young workers, substantial proportions of young households hold no equities at all due to liquidity buffers, information costs, and debt service.
