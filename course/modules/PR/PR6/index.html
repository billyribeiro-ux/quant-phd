Dynamic & Long-Horizon Allocation

Continuous-time stochastic control with state-dependent investment opportunities, predictive return dynamics, multi-period mean reversion, liability immunization, and optimal human-capital lifecycle paths: Merton (1971, 1973) intertemporal hedging demands, the Samuelson (1969, 1979) time diversification fallacy, Campbell & Viceira (2002) strategic allocation, and target-date glide path engineering.

  • Prerequisites: SC6 Stochastic Control, PR1 Mean-Variance in Practice
  • Concepts introduced: time-diversification, intertemporal-hedging-demand, horizon-effect, lifecycle-allocation, target-date-glide-path
  • Project: Lifecycle allocation model: Dynamic control solver (lifecycle.py) and institutional target-date fund evaluation report (report.md)

48 h lessons · 32 h project (40.0%)

Objectives

By the end of PR6 you can:

  1. Dissect the time diversification fallacy, proving that under i.i.d. returns and CRRA utility the optimal equity fraction is horizon-invariant while cumulative terminal wealth variance explodes exponentially.
  2. Derive Merton's continuous-time HJB dynamic programming equation and decompose optimal portfolio demand into static myopic mean-variance demand and intertemporal hedging demand against state variable risk.
  3. Quantify the investment horizon effect under return predictability, demonstrating how negative innovation correlation (\(\rho < 0\)) creates multi-period mean reversion and positive equity hedging demand.
  4. Formulate the term structure of risk for fixed-income assets, proving that cash is risky for long-horizon investors and designing liability-driven investing (LDI) duration-immunized portfolios.
  5. Model non-tradable human capital as an implicit asset, deriving the Bodie-Merton-Samuelson lifecycle financial allocation and evaluating the impact of wage cyclicality (\(\beta_H\)).
  6. Engineer institutional target-date fund glide paths, balancing human capital depletion against sequence-of-returns risk and calculating CER welfare losses relative to dynamic stochastic policies.

Prerequisites & Mathematical Foundations

PR6 builds directly on two prerequisite foundational pillars:

  • SC6 Stochastic Control: Continuous-time dynamic programming, the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, the continuous Merton fraction \(\frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\), and stochastic verification theorems.
  • PR1 Mean-Variance in Practice: Static portfolio optimization, optimizer error maximization, multi-asset covariance shrinkage, and the 1/N benchmark.
  • Foundational Decision Theory (E3): Expected utility under CRRA utility \(u(W) = \frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\) and the certainty equivalent return.

Placement diagnostic

Eight diagnostic questions covering prerequisite foundations and two implementation skill checks from project rungs 1 and 2. Mastery bar is 80% (at least 7 of 8 correct, including both skill checks).

  1. Skill check (rung 1): A 25-year-old worker earns a constant annual salary of \(\$50,000\) for 10 years until age 35. At a risk-free discount rate of \(r = 0.05\), what is the exact backward present value of this labour income stream \(H_{25} = \sum_{t=0}^{9} \frac{50000}{(1+0.05)^t}\)?

    \(H_{25} = 50000 \times \frac{1 - (1.05)^{-10}}{0.05} \times 1.05 = 50000 \times 7.721735 = 405,391\) if paid at start of year, or ordinary annuity due \(50000 \times (1 + \frac{1 - 1.05^{-9}}{0.05}) = 385,762.5\).

  2. Skill check (rung 2): An investor with CRRA risk aversion \(\gamma = 4.0\), equity premium \(\mu - r = 0.05\), and volatility \(\sigma = 0.18\) has an unconstrained Merton total-wealth target of \(\alpha^* = \frac{0.05}{4.0 \times 0.18^2} \approx 0.3858\). If financial wealth is \(W = \$450,000\) and safe human capital is \(H = \$225,907.3\), what is the optimal financial equity weight \(\alpha^F = \alpha^* (1 + H/W)\)?

    \(\frac{H}{W} = \frac{225907.3}{450000} \approx 0.5020\). Then \(\alpha^F = 0.385802 \times (1 + 0.502016) = 0.385802 \times 1.502016 \approx 0.5795\) (57.95%).

  3. From SC6: In continuous time with log return drift \(\mu = 0.08\), risk-free rate \(r = 0.02\), volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), and CRRA risk aversion \(\gamma = 2.0\), what is the Merton fraction \(\alpha^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\)?

    \(\alpha^* = \frac{0.08 - 0.02}{2.0 \times 0.20^2} = \frac{0.06}{0.08} = 0.7500\).

  4. From PR1: In a two-asset mean-variance universe without risk-free lending, what is the weight of asset 1 under the \(1/N\) benchmark with \(N=2\) or an allocation benchmark with equal split?

    Under \(1/N\) with \(N=2\) each weight is 0.50; for a 4-to-1 equity/bond allocation the ratio is 0.8000.

  5. From SC6: What condition guarantees that the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman dynamic programming PDE admits a unique classical solution that verifies the value function?

    Options

    The stochastic verification theorem guarantees that any smooth solution to the HJB PDE satisfying boundary and transversality conditions coincides with the true value function.

  6. From E3: What is the defining mathematical property of Constant Relative Risk Aversion (CRRA) power utility \(u(W) = \frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\)?

    Options

    CRRA utility exhibits scale invariance: doubling an investor's wealth leaves their optimal portfolio risky fractions unchanged.

  7. From SC2: If log-stock price follows arithmetic Brownian motion \(dx_t = \mu dt + \sigma dW_t\), what is the variance of the cumulative log return \(x_T - x_0\) over horizon \(T\)?

    Options

    The variance of cumulative Brownian motion increments scales linearly with elapsed time: \(\Var(x_T - x_0) = \sigma^2 T\).

  8. From PR1: Why does raw sample mean-variance optimization fail in institutional asset allocation?

    Options

    Michaud (1989) identified error maximization: unconstrained mean-variance treats noisy sample estimates as true parameters, concentrating capital in estimation error outliers.

Lesson map

Six doctoral lessons spanning 48 study hours (8 hours each):

  1. L01 · The Static Baseline & the Fallacy of Time Diversification

    Samuelson's (1969, 1979) critique of time diversification. Multi-period i.i.d. returns, CRRA scale invariance, annualized variance decay versus exponential terminal wealth dispersion, and the shortfall paradox.

    8 study hours · Introduces time-diversification

  2. L02 · Continuous-Time Dynamic Portfolio Choice & Merton Hedging Demand

    Merton (1971, 1973) continuous-time dynamic programming with stochastic state variables. Formal HJB derivation, decomposition into myopic mean-variance and intertemporal hedging demands, and zero hedging under log utility.

    8 study hours · Introduces intertemporal-hedging-demand

  3. L03 · Return Predictability, Mean Reversion & the Horizon Effect

    Predictive regressions and VAR(1) asset dynamics. Negative innovation correlation, multi-period variance ratio decay, and the Campbell & Viceira (2002) analytical long-horizon strategic asset allocation.

    8 study hours · Introduces horizon-effect

  4. L04 · Term Structure of Risk & Liability-Driven Investing (LDI)

    Stochastic interest rates and the reinvestment risk of rolling cash. Why zero-coupon bonds are the true riskless asset for long horizons, surplus optimization, and duration immunization for defined-benefit liabilities.

    8 study hours · Risk term structure

  5. L05 · Human Capital, Labour Income & Lifecycle Asset Allocation

    Decomposing total wealth into financial assets and non-tradable human capital. The Bodie-Merton-Samuelson lifecycle rule, age-dependent equity glide paths, borrowing constraints, and labour income cyclicality (\(\beta_H\)).

    8 study hours · Introduces lifecycle-allocation

  6. L06 · Target-Date Funds & Optimal Glide Path Design

    Target-date funds (TDFs) in defined contribution pensions. 'To' versus 'Through' retirement architectures, sequence-of-returns risk in the retirement red zone, and CER welfare comparison against dynamic optimal policies.

    8 study hours · Introduces target-date-glide-path

    Incorporate uninsurable idiosyncratic labour shocks, endogenous retirement timing, or post-retirement longevity pooling.
