Target-Date Funds & Optimal Glide Path Design

Target-date funds (TDFs) have become the default retirement investment vehicle for millions of workers worldwide, managing trillions in assets. At their core is a pre-programmed target-date glide path that automates portfolio de-risking as retirement approaches. However, standard commercial glide paths embody critical structural trade-offs between sequence-of-returns drawdown risk in the vulnerable retirement red zone and terminal longevity risk during decumulation. By evaluating deterministic heuristics against stochastic dynamic control benchmarks, quantitative asset managers can quantify the welfare losses of rigid glide paths and engineer state-contingent, personalized retirement solutions.

1. The Architecture of Target-Date Glide Paths

Following the US Pension Protection Act of 2006, target-date funds became the dominant Qualified Default Investment Alternative (QDIA) in defined-contribution retirement plans. As analyzed by Campbell (2006) and Campbell & Viceira (2002), TDFs seek to operationalize the lifecycle allocation principles derived from human capital depletion into a single turnkey mutual fund.

Definition 6.1 (Target-Date Glide Path)

Let \(T_R\) denote the investor's designated target retirement date, and \(\tau \equiv T_R - t\) denote the remaining career horizon. A target-date glide path is a deterministic vector-valued function \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t; T_R) \in \Delta^N\) mapping time or remaining horizon to asset class portfolio weights:

\[ \boldsymbol{\alpha}(t; T_R) = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_{\mathrm{equity}}(t) \\ \alpha_{\mathrm{bond}}(t) \\ \alpha_{\mathrm{cash}}(t) \\ \alpha_{\mathrm{inflation}}(t) \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i(t) = 1, \quad \alpha_i(t) \ge 0. \]

The scalar equity trajectory \(\alpha_E(t)\) typically satisfies monotonicity during career accumulation:

\[ \frac{\partial \alpha_E(t)}{\partial t} \le 0 \quad \text{for all } t \in [t_{\mathrm{start}}, T_R]. \]

In industry practice, glide paths are parameterized either as piecewise-linear splines, sigmoid curves, or exponential decay functions. For example, a canonical linear glide path with equity plateau \(\alpha_{\max}\), landing point \(\alpha_{\min}\), and transition start age \(A_{\mathrm{trans}}\) satisfies:

\[ \alpha_E(\text{age}) = \begin{cases} \alpha_{\max}, & \text{age} \le A_{\mathrm{trans}}, \\ \alpha_{\max} - (\alpha_{\max} - \alpha_{\min}) \frac{\text{age} - A_{\mathrm{trans}}}{A_{\mathrm{retire}} - A_{\mathrm{trans}}}, & A_{\mathrm{trans}} < \text{age} \le A_{\mathrm{retire}}, \\ \alpha_{\min}, & \text{age} > A_{\mathrm{retire}}. \end{cases} \]

2. "To Retirement" versus "Through Retirement" Philosophies

The institutional fund management industry is divided into two competing schools of glide path construction, distinguished by where the equity landing point is reached:

Definition 6.2 ("To" vs "Through" Glide Paths)
  1. "To-Retirement" Glide Path: De-risking ceases precisely at the retirement date \(T_R\). The equity allocation reaches its lifetime minimum \(\alpha_{\min}\) (typically 25%–35%) at age 65 and remains constant throughout retirement: \[ \alpha_E(t) = \alpha_{\min} \quad \text{for all } t \ge T_R. \]
  2. "Through-Retirement" Glide Path: De-risking continues for 15–20 years into the decumulation phase. The fund arrives at retirement date \(T_R\) with substantial growth exposure (typically 50% equity) and glides down to its terminal landing point \(\alpha_{\min}\) (typically 25%–30%) around age 80–85: \[ \alpha_E(T_R) > \alpha_{\min}, \quad \frac{\partial \alpha_E(t)}{\partial t} < 0 \quad \text{for } t \in [T_R, T_R + 15]. \]

The trade-off between these designs centers on the tension between two fundamental risks:

  • Sequence-of-Returns Risk: "To-Retirement" managers prioritize protecting the accumulated wealth nest egg right at retirement, when portfolio size is maximized and career earnings have ceased.
  • Longevity and Inflation Risk: "Through-Retirement" managers argue that a 65-year-old retiree has an expected remaining lifespan of 20–30 years. Locking in a 70% fixed-income portfolio at age 65 exposes retirees to catastrophic purchasing-power erosion and premature wealth exhaustion under modest inflation.

3. Sequence-of-Returns Risk & The Retirement "Red Zone"

The 5 to 10 years immediately preceding and following retirement are universally recognized in quantitative financial planning as the Retirement Red Zone. In this window, the mathematical symmetry of multi-period compounding breaks down due to net cash outflows.

Theorem 6.1 (Sequence-of-Returns Asymmetry Under Decumulation)

Consider an investor withdrawing a fixed real amount \(C > 0\) at the beginning of each period \(t = 0, \dots, K-1\). Wealth evolves according to:

\[ W_{t+1} = (W_t - C)(1 + R_{t+1}). \]

By recursive expansion, terminal wealth after \(K\) periods is:

\[ W_K = W_0 \prod_{j=1}^K (1 + R_j) - C \sum_{j=1}^K \prod_{m=j}^K (1 + R_m). \]

Unlike the accumulation phase without cash flows (where terminal wealth depends solely on the cumulative product \(\prod (1 + R_j)\), independent of permutation order), the second term depends strictly on the ordering of returns. Negative returns early in the sequence severely reduce subsequent terminal wealth and accelerate portfolio ruin.

Proof of Theorem 6.1

Let \(K = 2\). Consider two sequences with identical geometric and arithmetic returns: Sequence A has \(R_1 = -0.30\) followed by \(R_2 = +0.30\); Sequence B has \(R_1 = +0.30\) followed by \(R_2 = -0.30\). In the absence of withdrawals (\(C = 0\)), terminal wealth is identical: \(W_2^A = W_2^B = W_0 (1 - 0.30)(1 + 0.30) = 0.91 W_0\).

Now introduce annual withdrawals \(C > 0\). For Sequence A:

\[ W_2^A = [(W_0 - C)(0.70) - C](1.30) = 0.91 W_0 - 0.91 C - 1.30 C = 0.91 W_0 - 2.21 C. \]

For Sequence B:

\[ W_2^B = [(W_0 - C)(1.30) - C](0.70) = 0.91 W_0 - 0.91 C - 0.70 C = 0.91 W_0 - 1.61 C. \]

Comparing the terminal outcomes:

\[ W_2^B - W_2^A = (-1.61 C) - (-2.21 C) = +0.60 C > 0. \]

The sequence with early losses suffers a permanent deficit of \(0.60 C\) because the withdrawal \(C\) is extracted from a depressed asset base, permanently locking in capital destruction. This completes the proof.

During the accumulation phase, a market crash early in life is benign because contributions purchase equities at depressed valuations. In contrast, a 40% equity market crash when financial wealth is at its career peak (e.g. at age 64) followed immediately by retirement withdrawals can cause complete portfolio depletion within 10 years, even if equities subsequently embark on a multi-year bull market.

4. Computational Lab: Comparative Glide Path Risk Evaluation

In lab-PR6-L06-1.py, we conduct a Monte Carlo evaluation of 20,000 career accumulation paths from age 25 to 65 with an annual contribution of $7,500, comparing three representative institutional strategies:

  1. "To-Retirement" Glide Path: 90% equity until age 40, linearly de-risking to 30% equity at age 65.
  2. "Through-Retirement" Glide Path: 90% equity until age 40, linearly de-risking to 50% equity at age 65.
  3. Static 60/40 Portfolio: Constant 60% equity and 40% bond allocation throughout all 40 years.

Lab 1 · Target-Date Glide Path Evaluation & Drawdown Risk

Simulating 20,000 career paths over 40 years ($7,500/yr contribution, gamma=4.0) will demonstrate that "To-Retirement" glide paths minimize late sequence-of-returns drawdown risk (95% 5-year drawdown of ~22.6%) compared to static 60/40 (~30.9%), while "Through-Retirement" delivers higher median and certainty equivalent wealth by maintaining growth exposure.

"""Lab PR6-L06-1: Target-Date Fund Glide Path Design & Sequence-of-Returns Risk.

SIMULATED: Monte Carlo evaluation of "To", "Through", and Static 60/40 target-date
glide paths under sequence-of-returns drawdown risk and CRRA certainty equivalents.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def evaluate_glide_paths(
    start_age: int = 25,
    retire_age: int = 65,
    initial_wealth: float = 25000.0,
    annual_contribution: float = 7500.0,
    equity_mean: float = 0.08,
    equity_vol: float = 0.17,
    bond_mean: float = 0.03,
    bond_vol: float = 0.05,
    corr: float = -0.10,
    gamma: float = 4.0,
    n_paths: int = 10000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, dict[str, float]]:
    """Simulate asset returns and compare TDF glide paths over career accumulation."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    years = retire_age - start_age
    ages = np.arange(start_age, retire_age)

    # Correlated asset returns
    cov = corr * equity_vol * bond_vol
    cov_matrix = np.array([[equity_vol**2, cov], [cov, bond_vol**2]])
    chol = np.linalg.cholesky(cov_matrix)

    # 1. "To Retirement" Glide Path: 90% until 40, linearly down to 30% at 65
    alpha_to = np.zeros(years)
    for i, age in enumerate(ages):
        if age <= 40:
            alpha_to[i] = 0.90
        else:
            alpha_to[i] = 0.90 - (0.90 - 0.30) * ((age - 40) / (65 - 40))

    # 2. "Through Retirement" Glide Path: 90% until 40, 50% at 65, down to 30% at 80
    alpha_through = np.zeros(years)
    for i, age in enumerate(ages):
        if age <= 40:
            alpha_through[i] = 0.90
        else:
            alpha_through[i] = 0.90 - (0.90 - 0.50) * ((age - 40) / (65 - 40))

    # 3. Static 60/40 Portfolio
    alpha_static = np.full(years, 0.60)

    strategies = {
        "To-Retirement (90%->30%)": alpha_to,
        "Through-Retirement (90%->50%)": alpha_through,
        "Static 60/40": alpha_static,
    }

    # Generate annual market shocks across n_paths and years
    z = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, years, 2))
    shocks = np.einsum("ij,nyj->nyi", chol, z)
    ret_eq = equity_mean + shocks[:, :, 0]
    ret_bond = bond_mean + shocks[:, :, 1]

    metrics: dict[str, dict[str, float]] = {}

    for name, alpha_path in strategies.items():
        wealth = np.zeros((n_paths, years + 1))
        wealth[:, 0] = initial_wealth

        for t in range(years):
            r_p = alpha_path[t] * ret_eq[:, t] + (1.0 - alpha_path[t]) * ret_bond[:, t]
            wealth[:, t + 1] = wealth[:, t] * (1.0 + r_p) + annual_contribution

        terminal_w = wealth[:, -1]
        mean_w = float(np.mean(terminal_w))
        p5_w = float(np.percentile(terminal_w, 5))
        p50_w = float(np.median(terminal_w))

        # Certainty Equivalent Wealth: U = E[W^(1-gamma) / (1-gamma)] -> CE = ((1-gamma)*U)^(1/(1-gamma))
        # Protect against non-positive wealth
        safe_w = np.maximum(terminal_w, 1.0)
        expected_u = float(np.mean((safe_w ** (1.0 - gamma)) / (1.0 - gamma)))
        ce_wealth = float(((1.0 - gamma) * expected_u) ** (1.0 / (1.0 - gamma)))

        # Sequence of returns risk: Maximum drawdown in final 5 pre-retirement years (ages 60-65)
        # indices: years - 5 to years
        last_wealth = wealth[:, (years - 5) :]
        peaks = np.maximum.accumulate(last_wealth, axis=1)
        dd = (peaks - last_wealth) / peaks
        max_dd_late = np.max(dd, axis=1)
        p95_late_dd = float(np.percentile(max_dd_late, 95))

        metrics[name] = {
            "mean_wealth": mean_w,
            "median_wealth": p50_w,
            "p5_wealth": p5_w,
            "ce_wealth": ce_wealth,
            "p95_late_drawdown": p95_late_dd,
        }

    return metrics


def main() -> None:
    res = evaluate_glide_paths(
        start_age=25,
        retire_age=65,
        initial_wealth=25000.0,
        annual_contribution=7500.0,
        equity_mean=0.08,
        equity_vol=0.17,
        bond_mean=0.03,
        bond_vol=0.05,
        corr=-0.10,
        gamma=4.0,
        n_paths=20000,
        seed=42,
    )

    print("Target-Date Fund Glide Path Design & Risk Evaluation:")
    print("Accumulation: Age 25 to 65 (40 years), $7,500/yr contribution, CRRA gamma=4.0")
    print("=" * 92)
    print(
        f"{'Glide Path Strategy':<30} {'Mean W_T ($)':<14} {'Median W_T ($)':<16} "
        f"{'5th Pct ($)':<13} {'CE Wealth ($)':<15} {'95% Late DD':<12}"
    )
    print("-" * 92)

    for name, m in res.items():
        print(
            f"{name:<30} {m['mean_wealth']:<14.1f} {m['median_wealth']:<16.1f} "
            f"{m['p5_wealth']:<13.1f} {m['ce_wealth']:<15.1f} {m['p95_late_drawdown']:<12.4f}"
        )

    print("=" * 92)
    print("Key Tradeoffs:")
    print("1. 'To-Retirement' delivers lowest late sequence-of-returns drawdown risk (protecting near retirement).")
    print("2. 'Through-Retirement' captures higher median and certainty equivalent wealth by staying invested.")
    print("3. Static 60/40 exhibits excess drawdown risk late in career with lower early-stage equity growth.")

5. Welfare Loss Relative to Dynamic Stochastic Control

While commercial TDFs offer operational simplicity, deterministic glide paths are mathematically sub-optimal relative to the full stochastic dynamic control policies derived in Lessons 2, 3, and 5. Following Campbell & Viceira (2002), we formalize the welfare loss of heuristic glide paths.

Definition 6.3 (Certainty Equivalent Return Welfare Loss)

Let \(J^{\mathrm{opt}}(W_0, \mathbf{x}_0)\) denote the expected indirect utility achieved under the dynamic optimal feedback policy \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t^*(\mathbf{x}_t, W_t, H_t)\), and let \(J^{\mathrm{glide}}(W_0, \mathbf{x}_0)\) denote the indirect utility under a deterministic heuristic glide path \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t)\). The relative Certainty Equivalent (CE) welfare loss \(\mathcal{L}\) is defined by:

\[ U\left( (1 - \mathcal{L}) \mathrm{CE}^{\mathrm{opt}} \right) = J^{\mathrm{glide}} \implies \mathcal{L} \equiv 1 - \frac{\mathrm{CE}^{\mathrm{glide}}}{\mathrm{CE}^{\mathrm{opt}}}. \]

Empirical dynamic asset allocation literature reveals three structural reasons why heuristic glide paths suffer welfare losses of 1.5% to 4.0% in annual certainty equivalent consumption:

  • Failure to Exploit Return Predictability: A deterministic glide path de-risks on a fixed schedule regardless of valuation state variables (\(D/P\) or Shiller CAPE). If equity valuations crash and forward risk premia surge when an investor turns 60, a dynamic investor exploits the intertemporal hedging demand and horizon effect by increasing equity exposure, whereas a TDF mechanically sells equities at market bottoms.
  • Wealth-Path Insensitivity: A fixed glide path allocates identical weights to a 60-year-old who experienced stellar market returns and holds $3 million (well past their retirement funding target) and a 60-year-old who suffered poor returns and holds $200,000. Optimal stochastic control dictates that the funded status dictates risk appetite.
  • Neglect of Idiosyncratic Human Capital Risk: TDFs treat all participants of the same birth year identically, ignoring wage cyclicality \(\beta_H\) and defined-benefit pension claims.

Where the Canon Breaks

The institutional reality of commercial target-date funds deviates from academic finance along critical operational dimensions:

  • One-Size-Fits-All QDIA Homogeneity: Default 401(k) auto-enrollment places millions of workers into identical glide paths based purely on their birth year. A nurse with a guaranteed public pension and a tech worker with high equity compensation receive the exact same allocation, generating severe welfare distortions.
  • Fee Drag and Underlying Manager Stacking: Many commercial TDFs operate as "funds of funds," charging wrapper fees on top of the underlying active fund fees. Over a 40-year accumulation horizon, a 50-basis-point annual fee differential consumes over 18% of final terminal wealth.
  • Static Asset Class Categorization: Commercial glide paths typically divide portfolios into broad equities and aggregate bonds, ignoring the duration matching principles of Lesson 4. The fixed-income sleeve is rarely customized to the participant's liability duration.

Misconceptions

  1. "Target-date funds guarantee a safe, risk-free retirement income at the target date."

    Target-date funds do not guarantee principal, capital preservation, or retirement income. In 2008, 2010-vintage target-date funds (designed for individuals retiring in two years) lost an average of 25%–35% of their value due to excessive equity allocations (up to 65% equity) right at the retirement threshold.

  2. "A 'To-Retirement' glide path is strictly superior to a 'Through-Retirement' glide path because it avoids market drawdowns."

    While a 'To' glide path reduces mark-to-market drawdowns at age 65, it introduces severe longevity risk. By locking in a 70% fixed-income portfolio for 25–30 years of decumulation, retirees face heightened probabilities of running out of money before death due to real purchasing power erosion.

  3. "Glide paths are designed to capture time diversification over a long career."

    As proven in Lesson 1, time diversification is a mathematical fallacy under i.i.d. returns. The true justification for the downward glide path is human capital depletion (Lesson 5): young workers hold safe implicit bond assets that shrink as they age, requiring financial portfolios to de-risk.

Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

A linear glide path starts with \(\alpha_E = 0.90\) at age 35 and glides linearly to \(\alpha_E = 0.30\) at age 65. Compute the exact equity allocation at ages 40, 50, and 60.

Solution

The total horizon span is \(65 - 35 = 30\) years, and the total reduction in equity weight is \(0.90 - 0.30 = 0.60\). The annual de-risking rate is \(\frac{0.60}{30} = 0.020\) (2.0% per year).

  • Age 40: \(\alpha_E(40) = 0.90 - 0.020 \times (40 - 35) = 0.90 - 0.10 = 0.80\) (80%).
  • Age 50: \(\alpha_E(50) = 0.90 - 0.020 \times (50 - 35) = 0.90 - 0.30 = 0.60\) (60%).
  • Age 60: \(\alpha_E(60) = 0.90 - 0.020 \times (60 - 35) = 0.90 - 0.50 = 0.40\) (40%).

An investor enters retirement at age 65 with \(W_0 = \$1,000,000\) and plans to withdraw \(C = \$50,000\) at the start of each year for 2 years. Compare terminal wealth \(W_2\) under Sequence A (\(R_1 = -0.20, R_2 = +0.20\)) versus Sequence B (\(R_1 = +0.20, R_2 = -0.20\)).

Solution

Under Sequence A: Year 1 start wealth after withdrawal is \(W_0 - C = 1,000,000 - 50,000 = \$950,000\). After \(R_1 = -0.20\), wealth is \(W_1 = 950,000 \times 0.80 = \$760,000\). Year 2 start wealth after withdrawal is \(760,000 - 50,000 = \$710,000\). After \(R_2 = +0.20\), terminal wealth is \(W_2^A = 710,000 \times 1.20 = \$852,000\).

Under Sequence B: Year 1 start wealth after withdrawal is \(\$950,000\). After \(R_1 = +0.20\), wealth is \(W_1 = 950,000 \times 1.20 = \$1,140,000\). Year 2 start wealth after withdrawal is \(1,140,000 - 50,000 = \$1,090,000\). After \(R_2 = -0.20\), terminal wealth is \(W_2^B = 1,090,000 \times 0.80 = \$872,000\).

The sequence difference is \(W_2^B - W_2^A = \$872,000 - \$852,000 = \$20,000\), illustrating sequence-of-returns penalty from early losses.

A dynamic portfolio generates an expected utility of \(J^{\mathrm{opt}} = -0.00010\) under CRRA utility with \(\gamma = 3.0\), while a target-date fund generates \(J^{\mathrm{glide}} = -0.00012\). Compute the Certainty Equivalent Wealth for each and find the relative percentage welfare loss \(\mathcal{L}\).

Solution

For CRRA utility \(U(W) = \frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\), the Certainty Equivalent Wealth is \(\mathrm{CE} = [(1 - \gamma) J]^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}\). With \(\gamma = 3\), \(1 - \gamma = -2\), so \(\mathrm{CE} = [-2 J]^{-1/2}\).

  • \(\mathrm{CE}^{\mathrm{opt}} = [-2 (-0.00010)]^{-0.5} = [0.00020]^{-0.5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.00020}} \approx 70.7107\).
  • \(\mathrm{CE}^{\mathrm{glide}} = [-2 (-0.00012)]^{-0.5} = [0.00024]^{-0.5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.00024}} \approx 64.5497\).

The relative welfare loss is \(\mathcal{L} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{CE}^{\mathrm{glide}}}{\mathrm{CE}^{\mathrm{opt}}} = 1 - \frac{64.5497}{70.7107} = 1 - 0.9129 = 0.0871\) (8.71% welfare loss in certainty equivalent wealth).

Proof Tier

Prove that during pure accumulation with zero net deposits or withdrawals (\(W_{t+1} = W_t (1 + R_{t+1})\)), terminal wealth \(W_K\) is strictly invariant to any permutation of the return sequence \(\{R_1, \dots, R_K\}\).

Solution

Under zero interim cash flows, recursive substitution yields \(W_K = W_0 \prod_{t=1}^K (1 + R_t)\). Multiplication in the real field \(\R\) is associative and commutative. For any permutation \(\pi \in \mathcal{S}_K\) of the index set \(\{1, \dots, K\}\), \(\prod_{t=1}^K (1 + R_{\pi(t)}) = \prod_{t=1}^K (1 + R_t)\). Hence \(W_K(\pi) = W_K\). Terminal wealth is independent of sequence ordering if and only if there are no intermediate cash additions or subtractions.

Let \(W_t\) follow the decumulation equation \(W_{t+1} = (W_t - C)(1 + R_{t+1})\). Prove that the marginal sensitivity of terminal wealth \(W_K\) to a return shock \(R_m\) at step \(m \in \{1, \dots, K\}\) is strictly decreasing in \(m\) when expected returns are positive.

Solution

Expanding terminal wealth: \(W_K = W_0 \prod_{j=1}^K (1 + R_j) - C \sum_{j=1}^K \prod_{k=j}^K (1 + R_k)\). Differentiating with respect to \((1 + R_m)\):

\[ \frac{\partial W_K}{\partial (1 + R_m)} = W_0 \prod_{j \ne m} (1 + R_j) - C \sum_{j=1}^m \prod_{k=j, k \ne m}^K (1 + R_k) = \frac{W_m - C}{1 + R_m} \prod_{k=m}^K (1 + R_k). \]

Under the standard condition that initial wealth dominates annualized withdrawals (\(W_m \gg C\)) and gross returns exceed 1 on average, earlier shocks \(R_m\) are compounded by a larger number of remaining compounding periods \(\prod_{k=m+1}^K (1 + R_k)\). Consequently, an early negative shock severely compounds across all subsequent periods, proving that sequence sensitivity is highest at the start of decumulation.

Prove that under CRRA preferences, a state-contingent dynamic strategy that rebalances based on Shiller CAPE strictly dominates a deterministic age-based glide path in expected utility, provided CAPE possesses non-zero predictive power for excess returns.

Solution

Let \(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{det}}\) be the set of deterministic policies \(\alpha(t)\) measurable with respect to time alone (\(\sigma(t)\)), and let \(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\) be the set of feedback policies \(\alpha(t, x_t)\) measurable with respect to the state filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(x_s, W_s : s \le t)\). Deterministic glide paths form a proper subset: \(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{det}} \subsetneq \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\).

The stochastic control optimum is \(J^{\mathrm{opt}} = \sup_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{dyn}}} \E[U(W_T)]\), while the glide path optimum is \(J^{\mathrm{glide}} = \sup_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{det}}} \E[U(W_T)]\). By supremum over a larger set, \(J^{\mathrm{opt}} \ge J^{\mathrm{glide}}\). When CAPE has predictive power (\(\beta \ne 0\)), the first-order condition \(\alpha_t^* = \frac{\mu(x_t) - r}{\gamma \sigma^2} + \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}}(x_t)\) depends non-trivially on \(x_t\). Since no single deterministic path \(\alpha(t)\) can equal \(\alpha_t^*(x_t)\) almost surely, the inequality is strict: \(J^{\mathrm{opt}} > J^{\mathrm{glide}}\).

Empirical Tier

Analyze the performance of 2010 target-date funds during the 2008 Global Financial Crisis. Why did funds with identical target retirement dates exhibit return dispersions exceeding 25 percentage points?

Solution

In 2008, 2010-vintage target-date funds were within two years of retirement. However, fund providers lacked regulatory standardization in glide path design. Providers following an aggressive 'Through' philosophy held up to 68% equities at retirement (e.g. AllianceBernstein), suffering losses exceeding 35% in 2008. Providers following a conservative 'To' philosophy held as little as 25% equities (e.g. Wells Fargo), losing under 12%.

This massive 25% dispersion prompted joint SEC and Department of Labor congressional hearings in 2009, highlighting that fund names (e.g. 'Target 2010') gave participants a false impression of uniform conservatism while masking massive provider-level asset allocation risk.

In Lab 1, the 95th percentile maximum drawdown in the final 5 pre-retirement years was 22.56% for To-Retirement, 27.60% for Through-Retirement, and 30.93% for Static 60/40. What structural features explain this ordering?

Solution

In the final 5 years (ages 60 to 65), the To-Retirement strategy has an average equity exposure of \(\frac{42\% + 30\%}{2} = 36\%\), the Through-Retirement strategy has \(\frac{58\% + 50\%}{2} = 54\%\), and Static 60/40 maintains 60%.

Because portfolio volatility scales almost linearly with equity weight (\(\sigma_P \approx \alpha_E \sigma_E\)), the tail drawdown risk in the retirement red zone is strictly ordered by late-career equity exposure. The To-Retirement strategy successfully insulates the nest egg when dollar portfolio size is at its peak, reducing tail drawdown by over 800 basis points compared to static 60/40.

Explain how auto-enrollment under the 2006 US Pension Protection Act altered the demographics and risk profile of target-date fund investors.

Solution

Prior to 2006, 401(k) plans required active opt-in, resulting in low participation rates among young, lower-income, and financially unsophisticated employees. Default investments were restricted to money market cash funds to avoid sponsor liability.

The Pension Protection Act granted safe-harbor status to target-date funds as Qualified Default Investment Alternatives (QDIAs) with automatic enrollment and auto-escalation. This shifted hundreds of billions of dollars from cash into diversified equity-bond glide paths, radically increasing household equity market participation while concentrating default retirement assets into a handful of top commercial glide path providers (Vanguard, Fidelity, T. Rowe Price).

Formulate the optimal glide path problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) with state vector \(\mathbf{s}_t = (W_t, H_t, x_t)\) where \(x_t\) is the dividend-price ratio. Derive the dynamic programming Bellman equation.

Solution

The state vector is \(\mathbf{s}_t = (W_t, H_t, x_t) \in \R_+ \times \R_+ \times \R\). The Bellman equation for value function \(V(W_t, H_t, x_t, t)\) under CRRA terminal utility \(U(W_T) = \frac{W_T^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\) is:

\[ V(W_t, H_t, x_t, t) = \max_{\alpha_t \in [0, 1]} \E_t \left[ V(W_{t+1}, H_{t+1}, x_{t+1}, t+1) \right], \]

subject to transition laws: \(W_{t+1} = [W_t(1 + r_f + \alpha_t (r_{t+1} - r_f)) + s Y_t]\), \(x_{t+1} = \phi x_t + \eta_{t+1}\), and \(H_{t+1} = \E_{t+1}[\sum_{s=t+2}^T M_s Y_s]\). The optimal feedback control \(\alpha_t^*(\mathbf{s}_t)\) generates an adaptive, state-dependent glide path that depends on wealth buffer \(W_t\), remaining human capital \(H_t\), and valuation state \(x_t\).

How does integrating deferred life annuities (longevity insurance purchased at age 65 that begins payouts at age 85) resolve the tension between To-Retirement and Through-Retirement glide paths?

Solution

Deferred life annuities decouple the first 20 years of retirement from late-life longevity risk. By purchasing an Advanced Life Deferred Annuity (ALDA) at age 65 that pays guaranteed income starting at age 85, the retiree's post-retirement investment horizon is truncated to a finite 20-year window \([65, 85]\).

This allows the fund manager to adopt a conservative 'To-Retirement' glide path from 60 to 75 to eliminate sequence-of-returns drawdown risk without fearing late-life wealth depletion. Longevity risk is transferred to the insurance provider, allowing optimal asset allocation to focus purely on medium-term consumption smoothing.

Analyze the systemic macro-financial stability implications if 80% of defined-contribution assets are invested in deterministic, identical glide paths. What happens during an equity crash?

Solution

Uniform deterministic glide paths introduce coordinated mechanical rebalancing flows:

  1. Anti-Momentum Rebalancing: At high frequencies, TDFs must rebalance to maintain fixed daily target weights. Following a severe equity drop, TDFs mechanically buy equities and sell bonds, acting as short-term stabilizing market absorbers.
  2. Demographic Macro Liquidation: Over multi-year horizons, aging demographics (e.g. Baby Boomer retirement) force massive, programmatic asset rotations from equities into fixed income. If an entire demographic cohort enters the red zone simultaneously, automatic de-risking exerts persistent downward structural pressure on equity risk premiums and upward pressure on long-term bond valuations.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the primary structural difference between a 'To-Retirement' and a 'Through-Retirement' target-date glide path?

    Options

    'To-Retirement' hits its lowest equity allocation (e.g. 30%) at retirement age 65. 'Through-Retirement' lands at age 65 with ~50% equity and continues gliding down toward 30% around age 80.

  2. Why does sequence-of-returns risk matter decisively during the decumulation phase but not during pure accumulation without withdrawals?

    Options

    Without cash flows, terminal wealth is \(\prod (1+R_t)\), invariant to ordering. With withdrawals \(C > 0\), early negative returns force selling shares at depressed prices, permanently impairing the portfolio.

  3. What period in a retirement saver's lifecycle is termed the "Retirement Red Zone"?

    Options

    The 5–10 years around retirement are the Red Zone because portfolio balance is at its career peak and the worker has minimal remaining career earnings to offset major market losses.

  4. In Lab 1, what was the 95th percentile maximum drawdown percentage in the final 5 pre-retirement years for the 'To-Retirement' glide path?

    The 'To-Retirement' glide path limited the 95th percentile late drawdown to 22.56% (0.2256).

  5. In Lab 1, what was the 95th percentile maximum drawdown percentage in the final 5 pre-retirement years for the Static 60/40 portfolio?

    The Static 60/40 strategy exhibited a 95th percentile late drawdown of 30.93% (0.3093).

  6. Which legislation established target-date funds as safe-harbor Qualified Default Investment Alternatives (QDIAs) in the United States?

    Options

    The Pension Protection Act (PPA) of 2006 authorized employers to default auto-enrolled participants into target-date funds without fiduciary liability for market losses.

  7. Why do deterministic commercial glide paths suffer welfare loss relative to stochastic dynamic control policies?

    Options

    Deterministic glide paths de-risk on fixed schedules regardless of whether equities are historically cheap or expensive, whether the participant is ahead or behind savings targets, or whether career earnings are safe or cyclical.

  8. How does purchasing a deferred life annuity at age 65 alter the optimal post-retirement asset allocation glide path?

    Options

    By insuring lifetime income beyond age 85, a deferred annuity removes terminal longevity risk from liquid portfolio management, allowing the retiree to maintain a conservative portfolio during early retirement.
