The Static Baseline & the Fallacy of Time Diversification

A pervasive Wall Street adage asserts that equities become safer over long investment horizons because annualized return variance shrinks at rate \(1/T\). This heuristic, commonly termed time diversification, is mathematically fallacious under independent returns. Paul Samuelson proved that an investor with power utility over terminal wealth chooses identical portfolio weights regardless of horizon. While annualized volatility shrinks, cumulative dollar variance explodes exponentially, magnifying terminal shortfall magnitude in adverse states.

1. The Multi-Period Setup & Samuelson's Invariance Theorem

Consider an investor endowed with initial financial wealth \(W_0 > 0\) facing an investment horizon of \(T\) discrete periods \(t \in \{0, 1, \dots, T\}\). At each date \(t\), capital is allocated between a risk-free bond yielding gross return \(R_f = 1 + r\) and a risky asset (the market equity portfolio) yielding gross return \(R_{t+1} = 1 + r + Z_{t+1}\), where \(Z_{t+1}\) denotes the excess return.

We begin by formalizing the canonical baseline where returns are independently and identically distributed across periods, following Samuelson (1979) and Merton (1969).

Definition 1.1 (CRRA Terminal Wealth Utility)

An investor evaluates terminal wealth \(W_T\) through Constant Relative Risk Aversion (CRRA utility) preferences:

\[ u(W_T) = \begin{cases} \frac{W_T^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}, & \gamma > 0, \; \gamma \neq 1, \\ \ln W_T, & \gamma = 1, \end{cases} \]

where \(\gamma \equiv -W \frac{u''(W)}{u'(W)}\) denotes the Arrow-Pratt coefficient of relative risk aversion.

Wealth evolves dynamically under portfolio weight \(\alpha_t\) allocated to the risky asset at date \(t\):

\[ W_{t+1} = W_t \left( R_f + \alpha_t (R_{t+1} - R_f) \right) = W_t R_{p, t+1}(\alpha_t). \]

The investor solves the dynamic program:

\[ J(W_0, 0) = \sup_{\{\alpha_0, \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{T-1}\}} \E_0 \left[ u(W_T) \right]. \]
Theorem 1.1 (Samuelson's Horizon Invariance Theorem)

Assume risky gross returns \(R_t\) are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) across all periods \(t = 1, \dots, T\), and the investor has CRRA preferences with relative risk aversion \(\gamma > 0\). Then:

  1. The optimal portfolio weight \(\alpha_t^*\) is strictly independent of current wealth \(W_t\).
  2. The optimal portfolio weight \(\alpha_t^*\) is strictly independent of the remaining investment horizon \(T - t\).
  3. The multi-period dynamic problem collapses to repeated static single-period optimization:
\[ \alpha_t^* = \alpha^* \equiv \argmax_{\alpha} \E \left[ \frac{(R_f + \alpha(R - R_f))^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} \right], \quad \forall t \in \{0, 1, \dots, T-1\}. \]
Proof of Theorem 1.1

We proceed by backward induction. At date \(T-1\), terminal wealth is \(W_T = W_{T-1} R_{p, T}(\alpha_{T-1})\). The value function at \(T-1\) is:

\[ J(W_{T-1}, T-1) = \sup_{\alpha_{T-1}} \E_{T-1} \left[ \frac{\left( W_{T-1} R_{p, T}(\alpha_{T-1}) \right)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} \right] = \frac{W_{T-1}^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} \sup_{\alpha_{T-1}} \E \left[ R_{p, T}(\alpha_{T-1})^{1-\gamma} \right], \]

where the second equality exploits the scale-homogeneity of power utility and the assumption that \(R_T\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_{T-1}\). Define the scalar constant:

\[ K \equiv \sup_{\alpha} \E \left[ R_p(\alpha)^{1-\gamma} \right] = \E \left[ R_p(\alpha^*)^{1-\gamma} \right]. \]

The single-period optimizer \(\alpha^*\) depends only on the distribution of \(R\) and risk aversion \(\gamma\). The value function satisfies:

\[ J(W_{T-1}, T-1) = \frac{W_{T-1}^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} K. \]

Now consider date \(T-2\). By the Bellman equation:

\[ J(W_{T-2}, T-2) = \sup_{\alpha_{T-2}} \E_{T-2} \left[ J(W_{T-1}, T-1) \right] = \sup_{\alpha_{T-2}} \E_{T-2} \left[ \frac{(W_{T-2} R_{p, T-1}(\alpha_{T-2}))^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} K \right]. \]

Factoring out \(W_{T-2}^{1-\gamma} / (1-\gamma)\) and using the i.i.d. assumption on \(R_{T-1}\):

\[ J(W_{T-2}, T-2) = \frac{W_{T-2}^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} K \sup_{\alpha_{T-2}} \E \left[ R_{p, T-1}(\alpha_{T-2})^{1-\gamma} \right] = \frac{W_{T-2}^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} K^2. \]

The optimal policy is identically \(\alpha_{T-2}^* = \alpha^*\). By induction, for any date \(t \le T-1\):

\[ J(W_t, t) = \frac{W_t^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} K^{T-t}, \quad \alpha_t^* = \alpha^*. \]

The optimal weight is identical across every period, establishing horizon invariance.

2. Anatomy of the Time Diversification Fallacy

The heuristic doctrine of time diversification claims that because the annualized sample mean return has variance that shrinks at rate \(1/T\), equities become "essentially riskless" over horizons of 20 or 30 years. Let us formalize the arithmetic of log returns.

Let simple continuous returns follow a geometric random walk: \(d \ln S_t = (\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2) dt + \sigma dW_t\). Over horizon \(T\), cumulative log return is \(r_{0, T} \equiv \ln(S_T / S_0) \sim \mathcal{N}\left( (\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T, \sigma^2 T \right)\).

Remark 1.1 (Annualized vs. Cumulative Return Dispersion)

The annualized compound return is \(\bar{r}_T \equiv \frac{1}{T} \ln(S_T / S_0)\). Its mean and variance are:

\[ \E[\bar{r}_T] = \mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2, \quad \Var(\bar{r}_T) = \frac{\sigma^2}{T}. \]

As \(T \to \infty\), \(\Var(\bar{r}_T) \to 0\). This is simply the weak law of large numbers. However, the investor does not consume the annualized rate \(\bar{r}_T\); the investor consumes terminal wealth \(W_T = W_0 e^{r_{0, T}}\). The variance of cumulative log returns satisfies:

\[ \Var(r_{0, T}) = \Var(\ln W_T - \ln W_0) = \sigma^2 T \to \infty \quad \text{as } T \to \infty. \]

Furthermore, by the properties of the log-normal distribution, the variance of nominal terminal wealth is:

\[ \Var(W_T) = W_0^2 e^{2\mu T} \left( e^{\sigma^2 T} - 1 \right), \]

which explodes exponentially with the investment horizon.

Example 1.1 (Numerical Dispersion of Terminal Wealth)

Consider an investor with \(W_0 = \$100\), equity drift \(\mu = 0.08\), risk-free rate \(r = 0.02\), volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), and risk aversion \(\gamma = 3.0\). By the continuous Merton fraction, the optimal portfolio weight is \(\alpha^* = \frac{0.08 - 0.02}{3.0 \times 0.20^2} = 0.50\). The portfolio drift is \(\mu_p = 0.02 + 0.50(0.06) = 0.05\), and portfolio volatility is \(\sigma_p = 0.50 \times 0.20 = 0.10\).

  • At \(T = 1\) year: \(\E[W_1] = \$105.13\), \(\mathrm{Std}(W_1) = \$10.54\), annualized log variance = \(0.0100\).
  • At \(T = 10\) years: \(\E[W_{10}] = \$164.87\), \(\mathrm{Std}(W_{10}) = \$53.47\), annualized log variance = \(0.0010\).
  • At \(T = 30\) years: \(\E[W_{30}] = \$448.17\), \(\mathrm{Std}(W_{30}) = \$265.80\), annualized log variance = \(0.00033\).

While annualized variance declined by 96.7% from 1 year to 30 years, the standard deviation of terminal wealth expanded by a factor of 25.2 (\(\$10.54 \to \$265.80\)).

Theorem 1.2 (The Probability of Shortfall vs. Expected Shortfall Paradox)

Let \(S_t\) follow geometric Brownian motion with \(\mu > r\). Define the shortfall event as \(A_T \equiv \{W_T < W_0 e^{r T}\}\). As \(T \to \infty\):

  1. The probability of underperforming the risk-free benchmark converges to zero:
\[ \lim_{T \to \infty} \Prob(A_T) = \lim_{T \to \infty} \Phi\left( -\frac{(\mu - r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\sqrt{T}}{\sigma} \right) = 0 \quad \text{provided } \mu - r > \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2. \]
  1. However, conditional on shortfall occurring, the expected dollar magnitude of the loss expands without bound:
\[ \lim_{T \to \infty} \E\left[ W_0 e^{r T} - W_T \;\middle|\; A_T \right] = \infty. \]
Proof of Theorem 1.2

The shortfall condition \(W_T < W_0 e^{r T}\) is equivalent to \((\mu - r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2) T + \sigma_p W_T < 0\), or \(Z < -\frac{(\mu - r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2)\sqrt{T}}{\sigma_p}\) where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). Under the stated condition, the upper limit of integration converges to \(-\infty\), so \(\Prob(A_T) \to 0\).

Conditional on this tail event, terminal wealth is bounded above by \(W_0 e^{r T}\), while the benchmark grows at \(e^{r T}\). The shortfall is \(W_0 e^{r T}(1 - e^{(\mu - r - \sigma_p^2/2)T + \sigma_p \sqrt{T} Z})\). As \(Z \to -\infty\), \(e^{\sigma_p \sqrt{T} Z} \to 0\). Thus, in the shortfall tail, the investor loses virtually the entire benchmark value, which grows at exponential rate \(e^{r T}\). Formally, \(\E[W_0 e^{r T} - W_T \mid A_T] \ge W_0 e^{r T} (1 - \epsilon) \to \infty\).

Lab 1 · Evaluating Samuelson's Invariance & Terminal Dispersion

Simulating cumulative wealth distributions across horizons T in {1, 5, 10, 20, 30} under i.i.d. returns will show that the optimal CRRA fraction remains exactly constant at 0.50, while annualized log variance shrinks as 1/T and terminal wealth standard deviation expands from $10.60 to $266.64.

"""Lab PR6-L01-1: The Time Diversification Fallacy and Samuelson's Equivalence.

SIMULATED: Monte Carlo evaluation of terminal wealth dispersion, annualized vs
cumulative variance, and constant CRRA optimal allocation across investment horizons.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def evaluate_time_diversification(
    horizons: list[int],
    mu: float = 0.08,
    r: float = 0.02,
    sigma: float = 0.20,
    gamma: float = 3.0,
    w0: float = 100.0,
    n_paths: int = 10000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[int, dict[str, float]]:
    """Evaluate annualized variance, cumulative variance, terminal wealth dispersion, and optimal CRRA fraction."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    results: dict[int, dict[str, float]] = {}

    # Merton / Samuelson continuous-time optimal fraction for CRRA utility under i.i.d. returns
    alpha_star = (mu - r) / (gamma * sigma**2)

    for horizon in horizons:
        # Standard normal random shocks for cumulative horizon return
        z = rng.standard_normal(n_paths)
        # Log return on optimal portfolio: mean and variance scale linearly with horizon
        port_drift = r + alpha_star * (mu - r)
        port_vol = alpha_star * sigma
        log_ret = (port_drift - 0.5 * port_vol**2) * horizon + port_vol * math.sqrt(horizon) * z
        terminal_wealth = w0 * np.exp(log_ret)

        annualized_var = float(np.var(log_ret / horizon))
        cumulative_var = float(np.var(log_ret))
        mean_terminal = float(np.mean(terminal_wealth))
        std_terminal = float(np.std(terminal_wealth))

        # Shortfall metrics relative to initial capital
        shortfall_mask = terminal_wealth < w0
        p_shortfall = float(np.mean(shortfall_mask))
        worst_loss = float(np.max(w0 - terminal_wealth)) if np.any(shortfall_mask) else 0.0
        expected_shortfall = float(np.mean(w0 - terminal_wealth[shortfall_mask])) if np.any(shortfall_mask) else 0.0

        # Theoretical terminal wealth variance: W0^2 * exp(2 * port_drift * T) * (exp(port_vol^2 * T) - 1)
        theo_var = (w0**2) * math.exp(2.0 * port_drift * horizon) * (math.exp((port_vol**2) * horizon) - 1.0)
        theo_std = math.sqrt(theo_var)

        results[horizon] = {
            "alpha_star": alpha_star,
            "annualized_var": annualized_var,
            "cumulative_var": cumulative_var,
            "mean_terminal": mean_terminal,
            "std_terminal": std_terminal,
            "theo_std": theo_std,
            "p_shortfall": p_shortfall,
            "expected_shortfall": expected_shortfall,
            "worst_loss": worst_loss,
        }

    return results


def main() -> None:
    horizons = [1, 5, 10, 20, 30]
    mu = 0.08
    r = 0.02
    sigma = 0.20
    gamma = 3.0
    w0 = 100.0

    res = evaluate_time_diversification(
        horizons=horizons,
        mu=mu,
        r=r,
        sigma=sigma,
        gamma=gamma,
        w0=w0,
        n_paths=20000,
        seed=42,
    )

    print("Samuelson (1969, 1979) Time Diversification & Horizon Invariance Analysis:")
    print(f"Parameters: mu={mu:.2f}, r={r:.2f}, sigma={sigma:.2f}, gamma={gamma:.1f}, W0={w0:.1f}")
    print("=" * 86)
    print(
        f"{'Horizon (yr)':<14} {'Opt Alpha':<11} {'Ann LogVar':<13} {'Cum LogVar':<13} "
        f"{'E[W_T] ($)':<12} {'Std[W_T] ($)':<13} {'P(W_T<W0)':<10}"
    )
    print("-" * 86)

    for h in horizons:
        d = res[h]
        print(
            f"{h:<14d} {d['alpha_star']:<11.4f} {d['annualized_var']:<13.5f} {d['cumulative_var']:<13.4f} "
            f"{d['mean_terminal']:<12.2f} {d['std_terminal']:<13.2f} {d['p_shortfall']:<10.4f}"
        )

    print("=" * 86)
    print("Key Invariance: Optimal CRRA portfolio weight is strictly constant across all horizons.")
    print("Fallacy Proof: Ann LogVar -> 0 as 1/T, but Cumulative LogVar and Std[W_T] explode with T.")

Where the Canon Breaks

Samuelson's horizon invariance theorem rests entirely on two foundational pillars: CRRA utility and i.i.d. returns. In empirical financial markets, both assumptions break down decisively:

  • Predictable Investment Opportunities: As documented by Fama & French (1988) and Campbell & Shiller (1988), stock returns exhibit multi-year mean reversion. Valuation ratios (such as dividend-price and cyclically adjusted price-earnings) predict long-horizon returns. Under mean reversion, multi-period variance scales at less than \(T\), breaking the i.i.d. assumption and generating positive intertemporal hedging demands.
  • Non-Tradable Human Capital: Individual investors possess labour income streams that cannot be shorted or pledged as liquid collateral. As shown by Bodie, Merton & Samuelson (1992) and Campbell & Viceira (2002), human capital acts as an implicit safe bond for young workers, making their total wealth far less risky than their financial balance and rationalizing declining equity allocations over the lifecycle.
  • Stochastic Interest Rates: In multi-period settings, cash deposits face severe rollover/reinvestment risk. The true riskless asset for a long-horizon investor is not rolling cash, but a zero-coupon bond matching the investment horizon.

Misconceptions

  1. "Stocks are safer for long-term investors because annualized volatility approaches zero over 30 years."

    Annualized volatility \(\sigma / \sqrt{T}\) shrinks solely because of division by \(\sqrt{T}\), representing the variance of the average annual rate. But investors consume terminal dollar wealth \(W_T\), not an average percentage rate. The standard deviation of terminal wealth \(\Var(W_T)^{1/2} = W_0 e^{\mu T} \sqrt{e^{\sigma^2 T} - 1}\) explodes exponentially. In bad economic states, the dollar shortfall after 30 years is orders of magnitude worse than after 1 year.

  2. "Samuelson proved that young investors should never hold more stocks than retirees."

    Samuelson proved that under i.i.d. returns and CRRA utility over terminal financial wealth, the optimal portfolio weight is constant. However, young investors possess vast amounts of non-tradable human capital. Because future wages are largely bond-like, young workers' financial portfolios should be leveraged into equities to achieve their optimal total wealth target. Samuelson's invariance applies to total wealth, not financial wealth in isolation.

  3. "A declining probability of loss over time guarantees that time diversification is beneficial."

    Evaluating investment risk purely through the probability of loss \(\Prob(W_T < W_0)\) violates expected utility theory and second-order stochastic dominance. A risk-averse investor weighs the utility penalty of severe losses. As \(T\) expands, although \(\Prob(W_T < W_0)\) may decline due to positive expected drift, the severity of the loss conditional on underperformance (\(\E[W_0 - W_T \mid W_T < W_0]\)) grows without bound.

Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

An investor with \(W_0 = \$100,000\) invests in a market portfolio with expected return \(\mu = 0.07\) and volatility \(\sigma = 0.16\). Compute the standard deviation of annualized return \(\bar{r}_T\) and the standard deviation of cumulative log return \(r_{0, T}\) for horizons \(T = 1, 4, 16\), and \(25\) years.

Solution

For cumulative log return \(r_{0, T} \sim \mathcal{N}((\mu - \sigma^2/2)T, \sigma^2 T)\), the standard deviation is \(\sigma \sqrt{T}\):

  • \(T = 1\): \(\mathrm{Std}(r_{0, 1}) = 0.16 \times \sqrt{1} = 0.1600\) (16.00%); \(\mathrm{Std}(\bar{r}_1) = 0.16 / \sqrt{1} = 0.1600\) (16.00%).
  • \(T = 4\): \(\mathrm{Std}(r_{0, 4}) = 0.16 \times 2 = 0.3200\) (32.00%); \(\mathrm{Std}(\bar{r}_4) = 0.16 / 2 = 0.0800\) (8.00%).
  • \(T = 16\): \(\mathrm{Std}(r_{0, 16}) = 0.16 \times 4 = 0.6400\) (64.00%); \(\mathrm{Std}(\bar{r}_{16}) = 0.16 / 4 = 0.0400\) (4.00%).
  • \(T = 25\): \(\mathrm{Std}(r_{0, 25}) = 0.16 \times 5 = 0.8000\) (80.00%); \(\mathrm{Std}(\bar{r}_{25}) = 0.16 / 5 = 0.0320\) (3.20%).

Annualized standard deviation declines strictly by factor \(1/\sqrt{T}\), while cumulative standard deviation expands strictly by \(\sqrt{T}\).

Calculate the exact theoretical standard deviation of nominal terminal wealth \(\Var(W_T)^{1/2}\) for the investor in Problem M1 at \(T = 1\) and \(T = 25\) years with \(W_0 = \$100,000\).

Solution

The theoretical variance of log-normal terminal wealth is \(\Var(W_T) = W_0^2 e^{2\mu T} (e^{\sigma^2 T} - 1)\). Thus \(\mathrm{Std}(W_T) = W_0 e^{\mu T} \sqrt{e^{\sigma^2 T} - 1}\).

  • At \(T = 1\): \(\E[W_1] = 100000 \times e^{0.07} = \$107,250.82\). The term \(\sqrt{e^{0.16^2 \times 1} - 1} = \sqrt{e^{0.0256} - 1} = \sqrt{0.02593} \approx 0.16103\). \(\mathrm{Std}(W_1) = 107250.82 \times 0.16103 \approx \$17,270.84\).
  • At \(T = 25\): \(\E[W_{25}] = 100000 \times e^{0.07 \times 25} = 100000 \times e^{1.75} \approx \$575,460.27\). The term \(\sqrt{e^{0.16^2 \times 25} - 1} = \sqrt{e^{0.64} - 1} = \sqrt{1.89648 - 1} = \sqrt{0.89648} \approx 0.94683\). \(\mathrm{Std}(W_{25}) = 575460.27 \times 0.94683 \approx \$544,861.43\).

Terminal dollar dispersion expands from \(\$17,271\) at 1 year to \(\$544,861\) at 25 years, representing a 31.5-fold increase in uncertainty.

An investor with log utility (\(\gamma = 1\)) faces risk-free rate \(r = 0.03\), equity mean return \(\mu = 0.09\), and equity volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\). Verify that the single-period Merton fraction equals 1.50 and calculate the value function \(J(W_0, 0)\) for horizons \(T = 5\) and \(T = 20\).

Solution

For log utility, \(\alpha^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2} = \frac{0.09 - 0.03}{0.20^2} = \frac{0.06}{0.04} = 1.5000\). The portfolio growth rate is \(g^* = r + \alpha^*(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2}(\alpha^* \sigma)^2 = 0.03 + 1.50(0.06) - 0.5(1.50 \times 0.20)^2 = 0.03 + 0.09 - 0.5(0.09) = 0.12 - 0.045 = 0.0750\) (7.50% continuous growth).

The value function for log utility is \(J(W_0, 0) = \E[\ln W_T] = \ln W_0 + g^* T\):

  • For \(T = 5\): \(J(W_0, 0) = \ln W_0 + 0.0750 \times 5 = \ln W_0 + 0.3750\).
  • For \(T = 20\): \(J(W_0, 0) = \ln W_0 + 0.0750 \times 20 = \ln W_0 + 1.5000\).

The optimal policy is identically 1.50 across all dates and horizons.

Proof Tier

Prove that if the investor possesses quadratic utility \(u(W) = W - \frac{b}{2} W^2\), the optimal portfolio weight is NOT horizon-invariant, and that the static single-period equivalence breaks down.

Solution

Quadratic utility exhibits increasing relative risk aversion: \(\gamma_R(W) = \frac{b W}{1 - b W}\), which strictly increases with wealth \(W\). Under backward induction, the value function at date \(T-1\) is \(J(W_{T-1}, T-1) = W_{T-1} \E[R_p] - \frac{b}{2} W_{T-1}^2 \E[R_p^2]\). Maximizing with respect to \(\alpha_{T-1}\) yields an optimal weight that is a decreasing function of \(W_{T-1}\).

At date \(T-2\), when evaluating \(\E_{T-2}[J(W_{T-1}, T-1)]\), the expectation involves nonlinear terms in \(W_{T-1}\) and \(W_{T-1}^2\). Because \(\alpha_{T-1}^*\) depends on \(W_{T-1}\), the date \(T-2\) optimizer cannot factor out wealth, and the continuation value function is no longer quadratic. Consequently, the optimal weight \(\alpha_{T-2}^*\) depends explicitly on the remaining horizon \(T-1\) and current wealth \(W_{T-2}\). Power utility is the unique family satisfying scale invariance and horizon independence under i.i.d. returns.

Prove that for an investor with CRRA utility \(\gamma > 1\), the certainty equivalent return per period \(\text{CER} \equiv \left( (1-\gamma) \E[u(W_T)] \right)^{\frac{1}{T(1-\gamma)}} - 1\) is strictly constant across all horizons under the optimal policy \(\alpha^*\).

Solution

From Theorem 1.1, the value function satisfies \(J(W_0, 0) = \frac{W_0^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} K^T\), where \(K \equiv \E[R_p(\alpha^*)^{1-\gamma}]\). The certainty equivalent wealth \(\text{CE}_T\) is defined by \(\frac{\text{CE}_T^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} = J(W_0, 0) = \frac{W_0^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} K^T\). Solving for \(\text{CE}_T\) yields:

\[ \text{CE}_T = W_0 \left( K^T \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}} = W_0 \left( K^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}} \right)^T. \]

The per-period gross certainty equivalent return is \(R_{\text{CE}} \equiv \left( \frac{\text{CE}_T}{W_0} \right)^{1/T} = K^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}\). Since \(K\) is a constant determined entirely by the single-period return distribution and \(\gamma\), \(R_{\text{CE}}\) is strictly invariant to horizon \(T\). The annualized certainty equivalent return does not improve with horizon under i.i.d. returns.

Prove that under geometric Brownian motion, as \(T \to \infty\), the median terminal wealth is strictly less than the mean terminal wealth, and calculate the asymptotic ratio \(\mathrm{Median}(W_T) / \E[W_T]\).

Solution

Let \(\ln(W_T / W_0) \sim \mathcal{N}\left( (\mu_p - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2)T, \sigma_p^2 T \right)\). Because the median of a normal distribution is its mean, the median of log-wealth is \((\mu_p - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2)T\). Since the exponential function is strictly monotonic:

\[ \mathrm{Median}(W_T) = W_0 \exp\left( (\mu_p - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2)T \right). \]

In contrast, the expected value of nominal wealth is \(\E[W_T] = W_0 \exp(\mu_p T)\). Taking the ratio:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{Median}(W_T)}{\E[W_T]} = \frac{W_0 e^{(\mu_p - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2)T}}{W_0 e^{\mu_p T}} = \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2 T \right). \]

As \(T \to \infty\), this ratio converges exponentially to zero. Almost all realization paths fall far below the expected value, which is driven entirely by extreme positive tail outliers.

Empirical Tier

In a historical backtest of U.S. equities from 1926 to 2020, 20-year rolling returns never suffered a negative real return. Explain why this empirical observation does NOT validate the time diversification thesis for forward-looking portfolio choice.

Solution

This empirical observation suffers from three fundamental econometric fallacies:

  1. Survivorship and Conditioning Bias: The 1926–2020 U.S. sample represents the most successful capitalist economy of the 20th century. In other major equity markets (e.g., Germany, Russia, Austria, Japan), long-term investors suffered catastrophic total capital wiped out by war, hyperinflation, or regime collapse (Jorion & Goetzmann 1999).
  2. Effective Sample Size: Over a 94-year historical span, there are fewer than 5 non-overlapping 20-year periods. Rolling 20-year windows share 95% overlapping data, generating massive autocorrelation that artificially inflates statistical confidence.
  3. Fallacy of Incomplete Distribution: Even if \(\Prob(W_{20} < W_0) = 0\) in sample, forward-looking tail risk remains positive. A strategy that achieves high probability of profit but severe tail ruin is not safer for risk-averse investors.

Using the parameters from Lab 1 (\(\mu = 0.08, r = 0.02, \sigma = 0.20, \gamma = 3.0\)), verify that the simulated shortfall probability \(\Prob(W_T < W_0)\) drops from 32.3% at 1 year to 0.70% at 30 years, while the maximum observed dollar loss in the simulation expanded. Explain the economic significance for a pension trustee.

Solution

In Lab 1, \(\Prob(W_T < W_0)\) decreases from 0.3234 at \(T=1\) to 0.0070 at \(T=30\). However, conditional on hitting the 0.70% tail, the portfolio experiences sustained negative compounding over three decades, resulting in severe terminal deprivation.

For a defined-benefit pension trustee, liabilities grow deterministically with interest rates and inflation. A 0.70% probability of catastrophic deficit at \(T=30\) means the pension fund faces insolvency with zero remaining time to recover through contribution increases. The trustee cannot treat equities as safe simply because shortfall probability is low; downside severity must be hedged via liability-matching assets.

Calculate the variance ratio \(V(k) = \frac{\Var(r_{t, t+k})}{k \Var(r_{t, t+1})}\) for \(k = 5\) if annual log returns have first-order autocorrelation \(\rho_1 = -0.15\) and zero higher-order autocorrelations. How does this compare with the pure i.i.d. case?

Solution

For a stationary return series with autocovariances \(\gamma_j\):

\[ \Var(r_{t, t+k}) = k \gamma_0 + 2 \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} (k-j) \gamma_j. \]

Dividing by \(k \gamma_0\): \(V(k) = 1 + 2 \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \left(1 - \frac{j}{k}\right) \rho_j\). With \(\rho_1 = -0.15\) and \(\rho_j = 0\) for \(j \ge 2\):

\[ V(5) = 1 + 2 \left(1 - \frac{1}{5}\right) \rho_1 = 1 + 2(0.80)(-0.15) = 1 - 0.24 = 0.7600. \]

Under pure i.i.d. returns, \(V(5) \equiv 1.0000\). Negative autocorrelation reduces 5-year return variance by 24%, establishing empirical mean reversion that breaks Samuelson's horizon invariance.

Research Tier

Analyze why commercial target-date funds allocate 90% of assets to equities for 25-year-old investors and glide down to 30%–40% at age 65. If the time diversification argument is a mathematical fallacy, what is the rigorous economic justification for this institutional practice?

Solution

The economic justification for declining equity glide paths rests not on time diversification, but on non-tradable human capital (Bodie, Merton & Samuelson 1992; Campbell & Viceira 2002):

  1. Human Capital as an Implicit Bond: A 25-year-old worker possesses a present value of future labour earnings that dwarfs their financial portfolio (\(H_t \gg W_t\)). If wage income has low correlation with equity markets, human capital behaves like a non-tradable, inflation-indexed bond.
  2. Total Wealth Allocation: An investor optimizes allocation over total wealth \(W_t^{\text{tot}} = W_t + H_t\). If the optimal equity share of total wealth is \(\alpha^* \approx 40\%\), the dollar equity holding must be \(\alpha^*(W_t + H_t)\). Because all equities must reside in financial wealth, \(\alpha_t^F = \alpha^* (1 + H_t/W_t)\). For young workers, this easily exceeds 100%, justifying maximum permissible equity allocation.
  3. Lifecycle Depletion: As the worker ages, remaining career years diminish, depleting \(H_t\) while financial wealth \(W_t\) accumulates. As \(H_t / W_t \to 0\) at retirement, \(\alpha_t^F \to \alpha^*\). The glide path is an optimal response to changing balance-sheet composition, completely independent of time diversification.

Formulate the continuous-time HJB equation for an investor with Epstein-Zin recursive preferences over terminal wealth with risk aversion \(\gamma\) and elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(\psi\). Under what parameter restrictions does Samuelson's horizon invariance hold?

Solution

Under Epstein-Zin recursive preferences without intermediate consumption, utility over terminal wealth collapses to the Kreps-Porteus certainty equivalent aggregator. When returns are i.i.d., the investment opportunity set is constant over time. The elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(\psi\) governs the willingness to substitute consumption across time; in the absence of intermediate consumption, \(\psi\) drops out of the terminal wealth portfolio selection problem.

Consequently, the objective function reduces purely to maximizing the certainty equivalent of terminal wealth under risk aversion \(\gamma\). Because the power aggregator \(\E[W_T^{1-\gamma}]^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}\) retains homotheticity, backward induction produces a constant continuation factor \(K\), and the optimal portfolio weight is \(\alpha^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\) across all horizons. Samuelson's invariance holds for all values of \(\psi\) under i.i.d. returns when utility depends solely on terminal wealth.

Suppose an institutional endowment has a perpetual horizon (\(T = \infty\)) with a mandatory spending rule of 5% of assets annually. Explain why the Samuelson horizon invariance theorem fails to describe its optimal asset allocation.

Solution

Samuelson's theorem fails for three distinct reasons in the endowment context:

  1. Intermediate Consumption Friction: The endowment consumes 5% annually (\(C_t = 0.05 W_t\)). With ongoing payouts, wealth is subjected to sequence-of-returns risk: a severe drawdown combined with ongoing 5% withdrawals impairs capital recovery irreversibly.
  2. Liability Benchmark: The endowment's liability is funding operating budgets and faculty salaries in perpetuity, which grow with higher education inflation. The true riskless asset is an inflation-protected perpetuity (consol bond), not cash or single-period bills.
  3. State-Dependent Opportunity Set: Real-world endowments face stochastic inflation and time-varying real interest rates, generating substantial Merton intertemporal hedging demands to immunize long-term purchasing power.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What was Paul Samuelson's primary conclusion regarding the time diversification argument under i.i.d. returns and power utility?

    Options

    Samuelson (1969, 1979) proved that under i.i.d. returns and CRRA utility, the multi-period dynamic problem collapses to repeated static single-period optimization, rendering portfolio weights horizon-invariant.

  2. Why is the decay of annualized return variance \(\Var(\bar{r}_T) = \sigma^2 / T\) an invalid justification for increasing equity risk over longer horizons?

    Options

    Annualized variance measures the precision of the average rate, but utility is defined over terminal wealth \(W_T\). Cumulative variance scales as \(\sigma^2 T\), producing massive terminal wealth dispersion.

  3. If the annual volatility of a stock index is \(\sigma = 0.20\), what is the standard deviation of its annualized average return over a 4-year horizon (\(\sigma / \sqrt{4}\))?

    \(\mathrm{Std}(\bar{r}_4) = \frac{0.20}{\sqrt{4}} = \frac{0.20}{2} = 0.1000\) (10.0%).

  4. For the same stock index with \(\sigma = 0.20\), what is the standard deviation of the cumulative 4-year log return (\(\sigma \sqrt{4}\))?

    \(\mathrm{Std}(r_{0, 4}) = 0.20 \times \sqrt{4} = 0.20 \times 2 = 0.4000\) (40.0%).

  5. What happens to the ratio of median terminal wealth to expected terminal wealth as the investment horizon \(T\) goes to infinity under geometric Brownian motion?

    Options

    Because log-normal wealth has positive skewness, \(\mathrm{Median}(W_T) / \E[W_T] = \exp(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 T) \to 0\). The median path underperforms the mean increasingly with time.

  6. Under what empirical violation of Samuelson's assumptions do long-horizon equity investments genuinely become safer per unit of time?

    Options

    When stock prices mean-revert, return innovations are negatively correlated with future expected return shocks, dampening multi-period cumulative variance below linear scaling.

  7. Why does a declining shortfall probability \(\Prob(W_T < W_0)\) not imply that long-horizon equities are riskless?

    Options

    Although the likelihood of losing money may shrink under positive drift, in the rare states where losses occur, the dollar magnitude of shortfall is catastrophic.

  8. An investor has CRRA risk aversion \(\gamma = 2.0\), risk-free rate \(r = 0.02\), expected return \(\mu = 0.06\), and volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\). What is the optimal equity fraction \(\alpha^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\)?

    \(\alpha^* = \frac{0.06 - 0.02}{2.0 \times 0.04} = \frac{0.04}{0.08} = 0.5000\).
