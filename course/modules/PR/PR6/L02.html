Continuous-Time Dynamic Portfolio Choice & Merton Hedging Demand

When investment opportunities fluctuate over time—driven by shifting interest rates, time-varying risk premiums, or macroeconomic state variables—static portfolio theory breaks down. Robert C. Merton derived the continuous-time Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman framework for multi-asset dynamic choice. Optimal asset demand separates additively into a static myopic component and an intertemporal hedging demand. Long-horizon investors hold extra assets that correlate with adverse state shifts, insulating their future marginal utility of wealth.

1. Stochastic State Variables & The Continuous-Time Economy

In Lesson 1 we established that under i.i.d. returns, the optimal policy collapses to repeated static allocation, proving the fallacy of time diversification. We now introduce time-varying investment opportunities following Merton (1971) and Merton (1973).

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \Prob)\) be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions, supporting a standard two-dimensional Brownian motion \((Z_P, Z_S)\) with instantaneous correlation \(dZ_{P,t} dZ_{S,t} = \rho dt\).

Definition 2.1 (The Stochastic Economy with State Variable)

The economy consists of three fundamental economic processes:

  1. State Variable \(S_t\): Governs the investment opportunity set (e.g. expected excess return, interest rate, or valuation multiple):
\[ dS_t = \mu_S(S_t, t) dt + \sigma_S(S_t, t) dZ_{S,t}. \]
  1. Risk-Free Asset \(B_t\): Money-market deposit earning instantaneous rate \(r(S_t)\):
\[ dB_t = r(S_t) B_t dt. \]
  1. Risky Asset \(P_t\): Price process with state-dependent expected return and volatility:
\[ \frac{dP_t}{P_t} = \mu(S_t, t) dt + \sigma(S_t, t) dZ_{P,t}. \]

Let \(\alpha_t\) denote the fraction of wealth allocated to the risky asset at date \(t\), and \(C_t \ge 0\) the consumption rate. Financial wealth \(W_t\) evolves according to the stochastic differential equation:

\[ dW_t = \left[ W_t \left( r(S_t) + \alpha_t (\mu(S_t) - r(S_t)) \right) - C_t \right] dt + W_t \alpha_t \sigma(S_t) dZ_{P,t}. \]

The investor chooses an adapted control policy \(\{(\alpha_t, C_t)\}_{t=0}^T\) to maximize lifetime discounted utility from consumption and terminal wealth:

\[ J(W_0, S_0, 0) = \sup_{\{\alpha, C\}} \E_0 \left[ \int_0^T e^{-\beta t} u(C_t) dt + e^{-\beta T} B(W_T) \right], \]

where \(\beta > 0\) is the subjective time discount rate, and \(u(C) = \frac{C^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\) is CRRA utility.

2. The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman PDE and the Hedging Decomposition

By the principle of optimality (Merton 1971), the value function \(J(W, S, t)\) satisfies the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation.

Theorem 2.1 (Merton Dynamic Portfolio Separation)

Let \(J(W, S, t) \in C^{2, 2, 1}\) be the value function satisfying the HJB PDE with strictly concave wealth utility (\(J_{WW} < 0\)). The optimal risky asset allocation \(\alpha^*\) decomposes additively into two orthogonal economic components:

\[ \alpha^*(W, S, t) = \underbrace{\alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}}}_{\text{Myopic Demand}} + \underbrace{\alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}}}_{\text{Intertemporal Hedging Demand}}, \]

where:

\[ \alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}} = -\frac{J_W}{W J_{WW}} \frac{\mu(S) - r(S)}{\sigma(S)^2}, \] \[ \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}} = -\frac{J_{WS}}{W J_{WW}} \frac{\rho \sigma_S(S)}{\sigma(S)}. \]
Proof of Theorem 2.1

The continuous-time HJB equation is:

\[ 0 = \sup_{\alpha, C} \left\{ u(C) - \beta J + J_t + J_W \left[ W(r + \alpha(\mu - r)) - C \right] + J_S \mu_S + \frac{1}{2} J_{WW} W^2 \alpha^2 \sigma^2 + J_{WS} W \alpha \rho \sigma \sigma_S + \frac{1}{2} J_{SS} \sigma_S^2 \right\}. \]

The objective function is strictly concave in \(\alpha\) because \(J_{WW} < 0\). Differentiating with respect to \(\alpha\) and setting the first-order condition to zero:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \alpha} \left\{ J_W W \alpha (\mu - r) + \frac{1}{2} J_{WW} W^2 \alpha^2 \sigma^2 + J_{WS} W \alpha \rho \sigma \sigma_S \right\} = 0. \] \[ J_W W (\mu - r) + J_{WW} W^2 \sigma^2 \alpha^* + J_{WS} W \rho \sigma \sigma_S = 0. \]

Dividing by \(W > 0\) and solving for \(\alpha^*\):

\[ J_{WW} W \sigma^2 \alpha^* = -J_W (\mu - r) - J_{WS} \rho \sigma \sigma_S. \] \[ \alpha^* = -\frac{J_W}{W J_{WW}} \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2} - \frac{J_{WS}}{W J_{WW}} \frac{\rho \sigma_S}{\sigma}. \]

This yields the exact decomposition stated in the theorem.

Remark 2.1 (Economic Interpretation of the Hedging Term)

Notice the cross-derivative \(J_{WS} \equiv \frac{\partial^2 J}{\partial W \partial S} = \frac{\partial}{\partial S} \left( \frac{\partial J}{\partial W} \right)\). This term measures how marginal utility of wealth changes when the state variable shifts:

  • If \(S\) represents an improvement in investment opportunities (e.g. higher future Sharpe ratio), then for an investor with \(\gamma > 1\), the income effect dominates the substitution effect, making marginal utility lower when future returns are high: \(J_{WS} < 0\).
  • If the risky asset is negatively correlated with the state variable (\(\rho < 0\)), a drop in stock prices coincides with a rise in future expected returns (\(S\) increases). The stock naturally pays off when future opportunities deteriorate, making equities a hedge!
  • The coefficient \(-\frac{J_{WS}}{W J_{WW}} \frac{\rho \sigma_S}{\sigma}\) is strictly positive, expanding equity demand beyond the static myopic Merton fraction.
Theorem 2.2 (The Log Utility Hedging Neutrality Theorem)

If the investor has logarithmic utility (\(\gamma = 1\)), the intertemporal hedging demand is identically zero regardless of the correlation \(\rho\), the volatility of the state variable \(\sigma_S\), or the persistence of the investment opportunity set:

\[ \gamma = 1 \implies \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}}(W, S, t) \equiv 0, \quad \forall t \le T. \]
Proof of Theorem 2.2

For log utility, the value function is strictly additively separable in wealth and state variables (Merton 1971):

\[ J(W, S, t) = f(t) \ln W + g(S, t). \]

Differentiating with respect to wealth: \(J_W = \frac{f(t)}{W}\). Differentiating subsequently with respect to the state variable \(S\):

\[ J_{WS} = \frac{\partial}{\partial S} \left( \frac{f(t)}{W} \right) \equiv 0. \]

Because \(J_{WS} \equiv 0\), the hedging weight satisfies:

\[ \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}} = -\frac{J_{WS}}{W J_{WW}} \frac{\rho \sigma_S}{\sigma} = -\frac{0}{W J_{WW}} \frac{\rho \sigma_S}{\sigma} = 0. \]

For log utility, the income and substitution effects cancel exactly. The myopic policy is globally dynamic optimal.

Lab 1 · Merton Dynamic Decomposition & Hedging Demands

Computing Merton myopic vs intertemporal hedging demand across correlation rho in {-0.75, -0.40, 0.0, 0.40, 0.75} and gamma in {1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0} will prove that hedging demand is strictly zero for gamma=1 and for rho=0, and reaches 2.18 for gamma=2 and 3.93 for gamma=10 when rho=-0.75.

"""Lab PR6-L02-1: Merton (1971, 1973) Intertemporal Hedging Demand Decomposition.

SIMULATED: Analytical decomposition and numerical verification of myopic vs
intertemporal hedging demands across state correlation and risk aversion.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def merton_hedging_decomposition(
    rhos: list[float],
    gammas: list[float],
    excess_return: float = 0.06,
    sigma: float = 0.20,
    sigma_s: float = 0.15,
    kappa: float = 0.15,
    lambda_1: float = 0.50,
    horizon: float = 10.0,
) -> dict[tuple[float, float], dict[str, float]]:
    """Compute Merton myopic and intertemporal hedging portfolio demands.

    State variable S_t governs expected excess return: mu(S) - r = lambda_0 + lambda_1 * S.
    Value function J(W, S, t) = (W^(1-gamma) / (1-gamma)) * exp(B(tau)*S + A(tau)).
    In the linear approximation (Campbell & Viceira 2002, Kim & Omberg 1996),
    B(tau) = lambda_1 * (1 - exp(-kappa_star * tau)) / kappa_star where kappa_star adjusts for risk.
    Hedging demand is alpha_hedge = (rho * sigma_s / (gamma * sigma)) * B(tau) * (1 - gamma) / gamma...
    Specifically, the hedging weight is:
        alpha_hedge = ((1 - 1/gamma) * (rho * sigma_s / sigma) * (1 - exp(-kappa * tau)) / kappa) * (lambda_1 / sigma).
    """
    results: dict[tuple[float, float], dict[str, float]] = {}

    for gamma in gammas:
        myopic = excess_return / (gamma * sigma**2)
        for rho in rhos:
            if gamma == 1.0 or abs(rho) < 1e-12:
                # Log utility has identically zero hedging demand, as does uncorrelated state
                hedging = 0.0
            else:
                # Effective persistence factor for long horizon tau
                # When rho < 0 and lambda_1 > 0, bad return shocks correlate with improved future returns
                # Campbell & Viceira (2002) closed-form approximation:
                integral_factor = (1.0 - np.exp(-kappa * horizon)) / kappa
                hedging = (1.0 - 1.0 / gamma) * (-rho * sigma_s / sigma) * integral_factor * (excess_return / sigma**2)

            total = myopic + hedging
            hedge_pct = (hedging / total * 100.0) if abs(total) > 1e-8 else 0.0

            results[(gamma, rho)] = {
                "myopic": myopic,
                "hedging": hedging,
                "total": total,
                "hedge_pct": hedge_pct,
            }

    return results


def main() -> None:
    excess_return = 0.06
    sigma = 0.20
    sigma_s = 0.15
    kappa = 0.15
    horizon = 10.0
    gammas = [1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0]
    rhos = [-0.75, -0.40, 0.00, 0.40, 0.75]

    res = merton_hedging_decomposition(
        rhos=rhos,
        gammas=gammas,
        excess_return=excess_return,
        sigma=sigma,
        sigma_s=sigma_s,
        kappa=kappa,
        horizon=horizon,
    )

    print("Merton (1971, 1973) Intertemporal Hedging Demand Decomposition:")
    print(
        f"Parameters: Excess Ret={excess_return:.2f}, Asset Vol={sigma:.2f}, "
        f"State Vol={sigma_s:.2f}, Horizon={horizon:.0f}y"
    )
    print("=" * 86)
    print(
        f"{'Risk Aversion (gamma)':<24} {'Correlation (rho)':<19} {'Myopic Demand':<15} "
        f"{'Hedging Demand':<16} {'Total Allocation':<16}"
    )
    print("-" * 86)

    for gamma in gammas:
        for rho in rhos:
            d = res[(gamma, rho)]
            print(
                f"gamma={gamma:<18.1f} rho={rho:<15.2f} {d['myopic']:<15.4f} {d['hedging']:<16.4f} {d['total']:<16.4f}"
            )
        print("-" * 86)

    print("Key Theoretical Properties:")
    print("1. Log Utility (gamma=1): Hedging demand is strictly zero regardless of correlation rho.")
    print("2. Orthogonal State (rho=0): Hedging demand is strictly zero across all risk aversions.")
    print("3. Negative Correlation (rho<0): Investors with gamma > 1 exhibit substantial positive hedging demand.")

Where the Canon Breaks

While Merton's continuous-time decomposition is an intellectual triumph, practical implementation encounters severe institutional boundaries:

  • Unobservable State Variables: In practice, the state variable \(S_t\) (such as the latent conditional equity risk premium or expected inflation) is unobservable. Investors must infer \(S_t\) via Kalman filtering or Bayesian state-space estimation. Learning introduces estimation risk that dampens aggressive hedging demands.
  • Parameter Uncertainty and Stambaugh Bias: As shown by Stambaugh (1999), predictive regressions feature persistent regressors with correlated innovations, biasing OLS predictive coefficients upward. Relying on biased parameters exaggerates hedging demands.
  • Leverage and Liquidity Constraints: Merton's unconstrained formula frequently mandates leverage (\(\alpha^* > 2.0\) or \(\alpha^* < 0\)). Real-world institutional investors face margin constraints, regulatory caps, and redemption liquidity limits that preclude continuous rebalancing.

Misconceptions

  1. "An investor with log utility adjusts their portfolio to hedge against future drops in expected return."

    Log utility investors never hedge against shifts in the investment opportunity set. Because \(\gamma = 1\), the income effect (lower future returns make the investor poorer) exactly cancels the substitution effect (lower future returns make saving less attractive). Marginal utility of wealth is completely independent of future investment opportunities (\(J_{WS} \equiv 0\)), rendering hedging demand strictly zero.

  2. "Intertemporal hedging demand is always positive for risk-averse investors."

    Hedging demand is proportional to \(-\rho \sigma_S / \sigma\). If return innovations are positively correlated with state variable improvements (\(\rho > 0\)), bad stock returns coincide with worsening investment opportunities. In this regime, the stock amplifies state risk rather than hedging it, generating negative hedging demand that reduces optimal equity holdings below the myopic level.

  3. "Merton's hedging demand requires continuous option replication."

    Intertemporal hedging does not require derivative instruments. It is achieved entirely by tilting the physical allocation between existing cash, bonds, and equities. Because equities correlate with macroeconomic state variables, the underlying stock portfolio naturally hedges long-term consumption opportunities.

Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

An investor has CRRA risk aversion \(\gamma = 4.0\). The stock has expected excess return \(\mu - r = 0.05\) and volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\). Compute the myopic Merton fraction \(\alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}}\).

Solution

The static myopic Merton fraction is:

\[ \alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}} = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2} = \frac{0.05}{4.0 \times 0.20^2} = \frac{0.05}{4.0 \times 0.04} = \frac{0.05}{0.16} = 0.3125. \]

The myopic demand is exactly 31.25% of wealth allocated to equities.

Using the parameters from Problem M1, suppose the state variable has volatility \(\sigma_S = 0.12\), correlation with equities \(\rho = -0.70\), and the value function elasticity satisfies \(-\frac{J_{WS}}{W J_{WW}} = 0.80\). Compute the intertemporal hedging demand \(\alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}}\) and the total optimal allocation \(\alpha^*\).

Solution

The intertemporal hedging demand is:

\[ \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}} = \left( -\frac{J_{WS}}{W J_{WW}} \right) \frac{-\rho \sigma_S}{\sigma} = 0.80 \times \frac{-(-0.70) \times 0.12}{0.20} = 0.80 \times \frac{0.084}{0.20} = 0.80 \times 0.4200 = 0.3360. \]

The total optimal allocation is \(\alpha^* = \alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}} = 0.3125 + 0.3360 = 0.6485\) (64.85%). Hedging demand exceeds myopic demand, more than doubling total equity allocation.

Suppose state correlation switches to \(\rho = +0.50\) with all other parameters from Problem M2 unchanged. Calculate the new hedging demand and total portfolio weight.

Solution

With \(\rho = +0.50\):

\[ \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}} = 0.80 \times \frac{-(0.50) \times 0.12}{0.20} = 0.80 \times (-0.3000) = -0.2400. \]

Total optimal equity allocation is \(\alpha^* = 0.3125 - 0.2400 = 0.0725\) (7.25%). Positive correlation transforms equities into an amplifier of state risk, slashing equity demand by 76.8%.

Proof Tier

Prove that if the investment opportunity set is constant (\(\mu_S \equiv 0, \sigma_S \equiv 0\)), the HJB PDE reduces to the standard Merton (1969) ODE, and derive the optimal consumption-to-wealth ratio \(C^* / W\).

Solution

When \(\sigma_S = 0\), all state variable derivatives (\(J_S, J_{SS}, J_{WS}\)) vanish identically. The HJB equation reduces to:

\[ 0 = \sup_{\alpha, C} \left\{ \frac{C^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} - \beta J + J_t + J_W \left[ W(r + \alpha(\mu - r)) - C \right] + \frac{1}{2} J_{WW} W^2 \alpha^2 \sigma^2 \right\}. \]

The FOC for consumption is \(C^{-\gamma} = J_W \implies C^* = (J_W)^{-1/\gamma}\). The FOC for \(\alpha\) yields \(\alpha^* = -\frac{J_W}{W J_{WW}} \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\).

Conjecturing the separable form \(J(W, t) = f(t)^\gamma \frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\), we have \(J_W = f(t)^\gamma W^{-\gamma}\), which implies \(C^* = (f(t)^\gamma W^{-\gamma})^{-1/\gamma} = \frac{W}{f(t)}\). Thus, the optimal consumption-wealth ratio is \(C^* / W = 1 / f(t)\). Substituting into the HJB PDE yields an ordinary differential equation for \(f(t)\), verifying the Merton (1969) solution.

Prove that the value function \(J(W, S, t)\) under CRRA utility is strictly homogeneous of degree \(1-\gamma\) in wealth: \(J(k W, S, t) = k^{1-\gamma} J(W, S, t)\) for any scalar \(k > 0\).

Solution

Consider the scaling of wealth by factor \(k > 0\). Wealth dynamics satisfy:

\[ d(k W_t) = \left[ k W_t (r + \alpha(\mu - r)) - k C_t \right] dt + k W_t \alpha \sigma dZ_{P,t}. \]

Under the transformed control policy \(\tilde{C}_t = k C_t\) and identical portfolio weight \(\alpha_t\), the wealth trajectory is exactly \(\tilde{W}_t = k W_t\) for all \(t \in [0, T]\). The objective function under CRRA utility satisfies:

\[ \E \left[ \int_0^T e^{-\beta t} \frac{(k C_t)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} dt + e^{-\beta T} \frac{(k W_T)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} \right] = k^{1-\gamma} \E \left[ \int_0^T e^{-\beta t} \frac{C_t^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} dt + e^{-\beta T} \frac{W_T^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} \right]. \]

Taking the supremum over admissible policies on both sides proves that \(J(k W, S, t) = k^{1-\gamma} J(W, S, t)\). Homogeneity of degree \(1-\gamma\) guarantees that relative risk aversion \(-W J_{WW} / J_W \equiv \gamma\) is independent of wealth level.

Prove that for an investor with \(\gamma > 1\), the value function derivative with respect to expected return \(S\) is positive (\(J_S > 0\)), but the cross-derivative \(J_{WS}\) is negative.

Solution

Let \(S\) denote the expected return \(\mu(S)\) where higher \(S\) strictly expands the investment opportunity set. By the envelope theorem, the derivative of the maximum with respect to a parameter is the expectation of the parameter's marginal impact:

\[ J_S(W, S, t) = \E_t \left[ \int_t^T e^{-\beta(s-t)} J_W(W_s, S_s, s) W_s \alpha_s^* ds \right] > 0, \]

since \(J_W > 0, W_s > 0\), and \(\alpha_s^* > 0\). Thus, higher investment opportunities increase total expected utility (\(J_S > 0\)).

Now express the value function in separable form: \(J(W, S, t) = \frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} g(S, t)\). Because \(\gamma > 1\), \(\frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} < 0\). For \(J\) to be increasing in \(S\) (\(J_S > 0\)), the function \(g(S, t)\) must be negative and decreasing in \(S\), i.e., \(g_S(S, t) < 0\). Differentiating with respect to wealth: \(J_W = W^{-\gamma} g(S, t)\). Differentiating subsequently with respect to \(S\):

\[ J_{WS} = W^{-\gamma} g_S(S, t) < 0. \]

Because \(g_S < 0\), \(J_{WS}\) is strictly negative. Higher future opportunities lower the marginal utility of current wealth.

Empirical Tier

Campbell & Viceira (2002) estimate the correlation between U.S. stock return innovations and dividend-yield innovations to be \(\rho \approx -0.75\). Explain why this strong negative correlation is an empirical regularity of equity markets.

Solution

By the Campbell-Shiller (1988) log-linear present value identity, the log dividend-price ratio satisfies:

\[ d_t - p_t = \frac{k}{1-\rho_d} + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho_d^{j-1} \E_t [r_{t+j}] - \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho_d^{j-1} \E_t [\Delta d_{t+j}]. \]

When stock prices drop sharply (\(p_t \ll p_{t-1}\)), the price is in the denominator of the dividend-price ratio. Because dividend payments are sticky and smooth in the short run, an unexpected negative return shock mechanically spikes the dividend-price ratio \(d_t - p_t\). Thus, price innovations and dividend-yield innovations are negatively correlated by algebraic construction (\(\rho \approx -0.75\) to \(-0.85\)), making equities a powerful intertemporal hedge against discount-rate shocks.

In Lab 1, an investor with \(\gamma = 5.0\) facing \(\rho = -0.75\) exhibited a myopic demand of 0.3000 and a hedging demand of 3.4959, yielding an unconstrained allocation of 3.7959. Discuss why institutional mandates rarely permit 380% equity leverage.

Solution

The unconstrained allocation \(\alpha^* = 3.7959\) (379.6% equity) is unviable for institutional asset managers due to four structural constraints:

  1. Margin Calls and Pathwise Ruin: Unconstrained continuous-time models assume costless, friction-free borrowing. At 3.8x leverage, a 26.3% market crash triggers total liquidation and negative net worth.
  2. Borrowing Cost Premium: Retail and institutional investors borrow at rates significantly above \(R_f\) (margin rates, repo haircuts), which severely flattens the leveraged Sharpe ratio.
  3. Model and Parameter Uncertainty: The 3.50 hedging demand rests on point estimates of persistence \(\kappa\) and predictive slope \(\lambda_1\). Estimating these with standard errors implies that aggressive leverage concentrates fatal parameter estimation risk.

Suppose an econometrician estimates a predictive regression with monthly data and finds \(\hat{\beta} = 0.04\) with \(t\)-statistic 2.10. Explain how Stambaugh (1999) bias impacts the estimated hedging demand.

Solution

In the predictive system \(r_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta x_t + \varepsilon_{t+1}\) and \(x_{t+1} = \mu + \phi x_t + \eta_{t+1}\), Stambaugh (1999) showed that the OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}\) has finite-sample bias \(\E[\hat{\beta} - \beta] = \frac{\Cov(\varepsilon, \eta)}{\Var(\eta)} \E[\hat{\phi} - \phi]\). Because \(\phi \approx 0.95\) is persistent, the Kendall autoregressive bias \(\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi] < 0\) is negative. Combined with \(\Cov(\varepsilon, \eta) < 0\), the product is strictly positive: \(\E[\hat{\beta}] > \beta\).

The estimated predictability is artificially inflated. An investor using \(\hat{\beta}\) overestimates future return sensitivity to \(x_t\), calculating an exaggerated intertemporal hedging demand that overweights risky assets and exposes the portfolio to unhedged drawdowns.

Research Tier

Kim & Omberg (1996) solve the Merton dynamic portfolio problem in closed form with an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Sharpe ratio process. Explain the conditions under which the value function solution exhibits 'solvency bubbles' or non-existence of classical solutions.

Solution

Kim & Omberg (1996) analyze the system of Riccati ODEs governing the quadratic exponential value function \(J(W, S, t) = \frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} \exp(A(\tau) + B(\tau)S + \frac{1}{2} C(\tau) S^2)\). The ODE for \(C(\tau)\) is of matrix Riccati type:

\[ C'(\tau) = a_0 + a_1 C(\tau) + a_2 C(\tau)^2. \]

Non-existence occurs when the discriminant \(\Delta = a_1^2 - 4 a_0 a_2 < 0\) or when the solution diverges to \(+\infty\) at a finite horizon \(\tau^* < \infty\) (a 'solvency bubble' or blow-up). In economic terms, when \(\gamma < 1\) (low risk aversion) and the Sharpe ratio has high volatility and persistence, the investor takes such massive leveraged bets on high-Sharpe opportunities that expected terminal utility becomes infinite in finite time. A classical verification theorem fails, requiring stochastic control regularization through borrowing constraints.

How does Merton's continuous-time intertemporal CAPM (ICAPM) relate the cross-section of expected returns to macroeconomic state variable hedging demands?

Solution

In Merton's (1973) ICAPM, market clearing requires that aggregate investor demands equal total asset supplies. Because investors hold both myopic demand and state-variable hedging demands, the equilibrium expected excess return on any asset \(i\) satisfies a multi-factor pricing equation:

\[ \E[R_i] - R_f = \beta_{i, M} \lambda_M + \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_{i, S_k} \lambda_{S_k}, \]

where \(\lambda_M\) is the market risk premium and \(\lambda_{S_k}\) is the price of risk for state variable \(S_k\). Assets that provide insurance against adverse shifts in the investment opportunity set (positive covariance with state variables that increase marginal utility) command lower expected returns (negative risk premiums) because investors bid up their prices to satisfy intertemporal hedging demands.

Explain how ambiguity aversion (robust control following Hansen & Sargent) alters Merton's intertemporal hedging demand when the drift of the state variable is subject to model misspecification.

Solution

Under robust control, the investor fears that the true state drift \(\mu_S\) differs from the reference model by an adversarial distortion \(v_t\) subject to an entropy penalty \(\theta\). The investor solves a max-min game: \(\sup_\alpha \inf_v \{ \dots \}\). The worst-case drift distortion is proportional to the state-derivative of the value function: \(v_t^* \propto -J_S / \theta\).

Because the agent considers the worst-case scenario where investment opportunities deteriorate more rapidly, the effective persistence of good opportunity states is downgraded. This shrinks the state derivative \(J_S\) and dampens the cross-derivative \(J_{WS}\). Ambiguity aversion acts as an endogenous penalty on predictability, shrinking the aggressive Merton hedging demand toward the conservative static allocation.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What are the two additive components of Merton's optimal portfolio demand in continuous time?

    Options

    Merton (1971, 1973) proved that dynamic demand separates into static single-period myopic demand and intertemporal hedging demand against state variable risk.

  2. Why is intertemporal hedging demand strictly zero for an investor with log utility (\(\gamma = 1\))?

    Options

    Under log utility, \(J(W, S, t) = f(t)\ln W + g(S, t)\), so \(J_W = f(t)/W\) has zero derivative with respect to \(S\) (\(J_{WS} \equiv 0\)).

  3. What is the myopic Merton weight \(\alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}} = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\) for an investor with \(\gamma = 2.0\), expected excess return \(\mu - r = 0.04\), and volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\)?

    \(\alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}} = \frac{0.04}{2.0 \times 0.04} = 0.5000\).

  4. What is the theoretical intertemporal hedging demand \(\alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}}\) for any investor when the state variable innovation is perfectly uncorrelated with stock returns (\(\rho = 0\))?

    Because hedging demand is proportional to \(\rho \sigma_S / \sigma\), \(\rho = 0\) implies \(\alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}} = 0.0000\).

  5. Under what correlation condition does return predictability generate positive hedging demand for risk-averse investors (\(\gamma > 1\))?

    Options

    When \(\rho < 0\), negative stock returns coincide with increases in future investment opportunities, so stocks provide a natural hedge against unfavorable shifts in marginal utility.

  6. How does Merton's Intertemporal CAPM (ICAPM) explain multi-factor risk premiums?

    Options

    In ICAPM, market equilibrium prices state-variable covariance: investors accept lower expected returns on hedging assets and require higher expected returns on assets exposed to state risk.

  7. What econometric bias in predictive regressions leads investors to overestimate intertemporal hedging demand?

    Options

    Stambaugh (1999) proved that persistent predictors with correlated innovations induce positive finite-sample bias in the predictive slope \(\hat{\beta}\), exaggerating apparent predictability.

  8. For an investor with log utility (\(\gamma = 1\)), risk-free rate \(r = 0.02\), expected return \(\mu = 0.08\), and volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), what is the total optimal equity allocation \(\alpha^* = \alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}}\)?

    \(\alpha_{\mathrm{myopic}} = \frac{0.08 - 0.02}{1.0 \times 0.04} = 1.5000\), and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}} = 0\), so \(\alpha^* = 1.5000\).
