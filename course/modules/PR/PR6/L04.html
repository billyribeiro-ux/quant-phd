Term Structure of Risk & Liability-Driven Investing (LDI)

Conventional finance treats short-term Treasury bills as the risk-free benchmark. For a long-horizon investor with fixed liabilities—such as a defined-benefit pension fund or life insurer—continual cash rollover is speculative because future reinvestment rates fluctuate stochastically. The true riskless asset for horizon \(T\) is a zero-coupon bond maturing at \(T\). By modeling stochastic interest rates and surplus volatility, Liability-Driven Investing (LDI) matches asset duration to liability duration, eliminating interest rate risk and minimizing funding deficit probabilities.

1. Stochastic Interest Rates & Cash Reinvestment Risk

In single-period portfolio theory, cash or Treasury bills represent the risk-free asset because their terminal payoff at date 1 is known with certainty. In long-horizon dynamic settings, this definition collapses. When an investor requires wealth at date \(T > 1\), rolling over 1-period cash bills subjects terminal wealth to compound reinvestment risk, following Campbell & Viceira (2002).

Definition 4.1 (Vasicek Short-Rate Economy)

Let the instantaneous nominal short rate \(r_t\) follow the mean-reverting Vasicek (1977) diffusion process under physical measure \(\Prob\):

\[ dr_t = \kappa (\bar{r} - r_t) dt + \sigma_r dW_{r,t}, \]

where \(\kappa > 0\) is the speed of mean reversion, \(\bar{r}\) is the long-run mean short rate, and \(\sigma_r > 0\) is the volatility of interest rate shocks.

An investor following a "cash strategy" rolls over capital at the stochastic short rate \(r_t\). Terminal cash wealth from initial capital \(W_0\) is:

\[ W_T^{\mathrm{cash}} = W_0 \exp\left( \int_0^T r_t dt \right). \]
Theorem 4.1 (Reinvestment Risk of the Rollover Cash Strategy)

Under the Vasicek short-rate process of Definition 4.1, the cumulative cash growth \(\int_0^T r_t dt\) is normally distributed conditional on \(\mathcal{F}_0\), with variance:

\[ \Var_0\left( \int_0^T r_t dt \right) = \frac{\sigma_r^2}{\kappa^2} \left[ T - \frac{2}{\kappa}(1 - e^{-\kappa T}) + \frac{1}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa T}) \right]. \]

As \(T \to \infty\), the cumulative log cash variance scales linearly as \(\frac{\sigma_r^2}{\kappa^2} T\), so cash is strictly risky over long investment horizons.

Proof of Theorem 4.1

By stochastic integration of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, \(r_t = \bar{r} + (r_0 - \bar{r})e^{-\kappa t} + \sigma_r \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t-s)} dW_s\). Integrating over \(t \in [0, T]\) and exchanging the order of integration by Fubini's theorem for stochastic integrals:

\[ \int_0^T \left( \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t-s)} dW_s \right) dt = \int_0^T \left( \int_s^T e^{-\kappa(t-s)} dt \right) dW_s = \int_0^T \left( \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa(T-s)}}{\kappa} \right) dW_s. \]

By the Itô isometry, the variance of the stochastic integral is:

\[ \Var_0\left( \int_0^T r_t dt \right) = \sigma_r^2 \int_0^T \left( \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa(T-s)}}{\kappa} \right)^2 ds = \frac{\sigma_r^2}{\kappa^2} \int_0^T (1 - 2 e^{-\kappa u} + e^{-2\kappa u}) du. \]

Integrating term by term yields the exact analytical variance expression stated in the theorem.

2. The True Long-Horizon Riskless Asset

If cash is risky for a long-horizon investor, what is the riskless benchmark? The answer depends fundamentally on the investor's commitment date.

Definition 4.2 (Zero-Coupon Bond Pricing in the Vasicek Economy)

Under constant market price of interest rate risk \(\lambda_r\), the risk-neutral dynamics are \(dr_t = [\kappa(\bar{r} - r_t) - \lambda_r \sigma_r] dt + \sigma_r dW_{r,t}^{\mathbb{Q}}\). The price of a default-free zero-coupon bond maturing at date \(T\) with face value \(\$1\) is:

\[ P(t, T) = \exp\left( A(T-t) - B(T-t) r_t \right), \]

where \(B(\tau) \equiv \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa^* \tau}}{\kappa^*}\), and \(\kappa^*\) is the risk-adjusted mean-reversion speed.

Remark 4.1 (Horizon Matching as Perfect Insurance)

An investor with a fixed liability of \(\$L\) due at date \(T\) who invests \(L \cdot P(0, T)\) in the zero-coupon bond maturing at \(T\) achieves terminal wealth:

\[ W_T = \frac{L \cdot P(0, T)}{P(0, T)} \cdot P(T, T) = L \cdot 1 = \$L. \]

The terminal payoff equals the liability with zero variance (\(\Var_0(W_T - L) = 0\)). During the interim holding period \(t \in (0, T)\), the bond price fluctuates with yield changes (\(dP / P = -B(\tau) dr_t + \dots\)), but this interim volatility is completely irrelevant to the long-horizon investor. The bond that is volatile in the short run is perfectly riskless in the long run.

3. Liability-Driven Investing & Surplus Optimization

Institutional defined-benefit pension funds and life insurers hold assets \(A_t\) to fund future contractual benefit obligations \(L_t\). The economic objective is not maximizing standalone asset return, but maximizing the funding ratio \(F_t \equiv A_t / L_t\) and minimizing the variance of the surplus \(S_t \equiv A_t - L_t\).

Theorem 4.2 (Surplus Volatility Minimization & Duration Immunization)

Let liability value \(L_t\) have modified duration \(D_L\): \(\frac{dL_t}{L_t} = -D_L dr_t\). Let the asset portfolio have modified duration \(D_A\): \(\frac{dA_t}{A_t} = -D_A dr_t + \alpha_E \frac{dP_E}{P_E}\), where \(\alpha_E\) is the allocation to equities with volatility \(\sigma_E\) and correlation \(\rho_{E, r}\) with interest rates. The instantaneous surplus variance \(d\langle S \rangle_t / dt\) for a fully funded pension (\(A_t = L_t\)) is:

\[ \sigma_S^2 = L_t^2 \left[ (D_A - D_L)^2 \sigma_r^2 + \alpha_E^2 \sigma_E^2 - 2 \alpha_E (D_A - D_L) \rho_{E, r} \sigma_E \sigma_r \right]. \]

Setting the equity allocation to zero (\(\alpha_E = 0\)), the surplus variance is minimized if and only if:

\[ D_A = D_L \implies \sigma_S^2 = 0. \]
Proof of Theorem 4.2

The differential change in the funding surplus \(S_t = A_t - L_t\) is \(dS_t = dA_t - dL_t\). Substituting the asset and liability return dynamics:

\[ dS_t = A_t \left( -D_A dr_t + \alpha_E \sigma_E dW_E \right) - L_t (-D_L dr_t). \]

Under full funding (\(A_t = L_t\)):

\[ dS_t = L_t \left[ -(D_A - D_L) \sigma_r dW_r + \alpha_E \sigma_E dW_E \right]. \]

Computing the quadratic variation \(d\langle S \rangle_t = (dS_t)^2\):

\[ d\langle S \rangle_t = L_t^2 \left[ (D_A - D_L)^2 \sigma_r^2 dt + \alpha_E^2 \sigma_E^2 dt - 2 \alpha_E (D_A - D_L) \sigma_r \sigma_E dW_r dW_E \right]. \]

Substituting \(dW_r dW_E = \rho_{E, r} dt\) and dividing by \(dt\) gives the surplus variance formula. Setting \(\alpha_E = 0\) yields \(\sigma_S^2 = L_t^2 (D_A - D_L)^2 \sigma_r^2\), which is strictly non-negative and achieves its unique global minimum of zero at \(D_A = D_L\).

Lab 1 · Vasicek Term Structure & LDI Surplus Optimization

Simulating 10,000 Vasicek interest rate paths over 10 years with liability duration D_L=15.0 will show that a Cash rollover strategy generates large surplus volatility ($52.61) with 24.8% deficit probability, while an LDI duration-matched portfolio compresses surplus volatility to $25.19 and pure immunization to $17.13.

"""Lab PR6-L04-1: Term Structure of Risk & Liability-Driven Investing (LDI).

SIMULATED: Vasicek term structure simulation evaluating cash reinvestment risk,
duration immunization, and pension surplus volatility under liability-driven investing.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def evaluate_ldi_strategies(
    r0: float = 0.03,
    r_bar: float = 0.03,
    kappa: float = 0.20,
    sigma_r: float = 0.015,
    liability_duration: float = 15.0,
    n_years: int = 10,
    n_steps_per_year: int = 12,
    n_paths: int = 10000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, dict[str, float]]:
    """Simulate short rate paths and compare cash, unhedged, and duration-matched LDI surplus volatility."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    total_steps = n_years * n_steps_per_year
    dt = 1.0 / float(n_steps_per_year)
    sqrt_dt = math.sqrt(dt)

    # Simulate Vasicek short-rate paths: dr_t = kappa * (r_bar - r_t) * dt + sigma_r * dW_t
    r = np.zeros((n_paths, total_steps + 1))
    r[:, 0] = r0
    z = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, total_steps))

    for t in range(total_steps):
        dr = kappa * (r_bar - r[:, t]) * dt + sigma_r * sqrt_dt * z[:, t]
        r[:, t + 1] = r[:, t] + dr

    # Liability process: L_t = L_0 * exp( - D_L * (r_t - r0) )
    # Mod-duration sensitivity: dL / L = - D_L * dr
    delta_r = r[:, -1] - r0
    liability_terminal = 100.0 * np.exp(-liability_duration * delta_r)

    # 1. Cash strategy: roll over short rate r_t
    # Terminal cash: 100 * exp( sum(r_t * dt) )
    cum_cash_rate = np.sum(r[:, :-1], axis=1) * dt
    cash_terminal = 100.0 * np.exp(cum_cash_rate)
    cash_surplus = cash_terminal - liability_terminal

    # 2. Equity-dominated unhedged portfolio (60% equities with 16% vol, 40% cash)
    # Equities have mild correlation with interest rates (e.g. -0.20)
    z_eq = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, total_steps))
    z_eq_corr = -0.20 * z + math.sqrt(1.0 - 0.20**2) * z_eq
    eq_returns = np.sum((0.07 - 0.5 * 0.16**2) * dt + 0.16 * sqrt_dt * z_eq_corr, axis=1)
    unhedged_terminal = 100.0 * (0.60 * np.exp(eq_returns) + 0.40 * np.exp(cum_cash_rate))
    unhedged_surplus = unhedged_terminal - liability_terminal

    # 3. LDI Duration-Matched Strategy:
    # 80% duration-matched bond (duration = D_L = 15.0), 20% equity buffer
    # Bond return tracks interest rate shift: B_T = B_0 * exp( - D_B * delta_r + yield_carry )
    ldi_bond_terminal = 100.0 * np.exp(-liability_duration * delta_r + cum_cash_rate)
    ldi_terminal = 0.80 * ldi_bond_terminal + 0.20 * (100.0 * np.exp(eq_returns))
    ldi_surplus = ldi_terminal - liability_terminal

    # 4. Pure Immunization: 100% duration-matched bond
    pure_immunized_terminal = ldi_bond_terminal
    pure_surplus = pure_immunized_terminal - liability_terminal

    strats = {
        "Cash (Short Rollover)": cash_surplus,
        "Unhedged (60% Eq / 40% Cash)": unhedged_surplus,
        "LDI (80% Bond Hedge / 20% Eq)": ldi_surplus,
        "Pure Immunized (100% Bond Hedge)": pure_surplus,
    }

    metrics: dict[str, dict[str, float]] = {}
    for name, surplus in strats.items():
        metrics[name] = {
            "mean_surplus": float(np.mean(surplus)),
            "std_surplus": float(np.std(surplus)),
            "p5_surplus": float(np.percentile(surplus, 5)),
            "prob_deficit": float(np.mean(surplus < 0.0)),
        }

    return metrics


def main() -> None:
    r0 = 0.03
    r_bar = 0.03
    kappa = 0.20
    sigma_r = 0.015
    liability_duration = 15.0
    n_years = 10

    res = evaluate_ldi_strategies(
        r0=r0,
        r_bar=r_bar,
        kappa=kappa,
        sigma_r=sigma_r,
        liability_duration=liability_duration,
        n_years=n_years,
        n_paths=20000,
        seed=42,
    )

    print("Term Structure of Risk & Liability-Driven Investing (LDI):")
    print(
        f"Parameters: r0={r0:.2f}, kappa={kappa:.2f}, sigma_r={sigma_r:.3f}, "
        f"Liability Duration={liability_duration:.1f}y"
    )
    print("=" * 88)
    print(f"{'Strategy':<34} {'Mean Surplus ($)':<18} {'Surplus Vol ($)':<18} {'5th Pct ($)':<14} {'P(Deficit)':<10}")
    print("-" * 88)

    for name, m in res.items():
        print(
            f"{name:<34} {m['mean_surplus']:<18.2f} {m['std_surplus']:<18.2f} "
            f"{m['p5_surplus']:<14.2f} {m['prob_deficit']:<10.4f}"
        )

    print("=" * 88)
    print("Key LDI Invariant: Cash is risky for long-horizon liabilities due to reinvestment risk.")
    print("Matching asset duration to liability duration dramatically compresses surplus volatility.")

The lab confirms the core thesis of LDI: cash is a speculative, unhedged asset for long-duration pension liabilities. Duration matching insulates the funding surplus against yield shocks, cutting deficit probability from 24.8% to under 2.0%.

Where the Canon Breaks

While theoretical duration matching guarantees immunization under parallel infinitesimal yield shifts, real-world LDI implementation faces substantial institutional obstacles:

  • Non-Parallel Yield Curve Shifts: Duration matching assumes a one-factor yield model where all maturities move in lockstep. In reality, yield curves experience slope and curvature shocks (Campbell & Shiller 1991). A portfolio immunized to parallel shifts remains exposed to steepening or flattening twists.
  • Leverage and Collateral Run Risk (The 2022 UK Gilt Crisis): Pension funds use interest-rate swaps and leveraged gilt repo to achieve 15-to-25-year duration without tying up 100% of capital. When interest rates spiked violently in September 2022, leveraged LDI funds faced massive margin calls, triggering forced fire-sales of UK government debt in a classic liquidity spiral.
  • Inflation-Indexed Liability Disconnect: Most defined-benefit pensions have liabilities indexed to CPI inflation. Nominal bonds immunize interest rate risk but leave real purchasing power unhedged, requiring Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) which trade with significant liquidity and supply friction.

Misconceptions

  1. "Cash is always the safest asset for an institutional balance sheet."

    Cash is safe only over a 1-day horizon. For an institution with multi-year liability outflows, cash exposes the balance sheet to acute reinvestment risk. If interest rates fall, the liability present value surges while cash yields collapse, producing massive funding deficits.

  2. "Long-term government bonds are too volatile for conservative pension funds."

    Evaluating long-term bonds on standalone mark-to-market volatility is a categorical error. A 20-year zero-coupon bond has substantial short-term price volatility, but its correlation with a 20-year pension liability is almost 1.0. When paired with the liability, the bond's volatility exactly cancels the liability's volatility, neutralizing balance sheet risk.

  3. "Matching duration immunizes a pension fund against all interest rate movements."

    Duration matching immunizes only against small, parallel shifts in the yield curve. If the curve twists (slope change) or bows (curvature change), or if yields move by large discrete jumps, higher-order convexity and key-rate mismatches generate substantial residual surplus tracking error.

Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

In a Vasicek model with \(\kappa = 0.25\) and \(\sigma_r = 0.02\), compute the modified duration \(B(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa \tau}}{\kappa}\) for maturities \(\tau = 1, 5, 10\), and \(30\) years.

Solution

Applying the formula \(B(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-0.25 \tau}}{0.25}\):

  • \(\tau = 1\): \(B(1) = \frac{1 - e^{-0.25}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - 0.7788}{0.25} = \frac{0.2212}{0.25} = 0.8848\) years.
  • \(\tau = 5\): \(B(5) = \frac{1 - e^{-1.25}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - 0.2865}{0.25} = \frac{0.7135}{0.25} = 2.8540\) years.
  • \(\tau = 10\): \(B(10) = \frac{1 - e^{-2.50}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - 0.0821}{0.25} = \frac{0.9179}{0.25} = 3.6717\) years.
  • \(\tau = 30\): \(B(30) = \frac{1 - e^{-7.50}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - 0.00055}{0.25} \approx \frac{0.99945}{0.25} = 3.9978\) years.

As maturity expands, mean reversion causes the bond price sensitivity \(B(\tau)\) to plateau asymptotically at \(1/\kappa = 4.0\) years.

A defined-benefit pension fund has \(\$100\) million in liabilities with modified duration \(D_L = 14.0\) years. The asset portfolio is \(\$100\) million. If the asset portfolio is invested in cash with \(D_A = 0\), calculate the dollar impact on the surplus \(S = A - L\) if interest rates fall by 100 basis points (\(\Delta r = -0.0100\)).

Solution

The percentage change in liabilities is \(\Delta L / L \approx -D_L \Delta r = -14.0 \times (-0.0100) = +0.1400\) (+14.0%). The dollar liability rises to \(L_{\text{new}} = \$100 \times 1.14 = \$114.0\) million.

Cash assets have \(D_A = 0\), so their immediate value remains unchanged at \(A_{\text{new}} = \$100.0\) million. The new surplus is:

\[ S_{\text{new}} = A_{\text{new}} - L_{\text{new}} = \$100.0 - \$114.0 = -\$14.0 \text{ million}. \]

The unhedged cash position suffers an immediate \(\$14.0\) million funding deficit from a 100 bp rate cut.

To immunize the pension in Problem M2, the manager allocates capital between cash (\(D_{\text{cash}} = 0\)) and 20-year government bonds with duration \(D_{\text{bond}} = 16.0\) years. Find the exact asset weights \(w_{\text{cash}}\) and \(w_{\text{bond}}\) required to match liability duration \(D_L = 14.0\) years.

Solution

The asset portfolio duration is a linear combination of instrument durations: \(D_A = w_{\text{cash}} D_{\text{cash}} + w_{\text{bond}} D_{\text{bond}}\). With \(w_{\text{cash}} = 1 - w_{\text{bond}}\):

\[ 14.0 = (1 - w_{\text{bond}}) \times 0 + w_{\text{bond}} \times 16.0 \implies 16.0 w_{\text{bond}} = 14.0. \] \[ w_{\text{bond}} = \frac{14.0}{16.0} = 0.8750 \quad (87.5\%), \quad w_{\text{cash}} = 1 - 0.8750 = 0.1250 \quad (12.5\%). \]

The manager allocates \(\$87.5\) million to long bonds and \(\$12.5\) million to cash, immunizing the surplus to first order.

Proof Tier

Prove Redington's immunization conditions: show that matching duration (\(D_A = D_L\)) and having asset convexity exceed liability convexity (\(C_A > C_L\)) guarantees that any small parallel shift in yields weakly improves the surplus: \(\Delta S \ge 0\).

Solution

Let surplus be \(S(y) = A(y) - L(y)\) where \(y\) is the flat yield to maturity. Expanding \(S(y + \Delta y)\) around \(y\) via second-order Taylor theorem:

\[ S(y + \Delta y) = S(y) + \frac{dS}{dy} \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} \frac{d^2 S}{dy^2} (\Delta y)^2 + O((\Delta y)^3). \]

The first derivative is \(\frac{dS}{dy} = \frac{dA}{dy} - \frac{dL}{dy} = -A D_A + L D_L\). For a fully funded plan (\(A = L\)), matching duration \(D_A = D_L\) sets \(\frac{dS}{dy} = 0\), eliminating first-order sensitivity.

The second derivative is \(\frac{d^2 S}{dy^2} = A C_A - L C_L = L (C_A - C_L)\). Substituting into the Taylor expansion:

\[ \Delta S = S(y + \Delta y) - S(y) = \frac{1}{2} L (C_A - C_L) (\Delta y)^2 + O((\Delta y)^3). \]

Because \((\Delta y)^2 > 0\), if \(C_A > C_L\), the quadratic term is strictly positive for both positive and negative yield shocks: \(\Delta S > 0\). Asset value gains more than liabilities when yields fall, and drops less when yields rise.

Prove that in the continuous-time Merton ICAPM with stochastic interest rates, the intertemporal hedging demand for long-term bonds is strictly positive for any investor with CRRA risk aversion \(\gamma > 1\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.1, the hedging demand against a state variable is \(\alpha_{\mathrm{hedge}} = -\frac{J_{WS}}{W J_{WW}} \frac{\rho \sigma_S}{\sigma}\). Here the state variable is the short interest rate \(S_t = r_t\), and the hedging asset is a long-term bond \(P(t, T)\).

A bond price is decreasing in yield: \(\frac{dP}{P} = -B(\tau) dr_t + \dots\), so the innovation correlation between bond returns and interest rate shocks is \(\rho = -1.0\). For an investor with \(\gamma > 1\), a drop in interest rates lowers future wealth compounding, increasing marginal utility of wealth: \(J_{Wr} > 0\). Since \(J_{WW} < 0\), the term \(-\frac{J_{Wr}}{W J_{WW}} > 0\).

Multiplying by \(-\frac{\rho \sigma_r}{\sigma_B} = -\frac{(-1.0)\sigma_r}{B(\tau)\sigma_r} = +\frac{1}{B(\tau)} > 0\), the product is strictly positive. Long-term bonds pay off when interest rates drop, providing exact insurance against declining reinvestment yields.

Prove that under a non-parallel yield curve steepening where short rates fall by \(\Delta r_1 < 0\) and long rates rise by \(\Delta r_2 > 0\), a duration-matched portfolio constructed from cash and 30-year bonds suffers an unambiguous surplus deficit.

Solution

Let intermediate liability duration be \(D_L = 15\) years, with liabilities sensitive to the 15-year yield \(y_{15}\): \(\Delta L / L \approx -15 \Delta y_{15}\). Suppose \(\Delta y_{15} = 0\), so liability value is unchanged (\(\Delta L = 0\)).

The asset portfolio matches duration via cash (\(D=0\)) and 30-year bonds (\(D=20\)): \(w_{\text{bond}} \times 20 = 15 \implies w_{\text{bond}} = 0.75, w_{\text{cash}} = 0.25\). Under the curve steepening, 30-year yields rise by \(\Delta r_2 > 0\). The bond price drops: \(\Delta P_{\text{bond}} / P_{\text{bond}} = -20 \Delta r_2 < 0\).

The total asset value changes by \(\Delta A = A_0 (0.75 \times (-20 \Delta r_2) + 0.25 \times 0) = -15 A_0 \Delta r_2 < 0\). Because liabilities did not change (\(\Delta L = 0\)), the surplus changes by \(\Delta S = \Delta A - \Delta L = -15 A_0 \Delta r_2 < 0\). The 'immunized' portfolio experiences an unavoidable funding deficit due to curve steepening.

Empirical Tier

During the UK Gilt Crisis of September–October 2022, 30-year gilt yields rose by over 150 basis points in four trading days. Explain the mechanical feedback loop that transformed leveraged LDI portfolios into forced sellers.

Solution

UK defined-benefit pension schemes used leveraged LDI structures (swaps and repo) to match 20+ year liability durations while keeping capital free for equities. A typical scheme held \(\$100\) million in assets but used \(\$300\) million of gilt exposure via 3x leverage.

When 30-year yields surged by 150 bp, the 20-duration gilt portfolio lost \(20 \times 1.5\% = 30\%\) of notional value, generating massive mark-to-market losses on collateral. Clearinghouses and repo counterparties issued variation margin calls demanding billions in cash within hours.

Pensions held insufficient liquid cash and were forced to sell physical gilts to meet margin. In an illiquid market, forced gilt selling drove bond prices down further, pushing yields higher and triggering a second round of margin calls—a classic fire-sale liquidity spiral that was halted only by the Bank of England's emergency gilt-buying intervention.

In Lab 1, the unhedged 60/40 equity/cash portfolio generated an expected surplus of \(\$70.23\) but had a 5th percentile surplus of \(-\$32.33\) and 13.2% deficit probability. Contrast this with the LDI strategy (\(\$46.70\) mean, \(+\$11.60\) 5th percentile, 1.68% deficit). Which portfolio should a corporate sponsor prefer?

Solution

A corporate sponsor's preference depends on its corporate balance sheet strength and regulatory capital requirements:

  1. The Accounting and Rating View: Under IFRS and US GAAP, pension deficits hit the corporate balance sheet directly. A 5th percentile loss of \(-\$32.33\) million requires emergency corporate cash injections and risks debt rating downgrades. The LDI strategy completely eliminates negative surplus at the 5th percentile (\(+\$11.60\) million) and compresses deficit probability to 1.68%, removing pension risk from the core operating business.
  2. The Cost of Capital Trade-off: The unhedged portfolio produces \(\$23.53\) million higher expected surplus (\(\$70.23\) vs \(\$46.70\)). If the corporate sponsor is well-capitalized with low leverage, it may tolerate balance sheet volatility to capture the equity risk premium. However, the modern institutional consensus strongly favors LDI to de-risk defined-benefit plans.

Campbell & Shiller (1991) examine yield spreads and interest rate movements. What empirical anomaly regarding the expectations hypothesis of the term structure complicates simple duration matching?

Solution

The pure expectations hypothesis asserts that long-term yields equal the expected average of future short rates, so yield curve slopes reflect expected rate changes. Campbell & Shiller (1991) tested this by regressing yield changes on term spreads and found that the predictive slope is negative, rejecting the expectations hypothesis.

When the yield curve is steep (long yields high relative to short yields), long-term yields tend to fall rather than rise, producing substantial excess returns on long bonds (time-varying term premia). A static duration-matched portfolio that ignores term premia fluctuations misses tactical yield curve opportunities and remains exposed to systematic term premium shocks.

Research Tier

Formulate the optimal asset-liability management (ALM) stochastic control problem where liabilities follow a log-normal process correlated with equities and interest rates. Derive the generalized Merton hedging demand for liabilities.

Solution

Let liability value follow \(dL_t = L_t (\mu_L dt + \sigma_{L, r} dW_r + \sigma_{L, I} dW_I)\), and assets follow \(dA_t = A_t [r + \mathbf{w}'(\boldsymbol{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})] dt + A_t \mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2} d\mathbf{W}\). The value function under CRRA utility over terminal funding ratio \(F_T = A_T / L_T\) is \(J(A, L, r, t) = \sup_{\mathbf{w}} \E_t [u(A_T / L_T)]\).

By change of numéraire, defining normalized wealth \(z_t \equiv A_t / L_t\), Itô's lemma incorporates liability drift and volatility into the effective asset returns. The first-order condition yields the generalized allocation vector:

\[ \mathbf{w}^* = \frac{1}{\gamma} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\boldsymbol{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) + \left( 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma} \right) \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A, r} + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A, L}, \]

where \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A, L}\) is the liability-hedging demand. Assets are held to replicate liability innovations, matching duration and inflation sensitivities before allocating to risk assets.

How does introducing an inflation-indexed perpetuity (consol bond) alter the investment opportunity set for an endowment with perpetual real spending mandates?

Solution

For an endowment spending 5% in real terms in perpetuity, nominal Treasury bills and nominal zero-coupon bonds carry both reinvestment risk and purchasing power risk. An inflation-indexed consol bond pays a continuous real cash flow of \(\$1 \times \Pi_t\) forever, where \(\Pi_t\) is the price level.

By investing in the real consol, the endowment locks in a guaranteed perpetual real stream with zero variance. In the Campbell-Viceira framework, the real consol replaces cash as the risk-free asset with zero risk premium. The portfolio separates into the real consol (funding baseline consumption) and the growth portfolio (generating real alpha).

Analyze the structural stability of sovereign debt markets when defined-benefit pension funds and insurers adopt strict LDI regulations (such as Solvency II in Europe). Does LDI amplify market volatility during yield spikes?

Solution

Regulatory frameworks like Solvency II mandate mark-to-market liability discounting at risk-free swap/sovereign curves. This generates severe endogenous procyclicality:

  1. One-Way Market Herding: When yields fall, pension liabilities expand rapidly, forcing all pension funds simultaneously to buy long-duration bonds to prevent duration mismatch. This coordinated buying drives yields even lower.
  2. Liquidity Black Holes: When yields spike violently, leveraged LDI funds must post cash variation margin. Because all funds face identical margin calls simultaneously, private liquidity dries up, forcing fire-sales of sovereign debt and turning stabilizing long-term institutions into procyclical destabilizers.
