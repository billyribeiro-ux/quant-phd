Kelly Sizing Under Estimation Error

Theoretical growth optimality presumes that the true investment edge is known with certainty. In practical quantitative finance, expected returns must be estimated from finite historical data. Kelly sizing under estimation error confronts a lethal structural asymmetry: underestimating edge merely slows compounding, whereas overestimating edge drives leverage beyond double-Kelly, causing negative compounding and asymptotic ruin. Plugging noisy sample means into the Kelly formula extracts an analytical expected growth penalty of \(\frac{1}{2T}\). To survive, practitioners must deploy optimal shrinkage, scaling plug-in leverage by \(\alpha^* = \frac{T \operatorname{SR}^2}{T \operatorname{SR}^2 + 1}\).

1. The Lethal Asymmetry of Kelly Misestimation

Recall the continuous geometric growth function for an asset with excess drift \(\mu^e = \mu - r\) and volatility \(\sigma\):

\[ g(f) = r + f \mu^e - \frac{1}{2} f^2 \sigma^2. \]

The true optimal leverage is \(f^* = \frac{\mu^e}{\sigma^2}\), achieving maximum excess compounding \(g_e(f^*) = \frac{1}{2}\frac{(\mu^e)^2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SR}^2\).

Theorem 4.1 (Asymmetric Sizing Penalty)

Let \(\Delta f = f - f^*\) be sizing error around true optimal leverage. The realized excess growth rate satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} g_e(f) &= g_e(f^*) \\ &\quad - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 (\Delta f)^2. \end{aligned} \]

Consequently, the penalty is asymmetric with respect to survival: for any \(\Delta f < 0\) with \(f \in (0, f^*)\), \(g_e(f) > 0\) remains strictly positive. Conversely, for \(\Delta f > f^*\) (i.e. \(f > 2 f^*\)), \(g_e(f) < 0\), ensuring almost sure asymptotic bankruptcy.

In classical portfolio optimization (Michaud 1989), estimation error causes inefficiency. In Kelly growth-optimal sizing, positive estimation errors produce overbetting, which is absorbing and fatal.

2. Sampling Degradation and the 1/(2T) Penalty

Suppose an investor estimates excess drift \(\mu^e\) from a historical sample of \(T\) independent observations \(R_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu^e, \sigma^2)\). The sample mean is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mu}^e &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T R_t \\ &\sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mu^e, \frac{\sigma^2}{T}\right). \end{aligned} \]
Definition 4.1 (Naive Plug-In Kelly Estimator)

Assuming known volatility \(\sigma\), the naive plug-in sample Kelly estimator is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{f} &\equiv \frac{\hat{\mu}^e}{\sigma^2} \\ &\sim \mathcal{N}\left(f^*, \frac{1}{T \sigma^2}\right). \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 4.2 (The 1/(2T) Growth Degradation Theorem)

Let an investor execute the naive sample Kelly leverage \(\hat{f}\). The expected realized continuous geometric growth rate, averaged over the sampling distribution of \(\hat{\mu}^e\), satisfies:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}}[g(\hat{f})] = g(f^*) - \frac{1}{2T}. \]
Proof of Theorem 4.2

Under true parameters \(\mu^e\) and \(\sigma\), the realized growth rate under leverage \(\hat{f}\) is:

\[ g(\hat{f}) = r + \hat{f} \mu^e - \frac{1}{2} \hat{f}^2 \sigma^2. \]

Taking expectation with respect to the sampling distribution of \(\hat{f}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}] &= f^*, \\ \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}^2] &= (f^*)^2 + \frac{1}{T \sigma^2}. \end{aligned} \]

Substituting these sampling moments into the expected growth expression:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[g(\hat{f})] &= r + \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}] \mu^e - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}^2] \\ &= r + f^* \mu^e \\ &\quad - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \left( (f^*)^2 + \frac{1}{T \sigma^2} \right) \\ &= g(f^*) - \frac{1}{2T}. \end{aligned} \]

The variance of the sample mean extracts a constant penalty of \(\frac{1}{2T}\) per unit time.

Theorem 4.3 (Condition for Growth Destruction)

If the sample size \(T\) and true Sharpe ratio \(\operatorname{SR} = \mu^e / \sigma\) satisfy:

\[ T \cdot \operatorname{SR}^2 < 1, \]

then naive sample Kelly betting yields expected excess growth strictly below zero: \(\mathbb{E}[g(\hat{f})] < r\).

For example, a strategy with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.30 (\(\operatorname{SR}^2 = 0.09\)) requires at least \(T > 1 / 0.09 \approx 11.1\) years of data before naive sample Kelly achieves positive expected excess compounding. For smaller samples, naive Kelly destroys capital in expectation.

3. Optimal Linear Kelly Shrinkage

To eliminate the sampling penalty and optimize expected compounding, MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba (2011) and empirical Bayes theory (Efron & Morris 1973) prescribe linear shrinkage on plug-in leverage: \(f_\alpha = \alpha \hat{f}\), where \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\).

Theorem 4.4 (Optimal Kelly Shrinkage Factor)

The shrinkage multiplier \(\alpha^* \in (0, 1)\) that maximizes expected growth \(\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}}[g(\alpha \hat{f})]\) is given by:

\[ \alpha^* = \frac{T \cdot \operatorname{SR}^2}{T \cdot \operatorname{SR}^2 + 1}. \]

Under optimal shrinkage, the expected realized excess growth rate is strictly positive for all \(T > 0\) whenever \(\operatorname{SR} > 0\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[g(\alpha^* \hat{f})] - r = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SR}^2 \left( \frac{T \operatorname{SR}^2}{T \operatorname{SR}^2 + 1} \right) > 0. \]
Proof of Theorem 4.4

Consider the expected growth under shrunk leverage \(f_\alpha = \alpha \hat{f}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[g(\alpha \hat{f})] &= r + \alpha \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}] \mu^e - \frac{1}{2} \alpha^2 \sigma^2 \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}^2] \\ &= r + \alpha (f^*)^2 \sigma^2 \\ &\quad - \frac{1}{2} \alpha^2 \sigma^2 \left( (f^*)^2 + \frac{1}{T \sigma^2} \right). \end{aligned} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\alpha\) and setting to zero:

\[ \frac{d}{d\alpha} \mathbb{E}[g] = (f^*)^2 \sigma^2 - \alpha \sigma^2 \left( (f^*)^2 + \frac{1}{T \sigma^2} \right) = 0. \]

Solving for \(\alpha\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha^* &= \frac{(f^*)^2}{(f^*)^2 + \frac{1}{T \sigma^2}} \\ &= \frac{(f^*)^2 \sigma^2}{(f^*)^2 \sigma^2 + \frac{1}{T}} \\ &= \frac{\operatorname{SR}^2}{\operatorname{SR}^2 + \frac{1}{T}} \\ &= \frac{T \operatorname{SR}^2}{T \operatorname{SR}^2 + 1}. \end{aligned} \]

The second derivative is \(-\sigma^2 ((f^*)^2 + \frac{1}{T\sigma^2}) < 0\), confirming a global maximum.

Lab 1 · Kelly Sizing Under Parameter Estimation Error

Evaluates 10,000 trials across sample sizes T in {20, 50, 100, 250, 1000}, verifying that naive Kelly growth degradation equals exactly 1/(2T) and that shrinkage factor alpha* restores positive compounding.

"""Lab PR3-L04-1: Kelly Sizing Under Parameter Estimation Error.

SIMULATED: Monte Carlo evaluation of growth penalty under estimation error and recovery via shrinkage.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def evaluate_estimation_error(
    true_mu_e: float = 0.08,
    sigma: float = 0.20,
    r: float = 0.02,
    sample_sizes: list[int] | None = None,
    n_trials: int = 10000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[int, dict[str, float]]:
    """Compare realized growth rates of true Kelly, naive sample Kelly, and shrinkage Kelly."""
    if sample_sizes is None:
        sample_sizes = [20, 50, 100, 250, 1000]

    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    f_star = true_mu_e / (sigma**2)
    max_excess_g = 0.5 * (true_mu_e**2) / (sigma**2)

    results: dict[int, dict[str, float]] = {}

    for t in sample_sizes:
        # Standard error of the sample mean
        se_mu = sigma / np.sqrt(t)
        # Sample excess means drawn from N(true_mu_e, se_mu^2)
        mu_hat_e = rng.normal(loc=true_mu_e, scale=se_mu, size=n_trials)

        # 1. Naive plug-in Kelly: f_hat = mu_hat_e / sigma^2
        f_naive = mu_hat_e / (sigma**2)

        # 2. Optimal shrinkage Kelly: alpha* = (t * SR_hat^2) / (t * SR_hat^2 + 1)
        sr_hat = np.maximum(0.0, mu_hat_e / sigma)
        t_sr2 = t * (sr_hat**2)
        alpha_star = np.where(mu_hat_e > 0, t_sr2 / (t_sr2 + 1.0), 0.0)
        f_shrunk = alpha_star * np.maximum(0.0, f_naive)

        # Realized continuous excess growth under true parameters: f * true_mu_e - 0.5 * f^2 * sigma^2
        g_naive = f_naive * true_mu_e - 0.5 * (f_naive**2) * (sigma**2)
        g_shrunk = f_shrunk * true_mu_e - 0.5 * (f_shrunk**2) * (sigma**2)

        mean_g_naive = float(np.mean(g_naive))
        mean_g_shrunk = float(np.mean(g_shrunk))
        theo_naive_g = float(max_excess_g - 1.0 / (2.0 * t))
        overbet_rate = float(np.mean(f_naive > 2.0 * f_star))

        results[t] = {
            "mean_g_naive": mean_g_naive,
            "theo_naive_g": theo_naive_g,
            "mean_g_shrunk": mean_g_shrunk,
            "mean_alpha": float(np.mean(alpha_star)),
            "overbet_rate": overbet_rate,
        }

    return results


def main() -> None:
    mu_e = 0.08
    sigma = 0.20
    r = 0.02
    f_star = mu_e / (sigma**2)
    max_excess_g = 0.5 * (mu_e**2) / (sigma**2)  # 0.0800
    sample_sizes = [20, 50, 100, 250, 1000]

    sim = evaluate_estimation_error(
        true_mu_e=mu_e, sigma=sigma, r=r, sample_sizes=sample_sizes, n_trials=10000, seed=42
    )

    print(f"Estimation Error Impact on Kelly Sizing (True excess mu={mu_e:.2f}, sigma={sigma:.2f}):")
    print(f"Optimal Kelly Leverage f*: {f_star:.2f} | Max Excess Growth g*: {max_excess_g:.4f}\n")
    print(
        f"{'Sample T':<10} {'Naive Emp g':<13} {'Naive Theo g':<14} "
        f"{'Shrunk Emp g':<14} {'Mean Alpha*':<13} {'P(Overbet>2f*)':<15}"
    )
    print("-" * 79)

    for t in sample_sizes:
        res = sim[t]
        print(
            f"{t:<10} {res['mean_g_naive']:<13.4f} {res['theo_naive_g']:<14.4f} "
            f"{res['mean_g_shrunk']:<14.4f} {res['mean_alpha']:<13.3f} {res['overbet_rate']:<15.4f}"
        )

Where the Canon Breaks

The \(\frac{1}{2T}\) penalty and optimal shrinkage factor \(\alpha^*\) are derived under stationary Gaussian returns with known volatility. In live markets, three structural breakdowns alter estimation mechanics:

  1. Non-stationarity and structural breaks: Financial markets do not generate i.i.d. draws from a fixed stationary distribution. Historical observations older than 2–3 years frequently belong to extinct volatility and macro regimes, limiting effective sample size \(T_{\text{eff}}\).
  2. Fat-tailed return errors: When return innovations have excess kurtosis, the sample mean variance converges at a slower rate than \(\sigma^2 / T\), magnifying sampling variance and demanding more aggressive shrinkage.
  3. Multivariate error maximization: In a portfolio of \(N\) assets, sample covariance inversion inflates estimation error by a factor of \(\frac{N}{T}\). Without Ledoit-Wolf covariance shrinkage (MacLean et al. 2011), multi-asset Kelly sizing fails completely out of sample.

Misconceptions

  • Because the sample mean is an unbiased estimator (\(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}] = \mu\)), plugging it into the Kelly formula yields an unbiased growth rate.

    Growth is a non-linear quadratic function of leverage. By Jensen's inequality and parameter variance, \(\mathbb{E}[g(\hat{f})] < g(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}])\). The variance of the estimator extracts an unavoidable penalty of \(\frac{1}{2T}\) per period.

  • Parameter estimation errors in Kelly sizing are symmetric around the optimal fraction.

    Underestimating the edge reduces the compounding rate along a concave curve without threatening survival. Overestimating the edge pushes leverage past \(2f^*\), causing negative geometric compounding and almost sure bankruptcy.

  • Adding more assets to a multi-asset Kelly portfolio always increases compounding growth under estimation error.

    Inverting an unregularized \(N \times N\) sample covariance matrix amplifies estimation error in proportion to \(N / T\). For \(N\) comparable to \(T\), out-of-sample growth collapses to negative infinity unless ridge or Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage is applied.

Problem Set

Mechanics

An equity quant strategy has estimated annual Sharpe ratio \(\widehat{\operatorname{SR}} = 0.40\) based on \(T = 25\) annual observations. Compute the optimal shrinkage factor \(\alpha^*\) and the expected growth penalty of naive sample Kelly.

Solution

With \(T = 25\) and \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.40\), \(\operatorname{SR}^2 = 0.16\). Then \(T \cdot \operatorname{SR}^2 = 25 \times 0.16 = 4.0\). The optimal shrinkage factor is \(\alpha^* = \frac{4.0}{5.0} = 0.8000\) (shrink leverage to 80%). The expected growth penalty of naive plug-in Kelly is \(\frac{1}{2T} = \frac{1}{2(25)} = \frac{1}{50} = 0.0200\) (2.0% per annum).

A high-frequency desk estimates \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.20\) on a trade using \(T = 16\) daily periods. Determine whether naive sample Kelly achieves positive expected excess growth.

Solution

We evaluate the growth destruction condition \(T \cdot \operatorname{SR}^2\). Here, \(T \cdot \operatorname{SR}^2 = 16 \times (0.20)^2 = 16 \times 0.04 = 0.64 < 1.0\). Because \(T \cdot \operatorname{SR}^2 < 1\), the sampling penalty \(\frac{1}{2T} = \frac{1}{32} = 0.03125\) exceeds the maximum theoretical excess growth \(\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SR}^2 = 0.5(0.04) = 0.0200\). Naive sample Kelly yields negative expected excess compounding (\(-1.125\%\) per period).

An allocator requires that optimal shrinkage \(\alpha^*\) be at least 0.95 before deploying a strategy with \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.50\). Calculate the minimum sample size \(T\) required.

Solution

Set \(\alpha^* = \frac{T \operatorname{SR}^2}{T \operatorname{SR}^2 + 1} \ge 0.95\). With \(\operatorname{SR}^2 = 0.25\), let \(x = 0.25 T\). Then \(\frac{x}{x + 1} \ge 0.95 \implies x \ge 0.95(x + 1) \implies 0.05 x \ge 0.95 \implies x \ge 19\). Then \(0.25 T \ge 19 \implies T \ge 76\) observation periods.

Proof

Prove that optimal shrinkage leverage \(f^*_{\text{shrunk}} = \alpha^* \hat{f}\) always yields strictly positive expected excess growth \(\mathbb{E}[g(\alpha^* \hat{f})] - r > 0\) for any \(T > 0\) whenever true \(\operatorname{SR} > 0\).

Solution

From Theorem 4.4, expected excess growth under \(\alpha^*\) is \(\mathbb{E}[g_e(\alpha^* \hat{f})] = \alpha^* (f^*)^2 \sigma^2 - \frac{1}{2}(\alpha^*)^2 \sigma^2 ((f^*)^2 + \frac{1}{T\sigma^2})\). Substituting \(\alpha^* = \frac{(f^*)^2}{(f^*)^2 + \frac{1}{T\sigma^2}}\) yields \(\mathbb{E}[g_e] = \frac{1}{2}\alpha^* (f^*)^2 \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{SR}^2 \left( \frac{T \operatorname{SR}^2}{T \operatorname{SR}^2 + 1} \right)\). For any \(T > 0\) and \(\operatorname{SR} > 0\), the term \(\frac{T \operatorname{SR}^2}{T \operatorname{SR}^2 + 1} > 0\), ensuring that expected excess growth is strictly positive.

Prove that the probability of overbetting beyond double Kelly under naive sample Kelly \(\mathbb{P}(\hat{f} > 2 f^*)\) is given by \(1 - \Phi(\sqrt{T}\operatorname{SR})\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

Solution

Recall \(\hat{f} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(f^*, \frac{1}{T \sigma^2}\right)\). Standardizing: \(Z = \frac{\hat{f} - f^*}{1/(\sqrt{T}\sigma)} = \sqrt{T}\sigma(\hat{f} - f^*) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). The condition \(\hat{f} > 2 f^*\) is equivalent to \(\hat{f} - f^* > f^*\). Multiplying by \(\sqrt{T}\sigma\): \(Z > \sqrt{T}\sigma f^* = \sqrt{T}\sigma \frac{\mu^e}{\sigma^2} = \sqrt{T}\frac{\mu^e}{\sigma} = \sqrt{T}\operatorname{SR}\). Therefore, \(\mathbb{P}(\hat{f} > 2 f^*) = \mathbb{P}(Z > \sqrt{T}\operatorname{SR}) = 1 - \Phi(\sqrt{T}\operatorname{SR})\).

Prove that as sample size \(T \to \infty\), \(\alpha^* \to 1\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}) \to 0\), so that naive Kelly converges in probability to true full Kelly.

Solution

We evaluate the limit of \(\alpha^*(T) = \frac{T \operatorname{SR}^2}{T \operatorname{SR}^2 + 1} = \frac{\operatorname{SR}^2}{\operatorname{SR}^2 + 1/T}\). As \(T \to \infty\), \(1/T \to 0\), so \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \alpha^*(T) = \frac{\operatorname{SR}^2}{\operatorname{SR}^2} = 1\). Furthermore, \(\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f} - f^*)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}) = \frac{1}{T\sigma^2} \to 0\). By Chebyshev's inequality, \(\hat{f} \xrightarrow{p} f^*\). In the large-sample limit, estimation error vanishes and shrinkage recovers full Kelly.

Empirical

A trend-following manager with \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.50\) tests sizing on rolling 36-month windows. Calculate the overbetting ruin probability \(\mathbb{P}(\hat{f} > 2 f^*)\) and the optimal shrinkage fraction.

Solution

With \(T = 3\) years and \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.50\), \(\sqrt{T}\operatorname{SR} = \sqrt{3}(0.50) \approx 1.732 \times 0.50 = 0.866\). The probability of exceeding double-Kelly is \(1 - \Phi(0.866) \approx 1 - 0.8068 = 0.1932\) (19.32% chance of lethal overbetting). The optimal shrinkage factor is \(\alpha^* = \frac{3 \times 0.25}{3 \times 0.25 + 1} = \frac{0.75}{1.75} = 0.4286\). The manager must size at approximately 43% of the sample Kelly estimate.

Why does shrinkage toward a grand prior mean across multiple trading strategies outperform independent single-strategy Kelly estimators?

Solution

By the James-Stein paradox (Efron & Morris 1973), when estimating \(N \ge 3\) independent strategy Sharpe ratios, shrinking individual estimates toward a common cross-sectional mean strictly dominates separate maximum likelihood estimators in mean squared error, suppressing extreme outlier allocations.

In a 50-asset equity long/short portfolio, explain why plugging sample return vectors and sample covariance matrices into \(\bm{w}^* = \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})\) results in immediate strategy liquidation.

Solution

Inverting an empirical sample covariance matrix \(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}\) with \(N = 50\) assets over \(T = 100\) days produces extreme eigenvalue dispersion. Small sample eigenvalues are underestimated, causing the optimizer to place massive long and short bets on noisy factor noise (Michaud 1989). Gross leverage explodes, and minor out-of-sample deviations trigger liquidation.

Research

Formulate Bayesian Kelly sizing under a conjugate Normal-Inverse-Gamma prior on \((\mu, \sigma^2)\).

Solution

Under a Normal-Inverse-Gamma prior where \(\mu | \sigma^2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma^2 / \kappa_0)\) and \(\sigma^2\) has inverse-gamma parameters \((\alpha_0, \beta_0)\), the predictive distribution of returns is Student-t. The expected log growth rate is maximized by integrating over the joint posterior, resulting in an optimal Bayesian fraction that scales down leverage in proportion to posterior parameter uncertainty.

How do parameter uncertainty penalties in Kelly sizing relate to robust min-max portfolio optimization?

Solution

In robust optimization, the investor maximizes worst-case return over an ellipsoidal uncertainty set:

\[ \begin{aligned} (\hat{\bm{\mu}} - \bm{\mu})' \bm{\Sigma}^{-1} (\hat{\bm{\mu}} - \bm{\mu}) \le \frac{\kappa^2}{T}. \end{aligned} \]

For logarithmic utility, the worst-case drift penalty is mathematically equivalent to subtracting \(\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{T}}\) from the Sharpe ratio, generating shrinkage identical to Bayesian Kelly sizing.

Analyze the compounding degradation when volatility \(\sigma\) is also estimated with sample error.

Solution

When \(\sigma^2\) is estimated by sample variance \(s^2 \sim \frac{\sigma^2}{T-1} \chi^2_{T-1}\), Jensen's inequality implies \(\mathbb{E}[1/s^2] = \frac{T-1}{T-3}\frac{1}{\sigma^2} > \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\). Unbiased sample variance produces an upward bias in Kelly leverage, amplifying overbetting risk and necessitating an additional shrinkage discount of \(\frac{T-3}{T-1}\).
