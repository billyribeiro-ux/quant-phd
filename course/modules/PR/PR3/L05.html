Samuelson's Critique and Sizing in Practice

In 1979, Nobel laureate Paul Samuelson launched a historic attack against growth-optimal investing, writing his famous paper entirely in words of one syllable. Samuelson's critique of growth-optimal investing demonstrated that maximizing expected logarithmic wealth is an arbitrary utility assumption (CRRA relative risk aversion \(\gamma = 1\)). For any investor with higher risk aversion (\(\gamma > 1\)), the Law of Large Numbers does not justify Kelly sizing, and full Kelly produces catastrophic expected utility losses. Reconciling Kelly growth with economic decision theory reveals that fractional Kelly with \(c = 1/\gamma\) bridges growth theory and utility maximization.

1. Paul Samuelson's 1979 Indictment

In his landmark paper, Samuelson (1979) targeted the widely held belief among mathematicians and gamblers that "even if you do not like log utility in the short run, you should maximize mean log of wealth if you have many years to invest."

Theorem 5.1 (Samuelson's Fallacy of Large Numbers)

Let an investor have von Neumann-Morgenstern utility of terminal wealth \(U(W_T) = \frac{W_T^{1-\gamma} - 1}{1-\gamma}\) with constant relative risk aversion \(\gamma > 0, \gamma \neq 1\). Then for every investment horizon \(T \ge 1\), the policy that maximizes \(\mathbb{E}[U(W_T)]\) allocates the constant fraction:

\[ \pi^*(\gamma) = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}. \]

In particular, \(\pi^*(\gamma)\) is completely invariant to the time horizon \(T\). The policy does not converge to the Kelly fraction \(f^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\) as \(T \to \infty\).

Proof of Theorem 5.1

Under constant leverage \(\pi\), terminal wealth after \(T\) periods of continuous diffusion is lognormally distributed:

\[ \ln W_T = \ln W_0 + \left(r + \pi(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2}\pi^2\sigma^2\right)T + \pi\sigma\sqrt{T} Z, \quad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). \]

For \(\gamma \neq 1\), the expected utility of wealth is proportional to \(\mathbb{E}[W_T^{1-\gamma}]\). Using the moment generating function of the normal distribution, for any scalar \(k\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[e^{k \ln W_T}] = W_0^k \exp\left( k\left(r + \pi(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2}\pi^2\sigma^2\right)T + \frac{1}{2} k^2 \pi^2 \sigma^2 T \right). \]

Setting \(k = 1 - \gamma\), the exponent simplifies to:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Exponent} &= (1 - \gamma) T \left[ r + \pi(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2}\pi^2\sigma^2 + \frac{1}{2}(1 - \gamma)\pi^2\sigma^2 \right] \\ &= (1 - \gamma) T \left[ r + \pi(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2}\gamma \pi^2 \sigma^2 \right]. \end{aligned} \]

Maximizing expected utility is equivalent to maximizing the bracketed term for any \(T > 0\):

\[ \max_\pi \left\{ r + \pi(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2}\gamma \pi^2 \sigma^2 \right\}. \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\pi\) and setting to zero:

\[ (\mu - r) - \gamma \pi \sigma^2 = 0 \implies \pi^*(\gamma) = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}. \]

The optimal fraction \(\pi^*\) depends exclusively on \(\gamma\), drift, and volatility. The horizon \(T\) factors out completely.

2. Certainty Equivalent Growth and Utility Destruction

Why doesn't Breiman's Asymptotic Dominance Theorem (Theorem 2.2) compel a rational investor to adopt full Kelly? Samuelson (1979) resolved this paradox by analyzing the left tail of terminal wealth.

Definition 5.1 (Certainty Equivalent Growth Rate)

The continuous Certainty Equivalent Growth Rate (CEGR) for an investor with CRRA parameter \(\gamma > 0\) under leverage fraction \(f\) is:

\[ \operatorname{CEGR}(f; \gamma) \equiv r + f(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2} \gamma f^2 \sigma^2. \]

Evaluating the CEGR reveals the catastrophic utility penalty of unconstrained Kelly betting when \(\gamma > 1\):

Theorem 5.2 (Utility Destruction of Full Kelly)

Let an investor have risk aversion \(\gamma > 1\). If this investor is forced to adopt full Kelly leverage \(f^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\), their Certainty Equivalent Growth Rate satisfies:

\[ \operatorname{CEGR}(f^*; \gamma) = r + \left(1 - \frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\operatorname{SR}^2. \]

Whenever \(\gamma \ge 2\), the certainty equivalent excess return is non-positive (\(\operatorname{CEGR} \le r\)). For \(\gamma > 2\), full Kelly generates a certainty equivalent return strictly worse than holding 100% cash.

Proof of Theorem 5.2

Substitute \(f^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\) into Definition 5.1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{CEGR}(f^*; \gamma) &= r + \left(\frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\right)(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2}\gamma \left(\frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\right)^2 \sigma^2 \\ &= r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{\sigma^2} - \frac{1}{2}\gamma \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{\sigma^2} \\ &= r + \left(1 - \frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\operatorname{SR}^2. \end{aligned} \]

For \(\gamma = 2\), \(1 - \gamma/2 = 0\), so \(\operatorname{CEGR} = r\). For \(\gamma > 2\), \(1 - \gamma/2 < 0\), implying \(\operatorname{CEGR} < r\). For example, with \(\gamma = 4\) and \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.40\), \(\operatorname{CEGR} = r - 0.16\), an annual utility loss of 16% below cash.

Remark 5.1 (Resolving the Breiman-Samuelson Debate)

Breiman (1961) proved that \(\mathbb{P}(W^*_T > W_T) \to 1\). However, expected utility weights outcomes by marginal utility \(U'(W) = W^{-\gamma}\). For \(\gamma > 1\), marginal utility explodes as \(W \to 0\). The vanishingly small probability event \(\mathbb{P}(W^*_T < W_T)\) occurs in states of extreme poverty where \(W^{-\gamma}\) is massive. The catastrophic loss of utility in the left tail completely overwhelms the higher probability of superior wealth in the right tail.

3. Institutional Sizing in Practice

Professional quantitative asset managers bridge Kelly compounding and Samuelson's utility constraints through three operational mechanisms:

  1. The Fractional Bridge: Equating the fractional Kelly multiplier to inverse risk aversion, \(c = 1/\gamma\), satisfies Samuelson's expected utility theorem while retaining Kelly's proportional rebalancing mechanics (Merton 1969; MacLean et al. 2011).
  2. Drawdown Floor Protection: Combining fractional sizing with Grossman & Zhou (1993) drawdown floors guarantees that capital never drops below a pre-specified liquidation threshold.
  3. Volatility Targeting and Estimation Haircuts: Sizing leverage based on shrinkage-adjusted Sharpe ratios and dynamic volatility targets ensures that position sizes contract automatically during market stress.

Lab 1 · Samuelson Critique and CRRA Utility Evaluation

Simulates 15,000 5-year paths across risk aversion levels gamma in {1, 2, 4, 10}, verifying that full Kelly destroys certainty equivalent returns for gamma > 1 and that fractional Merton sizing maximizes utility.

"""Lab PR3-L05-1: Samuelson's Critique and CRRA Utility Evaluation.

SIMULATED: Verification of Samuelson's critique: expected utility of full Kelly vs fractional Merton sizing.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def evaluate_samuelson_utility(
    gamma_levels: list[float],
    fractions: list[float],
    mu: float = 0.10,
    r: float = 0.02,
    sigma: float = 0.20,
    t_years: float = 5.0,
    n_paths: int = 15000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[float, dict[float, dict[str, float]]]:
    """Calculate empirical and analytical certainty equivalent growth rates for CRRA investors."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    f_kelly = (mu - r) / (sigma**2)  # 2.00
    z = rng.standard_normal(n_paths)

    results: dict[float, dict[float, dict[str, float]]] = {}

    for gamma in gamma_levels:
        results[gamma] = {}
        for c in fractions:
            f = c * f_kelly

            # Analytical Certainty Equivalent Growth Rate: CEGR = r + f*(mu-r) - 0.5 * gamma * f^2 * sigma^2
            theo_cegr = r + f * (mu - r) - 0.5 * gamma * (f**2) * (sigma**2)

            # Simulated terminal wealth
            drift = (r + f * (mu - r) - 0.5 * (f**2) * (sigma**2)) * t_years
            diffusion = f * sigma * np.sqrt(t_years) * z
            log_w = drift + diffusion
            wealth = np.exp(log_w)

            # CRRA Certainty Equivalent: (E[W^(1-gamma)])^(1 / (1-gamma)) / t_years
            if abs(gamma - 1.0) < 1e-4:
                emp_cegr = float(np.mean(log_w) / t_years)
            else:
                power_w = np.power(wealth, 1.0 - gamma)
                mean_power = np.mean(power_w)
                if mean_power > 0:
                    ce_wealth = float(np.power(mean_power, 1.0 / (1.0 - gamma)))
                    emp_cegr = float(np.log(ce_wealth) / t_years)
                else:
                    emp_cegr = -np.inf

            results[gamma][c] = {
                "f": float(f),
                "theo_cegr": float(theo_cegr),
                "emp_cegr": float(emp_cegr),
            }

    return results


def main() -> None:
    mu = 0.10
    r = 0.02
    sigma = 0.20
    f_kelly = (mu - r) / (sigma**2)  # 2.00
    gamma_levels = [1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 10.0]
    # Sizing fractions: full Kelly (1.0), half (0.50), quarter (0.25), tenth (0.10)
    fractions = [1.0, 0.50, 0.25, 0.10]

    sim = evaluate_samuelson_utility(
        gamma_levels, fractions, mu=mu, r=r, sigma=sigma, t_years=5.0, n_paths=15000, seed=42
    )

    print(f"Samuelson Critique: Certainty Equivalent Growth Rates (mu={mu:.2f}, r={r:.2f}, sigma={sigma:.2f}):")
    print(f"Full Kelly Leverage f* = {f_kelly:.2f}\n")
    print(f"{'CRRA Gamma':<12} {'Policy':<18} {'Fraction c':<12} {'Lev f':<8} {'Theo CEGR':<12} {'Emp CEGR':<12}")
    print("-" * 74)

    for gamma in gamma_levels:
        opt_c = 1.0 / gamma
        for c in fractions:
            d = sim[gamma][c]
            is_opt = abs(c - opt_c) < 1e-4
            policy_label = "Merton Optimal" if is_opt else ("Full Kelly" if c == 1.0 else f"{c:.2f}-Kelly")
            print(
                f"{gamma:<12.1f} {policy_label:<18} {c:<12.2f} {d['f']:<8.2f} "
                f"{d['theo_cegr']:<12.4f} {d['emp_cegr']:<12.4f}"
            )
        print("-" * 74)


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

While utility theory resolves the long-term mathematical sizing debate, institutional finance operates under principal-agent boundaries that break classical expected utility models:

  1. Quarterly benchmark reviews: Fund managers are evaluated on trailing 12-month performance relative to a peer group. An optimal 10-year policy that experiences 2 consecutive years of underperformance leads to manager dismissal, rendering 10-year utility maximization economically irrelevant.
  2. Asymmetric compensation contracts: Performance fees (e.g. 20% of profits above hurdle) create a convex call-option payoff on fund assets, incentivizing fund managers to take more risk than the fund's investors would choose under standard CRRA utility.
  3. Career risk and career termination: A portfolio manager facing job termination upon a 25% drawdown has an effective risk aversion that approaches infinity at the drawdown boundary, requiring hard cushion floors rather than static power utility.

Misconceptions

  • Samuelson proved that the Kelly criterion does not maximize capital growth.

    Samuelson fully acknowledged that the Kelly criterion maximizes expected logarithmic growth. His proof demonstrated that maximizing logarithmic growth is an arbitrary utility specification not shared by risk-averse investors with \(\gamma > 1\).

  • Given a sufficiently long horizon (e.g. 30 years), every rational investor should converge to full Kelly sizing.

    The Fallacy of Large Numbers proves that optimal allocation is completely invariant to horizon \(T\). An investor with \(\gamma = 4\) should allocate 25% Kelly whether the horizon is 1 day, 10 years, or 100 years.

  • Fractional Kelly is an ad-hoc heuristic without theoretical justification.

    Fractional Kelly with multiplier \(c\) is the exact, mathematically optimal continuous portfolio policy for an expected utility maximizer with constant relative risk aversion \(\gamma = 1/c\).

Problem Set

Mechanics

An asset has excess return \(\mu - r = 0.08\) and volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\). Compute the optimal Merton fraction \(\pi^*\) and Certainty Equivalent Growth Rate for an investor with CRRA parameter \(\gamma = 4.0\). Compare with full Kelly.

Solution

Full Kelly leverage is \(f^* = \frac{0.08}{0.04} = 2.0000\). The Sharpe ratio is \(\operatorname{SR} = \frac{0.08}{0.20} = 0.40\). For \(\gamma = 4.0\), optimal Merton leverage is \(\pi^* = \frac{f^*}{4.0} = 0.5000\) (quarter-Kelly). The CEGR under \(\pi^*\) is \(r + 0.50(0.08) - 0.5(4.0)(0.25)(0.04) = r + 0.04 - 0.02 = r + 0.0200\) (+2.0% above cash). Under full Kelly (\(f = 2.0\)), \(\operatorname{CEGR} = r + 2.0(0.08) - 0.5(4.0)(4.0)(0.04) = r + 0.16 - 0.32 = r - 0.1600\) (-16.0% below cash).

Determine the threshold relative risk aversion \(\gamma_{\text{zero}}\) at which full Kelly leverage yields a Certainty Equivalent Growth Rate exactly equal to the risk-free rate.

Solution

From Theorem 5.2, \(\operatorname{CEGR}(f^*; \gamma) = r + (1 - \gamma/2)\operatorname{SR}^2\). Setting \(\operatorname{CEGR} = r\) requires \((1 - \gamma/2)\operatorname{SR}^2 = 0\). Since \(\operatorname{SR} > 0\), we have \(1 - \gamma/2 = 0 \implies \gamma_{\text{zero}} = 2.0\). For any investor with risk aversion \(\gamma > 2.0\), full Kelly generates lower utility than holding 100% cash.

An endowment CIO specifies that their risk preference corresponds to CRRA \(\gamma = 5.0\). What fractional Kelly multiplier \(c\) should their quantitative team implement?

Solution

By Remark 5.1, the fractional Kelly multiplier corresponding to CRRA parameter \(\gamma\) is \(c = 1/\gamma\). For \(\gamma = 5.0\), \(c = 1/5.0 = 0.2000\) (20% of full Kelly, or one-fifth Kelly).

Proof

Prove that if two independent gambles have identical positive expected growth rates \(g_1 = g_2 > 0\), an investor with CRRA \(\gamma > 1\) strictly prefers diversifying equally between them rather than holding either gamble in isolation.

Solution

Let each gamble have drift \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Holding either gamble has \(\operatorname{CEGR}_{\text{single}} = r + f\mu^e - \frac{1}{2}\gamma f^2 \sigma^2\). Allocating \(f/2\) to each independent gamble yields portfolio variance \((f/2)^2\sigma^2 + (f/2)^2\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{2} f^2 \sigma^2\). The diversified CEGR is \(\operatorname{CEGR}_{\text{div}} = r + f\mu^e - \frac{1}{4}\gamma f^2 \sigma^2\). The difference is \(\operatorname{CEGR}_{\text{div}} - \operatorname{CEGR}_{\text{single}} = \frac{1}{4}\gamma f^2 \sigma^2 > 0\) for all \(\gamma > 0\), proving strict preference for diversification.

Prove that the ratio of the certainty equivalent excess return under Merton optimal sizing to full Kelly sizing diverges to \(+\infty\) as \(\gamma \to \infty\).

Solution

Under Merton optimal sizing \(\pi^* = f^*/\gamma\), the excess CEGR is \(\operatorname{CEGR}^*_e = \frac{1}{2\gamma}\operatorname{SR}^2 > 0\). Under full Kelly, \(\operatorname{CEGR}_{\text{Kelly}, e} = (1 - \gamma/2)\operatorname{SR}^2\), which is negative for \(\gamma > 2\). The utility difference is \(\operatorname{CEGR}^*_e - \operatorname{CEGR}_{\text{Kelly}, e} = \left(\frac{1}{2\gamma} - 1 + \frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\operatorname{SR}^2\). As \(\gamma \to \infty\), the term \(\frac{\gamma}{2} \to \infty\), demonstrating that the utility advantage of fractional Merton sizing over full Kelly grows without bound.

Prove that for an investor with CRRA utility \(U(W) = \frac{W^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\), optimal leverage is invariant to current wealth \(W_t\).

Solution

Relative risk aversion is defined as \(-W \frac{U''(W)}{U'(W)}\). For power utility, \(U'(W) = W^{-\gamma}\) and \(U''(W) = -\gamma W^{-\gamma-1}\). Thus \(-W \frac{-\gamma W^{-\gamma-1}}{W^{-\gamma}} = \gamma\), which is constant and independent of \(W\). Because relative risk aversion is wealth-invariant, the proportion of wealth allocated to risky assets \(\pi^*\) is constant across all wealth levels.

Empirical

A multi-strategy fund operates with \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.80\). The CIO chooses \(\gamma = 3.0\). Calculate the fund's target portfolio volatility and compare it with the volatility of full Kelly.

Solution

Under continuous diffusion, full Kelly portfolio volatility is \(\sigma_{\text{Kelly}} = \operatorname{SR} = 0.80\) (80% annualized volatility!). Under \(\gamma = 3.0\), fractional multiplier is \(c = 1/3 \approx 0.3333\). Target portfolio volatility is \(\sigma_{\text{target}} = c \operatorname{SR} = \frac{0.80}{3} \approx 0.2667\) (26.67% volatility). Sizing with \(\gamma = 3.0\) prevents the unmanageable 80% volatility of full Kelly.

Explain why institutional funds utilizing dynamic risk parity or vol targeting implicitly implement fractional Kelly sizing with \(\gamma > 1\).

Solution

Risk parity and volatility targeting fix portfolio volatility at an institutional target (e.g. 10%–12%). If a strategy has \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.60\), full Kelly implies 60% volatility. Capping volatility at 12% imposes an effective fractional multiplier of \(c = 12\% / 60\% = 0.20\), which is mathematically equivalent to optimizing CRRA utility with risk aversion \(\gamma = 1/0.20 = 5.0\).

In a multi-year bear market where asset drift drops to zero, contrast the terminal wealth of full Kelly versus half-Kelly.

Solution

When \(\mu - r = 0\), true optimal leverage is zero. If a fund mistakenly maintains leverage \(f = 1.0\), continuous growth rate is \(g = r - 0.5 \sigma^2\). For \(\sigma = 0.20\), volatility drag is \(-0.0200\) (-2.0% per annum). Over 5 years, wealth decays by \(\exp(-0.10) = 0.905\). Under half-Kelly (\(f = 0.5\)), volatility drag is only \(-0.5(0.25)(0.04) = -0.0050\) (-0.5% per annum), preserving capital during edge decay.

Research

Evaluate the impact of habit formation preferences (Campbell-Cochrane) on growth-optimal sizing.

Solution

Under internal habit formation, utility depends on surplus consumption over a habit level \(C_t - H_t\). As wealth approaches the habit floor, effective risk aversion explodes toward infinity. Sizing rules must transition dynamically from fractional Kelly in high-surplus regimes to zero leverage as wealth nears the habit boundary.

How do high-water-mark performance fee structures alter the effective risk aversion of a hedge fund manager?

Solution

A performance fee gives the manager a call option on fund wealth. Deep in drawdown below the high-water mark, the option is out of the money, reducing the manager's effective risk aversion and creating moral hazard to gamble for resurrection, in direct conflict with the investor's CRRA utility.

Analyze the macroeconomic general equilibrium consequences if all market participants attempted to follow full Kelly sizing.

Solution

If all investors followed full Kelly, the aggregate market clearing leverage would require massive aggregate borrowing. In equilibrium, borrowing rates \(r_b\) would bid up until the aggregate equity risk premium collapsed, reducing excess returns until the market-clearing Kelly fraction equaled exactly 1.0 (100% equity, zero borrowing).
