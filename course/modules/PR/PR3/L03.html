Fractional Kelly and Volatility Drag

While full Kelly maximizes asymptotic capital accumulation, it extracts a brutal psychological and institutional toll. An investor following unconstrained full Kelly faces a 50% probability of suffering a 50% peak-to-trough drawdown over an infinite horizon. To mitigate this path-dependent severity, practitioners deploy fractional Kelly sizing, scaling the optimal leverage vector by a multiplier \(c \in (0, 1)\). Operating at half-Kelly (\(c = 0.50\)) captures 75% of maximum excess compounding while cutting volatility in half, variance by three-quarters, and collapsing the probability of a 50% drawdown from 50% down to 12.5%.

1. The Perils of Full Kelly Sizing

In continuous trading under geometric Brownian motion, let the optimal growth rate be \(g^* = r + \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SR}^2\) with volatility \(\sigma_p^* = \operatorname{SR}\). The price path of a full Kelly portfolio experiences immense volatility drag.

Definition 3.1 (Peak-to-Trough Drawdown)

Let \(W_t\) be a wealth process. The running maximum up to time \(t\) is \(M_t \equiv \sup_{0 \le s \le t} W_s\). The relative drawdown at time \(t\) is:

\[ D_t \equiv \frac{M_t - W_t}{M_t} \in [0, 1]. \]

The maximum drawdown over horizon \([0, T]\) is \(\operatorname{MDD}_T \equiv \sup_{0 \le t \le T} D_t\).

Theorem 3.1 (Full Kelly Asymptotic Drawdown Distribution)

Under continuous diffusion with constant drift and volatility, an investor betting full Kelly (\(c = 1\)) experiences an asymptotic maximum drawdown exceeding fraction \(d \in (0, 1)\) with probability:

\[ \mathbb{P}(\operatorname{MDD}_\infty \ge d) = 1 - d. \]

In particular, the probability of ever suffering a 50% drawdown (\(d = 0.50\)) is exactly 50%, and the probability of an 80% drawdown is 20%.

For institutional fund managers whose mandates terminate upon a 20% to 30% drawdown, full Kelly guarantees client liquidation with high probability.

2. Sizing with Fractional Kelly

Fractional Kelly replaces the full growth-optimal leverage \(\bm{w}^*\) with a scaled allocation \(\bm{w}_c = c \bm{w}^*\), where \(c \in (0, 1)\) is the fractional multiplier.

Definition 3.2 (Fractional Kelly Growth Function)

For multiplier \(c > 0\), the continuous compounding growth rate of portfolio \(\bm{w}_c = c \bm{w}^*\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} g(c) &\equiv r + c \bm{w}^{*\prime}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) - \frac{1}{2} c^2 \bm{w}^{*\prime} \bm{\Sigma} \bm{w}^* \\ &= r + c \operatorname{SR}^2 - \frac{1}{2} c^2 \operatorname{SR}^2. \end{aligned} \]

The excess growth rate above the risk-free rate is \(g_e(c) \equiv g(c) - r = \left(c - \frac{1}{2}c^2\right)\operatorname{SR}^2\).

Theorem 3.2 (The Half-Kelly 75/50 Invariant)

Setting \(c = 0.50\) (half-Kelly) achieves exactly 75% of the maximum excess geometric growth rate while cutting portfolio volatility by 50% and portfolio variance by 75%:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{g_e(0.50)}{g_e(1.00)} &= \frac{0.50 - \frac{1}{2}(0.50)^2}{1.00 - \frac{1}{2}(1.00)^2} = \frac{0.375}{0.500} = 0.75, \\ \frac{\sigma_p(0.50)}{\sigma_p(1.00)} &= 0.50, \quad \frac{\sigma_p^2(0.50)}{\sigma_p^2(1.00)} = 0.25. \end{aligned} \]

The derivative \(g_e'(c) = (1 - c)\operatorname{SR}^2\) demonstrates that the marginal growth benefit of additional leverage diminishes linearly from \(\operatorname{SR}^2\) at \(c=0\) to zero at \(c=1\). The second half of full Kelly leverage (\(c \in [0.5, 1.0]\)) provides only one-third of the growth contribution of the first half, while doubling portfolio volatility.

3. Asymptotic Drawdown and Shadow Risk Aversion

MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba (2011) and Magdon-Ismail et al. (2004) established the exact analytical distribution of maximum drawdown for fractional Kelly diffusion processes.

Theorem 3.3 (Fractional Kelly Drawdown Probability)

For a continuous diffusion with fractional Kelly multiplier \(c \in (0, 2)\), the probability of ever suffering a peak-to-trough drawdown of fraction \(d \in (0, 1)\) over an infinite horizon is:

\[ \mathbb{P}(\operatorname{MDD}_\infty \ge d) = (1 - d)^{\frac{2}{c} - 1}. \]

For \(c \ge 2\), the probability is identically 1.0.

Proof of Theorem 3.3

The log wealth process \(X_t = \ln W_t\) satisfies \(dX_t = g(c) dt + c \operatorname{SR} dW_t\). The running maximum of log wealth is \(M_t^X = \sup_{s \le t} X_s\), and drawdown is \(Y_t = M_t^X - X_t \ge 0\). When wealth drops by fraction \(d\), \(W_t = (1 - d) M_t\), which corresponds to \(Y_t = -\ln(1 - d) \equiv y > 0\).

By excursion theory of Brownian motion with drift (Magdon-Ismail et al. 2004), the process \(Y_t\) is a reflected diffusion with infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{A} v = -g(c) v' + \frac{1}{2} c^2 \operatorname{SR}^2 v''\). The probability that the maximum of \(Y_t\) ever exceeds threshold \(y\) is \(e^{-\theta y}\), where \(\theta\) is the positive root of \(-g(c) \theta + \frac{1}{2} c^2 \operatorname{SR}^2 \theta^2 = 0\):

\[ \theta = \frac{2 g_e(c)}{c^2 \operatorname{SR}^2} = \frac{2(c \operatorname{SR}^2 - \frac{1}{2}c^2 \operatorname{SR}^2)}{c^2 \operatorname{SR}^2} = \frac{2c - c^2}{c^2} = \frac{2}{c} - 1. \]

Substituting \(y = -\ln(1 - d)\) yields:

\[ \mathbb{P}(\operatorname{MDD}_\infty \ge d) = \exp(-\theta (-\ln(1 - d))) = (1 - d)^\theta = (1 - d)^{\frac{2}{c} - 1}. \]

This completes the derivation.

Remark 3.1 (Shadow Risk Aversion)

Comparing fractional Kelly leverage \(\bm{w}_c = c \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})\) to the continuous Merton portfolio fraction \(\bm{w}_{\text{Merton}} = \frac{1}{\gamma}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})\) (Merton 1969), we observe the exact isomorphism:

\[ \gamma = \frac{1}{c}. \]

An investor deploying half-Kelly (\(c = 0.50\)) is mathematically indistinguishable from an expected utility maximizer with constant relative risk aversion \(\gamma = 2.0\). Quarter-Kelly (\(c = 0.25\)) implies \(\gamma = 4.0\).

Lab 1 · Fractional Kelly Growth, Volatility, and Drawdown Risk

Simulates 3,000 5-year paths across c in {1.0, 0.75, 0.50, 0.25}, verifying that c = 0.50 captures 75% growth with 50% volatility and collapses 50% drawdown probability from 50% to 12.5%.

"""Lab PR3-L03-1: Fractional Kelly Sizing, Volatility Drag, and Drawdown Risk.

SIMULATED: Monte Carlo evaluation of growth-volatility trade-off and drawdown probabilities.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_fractional_kelly(
    c_values: list[float],
    mu: float = 0.10,
    r: float = 0.02,
    sigma: float = 0.20,
    d_threshold: float = 0.50,
    t_years: float = 5.0,
    n_steps: int = 1260,
    n_paths: int = 3000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[float, dict[str, float]]:
    """Simulate wealth paths under different fractional Kelly multipliers."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = t_years / float(n_steps)
    f_star = (mu - r) / (sigma**2)  # full Kelly leverage
    results: dict[float, dict[str, float]] = {}

    # Common random standard normal increments across fractions
    z = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, n_steps))

    for c in c_values:
        f = c * f_star
        drift = (r + f * (mu - r) - 0.5 * (f**2) * (sigma**2)) * dt
        diff = f * sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * z
        log_increments = drift + diff
        # Prepend zero for initial log wealth
        log_paths = np.column_stack([np.zeros(n_paths), np.cumsum(log_increments, axis=1)])
        wealth_paths = np.exp(log_paths)

        # Track running maximum and drawdowns
        running_max = np.maximum.accumulate(wealth_paths, axis=1)
        drawdown_paths = (running_max - wealth_paths) / running_max
        max_drawdowns = np.max(drawdown_paths, axis=1)

        # Empirical drawdown probability
        emp_dd_prob = float(np.mean(max_drawdowns >= d_threshold))

        # Theoretical asymptotic drawdown probability: (1 - d)^(2/c - 1)
        if 0.0 < c < 2.0:
            theo_dd_prob = float((1.0 - d_threshold) ** ((2.0 / c) - 1.0))
        elif c <= 0.0:
            theo_dd_prob = 0.0
        else:
            theo_dd_prob = 1.0

        # Excess growth rate and volatility
        theo_excess_g = float(c * (mu - r) * f_star - 0.5 * (c**2) * (f_star**2) * (sigma**2))
        realized_g = float(np.median(log_paths[:, -1]) / t_years)
        port_vol = float(f * sigma)

        results[c] = {
            "leverage": float(f),
            "port_vol": port_vol,
            "theo_excess_g": theo_excess_g,
            "realized_g": realized_g,
            "emp_dd_prob": emp_dd_prob,
            "theo_dd_prob": theo_dd_prob,
            "median_max_dd": float(np.median(max_drawdowns)),
        }

    return results


def main() -> None:
    mu = 0.10
    r = 0.02
    sigma = 0.20
    d_target = 0.50  # 50% drawdown
    c_values = [1.0, 0.75, 0.50, 0.25]

    res = simulate_fractional_kelly(
        c_values, mu=mu, r=r, sigma=sigma, d_threshold=d_target, t_years=5.0, n_steps=1260, n_paths=3000, seed=42
    )

    f_star = (mu - r) / (sigma**2)
    max_excess_g = 0.5 * ((mu - r) ** 2) / (sigma**2)

    print(f"Fractional Kelly Analysis (mu={mu:.2f}, r={r:.2f}, sigma={sigma:.2f}, f*={f_star:.2f}):")
    print(f"Max Excess Growth Rate (Full Kelly): {max_excess_g:.4f}\n")
    print(
        f"{'c':<6} {'Lev f':<8} {'Vol':<8} {'% Growth':<10} {'Median MaxDD':<14} "
        f"{'Emp P(DD>=50%)':<16} {'Theo P(DD>=50%)':<16}"
    )
    print("-" * 80)

    for c in c_values:
        d = res[c]
        pct_growth = (d["theo_excess_g"] / max_excess_g) * 100.0
        pct_str = f"{pct_growth:5.1f}%"
        print(
            f"{c:<6.2f} {d['leverage']:<8.2f} {d['port_vol']:<8.3f} {pct_str:<10} "
            f"{d['median_max_dd']:<14.3f} {d['emp_dd_prob']:<16.4f} {d['theo_dd_prob']:<16.4f}"
        )

Where the Canon Breaks

Fractional Kelly sizing offers elegant closed-form drawdown bounds under continuous diffusions, but breaks under three realistic market conditions:

  1. Discontinuous gap openings: Theorem 3.3 relies on continuous Brownian paths. When asset prices gap downward overnight (e.g. currency peg abandonments or market earnings shocks), realized drawdowns overshoot theoretical thresholds instantaneously.
  2. Path-dependent institutional redemptions: In asset management, investors redeem capital after a 15%–20% drawdown. The theoretical ability to recover geometrically over infinite horizons is severed when external capital outflows force asset sales at the trough.
  3. Time-varying volatility regimes: Scaling by a constant fraction \(c\) does not guarantee constant dollar volatility if market volatility \(\sigma_t\) clusters. In high-volatility regimes, a constant \(c\) exposes the portfolio to severe nominal drawdown spikes unless combined with dynamic volatility targeting.

Misconceptions

  • Fractional Kelly decreases the portfolio's Sharpe ratio.

    In continuous diffusion without financing spreads, scaling leverage by \(c\) scales excess return by \(c\) and volatility by \(c\). The Sharpe ratio \(\frac{c(\mu - r)}{c \sigma} = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma}\) is completely invariant to the fractional multiplier.

  • Half-Kelly cuts the growth rate in half.

    Because volatility drag is quadratic in leverage, half-Kelly retains exactly 75% of maximum excess compounding (\(c - \frac{1}{2}c^2 = 0.5 - 0.125 = 0.375 = 0.75 \times 0.5\)), not 50%.

  • Drawdown risk scales linearly with the Kelly fraction.

    Drawdown probability decays as a steep power law \((1 - d)^{\frac{2}{c} - 1}\). Moving from full Kelly (\(c=1\)) to half-Kelly (\(c=0.5\)) cubes the probability base, collapsing the likelihood of severe drawdowns by a factor of four.

Problem Set

Mechanics

An asset has excess return \(\mu - r = 0.09\) and volatility \(\sigma = 0.18\). Calculate full Kelly leverage \(f^*\) and half-Kelly leverage \(f_{0.5}\). Compute the continuous compounding rate under both.

Solution

Full Kelly leverage is \(f^* = \frac{0.09}{0.18^2} = \frac{0.09}{0.0324} = 2.7778\). Half-Kelly leverage is \(f_{0.5} = 0.5 f^* = 1.3889\). The Sharpe ratio is \(\operatorname{SR} = \frac{0.09}{0.18} = 0.50\). Under full Kelly, excess growth is \(0.5 \operatorname{SR}^2 = 0.5(0.25) = 0.1250\) (12.5%). Under half-Kelly, excess growth is \(0.75(0.1250) = 0.09375\) (9.375%).

Calculate the asymptotic probability of suffering a 30% drawdown (\(d = 0.30\)) under full Kelly (\(c = 1.0\)), half-Kelly (\(c = 0.50\)), and quarter-Kelly (\(c = 0.25\)).

Solution

For \(d = 0.30\), \(1 - d = 0.70\). For \(c = 1.0\), exponent is \(\frac{2}{1} - 1 = 1\), so \(P = 0.70^1 = 0.7000\) (70.0%). For \(c = 0.50\), exponent is \(\frac{2}{0.5} - 1 = 3\), so \(P = 0.70^3 = 0.3430\) (34.3%). For \(c = 0.25\), exponent is \(\frac{2}{0.25} - 1 = 7\), so \(P = 0.70^7 = 0.0824\) (8.24%).

An investor demands that the asymptotic probability of a 50% drawdown be strictly less than 1.0%. Determine the maximum fractional Kelly multiplier \(c\) permitted.

Solution

Set \((1 - 0.50)^{\frac{2}{c} - 1} \le 0.01 \implies (0.50)^{\frac{2}{c} - 1} \le 0.01\). Taking natural logs: \((\frac{2}{c} - 1) \ln(0.50) \le \ln(0.01)\). Since \(\ln(0.50) < 0\), divide and reverse: \(\frac{2}{c} - 1 \ge \frac{\ln(0.01)}{\ln(0.50)} = \frac{-4.6052}{-0.6931} = 6.6439\). Then \(\frac{2}{c} \ge 7.6439 \implies c \le \frac{2}{7.6439} \approx 0.2616\). The investor must bet at most 26.16% of full Kelly.

Proof

Prove that the ratio of expected doubling times between half-Kelly and full Kelly in continuous diffusion is exactly \(4/3\) (a 33.3% increase in time to double).

Solution

The time to double wealth \(W_T / W_0 = 2\) satisfies \(g(c) T = \ln(2) \implies T(c) = \frac{\ln(2)}{g(c)}\). Assuming \(r = 0\) for pure excess compounding, \(g(1.0) = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SR}^2\) and \(g(0.5) = \frac{3}{8}\operatorname{SR}^2\). The ratio of doubling times is \(\frac{T(0.5)}{T(1.0)} = \frac{g(1.0)}{g(0.5)} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SR}^2}{\frac{3}{8}\operatorname{SR}^2} = \frac{1/2}{3/8} = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.3333\). Half-Kelly requires only one-third longer to double capital.

Prove that the marginal growth gain per unit of portfolio volatility \(\frac{dg_e}{d\sigma_p}\) is strictly decreasing in the fractional multiplier \(c\).

Solution

We have \(\sigma_p(c) = c \operatorname{SR}\) and \(g_e(c) = c \operatorname{SR}^2 - \frac{1}{2} c^2 \operatorname{SR}^2\). Expressing \(g_e\) directly as a function of \(\sigma_p\): \(g_e(\sigma_p) = \operatorname{SR} \sigma_p - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2\). Differentiating with respect to \(\sigma_p\) gives \(\frac{dg_e}{d\sigma_p} = \operatorname{SR} - \sigma_p = \operatorname{SR}(1 - c)\). The second derivative is \(\frac{d^2 g_e}{d\sigma_p^2} = -1 < 0\). Marginal growth per unit volatility declines strictly as leverage increases, hitting zero at full Kelly (\(c=1\)).

Prove that for any \(c \in (0, 1)\), the probability of drawdown \(d\) is strictly convex in \(d\) for all \(c < 1\).

Solution

Let \(P(d) = (1 - d)^\theta\) where \(\theta = \frac{2}{c} - 1\). The first derivative is \(P'(d) = -\theta(1 - d)^{\theta - 1}\). The second derivative is \(P''(d) = \theta(\theta - 1)(1 - d)^{\theta - 2}\). For \(c < 1\), \(\theta = \frac{2}{c} - 1 > 2 - 1 = 1\). Since \(\theta > 1\), \(\theta(\theta - 1) > 0\). For all \(d \in (0, 1)\), \((1 - d)^{\theta - 2} > 0\), establishing that \(P''(d) > 0\) strictly. The drawdown probability function is strictly convex.

Empirical

A CTA trend-following strategy generates an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.60. Compare the 10-year median wealth and 95% worst-case drawdown between full Kelly and half-Kelly.

Solution

With \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.60\) and \(r = 0.02\): Full Kelly growth is \(g^* = 0.02 + 0.5(0.36) = 0.2000\) (20.0%). 10-year median wealth is \(\exp(10 \times 0.20) = 7.39\). Half-Kelly growth is \(g = 0.02 + 0.75(0.18) = 0.1550\) (15.5%). 10-year median wealth is \(\exp(10 \times 0.155) = 4.71\). However, the 95th-percentile drawdown under full Kelly satisfies \(1 - d = 0.05 \implies d = 95\%\), whereas under half-Kelly \((1 - d)^3 = 0.05 \implies 1 - d = 0.368 \implies d = 63.2\%\).

Why do institutional hedge fund mandates typically operate at fractional Kelly levels of \(c \in [0.20, 0.35]\) rather than half-Kelly (\(c = 0.50\))?

Solution

At \(c = 0.50\), the probability of a 30% drawdown is \((1 - 0.30)^3 = 0.343\) (34.3%). Most institutional clients redeem capital after a 15%–20% drawdown. Operating at \(c = 0.25\) yields a 30% drawdown probability of \((0.70)^7 = 8.2\%\), bringing risk within tolerable institutional fiduciary mandates.

In a trading backtest with fat-tailed returns (Student-t with \(\nu = 4\) degrees of freedom), how does the empirical drawdown compare to Theorem 3.3's Gaussian prediction?

Solution

Fat-tailed returns produce jump discontinuities that violate diffusion continuity. In empirical simulations with \(\nu = 4\), extreme return realizations cluster, causing drawdowns to breach theoretical bounds by 15%–30% relative to Gaussian diffusion predictions. Prudent risk management requires discounting \(c\) further under leptokurtic distributions.

Research

Analyze the interaction between fractional Kelly sizing and Grossman-Zhou drawdown control strategies.

Solution

Grossman & Zhou (1993) proved that to enforce a strict hard floor on maximum drawdown \(\operatorname{MDD} \le D_{\max}\), risky asset allocation must scale in proportion to the remaining cushion \(W_t - (1 - D_{\max})M_t\). Combining fractional Kelly with a cushion multiplier provides both optimal compounding in normal regimes and deterministic guarantee against floor breaches.

How does time-varying volatility clustering (GARCH) alter the optimal fractional Kelly multiplier?

Solution

Under stochastic volatility, constant leverage \(f\) induces time-varying growth drag \(\frac{1}{2} f^2 \sigma_t^2\). To preserve the 75/50 half-Kelly invariant dynamically, the manager must implement volatility-targeted fractional Kelly, scaling nominal leverage inversely with conditional volatility \(\sigma_t\).

Evaluate the impact of benchmark tracking error constraints on fractional Kelly sizing.

Solution

When evaluated against an equity benchmark \(S_B\), absolute drawdown metrics are replaced by relative tracking drawdowns. Fractional Kelly sizing applied to excess return over the benchmark preserves logarithmic information ratio growth while bounding underperformance horizons.
