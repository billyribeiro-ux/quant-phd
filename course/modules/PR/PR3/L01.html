The Kelly Criterion

In repeated favorable betting, maximizing expected arithmetic wealth leads to almost sure bankruptcy. An investor betting all capital on a favorable coin maximizes expected wealth yet goes broke with probability approaching one. J. L. Kelly Jr. solved this paradox by demonstrating that capital growth over long sequences depends on the geometric mean. The Kelly criterion maximizes the expected logarithmic growth rate of wealth, establishing the unique sizing boundary that maximizes asymptotic capital accumulation while preventing instantaneous ruin.

1. The Problem of Repeated Bets

Consider an investor facing a sequence of independent, identically distributed favorable investment opportunities. In each round, the investor allocates a fixed fraction \(f \in [0, 1]\) of their current wealth \(W_t\) to the wager.

Definition 1.1 (Binary Bet Structure)

Let \(X_{t+1}\) be a binary random variable representing the net return per unit bet at step \(t+1\):

\[ X_{t+1} = \begin{cases} b & \text{with probability } p, \\ -1 & \text{with probability } q = 1 - p, \end{cases} \]

where \(b > 0\) is the net odds (payoff ratio per dollar wagered), and \(p \in (0, 1)\). The expected edge per dollar wagered is \(\mathbb{E}[X] = bp - q\). The bet is favorable if and only if \(\mathbb{E}[X] > 0\), which requires \(p > \frac{1}{b + 1}\).

Under a proportional betting scheme where fraction \(f\) of wealth is staked at each trial, wealth evolves according to:

\[ W_{t+1} = W_t (1 + f X_{t+1}). \]
Theorem 1.1 (Failure of Expected Wealth Maximization)

Maximizing expected terminal wealth \(\mathbb{E}[W_T]\) requires allocating 100% of wealth (\(f = 1\)) to every favorable bet. However, under this policy, the probability of total bankruptcy converges to one as \(T \to \infty\):

\[ \lim_{T \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(W_T = 0) = 1. \]
Proof of Theorem 1.1

Because trials are independent, expected terminal wealth after \(T\) wagers is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[W_T] &= W_0 \prod_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}[1 + f X_t] \\ &= W_0 (1 + f(bp - q))^T. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(bp - q > 0\), this expected value is strictly increasing in \(f\) on \([0, 1]\). The maximum occurs at \(f^*_{\text{arith}} = 1\). However, when \(f = 1\), a single loss (\(X_t = -1\)) causes \(W_t = W_{t-1}(1 - 1) = 0\). Wealth survives to round \(T\) only if every single toss is a win. Hence:

\[ \mathbb{P}(W_T > 0) = p^T \xrightarrow{T \to \infty} 0. \]

The investor goes broke almost surely despite \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[W_T] = \infty\).

2. The Logarithmic Growth Objective

To eliminate the divergence between typical path realizations and arithmetic expectations, Kelly (1956) proposed maximizing the expected logarithmic growth rate of capital.

Definition 1.2 (Geometric Growth Rate)

The per-period geometric growth rate \(g(f)\) is the expected value of the logarithmic multiplier:

\[ g(f) \equiv \mathbb{E}[\ln(1 + f X)] = p \ln(1 + bf) + q \ln(1 - f). \]
Theorem 1.2 (The Binary Kelly Criterion)

For a favorable binary game with payoff ratio \(b\) and win probability \(p > \frac{1}{b+1}\), the fraction \(f^* \in (0, 1)\) that maximizes \(g(f)\) is unique and given by:

\[ f^* = \frac{bp - q}{b} = p - \frac{q}{b}. \]

If \(bp - q \le 0\), the optimal fraction is \(f^* = 0\).

Proof of Theorem 1.2

Differentiating \(g(f)\) with respect to \(f\) for \(f \in [0, 1)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} g'(f) &= \frac{bp}{1 + bf} - \frac{q}{1 - f} \\ &= \frac{bp(1 - f) - q(1 + bf)}{(1 + bf)(1 - f)} \\ &= \frac{bp - q - f b (p + q)}{(1 + bf)(1 - f)}. \end{aligned} \]

Using the identity \(p + q = 1\), the numerator simplifies to \((bp - q) - b f\). Setting \(g'(f) = 0\) yields:

\[ (bp - q) - b f^* = 0 \implies f^* = \frac{bp - q}{b}. \]

Evaluating the second derivative confirms strict concavity on \([0, 1)\):

\[ g''(f) = -\frac{b^2 p}{(1 + bf)^2} - \frac{q}{(1 - f)^2} < 0. \]

Thus \(f^*\) is the unique global maximum.

3. Asymptotic Dominance and Ruin Boundaries

The Kelly criterion possesses profound asymptotic properties established by Kelly (1956) and MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba (2011).

Theorem 1.3 (Strong Law of Large Numbers for Capital Growth)

Let \(W_T(f)\) denote wealth at step \(T\) under constant fraction \(f\). Then:

\[ \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \ln\left(\frac{W_T(f)}{W_0}\right) = g(f) \quad \text{almost surely.} \]

Consequently, for any alternative strategy with fixed fraction \(f \neq f^*\), the ratio of wealth satisfies:

\[ \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{W_T(f)}{W_T(f^*)} = 0 \quad \text{almost surely.} \]
Theorem 1.4 (Double-Kelly Ruin Boundary)

Let \(f^* > 0\). If the investor wagers exactly double the Kelly fraction, \(f = 2 f^*\), the expected excess log growth is non-positive. If \(f > 2 f^*\), then:

\[ \lim_{T \to \infty} W_T(f) = 0 \quad \text{almost surely.} \]
Proof of Theorem 1.4

By strict concavity of \(g(f)\), \(g(0) = 0\), and \(g'(f^*) = 0\). Using a second-order Taylor expansion around \(f = 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} g(f) &\approx f(bp - q) - \frac{1}{2} f^2 (b^2 p + q) \\ &= f b f^* - \frac{1}{2} f^2 b. \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(g(f) = 0\) reveals the roots \(f = 0\) and \(f \approx 2 f^*\). For any \(f > 2 f^*\), \(g(f) < 0\). By Theorem 1.3, \(\frac{1}{T} \ln W_T \to g(f) < 0\), implying \(\ln W_T \to -\infty\) and \(W_T \to 0\) almost surely.

Lab 1 · Discrete Kelly Compounding and Ruin Boundary

Simulates 1,000 independent 500-step paths under varying bet fractions, verifying that f* = 0.10 maximizes median wealth while 2f* = 0.20 yields zero excess growth and f = 0.25 induces ruin.

"""Lab PR3-L01-1: Discrete Kelly Criterion and Growth Rate Maximization.

SIMULATED: Monte Carlo evaluation of geometric compounding under Kelly sizing.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def run_betting_simulation(
    p: float,
    b: float,
    fractions: list[float],
    n_tosses: int = 500,
    n_paths: int = 1000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[float, dict[str, float]]:
    """Simulate repeated coin tossing across multiple bet fractions."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    results: dict[float, dict[str, float]] = {}

    # Pre-generate Bernoulli outcomes: 1 with prob p, 0 with prob 1-p
    outcomes = rng.random((n_paths, n_tosses)) < p

    for f in fractions:
        # Multipliers: (1 + b * f) on win, (1 - f) on loss
        multipliers = np.where(outcomes, 1.0 + b * f, 1.0 - f)
        # Check for non-positive wealth (ruin)
        if np.any(multipliers <= 0):
            ruined = True
            log_returns = np.zeros(n_paths)
            terminal_wealth = np.zeros(n_paths)
        else:
            ruined = False
            log_mults = np.log(multipliers)
            log_terminal = np.sum(log_mults, axis=1)
            terminal_wealth = np.exp(log_terminal)
            log_returns = log_terminal / float(n_tosses)

        median_w = float(np.median(terminal_wealth)) if not ruined else 0.0
        mean_log_g = float(np.mean(log_returns)) if not ruined else -np.inf
        ruin_rate = float(np.mean(terminal_wealth < 1e-4)) if not ruined else 1.0

        # Theoretical expected log growth rate: p * ln(1 + b*f) + (1-p) * ln(1 - f)
        q = 1.0 - p
        theo_g = p * np.log(1.0 + b * f) + q * np.log(1.0 - f) if 0.0 <= f < 1.0 else -np.inf

        results[f] = {
            "median_wealth": median_w,
            "mean_log_g": mean_log_g,
            "theo_g": float(theo_g),
            "ruin_rate": ruin_rate,
        }

    return results


def main() -> None:
    p = 0.55
    b = 1.0
    q = 1.0 - p
    f_star = (b * p - q) / b  # 0.10

    fractions = [0.025, 0.05, f_star, 0.20, 0.25]
    sim = run_betting_simulation(p, b, fractions, n_tosses=500, n_paths=1000, seed=42)

    print(f"Discrete Kelly Simulation (p={p:.2f}, b={b:.2f}, f*={f_star:.3f}):")
    print(f"{'Fraction':<10} {'Median Wealth':<15} {'Realized g':<12} {'Theoretical g':<15} {'Ruin Prob':<10}")
    print("-" * 65)

    for f in fractions:
        r = sim[f]
        print(
            f"{f:<10.3f} {r['median_wealth']:<15.2f} {r['mean_log_g']:<12.5f} "
            f"{r['theo_g']:<15.5f} {r['ruin_rate']:<10.4f}"
        )

Where the Canon Breaks

In idealized mathematical betting, the Kelly criterion guarantees that wealth never reaches zero because losses scale down proportionally (\(W_{t+1} = W_t(1 - f) > 0\) for \(f < 1\)). In practical financial markets, this continuous divisibility assumption collapses due to three structural boundaries:

  1. Discrete minimum lot sizes: When capital declines, minimum trading units force the effective bet fraction \(f_t\) to spike upward, transforming a fractional loss into total liquidation.
  2. Margin maintenance thresholds: Brokers liquidate leveraged positions when equity falls below maintenance margins (typically 25%–30%), creating an absorbing barrier well above zero wealth.
  3. Discontinuous gap risk: Financial assets exhibit jumps across market closes. A leveraged position sized under the assumption of continuous rebalancing can suffer gap losses exceeding 100% of equity overnight.

Misconceptions

  • The Kelly criterion maximizes expected terminal wealth.

    Expected wealth is maximized by betting 100% of capital on favorable bets (\(f = 1\)), which leads to almost sure bankruptcy (\(\mathbb{P}(W_T = 0) \to 1\)). The Kelly criterion maximizes the median of the wealth distribution and the geometric compounding rate, not the arithmetic mean.

  • Because full Kelly has zero probability of ruin, it is a conservative investment strategy.

    Full Kelly is aggressive. It exhibits extreme volatility and suffers a 50% probability of experiencing a 50% peak-to-trough drawdown over an infinite horizon, as documented by MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba (2011).

  • A bet with positive expectation should always be taken regardless of maximum possible loss.

    If an outcome exists where loss exceeds 100% (\(X \le -1/f\)), the logarithm of terminal wealth is undefined or negative infinity. Even with enormous positive expectation, the Kelly allocation is strictly zero whenever bankruptcy has positive probability.

Problem Set

Mechanics

A trader identifies a statistical arbitrage opportunity with win probability \(p = 0.55\) and even-money payoff (\(b = 1.0\)). Compute the optimal Kelly bet fraction \(f^*\) and the expected logarithmic growth rate \(g(f^*)\).

Solution

With \(p = 0.55\), \(q = 0.45\), and \(b = 1.0\), the Kelly fraction is \(f^* = \frac{bp - q}{b} = \frac{0.55 - 0.45}{1.0} = 0.1000\) (10% of capital). The expected growth rate is \(g(f^*) = 0.55 \ln(1 + 0.10) + 0.45 \ln(1 - 0.10) = 0.55(0.09531) + 0.45(-0.10536) = 0.05242 - 0.04741 = 0.00501\) (0.501% per trade).

An equity options strategy wins with probability \(p = 0.30\) and pays 4-to-1 net (\(b = 4.0\)). Calculate \(f^*\) and compare it to the fraction obtained if \(p\) were 0.20.

Solution

For \(p = 0.30\) and \(b = 4.0\), \(q = 0.70\). The edge is \(bp - q = 4(0.30) - 0.70 = 1.20 - 0.70 = 0.50\). The optimal fraction is \(f^* = \frac{0.50}{4.0} = 0.1250\) (12.5% of capital). If \(p = 0.20\), \(bp - q = 4(0.20) - 0.80 = 0.00\). The edge is zero, so \(f^* = 0.0000\).

For a bet with \(p = 0.60\) and \(b = 1.0\), compute the realized growth rate \(g(f)\) at \(f = f^*\), \(f = 2 f^*\), and \(f = 2.5 f^*\).

Solution

The optimal fraction is \(f^* = 0.60 - 0.40 = 0.20\). At \(f = 0.20\), \(g(0.20) = 0.60 \ln(1.20) + 0.40 \ln(0.80) = 0.60(0.18232) + 0.40(-0.22314) = 0.02014\). At \(f = 2 f^* = 0.40\), \(g(0.40) = 0.60 \ln(1.40) + 0.40 \ln(0.60) = 0.60(0.33647) + 0.40(-0.51083) = -0.00245 \approx 0\). At \(f = 0.50\), \(g(0.50) = 0.60 \ln(1.50) + 0.40 \ln(0.50) = 0.60(0.40547) + 0.40(-0.69315) = -0.03398\), driving wealth to zero exponentially.

Proof

Prove that if an investment has negative expectation (\(bp - q < 0\)), then \(g(f) < 0\) for all \(f \in (0, 1]\), so that the growth-optimal choice is strictly \(f^* = 0\).

Solution

By definition, \(g(0) = 0\). Differentiating \(g(f)\) gives \(g'(0) = bp - q < 0\). Furthermore, the second derivative \(g''(f) = -\frac{b^2 p}{(1 + bf)^2} - \frac{q}{(1 - f)^2} < 0\) for all \(f \in [0, 1)\). Because \(g(f)\) is strictly concave and starts with negative slope at zero, \(g(f) < 0\) for all \(f \in (0, 1)\). Hence the maximum over \([0, 1]\) is achieved uniquely at \(f^* = 0\).

Prove that for any two bet fractions \(f_1, f_2 \in [0, 1)\) with \(g(f_1) > g(f_2)\), the probability that strategy 1 exceeds strategy 2 converges to 1 as \(T \to \infty\).

Solution

Let \(W_T(f) = W_0 \prod_{t=1}^T (1 + f X_t)\). Consider the log ratio \(\frac{1}{T} \ln \frac{W_T(f_1)}{W_T(f_2)} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T [\ln(1 + f_1 X_t) - \ln(1 + f_2 X_t)]\). By Kolmogorov's Strong Law of Large Numbers, this sample average converges almost surely to \(\mathbb{E}[\ln(1 + f_1 X) - \ln(1 + f_2 X)] = g(f_1) - g(f_2) \equiv \Delta > 0\). Because \(\Delta > 0\), \(\ln \frac{W_T(f_1)}{W_T(f_2)} \to \infty\) almost surely, which implies \(\mathbb{P}(W_T(f_1) > W_T(f_2)) \to 1\).

Prove that \(f^* < p\) for all finite odds \(b > 0\) when \(p < 1\).

Solution

Recall the Kelly formula \(f^* = p - \frac{1 - p}{b}\). Since \(p < 1\), the numerator \(1 - p = q > 0\). Given \(b > 0\), the term \(\frac{q}{b} > 0\). Subtracting a strictly positive term from \(p\) yields \(f^* = p - \frac{q}{b} < p\). The optimal fraction is strictly less than the win probability for all finite odds.

Empirical

In a backtest of 1,000 simulated paths with \(p = 0.52\) and \(b = 1.0\) over 250 trading days, compare the median terminal wealth under \(f^* = 0.04\) versus \(f = 0.10\).

Solution

For \(p = 0.52\), \(f^* = 0.04\). The theoretical growth rate is \(g(0.04) = 0.52 \ln(1.04) + 0.48 \ln(0.96) \approx 0.00080\). Over 250 periods, median wealth is \(\exp(250 \times 0.00080) \approx 1.221\) (+22.1%). Under overbetting \(f = 0.10\) (which exceeds \(2f^* = 0.08\)), \(g(0.10) = 0.52 \ln(1.10) + 0.48 \ln(0.90) = -0.00105\). Median wealth is \(\exp(250 \times -0.00105) \approx 0.769\) (-23.1%), illustrating rapid capital erosion.

Explain why an empirical trading desk with positive edge may experience an empirical drawdown of 60% when sizing with full Kelly.

Solution

MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba (2011) prove that under full Kelly betting, the probability of ever suffering a peak-to-trough drawdown of fraction \(d\) is exactly \(1 - d\). For \(d = 0.60\), the probability is \(1 - 0.60 = 0.40\) (40%). Drawdowns of this magnitude are standard sample path properties of growth-optimal sizing rather than evidence of structural edge decay.

A trader estimates win rate \(\hat{p} = 0.58\) from a sample of \(N = 50\) trades. Why is betting the plug-in Kelly fraction hazardous?

Solution

The standard error of \(\hat{p}\) is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/50} \approx \sqrt{0.25/50} \approx 0.07\). The 95% confidence interval for \(p\) spans \([0.44, 0.72]\). If true \(p = 0.48\), the trade has negative edge and betting full sample Kelly leads to exponential ruin. Finite sample uncertainty requires aggressive shrinkage, as developed in Lesson 4.

Research

Discuss the economic relationship between Kelly's information rate maximization and Shannon channel capacity.

Solution

Kelly (1956) proved that when an investor receives noisy signals over a communication channel with capacity \(C\), the maximum achievable geometric growth rate of capital in betting equals the channel transmission rate. Capital growth serves as the financial realization of information theory, linking entropy reduction directly to economic compounding rate.

How do borrowing constraints and borrowing interest spreads modify the unconstrained Kelly fraction in margin accounts?

Solution

When the borrowing rate \(r_b\) exceeds the lending rate \(r_l\), the growth objective becomes piecewise differentiable with a kink at \(f = 1\). If the unconstrained Kelly leverage requires borrowing, the effective edge is reduced by the borrowing spread \((r_b - r_l)\), flattening the growth curve and shifting the optimal leverage downward toward the cash boundary.

Evaluate the impact of transaction costs on high-frequency Kelly rebalancing.

Solution

Maintaining a constant fraction \(f\) requires continuous rebalancing against price fluctuations. Proportional transaction costs generate a no-trade boundary around \(f^*\). Rebalancing only when wealth drift crosses the boundary avoids dissipating the compounding edge in quadratic variation turnover frictions.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the optimal Kelly fraction for a coin toss with win probability \(p = 0.60\) and even odds \(b = 1.0\)?

    \(f^* = \frac{1(0.60) - 0.40}{1} = 0.2000\).

  2. What happens to expected log wealth growth if an investor bets \(f = 2 f^*\) in a favorable game?

    Options

    By concavity and symmetry of the quadratic growth approximation, \(g(2f^*) = g(0) = 0\). Double-Kelly yields zero excess growth.

  3. Why does maximizing expected terminal wealth \(\mathbb{E}[W_T]\) lead to almost sure ruin?

    Options

    \(\mathbb{E}[W_T]\) is maximized by \(f=1\), where a single loss results in total bankruptcy.

  4. What is the optimal Kelly fraction for a game with \(p = 0.40\) and \(b = 1.0\)?

    Because \(bp - q = 1(0.40) - 0.60 = -0.20 < 0\), the edge is negative and \(f^* = 0\).

  5. Which mathematical property guarantees that the Kelly fraction achieves the highest asymptotic wealth?

    Options

    The SLLN guarantees that \(\frac{1}{T}\ln W_T \to \mathbb{E}[\ln(1 + fX)]\) almost surely.

  6. A bet pays 3-to-1 net (\(b = 3.0\)) with win probability \(p = 0.325\). Calculate \(f^*\).

    \(f^* = \frac{3(0.325) - 0.675}{3} = \frac{0.975 - 0.675}{3} = \frac{0.300}{3} = 0.1000\).

  7. Under full Kelly betting, what is the probability of ever suffering a 50% peak-to-trough drawdown?

    Options

    Under full Kelly (\(c=1\)), the drawdown probability formula is \(P(\text{drawdown} \ge d) = (1 - d)^1 = 1 - d\). For \(d = 0.50\), \(P = 0.50\).

  8. If an investment has an outcome with \(X = -1\) (total loss of the bet), what constraint is imposed on \(f\)?

    Options

    If \(f=1\) and \(X=-1\), wealth becomes zero, which is absorbing. To prevent ruin, \(f\) must be strictly less than 1.

  9. For a coin toss with \(p = 0.75\) and even odds \(b = 1.0\), what is \(f^*\)?

    \(f^* = \frac{1(0.75) - 0.25}{1} = 0.5000\).

  10. Who first published the connection between information rate in telecommunications and capital growth in gambling?

    Options

    J. L. Kelly Jr. introduced the criterion in his seminal 1956 Bell System Technical Journal paper.
