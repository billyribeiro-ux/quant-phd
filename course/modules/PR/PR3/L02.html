The Growth-Optimal Portfolio

Transitioning from discrete bets to continuous diffusion markets links the Kelly criterion directly to modern portfolio theory. In continuous time, geometric compounding depends explicitly on instantaneous mean and variance. The multi-asset growth-optimal portfolio (GOP) maximizes the drift of logarithmic wealth, achieving unconstrained leverage proportional to the inverse covariance matrix. Leo Breiman proved that the GOP dominates every other investment policy asymptotically and minimizes the expected time to achieve any arbitrary wealth goal, while serving as the unique strictly positive numeraire under which all deflated asset prices become martingales.

1. Continuous-Time Diffusion Compounding

Let \(N\) risky assets follow geometric Brownian motion diffusions under the physical probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\):

\[ \frac{dS_i(t)}{S_i(t)} = \mu_i dt + \sum_{k=1}^N \sigma_{ik} dW_k(t), \quad i=1,\dots,N, \]

with risk-free asset \(S_0(t) = e^{rt}\). Let \(\bm{\mu} = (\mu_1, \dots, \mu_N)'\) denote the drift vector, \(\bm{\Sigma} = \bm{\sigma}\bm{\sigma}'\) the \(N \times N\) positive definite covariance matrix, and \(\bm{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_N)'\) the vector of portfolio weights allocated to the risky assets.

Definition 2.1 (Continuous Wealth Process)

The wealth process \(W_t\) under self-financing allocation \(\bm{w}\) satisfies the stochastic differential equation:

\[ \frac{dW_t}{W_t} = (r + \bm{w}'(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})) dt + \bm{w}' \bm{\sigma} d\bm{W}_t. \]

Applying Itô's lemma to \(\ln W_t\) yields the continuous growth dynamics:

\[ \begin{aligned} d\ln W_t &= \frac{dW_t}{W_t} - \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{dW_t}{W_t}\right)^2 \\ &= g(\bm{w}) dt + \bm{w}' \bm{\sigma} d\bm{W}_t, \end{aligned} \]

where the deterministic instantaneous geometric growth rate is:

\[ g(\bm{w}) \equiv r + \bm{w}'(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) - \frac{1}{2} \bm{w}' \bm{\Sigma} \bm{w}. \]

2. Multi-Asset GOP Optimal Allocation

The growth-optimal portfolio is obtained by maximizing \(g(\bm{w})\) with respect to the weight vector \(\bm{w}\).

Theorem 2.1 (GOP Weight Vector and Maximum Growth)

Let \(\bm{\Sigma}\) be non-singular. The unconstrained weight vector \(\bm{w}^*\) that maximizes the continuous geometric growth rate \(g(\bm{w})\) is given by:

\[ \bm{w}^* = \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}). \]

The resulting maximum geometric compounding rate is:

\[ g^* \equiv g(\bm{w}^*) = r + \frac{1}{2}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})' \bm{\Sigma}^{-1} (\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) = r + \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SR}_{\max}^2, \]

where \(\operatorname{SR}_{\max}\) is the maximum achievable Sharpe ratio on the tangency portfolio.

Proof of Theorem 2.1

The objective function is strictly concave in \(\bm{w}\) because the Hessian is \(\nabla^2 g(\bm{w}) = -\bm{\Sigma}\), which is negative definite. Differentiating with respect to \(\bm{w}\) and setting the gradient to zero:

\[ \nabla g(\bm{w}) = (\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) - \bm{\Sigma}\bm{w} = \mathbf{0}. \]

Solving for \(\bm{w}\) yields \(\bm{w}^* = \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})\). Substituting \(\bm{w}^*\) back into \(g(\bm{w})\):

\[ \begin{aligned} g(\bm{w}^*) &= r + (\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})'\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) \\ &\quad - \frac{1}{2}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})'\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) \\ &= r + \frac{1}{2}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})'\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) \\ &= r + \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SR}_{\max}^2. \end{aligned} \]

This completes the proof.

Remark 2.1 (Equivalence to Merton Log-Utility)

Notice that \(\bm{w}^*\) is identical to the optimal risky asset allocation in continuous-time stochastic control (Merton 1969) for an investor maximizing logarithmic utility \(U(W) = \ln W\), corresponding to constant relative risk aversion \(\gamma = 1\).

3. Breiman's Dominance and the Numeraire Property

The growth-optimal portfolio occupies a singular position in financial economics, established by Breiman (1961) and detailed in MacLean et al. (2011).

Theorem 2.2 (Breiman's Asymptotic Dominance Theorem)

Let \(W^*(t)\) be the wealth process generated by the GOP \(\bm{w}^*\). Let \(W(t)\) be the wealth generated by any other causal, self-financing investment policy satisfying \(\mathbb{P}(W(t) > 0) = 1\). Then:

\[ \limsup_{t \to \infty} \frac{W(t)}{W^*(t)} \le 1 \quad \text{almost surely.} \]

Furthermore, for any wealth target \(M > W_0\), let \(\tau_M = \inf\{t \ge 0 : W(t) \ge M\}\). The expected stopping time \(\mathbb{E}[\tau_M]\) is asymptotically minimized by the GOP as \(M \to \infty\):

\[ \lim_{M \to \infty} \frac{\mathbb{E}[\tau_M(W^*)]}{\mathbb{E}[\tau_M(W)]} \le 1. \]
Theorem 2.3 (The Numeraire Portfolio Property)

Under the GOP numeraire \(W^*(t)\), every gross return process deflated by \(W^*(t)\) is a local martingale under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\). For any tradeable asset \(S_i(t)\):

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[ \left.\frac{S_i(t)}{W^*(t)} \right| \mathcal{F}_s \right] = \frac{S_i(s)}{W^*(s)}, \quad \forall t \ge s. \]
Proof of Theorem 2.3

Let \(Z_i(t) = \frac{S_i(t)}{W^*(t)}\). Applying Itô's quotient rule to \(Z_i = S_i / W^*\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dZ_i}{Z_i} &= \frac{dS_i}{S_i} - \frac{dW^*}{W^*} + \left(\frac{dW^*}{W^*}\right)^2 - \frac{dS_i}{S_i}\frac{dW^*}{W^*} \\ &= (\mu_i - r - \bm{w}^{*\prime}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})) dt \\ &\quad + \bm{w}^{*\prime}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{w}^* dt - (\bm{\Sigma}\bm{w}^*)_i dt + dM_i(t), \end{aligned} \]

where \(dM_i(t)\) is a local martingale increment. Because \(\bm{w}^* = \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})\), we have \((\bm{\Sigma}\bm{w}^*)_i = \mu_i - r\). The drift term simplifies to:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Drift} &= (\mu_i - r) - (\bm{\Sigma}\bm{w}^*)_i \\ &\quad - \bm{w}^{*\prime}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) + \bm{w}^{*\prime}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{w}^* \\ &= 0 - \bm{w}^{*\prime}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) + \bm{w}^{*\prime}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1}) \\ &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

The drift vanishes identically for all assets \(i=1,\dots,N\). Hence \(Z_i(t)\) is a \(\mathbb{P}\)-martingale.

Lab 1 · Multi-Asset GOP Weights and Numeraire Martingale Property

Verifies that the continuous multi-asset GOP formula achieves growth rate r + 0.5 * SR^2 = 0.1141 and that deflated asset price expectations equal 1.0000 across 20,000 Monte Carlo paths.

"""Lab PR3-L02-1: Continuous-Time Multi-Asset Growth-Optimal Portfolio and Numeraire Property.

SIMULATED: Verification of multi-asset GOP weights, growth rate, and numeraire martingale property.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def compute_gop(mu: np.ndarray, r: float, cov: np.ndarray) -> tuple[np.ndarray, float, float]:
    """Compute continuous multi-asset Growth-Optimal Portfolio weights, Sharpe, and growth rate."""
    excess_mu = mu - r
    w_star = np.linalg.solve(cov, excess_mu)
    port_var = float(w_star @ cov @ w_star)
    port_excess = float(w_star @ excess_mu)
    sharpe = float(np.sqrt(port_excess))
    # Growth rate: r + w*' excess_mu - 0.5 * w*' cov * w*
    growth = float(r + port_excess - 0.5 * port_var)
    return w_star, sharpe, growth


def verify_numeraire_property(
    mu: np.ndarray,
    r: float,
    cov: np.ndarray,
    t_horizon: float = 1.0,
    n_paths: int = 20000,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Verify in Monte Carlo that S_i(t) / W*(t) is a martingale with expectation 1.0."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_assets = len(mu)
    w_star, _, g_star = compute_gop(mu, r, cov)

    # Cholesky factor for asset returns: dS_i / S_i = mu_i dt + sigma_i dW
    chol = np.linalg.cholesky(cov)
    z = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, n_assets))
    diff = z @ chol.T * np.sqrt(t_horizon)

    # Asset prices at t_horizon: S_i(T) = S_i(0) * exp((mu_i - 0.5 * sigma_i^2) * T + diff_i)
    asset_vars = np.diag(cov)
    log_s = (mu - 0.5 * asset_vars) * t_horizon + diff
    s_t = np.exp(log_s)

    # GOP portfolio wealth at t_horizon:
    # dW* / W* = (r + w*' excess_mu) dt + w*' chol dW
    port_vol_shock = diff @ w_star
    log_w_star = g_star * t_horizon + port_vol_shock
    w_star_t = np.exp(log_w_star)

    # Deflated price: (S_i(t) / S_i(0)) / (W*(t) / W*(0))
    deflated = s_t / w_star_t[:, np.newaxis]
    expected_deflated = np.mean(deflated, axis=0)
    return np.asarray(expected_deflated, dtype=np.float64)


def main() -> None:
    r = 0.03
    mu = np.array([0.08, 0.10, 0.12], dtype=np.float64)
    cov = np.array(
        [
            [0.0400, 0.0120, 0.0080],
            [0.0120, 0.0625, 0.0150],
            [0.0080, 0.0150, 0.0900],
        ],
        dtype=np.float64,
    )

    w_star, sharpe, g_star = compute_gop(mu, r, cov)
    deflated_means = verify_numeraire_property(mu, r, cov, t_horizon=1.0, n_paths=20000, seed=42)

    print("Multi-Asset Growth-Optimal Portfolio (GOP):")
    print(f"Risk-free rate: {r:.4f}")
    print(f"Asset Expected Returns: {mu}")
    print(f"GOP Optimal Weights w*: {np.round(w_star, 4)}")
    print(f"Total Leverage sum(w*): {float(np.sum(w_star)):.4f}")
    print(f"Portfolio Sharpe Ratio: {sharpe:.4f}")
    print(f"Continuous Growth Rate g*: {g_star:.4f}")
    print(f"Identity check: r + 0.5 * SR^2 = {r + 0.5 * (sharpe**2):.4f}")
    print("\nNumeraire Martingale Property E[(S_i(t)/W*(t))] (Theoretical = 1.0000):")
    for i, mean_val in enumerate(deflated_means):
        print(f"Asset {i + 1} Deflated Expectation: {mean_val:.4f}")

Where the Canon Breaks

Continuous-time growth-optimal portfolio theory presumes frictionless, continuous portfolio rebalancing. In live institutional execution, the continuous GOP model breaks under three core frictions:

  1. Infinite quadratic variation rebalancing: A continuous diffusion path has non-zero quadratic variation. Maintaining constant weights \(\bm{w}^*\) requires continuous trading, accumulating infinite turnover and dissipating 100% of capital under proportional transaction costs.
  2. Borrowing rate asymmetry: The GOP frequently prescribes substantial gross leverage (\(\sum w_i^* > 1\)). Institutional prime brokers charge financing spreads (e.g. SOFR + 50–150 bps) on cash borrowings, significantly diminishing the net Sharpe ratio and reducing the optimal leverage.
  3. Covariance ill-conditioning: In large asset universes, the sample covariance matrix \(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}\) is ill-conditioned. Inverting \(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}\) magnifies estimation noise, causing GOP weights to fluctuate wildly and incurring catastrophic out-of-sample turnover.

Misconceptions

  • The Growth-Optimal Portfolio is always mean-variance efficient on simple single-period returns.

    The GOP is log-optimal over time, not mean-variance efficient on static simple returns. Because geometric compounding depends on \(\mathbb{E}[\ln(1+R)] \approx \mathbb{E}[R] - \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Var}(R)\), the GOP sits at a single specific point on the continuous capital allocation line, exhibiting far higher variance than conservative Markowitz portfolios (Markowitz 1952).

  • Breiman's theorem implies that the GOP outperforms every other portfolio in every period.

    Breiman's dominance is strictly asymptotic (\(t \to \infty\)). Over any finite horizon, a high-beta portfolio that takes excess risk can outperform the GOP with substantial probability if a favorable market drift persists.

  • The numeraire portfolio property requires the existence of a risk-neutral martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\).

    The numeraire property holds directly under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\). Pricing relative to the growth-optimal portfolio requires no change of measure and makes no assumption of complete markets or risk-neutrality.

Problem Set

Mechanics

A continuous asset has drift \(\mu = 0.12\), volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), and risk-free rate \(r = 0.04\). Compute the continuous Kelly leverage \(f^*\) and maximum growth rate \(g^*\).

Solution

The excess return is \(\mu - r = 0.12 - 0.04 = 0.08\). Variance is \(\sigma^2 = 0.20^2 = 0.04\). Optimal leverage is \(f^* = \frac{0.08}{0.04} = 2.0000\) (200% leverage). The Sharpe ratio is \(\operatorname{SR} = \frac{0.08}{0.20} = 0.40\). Maximum growth rate is \(g^* = r + 0.5 \operatorname{SR}^2 = 0.04 + 0.5(0.16) = 0.04 + 0.08 = 0.1200\) (12.0% per annum).

Consider two uncorrelated assets with excess drifts \(\mu_1 - r = 0.06\) and \(\mu_2 - r = 0.09\), and volatilities \(\sigma_1 = 0.15\) and \(\sigma_2 = 0.30\). Compute the GOP weights \(\bm{w}^* = (w_1^*, w_2^*)'\).

Solution

Because the assets are uncorrelated, \(\bm{\Sigma}\) is diagonal with \(\sigma_1^2 = 0.0225\) and \(\sigma_2^2 = 0.0900\). Optimal weights are \(w_1^* = \frac{0.06}{0.0225} = 2.6667\) and \(w_2^* = \frac{0.09}{0.0900} = 1.0000\). Total portfolio leverage is \(w_1^* + w_2^* = 3.6667\).

For the asset in Problem M1, compute the growth rate achieved if an investor chooses leverage \(f = 1.0\) (no borrowing) versus \(f = 3.0\) (over-leveraged).

Solution

Growth rate formula: \(g(f) = r + f(\mu - r) - 0.5 f^2 \sigma^2 = 0.04 + 0.08 f - 0.02 f^2\). For \(f = 1.0\), \(g(1.0) = 0.04 + 0.08(1.0) - 0.02(1.0) = 0.1000\) (10.0%). For \(f = 3.0\), \(g(3.0) = 0.04 + 0.08(3.0) - 0.02(9.0) = 0.04 + 0.24 - 0.18 = 0.1000\) (10.0%). Note that \(f=3.0\) incurs three times the volatility of \(f=1.0\) for the exact same compounding rate.

Proof

Prove that any portfolio whose leverage exceeds double the continuous Kelly leverage, \(f > 2 f^*\), achieves an expected growth rate strictly below the risk-free rate: \(g(f) < r\).

Solution

Recall \(g(f) = r + f(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2} f^2 \sigma^2\). Substituting \(f^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\) gives \(g(f) - r = f \sigma^2 f^* - \frac{1}{2} f^2 \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 f (2 f^* - f)\). For \(f > 2 f^*\), the term \((2 f^* - f) < 0\). Since \(\sigma^2 > 0\) and \(f > 0\), the product is strictly negative, proving \(g(f) < r\).

Prove that the growth-optimal portfolio weight vector \(\bm{w}^* = \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})\) maximizes the Sharpe ratio along the continuous efficient frontier.

Solution

The Sharpe ratio of portfolio \(\bm{w}\) is \(\operatorname{SR}(\bm{w}) = \frac{\bm{w}'(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})}{\sqrt{\bm{w}'\bm{\Sigma}\bm{w}}}\). Because the Sharpe ratio is scale-invariant (\(\operatorname{SR}(c\bm{w}) = \operatorname{SR}(\bm{w})\) for \(c > 0\)), its maximum is achieved by the tangency direction \(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})\). Since \(\bm{w}^*\) is a positive scalar multiple of this vector, \(\bm{w}^*\) lies exactly on the maximum-Sharpe ray, achieving \(\operatorname{SR}(\bm{w}^*) = \sqrt{(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})'\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1})} = \operatorname{SR}_{\max}\).

Prove that if two risky assets have identical expected returns and volatilities, but correlation \(\rho < 1\), the GOP allocates equal capital to both assets and achieves a higher growth rate than in isolation.

Solution

Let \(\bm{\mu} - r\mathbf{1} = \Delta \mathbf{1}\) and \(\bm{\Sigma} = \sigma^2 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Inverting \(\bm{\Sigma}\) gives \(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2(1 - \rho^2)} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\rho \\ -\rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(\bm{w}^* = \bm{\Sigma}^{-1} \Delta \mathbf{1} = \frac{\Delta}{\sigma^2(1 + \rho)} \mathbf{1}\). Both weights equal \(w^* = \frac{\Delta}{\sigma^2(1 + \rho)}\). The resulting growth rate is \(g^* = r + \frac{\Delta^2}{\sigma^2(1 + \rho)}\). Since \(\rho < 1\), \(1 + \rho < 2\), so \(g^* > r + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta^2}{\sigma^2}\), exceeding the single-asset growth rate.

Empirical

A quantitative fund implements GOP weights on a 10-asset universe. The in-sample annualized Sharpe ratio is 0.80, but out-of-sample realized Sharpe drops to 0.25. Explain the impact on realized geometric growth.

Solution

In-sample sizing sets leverage based on \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.80\), fixing leverage at \(f \propto 0.80\). Out of sample, true \(\operatorname{SR} = 0.25\). Sizing at \(f \approx 0.80/\sigma\) when true optimal leverage is \(0.25/\sigma\) means the fund overbets by a factor of \(0.80 / 0.25 = 3.2 > 2.0\). Because leverage exceeds \(2 f^*\), realized excess growth is negative, driving the fund into severe drawdown.

In a continuous market with daily rebalancing, calculate the annual turnover required to maintain constant weight \(w^* = 2.0\) on an asset with annual volatility \(\sigma = 0.30\).

Solution

Daily return shocks \(\Delta S / S \approx \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} Z\) alter the portfolio weight by \(\Delta w \approx w^*(1 - w^*) \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} Z\). Expected daily turnover is \(\mathbb{E}[|\Delta w|] = |w^*(1 - w^*)|\sigma \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi \times 252}}\). For \(w^* = 2.0\), \(|2(1-2)| = 2.0\). Annual turnover scales as \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N_{\text{steps}}})\), exceeding 300% annually and demonstrating why frictionless continuous rebalancing assumptions fail in practice.

Compare the expected time to reach 10x initial wealth under full Kelly (\(g^* = 0.10\)) versus half-Kelly (\(g = 0.08\)).

Solution

To reach \(W_T / W_0 = 10\), \(\ln(10) \approx 2.3026\). Under full Kelly, expected time is \(T \approx 2.3026 / 0.10 = 23.0\) years. Under half-Kelly, expected time is \(T \approx 2.3026 / 0.08 = 28.8\) years. Half-Kelly requires only 5.8 additional years while cutting volatility in half and collapsing drawdown risk.

Research

Discuss the equivalence between the GOP numeraire pricing framework and stochastic discount factor (SDF) theory.

Solution

In asset pricing, an SDF \(M_t\) satisfies \(\mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1} R_{i,t+1}] = 1\). Long (1990) and MacLean et al. (2011) proved that setting \(M_{t+1} = W^*(t) / W^*(t+1)\) defines an admissible, strictly positive SDF directly under \(\mathbb{P}\). The GOP is the reciprocal of the unique SDF that exists in any arbitrage-free market.

How do gross leverage constraints (\(\sum |w_i| \le L\)) alter the multi-asset GOP optimization problem?

Solution

Adding an \(L_1\)-norm constraint \(\sum |w_i| \le L\) transforms the unconstrained quadratic maximization into a constrained quadratic program. If \(\sum |w_i^*| > L\), the constraint binds, introducing Lagrange multipliers on long and short positions and shrinking smaller allocations to zero, inducing sparsity analogous to lasso regularization.

Analyze Breiman's stopping time minimization theorem when wealth goals are subject to intermediate liquidity requirements.

Solution

Breiman's theorem assumes wealth is unconstrained between \(t=0\) and \(\tau_M\). If the investor faces intermediate liquidity floors or margin calls, path-dependent drawdowns can trigger forced liquidation before \(\tau_M\) is reached, breaking asymptotic optimality and mandating fractional sizing.
