Selection Bias & Censoring in VC Return Data

Unlike public equities traded on continuous transparent exchanges, private venture valuations are observed only intermittently at liquidity events: successful refinancing rounds, initial public offerings, or trade sales. Because struggling and failing startups systematically fail to raise new rounds or exit, their declining valuations are unobserved—a severe form of endogenous selection. Naive empirical return indexes that average observed financing rounds drastically overstate returns, artificially suppress measured volatility, and obscure the true market beta of venture capital. Mastering selection-corrected econometric frameworks is essential for accurate risk premia estimation, institutional portfolio benchmarking, and liability-matching asset allocation.

1The Endogenous Observation Mechanism

In standard asset pricing models, price processes \(S_t\) are assumed to be observed at deterministic, evenly spaced intervals (daily, monthly, quarterly). In private venture capital, enterprise valuation is a latent variable. A market valuation is registered if and only if the firm undertakes a transaction: an equity financing round, a trade acquisition, or an initial public offering.

This transaction process is intrinsically endogenous. High-performing startups rapidly hit technical and commercial milestones, attracting syndicate interest and closing up-rounds every 12 to 18 months. In contrast, underperforming ventures exhaust their runway, fail to attract follow-on investors, and linger as "living dead" companies whose true economic values have collapsed to near zero without registering a recorded transaction. This induces severe selection bias in VC return data.

Definition 1.1 · Selection Bias in Venture Capital Returns

Let \(V_{i, t}\) denote the latent true enterprise value of venture \(i\) at time \(t\), and let \(R_{i, t_1, t_2} = \ln(V_{i, t_2} / V_{i, t_1})\) denote its latent log return over interval \([t_1, t_2]\). Let \(D_i \in \{0, 1\}\) be an observation indicator equal to \(1\) if a transaction occurs at \(t_2\) and \(0\) if the venture is unobserved or censored.

Selection bias in VC return data occurs when the probability of observing a valuation is conditionally dependent on the underlying value trajectory:

\[ \mathbb{P}(D_i = 1 \mid R_{i, t_1, t_2}, \mathbf{X}_i) \ne \mathbb{P}(D_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i) \]

where \(\frac{\partial \mathbb{P}(D_i = 1)}{\partial R_{i, t_1, t_2}} > 0\). Consequently, the conditional expected return of observed rounds systematically exceeds the unconditional population expected return:

\[ \mathbb{E}[R_{i, t_1, t_2} \mid D_i = 1] > \mathbb{E}[R_{i, t_1, t_2}] \]

Korteweg & Sorensen (2010) show that failing to correct for endogenous round observation leads to severe upward bias in venture capital alpha estimates and severe downward bias in venture capital systematic market beta. Because only surviving and growing firms report intermediate valuations, naive repeat-sales or transaction-based indexes suffer from compound survivorship bias.

Theorem 5.1 · Heckman Selection Bias in Log-Linear Venture Returns

Let latent venture log return \(y_i = \mathbf{x}_i' \boldsymbol{\beta} + \epsilon_i\) and selection propensity \(z_i^* = \mathbf{w}_i' \boldsymbol{\gamma} + u_i\), with transaction observed (\(D_i = 1\)) if and only if \(z_i^* > 0\). Assume the error terms follow a bivariate normal distribution:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \epsilon_i \\ u_i \end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0}, \, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_\epsilon^2 & \rho \sigma_\epsilon \\ \rho \sigma_\epsilon & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right) \]

The expected return conditional on observing a transaction satisfies:

\[ \mathbb{E}[y_i \mid D_i = 1, \mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{w}_i] = \mathbf{x}_i' \boldsymbol{\beta} + \rho \sigma_\epsilon \lambda(\mathbf{w}_i' \boldsymbol{\gamma}) \]

where \(\lambda(c) = \frac{\phi(c)}{\Phi(c)}\) is the Inverse Mills Ratio, \(\phi\) is the standard normal PDF, and \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF. Whenever \(\rho > 0\), the naive OLS estimator on observed rounds is strictly upward-biased by \(\rho \sigma_\epsilon \mathbb{E}[\lambda(\mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\gamma}) \mid D = 1] > 0\).

Proof

Conditioning on the event \(D_i = 1 \iff u_i > -\mathbf{w}_i' \boldsymbol{\gamma}\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[y_i \mid D_i = 1] = \mathbf{x}_i' \boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_i \mid u_i > -\mathbf{w}_i' \boldsymbol{\gamma}] \]

By standard properties of the bivariate normal distribution, the regression of \(\epsilon_i\) on \(u_i\) is linear: \(\epsilon_i = \rho \sigma_\epsilon u_i + \nu_i\), where \(\nu_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2(1 - \rho^2))\) is independent of \(u_i\). Therefore:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_i \mid u_i > -\mathbf{w}_i' \boldsymbol{\gamma}] = \rho \sigma_\epsilon \mathbb{E}[u_i \mid u_i > -\mathbf{w}_i' \boldsymbol{\gamma}] \]

For a standard normal variable \(u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), the truncated expectation above threshold \(-c\) is given by the Mills ratio:

\[ \mathbb{E}[u \mid u > -c] = \frac{\int_{-c}^\infty u \phi(u) \, du}{1 - \Phi(-c)} = \frac{\left[ -\phi(u) \right]_{-c}^\infty}{\Phi(c)} = \frac{\phi(c)}{\Phi(c)} = \lambda(c) \]

Setting \(c = \mathbf{w}_i' \boldsymbol{\gamma}\) yields the result. Because \(\phi(c) > 0\) and \(\Phi(c) \in (0, 1)\), \(\lambda(c) > 0\) for all finite \(c\). Since \(\rho > 0\) and \(\sigma_\epsilon > 0\), the selection correction term \(\rho \sigma_\epsilon \lambda(\mathbf{w}_i' \boldsymbol{\gamma})\) is strictly positive, proving the upward bias of naive sample averages.

2The Korteweg & Sorensen (2010) Dynamic Selection Model

While the static Heckman model illustrates the core intuition, venture capital investments involve sequential multi-year holding periods with time-varying macroeconomic market states. To resolve this, Korteweg & Sorensen (2010) formulate a dynamic continuous-time state-space framework estimated via Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).

Korteweg & Sorensen (2010) specify a latent valuation process coupled with an endogenous hazard function for financing and exit events across 15,000+ venture rounds. Let latent enterprise value \(V_{i, t}\) follow a diffusion process driven by systematic public market factors and company-specific shocks:

\[ d \ln V_{i, t} = \left( r_f + \alpha + \beta (r_{m, t} - r_f) - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \right) dt + \sigma_\epsilon dW_{i, t}^\epsilon + \beta \sigma_m dW_t^m \]

where \(\beta\) is the venture asset market beta, \(\alpha\) is abnormal risk-adjusted performance, and \(\sigma_\epsilon\) is idiosyncratic volatility.

Theorem 5.2 · Selection-Corrected Market Beta Discrepancy

Let \(\beta_{\text{naive}}\) denote the OLS regression coefficient of observed venture round returns on public market index returns over the holding interval, and let \(\beta_{\text{true}}\) denote the structural exposure of latent enterprise value to market returns. If market upturns increase both venture value growth and the probability of refinancing or exit, then:

\[ \beta_{\text{naive}} \ll \beta_{\text{true}} \]

Specifically, observed venture returns truncate down-market write-downs, muting the observed sensitivity to market crashes and creating an artificial appearance of market insensitivity.

Proof Intuition

In market down-cycles, true venture valuations fall precipitously due to high operating leverage, low liquidity, and heightened default risk. However, during market crashes, venture capitalists suspend fundraising rounds, extend runways through bridge debt, or delay exits, preventing falling valuations from being recorded as transactional data. Only ventures experiencing extraordinary idiosyncratic success manage to raise equity rounds in down markets. Consequently, the observed return sample during market contractions is heavily censored to include only positive outliers, severely compressing the observed downside slope and leading naive regressions to underestimate \(\beta\) by more than 50%.

Example 5.1 · Empirical Findings of Korteweg & Sorensen (2010)

Korteweg & Sorensen (2010) estimate that the true unconditional market beta of venture capital investments is \(\beta \approx 2.76\) (standard error 0.38), while naive repeat-sales models estimate \(\beta \approx 1.0\text{--}1.2\). Furthermore, after correcting for selection bias and living-dead censoring:

  • The true expected annual return of venture capital across the sample is substantially lower than unadjusted transaction averages, shrinking abnormal alpha \(\alpha\) to near zero.
  • Annualized idiosyncratic volatility \(\sigma_\epsilon\) exceeds 70% to 100%, reflecting massive company-specific uncertainty.
  • The high gross returns of successful venture portfolios represent fair risk compensation for bearing extreme systematic leverage (\(\beta \approx 2.8\)) combined with non-diversifiable illiquidity, rather than free excess alpha.

3Living-Dead Censoring and NAV Stale Pricing

In addition to transaction selection bias in commercial deal databases, institutional investors (Limited Partners) confront reporting bias in quarterly Net Asset Value (NAV) statements.

Phalippou & Gottschalg (2008) analyze private equity and venture performance and demonstrate that interim fund returns are significantly inflated by unrealized NAVs of mature, underperforming portfolio companies. General partners face severe agency conflicts when marking down assets:

Remark 5.1 · The Mechanics of "Volatility Laundering"

General partners report NAVs based on the most recent financing round until a subsequent financing or formal liquidation event occurs. If a company raised capital at a high valuation during a market peak, its recorded NAV remains artificially pegged at that valuation even as macroeconomic interest rates surge and public tech multiples collapse by 60%. This practice creates stale pricing and return smoothing. When evaluated on reported quarterly NAVs, venture funds appear to exhibit low correlation with public equities and low volatility—an illusion commonly termed "volatility laundering." When liquidated cash flows are evaluated using Public Market Equivalent (PME) metrics, the true market beta is fully revealed.

4Econometric Simulation: Quantifying Selection Bias

To demonstrate the magnitude of selection bias, we simulate 10,000 venture companies over a 3-year investment horizon (\(\Delta t = 3.0\) years). Latent company log-valuation growth follows Geometric Brownian Motion with true underlying parameters \(\mu_{\text{true}} = 12.0\%\) and \(\sigma = 50.0\%\). The endogenous observation probability follows the Korteweg & Sorensen (2010) logistic selection function:

\[ \mathbb{P}(\text{Observed} \mid \Delta \ln V) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\gamma (\Delta \ln V - \theta))} \]

with selection sensitivity \(\gamma = 2.5\) and censoring threshold \(\theta = -0.20\).

Lab 1 · Selection Bias & Censoring Simulation

Simulating 10,000 venture companies over a 3-year holding period under Geometric Brownian Motion with true mean return 12.0% and volatility 50.0%. Conditioning observation on value growth via the Korteweg & Sorensen (2010) logistic hazard function produces severe upward bias in the observed sample mean return (predicted gap > +1,500 bps) and censors over 70% of value-destructive companies.

"""Lab 1 for PM2 L05: Selection Bias and Endogenous Censoring in VC Return Data.

SIMULATED: Econometric simulation of venture capital return censoring following Korteweg & Sorensen (2010).
Simulates 10,000 venture companies over a 3-year investment horizon (dt = 3.0 years).
Latent company log-valuation growth follows a Geometric Brownian Motion:
    Delta ln(V) ~ Normal((mu - 0.5 * sigma^2) * dt, sigma * sqrt(dt))
with true underlying parameters:
    mu_true = 0.12 (12.0% true expected annual return)
    sigma = 0.50 (50.0% annual idiosyncratic + systematic volatility).

Endogenous Selection Mechanism:
An intermediate round or exit valuation is observed with probability:
    P(Observed | Delta ln(V)) = 1 / (1 + exp(-gamma * (Delta ln(V) - theta)))
where gamma = 2.5 (selection sensitivity) and theta = -0.20 (censoring threshold).
Firms experiencing value destruction are systematically less likely to refinance or exit,
leaving their declining valuations unobserved (censored).

Standard library and NumPy only; fully deterministic with fixed seed.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(101)
    n_firms = 10_000
    dt = 3.0
    mu_true = 0.12
    sigma = 0.50

    # 1. Latent Value Evolution
    drift = (mu_true - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt
    diffusion = sigma * np.sqrt(dt)
    z = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, n_firms)
    log_growth = drift + diffusion * z
    simple_annualized_returns = np.exp(log_growth / dt) - 1.0

    # True population parameters
    pop_mean_return = float(np.mean(simple_annualized_returns))
    pop_median_return = float(np.median(simple_annualized_returns))

    # 2. Endogenous Selection (Korteweg & Sorensen 2010)
    gamma = 2.5
    theta = -0.20
    p_observed = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-gamma * (log_growth - theta)))
    u = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, n_firms)
    observed_mask = u < p_observed

    # Sample statistics among observed rounds
    observed_annualized_returns = simple_annualized_returns[observed_mask]
    obs_mean_return = float(np.mean(observed_annualized_returns))
    obs_median_return = float(np.median(observed_annualized_returns))

    # Selection bias gap
    bias_mean = obs_mean_return - pop_mean_return
    obs_median_return - pop_median_return

    # Censoring rates for winners vs losers
    losers_mask = simple_annualized_returns < 0.0
    censored_losers_pct = (1.0 - float(np.mean(observed_mask[losers_mask]))) * 100.0
    winners_mask = simple_annualized_returns > 0.30
    censored_winners_pct = (1.0 - float(np.mean(observed_mask[winners_mask]))) * 100.0

    print(f"Total Simulated Ventures: {n_firms:,}")
    print(f"Observed Refinancing / Exit Events: {int(np.sum(observed_mask)):,} ({np.mean(observed_mask) * 100:.1f}%)")
    print(f"True Population Mean Return: {pop_mean_return * 100:.2f}% (Median: {pop_median_return * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"Observed Sample Mean Return: {obs_mean_return * 100:.2f}% (Median: {obs_median_return * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"Selection Bias Gap in Mean Return: +{bias_mean * 100:.2f}% (+{bias_mean * 1e4:.0f} bps)")
    print(f"Censoring Rate for Value-Destructive Ventures: {censored_losers_pct:.1f}%")
    print(f"Censoring Rate for High-Growth Ventures (>30% ret): {censored_winners_pct:.1f}%")


main()

The simulation delivers striking quantitative evidence of how censoring distorts venture analytics:

  • Massive Upward Bias in Observed Mean Returns: The true population mean annualized return across all 10,000 ventures is 3.37% (median -0.60%). However, among the 5,620 ventures that successfully completed a financing round or exit (56.2% observation rate), the observed sample mean annualized return is 19.44% (median 15.11%).

5Where the canon breaks

Canon Breaks: Where Standard Empirical Tools Fail in Venture Data

  • Repeat-Sales Regression: In residential real estate, repeat-sales indexes (Case-Shiller) provide robust price series because house sales are relatively exogenous to price shocks. In venture capital, repeat-sales indexes are severely biased upward because rounds occur because valuation grew, whereas stagnant companies experience no subsequent sales.
  • Reported Fund Volatility: PitchBook and Preqin standard deviation metrics calculated from quarterly NAVs typically report venture volatility between 12% and 18%. This is an artifact of quarterly smoothing and last-round marks. True continuous venture asset volatility exceeds 50% to 80%.
  • The "Tech Decorrelation" Myth: Investors frequently claim venture capital provides decorrelation from public tech equities (e.g., NASDAQ 100). In reality, venture companies are levered claims on the exact same technology growth factors; their true market beta is \(\beta \approx 2.5\text{--}3.0\), making venture an amplified proxy for tech equities rather than a hedge.

6Misconceptions

  1. “Averaging all deals in PitchBook yields an unbiased estimate of venture returns.”

    Databases record funding rounds when announced via press releases or SEC Form D filings. Underperforming startups that quietly fail or shut down without a formal bankruptcy filing do not issue press releases. The dataset is intrinsically conditioned on positive survival.

  2. “If a company has not raised a round in 4 years, it is still worth its last round post-money valuation.”

    Venture enterprise value decays rapidly when product-market traction stalls and runway evaporates. A 4-year-old last-round mark almost invariably overstates value by 80% to 100% ("living-dead" overhang).

  3. “Internal Rate of Return (IRR) solves the selection problem.”

    IRR calculations for unrealized funds rely directly on the general partner's ending NAV. Inflated unrealized valuations propagate directly into inflated interim IRRs, which decay significantly once the fund reaches mandatory 10-year dissolution.

7Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Heckman Selection Correction Calculation

A researcher estimates venture round returns using the Heckman model. The estimated correlation between return shocks and selection shocks is \(\rho = 0.65\), residual standard deviation is \(\sigma_\epsilon = 0.40\), and the average Inverse Mills Ratio across observed rounds is \(\bar{\lambda} = 0.85\). Calculate the exact upward selection bias in observed sample log returns.

Solution

Applying Theorem 5.1, the expected selection bias gap is: \(\text{Bias} = \rho \sigma_\epsilon \bar{\lambda} = 0.65 \times 0.40 \times 0.85 = 0.221 \quad (22.1\% \text{ in log return})\). A naive sample mean that ignores selection overstates expected returns by 22.1 percentage points.

M2 · Logistic Observation Probability

Using the Korteweg & Sorensen (2010) observation probability function from Lab 1 with \(\gamma = 2.5\) and \(\theta = -0.20\), calculate the probability that a venture round is observed if the firm's 3-year log-valuation change is: (a) \(\Delta \ln V = +0.80\) (strong up-round); (b) \(\Delta \ln V = -0.80\) (severe down-market destruction).

Solution

For (a) \(\Delta \ln V = +0.80\): \(z_a = -2.5 \times (0.80 - (-0.20)) = -2.50\). \(\mathbb{P}(\text{Observed}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2.50}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.08208} \approx 0.9241 \implies 92.4\%\). For (b) \(\Delta \ln V = -0.80\): \(z_b = -2.5 \times (-0.80 - (-0.20)) = +1.50\). \(\mathbb{P}(\text{Observed}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{1.50}} = \frac{1}{1 + 4.48169} \approx 0.1824 \implies 18.2\%\). The high-growth firm has a 92.4% chance of being observed, while the failing firm has only an 18.2% chance of being observed (81.8% censored).

M3 · Unsmoothing Venture NAV Returns

Suppose reported quarterly venture fund NAV returns \(r_t^{\text{rep}}\) follow a first-order autoregressive smoothing process: \(r_t^{\text{rep}} = (1 - \phi) r_t^{\text{true}} + \phi r_{t-1}^{\text{rep}}\) with smoothing parameter \(\phi = 0.60\). If the reported return in quarter \(t\) is \(+4.0\%\) and in quarter \(t-1\) was \(+8.0\%\), recover the true economic return \(r_t^{\text{true}}\).

Solution

Rearranging the autoregressive unsmoothing equation: \(r_t^{\text{true}} = \frac{r_t^{\text{rep}} - \phi r_{t-1}^{\text{rep}}}{1 - \phi} = \frac{0.04 - 0.60 \times 0.08}{1 - 0.60} = \frac{0.04 - 0.048}{0.40} = \frac{-0.008}{0.40} = -0.020 \quad (-2.0\%)\). While the GP reported a positive \(+4.0\%\) quarterly return, the true underlying economic asset return was \(-2.0\%\).

Proof

P1 · Derivation of the Inverse Mills Ratio Properties

Prove that the Inverse Mills Ratio \(\lambda(c) = \frac{\phi(c)}{\Phi(c)}\) satisfies \(\lambda'(c) = -\lambda(c)[c + \lambda(c)] < 0\) for all \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), and interpret this economically for venture selection.

Solution

Using the standard normal derivative \(\phi'(c) = -c \phi(c)\) and the quotient rule: \(\lambda'(c) = \frac{\phi'(c) \Phi(c) - \phi(c) \Phi'(c)}{[\Phi(c)]^2} = \frac{-c \phi(c) \Phi(c) - \phi(c) \phi(c)}{[\Phi(c)]^2} = -c \frac{\phi(c)}{\Phi(c)} - \left(\frac{\phi(c)}{\Phi(c)}\right)^2 = -\lambda(c)[c + \lambda(c)]\). By Mill's ratio inequalities for the standard normal distribution, \(\lambda(c) > -c\) for all \(c\), implying \(c + \lambda(c) > 0\). Since \(\lambda(c) > 0\), it follows that \(\lambda'(c) < 0\) strictly. Economically, as the threshold \(c = \mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\gamma}\) increases (i.e. refinancing becomes easier or the firm has higher observable quality), the severity of selection truncation diminishes, causing the upward bias \(\rho \sigma_\epsilon \lambda(c)\) to decline towards zero.

P2 · Selection-Corrected Market Beta Downward Bias

Prove that when down-market financing rounds freeze due to endogenous selection, naive OLS regression of observed venture returns on market returns underestimates true systematic beta.

Solution

Let true latent model be \(r_{i, t} = \alpha + \beta r_{m, t} + \epsilon_{i, t}\). In market downturns (\(r_{m, t} < 0\)), ventures survive and refinance only if they experience large positive idiosyncratic shocks \(\epsilon_{i, t} > -\theta - \beta r_{m, t}\). Thus, \(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i, t} \mid D=1, r_{m, t}]\) is a strictly decreasing function of \(r_{m, t}\). In the regression of observed returns \(r_{i, t}\) on \(r_{m, t}\), the error term conditional on observation is correlated with the regressor: \(\mathrm{Cov}(r_{m, t}, \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i, t} \mid D=1, r_{m, t}]) < 0\). The naive OLS estimator satisfies \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{naive}} \xrightarrow{p} \beta + \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(r_m, \mathbb{E}[\epsilon \mid D=1, r_m])}{\mathrm{Var}(r_m)} < \beta\). The downward covariance bias proves that naive regressions severely underestimate true systematic venture beta.

P3 · True PME vs. Reported NAV PME

Prove that if an institutional investor computes Kaplan-Schoar PME using reported interim NAV as the terminal cash flow, \(\mathrm{PME}_{\text{interim}} > \mathrm{PME}_{\text{true}}\) whenever unrealized investments are subject to living-dead mark-to-market lag.

Solution

The Kaplan-Schoar PME is defined as \(\mathrm{PME} = \frac{\sum_{t} \frac{D_t}{(1 + r_{m, t})^t} + \frac{\mathrm{NAV}_T}{(1 + r_{m, T})^T}}{\sum_{t} \frac{C_t}{(1 + r_{m, t})^t}}\) where \(D_t\) are cash distributions, \(C_t\) are capital calls, and \(\mathrm{NAV}_T\) is remaining fund net asset value at time \(T\). If living-dead companies are held at stale last-round marks \(V_{\text{last}}\) rather than their true economic salvage value \(V_{\text{salvage}} \ll V_{\text{last}}\), then \(\mathrm{NAV}_T^{\text{rep}} > \mathrm{NAV}_T^{\text{true}}\). Because the denominator (capital calls) and realized cash distributions are fixed historical facts, \(\mathrm{PME}_{\text{interim}} = \mathrm{PME}_{\text{true}} + \frac{\mathrm{NAV}_T^{\text{rep}} - \mathrm{NAV}_T^{\text{true}}}{(1 + r_{m, T})^T \sum \frac{C_t}{(1+r_t)^t}} > \mathrm{PME}_{\text{true}}\).

Empirical

E1 · CAPM Beta Underestimation

A naive regression of observed venture returns yields \(\beta_{\text{naive}} = 1.10\). An econometric selection correction following Korteweg & Sorensen (2010) reveals the true structural beta is \(\beta_{\text{true}} = 2.75\). If the risk-free rate is 4% and the expected equity market risk premium is 6%, calculate the true required expected return on venture equity versus the naive CAPM prediction.

Solution

Naive CAPM expected return: \(\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{naive}}] = 4\% + 1.10 \times 6\% = 4\% + 6.6\% = 10.6\%\). True selection-corrected CAPM expected return: \(\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{true}}] = 4\% + 2.75 \times 6\% = 4\% + 16.5\% = 20.5\%\). The true required return is 20.5%—almost double the naive estimate—showing that high venture returns are risk compensation for high systematic leverage.

E2 · Decomposing Observed Sample Return Bias

An institutional research team analyzes 1,000 venture investments. 500 companies succeed, raising Series B at an average annualized return of +35%. 500 companies fail; of these, only 100 raise an emergency down-round at -20% before shutting down, while 400 are silently liquidated with -100% total loss and never appear in transaction databases. Calculate: (a) the naive observed sample mean return; (b) the true population mean return; (c) the exact basis-point error of the naive index.

Solution

(a) Observed sample contains \(500 + 100 = 600\) companies: \(\bar{R}_{\text{obs}} = \frac{500 \times (+35\%) + 100 \times (-20\%)}{600} = \frac{155\%}{6} \approx +25.83\%\). (b) True population contains all 1,000 companies: \(\bar{R}_{\text{pop}} = \frac{500 \times (+35\%) + 100 \times (-20\%) + 400 \times (-100\%)}{1000} = \frac{-24500\%}{1000} = -24.50\%\). (c) The naive index reports \(+25.83\%\), whereas the true population return is \(-24.50\%\). The selection bias error is \(+25.83\% - (-24.50\%) = +50.33\%\) or 5,033 basis points.

E3 · Volatility Underestimation Under Return Smoothing

If quarterly reported returns follow \(r_t^{\text{rep}} = (1 - \phi) r_t^{\text{true}} + \phi r_{t-1}^{\text{rep}}\) with \(\phi = 0.50\), and true returns \(r_t^{\text{true}}\) are i.i.d. with variance \(\sigma_{\text{true}}^2\), calculate the ratio of reported variance \(\mathrm{Var}(r^{\text{rep}})\) to true variance \(\sigma_{\text{true}}^2\).

Solution

Expanding the AR(1) process as an infinite moving average: \(r_t^{\text{rep}} = (1 - \phi) \sum_{k=0}^\infty \phi^k r_{t-k}^{\text{true}}\). The variance of the sum of independent terms is: \(\mathrm{Var}(r^{\text{rep}}) = (1 - \phi)^2 \sum_{k=0}^\infty \phi^{2k} \sigma_{\text{true}}^2 = \frac{1 - \phi}{1 + \phi} \sigma_{\text{true}}^2\). For \(\phi = 0.50\): \(\frac{\mathrm{Var}(r^{\text{rep}})}{\sigma_{\text{true}}^2} = \frac{1 - 0.50}{1 + 0.50} = \frac{0.50}{1.50} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333\). The standard deviation ratio is \(\sqrt{1/3} \approx 0.577\). Reported volatility is artificially compressed to 57.7% of its true magnitude.

Research

R1 · Bayesian Data Augmentation in MCMC

In Korteweg & Sorensen (2010), how does the Albert & Chib (1993) data augmentation technique allow Gibbs sampling of latent venture valuations for censored companies?

Solution

For censored companies, the exact valuation \(V_{i, t}\) is unobserved; we only observe the discrete indicator \(D_i = 0\), implying the latent selection variable \(z_{i, t}^* \le 0\). Direct maximum likelihood integration over thousands of unobserved latent trajectories is computationally intractable. Data augmentation treats the unobserved latent variables \((\ln V_{i, t}, z_{i, t}^*)\) as missing parameters. In each Gibbs iteration: (1) conditional on structural parameters \((\alpha, \beta, \sigma)\), the algorithm samples latent values from truncated multivariate normal distributions conditioned on \(z^* \le 0\); (2) conditional on the augmented complete data trajectories, standard linear regression posterior distributions are sampled. This converts an intractable censored likelihood into standard conjugate updates.

R2 · Secondary Market Discounts on Mature Funds

An institutional LP seeks to sell its stake in a 2014 vintage venture fund in year 2024 (Fund age = 10 years). The GP reports an ending NAV of $50M, consisting of 6 remaining companies that have not raised capital or exited in over 5 years. Secondary market buyers bid $15M for the position (a 70% discount to reported NAV). Using the findings of Phalippou & Gottschalg (2008) and Korteweg & Sorensen (2010), explain whether this 70% secondary discount reflects an extreme market dislocation or an accurate selection correction.

Solution

The 70% discount reflects an accurate market adjustment for endogenous censoring and stale NAV marks rather than a liquidity dislocation. In a 10-year-old venture fund, companies that have gone 5 years without a financing round or exit are virtually all living-dead enterprises whose technologies have been superseded and whose key personnel have departed. The GP has avoided writing them down to zero because fund management agreements often calculate asset-based fees or delay admitting impairment until formal legal dissolution. Secondary buyers correctly price the latent economic salvage value ($15M) rather than the unadjusted accounting NAV ($50M).

R3 · Asset Allocation Rebalancing under Structural Beta

A university endowment has a 20% target allocation to Venture Capital, assuming a venture beta of \(\beta = 1.0\) and volatility of \(\sigma = 15\%\). If the true structural beta is \(\beta = 2.8\) and true volatility is \(\sigma = 60\%\) as documented by Korteweg & Sorensen (2010), analyze the endowment's true total portfolio risk exposure and drawdowns during a 40% public technology bear market.

Solution

Under the naive model, the venture sleeve contributes \(0.20 \times 1.0 = 0.20\) units of market beta, and during a 40% tech downturn is projected to lose \(0.20 \times (-40\%) = -8.0\%\) of portfolio value. Under the true structural parameters (\(\beta = 2.8\)), the venture sleeve has a true systemic factor loading of \(0.20 \times 2.8 = 0.56\) units of market beta. During a 40% public tech drawdown, the true economic loss on the venture portfolio is \(2.8 \times (-40\%) = -112\%\) (wiping out the entire equity value of vulnerable, high-burn ventures). The endowment's portfolio-wide loss is more than double its modeled risk tolerance, creating severe liquidity crunches and forced sales of liquid assets.
