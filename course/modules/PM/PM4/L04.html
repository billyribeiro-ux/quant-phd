Option-Adjusted Spread & Effective Risk Metrics

Because mortgage cash flows depend on the historical path of interest rates, static yield-to-maturity and zero-volatility spreads fail to isolate true credit and liquidity premia. The Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) strips out the embedded prepayment option via risk-neutral Monte Carlo simulation, providing an unencumbered compensation metric and enabling effective duration and convexity measurement under common random numbers.

1. Risk-Neutral Short Rate Path Simulation

Valuing mortgage-backed securities under stochastic interest rates requires generating interest rate paths under the risk-neutral pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\). Consider the continuous-time 1-factor short-rate model introduced by Vasicek (1977):

\[ d r_t = \kappa (\theta - r_t) d t + \sigma d W_t^{\mathbb{Q}} \]

where \(\kappa > 0\) is the speed of mean reversion, \(\theta > 0\) is the long-term risk-neutral mean short rate, \(\sigma > 0\) is instantaneous volatility, and \(W_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\) is a standard Brownian motion.

Theorem 4.1 · Exact Discrete Vasicek Transition Law

For a discrete simulation step \(\Delta t\), the conditional distribution of \(r_{t+\Delta t}\) given \(r_t\) is Gaussian with conditional mean and conditional variance:

\[ \E^{\mathbb{Q}}[r_{t+\Delta t} \mid r_t] = r_t e^{-\kappa \Delta t} + \theta (1 - e^{-\kappa \Delta t}) \] \[ \Var^{\mathbb{Q}}(r_{t+\Delta t} \mid r_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} (1 - e^{-2\kappa \Delta t}) \]
Proof

Applying Itô's lemma to \(e^{\kappa t} r_t\):

\[ d (e^{\kappa t} r_t) = \kappa e^{\kappa t} r_t d t + e^{\kappa t} d r_t = \kappa e^{\kappa t} r_t d t + e^{\kappa t} [ \kappa (\theta - r_t) d t + \sigma d W_t ] = \kappa \theta e^{\kappa t} d t + \sigma e^{\kappa t} d W_t \]

Integrating from \(t\) to \(t + \Delta t\):

\[ e^{\kappa (t + \Delta t)} r_{t + \Delta t} - e^{\kappa t} r_t = \theta (e^{\kappa (t + \Delta t)} - e^{\kappa t}) + \sigma \int_t^{t+\Delta t} e^{\kappa s} d W_s \]

Multiplying across by \(e^{-\kappa(t+\Delta t)}\):

\[ r_{t+\Delta t} = r_t e^{-\kappa \Delta t} + \theta (1 - e^{-\kappa \Delta t}) + \sigma \int_t^{t+\Delta t} e^{-\kappa(t+\Delta t - s)} d W_s \]

The stochastic integral has mean zero, yielding the conditional expectation. Using Itô's isometry:

\[ \Var\left( \sigma \int_t^{t+\Delta t} e^{-\kappa(t+\Delta t - s)} d W_s \right) = \sigma^2 \int_t^{t+\Delta t} e^{-2\kappa(t+\Delta t - s)} d s = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} (1 - e^{-2\kappa \Delta t}) \]

Since the integrand is deterministic, the transition distribution is strictly normal.

2. Path-Dependent Cash Flows & Burnout

Unlike European interest rate swaptions, mortgage prepayments exhibit severe path dependence. A low-rate path that occurs after a long period of high rates induces massive refinancing. However, if the same low rates occur after an extended rally, the pool exhibits burnout: the rate-sensitive borrowers have already exited.

Along path \(m \in \{1, \dots, M\}\) at month \(t\), the prevailing refinancing rate is modeled as \(R_t^{(m)} = r_t^{(m)} + m_{\text{orig}}\), where \(m_{\text{orig}}\) is the primary-secondary origination markup. The refinancing incentive is \(\xi_t^{(m)} = \max(0, \text{WAC} - R_t^{(m)})\). Cumulative incentive history tracks pool exhaustion:

\[ \Omega_t^{(m)} = \sum_{s=1}^t \xi_s^{(m)}, \quad B_t^{(m)} = \exp(-\gamma_{\text{burn}} \Omega_t^{(m)}) \]

The conditional prepayment rate is governed by a logistic S-curve scaled by the burnout multiplier:

\[ \text{CPR}_t^{(m)} = \left[ \text{CPR}_{\text{base}} + \frac{\text{CPR}_{\max} - \text{CPR}_{\text{base}}}{1 + \exp\left(-\beta (\text{WAC} - R_t^{(m)} - \mu)\right)} \right] \cdot B_t^{(m)} \]

The single monthly mortality \(\text{SMM}_t^{(m)} = 1 - (1 - \text{CPR}_t^{(m)})^{1/12}\) determines unscheduled principal \(PR_t^{(m)}\), giving total monthly cash flow \(CF_t^{(m)} = I_t^{(m)} + SP_t^{(m)} + PR_t^{(m)}\).

3. Option-Adjusted Spread Calibration via Bisection

The static zero-volatility spread (Z-spread) discounts cash flows along a single deterministic zero curve, penalizing MBS for prepayment options that have not been simulated. In contrast, the Option-Adjusted Spread evaluates expected discounted cash flows across the entire risk-neutral probability space.

Definition 4.1 · Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS)

The Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) is the constant continuous spread \(s\) added to the simulated short rate path \(\{r_t^{(m)}\}\) that equates the risk-neutral expected present value of pathwise cash flows to the observed market price \(P_{\text{mkt}}\):

\[ P_{\text{mkt}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \sum_{t=1}^N CF_t^{(m)} \exp\left( -\sum_{k=1}^t (r_k^{(m)} + s) \Delta t - (r_t^{(m)} + s) \frac{d_{\text{delay}}}{365} \right) \]
Theorem 4.2 · Monotonicity and Existence of Unique OAS

For any positive cash flow stream \(CF_t^{(m)} > 0\), the model price function \(P_{\text{model}}(s)\) is strictly decreasing in \(s\):

\[ \frac{\partial P_{\text{model}}(s)}{\partial s} < 0 \quad \forall s \in \R \]

Consequently, if \(\lim_{s \to -\infty} P_{\text{model}}(s) > P_{\text{mkt}} > \lim_{s \to \infty} P_{\text{model}}(s) = 0\), there exists a unique root \(s^* = \text{OAS}\) solvable via bisection.

Proof

Differentiating the discounted cash flow representation with respect to \(s\):

\[ \frac{\partial P_{\text{model}}}{\partial s} = -\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \sum_{t=1}^N \left( t \Delta t + \frac{d_{\text{delay}}}{365} \right) CF_t^{(m)} \exp\left( -\sum_{k=1}^t (r_k^{(m)} + s) \Delta t - (r_t^{(m)} + s) \frac{d_{\text{delay}}}{365} \right) \]

Since \(t \Delta t + \frac{d_{\text{delay}}}{365} > 0\), \(CF_t^{(m)} > 0\), and the exponential discount factor is strictly positive everywhere, every term in the double summation is strictly positive. The leading negative sign guarantees \(\frac{\partial P_{\text{model}}}{\partial s} < 0\). Strict monotonicity and continuity imply a unique solution by the intermediate value theorem.

Definition 4.2 · Option Cost Decomposition

The total yield premium demanded by investors over the Treasury curve decomposes into option compensation and unencumbered spread:

\[ \text{Option Cost} = \text{Z-spread} - \text{OAS} \]

Because the investor is short the borrower's prepayment call option, \(\text{Z-spread} \ge \text{OAS}\), meaning \(\text{Option Cost} \ge 0\). A wider option cost indicates greater prepayment volatility exposure.

4. Effective Duration and Convexity under Common Random Numbers

Standard analytical duration formulas (e.g., Macaulay duration) assume deterministic cash flows and fail completely for MBS because cash flows shift dynamically when discount rates move.

Definition 4.3 · Effective Duration and Effective Convexity

Let \(P(y)\) be the stochastic Monte Carlo price of an MBS security under calibrated spread \(s^*\). The effective duration and effective convexity under a symmetric yield shift \(\Delta y\) are defined by finite difference quotients:

\[ D_{\text{eff}} = \frac{P(y - \Delta y) - P(y + \Delta y)}{2 P(y) \Delta y} \] \[ C_{\text{eff}} = \frac{P(y + \Delta y) + P(y - \Delta y) - 2P(y)}{P(y) (\Delta y)^2} \]
Remark 4.1 · Common Random Numbers (CRN) Variance Reduction

When computing \(P(y + \Delta y)\) and \(P(y - \Delta y)\), independent Monte Carlo draws introduce simulation noise that swamps the small finite difference \(\Delta y\). Setting identical random seeds across base, up, and down simulations (Common Random Numbers) ensures that rate path perturbations \(\Delta W_t\) are identical across scenarios, isolating the pure derivative signal and drastically shrinking finite-difference variance.

Lab 1 · Option-Adjusted Spread Calibration & Stochastic Greeks

Simulating 500 Vasicek short rate paths with logistic prepayment and pathwise burnout will calibrate OAS for a $102.00 target price to within 0.01 tolerance, yielding a positive duration and confirming negative effective convexity ($C_{\text{eff}} < 0$).

"""Lab PM4-L04-1: Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) and Effective Duration/Convexity.

SIMULATED: Solves for the Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) of an agency MBS pass-through via Monte Carlo
risk-neutral simulation and computes effective duration and convexity under stochastic prepayment.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_vasicek_paths(
    r0: float,
    kappa: float,
    theta: float,
    sigma: float,
    n_months: int = 360,
    n_paths: int = 500,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Simulate exact Gaussian discrete transitions for 1-factor Vasicek short rate."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = 1.0 / 12.0
    exp_k = float(np.exp(-kappa * dt))
    std_step = float(np.sqrt((sigma**2) / (2.0 * kappa) * (1.0 - exp_k**2)))

    rates = np.zeros((n_paths, n_months))
    rates[:, 0] = r0

    for t in range(1, n_months):
        z = rng.normal(size=n_paths)
        rates[:, t] = rates[:, t - 1] * exp_k + theta * (1.0 - exp_k) + std_step * z

    return rates


def generate_path_cash_flows(
    rates: np.ndarray,
    notional: float = 100.0,
    wac: float = 0.065,
    pass_through_rate: float = 0.060,
    base_cpr: float = 0.06,
    max_cpr: float = 0.40,
    beta: float = 60.0,
    mu: float = 0.010,
    margin: float = 0.012,
    gamma_burnout: float = 0.05,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Generate path-dependent monthly cash flows under state-dependent prepayment and burnout."""
    n_paths, n_months = rates.shape
    wac_m = wac / 12.0
    net_m = pass_through_rate / 12.0

    balances = np.full(n_paths, notional)
    cumulative_incentive = np.zeros(n_paths)
    cash_flows = np.zeros((n_paths, n_months))

    for t in range(n_months):
        rem_m = n_months - t
        refi_rate = rates[:, t] + margin
        incentive = np.maximum(0.0, wac - refi_rate)
        cumulative_incentive += incentive
        burnout = np.exp(-gamma_burnout * cumulative_incentive)

        # Logistic refinancing incentive multiplied by burnout
        refi_spread = wac - refi_rate - mu
        cpr = (base_cpr + (max_cpr - base_cpr) / (1.0 + np.exp(-beta * refi_spread))) * burnout
        smm_t = 1.0 - (1.0 - cpr) ** (1.0 / 12.0)

        denom = 1.0 - (1.0 + wac_m) ** (-rem_m)
        pmt = balances * (wac_m / denom)
        gross_int = balances * wac_m
        sched_prin = pmt - gross_int
        prepay = (balances - sched_prin) * smm_t
        tot_prin = sched_prin + prepay

        net_int = balances * net_m
        cash_flows[:, t] = net_int + tot_prin
        balances -= tot_prin

    return cash_flows


def price_mc_spread(
    rates: np.ndarray,
    cash_flows: np.ndarray,
    spread: float,
    delay_days: int = 24,
) -> float:
    """Discount pathwise cash flows at path short rates plus spread."""
    dt = 1.0 / 12.0
    cum_r = np.cumsum((rates + spread) * dt, axis=1)
    delay_adj = (rates + spread) * (delay_days / 365.0)
    disc = np.exp(-(cum_r + delay_adj))
    return float(np.mean(np.sum(cash_flows * disc, axis=1)))


def calibrate_oas(
    market_price: float,
    rates: np.ndarray,
    cash_flows: np.ndarray,
    delay_days: int = 24,
) -> float:
    """Solve for OAS equating model expectation to market price via bisection."""
    low_s = -0.05
    high_s = 0.05
    for _ in range(50):
        mid_s = 0.5 * (low_s + high_s)
        p_mid = price_mc_spread(rates, cash_flows, mid_s, delay_days)
        if abs(p_mid - market_price) < 1e-4:
            break
        if p_mid > market_price:
            low_s = mid_s
        else:
            high_s = mid_s
    return float(mid_s * 10000.0)


def main() -> None:
    r0 = 0.045
    kappa = 0.15
    theta = 0.050
    sigma = 0.015
    notional = 100.0
    wac = 0.065
    net_c = 0.060
    delay_days = 24

    # Baseline simulation
    rates = simulate_vasicek_paths(r0, kappa, theta, sigma, n_months=360, n_paths=500, seed=42)
    cash_flows = generate_path_cash_flows(rates, notional, wac, net_c)

    p_zero_spread = price_mc_spread(rates, cash_flows, spread=0.0, delay_days=delay_days)
    print("MBS Monte Carlo Simulation Results:")
    print(f"Zero-Spread Model Price:     {p_zero_spread:8.4f}")

    # Market price observation
    target_market_price = 102.00
    oas_bps = calibrate_oas(target_market_price, rates, cash_flows, delay_days=delay_days)
    print(f"Target Market Price:         {target_market_price:8.4f}")
    print(f"Calibrated OAS:              {oas_bps:8.2f} bps")

    # Re-verify price at OAS
    p_at_oas = price_mc_spread(rates, cash_flows, spread=oas_bps / 10000.0, delay_days=delay_days)
    print(f"Price at Calibrated OAS:     {p_at_oas:8.4f}")
    assert abs(p_at_oas - target_market_price) < 0.01

    # Effective Greeks with common random numbers
    dy = 0.0025  # 25 bps
    spread_dec = oas_bps / 10000.0

    rates_up = simulate_vasicek_paths(r0 + dy, kappa, theta + dy, sigma, n_months=360, n_paths=500, seed=42)
    cf_up = generate_path_cash_flows(rates_up, notional, wac, net_c)
    p_up = price_mc_spread(rates_up, cf_up, spread=spread_dec, delay_days=delay_days)

    rates_down = simulate_vasicek_paths(r0 - dy, kappa, theta - dy, sigma, n_months=360, n_paths=500, seed=42)
    cf_down = generate_path_cash_flows(rates_down, notional, wac, net_c)
    p_down = price_mc_spread(rates_down, cf_down, spread=spread_dec, delay_days=delay_days)

    eff_duration = (p_down - p_up) / (2.0 * target_market_price * dy)
    eff_convexity = (p_up + p_down - 2.0 * target_market_price) / (target_market_price * (dy**2))

    print("\nStochastic Effective Risk Metrics:")
    print(f"Price (+25 bps shift):       {p_up:8.4f}")
    print(f"Price (-25 bps shift):       {p_down:8.4f}")
    print(f"Effective Duration:          {eff_duration:8.4f} years")
    print(f"Effective Convexity:         {eff_convexity:8.4f}")

    assert eff_duration > 0.0
    assert eff_convexity < 0.0  # Confirms negative convexity in Monte Carlo OAS model!


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

While OAS is the universal Wall Street benchmark for mortgage relative value, several foundational structural assumptions frequently fail:

  • Yield Curve Non-Parallel Shifts (Key Rate Risk): OAS duration assumes uniform parallel shifts across all maturities. In reality, yield curve steepening and twisting shocks alter short-term financing costs independently of 10-year mortgage rates, causing parallel OAS hedges to experience severe basis slippage (Gürkaynak et al. 2007).
  • Model Dependency and OAS Dispersion: Because OAS is a model-derived residual rather than a directly traded market price, different dealer models (e.g., Black-Derman-Toy vs Hull-White vs 2-factor Cheyette) calibrated to the exact same market price produce OAS estimates that differ by 20 to 50 basis points on the same pool.
  • Gaussian Negative Rate Drift in Vasicek: The standard 1-factor Vasicek model permits interest rates to go negative with positive probability \(\Phi\left( -\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 / (2\kappa)}} \right)\). In low-rate environments, unconstrained Gaussian models overestimate prepayment speeds by generating unrealistically negative mortgage borrowing rates.

Misconceptions

  • A wider OAS always indicates that an agency MBS pass-through is fundamentally cheap and undervalued.

    A wide OAS can reflect unmodeled risks rather than market mispricing. If the prepayment model underestimates prepayment speeds, underestimates interest rate volatility, or ignores liquidity risk, the model will compute an artificially high OAS to reconcile the lower market price.

  • The Option-Adjusted Spread can be directly observed on trade reporting feeds like TRACE.

    OAS is not a traded price or observable quotation. It is a model-dependent calculated metric derived by inverting a risk-neutral term structure model and an econometric prepayment engine against the traded market price.

  • Effective duration and modified duration yield identical risk sensitivities for mortgage securities.

    Modified duration assumes cash flows are fixed and independent of interest rates. Because mortgage cash flows contract in rallies and extend in sell-offs, effective duration is significantly shorter in rallies and longer in sell-offs than static modified duration.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An agency pass-through with par balance $100 has a static Zero-Volatility Spread (Z-spread) of 115 bps and an Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) of 45 bps. Calculate the monetary value of the embedded option cost in basis points, and explain what this spread compensates for.
Solution

Applying Definition 4.2: \[ \text{Option Cost} = \text{Z-spread} - \text{OAS} = 115\text{ bps} - 45\text{ bps} = 70\text{ bps} \] The 70 bps option cost represents the annual yield premium required by the investor to compensate for the borrower's par prepayment option (negative convexity and reinvestment risk). The remaining 45 bps OAS reflects unencumbered compensation for liquidity risk and servicer operational frictions.

In a 1-factor Vasicek model with \(\kappa = 0.20\), \(\theta = 0.05\), and \(\sigma = 0.02\), if the current short rate is \(r_0 = 0.03\), compute the expected short rate \(\E[r_1]\) and the conditional standard deviation \(\sqrt{\Var(r_1)}\) after one year (\(\Delta t = 1.0\)).
Solution

Applying Theorem 4.1 with \(\Delta t = 1.0\): \[ e^{-\kappa \Delta t} = e^{-0.20 \times 1.0} = e^{-0.20} \approx 0.81873 \] The expected short rate after one year is: \[ \E[r_1] = 0.03 \times 0.81873 + 0.05 \times (1 - 0.81873) = 0.02456 + 0.00906 = 0.03362\text{ (3.36\%)} \] The conditional variance is: \[ \Var(r_1) = \frac{0.02^2}{2 \times 0.20} (1 - e^{-2 \times 0.20}) = \frac{0.0004}{0.40} (1 - e^{-0.40}) = 0.0010 \times (1 - 0.67032) = 0.00032968 \] The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{\Var(r_1)} = \sqrt{0.00032968} \approx 0.01816\text{ (1.82\%)}\).

A Monte Carlo OAS engine values an MBS at base price \(P_0 = 102.00\). Shifting the short rate paths by \(\Delta y = \pm 25\text{ bps}\) under common random numbers produces \(P_+ = 101.1690\) and \(P_- = 102.7869\). Calculate the effective duration and effective convexity.
Solution

Effective duration is: \[ D_{\text{eff}} = \frac{P_- - P_+}{2 P_0 \Delta y} = \frac{102.7869 - 101.1690}{2 \times 102.00 \times 0.0025} = \frac{1.6179}{0.5100} \approx 3.1724\text{ years} \] Effective convexity is: \[ C_{\text{eff}} = \frac{P_+ + P_- - 2P_0}{P_0 (\Delta y)^2} = \frac{101.1690 + 102.7869 - 2(102.00)}{102.00 \times (0.0025)^2} = \frac{-0.0441}{102.00 \times 0.00000625} \approx -69.18 \] Negative convexity indicates the security suffers from upside price compression.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that if simulated cash flows \(CF_t^{(m)} > 0\) for all \(t, m\), the Monte Carlo model price \(P_{\text{model}}(s)\) is strictly decreasing and strictly convex in spread \(s\).
Solution

Let discount factor along path \(m\) be \(D_t^{(m)}(s) = \exp\left( -\sum_{k=1}^t (r_k^{(m)} + s)\Delta t - (r_t^{(m)} + s) \frac{d}{365} \right)\). Let \(T_t = t \Delta t + \frac{d}{365} > 0\). Then \(D_t^{(m)}(s) = D_t^{(m)}(0) e^{-s T_t}\). The model price is \(P(s) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m, t} CF_t^{(m)} D_t^{(m)}(0) e^{-s T_t}\). Differentiating with respect to \(s\): \[ \frac{\partial P}{\partial s} = -\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m, t} T_t CF_t^{(m)} D_t^{(m)}(0) e^{-s T_t} < 0 \] since all terms in the sum are positive. Differentiating a second time: \[ \frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial s^2} = +\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m, t} T_t^2 CF_t^{(m)} D_t^{(m)}(0) e^{-s T_t} > 0 \] Because \(T_t^2 > 0\) and \(CF_t^{(m)} > 0\), the second derivative is strictly positive, proving that \(P_{\text{model}}(s)\) is strictly decreasing and strictly convex in spread \(s\).

In a 1-factor Vasicek short-rate model, prove that the stationary long-run probability distribution of \(r_t\) as \(t \to \infty\) is \(\mathcal{N}\left(\theta, \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\right)\).
Solution

From Theorem 4.1, the conditional mean and conditional variance for finite horizon \(t\) starting from \(r_0\) are: \[ \E[r_t \mid r_0] = r_0 e^{-\kappa t} + \theta (1 - e^{-\kappa t}) \] \[ \Var(r_t \mid r_0] = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} (1 - e^{-2\kappa t}) \] Taking the limit as \(t \to \infty\), since \(\kappa > 0\), \(e^{-\kappa t} \to 0\) and \(e^{-2\kappa t} \to 0\): \[ \lim_{t \to \infty} \E[r_t \mid r_0] = 0 + \theta (1 - 0) = \theta \] \[ \lim_{t \to \infty} \Var(r_t \mid r_0] = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} (1 - 0) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} \] Since linear combinations of Gaussian increments remain Gaussian, the limiting distribution is uniquely identified as \(\mathcal{N}\left(\theta, \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\right)\).

Prove that under Common Random Numbers (CRN), the finite-difference estimator variance \(\Var\left(\frac{P(y+\Delta y) - P(y)}{\Delta y}\right)\) is strictly lower than under independent random paths if and only if \(\Cov(P_+, P_0) > \frac{1}{2} \Var(P_+)\).
Solution

Let \(\hat{\Delta} = \frac{P_+ - P_0}{\Delta y}\). Under independent sampling, \(\Cov_{\text{ind}}(P_+, P_0) = 0\), giving: \[ \Var_{\text{ind}}(\hat{\Delta}) = \frac{\Var(P_+) + \Var(P_0)}{(\Delta y)^2} \] Under common random numbers, the same random numbers drive both paths, creating positive correlation: \[ \Var_{\text{CRN}}(\hat{\Delta}) = \frac{\Var(P_+) + \Var(P_0) - 2 \Cov(P_+, P_0)}{(\Delta y)^2} \] Comparing the two variances: \[ \Var_{\text{ind}}(\hat{\Delta}) - \Var_{\text{CRN}}(\hat{\Delta}) = \frac{2 \Cov(P_+, P_0)}{(\Delta y)^2} \] Since bond price realizations are positively co-monotonic across parallel rate shifts (\(\Cov(P_+, P_0) > 0\)), the CRN variance is strictly lower whenever \(\Cov(P_+, P_0) > 0\). In the symmetric limit where \(\Var(P_+) \approx \Var(P_0)\), this reduces directly to the required condition.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Suppose an agency MBS portfolio manager observes that 30-year Fannie Mae 5.5% pass-throughs have an OAS of +25 bps when interest rate implied volatility (swaption vol) is 80 bps, but OAS tightens to -10 bps when swaption vol increases to 110 bps under an unchanged market price. Explain the empirical mechanism behind this OAS movement.
Solution

Higher interest rate volatility increases the probability that the short rate moves deeply into-the-money, expanding the frequency and magnitude of simulated prepayments. In the Monte Carlo pricer, higher volatility makes the embedded borrower call option more valuable, which depresses the zero-spread model price. To match the unchanged observed market price, the model must apply a lower (or negative) spread \(s\) to inflate the discounted cash flow. The tightening of OAS from +25 bps to -10 bps does not mean the bond became richer in reality; it indicates that the model attributed more of the bond's yield concession to option risk.

An empirical dealer model calibrated on Fannie Mae pools shows the following: Pool X (unseasoned, 6 months old) has an OAS of 40 bps and effective duration of 2.5 years. Pool Y (seasoned, 72 months old, heavily burned out) with the same coupon and market price has an OAS of 40 bps but an effective duration of 4.8 years. Explain why identical OAS produces radically different duration metrics.
Solution

The two pools have identical OAS because their current market prices equal model expected cash flows discounted at the same spread. However, Pool X has fresh, unexercised prepayment options with zero burnout; when rates fall, prepayments will surge, contracting duration to 2.5 years. Pool Y is heavily burned out (\(B_t \ll 1\)); its remaining borrowers are financially inert or credit-impaired and will not refinance when rates fall. Consequently, Pool Y's cash flows behave like an option-free bullet bond, maintaining an extended duration of 4.8 years across rate cycles.

Using the Gürkaynak, Sack, and Wright (2007) zero-coupon Treasury curve, a quantitative analyst discovers that calculating MBS OAS using a par curve vs a zero-coupon instantaneous forward curve creates an 18 bps divergence in OAS. Explain the origin of this discrepancy.
Solution

A par curve reflects yields of coupon-bearing bullet bonds, whereas mortgage pathwise Monte Carlo discounting requires instantaneous short rates \(r_t\) consistent with instantaneous forward rates \(f(0, t) = -\frac{\partial \ln P(0, t)}{\partial t}\). In an upward-sloping yield curve, forward rates lie substantially above par yields. Discounting cash flows with short rates calibrated to par yields underestimates future discounting rates in later years, distorting cash flow weights and producing an artificial ~18 bps bias in the calibrated OAS root.

Tier 4 · Research

Compare 1-factor short-rate models (e.g., Vasicek) with 2-factor models (e.g., Cheyette or Hull-White 2F) in mortgage valuation. Why is a 2-factor term structure model necessary to price prepayment burnout accurately?
Solution

In a 1-factor model, all points on the yield curve are perfectly correlated (\(d W_t\)); short-rate moves instantaneously determine long-term mortgage rates. However, empirical refinancing incentives depend on 30-year primary mortgage rates (tied to 10-year swap yields), while discounting depends on the short-term financing rate. A 2-factor model introduces a separate stochastic factor for curve slope/twist, allowing the yield curve to steepen or flatten independently of short-rate level. This uncoupling is essential for modeling burnout, where cumulative refinancing incentive can decouple from instantaneous money-market discounting rates.

During the European sovereign debt crisis and Japanese Abenomics, short-term benchmark rates became negative. Discuss the analytical failures of lognormal short-rate models (e.g., Black-Derman-Toy) in negative rate environments, and evaluate shifted-lognormal or normal (Bachelier) alternatives for mortgage OAS engines.
Solution

Lognormal models enforce \(r_t > 0\) by parameterizing \(d \ln r_t\), making negative interest rates mathematically impossible. In negative rate jurisdictions (e.g., Denmark's mortgage market), lognormal OAS trees completely broke down. To accommodate negative rates, quantitative desks transitioned either to normal models (Vasicek/Bachelier, where \(d r_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)d t + \sigma d W_t\)) or shifted-lognormal models (where \(r_t + \delta > 0\) for displacement \(\delta > 0\)), allowing short rates to enter negative territory while preserving analytical tractability.

Evaluate the impact of Quantitative Easing (QE) mortgage purchases on the econometric calibration of prepayment hazard models. How does central bank intervention distort the relationship between the refinancing spread and empirical prepayments?
Solution

Federal Reserve asset purchases under QE compressed secondary MBS yields, but primary retail mortgage rates did not decline proportionally due to originator capacity constraints and elevated guarantee fees. Prepayment models that use secondary market yields as a proxy for borrower refinancing incentives over-predict prepayment speeds during QE announcements. Econometric models must be recalibrated with endogenous primary-secondary spread dynamics and originator capacity variables to prevent severe mispricing of mortgage duration and OAS.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the core conceptual difference between the Zero-Volatility Spread (Z-spread) and the Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS)?

    Options
    Z-spread does not account for interest rate volatility or the embedded prepayment option. OAS strips out option risk via Monte Carlo simulation.

  2. Why is Common Random Numbers (CRN) essential when computing effective duration and convexity via Monte Carlo simulation?

    Options
    Independent random draws between scenarios introduce Monte Carlo noise that overwhelms the small finite-difference shift \(\Delta y\). CRN cancels this noise.
  3. If a mortgage pass-through has a Z-spread of 115 bps and an OAS of 45 bps, calculate its option cost in basis points.
    \(\text{Option Cost} = \text{Z-spread} - \text{OAS} = 115 - 45 = 70\) bps.
  4. With \(P_0 = 102.00\), \(P_+ = 101.1690\), \(P_- = 102.7869\), and \(\Delta y = 0.0025\), calculate the effective duration to four decimal places.
    \(D_{\text{eff}} = \frac{102.7869 - 101.1690}{2 \times 102.00 \times 0.0025} = \frac{1.6179}{0.5100} \approx 3.1724\) years.

  5. In the Vasicek short-rate model, what happens to the discrete transition variance \(\Var(r_{t+\Delta t} \mid r_t)\) as the mean reversion parameter \(\kappa\) increases?

    Options
    \(\Var(r_{t+\Delta t} \mid r_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa \Delta t})\). As \(\kappa \to \infty\), variance converges to 0.

  6. If implied interest rate volatility increases while market MBS prices remain unchanged, what happens to the model-calibrated OAS?

    Options
    Higher volatility raises option cost \(\text{Option Cost} = \text{Z-spread} - \text{OAS}\); if market price (and thus Z-spread) is fixed, OAS must drop.

  7. Why does a 1-factor short-rate model produce imperfect hedges for mortgage pass-through securities?

    Options
    A 1-factor model has a single Brownian motion, forcing all maturities to move together and missing curve twist/slope risk.

  8. What does pathwise "burnout" represent in the Monte Carlo cash flow generator?

    Options
    Burnout reflects pool exhaustion: as sensitive borrowers refinance out, the remaining pool is less responsive to subsequent low-rate environments.
