Commercial Real Estate Cash Flows, Cap Rates, & REIT Valuation

Commercial real estate valuation bridges direct physical asset pricing and public equity markets. Because GAAP accounting distorts real estate earnings through historical depreciation and ignores property appreciation, real estate financial analysts rely on Net Operating Income (NOI), the Gordon growth capitalization rate, balance-sheet Net Asset Value (NAV) bridges, and NAREIT cash flow metrics (FFO and AFFO) to assess public REIT pricing and private-market premiums.

1. Commercial Property Cash Flow Waterfall

Valuing direct income-producing commercial real estate (office, industrial, multifamily, retail) begins with the property-level operating cash flow waterfall. Unlike corporate EBITDA, property Net Operating Income (NOI) isolates unlevered fundamental earnings before debt service, income taxes, and corporate overhead.

Definition 5.1 · Net Operating Income (NOI)

Let \(PGI\) denote Potential Gross Income (contractual rent plus 100% occupancy recoveries), \(VCL\) denote Vacancy and Credit Loss, and \(OpEx\) denote operating expenses (property taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, and third-party management fees). The fundamental property cash flow metrics are:

\[ EGI = PGI - VCL \] \[ NOI = EGI - OpEx \]

Net Operating Income excludes capital expenditures, tenant improvement allowances, leasing commissions, and financing debt service.

Definition 5.2 · Capitalization Rate

The overall property capitalization rate (\(R\), or cap rate) is the ratio of first-year expected unlevered Net Operating Income to the transaction acquisition price or market property value \(V_0\):

\[ R = \frac{NOI_1}{V_0} \iff V_0 = \frac{NOI_1}{R} \]
Theorem 5.1 · Gordon Growth Equivalence of the Cap Rate

If a property's Net Operating Income is expected to grow indefinitely at a constant compound annual growth rate \(g\), and the investor's unlevered required rate of return (cost of capital) is \(r\), then the fair capitalization rate satisfies:

\[ R = r - g = (y_{\text{rf}} + \text{RP}_{\text{property}}) - g \]

where \(y_{\text{rf}}\) is the risk-free benchmark yield and \(\text{RP}_{\text{property}}\) is the property asset risk premium.

Proof

Under the continuous discounted cash flow framework, the fundamental value of the property is the infinite horizon present value of future Net Operating Income flows growing at rate \(g\):

\[ V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{NOI_t}{(1 + r)^t} = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{NOI_1 (1 + g)^{t-1}}{(1 + r)^t} \]

Factoring out \(\frac{NOI_1}{1 + g}\) and applying the infinite geometric series formula for \(\frac{1+g}{1+r} < 1\) (guaranteed since \(r > g\)):

\[ V_0 = \frac{NOI_1}{1 + g} \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{1 + g}{1 + r} \right)^t = \frac{NOI_1}{1 + g} \cdot \frac{\frac{1+g}{1+r}}{1 - \frac{1+g}{1+r}} = \frac{NOI_1}{1 + g} \cdot \frac{1 + g}{(1 + r) - (1 + g)} = \frac{NOI_1}{r - g} \]

Comparing \(V_0 = \frac{NOI_1}{r - g}\) with Definition 5.2 \(V_0 = \frac{NOI_1}{R}\) establishes the identity \(R = r - g\).

2. Public REIT Ecosystem & Corporate Architecture

Direct real estate assets are illiquid and lumpy. The Real Estate Investment Trust structure allows retail and institutional investors to trade diversified portfolios of commercial real estate on public stock exchanges.

Definition 5.3 · Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT)

A Real Estate Investment Trust is a specialized corporate entity that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate. Under Internal Revenue Code Sections 856–860, a qualified REIT pays zero corporate-level federal income taxes, provided it satisfies strict structural tests:

  • Asset Test: At least 75% of total asset value must consist of real estate assets, cash, or government securities.
  • Income Test: At least 75% of gross income must derive from real property rents, mortgage interest, or property sales gains.
  • Distribution Rule: The entity must distribute at least 90% of its taxable income annually to shareholders in the form of dividends.
  • Ownership Dispersion: Held by a minimum of 100 persons, with no more than 50% of shares owned by 5 or fewer individuals (5/50 rule).
Remark 5.1 · The UPREIT Structure and Section 721 Tax Deferral

Most large publicly traded equity REITs operate as Umbrella Partnership REITs (UPREITs). In an UPREIT, the public REIT entity does not own properties directly; instead, it serves as the general partner of an Operating Partnership (OP). When private real estate owners contribute properties to the portfolio, they receive Operating Partnership Units (OP Units) rather than cash or REIT shares. Under Internal Revenue Code Section 721, this contribution is treated as a tax-deferred exchange of partnership interests, allowing property owners to diversify into a liquid REIT without triggering immediate capital gains taxes until OP units are converted into public common stock.

3. Net Asset Value (NAV) Balance-Sheet Bridge

In equity research, valuing a REIT via GAAP book value is meaningless because accounting rules require real estate to be depreciated on a historical-cost straight-line basis over 27.5 to 39 years, even as properties appreciate in market value. Quantitative valuation relies on the Net Asset Value (NAV) per share bridge, which marks every real estate holding to fair private-market value (Damodaran 2012).

Definition 5.4 · REIT Net Asset Value (NAV) Bridge

Let a REIT own real estate across \(K\) distinct property sectors, where sector \(k\) generates annual Net Operating Income \(NOI_k\) and commands a prevailing market capitalization rate \(R_k\). The balance-sheet bridge proceeds as follows:

  1. Gross Real Estate Value: \(V_{\text{RE}} = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{NOI_k}{R_k}\)
  2. Gross Asset Value (GAV): \(GAV = V_{\text{RE}} + \text{Cash} + \text{Accounts Receivable} + \text{CIP}\) (Construction in Progress)
  3. Net Asset Value (NAV): \(NAV = GAV - (\text{Total Debt} + \text{Preferred Stock} + \text{Other Liabilities})\)
  4. NAV per Share: \(\text{NAV}_{\text{per share}} = \frac{NAV}{N_{\text{shares}} + N_{\text{OP units}}}\)

When the public market stock price \(P\) trades below \(\text{NAV}_{\text{per share}}\), the REIT trades at a discount to NAV (\(\frac{P - NAV}{NAV} < 0\)); when \(P > \text{NAV}_{\text{per share}}\), it trades at a premium to NAV.

4. Non-GAAP Performance Metrics: NAREIT FFO and AFFO

Because GAAP Net Income is heavily depressed by non-cash real estate depreciation, the National Association of Real Estate Investment Trusts (NAREIT) established standardized cash flow metrics to evaluate operating performance and dividend coverage.

Definition 5.5 · Funds From Operations (FFO) & Adjusted FFO (AFFO)

NAREIT Funds From Operations (FFO) adds back non-cash real estate depreciation and removes one-time gains from property sales:

\[ FFO = \text{GAAP Net Income} + \text{Depreciation \& Amortization} - \text{Gains on Sale of Real Estate} \]

Adjusted Funds From Operations (AFFO) (also known as Cash Available for Distribution, CAD) adjusts FFO for recurring capital expenditures required to maintain property quality and strips out straight-line rent accounting accruals:

\[ AFFO = FFO - \text{Recurring Maintenance CapEx} - \text{Straight-Line Rent Adjustment} + \text{Amortization of Debt Costs} \]
Theorem 5.2 · Dividend Sustainability Condition

A REIT dividend distribution \(DIV\) is fundamentally sustainable without external equity dilution or debt borrowing if and only if:

\[ DIV \le AFFO \]

If \(FFO \ge DIV > AFFO\), the REIT is paying dividends out of unreserved capital depreciation, underinvesting in property maintenance.

Proof

AFFO measures the net recurring cash flow generated by operations after paying all cash operating expenses, cash interest, and recurring maintenance capital expenditures required to keep the physical asset base fully productive. Total cash change before financing and external acquisitions is \(\Delta \text{Cash} = AFFO - DIV\). If \(DIV > AFFO\), then \(\Delta \text{Cash} < 0\). The entity must either draw down existing cash reserves, issue new dilutive common equity, take on incremental debt, or defer required maintenance, proving the condition.

"""Lab PM4-L05-1: Commercial Real Estate Cash Flows, Cap Rates, and REIT NAV Valuation.

SIMULATED: Models commercial property NOI, capitalization rate valuation sensitivity,
and the public REIT balance-sheet bridge from Gross Asset Value to NAV per share and FFO/AFFO.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def compute_property_noi(
    gross_potential_rent: float,
    expense_reimbursements: float,
    vacancy_credit_loss_rate: float,
    property_management_rate: float,
    real_estate_taxes: float,
    insurance_utilities_maintenance: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Compute property Net Operating Income (NOI)."""
    pgi = gross_potential_rent + expense_reimbursements
    vacancy_loss = pgi * vacancy_credit_loss_rate
    egi = pgi - vacancy_loss
    management_fee = egi * property_management_rate
    opex = real_estate_taxes + insurance_utilities_maintenance + management_fee
    noi = egi - opex
    return {
        "pgi": pgi,
        "vacancy_loss": vacancy_loss,
        "egi": egi,
        "opex": opex,
        "noi": noi,
    }


def compute_reit_nav(
    sector_nois: dict[str, float],
    sector_cap_rates: dict[str, float],
    cash_and_equivalents: float,
    total_debt: float,
    preferred_equity: float,
    shares_outstanding: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Compute REIT Gross Asset Value and Net Asset Value (NAV) per share."""
    gross_real_estate_value = 0.0
    for sector, noi in sector_nois.items():
        cap_rate = sector_cap_rates[sector]
        gross_real_estate_value += noi / cap_rate

    gross_asset_value = gross_real_estate_value + cash_and_equivalents
    total_obligations = total_debt + preferred_equity
    net_asset_value = gross_asset_value - total_obligations
    nav_per_share = net_asset_value / shares_outstanding

    return {
        "gross_real_estate_value": gross_real_estate_value,
        "gross_asset_value": gross_asset_value,
        "net_asset_value": net_asset_value,
        "nav_per_share": nav_per_share,
    }


def reconcile_ffo_affo(
    gaap_net_income: float,
    depreciation_amortization: float,
    gain_on_property_sales: float,
    recurring_capex: float,
    straight_line_rent_adj: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Reconcile GAAP Net Income to NAREIT FFO and AFFO."""
    ffo = gaap_net_income + depreciation_amortization - gain_on_property_sales
    affo = ffo - recurring_capex - straight_line_rent_adj
    return {
        "gaap_net_income": gaap_net_income,
        "ffo": ffo,
        "affo": affo,
    }

    print(f"Total Operating Expenses:       {prop['opex']:12.2f}")

    print(f"Capitalized Property Value:     {prop_val:12.2f}")
    print(f"Implied Required Return (r):    {cost_of_capital * 100:12.2f} %")

    # 2. REIT Multi-Sector NAV Bridge

    print(f"Total Gross Asset Value:        {nav_data['gross_asset_value']:12.2f}")
    print(f"Total Net Asset Value (NAV):    {nav_data['net_asset_value']:12.2f}")
    print(f"NAV Per Share:                  {nav_data['nav_per_share']:12.2f}")

    print(f"Funds From Operations (FFO):    {cash_metrics['ffo']:12.2f}")
    print(f"Adjusted FFO (AFFO):            {cash_metrics['affo']:12.2f}")

    assert prop["noi"] > 0.0

Where the Canon Breaks

Theoretical models of real estate capitalization and REIT pricing assume frictionless capital markets and perfect transparency, but empirical real estate markets reveal structural fractures:

  • GAAP Depreciation Book Value Distortion: Under US GAAP and IFRS, real estate assets must be carried at depreciated historical cost. For mature REITs holding properties acquired decades earlier, balance sheet book value represents an almost completely fictional understatement of actual economic asset value (Damodaran 2012).
  • Cap Rate Refinancing Maturity Walls: In low-interest-rate environments, cap rates compressed toward 3.5%–4.5%. When central banks rapidly raised benchmark rates by 500 bps, property cap rates expanded, depressing capitalized values by 20%–35%. Commercial borrowers facing maturing debt at 3% interest found themselves unable to refinance at 7% debt yields, triggering distressed sales.
  • Persistent Public NAV Discounts and Agency Frictions: While efficient market theory suggests public REIT shares should trade at NAV per share, empirical REIT equities frequently trade at 15% to 30% discounts to NAV for years. These discounts reflect corporate governance discounts, external management fee structures, capital allocation skepticism, and market pricing of private-market appraisal lags.

Misconceptions

  • A higher capitalization rate implies a higher property market value.

    The capitalization rate is an inverse pricing multiple (\(V_0 = NOI_1 / R\)). A higher cap rate represents a higher required return and lower price multiple, meaning property values drop as cap rates rise.

  • REITs pay high dividend yields because they have superior operating cash profit margins compared to standard corporations.

    High REIT dividend yields are legally mandated by the Internal Revenue Code requirement to distribute at least 90% of taxable income to maintain corporate tax exemption, not necessarily superior operational profitability.

  • Funds From Operations (FFO) accurately represents the free cash flow available to pay shareholder dividends.

    FFO ignores recurring maintenance capital expenditures (roof replacements, HVAC overhauls, tenant improvements). Without deducting these recurring capital charges (which AFFO does), paying 100% of FFO in dividends liquidates physical building capital over time.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A commercial office tower generates Potential Gross Income of $10,000,000 with a 8.0% vacancy loss. Annual operating expenses total $3,200,000. If the market capitalization rate is 6.50%, calculate the Effective Gross Income (EGI), Net Operating Income (NOI), and capitalized property value.
Solution

First, calculate Effective Gross Income: \[ EGI = PGI \times (1 - \text{Vacancy Rate}) = \$10,000,000 \times (1 - 0.08) = \$9,200,000 \] Next, calculate Net Operating Income: \[ NOI = EGI - OpEx = \$9,200,000 - \$3,200,000 = \$6,000,000 \] Capitalizing at 6.50%: \[ V_0 = \frac{NOI}{R} = \frac{\$6,000,000}{0.0650} \approx \$92,307,692.31 \] The property's capitalized fair value is approximately $92.31 million.

A REIT reports GAAP Net Income of $45,000,000, non-cash depreciation and amortization of $80,000,000, and a one-time gain on property sales of $12,000,000. During the year, recurring maintenance capital expenditures were $18,000,000 and straight-line rent adjustments were $4,000,000. Compute NAREIT FFO and AFFO.
Solution

Applying Definition 5.5: \[ FFO = \text{Net Income} + \text{Depreciation} - \text{Gains on Sale} = \$45\text{M} + \$80\text{M} - \$12\text{M} = \$113,000,000 \] Adjusted FFO accounts for recurring maintenance capex and non-cash straight-line rent: \[ AFFO = FFO - \text{Recurring CapEx} - \text{Straight-Line Rent} = \$113\text{M} - \$18\text{M} - \$4\text{M} = \$91,000,000 \] While GAAP Net Income was only $45M, actual sustainable operating cash flow (AFFO) was $91M.

A residential REIT has Gross Real Estate Value of $3,500,000,000, cash of $120,000,000, total debt obligations of $1,400,000,000, and preferred equity of $200,000,000. If there are 50,000,000 common shares and 10,000,000 operating partnership (OP) units outstanding, calculate the Net Asset Value per share.
Solution

First compute Gross Asset Value: \[ GAV = \$3,500,000,000 + \$120,000,000 = \$3,620,000,000 \] Subtract debt and preferred equity obligations to find Net Asset Value: \[ NAV = GAV - (\$1,400,000,000 + \$200,000,000) = \$3,620,000,000 - \$1,600,000,000 = \$2,020,000,000 \] Total diluted share equivalents include both common shares and OP units: \[ N_{\text{total}} = 50,000,000 + 10,000,000 = 60,000,000 \] Compute NAV per share: \[ \text{NAV}_{\text{per share}} = \frac{\$2,020,000,000}{60,000,000} \approx \$33.67\text{ per share} \] This establishes the private market value per public share equivalent.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that if Net Operating Income grows at constant rate \(g\) and all cash flows are discounted at cost of capital \(r > g\), the property value sensitivity to cap rate changes satisfies \(\frac{d V_0}{d R} = -\frac{V_0}{R}\).
Solution

By Theorem 5.1, property value is given by \(V_0 = \frac{NOI_1}{R}\). Differentiating \(V_0\) with respect to the capitalization rate \(R\): \[ \frac{d V_0}{d R} = \frac{d}{d R} \left( NOI_1 R^{-1} \right) = -NOI_1 R^{-2} = -\frac{NOI_1}{R} \cdot \frac{1}{R} \] Substituting \(V_0 = \frac{NOI_1}{R}\): \[ \frac{d V_0}{d R} = -\frac{V_0}{R} \] Expressed in percentage elasticity terms: \[ \frac{d V_0 / V_0}{d R / R} = -1 \] Thus, a 1% relative increase in the capitalization rate produces an exact 1% relative reduction in property value.

Prove that leverage magnifies REIT NAV volatility relative to underlying real estate asset volatility by factor \(\frac{1}{1 - L}\), where \(L = \frac{\text{Debt}}{GAV}\) is the loan-to-value leverage ratio.
Solution

Let \(GAV\) denote Gross Asset Value, \(D\) denote total fixed debt obligations, and \(NAV = GAV - D\). The leverage ratio is \(L = \frac{D}{GAV} \in (0, 1)\), which implies \(NAV = GAV(1 - L)\). Differentiating \(NAV\) with respect to \(GAV\), assuming fixed nominal debt \(D\): \[ d NAV = d GAV \] Dividing by \(NAV\): \[ \frac{d NAV}{NAV} = \frac{d GAV}{NAV} = \frac{d GAV}{GAV(1 - L)} = \left(\frac{1}{1 - L}\right) \frac{d GAV}{GAV} \] Taking variances: \[ \Var\left(\frac{d NAV}{NAV}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{1 - L}\right)^2 \Var\left(\frac{d GAV}{GAV}\right) \] Standard deviations scale by \(\frac{1}{1 - L}\). For a REIT with \(L = 0.50\) (50% leverage), equity NAV volatility is exactly \(2\times\) the underlying physical property volatility.

Prove that if a REIT trades at a persistent price discount to NAV (\(P < \text{NAV}\)), funding new property acquisitions by issuing common equity destroys shareholder value on a per-share NAV basis.
Solution

Let current NAV per share be \(\text{nav}_0 = \frac{NAV_0}{N_0}\). The REIT shares trade at price \(P = (1 - \delta) \text{nav}_0\) where \(\delta \in (0, 1)\) is the NAV discount. Suppose the REIT issues \(\Delta N\) new shares at price \(P\), raising capital \(\Delta C = \Delta N \cdot P\). The REIT invests \(\Delta C\) into direct real estate at fair market cap rates, adding exactly \(\Delta C\) to Gross Asset Value. The new total NAV becomes \(NAV_1 = NAV_0 + \Delta C\), and total shares become \(N_1 = N_0 + \Delta N\). The new NAV per share is: \[ \text{nav}_1 = \frac{NAV_0 + \Delta N \cdot P}{N_0 + \Delta N} = \frac{N_0 \text{nav}_0 + \Delta N (1 - \delta)\text{nav}_0}{N_0 + \Delta N} = \text{nav}_0 \left[ 1 - \frac{\delta \Delta N}{N_0 + \Delta N} \right] \] Since \(\delta > 0\) and \(\Delta N > 0\), the term in brackets is strictly less than 1: \[ \text{nav}_1 < \text{nav}_0 \] Thus, issuing equity at a discount to NAV dilutes NAV per share for existing shareholders, destroying value.

Tier 3 · Empirical

A real estate fund manager evaluates an industrial logistics center. The 10-year Treasury yield is 4.25%, the industrial property risk premium is 250 bps, and long-term industrial rent growth is projected at 2.75% per annum. Calculate the theoretical Gordon growth cap rate and compare it to a market quoted cap rate of 4.75%.
Solution

The total unlevered required cost of capital is: \[ r = y_{\text{rf}} + \text{RP}_{\text{property}} = 0.0425 + 0.0250 = 0.0675\text{ (6.75\%)} \] Applying Theorem 5.1, the theoretical equilibrium capitalization rate is: \[ R = r - g = 0.0675 - 0.0275 = 0.0400\text{ (4.00\%)} \] The market quoted cap rate is 4.75% (75 bps wider than model equilibrium). A wider cap rate means the property is priced at a lower multiple (\(21.05\times\) NOI vs \(25.00\times\) NOI), indicating that the property is relatively undervalued or that market participants anticipate lower future growth or higher macro risks.

During 2022–2023, the Federal Reserve raised rates by 500 bps. A multifamily property originally acquired in 2021 at a 3.75% cap rate for $200,000,000 has seen NOI rise from $7,500,000 to $8,250,000. If the market cap rate expanded to 5.50%, calculate the new property value and percentage change in value.
Solution

The new property value at the 5.50% expanded cap rate is: \[ V_1 = \frac{NOI_1}{R_1} = \frac{\$8,250,000}{0.0550} = \$150,000,000 \] The percentage change in property value is: \[ \frac{V_1 - V_0}{V_0} = \frac{\$150,000,000 - \$200,000,000}{\$200,000,000} = -25.0\% \] Despite a 10% increase in fundamental Net Operating Income ($7.5M to $8.25M), the 175 bps expansion in cap rate drove a 25% decline in total property asset value, illustrating the dominant impact of macro discount rates on real estate valuation.

An equity analyst observes that Public Storage (PSA) trades at $270.00 per share while consensus research estimates its NAV per share at $310.00. Simultaneously, Prologis (PLD) trades at $125.00 per share against an NAV per share of $115.00. Calculate the percentage premium/discount to NAV for both REITs and discuss institutional implications.
Solution

For Public Storage: \[ \text{Premium/Discount}_{\text{PSA}} = \frac{\$270.00 - \$310.00}{\$310.00} = \frac{-\$40.00}{\$310.00} \approx -12.90\%\text{ (Discount)} \] For Prologis: \[ \text{Premium/Discount}_{\text{PLD}} = \frac{\$125.00 - \$115.00}{\$115.00} = \frac{+\$10.00}{\$115.00} \approx +8.70\%\text{ (Premium)} \] Prologis possesses a "cost of capital advantage": because its stock trades at an 8.7% premium to NAV, it can accretively issue common equity to acquire new industrial properties. Public Storage trades at a 12.9% discount, rendering equity issuance dilutive; it must fund growth via retained operating cash flow (AFFO) or property asset recycling.

Tier 4 · Research

Analyze the structural tax advantages of an UPREIT Operating Partnership under Section 721 of the Internal Revenue Code compared to a direct taxable cash sale, and explain why private developers accept illiquid OP units over cash.
Solution

Under IRC Section 721, contributing appreciated property to an Operating Partnership in exchange for OP Units is a non-recognition event for federal and state income taxes. In a direct taxable cash sale, the developer would immediately trigger federal capital gains taxes (20%), state capital gains taxes (up to 13.3%), and Section 1250 depreciation recapture taxes (25%). By accepting OP Units, all capital gains are deferred until the developer chooses to exchange OP units for public REIT common stock or cash. Furthermore, if the developer holds OP units until death, their heirs receive a full step-up in tax basis under Section 1014, permanently eliminating the deferred capital gains liability.

Discuss the economic role of straight-line rent adjustments in long-term commercial leases. Why does NAREIT require subtracting straight-line rent accruals in the transition from FFO to AFFO?
Solution

Under GAAP ASC 842, commercial leases with contractual rent escalators (e.g., $100k in Year 1 escalating 3% annually) must be smoothed by averaging total contractual payments over the lease term and recognizing straight-line revenue. In early lease years, GAAP recognized revenue strictly exceeds cash rent received. This non-cash accounting accrual artificially inflates GAAP Net Income and FFO. AFFO strips out straight-line rent adjustments to isolate actual collected cash flow. Without this adjustment, a REIT paying dividends based on FFO would distribute uncollected paper income, creating cash liquidity shortfalls.

Evaluate Damodaran's (2012) framework on corporate governance discounts in public REIT valuation. Why do externally managed REITs historically trade at wider discounts to NAV than internally managed REITs?
Solution

In an externally managed REIT, property acquisition, leasing, and asset management are contracted to a third-party management company owned by the REIT's founders or sponsors. Management fees are typically calculated as a percentage of Gross Asset Value rather than net profit or per-share performance. This creates severe agency conflicts: external managers are incentivized to grow gross assets through dilutive acquisitions and excessive leverage, regardless of per-share NAV dilution. Public market investors recognize this misalignment and penalize externally managed REITs with persistent 15% to 30% discounts to NAV, higher costs of equity capital, and limited institutional ownership.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. Which of the following expenses is deducted when calculating property Net Operating Income (NOI)?

    Options
    NOI is an unlevered property-level operating metric. It includes operating expenses (taxes, insurance, management) but excludes debt service, income taxes, and depreciation.

  2. According to the Gordon growth cap rate model \(R = r - g\), what happens to property capitalized value if the required return \(r\) increases while expected NOI growth \(g\) remains unchanged?

    Options
    Higher required return expands cap rate \(R\). Because \(V_0 = NOI / R\), property value declines.
  3. If a commercial office building generates annual NOI of $6,000,000 and the prevailing market cap rate is 6.50%, calculate its capitalized value ($).
    \(V_0 = \frac{\$6,000,000}{0.0650} \approx \$92,307,692.31\).

  4. What minimum percentage of taxable net income must a Real Estate Investment Trust distribute annually to shareholders to maintain federal corporate tax exemption?

    Options
    IRC Section 857 mandates a minimum distribution of 90% of REIT taxable income.

  5. What primary structural benefit does an UPREIT Operating Partnership structure offer to private real estate contributors under IRC Section 721?

    Options
    Section 721 treats property contribution to an operating partnership as a tax-deferred exchange, deferring immediate capital gains taxation.
  6. A REIT reports GAAP Net Income of $45M, depreciation of $80M, and a gain on property sale of $12M. Calculate NAREIT FFO ($).
    \(FFO = \$45\text{M} + \$80\text{M} - \$12\text{M} = \$113,000,000\).

  7. Why is Adjusted Funds From Operations (AFFO) superior to FFO as a measure of dividend paying capacity?

    Options
    FFO ignores maintenance capital expenditures. AFFO subtracts recurring capex to measure true sustainable cash available for distributions.

  8. If a REIT's common stock trades at a 15% discount to its Net Asset Value (NAV) per share, what happens if management issues new equity to acquire properties at market cap rates?

    Options
    Issuing equity below NAV per share dilutes existing per-share net asset value, as proven in Theorem 5.3.
