The 2000s Housing Boom & Bust and Subprime Expansion

The 2007–2009 Great Financial Crisis originated in the catastrophic failure of private-label mortgage securitization. By isolating the credit supply shock from local income demand, econometric research proved that securitization fueled unprecedented subprime mortgage expansion, decoupling credit growth from household income, inflating housing bubbles, and unleashing systemic default contagion when home price appreciation halted.

1. The Securitization Credit Supply Shock

Between 2001 and 2006, the United States experienced an extraordinary expansion of residential mortgage debt, culminating in what economic historians term the 2000s housing boom and bust. While early narratives attributed this expansion to rational borrower demand driven by productivity growth or demographic shifts, rigorous empirical work established that the boom was propelled by an exogenous expansion in credit supply mediated by the private-label securitization market (Mian & Sufi 2009).

Definition 6.1 · The Originate-to-Distribute Securitization Model

In the traditional relationship-banking model, mortgage lenders held originations on balance sheet to maturity, bearing full credit default risk. In the originate-to-distribute (OTD) model, non-bank mortgage originators underwrote loans designed for immediate sale to Wall Street investment banks, which pooled and tranched them into Private-Label Mortgage-Backed Securities (PLS) and Collateralized Debt Obligations (CDOs). This severed the underwriter's economic skin-in-the-game, creating acute moral hazard that eroded documentation standards and credit score thresholds.

Theorem 6.1 · Credit Supply Identification (Mian & Sufi 2009)

Let \(i\) index a US zip code, and let \(\text{SubprimeShare}_{i, 1996}\) be the fraction of zip code residents with credit scores below 660 in 1996 (predating the private-label securitization surge). Between 2002 and 2005, mortgage credit growth \(\Delta \ln \text{Debt}_{i, 02-05}\) satisfies:

\[ \Delta \ln \text{Debt}_{i, 02-05} = \alpha + \beta_1 \text{SubprimeShare}_{i, 1996} + \beta_2 \Delta \ln \text{Income}_{i, 02-05} + \epsilon_i \]

Empirically, \(\beta_1 > 0\) while \(\beta_2 \le 0\). Credit growth expanded most rapidly in areas with low or declining income growth, proving that credit was driven by an outward shift in lender credit supply rather than fundamental household income demand.

Proof Outline

Under a standard credit demand hypothesis, households borrow more because their income and labor productivity are expanding (\(\Cov(\Delta \text{Debt}, \Delta \text{Income}) > 0\)). Under a credit supply shock hypothesis, securitization originators target historically credit-constrained or subprime populations who previously faced credit rationing. When lenders relax underwriting constraints, credit flows disproportionately into zip codes with high latent subprime shares, regardless of local economic fundamentals. Mian & Sufi (2009) show that across thousands of US zip codes, the correlation between mortgage credit growth and income growth flipped from positive in 1990–1995 (\(\rho \approx +0.35\)) to negative in 2002–2005 (\(\rho \approx -0.15\)), definitively ruling out income-driven demand.

2. Non-Conforming Mortgage Products & Affordability Engineering

To sustain mortgage volume as home prices escalated far beyond median wages, lenders engineered exotic non-conforming loan products designed to minimize initial monthly payments (Campbell 2006):

  • 2/28 Hybrid Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs): Offered an artificially low fixed "teaser" coupon for the initial 24 months, after which the rate reset to a floating benchmark (e.g., 6-month LIBOR) plus a substantial margin of 500 to 600 bps. Borrowers could service payments only during the teaser window and were structurally dependent on rapid home price appreciation to refinance before payment reset.
  • Option ARMs (Negative Amortization): Allowed borrowers each month to choose between a fully amortizing payment, an interest-only payment, or a minimum payment calculated at a 1% teaser rate. Choosing the minimum payment caused the unpaid principal balance to increase (negative amortization) until hitting a contractual recast cap (typically 115% to 125% of original balance), which triggered an immediate, devastating payment jump.
  • Piggyback Second Liens (80-20 Mortgages): Combined an 80% LTV primary mortgage with a 20% LTV second mortgage, enabling borrowers to purchase homes with zero down payment (100% combined LTV) while circumventing private mortgage insurance requirements.

3. Negative Equity, Foreclosure Cascades, & CDO Failure

When national home price appreciation peaked in mid-2006 and reversed, the structural fragility of subprime debt crystallized.

Definition 6.2 · Double-Trigger Mortgage Default Theory

A homeowner exercises the put option to default on a residential mortgage if and only if two simultaneous conditions occur:

  1. Negative Equity: The market value of the residential property drops below the outstanding mortgage balance (\(V_t < B_t \iff LTV > 100\%\)), placing the borrower's equity option deep out-of-the-money.
  2. Income or Payment Shock: The household suffers an liquidity disruption (job loss, divorce, illness) or faces an ARM rate reset that causes debt service payments to exceed cash flow capacity.

Under positive home price appreciation, underwater borrowers can sell or refinance to avoid default. When home prices fell by 30% nationwide from 2006 to 2009, over 12 million households entered negative equity, converting teaser-rate resets into an uncontrollable wave of residential foreclosures.

Theorem 6.2 · Senior Tranche Attachment Failure in CDOs

Let an ABS CDO pool \(J\) mezzanine tranches of BBB-rated subprime MBS, each having correlation \(\rho_{ij} = \rho\) and marginal default probability \(p\). Under the 1-factor Gaussian copula model, the joint probability of widespread systemic failure is an increasing function of asset correlation \(\rho\). If rating agencies calibrate subordination levels assuming a low pairwise default correlation \(\rho \approx 0.10\) when the realized correlation during a nationwide housing collapse reaches \(\rho \to 0.70\), the actual default probability of the senior AAA-rated tranche increases by several orders of magnitude, causing complete subordination attachment and total loss of principal.

Lab 1 · Mian & Sufi (2009) Panel Simulation & Credit Shock

Simulating a 2,000 zip-code cross-section stratified by 1996 subprime share will confirm that 2002–2005 credit growth is strongly correlated with subprime share (>0.85), negatively correlated with local income growth (<0.0), and directly predicts subsequent 2006–2009 default rates (>0.85).

"""Lab PM4-L06-1: The 2000s Housing Boom and Bust — Subprime Credit Expansion and Default Contagion.

SIMULATED: Replicates the empirical design of Mian & Sufi (2009) evaluating cross-sectional
mortgage credit growth, income growth, and subsequent mortgage default rates across zip-code deciles.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_mian_sufi_panel(
    n_zipcodes: int = 1000,
    seed: int = 101,
) -> dict[str, np.ndarray]:
    """Simulate a cross-section of zip codes reflecting 2002-2005 boom and 2006-2009 bust.

    Parameters
    ----------
    n_zipcodes : int
        Number of simulated zip-code observations.
    seed : int
        RNG seed for reproducibility.

    Returns
    -------
    dict[str, np.ndarray]
        Arrays of subprime share, credit growth, income growth, price appreciation, and default rate.
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    # 1996 Baseline subprime share (fraction with credit score < 660)
    subprime_share = rng.beta(2.0, 5.0, size=n_zipcodes)

    # Income growth 2002-2005: driven by fundamental economic productivity, uncorrelated with subprime
    income_growth = rng.normal(0.08, 0.04, size=n_zipcodes)

    # Credit supply shock: securitization drove mortgage credit into high-subprime zip codes
    # Independent of local income growth (Mian & Sufi 2009 identification)
    credit_growth = 0.10 + 0.85 * subprime_share - 0.15 * income_growth + rng.normal(0.0, 0.03, size=n_zipcodes)

    # House price appreciation 2002-2005: fueled by credit expansion
    hpa_boom = 0.05 + 0.65 * credit_growth + rng.normal(0.0, 0.04, size=n_zipcodes)

    # Default rate 2006-2009: catastrophic default clustered in high-subprime credit-boom areas
    noise = rng.normal(0.0, 0.015, size=n_zipcodes)
    base_def = 0.02 + 0.28 * subprime_share + 0.18 * credit_growth - 0.20 * hpa_boom * 0.1
    default_rate = np.clip(base_def + noise, 0.005, 0.35)

    return {
        "subprime_share": subprime_share,
        "income_growth": income_growth,
        "credit_growth": credit_growth,
        "hpa_boom": hpa_boom,
        "default_rate": default_rate,
    }


def compute_decile_averages(
    sorting_variable: np.ndarray,
    target_variables: dict[str, np.ndarray],
    n_bins: int = 4,
) -> dict[str, list[float]]:
    """Sort zip codes into quartiles and compute conditional group means."""
    quantiles = np.quantile(sorting_variable, np.linspace(0, 1, n_bins + 1))
    results: dict[str, list[float]] = {name: [] for name in target_variables}

    for b in range(n_bins):
        q_low, q_high = quantiles[b], quantiles[b + 1]
        mask = (sorting_variable >= q_low) & (sorting_variable <= q_high)
        for name, series in target_variables.items():
            results[name].append(float(np.mean(series[mask])))

    return results


def main() -> None:
    data = simulate_mian_sufi_panel(n_zipcodes=2000, seed=101)

    targets = {
        "Subprime Share": data["subprime_share"],
        "Credit Growth 02-05": data["credit_growth"],
        "Income Growth 02-05": data["income_growth"],
        "Price Apprec 02-05": data["hpa_boom"],
        "Default Rate 06-09": data["default_rate"],
    }

    quartile_stats = compute_decile_averages(data["subprime_share"], targets, n_bins=4)

    print("Mian-Sufi (2009) Cross-Sectional Zip-Code Quartile Stratification:")
    print("Quartile | Subprime Share | Credit Growth | Income Growth | Price Apprec | Default Rate")
    for q in range(4):
        s_sh = quartile_stats["Subprime Share"][q] * 100
        c_gr = quartile_stats["Credit Growth 02-05"][q] * 100
        i_gr = quartile_stats["Income Growth 02-05"][q] * 100
        p_ap = quartile_stats["Price Apprec 02-05"][q] * 100
        d_rt = quartile_stats["Default Rate 06-09"][q] * 100
        print(f"    Q{q + 1}   | {s_sh:13.2f}% | {c_gr:12.2f}% | {i_gr:12.2f}% | {p_ap:11.2f}% | {d_rt:11.2f}%")

    # Econometric correlation checks (Mian & Sufi core empirical results)
    corr_credit_subprime = float(np.corrcoef(data["credit_growth"], data["subprime_share"])[0, 1])
    corr_credit_income = float(np.corrcoef(data["credit_growth"], data["income_growth"])[0, 1])
    corr_default_credit = float(np.corrcoef(data["default_rate"], data["credit_growth"])[0, 1])

    print("\nEmpirical Correlation Matrix:")
    print(f"Corr(Credit Growth, Subprime Share): {corr_credit_subprime:7.4f}")
    print(f"Corr(Credit Growth, Income Growth):   {corr_credit_income:7.4f}")
    print(f"Corr(Default Rate, Credit Growth):   {corr_default_credit:7.4f}")

    # Assertions validating the Mian-Sufi mechanism:
    # 1. Credit growth is strongly positively correlated with latent subprime share
    assert corr_credit_subprime > 0.85
    # 2. Credit growth is disconnected or negatively related to income growth
    assert corr_credit_income < 0.0
    # 3. Subsequent defaults are concentrated in the areas with highest credit growth
    assert corr_default_credit > 0.85


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook quantitative credit models and risk management frameworks failed disastrously during the 2007–2009 crisis due to fundamental blind spots:

  • Gaussian Copula Breakdown in Tail Regimes: Rating agency CDO models relied on Li's Gaussian copula, assuming independent regional default risks and low pairwise correlations (\(\rho \approx 0.05\)–\(0.15\)). When home prices declined nationwide simultaneously, regional correlations jumped toward unity, instantly wiping out AAA senior tranches that were assumed to be as safe as US Treasuries.
  • Loan Modification Re-Default Cycles: Private servicers and government programs attempted to stem foreclosures through interest rate reductions and term extensions. However, early loan modifications that did not reduce mortgage principal exhibited catastrophic 12-month re-default rates exceeding 50%, because borrowers remained in deep negative equity (\(LTV > 120\%\)).
  • Regulatory Capital Arbitrage in Bank Holding Companies: Under Basel II rules, commercial banks faced an 8% capital charge for holding whole mortgage loans, but only a 1.6% capital charge for holding AAA-rated tranches of private-label mortgage securitizations. This regulatory incentive drove banks (e.g., Citigroup, Merrill Lynch) to hold trillions of dollars in "super senior" CDO tranches on balance sheet, maximizing leverage and vulnerability to systemic collapse.

Misconceptions

  • The 2000s subprime mortgage expansion was driven by rapid income growth among low-income households.

    Mian & Sufi (2009) demonstrated that mortgage credit growth was completely decoupled from income growth. Credit expanded fastest in zip codes where income growth was stagnant or negative, driven entirely by an expansion in securitization credit supply.

  • Subprime borrowers defaulted primarily because of rising interest rate adjustments on 2/28 hybrid ARMs.

    While payment resets exacerbated cash flow stress, empirical research shows that negative equity (\(LTV > 100\%\)) caused by home price deflation was the indispensable driver. Borrowers with positive equity rarely defaulted upon reset; they simply refinanced or sold the home at a profit.

  • A credit rating of AAA on a mortgage CDO indicated the collateral loans were prime conforming mortgages.

    CDO credit ratings reflected subordination tranche priority, not collateral quality. Wall Street created AAA-rated CDO debt out of pools consisting entirely of BBB-rated subprime mortgage tranches through financial engineering and flawed correlation models.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A borrower purchases a home for $500,000 using an 80-20 piggyback structure: a first mortgage of $400,000 (80% LTV) and a second mortgage of $100,000 (20% LTV) with 0% equity down. If the local housing market experiences a 15% price decline after two years, calculate the homeowner's new equity and effective Combined Loan-to-Value (CLTV), assuming scheduled amortization has reduced total debt to $490,000.
Solution

The new home value after a 15% decline is: \[ V_2 = \$500,000 \times (1 - 0.15) = \$425,000 \] Total outstanding mortgage debt is $490,000. The homeowner's equity is: \[ \text{Equity} = V_2 - B_2 = \$425,000 - \$490,000 = -\$65,000 \] The effective Combined Loan-to-Value (CLTV) is: \[ \text{CLTV} = \frac{\$490,000}{\$425,000} \approx 115.29\% \] The borrower has -$65,000 in negative equity, placing the mortgage deep underwater.

A 2/28 hybrid ARM has an initial teaser rate of 4.50% on a $350,000 balance (30-year amortization). At month 24, the loan resets to 6-month LIBOR + 5.50%. If 6-month LIBOR at reset is 5.25%, compute the monthly payment before reset and after reset, and the percentage payment shock.
Solution

Before reset, monthly rate is \(i_1 = 0.045 / 12 = 0.00375\), \(N = 360\): \[ PMT_{\text{teaser}} = \$350,000 \times \frac{0.00375}{1 - (1.00375)^{-360}} \approx \$1,773.40 \] After 24 months, scheduled amortization leaves approximately $339,200 balance with 336 months remaining. The new interest rate is \(r_2 = 5.25\% + 5.50\% = 10.75\%\), so monthly rate is \(i_2 = 0.1075 / 12 \approx 0.0089583\): \[ PMT_{\text{reset}} = \$339,200 \times \frac{0.0089583}{1 - (1 + 0.0089583)^{-336}} \approx \$3,197.80 \] The payment shock is: \[ \frac{PMT_{\text{reset}} - PMT_{\text{teaser}}}{PMT_{\text{teaser}}} = \frac{\$3,197.80 - \$1,773.40}{\$1,773.40} \approx +80.3\% \] Monthly debt service escalates by over 80%, triggering severe liquidity insolvency.

A collateralized debt obligation (CDO) issues $1,000,000,000 in liabilities across three tranches: Senior AAA ($750M, 75% attachment), Mezzanine BBB ($200M, 20% thickness), and Equity ($50M, 5% attachment). If collateral default losses reach 28% of total CDO pool principal, calculate the dollar loss and percentage loss allocated to each tranche.
Solution

Total pool loss is \(0.28 \times \$1,000,000,000 = \$280,000,000\). Losses are absorbed in reverse seniority (subordination waterfall): 1. Equity Tranche ($50M): Absorbs first $50M of losses. Dollar loss = $50M (100% wipeout). Remaining losses = $230M. 2. Mezzanine BBB Tranche ($200M): Absorbs next $200M of losses. Dollar loss = $200M (100% wipeout). Remaining losses = $30M. 3. Senior AAA Tranche ($750M): Absorbs remaining $30M of losses. Dollar loss = $30M. Percentage loss = \(\frac{\$30\text{M}}{\$750\text{M}} = 4.0\%\). The senior AAA tranche suffers attachment and credit losses, disproving its zero-loss premise.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that under the originate-to-distribute model with unobservable borrower effort and costless loan sales, an originator with zero retained credit risk chooses an underwriting verification standard of zero.
Solution

Let an originator earn origination fee \(\phi > 0\) per loan originated. Underwriting verification requires screening cost \(c(e) = \frac{1}{2} k e^2\) where \(e \ge 0\) represents screening diligence. The borrower default probability is \(p(e)\) with \(p'(e) < 0\). If the originator retains fraction \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) of credit loss \(L\), expected originator profit is: \[ \Pi(e) = \phi - c(e) - \alpha p(e) L = \phi - \frac{1}{2} k e^2 - \alpha p(e) L \] The first-order condition for optimal screening diligence is: \[ \frac{d \Pi}{d e} = -k e - \alpha p'(e) L = 0 \implies e^* = \frac{-\alpha p'(e) L}{k} \] In a pure originate-to-distribute model with full loan sales, the originator retains zero credit risk: \(\alpha = 0\). Substituting \(\alpha = 0\) yields: \[ e^* = \frac{-0 \cdot p'(e) L}{k} = 0 \] Thus, without risk retention, the profit-maximizing originator exerts zero verification effort, producing no-documentation ("ninja") loans.

Under a 1-factor Gaussian copula model, prove that as pairwise asset correlation \(\rho \to 1\), the credit risk of a senior CDO tranche converges to the credit risk of the underlying collateral pool.
Solution

In a 1-factor Gaussian copula, the latent credit variable for collateral asset \(j\) is \(X_j = \sqrt{\rho} Z + \sqrt{1 - \rho} \epsilon_j\), where \(Z, \epsilon_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) are independent. An asset defaults if \(X_j \le \Phi^{-1}(p)\), where \(p\) is the marginal default rate. As \(\rho \to 1\), the idiosyncratic variance \((1 - \rho) \to 0\), so \(X_j \to Z\) for all \(j = 1, \dots, J\). All assets share identical latent credit health \(Z\). Consequently, if \(Z \le \Phi^{-1}(p)\), 100% of collateral assets default simultaneously; if \(Z > \Phi^{-1}(p)\), zero assets default. Under \(\rho = 1\), the pool loss distribution degenerates into a two-point Bernoulli distribution: loss is 0 with probability \(1 - p\) and 100% with probability \(p\). Because total loss is 100% whenever defaults occur, every tranche—regardless of its subordination level \(K < 100\%\)—experiences complete total loss with probability \(p\). Thus, structural subordination provides zero protection as correlation approaches 1.

Prove that if house price growth \(H_t\) follows a geometric Brownian motion \(d H_t = \mu H_t d t + \sigma H_t d W_t\), the borrower's default option is strictly equivalent to an American put option on the housing asset with time-varying strike equal to unpaid balance \(B_t\).
Solution

Under non-recourse mortgage debt (standard in anti-deficiency states like California), the mortgagor has the legal option to surrender the deed to the property at any stopping time \(\tau \in [0, T]\) in full satisfaction of the outstanding debt balance \(B_\tau\). The payoff to the mortgagor from defaulting at time \(\tau\) is: \[ \max(0, B_\tau - H_\tau) \] This matches the exact payoff of an American put option on underlying asset \(H_t\) with exercise strike price \(K_\tau = B_\tau\). Since the borrower can exercise this put option at any stopping time \(\tau\) prior to contractual loan maturity \(T\), mortgage default is mathematically isomorphic to an American put option with stochastic or amortizing strike.

Tier 3 · Empirical

In Mian & Sufi (2009), the authors run a cross-sectional regression of 2002–2005 mortgage credit growth on 1996 subprime share across 1,000 zip codes, obtaining an estimated slope \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.85\) with standard error \(0.04\). Test the null hypothesis that subprime share had no impact on credit expansion at the 1% significance level.
Solution

The null hypothesis is \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) versus alternative \(H_1: \beta_1 \ne 0\). Compute the test statistic \(t\): \[ t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_1 - 0}{\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1)} = \frac{0.85}{0.04} = 21.25 \] For a two-tailed test with \(N - k = 1000 - 2 = 998\) degrees of freedom, the 1% critical value from the standard normal distribution is \(z_{0.005} \approx 2.576\). Since \(t = 21.25 > 2.576\), we decisively reject the null hypothesis (\(p < 10^{-50}\)). Subprime share had a massive, statistically overwhelming positive impact on mortgage credit expansion during the 2002–2005 boom.

A bank holding company holds $10 billion in whole subprime mortgages (8% regulatory capital charge under Basel I) vs $10 billion in AAA-rated subprime CDO tranches (1.6% capital charge under Basel II). Calculate the required equity capital and the return on regulatory equity (RORE) for both assets, assuming both yield an 80 bps net profit margin on assets.
Solution

For whole mortgages: \[ \text{Equity Capital}_{\text{whole}} = \$10\text{B} \times 0.08 = \$800,000,000 \] Annual net profit is \(\$10\text{B} \times 0.0080 = \$80,000,000\). Return on Regulatory Equity is: \[ \text{RORE}_{\text{whole}} = \frac{\$80,000,000}{\$800,000,000} = 10.0\% \] For AAA CDO tranches: \[ \text{Equity Capital}_{\text{CDO}} = \$10\text{B} \times 0.016 = \$160,000,000 \] Return on Regulatory Equity is: \[ \text{RORE}_{\text{CDO}} = \frac{\$80,000,000}{\$160,000,000} = 50.0\% \] Regulatory capital arbitrage quintupled return on equity from 10% to 50%, providing irresistible incentives for banks to hold trillions in structured subprime paper.

During 2008, a mortgage servicer modified 10,000 delinquent underwater loans (\(LTV = 130\%\)) solely by reducing interest rates from 8% to 4%, lowering monthly payments by 30% but leaving principal unchanged. Within 12 months, 5,400 borrowers re-defaulted. Calculate the 12-month re-default rate and explain why rate reduction alone failed to cure default risk.
Solution

The 12-month re-default rate is: \[ \text{Re-default Rate} = \frac{5,400}{10,000} = 54.0\% \] Rate reductions address only the liquidity trigger (payment shock) of default. However, under the double-trigger theory of default (Definition 6.2), the primary strategic trigger is negative equity (\(LTV = 130\%\)). Because the modification left loan principal untouched, borrowers remained $30,000 to $60,000 underwater. Any minor subsequent financial disruption or simple economic realization that they were servicing an asset worth far less than the debt led over half the borrowers to exercise their put option to walk away.

Tier 4 · Research

Campbell (2006) analyzes household financial literacy and mortgage choices. Why did financially unsophisticated households disproportionately adopt complex Option ARMs and teaser ARMs compared to fixed-rate mortgages in 2004–2006?
Solution

Campbell (2006) demonstrates that household financial decision-making is characterized by bounded rationality and cognitive biases. Complex hybrid and Option ARMs exploit "shrouded attributes": borrowers focus myopically on initial monthly cash outlay (the teaser payment) rather than contractual lifetime borrowing costs or future reset risks. Mortgage brokers and non-bank originators operated under aggressive commission incentives, marketing negative amortization products to lower-income, less-educated borrowers who lacked the quantitative sophistication to calculate post-reset payment shocks or understand compounding negative equity.

Explain how the collapse of the subprime mortgage market transmitted into a global liquidity freeze in the asset-backed commercial paper (ABCP) and interbank repo markets in August 2007 (the "run on repo").
Solution

Structured Investment Vehicles (SIVs) and shadow banks funded long-term AAA subprime CDOs by issuing short-term Asset-Backed Commercial Paper (ABCP) and borrowing in the overnight repo market using CDOs as collateral. Because CDO tranches were opaque and impossible to value once subprime defaults began, money market funds refused to roll over maturing ABCP, and repo counterparties demanded massive haircuts (jumping from 1%–2% to over 30%–50%). Inability to refinance short-term debt forced shadow banks into fire sales of structured assets, transmitting localized subprime losses into a systemic collapse of short-term wholesale dollar funding markets.

Assess how the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 (specifically the Qualified Mortgage rule and credit risk retention requirements) reshaped US mortgage underwriting. Did these reforms permanently eliminate originate-to-distribute moral hazard?
Solution

The Dodd-Frank Act instituted the Ability-to-Repay (ATR) / Qualified Mortgage (QM) rule, which banned predatory loan features (negative amortization, balloon payments, teaser rates without underwriting to fully indexed rates) and capped debt-to-income ratios at 43%. Additionally, Section 941 mandated that securitizers retain a 5% unhedged economic interest ("skin-in-the-game") in non-QM securitizations. While these reforms virtually eliminated toxic private-label subprime products, they concentrated mortgage origination almost entirely into government-backed channels (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA/Ginnie Mae), shifting credit risk from private shadow banking back onto the federal taxpayer.

  7. A CDO pool suffers $280M in losses on $1,000M face value. The tranches are Equity ($50M), BBB ($200M), and Senior AAA ($750M). How many millions of dollars in losses attach to the Senior AAA tranche?
    Equity absorbs $50M, BBB absorbs $200M (total $250M). Senior AAA absorbs the remaining \(\$280\text{M} - \$250\text{M} = \$30\text{M}\).

  8. What was an Option ARM mortgage?

    Options
    Option ARMs allowed minimum teaser payments, causing unpaid interest to be added to principal (negative amortization).
