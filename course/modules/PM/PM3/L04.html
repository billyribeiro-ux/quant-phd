Appraisal Smoothing, De-smoothing Models & Hidden Risk

Reported private credit returns exhibit extraordinarily high Sharpe ratios, minimal drawdowns, and low correlation with public debt markets. However, these attractive performance characteristics are largely artifacts of appraisal smoothing. Because illiquid private assets lack continuous secondary market pricing, quarterly Net Asset Values (NAVs) are appraised using backward-looking models that blend stale valuations with partial market updates. Unsmoothing private credit series is essential to unmask true economic volatility, accurate market betas, and genuine tail risk.

1. The Appraisal Marking Process & Return Autocorrelation

Appraisal smoothing and marking of private assets refers to the econometric dampening of reported return volatility and artificial induction of serial correlation resulting from subjective, infrequent, and backward-looking mark-to-model valuations of illiquid investments.

Under ASC 820, private credit loans and direct lending portfolios are classified as Level 3 assets. Valuation committees determine quarterly valuations using discounted cash flow (DCF) models with unobservable inputs. In practice, appraisers adjust marks sluggishly: loans performing contractually remain held at or near par even as market credit spreads widen, and appraisers incorporate new market clearing yields with substantial multi-quarter delays.

Definition 4.1 · The First-Order Autoregressive AR(1) Smoothing Model

Let \(R_t^{\text{true}}\) represent the unobserved, true economic return of the illiquid asset portfolio during quarter \(t\), with variance \(\Var(R_t^{\text{true}}) = \sigma_{\text{true}}^2\). The observed reported return \(R_t^{\text{obs}}\) follows a partial-adjustment process parameterized by smoothing decay factor \(\phi \in [0, 1)\):

\[ R_t^{\text{obs}} = \phi R_{t-1}^{\text{obs}} + (1 - \phi) R_t^{\text{true}} \]

When \(\phi = 0\), reporting is instantaneous (\(R_t^{\text{obs}} = R_t^{\text{true}}\)). As \(\phi \to 1\), marks become entirely backward-looking and sticky.

Getmansky, Lo & Makarov (2004) generalize the smoothing framework to a moving-average \(\text{MA}(q)\) specification, demonstrating that illiquidity induces persistent positive autocorrelation across multiple quarterly lags:

\[ R_t^{\text{obs}} = \sum_{k=0}^q \theta_k R_{t-k}^{\text{true}}, \quad \text{with } \sum_{k=0}^q \theta_k = 1, \quad \theta_k \ge 0 \]

2. Theoretical Theorems: Volatility Suppression & Beta Compression

Appraisal smoothing fundamentally distorts every standard risk and performance metric relied upon by institutional portfolio allocators.

Theorem 4.1 · Volatility Suppression Under AR(1) Smoothing

Assume that true economic returns \(R_t^{\text{true}}\) are serially uncorrelated with constant variance \(\sigma_{\text{true}}^2\). Under the AR(1) smoothing model \(R_t^{\text{obs}} = \phi R_{t-1}^{\text{obs}} + (1 - \phi) R_t^{\text{true}}\) with \(|\phi| < 1\), the variance of observed reported returns \(\sigma_{\text{obs}}^2\) is:

\[ \sigma_{\text{obs}}^2 = \frac{1 - \phi}{1 + \phi} \sigma_{\text{true}}^2 \]

and the theoretical ratio of true volatility to observed volatility is:

\[ \frac{\sigma_{\text{true}}}{\sigma_{\text{obs}}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \phi}{1 - \phi}} > 1 \quad \text{for all } \phi \in (0, 1) \]
Proof

Express the AR(1) equation in its infinite moving-average representation by recursive substitution:

\[ R_t^{\text{obs}} = (1 - \phi) \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \phi^j R_{t-j}^{\text{true}} \]

Since \(R_t^{\text{true}}\) is independent and identically distributed across time with variance \(\sigma_{\text{true}}^2\), the variance of the linear combination is:

\[ \Var(R_t^{\text{obs}}) = (1 - \phi)^2 \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \phi^{2j} \Var(R_{t-j}^{\text{true}}) = (1 - \phi)^2 \sigma_{\text{true}}^2 \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} (\phi^2)^j \]

Applying the infinite geometric series sum formula \(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} (\phi^2)^j = \frac{1}{1 - \phi^2}\) for \(|\phi| < 1\):

\[ \Var(R_t^{\text{obs}}) = \frac{(1 - \phi)^2}{1 - \phi^2} \sigma_{\text{true}}^2 = \frac{(1 - \phi)^2}{(1 - \phi)(1 + \phi)} \sigma_{\text{true}}^2 = \frac{1 - \phi}{1 + \phi} \sigma_{\text{true}}^2 \]

Taking square roots and inverting yields \(\frac{\sigma_{\text{true}}}{\sigma_{\text{obs}}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \phi}{1 - \phi}}\).

Theorem 4.2 · Beta Compression & Sharpe Ratio Inflation

Let \(R_t^m\) denote a serially uncorrelated public market benchmark (e.g. public leveraged loans) with variance \(\sigma_m^2\). Under the assumptions of Theorem 4.1, the observed market beta \(\beta_{\text{obs}}\) satisfies:

\[ \beta_{\text{obs}} = (1 - \phi) \beta_{\text{true}} \iff \beta_{\text{true}} = \frac{\beta_{\text{obs}}}{1 - \phi} \]

Furthermore, because expected returns are preserved (\(\E[R_t^{\text{obs}}] = \E[R_t^{\text{true}}]\)), the observed Sharpe ratio \(\text{SR}_{\text{obs}}\) is artificially inflated relative to true economic Sharpe ratio \(\text{SR}_{\text{true}}\):

\[ \text{SR}_{\text{obs}} = \text{SR}_{\text{true}} \sqrt{\frac{1 + \phi}{1 - \phi}} \]
Proof

1. Beta derivation: By definition, \(\beta_{\text{obs}} = \frac{\Cov(R_t^{\text{obs}}, R_t^m)}{\Var(R_t^m)}\). Substituting the moving-average expansion of \(R_t^{\text{obs}}\):

\[ \Cov(R_t^{\text{obs}}, R_t^m) = \Cov\left( (1 - \phi) R_t^{\text{true}} + (1 - \phi) \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \phi^j R_{t-j}^{\text{true}}, R_t^m \right) \]

Because market returns are serially uncorrelated, lagged true returns \(R_{t-j}^{\text{true}}\) are uncorrelated with contemporaneous market returns \(R_t^m\). Thus:

\[ \Cov(R_t^{\text{obs}}, R_t^m) = (1 - \phi) \Cov(R_t^{\text{true}}, R_t^m) \implies \beta_{\text{obs}} = (1 - \phi) \frac{\Cov(R_t^{\text{true}}, R_t^m)}{\Var(R_t^m)} = (1 - \phi) \beta_{\text{true}} \]

2. Sharpe ratio derivation: Taking expectations of \(R_t^{\text{obs}} = \phi R_{t-1}^{\text{obs}} + (1 - \phi) R_t^{\text{true}}\) in the stationary state gives \(\mu_{\text{obs}} = \phi \mu_{\text{obs}} + (1 - \phi) \mu_{\text{true}} \implies \mu_{\text{obs}} = \mu_{\text{true}}\). With risk-free rate \(R_f\):

\[ \text{SR}_{\text{obs}} = \frac{\mu_{\text{obs}} - R_f}{\sigma_{\text{obs}}} = \frac{\mu_{\text{true}} - R_f}{\sigma_{\text{true}} \sqrt{\frac{1 - \phi}{1 + \phi}}} = \text{SR}_{\text{true}} \sqrt{\frac{1 + \phi}{1 - \phi}} \]

This completes the proof.

3. Econometric De-Smoothing Inversion & State-Space Filtering

To recover true underlying economic performance, analysts invert the smoothing filter. In the AR(1) case, inverting the difference equation yields the recovered true series:

\[ R_t^{\text{true}} = \frac{R_t^{\text{obs}} - \phi R_{t-1}^{\text{obs}}}{1 - \phi}, \quad t \ge 1 \]

with the initial condition \(R_0^{\text{true}} = R_0^{\text{obs}}\). The smoothing parameter \(\phi\) is estimated empirically from the first-order sample autocorrelation \(\hat{\rho}_1 = \operatorname{Corr}(R_t^{\text{obs}}, R_{t-1}^{\text{obs}})\), because under serial independence of true returns, \(\rho_1 = \phi\).

Couts, Gonçalves & Rossi (2024) implement a modern state-space Kalman filter to unsmooth private capital returns, analyzing private equity, real estate, and private debt funds. They demonstrate that reported private credit return indices (such as the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index) severely understate true risk: once de-smoothed, private credit exhibits market betas to public leveraged loans near 1.0, annualized volatility doubles from ~3.5% to over 7.0%, and the apparent diversification free lunch completely disappears.

4. Inline Numerical Lab: Private Credit AR(1) De-Smoothing Engine

The numerical lab below simulates true unobserved private credit returns alongside a public syndicated leveraged loan proxy, generates reported returns under an AR(1) smoothing process (\(\phi = 0.55\)), and inverts the filter to recover true unobserved volatility and market beta.

Lab 1 · Private Credit Return Smoothing & AR(1) De-Smoothing Engine

Under 55% appraisal smoothing, reported annualized volatility collapses from 5.93% to 3.00%, reported loan beta drops from 1.025 to 0.421, and the reported Sharpe ratio artificially inflates from 1.049 to 2.004. Inverting the AR(1) filter expands volatility and beta back toward true values.

"""Lab PM3-L04-1: Private Credit Return Smoothing and AR(1) De-Smoothing Engine.

SIMULATED: Demonstrates appraisal smoothing distortions on illiquid private credit returns,
quantifying observed vs. true volatility, beta to leveraged loans, and Sharpe ratio inflation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_smoothed_credit_returns(
    n_quarters: int = 60,
    phi: float = 0.55,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Generate true and appraisal-smoothed private credit returns against a public loan proxy.

    Parameters
    ----------
    n_quarters : int
        Number of quarterly periods to simulate (e.g. 60 quarters = 15 years).
    phi : float
        Appraisal smoothing coefficient (decay factor in [0, 1)).
    seed : int
        RNG seed for deterministic evaluation.

    Returns
    -------
    tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]
        (r_loan, r_true, r_obs)
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    mu_m = 0.018  # 1.8% per quarter (~7.2% annualized)
    sigma_m = 0.035  # 3.5% per quarter (~7.0% annualized)

    # Public syndicated loan proxy returns
    r_loan = rng.normal(mu_m, sigma_m, size=n_quarters)

    # True unobserved private credit returns: beta = 1.05, alpha = 40 bps / qtr, idiosyncratic noise
    alpha = 0.004
    beta = 1.05
    noise = rng.normal(0.0, 0.012, size=n_quarters)
    r_true = alpha + beta * r_loan + noise

    # Observed reported private credit returns under AR(1) appraisal smoothing
    r_obs = np.zeros(n_quarters)
    r_obs[0] = r_true[0]
    for t in range(1, n_quarters):
        r_obs[t] = phi * r_obs[t - 1] + (1.0 - phi) * r_true[t]

    return r_loan, r_true, r_obs


def estimate_ar1_phi(returns: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """Estimate the first-order serial correlation coefficient phi."""
    r_t = returns[1:]
    r_lag = returns[:-1]
    cov = np.cov(r_t, r_lag)[0, 1]
    var_lag = np.var(r_lag, ddof=1)
    phi = float(cov / var_lag) if var_lag > 0 else 0.0
    return float(np.clip(phi, 0.0, 0.95))


def unsmooth_returns(r_obs: np.ndarray, phi: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Invert the AR(1) smoothing process to recover true unobserved returns."""
    r_unsmooth = np.zeros_like(r_obs)
    r_unsmooth[0] = r_obs[0]
    denom = 1.0 - phi
    for t in range(1, len(r_obs)):
        r_unsmooth[t] = (r_obs[t] - phi * r_obs[t - 1]) / denom
    return r_unsmooth


def compute_series_stats(r: np.ndarray, r_market: np.ndarray, rf_q: float = 0.0075) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate annualized mean, volatility, Sharpe ratio, and market beta."""
    mean_ann = float(np.mean(r) * 4.0)
    vol_ann = float(np.std(r, ddof=1) * 2.0)  # quarterly to annual: sqrt(4) = 2.0
    sharpe = float((mean_ann - rf_q * 4.0) / vol_ann) if vol_ann > 0 else 0.0

    cov_mat = np.cov(r, r_market)
    beta = float(cov_mat[0, 1] / cov_mat[1, 1]) if cov_mat[1, 1] > 0 else 0.0
    autocorr = float(np.corrcoef(r[1:], r[:-1])[0, 1])

    return {
        "mean_ann": mean_ann,
        "vol_ann": vol_ann,
        "sharpe": sharpe,
        "beta": beta,
        "autocorr": autocorr,
    }


def main() -> None:
    n_quarters = 60
    true_phi = 0.55
    r_loan, r_true, r_obs = simulate_smoothed_credit_returns(n_quarters, phi=true_phi, seed=42)

    hat_phi = estimate_ar1_phi(r_obs)
    r_recovered = unsmooth_returns(r_obs, hat_phi)

    rf_q = 0.0075  # 3.0% annualized risk-free rate

    stats_true = compute_series_stats(r_true, r_loan, rf_q)
    stats_obs = compute_series_stats(r_obs, r_loan, rf_q)
    stats_rec = compute_series_stats(r_recovered, r_loan, rf_q)
    stats_loan = compute_series_stats(r_loan, r_loan, rf_q)

    print("Private Credit Return Smoothing & De-Smoothing Engine Output:")
    print(f"True Simulation Smoothing Parameter (phi):  {true_phi:.4f}")
    print(f"Estimated Smoothing Parameter (hat_phi):   {hat_phi:.4f}\n")

    print("Metric                 | Public Loan Proxy | True Private Debt | Reported (Smoothed) | De-Smoothed")
    print("-" * 95)
    print(
        f"Annualized Mean (%)    | {stats_loan['mean_ann'] * 100:17.2f} | "
        f"{stats_true['mean_ann'] * 100:17.2f} | {stats_obs['mean_ann'] * 100:19.2f} | "
        f"{stats_rec['mean_ann'] * 100:11.2f}"
    )
    print(
        f"Annualized Vol (%)     | {stats_loan['vol_ann'] * 100:17.2f} | "
        f"{stats_true['vol_ann'] * 100:17.2f} | {stats_obs['vol_ann'] * 100:19.2f} | "
        f"{stats_rec['vol_ann'] * 100:11.2f}"
    )
    print(
        f"Annualized Sharpe      | {stats_loan['sharpe']:17.3f} | "
        f"{stats_true['sharpe']:17.3f} | {stats_obs['sharpe']:19.3f} | "
        f"{stats_rec['sharpe']:11.3f}"
    )
    print(
        f"Loan Market Beta       | {stats_loan['beta']:17.3f} | "
        f"{stats_true['beta']:17.3f} | {stats_obs['beta']:19.3f} | "
        f"{stats_rec['beta']:11.3f}"
    )
    print(
        f"First-Order Autocorr   | {stats_loan['autocorr']:17.3f} | "
        f"{stats_true['autocorr']:17.3f} | {stats_obs['autocorr']:19.3f} | "
        f"{stats_rec['autocorr']:11.3f}"
    )

    # Core theoretical assertions
    # 1. Observed reported volatility is strictly smaller than true volatility
    assert stats_obs["vol_ann"] < stats_true["vol_ann"] * 0.70

    # 2. Observed reported beta is strictly smaller than true beta
    assert stats_obs["beta"] < stats_true["beta"] * 0.70

    # 3. Observed Sharpe ratio is artificially inflated
    assert stats_obs["sharpe"] > stats_true["sharpe"] * 1.40

    # 4. De-smoothed volatility and beta expand towards true values
    assert stats_rec["vol_ann"] > stats_obs["vol_ann"] * 1.50
    assert stats_rec["beta"] > stats_obs["beta"] * 1.80
    assert np.isclose(stats_rec["vol_ann"], stats_true["vol_ann"], atol=0.015)
    assert np.isclose(stats_rec["beta"], stats_true["beta"], atol=0.25)

    print("\nDe-Smoothing Invariants: Verified true risk recovery, beta unbiasing, and Sharpe deflation.")


main()

Where the canon breaks

The standard mean-variance portfolio allocation canon (Markowitz 1952) prescribes that institutional investors should allocate massive fractions of their fixed income portfolios to private credit because reported historical correlations with public equities and corporate bonds are near zero (0.10 to 0.25). Couts, Gonçalves & Rossi (2024) prove that this low correlation is an econometric illusion caused by asynchronous and lagged appraisal marks. When private credit returns are unsmoothed against contemporaneous public market shocks, their true correlation with public leveraged loans exceeds 0.80. Mean-variance optimizers using reported unadjusted returns generate severely misallocated portfolios that take excessive tail risk during systemic liquidity shocks.

Misconceptions

  1. Private credit is less volatile than public leveraged loans because direct lenders work closely with borrowers to avoid distress.

    Direct lending borrowers are fundamentally smaller, more leveraged, and have lower credit ratings than public syndicated loan borrowers. The lower observed volatility of private credit indices arises entirely from Level 3 accounting conventions that hold loans at amortized cost until default, not from superior economic stability.

  2. De-smoothing an AR(1) return series alters the total cumulative dollar return delivered to limited partners over a 10-year fund life.

    De-smoothing inverts the timing of returns across periods without changing the cumulative multi-year wealth multiple. By cash flow conservation, the arithmetic sum of de-smoothed returns \(\sum_{t=1}^T R_t^{\text{true}}\) closely mirrors \(\sum_{t=1}^T R_t^{\text{obs}}\); de-smoothing reallocates variance across quarters to reflect when economic value was genuinely created or destroyed.

  3. An observed Sharpe ratio of 2.50 in a private debt fund proves that the manager generates extraordinary alpha over public high-yield debt.

    By Theorem 4.2, an appraisal smoothing parameter of \(\phi = 0.60\) inflates the observed Sharpe ratio by a factor of \(\sqrt{\frac{1 + 0.60}{1 - 0.60}} = \sqrt{4.0} = 2.0\). A fund with an unadjusted Sharpe ratio of 2.50 has a true economic Sharpe ratio of only 1.25 once accounting smoothing is stripped away.

Problem set

Mechanics

A reported private debt return series has an estimated first-order autocorrelation of \(\phi = 0.60\). The reported annualized volatility is 4.00%, and the reported annualized Sharpe ratio is 2.20 (with a risk-free rate of 3.00%). Calculate the theoretical true annualized volatility, the true Sharpe ratio, and the volatility expansion factor.

Worked solution

1. Volatility expansion factor: \(\frac{\sigma_{\text{true}}}{\sigma_{\text{obs}}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \phi}{1 - \phi}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 0.60}{1 - 0.60}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.60}{0.40}} = \sqrt{4.0} = 2.0000\).

2. True annualized volatility: \(\sigma_{\text{true}} = 4.00\% \times 2.0000 = 8.0000\%\).

3. True Sharpe ratio: \(\text{SR}_{\text{true}} = \frac{\text{SR}_{\text{obs}}}{2.0000} = \frac{2.20}{2.00} = 1.1000\).

A private fund reports quarterly returns for three consecutive quarters: \(R_1^{\text{obs}} = 2.00\%\), \(R_2^{\text{obs}} = 1.80\%\), and \(R_3^{\text{obs}} = 0.50\%\). Using an estimated AR(1) smoothing parameter of \(\phi = 0.50\), compute the recovered true economic returns \(R_2^{\text{true}}\) and \(R_3^{\text{true}}\).

Worked solution

1. Filter inversion formula: \(R_t^{\text{true}} = \frac{R_t^{\text{obs}} - \phi R_{t-1}^{\text{obs}}}{1 - \phi}\).

2. Quarter 2: \(R_2^{\text{true}} = \frac{1.80\% - 0.50 \times 2.00\%}{1 - 0.50} = \frac{1.80\% - 1.00\%}{0.50} = \frac{0.80\%}{0.50} = 1.60\%\).

3. Quarter 3: \(R_3^{\text{true}} = \frac{0.50\% - 0.50 \times 1.80\%}{1 - 0.50} = \frac{0.50\% - 0.90\%}{0.50} = \frac{-0.40\%}{0.50} = -0.80\%\).

The reported return of +0.50% in quarter 3 masked a true economic loss of -0.80%.

A reported private credit index exhibits a regression beta of \(\beta_{\text{obs}} = 0.35\) against the Morningstar LSTA Leveraged Loan Index. If the smoothing parameter is \(\phi = 0.55\), compute the de-smoothed market beta \(\beta_{\text{true}}\) assuming public loans are serially uncorrelated.

Worked solution

1. By Theorem 4.2, \(\beta_{\text{true}} = \frac{\beta_{\text{obs}}}{1 - \phi}\).

2. Calculation: \(\beta_{\text{true}} = \frac{0.35}{1 - 0.55} = \frac{0.35}{0.45} \approx 0.7778\).

3. The true systematic exposure to public leveraged loan risk is 0.7778, more than double the reported beta of 0.35.

Proof

Prove that under the moving-average smoothing model \(R_t^{\text{obs}} = \theta_0 R_t^{\text{true}} + \theta_1 R_{t-1}^{\text{true}}\) with \(\theta_0 + \theta_1 = 1\), the ratio of observed to true variance \(\frac{\Var(R_t^{\text{obs}})}{\Var(R_t^{\text{true}})}\) attains its minimum value of \(0.50\) when \(\theta_0 = \theta_1 = 0.50\).

Worked solution

Let \(\Var(R_t^{\text{true}}) = \sigma^2\) with \(\Cov(R_t^{\text{true}}, R_{t-1}^{\text{true}}) = 0\). The observed variance is:

\[ \Var(R_t^{\text{obs}}) = \theta_0^2 \sigma^2 + \theta_1^2 \sigma^2 = (\theta_0^2 + (1 - \theta_0)^2) \sigma^2 \]

To minimize \(\theta_0^2 + (1 - \theta_0)^2\), differentiate with respect to \(\theta_0\) and set to zero:

\[ \frac{d}{d\theta_0} \left( \theta_0^2 + 1 - 2\theta_0 + \theta_0^2 \right) = 4\theta_0 - 2 = 0 \implies \theta_0 = 0.50 \]

The second derivative is \(4 > 0\), confirming a minimum. Evaluating at \(\theta_0 = 0.50\):

\[ \frac{\Var(R_t^{\text{obs}})}{\sigma^2} = (0.50)^2 + (0.50)^2 = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.50 \]

Thus, two-period equal-weight smoothing reduces observed variance by exactly 50%.

Prove that for an AR(1) smoothed series, the true covariance between the asset and an arbitrary external factor \(F_t\) can be recovered from the sum of contemporaneous and lagged observed covariances: \(\Cov(R_t^{\text{true}}, F_t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \phi^k \Cov(R_{t+k}^{\text{obs}}, F_t)\).

Worked solution

Inverting the AR(1) relation: \(R_t^{\text{true}} = \frac{1}{1 - \phi} (R_t^{\text{obs}} - \phi R_{t-1}^{\text{obs}})\). For forward observations, \(R_{t+k}^{\text{obs}} = \phi^k R_t^{\text{obs}} + (1 - \phi) \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \phi^j R_{t+k-j}^{\text{true}}\). Taking covariances with \(F_t\) and applying the lead-lag covariance summation identity (Dimson 1979; Getmansky et al. 2004) demonstrates that summing contemporaneous and distributed forward covariances captures the full information flow delayed by appraisal lags: \(\beta_{\text{true}} = \beta_{\text{obs}} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \beta_{t+k}\).

Prove that if true returns are drawn from a symmetric distribution with zero skewness, the AR(1) smoothed observed return series also exhibits zero theoretical skewness, but strictly platykurtic or compressed tails relative to a normal distribution.

Worked solution

Because the AR(1) smoothing filter \(R_t^{\text{obs}} = (1 - \phi) \sum_{j=0}^\infty \phi^j R_{t-j}^{\text{true}}\) is a linear convolution of independent zero-skewness random variables, odd central moments vanish by symmetry: \(\E[(R_t^{\text{obs}} - \mu)^3] = (1 - \phi)^3 \sum \phi^{3j} \E[(R_{t-j}^{\text{true}} - \mu)^3] = 0\). For fourth moments, by the Central Limit Theorem applied to weighted sums of random variables, the linear combination of independent draws pulls the kurtosis of observed returns closer to 3 (Gaussian) than any heavy-tailed true distribution, mechanically masking tail default clustering.

Empirical

In their empirical study of illiquid fund returns, Couts, Gonçalves & Rossi (2024) estimate smoothing parameters and risk adjustments for private debt funds. What do they document regarding the de-smoothed volatility and public loan market beta of private debt?

Worked solution

Couts, Gonçalves & Rossi (2024, Tables 3 & 5) show that while reported private debt fund indices exhibit an annual volatility of only 3.8% and an apparent market beta of 0.38 against leveraged loans, de-smoothing reveals a true annual volatility of 8.2% and a market beta of 0.94. This empirical result proves that private debt possesses nearly identical systematic risk to public syndicated loans.

Compare the findings of Getmansky, Lo & Makarov (2004) on hedge fund illiquidity smoothing with the private credit findings of Couts et al. (2024). How do the estimated smoothing parameters (\(\phi\) or \(\theta_0\)) compare across asset classes?

Worked solution

Getmansky, Lo & Makarov (2004, Table 4) find that convertible arbitrage and fixed-income arbitrage hedge funds exhibit average first-order autocorrelation of 0.35 to 0.45, with contemporary weight \(\theta_0 \approx 0.60\). Couts et al. (2024) document even more severe smoothing in private debt funds, with estimated \(\phi\) reaching 0.50 to 0.65, driven by quarterly appraisal practices and Level 3 non-accrual delays.

How does the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index (CDLI) behave during systemic market stress periods (e.g., Q1 2020 COVID shock) relative to the Morningstar/LSTA Leveraged Loan Index? What explains the difference?

Worked solution

In Q1 2020, the public Morningstar LSTA Leveraged Loan Index plunged by -12.4% as secondary market loan trading prices collapsed to 82 cents on the dollar. In contrast, the CDLI reported a total return of -1.3% for the same quarter. This massive 11.1 percentage point divergence was driven by appraisal smoothing: private loan mark-to-model valuations assumed loans would pay off at maturity and incorporated only modest credit spread adjustments.

Research

Evaluate the institutional incentives of private credit fund managers (GPs) and institutional investors (LPs) regarding appraisal smoothing. Why might both parties have aligned incentives to maintain smoothed valuations?

Worked solution

Appraisal smoothing creates a mutual agency benefit: GPs report artificially low return volatility, high Sharpe ratios, and negligible drawdowns, which facilitates marketing and justifies 1.5% management fees. Simultaneously, institutional LP chief investment officers and pension trustees benefit because smoothed NAVs protect their balance sheets from reporting large paper mark-to-market drawdowns to state regulators, corporate boards, and beneficiaries, avoiding forced asset rebalancing or solvency impairment during market turmoil.

Discuss the econometric limitations of the simple AR(1) de-smoothing model when true economic returns exhibit time-varying volatility (GARCH effects) or regime-switching credit cycles.

Worked solution

The standard AR(1) inversion assumes a constant smoothing parameter \(\phi\) and homoskedastic errors. During severe crises, managers may become reluctant to adjust marks (increasing \(\phi\) endogenously), or conversely, auditors may force abrupt write-downs (inducing discrete jumps). Applying a constant \(\phi\) inverts historical returns improperly, creating spurious negative autocorrelation or excessive volatility spikes in normal regimes while under-correcting crisis periods. A regime-switching state-space filter with time-varying observation noise is required.

Design an empirical test using secondary market transaction prices of private debt fund LP interests (secondaries transactions) to directly estimate the bias and lag in reported NAVs.

Worked solution

Researchers can collect a database of executed secondary market trades where LPs sell fund stakes at negotiated prices \(P_t^{\text{sec}}\). By measuring the secondary discount \(\text{Disc}_t = (P_t^{\text{sec}} - \text{NAV}_t^{\text{rep}}) / \text{NAV}_t^{\text{rep}}\) and regressing subsequent reported quarterly NAV changes \(\Delta \text{NAV}_{t+k}\) on \(\text{Disc}_t\), one can quantify the predictive content of secondary pricing. If secondary discounts predict future downward NAV revisions with a 2-to-4 quarter lag, it directly identifies the magnitude and speed of appraisal marking delays.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is appraisal smoothing in private credit and illiquid asset funds?

    Options

    Appraisal smoothing arises when illiquid Level 3 asset marks sluggishly adjust to market reality, inducing serial correlation and compressing observed volatility.

  2. Under an AR(1) smoothing model with smoothing parameter \(\phi = 0.50\), compute the ratio of true return volatility to observed reported volatility, \(\sigma_{\text{true}} / \sigma_{\text{obs}}\).

    \(\frac{\sigma_{\text{true}}}{\sigma_{\text{obs}}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \phi}{1 - \phi}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 0.50}{1 - 0.50}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.50}{0.50}} = \sqrt{3.0} \approx 1.732\).

  3. How does appraisal smoothing affect the market beta of private debt relative to a public benchmark?

    Options

    Because lagged valuation marks do not react contemporaneously to market moves, contemporaneous covariance is multiplied by \(1 - \phi\), understating true market beta.

  4. A reported private credit fund has an observed beta of \(\beta_{\text{obs}} = 0.40\) against public leveraged loans. If the estimated smoothing parameter is \(\phi = 0.45\), calculate the de-smoothed true market beta \(\beta_{\text{true}} / \beta_{\text{obs}}\) expansion ratio.

    \(\beta_{\text{true}} = \frac{\beta_{\text{obs}}}{1 - \phi} = \frac{0.40}{1 - 0.45} = \frac{0.40}{0.55} \approx 0.7273\). The expansion ratio is \(\frac{1}{1 - 0.45} = \frac{1}{0.55} \approx 1.818\).

  5. What did Couts, Gonçalves & Rossi (2024) find when they unsmoothed reported private credit fund returns?

    Options

    Couts et al. (2024) demonstrate that de-smoothing unmasks private credit's true systematic risk, showing it has beta ~1.0 to leveraged loans and twice the reported volatility.

  6. A fund reports an observed Sharpe ratio of 2.60 under an AR(1) smoothing factor of \(\phi = 0.385\). If the inflation factor \(\sqrt{\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}}\) is equal to 1.50, compute the true economic Sharpe ratio \(\text{SR}_{\text{true}}\) rounded to two decimal places (e.g. 2.60 / 1.50 = 1.73).

    Inflation factor: \(\sqrt{\frac{1 + 0.385}{1 - 0.385}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.385}{0.615}} = \sqrt{2.252} \approx 1.50\). True Sharpe ratio: \(2.60 / 1.50 \approx 1.733\).

  7. How does an analyst invert the first-order AR(1) smoothing filter to recover unobserved true returns \(R_t^{\text{true}}\)?

    Options

    Inverting the difference equation \(R_t^{\text{obs}} = \phi R_{t-1}^{\text{obs}} + (1 - \phi) R_t^{\text{true}}\) yields the exact de-smoothing formula \(R_t^{\text{true}} = \frac{R_t^{\text{obs}} - \phi R_{t-1}^{\text{obs}}}{1 - \phi}\).

  8. Why does mean-variance portfolio optimization allocate excessively to private credit when using unadjusted reported historical returns?

    Options

    The optimizer takes reported volatility and correlation at face value, treating smoothed marks as an impossible "free lunch" of high yield without risk.
