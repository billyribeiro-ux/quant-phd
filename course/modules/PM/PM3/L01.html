Direct Lending & Middle-Market Credit

Following the 2008 global financial crisis, regulatory capital constraints and leveraged lending guidelines curtailed traditional bank financing for middle-market corporations. Nonbank direct lenders expanded to fill this financing void, originating bespoke senior secured loans directly to sponsor-backed borrowers. Evaluating private debt portfolios requires understanding how contractual rate floors, original issue discounts, and maintenance covenants generate attractive all-in yields while managing tail default risk.

1. Nonbank Intermediation & Direct Lending Foundations

Direct lending refers to the origination of nonbank loans directly negotiated between private debt funds or institutional asset managers and corporate borrowers, bypassing commercial bank syndication syndicates and public bond markets.

Chernenko, Erel & Prilmeier (2022) document that nonbank lenders primarily finance middle-market firms that are smaller, more leveraged, and have higher growth options than traditional bank borrowers. Unlike broadly syndicated loans (BSLs), which are arranged by investment banks and distributed to collateralized loan obligations (CLOs) and mutual funds, direct loans are typically held to maturity on the balance sheets of private debt funds, business development companies, or separately managed institutional accounts.

Definition 1.1 · Middle-Market Enterprise Profile

The core middle market comprises non-financial corporations with annual earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) between $10 million and $100 million. Borrowers frequently seek debt financing to execute private equity buyouts, recapitalizations, or add-on acquisitions.

In a comprehensive survey of 188 private debt fund managers, Block, Jang, Kaplan & Schulze (2024) find that direct lending funds target gross unlevered internal rates of return (IRRs) between 8% and 12%, and levered IRRs between 12% and 15%. Managers cite speed of closing, certainty of terms, flexibility of structural covenants, and confidentiality as the decisive non-price advantages that permit private lenders to command a premium spread over public syndicated debt.

2. Floating-Rate Loan Pricing, Floors & OID Math

Direct loans are predominantly floating-rate obligations. The coupon resets quarterly based on a reference benchmark rate—historically 3-month USD LIBOR, and now the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR)—plus a negotiated contractual credit spread \(s\). Two structural features protect lender cash flows across macroeconomic environments:

  • Reference Rate Floor (\(F\)): A contractual minimum on the benchmark rate (typically 1.00% = 100 bps), ensuring that lenders receive a minimum cash yield even if policy rates fall to zero.
  • Original Issue Discount (OID): An upfront deduction from the funded loan proceeds. If a borrower issues $100 million in face value at an OID of 2.00 points (a 98 purchase price), the borrower receives $98 million at closing but owes $100 million at maturity.
Theorem 1.1 · All-In Annualized Direct Loan Yield

Consider a floating-rate direct loan issued with reference benchmark rate \(R_{\text{ref}}\), contractual floor \(F\), credit spread \(s\), original issue discount \(\text{OID}\) (as a fraction of par), and weighted average life \(\text{WAL}\) in years. The linearized all-in annualized yield \(y_{\text{all-in}}\) is given by:

\[ y_{\text{all-in}} = \frac{\max(R_{\text{ref}}, F) + s + \frac{\text{OID}}{\text{WAL}}}{1 - \text{OID}} \]
Proof

Let par principal be normalized to 1.0. At inception \(t = 0\), the lender funds net capital equal to the purchase price \(P_0 = 1 - \text{OID}\). In each period, the loan pays cash coupon interest at the effective rate \(c = \max(R_{\text{ref}}, F) + s\) on par face value. Over the weighted average life \(\text{WAL}\), the upfront discount \(\text{OID}\) amortizes into income at the straight-line annual rate \(\frac{\text{OID}}{\text{WAL}}\). Total annual dollar income generated per unit of par is \(c + \frac{\text{OID}}{\text{WAL}}\). Dividing total annual dollar return by the initial invested capital \(P_0 = 1 - \text{OID}\) yields:

\[ y_{\text{all-in}} = \frac{\max(R_{\text{ref}}, F) + s + \frac{\text{OID}}{\text{WAL}}}{1 - \text{OID}} \]

This establishes the formula.

Remark 1.1 · Prepayment Call Protection

Because direct loans carry floating coupons, borrowers face an incentive to refinance when credit spreads tighten or after de-leveraging. Direct credit agreements mitigate early termination via contractual call protection: typically a "102 / 101" structure (a 2.0% penalty if prepaid in year 1, 1.0% in year 2, and par thereafter) or a 12-month make-whole premium.

3. Covenant Structures & EBITDA Add-Backs

Davydiuk, Marchuk & Rosen (2024) demonstrate that direct lenders employ financial maintenance covenants significantly more frequently than public syndicated loan arrangers. While broadly syndicated loans transitioned almost entirely to "covenant-lite" structures post-2015, direct loans traditionally required quarterly compliance with two primary balance-sheet ratios:

  • Leverage Covenant: Maximum Total Debt / Adjusted EBITDA (e.g., capped at 5.50x or 6.00x), often stepping down annually.
  • Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio (FCCR): Minimum (EBITDA − CapEx − Cash Taxes) / (Debt Service + Cash Interest), typically required to exceed 1.15x to 1.25x.
Definition 1.2 · EBITDA Add-Back Distortions

The denominator of leverage covenants is "Adjusted EBITDA," which permits borrowers and financial sponsors to inflate reported earnings by adding back projected cost synergies, restructuring expenses, transaction fees, and non-recurring charges. If an enterprise with $20 million in unadjusted EBITDA includes $5 million in prospective synergies, an apparent 5.0x leverage ratio ($125 million debt / $25 million Adjusted EBITDA) represents an effective 6.25x unadjusted leverage ratio.

4. Inline Numerical Lab: Floating-Rate Loan Yield Matrix

The numerical lab below implements the all-in yield formula under varying SOFR reference rate environments, verifying the non-linear protection afforded by benchmark floors and the sensitivity of net expected return to annual default rates and loss given default (LGD).

Lab 1 · Direct Lending All-In Yield & Return Sensitivity Engine

At reference rates below the contractual floor (SOFR ≤ 1.0%), all-in yields remain constant at 7.8231%, proving that rate floors insulate private credit investors against benchmark rate collapses.

"""Lab PM3-L01-1: Direct Lending All-In Yield and Contractual Return Sensitivity.

SIMULATED: Models floating-rate direct loans across SOFR rate regimes, quantifying the
impact of reference rate floors, original issue discounts (OID), and credit loss provisions.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def compute_all_in_yield(
    sofr: float,
    spread: float,
    floor: float,
    oid: float,
    wal: float,
) -> float:
    """Calculate the annualized all-in yield of a floating-rate direct loan.

    Parameters
    ----------
    sofr : float
        Current 3-month term SOFR benchmark rate (decimal, e.g. 0.04 for 4%).
    spread : float
        Contractual credit spread over benchmark (decimal, e.g. 0.06 for 600 bps).
    floor : float
        Contractual benchmark rate floor (decimal, e.g. 0.01 for 100 bps).
    oid : float
        Original issue discount percentage (decimal, e.g. 0.02 for 2 points / 98 price).
    wal : float
        Weighted average life of the loan facility in years.

    Returns
    -------
    float
        Annualized all-in yield as a decimal.
    """
    effective_base = max(sofr, floor)
    cash_coupon = effective_base + spread
    purchase_price = 1.0 - oid
    annualized_oid_amortization = oid / wal
    return float((cash_coupon + annualized_oid_amortization) / purchase_price)


def compute_net_expected_return(
    all_in_yield: float,
    annual_default_prob: float,
    loss_given_default: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute the net expected return after subtracting credit losses.

    Parameters
    ----------
    all_in_yield : float
        Annualized all-in yield as a decimal.
    annual_default_prob : float
        Annual default probability lambda (decimal).
    loss_given_default : float
        Loss given default (1 - recovery rate) as a decimal.

    Returns
    -------
    float
        Net expected annualized return as a decimal.
    """
    expected_loss = annual_default_prob * loss_given_default
    return float(all_in_yield - expected_loss)


def main() -> None:
    spread = 0.060  # 600 bps
    floor = 0.010  # 100 bps floor
    oid = 0.020  # 2.0% OID (issued at 98)
    wal = 3.0  # 3-year weighted average life
    annual_pd = 0.025  # 2.5% annual default probability
    lgd = 0.35  # 35% loss given default (65% recovery)

    sofr_scenarios = [0.000, 0.005, 0.010, 0.020, 0.030, 0.040, 0.050, 0.060]

    print("Direct Lending All-In Yield & Net Return Matrix:")
    print("SOFR (%) | Effective Base (%) | All-In Yield (%) | Expected Loss (%) | Net Return (%)")
    print("-" * 85)

    for r in sofr_scenarios:
        eff_base = max(r, floor)
        y_all = compute_all_in_yield(r, spread, floor, oid, wal)
        exp_loss = annual_pd * lgd
        net_ret = compute_net_expected_return(y_all, annual_pd, lgd)
        print(
            f"{r * 100:8.2f} | {eff_base * 100:18.2f} | {y_all * 100:16.4f} | "
            f"{exp_loss * 100:17.4f} | {net_ret * 100:14.4f}"
        )

    # Floor protection analysis at zero SOFR vs 50 bps SOFR
    y_zero = compute_all_in_yield(0.000, spread, floor, oid, wal)
    y_50bp = compute_all_in_yield(0.005, spread, floor, oid, wal)
    y_floor = compute_all_in_yield(0.010, spread, floor, oid, wal)

    # Both zero and 50 bps must bind to the 1.0% floor
    assert np.isclose(y_zero, y_floor, atol=1e-8)
    assert np.isclose(y_50bp, y_floor, atol=1e-8)

    # Yield at 5.0% SOFR
    y_5pct = compute_all_in_yield(0.050, spread, floor, oid, wal)
    assert y_5pct > y_floor
    print("\nFloor Protection Assertion: Verified binding floor at SOFR <= 1.0%.")
    print(f"Incremental yield from 1% to 5% SOFR: {(y_5pct - y_floor) * 100:.4f} percentage points.")


main()

Where the canon breaks

The standard textbook theory of credit spreads posits that wider loan spreads reflect compensation for default probability and loss given default. In middle-market direct lending, this canon breaks down due to structural non-price terms. Chernenko, Erel & Prilmeier (2022) establish that the 150–250 basis point spread premium paid by direct loan borrowers cannot be explained by credit risk differences alone. Instead, it reflects an illiquidity and regulatory arbitrage premium: bank regulations impose stringent capital charges on non-investment-grade middle-market loans, allowing unconstrained private funds to extract economic rents for providing flexible, non-syndicated balance-sheet commitments.

Misconceptions

  1. Direct lending is identical to traditional commercial bank lending, merely conducted through nonbank asset managers.

    Direct lenders operate with closed-end fund structures or permanent capital vehicles without deposit-taking liabilities. Consequently, they are not subject to Basel III liquidity coverage ratios (LCR) or the Interagency Guidance on Leveraged Lending, allowing them to fund loans with higher total debt multiples and flexible covenant packages that regulated depository institutions are legally constrained from underwriting.

  2. A direct loan with a maintenance covenant of 5.5x Total Debt to EBITDA provides strict protection against balance sheet over-leveraging.

    Because loan credit agreements define Adjusted EBITDA with expansive management add-backs—often including unvetted future cost synergies and speculative transaction adjustments—actual economic leverage at closing frequently exceeds the contractual ratio by 1.0x to 2.0x EBITDA turns, significantly delaying technical default triggers.

  3. Rising interest rates automatically enhance the net risk-adjusted returns of floating-rate private credit funds.

    While floating-rate coupons increase gross asset yields during monetary tightening, they simultaneously elevate borrower interest burdens. For highly leveraged borrowers, debt service can overwhelm operating cash flows, driving fixed-charge coverage ratios below 1.0x and triggering a non-linear surge in borrower default probabilities that can exceed the gross coupon gain.

Problem set

Mechanics

A middle-market borrower issues a 5-year direct loan with a par face value of $50 million. The contractual terms specify 3-month SOFR plus 625 bps, an original issue discount (OID) of 2.50 points, a SOFR floor of 1.00%, and an expected weighted average life (WAL) of 3.0 years. If 3-month SOFR is currently 4.50%, compute the exact cash coupon rate and the linearized all-in annualized yield.

Worked solution

1. Effective base rate: \(\max(4.50\%, 1.00\%) = 4.50\%\).

2. Contractual cash coupon: \(c = 4.50\% + 6.25\% = 10.75\%\).

3. Purchase price: \(P_0 = 1.0 - 0.025 = 0.975\).

4. Annualized OID amortization: \(\frac{0.025}{3.0} = 0.008333\) (83.33 bps).

5. Linearized all-in yield: \(y_{\text{all-in}} = \frac{0.1075 + 0.008333}{0.975} = \frac{0.115833}{0.975} \approx 0.118803\) or 11.8803%.

Using the loan in Problem 1, assume 3-month SOFR drops to 0.25%. Compute the revised cash coupon rate and all-in annualized yield, demonstrating the monetary benefit of the 1.00% SOFR floor.

Worked solution

1. With the floor binding: effective base rate is \(\max(0.25\%, 1.00\%) = 1.00\%\).

2. Cash coupon: \(c = 1.00\% + 6.25\% = 7.25\%\).

3. All-in yield: \(y_{\text{all-in}} = \frac{0.0725 + 0.008333}{0.975} = \frac{0.080833}{0.975} \approx 0.082906\) or 8.2906%.

4. Without the floor, the coupon would have collapsed to \(0.25\% + 6.25\% = 6.50\%\), resulting in an all-in yield of \(\frac{0.0650 + 0.008333}{0.975} = 7.5214\%\). The floor preserves 76.92 basis points of annualized yield.

A direct lending fund holds a portfolio of $500 million in senior secured middle-market loans with an average all-in gross yield of 11.50%. The fund manager estimates an annual default rate of 3.00% and an average recovery rate of 70.0% upon default. Calculate the annual expected dollar credit loss and the fund's net expected portfolio yield.

Worked solution

1. Loss given default (LGD): \(\text{LGD} = 1.0 - 0.70 = 0.30\) (30.0%).

2. Annual expected credit loss rate: \(\text{EL} = \text{PD} \times \text{LGD} = 0.030 \times 0.30 = 0.0090\) (90 basis points).

3. Annual dollar expected loss: \(\$500\text{M} \times 0.0090 = \$4.50\text{ million}\).

4. Net expected portfolio yield: \(y_{\text{net}} = 11.50\% - 0.90\% = 10.60\%\).

Proof

Prove that when original issue discount \(\text{OID} > 0\), an increase in benchmark reference rates \(R_{\text{ref}}\) expands the all-in yield by strictly more than one-for-one (i.e., \(\frac{\partial y_{\text{all-in}}}{\partial R_{\text{ref}}} > 1\) when \(R_{\text{ref}} > F\)).

Worked solution

Let the all-in yield be defined by \(y_{\text{all-in}} = \frac{\max(R_{\text{ref}}, F) + s + \frac{\text{OID}}{\text{WAL}}}{1 - \text{OID}}\). For \(R_{\text{ref}} > F\), \(\max(R_{\text{ref}}, F) = R_{\text{ref}}\). Differentiating with respect to \(R_{\text{ref}}\):

\[ \frac{\partial y_{\text{all-in}}}{\partial R_{\text{ref}}} = \frac{1}{1 - \text{OID}} \]

Since \(\text{OID} > 0\), we have \(0 < 1 - \text{OID} < 1\). Therefore, \(\frac{1}{1 - \text{OID}} > 1\). For instance, with an OID of 2% (\(0.02\)), the derivative is \(\frac{1}{0.98} \approx 1.0204\), confirming that the investor captures greater than 100% of benchmark rate increases due to buying the debt at a discount to par.

Prove that the interest coverage ratio of a floating-rate debt issuer decreases monotonically with the benchmark interest rate \(R_{\text{ref}}\) whenever operating earnings are invariant to interest rate shifts.

Worked solution

Let borrower EBITDA be denoted by \(E > 0\) and total debt principal by \(D > 0\). The interest coverage ratio is \(\text{ICR}(R) = \frac{E}{D \cdot (R + s)}\) for \(R \ge F\). Differentiating \(\text{ICR}\) with respect to \(R\):

\[ \frac{\partial \text{ICR}}{\partial R} = -\frac{E \cdot D}{(D \cdot (R + s))^2} = -\frac{E}{D (R + s)^2} \]

Because \(E > 0\) and \(D (R + s)^2 > 0\), the derivative is strictly negative everywhere on \(R \ge F\). Thus, coverage declines monotonically with rising policy rates, proving that floating-rate debt structures inherently heighten borrower default risk during monetary tightening cycles.

Let \(E_{\text{rep}}\) be reported EBITDA and let \(A \ge 0\) be management add-backs such that contractual Adjusted EBITDA is \(E_{\text{adj}} = E_{\text{rep}} + A\). Prove that for a fixed debt amount \(D\), the relative underestimation of leverage \(\frac{L_{\text{true}} - L_{\text{rep}}}{L_{\text{rep}}}\) is equal to \(\frac{A}{E_{\text{rep}}}\), where \(L_{\text{true}} = D / E_{\text{rep}}\) and \(L_{\text{rep}} = D / E_{\text{adj}}\).

Worked solution

By definition, \(L_{\text{true}} = \frac{D}{E_{\text{rep}}}\) and \(L_{\text{rep}} = \frac{D}{E_{\text{rep}} + A}\). The relative difference is:

\[ \frac{L_{\text{true}} - L_{\text{rep}}}{L_{\text{rep}}} = \frac{\frac{D}{E_{\text{rep}}} - \frac{D}{E_{\text{rep}} + A}}{\frac{D}{E_{\text{rep}} + A}} = \frac{\frac{D(E_{\text{rep}} + A) - D E_{\text{rep}}}{E_{\text{rep}}(E_{\text{rep}} + A)}}{\frac{D}{E_{\text{rep}} + A}} = \frac{\frac{D \cdot A}{E_{\text{rep}}(E_{\text{rep}} + A)}}{\frac{D}{E_{\text{rep}} + A}} = \frac{A}{E_{\text{rep}}} \]

This proves that the percentage under-reporting of true leverage is exactly equal to the ratio of add-backs to unadjusted reported earnings.

Empirical

In their empirical investigation of U.S. middle-market lending, Chernenko, Erel & Prilmeier (2022) compare nonbank direct loans to commercial bank loans. State the primary empirical differences in borrower debt-to-EBITDA leverage and loan pricing documented in their sample.

Worked solution

Chernenko, Erel & Prilmeier (2022, Table 2) document that nonbank direct loan borrowers have an average debt-to-EBITDA ratio of 4.9x, compared to 3.5x for bank borrowers. Furthermore, direct loans carry an average credit spread approximately 190 basis points wider than bank loans after controlling for observable firm characteristics, reflecting compensation for higher financial leverage and illiquidity.

Examine the findings of Davydiuk, Marchuk & Rosen (2024) regarding covenant structures in direct lending vs. broadly syndicated loans. What proportion of direct loans contain financial maintenance covenants relative to the syndicated market?

Worked solution

Davydiuk, Marchuk & Rosen (2024, Table 3) show that over 80% of direct loans maintain at least one active financial maintenance covenant (such as maximum debt-to-EBITDA or minimum fixed-charge coverage). In contrast, in the broadly syndicated loan market, covenant-lite loans exceeded 85% of total issuance over the same period, underscoring direct lenders' reliance on bilateral monitoring.

Based on the survey evidence in Block, Jang, Kaplan & Schulze (2024), what fraction of private debt general partners report exercising equity cure rights or amending loan terms when a borrower trips a covenant, versus liquidating the firm?

Worked solution

Block et al. (2024, Table 4) find that fewer than 5% of technical covenant breaches lead directly to formal bankruptcy or foreclosure liquidation. Instead, over 75% of managers negotiate covenant amendments, fee concessions, or equity cures (where the private equity sponsor injects fresh subordinated capital), preserving enterprise continuity while resetting debt metrics.

Research

Explain the regulatory hypothesis for the post-2008 rise of private credit. How did the 2013 Interagency Guidance on Leveraged Lending issued by the Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC catalyze bank disintermediation in the middle market?

Worked solution

The 2013 Leveraged Lending Guidance established that loans exceeding 6.0x Total Debt / EBITDA or unable to amortize senior debt within 5 to 7 years would attract supervisory scrutiny and severe regulatory capital penalties. Because mid-market buyout transactions frequently require leverage of 5.5x to 7.0x, regulated commercial banks sharply curtailed origination. Nonbank private debt funds, unconstrained by banking prudential supervision, expanded origination to capture the resulting borrower demand, earning substantial illiquidity premia.

Analyze the structural fragility of private debt funds during a prolonged macroeconomic downturn characterized by high interest rates and falling corporate margins. Contrast this with the systemic run risk of depository institutions.

Worked solution

Depository institutions face systemic run risk due to asset-liability duration and liquidity mismatches: demand deposits can be withdrawn instantaneously, forcing fire-sales of illiquid loans. In contrast, private credit funds operate as closed-end limited partnerships with 7-to-10-year locked commitments, eliminating classic depositor runs. However, fund fragility manifests as credit impairment and hidden valuation write-downs: high debt service triggers debt defaults, causing cash flow suspensions to institutional LPs and forcing debt-for-equity restructurings.

Propose an empirical research design to test whether private debt lenders provide superior monitoring and workout efficiency compared to syndicate banks. Identify the key identification challenges and required instrumental variables.

Worked solution

An effective design compares post-default recovery rates and reorganization durations between direct loans and syndicated loans for borrowers experiencing similar exogenous industry shocks. The primary identification challenge is endogeneity: direct loan borrowers are systematically riskier and more leveraged. Researchers can instrument lender type using institutional fund dry-powder shocks or regulatory bank capital stress-test stringency, isolating the causal effect of bilateral nonbank monitoring on distress outcomes.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the economic role of a benchmark rate floor (e.g. 1.00% SOFR floor) in a direct lending credit agreement?

    Options

    A floor ensures that the base reference rate in the floating coupon calculation cannot drop below the contractual floor \(F\), preserving lender yield in low-rate regimes.

  2. A direct loan is issued at an original issue discount of 2.0% (purchase price 98). The coupon is 3-month SOFR + 600 bps with a 1.0% floor. If current 3-month SOFR is 5.0% and the loan has a 3-year weighted average life, calculate the linearized all-in annualized yield as a percentage.

    Effective coupon: \(\max(5.0\%, 1.0\%) + 6.0\% = 11.0\%\). Annualized OID: \(2.0\% / 3 = 0.6667\%\). All-in yield: \((11.0\% + 0.6667\%) / 0.98 \approx 11.9048\%\). With tolerance 0.05 on formula variants.

  3. Why do financial sponsors negotiate EBITDA add-backs in middle-market credit agreements?

    Options

    Adding projected synergies and non-recurring expenses increases Adjusted EBITDA, making the enterprise appear less leveraged and preventing covenant trips.

  4. What structural difference distinguishes broadly syndicated loans (BSLs) from direct loans?

    Options

    BSLs are liquid, broadly distributed, and covenant-lite; direct loans are bilateral or small club deals held on fund balance sheets with ongoing monitoring.

  5. According to Chernenko, Erel & Prilmeier (2022), why do middle-market firms borrow from nonbanks despite higher credit spreads?

    Options

    Chernenko et al. (2022) show that borrowers trade higher interest spreads for access to greater leverage capacity and execution certainty that regulated banks cannot provide.

  6. A company has unadjusted reported EBITDA of $20 million and $125 million in senior debt. The sponsor includes $5 million in EBITDA add-backs. Calculate the true leverage ratio (Debt / Reported EBITDA) minus the contractual reported leverage ratio (Debt / Adjusted EBITDA).

    True leverage: \(125 / 20 = 6.25\text{x}\). Contractual leverage: \(125 / 25 = 5.00\text{x}\). Difference: \(6.25 - 5.00 = 1.25\) leverage turns.

  7. How do direct lenders typically respond when a borrower violates a financial maintenance covenant?

    Options

    Block et al. (2024) document that over 75% of covenant breaches result in consensual amendments, waiver fees, or equity cures rather than adversarial liquidation.

  8. What is call protection in a private debt agreement?

    Options

    Call protection compensates lenders for reinvestment risk by charging prepayment premiums (e.g. 102/101) if the borrower refinances early.
