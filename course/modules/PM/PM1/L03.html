Internal rates of return and multiples on invested capital cannot establish whether private equity managers generate true economic outperformance. Because IRR assumes intermediate distributions are reinvested at the fund's own internal rate and MOIC ignores holding duration entirely, private fund benchmarking requires discounting cash flows by liquid market indices via the Kaplan & Schoar (2005) Public Market Equivalent (KS-PME).

1Econometric and Mathematical Limitations of Private Equity IRR

Institutional marketing presentations emphasize the internal rate of return (IRR). However, IRR exhibits three fatal mathematical and financial flaws when applied to closed-end private partnerships:

  1. The Reinvestment Rate Fallacy: The standard continuous or annual IRR equation \(\sum_{i=0}^n \mathrm{CF}_i (1 + r)^{-t_i} = 0\) mathematically presumes that all intermediate cash distributions received by the LP are immediately reinvested in other assets earning exactly \(r\). For an exceptional fund generating a 30% IRR, assuming limited partners can compound distributed capital elsewhere at 30% per annum across a decade is economically impossible.
  2. Scale and Duration Insensitivity: IRR is scale-invariant. A fund that calls $1M and distributes $3M after one year generates an astronomical 200% IRR but produces only $2M in wealth. Conversely, a mega-fund that calls $10B and distributes $20B after 5 years produces a 14.87% IRR but delivers $10B in absolute institutional wealth.
  3. Multiplicity and Discontinuity: Because private equity cash flow vectors alternate signs multiple times (e.g. initial calls, followed by distributions, followed by subsequent add-on capital calls or clawbacks), Descartes' Rule of Signs implies the existence of multiple internal rates of return or complex roots with no economic meaning.

Similarly, the multiple on invested capital (\(\mathrm{TVPI} = \frac{\sum D_i + \mathrm{NAV}}{\sum C_i}\)) captures total dollar expansion but ignores duration completely: a 2.0x MOIC achieved over 3 years represents a 26.0% annualized return, whereas a 2.0x MOIC achieved over 10 years represents only a 7.18% annualized return.

2The Kaplan & Schoar (2005) KS-PME Framework

To eliminate reinvestment assumptions and establish an objective market benchmark, Kaplan & Schoar (2005) formulated the Public Market Equivalent.

Definition 3.1 (Public Market Equivalent, KS-PME)

Let a private equity fund experience capital calls \(C_i \ge 0\) and distributions \(D_i \ge 0\) at dates \(t_i \in [0, T]\), with an unrealized net asset value \(\mathrm{NAV}_T\) at horizon \(T\). Let \(I_{t_i}\) denote the price level of a public equity benchmark index (such as the S&P 500 total return index) observed on date \(t_i\). The public market equivalent (KS-PME) is defined as:

\[ \mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^n D_i \left(\frac{I_T}{I_{t_i}}\right) + \mathrm{NAV}_T}{\sum_{i=0}^n C_i \left(\frac{I_T}{I_{t_i}}\right)} = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^n \frac{D_i}{I_{t_i}} + \frac{\mathrm{NAV}_T}{I_T}}{\sum_{i=0}^n \frac{C_i}{I_{t_i}}} \]
Theorem 3.1 (The KS-PME Wealth Ratio Theorem)

Consider an institutional limited partner with access to a public market index. Let the investor construct a counterfactual benchmark portfolio: at each private fund capital call date \(t_i\), the investor invests \(C_i\) dollars into the public index; at each distribution date \(t_i\), the investor withdraws \(D_i\) dollars from the public index. Let \(W_{\text{PE}}\) denote the terminal wealth delivered by the private equity fund, and let \(W_{\text{bench}}\) denote the terminal wealth of the public index under the counterfactual strategy. Then:

\[ \mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} = \frac{W_{\text{PE}}}{W_{\text{bench}}} \]

Specifically:

  1. \(\mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} > 1.0 \iff W_{\text{PE}} > W_{\text{bench}}\): The private fund generated strictly greater terminal wealth than investing in the public index after all fees.
  2. \(\mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} = 1.0 \iff W_{\text{PE}} = W_{\text{bench}}\): The fund matched the public benchmark index return exactly.
  3. \(\mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} < 1.0 \iff W_{\text{PE}} < W_{\text{bench}}\): The private fund destroyed value relative to public equity indexation.
Proof

Terminal wealth from the private fund at horizon \(T\) comprises all realized cash distributions compounded at the LP's liquid opportunity cost of capital \(I_T / I_{t_i}\) plus terminal NAV: \(W_{\text{PE}} = \sum_{i=0}^n D_i \frac{I_T}{I_{t_i}} + \mathrm{NAV}_T\). In the counterfactual benchmark portfolio, the total compounded value of all capital called is \(W_{\text{calls}} = \sum_{i=0}^n C_i \frac{I_T}{I_{t_i}}\). Taking the ratio directly yields \(\frac{W_{\text{PE}}}{W_{\text{calls}}} = \mathrm{KS\text{-}PME}\). Dividing numerator and denominator by \(I_T\) eliminates the terminal index level, proving that KS-PME is invariant to whether cash flows are compounded forward to \(T\) or discounted backward to \(t=0\).

3Empirical Performance Benchmarking in the Literature

Three seminal empirical investigations define the modern econometric literature on private equity performance benchmarking:

Kaplan & Schoar (2005) analyze 746 buyout and venture capital funds from 1980 through 2001 using Venture Economics cash flow data. They document that for buyout funds, the average equal-weighted KS-PME is 0.97 (underperforming the S&P 500 net of fees), while the capital-weighted KS-PME is 1.05 (slightly outperforming due to larger funds earning superior returns). They find strong return persistence across consecutive partnerships of the same general partner.

Phalippou & Gottschalg (2009) analyze potential data distortions in commercial private equity databases. They demonstrate that historical data contain substantial "living-dead" funds—funds older than 10 years that report positive unrealized NAVs that are never liquidated. Writing down these stale NAVs and adjusting for sample selection reduces the average buyout KS-PME to 0.88–0.92, indicating that the median buyout fund underperformed public markets by approximately 3% per year after fees.

Harris, Jenkinson & Kaplan (2014) challenge earlier database selection critiques using the comprehensive Burgiss dataset comprising over 1,400 institutional funds through 2011. They find that US buyout funds outperformed the S&P 500 across almost every vintage year from 1984 through 2008, generating an average KS-PME between 1.20 and 1.27 (representing an outperformance spread of 3% to 4% per year over public equities).

Lab 1 · The Public Market Equivalent and IRR Market Regimes

A SIMULATED fund operates across two distinct public market environments: Fund A in a bull market (index surges 180%), and Fund B in a bear market (index falls 30%). We test whether Fund A earns a high 25% IRR but underperforms the market (KS-PME < 1.0), while Fund B earns a modest 10% IRR but substantially outperforms (KS-PME > 2.0).

"""Lab 1 for PM1 L03: The Public Market Equivalent (KS-PME) and IRR Distortions.

SIMULATED: compares two private equity funds across bull and bear public market regimes.
Demonstrates why Internal Rate of Return (IRR) can mislead limited partners:
- Fund A: Operates in a raging bull market (index surges). Generates 25% IRR, but KS-PME < 1.0.
- Fund B: Operates in a bear market (index crashes 30%). Generates 10% IRR, but KS-PME = 2.09.
Computes exact MOIC, IRR, and Kaplan-Schoar PME.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq


def main() -> None:
    times = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0])

    # Fund A: Invests $100M at t=0, exits at t=4 for $244.14M (IRR = 25.0%)
    # Public market index surges: 100 -> 130 -> 170 -> 220 -> 280 (180% gain, ~29.4% annualized)
    calls_a = np.array([100.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0])
    dist_a = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 244.14])
    prices_bull = np.array([100.0, 130.0, 170.0, 220.0, 280.0])

    # Fund B: Invests $100M at t=0, exits at t=4 for $146.41M (IRR = 10.0%)
    # Public market index collapses: 100 -> 90 -> 80 -> 75 -> 70 (-30% loss)
    calls_b = np.array([100.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0])
    dist_b = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 146.41])
    prices_bear = np.array([100.0, 90.0, 80.0, 75.0, 70.0])

    # 1. Evaluate Fund A
    def npv_a(r: float) -> float:
        return float(np.sum((dist_a - calls_a) * (1.0 + r) ** (-times)))

    irr_a = float(brentq(npv_a, -0.5, 2.0))
    pme_a = float(np.sum(dist_a / prices_bull)) / float(np.sum(calls_a / prices_bull))

    # 2. Evaluate Fund B
    def npv_b(r: float) -> float:
        return float(np.sum((dist_b - calls_b) * (1.0 + r) ** (-times)))

    irr_b = float(brentq(npv_b, -0.5, 2.0))
    pme_b = float(np.sum(dist_b / prices_bear)) / float(np.sum(calls_b / prices_bear))

    print("--- Fund A (Bull Market Regime) ---")
    print(f"Annualized IRR: {irr_a * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Kaplan-Schoar PME: {pme_a:.4f} (Result: {'Outperformed' if pme_a >= 1.0 else 'Underperformed'})")

    print("\n--- Fund B (Bear Market Regime) ---")
    print(f"Annualized IRR: {irr_b * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Kaplan-Schoar PME: {pme_b:.4f} (Result: {'Outperformed' if pme_b >= 1.0 else 'Underperformed'})")


main()
--- Fund A (Bull Market Regime) ---
Annualized IRR: 25.00%
Kaplan-Schoar PME: 0.8719 (Result: Underperformed)

--- Fund B (Bear Market Regime) ---
Annualized IRR: 10.00%
Kaplan-Schoar PME: 2.0916 (Result: Outperformed)

The experiment confirms the fundamental flaw of unbenchmarked IRR: Fund A achieved a nominal 25.00% IRR, but its KS-PME is 0.8719, meaning an investor in public equities beat the private fund. Fund B achieved only a 10.00% IRR, yet delivered a KS-PME of 2.0916, generating more than double the wealth of public markets during a market contraction.

4Where the canon breaks

The standard Kaplan–Schoar PME implicitly assumes an equity beta of \(\beta = 1.0\) relative to the chosen public market benchmark. Because buyout transactions apply substantial leverage (typically 60% to 70% debt-to-enterprise value per lbo-mechanics), the underlying equity of buyout portfolio companies possesses a public equity beta between 1.3 and 1.7. During prolonged bull markets, discounting leveraged private equity cash flows by an unleveraged \(\beta = 1.0\) public index mechanically overstates private equity alpha, attributing systematic levered beta gains to managerial skill.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A private equity fund with an IRR of 20% is always superior to a fund with an IRR of 12%.”

    IRR does not adjust for the macroeconomic return environment. A 20% IRR in a year when public equities returned 30% represents negative economic value add, whereas a 12% IRR during a severe market collapse represents massive alpha. Relative performance requires PME benchmarking.

  2. “KS-PME measures the annualized percentage rate of return earned by the fund.”

    KS-PME is a dimensionless wealth ratio (similar to MOIC), not an annualized percentage rate. A KS-PME of 1.20 indicates that the fund generated 20% more total wealth than a public market benchmark, but it does not specify whether this excess wealth took 3 years or 10 years to accumulate.

  3. “Choosing the S&P 500 or the Russell 2000 as the PME benchmark yields nearly identical results.”

    Because private equity portfolio companies are predominantly mid-market firms, benchmarking against small-cap indices (Russell 2000) or industry-specific indices materially alters the measured PME relative to large-cap indices like the S&P 500, particularly during periods of large-cap tech divergence.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Single Cash Flow KS-PME Calculation

A fund calls $100M on Day 0 when the benchmark index is 200. At Year 4, the fund distributes $220M when the benchmark index is 300. There is no unrealized NAV. Calculate the KS-PME of the fund.

Solution

Discounted call: \(\frac{100}{200} = 0.50\). Discounted distribution: \(\frac{220}{300} = 0.7333\). \(\mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} = \frac{0.7333}{0.50} = 1.4667\). The fund generated 46.67% more wealth than the public benchmark index.

M2 · Multi-Period KS-PME with Residual NAV

A fund calls $40M at \(t=0\) (\(I_0 = 100\)) and $60M at \(t=1\) (\(I_1 = 120\)). The fund distributes $50M at \(t=3\) (\(I_3 = 150\)) and retains $80M in NAV at \(t=5\) (\(I_5 = 160\)). Compute the KS-PME.

Solution

Discounted calls: \(\frac{40}{100} + \frac{60}{120} = 0.40 + 0.50 = 0.90\). Discounted distributions plus NAV: \(\frac{50}{150} + \frac{80}{160} = 0.3333 + 0.50 = 0.8333\). \(\mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} = \frac{0.8333}{0.90} = 0.9259\). The fund underperformed the benchmark index by 7.41%.

M3 · Breakeven Exit Proceeds for KS-PME = 1.0

A fund draws $100M at \(t=0\) when \(I_0 = 1000\). The public market index doubles to \(I_5 = 2000\) at Year 5. What single exit distribution at Year 5 is required for the fund to achieve exactly \(\mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} = 1.0\)?

Solution

For \(\mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} = 1.0\), \(\frac{D_5}{I_5} = \frac{C_0}{I_0} \implies D_5 = C_0 \times \left(\frac{I_5}{I_0}\right) = \$100\text{M} \times \left(\frac{2000}{1000}\right) = \$200\text{M}\). The fund must return exactly $200M (a 2.0x MOIC) to match the public benchmark.

Proof

P1 · Invariance of KS-PME to Compounding Horizon

Prove that for any arbitrary valuation date \(T^* \ge T\), compounding all cash flows and terminal NAV forward to \(T^*\) using the public index return leaves KS-PME strictly unchanged.

Solution

Let \(\mathrm{PME}(T^*) = \frac{\sum D_i \frac{I_{T^*}}{I_{t_i}} + \mathrm{NAV}_T \frac{I_{T^*}}{I_T}}{\sum C_i \frac{I_{T^*}}{I_{t_i}}}\). Factor out \(I_{T^*}\) from both numerator and denominator: \(\mathrm{PME}(T^*) = \frac{I_{T^*} \left(\sum \frac{D_i}{I_{t_i}} + \frac{\mathrm{NAV}_T}{I_T}\right)}{I_{T^*} \left(\sum \frac{C_i}{I_{t_i}}\right)} = \frac{\sum \frac{D_i}{I_{t_i}} + \frac{\mathrm{NAV}_T}{I_T}}{\sum \frac{C_i}{I_{t_i}}} = \mathrm{KS\text{-}PME}\). The scalar \(I_{T^*}\) cancels identically, proving complete invariance to compounding horizon.

P2 · Linear Additivity of PME under Portfolio Aggregation

Consider \(M\) private equity funds with discounted calls \(c_m = \sum_i \frac{C_{m,i}}{I_{t_i}}\) and discounted distributions \(d_m = \sum_i \frac{D_{m,i}}{I_{t_i}} + \frac{\mathrm{NAV}_{m,T}}{I_T}\). Prove that the KS-PME of the aggregate portfolio is a weighted average of individual fund PMEs, with weights equal to each fund's share of total discounted capital called.

Solution

Aggregate KS-PME is \(\mathrm{PME}_{\text{agg}} = \frac{\sum_{m=1}^M d_m}{\sum_{m=1}^M c_m}\). Let \(w_m = \frac{c_m}{\sum_{j=1}^M c_j}\). By construction, \(\sum_{m=1}^M w_m = 1\) and \(w_m \ge 0\). Then \(\sum_{m=1}^M w_m \mathrm{PME}_m = \sum_{m=1}^M \left(\frac{c_m}{\sum c_j}\right) \left(\frac{d_m}{c_m}\right) = \frac{\sum_{m=1}^M d_m}{\sum_{j=1}^M c_j} = \mathrm{PME}_{\text{agg}}\), proving linear aggregation under capital weighting.

P3 · Relationship between MOIC and KS-PME under Flat Benchmark

Prove that if the public market index is strictly constant (\(I_t = I_0\) for all \(t\)), KS-PME is identically equal to the fund's Multiple on Invested Capital (\(\mathrm{TVPI}\)).

Solution

If \(I_{t_i} = I_0\) for all \(t_i\), then \(\frac{I_T}{I_{t_i}} = \frac{I_0}{I_0} = 1.0\). Substituting into the KS-PME definition yields \(\mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} = \frac{\sum D_i \cdot 1 + \mathrm{NAV}_T}{\sum C_i \cdot 1} = \frac{\sum D_i + \mathrm{NAV}_T}{\sum C_i} = \mathrm{TVPI}\). Thus, TVPI is the special case of KS-PME when the benchmark discount factor is identically zero.

Empirical

E1 · The Burgiss vs. Venture Economics Spread

In Harris, Jenkinson & Kaplan (2014), how does the buyout KS-PME calculated from Burgiss data compare with Venture Economics data for 1990s and 2000s vintages?

Solution

Harris, Jenkinson & Kaplan (2014) show that Burgiss data (sourced directly from institutional LP accounting back-offices) yields buyout PMEs of ~1.20 to 1.25 across both 1990s and 2000s vintages. In contrast, Venture Economics suffered from survivor and reporting dropouts after 1999, artificially depressing post-2000 performance to ~0.95. This proved that earlier findings of post-2000 buyout underperformance were artifacts of data degradation.

E2 · Capital-Weighted vs. Equal-Weighted Buyout Outperformance

According to Kaplan & Schoar (2005), why is the capital-weighted buyout PME (1.05) higher than the equal-weighted buyout PME (0.97)?

Solution

Kaplan & Schoar (2005) demonstrate that top-performing general partners grow their fund sizes in subsequent fundraises, whereas poor-performing GPs struggle to raise capital or exit the industry. Consequently, a disproportionate share of total institutional capital flows into large, successful funds, pulling the capital-weighted PME significantly above the unweighted average.

E3 · Impact of Living-Dead NAV Write-Downs

How does the write-down methodology of Phalippou & Gottschalg (2009) affect older vintage funds in the Venture Economics database?

Solution

Phalippou & Gottschalg (2009) identify hundreds of partnerships past their 10-year term that reported static, positive NAVs for years without distributing cash. When these residual NAVs are written down to zero (reflecting insolvent or unmarketable assets), fund TVPI drops and average PME falls by approximately 0.08 to 0.12, turning marginal outperformance into net underperformance.

Research

R1 · Duration Mismatch in KS-PME Benchmarking

Evaluate the duration distortion that occurs when a private fund with long-dated cash flows (average holding period of 6 years) is benchmarked against the S&P 500 during an inverted yield curve regime.

Solution

KS-PME discounts cash flows using realized cumulative equity index returns between call and distribution dates. If the public index experiences an unexpected macroeconomic shock, the private fund's performance is contrasted with the equity index's duration profile. If private equity cash flows are less cyclical or more resilient than public small-caps during a recession, the PME will spike even if the private companies did not create operational value, reflecting systematic duration and credit mismatches.

R2 · Industry-Specific PME Benchmarking

Explain why institutional LPs increasingly replace broad indices like the S&P 500 with matched industry or sector indices (e.g. S&P Software & Services for tech PE, S&P Healthcare for healthcare PE).

Solution

Over the 2010–2021 cycle, buyout investments concentrated heavily in enterprise software and healthcare. Because public tech equities outperformed the broader S&P 500 by thousands of basis points, benchmarking a software buyout fund against the S&P 500 generated an artificially high PME (~1.40). When benchmarked against an industry-matched tech index, the PME dropped below 1.0, proving that outperformance was driven by sector allocation rather than managerial alpha.

R3 · Unsmoothing NAVs in PME Calculation

How does applying the return unsmoothing framework of F7 to intermediate private fund quarterly NAVs affect the volatility of the KS-PME metric across vintage years?

Solution

Reported private equity NAVs exhibit extreme serial correlation due to appraisal-based valuation practices (return smoothing). Unsmoothing reported NAVs using autoregressive filters restores the true contemporaneous covariance between private asset values and public equity markets. When applied to interim PME calculations, unsmoothing reveals that private equity performance is substantially more volatile across market cycles than raw quarterly valuations suggest.

  1. What is the central reinvestment rate assumption built into the Internal Rate of Return (IRR)?

    Options

    IRR mathematically assumes that intermediate cash distributions compound at the fund's internal discount rate.

    Discounted call: \(100 / 100 = 1.0\). Discounted distribution: \(180 / 150 = 1.20\). \(\mathrm{KS\text{-}PME} = 1.20 / 1.0 = 1.20\).

  3. What does a KS-PME of exactly 1.0 signify?

    Options

    A KS-PME of 1.0 means the fund matched the public benchmark index return identically in dollar-weighted terms.
