The Efficient Execution Frontier

Just as Markowitz modern portfolio theory organizes static asset allocation into an efficient risk–return frontier, optimal execution theory organizes dynamic trading trajectories into an efficient frontier of execution shortfall. Tracing the locus of Pareto-optimal execution trajectories in expected cost–variance space establishes the mathematical boundary of what an execution algorithm can achieve. Any heuristic strategy—such as fixed-volume participation (VWAP) or arbitrary percentage-of-volume slicing—lies strictly in the interior of this space, incurring unnecessary market risk or excessive price impact. Understanding the geometry and convexity of the efficient frontier enables quantitative desks to calibrate execution algorithms to an institutional mandate's exact risk tolerance.

1Pareto Optimality in Trade Execution

In Lesson 3, we derived the optimal trajectory \(x_k\) for a specific scalar risk aversion parameter \(\lambda\) in the Almgren–Chriss model. We now generalize this perspective across all possible risk preferences to characterize the global set of non-dominated execution strategies.

Definition 4.1 · Dominance and Pareto Efficiency
Let \(\mathcal{X}\) denote the space of all admissible execution trajectories \(x = (x_0, x_1, \dots, x_N)\) satisfying the boundary conditions \(x_0 = X\) and \(x_N = 0\). Each trajectory \(x \in \mathcal{X}\) maps to a two-dimensional performance vector \((V[x], E[x]) \in \R_{\ge 0}^2\), representing its execution cost variance and expected cost. A trajectory \(x \in \mathcal{X}\) is said to dominate another trajectory \(y \in \mathcal{X}\) if: \[E[x] \le E[y] \quad \text{and} \quad V[x] \le V[y],\] with at least one strict inequality. A trajectory \(x^* \in \mathcal{X}\) is Pareto-optimal (or efficient) if no admissible trajectory dominates \(x^*\).

Definition 4.2 · Efficient Execution Frontier
The efficient execution frontier is the locus of all Pareto-optimal performance pairs \((V(x^*), E(x^*))\) generated by minimizing the expected cost for a given ceiling on cost variance: \[E^*(V) = \min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} E[x] \quad \text{subject to } V[x] \le V.\] By Lagrangian duality, this curve is parameterized by the risk aversion parameter \(\lambda \in [0, \infty)\): \[x^*(\lambda) = \argmin_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \{E[x] + \lambda V[x]\}.\]

2Mathematical Construction and Frontier Limits

By sweeping the risk aversion parameter from \(\lambda = 0\) to \(\lambda \to \infty\), the efficient frontier traces out a continuous, one-dimensional manifold in \((V, E)\) coordinates.

Theorem 4.1 · Extremal Boundaries of the Efficient Frontier
Let \(X\) denote total shares liquidated over horizon \(T\) across \(N\) intervals of duration \(\tau = T/N\).
1. The Risk-Neutral Boundary (\(\lambda \to 0\)): The trajectory converges to linear TWAP: \(x_k = X(1 - k/N)\). Expected cost reaches its global minimum: \[E_{\min} = \frac{1}{2} \gamma X^2 + \frac{\eta X^2}{T}.\] Variance reaches its global maximum: \[V_{\max} = \frac{1}{3} \sigma^2 X^2 T \left(1 - \frac{1}{2N}\right) \left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\right) \approx \frac{1}{3} \sigma^2 X^2 T.\]
2. The Infinitely Risk-Averse Boundary (\(\lambda \to \infty\)): The trajectory executes the entire position in the first interval: \(n_1 = X, n_k = 0\) for \(k \ge 2\). Variance reaches its global minimum: \(V_{\min} = 0\). Expected cost reaches its global maximum: \[E_{\max} = \frac{1}{2} \gamma X^2 + \frac{\eta}{\tau} X^2.\]

Proof
Under linear TWAP, \(n_k = X/N\) for all \(k\). By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(\sum n_k^2 \ge \frac{1}{N} (\sum n_k)^2 = X^2 / N\), with equality if and only if all \(n_k\) are equal. Therefore, \(E_{\text{temp}} = \frac{\eta}{\tau} \sum n_k^2 = \frac{\eta}{\tau} \frac{X^2}{N} = \frac{\eta X^2}{T}\), which minimizes expected impact. The variance under TWAP evaluates the sum of squared integers: \[\sum_{k=1}^N \left(1 - \frac{k}{N}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} j^2 = \frac{(N-1)N(2N-1)}{6 N^2} = \frac{1}{3} N \left(1 - \frac{1}{2N}\right)\left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\right).\] Multiplying by \(\sigma^2 \tau X^2 = \sigma^2 (T/N) X^2\) yields \(V_{\max}\). In the limit \(\lambda \to \infty\), any non-zero holding \(x_k > 0\) for \(k \ge 1\) incurs an infinite variance penalty \(\lambda V[x] \to \infty\). The only trajectory with zero variance is \(x_k = 0\) for all \(k \ge 1\), forcing \(n_1 = X\) and \(n_k = 0\) for \(k > 1\). Expected cost is then \(E = \frac{1}{2}\gamma X^2 + \frac{\eta}{\tau} X^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Geometry and Strict Convexity of the Frontier

The geometric curvature of the efficient frontier dictates the marginal cost of risk reduction.

Theorem 4.2 · Strict Convexity of the Efficient Execution Frontier
In \((V, E)\) space, the efficient execution frontier \(E^*(V)\) is strictly decreasing and strictly convex: \[\frac{dE^*}{dV} = -\lambda < 0, \quad \frac{d^2 E^*}{dV^2} > 0, \quad \text{for all } V \in (0, V_{\max}).\]

Proof
Consider the primal problem \(E^*(V) = \min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \{E[x] : V[x] \le V\}\). The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = E[x] + \lambda (V[x] - V)\). By the envelope theorem: \[\frac{dE^*}{dV} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial V} = -\lambda.\] Since \(\lambda > 0\) for any interior point where the variance constraint binds, \(\frac{dE^*}{dV} = -\lambda < 0\). Furthermore, differentiating with respect to \(V\): \[\frac{d^2 E^*}{dV^2} = -\frac{d\lambda}{dV} = -\frac{1}{dV / d\lambda}.\] As established in Theorem 3.1, increasing risk aversion \(\lambda\) monotonically accelerates liquidation, reducing holdings \(x_k\) at every step, which strictly decreases variance: \(\frac{dV}{d\lambda} < 0\). Therefore, \(\frac{d^2 E^*}{dV^2} = -\frac{1}{dV/d\lambda} > 0\). The efficient frontier is strictly convex. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Almgren–Chriss efficient execution frontier solver

Sweeping risk aversion \(\lambda\) from \(0\) to \(10^{-4}\) will generate a strictly convex Pareto frontier where expected cost \(E[x]\) increases from $100k to $127k while cost variance \(V[x]\) collapses by over 60%, confirming that the marginal cost of risk reduction \(-dE/dV\) steepens rapidly.

"""Lab MS3-L04-1: Almgren-Chriss efficient execution frontier solver.

Traces the Pareto-optimal frontier of expected execution cost E[x] vs cost variance V[x]
across risk aversion parameter lambda and verifies strict convexity.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def compute_cost_and_variance(
    total_shares: float,
    trajectory: np.ndarray,
    tau: float,
    volatility: float,
    gamma: float,
    eta: float,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Compute expected execution cost E[x] and variance V[x]."""
    trades = trajectory[:-1] - trajectory[1:]
    perm_cost = 0.5 * gamma * (total_shares**2)
    temp_cost = (eta / tau) * float(np.sum(trades**2))
    expected_cost = perm_cost + temp_cost

    holdings = trajectory[1:]
    variance = float((volatility**2) * tau * np.sum(holdings**2))
    return expected_cost, variance


def almgren_chriss_trajectory(
    total_shares: float,
    horizon: float,
    n_steps: int,
    volatility: float,
    eta: float,
    risk_aversion: float,
) -> np.ndarray:
    if risk_aversion == 0.0 or volatility == 0.0:
        return np.array([total_shares * (1.0 - k / n_steps) for k in range(n_steps + 1)], dtype=np.float64)

    tau = horizon / n_steps
    times = np.linspace(0.0, horizon, n_steps + 1, dtype=np.float64)
    arg = 1.0 + 0.5 * (risk_aversion * (volatility**2) * (tau**2)) / eta
    kappa = math.acosh(arg) / tau
    sinh_kt = math.sinh(kappa * horizon)

    trajectory = np.zeros(n_steps + 1, dtype=np.float64)
    for k in range(n_steps + 1):
        trajectory[k] = total_shares * math.sinh(kappa * (horizon - times[k])) / sinh_kt
    trajectory[0] = total_shares
    trajectory[-1] = 0.0
    return trajectory


def main() -> None:
    x_total = 500_000.0  # 500,000 shares
    horizon = 1.0  # 1 day
    n_steps = 20  # 20 intervals
    tau = horizon / n_steps
    volatility = 0.02  # $1.00 daily sigma on $50 stock
    gamma = 2e-7  # permanent impact parameter
    eta = 1e-6  # temporary impact parameter

    lambdas = [0.0, 1e-7, 5e-7, 1e-6, 5e-6, 1e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4]

    print("Efficient Execution Frontier (X=500k, T=1.0d, N=20, sigma=0.02, eta=1e-6):")
    print("  Lambda       | Expected Cost E[x] ($) | Variance V[x] ($^2) | Std Dev ($)")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    e_costs: list[float] = []
    variances: list[float] = []

    for lam in lambdas:
        traj = almgren_chriss_trajectory(x_total, horizon, n_steps, volatility, eta, lam)
        e_c, var = compute_cost_and_variance(x_total, traj, tau, volatility, gamma, eta)
        e_costs.append(e_c)
        variances.append(var)
        std_dev = math.sqrt(var)
        print(f"  {lam:12.1e} | {e_c:22.2f} | {var:19.2f} | {std_dev:11.2f}")

    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    # Verify frontier convexity: dE/dV < 0 everywhere
    de = [e_costs[i + 1] - e_costs[i] for i in range(len(e_costs) - 1)]
    dv = [variances[i + 1] - variances[i] for i in range(len(variances) - 1)]
    slopes = [de / dv for de, dv in zip(de, dv, strict=True)]

    print(f"Minimum Expected Cost (Risk-Neutral TWAP, lambda=0): $ {e_costs[0]:,.2f}")
    print(f"Maximum Expected Cost (High Aversion, lambda=1e-4):   $ {e_costs[-1]:,.2f}")
    print(f"Variance Reduction from TWAP to lambda=1e-4:          {(1.0 - variances[-1] / variances[0]) * 100:.1f}%")
    print(f"Frontier Slope dE/dV is strictly negative: min={max(slopes):.6f}, max={min(slopes):.6f}")


main()

4Optimal Strategy Selection and Indifference Curves

How should a quantitative fund select its optimal operating point along the efficient frontier? The optimal choice corresponds to the tangency point between the efficient frontier and the investor's iso-utility indifference curves.

Definition 4.3 · Execution Indifference Curves
Under the mean-variance utility functional \(U_\lambda(x) = E[x] + \lambda V[x]\), an iso-utility indifference curve in \((V, E)\) coordinates is a straight line with constant slope \(-\lambda\): \[E = U_0 - \lambda V,\] where lower values of \(U_0\) represent higher economic utility (lower overall execution penalty). The optimal execution strategy \(x^*(\lambda)\) occurs where the indifference line is tangent to the efficient frontier: \[\frac{dE^*}{dV} = -\lambda.\]

Remark 4.1 · The Inefficiency of Heuristic VWAP
Many institutional desks execute orders via Volume-Weighted Average Price (VWAP) algorithms that replicate historical intraday volume curves (the "U-shaped" volume smile: heavy trading at the open and close, light trading at midday). While VWAP mimics market volume, it is generally Pareto-inefficient in \((V, E)\) space:
1. Because trading rate slows during midday, inventory is held longer, increasing exposure to afternoon price shocks and inflating variance \(V[x]\).
2. Because VWAP does not optimize the Euler–Lagrange trade-off between local temporary impact \(\eta\) and inventory risk \(\sigma^2\), it lies strictly inside the inefficient interior of the frontier. A properly calibrated Almgren–Chriss algorithm can achieve the identical expected cost as VWAP with 15% to 30% lower execution variance.

5Where the Canon Breaks

The clean geometry of the Almgren–Chriss efficient execution frontier depends upon several mathematical idealizations:

  • Non-Convexity Under Non-Linear Impact: When market impact follows the empirical square-root law (\(h(v) \propto v^{1/2}\)) rather than quadratic penalties (\(v^2\)), the cost functional is non-convex. The efficient frontier can develop linear segments or non-smooth corners, where slight changes in risk aversion trigger discrete jumps in optimal trading speed.
  • Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Tail Risk Frontiers: When institutional risk is measured by Value-at-Risk or Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) instead of variance \(V[x]\), execution trajectories must account for fat tails and skewness. The resulting CVaR-efficient frontier differs significantly from the mean-variance curve, demanding aggressive acceleration when downside return tail risk is elevated.
  • Multi-Asset Liquidity Spillover: For a multi-asset equity basket, covariance in asset returns creates cross-sectional inventory risk, while cross-impact in order books creates liquidity spillover. The efficient frontier becomes a high-dimensional surface where liquidating one stock can alter the optimal liquidation speed of a correlated peer.

6Misconceptions

  1. Misconception 1: The efficient execution frontier allows a trader to achieve zero cost and zero variance simultaneously.

    Why it is wrong: There is a strictly positive lower bound on execution cost. Even with infinite horizon and zero volatility, liquidating \(X\) shares incurs unavoidable permanent impact \(\frac{1}{2}\gamma X^2\) and minimum temporary impact \(\eta X^2 / T\). Zero variance requires instant liquidation, which maximizes temporary impact cost.

  2. Misconception 2: VWAP execution always lies on the efficient execution frontier.

    Why it is wrong: VWAP is designed to benchmark against the market average price, not to minimize the mean-variance trade-off between impact and volatility. VWAP schedules lie strictly in the interior of the frontier, leaving substantial risk reduction uncaptured.

  3. Misconception 3: The slope of the efficient frontier is positive because higher risk means higher return.

    Why it is wrong: Unlike Markowitz portfolio frontiers where expected return is plotted against variance (positive slope), the execution frontier plots expected cost against variance. Fast trading increases cost while reducing variance; hence \(dE / dV = -\lambda < 0\). The frontier has a strictly negative slope.

7Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 4.1: A trader liquidates \(X = 500{,}000\) shares over \(T = 1.0\) day with parameters \(\gamma = 10^{-7}\) and \(\eta = 10^{-6}\). Compute the minimum possible expected execution cost \(E_{\min}\) under any admissible liquidation strategy.

Solution

Minimum expected cost occurs in the risk-neutral limit (\(\lambda = 0\)), which is linear TWAP: \[E_{\min} = \frac{1}{2} \gamma X^2 + \frac{\eta X^2}{T}.\] Permanent impact cost: \[E_{\text{perm}} = 0.5 \times 10^{-7} \times (500{,}000)^2 = 0.5 \times 10^{-7} \times (2.5 \times 10^{11}) = \$12{,}500.00.\] Temporary impact cost: \[E_{\text{temp}} = \frac{10^{-6} \times (2.5 \times 10^{11})}{1.0} = \$250{,}000.00.\] Total minimum expected cost is: \[E_{\min} = \$12{,}500.00 + \$250{,}000.00 = \$262{,}500.00.\]

Problem 4.2: In Problem 4.1, if daily volatility is \(\sigma = 0.02\), compute the maximum possible cost variance \(V_{\max}\) on the efficient frontier in continuous time.

Solution

Maximum variance occurs at the risk-neutral TWAP boundary (\(\lambda = 0\)): \[V_{\max} = \frac{1}{3} \sigma^2 X^2 T = \frac{1}{3} \times (0.02)^2 \times (500{,}000)^2 \times 1.0.\] \[(0.02)^2 = 0.0004.\] \[X^2 = 2.5 \times 10^{11}.\] \[\sigma^2 X^2 = 0.0004 \times 2.5 \times 10^{11} = 1.0 \times 10^8.\] \[V_{\max} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^8}{3} \approx 3.333 \times 10^7 \text{ dollars}^2.\] The standard deviation of execution cost under TWAP is \(\sqrt{3.333 \times 10^7} \approx \$5{,}773.50\).

Problem 4.3: At a specific point on the efficient execution frontier, an algorithm operates with risk aversion \(\lambda = 4.0 \times 10^{-6}\). If variance is reduced by \(\Delta V = -10{,}000\), what is the approximate change in expected execution cost \(\Delta E\)?

Solution

By Theorem 4.2, the local slope of the efficient frontier is \(\frac{dE^*}{dV} = -\lambda\). Using the first-order Taylor approximation: \[\Delta E \approx -\lambda \Delta V = -(4.0 \times 10^{-6}) \times (-10{,}000) = +0.040 \text{ dollars}.\] Reducing variance by 10,000 units requires paying an additional $0.04 in expected temporary market impact.

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 4.4: Prove that any non-constant trading trajectory \(v(t)\) over \([0, T]\) incurs strictly higher expected temporary impact cost than linear TWAP.

Solution

The total temporary impact cost is \(C[v] = \eta \int_0^T v(t)^2 dt\) subject to \(\int_0^T v(t) dt = X\). Let \(\bar{v} = X / T\) denote the constant TWAP rate. Any admissible trajectory can be written as \(v(t) = \bar{v} + \delta(t)\), where \(\int_0^T \delta(t) dt = 0\). Substituting into the cost functional: \[\int_0^T v(t)^2 dt = \int_0^T (\bar{v} + \delta(t))^2 dt = \int_0^T (\bar{v}^2 + 2 \bar{v} \delta(t) + \delta(t)^2) dt.\] \[= \bar{v}^2 T + 2 \bar{v} \int_0^T \delta(t) dt + \int_0^T \delta(t)^2 dt = \frac{X^2}{T} + 0 + \int_0^T \delta(t)^2 dt.\] Multiplying by \(\eta\): \[C[v] = \frac{\eta X^2}{T} + \eta \int_0^T \delta(t)^2 dt \ge \frac{\eta X^2}{T} = C[\bar{v}].\] Equality holds if and only if \(\int_0^T \delta(t)^2 dt = 0\), which requires \(\delta(t) = 0\) almost everywhere. Hence, linear TWAP uniquely minimizes expected temporary impact cost. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 4.5: Prove that if an execution algorithm randomizes its execution rate such that \(\tilde{v}(t) = v^*(t) + \epsilon(t)\) with \(\E[\epsilon(t)] = 0\), expected cost increases and the algorithm falls strictly into the interior of the efficient frontier.

Solution

Under random perturbations \(\epsilon(t)\), the expected temporary impact is: \[\E[C[\tilde{v}]] = \eta \int_0^T \E[(v^*(t) + \epsilon(t))^2] dt = \eta \int_0^T \left( v^*(t)^2 + 2 v^*(t) \E[\epsilon(t)] + \E[\epsilon(t)^2] \right) dt.\] Since \(\E[\epsilon(t)] = 0\) and \(\E[\epsilon(t)^2] = \Var(\epsilon(t)) > 0\): \[\E[C[\tilde{v}]] = C[v^*] + \eta \int_0^T \Var(\epsilon(t)) dt > C[v^*].\] Randomization strictly inflates expected cost without reducing variance \(V[x]\), moving the performance pair \((V, E)\) into the dominated interior above the efficient frontier. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 4.6: Prove that the efficient execution frontier \(E^*(V)\) is strictly convex by showing that for any two efficient pairs \((V_1, E_1)\) and \((V_2, E_2)\), any convex combination satisfies \(E^*(\alpha V_1 + (1-\alpha) V_2) < \alpha E_1 + (1-\alpha) E_2\) for \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\).

Solution

Let \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) denote the unique optimal trajectories generating \((V_1, E_1)\) and \((V_2, E_2)\) with \(x_1 \ne x_2\). Consider the blended trajectory \(x_\alpha = \alpha x_1 + (1 - \alpha) x_2\). Because \(x_\alpha(0) = \alpha X + (1 - \alpha) X = X\) and \(x_\alpha(T) = 0\), \(x_\alpha\) is an admissible trajectory. The expected cost functional \(E[x] = \frac{1}{2}\gamma X^2 + \eta \int_0^T \dot{x}^2 dt\) is strictly convex in \(\dot{x}\). Therefore: \[E[x_\alpha] < \alpha E[x_1] + (1 - \alpha) E[x_2] = \alpha E_1 + (1 - \alpha) E_2.\] Similarly, variance \(V[x] = \sigma^2 \int_0^T x(t)^2 dt\) is strictly convex in \(x\): \[V[x_\alpha] \le \alpha V_1 + (1 - \alpha) V_2.\] By definition of the frontier: \[E^*(\alpha V_1 + (1 - \alpha) V_2) \le E[x_\alpha] < \alpha E_1 + (1 - \alpha) E_2.\] This establishes strict convexity of the efficient frontier. \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 4.7: An execution desk tracks 5,000 executions of a participation algorithm (VWAP) and an Almgren–Chriss algorithm with \(\lambda = 5 \times 10^{-6}\). The VWAP algorithm achieved an average cost of $142,000 with variance $8.5 \times 10^8$. The Almgren–Chriss algorithm achieved an average cost of $142,000 with variance $6.2 \times 10^8$. Quantify the economic efficiency gain of Almgren–Chriss in terms of standard deviation reduction.

Solution

Standard deviation of cost for VWAP: \[\text{SD}_{\text{VWAP}} = \sqrt{8.5 \times 10^8} \approx \$29{,}154.76.\] Standard deviation of cost for Almgren–Chriss: \[\text{SD}_{\text{AC}} = \sqrt{6.2 \times 10^8} \approx \$24{,}899.80.\] The risk reduction achieved at identical expected cost is: \[\Delta \text{SD} = \$29{,}154.76 - \$24{,}899.80 = \$4{,}254.96.\] In percentage terms: \[\frac{4{,}254.96}{29{,}154.76} \approx 14.60\%.\] Almgren–Chriss achieves a 14.6% reduction in execution risk with zero penalty in expected cost, illustrating the Pareto inefficiency of heuristic VWAP.

Problem 4.8: A quant hedge fund's Investment Committee imposes a maximum 95% Value-at-Risk constraint on execution cost: \(\text{VaR}_{0.95} = E[x] + 1.645 \sqrt{V[x]} \le \$200{,}000\). If the efficient frontier is parameterized as \(E(\lambda) = 150{,}000 + 5{,}000 \ln(1 + 10^5 \lambda)\) and \(V(\lambda) = \frac{4 \times 10^8}{1 + 2 \times 10^4 \lambda}\), find whether \(\lambda = 10^{-5}\) satisfies the VaR mandate.

Solution

Evaluate \(E\) and \(V\) at \(\lambda = 10^{-5}\): \[1 + 10^5 \lambda = 1 + 10^5 (10^{-5}) = 1 + 1 = 2.\] \[E(10^{-5}) = 150{,}000 + 5{,}000 \ln(2) \approx 150{,}000 + 5{,}000 \times 0.69315 = 150{,}000 + 3{,}466 = \$153{,}466.\] \[1 + 2 \times 10^4 (10^{-5}) = 1 + 0.20 = 1.20.\] \[V(10^{-5}) = \frac{4 \times 10^8}{1.20} \approx 3.333 \times 10^8 \implies \sqrt{V} = \sqrt{3.333 \times 10^8} \approx \$18{,}257.\] Compute 95% VaR: \[\text{VaR}_{0.95} = 153{,}466 + 1.645 \times 18{,}257 = 153{,}466 + 30{,}033 = \$183{,}499.\] Since \(\$183{,}499 \le \$200{,}000\), \(\lambda = 10^{-5}\) satisfies the Investment Committee's VaR mandate.

Problem 4.9: A broker-dealer executes client parent orders using a parameterized engine. A client requests an execution schedule that minimizes expected cost subject to cost standard deviation not exceeding $10,000. Under parameters \(\sigma = 0.02, T = 1.0, X = 100{,}000\), does linear TWAP satisfy the client's constraint?

Solution

Under linear TWAP, continuous variance is: \[V_{\text{TWAP}} = \frac{1}{3} \sigma^2 X^2 T = \frac{1}{3} \times (0.02)^2 \times (100{,}000)^2 \times 1.0 = \frac{1}{3} \times 0.0004 \times 10^{10} = \frac{4 \times 10^6}{3} \approx 1.333 \times 10^6 \text{ dollars}^2.\] Standard deviation is: \[\text{SD}_{\text{TWAP}} = \sqrt{1.333 \times 10^6} \approx \$1{,}154.70.\] Since \(\$1{,}154.70 \le \$10{,}000\), linear TWAP easily satisfies the client's risk ceiling, and since TWAP minimizes expected cost, it is the globally optimal choice for this client.

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 4.10: Formulate the efficient execution frontier when the asset price exhibits stochastic volatility \(\sigma_t\). How does conditioning execution speed on instantaneous volatility alter the frontier?

Solution

Under stochastic volatility (e.g., Heston model \(d\sigma_t^2 = \kappa_v (\theta - \sigma_t^2) dt + \xi \sigma_t dW_t^v\)), the static open-loop trajectory \(x(t)\) is strictly sub-optimal. The efficient frontier must be generalized to dynamic closed-loop feedback policies \(v^*(t, x, \sigma_t)\) solved via the continuous Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation. Because volatility is stochastic, the optimal feedback policy accelerates trading when instantaneous volatility \(\sigma_t\) is low (trading is cheap in variance terms) and slows trading when \(\sigma_t\) spikes, provided the temporary impact coefficient \(\eta\) does not rise proportionally. Dynamic feedback expands the efficient frontier downward, achieving lower expected cost for any variance ceiling than static scheduling.

Problem 4.11: Analyze the efficient execution frontier of a multi-asset portfolio rebalance under a factor risk model. How does cross-sectional asset return correlation alter the frontier compared to independent single-asset scheduling?

Solution

For an \(M\)-asset liquidation vector \(\mathbf{X} \in \R^M\) with covariance matrix \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\), total portfolio variance is \(V[\mathbf{x}] = \tau \sum_{k=1}^N \mathbf{x}_k^\top \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{x}_k\). If holdings have offsetting factor exposures (e.g., long stock A and short correlated stock B), portfolio variance can be low even when individual holdings are large. An optimal multi-asset scheduler coordinates executions: it delays liquidating the paired positions to maintain factor hedges, liquidating unhedged idiosyncratic risk first. Treating assets independently ignores hedging covariances, forcing unnecessary early liquidations that inflate impact costs by 20% to 40%.

Problem 4.12: Evaluate how regulatory dark pool caps (such as MiFID II double volume caps) shift the efficient execution frontier for large institutional orders.

Solution

Dark pools allow institutional orders to cross at the midpoint with zero temporary market impact (\(\eta_{\text{dark}} = 0\)), but suffer from execution uncertainty (fill probability \(P_{\text{fill}} < 1\)). When regulations restrict dark pool volume, orders must be routed to lit electronic books, paying the full bid–ask half-spread and eating into visible depth. This shifts the effective temporary impact parameter \(\eta\) upward, translating the entire efficient execution frontier upward in \((V, E)\) space. To maintain the same variance ceiling, institutional funds must pay substantially higher expected execution costs, reducing strategy capacity and net alpha.

9Retrieval Quiz

  1. What does a point on the efficient execution frontier represent?

    Options

    The efficient execution frontier represents the locus of non-dominated trading trajectories in expected cost–variance space.

  2. What is the minimum possible execution cost variance \(V_{\min}\) achievable on the efficient execution frontier?

    By liquidating the entire position immediately at \(t = 0\), the position has zero future price exposure, so \(V = 0.0\).

  3. Why is the slope of the efficient execution frontier \(\frac{dE^*}{dV}\) negative?

    Options

    By the envelope theorem, \(\frac{dE^*}{dV} = -\lambda < 0\). Reducing risk incurs higher temporary market impact.

  4. Which execution trajectory achieves the absolute minimum expected execution cost?

    Options

    Under quadratic temporary impact, Jensen's inequality proves that uniform constant-rate trading (TWAP) uniquely minimizes expected cost.

  5. Under continuous TWAP, what fraction of \(\sigma^2 X^2 T\) is the maximum execution cost variance \(V_{\max}\)? (Give as a decimal: 1/3).

    \(V_{\max} = \frac{1}{3} \sigma^2 X^2 T \approx 0.3333\).

  6. Why does a heuristic VWAP algorithm typically lie in the interior of the efficient frontier?

    Options

    VWAP matches historical volume profiles rather than solving the calculus of variations, holding inventory unnecessarily during midday and increasing variance.

  7. What does strict convexity \(\frac{d^2 E^*}{dV^2} > 0\) imply about risk reduction?

    Options

    Convexity means \(-dE/dV\) increases as \(V \to 0\): cutting variance becomes exponentially costly in temporary impact concessions.

  8. If an indifference curve has slope \(-1.5\), what is the trader's risk aversion parameter \(\lambda\)?

    The slope of the indifference line is \(-\lambda = -1.5 \implies \lambda = 1.5\).
