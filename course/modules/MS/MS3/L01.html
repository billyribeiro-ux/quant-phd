The Square-Root Impact Law

Institutional trades are metaorders: large parent orders sliced into thousands of child executions over hours or days. Naive execution models assume market impact is linear in traded volume, following Kyle's classical equilibrium. Empirical reality decisively contradicts linearity: across global equity, futures, and currency markets, metaorder impact scales strictly with the square root of order size. This sub-linear scaling means large trades pay far lower marginal impact than linear extrapolation predicts, while small trades move prices significantly more than anticipated. Understanding the square-root law is essential for accurate portfolio capacity estimation, transaction cost forecasting, and optimal execution design.

1Anatomy of Market Impact: Metaorders vs. Child Orders

In market microstructure theory, trade executions alter the market price through multiple distinct economic mechanisms. Building upon the Kyle continuous auction model and Kyle's lambda from MS1, we formalize the distinction between single child executions and institutional parent orders.

Definition 1.1 · Metaorder
A metaorder (or parent order) is a sequence of \(N\) child orders submitted by a single institutional market participant with the goal of executing a cumulative volume of \(Q\) shares over a finite trading horizon \([0, T]\). Let \(\{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N\} \subset [0, T]\) denote the execution timestamps, and let \(q_k\) denote the signed volume of child order \(k\) (where \(q_k > 0\) for buys and \(q_k < 0\) for sells). The total executed quantity satisfies: \[Q = \sum_{k=1}^N q_k.\]

Market impact is the expected price change conditioned on the execution of order flow. We decompose impact along two foundational dimensions:

  • Temporary Impact: The localized price concession required to attract immediate counterparty liquidity. Temporary impact dissipates rapidly once trading pauses and does not persist into the long-run price.
  • Permanent Impact: The lasting price shift caused by the information conveyed by the order flow, altering the consensus fundamental valuation of Bayesian market makers as established in Glosten & Milgrom (1985).

Definition 1.2 · Peak Impact and Permanent Impact
Let \(S_0\) denote the undisturbed mid-price immediately prior to the arrival of the first child order at \(t = 0\). Let \(S_t\) denote the mid-price at time \(t \ge 0\). The peak impact (or total impact) \(I_{\text{peak}}(Q)\) of a metaorder of size \(Q\) completed at horizon \(T\) is: \[I_{\text{peak}}(Q) = \E\left[\frac{S_T - S_0}{S_0} \;\middle|\; Q\right].\] The permanent impact \(I_{\text{perm}}(Q)\) is the asymptotic price displacement remaining after counterparty liquidity has fully relaxed: \[I_{\text{perm}}(Q) = \lim_{\Delta t \to \infty} \E\left[\frac{S_{T + \Delta t} - S_0}{S_0} \;\middle|\; Q\right].\]

2The Empirical Square-Root Law

Extensive empirical investigations across diverse asset classes, geographic venues, and historical regimes establish that peak market impact follows a universal sub-linear scaling relation.

Theorem 1.1 · The Square-Root Law of Market Impact
Let \(Q\) denote the total volume of a metaorder executed over horizon \(T\), let \(V\) denote the average daily trading volume (ADV) of the asset, and let \(\sigma\) denote the daily price return volatility. The expected peak market impact scales according to the square-root law of market impact: \[I_{\text{peak}}(Q) = Y \cdot \sigma \cdot \sqrt{\frac{|Q|}{V}} \cdot \operatorname{sign}(Q),\] where \(Y\) is a dimensionless prefactor of order unity (\(Y \approx 0.5 - 0.7\)).

Remark 1.1 · Universality and Duration Independence
The square-root law possesses two remarkable and counter-intuitive empirical features:
1. Universality: The exponent \(\alpha \approx 0.5\) holds across large-cap US equities, emerging market stocks, sovereign bond futures, commodity contracts, and cryptocurrency order books. As shown empirically by Tóth et al. (2011) and confirmed in direct institutional trade estimation by Robert Almgren et al. (2005), "Direct estimation of equity market impact", Risk, the scaling law holds over four orders of magnitude of relative order size (\(Q/V \in [10^{-4}, 10^{-1}]\)).
2. Duration Independence: Crucially, \(I_{\text{peak}}\) depends upon the total executed volume fraction \(Q/V\), but exhibits virtually zero dependence upon execution duration \(T\). Slicing an order of size \(Q\) over two hours or over two full days yields approximately the identical peak price displacement \(I_{\text{peak}}\).

Lab 1 · Calibration and validation of the square-root law

Simulating metaorder executions across four orders of ADV magnitude will demonstrate that ordinary least squares regression in log-log coordinates recovers an empirical power-law exponent \(\alpha \approx 0.50 \pm 0.05\), confirming sub-linear concavity and illustrating how linear impact models drastically overestimate the trading costs of large blocks.

"""Lab MS3-L01-1: Calibration and validation of the square-root law of market impact.

Simulates metaorder executions across 4 orders of magnitude of size Q,
fits power-law scaling I(Q) = Y * sigma * (Q/V)^alpha, and validates alpha ~ 0.5.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def simulate_metaorder_impact(
    metaorder_size: float,
    daily_volume: float,
    daily_volatility: float,
    y_true: float,
    alpha_true: float,
    noise_std: float,
    rng: random.Random,
) -> float:
    """Simulate observed market impact for a metaorder execution."""
    normalized_size = metaorder_size / daily_volume
    true_impact = y_true * daily_volatility * (normalized_size**alpha_true)
    observed_noise = float(rng.gauss(0.0, noise_std * true_impact))
    return float(max(1e-6, true_impact + observed_noise))


def fit_power_law(sizes: list[float], impacts: list[float], daily_volume: float) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Fit log(I) = c + alpha * log(Q/V) via ordinary least squares."""
    log_x = [math.log(q / daily_volume) for q in sizes]
    log_y = [math.log(i) for i in impacts]
    n = len(log_x)

    mean_x = sum(log_x) / n
    mean_y = sum(log_y) / n

    ss_xx = sum((x - mean_x) ** 2 for x in log_x)
    ss_xy = sum((x - mean_x) * (y - mean_y) for x, y in zip(log_x, log_y, strict=True))

    alpha = ss_xy / ss_xx
    intercept = mean_y - alpha * mean_x
    coeff = math.exp(intercept)
    return coeff, alpha


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    daily_volume = 5_000_000.0  # 5 million shares/day
    daily_volatility = 0.02  # 2% daily vol
    y_true = 0.60
    alpha_true = 0.50

    # Metaorder sizes spanning 0.01% to 10% of ADV (4 orders of magnitude)
    fractions = [0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.002, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10]
    sizes = [f * daily_volume for f in fractions]

    print("Square-Root Law Market Impact Calibration (ADV=5M, sigma=2.0%):")
    print("  Q (Shares) | % ADV | Obs Impact (bps) | Sqrt Model (bps) | Lin Model (bps)")
    print("----------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    observed_impacts: list[float] = []
    for q, frac in zip(sizes, fractions, strict=True):
        obs = simulate_metaorder_impact(q, daily_volume, daily_volatility, y_true, alpha_true, 0.02, rng)
        observed_impacts.append(obs)
        sqrt_model = y_true * daily_volatility * math.sqrt(q / daily_volume)
        # Linear model calibrated to match at median size (0.5% ADV)
        lin_model = y_true * daily_volatility * math.sqrt(0.005) * (frac / 0.005)

        obs_bps = obs * 10000
        sq_bps = sqrt_model * 10000
        lin_bps = lin_model * 10000
        print(f"{q:12,.0f} | {frac * 100:4.2f}% | {obs_bps:16.2f} | {sq_bps:16.2f} | {lin_bps:15.2f}")

    fitted_coeff, fitted_alpha = fit_power_law(sizes, observed_impacts, daily_volume)
    fitted_y = fitted_coeff / daily_volatility

    print("----------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    print(f"Log-log OLS Estimate: alpha = {fitted_alpha:.4f} (True = {alpha_true:.4f})")
    print(f"Calibrated Y Factor:  Y     = {fitted_y:.4f} (True = {y_true:.4f})")
    print("Sub-linear Concavity: dI/dQ strictly decreases with order size Q.")

3The Latent Order Book and Critical Liquidity Mechanics

Why does market impact scale with a square root rather than linearly? As emphasized by Bouchaud et al. (2018), visible limit order books display only a tiny fraction of market participants' true trading intentions. Most institutional liquidity resides in the latent order book.

Definition 1.3 · Latent Order Book
The latent order book represents the unrevealed supply and demand schedules of long-term investors who stand ready to buy or sell if price deviates from their reservation values, but who withhold public quotes to avoid free-option exposure.

Theorem 1.2 · Linear Latent Density Implies Square-Root Impact
Suppose the latent liquidity density \(\rho(p)\) (shares per dollar) near the current mid-price \(p_0\) is linear in price displacement: \[\rho(p) = \lambda_{\text{latent}} |p - p_0|, \quad \text{for } \lambda_{\text{latent}} > 0.\] Then the cumulative volume \(Q(p)\) consumed to shift the price by \(\Delta p = p - p_0\) satisfies: \[Q(\Delta p) = \int_{p_0}^{p_0 + \Delta p} \lambda_{\text{latent}} (p' - p_0) \, dp' = \frac{1}{2} \lambda_{\text{latent}} (\Delta p)^2.\] Inverting for price displacement as a function of volume \(Q\) yields: \[\Delta p(Q) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda_{\text{latent}}}} \cdot \sqrt{Q} \propto Q^{1/2}.\]

Proof
By symmetry, at the prevailing equilibrium mid-price \(p_0\), net order flow is balanced and the density of orders in the immediate neighborhood of \(p_0\) vanishes: \(\rho(p_0) = 0\). Assuming a Taylor series expansion of latent order density around \(p_0\), the leading non-zero term is linear: \(\rho(p) = \rho'(p_0)|p - p_0| + O(|p - p_0|^2)\). Integrating this linear density over the price range \([p_0, p_0 + \Delta p]\) gives \(Q = \frac{1}{2} \rho'(p_0) (\Delta p)^2\). Taking the principal square root yields \(\Delta p = \sqrt{2 / \rho'(p_0)} \sqrt{Q}\), which establishes the square-root scaling exponent \(\alpha = 1/2\). \(\blacksquare\)

4The Manipulation Arbitrage Dilemma

While the square-root law accurately characterizes peak metaorder impact, incorporating sub-linear impact into dynamic execution models poses a severe theoretical challenge: dynamic arbitrage.

Theorem 1.3 · Price Manipulation Under Instantaneous Concave Impact
Suppose an asset's price dynamics satisfy \(S_t = S_0 + f(Q_t)\), where \(f(Q) = \operatorname{sign}(Q) |Q|^\alpha\) is an instantaneous, permanent sub-linear impact function with \(0 < \alpha < 1\). Then there exists a deterministic round-trip trading strategy that generates strictly positive expected profits with zero net share change (price manipulation arbitrage).

Proof
Consider an investor who buys \(Q > 0\) shares in \(K\) small tranches of size \(q = Q/K\), and subsequently liquidates the entire position in a single large block trade of size \(Q\). Under sub-linear impact \(f(Q) = Q^\alpha\), the total cost of acquiring the position is: \[C_{\text{buy}} = \sum_{j=1}^K q \cdot [S_0 + f(j q)] = q K S_0 + q \sum_{j=1}^K (j q)^\alpha \approx Q S_0 + q^{1+\alpha} \int_0^K x^\alpha dx = Q S_0 + \frac{Q^{1+\alpha}}{1 + \alpha}.\] The liquidation trade of size \(Q\) occurs at the elevated price \(S_K = S_0 + Q^\alpha\). The gross proceeds from selling are: \[R_{\text{sell}} = Q \cdot (S_0 + Q^\alpha) = Q S_0 + Q^{1+\alpha}.\] The net profit from the round trip is: \[\Pi = R_{\text{sell}} - C_{\text{buy}} = Q^{1+\alpha} - \frac{Q^{1+\alpha}}{1 + \alpha} = \left(\frac{\alpha}{1 + \alpha}\right) Q^{1+\alpha} > 0.\] Because \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\), the profit \(\Pi\) is strictly positive, creating a free lunch. Therefore, permanent impact cannot be non-linear without violating no-arbitrage. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.2 · The Resolution
To eliminate price manipulation arbitrage while preserving the empirical square-root law, theoretical finance establishes that permanent impact must be linear in volume (\(I_{\text{perm}} \propto Q\)), while the concave component of impact must be transient, decaying over time as the order book relaxes. This insight leads directly to the propagator models developed in Lesson 2.

5Where the Canon Breaks

The square-root impact law is one of the most robust empirical relationships in financial economics, but it breaks down under distinct market stress regimes:

  • Crowded Liquidation Cascades: When multiple algorithmic funds attempt to liquidate identical factor exposures simultaneously (as during the August 2007 Quant Meltdown or the March 2020 Treasury basis dislocation), collective metaorder volume exhausts the latent order book. The square-root law collapses into super-linear impact (\(I \propto Q^\beta\) with \(\beta > 1\)), triggering liquidity black holes.
  • Extreme Illiquidity and Micro-Cap Regimes: In small-cap equities or illiquid bond issues where the number of active market participants is small, the latent liquidity density is zero rather than linear, causing individual orders to clear discrete price levels with erratic, step-like price changes.
  • Asymmetric Directionality: In equity markets subject to short-sale restrictions, fire sales and margin calls create asymmetric market impact: sell metaorders frequently exhibit higher effective impact than equivalent-sized buy metaorders.

6Misconceptions

  1. Misconception 1: Executing an order twice as fast doubles its total market impact.

    Why it is wrong: The empirical square-root law depends primarily upon the fraction of daily volume \(Q/V\), showing negligible sensitivity to execution horizon \(T\). Doubling execution speed increases instantaneous temporary impact concessions, but leaves peak metaorder price displacement virtually unchanged.

  2. Misconception 2: Kyle's lambda proves that institutional price impact is strictly linear in order size.

    Why it is wrong: Kyle (1985) establishes linear impact under Gaussian fundamental value distributions and exogenous noise trading. In real limit order books, endogenous liquidity provision and the non-uniform latent order schedule produce sub-linear square-root scaling across all major liquid markets.

  3. Misconception 3: Permanent price impact can be modeled directly with a square-root formula without creating arbitrage.

    Why it is wrong: As proved in Theorem 1.3, an instantaneous sub-linear permanent impact law permits deterministic round-trip price manipulation (a buy-small, sell-large strategy that generates riskless profits). Permanent impact must be linear; the square-root component must decay over time.

7Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 1.1: A portfolio manager needs to purchase \(Q = 200{,}000\) shares of an equity with average daily volume \(V = 5{,}000{,}000\) shares and daily volatility \(\sigma = 1.8\%\). Assuming \(Y = 0.50\), compute the expected peak market impact in basis points and in dollars if the stock trades at $50.00.

Solution

The relative order size is: \[\frac{Q}{V} = \frac{200{,}000}{5{,}000{,}000} = 0.04.\] Using the square-root law: \[I_{\text{peak}} = Y \cdot \sigma \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Q}{V}} = 0.50 \times 0.018 \times \sqrt{0.04} = 0.50 \times 0.018 \times 0.20 = 0.0018 = 18.0 \text{ bps}.\] In dollar terms, on a $50.00 stock: \[\Delta P = 50.00 \times 0.0018 = \$0.09 \text{ per share}.\]

Problem 1.2: If a hedge fund increases its parent order size by a factor of 9, by what factor does its expected market impact increase under (a) Kyle's linear impact model, and (b) the empirical square-root law?

Solution

(a) Under Kyle's linear impact model (\(I \propto Q\)), multiplying order size by 9 multiplies impact by \(9^1 = 9.0\).
(b) Under the square-root law (\(I \propto \sqrt{Q}\)), multiplying order size by 9 multiplies impact by \(\sqrt{9} = 3.0\). Linear extrapolation would overestimate the impact of the larger order by a factor of \(9 / 3 = 3.0\).

Problem 1.3: An execution algorithm executes 25,000 shares in a stock with ADV \(V = 1{,}000{,}000\) and daily vol \(\sigma = 2.5\%\), observing an impact of 15.0 basis points. What is the calibrated empirical prefactor \(Y\)?

Solution

The relative volume is \(Q/V = 25{,}000 / 1{,}000{,}000 = 0.025\). The square-root law gives \(I = Y \sigma \sqrt{Q/V}\). Solving for \(Y\): \[Y = \frac{I}{\sigma \sqrt{Q/V}} = \frac{0.0015}{0.025 \times \sqrt{0.025}} = \frac{0.0015}{0.025 \times 0.15811} = \frac{0.0015}{0.003953} \approx 0.3795.\]

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 1.4: Prove that if total metaorder market impact follows \(I(Q) = Y \sigma (Q/V)^\alpha\), the marginal market impact of an additional share \(dI / dQ\) is strictly decreasing if and only if \(\alpha < 1\).

Solution

Taking the first derivative with respect to \(Q\): \[\frac{dI}{dQ} = \frac{\alpha Y \sigma}{V} \left(\frac{Q}{V}\right)^{\alpha - 1}.\] Taking the second derivative: \[\frac{d^2 I}{dQ^2} = \frac{\alpha (\alpha - 1) Y \sigma}{V^2} \left(\frac{Q}{V}\right)^{\alpha - 2}.\] Because \(Y > 0, \sigma > 0, V > 0\), and \(Q > 0\), the sign of \(d^2 I / dQ^2\) is determined entirely by \(\alpha(\alpha - 1)\). For \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\), \(\alpha(\alpha - 1) < 0\), which implies \(d^2 I / dQ^2 < 0\). Hence, marginal market impact \(dI / dQ\) is strictly decreasing in order size if and only if \(\alpha < 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 1.5: Prove that under a general sub-linear instantaneous permanent impact model \(f(Q) = A Q^\alpha\) with \(0 < \alpha < 1\), the profit from a round-trip price manipulation strategy of buying in \(K\) equal slices of size \(q = Q/K\) and selling in one block of size \(Q\) satisfies \(\lim_{K \to \infty} \Pi(K) = \frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha} A Q^{1+\alpha}\).

Solution

Let \(q = Q/K\). The total purchase cost across \(K\) discrete slices is: \[C_{\text{buy}} = \sum_{j=1}^K q [S_0 + A (j q)^\alpha] = Q S_0 + A q^{1+\alpha} \sum_{j=1}^K j^\alpha.\] By Euler–Maclaurin summation or Riemann sum convergence: \[\lim_{K \to \infty} \frac{1}{K^{1+\alpha}} \sum_{j=1}^K j^\alpha = \int_0^1 x^\alpha dx = \frac{1}{1 + \alpha}.\] Since \(q = Q/K\), \(q^{1+\alpha} = Q^{1+\alpha} / K^{1+\alpha}\), so: \[C_{\text{buy}} = Q S_0 + A Q^{1+\alpha} \left(\frac{1}{1 + \alpha}\right).\] Selling the entire block of \(Q\) shares at the final market price \(S_K = S_0 + A Q^\alpha\) yields: \[R_{\text{sell}} = Q [S_0 + A Q^\alpha] = Q S_0 + A Q^{1+\alpha}.\] The round-trip profit is: \[\Pi = R_{\text{sell}} - C_{\text{buy}} = A Q^{1+\alpha} \left(1 - \frac{1}{1 + \alpha}\right) = \left(\frac{\alpha}{1 + \alpha}\right) A Q^{1+\alpha} > 0. \quad \blacksquare\]

Problem 1.6: Prove that if the latent order book density follows a power law \(\rho(p) = \lambda |p - p_0|^\gamma\) with \(\gamma > 0\), the resulting market impact scales as \(\Delta p \propto Q^{1 / (\gamma + 1)}\). Identify the value of \(\gamma\) that generates the square-root law.

Solution

Integrating the latent density from \(p_0\) to \(p_0 + \Delta p\): \[Q(\Delta p) = \int_{p_0}^{p_0 + \Delta p} \lambda (p' - p_0)^\gamma dp' = \frac{\lambda}{\gamma + 1} (\Delta p)^{\gamma + 1}.\] Inverting this relationship gives: \[\Delta p(Q) = \left(\frac{\gamma + 1}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma + 1}} Q^{\frac{1}{\gamma + 1}}.\] The market impact exponent is \(\alpha = \frac{1}{\gamma + 1}\). To recover the empirical square-root law where \(\alpha = 1/2\): \[\frac{1}{\gamma + 1} = \frac{1}{2} \implies \gamma + 1 = 2 \implies \gamma = 1.\] Thus, a strictly linear latent order book density (\(\gamma = 1\)) is the unique power law that yields the square-root law of market impact. \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 1.7: An empirical econometrician fits the model \(\ln(I_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(Q_i / V_i) + \beta_2 \ln(\sigma_i) + \epsilon_i\) on 10,000 institutional parent orders. The estimated coefficients are \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.52 \pm 0.02\) and \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 0.98 \pm 0.03\). Perform a Wald test of the joint hypothesis \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0.5 \text{ and } \beta_2 = 1.0\).

Solution

Assuming asymptotic normality and negligible covariance between \(\hat{\beta}_1\) and \(\hat{\beta}_2\): The individual \(z\)-statistics are: \[z_1 = \frac{\hat{\beta}_1 - 0.50}{\se(\hat{\beta}_1)} = \frac{0.52 - 0.50}{0.02} = 1.00.\] \[z_2 = \frac{\hat{\beta}_2 - 1.00}{\se(\hat{\beta}_2)} = \frac{0.98 - 1.00}{0.03} = -0.67.\] The joint Wald test statistic is: \[W = z_1^2 + z_2^2 = (1.00)^2 + (-0.67)^2 = 1.00 + 0.44 = 1.44.\] Under \(H_0\), \(W\) follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with 2 degrees of freedom. The critical value at \(\alpha = 0.05\) is \(\chi^2_{2, 0.05} = 5.99\). Since \(W = 1.44 < 5.99\), we fail to reject \(H_0\). The empirical execution data is fully consistent with the theoretical square-root law.

Problem 1.8: In a sample of equity executions, a quantitative desk measures an average peak impact of 24 bps for metaorders executing 1% of ADV on stocks with 2.0% daily volatility. If the desk scales a new execution to 4% of ADV on a stock with 3.0% daily volatility, what is the predicted peak impact?

Solution

First, calibrate the dimensionless prefactor \(Y\) from the baseline: \[Y = \frac{I_1}{\sigma_1 \sqrt{Q_1 / V_1}} = \frac{0.0024}{0.02 \times \sqrt{0.01}} = \frac{0.0024}{0.02 \times 0.10} = \frac{0.0024}{0.0020} = 1.20.\] Now apply the calibrated \(Y = 1.20\) to the new execution: \[I_2 = Y \cdot \sigma_2 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Q_2}{V_2}} = 1.20 \times 0.03 \times \sqrt{0.04} = 1.20 \times 0.03 \times 0.20 = 0.0072 = 72.0 \text{ bps}.\]

Problem 1.9: A strategy trades $500M notional annually. Under a linear impact model calibrated at $1M trades, estimated annual transaction costs are $4.2M. Under the square-root law, average execution slices pay 45% less than the linear model. What is the annual dollar savings from using the accurate square-root cost model in strategy capacity calculations?

Solution

The revised annual execution cost under the square-root law is: \[C_{\text{sqrt}} = C_{\text{linear}} \times (1 - 0.45) = \$4.2\text{M} \times 0.55 = \$2.31\text{M}.\] The annual dollar cost discrepancy is: \[\Delta C = \$4.20\text{M} - \$2.31\text{M} = \$1.89\text{M}.\] Using the linear model would artificially underestimate strategy capacity by over-penalizing large rebalancing trades by $1.89M annually.

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 1.10: Formulate why market impact universality implies that financial markets operate in a critical, self-organized state near a liquidity phase transition.

Solution

In physical systems, universal scaling exponents independent of microscopic details (such as molecular structure or temperature) arise near critical points of second-order phase transitions. In financial markets, as established by Tóth et al. (2011), the universality of the square-root exponent \(\alpha = 0.5\) across disparate microstructures (open outcry pits, electronic matching engines, dark pools) suggests that the vanishing of order book liquidity at the current price (\(\rho(p_0) \to 0\)) is a self-organized critical state. Market makers constantly adjust resting orders to balance adverse selection against execution opportunity, maintaining the order book in a state of marginal stability.

Problem 1.11: Evaluate how the square-root impact law alters the optimal portfolio size and capacity scaling of momentum vs mean-reversion strategies.

Solution

Under a linear impact model (\(I \propto Q\)), total transaction costs scale quadratically in fund AUM: \(\text{Cost} \propto Q \cdot I(Q) \propto Q^2\), creating a sharp capacity ceiling where net alpha decays rapidly. Under the square-root law, total transaction costs scale as \(Q \cdot I(Q) \propto Q^{3/2}\). Because \(Q^{3/2}\) grows substantially slower than \(Q^2\), institutional strategy capacity is substantially higher than linear models predict. However, momentum strategies (which demand immediacy and trade in the direction of past price trends) suffer compounding impact costs, whereas mean-reversion strategies can provide liquidity passively, earning spread rather than crossing it.

Problem 1.12: Propose an empirical test to distinguish whether post-trade market impact relaxation follows an exponential decay or a power-law decay.

Solution

To distinguish exponential decay \(I(t) \propto e^{-\lambda t}\) from power-law decay \(I(t) \propto t^{-\beta}\), construct empirical post-trade price paths on institutional metaorders. Normalize price change by peak impact: \(R(t) = (S_{T+t} - S_0) / (S_T - S_0)\) for \(t \in [1 \text{ min}, 2 \text{ days}]\). In semi-log coordinates \((\ln R(t) \text{ vs } t)\), an exponential decay plots as a straight line with slope \(-\lambda\), whereas a power-law exhibits convex curvature. In log-log coordinates \((\ln R(t) \text{ vs } \ln t)\), a power-law plots as a straight line with slope \(-\beta\). Empirical tests in Bouchaud et al. (2018) reject the exponential model in favor of slow power-law relaxation with \(\beta \approx 0.2 - 0.5\).

  2. Using \(I = 0.6 \sigma \sqrt{Q/V}\), if a stock has daily volatility \(\sigma = 2.0\%\) and an order executes \(Q/V = 1.0\%\) of daily volume, what is the expected peak impact in basis points?

    \(I = 0.6 \times 0.02 \times \sqrt{0.01} = 0.6 \times 0.02 \times 0.10 = 0.0012 = 12.0\) basis points.

  3. Why does instantaneous sub-linear permanent impact permit round-trip price manipulation?

    Options

    Under sub-linear impact \(Q^\alpha\), the integral of marginal impact \(\int_0^Q x^\alpha dx = \frac{Q^{1+\alpha}}{1+\alpha}\) is strictly less than the block revenue \(Q \cdot Q^\alpha = Q^{1+\alpha}\), generating a free lunch.

  4. What does the "duration independence" of market impact mean?

    Options

    Empirically, executing size \(Q\) over 1 hour or over 8 hours yields virtually the same peak price impact \(I_{\text{peak}}\).

  5. If parent order size \(Q\) increases by a factor of 4, by what factor does peak market impact increase under the square-root law?

    Under the square-root law, \(\sqrt{4} = 2.0\). Impact doubles when size quadruples.

  6. In latent order book theory, what functional form of latent order density \(\rho(p)\) generates the square-root law?

    Options

    As proved in Theorem 1.2, integrating a linear density \(\int_0^{\Delta p} \lambda x dx = \frac{1}{2} \lambda (\Delta p)^2 = Q\) yields \(\Delta p \propto \sqrt{Q}\).

  7. What distinguishes a metaorder from an individual child order?

    Options

    A metaorder is the parent investment decision executed via an algorithmic sequence of child slices.

  8. In the standard square-root law formula \(I = Y \sigma \sqrt{Q/V}\), what is the typical empirical magnitude of the dimensionless prefactor \(Y\)?

    Empirical studies consistently find \(Y\) of order unity, typically between 0.5 and 0.7.
