Propagator Models of Impact

In Lesson 1, we established that instantaneous concave impact permits round-trip price manipulation. To reconcile the empirical square-root law of market impact with market efficiency, market impact must be transient: each trade shifts the price immediately, but this disturbance decays over time according to a response kernel. Real market order flow exhibits long memory—buy orders are persistently followed by buy orders for thousands of ticks—yet asset returns remain virtually uncorrelated. Propagator models solve this puzzle through a delicate mathematical cancellation: the slow power-law decay of impact precisely offsets order flow persistence, preventing price manipulation and preserving martingale price dynamics.

1Transient Price Impact and the Propagator Framework

Classical microstructure models (such as Kyle 1985 or Glosten & Milgrom 1985) assume that order flow produces purely permanent price changes. However, empirical tick data reveals that when a metaorder completes, prices systematically mean-revert, relaxing by 30% to 50% from their peak displacement. This phenomenon requires a transient modeling framework.

Definition 2.1 · Propagator Model
In a propagator model, the price \(S_t\) at time \(t\) is represented as the linear convolution of past signed trade executions \(\{\epsilon_s\}\) with a causal time-dependent response kernel \(G(t - s)\), plus an exogenous martingale innovation: \[S_t = S_0 + \sum_{s < t} G(t - s) \epsilon_s + \int_0^t \sigma \, dW_s,\] where \(S_0\) is the initial price, \(\epsilon_s \in \{-1, +1\}\) (or signed volume \(q_s\)) is the trade sign at timestamp \(s\), \(G(\tau)\) is the bare impact propagator, and \(W_t\) is a standard Brownian motion independent of order flow.

In continuous time, when trading proceeds at an instantaneous rate \(v(s) = \dot{Q}(s)\) shares per unit time, the price trajectory satisfies:

\[S_t = S_0 + \int_0^t G(t - s) v(s) \, ds + \sigma W_t.\]

Example 2.1 · Constant Rate Execution
Consider a buy metaorder of size \(Q\) executed at a constant rate \(v = Q/T\) over horizon \([0, T]\). At any time \(t \le T\), the cumulative expected price displacement is: \[I(t) = \int_0^t G(t - s) v \, ds = \frac{Q}{T} \int_0^t G(u) \, du.\] At execution completion \(t = T\), peak impact is reached: \[I_{\text{peak}} = I(T) = \frac{Q}{T} \int_0^T G(u) \, du.\] For \(t > T\), after all child orders have completed, the price relaxes according to: \[I(t) = \int_0^T G(t - s) v \, ds = \frac{Q}{T} \int_{t - T}^t G(u) \, du.\]

2Response Kernels: Exponential vs. Power-Law Decay

The mathematical properties of a propagator model depend critically upon the functional form of the decay kernel \(G(t)\).

Definition 2.2 · Standard Impact Kernels
We distinguish two primary parametric specifications for the response kernel:
1. Exponential Kernel: \[G_{\text{exp}}(t) = G_0 e^{-\rho t}, \quad \text{for } \rho > 0,\] characterizing limit order books with memory timescale \(\tau = 1/\rho\).
2. Power-Law Kernel: \[G_{\text{pow}}(t) = \frac{\Gamma_0}{(1 + t / \tau_0)^\beta}, \quad \text{for } \beta \in (0, 1) \text{ and } \tau_0 > 0,\] characterizing order books with slow, long-memory relaxation dynamics.

Theorem 2.1 · Peak Impact Under Power-Law Propagators
Let \(G(t) = \Gamma_0 t^{-\beta}\) with \(\beta \in (0, 1)\) for \(t \gg \tau_0\). A metaorder of size \(Q\) executed at uniform rate \(v = Q/T\) produces peak market impact: \[I_{\text{peak}} = \frac{\Gamma_0}{1 - \beta} \cdot \frac{Q}{T^\beta} = \frac{\Gamma_0}{1 - \beta} \cdot v^{1-\beta} \cdot Q^\beta.\]

Proof
Substitute \(G(u) = \Gamma_0 u^{-\beta}\) into the peak impact integral of Example 2.1: \[I_{\text{peak}} = \frac{Q}{T} \int_0^T \Gamma_0 u^{-\beta} \, du = \frac{\Gamma_0 Q}{T} \left[ \frac{u^{1 - \beta}}{1 - \beta} \right]_0^T = \frac{\Gamma_0 Q}{T} \left( \frac{T^{1 - \beta}}{1 - \beta} \right) = \frac{\Gamma_0}{1 - \beta} \cdot Q \cdot T^{-\beta}.\] Rewriting \(T = Q / v\) yields: \[I_{\text{peak}} = \frac{\Gamma_0}{1 - \beta} Q \left(\frac{Q}{v}\right)^{-\beta} = \frac{\Gamma_0}{1 - \beta} v^\beta Q^{1 - \beta}.\] When execution duration \(T\) scales proportionally with order size \(Q\) (constant participation rate \(v\)), peak impact scales as \(Q^{1 - \beta}\). When \(\beta \approx 0.5\), this recovers the square-root scaling \(I_{\text{peak}} \propto Q^{1/2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Transient market impact and relaxation under the propagator model

Simulating trade execution under a power-law propagator kernel \(G(t) = \Gamma_0 (1 + t/\tau)^{-\beta}\) with \(\beta = 0.30\) will confirm that price impact accumulates monotonically during trading, reaches its global maximum at execution completion \(t = T_{\text{exec}}\), and subsequently mean-reverts smoothly towards the permanent impact level.

"""Lab MS3-L02-1: Transient market impact and relaxation under the propagator model.

Simulates price trajectory during and after metaorder execution under a
power-law propagator kernel G(t) = Gamma_0 * (1 + t / tau)^(-beta).
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def propagator_kernel(t: float, gamma_0: float, tau: float, beta: float) -> float:
    """Evaluate power-law response kernel G(t)."""
    if t < 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return float(gamma_0 / ((1.0 + t / tau) ** beta))


def simulate_propagator_impact(
    total_shares: float,
    execution_horizon: float,
    total_time: float,
    n_slices: int,
    gamma_0: float,
    tau: float,
    beta: float,
) -> tuple[list[float], list[float]]:
    """Simulate transient price impact over time using discrete trade slices."""
    dt = execution_horizon / n_slices
    slice_size = total_shares / n_slices

    eval_times: list[float] = []
    impacts: list[float] = []

    # Evaluate impact at regular intervals up to total_time
    n_eval = 20
    eval_dt = total_time / n_eval
    for step in range(n_eval + 1):
        t = step * eval_dt
        eval_times.append(t)

        # Sum of propagated responses from all prior trade slices
        cumulative_impact = 0.0
        for i in range(n_slices):
            trade_time = (i + 0.5) * dt
            if trade_time <= t:
                cumulative_impact += slice_size * propagator_kernel(t - trade_time, gamma_0, tau, beta)

        impacts.append(cumulative_impact)

    return eval_times, impacts


def main() -> None:
    total_shares = 100_000.0  # Metaorder size
    t_exec = 60.0  # Metaorder execution duration (minutes)
    t_total = 180.0  # Total observation window (minutes)
    n_slices = 60  # 1 slice per minute
    gamma_0 = 1.5e-6  # Peak instantaneous impact per share
    tau = 2.0  # Characteristic memory timescale (minutes)
    beta = 0.30  # Power-law decay exponent

    times, impacts = simulate_propagator_impact(total_shares, t_exec, t_total, n_slices, gamma_0, tau, beta)

    print(f"Propagator Model Impact Profile (Q=100k, T_exec=60min, beta={beta}):")
    print("  Time (min) | Phase       | Price Impact ($) | % of Peak Impact")
    print("----------------------------------------------------------------")

    peak_impact = max(impacts)
    for t, imp in zip(times, impacts, strict=True):
        phase = "Execution " if t <= t_exec else "Relaxation"
        pct_peak = (imp / peak_impact) * 100.0
        print(f"  {t:10.1f} | {phase}   | {imp:16.4f} | {pct_peak:15.1f}%")

    # Verify key theoretical properties
    # 1. Peak impact occurs at or immediately after execution completion
    peak_time = times[impacts.index(peak_impact)]
    # 2. Asymptotic decay: impact relaxes towards permanent baseline
    terminal_impact = impacts[-1]
    relaxation_pct = (1.0 - terminal_impact / peak_impact) * 100.0

    print("----------------------------------------------------------------")
    print(f"Peak Impact Achieved at t = {peak_time:.1f} min ($ {peak_impact:.4f})")
    print(f"Post-Trade Relaxation at t = {t_total:.1f} min: {relaxation_pct:.1f}% decayed")
    print("No Price Manipulation: Transient decay ensures round-trip cost is strictly positive.")

3Order Flow Autocorrelation and Martingale Efficiency

One of the most profound puzzles in empirical market microstructure is the apparent contradiction between order flow memory and price efficiency.

Definition 2.3 · Long Memory in Order Flow
Let \(\epsilon_t \in \{-1, +1\}\) denote the sign of the transaction at tick \(t\). The empirical autocorrelation function \(C(\ell) = \Corr(\epsilon_t, \epsilon_{t+\ell})\) exhibits power-law decay: \[C(\ell) \propto \ell^{-\gamma}, \quad \text{with } 0 < \gamma < 1,\] over lags spanning thousands of trades (several hours to days). Because \(\sum_{\ell=1}^\infty C(\ell) = \infty\), order flow possesses long memory and is mathematically non-integrable.

Theorem 2.2 · The Delicate Cancellation Principle
Let order flow autocorrelation decay as \(C(\ell) \propto \ell^{-\gamma}\) and let the impact propagator decay as \(G(\ell) \propto \ell^{-\beta}\). Price changes \(\Delta S_t = S_t - S_{t-1}\) are asymptotically uncorrelated (martingale price dynamics) if and only if: \[\beta = \frac{1 - \gamma}{2}.\] If \(\beta < (1 - \gamma)/2\), impact decays too slowly and prices exhibit momentum (super-diffusion). If \(\beta > (1 - \gamma)/2\), impact decays too rapidly and prices exhibit mean-reversion (sub-diffusion).

Proof Sketch
In discrete spectral representation, the price return at lag \(\ell\) can be expressed in terms of order flow innovations via the discrete Fourier transform. The spectral density of returns \(f_R(\omega)\) satisfies \(f_R(\omega) \approx |\hat{G}(\omega)|^2 f_\epsilon(\omega)\). For a power-law kernel \(G(t) \sim t^{-\beta}\), the Fourier transform scales as \(\hat{G}(\omega) \sim \omega^{\beta - 1}\). For long-memory order flow with \(C(\ell) \sim \ell^{-\gamma}\), the spectral density of trade signs diverges at low frequencies as \(f_\epsilon(\omega) \sim \omega^{\gamma - 1}\). Consequently, the return spectral density behaves near zero frequency as: \[f_R(\omega) \sim (\omega^{\beta - 1})^2 \omega^{\gamma - 1} = \omega^{2\beta - 2 + \gamma - 1} = \omega^{2\beta + \gamma - 1}.\] For price returns to be uncorrelated white noise (diffusion), the spectral density must be constant as \(\omega \to 0\) (\(\omega^0\)). Equating exponents yields: \[2\beta + \gamma - 1 = 0 \implies \beta = \frac{1 - \gamma}{2}. \quad \blacksquare\]

4Resolution of the Price Manipulation Paradox

In Theorem 1.3 of Lesson 1, we proved that instantaneous concave permanent impact enables round-trip price manipulation. We now demonstrate that the propagator model resolves this paradox.

Theorem 2.3 · Absence of Price Manipulation Under Propagators
Let \(G(t)\) be a non-negative, non-increasing, convex response kernel (\(G(t) \ge 0\), \(G'(t) \le 0\), and \(G''(t) \ge 0\) for all \(t > 0\)). Then for any deterministic round-trip trading strategy \(v(t)\) satisfying \(\int_0^T v(t) dt = 0\), the expected execution cost is strictly positive: \[\E[\text{Cost}] = \int_0^T \int_0^t G(t - s) v(s) v(t) \, ds \, dt \ge 0.\] Equality holds if and only if \(v(t) = 0\) almost everywhere.

Proof
By Bochner's theorem, a continuous, non-increasing, convex kernel on \([0, \infty)\) is positive definite. Symmetrizing the quadratic form gives: \[\int_0^T \int_0^t G(t - s) v(s) v(t) \, ds \, dt = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \int_0^T G(|t - s|) v(s) v(t) \, ds \, dt.\] Taking the Fourier transform of the symmetric kernel \(\bar{G}(t) = G(|t|)\), convexity implies that its Fourier transform \(\hat{G}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty G(|t|) e^{-i \omega t} dt\) is strictly positive for all \(\omega \in \R\). By Parseval's identity: \[\frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \int_0^T G(|t - s|) v(s) v(t) \, ds \, dt = \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \hat{G}(\omega) |\hat{v}(\omega)|^2 d\omega > 0,\] for any non-zero trading trajectory \(v(t)\). Therefore, round-trip price manipulation is strictly impossible. \(\blacksquare\)

5Where the Canon Breaks

The linear propagator model provides an elegant mathematical bridge between micro-structure order flow and macroscopic diffusive prices, but its assumptions fail in stressed regimes:

  • Non-Linear Cross-Trade Interference: The linear propagator assumes that the price response to trade \(k\) is independent of trade \(k-1\). In reality, rapid consecutive trades in the same direction deplete the order book non-linearly, causing effective impact to steepen.
  • Breakdown of the Cancellation Condition: The delicate cancellation \(\beta = (1 - \gamma)/2\) is an equilibrium property. During market opens, macroeconomic data releases, or liquidity runs, order flow persistence spikes (\(\gamma \to 0\)) while liquidity replenishment slows (\(\beta \to 0\)), destroying cancellation and generating intense price trending.
  • Endogenous Trading Speed: Real algorithmic traders do not execute at fixed deterministic rates \(v(t)\); they accelerate execution when prices move favorably and pause when prices run away, creating endogenous state-dependent feedback loops that violate the exogenous convolution assumption.

6Misconceptions

  1. Misconception 1: A trade changes the market price permanently, so impact never decays.

    Why it is wrong: Extensive empirical studies of institutional executions establish that between 30% and 50% of the peak price impact observed during a metaorder dissipates within hours after execution ceases, as counterparty limit orders refill the order book.

  2. Misconception 2: If order flow is highly correlated across time, asset returns must also be strongly autocorrelated.

    Why it is wrong: As proved in Theorem 2.2, long-memory order flow (\(C(\ell) \propto \ell^{-\gamma}\)) can coexist with completely uncorrelated return dynamics (\(\Corr(r_t, r_{t+1}) \approx 0\)) because the decay of transient price impact (\(G(t) \propto t^{-\beta}\)) precisely cancels the order flow autocorrelation.

  3. Misconception 3: Any transient impact model allows round-trip arbitrage.

    Why it is wrong: Round-trip price manipulation is prevented whenever the impact response kernel \(G(t)\) is convex and non-increasing. As proved in Theorem 2.3, the quadratic form of execution cost is strictly positive-definite under convex kernels.

7Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 2.1: Under an exponential response kernel \(G(t) = G_0 e^{-\rho t}\) with \(G_0 = \$0.02\) per share and resilience rate \(\rho = 0.5 \text{ min}^{-1}\), compute the residual price impact of a single 1,000-share buy order after (a) \(t = 0\) minutes, (b) \(t = 2\) minutes, and (c) \(t = 10\) minutes.

Solution

The impact of 1,000 shares at time \(t\) is \(I(t) = 1{,}000 \times G(t) = 1{,}000 \times 0.02 \times e^{-0.5 t} = 20.0 \times e^{-0.5 t}\) dollars.
(a) At \(t = 0\): \(I(0) = 20.0 \times e^0 = \$20.00\).
(b) At \(t = 2\): \(I(2) = 20.0 \times e^{-1.0} = 20.0 \times 0.36788 = \$7.36\).
(c) At \(t = 10\): \(I(10) = 20.0 \times e^{-5.0} = 20.0 \times 0.006738 = \$0.13\).

Problem 2.2: A metaorder of \(Q = 60{,}000\) shares is executed at a constant rate over \(T = 30\) minutes under power-law kernel \(G(t) = 10^{-5} t^{-0.4}\) ($/share/min). Compute the peak price impact at \(t = T\).

Solution

Using Theorem 2.1 with \(\Gamma_0 = 10^{-5}\), \(\beta = 0.4\), \(Q = 60{,}000\), and \(T = 30\): \[I_{\text{peak}} = \frac{\Gamma_0}{1 - \beta} \cdot Q \cdot T^{-\beta} = \frac{10^{-5}}{1 - 0.4} \times 60{,}000 \times 30^{-0.4}.\] \[\frac{10^{-5}}{0.6} \times 60{,}000 = \frac{0.6}{0.6} = 1.0.\] \[30^{-0.4} = \frac{1}{30^{0.4}} \approx \frac{1}{3.8986} \approx 0.2565.\] \[I_{\text{peak}} = 1.0 \times 0.2565 = \$0.2565 \text{ per share} \quad (25.65 \text{ cents}).\]

Problem 2.3: If order flow autocorrelation exhibits long memory with exponent \(\gamma = 0.40\), what must the power-law impact decay exponent \(\beta\) be to guarantee that returns are uncorrelated?

Solution

By the delicate cancellation principle (Theorem 2.2): \[\beta = \frac{1 - \gamma}{2} = \frac{1 - 0.40}{2} = \frac{0.60}{2} = 0.30.\] The response kernel must decay as \(G(t) \propto t^{-0.30}\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 2.4: Prove that under an exponential propagator \(G(t) = G_0 e^{-\rho t}\), the price displacement \(D_t = S_t - S_0\) satisfies an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck type stochastic differential equation \(dD_t = -\rho D_t dt + G_0 v(t) dt + \sigma dW_t\).

Solution

By definition of the continuous propagator with exponential kernel: \[D_t = \int_0^t G_0 e^{-\rho(t - s)} v(s) ds + \int_0^t \sigma dW_s.\] Differentiating with respect to \(t\) using Leibniz's rule on the first integral: \[\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_0^t G_0 e^{-\rho(t - s)} v(s) ds\right] = G_0 v(t) - \rho \int_0^t G_0 e^{-\rho(t - s)} v(s) ds = G_0 v(t) - \rho D_t^{\text{impact}}.\] Adding the Brownian increment \(\sigma dW_t\) gives: \[dD_t = -\rho D_t dt + G_0 v(t) dt + \sigma dW_t.\] This establishes that an exponential propagator is mechanically equivalent to an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck mean-reverting drift towards the fundamental price. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 2.5: Prove that if an execution desk executes an order at constant rate \(v\) over \([0, T]\), the ratio of average execution impact to peak impact under power-law kernel \(G(t) \propto t^{-\beta}\) is \(\frac{\bar{I}}{I_{\text{peak}}} = \frac{1}{2 - \beta}\).

Solution

Under uniform trading \(v = Q/T\), the impact at time \(t \in [0, T]\) is: \[I(t) = v \int_0^t u^{-\beta} du = \frac{v t^{1 - \beta}}{1 - \beta}.\] Peak impact at \(t = T\) is \(I_{\text{peak}} = \frac{v T^{1 - \beta}}{1 - \beta}\). The volume-weighted average impact paid across the execution is: \[\bar{I} = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T I(t) dt = \frac{v}{T(1 - \beta)} \int_0^T t^{1 - \beta} dt = \frac{v}{T(1 - \beta)} \left[ \frac{T^{2 - \beta}}{2 - \beta} \right] = \frac{v T^{1 - \beta}}{(1 - \beta)(2 - \beta)}.\] Dividing \(\bar{I}\) by \(I_{\text{peak}}\): \[\frac{\bar{I}}{I_{\text{peak}}} = \frac{\frac{v T^{1 - \beta}}{(1 - \beta)(2 - \beta)}}{\frac{v T^{1 - \beta}}{1 - \beta}} = \frac{1}{2 - \beta}.\] For \(\beta = 0.5\) (square-root scaling), \(\frac{\bar{I}}{I_{\text{peak}}} = \frac{1}{1.5} = \frac{2}{3}\). The average execution price concession is exactly two-thirds of peak impact. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 2.6: Prove that if \(G(t)\) is strictly convex, round-trip trading \(v(t) = \delta(t) - \delta(t - \Delta)\) incurs strictly positive expected cost.

Solution

A strategy of buying 1 share at \(t = 0\) and selling 1 share at \(t = \Delta\) executes trades at prices: \[P_0 = S_0 + G(0), \quad P_\Delta = S_0 + G(\Delta) - G(0).\] Total net cost of buying at \(P_0\) and selling at \(P_\Delta\) is: \[\text{Cost} = P_0 - P_\Delta = [S_0 + G(0)] - [S_0 + G(\Delta) - G(0)] = 2 G(0) - G(\Delta).\] Since \(G(t)\) is non-increasing and positive, \(G(0) \ge G(\Delta) \ge 0\). Therefore: \[\text{Cost} = G(0) + [G(0) - G(\Delta)] \ge G(0) > 0.\] The round trip incurs a strictly positive execution cost of at least \(G(0)\), eliminating round-trip arbitrage. \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 2.7: An empirical study fits the autocorrelation of NASDAQ order signs, estimating \(\gamma = 0.32\). According to the delicate cancellation theorem, what is the theoretical prediction for the impact decay exponent \(\beta\)? If the econometrician estimates \(\hat{\beta} = 0.45\), what sign of short-term return autocorrelation does this imply?

Solution

Under delicate cancellation: \[\beta_{\text{theory}} = \frac{1 - \gamma}{2} = \frac{1 - 0.32}{2} = 0.34.\] If empirical impact decays faster than the theoretical benchmark (\(\hat{\beta} = 0.45 > 0.34\)), transient impact over-compensates for order flow persistence. The spectral density of returns \(f_R(\omega) \sim \omega^{2\beta + \gamma - 1} = \omega^{2(0.45) + 0.32 - 1} = \omega^{0.22}\) vanishes at zero frequency, which implies short-term price mean-reversion (negative return autocorrelation at high frequencies, consistent with bid–ask bounce and liquidity replenishment).

Problem 2.8: An institutional execution desk observes that metaorders in large-cap equities experience a peak impact of 18 bps, which relaxes to 10 bps after 4 hours. Assuming a power-law relaxation \(I(t) = I_{\text{perm}} + (I_{\text{peak}} - I_{\text{perm}}) (1 + t / \tau)^{-\beta}\) with \(\tau = 10\) minutes and \(\beta = 0.40\), solve for the implied permanent impact \(I_{\text{perm}}\).

Solution

At \(t = 240\) minutes (4 hours), the decay factor is: \[\left(1 + \frac{240}{10}\right)^{-0.40} = (1 + 24)^{-0.40} = 25^{-0.40} \approx \frac{1}{3.6239} \approx 0.2760.\] The impact equation is: \[10.0 = I_{\text{perm}} + (18.0 - I_{\text{perm}}) \times 0.2760.\] \[10.0 = I_{\text{perm}} + 4.967 - 0.2760 I_{\text{perm}} = 0.7240 I_{\text{perm}} + 4.967.\] \[0.7240 I_{\text{perm}} = 10.0 - 4.967 = 5.033 \implies I_{\text{perm}} = \frac{5.033}{0.7240} \approx 6.95 \text{ bps}.\] Permanent impact is approximately 6.95 bps (roughly 39% of peak impact).

Problem 2.9: A high-frequency trading firm tests two execution strategies for a 100,000-share buy metaorder over 1 hour: Strategy A executes uniformly (TWAP). Strategy B executes 70,000 shares in the first 15 minutes, and 30,000 shares over the remaining 45 minutes. Under power-law kernel \(G(t) \propto t^{-0.5}\), which strategy incurs lower total execution costs?

Solution

Because the power-law kernel \(G(t)\) is convex and non-increasing, the total execution cost functional \(C[v] = \int_0^T \int_0^t G(t - s) v(s) v(t) ds dt\) is strictly convex in the trading rate trajectory \(v(t)\). By Jensen's inequality and the calculus of variations, the constant rate trajectory (TWAP, Strategy A) uniquely minimizes the impact cost integral. Front-loading volume in Strategy B concentrates executions into a narrow window, creating intense temporary impact spikes that significantly exceed the savings from later relaxation. Strategy A incurs strictly lower execution costs.

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 2.10: Formulate the continuous-time optimal execution problem under a general transient impact kernel \(G(t)\) as a deterministic calculus of variations problem. Derive the Fredholm integral equation of the first kind that governs the optimal trading rate \(v^*(t)\).

Solution

The total expected cost of executing \(Q\) shares over \([0, T]\) is: \[J[v] = \int_0^T v(t) \left[ \int_0^t G(t - s) v(s) ds \right] dt, \quad \text{subject to } \int_0^T v(t) dt = Q.\] Symmetrizing the kernel \(\bar{G}(t, s) = \frac{1}{2}[G(|t - s|)]\), the Lagrangian with multiplier \(\lambda\) is: \[\mathcal{L}[v, \lambda] = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \int_0^T G(|t - s|) v(s) v(t) ds dt - \lambda \left(\int_0^T v(t) dt - Q\right).\] Taking the functional derivative \(\frac{\delta \mathcal{L}}{\delta v(t)} = 0\) yields the Euler–Lagrange condition: \[\int_0^T G(|t - s|) v^*(s) ds = \lambda, \quad \text{for all } t \in [0, T].\] This is a Fredholm integral equation of the first kind. The optimal trading trajectory \(v^*(t)\) must generate a constant effective market potential \(\lambda\) across the entire execution interval.

Problem 2.11: Why does the solution to the Fredholm integral equation for power-law kernels \(G(t) \sim t^{-\beta}\) feature singular U-shaped trading rates (spikes at \(t = 0\) and \(t = T\))?

Solution

To keep the potential \(\int_0^T |t - s|^{-\beta} v(s) ds = \lambda\) constant near the boundaries \(t \to 0\) and \(t \to T\), the trading rate \(v(s)\) must diverge. At the boundaries, the domain of integration is truncated from both sides to one side (e.g., at \(t = 0\), all past history is absent). To compensate for the missing past kernel support, the algorithm must trade at a much higher intensity near \(t = 0\) and \(t = T\), yielding a U-shaped execution profile \(v^*(t) \sim [t(T - t)]^{-(1 - \beta) / 2}\). This mirrors the discrete boundary block trades of Obizhaeva & Wang (2013).

Problem 2.12: Evaluate the econometric challenges of estimating the bare kernel \(G(t)\) from empirical transaction data when order flow is endogenous to price changes.

Solution

Estimating \(G(t)\) via naive regression \(S_t - S_0 = \sum_{s < t} G(t - s) \epsilon_s\) suffers from severe endogeneity: trade signs \(\epsilon_s\) are not exogenous random shocks, but depend dynamically on past price movements (trend-following algorithms, momentum traders, stop-loss orders). Consequently, \(\Cov(\epsilon_s, W_t) \ne 0\). Ordinary least squares yields biased kernel estimates that confound true liquidity relaxation with feedback trading. Econometric identification requires instrumental variables (such as exogenous liquidity shocks, index rebalancing events, or isolated metaorders from institutional audit records) to isolate the true causal propagator.

9Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the central mechanism of a propagator model of market impact?

    Options

    A propagator model represents price as the linear convolution of past trade flow with a decaying response kernel \(G(t)\).

  2. If order flow autocorrelation decays with exponent \(\gamma = 0.50\), what is the decay exponent \(\beta = (1 - \gamma)/2\) required for diffusive returns?

    \(\beta = (1 - 0.50) / 2 = 0.50 / 2 = 0.25\).

  3. Why does a convex, non-increasing response kernel \(G(t)\) rule out round-trip price manipulation?

    Options

    Bochner's theorem guarantees that the Fourier transform of a symmetric convex kernel is positive, making the quadratic cost functional strictly positive.

  4. What happens to the price after a metaorder finishes executing under a propagator model?

    Options

    Once trading ceases, the past kernel contributions decay, resulting in post-trade price relaxation.

  5. For a uniform metaorder execution under power-law kernel with \(\beta = 0.5\), what is the ratio of average execution impact to peak impact: \(1 / (2 - \beta)\)?

    \(1 / (2 - 0.5) = 1 / 1.5 = 2/3 \approx 0.6667\).

  6. What does long memory in order flow mean?

    Options

    Order sign autocorrelation follows \(C(\ell) \propto \ell^{-\gamma}\) with \(\gamma < 1\), which is mathematically non-integrable.

  7. If impact decays faster than the delicate cancellation rate (\(\beta > (1 - \gamma)/2\)), what behavior do asset returns exhibit?

    Options

    When impact decays too fast, the transient price relaxation over-compensates for order flow persistence, creating negative return autocorrelation.

  8. Under an exponential propagator \(G(t) = 10.0 e^{-0.1 t}\), what is the instantaneous impact ($) remaining after \(t = 6.9315\) minutes (since \(\ln(2) \approx 0.69315\))?

    \(e^{-0.1 \times 6.9315} = e^{-0.69315} = 0.50\). Impact is \(10.0 \times 0.50 = 5.0\).
