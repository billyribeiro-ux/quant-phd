The Obizhaeva–Wang Model

In the Almgren–Chriss framework of Lesson 3, temporary impact is purely instantaneous: each trade moves the price for a moment and vanishes, punishing large lumps with quadratic penalties (\(\eta v^2\)). Consequently, Almgren–Chriss forbids discrete block trades. In the pioneering model of Anna Obizhaeva and Jiang Wang (2013), price impact arises mechanically from walking a limit order book of finite depth, while book resilience refills liquidity at an exponential rate \(\rho\). This dynamic supply-and-demand structure produces a striking structural breakthrough: the optimal execution strategy features discrete boundary block trades at the very beginning and very end of the trading horizon, separated by a steady continuous rate.

1Limit Order Book Depth Density and Resilience

In electronic matching engines, incoming market orders eat through the resting limit order queue. While classical static models treat order book depth as fixed, real order books exhibit resilience: market makers and algorithmic liquidity providers post new limit orders to replenish depleted queues.

Definition 5.1 · Depth Density and Resilience
In a limit order book:
1. Depth Density (\(q\)): Let \(q\) denote the uniform density of resting shares per unit price (shares per dollar). Executing an instantaneous market order of size \(x\) shares eats through the book to depth \(\Delta p = x / q\), shifting the instantaneous mid-price by \(\Delta p\).
2. Resilience Rate (\(\rho\)): Let \(D_t = S_t - P_t\) denote the displacement of the prevailing market price \(S_t\) from the unimpacted fundamental price \(P_t\). In the absence of further trading, the displaced order book recovers toward the fundamental equilibrium at exponential rate \(\rho > 0\): \[\frac{d D_t}{dt} = -\rho D_t.\]

This formulation provides a microscopic physical foundation for the exponential propagator kernel \(G(t) = \frac{1}{q} e^{-\rho t}\) examined in Lesson 2.

2Mathematical Formulation of the Obizhaeva–Wang Framework

We formalize the continuous-time stochastic control problem introduced by Obizhaeva & Wang (2013).

Definition 5.2 · The Obizhaeva–Wang Model
In the Obizhaeva–Wang model, an institutional trader liquidates \(X\) shares over a finite horizon \([0, T]\). The cumulative executed shares \(x(t)\) is a non-decreasing right-continuous process with left limits (RCLL), decomposed into continuous trading rate \(v(t) \ge 0\) and discrete lump trades \(\Delta x(t_i)\): \[ \begin{aligned} x(t) &= \int_0^t v(s) \, ds + \sum_{t_i \le t} \Delta x(t_i), \\ &\quad x(0^-) = 0, \quad x(T) = X. \end{aligned} \] The price displacement state variable \(D_t\) follows the stochastic dynamics: \[dD_t = -\rho D_t \, dt + \frac{1}{q} \, dx(t).\] The actual execution price paid for a trade increment \(dx(t)\) is \(P_t + D_t + \frac{1}{2q} dx(t)\), where \(P_t = P_0 + \sigma W_t\) is the unimpacted Brownian fundamental value.

Theorem 5.1 · Expected Execution Cost Functional
The expected total execution cost of liquidating \(X\) shares over \([0, T]\) relative to fundamental value \(P_0\) is: \[ \begin{aligned} J[x] &= \int_0^T \left( D_t + \frac{1}{2q} v(t) \right) v(t) \, dt \\ &\quad + \sum_{t_i} \left( D_{t_i^-} + \frac{1}{2q} \Delta x(t_i) \right) \Delta x(t_i). \end{aligned} \] In terms of the displacement state variable \(D_t\), the cost functional can be rewritten as: \[J[x] = \frac{q}{2} \left[ D_T^2 - D_0^2 + \rho \int_0^T D_t^2 \, dt \right].\]

3The Optimal Execution Schedule: Boundary Blocks and Steady Rate

Minimizing the quadratic cost functional subject to total share conservation yields the celebrated Obizhaeva–Wang trading schedule.

Theorem 5.2 · Obizhaeva–Wang Optimal Execution Policy
For a risk-neutral trader liquidating \(X\) shares over horizon \([0, T]\) in a limit order book with depth density \(q\) and resilience \(\rho > 0\), the optimal trading strategy decomposes uniquely into:
1. An initial discrete block trade at \(t = 0\): \[x_0 = \frac{X}{2 + \rho T}.\]
2. A constant continuous trading rate on the open interval \(t \in (0, T)\): \[v^*(t) = \frac{\rho X}{2 + \rho T}.\]
3. A terminal discrete block trade at \(t = T\): \[x_T = \frac{X}{2 + \rho T}.\]

Proof
Since \(D_0 = 0\) prior to trading, an initial block trade of size \(x_0\) jumps displacement to \(D_{0^+} = x_0 / q\). During \((0, T)\), if the trader executes at continuous rate \(v(t)\), the displacement evolves as \(\dot{D}_t = -\rho D_t + v(t) / q\). To minimize the integral \(\int_0^T D_t^2 dt\), the displacement must remain completely flat: \(\dot{D}_t = 0\). Setting \(\dot{D}_t = 0\) requires: \[-\rho D_t + \frac{v(t)}{q} = 0 \implies v(t) = \rho q D_t = \rho x_0 = v^* \quad \text{(a constant rate!)}\] At the terminal horizon \(t = T\), the remaining unexecuted shares are sold in a terminal lump trade of size \(x_T\). Conservation of total shares requires: \[X = x_0 + \int_0^T v^*(t) \, dt + x_T = x_0 + (\rho x_0) T + x_T.\] By symmetry of the cost functional, the terminal block trade must equal the initial block trade: \(x_T = x_0\). Substituting \(x_T = x_0\) into the conservation equation: \[X = x_0 + \rho T x_0 + x_0 = x_0 (2 + \rho T).\] Solving for \(x_0\): \[x_0 = \frac{X}{2 + \rho T}.\] Consequently, \(x_T = \frac{X}{2 + \rho T}\), and the steady rate is \(v^* = \frac{\rho X}{2 + \rho T}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Obizhaeva–Wang limit order book resilience simulator

Simulating the Obizhaeva–Wang optimal schedule across varied resilience rates \(\rho\) will demonstrate that: (1) total volume strictly satisfies share conservation \(x_0 + v^* T + x_T = X\); (2) as \(\rho \to 0\), the strategy converges to two equal 50,000-share boundary blocks; (3) as \(\rho \to \infty\), boundary blocks vanish and execution recovers continuous uniform TWAP.

"""Lab MS3-L05-1: Obizhaeva-Wang optimal execution with limit order book resilience.

Simulates optimal execution under transient impact with exponential resilience rho,
demonstrating discrete boundary block trades and steady continuous rate.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def obizhaeva_wang_schedule(
    total_shares: float,
    horizon: float,
    resilience: float,
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Compute Obizhaeva-Wang (2013) optimal execution strategy:

    x_0 = X / (2 + rho * T)
    v^* = rho * X / (2 + rho * T)
    x_t = X / (2 + rho * T)
    """
    denom = 2.0 + resilience * horizon
    x_0 = total_shares / denom
    v_star = (resilience * total_shares) / denom
    x_t = total_shares / denom
    return x_0, v_star, x_t


def main() -> None:
    total_shares = 100_000.0  # 100,000 shares
    horizon = 1.0  # 1 trading day

    # Resilience parameter rho (replenishment rate per day)
    resilience_values = [0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0, 50.0]

    print("Obizhaeva-Wang Optimal Execution Strategy (X=100k, T=1.0d):")
    print("  Resilience rho | Initial Block x_0 | Steady Rate v^* (sh/d) | Terminal Block x_t | Sum Traded")
    print("------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    for rho in resilience_values:
        x_0, v_star, x_t = obizhaeva_wang_schedule(total_shares, horizon, rho)
        continuous_shares = v_star * horizon
        total_traded = x_0 + continuous_shares + x_t
        print(f"  {rho:14.1f} | {x_0:17,.1f} | {v_star:22,.1f} | {x_t:18,.1f} | {total_traded:10,.1f}")

    print("------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    print("Theoretical limits:")
    print("  1. Zero resilience (rho -> 0): Order splits into two equal lump trades of 50k at t=0 and t=T.")
    print("  2. Infinite resilience (rho -> inf): Boundary trades vanish, recovering continuous TWAP.")
    print("Exact conservation: x_0 + v^* * T + x_t = 100,000 shares across all resilience values.")

4Structural Comparison: Obizhaeva–Wang vs. Almgren–Chriss

The mathematical architectures of Obizhaeva–Wang and Almgren–Chriss represent two fundamentally distinct philosophies of market liquidity.

DimensionAlmgren–Chriss (2001)Obizhaeva–Wang (2013)
Impact Mechanism Instantaneous friction (\(\eta v\)) + permanent drift (\(\gamma v\)) Transient limit order book walking with exponential resilience (\(\rho\))
Block Trades Strictly prohibited (incurs infinite \(\eta v^2\) penalty) Optimally present at boundaries (\(t=0\) and \(t=T\))
Trading Pacing Hyperbolic smooth curve (continuous acceleration) Flat steady rate \(v^*\) bounded by two discrete jumps
Risk Objective Mean-variance utility \(E[x] + \lambda V[x]\) Risk-neutral expected cost (or mean-variance extension)
Liquidity Parameter Temporary parameter \(\eta\) (dollars per share/sec) Book depth \(q\) (shares/$) and resilience \(\rho\) (time\(^{-1}\))

5Where the Canon Breaks

While the Obizhaeva–Wang model provides an intuitive mechanism for discrete block trading, several assumptions break down in production environments:

  • Exponential vs Power-Law Resilience: Obizhaeva–Wang assumes exponential decay (\(e^{-\rho t}\)). As established in Lesson 2, empirical market relaxation is heavy-tailed and follows a power law (\(t^{-\beta}\)). Under power-law kernels, boundary trades remain optimal, but the interior continuous rate is U-shaped rather than strictly flat.
  • Information Leakage of Initial Blocks: Executing a large discrete block trade \(x_0\) at \(t = 0\) alerts predatory algorithms to an institutional liquidation in progress. In modern electronic markets, submitting an uncamouflaged block order invites front-running and destroys book resilience.
  • State-Dependent Depth: Depth density \(q\) is treated as an exogenous constant. In real market crashes, depth collapses when volatility rises, invalidating constant-density walking.

6Misconceptions

  1. Misconception 1: Executing discrete block trades in continuous execution is always sub-optimal.

    Why it is wrong: In Almgren–Chriss, block trades are ruled out because temporary impact is modeled as instantaneous quadratic friction. In Obizhaeva–Wang, where liquidity replenishment takes finite time, discrete block trades at \(t=0\) and \(t=T\) are the mathematically proven unique optimal policy.

  2. Misconception 2: In Obizhaeva–Wang, the continuous trading rate accelerates over time.

    Why it is wrong: On the open interval \((0, T)\), the optimal continuous trading rate is strictly constant: \(v^*(t) = \frac{\rho X}{2 + \rho T}\). This keeps order book displacement \(D_t\) perfectly flat, minimizing the quadratic integral of price distortion.

  3. Misconception 3: If resilience is zero (\(\rho = 0\)), optimal execution is impossible.

    Why it is wrong: When \(\rho = 0\) (zero replenishment, purely permanent impact), the formula yields \(x_0 = X/2, v^* = 0, x_T = X/2\). The optimal strategy is to execute exactly half the position at \(t = 0\) and the remaining half at \(t = T\).

7Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 5.1: A trader liquidates \(X = 600{,}000\) shares over \(T = 2.0\) days in a market with resilience \(\rho = 1.0 \text{ day}^{-1}\). Compute the initial block trade \(x_0\), the steady continuous trading rate \(v^*\), and the terminal block trade \(x_T\).

Solution

The denominator is \(2 + \rho T = 2 + (1.0 \times 2.0) = 2 + 2 = 4\). Initial block trade: \[x_0 = \frac{X}{2 + \rho T} = \frac{600{,}000}{4} = 150{,}000 \text{ shares}.\] Continuous trading rate: \[v^* = \frac{\rho X}{2 + \rho T} = \frac{1.0 \times 600{,}000}{4} = 150{,}000 \text{ shares per day}.\] Terminal block trade: \[x_T = \frac{X}{2 + \rho T} = 150{,}000 \text{ shares}.\] Verification: \(x_0 + v^* T + x_T = 150{,}000 + 300{,}000 + 150{,}000 = 600{,}000\) shares.

Problem 5.2: If limit order book depth density is \(q = 50{,}000\) shares per dollar, what is the instantaneous price displacement \(\Delta p\) immediately after executing the initial block trade of 150,000 shares in Problem 5.1?

Solution

Executing \(x_0 = 150{,}000\) shares against depth density \(q = 50{,}000\) shares/$: \[\Delta p = \frac{x_0}{q} = \frac{150{,}000}{50{,}000} = \$3.00 \text{ per share}.\] The market mid-price is depressed by $3.00 at \(t = 0^+\).

Problem 5.3: In Problem 5.1, what fraction of the total 600,000-share order is executed continuously vs. in discrete block trades?

Solution

Discrete block volume: \(x_0 + x_T = 150{,}000 + 150{,}000 = 300{,}000\) shares (50.0%). Continuous volume: \(v^* T = 150{,}000 \times 2.0 = 300{,}000\) shares (50.0%). Exactly half the order is executed in boundary blocks, and half is executed in the continuous stream.

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 5.4: Prove that in the limit as resilience \(\rho \to \infty\), the Obizhaeva–Wang strategy converges to continuous uniform TWAP with zero boundary block trades.

Solution

Evaluate the initial block trade as \(\rho \to \infty\): \[\lim_{\rho \to \infty} x_0 = \lim_{\rho \to \infty} \frac{X}{2 + \rho T} = 0.\] Similarly, \(\lim_{\rho \to \infty} x_T = 0\). Evaluate the continuous trading rate: \[\lim_{\rho \to \infty} v^* = \lim_{\rho \to \infty} \frac{\rho X}{2 + \rho T} = \lim_{\rho \to \infty} \frac{X}{\frac{2}{\rho} + T} = \frac{X}{T}.\] The boundary block trades vanish, and the continuous rate converges to the constant rate \(v(t) = X / T\), which is continuous uniform TWAP. Instantaneous order book resilience eliminates the need for boundary blocks. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 5.5: Prove that the total expected execution cost in the Obizhaeva–Wang model is \(J^* = \frac{X^2}{2 q (2 + \rho T)}\).

Solution

Using Theorem 5.1, the cost is \(J = \frac{q}{2}[D_T^2 - D_0^2 + \rho \int_0^T D_t^2 dt]\). With \(D_0 = 0\), initial displacement is \(D_{0^+} = x_0 / q\). During \((0, T)\), \(D_t = D_{0^+} = x_0 / q\) is constant. Therefore, \(\int_0^T D_t^2 dt = T (x_0 / q)^2 = T x_0^2 / q^2\). At terminal horizon \(T\), before the final trade, displacement is \(D_{T^-} = x_0 / q\). Executing \(x_T = x_0\) incurs terminal lump cost \((D_{T^-} + \frac{x_T}{2q}) x_T = (\frac{x_0}{q} + \frac{x_0}{2q}) x_0 = \frac{3 x_0^2}{2q}\). Summing all components: Initial trade cost: \(\frac{1}{2q} x_0^2\). Continuous trading cost: \(\int_0^T D_t v^* dt = \int_0^T \left(\frac{x_0}{q}\right) (\rho x_0) dt = \frac{\rho T x_0^2}{q}\). Terminal trade cost: \(\frac{3 x_0^2}{2q}\). Total cost: \[ \begin{aligned} J^* &= \frac{x_0^2}{2q} + \frac{\rho T x_0^2}{q} + \frac{x_0^2}{2q} + \frac{x_0^2}{q} \\ &= \frac{x_0^2}{2q} (2 + \rho T). \end{aligned} \] Substitute \(x_0 = \frac{X}{2 + \rho T}\): \[J^* = \frac{1}{2q (2 + \rho T)^2} X^2 (2 + \rho T) = \frac{X^2}{2 q (2 + \rho T)}. \quad \blacksquare\]

Problem 5.6: Prove that if an investor delays the terminal block trade by executing it at \(T + \Delta\), total execution cost strictly increases.

Solution

Suppose the investor completes continuous trading at \(T\), waits until \(T + \Delta\), and then liquidates \(x_T\). During \([T, T + \Delta]\), displacement decays as \(D(T + \Delta) = D_T e^{-\rho \Delta} = \frac{x_0}{q} e^{-\rho \Delta}\). However, the investor incurs an opportunity cost from unexecuted inventory. Under risk aversion or terminal deadline constraints, the cost of delaying increases. Furthermore, in the original Obizhaeva–Wang framework, the time horizon is strictly bounded by \(T\); any trade outside \([0, T]\) violates the horizon constraint. \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 5.7: An econometrician estimates order book parameters on an S&P 500 stock: depth density is \(q = 80{,}000\) shares/$, and resilience half-life is \(t_{1/2} = 15\) minutes in a 390-minute trading day (\(t_{1/2} = 15 / 390 = 0.03846\) days). Compute the daily resilience rate \(\rho\) and the optimal initial block size for a 200,000-share 1-day liquidation.

Solution

The resilience rate satisfies \(\rho = \frac{\ln(2)}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{0.69315}{0.03846} \approx 18.02 \text{ day}^{-1}\). With \(T = 1.0\) day and \(X = 200{,}000\) shares: \[2 + \rho T = 2 + (18.02 \times 1.0) = 20.02.\] The initial block trade is: \[x_0 = \frac{X}{2 + \rho T} = \frac{200{,}000}{20.02} \approx 9{,}990 \text{ shares}.\] The steady continuous trading rate is: \[v^* = \rho x_0 = 18.02 \times 9{,}990 \approx 180{,}020 \text{ shares per day}.\] Boundary trades account for roughly 5% each, while 90% is traded continuously.

Problem 5.8: Compare the expected execution cost of liquidating 100,000 shares under Obizhaeva–Wang when resilience is fast (\(\rho = 20\)) vs slow (\(\rho = 2\)) on a stock with depth density \(q = 25{,}000\) shares/$ over \(T = 1\) day.

Solution

Using the cost formula from Problem 5.5: \(J^* = \frac{X^2}{2 q (2 + \rho T)}\). Numerator: \(X^2 = (100{,}000)^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{10}\). Denominator prefactor: \(2 q = 2 \times 25{,}000 = 50{,}000\). For slow resilience (\(\rho = 2\)): \[J^*_{\text{slow}} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{10}}{50{,}000 \times 4} = \$50{,}000.00.\] For fast resilience (\(\rho = 20\)): \[J^*_{\text{fast}} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{10}}{50{,}000 \times 22} \approx \$9{,}090.91.\] Fast resilience reduces execution cost by over 81% ($40,909 savings).

Problem 5.9: An execution algorithm in production splits orders into \(x_0, v^*, x_T\). In a live deployment, submitting the initial block of 25,000 shares triggered adverse price momentum, increasing effective trading costs by 35 bps. Why did the theoretical Obizhaeva–Wang strategy underperform in practice?

Solution

The Obizhaeva–Wang model assumes that price displacement is purely non-informational and that order book depth density \(q\) and resilience \(\rho\) are invariant to trading actions. In real electronic markets, submitting a large discrete block order of 25,000 shares signals adverse institutional order flow to high-frequency market makers. Counterparties widen spreads, cancel resting limit orders, and front-run the remaining continuous stream. This endogeneity destroys book resilience, causing temporary impact to transform into permanent adverse selection.

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 5.10: Formulate the Obizhaeva–Wang model with risk aversion \(\lambda > 0\) under Brownian price volatility \(\sigma\). How does inventory risk alter the relative size of the initial block \(x_0\) vs the terminal block \(x_T\)?

Solution

When the trader penalizes inventory variance \(\lambda \Var(x) = \lambda \sigma^2 \int_0^T x(t)^2 dt\), the symmetry between the initial and terminal block trades breaks. Holding inventory is costly. Therefore, the trader increases the initial block trade (\(x_0 > x_T\)) to immediately dump a large fraction of the position, reducing variance exposure throughout the entire horizon. The continuous rate \(v^*(t)\) is no longer constant, but decays monotonically over \((0, T)\), and the terminal block trade shrinks (\(x_T < x_0\)). In the extreme limit \(\lambda \to \infty\), \(x_0 = X\), and both \(v^*\) and \(x_T\) vanish.

Problem 5.11: Extend the Obizhaeva–Wang framework to discrete trading intervals with execution latency \(\Delta t\). How does matching engine latency alter the discrete boundary block sizes?

Solution

Under discrete matching with interval \(\Delta t\), an initial order cannot execute at an idealized infinitesimal point \(t = 0\); it must clear the queue over interval \([0, \Delta t]\). During this interval, queue depletion occurs while resilience refilling has not yet commenced. Alfonsi, Schied & Schulz (2010) show that discrete-time limit order book resilience models preserve the discrete block structure, but the optimal initial trade is attenuated by a factor dependent on \(\rho \Delta t\), preventing the trader from over-shooting the queue capacity within a single latency window.

Problem 5.12: Formulate an optimal routing problem across a lit exchange with Obizhaeva–Wang resilience and a dark pool with midpoint matching. When should the algorithm route the initial block to the dark pool?

Solution

Let the lit exchange have depth density \(q\) and resilience \(\rho\), while the dark pool executes orders at the midpoint with zero price impact but fill probability \(P_{\text{dark}} < 1\). If the algorithm routes the initial block \(x_0\) to the lit book, execution is guaranteed but incurs impact cost \(\frac{x_0^2}{2q}\). If routed to the dark pool, it saves the impact cost with probability \(P_{\text{dark}}\), but with probability \(1 - P_{\text{dark}}\) it fails to execute, delaying liquidation and increasing risk. The optimal routing policy routes the initial block to the dark pool whenever: \[P_{\text{dark}} \frac{x_0^2}{2q} > (1 - P_{\text{dark}}) \lambda \sigma^2 x_0^2 \Delta t,\] which holds when position size \(x_0\) is large and dark pool fill rates are high.

9Retrieval Quiz

  1. What distinguishes the Obizhaeva–Wang optimal execution strategy from the Almgren–Chriss strategy?

    Options

    Because limit order book depth density is finite and resilience refills the book over time, discrete lump trades at \(t=0\) and \(t=T\) are mathematically optimal.

  2. If a trader liquidates \(X = 100{,}000\) shares over \(T = 1.0\) day with resilience \(\rho = 2.0 \text{ day}^{-1}\), what is the size of the initial block trade \(x_0 = X / (2 + \rho T)\)?

    \(2 + \rho T = 2 + (2.0 \times 1.0) = 4\). \(x_0 = 100{,}000 / 4 = 25{,}000\) shares.

  3. Why is the continuous trading rate \(v^*(t)\) constant across the open interval \((0, T)\) in the risk-neutral Obizhaeva–Wang model?

    Options

    Setting \(v^* = \rho q D_t\) holds \(\dot{D}_t = -\rho D_t + v^*/q = 0\), keeping price distortion constant and minimizing the quadratic cost integral.

  4. What does the limit order book parameter \(\rho\) measure?

    Options

    Resilience \(\rho\) is the rate of exponential mean-reversion of displaced order book depth back toward the unimpacted price.

  5. In Problem 5.2, what is the size of the continuous volume executed across \((0, T)\) if \(X = 100{,}000\), \(\rho = 2.0\), and \(T = 1.0\)?

    \(v^* = \rho X / (2 + \rho T) = 2.0 \times 100{,}000 / 4 = 50{,}000\) shares per day. Continuous volume is \(v^* \times 1.0 = 50{,}000\) shares.

  6. What does the Obizhaeva–Wang strategy converge to in the limit of infinite resilience (\(\rho \to \infty\))?

    Options

    As \(\rho \to \infty\), \(x_0 \to 0, x_T \to 0\), and \(v^* \to X/T\), recovering continuous uniform TWAP.

  7. What does the Obizhaeva–Wang strategy converge to if resilience is zero (\(\rho = 0\))?

    Options

    When \(\rho = 0\), \(x_0 = X / 2\), \(x_T = X / 2\), and \(v^* = 0\). The order is split equally between the start and finish.

  8. In Problem 5.2, what is the size of the terminal block trade \(x_T\)?

    By symmetry, \(x_T = x_0 = 25{,}000\) shares.
