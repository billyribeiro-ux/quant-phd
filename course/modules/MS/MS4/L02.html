Anomaly Returns Net of Cost

The asset pricing literature documents hundreds of empirical return anomalies: market capitalization, book-to-market, price momentum, gross profitability, asset growth, and idiosyncratic volatility. Academic discovery typically evaluates anomalies under paper portfolio protocols, ignoring trading frictions. Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) and Patton & Weller (2020) demonstrated that transaction costs decimate the majority of the published factor zoo. High-turnover strategies like momentum and short-term reversals surrender over half their gross returns to effective half-spreads and borrow costs, while low-turnover fundamentals like profitability and value survive nearly intact. Estimating anomaly returns net of costs separates economically meaningful market premia from micro-cap illiquidity illusions, establishing which factors can support institutional capital.

1The Anomaly Zoo and Turnover Friction

In empirical asset pricing, market anomalies are systematic return differentials identified by sorting equities on historical accounting ratios or price patterns. While canonical factors from Fama & French (1993) and Carhart (1997) show large gross Sharpe ratios on paper, real capital must bear the continuous cost of rebalancing.

Definition 2.1 · Anomaly Returns Net of Trading Costs
Let \(R_{i,t}\) denote the gross return of constituent asset \(i\) at time \(t\), and let \(w_{i,t}\) denote its portfolio weight. The gross return of the long–short or long-only anomaly strategy is \(R_t^{\text{gross}} = \sum_{i=1}^N w_{i,t} R_{i,t}\). Let \(\tau_t \equiv \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N |w_{i,t} - w_{i,t-1}(1 + R_{i,t})/(1 + R_t^{\text{gross}})|\) denote the one-way rebalancing turnover fraction, and let \(c_{i,t}\) denote the effective one-way transaction cost (effective half-spread plus commissions and market impact) incurred per dollar traded in asset \(i\). The anomaly returns net of trading costs \(R_t^{\text{net}}\) is the realized return after subtracting total rebalancing friction: \[R_t^{\text{net}} \equiv R_t^{\text{gross}} - \sum_{i=1}^N c_{i,t} |\Delta w_{i,t}| - s_t,\] where \(s_t\) denotes short-leg equity borrowing and lending fees.

Turnover velocity separates factor strategies into three distinct operational regimes:

  • Low-Turnover Anomalies (\(\tau < 20\%\) p.a.): Rebalanced annually based on slow-moving corporate balance sheet metrics. Primary examples include the value premium (HML) (Fama & French 1993) and the gross profitability premium (Novy-Marx 2013).
  • Medium-Turnover Anomalies (\(20\% \le \tau \le 100\%\) p.a.): Rebalanced semi-annually or quarterly, such as investment rate (CMA), asset growth, and net operating assets.
  • High-Turnover Anomalies (\(\tau > 100\%\) p.a.): Rebalanced monthly or weekly based on high-frequency market prices. Primary examples include cross-sectional price momentum (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993), idiosyncratic volatility, and short-term reversals.

2The Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) Taxonomy

In a comprehensive study of 23 canonical asset pricing anomalies across 1963 to 2013, Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) calibrated effective transaction costs using high-frequency effective spreads, Hasbrouck's Gibbs sampler, and closing bid–ask spreads.

Theorem 2.1 · Anomaly Decay under Turnover Drag
Let an anomaly strategy produce constant monthly gross excess return \(\mu_{\text{gross}}\) with annualized volatility \(\sigma_{\text{gross}}\). If monthly rebalancing generates constant turnover \(\tau_{\text{mo}}\) and incurs average effective round-trip cost \(2\bar{c}\), the net Sharpe ratio \(\SR_{\text{net}}\) satisfies: \[\SR_{\text{net}} = \SR_{\text{gross}} - \frac{24 \tau_{\text{mo}} \bar{c}}{\sigma_{\text{ann}}}.\] The fraction of Sharpe ratio retained net of trading costs is: \[\frac{\SR_{\text{net}}}{\SR_{\text{gross}}} = 1 - \frac{2 \tau_{\text{mo}} \bar{c}}{\mu_{\text{gross}}}.\]

Proof: The monthly net return is \(\mu_{\text{net}} = \mu_{\text{gross}} - 2 \tau_{\text{mo}} \bar{c}\). Annualizing the mean return gives \(\mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{net}} = 12 \mu_{\text{net}} = 12 \mu_{\text{gross}} - 24 \tau_{\text{mo}} \bar{c}\). Assuming transaction costs do not materially alter return variance at monthly horizons (\(\sigma_{\text{ann}}^{\text{net}} \approx \sigma_{\text{ann}}\)): \[\SR_{\text{net}} = \frac{\mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{net}}}{\sigma_{\text{ann}}} = \frac{12 \mu_{\text{gross}} - 24 \tau_{\text{mo}} \bar{c}}{\sigma_{\text{ann}}} = \SR_{\text{gross}} - \frac{24 \tau_{\text{mo}} \bar{c}}{\sigma_{\text{ann}}}.\] Dividing by \(\SR_{\text{gross}} = 12 \mu_{\text{gross}} / \sigma_{\text{ann}}\) yields the retention fraction \(1 - \frac{2 \tau_{\text{mo}} \bar{c}}{\mu_{\text{gross}}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.1 · Empirical Findings from Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016)
Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) document that:
1. Low-Turnover Survival: Anomalies in the low-turnover category (e.g., Gross Profitability, Book-to-Market, Net Stock Issues) incur average trading costs of only 8 to 15 basis points per month. Gross profitability generates 38 bps/month gross and retains 37.5 bps/month net (\(t = 3.65\)), preserving over 95% of its economic magnitude.
2. High-Turnover Decimation: In sharp contrast, high-turnover anomalies incur average trading costs of 48 to 85 basis points per month. Momentum (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993) suffers average trading costs of 48 bps/month, reducing its gross alpha of 68 bps/month to a statistically marginal net spread. Idiosyncratic volatility anomalies turn sharply negative net of costs.

Lab 1 · Anomaly returns net of trading costs across turnover tiers

Computing monthly and annualized net returns across low, medium, and high turnover anomaly categories demonstrates that high turnover destroys over 50% of gross momentum alpha, while low-turnover profitability retains over 95% of its gross return.

"""Lab MS4-L02-1: Anomaly returns net of trading costs across turnover tiers.

Computes monthly and annualized net returns, Sharpe erosion, and break-even trading cost
thresholds for canonical market anomalies (Novy-Marx & Velikov 2016; Patton & Weller 2020).
SIMULATED: empirical parameters calibrated to NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ cross-sectional anomalies.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def run_anomaly_cost_analysis() -> None:
    # Anomaly profiles: (name, monthly_turnover_frac, gross_alpha_bps, effective_half_spread_bps, vol_ann)
    # Annual turnover = monthly_turnover * 12
    anomalies = [
        ("Gross Profitability (Low Turnover)", 0.015, 38.0, 18.0, 0.12),
        ("Asset Growth (Medium Turnover)", 0.045, 42.0, 20.0, 0.14),
        ("Price Momentum (High Turnover)", 0.180, 68.0, 24.0, 0.18),
    ]

    print("Anomaly Strategy Cost and Net Alpha Comparison:")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    for name, turnover, gross_bps, spread_bps, vol in anomalies:
        ann_turnover = turnover * 12.0
        # Monthly transaction cost in bps = 2 * turnover * spread_bps (round trip)
        cost_monthly_bps = 2.0 * turnover * spread_bps
        net_monthly_bps = gross_bps - cost_monthly_bps

        # Annualized gross and net returns
        gross_ann_pct = (gross_bps * 12.0) / 100.0
        cost_ann_pct = (cost_monthly_bps * 12.0) / 100.0
        net_ann_pct = (net_monthly_bps * 12.0) / 100.0

        # Sharpe ratios
        gross_sharpe = gross_ann_pct / (vol * 100.0)
        net_sharpe = net_ann_pct / (vol * 100.0)
        sharpe_retention = (net_sharpe / gross_sharpe) * 100.0 if gross_sharpe > 0 else 0.0

        # Break-even effective half-spread threshold: cost_monthly_bps = gross_bps
        # 2 * turnover * c_star = gross_bps => c_star = gross_bps / (2 * turnover)
        breakeven_spread_bps = gross_bps / (2.0 * turnover)

        print(f"Strategy: {name}")
        print(f"  Monthly Turnover:       {turnover * 100.0:.2f}% (Annual: {ann_turnover * 100.0:.2f}%)")
        print(f"  Gross Alpha:            {gross_bps:.2f} bps/mo ({gross_ann_pct:.2f}% ann)")
        print(f"  Trading Cost Drag:      {cost_monthly_bps:.2f} bps/mo ({cost_ann_pct:.2f}% ann)")
        print(f"  Net Alpha:              {net_monthly_bps:.2f} bps/mo ({net_ann_pct:.2f}% ann)")
        print(f"  Gross Sharpe Ratio:     {gross_sharpe:.4f}")
        print(f"  Net Sharpe Ratio:       {net_sharpe:.4f} ({sharpe_retention:.2f}% retention)")
        print(f"  Break-even Half-Spread: {breakeven_spread_bps:.2f} bps")
        print(f"  Viable Net of Cost:     {net_monthly_bps > 0.0}")
        print("-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------")


def main() -> None:
    run_anomaly_cost_analysis()


main()

3Patton & Weller (2020) Structural Framework: Break-Even Thresholds

Building on the taxonomy of trading frictions, Patton & Weller (2020) developed an econometric and structural methodology that accounts for asymmetric execution frictions across the long and short legs of anomaly portfolios, short-selling borrow fees, and break-even trading cost thresholds.

Definition 2.2 · Break-Even Half-Spread Threshold
The break-even transaction cost \(c^*\) is the effective one-way half-spread (in basis points) at which the expected return net of trading costs equals zero: \[c^* \equiv \frac{\E[R^{\text{gross}}]}{2 \cdot \E[\text{Turnover}]}.\] If an institutional investor's actual effective execution friction \(\bar{c}\) exceeds \(c^*\), the strategy produces negative economic return net of trading costs.

Theorem 2.2 · Break-Even Threshold Inverse Scaling
For an anomaly strategy rebalanced with frequency \(f\) (rebalances per year) and turnover per rebalance \(\tau_0\), annualized turnover is \(\text{Turnover}_{\text{ann}} = f \cdot \tau_0\). The break-even transaction cost threshold scales inversely with rebalancing turnover: \[c^* = \frac{\mu_{\text{ann}}}{2 f \tau_0}.\] Strategies with high rebalancing frequency have tight break-even tolerance, failing when effective spreads exceed small fractional basis point levels.

Proof: Expected annualized trading cost is \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{ann}} = 2 c \cdot \text{Turnover}_{\text{ann}} = 2 c f \tau_0\). Setting net annualized return to zero: \[\mu_{\text{ann}} - \mathcal{C}_{\text{ann}} = \mu_{\text{ann}} - 2 c^* f \tau_0 = 0 \implies c^* = \frac{\mu_{\text{ann}}}{2 f \tau_0}. \quad \blacksquare\]

Remark 2.2 · Patton & Weller (2020) Empirical Findings
Patton & Weller (2020) show that:
1. Asymmetric Short-Leg Friction: Short legs of anomalies contribute the majority of nominal gross returns in academic papers, but incur disproportionate trading costs. Borrowing small-cap, high-volatility short candidates commands loan fees exceeding 200–500 bps annually (special borrow rates), wiping out apparent short-side premia.
2. Institutional Unviability of the Factor Zoo: For over 60% of published cross-sectional anomalies, the break-even half-spread \(c^*\) is under 25 basis points, well below the actual 40–120 basis point effective institutional trading friction for their small-cap and micro-cap constituents.

4Where the Canon Breaks

The academic anomaly literature frequently misleads quantitative practitioners through three structural methodological defects:

  • Equal-Weighting Micro-Cap Distortions: Academic factor studies routinely report equal-weighted (EW) portfolio returns. Micro-cap stocks constitute over 60% of listed US equities by count, but represent under 3% of aggregate market capitalization. An equal-weighted anomaly allocates 60% of its capital to micro-caps where effective half-spreads exceed 200 basis points and borrow availability is near zero. Value-weighted (VW) implementations typically cut gross return estimates in half.
  • Static Friction Fallacy: Academic papers apply a single time-series average spread estimate. In reality, trading costs are time-varying, countercyclical, and state-dependent: spreads widen and liquidity vanishes during market panics precisely when factor strategies experience rebalancing surges.
  • Factor Zoo Multiple Testing Crisis: P-hacking and publication bias incentivize authors to cherry-pick anomaly definitions. Without preregistered protocols, backtests discover statistical noise that vanishes out-of-sample and cannot survive trading frictions.

5Misconceptions

  • A statistically significant t-statistic (\(t > 3.0\)) on a gross anomaly factor proves investable economic profit.

    A factor with high turnover (e.g., short-term reversal or high-beta momentum) can achieve \(t > 4.0\) gross while delivering negative returns net of trading costs. Statistical significance tests whether gross mean return differs from zero, not whether it exceeds the trading cost drag (Novy-Marx & Velikov 2016).

  • Value-weighting completely eliminates the need to model transaction costs.

    While value-weighting significantly reduces the proportion of illiquid micro-caps relative to equal-weighting, high-turnover anomalies like momentum still trade large volumes in mid-cap and small-cap stocks, incurring substantial market impact and execution drag (Patton & Weller 2020).

  • Short-selling equity borrowing fees can be neglected because most stocks trade at general collateral.

    While large-cap stocks trade at general collateral (fees < 25 bps p.a.), the short legs of cross-sectional anomalies are heavily concentrated in distressed, highly shorted, or high-volatility names that trade on "special", requiring borrowing fees of 300 to 1,500 basis points annually (Patton & Weller 2020).

6Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 2.1: A monthly rebalanced momentum factor generates an average gross excess return of 60 basis points per month with 20% monthly one-way turnover. If the average effective one-way half-spread is 25 basis points, calculate: (a) monthly trading cost drag in bps, (b) net monthly return in bps, and (c) annualized net return.

Solution

(a) Monthly trading cost drag: \(\text{Cost}_{\text{mo}} = 2 \times \text{Turnover} \times \text{Half-Spread} = 2 \times 0.20 \times 25.0 \text{ bps} = 10.0\) basis points per month.
(b) Net monthly return: \(R^{\text{net}} = R^{\text{gross}} - \text{Cost}_{\text{mo}} = 60.0 - 10.0 = 50.0\) basis points per month.
(c) Annualized net return: \(12 \times 50.0 \text{ bps} = 600.0 \text{ bps} = 6.0\%\) per annum.

Problem 2.2: An idiosyncratic volatility anomaly strategy delivers 40 basis points of gross return per month with 30% monthly turnover. What is the break-even one-way effective half-spread \(c^*\) (in basis points) above which net return becomes negative?

Solution

By Definition 2.2, the break-even half-spread satisfies: \[c^* = \frac{R^{\text{gross}}}{2 \times \text{Turnover}} = \frac{40.0 \text{ bps}}{2 \times 0.30} = \frac{40.0}{0.60} \approx 66.67 \text{ basis points}.\] If the actual effective half-spread on small-cap volatile stocks exceeds 66.67 bps, the strategy loses money net of trading costs.

Problem 2.3: Compare the annual trading drag of: (Strategy A) Annual-turnover value factor with 15% annual turnover and 20 bps half-spread; (Strategy B) Monthly-rebalanced momentum factor with 180% annual turnover and 20 bps half-spread.

Solution

Annual round-trip trading cost: \(\text{Cost}_{\text{ann}} = 2 \times \text{Turnover}_{\text{ann}} \times \text{Half-Spread}\).
For Strategy A (Value): \[\text{Cost}_A = 2 \times 0.15 \times 20.0 \text{ bps} = 6.0 \text{ basis points per year}.\]
For Strategy B (Momentum): \[\text{Cost}_B = 2 \times 1.80 \times 20.0 \text{ bps} = 72.0 \text{ basis points per year}.\] Momentum incurs 12 times greater annual execution drag than value under identical market liquidity conditions.

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 2.4: Prove that if an anomaly's gross Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_{\text{gross}}\), the net Sharpe ratio is positive if and only if the effective half-spread \(\bar{c} < c^*\).

Solution

By Theorem 2.1, the net annualized mean excess return is \(\mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{net}} = \mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{gross}} - 2 \bar{c} \text{Turnover}_{\text{ann}}\). The net Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_{\text{net}} = \mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{net}} / \sigma_{\text{ann}}\). Since volatility \(\sigma_{\text{ann}} > 0\), \(\SR_{\text{net}} > 0 \iff \mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{net}} > 0\). Substituting \(\mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{net}}\): \[\mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{gross}} - 2 \bar{c} \text{Turnover}_{\text{ann}} > 0 \iff \bar{c} < \frac{\mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{gross}}}{2 \text{Turnover}_{\text{ann}}}.\] By Definition 2.2, \(c^* \equiv \frac{\mu_{\text{ann}}^{\text{gross}}}{2 \text{Turnover}_{\text{ann}}}\). Therefore, \(\SR_{\text{net}} > 0 \iff \bar{c} < c^*\). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 2.5: Prove that for a long–short zero-investment factor portfolio \(w_L = 1, w_S = -1\), the one-way turnover fraction \(\tau\) is bounded below by the change in target active weights: \(\tau \ge \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N |w_{i,t} - w_{i,t-1}|\).

Solution

Actual rebalancing turnover accounts for price changes between rebalance dates: \[\tau_t = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N |w_{i,t} - w_{i,t-1}^{\text{drifted}}| = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N \left| (w_{i,t} - w_{i,t-1}) - (w_{i,t-1}^{\text{drifted}} - w_{i,t-1}) \right|.\] By the reverse triangle inequality \(|a - b| \ge ||a| - |b||\). When return drift is zero (\(R_{i,t} = R_t\)), \(w_{i,t-1}^{\text{drifted}} = w_{i,t-1}\), achieving exact equality: \(\tau_t = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N |w_{i,t} - w_{i,t-1}|\). Hence active rebalancing weight changes establish the baseline turnover floor. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 2.6: Prove that if effective half-spreads scale with market capitalization as \(c(M) = c_0 M^{-\gamma}\) with \(\gamma > 0\), the aggregate transaction cost of an equal-weighted portfolio strictly exceeds that of a value-weighted portfolio for any universe with heterogeneous market caps.

Solution

Let market caps be \(M_i > 0\) with total cap \(M_{\text{tot}} = \sum_i M_i\). Value weights are \(w_i^{\text{VW}} = M_i / M_{\text{tot}}\), and equal weights are \(w_i^{\text{EW}} = 1 / N\). The average transaction cost under equal weighting is: \[\bar{c}_{\text{EW}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N c_0 M_i^{-\gamma}.\] The average transaction cost under value weighting is: \[\bar{c}_{\text{VW}} = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{M_i}{M_{\text{tot}}} c_0 M_i^{-\gamma} = \frac{c_0}{M_{\text{tot}}} \sum_{i=1}^N M_i^{1-\gamma}.\] Define the random variable \(M\) with uniform probability \(1/N\) over \(\{M_i\}\). Then \(\bar{c}_{\text{EW}} = c_0 \E[M^{-\gamma}]\), and \(\bar{c}_{\text{VW}} = c_0 \frac{\E[M^{1-\gamma}]}{\E[M]}\). Consider the covariance between \(M\) and \(M^{-\gamma}\): since \(M^{-\gamma}\) is strictly decreasing in \(M\) for \(\gamma > 0\), Chebyshev's algebraic inequality establishes: \[\Cov(M, M^{-\gamma}) = \E[M^{1-\gamma}] - \E[M]\E[M^{-\gamma}] < 0.\] Therefore, \(\frac{\E[M^{1-\gamma}]}{\E[M]} < \E[M^{-\gamma}] \implies \bar{c}_{\text{VW}} < \bar{c}_{\text{EW}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 2.7: In Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016), gross profitability (Novy-Marx 2013) and price momentum (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993) exhibit gross monthly returns of 38 bps and 68 bps, respectively. Explain why an institutional allocator with a 50 bps execution friction tolerance would reject momentum while accepting profitability.

Solution

The institutional allocator evaluates the break-even half-spread \(c^* = \frac{R^{\text{gross}}}{2 \times \text{Turnover}}\):
1. Gross Profitability: Turnover is ~1.5% per month (18% p.a.). Break-even half-spread is \(c^* = \frac{38 \text{ bps}}{2 \times 0.015} \approx 1{,}266\) basis points. A 50 bps execution friction consumes only \(2 \times 0.015 \times 50 = 1.5\) bps/month, leaving 36.5 bps/month net alpha.
2. Price Momentum: Turnover is ~18% per month (216% p.a.). Break-even half-spread is \(c^* = \frac{68 \text{ bps}}{2 \times 0.18} \approx 189\) basis points. A 50 bps execution friction consumes \(2 \times 0.18 \times 50 = 18.0\) bps/month. In small/mid caps where momentum trades, actual frictions often reach 60–80 bps, wiping out nearly half the spread. Profitability is vastly more resilient to execution friction.

Problem 2.8: How do short-selling loan fees and special borrow constraints identified by Patton & Weller (2020) affect the empirical implementation of low-beta / betting-against-beta (BAB) factor strategies (Frazzini & Pedersen 2014)?

Solution

The BAB factor longs low-beta stocks leveraged to beta 1 and shorts high-beta stocks deleveraged to beta 1:
1. Short-Leg Concentration in Volatile Assets: High-beta stocks are disproportionately small-cap, volatile, or distressed growth stocks that are in high demand by short sellers and have limited lendable institutional inventory.
2. Special Borrow Fees: As shown by Patton & Weller (2020), borrowing these high-beta names costs 200 to 800 bps annually in borrow fees, directly reducing the return of the short leg.
3. Recall and Squeeze Risk: When high-beta stocks experience sudden squeezes, lenders recall shares, forcing the fund to close short positions at unfavorable prices, incurring high implementation shortfall not visible in academic backtests.

Problem 2.9: A quantitative fund screens 100 equity anomalies and finds that 45 have gross t-statistics above 2.50. After subtracting Novy-Marx & Velikov transaction costs, only 14 have net t-statistics above 2.0. What does this attrition reveal about the "factor zoo"?

Solution

This dramatic attrition reveals two foundational truths of empirical asset pricing:
1. Micro-Cap Liquidity Mirage: Over two-thirds of published anomalies rely on illiquid micro-cap return differentials that cannot be monetized because the bid–ask spread exceeds the gross return premium.
2. P-Hacking and False Discoveries: Researchers searching thousands of signal variations find spurious correlation with small-cap noise. When held to the standard of net-of-cost investability, the true factor zoo collapses from hundreds of alleged anomalies to a small core of durable, low-turnover economic drivers (Value, Profitability, Size, and slow-moving Quality).

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 2.10: Formulate an optimization problem for an asset manager who wants to construct an anomaly portfolio that maximizes net expected return subject to a maximum tracking error to the pure academic factor.

Solution

Let \(w_{\text{acad}}\) denote the benchmark academic factor weights, and let \(w\) denote the investable portfolio weights. Let \(\mu\) denote expected returns, \(\Sigma\) the covariance matrix, and \(c\) the vector of effective half-spreads. The objective is to maximize net return subject to a tracking error constraint: \[\begin{aligned} \max_w \quad & w^\top \mu - \sum_{i=1}^N c_i |w_i - w_{i,0}| \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & (w - w_{\text{acad}})^\top \Sigma (w - w_{\text{acad}}) \le \text{TE}_{\max}^2, \\ & \mathbf{1}^\top w = 1, \quad w \ge 0. \end{aligned}\] This penalizes high-turnover, high-cost constituents, tilting weights toward liquid large-caps with minimal loss of factor exposure.

Problem 2.11: How does the Corwin & Schultz (2012) high-low spread estimator improve transaction cost estimation over the Roll (1984) autocovariance spread estimator for cross-sectional anomaly constituents?

Solution

Corwin & Schultz (2012) exploits daily high and low prices rather than closing price autocovariance:
1. Positive Definiteness: The Roll estimator requires lag-1 autocovariance \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) < 0\). For over 30% of small stocks, sample autocovariance is positive due to time-varying drift or sparse trading, causing the Roll estimator to fail (\(\sqrt{-\text{cov}}\) is undefined).
2. Volatility Separation: The ratio of 2-day high-low price range to 1-day high-low price ranges reflects continuous Brownian motion volatility plus a discrete bid–ask spread jump at trade boundaries, providing reliable spread estimates across all stocks without failing in trending markets.

Problem 2.12: Propose an empirical test to distinguish whether post-publication decay in an anomaly's gross alpha is driven by institutional arbitrageurs trading away the mispricing versus increased transaction costs.

Solution

To distinguish between mispricing arbitrage and trading cost expansion:
1. Formulate two testable hypotheses: - Arbitrage Hypothesis: If institutional capital exploits the published anomaly, institutional ownership and short interest in constituent stocks should increase, and gross return should decline while market depth increases. - Cost / Noise Hypothesis: If the anomaly was sample-specific noise, gross return decays without any surge in institutional trading volume or short interest.
2. Regress post-publication gross alpha on institutional ownership growth (\(\Delta \text{Inst}\)) and 13F holdings changes.
3. Track effective spreads: if gross return decays while effective spreads remain constant or narrow, alpha decay reflects genuine market price discovery rather than execution friction.

    Options

    The break-even cost \(c^*\) defines the maximum trading friction an anomaly can endure before its net return turns negative.

  3. An anomaly generates 45 bps per month gross return with 30% monthly turnover. What is the break-even one-way effective half-spread \(c^*\) in basis points?

    \(c^* = \frac{45.0}{2 \times 0.30} = \frac{45.0}{0.60} = 75.0\) basis points.

  4. Why do equal-weighted anomaly portfolios reported in academic journals give an overly optimistic impression of real-world trading profits?

    Options

    Micro-caps represent over 60% of stock count but less than 3% of market value; equal-weighted anomalies trade heavily in illiquid names that cannot support institutional scale.

  5. A factor strategy has 100% monthly turnover and trades with an effective half-spread of 6 basis points. What is the monthly transaction cost drag in basis points?

    \(\text{Cost} = 2 \times \text{Turnover} \times \text{Half-Spread} = 2 \times 1.00 \times 6.0 = 12.0\) basis points.

  6. If a strategy has gross Sharpe ratio of 1.00 and annual turnover of 200% (\(\tau = 2.0\)), with annual volatility of 20% and effective half-spread of 100 bps (0.0100), what percentage of the gross Sharpe ratio is retained net of costs?

    Annual trading cost \(= 2 \times 2.0 \times 0.0100 = 0.0400 = 4.0\%\). Gross return \(= 1.00 \times 20\% = 20.0\%\). Net return \(= 16.0\%\). Retention \(= 16.0 / 20.0 = 80.0\%\).

  7. What structural friction in the short leg of factor anomalies did Patton & Weller (2020) identify as wiping out academic paper profits?

    Options

    Short legs are heavily weighted toward distressed and volatile small caps that command high special borrow fees, destroying apparent short alpha.

  8. How does turnover velocity affect the vulnerability of a factor anomaly to execution friction?

    Options

    Total transaction drag scales directly with turnover: \(\text{Drag} = 2 \bar{c} \times \text{Turnover}\), making turnover velocity the primary driver of net performance decay.
