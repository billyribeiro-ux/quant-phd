Strategy Capacity Modeling & Alpha Degradation

Every successful quantitative strategy contains the seeds of its own destruction: as assets under management (AUM) expand, portfolio rebalancing trades collide with the limits of market liquidity. Under the empirical square-root law of market impact, dollar transaction costs scale as \(A^{3/2}\), causing net alpha to decay strictly with \(\sqrt{A}\). Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018) analyzed over $1 trillion of live institutional trades, discovering that while real-world capacity far exceeds naive academic estimates, alpha degradation remains an unyielding physical constraint. Modeling strategy capacity allows asset managers to identify break-even scale, maximize total firm dollar profits at \(\frac{4}{9}\) of break-even AUM, and prevent over-allocation from destroying client returns.

1The Physics of Portfolio Market Impact and Cost Scaling

In Lesson 2, we evaluated anomaly returns net of trading costs under fixed fractional spread assumptions. When an institutional fund manages substantial assets under management \(A\), trading volume represents a non-trivial fraction of market liquidity. Trading costs cease to be constant basis-point fees and instead expand dynamically per the square-root law of market impact (Almgren & Chriss 2001; Bouchaud et al. 2018).

Definition 3.1 · Strategy Capacity
Strategy capacity is the scale of assets under management (AUM) that an investment strategy can deploy before execution frictions erode risk-adjusted returns to critical institutional thresholds:
1. Break-Even Capacity \(A^*\): The maximum AUM at which expected net excess return (net alpha) equals zero: \(\alpha^{\text{net}}(A^*) = 0\).
2. Profit-Maximizing Capacity \(A_{\text{opt}}\): The AUM scale that maximizes total dollar value added (firm dollar alpha): \(A_{\text{opt}} \equiv \argmax_A [A \cdot \alpha^{\text{net}}(A)]\).

Theorem 3.1 · Three-Halves Power Law of Portfolio Impact Costs
Consider a portfolio of \(N\) assets rebalancing from initial weights \(w_{i,0}\) to target weights \(w_i^*\) at total fund AUM \(A\). Let \(\text{ADV}_i\) denote daily dollar trading volume, \(\sigma_i\) daily return volatility, and \(Y\) the dimensionless impact prefactor. Under the square-root market impact law, the total rebalancing transaction cost \(\mathcal{C}(A)\) in dollars scales as \(A^{3/2}\): \[\mathcal{C}(A) = \kappa A^{3/2},\] where the aggregate portfolio friction coefficient \(\kappa\) is: \[\kappa \equiv Y \sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i \frac{|w_i^* - w_{i,0}|^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_i}}.\] Expressed as a fraction of fund AUM, unit transaction cost scales as the square root of assets: \[\frac{\mathcal{C}(A)}{A} = \kappa \sqrt{A}.\]

Proof: At fund AUM \(A\), the dollar trade required in asset \(i\) is \(D_i = A |w_i^* - w_{i,0}|\). Under the square-root impact law, executing dollar volume \(D_i\) against daily dollar volume \(\text{ADV}_i\) incurs execution price concession: \[I_i = Y \sigma_i \sqrt{\frac{D_i}{\text{ADV}_i}} = Y \sigma_i \sqrt{\frac{A |w_i^* - w_{i,0}|}{\text{ADV}_i}} = Y \sqrt{A} \sigma_i \frac{\sqrt{|w_i^* - w_{i,0}|}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_i}}.\] The dollar transaction cost incurred in asset \(i\) is the product of trade size \(D_i\) and percentage impact \(I_i\): \[\mathcal{C}_i(A) = D_i \cdot I_i = \left( A |w_i^* - w_{i,0}| \right) \left( Y \sqrt{A} \sigma_i \frac{\sqrt{|w_i^* - w_{i,0}|}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_i}} \right) = Y A^{3/2} \sigma_i \frac{|w_i^* - w_{i,0}|^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_i}}.\] Summing across all \(N\) portfolio assets: \[\mathcal{C}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathcal{C}_i(A) = Y A^{3/2} \sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i \frac{|w_i^* - w_{i,0}|^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_i}} = \kappa A^{3/2}.\] Dividing by fund AUM \(A\) yields the unit drag \(\mathcal{C}(A)/A = \kappa A^{1/2} = \kappa \sqrt{A}\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Break-Even Capacity and Profit-Maximizing AUM

Because unit transaction costs grow continuously with \(\sqrt{A}\), gross strategy alpha degrades as the fund scales. This creates an exact analytical trade-off between percentage alpha and total dollar profit.

Theorem 3.2 · Closed-Form Capacity Extrema
Let a strategy possess gross annualized alpha \(\alpha_0 > 0\) and portfolio friction coefficient \(\kappa > 0\), so that net alpha is: \[\alpha^{\text{net}}(A) = \alpha_0 - \kappa \sqrt{A}.\] Then:
1. Break-Even Capacity: Net alpha drops to zero at: \[A^* = \left(\frac{\alpha_0}{\kappa}\right)^2.\]
2. Profit-Maximizing Capacity: Total dollar alpha \(\mathcal{P}(A) \equiv A \cdot \alpha^{\text{net}}(A)\) is maximized at exactly \(\frac{4}{9}\) of break-even capacity: \[A_{\text{opt}} = \left(\frac{2\alpha_0}{3\kappa}\right)^2 = \frac{4}{9} A^* \approx 0.4444 A^*.\]
3. Net Alpha at Optimum: At the profit-maximizing AUM \(A_{\text{opt}}\), net alpha is exactly one-third of gross alpha: \[\alpha^{\text{net}}(A_{\text{opt}}) = \frac{1}{3} \alpha_0.\]
4. Maximum Dollar Alpha: Peak annual dollar value added is: \[\mathcal{P}(A_{\text{opt}}) = \frac{4}{27} \frac{\alpha_0^3}{\kappa^2}.\]

Proof:
1. Setting \(\alpha^{\text{net}}(A^*) = \alpha_0 - \kappa \sqrt{A^*} = 0\) immediately gives \(\sqrt{A^*} = \alpha_0 / \kappa \implies A^* = (\alpha_0 / \kappa)^2\).
2. The total dollar alpha function is: \[\mathcal{P}(A) = A (\alpha_0 - \kappa A^{1/2}) = \alpha_0 A - \kappa A^{3/2}.\] Differentiating with respect to \(A\): \[\frac{d\mathcal{P}}{dA} = \alpha_0 - \frac{3}{2} \kappa A^{1/2}.\] Setting the first derivative to zero: \[\alpha_0 - \frac{3}{2} \kappa A^{1/2} = 0 \implies A^{1/2} = \frac{2\alpha_0}{3\kappa} \implies A_{\text{opt}} = \left(\frac{2\alpha_0}{3\kappa}\right)^2 = \frac{4}{9}\left(\frac{\alpha_0}{\kappa}\right)^2 = \frac{4}{9} A^*.\] The second derivative is \(\frac{d^2\mathcal{P}}{dA^2} = -\frac{3}{4} \kappa A^{-1/2} < 0\) for all \(A > 0\), confirming that \(A_{\text{opt}}\) is the unique global maximum.
3. Substituting \(A_{\text{opt}}^{1/2} = \frac{2\alpha_0}{3\kappa}\) into the net alpha function: \[\alpha^{\text{net}}(A_{\text{opt}}) = \alpha_0 - \kappa \left(\frac{2\alpha_0}{3\kappa}\right) = \alpha_0 - \frac{2}{3}\alpha_0 = \frac{1}{3}\alpha_0.\]
4. Evaluating \(\mathcal{P}(A_{\text{opt}})\): \[\mathcal{P}(A_{\text{opt}}) = A_{\text{opt}} \cdot \alpha^{\text{net}}(A_{\text{opt}}) = \left( \frac{4}{9} \frac{\alpha_0^2}{\kappa^2} \right) \left( \frac{1}{3}\alpha_0 \right) = \frac{4}{27} \frac{\alpha_0^3}{\kappa^2}. \quad \blacksquare\]

Example 3.1 · Factor Strategy Capacity Numerical Calibration
A quantitative equity market-neutral strategy operates with gross annualized alpha \(\alpha_0 = 8.0\%\) (800 bps) and calibrated portfolio friction \(\kappa = 4.0 \times 10^{-7}\).
1. Break-even capacity: \[A^* = \left(\frac{0.08}{4.0 \times 10^{-7}}\right)^2 = (200{,}000)^2 = \$40{,}000{,}000{,}000 \quad (\$40\text{B}).\]
2. Profit-maximizing capacity: \[A_{\text{opt}} = \frac{4}{9} \times \$40\text{B} \approx \$17{,}777{,}777{,}778 \quad (\$17.78\text{B}).\]
3. Performance at optimal scale: Net alpha is \(\frac{1}{3} \times 8.0\% = 2.67\%\) (266.7 bps). Total firm annual dollar alpha generated is: \[\mathcal{P}(A_{\text{opt}}) = \$17.78\text{B} \times 0.02667 \approx \$474{,}074{,}074 \quad (\$474.1\text{M}).\] If the fund accepts additional capital up to $30B, net alpha falls to \(0.08 - 4.0 \times 10^{-7} \sqrt{3 \times 10^{10}} = 0.08 - 0.06928 = 1.07\%\), and dollar alpha drops to \(\$30\text{B} \times 0.01072 = \$321.6\text{M}\), destroying over $152M of firm profit.

Lab 1 · Strategy capacity modeling and alpha degradation under square-root impact

Deriving the portfolio friction coefficient kappa on a 10-asset universe proves that profit-maximizing AUM occurs strictly at 4/9 of break-even AUM, where net alpha equals exactly one-third of gross alpha.

"""Lab MS4-L03-1: Strategy capacity modeling and alpha degradation under square-root impact.

Derives the portfolio market impact friction coefficient kappa, solves for break-even AUM
and profit-maximizing AUM, and traces net alpha decay (Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz 2018).
SIMULATED: deterministic calibrated equity universe and rebalancing targets.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def run_strategy_capacity_modeling() -> None:
    # Portfolio universe: 10 representative assets
    # (weight_initial, weight_target, daily_adv_dollars, daily_vol)
    universe = [
        (0.12, 0.02, 45000000.0, 0.018),
        (0.08, 0.16, 28000000.0, 0.022),
        (0.15, 0.05, 60000000.0, 0.016),
        (0.05, 0.15, 22000000.0, 0.025),
        (0.10, 0.10, 35000000.0, 0.019),
        (0.06, 0.14, 18000000.0, 0.028),
        (0.14, 0.04, 50000000.0, 0.017),
        (0.09, 0.11, 30000000.0, 0.021),
        (0.11, 0.09, 40000000.0, 0.020),
        (0.10, 0.14, 25000000.0, 0.024),
    ]

    prefactor_y = 0.50
    gross_alpha = 0.08  # 8.0% annualized gross alpha

    # Compute portfolio friction coefficient kappa
    # kappa = Y * sum( sigma_i * |Delta w_i|^{1.5} / sqrt(ADV_i) )
    kappa = prefactor_y * sum(
        vol * (abs(w_tgt - w_init) ** 1.5) / math.sqrt(adv) for w_init, w_tgt, adv, vol in universe
    )

    # Break-even capacity A* (where net alpha hits 0)
    # alpha_0 - kappa * sqrt(A*) = 0 => A* = (alpha_0 / kappa)^2
    a_star = (gross_alpha / kappa) ** 2

    # Profit-maximizing capacity A_opt = (4/9) * A*
    a_opt = (4.0 / 9.0) * a_star

    # Maximum dollar alpha at A_opt
    max_dollar_alpha = (4.0 / 27.0) * (gross_alpha**3) / (kappa**2)
    net_alpha_at_opt = gross_alpha / 3.0

    print(f"Gross Annualized Alpha:      {gross_alpha * 100.0:.2f}% ({gross_alpha * 10000.0:.1f} bps)")
    print(f"Friction Coefficient Kappa:  {kappa:.8e}")
    print(f"Break-even Capacity A*:      {a_star:.2f}")
    print(f"Profit-maximizing AUM A_opt: {a_opt:.2f}")
    print(f"Capacity Ratio A_opt / A*:   {a_opt / a_star:.4f}")
    print(f"Net Alpha at A_opt:          {net_alpha_at_opt * 100.0:.2f}% ({net_alpha_at_opt * 10000.0:.1f} bps)")
    print(f"Max Annual Dollar Alpha:     {max_dollar_alpha:.2f}")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    print("AUM Level            | Unit Cost bps | Net Alpha (%) | Dollar Alpha ($)")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    grid_fractions = [0.10, 4.0 / 9.0, 0.75, 1.00, 1.25]
    for frac in grid_fractions:
        aum = frac * a_star
        cost_dollars = kappa * (aum**1.5)
        cost_fraction = cost_dollars / aum
        cost_bps = cost_fraction * 10000.0
        net_alpha = gross_alpha - cost_fraction
        dollar_alpha = aum * net_alpha
        print(f"{aum:20.2f} | {cost_bps:13.2f} | {net_alpha * 100.0:13.2f} | {dollar_alpha:16.2f}")


def main() -> None:
    run_strategy_capacity_modeling()

3The Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018) Evidence

In the most comprehensive empirical examination of institutional trading execution to date, Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018) analyzed a proprietary dataset comprising over $1.7 trillion of live equity trades executed across 21 developed markets between 1998 and 2013 by AQR Capital Management.

Remark 3.1 · Institutional Trading Frictions vs Academic Models
Frazzini et al. (2018) report that actual institutional execution costs are substantially lower than standard academic model estimates (such as Hasbrouck's effective spreads or Perold's early figures):
1. Execution Patience: Institutional quants do not execute entire metaorders via immediate market orders across lit exchanges. Instead, they schedule parent orders over multiple hours or days, trade patiently using algorithmic execution (TWAP, VWAP, and Almgren–Chriss implementations), and utilize non-displayed crossing networks (dark pools).
2. Liquidity Provision: By providing liquidity at the passive side of the spread on a substantial fraction of child orders, institutional traders earn spread rebates rather than paying full crossing costs, cutting effective transaction costs to 1/2 or 1/3 of standard academic estimates.

Remark 3.2 · Realized Strategy Capacities for Canonical Factors
Using their calibrated live cost functions, Frazzini et al. (2018) estimate strategy capacity for the classic equity factors:
1. Value (HML) and Size (SMB): Because value and size rebalance slowly, their strategy capacities reach tens to hundreds of billions of dollars ($50B to $150B+) before net alpha is eliminated.
2. Momentum (WML): Because momentum turns over rapidly, its capacity is an order of magnitude smaller ($5B to $15B). However, when managed via turnover mitigation (Lesson 4), momentum capacity expands substantially beyond naive academic estimates.

4Where the Canon Breaks

Capacity models built upon the square-root law assume independent execution in an exogenous market environment. The canon breaks when institutional reality intervenes:

  • Endogenous Crowding and Correlated Impact: The square-root law models a single firm trading against an unperturbed market. In reality, multiple multi-billion-dollar quantitative funds trade identical factor signals simultaneously. When dozens of funds attempt to liquidate momentum losers and buy momentum winners at the same month-end window, aggregate market impact escalates dramatically, collapsing strategy capacity.
  • Regime Shifts and Liquidity Disappearance: Capacity models assume constant average daily volume (ADV) and stable volatility. During market panics, equity volatility surges and liquidity providers withdraw from the book, causing friction \(\kappa\) to spike by a factor of 3 to 5. Strategies operating safely at \(A_{\text{opt}}\) in normal regimes abruptly find themselves operating beyond break-even capacity \(A^*\).
  • Factor Crowding Unwinds: As observed during the August 2007 "Quant Meltdown", crowded factor strategies face liquidity black holes where fire-sale liquidations trigger self-reinforcing downward price spirals across uncorrelated portfolios.

5Misconceptions

  • Doubling fund AUM doubles trading cost drag as a percentage of assets.

    Under the empirical square-root law of market impact, dollar transaction costs scale as \(A^{3/2}\), meaning unit costs as a fraction of fund assets scale with \(\sqrt{A}\). Doubling AUM multiplies unit drag by \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\), not 2.0. Linear impact models incorrectly predict that unit costs scale with \(A\), severely overestimating cost drag for large funds.

  • A commercial asset manager should maximize client AUM up to the break-even capacity \(A^*\).

    Expanding AUM to \(A^*\) drives net alpha to zero, leaving clients with index-like returns and exposing the manager to fee compression. Profit-maximizing capacity occurs at \(A_{\text{opt}} = \frac{4}{9} A^*\), where net alpha remains at one-third of its gross level and total firm dollar profits are maximized.

  • Factor capacity is a fixed, immutable mathematical constant for each factor.

    Capacity is dynamic: it expands with market trading volume, contracts with volatility, and depends heavily on portfolio construction. Employing multi-day execution scheduling, buy–hold buffer bands, and internal cross-sleeve netting can expand a strategy's capacity by 3x to 5x (Frazzini et al. 2018).

6Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 3.1: A factor portfolio has gross annualized alpha \(\alpha_0 = 10.0\%\) (0.10) and portfolio friction coefficient \(\kappa = 5.0 \times 10^{-7}\). Calculate: (a) break-even capacity \(A^*\), (b) profit-maximizing capacity \(A_{\text{opt}}\), and (c) net alpha at \(A_{\text{opt}}\) in percent.

Solution

(a) Break-even capacity: \[A^* = \left(\frac{\alpha_0}{\kappa}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{0.10}{5.0 \times 10^{-7}}\right)^2 = (200{,}000)^2 = \$40{,}000{,}000{,}000 \quad (\$40\text{B}).\] (b) Profit-maximizing capacity: \[A_{\text{opt}} = \frac{4}{9} A^* = \frac{4}{9} \times \$40\text{B} \approx \$17{,}777{,}777{,}778 \quad (\$17.78\text{B}).\] (c) Net alpha at \(A_{\text{opt}}\): \[\alpha^{\text{net}}(A_{\text{opt}}) = \frac{1}{3} \alpha_0 = \frac{1}{3} \times 10.0\% \approx 3.33\%.\]

Problem 3.2: If a fund operates at AUM \(A = \$10\text{B}\) and incurs unit transaction costs of 200 bps, what will unit transaction costs be if AUM expands to \(A = \$40\text{B}\) under (a) the square-root impact model, and (b) a naive linear impact model?

Solution

The AUM expands by a factor of \(\$40\text{B} / \$10\text{B} = 4.0\).
(a) Under the square-root impact model, unit costs scale with \(\sqrt{A}\). Multiplying AUM by 4 multiplies unit cost by \(\sqrt{4} = 2.0\). New unit cost \(= 200 \text{ bps} \times 2.0 = 400\) basis points.
(b) Under a naive linear impact model, unit costs scale with \(A\). Multiplying AUM by 4 multiplies unit cost by 4.0. New unit cost \(= 200 \text{ bps} \times 4.0 = 800\) basis points. Linear impact drastically overestimates execution drag at scale.

Problem 3.3: In a 2-asset universe with equal turnover, Asset 1 has daily volume \(\text{ADV}_1 = \$100\text{M}\) and Asset 2 has \(\text{ADV}_2 = \$25\text{M}\), both with daily volatility \(\sigma = 0.02\). If trade sizes are identical, by what factor does the impact cost incurred in Asset 2 exceed that of Asset 1?

Solution

Under square-root impact, cost per trade scales with \(\sigma \sqrt{D / \text{ADV}}\). With identical \(D\) and \(\sigma\), cost scales with \(1 / \sqrt{\text{ADV}}\). The cost ratio is: \[\frac{\mathcal{C}_2}{\mathcal{C}_1} = \frac{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_1}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{\$100\text{M}}{\$25\text{M}}} = \sqrt{4.0} = 2.0.\] Asset 2 incurs exactly twice the dollar impact cost of Asset 1.

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 3.4: Prove that if market impact follows a general power law \(I(D) = Y \sigma (D / \text{ADV})^\beta\) with exponent \(\beta > 0\), the ratio of profit-maximizing capacity to break-even capacity is \(A_{\text{opt}} / A^* = (1 + \beta)^{-1/\beta}\).

Solution

Total dollar cost scales as \(\mathcal{C}(A) = \kappa A^{1+\beta}\), and unit cost scales as \(\kappa A^\beta\). Net alpha is \(\alpha^{\text{net}}(A) = \alpha_0 - \kappa A^\beta\). Break-even capacity satisfies \(\alpha_0 - \kappa (A^*)^\beta = 0 \implies (A^*)^\beta = \alpha_0 / \kappa \implies A^* = (\alpha_0 / \kappa)^{1/\beta}\). Dollar profit is \(\mathcal{P}(A) = A \alpha^{\text{net}}(A) = \alpha_0 A - \kappa A^{1+\beta}\). Differentiating with respect to \(A\): \[\frac{d\mathcal{P}}{dA} = \alpha_0 - (1 + \beta) \kappa A^\beta = 0 \implies A_{\text{opt}}^\beta = \frac{\alpha_0}{(1 + \beta)\kappa} = \frac{1}{1 + \beta} (A^*)^\beta.\] Taking the \((1/\beta)\)-th power of both sides: \[A_{\text{opt}} = (1 + \beta)^{-1/\beta} A^* \implies \frac{A_{\text{opt}}}{A^*} = (1 + \beta)^{-1/\beta}.\] For square-root impact (\(\beta = 1/2\)): \[\frac{A_{\text{opt}}}{A^*} = \left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1/(1/2)} = \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{-2} = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2 = \frac{4}{9}. \quad \blacksquare\]

Problem 3.5: Prove that at profit-maximizing capacity under general power-law impact exponent \(\beta\), net alpha is a constant fraction \(\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\) of gross alpha.

Solution

From Problem 3.4, at \(A_{\text{opt}}\), \(\kappa A_{\text{opt}}^\beta = \frac{\alpha_0}{1 + \beta}\). Net alpha is: \[\alpha^{\text{net}}(A_{\text{opt}}) = \alpha_0 - \kappa A_{\text{opt}}^\beta = \alpha_0 - \frac{\alpha_0}{1 + \beta} = \alpha_0 \left(1 - \frac{1}{1 + \beta}\right) = \alpha_0 \left(\frac{\beta}{1 + \beta}\right).\] For square-root impact (\(\beta = 1/2\)): \[\frac{\beta}{1 + \beta} = \frac{1/2}{1 + 1/2} = \frac{1/2}{3/2} = \frac{1}{3}. \quad \blacksquare\]

Problem 3.6: Prove that if an asset manager can reduce rebalancing turnover by a factor of \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) (i.e., \(\tau_{\text{new}} = \lambda \tau\)) without altering gross alpha or constituent liquidity, strategy capacity expands by a factor of \(\lambda^{-3}\).

Solution

The friction coefficient is \(\kappa = Y \sum \sigma_i \frac{|\Delta w_i|^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_i}}\). Scaling all active rebalancing weight changes by \(\lambda\): \[\kappa_{\text{new}} = Y \sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_i \frac{|\lambda \Delta w_i|^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_i}} = \lambda^{3/2} \kappa.\] Break-even capacity is \(A^* = (\alpha_0 / \kappa)^2\). Under the new friction coefficient: \[A_{\text{new}}^* = \left(\frac{\alpha_0}{\kappa_{\text{new}}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\alpha_0}{\lambda^{3/2}\kappa}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^3}\left(\frac{\alpha_0}{\kappa}\right)^2 = \lambda^{-3} A^*.\] For example, cutting turnover in half (\(\lambda = 0.5\)) expands capacity by \((0.5)^{-3} = 2^3 = 8.0\) times. \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 3.7: In Frazzini et al. (2018), estimated capacity for value (HML) is approximately $52B, whereas capacity for momentum (WML) is approximately $5B. Using the \(\lambda^{-3}\) scaling theorem, explain why the turnover differential between annual value and monthly momentum fully explains this order-of-magnitude capacity divergence.

Solution

The turnover ratio between value and momentum is approximately \(\lambda = \frac{\tau_{\text{value}}}{\tau_{\text{momentum}}} \approx \frac{0.18}{1.80} = 0.10\) (value turnover is roughly one-tenth of momentum). By Theorem 3.6, capacity scales as \(\tau^{-3}\) under square-root impact if gross alpha and universe liquidity were identical. Here, momentum possesses slightly higher gross alpha (68 bps vs 38 bps gross monthly), but its 10x higher turnover elevates \(\kappa\) by \(10^{1.5} \approx 31.6\). Because capacity scales as \(\kappa^{-2}\), the 10x turnover penalty suppresses capacity by \((31.6)^2 = 1{,}000\), offset by higher alpha squared \((68/38)^2 \approx 3.2\). This predicts momentum capacity should be roughly \(\frac{3.2}{1{,}000} \approx \frac{1}{300}\) of value on pure mechanical turnover, which institutional algorithmic execution and buffer mitigation moderate to roughly one-tenth ($5B vs $52B).

Problem 3.8: During market stress events (e.g. March 2020), equity market volatility \(\sigma\) typically doubles while average trading volume ADV drops by 30%. By what factor does portfolio friction \(\kappa\) increase, and what happens to strategy capacity \(A^*\)?

Solution

Friction scales as \(\kappa \propto \sigma / \sqrt{\text{ADV}}\). With \(\sigma_{\text{new}} = 2.0 \sigma\) and \(\text{ADV}_{\text{new}} = 0.70 \text{ADV}\): \[\frac{\kappa_{\text{new}}}{\kappa} = \frac{2.0}{\sqrt{0.70}} = \frac{2.0}{0.8367} \approx 2.39.\] Portfolio friction \(\kappa\) increases by 139% (a factor of 2.39). Capacity scales as \(A^* \propto \kappa^{-2}\): \[\frac{A_{\text{new}}^*}{A^*} = \left(\frac{1}{2.39}\right)^2 \approx 0.175.\] Strategy capacity collapses by over 82%, dropping to less than one-fifth of its calm-market scale.

Problem 3.9: An institutional multi-manager allocator observes that a quantitative fund operating at $15B AUM produced a net Sharpe ratio of 1.20 five years ago at $2B AUM, but its net Sharpe ratio has steadily decayed to 0.45 today. The manager claims this decay is due to "unfavorable macroeconomic factor headwinds." How can capacity modeling evaluate this claim?

Solution

To evaluate whether decay is capacity-driven versus macro headwinds:
1. Evaluate Paper Alpha: Reconstruct the fund's gross paper returns (unexecuted model signals) over the same period. If gross paper Sharpe ratio remained stable at ~1.20 while net Sharpe ratio decayed, macroeconomic factor efficacy is intact.
2. Test \(\sqrt{A}\) Scaling: Measure the fund's realized implementation shortfall. If average execution drag scaled with \(\sqrt{A}\) (from \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41\) to \(\sqrt{15} \approx 3.87\), a 2.74x increase in unit execution cost), the return decay matches the exact theoretical signature of capacity exhaustion.
3. Participation Rates: Verify whether child orders now exceed 5%–10% of ADV in small-cap constituents. If so, the fund has exceeded its profit-maximizing scale \(A_{\text{opt}}\).

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 3.10: How does endogenous factor crowding alter the portfolio friction coefficient \(\kappa\)? Formulate a model where \(M\) independent funds concurrently trade the identical factor signal.

Solution

Let fund \(m \in \{1, \dots, M\}\) manage AUM \(A_m\) and trade identical directional weights \(\Delta w_i\). Total market-wide order flow in asset \(i\) is: \[D_i^{\text{tot}} = \sum_{m=1}^M A_m |\Delta w_i| = A_{\text{agg}} |\Delta w_i|, \quad \text{where } A_{\text{agg}} \equiv \sum_{m=1}^M A_m.\] The market impact on asset \(i\) is determined by aggregate order flow: \[I_i = Y \sigma_i \sqrt{\frac{A_{\text{agg}} |\Delta w_i|}{\text{ADV}_i}}.\] The dollar cost incurred by fund \(m\) trading size \(D_{i, m} = A_m |\Delta w_i|\) is: \[\mathcal{C}_{i, m} = D_{i, m} I_i = (A_m |\Delta w_i|) \left( Y \sigma_i \frac{\sqrt{A_{\text{agg}} |\Delta w_i|}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_i}} \right) = Y A_m \sqrt{A_{\text{agg}}} \sigma_i \frac{|\Delta w_i|^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\text{ADV}_i}}.\] Unit cost for fund \(m\) is: \[\frac{\mathcal{C}_m}{A_m} = \kappa \sqrt{A_{\text{agg}}}.\] Each fund's unit execution drag depends not on its own AUM, but on the square root of aggregate industry crowded capital \(A_{\text{agg}}\). A small fund managing only $100M incurs severe drag if giant peers trade the identical signal.

Problem 3.11: Propose an optimal capital allocation algorithm for a multi-strategy quantitative fund allocating total capital \(A_{\text{firm}}\) across \(K\) orthogonal factor strategies, each characterized by gross alpha \(\alpha_k\) and friction \(\kappa_k\).

Solution

Let \(A_k\) denote the capital allocated to strategy \(k\), with \(\sum_{k=1}^K A_k = A_{\text{firm}}\) and \(A_k \ge 0\). Total firm dollar profit is the sum of strategy profits: \[\max_{\{A_k\}} \sum_{k=1}^K \left[ \alpha_k A_k - \kappa_k A_k^{3/2} \right] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{k=1}^K A_k = A_{\text{firm}}.\] Forming the Lagrangian with multiplier \(\lambda\): \[\mathcal{L} = \sum_{k=1}^K \left[ \alpha_k A_k - \kappa_k A_k^{3/2} \right] - \lambda \left(\sum_{k=1}^K A_k - A_{\text{firm}}\right).\] First-order conditions with respect to \(A_k\): \[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial A_k} = \alpha_k - \frac{3}{2}\kappa_k A_k^{1/2} - \lambda = 0 \implies A_k^{1/2} = \frac{\alpha_k - \lambda}{\frac{3}{2}\kappa_k} \implies A_k = \left(\frac{\max(0, \alpha_k - \lambda)}{\frac{3}{2}\kappa_k}\right)^2.\] The shadow cost \(\lambda\) is solved via one-dimensional bisection to satisfy \(\sum A_k(\lambda) = A_{\text{firm}}\). Capital is allocated so that marginal dollar alpha \(\frac{d\mathcal{P}_k}{dA_k} = \alpha_k - \frac{3}{2}\kappa_k \sqrt{A_k}\) is equalized across all active sleeves.

Problem 3.12: How does trade execution horizon expansion (e.g. slicing metaorders over 5 days instead of 1 day) alter the effective daily volume denominator in the square-root impact model, and what risk does horizon lengthening introduce?

Solution

If an order of size \(Q\) is executed over \(H\) trading days:
1. Volume Expansion: The total available market volume across the execution window is \(V_{\text{tot}} = H \cdot \text{ADV}\). Daily trade slice is \(Q / H\). Market impact per slice is \(I_{\text{slice}} = Y \sigma \sqrt{(Q/H)/\text{ADV}} = Y \sigma \sqrt{Q / (H \cdot \text{ADV})}\). Unit impact cost is reduced by a factor of \(1 / \sqrt{H}\).
2. Inventory Risk Penalty: Lengthening execution horizon exposes the portfolio to inventory price risk: variance of unexecuted holdings grows linearly with \(H\) (\(\Var = \sigma^2 H\)). In the Almgren–Chriss framework, optimal horizon \(H^*\) balances the \(H^{-1/2}\) market impact savings against the linear \(\lambda_{\text{risk}} \sigma^2 H\) inventory risk penalty.

8Retrieval Quiz

  1. Under the empirical square-root law of market impact, how does total dollar transaction cost \(\mathcal{C}(A)\) scale with portfolio AUM \(A\)?

    Options

    Trade size scales as \(A\), and square-root impact scales as \(\sqrt{A}\); their product yields total dollar cost scaling as \(A \times \sqrt{A} = A^{3/2}\).

  2. What percentage of break-even capacity \(A^*\) maximizes total dollar profit added (\(100 \times A_{\text{opt}} / A^*\)) under square-root impact?

    \(A_{\text{opt}} / A^* = 4/9 \approx 44.44\%\).

  3. What is the value of net alpha \(\alpha^{\text{net}}(A_{\text{opt}})\) at profit-maximizing capacity relative to gross alpha \(\alpha_0\)?

    Options

    Theorem 3.2 proves that \(\alpha^{\text{net}}(A_{\text{opt}}) = \alpha_0 - \kappa \left(\frac{2\alpha_0}{3\kappa}\right) = \frac{1}{3}\alpha_0\).

  4. A factor strategy has gross alpha of 10.0% and friction \(\kappa = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}\). What is its break-even capacity \(A^*\) in billions of dollars?

    \(A^* = (0.10 / (2.0 \times 10^{-6}))^2 = (50{,}000)^2 = 2{,}500{,}000{,}000 = 2.5\) billion dollars.

  5. Why did Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018) find that live institutional trading costs are substantially lower than standard academic models predict?

    Options

    Real-world algorithmic execution exploits liquidity provision, crossing networks, and multi-day scheduling to reduce effective execution drag.

  6. What happens to a fund that expands its AUM past \(A_{\text{opt}} = \frac{4}{9} A^*\) toward \(A^*\)?

    Options

    Beyond \(A_{\text{opt}}\), marginal transaction costs exceed marginal gross return, destroying total firm dollar alpha.

  7. If a quantitative team implements an optimization technique that reduces portfolio turnover by exactly 50% (\(\lambda = 0.5\)), by what factor does strategy capacity \(A^*\) expand under square-root market impact?

    By Theorem 3.6, capacity scales as \(\lambda^{-3} = (0.5)^{-3} = 2^3 = 8.0\).

  8. How does market-wide factor crowding affect individual fund execution costs?

    Options

    When multiple funds trade the same signal, order flows aggregate into massive collective metaorders that exhaust limit order book liquidity.
