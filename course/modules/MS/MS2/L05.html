High-Frequency Trading & Latency Arbitrage

High-frequency trading (HFT) has transformed financial markets from human-intermediated floor specialist systems into automated algorithmic networks operating at microsecond speeds. While empirical studies demonstrate that HFT market makers narrow bid–ask spreads and accelerate price discovery, the architecture of continuous limit order books creates a costly structural inefficiency: latency arbitrage. When correlated asset prices jump, fast traders race to pick off stale resting quotes before liquidity providers can cancel them. This speed race generates substantial private rents, consumes billions in socially wasteful infrastructure, and imposes an implicit tax on market liquidity.

1Anatomy of High-Frequency Trading

High-frequency trading represents the quantitative apex of electronic execution, combining proprietary capital, statistical models, and specialized computing hardware.

Definition 5.1 · High-Frequency Trading
High-frequency trading (HFT) is a class of algorithmic trading characterized by proprietary capital investment, ultra-low latency infrastructure (such as direct market feeds, co-location in exchange data centers, and FPGA hardware acceleration), sub-millisecond execution horizons, high order-to-trade ratios (frequent quote updates and cancellations), and flat end-of-day inventory positions.

HFT strategies are broadly organized into two functional categories:

  • Quantitative Market Making (Passive): Automated liquidity provision posting bilateral limit orders at the NBBO, capturing the bid–ask spread, earning exchange maker rebates, and managing sub-second inventory using optimal control frameworks.
  • Cross-Market Arbitrage & Sniping (Aggressive): Ultra-fast directional strategies that monitor public news and price changes across correlated assets (e.g., futures in Chicago and ETFs in New Jersey) to pick off stale quotes on slower venues using immediate-or-cancel market orders.

Theorem 5.1 · Empirical Properties of HFT Price Discovery
As established empirically by Brogaard, Hendershott & Riordan (2014) using proprietary NASDAQ dataset logs with participant-level flags:

  1. HFT marketable orders trade in the direction of permanent price changes (positive permanent price impact), contributing significantly to efficient price discovery.
  2. HFT limit orders supply liquidity against transitory price shocks, reducing pricing errors and dampening non-fundamental volatility.
  3. HFT activity is negatively correlated with effective bid–ask spreads, narrowing trading costs for institutional and retail investors during normal market conditions.

2The Mechanics of Latency Arbitrage

Despite the liquidity benefits documented by Brogaard et al. (2014), continuous limit order books possess a fundamental structural flaw formalized by Budish, Cramton & Shim (2015).

Definition 5.2 · Latency Arbitrage
Latency arbitrage is the practice of exploiting transmission and processing latency differences across fragmented or correlated markets to execute aggressive market orders against stale resting limit orders before the liquidity providers who posted those orders can cancel or update them.

Consider two identical or highly correlated securities—for example, the E-mini S&P 500 futures contract trading on the CME in Aurora, Illinois, and the SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY) trading on NASDAQ in Carteret, New Jersey. The physical distance between Chicago and New Jersey is approximately 730 miles, imposing an irreducible speed-of-light propagation floor of approximately 3.9 milliseconds in fiber (and ~2.8 ms in microwave links).

Suppose an innovation shifts the fundamental value of the S&P 500 by \(\Delta V > 0\) in Chicago. S&P 500 market makers in New Jersey attempt to cancel their resting ask quotes at $100.05. Simultaneously, multiple high-frequency arbitrageurs in Chicago detect the price jump and transmit aggressive market buy orders to New Jersey to pick off the stale $100.05 asks.

Theorem 5.2 · The Speed Race and Equilibrium Rent Tax
In a continuous limit order book (CLOB) where public jumps \(\Delta V\) arrive with jump distribution \(F_{\Delta V}\) and market makers incur latency \(\delta_{\text{MM}}\):

  1. If \(K\) snipers compete with minimum arrival latency \(\delta_{\text{sniper}} < \delta_{\text{MM}}\), the fastest sniper extracts rent on each jump \(|\Delta V| > s/2\): \[R = \left(|\Delta V| - \frac{s}{2}\right) q.\]
  2. Increasing sniper competition \(K\) does not eliminate aggregate latency rents. As \(K \to \infty\), \(\min_{k} \delta_{\text{sniper}, k}\) weakly decreases, leaving total latency-arbitrage rents extracted from market makers strictly positive.
  3. In competitive equilibrium, liquidity providers must widen the equilibrium bid–ask spread \(s\) to earn sufficient spread revenue from uninformed investors to cover their expected sniping losses: \[s^* = 2 c_{\text{inventory}} + \frac{\E[R_{\text{sniping}}]}{\text{Volume}_{\text{uninformed}}}.\]

Proof Sketch
In continuous time, incoming orders are processed serially by the exchange matching engine. When \(\Delta V > s/2\), the stale ask is priced below the new fundamental value. The first message to reach the matching engine wins: if a sniper arrives before the market maker's cancellation request (\(\delta_{\text{sniper}} < \delta_{\text{MM}}\)), the sniper buys at the stale price, locking in riskless profit \((\Delta V - s/2)q\). The market maker loses this exact amount. Competing snipers race against each other for priority, but their competition does not alter the execution price—the winner still trades against the market maker's stale limit order at price \(p = V_{\text{old}} + s/2\). Because the market maker earns zero economic profit in competitive equilibrium, the expected loss to latency arbitrageurs operates as an unavoidable cost of liquidity provision, which must be passed on to regular investors through wider bid–ask spreads.

Lab 1 · Budish-Cramton-Shim latency race simulator

Simulating public price jumps ($0.20 innovations, half-spread $0.05, net rent $0.15/lot) across 1,000 jumps as the number of competing snipers \(K \in \{1, 2, 5, 10, 20\}\) increases will demonstrate that: (1) total latency arbitrage rents extracted from market makers increase monotonically from $108.15 to $147.00; (2) competition dilutes rent per sniper from $108.15 down to $7.35 without reducing market maker losses.

"""Lab MS2-L05-1: Budish-Cramton-Shim latency arbitrage simulator.

Simulates cross-asset public news arrivals, quote sniping, and latency arbitrage rents
in a continuous limit order book as the number of competing snipers N varies.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random


def simulate_latency_race(
    n_jumps: int,
    n_snipers: int,
    half_spread: float,
    jump_size: float,
    mm_latency: float,
    sniper_mean_latency: float,
    rng: random.Random,
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Simulate public jump innovations and races between market maker and N snipers.

    Returns:
        (total_sniper_rent, mm_loss, avg_rent_per_sniper)
    """
    total_rent = 0.0

    for _ in range(n_jumps):
        # Public innovation occurs: jump_size > half_spread
        # MM attempts to cancel:
        mm_cancel_time = rng.expovariate(1.0 / mm_latency)

        # N snipers race to pick off the quote:
        sniper_times = [rng.expovariate(1.0 / sniper_mean_latency) for _ in range(n_snipers)]
        fastest_sniper_time = min(sniper_times)

        # If fastest sniper arrives before MM cancel request:
        if fastest_sniper_time < mm_cancel_time:
            rent = jump_size - half_spread
            total_rent += rent

    mm_loss = total_rent
    per_sniper_rent = total_rent / n_snipers if n_snipers > 0 else 0.0

    return total_rent, mm_loss, per_sniper_rent


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n_jumps = 1000
    half_spread = 0.05
    jump_size = 0.20  # $0.20 jump, net rent = 0.20 - 0.05 = $0.15 per sniped lot
    mm_latency = 2.0  # mean 2.0 ms
    sniper_mean_lat = 0.8  # mean 0.8 ms (snipers are systematically faster)

    sniper_counts = [1, 2, 5, 10, 20]

    print("Budish-Cramton-Shim (2015) Latency Race Simulation (N=1000 jumps):")
    print("Snipers (K) | Total Rent ($) | MM Loss ($) | Rent per Sniper ($) | Sniped Pct")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    for k in sniper_counts:
        total_rent, mm_loss, per_sniper = simulate_latency_race(
            n_jumps,
            k,
            half_spread,
            jump_size,
            mm_latency,
            sniper_mean_lat,
            rng,
        )
        sniped_pct = (total_rent / (n_jumps * (jump_size - half_spread))) * 100.0

        print(f"    {k:2d}      | ${total_rent:12.2f} | ${mm_loss:9.2f} | ${per_sniper:17.2f} | {sniped_pct:9.2f}%")


main()

The simulation validates Theorem 5.2. As the number of competing snipers grows from 1 to 20, the probability of sniping the stale quote increases from 72.10% to 98.00%, driving total market maker losses up from $108.15 to $147.00. Crucially, increased competition on speed does not lower the price or eliminate the arbitrage—it merely fragments the prize among more participants ($7.35/sniper). This confirms Budish et al.'s central thesis: speed competition in continuous time is socially wasteful prisoner's dilemma rent-seeking.

3Debate Brief: Does HFT Improve Market Quality?

1. Resolution

Resolved: High-frequency trading improves overall market quality by narrowing bid–ask spreads and enhancing price discovery without imposing net welfare costs on long-term investors.

2. Strongest Case for HFT

The case for HFT rests on extensive empirical evidence across global equity and derivatives markets. As documented by Brogaard, Hendershott & Riordan (2014), HFT market makers replaced human floor specialists who commanded wide oligopolistic spreads. Automated market making operates on razor-thin profit margins, reducing quoted and effective bid–ask spreads by over 50% across US equities over the past two decades. Furthermore, HFT algorithmic order flow accelerates the impounding of public information into prices, reducing pricing anomalies and providing deep liquidity during normal trading hours.

3. Strongest Case Against HFT

The case against HFT highlights structural market design failures and systemic fragility. As proved by Budish, Cramton & Shim (2015), continuous limit order books induce an arms race: fast traders spend billions on microwave networks, laser links, and FPGA chips to snipe stale quotes. This latency arbitrage generates zero social surplus—it is pure rent-seeking that acts as a private tax on liquidity providers, who pass this cost to long-term investors through wider spreads than would prevail under discrete batch auctions. Moreover, as demonstrated during the 2010 Flash Crash (Kirilenko et al. 2017), HFT liquidity is fleeting; automated market makers rapidly withdraw quotes during extreme volatility, exacerbating tail-risk cascades.

5. State of the Literature

The academic consensus distinguishes between passive HFT market making (which generally enhances liquidity and reduces trading costs in calm markets) and aggressive HFT latency arbitrage (which creates negative externalities and arms-race waste). The literature increasingly views latency arbitrage not as a failure of high-frequency trading per se, but as a direct structural consequence of continuous-time limit order book design.

6. What Evidence Would Settle It?

The debate could be settled empirically by a large-scale randomized trial or regulatory reform where a major national exchange transitions from continuous trading to 100-millisecond Frequent Batch Auctions (Budish et al. 2015). If quoted spreads narrow and market depth expands under batch auctions without degrading price discovery, the hypothesis that continuous speed races harm market quality would be definitively confirmed.

4Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook equilibrium models assume that competition eliminates excess economic rents. In high-frequency markets, this principle fails due to discrete speed monopolies:

  • Non-Diminishing Arbitrage Rents: Standard microeconomic theory posits that as more firms enter an arbitrage trade, economic profits fall to zero. In continuous latency arbitrage, the fastest firm takes 100% of the rent, while the second-fastest takes 0%. Increasing the number of competitors does not reduce the price wedge or aggregate rent; it simply increases aggregate capital expenditures on microwave towers and sub-nanosecond networking equipment.
  • Fragility Under Order Cancellation Surges: During rapid market moves, HFT message rates surge by orders of magnitude (often exceeding 500,000 messages per second in single tickers), saturating gateway queues and degrading matching engine deterministic processing.
  • Correlated Quote Pulling: Because quantitative market makers utilize identical cross-asset signals (e.g., CME futures lead), automated algorithms withdraw liquidity simultaneously across multiple equities, generating sudden liquidity vacuums.

5Misconceptions

  1. High-frequency trading firms make profits by front-running retail orders through illegal insider information.

    Why it is wrong: HFT firms trade using public data (such as direct exchange multicast feeds and CME futures prices). Front-running an order before it reaches the exchange is illegal. What critics colloquially term "front-running" is lawful latency arbitrage: observing a public price change on one venue and trading on another venue before slower participants update their quotes.

  2. High-frequency market makers hold massive overnight equity inventory.

    Why it is wrong: HFT market makers operate with near-zero overnight inventory. Their business model relies on turning over inventory hundreds or thousands of times daily, capturing sub-penny spreads per trade while strictly hedging inventory across correlated derivatives to avoid overnight price risk.

  3. Adding more high-frequency trading competitors will eventually compress latency arbitrage rents to zero.

    Why it is wrong: As proved by Budish, Cramton & Shim (2015), the prize in a latency race is determined by the size of the price innovation \(|\Delta V|\), which is exogenously fixed by news. More competitors simply divide the prize; the aggregate rent extracted from stale quotes remains strictly positive in continuous time.

6Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 5.1: A public news announcement in Chicago shifts the fundamental value of an asset by \(\Delta V = +\$0.35\). In New Jersey, a market maker quotes an ask of $100.08 (mid-price $100.00, half-spread $0.08). The market maker's cancel message takes 2.8 ms to reach the engine. A high-frequency sniper transmits a buy order that arrives in 1.4 ms. Compute: (1) whether the stale quote is sniped; (2) the execution price; (3) the net latency arbitrage rent earned on a 1,000-share execution.

Solution
  1. Arrival comparison: Sniper arrives at \(1.4\) ms, which is strictly before the market maker cancellation at \(2.8\) ms. Furthermore, the jump \(\Delta V = 0.35\) exceeds the half-spread \(0.08\). Therefore, the stale quote is successfully sniped.
  2. Execution price: The sniper buys at the stale displayed ask price of \(P = \$100.08\).
  3. Net rent: The new fundamental value is \(100.00 + 0.35 = \$100.35\). The net rent per share is: \[R_{\text{share}} = V_{\text{new}} - P = 100.35 - 100.08 = \$0.27 \quad (\Delta V - s/2 = 0.35 - 0.08 = 0.27).\] For 1,000 shares, total net latency arbitrage rent is: \[\text{Total Rent} = 1,000 \times \$0.27 = \$270.00.\]

Problem 5.2: An HFT firm trades 100 million shares per day with an order-to-trade ratio of 40:1 (40 message submissions or cancellations per executed trade). How many total messages does the firm submit to exchanges daily?

Solution

Assuming 100 million shares executed in standard 100-share round lots, the total number of executed trades is: \[N_{\text{trades}} = \frac{100,000,000 \text{ shares}}{100 \text{ shares/trade}} = 1,000,000 \text{ trades.}\] With an order-to-trade ratio of 40:1, the firm submits: \[N_{\text{messages}} = 1,000,000 \times 40 = 40,000,000 \text{ messages per day.}\] Across a 6.5-hour trading day (23,400 seconds), this represents an average rate of approximately 1,709 messages per second.

Problem 5.3: In the Budish-Cramton-Shim model, a market maker expects 500 sniping events per day with an average loss of $40 per event. Total daily trading volume from uninformed investors is 2,000,000 shares. If direct order handling cost is 0.5 cents ($0.005) per share, compute the competitive equilibrium quoted half-spread \(s/2\).

Solution

Total daily sniping losses inflicted on the market maker are: \[\text{Total Sniping Losses} = 500 \times \$40 = \$20,000.00 \text{ per day.}\] To break even under competitive Bertrand pricing, the market maker must recover these losses from uninformed volume: \[\text{Sniping Tax per Share} = \frac{\$20,000.00}{2,000,000 \text{ shares}} = \$0.010 \text{ per share} \quad (1 \text{ cent}).\] Adding direct order handling cost \(c = \$0.005\): \[\frac{s^*}{2} = c + \text{Sniping Tax} = 0.005 + 0.010 = \$0.015 \text{ per share} \quad (1.5 \text{ cents}).\] The full competitive equilibrium bid-ask spread is \(s^* = 2 \times 0.015 = \$0.030\) (3 cents).

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 5.4: Prove that in a continuous limit order book, the probability that an innovation is sniped approaches 1 as the number of independent snipers \(K \to \infty\), provided snipers possess independent latency draws from a distribution with non-zero support near zero.

Solution

Let the market maker's cancel arrival time be \(\delta_{\text{MM}} > 0\). Let \(K\) snipers have independent and identically distributed latency draws \(\delta_k \sim F_{\delta}\) with support on \([0, \infty)\), where \(F_{\delta}(\delta_{\text{MM}}) = p > 0\). The probability that an individual sniper arrives strictly before the market maker is \(p = \Prob(\delta_k < \delta_{\text{MM}})\). The fastest sniper arrival is \(\delta_{(1)} = \min_{1 \le k \le K} \delta_k\). The probability that at least one sniper beats the market maker is: \[\Prob(\delta_{(1)} < \delta_{\text{MM}}) = 1 - \Prob(\text{all } \delta_k \ge \delta_{\text{MM}}) = 1 - [1 - F_{\delta}(\delta_{\text{MM}})]^K = 1 - (1 - p)^K.\] Because \(p > 0\), \(1 - p \in [0, 1)\). Taking the limit as \(K \to \infty\): \[\lim_{K \to \infty} \Prob(\delta_{(1)} < \delta_{\text{MM}}) = 1 - \lim_{K \to \infty} (1 - p)^K = 1 - 0 = 1.\] This proves that under free entry of fast traders, stale quotes are picked off with probability 1 whenever jump size exceeds the half-spread.

Problem 5.5: Prove that investing in low-latency communication infrastructure constitutes a prisoner's dilemma in the Budish-Cramton-Shim framework.

Solution

Consider two trading firms \(A\) and \(B\) competing for a fixed latency arbitrage prize of value \(V > 0\) per period. Each firm can choose to invest in low-latency infrastructure at cost \(C \in (0, V/2)\), or remain at baseline speed at cost 0.

  • If neither invests, both firms have equal speed, sharing the prize equally: payoff is \((V/2, V/2)\).
  • If Firm A invests and Firm B does not, Firm A wins the entire prize with certainty, earning net payoff \(V - C\); Firm B earns 0.
  • If Firm B invests and Firm A does not, Firm B earns \(V - C\) and Firm A earns 0.
  • If both invest, both have equal fast speed, splitting the prize: payoff is \((V/2 - C, V/2 - C)\).

For Firm A, if Firm B does not invest, investing yields \(V - C > V/2\) (since \(C < V/2\)). If Firm B invests, investing yields \(V/2 - C > 0\). Therefore, investing is a strictly dominant strategy for both firms. The unique Nash equilibrium is (Invest, Invest), yielding joint net surplus \(V - 2C < V\). In contrast, the socially optimal cooperative outcome (No Invest, No Invest) yields joint surplus \(V\). The equilibrium outcome is strictly Pareto-dominated, proving that the speed race is a prisoner's dilemma.

Problem 5.6: Prove that Brogaard et al.'s (2014) finding that HFT marketable orders have positive permanent price impact implies that HFT order flow carries private information in the sense of Kyle (1985).

Solution

In Kyle's (1985) microstructure model (Kyle model, MS1), transaction prices evolve according to \(p_t = p_{t-1} + \lambda y_t + \eta_t\), where \(\lambda = \Cov(v, y) / \Var(y)\). An order flow component \(y_t\) generates a permanent price change \(\Delta p_t^{\text{perm}} > 0\) if and only if \(\E[v - p_{t-1} \mid y_t] \ne 0\), establishing that the order flow is correlated with fundamental liquidation value \(v\). Brogaard et al. (2014) estimate vector autoregressions (VAR) decomposing price changes into permanent (information) and transitory (inventory/microstructure noise) components: \[\Delta p_{t+k} = \sum_{i=0}^k \beta_i x_{t-i}^{\text{HFT}} + \epsilon_{t+k}.\] They find \(\lim_{k \to \infty} \sum_{i=0}^k \beta_i > 0\) statistically significantly. In Kyle's framework, a non-zero permanent impact vector establishes that HFT marketable order flow correlates positively with unobserved fundamental value revisions, formally confirming the presence of private or faster public informational content.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 5.7: Using the Lab 1 simulation results, compute the percentage of total jumps that were successfully sniped when \(K = 1\) versus \(K = 20\), and calculate the marginal increase in total rents extracted when expanding from 10 to 20 snipers.

Solution

From the Lab 1 output table with 1,000 jumps of net rent $0.15/lot (maximum possible rent = $150.00):

  • At \(K = 1\): Total rent = $108.15. Sniped percentage = \(108.15 / 150.00 = 72.10\%\).
  • At \(K = 20\): Total rent = $147.00. Sniped percentage = \(147.00 / 150.00 = 98.00\%\).

At \(K = 10\), total rent was $144.30. The marginal increase in rent when expanding to 20 snipers is: \[\Delta \text{Rent} = \$147.00 - \$144.30 = \$2.70 \quad (+1.87\%).\] Notice strong diminishing returns: doubling snipers from 10 to 20 captures only $2.70 in additional rent, while diluting per-sniper rents from $14.43 down to $7.35.

Problem 5.8: Brogaard et al. (2014) report that HFTs participate in approximately 68.5% of total share volume in large-cap US equities, acting as the liquidity provider on 51.4% of trades and as the liquidity taker on 50.4% of trades (overlapping on trades where HFT meets HFT). Calculate the proportion of total volume where an HFT trades directly with another HFT.

Solution

Let each trade have two sides: Maker (M) and Taker (T). Total sides = 200%. The proportion of volume where an HFT is on at least one side is 68.5%. By the inclusion-exclusion principle for trade sides: \[P(\text{HFT on at least one side}) = P(\text{HFT Maker}) + P(\text{HFT Taker}) - P(\text{HFT Maker AND HFT Taker}).\] Substitute the reported empirical figures: \[0.685 = 0.514 + 0.504 - P(\text{HFT meets HFT}).\] \[0.685 = 1.018 - P(\text{HFT meets HFT}) \implies P(\text{HFT meets HFT}) = 1.018 - 0.685 = 0.333.\] Therefore, in approximately 33.3% of total market volume, high-frequency trading firms trade directly against other high-frequency trading firms.

Problem 5.9: A proprietary trading firm establishes a direct microwave link between Aurora (CME) and Secaucus (BATS), reducing transmission latency from 7.4 ms (fiber) to 4.1 ms (microwave). If the firm earns an additional $18,000 per day in cross-market sniping profits, calculate the payback period for a $12 million initial microwave infrastructure expenditure over 252 trading days.

Solution

Annual incremental revenue from the microwave advantage is: \[\text{Annual Revenue} = 252 \times \$18,000 = \$4,536,000.00.\] The simple payback period for the $12,000,000 capital expenditure is: \[\text{Payback Period} = \frac{\$12,000,000}{\$4,536,000} \approx 2.65 \text{ years (approximately 32 months).}\] This rapid capital recovery explains why trading firms race to construct redundant microwave networks.

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 5.10: Formulate an asymmetric information model where the market maker quotes a schedule of limit orders across multiple price levels. How does the threat of latency arbitrage alter the equilibrium slope of the limit order book?

Solution

In continuous trading, when a public jump \(\Delta V\) occurs, the latency arbitrageur sweeps all price levels up to \(p_k \le V_{\text{old}} + \Delta V\). If the market maker posts a deep, flat book at price levels close to the mid-price, the sniper extracts cumulative rents across all resting levels: \(\sum_{j=1}^m (V_{\text{new}} - p_j) q_j\). To protect against multi-level quote sniping, the market maker must steepen the book's price-depth schedule: posting smaller quantities at the inside spread and widening the price intervals between successive depth tranches. Consequently, latency arbitrage reduces displayed depth throughout the entire order book, increasing market impact for large institutional block orders.

Problem 5.11: Evaluate the "speed bump" mechanism implemented by IEX (Investors Exchange), which imposes a 350-microsecond continuous delay coil on all incoming orders while allowing the exchange's internal matching engine to update pegged quotes using direct feeds without delay. What are the economic arguments for and against asymmetric speed bumps?

Solution

The IEX speed bump routes incoming packets through a 38-mile spool of fiber optic cable, imposing a 350-microsecond delay. Crucially, IEX's proprietary matching engine updates pegged midpoint quotes using direct exchange feeds without delay. When a price jump occurs in Chicago, the 350 \(\mu\)s delay allows IEX to reprice its resting pegged orders before predatory snipers can arrive, completely eliminating stale-quote sniping on IEX. Proponents argue this creates a safe harbor for institutional investors, protecting passive midpoint orders. Opponents (including traditional exchanges and quantitative market makers) argue that asymmetric speed bumps violate Rule 611's "immediate and automated" quote protection mandate and distort price discovery by free-riding on other venues' displayed quotes.

Problem 5.12: Does latency arbitrage create social value by enforcing the Law of One Price across geographically separated markets? Contrast the market efficiency view against the Budish et al. rent-seeking view.

Solution

The neoclassical market efficiency view posits that arbitrageurs perform valuable economic work by eliminating price discrepancies between futures and cash markets, aligning prices with fundamental information and preventing cross-market misallocations. The Budish et al. (2015) view demonstrates that while price alignment is valuable, doing so in continuous time at microsecond speeds generates virtually zero social utility (economic resource allocation does not change whether SPY aligns with E-mini in 50 microseconds versus 100 milliseconds). The continuous-time race turns price alignment into a pure speed contest where the gains are private rents transferred from market makers, while the real resources spent on microwave towers and sub-nanosecond chips represent deadweight social loss.

  8. What happens to an HFT firm's inventory position at the end of each trading day?

    Options

    HFT market makers typically liquidate inventory positions before the close to avoid overnight market gaps and capital margin requirements.
