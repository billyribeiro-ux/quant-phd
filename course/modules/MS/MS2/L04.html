Dark Pools & Payment for Order Flow

In contemporary equity markets, over 40% of share volume executes off-exchange away from public limit order books. Non-displayed venues—comprising dark pools and retail internalizers—allow participants to execute without displaying quotes to the broader market. Retail brokerages route customer orders to wholesale market makers who purchase order flow via payment for order flow (PFOF). Because retail order flow is overwhelmingly uninformed, internalizers trade against it profitably while granting price improvement. However, this off-exchange segmentation inflicts a severe cream-skimming externality: toxic informed flow is concentrated on public lit exchanges, widening quoted spreads for the entire market.

1Alternative Trading Systems and Dark Pools

A lit exchange displays bid and ask quotes to the entire market, establishing the National Best Bid and Offer (NBBO) analyzed in Lesson 3. In contrast, non-displayed venues execute trades without publicly disseminating pre-trade quotes.

Definition 4.1 · Dark Pool
A dark pool is an Alternative Trading System (ATS) registered under SEC Regulation ATS that matches buy and sell orders electronically without displaying pre-trade quotations (prices or sizes) to the public.

Dark pools are classified into three primary operational structures:

  • Midpoint Crossing Networks: Passive matching engines that execute orders strictly at the midpoint of the prevailing NBBO: \[P_{\text{cross}} = \frac{\text{NBB} + \text{NBO}}{2}.\] Both buyers and sellers avoid paying the half-spread, executing with zero price impact.
  • Broker-Dealer Internalizers: Proprietary matching systems operated by investment banks and independent wholesalers that cross client orders against dealer capital or institutional inventory.
  • Exchange-Operated Dark Books: Non-displayed order types hosted directly within registered national exchanges (such as midpoint peg and hidden limit orders).

2Payment for Order Flow (PFOF)

Retail equity orders possess unique structural characteristics: they are small, uncoordinated, and trade primarily for liquidity or rebalancing purposes rather than private short-term informational advantage. Retail order flow is overwhelmingly "uninformed" in the formal sense of the Glosten–Milgrom model from MS1.

Definition 4.2 · Payment for Order Flow
Payment for order flow (PFOF) is the compensation (in the form of cash rebates, fee discounts, or services) that a wholesale market maker transfers to an introducing retail broker-dealer in exchange for the broker directing customer order flow to the wholesaler for off-exchange execution.

Under a typical PFOF contract, a retail broker routes 100% of its marketable customer orders to a designated wholesaler. The wholesaler commits to execute each order at or inside the NBBO, providing price improvement: buying at slightly above the National Best Bid or selling at slightly below the National Best Offer. In return, the wholesaler pays the broker a fee (typically 10 to 20 cents per 100 shares, or $0.0010–$0.0020 per share).

Example 4.1 · Economics of Retail Internalization
Suppose Stock ABC has an NBBO of Bid $100.00 (1,000 shares) and Ask $100.04 (1,000 shares), with spread \(s = \$0.04\). A retail customer submits a market buy order for 100 shares. If routed to a lit exchange, the customer pays the ask of $100.04. Instead, the retail broker routes to a wholesale internalizer. The internalizer executes the buy at $100.035, delivering $0.005 per share ($0.50) in price improvement to the retail customer. The internalizer pays the retail broker a PFOF rebate of $0.0015 per share ($0.15). Because retail order flow carries zero adverse selection, the internalizer's expected holding profit relative to the mid-price ($100.02) is: \[\text{Gross Margin} = \$100.035 - \$100.020 = \$0.015 \text{ per share.}\] Net of the $0.0015 PFOF fee, the internalizer captures a riskless spread profit of $0.0135 per share ($1.35 on the trade).

3The Cream-Skimming Equilibrium and Lit Spread Widening

While retail investors receive modest price improvement and retail brokers generate substantial fee revenue, off-exchange internalization imposes a severe negative externality on the broader market known as cream-skimming.

Theorem 4.1 · Cream-Skimming Spread Widening
Let the total population of market orders consist of fraction \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\) uninformed retail orders and fraction \(1 - \alpha\) informed institutional orders with adverse selection impact \(\delta > 0\). Market makers incur order-handling cost \(c > 0\).

  1. Consolidated Lit Market: When all orders execute on a single lit exchange, the competitive half-spread is: \[H_{\text{lit}} = (1 - \alpha) \delta + c.\]
  2. Segmented Market with PFOF: When fraction \(\rho \in (0, 1]\) of uninformed retail flow is internalized off-exchange, the probability of an order on the lit exchange being informed rises to: \[\alpha_{\text{lit}}^* = \frac{1 - \alpha}{(1 - \alpha) + \alpha(1 - \rho)} > 1 - \alpha.\] The equilibrium competitive half-spread quoted on the lit exchange widens to: \[H_{\text{lit}}' = \alpha_{\text{lit}}^* \delta + c > H_{\text{lit}}.\]

Proof
Under Bertrand competition among liquidity providers, the quoted half-spread must equal expected adverse selection losses plus order handling costs: \(H = \Prob(\text{informed}) \cdot \delta + c\). In the consolidated market, total arrivals are \(N\). Uninformed arrivals are \(\alpha N\) and informed arrivals are \((1 - \alpha) N\). The probability of an order being informed is \((1 - \alpha)N / N = 1 - \alpha\), establishing (1). When internalizers skim off fraction \(\rho\) of retail orders, the volume of retail orders reaching the lit exchange drops to \(\alpha(1 - \rho) N\). Informed orders cannot access internalizers and are routed in full to the lit exchange, contributing \((1 - \alpha) N\) orders. Total lit volume is \((1 - \alpha) N + \alpha(1 - \rho) N\). By Bayes' rule, the proportion of informed orders on the lit exchange is: \[\alpha_{\text{lit}}^* = \frac{(1 - \alpha)N}{(1 - \alpha)N + \alpha(1 - \rho)N} = \frac{1 - \alpha}{(1 - \alpha) + \alpha(1 - \rho)}.\] Because \(\rho > 0\), \(\alpha(1 - \rho) < \alpha\), so the denominator is strictly smaller than 1, implying \(\alpha_{\text{lit}}^* > 1 - \alpha\). Substituting \(\alpha_{\text{lit}}^*\) into the zero-profit condition yields \(H_{\text{lit}}' = \alpha_{\text{lit}}^* \delta + c > H_{\text{lit}}\), proving the theorem.

Lab 1 · PFOF cream-skimming simulator

Simulating an order stream (N=10,000 orders) with 65% retail flow, informed impact \(\delta = \$0.20\), and handling cost \(c = \$0.02\) across internalization rates \(\rho \in [0.0, 0.90]\) will demonstrate that: (1) internalizing retail flow increases the informed share on the lit exchange from 35.0% to 83.4%; (2) the quoted lit spread more than doubles, expanding from $0.1802 to $0.3736 (+107.3% widening).

"""Lab MS2-L04-1: Dark pool and PFOF cream-skimming simulator.

Simulates order segmentation between retail (uninformed) and institutional (informed)
traders, demonstrating how off-exchange internalization widens lit bid-ask spreads.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random


def simulate_market_segmentation(
    n_orders: int,
    retail_share: float,
    internalize_rate: float,
    informed_impact: float,
    order_processing_cost: float,
    rng: random.Random,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate order flow routing with off-exchange retail internalization.

    Returns:
        dict of metrics for the lit exchange and overall market.
    """
    lit_informed = 0
    lit_uninformed = 0
    internalized_retail = 0

    for _ in range(n_orders):
        is_retail = rng.random() < retail_share
        if is_retail:
            if rng.random() < internalize_rate:
                # Internalized by wholesaler off-exchange
                internalized_retail += 1
            else:
                # Spills over to lit exchange
                lit_uninformed += 1
        else:
            # Informed / toxic flow must execute on lit exchange
            lit_informed += 1

    total_lit = lit_informed + lit_uninformed
    lit_informed_share = lit_informed / total_lit if total_lit > 0 else 0.0

    # Competitive half-spread on lit exchange under zero-profit condition:
    # H = (P(informed | lit) * informed_impact) + order_processing_cost
    lit_half_spread = lit_informed_share * informed_impact + order_processing_cost

    return {
        "total_orders": float(n_orders),
        "internalized_retail": float(internalized_retail),
        "lit_orders": float(total_lit),
        "lit_informed_share": round(lit_informed_share, 4),
        "lit_half_spread": round(lit_half_spread, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n_orders = 10000
    retail_share = 0.65  # 65% of all orders are uninformed retail
    informed_impact = 0.20  # adverse selection cost of informed trade ($0.20)
    proc_cost = 0.02  # order handling cost ($0.02)

    # Evaluate internalization rate from 0% (consolidated lit market) to 90% (heavy PFOF)
    internalize_rates = [0.0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 0.90]

    print("PFOF Cream-Skimming: Internalization Rate vs Lit Spread (N=10000 orders):")
    print("Internalize % | Lit Orders | Lit Informed Share | Lit Quoted Spread ($) | Spread Widening")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    base_spread = 0.0
    for rho in internalize_rates:
        res = simulate_market_segmentation(
            n_orders,
            retail_share,
            rho,
            informed_impact,
            proc_cost,
            rng,
        )
        quoted_spread = 2.0 * res["lit_half_spread"]
        if rho == 0.0:
            base_spread = quoted_spread
        widening_pct = ((quoted_spread - base_spread) / base_spread) * 100.0 if base_spread > 0 else 0.0

        print(
            f"   {rho * 100.0:5.1f}%     | {int(res['lit_orders']):10d} | "
            f"{res['lit_informed_share']:18.4f} | ${quoted_spread:20.4f} | {widening_pct:14.2f}%"
        )


main()

4Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook models often depict dark pools as frictionless crossing mechanisms that provide win-win execution. In actual practice, off-exchange trading introduces severe structural distortions:

  • Circular Benchmarking (The NBBO Parasite Problem): Dark pools and internalizers do not engage in price discovery; they execute at prices derived from the lit NBBO. Yet as more order flow migrates off-exchange, the lit NBBO is determined by a diminishing, highly toxic fraction of total volume. Off-exchange venues free-ride on the price discovery subsidized by lit limit order placers.
  • Adverse Selection Within Dark Pools: Midpoint crossing networks suffer from conditional fill probability bias. Orders in dark pools fill with high probability when an informed institutional sweep enters the market, and fail to fill when the market is calm. An unfilled dark pool order is eventually forced to cross the spread on a lit exchange after prices have already moved unfavorably.
  • Broker Conflict of Interest: Under PFOF, brokers face an agency conflict between routing to the venue offering the highest rebate versus the venue offering the best execution quality. Empirical audits frequently document routing biases toward wholesalers paying higher per-share PFOF fees.

5Misconceptions

  1. Retail investors lose money from payment for order flow because internalizers execute their orders at inferior prices.

    Why it is wrong: Internalizers are legally required under FINRA and SEC rules to provide price improvement relative to the displayed NBBO. Retail investors execute at prices equal to or better than the prevailing public lit quotes. The true economic cost is market-wide: off-exchange internalization widens the baseline lit NBBO against which price improvement is benchmarked.

  2. Dark pools hide transaction prices from the public tape.

    Why it is wrong: Dark pools hide pre-trade quotations (bids and asks), but post-trade reporting is mandatory. Under FINRA regulations, all off-exchange trades must be reported to a Trade Reporting Facility (TRF) within 10 seconds (and typically within milliseconds) for public dissemination on the consolidated tape.

  3. Institutions trade in dark pools primarily to avoid paying broker commissions.

    Why it is wrong: Institutional investors use dark pools primarily to minimize market impact. A public limit order for 500,000 shares displayed on a lit book immediately signals institutional buying pressure, causing other traders to step ahead and push prices up. Midpoint dark pools allow institutions to cross blocks without pre-trade information leakage.

6Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 4.1: A stock has an NBBO of Bid $50.00 and Ask $50.06. A retail buy order for 200 shares is internalized by a wholesale market maker at $50.045. Compute: (1) the price improvement per share; (2) total dollar savings delivered to the customer relative to the NBBO; (3) the wholesaler's gross margin relative to the midpoint if fundamental value is at the mid-price.

Solution

The NBBO midpoint is \((50.00 + 50.06) / 2 = \$50.03\). The displayed ask is $50.06.

  1. Price improvement per share: \(\text{Ask} - P_{\text{exec}} = 50.06 - 50.045 = \$0.015 \text{ per share} \quad (1.5 \text{ cents}).\)
  2. Total dollar savings: \(200 \times \$0.015 = \$3.00.\)
  3. Wholesaler gross margin: \(P_{\text{exec}} - \text{Midpoint} = 50.045 - 50.030 = \$0.015 \text{ per share}\), yielding \(200 \times \$0.015 = \$3.00\) gross revenue on 200 shares.

Problem 4.2: In a market with 60% retail orders and 40% informed orders, wholesalers internalize 75% of all retail orders off-exchange. Compute the proportion of orders reaching the lit exchange that are informed.

Solution

Let total orders be normalized to 100. Uninformed retail orders = 60; informed orders = 40. Wholesalers internalize \(75\% \times 60 = 45\) retail orders. The remaining retail orders reaching the lit exchange are \(60 - 45 = 15\). All 40 informed orders reach the lit exchange. Total lit orders = \(15 + 40 = 55\). The proportion of lit orders that are informed is: \[\alpha_{\text{lit}}^* = \frac{40}{55} = \frac{8}{11} \approx 0.7273 \quad (72.73\%).\] Informed share on the lit exchange surges from 40% to 72.73%.

Problem 4.3: An institutional trader places a midpoint buy order in a dark pool for 10,000 shares when the NBBO is $100.00 to $100.08. If the order executes completely at the midpoint, how much cash does the trader save compared to executing against the lit ask?

Solution

The lit ask is $100.08. The midpoint execution price is \((100.00 + 100.08)/2 = \$100.04\). The savings per share is \(100.08 - 100.04 = \$0.04\) (the full half-spread). For 10,000 shares: \[\text{Total Cash Savings} = 10,000 \times \$0.04 = \$400.00.\]

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 4.4: Prove that as the retail internalization rate \(\rho \to 1\), the lit quoted half-spread approaches the maximum adverse selection cost \(\delta + c\), and prove that \(\partial H_{\text{lit}}' / \partial \rho > 0\) for all \(\rho \in [0, 1)\).

Solution

From Theorem 4.1, the lit quoted half-spread is: \[H_{\text{lit}}'(\rho) = \frac{1 - \alpha}{(1 - \alpha) + \alpha(1 - \rho)} \delta + c.\] Taking the limit as \(\rho \to 1\): \[\lim_{\rho \to 1} H_{\text{lit}}'(\rho) = \frac{1 - \alpha}{(1 - \alpha) + \alpha(0)} \delta + c = \frac{1 - \alpha}{1 - \alpha} \delta + c = \delta + c.\] To evaluate the derivative, define \(D(\rho) = (1 - \alpha) + \alpha(1 - \rho)\). Note that \(dD/d\rho = -\alpha < 0\). Using the chain rule: \[\frac{\partial H_{\text{lit}}'}{\partial \rho} = (1 - \alpha)\delta \cdot \left( -\frac{1}{D(\rho)^2} \right) \cdot \left( -\alpha \right) = \frac{\alpha (1 - \alpha) \delta}{[(1 - \alpha) + \alpha(1 - \rho)]^2}.\] Because \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\) and \(\delta > 0\), the numerator is strictly positive. The denominator is a squared positive term. Therefore, \(\partial H_{\text{lit}}' / \partial \rho > 0\) for all \(\rho \in [0, 1)\), proving that the lit spread widens monotonically with the internalization rate.

Problem 4.5: Prove that if an internalizer provides price improvement \(\pi > 0\) per share benchmarked to the lit NBBO, retail investors are strictly worse off in net execution price whenever the cream-skimming spread widening exceeds \(2\pi\).

Solution

In a consolidated market without internalization (\(\rho = 0\)), let the lit ask be \(A_0 = M + H_{\text{lit}}\), where \(M\) is the true mid-price and \(H_{\text{lit}}\) is the consolidated half-spread. A retail buyer pays \(P_0 = A_0 = M + H_{\text{lit}}\). In a segmented market with internalization rate \(\rho > 0\), the lit half-spread widens to \(H_{\text{lit}}' > H_{\text{lit}}\). The new lit ask is \(A' = M + H_{\text{lit}}'\). The internalizer executes the retail buy order at price improved by \(\pi\): \[P_{\text{internalized}} = A' - \pi = M + H_{\text{lit}}' - \pi.\] Compare the internalized execution price against the consolidated market price: \[P_{\text{internalized}} - P_0 = (M + H_{\text{lit}}' - \pi) - (M + H_{\text{lit}}) = (H_{\text{lit}}' - H_{\text{lit}}) - \pi.\] The retail investor is strictly worse off (\(P_{\text{internalized}} > P_0\)) if and only if: \[H_{\text{lit}}' - H_{\text{lit}} > \pi.\] In terms of the full quoted spread \(S = 2H\), the condition is \(\Delta S = 2(H_{\text{lit}}' - H_{\text{lit}}) > 2\pi\). Thus, whenever the cream-skimming externality widens the quoted spread by more than twice the price improvement, retail investors pay a higher absolute execution price despite receiving nominal price improvement.

Problem 4.6: Prove that in a dark pool operating at the NBBO midpoint, conditional fill probability creates an adverse selection cost for resting institutional block orders.

Solution

Let an institutional trader rest a buy order of size \(Q\) at the midpoint \(M_t = (B_t + A_t)/2\). Market events over the execution horizon consist of uninformed crossing matches (arrival rate \(\lambda_U\)) and informed aggressive sweeps (arrival rate \(\lambda_I\)) where informed counterparties possess information that fundamental value is \(V_{\text{true}} < B_t < M_t\). If informed traders route aggressively to dark pools to sweep non-displayed liquidity before hitting lit books, the fill probability conditional on adverse information arriving is \(\Prob(\text{fill} \mid \text{bad news}) \approx 1\), whereas when favorable news arrives (\(V_{\text{true}} > A_t\)), sellers cancel dark pool orders to sell on lit books at higher prices, driving \(\Prob(\text{fill} \mid \text{good news}) \approx 0\). Consequently: \[\E[V_{t+\Delta t} - M_t \mid \text{fill in dark pool}] < 0,\] proving that resting in a dark pool imposes adverse selection due to asymmetric fill conditioning.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 4.7: Using the Lab 1 simulation results, compute the percentage widening in quoted lit spread when the internalization rate increases from 0% to 50% versus from 50% to 90%, and explain why spread widening accelerates at high internalization rates.

Solution

From the Lab 1 output table:

  • At \(\rho = 0.0\): Quoted lit spread = $0.1802.
  • At \(\rho = 0.50\): Quoted lit spread = $0.2424. Widening: \((0.2424 - 0.1802) / 0.1802 = +34.52\%\).
  • At \(\rho = 0.90\): Quoted lit spread = $0.3736. Widening relative to \(\rho = 0.50\): \((0.3736 - 0.2424) / 0.2424 = +54.13\%\).

Widening relative to baseline at 90% is \(+107.33\%\). The spread widening accelerates because the lit informed share \(\alpha_{\text{lit}}^* = (1-\alpha) / [(1-\alpha) + \alpha(1-\rho)]\) is a convex rational function of \(\rho\). As \(\rho \to 1\), the remaining uninformed liquidity cushion \(\alpha(1-\rho)\) approaches zero, causing the informed concentration on the lit book to increase hyperbolically toward 100%.

Problem 4.8: A retail brokerage firm discloses under Rule 606 that it received $120 million in payment for order flow from wholesale market makers during the year. Total customer shares routed were 80 billion shares. Calculate the average PFOF capture in cents per 100 shares and mils per share.

Solution

Total PFOF revenue = $120,000,000. Total shares = 80,000,000,000 shares (800,000,000 round lots of 100 shares).

  1. PFOF per 100 shares: \(\frac{\$120,000,000}{800,000,000 \text{ round lots}} = \$0.15 \text{ per 100 shares} \quad (15 \text{ cents per 100 shares}).\)
  2. PFOF per share: \(\frac{\$120,000,000}{80,000,000,000 \text{ shares}} = \$0.0015 \text{ per share} = 1.5 \text{ mils per share}.\)

Problem 4.9: An empirical study evaluates 500,000 dark pool midpoint orders. Orders that filled achieved an average 1-minute post-trade price drift of \(-0.8\) bps against the trader. Orders that failed to fill after 10 seconds were routed to lit exchanges with an average slippage of \(+2.4\) bps. Calculate the net implementation shortfall of the dark-first routing strategy if the dark pool fill rate was 30%.

Solution

The total implementation shortfall is the probability-weighted average of filled and unfilled outcomes: \[\text{Expected Shortfall} = P(\text{fill}) \cdot \text{Cost}_{\text{filled}} + P(\text{unfilled}) \cdot \text{Cost}_{\text{unfilled}}.\] For filled orders (30% probability), execution occurred at midpoint with adverse selection drift of 0.8 bps. For unfilled orders (70% probability), the order failed to execute in the dark pool and was routed to the lit exchange, incurring 2.4 bps of slippage. \[\text{Expected Shortfall} = 0.30 \times (0.8 \text{ bps}) + 0.70 \times (2.4 \text{ bps}) = 0.24 + 1.68 = 1.92 \text{ basis points.}\] The overall implementation shortfall is 1.92 basis points.

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 4.10: Formulate an empirical structural model to estimate the cream-skimming externality using the SEC's 2022 proposed Order Competition Rule, which would mandate that retail orders be routed to fair-competition auctions before internalization.

Solution

Under the SEC's proposed Order Competition Rule, retail orders cannot be internalized bilateral-by-wholesaler unless they are first exposed in an open, competitive auction for at least 100 milliseconds. To model this counterfactual, specify an econometric regression across stock-day panels: \[S_{i, t} = \gamma_i + \delta_t + \beta_1 \text{OffExchangeShare}_{i, t} + \beta_2 \text{Volatility}_{i, t} + \beta_3 \log(\text{Volume}_{i, t}) + \epsilon_{i, t},\] where \(S_{i, t}\) is the quoted NBBO spread. Under cream-skimming theory, \(\beta_1 > 0\). Using exogenous regulatory pilot programs (such as the 2016 SEC Tick Size Pilot Trade-at Prohibition) as instrumental variables for off-exchange share isolates the causal effect of order segmentation, estimating the exact basis-point spread contraction expected if retail flow were returned to public auctions.

Problem 4.11: Analyze why the United Kingdom and the European Union banned payment for order flow under MiFID II revisions, whereas the US SEC opted for enhanced Rule 605/606 disclosures. Evaluate the welfare trade-offs of an outright ban on PFOF.

Solution

European regulators banned PFOF on the grounds of irreducible agency conflict: a broker receiving monetary incentives from wholesalers cannot fulfill its legal fiduciary duty of best execution. In contrast, US regulators historically permitted PFOF because retail brokerages used PFOF revenue to cross-subsidize zero-commission trading and eliminate account minimums, vastly democratizing equity market access. An outright ban on PFOF in the US would force retail brokers to reinstate per-trade commissions ($5 to $10 per trade) or increase margin borrow rates, but would eliminate the cream-skimming externality, consolidating liquidity on lit exchanges and narrowing quoted spreads for mutual funds, pensions, and institutional investors.

Problem 4.12: How do retail internalizers manage inventory risk when purchasing one-sided retail flow during meme-stock trading frenzies (such as GameStop in January 2021)?

Solution

During extreme speculative retail frenzies, retail order flow loses its statistical independence and uncoordinated nature; millions of retail traders simultaneously buy call options and shares, creating massive directional order imbalances. Internalizers cannot passively absorb this flow without incurring catastrophic short inventory risk. To hedge, wholesalers aggressively purchase shares on lit exchanges and futures markets, transmitting retail buying pressure directly to public markets. When clearinghouse deposit requirements surge due to settlement volatility (as occurred with DTCC capital calls in January 2021), brokers and internalizers are forced to restrict trading, revealing the systemic fragility of non-displayed internalization during one-sided market stress.

    Options

    Retail flow is uncoordinated and largely uninformed, presenting minimal adverse selection risk and allowing internalizers to earn consistent spread profits.

  3. If the NBBO is Bid $100.02 and Ask $100.04, at what price does a midpoint dark pool execute?

    The midpoint price is \((100.02 + 100.04) / 2 = 100.03\).

  4. What is the cream-skimming externality caused by payment for order flow?

    Options

    As proved in Theorem 4.1, removing uninformed retail orders increases the informed proportion on lit venues, forcing lit market makers to widen quoted spreads.

  5. If an internalizer buys from a retail seller at $50.015 when the National Best Bid is $50.00, how many dollars of price improvement per share did the retail customer receive?

    Price improvement is \(50.015 - 50.000 = 0.015\) dollars per share (1.5 cents).

  6. What reporting requirement applies to off-exchange trades executed in dark pools or internalizers?

    Options

    Under FINRA rules, off-exchange trades must be submitted to a TRF within seconds for tape publication.

  7. Why do institutional block traders face adverse selection when resting orders in midpoint dark pools?

    Options

    Fills in dark pools occur disproportionately when informed traders sweep dark liquidity, leaving orders unfilled when the market moves favorably.

    Options
