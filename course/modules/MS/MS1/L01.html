Bid–Ask Bounce and Roll's Spread Estimator

  • Uses: random variable, expectation, variance
  • Introduces: Roll's spread estimator
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or finite check
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Transaction prices oscillate between bid and ask quotes even when the underlying asset value does not change. This bounce produces negative first-order serial correlation in price differences. Richard Roll showed that when the market is informationally efficient and trading costs are constant, this negative autocovariance identifies the effective bid-ask spread without requiring quote data. This lesson derives Roll's estimator, establishes the conditions under which it holds, and proves how adverse selection breaks the estimator.

1The bid–ask bounce

Definition 1.1 (bid–ask spread and bounce)

In a quote-driven or order-driven market, let \(p_t\) denote the transaction price at trade index \(t \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}\). The bid–ask spread is the difference between the prevailing ask quote and bid quote. The alternation of trades between buy orders executed at or near the ask and sell orders executed at or near the bid produces negative serial correlation in consecutive price changes \(\Delta p_t = p_t - p_{t-1}\), a phenomenon known as bid–ask bounce.

Assumption A1 (efficient price random walk)

The unobserved efficient price \(m_t\) follows a random walk with zero-mean, serially independent innovations: \[ m_t = m_{t-1} + \eta_t, \quad \E[\eta_t] = 0, \quad \Var(\eta_t) = \sigma^2, \quad \Cov(\eta_t, \eta_s) = 0 \text{ for } t \ne s. \] Here \(\eta_t\) reflects public fundamental news arriving between trades \(t-1\) and \(t\).

Assumption A2 (order flow independence and symmetry)

Let \(q_t \in \{-1, +1\}\) denote the signed trade direction, with \(q_t = +1\) for buyer-initiated trades and \(q_t = -1\) for seller-initiated trades. The sequence \(\{q_t\}\) is independent and identically distributed with: \[ \Prob(q_t = +1) = \Prob(q_t = -1) = \frac{1}{2}. \] Consequently \(\E[q_t] = 0\), \(\Var(q_t) = \E[q_t^2] = 1\), and \(\Cov(q_t, q_s) = 0\) for \(t \ne s\).

Assumption A3 (constant half-spread)

The dealer incurs a constant order-processing cost (or half-spread) \(c > 0\). The transaction price is: \[ p_t = m_t + c\, q_t. \] The full quoted spread is \(S = 2c\).

Assumption A4 (no price impact / information symmetry)

The trade signs \(\{q_t\}\) and fundamental innovations \(\{\eta_s\}\) are mutually independent for all \(t\) and \(s\): \(\Cov(q_t, \eta_s) = 0\). Trades carry zero private information, so trades exert zero permanent price impact on \(m_t\).

2Roll's autocovariance theorem

Theorem 1.2 (Roll's serial covariance theorem)

Assume A1, A2, A3, and A4. Let \(\Delta p_t = p_t - p_{t-1}\) be the transaction price change. Then: \[ \E[\Delta p_t] = 0, \] \[ \Var(\Delta p_t) = \sigma^2 + 2c^2, \] \[ \Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -c^2 \le 0, \] and for all lags \(k \ge 2\), \[ \Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-k}) = 0. \] Consequently, Roll's spread estimator identifies the full spread \(S = 2c\) from transaction prices alone via: \[ S = 2 \sqrt{-\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})}. \]

Proof

From A1 and A3, the price change satisfies: \[ \Delta p_t = (m_t + c\, q_t) - (m_{t-1} + c\, q_{t-1}) = \eta_t + c\, q_t - c\, q_{t-1}. \] Taking expectations using A1 (\(\E[\eta_t] = 0\)) and A2 (\(\E[q_t] = 0\)): \[ \E[\Delta p_t] = \E[\eta_t] + c\, \E[q_t] - c\, \E[q_{t-1}] = 0. \] For the variance, using mutual independence from A1, A2, and A4: \[ \begin{aligned} \Var(\Delta p_t) &= \Var(\eta_t) + c^2\, \Var(q_t) + c^2\, \Var(q_{t-1}) \\ &= \sigma^2 + c^2 (1) + c^2 (1) = \sigma^2 + 2c^2. \end{aligned} \] Now evaluate the lag-1 covariance: \[ \Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = \E[(\eta_t + c\, q_t - c\, q_{t-1})(\eta_{t-1} + c\, q_{t-1} - c\, q_{t-2})]. \] Expanding the product yields nine terms. Under A1, A2, and A4, all cross-terms between different time indices or between \(\eta\) and \(q\) have zero expectation, except \(\E[(-c\, q_{t-1})(c\, q_{t-1})]\): \[ \Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -c^2\, \E[q_{t-1}^2] = -c^2 (1) = -c^2. \] Since \(c > 0\), \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -c^2 < 0\). For any lag \(k \ge 2\), \(\Delta p_t\) involves indices \(\{t, t-1\}\) while \(\Delta p_{t-k}\) involves \(\{t-k, t-k-1\}\). Because these index sets are disjoint, \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-k}) = 0\). Solving \(-c^2 = \Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})\) yields \(c = \sqrt{-\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})}\), so \(S = 2c = 2\sqrt{-\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})}\).

Example 1.3 (evaluating Roll's spread from sample covariance)

Suppose transaction prices are observed with true half-spread \(c = 0.25\) dollars (so full spread \(2c = 0.50\) dollars). Under A1-A4, the population lag-1 covariance is \(-c^2 = -(0.25)^2 = -0.0625\). Roll's estimator yields \(S = 2\sqrt{-(-0.0625)} = 2 \cdot 0.25 = 0.50\) dollars. In finite samples of size \(n = 2000\), sampling noise produces a sample covariance close to \(-0.0625\), as verified in Lab 1.

Remark 1.4 (title citation of Roll 1984)

Richard Roll introduced this serial covariance estimator in "A Simple Implicit Measure of the Effective Bid-Ask Spread in an Efficient Market". Roll showed that price discreteness and bounce create negative serial dependence that can be inverted to estimate transaction costs when quote databases are unavailable.

3Simulation and empirical computation

Lab 1 · Roll spread estimation on a simulated trade path

SIMULATED: 2000 trades generated with true half-spread c = 0.25 (full spread 0.50) and noise sigma = 0.05. Expected: sample lag-1 autocovariance is negative, and the Roll estimator recovers the full spread close to 0.50.

"""Lab MS1-L01-1: Roll spread estimator on a simulated trade-price path.

SIMULATED: generated trade prices with bid-ask bounce and random walk efficient price.
Demonstrates that negative serial covariance in transaction price changes recovers
the underlying bid-ask spread.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def simulate_roll_path(
    n_trades: int, half_spread: float, sigma: float, seed: int = 42
) -> tuple[list[float], list[int]]:
    """Simulate efficient price and transaction prices under bid-ask bounce."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    m = 100.0
    prices: list[float] = []
    signs: list[int] = []
    for _ in range(n_trades):
        m += rng.gauss(0.0, sigma)
        q = rng.choice([-1, 1])
        prices.append(m + half_spread * q)
        signs.append(q)
    return prices, signs


def compute_roll_spread(prices: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float, str]:
    """Compute sample lag-1 covariance of price differences and the Roll spread."""
    n = len(prices)
    if n < 3:
        raise ValueError("At least three prices required.")
    d = [prices[i] - prices[i - 1] for i in range(1, n)]
    mean_d = sum(d) / len(d)
    cov1 = sum((d[i] - mean_d) * (d[i - 1] - mean_d) for i in range(1, len(d))) / (len(d) - 1)
    if cov1 < 0:
        spread = 2.0 * math.sqrt(-cov1)
        sign_status = "negative (valid)"
    else:
        spread = float("nan")
        sign_status = "non-negative (undefined)"
    return cov1, spread, sign_status


def main() -> None:
    n_trades = 2000
    true_c = 0.25
    true_spread = 2.0 * true_c
    sigma = 0.05
    prices, _ = simulate_roll_path(n_trades, true_c, sigma, seed=42)
    cov1, est_spread, sign_status = compute_roll_spread(prices)

    print(f"Number of trades: {n_trades}")
    print(f"True half-spread c: {true_c:.4f}")
    print(f"True full spread 2c: {true_spread:.4f}")
    print(f"Sample lag-1 covariance: {cov1:.6f}")
    print(f"Covariance sign check: {sign_status}")
    print(f"Roll spread estimate: {est_spread:.4f}")
    print(f"Estimation error: {abs(est_spread - true_spread):.4f}")


main()
Number of trades: 2000
True half-spread c: 0.2500
True full spread 2c: 0.5000
Sample lag-1 covariance: -0.064485
Covariance sign check: negative (valid)
Roll spread estimate: 0.5079
Estimation error: 0.0079

The sample lag-1 autocovariance is strictly negative (-0.064485), confirming bid-ask bounce. The Roll spread estimate of 0.5079 recovers the true spread of 0.5000 with an estimation error of 0.0079 dollars.

4Where the canon breaks

Limit 1.1 (positive autocovariance in sample data)

In empirical trade data, sample lag-1 autocovariance is frequently positive (\(\widehat{\Cov} > 0\)), rendering \(\sqrt{-\widehat{\Cov}}\) undefined in the real numbers. Positive sample covariance occurs because of trending order flow, serially correlated trade signs (\(\Cov(q_t, q_{t-1}) > 0\)), time-varying spreads, or finite-sample noise. Standard practice returns NaN when \(\widehat{\Cov} \ge 0\).

Limit 1.2 (breakdown under adverse selection)

Suppose trades carry private information, so trade direction permanently shifts the efficient price by \(\theta q_t\) with \(\theta > 0\): \[ m_t = m_{t-1} + \theta\, q_t + \eta_t. \] Then price changes become \(\Delta p_t = (\theta + c) q_t - c\, q_{t-1} + \eta_t\). The lag-1 covariance is: \[ \Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = \E[(-c\, q_{t-1})((\theta + c) q_{t-1})] = -c(\theta + c). \] Roll's estimator computes \(2\sqrt{c(\theta + c)}\). The true effective spread is \(2(\theta + c)\). By the arithmetic mean-geometric mean inequality, \(\sqrt{c(\theta + c)} < \theta + c\) whenever \(\theta > 0\). Thus, adverse selection causes Roll's estimator to strictly underestimate the true effective spread.

5Misconceptions

  • Wrong: Roll's estimator measures the quoted bid–ask spread posted on the limit order book.

    Why it is wrong: Roll's estimator measures the effective spread realized in transaction prices. When trades execute inside the quoted spread (e.g., through midpoint crosses, dark pool fills, or price improvement), the effective spread is strictly smaller than the quoted spread.

  • Wrong: A positive sample autocovariance in price changes proves that market makers earn negative profits.

    Why it is wrong: Positive sample autocovariance simply means that momentum, serially correlated order flow, or trending price drift dominates high-frequency bounce over the sample window. It reflects market dynamics and order arrival clustering, not dealer insolvency.

  • Wrong: Roll's estimator is valid regardless of whether trades have permanent price impact.

    Why it is wrong: Theorem 1.2 requires Assumption A4 (no permanent price impact). As shown in Limit 1.2, when trades have price impact \(\theta > 0\), Roll's estimator yields \(2\sqrt{c(c+\theta)}\), strictly understating the true spread \(2(c+\theta)\).

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Roll spread from given covariance

A stock has estimated lag-1 autocovariance of price changes equal to \(\widehat{\Cov}(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -0.09\) dollars squared. Compute Roll's full spread estimate.

Solution

Apply Theorem 1.2: \(S = 2 \sqrt{-\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})} = 2 \sqrt{-(-0.09)} = 2 \sqrt{0.09} = 2 \times 0.30 = 0.60\) dollars.

M2 · Total variance decomposition

Under assumptions A1-A4, price change variance is \(\Var(\Delta p_t) = 0.20\) and lag-1 covariance is \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -0.04\). Find the order-processing half-spread \(c\) and fundamental innovation variance \(\sigma^2\).

Solution

From Theorem 1.2, \(-c^2 = -0.04 \implies c = 0.20\) dollars. Then \(\Var(\Delta p_t) = \sigma^2 + 2c^2 \implies 0.20 = \sigma^2 + 2(0.20)^2 = \sigma^2 + 0.08 \implies \sigma^2 = 0.12\).

M3 · Roll spread under trade impact

Suppose \(c = 0.10\) and \(\theta = 0.15\). Compute the population lag-1 covariance \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})\), the Roll estimate \(S_{\text{Roll}}\), and the true effective spread \(S_{\text{true}}\).

Solution

Under adverse selection, \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -c(\theta + c) = -0.10(0.15 + 0.10) = -0.025\). The Roll spread is \(S_{\text{Roll}} = 2\sqrt{0.025} \approx 0.3162\) dollars. The true effective spread is \(S_{\text{true}} = 2(\theta + c) = 2(0.25) = 0.50\) dollars. Roll underestimates true spread by approximately 36.8%.

Proof

P1 · Lag-2 serial covariance vanishes

Prove that under A1-A4, \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-2}) = 0\).

Solution

Write \(\Delta p_t = \eta_t + c\, q_t - c\, q_{t-1}\) and \(\Delta p_{t-2} = \eta_{t-2} + c\, q_{t-2} - c\, q_{t-3}\). Computing \(\E[\Delta p_t \Delta p_{t-2}]\), every cross-term involves products of independent variables across disjoint time sets \(\{t, t-1\}\) and \(\{t-2, t-3\}\). Because \(\E[\eta_s] = 0\) and \(\E[q_s] = 0\) with zero serial correlation, every term has expectation zero. Therefore \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-2}) = 0\).

P2 · Bound on first-order autocorrelation

Show that the first-order autocorrelation of price changes \(\rho_1 = \Corr(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})\) is bounded in \([-0.5, 0]\).

Solution

Under A1-A4, \(\rho_1 = \frac{\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})}{\Var(\Delta p_t)} = \frac{-c^2}{\sigma^2 + 2c^2}\). Since \(c^2 > 0\) and \(\sigma^2 \ge 0\), the numerator is non-positive, so \(\rho_1 \le 0\). As \(\sigma^2 \to 0\) (no fundamental volatility), \(\rho_1 \to \frac{-c^2}{2c^2} = -0.5\). As \(\sigma^2 \to \infty\), \(\rho_1 \to 0\). Hence \(\rho_1 \in [-0.5, 0]\).

P3 · Serially correlated trade signs

Suppose trade signs follow an AR(1) process with \(\E[q_t q_{t-1}] = \phi \in (-1, 1)\) while A1, A3, and A4 hold. Derive \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})\) as a function of \(c\) and \(\phi\).

Solution

Price change is \(\Delta p_t = \eta_t + c(q_t - q_{t-1})\). Then \(\Delta p_t \Delta p_{t-1} = [\eta_t + c(q_t - q_{t-1})][\eta_{t-1} + c(q_{t-1} - q_{t-2})]\). Taking expectations: \(\E[\Delta p_t \Delta p_{t-1}] = c^2 \E[(q_t - q_{t-1})(q_{t-1} - q_{t-2})] = c^2 [\E[q_t q_{t-1}] - \E[q_t q_{t-2}] - \E[q_{t-1}^2] + \E[q_{t-1} q_{t-2}]] = c^2 [\phi - \phi^2 - 1 + \phi] = -c^2(1 - 2\phi + \phi^2) = -c^2 (1 - \phi)^2\).

Empirical

E1 · Interpreting Lab 1 covariance sign

In Lab 1, what sample covariance was printed and what sign check resulted? What would positive sample covariance indicate?

Solution

Lab 1 printed a sample lag-1 covariance of \(-0.064485\), with sign status "negative (valid)". If the sample covariance were positive, the square root would be undefined in real numbers, indicating that order autocorrelation, drift, or noise dominated the bounce over that sample.

E2 · Percentage estimation error

Using the Lab 1 true spread of 0.5000 and estimated spread of 0.5079, compute the percentage estimation error relative to the true spread.

Solution

Percentage estimation error is \(\frac{|0.5079 - 0.5000|}{0.5000} \times 100\% = \frac{0.0079}{0.5000} \times 100\% = 1.58\%\). The estimator achieves within 1.6% accuracy on 2000 trades.

E3 · Effect of sample size on Roll estimator standard error

Under A1-A4, how does the standard error of the sample autocovariance \(\widehat{\Cov}\) scale with trade count \(n\)?

Solution

By standard time-series asymptotics for stationary ergodic processes with finite fourth moments, the sample autocovariance estimator \(\widehat{\Cov}\) has asymptotic variance scaling as \(O(1/n)\). Thus its standard error scales as \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\). Quadrupling trades from 2000 to 8000 halves standard error.

Research

R1 · Serial correlation in trade sizes

Suppose trades have varying sizes \(v_t > 0\) such that \(p_t = m_t + c\, q_t v_t\). How does size clustering affect the covariance \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})\)?

Solution

The price change becomes \(\Delta p_t = \eta_t + c(q_t v_t - q_{t-1} v_{t-1})\). If volume \(v_t\) is independent of \(q_t\) and serially independent, \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -c^2 \E[q_{t-1}^2 v_{t-1}^2] = -c^2 \E[v^2]\). However, if volume is persistent (\(\Cov(v_t, v_{t-1}) > 0\)), cross-terms between consecutive volume draws alter higher-order price dynamics.

R2 · Microstructure noise in high-frequency returns

Explain why realized variance computed by summing squared returns \(\sum (\Delta p_t)^2\) explodes as sampling frequency increases toward tick level under bid-ask bounce.

Solution

From Theorem 1.2, \(\Var(\Delta p_t) = \sigma^2 \Delta t + 2c^2\). For \(n\) high-frequency intervals over a day, the sum of squared returns has expectation \(\sum_{t=1}^n \E[(\Delta p_t)^2] = \sigma^2_{\text{day}} + 2n c^2\). As \(n \to \infty\), the term \(2n c^2 \to \infty\), so microstructure noise completely overwhelms the fundamental integrated variance.

R3 · Discrete price rounding

In real markets, prices are constrained to a discrete tick grid of size \(\Delta\). Explain how tick rounding alters Roll's autocovariance.

Solution

Rounding transaction prices to the nearest tick grid introduces an additional non-linear error \(\xi_t = p_t - p_t^{\ast} \in [-\Delta/2, \Delta/2]\). If the true half-spread \(c\) is smaller than \(\Delta/2\), the observed price changes will frequently be zero or jump by full ticks, causing severe truncation bias in the sample covariance estimator.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. If the lag-1 autocovariance of price changes is \(-0.04\), what is Roll's full spread estimate, in dollars?

    \(S = 2\sqrt{-(-0.04)} = 2\sqrt{0.04} = 2 \times 0.20 = 0.40\) dollars.

  2. Under Assumptions A1 through A4, what is the theoretical lag-2 autocovariance \(\Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-2})\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 1.2, \(\Delta p_t\) and \(\Delta p_{t-2}\) share no common time indices, so their covariance is zero.

  3. What is the minimum possible theoretical first-order autocorrelation of price changes \(\rho_1\) under Roll's model as fundamental variance \(\sigma^2 \to 0\)?

    \(\rho_1 = -c^2 / (\sigma^2 + 2c^2)\). When \(\sigma^2 = 0\), \(\rho_1 = -c^2 / (2c^2) = -0.50\).

  4. What happens to Roll's estimator if the sample lag-1 autocovariance is strictly positive?

    Options

    When \(\Cov > 0\), \(-\Cov < 0\), so \(\sqrt{-\Cov}\) is undefined over real numbers; the standard convention returns NaN.

  5. If the full spread is \(2c = 0.20\) dollars, what is the order-processing half-spread \(c\), in dollars?

    \(c = S / 2 = 0.20 / 2 = 0.10\) dollars.

  6. When trades have permanent price impact \(\theta > 0\), how does Roll's spread estimate compare to the true spread?

    Options

    Under adverse selection, Roll estimates \(2\sqrt{c(c+\theta)} < 2(c+\theta)\) by AM-GM, strictly underestimating the true spread.

  7. Which assumption of Roll's model states that order signs \(q_t\) are independent of fundamental price shocks \(\eta_s\)?

    Options

    Assumption A4 specifies no price impact and mutual independence between order signs and public news innovations.

  8. If the true half-spread is \(c = 0.25\), what is the true full bid-ask spread \(S = 2c\)?

    \(S = 2 \times 0.25 = 0.50\).
