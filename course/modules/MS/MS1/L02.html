Glosten–Milgrom Quotes Under Asymmetric Information

  • Uses: adverse selection, Bayesian inference, conditional expectation, expectation, random variable
  • Introduces: Glosten–Milgrom model
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or finite check

Why is there a bid-ask spread even when market makers are competitive, risk-neutral, and face zero order-processing costs? Lawrence Glosten and Paul Milgrom solved this puzzle by showing that order flow conveys information. When an informed trader buys, the stock is more likely to be valuable; when an informed trader sells, it is more likely to be overvalued. To avoid systematic losses to informed traders, a market maker must quote an ask above the expected value and a bid below it. This lesson derives this pure adverse-selection spread in closed form.

1The sequential trade environment

Definition 2.1 (asset value and prior)

A risky asset has a true liquidation value \(V\) taking one of two discrete values: \[ V \in \{v_L, v_H\}, \quad \text{with } 0 < v_L < v_H. \] At time \(t=0\), the public prior probability of the high state is: \[ \pi_0 = \Prob(V = v_H) \in (0, 1), \quad \Prob(V = v_L) = 1 - \pi_0. \] The prior expectation of the asset value is \(\E[V] = \pi_0 v_H + (1 - \pi_0) v_L = v_L + \pi_0 (v_H - v_L)\).

Definition 2.2 (trader types and order arrival)

At each discrete trading round \(t = 1, 2, \ldots\), a single trader is drawn at random from a population consisting of two groups:

  • Informed traders: fraction \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\) of the population. They observe the true value \(V\) with certainty before submitting an order. If \(V = v_H\), they submit a buy order (\(q_t = +1\)). If \(V = v_L\), they submit a sell order (\(q_t = -1\)).
  • Uninformed (noise) traders: fraction \(1 - \alpha\) of the population. They trade for exogenous liquidity reasons unrelated to \(V\). They submit a buy (\(q_t = +1\)) with probability \(\frac{1}{2}\) and a sell (\(q_t = -1\)) with probability \(\frac{1}{2}\), independent of \(V\) and all other past trades.

Assumption A1 (competitive, risk-neutral dealer)

Quotes are posted by a risk-neutral market maker who faces Bertrand competition from potential entrants. Consequently, the market maker earns zero expected economic profit on every trade.

Assumption A2 (zero order processing and inventory costs)

The market maker incurs zero order-handling fees (\(c = 0\)) and holds zero inventory penalties.

2Bayesian quote updating and equilibrium

Proposition 2.3 (Glosten–Milgrom quote formulas)

Under A1 and A2, let \(\pi_{t-1} = \Prob(V = v_H \mid \mathcal{H}_{t-1})\) denote the public prior at the start of trade round \(t\). The ask quote \(A_t\) and bid quote \(B_t\) in the Glosten–Milgrom model satisfy: \[ A_t = \E[V \mid q_t = +1, \mathcal{H}_{t-1}] = v_L + \pi_t^+\, (v_H - v_L), \] \[ B_t = \E[V \mid q_t = -1, \mathcal{H}_{t-1}] = v_L + \pi_t^-\, (v_H - v_L), \] where the updated posterior probabilities under Bayesian inference are: \[ \pi_t^+ = \frac{\pi_{t-1} (1 + \alpha)}{1 + \alpha (2\pi_{t-1} - 1)}, \] \[ \pi_t^- = \frac{\pi_{t-1} (1 - \alpha)}{1 - \alpha (2\pi_{t-1} - 1)}. \] The resulting bid–ask spread is strictly positive: \[ S_t = A_t - B_t = (v_H - v_L) (\pi_t^+ - \pi_t^-) = \frac{2\alpha\, \pi_{t-1}(1 - \pi_{t-1})\, (v_H - v_L)}{[1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)][1 - \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)]} > 0. \]

Proof

Zero expected profit under Bertrand competition (A1) implies that the ask quote must equal the expected value of the asset conditional on a buyer-initiated trade, and the bid quote must equal the expected value conditional on a seller-initiated trade: \[ A_t = \E[V \mid q_t = +1, \mathcal{H}_{t-1}], \quad B_t = \E[V \mid q_t = -1, \mathcal{H}_{t-1}]. \] We evaluate the probability of a buy order conditional on each true state: \[ \Prob(q_t = +1 \mid V = v_H) = \alpha \cdot 1 + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1 + \alpha}{2}, \] \[ \Prob(q_t = +1 \mid V = v_L) = \alpha \cdot 0 + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1 - \alpha}{2}. \] The unconditional probability of a buy given prior \(\pi_{t-1}\) is: \[ \Prob(q_t = +1) = \pi_{t-1} \frac{1+\alpha}{2} + (1 - \pi_{t-1})\frac{1-\alpha}{2} = \frac{1 - \alpha + 2\alpha \pi_{t-1}}{2} = \frac{1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)}{2}. \] Applying Bayes' rule for the posterior \(\pi_t^+ = \Prob(V = v_H \mid q_t = +1)\): \[ \pi_t^+ = \frac{\Prob(V = v_H) \Prob(q_t = +1 \mid V = v_H)}{\Prob(q_t = +1)} = \frac{\pi_{t-1} \frac{1+\alpha}{2}}{\frac{1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)}{2}} = \frac{\pi_{t-1} (1 + \alpha)}{1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)}. \] Similarly, for a sell order \(q_t = -1\): \[ \Prob(q_t = -1 \mid V = v_H) = \frac{1 - \alpha}{2}, \quad \Prob(q_t = -1 \mid V = v_L) = \frac{1 + \alpha}{2}, \] \[ \Prob(q_t = -1) = \frac{1 - \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)}{2}, \] yielding the sell posterior: \[ \pi_t^- = \frac{\pi_{t-1} (1 - \alpha)}{1 - \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)}. \] Since \(1+\alpha > 1-\alpha + 2\alpha\pi_{t-1}\), we have \(\pi_t^+ > \pi_{t-1} > \pi_t^-\). The spread is \(S_t = (v_H - v_L)(\pi_t^+ - \pi_t^-)\). Subtracting the fractions: \[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t^+ - \pi_t^- &= \frac{\pi_{t-1}(1+\alpha)[1 - \alpha(2\pi_{t-1}-1)] - \pi_{t-1}(1-\alpha)[1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1}-1)]}{[1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1}-1)][1 - \alpha(2\pi_{t-1}-1)]} \\ &= \frac{2\alpha\, \pi_{t-1}(1 - \pi_{t-1})}{[1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1}-1)][1 - \alpha(2\pi_{t-1}-1)]}. \end{aligned} \] Because \(\alpha > 0\), \(\pi_{t-1} \in (0, 1)\), and \(v_H > v_L\), the numerator is strictly positive, proving \(S_t > 0\).

Proposition 2.4 (law of iterated expectations for posteriors)

Under A1 and A2, the expected posterior probability after the arrival of trade \(t\) equals the prior probability: \[ \E[\pi_t \mid \mathcal{H}_{t-1}] = \Prob(q_t = +1)\, \pi_t^+ + \Prob(q_t = -1)\, \pi_t^- = \pi_{t-1}. \] Consequently, quote midpoints and beliefs follow a process whose expected next value equals the current expectation by conditional expectation.

Proof

Using the definitions of \(\Prob(q_t = +1)\) and \(\pi_t^+\): \[ \Prob(q_t = +1)\, \pi_t^+ = \frac{1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)}{2} \cdot \frac{\pi_{t-1}(1+\alpha)}{1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)} = \frac{\pi_{t-1}(1+\alpha)}{2}. \] Similarly for the sell branch: \[ \Prob(q_t = -1)\, \pi_t^- = \frac{1 - \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)}{2} \cdot \frac{\pi_{t-1}(1-\alpha)}{1 - \alpha(2\pi_{t-1} - 1)} = \frac{\pi_{t-1}(1-\alpha)}{2}. \] Summing the two probabilities: \[ \E[\pi_t \mid \mathcal{H}_{t-1}] = \frac{\pi_{t-1}(1+\alpha)}{2} + \frac{\pi_{t-1}(1-\alpha)}{2} = \pi_{t-1} \frac{(1+\alpha) + (1-\alpha)}{2} = \pi_{t-1}. \] This establishes the result algebraically from Bayes' rule.

Example 2.5 (numerical quotes in exact Fraction arithmetic)

Let \(v_L = 10\), \(v_H = 20\), \(\alpha = 1/4\), and \(\pi_0 = 1/2\). Before the first trade: \[ \Prob(q_1 = +1) = \frac{1 + (1/4)(0)}{2} = \frac{1}{2}. \] \[ \pi_1^+ = \frac{(1/2)(5/4)}{1} = \frac{5}{8}, \quad \pi_1^- = \frac{(1/2)(3/4)}{1} = \frac{3}{8}. \] The quotes are \(A_1 = 10 + \frac{5}{8}(10) = 16.25\) and \(B_1 = 10 + \frac{3}{8}(10) = 13.75\). The spread is \(A_1 - B_1 = 2.50\). If trade 1 is a buy, the prior for trade 2 becomes \(\pi_1 = 5/8\). Then \(\pi_2^+ = 25/34 \approx 0.7353\), and \(A_2 = 10 + (25/34)(10) = 295/17 \approx 17.3529\), as verified in Lab 1.

Remark 2.6 (title citation of Glosten & Milgrom 1985)

This sequential trade framework was introduced by Lawrence Glosten and Paul Milgrom in "Bid, ask and transaction prices in a specialist market with heterogeneously informed traders". They proved that asymmetric information alone creates an endogenous spread that narrows as learning accumulates.

3Exact computational implementation

Lab 1 · Glosten–Milgrom sequential Bayesian quote updating

Exact Fraction arithmetic with v_L = 10, v_H = 20, alpha = 1/4, pi_0 = 1/2. Expected: after trades [buy, buy, sell, buy], quotes update monotonically with buys and regress with sells, and expected posterior equals prior at every step.

"""Lab MS1-L02-1: Glosten-Milgrom quotes and Bayesian updating.

Sequential trade model with informed and uninformed traders.
All calculations use exact Fraction arithmetic, demonstrating that adverse
selection generates an endogenous bid-ask spread even with zero order-processing cost.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def glosten_milgrom_step(
    v_l: Fraction, v_h: Fraction, alpha: Fraction, pi: Fraction
) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction, Fraction, Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Compute ask, bid, posteriors, and check the law of iterated expectations."""
    # Probabilities of order signs conditional on state and unconditional
    p_buy = (1 - alpha + 2 * alpha * pi) / 2
    p_sell = (1 + alpha - 2 * alpha * pi) / 2

    # Bayes rule posteriors
    pi_plus = (pi * (1 + alpha)) / (1 - alpha + 2 * alpha * pi)
    pi_minus = (pi * (1 - alpha)) / (1 + alpha - 2 * alpha * pi)

    # Market maker quotes: expected value conditional on incoming trade direction
    ask = v_l + pi_plus * (v_h - v_l)
    bid = v_l + pi_minus * (v_h - v_l)

    # Law of iterated expectations check: E[pi_{t} | pi_{t-1}] == pi_{t-1}
    expected_posterior = p_buy * pi_plus + p_sell * pi_minus
    if expected_posterior != pi:
        raise ValueError("Law of iterated expectations violated in posterior calculation.")

    return ask, bid, pi_plus, pi_minus, ask - bid


def main() -> None:
    v_l = Fraction(10, 1)
    v_h = Fraction(20, 1)
    alpha = Fraction(1, 4)
    pi = Fraction(1, 2)
    trade_sequence = ["buy", "buy", "sell", "buy"]

    print("Initial state:")
    print(f"  v_L = {v_l}, v_H = {v_h}, alpha = {alpha}, initial prior pi_0 = {pi}")
    print("Sequential quote updating:")

    for step, trade in enumerate(trade_sequence, start=1):
        ask, bid, pi_plus, pi_minus, spread = glosten_milgrom_step(v_l, v_h, alpha, pi)
        print(
            f"Step {step} before trade ({trade}): "
            f"Ask={ask} ({float(ask):.4f}), Bid={bid} ({float(bid):.4f}), Spread={spread} ({float(spread):.4f})"
        )
        pi = pi_plus if trade == "buy" else pi_minus

    print(f"Final posterior probability pi_{len(trade_sequence)}: {pi} ({float(pi):.4f})")
    term_plus = (pi * (1 + alpha)) / (1 - alpha + 2 * alpha * pi)
    term_minus = (pi * (1 - alpha)) / (1 + alpha - 2 * alpha * pi)
    final_spread = (v_h - v_l) * (term_plus - term_minus)
    print(f"Adverse-selection spread at final state: {final_spread} ({float(final_spread):.4f})")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

Limit 2.1 (binary state simplification)

The two-state assumption \(V \in \{v_L, v_H\}\) simplifies Bayesian updating to a scalar probability \(\pi_t\). In real markets, asset values follow continuous distributions with non-linear information structures. When informed traders possess noisy or continuous signals, order size becomes an endogenous choice, leading to the multi-quantity analysis developed in subsequent modules.

Limit 2.2 (absence of inventory and dealer risk aversion)

The Glosten–Milgrom model assumes the dealer is risk-neutral and incurs zero inventory holding cost. In practice, dealers accumulate inventory imbalances that expose them to overnight return volatility, requiring quote skews independent of information updates (as studied in Lesson 3).

5Misconceptions

  • Wrong: The bid–ask spread in Glosten–Milgrom reflects dealer monopoly power or profit margins.

    Why it is wrong: By Assumption A1, the market maker faces perfect Bertrand competition and earns exactly zero expected economic profit. The spread compensates the dealer for losses suffered against informed traders by extracting surplus from uninformed noise traders.

  • Wrong: A buy order always permanently increases the dealer's quoted spread.

    Why it is wrong: The spread \(S_t \propto \pi_{t-1}(1 - \pi_{t-1})\) is maximized at \(\pi = 0.5\). When the prior belief is already high (e.g. \(\pi > 0.5\)), an additional buy order drives \(\pi\) closer to 1, resolving uncertainty and strictly narrowing the spread.

  • Wrong: If noise traders execute equal numbers of buys and sells, the posterior must return to 0.5.

    Why it is wrong: The sequence of trades matters. While equal counts of buys and sells return the scalar odds ratio to its origin in the binary symmetric model, any net trade imbalance permanently updates beliefs and shifts transaction prices.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Initial quotes with asymmetric prior

Let \(v_L = 20\), \(v_H = 60\), \(\alpha = 0.20\), and \(\pi_0 = 0.60\). Compute \(\pi_1^+\), \(\pi_1^-\), ask quote \(A_1\), bid quote \(B_1\), and the spread \(S_1\).

Solution

We have \(2\pi_0 - 1 = 1.2 - 1 = 0.2\). Denominator for buy is \(1 + \alpha(2\pi_0 - 1) = 1 + 0.2(0.2) = 1.04\). Then \(\pi_1^+ = \frac{0.60(1 + 0.2)}{1.04} = \frac{0.72}{1.04} = \frac{9}{13} \approx 0.6923\). Denominator for sell is \(1 - 0.2(0.2) = 0.96\). Then \(\pi_1^- = \frac{0.60(1 - 0.2)}{0.96} = \frac{0.48}{0.96} = 0.50\). Value difference is \(v_H - v_L = 40\). Ask: \(A_1 = 20 + \frac{9}{13}(40) = 20 + 27.6923 = 47.6923\). Bid: \(B_1 = 20 + 0.50(40) = 40.00\). Spread: \(S_1 = 47.6923 - 40.00 = 7.6923\) dollars.

M2 · Maximal spread condition

For fixed \(\alpha, v_L, v_H\), at what prior probability \(\pi\) is the spread \(S(\pi)\) maximized in the symmetric model?

Solution

The spread formula is \(S(\pi) = (v_H - v_L) \frac{2\alpha \pi (1-\pi)}{1 - \alpha^2(2\pi - 1)^2}\). Let \(u = 2\pi - 1 \in [-1, 1]\). Then \(\pi(1-\pi) = \frac{1 - u^2}{4}\). The spread is \(\frac{\alpha(v_H - v_L)}{2} \frac{1 - u^2}{1 - \alpha^2 u^2}\). The function \(f(x) = \frac{1 - x}{1 - \alpha^2 x}\) for \(x = u^2 \in [0, 1]\) has derivative \(\frac{-(1 - \alpha^2 x) + \alpha^2(1 - x)}{(1 - \alpha^2 x)^2} = \frac{\alpha^2 - 1}{(1 - \alpha^2 x)^2} < 0\) since \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\). Thus the maximum occurs at \(x = 0 \implies u = 0 \implies \pi = 0.5\).

M3 · Dealer profit on noise traders

In Example 2.5 (\(v_L=10, v_H=20, \alpha=1/4, \pi_0=1/2\)), compute the market maker's expected profit on an uninformed trade and expected loss on an informed trade.

Solution

At \(\pi_0 = 1/2\), the unconditional expected value is \(\E[V] = 15.00\). Ask is 16.25 and bid is 13.75. When an uninformed trader arrives, they buy or sell with prob 1/2. On a buy, dealer earns \(A - \E[V] = 16.25 - 15 = 1.25\). On a sell, dealer earns \(\E[V] - B = 15 - 13.75 = 1.25\). Expected profit on noise trade is 1.25. When an informed trader arrives: if \(V = 20\), trader buys at 16.25, dealer loses \(20 - 16.25 = 3.75\). If \(V = 10\), trader sells at 13.75, dealer loses \(13.75 - 10 = 3.75\). Expected loss on informed trade is 3.75. Overall expected profit is \((1 - \alpha)(1.25) - \alpha(3.75) = \frac{3}{4}(1.25) - \frac{1}{4}(3.75) = 0.9375 - 0.9375 = 0\), confirming Bertrand zero-profit equilibrium.

Proof

P1 · Monotonicity of posterior in informed fraction

Prove that for any \(\pi \in (0, 1)\), the posterior \(\pi^+\) after a buy is strictly increasing in the informed fraction \(\alpha\).

Solution

Write \(\pi^+(\alpha) = \frac{\pi(1+\alpha)}{1 - \alpha + 2\alpha\pi} = \frac{\pi(1+\alpha)}{1 + \alpha(2\pi-1)}\). Differentiating with respect to \(\alpha\): \(\frac{\partial \pi^+}{\partial \alpha} = \frac{\pi[1 + \alpha(2\pi-1)] - \pi(1+\alpha)(2\pi-1)}{[1 + \alpha(2\pi-1)]^2} = \frac{\pi[1 - (2\pi-1)]}{[1 + \alpha(2\pi-1)]^2} = \frac{2\pi(1-\pi)}{[1 + \alpha(2\pi-1)]^2}\). For all \(\pi \in (0, 1)\), the numerator is strictly positive, hence \(\frac{\partial \pi^+}{\partial \alpha} > 0\). More informed trading makes a buy order more informative.

P2 · Log-odds update representation

Show that the log-odds ratio \(\ln\left(\frac{\pi_t}{1 - \pi_t}\right)\) updates by a constant step \(\pm \ln\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\) after each trade.

Solution

After a buy: \(\pi_t^+ = \frac{\pi_{t-1}(1+\alpha)}{1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1}-1)}\) and \(1 - \pi_t^+ = \frac{(1 - \pi_{t-1})(1-\alpha)}{1 + \alpha(2\pi_{t-1}-1)}\). Dividing: \(\frac{\pi_t^+}{1 - \pi_t^+} = \frac{\pi_{t-1}}{1 - \pi_{t-1}} \cdot \frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\). Taking logs: \(\ln\left(\frac{\pi_t^+}{1 - \pi_t^+}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{\pi_{t-1}}{1 - \pi_{t-1}}\right) + \ln\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\). After a sell, the ratio multiplies by \(\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}\), subtracting the exact same constant. Belief log-odds follow a simple random walk.

P3 · Convergence of beliefs and vanishing spreads

Prove that as \(t \to \infty\), beliefs \(\pi_t\) converge almost surely to the true state indicator \(\indic_{\{V = v_H\}}\), and the spread \(S_t \to 0\).

Solution

By Proposition 2.4, \(\pi_t\) is a bounded sequence whose expectation conditional on past history equals the previous value. By Doob's martingale convergence theorem, \(\pi_t\) converges almost surely to a limit \(\pi_\infty\). If the limit were interior (\(\pi_\infty \in (0, 1)\)), the step size \(\ln\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right) \ne 0\) would prevent convergence. Hence \(\pi_\infty \in \{0, 1\}\). If \(V = v_H\), the drift of log-odds is \(\frac{1+\alpha}{2} \ln\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right) + \frac{1-\alpha}{2} \ln\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}\right) = \alpha \ln\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right) > 0\). Thus \(\pi_t \to 1\) a.s. when \(V = v_H\) and \(\pi_t \to 0\) a.s. when \(V = v_L\). As \(\pi_t \to \{0, 1\}\), \(S_t \propto \pi_t(1-\pi_t) \to 0\).

Empirical

E1 · Reading Lab 1 sequential quotes

From Lab 1 output, report the ask and bid quotes after Step 2 (second buy) and verify the spread.

Solution

Lab 1 prints: Ask = 295/17 (\(\approx 17.3529\)), Bid = 15 (\(= 15.0000\)), Spread = 40/17 (\(\approx 2.3529\)). Check: \(295/17 - 255/17 = 40/17 \approx 2.3529\), confirming the spread calculation exactly.

E2 · Quote asymmetry after buys

In Lab 1, why does the bid quote remain at 15.0000 before trade 2, even though prior belief rose from 1/2 to 5/8?

Solution

With prior \(\pi = 5/8\), a sell order updates belief to \(\pi^- = \frac{(5/8)(3/4)}{1 - (1/4)(2(5/8)-1)} = \frac{15/32}{1 - (1/4)(1/4)} = \frac{15/32}{15/16} = \frac{1}{2}\). Thus a sell immediately following a buy cancels the buy's informational impact, returning belief to \(1/2\) and bid quote to \(10 + 0.5(10) = 15\).

E3 · Effect of informed fraction on quote spread

If the informed fraction \(\alpha\) increases from 0.25 to 0.50 at \(\pi = 0.5\) with \(v_L = 10, v_H = 20\), by what percentage does the initial spread increase?

Solution

At \(\pi = 0.5\), the spread formula simplifies to \(S = \alpha (v_H - v_L) = 10 \alpha\). For \(\alpha = 0.25\), \(S = 2.50\). For \(\alpha = 0.50\), \(S = 5.00\). The spread doubles, an increase of 100%.

Research

R1 · Endogenous order timing

If informed traders can choose when to submit orders rather than arriving exogenously, how does their arrival strategy alter the informational content of trades?

Solution

Informed traders with private information will trade aggressively when spreads are narrow and delay when spreads widen, creating informational clustering. Quote updating must then account for time between trades (trade duration), leading to duration-dependent models such as Easley and O'Hara (1992).

R2 · Multiple price levels and order sizes

How does allowing traders to submit variable order sizes \(q_t \in \{1, 2, \ldots, K\}\) affect adverse selection in quote-setting?

Solution

Informed traders with larger information advantages trade larger quantities to maximize profit. Consequently, larger trade sizes carry a higher probability of informed trading, causing quotes to widen with trade size and creating an upward-sloping supply curve in the limit order book.

R3 · Discretionary noise trading

Suppose noise traders can choose between trading immediately or waiting for spreads to narrow. Does a Glosten–Milgrom equilibrium always exist?

Solution

If uninformed traders selectively withdraw when spreads widen, the proportion of informed trades among remaining order flow increases, further widening the spread. This feedback loop can cause market breakdown or liquidity black holes, as shown by Admati and Pfleiderer (1988).

  1. With \(v_L = 10\), \(v_H = 20\), \(\alpha = 0.25\), and \(\pi_0 = 0.50\), what is the initial spread \(A_1 - B_1\), in dollars?

    At \(\pi_0 = 0.5\), \(S = \alpha(v_H - v_L) = 0.25(20 - 10) = 2.50\) dollars.

  2. What economic condition determines the ask and bid quotes in the Glosten–Milgrom model?

    Options

    Bertrand competition among risk-neutral market makers forces expected economic profit to zero conditional on order sign.

  3. With prior \(\pi_0 = 0.5\) and \(\alpha = 0.25\), what is the posterior probability \(\pi_1^+\) after observing a buy order?

    \(\pi_1^+ = \frac{0.5(1 + 0.25)}{1 + 0.25(0)} = 0.5 \times 1.25 = 5/8 = 0.625\).

  4. What does the law of iterated expectations imply for beliefs \(\pi_t\) before trade \(t\) occurs?

    Options

    By Proposition 2.4, the expected next posterior conditional on current information equals the current belief.

  5. At what prior probability \(\pi\) is the Glosten–Milgrom spread maximized?

    The spread is proportional to \(\pi(1-\pi)\), which achieves its maximum at \(\pi = 0.50\).

  6. As the informed trader fraction \(\alpha\) increases toward 1, what happens to the spread?

    Options

    When \(\alpha \to 1\), every buy implies \(V=v_H\) and every sell implies \(V=v_L\), widening quotes to \(A = v_H\) and \(B = v_L\), so the spread approaches \(v_H - v_L\).

  7. In Example 2.5 (\(v_L = 10, v_H = 20, \alpha = 0.25, \pi_0 = 0.50\)), what is the ask price \(A_1\), in dollars?

    \(A_1 = 10 + \frac{5}{8}(10) = 10 + 6.25 = 16.25\) dollars.

  8. Why does the bid–ask spread remain strictly positive even when the dealer incurs zero order-processing cost and is risk-neutral?

    Options

    Informed traders trade only when profitable, imposing losses on the dealer. The spread extracts compensating revenue from uninformed noise traders.
