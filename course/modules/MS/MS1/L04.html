An observed bid–ask spread is not a single economic cost. Part of the spread covers the dealer's order-processing expenses and clearing fees, while another part compensates for adverse selection when trading against informed counterparties. Knowing how much of a spread stems from information asymmetry versus mechanical processing costs is crucial for evaluating market quality, exchange fee models, and execution costs. This lesson formulates the structural spread decomposition model, derives its regression identities, and shows how trade data identifies each component.

1The repeated-trade microstructure model

Definition 4.1 (spread components)

The effective half-spread \(H > 0\) paid by liquidity demanders consists of two distinct economic components:

  • Order-processing cost \(c > 0\): the direct expense of market making, including exchange connectivity, clearing, execution infrastructure, and financing.
  • Adverse-selection cost \(\theta \ge 0\): the permanent price response per signed trade reflecting private information conveyed by order flow.
The total effective half-spread is \(H = c + \theta\), and spread decomposition is the econometric separation of \(H\) into \(c\) and \(\theta\).

Assumption A1 (linear repeated-trade structure)

Let \(q_t \in \{-1, +1\}\) be i.i.d. signed trade directions with \(\Prob(q_t = +1) = 1/2\) and \(\Prob(q_t = -1) = 1/2\). The unobserved efficient price \(m_t\) and observed transaction price \(p_t\) satisfy: \[ m_t = m_{t-1} + \theta\, q_t + \eta_t, \] \[ p_t = m_t + c\, q_t, \] where \(c > 0\), \(\theta \ge 0\), and public information innovations \(\eta_t\) are i.i.d. with \(\E[\eta_t] = 0\), \(\Var(\eta_t) = \sigma^2\), independent of all past and current trade signs.

2Regression identification of spread components

Theorem 4.2 (regression decomposition identities)

Under Assumption A1, the transaction price change satisfies: \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta p_t &= p_t - p_{t-1} \\ &= (\theta + c)\, q_t - c\, q_{t-1} + \eta_t. \end{aligned} \] The least-squares projection of \(\Delta p_t\) on \((q_t, q_{t-1})\) without intercept: \[ \Delta p_t = b_0\, q_t + b_1\, q_{t-1} + e_t \] uniquely identifies the structural parameters: \[ b_0 = \theta + c = H, \quad b_1 = -c, \] from which the components are recovered: \[ c = -b_1, \quad \theta = b_0 + b_1, \quad H = b_0. \]

Proof

Substitute the expressions for \(p_t\) and \(p_{t-1}\): \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta p_t &= (m_t + c\, q_t) \\ &\quad - (m_{t-1} + c\, q_{t-1}) \\ &= m_{t-1} + \theta\, q_t + \eta_t + c\, q_t \\ &\quad - (m_{t-1} + c\, q_{t-1}) \\ &= (\theta + c)\, q_t - c\, q_{t-1} + \eta_t. \end{aligned} \] Since \(\{q_t\}\) is i.i.d. with mean zero and unit variance, \(\Var(q_t) = 1\), \(\Var(q_{t-1}) = 1\), and \(\Cov(q_t, q_{t-1}) = 0\). The population regressor covariance matrix is the identity matrix \(I_2\). Computing covariances with \(\Delta p_t\): \[ \begin{aligned} \Cov(\Delta p_t, q_t) &= (\theta + c)\E[q_t^2] - c\E[q_t q_{t-1}] + \E[\eta_t q_t] \\ &= \theta + c, \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \Cov(\Delta p_t, q_{t-1}) &= (\theta + c)\E[q_t q_{t-1}] - c\E[q_{t-1}^2] + \E[\eta_t q_{t-1}] \\ &= -c. \end{aligned} \] The population normal equations are: \[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} b_0 \\ b_1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \theta + c \\ -c \end{pmatrix}, \] giving \(b_0 = \theta + c\) and \(b_1 = -c\). Solving for \(c\) and \(\theta\) yields \(c = -b_1\) and \(\theta = b_0 - c = b_0 + b_1\). The identity \(H = c + \theta\) holds identically since \((-b_1) + (b_0 + b_1) = b_0\).

Theorem 4.3 (Roll spread under adverse selection)

Under Assumption A1, the first-order serial autocovariance of price changes is: \[ \Cov(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) = -c(\theta + c) = -c\, H. \] Consequently, Roll's spread estimator computes: \[ S_{\text{Roll}} = 2 \sqrt{c(\theta + c)} = 2 \sqrt{c\, H}. \] When \(\theta = 0\), \(S_{\text{Roll}} = 2c = 2H\). When \(\theta > 0\), \(S_{\text{Roll}} < 2H\), with the gap strictly increasing in the adverse selection share.

Proof

Using the price-change expressions for \(\Delta p_t\) and \(\Delta p_{t-1}\): \[ \begin{aligned} \E[\Delta p_t \Delta p_{t-1}] &= \E[(-c\, q_{t-1})((\theta + c)q_{t-1})] \\ &= -c(\theta + c)\,\E[q_{t-1}^2] = -c(\theta + c) = -c\, H. \end{aligned} \] The Roll formula evaluates \(2\sqrt{-\Cov} = 2\sqrt{c(\theta + c)}\). By the AM-GM inequality: \[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{c(\theta + c)} &< \frac{c + (\theta + c)}{2} = \frac{\theta + 2c}{2} \\ &\le \theta + c = H \quad \text{for all } \theta > 0. \end{aligned} \] Multiplying by 2 proves \(S_{\text{Roll}} < 2H\).

Example 4.4 (numerical decomposition)

Let \(\theta = 0.15\) and \(c = 0.20\), so total half-spread is \(H = 0.35\) and adverse share is \(0.15 / 0.35 \approx 0.4286\). The population regression coefficients are \(b_0 = 0.35\) and \(b_1 = -0.20\). The population Roll autocovariance is \(-c(\theta + c) = -0.20(0.35) = -0.0700\), giving Roll spread \(2\sqrt{0.07} \approx 0.5292\), whereas the true full spread is \(2H = 0.7000\). Lab 1 checks this recovery on a simulated sample of 5000 trades.

Remark 4.5 (attributions to Roll 1984 and Glosten–Milgrom 1985)

Roll (1984) established order-processing bounce, and Glosten and Milgrom (1985) established adverse-selection price impact. The regression framework of Theorem 4.2 combines both models into an estimable linear system.

3Sample estimation and identity checks

Lab 1 · Spread decomposition regression on simulated trades

SIMULATED: 5000 trades with true theta = 0.1500, c = 0.2000, and sigma = 0.05. Expected: no-intercept OLS estimates b_0 and b_1 recover H = 0.3500, c = 0.2000, and theta = 0.1500, satisfying the decomposition identity |H - (c + theta)| = 0 to floating-point precision.

"""Lab MS1-L04-1: Bid-ask spread decomposition by regression.

SIMULATED: generated trade prices with both order-processing cost and adverse-selection impact.
Recovers the half-spread, order-processing cost, and adverse-selection component from
a no-intercept regression of price changes on current and lagged trade signs.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random


def simulate_decomposition_sample(
    n_trades: int, theta: float, c: float, sigma: float, seed: int = 42
) -> tuple[list[float], list[int]]:
    """Simulate trade prices with order processing and adverse selection."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    m = 100.0
    prices: list[float] = []
    signs: list[int] = []
    for _ in range(n_trades):
        q = rng.choice([-1, 1])
        eta = rng.gauss(0.0, sigma)
        m += theta * q + eta
        prices.append(m + c * q)
        signs.append(q)
    return prices, signs


def decompose_spread(prices: list[float], signs: list[int]) -> tuple[float, float, float, float, float]:
    """Perform no-intercept OLS of price differences on current and lagged trade signs."""
    n = len(prices)
    if n < 3:
        raise ValueError("At least three trades are needed")
    if len(signs) != n:
        raise ValueError("Prices and signs must have the same length")

    d = [prices[t] - prices[t - 1] for t in range(1, n)]
    q_curr = [signs[t] for t in range(1, n)]
    q_lag = [signs[t - 1] for t in range(1, n)]

    s00 = sum(x * x for x in q_curr)
    s11 = sum(x * x for x in q_lag)
    s01 = sum(x * y for x, y in zip(q_curr, q_lag, strict=False))
    det = s00 * s11 - s01 * s01
    if det == 0:
        raise ValueError("Singular design matrix")

    y0 = sum(di * qi for di, qi in zip(d, q_curr, strict=False))
    y1 = sum(di * qi for di, qi in zip(d, q_lag, strict=False))

    b0 = (s11 * y0 - s01 * y1) / det
    b1 = (s00 * y1 - s01 * y0) / det

    half_spread = b0
    order_processing = -b1
    adverse_selection = b0 + b1
    adverse_share = adverse_selection / half_spread if half_spread != 0.0 else float("nan")
    identity_gap = abs(half_spread - (order_processing + adverse_selection))

    return half_spread, order_processing, adverse_selection, adverse_share, identity_gap


def main() -> None:
    n_trades = 5000
    theta_true = 0.1500
    c_true = 0.2000
    sigma = 0.05
    prices, signs = simulate_decomposition_sample(n_trades, theta_true, c_true, sigma, seed=42)

    half_s, order_proc, adv_sel, adv_share, id_gap = decompose_spread(prices, signs)

    print("Spread Decomposition Regression Results:")
    print(f"  Number of trades: {n_trades}")
    print(f"  True half-spread H = theta + c: {theta_true + c_true:.4f}")
    print(f"  Estimated half-spread H (b_0): {half_s:.4f}")
    print(f"  True order processing c: {c_true:.4f}")
    print(f"  Estimated order processing c (-b_1): {order_proc:.4f}")
    print(f"  True adverse selection theta: {theta_true:.4f}")
    print(f"  Estimated adverse selection theta (b_0 + b_1): {adv_sel:.4f}")
    print(f"  Adverse selection share: {adv_share:.4f} (true: {theta_true / (theta_true + c_true):.4f})")
    print(f"  Decomposition identity |H - (c + theta)|: {id_gap:.2e}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

Limit 4.1 (omission of dealer inventory costs)

In this two-component model, dealer inventory is zero by construction: order flow does not accumulate in a dealer position, so inventory holding costs are absent. If dealers hold inventory as in inventory models, price changes also depend on cumulative signed inventory, adding a third component to the spread.

Limit 4.2 (constant parameters and trade sign independence)

The model assumes constant parameters \(c\) and \(\theta\), and serially independent trade signs \(\{q_t\}\). In practice, trade signs exhibit autocorrelation due to order splitting, and adverse selection spikes around news announcements, inducing time variation in both components.

5Misconceptions

  • Wrong: The regression requires an intercept term to capture average drift.

    Why it is wrong: By Assumption A1, \(\E[\eta_t] = 0\) and \(\E[q_t] = 0\), so the population price change has mean zero. Adding an intercept consumes a degree of freedom and introduces collinearity when trade signs are persistent.

  • Wrong: If the adverse selection component \(\theta = 0\), transaction prices follow a pure martingale.

    Why it is wrong: When \(\theta = 0\), transaction prices still oscillate around the efficient price by \(\pm c q_t\). The efficient price \(m_t\) is a random walk, but the transaction price \(p_t\) exhibits negative serial correlation due to bid-ask bounce.

  • Wrong: A higher Roll spread implies greater adverse selection.

    Why it is wrong: Theorem 4.3 shows that the Roll spread \(2\sqrt{c(c+\theta)}\) is primarily driven by order-processing cost \(c\). If \(c = 0\), Roll's covariance vanishes completely regardless of how large \(\theta\) is.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Recovering components from regression slopes

A regression of \(\Delta p_t\) on \((q_t, q_{t-1})\) yields \(\hat{b}_0 = 0.28\) and \(\hat{b}_1 = -0.12\). Compute the estimated half-spread \(H\), order processing \(c\), adverse selection \(\theta\), and the adverse share.

Solution

Apply Theorem 4.2: \(H = b_0 = 0.28\) dollars. Order-processing cost is \(c = -b_1 = -(-0.12) = 0.12\) dollars. Adverse selection is \(\theta = b_0 + b_1 = 0.28 - 0.12 = 0.16\) dollars. Adverse share is \(\theta / H = 0.16 / 0.28 = 4/7 \approx 0.5714\) (57.14%).

M2 · Roll spread under estimated parameters

Using the parameters from M1 (\(c = 0.12, \theta = 0.16\)), compute the expected Roll spread \(S_{\text{Roll}}\) and compare it to the true full spread \(2H\).

Solution

The true full spread is \(2H = 2(0.28) = 0.56\) dollars. The Roll spread is \(S_{\text{Roll}} = 2\sqrt{c(\theta + c)} = 2\sqrt{0.12(0.28)} = 2\sqrt{0.0336} \approx 2(0.1833) \approx 0.3666\) dollars. Roll underestimates the true spread by \(0.56 - 0.3666 = 0.1934\) dollars (approx 34.5%).

M3 · Effective half-spread expectation

Verify that the population quantity \(\E[q_t(p_t - m_{t-1})]\) equals \(\theta + c\).

Solution

Write \(p_t - m_{t-1} = (\theta + c)q_t + \eta_t\). Multiplying by \(q_t\) gives \(q_t(p_t - m_{t-1}) = (\theta + c)q_t^2 + q_t \eta_t\). Taking expectations: \[ \begin{aligned} \E[q_t(p_t - m_{t-1})] &= (\theta + c)\E[q_t^2] + \E[q_t \eta_t] \\ &= \theta + c. \end{aligned} \]

P1 · Orthogonality of error term

Prove that the disturbance \(\eta_t\) in the price change equation is orthogonal to both regressors \(q_t\) and \(q_{t-1}\).

Solution

By Assumption A1, \(\eta_t\) is independent of all current and past trade signs \(\{q_s\}_{s \le t}\). Therefore \(\E[\eta_t q_t] = 0\) and \(\E[\eta_t q_{t-1}] = 0\). Orthogonality guarantees that OLS is consistent for \(b_0\) and \(b_1\).

P2 · Non-singularity of normal equations

Prove that the \(2 \times 2\) design matrix \(\sum_{t=2}^n \begin{pmatrix} q_t^2 & q_t q_{t-1} \\ q_t q_{t-1} & q_{t-1}^2 \end{pmatrix}\) is non-singular if and only if trade signs do not alternate deterministically or remain constant.

Solution

The determinant is: \[ D = \Bigl(\sum_{t=2}^n q_t^2\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{t=2}^n q_{t-1}^2\Bigr) - \Bigl(\sum_{t=2}^n q_t q_{t-1}\Bigr)^2. \] By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, \(D \ge 0\), with equality if and only if the vector \((q_2, \ldots, q_n)\) is a scalar multiple of \((q_1, \ldots, q_{n-1})\). Since each \(q_t \in \{-1, +1\}\), the scalar multiple can only be \(+1\) (all signs equal) or \(-1\) (perfectly alternating signs). Hence the matrix is non-singular whenever signs vary non-alternatingly.

P3 · Ratio of Roll spread to true spread

Let \(\kappa = \theta / c \ge 0\) be the ratio of adverse selection to order processing. Derive the ratio \(S_{\text{Roll}} / (2H)\) as a function of \(\kappa\) and show it strictly decreases from 1 to 0 as \(\kappa \to \infty\).

Solution

The true spread is \(2H = 2c(1+\kappa)\). The Roll spread is \(S_{\text{Roll}} = 2c\sqrt{1+\kappa}\). The ratio is: \[ \frac{S_{\text{Roll}}}{2H} = \frac{2c\sqrt{1+\kappa}}{2c(1+\kappa)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\kappa}}. \] At \(\kappa = 0\), the ratio is 1. As \(\kappa \to \infty\), it approaches 0. The derivative with respect to \(\kappa\) is \(-\frac{1}{2}(1+\kappa)^{-3/2} < 0\), so the ratio strictly decreases.

Empirical

E1 · Interpreting Lab 1 estimates

From Lab 1 output, report the recovered half-spread \(H\), order processing \(c\), adverse selection \(\theta\), and verify the decomposition identity.

Solution

Lab 1 prints: \(H = 0.3512\), \(c = 0.2000\), and \(\theta = 0.1512\). Verification: \(c + \theta = 0.2000 + 0.1512 = 0.3512\). The difference \(|H - (c + \theta)|\) is \(0.00e+00\), confirming exact numerical articulation of the identity.

E2 · Adverse share comparison

Compare the estimated adverse selection share from Lab 1 to the true population value. What explains the difference?

Solution

The true adverse selection share is \(\frac{0.15}{0.35} = \frac{3}{7} \approx 0.4286\). Lab 1 yields \(0.4305\). The slight difference of \(0.4305 - 0.4286 = 0.0019\) (less than 0.2%) is due to finite-sample variation in the 5000 random normal draws \(\eta_t\).

E3 · Minimum trade count for reliable decomposition

Why do empirical studies typically require several hundred trades per day to estimate spread decompositions reliably?

Solution

The standard error of OLS regression coefficients scales as \(\sigma / \sqrt{n}\). With high return volatility \(\sigma\) and small spreads (a few cents), samples with fewer than several hundred trades yield wide confidence intervals that frequently encompass negative estimates of \(\theta\) or \(c\).

Research

R1 · Trade sign classification algorithms

In market databases where trade signs are not reported, the tick rule assigns \(q_t = +1\) if \(p_t > p_{t-1}\) and \(q_t = -1\) if \(p_t < p_{t-1}\). How does tick-rule misclassification bias the regression estimates \(b_0\) and \(b_1\)?

Solution

Misclassifying trade signs introduces classical errors-in-variables measurement error into the regressors \(q_t\) and \(q_{t-1}\). This creates attenuation bias toward zero in \(\hat{b}_0\) and \(\hat{b}_1\), causing underestimation of both order-processing and adverse-selection components.

R2 · Multi-lag price impact specifications

Why do empirical researchers include multiple lags of trade signs (e.g. \(q_{t-1}, \ldots, q_{t-k}\)) when decomposing spreads in modern electronic markets?

Solution

Institutional meta-orders are executed as parent orders split into hundreds of child orders, generating persistent trade sign correlation spanning multiple minutes. Multi-lag specifications capture slow price discovery and inventory mean reversion over longer trading horizons.

R3 · Asymmetric buyer and seller price impacts

Suppose short-sale constraints make sell trades less informed than buy trades (\(\theta_{\text{buy}} > \theta_{\text{sell}}\)). How would you generalize the regression model?

Solution

Replace \(q_t\) with separate indicator variables \(q_t^+ = \indic_{\{q_t = +1\}}\) and \(q_t^- = \indic_{\{q_t = -1\}}\). The regression equation becomes:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta p_t &= (\theta^+ + c)q_t^+ \\ &\quad - (\theta^- + c)q_t^- \\ &\quad - c\, q_{t-1}^+ \\ &\quad + c\, q_{t-1}^- + \eta_t, \end{aligned} \]

identifying asymmetric information parameters.

    \(c = -b_1 = -(-0.20) = 0.20\) dollars.

  3. What is the total effective half-spread \(H\) if \(b_0 = 0.35\)?

    \(H = b_0 = 0.35\) dollars.

  4. Why does the linear regression of price changes on trade signs omit an intercept?

    Options

    By Assumption A1, \(\E[\eta_t] = 0\) and \(\E[q_t] = 0\), implying \(\E[\Delta p_t] = 0\).

  5. What is the population lag-1 autocovariance of price changes when \(c = 0.10\) and \(\theta = 0.20\)?

    Options

    \(\Cov = -c(\theta + c) = -0.10(0.20 + 0.10) = -0.10(0.30) = -0.030\).

  6. If \(c = 0.15\) and \(\theta = 0.15\), what is the adverse selection share \(\theta / H\)?

    \(H = c + \theta = 0.30\). Adverse share is \(0.15 / 0.30 = 0.50\).

  7. If a market has significant adverse selection (\(\theta > 0\)), how does Roll's spread estimator \(2\sqrt{-\Cov}\) compare to the true spread \(2H\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 4.3, \(2\sqrt{c(c+\theta)} < 2(c+\theta)\) by the AM-GM inequality.

  8. In the structural identity \(H = c + \theta\), what is the theoretical difference \(|H - (c + \theta)|\)?

    By definition \(H = c + \theta\), so the difference is identically 0.00.
