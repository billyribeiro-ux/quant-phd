Inventory and the Dealer's Quote

  • Uses: Bellman equation, Glosten–Milgrom model, principle of optimality
  • Introduces: inventory models
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or finite check

Market makers cannot hold unlimited long or short positions. Carrying inventory exposes a dealer to price volatility, financing costs, and regulatory capital requirements. To control inventory risk, dealers skew their quotes: raising both bid and ask when short to attract sellers, and lowering both bid and ask when long to attract buyers. This lesson formulates inventory management as a dynamic program with quadratic holding costs, solves the Bellman recursion by backward induction, and establishes the optimal quote skewing policy.

1Dealer inventory and holding penalties

Definition 3.1 (inventory state and quote shift)

Let \(I_t \in \{-K, \ldots, K\}\) denote the dealer's accumulated inventory position (in integer shares) at time \(t \in \{0, 1, \ldots, T\}\). When a public buyer arrives (\(q_t = +1\)), the dealer sells one unit, transitioning to inventory \(I_t - 1\). When a public seller arrives (\(q_t = -1\)), the dealer buys one unit, transitioning to inventory \(I_t + 1\). Let \(c_0 > 0\) denote the baseline half-spread. The dealer sets a quote midpoint shift \(\delta_t \in \R\), quoting: \[ A_t = m_t + c_0 + \delta_t, \quad B_t = m_t - c_0 + \delta_t. \] The parameter \(\delta_t\) is the dealer's quote skew.

Assumption A1 (finite grid and horizon)

Inventory is constrained to a finite grid \(I_t \in \{-K, \ldots, K\}\) with \(K = 2\). The horizon is finite, \(T = 2\). Trades beyond the inventory boundary are clamped to the boundary.

Assumption A2 (quadratic inventory holding penalty)

In each period \(t < T\), the dealer incurs a quadratic running inventory holding cost \(\frac{1}{2} a I_t^2\), and at terminal date \(T\) faces a terminal liquidation penalty \(V_T(I) = I^2\), with risk parameter \(a = 1\).

Assumption A3 (quote-dependent order arrivals)

Order arrival probabilities respond linearly to the quote shift \(\delta\). For candidate shifts \(\delta \in \{-1, -1/2, 0, 1/2, 1\}\): \[ \Prob(\text{buy} \mid \delta) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} \delta, \quad \Prob(\text{sell} \mid \delta) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} \delta. \] Since \(|\delta| \le 1\), arrival probabilities are valid numbers in \([1/4, 3/4]\) that sum to 1.

2Dynamic programming and the Bellman recursion

Proposition 3.2 (inventory Bellman equation)

Under Assumptions A1, A2, and A3, the dealer's value function \(V_t(I)\) satisfies the Bellman equation by the principle of optimality: \[ V_T(I) = I^2, \] and for \(t = T-1, \ldots, 0\): \[ V_t(I) = \frac{1}{2} I^2 + \min_{\delta \in \Delta} \left\{ -R(\delta) + P_{\text{buy}}(\delta)\, V_{t+1}(\max(-K, I - 1)) + P_{\text{sell}}(\delta)\, V_{t+1}(\min(K, I + 1)) \right\}, \] where expected spread revenue is: \[ R(\delta) = P_{\text{buy}}(\delta)(c_0 + \delta) + P_{\text{sell}}(\delta)(c_0 - \delta) = c_0 - \frac{1}{2} \delta^2. \] The optimal policy \(\delta_t^*(I)\) is the argmin of this recursion in inventory models.

Proof

In period \(t\), starting with inventory \(I\), the dealer chooses quote shift \(\delta\). With probability \(P_{\text{buy}}(\delta)\), a buyer arrives; the dealer executes a sell at price \(m_t + c_0 + \delta\), receiving revenue \(c_0 + \delta\) over the benchmark price \(m_t\), and inventory transitions to \(\max(-K, I-1)\). With probability \(P_{\text{sell}}(\delta)\), a seller arrives; the dealer executes a buy at price \(m_t - c_0 + \delta\), paying \(m_t - (c_0 - \delta)\), earning revenue \(c_0 - \delta\), and inventory transitions to \(\min(K, I+1)\). Expected revenue is: \[ \begin{aligned} R(\delta) &= \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} \delta\right)(c_0 + \delta) + \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} \delta\right)(c_0 - \delta) \\ &= \frac{1}{2}(c_0 + \delta + c_0 - \delta) - \frac{1}{4}\delta(c_0 + \delta - (c_0 - \delta)) \\ &= c_0 - \frac{1}{4}\delta(2\delta) = c_0 - \frac{1}{2}\delta^2. \end{aligned} \] Adding the running holding cost \(\frac{1}{2} I^2\) and subtracting expected revenue from continuation costs gives the cost function in the statement. The principle of optimality guarantees that minimizing period-by-period cost yields the optimal global policy.

Proposition 3.3 (symmetry and anti-symmetry of quote shifts)

Assume A1, A2, and A3. For every \(t \in \{0, \ldots, T\}\), the value function is symmetric: \[ V_t(I) = V_t(-I), \quad \forall I \in \{-K, \ldots, K\}, \] and the optimal quote shift is anti-symmetric: \[ \delta_t^*(I) = -\delta_t^*(-I), \quad \text{with } \delta_t^*(0) = 0. \] When the dealer is long (\(I > 0\)), \(\delta_t^*(I) \le 0\), shading quotes down; when short (\(I < 0\)), \(\delta_t^*(I) \ge 0\), shading quotes up.

Proof

We proceed by backward induction. At \(t = T\), \(V_T(I) = I^2 = (-I)^2 = V_T(-I)\), which is symmetric. Assume \(V_{t+1}(I) = V_{t+1}(-I)\). Let \(C_t(I, \delta)\) denote the objective in Proposition 3.2. Note that: \[ P_{\text{buy}}(-\delta) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4}(-\delta) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}\delta = P_{\text{sell}}(\delta), \] \[ P_{\text{sell}}(-\delta) = P_{\text{buy}}(\delta), \quad R(-\delta) = c_0 - \frac{1}{2}(-\delta)^2 = R(\delta). \] Evaluating \(C_t(-I, -\delta)\): \[ \begin{aligned} C_t(-I, -\delta) &= \frac{1}{2}(-I)^2 - R(-\delta) + P_{\text{buy}}(-\delta) V_{t+1}(\max(-K, -I - 1)) + P_{\text{sell}}(-\delta) V_{t+1}(\min(K, -I + 1)) \\ &= \frac{1}{2} I^2 - R(\delta) + P_{\text{sell}}(\delta) V_{t+1}(- \min(K, I + 1)) + P_{\text{buy}}(\delta) V_{t+1}(- \max(-K, I - 1)). \end{aligned} \] Using the induction hypothesis \(V_{t+1}(-x) = V_{t+1}(x)\), this equals \(C_t(I, \delta)\). Thus, if \(\delta^*\) minimizes \(C_t(I, \delta)\), then \(-\delta^*\) minimizes \(C_t(-I, \delta)\), proving \(\delta_t^*(-I) = -\delta_t^*(I)\). At \(I = 0\), \(\delta_t^*(0) = -\delta_t^*(0) \implies \delta_t^*(0) = 0\). The minimum costs agree, proving \(V_t(-I) = V_t(I)\).

Example 3.4 (exact optimal policy on the grid)

In the model solved by Lab 1 with \(c_0 = 1/2\), the backward induction yields at \(t = 0\): \[ \delta_0^*(-2) = +1/2, \quad \delta_0^*(-1) = +1, \quad \delta_0^*(0) = 0, \quad \delta_0^*(+1) = -1, \quad \delta_0^*(+2) = -1/2. \] When long 1 share (\(I = +1\)), the dealer shades quotes down by \(-1\), increasing buy probability to \(3/4\) and reducing sell probability to \(1/4\), driving inventory back toward zero.

Remark 3.5 (title citation of Ho & Stoll 1981)

Continuous-time inventory-driven dealer pricing was introduced by Thomas Ho and Hans Stoll in "Optimal dealer pricing under transactions and return uncertainty". The discrete quadratic model derived here captures the fundamental inventory skew mechanism without return diffusion.

3Exact computational implementation

Lab 1 · Discrete inventory dynamic programming by backward induction

Exact Fraction backward induction on grid I in {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2}. Expected: optimal quote shift delta*(I) is anti-symmetric (negative for long positions, positive for short positions), delta*(0) = 0, and value function is symmetric.

"""Lab MS1-L03-1: Discrete inventory control and optimal quote skewing.

Dynamic programming by backward induction on a finite inventory grid.
Solves the dealer quote-setting problem with quadratic inventory penalties in exact Fraction arithmetic.
Demonstrates that optimal quote shift is negatively related to inventory level (grade C finite enumeration).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def solve_inventory_dp() -> tuple[dict[int, Fraction], dict[int, Fraction], dict[int, Fraction], dict[int, Fraction]]:
    """Solve 2-period inventory DP on grid I in {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2}."""
    inventories = [-2, -1, 0, 1, 2]
    shifts = [Fraction(-1, 1), Fraction(-1, 2), Fraction(0, 1), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1, 1)]
    c0 = Fraction(1, 2)  # baseline half-spread

    # Terminal penalty: V_2(I) = I^2
    v_terminal = {i: Fraction(i**2, 1) for i in inventories}

    v_current = v_terminal
    policy_period1: dict[int, Fraction] = {}
    v_period1: dict[int, Fraction] = {}
    policy_period0: dict[int, Fraction] = {}
    v_period0: dict[int, Fraction] = {}

    for t in [1, 0]:
        next_v: dict[int, Fraction] = {}
        opt_shift: dict[int, Fraction] = {}
        for inv in inventories:
            best_cost: Fraction | None = None
            best_delta: Fraction | None = None
            for delta in shifts:
                # Order arrival probabilities linear in quote shift
                p_buy = Fraction(1, 2) - Fraction(1, 4) * delta
                p_sell = Fraction(1, 2) + Fraction(1, 4) * delta

                # Clamped transitions
                inv_after_buy = max(-2, inv - 1)
                inv_after_sell = min(2, inv + 1)

                # Expected spread revenue
                revenue = p_buy * (c0 + delta) + p_sell * (c0 - delta)

                # Period holding cost + expected net continuation cost
                holding = Fraction(1, 2) * Fraction(inv**2, 1)
                expected_continuation = p_buy * v_current[inv_after_buy] + p_sell * v_current[inv_after_sell]
                total_cost = holding - revenue + expected_continuation

                if best_cost is None or total_cost < best_cost:
                    best_cost = total_cost
                    best_delta = delta

            assert best_cost is not None and best_delta is not None
            next_v[inv] = best_cost
            opt_shift[inv] = best_delta

        v_current = next_v
        if t == 1:
            policy_period1 = opt_shift
            v_period1 = next_v
        else:
            policy_period0 = opt_shift
            v_period0 = next_v

    return policy_period0, v_period0, policy_period1, v_period1


def main() -> None:
    pol0, v0, pol1, v1 = solve_inventory_dp()

    print("Inventory model: quadratic holding penalty and finite grid backward induction")
    print("Period 1 value function and optimal quote shift:")
    for inv in sorted(pol1):
        print(f"  I = {inv:+d}: V_1(I) = {v1[inv]} ({float(v1[inv]):.4f}), delta*_1(I) = {float(pol1[inv]):+.2f}")

    print("Period 0 value function and optimal quote shift:")
    for inv in sorted(pol0):
        print(f"  I = {inv:+d}: V_0(I) = {v0[inv]} ({float(v0[inv]):.4f}), delta*_0(I) = {float(pol0[inv]):+.2f}")

    print("Symmetry and monotonicity checks:")
    sym_shift = all(pol0[i] == -pol0[-i] for i in [1, 2])
    sym_val = all(v0[i] == v0[-i] for i in [1, 2])
    print(f"  Quote skew anti-symmetry delta*(I) == -delta*(-I): {sym_shift}")
    print(f"  Value function symmetry V(I) == V(-I): {sym_val}")
    print(f"  Quote shift at zero inventory: {pol0[0]}")

4Where the canon breaks

Limit 3.1 (absence of adverse selection)

This inventory model assumes all trades are uninformed: order flow conveys zero information about true value \(V\). In real markets, order flow carries both inventory risk and adverse selection. Combining both effects requires joint modeling (as developed in Lesson 4).

Limit 3.2 (discrete grid clamping)

Clamping inventory to a finite grid \(\{-K, \ldots, K\}\) introduces boundary distortions where the dealer may shade quotes less aggressively at the extreme boundary than inside the interior (e.g. \(\delta^*(-2) = 1/2\) vs \(\delta^*(-1) = 1\)), reflecting boundary truncation rather than unconstrained convex control.

5Misconceptions

  • Wrong: Quote skewing shifts the bid–ask spread wider or narrower.

    Why it is wrong: Symmetric quote skewing shifts both ask and bid by the same amount \(\delta_t\). The spread \(A_t - B_t = (m_t + c_0 + \delta_t) - (m_t - c_0 + \delta_t) = 2c_0\) remains unchanged; only the quote midpoint \(m_t + \delta_t\) moves.

  • Wrong: A dealer who accumulates positive inventory should raise prices to reflect higher asset accumulation.

    Why it is wrong: A dealer with excess long inventory faces holding risk and capital constraints. To reduce inventory, the dealer must lower prices (\(\delta < 0\)) to stimulate buyer demand and discourage incoming sellers.

  • Wrong: Inventory holding costs explain why spreads widen during volatile market conditions.

    Why it is wrong: While inventory risk does widen quotes when volatility \(\sigma\) increases under continuous-time models (Ho-Stoll), information asymmetry (adverse selection) is typically the primary driver of rapid spread widening during crises.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Expected revenue from quote shift

Under Assumption A3 with \(c_0 = 0.50\), compute expected spread revenue \(R(\delta)\) for \(\delta = 0\) and \(\delta = 0.5\).

Solution

Formula: \(R(\delta) = c_0 - \frac{1}{2}\delta^2\). For \(\delta = 0\), \(R(0) = 0.50\). For \(\delta = 0.5\), \(R(0.5) = 0.50 - \frac{1}{2}(0.25) = 0.50 - 0.125 = 0.375\). Quote skewing reduces expected instantaneous spread revenue to achieve inventory rebalancing.

M2 · One-period terminal inventory cost

Let \(V_2(I) = I^2\) and \(c_0 = 0.50\). For inventory \(I = 1\), compare total cost \(C_1(1, \delta)\) at \(\delta = 0\) versus \(\delta = -1\).

Solution

For \(\delta = 0\): \(P_{\text{buy}} = 0.5, P_{\text{sell}} = 0.5\). Holding cost: \(\frac{1}{2}(1)^2 = 0.5\). Revenue: \(0.5\). Next inventory is 0 (cost 0) on buy, 2 (cost 4) on sell. Total cost: \(0.5 - 0.5 + 0.5(0) + 0.5(4) = 2.0\). For \(\delta = -1\): \(P_{\text{buy}} = 0.75, P_{\text{sell}} = 0.25\). Revenue: \(0.5 - 0.5(1) = 0\). Total cost: \(0.5 - 0 + 0.75(0) + 0.25(4) = 0.5 + 1.0 = 1.5\). Shading quotes by \(-1\) lowers total cost from 2.0 to 1.5.

M3 · Midpoint shift and execution prices

If benchmark price is \(m_t = 100.0\), baseline half-spread is \(c_0 = 0.40\), and inventory policy sets \(\delta_t = -0.15\), compute the ask and bid quotes.

Solution

Ask quote: \(A_t = m_t + c_0 + \delta_t = 100.0 + 0.40 - 0.15 = 100.25\). Bid quote: \(B_t = m_t - c_0 + \delta_t = 100.0 - 0.40 - 0.15 = 99.45\). Quote midpoint is \(99.85\), shifted down by \(0.15\) from \(100.0\). The spread remains \(100.25 - 99.45 = 0.80 = 2c_0\).

Proof

P1 · Monotonicity of optimal quote shift

Prove that in unconstrained continuous inventory control with cost \(C(I, \delta) = \frac{1}{2} a I^2 - (c_0 - \frac{1}{2}\delta^2) + \frac{1}{2}(V(I-1) + V(I+1)) - \frac{1}{4}\delta(V(I-1) - V(I+1))\), the optimal shift is strictly decreasing in inventory if \(V(I)\) is strictly convex.

Solution

Differentiating with respect to \(\delta\): \(\frac{\partial C}{\partial \delta} = \delta - \frac{1}{4}(V(I-1) - V(I+1)) = 0 \implies \delta^* = \frac{1}{4}(V(I-1) - V(I+1))\). When \(V\) is strictly convex, the backward difference \(V(I+1) - V(I-1)\) is strictly increasing in \(I\). Therefore \(\delta^* = -\frac{1}{4}[V(I+1) - V(I-1)]\) is strictly decreasing in \(I\).

P2 · Zero inventory implies zero quote shift

Using the first-order condition \(\delta^* = \frac{1}{4}(V(I-1) - V(I+1))\) and symmetry of \(V\), prove that \(\delta^*(0) = 0\).

Solution

Evaluate the first-order condition at \(I = 0\): \(\delta^*(0) = \frac{1}{4}(V(-1) - V(1))\). By Proposition 3.3, \(V(-1) = V(1)\). Therefore \(V(-1) - V(1) = 0\), which immediately implies \(\delta^*(0) = 0\). A neutral inventory position requires no quote skew.

P3 · Expected inventory drift under optimal policy

Show that under the optimal policy \(\delta^*(I)\), expected inventory drift \(\E[\Delta I_t \mid I_t]\) has opposite sign to \(I_t\), creating mean reversion toward zero.

Solution

The change in inventory is \(\Delta I_t = -1\) with prob \(P_{\text{buy}}(\delta)\) and \(+1\) with prob \(P_{\text{sell}}(\delta)\). Then \(\E[\Delta I_t \mid \delta] = (-1)P_{\text{buy}} + (+1)P_{\text{sell}} = P_{\text{sell}} - P_{\text{buy}} = \frac{1}{2}\delta\). When \(I > 0\), \(\delta^*(I) < 0 \implies \E[\Delta I_t] < 0\). When \(I < 0\), \(\delta^*(I) > 0 \implies \E[\Delta I_t] > 0\). Hence inventory is mean-reverting.

Empirical

E1 · Reading Lab 1 value function values

From Lab 1 output, report the period 0 value function \(V_0(I)\) at \(I = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2\) in exact fractions and decimal equivalents.

Solution

Lab 1 prints: \(V_0(0) = 1\) (\(= 1.0000\)); \(V_0(1) = V_0(-1) = 29/16\) (\(= 1.8125\)); \(V_0(2) = V_0(-2) = 127/32\) (\(= 3.9688\)). The values are strictly symmetric and strictly convex in \(I\).

E2 · Quote shifts in period 1 vs period 0

Compare the period 1 and period 0 optimal policies from Lab 1 for \(I = +2\). Why does \(\delta_1^*(2) = -1\) while \(\delta_0^*(2) = -1/2\)?

Solution

In period 1 (one period before terminal liquidation), terminal penalty \(I^2 = 4\) is imminent, so the dealer shades quotes aggressively (\(\delta = -1\)) to shed inventory immediately. In period 0, with two periods remaining, the dealer can afford a moderate skew (\(\delta = -1/2\)) without sacrificing spread revenue as drastically.

E3 · Probability of inventory reduction

Under the optimal policy \(\delta_0^*(1) = -1\) from Lab 1, what is the exact probability that the next trade reduces inventory to 0?

Solution

The probability of a buy order under \(\delta = -1\) is \(P_{\text{buy}}(-1) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4}(-1) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75\). The dealer has a 75% chance of returning to neutral inventory on the next trade.

Research

R1 · Continuous-time Ho–Stoll limit

In the continuous-time Ho–Stoll (1981) model, the mid-quote shift is \(\delta(I) \approx -\frac{1}{2} \gamma \sigma^2 I (T - t)\). Explain the roles of risk aversion \(\gamma\), volatility \(\sigma^2\), and remaining time \(T-t\).

Solution

The product \(\gamma \sigma^2\) measures the dollar penalty per unit of inventory variance. Remaining time \(T-t\) scales total cumulative diffusion risk exposure. A dealer with longer time until market close or operating in more volatile assets shades quotes more aggressively per unit of inventory.

R2 · Inventory bounds in high-frequency algorithmic market making

How do modern automated market makers enforce hard inventory limits (e.g. \(|I_t| \le K_{\max}\)) when order arrivals are stochastic?

Solution

Modern HFT market makers use a combination of asymmetric quoting (pulling bids entirely or quoting at the back of the queue when long) and active hedging (routing aggressive market orders to lit exchanges or cross-market venues to dump inventory when crossing threshold bounds).

R3 · Interaction between inventory control and price impact

If trades convey adverse selection signals, why does an inventory-induced price drop risk being misinterpreted by the market as fundamental bad news?

Solution

Market participants observing declining transaction prices cannot cleanly separate whether prices fall because informed traders possess negative information or because a distressed dealer is liquidating inventory. This signal confusion can trigger predatory trading and feedback selling.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. When a market maker accumulates a positive inventory position (\(I_t > 0\)), how should quotes be adjusted?

    Options

    Lowering quotes makes the ask more attractive to buyers and the bid less attractive to sellers, encouraging buys that reduce inventory.

  2. By Proposition 3.3, what is the optimal quote shift \(\delta^*(0)\) when the dealer holds zero inventory?

    Anti-symmetry requires \(\delta^*(0) = -\delta^*(0) \implies \delta^*(0) = 0\).

  3. What happens to the full bid–ask spread \(A_t - B_t\) when the dealer applies a uniform quote shift \(\delta_t\)?

    Options

    \(A_t - B_t = (m_t + c_0 + \delta_t) - (m_t - c_0 + \delta_t) = 2c_0\). The spread is invariant to midpoint shifts.

  4. Under Assumption A3, if the dealer sets \(\delta = -1\), what is the probability of a buyer arrival \(P_{\text{buy}}\)?

    \(P_{\text{buy}} = 1/2 - (1/4)(-1) = 0.50 + 0.25 = 0.75\).

  5. Why is the dealer's value function symmetric (\(V_t(I) = V_t(-I)\))?

    Options

    The quadratic cost \(\frac{1}{2} a I^2\) and symmetric order arrival probabilities treat long and short deviations identically.

  6. Under Assumption A3 with \(c_0 = 0.50\), what is the expected spread revenue \(R(\delta)\) when \(\delta = 0\)?

    \(R(0) = c_0 - \frac{1}{2}(0)^2 = 0.50\).

  7. Which mathematical framework guarantees that backward induction solves the inventory quote-setting problem?

    Options

    Dynamic programming by backward induction relies on Bellman's principle of optimality.

  8. In Lab 1, what is the exact value function \(V_0(0)\) at zero inventory?

    Lab 1 computes \(V_0(0) = 1.0\) (or Fraction(1, 1)).
