Quantitative backtesting assumes that simulated historical investment decisions reflect reality. In practice, modern databases silently overwrite historical records when corporations restate earnings or government agencies revise macroeconomic statistics. A model that reads today's snapshot of historical fundamentals trains on numbers that no trader could have known at the simulated decision date. This look-ahead leakage creates fictional alpha signals that collapse in live deployment. Point-in-time architecture preserves every vintage of historical data, guaranteeing that simulated information sets match historical reality bit for bit.

1Bitemporal Data Architecture

Building on the temporal mechanics of the ASOF join, point-in-time architecture requires distinguishing event dates from knowledge dates.

Definition 5.1 (Point-in-Time Data and Bitemporal Schema)

Point-in-time data is a data management discipline ensuring that any historical state reconstructed at simulated decision time \(t\) contains strictly the information published, disseminated, and recorded at or prior to \(t\). A bitemporal schema tracks two distinct time dimensions for every observation:

  1. Valid Time (\(t_v\), Event Time): The real-world date or period to which the measurement applies (e.g. Q1 GDP or fiscal year earnings).
  2. Transaction Time (\(t_x\), Knowledge Time): The exact timestamp at which the data value was released by the issuer and ingested into the database.
An observation is denoted \(X(t_v, t_x)\). If an issuer subsequently issues a revision, a new record \(X(t_v, t'_x)\) is appended with \(t'_x > t_x\) without mutating the prior vintage.

Remark 5.1 (Point-in-Time Query Predicate)

A point-in-time query evaluated at simulated strategy time \(t\) requires evaluating: \[ \text{val}(t_v; t) = \operatorname{arg\,max}_{t_x \le t} \{X(t_v, t_x) : t_v \le t \land t_x \le t\}. \] Both inequalities are essential: \(t_v \le t\) ensures the underlying economic event occurred, and \(t_x \le t\) ensures the observation had been officially published and retrieved.

2Point-in-Time Conditioning and Filtration

The mathematical foundation of empirical quantitative research relies on strict filtration conditioning.

Theorem 5.1 (Point-in-Time Information Conditioning)

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \mathbb{P})\) be the historical market filtration. Define the point-in-time empirical information set at time \(t\) as the sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra: \[ \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{PIT}} = \sigma\left(\{X(t_v, t_x) : t_v \le t \land t_x \le t\}\right). \] Then for any decision rule or estimator \(\hat{\theta}_t = g(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{PIT}})\): (1) \(\hat{\theta}_t\) is strictly \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-adapted; and (2) for any subsequent revision \(X(t_v, t'_x)\) released at \(t'_x > t\), \(\hat{\theta}_t\) is statistically independent of the revision innovation \(\epsilon = X(t_v, t'_x) - X(t_v, t_x)\) conditional on \(\mathcal{F}_t\).

Proof

By Definition 5.1, the generating set for \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{PIT}}\) consists exclusively of observations whose transaction time \(t_x\) satisfies \(t_x \le t\). Because real-world information arrival is non-decreasing, all events occurring at or prior to \(t\) belong to \(\mathcal{F}_t\). Therefore, \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{PIT}} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t\), establishing that any \(g(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{PIT}})\) is \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-adapted. For (2), the future revision \(X(t_v, t'_x)\) occurs at \(t'_x > t\). Under the definition of the filtration, \(\epsilon\) is measurable with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_{t'_x}\) but strictly unmeasurable with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_t\). Because \(\hat{\theta}_t\) is \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{PIT}}\)-measurable and \(\mathcal{F}_t^{\text{PIT}} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t\), conditioning on \(\mathcal{F}_t\) renders \(\hat{\theta}_t\) a known constant. A constant is trivially independent of any random variable, proving conditional independence.

Example 5.1 (Macroeconomic Revision Cycles)

The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) releases GDP estimates in three sequential vintages for each quarter: the "advance" estimate (one month after quarter end), the "second" estimate (two months after), and the "third" estimate (three months after), followed by annual comprehensive benchmark revisions. A strategy deciding asset allocation on May 15, 2020 must observe the April 30 advance estimate, not the June 25 third revision.

Lab 1 · Point-in-Time Data and Look-Ahead Bias Prevention

Verifies that point-in-time queries over a simulated macroeconomic revision history strictly prevent look-ahead bias across decision dates, while naive terminal-value queries contaminate 80% of evaluation dates.

"""CS3 Lesson 5 Inline Lab 1: Point-in-time data and revision history.

Demonstrates bitemporal point-in-time reads versus naive latest-value reads.
Counts look-ahead observations and measures distortion across historical decision dates.
Data: SIMULATED GDP growth revision history.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import NamedTuple


class MacroRevision(NamedTuple):
    ref_period: str
    release_date: str
    value_pct: float
    vintage: str


def get_point_in_time_value(revisions: list[MacroRevision], ref_period: str, as_of_date: str) -> float | None:
    """Return the most recent observation known as of as_of_date (release_date <= as_of_date)."""
    candidates = [r for r in revisions if r.ref_period == ref_period and r.release_date <= as_of_date]
    if not candidates:
        return None
    latest = max(candidates, key=lambda r: r.release_date)
    return latest.value_pct


def get_naive_latest_value(revisions: list[MacroRevision], ref_period: str) -> float | None:
    """Naive lookup returning the terminal revision regardless of decision date (look-ahead)."""
    candidates = [r for r in revisions if r.ref_period == ref_period]
    if not candidates:
        return None
    latest = max(candidates, key=lambda r: r.release_date)
    return latest.value_pct


def main() -> None:
    # SIMULATED macroeconomic revision history
    revisions = [
        MacroRevision("2020-Q1", "2020-04-30", 1.2, "advance"),
        MacroRevision("2020-Q1", "2020-05-28", 1.5, "second"),
        MacroRevision("2020-Q1", "2020-06-25", 1.4, "third"),
        MacroRevision("2020-Q2", "2020-07-30", -5.0, "advance"),
        MacroRevision("2020-Q2", "2020-08-27", -4.8, "second"),
        MacroRevision("2020-Q2", "2020-09-24", -4.9, "third"),
    ]

    decision_dates = [
        ("2020-04-15", "2020-Q1"),  # Before advance release -> None
        ("2020-05-15", "2020-Q1"),  # Between advance and second -> 1.2%
        ("2020-06-15", "2020-Q1"),  # Between second and third -> 1.5%
        ("2020-07-15", "2020-Q1"),  # After third release -> 1.4%
        ("2020-08-15", "2020-Q2"),  # Between advance and second for Q2 -> -5.0%
    ]

    lookahead_count = 0
    total_evals = len(decision_dates)

    print(f"Total historical revisions in database: {len(revisions)}")
    for decision_date, period in decision_dates:
        pit_val = get_point_in_time_value(revisions, period, decision_date)
        naive_val = get_naive_latest_value(revisions, period)

        is_lookahead = pit_val != naive_val
        if is_lookahead:
            lookahead_count += 1

        pit_str = f"{pit_val:+.1f}%" if pit_val is not None else "None"
        naive_str = f"{naive_val:+.1f}%" if naive_val is not None else "None"
        print(
            f"Decision Date: {decision_date} (Period: {period}) -> "
            f"PIT: {pit_str:>6} | Naive Latest: {naive_str:>6} | Look-ahead: {is_lookahead}"
        )

    print(f"Total evaluation dates: {total_evals}")
    print(f"Look-ahead contaminated dates: {lookahead_count} of {total_evals}")
    print(f"Look-ahead rate: {lookahead_count / total_evals:.1%}")

    # Invariants verification
    assert lookahead_count == 4
    print("Point-in-time discipline successfully prevented 4 look-ahead leaks: True")

3Where the Canon Breaks

Point-in-time data systems face practical limitations in real-world quant environments:

  • Filing Timestamp versus Ingestion Latency: An SEC EDGAR 10-Q filing stamped 16:00:00 EST on Tuesday is not immediately readable by an algorithm. Parsing XBRL, mapping entity identifiers, and updating database tables takes seconds or minutes. Assuming instantaneous availability at the exact filing timestamp introduces microsecond-level look-ahead bias.
  • Silent Retroactive Web Scrapes: Financial datasets acquired by periodic web scraping often lack formal publication timestamps. If a data vendor retroactively overwrites a web table, an analyst scraping today captures an unversioned mix of current and revised numbers.
  • Storage Explosion of Full History: Retaining every tick, quote, and revision permanently in an append-only store requires orders of magnitude more storage than maintaining a mutable latest-state database, requiring tiered cold storage and columnar compression.

4Misconceptions

  1. Using historical observation dates (event dates) is sufficient to prevent look-ahead bias.

    Observation dates indicate when economic activity occurred, not when the number was released. Quarterly GDP for Q1 occurred in January–March, but was first published at the end of April. Using the observation date allows strategies to trade in February on numbers published in late April.

  2. Restated earnings are more accurate, so models trained on restated numbers make better predictions.

    Restated numbers are more accurate historical records, but they were physically unavailable on the historical trading day. Training a model on restated numbers fits coefficients to future information that will not exist when trading out-of-sample live.

  3. A point-in-time database can be constructed by running UPDATE statements on corrected rows.

    Running SQL UPDATE destroys previous vintages, erasing the historical record of what was known. Point-in-time databases must be strictly append-only, inserting new revisions as new rows with distinct transaction timestamps.

5Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An accounting metric for 2020-Q1 has three recorded revisions: (1) released 2020-04-30 with value 100; (2) released 2020-06-15 with value 105; (3) released 2020-10-01 with value 102. State the value returned by a point-in-time query at: (a) 2020-05-15; (b) 2020-07-01; and (c) 2020-04-15.

Solution

(a) At 2020-05-15, only revision 1 has occurred (\(t_x \le \text{2020-05-15}\)), so value is 100. (b) At 2020-07-01, revisions 1 and 2 qualify; maximal \(t_x\) is revision 2, so value is 105. (c) At 2020-04-15, no revision has occurred yet, so query returns None (NULL).

Explain the difference between valid time and transaction time in a corporate 10-K earnings filing.

Solution

Valid time is the fiscal period covered by the financial report (e.g., the fiscal year ending 2020-12-31). Transaction time is the exact date and time the 10-K was formally filed with the SEC EDGAR system and published to the public (e.g., 2021-02-25T16:05:00Z).

A trader queries a non-point-in-time corporate fundamentals table on 2020-03-01 and sees 2019 Net Income of \$500M. The 10-K was filed on 2020-03-15. How many days of look-ahead leakage occurred?

Solution

The information was made available to the simulated strategy on 2020-03-01, but was not officially filed until 2020-03-15. The query introduces \(15 - 1 = 14\) calendar days of look-ahead bias.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that any database schema supporting full point-in-time query reconstruction must be append-only with respect to historical observations.

Solution

Suppose for contradiction that a mutation operator \(U\) overwrites an existing observation \(X(t_v, t_x)\) with updated value \(X'(t_v)\) at time \(t_{\text{mut}} > t_x\). After the update, the original value \(X(t_v, t_x)\) is deleted from physical storage. Now consider a point-in-time query evaluating at decision time \(\tau\) where \(t_x \le \tau < t_{\text{mut}}\). To reconstruct state at \(\tau\), the query requires evaluating \(X(t_v, t_x)\). Since that value no longer exists in storage, the historical state cannot be reconstructed. Hence, physical record retention must be strictly monotonic (append-only).

Prove that if revision errors \(\epsilon_t = X(t_v, t'_x) - X(t_v, t_x)\) have positive correlation with subsequent asset returns \(R_{t+1}\), training on non-PIT terminal data positively biases the estimated Sharpe ratio.

Solution

Under terminal data, the signal evaluated at \(t\) is \(S_{\text{term}} = X(t_v, t'_x) = X(t_v, t_x) + \epsilon_t\). If trading position is proportional to signal \(w_t = k S_{\text{term}}\), strategy return is \(r_{p, t+1} = k (X(t_v, t_x) + \epsilon_t) R_{t+1}\). The expected portfolio return is \(\mathbb{E}[r_{p}] = k \mathbb{E}[X R] + k \operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_t, R_{t+1})\). Since \(\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_t, R_{t+1}) > 0\), the simulated expected return strictly exceeds the true attainable return \(k \mathbb{E}[X R]\), positively biasing the sample Sharpe ratio.

Prove that point-in-time ASOF queries are idempotent: querying the same key and timestamp \((K, t)\) on an immutable store always yields the identical result.

Solution

In an immutable store, the set of observations \(\mathcal{S} = \{X_i\}\) is fixed. A point-in-time query for \((K, t)\) evaluates the deterministic selection predicate \(\theta(X) = (X.K = K \land X.t_v \le t \land X.t_x \le t)\) and selects the maximal record according to a fixed total ordering (timestamp descending, sequence descending). Because \(\mathcal{S}\) is immutable and the ordering relation is a strict total order, the maximal element exists and is unique. Thus, \(\text{query}(K, t)\) is a deterministic pure function of \((\mathcal{S}, K, t)\), guaranteeing idempotence.

Tier 3 · Empirical

A quantitative macroeconomic momentum strategy claims an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.80 backtested over 20 years using FRED terminal historical series. When re-evaluated using point-in-time ALFRED vintages, the true Sharpe ratio drops to 0.60. Quantify the proportion of strategy performance generated by look-ahead bias.

Solution

The true economic Sharpe ratio of the strategy is 0.60, while 1.80 was reported. The performance attributable to look-ahead bias is \(1.80 - 0.60 = 1.20\). As a proportion of total reported performance, look-ahead bias generated \(1.20 / 1.80 = 0.667\) or 66.7% of the backtest's purported Sharpe ratio.

A database holds 5 years of daily financial metrics across 1,000 tickers. If each ticker experiences an average of 4 corporate financial restatements per year, calculate the total number of records in a point-in-time bitemporal table versus a mutable single-state table.

Solution

A single-state mutable table stores only the current state: \(5 \text{ years} \times 252 \text{ trading days} \times 1{,}000 \text{ tickers} = 1{,}260{,}000\) records. In a bitemporal table, initial observations contribute 1,260,000 records. Restatements add \(5 \text{ years} \times 4 \text{ revisions} \times 1{,}000 \text{ tickers} = 20{,}000\) additional revision records. Total bitemporal records = \(1{,}260{,}000 + 20{,}000 = 1{,}280{,}000\) records (a 1.59% increase in row storage).

An equity analyst calculates the historical Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio on 2020-02-01. The company reported EPS of \$4.00 on 2020-02-15 for the year ending 2019-12-31. The previous available EPS from 2018 was \$3.20. What EPS value must a point-in-time calculation use, and what percentage error is avoided?

Solution

On 2020-02-01, the 2019 EPS was not yet published. The point-in-time calculation must use the prevailing published EPS of \$3.20. Using \$4.00 would overstate available earnings by \((\$4.00 - \$3.20) / \$3.20 = 0.25\) or 25.0%, creating an erroneous valuation signal.

Tier 4 · Research

Analyze why survivorship bias and look-ahead bias frequently compound each other when quantitative backtests select historical index constituent universes from current index memberships.

Solution

When an analyst selects current S&P 500 members and backtests their returns over the past 20 years, two lethal biases compound: (1) Survivorship bias: companies that went bankrupt, merged, or were delisted during the 20 years are excluded, conditioning the sample on multi-decade survival; (2) Look-ahead bias: membership in today's index reflects market capitalization growth that occurred after the historical trade date. Point-in-time index reconstitution tables (historical constituent memberships by date) are mandatory to eliminate both biases simultaneously.

Evaluate the design of an append-only bitemporal relational schema using surrogate primary keys versus composite natural keys for high-throughput corporate action processing.

Solution

A composite natural key (ticker, valid_date, transaction_timestamp) enforces natural uniqueness but requires multi-column index leaf traversals and suffers from foreign key migration headaches when corporate actions change tickers (e.g., CUSIP renaming or ticker reuse). A surrogate autoincrement primary key (seq INTEGER PRIMARY KEY) guarantees monotonic insertion ordering, simplifies foreign key joins, and allows deterministic tie-breaking on ingestion sequence (\(\text{seq}\)) while maintaining an auxiliary composite index over (ticker, valid_date, transaction_timestamp) for range lookups.

How does SEC Rule 10b-5 insider trading liability conceptually relate to the mathematical requirement of filtration measurability in quantitative research?

Solution

SEC Rule 10b-5 prohibits trading on material non-public information (MNPI)—information that has occurred in reality but has not been publicly disseminated to the marketplace. Mathematically, an event has occurred in the real world at valid time \(t_v\), but public dissemination occurs at transaction time \(t_x > t_v\). Trading on \(X(t_v)\) when \(t < t_x\) is both a legal violation of insider trading regulations and a mathematical violation of filtration measurability (\(X \notin \mathcal{F}_t^{\text{public}}\)). Point-in-time data discipline enforces that quantitative algorithms trade strictly on public filtration sets.

7Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the distinction between valid time and transaction time in bitemporal data?

    Options

    Valid time represents the real-world occurrence period; transaction time represents database recording/publication time.

  2. What condition must be satisfied for an observation X(t_v, t_x) to be visible to a point-in-time query at time t?

    Options

    Both inequalities are mandatory: the event must have occurred (\(t_v \le t\)) and been recorded/published (\(t_x \le t\)).

  3. Why does using SQL UPDATE statements on historical records break point-in-time reproducibility?

    Options

    UPDATE destroys the historical record of what was previously known, rendering historical reconstruction impossible.

  4. How many temporal dimensions does a standard bitemporal schema track for each record?

    Bitemporal schemas track exactly 2 dimensions: valid time and transaction time.

  5. What type of bias is introduced when a 2018 corporate earnings figure is backtested using restated numbers published in 2021?

    Options

    Trading on future restatements incorporates future information not available on the historical trade date (look-ahead bias).

  6. What does ALFRED provide relative to standard FRED macroeconomic data?

    Options

    ALFRED (Archival Federal Reserve Economic Data) stores point-in-time vintages of economic series as published over time.

  7. In an append-only store with multiple revisions of an observation, which revision wins in a point-in-time query at time t?

    Options

    A point-in-time query selects the most recent information available as of \(t\) (\(\max t_x \le t\)).

  8. What bias occurs when a historical stock screener selects only companies currently listed in the S&P 500?

    Options

    Selecting only companies that survived to the present excludes bankrupt or acquired firms, inducing survivorship bias.
