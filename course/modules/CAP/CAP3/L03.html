Field Prelim II: Cross-Sectional & Time-Series Econometrics

Field Prelim II evaluates advanced econometric foundations, asymptotic theory, and structural hypothesis testing across empirical asset pricing and macro-econometrics. Candidates must formulate non-linear Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) estimators, derive efficient weighting matrices, and evaluate overidentifying restrictions via the Hansen J-test. In the cross-section, candidates must master Fama-MacBeth regressions, correct for generated regressor errors-in-variables via Shanken asymptotic expansions, and execute finite-sample Hotelling tests via the Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS) statistic. This lesson develops the econometric derivations tested on doctoral field prelims.

1. Generalized Method of Moments & Overidentifying Diagnostics

The Generalized Method of Moments provides the unified econometric framework for estimating dynamic macroeconomic and asset pricing models.

Let \(\{z_t\}_{t=1}^T\) denote an observed stationary ergodic stochastic process. A structural economic model implies a set of \(m\) moment conditions depending on a \(k\)-dimensional parameter vector \(\theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^k\) (with \(m \ge k\)):

\[ \E[g(z_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}_m \]

Theorem 3.1 (Hansen 1982 Asymptotic Normality & Efficient GMM) Let \(g_T(\theta) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T g(z_t, \theta)\) denote the sample moment vector, and let \(W_T \xrightarrow{p} W\) be a positive definite \(m \times m\) weighting matrix. The GMM estimator \(\hat{\theta}_T = \arg\min_{\theta} g_T(\theta)^\top W_T g_T(\theta)\) satisfies: \[ \sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0}_k, (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}\right) \] where \(D = \E\left[ \frac{\partial g(z_t, \theta_0)}{\partial \theta^\top} \right]\) has full column rank \(k\), and \(S = \sum_{j=-\infty}^\infty \E[g(z_t, \theta_0) g(z_{t-j}, \theta_0)^\top]\) is the spectral density matrix at frequency zero. The optimal weighting matrix setting asymptotic variance to its minimum bound is \(W^* = S^{-1}\), yielding: \[ \operatorname{Avar}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{eff}}) = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} \]

Proof The first-order condition for the minimization of \(g_T(\theta)^\top W_T g_T(\theta)\) is: \[ D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T g_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = \mathbf{0}_k \] where \(D_T(\theta) = \frac{\partial g_T(\theta)}{\partial \theta^\top}\). Expanding \(g_T(\hat{\theta}_T)\) around the true parameter value \(\theta_0\) via the Mean Value Theorem: \[ g_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = g_T(\theta_0) + D_T(\bar{\theta}) (\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) \] for some \(\bar{\theta}\) on the line segment connecting \(\hat{\theta}_T\) and \(\theta_0\). Substituting into the first-order condition: \[ D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T \left[ g_T(\theta_0) + D_T(\bar{\theta}) (\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) \right] = \mathbf{0}_k \] Rearranging terms: \[ \left[ D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T D_T(\bar{\theta}) \right] (\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) = -D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T g_T(\theta_0) \] Multiplying by \(\sqrt{T}\) and inverting the leading matrix: \[ \sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) = -\left[ D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T D_T(\bar{\theta}) \right]^{-1} D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T \left[ \sqrt{T} g_T(\theta_0) \right] \] By the Central Limit Theorem for stationary ergodic processes, \(\sqrt{T} g_T(\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, S)\). By consistency and the Uniform Law of Large Numbers, \(D_T(\hat{\theta}_T) \xrightarrow{p} D, D_T(\bar{\theta}) \xrightarrow{p} D\), and \(W_T \xrightarrow{p} W\). Applying Slutsky's theorem yields the asymptotic normality result. Substituting \(W = S^{-1}\) verifies the optimal covariance bound \((D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\). This completes the derivation.

Theorem 3.2 (Hansen 1982 J-Test of Overidentifying Restrictions) When \(m > k\) and the efficient weighting matrix estimator \(\hat{S}_T^{-1}\) is utilized, the minimized criterion function: \[ J_T = T g_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top \hat{S}_T^{-1} g_T(\hat{\theta}_T) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(m - k) \] under the null hypothesis that all \(m\) moment restrictions \(\E[g(z_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}\) are simultaneously valid.

The Hansen J-test is a universal diagnostic in doctoral asset pricing prelims. Failure of the J-test indicates that no structural parameter vector \(\theta\) can reconcile the empirical pricing moments across the test asset universe.

2. Fama-MacBeth Regressions & The Shanken Correction

In cross-sectional asset pricing, linear factor models hypothesize that expected returns are linear in factor loadings: \(\E[R_i] = \beta_i^\top \lambda\). The Fama-MacBeth two-pass regression estimates factor risk premia \(\lambda\) across time and assets.

Let \(R_{i,t}\) denote the excess return on asset \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) and \(f_t\) denote a \(K\)-dimensional factor vector at time \(t \in \{1, \dots, T\}\).

  • Pass 1 (Time-Series OLS): For each asset \(i\), estimate factor loadings: \[ R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i^\top f_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \quad t = 1, \dots, T \] Stack estimated loadings into the \(N \times K\) matrix \(\hat{\beta}\).
  • Pass 2 (Cross-Sectional Regression): At each period \(t\), run a cross-sectional regression across all \(N\) assets: \[ R_{i,t} = \hat{\beta}_i^\top \lambda_t + u_{i,t}, \quad i = 1, \dots, N \] The point estimator is the time-series mean: \(\hat{\lambda} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \hat{\lambda}_t = (\hat{\beta}^\top \hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta}^\top \bar{R}\).

Theorem 3.3 (Shanken 1992 Asymptotic Standard Error Correction) Let \(\hat{\beta}\) be the first-pass OLS loading matrix with residual covariance matrix \(\Sigma_\varepsilon = \Cov(\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_t)\), and let \(\Sigma_f = \Cov(f_t, f_t)\) be the factor covariance matrix. Under the null hypothesis that the beta-pricing model holds with risk premium \(\lambda\), the asymptotic variance of the two-pass estimator \(\hat{\lambda}\) is: \[ \operatorname{Avar}(\hat{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{T} \left( \Sigma_f + c_{\text{shanken}} (\beta^\top \beta)^{-1} \beta^\top \Sigma_\varepsilon \beta (\beta^\top \beta)^{-1} \right) \] where the Shanken correction factor is: \[ c_{\text{shanken}} = 1 + \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda > 1.0 \]

Proof The second-pass estimator uses estimated regressors \(\hat{\beta} = \beta + \Delta \beta\), where \(\Delta \beta\) represents first-pass estimation noise. Decomposing the estimator: \[ \hat{\lambda} - \lambda = (\hat{\beta}^\top \hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta}^\top \bar{R} - \lambda \] Substituting \(\bar{R} = \beta \lambda + \alpha + \bar{\varepsilon}\), with \(\alpha = \mathbf{0}\) under the null: \[ \hat{\lambda} - \lambda = (\hat{\beta}^\top \hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta}^\top (\beta \lambda + \bar{\varepsilon}) - \lambda = (\hat{\beta}^\top \hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta}^\top \bar{\varepsilon} - (\hat{\beta}^\top \hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta}^\top (\hat{\beta} - \beta) \lambda \] Using asymptotic expansions, the term \((\hat{\beta} - \beta) \lambda = \Delta \beta \lambda\) contributes additional sampling variance that correlates with factor realizations. Shanken (1992) proves via delta-method asymptotics that the covariance between first-pass loading estimation error and second-pass residuals inflates the residual variance component by precisely the quadratic form \(\lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\). Summing the pure factor variation \(\frac{1}{T} \Sigma_f\) with the inflated regressor error establishes the corrected variance formula. This completes the proof.

3. The GRS Test & Multiple-Testing Factor Zoos

While Fama-MacBeth tests factor premia \(\lambda\), the Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS 1989) test evaluates whether candidate factor portfolios span the mean-variance frontier by testing joint significance of time-series alphas: \(H_0: \alpha_1 = \dots = \alpha_N = 0\).

Theorem 3.4 (Gibbons-Ross-Shanken 1989 Statistic) Assuming normal, independently and identically distributed residuals \(\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma_\varepsilon)\), the exact finite-sample test statistic is: \[ GRS = \frac{T - N - K}{N} \frac{\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha}}{1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}} \sim F(N, T - N - K) \] where \(\bar{f}\) is the sample mean factor vector, and \(\hat{\Sigma}_f\) is the unadjusted maximum likelihood factor covariance matrix.

Remark 3.1 (Multiple-Testing Hurdles & The Cochrane Factor Zoo) Over 400 equity anomalies were published between 1970 and 2015. Under the standard \(t \ge 1.96\) significance threshold, search over 400 candidate variables produces dozens of spurious discoveries purely through Type I error. In their influential synthesis, Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) demonstrated that controlling the false discovery rate across the literature requires raising the anomaly discovery threshold to \(t \ge 3.00\) (\(p < 0.0027\)). An empirical doctoral qualifying exam submission citing \(t = 2.05\) as conclusive evidence of a new risk factor fails the doctoral evidence standard.

Lab 1 · Two-Step Efficient GMM & Hansen J-Test

Estimating non-linear Euler equations verifies that efficient two-step GMM weighting converges to optimal parameter values and generates a valid Hansen J-test statistic.

"""Lab CAP3-L03-1: Field Prelim II, Problem A: Two-Step Efficient GMM & Hansen J-Test.

Estimates consumption Euler equation parameters via two-step GMM and tests
overidentifying restrictions using the Hansen (1982) J-test.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.stats import chi2


def main() -> None:
    # 1. Simulate consumption growth and gross return instruments
    np.random.seed(101)
    t_obs = 300
    true_beta = 0.98
    true_gamma = 2.5

    # Simulated log consumption growth and gross asset returns
    gc = np.random.normal(0.015, 0.020, size=t_obs)
    # R gross satisfying E[beta * (C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-gamma} * R_{t+1} - 1 | Z_t] = 0
    c_growth = np.exp(gc)
    sdf_true = true_beta * (c_growth ** (-true_gamma))
    r_equity = (1.0 + np.random.normal(0.025, 0.05, size=t_obs)) / np.mean(sdf_true)
    r_bond = (1.0 + np.random.normal(0.008, 0.02, size=t_obs)) / np.mean(sdf_true)

    # 4 moment conditions: [R_e * m - 1, R_b * m - 1, (R_e * m - 1) * z_t, (R_b * m - 1) * z_t]
    z_inst = np.random.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=t_obs)

    def moment_func(params: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        b, g = params
        m = b * (c_growth ** (-g))
        e1 = r_equity * m - 1.0
        e2 = r_bond * m - 1.0
        m1 = e1
        m2 = e2
        m3 = e1 * z_inst
        m4 = e2 * z_inst
        return np.column_stack([m1, m2, m3, m4])  # T x 4

    # Step 1: First-stage GMM with identity weighting
    w1 = np.eye(4)

    def gmm_obj(params: np.ndarray, w_mat: np.ndarray) -> float:
        f = moment_func(params)
        g_bar = np.mean(f, axis=0)
        return float(g_bar @ w_mat @ g_bar)

    res1 = minimize(gmm_obj, np.array([0.95, 2.0]), args=(w1,), method="Nelder-Mead")
    theta1 = res1.x

    # Step 2: Compute spectral density matrix S and efficient weighting matrix W = S^{-1}
    f_res1 = moment_func(theta1)
    s_mat = (f_res1.T @ f_res1) / float(t_obs)
    w_eff = np.linalg.inv(s_mat)

    res2 = minimize(gmm_obj, theta1, args=(w_eff,), method="Nelder-Mead")
    theta_eff = res2.x

    # Step 3: Hansen J-test
    f_res2 = moment_func(theta_eff)
    g_bar_eff = np.mean(f_res2, axis=0)
    j_stat = float(t_obs * (g_bar_eff @ w_eff @ g_bar_eff))
    df = 4 - 2  # 4 moments, 2 parameters
    p_val = float(chi2.sf(j_stat, df))

    print(f"Sample Size (T): {t_obs}")
    print(f"First-Stage Estimates: beta = {theta1[0]:.4f}, gamma = {theta1[1]:.4f}")
    print(f"Efficient Two-Step Estimates: beta = {theta_eff[0]:.4f}, gamma = {theta_eff[1]:.4f}")
    print(f"Hansen J-Statistic: {j_stat:.4f} (df = {df})")
    print(f"J-Test p-value: {p_val:.4f} (Model Valid: {p_val > 0.05})")


main()
Sample Size (T): 300
First-Stage Estimates: beta = 0.9461, gamma = 1.2704
Efficient Two-Step Estimates: beta = 0.9402, gamma = 0.5464
Hansen J-Statistic: 25.7062 (df = 2)
J-Test p-value: 0.0000 (Model Valid: False)

The two-step GMM procedure estimates beta = 0.9402 and gamma = 0.5464. The Hansen J-statistic of 25.7062 with df = 2 yields p = 0.0000, rejecting the overidentifying restrictions of the simplified power utility specification.

Lab 2 · Fama-MacBeth Two-Pass Regression & Shanken Correction

Comparing naive OLS standard errors against the Shanken correction demonstrates that factor risk premium standard errors expand by the quadratic factor curvature factor.

"""Lab CAP3-L03-2: Field Prelim II, Problem B: Fama-MacBeth & Shanken Correction.

Performs two-pass cross-sectional regression, computes the Shanken (1992)
asymptotic standard error adjustment, and evaluates the GRS (1989) test statistic.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    np.random.seed(202)
    t_obs = 180
    n_assets = 5
    n_factors = 2

    # Simulate factor series and test assets
    factor_means = np.array([0.007, 0.003])
    factor_cov = np.array([[0.0020, 0.0004], [0.0004, 0.0016]])
    factors = np.random.multivariate_normal(factor_means, factor_cov, size=t_obs)

    true_betas = np.array([[0.8, -0.1], [1.1, 0.3], [0.9, 0.0], [1.2, -0.2], [0.7, 0.4]])
    true_alphas = np.array([0.001, -0.001, 0.0005, 0.002, -0.0015])
    noise = np.random.normal(0.0, 0.02, size=(t_obs, n_assets))
    returns = np.zeros((t_obs, n_assets))
    for t in range(t_obs):
        returns[t] = true_alphas + true_betas @ factors[t] + noise[t]

    # Pass 1: Time-series OLS to estimate factor loadings
    x_mat = np.column_stack([np.ones(t_obs), factors])
    x_inv = np.linalg.inv(x_mat.T @ x_mat)
    alphas = np.zeros(n_assets)
    betas = np.zeros((n_assets, n_factors))
    residuals = np.zeros((t_obs, n_assets))

    for i in range(n_assets):
        y = returns[:, i]
        coeff = x_inv @ (x_mat.T @ y)
        alphas[i] = coeff[0]
        betas[i, :] = coeff[1:]
        residuals[:, i] = y - x_mat @ coeff

    # Covariance matrices
    sigma_eps = (residuals.T @ residuals) / float(t_obs)
    f_bar = np.mean(factors, axis=0)
    f_dev = factors - f_bar
    sigma_f = (f_dev.T @ f_dev) / float(t_obs)
    sigma_f_inv = np.linalg.inv(sigma_f)

    # Pass 2: Cross-sectional regression of mean returns on betas
    mean_ret = np.mean(returns, axis=0)
    beta_cov_inv = np.linalg.inv(betas.T @ betas)
    lambda_premia = beta_cov_inv @ (betas.T @ mean_ret)

    # Naive vs Shanken standard errors
    c_shanken = float(1.0 + lambda_premia @ sigma_f_inv @ lambda_premia)
    v_naive = (1.0 / float(t_obs)) * (sigma_f + (beta_cov_inv @ (betas.T @ sigma_eps @ betas) @ beta_cov_inv))
    v_shanken = (1.0 / float(t_obs)) * (
        sigma_f + c_shanken * (beta_cov_inv @ (betas.T @ sigma_eps @ betas) @ beta_cov_inv)
    )

    se_naive = np.sqrt(np.diag(v_naive))
    se_shanken = np.sqrt(np.diag(v_shanken))

    # GRS (1989) statistic
    eps_inv = np.linalg.inv(sigma_eps)
    alpha_form = float(alphas @ eps_inv @ alphas)
    curvature = float(1.0 + f_bar @ sigma_f_inv @ f_bar)
    grs_stat = float(((t_obs - n_assets - n_factors) / n_assets) * (alpha_form / curvature))

    print(
        f"Factor 1 Risk Premium: {lambda_premia[0]:.4f} | Naive SE: {se_naive[0]:.4f} | Shanken SE: {se_shanken[0]:.4f}"
    )
    print(
        f"Factor 2 Risk Premium: {lambda_premia[1]:.4f} | Naive SE: {se_naive[1]:.4f} | Shanken SE: {se_shanken[1]:.4f}"
    )
    print(f"Shanken Correction Factor (1 + lambda' Sigma_f^{{-1}} lambda): {c_shanken:.4f}")
    print(f"GRS Test Statistic F: {grs_stat:.4f}")


main()
Factor 1 Risk Premium: 0.0129 | Naive SE: 0.0034 | Shanken SE: 0.0035
Factor 2 Risk Premium: -0.0055 | Naive SE: 0.0042 | Shanken SE: 0.0043
Shanken Correction Factor (1 + lambda' Sigma_f^{-1} lambda): 1.1403
GRS Test Statistic F: 1.2746

The Shanken factor of 1.1403 inflates the standard error from 0.0034 to 0.0035 for Factor 1 and from 0.0042 to 0.0043 for Factor 2. The GRS statistic of 1.2746 tests joint alpha efficiency.

Where the canon breaks

Doctoral econometrics prelims frequently probe the boundaries where standard asymptotic properties break down:

  • Weak Identification in GMM: When instruments correlate weakly with the Jacobian of the moment conditions (\(D \approx \mathbf{0}\)), the GMM objective surface becomes flat. The standard normal asymptotic distribution collapses, creating non-standard bimodal distributions and invalidating Wald tests.
  • Spurious Cross-Sectional Factors: Kan, Robotti & Shanken (2013) prove that when a candidate factor is unspanned or weakly correlated with asset returns, second-pass cross-sectional \(R^2\) can approach 100 percent even though the factor has zero true pricing power.
  • Non-Normality in the GRS Test: The exact F-distribution of the GRS statistic depends strictly on the assumption that return residuals \(\varepsilon_t\) are conditionally normal and homoskedastic. Under fat-tailed conditional heteroskedasticity (e.g. GARCH), GRS severely over-rejects the null hypothesis.

Misconceptions

  1. The standard Fama-MacBeth second-pass standard error accounts for estimation uncertainty in first-pass betas.

    Standard Fama-MacBeth standard errors compute the time-series sample standard deviation of \(\lambda_t\), treating estimated \(\hat{\beta}_i\) as fixed, non-stochastic constants. They ignore sampling error in \(\hat{\beta}\) and are systematically biased downward. The Shanken (1992) correction is strictly required.

  2. The Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS) test is valid under arbitrary heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation.

    The GRS test is derived strictly under the assumption of independent, identically distributed Gaussian residuals. When returns exhibit time-varying volatility or serial correlation, GMM or Newey-West Wald tests must be used instead.

  3. Minimizing sample pricing errors in GMM guarantees that the estimated structural parameters are economically meaningful.

    GMM minimizes a statistical quadratic form. In asset pricing models, unconstrained minimization can yield discount factors \(\beta > 1\) or risk aversion \(\gamma < 0\) that achieve small pricing errors by violating basic economic axioms.

Problem set

Mechanics problems

In a two-factor Fama-MacBeth regression, the estimated factor risk premia vector is \(\hat{\lambda} = [0.005, 0.003]^\top\). The factor covariance matrix is \(\Sigma_f = \begin{bmatrix} 0.0016 & 0.0004 \\ 0.0004 & 0.0009 \end{bmatrix}\). Calculate the Shanken correction factor \(c_{\text{shanken}} = 1 + \hat{\lambda}^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda}\).

Solution

The determinant of \(\Sigma_f\) is \(\det = 0.0016 \times 0.0009 - 0.0004^2 = 0.00000144 - 0.00000016 = 0.00000128\). The inverse is \(\Sigma_f^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.00000128} \begin{bmatrix} 0.0009 & -0.0004 \\ -0.0004 & 0.0016 \end{bmatrix}\). Evaluating the quadratic form: \(\hat{\lambda}^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda} = \frac{1}{0.00000128} [0.0009 (0.005)^2 - 2(0.0004)(0.005)(0.003) + 0.0016 (0.003)^2] = \frac{1}{0.00000128} [0.0000000225 - 0.0000000120 + 0.0000000144] = \frac{0.0000000249}{0.00000128} \approx 0.019453\). Therefore, \(c_{\text{shanken}} = 1 + 0.019453 = 1.01945\).

In testing a three-factor model across \(N = 10\) industry portfolios over \(T = 240\) months, the estimated alphas yield \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha} = 0.30\). The factor quadratic form is \(\bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f} = 0.08\). Compute the GRS test statistic and determine whether to reject the null hypothesis at the 5 percent level (critical value \(F_{0.05}(10, 227) \approx 1.87\)).

Solution

Applying the GRS formula: \(GRS = \frac{T - N - K}{N} \frac{\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha}}{1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}}\). Here \(T = 240, N = 10, K = 3\). The degrees of freedom scaling factor is \(\frac{240 - 10 - 3}{10} = \frac{227}{10} = 22.7\). The quadratic ratio is \(\frac{0.30}{1 + 0.08} = \frac{0.30}{1.08} \approx 0.277778\). Multiplying yields: \(GRS = 22.7 \times 0.277778 \approx 6.3056\). Because \(6.3056 > 1.87\) (p-value \(\approx 1.3 \times 10^{-8}\)), the null hypothesis of zero pricing errors is decisively rejected.

A researcher estimates a dynamic stochastic volatility model using GMM with \(m = 8\) moments and \(k = 3\) parameters across \(T = 400\) observations. The two-step minimized GMM criterion is \(T g_T^\top \hat{S}^{-1} g_T = 12.8\). Calculate the degrees of freedom for the Hansen J-test and evaluate model validity at \(\alpha = 0.05\) (critical value \(\chi^2_{0.05}(5) = 11.07\)).

Solution

The degrees of freedom equal the number of overidentifying restrictions: \(df = m - k = 8 - 3 = 5\). The test statistic is \(J = 12.8\). Comparing against the 5 percent critical value: \(12.8 > 11.07\). The p-value is \(\approx 0.0253 < 0.05\). The overidentifying restrictions are rejected at the 5 percent level, indicating model misspecification.

Proof problems

Prove that the optimal GMM weighting matrix is \(W^* = S^{-1}\), where \(S\) is the spectral density matrix of the moment conditions at frequency zero.

Solution

From Theorem 3.1, the asymptotic variance of the GMM estimator is \(V(W) = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}\). For \(W = S^{-1}\), this evaluates to \(V(S^{-1}) = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} S S^{-1} D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\). To prove optimality, we examine the difference matrix \(\Delta = V(W) - V(S^{-1})\). Let \(A = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W\) and \(B = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1}\). Consider the variance of the linear combination \((A - B) \sqrt{T} g_T\): \(\E[(A - B) \sqrt{T} g_T (\sqrt{T} g_T)^\top (A - B)^\top] = (A - B) S (A - B)^\top \ge 0\) because \(S\) is positive definite. Expanding: \((A - B) S (A - B)^\top = A S A^\top - A S B^\top - B S A^\top + B S B^\top\). Note that \(B S B^\top = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\) and \(A S B^\top = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S S^{-1} D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = B S B^\top\). Thus, \(A S B^\top = B S A^\top = B S B^\top\). The expression reduces to \(A S A^\top - B S B^\top = V(W) - V(S^{-1}) \ge 0\). Hence, \(V(W) \ge V(S^{-1})\) in the positive semi-definite matrix sense, establishing that \(W^* = S^{-1}\) minimizes the asymptotic variance.

Prove that in the GRS test, when candidate factors are traded assets, the quadratic form \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha}\) equals the difference between the squared Sharpe ratio of the ex-post tangency portfolio of all assets and factors and the squared Sharpe ratio of the factor portfolio.

Solution

Let \(R_t = [f_t^\top, r_t^\top]^\top\) denote the combined vector of \(K\) factors and \(N\) test assets. By standard regression properties, the maximum Sharpe ratio attainable from the entire universe of \(N + K\) assets satisfies \(SR_{N+K}^2 = \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu\). Partitioning the covariance matrix and using the block inversion formula (Schur complement), \(\Sigma_{r \cdot f} = \Sigma_\varepsilon\). The maximum Sharpe ratio from the combined universe decomposes exactly into: \(SR_{N+K}^2 = SR_f^2 + \alpha^\top \Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1} \alpha\). Substituting sample estimates yields: \(SR_{N+K}^2 - SR_f^2 = \hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha}\). Thus, the GRS test statistic evaluates whether adding the \(N\) test assets generates a statistically significant expansion of the mean-variance efficient frontier beyond that spanned by the \(K\) factors alone.

Prove that under the null hypothesis \(H_0: \E[g(z_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}\), the Hansen J-statistic satisfies \(J_T \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(m - k)\).

Solution

Under efficient weighting \(W_T = S_T^{-1}\), the first-order Taylor expansion yields \(\sqrt{T} g_T(\hat{\theta}) = [I - D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1}] \sqrt{T} g_T(\theta_0) + o_p(1)\). Define the normalized moment vector \(u_T = S^{-1/2} \sqrt{T} g_T(\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I_m)\). Pre-multiplying by \(S^{-1/2}\): \(S^{-1/2} \sqrt{T} g_T(\hat{\theta}) = [I_m - S^{-1/2} D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1/2}] u_T + o_p(1)\). Define the projection matrix \(P = S^{-1/2} D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1/2}\). Notice that \(P\) is symmetric and idempotent (\(P^2 = P\)) with rank \(k\). Consequently, \(M = I_m - P\) is an idempotent projection matrix with rank \(\operatorname{tr}(I_m - P) = m - k\). The GMM objective function is \(J_T = [S^{-1/2} \sqrt{T} g_T(\hat{\theta})]^\top [S^{-1/2} \sqrt{T} g_T(\hat{\theta})] = u_T^\top M u_T + o_p(1)\). Because \(u_T\) is asymptotically standard Gaussian and \(M\) is idempotent with rank \(m - k\), the quadratic form \(u_T^\top M u_T\) converges to \(\chi^2(m - k)\), proving the theorem.

Empirical problems

An empirical asset pricing paper estimates the Fama-French three-factor model across 25 size and book-to-market portfolios. The reported second-pass risk premium on the value factor HML is \(\hat{\lambda}_{\text{HML}} = 0.0035\) per month with naive standard error \(SE_{\text{naive}} = 0.0011\) (\(t = 3.18\)). If \(\hat{\lambda}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda} = 0.44\), compute the Shanken-corrected standard error and t-statistic, and evaluate whether the factor remains significant under the Harvey-Liu-Zhu \(t \ge 3.0\) hurdle.

Solution

The Shanken correction factor is \(c = 1 + 0.44 = 1.44\). In standard two-pass estimation where residual variance dominates, the standard error scales as \(SE_{\text{Shanken}} \approx \sqrt{1.44} \times SE_{\text{naive}} = 1.20 \times 0.0011 = 0.00132\). The corrected t-statistic is \(t_{\text{Shanken}} = \frac{0.0035}{0.00132} \approx 2.65\). While the factor was statistically significant under naive OLS (\(t = 3.18 > 1.96\)), after applying the Shanken correction, the t-statistic drops to 2.65, failing the Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) doctoral hurdle of \(t \ge 3.00\).

In estimating consumption Euler equations via GMM, a researcher compares an identity weighting matrix \(W_1 = I\) with the efficient two-step weighting matrix \(W_2 = \hat{S}^{-1}\). The estimated risk aversion is \(\hat{\gamma}_1 = 2.4\) with standard error 0.95, and \(\hat{\gamma}_2 = 1.8\) with standard error 0.42. Explain why the efficient estimator achieves a smaller standard error and state the potential trade-off in small samples.

Solution

By Theorem 3.1, setting \(W = S^{-1}\) minimizes the asymptotic covariance matrix \((D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}\) to \((D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\), which reduces the standard error from 0.95 to 0.42. However, in finite samples, inverting an estimated spectral density matrix \(\hat{S}\) introduces substantial sampling noise, especially when the number of moments \(m\) is large relative to sample size \(T\). In small samples, two-step GMM parameter estimates can be severely biased, and Altonji and Segal (1996) show that identity or diagonal weighting often exhibits superior root mean squared error despite larger asymptotic standard errors.

A quantitative hedge fund tests 500 trading anomalies against the CAPM. Exactly 45 anomalies produce in-sample t-statistics \(t \ge 2.0\). Using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure at a 10 percent false discovery rate (FDR), describe how to determine the empirical discovery cutoff and explain why simple OLS p-values overstate factor validity.

Solution

Sort the two-tailed p-values of all \(M = 500\) anomalies in ascending order: \(p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \dots \le p_{(M)}\). For target FDR \(q = 0.10\), find the largest rank \(k\) such that \(p_{(k)} \le \frac{k}{M} q = \frac{k}{500} \times 0.10 = 0.0002 k\). Reject all null hypotheses with rank \(i \le k\). Simple OLS p-values evaluate each test in isolation under the false presumption that only one hypothesis was tested. In a pool of 500 tests under a true null, purely by chance \(500 \times 0.05 = 25\) anomalies will yield \(t \ge 1.96\). Failing to adjust for multi-testing leads to backtest overfitting and rapid post-deployment strategy decay.

Research problems

Formulate a doctoral prelim defense answering why the Fama-French five-factor model (2015) fails the GRS test across standard 25 size and book-to-market portfolios, and defend whether this failure invalidates the model for practical risk adjustment.

Solution

The GRS test of the FF5 model across 25 size and book-to-market portfolios decisively rejects the null hypothesis of zero joint alphas (p-value \(< 0.001\)), primarily because the model fails to price small-growth stocks (the extreme short-leg small-growth anomaly). In defending the model, the candidate argues that GRS is a strict statistical test of exact mean-variance efficiency; with large \(T\) (over 600 months), even minor economic pricing errors of 10 to 15 basis points per year achieve statistical significance. For practical risk adjustment, what matters is the economic reduction in pricing errors: the FF5 model reduces average absolute alpha from ~35 bps per month under the CAPM to ~8 bps per month, explaining over 80 percent of cross-sectional return variation across diverse asset classes.

Analyze the econometric consequences of testing factor models using portfolios sorted on characteristics versus using individual stock returns.

Solution

Sorting stocks into characteristic portfolios (e.g., size or value deciles) averages out idiosyncratic risk \(\Var(\varepsilon_i)\), dramatically raising the precision of estimated factor loadings (\(\beta_p\)) and alleviating the errors-in-variables attenuation problem in Fama-MacBeth regressions. However, following Lewellen, Nagel & Shanken (2010), sorting on characteristics collapses the effective dimensionality of the asset covariance matrix to a low-rank factor structure. Consequently, almost any proposed macroeconomic factor that correlates weakly with size or book-to-market can generate high cross-sectional \(R^2\). Testing on individual stocks avoids artificial factor structure and preserves high-dimensional variation, but introduces substantial idiosyncratic noise and non-normality requiring machine learning shrinkage or instrumental variable estimators (such as Kelly, Pruitt & Su 2019 IPCA).

Critique the use of pre-formation rolling betas versus full-sample post-formation betas in two-pass asset pricing tests.

Solution

Pre-formation rolling betas (e.g. estimated over 36 to 60 prior months) ensure that portfolio formation and trading strategies are strictly out-of-sample and free of look-ahead bias. However, because individual stock betas are noisy, sorting on estimated rolling betas creates substantial regression-to-the-mean (selection bias): stocks categorized into extreme high-beta deciles tend to have positive estimation errors that revert toward unity post-formation. Using full-sample post-formation betas eliminates sorting noise and yields consistent estimates of portfolio risk exposures over the holding period, but assumes parameter stability over decadal horizons. Modern doctoral practice combines both: sorting on rolling pre-formation betas to construct portfolios, and estimating post-formation time-series betas to evaluate pricing performance.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the optimal GMM weighting matrix under Hansen's (1982) asymptotic efficiency theorem?

    Options

    Weighting moments inversely by their asymptotic covariance matrix \(S\) minimizes the asymptotic variance of the GMM parameter estimator.

  2. Under the null hypothesis of valid moment conditions, what is the asymptotic distribution of the Hansen J-statistic with \(m\) moments and \(k\) parameters?

    Options

    The test evaluates the \(m - k\) overidentifying restrictions, converging asymptotically to a chi-squared distribution with degrees of freedom \(m - k\).

  3. Why are naive Fama-MacBeth second-pass standard errors systematically biased downward?

    Options

    Naive Fama-MacBeth treats first-pass betas as known non-stochastic constants, ignoring the errors-in-variables variance contribution resolved by the Shanken correction.

  4. If the factor quadratic form is \(\hat{\lambda}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda} = 0.25\), what is the value of the Shanken correction factor \(c_{\text{shanken}} = 1 + \hat{\lambda}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda}\)?

    \(c_{\text{shanken}} = 1 + 0.25 = 1.25\).

  5. What null hypothesis does the Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS 1989) test evaluate?

    Options

    The GRS test is an exact finite-sample test evaluating whether candidate factors span the mean-variance efficient frontier by testing joint zero intercepts.

  6. Why is the Harvey-Liu-Zhu t-statistic hurdle set to \(t \ge 3.0\) rather than \(t \ge 1.96\)?

    Options

    When researchers test hundreds of candidate factors, the probability of false discovery under \(t \ge 1.96\) approaches 100 percent, necessitating a higher statistical bar.

  7. What critical distributional assumption is required for the exact finite-sample F-distribution of the GRS test?

    Options

    The exact Hotelling \(T^2\) and F-distributions rely strictly on the assumption that residuals are conditionally normal and homoskedastic.

  8. What happens in GMM estimation when moment conditions are weakly identified (the Jacobian matrix \(D\) is near rank-deficient)?

    Options

    Weak identification prevents the parameter estimator from achieving standard \(\sqrt{T}\) asymptotic normality, creating non-standard bimodal or heavy-tailed distributions.
