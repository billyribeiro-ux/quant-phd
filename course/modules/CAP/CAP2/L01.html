Capital Markets Foundations: Market Efficiency & The Equity Risk Premium

Modern finance rests upon two foundational pillars: whether market prices efficiently incorporate all available information, and whether the substantial historical premium earned by equities represents fair compensation for macroeconomic risk. Without resolving these debates, one can neither price derivative claims nor distinguish genuine quantitative alpha from unhedged tail disaster risk.

1. The Epistemology of Financial Debates

Definition 1.1 (Debate Resolution)

A debate resolution is a formal, falsifiable propositional statement asserting a specific economic mechanism, structural invariance, or econometric property across financial markets, structured to permit mutually exclusive affirmative and negative steelman formulations.

A rigorous quantitative debate differs fundamentally from colloquial disagreement. Every debate resolution requires defining the underlying probability space, information filtrations, and equilibrium conditions. Contested claims cannot be evaluated without an explicit epistemic architecture.

Definition 1.2 (Evidence Map)

An evidence map is a multidimensional structured synthesis categorizing empirical and theoretical findings regarding a debate resolution according to econometric identification strategy, sample window, market frictions, statistical hurdles, and formal evidentiary grades.

To settle debates, researchers must state ex-ante what observational evidence would invalidate their preferred paradigm.

Definition 1.3 (What Would Settle It)

What evidence would settle a debate is a pre-registered, operational decision rule specifying the exact statistical test, minimum effect size, out-of-sample observation horizon, and identification threshold that would cause a rational Bayesian researcher to reject their prior hypothesis in favor of the counter-hypothesis.

2. Resolution 1: Markets Are Efficient

The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) formalizes the proposition that asset prices fully reflect all available information. Formally, let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \Prob)\) be a filtered probability space. Under the information filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t\), the fundamental pricing relation requires:

\[ \begin{aligned} P_t &= \E\left[ M_{t+1} (P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}) \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_t \right] \end{aligned} \]

where \(M_{t+1}\) is the stochastic discount factor. In the special case of risk-neutrality or constant expected returns \(R\), the discounted price process \(P_t / (1+R)^t\) forms a \(\Prob\)-martingale.

Theorem 1.1 · Fama's Joint Hypothesis Theorem

Any empirical test of informational market efficiency is necessarily a joint test of market efficiency and an assumed model of market equilibrium (the stochastic discount factor \(M_{t+1}\)). A rejection of the null hypothesis \(\E[R_{i, t+1} - R_{f, t} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\) cannot distinguish between informational inefficiency (market irrationality) and misspecification of the equilibrium asset pricing model.

Proof

Let observed excess returns be \(r_{i, t+1} = R_{i, t+1} - R_{f, t}\). In equilibrium, the no-arbitrage condition implies \(\E[M_{t+1} r_{i, t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\). Expanding the conditional expectation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \E[r_{i, t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] + \Cov_t(M_{t+1}, r_{i, t+1}) &= 0 \\ \E[r_{i, t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] &= -\frac{\Cov_t(M_{t+1}, r_{i, t+1})}{\E[M_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]} \end{aligned} \]

An empirical test evaluates whether the residual \(\epsilon_{i, t+1} = r_{i, t+1} - \hat{\E}[r_{i, t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) satisfies \(\E[\epsilon_{i, t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\). If an econometrician observes \(\E[\epsilon_{i, t+1} z_t] \neq 0\) for some instrument \(z_t \in \mathcal{F}_t\), the non-zero covariance may arise either because agents failed to condition on \(z_t\) (informational inefficiency), or because the true conditional covariance \(\Cov_t(M_{t+1}, r_{i, t+1})\) depends on \(z_t\) in a manner uncaptured by the proxy model \(\hat{M}_{t+1}\). The two hypotheses are observationally equivalent without independent measurement of \(M_{t+1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Shiller (1981) challenged the EMH by showing that real stock prices exhibit volatility far exceeding the theoretical bound implied by the present value of realized future dividends. Defining the perfect-foresight price \(P_t^* = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left(\frac{1}{1+r}\right)^{k+1} D_{t+k}\), under constant discount rates market efficiency requires \(P_t = \E[P_t^* \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). By the properties of conditional expectation, \(\Var(P_t^*) = \Var(P_t) + \Var(P_t^* - P_t) \ge \Var(P_t)\). Empirically, \(\sigma(P_t)\) is 5 to 13 times larger than \(\sigma(P_t^*)\), violating the inequality under constant discount rates.

Neoclassical defenders, synthesized in Campbell & Shiller (1988) and Cochrane (2011), demonstrated via the log-linear dividend-price decomposition that virtually 100% of price volatility reflects time-varying discount rates \(\E_t[r_{t+j}]\) rather than dividend growth expectations \(\E_t[\Delta d_{t+j}]\), re-establishing compatibility with the Joint Hypothesis Theorem.

3. Resolution 2: The Equity Premium Is a Risk Premium

In standard neoclassical economics, the return of the market portfolio over the risk-free rate compensates risk-averse investors for covariance with marginal utility. In a Lucas exchange economy with power utility \(u(C_t) = \frac{C_t^{1-\gamma}-1}{1-\gamma}\), the stochastic discount factor is \(M_{t+1} = \beta \left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{-\gamma}\).

Theorem 1.2 · Mehra-Prescott Equity Premium Calibration

Under log-normal consumption growth and equity returns, the expected real equity premium satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[r_{e, t+1} - r_{f, t}] + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_e^2 &\approx \gamma \cdot \Cov(\Delta c_{t+1}, r_{e, t+1}) \\ &= \gamma \cdot \rho_{c, e} \sigma_c \sigma_e \end{aligned} \]

Given historical US estimates where the real equity premium is \(\approx 6.0\%\), consumption growth volatility is \(\sigma_c \approx 1.5\%\), equity volatility is \(\sigma_e \approx 18.0\%\), and their correlation is \(\rho_{c, e} \approx 0.22\), reconciling the equity premium requires relative risk aversion \(\gamma > 30\), conflicting with microeconomic evidence bounding \(\gamma \in [1, 5]\).

The resolution of this puzzle has bifurcated into two major schools of thought:

  • Disaster Risk: Barro (2006), reviving Rietz (1988), shows that an annual disaster probability of 1.7% with an average economic contraction of 29% resolves the puzzle with \(\gamma \approx 3.5\) to \(4.0\).
  • Habit Formation & Long-Run Risks: Campbell & Cochrane (1999) demonstrate that slow-moving external consumption habits induce countercyclical effective risk aversion, generating large time-varying risk premia without excess consumption volatility. Similarly, Bansal & Yaron (2004) demonstrate that persistent growth rate components and fluctuating economic uncertainty generate substantial equity premia under Epstein-Zin recursive preferences.

Lab 1 · Variance Ratio Tests & Equity Premium Calibration

We test weak-form market efficiency via Lo-MacKinlay variance ratio tests and calibrate the implied coefficient of relative risk aversion under Mehra-Prescott macro-finance parameters.

"""Lab CAP2-L01-1: Quantitative evidence tests for market efficiency and the equity premium.

Implements Lo-MacKinlay (1988) variance ratio tests for random walk behavior,
Shiller (1981) excess volatility inequality checks, and Mehra-Prescott (1985)
relative risk aversion calibration for the equity risk premium.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats


def lo_mackinlay_variance_ratio(log_prices: np.ndarray, q: int) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Compute Lo-MacKinlay homoskedastic and heteroskedastic variance ratio test."""
    n = len(log_prices) - 1
    rets = np.diff(log_prices)
    mu = float(np.mean(rets))

    # 1-period variance
    sigma_1_sq = float(np.sum((rets - mu) ** 2) / (n - 1))

    # q-period returns
    rets_q = log_prices[q:] - log_prices[:-q]
    m_q = q * mu
    m_denom = float(q * (n - q + 1) * (1.0 - float(q) / float(n)))
    sigma_q_sq = float(np.sum((rets_q - m_q) ** 2) / m_denom)

    vr = sigma_q_sq / sigma_1_sq

    # Asymptotic variance under homoskedasticity: phi_1 = 2 * (2q - 1) * (q - 1) / (3 * q * n)
    phi_1 = 2.0 * (2.0 * q - 1.0) * (q - 1.0) / (3.0 * q * n)
    z_stat = (vr - 1.0) / np.sqrt(phi_1)
    p_val = float(2.0 * (1.0 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z_stat))))

    return vr, float(z_stat), p_val


def shiller_excess_volatility_test(
    prices: np.ndarray,
    dividends: np.ndarray,
    discount_rate: float,
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Evaluate Shiller (1981) bound: Var(P) <= Var(P*)."""
    t = len(prices)
    # Perfect foresight price P*_t = sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1} (1/(1+r))^{k+1} D_{t+k} + terminal discounted
    p_star = np.zeros(t, dtype=np.float64)
    beta = 1.0 / (1.0 + discount_rate)
    terminal_val = float(prices[-1])

    for i in range(t):
        cf_sum = 0.0
        for k in range(t - i):
            cf_sum += (beta ** (k + 1)) * dividends[i + k]
        cf_sum += (beta ** (t - i)) * terminal_val
        p_star[i] = cf_sum

    var_p = float(np.var(prices, ddof=1))
    var_p_star = float(np.var(p_star, ddof=1))
    vol_ratio = np.sqrt(var_p) / np.sqrt(var_p_star)

    return var_p, var_p_star, vol_ratio


def mehra_prescott_calibration(
    equity_premium: float,
    consumption_growth_vol: float,
    equity_consumption_corr: float,
    equity_return_vol: float,
) -> float:
    """Calibrate Mehra-Prescott (1985) coefficient of relative risk aversion gamma.

    Under power utility, E[R_e - R_f] approx gamma * Cov(Delta c, R_e).
    """
    cov_c_e = equity_consumption_corr * consumption_growth_vol * equity_return_vol
    gamma = equity_premium / cov_c_e
    return float(gamma)


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(1988)

    # 1. Simulate monthly asset log prices (120 months)
    innovations = rng.normal(0.007, 0.045, size=120)
    log_p = np.cumsum(np.concatenate([[4.6], innovations]))

    vr_2, z_2, p_2 = lo_mackinlay_variance_ratio(log_p, q=2)
    vr_12, z_12, p_12 = lo_mackinlay_variance_ratio(log_p, q=12)

    print("--- Resolution 1: Market Efficiency & Random Walk Tests ---")
    print(f"Variance Ratio VR(2):  {vr_2:.4f} (z-stat: {z_2:.3f}, p-val: {p_2:.4f})")
    print(f"Variance Ratio VR(12): {vr_12:.4f} (z-stat: {z_12:.3f}, p-val: {p_12:.4f})")

    # 2. Shiller (1981) Volatility Bounds
    divs = rng.normal(2.5, 0.15, size=len(log_p))
    prices = np.exp(log_p)
    var_p, var_p_star, vol_ratio = shiller_excess_volatility_test(prices, divs, discount_rate=0.06 / 12.0)

    print("\n--- Shiller Excess Volatility Inequality ---")
    print(f"Sample Stock Price Std Dev:  {np.sqrt(var_p):.2f}")
    print(f"Ex-Post Fundamental Std Dev: {np.sqrt(var_p_star):.2f}")
    print(f"Excess Volatility Ratio (sigma_P / sigma_P*): {vol_ratio:.3f}")

    # 3. Resolution 2: Equity Risk Premium Calibration
    # Historic US values: Equity premium ~ 6.0%, Consumption vol ~ 1.5%, Equity vol ~ 18%, Corr ~ 0.20
    ep = 0.060
    sigma_c = 0.015
    sigma_e = 0.180
    corr = 0.220

    gamma_implied = mehra_prescott_calibration(ep, sigma_c, corr, sigma_e)
    print("\n--- Resolution 2: The Equity Premium as a Risk Premium ---")
    print(f"Historical US Real Equity Premium: {ep * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print(f"Consumption Growth Volatility:    {sigma_c * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print(f"Equity Volatility:                {sigma_e * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print(f"Equity-Consumption Correlation:   {corr:.3f}")
    print(f"Mehra-Prescott Implied Risk Aversion (gamma): {gamma_implied:.1f}")

4. Where the Canon Breaks

Standard textbook finance asserts that arbitrage eliminates price discrepancies immediately and that prices equal discounted cash flows. In practice, the canon breaks on structural frictions:

  • Limits to Arbitrage: Capital constraints, margin requirements, and short-sale borrowing fees prevent rational arbitrageurs from closing price wedges, allowing noise trader sentiment to dictate prices for extended horizons.
  • Survivorship & Peso Problems: Econometricians observe realized US returns across the 20th century—the single most successful capitalist economy—biasing observed premia upwards compared to ex-ante expectations that incorporated possibilities of regime collapse.

5. Debate Briefs

Debate 1 · Resolution: "Markets are efficient."

Resolution: Markets are efficient.

Steelman For: Fama (1970, 1991). Professional mutual funds and active managers exhibit zero net-of-fee alpha on average (Fama & French 2010). Apparent return predictability is entirely absorbed by time-varying macroeconomic risk premia (Cochrane 2011). Under the Joint Hypothesis Problem, anomalies merely indicate incomplete factor benchmarks rather than unexploited profit opportunities.

Steelman Against: Shiller (1981). Stock market aggregate volatility is orders of magnitude greater than fundamental dividend volatility. Limits to arbitrage permit persistent anomalies, such as negative stub values in corporate carve-outs and post-earnings announcement drift, where prices fail to reflect public information.

State of the Literature: Broad consensus on micro-efficiency (cross-sectional arbitrage eliminates pure riskless profit wedges rapidly), but intense ongoing debate regarding macro-efficiency (aggregate price levels exhibit long-horizon mean reversion and sentiment cycles).

What Would Settle It: Demonstration of a persistent trading strategy in large-cap, liquid equities that generates a Sharpe ratio \(> 1.5\) net of all borrowing costs and transaction fees across a 30-year out-of-sample horizon with exactly zero correlation to consumption, wealth, or aggregate disaster indicators.

Debate 2 · Resolution: "The equity premium is a risk premium."

Resolution: The equity premium is a risk premium.

Steelman For: Barro (2006) and Campbell & Cochrane (1999). Equities perform catastrophically during severe macroeconomic contractions, wartime devastations, and systemic banking collapses when marginal utility spikes. The equity premium is the rational market-clearing compensation for bearing non-diversifiable aggregate catastrophe risk.

Steelman Against: Mehra & Prescott (1985). Smooth observed aggregate consumption growth cannot generate the historical equity premium under plausible risk aversion. The historical US premium reflects survivorship bias, unexpected geopolitical dominance, and institutional market participation barriers rather than fundamental risk pricing.

State of the Literature: Neoclassical macro-finance largely accepts rare disasters and habit persistence as formal mathematical resolutions, though empirical skepticism remains regarding whether calibration parameters match unconstrained household behavior.

What Would Settle It: A cross-country panel study over a century of data showing that equity returns in nations experiencing severe disaster shocks recovered at rates identical to non-disaster nations, proving that equity holders were never compensated for disaster exposure.

6. Misconceptions

  • Rejecting a random walk proves that financial markets are informationally inefficient.

    Asset prices are only expected to follow a random walk under the highly restrictive assumption of constant expected returns. In equilibrium, time-varying risk aversion or time-varying economic risk causes expected returns to fluctuate with the business cycle, inducing predictable return variations that are fully consistent with informational market efficiency.

  • Fama's Joint Hypothesis Problem implies that market efficiency can never be scientifically questioned or falsified.

    While market efficiency cannot be rejected in isolation, it can be rejected jointly with reasonable bounds on the pricing kernel. If an empirical strategy generates Sharpe ratios exceeding the maximum Hansen-Jagannathan bound of plausible macroeconomic stochastic discount factors, the joint hypothesis is decisively refuted.

  • The equity premium puzzle means stocks are guaranteed to outperform risk-free bonds over sufficiently long investment horizons.

    The equity premium puzzle is an explanation puzzle, not a guarantee of excess return. The premium compensates for catastrophic state-contingent losses. If rare disasters occur, equity wealth can be permanently impaired, disproving the folk theorem of long-horizon stock safety.

7. Problem Set

Tier 1: Mechanics

Calculate the Lo-MacKinlay variance ratio \(VR(2)\) given 1-period variance \(\sigma_1^2 = 0.0016\) and 2-period variance \(\sigma_2^2 = 0.0030\).

Solution

The variance ratio is defined as \(VR(q) = \frac{\sigma_q^2}{q \sigma_1^2}\). For \(q = 2\):

\[ VR(2) = \frac{0.0030}{2 \times 0.0016} = \frac{0.0030}{0.0032} = 0.9375 \]

The variance ratio is 0.9375, indicating slight negative serial correlation in returns.

Given an equity volatility of \(\sigma_e = 0.16\), consumption volatility \(\sigma_c = 0.02\), correlation \(\rho_{c, e} = 0.25\), and an observed equity premium of \(5.0\%\), compute the implied Mehra-Prescott risk aversion \(\gamma\).

Solution

Under the Mehra-Prescott relation, \(\E[R_e - R_f] \approx \gamma \rho_{c, e} \sigma_c \sigma_e\). Rearranging for \(\gamma\):

\[ \gamma = \frac{0.050}{0.25 \times 0.02 \times 0.16} = \frac{0.050}{0.0008} = 62.5 \]

The implied relative risk aversion is \(\gamma = 62.5\).

Under Barro's (2006) rare disaster model, if the disaster probability is \(p = 0.02\) and consumption falls by \(b = 0.30\) during a disaster with \(\gamma = 4\), compute the approximate disaster risk premium contribution \(\Delta \approx p \cdot b \cdot \gamma\).

Solution

Using the first-order approximation for the disaster risk premium:

\[ \text{Premium} \approx p \times \left[(1-b)^{-\gamma} - 1\right] \times b \]

Using the linear calibration formula \(\Delta \approx 0.02 \times 0.30 \times 4.0 = 0.024\) (or 2.40% annualized).

Tier 2: Proof

Prove that under constant discount rates and informational market efficiency, the variance of perfect-foresight prices \(P_t^*\) must be greater than or equal to the variance of market prices \(P_t\).

Solution

By definition of conditional expectation under the EMH, \(P_t = \E[P_t^* \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). We decompose the perfect-foresight price as \(P_t^* = P_t + \epsilon_t\), where \(\epsilon_t = P_t^* - \E[P_t^* \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) is the forecast error. By the law of iterated expectations, \(\E[\epsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\), which implies that \(\Cov(P_t, \epsilon_t) = 0\). Taking the variance of both sides:

\[ \Var(P_t^*) = \Var(P_t + \epsilon_t) = \Var(P_t) + \Var(\epsilon_t) + 2\Cov(P_t, \epsilon_t) = \Var(P_t) + \Var(\epsilon_t) \]

Since \(\Var(\epsilon_t) \ge 0\), it follows directly that \(\Var(P_t^*) \ge \Var(P_t)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that the Hansen-Jagannathan bound sets a lower bound on the volatility of any stochastic discount factor \(M\) that prices excess returns \(R^e\).

Solution

For any excess return \(R^e\), the Euler equation requires \(\E[M R^e] = 0\). Expanding into mean and covariance:

\[ \E[M]\E[R^e] + \Cov(M, R^e) = 0 \implies \E[R^e] = -\frac{\Cov(M, R^e)}{\E[M]} \]

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, \(|\Cov(M, R^e)| \le \sigma_M \sigma_{R^e}\). Substituting:

\[ |\E[R^e]| \le \frac{\sigma_M \sigma_{R^e}}{\E[M]} \implies \frac{\sigma_M}{\E[M]} \ge \frac{|\E[R^e]|}{\sigma_{R^e}} = |SR(R^e)| \]

Thus, the coefficient of variation of the SDF must exceed the Sharpe ratio of any tradeable asset. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that if asset log returns are i.i.d., the variance ratio \(VR(q) = 1\) for all integer \(q \ge 1\).

Solution

Let \(r_t = \ln P_t - \ln P_{t-1}\) be i.i.d. with variance \(\sigma^2\). The \(q\)-period return is \(r_t(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{q-1} r_{t-k}\). Taking the variance:

\[ \Var(r_t(q)) = \Var\left(\sum_{k=0}^{q-1} r_{t-k}\right) = \sum_{k=0}^{q-1} \Var(r_{t-k}) + 2 \sum_{j < k} \Cov(r_{t-j}, r_{t-k}) \]

By the independence assumption, all cross-covariances are zero. Therefore, \(\Var(r_t(q)) = q \sigma^2\). The variance ratio is \(VR(q) = \frac{\Var(r_t(q))}{q \Var(r_t)} = \frac{q \sigma^2}{q \sigma^2} = 1.0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3: Empirical

An analyst measures a 12-month return variance of 0.0450 and a 1-month return variance of 0.0035. Calculate \(VR(12)\) and interpret the implied autocorrelation structure.

Solution

The 12-period variance ratio is:

\[ VR(12) = \frac{0.0450}{12 \times 0.0035} = \frac{0.0450}{0.0420} \approx 1.0714 \]

Since \(VR(12) > 1.0\), the series exhibits positive serial correlation over 12-month horizons, consistent with medium-term momentum.

In a Campbell-Shiller dividend-price variance decomposition, if 90% of \(\Var(d_t - p_t)\) is attributed to future return variation and 10% to dividend growth variation, what does this imply for market efficiency?

Solution

Under constant discount rates, 100% of price variation must reflect future dividend growth expectations. The empirical finding that 90% reflects future discount rate variation refutes constant-expected-return models, but remains consistent with time-varying risk premium models of market efficiency.

Suppose a country experiences a stock market wealth collapse of 80% during a financial crisis with consumption falling 25%. Calibrate the change in marginal utility for an agent with \(\gamma = 3\).

Solution

Marginal utility with power utility is \(u'(C) = C^{-\gamma}\). The relative change in marginal utility is:

\[ \frac{u'(0.75 C_0)}{u'(C_0)} = (0.75)^{-3} = \frac{1}{0.421875} \approx 2.370 \]

Marginal utility spikes by 137%, demonstrating why equities must offer large ex-ante premia to induce risk-averse agents to bear disaster risk.

Tier 4: Research

How does the rise of index funds and passive investing affect the Grossman-Stiglitz (1980) informational equilibrium?

Solution

The Grossman-Stiglitz paradox proves that if markets were perfectly informationally efficient, no active manager would expend resources acquiring information, leading to market breakdown. As passive investing share rises, the fraction of noise-trader or informed arbitrage capital declines, potentially widening mispricings until gross returns to active research offset research costs.

Formulate a testable empirical design to distinguish Campbell-Cochrane habit persistence from Barro rare disaster models.

Solution

Habit models predict that risk premia vary smoothly and countercyclically with the surplus consumption ratio, implying strong return predictability via macroeconomic business cycle indicators. In contrast, disaster models predict that premia jump discretely with changes in the objective probability of rare disasters (e.g., deep out-of-the-money put option prices), exhibiting weak predictability during tranquil expansions.

Evaluate the epistemic status of Fama's Joint Hypothesis Problem: is it an empirical challenge or a philosophical tautology?

Solution

It is a structural identification limit arising from the mathematical structure of no-arbitrage asset pricing. Because the true stochastic discount factor \(M\) is unobservable, any test of pricing efficiency must condition on a model for \(M\). It acts as a scientific demarcation principle preventing isolated falsification of market efficiency.

9. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What does Fama's Joint Hypothesis Problem establish regarding empirical tests of market efficiency?

    Options

    Fama proved that any empirical test of informational efficiency requires an auxiliary model of equilibrium expected returns.

  2. Under Shiller's (1981) volatility bound, what must hold if discount rates are constant and markets are efficient?

    Options

    Because market price is the conditional expectation of perfect-foresight price \(P_t^*\), its variance cannot exceed the variance of \(P_t^*\).

  3. Why does power utility require \(\gamma > 30\) to explain the equity premium in Mehra and Prescott (1985)?

    Options

    Because \(\Cov(\Delta c, r_e)\) is very small, matching a 6% equity premium requires enormous risk aversion \(\gamma\).

  4. How does Barro's (2006) rare disaster model resolve the equity premium puzzle?

    Options

    Infrequent but severe economic collapses create substantial risk premia even with moderate risk aversion \(\gamma \in [3, 4]\).

  5. What did Campbell & Shiller (1988) and Cochrane (2011) discover regarding price-dividend ratio fluctuations?

    Options

    Virtually all variation in the price-dividend ratio reflects time-varying discount rates rather than revisions in dividend growth expectations.

  6. In a Lo-MacKinlay variance ratio test, what does \(VR(q) < 1\) indicate?

    Options

    When \(VR(q) < 1\), the variance of \(q\)-period returns is less than \(q\) times the 1-period variance, implying negative serial correlation.

  7. How does Campbell-Cochrane (1999) habit persistence generate countercyclical risk premia?

    Options

    As consumption approaches the habit level during recessions, surplus consumption shrinks, driving curvature of utility and effective risk aversion upward.

  8. What constitutes a valid "what would settle it" epistemic standard for a financial debate?

    Options

    A rigorous "what would settle it" condition requires operational, testable, and falsifiable decision criteria stated ex-ante.
