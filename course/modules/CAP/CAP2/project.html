Rungs 1 and 2

Lab 1 · Worked example: End-to-end debate evidence pipeline

Demonstrates the complete empirical debate evaluation pipeline: computing Bonferroni multiple testing hurdles across candidate models, estimating Empirical Bayes shrinkage intensity, and measuring post-discovery return decay.

"""Lab CAP2-project-1: Automated evidence brief evaluation for asset pricing debate.

Demonstrates end-to-end multiple testing hurdle evaluation, Empirical Bayes
shrinkage of contested anomaly premia, out-of-sample decay testing, and
counterfactual policy divergence analysis.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats


def evaluate_debate_brief(
    t_stats: np.ndarray,
    num_tested: int,
    empirical_means: np.ndarray,
    standard_errors: np.ndarray,
    is_returns: np.ndarray,
    oos_returns: np.ndarray,
) -> None:
    # 1. Multiple testing hurdles (Harvey, Liu & Zhu 2016)
    alpha = 0.05
    alpha_bonf = alpha / float(num_tested)
    hurdle = float(stats.norm.ppf(1.0 - alpha_bonf / 2.0))
    bonf_sig = np.abs(t_stats) >= hurdle

    print(f"Total Universe Hypotheses Tested: {num_tested}")
    print(f"Multiple Testing t-Hurdle (alpha={alpha:.2f}): {hurdle:.4f}")
    print(f"Claims Evaluated: {len(t_stats)}")
    print(f"Claims Passing Bonferroni Hurdle: {int(np.sum(bonf_sig))}/{len(t_stats)}")

    # 2. Empirical Bayes shrinkage
    k = len(empirical_means)
    sample_var = float(np.var(empirical_means, ddof=1)) if k > 1 else float(np.mean(empirical_means**2))
    avg_se_sq = float(np.mean(standard_errors**2))
    tau_sq = max(1e-8, sample_var - avg_se_sq)

    weights = tau_sq / (tau_sq + standard_errors**2)
    shrunken_means = weights * empirical_means

    print(f"Estimated True Prior Dispersion (tau): {np.sqrt(tau_sq):.5f}")
    print(f"Average Empirical Mean: {float(np.mean(empirical_means)):.5f}")
    print(f"Average Shrunken Mean: {float(np.mean(shrunken_means)):.5f}")
    print(f"Average Shrinkage Weight (Signal-to-Total): {float(np.mean(weights)):.4f}")

    # 3. Out-of-sample decay analysis (McLean & Pontiff 2016)
    m_is = float(np.mean(is_returns))
    m_oos = float(np.mean(oos_returns))
    s_is = float(np.std(is_returns, ddof=1))
    s_oos = float(np.std(oos_returns, ddof=1))

    sr_is = m_is / s_is * np.sqrt(12.0)
    sr_oos = m_oos / s_oos * np.sqrt(12.0)
    retention = m_oos / m_is if abs(m_is) > 1e-12 else 0.0
    decay = 1.0 - retention

    denom = np.sqrt(s_is**2 / len(is_returns) + s_oos**2 / len(oos_returns))
    t_decay = (m_is - m_oos) / denom
    p_decay = 2.0 * (1.0 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(t_decay)))

    print(f"In-Sample Annualized Sharpe Ratio: {sr_is:.4f}")
    print(f"Out-of-Sample Annualized Sharpe Ratio: {sr_oos:.4f}")
    print(f"Post-Discovery Return Decay: {decay * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print(f"Welch Decay t-stat: {t_decay:.4f} (p-val: {p_decay:.4f})")


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

    # Seeded synthetic brief data
    t_stats = np.array([3.82, 2.95, 2.45, 1.88, 3.21, 1.42, 2.76, 3.44])
    num_tested = 250

    empirical_means = np.array([0.0085, 0.0062, 0.0051, 0.0038, 0.0074, 0.0029])
    standard_errors = np.array([0.0022, 0.0021, 0.0025, 0.0020, 0.0023, 0.0021])

    is_returns = rng.normal(0.0080, 0.042, size=120)
    oos_returns = rng.normal(0.0042, 0.045, size=60)

    evaluate_debate_brief(t_stats, num_tested, empirical_means, standard_errors, is_returns, oos_returns)


main()

Rung 3 · Specification

Context
In doctoral academic finance and quantitative investment management, the most critical capital allocation and policy decisions rest upon contested theoretical and empirical debates. You are tasked with serving as lead quantitative debater and research architect. You must build an automated evidence evaluation engine and author formal debate briefs across four randomly assigned resolutions from the twelve canonical debates, providing ironclad steelman cases for both sides, synthesizing graded empirical evidence, and defining formal counterfactual tests.
Inputs
A panel of empirical test statistics, factor return series, macroeconomic aggregates, and policy intervention timelines across the twelve canonical resolutions. Inputs include candidate factor t-statistic distributions, in-sample and out-of-sample return series, baseline time-series trajectories for counterfactual simulation, and empirical performance metrics.
Required outputs
Two unified deliverables:
  1. An autograded computational Python module debate_engine.py implementing:
    • compute_empirical_hurdles: Harvey-Liu-Zhu multiple testing thresholds, Bonferroni adjusted bounds, and Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate q-values.
    • apply_bayesian_shrinkage: Empirical Bayes shrinkage of contested anomaly returns toward zero, calculating posterior standard deviations and credible intervals.
    • evaluate_oos_decay: Post-discovery Sharpe ratio and mean decay evaluation with Welch two-sample break tests and variance ratios.
    • simulate_counterfactual_resolution: Policy intervention counterfactual trajectory simulation with Kolmogorov-Smirnov distribution testing.
    • score_debate_evidence: Multi-pillar evidence scoring mapping empirical metrics into composite scores and evidence grades (A, B, C, D).
  2. A portfolio of eight formal debate briefs (both sides for four assigned resolutions) formatted with formal steelman arguments, graded empirical tables, and definitive "what would settle it" empirical criteria.
Constraints
Deterministic execution under Python 3.14 with NumPy and SciPy; all claims in the briefs must cite verified literature keys from the program registry; all formulas must use registered macros; all tabular data must articulate with empirical estimations.
Grading mode
The curriculum grades this project by both autograder and rubric: the computational module is graded automatically against deterministic pytest properties across 50 random seeds; the written debate briefs are evaluated by rubric across 5 criteria and four performance levels, calibrated against annotated exemplars.

Exemplar · Level 4 (Advanced Doctoral Mastery)

Resolution: "Most published equity factor anomalies are false discoveries."

Affirmative Steelman: Under the multiple testing framework of Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016), testing \(M = 316\) candidate anomaly factors requires raising the nominal significance threshold from \(|t| \ge 1.96\) to \(|t| \ge 3.00\) to maintain a family-wise error rate of 5%. Furthermore, Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020) show that upon applying NYSE size breakpoints and value-weighting, 65% of 437 published anomalies fail to achieve statistical significance at the 5% level. Accounting for publication bias, McLean & Pontiff (2016) document an average post-publication return decay of 58%, consistent with data snooping and competitive arbitrage capital.

Negative Steelman: The claim that factors are false discoveries conflates statistical artifacts with economic limits to arbitrage. Value-weighting excessively penalizes microcap anomalies where information costs and arbitrage frictions are most severe. As Chen & Zimmermann (2022) demonstrate across 205 open-source replicated factors, 82% reproduce with signs identical to original publications when evaluated using independent software implementations and identical universe definitions. Return decay is the predicted consequence of market efficiency assimilating genuine discoveries rather than proof of original fabrication.

What Would Settle It: A pre-registered out-of-sample tournament evaluating 100 candidate factors on international microcaps and private markets over a 10-year forward window. If post-publication Sharpe ratios decay by more than 75% while bid-ask spreads and short borrow costs remain flat, the false discovery hypothesis is definitively established; if premium retention exceeds 50% net of transaction costs, the risk-and-frictions model stands.

Annotation: Demonstrates Level 4 mastery across C1-C5. Fully cites verified registry keys with exact table locators, presents rigorous steelman formulations on both sides, applies multiple testing and shrinkage logic, and specifies an exact, falsifiable pre-registered criterion.

Exemplar · Level 2 (Developing)

Resolution: "Most published equity factor anomalies are false discoveries."

Case For: Many papers published in finance are p-hacked because researchers test hundreds of variables until they find a t-statistic above 2.0. When people re-run the regressions on new data, most anomalies disappear or make much less money. Therefore, most factors are not real.

Case Against: Factors are real because investors take risks when buying small or distressed stocks. If factors were fake, nobody would trade them. Just because returns drop after a paper is published does not mean the author lied; it just means traders noticed the strategy.

What Would Settle It: Looking at more data in 10 or 20 years to see if the factors work again.

Annotation: Level 2 performance. No formal econometric multiple-testing thresholds (omits Harvey-Liu-Zhu or Bonferroni adjustments), no quantitative citations or locators, and the "what would settle it" section is entirely vague ("looking at more data") with no test statistic or decision rule.

Adversarial defense question bank

Prepare to defend your quantitative briefs against these 16 adversarial attack angles:

  • 1. How does your multiple testing threshold account for cross-factor correlation rather than assuming independence?
  • 2. Why does value-weighting not simply discard the economic segment where market inefficiencies are largest?
  • 3. In your Empirical Bayes shrinkage, what justifies assuming a Gaussian prior over a heavy-tailed Student-t prior?
  • 4. How do you distinguish post-publication return decay caused by statistical bias from arbitrage capital inflows?
  • 5. Why does your Welch test assume stationarity when factor volatility exhibits strong clustering?
  • 6. In your counterfactual simulation, how do you rule out the Lucas critique regarding policy regime changes?
  • 7. How does the choice of delisting return imputation (-30% vs -55%) impact your anomaly survival rates?
  • 8. If market efficiency is protected by the joint hypothesis problem, how can any empirical test definitively reject it?
  • 9. Does your Sharpe ratio calculation account for time-varying margin constraints and financing haircuts?
  • 10. How do you address specification curve sensitivity where minor sample filter changes overturn statistical significance?
  • 11. In the FTPL debate, how do you empirically distinguish unbacked fiscal stimulus from anticipated future deficits?
  • 12. Why should high-frequency quote cancellations be treated as phantom liquidity rather than price discovery?
  • 13. Does the permanent decline in r* reflect demographic secular stagnation or global savings imbalances?
  • 14. In UIP regressions, how do you verify that negative slope coefficients are not generated by peso problem disaster risks?
  • 15. How do you control for look-ahead bias when calculating historical valuation spreads?
  • 16. If double descent occurs in over-parameterized models, why do simple parsimonious linear models frequently dominate out-of-sample?
