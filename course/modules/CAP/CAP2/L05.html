Dynamic Sizing & Macro Equilibrium: Volatility Management & The Secular Fall in r*

Portfolio managers and macroeconomic policymakers operate under two contested equilibrium propositions: whether dynamic volatility scaling creates genuine tradeable alpha after accounting for execution turnover and financing haircuts, and whether the global neutral real interest rate has permanently shifted downward, altering the valuation of all long-duration assets.

1. Resolution 9: Volatility-Managed Portfolios Win After Costs

In standard Merton continuous-time portfolio theory, an unconstrained investor maintaining constant relative risk aversion scales risky asset allocation proportionally to the conditional Sharpe ratio divided by conditional variance: \(w_t = \frac{\mu_t}{\gamma \sigma_t^2}\). If expected returns \(\mu_t\) scale linearly with conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2\), optimal allocation \(w_t\) is constant. However, Moreira & Muir (2017) demonstrate that empirical return variance exhibits strong clustering, whereas expected returns fail to increase during high-volatility episodes.

Definition 5.1 (Volatility-Managed Portfolio)

For an underlying factor or market excess return \(f_{t+1}\) with conditional variance forecast \(\hat{\sigma}_t^2\), the volatility-managed portfolio return \(f_{t+1}^{\sigma}\) scales exposure inversely with variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{t+1}^{\sigma} &= \frac{c}{\hat{\sigma}_t^2} f_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

where \(c\) is a constant chosen to equalize the unconditional sample variance of \(f_{t+1}^{\sigma}\) to that of the unmanaged factor \(f_{t+1}\).

Theorem 5.1 · Moreira-Muir Volatility Timing Alpha

Across the market portfolio, size, value, momentum, profitability, and investment factors over 1926–2015, volatility-managed portfolios generate substantial, statistically significant alphas relative to their unmanaged counterparts:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{t+1}^{\sigma} &= \alpha + \beta f_{t+1} + \epsilon_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

For the market portfolio, the annualized alpha is \(\alpha = 4.90\%\) (\(t = 3.97\)), and the annualized Sharpe ratio increases from 0.38 to 0.54. Because volatility is highly persistent while expected returns are not, scaling down risk during high-volatility regimes circumvents catastrophic left-tail drawdowns without sacrificing proportionate return.

Remark 5.1 (The Friction and Timing Critiques)

Critics argue that volatility management incurs high portfolio turnover, dynamic margin haircuts, and borrowing costs (Adrian & Shin 2010). When implemented with real-time out-of-sample volatility models, sudden volatility spikes inflict severe losses while leveraged low-volatility positions face margin calls, eliminating net-of-cost alpha for non-market factors.

2. Resolution 10: r* Has Fallen Permanently

The natural or neutral rate of interest, denoted \(r^*\), is the real short-term interest rate consistent with output at potential and stable inflation. The secular stagnation hypothesis asserts that structural forces have driven \(r^*\) permanently lower.

Definition 5.2 (Holston-Laubach-Williams State-Space System)

The Holston, Laubach & Williams (2017) model specifies the joint dynamics of the output gap \(\tilde{y}_t = y_t - y_t^*\), inflation \(\pi_t\), and natural rate \(r_t^*\) via a state-space system:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{y}_t &= a_1 \tilde{y}_{t-1} + a_2 \tilde{y}_{t-2} - \frac{a_r}{2} \sum_{j=1}^2 \left( r_{t-j} - r_{t-j}^* \right) + \epsilon_{\tilde{y}, t} \\ \pi_t &= b_1 \pi_{t-1} + (1 - b_1) \pi_{t-2, t-4} + b_y \tilde{y}_{t-1} + \epsilon_{\pi, t} \\ r_t^* &= g_t + z_t \end{aligned} \]

where \(g_t\) is trend potential growth and \(z_t\) captures secular shifts in savings and investment preferences, both following random walks.

Theorem 5.2 · Empirical Natural Rate Compression

Across the United States, Eurozone, United Kingdom, and Canada, Kalman filter estimates of \(r^*\) fell from approximately 2.5% to 3.5% in the 1960–1980 era to near zero (0.0% to 0.75%) by 2016:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta r_{1990 \to 2016}^* &\approx -180 \text{ to } -250 \text{ bps} \end{aligned} \]

The decline is overwhelmingly attributed to demographic aging (increasing desired savings), slowing total factor productivity growth \(g_t\), and a global savings glut desiring safe dollar assets.

Conversely, skeptics highlight that historical centuries of real interest rate data exhibit persistent mean reversion around capital returns. The post-2022 resurgence in global inflation, coupled with massive fiscal debt issuance and artificial intelligence capital expenditures, saw real yields rise back to 2.0%+, challenging the permanence of the secular stagnation thesis (Blanchard 2019).

Lab 1 · Volatility Management Net of Frictions & Kalman r* Estimation

We evaluate the gross vs net Sharpe ratio of a Moreira-Muir volatility-managed portfolio under proportional transaction costs, and estimate a state-space Kalman filter proxy for the natural rate of interest r*.

"""Lab CAP2-L05-1: Empirical tests for volatility management after costs and the secular r* decline.

Evaluates Moreira-Muir (2017) volatility-timing portfolio performance net of
turnover costs, and implements a state-space Kalman filter proxy for the natural
real rate of interest r* (Holston, Laubach & Williams 2017).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def evaluate_volatility_management(
    base_returns: np.ndarray,
    realized_vols: np.ndarray,
    target_vol: float = 0.12,
    transaction_cost_bps: float = 10.0,
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Compute gross and net Sharpe ratios of Moreira-Muir volatility-managed portfolio."""
    # Sizing: w_t = c / sigma_t^2, normalized so average vol matches target_vol
    raw_weights = 1.0 / (realized_vols**2)
    c_scale = target_vol / float(np.std(raw_weights * base_returns, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))
    weights = c_scale * raw_weights

    # Gross returns
    r_gross = weights * base_returns

    # Turnover and transaction costs
    turnover = np.abs(np.diff(weights))
    cost_drag = (transaction_cost_bps * 1e-4) * np.concatenate([[0.0], turnover])
    r_net = r_gross - cost_drag

    sr_base = float(np.mean(base_returns) / np.std(base_returns, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))
    sr_gross = float(np.mean(r_gross) / np.std(r_gross, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))
    sr_net = float(np.mean(r_net) / np.std(r_net, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0))
    mean_turnover = float(np.mean(turnover) * 12.0)

    return sr_base, sr_gross, sr_net, mean_turnover


def kalman_rstar_estimate(
    real_rate_series: np.ndarray,
    output_gap_series: np.ndarray,
) -> tuple[float, float, np.ndarray]:
    """Estimate natural real rate r* via 1D state-space Kalman filter proxy."""
    # State equation: r*_t = r*_{t-1} + e_{r, t}, e_r ~ N(0, sigma_r^2)
    # Observation equation: r_t = r*_t + theta * y_t + v_t, v ~ N(0, sigma_v^2)
    n = len(real_rate_series)
    sigma_r_sq = 0.0001
    sigma_v_sq = 0.0004
    theta = 0.25

    # Prior state
    x_hat = float(real_rate_series[0])
    p_hat = 0.01

    r_star_path = np.zeros(n)
    for t in range(n):
        # Time update (prediction)
        x_pred = x_hat
        p_pred = p_hat + sigma_r_sq

        # Measurement update
        y_obs = real_rate_series[t] - theta * output_gap_series[t]
        y_resid = y_obs - x_pred
        f_val = p_pred + sigma_v_sq
        k_gain = p_pred / f_val

        x_hat = x_pred + k_gain * y_resid
        p_hat = (1.0 - k_gain) * p_pred
        r_star_path[t] = x_hat

    start_rstar = float(r_star_path[0])
    terminal_rstar = float(r_star_path[-1])

    return start_rstar, terminal_rstar, r_star_path


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2017)

    # 1. Resolution 9: Volatility-Managed Portfolios After Frictions
    n_months = 240
    # Regime-switching / heteroskedastic return series
    vol_regimes = rng.choice([0.10, 0.25], size=n_months, p=[0.75, 0.25])
    month_vols = vol_regimes / np.sqrt(12.0)
    market_returns = rng.normal(0.006, month_vols)

    sr_base, sr_gross, sr_net, ann_turnover = evaluate_volatility_management(
        market_returns, month_vols, target_vol=0.12, transaction_cost_bps=12.0
    )

    print("--- Resolution 9: Volatility-Managed Portfolios After Frictions ---")
    print(f"Unmanaged Market Sharpe Ratio:         {sr_base:.3f}")
    print(f"Volatility-Managed Gross Sharpe Ratio: {sr_gross:.3f}")
    print(f"Volatility-Managed Net Sharpe Ratio:   {sr_net:.3f}")
    print(f"Annual Portfolio Turnover:             {ann_turnover:.1f}x")
    print(f"Net Alpha Retention:                   {'POSITIVE EDGE' if sr_net > sr_base else 'EROSION'}")

    # 2. Resolution 10: Secular r* Decline
    # 40 quarters (10 years) of real rates showing secular drop from 2.5% to 0.5%
    trends = np.linspace(0.025, 0.005, 40)
    real_rate = trends + rng.normal(0.0, 0.004, size=40)
    out_gap = rng.normal(0.0, 0.015, size=40)

    start_r, end_r, _ = kalman_rstar_estimate(real_rate, out_gap)

    print("\n--- Resolution 10: Has r* Fallen Permanently? ---")
    print(f"Initial Estimated r* (Annualized):  {start_r * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print(f"Terminal Estimated r* (Annualized): {end_r * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print(f"Estimated Secular r* Shift:        {(end_r - start_r) * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print(f"Structural Break Significance:     {'PERMANENT DECLINE' if end_r < 0.015 else 'STABLE'}")


main()

3. Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook equilibrium asset pricing assumes costless leverage and stable natural interest rates:

  • Leverage Constraints & Margin Haircuts: Volatility management requires high leverage precisely during low-volatility regimes. When volatility abruptly spikes, prime brokers raise margin requirements, forcing fire-sale liquidations at market bottoms.
  • Fiscal-Monetary Dominance Shifts: The assumption that \(r^*\) is purely determined by real demographic factors breaks down when sovereign debt ratios exceed 100% of GDP, forcing central banks to balance inflation stabilization against government debt servicing sustainability.

4. Debate Briefs

Debate 9 · Resolution: "Vol-managed portfolios win after costs."

Resolution: Vol-managed portfolios win after costs.

Steelman For: Moreira & Muir (2017). Volatility clustering is one of the most robust statistical properties of financial time series, while expected returns do not spike during volatility crises. Scaling down exposure during turbulent markets sidesteps major crashes (e.g. 1929, 2008). In liquid futures and ETF markets, turnover costs are under 5 bps, preserving substantial net alpha.

Steelman Against: Real-time volatility estimates suffer look-ahead bias and lag sudden structural breaks. Realized drawdowns in levered low-volatility regimes wipe out years of accumulated alpha. For individual equity anomalies and illiquid factors, high portfolio rebalancing turnover and short borrow costs erode 100% of theoretical alpha.

State of the Literature: Broad consensus that volatility management enhances Sharpe ratios on broad equity index futures where liquidity is deep, but strong evidence that it fails after costs on cross-sectional individual stock anomalies.

What Would Settle It: A 10-year live institutional fund audit trading volatility-managed vs unmanaged multi-asset strategies with prime broker financing slips; if net Sharpe ratio outperformance exceeds 0.20, the affirmative thesis is definitively established.

Debate 10 · Resolution: "r* has fallen permanently."

Resolution: r* has fallen permanently.

Steelman For: Holston, Laubach & Williams (2017). Irreversible secular demographic shifts—prolonged life expectancy, aging workforces, and low fertility—guarantee high desired savings and lower capital investment requirements. Coupled with permanent slowdowns in total factor productivity growth, the equilibrium real rate \(r^*\) is structurally anchored near zero.

Steelman Against: The apparent fall in \(r^*\) was an artifact of post-GFC debt deleveraging and central bank quantitative easing. Massive structural green-energy capital transitions, geopolitical fragmentation, and post-pandemic fiscal dominance demand immense capital, pushing equilibrium real rates back toward historical norms of 2.0% to 3.0% (Blanchard 2019).

State of the Literature: Deeply divided between structural secular stagnation advocates and monetary-fiscal regime theorists who view low real rates as a transient cycle.

What Would Settle It: A 15-year period of sustained real economic growth (> 2.5%) where sovereign 10-year real yields trade above 2.5% without inducing private credit defaults or deflationary collapse.

5. Misconceptions

  • Scaling risk inversely to volatility always reduces the portfolio's maximum potential drawdown.

    Because volatility management applies high leverage during quiet market regimes, a sudden jump in volatility (such as the 1987 crash or 2020 pandemic onset) hits the portfolio at peak leverage, generating severe initial drawdown before positions can be de-risked.

  • A lower neutral rate r* means that stock prices will continually appreciate at faster rates.

    While a lower discount rate elevates initial valuation multiples, future expected cash flow growth is often lower in a secular stagnation regime, resulting in lower subsequent expected equity returns.

  • Central banks set the equilibrium natural real rate of interest r*.

    Central banks control the nominal policy rate in the short run; the natural real rate \(r^*\) is determined by structural real factors: household savings preferences, demographics, capital productivity, and fiscal policy.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1: Mechanics

An unmanaged market portfolio has an annualized mean return of 8.0% and volatility of 16.0%. A volatility-managed portfolio achieves an annualized return of 9.5% with the same 16.0% volatility. Calculate the Sharpe ratio improvement.

Solution

The unmanaged Sharpe ratio is \(SR_0 = 0.08 / 0.16 = 0.50\). The volatility-managed Sharpe ratio is \(SR_\sigma = 0.095 / 0.16 = 0.59375\). The Sharpe ratio improvement is:

\[ \Delta SR = 0.59375 - 0.50000 = +0.09375 \]

The Sharpe ratio increases by approximately 0.094.

If a volatility-managed strategy incurs an annual turnover of 6.0x and trading costs are 10 bps per one-way trade, calculate the annual transaction cost drag in percent.

Solution

Total cost drag is annual turnover multiplied by one-way trading cost:

\[ \text{Cost Drag} = 6.0 \times 10 \text{ bps} = 60 \text{ bps} = 0.60\% \]

The strategy loses 0.60% annually to execution frictions.

Under the Holston-Laubach-Williams decomposition \(r^* = g + z\), if trend growth is estimated at \(g = 1.2\%\) and the preference component is \(z = -0.5\%\), compute \(r^*\).

Solution

Substituting into the definition:

\[ r^* = 1.2\% + (-0.5\%) = 0.7\% \]

The estimated natural rate of interest is 0.70%.

Tier 2: Proof

Prove that if conditional expected returns are constant (\(\mu_t = \mu\)) while conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2\) varies, the volatility-managed portfolio \(w_t = c / \sigma_t^2\) generates higher expected utility for a mean-variance investor.

Solution

A mean-variance objective is \(U(w_t) = w_t \mu_t - \frac{\gamma}{2} w_t^2 \sigma_t^2\). The first-order condition with respect to \(w_t\) is \(\mu_t - \gamma w_t \sigma_t^2 = 0 \implies w_t^* = \frac{\mu_t}{\gamma \sigma_t^2}\). If \(\mu_t = \mu\) is constant while \(\sigma_t^2\) varies, the static constant-weight portfolio \(w_t = \bar{w}\) is sub-optimal. The dynamic weight \(w_t^* \propto 1/\sigma_t^2\) strictly achieves the unconstrained maximum of expected utility period by period, yielding higher expected utility. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that in the Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans neoclassical growth model, the steady-state natural real interest rate satisfies \(r^* = \theta g + \rho\).

Solution

Consider a representative household maximizing \(\int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t} \frac{C_t^{1-\theta}-1}{1-\theta} dt\). The Euler equation for consumption growth is \(\frac{\dot{C}_t}{C_t} = \frac{1}{\theta} (r_t - \rho)\). In steady state, consumption grows at the rate of labor-augmenting technological progress \(g = \frac{\dot{C}_t}{C_t}\). Equating and solving for \(r_t = r^*\):

\[ g = \frac{1}{\theta} (r^* - \rho) \implies r^* = \theta g + \rho \]

The natural rate is determined by the intertemporal elasticity of substitution \(\theta\), trend productivity growth \(g\), and the rate of time preference \(\rho\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that proportional transaction costs \(\kappa\) reduce the Sharpe ratio of an active strategy with turnover \(T\) and volatility \(\sigma\).

Solution

Let gross return have mean \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\). Net return is \(\mu_{\text{net}} = \mu - \kappa T\), while volatility \(\sigma_{\text{net}} \approx \sigma\) (since deterministic trading costs have zero variance). The net Sharpe ratio is:

\[ SR_{\text{net}} = \frac{\mu - \kappa T}{\sigma} = \frac{\mu}{\sigma} - \kappa \frac{T}{\sigma} = SR_{\text{gross}} - \kappa \frac{T}{\sigma} \]

Since \(\kappa, T, \sigma > 0\), \(SR_{\text{net}} < SR_{\text{gross}}\), with the performance penalty proportional to the turnover-to-volatility ratio. \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3: Empirical

In an empirical backtest of a volatility-managed strategy on momentum, the gross alpha is 3.5% with annual turnover of 8.0x. If institutional execution costs are 25 bps per one-way turn, determine whether the net alpha is positive.

Solution

Total transaction cost drag is:

\[ \text{Cost} = 8.0 \times 0.0025 = 0.0200 \text{ (or } 2.00\% \text{)} \]

The net alpha is \(\alpha_{\text{net}} = 3.5\% - 2.0\% = +1.5\%\). The net alpha remains positive at 1.50% per year.

Explain why the Laubach-Williams Kalman filter estimates of r* experienced substantial revisions and wide confidence intervals during the 2020–2023 pandemic episode.

Solution

The state-space model identifies \(r^*\) by relating output gap deviations to real interest rate gaps. During 2020–2023, extreme supply-side shutdowns and fiscal transfers caused massive output swings that were uncoupled from monetary interest rate transmission. The Kalman filter struggled to decompose whether GDP fluctuations were transitory supply shocks or permanent shifts in trend potential, widening parameter uncertainty.

Suppose a prime broker imposes a 20% margin requirement on equity index futures during tranquil regimes, but escalates it to 40% when the VIX exceeds 30. How does this impact a Moreira-Muir portfolio?

Solution

During tranquil regimes, the portfolio is levered up (e.g. 1.5x to 2.0x). When the VIX spikes above 30, the portfolio needs to de-risk. However, the simultaneous doubling of the margin requirement from 20% to 40% forces immediate asset sales to meet margin calls precisely when liquidity is low and bid-ask spreads are wide, creating severe execution friction.

Tier 4: Research

Propose a non-linear volatility scaling rule that mitigates crash risk during sudden volatility regime switches.

Solution

Replace linear inverse-variance scaling \(w_t \propto 1/\sigma_t^2\) with an asymmetric power scaling rule conditioned on forward option-implied skewness: \(w_t = \frac{c}{\sigma_t^\alpha} \times (1 - \lambda \cdot \text{Skew}_t)\), where \(\alpha \in [0.5, 1.0]\) prevents extreme leverage in quiet markets, and \(\text{Skew}_t\) automatically downsizes exposure when market participants aggressively bid for deep out-of-the-money disaster puts.

How does the expansion of government debt past 120% of GDP alter the determination of r* in overlapping generations models?

Solution

In overlapping generations models (e.g. Diamond 1965, Blanchard 2019), public debt absorbs private savings that would otherwise fund productive capital. High debt crowd-out reduces physical capital per worker, raising the marginal product of capital and pushing \(r^*\) upward, conflicting with the secular stagnation thesis of permanently depressed real rates.

Formulate a testable hypothesis linking global demographic dependency ratios to the cross-sectional distribution of sovereign real rates.

Solution

According to the life-cycle hypothesis, workers aged 40–60 have the highest propensity to save, while retirees aged 65+ dissave. Regress sovereign 10-year real yields on the ratio of prime savers to retirees across 30 OECD countries: \(\text{RealRate}_{i, t} = \alpha_i + \beta \left(\frac{\text{Workers}_{40-60}}{\text{Retirees}_{65+}}\right)_{i, t} + \epsilon_{i, t}\). A negative coefficient \(\beta < 0\) confirms demographic savings saturation.
