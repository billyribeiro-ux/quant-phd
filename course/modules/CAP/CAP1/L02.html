Statistical significance in observational financial panels is cheap; credible identification is rare. When evaluating empirical claims as a referee or researcher, you must pierce the veil of researcher degrees of freedom. By auditing the garden of forking paths, constructing specification curves, and applying Shanken errors-in-variables corrections, you determine whether an asset pricing premium reflects genuine economic compensation or econometric fragility.

1The Garden of Forking Paths in Empirical Asset Pricing

Researchers rarely test a single pre-specified hypothesis on a pristine dataset. Instead, empirical asset pricing proceeds through what Gelman & Loken (2014) define as the "garden of forking paths": a vast sequence of data-contingent choices made during data cleaning, sample filtering, variable definition, and model specification. Even in the complete absence of malicious intent or deliberate p-hacking, researcher degrees of freedom lead researchers to select choices that yield statistically significant results aligning with economic intuition.

In cross-sectional asset pricing, these choices compound exponentially. Consider an investigator examining a new fundamental signal:

  • Sample Period: Full sample, post-1963 (post-Compustat coverage), post-1972 (post-NASDAQ inclusion), or omitting recession quarters.
  • Universe Filters: NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ ordinary common stocks (SHRCD 10/11) vs all equity securities; minimum share price ($1, $5, or none); book equity positivity filters.
  • Signal Definition: Ratios computed with fiscal year-end vs quarterlyCompustat filings; lagged 3 months vs 6 months to ensure public disclosure.
  • Outlier Treatment: Unadjusted vs trimming vs winsorization at 0.5%, 1.0%, or 2.5% thresholds, computed pooled or cross-sectionally.
  • Portfolio Weighting: Equal-weighted vs value-weighted; independent vs conditional multi-way sorts; all-stock breakpoints vs NYSE-only breakpoints.

Lo & MacKinlay (1990) established that repeated conditioning on the same historical CRSP/Compustat dataset biases test statistics upward, transforming statistical noise into apparent anomalies. When thousands of academic and industry researchers query the same 60 years of US equity history, the effective number of trials exceeds the nominal degrees of freedom by orders of magnitude.

2Specification Curve Analysis

To audit whether an empirical result is robust or sits upon an isolated branch of the forking path, referees employ specification curve analysis. Instead of relying on an arbitrary "baseline" table and a handful of ad-hoc robustness checks, the investigator articulates the full combinatorial grid of all defensible, non-redundant methodological specifications.

Definition 2.1 (Specification Grid)

A specification grid \(\mathcal{S}\) is the Cartesian product of discrete methodological choices across sample definitions \(\mathcal{U}_{\text{sample}}\), variable definitions \(\mathcal{U}_{\text{var}}\), outlier filters \(\mathcal{U}_{\text{outlier}}\), and inference models \(\mathcal{U}_{\text{inf}}\): \[ \begin{gathered} \mathcal{S} = \mathcal{U}_{\text{sample}} \times \mathcal{U}_{\text{var}} \\ \times \mathcal{U}_{\text{outlier}} \times \mathcal{U}_{\text{inf}} \end{gathered} \] For each specification \(s \in \mathcal{S}\), the researcher estimates the point estimate \(\hat{\theta}_s\) and standard error \(\widehat{\se}(\hat{\theta}_s)\). The specification curve is the ordered sequence of estimates \(\hat{\theta}_{(k)}\) (\(k = 1, \dots, |\mathcal{S}|\)) plotted alongside 95% confidence intervals and indicator panels designating the methodological choices active in each trial.

A credible empirical claim displays a specification curve whose median effect size remains economically meaningful, and whose interquartile range excludes zero. Conversely, if the reported published specification occupies the extreme upper tail of \(\mathcal{S}\), with the vast majority of reasonable perturbations collapsing to zero, the referee report must identify the finding as an artifact of specification hunting.

3Errors-in-Variables & The Shanken (1992) Correction

In two-pass cross-sectional regressions (Fama & MacBeth 1973), risk premia \(\lambda\) are estimated by regressing mean excess asset returns \(\bar{R}\) onto factor betas \(\hat{\beta}\) estimated from a first-pass time-series regression. Because the true betas \(\beta\) are unobservable, first-pass estimates contain sampling error: \[ \hat{\beta}_i = \beta_i + \nu_i \] This errors-in-variables (EIV) problem introduces two distinct econometric hazards: attenuation bias in the point estimates, and severe underestimation of second-pass standard errors if naive OLS formulas are used.

Theorem 2.1 (Shanken Factor Risk Premium Variance Inflation)

Let \(N\) test assets have returns governed by a \(K\)-factor linear model over \(T\) periods: \[ \begin{gathered} R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i' f_t + \epsilon_{i,t} \\ \E[\epsilon_t] = 0 \\ \Cov(\epsilon_t) = \Sigma_\epsilon \\ \Cov(f_t) = \Sigma_f \end{gathered} \] In the second pass, mean returns \(\bar{R}\) are regressed on estimated betas: \[ \bar{R} = \hat{\beta} \lambda + \alpha \] Under standard asymptotic assumptions as \(T \to \infty\) with \(N\) fixed, the asymptotic covariance matrix of the two-pass risk premium estimator \(\hat{\lambda}\) satisfies: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{AsyVar}(\hat{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{T} \Big[ &(\beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \beta)^{-1} (1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda) \\ &+ \Sigma_f \Big] \end{aligned} \] In particular, because \(\Sigma_f\) is positive definite, the quadratic form \(\lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda > 0\) whenever \(\lambda \neq 0\). Consequently, the Shanken correction factor \((1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda)\) strictly exceeds unity, proving that naive Fama-MacBeth standard errors systematically underestimate true sampling dispersion.

Proof

We trace the asymptotic expansion of \(\hat{\lambda}\) following Shanken (1992). The second-pass GLS estimator is: \[ \hat{\lambda} = (\hat{\beta}' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta}' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \bar{R} \] Decompose sample mean returns as \(\bar{R} = \beta \lambda + \beta \bar{f} + \bar{\epsilon}\). Since \(\hat{\beta} = \beta + \Delta_\beta\), where \(\sqrt{T} \text{vec}(\Delta_\beta) \dto \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_f^{-1} \otimes \Sigma_\epsilon)\): \[ \bar{R} = \hat{\beta} \lambda - \Delta_\beta \lambda + \beta \bar{f} + \bar{\epsilon} \] Substituting this into the estimator: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\lambda} - \lambda &= (\hat{\beta}' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta}' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \\ &\quad \times \left( -\Delta_\beta \lambda + \beta \bar{f} + \bar{\epsilon} \right) \end{aligned} \] As \(T \to \infty\), \(\hat{\beta} \pto \beta\). Therefore, the normalized error vector satisfies: \[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{T}(\hat{\lambda} - \lambda) &= (\beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \beta)^{-1} \beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \\ &\quad \times \left( -\sqrt{T}\Delta_\beta \lambda + \sqrt{T}\bar{\epsilon} \right) + \sqrt{T}\bar{f} + o_p(1) \end{aligned} \] The first-pass estimation error term \(-\Delta_\beta \lambda\) is uncorrelated with factor realizations \(\bar{f}\). The asymptotic variance of the bracketed term \(-\sqrt{T}\Delta_\beta \lambda + \sqrt{T}\bar{\epsilon}\) is given by: \[ \begin{aligned} \Var\left( -\sqrt{T}\Delta_\beta \lambda + \sqrt{T}\bar{\epsilon} \right) &= \Sigma_\epsilon (\lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda) + \Sigma_\epsilon \\ &= \Sigma_\epsilon (1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda) \end{aligned} \] Pre-multiplying by \((\beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \beta)^{-1} \beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1}\) and post-multiplying by its transpose: \[ \begin{aligned} &(\beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \beta)^{-1} \beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \left[ \Sigma_\epsilon (1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda) \right] \\ &\quad \times \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \beta (\beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \beta)^{-1} = (\beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \beta)^{-1} (1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda) \end{aligned} \] Adding the independent factor realization variance \(\Sigma_f\) from \(\sqrt{T}\bar{f}\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{AsyVar}(\hat{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{T} \Big[ &(\beta' \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \beta)^{-1} (1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda) \\ &+ \Sigma_f \Big] \end{aligned} \] Since \(\Sigma_f\) is positive definite, for any non-zero risk premium vector \(\lambda \neq 0\), the quadratic form \(\lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda > 0\). Thus: \[ 1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda > 1 \] The naive variance estimator omits this factor, proving that naive standard errors are strictly downward biased.

Lab 1 · Specification curve and Shanken (1992) correction calculator

This lab calculates the Shanken (1992) asymptotic variance multiplier for a two-factor asset pricing model and compares naive Fama-MacBeth standard errors against Shanken-corrected standard errors across simulated factor realizations.

"""Lab 2: Specification curve generator and Shanken (1992) correction calculator."""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def compute_shanken_adjustment(
    lambda_vec: np.ndarray,
    factor_cov: np.ndarray,
    naive_se: np.ndarray,
    t_obs: int,
) -> tuple[float, np.ndarray]:
    """Compute Shanken (1992) asymptotic correction factor for factor risk premia.

    Correction factor: c = 1 + lambda' * Sigma_f^(-1) * lambda
    Shanken SE = sqrt(naive_var * c + diag(Sigma_f) / T)
    """
    inv_factor_cov = np.linalg.pinv(factor_cov)
    quad_form = float(lambda_vec.T @ inv_factor_cov @ lambda_vec)
    shanken_factor = 1.0 + quad_form

    naive_var = naive_se**2
    shanken_var = naive_var * shanken_factor + np.diag(factor_cov) / float(t_obs)
    shanken_se = np.sqrt(shanken_var)
    return shanken_factor, shanken_se


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
    t_obs = 360

    # 2 Factors: Market and Value
    f_returns = rng.multivariate_normal(mean=[0.006, 0.003], cov=[[0.0020, 0.0004], [0.0004, 0.0016]], size=t_obs)
    factor_cov = np.cov(f_returns, rowvar=False)

    # Estimated risk premia lambda (annualized ~ 7.2% and 3.6%)
    lambda_hat = np.array([0.0060, 0.0030])
    naive_se = np.array([0.0023, 0.0020])

    shanken_factor, shanken_se = compute_shanken_adjustment(lambda_hat, factor_cov, naive_se, t_obs)

    print("Shanken (1992) Factor Premium Inference:")
    print(f"  T = {t_obs} observations")
    print(f"  Quadratic form lambda' Sigma_f^(-1) lambda: {shanken_factor - 1.0:.4f}")
    print(f"  Shanken multiplicative factor (1 + q): {shanken_factor:.4f}")
    r0 = shanken_se[0] / naive_se[0]
    r1 = shanken_se[1] / naive_se[1]
    t0_naive = lambda_hat[0] / naive_se[0]
    t0_shanken = lambda_hat[0] / shanken_se[0]
    print(f"  Factor 1: Naive SE = {naive_se[0]:.5f} -> Shanken SE = {shanken_se[0]:.5f} (SE ratio = {r0:.2f})")
    print(f"  Factor 2: Naive SE = {naive_se[1]:.5f} -> Shanken SE = {shanken_se[1]:.5f} (SE ratio = {r1:.2f})")
    print(f"  Naive t-stat Factor 1: {t0_naive:.2f} -> Shanken t-stat: {t0_shanken:.2f}")

4Where the Canon Breaks

The standard textbook treatment of cross-sectional asset pricing asserts that Fama-MacBeth two-pass regressions automatically resolve cross-sectional correlation by averaging monthly slope coefficients. This canon breaks down in empirical practice:

  • Serial Correlation in Risk Premia: While Fama-MacBeth standard errors account for cross-sectional residual correlation within month \(t\), they assume that risk premia \(\lambda_t\) are serially uncorrelated over time. In macroeconomic and volatility-state factors, \(\lambda_t\) exhibits severe autocorrelation, requiring Newey-West lag corrections.
  • Finite-Sample Beta Instability: The Shanken correction assumes that the number of test assets \(N\) is fixed as time periods \(T \to \infty\). When \(N\) is large relative to \(T\) (e.g. \(N = 100\), \(T = 300\)), sample covariance matrix inversion introduces substantial finite-sample noise that standard asymptotic theory ignores.
  • Specification Cherry-Picking: Authors routinely report only the single specification that survives Fama-MacBeth and Shanken testing, concealing the hundreds of negative specification permutations that were discarded during exploratory analysis.

5Misconceptions

  1. “If a t-statistic exceeds 2.0 in an observational panel regression, the underlying relationship is statistically reliable.”

    A t-statistic of 2.0 corresponds to \(p = 0.05\) only under a single pre-registered hypothesis test. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) show that when researchers test multiple specifications across the factor zoo, a nominal t-statistic of 2.0 has an empirical p-value closer to 0.40 due to multiple testing.

  2. “The Shanken correction is only necessary when first-pass beta estimation regressions have low R-squared values.”

    The Shanken multiplicative factor \(1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\) depends on the magnitude of the factor risk premium \(\lambda\) and factor covariance \(\Sigma_f\), not on the idiosyncratic fit of test assets. Even with high first-pass precision, non-zero risk premia strictly inflate second-pass asymptotic variance.

  3. “A specification curve analysis is successful only if every single specification in the grid yields a statistically significant positive coefficient.”

    Real-world financial signals are naturally sensitive to sample period variations and outlier treatments. A successful specification curve requires that the central tendency (median) is robust and that rejections occur only under extreme, poorly justified parameter combinations.

Mechanics

M1 · Calculating the Shanken Multiplier

A two-factor asset pricing model estimates annualized factor risk premia \(\lambda = [0.06, 0.04]'\). The factor covariance matrix is \(\Sigma_f = \begin{bmatrix} 0.04 & 0.01 \\ 0.01 & 0.02 \end{bmatrix}\). Calculate the quadratic form \(\lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\) and the exact Shanken multiplicative factor \((1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda)\).

Solution

First, invert the \(2 \times 2\) covariance matrix. The determinant is: \[ \det(\Sigma_f) = 0.04 \times 0.02 - 0.01 \times 0.01 = 0.0008 - 0.0001 = 0.0007 \] The inverse matrix is: \[ \Sigma_f^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.0007} \begin{bmatrix} 0.02 & -0.01 \\ -0.01 & 0.04 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 28.5714 & -14.2857 \\ -14.2857 & 57.1429 \end{bmatrix} \] Compute the quadratic form \(\lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\): \[ \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 28.5714(0.06) - 14.2857(0.04) \\ -14.2857(0.06) + 57.1429(0.04) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.7143 - 0.5714 \\ -0.8571 + 2.2857 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.1429 \\ 1.4286 \end{bmatrix} \] Multiplying by \(\lambda'\): \[ \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda = 0.06(1.1429) + 0.04(1.4286) = 0.06857 + 0.05714 = 0.12571 \] The Shanken factor is: \[ 1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda = 1.0 + 0.12571 \approx 1.1257 \] The naive second-pass variance must be inflated by 12.57%.

M2 · Impact of Shanken Correction on Factor T-Statistics

Suppose a Fama-MacBeth regression reports a naive factor risk premium of \(\hat{\lambda} = 0.0050\) per month with a naive standard error of \(\widehat{\se}_{\text{naive}} = 0.0022\) (\(t_{\text{naive}} = 2.27\)). If the Shanken factor is \(1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda = 1.35\) and \(T = 360\) months with factor variance \(\sigma_f^2 = 0.0016\), calculate the corrected Shanken standard error and new t-statistic.

Solution

Under Theorem 2.1, the corrected variance is: \[ \Var_{\text{Shanken}} = \Var_{\text{naive}} \times (1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda) + \frac{\sigma_f^2}{T} \] Compute each component: \[ \Var_{\text{naive}} = (0.0022)^2 = 4.84 \times 10^{-6} \] \[ \Var_{\text{naive}} \times 1.35 = 4.84 \times 10^{-6} \times 1.35 = 6.534 \times 10^{-6} \] \[ \frac{\sigma_f^2}{T} = \frac{0.0016}{360} \approx 4.444 \times 10^{-6} \] Total Shanken variance: \[ \Var_{\text{Shanken}} = 6.534 \times 10^{-6} + 4.444 \times 10^{-6} = 1.0978 \times 10^{-5} \] Taking the square root: \[ \widehat{\se}_{\text{Shanken}} = \sqrt{1.0978 \times 10^{-5}} \approx 0.003313 \] The corrected t-statistic is: \[ t_{\text{Shanken}} = \frac{0.0050}{0.003313} \approx 1.51 \] The factor was nominally significant at \(t = 2.27\), but drops below statistical significance (\(t = 1.51\)) after correcting for generated regressors.

M3 · Combinatorics of a Specification Grid

A referee audits an empirical study with 4 sample start dates, 3 minimum share price filters, 2 winsorization thresholds (1% vs 5%), 2 weighting schemes (EW vs VW), and 3 factor model benchmarks (CAPM, FF3, FF5). Calculate the total number of specifications in the resulting grid.

Solution

The total cardinality of the specification grid is the product of the number of options along each methodological dimension: \[ |\mathcal{S}| = 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 = 144 \text{ unique specifications} \] A complete specification curve analysis requires estimating and reporting results across all 144 model configurations.

P1 · Monotonicity of the Shanken Factor

Prove that for any two risk premium vectors \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\), if \(\lambda_2 = c \lambda_1\) with scalar \(|c| > 1\), the Shanken inflation factor satisfies \(1 + \lambda_2' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda_2 > 1 + \lambda_1' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda_1\).

Solution

Substitute \(\lambda_2 = c \lambda_1\) into the quadratic form: \[ \lambda_2' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda_2 = (c \lambda_1)' \Sigma_f^{-1} (c \lambda_1) = c^2 (\lambda_1' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda_1) \] Since \(\Sigma_f\) is positive definite and \(\lambda_1 \neq 0\), the quadratic form \(\lambda_1' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda_1 > 0\). If \(|c| > 1\), then \(c^2 > 1\). Therefore: \[ c^2 (\lambda_1' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda_1) > \lambda_1' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda_1 \implies 1 + \lambda_2' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda_2 > 1 + \lambda_1' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda_1 \] Larger estimated factor risk premia generate strictly larger errors-in-variables variance inflation factors.

P2 · Non-Negativity of Cameron-Gelbach-Miller Clustered Variances

In two-way clustering, the asymptotic covariance is estimated as \(V_{\text{2way}} = V_1 + V_2 - V_{12}\). Construct a formal argument explaining why \(V_{\text{2way}}\) is not guaranteed to be positive semi-definite in finite samples, and state the standard spectral truncation remedy.

Solution

Let \(V_1\) and \(V_2\) be the cluster covariance matrices along dimensions 1 and 2, and let \(V_{12}\) be the intersection cluster covariance matrix. While \(V_1, V_2, V_{12}\) are each individually positive semi-definite by construction, their linear combination \(V_1 + V_2 - V_{12}\) is the difference of positive semi-definite matrices. In finite samples with high collinearity or small cluster counts, the eigenvalues of \(V_{12}\) can exceed the corresponding eigenvalues of \(V_1 + V_2\), producing negative eigenvalues in \(V_{\text{2way}}\). The standard econometric remedy (Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2011) is spectral decomposition: write \(V_{\text{2way}} = Q \Lambda Q'\), where \(\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_K)\), and replace all negative eigenvalues with zero: \(\Lambda^+ = \text{diag}(\max(0, \lambda_k))\), defining \(V_{\text{2way}}^+ = Q \Lambda^+ Q'\).

P3 · Independence of Residual Orthogonalization and OLS Slopes

In the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) theorem, let \(Y = X_1 \beta_1 + X_2 \beta_2 + \epsilon\). Prove that the OLS estimate \(\hat{\beta}_1\) obtained from the multivariate regression is identical to the estimate obtained from regressing \(M_{X_2} Y\) on \(M_{X_2} X_1\), where \(M_{X_2} = I - X_2(X_2'X_2)^{-1}X_2'\).

Solution

The OLS normal equations for the partitioned system are: \[ \begin{bmatrix} X_1'X_1 & X_1'X_2 \\ X_2'X_1 & X_2'X_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\beta}_1 \\ \hat{\beta}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} X_1'Y \\ X_2'Y \end{bmatrix} \] From the second block of equations: \[ X_2'X_1 \hat{\beta}_1 + X_2'X_2 \hat{\beta}_2 = X_2'Y \implies \hat{\beta}_2 = (X_2'X_2)^{-1} X_2'(Y - X_1 \hat{\beta}_1) \] Substitute \(\hat{\beta}_2\) into the first equation: \[ \begin{aligned} X_1'X_1 \hat{\beta}_1 + X_1'X_2 &(X_2'X_2)^{-1} X_2' \\ &\times (Y - X_1 \hat{\beta}_1) = X_1'Y \end{aligned} \] Recognize that \(P_{X_2} = X_2(X_2'X_2)^{-1}X_2'\). Rearranging: \[ X_1'(I - P_{X_2})X_1 \hat{\beta}_1 = X_1'(I - P_{X_2})Y \] Since \(M_{X_2} = I - P_{X_2}\) is idempotent and symmetric (\(M_{X_2}' M_{X_2} = M_{X_2}\)): \[ \begin{aligned} (X_1' M_{X_2} X_1) \hat{\beta}_1 &= X_1' M_{X_2} Y \\ (M_{X_2}X_1)' (M_{X_2}X_1) \hat{\beta}_1 &= (M_{X_2}X_1)' (M_{X_2}Y) \end{aligned} \] Solving for \(\hat{\beta}_1\): \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\beta}_1 &= \left[ (M_{X_2}X_1)' (M_{X_2}X_1) \right]^{-1} \\ &\quad \times (M_{X_2}X_1)' (M_{X_2}Y) \end{aligned} \] This is the OLS slope from regressing the residualized outcome \(M_{X_2} Y\) on the residualized covariate \(M_{X_2} X_1\).

Empirical

E1 · Auditing a Cross-Sectional Size Anomaly

An author claims to discover an ultra-high return anomaly based on short-term patent growth. Decile 10 generates 1.40% per month, but 85% of the firms in Decile 10 are microcaps trading below $5 per share. How should the referee instruct the author to test robustness?

Solution

The referee should instruct the author to: (1) Re-estimate the decile portfolio returns using NYSE-only size breakpoints rather than pooled breakpoints, ensuring that Decile 10 does not represent an uninvestable microcap bucket. (2) Report value-weighted portfolio returns alongside equal-weighted returns. (3) Exclude stocks trading below $5 per share (penny stocks) at portfolio formation. (4) Compute net-of-cost returns applying transaction cost estimates from Novy-Marx & Velikov (2015).

E2 · Evaluating Specification Curve Skewness

A specification curve analysis across 200 model configurations yields point estimates ranging from -0.10% to +0.85%. The 10th percentile is -0.02%, the 50th percentile is +0.08% (p = 0.35), the 90th percentile is +0.45%, and the single specification highlighted in the paper is +0.85% (p = 0.01). Write the referee evaluation of this finding.

Solution

The referee evaluation must conclude: "The paper's reported primary result (+0.85%, p = 0.01) sits at the extreme 99th percentile of the defensible specification curve. The median empirical specification yields an economically negligible effect of +0.08% that is statistically indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.35). The reported finding is an extreme cherry-picked specification that does not survive standard robustness evaluation, indicating severe p-hacking or publication bias."

E3 · Errors-in-Variables in Industry Portfolios

An asset pricing paper tests the CAPM on 30 industry portfolios. The first-pass market betas range from 0.70 to 1.40. The author uses standard Fama-MacBeth second-pass standard errors to test whether the market risk premium is equal to historical average market excess returns. Why is this inference invalid?

Solution

Industry betas are estimated with finite-sample measurement error. When the second pass regresses mean returns on estimated betas, the sampling error \(\hat{\beta}_i - \beta_i\) induces classical errors-in-variables, attenuating the estimated market risk premium slope toward zero and inflating the estimated pricing intercept \(\alpha\). Furthermore, standard Fama-MacBeth standard errors omit the Shanken (1992) correction factor \(1 + \lambda_m^2 / \sigma_m^2\), producing falsely narrow confidence intervals and over-rejecting the null hypothesis.

Research

R1 · Refereeing Machine Learning Factor Models

A submitted paper applies a deep neural network with 10 hidden layers to predict monthly stock returns from 100 firm characteristics, reporting an annualized out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 3.2. As a referee, what structural identification and data leakages would you interrogate first?

Solution

The referee must systematically interrogate: (1) Standardization leakage: were characteristic transformations (e.g. cross-sectional rank normalization, winsorization thresholds) computed out-of-sample period by period, or fit once across the full time series? Full-sample scaling leaks future distributions into past training data. (2) Microcap weighting: is the high Sharpe ratio driven by equal-weighted positions in tiny, illiquid stocks that cannot be shorted at scale? (3) Restatement look-ahead: did the feature matrix use point-in-time Compustat data or restated historical files? (4) Hyperparameter snooping: were architecture choices tuned by evaluating performance on the test period?

R2 · Pre-Analysis Plans in Financial Economics

Why are formal pre-analysis plans (PAPs) widely adopted in clinical trials and randomized controlled trials in development economics, but rarely utilized in empirical financial economics? What are the structural barriers?

Solution

In randomized trials, the researcher generates new experimental data after writing the pre-analysis plan. In financial economics, the data (historical prices and balance sheets) already exist and are known to researchers before any prospective study commences. A researcher could explore the historical dataset informally, discover which specification succeeds, and subsequently write a "pre-analysis plan" tailored to that known finding. Pre-registration in finance is only fully credible for genuinely prospective out-of-sample data (e.g., locking a model before 2026 data unfolds) or proprietary datasets newly opened to researchers.

R3 · Disentangling Risk Premia from Behavioral Mispricing

An author claims that an anomaly reflects compensation for systematic distress risk because the long-short spread co-moves with market downturns. Design a referee request requiring an empirical test that formally separates risk compensation from mispricing.

Solution

The referee should demand a two-pronged test: (1) Spanning and factor regression test: evaluate whether the anomaly's alpha survives after controlling for established traded risk factors (Fama-French 5-factor plus momentum and liquidity). (2) Characteristic vs covariance test per Daniel & Titman (1997): construct triple-sorted portfolios that hold factor factor loadings constant while varying the characteristic, and vice versa. If expected returns vary with the characteristic after fixing factor betas, the return spread reflects mispricing or characteristic-based demand rather than systematic covariance risk.

8Retrieval Quiz

  1. What does the term "the garden of forking paths" refer to in the context of empirical research?

    Options

    Gelman & Loken (2014) define the garden of forking paths as researcher degrees of freedom that influence empirical results through plausible, data-dependent choices.

  2. Why does the Shanken (1992) asymptotic variance correction factor always strictly exceed 1.0 when factor risk premia are non-zero?

    Options

    For any positive definite covariance matrix \(\Sigma_f\) and non-zero vector \(\lambda\), the quadratic form \(\lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\) is strictly positive, ensuring \(1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda > 1\).

  3. What is a specification curve analysis?

    Options

    Specification curve analysis visualizes the entire distribution of estimates across all reasonable methodological permutations.

  4. What consequence does classical measurement error in a predictive regressor have on the estimated OLS slope?

    Options

    Classical measurement error scales the true slope by the reliability ratio \(\Var(X)/(\Var(X) + \sigma_\nu^2) < 1\), attenuating the estimate toward zero.

  5. If the quadratic form \(\lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda = 0.25\), what is the Shanken multiplicative factor?

    The Shanken factor is \(1 + \lambda' \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda = 1.0 + 0.25 = 1.25\).

  6. When two-way clustering covariance yields negative eigenvalues in finite samples, what is the standard econometric fix?

    Options

    Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2011) recommend spectral truncation, setting negative eigenvalues to zero to preserve positive semi-definiteness.

  7. How does data snooping bias arise in empirical asset pricing according to Lo & MacKinlay (1990)?

    Options

    Lo & MacKinlay (1990) show that when researchers repeatedly query the same historical sample, statistical properties of tests are biased toward rejection.

  8. In the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, what does regressing residualized outcome on residualized covariate isolate?

    Options

    The FWL theorem shows that partial regression isolates the slope after orthogonalizing with respect to the control matrix.

  9. Why are Fama-MacBeth standard errors potentially downward biased when applied to macroeconomic factor risk premia?

    Options

    Fama-MacBeth slope standard errors assume i.i.d. draws over time. If monthly slopes \(\lambda_t\) are serially correlated, Newey-West adjustments are required.

  10. How many unique model specifications exist in a grid with 3 sample periods, 4 control configurations, 3 outlier rules, and 4 clustering choices?

    \(3 \times 4 \times 3 \times 4 = 144\) specifications.
