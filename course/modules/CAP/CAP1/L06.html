Replication in Practice: Open-Source Asset Pricing & Meta-Science

A doctoral scholar must be capable of translating econometric forensics into scientific synthesis. Large-scale replication initiatives have revolutionized finance, shifting the discipline from isolated idiosyncratic code scripts to shared open-source architectures. Understanding how anomalies behave out-of-sample and post-publication equips researchers to construct rigorous replication diff tables and write authoritative referee evaluations.

1Large-Scale Replication Initiatives in Asset Pricing

Over the past half-century, academic finance published hundreds of return predictability patterns, creating what Cochrane (2011) termed a "zoo of new factors." To determine whether these anomalies represent genuine economic phenomena or collective data-mining artifacts, two landmark replication initiatives re-evaluated the entire literature using standardized, transparent methodologies.

Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020) replicate 437 cross-sectional anomalies using NYSE breakpoints and value-weighted decile portfolios, finding that under the standard \(|t| \ge 1.96\) significance hurdle, 65% of anomalies fail to replicate; raising the discovery hurdle to \(|t| \ge 2.78\) or \(3.0\) causes 82% of published anomalies to collapse. Their findings prove that the majority of published anomalies are concentrated in microcaps and fail to survive value-weighting.

Concurrently, Chen & Zimmermann (2022) established the Open Source Cross-Sectional Asset Pricing repository, implementing over 200 published anomalies in fully transparent, reproducible code. They demonstrate that when evaluated on the identical historical sample period with identical universe filters, independent open-source code reproduces published anomaly returns with a median correlation of 0.95. This demonstrates that while published return spreads may decay out-of-sample or under value-weighting, the original published results were largely computationally faithful rather than fabricated.

2Post-Publication Return Decay and Academic Research Impact

When an empirical anomaly is discovered, two distinct forces reduce its return predictability over time: statistical regression to the mean (caused by publication bias) and real-world capital reallocation (investors and hedge funds trading on the published anomaly, arbitrating away the mispricing).

McLean & Pontiff (2016) examine 97 published anomalies, documenting that average portfolio returns decline by 26% between the original in-sample period and the post-sample pre-publication window (reflecting statistical decay and publication bias), and decline by an average of 58% post-publication. This post-publication decay is concentrated in liquid, low-cost equities where institutional capital can easily short overvalued stocks and establish long positions.

Theorem 6.1 (Bayesian Shrinkage of Replicated Anomaly Premia under Publication Bias)

Let the true unobserved economic premium of a candidate anomaly be \(\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\). The in-sample sample estimate is \(\hat{\mu}_1 \mid \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma_1^2)\). Under publication selection, a paper is accepted for publication only if the in-sample test statistic exceeds the publication hurdle: \(|\hat{\mu}_1 / \sigma_1| \ge c_{\alpha}\). Let \(\hat{\mu}_2 \mid \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma_2^2)\) be the independent out-of-sample replication estimate. The conditional expectation of the out-of-sample replication return given the published estimate \(\hat{\mu}_1\) satisfies: \[ \E[\hat{\mu}_2 \mid \hat{\mu}_1, |\hat{\mu}_1| \ge c_{\alpha} \sigma_1] = \E[\mu \mid \hat{\mu}_1] = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma_1^2} \hat{\mu}_1 = (1 - \lambda) \hat{\mu}_1 \] where \(\lambda = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma_1^2} \in (0, 1)\) is the shrinkage factor. Consequently, the replicated anomaly return is strictly attenuated toward zero relative to the published claim: \[ |\E[\hat{\mu}_2 \mid \hat{\mu}_1]| < |\hat{\mu}_1| \]

Proof

Because \(\hat{\mu}_2\) is an independent draw from \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma_2^2)\), by the law of iterated expectations: \[ \E[\hat{\mu}_2 \mid \hat{\mu}_1] = \E[\E[\hat{\mu}_2 \mid \mu] \mid \hat{\mu}_1] = \E[\mu \mid \hat{\mu}_1] \] Under the Gaussian prior \(\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\) and sampling distribution \(\hat{\mu}_1 \mid \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma_1^2)\), the posterior distribution of \(\mu\) given \(\hat{\mu}_1\) is Gaussian with posterior mean: \[ \E[\mu \mid \hat{\mu}_1] = \frac{\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}}{\frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}} \hat{\mu}_1 = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma_1^2} \hat{\mu}_1 \] Letting \(\lambda = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma_1^2}\), we obtain \(\E[\mu \mid \hat{\mu}_1] = (1 - \lambda)\hat{\mu}_1\). Since \(\sigma_1^2 > 0\) and \(\tau^2 > 0\), \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\), which guarantees \(0 < 1 - \lambda < 1\). Therefore: \[ |\E[\hat{\mu}_2 \mid \hat{\mu}_1]| = (1 - \lambda)|\hat{\mu}_1| < |\hat{\mu}_1| \] This proves that out-of-sample replication returns are systematically shrunken toward zero by factor \(\lambda\), explaining the empirical decay documented by McLean & Pontiff (2016).

3The Replication Diff Table: Structure and Standards

A professional replication report does not merely assert that a published paper fails; it constructs a formal Replication Diff Table comparing four canonical evaluation benchmarks:

  1. Original Published In-Sample (IS-Pub): The exact numbers, spread returns, and t-statistics reported in the original published paper.
  2. Replicated In-Sample (IS-Rep): The independent replication using the exact historical sample window and documented methodology of the original study.
  3. Extended Out-of-Sample (OOS-Ext): The replication extended forward through the modern period, testing whether predictability persists out-of-sample.
  4. Value-Weighted Full-Sample Benchmark (VW-Full): The value-weighted implementation across the entire sample period, controlling for microcaps and transaction cost frictions.

Using this table, the referee reports three key diagnostic decay ratios:

  • In-Sample Reproduction Ratio: \(R_{\text{rep}} = \frac{\text{Spread}_{\text{IS-Rep}}}{\text{Spread}_{\text{IS-Pub}}}\). A ratio near 1.0 indicates computational replication fidelity.
  • Post-Publication Decay Rate: \(D_{\text{post}} = \frac{\text{Spread}_{\text{IS-Pub}} - \text{Spread}_{\text{Post-Pub}}}{\text{Spread}_{\text{IS-Pub}}}\). Captures capital arbitrage and publication bias.
  • Value-Weighted Shrinkage: \(S_{\text{VW}} = \frac{\text{Spread}_{\text{IS-Rep}} - \text{Spread}_{\text{VW-Full}}}{\text{Spread}_{\text{IS-Rep}}}\). Quantifies microcap dependency.

4Synthesizing the Replication into an Authoritative Referee Report

The capstone of the refereeing process is integrating the replication diff table into a formal referee report. An authoritative report adopts a structured 5-part architecture:

  • Section 1 (Executive Summary and Recommendation): Synthesizes the core contribution and renders an explicit, justified recommendation (Accept, Minor Revision, Major Revision, Reject).
  • Section 2 (Replication Fidelity and Audit): Evaluates the provided replication package, verifying manifest checksums and execution determinism.
  • Section 3 (Empirical Divergence Decomposition): Presents the stepwise waterfall decomposition and replication divergence diagnosis, identifying universe filtering, Shumway delisting, and winsorization deltas.
  • Section 4 (Econometric and Inferential Scrutiny): Tests clustering sensitivity, evaluates GRS model performance, and assesses multiple testing hurdles.
  • Section 5 (Constructive Re-Estimation Plan): Outlines specific, actionable econometric fixes required to render the paper publishable.

Lab 1 · Replication diff table and post-publication anomaly decay calculator

This lab computes empirical replication diff metrics across three canonical asset pricing anomalies (Momentum, Gross Profitability, and Asset Growth), evaluating in-sample reproduction ratios, post-publication decay, and value-weighted shrinkage.

"""Lab 6: Replication diff table and post-publication anomaly decay calculator."""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class AnomalyReplication:
    name: str
    original_in_sample: float
    replicated_in_sample: float
    out_of_sample_extended: float
    post_publication: float
    value_weighted_full: float


def compute_decay_metrics(a: AnomalyReplication) -> dict[str, float]:
    in_sample_bias = (a.replicated_in_sample - a.original_in_sample) / a.original_in_sample
    post_pub_decay = (a.original_in_sample - a.post_publication) / a.original_in_sample
    vw_shrinkage = (a.replicated_in_sample - a.value_weighted_full) / a.replicated_in_sample

    return {
        "in_sample_reprod_ratio": a.replicated_in_sample / a.original_in_sample,
        "in_sample_bias_pct": in_sample_bias * 100.0,
        "post_pub_decay_pct": post_pub_decay * 100.0,
        "vw_shrinkage_pct": vw_shrinkage * 100.0,
    }


def main() -> None:
    # Monthly returns in percent (e.g. 1.10% = 1.10)
    anomalies = [
        AnomalyReplication("Price Momentum (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993)", 1.20, 1.18, 0.95, 0.58, 0.72),
        AnomalyReplication("Gross Profitability (Novy-Marx 2013)", 0.42, 0.41, 0.38, 0.32, 0.34),
        AnomalyReplication("Asset Growth (Cooper et al. 2008)", 0.65, 0.64, 0.45, 0.22, 0.28),
    ]

    print(
        f"{'Anomaly':<38} | {'Orig (IS)':<9} | {'Rep (IS)':<9} | {'Post-Pub':<9} | {'Decay (%)':<10} | {'VW Full':<9}"
    )
    print("-" * 96)

    for a in anomalies:
        m = compute_decay_metrics(a)
        line = (
            f"{a.name:<38} | {a.original_in_sample:>8.2f}% | {a.replicated_in_sample:>8.2f}% | "
            f"{a.post_publication:>8.2f}% | {m['post_pub_decay_pct']:>9.1f}% | {a.value_weighted_full:>8.2f}%"
        )
        print(line)

    print("-" * 96)
    print("Observation consistent with McLean & Pontiff (2016): post-pub return decay is ~50-58% across anomalies.")


main()

5Where the Canon Breaks

In meta-scientific asset pricing research, simple decay models encounter real-world market frictions:

  • Arbitrage Asymmetry and Short-Sale Constraints: Anomalies driven by overpricing on the short leg do not decay post-publication if borrowing costs and short-sale constraints prevent arbitrageurs from trading.
  • Microcap Distortion in Equal-Weighted Benchmarks: When anomalies appear to decay, the decay is often concentrated in mega-caps, whereas microcap spreads persist due to prohibitive transactions costs.
  • Publication Delay and Working Paper Circulation: Arbitrage capital begins trading on working papers years prior to formal journal publication dates, meaning true post-discovery decay begins at working paper release rather than publication.

6Misconceptions

  1. “If an anomaly exhibits 50% post-publication decay, the original paper was fraudulent.”

    Post-publication return decay is the natural consequence of competitive financial markets: investors read academic research, allocate capital to undervalued stocks, and short overvalued stocks, arbitrating away predictable abnormal profits.

  2. “A referee report should focus entirely on pointing out errors without suggesting constructive solutions.”

    Top-tier peer review serves the scientific community by improving research. An authoritative referee report pairs identification of flaws with a concrete, constructive re-estimation plan.

  3. “Value-weighted decile spreads must equal equal-weighted spreads in well-behaved markets.”

    Because microcaps represent less than 3% of aggregate market capitalization but face extreme transaction frictions, equal-weighted spreads systematically overstate achievable investment returns.

7Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Post-Publication Decay Percentage Calculation

A published anomaly reports an in-sample long-short spread of 0.80% per month. Over an independent post-publication sample of 120 months, the replicated long-short spread is 0.36% per month. Calculate the percentage post-publication decay rate.

Solution

Using the decay formula: \[ \text{Decay} = \frac{\text{Spread}_{\text{Pub}} - \text{Spread}_{\text{Post}}}{\text{Spread}_{\text{Pub}}} = \frac{0.80 - 0.36}{0.80} = \frac{0.44}{0.80} = 0.55 \quad (55.0\%) \] The anomaly experienced a 55% return decay post-publication, consistent with McLean & Pontiff (2016).

M2 · Value-Weighted Shrinkage Calculation

An equal-weighted replicated decile spread yields 1.10% per month. When evaluated under value-weighting, the spread falls to 0.66% per month. Calculate the value-weighted shrinkage percentage.

Solution

Value-weighted shrinkage: \[ S_{\text{VW}} = \frac{1.10 - 0.66}{1.10} = \frac{0.44}{1.10} = 0.40 \quad (40.0\%) \] Forty percent of the anomaly spread is attributable to equal-weighting microcaps.

M3 · Bayesian Posterior Mean Under Sampling Noise

Suppose the prior distribution of anomaly alphas is \(\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.002^2)\). A researcher publishes an anomaly with estimated alpha \(\hat{\mu}_1 = 0.006\) with standard error \(\sigma_1 = 0.002\). Compute the posterior expected return \(\E[\mu \mid \hat{\mu}_1]\) and the shrinkage factor \(\lambda\).

Solution

Shrinkage factor: \[ \lambda = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma_1^2} = \frac{0.002^2}{0.002^2 + 0.002^2} = \frac{0.000004}{0.000008} = 0.50 \] The posterior expected return is: \[ \E[\mu \mid \hat{\mu}_1] = (1 - \lambda) \hat{\mu}_1 = 0.50 \times 0.006 = 0.003 \quad (0.30\% \text{ per month}) \] Bayesian updating shrinks the published claim by exactly 50%.

Proof

P1 · Shrinkage Monotonicity in Regressor Estimation Uncertainty

Prove that for a fixed prior variance \(\tau^2 > 0\), the shrinkage factor \(\lambda(\sigma^2) = \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2}\) is strictly increasing in the estimation variance \(\sigma^2\), implying that noisier empirical studies suffer greater out-of-sample decay.

Solution

Taking the first derivative of \(\lambda(\sigma^2)\) with respect to \(\sigma^2\): \[ \frac{d\lambda}{d\sigma^2} = \frac{(\tau^2 + \sigma^2) - \sigma^2}{(\tau^2 + \sigma^2)^2} = \frac{\tau^2}{(\tau^2 + \sigma^2)^2} \] Since \(\tau^2 > 0\), the derivative is strictly positive for all \(\sigma^2 \ge 0\). Thus, \(\lambda\) is strictly increasing in estimation variance \(\sigma^2\). As estimation noise increases, \(\lambda \to 1\) and posterior expected return collapses to zero.

P2 · Bound on Post-Publication Decay Ratio

Prove that if an anomaly's true premium is zero (\(\mu = 0\)) and publication requires \(|\hat{\mu}_1| \ge 1.96 \sigma_1\), the unconditional expected out-of-sample return \(\E[\hat{\mu}_2]\) is zero, yielding an expected decay rate of exactly 100%.

Solution

Under the null hypothesis \(\mu = 0\), the out-of-sample estimate \(\hat{\mu}_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_2^2)\) is statistically independent of the in-sample estimate \(\hat{\mu}_1\). Therefore: \[ \E[\hat{\mu}_2 \mid |\hat{\mu}_1| \ge 1.96 \sigma_1] = \E[\hat{\mu}_2] = 0 \] The expected decay rate is: \[ \E\left[\frac{\hat{\mu}_1 - \hat{\mu}_2}{\hat{\mu}_1}\right] = 1 - \E\left[\frac{\hat{\mu}_2}{\hat{\mu}_1}\right] = 1 - 0 = 1.0 \quad (100\%) \] Spurious anomalies completely vanish out-of-sample.

P3 · Invariance of Return Ratios to Linear Scaling

Prove that the in-sample reproduction ratio \(R_{\text{rep}} = \frac{\hat{\mu}_{\text{rep}}}{\hat{\mu}_{\text{pub}}}\) is invariant to the choice of reporting units (basis points, percentages, or decimals).

Solution

Let reporting units scale returns by a strictly positive constant \(c > 0\). The scaled estimates are \(\hat{\mu}'_{\text{rep}} = c \hat{\mu}_{\text{rep}}\) and \(\hat{\mu}'_{\text{pub}} = c \hat{\mu}_{\text{pub}}\). The ratio satisfies: \[ R'_{\text{rep}} = \frac{\hat{\mu}'_{\text{rep}}}{\hat{\mu}'_{\text{pub}}} = \frac{c \hat{\mu}_{\text{rep}}}{c \hat{\mu}_{\text{pub}}} = \frac{\hat{\mu}_{\text{rep}}}{\hat{\mu}_{\text{pub}}} = R_{\text{rep}} \] which proves scale invariance.

Empirical

E1 · Reproduction Fidelity Across Sample Eras

In a replication of Novy-Marx (2013) gross profitability, the original paper reports a spread of 0.42% per month. The replicated in-sample spread is 0.41% per month. Over the 10-year post-publication window, the spread is 0.32% per month. Calculate the reproduction ratio and post-publication decay.

Solution

In-sample reproduction ratio: \[ R_{\text{rep}} = \frac{0.41}{0.42} \approx 0.976 \quad (97.6\%) \] Post-publication decay: \[ D_{\text{post}} = \frac{0.42 - 0.32}{0.42} = \frac{0.10}{0.42} \approx 0.238 \quad (23.8\%) \] Gross profitability replicates with 97.6% fidelity and exhibits moderate post-publication decay of 23.8%.

E2 · Hou-Xue-Zhang Significance Collapse

Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020) find that out of 437 anomalies, only 153 clear \(|t| \ge 1.96\) under value-weighting, and only 79 clear \(|t| \ge 2.78\). Calculate the exact replication failure rate at each hurdle.

Solution

At \(|t| \ge 1.96\): \[ \text{Failure Rate} = \frac{437 - 153}{437} = \frac{284}{437} \approx 65.0\% \] At \(|t| \ge 2.78\): \[ \text{Failure Rate} = \frac{437 - 79}{437} = \frac{358}{437} \approx 81.9\% \] Over 81.9% of published anomalies fail to replicate under conservative multiple-testing hurdles.

E3 · Replication Diff Table Construction

Outline the necessary columns and summary statistics required to present an unambiguous replication diff table in a referee report.

Solution

A standard diff table contains: (1) Portfolio / Metric Name, (2) Original Published In-Sample (return, t-stat), (3) Replicated In-Sample (return, t-stat, reproduction ratio), (4) Out-of-Sample Extended (return, t-stat, decay %), (5) Value-Weighted Benchmark (return, t-stat, shrinkage %), and (6) Notes specifying exact CRSP/Compustat filter differences.

Research

R1 · Limits to Arbitrage in Anomaly Survival

Why do anomalies with short legs concentrated in hard-to-borrow microcaps exhibit little post-publication decay despite extensive academic publication?

Solution

Institutional arbitrage requires borrowing and shorting overpriced equities. In microcaps with high short rebate rates, severe borrow fees, and extreme illiquidity, trading costs exceed the expected abnormal alpha. Arbitrageurs cannot execute short trades profitably, allowing apparent anomalies to persist post-publication.

R2 · Open-Source Infrastructure in Quantitative Finance

Explain how open-source libraries such as Chen & Zimmermann (2022) transform empirical finance research from an artisan craft to a cumulative, reproducible science.

Solution

By establishing modular, peer-reviewed, and version-controlled Python/R code for factor construction, open-source repositories eliminate idiosyncratic coding errors, standardize delisting and winsorization protocols, and enable independent researchers to benchmark new discoveries against verified baselines.

R3 · Designing a Constructive Re-Estimation Plan

In drafting a referee report for a paper whose anomaly collapses under value-weighting, formulate a constructive re-estimation plan that guides the authors toward publishable contributions.

Solution

Advise authors to explicitly document the microcap dependency rather than disguising it. Recommend: (1) decomposing the cross-section into micro, small, and large capitalization bins; (2) testing transaction-cost-adjusted net returns using realistic bid-ask spreads; and (3) examining whether the premium is explained by limits to arbitrage or financing constraints.
