When an independent replication fails to reproduce a published asset pricing anomaly, junior researchers often attribute the gap to author fraud or sample decay. In rigorous quantitative finance, discrepancies arise from specific, identifiable methodological differences in data extraction, filtering, and adjustment. Mastering empirical divergence diagnosis equips the financial econometrician to isolate every basis point of numerical drift between original and replicated results.

1The Anatomy of Empirical Divergences

Independent replications in empirical asset pricing rarely match published tables perfectly. Even when using the identical nominal data vendor (such as CRSP or Compustat) and the identical sample period, researchers routinely discover discrepancies of 10 to 50 basis points per month in long-short spread returns and substantial divergences in test statistics. Rather than treating these discrepancies as inscrutable noise, rigorous econometrics relies on a formal diagnostic framework.

Definition 4.1 (Replication Divergence Diagnosis)

A replication divergence diagnosis is the systematic taxonomical decomposition and empirical quantification of numerical differences between an original published finding and an independent replication, isolating the marginal contribution of data source versions, universe selection rules, survival adjustments, outlier treatments, and weighting schemes.

Across the empirical literature, replication divergences concentrate into five primary operational failure modes:

  1. Sample Window and Vintage Drift: Discrepancies introduced by database restatements, historical corrections by CRSP/Compustat, or inadvertent mismatches in start/end months.
  2. Universe Selection and Share Code Filtering: Differences in the definition of ordinary common stock versus foreign equities, closed-end funds, or real estate investment trusts.
  3. Delisting Adjustments: The treatment of missing returns when equities exit the CRSP database due to bankruptcy, liquidation, or merger.
  4. Cross-Sectional Winsorization Discipline: Methodological variation in handling extreme characteristic outliers (cross-sectional monthly versus pooled panel winsorization).
  5. Portfolio Weighting Scheme: Disparities between equal-weighted and value-weighted portfolio returns, reflecting microcap sensitivity.

2CRSP Universe Filtering: Share Codes and Exchange Restrictions

The Centre for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) contains daily and monthly data for a broad universe of securities. However, standard asset pricing anomalies are hypothesized to hold specifically on US domestic operating companies. Failing to filter out non-operating corporate structures distorts portfolio return series.

The standard filter requires retaining only ordinary common shares, identified by CRSP two-digit share code SHRCD:

  • SHRCD = 10: Ordinary common shares, company incorporated in the United States, no special status.
  • SHRCD = 11: Ordinary common shares, company incorporated in the United States, need not state status.

Securities with share codes 12 (companies incorporated outside US), 14, 18, 20, 40, 70, and 72 represent American Depositary Receipts (ADRs), closed-end investment funds, units, shares of beneficial interest, and Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs). Inclusion of these non-standard securities introduces severe anomalies, as closed-end funds trade at premiums or discounts to net asset value, and REIT distributions carry specific tax treatments that distort standard cash-flow metrics.

Similarly, researchers must filter by major exchange listing using CRSP exchange code EXCHCD: 1 (New York Stock Exchange), 2 (American Stock Exchange / NYSE American), and 3 (Nasdaq Stock Market). Including over-the-counter (OTC) Bulletin Board or Pink Sheet securities introduces illiquid microcaps with stale prices and massive bid-ask bounces.

3Delisting Returns and Shumway Corrections

One of the most consequential sources of divergence in asset pricing is delisting bias. When a firm delists from an exchange, CRSP records a delisting date and a delisting code DLSTCD. If CRSP can determine the post-delisting price through secondary trading or liquidation payouts, it records a delisting return DLRET. When DLRET is available, the total return in the delisting month is computed by compounding the regular return RET and the delisting return DLRET:

\[ 1 + R_{i,t}^{\text{adj}} = (1 + R_{i,t}) \times (1 + R_{i,t}^{\text{delist}}) \]

However, when a firm ceases trading due to distress, liquidation, or bankruptcy, CRSP frequently leaves DLRET as missing (recorded as -99.0, NaN, or null). If an empirical pipeline naively drops the firm or treats the missing delisting return as zero, it commits severe survivorship bias.

Theorem 4.1 (Delisting Bias Underestimation in Failure-Prone Portfolios)

Let a portfolio sort place distressed firms into portfolio \(P_{\text{distress}}\) in month \(t-1\). Suppose a fraction \(p \in (0, 1)\) of these firms delist during month \(t\) due to financial failure (CRSP delisting codes 500, 520–584). Let \(R_{\text{obs}}\) denote the return of surviving firms and non-distress delistings, and let the true unobserved post-delisting payoff yield return \(R_{\text{fail}} = -1 + \delta\), where \(\delta \ge 0\) represents recovery value. If the econometrician imputes a default delisting return \(R_{\text{imp}} > R_{\text{fail}}\) (such as \(R_{\text{imp}} = 0\)), the upward bias in the estimated portfolio return is: \[ \text{Bias}(\hat{R}_{P,t}) = \E[\hat{R}_{P,t}] - R_{P,t}^* = p \, (R_{\text{imp}} - R_{\text{fail}}) > 0 \] Consequently, in any long-short anomaly strategy that is short distressed equities (such as momentum, profitability, or distress risk), omitting delisting adjustments biases the long-short return downward by \(p(R_{\text{imp}} - R_{\text{fail}})\); whereas in strategies that go long distressed equities, it biases the spread upward.

Proof

The true average return of the portfolio is the convex combination: \[ R_{P,t}^* = (1 - p) R_{\text{obs}} + p R_{\text{fail}} \] Under naive imputation of return \(R_{\text{imp}}\), the computed portfolio return is: \[ \hat{R}_{P,t} = (1 - p) R_{\text{obs}} + p R_{\text{imp}} \] Subtracting \(R_{P,t}^*\) from \(\hat{R}_{P,t}\) yields: \[ \hat{R}_{P,t} - R_{P,t}^* = p \, (R_{\text{imp}} - R_{\text{fail}}) \] Because financial failure implies severe loss (e.g. \(R_{\text{fail}} \approx -0.30\) to \(-0.55\)), setting \(R_{\text{imp}} = 0\) produces a strictly positive bias \(\text{Bias}(\hat{R}_{P,t}) = p |R_{\text{fail}}| > 0\). For a zero-cost spread \(R_{\text{long}} - R_{\text{short}}\) where the short leg contains the distressed firms, the estimated spread satisfies: \[ \E[\widehat{\text{Spread}}] = \E[R_{\text{long}}] - \E[\hat{R}_{\text{distress}}] = \text{Spread}^* - p(R_{\text{imp}} - R_{\text{fail}}) < \text{Spread}^* \] This proves the downward attenuation of momentum or quality spreads under missing delisting return neglect.

Shumway (1997) shows that distress delistings omitted from CRSP incur an average post-delisting return of \(-30\%\) for NYSE/AMEX firms and \(-55\%\) for Nasdaq firms. Modern replication standards require replacing missing delisting returns on performance-related delistings (delisting codes in the 500 series) with these empirical benchmarks.

4Winsorization and Outlier Handling: Monthly vs Pooled Discipline

Accounting ratios and predictive signals in Compustat frequently display extreme outliers due to near-zero denominators (e.g. market-to-book ratios during restructuring) or data entry anomalies. To prevent regression estimates from being dominated by extreme points, researchers apply winsorization or truncation.

A frequent error in empirical replication is confusing monthly cross-sectional winsorization with pooled panel winsorization:

  • Monthly Cross-Sectional Winsorization: In each individual month \(t\), the characteristic distribution \(x_{i,t}\) across all active firms \(i=1,\dots,N_t\) is evaluated, and values below the 1st percentile \(q_{0.01, t}\) or above the 99th percentile \(q_{0.99, t}\) are set equal to \(q_{0.01, t}\) and \(q_{0.99, t}\) respectively. This preserves the relative ranking of firms within each period without allowing macroeconomic regime shifts to distort cross-sectional thresholds.
  • Pooled Panel Winsorization: The thresholds \(q_{0.01}\) and \(q_{0.99}\) are computed across the entire panel of observations spanning all firms and all decades. This violates temporal conditioning by allowing future inflation or valuation regimes to dictate historical clipping levels, contaminating out-of-sample validity.

5The Stepwise Waterfall Divergence Decomposition

To pinpoint the exact source of empirical disagreement between a published paper and an independent replication, the researcher constructs a stepwise waterfall divergence decomposition.

Let \(\hat{\beta}_0\) be the baseline estimate reported in the published paper, and let \(\hat{\beta}_K\) be the final replicated estimate. The econometrician defines a monotonic sequence of \(K\) operational adjustments: \[ \Phi_0 \xrightarrow{\Delta_1} \Phi_1 \xrightarrow{\Delta_2} \Phi_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta_3} \dots \xrightarrow{\Delta_K} \Phi_K \] where each stage \(\Phi_k\) modifies exactly one operational dimension while holding all other parameters fixed. The total divergence satisfies the exact articulation identity: \[ \Delta_{\text{total}} = \hat{\beta}_K - \hat{\beta}_0 = \sum_{k=1}^K (\hat{\beta}_k - \hat{\beta}_{k-1}) \] By auditing the marginal difference \(\delta_k = \hat{\beta}_k - \hat{\beta}_{k-1}\) at each step, the researcher reveals whether the divergence is driven by data vintage, share code filtering, delisting adjustments, winsorization, or portfolio weighting.

Lab 1 · Empirical divergence decomposition and step-by-step waterfall auditor

This lab audits an empirical research replication pipeline across six sequential operational stages, decomposing the total spread divergence between published results and the replicated benchmark into exact marginal step contributions.

"""Lab 4: Empirical divergence decomposition and step-by-step waterfall auditor."""

from __future__ import annotations


def decompose_divergences(stages: dict[str, float]) -> list[tuple[str, float, float, float]]:
    stage_keys = list(stages.keys())
    base_val = stages[stage_keys[0]]
    results: list[tuple[str, float, float, float]] = []

    for i, key in enumerate(stage_keys):
        val = stages[key]
        delta_step = 0.0 if i == 0 else val - stages[stage_keys[i - 1]]
        delta_cumulative = val - base_val
        results.append((key, val, delta_step, delta_cumulative))

    return results


def main() -> None:
    # Tracking gross profitability or momentum spread return across pipeline variations
    pipeline_steps = {
        "0. Published Paper (Reported Table)": 0.840,
        "1. Raw Unfiltered Sample": 0.950,
        "2. Ordinary Common Share Filter (SHRCD 10/11)": 0.820,
        "3. Shumway (1997) Delisting Imputation": 0.650,
        "4. Cross-Sectional Winsorization (1%/99%)": 0.620,
        "5. Value-Weighted Portfolio (Replicated Benchmark)": 0.440,
    }

    waterfall = decompose_divergences(pipeline_steps)

    print(f"{'Pipeline Stage':<50} | {'Spread (%)':<10} | {'Step Delta':<10} | {'Cumulative':<10}")
    print("-" * 88)
    for stage, val, step, cum in waterfall:
        print(f"{stage:<50} | {val:>10.3f} | {step:>+10.3f} | {cum:>+10.3f}")

    vw_target = pipeline_steps["5. Value-Weighted Portfolio (Replicated Benchmark)"]
    pub_target = pipeline_steps["0. Published Paper (Reported Table)"]
    total_wedge = vw_target - pub_target
    print("-" * 88)
    print(f"Total Divergence Wedge: {total_wedge:+.3f}% per month ({total_wedge * 12:+.2f}% annualized)")
    print("Primary drivers: Shumway delisting (-0.170%) and Value-weighting (-0.180%)")

6Where the Canon Breaks

Doctoral candidates must abandon the textbook illusion that empirical asset pricing datasets are pristine, objective ground truth. The following operational pitfalls routinely distort empirical replications:

  • Ignoring Compounded Delisting Returns: When calculating returns for a delisting month, adding RET + DLRET instead of compounding \((1 + RET)(1 + DLRET) - 1\) introduces systematic compounding errors, particularly when monthly losses exceed 20%.
  • Equal-Weighting vs Value-Weighting Conflation: Equal-weighted portfolios weight every equity equally, allowing the bottom 3% of market capitalization (microcaps) to dictate over 60% of portfolio variation. Value-weighting is essential to establish whether an anomaly survives real-world capitalization constraints.
  • Inadvertent Forward-Looking Restatements: Using point-in-time Compustat data is mandatory when testing accounting anomalies. Standard annual Compustat files record the final restated accounting numbers published years later, leaking future information into historical signals.

7Misconceptions

  1. “If a replication differs from the published table by 20 basis points, the original authors must have falsified their findings.”

    In cross-sectional finance, 20 basis points of monthly spread divergence is routinely produced by subtle, unstated operational differences: whether delisting returns were compounded, whether ADRs were filtered out, or whether winsorization was performed monthly or pooled.

  2. “Setting missing delisting returns to zero is conservative because zero return means no gain or loss.”

    When a distressed firm delists due to insolvency, its true return is deeply negative (between \(-30\%\) and \(-55\%\)). Setting the return to zero imputes an artificial positive gain relative to actual economic loss, heavily biasing returns upward.

  3. “Filtering CRSP by share codes 10 and 11 discards valuable large-cap stocks.”

    Share codes 10 and 11 capture all US domestic operating corporations, representing over 95% of total US equity market capitalization. Non-10/11 securities are foreign ADRs, closed-end funds, and specialized vehicles that violate standard corporate pricing assumptions.

8Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Shumway Delisting Return Compounding

A firm experiences a regular monthly return of \(R_{\text{reg}} = -0.15\) in its final trading month on Nasdaq, and delists due to insolvency with CRSP recording missing delisting return. Applying the Shumway (1997) replacement rule for Nasdaq distress delistings (\(R_{\text{delist}} = -0.55\)), compute the compounded monthly delisting-adjusted return \(R^{\text{adj}}\).

Solution

Using the exact compounding identity: \[ 1 + R^{\text{adj}} = (1 + R_{\text{reg}}) \times (1 + R_{\text{delist}}) = (1 - 0.15) \times (1 - 0.55) = 0.85 \times 0.45 = 0.3825 \] Therefore, \(R^{\text{adj}} = 0.3825 - 1 = -0.6175\) (or \(-61.75\%\)).

M2 · Naive Addition Error Quantification

If a researcher mistakenly computes simple addition instead of compounding: \(R^{\text{naive}} = R_{\text{reg}} + R_{\text{delist}} = -0.15 + (-0.55) = -0.70\), what is the sign and magnitude of the error relative to true compounding?

Solution

The error is \(R^{\text{naive}} - R^{\text{adj}} = -0.70 - (-0.6175) = -0.0825\) (an overstatement of the loss by 8.25 percentage points). Simple addition overlooks the cross-product term \(R_{\text{reg}} \times R_{\text{delist}} = (-0.15)(-0.55) = +0.0825\).

M3 · Share Code Classification Verification

Classify CRSP share codes 10, 11, 12, and 70, identifying which codes must be retained for standard empirical asset pricing portfolios of US common operating companies.

Solution

Codes 10 and 11 represent ordinary common shares incorporated in the United States and are retained. Code 12 represents ordinary shares incorporated outside the US (foreign equity), and code 70 represents Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs). Both 12 and 70 are excluded.

Proof

P1 · Compounding Loss vs Simple Sum Inequality

Prove that for any two sequential return rates \(r_1, r_2 \in (-1, 0)\), the simple sum \(r_1 + r_2\) strictly overstates the compound loss (i.e. \(r_1 + r_2 < (1+r_1)(1+r_2) - 1\)).

Solution

Let \(r_1, r_2 \in (-1, 0)\). Expanding the compound return: \[ (1 + r_1)(1 + r_2) - 1 = 1 + r_1 + r_2 + r_1 r_2 - 1 = r_1 + r_2 + r_1 r_2 \] Because \(r_1 < 0\) and \(r_2 < 0\), the product \(r_1 r_2 > 0\). Thus: \[ (1 + r_1)(1 + r_2) - 1 = (r_1 + r_2) + r_1 r_2 > r_1 + r_2 \] Hence the simple sum is strictly more negative than the true compound return, overstating total loss.

P2 · Telescoping Waterfall Articulation Identity

Prove that the articulation identity \(\Delta_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k=1}^K (\hat{\beta}_k - \hat{\beta}_{k-1})\) is a telescoping sum that holds identically regardless of the specific empirical values \(\hat{\beta}_k\).

Solution

Expanding the sum: \[ \sum_{k=1}^K (\hat{\beta}_k - \hat{\beta}_{k-1}) = (\hat{\beta}_1 - \hat{\beta}_0) + (\hat{\beta}_2 - \hat{\beta}_1) + \dots + (\hat{\beta}_K - \hat{\beta}_{K-1}) \] Notice that every intermediate term \(\hat{\beta}_k\) for \(k=1,\dots,K-1\) appears once with a positive sign and once with a negative sign. All intermediate terms cancel out, leaving: \[ \sum_{k=1}^K (\hat{\beta}_k - \hat{\beta}_{k-1}) = \hat{\beta}_K - \hat{\beta}_0 = \Delta_{\text{total}} \] This confirms the articulation identity is an exact mathematical property of the telescoping sum.

P3 · Upward Return Bias Under Default Imputation

Prove that when the proportion \(p \in (0,1)\) of delisting firms experiences an economic loss \(R_{\text{fail}} < 0\), any non-negative imputed return \(R_{\text{imp}} \ge 0\) induces a strictly positive upward portfolio bias \(\E[\hat{R}_P] - R_P^* > 0\).

Solution

From Theorem 4.1, the bias is \(\text{Bias} = p(R_{\text{imp}} - R_{\text{fail}})\). Since \(p > 0\), \(R_{\text{imp}} \ge 0\), and \(R_{\text{fail}} < 0\), it follows that \(R_{\text{imp}} - R_{\text{fail}} \ge 0 - R_{\text{fail}} = |R_{\text{fail}}| > 0\). Thus: \[ \text{Bias} = p(R_{\text{imp}} - R_{\text{fail}}) \ge p |R_{\text{fail}}| > 0 \] which guarantees a strictly positive upward bias in the portfolio return.

Empirical

E1 · Anomaly Spread Waterfall Analysis

In a quantitative replication of an investment anomaly, the published long-short spread is 0.75% per month. The replication waterfall yields: Raw = 0.88%, Share Code Filter = 0.76%, Shumway Imputation = 0.62%, Winsorization = 0.60%, Value-Weighting = 0.45%. Calculate the total divergence wedge and identify which step caused the largest absolute reduction in anomaly spread.

Solution

Total divergence wedge: \[ \Delta_{\text{total}} = 0.45\% - 0.75\% = -0.30\% \text{ per month} \quad (-3.60\% \text{ annualized}) \] Step deltas: - Raw to Share Code: \(0.76 - 0.88 = -0.12\%\) - Share Code to Shumway: \(0.62 - 0.76 = -0.14\%\) - Shumway to Winsorization: \(0.60 - 0.62 = -0.02\%\) - Winsorization to Value-Weighting: \(0.45 - 0.60 = -0.15\%\) The largest single reduction is moving from equal-weighting to value-weighting (\(-0.15\%\) per month), followed closely by Shumway delisting imputation (\(-0.14\%\) per month).

E2 · Missing Delisting Return Inflation

A distressed decile portfolio contains 200 stocks, of which 6 delist in month \(t\) with performance-related delisting codes. CRSP reports missing delisting returns for all 6. If the researcher sets their returns to 0% instead of imputing the Shumway \(-30\%\) penalty, calculate the basis-point upward bias in that month's equal-weighted decile return.

Solution

The fraction of delisting firms is \(p = 6 / 200 = 0.03\) (or 3%). The imputation error per firm is \(R_{\text{imp}} - R_{\text{fail}} = 0.0 - (-0.30) = +0.30\). The upward bias in the portfolio return is: \[ \text{Bias} = p \times 0.30 = 0.03 \times 0.30 = 0.009 \quad (0.90\% \text{ or } 90 \text{ basis points}) \] Omitting the penalty inflates the distressed portfolio return by 90 basis points in that month.

E3 · Look-Ahead Contamination in Pooled Winsorization

Why does pooled panel winsorization across decades create look-ahead contamination when evaluating real-time trading strategy predictability?

Solution

Pooled panel winsorization computes quantile thresholds using the entire sample span, including future periods. If future decades experience unprecedented valuation spikes (e.g. the 1999 technology bubble), historical observations are clipped according to future distribution parameters that were unknown to investors at the time.

Research

R1 · Value-Weighting Fragility in Published Anomalies

In their systematic study of over 400 anomalies, Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020) find that over 65% of published anomalies fail to replicate at standard significance levels under value-weighting. Why are equal-weighted anomalies particularly vulnerable to replication failure?

Solution

Equal-weighting allocates identical weights to microcaps and mega-caps. Microcaps represent the vast majority of firms by count but less than 3% of aggregate market capitalization. Microcaps suffer from severe illiquidity, wide bid-ask spreads, high borrowing fees, and missing delisting returns. When an anomaly exists solely in microcaps, value-weighting eliminates the statistical significance.

R2 · Two-Way Sensitivity Grid: Data vs Inference

Describe the steps required to audit whether an anomaly's reported t-statistic divergence is caused by clustering choices or sample filtering.

Solution

First, fix the sample data to the exact published filtered dataset and estimate the regression under OLS, White, Newey-West, and clustered standard errors to isolate standard error inflation. Second, hold the standard error estimator fixed while toggling filtering options (delisting, share codes) one at a time. The resulting two-way grid separates sample variation from inferential variance.

R3 · Distress Risk Anomaly Reversal

Explain how neglecting Shumway delisting corrections in financial distress anomalies (e.g. Campbell, Hilscher & Szilagyi 2008) can cause high-distress portfolios to appear to yield positive returns when they actually incur severe losses.

Solution

Distressed equities have high delisting exit rates. If defaulting firms are dropped or assigned zero returns upon delisting, the severe loss upon terminal failure is omitted from the historical sample. Surviving distressed firms appear to generate high returns, creating an artificial distress puzzle entirely driven by survivorship bias.
