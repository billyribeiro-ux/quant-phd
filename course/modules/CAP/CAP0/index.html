Finance Degree Capstone

Integrating the complete corporate finance and equity curriculum on one live corporate enterprise. In this senior doctoral-track capstone, you will construct a fully articulated three-statement financial model, perform forensic data-room audits of earnings quality, build dynamic discounted cash flow and APV valuations, optimize debt and liquidity pacing against insolvency and covenant risk, design an ASC 815 corporate hedging program, and defend your strategic synthesis against adversarial interrogation before a simulated Board of Directors and Investment Committee.

  • Prerequisites: FC4, CF7, CF8, CF9, BK5, PC2
  • Unlocks: CAP1, Degree Certification
  • Project: Integrated corporate finance and equity case, autograder and rubric (60 hours)

Objectives

  1. Reconstruct fully integrated three-statement financial models from audited financial reports, enforcing exact balance sheet articulation where assets equal liabilities plus equity across all forecast horizons.
  2. Audit corporate earnings quality and forensic accounting integrity using Sloan (1996) operating accruals ratios and the 8-variable Beneish (1999) manipulation index within a virtual data room environment.
  3. Construct dynamic discounted cash flow (DCF) models incorporating path-dependent costs of capital, time-varying leverage, and mid-year cash flow discounting conventions per Damodaran (2012).
  4. Optimize corporate capital structure and dividend payout policy by balancing interest tax shields against nonlinear financial distress costs and debt covenant constraints per Myers (1984).
  5. Formulate an ASC 815 / IFRS 9 compliant corporate risk management program, calculating minimum-variance hedge ratios and testing regression effectiveness for foreign exchange exposures per Froot, Scharfstein & Stein (1993).
  6. Defend an integrated equity initiation and capital allocation thesis before a hostile Board of Directors and Investment Committee using structured adversarial pre-mortems.

Diagnostic Placement Assessment

This placement quiz assesses your command of the quantitative prerequisites and the core modeling skills developed in Rungs 1 and 2. Achieving 80% mastery (including both skill-tagged items) permits skipping the worked example and completion problems.

  1. In an integrated three-statement financial model, if a firm increases its capital expenditures by $50M funded entirely by drawing on a revolving debt credit line (with zero cash change and zero depreciation in that period), how do Total Assets and Total Liabilities plus Equity change?

    CapEx increases gross and net PP&E on the asset side by $50M. The revolving debt draw increases short/long-term liabilities by $50M. The Cash Flow Statement shows \(\text{CFI} = -\$50\text{M}\) and \(\text{CFF} = +\$50\text{M}\), leaving ending cash unchanged. Both sides of the balance sheet expand by exactly $50M.

  2. Following Sloan (1996), a firm reports: Current Assets increase by $80M, Cash increases by $20M, Current Liabilities increase by $35M, Short-Term Debt increases by $5M, and Depreciation Expense is $45M. What is the firm's Operating Accruals measure?

    Under the balance sheet accruals formula: \(\text{Accruals} = (\Delta\text{CA} - \Delta\text{Cash}) - (\Delta\text{CL} - \Delta\text{STD}) - \text{Depr}\). Here: \((80 - 20) - (35 - 5) - 45 = 60 - 30 - 45 = -15\text{M}\).

  3. A company maintains a capital structure of 70% equity and 30% debt. The cost of equity is 9.5%, the pre-tax cost of debt is 5.0%, and the marginal corporate tax rate is 25%. What is the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) in percent?

    \(\text{WACC} = w_e r_e + w_d r_d (1 - \tau) = 0.70 \times 9.5\% + 0.30 \times 5.0\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 6.65\% + 1.125\% = 7.775\%\).

  4. Under the 8-variable Beneish (1999) M-Score model, which score cutoff indicates a high probability that the firm is manipulating its reported earnings?

    Beneish (1999) establishes that an M-Score greater than -1.78 (e.g. -1.45 or +0.50) indicates a statistically significant probability of earnings manipulation.

  5. Why do corporations hedge financial risks according to Froot, Scharfstein & Stein (1993) and Smith & Stulz (1985)?

    Under capital market imperfections and convex external financing costs, internal funds are cheaper than external equity/debt. Hedging stabilizes internal cash flows, ensuring the firm does not have to curtail high-NPV capital projects.
