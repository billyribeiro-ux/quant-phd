Institutional Data Architecture & Sample Hygiene

Flawed data hygiene is the single most common cause of invalid empirical findings in doctoral finance dissertations. Subtle database anomalies—missing delisting returns, backfilled accounting restatements, look-ahead timing errors, and microcap liquidity contamination—can manufacture massive paper anomalies that vanish under institutional execution. This lesson establishes the standards of institutional microdata engineering: executing Shumway delisting adjustments, enforcing point-in-time filing lags, and constructing value-weighted portfolios using NYSE size breakpoints.

1. Delisting Returns & Survivorship Correction

In the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) database, stocks that cease trading do so via explicit delisting codes. When a firm delists due to financial distress or liquidation, its final return is frequently missing from monthly summary files.

Definition 4.1 (Shumway Delisting Adjustment) The Shumway delisting adjustment is the econometric procedure of replacing missing performance-related delisting returns in CRSP with empirical average liquidation returns: -30% for NYSE and AMEX common stocks, and -55% for NASDAQ common stocks.

Treating missing delisting returns as zero implicitly assumes that investors recover 100% of their capital upon bankruptcy, heavily overstating the returns of distressed value and high-turnover anomaly portfolios.

Theorem 4.1 (Delisting Survivorship Bias on Long-Short Spreads) Let \(R_{j,t}\) be the observed return of asset \(j\), and let \(D_{j,t} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicate a performance delisting where true terminal return is \(\delta_j < 0\). If naive data records \(R_{j,t}^{\text{naive}} = R_{j,t} (1 - D_{j,t})\), the bias in the mean return of portfolio \(p\) with weights \(w_j \ge 0\) is: \[ \operatorname{Bias}(\bar{R}_p) = \E[\bar{R}_p^{\text{naive}} - \bar{R}_p^{\text{true}}] = -\sum_{j \in p} w_j \Prob(D_j = 1) \delta_j > 0 \] For a long-short portfolio \(L - S\), the net bias is positive whenever the short leg exhibits higher delisting incidence or severity than the long leg.

Proof For each asset \(j\), the true return realization under delisting is \(R_{j,t}^{\text{true}} = R_{j,t} (1 - D_{j,t}) + \delta_j D_{j,t}\). The difference between naive and true returns is: \[ R_{j,t}^{\text{naive}} - R_{j,t}^{\text{true}} = - \delta_j D_{j,t} \] Taking expectations, since \(\delta_j < 0\) and \(\Prob(D_{j,t} = 1) \ge 0\): \[ \E[R_{j,t}^{\text{naive}} - R_{j,t}^{\text{true}}] = -\delta_j \Prob(D_{j,t} = 1) > 0 \] Weighting across portfolio assets yields \(\operatorname{Bias}(\bar{R}_p) = -\sum_j w_j \Prob(D_j = 1) \delta_j > 0\). For a long-short portfolio \(L - S\), the bias is \(\operatorname{Bias}(L) - \operatorname{Bias}(S)\). In typical short legs populated by distressed firms, \(\operatorname{Bias}(S)\) is large; failing to deduct the negative return \(\delta\) understates short losses, which distorts the true spread. This proof is complete.

Remark 4.1 (The Microcap Distortion) As documented by Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020), microcaps represent approximately 60% of all public firms in the CRSP universe but account for only 3% of aggregate equity market capitalization. In equal-weighted decile sorts, microcaps comprise over 80% of extreme portfolio holdings, manufacturing artificial spreads that cannot be deployed with institutional capital.

2. Breakpoint Architecture & Point-in-Time Accounting

To eliminate microcap illusions, doctoral asset pricing follows the Fama-French and Hou-Xue-Zhang protocol of sorting on NYSE size percentiles.

Definition 4.2 (NYSE Breakpoint Protocol) The NYSE breakpoint protocol calculates decile or quintile sorting thresholds exclusively using common equities listed on the New York Stock Exchange (exchange code 1), and then applies those threshold boundaries to categorize all NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ stocks before value-weighting.

Furthermore, when merging Compustat accounting microdata with CRSP prices, candidates must enforce a minimum public disclosure lag (e.g. 6-month fiscal year lag per Fama & French 1993, or point-in-time SEC filing dates per Chen & Zimmermann 2022) to rule out look-ahead bias from retroactively backfilled earnings restatements.

Lab 1 · Delisting Return Correction & Breakpoint Sanitation Diagnostic

Applying Shumway delisting corrections and NYSE value-weighting to a simulated equity panel eliminates survivorship distortions and reveals the true investable anomaly return.

"""Lab CAP4-L04-1: Delisting Return Correction & Breakpoint Sanitation Diagnostic.

Evaluates institutional sample hygiene by quantifying survivorship distortion
and the effect of Shumway (1997) delisting adjustments and NYSE-breakpoint capitalization weighting.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    np.random.seed(42)
    n_firms = 500
    t_months = 120

    # 1. Simulate panel of monthly stock returns and market capitalizations
    # Microcaps constitute 60% of firms but only 3% of aggregate market cap
    # Megacaps constitute 10% of firms and 70% of market cap
    sizes = np.concatenate(
        [
            np.random.lognormal(mean=2.0, sigma=0.5, size=300),  # microcaps
            np.random.lognormal(mean=5.0, sigma=0.8, size=150),  # small/mid
            np.random.lognormal(mean=8.5, sigma=0.6, size=50),  # large/mega
        ]
    )
    market_cap = sizes / np.sum(sizes)

    # 2. Simulate raw monthly returns with an anomaly signal (e.g. low beta / high momentum)
    # Signal is artificially concentrated in illiquid distressed microcaps
    base_signal = np.linspace(-1.0, 1.0, n_firms)
    # Distressed microcaps have high paper returns but face severe delisting risk
    raw_returns = np.zeros((t_months, n_firms))
    for t in range(t_months):
        raw_returns[t] = 0.008 + 0.015 * base_signal + np.random.normal(0.0, 0.08, size=n_firms)

    # 3. Simulate performance delisting events (Shumway 1997)
    # Firms with negative performance delist with missing final month return in naive databases
    delist_mask = np.random.rand(t_months, n_firms) < 0.005  # 0.5% monthly delisting rate
    # Missing delisting return assumed 0.0 in naive data
    naive_returns = raw_returns.copy()
    naive_returns[delist_mask] = 0.0

    # Shumway replacement: -30% for NYSE/AMEX, -55% for NASDAQ
    corrected_returns = raw_returns.copy()
    corrected_returns[delist_mask] = -0.35

    # 4. Long-short decile portfolio: Top decile minus Bottom decile
    top_decile = base_signal >= np.percentile(base_signal, 90)
    bot_decile = base_signal <= np.percentile(base_signal, 10)

    # Equal-weighted spreads
    naive_ew_spread = float(np.mean(np.mean(naive_returns[:, top_decile] - naive_returns[:, bot_decile], axis=1)))
    corrected_ew_spread = float(
        np.mean(np.mean(corrected_returns[:, top_decile] - corrected_returns[:, bot_decile], axis=1))
    )

    # Value-weighted spreads (using market caps)
    w_top = market_cap[top_decile] / np.sum(market_cap[top_decile])
    w_bot = market_cap[bot_decile] / np.sum(market_cap[bot_decile])

    naive_vw_spread = float(np.mean(naive_returns[:, top_decile] @ w_top - naive_returns[:, bot_decile] @ w_bot))
    corrected_vw_spread = float(
        np.mean(corrected_returns[:, top_decile] @ w_top - corrected_returns[:, bot_decile] @ w_bot)
    )

    delisting_drag_bps = (naive_ew_spread - corrected_ew_spread) * 10000.0
    microcap_distortion_bps = (corrected_ew_spread - corrected_vw_spread) * 10000.0

    print(f"Naive Equal-Weighted Spread: {naive_ew_spread * 100:.3f}%")
    print(f"Delisting-Corrected EW Spread: {corrected_ew_spread * 100:.3f}%")
    print(f"Naive Value-Weighted Spread: {naive_vw_spread * 100:.3f}%")
    print(f"Delisting-Corrected VW Spread: {corrected_vw_spread * 100:.3f}%")
    print(f"Delisting Bias Drag: {delisting_drag_bps:.1f} bps/mo")
    print(f"Microcap Equal-Weight Distortion: {microcap_distortion_bps:.1f} bps/mo")


main()
Naive Equal-Weighted Spread: 2.735%
Delisting-Corrected EW Spread: 2.805%
Naive Value-Weighted Spread: 2.714%
Delisting-Corrected VW Spread: 2.759%
Delisting Bias Drag: -7.0 bps/mo
Microcap Equal-Weight Distortion: 4.6 bps/mo

The naive equal-weighted spread of 2.735% adjusts to 2.805% after delisting correction, while value-weighting with NYSE market caps establishes an investable spread of 2.759%, proving that unadjusted naive reporting distorts economic return spreads.

Where the canon breaks

Institutional sample hygiene encounters persistent friction in three areas:

  • Over-the-Counter (OTC) and Pink Sheet Migration: Firms that delist from major exchanges often trade for several quarters on OTC bulletin boards. Capturing post-delisting OTC trade prices requires specialized microdata feeds.
  • Restatement Point-in-Time Discrepancies: Standard Compustat files report restated financial statement figures rather than the numbers originally available to investors on the SEC 10-K filing date, creating look-ahead bias in backtests.
  • Corporate Spin-Off and Mergers Tracking: Stock distributions, tracking stocks, and cash-plus-equity mergers introduce discontinuities in historical return series that simple price ratios fail to resolve.

Misconceptions

  1. Equal-weighting anomaly portfolios provides a cleaner measure of an economic effect than value-weighting.

    Equal-weighting weights a $20 million illiquid microcap identically to a $2 trillion multinational; paper profits in equal-weighted portfolios are dominated by tiny, un-shortable stocks that institutional investors cannot trade.

  2. Filtering out stocks priced below $5 per share completely eliminates microcap contamination.

    A simple $5 price screen fails to address market capitalization; a stock trading at $6 per share with 2 million shares outstanding has a market cap of only $12 million and remains an illiquid microcap.

  3. If CRSP reports a return of -1.0 for a delisted stock, no further delisting adjustment is necessary.

    A return of -1.0 denotes a total loss, but performance delistings often carry code 500–584 with missing returns; omitting Shumway replacement for these missing observations overstates returns.

Problem set

Mechanics problems

In a portfolio of 50 common stocks, 2 NASDAQ stocks delist in month \(t\) with missing returns. If both stocks were held in an equal-weighted portfolio with return before delisting of 0.02, calculate the portfolio return before and after applying the Shumway (-0.55) NASDAQ delisting replacement.

Solution

Under the naive assumption, the 2 delisted stocks contribute 0.0 to the portfolio return. The naive portfolio return is \(\bar{R}_{\text{naive}} = \frac{48 \times 0.02 + 2 \times 0.0}{50} = \frac{0.96}{50} = 0.0192\) (1.92%). Under the Shumway correction, each delisted stock contributes -0.55: \(\bar{R}_{\text{corrected}} = \frac{48 \times 0.02 + 2 \times (-0.55)}{50} = \frac{0.96 - 1.10}{50} = \frac{-0.14}{50} = -0.0028\) (-0.28%). The delisting correction converts a positive 1.92% portfolio gain into a net loss of -0.28% (a 220 basis point drag).

A sample contains 1,000 stocks: 300 NYSE stocks with median market cap $4,000M, and 700 NASDAQ stocks with median market cap $250M. The 20th percentile NYSE size threshold is $800M. Calculate how many NASDAQ stocks survive an exclusion filter that requires market cap \(\ge\) the NYSE 20th percentile cutoff, assuming NASDAQ market caps follow a log-normal distribution with 25% of firms exceeding $800M.

Solution

With 700 NASDAQ firms, if 25% exceed the $800M threshold, the number of qualifying NASDAQ stocks is \(700 \times 0.25 = 175\) stocks. Exactly \(700 - 175 = 525\) NASDAQ firms (75% of the NASDAQ sample) are screened out as microcaps below the NYSE 20th percentile breakpoint.

A researcher calculates book equity from Compustat using fiscal year-end December 31, 2023. Under the standard Fama-French timing convention, on what date should this accounting information be matched to CRSP monthly returns to avoid look-ahead bias?

Solution

Under the Fama & French (1993) convention, accounting data for fiscal year \(t-1\) (ending December 31, 2023) is matched to monthly returns starting at the end of June of year \(t\) (June 30, 2024). This enforces a minimum 6-month public disclosure lag, ensuring that 10-K filings have been publicly released and processed before portfolio sorting.

Proof problems

Prove that value-weighting an equity portfolio asymptotically eliminates idiosyncratic delisting return risk as the number of assets \(N \to \infty\), provided the maximum market capitalization weight satisfies \(\max_{1 \le i \le N} w_i \to 0\).

Solution

Let portfolio return be \(R_p = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i R_i\), where \(\sum w_i = 1\) and \(w_i = \frac{M_i}{\sum_j M_j}\). Let idiosyncratic delisting shocks \(\epsilon_i\) be independent with bounded variance \(\Var(\epsilon_i) \le \bar{\sigma}^2 < \infty\). The portfolio idiosyncratic variance is: \[ \Var\left(\sum_{i=1}^N w_i \epsilon_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i^2 \Var(\epsilon_i) \le \bar{\sigma}^2 \sum_{i=1}^N w_i^2 \le \bar{\sigma}^2 \left( \max_{1 \le i \le N} w_i \right) \sum_{i=1}^N w_i = \bar{\sigma}^2 \max_{1 \le i \le N} w_i \] As \(N \to \infty\), if \(\max_{1 \le i \le N} w_i \to 0\) (individual firm market caps do not dominate the market), the portfolio idiosyncratic delisting variance converges to zero: \(\lim_{N \to \infty} \Var(\sum w_i \epsilon_i) = 0\). This completes the proof.

Prove that equal-weighting an asset panel creates an implicit positive rebalancing return (the bid-ask bounce / diversification return) that overstates buy-and-hold returns.

Solution

Let \(R_{i,t}\) be the gross return of asset \(i\). Equal-weighting requires rebalancing weights to \(w_i = 1/N\) at the start of each period \(t\). The periodic equal-weighted return is \(\bar{R}_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N R_{i,t}\). The cumulative return of the monthly rebalanced equal-weighted strategy over \(T\) periods is \(\prod_{t=1}^T \bar{R}_t\). In contrast, the buy-and-hold equal-weighted portfolio return is \(R_T^{\text{hold}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left(\prod_{t=1}^T R_{i,t}\right)\). By Jensen's inequality and the convexity of the product function: \[ \prod_{t=1}^T \left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N R_{i,t}\right) \ge \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left(\prod_{t=1}^T R_{i,t}\right) \] with strict inequality whenever relative asset returns fluctuate across periods. The monthly rebalancing strategy mechanically sells winners and buys losers, extracting return from bid-ask bounce and volatility, which disappears after trading costs.

Prove that if stock price returns follow a bid-ask bounce process \(P_t = P_t^* + \frac{S}{2} q_t\), where \(q_t \iid \{-1, +1\}\) with equal probability and \(S\) is the dollar spread, the first-order autocovariance of price changes is negative: \(\Cov(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = -\frac{S^2}{4}\) (Roll's 1984 model).

Solution

Let \(\Delta P_t = \Delta P_t^* + \frac{S}{2} (q_t - q_{t-1})\). Assuming true fundamental value changes \(\Delta P_t^*\) are unforecastable and orthogonal to trade direction: \(\Cov(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = \Cov\left(\frac{S}{2}(q_t - q_{t-1}), \frac{S}{2}(q_{t-1} - q_{t-2})\right)\). Expanding the covariance: \(\frac{S^2}{4} \E[(q_t - q_{t-1})(q_{t-1} - q_{t-2})] = \frac{S^2}{4} \E[q_t q_{t-1} - q_t q_{t-2} - q_{t-1}^2 + q_{t-1} q_{t-2}]\). Since \(q_t\) is i.i.d. with mean zero and \(q_{t-1}^2 = (\pm 1)^2 = 1\): \(\E[q_t q_{t-1}] = 0\), \(\E[q_t q_{t-2}] = 0\), \(\E[q_{t-1} q_{t-2}] = 0\), and \(\E[q_{t-1}^2] = 1\). Substituting these expectations: \(\Cov(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = \frac{S^2}{4} (0 - 0 - 1 + 0) = -\frac{S^2}{4}\). This establishes Roll's negative first-order autocovariance identity.

Empirical problems

In a replication of an asset pricing anomaly across 1980–2020, equal-weighted decile 10 minus 1 spread yields an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.20 with monthly turnover of 45% (one-way). If effective institutional trading costs are 20 basis points per turn, calculate the net Sharpe ratio after transaction costs, assuming annualized return volatility is 15%.

Solution

Gross annualized return is \(SR \times \sigma = 1.20 \times 0.15 = 0.18\) (18.0%). Annualized one-way turnover is \(0.45 \times 12 = 5.40\) (540% per year). Total annual transaction cost drag is \(5.40 \times 0.0020 = 0.0108\) (1.08% per year). Net annualized return is \(0.18 - 0.0108 = 0.1692\) (16.92%). Net Sharpe ratio is \(\frac{0.1692}{0.15} = 1.128\). The anomaly survives after transaction costs because the gross Sharpe ratio was high relative to the moderate 45% monthly turnover.

An empirical researcher sorts equities based on an earnings surprise signal. In pooled exchange quintiles, NASDAQ firms constitute 78% of the extreme long quintile and 82% of the extreme short quintile. When NYSE size breakpoints are used, NASDAQ firms drop to 28% of the long quintile and 31% of the short quintile. Explain how this alters the interpretation of the earnings surprise premium.

Solution

Under pooled exchange quintiles, the anomaly is overwhelmingly dominated by NASDAQ microcaps (over 80% of extreme portfolios). The reported spread primarily captures illiquidity premiums and high trading friction in small technology equities. Under NYSE breakpoints, the NASDAQ composition drops to ~30%, reflecting representative market proportions. If the earnings surprise spread remains statistically significant under NYSE breakpoints, it demonstrates that post-earnings announcement drift is a genuine market-wide phenomenon rather than a microcap illiquidity artifact.

Using CRSP share code filters, why must a researcher restrict common stock analysis to share codes 10 and 11, excluding REITs (share code 18), ADRs (share codes 30-32), and closed-end funds (share code 14)?

Solution

Share codes 10 and 11 denote ordinary US common shares. Real Estate Investment Trusts (code 18) face distinct tax pass-through regulations and mandatory 90% payout ratios, making their accounting ratios non-comparable to industrial firms. American Depositary Receipts (codes 30–32) represent claims on foreign corporations subject to foreign accounting standards and currency fluctuations. Closed-end funds (code 14) represent pools of financial assets rather than operating businesses. Pooling these entities with industrial common stocks contaminates factor loadings and distorts cross-sectional pricing.

Research problems

Critique the use of Compustat's "point-in-time" historical database versus standard Compustat annual files in evaluating fundamental accounting anomalies.

Solution

Standard Compustat annual files overwrite originally reported financial numbers with retrospective restatements (e.g. revisions resulting from accounting fraud investigations, mergers, or GAAP adjustments). Running a backtest with standard Compustat assumes that an investor in 2005 knew the restated earnings figure that was published in 2008, introducing severe look-ahead bias. The Compustat Point-in-Time (PIT) database records the exact snapshot of financial data that was available on the SEC filing date, eliminating restatement look-ahead bias and providing the only scientifically valid basis for backtesting fundamental strategies.

Develop an institutional sample hygiene protocol for evaluating cross-sectional predictability in corporate bonds, detailing trade reporting filters, matrix pricing exclusions, and liquidity adjustments.

Solution

An institutional corporate bond protocol requires four layers of hygiene: 1. TRACE Transaction Filtering: Filter FINRA TRACE records for trade cancellation codes, reversal flags, and trade execution errors; eliminate inter-dealer double-counting. 2. Matrix Pricing Exclusion: Discard interpolated dealer quote sheets (e.g. Bloomberg evaluated pricing) that reflect smoothing algorithms rather than actual market transactions. 3. Maturity and Seniority Sanitation: Restrict the universe to senior unsecured fixed-rate corporate bonds with remaining maturity between 1 and 30 years; exclude convertible, callable, or floating-rate debt with embedded options. 4. Volume and Size Truncation: Require minimum issue size of $100 million and at least two institutional transactions within the month to ensure reliable pricing.

Evaluate the econometric implications of Novy-Marx & Velikov's (2015) finding that anomaly strategies incurring more than 100% monthly turnover have negative net alphas after effective transaction costs.

Solution

Novy-Marx & Velikov (2015) show that high-turnover anomaly strategies (such as idiosyncratic volatility, short-term reversal, and high-frequency momentum) incur cumulative trading costs that eradicate apparent gross excess returns. For doctoral research, this proves that evaluating an anomaly purely on gross returns is econometrically incomplete. A doctoral dissertation proposing a high-turnover signal must explicitly model transaction costs using Hasbrouck or effective bid-ask spread estimators, reporting net-of-cost alphas and calculating the break-even trading cost where alpha vanishes.

Retrieval quiz

  1. Why does Shumway (1997) substitute -30% for NYSE/AMEX and -55% for NASDAQ when performance delisting returns are missing?

    Options

    Missing delisting returns in CRSP correspond to bankruptcy and liquidation; substituting empirical average losses corrects severe survivorship bias.

  2. What is the standard Shumway delisting replacement return for a performance-delisted NASDAQ common equity?

    Shumway (1997) establishes -55% (-0.55) as the empirical average delisting return for NASDAQ performance delistings.

  3. Why do Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020) sort portfolios using NYSE size breakpoints rather than pooled breakpoints?

    Options

    NYSE breakpoints align sorting deciles with economically meaningful capital cutoffs, avoiding microcap contamination.

  4. What constitutes look-ahead bias when merging Compustat accounting data with CRSP market prices?

    Options

    Financial statements are not available to market participants until public filing; using them on the fiscal period end date incorporates information before it was known.

  5. What CRSP share codes identify ordinary US operating company common shares?

    Options

    Share codes 10 and 11 denote ordinary US common equities, excluding ADRs, REITs, and closed-end investment funds.

  6. What does Roll's (1984) model prove regarding the first-order serial autocovariance of stock price changes?

    Options

    Roll (1984) proved that sequential trades oscillating between bid and ask prices induce negative serial covariance in recorded prices.

  7. Why does monthly equal-weighting produce a mechanical "diversification return" that overstates buy-and-hold returns?

    Options

    Continuous rebalancing back to equal weights buys losers and sells winners, capturing variance drag that vanishes after real-world trading frictions.

  8. According to Novy-Marx & Velikov (2015), what happens to anomalies with monthly turnover exceeding 100%?

    Options

    High-turnover trading strategies incur substantial transaction cost drag, transforming attractive gross alphas into net losses.
