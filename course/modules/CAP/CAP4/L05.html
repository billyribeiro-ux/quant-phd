Identification Strategy & Causal Econometrics

Establishing causal economic mechanisms distinguishes doctoral research from descriptive correlation analysis. When explanatory variables correlate with unobserved productivity shocks or suffer from reverse causality, naive OLS estimators are biased and inconsistent. In asset pricing, factor loadings are estimated with sampling error, creating generated regressor bias. This lesson formalizes identification strategies: two-stage least squares (2SLS), weak instrument diagnostics, and the Shanken asymptotic variance correction.

1. Instrumental Variables & Two-Stage Least Squares

In financial economics, explanatory variables such as corporate leverage, executive compensation, or dealer inventory are endogenously chosen by optimizing economic agents.

Definition 5.1 (Instrumental Variable Identification) An instrumental variable \(Z\) for endogenous regressor \(X\) in structural model \(Y = X \beta + u\) satisfies two foundational identifying restrictions:
1. Relevance: \(\Cov(Z, X) \ne 0\) (or \(\operatorname{rank}(\E[Z X^\top]) = K\)).
2. Exclusion Restriction: \(\Cov(Z, u) = 0\) (\(Z\) affects outcome \(Y\) exclusively through its impact on \(X\)).

Under these conditions, the two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimator projects \(X\) onto the subspace spanned by instruments \(Z\) before estimating the structural parameter as established by Angrist & Pischke (2009).

Theorem 5.1 (Weak Instrument Asymptotic Bias Bound) In a linear IV model with \(K\) excluded instruments, when the first-stage concentration parameter \(\mu^2 = \gamma^\top Z^\top Z \gamma / \sigma_v^2\) is small, the asymptotic relative bias of 2SLS toward OLS is approximately: \[ \frac{\E[\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} - \beta]}{\E[\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta]} \approx \frac{K}{\mu^2 / K} = \frac{1}{F - 1} \] where \(F\) is the first-stage effective F-statistic. To bound relative bias below 10%, the Stock-Yogo rule requires \(F > 10.0\).

Proof Following Staiger & Stock (1997) and Stock & Yogo (2005), expand the 2SLS estimator under weak instrument asymptotics where \(\gamma = C / \sqrt{N}\). The second-stage error is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} - \beta = (X^\top P_Z X)^{-1} X^\top P_Z u\). In expectations, the projection operator \(P_Z = Z (Z^\top Z)^{-1} Z^\top\) has trace \(K\). When instrument strength \(\mu^2\) is small, the denominator matrix \((X^\top P_Z X)\) is non-central Wishart with non-centrality \(\mu^2\) and dimension \(K\). Evaluating the ratio of expectations via the expectation of inverse Wishart matrices yields the leading term \(\frac{\Cov(u, v)}{\sigma_v^2} \frac{K}{\mu^2}\). Since OLS asymptotic bias is \(\frac{\Cov(u, v)}{\sigma_v^2}\), the relative bias ratio is \(\frac{K}{\mu^2} \approx \frac{1}{F - 1}\). Setting \(\frac{1}{F - 1} \le 0.10\) yields \(F - 1 \ge 10\), confirming \(F \ge 11\) (conventionally approximated as \(F > 10.0\)). This proof is complete.

Remark 5.1 (Generated Regressors and Shanken Invariance) In cross-sectional asset pricing, factor loadings \(\beta\) are not observed; they are estimated from first-pass time-series regressions. As proven by Shanken (1992), sampling error in first-pass betas inflates the second-pass asymptotic variance by the multiplicative factor \(c = 1 + \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\). Omitting this correction overstates t-statistics whenever factor risk premia \(\lambda\) are large.

2. The Shanken Asymptotic Variance Correction

In two-pass Fama-MacBeth regressions, the second-pass OLS regression of mean returns \(\bar{R}\) on estimated betas \(\hat{\beta}\) yields factor premia \(\hat{\lambda} = (\hat{\beta}^\top \hat{\beta})^{-1} \hat{\beta}^\top \bar{R}\).

Definition 5.2 (Shanken Asymptotic Covariance) The Shanken asymptotic covariance matrix of two-pass cross-sectional risk premia \(\hat{\lambda}\) is: \[ \operatorname{AsyVar}(\hat{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{T} \left( \Sigma_f + (1 + \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda) (\beta^\top \beta)^{-1} \beta^\top \Sigma_\epsilon \beta (\beta^\top \beta)^{-1} \right) \] where \(\Sigma_f\) is factor covariance, \(\Sigma_\epsilon\) is pricing residual covariance, and \(c = 1 + \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\) is the Shanken correction factor.

When factors are traded, \(\Sigma_f\) represents the benchmark variance of factor returns; the second term reflects the compounded error from estimating test asset betas in finite time samples.

Lab 1 · Two-Stage Instrumental Variables Identification & Weak Instrument Diagnostic

Under endogenous regressor contamination, naive OLS produces biased slope estimates, whereas 2SLS with strong instruments (first-stage F > 100) recovers the true causal parameter and satisfies the Sargan overidentification test.

"""Lab CAP4-L05-1: Two-Stage IV Identification & Weak Instrument Diagnostic.

Evaluates causal identification by comparing OLS vs 2SLS estimates under endogenous factor loadings,
computing first-stage Montiel Olea-Pflueger / Stock-Yogo F-statistics, and verifying the Sargan overidentification test.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats  # type: ignore[import-untyped]


def main() -> None:
    np.random.seed(42)
    n_obs = 300

    # 1. Structural model: Y = alpha + beta * X + epsilon
    # True causal parameter beta = 0.50
    # Endogeneity: Cov(X, epsilon) > 0 (e.g. unobserved managerial skill or confounding shock)
    true_beta = 0.50
    u_confound = np.random.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n_obs)
    epsilon = 0.6 * u_confound + np.random.normal(0.0, 0.8, size=n_obs)

    # 2. Instruments Z = [Z1, Z2] (two exogenous instruments)
    # Z1 is strong, Z2 is moderately strong
    z1 = np.random.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n_obs)
    z2 = np.random.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n_obs)
    z_matrix = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_obs), z1, z2])

    # Endogenous regressor X = gamma0 + gamma1 * Z1 + gamma2 * Z2 + 0.8 * u_confound + v
    gamma = np.array([0.2, 0.7, 0.4])
    v_noise = np.random.normal(0.0, 0.5, size=n_obs)
    x = z_matrix @ gamma + 0.7 * u_confound + v_noise

    y = 0.1 + true_beta * x + epsilon

    # 3. Naive OLS Estimation
    x_mat = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_obs), x])
    beta_ols = np.linalg.inv(x_mat.T @ x_mat) @ (x_mat.T @ y)
    ols_causal_slope = float(beta_ols[1])

    # 4. First-Stage Regression: X on Z
    gamma_hat = np.linalg.inv(z_matrix.T @ z_matrix) @ (z_matrix.T @ x)
    x_hat = z_matrix @ gamma_hat
    resid_first = x - x_hat
    # F-statistic for excluded instruments (Z1, Z2)
    k_excl = 2
    r2_first = float(1.0 - np.var(resid_first) / np.var(x))
    f_stat_first = float((r2_first / k_excl) / ((1.0 - r2_first) / (n_obs - 3)))

    # 5. Second-Stage Regression (2SLS): Y on [1, X_hat]
    x_hat_mat = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_obs), x_hat])
    beta_2sls = np.linalg.inv(x_hat_mat.T @ x_hat_mat) @ (x_hat_mat.T @ y)
    iv_causal_slope = float(beta_2sls[1])

    # 6. Sargan Overidentification Test
    # Residuals computed with actual X: e_iv = y - beta_0 - beta_1 * X
    resid_2sls = y - (x_mat @ beta_2sls)
    # Regress e_iv on Z
    gamma_sargan = np.linalg.inv(z_matrix.T @ z_matrix) @ (z_matrix.T @ resid_2sls)
    fitted_sargan = z_matrix @ gamma_sargan
    r2_sargan = float(np.var(fitted_sargan) / np.var(resid_2sls))
    sargan_stat = float(n_obs * r2_sargan)
    # Degrees of freedom = L - K = 2 - 1 = 1
    sargan_p = float(1.0 - stats.chi2.cdf(sargan_stat, df=1))

    is_weak = bool(f_stat_first < 10.0)

    print(f"True Causal Beta: {true_beta:.4f}")
    print(f"Naive OLS Beta: {ols_causal_slope:.4f}")
    print(f"2SLS Identified Beta: {iv_causal_slope:.4f}")
    print(f"First-Stage F-Statistic: {f_stat_first:.2f}")
    print(f"Weak Instrument Flag (F < 10): {is_weak}")
    print(f"Sargan Overid Stat (df=1): {sargan_stat:.4f}")
    print(f"Sargan p-value: {sargan_p:.4f}")


main()
True Causal Beta: 0.5000
Naive OLS Beta: 0.7272
2SLS Identified Beta: 0.4537
First-Stage F-Statistic: 132.29
Weak Instrument Flag (F < 10): False
Sargan Overid Stat (df=1): 0.2309
Sargan p-value: 0.6308

Naive OLS estimates an upward-biased slope of 0.7272 (true beta is 0.5000) due to positive confounding. The 2SLS estimator eliminates the bias, recovering 0.4537 with an effective first-stage F-statistic of 132.29 and an overidentification Sargan p-value of 0.6308, confirming instrument validity.

Where the canon breaks

Causal identification in asset pricing breaks down in three prominent settings:

  • Pervasive Unobserved Confounding: In macro-finance, aggregate shocks (e.g. monetary surprises) affect all firms simultaneously; finding a truly exogenous instrument that satisfies the exclusion restriction without affecting credit or expectations is difficult.
  • Weak Identification Robustness Breakdown: When instruments are weak (\(F < 10\)), standard Wald test confidence intervals have incorrect coverage; researchers must report Anderson-Rubin confidence sets that invert the objective function.
  • High-Dimensional Beta Uncertainty: When testing hundreds of anomaly factors simultaneously, factor covariance \(\Sigma_f\) becomes ill-conditioned, causing the Shanken quadratic form \(\lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\) to explode due to sample inversion instability.

Misconceptions

  1. A statistically significant first-stage t-statistic of 2.1 proves that an instrumental variable is strong.

    A t-statistic of 2.1 corresponds to an F-statistic of \(2.1^2 = 4.41\), which falls far below the Stock-Yogo hurdle of \(F > 10\); using this instrument produces severe weak-instrument bias and unreliable inference.

  2. Passing the Sargan or Hansen J-test of overidentifying restrictions proves that all instruments are exogenous.

    The Sargan test requires at least one valid instrument to benchmark against; if all instruments are mutually contaminated by the same unobserved macroeconomic confounder, the Sargan test cannot detect the invalidity.

  3. The Shanken correction factor is only necessary when sample size T is small.

    The Shanken correction \(c = 1 + \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\) is an asymptotic adjustment; even as \(T \to \infty\), beta estimation error inflates the second-pass variance, meaning the Shanken factor never converges to 1.0 when factor risk premia are non-zero.

Problem set

Mechanics problems

In an instrumental variables regression with \(T = 250\) observations, the first stage regresses endogenous regressor \(X\) on constant and two excluded instruments \(Z_1, Z_2\), yielding \(R^2 = 0.14\). Calculate the first-stage F-statistic and evaluate whether the instruments exceed the Stock-Yogo weak instrument threshold \(F > 10.0\).

Solution

The F-statistic formula for \(K = 2\) excluded instruments with \(T = 250\) and 3 total regressors (including constant) is: \[ F = \frac{R^2 / K}{(1 - R^2) / (T - K - 1)} = \frac{0.14 / 2}{(1 - 0.14) / (250 - 2 - 1)} = \frac{0.07}{0.86 / 247} = \frac{0.07}{0.0034818} \approx 20.10 \] Because \(F = 20.10 > 10.0\), the instruments satisfy the Stock-Yogo relevance threshold, ruling out severe weak instrument bias.

In a two-pass Fama-MacBeth estimation with two factors, the estimated factor risk premia are \(\hat{\lambda} = [0.006, 0.004]^\top\) and factor covariance is \(\Sigma_f = \begin{bmatrix} 0.0020 & 0.0004 \\ 0.0004 & 0.0016 \end{bmatrix}\). Calculate the Shanken correction factor \(c\).

Solution

Invert \(\Sigma_f\): \(\det(\Sigma_f) = 0.0020 \times 0.0016 - 0.0004^2 = 0.00000320 - 0.00000016 = 0.00000304\). \[ \Sigma_f^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.00000304} \begin{bmatrix} 0.0016 & -0.0004 \\ -0.0004 & 0.0020 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 526.316 & -131.579 \\ -131.579 & 657.895 \end{bmatrix} \] Compute \(\hat{\lambda}^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda}\): \(\Sigma_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 526.316(0.006) - 131.579(0.004) \\ -131.579(0.006) + 657.895(0.004) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.1579 - 0.5263 \\ -0.7895 + 2.6316 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.6316 \\ 1.8421 \end{bmatrix}\). \(\hat{\lambda}^\top (\Sigma_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda}) = 0.006(2.6316) + 0.004(1.8421) = 0.01579 + 0.007368 = 0.023158\). The Shanken factor is \(c = 1 + 0.023158 \approx 1.0232\).

In a 2SLS regression with \(T = 400\) observations, 1 endogenous regressor, and 3 instruments (\(L = 3\)), the second-stage residuals produce an uncentered \(R^2\) of 0.015 when regressed on the instruments. Calculate the Sargan overidentification test statistic and state the degrees of freedom.

Solution

The Sargan test statistic is \(S = T \times R^2 = 400 \times 0.015 = 6.00\). The degrees of freedom equal the number of overidentifying restrictions: \(df = L - K = 3 - 1 = 2\). Under the null hypothesis of valid instruments, \(S \sim \chi^2(2)\). The critical value at \(\alpha = 0.05\) is 5.991. Because \(6.00 > 5.991\) (\(p \approx 0.0498\)), the null hypothesis of instrument exogeneity is rejected at the 5 percent significance level.

Proof problems

Prove that when an endogenous regressor \(X\) is instrumented with \(Z\) under exact identification (\(L = K = 1\)), the 2SLS estimator is mathematically identical to the Indirect Least Squares (Wald) estimator: \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} = \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(Z, Y)}{\widehat{\Cov}(Z, X)}\).

Solution

In matrix form, the 2SLS estimator is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} = (X^\top P_Z X)^{-1} X^\top P_Z Y\), where \(P_Z = Z (Z^\top Z)^{-1} Z^\top\). For a single instrument \(Z\), \(P_Z X = Z (Z^\top Z)^{-1} (Z^\top X)\). Thus: \[ X^\top P_Z X = (X^\top Z) (Z^\top Z)^{-1} (Z^\top X) = \frac{(Z^\top X)^2}{Z^\top Z} \] Similarly: \[ X^\top P_Z Y = (X^\top Z) (Z^\top Z)^{-1} (Z^\top Y) = \frac{(Z^\top X)(Z^\top Y)}{Z^\top Z} \] Taking the ratio: \[ \hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} = \frac{\frac{(Z^\top X)(Z^\top Y)}{Z^\top Z}}{\frac{(Z^\top X)^2}{Z^\top Z}} = \frac{Z^\top Y}{Z^\top X} = \frac{\frac{1}{T} \sum Z_t Y_t}{\frac{1}{T} \sum Z_t X_t} = \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(Z, Y)}{\widehat{\Cov}(Z, X)} \] which is the classical Wald estimator. This completes the proof.

Prove that the Shanken correction factor \(c = 1 + \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\) is strictly bounded below by 1.0 for any positive-definite factor covariance matrix \(\Sigma_f\).

Solution

Because \(\Sigma_f\) is a valid covariance matrix of non-redundant factors, it is symmetric and positive-definite (\(\Sigma_f \succ 0\)). Its inverse \(\Sigma_f^{-1}\) is also symmetric and positive-definite. By definition of positive-definiteness, for any vector \(\lambda \in \R^K\): \[ \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda \ge 0 \] with strict equality if and only if \(\lambda = 0\). Therefore: \[ c = 1 + \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda \ge 1.0 \] with \(c > 1.0\) whenever at least one factor commands a non-zero risk premium \(\lambda \ne 0\). This completes the proof.

Prove that in the presence of unobserved confounding \(\Cov(X, u) = \sigma_{Xu} > 0\), the OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = \beta + \frac{\sum X_i u_i}{\sum X_i^2}\) converges in probability to \(\beta + \frac{\sigma_{Xu}}{\sigma_X^2} \ne \beta\).

Solution

Write the OLS estimator as \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = \beta + \frac{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N X_i u_i}{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N X_i^2}\). By the Weak Law of Large Numbers, under finite second moments: \(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N X_i u_i \pto \E[X u] = \Cov(X, u) + \E[X]\E[u] = \sigma_{Xu}\), and \(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N X_i^2 \pto \E[X^2] = \sigma_X^2\). By the continuous mapping theorem: \[ \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \pto \beta + \frac{\sigma_{Xu}}{\sigma_X^2} \] Since \(\sigma_{Xu} \ne 0\), \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} \ne \beta\), proving asymptotic inconsistency.

Empirical problems

An empirical corporate finance study regresses firm capital expenditures on CEO option delta. The OLS slope is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = 0.42\) (\(t = 4.8\)). When instrumenting option delta with FAS 123R accounting rule exposure, the first-stage F-statistic is \(F = 34.2\), and the 2SLS slope is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} = 0.18\) (\(t = 2.4\)). Interpret the difference between OLS and 2SLS estimates.

Solution

The OLS estimate (\(0.42\)) is more than double the 2SLS estimate (\(0.18\)), indicating that naive OLS suffers from substantial positive omitted variable bias. High-growth firms with abundant investment opportunities naturally grant larger option packages to executives and simultaneously invest more, generating a spurious positive correlation. The strong instrument (\(F = 34.2 > 10\)) purges this endogeneity, revealing that the true causal impact of executive risk incentives on capital expenditure is 0.18, less than half the naive OLS figure.

In a cross-sectional asset pricing test of an intermediary capital factor, the naive Fama-MacBeth standard error on the factor risk premium is \(SE_{\text{naive}} = 0.0015\). If the factor variance is \(\sigma_f^2 = 0.0025\) and the estimated premium is \(\hat{\lambda} = 0.0050\), compute the Shanken correction factor and the corrected standard error, assuming residual beta uncertainty accounts for 80% of the second-pass variance.

Solution

The single-factor quadratic form is \(\frac{\hat{\lambda}^2}{\sigma_f^2} = \frac{0.0050^2}{0.0025} = \frac{0.000025}{0.0025} = 0.010\). The Shanken correction factor is \(c = 1 + 0.010 = 1.010\). If beta uncertainty accounts for 80% of the variance: \(V_{\text{total}} = 0.20 \times SE^2 + 0.80 \times c \times SE^2 = SE^2 (0.20 + 0.80 \times 1.010) = SE^2 (0.20 + 0.808) = 1.008 SE^2\). \(SE_{\text{corrected}} = \sqrt{1.008} \times 0.0015 \approx 1.00399 \times 0.0015 \approx 0.001506\). The standard error inflation is approximately 0.4%.

A study uses weather shocks as an instrument for local economic activity to test whether regional recessions reduce municipal bond yields. A seminar participant objects that extreme weather directly damages municipal infrastructure, creating rebuilding costs. Evaluate this critique in the context of IV exclusion restrictions.

Solution

The seminar participant's critique invalidates the instrument by identifying a direct structural violation of the exclusion restriction. An instrument \(Z\) (weather shocks) must affect the outcome \(Y\) (municipal bond yields) exclusively through the channel of interest \(X\) (local economic activity). If extreme weather physically destroys roads, water systems, and municipal facilities, the city must issue emergency repair bonds or face fiscal insolvency, directly altering bond default risk and yields independent of local GDP. Because \(\Cov(Z, u) \ne 0\), the instrument is invalid and 2SLS estimates are biased.

Research problems

Critique the use of lagged explanatory variables \(X_{t-1}\) as instruments for current variables \(X_t\) in panel asset pricing regressions.

Solution

Lagged variables are valid instruments if and only if the structural error term is serially uncorrelated (\(\E[u_t u_{t-1}] = 0\)). In asset pricing and corporate finance panels, unobserved firm characteristics (such as corporate culture, brand capital, or persistent unmeasured risk) induce strong serial correlation in error terms (\(u_{i,t} = \rho u_{i,t-1} + e_{i,t}\)). Under serial correlation, \(\Cov(X_{i,t-1}, u_{i,t}) = \rho \Cov(X_{i,t-1}, u_{i,t-1}) \ne 0\), directly violating the exclusion restriction. Using lagged variables mechanically provides high first-stage F-statistics while failing the exclusion restriction, compounding bias.

Formulate a quasi-experimental identification strategy to isolate the causal effect of algorithmic low-latency trading on market liquidity, detailing the source of exogenous variation and placebo diagnostics.

Solution

Exogenous Variation: Exploit exchange co-location policy changes or physical optical fiber infrastructure rollouts (e.g. the Spread Networks latency reduction between Chicago and New Jersey) that reduced latency for specific fiber routes on discrete calendar dates.
Identification Framework: A difference-in-differences model comparing equities whose order books trade across geographically dispersed exchanges (treatment) against single-venue stocks (control) around the activation date.
Placebo Diagnostic: Test for pre-treatment parallel trends and run placebo regressions on synthetic activation dates 6 months prior to the true network activation.

Explain why Lewellen, Nagel & Shanken (2010) advocate testing asset pricing models against non-equity portfolios (such as corporate bonds or currency portfolios) to verify causal identification.

Solution

Lewellen, Nagel & Shanken (2010) demonstrate that standard US equity test portfolios (e.g. the 25 Fama-French size and value sorts) possess an extremely tight factor structure dominated by three principal components. Consequently, almost any proposed economic factor with weak correlation to market or size betas will achieve a high cross-sectional \(R^2\) by chance. Testing candidate models against corporate bond portfolios or currency carry portfolios breaks this equity factor structure. If a factor represents a genuine causal state variable pricing aggregate marginal utility, it must price cross-asset returns without generating massive pricing errors.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What are the two mandatory mathematical conditions that a valid instrumental variable \(Z\) must satisfy?

    Options

    An instrument must correlate with the endogenous regressor (relevance) while being orthogonal to the structural error term (exclusion).

  2. What is the standard Stock-Yogo rule-of-thumb first-stage F-statistic threshold required to rule out severe weak instrument bias?

    The Stock & Yogo (2005) threshold requires an effective first-stage F-statistic exceeding 10.0.

  3. Why does the Shanken (1992) correction factor \(1 + \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\) strictly exceed 1.0 when factor risk premia are non-zero?

    Options

    Sampling noise in estimated betas propagates into the second pass, requiring variance inflation by \(1 + \lambda^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \lambda\).

  4. In an exactly identified linear IV model (\(L = K = 1\)), what classical estimator is mathematically identical to 2SLS?

    Options

    Under single-instrument exact identification, the 2SLS projection collapses to the ratio of sample covariances, which is the Wald estimator.

  5. What does the Sargan test evaluate in overidentified 2SLS regressions?

    Options

    The Sargan test checks whether overidentifying moment conditions hold, testing instrument orthogonality under the assumption of at least one valid benchmark instrument.

  6. Why are lagged explanatory variables \(X_{t-1}\) frequently invalid as instruments for \(X_t\)?

    Options

    Persistent unobserved firm characteristics generate autocorrelated error terms, causing lagged regressors to correlate with current disturbances.

  7. What is the asymptotic consequence of weak instruments (\(F \to 0\)) on 2SLS estimators?

    Options

    Theorem 5.1 proves that as \(F\) falls toward 1, the 2SLS relative bias approaches 100%, causing 2SLS to inherit the full bias of OLS.

  8. Why does testing an asset pricing model across out-of-market test assets (e.g. corporate bonds) strengthen identification?

    Options

    Lewellen, Nagel & Shanken (2010) show that testing on cross-asset classes rules out spurious factors that appear successful only because of equity factor structure.
