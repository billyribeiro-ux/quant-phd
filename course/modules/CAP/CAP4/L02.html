Literature Synthesis & Theoretical Framing

An empirical dissertation without structural theoretical framing is vulnerable to the charge of measurement without theory. A doctoral thesis must anchor its empirical hypotheses in stochastic discount factor restrictions and intertemporal Euler equations. This lesson establishes the discipline of theoretical framing: translating economic axioms into moment conditions, mapping state variables to marginal utility shocks, and deriving testable comparative statics.

1. The Stochastic Discount Factor Architecture

Modern doctoral research organizes all asset pricing models under the unifying stochastic discount factor (SDF) framework formalized by Cochrane (2005).

Definition 2.1 (Theoretical Framing) Theoretical framing is the mathematical specification of an investor's intertemporal optimization problem, market clearing conditions, and information filtration that endogenously generates a pricing kernel \(m_{t+1}\) governing asset excess returns via the Euler condition \(\E_t[m_{t+1} R_{t+1}^e] = 0\).

Rather than asserting ad hoc linear factor specifications, the researcher must show that candidate factors proxy for state variables that drive innovations in investor marginal utility.

Theorem 2.1 (Affine SDF Pricing Identity) Let \(m_{t+1} = 1 - b^\top (f_{t+1} - \E[f_{t+1}])\) be an affine candidate stochastic discount factor, where \(f_{t+1}\) is a \(K\)-dimensional vector of macroeconomic or factor innovations with covariance matrix \(\Sigma_f\). If \(m_{t+1}\) prices a vector of excess returns \(R_{t+1}^e\) with covariance \(\Sigma_{R^e}\) and factor exposures \(\beta = \Cov(R_{t+1}^e, f_{t+1}) \Sigma_f^{-1}\), then expected excess returns satisfy the exact multi-factor beta representation: \[ \E[R_{t+1}^e] = \beta \lambda \] where the vector of factor risk premia is uniquely determined by \(\lambda = \Sigma_f b\).

Proof The fundamental Euler condition requires \(\E[m_{t+1} R_{t+1}^e] = 0\). Expanding the expectation: \[ \E[m_{t+1}] \E[R_{t+1}^e] + \Cov(m_{t+1}, R_{t+1}^e) = 0 \] Substituting \(m_{t+1} = 1 - b^\top (f_{t+1} - \E[f_{t+1}])\), we have \(\E[m_{t+1}] = 1\), and: \[ \Cov(m_{t+1}, R_{t+1}^e) = \Cov(-b^\top f_{t+1}, R_{t+1}^e) = -\Cov(R_{t+1}^e, f_{t+1}) b \] Substituting back into the Euler equation: \[ \E[R_{t+1}^e] = \Cov(R_{t+1}^e, f_{t+1}) b \] By definition of multivariate regression betas, \(\beta = \Cov(R_{t+1}^e, f_{t+1}) \Sigma_f^{-1}\), which implies \(\Cov(R_{t+1}^e, f_{t+1}) = \beta \Sigma_f\). Therefore: \[ \E[R_{t+1}^e] = \beta (\Sigma_f b) = \beta \lambda \] where \(\lambda = \Sigma_f b\). This proof is complete.

Remark 2.1 (ICAPM State Variable Restrictions) Following Merton (1973), a factor can earn a risk premium in equilibrium if and only if it proxies for shifts in the investment opportunity set (e.g., changes in future volatility, interest rates, or borrowing constraints). Rejecting an anomaly that cannot be linked to marginal utility state variables preserves theoretical rigor.

2. Cross-Sectional Moment Conditions & Hansen-Jagannathan Distance

When evaluating candidate asset pricing models, doctoral research relies on invariant distance metrics rather than simple regression \(R^2\).

Definition 2.2 (Hansen-Jagannathan Distance) Given an \(N\)-dimensional vector of excess returns with second-moment matrix \(\Omega = \E[R^e R^{e\top}]\), the Hansen-Jagannathan distance of candidate SDF \(m\) is: \[ HJ(m) = \sqrt{\alpha(m)^\top \Omega^{-1} \alpha(m)} \] where \(\alpha(m) = \E[m R^e]\) is the vector of cross-sectional Euler equation pricing errors.

The Hansen-Jagannathan distance is invariant to non-singular portfolio repackaging, providing an objective metric for comparing non-nested models across diverse asset classes.

Lab 1 · SDF Theoretical Euler Invariant & Pricing Error Diagnostic

An optimal affine SDF satisfies Euler moment restrictions E[m R^e] = 0 with zero pricing error, whereas a misspecified candidate factor yields strictly positive pricing errors and a non-zero Hansen-Jagannathan distance.

"""Lab CAP4-L02-1: SDF Theoretical Euler Invariant & Pricing Error Diagnostic.

Evaluates theoretical framing by deriving Hansen-Jagannathan SDF bounds,
evaluating Euler equation pricing errors E[m R^e] = 0, and computing Hansen-Jagannathan distance.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    np.random.seed(42)
    n_assets = 5
    rf = 0.02
    mean_sdf = 1.0 / (1.0 + rf)

    # 1. Calibrated cross-sectional excess returns and covariance
    mu_excess = np.array([0.05, 0.07, 0.04, 0.08, 0.06])
    volatilities = np.array([0.16, 0.22, 0.14, 0.25, 0.18])
    corr = np.array(
        [
            [1.00, 0.50, 0.35, 0.60, 0.45],
            [0.50, 1.00, 0.40, 0.65, 0.50],
            [0.35, 0.40, 1.00, 0.40, 0.30],
            [0.60, 0.65, 0.40, 1.00, 0.55],
            [0.45, 0.50, 0.30, 0.55, 1.00],
        ]
    )
    cov_excess = np.outer(volatilities, volatilities) * corr
    inv_cov = np.linalg.inv(cov_excess)

    # 2. Maximum Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio
    sr_tangency = float(np.sqrt(mu_excess @ inv_cov @ mu_excess))
    hj_vol_bound = float(mean_sdf * sr_tangency)

    # 3. Candidate affine SDF: m = mean_sdf * (1 - b^T (f - E[f]))
    # For a correctly specified single-factor model tracking tangency: b = inv_cov @ mu_excess
    b_optimal = inv_cov @ mu_excess
    var_f = float(mu_excess @ inv_cov @ mu_excess)
    sdf_vol = float(mean_sdf * np.sqrt(var_f))

    # 4. Misspecified candidate SDF: naive equal-weighted factor
    b_misspec = np.ones(n_assets) * 0.2
    # Implied pricing errors: alpha = mu_excess - Cov(R^e, m) / E[m]
    # Here Cov(R^e, m) = -mean_sdf * cov_excess @ b
    cov_re_m = -mean_sdf * (cov_excess @ b_misspec)
    pricing_errors = mu_excess + (cov_re_m / mean_sdf)

    # 5. Hansen-Jagannathan Distance: HJ = sqrt(alpha^T E[R R^T]^{-1} alpha)
    # Using second-moment matrix for excess returns: Omega = cov_excess + outer(mu, mu)
    second_moment = cov_excess + np.outer(mu_excess, mu_excess)
    inv_second_moment = np.linalg.inv(second_moment)
    hj_distance = float(np.sqrt(pricing_errors @ inv_second_moment @ pricing_errors))

    # Perfect SDF Euler error
    cov_re_m_opt = -mean_sdf * (cov_excess @ b_optimal)
    optimal_pricing_errors = mu_excess + (cov_re_m_opt / mean_sdf)
    max_euler_error = float(np.max(np.abs(optimal_pricing_errors)))

    print(f"Tangency Sharpe Ratio: {sr_tangency:.4f}")
    print(f"Hansen-Jagannathan Volatility Bound: {hj_vol_bound:.4f}")
    print(f"Optimal SDF Volatility: {sdf_vol:.4f}")
    print(f"Optimal Model Max Euler Error: {max_euler_error:.2e}")
    print(f"Misspecified Mean Abs Pricing Error: {float(np.mean(np.abs(pricing_errors))):.4f}")
    print(f"Misspecified Hansen-Jagannathan Distance: {hj_distance:.4f}")


main()
Tangency Sharpe Ratio: 0.4191
Hansen-Jagannathan Volatility Bound: 0.4109
Optimal SDF Volatility: 0.4109
Optimal Model Max Euler Error: 1.39e-17
Misspecified Mean Abs Pricing Error: 0.0383
Misspecified Hansen-Jagannathan Distance: 0.2568

The optimal SDF achieves an Euler error of 1.39e-17 and hits the Hansen-Jagannathan volatility bound of 0.4109, whereas the misspecified model exhibits a mean absolute pricing error of 0.0383 and an HJ distance of 0.2568.

Where the canon breaks

Linear factor models encounter structural failures in three prominent regimes:

  • Non-Linear State Dependence: Linear beta pricing \(\E[R^e] = \beta \lambda\) assumes constant risk prices. In periods of intermediary distress or liquidity crunches, marginal utility spikes non-linearly, causing linear models to underprice tail insurance.
  • Cross-Asset Pricing Divergence: Factor models calibrated exclusively on US equity deciles (e.g. Fama-French 5-factor) fail catastrophically when applied to corporate credit, currency carry, or sovereign debt, generating large HJ distances.
  • Negative SDF Realizations: Affine models \(m = 1 - b^\top (f - \E[f])\) can take negative values during market crashes, violating the no-arbitrage axiom that strictly positive pricing kernels must exist.

Misconceptions

  1. A high cross-sectional \(R^2\) in a Fama-MacBeth regression proves that the underlying theoretical model is correctly specified.

    Lewellen, Nagel & Shanken (2010) prove that cross-sectional \(R^2\) on portfolios with strong factor structure (such as size and value sorts) is an uninformative diagnostic that can be trivially inflated by adding any factor with non-zero correlation to the market.

  2. Theoretical framing requires developing a complex dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) model with dozens of unobservable parameters.

    Rigor does not require unnecessary mathematical ornamentation; a parsimonious stochastic discount factor model that directly links asset returns to marginal utility state variables provides far greater clarity than an over-parameterized DSGE system.

  3. Stochastic discount factors are only applicable to continuous-time mathematics.

    The fundamental pricing relation \(P = \E[m X]\) is a universal, model-free identity derived directly from no-arbitrage, applicable across discrete-time panels, option pricing surfaces, and macroeconomic aggregates alike.

Problem set

Mechanics problems

Given an affine stochastic discount factor \(m = 1 - 2.5 (f - \E[f])\), where factor variance is \(\Var(f) = 0.04\), calculate the implied factor risk premium \(\lambda\).

Solution

Under Theorem 2.1, the factor risk premium satisfies \(\lambda = \Sigma_f b\). Substituting the given values: \(\lambda = 0.04 \times 2.5 = 0.10\) (10.0% annualized risk premium). A test asset with factor loading \(\beta = 1.2\) commands an expected excess return of \(\E[R^e] = \beta \lambda = 1.2 \times 0.10 = 0.12\) (12.0%).

A two-asset economy has expected excess returns \(\mu = [0.06, 0.08]^\top\) and second-moment return matrix \(\Omega = \begin{bmatrix} 0.04 & 0.02 \\ 0.02 & 0.05 \end{bmatrix}\). A candidate model produces pricing errors \(\alpha = [0.015, -0.010]^\top\). Calculate the Hansen-Jagannathan distance \(HJ\).

Solution

First invert \(\Omega\): \(\det(\Omega) = 0.04 \times 0.05 - 0.02^2 = 0.0020 - 0.0004 = 0.0016\). \[ \Omega^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.0016} \begin{bmatrix} 0.05 & -0.02 \\ -0.02 & 0.04 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 31.25 & -12.50 \\ -12.50 & 25.00 \end{bmatrix} \] Compute the quadratic form: \(\Omega^{-1} \alpha = \begin{bmatrix} 31.25(0.015) - 12.50(-0.010) \\ -12.50(0.015) + 25.00(-0.010) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.46875 + 0.125 \\ -0.1875 - 0.250 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.59375 \\ -0.4375 \end{bmatrix}\). \(\alpha^\top \Omega^{-1} \alpha = 0.015(0.59375) + (-0.010)(-0.4375) = 0.00890625 + 0.004375 = 0.01328125\). \(HJ = \sqrt{0.01328125} \approx 0.11524\).

In an economy with risk-free rate \(R_f = 1.02\) and maximum attainable Sharpe ratio \(SR^* = 0.45\), calculate the Hansen-Jagannathan lower bound on the volatility of any admissible stochastic discount factor \(\sigma(m)\).

Solution

The mean stochastic discount factor is \(\E[m] = \frac{1}{R_f} = \frac{1}{1.02} \approx 0.98039\). The Hansen-Jagannathan volatility bound states that \(\sigma(m) \ge \E[m] SR^*\). Substituting the given values: \(\sigma(m) \ge 0.98039 \times 0.45 \approx 0.44118\). Thus, any pricing kernel capable of pricing asset payoffs must exhibit a standard deviation of at least 44.12%.

Proof problems

Prove that in the Campbell & Shiller (1988) log-linearization framework, the log price-dividend ratio \(p_t - d_t\) satisfies the identity \(p_t - d_t = \frac{k}{1 - \rho} + \sum_{j=0}^\infty \rho^j \E_t[\Delta d_{t+1+j} - r_{t+1+j}]\), and state the transversality condition required for convergence.

Solution

The Campbell-Shiller approximation linearizes log return \(r_{t+1} \approx k + \rho (p_{t+1} - d_{t+1}) + \Delta d_{t+1} - (p_t - d_t)\), where \(\rho = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\overline{d-p}}}\) and \(k = -\ln \rho - (1 - \rho) \ln(1/\rho - 1)\). Rearranging for the price-dividend ratio: \(p_t - d_t = k + \rho (p_{t+1} - d_{t+1}) + \Delta d_{t+1} - r_{t+1}\). Taking conditional expectations \(\E_t[\cdot]\) and iterating forward \(N\) periods yields: \[ p_t - d_t = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \rho^j (k + \E_t[\Delta d_{t+1+j} - r_{t+1+j}]) + \rho^N \E_t[p_{t+N} - d_{t+N}] \] Assuming the transversality condition \(\lim_{N \to \infty} \rho^N \E_t[p_{t+N} - d_{t+N}] = 0\) (which rules out rational valuation bubbles), taking the limit as \(N \to \infty\) establishes the infinite-horizon present-value identity.

Prove that the Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS) statistic follows an exact central \(F(N, T - N - K)\) distribution under the null hypothesis of zero pricing errors \(\alpha = 0\) when regression residuals are i.i.d. normal.

Solution

Under the null hypothesis \(\alpha = 0\), the estimated intercepts \(\hat{\alpha} \sim \Normal(0, \frac{1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}}{T} \Sigma_\epsilon)\). The quadratic form \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \Sigma_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha}\) is a scaled \(\chi^2(N)\) variate. Independently, the residual covariance estimator \((T - K - 1) \hat{\Sigma}_\epsilon \sim \mathcal{W}_N(\Sigma_\epsilon, T - K - 1)\) follows an \(N\)-dimensional Wishart distribution with \(T - K - 1\) degrees of freedom. By the properties of Hotelling's \(T^2\) and Wishart distributions, the ratio of quadratic forms: \[ \frac{T - N - K}{N(T - K - 1)} \hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha} \] cancels the nuisance parameter \(\Sigma_\epsilon\) and follows an \(F(N, T - N - K)\) distribution when scaled by the factor normalization \(1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}\). This completes the proof.

Prove that adding an orthogonal noise factor \(g_{t+1} \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) uncorrelated with asset returns and the true SDF cannot reduce the population Hansen-Jagannathan distance.

Solution

Let \(m(b)\) be the original candidate SDF and \(m(b, \gamma) = m(b) - \gamma g_{t+1}\) be the augmented candidate SDF. The pricing error vector on excess returns is \(\alpha(b, \gamma) = \E[m(b, \gamma) R^e] = \E[m(b) R^e] - \gamma \E[g R^e]\). Because \(g\) is orthogonal to asset returns (\(\E[g R^e] = 0\)), \(\alpha(b, \gamma) = \alpha(b)\) identically for all \(\gamma\). The Hansen-Jagannathan distance is \(HJ = \sqrt{\alpha^\top \Omega^{-1} \alpha}\), which depends solely on \(\alpha(b)\) and the return second-moment matrix \(\Omega\). Since \(\alpha(b, \gamma) = \alpha(b)\), \(HJ(m(b, \gamma)) = HJ(m(b))\) for all \(\gamma\). Thus, adding an orthogonal noise factor cannot reduce the population HJ distance.

Empirical problems

A researcher evaluates a 3-factor model on 25 size and book-to-market portfolios across \(T = 360\) months. The estimated time-series intercepts yield \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha} = 0.52\), and the mean factor vector yields \(\bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f} = 0.15\). Compute the GRS test statistic and state the critical value at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Solution

Degrees of freedom: \(N = 25\), \(T = 360\), \(K = 3\). The degrees of freedom denominator is \(T - N - K = 360 - 25 - 3 = 332\). The scaling constant is \(\frac{T - N - K}{N} = \frac{332}{25} = 13.28\). The quadratic ratio is \(\frac{0.52}{1 + 0.15} = \frac{0.52}{1.15} \approx 0.452174\). Multiplying: \(GRS = 13.28 \times 0.452174 \approx 6.0049\). The critical value for an \(F(25, 332)\) distribution at \(\alpha = 0.05\) is approximately 1.54. Because \(6.0049 \gg 1.54\), the 3-factor model is decisively rejected.

In a two-pass Fama-MacBeth estimation of the Fama-French 5-factor model, the unadjusted OLS t-statistic on the profitability factor (RMW) is \(t_{\text{naive}} = 3.25\). If \(\hat{\lambda}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda} = 0.44\), compute the Shanken correction factor and the Shanken-adjusted t-statistic.

Solution

The Shanken correction factor is \(c = 1 + \hat{\lambda}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \hat{\lambda} = 1 + 0.44 = 1.44\). In the asymptotic approximation where residual estimation uncertainty dominates, the standard error scales by \(\sqrt{c} = \sqrt{1.44} = 1.20\). The Shanken-corrected t-statistic is \(t_{\text{Shanken}} = \frac{t_{\text{naive}}}{\sqrt{c}} = \frac{3.25}{1.20} \approx 2.708\). Notice that the t-statistic falls from 3.25 down to 2.71, dropping below the Harvey-Liu-Zhu hurdle of 3.0.

A macro-finance study examines the Bansal-Yaron (2004) long-run risk model. Consumption growth volatility is calibrated to \(\sigma_c = 0.015\) annually, with persistence \(\rho = 0.979\). If risk aversion is \(\gamma = 10\) and intertemporal elasticity of substitution is \(\psi = 1.5\), explain why an increase in economic uncertainty reduces the price-dividend ratio.

Solution

In Epstein-Zin preferences, when \(\psi > 1\) and \(\gamma > 1\), the agent has preference for early resolution of uncertainty (\(\gamma > 1/\psi\)). In this parameter regime, the intertemporal substitution effect dominates the wealth effect. When economic uncertainty (volatility of expected consumption growth) rises, agents perceive future growth states to be riskier. Because \(\psi > 1\), the willingness to substitute consumption intertemporally induces a flight to safe assets and a severe reduction in equity demand, lowering equity prices relative to dividends.

Research problems

Evaluate why Campbell and Cochrane's (1999) external habit formation model requires a countercyclical price of risk rather than countercyclical consumption volatility to explain the equity premium puzzle.

Solution

Empirical macroeconomic data shows that aggregate consumption growth is nearly i.i.d. with smooth volatility that does not exhibit large countercyclical surges. To explain why the equity premium and Sharpe ratio are strongly countercyclical without countercyclical macroeconomic volatility, Campbell and Cochrane introduce an external habit \(H_t\) where surplus consumption \(S_t = \frac{C_t - H_t}{C_t}\) falls toward zero during recessions. Effective relative risk aversion is \(\gamma_t = \frac{\gamma}{S_t}\). As \(S_t\) drops in economic downturns, investors become extremely risk averse, driving up the Sharpe ratio \(\frac{\sigma(m)}{\E[m]}\) and generating volatile risk premia despite smooth consumption growth.

Develop an adversarial theoretical critique of an empirical thesis proposing a 12-factor linear model that includes multiple firm accounting ratios without an explicit budget constraint or Euler equation derivation.

Solution

The critique highlights three foundational defects. First, by the Hansen-Jagannathan bound, any valid multi-factor model must generate an SDF whose volatility satisfies \(\sigma(m) \ge \E[m] SR^*\); packing 12 correlated accounting factors without checking \(\Sigma_f\) conditioning causes near-singular inversion and arbitrary factor risk prices. Second, under Cochrane (2005), factors must represent marginal utility state variables; firm accounting characteristics (e.g. inventory turnover or accruals) are firm-level choices rather than aggregate state variables, violating the ICAPM. Third, without testing whether the 12 factors span the efficient frontier via GRS or HJ-distance against the Fama-French or q-factor baselines, the model represents arbitrary statistical factor proliferation.

Formulate the theoretical mapping between intermediary equity constraints in He & Krishnamurthy (2013) and the cross-sectional pricing kernel, explaining why intermediary capital earns a higher risk premium during market stress.

Solution

In He & Krishnamurthy (2013), households can only access risky asset markets through equity investments in specialized financial intermediaries. When intermediary net worth \(W_t\) is abundant, equity constraints are slack, and marginal utility tracks standard household consumption. When large macroeconomic shocks erode intermediary equity, the regulatory or funding constraint binds, and the intermediary's marginal value of wealth surges. The effective stochastic discount factor becomes \(m_{t+1} = \beta \frac{u'(C_{t+1})}{u'(C_t)} \frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t}\), where \(\lambda_t\) is the shadow value of intermediary capital. Assets that pay off poorly when intermediary capital is depleted command massive risk premia, explaining credit spread spikes and basis divergences during crises.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the fundamental Euler equation that every valid stochastic discount factor \(m_{t+1}\) must satisfy for any excess return \(R_{t+1}^e\)?

    Options

    An excess return is a zero-cost investment payoff; absence of arbitrage requires its present discounted value to be identically zero: \(\E[m R^e] = 0\).

  2. Under Theorem 2.1, how is the factor risk premium vector \(\lambda\) related to the affine SDF coefficient vector \(b\) and factor covariance \(\Sigma_f\)?

    Options

    Substituting \(\Cov(R^e, f) = \beta \Sigma_f\) into \(\E[R^e] = \Cov(R^e, f) b\) yields \(\E[R^e] = \beta (\Sigma_f b) = \beta \lambda\), so \(\lambda = \Sigma_f b\).

  3. What does the Hansen-Jagannathan distance measure for a candidate asset pricing model?

    Options

    The HJ distance evaluates cross-sectional pricing errors weighted by the inverse second-moment matrix of returns, providing a metric invariant to portfolio repackaging.

  4. What is the expected value of an affine candidate SDF \(m = 1 - b^\top (f - \E[f])\) normalized to price sure payoffs at gross interest rate \(R_f = 1\)?

    Because \(\E[f - \E[f]] = 0\), \(\E[m] = 1 - b^\top(0) = 1.0\).

  5. Why does Merton's (1973) ICAPM restrict which variables can command non-zero risk premia in equilibrium?

    Options

    Merton's ICAPM establishes that multi-factor risk premia arise from investors hedging adverse shifts in future investment opportunities.

  6. What condition in the Campbell-Shiller present value identity rules out rational stock market bubbles?

    Options

    Transversality ensures that the present value of terminal asset prices vanishes as the horizon extends to infinity, eliminating explosive bubble paths.

  7. Why is an affine candidate SDF \(m = 1 - b^\top(f - \E[f])\) vulnerable to arbitrage violations during severe market downturns?

    Options

    No-arbitrage requires \(m > 0\) in all states of the world. Affine specifications lack boundary constraints and can turn negative under extreme market draws.

  8. Why is reporting cross-sectional \(R^2\) alone insufficient to validate an asset pricing model according to Lewellen, Nagel & Shanken (2010)?

    Options

    Lewellen, Nagel & Shanken (2010) show that standard test portfolios (like 25 Fama-French sorts) have low dimensionality, allowing spurious factors to achieve high \(R^2\).
