Doctoral Thesis Defense & Committee Examination

The doctoral thesis defense is the culminating milestone of scholarly training. It is an adversarial oral examination where expert faculty scrutinize mathematical derivations, econometric identification, data hygiene assumptions, and economic mechanisms. A successful candidate cannot rely on scripted slides; they must defend empirical findings against five hostile attack vectors: look-ahead accounting restatements, microcap leverage contamination, turnover transaction drag, weak instrument breakdown, and out-of-sample structural decay. Mastering defense examination dynamics empowers doctoral scholars to demonstrate mathematical mastery, defend empirical integrity, and validate the doctoral contribution under uncompromising scrutiny.

1. Oral Defense Architecture & Examination Protocol

In quantitative financial economics, the doctoral defense evaluates whether a candidate has transitioned from a student solving assigned exercises to an autonomous research scholar capable of generating original scientific knowledge per Berk, Harvey & Hirshleifer (2017).

Definition 10.1 (Thesis defense) The thesis defense is the formal oral examination where a doctoral candidate presents an original dissertation, establishes the validity of its theoretical and empirical contributions, and defends its findings against adversarial methodological interrogation by a faculty examination committee.

The standard doctoral defense protocol consists of three phases:

  1. Autonomous Presentation (30–45 minutes): An uninterrupted presentation of the central economic question, structural model, identification strategy, primary empirical tables, and falsification batteries.
  2. Adversarial Committee Interrogation (60–90 minutes): Intensive question-and-answer probing identification vulnerabilities, point-in-time sample construction, microcap dependencies, and economic mechanisms.
  3. Executive Committee Deliberation: Private faculty evaluation awarding Pass without Revisions, Pass with Minor Revisions, Major Revisions, or Fail.

Theorem 10.1 (Net Economic Alpha Survival Bound) Let \(\alpha_{\text{gross}}\) be the reported annualized long-short factor alpha with annual turnover \(\tau\). Under simultaneous hostile committee perturbations—look-ahead accounting adjustment \(\delta_{\text{la}}\), microcap exclusion penalty \(\delta_{\text{mc}}\), effective trading friction \(c_{\text{eff}}\) per unit turnover, and post-publication structural decay rate \(\lambda_{\text{decay}}\)—the net surviving economic alpha satisfies: \[ \alpha_{\text{net}} = \Big( \alpha_{\text{gross}} - \delta_{\text{la}} - \delta_{\text{mc}} - \tau \cdot c_{\text{eff}} \Big) (1 - \lambda_{\text{decay}}) \] The candidate's empirical contribution survives committee examination if and only if \(\alpha_{\text{net}} > 0\) and the break-even transaction cost \(c_{\text{break}} = \frac{\alpha_{\text{gross}} - \delta_{\text{la}} - \delta_{\text{mc}}}{\tau}\) strictly exceeds institutional execution friction \(c_{\text{inst}}\).

Proof Total gross return is subject to additive accounting and universe hygiene reductions: \(\alpha_{\text{adj}} = \alpha_{\text{gross}} - \delta_{\text{la}} - \delta_{\text{mc}}\). Annual transaction drag is the product of annual portfolio turnover \(\tau\) and round-trip execution cost \(c_{\text{eff}}\): \(\operatorname{Drag} = \tau \cdot c_{\text{eff}}\). Subtracting execution drag yields pre-decay net alpha: \(\alpha_{\text{pre}} = \alpha_{\text{adj}} - \tau c_{\text{eff}}\). By McLean & Pontiff (2016), post-publication arbitrage capital induces structural attenuation by a multiplicative fraction \(\lambda_{\text{decay}}\), leaving surviving alpha \(\alpha_{\text{net}} = \alpha_{\text{pre}} (1 - \lambda_{\text{decay}})\). Setting \(\alpha_{\text{net}} = 0\) and solving for \(c_{\text{eff}}\) yields the break-even threshold: \[ \alpha_{\text{adj}} - \tau c_{\text{break}} = 0 \implies c_{\text{break}} = \frac{\alpha_{\text{gross}} - \delta_{\text{la}} - \delta_{\text{mc}}}{\tau} \] If \(c_{\text{break}} > c_{\text{inst}}\), the strategy generates strictly positive abnormal returns net of all institutional execution costs, completing the proof.

Remark 10.1 (Post-Publication Alpha Attenuation) Empirical asset pricing literature demonstrates that anomalies decay following publication. McLean & Pontiff (2016) document an average 58% out-of-sample alpha attenuation, driven by institutional arbitrage capital deployment. A robust thesis must demonstrate that economic risk premia remain economically viable even after factoring in 35% to 50% structural post-publication decay.

2. The Five Adversarial Committee Attack Vectors

During the oral defense, examination committees systematically deploy five standard attack vectors to test empirical resilience:

Example 10.1 (Look-Ahead Restatement Attack) A committee member notes that standard Compustat files record restated earnings rather than originally reported numbers. If an accounting anomaly uses restated earnings that were not available to investors on the portfolio formation date, the candidate must demonstrate point-in-time robustness using un-restated real-time Compustat snapshots, quantifying the look-ahead drag \(\delta_{\text{la}}\).

The five core attack angles evaluated in doctoral defenses are:

  1. Look-Ahead Restatement Bias: Probing whether corporate earnings or balance sheet values were restated retroactively.
  2. Microcap Leverage Contamination: Testing whether long-short returns are concentrated in the bottom 20% of NYSE market capitalization.
  3. Turnover Friction & Price Impact: Auditing whether high portfolio rebalancing turnover eliminates net abnormal returns after Novy-Marx & Velikov (2015) execution costs.
  4. Weak Identification & Instrumental Strength: In instrumental variable regressions, evaluating whether the first-stage \(F\)-statistic exceeds the Stock-Yogo critical value (\(F > 10\)).
  5. Post-Sample Structural Breaks: Testing parameter stability across major macroeconomic regime shifts (e.g. 2008 Lehman collapse, 2020 pandemic, 2022 inflation shock).

Lab 1 · Dissertation Defense Adversarial Attack Resilience Diagnostic

Simulating cumulative committee attack vectors—look-ahead restatements, microcap concentration, turnover friction, and out-of-sample decay—determines net economic alpha survival and defense pass viability.

"""Lab CAP4-L10-1: Dissertation Defense Adversarial Attack Resilience Diagnostic.

Evaluates oral defense committee examination readiness by simulating five hostile attack vectors:
look-ahead restatement bias, weak instrument inflation, microcap leverage contamination,
turnover transaction drag, and post-sample structural break decay.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    np.random.seed(42)

    # 1. Baseline reported thesis findings
    reported_alpha_annual = 0.0480  # 4.80% annual long-short alpha
    reported_t_stat = 3.65
    annual_turnover = 3.20  # 320% one-way turnover per year

    # 2. Defense Committee Attack Vector 1: Look-ahead bias in accounting restatements
    # Compustat point-in-time vs restated data removes ~35 bps
    look_ahead_penalty = 0.0035

    # 3. Defense Committee Attack Vector 2: Microcap concentration & liquidity
    # Excluding bottom 20% NYSE size percentile reduces spread by ~65 bps
    microcap_penalty = 0.0065

    # 4. Defense Committee Attack Vector 3: Transaction costs & price impact
    # Novy-Marx & Velikov (2015) effective trading costs: ~15 bps per turn
    effective_tcost_per_turn = 0.0015
    turnover_drag = annual_turnover * effective_tcost_per_turn  # 48 bps

    # 5. Defense Committee Attack Vector 4: Post-publication / Out-of-sample decay
    # McLean & Pontiff (2016) find ~50% decay out of sample
    # Stress-test under 35% exogenous structural decay
    oos_decay_rate = 0.35

    # 3. Compute Net Economic Alpha under Cumulative Hostile Committee Attacks
    pre_decay_net_alpha = reported_alpha_annual - look_ahead_penalty - microcap_penalty - turnover_drag
    stressed_net_alpha = pre_decay_net_alpha * (1.0 - oos_decay_rate)

    # 4. Break-even transaction cost (cost per turn where net alpha hits zero)
    # 0 = (reported_alpha - look_ahead - microcap) * (1 - decay) - turnover * tcost
    unhedged_margin = reported_alpha_annual - look_ahead_penalty - microcap_penalty
    breakeven_cost_bps = (unhedged_margin / annual_turnover) * 10000.0

    # 5. Committee Defense Resilience Index (0 to 100)
    # Scaled by surviving alpha and t-statistic resilience
    t_stat_stressed = reported_t_stat * (stressed_net_alpha / reported_alpha_annual)
    resilience_index = float(np.clip(100.0 * (stressed_net_alpha / 0.0200) * (t_stat_stressed / 2.0), 0.0, 100.0))
    defense_pass = bool(resilience_index >= 50.0 and stressed_net_alpha > 0.015)

    print(f"Reported Gross Alpha: {reported_alpha_annual * 100:.2f}% (t={reported_t_stat:.2f})")
    print(f"Look-Ahead Bias Drag: {look_ahead_penalty * 10000:.1f} bps")
    print(f"Microcap Exclusion Drag: {microcap_penalty * 10000:.1f} bps")
    print(f"Annual Turnover Drag: {turnover_drag * 10000:.1f} bps")
    print(f"Stressed Net Alpha: {stressed_net_alpha * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Break-Even Trading Cost: {breakeven_cost_bps:.1f} bps/turn")
    print(f"Stressed Defense t-Stat: {t_stat_stressed:.2f}")
    print(f"Defense Resilience Index: {resilience_index:.1f}")
    print(f"Dissertation Defense Passed: {defense_pass}")


main()

The candidate presents a reported gross alpha of 4.80% (\(t = 3.65\)) with 320% annual turnover. Subjecting the strategy to 35 bps look-ahead drag, 65 bps microcap penalty, and 48 bps turnover drag leaves 3.32% pre-decay alpha. Incorporating a 35% out-of-sample decay rate yields a stressed net alpha of 2.16% (\(t = 1.64\)) and a break-even trading cost of 118.8 bps per turn. Where the canon breaks

Oral defenses collapse when candidates fail to anticipate predictable methodological challenges:

  • Defensive Denial: Denying known econometric limitations rather than acknowledging them and presenting pre-computed sensitivity grids damages candidate credibility.
  • Inability to Reproduce Live Numbers: Failing to explain how an entry in Table 3 was computed from the underlying data files reveals a lack of pipeline command.
  • Conflating Statistical Significance with Economic Mechanism: Asserting that a \(t\)-statistic of 3.2 constitutes economic proof without articulating who is on the other side of the trade and why arbitrageurs fail to eliminate the premium.

Misconceptions

  1. The defense committee expects the candidate to prove their model is 100% true in all states of the world.

    All economic models are approximations. Faculty committees evaluate whether the candidate understands the exact domain where the model functions, the institutional frictions that generate the risk premium, and the specific failure modes where the model breaks down.

  2. A successful defense requires answering every question instantly without hesitation.

    Thoughtful candidates pause, verify the premise of the question, and articulate the econometric principles involved. Rushing into an ill-considered response often creates unforced contradictions.

  3. Transaction costs can be ignored in a doctoral thesis because academic research focuses only on frictionless equilibrium pricing.

    Top finance faculties reject frictionless anomalies if trading costs exceed gross returns. If an anomaly requires 1200% annual turnover, a gross alpha of 4% represents zero net economic profit, rendering the finding an artifact of execution friction.

Problem set

Mechanics problems

A doctoral thesis reports a factor portfolio with gross annualized alpha \(\alpha_{\text{gross}} = 0.0520\) (5.20%) and annual turnover \(\tau = 4.0\) (400% per year). Execution costs are \(c_{\text{eff}} = 0.0020\) (20 bps per turn). Calculate the annual turnover drag and net alpha before post-publication decay.

Solution

Turnover drag: \[ \operatorname{Drag} = \tau \times c_{\text{eff}} = 4.0 \times 0.0020 = 0.0080 = 80 \text{ bps} \] Net alpha before decay: \[ \alpha_{\text{net}} = \alpha_{\text{gross}} - \operatorname{Drag} = 0.0520 - 0.0080 = 0.0440 = 4.40\% \] The strategy retains 4.40% net alpha after paying execution costs.

A reported gross spread of 6.00% is subject to an 80 bps look-ahead bias adjustment and a 120 bps microcap penalty. If annual turnover is 3.50 (350%), calculate the break-even transaction cost in basis points per turn where net alpha reaches exactly zero.

Solution

Adjusted gross spread: \[ \alpha_{\text{adj}} = 0.0600 - 0.0080 - 0.0120 = 0.0400 = 4.00\% \] Break-even cost: \[ c_{\text{break}} = \frac{\alpha_{\text{adj}}}{\tau} = \frac{0.0400}{3.50} \approx 0.011428 = 114.3 \text{ bps/turn} \] The strategy remains profitable as long as one-way execution costs do not exceed 114.3 basis points.

Under the McLean & Pontiff (2016) post-publication decay benchmark of 58%, calculate the expected post-publication alpha of an anomaly that generated 3.60% annualized alpha in the published in-sample period.

Solution

Expected post-publication alpha: \[ \alpha_{\text{post}} = \alpha_{\text{in-sample}} \times (1 - \lambda_{\text{decay}}) = 0.0360 \times (1 - 0.58) = 0.0360 \times 0.42 = 0.01512 = 1.512\% \] The expected post-publication alpha is approximately 1.51% per year.

Proof problems

Prove that in a two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression with endogenous regressor \(X\) and instrument \(Z\), the asymptotic bias of the 2SLS estimator relative to OLS satisfies \(\frac{\operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} - \beta}{\operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Z, \epsilon)}{\operatorname{Cov}(X, \epsilon)} \cdot \frac{1}{\pi}\) where \(\pi\) is the first-stage coefficient \(X = \pi Z + v\), proving that weak instruments (\(\pi \approx 0\)) amplify second-stage bias to infinity when \(Z\) is even slightly endogenous.

Solution

In the structural model \(y = \beta X + \epsilon\), the OLS estimator has asymptotic probability limit: \[ \operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = \beta + \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, \epsilon)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)} \] The 2SLS estimator using instrument \(Z\) has probability limit: \[ \operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} = \beta + \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Z, \epsilon)}{\operatorname{Cov}(Z, X)} \] From the first-stage projection \(X = \pi Z + v\), we have \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z, X) = \pi \operatorname{Var}(Z)\).
Therefore: \[ \operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} - \beta = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Z, \epsilon)}{\pi \operatorname{Var}(Z)} \] Comparing the two biases: \[ \frac{\operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} - \beta}{\operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Z, \epsilon)}{\operatorname{Cov}(X, \epsilon)} \cdot \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\pi \operatorname{Var}(Z)} \] As the instrument becomes weak (\(\pi \to 0\)), the denominator approaches 0. If \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z, \epsilon) \neq 0\) (even an imperceptibly small violation of instrument exogeneity), the 2SLS bias approaches infinity, far exceeding the original OLS bias. This completes the proof and formalizes the Stock-Yogo weak instrument defense.

Prove that in a cross-sectional Fama-MacBeth regression, omitting an unobserved priced common factor \(f_2\) whose loadings \(\beta_{i2}\) correlate with included factor loadings \(\beta_{i1}\) introduces systematic omitted variable bias into the estimated risk premium \(\hat{\lambda}_1\).

Solution

Let true expected returns satisfy \(\mathbb{E}[R_i] = \lambda_0 + \lambda_1 \beta_{i1} + \lambda_2 \beta_{i2}\).
If the econometrician estimates the misspecified model \(\bar{R}_i = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \beta_{i1} + u_i\), the OLS cross-sectional slope estimator is: \[ \hat{\gamma}_1 = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\bar{R}_i, \beta_{i1})}{\operatorname{Var}(\beta_{i1})} \] Substituting the true model: \[ \operatorname{Cov}(\bar{R}_i, \beta_{i1}) = \operatorname{Cov}(\lambda_0 + \lambda_1 \beta_{i1} + \lambda_2 \beta_{i2} + e_i, \beta_{i1}) = \lambda_1 \operatorname{Var}(\beta_{i1}) + \lambda_2 \operatorname{Cov}(\beta_{i1}, \beta_{i2}) \] Dividing by \(\operatorname{Var}(\beta_{i1})\): \[ \operatorname{plim} \hat{\gamma}_1 = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\beta_{i1}, \beta_{i2})}{\operatorname{Var}(\beta_{i1})} \] If the omitted factor is priced (\(\lambda_2 \neq 0\)) and its loadings correlate across assets with the included factor loadings (\(\operatorname{Cov}(\beta_{i1}, \beta_{i2}) \neq 0\)), the estimated premium \(\hat{\gamma}_1\) is asymptotically biased away from \(\lambda_1\). This completes the proof.

Prove that the Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS) statistic reduces to the Hotelling \(T^2\) statistic when the factor portfolio has zero mean return (\(\bar{f} = \mathbf{0}\)).

Solution

The GRS test statistic is given by: \[ GRS = \frac{T - N - K}{N} \left( \frac{\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha}}{1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}} \right) \] When \(\bar{f} = \mathbf{0}\), the quadratic form in the denominator satisfies \(\bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f} = 0\), so the denominator simplifies to \(1 + 0 = 1\).
The statistic becomes: \[ GRS = \frac{T - N - K}{N} \hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha} \] Hotelling's \(T^2\) statistic testing \(H_0: \boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{0}\) for an \(N\)-dimensional sample mean with sample covariance \(\hat{\Sigma}\) is \(T^2 = T \bar{x}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \bar{x}\). Under normal theory, the \(F\)-transformation of Hotelling's \(T^2\) is: \[ F = \frac{T - N}{N(T - 1)} T^2 = \frac{T - N}{N} \bar{x}^\top \left( \frac{T - 1}{T} \hat{\Sigma} \right)^{-1} \bar{x} \] which corresponds exactly to the degrees-of-freedom scaled quadratic form of the GRS test with \(K = 0\). This completes the proof.

Empirical & practical problems

A committee member asks: "How do you know your factor is not simply proxying for high idiosyncratic volatility or lottery-like demand?" Formulate the empirical test battery and table design to refute this challenge.

Solution

Empirical Strategy:
1. Independent Double Sorts: Construct a \(5 \times 5\) independently sorted portfolio matrix crossing the candidate factor against idiosyncratic volatility (computed relative to the Fama-French 5-factor model plus momentum).
2. Spread Portfolio Persistence: Report the candidate factor spread portfolio return within each idiosyncratic volatility quintile. If the spread remains economically large (e.g. >35 bps/month) and statistically significant across all volatility quintiles, the factor cannot be an artifact of idiosyncratic volatility.
3. Fama-MacBeth Panel Regressions: Run monthly cross-sectional regressions controlling for IVOL, MAX (maximum daily return in month \(t-1\)), skewness, and illiquidity (Amihud), reporting robust \(t\)-statistics.

Explain the practical procedure for conducting an oral defense rehearsal ("dry run") with senior doctoral peers, detailing role assignments and adversarial timing protocols.

Solution

Dry Run Protocol:
1. Persona Assignment: Assign senior peers specific faculty personas:

  • The Econometric Skeptic: Interrogates clustering, weak instruments, and endogeneity.
  • The Institutional Realist: Attacks liquidity, microcaps, transaction costs, and short-sale borrow fees.
  • The Theory Purist: Demands general equilibrium derivation and agent utility microfoundations.

2. Strict Interruption Timing: Permit uninterrupted presentation for the first 15 minutes, followed by 45 minutes of live interruptions whenever assertions are made without citing table numbers.
3. Post-Rehearsal Gap Audit: Catalog all unanswerable questions into a "defense punch-list" requiring dedicated appendix backup slides and updated empirical tables before the real defense.

Design an appendix slide structure for an oral defense presentation that allows instantaneous navigation to 20+ backup robustness tables without disrupting presentation flow.

Solution

Navigation Architecture:
1. Hyperlinked Appendix Index: Immediately following the conclusion slide, create an "Appendix Roadmap" slide with hyperlinked categories:

  • [Data Hygiene & Filters] (Slides A1–A5)
  • [Alternative Risk Models & GRS] (Slides A6–A10)
  • [Subperiod & Crisis Robustness] (Slides A11–A15)
  • [Transaction Cost Net Returns] (Slides A16–A20)

2. Slide Footer Return Links: Every appendix slide contains a discrete bottom-right "Return to Main" button linking back to the slide where the question originated.
3. Direct Answer Tables: Each backup slide begins with a bold 1-sentence verdict answering the expected question, followed by the complete LaTeX table.

Research & defense problems

During your defense, the external committee chair asks: "If your anomaly is real and generates 4% net alpha, why hasn't Citadel or Millennium already traded it away?" Provide the theoretical and empirical defense based on limits to arbitrage.

Solution

Defense: "Our empirical findings are completely consistent with equilibrium limits to arbitrage (Shleifer & Vishny 1997). First, the long-short factor requires holding short positions in small- and mid-cap equities that incur non-trivial borrow fees and recall risk during market downturns. Second, hedge funds operate with finite capital horizons and strict stop-loss risk limits; our anomaly exhibits idiosyncratic tracking error relative to standard benchmarks, exposing arbitrageurs to fundamental noise trader risk that cannot be perfectly hedged. Third, as documented in our Table 7, the premium surges precisely when intermediary balance sheet constraints bind (measured by the He-Kelly-Manela capital ratio). Thus, the 4% net return represents an equilibrium risk compensation earned by patient capital for absorbing constraint risk, rather than a free unexploited lunch."

How does Chen & Velikov (2019) or Chen (2019) establish that publication bias selectively inflates reported empirical anomalies across the finance literature?

Solution

Chen (2019) utilizes meta-analysis and machine learning techniques to evaluate the distribution of reported \(t\)-statistics across thousands of published asset pricing regressions. They find a sharp discontinuity in the empirical density of \(t\)-statistics at \(t = 1.96\), with a conspicuous deficit of reported statistics between 1.50 and 1.95 and a large spike immediately above 1.96. This caliper discontinuity confirms publication selection bias: academic journals systematically reject insignificant results, incentivizing researchers to modify specifications until \(t \ge 1.96\) is achieved. This evidence underscores why doctoral defense committees require pre-registration and out-of-sample validation."

Synthesize the formal criteria used by doctoral examination committees to distinguish between a dissertation requiring "Minor Revisions" versus "Major Revisions".

Solution

Criteria Matrix:
1. Minor Revisions:

  • Core econometric identification and theoretical proofs are validated and accepted by all committee members.
  • Empirical results are accepted as robust and reproducible.
  • Requested changes are limited to expositional clarification, expanding literature discussions, adding supplementary appendix robustness tables, or correcting typographical and formatting errors.
  • Revisions are approved directly by the dissertation chair without reconvening the full committee.

2. Major Revisions:
  • A core identification assumption is disputed (e.g. severe endogeneity, invalid instruments, or look-ahead contamination).
  • Primary empirical results collapse under standard robustness checks (e.g. NYSE breakpoints or crisis trimming).
  • The candidate must execute substantive new econometric estimations, reconstruct datasets, or reformulate the theoretical model.
  • The full committee must reconvene or formally re-review the revised manuscript before granting doctoral conferral.

    Options

    The defense is an adversarial examination where faculty evaluate the scholarly independence, originality, and methodological rigor of the dissertation.

  2. According to McLean & Pontiff (2016), approximately what percentage of stock return predictability decays after academic publication?

    Options

    McLean & Pontiff (2016) find an average 58% decline in anomaly returns post-publication, reflecting the impact of arbitrage trading.

  3. What is the standard Staiger-Stock rule-of-thumb first-stage F-statistic threshold below which instruments are classified as weak?

    Staiger and Stock (1997) establish that a first-stage \(F\)-statistic below 10.0 indicates weak instrument vulnerabilities.

  4. Why must a doctoral candidate evaluate portfolio turnover and execution drag when proposing a novel asset pricing factor?
