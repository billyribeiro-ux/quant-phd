Exchange Options and the Margrabe Formula

  • Uses: change of numeraire, Girsanov's theorem, geometric Brownian motion, Itô's lemma, Brownian motion, quadratic variation
  • Introduces: exchange-option
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled

Many financial transactions involve exchanging one risky asset for another: stock-for-stock corporate takeovers, crack spreads between crude oil and refined fuels, cross-currency spread options, and outperformance incentives. Pricing an exchange option under the traditional cash numeraire requires evaluating a two-dimensional correlated lognormal integral. By selecting the delivered asset as numeraire, the dimension of the problem collapses from two to one, yielding Margrabe's elegant closed-form formula independent of the risk-free rate.

1The Exchange Option Contract

Definition 1.1 (Exchange Option)

An exchange option (or Margrabe option) confers on its holder the right, but not the obligation, to surrender one unit of asset 2 in exchange for one unit of asset 1 at expiration maturity \(H\). Its payoff at time \(H\) is:

\[ V_H = \max(S_1(H) - S_2(H), 0) = (S_1(H) - S_2(H))^+. \]

Suppose both assets follow correlated geometric Brownian motions with continuous dividend yields \(q_1, q_2 \ge 0\) under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\):

\[ \frac{dS_1(t)}{S_1(t)} = (r - q_1) dt + \sigma_1 dW_1(t), \quad \frac{dS_2(t)}{S_2(t)} = (r - q_2) dt + \sigma_2 dW_2(t), \]

where \(W_1, W_2\) are standard Brownian motions with constant correlation \(d\langle W_1, W_2 \rangle_t = \rho dt\) with \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\), and \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2 > 0\).

2Derivation via Asset 2 Numeraire

Because asset 2 pays continuous dividend yield \(q_2\), the total return process formed by continuously reinvesting dividends is \(N_t \coloneqq S_2(t) e^{q_2 t}\). Because \(S_2(t) > 0\) almost surely, \(N_t\) is a valid numeraire.

Theorem 1.2 (Relative Price Dynamics under Asset 2 Numeraire)

Assumptions: (A1) \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) follow correlated geometric Brownian motions; (A2) \(N_t = S_2(t) e^{q_2 t}\) is chosen as numeraire, defining measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}\).

Statement: The relative price of the dividend-reinvested first asset:

\[ Z_t \coloneqq \frac{S_1(t) e^{q_1 t}}{S_2(t) e^{q_2 t}} \]

is a geometric Brownian motion martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}\) with zero drift and effective volatility:

\[ \sigma_M \coloneqq \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2}, \]

satisfying the SDE:

\[ \frac{dZ_t}{Z_t} = \sigma_M dW_t^{S_2}, \]

where \(W_t^{S_2}\) is a standard Brownian motion under \(\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}\).

Proof

By the change of numeraire theorem, because \(S_1(t) e^{q_1 t}\) is a tradable asset and \(N_t = S_2(t) e^{q_2 t}\) is the numeraire, the relative price \(Z_t\) must be an \((\mathcal{F}_t, \mathbb{Q}^{S_2})\)-martingale, having zero drift.

To determine its volatility, compute the quadratic variation of \(\log Z_t\):

\[ \log Z_t = \log S_1(t) - \log S_2(t) + (q_1 - q_2) t. \]

By Itô's lemma, the diffusion increments are:

\[ d\log Z_t = \dots dt + \sigma_1 dW_1(t) - \sigma_2 dW_2(t). \]

The rate of quadratic variation accumulation is:

\[ \frac{d\langle \log Z, \log Z \rangle_t}{dt} = (\sigma_1)^2 - 2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \frac{d\langle W_1, W_2 \rangle_t}{dt} + (\sigma_2)^2 = \sigma_1^2 - 2\rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2 = \sigma_M^2. \]

Because quadratic variation is invariant under equivalent measure change, the diffusion coefficient of \(Z_t\) under \(\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}\) is identically \(\sigma_M\). Since \(Z_t\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}\)-martingale, its SDE is \(dZ_t = \sigma_M Z_t dW_t^{S_2}\).

Theorem 1.3 (Margrabe's Formula)

Assumptions: (A1) \(S_1, S_2\) follow correlated geometric Brownian motions; (A2) Dividend yields \(q_1, q_2 \ge 0\) and correlation \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\) are constant.

Statement: The arbitrage-free value of the exchange option at \(t = 0\) is given by Margrabe's (1978) formula:

\[ V_0 = S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H} \Phi(d_1) - S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H} \Phi(d_2), \]

where

\[ d_1 \coloneqq \frac{\log\left(\frac{S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H}}{S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H}}\right) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_M^2 H}{\sigma_M \sqrt{H}}, \quad d_2 \coloneqq d_1 - \sigma_M \sqrt{H}. \]
Proof

Express the terminal payoff in terms of numeraire \(N_H = S_2(H) e^{q_2 H}\):

\[ V_H = (S_1(H) - S_2(H))^+ = e^{-q_2 H} S_2(H) e^{q_2 H} \left( \frac{S_1(H)}{S_2(H)} - 1 \right)^+ = e^{-q_2 H} N_H \left( Z_H e^{-(q_1 - q_2)H} - 1 \right)^+. \]

Let \(Y_H \coloneqq Z_H e^{-(q_1 - q_2)H} = S_1(H) / S_2(H)\). Under the generalized valuation equation (Theorem 1.3 in Lesson 4) with numeraire \(N\):

\[ V_0 = N_0 \E^{\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}}\left[ \frac{V_H}{N_H} \right] = S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H} \E^{\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}}[(Y_H - 1)^+]. \]

Notice that \(Y_H = Z_H e^{-(q_1 - q_2)H}\), and \(Z_H = Z_0 \exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_M^2 H + \sigma_M W_H^{S_2})\). Therefore \(Y_H\) is lognormally distributed with initial forward ratio:

\[ Y_0 = Z_0 e^{-(q_1 - q_2)H} = \frac{S_1(0) e^{q_1 \cdot 0}}{S_2(0) e^{q_2 \cdot 0}} e^{-(q_1 - q_2)H} = \frac{S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H}}{S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H}} = \frac{F_1}{F_2}. \]

Evaluating the expectation of a lognormal variable with strike \(K = 1\) yields the standard Black formula: \(\E^{\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}}[(Y_H - 1)^+] = Y_0 \Phi(d_1) - 1 \cdot \Phi(d_2)\). Multiplying by \(S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H}\) gives:

\[ V_0 = S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H} \left( \frac{S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H}}{S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H}} \Phi(d_1) - \Phi(d_2) \right) = S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H} \Phi(d_1) - S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H} \Phi(d_2). \]
Remark 1.4 (Independence from the Risk-Free Rate)

When neither asset pays dividends (\(q_1 = q_2 = 0\)), Margrabe's formula reduces to:

\[ V_0 = S_1(0) \Phi(d_1) - S_2(0) \Phi(d_2), \quad d_1 = \frac{\log(S_1(0) / S_2(0)) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_M^2 H}{\sigma_M \sqrt{H}}. \]

The risk-free interest rate \(r\) completely disappears from the formula. Because one risky asset acts as the numeraire and the other as the underlying, no cash account is required to replicate the option.

3Structural Invariants: Homogeneity and Parity

Theorem 1.5 (Homogeneity of Degree 1)

Assumptions: (A1) \(V(S_1, S_2)\) denotes the Margrabe exchange option price; (A2) \(c > 0\) is any positive scaling constant.

Statement: The pricing function is linearly homogeneous of degree 1:

\[ V(c S_1, c S_2) = c V(S_1, S_2). \]
Proof

In Theorem 1.3, the argument \(d_1\) depends on prices solely through the ratio \(S_1 / S_2\): \(\log((c S_1 e^{-q_1 H}) / (c S_2 e^{-q_2 H})) = \log(S_1 e^{-q_1 H} / S_2 e^{-q_2 H})\). Hence \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) are completely invariant under scalar multiplication. Multiplying \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) by \(c\) multiplies the entire formula by \(c\):

\[ V(c S_1, c S_2) = (c S_1) e^{-q_1 H} \Phi(d_1) - (c S_2) e^{-q_2 H} \Phi(d_2) = c [S_1 e^{-q_1 H}\Phi(d_1) - S_2 e^{-q_2 H}\Phi(d_2)] = c V(S_1, S_2). \]
Theorem 1.6 (Put-Call Exchange Parity / Symmetry)

Assumptions: (A1) \(\text{Call}(S_1, S_2)\) is the option to exchange \(S_2\) for \(S_1\); (A2) \(\text{Call}(S_2, S_1)\) is the option to exchange \(S_1\) for \(S_2\).

Statement: The two exchange options satisfy the exact no-arbitrage parity relation:

\[ \text{Call}(S_1, S_2) - \text{Call}(S_2, S_1) = S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H} - S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H}. \]
Proof

At expiration \(H\), using the algebraic identity \(\max(x - y, 0) - \max(y - x, 0) = x - y\):

\[ (S_1(H) - S_2(H))^+ - (S_2(H) - S_1(H))^+ = S_1(H) - S_2(H). \]

Discounting by \(e^{-rH}\) and taking expectations under \(\mathbb{Q}\):

\[ \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH}(S_1(H) - S_2(H))^+] - \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH}(S_2(H) - S_1(H))^+] = \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH}S_1(H)] - \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH}S_2(H)]. \]

Because discounted reinvested assets are martingales, \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH}S_i(H)] = S_i(0) e^{-q_i H}\). This establishes the parity identity.

Lab 1 · Margrabe exchange option formula, homogeneity, and exchange parity

An exchange option gives the holder the right to exchange asset 2 for asset 1, payoff max(S_1(T) - S_2(T), 0). By Margrabe (1978), using asset 2 as numeraire produces a closed form independent of the risk-free rate r: V_0 = S_1(0) * exp(-q_1 * T) * Phi(d_1) - S_2(0) * exp(-q_2 * T) * Phi(d_2) where sigma_M = sqrt(sigma_1^2 - 2 * rho * sigma_1 * sigma_2 + sigma_2^2) is the relative volatility. This lab verifies the Margrabe closed form against 2D Monte Carlo (20,000 paths, SIMULATED), and checks two exact mathematical invariants: 1. Homogeneity of degree 1: V(c * S_1, c * S_2) = c * V(S_1, S_2) for any c > 0. 2. Put-call exchange parity: Call(S_1, S_2) - Call(S_2, S_1) = S_1 * exp(-q_1 * T) - S_2 * exp(-q_2 * T).

"""Lab 1 · Margrabe exchange option formula, homogeneity, and exchange parity.

An exchange option gives the holder the right to exchange asset 2 for asset 1, payoff max(S_1(T) - S_2(T), 0).
By Margrabe (1978), using asset 2 as numeraire produces a closed form independent of the risk-free rate r:
    V_0 = S_1(0) * exp(-q_1 * T) * Phi(d_1) - S_2(0) * exp(-q_2 * T) * Phi(d_2)
where sigma_M = sqrt(sigma_1^2 - 2 * rho * sigma_1 * sigma_2 + sigma_2^2) is the relative volatility.
This lab verifies the Margrabe closed form against 2D Monte Carlo (20,000 paths, SIMULATED),
and checks two exact mathematical invariants:
1. Homogeneity of degree 1: V(c * S_1, c * S_2) = c * V(S_1, S_2) for any c > 0.
2. Put-call exchange parity: Call(S_1, S_2) - Call(S_2, S_1) = S_1 * exp(-q_1 * T) - S_2 * exp(-q_2 * T).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def margrabe_price(
    s1: float,
    s2: float,
    sig1: float,
    sig2: float,
    rho: float,
    q1: float,
    q2: float,
    t: float,
) -> float:
    sig_m = math.sqrt(max(sig1**2 - 2.0 * rho * sig1 * sig2 + sig2**2, 0.0))
    if sig_m <= 1e-12 or t <= 1e-12:
        f1_val = s1 * math.exp(-q1 * t)
        f2_val = s2 * math.exp(-q2 * t)
        return max(f1_val - f2_val, 0.0)

    f1 = s1 * math.exp(-q1 * t)
    f2 = s2 * math.exp(-q2 * t)
    sq_t = math.sqrt(t)
    d1 = (math.log(f1 / f2) + 0.5 * (sig_m**2) * t) / (sig_m * sq_t)
    d2 = d1 - sig_m * sq_t
    return f1 * norm_cdf(d1) - f2 * norm_cdf(d2)


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    s1 = 100.0
    s2 = 90.0
    sig1 = 0.25
    sig2 = 0.20
    rho = 0.40
    q1 = 0.02
    q2 = 0.01
    r = 0.04
    t = 1.0
    n_paths = 20000

    exact_price = margrabe_price(s1, s2, sig1, sig2, rho, q1, q2, t)

    # 2D Monte Carlo under risk-neutral measure Q
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    sq_t = math.sqrt(t)
    drift1 = (r - q1 - 0.5 * (sig1**2)) * t
    vol1 = sig1 * sq_t
    drift2 = (r - q2 - 0.5 * (sig2**2)) * t
    vol2 = sig2 * sq_t
    df = math.exp(-r * t)

    payoffs: list[float] = []
    for _ in range(n_paths):
        z1 = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        z2 = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        w1 = z1
        w2 = rho * z1 + math.sqrt(max(1.0 - rho**2, 0.0)) * z2

        s1_t = s1 * math.exp(drift1 + vol1 * w1)
        s2_t = s2 * math.exp(drift2 + vol2 * w2)
        payoffs.append(df * max(s1_t - s2_t, 0.0))

    mc_price = sum(payoffs) / n_paths
    var_mc = sum((x - mc_price) ** 2 for x in payoffs) / (n_paths - 1)
    se_mc = math.sqrt(var_mc / n_paths)

    # Invariant 1: Homogeneity of degree 1
    scale = 2.5
    scaled_price = margrabe_price(scale * s1, scale * s2, sig1, sig2, rho, q1, q2, t)
    homogeneity_error = abs(scaled_price - scale * exact_price)

    # Invariant 2: Put-call exchange parity
    # Call(S1, S2) - Call(S2, S1) = F1 - F2
    reverse_price = margrabe_price(s2, s1, sig2, sig1, rho, q2, q1, t)
    f1 = s1 * math.exp(-q1 * t)
    f2 = s2 * math.exp(-q2 * t)
    parity_error = abs((exact_price - reverse_price) - (f1 - f2))

    print(f"analytical Margrabe price: {exact_price:.4f}")
    print(f"2D Monte Carlo price: {mc_price:.4f} (se {se_mc:.4f})")
    print(f"homogeneity error at scale {scale}: {homogeneity_error:.2e}")
    print(f"put-call exchange parity error: {parity_error:.2e}")
    print("MC inside 2*se band:", abs(mc_price - exact_price) <= 2.0 * se_mc)
    print("homogeneity holds to 1e-12:", homogeneity_error < 1e-12)
    print("exchange parity holds to 1e-12:", parity_error < 1e-12)

4Where the canon breaks

Margrabe's formula assumes that the relative price process \(Z_t = S_1(t) / S_2(t)\) is a geometric Brownian motion with continuous diffusion. If either asset is subject to discontinuous jumps (such as credit defaults, merger announcements, or sudden currency devaluations), the relative price \(Z_t\) exhibits jump discontinuities. In the presence of jumps, the quadratic covariation contains discrete product terms \(\Delta S_1 \Delta S_2\), the market becomes incomplete, and Margrabe's closed form fails, requiring jump-diffusion or PIDE pricing.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Margrabe's formula requires an explicit strike price \(K\).”

    In an exchange option, asset 2 plays the role of a floating strike price. The contract delivers \((S_1 - S_2)^+\), so the strike is not a fixed dollar amount but the uncertain terminal price of another tradable security.

  2. “Higher correlation between the two assets increases the value of an exchange option.”

    Higher correlation strictly decreases the exchange option price. The relative volatility is \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2}\). Because \(\partial \sigma_M / \partial \rho = -\sigma_1\sigma_2 / \sigma_M < 0\), increasing correlation makes the two assets move together, reducing the likelihood that \(S_1\) significantly outperforms \(S_2\).

  3. “When neither asset pays dividends, the exchange option price depends on the risk-free interest rate.”

    When \(q_1 = q_2 = 0\), Margrabe's formula is completely independent of the risk-free rate \(r\). Cash is not used as numeraire, and no borrowing or lending in the money-market account is required to replicate the payoff.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Margrabe volatility calculation

Compute the effective Margrabe volatility \(\sigma_M\) for two assets with \(\sigma_1 = 0.30\), \(\sigma_2 = 0.20\), and correlation \(\rho \in \{-0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 1.0\}\).

Solution

Formula: \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2} = \sqrt{0.09 - 2\rho(0.06) + 0.04} = \sqrt{0.13 - 0.12\rho}\). For \(\rho = -0.5\): \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{0.13 + 0.06} = \sqrt{0.19} \approx 0.4359\). For \(\rho = 0.0\): \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{0.13} \approx 0.3606\). For \(\rho = 0.5\): \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{0.13 - 0.06} = \sqrt{0.07} \approx 0.2646\). For \(\rho = 1.0\): \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{0.13 - 0.12} = \sqrt{0.01} = 0.1000\). Notice that \(\sigma_M\) decreases monotonically as \(\rho\) increases.

M2 · Perfect correlation collapse

Suppose two assets have identical volatilities \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 0.20\) and perfect positive correlation \(\rho = 1.0\) with zero dividend yields. If \(S_1(0) = 110\) and \(S_2(0) = 100\), compute the exchange option price.

Solution

The effective volatility is \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - 2(1.0)\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_1^2} = 0\). The relative price \(Z_t = S_1(t) / S_2(t)\) has zero volatility, meaning \(Z_t = Z_0 = 110 / 100 = 1.10\) deterministically. The terminal payoff is \((S_1(H) - S_2(H))^+ = S_2(H)(1.10 - 1.0) = 0.10 S_2(H)\). Its current price is \(0.10 S_2(0) = 0.10(100) = 10\). Equivalently, \(S_1(0) - S_2(0) = 110 - 100 = 10\).

M3 · Margrabe price evaluation

Evaluate Margrabe's formula for \(S_1 = 50, S_2 = 50, \sigma_1 = 0.20, \sigma_2 = 0.20, \rho = 0.0, q_1 = q_2 = 0, H = 1.0\).

Solution

\(\sigma_M = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.04} = \sqrt{0.08} \approx 0.2828\). Since \(S_1 = S_2\), \(\log(S_1 / S_2) = 0\). Then \(d_1 = \frac{0.5 \sigma_M^2}{\sigma_M} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma_M = 0.1414\). And \(d_2 = -0.1414\). \(\Phi(0.1414) \approx 0.5562\) and \(\Phi(-0.1414) = 1 - 0.5562 = 0.4438\). The price is \(V_0 = 50(0.5562) - 50(0.4438) = 50(0.5562 - 0.4438) = 50(0.1124) = 5.62\).

Proof

P1 · Standard Black-Scholes as a Margrabe special case

Prove that when \(S_2(t) = K e^{-r(H-t)}\) is a deterministic cash bond, Margrabe's formula reduces identically to the standard Black-Scholes European call formula.

Solution

Let \(S_2(0) = K e^{-rH}\), \(q_2 = 0\), and \(\sigma_2 = 0\). Then \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - 0 + 0} = \sigma_1\). The forward ratio is \(F_1 / F_2 = (S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H}) / (K e^{-rH}) = \frac{S_1(0)}{K} e^{(r - q_1)H}\). Substituting into \(d_1\): \(d_1 = \frac{\log(S_1(0)/K) + (r - q_1 + \sigma_1^2/2)H}{\sigma_1 \sqrt{H}}\). The formula becomes: \(V_0 = S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H}\Phi(d_1) - (K e^{-rH})\Phi(d_2)\), which is the Merton (1973) dividend-adjusted Black-Scholes call formula.

P2 · Negative correlation sensitivity derivative

Prove that the partial derivative of the Margrabe option price with respect to correlation \(\rho\) is strictly negative: \(\frac{\partial V_0}{\partial \rho} = -S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H} n(d_1) \frac{\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \sqrt{H}}{\sigma_M} < 0\).

Solution

By the chain rule: \(\frac{\partial V_0}{\partial \rho} = \frac{\partial V_0}{\partial \sigma_M} \frac{\partial \sigma_M}{\partial \rho}\). Because \(V_0\) is formally a Black call option on \(F_1\) with strike \(F_2\) and volatility \(\sigma_M\), its Vega is \(\frac{\partial V_0}{\partial \sigma_M} = F_1 \sqrt{H} n(d_1) = S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H}\sqrt{H} n(d_1) > 0\). Differentiating \(\sigma_M = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2}\) with respect to \(\rho\): \(\frac{\partial \sigma_M}{\partial \rho} = \frac{-2\sigma_1\sigma_2}{2\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 + \sigma_2^2}} = -\frac{\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_M}\). Multiplying the two terms gives \(\frac{\partial V_0}{\partial \rho} = -S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H} n(d_1) \frac{\sigma_1\sigma_2 \sqrt{H}}{\sigma_M} < 0\).

P3 · Euler's theorem on homogeneous pricing functions

By Theorem 1.5, \(V(S_1, S_2)\) is homogeneous of degree 1. Prove using Euler's homogeneous function theorem that \(V = S_1 \frac{\partial V}{\partial S_1} + S_2 \frac{\partial V}{\partial S_2}\), and evaluate \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S_1}\) and \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S_2}\).

Solution

Euler's homogeneous function theorem states that if \(f(c x, c y) = c^k f(x, y)\), then \(x \partial_x f + y \partial_y f = k f(x, y)\). Here \(k = 1\), so \(S_1 \partial_{S_1} V + S_2 \partial_{S_2} V = V\). Differentiating Margrabe's formula: \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S_1} = e^{-q_1 H} \Phi(d_1) + S_1 e^{-q_1 H} n(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S_1} - S_2 e^{-q_2 H} n(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial S_1}\). By the standard Black-Scholes identity, \(S_1 e^{-q_1 H} n(d_1) = S_2 e^{-q_2 H} n(d_2)\), so the derivative terms cancel, leaving \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S_1} = e^{-q_1 H}\Phi(d_1)\). Similarly, \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S_2} = -e^{-q_2 H}\Phi(d_2)\). Thus \(S_1(e^{-q_1 H}\Phi(d_1)) + S_2(-e^{-q_2 H}\Phi(d_2)) = V\).

Empirical

E1 · Equity pair takeover arbitrage

In a stock-for-stock acquisition, Company A offers 0.8 shares of A for each share of Company B. What is the equivalent Margrabe option representation for a hedge fund holding a merger arbitrage position?

Solution

The takeover value of B's share is \(0.8 S_A\). An investor holding share B who can accept the offer has payoff \(\max(0.8 S_A, S_B) = S_B + \max(0.8 S_A - S_B, 0)\). This is a long position in stock B plus an exchange option to swap \(S_B\) for \(0.8 S_A\). Here \(S_1 = 0.8 S_A\) and \(S_2 = S_B\). Because merger announcements induce high correlation between \(S_A\) and \(S_B\) (\(\rho \approx 0.90\)), the effective volatility \(\sigma_M\) is small, reflecting narrow spread risk.

E2 · Crack spread option volatility

An energy trader prices an option on the spread between heating oil (\(\sigma_1 = 0.35\)) and crude oil (\(\sigma_2 = 0.30\)) with historical correlation \(\rho = 0.80\). Compute the effective spread volatility.

Solution

\(\sigma_M = \sqrt{(0.35)^2 - 2(0.80)(0.35)(0.30) + (0.30)^2} = \sqrt{0.1225 - 0.1680 + 0.0900} = \sqrt{0.0445} \approx 0.2109\) (21.09%). Even though both individual commodities have high volatilities (35% and 30%), their 80% correlation compresses the spread volatility to 21.09%.

E3 · Hedging exchange options

How does a trading desk delta-hedge an exchange option with \(\Delta_1 = \partial V / \partial S_1 = e^{-q_1 H}\Phi(d_1)\) and \(\Delta_2 = \partial V / \partial S_2 = -e^{-q_2 H}\Phi(d_2)\)?

Solution

The desk hedges by taking a long position of \(\Delta_1 = e^{-q_1 H}\Phi(d_1)\) shares of asset 1 and a short position of \(|\Delta_2| = e^{-q_2 H}\Phi(d_2)\) shares of asset 2. By Euler's theorem (Problem P3), the net value of this equity portfolio is \(\Delta_1 S_1 + \Delta_2 S_2 = V_0\). Thus the hedge portfolio perfectly finances itself with zero net borrowing or lending in the money-market account.

Research

R1 · Multi-asset spread options via Kirk's approximation

Explain why a spread option with non-zero dollar strike \(\max(S_1 - S_2 - K, 0)\) with \(K > 0\) lacks an exact closed form, and how Kirk's (1995) approximation uses numeraire change.

Solution

When \(K > 0\), the strike consists of a portfolio of asset 2 and cash: \(S_2 + K\). The sum of a lognormal variable and a constant is not lognormal. Choosing \(S_2\) as numeraire leaves a cash term \(K / S_2\) that is stochastic. Kirk's approximation approximates the denominator \(S_2 + K e^{-r(H-t)}\) as a single lognormal asset with effective volatility \(\sigma_K = \frac{S_2}{S_2 + K e^{-rt}}\sigma_2\), recovering a Margrabe-style analytical formula that is accurate to within a few basis points.

R2 · Exchange options under CEV models

How does Margrabe's formula generalize when the two assets follow Constant Elasticity of Variance (CEV) diffusions \(dS_i = \sigma_i S_i^{\gamma_i} dB_i\)?

Solution

In CEV models with elasticity \(\gamma_i \neq 1\), the volatility is state-dependent, and the ratio \(S_1 / S_2\) is no longer a geometric Brownian motion. However, choosing \(S_2\) as numeraire still makes \(S_1 / S_2\) a martingale. The pricing equation becomes a non-linear diffusion on the half-line, which can be solved using non-central chi-squared transition densities or numerical finite differences.

R3 · Maximum of two assets under numeraire change

Using the identity \(\max(S_1, S_2) = S_1 + \max(S_2 - S_1, 0)\), price an option that delivers the maximum of two assets at maturity \(H\).

Solution

The payoff is \(V_H = \max(S_1(H), S_2(H)) = S_1(H) + (S_2(H) - S_1(H))^+\). Taking discounted risk-neutral expectation: \(V_0 = \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH}S_1(H)] + \text{Margrabe}(S_2, S_1) = S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H} + [S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H}\Phi(d_1') - S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H}\Phi(d_2')]\), where \(d_1'\) and \(d_2'\) are computed for the option to exchange \(S_1\) for \(S_2\). Collecting terms gives \(V_0 = S_1(0) e^{-q_1 H}\Phi(-d_2') + S_2(0) e^{-q_2 H}\Phi(d_1')\), which is Stulz's (1982) formula for options on the maximum of two risky assets.
