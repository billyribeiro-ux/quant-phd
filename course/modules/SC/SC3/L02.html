Market Price of Risk and the Risk-Neutral Measure

  • Uses: Girsanov's theorem, geometric Brownian motion, Itô's lemma, Brownian motion, continuous-time martingale, filtration
  • Introduces: market price of risk

  • Areplicated

In real financial markets, investors demand an equity risk premium to hold volatile assets. Consequently, discounted asset prices are submartingales with positive physical drift, making direct valuation via expectations impossible. By defining the market price of risk as the ratio of excess return to diffusion volatility, Girsanov's theorem constructs an equivalent probability measure where all discounted tradable assets become martingales, establishing the mathematical foundation of risk-neutral pricing.

1Discounted Asset Dynamics under the Physical Measure

Consider a continuous-time market on horizon \([0, H]\) with a risk-free money-market account \(B_t^0 = \exp(\int_0^t r_s ds)\) (with constant rate \(r > 0\) for simplicity) and a risky asset \((S_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\) following geometric Brownian motion under the physical measure \(\Prob\):

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t, \quad S_0 > 0, \]

where \(\mu \in \R\) is the instantaneous expected rate of return and \(\sigma > 0\) is the volatility. To compare cash flows occurring at different times, we discount prices by the money-market numeraire:

\[ S^*_t \coloneqq \frac{S_t}{B_t^0} = e^{-rt} S_t. \]
Theorem 2.1 (Physical Dynamics of the Discounted Asset)

Assumptions: (A1) \(S_t\) satisfies \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\); (A2) \(B_t^0 = e^{rt}\) with constant \(r\).

Statement: The discounted asset price \(S^*_t = e^{-rt}S_t\) satisfies the stochastic differential equation:

\[ dS^*_t = (\mu - r) S^*_t dt + \sigma S^*_t dB_t = \sigma S^*_t \left( \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma} dt + dB_t \right). \]
Proof

Define \(f(t, s) = e^{-rt} s\). The partial derivatives are \(\partial_t f = -r e^{-rt} s\), \(\partial_s f = e^{-rt}\), and \(\partial_{ss} f = 0\). Applying Itô's lemma:

\[ dS^*_t = \partial_t f dt + \partial_s f dS_t + \tfrac{1}{2} \partial_{ss} f (dS_t)^2 = -r e^{-rt} S_t dt + e^{-rt}(\mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t) + 0 = (\mu - r) S^*_t dt + \sigma S^*_t dB_t. \]

Factoring out \(\sigma S^*_t\) completes the proof.

2The Market Price of Risk and Drift Elimination

Whenever \(\mu \neq r\), the discounted price \(S^*_t\) has non-zero drift \((\mu - r)S^*_t dt\). Under \(\Prob\), \(\E^\Prob[S^*_t] = S_0 e^{(\mu - r)t} \neq S_0\), meaning \(S^*_t\) fails to be a martingale. To eliminate this drift via Girsanov's theorem, we must isolate the noise driving the asset.

Definition 2.2 (Market Price of Risk)

The market price of risk (also called the Sharpe ratio of the Brownian motion) associated with diffusion \(S_t\) is defined as the normalized excess return per unit of diffusion volatility:

\[ \lambda_t \coloneqq \frac{\mu_t - r_t}{\sigma_t}. \]

In the constant parameter Black-Scholes market, \(\lambda = (\mu - r) / \sigma\) is a constant.

Theorem 2.3 (Existence of the Risk-Neutral Measure)

Assumptions: (A1) \(\lambda_t = (\mu_t - r_t)/\sigma_t\) satisfies Novikov's condition \(\E^\Prob[\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^H \lambda_t^2 dt)] < \infty\); (A2) the measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is defined on \(\mathcal{F}_H\) by the Radon–Nikodym derivative:

\[ Z_H \coloneqq \frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\Prob} = \exp\left(-\int_0^H \lambda_s dB_s - \frac{1}{2}\int_0^H \lambda_s^2 ds\right). \]

Statement: Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the process \(W_t = B_t + \int_0^t \lambda_s ds\) is a standard Brownian motion, and the discounted asset price \(S^*_t\) is a continuous \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale satisfying:

\[ dS^*_t = \sigma S^*_t dW_t. \]
Proof

By Girsanov's theorem (Lesson 1), since \(\lambda\) satisfies Novikov's condition, \(W_t = B_t + \int_0^t \lambda_s ds\) is a standard Brownian motion under \(\mathbb{Q}\). Substituting \(dB_t = dW_t - \lambda_t dt = dW_t - \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma} dt\) into Theorem 2.1:

\[ dS^*_t = (\mu - r) S^*_t dt + \sigma S^*_t \left( dW_t - \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma} dt \right) = \sigma S^*_t dW_t. \]

The drift vanishes identically. Because \(S^*_t = S_0 \exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t + \sigma W_t)\) is a geometric Brownian motion with drift zero, it is a square-integrable true martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\), satisfying \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[S^*_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = S^*_s\) for all \(s \le t\).

Remark 2.4 (Undiscounted Asset Dynamics under Q)

Applying Itô's lemma to \(S_t = e^{rt} S^*_t\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\):

\[ dS_t = d(e^{rt} S^*_t) = r e^{rt} S^*_t dt + e^{rt} dS^*_t = r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t. \]

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the drift of the asset price equals the risk-free interest rate \(r\), completely independent of the physical drift \(\mu\).

3Multi-Asset Markets and No-Arbitrage

Consider a continuous market with \(d\) risky assets \(\mathbf{S}_t = (S_{1, t}, \dots, S_{d, t})^\top\) driven by \(m\) independent Brownian motions \(\mathbf{B}_t = (B_{1, t}, \dots, B_{m, t})^\top\):

\[ \frac{dS_{i, t}}{S_{i, t}} = \mu_{i, t} dt + \sum_{j=1}^m \sigma_{i, j, t} dB_{j, t}, \quad i = 1, \dots, d. \]

In vector notation, \(\mathbf{S}^*_t = e^{-rt}\mathbf{S}_t\) has dynamics \(d\mathbf{S}^*_t = \text{diag}(\mathbf{S}^*_t)[(\boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r \mathbf{1}) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t d\mathbf{B}_t]\), where \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t\) is a \(d \times m\) volatility matrix.

Theorem 2.5 (Multi-Asset Market Price of Risk Equation)

Assumptions: (A1) The market contains \(d\) assets and \(m\) Brownian motions; (A2) There exists an equivalent measure \(\mathbb{Q} \sim \Prob\) with density \(Z_t = \mathcal{E}(-\boldsymbol{\lambda} \cdot \mathbf{B})_t\) under which all discounted assets are local martingales.

Statement: The market price of risk vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t \in \R^m\) must satisfy the linear system:

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1}. \]
Proof

By Girsanov's theorem, under \(\mathbb{Q}\) the vector \(\mathbf{W}_t = \mathbf{B}_t + \int_0^t \boldsymbol{\lambda}_s ds\) is a standard \(m\)-dimensional Brownian motion. Substituting \(d\mathbf{B}_t = d\mathbf{W}_t - \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t dt\) into the discounted dynamics:

\[ (\boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1}) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t (d\mathbf{W}_t - \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t dt) = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1} - \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t d\mathbf{W}_t. \]

For each component to be a local martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the drift vector must vanish identically: \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1} - \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t = \mathbf{0} \implies \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1}\).

Lab 1 · The market price of risk and discounted asset martingality

In the Black-Scholes market, the risky asset follows dS_t = mu * S_t * dt + sigma * S_t * dB_t. With risk-free rate r, the discounted asset S*_t = exp(-r*t) * S_t has physical drift (mu - r). The market price of risk is lambda = (mu - r) / sigma. By Girsanov's theorem, changing measure with density Z_t = exp(-lambda * B_t - (1/2) * lambda^2 * t) eliminates the excess drift, rendering S*_t a Q-martingale: E^Q[S*_t] = S_0 for all t >= 0. """Lab 1 · The market price of risk and discounted asset martingality.

In the Black-Scholes market, the risky asset follows dS_t = mu * S_t * dt + sigma * S_t * dB_t.
With risk-free rate r, the discounted asset S*_t = exp(-r*t) * S_t has physical drift (mu - r).
The market price of risk is lambda = (mu - r) / sigma.
By Girsanov's theorem, changing measure with density Z_t = exp(-lambda * B_t - (1/2) * lambda^2 * t)
eliminates the excess drift, rendering S*_t a Q-martingale: E^Q[S*_t] = S_0 for all t >= 0.
This lab simulates 10,000 asset paths (SIMULATED) under physical measure P and verifies that
the discounted asset exhibits positive drift under P but zero drift under Q within sampling error.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n_paths = 10000
    s0 = 100.0
    mu = 0.08
    sigma = 0.20
    r = 0.03
    horizon = 1.0

    # Market price of risk
    lambda_mpr = (mu - r) / sigma
    sq_h = math.sqrt(horizon)

    # Physical theoretical discounted expectation: S_0 * exp((mu - r) * H)
    target_p_disc = s0 * math.exp((mu - r) * horizon)
    target_q_disc = s0

    s_star_vals: list[float] = []
    weighted_s_star: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(n_paths):
        b_h = sq_h * rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        # S_H under physical measure P
        s_h = s0 * math.exp((mu - 0.5 * (sigma**2)) * horizon + sigma * b_h)
        # Discounted asset price S*_H = exp(-r * H) * S_H
        s_star = math.exp(-r * horizon) * s_h
        # Radon-Nikodym weight Z_H
        z = math.exp(-lambda_mpr * b_h - 0.5 * (lambda_mpr**2) * horizon)

        s_star_vals.append(s_star)
        weighted_s_star.append(z * s_star)

    # Physical expectation E^P[S*_H]
    mean_p = sum(s_star_vals) / n_paths
    var_p = sum((x - mean_p) ** 2 for x in s_star_vals) / (n_paths - 1)
    se_p = math.sqrt(var_p / n_paths)

    # Risk-neutral expectation E^Q[S*_H] = E^P[Z * S*_H]
    mean_q = sum(weighted_s_star) / n_paths
    var_q = sum((x - mean_q) ** 2 for x in weighted_s_star) / (n_paths - 1)
    se_q = math.sqrt(var_q / n_paths)

    print(f"market price of risk lambda: {lambda_mpr:.4f}")
    print(f"E^P[S*_H]: {mean_p:.4f} (target {target_p_disc:.4f}, se {se_p:.4f})")
    print(f"E^Q[S*_H]: {mean_q:.4f} (target {target_q_disc:.4f}, se {se_q:.4f})")
    print("P-expectation inside 3*se:", abs(mean_p - target_p_disc) <= 3.0 * se_p)
    print("Q-martingale property |E^Q[S*] - S0| <= 3*se:", abs(mean_q - target_q_disc) <= 3.0 * se_q)


main()

4Where the canon breaks

In single-factor models with \(d = 1\) and \(m = 1\), the market price of risk \(\lambda = (\mu - r)/\sigma\) is uniquely determined. However, in incomplete markets where the number of Brownian shocks exceeds the number of tradable assets (\(m > d\)), the linear system \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1}\) is underdetermined. Infinitely many distinct market price of risk vectors \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t\) satisfy the no-arbitrage condition, corresponding to infinitely many distinct equivalent martingale measures \(\mathbb{Q}\). Under each valid measure, contingent claims that cannot be replicated by existing assets have different prices. Selecting a specific pricing measure in an incomplete market requires specifying investor preferences, utility functions, or equilibrium conditions (e.g. minimal martingale measure or entropy-minimizing measure).

5Misconceptions

  1. “The risk-neutral measure assumes all market participants are risk-neutral.”

    Risk-neutral pricing is a mathematical consequence of no-arbitrage, not an assumption about investor psychology. In a complete market, options are priced by constructing a replicating portfolio whose value is independent of risk preferences. The measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is an artificial computational device constructed by removing the market price of risk via Girsanov's theorem.

  2. “Option prices depend on the physical drift \(\mu\) of the underlying stock.”

    In the Black-Scholes formula, the physical drift \(\mu\) never appears; it is completely replaced by the risk-free rate \(r\). By Girsanov's theorem, changing to \(\mathbb{Q}\) shifts the stock drift to \(r\), and the price of any derivative is its discounted expected payoff under \(\mathbb{Q}\).

  3. “If two assets have different drifts \(\mu_1 \neq \mu_2\) but the same Brownian driver, they can have different market prices of risk.”

    In a frictionless market driven by a single Brownian motion, if two assets had different market prices of risk \(\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2\), an arbitrageur could form a zero-volatility portfolio with positive expected return, violating no-arbitrage. No-arbitrage demands a unique market price of risk for each independent source of Brownian risk.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Single asset market price of risk

A stock has expected return \(\mu = 0.12\) and volatility \(\sigma = 0.25\). The risk-free rate is \(r = 0.04\). Compute the market price of risk \(\lambda\). If the stock price is \(S_0 = 50\), compute \(\E^\Prob[S_1]\) and \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[S_1]\) at horizon \(H = 1.0\).

Solution

The market price of risk is \(\lambda = (\mu - r) / \sigma = (0.12 - 0.04) / 0.25 = 0.08 / 0.25 = 0.32\). Under the physical measure \(\Prob\), the stock drift is \(\mu = 0.12\), so \(\E^\Prob[S_1] = S_0 e^{\mu} = 50 e^{0.12} \approx 50(1.1275) = 56.37\). Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the drift is \(r = 0.04\), so \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[S_1] = S_0 e^r = 50 e^{0.04} \approx 50(1.0408) = 52.04\).

M2 · Two-asset single-factor consistency

Two assets are driven by the same standard Brownian motion: \(dS_1 / S_1 = \mu_1 dt + \sigma_1 dB_t\) and \(dS_2 / S_2 = \mu_2 dt + \sigma_2 dB_t\) with \(\sigma_1 = 0.20\) and \(\sigma_2 = 0.30\). If \(r = 0.05\) and \(\mu_1 = 0.11\), determine the unique no-arbitrage drift \(\mu_2\).

Solution

Because there is only one Brownian shock, no-arbitrage requires both assets to share the identical market price of risk: \(\lambda = (\mu_1 - r)/\sigma_1 = (\mu_2 - r)/\sigma_2\). Evaluating asset 1: \(\lambda = (0.11 - 0.05) / 0.20 = 0.06 / 0.20 = 0.30\). For asset 2: \((\mu_2 - 0.05) / 0.30 = 0.30 \implies \mu_2 - 0.05 = 0.09 \implies \mu_2 = 0.14\). If \(\mu_2 \neq 0.14\), a long-short portfolio hedging Brownian risk would yield a risk-free return different from \(r\).

M3 · Radon–Nikodym density variance with MPR

For \(\lambda = 0.30\) and horizon \(H = 2.0\), compute \(\E^\Prob[Z_2^2]\) and \(\Var^\Prob(Z_2)\) for the density process \(Z_t = \mathcal{E}(-\lambda \cdot B)_t\).

Solution

Since \(\lambda\) is constant, \(Z_2 = \exp(-0.3 B_2 - \tfrac{1}{2}(0.09)(2)) = \exp(-0.3 B_2 - 0.09)\). Squaring: \(Z_2^2 = \exp(-0.6 B_2 - 0.18)\). Since \(B_2 \sim \Normal(0, 2)\), \(\E[e^{-0.6 B_2}] = \exp(\tfrac{1}{2}(-0.6)^2 \cdot 2) = \exp(0.36)\). Therefore \(\E[Z_2^2] = e^{-0.18 + 0.36} = e^{0.18} \approx 1.1972\). Since \(\E[Z_2] = 1\), the variance is \(\Var(Z_2) = 1.1972 - 1 = 0.1972\).

P1 · Arbitrage from unequal market prices of risk

Prove that if two assets driven by the same Brownian motion satisfy \(\lambda_1 > \lambda_2\), there exists an explicit arbitrage portfolio.

Solution

Consider a portfolio holding \(\alpha_1 = 1 / (\sigma_1 S_1)\) shares of asset 1 and \(\alpha_2 = -1 / (\sigma_2 S_2)\) shares of asset 2. The dollar values invested are \(\Pi_1 = 1 / \sigma_1\) and \(\Pi_2 = -1 / \sigma_2\), with net initial investment \(\Pi_0 = 1/\sigma_1 - 1/\sigma_2\). Finance \(\Pi_0\) by borrowing from the money-market account. The differential portfolio gain is: \(d\Pi_t = \alpha_1 dS_1 + \alpha_2 dS_2 - r \Pi_0 dt = (\frac{\mu_1}{\sigma_1} dt + dB_t) - (\frac{\mu_2}{\sigma_2} dt + dB_t) - r(\frac{1}{\sigma_1} - \frac{1}{\sigma_2})dt\). The Brownian terms cancel identically: \(d\Pi_t = [(\frac{\mu_1 - r}{\sigma_1}) - (\frac{\mu_2 - r}{\sigma_2})] dt = (\lambda_1 - \lambda_2) dt\). Since \(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2 > 0\), the portfolio generates a strictly positive, risk-free profit with zero net risk, constituting an arbitrage.

P2 · Martingale property of discounted gains from trade

Let \(\Delta_t\) be an \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-adapted trading strategy satisfying \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[\int_0^H (\Delta_t \sigma S^*_t)^2 dt] < \infty\). Prove that the discounted portfolio value \(\Pi^*_t = \Pi^*_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_s dS^*_s\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale.

Solution

By Theorem 2.3, \(dS^*_t = \sigma S^*_t dW_t\) where \(W_t\) is a standard Brownian motion under \(\mathbb{Q}\). Substituting into the portfolio equation: \(\Pi^*_t = \Pi^*_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_s \sigma S^*_s dW_s\). The integrand \(H_s = \Delta_s \sigma S^*_s\) is \(\mathcal{F}_s\)-adapted and satisfies \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[\int_0^H H_s^2 ds] < \infty\). By the Itô integral properties established in SC2, any stochastic integral of a square-integrable adapted process against Brownian motion is a true square-integrable martingale. Thus \(\Pi^*_t\) is a continuous \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale with \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[\Pi^*_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \Pi^*_s\).

P3 · Uniqueness of the market price of risk in invertible markets

Prove that if the volatility matrix \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \in \R^{d \times d}\) is invertible for all \(t\), then the market price of risk vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t\) is uniquely determined.

Solution

By Theorem 2.5, no-arbitrage requires \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1}\). Since \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t\) is square (\(d = m\)) and invertible, its matrix inverse \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^{-1}\) exists. Multiplying both sides by \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^{-1}\) gives: \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t = \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1})\). Because the matrix inverse is unique, there exists exactly one vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t\) satisfying the equation. By the First and Second Fundamental Theorems of Asset Pricing, the market is both arbitrage-free and complete.

Empirical

E1 · Historical equity market price of risk

Using historical US equity data with annualized market return \(\mu \approx 0.08\), risk-free rate \(r \approx 0.02\), and volatility \(\sigma \approx 0.16\), compute the empirical market price of risk and interpret its magnitude.

Solution

The annualized Sharpe ratio is \(\lambda = (0.08 - 0.02) / 0.16 = 0.06 / 0.16 = 0.375\). This indicates that the market demands approximately 0.375 units of excess annualized return per unit of annualized standard deviation. Over a 1-year horizon, the drift shift applied to Brownian motion when moving to the risk-neutral measure is \(\theta = 0.375\).

E2 · Time-varying interest rates and market price of risk

If the short rate follows a Vasicek process \(dr_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)dt + \sigma_r dB_t\), how does a time-varying rate affect the market price of risk of an equity asset with constant \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\)?

Solution

The market price of risk becomes stochastic: \(\lambda_t = (\mu - r_t)/\sigma\). As \(r_t\) fluctuates, \(\lambda_t\) fluctuates inversely: higher interest rates reduce the equity premium \(\mu - r_t\), decreasing \(\lambda_t\). Because \(r_t\) is Gaussian in the Vasicek model, \(\int_0^H \lambda_t^2 dt\) is a Gaussian quadratic form, which satisfies Novikov's condition for sufficiently small \(H\) or moderate parameters.

E3 · Monte Carlo pricing bias from physical drift

An analyst erroneously simulates a European call option under \(\Prob\) with drift \(\mu = 0.10\) instead of \(r = 0.04\). If \(S_0 = 100, K = 100, \sigma = 0.20, T = 1.0\), explain whether the resulting discounted expectation will overprice or underprice the option.

Solution

Because \(\mu > r\), the physical stock price distribution is shifted to the right: \(\E^\Prob[S_T] = 100 e^{0.10} \approx 110.52\) compared to \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[S_T] = 100 e^{0.04} \approx 104.08\). The call payoff \((S_T - K)^+\) is a convex increasing function of \(S_T\). By Jensen's inequality and first-order stochastic dominance, \(\E^\Prob[(S_T - K)^+] > \E^\mathbb{Q}[(S_T - K)^+]\). Discounting at \(e^{-rT}\) produces a severely overstated option price (approximately 14.50 vs 9.92), creating a fictitious arbitrage profit.

Research

R1 · First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing

State how the existence of a valid market price of risk relates to the First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (Delbaen & Schachermayer 1994).

Solution

The First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (Delbaen & Schachermayer 1994) establishes that a market model satisfies No Free Lunch with Vanishing Risk (NFLVR) if and only if there exists an equivalent local martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q} \sim \Prob\). In diffusion markets, the existence of \(\mathbb{Q}\) is equivalent to the existence of an adapted process \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t\) solving \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1}\) such that the stochastic exponential \(\mathcal{E}(-\boldsymbol{\lambda} \cdot \mathbf{B})\) is a true martingale.

R2 · Minimal martingale measure in incomplete markets

Define Föllmer and Schweizer's minimal martingale measure for an incomplete diffusion market with \(m > d\).

Solution

In an incomplete market where \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t\) has rank \(d < m\), the kernel of \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t\) is non-trivial. The minimal martingale measure chooses \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t^*\) to be the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse solution: \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t^* = \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^\top(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^\top)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1})\). This minimizes the \(L^2\)-norm \(\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t\|^2\), ensuring that \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t^*\) lies entirely in the range of \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t^\top\), leaving all orthogonal Brownian components unaffected.

R3 · Stochastic discount factor duality

Show that the stochastic discount factor (or state price deflator) \(M_t = e^{-rt} Z_t\) satisfies \(S_t = \E^\Prob[M_H S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t] / M_t\).

Solution

By risk-neutral pricing under \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(e^{-rt} S_t = \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH} S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). Applying the abstract Bayes formula to convert to \(\Prob\): \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH} S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \E^\Prob[e^{-rH} S_H Z_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t] / Z_t\). Multiplying both sides by \(e^{rt}\): \(S_t = \E^\Prob[e^{-r(H-t)} \frac{Z_H}{Z_t} S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). Defining \(M_t \coloneqq e^{-rt} Z_t\), we have \(M_H / M_t = e^{-r(H-t)} (Z_H / Z_t)\). Thus \(S_t = \E^\Prob[\frac{M_H}{M_t} S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \frac{1}{M_t}\E^\Prob[M_H S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\), establishing the pricing duality between equivalent martingale measures and state price deflators.

  3. Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), what is the instantaneous expected growth rate (drift) of the asset price \(S_t\)?

    Remark 2.4: Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(dS_t = r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t\), so the asset price grows at the risk-free rate \(r\).

  4. What property does the discounted asset price \(S^*_t = e^{-rt} S_t\) satisfy under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\)?

    Options

    Theorem 2.3: Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(dS^*_t = \sigma S^*_t dW_t\) is driftless, making \(S^*_t\) a true martingale.

  5. If \(S_0 = 100\), what is the risk-neutral expectation of the discounted asset price \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[S^*_t]\) for any \(t > 0\)?

    Because \(S^*_t\) is a martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(\E^\mathbb{Q}[S^*_t] = S^*_0 = S_0 = 100\).

  6. In a multi-asset market with volatility matrix \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t\), what linear system must the market price of risk vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_t\) satisfy?

    Options

    Theorem 2.5: The no-arbitrage drift condition requires \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_t \boldsymbol{\lambda}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu}_t - r_t \mathbf{1}\).

  7. If the number of independent Brownian motions exceeds the number of tradable assets (\(m > d\)), how many risk-neutral measures exist?

    Options

    When \(m > d\), the linear system is underdetermined, giving an infinite family of equivalent martingale measures.

  8. Why does the historical expected return \(\mu\) not appear in the Black-Scholes option pricing formula?

    Options

    Girsanov's theorem replaces \(\mu\) with \(r\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\), so the physical expected return drops out entirely.
