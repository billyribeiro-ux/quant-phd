The Forward Measure and Stochastic Interest Rates

When interest rates are stochastic, the discounting factor \(\exp(-\int_0^H r_s ds)\) is a random variable correlated with the terminal payoff. Under the risk-neutral measure, pricing an option requires evaluating a coupled expectation that cannot be factored. Choosing the zero-coupon bond maturing at the option horizon as numeraire constructs the forward measure. Under this measure, the bond price becomes 1 at maturity, completely factoring out discounting and reducing the problem to an expectation of the forward price.

1The Challenge of Stochastic Discounting

Let \(r_t\) be a stochastic short rate process adapted to the market filtration \((\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\). The money-market account is \(B_t^0 = \exp(\int_0^t r_s ds)\). Under the standard risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the price of a European derivative with payoff \(V_H\) at maturity \(H\) is:

\[ V_0 = \E^\mathbb{Q}\left[ \exp\left(-\int_0^H r_s ds\right) V_H \right]. \]

If interest rates are stochastic and correlated with the underlying asset price \(S_t\), the discounting exponential is not independent of \(V_H\). Consequently:

\[ \E^\mathbb{Q}\left[ \exp\left(-\int_0^H r_s ds\right) V_H \right] \neq \E^\mathbb{Q}\left[ \exp\left(-\int_0^H r_s ds\right) \right] \E^\mathbb{Q}[V_H]. \]

Direct calculation under \(\mathbb{Q}\) requires solving coupled partial differential equations or running computationally demanding two-factor pathwise simulations.

2Definition of the T-Forward Measure

Let \(P(t, H)\) denote the price at time \(t\) of a default-free zero-coupon bond delivering $1 at maturity \(H\). Because \(P(t, H) > 0\) for all \(t \in [0, H]\) and pays no intermediate coupons, it is a valid numeraire. Crucially, its terminal value is deterministic:

\[ P(H, H) = 1. \]
Definition 2.1 (Forward Measure)

The forward measure (or \(H\)-forward measure) \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) is the equivalent martingale measure associated with the zero-coupon bond numeraire \(N_t = P(t, H)\). By the change of numeraire theorem, its Radon–Nikodym derivative with respect to the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) on \(\mathcal{F}_t\) is:

\[ Z_t^H \coloneqq \frac{d\mathbb{Q}^H}{d\mathbb{Q}}\Bigg|_{\mathcal{F}_t} = \frac{P(t, H) / P(0, H)}{B_t^0 / B_0^0} = \frac{P(t, H)}{P(0, H) \exp\left(\int_0^t r_s ds\right)}. \]

At terminal maturity \(t = H\), using \(P(H, H) = 1\):

\[ Z_H^H = \frac{1}{P(0, H) \exp\left(\int_0^H r_s ds\right)}. \]
Theorem 2.2 (Decoupling Stochastic Discounting)

Assumptions: (A1) \(P(t, H) > 0\) is the price of an \(H\)-maturity zero-coupon bond; (A2) \(V_H \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_H, \mathbb{Q}^H)\) is a contingent payoff.

Statement: The derivative price at time 0 factors cleanly as:

\[ V_0 = P(0, H) \E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[V_H]. \]

At any intermediate time \(t \le H\), the price satisfies:

\[ V_t = P(t, H) \E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[V_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t]. \]
Proof

Apply the generalized valuation equation (Theorem 1.3 in Lesson 4) with numeraire \(N_t = P(t, H)\):

\[ V_0 = N_0 \E^{\mathbb{Q}^N}\left[ \frac{V_H}{N_H} \right] = P(0, H) \E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}\left[ \frac{V_H}{P(H, H)} \right]. \]

Because \(P(H, H) = 1\) identically, this simplifies immediately to \(V_0 = P(0, H) \E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[V_H]\). The intermediate formula follows by taking conditional expectations under \(\mathbb{Q}^H\).

3Forward Prices and Black's Formula under Stochastic Rates

Definition 2.3 (Forward Price Process)

The forward price \(F(t, H)\) of an asset \(S_t\) for delivery at maturity \(H\) is defined as the relative price:

\[ F(t, H) \coloneqq \frac{S_t}{P(t, H)}. \]
Theorem 2.4 (Martingality of Forward Prices)

Assumptions: (A1) \(S_t\) is a non-dividend-paying tradable asset; (A2) \(P(t, H)\) is a zero-coupon bond numeraire.

Statement: The forward price process \(F(t, H)\) is an \((\mathcal{F}_t, \mathbb{Q}^H)\)-martingale. In particular, \(\E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = F(t, H)\), and at \(t = 0\):

\[ \E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[S_H] = F(0, H) = \frac{S_0}{P(0, H)}. \]
Proof

By the change of numeraire theorem (Theorem 1.2 in Lesson 4), the ratio of any tradable asset to the numeraire is a martingale under the numeraire measure. Because \(S_t\) is tradable and \(P(t, H)\) is the numeraire for \(\mathbb{Q}^H\), the relative price \(F(t, H) = S_t / P(t, H)\) is an \((\mathcal{F}_t, \mathbb{Q}^H)\)-martingale. Because \(P(H, H) = 1\), \(F(H, H) = S_H / 1 = S_H\). By the martingale property, \(\E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[F(H, H) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = F(t, H)\).

Theorem 2.5 (Black's Formula under Gaussian Stochastic Rates)

Assumptions: (A1) Short rates follow a Gaussian diffusion (e.g. Vasicek) and stock price follows geometric Brownian motion with correlation \(\rho\); (A2) The forward price diffusion under \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) satisfies \(dF(t, H) / F(t, H) = \sigma_F(t) dW_t^H\) with deterministic volatility \(\sigma_F(t)\); (A3) The total forward variance is \(v_F^2 = \int_0^H \sigma_F(t)^2 dt\).

Statement: The European call option price on \(S\) with strike \(K\) and maturity \(H\) is given analytically by:

\[ V_0 = P(0, H) [F(0, H) \Phi(d_1) - K \Phi(d_2)], \]

where \(d_1 = \frac{\log(F(0, H) / K) + \frac{1}{2}v_F^2}{v_F}\) and \(d_2 = d_1 - v_F\). This extends Black's (1976) formula to stochastic rates.

Proof

By Theorem 2.2, \(V_0 = P(0, H) \E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[(S_H - K)^+] = P(0, H) \E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[(F(H, H) - K)^+]\). Under assumption A2, \(F(t, H) = F(0, H) \exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^t \sigma_F(s)^2 ds + \int_0^t \sigma_F(s) dW_s^H)\). At \(t = H\), \(\log F(H, H)\) is normally distributed under \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) with mean \(\log F(0, H) - \tfrac{1}{2}v_F^2\) and variance \(v_F^2\). Standard Gaussian integration of \((F - K)^+\) reproduces the Black-76 formula scaled by \(P(0, H)\).

Lab 1 · The T-forward measure under stochastic Vasicek interest rates

With stochastic interest rates, discounting exp(-int_0^T r_s ds) is correlated with terminal stock price S_T. Under the risk-neutral measure Q, the option price is E^Q[exp(-int_0^T r_s ds) * (S_T - K)^+]. Under the T-forward measure Q^T (with zero-coupon bond P(t, T) as numeraire): V_0 = P(0, T) * E^{Q^T}[(F_T - K)^+] where F_t = S_t / P(t, T) is a Q^T-martingale. In the Gaussian Vasicek model, the integrated forward volatility v_F^2 is available analytically, yielding a closed-form Black-76 forward formula. This lab simulates 10,000 coupled (r, S) paths under Q (SIMULATED) and verifies that the path-discounted Monte Carlo price matches the analytical T-forward price within 2 standard errors.

"""Lab 1 · The T-forward measure under stochastic Vasicek interest rates.

With stochastic interest rates, discounting exp(-int_0^T r_s ds) is correlated with terminal stock price S_T.
Under the risk-neutral measure Q, the option price is E^Q[exp(-int_0^T r_s ds) * (S_T - K)^+].
Under the T-forward measure Q^T (with zero-coupon bond P(t, T) as numeraire):
    V_0 = P(0, T) * E^{Q^T}[(F_T - K)^+]
where F_t = S_t / P(t, T) is a Q^T-martingale. In the Gaussian Vasicek model, the integrated forward volatility
v_F^2 is available analytically, yielding a closed-form Black-76 forward formula.
This lab simulates 10,000 coupled (r, S) paths under Q (SIMULATED) and verifies that the path-discounted
Monte Carlo price matches the analytical T-forward price within 2 standard errors.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def vasicek_bond(r0: float, kappa: float, theta: float, sig_r: float, tau: float) -> float:
    b = (1.0 - math.exp(-kappa * tau)) / kappa
    a = (theta - (sig_r**2) / (2.0 * (kappa**2))) * (b - tau) - ((sig_r**2) / (4.0 * kappa)) * (b**2)
    return math.exp(a - b * r0)


def forward_call_price(
    s0: float,
    k: float,
    r0: float,
    kappa: float,
    theta: float,
    sig_r: float,
    sig_s: float,
    rho: float,
    t: float,
) -> float:
    p0 = vasicek_bond(r0, kappa, theta, sig_r, t)
    f0 = s0 / p0

    # Integrated bond volatility components:
    # sigma_P(tau) = (sig_r / kappa) * (1 - exp(-kappa * tau))
    int_sig_p = (sig_r / kappa) * (t - (1.0 - math.exp(-kappa * t)) / kappa)
    int_sig_p2 = ((sig_r**2) / (kappa**2)) * (
        t - (2.0 / kappa) * (1.0 - math.exp(-kappa * t)) + (1.0 / (2.0 * kappa)) * (1.0 - math.exp(-2.0 * kappa * t))
    )
    vf2 = (sig_s**2) * t + int_sig_p2 - 2.0 * rho * sig_s * int_sig_p
    vf = math.sqrt(vf2)

    d1 = (math.log(f0 / k) + 0.5 * vf2) / vf
    d2 = d1 - vf
    return p0 * (f0 * norm_cdf(d1) - k * norm_cdf(d2))


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    r0 = 0.04
    kappa = 0.5
    theta = 0.05
    sig_r = 0.02
    s0 = 100.0
    k = 100.0
    sig_s = 0.20
    rho = 0.30
    t = 1.0

    p0 = vasicek_bond(r0, kappa, theta, sig_r, t)
    ana_price = forward_call_price(s0, k, r0, kappa, theta, sig_r, sig_s, rho, t)

    # Monte Carlo simulation under Q with pathwise discounting
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n_steps = 50
    n_paths = 10000
    dt = t / n_steps
    sq_dt = math.sqrt(dt)

    payoffs: list[float] = []
    for _ in range(n_paths):
        r = r0
        s = s0
        int_r = 0.0
        for _ in range(n_steps):
            z1 = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            z2 = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            zr = z1
            zs = rho * z1 + math.sqrt(max(1.0 - rho**2, 0.0)) * z2

            int_r += r * dt
            r += kappa * (theta - r) * dt + sig_r * sq_dt * zr
            s *= math.exp((r - 0.5 * (sig_s**2)) * dt + sig_s * sq_dt * zs)

        payoffs.append(math.exp(-int_r) * max(s - k, 0.0))

    mc_price = sum(payoffs) / n_paths
    var_mc = sum((x - mc_price) ** 2 for x in payoffs) / (n_paths - 1)
    se_mc = math.sqrt(var_mc / n_paths)

    print(f"zero-coupon bond price P(0, T): {p0:.6f}")
    print(f"analytical T-forward call price: {ana_price:.4f}")
    print(f"path-discounted Q Monte Carlo price: {mc_price:.4f} (se {se_mc:.4f})")
    print(f"absolute difference: {abs(mc_price - ana_price):.4f}")
    print("MC inside 2*se band:", abs(mc_price - ana_price) <= 2.0 * se_mc)

Under stochastic Vasicek rates (\(\kappa = 0.5, \theta = 0.05, \sigma_r = 0.02\)), the zero-coupon bond price is \(P(0, H) = 0.958789\). The analytical \(H\)-forward price is 9.9446. 4Where the canon breaks

The forward measure requires a liquidly traded zero-coupon bond for the exact maturity date \(H\) of the option contract. For short horizons (e.g. 1 to 5 years), government Treasury bills and strip bonds provide accurate bond prices \(P(0, H)\). However, for long-dated contracts (e.g. 20 to 30 years) or broken maturity dates (e.g. 137 days), no pure zero-coupon bond trades. The bond price must be interpolated from swap curves and yield models, introducing model risk. Furthermore, if interest rates exhibit non-Gaussian features—such as lower bounds near zero (shadow rates) or stochastic volatility—the forward price volatility \(\sigma_F(t)\) is stochastic, and Black's closed form breaks down, requiring numerical PDE or Fourier transform methods (such as Heston–Vasicek hybrid models).

5Misconceptions

  1. “The forward price \(F(t, H)\) is a martingale under the standard risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\).”

    Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the numeraire is the money-market account \(B_t^0\), so only discounted asset prices \(S_t / B_t^0\) are martingales. The forward price \(F(t, H) = S_t / P(t, H)\) is a ratio of two risky assets, which is a martingale if and only if evaluated under the \(H\)-forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\).

  2. “The forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) is the same for all derivative contracts.”

    The forward measure is indexed by maturity \(H\). An option expiring at \(H_1 = 1\) year uses the 1-year forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{H_1}\), while an option expiring at \(H_2 = 5\) years uses \(\mathbb{Q}^{H_2}\). Different maturities correspond to different zero-coupon bond numeraires and different probability measures.

  3. “Under stochastic rates, Black's formula cannot be used.”

    Whenever the forward price volatility \(\sigma_F(t)\) is deterministic (which holds in Gaussian short-rate models like Vasicek or Ho-Lee coupled with lognormal asset diffusion), the forward price remains lognormal under \(\mathbb{Q}^H\), and Black's formula holds exactly with integrated variance \(v_F^2\).

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Forward price computation

A stock trades at \(S_0 = 80\). The 2-year zero-coupon bond trades at \(P(0, 2) = 0.90\). What is the 2-year forward price \(F(0, 2)\)? If the stock pays a continuous dividend yield of \(q = 0.02\), compute the dividend-adjusted forward price.

Solution

For a non-dividend paying stock: \(F(0, 2) = S_0 / P(0, 2) = 80 / 0.90 = 88.8889\). With continuous dividend yield \(q = 0.02\), holding the stock forfeits dividends, so the forward price is \(F(0, 2) = S_0 e^{-q H} / P(0, H) = 80 e^{-0.04} / 0.90 = 80(0.960789) / 0.90 = 76.8631 / 0.90 = 85.4035\).

M2 · Forward measure density process

Under constant interest rate \(r\), what does the forward measure density \(Z_t^H = \frac{P(t, H)}{P(0, H) e^{rt}}\) reduce to?

Solution

Under constant interest rates, the zero-coupon bond price is deterministic: \(P(t, H) = e^{-r(H-t)}\) and \(P(0, H) = e^{-rH}\). Substituting into the density: \(Z_t^H = \frac{e^{-r(H-t)}}{e^{-rH} e^{rt}} = \frac{e^{-rH} e^{rt}}{e^{-rH} e^{rt}} = 1\). When interest rates are constant, \(Z_t^H \equiv 1\) identically, so the forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) coincides with the standard risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\).

M3 · Forward measure call option valuation

Let \(P(0, 1) = 0.95\), \(F(0, 1) = 105\), strike \(K = 100\), and forward volatility \(v_F = 0.20\). Compute the European call price using Black's formula under the forward measure.

Solution

\(d_1 = \frac{\log(105/100) + 0.5(0.04)}{0.20} = \frac{0.04879 + 0.02}{0.20} = \frac{0.06879}{0.20} = 0.3440\). Then \(d_2 = 0.3440 - 0.20 = 0.1440\). \(\Phi(0.3440) \approx 0.6346\) and \(\Phi(0.1440) \approx 0.5572\). The undiscounted expectation is \(105(0.6346) - 100(0.5572) = 66.633 - 55.72 = 10.913\). Multiplying by \(P(0, 1)\): \(V_0 = 0.95 \times 10.913 = 10.367\).

P1 · Forward price volatility from bond and stock diffusions

Let \(dS_t / S_t = r_t dt + \sigma_S dW_t^S\) and \(dP(t, H) / P(t, H) = r_t dt + \sigma_P(t, H) dW_t^P\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\) with \(d\langle W^S, W^P \rangle_t = \rho dt\). Prove that the forward price volatility under \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) is \(\sigma_F(t)^2 = \sigma_S^2 - 2\rho\sigma_S\sigma_P(t, H) + \sigma_P(t, H)^2\).

Solution

Consider the forward price \(F(t, H) = S_t / P(t, H)\). Taking logarithms: \(\log F = \log S_t - \log P(t, H)\). By Itô's lemma, the diffusion increment of \(\log S_t\) is \(\sigma_S dW_t^S\) and of \(\log P\) is \(\sigma_P dW_t^P\). The diffusion increment of \(\log F\) is \(d\log F = \dots + \sigma_S dW_t^S - \sigma_P dW_t^P\). The quadratic variation rate is: \(\frac{d\langle \log F, \log F \rangle_t}{dt} = \sigma_S^2 - 2\rho \sigma_S \sigma_P + \sigma_P^2\). Because quadratic variation is invariant under equivalent measure change (Problem P2 in Lesson 1), \(\sigma_F(t)^2\) under \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) is identically \(\sigma_S^2 - 2\rho \sigma_S \sigma_P(t, H) + \sigma_P(t, H)^2\).

P2 · Forward measure Girsanov kernel

Prove that the Girsanov drift shift from \(\mathbb{Q}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) is \(\theta_t = -\sigma_P(t, H)\), where \(\sigma_P(t, H)\) is the volatility of the zero-coupon bond.

Solution

The density is \(Z_t^H = \frac{P(t, H)}{P(0, H) e^{\int_0^t r_s ds}}\). By Itô's formula, \(dZ_t^H = -r_t Z_t^H dt + \frac{1}{P(0, H)e^{\int r}} dP(t, H)\). Since \(dP(t, H) = P(t, H)(r_t dt + \sigma_P(t, H) dW_t)\), the drift terms cancel: \(dZ_t^H = \sigma_P(t, H) Z_t^H dW_t\). Matching with the canonical Doléans-Dade form \(dZ_t / Z_t = -\theta_t dW_t\) gives \(\theta_t = -\sigma_P(t, H)\). By Girsanov's theorem, the Brownian motion under \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) is \(W_t^H = W_t - \int_0^t \sigma_P(s, H) ds\).

P3 · Forward measure put-call parity

Prove that under the forward measure, put-call parity can be written as \(C(t) - P(t) = P(t, H)(F(t, H) - K)\).

Solution

By Theorem 2.2, \(C(t) = P(t, H)\E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[(S_H - K)^+ \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) and \(P(t) = P(t, H)\E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[(K - S_H)^+ \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). Subtracting: \(C(t) - P(t) = P(t, H)\E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[(S_H - K)^+ - (K - S_H)^+ \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = P(t, H)\E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[S_H - K \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). By Theorem 2.4, \(\E^{\mathbb{Q}^H}[S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = F(t, H)\). Thus \(C(t) - P(t) = P(t, H)(F(t, H) - K)\).

Empirical

E1 · Correlation between rates and stock prices

If the correlation between interest rates and stock returns is positive (\(\rho > 0\)), how does forward price volatility compare to spot stock volatility for short-to-medium horizons?

Solution

From Problem P1, \(\sigma_F^2 = \sigma_S^2 - 2\rho\sigma_S\sigma_P + \sigma_P^2\). For equity options with \(\sigma_S \approx 0.20\) and bond volatility \(\sigma_P \approx 0.02\), if \(\rho > 0\), the cross term \(-2\rho \sigma_S \sigma_P = -2\rho(0.004) < 0\) exceeds \(\sigma_P^2 = 0.0004\). Consequently, \(\sigma_F^2 < \sigma_S^2\): positive correlation reduces forward volatility below spot volatility, narrowing option prices.

E2 · Vasicek bond price volatility curve

In the Vasicek model with \(\kappa = 0.5\) and \(\sigma_r = 0.02\), compute the bond volatility \(\sigma_P(\tau) = \frac{\sigma_r}{\kappa}(1 - e^{-\kappa \tau})\) for maturities \(\tau \in \{0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0\}\) years.

Solution

The asymptotic volatility ceiling is \(\sigma_r / \kappa = 0.02 / 0.5 = 0.04\) (400 bps). For \(\tau = 0.5\): \(\sigma_P = 0.04(1 - e^{-0.25}) = 0.04(1 - 0.7788) = 0.04(0.2212) = 0.0088\) (88 bps). For \(\tau = 1.0\): \(0.04(1 - e^{-0.5}) = 0.04(1 - 0.6065) = 0.04(0.3935) = 0.0157\) (157 bps). For \(\tau = 5.0\): \(0.04(1 - e^{-2.5}) = 0.04(1 - 0.0821) = 0.0367\) (367 bps). For \(\tau = 10.0\): \(0.04(1 - e^{-5.0}) = 0.04(1 - 0.0067) = 0.0397\) (397 bps).

E3 · Negative interest rates and zero-coupon bond prices

During the European negative interest rate era (2014–2022), German Bund yields traded negative, so \(P(0, H) > 1\). Does the forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) remain valid when \(P(0, H) > 1\)?

Solution

Yes. A valid numeraire requires only that \(P(t, H) > 0\) almost surely (strict positivity). If interest rates are negative, \(\int_0^t r_s ds < 0\), so zero-coupon bond prices trade at a premium above par (\(P(0, H) > 1\)). Because \(P(t, H) > 0\) strictly, the relative price \(S_t / P(t, H)\) is well-defined and positive, and the forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) remains mathematically valid.

Research

R1 · Jamshidian's trick for European swaptions

Explain how Jamshidian (1989) uses the forward measure and zero-coupon bond decomposition to price European options on coupon bonds and swaptions in one-factor interest rate models.

Solution

A coupon bond is a portfolio of zero-coupon bonds \(\sum_i c_i P(t, T_i)\). In a one-factor model, all bond prices \(P(t, T_i)\) are strictly monotonic functions of the short rate \(r_t\). An option to buy the coupon bond at strike \(K\) is exercised if and only if \(r_t\) falls below a critical threshold \(r^*\). Jamshidian's trick decomposes the option on the portfolio into a portfolio of European options on the individual zero-coupon bonds with strikes \(K_i = P(T, T_i; r^*)\), each of which is priced analytically using the forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{T_i}\).

R2 · Heath–Jarrow–Morton forward rate drift condition

How does the forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) simplify the Heath–Jarrow–Morton (HJM) instantaneous forward rate dynamics \(f(t, T)\)?

Solution

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the HJM no-arbitrage condition forces the forward rate drift to be \(\alpha(t, T) = \sigma_f(t, T) \int_t^T \sigma_f(t, u) du\). Under the \(T\)-forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^T\), the instantaneous forward rate maturing at \(T\) becomes a martingale: \(df(t, T) = \sigma_f(t, T) dW_t^T\), completely eliminating the non-linear integral drift term.

R3 · Libor market model under terminal forward measure

State how the Brace–Gatarek–Musiela (BGM) Libor market model expresses discrete forward rates under the terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{T_N}\).

Solution

The Libor market model defines discrete forward rates \(L_k(t) = \frac{P(t, T_k) - P(t, T_{k+1})}{\tau_k P(t, T_{k+1})}\). Under each forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{T_{k+1}}\), \(L_k(t)\) is a driftless martingale. To price complex multi-period interest rate exotics (Bermudan swaptions), all forward rates are transformed to a single universal terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{T_N}\), where each \(L_k(t)\) acquires a state-dependent drift given by the product of covariance terms with downstream forward rates.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. What asset serves as the numeraire for the \(H\)-forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\)?

    Options

    Definition 2.1: The \(H\)-forward measure uses the zero-coupon bond maturing at \(H\) as numeraire.

  2. What is the terminal price \(P(H, H)\) of an \(H\)-maturity zero-coupon bond?

    A zero-coupon bond pays exactly $1 at maturity: \(P(H, H) = 1\).

  3. How does changing to the forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) simplify derivative valuation under stochastic interest rates?

    Options

    Theorem 2.2: Because \(P(H, H) = 1\), the discount factor is factored completely outside the expectation.

  4. Under what measure is the forward price process \(F(t, H) = S_t / P(t, H)\) a martingale?

    Options

    Theorem 2.4: The forward price is the ratio of asset to bond numeraire, so it is a martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}^H\).

  5. If \(S_0 = 90\) and \(P(0, 1) = 0.90\), what is the 1-year forward price \(F(0, 1)\) of the non-dividend-paying stock?

    \(F(0, 1) = S_0 / P(0, 1) = 90 / 0.90 = 100\).

  6. When interest rates are constant (\(r_t \equiv r\)), how does the forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\) relate to the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\)?

    Options

    Problem M2: When rates are constant, \(Z_t^H = \frac{e^{-r(H-t)}}{e^{-rH} e^{rt}} = 1\), so both measures coincide.

  7. What is the Girsanov drift shift applied to Brownian motion when moving from \(\mathbb{Q}\) to the forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^H\)?

    Options

    Problem P2: The bond volatility \(\sigma_P(t, H)\) is the diffusion of the numeraire, giving drift shift \(-\sigma_P\).

  8. Why does Black's formula hold under stochastic interest rates when rates follow a Gaussian model like Vasicek?

    Options

    Theorem 2.5: In Gaussian rate models, forward price volatility is deterministic, preserving lognormality.
