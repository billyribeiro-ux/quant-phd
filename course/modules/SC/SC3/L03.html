The Martingale Representation Theorem and Replicating Portfolios

Derivative pricing relies on the ability to replicate a contingent payoff through dynamic trading in underlying assets. The martingale representation theorem is the mathematical foundation of complete markets: it proves that every martingale with respect to the Brownian filtration can be represented as an Itô integral against the underlying Brownian motion. This guarantees the existence of a self-financing delta-hedging portfolio that perfectly replicates any European derivative.

1Representation of Brownian Martingales

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in [0, H]}, \Prob)\) be the augmented filtered probability space generated by a standard 1-dimensional Brownian motion \((B_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\). That is, \(\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(B_s, 0 \le s \le t) \vee \mathcal{N}\), where \(\mathcal{N}\) contains all \(\Prob\)-null sets.

Theorem 1.1 (Martingale Representation Theorem)

Assumptions: (A1) \((\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\) is the augmented filtration of standard Brownian motion \(B\); (A2) \((M_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\) is a local martingale with respect to \((\mathcal{F}_t, \Prob)\).

Statement: There exists a unique \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-progressively measurable process \((\phi_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\) satisfying \(\Prob(\int_0^H \phi_s^2 ds < \infty) = 1\) such that for all \(t \in [0, H]\):

\[ M_t = M_0 + \int_0^t \phi_s dB_s \quad \text{almost surely}. \]

If \(M_t\) is a square-integrable martingale (\(\sup_{t \le H} \E[M_t^2] < \infty\)), then \(\E[\int_0^H \phi_s^2 ds] = \E[M_H^2] - \E[M_0^2] < \infty\). This result is the martingale representation theorem.

Proof (Sketch via Itô Isometry and Dense Subspaces)

Let \(\mathcal{H}^2\) be the Hilbert space of square-integrable, \(\mathcal{F}_H\)-measurable random variables with inner product \(\langle X, Y \rangle = \E[XY]\). Consider the linear subspace \(\mathcal{I} \subset \mathcal{H}^2\) consisting of stochastic integrals:

\[ \mathcal{I} = \left\{ \int_0^H \phi_s dB_s \;\middle|\; \phi \text{ is adapted and } \E\left[\int_0^H \phi_s^2 ds\right] < \infty \right\}. \]

By the Itô isometry, the mapping \(\phi \mapsto \int_0^H \phi_s dB_s\) is an isometric isomorphism from \(L^2([0, H] \times \Omega)\) onto \(\mathcal{I}\). Consequently, \(\mathcal{I}\) is a closed linear subspace of \(\mathcal{H}^2\).

To prove \(\mathcal{I} = L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_H, \Prob) \ominus \R\), suppose random variable \(\xi \in \mathcal{H}^2\) is orthogonal to \(\mathcal{I}\), meaning \(\E[\xi \int_0^H \phi_s dB_s] = 0\) for all \(\phi\). Choosing exponential martingales \(\phi_s = \lambda \mathcal{E}(\lambda B)_s\) generates the family of random variables \(\exp(\lambda B_H - \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^2 H)\). Because the linear span of such exponentials is dense in \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_H, \Prob)\) (by completeness of Hermite polynomials), orthogonality implies \(\xi = \E[\xi]\) almost surely. Hence any zero-mean square-integrable variable \(M_H - M_0\) belongs to \(\mathcal{I}\). Taking conditional expectations gives \(M_t = M_0 + \int_0^t \phi_s dB_s\). Localization extends the result to local martingales.

2Representation under Equivalent Measure Change

Theorem 1.2 (Martingale Representation under Q)

Assumptions: (A1) \(\mathbb{Q} \sim \Prob\) is an equivalent martingale measure defined by \(d\mathbb{Q}/d\Prob = \mathcal{E}(-\theta \cdot B)_H\); (A2) \(W_t = B_t + \int_0^t \theta_s ds\) is the \(\mathbb{Q}\)-Brownian motion; (A3) \(N_t\) is an \((\mathcal{F}_t, \mathbb{Q})\)-local martingale.

Statement: There exists a unique adapted process \((\psi_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\) such that:

\[ N_t = N_0 + \int_0^t \psi_s dW_s. \]
Proof

Define \(Z_t = \mathcal{E}(-\theta \cdot B)_t\). By the abstract Bayes formula, \(N_t\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-local martingale if and only if \(Y_t \coloneqq Z_t N_t\) is a \(\Prob\)-local martingale. By Theorem 1.1, \(Y_t\) admits an integral representation under \(\Prob\): \(Y_t = Y_0 + \int_0^t \gamma_s dB_s\). Applying Itô's formula to \(N_t = Y_t / Z_t = Y_t Z_t^{-1}\):

\[ dN_t = Z_t^{-1} dY_t + Y_t d(Z_t^{-1}) + d\langle Y, Z^{-1} \rangle_t. \]

Since \(d(Z_t^{-1}) = \theta_t Z_t^{-1} dB_t + \theta_t^2 Z_t^{-1} dt\) and \(d\langle Y, Z^{-1} \rangle_t = \gamma_t (\theta_t Z_t^{-1}) dt\):

\[ dN_t = Z_t^{-1} (\gamma_t dB_t) + Y_t \theta_t Z_t^{-1} dB_t + \dots = Z_t^{-1} (\gamma_t + \theta_t Y_t) (dW_t - \theta_t dt) + \dots \]

Because \(N_t\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-local martingale, all \(dt\) drift terms cancel identically, leaving \(dN_t = \psi_t dW_t\), where \(\psi_t = Z_t^{-1}(\gamma_t + \theta_t Y_t)\).

3Market Completeness and Replicating Portfolios

Let the financial market consist of a money-market account \(B_t^0 = e^{rt}\) and a risky asset \(S_t\) satisfying \(dS_t = r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\). Consider a European contingent claim with payoff \(X \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_H, \mathbb{Q})\) at maturity \(H\).

Definition 1.3 (Replicating Self-Financing Portfolio)

A self-financing trading strategy is a pair of adapted processes \((\Delta_t, \eta_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\) representing holdings in the stock and the money-market account, such that the portfolio value \(\Pi_t = \Delta_t S_t + \eta_t B_t^0\) satisfies the self-financing condition:

\[ d\Pi_t = \Delta_t dS_t + \eta_t dB_t^0 = \Delta_t dS_t + r (\Pi_t - \Delta_t S_t) dt. \]

The portfolio replicates the claim \(X\) if \(\Pi_H = X\) almost surely.

Theorem 1.4 (Completeness of the Black-Scholes Market)

Assumptions: (A1) The market has one risky asset \(dS_t = r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t\) with \(\sigma > 0\); (A2) \(X \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_H, \mathbb{Q})\) is an arbitrary European claim.

Statement: The claim \(X\) can be uniquely replicated by a self-financing portfolio with initial capital:

\[ V_0 = \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH} X] \]

and dynamic stock holding:

\[ \Delta_t = \frac{\psi_t}{\sigma S^*_t} = \frac{e^{rt} \psi_t}{\sigma S_t}, \]

where \(\psi_t\) is the integrand in the martingale representation of the discounted claim value \(V^*_t = \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH} X \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = V_0 + \int_0^t \psi_s dW_s\).

Proof

Define the discounted value process \(V^*_t \coloneqq \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH} X \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). By the tower property, \(V^*_t\) is a square-integrable \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale. By Theorem 1.2, there exists an adapted process \(\psi_t\) such that:

\[ V^*_t = V_0 + \int_0^t \psi_s dW_s. \]

Now consider a self-financing portfolio \(\Pi_t\). Its discounted value \(\Pi^*_t = e^{-rt}\Pi_t\) satisfies:

\[ d\Pi^*_t = d(e^{-rt}\Pi_t) = -r e^{-rt}\Pi_t dt + e^{-rt} d\Pi_t = e^{-rt}(\Delta_t dS_t - r \Delta_t S_t dt) = \Delta_t d(e^{-rt}S_t) = \Delta_t dS^*_t. \]

Substituting \(dS^*_t = \sigma S^*_t dW_t\) (from Lesson 2):

\[ d\Pi^*_t = \Delta_t \sigma S^*_t dW_t \implies \Pi^*_t = \Pi_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_s \sigma S^*_s dW_s. \]

Setting \(\Pi_0 = V_0\) and matching the diffusions \(\Delta_t \sigma S^*_t = \psi_t\) yields \(\Delta_t = \psi_t / (\sigma S^*_t)\). Then \(\Pi^*_t = V^*_t\) for all \(t \in [0, H]\). At maturity \(H\), \(\Pi_H = e^{rH} \Pi^*_H = e^{rH} V^*_H = e^{rH} (e^{-rH} X) = X\) almost surely. Thus the portfolio replicates \(X\) exactly.

Remark 1.5 (Clark–Ocone Formula and Delta Hedging)

When the contingent claim is a smooth function of the terminal asset price \(X = g(S_H)\), Malliavin calculus provides the Clark–Ocone formula: \(\psi_t = e^{-rH} \E^\mathbb{Q}[D_t g(S_H) \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). For vanilla options, this matches the classical Black-Scholes delta: \(\Delta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}(t, S_t)\).

Lab 1 · Discrete delta hedging and martingale representation convergence

By the Martingale Representation Theorem, any square-integrable contingent claim X in the Brownian filtration admits an exact representation as a stochastic integral of an adapted hedging strategy Delta_t = partial V / partial S. In continuous time, the tracking error is identically zero. In discrete time with N rebalancing intervals of size dt = H / N, the hedging error variance Var(Pi_H - (S_H - K)^+) scales as O(1 / N). This lab simulates discrete delta-hedging across N in {10, 40, 160} on 2,000 paths (SIMULATED), verifying that tracking error variance diminishes inversely with the rebalancing step count.

"""Lab 1 · Discrete delta hedging and martingale representation convergence.

By the Martingale Representation Theorem, any square-integrable contingent claim X in the
Brownian filtration admits an exact representation as a stochastic integral of an adapted hedging
strategy Delta_t = partial V / partial S. In continuous time, the tracking error is identically zero.
In discrete time with N rebalancing intervals of size dt = H / N, the hedging error variance
Var(Pi_H - (S_H - K)^+) scales as O(1 / N).
This lab simulates discrete delta-hedging across N in {10, 40, 160} on 2,000 paths (SIMULATED),
verifying that tracking error variance diminishes inversely with the rebalancing step count.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def bs_call_price_and_delta(s: float, k: float, r: float, sigma: float, tau: float) -> tuple[float, float]:
    if tau <= 1e-12:
        val = max(s - k, 0.0)
        delta = 1.0 if s > k else 0.0
        return val, delta
    sq_tau = math.sqrt(tau)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * (sigma**2)) * tau) / (sigma * sq_tau)
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sq_tau
    price = s * norm_cdf(d1) - k * math.exp(-r * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)
    delta = norm_cdf(d1)
    return price, delta


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    s0 = 100.0
    k = 100.0
    r = 0.05
    sigma = 0.20
    horizon = 1.0
    n_paths = 2000

    n_steps_list = [10, 40, 160]
    variances: list[float] = []

    for n_steps in n_steps_list:
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        dt = horizon / n_steps
        sq_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
        drift = (r - 0.5 * (sigma**2)) * dt
        vol = sigma * sq_dt
        df = math.exp(r * dt)

        errors: list[float] = []

        for _ in range(n_paths):
            s = s0
            # Initial portfolio: replicate call
            price0, delta0 = bs_call_price_and_delta(s, k, r, sigma, horizon)
            cash = price0 - delta0 * s
            delta = delta0

            for step in range(n_steps):
                z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
                s = s * math.exp(drift + vol * z)
                tau = max(horizon - (step + 1) * dt, 0.0)
                portfolio_val = delta * s + cash * df
                if step < n_steps - 1:
                    _, new_delta = bs_call_price_and_delta(s, k, r, sigma, tau)
                    cash = portfolio_val - new_delta * s
                    delta = new_delta
                else:
                    payoff = max(s - k, 0.0)
                    errors.append(portfolio_val - payoff)

        mean_err = sum(errors) / n_paths
        var_err = sum((e - mean_err) ** 2 for e in errors) / (n_paths - 1)
        variances.append(var_err)

    ratio_1 = variances[0] / variances[1]
    ratio_2 = variances[1] / variances[2]

    print(f"S0: {s0:.1f}, K: {k:.1f}, r: {r:.2f}, sigma: {sigma:.2f}, H: {horizon:.1f}")
    for n_steps, var in zip(n_steps_list, variances, strict=True):
        print(f"N = {n_steps:3d} rebalancing steps: tracking variance = {var:.6f}")
    print(f"variance ratio N=10 to N=40: {ratio_1:.2f} (theoretical ~4.0)")
    print(f"variance ratio N=40 to N=160: {ratio_2:.2f} (theoretical ~4.0)")
    print("variance monotonically decreasing with N:", variances[0] > variances[1] > variances[2])


main()
S0: 100.0, K: 100.0, r: 0.05, sigma: 0.20, H: 1.0
N =  10 rebalancing steps: tracking variance = 4.158689
N =  40 rebalancing steps: tracking variance = 1.209338
N = 160 rebalancing steps: tracking variance = 0.285776
variance ratio N=10 to N=40: 3.44 (theoretical ~4.0)
variance ratio N=40 to N=160: 4.23 (theoretical ~4.0)
variance monotonically decreasing with N: True

In continuous time, the Martingale Representation Theorem guarantees zero tracking error. On a discrete grid, rebalancing delay introduces an error proportional to \(\Delta t = H / N\). As \(N\) quadruples from 10 to 40, the tracking error variance drops from 4.158689 to 1.209338 (a factor of 3.44x, close to the theoretical ratio of 4.0x). Quadrupling again to 160 reduces variance to 0.285776 (a factor of 4.23x), confirming \(O(1/N)\) convergence.

4Where the canon breaks

The Martingale Representation Theorem requires that the filtration \((\mathcal{F}_t)\) is strictly generated by the Brownian motion \(B_t\) alone. If the filtration contains additional sources of randomness—such as discrete Poisson jump times, an independent unobservable Markov regime-switching state, or initial private information not generated by \(B\)—the representation fails. A general martingale \(M_t\) will then decompose into \(M_t = M_0 + \int_0^t \phi_s dB_s + L_t\), where \(L_t\) is a martingale orthogonal to \(B\) (\(\langle L, B \rangle_t = 0\)). The orthogonal component \(L_t\) represents unhedgeable basis risk that cannot be eliminated by trading the underlying asset, breaking market completeness.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Any continuous martingale can be represented as an integral against Brownian motion in any filtration.”

    The representation theorem holds if and only if the filtration is the augmented natural filtration of the driving Brownian motion. If the filtration is larger (e.g. including independent shocks or external filtration enlargements), orthogonal martingales exist that cannot be represented as Itô integrals against \(B\).

  2. “Delta hedging with discrete rebalancing perfectly eliminates risk.”

    Continuous-time replication is an idealization. In practice, discrete rebalancing incurs tracking error variance of order \(O(\Delta t) = O(1/N)\), along with transaction costs and gamma risk between rebalancing dates.

  3. “The hedging strategy \(\Delta_t\) depends on the investor's risk aversion.”

    Because the Black-Scholes market is complete, the replicating strategy \(\Delta_t = \psi_t / (\sigma S^*_t)\) is unique and pathwise identical for all market participants, completely independent of risk preferences or utility functions.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Representation of squared Brownian motion

The process \(M_t = B_t^2 - t\) is a continuous-time martingale. Express \(M_t\) explicitly in the form \(M_0 + \int_0^t \phi_s dB_s\) and identify \(\phi_s\).

Solution

Applying Itô's lemma to \(f(t, B_t) = B_t^2 - t\): \(\partial_t f = -1\), \(\partial_b f = 2 B_t\), and \(\partial_{bb} f = 2\). The differential is: \(dM_t = \partial_t f dt + \partial_b f dB_t + \tfrac{1}{2} \partial_{bb} f (dB_t)^2 = -1 dt + 2 B_t dB_t + \tfrac{1}{2}(2)dt = 2 B_t dB_t\). Since \(M_0 = 0\), integrating yields \(M_t = \int_0^t 2 B_s dB_s\). The integrand is \(\phi_s = 2 B_s\).

M2 · Representation of geometric Brownian motion

Let \(S_t = S_0 \exp((r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t + \sigma W_t)\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\). Find the integrand \(\psi_s\) in the representation of the discounted martingale \(S^*_t = S_0 + \int_0^t \psi_s dW_s\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.1 in Lesson 2, the discounted asset satisfies \(dS^*_t = \sigma S^*_t dW_t\). Since \(S^*_0 = S_0\), integrating directly gives: \(S^*_t = S_0 + \int_0^t \sigma S^*_s dW_s\). The integrand is \(\psi_s = \sigma S^*_s = \sigma e^{-rs} S_s\).

M3 · Forward contract replication

A forward contract has payoff \(X = S_H - K\) at maturity \(H\). Determine the replicating strategy \((\Delta_t, \eta_t)\) and initial capital \(V_0\).

Solution

The discounted value process is \(V^*_t = \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH}(S_H - K) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \E^\mathbb{Q}[e^{-rH} S_H \mid \mathcal{F}_t] - K e^{-rH} = S^*_t - K e^{-rH}\). The differential is \(dV^*_t = dS^*_t = \sigma S^*_t dW_t\). Matching with \(\Delta_t \sigma S^*_t dW_t\) gives \(\Delta_t = 1\) share for all \(t \in [0, H]\). The money-market position is \(\eta_t B_t^0 = \Pi_t - \Delta_t S_t = (S_t - K e^{-r(H-t)}) - S_t = -K e^{-r(H-t)}\). The initial capital is \(V_0 = S_0 - K e^{-rH}\).

P1 · Uniqueness of the representation integrand

Prove that if a martingale admits two representations \(M_t = M_0 + \int_0^t \phi_s^{(1)} dB_s = M_0 + \int_0^t \phi_s^{(2)} dB_s\), then \(\phi^{(1)} = \phi^{(2)}\) almost everywhere on \([0, H] \times \Omega\).

Solution

Subtracting the two representations gives \(\int_0^H (\phi_s^{(1)} - \phi_s^{(2)}) dB_s = 0\) almost surely. Taking the expected square and applying the Itô isometry: \(0 = \E[(\int_0^H (\phi_s^{(1)} - \phi_s^{(2)}) dB_s)^2] = \E[\int_0^H (\phi_s^{(1)} - \phi_s^{(2)})^2 ds]\). Because the integrand is non-negative, its integral over \([0, H] \times \Omega\) is zero if and only if \(\phi_s^{(1)}(\omega) = \phi_s^{(2)}(\omega)\) for \(ds \times d\Prob\)-almost every \((s, \omega)\). The representation is unique.

P2 · Self-financing invariance under discounting

Prove that a trading strategy \((\Delta_t, \eta_t)\) is self-financing in undiscounted terms (\(d\Pi_t = \Delta_t dS_t + \eta_t dB_t^0\)) if and only if \(d\Pi^*_t = \Delta_t dS^*_t\) in discounted terms.

Solution

Let \(\Pi_t = \Delta_t S_t + \eta_t B_t^0\) with \(B_t^0 = e^{rt}\). By definition, \(\Pi^*_t = e^{-rt}\Pi_t\). Applying Itô's product rule: \(d\Pi^*_t = d(e^{-rt}\Pi_t) = -r e^{-rt}\Pi_t dt + e^{-rt}d\Pi_t\). Substitute \(\Pi_t = \Delta_t S_t + \eta_t e^{rt}\): \(d\Pi^*_t = -r e^{-rt}(\Delta_t S_t + \eta_t e^{rt})dt + e^{-rt}d\Pi_t = -r \Delta_t S^*_t dt - r \eta_t dt + e^{-rt}d\Pi_t\). Under the self-financing condition, \(d\Pi_t = \Delta_t dS_t + \eta_t r e^{rt} dt\). Substituting this gives: \(d\Pi^*_t = -r \Delta_t S^*_t dt - r \eta_t dt + e^{-rt}(\Delta_t dS_t + r \eta_t e^{rt}dt) = \Delta_t(e^{-rt}dS_t - r e^{-rt} S_t dt) = \Delta_t d(e^{-rt}S_t) = \Delta_t dS^*_t\). The converse holds by reversing the substitution.

P3 · Representation of terminal indicator

For a digital call option with payoff \(X = \indic_{S_H > K}\), prove that the integrand in \(V^*_t = V_0 + \int_0^t \psi_s dW_s\) is \(\psi_t = e^{-rH} \frac{n(d_2)}{\sqrt{H-t}}\), where \(n(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}\).

Solution

The discounted value is \(V^*_t = e^{-rH} \E^\mathbb{Q}[\indic_{S_H > K} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = e^{-rH} \Phi(d_2(S_t, H-t))\), where \(d_2(s, \tau) = \frac{\log(s/K) + (r - \sigma^2/2)\tau}{\sigma \sqrt{\tau}}\). Applying Itô's lemma to \(V^*(t, S_t)\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\): since \(V^*\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale, its \(dt\) drift is zero. Its diffusion is: \(\frac{\partial V^*}{\partial S} \sigma S_t dW_t\). Differentiating: \(\frac{\partial V^*}{\partial S} = e^{-rH} n(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial S} = e^{-rH} n(d_2) \frac{1}{\sigma S_t \sqrt{H-t}}\). Multiplying by \(\sigma S_t\) gives the diffusion coefficient: \(\psi_t = \frac{\partial V^*}{\partial S} \sigma S_t = e^{-rH} \frac{n(d_2)}{\sqrt{H-t}}\).

Empirical

E1 · Tracking error variance vs rebalancing frequency

Suppose an options desk hedges a European call with \(H = 0.5\) years using daily rebalancing (\(N = 126\) steps) versus weekly rebalancing (\(N = 26\) steps). By what theoretical factor does the daily hedging tracking error variance decrease?

Solution

As established in Lab 1, the discrete delta hedging error variance scales as \(O(1/N)\). The ratio of rebalancing frequencies is \(126 / 26 \approx 4.85\). Therefore, daily hedging reduces the tracking error variance by a factor of approximately 4.85x (standard deviation reduced by \(\sqrt{4.85} \approx 2.20\)).

E2 · Gamma risk and discrete hedging error

Show that the local tracking error of discrete delta hedging over interval \(\Delta t\) is proportional to the option's Gamma \(\Gamma_t = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\).

Solution

Over interval \([t, t+\Delta t]\), Taylor expanding the option price around \(S_t\): \(V(t+\Delta t, S_{t+\Delta t}) - V(t, S_t) \approx \partial_t V \Delta t + \Delta_t \Delta S + \tfrac{1}{2} \Gamma_t (\Delta S)^2\). The hedging portfolio gains \(\Delta_t \Delta S + r(\Pi_t - \Delta_t S_t)\Delta t\). By the Black-Scholes PDE, \(\partial_t V + r S_t \Delta_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S_t^2 \Gamma_t = r V\). The net tracking error increment is: \(\Delta \epsilon \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \Gamma_t [(\Delta S)^2 - \sigma^2 S_t^2 \Delta t]\). The variance of \(\Delta \epsilon\) is \(\Var(\Delta \epsilon) \approx \tfrac{1}{4} \Gamma_t^2 \Var((\Delta S)^2) = \tfrac{1}{2} \Gamma_t^2 \sigma^4 S_t^4 (\Delta t)^2\). Discrete hedging error is directly driven by Gamma.

E3 · Transaction costs vs hedging error trade-off

If proportional transaction costs are \(c\) per dollar traded, how does total cost scale with rebalancing step count \(N\)?

Solution

Hedging error standard deviation decreases as \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\). However, total variation of the delta hedge \(\sum_{k=1}^N |\Delta_{k+1} - \Delta_k|\) scales as \(O(\sqrt{N})\) because Brownian increments scale as \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\). Thus transaction costs grow as \(O(c \sqrt{N})\). The optimal rebalancing frequency balances \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\) tracking error against \(O(c\sqrt{N})\) transaction costs (Leland 1985).

Research

R1 · Second Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing

State the Second Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing and connect it to the Martingale Representation Theorem.

Solution

The Second Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (Harrison & Pliska 1981) asserts that an arbitrage-free market is complete (every contingent claim can be replicated) if and only if the equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is unique. When \(\mathbb{Q}\) is unique, the Brownian filtration is strictly generated by the tradable assets, and the Martingale Representation Theorem guarantees that every \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale can be represented as an Itô integral with respect to discounted asset prices.

R2 · Föllmer–Schweizer decomposition in incomplete markets

Explain the Föllmer–Schweizer decomposition for contingent claims in incomplete diffusion markets.

Solution

In an incomplete market, a contingent claim \(X \in L^2\) cannot be replicated exactly. The Föllmer–Schweizer decomposition represents \(X\) as \(X = V_0 + \int_0^H \Delta_s dS_s + L_H\), where \(\int_0^t \Delta_s dS_s\) is a tradable gain process and \(L_t\) is a square-integrable martingale orthogonal to the asset price process (\(\langle L, S \rangle = 0\)). The strategy \(\Delta_s\) minimizes the remaining unhedgeable intrinsic risk \(\E[L_H^2]\).

R3 · Clark–Ocone formula under measure change

How does the Clark–Ocone formula generalize when the probability measure is changed via Girsanov's theorem?

Solution

Under physical measure \(\Prob\), the standard Clark–Ocone formula represents \(F = \E[F] + \int_0^H \E[D_t F \mid \mathcal{F}_t] dB_t\). Under an equivalent measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) with density \(Z_H = \mathcal{E}(-\theta \cdot B)_H\), Karatzas, Ocone and Li (1991) proved that \(\psi_t = \E^\mathbb{Q}[D_t X - X \int_t^H D_t \theta_s dW_s \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). When \(\theta\) is constant, the second term vanishes, reducing to \(\psi_t = \E^\mathbb{Q}[D_t X \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\).
