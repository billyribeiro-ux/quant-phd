Standard Brownian motion can take negative values, making it unviable as a direct model for limited-liability asset prices. Geometric Brownian motion resolves this by specifying that percentage price changes, rather than dollar changes, follow a diffusion. Applying Itô's lemma to the logarithmic transform yields the exact closed-form solution with its fundamental convexity correction \(-\sigma^2/2\). This process forms the canonical baseline of continuous-time asset pricing, derivatives valuation, and quantitative risk management.

1Definition and the Logarithmic Transformation

Definition 1.1 (Geometric Brownian Motion)

A stochastic process \((S_t)_{t \ge 0}\) on a filtered probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t), \Prob)\) is a geometric Brownian motion (GBM) with drift \(\mu \in \R\), volatility \(\sigma > 0\), and initial price \(S_0 = s_0 > 0\) if it satisfies the stochastic differential equation:

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dB_t, \quad S_0 = s_0 > 0. \]
Theorem 1.2 (Explicit Solution via Itô's Lemma)

Assumptions: (A1) \(S_t\) satisfies \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\) with \(S_0 = s_0 > 0\); (A2) \(S_t > 0\) almost surely for all \(t \ge 0\).

Statement: The unique strong solution to the geometric Brownian motion SDE is:

\[ S_t = s_0 \exp\left( \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma B_t \right). \]
Proof

Define \(f(x) = \log x\) on domain \(x \in (0, \infty)\). The function is twice continuously differentiable with derivatives \(f'(x) = 1/x\) and \(f''(x) = -1/x^2\). Define \(Y_t = f(S_t) = \log S_t\). By Itô's lemma (Theorem 1.3 of Lesson 3), with drift \(a_t = \mu S_t\) and diffusion \(b_t = \sigma S_t\):

\[ d(\log S_t) = \left( 0 + \mu S_t \frac{1}{S_t} + \frac{1}{2} (\sigma S_t)^2 \left(-\frac{1}{S_t^2}\right) \right) dt + \sigma S_t \frac{1}{S_t} \, dB_t. \]

The state variable \(S_t\) cancels out completely in both terms:

\[ d(\log S_t) = \left( \mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \right) dt + \sigma \, dB_t. \]

Integrating from \(0\) to \(t\):

\[ \log S_t - \log S_0 = \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma (B_t - B_0) = \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma B_t. \]

Exponentiating both sides yields the stated formula:

\[ S_t = s_0 \exp\left( \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma B_t \right). \]

Since the exponential map is strictly positive for all real exponents, \(S_t > 0\) almost surely for all \(t \ge 0\), validating assumption A2 retroactively.

2Moments and Probability Law of the Asset Price

Theorem 2.1 (Moments of Geometric Brownian Motion)

Assumptions: (A1) \(S_t = s_0 \exp((\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma B_t)\) with \(s_0 > 0, \sigma > 0\); (A2) \(B_t \sim \Normal(0, t)\) by the normal distribution.

Statement: The expected value, second moment, and variance of \(S_t\) are given in closed form by:

\[ \E[S_t] = s_0 e^{\mu t}, \quad \E[S_t^2] = s_0^2 e^{(2\mu + \sigma^2)t}, \quad \Var(S_t) = s_0^2 e^{2\mu t}(e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1). \]

More generally, for any real power \(p \in \R\):

\[ \E[S_t^p] = s_0^p \exp\left( \left( p\mu + \frac{1}{2}p(p-1)\sigma^2 \right) t \right). \]
Proof

For any normal variable \(Z \sim \Normal(0, \sigma^2 t)\), its moment generating function is \(\E[e^{u Z}] = \exp(\tfrac{1}{2} u^2 \sigma^2 t)\). Setting \(u = 1\) on \(Z = \sigma B_t\):

\[ \E[e^{\sigma B_t}] = e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t}. \]

Using the closed-form solution for \(S_t\):

\[ \E[S_t] = \E\left[ s_0 e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t} e^{\sigma B_t} \right] = s_0 e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t} \E[e^{\sigma B_t}] = s_0 e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t} e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t} = s_0 e^{\mu t}. \]

Notice that the convexity correction \(-\sigma^2/2\) in the exponent exactly cancels the moment-generating boost \(+\sigma^2/2\), yielding the expected return \(\mu\).

For \(S_t^2 = s_0^2 e^{(2\mu - \sigma^2)t} e^{2\sigma B_t}\), applying \(\E[e^{2\sigma B_t}] = e^{\frac{1}{2}(2\sigma)^2 t} = e^{2\sigma^2 t}\):

\[ \E[S_t^2] = s_0^2 e^{(2\mu - \sigma^2)t} e^{2\sigma^2 t} = s_0^2 e^{(2\mu + \sigma^2)t}. \]

The variance follows from \(\Var(S_t) = \E[S_t^2] - (\E[S_t])^2 = s_0^2 e^{(2\mu + \sigma^2)t} - s_0^2 e^{2\mu t} = s_0^2 e^{2\mu t}(e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1)\).

For arbitrary power \(p\), \(S_t^p = s_0^p \exp(p(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + p\sigma B_t)\). Evaluating the expectation via \(\E[e^{p\sigma B_t}] = \exp(\tfrac{1}{2}p^2 \sigma^2 t)\):

\[ \E[S_t^p] = s_0^p \exp\left( p\left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \frac{1}{2}p^2\sigma^2 t \right) = s_0^p \exp\left( \left(p\mu + \frac{1}{2}p(p-1)\sigma^2\right)t \right). \]
Remark 2.2 (Median Path versus Mean Path)

The median of the log-normal distribution corresponds to the realization where \(B_t = 0\):

\[ \operatorname{Median}(S_t) = s_0 \exp\left(\left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t\right). \]

Because \(\sigma^2 > 0\), the median path grows at rate \(\mu - \sigma^2/2\), which is strictly lower than the expected path growth rate \(\mu\). In long horizons, almost every sample trajectory grows at the geometric rate \(\mu - \sigma^2/2\) by the strong law of large numbers for Brownian motion (\(B_t/t \to 0\) a.s.), while the expectation \(\E[S_t] = s_0 e^{\mu t}\) is driven by an exponentially vanishing fraction of extremely high-valued paths.

Lab 1 · Geometric Brownian motion: exact closed form versus Euler discretization

For GBM with \(s_0 = 100\), \(\mu = 0.05\), \(\sigma = 0.20\), and \(H = 1.0\), the theoretical expectation is \(s_0 e^{\mu H} = 100 e^{0.05} \approx 105.127110\). A Monte Carlo sample of 5,000 paths (SIMULATED) produces a sample mean within 5 standard errors of theory, and reveals that discrete Euler simulation on prices exhibits measurable discretization bias relative to the exact log-solution.

"""Lab 1 · Geometric Brownian motion: exact closed form versus Euler discretization.

The geometric Brownian motion dS_t = mu S_t dt + sigma S_t dB_t has the exact Itô solution S_t = s0 exp((mu -
sigma^2/2)t + sigma B_t), with mean E[S_t] = s0 exp(mu t). The standard Euler discretization S_{k+1} = S_k(1 + mu
Delta t + sigma Delta B_k) introduces discretization bias on discrete price paths. This lab simulates 5,000 paths on a
50-step grid over [0, 1] with s0 = 100, mu = 0.05, sigma = 0.20 from seed 42 (SIMULATED), comparing exact pathwise
terminals with Euler terminals and verifying the closed-form expectation against Monte Carlo standard error.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    s0 = 100.0
    mu = 0.05
    sigma = 0.20
    horizon = 1.0
    n_steps = 50
    dt = horizon / n_steps
    sq_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
    n_paths = 5000
    seed = 42

    exact_drift = (mu - 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * dt
    exact_vol = sigma * sq_dt

    rng = random.Random(seed)
    exact_terminals: list[float] = []
    euler_terminals: list[float] = []
    abs_errors: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(n_paths):
        s_exact = s0
        s_euler = s0
        for _ in range(n_steps):
            z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            db = sq_dt * z
            s_exact *= math.exp(exact_drift + exact_vol * z)
            s_euler += mu * s_euler * dt + sigma * s_euler * db
        exact_terminals.append(s_exact)
        euler_terminals.append(s_euler)
        abs_errors.append(abs(s_exact - s_euler))

    theory_mean = s0 * math.exp(mu * horizon)
    sample_mean_exact = sum(exact_terminals) / n_paths
    var_exact = sum((x - sample_mean_exact) ** 2 for x in exact_terminals) / (n_paths - 1)
    se_exact = math.sqrt(var_exact / n_paths)

    sample_mean_euler = sum(euler_terminals) / n_paths
    mae = sum(abs_errors) / n_paths

    print(f"theoretical expectation E[S_H]: {theory_mean:.6f}")
    print(f"exact solution sample mean: {sample_mean_exact:.6f} (se {se_exact:.6f})")
    print(f"Euler approximation sample mean: {sample_mean_euler:.6f}")
    print(f"mean absolute pathwise error: {mae:.6f}")
    print("exact sample mean within 5*se of theory:", abs(sample_mean_exact - theory_mean) <= 5.0 * se_exact)


main()

exact solution sample mean: 105.202713 (se 0.299538)
Euler approximation sample mean: 105.192708
mean absolute pathwise error: 0.336860

The exact simulation produces sample mean 105.2027 (se 0.2995), well within 1 standard error of the closed-form expectation 105.1271. The mean absolute error between Euler and exact price paths is 0.3369. Simulating GBM by updating the price \(S_{k+1} = S_k(1 + \mu \Delta t + \sigma \Delta B_k)\) incurs discretization bias, whereas simulating the log-price \(\log S_{k+1} = \log S_k + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)\Delta t + \sigma \Delta B_k\) is exact on any grid.

3Where the canon breaks

The standard geometric Brownian motion hypothesis assumes constant drift \(\mu\) and constant volatility \(\sigma\). Under this assumption, the distribution of log-returns \(\log(S_{t+\Delta t}/S_t)\) is strictly Gaussian with kurtosis 3, and implied volatilities across all option strikes must be identical (a flat volatility surface). In real market data, this hypothesis fails systematically: observed equity return distributions exhibit negative skewness and heavy tails (leptokurtosis), and options display pronounced volatility smiles and skews. When asset prices experience sudden discrete jumps (e.g. earnings announcements or macroeconomic shocks), the continuous path assumption of GBM breaks down entirely, necessitating jump-diffusion models (Merton) or stochastic volatility diffusions (Heston).

4Misconceptions

  1. “A typical stock path grows at the expected return \(\mu\).”

    By the strong law of large numbers, \(\lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{1}{t} \log(S_t/s_0) = \mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\) almost surely. Almost every single realized trajectory grows at the lower rate \(\mu - \sigma^2/2\). The ensemble expectation \(\E[S_t] = s_0 e^{\mu t}\) is skewed upward by rare paths with massive exponential payoffs.

  2. “Euler–Maruyama simulation of the price SDE \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\) is numerically exact.”

    Euler discretization of the price SDE is only an approximation with strong convergence order 1/2. On large time steps or volatile assets, Euler steps can even produce negative prices \(S_{k+1} < 0\) if \(\Delta B_k < -(1 + \mu \Delta t)/\sigma\). To simulate GBM exactly and preserve strict positivity on any grid, one must discretize the linear SDE for \(\log S_t\).

  3. “Because \(S_t = s_0 e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma B_t}\), its percentage returns are normally distributed.”

    Percentage returns \((S_{t+\Delta t} - S_t)/S_t = e^{\Delta \log S} - 1\) are not normally distributed; they follow a shifted log-normal distribution. It is the log-returns \(\log(S_{t+\Delta t}/S_t)\) that are normally distributed.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Closed-form moment calculation

Let \(S_0 = 50\), \(\mu = 0.08\), and \(\sigma = 0.25\). Compute the exact expected price \(\E[S_2]\) and variance \(\Var(S_2)\) at horizon \(H = 2\) years.

Solution

Expected price: \(\E[S_2] = s_0 e^{\mu H} = 50 e^{0.08 \times 2} = 50 e^{0.16} \approx 50 \times 1.173511 = 58.67554\). For variance: \(\Var(S_2) = s_0^2 e^{2\mu H}(e^{\sigma^2 H} - 1) = (58.67554)^2 (e^{0.25^2 \times 2} - 1) = (3442.82) (e^{0.125} - 1)\). Since \(e^{0.125} - 1 \approx 0.133148\), \(\Var(S_2) \approx 3442.82 \times 0.133148 \approx 458.41\).

M2 · Median vs mean price divergence

For \(S_0 = 100\), \(\mu = 0.10\), and \(\sigma = 0.30\), compute the expected price \(\E[S_{10}]\) and median price \(\operatorname{Median}(S_{10})\) at \(t = 10\) years.

Solution

Expected price: \(\E[S_{10}] = 100 e^{0.10 \times 10} = 100 e^1 \approx 271.83\). Median price: \(\operatorname{Median}(S_{10}) = 100 \exp((0.10 - 0.5 \times 0.09) \times 10) = 100 \exp(0.055 \times 10) = 100 e^{0.55} \approx 173.33\). The expected price is 56.8% higher than the median price due to positive skewness.

M3 · Reciprocal process dynamics

Apply Itô's lemma to the reciprocal price \(Y_t = 1 / S_t\). Identify the drift and diffusion coefficients of \(d(1/S_t)\).

Solution

Let \(f(s) = 1/s\). Then \(f'(s) = -1/s^2\) and \(f''(s) = 2/s^3\). By Itô's lemma:

\[ dY_t = \left( \mu S_t \left(-\frac{1}{S_t^2}\right) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S_t^2 \left(\frac{2}{S_t^3}\right) \right) dt + \sigma S_t \left(-\frac{1}{S_t^2}\right) dB_t = \frac{\sigma^2 - \mu}{S_t} dt - \frac{\sigma}{S_t} dB_t. \]

In terms of \(Y_t\): \(dY_t = (\sigma^2 - \mu) Y_t dt - \sigma Y_t dB_t\). The reciprocal process is also a geometric Brownian motion with drift \(\sigma^2 - \mu\) and volatility \(-\sigma\).

P1 · Strict positivity of GBM paths

Prove that if \(S_0 = s_0 > 0\), then \(\Prob(S_t > 0 \text{ for all } t \in [0, H]) = 1\).

Solution

By Theorem 1.2, \(S_t = s_0 \exp((\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma B_t)\). Since standard Brownian motion \(B_t(\omega)\) has almost surely continuous sample paths on \([0, H]\), the function \(t \mapsto (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma B_t(\omega)\) is continuous and bounded on \([0, H]\) for almost all \(\omega\). Because \(\exp(x) > 0\) for all \(x \in \R\), \(S_t(\omega) > 0\) for all \(t \in [0, H]\) almost surely.

P2 · Discounted GBM as a martingale

Let \(S_t\) be a GBM with drift \(\mu = r\) (risk-neutral drift). Prove that the discounted asset price \(S_t^* = e^{-rt} S_t\) is a continuous martingale.

Solution

Apply the Itô product rule to \(S_t^* = f(t, S_t) = e^{-rt} S_t\). Here \(\partial_t f = -r e^{-rt} S_t\), \(\partial_s f = e^{-rt}\), and \(\partial_{ss} f = 0\). Using \(dS_t = r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\):

\[ dS_t^* = \left( -r e^{-rt} S_t + r S_t e^{-rt} + 0 \right) dt + \sigma S_t e^{-rt} dB_t = \sigma S_t^* \, dB_t. \]

Since \(S_t^*\) is driftless and \(\E[\int_0^H (\sigma S_s^*)^2 ds] = \sigma^2 s_0^2 \int_0^H e^{-2rs} e^{(2r+\sigma^2)s} ds = \sigma^2 s_0^2 (e^{\sigma^2 H}-1)/\sigma^2 < \infty\), \(S_t^*\) is a square-integrable martingale.

P3 · Probability of price doubling before halving

For standard geometric Brownian motion without drift (\(\mu = 0\)), compute the probability that the price doubles (\(S_t = 2 s_0\)) before it halves (\(S_t = s_0/2\)).

Solution

Consider the process \(Y_t = \log(S_t / s_0) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t + \sigma B_t\). If we instead use the discounted martingale \(S_t\) under \(\mu = 0\), \(S_t\) is a continuous martingale. Let \(\tau = \tau_u \wedge \tau_d\) where \(\tau_u = \inf\{t : S_t = 2 s_0\}\) and \(\tau_d = \inf\{t : S_t = s_0 / 2\}\). By the optional stopping theorem on the bounded martingale \(S_{t \wedge \tau}\):

\[ \E[S_\tau] = S_0 = s_0. \]

Let \(p = \Prob(S_\tau = 2 s_0)\). Then \(\E[S_\tau] = 2 s_0 p + \frac{s_0}{2}(1 - p) = s_0\). Dividing by \(s_0\): \(2p + \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}p = 1 \implies \tfrac{3}{2}p = \tfrac{1}{2} \implies p = 1/3\). The probability of doubling before halving is exactly \(1/3\).

Empirical

E1 · Probability of negative terminal return

For a stock with \(S_0 = 100\), \(\mu = 0.08\), and \(\sigma = 0.20\), compute the exact probability that the price at \(H = 1\) is below \(S_0\), i.e., \(\Prob(S_1 < 100)\).

Solution

\(S_1 < 100 \iff \log(S_1 / 100) < 0 \iff (\mu - \sigma^2/2)(1) + \sigma B_1 < 0\). Here \(\mu - \sigma^2/2 = 0.08 - 0.02 = 0.06\). The inequality is \(0.06 + 0.20 B_1 < 0 \iff B_1 < -0.06 / 0.20 = -0.30\). Since \(B_1 \sim \Normal(0, 1)\), \(\Prob(B_1 < -0.30) = \Phi(-0.30) \approx 0.3821 = 38.21\%\).

E2 · Negative price probability in discrete Euler scheme

Suppose an analyst uses a single Euler step \(\Delta t = 1.0\) to simulate \(S_1 = S_0(1 + \mu \Delta t + \sigma \Delta B)\) with \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 0.40\). What is the probability that \(S_1 \le 0\)?

Solution

\(S_1 \le 0 \iff 1 + 0.40 \Delta B \le 0 \iff \Delta B \le -1 / 0.40 = -2.5\). Since \(\Delta B \sim \Normal(0, 1)\), \(\Prob(\Delta B \le -2.5) = \Phi(-2.5) \approx 0.0062 = 0.62\%\). The discrete Euler simulation generates an unphysical negative stock price in 62 out of 10,000 paths.

E3 · Volatility drag calculation

Compare the expected annual return \(\mu = 10\%\) with the median growth rate \(\mu - \sigma^2/2\) for volatilities \(\sigma \in \{0.10, 0.20, 0.40, 0.80\}\).

Solution

The "volatility drag" is \(\sigma^2 / 2\): For \(\sigma = 0.10\): drag is \(0.01 / 2 = 0.005\) (50 bps), median growth is \(9.5\%\). For \(\sigma = 0.20\): drag is \(0.04 / 2 = 0.020\) (200 bps), median growth is \(8.0\%\). For \(\sigma = 0.40\): drag is \(0.16 / 2 = 0.080\) (800 bps), median growth is \(2.0\%\). For \(\sigma = 0.80\): drag is \(0.64 / 2 = 0.320\) (3200 bps), median growth is \(-22.0\%\). At \(\sigma = 0.80\), despite a positive expected return of +10%, typical paths decline at 22% per year.

Research

R1 · Black–Scholes–Merton connection

Explain how the GBM specification for the underlying stock leads directly to the Black–Scholes partial differential equation for European call options via delta-hedging.

Solution

Consider a portfolio \(V_t = C(t, S_t) - \Delta_t S_t\). Applying Itô's lemma to \(C(t, S_t)\) gives \(dC = (\partial_t C + \mu S \partial_s C + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \partial_{ss} C)dt + \sigma S \partial_s C dB_t\). Setting \(\Delta_t = \partial_s C\) eliminates the Brownian noise \(dB_t\), making the portfolio locally riskless: \(dV_t = (\partial_t C + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \partial_{ss} C)dt\). To prevent arbitrage, \(dV_t = r V_t dt = r (C - S \partial_s C)dt\). Equating drift coefficients yields the Black–Scholes PDE: \(\partial_t C + r S \partial_s C + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \partial_{ss} C - r C = 0\).

R2 · Local volatility and Dupire's formula

If the constant volatility \(\sigma\) in GBM is replaced by a deterministic local volatility function \(\sigma(t, S)\), state how Dupire's formula recovers \(\sigma(T, K)\) from market call option prices \(C(T, K)\).

Solution

Dupire's formula expresses local variance directly in terms of market option price derivatives:

\[ \sigma^2(T, K) = \frac{\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} + r K \frac{\partial C}{\partial K}}{\frac{1}{2} K^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}}. \]

This allows exact calibration of a diffusion model to observed market volatility smiles.

R3 · CEV model interpolation between GBM and normal diffusion

The Constant Elasticity of Variance (CEV) model specifies \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t^\gamma dB_t\). Contrast the boundary behavior at \(S = 0\) for \(\gamma = 1\) (GBM) versus \(\gamma < 1\).

Solution

When \(\gamma = 1\), the origin \(S = 0\) is a natural boundary that is unreachable in finite time (\(S_t > 0\) a.s. for all \(t\)). When \(\gamma < 1\) (such as \(\gamma = 1/2\) in the square-root diffusion), the origin is an exit or regular boundary: the process can reach zero in finite time with positive probability. If zero is treated as an absorbing boundary, the model naturally incorporates bankruptcy risk.

7Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the exact closed-form solution to the geometric Brownian motion SDE \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 1.2, applying Itô's lemma to \(\log S_t\) generates the Itô correction \(-\sigma^2/2\).

  2. If \(S_0 = 100\), \(\mu = 0.09531\), and \(H = 1.0\), what is the expected price \(\E[S_1]\)? (Use \(100 e^{0.09531} = 110\).)

    \(\E[S_H] = s_0 e^{\mu H} = 100 e^{0.09531} = 110\).

  3. What rate governs the long-term growth of almost every individual path of geometric Brownian motion?

    Options

    By the strong law of large numbers for Brownian motion (\(B_t/t \to 0\)), \(\frac{1}{t}\log S_t \to \mu - \sigma^2/2\) almost surely.

  4. What is the probability that a geometric Brownian motion starting at \(s_0 > 0\) ever touches or crosses zero in finite time?

    Because the exponential function is strictly positive, \(\Prob(S_t \le 0) = 0\) for all \(t \ge 0\).

  5. Why does Euler–Maruyama discretization of \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\) introduce discretization bias?

    Options

    The Euler update \(S_{k+1} = S_k(1 + \mu \Delta t + \sigma \Delta B_k)\) truncates the true exponential update \(S_k \exp((\mu-\sigma^2/2)\Delta t + \sigma \Delta B_k)\).

  6. Which transformation of a geometric Brownian motion produces an exact linear SDE with additive noise?

    Options

    By Theorem 1.2, \(d(\log S_t) = (\mu - \sigma^2/2)dt + \sigma dB_t\), which has constant coefficients and additive noise.

  7. If an asset has volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), how many basis points of volatility drag (\(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\)) reduce the median growth rate below the expected return \(\mu\)?

    \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.04) = 0.02 = 200\) basis points.

  8. What is the probability distribution of \(\log(S_t / S_0)\) under geometric Brownian motion?

    Options

    Log-returns are normally distributed with mean \((\mu - \sigma^2/2)t\) and variance \(\sigma^2 t\).
