Itô Isometry and the L2 Extension

  • Uses: Itô integral, conditional expectation as an L2 projection, Lebesgue integral, Cauchy sequence, completeness of the reals
  • Introduces: Itô isometry

  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled

Defining the Itô integral only for step processes restricts financial modeling to trading strategies rebalanced at fixed discrete dates. To model continuous portfolio hedging and general stochastic differential equations, the integral must extend to all square-integrable adapted processes. The Itô isometry is the central engine of this extension: it establishes that the stochastic integral map is an exact distance-preserving isometry from \(L^2([0, H] \times \Omega)\) to \(L^2(\Omega)\). Completeness of \(L^2\) then allows taking limits of simple approximations.

1The Itô Isometry for Simple Integrands

Definition 1.1 (Square-Integrable Adapted Space \(\mathcal{L}^2\))

Let \(\mathcal{L}^2([0, H])\) denote the Hilbert space of progressively measurable processes \(H_t(\omega)\) with respect to \((\mathcal{F}_t)\) such that the norm:

\[ \|H\|_{\mathcal{L}^2} \coloneqq \left( \E\left[ \int_0^H H_s^2 \, ds \right] \right)^{1/2} < \infty. \]

The space of simple adapted step processes \(\mathcal{H}_0^2\) defined in Lesson 1 is a subspace of \(\mathcal{L}^2([0, H])\).

Theorem 1.2 (Itô Isometry for Simple Processes)

Assumptions: (A1) \(B\) is standard Brownian motion with filtration \((\mathcal{F}_t)\); (A2) \(H \in \mathcal{H}_0^2\) is a simple adapted process on partition \(\Pi = \{0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = H\}\).

Statement: The Itô isometry holds:

\[ \E\left[ \left( \int_0^H H_s \, dB_s \right)^2 \right] = \E\left[ \int_0^H H_s^2 \, ds \right]. \]
Proof

By definition of the Itô integral for simple processes:

\[ \int_0^H H_s \, dB_s = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k \Delta B_k. \]

Squaring the finite sum yields diagonal and cross terms:

\[ \left( \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k \Delta B_k \right)^2 = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k^2 (\Delta B_k)^2 + 2 \sum_{0 \le j < k \le n-1} H_j \Delta B_j H_k \Delta B_k. \]

Taking expectations, we evaluate each term. For \(j < k\), \(t_{j+1} \le t_k\), so \(H_j \Delta B_j H_k\) is \(\mathcal{F}_{t_k}\)-measurable. By the tower property:

\[ \E[H_j \Delta B_j H_k \Delta B_k] = \E\left[ \E[H_j \Delta B_j H_k \Delta B_k \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_k}] \right] = \E\left[ H_j \Delta B_j H_k \E[\Delta B_k \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_k}] \right]. \]

Since \(\Delta B_k\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_{t_k}\) and \(\E[\Delta B_k] = 0\), all cross terms vanish: \(\E[H_j \Delta B_j H_k \Delta B_k] = 0\).

For the diagonal terms, \(H_k^2\) is \(\mathcal{F}_{t_k}\)-measurable and \(\Delta B_k \sim \Normal(0, \Delta t_k)\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_{t_k}\):

\[ \E[H_k^2 (\Delta B_k)^2] = \E\left[ H_k^2 \E[(\Delta B_k)^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_k}] \right] = \E[H_k^2 \Delta t_k] = \E[H_k^2] \Delta t_k. \]

Summing over \(k=0, \dots, n-1\):

\[ \E\left[ \left( \int_0^H H_s \, dB_s \right)^2 \right] = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \E[H_k^2] \Delta t_k = \E\left[ \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_k^2 \Delta t_k \right] = \E\left[ \int_0^H H_s^2 \, ds \right]. \]

This completes the proof of the isometry.

2Extension to Square-Integrable Adapted Integrands

Theorem 2.1 (Extension to \(\mathcal{L}^2([0, H])\))

Assumptions: (A1) \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t), \Prob)\) is a filtered probability space with standard Brownian motion \(B\); (A2) \(H \in \mathcal{L}^2([0, H])\), so \(\E[\int_0^H H_s^2 ds] < \infty\).

Statement: There exists a sequence of simple adapted processes \((H^{(n)})_{n \ge 1} \subset \mathcal{H}_0^2\) such that \(\|H^{(n)} - H\|_{\mathcal{L}^2} \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\). The sequence of stochastic integrals \(I(H^{(n)}) = \int_0^H H_s^{(n)} dB_s\) forms a Cauchy sequence in \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_H, \Prob)\). Its limit, denoted:

\[ \int_0^H H_s \, dB_s \coloneqq \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_0^H H_s^{(n)} \, dB_s \quad \text{in } L^2(\Omega), \]

is uniquely defined independent of the approximating sequence, satisfies the Itô isometry, and has expectation zero.

Proof sketch

Step 1 (density): The simple adapted processes \(\mathcal{H}_0^2\) are dense in \(\mathcal{L}^2([0, H])\). The proof of density approximates \(H\) first by bounded continuous processes, then by step functions via convolution and discretization; this step is standard in measure theory and skipped here.

Step 2 (Cauchy sequence): Given \(\|H^{(n)} - H\|_{\mathcal{L}^2} \to 0\), by linearity and the Itô isometry on simple processes:

\[ \E\left[ \left| I(H^{(n)}) - I(H^{(m)}) \right|^2 \right] = \E\left[ \left| I(H^{(n)} - H^{(m)}) \right|^2 \right] = \left\| H^{(n)} - H^{(m)} \right\|_{\mathcal{L}^2}^2. \]

As \(n, m \to \infty\), \(\|H^{(n)} - H^{(m)}\|_{\mathcal{L}^2} \to 0\), so \((I(H^{(n)}))_{n \ge 1}\) is a Cauchy sequence in \(L^2(\Omega)\).

Step 3 (completeness and isometry): By completeness of \(L^2(\Omega)\), the limit \(I(H) \in L^2(\Omega)\) exists. Continuity of the \(L^2\) norm preserves the isometry: \(\E[(I(H))^2] = \lim_{n \to \infty} \E[(I(H^{(n)}))^2] = \lim_{n \to \infty} \|H^{(n)}\|_{\mathcal{L}^2}^2 = \|H\|_{\mathcal{L}^2}^2\).

3Left-Endpoint Non-Anticipation versus Right-Endpoint Anticipation

To understand why the Itô isometry depends critically on left-endpoint evaluation, consider integrating Brownian motion against itself: \(H_s = B_s\) on \([0, H]\). By the isometry, \(\E[(\int_0^H B_s dB_s)^2] = \int_0^H \E[B_s^2] ds = \int_0^H s \, ds = H^2 / 2\), and \(\E[\int_0^H B_s dB_s] = 0\).

Now contrast the left-endpoint discrete sum with the right-endpoint sum over partition \(\{t_k\}\):

  • Left-endpoint (Itô): \(S_L = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_{t_k} \Delta B_k\). Here \(B_{t_k}\) is independent of \(\Delta B_k\), so \(\E[S_L] = 0\).
  • Right-endpoint (anticipating): \(S_R = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_{t_{k+1}} \Delta B_k\). Notice that \(B_{t_{k+1}} = B_{t_k} + \Delta B_k\), so: \[ S_R = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (B_{t_k} + \Delta B_k)\Delta B_k = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_{t_k}\Delta B_k + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (\Delta B_k)^2 = S_L + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (\Delta B_k)^2. \] Taking expectations: \(\E[S_R] = \E[S_L] + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = 0 + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \Delta t_k = H\).

Lab 1 · Itô isometry and left-endpoint versus right-endpoint Riemann sums

For \(H_s = B_s\) on \([0, 1.0]\) with \(N = 50\) steps across 5,000 paths (SIMULATED), the left-endpoint sum has sample mean near 0 within 5 standard errors, while the right-endpoint sum has sample mean near \(H = 1.0\) within 5 standard errors.

"""Lab 1 · Itô isometry and left-endpoint versus right-endpoint Riemann sums.

For the integrand H_s = B_s on [0, H], evaluating at the left endpoint t_k gives the Itô integral
sum_k B_{t_k} Delta B_k, whose expectation is identically zero. Evaluating at the right endpoint t_{k+1} is
anticipating: since B_{t_{k+1}} = B_{t_k} + Delta B_k, the product contains (Delta B_k)^2 whose expectation is
Delta t_k, so the sum has expectation H. This lab simulates 5,000 paths on a 50-step grid with H = 1.0 from seed 42
(SIMULATED), printing the sample means and standard errors for both sums alongside the theoretical values.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    horizon = 1.0
    n_steps = 50
    dt = horizon / n_steps
    sq_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
    n_paths = 5000
    seed = 42

    rng = random.Random(seed)
    left_sums: list[float] = []
    right_sums: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(n_paths):
        b = 0.0
        s_left = 0.0
        s_right = 0.0
        for _ in range(n_steps):
            db = rng.gauss(0.0, sq_dt)
            b_next = b + db
            s_left += b * db
            s_right += b_next * db
            b = b_next
        left_sums.append(s_left)
        right_sums.append(s_right)

    mean_left = sum(left_sums) / n_paths
    var_left = sum((x - mean_left) ** 2 for x in left_sums) / (n_paths - 1)
    se_left = math.sqrt(var_left / n_paths)

    mean_right = sum(right_sums) / n_paths
    var_right = sum((x - mean_right) ** 2 for x in right_sums) / (n_paths - 1)
    se_right = math.sqrt(var_right / n_paths)

    print("theoretical mean (left endpoint, Itô): 0.000000")
    print(f"sample mean (left endpoint): {mean_left:.6f} (se {se_left:.6f})")
    print(f"theoretical mean (right endpoint, anticipating): {horizon:.6f}")
    print(f"sample mean (right endpoint): {mean_right:.6f} (se {se_right:.6f})")
    print("left mean within 5*se of 0:", abs(mean_left) <= 5.0 * se_left)
    print("right mean within 5*se of H:", abs(mean_right - horizon) <= 5.0 * se_right)

In stark contrast, the right-endpoint sum produces mean 0.99701 (se 0.01036), confirming the theoretical expectation \(H = 1.0\). 4Where the canon breaks

The Itô isometry fails completely if the integrand is anticipating. If an integrand \(H_t\) is allowed to depend on future Brownian increments \(\{B_u - B_t : u > t\}\), the cross-terms \(\E[H_j \Delta B_j H_k \Delta B_k]\) in the squared expansion do not vanish, and \(\E[H_k \Delta B_k] \neq 0\). For example, if \(H_t = B_H\) is constant in time but evaluated at the terminal horizon, \(\int_0^H B_H dB_s = B_H (B_H - B_0) = B_H^2\), which has expectation \(H \neq 0\) and variance \(2 H^2 \neq \int_0^H \E[B_H^2] ds = H^2\). Extending integration to anticipating integrands requires the Skorokhod integral and Malliavin calculus, where the divergence operator replaces the Itô integral and the isometry acquires an additional Malliavin derivative trace term.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The Itô isometry holds pathwise for every realization: \((\int_0^H H_s dB_s)^2 = \int_0^H H_s^2 ds\).”

    The Itô isometry is strictly an \(L^2(\Omega)\) expectation identity: \(\E[(\int H dB)^2] = \E[\int H^2 ds]\). For any single realization \(\omega\), the squared stochastic integral \((\int_0^H H_s(\omega) dB_s(\omega))^2\) is a non-negative random number that almost never equals the pathwise integral \(\int_0^H H_s(\omega)^2 ds\). For example, for \(H_s = 1\), \((B_H)^2\) is a chi-squared random variable, whereas \(\int_0^H 1^2 ds = H\) is a constant.

  2. “The Itô isometry applies to any integrand with finite second moment \(\E[H_t^2] < \infty\).”

    Finite moment is necessary but not sufficient: the process \(H_t\) must be adapted to the Brownian filtration \((\mathcal{F}_t)\). If \(H_t\) is anticipating (such as \(H_t = B_H\)), the isometry fails even though \(\E[B_H^2] = H < \infty\).

  3. “The right-endpoint Riemann sum converges to the Stratonovich integral.”

    The right-endpoint sum \(\sum B_{t_{k+1}}\Delta B_k\) has expectation \(H\). The Stratonovich integral evaluates at the midpoint \((t_k + t_{k+1})/2\) and has expectation \(H/2\). The right-endpoint sum is neither Itô nor Stratonovich; it is an anticipating Riemann sum that diverges from classical stochastic calculus.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Variance of time-weighted Brownian integral

Using the Itô isometry, compute the exact variance of \(I = \int_0^H s \, dB_s\).

Solution

Since \(H_s = s\) is deterministic and square-integrable on \([0, H]\), the Itô isometry gives:

\[ \Var(I) = \E[I^2] = \int_0^H s^2 \, ds = \left[ \frac{s^3}{3} \right]_0^H = \frac{H^3}{3}. \]
M2 · Variance of Brownian motion integral

Using the Itô isometry, compute the exact variance of \(I = \int_0^H B_s \, dB_s\).

Solution

The process \(H_s = B_s\) is adapted and square-integrable: \(\E[\int_0^H B_s^2 ds] = \int_0^H \E[B_s^2] ds = \int_0^H s ds = H^2 / 2 < \infty\). By the Itô isometry:

\[ \Var(I) = \E[I^2] = \E\left[ \int_0^H B_s^2 \, ds \right] = \int_0^H s \, ds = \frac{H^2}{2}. \]
M3 · Inner product between two stochastic integrals

Let \(H_s = 1\) and \(G_s = s\) on \([0, H]\). Use the polarization identity to compute \(\E[(\int_0^H dB_s)(\int_0^H s dB_s)]\).

Solution

By the polarized Itô isometry, \(\E[\int_0^H H_s dB_s \int_0^H G_s dB_s] = \E[\int_0^H H_s G_s ds]\). Here \(H_s G_s = 1 \cdot s = s\), so the covariance is \(\int_0^H s ds = H^2 / 2\).

Proof

P1 · Polarized Itô isometry

Prove that for any two processes \(H, G \in \mathcal{H}_0^2\), the cross-isometry holds: \(\E[(\int_0^H H_s dB_s)(\int_0^H G_s dB_s)] = \E[\int_0^H H_s G_s ds]\).

Solution

Apply Theorem 1.2 to \(H + G\) and \(H - G\):

\[ \E\left[ (I(H + G))^2 \right] = \|H + G\|_{\mathcal{L}^2}^2, \quad \E\left[ (I(H - G))^2 \right] = \|H - G\|_{\mathcal{L}^2}^2. \]

Subtracting the two equations and using the polarization identity for inner product spaces (\(4\langle u, v\rangle = \|u+v\|^2 - \|u-v\|^2\)) yields \(4 \E[I(H)I(G)] = 4 \E[\int_0^H H_s G_s ds]\), proving the result.

P2 · Martingale property of the extended integral

Prove that for any \(H \in \mathcal{L}^2([0, H])\), the process \(M_t = \int_0^t H_s dB_s\) is an \((\mathcal{F}_t)\)-martingale.

Solution

Let \((H^{(n)})\) be a sequence in \(\mathcal{H}_0^2\) converging to \(H\) in \(\mathcal{L}^2\). For each \(n\), \(M_t^{(n)} = \int_0^t H_s^{(n)} dB_s\) is a martingale by Theorem 1.5 of Lesson 1, so \(\E[M_t^{(n)} \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = M_s^{(n)}\) for \(s \le t\). By the Itô isometry, \(M_t^{(n)} \to M_t\) in \(L^2(\Omega)\). Since conditional expectation with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_s\) is an \(L^2\) projection (hence a continuous operator on \(L^2\)):

\[ \E[M_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \E\left[ \lim_{n \to \infty} M_t^{(n)} \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_s \right] = \lim_{n \to \infty} \E[M_t^{(n)} \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \lim_{n \to \infty} M_s^{(n)} = M_s. \]

Hence \(M_t\) is an \((\mathcal{F}_t)\)-martingale.

P3 · Quadratic variation of the Itô integral process

Prove that the continuous martingale \(M_t = \int_0^t H_s dB_s\) has quadratic variation \([M, M]_t = \int_0^t H_s^2 ds\).

Solution

For a simple process \(H_t = \sum_k H_k \indic_{(t_k, t_{k+1}]}(t)\), the increment is \(\Delta M_k = H_k \Delta B_k\). The sum of squared increments is \(\sum_k (\Delta M_k)^2 = \sum_k H_k^2 (\Delta B_k)^2\). In SC1, we established that \(\sum_k (\Delta B_k)^2 \to \Delta t_k\) in \(L^2\). Therefore \(\sum_k H_k^2 (\Delta B_k)^2 \to \sum_k H_k^2 \Delta t_k = \int_0^t H_s^2 ds\) in \(L^1(\Omega)\). By density, this extends to all \(H \in \mathcal{L}^2\), confirming \([M, M]_t = \int_0^t H_s^2 ds\).

Empirical

E1 · Simulation of exponential integrand variance

Let \(H_s = e^s\) on \([0, 1.0]\). What is the exact variance of \(\int_0^1 e^s dB_s\), and if estimated across 5,000 Monte Carlo paths, what is the standard error of the sample variance?

Solution

Exact variance: \(\int_0^1 (e^s)^2 ds = \int_0^1 e^{2s} ds = [e^{2s}/2]_0^1 = (e^2 - 1)/2 \approx (7.389056 - 1)/2 = 3.194528\). Since the integral is Gaussian with variance \(\sigma^2 = 3.1945\), the standard error of the sample variance across \(N = 5000\) paths is \(\sqrt{2 \sigma^4 / (N - 1)} = \sqrt{2(3.194528^2)/4999} \approx \sqrt{20.41/4999} \approx 0.0639\).

E2 · Discretization step impact on right-endpoint sum

Compute \(\E[S_R]\) for \(S_R = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_{t_{k+1}} \Delta B_k\) on \([0, 2.0]\) for \(n = 10\) and \(n = 1000\).

Solution

For any partition of \([0, H]\), \(\E[S_R] = \sum_k \E[(B_{t_k} + \Delta B_k)\Delta B_k] = \sum_k \Delta t_k = H\). Thus for both \(n = 10\) and \(n = 1000\), \(\E[S_R] = 2.0\). The bias is invariant to partition refinement because the sum of interval lengths always equals the total horizon \(H\).

E3 · Variance comparison: Itô vs right-endpoint sum

Compute the theoretical variance of the right-endpoint sum \(S_R = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_{t_{k+1}}\Delta B_k\) for \(H = 1\) on an equal partition of \(n\) steps.

Solution

Since \(S_R = S_L + \sum_k (\Delta B_k)^2\), with \(\E[S_L] = 0\) and \(\E[\sum (\Delta B_k)^2] = 1\), \(\Var(S_R) = \Var(S_L) + \Var(\sum (\Delta B_k)^2) + 2 \Cov(S_L, \sum (\Delta B_k)^2)\). As \(n \to \infty\), \(\Var(\sum (\Delta B_k)^2) = n \cdot 2 (\Delta t)^2 = 2/n \to 0\). The covariance also vanishes. In the limit, \(\Var(S_R) \to \Var(S_L) = 1/2\), but \(\E[S_R] = 1.0 \neq 0\).

Research

R1 · Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequalities

State how the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy (BDG) inequality extends the Itô isometry from \(p = 2\) to general \(p \ge 1\).

Solution

The BDG inequality states that for any continuous local martingale \(M_t = \int_0^t H_s dB_s\) with \(M_0 = 0\) and any \(p \in [1, \infty)\), there exist universal constants \(c_p, C_p > 0\) such that:

\[ c_p \E\left[ [M, M]_H^{p/2} \right] \le \E\left[ \sup_{0 \le t \le H} |M_t|^p \right] \le C_p \E\left[ [M, M]_H^{p/2} \right]. \]

For \(p = 2\), \([M, M]_H = \int_0^H H_s^2 ds\), and the BDG bounds reduce to Doob's maximal inequality combined with the Itô isometry.

R2 · Local martingales and the failure of true martingale property

Consider the process \(H_s\) satisfying \(\int_0^H H_s^2 ds < \infty\) almost surely, but \(\E[\int_0^H H_s^2 ds] = \infty\). Explain why \(\int_0^t H_s dB_s\) is a local martingale but not necessarily a true martingale.

Solution

When \(\E[\int_0^H H_s^2 ds] = \infty\), the integrand fails assumption A2 of Theorem 2.1. Localization via stopping times \(\tau_k = \inf\{t : \int_0^t H_s^2 ds \ge k\} \wedge H\) yields stopped processes \(H^{(k)}_s = H_s \indic_{[0, \tau_k]}(s)\) that satisfy the isometry. Hence \(M_t = \int_0^t H_s dB_s\) is a continuous local martingale. However, \(M_t\) can have non-zero drift or strict submartingale/supermartingale behavior (such as 3D Bessel process inverses), failing to be a true martingale.

R3 · Connection between Stratonovich and Itô integrals

Using the discrete representations \(S_L = \sum B_{t_k} \Delta B_k\) and \(S_M = \sum B_{(t_k+t_{k+1})/2} \Delta B_k\), derive the conversion formula between Stratonovich and Itô integrals for \(B_t dB_t\).

Solution

Expand \(B_{(t_k+t_{k+1})/2} = B_{t_k} + (B_{(t_k+t_{k+1})/2} - B_{t_k})\). The midpoint sum is:

\[ S_M = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_{t_k} \Delta B_k + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (B_{(t_k+t_{k+1})/2} - B_{t_k})\Delta B_k. \]

The first term converges to \(\int_0^H B_s dB_s\). In the second term, write \(\Delta B_k = (B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{(t_k+t_{k+1})/2}) + (B_{(t_k+t_{k+1})/2} - B_{t_k})\). The product contains \((B_{(t_k+t_{k+1})/2} - B_{t_k})^2\), whose expectation is \(\Delta t_k / 2\). Summing over \(k\) yields \(H / 2\). Thus \(\int_0^H B_s \circ dB_s = \int_0^H B_s dB_s + \tfrac{1}{2} H\).

  5. How is the Itô integral extended from simple step processes to all processes in \(\mathcal{L}^2([0, H])\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 2.1, the isometry maps Cauchy sequences in \(\mathcal{L}^2\) to Cauchy sequences in \(L^2(\Omega)\), whose limit defines the integral.

  6. What is the exact variance of \(\int_0^1 B_s \, dB_s\)?

    \(\Var(\int_0^1 B_s dB_s) = \int_0^1 \E[B_s^2] ds = \int_0^1 s ds = 1/2 = 0.5\).

  7. What happens to the Itô isometry if the integrand \(H_t\) is anticipating (e.g. \(H_t = B_H\))?

    Options

    Anticipating integrands correlate with forward Brownian increments, violating the independence required for the isometry.

  8. Which mathematical space contains the stochastic integral \(\int_0^H H_s dB_s\) for \(H \in \mathcal{L}^2([0, H])\)?

    Options

    The stochastic integral is a square-integrable random variable on the underlying probability space.
