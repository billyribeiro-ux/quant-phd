The Euler–Maruyama scheme suffers from a fundamental bottleneck: for diffusions with state-dependent volatility, freezing the diffusion coefficient at the left endpoint caps global strong convergence at order 1/2. Achieving higher pathwise accuracy requires expanding the diffusion coefficient via Itô's lemma to capture second-order stochastic variations. The resulting Milstein scheme incorporates an iterated Itô integral evaluated in closed form, elevating strong convergence to order 1.0 and dramatically reducing pathwise error in quantitative simulations.

1The Itô–Taylor Expansion and Iterated Integrals

Consider the scalar autonomous stochastic differential equation:

\[ dX_t = a(X_t) \, dt + b(X_t) \, dB_t. \]

In integral form over a discrete time step \([t_k, t_{k+1}]\) of width \(\Delta t = t_{k+1} - t_k\):

\[ X_{t_{k+1}} = X_{t_k} + \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} a(X_s) \, ds + \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} b(X_s) \, dB_s. \]

The Euler–Maruyama scheme approximates \(a(X_s) \approx a(X_{t_k})\) and \(b(X_s) \approx b(X_{t_k})\). To improve accuracy, we apply Itô's lemma (Theorem 1.1 of Lesson 3) to the diffusion coefficient \(b(X_s)\):

\[ b(X_s) = b(X_{t_k}) + \int_{t_k}^s \left( a(X_u) b'(X_u) + \frac{1}{2} b(X_u)^2 b''(X_u) \right) du + \int_{t_k}^s b(X_u) b'(X_u) \, dB_u. \]

Freezing the coefficients inside the inner stochastic integral at \(u = t_k\) gives the approximation \(b(X_s) \approx b(X_{t_k}) + b(X_{t_k}) b'(X_{t_k}) (B_s - B_{t_k})\). Substituting this back into the outer stochastic integral produces:

\[ \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} b(X_s) \, dB_s \approx b(X_{t_k}) \Delta B_k + b(X_{t_k}) b'(X_{t_k}) \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} (B_s - B_{t_k}) \, dB_s. \]
Theorem 1.1 (Iterated Stochastic Integral via Itô's Lemma)

Assumptions: (A1) \(B_t\) is a standard 1-dimensional Brownian motion; (A2) \(\Delta B_k = B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k}\) and \(\Delta t = t_{k+1} - t_k\).

Statement: The double stochastic integral satisfies the exact algebraic identity:

\[ I_{(1, 1)} = \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} \int_{t_k}^s dB_u \, dB_s = \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} (B_s - B_{t_k}) \, dB_s = \frac{1}{2} \left( (\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t \right). \]
Proof

Let \(W_s = B_s - B_{t_k}\) for \(s \in [t_k, t_{k+1}]\). Then \(W_{t_k} = 0\) and \(dW_s = dB_s\). Consider the function \(f(w) = w^2\). Its derivatives are \(f'(w) = 2w\) and \(f''(w) = 2\). Applying Itô's lemma to \(f(W_s)\):

\[ d(W_s^2) = 2 W_s \, dW_s + \frac{1}{2}(2) \, ds = 2 W_s \, dB_s + ds. \]

Integrating from \(s = t_k\) to \(s = t_{k+1}\):

\[ W_{t_{k+1}}^2 - W_{t_k}^2 = 2 \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} W_s \, dB_s + \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} ds. \]

Since \(W_{t_k} = 0\) and \(W_{t_{k+1}} = \Delta B_k\), this yields:

\[ (\Delta B_k)^2 = 2 \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} (B_s - B_{t_k}) \, dB_s + \Delta t. \]

Rearranging and dividing by \(2\):

\[ \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} (B_s - B_{t_k}) \, dB_s = \frac{1}{2} \left( (\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t \right). \]

This completes the proof.

2The Milstein Scheme and Convergence Order

Definition 2.1 (Milstein Scheme)

For the scalar SDE \(dX_t = a(t, X_t) \, dt + b(t, X_t) \, dB_t\) with continuously differentiable diffusion coefficient \(b(t, x)\), the Milstein scheme updates discrete path values according to:

\[ Y_{k+1} = Y_k + a(t_k, Y_k) \, \Delta t + b(t_k, Y_k) \, \Delta B_k + \frac{1}{2} b(t_k, Y_k) b'(t_k, Y_k) \left( (\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t \right), \]

where \(b'(t, x) = \frac{\partial b}{\partial x}(t, x)\) denotes the spatial derivative of the diffusion coefficient.

Remark 2.2 (Milstein for Geometric Brownian Motion)

For geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\), the diffusion is \(b(s) = \sigma s\). Its spatial derivative is the constant \(b'(s) = \sigma\). The product is \(b(s) b'(s) = \sigma^2 s\). Hence, the Milstein recursion for GBM takes the elegant closed form:

\[ S_{k+1} = S_k \left[ 1 + \mu \Delta t + \sigma \Delta B_k + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \left( (\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t \right) \right]. \]

Notice that the term \(1 + \sigma \Delta B_k + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 (\Delta B_k)^2\) is the exact second-order Taylor expansion of the continuous solution factor \(\exp(\sigma \Delta B_k)\), while \(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \Delta t\) captures the Itô convexity correction.

Theorem 2.3 (One-Step Local Truncation Error)

Assumptions: (A1) \(a \in C^1\) and \(b \in C^2\) have bounded derivatives; (A2) \(X_{t_{k+1}}\) is the exact solution starting from \(X_{t_k} = Y_k\); (A3) \(Y_{k+1}\) is the one-step Milstein update.

Statement: The one-step local mean-square truncation error satisfies:

\[ \E\left[ |X_{t_{k+1}} - Y_{k+1}|^2 \right] \le C (\Delta t)^3, \]

corresponding to a local root-mean-square accuracy of order \(O((\Delta t)^{3/2})\).

Proof

By the second-order stochastic Taylor expansion, the remainder terms omitted by Milstein consist of the mixed Riemann-Itô integrals \(\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} \int_{t_k}^s ds' dB_s\) and \(\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} \int_{t_k}^s dB_s' ds\), along with the triple Itô integral \(\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} \int_{t_k}^s \int_{t_k}^u dB_v dB_u dB_s\). By the Itô isometry, the expectation of the square of the triple Itô integral is:

\[ \E\left[ \left(\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} \int_{t_k}^s \int_{t_k}^u dB_v dB_u dB_s\right)^2 \right] = \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} \int_{t_k}^s \int_{t_k}^u 1 \, dv \, du \, ds = \frac{(\Delta t)^3}{3!} = \frac{(\Delta t)^3}{6}. \]

Similarly, the mixed integrals contribute terms of order \((\Delta t)^3\). Thus, the sum of all omitted terms in mean square is bounded by \(C (\Delta t)^3\). Taking the square root yields root-mean-square local error \(O((\Delta t)^{3/2})\).

Theorem 2.4 (Global Strong Convergence of the Milstein Scheme)

Assumptions: (A1) \(a, b\) are twice continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives on \([0, H] \times \R\); (A2) Initial condition satisfies \(\E[|x_0|^2] < \infty\).

Statement: The Milstein scheme converges strongly to the exact solution with order \(\gamma = 1.0\):

\[ \E\left[ \sup_{0 \le k \le N} |X_{t_k} - Y_k| \right] \le C_H \, \Delta t, \]

where \(C_H\) is a constant independent of the partition step size \(\Delta t\).

Proof Sketch

Accumulating \(N = H / \Delta t\) local errors of order \(O((\Delta t)^{3/2})\) produces global root-mean-square error \(N \times O((\Delta t)^{3/2}) \times (\text{Gronwall amplification}) = O(\Delta t)\). Standard discrete martingale inequalities (Kloeden and Platen 1992, Chapter 10) bound the maximum deviation across all grid points by \(C_H \Delta t\).

Lab 1 · Milstein scheme versus Euler–Maruyama for multiplicative noise (GBM)

For geometric Brownian motion with \(s_0 = 1.0\), \(\mu = 0.10\), and \(\sigma = 0.40\) on \([0, 1]\), Euler–Maruyama achieves strong convergence order 1/2, while the Milstein scheme incorporates the second-order correction \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S_k ((\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t)\) to attain strong order 1.0. Across 2,000 paths (SIMULATED), the Milstein RMS error halves when \(\Delta t\) halves, widening the error ratio between Euler and Milstein as \(\Delta t \to 0\).

"""Lab 1 · Milstein scheme versus Euler-Maruyama for multiplicative noise (GBM).

When the diffusion coefficient sigma(X) is state-dependent, the Euler-Maruyama scheme attains only strong order 1/2.
The Milstein scheme incorporates the second-order diffusion correction (1/2) sigma(X_k) sigma'(X_k) ((Delta B_k)^2 -
Delta t), restoring strong order 1. For GBM dS_t = mu S_t dt + sigma S_t dB_t, sigma(S) = sigma S and sigma'(S) = sigma,
so the correction is (1/2) sigma^2 S_k ((Delta B_k)^2 - Delta t). This lab simulates 2,000 paths of GBM with s0 = 1.0,
mu = 0.10, sigma = 0.40 over [0, 1] from seed 42 (SIMULATED), computes terminal RMS error against the exact Itô solution
for step counts N in {4, 8, 16, 32}, prints a sample Milstein correction term, and displays the expanding Euler/Milstein
error ratio.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    s0 = 1.0
    mu = 0.10
    sigma = 0.40
    horizon = 1.0
    n_paths = 2000
    seed = 42

    levels = [4, 8, 16, 32]
    print("Comparing Euler vs Milstein strong error for GBM:")

    for n_steps in levels:
        dt = horizon / n_steps
        sq_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        euler_errs: list[float] = []
        milstein_errs: list[float] = []
        sample_correction = 0.0

        for path_idx in range(n_paths):
            s_euler = s0
            s_milstein = s0
            b_tot = 0.0
            for step_idx in range(n_steps):
                db = rng.gauss(0.0, sq_dt)
                b_tot += db
                corr = 0.5 * (sigma**2) * s_milstein * (db * db - dt)
                if path_idx == 0 and step_idx == 0:
                    sample_correction = corr
                s_euler += mu * s_euler * dt + sigma * s_euler * db
                s_milstein += mu * s_milstein * dt + sigma * s_milstein * db + corr

            s_exact = s0 * math.exp((mu - 0.5 * (sigma**2)) * horizon + sigma * b_tot)
            euler_errs.append((s_euler - s_exact) ** 2)
            milstein_errs.append((s_milstein - s_exact) ** 2)

        rms_euler = math.sqrt(sum(euler_errs) / n_paths)
        rms_milstein = math.sqrt(sum(milstein_errs) / n_paths)
        ratio = rms_euler / rms_milstein
        print(
            f"N = {n_steps:2d}, dt = {dt:.4f}: Euler RMS = {rms_euler:.5f}, "
            f"Milstein RMS = {rms_milstein:.5f}, Ratio E/M = {ratio:.2f}"
        )

    print(f"sample initial Milstein correction (path 0, step 0, N=32): {sample_correction:.6e}")


main()

The numerical simulation confirms that Milstein dramatically outperforms Euler–Maruyama for multiplicative noise. At \(N = 4\) (\(\Delta t = 0.2500\)), Euler RMS error is \(0.06502\) versus Milstein's \(0.01408\) (ratio 4.62). As the grid is refined to \(N = 32\) (\(\Delta t = 0.0312\)), Milstein RMS drops by a factor of 7 to \(0.00204\), whereas Euler RMS only drops by a factor of 2.6 to \(0.02491\). The Euler-to-Milstein error ratio expands from 4.62 to 12.19, demonstrating the superiority of order 1.0 over order 0.5.

3Where the canon breaks

The Milstein scheme delivers optimal pathwise convergence in one dimension, but breaks down when applied to multidimensional diffusions or non-differentiable coefficients:

  • Multidimensional Lévy Areas: For a system driven by a \(d\)-dimensional Brownian motion \((B_t^1, \dots, B_t^d)\) with \(d \ge 2\), the stochastic Taylor expansion generates cross-integrals \(\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} (B_s^j - B_{t_k}^j) dB_s^i\). Unlike the 1-dimensional case, these cross-integrals cannot be evaluated using endpoint increments \(\Delta B^i, \Delta B^j\) alone; they depend on the stochastic area (Lévy area) enclosed by the Brownian curve. Simulating Lévy areas requires infinite Fourier series approximations, making the multidimensional Milstein scheme computationally unwieldy unless the noise satisfies the commutativity condition \(\sum_{k} b_{k, j} \frac{\partial b_{m, i}}{\partial x_k} = \sum_k b_{k, i} \frac{\partial b_{m, j}}{\partial x_k}\).
  • Non-Differentiable Volatility: The Milstein correction explicitly requires evaluating \(b'(x)\). In models with non-smooth volatility surfaces (such as local volatility with spline interpolation, or absolute value diffusions \(b(x) = \sigma |x|\)), the derivative does not exist at points of non-differentiability.
  • Boundary Transgression: Although Milstein is much more accurate than Euler for small time steps, on coarse grids the quadratic term \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S_k (\Delta B_k)^2\) can produce severe overshooting if \(|\Delta B_k|\) is unusually large.

4Misconceptions

  1. “The Milstein scheme improves the weak convergence order for estimating derivative option prices.”

    Both Euler–Maruyama and Milstein possess weak convergence order \(\beta = 1.0\). The Milstein correction \(\frac{1}{2}bb'((\Delta B)^2 - \Delta t)\) has expectation zero because \(\E[(\Delta B)^2] = \Delta t\). Therefore, it adds no net drift in expectation and leaves the weak order at 1.0.

  2. “The Milstein term \(\frac{1}{2}b(X_k)b'(X_k)((\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t)\) represents an extra deterministic drift.”

    Because \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t] = 0\), the Milstein term is a pure martingale difference increment. It adjusts pathwise curvature without altering the unconditional expectation of the update.

  3. “Milstein is always superior to Euler–Maruyama regardless of the diffusion coefficient.”

    If the diffusion coefficient is constant (additive noise \(b(x) \equiv \sigma\)), then \(b'(x) = 0\). The Milstein correction is identically zero, and the Milstein scheme collapses exactly to Euler–Maruyama, which already achieves strong order 1.0.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Milstein update for square-root diffusion

Consider the diffusion \(b(x) = \sigma \sqrt{x}\) for \(x > 0\). Derive the Milstein correction term \(\frac{1}{2} b(x) b'(x) ((\Delta B)^2 - \Delta t)\).

Solution

Compute the derivative: \(b'(x) = \frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{x}}\). The product is:

\[ b(x) b'(x) = (\sigma \sqrt{x}) \left( \frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{x}} \right) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2. \]

The Milstein correction term is therefore constant in \(x\):

\[ \frac{1}{2} b(x) b'(x) \left( (\Delta B)^2 - \Delta t \right) = \frac{1}{4}\sigma^2 \left( (\Delta B)^2 - \Delta t \right). \]
M2 · Numerical comparison of one-step updates

Let \(S_0 = 100\), \(\mu = 0.05\), \(\sigma = 0.40\), and \(\Delta t = 0.01\). For Brownian increment \(\Delta B = 0.20\), compute both the Euler update \(S_1^E\) and the Milstein update \(S_1^M\).

Solution

Euler update: \(S_1^E = 100(1 + 0.05(0.01) + 0.40(0.20)) = 100(1 + 0.0005 + 0.08) = 100(1.0805) = 108.05\).

Milstein correction: \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S_0 ((\Delta B)^2 - \Delta t) = \frac{1}{2}(0.16)(100)(0.04 - 0.01) = 8.0 \times 0.03 = 0.24\).

Milstein update: \(S_1^M = S_1^E + 0.24 = 108.05 + 0.24 = 108.29\).

M3 · Strong error comparison at fine grids

At \(\Delta t = 0.01\), Euler has RMS error \(0.020\) and Milstein has RMS error \(0.002\). If \(\Delta t\) is reduced to \(0.0001\) (100-fold reduction), predict the new RMS errors for both schemes.

Solution

For Euler (\(\gamma = 0.5\)): error reduces by \(\sqrt{100} = 10\), giving \(\mathrm{RMS}_E = 0.020 / 10 = 0.0020\).

For Milstein (\(\gamma = 1.0\)): error reduces by \(100\), giving \(\mathrm{RMS}_M = 0.002 / 100 = 0.000020\).

The Milstein scheme is 100 times more accurate at the finer step size.

Proof

P1 · Zero expectation of the Milstein correction

Prove that \(\E\left[ \frac{1}{2} b(Y_k) b'(Y_k) ((\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t) \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_{t_k} \right] = 0\).

Solution

Since \(Y_k\) is \(\mathcal{F}_{t_k}\)-measurable and \(\Delta B_k\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_{t_k}\):

\[ \E\left[ \frac{1}{2} b(Y_k) b'(Y_k) ((\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t) \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_{t_k} \right] = \frac{1}{2} b(Y_k) b'(Y_k) \E\left[ (\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t \right]. \]

Since \(\Delta B_k \sim \Normal(0, \Delta t)\), \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = \Delta t\), so \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t] = \Delta t - \Delta t = 0\).

P2 · Variance of the Milstein correction

Compute \(\Var\left( \frac{1}{2} b(x) b'(x) ((\Delta B)^2 - \Delta t) \right)\) for a fixed state \(x\).

Solution

Let \(Z = \Delta B / \sqrt{\Delta t} \sim \Normal(0, 1)\). Then \((\Delta B)^2 - \Delta t = \Delta t (Z^2 - 1)\). Since \(Z \sim \Normal(0, 1)\), \(\Var(Z^2) = \E[Z^4] - (\E[Z^2])^2 = 3 - 1 = 2\). Therefore, \(\Var((\Delta B)^2 - \Delta t) = (\Delta t)^2 \Var(Z^2 - 1) = 2 (\Delta t)^2\). The variance of the correction is \(\frac{1}{4} (b(x) b'(x))^2 \times 2 (\Delta t)^2 = \frac{1}{2} (b(x) b'(x))^2 (\Delta t)^2\).

P3 · Vanishing Milstein correction for additive noise

Prove that if \(b(t, x) = g(t)\) is independent of \(x\), the Milstein scheme is identical to the Euler–Maruyama scheme.

Solution

Since \(b(t, x) = g(t)\), the spatial partial derivative is \(\frac{\partial b}{\partial x}(t, x) = 0\). The Milstein correction factor is \(\frac{1}{2} b(t, x) \frac{\partial b}{\partial x}(t, x) = \frac{1}{2} g(t) \cdot 0 = 0\). Thus, the Milstein update equation reduces identically to \(Y_{k+1} = Y_k + a(t_k, Y_k)\Delta t + g(t_k)\Delta B_k\), which is the Euler–Maruyama update.

Empirical

E1 · Milstein error regression verification

In Lab 1, the Milstein RMS errors at \(N \in \{4, 8, 16, 32\}\) are \(0.01408, 0.00746, 0.00357, 0.00204\). Estimate the empirical convergence order by computing \(\log_2(\mathrm{RMS}_4 / \mathrm{RMS}_{32}) / \log_2(32 / 4)\).

Solution

Step-size reduction ratio is \(32 / 4 = 8 = 2^3\), so \(\log_2(8) = 3\). The error reduction ratio is \(0.01408 / 0.00204 \approx 6.902\). \(\log_2(6.902) \approx 2.787\). The empirical order is \(\hat{\gamma} = 2.787 / 3 \approx 0.93\), which is close to the asymptotic theoretical strong order of \(1.0\).

E2 · Derivative-free Runge–Kutta Milstein

When \(b'(x)\) is difficult to compute analytically, the derivative-free Milstein scheme replaces \(b(x) b'(x) \Delta B\) with \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}[b(x + b(x)\sqrt{\Delta t}) - b(x)]\). Verify that this finite difference has the correct leading order.

Solution

Taylor expanding \(b(x + b(x)\sqrt{\Delta t}) = b(x) + b'(x) b(x)\sqrt{\Delta t} + O(\Delta t)\). Subtracting \(b(x)\) gives \(b'(x) b(x)\sqrt{\Delta t} + O(\Delta t)\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) yields \(b(x) b'(x) + O(\sqrt{\Delta t})\). Thus, the finite difference recovers \(b(x) b'(x)\) up to higher-order error terms, preserving strong order 1.0 without analytic differentiation.

E3 · Runtime comparison between Euler and Milstein

Profiling shows that a Milstein step requires 1.35 times the CPU operations of an Euler step. For a target strong error of \(\epsilon = 0.001\) on a multiplicative noise SDE, which scheme uses fewer total CPU operations?

Solution

To reach error \(\epsilon\), Euler requires \(N_E \propto \epsilon^{-2} = 10^6\) steps. Milstein requires \(N_M \propto \epsilon^{-1} = 10^3\) steps. The CPU work ratio is \(\frac{\mathrm{Work}_E}{\mathrm{Work}_M} = \frac{10^6 \times 1.0}{10^3 \times 1.35} \approx \frac{1000}{1.35} \approx 740\). The Milstein scheme is approximately 740 times faster to achieve the target error.

Research

R1 · Commutative noise condition in multi-dimensions

For a 2D diffusion driven by 2 independent Brownian motions, state the commutativity condition on diffusion vector fields \(b_1\) and \(b_2\) that eliminates the need to simulate Lévy areas.

Solution

Define the first-order differential operators \(L_j = \sum_{k=1}^d b_{k, j}(x) \frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\). The commutativity condition is \([L_1, L_2] = L_1 L_2 - L_2 L_1 = 0\), or in coordinates: \(\sum_{k=1}^d b_{k, 1}(x) \frac{\partial b_{m, 2}}{\partial x_k}(x) = \sum_{k=1}^d b_{k, 2}(x) \frac{\partial b_{m, 1}}{\partial x_k}(x)\) for all \(m\). When this Lie bracket vanishes, the antisymmetric part of the iterated integrals cancels out, allowing Milstein to be evaluated strictly from \(\Delta B_k^1\) and \(\Delta B_k^2\).

R2 · Itô–Taylor expansion order hierarchy

In the stochastic Taylor expansion, list all terms required to construct a numerical scheme with global strong convergence order \(\gamma = 1.5\).

Solution

A strong order 1.5 scheme requires: the first-order drift increment \(a \Delta t\); the second-order drift increment \(\frac{1}{2} L_0 a (\Delta t)^2\); the first-order diffusion \(b \Delta B\); the second-order diffusion \(L_1 b I_{(1, 1)}\); the mixed increments \(L_0 b I_{(0, 1)} + L_1 a I_{(1, 0)}\); and the third-order diffusion \(L_1(L_1 b) I_{(1, 1, 1)}\), where \(I_{(1, 1, 1)} = \frac{1}{6}((\Delta B)^3 - 3 \Delta B \Delta t)\).

R3 · Symplectic and structure-preserving SDE integrators

Explain why Milstein applied to Hamiltonian systems with stochastic perturbations does not preserve phase space volume, and describe how split-step integrators address this.

Solution

The standard Milstein update is explicit and non-symplectic: its Jacobian determinant \(\det(\partial Y_{k+1}/\partial Y_k) \neq 1\), leading to artificial energy dissipation or explosion over long horizons. Split-step or stochastic Störmer–Verlet integrators partition the vector field into kinetic and potential components, alternating symplectic sub-steps to preserve the symplectic 2-form and phase volume.

    Options

    By applying Itô's lemma to \(W_s^2\), \(d(W_s^2) = 2 W_s dB_s + ds\), so \(\int W_s dB_s = \frac{1}{2}((\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t)\).

  2. What is the theoretical global strong convergence order \(\gamma\) of the Milstein scheme for an SDE with multiplicative noise?

    The Milstein scheme achieves strong convergence order \(\gamma = 1.0\).

  3. What happens to the Milstein correction term when the diffusion coefficient is constant (\(b(t, x) \equiv \sigma\))?

    Options

    Because \(b'(x) = 0\), the product \(b(x)b'(x) = 0\), eliminating the Milstein term.

  4. What is the unconditional expectation of the Milstein correction term \(\frac{1}{2} b(Y_k) b'(Y_k) ((\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t)\)?

    Options

    Because \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = \Delta t\), \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t] = 0\), meaning the Milstein correction is a centered martingale increment.

  5. Why is the multidimensional Milstein scheme difficult to implement in practice when \(d \ge 2\)?

    Options

    For \(i \neq j\), the iterated integral is the Lévy area, requiring infinite series simulations unless the noise fields commute.

  6. What is the Milstein update for geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\)?

    Options

    With \(b(s) = \sigma s\) and \(b'(s) = \sigma\), the Milstein term is \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S_k ((\Delta B_k)^2 - \Delta t)\).

  7. How does the weak convergence order of Milstein compare with the weak convergence order of Euler–Maruyama?

    Options

    Because the Milstein correction has expectation zero, it does not improve the weak order: both Euler and Milstein have weak order 1.0.

  8. What regularity condition on the diffusion coefficient \(b(x)\) is required by the classical Milstein scheme?

    Options

    The spatial derivative \(b'(x)\) must exist and be continuous to define the Milstein correction term.
