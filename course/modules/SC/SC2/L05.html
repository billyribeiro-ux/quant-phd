The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Process

  • Uses: Itô's lemma, Itô isometry

Many financial variables, including interest rates, volatility surfaces, credit spreads, and commodity price premia, display persistent mean reversion rather than pure geometric growth. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is the canonical continuous-time model for such mean-reverting dynamics. By employing an integrating factor alongside Itô's lemma, the linear SDE can be integrated exactly into an explicit Gaussian process whose transient variance asymptotically approaches a finite stationary upper bound.

1Definition and Exact Solution via Integrating Factor

Definition 1.1 (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Process)

A stochastic process \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) on a filtered probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t), \Prob)\) is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (OU process) with mean-reversion speed \(\kappa > 0\), long-run equilibrium level \(\theta \in \R\), volatility \(\sigma > 0\), and initial state \(X_0 = x_0 \in \R\) if it satisfies the linear stochastic differential equation:

\[ dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t) \, dt + \sigma \, dB_t, \quad X_0 = x_0. \]

The drift term \(\kappa(\theta - X_t)\) exerts an elastic restoring pull toward the equilibrium level \(\theta\). When \(X_t > \theta\), the drift is negative, pushing the state downward; when \(X_t < \theta\), the drift is positive, pushing the state upward. The speed \(\kappa\) governs the intensity of this restoring pull, while the diffusion term \(\sigma \, dB_t\) continuously injects random perturbations.

Theorem 1.2 (Explicit Solution via Integrating Factor)

Assumptions: (A1) \(X_t\) satisfies \(dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t) \, dt + \sigma \, dB_t\) with \(\kappa > 0\), \(\theta \in \R\), and \(\sigma > 0\); (A2) \(B_t\) is a standard Brownian motion with natural filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t\).

Statement: The unique strong solution of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE is given explicitly by:

\[ X_t = x_0 e^{-\kappa t} + \theta (1 - e^{-\kappa t}) + \sigma \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t - s)} \, dB_s. \]
Proof

Consider the integrating factor \(e^{\kappa t}\) and define the auxiliary process \(Y_t = f(t, X_t) = e^{\kappa t} X_t\). The function \(f(t, x) = e^{\kappa t} x\) is infinitely differentiable on \([0, \infty) \times \R\), with partial derivatives:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = \kappa e^{\kappa t} x, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = e^{\kappa t}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = 0. \]

Applying Itô's lemma (Theorem 1.3 of Lesson 3) to \(Y_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} dY_t &= \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \kappa(\theta - X_t) \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} \right) dt + \sigma \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \, dB_t \\ &= \left( \kappa e^{\kappa t} X_t + \kappa(\theta - X_t) e^{\kappa t} + 0 \right) dt + \sigma e^{\kappa t} \, dB_t \\ &= \left( \kappa e^{\kappa t} X_t + \kappa \theta e^{\kappa t} - \kappa e^{\kappa t} X_t \right) dt + \sigma e^{\kappa t} \, dB_t \\ &= \kappa \theta e^{\kappa t} \, dt + \sigma e^{\kappa t} \, dB_t. \end{aligned} \]

The state-dependent cross term \(\kappa e^{\kappa t} X_t\) cancels out entirely. Integrating both sides from \(0\) to \(t\):

\[ Y_t - Y_0 = \int_0^t \kappa \theta e^{\kappa s} \, ds + \sigma \int_0^t e^{\kappa s} \, dB_s. \]

Evaluating the deterministic Riemann integral on the right gives \(\kappa \theta \left[ \frac{e^{\kappa s}}{\kappa} \right]_0^t = \theta (e^{\kappa t} - 1)\). Recalling \(Y_0 = X_0 = x_0\) and \(Y_t = e^{\kappa t} X_t\):

\[ e^{\kappa t} X_t = x_0 + \theta (e^{\kappa t} - 1) + \sigma \int_0^t e^{\kappa s} \, dB_s. \]

Multiplying both sides by the decaying exponential \(e^{-\kappa t}\) yields:

\[ X_t = x_0 e^{-\kappa t} + \theta (1 - e^{-\kappa t}) + \sigma \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t - s)} \, dB_s. \]

This establishes the explicit solution formula.

Remark 1.3 (Structure of the Stochastic Integral)

The stochastic term \(I_t = \sigma \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t - s)} \, dB_s\) is an Itô integral whose integrand \(g(s) = \sigma e^{-\kappa(t - s)}\) is a deterministic, continuous function of \(s\). Consequently, \(I_t\) is a centered Gaussian random variable for every fixed \(t \ge 0\).

2Transient Moments and the Itô Isometry

Theorem 2.1 (Expectation and Variance Trajectories)

Assumptions: (A1) \(X_t\) is the solution to the OU SDE with parameters \(x_0 \in \R\), \(\kappa > 0\), \(\theta \in \R\), and \(\sigma > 0\); (A2) \(B_t\) is a standard Brownian motion.

Statement: For every \(t \ge 0\), the expectation and variance of \(X_t\) are given by:

\[ \E[X_t] = x_0 e^{-\kappa t} + \theta (1 - e^{-\kappa t}), \] \[ \Var(X_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\left(1 - e^{-2\kappa t}\right). \]
Proof

Taking expectations of the explicit solution expression from Theorem 1.2:

\[ \E[X_t] = x_0 e^{-\kappa t} + \theta (1 - e^{-\kappa t}) + \sigma \, \E\left[ \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t - s)} \, dB_s \right]. \]

Since the integrand \(s \mapsto e^{-\kappa(t-s)}\) is square-integrable on \([0, t]\), the Itô integral is a continuous martingale starting at zero, so its expectation vanishes:

\[ \E\left[ \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t - s)} \, dB_s \right] = 0. \]

Thus, \(\E[X_t] = x_0 e^{-\kappa t} + \theta (1 - e^{-\kappa t})\). For the variance, subtract the mean to isolate the fluctuation:

\[ X_t - \E[X_t] = \sigma \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t - s)} \, dB_s. \]

Squaring and taking expectation, we apply the Itô isometry (Theorem 1.1 of Lesson 2):

\[ \Var(X_t) = \E\left[ (X_t - \E[X_t])^2 \right] = \sigma^2 \, \E\left[ \left( \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t - s)} \, dB_s \right)^2 \right] = \sigma^2 \int_0^t \left( e^{-\kappa(t - s)} \right)^2 \, ds. \]

Expanding the integrand gives \(e^{-2\kappa(t - s)}\). Evaluating the deterministic integral:

\[ \int_0^t e^{-2\kappa(t - s)} \, ds = e^{-2\kappa t} \int_0^t e^{2\kappa s} \, ds = e^{-2\kappa t} \left[ \frac{e^{2\kappa s}}{2\kappa} \right]_0^t = e^{-2\kappa t} \left( \frac{e^{2\kappa t} - 1}{2\kappa} \right) = \frac{1 - e^{-2\kappa t}}{2\kappa}. \]

Multiplying by \(\sigma^2\) yields the stated variance:

\[ \Var(X_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\left(1 - e^{-2\kappa t}\right). \]
Theorem 2.2 (Stationary Limits)

Assumptions: (A1) \(X_t\) satisfies the OU SDE with \(\kappa > 0\), \(\theta \in \R\), \(\sigma > 0\), and arbitrary initial condition \(x_0 \in \R\).

Statement: In the long-run limit as \(t \to \infty\), the expectation and variance converge to constant equilibrium limits:

\[ \lim_{t \to \infty} \E[X_t] = \theta, \quad \lim_{t \to \infty} \Var(X_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}. \]
Proof

Because \(\kappa > 0\), \(\lim_{t \to \infty} e^{-\kappa t} = 0\) and \(\lim_{t \to \infty} e^{-2\kappa t} = 0\). Substituting into the formulas of Theorem 2.1 yields \(\E[X_t] \to \theta\) and \(\Var(X_t) \to \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\).

Lab 1 · The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: transient moments and stationary variance limit

For an OU process with \(x_0 = 0.5\), \(\kappa = 2.0\), \(\theta = 1.0\), and \(\sigma = 0.30\), the stationary variance limit is \(\sigma^2 / (2\kappa) = 0.09 / 4.0 = 0.022500\). A Monte Carlo sample of 4,000 paths (SIMULATED) produces sample means and variances matching closed-form theoretical trajectories at times \(t \in \{0.25, 0.50, 1.00, 2.00\}\), with empirical variance approaching 0.022500.

"""Lab 1 · The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process: transient moments and stationary variance limit.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation dX_t = kappa(theta - X_t) dt + sigma dB_t with X_0 = x0 has the explicit solution
X_t = x0 e^{-kappa t} + theta(1 - e^{-kappa t}) + sigma int_0^t e^{-kappa(t-s)} dB_s. Its expectation is
x0 e^{-kappa t} + theta(1 - e^{-kappa t}) and its variance is (sigma^2 / (2 kappa))(1 - e^{-2 kappa t}), approaching
the stationary variance sigma^2 / (2 kappa) as t -> infinity. This lab simulates 4,000 paths with x0 = 0.5, kappa = 2.0,
theta = 1.0, sigma = 0.30 from seed 42 (SIMULATED), recording sample statistics at times t in {0.25, 0.50, 1.00, 2.00}
and comparing them against closed-form values and the stationary limit.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    x0 = 0.5
    kappa = 2.0
    theta = 1.0
    sigma = 0.30
    stationary_var = (sigma * sigma) / (2.0 * kappa)

    times = [0.25, 0.50, 1.00, 2.00]
    dt = 0.005
    sq_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
    n_paths = 4000
    seed = 42

    time_steps = {t: round(t / dt) for t in times}
    max_steps = max(time_steps.values())
    samples: dict[float, list[float]] = {t: [] for t in times}

    rng = random.Random(seed)
    for _ in range(n_paths):
        x = x0
        for step in range(1, max_steps + 1):
            db = rng.gauss(0.0, sq_dt)
            x += kappa * (theta - x) * dt + sigma * db
            for t_val, target_step in time_steps.items():
                if step == target_step:
                    samples[t_val].append(x)

    print(f"stationary variance limit: {stationary_var:.6f}")
    for t_val in times:
        exact_mean = x0 * math.exp(-kappa * t_val) + theta * (1.0 - math.exp(-kappa * t_val))
        exact_var = stationary_var * (1.0 - math.exp(-2.0 * kappa * t_val))
        vals = samples[t_val]
        m = sum(vals) / n_paths
        v = sum((y - m) ** 2 for y in vals) / (n_paths - 1)
        se_m = math.sqrt(v / n_paths)
        print(
            f"t = {t_val:.2f}: theory mean {exact_mean:.4f}, sample {m:.4f} (se {se_m:.4f}) | "
            f"theory var {exact_var:.5f}, sample {v:.5f}"
        )


main()

t = 0.25: theory mean 0.6967, sample 0.6993 (se 0.0019) | theory var 0.01422, sample 0.01468
t = 0.50: theory mean 0.8161, sample 0.8156 (se 0.0022) | theory var 0.01945, sample 0.01953
t = 1.00: theory mean 0.9323, sample 0.9315 (se 0.0024) | theory var 0.02209, sample 0.02329
t = 2.00: theory mean 0.9908, sample 0.9886 (se 0.0024) | theory var 0.02249, sample 0.02304

The numerical simulation confirms that the sample mean transitions from \(x_0 = 0.5\) toward the long-run mean \(\theta = 1.0\), reaching \(0.9886\) at \(t = 2.00\) (theory \(0.9908\), within sample error). The variance grows from zero at \(t=0\) to \(0.01468\) at \(t = 0.25\) and settles near \(0.02304\) at \(t = 2.00\), aligning closely with the theoretical asymptotic stationary variance of \(0.022500\).

3Where the canon breaks

The linear drift and constant additive noise of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process produce an exact Gaussian distribution at every time \(t > 0\). As a result, \(\Prob(X_t < 0) = \Phi(-\E[X_t]/\sqrt{\Var(X_t)}) > 0\). When modeling strictly positive financial quantities—such as nominal prices, equity volatilities, default hazard rates, or physical commodity inventories—the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process can and will generate negative values. To remedy this while retaining mean reversion, financial models introduce square-root diffusions (such as \(\sigma \sqrt{X_t} dB_t\)) or exponential transforms, which sacrifice the simple linear integrating factor and require specialized boundary analysis.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Because the drift pulls toward \(\theta\), every single realization settles down to a flat constant \(\theta\) as \(t \to \infty\).”

    The restoring drift only governs the statistical center of the distribution. Realized sample paths continue to fluctuate permanently due to Brownian increments, generating a non-degenerate stationary distribution with variance \(\sigma^2 / (2\kappa)\).

  2. “The variance of any Brownian-driven diffusion grows without bound over time (\(\Var \propto t\)).”

    In standard Brownian motion, variance grows linearly without bound. In the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, the negative feedback drift \(-\kappa X_t\) dissipates variance at exponential rate \(2\kappa\), establishing a strict upper bound \(\sigma^2 / (2\kappa)\) for all time.

  3. “Explicit Euler simulation is unconditionally stable for any time step \(\Delta t\).”

    The discrete Euler update multiplier is \(1 - \kappa \Delta t\). If the discretization grid is too coarse such that \(\kappa \Delta t > 2\), the scheme becomes numerically unstable and exhibits explosive alternating oscillations, even though the continuous process is unconditionally stable for all \(\kappa > 0\).

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Transient variance calculation

Let an OU process have mean reversion speed \(\kappa = 1.5\) and volatility \(\sigma = 0.60\). Compute the exact transient variance at \(t = 0.5\) and the stationary variance limit.

Solution

The stationary variance limit is \(V_\infty = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} = \frac{0.36}{3.0} = 0.12\). At \(t = 0.5\), \(2\kappa t = 1.5\). The transient variance is \(\Var(X_{0.5}) = 0.12(1 - e^{-1.5}) = 0.12(1 - 0.223130) \approx 0.093224\).

M2 · Mean trajectory midpoint

Let \(x_0 = 3.0\), \(\theta = 1.0\), and \(\kappa = 0.8\). Find the exact time \(t^*\) at which the expected value \(\E[X_{t^*}] = 2.0\).

Solution

The expectation is \(\E[X_t] = 1.0 + (3.0 - 1.0)e^{-0.8 t} = 1.0 + 2.0 e^{-0.8 t}\). Setting this to \(2.0\) gives \(2.0 e^{-0.8 t^*} = 1.0 \implies e^{-0.8 t^*} = 0.5 \implies t^* = \frac{\ln 2}{0.8} \approx \frac{0.693147}{0.8} \approx 0.866434\).

M3 · Autocovariance ratio

For an OU process in its stationary regime, compute the correlation \(\mathrm{Corr}(X_s, X_{s+\tau})\) for lag \(\tau = 1.0\) when \(\kappa = 0.5\).

Solution

In the stationary regime, the correlation between states separated by lag \(\tau\) is \(\mathrm{Corr}(X_s, X_{s+\tau}) = e^{-\kappa \tau}\). For \(\kappa = 0.5\) and \(\tau = 1.0\), \(\mathrm{Corr} = e^{-0.5} \approx 0.606531\).

Proof

P1 · Stochastic integral expectation

Prove that for any continuous deterministic function \(g(s)\), \(\E[\int_0^t g(s) dB_s] = 0\).

Solution

Since \(g(s)\) is deterministic and continuous on \([0, t]\), \(\int_0^t g(s)^2 ds < \infty\). By the definition of the Itô integral for square-integrable adapted integrands, the integral process \(M_t = \int_0^t g(s) dB_s\) is a continuous martingale starting at \(M_0 = 0\). Therefore \(\E[M_t] = \E[M_0] = 0\).

P2 · Variance derivation via Itô isometry

Prove from the definition of the Itô isometry that \(\Var(\sigma \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t-s)} dB_s) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t})\).

Solution

By the Itô isometry, \(\E[(\sigma \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t-s)} dB_s)^2] = \sigma^2 \int_0^t (e^{-\kappa(t-s)})^2 ds = \sigma^2 e^{-2\kappa t} \int_0^t e^{2\kappa s} ds = \sigma^2 e^{-2\kappa t} [\frac{e^{2\kappa s}}{2\kappa}]_0^t = \sigma^2 e^{-2\kappa t} \frac{e^{2\kappa t} - 1}{2\kappa} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t})\). Since the mean is zero, this equals the variance.

P3 · Positive probability of negative values

Prove that for any finite \(t > 0\) and parameters \(x_0, \theta, \kappa, \sigma > 0\), \(\Prob(X_t < 0) > 0\).

Solution

At any time \(t > 0\), \(X_t\) is normally distributed with mean \(\mu_t = x_0 e^{-\kappa t} + \theta(1 - e^{-\kappa t})\) and variance \(v_t = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t}) > 0\). The probability of being negative is \(\Prob(X_t < 0) = \Phi(-\mu_t / \sqrt{v_t})\). Since the normal cumulative distribution function \(\Phi(z) > 0\) for all real \(z \in (-\infty, \infty)\), \(\Prob(X_t < 0) > 0\).

Empirical

E1 · AR(1) mapping of discrete OU

Show that a discrete observation of OU with step \(\Delta t\) corresponds to an AR(1) process \(X_{k+1} = a + b X_k + \epsilon_k\), and express continuous parameters \(\kappa, \theta\) in terms of regression slope \(b\) and intercept \(a\).

Solution

From \(\E[X_{k+1} \mid X_k] = X_k e^{-\kappa \Delta t} + \theta(1 - e^{-\kappa \Delta t})\), we match \(b = e^{-\kappa \Delta t}\) and \(a = \theta(1 - b)\). Inverting gives \(\kappa = -\frac{\ln b}{\Delta t}\) and \(\theta = \frac{a}{1 - b}\).

E2 · Monte Carlo stationary variance standard error

Suppose you simulate \(N = 10,000\) independent realizations of an OU process at horizon \(t = 5.0\) with \(\kappa = 2.0\) and \(\sigma = 0.40\). Estimate the theoretical standard error of the sample mean.

Solution

At \(t = 5.0\), \(2\kappa t = 20\), so \(e^{-20} \approx 2 \times 10^{-9}\) and the variance is practically the stationary value \(V_\infty = \frac{0.16}{4.0} = 0.04\). The standard error of the sample mean is \(\se = \sqrt{V_\infty / N} = \sqrt{0.04 / 10000} = \sqrt{4 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.002\).

E3 · Euler stability boundary

If \(\kappa = 10.0\), determine the maximum step size \(\Delta t\) for which the explicit Euler discretization of OU remains non-oscillatory (\(1 - \kappa \Delta t > 0\)) and stable (\(|1 - \kappa \Delta t| < 1\)).

Solution

For non-oscillatory decay: \(1 - \kappa \Delta t > 0 \implies \Delta t < 1/\kappa = 0.10\). For absolute stability: \(|1 - \kappa \Delta t| < 1 \iff 0 < \kappa \Delta t < 2 \implies \Delta t < 2/\kappa = 0.20\).

Research

R1 · Vasicek bond pricing connection

In interest rate modeling, when the short rate follows an OU process \(dr_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)dt + \sigma dB_t\), explain why zero-coupon bond prices take the affine form \(P(t, \bar{t}) = \exp(-A(\bar{t}-t) - B(\bar{t}-t)r_t)\).

Solution

Because the drift is linear and volatility is constant, the integrated short rate \(\int_t^{\bar{t}} r_s ds\) is conditionally Gaussian given \(r_t\). The expectation of an exponential of a Gaussian variable is an exponential of a quadratic in the mean and variance. Since the conditional mean of the integral is linear in \(r_t\) and the conditional variance is independent of \(r_t\), the bond price expectation is an exponential of an affine function of \(r_t\).

R2 · Quadrature versus exact discrete transition

Contrast exact Gaussian path simulation of OU with Euler discretization. Why does exact simulation introduce zero discretization bias?

Solution

Exact simulation draws \(X_{k+1} = X_k e^{-\kappa \Delta t} + \theta(1 - e^{-\kappa \Delta t}) + \sigma \sqrt{\frac{1 - e^{-2\kappa \Delta t}}{2\kappa}} Z_k\) with \(Z_k \sim \Normal(0, 1)\). Because the conditional distribution of \(X_{t+\Delta t} \mid X_t\) is exactly Gaussian with these parameters, the joint distribution of the sampled points on any finite grid matches the continuous-time process law exactly.

R3 · Ergodicity and long-run sample variance

Show that the time-average \(\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T (X_t - \theta)^2 dt\) converges almost surely to the stationary variance \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\) as \(T \to \infty\).

Solution

The OU process is an ergodic diffusion with unique invariant probability measure \(\mu(dx) = \Normal(\theta, \sigma^2/(2\kappa))\). By Birkhoff's ergodic theorem for Markov processes with an invariant measure, for any integrable function \(g\), \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T g(X_t) dt = \int g(x) \mu(dx)\) almost surely. Setting \(g(x) = (x - \theta)^2\) yields \(\int (x - \theta)^2 \mu(dx) = \Var_\mu(X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\).

7Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE \(dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t)dt + \sigma dB_t\), what role does the parameter \(\kappa > 0\) play?

    Options

    The parameter \(\kappa\) governs the strength of the restoring pull: larger \(\kappa\) forces the process to revert more rapidly toward \(\theta\).

  2. Which integrating factor allows direct integration of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE?

    Options

    Applying Itô's lemma to \(Y_t = e^{\kappa t} X_t\) cancels the state-dependent drift term \(-\kappa X_t\), yielding \(d(e^{\kappa t} X_t) = \kappa \theta e^{\kappa t} dt + \sigma e^{\kappa t} dB_t\).

  3. What is the expected value \(\E[X_t]\) of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process starting at \(x_0\)?

    Options

    The expectation is a convex combination of initial state \(x_0\) and target \(\theta\) with weights \(e^{-\kappa t}\) and \(1 - e^{-\kappa t}\).

  4. What is the asymptotic variance of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process as \(t \to \infty\)?

    Options

    Taking \(t \to \infty\) in \(\Var(X_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t})\) leaves \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\).

  5. Why does the Itô isometry apply cleanly to \(\sigma \int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t-s)} dB_s\)?

    Options

    Deterministic square-integrable functions satisfy the \(\mathcal{L}^2\) integrand requirement of the Itô isometry with identity \(\E[(\int g dB)^2] = \int g^2 ds\).

  6. If \(\sigma = 0.30\) and \(\kappa = 2.0\), what is the stationary variance limit \(\sigma^2 / (2\kappa)\)?

    \(0.30^2 / (2 \times 2.0) = 0.09 / 4.0 = 0.0225\).

  7. What is the fundamental financial limitation of the standard Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process when modeling nominal asset prices or default intensities?

    Options

    The support of the Gaussian distribution is \((-\infty, \infty)\), meaning \(\Prob(X_t < 0) > 0\) for any finite parameters.

  8. In discrete simulation with time step \(\Delta t\), what condition causes the explicit Euler scheme for OU to become numerically unstable?

    Options

    The Euler multiplier is \(1 - \kappa \Delta t\). When \(\kappa \Delta t > 2\), \(1 - \kappa \Delta t < -1\), leading to alternating explosive divergence.
