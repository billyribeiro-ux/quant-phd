Strong versus Weak Convergence

  • Uses: Euler–Maruyama scheme, Milstein scheme, uniform convergence
  • Introduces: Strong versus weak convergence
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled

In quantitative finance, numerical simulations serve two fundamentally distinct purposes: reproducing pathwise trajectories (scenario stress-testing, hedging backtests, filtering) and computing expectations (derivative pricing, expected shortfall, valuation adjustments). These two goals require different mathematical criteria. Strong convergence measures path-by-path proximity on a shared probability space, while weak convergence measures the proximity of probability distributions. Understanding this divergence prevents severe over-engineering in Monte Carlo pricing engines.

1Definitions and Theoretical Criteria

Definition 1.1 (Strong and Weak Convergence Orders)

Let \((X_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\) be the exact solution to an Itô SDE, and let \(Y^\Delta = (Y_0, Y_1, \dots, Y_N)\) denote a discrete numerical approximation generated on an equidistant grid of step size \(\Delta t = H / N\). Let \(Y_N^\Delta\) denote the terminal approximation at horizon \(H\).

  • Strong convergence: The scheme converges strongly with order \(\gamma > 0\) if there exists a positive constant \(C\) such that for all sufficiently small \(\Delta t\): \[ \E\left[ \left| X_H - Y_N^\Delta \right| \right] \le C (\Delta t)^\gamma. \] Strong convergence evaluates pathwise fidelity on a common probability space \(\Omega\) where the numerical scheme and continuous process are driven by the identical Brownian path.
  • Weak convergence: The scheme converges weakly with order \(\beta > 0\) if for every polynomial test function \(g \in C_b^{2(\beta+1)}(\R)\) with polynomial growth, there exists a positive constant \(C_g\) such that for all sufficiently small \(\Delta t\): \[ \left| \E[g(X_H)] - \E[g(Y_N^\Delta)] \right| \le C_g (\Delta t)^\beta. \] Weak convergence evaluates distributional fidelity, requiring only that expectations of smooth payoff functions converge.
Remark 1.2 (Pathwise Cancellation under Expectation)

In strong convergence, path deviations do not cancel: the absolute value \(|X_H - Y_N|\) accumulates fluctuations across paths, penalizing any phase lag or amplitude discrepancy. In weak convergence, positive and negative pathwise discrepancies cancel out under the outer expectation \(\E[\cdot]\). Consequently, a scheme can achieve a higher weak convergence order than its strong convergence order.

2Hierarchy of Classical Schemes

Theorem 2.1 (Convergence Orders of Euler–Maruyama and Milstein)

Assumptions: (A1) The drift \(a(t, x)\) and diffusion \(b(t, x)\) satisfy global Lipschitz and linear growth conditions; (A2) \(a\) and \(b\) are sufficiently smooth (\(C^4\)); (A3) The payoff test function \(g\) is smooth with bounded derivatives.

Statement: For general scalar diffusions with state-dependent (multiplicative) noise \(b'(x) \neq 0\):

  • The Euler–Maruyama scheme has strong order \(\gamma = 1/2\) and weak order \(\beta = 1.0\).
  • The Milstein scheme has strong order \(\gamma = 1.0\) and weak order \(\beta = 1.0\).

If the diffusion coefficient is constant (additive noise \(b(t, x) \equiv \sigma\)), Euler–Maruyama achieves strong order \(\gamma = 1.0\) and weak order \(\beta = 1.0\).

Proof Sketch

The strong convergence proofs were established in Lessons 7 and 8 via the Itô isometry and Gronwall's inequality. For weak convergence, let \(u(t, x) = \E[g(X_H) \mid X_t = x]\). By Kolmogorov's backward equation, \(u\) satisfies the partial differential equation \(\partial_t u + a(x) \partial_x u + \frac{1}{2} b(x)^2 \partial_{xx} u = 0\) with terminal condition \(u(H, x) = g(x)\). Applying Itô's formula to \(u(t_{k+1}, Y_{k+1})\) around \((t_k, Y_k)\) and taking expectations reveals that the leading stochastic error terms \(\partial_x u \, b \Delta B\) and \(\frac{1}{2} \partial_{xx} u \, b^2 ((\Delta B)^2 - \Delta t)\) vanish in expectation. The remaining deterministic expansion error is of order \(O((\Delta t)^2)\) per step. Summing over \(N = H / \Delta t\) steps yields global weak error of order \(O(\Delta t)\), proving \(\beta = 1.0\) for both schemes (Kloeden and Platen 1992, Chapter 9).

Over 5,000 realization paths (SIMULATED) at step sizes \(\Delta t = H / 2^k\) (\(N \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}\)), the pathwise strong error \(\E[|S_H - S_N^\Delta|]\) scales as \(O(\Delta t^{1/2})\), while the analytical weak error \(|\E[S_N^\Delta] - \E[S_H]|\) scales as \(O(\Delta t)\).

"""Lab 1 · Euler-Maruyama strong versus weak convergence for geometric Brownian motion.

Curriculum lab: measures the empirical rate of strong convergence E[|S_H - S^{Delta}_H|] and weak convergence
|E[S_H] - E[S^{Delta}_H]| for the Euler-Maruyama scheme applied to geometric Brownian motion dS_t = mu S_t dt + sigma
S_t dB_t with s0 = 1.0, mu = 0.05, sigma = 0.30, H = 1.0. For 5,000 realization paths from seed 42 (SIMULATED), step
sizes Delta t = H / 2^k with k in {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} (N in {2, 4, 8, 16, 32}) are evaluated. The strong error exhibits
an O(Delta t^{1/2}) rate, while the weak error exhibits an O(Delta t) rate, verified both against Monte Carlo sample
means and against the exact analytical Euler expectation E[S_N] = s0(1 + mu Delta t)^N.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    s0 = 1.0
    mu = 0.05
    sigma = 0.30
    horizon = 1.0
    n_paths = 5000
    theory_mean = s0 * math.exp(mu * horizon)

    levels = [2, 4, 8, 16, 32]
    print(f"theoretical expectation E[S_H]: {theory_mean:.6f}")
    print("Euler for GBM: strong and weak errors across step sizes:")

    for n_steps in levels:
        dt = horizon / n_steps
        sq_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
        rng = random.Random(42)
        pathwise_diffs: list[float] = []
        euler_terminals: list[float] = []

        for _ in range(n_paths):
            s_euler = s0
            b_tot = 0.0
            for _ in range(n_steps):
                db = rng.gauss(0.0, sq_dt)
                b_tot += db
                s_euler += mu * s_euler * dt + sigma * s_euler * db
            s_exact = s0 * math.exp((mu - 0.5 * (sigma**2)) * horizon + sigma * b_tot)
            pathwise_diffs.append(abs(s_euler - s_exact))
            euler_terminals.append(s_euler)

        strong_err = sum(pathwise_diffs) / n_paths
        var_strong = sum((x - strong_err) ** 2 for x in pathwise_diffs) / (n_paths - 1)
        strong_se = math.sqrt(var_strong / n_paths)

        sample_mean_euler = sum(euler_terminals) / n_paths
        weak_err_mc = abs(sample_mean_euler - theory_mean)
        var_euler = sum((x - sample_mean_euler) ** 2 for x in euler_terminals) / (n_paths - 1)
        weak_se = math.sqrt(var_euler / n_paths)

        exact_euler_mean = s0 * ((1.0 + mu * dt) ** n_steps)
        analytical_weak_err = abs(exact_euler_mean - theory_mean)

        print(
            f"N = {n_steps:2d}, dt = {dt:.4f}: Strong error = {strong_err:.5f} (se {strong_se:.5f}) | "
            f"Weak error MC = {weak_err_mc:.5f} (se {weak_se:.5f}) | Weak analytical = {analytical_weak_err:.6f}"
        )

The curriculum lab highlights the critical distinction between strong and weak convergence. The analytical weak error drops by exactly a factor of 2 at each step-size halving (\(0.000646 \to 0.000326 \to 0.000164 \to 0.000082 \to 0.000041\)), confirming first-order weak convergence \(\beta = 1.0\). Meanwhile, the pathwise strong error decays at the slower square-root rate (\(0.03420 \to 0.02501 \to 0.01827 \to 0.01344 \to 0.00963\)), confirming strong order \(\gamma = 0.5\). Crucially, the Monte Carlo sample weak error is clouded by sampling noise (\(\se \approx 0.0045\)), demonstrating that Monte Carlo sample variance easily obscures weak discretization bias unless analytical solutions or massive path counts are employed.

3Where the canon breaks

The standard weak convergence theorem assumes smooth test functions \(g \in C_b^4(\R)\). In financial derivative engineering, this smoothness hypothesis is routinely violated:

  • Discontinuous Payoffs (Digital Options): A cash-or-nothing digital option has payoff \(g(x) = \indic_{x \ge K}\), which possesses an essential jump discontinuity at strike \(K\). Because derivatives of \(g\) diverge to Dirac delta distributions, the standard PDE error expansion fails. On discrete grids, the weak convergence rate can deteriorate from \(\beta = 1.0\) to \(\beta = 0.5\) unless specialized smoothing techniques (such as integration by parts or shift-sampling) are applied.
  • Path-Dependent Barrier Monitoring: For down-and-out barrier options with payoff depending on \(\min_{0 \le t \le H} S_t\), discrete Euler monitoring only detects whether the path breached the barrier at grid points \(t_k\). The continuous Brownian path can easily cross the barrier between grid points and return, leading to a persistent \(O(\Delta t^{1/2})\) barrier-touching bias that dominates the \(O(\Delta t)\) terminal weak error.
  • Kinked Vanilla Options: Standard European calls \(g(x) = (x - K)^+\) have a discontinuous first derivative at \(x = K\). While weak order 1.0 is generally preserved for vanilla options due to diffusion smoothing, higher-order weak schemes (e.g. order 2.0 Runge–Kutta schemes) degrade to order 1.0 unless the grid aligns with the strike.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A high strong convergence order is required to accurately price European options via Monte Carlo.”

    Pricing a European derivative involves evaluating an expectation \(\E[g(X_H)]\). This is an exercise in weak convergence, governed by weak order \(\beta\). The Euler–Maruyama scheme already achieves weak order \(\beta = 1.0\); its lower strong order \(\gamma = 0.5\) does not degrade option pricing accuracy.

  2. “Monte Carlo sample averages computed with 5,000 paths directly reveal weak discretization error.”

    The sample average contains both weak discretization bias \(O(\Delta t)\) and statistical sampling error \(\sigma / \sqrt{M}\). With 5,000 paths and volatility \(\sigma = 0.30\), the sampling standard error is \(\approx 0.0045\), which is larger than the true weak discretization error (\(0.000041\) at \(N = 32\)). Detecting weak discretization error empirically requires millions of paths or analytical expectations.

  3. “Strong convergence order \(\gamma\) always equals weak convergence order \(\beta\).”

    For Euler–Maruyama with multiplicative noise, strong order is \(0.5\) while weak order is \(1.0\). Weak convergence is strictly easier to satisfy than strong convergence because mean-zero stochastic errors average out under expectation.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Analytical expectation of the discrete Euler terminal

For geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\), prove that the expectation of the discrete Euler approximation after \(N\) steps with \(\Delta t = H / N\) is \(\E[S_N] = s_0(1 + \mu \Delta t)^N\).

Solution

The Euler update is \(S_{k+1} = S_k(1 + \mu \Delta t + \sigma \Delta B_k)\). Taking conditional expectation: \(\E[S_{k+1} \mid S_k] = S_k(1 + \mu \Delta t + \sigma \E[\Delta B_k]) = S_k(1 + \mu \Delta t)\). By the tower property of expectation: \(\E[S_N] = \E[S_{N-1}](1 + \mu \Delta t) = \dots = S_0 (1 + \mu \Delta t)^N\).

M2 · Weak error Taylor expansion

Using the identity \((1 + x)^N = \exp(N \ln(1 + x))\), expand \(\E[S_N] = s_0(1 + \mu \Delta t)^N\) for small \(\Delta t\) and show that the leading error relative to \(s_0 e^{\mu H}\) is proportional to \(\Delta t\).

Solution

Since \(N = H / \Delta t\): \(\ln(1 + \mu \Delta t) = \mu \Delta t - \frac{1}{2}\mu^2 (\Delta t)^2 + O((\Delta t)^3)\). Multiplying by \(N = H / \Delta t\): \(N \ln(1 + \mu \Delta t) = \mu H - \frac{1}{2}\mu^2 H \Delta t + O((\Delta t)^2)\). Exponentiating: \((1 + \mu \Delta t)^N = e^{\mu H} \exp(-\frac{1}{2}\mu^2 H \Delta t + O((\Delta t)^2)) = e^{\mu H}(1 - \frac{1}{2}\mu^2 H \Delta t + O((\Delta t)^2))\). The error is \(|\E[S_N] - s_0 e^{\mu H}| = \frac{1}{2} s_0 \mu^2 H e^{\mu H} \Delta t + O((\Delta t)^2)\), confirming weak order \(\beta = 1.0\).

M3 · Strong error scaling calculation

A simulation with Euler–Maruyama exhibits strong error \(0.040\) at \(\Delta t = 0.04\). What is the expected strong error at \(\Delta t = 0.01\)?

Solution

For multiplicative noise, strong error scales as \(\epsilon \propto (\Delta t)^{0.5}\). The step size decreases by a factor of \(0.04 / 0.01 = 4\). The error decreases by \(\sqrt{4} = 2\), yielding an expected strong error of \(0.040 / 2 = 0.020\).

Proof

P1 · Strong convergence implies weak convergence

Prove that if a numerical scheme converges strongly with order \(\gamma\) in \(L^1\), it converges weakly with order at least \(\gamma\) for all Lipschitz test functions \(g\).

Solution

Let \(g\) be a Lipschitz continuous test function with constant \(L_g\): \(|g(x) - g(y)| \le L_g |x - y|\). Then:

\[ |\E[g(X_H)] - \E[g(Y_N)]| = |\E[g(X_H) - g(Y_N)]| \le \E[|g(X_H) - g(Y_N)|] \le L_g \E[|X_H - Y_N|]. \]

By the strong convergence assumption, \(\E[|X_H - Y_N|] \le C (\Delta t)^\gamma\). Therefore, \(|\E[g(X_H)] - \E[g(Y_N)]| \le (L_g C) (\Delta t)^\gamma\), establishing weak convergence of order at least \(\gamma\).

P2 · Weak convergence does not imply strong convergence

Construct a sequence of random variables \(Y_n\) that converges weakly to \(X \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) with order \(\infty\) but fails to converge strongly.

Solution

Let \(X \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) and define \(Y_n = -X\) for all \(n\). For every function \(g\), \(Y_n \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) has the identical distribution as \(X\), so \(|\E[g(X)] - \E[g(Y_n)]| = 0\) for all \(n\) (weak error is identically zero). However, \(\E[|X - Y_n|] = \E[|2X|] = 2 \sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 1.5957 \neq 0\). The sequence does not converge strongly to \(X\).

P3 · Discrete martingale property and weak drift preservation

Prove that if drift \(a(x) = \mu x\) and diffusion \(b(x) = \sigma x\), any scheme that preserves \(\E[Y_{k+1} \mid Y_k] = Y_k(1 + \mu \Delta t)\) has zero volatility-induced bias in the expectation of the price.

Solution

Conditioning on \(Y_k\), \(\E[Y_{k+1}] = \E[\E[Y_{k+1} \mid Y_k]] = (1 + \mu \Delta t)\E[Y_k]\). This recursive equation is completely independent of the volatility parameter \(\sigma\). Hence, the volatility \(\sigma\) generates zero bias in the expectation of \(Y_N\), which depends solely on \(\mu\) and \(\Delta t\).

Empirical

E1 · Minimum Monte Carlo sample size to detect weak bias

Suppose weak discretization bias is \(\epsilon_w = 0.0001\) and sample volatility is \(\sigma = 0.20\). How many Monte Carlo paths \(M\) are required to make the statistical standard error \(\se = \sigma / \sqrt{M}\) smaller than \(\epsilon_w / 2\)?

Solution

We require \(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{M}} \le \frac{\epsilon_w}{2} \implies \frac{0.20}{\sqrt{M}} \le 0.00005 \implies \sqrt{M} \ge \frac{0.20}{0.00005} = 4000\). Squaring both sides: \(M \ge 4000^2 = 16,000,000\) paths. Sixteen million paths are required to resolve the discretization bias from sampling noise.

E2 · Antithetic variates for weak convergence testing

Explain how simulating antithetic path pairs \(+dB\) and \(-dB\) reduces the Monte Carlo standard error when evaluating weak convergence of odd polynomial test functions.

Solution

Let \(Y_N^+\) be generated by \(\Delta B_k\) and \(Y_N^-\) by \(-\Delta B_k\). The antithetic estimator is \(\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{2}(Y_N^+ + Y_N^-)\). For linear and odd-powered terms, the fluctuations generated by \(\Delta B\) cancel out identically, dramatically reducing variance and allowing the deterministic \(O(\Delta t)\) bias to be measured with fewer simulation paths.

E3 · Optimal time step allocation under fixed compute budget

Given total compute budget \(C = M \times N\), total MSE of option pricing is \(\mathrm{MSE} \approx c_1 (\Delta t)^{2\beta} + c_2 / M = c_1 (H/N)^2 + c_2 N / C\). Find the optimal scaling of \(N\) with respect to total budget \(C\).

Solution

Substitute \(M = C / N\). Then \(\mathrm{MSE} = c_1 H^2 N^{-2} + c_2 C^{-1} N\). Differentiating with respect to \(N\) and setting to zero: \(-2 c_1 H^2 N^{-3} + c_2 C^{-1} = 0 \implies N^3 = \frac{2 c_1 H^2}{c_2} C \implies N \propto C^{1/3}\). Thus, as compute budget expands, steps should grow as \(C^{1/3}\) while paths grow as \(M = C / N \propto C^{2/3}\).

Research

R1 · Malliavin calculus for irregular payoff weak convergence

Bally and Talay (1996) proved that Euler–Maruyama retains weak order 1.0 even for discontinuous indicator functions \(g(x) = \indic_{x \ge K}\) under non-degenerate hypoellipticity. Explain the role of Malliavin integration by parts in their proof.

Solution

The standard weak error proof requires four derivatives of \(g\). By using the Malliavin integration by parts formula \(\E[g'(X) u] = \E[g(X) \delta(u D X^{-1})]\), derivatives can be transferred from the non-smooth test function \(g\) onto the Malliavin weights of the diffusion. This transforms the error into an expectation of \(g\) multiplied by smooth Sobolev weights, establishing that diffusion smoothing restores weak order 1.0 for measurable bounded payoffs.

R2 · Exact simulation of SDEs without discretization bias

Beskos and Roberts (2005) introduced the Exact Algorithm (EA) for simulating diffusions without any time-discretization error (\(\beta = \infty\)). State the core condition on the drift that permits rejection sampling from Brownian motion.

Solution

The Exact Algorithm applies Girsanov's theorem to transform the diffusion into a Brownian motion. By Itô's formula, the stochastic integral in the Girsanov density is converted into a boundary potential difference \(\Phi(X_H) - \Phi(X_0)\) and a path integral \(\int_0^H \alpha(X_s) ds\). If the drift potential \(\alpha(x) = \frac{1}{2}(b(x)^2 + b'(x))\) is bounded from below, the Girsanov density can be bounded by a constant, enabling exact rejection sampling of Brownian bridge skeletons with zero discretization error.

R3 · Weak order 2.0 Runge–Kutta schemes

Why do weak second-order schemes (such as the Talay–Tubaro or Ninomiya–Victoir schemes) require only discrete three-point distributed random increments rather than continuous Gaussian draws?

Solution

Weak convergence of order 2.0 requires matching moments of Brownian motion up to order 5: \(\E[\Delta B] = 0\), \(\E[(\Delta B)^2] = \Delta t\), \(\E[(\Delta B)^3] = 0\), \(\E[(\Delta B)^4] = 3(\Delta t)^2\), and \(\E[(\Delta B)^5] = 0\). A discrete three-point distribution \(\Prob(\Delta B = \pm \sqrt{3\Delta t}) = 1/6\) and \(\Prob(\Delta B = 0) = 2/3\) matches all five moments exactly, eliminating the need to sample Gaussian random variables while guaranteeing second-order weak convergence.

  1. What distinguishes strong convergence from weak convergence of an SDE numerical scheme?

    Options

  2. What is the weak convergence order \(\beta\) of the Euler–Maruyama scheme for smooth payoff functions?

    Euler–Maruyama achieves weak convergence order \(\beta = 1.0\).

  3. Why does Euler–Maruyama have a higher weak convergence order (\(\beta = 1.0\)) than strong convergence order (\(\gamma = 0.5\)) for multiplicative noise?

    Options

    Stochastic fluctuations of order \(O(\Delta t^{1/2})\) have zero mean, so they cancel out when taking expectations of smooth functions.

  4. For pricing a standard European call option with a smooth underlying SDE, which convergence criterion governs the discretization error?

    Options

    Derivative pricing calculates the expected payoff \(\E[g(S_H)]\), which is governed strictly by weak convergence.

  5. What challenge arises when assessing weak convergence numerically in a Monte Carlo simulation?

    Options

    Unless \(M\) is very large (millions of paths), statistical sampling noise dominates the tiny weak discretization error.

  6. What type of derivative payoff can cause the weak convergence order of Euler–Maruyama to deteriorate below 1.0?

    Options

    Discontinuities violate the smooth test function requirement, while barrier options suffer from discrete monitoring boundary bias.

  7. What is the analytical expectation \(\E[S_N]\) of an Euler approximation of GBM with drift \(\mu\) after \(N\) steps with \(\Delta t = H / N\)?

    Options

    By the tower property, \(\E[S_{k+1} \mid S_k] = S_k(1 + \mu \Delta t)\), so \(\E[S_N] = s_0(1 + \mu \Delta t)^N\).
