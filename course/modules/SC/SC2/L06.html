Existence and Uniqueness of SDE Solutions

  • Uses: Itô isometry, contraction mapping, Banach fixed-point theorem, Cauchy sequence, completeness of reals

Before designing numerical simulation algorithms or estimating parameters from market time series, one must verify that the underlying continuous-time differential equation possesses a well-defined mathematical solution. In deterministic calculus, Cauchy and Picard established that Lipschitz continuity guarantees a unique trajectory. For stochastic differential equations driven by Brownian motion, the Itô-Picard iteration combined with Gronwall's inequality establishes that global Lipschitz continuity prevents branch splitting while linear growth rules out finite-time explosion.

1Formulation and Regularity Hypotheses

Definition 1.1 (Strong Solution to an Itô SDE)

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \in [0, H]}, \Prob)\) be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions, and let \((B_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\) be a standard 1-dimensional Brownian motion adapted to \((\mathcal{F}_t)\). Given measurable coefficient functions \(b: [0, H] \times \R \to \R\) and \(\sigma: [0, H] \times \R \to \R\) and an \(\mathcal{F}_0\)-measurable initial condition \(X_0 = x_0\) with \(\E[|x_0|^2] < \infty\), an adapted, almost surely continuous stochastic process \((X_t)_{t \in [0, H]}\) is a strong solution to the stochastic differential equation:

\[ dX_t = b(t, X_t) \, dt + \sigma(t, X_t) \, dB_t, \quad X_0 = x_0, \]

if for all \(t \in [0, H]\), the trajectories satisfy \(\int_0^t |b(s, X_s)| \, ds < \infty\) and \(\int_0^t |\sigma(s, X_s)|^2 \, ds < \infty\) almost surely, and:

\[ X_t = x_0 + \int_0^t b(s, X_s) \, ds + \int_0^t \sigma(s, X_s) \, dB_s \quad \text{almost surely.} \]
Definition 1.2 (Global Lipschitz and Linear Growth Conditions)

The coefficients \(b(t, x)\) and \(\sigma(t, x)\) satisfy:

  • (A1) Global Lipschitz condition: There exists a uniform constant \(K < \infty\) such that for all \(t \in [0, H]\) and all \(x, y \in \R\): \[ |b(t, x) - b(t, y)| + |\sigma(t, x) - \sigma(t, y)| \le K |x - y|. \]
  • (A2) Linear growth condition: There exists a uniform constant \(C < \infty\) such that for all \(t \in [0, H]\) and all \(x \in \R\): \[ |b(t, x)|^2 + |\sigma(t, x)|^2 \le C^2 (1 + |x|^2). \]
Remark 1.3 (Role of the Two Conditions)

The Lipschitz condition prevents trajectories from branching or splitting at any point, guaranteeing pathwise uniqueness. The linear growth condition bounds the velocity of the process at large amplitudes, ensuring that paths do not blow up to infinity in finite time (\(\Prob(\sup_{t \in [0, H]} |X_t| < \infty) = 1\)). In fact, if \(b(t, 0)\) and \(\sigma(t, 0)\) are bounded, condition A1 automatically implies condition A2 via the triangle inequality.

2Gronwall's Inequality and Pathwise Uniqueness

Lemma 2.1 (Gronwall's Integral Inequality)

Assumptions: (A1) \(\phi: [0, H] \to [0, \infty)\) is a non-negative, continuous function; (A2) \(\alpha \ge 0\) and \(\beta \ge 0\) are non-negative constants such that for all \(t \in [0, H]\):

\[ \phi(t) \le \alpha + \beta \int_0^t \phi(s) \, ds. \]

Statement: For all \(t \in [0, H]\), the function satisfies:

\[ \phi(t) \le \alpha e^{\beta t}. \]

In particular, if \(\alpha = 0\), then \(\phi(t) = 0\) for all \(t \in [0, H]\).

Proof

Define the integral function \(\psi(t) = \alpha + \beta \int_0^t \phi(s) \, ds\). Because \(\phi\) is continuous, \(\psi\) is continuously differentiable by the fundamental theorem of calculus, with derivative \(\psi'(t) = \beta \phi(t)\) and initial value \(\psi(0) = \alpha\). By hypothesis, \(\phi(t) \le \psi(t)\). Therefore:

\[ \psi'(t) = \beta \phi(t) \le \beta \psi(t). \]

Rearranging and multiplying by the integrating factor \(e^{-\beta t} > 0\):

\[ e^{-\beta t} \psi'(t) - \beta e^{-\beta t} \psi(t) \le 0 \implies \frac{d}{dt}\left( e^{-\beta t} \psi(t) \right) \le 0. \]

Integrating this differential inequality from \(0\) to \(t\):

\[ e^{-\beta t} \psi(t) - \psi(0) \le 0 \implies \psi(t) \le \psi(0) e^{\beta t} = \alpha e^{\beta t}. \]

Finally, since \(\phi(t) \le \psi(t)\), we conclude \(\phi(t) \le \alpha e^{\beta t}\). If \(\alpha = 0\), then \(0 \le \phi(t) \le 0\), proving \(\phi(t) \equiv 0\).

Theorem 2.2 (Pathwise Uniqueness)

Assumptions: (A1) \(b(t, x)\) and \(\sigma(t, x)\) satisfy the global Lipschitz condition with constant \(K\); (A2) \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\) are two continuous strong solutions to the same SDE driven by the same Brownian motion \(B_t\) with identical initial condition \(X_0 = Y_0 = x_0\).

Statement: The solutions are indistinguishable: \(\Prob(X_t = Y_t \text{ for all } t \in [0, H]) = 1\).

Proof

For any \(t \in [0, H]\), subtracting the integral equations for \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\):

\[ X_t - Y_t = \int_0^t \big( b(s, X_s) - b(s, Y_s) \big) \, ds + \int_0^t \big( \sigma(s, X_s) - \sigma(s, Y_s) \big) \, dB_s. \]

Using the algebraic inequality \((u + v)^2 \le 2u^2 + 2v^2\):

\[ |X_t - Y_t|^2 \le 2 \left( \int_0^t |b(s, X_s) - b(s, Y_s)| \, ds \right)^2 + 2 \left( \int_0^t \big(\sigma(s, X_s) - \sigma(s, Y_s)\big) \, dB_s \right)^2. \]

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the deterministic integral gives \((\int_0^t 1 \cdot |b_s - b_s'| ds)^2 \le t \int_0^t |b_s - b_s'|^2 ds \le H \int_0^t |b(s, X_s) - b(s, Y_s)|^2 ds\). Applying the Itô isometry (Theorem 1.1 of Lesson 2) to the stochastic integral:

\[ \E\left[ |X_t - Y_t|^2 \right] \le 2 H \int_0^t \E\left[ |b(s, X_s) - b(s, Y_s)|^2 \right] ds + 2 \int_0^t \E\left[ |\sigma(s, X_s) - \sigma(s, Y_s)|^2 \right] ds. \]

By the Lipschitz condition, \(|b(s, x) - b(s, y)|^2 \le K^2 |x - y|^2\) and \(|\sigma(s, x) - \sigma(s, y)|^2 \le K^2 |x - y|^2\). Substituting these bounds:

\[ \E\left[ |X_t - Y_t|^2 \right] \le 2 K^2 (H + 1) \int_0^t \E\left[ |X_s - Y_s|^2 \right] ds. \]

Define \(\phi(t) = \E[|X_t - Y_t|^2]\) and constant \(\beta = 2 K^2 (H + 1)\). The inequality reads \(\phi(t) \le \beta \int_0^t \phi(s) ds\) with \(\alpha = 0\). By Lemma 2.1 (Gronwall's inequality), \(\phi(t) = 0\) for all \(t \in [0, H]\). Since \(X\) and \(Y\) have continuous sample paths, \(\Prob(X_t = Y_t \text{ for all } t \in [0, H]) = 1\).

3Picard Iteration and Existence of Strong Solutions

Definition 3.1 (Picard Iteration Scheme)

The Picard iteration sequence \((X_t^{(n)})_{n \ge 0}\) is defined inductively by:

\[ X_t^{(0)} = x_0, \] \[ X_t^{(n+1)} = x_0 + \int_0^t b\big(s, X_s^{(n)}\big) \, ds + \int_0^t \sigma\big(s, X_s^{(n)}\big) \, dB_s \quad \text{for } n \ge 0. \]
Theorem 3.2 (Existence via Contraction Mapping)

Assumptions: (A1) \(b(t, x)\) and \(\sigma(t, x)\) satisfy global Lipschitz condition A1 and linear growth condition A2 on \([0, H] \times \R\); (A2) \(\E[|x_0|^2] < \infty\).

Statement: The Picard iterates form a Cauchy sequence in the Banach space of continuous adapted processes endowed with the weighted norm \(\|X\|_\lambda^2 = \E[\sup_{t \in [0, H]} e^{-\lambda t} |X_t|^2]\). By the completeness of reals and the Banach fixed-point theorem for contraction mappings, there exists a unique strong solution \(X \in \mathcal{C}([0, H]; L^2(\Omega))\) satisfying the SDE (Øksendal, 2003, Theorem 5.2.1).

Proof Sketch

By induction and the linear growth condition, each iterate \(X^{(n)}\) has finite second moments: \(\sup_{t \in [0, H]} \E[|X_t^{(n)}|^2] \le M < \infty\). Let \(D_n(t) = \E[|X_t^{(n+1)} - X_t^{(n)}|^2]\). Applying Cauchy–Schwarz and the Itô isometry as in Theorem 2.2:

\[ D_n(t) \le C_1 \int_0^t D_{n-1}(s) \, ds, \quad \text{where } C_1 = 2 K^2 (H + 1). \]

Iterating this integral inequality \(n\) times starting from \(D_0(s) \le C_0\) yields the geometric bound:

\[ D_n(t) \le C_0 \frac{(C_1 t)^n}{n!} \le C_0 \frac{(C_1 H)^n}{n!}. \]

Applying Doob's maximal inequality to the martingale part bounds \(\E[\sup_{t \in [0, H]} |X_t^{(n+1)} - X_t^{(n)}|^2] \le C_2 \frac{(C_1 H)^n}{n!}\). By the Weierstrass M-test and the Borel–Cantelli lemma, the sequence of sample paths converges uniformly on \([0, H]\) almost surely. Passing to the limit inside the integrals demonstrates that the limit process satisfies the SDE.

Lab 1 · Picard iteration for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE on a fixed Brownian path

For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE \(dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t)dt + \sigma dB_t\) with \(x_0 = 0.5\), \(\kappa = 2.0\), \(\theta = 1.0\), and \(\sigma = 0.30\) on \([0, 1]\), the Picard iteration mapping contracts geometrically. Successive iterates \(n = 1\) to \(6\) evaluated along a single fixed Brownian path (SIMULATED) exhibit strictly decaying supremum errors \(\sup_{t \in [0, 1]} |X_t^{(n)} - X_t^{\mathrm{exact}}|\).

"""Lab 1 · Picard iteration for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck SDE on a fixed Brownian path.

Under global Lipschitz and linear growth conditions, the Picard iteration X^{(0)}_t = x0, X^{(n+1)}_t = x0 +
int_0^t b(s, X^{(n)}_s) ds + int_0^t sigma(s, X^{(n)}_s) dB_s converges uniformly on compact intervals to the unique
solution. For the OU equation dX_t = kappa(theta - X_t) dt + sigma dB_t, the Picard map contracts geometrically. This
lab generates one fixed Brownian path with N = 100 steps over [0, 1] from seed 42 (SIMULATED), computes successive
Picard iterates n = 1 through 6, and prints the supremum norm error sup_{t in [0, 1]} |X^{(n)}_t - X^{exact}_t| against
the reference path, demonstrating geometric contraction.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    x0 = 0.5
    kappa = 2.0
    theta = 1.0
    sigma = 0.30
    horizon = 1.0
    n_steps = 100
    dt = horizon / n_steps
    sq_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
    seed = 42

    rng = random.Random(seed)
    b_path = [0.0]
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        b_path.append(b_path[-1] + rng.gauss(0.0, sq_dt))

    # Reference exact path
    x_exact = [x0]
    for k in range(n_steps):
        db = b_path[k + 1] - b_path[k]
        x_val = x_exact[-1] + kappa * (theta - x_exact[-1]) * dt + sigma * db
        x_exact.append(x_val)

    # Picard iterates: X^{(0)}_k = x0
    x_n = [x0] * (n_steps + 1)
    print("Picard iterate supremum errors on [0, 1]:")
    for it in range(1, 7):
        x_iter_path = [x0]
        for k in range(1, n_steps + 1):
            int_x = sum(x_n[j] for j in range(k)) * dt
            val = x0 + kappa * theta * (k * dt) - kappa * int_x + sigma * b_path[k]
            x_iter_path.append(val)
        sup_err = max(abs(x_iter_path[k] - x_exact[k]) for k in range(n_steps + 1))
        print(f"iterate {it}: sup-error = {sup_err:.6e}")
        x_n = x_iter_path

4Where the canon breaks

The existence and uniqueness theorem rests on global Lipschitz continuity and linear growth. When either hypothesis is breached, solutions can fail to exist, blow up in finite time, or bifurcate into non-unique trajectories:

  • Finite-Time Blow-Up (Superlinear Drift): Consider the SDE \(dX_t = X_t^2 \, dt + dB_t\) with \(X_0 = 1\). The drift \(b(x) = x^2\) violates linear growth. In finite positive time, paths escape to \(+\infty\), meaning a global solution does not exist on \([0, H]\).
  • Non-Uniqueness (Hölder Drift): Consider \(dX_t = 3 X_t^{2/3} \, dt\) starting at \(X_0 = 0\). The function \(x \mapsto 3 x^{2/3}\) is continuous but not Lipschitz at the origin (its derivative diverges as \(x \to 0\)). This equation admits infinitely many solutions: \(X_t \equiv 0\), or \(X_t = t^3\), or paths that stay at zero until an arbitrary stopping time and then follow \((t - \tau)^3\).
  • Square-Root Volatility: The Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) interest rate model features diffusion \(\sigma(x) = \sigma \sqrt{x}\). Since \(\sqrt{x}\) is \(1/2\)-Hölder continuous rather than Lipschitz at \(x = 0\), the classical Picard theorem does not apply directly. Uniqueness must instead be established via the specialized Yamada–Watanabe theorem.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Continuity of the drift and diffusion coefficients is sufficient to guarantee a unique solution.”

    Continuity guarantees existence of weak solutions (Peano-type theorems), but does not guarantee uniqueness. As demonstrated by \(dX_t = 3 X_t^{2/3} dt\), continuous coefficients can exhibit multiple distinct solutions from the same initial state unless Lipschitz continuity is satisfied.

  2. “Local Lipschitz continuity guarantees that solutions exist for all time.”

    Local Lipschitz continuity guarantees a unique local solution up to an explosion time \(\tau\). If linear growth fails (such as \(b(x) = x^3\)), the explosion time can be finite (\(\tau < \infty\)) with positive probability, terminating the solution prematurely.

  3. “Weak uniqueness is identical to pathwise uniqueness.”

    Pathwise uniqueness means any two solutions driven by the exact same Brownian motion agree path-for-path (\(X_t(\omega) = Y_t(\omega)\)). Weak uniqueness means any two solutions have the same probability distribution on path space, which is a strictly weaker condition.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Checking the Lipschitz condition for GBM

Verify that geometric Brownian motion coefficients \(b(x) = \mu x\) and \(\sigma(x) = \sigma x\) satisfy the global Lipschitz condition on any compact subset \([-M, M]\) and find the Lipschitz constant.

Solution

For any \(x, y \in \R\): \(|b(x) - b(y)| = |\mu(x - y)| = |\mu| |x - y|\) and \(|\sigma(x) - \sigma(y)| = |\sigma(x - y)| = \sigma |x - y|\). Summing them gives \(|b(x) - b(y)| + |\sigma(x) - \sigma(y)| = (|\mu| + \sigma)|x - y|\). Thus, GBM satisfies the global Lipschitz condition on all of \(\R\) with constant \(K = |\mu| + \sigma\).

M2 · Gronwall bound evaluation

Suppose a non-negative continuous function satisfies \(\phi(t) \le 0.05 + 4.0 \int_0^t \phi(s) ds\). Compute the upper bound on \(\phi(0.5)\) provided by Gronwall's inequality.

Solution

Applying Lemma 2.1 with \(\alpha = 0.05\) and \(\beta = 4.0\):

\[ \phi(t) \le \alpha e^{\beta t} \implies \phi(0.5) \le 0.05 e^{4.0 \times 0.5} = 0.05 e^{2.0} \approx 0.05 \times 7.389056 = 0.369453. \]
M3 · Linear growth constant for OU

For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with \(b(x) = \kappa(\theta - x)\) and \(\sigma(x) = \sigma\), establish a linear growth bound of the form \(|b(x)|^2 + |\sigma(x)|^2 \le C^2(1 + x^2)\).

Solution

Using \((u - v)^2 \le 2u^2 + 2v^2\): \(|b(x)|^2 = \kappa^2(\theta - x)^2 \le 2\kappa^2\theta^2 + 2\kappa^2 x^2\). Adding \(|\sigma(x)|^2 = \sigma^2\) gives \(|b(x)|^2 + |\sigma(x)|^2 \le (2\kappa^2\theta^2 + \sigma^2) + 2\kappa^2 x^2 \le \max(2\kappa^2\theta^2 + \sigma^2, 2\kappa^2)(1 + x^2)\). Setting \(C^2 = \max(2\kappa^2\theta^2 + \sigma^2, 2\kappa^2)\) satisfies the condition.

P1 · Gronwall with time-dependent kernel

Let \(\phi(t) \le \alpha + \int_0^t \beta(s) \phi(s) ds\) where \(\beta(s) \ge 0\) is integrable. Prove that \(\phi(t) \le \alpha \exp(\int_0^t \beta(s) ds)\).

Solution

Let \(\psi(t) = \alpha + \int_0^t \beta(s) \phi(s) ds\). Then \(\psi'(t) = \beta(t) \phi(t) \le \beta(t) \psi(t)\). Multiplying by \(\exp(-\int_0^t \beta(s) ds)\) yields \(\frac{d}{dt}[\psi(t) \exp(-\int_0^t \beta(s) ds)] \le 0\). Integrating from \(0\) to \(t\) gives \(\psi(t) \le \psi(0) \exp(\int_0^t \beta(s) ds) = \alpha \exp(\int_0^t \beta(s) ds)\). Since \(\phi(t) \le \psi(t)\), the bound follows.

P2 · Non-Lipschitz branching proof

Prove that \(dX_t = 2 \sqrt{|X_t|} dt\) with \(X_0 = 0\) has at least two distinct continuous solutions for \(t \ge 0\).

Solution

First, \(X_t^{(1)} \equiv 0\) satisfies \(X_0^{(1)} = 0\) and \(d(0) = 0 = 2\sqrt{0} dt\). Second, let \(X_t^{(2)} = t^2\). Then \(X_0^{(2)} = 0\) and \(\frac{d}{dt}(t^2) = 2t = 2\sqrt{t^2} = 2\sqrt{|X_t^{(2)}|}\). Both trajectories are valid continuous strong solutions, demonstrating failure of uniqueness due to the non-Lipschitz behavior of \(\sqrt{|x|}\) at \(x = 0\).

P3 · Continuous dependence on initial data

Let \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\) be solutions to the same Lipschitz SDE with different initial values \(X_0 = x_0\) and \(Y_0 = y_0\). Prove that \(\E[|X_t - Y_t|^2] \le 2 |x_0 - y_0|^2 e^{C t}\).

Solution

Subtracting gives \(X_t - Y_t = (x_0 - y_0) + \int_0^t (b_s - b_s') ds + \int_0^t (\sigma_s - \sigma_s') dB_s\). Squaring and taking expectations with \((u + v)^2 \le 2u^2 + 2v^2\): \(\E[|X_t - Y_t|^2] \le 2|x_0 - y_0|^2 + 2 C_1 \int_0^t \E[|X_s - Y_s|^2] ds\). Applying Gronwall's inequality yields \(\E[|X_t - Y_t|^2] \le 2 |x_0 - y_0|^2 e^{2 C_1 t}\).

E1 · Contraction rate estimation

From the Lab 1 output, the supremum errors for iterates 4, 5, and 6 are \(8.146 \times 10^{-2}\), \(2.673 \times 10^{-2}\), and \(7.385 \times 10^{-3}\). Calculate the empirical contraction ratio between successive iterations.

Ratio 5 to 4: \(\frac{2.6726 \times 10^{-2}}{8.1463 \times 10^{-2}} \approx 0.328\). Ratio 6 to 5: \(\frac{7.3847 \times 10^{-3}}{2.6726 \times 10^{-2}} \approx 0.276\). Both ratios are strictly less than 1, confirming rapid geometric convergence on \([0, 1]\).

E2 · Explosion detection in numerical simulation

Simulating \(dX_t = X_t^3 dt + 0.1 dB_t\) with Euler steps on \([0, 1]\) from \(X_0 = 2.0\) produces floating-point overflow. Explain why adaptive time-stepping cannot resolve the mathematical blow-up.

Solution

The ODE \(\dot{x} = x^3\) has solution \(x(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x_0^{-2} - 2t}}\). For \(x_0 = 2\), the denominator vanishes at \(t^* = 1/(2 \times 4) = 0.125\). At \(t^*\), \(x(t) \to \infty\). Adaptive time-stepping will shrink \(\Delta t \to 0\) as \(t \to t^*\) and stall completely, because the continuous solution itself escapes to infinity in finite time.

E3 · Stopping times for local solutions

When coefficients are only locally Lipschitz, define the sequence of stopping times \(\tau_R = \inf\{t \ge 0 : |X_t| \ge R\}\). Explain how \(\tau_R\) allows one to construct a maximal local solution.

Solution

On the truncated domain \([-R, R]\), one can modify \(b(t, x)\) and \(\sigma(t, x)\) smoothly to be globally Lipschitz with constant \(K_R\). The global theorem provides a unique solution \(X_t^R\) on \([0, \tau_R]\). As \(R \to \infty\), \(\tau_R\) is non-decreasing. The limit \(\tau_\infty = \lim_{R \to \infty} \tau_R\) is the explosion time, and the pasted process \(X_t\) is the unique strong solution on \([0, \tau_\infty)\).

Research

R1 · Yamada–Watanabe theorem for non-Lipschitz diffusion

The Yamada–Watanabe theorem states that if \(|\sigma(x) - \sigma(y)| \le \rho(|x - y|)\) where \(\int_0^\epsilon \rho(u)^{-2} du = \infty\), pathwise uniqueness holds. Verify whether \(\rho(u) = \sqrt{u}\) satisfies this integral condition.

Solution

For \(\rho(u) = \sqrt{u}\), \(\rho(u)^2 = u\). The integral is \(\int_0^\epsilon \frac{1}{u} du = [\ln u]_0^\epsilon = \ln \epsilon - (-\infty) = \infty\). Because the integral diverges at the lower limit, the Yamada–Watanabe condition is satisfied, proving that square-root diffusion diffusions (such as CIR) possess pathwise uniqueness despite failing Lipschitz continuity.

R2 · Strong versus weak solutions

Define what constitutes a weak solution to an SDE and explain why Tanaka's equation \(dX_t = \mathrm{sgn}(X_t) dB_t\) has a weak solution but no strong solution.

Solution

A strong solution requires the path \(X_t\) to be adapted to the pre-specified filtration of a given Brownian motion \(B_t\). A weak solution consists of a triple \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \Prob)\), a filtration, a Brownian motion \(B_t\), and an adapted process \(X_t\) satisfying the equation. For Tanaka's SDE, if \(X_t = W_t\) is a Brownian motion, then \(B_t = \int_0^t \mathrm{sgn}(W_s) dW_s\) is a Brownian motion by Lévy's characterization, giving a weak solution. However, Tanaka proved that \(\mathcal{F}_t^B = \mathcal{F}_t^{|W|}\), which cannot distinguish the sign of \(W_t\), meaning \(X_t\) cannot be adapted to \(\mathcal{F}_t^B\).

R3 · Picard iteration in weighted Bielecki norm

Define the Bielecki norm \(\|X\|_\beta = \sup_{t \in [0, H]} e^{-\beta t} \sqrt{\E[|X_t|^2]}\). Show that choosing \(\beta > K^2(H + 1)\) makes the Picard operator a strict contraction without subdividing time.

Solution

Let \(\mathcal{T}(X)_t = x_0 + \int_0^t b(s, X_s) ds + \int_0^t \sigma(s, X_s) dB_s\). By the Lipschitz estimates, \(\E[|\mathcal{T}(X)_t - \mathcal{T}(Y)_t|^2] \le 2 K^2(H + 1) \int_0^t \E[|X_s - Y_s|^2] ds\). Multiplying by \(e^{-2\beta t}\) and taking the supremum reveals that \(\|\mathcal{T}(X) - \mathcal{T}(Y)\|_\beta^2 \le \frac{2K^2(H+1)}{2\beta} \|X - Y\|_\beta^2\). For \(\beta > K^2(H+1)\), the constant is strictly less than 1, proving \(\mathcal{T}\) is a strict contraction on the entire interval \([0, H]\).

    Options

    Linear growth prevents trajectories from growing faster than an exponential envelope, preventing finite-time explosion.

  2. In the proof of pathwise uniqueness, which inequality directly converts \(\E[|X_t - Y_t|^2] \le \beta \int_0^t \E[|X_s - Y_s|^2] ds\) into \(\E[|X_t - Y_t|^2] = 0\)?

    Options

    Gronwall's lemma states that \(\phi(t) \le \alpha e^{\beta t}\). Setting \(\alpha = 0\) forces \(\phi(t) \equiv 0\).

  3. Why does the equation \(dX_t = 3 X_t^{2/3} dt\) with \(X_0 = 0\) violate the standard uniqueness theorem?

    Options

    The derivative \(\frac{d}{dx}(3x^{2/3}) = 2 x^{-1/3}\) diverges to infinity as \(x \to 0\), violating the Lipschitz condition.

  4. If \(\phi(t) \le 5.0 \int_0^t \phi(s) ds\) on \([0, 1]\) with \(\phi(t) \ge 0\), what is the maximum value of \(\phi(1)\)?

    By Gronwall's inequality with \(\alpha = 0\), \(\phi(t) \le 0 \cdot e^{5t} = 0\).

  5. How does the Picard iteration scheme generate successive trajectory approximations?

    Options

    Picard iteration substitutes the previous path iterate \(X^{(n)}\) into the integral SDE equation to generate \(X^{(n+1)}\).

  6. What rate of convergence does the Picard iteration sequence achieve in mean square error \(\E[|X_t^{(n+1)} - X_t^{(n)}|^2]\)?

    Options

    Repeated integration of the bound over the simplex yields the factorial factor \(1/n!\).

  7. What distinguishes a strong solution from a weak solution of an SDE?

    Options

    In a strong solution, the probability space and Brownian motion are fixed in advance, and the solution path is adapted to that specific Brownian filtration.

  8. Which specialized theorem establishes pathwise uniqueness for the square-root diffusion \(\sigma \sqrt{X_t}\) of the CIR model?

    Options

    The Yamada–Watanabe theorem provides pathwise uniqueness for non-Lipschitz diffusions whose modulus of continuity satisfies \(\int_0^\epsilon \rho(u)^{-2} du = \infty\).
