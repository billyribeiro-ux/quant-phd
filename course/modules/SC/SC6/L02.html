The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman Equation

  • Prerequisites: Itô's lemma, Taylor's theorem, partial derivative, gradient, Hessian
  • Introduces: Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study

Discrete dynamic programming requires recursive backward induction over state grids at each epoch. In continuous time, taking the infinitesimal limit of the Dynamic Programming Principle collapses the discrete recursion into a single non-linear partial differential equation: the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation. Solving this equation reduces dynamic stochastic optimization to static pointwise maximization of a Hamiltonian, identifying optimal feedback policies in closed form or via targeted numerical solvers.

1Infinitesimal Generators of Controlled Diffusions

Let \(X_t\) satisfy the controlled stochastic differential equation on \(\R^d\):

\[ dX_s = b(s, X_s, u_s) \, ds + \sigma(s, X_s, u_s) \, dB_s, \]

where \(B\) is an \(m\)-dimensional standard Brownian motion. For each fixed control value \(u \in U\), we associate a second-order linear differential operator acting on test functions.

Definition 2.1 (Controlled Infinitesimal Generator)

For a fixed control vector \(u \in U\) and a function \(\phi \in C^2(\R^d)\), the controlled infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{A}^u\) is defined by:

\[ \mathcal{A}^u \phi(x) \coloneqq b(x, u) \cdot \nabla \phi(x) + \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr}\left( \sigma(x, u) \sigma(x, u)^\top \nabla^2 \phi(x) \right). \]

In coordinates, writing \(a(x, u) \coloneqq \sigma(x, u)\sigma(x, u)^\top \in \R^{d \times d}\):

\[ \mathcal{A}^u \phi(x) = \sum_{i=1}^d b_i(x, u) \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x_i}(x) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^d \sum_{j=1}^d a_{ij}(x, u) \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}(x). \]
Remark 2.2 (Probabilistic Interpretation)

By Dynkin's formula, for any stopping time \(\tau\) with finite expectation:

\[ \E\left[ \phi(X_\tau) \;\middle|\; X_0 = x \right] = \phi(x) + \E\left[ \int_0^\tau \mathcal{A}^{u_s} \phi(X_s) \, ds \;\middle|\; X_0 = x \right]. \]

Hence \(\mathcal{A}^u \phi(x) = \lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{\E[\phi(X_h^u) \mid X_0 = x] - \phi(x)}{h}\), representing the expected instantaneous rate of change of \(\phi\) along the diffusion path.

2Derivation of the HJB Equation from the DPP

Assume that the value function \(V(t, x)\) is continuously differentiable in time \(t\) and twice continuously differentiable in state \(x\), i.e., \(V \in C^{1, 2}([0, T) \times \R^d)\). We derive the differential governing equation directly from Theorem 1.4.

Theorem 2.3 (Heuristic Derivation of the HJB PDE)

Assumptions: (A1) \(V \in C^{1, 2}([0, T) \times \R^d)\); (A2) Coefficients \(b, \sigma, f\) are continuous in \((t, x, u)\); (A3) The Dynamic Programming Principle holds on \([t, t + h]\).

Statement: At every point \((t, x) \in [0, T) \times \R^d\), the value function satisfies:

\[ \partial_t V(t, x) - \rho V(t, x) + \sup_{u \in U} \left\{ \mathcal{A}^u V(t, x) + f(t, x, u) \right\} = 0. \]
Derivation

Fix \((t, x)\) and a small time increment \(h > 0\). Choose a constant control \(u_s \equiv u \in U\) for \(s \in [t, t + h]\). By the Dynamic Programming Principle (Theorem 1.4):

\[ V(t, x) \ge \E\left[ \int_t^{t+h} e^{-\rho(s - t)} f(s, X_s^u, u) \, ds + e^{-\rho h} V(t + h, X_{t+h}^u) \;\middle|\; X_t = x \right]. \]

Applying Itô's lemma to the discounted process \(Y_s \coloneqq e^{-\rho(s - t)} V(s, X_s^u)\) on \([t, t+h]\):

\[ e^{-\rho h} V(t + h, X_{t+h}^u) - V(t, x) = \int_t^{t+h} e^{-\rho(s - t)} \left( \partial_t V - \rho V + \mathcal{A}^u V \right)(s, X_s^u) \, ds + \int_t^{t+h} e^{-\rho(s - t)} \nabla V(s, X_s^u) \sigma(s, X_s^u, u) \, dB_s. \]

Taking the conditional expectation eliminates the stochastic integral martingale term. Substituting back into the DPP inequality yields:

\[ 0 \ge \E\left[ \int_t^{t+h} e^{-\rho(s - t)} \left( \partial_t V(s, X_s^u) - \rho V(s, X_s^u) + \mathcal{A}^u V(s, X_s^u) + f(s, X_s^u, u) \right) \, ds \right]. \]

Dividing by \(h\) and letting \(h \downarrow 0\), the mean value theorem gives:

\[ 0 \ge \partial_t V(t, x) - \rho V(t, x) + \mathcal{A}^u V(t, x) + f(t, x, u). \]

Since this inequality holds for all \(u \in U\), it holds for the supremum:

\[ \partial_t V(t, x) - \rho V(t, x) + \sup_{u \in U} \left\{ \mathcal{A}^u V(t, x) + f(t, x, u) \right\} \le 0. \]

Conversely, if an optimal control \(u^*\) exists, the DPP holds with exact equality along \(u^*\), establishing the reverse inequality \(\ge 0\). Hence equality holds, establishing the HJB equation.

3Formulation of the HJB Equation

Definition 2.4 (Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman Equation)

The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation for finite-horizon stochastic control is the non-linear terminal-value partial differential equation:

\[ \begin{cases} \partial_t V(t, x) - \rho V(t, x) + \sup_{u \in U} \left\{ b(t, x, u) \cdot \nabla V(t, x) + \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr}\left( \sigma(t, x, u) \sigma(t, x, u)^\top \nabla^2 V(t, x) \right) + f(t, x, u) \right\} = 0, \\ V(T, x) = g(x), \quad x \in \R^d. \end{cases} \]

For an infinite-horizon discounted problem where \(f(t, x, u) = f(x, u)\) and \(T = \infty\), the value function is stationary (\(\partial_t V = 0\)), yielding the stationary HJB equation:

\[ \rho V(x) - \sup_{u \in U} \left\{ b(x, u) \cdot \nabla V(x) + \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr}\left( \sigma(x, u) \sigma(x, u)^\top \nabla^2 V(x) \right) + f(x, u) \right\} = 0. \]

4The Continuous-Time Hamiltonian

Definition 2.5 (Continuous-Time Hamiltonian)

The Hamiltonian function \(\mathcal{H}: \R^d \times \R^d \times \mathcal{S}^d \to \R\) (where \(\mathcal{S}^d\) is the space of symmetric \(d \times d\) matrices) is defined by:

\[ \mathcal{H}(x, p, M) \coloneqq \sup_{u \in U} \left\{ b(x, u) \cdot p + \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr}\left( \sigma(x, u) \sigma(x, u)^\top M \right) + f(x, u) \right\}. \]

In terms of \(\mathcal{H}\), the stationary HJB equation takes the compact operator form:

\[ \rho V(x) - \mathcal{H}(x, \nabla V(x), \nabla^2 V(x)) = 0. \]
Example 2.6 (Linear-Quadratic Regulator Hamiltonian)

Consider a 1D scalar diffusion \(dX_t = (a X_t + u_t) dt + \sigma dB_t\) with running reward \(f(x, u) = -\frac{1}{2}(q x^2 + r_u u^2)\) where \(q, r_u > 0\). The Hamiltonian is:

\[ \mathcal{H}(x, p, M) = a x p + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 M - \frac{1}{2} q x^2 + \sup_{u \in \R} \left\{ p u - \frac{1}{2} r_u u^2 \right\}. \]

The unconstrained maximum over \(u\) occurs where the derivative vanishes: \(p - r_u u = 0 \implies u^*(p) = \frac{p}{r_u}\). Substituting this optimal feedback gives:

\[ \mathcal{H}(x, p, M) = a x p + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 M - \frac{1}{2} q x^2 + \frac{p^2}{2 r_u}. \]

Lab 1 · Discrete Bellman operator convergence to the continuous HJB operator

Under the exact continuous solution \(V(w)\) of Merton's portfolio problem, the discrete-time Bellman operator continuation difference \(\frac{T_{\Delta t}[V](w) - V(w)}{\Delta t}\) computed via Gauss–Hermite quadrature converges to zero at linear rate \(O(\Delta t)\) as \(\Delta t \downarrow 0\).

r"""Lab 1 · Discrete Bellman operator convergence to the continuous HJB operator.

Demonstrates that the discrete-time dynamic programming operator
T_{dt}[V](w) = U(c*) dt + e^(-rho dt) E[V(w + dw)]
satisfies (T_{dt}[V](w) - V(w)) / dt -> 0 with O(dt) error as dt -> 0
along the exact continuous solution V(w).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def main() -> None:
    # Model parameters (SIMULATED)
    r = 0.03
    mu = 0.08
    sigma = 0.20
    gamma = 2.0
    rho = 0.05
    w0 = 2.0

    # Unconstrained Merton solution
    pi_star = (mu - r) / (gamma * sigma**2)
    k = r + pi_star * (mu - r) - 0.5 * gamma * (pi_star**2) * (sigma**2)
    nu = (rho - (1.0 - gamma) * k) / gamma

    def value(w: float) -> float:
        return float((nu ** (-gamma)) / (1.0 - gamma) * (w ** (1.0 - gamma)))

    c_star = nu * w0
    u_c = (c_star ** (1.0 - gamma)) / (1.0 - gamma)
    v0 = value(w0)

    # 5-point Gauss-Hermite quadrature for standard normal expectation
    gh_nodes = [
        -2.8569700138728056,
        -1.355626179974266,
        0.0,
        1.355626179974266,
        2.8569700138728056,
    ]
    gh_weights = [
        0.0112574113277207,
        0.2220759220056126,
        0.5333333333333333,
        0.2220759220056126,
        0.0112574113277207,
    ]

    dt_values = [0.04, 0.01, 0.0025]
    residuals: list[float] = []

    print(f"V(w0) exact: {v0:.6f}")
    print(f"pi*: {pi_star:.4f}, c*: {c_star:.4f}")

    drift_w = (r + pi_star * (mu - r)) * w0 - c_star
    vol_w = pi_star * sigma * w0

    for dt in dt_values:
        sq_dt = math.sqrt(dt)
        e_cont = 0.0
        for z, w_weight in zip(gh_nodes, gh_weights, strict=True):
            w_next = w0 + drift_w * dt + vol_w * sq_dt * z
            e_cont += w_weight * value(w_next)

        t_dt = u_c * dt + math.exp(-rho * dt) * e_cont
        res = (t_dt - v0) / dt
        residuals.append(res)
        print(f"dt = {dt:.4f}: (T_dt[V] - V) / dt = {res:+.6e}")

    # Verify first-order O(dt) convergence: ratio should be approximately 4.0
    ratio1 = residuals[0] / residuals[1]
    ratio2 = residuals[1] / residuals[2]
    print(f"convergence ratio dt=0.04 to 0.01: {ratio1:.3f}")
    print(f"convergence ratio dt=0.01 to 0.0025: {ratio2:.3f}")
    print(f"linear convergence confirmed (ratios in [3.9, 4.1]): {3.9 <= ratio1 <= 4.1 and 3.9 <= ratio2 <= 4.1}")


main()

The lab confirms the heuristic derivation of Theorem 2.3: as the time increment \(\Delta t\) decreases by a factor of 4 (from 0.04 to 0.01 to 0.0025), the discrete Bellman operator residual scales down by factors of 3.999 and 4.000, establishing exact \(O(\Delta t)\) first-order convergence to the continuous HJB operator.

Where the canon breaks

The derivation of the HJB PDE assumes that the value function is twice continuously differentiable (\(C^2\)). In many core quantitative finance problems, this classical regularity assumption fails:

  • Non-smooth value functions and kinks: Under constraints or non-smooth payoffs (such as American option exercise boundaries, borrowing caps, or transaction cost zones), \(V(x)\) is only \(C^1\) or Lipschitz continuous, with discontinuous second derivatives \(\nabla^2 V\). The classical HJB PDE cannot be evaluated pointwise at these kinks.
  • Degenerate diffusion matrices: When \(\sigma(x, u)\sigma(x, u)^\top\) is not strictly positive definite, the HJB PDE is degenerate parabolic or first-order hyperbolic. Standard elliptic/parabolic smoothing fails, permitting shock waves and multi-valued solutions.
  • Resolution via Viscosity Solutions: To restore uniqueness and well-posedness when \(C^2\) regularity breaks, Crandall, Evans, and Lions developed the theory of viscosity solutions, which characterizes \(V\) using sub- and super-jets without requiring differentiability as analyzed in Pham (2009).

Misconceptions

  1. Wrong: The HJB partial differential equation is an elliptic PDE because of the presence of the second spatial derivative.

    For finite-horizon problems, the presence of \(\partial_t V\) makes the HJB equation a non-linear parabolic PDE running backward in time from terminal condition \(V(T, x) = g(x)\). It is stationary (elliptic) only in the infinite-horizon limit where \(\partial_t V = 0\).

  2. Wrong: Finding a smooth function \(v(t, x)\) that satisfies the HJB PDE proves that \(v\) is the value function.

    A solution to the PDE is merely a candidate value function. The HJB derivation is a necessary condition under smoothness, not a sufficient one. Proving that \(v = V\) requires a formal verification theorem checking boundary conditions and transversality.

  3. Wrong: The supremum inside the HJB equation can be moved outside the expectation or differentiated through without checking convexity.

    The supremum inside the HJB operator \(\sup_{u \in U} \{ \mathcal{A}^u V + f \}\) is evaluated pointwise at each state \(x\). If the objective is non-concave in \(u\), first-order conditions \(\nabla_u = 0\) can identify local minima or saddle points rather than the global maximizer.

Problem set

Let \(dX_t = u_t dt + dB_t\) with running reward \(f(u) = -\tfrac{1}{2} u^2\) and discount rate \(\rho > 0\). Compute the Hamiltonian \(\mathcal{H}(p, M)\) explicitly by maximizing over \(u \in \R\).

Solution

The Hamiltonian is \(\mathcal{H}(p, M) = \sup_{u \in \R} \{ p u + \tfrac{1}{2} M - \tfrac{1}{2} u^2 \}\). The objective is strictly concave in \(u\). Differentiating with respect to \(u\) gives \(p - u = 0 \implies u^* = p\). Substituting \(u^*\) into the objective yields \(\mathcal{H}(p, M) = \tfrac{1}{2} p^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} M\).

Write down the stationary HJB equation for an investor with log utility \(U(c) = \log c\), constant investment opportunities \((r, \mu, \sigma)\), discount rate \(\rho\), and wealth dynamics \(dW_t = [(r + \pi_t(\mu - r))W_t - c_t]dt + \pi_t \sigma W_t dB_t\).

Solution

The stationary HJB equation is \(\rho V(w) = \sup_{\pi, c > 0} \{ \log c + V'(w)[(r + \pi(\mu - r))w - c] + \tfrac{1}{2} V''(w) \pi^2 \sigma^2 w^2 \}\). Pointwise first-order conditions yield \(c^* = \frac{1}{V'(w)}\) and \(\pi^* = -\frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2} \frac{V'(w)}{w V''(w)}\).

For the scalar diffusion \(dX_t = -\kappa X_t dt + \sigma dB_t\) with \(\kappa, \sigma > 0\), evaluate the controlled generator \(\mathcal{A} \phi(x)\) on the candidate quadratic function \(\phi(x) = A x^2 + B\).

Solution

First and second derivatives are \(\phi'(x) = 2 A x\) and \(\phi''(x) = 2 A\). The generator is \(\mathcal{A} \phi(x) = b(x)\phi'(x) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \phi''(x) = (-\kappa x)(2 A x) + \tfrac{1}{2} \sigma^2 (2 A) = -2 \kappa A x^2 + A \sigma^2\).

Prove that the Hamiltonian \(\mathcal{H}(x, p, M)\) is convex in the momentum and curvature variables \((p, M)\).

Solution

For each fixed control \(u \in U\), the function \(g_u(p, M) \coloneqq b(x, u) \cdot p + \tfrac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr}(\sigma(x, u)\sigma(x, u)^\top M) + f(x, u)\) is an affine function of \((p, M)\). Because affine functions are convex, and the pointwise supremum of any collection of convex functions is convex, \(\mathcal{H}(x, p, M) = \sup_{u \in U} g_u(p, M)\) is a convex function of \((p, M)\).

Prove that if \(\sigma(x, u) \equiv 0\) (deterministic control), the HJB equation reduces to the classical Hamilton–Jacobi PDE of analytical mechanics.

Solution

When \(\sigma \equiv 0\), the diffusion matrix \(a(x, u) = 0\), eliminating the second-order trace term \(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\sigma \sigma^\top \nabla^2 V) = 0\). The equation becomes \(\partial_t V(t, x) - \rho V(t, x) + \sup_{u \in U} \{ b(t, x, u) \cdot \nabla V(t, x) + f(t, x, u) \} = 0\), which is a first-order non-linear PDE matching the Hamilton–Jacobi equation with Hamiltonian \(\mathcal{H}(x, p) = \sup_u \{ b \cdot p + f \}\).

Show that the Legendre transform of a strictly convex Lagrangian \(L(u) = \tfrac{1}{2} u^\top R u\) with \(R \succ 0\) recovers the quadratic Hamiltonian term \(\tfrac{1}{2} p^\top R^{-1} p\).

Solution

The Legendre transform is \(L^*(p) = \sup_{u \in \R^k} \{ p^\top u - \tfrac{1}{2} u^\top R u \}\). The first-order condition is \(p - R u = 0 \implies u^* = R^{-1} p\). Substituting this maximizer into the objective yields \(p^\top (R^{-1} p) - \tfrac{1}{2} (R^{-1} p)^\top R (R^{-1} p) = p^\top R^{-1} p - \tfrac{1}{2} p^\top R^{-1} p = \tfrac{1}{2} p^\top R^{-1} p\).

In Lab 1, the discrete Bellman operator difference \(\frac{T_{\Delta t}[V] - V}{\Delta t}\) is \(-1.245 \times 10^{-2}\) at \(\Delta t = 0.04\) and \(-3.114 \times 10^{-3}\) at \(\Delta t = 0.01\). Verify the empirical convergence rate.

Solution

The ratio of step sizes is \(\frac{0.04}{0.01} = 4.0\). The ratio of residuals is \(\frac{-1.24525 \times 10^{-2}}{-3.11376 \times 10^{-3}} = 3.999\). Taking the logarithm, \(\frac{\log(3.999)}{\log(4.0)} = \frac{1.3860}{1.3863} = 0.9998 \approx 1.0\). This confirms exact first-order \(O(\Delta t)\) convergence of the discrete dynamic programming recursion to the continuous HJB operator.

A numerical solver evaluates the HJB residual on a fine grid of 500 points for a portfolio optimization problem and finds that the residual is \(10^{-8}\) everywhere except at wealth \(w = 1.0\) (a collateral boundary), where it jumps to \(0.45\). Explain what this indicates about the regularity of \(V\).

Solution

A jump in the PDE residual at a specific boundary node indicates that the value function has a kink (discontinuous first derivative) or a jump in curvature (discontinuous second derivative) at \(w = 1.0\). At this boundary, the classical \(C^2\) HJB equation fails, and the solution must be characterized as a viscosity solution using generalized directional sub- and super-differentials.

Describe how an empirical asset pricing desk can test whether market makers are following an HJB-optimal inventory management policy by analyzing quotes around order flow imbalances.

Solution

Under an HJB-optimal inventory model (such as Avellaneda–Stoikov), the optimal bid-ask skew \(\delta^a - \delta^b\) is proportional to inventory \(q\) times risk aversion \(\gamma \sigma^2\). The desk can run a panel regression of quoted midpoint shifts against inventory and volatility to test whether the empirical slope matches the HJB Hamiltonian first-order condition.

Formulate the definition of a viscosity sub-solution and super-solution for the stationary HJB equation \(\rho V(x) - \mathcal{H}(x, \nabla V(x), \nabla^2 V(x)) = 0\).

Solution

A continuous function \(v\) is a viscosity sub-solution if for any \(x_0\) and test function \(\phi \in C^2\) such that \(v - \phi\) has a local maximum at \(x_0\) with \(v(x_0) = \phi(x_0)\), \(\rho v(x_0) - \mathcal{H}(x_0, \nabla \phi(x_0), \nabla^2 \phi(x_0)) \le 0\). Conversely, \(v\) is a viscosity super-solution if whenever \(v - \phi\) has a local minimum at \(x_0\), \(\rho v(x_0) - \mathcal{H}(x_0, \nabla \phi(x_0), \nabla^2 \phi(x_0)) \ge 0\). It is a viscosity solution if it is both a sub- and super-solution.

Explain the economic and mathematical role of the viscosity solution comparison principle in proving uniqueness of HJB solutions.

Solution

The comparison principle states that if \(u\) is a sub-solution and \(v\) is a super-solution with \(u \le v\) on the boundary, then \(u \le v\) everywhere in the interior. This directly implies uniqueness: if \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) are both viscosity solutions, then \(v_1 \le v_2\) and \(v_2 \le v_1\), so \(v_1 = v_2\). Economically, it guarantees that numerical approximations converge to the true unique economic value function.

Contrast the HJB approach to stochastic control with Pontryagin's Stochastic Maximum Principle (SMP), highlighting the trade-off between forward-backward SDEs and non-linear PDEs.

Solution

The HJB approach is a dynamic programming method yielding a single non-linear PDE for the scalar value function on the state space \(\R^d\), suffering from Bellman's curse of dimensionality for large \(d\). Pontryagin's SMP characterizes optimality via a coupled system of Forward-Backward Stochastic Differential Equations (FBSDEs) involving an adjoint costate process \(p_t\), which scales well to high-dimensional states but requires solving non-linear boundary-value SDEs.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What type of partial differential equation is the finite-horizon Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation?

    Options

    The finite-horizon HJB equation contains time derivative \(\partial_t V\) and diffusion \(\nabla^2 V\), making it a non-linear parabolic PDE running backward from terminal condition \(V(T, x) = g(x)\).

  2. In the HJB equation, what mathematical operation reduces the dynamic intertemporal problem to a static problem at each state?

    Options

    The HJB equation isolates control choice to pointwise static maximization of \(\mathcal{A}^u V + f\) over \(u \in U\).

  3. For a scalar controlled diffusion \(dX_t = b(x, u)dt + \sigma(x, u)dB_t\), what is the coefficient of \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}\) in the infinitesimal generator?

    Options

    By Itô's lemma, the second-order diffusion expansion introduces the half-variance factor \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\).

  4. What structural mathematical property does the Hamiltonian \(\mathcal{H}(x, p, M)\) always satisfy with respect to \((p, M)\)?

    Options

    Because the objective inside the supremum is affine in \((p, M)\), the supremum over \(u\) is convex.

  5. For Hamiltonian \(\mathcal{H}(p) = \sup_{u \in \R} \{ p u - \tfrac{1}{4} u^2 \}\), what is the value of the maximized term \(\mathcal{H}(p)\) when \(p = 1.0\)?

    Maximizing \(p u - \tfrac{1}{4}u^2\) gives \(p - \tfrac{1}{2}u = 0 \implies u^* = 2p = 2.0\). Then \(p u^* - \tfrac{1}{4}(u^*)^2 = 1.0(2.0) - \tfrac{1}{4}(4.0) = 2.0 - 1.0 = 1.0\).

  6. What mathematical framework resolves the failure of classical \(C^2\) solutions to HJB equations when value functions develop kinks?

    Options

    Viscosity solutions provide the correct generalized framework for non-linear first- and second-order PDEs satisfying the maximum principle.

  7. In an infinite-horizon control problem with subjective discount rate \(\rho > 0\), what happens to the time derivative term \(\partial_t V\)?

    Options

    Autonomous infinite-horizon control problems are stationary, so the value function does not depend on time \(t\).

  8. What is the rate of convergence of the discrete Bellman continuation operator to the continuous HJB operator as \(\Delta t \downarrow 0\)?

    Options

    Taylor expansion of the continuation value yields \(V(t+\Delta t, X_{t+\Delta t}) - V(t, x) = (\partial_t V + \mathcal{A}^u V)\Delta t + O(\Delta t^2)\), so dividing by \(\Delta t\) leaves an \(O(\Delta t)\) truncation error.
