The Merton Fraction & Closed-Form Allocation

The Merton fraction is one of the most celebrated formulas in quantitative economics, dictating the exact proportion of wealth an investor should commit to risky equities. Decomposing the allocation reveals three transparent drivers: the market Sharpe ratio, the investor's relative risk tolerance, and the asset's standalone return volatility. Proving that this allocation is horizon-invariant explains why myopic rebalancing rules remain mathematically optimal under constant investment opportunities, and provides the baseline against which all constrained and machine-learned portfolio models are benchmarked.

1Definition and Decomposition of the Merton Fraction

Definition 1.1 (Merton Fraction)

In a continuous-time financial market with risk-free rate \(r\), risky asset drift \(\mu\), and volatility \(\sigma\), the optimal portfolio weight in the risky asset for an investor with constant relative risk aversion \(\gamma > 0\) is the Merton fraction derived by Merton (1969, 1971):

\[ \pi^* \coloneqq \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}. \]
Remark 1.2 (Economic Decomposition)

Defining the market Sharpe ratio (or market price of risk) by \(\lambda \coloneqq \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma}\), the Merton fraction factors as:

\[ \pi^* = \left( \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma} \right) \cdot \left( \frac{1}{\sigma} \right) \cdot \left( \frac{1}{\gamma} \right) = \frac{\lambda}{\gamma \sigma}. \]

The optimal exposure is directly proportional to the risk-adjusted return (\(\lambda\)), inversely proportional to asset risk (\(\sigma\)), and inversely proportional to risk aversion (\(\gamma\)). The factor \(\tau \coloneqq 1/\gamma\) represents the investor's relative risk tolerance.

2Continuous-Time Two-Fund Separation

Now consider an \(n\)-dimensional financial market consisting of a risk-free bond \(B_t\) and \(n\) risky stocks with price vector \(S_t \in \R^n\) following coupled geometric Brownian motions:

\[ dS_t = \operatorname{diag}(S_t) \left[ \mu \, dt + \Sigma^{1/2} \, dB_t \right], \]

where \(\mu \in \R^n\) is the vector of expected returns, \(\Sigma \in \R^{n \times n}\) is the positive definite covariance matrix, and \(B_t\) is an \(n\)-dimensional standard Brownian motion.

Theorem 2.1 (Continuous-Time Two-Fund Separation)

Assumptions: (A1) \(\Sigma \succ 0\) is strictly positive definite; (A2) All investors have CRRA utility \(U_i(c) = \frac{c^{1-\gamma_i}}{1-\gamma_i}\) with possibly heterogeneous risk aversion \(\gamma_i > 0\); (A3) Market parameters \((r, \mu, \Sigma)\) are constant.

Statement: The vector of optimal risky portfolio weights \(\pi_i^* \in \R^n\) satisfies:

\[ \pi_i^* = \frac{1}{\gamma_i} \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1}). \]

Every investor allocates capital between exactly two funds: the risk-free bond, and a single mutual fund of risky assets with normalized weight vector \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{tangency}} \coloneqq \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1})}{\mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1})}\). Differences in risk aversion \(\gamma_i\) affect only the total leverage allocated to this tangency fund, not its internal asset composition.

Proof

Let \(\pi \in \R^n\) denote the vector of portfolio weights in the risky assets. The wealth SDE is \(dW_t = [(r + \pi^\top (\mu - r \mathbf{1}))W_t - c_t]dt + W_t \pi^\top \Sigma^{1/2} dB_t\). The quadratic term in the HJB Hamiltonian is:

\[ \sup_{\pi \in \R^n} \left\{ V'(w) W_t \pi^\top (\mu - r \mathbf{1}) + \frac{1}{2} V''(w) W_t^2 \pi^\top \Sigma \pi \right\}. \]

Because \(V''(w) < 0\) and \(\Sigma \succ 0\), the objective is strictly concave in \(\pi\). The vector gradient first-order condition is:

\[ V'(w) (\mu - r \mathbf{1}) + V''(w) W_t \Sigma \pi^* = 0 \implies \pi^* = -\frac{V'(w)}{w V''(w)} \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1}). \]

For CRRA utility, \(-\frac{V'(w)}{w V''(w)} = \frac{1}{\gamma_i}\). Substituting this scalar yields \(\pi_i^* = \frac{1}{\gamma_i}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1})\). Normalizing the relative stock weights confirms that the relative proportions within the risky portfolio are \(\frac{\pi_{ik}^*}{\sum_j \pi_{ij}^*} = \frac{[\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1})]_k}{\mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1})}\), which is completely independent of \(\gamma_i\).

3Horizon Invariance and Myopic Portfolio Choice

Theorem 2.2 (Horizon Invariance of the Merton Allocation)

Assumptions: (A1) The investment opportunity set \((r, \mu, \sigma)\) is constant; (A2) Utility is CRRA with \(\gamma > 0\); (A3) The investment horizon \(T\) is finite or infinite.

Statement: The optimal portfolio fraction \(\pi^*(t) = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\) is identical for all \(t \in [0, T]\), regardless of the remaining time horizon \(T - t\). An investor with a 30-year horizon chooses the exact same asset allocation as an investor with a 1-day horizon.

Proof

For a finite-horizon problem with terminal utility \(U(W_T) = \frac{W_T^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\), propose candidate value function \(V(t, w) = f(t) \frac{w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\) with terminal condition \(f(T) = 1\). Computing derivatives:

\[ V'(t, w) = f(t) w^{-\gamma}, \quad V''(t, w) = -\gamma f(t) w^{-\gamma - 1}. \]

The optimal portfolio fraction is \(\pi^*(t) = -\frac{V'(t, w)}{w V''(t, w)} \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2} = -\frac{f(t) w^{-\gamma}}{-\gamma w f(t) w^{-\gamma - 1}} \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2} = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\). The time-dependent function \(f(t)\) cancels out algebraically from the numerator and denominator, proving that the optimal portfolio allocation is strictly invariant to \(T - t\).

4Comparative Statics and Leverage Boundaries

The Merton fraction displays characteristic comparative statics across market parameters:

Parameter shift Partial derivative Direction Economic mechanism
Increase in excess return \(\mu - r\) \(\frac{\partial \pi^*}{\partial (\mu - r)} = \frac{1}{\gamma \sigma^2}\) Positive (\(> 0\)) Higher Sharpe ratio increases compensation per unit variance.
Increase in volatility \(\sigma\) \(\frac{\partial \pi^*}{\partial \sigma} = -\frac{2(\mu - r)}{\gamma \sigma^3}\) Negative (\(< 0\)) Variance penalty scales quadratically, inducing sharp deleveraging.
Increase in risk aversion \(\gamma\) \(\frac{\partial \pi^*}{\partial \gamma} = -\frac{\mu - r}{\gamma^2 \sigma^2}\) Negative (\(< 0\)) Lower risk tolerance reduces exposure to diffusive wealth shocks.

Lab 1 · Merton fraction vs. simulated expected utility

Simulating 30,000 realization paths of terminal wealth under CRRA utility (\(\gamma = 3.0, r = 0.03, \mu = 0.09, \sigma = 0.20\)) across constant allocation fractions \(\pi \in [0.0, 1.0]\) demonstrates that the sample expected utility peaks exactly at the theoretical Merton fraction \(\pi^* = 0.5000\).

r"""Lab 1 · Merton fraction vs. simulated expected utility.

Simulates CRRA utility of terminal wealth across portfolio allocation fractions pi in [0.0, 1.0],
demonstrating that the sample expected utility peaks exactly at the theoretical Merton fraction
pi* = (mu - r) / (gamma * sigma^2).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    # Model parameters (SIMULATED)
    seed = 42
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    r = 0.03
    mu = 0.09
    sigma = 0.20
    gamma = 3.0
    t_horiz = 1.0
    w0 = 1.0

    # Theoretical Merton fraction
    pi_star = (mu - r) / (gamma * (sigma**2))
    print(f"theoretical Merton fraction pi*: {pi_star:.4f}")

    n_paths = 30000
    z_draws = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n_paths)]

    pi_grid = [round(0.1 * i, 2) for i in range(11)]
    results: list[tuple[float, float, float]] = []

    for pi in pi_grid:
        drift = (r + pi * (mu - r) - 0.5 * (pi * sigma) ** 2) * t_horiz
        vol = pi * sigma * math.sqrt(t_horiz)

        sample_utilities: list[float] = []
        for z in z_draws:
            w_t = w0 * math.exp(drift + vol * z)
            u = (w_t ** (1.0 - gamma)) / (1.0 - gamma)
            sample_utilities.append(u)

        mean_u = sum(sample_utilities) / n_paths

        # Exact theoretical expected utility:
        # E[W^(1-gamma)] = exp((1-gamma)*drift + 0.5*(1-gamma)^2 * vol^2)
        exact_exp = math.exp((1.0 - gamma) * drift + 0.5 * ((1.0 - gamma) ** 2) * (vol**2))
        exact_u = exact_exp / (1.0 - gamma)

        results.append((pi, mean_u, exact_u))
        print(f"pi = {pi:4.2f}: sample E[U] = {mean_u:.6f}, exact E[U] = {exact_u:.6f}")

    best_sample_pi = max(results, key=lambda x: x[1])[0]
    best_exact_pi = max(results, key=lambda x: x[2])[0]

    print(f"sample optimal fraction: {best_sample_pi:.2f}")
    print(f"exact analytical optimal fraction: {best_exact_pi:.2f}")
    print(f"sample peak coincides with Merton fraction: {abs(best_sample_pi - pi_star) < 1e-6}")

Where the canon breaks

Horizon invariance is widely known as Merton's myopia puzzle. In practical asset management, target-date retirement funds systematically reduce equity exposure as investors age (the "glidepath"). Why does the canon break in practice?

  • Return predictability and intertemporal hedging demand: If stock returns are mean-reverting (dividend yield predicts returns), investment opportunities vary stochastically over time. Long-horizon investors hold an additional hedging demand against worsening investment opportunities, making optimal equity exposure horizon-dependent as proven in Merton (1971).
  • Parameter uncertainty and estimation risk: The true drift \(\mu\) is unobservable and estimated with massive standard errors. Longer horizons compound parameter uncertainty, inducing Bayesian updating that alters risk allocations dynamically.
  • Borrowing and leverage constraints: If \(\pi^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2} > 1\), the investor must borrow at the risk-free rate. Institutional margin limits and higher borrowing spreads (\(r_b > r\)) constrain leverage, preventing investors from attaining the unconstrained fraction.

Misconceptions

  1. Wrong: Time diversification implies that young investors with long horizons should hold a higher equity fraction because stock market risk "averages out" over time.

    Samuelson (1969) and Merton (1969, 1971) proved that for an investor with CRRA preferences and independent return increments, total return variance grows linearly with horizon \(T\), exactly canceling the time-averaging effect. The optimal Merton fraction \(\pi^*\) is mathematically independent of the time horizon under random walk dynamics.

  2. Wrong: The Merton fraction dictates holding a fixed dollar amount in the stock.

    The Merton fraction \(\pi^*\) is a constant proportion of wealth, not a constant dollar investment. If wealth doubles from \$1M to \$2M, dollar equity exposure doubles from \(\$500\text{k}\) to \(\$1\text{M}\) to maintain the fixed 50% fraction.

  3. Wrong: The Merton fraction can be computed by maximizing expected portfolio return minus variance \(\mu_p - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\sigma_p^2\) in discrete time.

    Single-period mean-variance utility is only a local quadratic approximation. The Merton fraction arises from continuous geometric Brownian motion where Itô's second-order correction \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_p^2 dt\) explicitly accounts for compounding variance drag.

Problem set

Calculate the Merton fraction \(\pi^*\) for an endowment with risk aversion \(\gamma = 4.0\), risk-free rate \(r = 0.025\), stock drift \(\mu = 0.085\), and stock volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\).

Solution

The excess return is \(\mu - r = 0.085 - 0.025 = 0.060\). The variance is \(\sigma^2 = 0.20^2 = 0.040\). The Merton fraction is \(\pi^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2} = \frac{0.060}{4.0 \times 0.040} = \frac{0.060}{0.160} = 0.375\) (or 37.5% in the risky asset, with 62.5% in cash).

If volatility increases from \(\sigma = 0.20\) to \(\sigma = 0.30\) while the Sharpe ratio \(\lambda = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma} = 0.30\) remains constant, what is the new Merton fraction for \(\gamma = 3.0\)?

Solution

Using the decomposition \(\pi^* = \frac{\lambda}{\gamma \sigma}\): with \(\lambda = 0.30\), \(\gamma = 3.0\), and \(\sigma = 0.30\), \(\pi^* = \frac{0.30}{3.0 \times 0.30} = \frac{0.30}{0.90} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.3333\). (Previously at \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(\pi^* = \frac{0.30}{3.0 \times 0.20} = 0.50\); hence rising volatility cuts allocation by one-third even with constant Sharpe ratio).

An investor has risk aversion \(\gamma = 1.5\), \(r = 0.03\), \(\mu = 0.09\), and \(\sigma = 0.20\). Calculate the Merton fraction. Does this strategy require leverage?

Solution

\(\pi^* = \frac{0.09 - 0.03}{1.5 \times 0.20^2} = \frac{0.06}{1.5 \times 0.04} = \frac{0.06}{0.06} = 1.00\). The optimal fraction is exactly 100% in stock and 0% in cash. It is fully invested without requiring leverage or borrowing.

Prove that for an investor holding a constant fraction \(\pi\) in stock, the expected compounded growth rate of wealth \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \log(W_T / W_0)\) is maximized at \(\pi = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\), which corresponds to the Merton fraction for log utility (\(\gamma = 1\)).

Solution

Wealth follows \(d\log W_t = [r + \pi(\mu - r) - \tfrac{1}{2}\pi^2 \sigma^2] dt + \pi \sigma dB_t\). By the strong law of large numbers for Brownian motion, \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \log(W_T / W_0) = r + \pi(\mu - r) - \tfrac{1}{2}\pi^2 \sigma^2\) almost surely. Differentiating with respect to \(\pi\) yields \(\mu - r - \pi \sigma^2 = 0 \implies \pi = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\). Setting \(\gamma = 1\) in the Merton fraction formula gives \(\pi^* = \frac{\mu - r}{1 \times \sigma^2} = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\), proving that log utility maximizes the asymptotic growth rate (the Kelly criterion).

Prove that if \(\gamma > 1\), the Merton fraction is strictly less than the Kelly growth-maximizing fraction: \(\pi_{\text{CRRA}}^* < \pi_{\text{Kelly}}^*\).

Solution

The Kelly growth-maximizing fraction is \(\pi_{\text{Kelly}}^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\). For an investor with CRRA risk aversion \(\gamma\), the Merton fraction is \(\pi_{\text{CRRA}}^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\gamma} \pi_{\text{Kelly}}^*\). For any \(\gamma > 1\), \(\frac{1}{\gamma} < 1\), so \(\pi_{\text{CRRA}}^* = \frac{1}{\gamma} \pi_{\text{Kelly}}^* < \pi_{\text{Kelly}}^*\), establishing that risk-averse investors hold a strictly smaller equity fraction than the pure growth-maximizer.

Show that the Merton fraction satisfies the continuous-time capital asset pricing model (CAPM) beta relationship when aggregated across a continuum of CRRA investors.

Solution

Let investor \(i\) have wealth \(W_i\) and risk aversion \(\gamma_i\). By two-fund separation, each investor holds risky assets in proportion \(\pi_i^* = \frac{1}{\gamma_i} \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1})\). Total aggregate dollar investment in risky assets is \(W_M = \sum_i \pi_i^* W_i = (\sum_i \frac{W_i}{\gamma_i}) \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1})\). Let \(\bar{\gamma} \coloneqq (\sum_i \frac{W_i}{\gamma_i} / \sum_i W_i)^{-1}\) be the wealth-weighted harmonic mean risk aversion. Then the market portfolio satisfies \(\mathbf{w}_M = \frac{1}{\bar{\gamma}} \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r \mathbf{1}) \implies \mu - r \mathbf{1} = \bar{\gamma} \Sigma \mathbf{w}_M\). The covariance with the market is \(\operatorname{Cov}(R, R_M) = \Sigma \mathbf{w}_M\), so \(\mu_k - r = \beta_k (\mu_M - r)\) with \(\beta_k = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_k, R_M)}{\Var(R_M)}\).

Using historical US equity parameters (\(r = 0.03, \mu = 0.09, \sigma = 0.18\)), compute the Merton fraction for risk aversions \(\gamma \in \{2, 5, 10\}\). Compare the results to observed household equity allocations.

Solution

The excess return is \(0.06\) and variance is \(0.18^2 = 0.0324\). For \(\gamma = 2\): \(\pi^* = \frac{0.06}{2 \times 0.0324} = 0.926\) (92.6%). For \(\gamma = 5\): \(\pi^* = \frac{0.06}{5 \times 0.0324} = 0.370\) (37.0%). For \(\gamma = 10\): \(\pi^* = \frac{0.06}{10 \times 0.0324} = 0.185\) (18.5%). Survey data indicates median US household equity allocation is roughly 40-50% among stock-owning households, consistent with effective risk aversion \(\gamma \approx 4\) to \(5\).

In Lab 1, the sample expected utility under allocation \(\pi = 1.0\) is \(-0.470599\), which is lower than the expected utility \(-0.456836\) achieved at \(\pi^* = 0.50\). Calculate the equivalent certainty wealth drop caused by holding 100% stock instead of the optimal 50% fraction.

Solution

For CRRA utility \(U(w) = \frac{w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\) with \(\gamma = 3\), \(U(w) = -\frac{1}{2 w^2} \implies w = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-2 U}}\). At the optimal fraction \(\pi^* = 0.50\), certainty equivalent wealth is \(w_{\text{CE}}^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \times 0.456836}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.913672}} = 1.0461\). At \(\pi = 1.00\), \(w_{\text{CE}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \times 0.470599}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.941198}} = 1.0307\). The relative welfare loss is \(\frac{1.0461 - 1.0307}{1.0461} = 0.0147\) (a 1.47% permanent certainty-equivalent wealth penalty from excessive equity exposure).

Explain how volatility drag affects leveraged exchange-traded funds (such as 3x leveraged S&P 500 ETFs) through the lens of Merton's quadratic variance penalty \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi^2 \sigma^2\).

Solution

For a 3x leveraged fund (\(\pi = 3\)), the compounding drift of log wealth contains the variance penalty \(-\tfrac{1}{2}(3)^2 \sigma^2 = -4.5 \sigma^2\). For typical index volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), this variance drag is \(4.5 \times 0.04 = 0.18\) (18% per year!). Unless the underlying equity drift exceeds \(r + 4.5 \sigma^2 / 3\), the leveraged ETF will lose value over long horizons even if the benchmark index appreciates.

Derive Merton's intertemporal hedging demand formula when the expected return follows an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \(d\mu_t = \kappa(\bar{\mu} - \mu_t)dt + \sigma_\mu dB_t^{(2)}\) with correlation \(\operatorname{Corr}(dB_t^{(1)}, dB_t^{(2)}) = \rho_{\mu}\).

Solution

When investment opportunities are stochastic, \(V = V(t, w, \mu)\). The HJB first-order condition with respect to \(\pi\) gives \(V_w (\mu - r)w + V_{ww}\pi \sigma^2 w^2 + V_{w\mu} \rho_\mu \sigma \sigma_\mu w = 0\). Solving for \(\pi^*\) yields \(\pi^*(t, w, \mu) = -\frac{V_w}{w V_{ww}}\frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2} - \frac{V_{w\mu}}{w V_{ww}}\frac{\rho_\mu \sigma_\mu}{\sigma}\). The first term is the myopic Merton fraction. The second term is Merton's hedging demand, which hedges the investor against adverse shifts in future investment opportunities.

Analyze why the presence of unhedgeable labor income creates an effective declining glidepath in life-cycle finance, reconciling empirical practice with Merton's theory.

Solution

Bodie, Merton, and Samuelson (1992) model total wealth as the sum of financial wealth \(W_t\) and human capital \(H_t\) (the present value of future labor income). If labor income is riskless (bond-like), total wealth is \(W_t + H_t\). The Merton fraction applies to total wealth: \(\text{Stock}_t^* = \pi^* (W_t + H_t)\). The equity fraction of financial wealth is \(\frac{\text{Stock}_t^*}{W_t} = \pi^* (1 + \frac{H_t}{W_t})\). For young workers, \(H_t / W_t \gg 1\), justifying a high financial equity fraction (or leverage). As the worker ages, \(H_t \to 0\), causing the financial equity fraction to decline toward \(\pi^*\), generating the target-date glidepath.

Examine how parameter uncertainty in expected return \(\mu\) alters optimal portfolio choice under continuous-time Bayesian filtering.

Solution

When \(\mu\) is an unobservable random variable with prior \(\mu \sim \Normal(\hat{\mu}_0, \sigma_0^2)\), filtering theory shows the posterior estimate satisfies \(d\hat{\mu}_t = \frac{\sigma_t^2}{\sigma} d\tilde{B}_t\). The state space expands to \((W_t, \hat{\mu}_t)\). Because stock return surprises revise beliefs about \(\mu\), \(\operatorname{Corr}(d\tilde{B}_t, d\hat{\mu}_t) = 1 > 0\). For \(\gamma > 1\), the hedging demand against belief revisions is negative, causing the investor to hold a strictly lower equity allocation than the plug-in Merton fraction \(\frac{\hat{\mu}_t - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\).

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the formula for the unconstrained Merton fraction \(\pi^*\) in a Black–Scholes market?

    Options

    By Merton (1969, 1971), the optimal allocation is the excess return divided by risk aversion times return variance.

  2. What does the two-fund separation theorem state for continuous-time portfolio selection?

    Options

    Two-fund separation proves that the relative composition of the risky tangency portfolio is independent of risk aversion \(\gamma\).

  3. Why is the optimal portfolio fraction horizon-invariant for CRRA utility under constant market parameters?

    Options

    For homothetic power utility, the continuation factor \(f(t)\) scales both \(V'\) and \(V''\) proportionally, canceling out of the optimal policy.

  4. For market parameters \(r = 0.03\), \(\mu = 0.09\), \(\sigma = 0.20\), and risk aversion \(\gamma = 3.0\), what is the optimal Merton fraction \(\pi^*\)?

    \(\pi^* = \frac{0.09 - 0.03}{3.0 \times 0.20^2} = \frac{0.06}{0.12} = 0.50\).

  5. What risk aversion \(\gamma\) corresponds to the Kelly growth-optimal portfolio fraction \(\pi^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\)?

    Options

    Logarithmic utility (\(\gamma = 1\)) maximizes the asymptotic expected growth rate of log wealth, matching Kelly's criterion.

  6. How does an increase in asset volatility \(\sigma\) affect the optimal Merton fraction \(\pi^*\) when the excess return \(\mu - r\) is held constant?

    Options

    The Merton formula divides by \(\sigma^2\), so exposure scales inversely with return variance.

  7. What economic mechanism introduces an intertemporal hedging demand that breaks horizon invariance in continuous time?

    Options

    When investment opportunities vary over time, long-horizon investors hedge against future shifts in investment returns.

  8. In Lab 1, why does simulated sample expected utility decline when allocation increases beyond \(\pi^* = 0.50\)?

    Options

    Beyond \(\pi^*\), the quadratic variance penalty dominates the linear excess return, reducing expected utility.
