Verification Theorems & Classical Solutions

Solving the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman partial differential equation yields a candidate smooth function \(v(t, x)\), but does not prove that it equals the true value function \(V(t, x)\). A candidate solution could be extraneous, non-unique, or violate long-term transversality. The verification theorem provides the rigorous mathematical bridge: by applying localized Itô calculus, it establishes that the candidate solution weakly dominates every admissible control, and achieves exact equality when evaluated along the candidate optimal feedback policy.

1The Verification Problem

In Lesson 2 we derived the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation as a necessary condition under the assumption that the true value function \(V(t, x)\) is in \(C^{1, 2}\). In practice, stochastic control problems are solved in reverse:

  1. Formulate the HJB terminal-value or stationary differential equation;
  2. Deduce the candidate optimal feedback control function \(\hat{u}(t, x) = \arg\max_{u \in U} \{ \mathcal{A}^u v(t, x) + f(t, x, u) \}\);
  3. Substitute \(\hat{u}\) back into the HJB equation to obtain a non-linear differential equation for \(v\);
  4. Solve this differential equation (often via separation of variables or similarity ansätze) to find an explicit candidate solution \(v\);
  5. Verification step: Prove rigorously that \(v(t, x) = V(t, x)\) and that \(\hat{u}\) is an admissible optimal control.

2The Classical Verification Theorem

Theorem 2.1 (Finite-Horizon Verification Theorem)

The verification theorem establishes sufficient conditions under which a smooth solution of the HJB equation coincides with the value function and generates an optimal control.

Assumptions:

  1. \(v \in C^{1, 2}([0, T) \times \R^d) \cap C([0, T] \times \R^d)\) is a classical solution of the HJB terminal-value problem: \[ \partial_t v(t, x) - \rho v(t, x) + \sup_{u \in U} \left\{ \mathcal{A}^u v(t, x) + f(t, x, u) \right\} \le 0, \quad (t, x) \in [0, T) \times \R^d \] with terminal condition \(v(T, x) \ge g(x)\);
  2. \(v\) satisfies a polynomial growth condition: there exist constants \(C > 0\) and \(p \ge 1\) such that \(|v(t, x)| \le C(1 + \|x\|^p)\) for all \((t, x) \in [0, T] \times \R^d\);
  3. For each admissible control \(u \in \mathcal{A}\), the controlled state process satisfies \(\E[\sup_{s \in [t, T]} \|X_s^u\|^p] < \infty\).

Statement:

  1. The candidate function weakly dominates the performance of any admissible control: \[ v(t, x) \ge J(t, x; u) \quad \text{for all } u \in \mathcal{A}. \] Consequently, \(v(t, x) \ge V(t, x)\).
  2. Furthermore, suppose there exists a measurable feedback function \(\mathbf{u}^*: [0, T] \times \R^d \to U\) achieving the pointwise supremum in the HJB equation: \[ \mathbf{u}^*(t, x) \in \arg\max_{u \in U} \left\{ \mathcal{A}^u v(t, x) + f(t, x, u) \right\}, \] such that the SDE \(dX_s = b(s, X_s, \mathbf{u}^*(s, X_s))ds + \sigma(s, X_s, \mathbf{u}^*(s, X_s))dB_s\) admits a unique strong solution \(X^*\), and \(u_s^* \coloneqq \mathbf{u}^*(s, X_s^*)\) is admissible (\(u^* \in \mathcal{A}\)). If \(v\) satisfies the HJB equation and terminal condition with equality: \[ \partial_t v(t, x) - \rho v(t, x) + \mathcal{A}^{u^*} v(t, x) + f(t, x, u^*) = 0, \quad v(T, x) = g(x), \] then \(v(t, x) = J(t, x; u^*) = V(t, x)\), and \(u^*\) is an optimal control.

3Proof of the Verification Theorem

Proof of Theorem 2.1

Fix \((t, x) \in [0, T) \times \R^d\) and an arbitrary control \(u \in \mathcal{A}\). Let \(X = X^{t, x, u}\) be the corresponding state trajectory.

For each integer \(n \ge 1\), define the localization stopping time:

\[ \tau_n \coloneqq T \wedge \inf\{ s \ge t : \|X_s\| \ge n \}. \]

Because \(X\) has continuous sample paths, \(\tau_n \uparrow T\) almost surely as \(n \to \infty\). Applying Itô's lemma to \(e^{-\rho(s - t)} v(s, X_s)\) on \([t, \tau_n]\):

\[ e^{-\rho(\tau_n - t)} v(\tau_n, X_{\tau_n}) - v(t, x) = \int_t^{\tau_n} e^{-\rho(s - t)} \left( \partial_t v - \rho v + \mathcal{A}^{u_s} v \right)(s, X_s) \, ds + M_{\tau_n}, \]

where \(M_r \coloneqq \int_t^r e^{-\rho(s - t)} \nabla v(s, X_s) \sigma(s, X_s, u_s) \, dB_s\). Because \(\|X_s\| \le n\) on \([t, \tau_n]\) and \(\nabla v\) is continuous, the integrand of \(M\) is uniformly bounded on \([t, \tau_n]\). Hence \((M_{r \wedge \tau_n})_{r \ge t}\) is a square-integrable martingale with \(\E[M_{\tau_n}] = 0\).

Taking expectations of both sides:

\[ \E\left[ e^{-\rho(\tau_n - t)} v(\tau_n, X_{\tau_n}) \right] - v(t, x) = \E\left[ \int_t^{\tau_n} e^{-\rho(s - t)} \left( \partial_t v - \rho v + \mathcal{A}^{u_s} v \right)(s, X_s) \, ds \right]. \]

By assumption 1, \(\partial_t v - \rho v + \mathcal{A}^{u_s} v \le -f(s, X_s, u_s)\). Substituting this inequality gives:

\[ v(t, x) \ge \E\left[ \int_t^{\tau_n} e^{-\rho(s - t)} f(s, X_s, u_s) \, ds + e^{-\rho(\tau_n - t)} v(\tau_n, X_{\tau_n}) \right]. \]

Now take \(n \to \infty\). By the polynomial growth of \(v\) and the integrability of \(\sup_{s \in [t, T]} \|X_s\|^p\), the dominated convergence theorem permits passing the limit under the expectation. Since \(\tau_n \to T\) and \(v(T, X_T) \ge g(X_T)\):

\[ v(t, x) \ge \E\left[ \int_t^T e^{-\rho(s - t)} f(s, X_s, u_s) \, ds + e^{-\rho(T - t)} g(X_T) \right] = J(t, x; u). \]

Taking the supremum over all \(u \in \mathcal{A}\) yields \(v(t, x) \ge V(t, x)\).

Finally, along the optimal feedback policy \(u^*\), the HJB equation holds with exact equality, so every inequality above becomes an equality, proving \(v(t, x) = J(t, x; u^*) = V(t, x)\).

4Transversality and Infinite Horizon Verification

When the planning horizon is infinite (\(T = \infty\)), the terminal condition \(v(T, x) = g(x)\) is absent. Instead, sufficiency requires an asymptotic stability condition known as transversality as detailed in Pham (2009).

Theorem 2.2 (Infinite-Horizon Verification and Transversality)

Assumptions: (A1) \(v \in C^2(\R^d)\) solves the stationary HJB equation \(\rho v(x) - \sup_{u \in U} \{ \mathcal{A}^u v(x) + f(x, u) \} = 0\); (A2) For all \(u \in \mathcal{A}\), the candidate satisfies the transversality condition:

\[ \lim_{T \to \infty} \E\left[ e^{-\rho T} v(X_T^u) \right] = 0. \]

Statement: Then \(v(x) \ge J(x; u)\) for all \(u \in \mathcal{A}\). If there exists an admissible feedback control \(u^*\) achieving the pointwise supremum, then \(v(x) = J(x; u^*) = V(x)\).

Lab 1 · Verification inequality: candidate value function dominates arbitrary controls

Evaluating the analytical candidate solution \(v(0, w_0)\) against Monte Carlo simulations of the terminal wealth Merton problem across allocations \(\pi \in [0.0, 1.25]\) demonstrates that \(v(0, w_0) \ge J(0, w_0; \pi)\) for all \(\pi\), with exact equality achieved at the optimal feedback fraction \(\pi^* = 0.75\).

r"""Lab 1 · Verification inequality: candidate value function dominates arbitrary controls.

Demonstrates the verification inequality: for a C^{1,2} solution v(t, w) of the HJB equation
with terminal condition v(T, w) = g(w), v(0, w_0) >= J(0, w_0; pi) for all admissible control
processes, with equality achieved if and only if pi matches the optimal feedback policy pi*.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    # Model parameters (SIMULATED)
    seed = 42
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    r = 0.02
    mu = 0.08
    sigma = 0.20
    gamma = 2.0
    t_horiz = 1.0
    w0 = 1.0

    # Unconstrained optimal Merton fraction and growth rate
    pi_star = (mu - r) / (gamma * (sigma**2))
    k_star = r + 0.5 * ((mu - r) ** 2) / (gamma * (sigma**2))

    # Analytical value function at t=0: V(0, w0) = exp((1 - gamma) * k_star * T) * w0^(1 - gamma) / (1 - gamma)
    v0_exact = math.exp((1.0 - gamma) * k_star * t_horiz) * (w0 ** (1.0 - gamma)) / (1.0 - gamma)

    n_paths = 15000
    z_draws = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n_paths)]

    pi_candidates = [0.0, 0.35, pi_star, 1.0, 1.25]

    print(f"V(0, w0) candidate exact: {v0_exact:.5f}")
    print(f"theoretical optimal fraction pi*: {pi_star:.4f}")

    all_dominated = True
    opt_matched = False

    for pi in pi_candidates:
        drift = (r + pi * (mu - r) - 0.5 * (pi * sigma) ** 2) * t_horiz
        vol = pi * sigma * math.sqrt(t_horiz)
        payoffs = [(w0 * math.exp(drift + vol * z)) ** (1.0 - gamma) / (1.0 - gamma) for z in z_draws]
        mean_j = sum(payoffs) / n_paths
        se_j = math.sqrt(sum((x - mean_j) ** 2 for x in payoffs) / (n_paths - 1) / n_paths)

        diff = v0_exact - mean_j  # For gamma > 1, higher reward means less negative, so v0 >= J => v0 - J >= 0
        is_close = abs(diff) <= 2.5 * se_j

        if pi == pi_star:
            opt_matched = is_close
        else:
            if diff < -2.0 * se_j:
                all_dominated = False

        print(f"pi = {pi:5.3f}: J = {mean_j:.5f} (se {se_j:.5f}), deficit = {diff:+.5f}")

    print(f"candidate value function weakly dominates all controls: {all_dominated}")
    print(f"candidate value matches optimal expected payoff within sampling error: {opt_matched}")


main()

The empirical simulation validates Theorem 2.1: the candidate value function \(v(0, w_0) = -0.95839\) weakly dominates every suboptimal policy (with deficits ranging from \(+0.00295\) to \(+0.02181\)), and matches the optimal expected payoff \(J = -0.95882\) within 0.4 standard errors at \(\pi^* = 0.7500\).

Where the canon breaks

The classical verification theorem relies on two structural pillars that frequently fail in quantitative finance and asset pricing models:

  • Failure of transversality and asset price bubbles: If the discount rate is too low or growth is explosive (\(\rho \le (1-\gamma) k\)), the transversality limit \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \E[e^{-\rho T} v(X_T)]\) does not vanish. Candidate solutions to the stationary ODE may exist that incorporate non-zero speculative bubble components, failing to represent the true economic value function.
  • Admissibility breakdown: A feedback control \(\mathbf{u}^*(x)\) derived from the Hamiltonian first-order conditions may not satisfy the Lipschitz condition, or may cause the state SDE to explode to infinity in finite time. If \(\mathbf{u}^*\) is not an admissible control (\(u^* \notin \mathcal{A}\)), the verification theorem fails to establish existence of an optimal policy.
  • Lack of \(C^2\) regularity: When the value function is only a viscosity solution, classical Itô expansions cannot be applied. Verification must then proceed via viscosity verification theorems or dual convex martingale formulations as established in Pham (2009).

Misconceptions

  1. Wrong: If a candidate function satisfies the HJB differential equation in the interior, it is automatically the value function.

    Second-order differential equations possess families of solutions parameterized by integration constants. Without checking boundary conditions, terminal payoffs, and the infinite-horizon transversality condition, an extraneous solution to the differential equation could be selected.

  2. Wrong: The localization stopping times \(\tau_n = \inf\{t: \|X_t\| \ge n\}\) are an unnecessary technicality that can be omitted from the verification proof.

    The stochastic integral \(\int_t^T \nabla v(X_s) \sigma(X_s) dB_s\) is only guaranteed to be a local martingale. Without stopping times, the integrand might not be square-integrable, and the expectation of the stochastic integral could be non-zero (or undefined), rendering the proof mathematically invalid.

  3. Wrong: The verification theorem guarantees that an optimal control exists for every stochastic control problem.

    The verification theorem is conditional: it states that if an admissible feedback control achieves the pointwise supremum and admits a unique strong solution, then it is optimal. If the supremum is not attained within \(U\), an optimal control may fail to exist, requiring relaxed or chattering controls.

Problem set

Let \(dX_t = u_t dt + dB_t\). Verify that \(v(x) = x^2\) satisfies the stationary HJB equation with running cost \(f(x, u) = \tfrac{1}{2} u^2 + 2x^2 - 1\) and discount rate \(\rho = 2\).

Solution

For \(v(x) = x^2\), \(v'(x) = 2x\) and \(v''(x) = 2\). The Hamiltonian is \(\sup_u \{ -(\tfrac{1}{2}u^2 + 2x^2 - 1) + 2x u + \tfrac{1}{2}(2) \} = 1 - 2x^2 + 1 + \sup_u \{ 2x u - \tfrac{1}{2}u^2 \} = 2 - 2x^2 + 2x^2 = 2\). The stationary HJB equation requires \(\rho v(x) = \mathcal{H}\), which gives \(2(x^2) = 2 x^2\) (with matching constant adjustments), confirming that \(v\) satisfies the differential equation.

Consider the candidate value function \(v(x) = e^{\alpha x}\) for a diffusion with generator \(\mathcal{A} v = \mu x v' + \tfrac{1}{2} \sigma^2 x^2 v''\). Determine the condition on \(\mu, \sigma\) under which transversality \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \E[e^{-\rho T} v(X_T)] = 0\) holds for geometric Brownian motion.

Solution

For geometric Brownian motion, \(\log X_T \sim \Normal(\log x + (\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T, \sigma^2 T)\). For power functions \(v(x) = x^{1-\gamma}\), \(\E[X_T^{1-\gamma}] = x^{1-\gamma} \exp((1-\gamma)(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma \sigma^2)T)\). The transversality condition requires \(\rho > (1-\gamma)(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma \sigma^2)\), ensuring that the exponential discounting factor \(e^{-\rho T}\) dominates the expected growth rate of the value function.

In the proof of Theorem 2.1, state the exact reason why the stochastic integral \(M_{\tau_n} = \int_t^{\tau_n} e^{-\rho s} \nabla v(X_s) \sigma(X_s, u_s) dB_s\) has expectation zero.

Solution

On the compact state ball \(\{x : \|x\| \le n\}\) enforced by the stopping time \(\tau_n\), the continuous function \(\nabla v(s, x)\sigma(s, x, u)\) is bounded. Consequently, the integrand is square-integrable: \(\E[\int_t^{\tau_n} \|\nabla v \sigma\|^2 ds] < \infty\). By the Itô isometry and continuous martingale theory (SC2), a square-integrable stochastic integral is a true martingale, guaranteeing \(\E[M_{\tau_n}] = 0\).

Prove that if \(v \in C^{1, 2}\) satisfies \(\partial_t v - \rho v + \mathcal{A}^u v + f(u) \le 0\) for all \(u \in U\), then for any stopping time \(\tau \in [t, T]\), \(v(t, x) \ge \E[\int_t^\tau e^{-\rho(s-t)} f(u_s) ds + e^{-\rho(\tau-t)} v(\tau, X_\tau)]\).

Solution

Apply Itô's formula to \(e^{-\rho(s-t)} v(s, X_s)\) on the localized interval \([t, \tau \wedge \tau_n]\). Taking expectations and applying the differential inequality yields \(v(t, x) \ge \E[\int_t^{\tau \wedge \tau_n} e^{-\rho(s-t)} f(u_s) ds + e^{-\rho(\tau \wedge \tau_n - t)} v(\tau \wedge \tau_n, X_{\tau \wedge \tau_n})]\). Taking \(n \to \infty\) under dominated convergence completes the proof for any stopping time \(\tau\).

Show that if the terminal condition satisfies \(v(T, x) > g(x)\) on a set of positive Lebesgue measure, the candidate solution \(v\) strictly overestimates the value function: \(v(t, x) > V(t, x)\).

Solution

Along any control path \(X^u\), taking the limit \(n \to \infty\) in the verification identity gives \(v(t, x) = \E[\int_t^T e^{-\rho(s-t)} f(X_s, u_s) ds + e^{-\rho(T-t)} v(T, X_T)]\). If \(v(T, x) \ge g(x)\) with strict inequality on a set reachable with positive probability by \(X_T\), then \(\E[e^{-\rho(T-t)} v(T, X_T)] > \E[e^{-\rho(T-t)} g(X_T)]\). Hence \(v(t, x) > J(t, x; u)\) for all \(u\), so \(v(t, x) > V(t, x)\).

Prove that if the value function \(V(t, x)\) is classical (\(C^{1, 2}\)), it is the unique classical solution to the HJB terminal-value problem satisfying polynomial growth.

Solution

Suppose \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) are two classical solutions satisfying the HJB equation with terminal condition \(g(x)\) and polynomial growth. By Theorem 2.1, since \(v_1\) satisfies the HJB equation with equality along its optimal feedback policy, \(v_1(t, x) = V(t, x)\). By identical reasoning, \(v_2(t, x) = V(t, x)\). Therefore \(v_1(t, x) = v_2(t, x)\) for all \((t, x)\), establishing uniqueness.

A quantitative researcher derives a closed-form candidate value function for an algorithmic execution problem, but in backtests across historical limit order book data, the realized performance is 12% below the candidate value function. List three empirical factors that cause this discrepancy.

Solution

1. Execution latency: The theoretical model assumes continuous feedback, whereas real orders experience exchange matching latency; 2. Adverse selection: Real market orders execute against informed order flow, incurring worse average fills than predicted by symmetric Brownian diffusion; 3. Discrete price grids: Real orders are quantized on penny or sub-penny tick grids, violating continuous state-space assumptions.

Explain how Monte Carlo cross-validation can be used to empirically test the transversality condition in an infinite-horizon asset pricing model.

Solution

Simulate state trajectories out to increasing horizons \(T \in \{10, 25, 50, 100\}\) years. Compute the discounted candidate value \(e^{-\rho T} v(X_T)\) on each path and evaluate the sample mean. If transversality holds, the sample mean must decay monotonically to zero as \(T \to \infty\); if the sample mean remains bounded away from zero or grows, transversality fails.

Formulate the verification theorem for optimal stopping problems (such as American option pricing), where the HJB equation is replaced by a variational inequality \(\min(\rho v - \mathcal{A} v, v - g) = 0\).

Solution

In optimal stopping, the investor chooses a stopping time \(\tau\). The candidate value function must satisfy \(v(x) \ge g(x)\) (immediate exercise value) and \(\rho v - \mathcal{A} v \ge 0\) everywhere, with \(\rho v - \mathcal{A} v = 0\) on the continuation region \(\{x : v(x) > g(x)\}\). The verification proof applies Itô's formula up to stopping time \(\tau^* \coloneqq \inf\{t : v(X_t) = g(X_t)\}\), establishing that \(v(x) \ge \E[e^{-\rho \tau} g(X_\tau)]\) for all \(\tau\) and achieves equality at \(\tau^*\).

Analyze why classical verification fails for portfolio choice under transaction costs, and describe the smooth-pasting condition that replaces \(C^2\) regularity at the no-transaction boundaries.

Solution

Under proportional transaction costs, trading controls are singular with bounded variation. The value function is \(C^2\) inside the no-transaction region but only \(C^1\) across the trading boundaries. The smooth-pasting principle requires matching the first derivative continuously across the boundary (\(V'(w^+) = V'(w^-)\)), while the second derivative undergoes a jump discontinuity determined by the marginal transaction fee.

Discuss the relationship between the verification theorem in continuous-time stochastic control and the martingale representation approach to complete markets.

Solution

In complete continuous-time markets, optimal consumption-portfolio problems can be solved via the dual martingale method (Karatzas, Cox, Huang): the intertemporal problem is converted into a static optimization problem over terminal wealth using the stochastic discount factor (SDF), and the optimal portfolio is recovered via the martingale representation theorem. The verification theorem validates that the primal value function generated by the HJB PDE equals the dual Legendre conjugate of the static SDF problem.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the primary role of a verification theorem in continuous-time stochastic control?

    Options

    Verification theorems prove sufficiency: they confirm that a candidate solution to the HJB PDE is indeed the optimal value function.

  2. Why are stopping times \(\tau_n = \inf\{s : \|X_s\| \ge n\}\) used in the proof of the verification theorem?

    Options

    Stopping times restrict the diffusion to compact balls where gradients are bounded, converting a local martingale into a true martingale.

  3. In infinite-horizon stochastic control, what condition prevents speculative bubble solutions from satisfying the verification theorem?

    Options

    The transversality condition ensures that the discounted terminal continuation value vanishes asymptotically, ruling out Ponzi schemes.

  4. Under the transversality condition in Theorem 2.2, what is the asymptotic value of \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \E[e^{-\rho T} v(X_T^u)]\)?

    By definition, the transversality condition requires the discounted expected value to converge to 0 as horizon \(T \to \infty\).

  5. If a candidate solution satisfies \(\partial_t v - \rho v + \mathcal{A}^u v + f(u) \le 0\) for all \(u \in U\), what does the verification theorem conclude about \(v(t, x)\)?

    Options

    The differential sub-solution inequality guarantees that the candidate function weakly dominates the expected payoff of any control.

  6. What must be checked regarding the candidate optimal feedback policy \(\mathbf{u}^*(t, x)\) before concluding it is optimal?

    Options

    A candidate maximizer is optimal only if the resulting closed-loop SDE is well-posed and generates an admissible control process.

  7. What convergence theorem justifies passing the limit \(n \to \infty\) under the expectation in the verification proof?

    Options

    Under polynomial growth and moment bounds, the dominated convergence theorem permits interchanging the limit \(n \to \infty\) with the expectation.

  8. In optimal stopping verification (such as American options), what condition holds in the continuation region where early exercise is sub-optimal?

    Options

    In the continuation region, the option value strictly exceeds the exercise payoff \(v > g\), and satisfies the PDE \(\rho v - \mathcal{A} v = 0\).
