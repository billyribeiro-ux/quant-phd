Finite Difference Discretization & Monotonicity

  • Prerequisites: HJB equation, Merton portfolio problem, finite difference derivative, Taylor's theorem
  • Introduces: upwind differencing, M-matrix property, discrete maximum principle, Barles–Souganidis convergence
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study

Analytical closed-form solutions to the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation exist only for special cases like unconstrained power utility. Realistic market frictions—such as borrowing constraints, collateral haircuts, and non-linear funding spreads—require numerical PDE solvers. Standard central difference schemes fail because strong convection generates spurious oscillations and negative probabilities. Designing monotone upwind stencils guarantees the M-matrix property, satisfying the Barles–Souganidis conditions that ensure stable numerical convergence to the true viscosity solution.

1Spatial Grid and Derivative Stencils

Consider the stationary 1D HJB equation on bounded domain \(\Omega = (w_{\min}, w_{\max})\) with \(0 < w_{\min} < w_{\max} < \infty\):

\[ \rho V(w) - \sup_{u \in U} \left\{ b(w, u) V'(w) + \frac{1}{2} \sigma(w, u)^2 V''(w) + f(w, u) \right\} = 0. \]

Discretize \(\Omega\) into a uniform grid with \(N\) nodes:

\[ w_i = w_{\min} + i \Delta w, \quad i = 0, 1, \dots, N-1, \quad \Delta w = \frac{w_{\max} - w_{\min}}{N-1}. \]

Let \(V_i \approx V(w_i)\) denote the discrete numerical value function vector \(\mathbf{V} \in \R^N\).

2Monotonicity and the Upwind Scheme

Approximating the diffusion term \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 V''\) uses the standard second-order central difference stencil:

\[ V''(w_i) \approx \frac{V_{i+1} - 2 V_i + V_{i-1}}{(\Delta w)^2}. \]

Approximating the convection drift term \(b(w_i, u) V'(w_i)\) requires careful stencil selection. A central difference \(\frac{V_{i+1} - V_{i-1}}{2 \Delta w}\) introduces severe numerical instability whenever convection dominates diffusion.

Definition 2.1 (Upwind Finite Difference Scheme)

The upwind difference stencil chooses the direction of spatial differentiation based on the sign of the local drift velocity \(b(w_i, u)\):

\[ V'(w_i) \approx \begin{cases} \frac{V_{i+1} - V_i}{\Delta w} \quad (\text{forward difference}), & \text{if } b(w_i, u) \ge 0, \\ \frac{V_i - V_{i-1}}{\Delta w} \quad (\text{backward difference}), & \text{if } b(w_i, u) < 0. \end{cases} \]

This formulation ensures that information flows strictly in the direction of the characteristic drift velocity.

3The M-Matrix Property and Discrete Maximum Principle

For a fixed control policy \(\mathbf{u} = (u_i)_{i=0}^{N-1}\), assembling the discretized differential operator at interior nodes \(i = 1, \dots, N-2\) yields the linear equation:

\[ \rho V_i - \left[ \alpha_i V_{i-1} - \beta_i V_i + \gamma_i V_{i+1} \right] = f(w_i, u_i), \]

where the coefficients under the upwind scheme are:

  • If \(b(w_i, u_i) \ge 0\): \[ \alpha_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{2 (\Delta w)^2}, \quad \gamma_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{2 (\Delta w)^2} + \frac{b(w_i, u_i)}{\Delta w}, \quad \beta_i = \alpha_i + \gamma_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{(\Delta w)^2} + \frac{b(w_i, u_i)}{\Delta w}; \]
  • If \(b(w_i, u_i) < 0\): \[ \alpha_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{2 (\Delta w)^2} - \frac{b(w_i, u_i)}{\Delta w}, \quad \gamma_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{2 (\Delta w)^2}, \quad \beta_i = \alpha_i + \gamma_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{(\Delta w)^2} - \frac{b(w_i, u_i)}{\Delta w}. \]

In matrix notation, this forms the linear system \((\rho I - A[\mathbf{u}]) \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{f}[\mathbf{u}]\), where \(A[\mathbf{u}]\) is a tridiagonal infinitesimal generator matrix.

Theorem 2.2 (M-Matrix Property of the Upwind Operator)

Assumptions: (A1) \(\rho > 0\); (A2) \(\sigma(w_i, u_i) > 0\); (A3) The first derivative is discretized using the upwind scheme (Definition 2.1).

Statement: The matrix \(M \coloneqq \rho I - A[\mathbf{u}] \in \R^{N \times N}\) is a strictly diagonally dominant M-matrix:

  1. The diagonal entries are strictly positive: \(M_{ii} = \rho + \beta_i > 0\);
  2. All off-diagonal entries are non-positive: \(M_{i, i-1} = -\alpha_i \le 0\) and \(M_{i, i+1} = -\gamma_i \le 0\);
  3. \(M\) is strictly diagonally dominant: \(M_{ii} - \sum_{j \neq i} |M_{ij}| = \rho + \beta_i - (\alpha_i + \gamma_i) = \rho > 0\);
  4. The inverse matrix is element-wise non-negative: \(M^{-1} \ge 0\).
Proof

Because \(\sigma^2 > 0\), \(\Delta w > 0\), and upwinding selects \(\pm b \ge 0\), both \(\alpha_i\) and \(\gamma_i\) are strictly positive for all \(i\). Hence the off-diagonal entries \(-\alpha_i\) and \(-\gamma_i\) are strictly negative. The row sum of \(M\) is \(M_{ii} + M_{i, i-1} + M_{i, i+1} = (\rho + \beta_i) - \alpha_i - \gamma_i = \rho + \beta_i - \beta_i = \rho > 0\). Because \(M\) is a \(Z\)-matrix (non-positive off-diagonals) with strictly positive diagonal dominance, it is a non-singular M-matrix, guaranteeing that \(M^{-1} \ge 0\).

Remark 2.3 (Peclet Condition and Failure of Central Differences)

If central differencing \(\frac{V_{i+1} - V_{i-1}}{2 \Delta w}\) were used, the lower off-diagonal entry would be \(-(\frac{\sigma_i^2}{2 (\Delta w)^2} - \frac{b_i}{2 \Delta w})\). Define the local mesh Peclet number:

\[ P_e \coloneqq \frac{|b_i| \Delta w}{\sigma_i^2}. \]

If \(P_e > 1\), the lower off-diagonal becomes strictly positive, violating the M-matrix structure and causing \(M^{-1}\) to develop negative entries. This destroys the discrete maximum principle, generating spurious oscillatory waves in the numerical solution.

4Viscosity Solutions and Barles–Souganidis Convergence

How do we know that the discrete solution \(\mathbf{V}^{(\Delta w)}\) converges to the true continuous value function \(V\) as \(\Delta w \downarrow 0\)?

Theorem 2.4 (Barles–Souganidis Convergence Theorem)

Assumptions: (A1) The continuous HJB equation satisfies the strong comparison principle (uniqueness of viscosity solutions); (A2) The numerical finite difference scheme \(S(\Delta w, w, V(w), [V]) = 0\) satisfies:

  • Consistency: For any smooth test function \(\phi \in C^\infty\), \(S(\Delta w, w, \phi(w), [\phi]) \to \rho \phi(w) - \mathcal{H}(w, \phi', \phi'')\) as \(\Delta w \downarrow 0\);
  • Monotonicity: For any grid vectors \(u \le v\), \(S(\Delta w, w, r, [u]) \ge S(\Delta w, w, r, [v])\);
  • Stability: For each \(\Delta w > 0\), the scheme admits a uniformly bounded solution \(\|V^{(\Delta w)}\|_\infty \le C\).

Statement: As \(\Delta w \downarrow 0\), the discrete numerical solution \(V^{(\Delta w)}(w)\) converges locally uniformly to the unique continuous viscosity solution \(V(w)\) of the HJB equation.

Lab 1 · Upwind vs. central finite difference stability and M-matrix monotonicity

When the mesh Peclet number exceeds 1 (\(P_e = 4.17\)), central differencing produces negative off-diagonal coefficients and negative entries in \((M_{\text{central}})^{-1}\) (violating monotonicity), whereas the upwind scheme guarantees non-negative off-diagonals and an inverse with strictly non-negative entries (\(\ge 0\)).

r"""Lab 1 · Upwind vs. central finite difference stability and M-matrix monotonicity.

Demonstrates that central differencing of the drift term in the HJB equation violates the M-matrix
property when the mesh Peclet number exceeds 1, generating negative entries in (rho I - A)^(-1) and
violating the discrete maximum principle, whereas the upwind scheme guarantees non-negative off-diagonals
and an inverse with strictly non-negative entries for all mesh sizes.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_tridiagonal(sub: list[float], diag: list[float], sup: list[float], rhs: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    n = len(diag)
    c_prime = [0.0] * n
    d_prime = [0.0] * n
    c_prime[0] = sup[0] / diag[0]
    d_prime[0] = rhs[0] / diag[0]
    for i in range(1, n):
        denom = diag[i] - sub[i] * c_prime[i - 1]
        if i < n - 1:
            c_prime[i] = sup[i] / denom
        d_prime[i] = (rhs[i] - sub[i] * d_prime[i - 1]) / denom
    x = [0.0] * n
    x[-1] = d_prime[-1]
    for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
        x[i] = d_prime[i] - c_prime[i] * x[i + 1]
    return x


def invert_tridiagonal(sub: list[float], diag: list[float], sup: list[float]) -> list[list[float]]:
    n = len(diag)
    cols: list[list[float]] = []
    for j in range(n):
        e_j = [1.0 if i == j else 0.0 for i in range(n)]
        col_j = solve_tridiagonal(sub, diag, sup, e_j)
        cols.append(col_j)
    # Transpose cols to get rows
    return [[cols[j][i] for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def main() -> None:
    # Wealth grid with mesh spacing dw
    n = 21
    w_min = 1.0
    w_max = 5.0
    dw = (w_max - w_min) / (n - 1)
    rho = 0.05

    # Convection-diffusion parameters where drift dominates diffusion (SIMULATED)
    # Drift b(w) = 0.5 w, diffusion coefficient d(w) = 0.02 w^2
    w_nodes = [w_min + i * dw for i in range(n)]
    b = [0.5 * x for x in w_nodes]
    diff = [0.02 * (x**2) for x in w_nodes]

    # Calculate local Peclet numbers: Pe_i = b_i * dw / diff_i
    peclet_numbers = [(b[i] * dw) / diff[i] for i in range(1, n - 1)]
    max_pe = max(peclet_numbers)
    print(f"grid nodes n = {n}, mesh spacing dw = {dw:.2f}")
    print(f"maximum mesh Peclet number: {max_pe:.2f} (convection dominates diffusion, Pe > 1)")

    # 1. Central difference system (rho I - A_central)
    sub_c = [0.0] * n
    diag_c = [0.0] * n
    sup_c = [0.0] * n
    diag_c[0] = 1.0
    diag_c[-1] = 1.0

    for i in range(1, n - 1):
        alpha = diff[i] / (dw**2) - b[i] / (2.0 * dw)
        beta = rho + 2.0 * diff[i] / (dw**2)
        gamma = diff[i] / (dw**2) + b[i] / (2.0 * dw)
        sub_c[i] = -alpha
        diag_c[i] = beta
        sup_c[i] = -gamma

    # 2. Upwind difference system (rho I - A_upwind)
    sub_u = [0.0] * n
    diag_u = [0.0] * n
    sup_u = [0.0] * n
    diag_u[0] = 1.0
    diag_u[-1] = 1.0

    for i in range(1, n - 1):
        alpha = diff[i] / (dw**2)
        beta = rho + 2.0 * diff[i] / (dw**2) + b[i] / dw
        gamma = diff[i] / (dw**2) + b[i] / dw
        sub_u[i] = -alpha
        diag_u[i] = beta
        sup_u[i] = -gamma

    # Off-diagonal sign check: in an M-matrix, off-diagonals of (rho I - A) must be <= 0
    # That means -alpha <= 0 and -gamma <= 0, so alpha >= 0 and gamma >= 0.
    min_alpha_c = min(-sub_c[i] for i in range(1, n - 1))
    min_alpha_u = min(-sub_u[i] for i in range(1, n - 1))

    print(f"central difference min alpha (lower off-diagonal): {min_alpha_c:.4f} (negative!)")
    print(f"upwind difference min alpha (lower off-diagonal): {min_alpha_u:.4f} (non-negative)")

    # Invert both matrices to test inverse positivity
    inv_c = invert_tridiagonal(sub_c, diag_c, sup_c)
    inv_u = invert_tridiagonal(sub_u, diag_u, sup_u)

    min_inv_c = min(min(row) for row in inv_c)
    min_inv_u = min(min(row) for row in inv_u)

    print(f"central scheme inverse min element: {min_inv_c:.4f} (violates positivity)")
    print(f"upwind scheme inverse min element: {min_inv_u:.4f} (preserves positivity)")
    print(f"upwind scheme guarantees M-matrix property: {min_inv_u >= -1e-12}")


main()

The experiment confirms Theorem 2.2: with \(P_e = 4.17\), central differencing yields a negative off-diagonal \(\alpha = -0.7800\) and generates negative inverse entries (\(-0.3646\)), violating the discrete maximum principle. In contrast, upwinding preserves positive off-diagonals (\(\alpha = 0.7200\)) and non-negative inverse entries (\(0.0000\)), proving M-matrix stability.

Where the canon breaks

While 1D monotone finite difference schemes are robust and well-understood, standard grid discretization encounters severe limitations in institutional multi-asset portfolios:

  • Curse of dimensionality: A regular grid with \(N\) nodes per dimension requires \(N^d\) total nodes. For a portfolio with \(d = 10\) assets, evaluating \(100^{10} = 10^{20}\) grid nodes is computationally impossible, requiring deep neural network PDE solvers (physics-informed neural networks or deep BSDE solvers).
  • Cross-derivative monotonicity failure: When asset returns are correlated (\(\Sigma_{ij} \neq 0\)), the second-order cross-derivative term \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial w_i \partial w_j}\) requires multi-dimensional diagonal stencils. If the correlation is strong, standard finite-difference stencils fail monotonicity unless wide stencils aligned with the principal axes of \(\Sigma\) are deployed.
  • Boundary layer singularities: At borrowing and collateral boundaries, gradients can become infinite or discontinuous. Uniform grids waste resolution in flat regions while under-resolving boundary layers, requiring adaptive non-uniform mesh refinement.

Misconceptions

  1. Wrong: Higher-order central differencing (\(O(\Delta w^2)\)) is always superior to first-order upwind differencing (\(O(\Delta w)\)) for solving HJB equations.

    While central differences offer second-order formal truncation error, they violate monotonicity when \(P_e > 1\). By the Godunov barrier theorem, high-order linear schemes produce non-physical oscillations and fail to converge to the viscosity solution in regions of non-smoothness. First-order upwinding is unconditionally monotone and guarantees convergence.

  2. Wrong: An M-matrix is simply any symmetric positive definite matrix.

    An M-matrix is a \(Z\)-matrix (off-diagonals \(\le 0\)) whose inverse is element-wise non-negative (\(A^{-1} \ge 0\)). It does not need to be symmetric. In fact, convection-diffusion generator matrices \(A\) with non-zero drift are inherently asymmetric.

  3. Wrong: Discretizing the HJB equation with an explicit time-stepping scheme is computationally cheaper than implicit solvers.

    Explicit schemes for second-order parabolic PDEs face the severe Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) stability constraint \(\Delta t \le \frac{(\Delta w)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\). For fine grids, this forces astronomically small time steps (millions of iterations), whereas implicit tridiagonal solvers take \(O(N)\) time per step without time-step restrictions.

Problem set

Calculate the mesh Peclet number \(P_e\) for grid step \(\Delta w = 0.10\), drift \(b = 0.50\), and diffusion coefficient \(\sigma^2 = 0.04\). Determine whether central differencing is monotone.

Solution

The mesh Peclet number is \(P_e = \frac{|b| \Delta w}{\sigma^2} = \frac{0.50 \times 0.10}{0.04} = \frac{0.05}{0.04} = 1.25\). Because \(P_e = 1.25 > 1.0\), the lower off-diagonal coefficient \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2 (\Delta w)^2} - \frac{b}{2 \Delta w} = \frac{0.04}{0.02} - \frac{0.50}{0.20} = 2.0 - 2.5 = -0.5 < 0\) becomes negative, violating monotonicity. Central differencing is not monotone.

For a uniform grid with \(N = 5\) nodes, \(\Delta w = 0.5\), \(\rho = 0.1\), \(b = 1.0 > 0\), and \(\sigma^2 = 0.5\), write out the interior row coefficients \((\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i)\) under the upwind scheme.

Solution

Because \(b = 1.0 > 0\), the forward upwind scheme applies. The lower coefficient is \(\alpha = \frac{\sigma^2}{2 (\Delta w)^2} = \frac{0.5}{2(0.25)} = 1.0\). The upper coefficient is \(\gamma = \frac{\sigma^2}{2 (\Delta w)^2} + \frac{b}{\Delta w} = 1.0 + \frac{1.0}{0.5} = 1.0 + 2.0 = 3.0\). The diagonal coefficient is \(\beta = \alpha + \gamma = 4.0\). The row in \(\rho I - A\) is \([-1.0, 4.1, -3.0]\), with row sum \(4.1 - 1.0 - 3.0 = 0.1 = \rho > 0\).

Explain why the Thomas algorithm (TDMA) solves a tridiagonal M-matrix linear system in \(O(N)\) floating-point operations without requiring pivoting.

Solution

A tridiagonal M-matrix is strictly diagonally dominant (\(|M_{ii}| > |M_{i, i-1}| + |M_{i, i+1}|\)). By the Wilkinson stability theorem, Gaussian elimination without partial pivoting is unconditionally numerically stable for strictly diagonally dominant matrices, avoiding division by small pivots. Eliminating the sub-diagonal and back-substituting takes exactly \(8N\) operations, scaling linearly as \(O(N)\).

Prove that any matrix \(M \in \R^{N \times N}\) with \(M_{ii} > 0\), \(M_{ij} \le 0\) for \(i \neq j\), and strict row diagonal dominance \(\sum_j M_{ij} > 0\) satisfies the discrete maximum principle.

Solution

Let \(M \mathbf{v} \le 0\). We show that \(\mathbf{v} \le 0\). Suppose for contradiction that \(\max_i v_i > 0\), and let \(k \in \arg\max_i v_i\), so \(v_k > 0\) and \(v_k \ge v_j\) for all \(j\). Then \((M \mathbf{v})_k = M_{kk} v_k + \sum_{j \neq k} M_{kj} v_j \ge M_{kk} v_k + \sum_{j \neq k} M_{kj} v_k = (\sum_j M_{kj}) v_k\). Because \(\sum_j M_{kj} > 0\) and \(v_k > 0\), this product is strictly positive, contradicting \((M \mathbf{v})_k \le 0\). Hence \(\max_i v_i \le 0\), proving the discrete maximum principle.

Show that the upwind approximation of the first derivative has truncation error \(O(\Delta w)\) with leading error term proportional to \(\tfrac{1}{2} |b(x)| \Delta w V''(x)\) (numerical diffusion).

Solution

Consider \(b > 0\) and forward difference \(\frac{V(x + \Delta w) - V(x)}{\Delta w}\). By Taylor's theorem, \(V(x + \Delta w) = V(x) + V'(x)\Delta w + \tfrac{1}{2}V''(x)(\Delta w)^2 + O(\Delta w^3)\). Dividing by \(\Delta w\) gives \(V'(x) + \tfrac{1}{2}V''(x)\Delta w + O(\Delta w^2)\). Multiplying by \(b\) yields \(b V'(x) + \tfrac{1}{2} b \Delta w V''(x)\). The leading truncation error acts as an artificial diffusion coefficient \(\sigma_{\text{num}}^2 = b \Delta w\), which stabilizes the numerical scheme at the cost of first-order accuracy.

Prove that the Barles–Souganidis monotonicity condition is violated if \(M^{-1}\) has even one negative entry.

Solution

Let \(M \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{f} \implies \mathbf{V} = M^{-1} \mathbf{f}\). Suppose \(M^{-1}\) has a negative entry \((M^{-1})_{ij} < 0\). Consider two payoff vectors \(\mathbf{f}_1 \le \mathbf{f}_2\) differing only at coordinate \(j\), so \(\mathbf{f}_2 - \mathbf{f}_1 = \delta \mathbf{e}_j\) with \(\delta > 0\). Then \(V_2(i) - V_1(i) = (M^{-1} (\mathbf{f}_2 - \mathbf{f}_1))_i = \delta (M^{-1})_{ij} < 0\). Increasing the payoff reduces the computed continuation value, violating monotonicity.

In Lab 1, the central difference scheme generates minimum inverse element \(-0.3646\), while the upwind scheme generates \(0.0000\). Demonstrate how this negative element can produce non-monotonic portfolio allocations in volatile markets.

Solution

A negative inverse entry \((M^{-1})_{ij} < 0\) means an increase in reward at node \(j\) reduces the computed value function at node \(i\). This induces artificial non-monotonicity (oscillations) in \(\mathbf{V}\), causing \(V'(w)\) to alternate between excessively steep and negative values. Because optimal allocation is \(\pi^* \propto -V' / (w V'')\), oscillations in derivatives cause the policy \(\pi(w)\) to chatter between zero and maximum leverage on adjacent grid nodes.

A quant desk solves an HJB PDE on a grid of 100 points and observes that the discrete solution matches the exact closed form with maximum relative error 2.5%. If the grid is refined to 400 points, predict the new maximum relative error under the upwind scheme.

Solution

Under the first-order upwind scheme, discretization error scales linearly as \(O(\Delta w)\). Increasing grid nodes by a factor of 4 (from 100 to 400) reduces the mesh spacing \(\Delta w\) by a factor of 4. Therefore, the maximum relative error is predicted to scale down by approximately a factor of 4: \(\text{Error} \approx \frac{2.5\%}{4} \approx 0.625\%\).

Explain how non-uniform logarithmic wealth grids \(w_i = w_{\min} e^{i \Delta y}\) improve numerical accuracy in Merton's problem compared to uniform grids.

Solution

In Merton's problem with CRRA utility, curvature \(|V''(w)| \propto w^{-\gamma - 1}\) is enormous near \(w_{\min}\) and flat for large \(w\). A uniform grid severely under-resolves the steep curvature near \(w_{\min}\) while placing redundant points at large wealth. A logarithmic grid clusters grid points exponentially near \(w_{\min}\), keeping the local relative truncation error \(\frac{\Delta w}{w}\) constant across the entire state domain.

Formulate the Kushner–Dupuis Markov chain approximation method and explain why it is mathematically equivalent to the upwind finite difference scheme.

Solution

Kushner and Dupuis approximate the continuous-time diffusion by a continuous-time Markov chain on the discrete grid. The jump rates to adjacent nodes \(w_{i+1}\) and \(w_{i-1}\) must be non-negative and sum to the total transition rate \(\beta_i\). When drift is positive, the transition probability to \(w_{i+1}\) is increased by \(\frac{b_i \Delta t}{\Delta w}\), matching the upwind forward difference stencil. Hence the upwind scheme is the infinitesimal generator of a locally consistent Markov chain.

Analyze why monotone finite difference schemes for multi-asset HJB equations require wide stencils (Kushner–Dupuis stencils) when assets are highly correlated.

Solution

In 2D with correlation \(\rho_{12}\), the cross-derivative \(\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial w_1 \partial w_2}\) discretized on standard Cartesian axes requires corner stencils \((V_{i+1, j+1} - V_{i+1, j-1} - V_{i-1, j+1} + V_{i-1, j-1})\). If \(|\rho_{12}| > \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}\), the off-diagonal entries become positive, violating the M-matrix property. Wide stencils rotate the difference axes along the eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\), preserving non-negative transition rates.

Discuss how Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) solve high-dimensional HJB equations without grids, and compare their convergence guarantees to the Barles–Souganidis theorem.

Solution

PINNs approximate \(V(t, x)\) by a deep neural network \(\mathcal{N}_\theta(t, x)\), optimizing weights \(\theta\) to minimize the mean squared HJB residual over random collocation points sampled via Monte Carlo. PINNs overcome the curse of dimensionality, scaling to \(d = 100\). However, unlike finite difference schemes governed by the Barles–Souganidis theorem, PINNs lack monotonicity guarantees: non-convex loss landscapes can cause gradient descent to stall in local minima that do not correspond to the viscosity solution.

Retrieval quiz

  1. How does the upwind finite difference scheme choose between forward and backward differencing for \(V'(w)\)?

    Options

    Upwinding aligns the stencil with the direction of characteristic flow, ensuring off-diagonal matrix entries remain non-negative.

  2. What essential property must the discretized system matrix \((\rho I - A)\) possess to satisfy the discrete maximum principle?

    Options

    An M-matrix guarantees that the inverse operator is element-wise non-negative, preventing non-physical oscillations.

  3. When does a central difference scheme for the drift term violate the M-matrix property?

    Options

    When \(P_e > 1\), central differencing causes the lower off-diagonal entry to become positive, destroying M-matrix monotonicity.

  4. What is the critical threshold value of the mesh Peclet number \(P_e\) above which central differencing fails the discrete maximum principle?

    The stability threshold is \(P_e = 1.0\). For \(P_e > 1.0\), central differencing fails monotonicity.

  5. What three conditions in the Barles–Souganidis theorem guarantee that a numerical scheme converges to the unique viscosity solution?

    Options

    By Barles & Souganidis (1991), consistency, monotonicity, and stability are necessary and sufficient for convergence to the viscosity solution.

  6. What computational complexity does the Thomas algorithm achieve for solving an \(N\)-node tridiagonal linear system?

    Options

    The tridiagonal Thomas algorithm eliminates the sub-diagonal in a single forward pass and back-substitutes in \(O(N)\) operations.

  7. In Lab 1, what symptom indicated that the central difference scheme violated the discrete maximum principle?

    Options

    The violation manifested as a negative off-diagonal in the system matrix and negative inverse entries.

  8. What leading numerical artifact is introduced by the first-order upwind differencing scheme?

    Options

    The truncation error of upwind differencing acts as an artificial diffusion coefficient that damps oscillations.
