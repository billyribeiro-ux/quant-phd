The Merton Portfolio & Consumption Problem

  • Prerequisites: Itô's lemma, geometric Brownian motion, HJB equation, verification theorem

Robert Merton's 1969 and 1971 papers revolutionized dynamic economics by bringing continuous-time stochastic calculus into intertemporal finance. Prior mean-variance frameworks were inherently static, treating wealth and risk as single-period decisions. Merton formulated continuous portfolio rebalancing and lifetime consumption as a stochastic control problem. For constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) preferences, a homothetic scaling ansatz collapses the non-linear HJB partial differential equation into an exact algebraic root equation for consumption and investment.

1Financial Market Model & Wealth Dynamics

Consider an investor operating in a continuous-time friction-free securities market over an infinite horizon. The financial opportunity set consists of two assets:

  1. A risk-free money market bond \(B_t\) with constant interest rate \(r > 0\): \[ dB_t = r B_t \, dt; \]
  2. A risky stock \(S_t\) modeled as a geometric Brownian motion with constant expected return \(\mu > r\) and volatility \(\sigma > 0\): \[ dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dB_t, \] where \((B_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a one-dimensional standard Brownian motion.
Definition 1.1 (Self-Financing Wealth Dynamics)

Let \(W_t\) denote the investor's total wealth at time \(t \ge 0\). At each instant \(t\), the investor chooses:

  • \(\pi_t \in \R\): the fraction of wealth allocated to the risky asset \(S\), with the remainder \(1 - \pi_t\) held in the risk-free bond \(B\);
  • \(c_t \ge 0\): the continuous consumption expenditure rate per unit time.

Under the self-financing condition with continuous consumption outflow, wealth satisfies the stochastic differential equation:

\[ dW_t = \left[ (1 - \pi_t) r W_t + \pi_t \mu W_t - c_t \right] dt + \pi_t \sigma W_t \, dB_t = \left[ (r + \pi_t(\mu - r)) W_t - c_t \right] dt + \pi_t \sigma W_t \, dB_t. \]
Remark 1.2 (No Bankruptcy Condition)

To prevent infinite borrowing and Ponzi strategies, the investor faces the strict solvency requirement that wealth must remain non-negative for all \(t \ge 0\): \(W_t \ge 0\) almost surely. If wealth reaches zero, consumption must drop to zero permanently.

2The Merton Control Problem

Definition 1.3 (Merton Portfolio and Consumption Problem)

The Merton portfolio and consumption problem introduced by Merton (1969, 1971) is the stochastic control problem of choosing an admissible control pair \((\pi_t, c_t)_{t \ge 0}\) to maximize expected discounted lifetime utility of consumption:

\[ V(w) \coloneqq \sup_{(\pi, c) \in \mathcal{A}} \E\left[ \int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t} U(c_t) \, dt \;\middle|\; W_0 = w \right], \]

where \(\rho > 0\) is the subjective rate of time preference, and \(U: (0, \infty) \to \R\) is a strictly increasing, strictly concave, twice continuously differentiable utility function satisfying the Inada conditions \(\lim_{c \downarrow 0} U'(c) = \infty\) and \(\lim_{c \to \infty} U'(c) = 0\).

3The HJB Equation for Merton's Problem

Theorem 1.4 (Stationary HJB Equation for the Merton Problem)

Assumptions: (A1) Market parameters \(r, \mu, \sigma\) are constant with \(\mu > r\) and \(\sigma > 0\); (A2) Discount rate \(\rho > 0\); (A3) The value function \(V \in C^2(0, \infty)\) is strictly concave (\(V''(w) < 0\)).

Statement: The value function satisfies the stationary non-linear ordinary differential equation:

\[ \rho V(w) = \sup_{c \ge 0, \, \pi \in \R} \left\{ U(c) + V'(w)\left[ (r + \pi(\mu - r))w - c \right] + \frac{1}{2} V''(w) \pi^2 \sigma^2 w^2 \right\}. \]
Proof of Hamiltonian Optimization

The objective inside the supremum separates into independent terms for consumption \(c\) and portfolio fraction \(\pi\):

\[ \sup_{c \ge 0} \left\{ U(c) - c V'(w) \right\} + V'(w) r w + \sup_{\pi \in \R} \left\{ V'(w)\pi(\mu - r)w + \frac{1}{2} V''(w)\pi^2 \sigma^2 w^2 \right\}. \]

1. Optimal consumption: Because \(U\) is strictly concave, differentiating with respect to \(c\) yields \(U'(c) - V'(w) = 0\). Using the inverse marginal utility function \(I \coloneqq (U')^{-1}\):

\[ c^*(w) = I(V'(w)) = (U')^{-1}(V'(w)). \]

2. Optimal portfolio allocation: The objective in \(\pi\) is a quadratic function. Because \(V''(w) < 0\), it is strictly concave. Differentiating with respect to \(\pi\) and setting the derivative to zero:

\[ V'(w)(\mu - r)w + V''(w)\pi \sigma^2 w^2 = 0 \implies \pi^*(w) = -\frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2} \frac{V'(w)}{w V''(w)}. \]

Substituting \(c^*(w)\) and \(\pi^*(w)\) into the HJB equation eliminates the supremum, yielding the simplified non-linear ODE.

4CRRA Power Utility and Algebraic Separation

Consider the canonical family of Constant Relative Risk Aversion (CRRA) preferences with relative risk aversion parameter \(\gamma > 0\), \(\gamma \neq 1\):

\[ U(c) = \frac{c^{1 - \gamma}}{1 - \gamma}. \]
Theorem 1.5 (Closed-Form Merton Solution for Power Utility)

Assumptions: (A1) \(U(c) = \frac{c^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\) with \(\gamma > 0, \gamma \neq 1\); (A2) Market parameters satisfy the transversality condition:

\[ \nu \coloneqq \frac{1}{\gamma} \left[ \rho - (1 - \gamma)\left( r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{2 \gamma \sigma^2} \right) \right] > 0. \]

Statement: The stationary HJB equation admits the exact closed-form solution:

\[ V(w) = \frac{\nu^{-\gamma}}{1 - \gamma} w^{1 - \gamma}, \]

with optimal constant consumption-wealth ratio and constant portfolio fraction:

\[ c^*(w) = \nu w, \quad \pi^*(w) = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}. \]
Proof by Separation Ansatz

Because CRRA utility is homothetic of degree \(1 - \gamma\), we propose the scaling ansatz following Merton (1969):

\[ V(w) = A \frac{w^{1 - \gamma}}{1 - \gamma}, \]

where \(A > 0\) is an undetermined constant. Evaluating the derivatives of this ansatz:

\[ V'(w) = A w^{-\gamma}, \quad V''(w) = -\gamma A w^{-\gamma - 1}. \]

Notice the ratio: \(-\frac{V'(w)}{w V''(w)} = -\frac{A w^{-\gamma}}{w(-\gamma A w^{-\gamma - 1})} = \frac{1}{\gamma}\). Thus the optimal portfolio fraction is constant:

\[ \pi^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}. \]

For consumption, \(U'(c) = c^{-\gamma} = V'(w) = A w^{-\gamma} \implies c^*(w) = A^{-1/\gamma} w\). Define \(\nu \coloneqq A^{-1/\gamma}\), so that \(c^*(w) = \nu w\) and \(A = \nu^{-\gamma}\).

Substitute \(V(w), V'(w), V''(w), \pi^*\) and \(c^*(w)\) into the stationary HJB equation:

\[ \rho A \frac{w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} = \frac{(\nu w)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} + A w^{-\gamma}\left[ (r + \pi^*(\mu - r))w - \nu w \right] - \frac{1}{2} \gamma A w^{-\gamma - 1} (\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2 w^2. \]

Divide the entire equation by \(\frac{w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\):

\[ \rho A = \nu^{1-\gamma} + (1 - \gamma) A \left[ r + \pi^*(\mu - r) - \nu \right] - \frac{1}{2} \gamma (1 - \gamma) A (\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2. \]

Notice that \(\nu^{1-\gamma} = \nu \cdot \nu^{-\gamma} = \nu A\). Expanding the brackets:

\[ \rho A = \nu A + (1 - \gamma) A \left[ r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{\gamma \sigma^2} - \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{2 \gamma \sigma^2} \right] - (1 - \gamma) \nu A. \]

Divide through by \(A > 0\):

\[ \rho = \nu - (1 - \gamma)\nu + (1 - \gamma) \left[ r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{2 \gamma \sigma^2} \right] = \gamma \nu + (1 - \gamma) k, \]

where \(k \coloneqq r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{2 \gamma \sigma^2}\). Solving this linear algebraic equation for \(\nu\) yields:

\[ \nu = \frac{\rho - (1 - \gamma) k}{\gamma}, \]

and \(A = \nu^{-\gamma}\). Applying the infinite-horizon verification theorem (Theorem 2.2) completes the proof.

Lab 1 · Merton ODE algebraic reduction and consumption-wealth ratio

Across relative risk aversion values \(\gamma \in [1.0, 5.0]\), the normalized algebraic HJB residual \(\rho - \gamma \nu - (1-\gamma)k\) vanishes to machine precision (\(< 10^{-12}\)), and the consumption-wealth ratio \(\nu\) approaches the subjective discount rate \(\rho = 0.05\) as \(\gamma \to 1\).

r"""Lab 1 · Merton ODE algebraic reduction and consumption-wealth ratio.

Demonstrates that the non-linear stationary HJB equation for power utility V(w) = A w^(1-gamma) / (1-gamma)
reduces to the closed-form algebraic root nu = A^(-1/gamma) = (rho - (1-gamma) k) / gamma,
confirming that the HJB algebraic residual vanishes to machine precision and that nu -> rho as gamma -> 1.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def main() -> None:
    # Market and preference parameters (SIMULATED)
    r = 0.025
    mu = 0.085
    sigma = 0.20
    rho = 0.05

    gamma_grid = [1.0001, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, 5.0]

    print(f"market parameters: r = {r:.3f}, mu = {mu:.3f}, sigma = {sigma:.2f}, rho = {rho:.3f}")

    all_vanish = True

    for gamma in gamma_grid:
        pi_star = (mu - r) / (gamma * (sigma**2))
        k = r + 0.5 * ((mu - r) ** 2) / (gamma * (sigma**2))
        nu = (rho - (1.0 - gamma) * k) / gamma
        nu ** (-gamma)

        # HJB algebraic equation residual: rho * A - gamma * A^(1 - 1/gamma) - (1-gamma)*k*A
        # Dividing by A: rho - gamma * nu - (1 - gamma) * k = 0
        normalized_residual = rho - gamma * nu - (1.0 - gamma) * k

        if abs(normalized_residual) > 1e-12:
            all_vanish = False

        print(
            f"gamma = {gamma:5.2f}: pi* = {pi_star:5.3f}, k = {k:6.4f}, "
            f"nu = {nu:6.4f}, residual = {normalized_residual:+.2e}"
        )

    # Check limiting log-utility behavior (gamma -> 1)
    nu_log_limit = (rho - (1.0 - 1.0001) * (r + 0.5 * ((mu - r) ** 2) / (sigma**2))) / 1.0001
    print(f"log-utility limit nu at gamma=1.0001: {nu_log_limit:.6f} vs rho={rho:.6f}")
    print(f"HJB equation satisfied exactly across all gamma: {all_vanish}")

The numerical test validates Theorem 1.5: the algebraic residual vanishes exactly (\(0.00 \times 10^{-17}\)), and as \(\gamma \to 1.0001\), the consumption-wealth ratio converges to \(0.050002\), exactly matching the subjective discount rate \(\rho = 0.05\) predicted for logarithmic utility.

Where the canon breaks

Merton's closed-form solution relies on constant investment opportunities and complete market spanning. In practical quantitative portfolio construction, several canonical assumptions fail:

  • Stochastic investment opportunities: Expected returns \(\mu_t\) and volatilities \(\sigma_t\) vary over time with macroeconomic state variables (interest rates, credit spreads, P/E ratios). The value function becomes state-dependent \(V(t, w, x)\), introducing intertemporal hedging demand terms.
  • Non-tradable labor income: Real-world investors receive labor earnings that cannot be capitalized or traded frictionlessly. Labor income acts as an implicit, imperfectly tradeable bond holding, causing the effective optimal stock fraction to vary dramatically with age and human capital.
  • Margin borrowing rate spreads: Prime brokers charge borrowing rates \(r_b > r\) on leveraged long positions (\(\pi > 1\)). This creates an endogenous kink in the wealth dynamics, destroying the scale-invariant homothetic ansatz and necessitating numerical HJB solvers.

Misconceptions

  1. Wrong: For all utility functions, the optimal consumption rate is a constant linear fraction of wealth \(c^*(w) = \nu w\).

    Linear consumption \(c^*(w) = \nu w\) holds exclusively for homothetic CRRA utility. For non-homothetic preferences (such as CARA exponential utility \(U(c) = -e^{-\alpha c}\) or Stone–Geary subsistence utility \(U(c) = \log(c - \underline{c})\)), the optimal consumption policy contains an additive affine or non-linear term.

  2. Wrong: Higher stock return \(\mu\) always causes the investor to consume a larger fraction of their wealth \(\nu\).

    A higher Sharpe ratio increases the investment growth rate \(k\). Its effect on the consumption fraction \(\nu = \frac{1}{\gamma}[\rho - (1-\gamma)k]\) depends on risk aversion \(\gamma\). If \(\gamma > 1\), the income effect dominates the substitution effect, and a higher return causes the investor to save more and reduce their consumption fraction \(\nu\).

  3. Wrong: The transversality condition \(\nu > 0\) is automatically satisfied whenever the discount rate \(\rho\) is positive.

    If \(\gamma < 1\), the factor \(1 - \gamma > 0\). If the stock has an exceptionally high Sharpe ratio, \((1-\gamma)k\) can exceed \(\rho\), resulting in \(\nu < 0\). In that case, the investment opportunity generates infinite expected lifetime utility, and no finite value function exists.

Problem set

Calculate the optimal consumption-wealth ratio \(\nu\) for an investor with \(\gamma = 2.0\), \(\rho = 0.06\), risk-free rate \(r = 0.02\), stock drift \(\mu = 0.08\), and stock volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\).

Solution

Compute the growth factor \(k = r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{2 \gamma \sigma^2} = 0.02 + \frac{(0.08 - 0.02)^2}{2(2.0)(0.20^2)} = 0.02 + \frac{0.0036}{0.16} = 0.02 + 0.0225 = 0.0425\). With \(\gamma = 2.0\) and \(\rho = 0.06\): \(\nu = \frac{\rho - (1 - \gamma)k}{\gamma} = \frac{0.06 - (1 - 2.0)(0.0425)}{2.0} = \frac{0.06 + 0.0425}{2.0} = \frac{0.1025}{2.0} = 0.05125\) (or 5.125%).

For the parameters in problem M1, compute the value function multiplier \(A = \nu^{-\gamma}\) and evaluate \(V(w)\) at wealth \(w = 100\).

Solution

\(A = \nu^{-2} = (0.05125)^{-2} = \frac{1}{0.00262656} \approx 380.727\). The value function is \(V(w) = A \frac{w^{1 - \gamma}}{1 - \gamma} = 380.727 \frac{100^{-1}}{-1} = -\frac{380.727}{100} = -3.80727\).

Show that for log utility \(U(c) = \log c\) (\(\gamma \to 1\)), the HJB equation yields \(c^*(w) = \rho w\) directly.

Solution

For log utility, candidate value function has the form \(V(w) = \frac{1}{\rho}\log w + C\). Differentiating gives \(V'(w) = \frac{1}{\rho w}\). The optimal consumption condition is \(U'(c) = \frac{1}{c} = V'(w) = \frac{1}{\rho w}\). Inverting yields \(c^*(w) = \rho w\), showing that the consumption fraction equals \(\rho\) regardless of market investment opportunities.

Prove that for power utility with \(\gamma > 1\), the transversality condition \(\nu > 0\) is satisfied for all positive values of \(r, \mu > r, \sigma > 0\), and \(\rho > 0\).

Solution

Recall \(\nu = \frac{1}{\gamma}[\rho - (1-\gamma)k]\). For \(\gamma > 1\), \(1 - \gamma < 0\), so \(-(1-\gamma) = \gamma - 1 > 0\). The growth factor \(k = r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{2\gamma \sigma^2} > 0\) because \(r > 0\) and \(\mu > r\). Therefore, \(\rho - (1-\gamma)k = \rho + (\gamma - 1)k\). Because \(\rho > 0\) and \((\gamma - 1)k > 0\), the sum is strictly positive. Dividing by \(\gamma > 0\) establishes that \(\nu > 0\) holds unconditionally for all \(\gamma > 1\).

Prove that under the optimal policy \((c^*, \pi^*)\), the logarithm of wealth \(\log W_t\) follows a Brownian motion with constant drift and volatility.

Solution

Substituting \(c_t^* = \nu W_t\) and \(\pi_t^* = \pi^*\) into the wealth dynamics gives \(dW_t = [r + \pi^*(\mu - r) - \nu] W_t dt + \pi^* \sigma W_t dB_t\). Define \(Y_t = \log W_t\). By Itô's lemma, \(dY_t = \frac{1}{W_t} dW_t - \frac{1}{2 W_t^2} (dW_t)^2 = [r + \pi^*(\mu - r) - \nu - \frac{1}{2}(\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2] dt + \pi^* \sigma dB_t\). Since all coefficients are constants, \(\log W_t\) is an arithmetic Brownian motion with Gaussian increments.

Show that the marginal value of wealth equals the marginal utility of consumption along the optimal path: \(V'(W_t) = U'(c_t^*)\).

Solution

The first-order condition in the HJB equation is \(\frac{\partial}{\partial c}[U(c) - c V'(w)] = U'(c) - V'(w) = 0\). Evaluating at \(c = c^*(w)\) gives \(U'(c^*(w)) = V'(w)\). Along the state trajectory \(W_t\), this establishes \(U'(c_t^*) = V'(W_t)\), equating the marginal benefit of immediate consumption to the shadow value of reinvestment.

In Lab 1, as risk aversion increases from \(\gamma = 1.5\) to \(\gamma = 5.0\), the consumption-wealth ratio \(\nu\) decreases from \(0.0517\) to \(0.0372\). Provide the economic interpretation of this empirical pattern.

Solution

As risk aversion \(\gamma\) increases, the investor's elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(1/\gamma\) drops. Because \(\gamma > 1\), the income effect dominates the substitution effect: the investor perceives financial market uncertainty as a threat to future solvency, leading to precautionary saving. The investor curtails immediate consumption, lowering the consumption fraction \(\nu\) from 5.17% to 3.72%.

A robo-advisor calibrates Merton's consumption policy to a client with wealth $1,000,000 using \(\nu = 0.045\). If the equity market experiences a 30% crash in one month, calculate the dollar consumption reduction mandated by Merton's rule, and discuss why real clients often resist this policy.

Solution

Prior to the crash, annual consumption is \(0.045 \times \$1,000,000 = \$45,000\). If wealth drops 30% to \(\$700,000\), Merton's policy mandates immediate reduction to \(0.045 \times \$700,000 = \$31,500\), a \$13,500 (30%) cut. Real clients resist because consumption exhibits habit persistence and committed living expenditures (mortgages, healthcare), making immediate 30% lifestyle cuts psychologically and contractually infeasible.

Explain how empirical calibrations of relative risk aversion \(\gamma\) in asset pricing (such as the equity premium puzzle where \(\gamma > 20\) is implied) affect the plausibility of Merton's consumption-wealth ratio \(\nu\).

Solution

If \(\gamma = 25\), \(\pi^* = \frac{0.06}{25 \times 0.04} = 0.06\), implying households should hold only 6% equity. Furthermore, \(\nu = \frac{1}{25}[\rho - (1-25)k] \approx \frac{\rho + 24k}{25} \approx k\). If \(k \approx 0.03\), the consumption-wealth ratio is pinned closely to the interest rate, decoupling consumption from equity market fluctuations.

Formulate the Merton problem under Epstein–Zin recursive preferences, separating the coefficient of relative risk aversion \(\gamma\) from the elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(\psi\).

Solution

Under continuous-time Epstein–Zin preferences (Duffie–Epstein), the aggregator is \(f(c, V) = \frac{\rho}{1 - 1/\psi} \frac{c^{1 - 1/\psi} - ((1-\gamma)V)^{\frac{1 - 1/\psi}{1-\gamma}}}{((1-\gamma)V)^{\frac{1 - 1/\psi}{1-\gamma} - 1}}\). The portfolio choice remains myopic with \(\pi^* = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}\) (governed by \(\gamma\)), while the consumption-wealth ratio is \(\nu = \rho^\psi [ (1-\gamma) A ]^{\frac{\psi - 1}{1-\gamma}}\) (governed primarily by EIS \(\psi\)), resolving the entanglement of risk aversion and intertemporal substitution.

Analyze the HJB equation for Merton's problem when the investor has internal habit formation with habit level \(H_t\) satisfying \(dH_t = \delta(C_t - H_t) dt\) and utility \(U(C - H)\).

Solution

Habit formation introduces \(H_t\) as an endogenous second state variable. The value function becomes \(V(W, H)\). The HJB equation includes the cross-derivative \(\partial_H V\) multiplied by \(\delta(c - H)\). The optimal consumption policy satisfies \(U'(c - H) = V_W(W, H) + \delta V_H(W, H)\). The state space is bounded by the solvency constraint \(W_t \ge \frac{H_t}{r + \delta}\), making the value function non-homothetic and inducing history-dependent risk aversion.

Discuss how the introduction of borrowing constraints \(\pi \le \pi_{\max}\) alters the separation ansatz and creates an endogenous consumption distortion.

Solution

When the borrowing constraint binds (\(\pi^* > \pi_{\max}\)), the investor is forced to take less equity risk than desired. While the problem remains homothetic under pure proportional constraints (preserving \(V(w) \propto w^{1-\gamma}\)), the effective portfolio return drops to \(k_{\text{constr}} = r + \pi_{\max}(\mu - r) - \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma \pi_{\max}^2 \sigma^2 < k\). This reduction in investment efficiency distorts the optimal consumption fraction to \(\nu_{\text{constr}} = \frac{1}{\gamma}[\rho - (1-\gamma)k_{\text{constr}}]\), altering the investor's intertemporal savings trajectory.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What functional form does the value function \(V(w)\) take for CRRA power utility \(U(c) = \frac{c^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\) in Merton's problem?

    Options

    Because CRRA preferences are homothetic of degree \(1-\gamma\), the value function inherits the same power scaling law in wealth.

  2. In Merton's problem, what relationship connects the optimal consumption rate \(c^*(w)\) to the value function \(V(w)\)?

    Options

    The first-order condition in the HJB Hamiltonian equates marginal utility of consumption to the marginal shadow value of wealth \(V'(w)\).

  3. What is the optimal consumption-wealth ratio \(\nu = c^*(w)/w\) for logarithmic utility (\(\gamma = 1\))?

    Options

    For log utility, substitution and income effects cancel exactly, making the consumption-wealth ratio equal to the subjective discount rate \(\rho\).

  4. For an investor with log utility (\(\gamma = 1.0\)) and subjective discount rate \(\rho = 0.05\), what is the optimal consumption-wealth ratio \(\nu\)?

    For log utility, \(\nu = \rho = 0.05\).

  5. What is the effective growth factor \(k\) in the Merton portfolio problem?

    Options

    The certainty equivalent growth rate is \(r + \pi^*(\mu - r) - \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma(\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2 = r + \frac{(\mu - r)^2}{2\gamma \sigma^2}\).

  6. When relative risk aversion \(\gamma > 1\), what condition guarantees that transversality holds and the value function is well-defined?

    Options

    When \(\gamma > 1\), \(-(1-\gamma)k = (\gamma - 1)k > 0\), ensuring \(\rho + (\gamma - 1)k > 0\) for all positive parameters.

  7. What happens to the optimal portfolio fraction \(\pi^*\) when wealth \(W_t\) doubles under CRRA utility?

    Options

    CRRA preferences scale homothetically; risk tolerance is linear in wealth, keeping the optimal portfolio weight constant.

  8. Why can an investor with CRRA preferences never go bankrupt (\(W_t = 0\)) in finite time?

    Options

    Inada conditions penalize zero consumption infinitely, and geometric Brownian motion dynamics prevent wealth from hitting zero in finite time.
