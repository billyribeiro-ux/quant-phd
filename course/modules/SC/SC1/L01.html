Brownian Motion as a Gaussian Process

Brownian motion is the basic continuous-time model of random noise in this unit. It is defined in §1 (Definition 1.1) by three conditions on the process and its increments: the process starts at zero, each increment is normal with variance equal to its length, and increments over disjoint intervals are independent. Under Assumption A1(a), the covariance follows: \(B_s\) and \(B_t\) share the increments up to time \(\min(s,t)\), and Proposition 1.2 proves \(\Cov(B_s,B_t)=\min(s,t)\). Lesson 2 constructs a process with these properties and continuous paths on \([0,1]\), under Assumption A2 and Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 2. This lesson does not establish path continuity, and §4 gives an example showing that the definition alone does not settle it.

1Definition and the laws it fixes

A process is a family \((B_t)_{t\ge 0}\) of random variables indexed by time, all defined on one probability space. For a fixed outcome \(\omega\), the function \(t\mapsto B_t(\omega)\) is a path of the process. A statement about finitely many times \(t_1,\dots,t_r\) concerns the joint law of \((B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_r})\), which is a finite-dimensional law.

Definition 1.1 (standard Brownian motion)

A process \((B_t)_{t\ge 0}\) is a standard Brownian motion when the following three conditions hold.

  1. (D1) \(B_0 = 0\).
  2. (D2) For \(0\le s<t\), the increment \(B_t-B_s\) has the normal law \(\Normal(0,\,t-s)\). Its mean is 0 and its variance is \(t-s\).
  3. (D3) For every \(0\le t_0<t_1<\dots<t_r\), the increments \(B_{t_1}-B_{t_0},\ \dots,\ B_{t_r}-B_{t_{r-1}}\) are mutually independent.

"Standard" fixes the variance per unit of time at 1, because (D1) and (D2) give \(\Var(B_t)=t\) for every \(t\ge 0\): (D1) gives the case \(t=0\), and (D2) with \(s=0\) gives the case \(t>0\). (D2) and (D3) are statements about laws. (D1) is pathwise only at time 0: it says that every path starts at 0. The three conditions do not force continuity between times: §4 gives a process that satisfies all three with discontinuous paths, so Lesson 2 has a real task to do.

2Covariance and finite-dimensional normality

Assumption A1 (standard facts, stated without proof)
  1. (a) If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent and integrable, then \(\E[XY]=\E[X]\,\E[Y]\).
  2. (b) A finite linear combination of independent normal random variables is normal. A vector whose coordinates are such combinations is multivariate normal.
  3. (c) Two coordinates of a multivariate normal vector with covariance zero are independent.
  4. (d) If a sequence of random variables converges at every outcome of a measurable event \(E\), then the limit on \(E\), set to 0 off \(E\), is a random variable.
  5. (e) Functions of disjoint subfamilies of an independent family are independent. A family of mutually independent blocks, each internally independent, is mutually independent.

Part (a) is the product rule for integrable independent variables. Parts (b) and (c) concern normal variables: (b) is closure under finite linear combinations of independent normals, and (c) is independence of uncorrelated coordinates of a jointly normal vector. Part (d) is a measurability fact, and part (e) is a fact about independent families. This page states the five parts, does not prove them, and cites no source for them. A result below that uses a part says "Assuming A1" and the part.

Proposition 1.2 (covariance of Brownian motion)
  1. (H1) \((B_t)_{t\ge 0}\) is a standard Brownian motion (Definition 1.1).
  2. (H2) Assuming A1(a).

Conclusion: for all \(s,t\ge 0\), \(\Cov(B_s,B_t)=\min(s,t)\).

Proof

Since \(\Cov\) is symmetric, it suffices to take \(s\le t\). If \(s=0\), then \(B_s=B_0=0\) by (D1), so both sides are 0. If \(s=t\), then \(\Cov(B_s,B_s)=\Var(B_s)\), and (D1) with (D2) for the pair \(0<s\) gives \(B_s=B_s-B_0\sim\Normal(0,s)\), so \(\Var(B_s)=s=\min(s,t)\). It remains to treat \(0<s<t\).

Put \(X=B_s-B_0=B_s\) and \(Y=B_t-B_s\). The times \(0<s<t\) satisfy the hypothesis of (D3) with \(t_0=0\), \(t_1=s\), \(t_2=t\), so \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent. By (D2), \(X\sim\Normal(0,s)\) and \(Y\sim\Normal(0,t-s)\), so both have mean 0, \(\E X^2=s\), and both are square-integrable. Since \(|XY|\le (X^2+Y^2)/2\), and \(X^2\) and \(Y^2\) are integrable (\(\E X^2=s\), \(\E Y^2=t-s\)), the product \(XY\) is integrable. Assuming A1(a), \(\E[XY]=\E[X]\,\E[Y]=0\).

Now \(B_s=X\) and \(B_t=X+Y\). Using \(\E B_s=\E B_t=0\), \[\Cov(B_s,B_t)=\E[B_sB_t]=\E[X(X+Y)]=\E X^2+\E[XY]=s+0=s.\] Since \(s<t\), \(s=\min(s,t)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.3 (finite-dimensional laws are multivariate normal)
  1. (H1) \((B_t)_{t\ge 0}\) is a standard Brownian motion.
  2. (H2) Assuming A1(b) and A1(a).

Conclusion: for \(0<t_1<\dots<t_r\), the vector \((B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_r})\) is multivariate normal with mean 0 and covariance matrix \(\bigl(\min(t_i,t_j)\bigr)_{i,j=1}^{r}\).

Proof

Put \(t_0=0\) and \(\Delta_i=B_{t_i}-B_{t_{i-1}}\) for \(1\le i\le r\). Since \(B_{t_0}=0\) by (D1), \(B_{t_k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Delta_i\) for each \(k\). By (D3) the \(\Delta_i\) are independent, and by (D2) each is \(\Normal(0,\,t_i-t_{i-1})\). Each \(B_{t_k}\) is therefore a linear combination of independent normal variables, and by A1(b) the vector \((B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_r})\) is multivariate normal. Its mean is 0 because each \(\Delta_i\) has mean 0. Its covariance matrix has entries \(\min(t_i,t_j)\) by Proposition 1.2. \(\blacksquare\)

A process is a Gaussian process when every vector \((B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_r})\) with \(0<t_1<\dots<t_r\) is multivariate normal. Assuming A1(a) and A1(b), Remark 1.3 says that a standard Brownian motion is one, with mean zero and covariance \(\min(s,t)\) for times \(s,t>0\).

3The scale of an increment

Example 1.4 (a short increment)
  1. (H1) \((B_t)_{t\ge 0}\) is a standard Brownian motion, \(t\ge 0\), and \(h>0\).

Conclusion: the standard deviation of \(B_{t+h}-B_t\) is \(\sqrt h\), so its ratio to \(h\) is \(1/\sqrt h\).

Proof

Apply (D2) to the pair \(t<t+h\). The increment \(B_{t+h}-B_t\) then has variance \(h\). Its standard deviation is the positive square root, \(\sqrt h\). The ratio of the standard deviation to \(h\) is \(\sqrt h/h=1/\sqrt h\). \(\blacksquare\)

For \(h=1/4\), the standard deviation is \(1/2\), and for \(h=1/100\) it is \(1/10\). For every \(h\) in \((0,1)\), \(\sqrt h>h\), so the standard deviation of the increment exceeds the length of the interval. The ratio \(1/\sqrt h\) grows without bound as \(h\to 0\). A dyadic partition of \([0,t]\) of depth \(n\) is the partition that cuts it into \(2^n\) pieces of length \(t/2^n\).

Lab 1 · Exact checks of the covariance identity on finite grids

Expected result: a dyadic partition of \([0,t]\) of depth \(n\) cuts \([0,t]\) into \(2^n\) equal pieces of length \(t/2^n\). The variances of the increments over such a partition sum to exactly \(t\), for each of the three times \(t\) in the lab and each depth from 0 to 4, so the count of agreeing cases is 15 of 15. On the grid \(t_i=i/8\), \(i=0,\dots,8\), the covariance computed from independent increments equals \(\min(t_i,t_j)\) for each of the 81 ordered pairs, so the count is 81 of 81. Every comparison is between exact Fraction values, so no tolerance is involved.

"""SC1 lab L01: exact checks of the covariance identity of Brownian motion on finite rational grids.

Standard library only. Nothing is random and nothing is read from a file, so every run prints the same lines.
Every time, increment variance and covariance is a fractions.Fraction, so each printed comparison is exact.
The lab simulates no path. It checks two identities on finite grids. The increment variances over a dyadic partition
of [0, t] sum to t; this is arithmetic on the piece lengths, which Definition 1.1 (D2) fixes. The covariance computed
from independent increments equals min(s, t) at every pair of grid times; this is the identity of Proposition 1.2,
checked on a grid.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

TIMES = (Fraction(1), Fraction(3, 4), Fraction(5, 2))
DEPTH = 4
GRID = 8


def dyadic_variance_sum(t: Fraction, n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Sum of the variances of the 2**n increments of B over the dyadic partition of [0, t].

    Each piece has length t / 2**n. By Definition 1.1 (D2) its increment has variance equal to its length.
    """
    width = t / 2**n
    return sum((width for _ in range(2**n)), Fraction(0))


def covariance_from_increments(variances: list[Fraction], i: int, j: int) -> Fraction:
    """Cov(B at grid time i, B at grid time j) when B at time 0 is 0 and B at grid time k is the sum of the first k
    increments, which are independent and have the given variances.

    Both values contain the first min(i, j) increments and no other shared increment, so the covariance is the sum
    of their variances.
    """
    return sum(variances[: min(i, j)], Fraction(0))


def main() -> None:
    dyadic_cases = 0
    dyadic_equal = 0
    for t in TIMES:
        for n in range(DEPTH + 1):
            total = dyadic_variance_sum(t, n)
            dyadic_cases += 1
            dyadic_equal += total == t
        print(f"t = {t}: variances of the {2**DEPTH} increments sum to {dyadic_variance_sum(t, DEPTH)}")
    print(f"dyadic variance sums equal t: {dyadic_equal} of {dyadic_cases} cases (depths 0 to {DEPTH})")

    grid = [Fraction(i, GRID) for i in range(GRID + 1)]
    variances = [Fraction(1, GRID)] * GRID
    pairs = 0
    agree = 0
    for i in range(GRID + 1):
        for j in range(GRID + 1):
            pairs += 1
            agree += covariance_from_increments(variances, i, j) == min(grid[i], grid[j])
    example_i, example_j = 2, 5
    example_cov = covariance_from_increments(variances, example_i, example_j)
    example_min = min(grid[example_i], grid[example_j])
    print(f"Cov(B at {grid[example_i]}, B at {grid[example_j]}) = {example_cov}; min of the two times = {example_min}")
    print(f"covariance equals min(s, t) on the grid: {agree} of {pairs} pairs")


main()

t = 3/4: variances of the 16 increments sum to 3/4
t = 5/2: variances of the 16 increments sum to 5/2
dyadic variance sums equal t: 15 of 15 cases (depths 0 to 4)
Cov(B at 1/4, B at 5/8) = 1/4; min of the two times = 1/4

The lab checks two identities on finite rational grids. The dyadic variance sums are arithmetic on the piece lengths that (D2) fixes. The covariance line is the identity of Proposition 1.2, checked at grid pairs. It does not simulate a path, and it adds no evidence beyond the proof. The first three lines show the dyadic variance sums for the three times at depth 4, each equal to its time. The count line reports the depth check. 4Where the canon breaks

Each item below names a hypothesis whose failure changes the conclusion.

  • The definition does not force continuous paths. Let \((B_t)_{t\ge 0}\) be a standard Brownian motion whose paths are continuous for every outcome. Assuming A1(a), A1(b), A1(e), A2 and Assumption 2.1 (both stated in Lesson 2), Remark 2.6 of Lesson 2 gives such a process \(B\) on a probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\) that carries independent coefficient families as in Hypothesis H1 of Lesson 2. Form the product space \((\Omega\times(0,1),\ \mathcal F\otimes\operatorname{Bor}(0,1),\ \Prob\otimes\mathrm{Leb})\), where \(\operatorname{Bor}(0,1)\) is the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra of \((0,1)\) and \(\mathrm{Leb}\) is Lebesgue measure on \((0,1)\), and extend \(B\) to it by \(B_t(\omega,u)=B_t(\omega)\). Let \(U(\omega,u)=u\). Then \(U\) is uniform on \((0,1)\) and independent of \(B\). Every event of \(\sigma(B)\) has the form \(A\times(0,1)\) with \(A\in\mathcal F\), and for such \(A\) and every Borel \(C\subseteq(0,1)\) the product measure gives \(\Prob\bigl((A\times(0,1))\cap(\Omega\times C)\bigr)=\Prob(A)\,\mathrm{Leb}(C)\), which is the product of \(\Prob(A\times(0,1))=\Prob(A)\) and \(\Prob(\Omega\times C)=\mathrm{Leb}(C)\). The extended \(B\) still has every path continuous, since the path at \((\omega,u)\) is the path at \(\omega\). Define \(\tilde B_t=B_t+\indic\{t=U\}\). Since \(U\in(0,1)\), we have \(\tilde B_0=B_0=0\), which is (D1). For every finite set of times \(G\), \(\Prob(U\in G)=0\), so \(\tilde B_t=B_t\) almost surely for each \(t\in G\). The finite-dimensional laws of \(\tilde B\) therefore equal those of \(B\), and (D2) and (D3) hold for \(\tilde B\). Yet for every outcome, \(\tilde B_U=B_U+1\), while \(\tilde B_t=B_t\) for \(t\ne U\). Because \(B\) is continuous, \(\tilde B_t\to B_U\) as \(t\to U\), so \(\tilde B\) is discontinuous at \(U\) on every outcome.
  • Whole-path properties are not fixed by the finite-dimensional laws. Under the assumptions of the first item, the event that the path is continuous holds for \(B\) on every outcome and for \(\tilde B\) on no outcome, although the two processes have the same finite-dimensional laws. A statement about the whole path needs the paths themselves, not only the laws.
  • A non-normal increment law breaks Remark 1.3. Proposition 1.2 uses \(B_0=0\) (D1), the mean 0 and variance \(t-s\) of each increment (D2), the independence of increments (D3), and Assumption A1(a). If (D2) is replaced by a non-normal law with the same mean and variance, Proposition 1.2 still holds. Remark 1.3 does not, because Assumption A1(b) is a statement about normal variables. For example, let \(B_1\) take the values \(+1\) and \(-1\) with probability \(1/2\) each. Then \(B_1\) has mean 0 and variance 1, as (D2) requires, but \(\Prob(B_1=1)=1/2\neq 0\). A normal variable has \(\Prob(X=x)=0\) for every \(x\), so \(B_1\) is not normal, and the vector \((B_1)\) is not multivariate normal. Once (D2) is replaced, Remark 1.3 cannot conclude that \((B_1)\) is multivariate normal, because its normality came from (D2).

5Misconceptions

  1. “The values \(B_s\) and \(B_t\) are independent, because the increments are.”

    (D3) gives independence of increments over disjoint intervals, and it says nothing about the values \(B_s\) and \(B_t\). For \(s=1/2\) and \(t=1\), Assuming A1(a), Proposition 1.2 gives \(\Cov(B_{1/2},B_1)=1/2\), which is not zero. Independent variables have covariance zero by A1(a), so \(B_{1/2}\) and \(B_1\) are not independent.

  2. “An increment over a short interval of length \(h\) is of order \(h\).”

    The standard deviation of \(B_{t+h}-B_t\) is \(\sqrt h\), by Example 1.4. For \(h=1/100\) it is \(1/10\), which is ten times the length \(h\), not of the same order. The ratio \(1/\sqrt h\) grows without bound as \(h\to 0\).

  3. “Definition 1.1 fixes the paths, so Brownian motion is one function.”

    Definition 1.1 fixes finite-dimensional laws, not the set of paths. The process \(\tilde B\) of §4 satisfies (D1)–(D3) and has the same finite-dimensional laws as \(B\), yet its paths differ from those of \(B\) and are discontinuous at \(U\) on every outcome.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · Covariance and variance at two times

For a standard Brownian motion, assuming A1(a), compute \(\Cov(B_{1/3},B_{2/3})\) and \(\Var(B_{2/3}-B_{1/3})\).

Solution

By Proposition 1.2, \(\Cov(B_{1/3},B_{2/3})=\min(1/3,2/3)=1/3\). By (D2) with \(s=1/3\) and \(t=2/3\), the increment \(B_{2/3}-B_{1/3}\) has variance \(2/3-1/3=1/3\). Both values are \(1/3\).

M2 · Variance of a difference of values

Let \(0<s<t\). Assuming A1(a), show that \(\Cov(B_s,B_t-B_s)=0\), and find \(\Var(B_t)-\Var(B_s)\).

Solution

The covariance is \(\E[B_s(B_t-B_s)]-\E B_s\,\E(B_t-B_s)\). Both means are 0. The variables \(B_s\) and \(B_t-B_s\) are independent by (D3), so A1(a) gives \(\E[B_s(B_t-B_s)]=\E B_s\,\E(B_t-B_s)=0\). Hence the covariance is 0. By (D2), \(\Var(B_t)=t\) and \(\Var(B_s)=s\), so \(\Var(B_t)-\Var(B_s)=t-s\).

M3 · A linear combination at two times

For a standard Brownian motion, find the mean and the variance of \(3B_1-B_2\), assuming A1(a).

Solution

The mean is \(3\cdot 0-0=0\). The variance expands as \(\Var(3B_1-B_2)=9\Var(B_1)+\Var(B_2)-6\Cov(B_1,B_2)\). Here \(\Var(B_1)=1\), \(\Var(B_2)=2\), and \(\Cov(B_1,B_2)=\min(1,2)=1\) by Proposition 1.2. So the variance is \(9+2-6=5\).

Proof

P1 · The case \(s=t\) without A1

Using only (D1) and (D2), prove that \(\Var(B_s)=s\) for every \(s>0\).

Solution

By (D1), \(B_0=0\), so \(B_s=B_s-B_0\). Condition (D2) with the pair \(0<s\) states that \(B_s-B_0\) has the normal law \(\Normal(0,s)\), whose variance is \(s\). So \(\Var(B_s)=s\). No part of A1 is used, because only one normal law is involved.

P2 · Independence of a value and a later increment

Show that \(B_{1/2}\) and \(B_1-B_{1/2}\) are independent, and state which condition gives it.

Solution

Apply (D3) with \(t_0=0\), \(t_1=1/2\), and \(t_2=1\). The increments \(B_{1/2}-B_0=B_{1/2}\) and \(B_1-B_{1/2}\) are independent. This uses (D1), which gives \(B_0=0\), and (D3). No part of A1 is needed, because independence is given directly.

P3 · The covariance for \(s>t\)

Let \(s>t\). Assuming A1(a), and using Proposition 1.2 for the pair \((t,s)\), show that \(\Cov(B_s,B_t)=t\). Name the property of covariance used.

Solution

Covariance is symmetric: \(\Cov(X,Y)=\E[XY]-\E X\,\E Y=\Cov(Y,X)\), because the product \(XY\) is the same as \(YX\). So \(\Cov(B_s,B_t)=\Cov(B_t,B_s)\). Proposition 1.2 for the pair \(t<s\) gives \(\Cov(B_t,B_s)=\min(t,s)=t\). The property used is symmetry of the covariance.

Empirical

E1 · The dyadic pieces for \(t=3/4\)

Lab 1 prints that the 16 increment variances over the dyadic partition of \([0,3/4]\) sum to \(3/4\). Find the length of each piece and check the sum.

Solution

The pieces have length \((3/4)/16=3/64\). Each has variance \(3/64\) by (D2), and \(16\cdot 3/64=48/64=3/4\). This matches the printed line for \(t=3/4\), so the printed sum is the sum of the variances of the pieces.

E2 · The covariance at \(1/4\) and \(5/8\)

Lab 1 prints \(\Cov(B_{1/4},B_{5/8})=1/4\). Assuming A1(a), derive this value from the increments on the grid of eighths.

Solution

On the grid \(i/8\), each increment has variance \(1/8\). The value at \(1/4=2/8\) contains the first two increments, and the value at \(5/8\) contains the first five. They share the first two, so the covariance is \(2\cdot 1/8=1/4\). The minimum of \(1/4\) and \(5/8\) is \(1/4\), which agrees with the printed line.

E3 · What the 81 of 81 count tests

Lab 1 prints 81 of 81 pairs. Explain how many pairs there are, and what the count tests.

Solution

The grid has nine times, \(i/8\) for \(i=0,\dots,8\). Ordered pairs \((i,j)\) with \(0\le i,j\le 8\) number \(9\cdot 9=81\), and the diagonal pairs are included. The count tests the identity \(\Cov(B_{t_i},B_{t_j})=\min(t_i,t_j)\) of Proposition 1.2 at each of these 81 pairs. It is an exact algebraic check on a grid, not a simulation and not a limit.

Research

R1 · What the covariance proof uses

Let \((X_t)\) be a process with \(X_0=0\), with independent increments over disjoint intervals, and with each increment of mean 0 and variance equal to its length. Show that \(\Cov(X_s,X_t)=\min(s,t)\), assuming A1(a), and state which properties of the process the proof of Proposition 1.2 uses.

Solution

The proof of Proposition 1.2 uses (D1) for \(X_0=0\), independence of \(X_s\) and \(X_t-X_s\), the mean 0 and the variance of each increment, and A1(a). It does not use normality, so the same argument gives \(\Cov(X_s,X_t)=s=\min(s,t)\) for \(0<s<t\). The case \(s=t\) and the symmetry step are unchanged.

R2 · A counterexample to uniqueness of paths

Under the assumptions of §4, state and check a counterexample to the claim: two processes with the same finite-dimensional laws have the same paths.

Solution

Take \(B\) with continuous paths and \(\tilde B\) as in §4. The processes have the same finite-dimensional laws, because each finite set of times avoids the outcome \(U\) with probability one. Their paths differ at \(U\), where \(\tilde B_U=B_U+1\). So the claim fails.

R3 · The continuity event for \(B\) and \(\tilde B\)

Under the assumptions of §4, find the probability of the event that the path is continuous, for \(B\) and for \(\tilde B\).

Solution

For \(B\), the path is continuous on every outcome, so the event is the whole space and has probability 1. For \(\tilde B\), the path is discontinuous at \(U(\omega)\) on every outcome \(\omega\), so the event contains no outcome and has probability 0. The two values differ, which shows that the continuity event is not a function of the finite-dimensional laws.

  8. For a standard Brownian motion, what is \(\Var(3B_1-B_2)\), assuming A1(a)?

    The variance is \(9\cdot 1+2-6\cdot\min(1,2)=9+2-6=5\) (Problem M3).

  10. For a standard Brownian motion, what is \(\E[B_3^2]\)?

    \(B_3\) has mean 0, so \(\E[B_3^2]=\Var(B_3)=3\) by (D2).
