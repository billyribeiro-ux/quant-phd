Quadratic Variation

  • Uses: Brownian motion as defined in Lesson 1; the normal distribution, variance, expectation and independence from M4
  • Introduces: quadratic variation (defined in §1)
  • Ttheorem, proved on the page
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

The sums of squared increments of Brownian motion do not vanish under refinement, unlike those of a smooth function. Each squared increment of \(B\) has mean equal to the length of its interval, so the sum of squared increments over a partition (defined in §1) has mean equal to the elapsed time, however fine the partition. For the smooth function \(s \mapsto s^2\) the same sums shrink to zero. This lesson proves both statements with explicit constants. The limit holds in mean square, that is, the expected squared error tends to zero under Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2 (stated below, in §1). Lab 1 simulates Brownian increments on partitions into \(2^k\) equal steps and prints the sample means with standard errors.

1Quadratic variation along partitions

Let \(0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_N = t\) be a partition \(\mathcal P\) of \([0,t]\). Its mesh is \(|\mathcal P| = \max_k (t_{k+1} - t_k)\), the length of its longest interval. Write \(\Delta_k = t_{k+1} - t_k\) for the length of the \(k\)-th interval and \(\Delta B_k = B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k}\) for the increment of \(B\) over it. The partition is finite and fixed. By Lesson 1, \(B_0 = 0\), each \(\Delta B_k\) is normal with mean zero and variance \(\Delta_k\), and increments over disjoint intervals are independent. A partition of \([0,t]\) into \(N=2^k\) equal steps, for an integer \(k\ge 0\), is a dyadic partition of depth \(k\).

Definition 1.1 (quadratic variation along a partition)

For a partition \(\mathcal P\) of \([0,t]\), the sum \(Q(\mathcal P) = \sum_k (\Delta B_k)^2\) of squared increments is the quadratic variation of \(B\) along \(\mathcal P\). The quadratic variation of \(B\) on \([0,t]\) is the limit of \(Q(\mathcal P_n)\) along a sequence of partitions with mesh tending to zero, in mean square. Theorem 2.3 shows that this limit exists and equals \(t\), under Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2 (stated below).

The sum depends on the partition, so a limit needs a condition on how the partitions are refined. The condition is the mesh condition below.

Assumption 3.1 (mesh tends to zero)

\((\mathcal P_n)\) is a sequence of partitions of \([0,t]\) with \(|\mathcal P_n| \to 0\).

Assumption 3.2 (independence and additivity of variance)

(a) If \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) are independent, then so are \(g_1(X_1), \dots, g_N(X_N)\) for measurable functions \(g_k\), in particular \(X_1^2, \dots, X_N^2\). (b) If \(U_1, \dots, U_N\) are independent with finite second moments, then \(\Var(U_1 + \dots + U_N) = \sum_k \Var(U_k)\). The page does not prove these facts. Each result that uses them says "Assuming Assumption 3.2".

2The mean-square limit

Lemma 2.1 (moments of the standard normal)

Let \(Z\) have the standard normal law, with density \(\varphi(z) = e^{-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\). Then \(\E[Z^2] = 1\) and \(\E[Z^4] = 3\).

Proof

The standard normal has variance 1, so \(\E[Z^2] = 1\). Differentiating gives \(\varphi'(z) = -z\,\varphi(z)\), so \(z\,\varphi(z) = -\varphi'(z)\). For \(R > 0\), integrate by parts on \([-R, R]\):

\[\int_{-R}^{R} z^4 \varphi(z)\,dz = \int_{-R}^{R} z^3 \bigl(z\varphi(z)\bigr)\,dz = -\int_{-R}^{R} z^3 \varphi'(z)\,dz = -\bigl[z^3\varphi(z)\bigr]_{-R}^{R} + \int_{-R}^{R} 3z^2 \varphi(z)\,dz.\]

The boundary term \(-\bigl[z^3\varphi(z)\bigr]_{-R}^{R} = -2R^3\varphi(R)\) tends to zero as \(R \to \infty\), because \(e^{-R^2/2}\) decays faster than any power of \(R\) grows. The integrals converge absolutely, so letting \(R \to \infty\) gives \(\E[Z^4] = 3\,\E[Z^2] = 3\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 2.2 (moments of one increment)

Let \(X\) be normal with mean zero and variance \(\Delta > 0\). Then \(\E[X^2] = \Delta\), \(\E[X^4] = 3\Delta^2\), and \(\Var(X^2) = 2\Delta^2\).

Proof

Write \(X = \sqrt{\Delta}\,Z\) with \(Z\) standard normal. Then \(X^2 = \Delta Z^2\), so \(\E[X^2] = \Delta\,\E[Z^2] = \Delta\). Also \(X^4 = \Delta^2 Z^4\), so \(\E[X^4] = 3\Delta^2\) by Lemma 2.1. Hence \(\Var(X^2) = \E[X^4] - (\E[X^2])^2 = 3\Delta^2 - \Delta^2 = 2\Delta^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 2.3 (mean-square limit of the sums of squared increments)
  1. (H1) \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion (Lesson 1, Definition 1.1), and \(\mathcal P\) is a partition of \([0,t]\) with lengths \(\Delta_k\) and increments \(\Delta B_k\), as in §1.
  2. (H2) Assuming Assumption 3.2.

Conclusion: \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)] = t\), and \(\E\bigl[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2\bigr] = 2\sum_k \Delta_k^2 \le 2\,t\,|\mathcal P|\). Assuming also Assumption 3.1, for every sequence \((\mathcal P_n)\) of partitions of \([0,t]\) the right-hand side tends to zero, so \(Q(\mathcal P_n) \to t\) in mean square.

Proof

Mean. By Lemma 2.2 with \(\Delta = \Delta_k\), \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = \Delta_k\). Summing and telescoping, \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)] = \sum_k \Delta_k = \sum_k (t_{k+1} - t_k) = t_N - t_0 = t\).

Independence and variance. The increments \(X_k = \Delta B_k\) are independent, by Lesson 1. By Assumption 3.2(a), the squares \(U_k = X_k^2\) are independent. Each has finite second moment, since \(\E[U_k^2] = 3\Delta_k^2\) by Lemma 2.2. By Assumption 3.2(b) and Lemma 2.2,

\[\Var(Q(\mathcal P)) = \sum_k \Var(U_k) = \sum_k 2\Delta_k^2.\]

Error bound. Since \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)] = t\), \(\E[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2] = \Var(Q(\mathcal P)) = 2\sum_k \Delta_k^2\). Also \(\sum_k \Delta_k^2 \le \bigl(\max_k \Delta_k\bigr)\sum_k \Delta_k = |\mathcal P|\,t\). So \(\E[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2] \le 2t|\mathcal P|\). Under Assumption 3.1, \(|\mathcal P_n| \to 0\), so the bound tends to zero. \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 2.3 is a mean-square statement. It says nothing about the limit of \(Q(\mathcal P_n)\) for a single path, and this page does not claim an almost-sure limit. The limit \(t\) is a constant: the random sums converge to a deterministic value in mean square, and, under Assumption 3.2, the mean-square error is exactly \(2\sum_k \Delta_k^2\).

Proposition 2.4 (the same sums for \(s \mapsto s^2\))
  1. (H1) \(f(s) = s^2\), and \(\mathcal P\) is a partition of \([0,H]\), with \(H\) the endpoint. The endpoint is written \(H\) here and in Lab 1, because the program's notation reserves \(T\) for the number of return observations.

Conclusion: (a) \(\sum_k \bigl(f(t_{k+1}) - f(t_k)\bigr)^2 \le 4H^3\,|\mathcal P|\). (b) If \(\mathcal P\) splits \([0,H]\) into \(N\) equal steps, the sum equals \(H^4(4N^2 - 1)/(3N^3)\) exactly.

Proof

(a) For \(0 \le a < b \le H\), \(b^2 - a^2 = (b - a)(b + a)\) with \(0 \le b + a \le 2H\). So \((b^2 - a^2)^2 \le 4H^2 (b - a)^2\). Summing over the intervals and using \(\sum_k \Delta_k^2 \le |\mathcal P|\,H\) from the proof of Theorem 2.3 gives \(\sum_k (f(t_{k+1}) - f(t_k))^2 \le 4H^2 \cdot |\mathcal P|\,H = 4H^3|\mathcal P|\).

(b) For the equal partition, \(t_j = Hj/N\), so \(t_{j+1}^2 - t_j^2 = H^2(2j+1)/N^2\). The sum of squares is \(H^4 S(N)/N^4\), where \(S(N) = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} (2j+1)^2\). The claim \(S(N) = N(4N^2 - 1)/3\) holds for \(N = 1\), since \(S(1) = 1 = 3/3\). If it holds for \(N\), then \(S(N+1) = N(4N^2 - 1)/3 + (2N+1)^2 = (2N+1)(N+1)(2N+3)/3\), which equals \((N+1)\bigl(4(N+1)^2 - 1\bigr)/3\). By induction it holds for every \(N \ge 1\). Hence the sum is \(H^4 N(4N^2 - 1)/(3N^4) = H^4(4N^2 - 1)/(3N^3)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Squared increments of simulated Brownian paths, and the same sums for \(s^2\) (SIMULATED)

Predicted before running. The lab simulates 400 paths on \([0,H]\) for \(H = 1\) and \(H = 2\), each split into \(N = 2^k\) equal steps for \(k\) from 0 to 7, with a fixed seed. The mean of the sum of squared increments should be near \(H\), by Theorem 2.3. The theoretical standard error of the mean is \(H\sqrt{2/N}/20\), because \(\Var(Q) = 2H^2/N\) assuming Assumption 3.2 and the sample has 400 paths. Every \(z = (\text{mean} - H)/\text{se}\) should lie within \(\pm 3\). The exact sums for \(s^2\) should equal \(H^4(4N^2 - 1)/(3N^3)\) at every level.

"""SC1 lab L03: squared increments of a simulated Brownian path over dyadic partitions, and the same sums for t squared.

SIMULATED. The Brownian part draws Gaussian increments with variance equal to the step length, from
random.Random(SEED), so every run prints the same lines. For each horizon H and each dyadic partition of [0, H] into
N = 2**k steps, k = 0 to 7, the lab simulates PATHS independent paths and records the sum of squared increments of
each path. It prints the sample mean, the standard error (sample standard deviation divided by the square root of
PATHS, with divisor PATHS - 1) and z = (mean - H) / se.

The t-squared part is exact. It sums the squared increments of f(s) = s * s over the same partition points H * j / N,
using fractions.Fraction, so no randomness enters it. The lab checks each exact sum against the closed form
H**4 * (4 N**2 - 1) / (3 N**3).

A simulation is a finite sample on finitely many partitions. It illustrates the limit of Lesson 3; it does not verify
any statement about all partitions or about almost-sure behaviour.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import pairwise

SEED = 20260503
PATHS = 400
LEVELS = range(0, 8)
HORIZONS = (1.0, 2.0)
EXACT_HORIZONS = (Fraction(1), Fraction(2))


def squared_increment_sums(horizon: float, steps: int, paths: int, rng: random.Random) -> list[float]:
    """For each of `paths` simulated paths on [0, horizon], the sum of squared increments over `steps` equal steps.

    Each increment is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance horizon / steps, drawn from `rng`.
    """
    step_sd = math.sqrt(horizon / steps)
    sums: list[float] = []
    for _ in range(paths):
        total = 0.0
        for _ in range(steps):
            increment = rng.gauss(0.0, step_sd)
            total += increment * increment
        sums.append(total)
    return sums


def mean_and_standard_error(values: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """The sample mean and the standard error sd / sqrt(n), with sd computed by divisor n - 1."""
    n = len(values)
    mean = sum(values) / n
    variance = sum((v - mean) ** 2 for v in values) / (n - 1)
    return mean, math.sqrt(variance / n)


def t_squared_sum(horizon: Fraction, steps: int) -> Fraction:
    """The sum of squared increments of f(s) = s * s over the equal partition of [0, horizon] into `steps` pieces."""
    points = [horizon * j / steps for j in range(steps + 1)]
    return sum(((b * b - a * a) ** 2 for a, b in pairwise(points)), Fraction(0))


def t_squared_closed_form(horizon: Fraction, steps: int) -> Fraction:
    """The closed form H**4 * (4 N**2 - 1) / (3 N**3) for the sum computed by t_squared_sum."""
    return horizon**4 * (4 * steps * steps - 1) / (3 * steps**3)


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    for horizon in HORIZONS:
        print(f"H = {horizon:g}: mean of the sum of squared increments over {PATHS} simulated paths")
        for k in LEVELS:
            steps = 2**k
            mean, se = mean_and_standard_error(squared_increment_sums(horizon, steps, PATHS, rng))
            z = (mean - horizon) / se
            print(f"  N = {steps:3d} steps: mean {mean:.4f}, se {se:.4f}, z = {z:.2f}")
    print("t squared, exact sums over the same partitions of [0, H]:")
    for horizon_exact in EXACT_HORIZONS:
        for k in LEVELS:
            steps = 2**k
            exact = t_squared_sum(horizon_exact, steps)
            matches = exact == t_squared_closed_form(horizon_exact, steps)
            print(f"  H = {horizon_exact}, N = {steps:3d}: sum = {float(exact):.6f}, closed form matches: {matches}")

3Where the canon breaks

  • The mean-square limit holds under a fourth-moment bound, without the Gaussian law. Let the increments be independent with mean zero, variance \(\Delta_k\), and \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^4] \le c\,\Delta_k^2\) for a constant \(c\). Then \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = \Delta_k\) and \(\Var((\Delta B_k)^2) \le c\,\Delta_k^2\), so the proof of Theorem 2.3 gives \(\E[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2] \le c\,t\,|\mathcal P|\), assuming Assumption 3.2. The constant changes from 2 to \(c\). The proof uses the second and fourth moments of each increment, not the Gaussian law.
  • Independence is not optional. Take, for each \(N\), the \(N\) increments of the equal partition of \([0,t]\) all equal to one variable \(X_N\) with law \(\Normal(0,t/N)\). Then \(Q = N X_N^2\), so \(\E[Q] = t\) and \(\Var(Q) = N^2 \cdot 2(t/N)^2 = 2t^2\) for every \(N\). The mesh tends to zero, but the variance does not, so the mean-square conclusion of Theorem 2.3 fails for this family. The family is not a standard Brownian motion, so the theorem does not apply to it, and the example shows that the independence in Assumption 3.2 cannot be dropped.
  • The page does not state an almost-sure limit. Lab 1 is a finite sample on finitely many partitions. It can show that the sums are consistent with \(t\) at these sizes. It cannot establish a pathwise limit, and no sentence on this page claims one.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A smooth function has the same quadratic variation as Brownian motion.”

    For \(f(s) = s^2\), Proposition 2.4(a) gives \(\sum_k (f(t_{k+1}) - f(t_k))^2 \le 4H^3|\mathcal P|\), which tends to zero as the mesh does. For \(H = 2\) and \(N = 128\), Lab 1 prints the exact sum 0.166664, while the Brownian sum over the same partition has mean \(H = 2\) (Theorem 2.3). For \(s \mapsto s^2\) the sums vanish in the limit, and for Brownian motion their mean is the elapsed time for every partition, with mean-square error tending to zero (Theorem 2.3).

  2. “The total absolute variation of Brownian motion converges as the partition is refined, as the length of a smooth curve does.”

    For \(N\) equal steps of \([0,t]\), the increments are normal with variance \(t/N\). Using \(\E|Z| = 2\varphi(0) = \sqrt{2/\pi}\), which follows from \(z\varphi(z) = -\varphi'(z)\) as in Problem P3, the expected sum of absolute increments is \(N\sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt{t/N} = \sqrt{2Nt/\pi}\). This grows without bound in \(N\). The absolute sum therefore has no finite limit in expectation, whereas Theorem 2.3 controls the squared sum.

  3. “The quadratic variation of a Brownian path is a random number that changes from path to path.”

    Theorem 2.3 gives the mean-square limit \(t\), which is a constant. The random sums \(Q(\mathcal P_n)\) fluctuate around \(t\) with mean-square error at most \(2t|\mathcal P_n|\), and that bound tends to zero. The randomness of the sums vanishes in mean square as the mesh does. The page makes no pathwise claim about a single path.

Mechanics

M1 · A two-step partition

Let \(t = 1\) and let \(\mathcal P\) be the partition \(0 < 1/2 < 1\). Compute \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)]\) and \(\Var(Q(\mathcal P))\) exactly, assuming Assumption 3.2.

Solution

Here \(\Delta_1 = \Delta_2 = 1/2\). By Lemma 2.2, each \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = 1/2\), so \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)] = 1\). Also \(\Var((\Delta B_k)^2) = 2(1/2)^2 = 1/2\). By Assumption 3.2(a) the two squares are independent, and by 3.2(b) the variance of their sum is the sum of the variances, so \(\Var(Q(\mathcal P)) = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1\). This equals \(2\sum_k \Delta_k^2 = 2 \cdot 1/2 = 1\).

M2 · Moments of a normal with variance 4

Let \(X\) be normal with mean zero and variance 4. Compute \(\E[X^2]\), \(\E[X^4]\) and \(\Var(X^2)\).

Solution

Here \(\Delta = 4\) in Lemma 2.2. So \(\E[X^2] = 4\), \(\E[X^4] = 3 \cdot 4^2 = 48\), and \(\Var(X^2) = 2 \cdot 4^2 = 32\). As a check, \(\E[X^4] - (\E[X^2])^2 = 48 - 16 = 32\).

M3 · A smooth function over equal steps

Let \(f(s) = s\) and let \(\mathcal P\) be the equal partition of \([0,1]\) into \(N\) steps. Compute \(\sum_k \bigl(f(t_{k+1}) - f(t_k)\bigr)^2\) exactly, and find its limit as \(N\) grows.

Solution

Each increment of \(f\) is \(1/N\), so each squared increment is \(1/N^2\). There are \(N\) of them, so the sum is \(N \cdot 1/N^2 = 1/N\). As \(N\) grows the sum tends to 0.

Proof

P1 · The mean and variance of a sum of squared independent normals

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) be independent, with \(X_k\) normal with mean zero and variance \(\Delta_k\), and let \(S = \sum_k X_k^2\). Assuming Assumption 3.2, prove \(\E[S] = \sum_k \Delta_k\) and \(\Var(S) = 2\sum_k \Delta_k^2\), and state each assumption used.

Solution

By Lemma 2.2, \(\E[X_k^2] = \Delta_k\), so \(\E[S] = \sum_k \Delta_k\) by linearity of expectation. By Assumption 3.2(a), the squares \(X_1^2, \dots, X_N^2\) are independent, and each has finite second moment by Lemma 2.2. By Assumption 3.2(b), \(\Var(S) = \sum_k \Var(X_k^2)\). Lemma 2.2 gives \(\Var(X_k^2) = 2\Delta_k^2\), so \(\Var(S) = 2\sum_k \Delta_k^2\). The assumptions used are Lemma 2.2, Lemma 2.1 through it, and Assumption 3.2 parts (a) and (b).

P2 · A bound on squared differences of \(s^2\)

Prove that \((b^2 - a^2)^2 \le 4H^2(b - a)^2\) for \(0 \le a < b \le H\).

Solution

Factor \(b^2 - a^2 = (b - a)(b + a)\). Since \(0 \le a < b \le H\), we have \(0 \le b + a \le 2H\). Squaring the factored form gives \((b^2 - a^2)^2 = (b - a)^2 (b + a)^2 \le (b - a)^2 \cdot 4H^2\).

P3 · The mean of the absolute value of a standard normal

Let \(Z\) be standard normal. Prove that \(\E|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}\).

Solution

The density is even, so \(\E|Z| = 2\int_0^\infty z\,\varphi(z)\,dz\). Using \(z\varphi(z) = -\varphi'(z)\), \(\int_0^R z\varphi(z)\,dz = \varphi(0) - \varphi(R)\). Letting \(R \to \infty\) and using \(\varphi(R) \to 0\) gives \(\int_0^\infty z\varphi(z)\,dz = \varphi(0) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}\). Hence \(\E|Z| = 2/\sqrt{2\pi} = \sqrt{2/\pi}\).

Empirical

E1 · The largest z value

Lab 1 prints 16 values of \(z\). Which values have \(|z| > 2\), and what does a single such value show?

Solution

Only one value exceeds 2 in absolute value: \(z = 2.39\), at \(H = 1\) and \(N = 2\). All 16 values lie within \(\pm 3\), which is consistent with mean \(H\). A single seeded run cannot tell chance from a bias of that size, so the z values check one sample, not the theorem.

E2 · Recomputing an exact sum

Lab 1 prints a sum of 2.656250 for \(H = 2\) and \(N = 8\). Recompute it from \(H^4(4N^2 - 1)/(3N^3)\).

Solution

Here \(H^4 = 16\), \(4N^2 - 1 = 255\) and \(3N^3 = 1536\). So the sum is \(16 \cdot 255/1536 = 4080/1536 = 2.65625\), which is the printed value, to six decimal places.

E3 · A theoretical standard error

Lab 1 prints a standard error of 0.0125 for \(H = 2\) and \(N = 128\). Compute the theoretical value \(H\sqrt{2/N}/20\) and compare.

Solution

\(\sqrt{2/128} = \sqrt{1/64} = 1/8\), so \(H\sqrt{2/N} = 2/8 = 1/4\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{400} = 20\) gives \(1/80 = 0.0125\). The printed sample standard error agrees with the theoretical value at four decimal places for this seed.

Research

R1 · The equal partition in closed form

For the equal partition of \([0,t]\) into \(N\) steps, prove \(\E[Q] = t\) and \(\E[(Q - t)^2] = 2t^2/N\), assuming Assumption 3.2.

Solution

Each \(\Delta_k = t/N\), so \(\E[Q] = N \cdot t/N = t\) by Theorem 2.3. The mean-square error is \(2\sum_k \Delta_k^2 = 2N(t/N)^2 = 2t^2/N\).

R2 · The least constant in the mean-square bound

Show that \(c = 2\) is the least constant with \(\E[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2] \le c\,t\,|\mathcal P|\) for every partition \(\mathcal P\) of \([0,t]\), assuming Assumption 3.2.

Solution

Theorem 2.3 shows that \(c = 2\) works. For the equal partition into \(N\) steps, \(|\mathcal P| = t/N\) and \(\sum_k \Delta_k^2 = t^2/N = t|\mathcal P|\), so \(\E[(Q - t)^2] = 2t|\mathcal P|\) exactly. A constant \(c < 2\) fails at this partition, because \(c\,t|\mathcal P| < 2t|\mathcal P|\) for \(t > 0\).

R3 · Dependent increments

For each \(N\), let all \(N\) increments of the equal partition of \([0,t]\) equal one variable \(X_N\) with law \(\Normal(0,t/N)\). Compute \(\E[Q]\) and \(\Var(Q)\), and name the hypothesis of Theorem 2.3 that fails.

Solution

Here \(Q = N X_N^2\). So \(\E[Q] = N \cdot t/N = t\), and \(\Var(Q) = N^2 \cdot 2(t/N)^2 = 2t^2\) for every \(N\), by Lemma 2.2. The mesh \(t/N\) tends to zero, so Assumption 3.1 holds, but the variance does not tend to zero. Hypothesis (H1) fails, because the increments are not independent as (D3) requires, so the family is not a standard Brownian motion. (H2) does not apply here, because Assumption 3.2 is stated for independent variables, and these squares are not independent.

7Retrieval quiz

Answer from memory, then check. Finishing the quiz adds this lesson's cards to your review deck.

  1. For a partition \(\mathcal P\) of \([0,t]\), what is \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)]\)?

    Options

    Each \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = \Delta_k\) by Lemma 2.2, and the \(\Delta_k\) add up to \(t\) (Theorem 2.3, mean step).

  2. If \(X\) is normal with mean zero and variance 4, what is \(\E[X^4]\)?

    Lemma 2.2 gives \(\E[X^4] = 3\Delta^2 = 3 \cdot 16 = 48\) with \(\Delta = 4\) (Problem M2).

  3. What is \(\E[Z^4]\) for a standard normal \(Z\)?

    Options

    Lemma 2.1: integration by parts gives \(\E[Z^4] = 3\,\E[Z^2] = 3\).

  4. For \(H = 1\) and \(N = 2\) equal steps, what is the exact sum of squared increments of \(s \mapsto s^2\)?

    Proposition 2.4(b): \(1^4 (4 \cdot 4 - 1)/(3 \cdot 8) = 15/24 = 0.625\). Lab 1 prints 0.625000.

  5. Which assumption, added in the conclusion of Theorem 2.3, is the mesh condition?

    Options

    Assumption 3.1 is the mesh condition. The conclusion of Theorem 2.3 adds it ("Assuming also Assumption 3.1"). Assumption 3.2 is the independence and additivity fact, which is hypothesis (H2) of Theorem 2.3.

  6. Is the quadratic variation of Brownian motion on \([0,t]\) a random number?

    Options

    Theorem 2.3: the sums converge to \(t\) in mean square, and the limit is a constant. The page makes no pathwise claim.

  7. For \(H = 2\) and \(N = 8\) equal steps, what is the exact sum of squared increments of \(s \mapsto s^2\)?

    Proposition 2.4(b): \(2^4 \cdot 255/(3 \cdot 512) = 4080/1536 = 2.65625\) (Problem E2).

  8. Why does the sum of squared increments of \(s \mapsto s^2\) tend to zero as the mesh shrinks?

    Options

    Proposition 2.4(a): the sum is at most \(4H^3|\mathcal P|\), which tends to zero with the mesh.

  9. For \(N\) equal steps of \([0,t]\), what is the expected sum of absolute increments of Brownian motion?

    Options

    Each increment has mean absolute value \(\sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt{t/N}\), by Problem P3, so the sum over \(N\) steps has expectation \(\sqrt{2Nt/\pi}\).

  10. What does Lab 1 check against simulated values?

    Options

    Lab 1 is a seeded finite sample. It checks consistency with the theorem and the exact identity for \(s^2\), and it does not check any pathwise limit.
