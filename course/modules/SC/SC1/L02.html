Constructing Brownian Motion

Definition 1.1 says what the finite-dimensional laws of a Brownian motion must be. It does not show that a process with those laws and continuous paths exists. This lesson gives a construction on \([0,1]\). It starts from independent standard normal coefficients, adds the piecewise linear bumps of Definition 2.1 level by level, and shows that the partial sums of Definition 2.1 converge uniformly with probability one, so the limit has continuous paths. The identification of the laws at all times is not proved here. It is stated as Assumption A2, and the sketch names the step it skips.

1The setting and the hat functions

Assume the probability space carries a family of random variables \(\xi_0\) and \(\xi_{m,j}\), for \(m\ge 1\) and \(1\le j\le 2^{m-1}\), with the following property.

Hypothesis H1 (the coefficients)

The family \(\xi_0\) and \(\xi_{m,j}\) is mutually independent, and each member has the standard normal law \(\Normal(0,1)\). The family is countable, so it can be enumerated as a sequence.

Definition 2.1 (hats and partial sums)

For \(m\ge 1\) and \(1\le j\le 2^{m-1}\), the hat function of level \(m\) and index \(j\) is \[\Lambda_{m,j}(t)=\max\{0,\;1-2^{m}\,|t-(2j-1)2^{-m}|\}, \qquad t\in[0,1].\] For \(M\ge 0\), the partial sum at level \(M\) is \[S_M(t)=\xi_0\,t+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\ \sum_{j=1}^{2^{m-1}}\xi_{m,j}\,2^{-(m+1)/2}\,\Lambda_{m,j}(t).\] The construction of Brownian motion is the sequence of these partial sums, and its limit \(S(t)=\lim_M S_M(t)\), which Proposition 2.4 shows exists on an event of probability one.

Each \(\Lambda_{m,j}\) has its peak 1 at the centre \((2j-1)2^{-m}\) and is zero outside the open interval of radius \(2^{-m}\) around that centre. The level-\(m\) term of the sum is added to the level-\((m-1)\) approximation by midpoint refinement, which the proof of Lemma 2.3 makes precise. The scale \(2^{-(m+1)/2}\) is the one that gives the increments the right variances, as Lemma 2.3 shows. A dyadic time of depth \(n\) is a point \(k2^{-n}\) of \([0,1]\) with \(k\) an integer, and a dyadic partition of depth \(n\) cuts \([0,1]\) into \(2^n\) pieces of length \(2^{-n}\).

Lemma 2.2 (structure of the hats)
  1. (H1) Definition 2.1, with \(m\ge 1\), \(1\le j\le 2^{m-1}\) and \(n\ge 0\).

Conclusion. (a) Each \(\Lambda_{m,j}\) is continuous, takes values in \([0,1]\), equals 1 at its centre, is linear between the points of \(2^{-m}\mathbb Z\), and vanishes outside the open interval of radius \(2^{-m}\) about its centre. (b) At each \(t\), at most one of the \(\Lambda_{m,j}\), \(j\) varying, is non-zero. (c) If \(n<m\), then \(\Lambda_{m,j}(k2^{-n})=0\) for every integer \(k\) with \(k2^{-n}\in[0,1]\). (d) For \(M\ge n\) and \(0\le k\le 2^n\), \(S_M(k2^{-n})=S_n(k2^{-n})\). (e) \(S_n\) is linear on each interval \([k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}]\).

Proof

(a) The function \(\Lambda_{m,j}\) is the maximum of zero and an affine function of \(t\) on each side of its centre, so it is continuous and piecewise linear, with breakpoints at \((2j-2)2^{-m}\), \((2j-1)2^{-m}\) and \(2j\cdot 2^{-m}\). Its values lie in \([0,1]\) because the argument of the maximum is at most 1, and the value at the centre is 1. The value is positive only when \(|t-(2j-1)2^{-m}|<2^{-m}\).

(b) Two centres of the level-\(m\) hats differ by at least \(2\cdot 2^{-m}\), so their open intervals of radius \(2^{-m}\) are disjoint. A point lies in at most one of them.

(c) Let \(t=k2^{-n}\) with \(n<m\). Then \(t=(k\,2^{m-n})\,2^{-m}\), and \(k\,2^{m-n}\) is an even integer. Write it as \(2i\). The distance from \(t\) to the centre is \(|2i-(2j-1)|\,2^{-m}\), and \(2i-(2j-1)\) is an odd integer, so its absolute value is at least 1. Hence \(|t-(2j-1)2^{-m}|\ge 2^{-m}\), and \(\Lambda_{m,j}(t)=0\) by (a).

(d) The difference \(S_M-S_n\) is the sum of the level-\(m\) terms for \(n<m\le M\). Each of them vanishes at \(k2^{-n}\) by (c), so the difference is zero there.

(e) The linear term \(\xi_0 t\) is linear. A level-\(m\) hat with \(m\le n\) has its breakpoints at multiples of \(2^{-m}\), and each such multiple is an integer multiple of \(2^{-n}\) because \(2^{-m}=2^{n-m}\,2^{-n}\). So each hat is linear on every interval of the level-\(n\) grid. \(\blacksquare\)

2Dyadic laws and the Gaussian tail

Lemma 2.1 (Gaussian tail)
  1. (H1) \(\eta\) has the law \(\Normal(0,1)\), and \(\lambda\ge 0\).

Conclusion: \(\Prob(|\eta|>\lambda)\le 2e^{-\lambda^2/2}\).

Proof

Let \(\varphi\) be the standard normal density. For real \(\theta\), completing the square gives \(\theta x-x^2/2=-(x-\theta)^2/2+\theta^2/2\), so \[\E e^{\theta\eta}=\int e^{\theta x}\varphi(x)\,dx=e^{\theta^2/2}\int \varphi(x-\theta)\,dx=e^{\theta^2/2},\] because \(x\mapsto\varphi(x-\theta)\) is the \(\Normal(\theta,1)\) density and integrates to 1.

Let \(\theta\ge 0\). Pointwise, \(\indic\{\eta>\lambda\}\le e^{\theta(\eta-\lambda)}\): if \(\eta>\lambda\), the right side is at least 1, and otherwise the left side is 0. Taking expectations (monotonicity of the integral, M6) gives \(\Prob(\eta>\lambda)\le e^{-\theta\lambda}e^{\theta^2/2}\). Choosing \(\theta=\lambda\) gives \(\Prob(\eta>\lambda)\le e^{-\lambda^2/2}\).

Since \(\varphi\) is symmetric, \(\Prob(\eta<-\lambda)=\Prob(\eta>\lambda)\), and \(\{|\eta|>\lambda\}\) is the disjoint union of \(\{\eta>\lambda\}\) and \(\{\eta<-\lambda\}\). Adding the two bounds gives the claim. \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 2.3 (dyadic increments are independent normals)
  1. (H1) The family of Hypothesis H1.
  2. (H2) Assuming A1(b), A1(c) and A1(e).

Conclusion: for every \(n\ge 0\), the increments \(D^{(n)}_k=S_n(k2^{-n})-S_n((k-1)2^{-n})\), \(1\le k\le 2^n\), are mutually independent, each with law \(\Normal(0,2^{-n})\). Consequently, for dyadic times \(0=t_0<t_1<\dots<t_r\le 1\), the increments \(S(t_i)-S(t_{i-1})\), where \(S(t)=\lim_M S_M(t)\), are independent with laws \(\Normal(0,\,t_i-t_{i-1})\).

Proof

Base, \(n=0\). Here \(S_0(t)=\xi_0 t\), so \(D^{(0)}_1=\xi_0\), which is \(\Normal(0,1)\) by H1.

Step. Assume the claim for \(n\). Write \(D_j=D^{(n)}_j\), \(\eta_j=\xi_{n+1,j}\) and \(c=2^{-(n+2)/2}\). The level-\((n+1)\) grid consists of the level-\(n\) points, at even indices, and the midpoints \(c_j=(2j-1)2^{-(n+1)}\), at odd indices. Write \(X_k=S_n(k2^{-n})\).

By Lemma 2.2(d), \(S_{n+1}\) agrees with \(S_n\) at the level-\(n\) points. At the midpoint \(c_j\), the level-\((n+1)\) hat \(\Lambda_{n+1,j}\) equals 1, and every other level-\((n+1)\) hat vanishes by Lemma 2.2(b). So \(S_{n+1}(c_j)=S_n(c_j)+c\,\eta_j\). By Lemma 2.2(e), \(S_n\) is linear on \([(j-1)2^{-n},j2^{-n}]\), so \(S_n(c_j)=(X_{j-1}+X_j)/2\). Therefore \[S_{n+1}(c_j)=\tfrac12(X_{j-1}+X_j)+c\,\eta_j,\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{n+1}\bigl((2j-1)2^{-(n+1)}\bigr)-X_{j-1}=\tfrac12 D_j+c\,\eta_j.\] The level-\((n+1)\) increments over the two halves of the \(j\)th level-\(n\) interval are therefore \[U_j=\tfrac12 D_j+c\,\eta_j,\qquad V_j=\tfrac12 D_j-c\,\eta_j.\]

Each of \(U_j\) and \(V_j\) is a linear combination of the independent normal variables \(D_j\) and \(\eta_j\), with variance \(\tfrac14\cdot 2^{-n}+c^2=\tfrac14\cdot 2^{-n}+2^{-(n+2)}=2^{-(n+1)}\), so each is normal with that variance, by A1(b).

Independence. The variables \(D_1,\dots,D_{2^n}\) are functions of the coefficients \(\xi_0\) and \(\xi_{m,j}\) with \(m\le n\), and the \(\eta_j=\xi_{n+1,j}\) are functions of disjoint coefficients. By H1 and A1(e), the two groups are independent, and by the induction hypothesis the \(D_j\) are mutually independent. So, by A1(e) again, the \(2^{n+1}\) variables \(D_j,\eta_j\) are mutually independent. The pair \((U_j,V_j)\) is a function of \((D_j,\eta_j)\), and the pairs for different \(j\) are functions of disjoint blocks of this family. By A1(e), the pairs are mutually independent. Within a pair, \(U_j\) and \(V_j\) are jointly normal by A1(b), and their covariance is \(\tfrac14\cdot 2^{-n}-c^2=\tfrac14\cdot 2^{-n}-2^{-(n+2)}=0\). By A1(c) they are independent. So, by A1(e) applied to the blocks \(\{U_j,V_j\}\), each internally independent, the \(2^{n+1}\) increments \(U_j,V_j\) are mutually independent, each \(\Normal(0,2^{-(n+1)})\). This completes the step.

Consequence. Let the dyadic times all be level-\(N\) points, with \(N\) large enough. By Lemma 2.2(d), \(S(t)=S_N(t)\) for each such \(t\), because \(S_M(t)\) is constant for \(M\ge N\). Each increment \(S(t_i)-S(t_{i-1})\) is a sum of consecutive level-\(N\) increments, and, by A1(e), blocks of mutually independent variables give independent sums. By A1(b), each sum is normal with variance \(2^{-N}\) times the number of its level-\(N\) increments, which is \(t_i-t_{i-1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Uniform convergence and continuous paths

Proposition 2.4 (uniform convergence on an almost sure event)
  1. (H1) The family of Hypothesis H1.
  2. (H2) For \(m\ge 1\), let \(\lambda_m=\sqrt{4m\log 2}\), let \(E_m=\{\max_{1\le j\le 2^{m-1}}|\xi_{m,j}|\le\lambda_m\}\), let \(A_{m_0}=\bigcap_{m\ge m_0}E_m\), and let \(\Omega^\ast=\bigcup_{m_0\ge 1}A_{m_0}\).

Conclusion: (a) \(\Prob(\Omega^\ast)=1\). (b) For every outcome in \(\Omega^\ast\), the limit \(S(t)=\lim_M S_M(t)\) exists for every \(t\in[0,1]\), the convergence is uniform on \([0,1]\), and \(t\mapsto S(t)\) is continuous.

Proof

(a). By Lemma 2.1 and a union bound over the \(2^{m-1}\) coefficients of level \(m\), using \(2e^{-\lambda_m^2/2}=2\cdot 2^{-2m}\), we get \[\Prob(E_m^c)\le 2^{m-1}\cdot 2\,e^{-\lambda_m^2/2}=2^{m}\cdot 4^{-m}=2^{-m}.\] Hence \(\Prob(A_{m_0}^c)\le\sum_{m\ge m_0}2^{-m}=2^{1-m_0}\). The sets \(A_{m_0}\) increase with \(m_0\), so by continuity from below, \(\Prob(\Omega^\ast)=\lim_{m_0}\Prob(A_{m_0})\ge\lim_{m_0}(1-2^{1-m_0})=1\).

(b), step 1: a uniform bound. Let \(Y_m(t)=\sum_j\xi_{m,j}2^{-(m+1)/2}\Lambda_{m,j}(t)\). By Lemma 2.2(b) at most one term is non-zero at each \(t\), and \(0\le\Lambda_{m,j}\le 1\), so \(|Y_m(t)|\le\max_j|\xi_{m,j}|\,2^{-(m+1)/2}\). On \(A_{m_0}\), for every \(m\ge m_0\), \[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|Y_m(t)|\le M_m:=\lambda_m 2^{-(m+1)/2}=\sqrt{2\log 2}\,\sqrt m\,2^{-m/2}.\]

Step 2: the bounds are summable. Since \(\sqrt m\le m\), we have \(M_m\le\sqrt{2\log 2}\cdot m\,r^m\) with \(r=2^{-1/2}\in(0,1)\). For \(0\le r<1\), differentiating the geometric series gives \(\sum_{m\ge 1}m r^m=r/(1-r)^2\), which is finite. So \(\sum_m M_m<\infty\).

Step 3: uniform convergence. Fix an outcome in \(A_{m_0}\). For \(m_0\le M<N\), the triangle inequality and step 1 give \[\sup_{t}|S_N(t)-S_M(t)|\le\sum_{m=M+1}^{N}M_m\le\sum_{m>M}M_m,\] which tends to 0 as \(M\to\infty\). So \((S_M(t))_M\) is Cauchy for each \(t\), and by completeness of the reals (M5) it converges to a limit \(S(t)\). Letting \(N\to\infty\) in the displayed bound gives \(\sup_t|S(t)-S_M(t)|\le\sum_{m>M}M_m\) for every \(M\ge m_0\), so the convergence is uniform.

Step 4: continuity. Each \(S_M\) is continuous, being a finite sum of continuous functions. Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose \(M\ge m_0\) with \(\sup_t|S(t)-S_M(t)|<\varepsilon/3\), and then \(\delta>0\) with \(|S_M(t)-S_M(t_0)|<\varepsilon/3\) when \(|t-t_0|<\delta\). Then \(|S(t)-S(t_0)|<\varepsilon\) for those \(t\). So \(S\) is continuous on \([0,1]\). \(\blacksquare\)

"""SC1 lab L02: a seeded partial sum of hat functions on [0, 1], with exact checks on dyadic points.

SIMULATED. The coefficients are standard normal draws from random.Random(SEED). The dyadic count and the grid-supremum
line are identities that hold for every seed; the bound lines describe one seeded sample. Standard library only; no
network; nothing is read from a file. The hat functions are evaluated at dyadic points, which binary floats
represent exactly, so the dyadic checks compare exact values. Numbers are printed to six decimal places from
unrounded values.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random

SEED = 20260101
LEVELS = 8
GRID_DEPTH = 10


def hat(m: int, j: int, t: float) -> float:
    """The level-m hat max(0, 1 - 2**m |t - (2j - 1) 2**(-m)|), for m >= 1 and 1 <= j <= 2**(m-1).

    Its peak value 1 sits at the centre (2j - 1) 2**(-m), and it vanishes outside the open interval of radius 2**(-m).
    """
    centre = (2 * j - 1) / 2.0**m
    return max(0.0, 1.0 - 2.0**m * abs(t - centre))


def partial_sum(xi0: float, coeffs: list[list[float]], levels: int, t: float) -> float:
    """S_levels(t) = xi0 t + the sum over m = 1..levels and j of coeffs[m-1][j-1] 2**(-(m+1)/2) hat(m, j, t)."""
    total = xi0 * t
    for m in range(1, levels + 1):
        scale = 2 ** (-(m + 1) / 2)
        for j in range(1, 2 ** (m - 1) + 1):
            total += coeffs[m - 1][j - 1] * scale * hat(m, j, t)
    return total


def level_term_sup(coeffs_m: list[float], m: int) -> float:
    """The supremum over t in [0, 1] of |Y_m(t)|, where Y_m is the level-m term of the sum.

    At most one hat of level m is non-zero at any t, and each hat peaks at 1, so the supremum is the largest
    coefficient in absolute value times 2**(-(m+1)/2).
    """
    return max(abs(c) for c in coeffs_m) * 2 ** (-(m + 1) / 2)


def bound(m: int) -> float:
    """The bound lambda_m 2**(-(m+1)/2) with lambda_m = sqrt(4 m log 2), used in the proof of the page."""
    return math.sqrt(4 * m * math.log(2)) * 2 ** (-(m + 1) / 2)


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    xi0 = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
    coeffs = [[rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(2 ** (m - 1))] for m in range(1, LEVELS + 1)]

    fixed_cases = 0
    fixed_equal = 0
    for n in range(5):
        for k in range(2**n + 1):
            t = k / 2**n
            base = partial_sum(xi0, coeffs, n, t)
            for levels in range(n, LEVELS + 1):
                fixed_cases += 1
                fixed_equal += partial_sum(xi0, coeffs, levels, t) == base
    print(f"dyadic values fixed from level n on (n <= 4, n <= M <= {LEVELS}): {fixed_equal} of {fixed_cases} cases")

    grid = [i / 2**GRID_DEPTH for i in range(2**GRID_DEPTH + 1)]
    sup_agree = 0
    for m in range(1, LEVELS + 1):
        scale = 2 ** (-(m + 1) / 2)
        grid_sup = max(
            abs(sum(coeffs[m - 1][j - 1] * hat(m, j, t) for j in range(1, 2 ** (m - 1) + 1)) * scale) for t in grid
        )
        sup_agree += grid_sup == level_term_sup(coeffs[m - 1], m)
    print(f"grid supremum of each level term equals the formula for levels 1 to {LEVELS}: {sup_agree == LEVELS}")

    within = 0
    for m in range(1, LEVELS + 1):
        observed = level_term_sup(coeffs[m - 1], m)
        ok = observed <= bound(m)
        within += ok
        print(f"m = {m}: observed sup {observed:.6f}, bound {bound(m):.6f}, observed <= bound: {ok}")
    print(f"levels whose observed sup is within the bound: {within} of {LEVELS}")

    tail_grid_sup = max(abs(partial_sum(xi0, coeffs, LEVELS, t) - partial_sum(xi0, coeffs, 4, t)) for t in grid)
    tail_bound = sum(bound(m) for m in range(5, LEVELS + 1))
    print(f"grid supremum of |S_8 - S_4|: {tail_grid_sup:.6f}; sum of the bounds for levels 5 to 8: {tail_bound:.6f}")

Assumption A2 (the laws at all times)

For every finite set of times \(0\le t_0<t_1<\dots<t_r\le 1\), the increments \(S(t_i)-S(t_{i-1})\), \(1\le i\le r\), are independent, with laws \(\Normal(0,\,t_i-t_{i-1})\). Here \(S\) is the limit of Proposition 2.4, which is defined on \(\Omega^\ast\), an event of probability one.

Lemma 2.3 proves this for dyadic times. A2 is the extension to all times, and this page does not prove it.

Sketch, not a proof. For \(t\in[0,1]\), choose dyadic times \(t^{(k)}\) decreasing to \(t\), and similarly for \(s\). By Proposition 2.4(b), \(S(t^{(k)})\to S(t)\) on \(\Omega^\ast\). The increments \(S(t^{(k)})-S(s^{(k)})\) have the dyadic laws of Lemma 2.3, so their characteristic functions are \(e^{-u^2(t^{(k)}-s^{(k)})/2}\), the characteristic function of a normal law with that variance. These converge to \(e^{-u^2(t-s)/2}\). The step skipped: the passage of characteristic functions to the limit, which needs the convergence on \(\Omega^\ast\) of the random variables to be carried through the characteristic functions (a bounded-convergence argument this page does not state), and the joint version of the same argument for several increments at once, which is not written out.

Theorem 2.5 (a Brownian motion on [0, 1] with continuous paths)
  1. (H1) The family of Hypothesis H1.
  2. (H2) Assuming A2.

Conclusion: define \(B_t(\omega)=S(\omega,t)\) for \(\omega\in\Omega^\ast\) and \(B_t(\omega)=0\) otherwise, for \(t\in[0,1]\). Then \((B_t)_{t\in[0,1]}\) satisfies (D1)–(D3) of Definition 1.1 for times in \([0,1]\), and every path \(t\mapsto B_t(\omega)\) is continuous.

Proof

By Proposition 2.4(a), \(\Prob(\Omega^\ast)=1\), so \(B_t=S(t)\) almost surely for each \(t\), and the finite-dimensional laws of \(B\) are those of \(S\). (D1): each hat vanishes at 0, so \(S_M(0)=0\) for every \(M\) (Problem P2), hence \(S(0)=0\) on \(\Omega^\ast\), and \(B_0=0\) off \(\Omega^\ast\) by definition. So \(B_0=0\) for every outcome. (D2) and (D3): on \(\Omega^\ast\) the increments of \(B\) are the increments of \(S\). Assumption A2 gives independence and normal laws with the right variances for every finite set of times in \([0,1]\), which is (D2) and (D3). Continuity: on \(\Omega^\ast\), \(t\mapsto S(t)\) is continuous by Proposition 2.4(b), and off \(\Omega^\ast\) the path is identically zero. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.6 (from [0, 1] to [0, \(\infty\)))
  1. (H1) For each \(k\ge 0\), \(B^{(k)}\) is a copy of the construction of Theorem 2.5 on \([0,1]\), built from its own coefficients, and the copies \((B^{(k)})_{k\ge 0}\) are mutually independent.
  2. (H2) Assuming A1(a), A1(b), A1(e) and A2.

Each \((v_{l-1},v_l]\) lies in one \([k,k+1]\), so by the formula above \(W_{v_l}-W_{v_{l-1}}=B^{(k)}_{v_l-k}-B^{(k)}_{v_{l-1}-k}\), an increment of the single copy \(B^{(k)}\) over a sub-interval of \([0,1]\). Each unit interval contains at most one such piece, so the pieces of one increment of \(W\) come from distinct copies.

Then \(W_t-W_s\) is a sum of pieces, each \(\Normal(0,\text{length})\) by (D2) for its copy, and the pieces come from distinct copies, so they are independent by (H1). By A1(b) the sum is normal. By A1(a) the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances, which is the sum of the lengths, namely \(t-s\). So \(W_t-W_s\sim\Normal(0,t-s)\).

(D3). Take \(t_0<\dots<t_r\) and refine as above to the points \(v_0<\dots<v_p\), which contain every \(t_i\). Let \(\mathcal F\) be the family of all refined increments. For a fixed copy \(k\), its refined increments are increments of \(B^{(k)}\) over disjoint intervals of \([0,1]\). Adding the gaps between them gives a partition of \([0,1]\), so by (D3) for \(B^{(k)}\) (Theorem 2.5, under A2) they are mutually independent. Different copies are independent by (H1). So \(\mathcal F\) is a family of mutually independent blocks, each internally independent, and by A1(e) it is mutually independent. Each increment \(W_{t_i}-W_{t_{i-1}}\) is the sum of the refined increments over the refined intervals inside \((t_{i-1},t_i]\). These index sets are disjoint for different \(i\), so by A1(e) the increments \(W_{t_i}-W_{t_{i-1}}\) are mutually independent. \(\blacksquare\)

4Where the canon breaks

  • A bound that is not summable breaks Step 3. The uniform Cauchy argument needs \(\sum_m M_m<\infty\). For a level bound whose sum diverges, Step 3 does not apply, and this argument does not establish uniform convergence.
  • Non-normal coefficients break Lemma 2.3. Lemma 2.3 uses normality through A1(b) and A1(c), and the tail bound of Lemma 2.1 uses the normal law. With non-normal coefficients, neither of these facts is available, so the increment laws of Lemma 2.3 and the probability bound of Proposition 2.4(a) do not follow from this page's arguments.
  • Pointwise limits of continuous functions need not be continuous. For \(f_n(t)=t^n\) on \([0,1]\), each \(f_n\) is continuous, and \(f_n(t)\to 0\) for \(t<1\) while \(f_n(1)=1\). The limit is discontinuous at 1. This is why Step 3 proves uniform convergence before Step 4 concludes continuity.
  • Dyadic times do not determine the laws at other times. Lemma 2.3 identifies the laws at dyadic times. The laws at all times are the content of Assumption A2, which this page does not prove.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A series whose terms tend to zero converges.”

    The terms \(1/m\) tend to zero, but \(\sum_{m=1}^{N}1/m\) is unbounded. Split \(\sum_{m=1}^{2^k}1/m\) into the term \(m=1\) and \(k\) blocks \(\{2^{i-1}+1,\dots,2^i\}\) for \(i=1,\dots,k\). Each block has \(2^{i-1}\) terms, all at least \(2^{-i}\). Each block sums to at least \(1/2\), so the partial sum to \(2^k\) is at least \(1+k/2\). Hence the partial sums grow without bound. The argument of Step 2 needs summability, not decay of the terms.

  2. “Any coefficients that decay to zero give Brownian motion.”

    The proof of Lemma 2.3 fixes the coefficient. The variance of \(U_j\) is \(\tfrac14\cdot 2^{-n}+c^2\), which equals \(2^{-(n+1)}\) only when \(c^2=2^{-(n+2)}\). Any other scale \(c\) gives increments of the wrong variance, so the scale \(2^{-(m+1)/2}\) is forced by the variance bookkeeping.

  3. “A pointwise limit of continuous partial sums is continuous.”

    For \(f_n(t)=t^n\) on \([0,1]\), each \(f_n\) is continuous, but the pointwise limit is 0 on \([0,1)\) and 1 at \(t=1\). That limit is discontinuous. Continuity of \(S\) in Proposition 2.4 needs the uniform convergence of Step 3, not pointwise convergence.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · The hat of level 2

Write \(\Lambda_{2,1}\) explicitly, and evaluate it at \(t=0\), \(t=1/4\) and \(t=1/2\).

Solution

Here \(m=2\) and \(j=1\), so the centre is \((2-1)2^{-2}=1/4\), and \(\Lambda_{2,1}(t)=\max\{0,1-4|t-1/4|\}\). At \(t=0\), \(1-4\cdot 1/4=0\), so the value is 0. At \(t=1/4\) the value is \(1\). At \(t=1/2\), \(1-4\cdot 1/4=0\), so the value is 0. These values match Lemma 2.2(a) and (c).

M2 · The first partial sum at the midpoint

Compute \(S_1(1/2)\) in terms of \(\xi_0\) and \(\xi_{1,1}\), and check it against the midpoint step in the proof of Lemma 2.3.

Solution

\(S_1(t)=\xi_0 t+\xi_{1,1}\,2^{-1}\Lambda_{1,1}(t)\), with \(\Lambda_{1,1}(t)=\max\{0,1-2|t-1/2|\}\). At \(t=1/2\), \(\Lambda_{1,1}(1/2)=1\) and \(S_1(1/2)=\xi_0/2+\xi_{1,1}/2\). The midpoint step of Lemma 2.3 gives \((S_1(0)+S_1(1))/2+\tfrac12\xi_{1,1}=\xi_0/2+\xi_{1,1}/2\), since \(S_1(0)=0\) and \(S_1(1)=\xi_0\). The two expressions agree.

M3 · The variance of a half-interval increment at level 1

For \(n=1\) in Lemma 2.3, find the variance of \(U_1=\tfrac12 D_1+c\,\eta_1\) with \(c=2^{-3/2}\), where \(D_1\sim\Normal(0,1/2)\) and \(\eta_1\sim\Normal(0,1)\) are independent.

Solution

The variables \(D_1\) and \(\eta_1\) are independent, so the variance of the combination is the sum of the variances of its two parts, each multiplied by the square of its coefficient. This gives \(\Var(U_1)=\tfrac14\Var(D_1)+c^2\Var(\eta_1)\). With \(\Var(D_1)=\tfrac12\), \(\Var(\eta_1)=1\) and \(c^2=2^{-3}=\tfrac18\), this is \(\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac12+\tfrac18=\tfrac18+\tfrac18=\tfrac14\). This equals \(2^{-(n+1)}=2^{-2}\), as Lemma 2.3 requires for \(n=1\).

Proof

P1 · The hats vanish at dyadic points of coarser depth

Prove Lemma 2.2(c) from Lemma 2.2(a) and the definition of \(\Lambda_{m,j}\).

Solution

Let \(n<m\) and \(t=k2^{-n}\). Then \(t=2i\cdot 2^{-m}\) with \(i=k\,2^{m-n-1}\), an integer. The distance \(|t-(2j-1)2^{-m}|\) equals \(|2i-(2j-1)|\,2^{-m}\). The integer \(2i-(2j-1)\) is odd, so its absolute value is at least 1. The distance is therefore at least \(2^{-m}\), the radius of the support, and \(\Lambda_{m,j}(t)=0\).

P2 · The endpoint value \(S_M(1)\)

Show that \(S_M(0)=0\) and \(S_M(1)=\xi_0\) for every \(M\ge 0\).

Solution

Each hat vanishes at \(0\), because \(0=0\cdot 2^{-m}\) is an even multiple of \(2^{-m}\) and Lemma 2.2(c) applies with \(n=0\). So \(S_M(0)=\xi_0\cdot 0=0\). At \(t=1\), apply Lemma 2.2(c) with \(n=0\) and \(k=1\): every hat with \(m\ge 1\) vanishes at \(1\), so \(S_M(1)=\xi_0\).

P3 · At most one hat is non-zero at a point

Prove Lemma 2.2(b).

Solution

The non-zero region of \(\Lambda_{m,j}\) is the open interval of radius \(2^{-m}\) about \((2j-1)2^{-m}\). Consecutive centres differ by \(2\cdot 2^{-m}\), so two open intervals of radius \(2^{-m}\) around distinct centres are disjoint. A point in one of them is in no other, so at most one hat is non-zero there.

Empirical

E1 · Why the grid supremum is exact

Lab 1 compares the supremum of each level term over a grid of \(2^{-10}\) steps with the formula. Explain why the grid captures each supremum exactly for levels 1 to 8.

Solution

The supremum of a level-\(m\) term is attained at a hat centre \((2j-1)2^{-m}\), where the hat equals 1. For \(m\le 8\), each centre is an integer multiple of \(2^{-8}\), and therefore of \(2^{-10}\). So each centre is a grid point, and the grid contains the peak of every hat. The grid maximum is then exactly the formula, which is what the printed line reports.

E2 · The count 216 of 216

Lab 1 prints 216 of 216 cases for the dyadic check. Count the cases and confirm the number.

Solution

At depth \(n\) there are \(2^n+1\) dyadic points, and for each the comparison runs over levels \(M=n,\dots,8\), which is \(9-n\) values. The totals are \(2\cdot 9=18\) at depth 0, \(3\cdot 8=24\) at depth 1, \(5\cdot 7=35\) at depth 2, \(9\cdot 6=54\) at depth 3 and \(17\cdot 5=85\) at depth 4. The sum is \(18+24+35+54+85=216\), which matches the printed count.

E3 · The level-1 bound from the printed numbers

Lab 1 prints an observed supremum of 0.116589 and a bound of 0.832555 at level 1. Derive both from the definitions.

Solution

At level 1 the observed supremum is \(|\xi_{1,1}|\,2^{-1}\), so the printed value implies \(|\xi_{1,1}|\approx 0.233178\). The bound is \(\lambda_1 2^{-1}\) with \(\lambda_1=\sqrt{4\log 2}\). Numerically \(\sqrt{4\log 2}\approx 1.665109\), and half of it is \(0.832555\), which matches the printed bound. Since \(0.116589\le 0.832555\), the level is within its bound in this sample.

Research

R1 · Uncorrelated is not enough

Lemma 2.3 shows that \(U_j\) and \(V_j\) have covariance 0. Explain why this alone does not give independence, and identify the assumption that does.

Solution

Uncorrelated variables need not be independent in general. The step from zero covariance to independence uses that \((U_j,V_j)\) is jointly normal, which follows from A1(b), because both are linear combinations of the independent normal variables \(D_j\) and \(\eta_j\). Under that joint normality, A1(c) gives independence. So the normal assumption is what carries the step.

R2 · Probability one from the tail bound

Using Lemma 2.1 and the union bound, show that \(\sum_{m\ge 1}\Prob(E_m^c)\) is finite, and deduce that \(\Prob(\Omega^\ast)=1\) without using Borel–Cantelli.

Solution

Each \(\Prob(E_m^c)\le 2^{-m}\), so the series is at most \(\sum_m 2^{-m}=1\), which is finite. For the probability-one claim, the proof of Proposition 2.4(a) uses the bound \(\Prob(A_{m_0}^c)\le 2^{1-m_0}\), which follows from the same bounds for \(m\ge m_0\), together with continuity from below. No Borel–Cantelli argument is needed.

R3 · The tail difference of partial sums

On \(A_{m_0}\), for \(m_0\le M<N\), show that \(\sup_t|S_N(t)-S_M(t)|\le\sum_{m=M+1}^{N}M_m\).

Solution

\(S_N-S_M\) is the sum of the level-\(m\) terms \(Y_m\) for \(M<m\le N\), since the linear term cancels. The triangle inequality gives \(\sup_t|S_N-S_M|\le\sum_{m=M+1}^{N}\sup_t|Y_m|\). Each level \(m\ge m_0\) satisfies \(\sup_t|Y_m|\le M_m\) on \(A_{m_0}\), by step 1 of Proposition 2.4, and \(M+1\ge m_0\). The bound follows.
