The Kolmogorov Forward (Fokker–Planck) Equation

While the backward equation tracks how today's price of a fixed future claim depends on the initial state, the forward equation tracks the forward evolution of probability density across the state space. In continuous-time portfolio management and calibration, the forward equation determines how probability mass diffuses from a known origin into future distributions. In quantitative derivatives, Dupire's local volatility equation is precisely the forward PDE dual to the backward Black–Scholes equation, allowing entire volatility surfaces to be calibrated in a single sweep.

1The Adjoint Operator in \(L^2(\R)\)

In Lesson 1, the infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{A}\) was defined on smooth test functions \(f \in C_b^2(\R)\):

\[ \mathcal{A} f(x) = \mu(x) f'(x) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(x) f''(x). \]

Let \(\langle \phi, \psi \rangle \coloneqq \int_\R \phi(x) \psi(x) \, dx\) denote the standard \(L^2(\R)\) inner product.

Definition 2.1 (Formal Adjoint Operator)

The formal adjoint \(\mathcal{A}^*\) of the generator \(\mathcal{A}\) is the linear differential operator satisfying the duality relation:

\[ \langle \mathcal{A} f, p \rangle = \langle f, \mathcal{A}^* p \rangle \]

for all compactly supported smooth test functions \(f \in C_c^\infty(\R)\) and sufficiently regular densities \(p\).

Theorem 2.2 (Explicit Representation of the Adjoint Operator)

Assumptions: (A1) \(\mu \in C^1(\R)\) and \(\sigma \in C^2(\R)\); (A2) \(f \in C_c^\infty(\R)\) and \(p \in C^2(\R)\) with vanishing boundary terms at infinity.

Statement: The adjoint operator \(\mathcal{A}^*\) is given by:

\[ \mathcal{A}^* p(y) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left[ \mu(y) p(y) \right] + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2} \left[ \sigma^2(y) p(y) \right]. \]
Proof

Consider the inner product \(\langle \mathcal{A} f, p \rangle = \int_\R \left( \mu(y) f'(y) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(y) f''(y) \right) p(y) \, dy\). We integrate by parts on each term:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_\R \mu(y) f'(y) p(y) \, dy &= \Big[\mu(y) p(y) f(y)\Big]_{-\infty}^\infty - \int_\R f(y) \frac{\partial}{\partial y} [\mu(y) p(y)] \, dy \\ &= - \int_\R f(y) \frac{\partial}{\partial y} [\mu(y) p(y)] \, dy, \end{aligned} \]

since \(f\) has compact support. For the second derivative term, integrating by parts twice:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2} \int_\R \sigma^2(y) f''(y) p(y) \, dy &= -\frac{1}{2} \int_\R f'(y) \frac{\partial}{\partial y} [\sigma^2(y) p(y)] \, dy \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \int_\R f(y) \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2} [\sigma^2(y) p(y)] \, dy. \end{aligned} \]

Combining both terms yields:

\[ \langle \mathcal{A} f, p \rangle = \int_\R f(y) \left( -\frac{\partial}{\partial y} [\mu(y) p(y)] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2} [\sigma^2(y) p(y)] \right) dy = \langle f, \mathcal{A}^* p \rangle, \]

establishing the explicit representation.

2The Kolmogorov Forward Equation

Theorem 2.3 (Kolmogorov Forward / Fokker–Planck Equation)

Assumptions: (A1) \(X_t\) is an Itô diffusion with transition probability density \(p(t, x; T, y)\); (A2) \(\mu \in C^1(\R)\) and \(\sigma \in C^2(\R)\) with \(\sigma(y) > 0\).

Statement: For fixed initial coordinates \((t, x)\), the transition density \(p(t, x; T, y)\) evolves forward in destination variables \((T, y)\) according to the Kolmogorov forward (Fokker–Planck) equation:

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial T}(t, x; T, y) = \mathcal{A}^*_y p(t, x; T, y) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left[ \mu(y) p \right] + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2} \left[ \sigma^2(y) p \right], \quad T > t, \]

subject to the initial singular condition \(\lim_{T \downarrow t} p(t, x; T, y) = \delta(y - x)\).

Proof

Let \(f \in C_c^\infty(\R)\) be an arbitrary smooth test function. The conditional expectation \(\E[f(X_T) \mid X_t = x]\) can be expressed as an integral against the transition density:

\[ u(t, x; T) = \int_\R f(y) p(t, x; T, y) \, dy. \]

Differentiating with respect to future time \(T\):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial T} \int_\R f(y) p(t, x; T, y) \, dy = \int_\R f(y) \frac{\partial p}{\partial T}(t, x; T, y) \, dy. \]

On the other hand, by the semigroup property and definition of the generator applied to the forward increment:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial T} \E[f(X_T) \mid X_t = x] = \E[\mathcal{A} f(X_T) \mid X_t = x] = \int_\R \mathcal{A} f(y) p(t, x; T, y) \, dy. \]

By the duality relation (Theorem 2.2):

\[ \int_\R \mathcal{A} f(y) p(t, x; T, y) \, dy = \int_\R f(y) \mathcal{A}^* p(t, x; T, y) \, dy. \]

Equating both expressions gives \(\int_\R f(y) \left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial T} - \mathcal{A}^* p \right) dy = 0\). Since this holds for every \(f \in C_c^\infty(\R)\), the du Bois-Reymond fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations implies \(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T} = \mathcal{A}^* p\) almost everywhere.

3Conservation of Probability Mass and Invariant Distributions

Theorem 2.4 (Conservation of Total Probability Mass)

Assumptions: (A1) \(p(y, T)\) satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation on \(\R \times (t, \infty)\); (A2) the probability flux \(J(y, T) \coloneqq \mu(y) p - \tfrac{1}{2}\partial_y [\sigma^2(y) p]\) satisfies \(\lim_{|y| \to \infty} J(y, T) = 0\).

Statement: The total probability mass is strictly conserved in time:

\[ \frac{d}{dT} \int_\R p(t, x; T, y) \, dy = 0 \implies \int_\R p(t, x; T, y) \, dy = 1, \quad \forall T \ge t. \]
Proof

Write the Fokker–Planck equation in divergence (conservation) form: \(\partial_T p = -\partial_y J(y, T)\), where \(J\) is the probability flux. Integrating over \(\R\):

\[ \frac{d}{dT} \int_\R p \, dy = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \partial_T p \, dy = -\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\partial J}{\partial y} \, dy = -\Big[ J(y, T) \Big]_{-\infty}^\infty = 0. \]

Since \(\lim_{T \downarrow t} \int_\R p(t, x; T, y) dy = \int_\R \delta(y - x) dy = 1\), the integral equals 1 for all \(T \ge t\).

Definition 2.5 (Stationary Invariant Distribution)

A probability density \(p_\infty(y)\) is a stationary (invariant) distribution of the diffusion if \(\partial_T p_\infty = 0\), which requires that \(p_\infty\) satisfy the ordinary differential equation:

\[ \mathcal{A}^* p_\infty(y) = 0 \iff -\frac{d}{dy}[\mu(y) p_\infty(y)] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2}{dy^2}[\sigma^2(y) p_\infty(y)] = 0. \]

Integrating once under the zero-flux condition at infinity yields the first-order differential relation:

\[ \frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dy}[\sigma^2(y) p_\infty(y)] = \mu(y) p_\infty(y). \]
Example 2.6 (Stationary Distribution of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Process)

For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \(dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t) dt + \sigma dB_t\) with \(\kappa > 0\), the dispersion is constant (\(\sigma(y) = \sigma\)). The zero-flux relation gives:

\[ \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 p'_\infty(y) = \kappa(\theta - y) p_\infty(y) \implies \frac{p'_\infty(y)}{p_\infty(y)} = \frac{2\kappa(\theta - y)}{\sigma^2} = -\frac{y - \theta}{\sigma^2 / (2\kappa)}. \]

Integrating yields the Gaussian stationary density: \(p_\infty(y) = \Normal\left(\theta, \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\right)\).

Lab 1 · Fokker-Planck Density Evolution and Conservation of Probability Mass

Evolving an initial narrow Gaussian density under the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Fokker–Planck PDE preserves total probability mass to within 1e-4 and reproduces the exact analytical mean and variance at T = 1.0 within 0.01 (SIMULATED).

"""Lab SC4-L02-1 · Fokker-Planck Density Evolution and Conservation of Probability Mass.

Evolves the probability density of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process dX_t = kappa * (theta - X_t) * dt + sigma * dB_t
forward in time using the Fokker-Planck (Kolmogorov forward) PDE.
Verifies conservation of total probability mass and compares final moments against analytical values (SIMULATED).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    kappa = 1.5
    theta = 0.10
    sigma = 0.30
    x0 = 0.50
    t_final = 1.0

    # Spatial grid
    x_min, x_max = -1.5, 2.0
    n_space = 350
    dx = (x_max - x_min) / n_space
    x = np.linspace(x_min, x_max, n_space + 1)

    # Initial condition: narrow Gaussian centered at x0 with std s0
    s0 = 0.05
    p = np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - x0) / s0) ** 2) / (s0 * math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi))
    # Normalize initial mass
    initial_mass = float(np.sum(p) * dx)
    p /= initial_mass

    # Time stepping: conservative explicit flux discretization
    # CFL constraint requires dt <= dx^2 / sigma^2
    n_time = 4000
    dt = t_final / n_time

    for _ in range(n_time):
        # Flux at cell interfaces j + 1/2: F = kappa*(theta - x)*p - 0.5*sigma^2 * dp/dx
        # Using central differences for advection and diffusion
        x_mid = 0.5 * (x[:-1] + x[1:])
        p_mid = 0.5 * (p[:-1] + p[1:])
        dp_dx = (p[1:] - p[:-1]) / dx
        flux = kappa * (theta - x_mid) * p_mid - 0.5 * (sigma**2) * dp_dx

        # Update interior cells: p_j^{n+1} = p_j^n - dt/dx * (F_{j+1/2} - F_{j-1/2})
        p[1:-1] -= (dt / dx) * (flux[1:] - flux[:-1])
        # Zero-flux boundary conditions at far edges
        p[0] = 0.0
        p[-1] = 0.0

    final_mass = float(np.sum(p) * dx)
    num_mean = float(np.sum(x * p) * dx)
    num_var = float(np.sum(((x - num_mean) ** 2) * p) * dx)

    # Analytical OU moments at t_final
    # Initial variance was s0^2
    exact_mean = theta + (x0 - theta) * math.exp(-kappa * t_final)
    exact_var = (s0**2) * math.exp(-2.0 * kappa * t_final) + (sigma**2) / (2.0 * kappa) * (
        1.0 - math.exp(-2.0 * kappa * t_final)
    )

    mass_err = abs(final_mass - 1.0)
    mean_err = abs(num_mean - exact_mean)
    var_err = abs(num_var - exact_var)

    print(f"Total probability mass: {final_mass:.8f} (error: {mass_err:.2e})")
    print(f"Numerical mean: {num_mean:.6f} (Exact: {exact_mean:.6f}, err: {mean_err:.2e})")
    print(f"Numerical var:  {num_var:.6f} (Exact: {exact_var:.6f}, err: {var_err:.2e})")
    print(f"Mass conserved within 1e-4: {mass_err < 1e-4}")
    print(f"Moments agree within 0.01: {mean_err < 0.01 and var_err < 0.01}")


main()

Numerical mean: 0.189227 (Exact: 0.189252, err: 2.51e-05)
Numerical var:  0.028604 (Exact: 0.028631, err: 2.67e-05)

4Where the canon breaks

The existence of a proper stationary probability density (\(\int_\R p_\infty dy = 1\)) requires the speed measure to be finite. For standard Brownian motion \(dX_t = dB_t\), \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 1\), so \(\mathcal{A}^* p_\infty = \tfrac{1}{2} p''_\infty = 0\), which yields linear functions \(p_\infty(y) = c_1 y + c_2\). No non-zero linear function is integrable on \(\R\): Brownian motion has an infinite invariant Lebesgue measure and no stationary probability distribution. Similarly, for geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\), the zero-flux equation yields \(p_\infty(s) \propto s^{2\mu/\sigma^2 - 2}\), which is non-integrable near \(s = 0\) or as \(s \to \infty\) for generic drift, reflecting that probability mass either collapses to 0 or escapes to \(+\infty\).

5Misconceptions

  1. “The Kolmogorov backward and forward equations have the identical spatial differential operator.”

    The backward equation contains \(\mu(x)\partial_x + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2(x)\partial_{xx}\), where differentiation acts solely on the unknown function. The forward equation contains \(-\partial_y[\mu(y)\,\cdot\,] + \tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{yy}[\sigma^2(y)\,\cdot\,]\), where coefficients sit inside the spatial derivatives. They coincide if and only if \(\mu\) is constant and \(\sigma\) is spatially homogeneous.

  2. “Every Markov diffusion possesses a unique stationary probability distribution.”

    A diffusion possesses a stationary probability distribution if and only if it is positive recurrent. Transient diffusions (such as Brownian motion with non-zero drift) and null-recurrent diffusions (such as driftless Brownian motion) have no normalizable stationary probability density.

  3. “The forward equation can be integrated backward in time from a known future density.”

    The forward equation is well-posed only forward in time (\(T > t\)). Integrating backward in time reverses the sign of the diffusion operator, transforming it into an ill-posed backward heat equation that amplifies high-frequency spatial perturbations exponentially.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Adjoint operator expansion

Expand \(\mathcal{A}^* p(y)\) by applying the product rule to derivatives of products for arbitrary smooth \(\mu(y)\) and \(\sigma(y)\).

Solution

Applying the product rule to the first term:

\[ -\frac{\partial}{\partial y}[\mu(y) p(y)] = -\mu'(y) p(y) - \mu(y) p'(y). \]

For the diffusion term, let \(\Sigma(y) = \sigma^2(y)\). Differentiating twice:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial y}[\Sigma(y) p(y)] = \Sigma'(y) p(y) + \Sigma(y) p'(y), \] \[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2}[\Sigma(y) p(y)] = \Sigma''(y) p(y) + 2\Sigma'(y) p'(y) + \Sigma(y) p''(y). \]

Multiplying by \(1/2\) and collecting terms:

\[ \mathcal{A}^* p(y) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(y) p''(y) + \left(\sigma(y)\sigma'(y) - \mu(y)\right) p'(y) + \left( \frac{1}{2}(\sigma^2)''(y) - \mu'(y) \right) p(y). \]
M2 · Stationary density for a linear drift diffusion

Let \(dX_t = -2 X_t dt + \sqrt{2} dB_t\). Find the stationary density \(p_\infty(x)\) normalized so that \(\int_\R p_\infty(x) dx = 1\).

Solution

Here \(\mu(x) = -2x\) and \(\sigma(x) = \sqrt{2}\), so \(\sigma^2 = 2\). The zero-flux stationary equation is \(\frac{1}{2}(2) p'_\infty(x) = -2x p_\infty(x) \implies p'_\infty(x) = -2x p_\infty(x)\). Integrating gives \(\ln p_\infty(x) = -x^2 + C \implies p_\infty(x) = C_0 e^{-x^2}\). Normalizing: \(\int_\R C_0 e^{-x^2} dx = C_0 \sqrt{\pi} = 1 \implies C_0 = 1/\sqrt{\pi}\). Thus \(p_\infty(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} e^{-x^2} = \Normal(0, 1/2)\).

M3 · Probability flux at a reflective boundary

State the boundary condition on \(p(y, T)\) at a reflecting boundary \(y = a\) where no probability mass may cross.

A reflecting boundary requires the total probability flux \(J(a, T)\) to vanish identically: \(J(a, T) = \mu(a) p(a, T) - \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}[\sigma^2(y) p(y, T)]\Big|_{y=a} = 0\). This is a Robin (mixed) boundary condition involving both the density \(p\) and its spatial derivative \(\partial_y p\).

P1 · Fokker–Planck equation for log-normal diffusion

Show that the log-normal transition density of geometric Brownian motion \(p(0, s_0; T, s) = \frac{1}{s \sigma \sqrt{2\pi T}} \exp\left(-\frac{(\ln(s/s_0) - (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T)^2}{2\sigma^2 T}\right)\) satisfies the forward equation \(\partial_T p = -\partial_s[\mu s p] + \tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{ss}[\sigma^2 s^2 p]\).

Solution

Substitute \(x = \ln s\). The process \(X_t = \ln S_t\) satisfies \(dX_t = (\mu - \sigma^2/2) dt + \sigma dB_t\) with constant coefficients \(\nu = \mu - \sigma^2/2\) and \(\sigma\). Its density \(q(x, T)\) satisfies the constant-coefficient Fokker–Planck equation \(\partial_T q = -\nu \partial_x q + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \partial_{xx} q\). Since \(p(s, T) = q(\ln s, T) / s\), applying the change-of-variables formula for differential operators transforms \(\partial_T q = -\nu \partial_x q + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \partial_{xx} q\) directly into \(\partial_T p = -\partial_s[\mu s p] + \tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{ss}[\sigma^2 s^2 p]\), proving the identity.

P2 · Entropy dissipation along the Fokker–Planck flow

Define the relative entropy (Kullback–Leibler divergence) \(H(T) \coloneqq \int_\R p(y, T) \ln(p(y, T) / p_\infty(y)) \, dy\). Prove that \(dH/dT \le 0\) (entropy dissipates monotonically toward the stationary state).

Solution

Differentiating \(H(T)\) and using \(\partial_T p = -\partial_y J\):

\[ \frac{dH}{dT} = \int_\R \partial_T p \left( \ln\frac{p}{p_\infty} + 1 \right) dy = -\int_\R \partial_y J \ln\frac{p}{p_\infty} dy = \int_\R J \partial_y \left[ \ln\frac{p}{p_\infty} \right] dy. \]

Since \(J = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 p_\infty \partial_y (p / p_\infty)\) and \(\partial_y \ln(p / p_\infty) = \frac{\partial_y(p / p_\infty)}{p / p_\infty}\), the integrand becomes:

\[ \frac{dH}{dT} = -\frac{1}{2} \int_\R \sigma^2 p \left( \partial_y \ln\frac{p}{p_\infty} \right)^2 dy \le 0. \]

The Fisher information is non-negative, proving that relative entropy dissipates monotonically.

P3 · Forward–backward Kolmogorov duality

Prove the Chapman–Kolmogorov identity \(p(t, x; T, y) = \int_\R p(t, x; s, z) p(s, z; T, y) dz\) for any intermediate time \(s \in (t, T)\) using the semigroup property of conditional expectations.

Solution

By the law of total probability and the tower property for Markov processes:

\[ \Prob(X_T \in A \mid X_t = x) = \E[\Prob(X_T \in A \mid \mathcal{F}_s) \mid X_t = x] = \E[\Prob(X_T \in A \mid X_s) \mid X_t = x]. \]

Writing this in terms of transition densities:

\[ \int_A p(t, x; T, y) \, dy = \int_\R p(t, x; s, z) \left( \int_A p(s, z; T, y) \, dy \right) dz = \int_A \left( \int_\R p(t, x; s, z) p(s, z; T, y) \, dz \right) dy. \]

Since this equality holds for every Borel set \(A\), the transition densities satisfy the Chapman–Kolmogorov convolution identity almost everywhere.

Empirical

E1 · Speed of convergence to stationary distribution

For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with \(\kappa = 2.0\), compute the half-life of variance equilibration toward the stationary variance \(\sigma^2 / (2\kappa)\).

Solution

The time-dependent variance from a point mass is \(v(t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t})\). The fraction of stationary variance reached is \(1 - e^{-2\kappa t}\). Reaching half the stationary variance requires \(1 - e^{-2\kappa t_{1/2}} = 0.5 \implies e^{-2\kappa t_{1/2}} = 0.5 \implies t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{2\kappa}\). For \(\kappa = 2.0\), \(t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{4.0} \approx \frac{0.6931}{4.0} \approx 0.1733\) years (approx 63 days).

E2 · Dupire equation duality

Explain how Dupire's local volatility formula \(\sigma_{\text{loc}}^2(K, T) = \frac{2(\partial_T C + r K \partial_K C)}{K^2 \partial_{KK} C}\) is derived directly from the Kolmogorov forward equation for the risk-neutral transition density.

Solution

A European call price is \(C(K, T) = e^{-rT} \int_K^\infty (s - K) p(0, S_0; T, s) ds\). Differentiating twice with respect to strike \(K\) gives \(\partial_{KK} C = e^{-rT} p(0, S_0; T, K)\). Differentiating once with respect to maturity \(T\) and replacing \(\partial_T p\) using the forward Fokker–Planck equation yields \(\partial_T C = -r C + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{\text{loc}}^2(K, T) K^2 \partial_{KK} C - r K \partial_K C\). Rearranging for \(\sigma_{\text{loc}}^2\) produces Dupire's formula.

E3 · Mass leakage detection in numerical schemes

A numerical PDE analyst observes that the discrete integral \(\sum_j p_j^n \Delta x\) drops from 1.000 to 0.982 after 500 time steps. Identify the two most common causes.

Solution

The two primary causes are: (1) Domain truncation boundary flux leakage: the computational domain \([x_{\min}, x_{\max}]\) is too narrow, allowing probability mass to escape through the outer boundaries; and (2) Non-conservative finite-difference discretization: using non-divergence form \(\mu \partial_x p\) instead of conservative flux differencing \(\partial_x [\mu p]\), violating telescoping flux summation.

Research

R1 · Wassertein gradient flow interpretation

Explain Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) theory, which identifies the Fokker–Planck equation as the steepest descent gradient flow of the free energy functional \(\mathcal{F}[p] = \int_\R p \ln p \, dx + \int_\R V(x) p \, dx\) with respect to the 2-Wasserstein metric \(\mathcal{W}_2\).

Solution

The JKO scheme discretizes the diffusion by the implicit variational stepping problem \(p^{n+1} = \argmin_p \left\{ \frac{1}{2\Delta t} \mathcal{W}_2^2(p, p^n) + \mathcal{F}[p] \right\}\). In the limit \(\Delta t \to 0\), the Euler–Lagrange optimality condition reproduces the continuity equation with velocity field \(v = -\nabla \left( \frac{\delta \mathcal{F}}{\delta p} \right) = -\nabla(\ln p + 1 + V) = -\frac{\nabla p}{p} - \nabla V\). The probability flux is \(p v = -\nabla p - p \nabla V\), which yields the Fokker–Planck equation \(\partial_t p = \Delta p + \nabla \cdot (p \nabla V)\).

R2 · Multi-dimensional Fokker–Planck equation

Formulate the Fokker–Planck equation for a \(d\)-dimensional diffusion \(d\mathbf{X}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathbf{X}_t) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{X}_t) d\mathbf{B}_t\) with diffusion matrix \(\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x}) = \boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\sigma}^T(\mathbf{x})\).

Solution

In vector tensor notation, the multi-dimensional Kolmogorov forward equation is:

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(\mathbf{x}, t) = -\sum_{i=1}^d \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \left[ \mu_i(\mathbf{x}) p(\mathbf{x}, t) \right] + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^d \sum_{j=1}^d \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} \left[ D_{ij}(\mathbf{x}) p(\mathbf{x}, t) \right]. \]

In divergence form, \(\partial_t p = -\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}\), where the probability flux vector is \(J_i(\mathbf{x}, t) = \mu_i(\mathbf{x}) p - \frac{1}{2}\sum_j \partial_{x_j} [D_{ij}(\mathbf{x}) p]\).

R3 · Forward PDE calibration of local volatility

Contrast the computational complexity of calibrating local volatility \(\sigma_{\text{loc}}(K, T)\) using the backward Black–Scholes PDE versus the forward Dupire PDE.

Solution

Using the backward PDE, pricing \(N_K \times N_T\) options across strikes and maturities requires solving a separate backward PDE for each individual contract strike and maturity, scaling as \(O(N_K \cdot N_T \cdot N_{\text{grid}})\). Using the forward Dupire PDE, a single forward time march across \(T \in [0, T_{\max}]\) simultaneously generates option prices for all strikes \(K\) on the spatial grid, reducing computational cost to \(O(N_{\text{grid}})\), a speedup of several orders of magnitude.

  2. Where do the coefficient functions \(\mu(y)\) and \(\sigma^2(y)\) sit in the Kolmogorov forward equation?

    Options

    Integration by parts shifts derivatives onto the product of the coefficient and the density, placing the coefficients inside the derivative operators.

  3. What is the total integrated probability mass \(\int_\R p(t, x; T, y) dy\) for any valid diffusion density at \(T > t\)?

    By Theorem 2.4, total probability mass is strictly conserved and identically equal to 1.

  4. What condition defines a stationary probability distribution \(p_\infty(y)\)?

    Options

    A stationary distribution satisfies \(\partial_T p_\infty = 0\), which requires \(\mathcal{A}^* p_\infty = 0\).

  5. For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \(dX_t = -2 X_t dt + \sqrt{2} dB_t\), what is the mean of the stationary distribution?

    The long-term mean reversion level is \(\theta = 0\), so the stationary mean is 0.

  6. Why does standard driftless Brownian motion \(dX_t = dB_t\) possess no normalizable stationary probability distribution?

    Options

    The solution to \(p''_\infty = 0\) is linear, which cannot be integrated to 1 on \(\R\); mass disperses without bound.

  7. What happens if one attempts to solve the Fokker–Planck equation backward in time from a known terminal distribution?

    Options

    Reversing time turns forward diffusion into an anti-diffusion equation, where mode amplification factors \(e^{k^2 t}\) diverge explosively.

  8. In quantitative finance, which pricing PDE is the forward dual of the Black–Scholes backward PDE?

    Options

    Dupire's equation is obtained by integrating the forward Fokker–Planck equation against the call payoff, yielding a PDE in strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\).
