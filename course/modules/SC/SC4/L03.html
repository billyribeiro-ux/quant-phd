The Feynman–Kac formula is the fundamental bridge between stochastic calculus and partial differential equations. It establishes that solving a linear second-order parabolic PDE with terminal and running costs is mathematically identical to computing the expected discounted payoff of a diffusion process. In quantitative finance, this duality is ubiquitous: PDE solvers compute the price and Greeks across all asset levels simultaneously on low-dimensional meshes, while Monte Carlo simulations evaluate the Feynman–Kac expectation along discrete paths for complex, high-dimensional payoffs.

1From Infinitesimal Generators to Discounted Expectations

Consider an Itô diffusion \(X_t \in \R^d\) governed by the stochastic differential equation:

\[ dX_s = \mu(X_s, s) \, ds + \sigma(X_s, s) \, dW_s, \quad s \ge t, \quad X_t = x \in \R^d, \]

where \(W_s\) is an \(m\)-dimensional standard Brownian motion, \(\mu: \R^d \times [0, T] \to \R^d\) is the drift vector, and \(\sigma: \R^d \times [0, T] \to \R^{d \times m}\) is the diffusion matrix. The spatial infinitesimal generator associated with \(X\) at time \(t\) acts on test functions \(u \in C^2(\R^d)\) by:

\[ \mathcal{A}_t u(x) \coloneqq \sum_{i=1}^d \mu_i(x, t) \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i}(x) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^d \sum_{j=1}^d \left( \sigma(x, t) \sigma(x, t)^\top \right)_{ij} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}(x). \]

In financial derivative pricing, future payoffs are discounted by a short rate \(r(x, t) \ge 0\), and intermediate cash flows (such as continuous dividend yield or coupons) are collected via a running source term \(g(x, t)\). This gives rise to the backward Cauchy problem for the function \(u(x, t)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x, t) + \mathcal{A}_t u(x, t) - r(x, t) u(x, t) + g(x, t) &= 0 \quad \text{for } (x, t) \in \R^d \times [0, T), \\ u(x, T) &= \psi(x) \quad \text{for } x \in \R^d. \end{aligned} \]
Definition 3.1 (Discount Factor Process)

For any starting time \(t \in [0, T]\) and path \(X\), the continuous discount factor process \(D_t^s\) from time \(t\) to time \(s \ge t\) is defined by:

\[ D_t^s \coloneqq \exp\left( -\int_t^s r(X_v, v) \, dv \right). \]

2Rigorous Proof of the Feynman–Kac Formula

Theorem 3.2 (Feynman–Kac formula)

Assumptions:

  1. (A1) The coefficients \(\mu(x, t)\) and \(\sigma(x, t)\) satisfy uniform Lipschitz continuity and linear growth conditions, ensuring a unique strong solution to the SDE for \(X_s\).
  2. (A2) The discount rate \(r(x, t)\) is continuous and bounded below by a constant \(r_{\min} > -\infty\).
  3. (A3) The function \(u(x, t) \in C^{2,1}(\R^d \times [0, T])\) is a classical solution to the terminal-value PDE: \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathcal{A}_t u - r u + g = 0, \quad u(x, T) = \psi(x). \]
  4. (A4) The gradient satisfies the integrability condition: \[ \E_{t,x}\left[ \int_t^T \left( D_t^s \left\| \nabla_x u(X_s, s)^\top \sigma(X_s, s) \right\| \right)^2 ds \right] < \infty. \]

Statement: Then \(u(x, t)\) admits the stochastic representation:

\[ u(x, t) = \E_{t,x}\left[ \exp\left(-\int_t^T r(X_s, s) \, ds\right) \psi(X_T) + \int_t^T \exp\left(-\int_t^s r(X_v, v) \, dv\right) g(X_s, s) \, ds \right]. \]
Proof

Fix \((x, t) \in \R^d \times [0, T]\). For \(s \in [t, T]\), define the discounted cumulative payoff process \(M_s\) by:

\[ M_s \coloneqq D_t^s u(X_s, s) + \int_t^s D_t^v g(X_v, v) \, dv. \]

Notice that \(D_t^s\) satisfies the differential relation \(d D_t^s = -r(X_s, s) D_t^s \, ds\) with \(D_t^t = 1\). By Itô's product rule and the multi-dimensional Itô formula applied to \(u(X_s, s)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} d M_s &= d\left( D_t^s u(X_s, s) \right) + D_t^s g(X_s, s) \, ds \\ &= \left( d D_t^s \right) u(X_s, s) + D_t^s \, du(X_s, s) + d\langle D_t^\cdot, u(X_\cdot, \cdot) \rangle_s + D_t^s g(X_s, s) \, ds. \end{aligned} \]

Because \(D_t^s\) is of finite variation, the cross-variation term \(d\langle D_t^\cdot, u(X_\cdot, \cdot) \rangle_s = 0\). Applying Itô's formula to \(u(X_s, s)\):

\[ du(X_s, s) = \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(X_s, s) + \mathcal{A}_s u(X_s, s) \right) ds + \nabla_x u(X_s, s)^\top \sigma(X_s, s) \, dW_s. \]

Substituting into the differential of \(M_s\):

\[ \begin{aligned} d M_s &= -r(X_s, s) D_t^s u(X_s, s) \, ds + D_t^s \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathcal{A}_s u \right) ds + D_t^s g(X_s, s) \, ds \\ &\quad + D_t^s \nabla_x u(X_s, s)^\top \sigma(X_s, s) \, dW_s \\ &= D_t^s \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathcal{A}_s u - r u + g \right)(X_s, s) \, ds + D_t^s \nabla_x u(X_s, s)^\top \sigma(X_s, s) \, dW_s. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(u\) solves the PDE, the drift term identically vanishes: \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathcal{A}_s u - r u + g = 0\). Therefore, \(d M_s\) reduces to a pure stochastic integral:

\[ d M_s = D_t^s \nabla_x u(X_s, s)^\top \sigma(X_s, s) \, dW_s. \]

By assumption (A4), the integrand is in \(L^2(\Omega \times [t, T])\), which guarantees that \(M_s\) is a true martingale on \([t, T]\) under \(\Prob_{t,x}\). In particular, its expectation is constant over time:

\[ \E_{t,x}[M_T] = \E_{t,x}[M_t] = M_t = D_t^t u(X_t, t) = u(x, t). \]

Evaluating \(M_T\) at the terminal time \(s = T\) using the terminal boundary condition \(u(X_T, T) = \psi(X_T)\):

\[ M_T = D_t^T \psi(X_T) + \int_t^T D_t^v g(X_v, v) \, dv. \]

Taking conditional expectations gives:

\[ u(x, t) = \E_{t,x}\left[ \exp\left(-\int_t^T r(X_s, s) \, ds\right) \psi(X_T) + \int_t^T \exp\left(-\int_t^s r(X_v, v) \, dv\right) g(X_s, s) \, ds \right], \]

which completes the proof.

3Connection to the Black–Scholes PDE

Under the risk-neutral pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the underlying asset price follows a geometric Brownian motion:

\[ dS_s = r S_s \, ds + \sigma S_s \, dW_s^{\mathbb{Q}}, \quad S_t = S. \]

Here, the drift is \(\mu(S, t) = r S\), the diffusion is \(\sigma(S, t) = \sigma S\), and the discount rate is constant \(r\). The spatial infinitesimal generator is:

\[ \mathcal{A} V = r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}. \]

Applying the Feynman–Kac theorem to the discounted expected payoff of a European option \(V(S, t) = \E^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t,S}\left[ e^{-r(T-t)} \psi(S_T) \right]\) immediately recovers the classical Black–Scholes partial differential equation (Black & Scholes 1973; Shreve 2004):

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - r V = 0, \quad V(S, T) = \psi(S). \]
Remark 3.3 (Forward vs. Backward Time Coordinate)

In financial engineering literature, time to maturity \(\tau \coloneqq T - t\) is frequently introduced as the independent temporal variable. Setting \(u(S, \tau) \coloneqq V(S, T - \tau)\), the chain rule gives \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau}\), transforming the terminal-value backward PDE into a standard forward-in-time initial-value parabolic problem:

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial S^2} + r S \frac{\partial u}{\partial S} - r u, \quad u(S, 0) = \psi(S). \]

All finite-difference stepping schemes (FTCS, BTCS, Crank–Nicolson) operate in this forward \(\tau\)-coordinate.

Lab 1 · Feynman-Kac Stochastic Representation vs. Parabolic PDE Analytical Solution

Verifies the Feynman-Kac equivalence between the conditional expectation of discounted terminal payoff and the analytical solution of the Black-Scholes parabolic PDE using antithetic Monte Carlo simulation within 3 standard errors (SIMULATED).

"""Lab SC4-L03-1 · Feynman-Kac Stochastic Representation vs. Parabolic PDE Analytical Solution.

Verifies the Feynman-Kac equivalence between the conditional expectation of discounted terminal payoff
and the solution of the Black-Scholes parabolic PDE using Monte Carlo simulation (SIMULATED).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def main() -> None:
    s0 = 100.0
    k = 105.0
    horizon = 1.0
    rate = 0.05
    vol = 0.20
    n_pairs = 25000  # 50,000 total antithetic paths
    seed = 42

    # 1. Analytical PDE solution via Black-Scholes formula
    sqrt_h = math.sqrt(horizon)
    d1 = (math.log(s0 / k) + (rate + 0.5 * vol * vol) * horizon) / (vol * sqrt_h)
    d2 = d1 - vol * sqrt_h
    disc = math.exp(-rate * horizon)
    pde_price = s0 * norm_cdf(d1) - k * disc * norm_cdf(d2)

    # 2. Feynman-Kac conditional expectation via Monte Carlo
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    drift_term = (rate - 0.5 * vol * vol) * horizon
    vol_term = vol * sqrt_h

    payoffs: list[float] = []
    for _ in range(n_pairs):
        z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        s_pos = s0 * math.exp(drift_term + vol_term * z)
        s_neg = s0 * math.exp(drift_term - vol_term * z)
        pay_pos = disc * max(s_pos - k, 0.0)
        pay_neg = disc * max(s_neg - k, 0.0)
        payoffs.append(0.5 * (pay_pos + pay_neg))

    mc_estimate = sum(payoffs) / n_pairs
    sample_var = sum((x - mc_estimate) ** 2 for x in payoffs) / (n_pairs - 1)
    se = math.sqrt(sample_var / n_pairs)
    z_stat = abs(mc_estimate - pde_price) / se

    print(f"Analytical PDE solution (Black-Scholes): {pde_price:.6f}")
    print(f"Feynman-Kac Monte Carlo estimate:       {mc_estimate:.6f} (se: {se:.6f})")
    print(f"Pricing absolute difference:            {abs(mc_estimate - pde_price):.4f}")
    print(f"Standardized z-score:                  {z_stat:.3f}")
    print(f"Estimate within 3 standard errors:      {z_stat <= 3.0}")


main()
Analytical PDE solution (Black-Scholes): 8.021352
Feynman-Kac Monte Carlo estimate:       8.006632 (se: 0.046329)
Pricing absolute difference:            0.0147
Standardized z-score:                  0.318
Estimate within 3 standard errors:      True

The Monte Carlo estimate matches the Black-Scholes parabolic PDE solution within standard error bounds, confirming the Feynman-Kac duality between terminal-value PDEs and discounted expectations.

4Where the canon breaks

The classical Feynman–Kac representation relies on several strong structural assumptions that fail in broader financial contexts:

  • High Dimensions & Curse of Dimensionality: Finite-difference PDE solvers discretize the state space into \(N^d\) mesh points. For basket options, stochastic volatility with jump-diffusion, or multi-curve term structure models (\(d \ge 4\)), PDE discretizations become computationally intractable. In such regimes, the stochastic Feynman–Kac expectation evaluated via Monte Carlo is the only viable method because Monte Carlo convergence rate \(O(N^{-1/2})\) is independent of dimension \(d\).
  • American Options & Free Boundary Problems: When early exercise is permitted, the option price satisfies a variational inequality rather than a linear PDE: \[ \min\left\{ -\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} - \mathcal{A} V + r V, \; V(S, t) - (S - K)^+ \right\} = 0. \] This free-boundary obstacle problem breaks the classical linear Feynman–Kac theorem. Its stochastic counterpart is an optimal stopping problem \(\sup_{\tau \in [t, T]} \E^{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{-r(\tau - t)} \psi(S_\tau)]\), requiring Snell envelopes rather than simple expectations.
  • Path-Dependent Payoffs: Payoffs depending on the running path \(\int_0^T S_u \, du\) (Asian options) or running maximum \(\max_{u \le T} S_u\) (lookback options) violate the Markovian framework unless auxiliary state variables are added, increasing the PDE dimension.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The drift \(\mu(S, t)\) appearing in the Feynman–Kac derivative pricing PDE represents the physical expected return of the stock.”

    The Black–Scholes PDE is derived from a self-financing replicating portfolio that eliminates diffusion risk. No-arbitrage pricing corresponds to taking the Feynman–Kac expectation under an equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), wherein the drift of the tradable underlying asset is strictly the risk-free rate \(r\) (less any dividend yield \(q\)), entirely independent of the investor's physical risk preference \(\mu\).

  2. “Any classical solution to the backward Cauchy problem must coincide with the Feynman–Kac stochastic expectation.”

    Parabolic PDEs can admit non-unique solutions if growth at infinity is unrestricted. For example, Tychonoff's classic counterexample demonstrates that the heat equation \(u_t = u_{xx}\) on \(\R\) with initial condition \(u(x, 0) = 0\) admits non-trivial, rapidly growing solutions. The Feynman–Kac expectation selects the unique classical solution satisfying polynomial or exponential growth conditions consistent with \(L^2\) martingale integrability.

  3. “A numerical PDE solver can march forward in calendar time \(t\) to solve the backward terminal-value PDE.”

    In calendar time \(t\), the operator has a positive sign on \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\) and a positive sign on \(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial S^2}\). Stepping forward in \(t\) is mathematically equivalent to backward heat conduction, which is severely ill-posed in the sense of Hadamard (arbitrarily high frequencies explode instantly). One must reverse time by substituting \(\tau = T - t\), transforming the problem into a forward parabolic equation \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial S^2} + r S \frac{\partial u}{\partial S} - r u\).

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Heat equation expectation

Let \(W_t\) be standard Brownian motion and \(u(x, t) = \E[ (W_T)^2 \mid W_t = x]\). State the PDE satisfied by \(u(x, t)\) for \(t < T\) with terminal condition \(u(x, T)\), and solve for \(u(x, t)\) explicitly.

Solution

For standard Brownian motion, \(\mu(x) = 0\), \(\sigma(x) = 1\), and \(r = 0\). The infinitesimal generator is \(\mathcal{A} u = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}\). By the Feynman–Kac formula, \(u\) satisfies the backward heat equation:

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = 0, \quad u(x, T) = x^2. \]

Using the independent increments property of Brownian motion, \(W_T = W_t + (W_T - W_t)\). Since \(W_T - W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, T - t)\) is independent of \(W_t = x\):

\[ u(x, t) = \E\left[ (x + W_T - W_t)^2 \right] = x^2 + 2x \E[W_T - W_t] + \E[(W_T - W_t)^2] = x^2 + (T - t). \]

Direct differentiation verifies: \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -1\), \(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = 2\), so \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = -1 + \frac{1}{2}(2) = 0\).

M2 · SDE coefficient matching

Identify the drift \(\mu(x, t)\), diffusion \(\sigma(x, t)\), discount rate \(r(x, t)\), and source term \(g(x, t)\) corresponding to the parabolic PDE: \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + 3x \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + 2 x^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} - 0.04 u + e^{-t} = 0. \]

Solution

Matching the given PDE with the canonical Feynman–Kac form \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mu \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} - r u + g = 0\):

  • Drift coefficient: \(\mu(x, t) = 3x\).
  • Diffusion coefficient: \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 = 2 x^2 \implies \sigma^2 = 4 x^2 \implies \sigma(x, t) = 2|x|\).
  • Discount rate: \(r(x, t) = 0.04\).
  • Running source: \(g(x, t) = e^{-t}\).
M3 · Exponential solution ansatz

Verify by direct differentiation that \(u(x, t) = \exp(a x + b(T - t))\) satisfies \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = 0\) for a specific relation between \(a\) and \(b\).

Solution

Compute the partial derivatives of \(u(x, t) = e^{a x + b(T - t)}\):

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -b e^{a x + b(T-t)}, \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = a u, \quad \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = a^2 u. \]

Substitute into the backward heat equation:

\[ -b u + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 a^2 u = 0 \implies b = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 a^2. \]

Hence \(u(x, t) = \exp\left( a x + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 a^2 (T - t) \right)\), reflecting the exponential martingale property \(\E[e^{a W_T} \mid W_t = x] = e^{a x + \frac{1}{2} a^2 (T - t)}\) for \(\sigma = 1\).

P1 · Vasicek bond pricing PDE

Consider the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \(dX_t = -\kappa X_t \, dt + \sigma \, dW_t\) with constant \(\kappa, \sigma > 0\). Formulate the Feynman–Kac PDE for the bond price \(u(x, t) = \E_{t,x}\left[ \exp\left( -\int_t^T X_s \, ds \right) \right]\). State the terminal condition.

Solution

Here the state variable is the short rate \(X_s\), the drift is \(\mu(x) = -\kappa x\), the diffusion is \(\sigma(x) = \sigma\), and the discount rate is \(r(x) = x\). The terminal payoff is \(\psi(x) \equiv 1\), with running source \(g \equiv 0\).

Applying the Feynman–Kac theorem gives the Vasicek zero-coupon bond PDE:

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} - \kappa x \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} - x u = 0, \quad u(x, T) = 1. \]
P2 · Non-negativity and maximum principle

Prove that if \(\psi(x) \ge 0\) and \(g(x, t) \ge 0\) everywhere, and \(r(x, t) \ge 0\), then any classical solution \(u(x, t)\) to the Feynman–Kac PDE must satisfy \(u(x, t) \ge 0\) for all \((x, t) \in \R^d \times [0, T]\).

Solution

By the Feynman–Kac representation theorem (Theorem 3.2):

\[ u(x, t) = \E_{t,x}\left[ \exp\left(-\int_t^T r(X_s, s) \, ds\right) \psi(X_T) + \int_t^T \exp\left(-\int_t^s r(X_v, v) \, dv\right) g(X_s, s) \, ds \right]. \]

Because exponential functions are strictly positive (\(\exp(\cdot) > 0\)), \(\psi(X_T) \ge 0\) almost surely, and \(g(X_s, s) \ge 0\) almost surely, the integrand and terminal term inside the conditional expectation are non-negative random variables almost surely. By the monotonicity of conditional expectation, \(\E_{t,x}[Y] \ge 0\) whenever \(Y \ge 0\) a.s. Thus \(u(x, t) \ge 0\). This proves the maximum principle from a probabilistic standpoint.

P3 · Merton continuous dividend PDE derivation

Suppose an asset pays a continuous dividend yield \(q \ge 0\). Show how the risk-neutral SDE changes and derive the modified Black–Scholes PDE via the Feynman–Kac formula.

Solution

Under a continuous dividend yield \(q\), the total return of the stock (capital gains plus dividends) must equal the risk-free rate under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\): \(d S_t + q S_t dt = r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\). Thus the risk-neutral SDE for the spot price is:

\[ dS_t = (r - q) S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}. \]

The drift is \(\mu(S) = (r - q) S\). Applying Feynman–Kac with discounting at rate \(r\):

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + (r - q) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - r V = 0, \quad V(S, T) = \psi(S). \]

This is the Merton (1973) continuous-dividend option pricing PDE.

Empirical

E1 · Monte Carlo vs. PDE dimension crossover

For a multi-asset option on \(d\) correlated stocks, estimate the dimension \(d^*\) where Monte Carlo simulation with \(N = 10^6\) samples achieves a smaller error than a finite difference PDE scheme with \(N_{\text{mesh}} = 100\) grid points per spatial dimension under equal computational budgets.

Solution

A standard finite-difference PDE solver on a \(d\)-dimensional tensor mesh requires solving a system of size \(M = 100^d\). For \(d = 1\), \(M = 100\); for \(d = 2\), \(M = 10^4\); for \(d = 3\), \(M = 10^6\); for \(d \ge 4\), \(M \ge 10^8\), which exceeds typical RAM and compute budgets. Meanwhile, Monte Carlo scales as \(O(d \cdot N)\), where \(N = 10^6\) runs in seconds regardless of \(d\). The crossover point occurs at \(d^* = 3\) to \(4\): beyond 3 spatial dimensions, the Feynman–Kac Monte Carlo representation strictly outperforms tensor-grid PDE discretization.

E2 · Log-moneyness coordinate transformation

Use the log-transformation \(x = \ln(S/K)\) to transform the Black–Scholes PDE into a constant-coefficient PDE, and compute the resulting advection and diffusion coefficients.

Solution

Let \(x = \ln(S/K)\). Using the chain rule, \(S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\) and \(S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\). Substituting into the Black–Scholes PDE:

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} - r u = 0. \]

The constant advection coefficient is \(\nu = r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\) and the diffusion coefficient is \(D = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\).

E3 · Boundary absorption in barrier options

An equity derivative pricer observes that a down-and-out call option price computed via Monte Carlo has a small positive bias compared to the analytical PDE solution when barrier hits are checked only at discrete daily time steps. Explain this discrepancy.

Solution

Discrete monitoring of the barrier allows the asset price to cross below the barrier between observation dates and recover before the next check, failing to register a knockout that continuous monitoring (enforced by PDE boundary conditions \(V(B, t) = 0\)) would capture. This discrete monitoring bias inflates the Monte Carlo price by an amount approximately equal to \(\beta \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} \approx 0.5826 \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t}\) in the effective barrier level.

R1 · Tychonoff non-uniqueness counterexample

Explain how Tychonoff's counterexample demonstrates that the Cauchy problem for the heat equation \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = 0\) with terminal condition \(u(x, T) = 0\) can admit non-zero solutions if growth conditions at infinity are omitted.

Solution

Tychonoff constructed an explicit series solution \(u(x, \tau) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!} g^{(k)}(\tau)\), where \(\tau = T - t\) and \(g(\tau) = \exp(-\tau^{-2})\) for \(\tau > 0\) and \(0\) for \(\tau \le 0\). Because all derivatives of \(g\) vanish at \(\tau = 0\), the terminal condition \(u(x, 0) \equiv 0\) is satisfied. However, for \(\tau > 0\), the series converges to a non-zero solution that grows faster than \(\exp(c x^2)\) as \(|x| \to \infty\). The Feynman–Kac stochastic representation \(\E_{t,x}[0] = 0\) rules out this pathology by enforcing \(L^2\) martingale integrability.

R2 · Bismut–Elworthy–Li formula for Greeks

Contrast computing option Delta \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\) by finite-differencing the PDE solution versus computing Delta via the Bismut–Elworthy–Li Malliavin weight formula in Monte Carlo.

Solution

In PDE finite differences, Delta is computed by central difference \(\frac{V_{j+1} - V_{j-1}}{2\Delta S}\), which has \(O(\Delta S^2)\) truncation error and requires no extra solves since the full grid is available. In Monte Carlo, pathwise differentiation fails for discontinuous payoffs (such as digital options) because the derivative \(\psi'(S)\) is a Dirac delta. The Bismut–Elworthy–Li formula expresses Delta as an expectation without payoff derivatives: \(\Delta = e^{-r(T-t)} \E[\psi(S_T) \frac{W_T}{S \sigma T}]\), enabling smooth Greek estimates directly from discontinuous terminal payouts.

R3 · Variational inequalities for American early exercise

Formulate the obstacle problem satisfied by an American put option and explain why it cannot be expressed as a single classical linear Feynman–Kac expectation.

Solution

An American option allows early exercise at any stopping time \(\tau \in [t, T]\). The value function satisfies the linear complementarity problem \(\max\left\{ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \mathcal{A} V - r V, \; (K - S)^+ - V \right\} = 0\). The state space is partitioned into a continuation region \(\mathcal{C}\) where \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \mathcal{A} V - r V = 0\) and an exercise region \(\mathcal{E}\) where \(V(S, t) = K - S\). The boundary \(\partial \mathcal{C}\) is unknown a priori (free boundary). A single linear expectation cannot capture this state-dependent optimal stopping without solving for the optimal stopping time \(\tau^*\) (Snell envelope).

8Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Feynman–Kac theorem, what role does the square-integrability assumption on the gradient play?

    Options

    Square integrability guarantees that the expectation of the stochastic integral is 0, so the discounted process is a true martingale.

  2. When substituting \(\tau = T - t\) into the backward Black–Scholes PDE, what happens to the sign of \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\)?

    Options

    By the chain rule, \(\frac{\partial \tau}{\partial t} = -1\), transforming the backward PDE into a forward evolution equation \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} = \mathcal{A} u - r u\).

  3. Why cannot the classical linear Feynman–Kac theorem be directly applied to American options?

    Options

    Early exercise creates an optimal stopping problem, leading to a variational inequality (obstacle problem) rather than a linear PDE.

  4. Which risk-neutral drift \(\mu(S)\) appears in the Black–Scholes PDE for a stock with continuous dividend yield \(q\)?

    Options

    Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), total return equals \(r\), so price drift is \((r - q)S\).

  5. In the terminal condition \(u(x, T) = 0\) for the backward heat equation with no source term, what is the unique bounded classical solution at \(t < T\)?

    By the Feynman–Kac formula, \(u(x, t) = \E[0] = 0\).

  6. What happens if a numerical solver attempts to integrate the backward heat equation forward in calendar time \(t\)?

    Options

    Forward marching on a negative diffusion operator amplifies high frequencies exponentially, causing catastrophic blow-up.

  7. In the stopped Feynman–Kac formula for an up-and-out call option on domain \((0, H)\), what boundary condition holds at \(S = H\)?

    Options

    Upon touching the knockout barrier \(H\), the option payoff is 0, giving a Dirichlet boundary condition \(V(H, t) = 0\).

  8. What is the standard error convergence rate of Monte Carlo evaluation of the Feynman–Kac formula in \(d\) dimensions?

    Options

    By the central limit theorem, Monte Carlo standard error is \(O(N^{-1/2})\) regardless of dimension \(d\).
