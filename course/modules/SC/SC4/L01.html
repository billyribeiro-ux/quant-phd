The Infinitesimal Generator & Kolmogorov Backward Equation

Derivative pricing evaluates expected future cash flows conditioned on today's state. The infinitesimal generator captures how a diffusion acts locally on smooth test functions. Through Dynkin's formula, expectations of terminal payoffs over continuous paths collapse into linear parabolic partial differential equations. The Kolmogorov backward equation governs how option values evolve as the observation time and current state change, providing the analytical foundation for deterministic PDE pricing schemes.

1The Infinitesimal Generator of an Itô Diffusion

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \Prob)\) be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions, carrying a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion \((B_t)_{t \ge 0}\). Consider the autonomous stochastic differential equation:

\[ dX_t = \mu(X_t) \, dt + \sigma(X_t) \, dB_t, \quad X_0 = x \in \R, \]

where drift \(\mu: \R \to \R\) and dispersion \(\sigma: \R \to \R\) satisfy global Lipschitz and linear growth conditions, ensuring the existence of a unique strong Markov solution (Theorem 6.1 in SC2).

Definition 1.1 (Infinitesimal Generator)

Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a time-homogeneous Markov diffusion. The infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{A}\) is the linear operator defined on the domain \(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}) \subset C_0(\R)\) by:

\[ \mathcal{A} f(x) \coloneqq \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \frac{\E^x[f(X_t)] - f(x)}{t}, \]

where \(\E^x[\,\cdot\,] \coloneqq \E[\,\cdot \mid X_0 = x]\). The domain \(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\) consists of all functions \(f \in C_0(\R)\) for which this limit exists uniformly in \(x\) in the Banach space \((C_0(\R), \|\cdot\|_\infty)\).

Theorem 1.2 (Differential Operator Representation of the Generator)

Assumptions: (A1) \(X_t\) satisfies \(dX_t = \mu(X_t) dt + \sigma(X_t) dB_t\) with Lipschitz coefficients; (A2) \(f \in C_b^2(\R)\) (twice continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives).

Statement: The function \(f\) belongs to \(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\), and the generator is the second-order linear differential operator:

\[ \mathcal{A} f(x) = \mu(x) f'(x) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(x) f''(x). \]
Proof

By Itô's formula for \(f(X_t)\):

\[ f(X_t) - f(x) = \int_0^t \left(\mu(X_s) f'(X_s) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(X_s) f''(X_s)\right) ds + \int_0^t \sigma(X_s) f'(X_s) dB_s. \]

Since \(f' \in C_b(\R)\) and \(\sigma\) satisfies linear growth, \(\E^x\left[\int_0^t \sigma^2(X_s) (f'(X_s))^2 ds\right] < \infty\), so the stochastic integral is a true square-integrable martingale with zero expectation. Taking conditional expectations \(\E^x[\,\cdot\,]\):

\[ \E^x[f(X_t)] - f(x) = \E^x\left[\int_0^t \left(\mu(X_s) f'(X_s) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(X_s) f''(X_s)\right) ds\right]. \]

Dividing by \(t\) and taking the limit as \(t \downarrow 0\), path continuity of \(X_s\) and the dominated convergence theorem imply:

\[ \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \frac{\E^x[f(X_t)] - f(x)}{t} = \mu(x) f'(x) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(x) f''(x), \]

establishing the differential operator representation.

Example 1.3 (Generators of Canonical Financial Processes)

For standard Brownian motion \(dX_t = dB_t\), the generator is the Laplace operator \(\mathcal{A} f = \tfrac{1}{2} f''\). For geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\), \(\mathcal{A} f(s) = \mu s f'(s) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 s^2 f''(s)\). For the mean-reverting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \(dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t) dt + \sigma dB_t\), \(\mathcal{A} f(x) = \kappa(\theta - x) f'(x) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 f''(x)\).

2Dynkin's Formula and Martingale Properties

Theorem 1.4 (Dynkin's Formula)

Assumptions: (A1) \(X_t\) is an Itô diffusion with generator \(\mathcal{A}\); (A2) \(f \in C_b^2(\R)\); (A3) \(\tau\) is an almost surely finite stopping time with \(\E^x[\tau] < \infty\).

Statement: The expected value of \(f(X_\tau)\) satisfies:

\[ \E^x[f(X_\tau)] = f(x) + \E^x\left[\int_0^\tau \mathcal{A} f(X_s) \, ds\right]. \]
Proof

Define the compensated process \(M_t \coloneqq f(X_t) - f(x) - \int_0^t \mathcal{A} f(X_s) ds\). By Theorem 1.2, \(M_t = \int_0^t \sigma(X_s) f'(X_s) dB_s\), which is a continuous local martingale. Boundedness of \(f'\) guarantees that \(M_t\) is a true martingale. Applying Doob's optional stopping theorem to \(M_{t \wedge \tau}\) and taking \(t \to \infty\) under bounded stopping time expectation yields \(\E^x[M_\tau] = 0\), whence Dynkin's identity follows immediately.

Remark 1.5 (Dynkin's Formula as Stochastic Fundamental Theorem of Calculus)

Dynkin's formula generalizes the fundamental theorem of calculus \(f(t) - f(0) = \int_0^t f'(s) ds\) to stochastic processes. The deterministic derivative \(f'\) is replaced by the generator \(\mathcal{A}\), and ordinary integration is integrated under the expectation operator \(\E^x[\,\cdot\,]\).

3The Kolmogorov Backward Equation

Consider the conditional expectation of a terminal payoff \(g(X_H)\) evaluated at time \(t \in [0, H]\) given current state \(X_t = x\):

\[ u(t, x) \coloneqq \E[g(X_H) \mid X_t = x]. \]
Theorem 1.6 (Kolmogorov Backward Equation for Terminal Value Problems)

Assumptions: (A1) \(X_t\) is a Markov diffusion with generator \(\mathcal{A} = \mu(x)\partial_x + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2(x)\partial_{xx}\); (A2) \(g \in C_b^2(\R)\); (A3) \(\mu, \sigma\) are smooth with bounded derivatives.

Statement: The value function \(u(t, x)\) is continuously differentiable in \(t\) and twice continuously differentiable in \(x\) on \([0, H) \times \R\), and satisfies the Kolmogorov backward equation:

\[ \partial_t u(t, x) + \mathcal{A} u(t, x) = 0, \quad (t, x) \in [0, H) \times \R, \]

subject to the terminal Cauchy condition \(u(H, x) = g(x)\).

Proof

By the Markov property and time-homogeneity of the diffusion increments, \(u(t, x) = \E^x[g(X_{H-t})]\). Define \(\phi(\tau, x) \coloneqq \E^x[g(X_\tau)]\) for \(\tau = H - t \ge 0\). Using the semigroup property \(P_{\tau+h} = P_h P_\tau\):

\[ \frac{\phi(\tau + h, x) - \phi(\tau, x)}{h} = \frac{\E^x[\phi(\tau, X_h)] - \phi(\tau, x)}{h}. \]

Taking \(h \downarrow 0\), the right-hand side converges by definition of the generator to \(\mathcal{A} \phi(\tau, x)\). Thus \(\partial_\tau \phi(\tau, x) = \mathcal{A} \phi(\tau, x)\). Since \(\tau = H - t\), the chain rule gives \(\partial_t u = -\partial_\tau \phi\). Substituting yields:

\[ -\partial_t u(t, x) = \mathcal{A} u(t, x) \iff \partial_t u(t, x) + \mathcal{A} u(t, x) = 0. \]

At \(t = H\), \(\tau = 0\), so \(u(H, x) = \E^x[g(X_0)] = g(x)\), completing the proof.

Definition 1.7 (Transition Probability Density Backward Equation)

Let \(p(t, x; T, y)\) denote the transition probability density of reaching state \(y\) at future time \(T > t\) starting from \(x\) at time \(t\). The transition density satisfies the backward equation with respect to the initial (backward) variables \((t, x)\):

\[ \partial_t p(t, x; T, y) + \mu(x)\partial_x p(t, x; T, y) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(x)\partial_{xx} p(t, x; T, y) = 0, \quad t < T, \]

subject to the singular Dirac terminal condition \(\lim_{t \uparrow T} p(t, x; T, y) = \delta(x - y)\).

Lab 1 · Verification of the Kolmogorov Backward Equation for Geometric Brownian Motion

The Black–Scholes call pricing formula \(u(t, x)\) satisfies the backward differential operator \(\partial_t u + r x \partial_x u + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 x^2 \partial_{xx} u - r u = 0\) to machine precision (residual < 1e-12) across spatial prices \(x \in [80, 120]\) at \(t = 0.25\) (SIMULATED).

"""Lab SC4-L01-1 · Verification of Kolmogorov Backward Equation for Geometric Brownian Motion.

Checks that the analytical Black-Scholes call pricing function satisfies the Kolmogorov backward PDE:
    partial_t u + r * x * partial_x u + 0.5 * sigma^2 * x^2 * partial_xx u - r * u = 0
across spatial prices x in [80, 120] at calendar time t = 0.25 with horizon H = 1.0 (SIMULATED).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def norm_pdf(x: float) -> float:
    return math.exp(-0.5 * x * x) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)


def bs_call_and_derivatives(
    x: float, k: float, t: float, horizon: float, r: float, sigma: float
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Compute call price u, and partial derivatives u_t, u_x, u_xx."""
    tau = horizon - t  # time to maturity
    sqrt_tau = math.sqrt(tau)
    d1 = (math.log(x / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * tau) / (sigma * sqrt_tau)
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sqrt_tau

    disc = math.exp(-r * tau)
    price = x * norm_cdf(d1) - k * disc * norm_cdf(d2)

    # Derivatives with respect to backward variables (t, x)
    # Note: partial_t u = - partial_tau u
    theta = -(x * norm_pdf(d1) * sigma) / (2.0 * sqrt_tau) - r * k * disc * norm_cdf(d2)
    u_t = theta
    u_x = norm_cdf(d1)
    u_xx = norm_pdf(d1) / (x * sigma * sqrt_tau)

    return price, u_t, u_x, u_xx


def main() -> None:
    k = 100.0
    r = 0.05
    sigma = 0.20
    horizon = 1.0
    t = 0.25

    spot_values = np.linspace(80.0, 120.0, 9)
    residuals: list[float] = []

    for x in spot_values:
        u, u_t, u_x, u_xx = bs_call_and_derivatives(float(x), k, t, horizon, r, sigma)
        kbe_operator = u_t + r * x * u_x + 0.5 * (sigma**2) * (x**2) * u_xx - r * u
        residuals.append(abs(kbe_operator))

    max_residual = max(residuals)
    mean_residual = sum(residuals) / len(residuals)

    print(f"KBE check across {len(spot_values)} spot prices in [{spot_values[0]:.1f}, {spot_values[-1]:.1f}]:")
    print(f"Maximum absolute KBE residual: {max_residual:.4e}")
    print(f"Mean absolute KBE residual:    {mean_residual:.4e}")
    print(f"Residual vanishes within 1e-12: {max_residual < 1e-12}")


main()
KBE check across 9 spot prices in [80.0, 120.0]:
Maximum absolute KBE residual: 8.8818e-16
Mean absolute KBE residual:    3.9783e-16
Residual vanishes within 1e-12: True

The numerical residual vanishes to within floating-point roundoff (on the order of 1e-15), verifying that the analytical call price is an exact classical solution to the Kolmogorov backward equation.

4Where the canon breaks

Classical parabolic PDE theory requires uniform parabolicity: there must exist \(\lambda_0 > 0\) such that \(\sigma^2(x) \ge \lambda_0 > 0\) for all \(x \in \R\). In quantitative finance, canonical models violate this everywhere. In geometric Brownian motion, \(\sigma(x) = \sigma x\), so the diffusion coefficient vanishes at \(x = 0\), degenerating into a first-order hyperbolic equation. In stochastic volatility models (Heston), \(\sigma(v) = \xi \sqrt{v}\) vanishes at zero variance. When parabolicity degenerates, boundary conditions cannot be prescribed arbitrarily: the Feller boundary classification dictates whether boundary conditions are legally required or over-determined. Furthermore, when coefficients lack Lipschitz regularity, the Kolmogorov backward equation admits non-unique weak solutions, and classical smooth solutions fail to exist.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The Kolmogorov backward equation evolves forward in calendar time from an initial condition at \(t = 0\).”

    The backward equation operates backward in calendar time from a terminal payoff condition at \(t = H\). The partial derivative \(\partial_t\) has positive sign alongside \(\mathcal{A}\), reflecting that as calendar time \(t\) advances toward \(H\), the remaining time-to-maturity decreases.

  2. “The infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{A}\) can be applied directly to any financial payoff function \(g(x)\).”

    Standard European option payoffs (such as \(\max(S - K, 0)\)) have discontinuous first derivatives at the strike \(S = K\), so \(g''(K)\) is undefined in the classical sense. The generator is strictly defined on \(C^2\) domains; applying it to non-smooth payoffs requires Sobolev weak formulations or viscosity solutions.

  3. “The transition density \(p(t, x; T, y)\) satisfies the backward equation with respect to the future destination state \(y\).”

    The backward equation differentiates exclusively with respect to the initial observation coordinates \((t, x)\). Evolution with respect to destination variables \((T, y)\) is governed by the forward (Fokker–Planck) equation, which features the adjoint operator \(\mathcal{A}^*\).

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Infinitesimal generator calculation

Compute \(\mathcal{A} f(x)\) for \(f(x) = x^3\) under the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \(dX_t = \kappa(\theta - X_t) dt + \sigma dB_t\).

Solution

The first and second derivatives of \(f(x) = x^3\) are \(f'(x) = 3x^2\) and \(f''(x) = 6x\). By Theorem 1.2:

\[ \mathcal{A} f(x) = \kappa(\theta - x)(3x^2) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 (6x) = 3\kappa\theta x^2 - 3\kappa x^3 + 3\sigma^2 x. \]

The generator produces a third-degree polynomial with mean-reverting drift damping.

M2 · Dynkin formula stopping expectation

Let \(B_t\) be standard Brownian motion starting at 0, and let \(\tau = \inf\{t \ge 0 : B_t \notin (-a, b)\}\) for \(a, b > 0\). Using \(f(x) = x^2\) and Dynkin's formula, compute \(\E[\tau]\).

Solution

For standard Brownian motion, \(\mathcal{A} f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} f''(x)\). For \(f(x) = x^2\), \(f''(x) = 2\), so \(\mathcal{A} f(x) = 1\). By Dynkin's formula (Theorem 1.4):

\[ \E^0[f(B_\tau)] = f(0) + \E^0\left[\int_0^\tau 1 \, ds\right] \implies \E^0[B_\tau^2] = 0 + \E^0[\tau]. \]

Since \(B_\tau\) takes value \(-a\) with probability \(b/(a+b)\) and \(b\) with probability \(a/(a+b)\), we have \(\E[B_\tau^2] = a^2 \frac{b}{a+b} + b^2 \frac{a}{a+b} = \frac{ab(a+b)}{a+b} = ab\). Hence \(\E[\tau] = ab\).

M3 · Time reversal transformation

Show that the time substitution \(\tau = H - t\) transforms the backward equation \(\partial_t u + \mathcal{A} u = 0\) with terminal condition \(u(H, x) = g(x)\) into a forward initial-value parabolic equation.

Solution

Define \(v(\tau, x) \coloneqq u(H - \tau, x)\). By the chain rule, \(\partial_\tau v = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} \frac{dt}{d\tau} = -\partial_t u\). Substituting into the backward equation yields \(-\partial_\tau v + \mathcal{A} v = 0 \implies \partial_\tau v = \mathcal{A} v\). At \(\tau = 0\), \(t = H\), so the initial condition is \(v(0, x) = u(H, x) = g(x)\). The backward PDE becomes a forward diffusion initial-value problem.

P1 · Generator domain inclusion for polynomials

Prove that for the geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\), every polynomial \(f(s) = s^m\) (\(m \in \N\)) belongs to the local domain of the generator, and compute \(\mathcal{A} s^m\).

Solution

For \(f(s) = s^m\), the derivatives are \(f'(s) = m s^{m-1}\) and \(f''(s) = m(m-1) s^{m-2}\). Both are continuous on \((0, \infty)\). Applying Itô's formula to \(S_t^m\):

\[ d(S_t^m) = \left( \mu S_t \cdot m S_t^{m-1} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S_t^2 \cdot m(m-1) S_t^{m-2} \right) dt + m \sigma S_t^m dB_t = \left( m\mu + \frac{1}{2}m(m-1)\sigma^2 \right) S_t^m dt + m\sigma S_t^m dB_t. \]

Taking expectations, \(\E^s[S_t^m] = s^m \exp\left(\left(m\mu + \frac{1}{2}m(m-1)\sigma^2\right) t\right)\). Differentiating at \(t=0\) gives \(\lim_{t \downarrow 0} \frac{\E^s[S_t^m] - s^m}{t} = \left(m\mu + \frac{1}{2}m(m-1)\sigma^2\right) s^m\), establishing domain inclusion.

P2 · Maximum principle for the backward equation

Prove that if \(u(t, x)\) satisfies \(\partial_t u + \mathcal{A} u = 0\) on \([0, H) \times \R\) with bounded terminal data \(g(x) \ge 0\), then \(u(t, x) \ge 0\) everywhere on \([0, H] \times \R\).

Solution

By Theorem 1.6, \(u(t, x) = \E[g(X_H) \mid X_t = x]\). Since \(g(y) \ge 0\) for all \(y \in \R\) and probability measures are non-negative, the conditional expectation of a non-negative random variable is non-negative almost surely: \(\E[g(X_H) \mid X_t = x] \ge 0\). This proves the non-negativity property and establishes the weak maximum principle for the backward parabolic operator.

P3 · Uniqueness of classical solutions via energy methods

Prove that the terminal value problem \(\partial_t u + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \partial_{xx} u = 0\) on \([0, H) \times \R\) with \(u(H, x) = 0\) and compact spatial support has only the trivial solution \(u \equiv 0\).

Solution

Consider the backward energy functional \(E(t) = \int_\R u^2(t, x) dx\). Differentiating with respect to \(t\):

\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = 2 \int_\R u \partial_t u \, dx = -2 \int_\R u \left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \partial_{xx} u\right) dx = \sigma^2 \int_\R (\partial_x u)^2 dx \ge 0, \]

using integration by parts with vanishing boundary terms. Thus \(E(t)\) is a non-decreasing function of \(t\). At terminal time \(t = H\), \(u(H, x) = 0\), so \(E(H) = 0\). Since \(E(t) \ge 0\) and \(E(t) \le E(H) = 0\), it follows that \(E(t) = 0\) for all \(t \in [0, H]\), proving \(u(t, x) = 0\) identically.

Empirical

E1 · Numerical verification of generator on Gaussian test function

For \(dX_t = -0.5 X_t dt + 0.3 dB_t\) and \(f(x) = e^{-x^2}\), evaluate \(\mathcal{A} f(1.0)\) analytically and compare with a discrete difference quotient with \(h = 10^{-4}\).

Solution

The derivatives are \(f'(x) = -2x e^{-x^2}\) and \(f''(x) = (4x^2 - 2)e^{-x^2}\). At \(x = 1.0\), \(f'(1) = -2e^{-1}\) and \(f''(1) = 2e^{-1}\). The analytical generator value is:

\[ \mathcal{A} f(1) = (-0.5 \cdot 1)(-2e^{-1}) + \frac{1}{2}(0.09)(2e^{-1}) = (1.0 + 0.09)e^{-1} = 1.09 e^{-1} \approx 0.400985. \]

A second-order central difference quotient \(\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}\) and \(\frac{f(x+h) - 2f(x) + f(x-h)}{h^2}\) with \(h = 10^{-4}\) reproduces this value to 8 significant figures.

E2 · Monte Carlo verification of Dynkin's identity

For standard Brownian motion on \([-1, 2]\), simulate 10,000 paths to estimate exit time \(\tau\). What is the theoretical exit time and its Monte Carlo standard error?

Solution

By Problem M2, the exact expected exit time for \(a = 1, b = 2\) is \(\E[\tau] = ab = 1 \times 2 = 2.0\). For an exit time distribution with variance \(\Var(\tau) \approx \tfrac{2}{3} ab(a^2 + b^2 - ab) = \tfrac{2}{3}(2)(1 + 4 - 2) = 4.0\), a sample of \(N = 10{,}000\) paths produces a standard error of \(\se \approx \sqrt{4.0 / 10000} = 0.02\). The sample mean will fall in \([1.94, 2.06]\) with 99.7% confidence.

E3 · Drift distortion in backward equation

Suppose an analyst erroneously omits the Itô convexity correction \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 x^2 \partial_{xx} u\) when solving the backward equation for a call option. What is the impact on option prices?

Solution

Omitting the second-order diffusion term reduces the parabolic PDE to a first-order transport equation \(\partial_t u + r x \partial_x u - r u = 0\). The solution to this transport equation is the intrinsic forward value \(e^{-r(H-t)} \max(x e^{r(H-t)} - K, 0) = \max(x - K e^{-r(H-t)}, 0)\). The option's volatility time value is completely erased, underpricing the option by the full value of the Black–Scholes optionality premium.

Research

R1 · Hypoelliptic generators and Hörmander's condition

Consider the two-dimensional degenerate diffusion \(dX_t = Y_t dt\) and \(dY_t = -Y_t dt + dB_t\). Explain why the generator \(\mathcal{A} = y \partial_x - y \partial_y + \tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{yy}\) lacks uniform parabolicity yet possesses a smooth transition density.

Solution

The diffusion acts only in the \(y\)-coordinate (\(\sigma_1 = 0, \sigma_2 = 1\)), so the diffusion matrix is rank 1 in \(\R^2\). However, the vector fields \(V_0 = y \partial_x - y \partial_y\) and \(V_1 = \partial_y\) satisfy Hörmander's bracket condition: the Lie bracket is \([V_0, V_1] = V_0 V_1 - V_1 V_0 = -\partial_x + \partial_y\). The span of \(\{V_1, [V_0, V_1]\}\) is \(\R^2\) everywhere. By Hörmander's theorem, the operator \(\partial_t + \mathcal{A}\) is hypoelliptic, guaranteeing that the transition density \(p(t, x, y)\) is \(C^\infty\) despite spatial degeneracy.

R2 · Transition density short-time asymptotics

Explain how the Riemannian distance metric \(d(x, y)\) induced by the inverse diffusion tensor \(\sigma^{-2}(x)\) determines the short-time asymptotic behavior of the transition density \(\lim_{t \downarrow 0} t \log p(0, x; t, y)\).

Solution

By Varadhan's large deviations theorem, the short-time behavior of diffusion transition densities is governed by the geodesic distance on the Riemannian manifold with metric tensor \(g_{ij}(x) = (\sigma \sigma^T)^{-1}_{ij}\):

\[ \lim_{t \downarrow 0} 2t \log p(0, x; t, y) = -d^2(x, y), \]

where \(d(x, y) = \int_x^y \frac{1}{\sigma(s)} ds\). In financial terms, this implies that deep out-of-the-money option prices at short expiries decay exponentially according to the geodesic distance under the volatility metric.

R3 · Boundary classification of Feller

For the CEV process \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t^\gamma dB_t\) with \(\gamma < 1\), determine the nature of the boundary at \(S = 0\) using Feller's scale and speed measures.

Solution

The scale density is \(s(x) = \exp\left(-\int^x \frac{2\mu y}{\sigma^2 y^{2\gamma}} dy\right)\). When \(\gamma < 1\), the boundary at \(S = 0\) is an exit boundary if \(\mu \le 0\) or \(\gamma < 1/2\): the asset price can reach zero in finite time with positive probability. Once at zero, the process must be absorbed (\(S_t = 0\) for all subsequent \(t\)) to prevent negative asset prices and arbitrage, requiring an explicit Dirichlet absorbing condition \(u(t, 0) = g(0)\) in the backward equation.

  1. What is the defining limit of the infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{A} f(x)\)?

    Options

    By Definition 1.1, the infinitesimal generator measures the initial rate of change of the conditional expectation of \(f(X_t)\).

  2. For standard Brownian motion \(B_t\), what is the generator value \(\mathcal{A} f(x)\) applied to the linear function \(f(x) = 5x + 3\)?

    For standard Brownian motion, \(\mathcal{A} f = \tfrac{1}{2} f''\). Since \(f''(x) = 0\), \(\mathcal{A} f = 0\).

  3. What is the terminal condition for the Kolmogorov backward equation \(u(t, x) = \E[g(X_H) \mid X_t = x]\) at \(t = H\)?

    Options

    At terminal horizon \(t = H\), the remaining time is zero, so \(u(H, x) = \E[g(X_H) \mid X_H = x] = g(x)\).

  4. Under Dynkin's formula, which stochastic process is a martingale?

    Options

    Subtracting the integrated drift \(\int_0^t \mathcal{A} f(X_s) ds\) compensates \(f(X_t)\), leaving the Itô diffusion martingale part.

  5. For standard Brownian motion on \([-1, 2]\), what is the expected exit time \(\E[\tau]\)?

    By Problem M2, \(\E[\tau] = a \cdot b = 1 \cdot 2 = 2\).

  6. Why is the Kolmogorov backward equation written with a positive \(\partial_t\) derivative (\(\partial_t u + \mathcal{A} u = 0\))?

    Options

    The substitution \(\tau = H - t\) gives \(\partial_t u = -\partial_\tau u\), yielding the standard forward diffusion \(\partial_\tau u = \mathcal{A} u\).

  7. Which variables in the transition density \(p(t, x; T, y)\) does the backward equation differentiate with respect to?

    Options

    The backward equation operates backward from the initial point \((t, x)\), while the forward equation differentiates with respect to \((T, y)\).

  8. What happens to the parabolicity of the Black–Scholes PDE as the spot price approaches \(S = 0\)?

    Options

    At \(S = 0\), \(\sigma^2 S^2 = 0\), destroying uniform parabolicity and reducing the PDE to a first-order degenerate boundary.
