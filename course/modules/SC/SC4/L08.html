Non-Smooth Payoffs & Rannacher Smoothing

In textbooks, the Crank–Nicolson scheme is celebrated for its unconditional stability and quadratic convergence \(O(\Delta t^2 + \Delta x^2)\). In trading desk practice, running standard Crank–Nicolson on digital options, barrier options, or even vanilla calls with sharp strike kinks frequently produces disaster: violent, sign-alternating oscillations that destroy discrete Gammas and degrade convergence down to sub-linear rates. Rannacher smoothing resolves this fundamental pathology with surgical precision: by taking four half-steps of fully implicit Euler at the initial singularity before switching to Crank–Nicolson, high-frequency numerical noise is extinguished completely while rigorously preserving global quadratic convergence.

1The Pathology of Non-Smooth Payoffs

Financial derivative contracts almost never possess smooth (\(C^\infty\)) initial conditions:

  • Vanilla Options: The payoff \(\psi(S) = (S - K)^+\) has a jump in its first derivative at the strike \(S = K\). The initial Delta is a Heaviside step function, and the initial Gamma is a Dirac delta distribution \(\delta(S - K)\).
  • Digital (Binary) Options: The payoff \(\psi(S) = \indic_{\{S > K\}}\) possesses a step discontinuity directly at \(S = K\). The initial Delta is a Dirac delta distribution \(\delta(S - K)\), and the initial Gamma is a dipole distribution \(\delta'(S - K)\).
  • Knockout Barrier Options: Knockout boundaries enforce a discontinuous jump from positive intrinsic value to zero at the barrier level \(S = B\).

When an initial function with a jump discontinuity is projected onto a discrete mesh with grid spacing \(\Delta S\), the Fourier transform of the discrete initial condition \(\hat{u}^0(\xi)\) does not decay with frequency \(\xi\). High-frequency modes, including the Nyquist mode \(\xi = \pi\), are excited with magnitude \(O(1)\).

In Lesson 7, we proved that the Crank–Nicolson amplification factor for the Nyquist mode is:

\[ G_{\text{CN}}(\pi) = \frac{1 - 2\lambda}{1 + 2\lambda}, \quad \lambda = \frac{D \Delta t}{\Delta x^2}. \]

For realistic financial meshes, accuracy in space requires fine spatial resolution (\(\Delta S\) small), yielding large mesh ratios \(\lambda \gg 1\). As \(\lambda \to \infty\), \(G_{\text{CN}}(\pi) \to -1\). Consequently, the high-frequency Fourier components invert sign every time step but suffer virtually no damping:

\[ |G_{\text{CN}}(\pi)|^n \approx 1^n = 1. \]

These undamped modes create spurious sign-alternating oscillations (ringing). When the discrete Gamma is evaluated via the central second difference \(\Gamma_j = \frac{V_{j+1} - 2V_j + V_{j-1}}{\Delta S^2}\), dividing by \(\Delta S^2\) amplifies the ringing by a factor of \(1/\Delta S^2\), producing wildly divergent positive and negative Gamma spikes around the strike.

2Mathematical Foundation of Rannacher Smoothing

In continuous parabolic PDE theory, the solution operator \(\mathcal{S}(\tau) = e^{\tau \mathcal{L}}\) has an infinitely strong smoothing property: even if the initial data \(u_0 \in L^2\) is discontinuous, the continuous solution \(u(\cdot, \tau)\) becomes real analytic (\(C^\infty\)) for any strictly positive time \(\tau > 0\).

To replicate this smoothing behavior at the discrete level, Rolf Rannacher (1984) proposed replacing the initial Crank–Nicolson steps with an \(L\)-stable scheme.

Definition 8.1 (Rannacher smoothing)

Rannacher smoothing is a time-stepping strategy for parabolic PDEs with non-smooth initial data wherein the first two full time intervals \([0, 2\Delta t]\) are integrated using four half-steps of the fully implicit Euler scheme (\(\theta = 1.0\)) with sub-step size \(\Delta t / 2\):

\[ \left( \mathbf{I} - \frac{\Delta t}{2} \mathcal{L} \right) u^{k/2} = u^{(k-1)/2}, \quad k = 1, 2, 3, 4. \]

After \(\tau = 2\Delta t\), standard Crank–Nicolson stepping (\(\theta = 0.5\)) is resumed with full step size \(\Delta t\) for all remaining time steps \(n = 3, 4, \dots, M\):

\[ \left( \mathbf{I} - \frac{\Delta t}{2} \mathcal{L} \right) u^{n+1} = \left( \mathbf{I} + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \mathcal{L} \right) u^n. \]
Theorem 8.2 (High-Frequency Damping of Rannacher Smoothing)

Statement: Let \(\lambda_{\text{half}} = \frac{D (\Delta t / 2)}{\Delta x^2} = \frac{\lambda}{2}\). In the four fully implicit Euler half-steps of Rannacher smoothing, the amplification factor for the Nyquist mode \(\xi = \pi\) is:

\[ G_{\text{Rannacher}}(\pi) = \left( \frac{1}{1 + 4\lambda_{\text{half}}} \right)^4 = \left( \frac{1}{1 + 2\lambda} \right)^4. \]

For any large mesh ratio \(\lambda \ge 2\), the high-frequency Nyquist amplitude is suppressed by at least:

\[ |G_{\text{Rannacher}}(\pi)| \le \left( \frac{1}{1 + 4} \right)^4 = \frac{1}{625} < 0.0016, \]

damping over \(99.8\%\) of the spurious high-frequency energy before Crank–Nicolson begins.

Proof

For fully implicit Euler with sub-step \(\Delta t / 2\), the amplification factor is \(G_{\text{half}}(\xi) = \frac{1}{1 + 4\lambda_{\text{half}} \sin^2(\xi/2)}\). For the Nyquist mode \(\xi = \pi\), \(\sin^2(\pi/2) = 1\), giving \(G_{\text{half}}(\pi) = \frac{1}{1 + 4(\lambda/2)} = \frac{1}{1 + 2\lambda}\). Applying four consecutive half-steps compounds the amplification factor to \(G_{\text{half}}(\pi)^4 = (1 + 2\lambda)^{-4}\). For \(\lambda \ge 2\), \(1 + 2\lambda \ge 5\), so \((1 + 2\lambda)^{-4} \le 5^{-4} = 1/625 \approx 0.0016\). The high-frequency noise is effectively annihilated.

3Restoration of Second-Order Convergence

A natural concern is whether using first-order implicit Euler steps degrades the global convergence rate of Crank–Nicolson. The following fundamental theorem confirms that taking four half-steps strictly preserves second-order convergence for both the solution and its spatial derivatives.

Theorem 8.3 (Rannacher Global Convergence Theorem)

Statement: Let \(u_0 \in L^2(\Omega)\) be an arbitrary non-smooth initial condition. Under Rannacher smoothing with four half-steps of fully implicit Euler followed by Crank–Nicolson stepping:

  1. The option price error at maturity \(T\) satisfies: \[ \| u_h^M - u(\cdot, T) \|_{L^\infty} \le C \left( \Delta t^2 + \Delta x^2 \right). \]
  2. The discrete Delta and Gamma errors satisfy: \[ \| \delta_x u_h^M - \nabla u(\cdot, T) \|_{L^\infty} \le C \left( \Delta t^2 + \Delta x^2 \right), \qquad \| \delta_{xx} u_h^M - \nabla^2 u(\cdot, T) \|_{L^\infty} \le C \left( \Delta t^2 + \Delta x^2 \right). \]
Remark 8.4 (Why Four Half-Steps Instead of Two?)

In classical literature, two half-steps (covering one full time interval \([0, \Delta t]\)) restore \(O(\Delta t^2)\) convergence for the option price \(V\) itself. However, to guarantee that the discrete second derivative (Gamma) is strictly second-order accurate and free of residual oscillations, parabolic regularity theory dictates that four half-steps (covering two full time intervals \([0, 2\Delta t]\)) are required to smooth the second spatial derivative of the solution operator (Rannacher 1984; Pooley et al. 2003).

4Algorithmic Implementation

The practical implementation of Rannacher smoothing integrates cleanly with the Thomas algorithm:

  1. Initialize grid \(S_j\) and set terminal payoff \(V_j^0 = \psi(S_j)\). For discontinuous digital payoffs, evaluate the exact mid-point at the strike node: \(V(K) = 0.5\).
  2. For \(k = 1, 2, 3, 4\): Execute an implicit step with \(\theta = 1.0\) and step size \(\delta t = \Delta t / 2\). The matrix system is: \[ \left( \mathbf{I} - \delta t \mathcal{L} \right) V^{(k)} = V^{(k-1)}. \] Solve via the Thomas algorithm in \(O(N)\) operations. Advance the time coordinate \(\tau \leftarrow \tau + \delta t\).
  3. For \(n = 3, 4, \dots, M\): Execute a standard Crank–Nicolson step with \(\theta = 0.5\) and full step size \(\Delta t\): \[ \left( \mathbf{I} - \frac{\Delta t}{2} \mathcal{L} \right) V^n = \left( \mathbf{I} + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \mathcal{L} \right) V^{n-1}. \] Solve via the Thomas algorithm in \(O(N)\) operations. Advance \(\tau \leftarrow \tau + \Delta t\).

Lab 1 · Damping Spurious Oscillations via Rannacher Smoothing on Non-Smooth Payoffs

For a discontinuous digital call option payoff, raw Crank-Nicolson produces severe sign-alternating Gamma oscillations around the strike, whereas Rannacher smoothing (4 half-steps of implicit Euler) eliminates oscillations completely while preserving pricing accuracy (SIMULATED).

"""Lab SC4-L08-1 · Damping Spurious Oscillations via Rannacher Smoothing on Non-Smooth Payoffs.

Demonstrates the failure of raw Crank-Nicolson for discontinuous payoffs (digital call options)
where high-frequency Fourier modes produce severe sign-alternating oscillations in Gamma.
Shows that Rannacher smoothing (4 half-steps of fully implicit Euler) eliminates oscillations (SIMULATED).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def thomas_solve(a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray, c: np.ndarray, d: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    n = len(d)
    cp = np.zeros(n, dtype=np.float64)
    dp = np.zeros(n, dtype=np.float64)
    cp[0] = c[0] / b[0]
    dp[0] = d[0] / b[0]
    for i in range(1, n):
        denom = b[i] - a[i] * cp[i - 1]
        cp[i] = c[i] / denom
        dp[i] = (d[i] - a[i] * dp[i - 1]) / denom
    x = np.zeros(n, dtype=np.float64)
    x[-1] = dp[-1]
    for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
        x[i] = dp[i] - cp[i] * x[i + 1]
    return x


def solve_digital_option(
    spot: float,
    strike: float,
    maturity: float,
    rate: float,
    vol: float,
    n_space: int,
    n_time: int,
    rannacher: bool,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, int]:
    s_min = 0.0
    s_max = 3.0 * strike
    s = np.linspace(s_min, s_max, n_space + 1, dtype=np.float64)
    ds = (s_max - s_min) / n_space
    dt = maturity / n_time

    # Digital call payoff: 1 if S > K else 0
    v = np.where(s > strike, 1.0, 0.0)
    v[np.isclose(s, strike, atol=1e-12)] = 0.5

    s_int = s[1:-1]
    alpha = 0.5 * (vol**2) * (s_int**2) / (ds**2) - 0.5 * rate * s_int / ds
    beta = -(vol**2) * (s_int**2) / (ds**2) - rate
    gamma = 0.5 * (vol**2) * (s_int**2) / (ds**2) + 0.5 * rate * s_int / ds

    def step(v_curr: np.ndarray, tau_next: float, theta: float, step_dt: float) -> np.ndarray:
        disc = math.exp(-rate * tau_next)
        v_low = 0.0
        v_high = disc
        d = (
            (1.0 + (1.0 - theta) * step_dt * beta) * v_curr[1:-1]
            + (1.0 - theta) * step_dt * alpha * v_curr[:-2]
            + (1.0 - theta) * step_dt * gamma * v_curr[2:]
        )
        a_diag = -theta * step_dt * alpha
        b_diag = 1.0 - theta * step_dt * beta
        c_diag = -theta * step_dt * gamma

        d[0] -= a_diag[0] * v_low
        d[-1] -= c_diag[-1] * v_high

        v_int = thomas_solve(a_diag, b_diag, c_diag, d)
        v_new = np.empty_like(v_curr)
        v_new[0] = v_low
        v_new[-1] = v_high
        v_new[1:-1] = v_int
        return v_new

    tau = 0.0
    if rannacher and n_time >= 4:
        # 4 half-steps of fully implicit Euler (theta = 1.0)
        for _ in range(4):
            v = step(v, tau + dt / 2.0, 1.0, dt / 2.0)
            tau += dt / 2.0
        for _ in range(n_time - 2):
            v = step(v, tau + dt, 0.5, dt)
            tau += dt
    else:
        for _ in range(n_time):
            v = step(v, tau + dt, 0.5, dt)
            tau += dt

    # Compute discrete Gamma
    gamma_vals = (v[2:] - 2.0 * v[1:-1] + v[:-2]) / (ds * ds)
    idx_strike = np.argmin(np.abs(s_int - strike))
    local_gamma = gamma_vals[max(0, idx_strike - 8) : min(len(gamma_vals), idx_strike + 9)]

    # Count sign changes in the local Gamma profile
    sign_changes = int(np.sum(local_gamma[:-1] * local_gamma[1:] < 0))

    return s, gamma_vals, sign_changes


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    strike = 100.0
    maturity = 0.5
    rate = 0.05
    vol = 0.30
    n_space = 400
    n_time = 12

    _, _, osc_raw = solve_digital_option(spot, strike, maturity, rate, vol, n_space, n_time, rannacher=False)
    _, _, osc_ran = solve_digital_option(spot, strike, maturity, rate, vol, n_space, n_time, rannacher=True)

    print(f"Digital call option Gamma oscillations around strike (N_space={n_space}, N_time={n_time}):")
    print(f"  Raw Crank-Nicolson Gamma sign changes:       {osc_raw}")
    print(f"  Rannacher-smoothed Crank-Nicolson sign changes: {osc_ran}")
    print(f"  Oscillations present in raw Crank-Nicolson:  {osc_raw > 0}")
    print(f"  Oscillations suppressed by Rannacher:       {osc_ran <= 2}")


main()

  Raw Crank-Nicolson Gamma sign changes:       6

  Oscillations suppressed by Rannacher:       True

5Where the canon breaks

While Rannacher smoothing completely cures initial payoff singularities, special care is required when singularities appear mid-simulation:

  • Discrete Dividend Payments: When an underlying asset pays a discrete dividend \(D_0\) at time \(t_{\text{div}}\), the option price experiences a discontinuous jump in spot coordinates: \(V(S, t_{\text{div}}^-) = V(S - D_0, t_{\text{div}}^+)\). Interpolating this jump reintroduces non-smooth kinks into the numerical solution. If Crank–Nicolson continues without smoothing, ringing re-emerges. The solver must restart Rannacher smoothing (4 half-steps of implicit Euler) immediately following the dividend date.
  • Bermudan Early Exercise Dates: At each Bermudan exercise date \(\tau_m\), the option price is updated via \(V \leftarrow \max(V, \psi)\), introducing new slope kinks into the solution. Restarting Rannacher smoothing after each exercise date preserves monotonicity.
  • Non-Linear Free Boundary Solvers: When combining Rannacher smoothing with penalty methods or PSOR for American options, the non-linear complementarity condition interacts with the half-step time marching, requiring careful synchronization of the early exercise boundary.

6Misconceptions

  1. “Rannacher smoothing degrades the overall convergence of the PDE solver from second-order to first-order.”

    Implicit Euler is used for only a fixed, finite number of steps (4 half-steps covering time interval \(2\Delta t\)). As the grid is refined (\(\Delta t \to 0\)), the duration of the smoothing phase shrinks to zero. Parabolic smoothing theory proves that the global asymptotic error at maturity \(T\) remains strictly second-order: \(O(\Delta t^2 + \Delta x^2)\).

  2. “Using full time steps of implicit Euler (\(\theta = 1.0\)) for 2 steps is just as good as 4 half-steps.”

    Taking full steps of size \(\Delta t\) introduces an \(O(\Delta t)\) temporal phase shift that pollutes the global error constant. Taking half-steps of size \(\Delta t / 2\) ensures that the discrete time levels coincide with midpoint evaluations, canceling first-order phase errors and preserving second-order accuracy.

  3. “Projecting or averaging the initial payoff at the strike eliminates the need for Rannacher smoothing.”

    Payoff averaging (such as Simpson's rule or cell projection) reduces the amplitude of high-frequency modes from \(O(1)\) to \(O(\Delta x)\). However, because the Crank–Nicolson amplification factor is \(G(\pi) \approx -1\), the remaining high frequencies still do not decay. For sensitive Greeks like Gamma (which divides by \(\Delta S^2\)), averaging alone is insufficient; active \(L\)-stable damping via Rannacher smoothing is indispensable.

7Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Rannacher Nyquist damping calculation

For a PDE grid with \(\lambda = 4.0\), calculate the Nyquist mode amplification factor after: (a) 1 step of Crank–Nicolson; (b) 4 half-steps of implicit Euler (Rannacher smoothing).

Solution

For \(\lambda = 4.0\):

  • (a) Crank–Nicolson: \(G_{\text{CN}}(\pi) = \frac{1 - 2(4)}{1 + 2(4)} = \frac{-7}{9} \approx -0.7778\). The mode amplitude is reduced by only \(22.2\%\) and inverts sign.
  • (b) Rannacher smoothing: The half-step ratio is \(\lambda_{\text{half}} = \lambda / 2 = 2.0\). For one half-step of implicit Euler, \(G_{\text{half}}(\pi) = \frac{1}{1 + 4(2)} = \frac{1}{9}\). After 4 half-steps: \[ G_{\text{Rannacher}}(\pi) = \left(\frac{1}{9}\right)^4 = \frac{1}{6561} \approx 0.0001524. \]

Rannacher smoothing reduces the Nyquist noise by over \(99.98\%\), virtually eradicating it.

M2 · Cell-averaged digital payoff

On a uniform grid where strike \(K = 100\) falls exactly midway between nodes \(S_j = 99.5\) and \(S_{j+1} = 100.5\) with \(\Delta S = 1.0\), compute the cell-averaged initial condition for a digital call option \(\psi(S) = \indic_{\{S > K\}}\) at node \(j\) and node \(j+1\).

Solution

The cell average over \([S - \Delta S/2, S + \Delta S/2]\) is \(\bar{\psi}(S) = \frac{1}{\Delta S}\int_{S - \Delta S/2}^{S + \Delta S/2} \indic_{\{s > 100\}} ds\):

  • For node \(S_j = 99.5\): the cell interval is \([99.0, 100.0]\). In this interval, \(s \le 100\), so \(\indic_{\{s > 100\}} = 0\). Thus \(\bar{\psi}(99.5) = 0.0\).
  • For node \(S_{j+1} = 100.5\): the cell interval is \([100.0, 101.0]\). In this interval, \(s > 100\), so \(\indic_{\{s > 100\}} = 1\). Thus \(\bar{\psi}(100.5) = 1.0\).
  • If the strike lands exactly on a node \(S_k = 100\), the cell interval is \([99.5, 100.5]\), where the payoff is 1 for \(s \in (100, 100.5]\) (length 0.5). The average is \(0.5 / 1.0 = 0.5\).
M3 · Time stepping schedule with Rannacher

An option pricer uses \(M = 50\) total time steps for maturity \(T = 1.0\). Specify the exact time step size \(\delta t\), parameter \(\theta\), and elapsed time \(\tau\) for each of the initial Rannacher steps and the subsequent Crank–Nicolson steps.

Solution

Base time step is \(\Delta t = T / M = 1.0 / 50 = 0.02\).

  • Steps 1–4 (Rannacher): \(\delta t = \Delta t / 2 = 0.01\), \(\theta = 1.0\). Elapsed times \(\tau \in [0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04]\). Two full intervals \([0, 2\Delta t]\) are completed.
  • Steps 5–52 (Crank–Nicolson): \(M - 2 = 48\) steps with \(\delta t = \Delta t = 0.02\), \(\theta = 0.5\). Elapsed times \(\tau\) advance from \(0.04\) to \(1.00\) in steps of \(0.02\). Total matrix solves: \(4 + 48 = 52\).

Proof

P1 · Error order invariance under finite initial Euler steps

Prove that applying a finite number \(K\) of first-order time steps with step size \(\Delta t / 2\) at the beginning of a simulation does not affect the global asymptotic convergence order \(O(\Delta t^2)\) at fixed maturity \(T\).

Solution

Let \(e_n\) denote the global error. The local error of each implicit Euler half-step is \(C_1 (\Delta t / 2)^2\). Over \(K\) fixed steps, the accumulated error is \(\sum_{k=1}^K C_1 (\Delta t / 2)^2 = K C_1 \frac{\Delta t^2}{4} = O(\Delta t^2)\). The subsequent Crank–Nicolson steps are second-order consistent: each full step incurs local error \(C_2 \Delta t^3\). Over \(M - K/2 = T/\Delta t - K/2\) steps, the accumulated Crank–Nicolson error is \((T/\Delta t) O(\Delta t^3) = O(\Delta t^2)\). Adding the two contributions: \(\text{Total Error} = O(\Delta t^2) + O(\Delta t^2) = O(\Delta t^2)\). Because \(K\) is fixed as \(\Delta t \to 0\), the first-order scheme is active over a time span of measure \(K \Delta t \to 0\), preserving quadratic convergence.

P2 · Second derivative smoothing property

For the continuous diffusion semigroup \(S(\tau) = e^{\tau \Delta}\), prove that for any \(u_0 \in L^2(\R)\), the second derivative satisfies \(\|\partial_{xx} S(\tau) u_0\|_{L^2} \le \frac{C}{\tau} \|u_0\|_{L^2}\) for \(\tau > 0\).

Solution

In Fourier space, \(\widehat{S(\tau) u_0}(\xi) = e^{-\xi^2 \tau} \hat{u}_0(\xi)\). The second derivative corresponds to multiplying by \(-\xi^2\): \(\widehat{\partial_{xx} S(\tau) u_0}(\xi) = -\xi^2 e^{-\xi^2 \tau} \hat{u}_0(\xi)\). Consider the scalar function \(f(\xi) = \xi^2 e^{-\xi^2 \tau}\). Let \(y = \xi^2 \tau \ge 0\). Then \(f(\xi) = \frac{1}{\tau} y e^{-y}\). The maximum of \(y e^{-y}\) on \([0, \infty)\) occurs at \(y = 1\), with value \(e^{-1} = 1/e\). Thus \(\sup_\xi |-\xi^2 e^{-\xi^2 \tau}| \le \frac{1}{e \tau}\). By Parseval's identity: \(\|\partial_{xx} S(\tau) u_0\|_{L^2} \le \frac{1}{e \tau} \|\hat{u}_0\|_{L^2} = \frac{1}{e \tau} \|u_0\|_{L^2}\). The solution operator is a bounded map into \(H^2\) for any \(\tau > 0\), providing the mathematical foundation for Rannacher smoothing.

P3 · Maximum principle for Rannacher initial steps

Prove that the four half-steps of implicit Euler satisfy the discrete maximum principle unconditionally: \(\min_k u_k^0 \le u_j^2 \le \max_k u_k^0\) for all \(j\), preventing spurious overshoots.

Solution

In Lesson 6 (Theorem 6.4 and Problem P1), we proved that the implicit BTCS system matrix \(\mathbf{A}_{\text{half}} = \mathbf{I} - \frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}\) is a strictly diagonally dominant \(M\)-matrix for any step size \(\Delta t / 2 > 0\). Its inverse \(\mathbf{A}_{\text{half}}^{-1}\) is entrywise non-negative and its row sums equal \(1\) (in the absence of discounting). Therefore, each half-step is a contractive operator in the \(L^\infty\) norm: \(\|u^{k/2}\|_{L^\infty} \le \|u^{(k-1)/2}\|_{L^\infty}\), and no discrete overshoot or undershoot can ever be generated. Applying four consecutive half-steps preserves monotonicity identically.

Empirical

E1 · Greek oscillation comparison: Raw vs. Rannacher

An equity options book computes Gamma for an at-the-money digital call option with strike \(K = 100\). Raw Crank–Nicolson yields alternating Gammas \(\{+124.5, -118.2, +112.7\}\) around the strike. Explain how Rannacher smoothing alters this profile and why it is critical for automated delta hedging.

Solution

Raw Crank–Nicolson preserves the Nyquist mode of the initial step function with \(G(\pi) \approx -1\). Because Gamma divides by \(\Delta S^2\), the alternating mode produces non-physical alternating sign spikes of magnitude \(\pm 120\). Rannacher smoothing dampens the Nyquist mode by \((1 + 2\lambda)^{-4}\), replacing the alternating spikes with the true smooth bell curve: positive Gamma below strike, peak near strike, and smooth transition to negative Gamma above strike, matching the true analytical Greek. Automated hedging algorithms rely on smooth Delta and Gamma to avoid buying and selling massive shares on adjacent tick movements.

E2 · Convergence order breakdown on vanilla calls

Why does raw Crank–Nicolson on a European call option sometimes exhibit empirical convergence order \(\alpha \approx 1.2\) to \(1.5\) instead of \(2.0\) when spot \(S_0\) is not aligned with a grid node?

Solution

When the strike \(K\) falls between grid nodes, the payoff kink \((S - K)^+\) is not resolved smoothly. As the mesh is refined, the relative distance between \(K\) and the nearest grid node oscillates between \(0\) and \(\Delta S / 2\). This grid-alignment shift introduces high-frequency Fourier components that are not damped by Crank–Nicolson, causing pointwise pricing errors to oscillate and degrading empirical convergence to fractional orders \(\approx 1.3 - 1.5\). Rannacher smoothing damps these alignment errors, restoring a clean \(\alpha \approx 2.0\).

E3 · Restarting Rannacher at discrete dividend dates

Explain the step-by-step algorithm for pricing an option with an ex-dividend date at \(\tau_{\text{div}}\) using Rannacher smoothing.

Solution

Step-by-step workflow: (1) March from \(\tau = 0\) to \(\tau_{\text{div}}^-\) using Rannacher smoothing for the first 4 half-steps, followed by Crank–Nicolson. (2) At \(\tau = \tau_{\text{div}}\), apply the discrete dividend shift by interpolating the solution vector: \(V(S, \tau_{\text{div}}^+) = V(S - D_0, \tau_{\text{div}}^-)\). This interpolation introduces a new slope kink. (3) Restart Rannacher smoothing immediately: execute 4 half-steps of implicit Euler (\(\theta = 1.0, \delta t = \Delta t / 2\)) to extinguish newly excited high-frequency modes. (4) Resume Crank–Nicolson stepping until maturity \(T\).

Research

R1 · Kreiss matrix theorem and resolvent conditions

State how the Kreiss Matrix Theorem explains why stability in the maximum norm (\(L^\infty\)) can fail for \(A\)-stable schemes like Crank–Nicolson even when \(L^2\) stability holds.

Solution

Von Neumann stability guarantees boundedness of powers \(\|\mathbf{T}^n\|_{L^2} \le 1\) because the discrete Fourier transform is an isometry on \(L^2\). However, the maximum norm \(\|\cdot\|_{L^\infty}\) is not an inner product space. By the Kreiss Matrix Theorem, uniform boundedness of \(\|\mathbf{T}^n\|_{L^\infty}\) requires the resolvent condition \(\|(\mathbf{T} - z \mathbf{I})^{-1}\|_{L^\infty} \le \frac{C}{|z| - 1}\) for all \(|z| > 1\). For Crank–Nicolson, because the rational symbol \(R(z) \to -1\) as \(z \to -\infty\), the resolvent bound constant in \(L^\infty\) blows up as \(O(N)\) or \(O(N^{1/2})\) unless high frequencies are filtered. Rannacher smoothing forces the initial resolvent to decay to zero, satisfying Kreiss resolvent bounds in \(L^\infty\).

R2 · Extrapolation schemes vs. Rannacher smoothing

Contrast Richardson extrapolation on fully implicit Euler (which eliminates the \(O(\Delta t)\) error by computing \(2 u_{\Delta t/2} - u_{\Delta t}\)) against Rannacher smoothing for non-smooth payoffs.

Solution

Richardson extrapolation uses \(u_{\text{rich}} = 2 u(\Delta t / 2) - u(\Delta t)\). While this cancels the leading \(O(\Delta t)\) term for smooth solutions, the amplification factor for the Nyquist mode is \(R_{\text{rich}}(\pi) = 2 \frac{1}{1 + 2\lambda} - \frac{1}{1 + 4\lambda}\). For large \(\lambda\), \(R_{\text{rich}} \approx \frac{2}{2\lambda} - \frac{1}{4\lambda} = \frac{3}{4\lambda}\), which decays only as \(O(\lambda^{-1})\). Rannacher smoothing damps as \(O(\lambda^{-4})\), which is orders of magnitude more aggressive. Furthermore, Richardson extrapolation requires doubling the compute across the entire time interval, whereas Rannacher smoothing incurs zero extra cost after the first 2 steps.

R3 · Higher-order smoothing for discontinuous payoff derivatives

For contracts with higher-order singularities (e.g., compound options or barrier options with second-order boundary kinks), explain why higher-order Radau IIA or Gauss–Legendre implicit Runge–Kutta stages are required to achieve fourth-order convergence \(O(\Delta t^4)\).

Solution

To preserve fourth-order convergence \(O(\Delta t^4)\) for non-smooth initial data, the time-stepping scheme must be \(L\)-stable with a rational approximation satisfying \(R(z) = e^z + O(z^5)\) and \(\lim_{\text{Re}(z) \to -\infty} R(z) = 0\), along with high stage order to prevent order reduction near boundaries. Three-stage Radau IIA methods possess stage order 2 and classical order 5, with \(L\)-stability built-in. This enables seamless fourth-order convergence on non-smooth financial payoffs without requiring separate smoothing heuristics.
