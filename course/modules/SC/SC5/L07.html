The defining empirical hallmark of modern option markets is the implied volatility smile: Black–Scholes implied volatilities vary steeply across strikes and maturities. Why do continuous stochastic volatility models fail to fit short-dated options, whereas jump processes succeed effortlessly? Because under continuous diffusions, central limit effects enforce that volatility skews flatten as expiration approaches. In sharp contrast, jump processes generate explosive short-tenor skewness behaving as \(O(1/\tau)\) or \(O(1/\sqrt{\tau})\). Calibrating Lévy models to cross-sections of market options requires designing robust nonlinear objective functions, solving inverse problems, and resolving parameter collinearity between jump frequency and jump size.

1The Jump-Induced Volatility Smile & Short-Tenor Asymptotics

Let \(C(K, \tau)\) be the European call option price for strike \(K\) and maturity \(\tau = T - t\). The Black–Scholes implied volatility \(\sigma_{imp}(K, \tau)\) is the unique positive root satisfying \(C_{BS}(S_0, K, \tau, r, q, \sigma_{imp}) = C(K, \tau)\). In financial markets, the slope of the smile with respect to log-moneyness \(k = \ln(K / F)\), termed the implied volatility skew \(\mathcal{S}(\tau) \equiv \left| \frac{\partial \sigma_{imp}}{\partial k} \right|_{k=0}\), behaves fundamentally differently under diffusions versus jump processes (Cont & Tankov 2004).

Theorem 1.1 (Short-Tenor Skew Asymptotics: Diffusion vs. Jumps)

Let \(\tau \downarrow 0\) denote the limit as option maturity approaches expiration. The limiting behavior of the implied volatility skew depends deterministically on the presence of jumps:

  1. Continuous Stochastic Volatility: In any continuous diffusion model with Brownian volatility, the at-the-money skew converges to a finite constant: \[ \lim_{\tau \downarrow 0} \left| \frac{\partial \sigma_{imp}}{\partial \ln K} \right| = \frac{|\rho| \nu_{vol}}{2} < \infty. \]
  2. Compound Poisson Jump-Diffusion: In a jump-diffusion model with non-zero jump intensity \(\lambda > 0\) and asymmetric jump distribution (such as Merton 1976), the at-the-money skew diverges at the rate: \[ \left| \frac{\partial \sigma_{imp}}{\partial \ln K} \right| \sim O\left( \frac{1}{\tau} \right) \quad \text{as } \tau \downarrow 0. \]
  3. Pure-Jump Infinite Activity Lévy Models: In pure-jump models without diffusion (such as Variance Gamma or CGMY), the implied volatility skew diverges at the rate: \[ \left| \frac{\partial \sigma_{imp}}{\partial \ln K} \right| \sim O\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}} \right) \quad \text{as } \tau \downarrow 0. \]
Proof Outline

In continuous diffusions, extreme out-of-the-money moves require cascades of continuous Gaussian increments, which decay at rate \(\exp(-k^2 / (2 \sigma^2 \tau))\). Matching this to the Black–Scholes Gaussian kernel yields a bounded skew \(\frac{|\rho| \nu_{vol}}{2}\). Under jump models, the probability of an out-of-the-money price shock over a short interval \(\tau\) is dominated by a single jump arrival: \(\Prob(S_\tau > K) \approx \lambda \tau \Prob(e^Y > K / S_0) = O(\tau)\). In contrast, Black–Scholes with constant volatility \(\sigma\) assigns probability \(O(\exp(-k^2 / (2 \sigma^2 \tau)))\) to the same event. Equating the two probabilities requires the implied volatility \(\sigma_{imp}(k, \tau)\) to blow up as \(k \ne 0\) and \(\tau \to 0\), yielding the explosive rates \(O(1/\tau)\) or \(O(1/\sqrt{\tau})\).

2Mathematical Formulation of the Calibration Problem

Calibrating a Lévy model to an observed market option surface involves solving a constrained nonlinear inverse problem (Cont & Tankov 2004).

Definition 2.1 (Calibration Loss Functions)

Let \(\{(K_i, T_i, C_i^{mkt}, \sigma_i^{mkt})\}_{i=1}^M\) denote a collection of \(M\) market option quotes. Let \(\Theta\) denote the parameter vector of the candidate Lévy model (\(\Theta_{Merton} = (\sigma, \lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J)\) or \(\Theta_{VG} = (\sigma, \nu, \theta)\)). The calibration problem seeks an optimal parameter vector \(\Theta^*\) minimizing a penalized discrepancy metric:

\[ \Theta^* = \arg\min_{\Theta \in \mathcal{D}} \mathcal{L}(\Theta), \]

where the standard market loss functions are:

  1. Implied Volatility Root-Mean-Square Error (IV-RMSE): \[ \mathcal{L}_{IV}(\Theta) = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \left( \sigma_{imp}\left( C^{model}(K_i, T_i; \Theta) \right) - \sigma_i^{mkt} \right)^2 }. \]
  2. Vega-Weighted Price Root-Mean-Square Error: \[ \mathcal{L}_{Vega}(\Theta) = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \left( \frac{C^{model}(K_i, T_i; \Theta) - C_i^{mkt}}{\mathcal{V}_i^{mkt}} \right)^2 }, \] where \(\mathcal{V}_i^{mkt} = \frac{\partial C_{BS}}{\partial \sigma}\Big|_{\sigma_i^{mkt}} = S_0 e^{-q T_i} \sqrt{T_i} \, n(d_1)\) is the Black–Scholes vega.
Remark 2.2 (Why Direct Dollar Price Loss Fails)

Calibrating against raw unweighted dollar price errors \(\sum (C_i^{model} - C_i^{mkt})^2\) is a severe modeling blunder. In-the-money options and long-dated options have high absolute prices (e.g., $25.00), whereas out-of-the-money short-dated options trade for pennies. An unweighted dollar objective focuses 99% of optimizer effort on matching in-the-money options where vega is minimal, completely ignoring the out-of-the-money wings where jump parameters are identified. By Taylor expansion, \(\Delta C_i \approx \mathcal{V}_i \Delta \sigma_{imp}\), so vega-weighting dollar errors is mathematically equivalent to minimizing implied volatility errors directly.

3Optimization Algorithms & Parameter Identification

Because the mapping \(\Theta \mapsto C^{model}(\Theta)\) is non-convex and non-linear, numerical optimization requires careful selection of algorithms and bounded parameter domains (Cont & Tankov 2004).

Theorem 3.1 (Trust-Region Reflective Nonlinear Least Squares)

Let \(r_i(\Theta) = \frac{C^{model}(K_i, T_i; \Theta) - C_i^{mkt}}{\mathcal{V}_i^{mkt}}\) be the vector of residual discrepancies. The Trust-Region Reflective (TRF) algorithm solves the bounded minimization problem by iteratively solving trust-region subproblems:

\[ \min_{p} \left\{ \|r(\Theta_k) + J_k p\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|D_k p\| \le \Delta_k, \quad l \le \Theta_k + p \le u \right\}, \]

where \(J_k = \nabla_\Theta r(\Theta_k)\) is the Jacobian matrix evaluated via finite differences or complex-step differentiation, and \(D_k\) is an adaptive diagonal scaling matrix handling disparate parameter units.

Definition 3.2 (Parameter Identification & Collinearity)

Both models face well-documented ill-posed inverse problems during calibration:

  • Merton Jump-Diffusion Collinearity: The total jump variance in Merton is \(\lambda (\mu_J^2 + \sigma_J^2)\). When calibrating to options at a single maturity, a regime with frequent small jumps (\(\lambda = 5.0, \sigma_J = 0.08\)) can produce virtually the identical implied volatility smile as rare catastrophic jumps (\(\lambda = 0.5, \sigma_J = 0.25\)). Resolving this requires multi-tenor calibration.
  • Variance Gamma Parsimony: Variance Gamma possesses only 3 parameters \((\sigma, \nu, \theta)\) compared to Merton's 4. Because \(\sigma_{diff} = 0\), \(\sigma\) represents the scale of subordinated jumps, \(\theta\) controls skewness, and \(\nu\) controls kurtosis. Parameter identification is significantly more orthogonal, yielding higher calibration stability across consecutive trading dates.

Lab 7 · Jump-induced smile: implied volatility skew and short-tenor steepness

Under jump dynamics (Cont & Tankov 2004), the implied volatility skew steepens dramatically as maturity approaches zero (\(\tau \to 0\)), scaling as \(O(1/\tau)\) or \(O(1/\sqrt{\tau})\). For Merton jump-diffusion and Variance Gamma models, recovering Black–Scholes implied volatilities across strikes \(K \in \{90, 95, 100, 105, 110\}\) at short tenor \(\tau = 0.05\) versus medium tenor \(\tau = 0.50\) demonstrates a steepening of the skew slope by more than 2x.

"""Lab 7 · Jump-induced smile: implied volatility skew and short-tenor steepness.

Lévy jump processes generate pronounced implied volatility smiles and skews. A defining empirical and theoretical
property (Cont & Tankov 2004) is that under jump dynamics, the implied volatility skew |d sigma_imp / d ln K|
increases dramatically at short tenors (tau -> 0), behaving as O(1/tau) or O(1/sqrt(tau)), whereas under continuous
diffusions with constant volatility the smile is flat, and under stochastic volatility it flattens as tau -> 0.
This lab computes characteristic functions for Merton jump-diffusion and Variance Gamma models, evaluates European call
prices via Carr-Madan damped Fourier inversion, inverts the Black-Scholes formula to recover implied volatilities
across strikes K in {90, 95, 100, 105, 110} at short tenor tau = 0.05 and medium tenor tau = 0.50 (SIMULATED),
and quantifies the short-tenor skew amplification.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from typing import Any

import numpy as np
import scipy.optimize as opt  # type: ignore[import-untyped]
import scipy.stats as stats  # type: ignore[import-untyped]


def _bs_call(spot: float, strike: float, tau: float, r: float, vol: float) -> float:
    if vol <= 0.0 or tau <= 0.0:
        return max(0.0, spot - strike * math.exp(-r * tau))
    d1 = (math.log(spot / strike) + (r + 0.5 * vol**2) * tau) / (vol * math.sqrt(tau))
    d2 = d1 - vol * math.sqrt(tau)
    return float(spot * stats.norm.cdf(d1) - strike * math.exp(-r * tau) * stats.norm.cdf(d2))


def _implied_vol(price: float, spot: float, strike: float, tau: float, r: float) -> float:
    discount = math.exp(-r * tau)
    intrinsic = max(0.0, spot - strike * discount)
    if price <= intrinsic:
        return 1e-4

    def obj(v: float) -> float:
        return _bs_call(spot, strike, tau, r, v) - price

    return float(opt.brentq(obj, 1e-4, 5.0, xtol=1e-7))


def merton_cf(
    u: complex | np.ndarray,
    tau: float,
    spot: float,
    r: float,
    sigma: float,
    lam: float,
    mu_j: float,
    sigma_j: float,
) -> complex | np.ndarray:
    kappa = math.exp(mu_j + 0.5 * sigma_j**2) - 1.0
    drift = r - 0.5 * sigma**2 - lam * kappa
    jump_part = lam * (np.exp(1j * u * mu_j - 0.5 * (sigma_j**2) * (u**2)) - 1.0)
    psi = 1j * u * drift - 0.5 * (sigma**2) * (u**2) + jump_part
    return np.exp(1j * u * math.log(spot)) * np.exp(tau * psi)


def carr_madan_prices(
    spot: float,
    strikes: np.ndarray,
    tau: float,
    r: float,
    cf_func: Any,
    alpha: float = 1.5,
    n_pts: int = 512,
    v_max: float = 150.0,
) -> np.ndarray:
    k = np.log(strikes)
    v = np.linspace(0.0, v_max, n_pts + 1)
    dv = v_max / n_pts
    weights = np.ones(n_pts + 1)
    weights[1:-1:2] = 4.0
    weights[2:-2:2] = 2.0
    weights = weights * (dv / 3.0)

    u = v - (alpha + 1.0) * 1j
    phi = cf_func(u)
    denom = (alpha**2 + alpha - v**2) + 1j * (2.0 * alpha + 1.0) * v
    psi = np.exp(-r * tau) * phi / denom
    exp_term = np.exp(-1j * np.outer(k, v))
    integrand = (exp_term * psi).real
    integral = np.sum(integrand * weights, axis=1)
    res: np.ndarray = np.asarray(np.maximum(0.0, np.exp(-alpha * k) / math.pi * integral), dtype=float)
    return res


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    r = 0.03
    strikes = np.array([90.0, 95.0, 100.0, 105.0, 110.0])

    # Merton parameters with negative mean jump (crash risk)
    m_sigma = 0.15
    m_lam = 0.60
    m_mu_j = -0.12
    m_sigma_j = 0.15

    tenors = [0.05, 0.50]
    skews: dict[float, float] = {}

    print("Merton jump-diffusion implied volatility surface (SIMULATED):")
    for tau in tenors:

        def cf_at_tau(u: complex | np.ndarray, t_val: float = tau) -> complex | np.ndarray:
            return merton_cf(u, t_val, spot, r, m_sigma, m_lam, m_mu_j, m_sigma_j)

        prices = carr_madan_prices(spot, strikes, tau, r, cf_at_tau)
        ivs = [_implied_vol(p, spot, k, tau, r) for k, p in zip(strikes, prices, strict=True)]

        # Skew measure: (IV(90) - IV(110)) / (ln(110/90))
        d_log_k = math.log(110.0 / 90.0)
        skew = (ivs[0] - ivs[-1]) / d_log_k
        skews[tau] = skew

        iv_str = ", ".join(f"K={k:.0f}:{v:.4f}" for k, v in zip(strikes, ivs, strict=True))
        print(f"  tau = {tau:.2f}: {iv_str} | skew = {skew:.4f}")

    ratio_steepness = skews[0.05] / skews[0.50]
    print(f"skew steepness amplification ratio (tau=0.05 vs tau=0.50): {ratio_steepness:.2f}")
    print("short-tenor skew is strictly steeper:", skews[0.05] > skews[0.50])
    print("amplification ratio exceeds 2.0x:", ratio_steepness >= 2.0)

4Where the canon breaks

Calibrating jump processes against real market option surfaces exposes several hard realities that shatter textbook dogma. Both Merton and Variance Gamma assume autonomous Lévy increments. In real option markets, a single parameter set calibrated to 1-month options will systematically understate implied volatilities at 2-year maturities, because autonomous Lévy cumulants decay too rapidly (\(\kappa_3 \sim 1/\sqrt{\tau}\)). Industrial practitioners must use time-inhomogeneous additive processes (Sato processes) or stochastic clock subordinators (Cont & Tankov 2004). Furthermore, textbook calibration examples often optimize unweighted squared pricing error. In live production trading desks, this results in disastrous mispricing of wing options; all commercial pricing libraries calibrate against implied volatilities or vega-weighted errors.

5Misconceptions

  1. “If a model's calibration achieves an RMSE of zero on today's closing prices, it is the correct physical model of the underlying asset.”

    Option calibration recovers the risk-neutral pricing measure \(\Prob^*\), not the physical statistical measure \(\Prob\). A perfect fit to an option surface merely proves the model has sufficient mathematical flexibility to interpolate static quotes today; it provides zero guarantee that future dynamics or hedging performance will be valid.

  2. “The Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm will always find the global minimum of the calibration surface.”

    The calibration objective surface is highly non-convex with numerous local minima and flat valleys (especially for Merton jump-diffusion). Local gradient-based methods easily get trapped in spurious local minima unless initialized with a smart multi-start heuristic or a global search algorithm like differential evolution.

  3. “Black–Scholes implied volatility inversion is undefined for deep out-of-the-money options under jump models.”

    Provided the option price satisfies the strict no-arbitrage bounds \((S_0 e^{-q T} - K e^{-r T})^+ < C < S_0 e^{-q T}\), the Black–Scholes pricing formula is strictly monotonically increasing with respect to \(\sigma\). By the intermediate value theorem, a unique positive implied volatility \(\sigma_{imp} > 0\) always exists and can be recovered to machine precision via Brent's method.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Vega-weighted error calculation

A market European call option with strike \(K = 105\) and maturity \(T = 0.25\) is quoted at price \(C^{mkt} = 2.15\). The Black–Scholes vega at this quote is \(\mathcal{V} = 18.50\). If a candidate Merton model prices the call at \(C^{model} = 2.05\), compute the raw dollar error and the vega-weighted error.

Solution

The raw dollar error is: \(e_{dollar} = C^{model} - C^{mkt} = 2.05 - 2.15 = -0.10\). The vega-weighted error is: \(e_{vega} = \frac{C^{model} - C^{mkt}}{\mathcal{V}} = \frac{-0.10}{18.50} \approx -0.0054054\) (or \(-0.54\) volatility points).

M2 · Implied volatility RMSE calculation

Consider three option quotes with vega-weighted residuals \(r_1 = +0.004\), \(r_2 = -0.006\), and \(r_3 = +0.002\). Calculate the Root-Mean-Square Error (RMSE) in volatility percentage terms.

Solution

The mean of squared residuals is: \(\frac{1}{3} ( (0.004)^2 + (-0.006)^2 + (0.002)^2 ) = \frac{1}{3} ( 0.000016 + 0.000036 + 0.000004 ) = \frac{0.000056}{3} \approx 0.000018667\). Taking the square root: \(\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{0.000018667} \approx 0.0043205\). In percentage points: \(0.0043205 \times 100\% \approx 0.432\%\) (43.2 basis points of implied volatility).

M3 · Variance Gamma admissibility verification

In calibrating Variance Gamma, the current iterate is \(\sigma = 0.25\), \(\nu = 0.80\), and \(\theta = -0.15\). Check whether this parameter combination satisfies the risk-neutral martingale constraint \(\theta \nu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu < 1\).

Solution

Compute \(\theta \nu = -0.15 \times 0.80 = -0.12\). Compute \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu = 0.5 \times (0.25)^2 \times 0.80 = 0.5 \times 0.0625 \times 0.80 = 0.025\). Sum the two terms: \(-0.12 + 0.025 = -0.095\). Because \(-0.095 < 1\), the parameter set strictly satisfies the admissibility constraint.

P1 · Monotonicity of Black–Scholes call price with respect to volatility

Prove that the Black–Scholes call price \(C_{BS}(\sigma)\) is a strictly monotonically increasing function of volatility \(\sigma\) on \((0, \infty)\) for any strike \(K > 0\) and expiration \(T > 0\), and deduce that the implied volatility \(\sigma_{imp}\) is unique whenever a solution exists.

Solution

The derivative of the Black–Scholes call price with respect to volatility is the vega: \(\frac{\partial C_{BS}}{\partial \sigma} = S_0 e^{-q T} \sqrt{T} \, n(d_1)\), where \(n(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2 / 2} > 0\) is the standard Gaussian density. For any \(S_0 > 0\) and \(T > 0\), \(S_0 e^{-q T} \sqrt{T} > 0\) and \(n(d_1) > 0\), meaning \(\frac{\partial C_{BS}}{\partial \sigma} > 0\) strictly for all \(\sigma \in (0, \infty)\). Because \(C_{BS}\) is strictly monotonically increasing on \((0, \infty)\), its inverse function is well-defined and strictly single-valued. Therefore, if there exists any \(\sigma^* > 0\) such that \(C_{BS}(\sigma^*) = C^{mkt}\), that solution \(\sigma_{imp} = \sigma^*\) is strictly unique.

P2 · Diffusion vs. jump short-tenor skew asymptotic distinction

Explain why the implied volatility skew \(\left| \frac{\partial \sigma_{imp}}{\partial \ln K} \right|\) at short tenors \(\tau \to 0\) can distinguish between a pure diffusion model and a jump process, whereas at long tenors \(\tau \to 2\) years, the two models become virtually indistinguishable.

Solution

At short tenors \(\tau \to 0\), the paths of diffusions are continuous, and extreme out-of-the-money moves require improbable cascades of Gaussian increments, forcing the implied volatility skew to remain bounded at \(\frac{|\rho|\nu_{vol}}{2}\). Under jump models, a single jump can transport the asset price across strikes instantaneously with probability \(O(\tau)\), causing the implied volatility skew to blow up as \(O(1/\tau)\) or \(O(1/\sqrt{\tau})\). At long maturities \(\tau \to \infty\), by the central limit theorem for independent increments, the sum of jumps \(\sum Y_k\) converges to a Gaussian distribution, and the law of large numbers smooths out discrete discontinuities. Consequently, both jump processes and stochastic volatility diffusions converge to asymptotic normal behavior, flattening the implied volatility smile into an indistinguishable parabolic curve.

P3 · Precision of complex-step differentiation

In nonlinear least-squares calibration, why does calculating Jacobians via the complex-step derivative approximation \(f'(x) \approx \frac{\operatorname{Im}[f(x + i h)]}{h}\) outperform standard finite differences \(f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}\)?

Solution

Standard finite difference formulas require subtracting two floating-point numbers \(f(x + h) - f(x)\) that are nearly identical when \(h\) is small. For \(h < 10^{-8}\), catastrophic subtractive cancellation destroys 8 to 12 digits of floating-point precision, forcing a compromise step size \(h \approx 10^{-8}\) that limits gradient accuracy to \(O(10^{-8})\). In contrast, Taylor expanding in the complex plane yields \(f(x + i h) = f(x) + i h f'(x) - \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) - i \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \dots\). Taking the imaginary part gives \(\operatorname{Im}[f(x + i h)] = h f'(x) - \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \dots\). Dividing by \(h\) yields \(f'(x) + O(h^2)\) without any subtraction whatsoever. One can safely set \(h = 10^{-30}\), achieving exact machine-precision derivatives (\(10^{-16}\)) with zero subtractive cancellation.

Empirical

E1 · Merton jump variance proportion

Calibrating a Merton jump-diffusion model to an SPX option surface yields \(\sigma = 0.12\), \(\lambda = 0.80\), \(\mu_J = -0.15\), and \(\sigma_J = 0.10\). Calculate the proportion of total return variance attributable to jumps versus continuous diffusion.

Solution

The total annualized variance is \(\Var(X_1) = \sigma^2 + \lambda (\mu_J^2 + \sigma_J^2)\). Diffusion variance is \(\sigma^2 = (0.12)^2 = 0.0144\). The second moment of jumps is \(\E[Y^2] = \mu_J^2 + \sigma_J^2 = (-0.15)^2 + (0.10)^2 = 0.0225 + 0.0100 = 0.0325\). Jump variance is \(\lambda \E[Y^2] = 0.80 \times 0.0325 = 0.0260\). Total variance is \(0.0144 + 0.0260 = 0.0404\). The proportion attributable to jumps is: \(\frac{0.0260}{0.0404} = \frac{260}{404} \approx 0.6436\) (or \(64.36\%\)). Jumps account for nearly two-thirds of total asset variance.

E2 · Variance Gamma short-tenor skew amplification

In calibrating Variance Gamma to an equity index option surface, the optimizer converges to \(\sigma = 0.22\), \(\nu = 0.45\), and \(\theta = -0.18\). Calculate the theoretical implied volatility skew slope at \(\tau = 0.10\) years and compare it to \(\tau = 1.0\) year.

Solution

From Theorem 1.1 and the skewness scaling of Variance Gamma, the implied volatility skew scales as \(O(1 / \sqrt{\tau})\). The ratio of the skew at \(\tau = 0.10\) to the skew at \(\tau = 1.0\) is approximately: \(\frac{\mathcal{S}(0.10)}{\mathcal{S}(1.0)} \approx \sqrt{\frac{1.0}{0.10}} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162\). The short-dated option smile is more than three times steeper than the 1-year smile, illustrating why jump processes are essential for capturing front-month options.

E3 · Parameter identification valley in Merton calibration

An analyst calibrating Merton's model on 10 option quotes observes that running SciPy `least_squares` from initial guess \((0.15, 0.5, -0.05, 0.10)\) yields RMSE \(0.0042\), while initializing from \((0.10, 4.0, -0.02, 0.04)\) yields RMSE \(0.0044\). Explain what this indicates about the parameter identification landscape.

Solution

This discrepancy demonstrates the classical identification valley of Merton's model: the two parameter sets represent two distinct physical regimes—rare large jumps (\(\lambda = 0.5\), \(\sigma_J = 0.10\)) versus frequent micro-jumps (\(\lambda = 4.0\), \(\sigma_J = 0.04\))—that produce almost identical option prices across the 10 strikes. The objective function possesses multiple shallow local minima separated by low barriers. To resolve this collinearity, the analyst must incorporate additional maturities into the calibration set, include higher-order moments from time-series log-returns, or add a regularization penalty (Tikhonov regularization) toward a prior parameter estimate.

Research

R1 · Physical vs. risk-neutral jump intensity and the jump risk premium

In quantitative asset management, why do risk managers prefer calibrating to the risk-neutral measure \(\Prob^*\) via option prices, while portfolio managers calibrate to the physical measure \(\Prob\) via historical time series of daily returns? How do the calibrated jump intensities \(\lambda^*\) and \(\lambda^{\Prob}\) typically compare?

Solution

Risk managers pricing derivatives must enforce the absence of arbitrage, requiring the risk-neutral measure \(\Prob^*\) where discounted asset prices are martingales. Portfolio managers forecasting future returns and Sharpe ratios require the real-world physical measure \(\Prob\). In empirical markets, the risk-neutral jump arrival rate \(\lambda^*\) is substantially higher than the physical historical jump arrival rate \(\lambda^{\Prob}\) (often \(\lambda^* / \lambda^{\Prob} \approx 2\) to \(4\)), and risk-neutral jump mean \(\mu_J^*\) is significantly more negative than physical \(\mu_J^{\Prob}\). This divergence reflects the jump risk premium: risk-averse investors pay a substantial premium for out-of-the-money puts to insure against market crashes, which inflates \(\lambda^*\) under \(\Prob^*\).

R2 · The Bates (1996) stochastic volatility jump-diffusion model

Contrast the calibration of static Lévy processes with the Bates (1996) model (Merton jump-diffusion + Heston stochastic volatility). What does adding Heston stochastic volatility accomplish that pure Lévy processes cannot?

Solution

A pure Lévy process has stationary independent increments, forcing cumulants to scale strictly linearly in time: \(\kappa_n(t) = t \kappa_n(1)\). This enforces that implied volatility skew decays as \(O(1/\sqrt{\tau})\) and cannot accommodate non-trivial term structures of skewness or persistent volatility clustering. The Bates (1996) model couples Merton jumps with Heston mean-reverting stochastic variance \(V_t\). The jump component captures the steep, explosive skew at short tenors (\(\tau < 3\) months), while the mean-reverting continuous variance process \(d V_t = \kappa(\theta - V_t) dt + \sigma_v \sqrt{V_t} d W_t^v\) governs the persistence of the volatility smile across medium and long tenors (\(\tau > 1\) year), providing an accurate fit across the entire two-dimensional volatility surface.

R3 · Variable projection (VarPro) for separated jump calibration

Outline the formulation of a variable projection (VarPro) algorithm for calibrating a Lévy jump-diffusion model where linear parameters (such as drift or scale weights) are separated from nonlinear parameters.

Solution

Variable projection separates the parameter vector into linear parameters \(\mathbf{c}\) and nonlinear parameters \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\). In multi-component jump models (e.g., a mixture of jump densities \(k(x) = \sum_{j=1}^p c_j k_j(x; \boldsymbol{\eta})\)), option prices depend linearly on \(\mathbf{c}\): \(C(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\eta}) = \Phi(\boldsymbol{\eta}) \mathbf{c}\). For any fixed \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\), the optimal linear parameters are computed in closed form via Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse: \(\mathbf{c}^*(\boldsymbol{\eta}) = \Phi(\boldsymbol{\eta})^\dagger \mathbf{C}^{mkt}\). Substituting \(\mathbf{c}^*(\boldsymbol{\eta})\) back into the loss function creates a reduced objective \(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}) = \|\mathbf{C}^{mkt} - \Phi(\boldsymbol{\eta})\Phi(\boldsymbol{\eta})^\dagger \mathbf{C}^{mkt}\|_2^2\) that depends exclusively on the nonlinear parameters \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\). This reduces the dimensionality of the nonlinear optimization problem, accelerating convergence and eliminating parameter valleys.

8Formative Quiz

  1. What asymptotic behavior distinguishes the implied volatility skew of jump processes from continuous diffusion models as maturity \(\tau \to 0\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 1.1, continuous diffusions have bounded short-tenor skew \(\frac{|\rho|\nu_{vol}}{2}\), whereas jump models generate explosive skew divergence.

  2. Why is calibrating against vega-weighted price residuals preferred over raw dollar price errors?

    Options

    By Remark 2.2, \(\Delta C \approx \mathcal{V} \Delta \sigma_{imp}\), so vega weighting normalizes errors into implied volatility units across all strikes.

  3. How many parameters must be calibrated in the standard Variance Gamma model \((\sigma, \nu, \theta)\)?

    Variance Gamma has exactly 3 parameters: volatility scale \(\sigma\), time variance \(\nu\), and drift/skewness parameter \(\theta\).

  4. How many parameters must be calibrated in Merton's jump-diffusion model \((\sigma, \lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J)\)?

    Merton's model has 4 parameters: continuous diffusion \(\sigma\), jump arrival intensity \(\lambda\), mean jump size \(\mu_J\), and jump standard deviation \(\sigma_J\).

  5. What parameter identification issue commonly arises when calibrating Merton's model to options at a single maturity?

    Options

    By Definition 3.2, \(\lambda\) and \(\sigma_J^2\) trade off along an objective valley of nearly identical variance and option prices.

  6. Why do time-homogeneous Lévy processes systematically struggle to calibrate simultaneously across a wide range of option maturities (\(\tau = 1\) month to \(\tau = 2\) years)?

    Options

    Lévy processes have linear cumulant scaling \(\kappa_n(t) = t \kappa_n(1)\), meaning higher moments vanish too rapidly at long maturities compared to market quotes.

  7. What mathematical technique allows computing Jacobians for nonlinear least squares with machine precision without subtractive cancellation?

    Options

    Complex-step differentiation avoids floating-point subtraction, maintaining 16 decimal digits of accuracy for step sizes down to \(h = 10^{-30}\).

  8. What is the theoretical continuous Gaussian diffusion parameter \(\sigma_{diff}\) in the Variance Gamma model?

    In the Variance Gamma model, continuous diffusion is set to zero (\(\sigma_{diff} = 0\)); all price variation is driven by the pure-jump Lévy driver.
