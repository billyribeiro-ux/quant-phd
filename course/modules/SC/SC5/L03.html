Continuous-path diffusions like Black–Scholes assume asset prices diffuse smoothly over infinitesimal time steps. Financial reality defies this assumption: markets undergo instantaneous crashes, discrete earnings jumps, and bursts of high-frequency micro-jumps. Lévy processes provide the unifying mathematical framework for all stationary, independent-increment continuous-time jump-diffusion processes. Through the celebrated Lévy–Khintchine representation, any such process is uniquely governed by a characteristic triplet \((b, \sigma^2, \nu)\). Mastering this architecture is prerequisite to pricing exotic derivatives, calibrating implied volatility skews, and understanding modern non-Gaussian asset models.

1Axiomatic Construction of Lévy Processes

In classical mathematical finance, Brownian motion models continuous diffusion, whereas compound Poisson processes model discrete, isolated arrivals. Both share two fundamental structural properties: stationary increments and independent increments. A Lévy process is the general continuous-time stochastic process possessing these exact symmetries (Cont & Tankov 2004).

Definition 1.1 (Lévy Process)

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \Prob)\) be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions. An adapted stochastic process \(X = (X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) with values in \(\R\) is called a Lévy process if it satisfies the following four axioms:

  1. Initial condition: \(X_0 = 0\) almost surely.
  2. Independent increments: For any sequence of ordered times \(0 \le t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n\), the increments \(X_{t_1} - X_{t_0}, X_{t_2} - X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n} - X_{t_{n-1}}\) are mutually independent.
  3. Stationary increments: For any \(s < t\), the distribution of the increment \(X_t - X_s\) depends only on the elapsed time \(t - s\), so that \(X_t - X_s \stackrel{d}{=} X_{t - s}\).
  4. Stochastic continuity: For all \(s \ge 0\) and any \(\epsilon > 0\), \(\lim_{t \to s} \Prob(|X_t - X_s| > \epsilon) = 0\).

By standard regularisation theorems, every Lévy process admits a unique cádlág (right-continuous with left limits) modification, which we take as the canonical sample path version.

Remark 1.2 (Stochastic Continuity vs. Path Continuity)

Axiom 4 states that the probability of a jump occurring at any predetermined fixed time \(s\) is zero. It does not mean that sample paths are continuous functions of time. Jumps occur at totally inaccessible random stopping times, never at deterministic milestones.

2Infinite Divisibility

The requirement of stationary and independent increments imposes an algebraic symmetry on the marginal distributions of \(X_t\). Specifically, any realization over horizon \(t\) can be subdivided into \(n\) identically distributed independent increments of length \(t / n\).

Definition 2.1 (Infinitely Divisible Distribution)

A probability distribution \(F\) on \(\R\) (or random variable \(Y\)) is called infinitely divisible if for every positive integer \(n \in \N_{\ge 1}\), there exist independent, identically distributed random variables \(Y_1^{(n)}, \dots, Y_n^{(n)}\) such that:

\[ Y \stackrel{d}{=} \sum_{k=1}^n Y_k^{(n)}. \]

Equivalently, the characteristic function \(\phi_Y(u) = \E[e^{i u Y}]\) has an \(n\)-th root \(\phi_n(u) = (\phi_Y(u))^{1/n}\) that is itself a valid characteristic function for every \(n \ge 1\).

Theorem 2.2 (Equivalence of Lévy Processes and Infinite Divisibility)

Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a Lévy process. Then for every \(t \ge 0\), the distribution of \(X_t\) is infinitely divisible. Conversely, given any infinitely divisible distribution \(F\), there exists a unique (in law) Lévy process \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) such that \(X_1 \sim F\).

Proof

For any \(t > 0\) and integer \(n \ge 1\), partition the interval \([0, t]\) into \(n\) equal subintervals \([k t / n, (k+1) t / n]\). The value \(X_t\) decomposes telescopically as:

\[ X_t = \sum_{k=1}^n \left( X_{\frac{k t}{n}} - X_{\frac{(k-1) t}{n}} \right). \]

By Axiom 2 (independent increments), the summands are mutually independent. By Axiom 3 (stationary increments), each summand has the exact distribution of \(X_{t/n}\). Hence \(X_t\) is the sum of \(n\) i.i.d. increments, proving infinite divisibility for all \(n\). The converse follows from Kolmogorov's extension theorem combined with the stochastic continuity axiom.

Theorem 2.3 (Linearity of the Characteristic Exponent)

Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a Lévy process with characteristic function \(\phi_{X_t}(u) = \E[e^{i u X_t}]\). There exists a continuous function \(\psi: \R \to \mathbb{C}\), termed the characteristic exponent (or Lévy exponent), such that for all \(t \ge 0\) and all \(u \in \R\):

\[ \phi_{X_t}(u) = \E\left[e^{i u X_t}\right] = e^{t \psi(u)}. \]
Proof

Fix \(u \in \R\) and define \(g(t) = \E[e^{i u X_t}]\). For any \(s, t \ge 0\), by independent and stationary increments:

\[ g(t + s) = \E\left[e^{i u (X_{t+s} - X_t + X_t)}\right] = \E\left[e^{i u (X_{t+s} - X_t)}\right] \E\left[e^{i u X_t}\right] = g(s) g(t). \]

This is Cauchy's exponential functional equation. By stochastic continuity, \(g(t) \to g(0) = 1\) as \(t \downarrow 0\), implying \(g\) is continuous. The only non-vanishing continuous solutions to \(g(t + s) = g(t) g(s)\) on \([0, \infty)\) with \(g(0) = 1\) are of the form \(g(t) = e^{t \psi(u)}\) for some constant \(\psi(u) \in \mathbb{C}\).

3The Lévy–Khintchine Representation & Characteristic Triplet

The fundamental structural classification of all infinitely divisible characteristic functions was established by Paul Lévy (1934) and Aleksandr Khinchin (1937). It establishes that every Lévy process is uniquely characterised by three geometric components: a deterministic drift, a Gaussian diffusion, and a jump measure (Cont & Tankov 2004).

Definition 3.1 (Lévy Measure)

A Borel measure \(\nu\) on \(\R \setminus \{0\}\) is called a Lévy measure if it satisfies the integrability condition:

\[ \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) < \infty. \]

Equivalently: \(\int_{|x| \le 1} x^2 \nu(dx) < \infty\) and \(\nu(\{x \in \R : |x| > 1\}) < \infty\).

Theorem 3.2 (The Lévy–Khintchine Representation)

Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a Lévy process on \(\R\). The characteristic exponent \(\psi(u)\) in \(\E[e^{i u X_t}] = e^{t \psi(u)}\) admits the unique Lévy–Khintchine representation:

\[ \psi(u) = i b u - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 + \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \left( e^{i u x} - 1 - i u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}} \right) \nu(dx), \]

where \(b \in \R\) is the drift parameter under truncation function \(h(x) = x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\), \(\sigma \ge 0\) is the Gaussian diffusion volatility, and \(\nu\) is a Lévy measure on \(\R \setminus \{0\}\). The triplet \((b, \sigma^2, \nu)\) is called the characteristic triplet of \(X\).

Proof Outline

By Theorem 2.2, \(X_1\) is infinitely divisible. For each \(n \ge 1\), write \(X_1 = \sum_{k=1}^n Y_k^{(n)}\). Let \(\phi_n(u) = \E[e^{i u Y_1^{(n)}}]\). By Taylor expanding \(e^{i u x} - 1\), separating jumps smaller and larger than 1, and passing to the weak limit as \(n \to \infty\) using Helly's selection theorem, the limit of the exponent converges uniquely to \(\psi(u)\). The integrability condition \(\int \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) < \infty\) is necessary and sufficient for \(\psi(u)\) to be continuous and conditionally negative definite, ensuring \(e^{\psi(u)}\) is a valid characteristic function by Bochner's theorem.

Remark 3.3 (Role of the Truncation Compensator)

The term \(- i u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) acts as a mathematical centering device. Near the origin \(x \to 0\), the integrand satisfies \(e^{i u x} - 1 - i u x = -\frac{1}{2} u^2 x^2 + O(x^3)\). Because \(\int_{|x| \le 1} x^2 \nu(dx) < \infty\), the integrand is bounded by \(C x^2\) near 0, making the integral absolutely convergent.

Theorem 3.4 (Lévy–Itô Pathwise Decomposition)

Let \(X\) be a Lévy process with characteristic triplet \((b, \sigma^2, \nu)\). Almost surely, for all \(t \ge 0\), \(X_t\) decomposes as:

\[ X_t = b t + \sigma W_t + \int_0^t \int_{|x| > 1} x N(ds, dx) + \lim_{\epsilon \downarrow 0} \int_0^t \int_{\epsilon < |x| \le 1} x \left( N(ds, dx) - \nu(dx) ds \right), \]

where \(b t\) is deterministic drift, \(W_t\) is standard Brownian motion, \(N\) is a Poisson random measure with intensity \(dt \nu(dx)\), and the final term is a pure-jump square-integrable martingale of compensated small jumps. All four components are mutually independent.

Lab 3 · Infinite divisibility and characteristic exponent scaling in pure-jump Lévy processes

Under the Lévy–Khintchine theorem, the log characteristic function of any Lévy process scales strictly linearly in time: \(\ln \phi_{X_{t_2}}(u) / \ln \phi_{X_{t_1}}(u) = t_2 / t_1\). For a compound Poisson jump process with \(\lambda = 3.0\), \(\mu_Y = 0.10\), \(\sigma_Y = 0.20\) at \(t_1 = 0.5\) and \(t_2 = 1.0\), simulated empirical log-CF values across 5,000 realization paths (SIMULATED) at frequency \(u = 1.5\) match the theoretical ratio 2.0 within 5% tolerance.

"""Lab 3 · Infinite divisibility and characteristic exponent scaling in pure-jump Lévy processes.

By the Lévy-Khintchine theorem, every Lévy process (X_t)_{t >= 0} has a characteristic function that scales
linearly in time: E[exp(i*u*X_t)] = exp(t * psi(u)), where psi(u) is the characteristic exponent.
Consequently, for any two horizons t_1 and t_2, the log characteristic functions satisfy the exact scaling:
ln(phi_{X_{t_2}}(u)) / ln(phi_{X_{t_1}}(u)) = t_2 / t_1.
This lab simulates 5,000 realization paths (SIMULATED) of a compound Poisson process with lambda = 3.0 and
jump distribution Normal(0.1, 0.2^2) at t_1 = 0.5 and t_2 = 1.0 using seed 77, computes the empirical
characteristic functions across a frequency grid, and verifies the linear scaling ratio within 5% tolerance.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import cmath
import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    seed = 77
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    lam = 3.0
    mu_y = 0.10
    sigma_y = 0.20
    t1 = 0.5
    t2 = 1.0
    n_paths = 5000

    # Draw jumps for t1 and increments from t1 to t2
    x_t1: list[float] = []
    x_t2: list[float] = []

    def draw_poisson(l_param: float) -> int:
        p = 1.0
        k = 0
        l_exp = math.exp(-l_param)
        while True:
            p *= rng.random()
            if p <= l_exp:
                break
            k += 1
        return k

    for _ in range(n_paths):
        n1 = draw_poisson(lam * t1)
        jumps1 = sum(rng.gauss(mu_y, sigma_y) for _ in range(n1))
        x_t1.append(jumps1)

        n_inc = draw_poisson(lam * (t2 - t1))
        jumps_inc = sum(rng.gauss(mu_y, sigma_y) for _ in range(n_inc))
        x_t2.append(jumps1 + jumps_inc)

    # Evaluate empirical characteristic functions at test frequency u = 1.5
    u = 1.5
    theo_ratio = t2 / t1

    emp_phi_t1 = sum(cmath.exp(1j * u * x) for x in x_t1) / n_paths
    emp_phi_t2 = sum(cmath.exp(1j * u * x) for x in x_t2) / n_paths

    log_phi_t1 = cmath.log(emp_phi_t1)
    log_phi_t2 = cmath.log(emp_phi_t2)

    emp_ratio_real = log_phi_t2.real / log_phi_t1.real
    emp_ratio_imag = log_phi_t2.imag / log_phi_t1.imag

    print(f"simulated paths (SIMULATED): {n_paths}, horizons: t1 = {t1}, t2 = {t2}")
    print(f"theoretical scaling ratio t2 / t1 = {theo_ratio:.4f}")
    print(f"empirical log CF at t1: real = {log_phi_t1.real:.6f}, imag = {log_phi_t1.imag:.6f}")
    print(f"empirical log CF at t2: real = {log_phi_t2.real:.6f}, imag = {log_phi_t2.imag:.6f}")
    print(f"empirical ratio: real part = {emp_ratio_real:.4f}, imaginary part = {emp_ratio_imag:.4f}")
    print(
        "real part scaling within 5% of theoretical 2.0:",
        abs(emp_ratio_real - theo_ratio) <= 0.05 * theo_ratio,
    )
    print(
        "imaginary part scaling within 5% of theoretical 2.0:",
        abs(emp_ratio_imag - theo_ratio) <= 0.05 * theo_ratio,
    )

4Where the canon breaks

In classical quantitative curricula centered exclusively on the Black–Scholes paradigm, several foundational concepts break down when confronted with market data and Lévy dynamics. High-frequency equity order book data demonstrates that prices do not diffuse across every intermediate dollar cent. When market orders deplete standing liquidity at the best quote, or when macroeconomic releases hit wire services, prices transition instantaneously over discrete gaps (Cont & Tankov 2004). Furthermore, classical finance assumes all processes are diffusions with quadratic variation \([X, X]_t = \sigma^2 t\). Under pure-jump Lévy processes, continuous quadratic variation is zero (\([X^c, X^c]_t = 0\)), yet the process exhibits rich stochastic variance driven entirely by discrete jump shocks.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Stochastic continuity means that the sample paths of the process cannot jump.”

    Stochastic continuity requires \(\lim_{h \to 0} \Prob(|X_{t+h} - X_t| > \epsilon) = 0\) at any fixed deterministic time \(t\). A Poisson process satisfies \(\Prob(N_{t+h} - N_t \ge 1) = 1 - e^{-\lambda h} \to 0\), so it is perfectly stochastically continuous, even though 100% of its sample paths are step functions with discontinuous jumps.

  2. “The cutoff radius 1 in the truncation term \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) represents a physical threshold in market dynamics.”

    The cutoff at \(|x| \le 1\) is an arbitrary mathematical convention. One can replace \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) with \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}}\) for any \(c > 0\). Changing the truncation radius changes the numerical drift parameter \(b_c\), but leaves the underlying process and its distribution completely identical.

  3. “Infinite activity implies that the total variation of the path is infinite.”

    Infinite activity means that \(\nu(\R \setminus \{0\}) = \infty\), so an infinite number of microscopic jumps arrive in any interval. However, if \(\int_{|x| \le 1} |x| \nu(dx) < \infty\), the sum of the absolute magnitudes of these infinite jumps converges almost surely. Such a process (e.g., Variance Gamma) has infinite jump activity yet strictly finite total variation.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Compound Poisson Lévy measure evaluation

Consider a compound Poisson process with arrival intensity \(\lambda = 4.0\) and two-point jump distribution \(\Prob(Y = +0.02) = 0.60\) and \(\Prob(Y = -0.05) = 0.40\). Express its Lévy measure \(\nu(dx)\) as a linear combination of Dirac delta measures, and evaluate \(\int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx)\).

Solution

For a compound Poisson process, the Lévy measure is \(\nu(dx) = \lambda F_Y(dx)\). Here the jump distribution has discrete atoms at \(+0.02\) and \(-0.05\). Thus:

\[ \nu(dx) = 4.0 \times \left( 0.60 \, \delta_{+0.02}(dx) + 0.40 \, \delta_{-0.05}(dx) \right) = 2.4 \, \delta_{+0.02}(dx) + 1.6 \, \delta_{-0.05}(dx). \]

Because both jump sizes satisfy \(|x| \le 1\), \(\min(1, x^2) = x^2\). Evaluating the integral: \(\int \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) = 2.4 \times (0.02)^2 + 1.6 \times (-0.05)^2 = 2.4 \times 0.0004 + 1.6 \times 0.0025 = 0.00096 + 0.00400 = 0.00496\).

M2 · Cauchy process variation analysis

Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a standard Cauchy process whose density at \(t=1\) is \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi (1 + x^2)}\). What is its characteristic exponent \(\psi(u)\), and does this process have finite or infinite total variation?

Solution

The characteristic function of a standard Cauchy distribution at \(t=1\) is \(\phi(u) = e^{-|u|}\). Since \(\phi_{X_t}(u) = e^{t \psi(u)}\), we have \(\psi(u) = -|u|\). The corresponding Lévy measure is \(\nu(dx) = \frac{1}{\pi x^2} dx\). In the small-jump neighborhood near zero, \(\int_{-1}^1 |x| \nu(dx) = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^1 \frac{x}{x^2} dx = \frac{2}{\pi} [\ln x]_0^1 = \infty\). Because the absolute jump integral diverges at the origin, the standard Cauchy process has paths of strictly infinite total variation.

M3 · Jump activity and variation classification

A pure-jump Lévy process has Lévy measure \(\nu(dx) = \frac{5}{|x|} e^{-2 |x|} dx\). Determine whether this process exhibits (a) finite or infinite jump activity, and (b) finite or infinite total variation.

Solution

First, test jump activity by integrating \(\nu\) over \(\R \setminus \{0\}\): near \(x = 0\), the integrand behaves as \(\frac{5}{|x|}\). The integral \(\int_0^1 \frac{5}{x} dx = 5 [\ln x]_0^1 = \infty\). Thus \(\nu(\R \setminus \{0\}) = \infty\), proving infinite jump activity. Next, test total variation by integrating \(|x| \nu(dx)\) on \([-1, 1]\): \(\int_{-1}^1 |x| \frac{5}{|x|} e^{-2|x|} dx = 2 \int_0^1 5 e^{-2 x} dx = 5 (1 - e^{-2}) \approx 4.3233 < \infty\). Because this integral converges, the process exhibits finite total variation.

P1 · Linear expectation and martingale property

Prove that if \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a Lévy process with \(\E[|X_t|] < \infty\), then \(\E[X_t] = t \E[X_1]\), and deduce that \(M_t = X_t - t \E[X_1]\) is a martingale with respect to its natural filtration.

Solution

Let \(m(t) = \E[X_t]\). For any \(s, t \ge 0\), stationary and independent increments imply \(\E[X_{t+s}] = \E[X_t + (X_{t+s} - X_t)] = \E[X_t] + \E[X_s]\), so \(m(t + s) = m(t) + m(s)\). Stochastic continuity implies \(m(t)\) is continuous in \(t\). The only continuous solution to Cauchy's additive functional equation is \(m(t) = c t\), where \(c = m(1) = \E[X_1]\). Now consider \(M_t = X_t - c t\). For \(s \le t\), \(\E[M_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \E[X_s + (X_t - X_s) - c t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = X_s + \E[X_t - X_s] - c t = X_s + c(t - s) - c t = X_s - c s = M_s\). Hence \(M_t\) is a continuous-time martingale.

P2 · Real part non-positivity of characteristic exponents

Prove that the characteristic exponent \(\psi(u)\) of any Lévy process satisfies \(\psi(0) = 0\) and \(\overline{\psi(u)} = \psi(-u)\), and deduce that the real part \(\Re(\psi(u)) \le 0\) for all \(u \in \R\).

Solution

At \(u = 0\), \(\E[e^{i 0 X_1}] = \E[1] = 1 = e^{\psi(0)}\), which requires \(\psi(0) = 0\). For complex conjugation, \(\overline{\E[e^{i u X_1}]} = \E[\overline{e^{i u X_1}}] = \E[e^{-i u X_1}] = e^{\psi(-u)}\). Simultaneously, \(\overline{e^{\psi(u)}} = e^{\overline{\psi(u)}}\), implying \(\overline{\psi(u)} = \psi(-u)\). For the real part, by Jensen's inequality and the property of characteristic functions, \(|\phi_{X_1}(u)| = |e^{\psi(u)}| = e^{\Re(\psi(u))} \le \E[|e^{i u X_1}|] = 1\). Taking natural logarithms of both sides yields \(\Re(\psi(u)) \le 0\) for all \(u \in \R\).

P3 · Invariance of characteristic exponent under truncation shift

Let \((b, \sigma^2, \nu)\) be the characteristic triplet with standard truncation \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\). Suppose we shift to an alternative truncation radius \(c > 0\). Show that the new drift parameter \(b_c\) is given by \(b_c = b + \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} x \left( \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}} - \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}} \right) \nu(dx)\), and confirm that \(\psi(u)\) is invariant to the choice of \(c\).

Solution

The characteristic exponent under truncation radius \(c\) is \(\psi(u) = i b_c u - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 + \int (e^{i u x} - 1 - i u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}}) \nu(dx)\). Equating this with the expression under truncation radius 1 yields: \(i b_c u - i \int u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}} \nu(dx) = i b u - i \int u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}} \nu(dx)\). Dividing by \(i u\) and rearranging gives \(b_c = b + \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} x (\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}} - \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}) \nu(dx)\). The difference between the two indicator functions is supported on \([\min(1, c), \max(1, c)]\), which is bounded away from zero, so this integral is strictly finite. Substituting \(b_c\) back into the formula shows the terms cancel identically, leaving \(\psi(u)\) invariant.

Empirical

E1 · Jump-diffusion annual return and variance

A financial risk manager estimates the characteristic triplet of an asset log-return process as \(b = 0.05\), \(\sigma = 0.12\), and \(\nu(dx) = 0.5 \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi (0.04)^2}} e^{-\frac{(x + 0.03)^2}{2(0.04)^2}} dx\). Calculate the theoretical annual mean \(\E[X_1]\) and annual variance \(\Var(X_1)\).

Solution

This is a Merton jump-diffusion model with \(\lambda = 0.5\), \(\mu_Y = -0.03\), and \(\sigma_Y = 0.04\). The jump magnitude satisfies \(|x| \le 1\) with probability exceeding \(1 - 10^{-15}\). For this finite activity model: \(\E[X_1] = b + \sigma \E[W_1] + \lambda \mu_Y = 0.05 + 0.5 \times (-0.03) = 0.05 - 0.015 = 0.035\). Total variance is the sum of diffusion and jump variance: \(\Var(X_1) = \sigma^2 + \lambda \E[Y^2] = (0.12)^2 + 0.5 \times ((-0.03)^2 + (0.04)^2) = 0.0144 + 0.5 \times 0.0025 = 0.0144 + 0.00125 = 0.01565\).

E2 · Variance Gamma Lévy density parameters

Consider a Variance Gamma process with \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(\nu = 0.25\), and \(\theta = -0.10\). Its Lévy density is \(k_{VG}(x) = \frac{1}{\nu |x|} e^{A x - B |x|}\) where \(A = \frac{\theta}{\sigma^2}\) and \(B = \frac{\sqrt{\theta^2 + 2 \sigma^2 / \nu}}{\sigma^2}\). Evaluate \(A\) and \(B\), and compute the jump arrival rate of positive jumps larger than \(\epsilon = 0.05\).

Solution

First compute \(A\) and \(B\): \(\sigma^2 = 0.04\). \(A = \frac{-0.10}{0.04} = -2.5\). Next, \(\theta^2 + \frac{2 \sigma^2}{\nu} = (-0.10)^2 + \frac{2 \times 0.04}{0.25} = 0.01 + 0.32 = 0.33\). Then \(\sqrt{0.33} \approx 0.574456\). Thus \(B = \frac{0.574456}{0.04} \approx 14.3614\). For positive jumps \(x > 0\), the decay rate is \(B - A = 14.3614 - (-2.5) = 16.8614\). The arrival rate of positive jumps exceeding \(\epsilon = 0.05\) is \(\int_{0.05}^\infty \frac{1}{0.25 x} e^{-16.8614 x} dx = 4 \int_{0.05}^\infty \frac{e^{-16.8614 x}}{x} dx = 4 E_1(16.8614 \times 0.05) \approx 4 \times 0.315 = 1.26\) jumps per year.

E3 · Characteristic function decay and Fourier pricing

In numerical Fourier inversion of a Lévy characteristic function, explain why high jump activity (large \(\nu(\R)\)) accelerates decay of \(|\phi(u)|\) as \(u \to \infty\), whereas jump-diffusions with \(\sigma = 0\) and low jump activity decay slowly.

Solution

The modulus of the characteristic function is \(|\phi_{X_t}(u)| = \exp(t \Re(\psi(u)))\). The real part of the characteristic exponent is \(\Re(\psi(u)) = -\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 + \int (\cos(u x) - 1) \nu(dx)\). If \(\sigma > 0\), the Gaussian quadratic term \(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2\) guarantees super-exponential decay of order \(e^{-O(u^2)}\). If \(\sigma = 0\), decay is dictated exclusively by the jump integral \(\int (\cos(u x) - 1) \nu(dx) \le 0\). For infinite activity models with a singularity at the origin like CGMY with \(Y > 0\), \(\Re(\psi(u)) \sim - C |u|^Y\) as \(|u| \to \infty\), producing polynomial or sub-exponential decay. For finite activity compound Poisson models without Brownian diffusion, the jump integral is bounded by \(-2 \lambda\), so \(|\phi(u)|\) does not decay to zero as \(u \to \infty\), necessitating analytical damping for Fourier inversion.

Research

R1 · Spectrally negative Lévy processes in credit risk

In quantitative credit risk and sovereign default modeling, structural models often replace Brownian motion with a spectrally negative Lévy process (a Lévy process with no positive jumps, so \(\nu((0, \infty)) = 0\)). What mathematical advantage does spectral negativity offer for first-passage time and default probability calculations?

Solution

Spectrally negative Lévy processes possess the fundamental property of creeping: because the process can only jump downwards, it can only cross an upper barrier continuously without jumping over it. By spatial inversion, the first passage time below a lower barrier (default) possesses an explicit analytical Laplace transform governed by the scale functions \(W^{(q)}(x)\) and \(Z^{(q)}(x)\) (the fluctuation theory of Lévy processes). Unlike two-sided jump models where the asset price can overshoot the default boundary by an unknown random deficit, spectrally negative processes yield exact closed-form expressions for ruin probabilities and debt valuation.

R2 · Sato processes vs. autonomous Lévy dynamics

Contrast the Lévy–Khintchine representation of an autonomous Lévy process with the time-inhomogeneous additive process framework of Sato. When calibrating across multiple option maturities simultaneously, why are autonomous Lévy processes often inadequate?

Solution

An autonomous Lévy process has stationary increments, requiring that the characteristic exponent satisfies \(\psi_t(u) = t \psi(u)\). Consequently, its cumulants scale strictly linearly with time: \(\kappa_n(t) = t \kappa_n(1)\). This enforces that skewness decays as \(O(1/\sqrt{t})\) and excess kurtosis decays as \(O(1/t)\) as tenor increases. Empirical market volatility surfaces frequently exhibit term structures of skewness and kurtosis that decay much more slowly or exhibit term-dependent humps. Sato processes generalize Lévy processes by preserving independent increments but allowing time-inhomogeneous characteristic triplets \((b(t), \sigma^2(t), \nu_t)\), providing the parametric flexibility required to calibrate across all option expiries simultaneously.

R3 · Bochner subordination and subordinated Brownian motion

Discuss the concept of subordination in the sense of Bochner. If \(W_t\) is standard Brownian motion and \(T_t\) is an independent non-decreasing Lévy process (a subordinator) with Laplace exponent \(\ell(u) = -\ln \E[e^{-u T_1}]\), derive the characteristic function of the subordinated process \(X_t = W_{T_t}\).

Solution

Subordination evaluates a base process along a stochastic time clock \(T_t\). By the law of total expectation conditioning on the filtration generated by \(T_t\): \(\E[e^{i u X_t}] = \E\left[\E[e^{i u W_{T_t}} \mid T_t]\right]\). Because \(W\) is standard Brownian motion independent of \(T\), \(\E[e^{i u W_\tau} \mid T_t = \tau] = e^{-\frac{1}{2} u^2 \tau}\). Therefore: \(\E[e^{i u X_t}] = \E\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2} u^2 T_t}\right]\). By the definition of the subordinator Laplace exponent \(\E[e^{-s T_t}] = e^{-t \ell(s)}\), substituting \(s = \frac{1}{2} u^2\) yields: \(\E[e^{i u X_t}] = \exp\left(- t \, \ell\left(\tfrac{1}{2} u^2\right)\right)\). This demonstrates that subordinated Brownian motions are themselves Lévy processes whose characteristic exponent is given by \(\psi(u) = -\ell(\frac{1}{2} u^2)\).

8Formative Quiz

  1. Which of the following conditions is required for a measure \(\nu\) to be a valid Lévy measure on \(\R\)?

    Options

    By Definition 3.1, a Lévy measure need not be finite (\(\nu(\R) = \infty\) for infinite activity) nor integrate \(|x|\), but it must satisfy \(\int \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) < \infty\).

  2. What is the primary role of the linear term \(- i u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) in the Lévy–Khintchine representation?

    Options

    Near 0, \(e^{i u x} - 1 - i u x \approx -\frac{1}{2} u^2 x^2\), which is integrable against \(x^2 \nu(dx)\), ensuring convergence without requiring \(\int |x| \nu(dx) < \infty\).

  3. Under the Lévy–Khintchine theorem, what is the exact theoretical ratio of \(\ln \phi_{X_{1.0}}(u)\) to \(\ln \phi_{X_{0.5}}(u)\)?

    Because \(\ln \phi_{X_t}(u) = t \psi(u)\), the ratio is \(\frac{t_2 \psi(u)}{t_1 \psi(u)} = \frac{1.0}{0.5} = 2.0\).

  4. A Lévy process has \(\sigma = 0\) and \(\nu(dx) = \frac{3}{|x|^{1.5}} \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}} dx\). What are its jump activity and path variation?

    Options

    \(\int_0^1 x^{-1.5} dx = [-2 x^{-0.5}]_0^1 = \infty\) (infinite activity). But \(\int_0^1 x \cdot x^{-1.5} dx = \int_0^1 x^{-0.5} dx = [2 x^{0.5}]_0^1 = 2 < \infty\) (finite variation).

  5. Why does every Lévy process admit an orthogonal decomposition into independent parts (drift, diffusion, large jumps, compensated small jumps)?

    Options

    Theorem 3.4 (Lévy–Itô decomposition) proves that any Lévy process is the sum of four mutually independent stochastic processes.

  6. What is the continuous quadratic variation \([X^c, X^c]_1\) of a pure-jump Lévy process with \(\sigma = 0\)?

    By the Lévy–Itô decomposition, when the Gaussian component \(\sigma = 0\), the continuous martingale part \(X^c\) is identically zero, so \([X^c, X^c]_t = 0\).

  7. If \(X_t\) is a Lévy process with \(\sigma = 0.20\), what is the path variation of \(X_t\)?

    Options

    Standard Brownian motion has unbounded 1-variation almost surely on any compact interval; thus any process with \(\sigma > 0\) has infinite total variation.

  8. Under what condition can the truncation indicator \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) be entirely removed from the Lévy–Khintchine formula?

    Options

    When the jump component has finite variation, \(\int_{|x| \le 1} x \nu(dx)\) converges absolutely, allowing the linear centering term to be absorbed into the drift \(b_0\).
