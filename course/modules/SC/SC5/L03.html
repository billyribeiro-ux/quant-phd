Lévy Processes & The Lévy–Khintchine Representation

Continuous-path diffusions like Black–Scholes assume asset prices diffuse smoothly over infinitesimal time steps. Financial reality defies this assumption: markets undergo instantaneous crashes, discrete earnings jumps, and bursts of high-frequency micro-jumps. Lévy processes provide the unifying mathematical framework for all stationary, independent-increment continuous-time jump-diffusion processes. Through the celebrated Lévy–Khintchine representation, any such process is uniquely governed by a characteristic triplet \((b, \sigma^2, \nu)\). Mastering this architecture is prerequisite to pricing exotic derivatives, calibrating implied volatility skews, and understanding modern non-Gaussian asset models.

1Axiomatic Construction of Lévy Processes

In classical mathematical finance, Brownian motion models continuous diffusion, whereas compound Poisson processes model discrete, isolated arrivals. Both share two fundamental structural properties: stationary increments and independent increments. A Lévy process is the general continuous-time stochastic process possessing these exact symmetries [cont2003financial].

Definition 1.1 (Lévy Process) Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \Prob)\) be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions. An adapted stochastic process \(X = (X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) with values in \(\R\) is called a Lévy process if it satisfies the following four axioms:

  1. Initial condition: \(X_0 = 0\) almost surely.
  2. Independent increments: For any sequence of ordered times \(0 \le t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n\), the increments \(X_{t_1} - X_{t_0}, X_{t_2} - X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n} - X_{t_{n-1}}\) are mutually independent.
  3. Stationary increments: For any \(s < t\), the distribution of the increment \(X_t - X_s\) depends only on the elapsed time \(t - s\), so that \(X_t - X_s \stackrel{d}{=} X_{t - s}\).
  4. Stochastic continuity: For all \(s \ge 0\) and any \(\epsilon > 0\), \(\lim_{t \to s} \Prob(|X_t - X_s| > \epsilon) = 0\).

By standard regularisation theorems, every Lévy process admits a unique cádlág (right-continuous with left limits) modification, which we take as the canonical sample path version.

Remark 1.1 (Stochastic Continuity vs. Sample Path Continuity) Axiom 4 states that the probability of a jump occurring at any predetermined fixed time \(s\) is zero. It does not mean that sample paths are continuous functions of time. Jumps occur at totally inaccessible random stopping times, never at deterministic milestones.

2Infinite Divisibility

The requirement of stationary and independent increments imposes an algebraic symmetry on the marginal distributions of \(X_t\). Specifically, any realization over horizon \(t\) can be subdivided into \(n\) identically distributed independent increments of length \(t / n\).

Definition 2.1 (Infinitely Divisible Distribution) A probability distribution \(F\) on \(\R\) (or random variable \(Y\)) is called infinitely divisible if for every positive integer \(n \in \N_{\ge 1}\), there exist independent, identically distributed random variables \(Y_1^{(n)}, \dots, Y_n^{(n)}\) such that:

\[ Y \stackrel{d}{=} \sum_{k=1}^n Y_k^{(n)}. \]

Equivalently, the characteristic function \(\phi_Y(u) = \E[e^{i u Y}]\) has an \(n\)-th root \(\phi_n(u) = (\phi_Y(u))^{1/n}\) that is itself a valid characteristic function for every \(n \ge 1\).

Theorem 2.1 (Equivalence of Lévy Processes and Infinite Divisibility) Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a Lévy process. Then for every \(t \ge 0\), the distribution of \(X_t\) is infinitely divisible. Conversely, given any infinitely divisible distribution \(F\), there exists a unique (in law) Lévy process \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) such that \(X_1 \sim F\).

Proof For any \(t > 0\) and integer \(n \ge 1\), partition the interval \([0, t]\) into \(n\) equal subintervals \([k t / n, (k+1) t / n]\). The value \(X_t\) decomposes telescopically as:

\[ X_t = \sum_{k=1}^n \left( X_{\frac{k t}{n}} - X_{\frac{(k-1) t}{n}} \right). \]

By Axiom 2 (independent increments), the summands are mutually independent. By Axiom 3 (stationary increments), each summand has the exact distribution of \(X_{t/n}\). Hence \(X_t\) is the sum of \(n\) i.i.d. increments, proving infinite divisibility for all \(n\). The converse follows from Kolmogorov's extension theorem combined with the stochastic continuity axiom [cont2003financial].

Theorem 2.2 (Linearity of the Characteristic Exponent) Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a Lévy process with characteristic function \(\phi_{X_t}(u) = \E[e^{i u X_t}]\). There exists a continuous function \(\psi: \R \to \C\), termed the characteristic exponent (or Lévy exponent), such that for all \(t \ge 0\) and all \(u \in \R\):

\[ \phi_{X_t}(u) = \E\left[e^{i u X_t}\right] = e^{t \psi(u)}. \]

Proof Fix \(u \in \R\) and define \(g(t) = \E[e^{i u X_t}]\). For any \(s, t \ge 0\), by independent and stationary increments:

\[ g(t + s) = \E\left[e^{i u (X_{t+s} - X_t + X_t)}\right] = \E\left[e^{i u (X_{t+s} - X_t)}\right] \E\left[e^{i u X_t}\right] = g(s) g(t). \]

This is Cauchy's exponential functional equation. By stochastic continuity, \(g(t) \to g(0) = 1\) as \(t \downarrow 0\), implying \(g\) is continuous. The only non-vanishing continuous solutions to \(g(t + s) = g(t) g(s)\) on \([0, \infty)\) with \(g(0) = 1\) are of the form \(g(t) = e^{t \psi(u)}\) for some constant \(\psi(u) \in \C\).

3The Lévy–Khintchine Representation & Characteristic Triplet

The fundamental structural classification of all infinitely divisible characteristic functions was established by Paul Lévy (1934) and Aleksandr Khinchin (1937). It establishes that every Lévy process is uniquely characterised by three geometric components: a deterministic drift, a Gaussian diffusion, and a jump measure [cont2003financial].

Definition 3.1 (Lévy Measure) A Borel measure \(\nu\) on \(\R \setminus \{0\}\) is called a Lévy measure if it satisfies the integrability condition:

\[ \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) < \infty. \]

Equivalently: \(\int_{|x| \le 1} x^2 \nu(dx) < \infty\) and \(\nu(\{x \in \R : |x| > 1\}) < \infty\).

Theorem 3.1 (The Lévy–Khintchine Representation) Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a Lévy process on \(\R\). The characteristic exponent \(\psi(u)\) in \(\E[e^{i u X_t}] = e^{t \psi(u)}\) admits the unique Lévy–Khintchine representation:

\[ \psi(u) = i b u - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 + \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \left( e^{i u x} - 1 - i u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}} \right) \nu(dx), \]

where:

  • \(b \in \R\) is the drift parameter (under truncation function \(h(x) = x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\)),
  • \(\sigma \ge 0\) is the Gaussian diffusion volatility, and
  • \(\nu\) is a Lévy measure on \(\R \setminus \{0\}\).

The triplet \((b, \sigma^2, \nu)\) is called the characteristic triplet of \(X\). It determines the law of the Lévy process uniquely.

Proof Outline By Theorem 2.1, \(X_1\) is infinitely divisible. For each \(n \ge 1\), write \(X_1 = \sum_{k=1}^n Y_k^{(n)}\). Let \(\phi_n(u) = \E[e^{i u Y_1^{(n)}}]\). By Taylor expanding \(e^{i u x} - 1\), separating jumps smaller and larger than 1, and passing to the weak limit as \(n \to \infty\) using Helly's selection theorem and the Kolmogorov representation for distributions with finite variance, the limit of the exponent converges uniquely to \(\psi(u)\). The integrability condition \(\int \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) < \infty\) is both necessary and sufficient for \(\psi(u)\) to be negative definite and continuous at the origin, ensuring \(e^{\psi(u)}\) is a valid characteristic function by Bochner's theorem [cont2003financial].

Remark 3.1 (Role of the Truncation Function) The term \(- i u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) acts as a mathematical centering device. Near the origin \(x \to 0\), the integrand satisfies:

\[ e^{i u x} - 1 - i u x = -\frac{1}{2} u^2 x^2 + O(x^3). \]

Because \(\int_{|x| \le 1} x^2 \nu(dx) < \infty\), the integrand is bounded by \(C x^2\) near 0, making the integral absolutely convergent. Without the linear compensator \(- i u x\), the integrand \(e^{i u x} - 1 \sim i u x\) would require \(\int_{|x| \le 1} |x| \nu(dx) < \infty\), which is not satisfied by general Lévy processes (e.g., Cauchy or \(\alpha\)-stable processes with \(\alpha \ge 1\)).

4The Lévy–Itô Decomposition

The Lévy–Khintchine formula is not merely an analytical identity for characteristic functions; it corresponds to an explicit pathwise decomposition of the stochastic trajectory into four mutually independent, orthogonal physical mechanisms.

Theorem 4.1 (Lévy–Itô Pathwise Decomposition) Let \(X\) be a Lévy process with characteristic triplet \((b, \sigma^2, \nu)\). Almost surely, for all \(t \ge 0\), \(X_t\) decomposes as:

\[ X_t = b t + \sigma W_t + \int_0^t \int_{|x| > 1} x N(ds, dx) + \lim_{\epsilon \downarrow 0} \int_0^t \int_{\epsilon < |x| \le 1} x \left( N(ds, dx) - \nu(dx) ds \right), \]

where:

  1. \(b t\) is a deterministic linear drift;
  2. \(W_t\) is a standard Brownian motion;
  3. \(N(dt, dx) = \sum_{s: \Delta X_s \ne 0} \delta_{(s, \Delta X_s)}(dt, dx)\) is a Poisson random measure on \([0, \infty) \times (\R \setminus \{0\})\) with intensity measure \(dt \nu(dx)\);
  4. \(\int_0^t \int_{|x| > 1} x N(ds, dx) = \sum_{0 < s \le t, |\Delta X_s| > 1} \Delta X_s\) is a compound Poisson process representing all jumps with magnitude strictly greater than 1 (almost surely finite in number on any compact interval);
  5. The final term is a pure-jump square-integrable martingale representing the limit of compensated small jumps, converging uniformly on compact time intervals almost surely.

Furthermore, all four constituent processes are mutually independent.

Proof Outline For any \(\epsilon > 0\), the jump arrivals with \(|\Delta X_s| > \epsilon\) form a compound Poisson process with intensity \(\lambda_\epsilon = \nu(\{x: |x| > \epsilon\}) < \infty\). Subtracting this compound Poisson jump component leaves a Lévy process with bounded jumps. As \(\epsilon \downarrow 0\), the compensated sum of small jumps \(\int_0^t \int_{\epsilon < |x| \le 1} x \tilde{N}(ds, dx)\) forms an \(L^2\)-Cauchy sequence because \(\E\left[\left(\int_0^t \int_{\epsilon < |x| \le 1} x \tilde{N}\right)^2\right] = t \int_{\epsilon < |x| \le 1} x^2 \nu(dx) \le t \int_{|x| \le 1} x^2 \nu(dx) < \infty\). By Doob's martingale inequality, the sequence converges uniformly almost surely to a continuous-time martingale. The residual process is continuous, has stationary independent increments, and must therefore be of the form \(b t + \sigma W_t\) by the Lévy characterization of Brownian motion [cont2003financial].

5Jump Activity vs. Jump Variation

In quantitative modeling, understanding the path roughness of a Lévy process requires categorizing its jump activity and total variation.

Definition 5.1 (Activity and Variation Regimes) Let \(\nu\) be a Lévy measure. We classify the process into distinct analytical regimes:

Property Mathematical Condition Path Consequence Canonical Financial Model
Finite Activity \(\int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \nu(dx) < \infty\) Finitely many jumps in any compact interval \([0, T]\); compound Poisson jumps. Merton (1976), Kou (2002)
Infinite Activity \(\int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \nu(dx) = \infty\) Infinitely many small jumps in every time interval; dense jump arrivals. Variance Gamma, CGMY (\(Y \ge 0\))
Finite Variation \(\sigma = 0\) and \(\int_{|x| \le 1} |x| \nu(dx) < \infty\) Total variation \(\sum_{s \le t} |\Delta X_s| < \infty\) almost surely; no centering needed. Variance Gamma, CGMY (\(Y < 1\))
Infinite Variation \(\sigma > 0\) or \(\int_{|x| \le 1} |x| \nu(dx) = \infty\) Sample paths have unbounded variation over any non-empty interval; centering required. CGMY (\(1 \le Y < 2\)), Merton (\(\sigma > 0\))

Theorem 5.1 (Characteristic Exponent for Finite Variation Processes) If a Lévy process has paths of finite variation (\(\sigma = 0\) and \(\int_{|x| \le 1} |x| \nu(dx) < \infty\)), the truncation function can be omitted entirely, yielding the simplified representation:

\[ \psi(u) = i b_0 u + \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \left( e^{i u x} - 1 \right) \nu(dx), \]

where \(b_0 = b - \int_{|x| \le 1} x \nu(dx)\) represents the true drift of the process.

Proof Because \(\int_{|x| \le 1} |x| \nu(dx) < \infty\), the integral \(\int_{|x| \le 1} x \nu(dx)\) converges absolutely. We can therefore split the integrands:

\[ \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \left(e^{i u x} - 1 - i u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\right) \nu(dx) = \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} (e^{i u x} - 1) \nu(dx) - i u \int_{|x| \le 1} x \nu(dx). \]

Combining the linear imaginary term with \(i b u\) defines the un-truncated drift \(b_0 = b - \int_{|x| \le 1} x \nu(dx)\).

7Canon Breaks & Market Realities

In classical quantitative curricula centered exclusively on the Black–Scholes paradigm, several foundational concepts break down when confronted with market data and Lévy dynamics:

  • The Illusion of Continuity: High-frequency equity order book data demonstrates that prices do not diffuse across every intermediate dollar cent. When market orders deplete standing liquidity at the best quote, or when macroeconomic releases hit wire services, prices transition instantaneously over discrete gaps.
  • Path Variation Dichotomy: Classical finance assumes all processes are diffusions with quadratic variation \([X, X]_t = \sigma^2 t\). Under pure-jump Lévy processes (such as Variance Gamma), continuous quadratic variation is zero (\([X^c, X^c]_t = 0\)), yet the process exhibits rich stochastic variance driven entirely by discrete jump shocks.
  • Market Incompleteness: Under continuous Brownian motion with one source of risk, every contingent claim can be perfectly replicated by dynamic delta-hedging. Under jump processes with an infinite variety of jump sizes, perfect hedging is structurally impossible: jumps generate unhedgeable jump-to-default or jump-size risk, breaking Black–Scholes complete-market pricing.

8Common Pitfalls & Misconceptions

Misconception 1: "Stochastic continuity means that the sample paths of the process cannot jump."

Correction: Stochastic continuity requires \(\lim_{h \to 0} \Prob(|X_{t+h} - X_t| > \epsilon) = 0\) at any fixed deterministic time \(t\). A Poisson process with intensity \(\lambda\) satisfies \(\Prob(N_{t+h} - N_t \ge 1) = 1 - e^{-\lambda h} \to 0\) as \(h \to 0\), so it is perfectly stochastically continuous, even though 100% of its sample paths are step functions with discontinuous jumps.

Misconception 2: "The cutoff radius 1 in the truncation term \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) represents a physical threshold in market dynamics."

Correction: The cutoff at \(|x| \le 1\) is an arbitrary mathematical convention. One can replace \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) with \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}}\) for any \(c > 0\), or with any smooth truncation function \(h(x)\) such that \(h(x) \sim x\) near 0 and \(h(x) \to 0\) at infinity. Changing the truncation radius changes the numerical drift parameter \(b_c = b + \int (x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}} - x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}) \nu(dx)\), but leaves the underlying process and its distribution completely identical.

Misconception 3: "Infinite activity implies that the total variation of the path is infinite."

Correction: Infinite activity means that \(\nu(\R \setminus \{0\}) = \infty\), so an infinite number of microscopic jumps arrive in any interval. However, if \(\int_{|x| \le 1} |x| \nu(dx) < \infty\), the sum of the absolute magnitudes of these infinite jumps converges almost surely. Such a process (e.g., Variance Gamma) has infinite jump activity yet strictly finite total variation.

9Comprehensive Problem Set

Tier 1: Mechanics

Problem 1.1: Consider a compound Poisson process with arrival intensity \(\lambda = 4.0\) and two-point jump distribution \(\Prob(Y = +0.02) = 0.60\) and \(\Prob(Y = -0.05) = 0.40\). Express its Lévy measure \(\nu(dx)\) as a linear combination of Dirac delta measures, and evaluate \(\int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx)\).

Solution

For a compound Poisson process, the Lévy measure is \(\nu(dx) = \lambda F_Y(dx)\). Here the jump distribution has discrete atoms at \(+0.02\) and \(-0.05\). Thus:

\[ \nu(dx) = 4.0 \times \left( 0.60 \, \delta_{+0.02}(dx) + 0.40 \, \delta_{-0.05}(dx) \right) = 2.4 \, \delta_{+0.02}(dx) + 1.6 \, \delta_{-0.05}(dx). \]

Because both jump sizes satisfy \(|x| \le 1\), \(\min(1, x^2) = x^2\). Evaluating the integral:

\[ \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) = 2.4 \times (0.02)^2 + 1.6 \times (-0.05)^2 = 2.4 \times 0.0004 + 1.6 \times 0.0025 = 0.00096 + 0.00400 = 0.00496. \]

Problem 1.2: Let \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a standard Cauchy process whose density at \(t=1\) is \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi (1 + x^2)}\). What is its characteristic exponent \(\psi(u)\), and does this process have finite or infinite total variation?

Solution

The characteristic function of a standard Cauchy distribution at \(t=1\) is \(\phi(u) = e^{-|u|}\). Since \(\phi_{X_t}(u) = e^{t \psi(u)}\), we have \(\psi(u) = -|u|\). The corresponding Lévy measure is \(\nu(dx) = \frac{1}{\pi x^2} dx\). In the small-jump neighborhood near zero, \(\int_{-1}^1 |x| \nu(dx) = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^1 \frac{x}{x^2} dx = \frac{2}{\pi} [\ln x]_0^1 = \infty\). Because the absolute jump integral diverges at the origin, the standard Cauchy process has paths of strictly infinite total variation.

Problem 1.3: A pure-jump Lévy process has Lévy measure \(\nu(dx) = \frac{5}{|x|} e^{-2 |x|} dx\). Determine whether this process exhibits (a) finite or infinite jump activity, and (b) finite or infinite total variation.

Solution

First, test jump activity by integrating \(\nu\) over \(\R \setminus \{0\}\): near \(x = 0\), the integrand behaves as \(\frac{5}{|x|}\). The integral \(\int_0^1 \frac{5}{x} dx = 5 [\ln x]_0^1 = \infty\). Thus \(\nu(\R \setminus \{0\}) = \infty\), proving the process exhibits infinite jump activity. Next, test total variation by integrating \(|x| \nu(dx)\) on \([-1, 1]\): \(\int_{-1}^1 |x| \frac{5}{|x|} e^{-2|x|} dx = 2 \int_0^1 5 e^{-2 x} dx = 10 \left[ -\frac{1}{2} e^{-2x} \right]_0^1 = 5 (1 - e^{-2}) \approx 4.3233 < \infty\). Because this integral converges, the process exhibits finite total variation.

Tier 2: Proof & Theory

Problem 2.1: Prove that if \((X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a Lévy process with \(\E[|X_t|] < \infty\), then \(\E[X_t] = t \E[X_1]\), and deduce that \(M_t = X_t - t \E[X_1]\) is a martingale with respect to its natural filtration.

Solution

Let \(m(t) = \E[X_t]\). For any \(s, t \ge 0\), stationary and independent increments imply \(\E[X_{t+s}] = \E[X_t + (X_{t+s} - X_t)] = \E[X_t] + \E[X_s]\), so \(m(t + s) = m(t) + m(s)\). Stochastic continuity implies \(m(t)\) is continuous in \(t\). The only continuous solution to Cauchy's additive functional equation is \(m(t) = c t\), where \(c = m(1) = \E[X_1]\). Now consider \(M_t = X_t - c t\). For \(s \le t\), \(\E[M_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \E[X_s + (X_t - X_s) - c t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = X_s + \E[X_t - X_s] - c t = X_s + c(t - s) - c t = X_s - c s = M_s\). Hence \(M_t\) is a continuous-time martingale.

Problem 2.2: Prove that the characteristic exponent \(\psi(u)\) of any Lévy process satisfies \(\psi(0) = 0\) and \(\overline{\psi(u)} = \psi(-u)\), and deduce that the real part \(\Re(\psi(u)) \le 0\) for all \(u \in \R\).

Solution

At \(u = 0\), \(\E[e^{i 0 X_1}] = \E[1] = 1 = e^{\psi(0)}\), which requires \(\psi(0) = 0\). For complex conjugation, \(\overline{\E[e^{i u X_1}]} = \E[\overline{e^{i u X_1}}] = \E[e^{-i u X_1}] = e^{\psi(-u)}\). Simultaneously, \(\overline{e^{\psi(u)}} = e^{\overline{\psi(u)}}\), implying \(\overline{\psi(u)} = \psi(-u)\). For the real part, by Jensen's inequality and the property of characteristic functions, \(|\phi_{X_1}(u)| = |e^{\psi(u)}| = e^{\Re(\psi(u))} \le \E[|e^{i u X_1}|] = 1\). Taking natural logarithms of both sides yields \(\Re(\psi(u)) \le 0\) for all \(u \in \R\).

Problem 2.3: Let \((b, \sigma^2, \nu)\) be the characteristic triplet with standard truncation \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\). Suppose we shift to an alternative truncation radius \(c > 0\). Show that the new drift parameter \(b_c\) is given by \(b_c = b + \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} x \left( \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}} - \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}} \right) \nu(dx)\), and confirm that \(\psi(u)\) is invariant to the choice of \(c\).

Solution

The characteristic exponent can be expressed under truncation radius \(c\) as \(\psi(u) = i b_c u - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 + \int (e^{i u x} - 1 - i u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}}) \nu(dx)\). Equating this with the expression under truncation radius 1 yields: \(i b_c u - i \int u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}} \nu(dx) = i b u - i \int u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}} \nu(dx)\). Dividing by \(i u\) and rearranging gives \(b_c = b + \int_{\R \setminus \{0\}} x (\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le c\}} - \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}) \nu(dx)\). The difference between the two indicator functions is supported on \([\min(1, c), \max(1, c)]\), which is bounded away from zero, so this integral is strictly finite. Substituting \(b_c\) back into the formula shows the terms identically cancel, leaving \(\psi(u)\) invariant.

Tier 3: Empirical & Numerical

Problem 3.1: A financial risk manager estimates the characteristic triplet of an asset log-return process as \(b = 0.05\), \(\sigma = 0.12\), and \(\nu(dx) = 0.5 \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi (0.04)^2}} e^{-\frac{(x + 0.03)^2}{2(0.04)^2}} dx\). Calculate the theoretical annual mean \(\E[X_1]\) and annual variance \(\Var(X_1)\).

Solution

This is a Merton jump-diffusion model with \(\lambda = 0.5\), \(\mu_Y = -0.03\), and \(\sigma_Y = 0.04\). The jump magnitude satisfies \(|x| \le 1\) with overwhelming probability (\(> 1 - 10^{-15}\)). For this finite activity model, the drift in uncompensated form is \(b_0 = b - \int_{|x| \le 1} x \nu(dx) \approx b - \lambda \mu_Y\). However, working directly from the compound Poisson moments: \(\E[X_1] = b + \sigma \E[W_1] + \lambda \mu_Y = 0.05 + 0.5 \times (-0.03) = 0.05 - 0.015 = 0.035\). The total variance is the sum of diffusion and jump variance: \(\Var(X_1) = \sigma^2 + \lambda \E[Y^2] = (0.12)^2 + 0.5 \times ((-0.03)^2 + (0.04)^2) = 0.0144 + 0.5 \times (0.0009 + 0.0016) = 0.0144 + 0.5 \times 0.0025 = 0.0144 + 0.00125 = 0.01565\).

Problem 3.2: Consider a Variance Gamma process with \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(\nu = 0.25\), and \(\theta = -0.10\). Its Lévy density is \(k_{VG}(x) = \frac{1}{\nu |x|} e^{A x - B |x|}\) where \(A = \frac{\theta}{\sigma^2}\) and \(B = \frac{\sqrt{\theta^2 + 2 \sigma^2 / \nu}}{\sigma^2}\). Evaluate \(A\) and \(B\), and compute the jump arrival rate of positive jumps larger than \(\epsilon = 0.05\).

Solution

First compute \(A\) and \(B\): \(\sigma^2 = 0.04\). \(A = \frac{-0.10}{0.04} = -2.5\). Next, \(\theta^2 + \frac{2 \sigma^2}{\nu} = (-0.10)^2 + \frac{2 \times 0.04}{0.25} = 0.01 + 0.32 = 0.33\). Then \(\sqrt{0.33} \approx 0.574456\). Thus \(B = \frac{0.574456}{0.04} \approx 14.3614\). For positive jumps \(x > 0\), the decay rate is \(B - A = 14.3614 - (-2.5) = 16.8614\). The arrival rate of positive jumps exceeding \(\epsilon = 0.05\) is \(\int_{0.05}^\infty \frac{1}{0.25 x} e^{-16.8614 x} dx = 4 \int_{0.05}^\infty \frac{e^{-16.8614 x}}{x} dx = 4 E_1(16.8614 \times 0.05) = 4 E_1(0.84307)\). Using the exponential integral approximation \(E_1(0.843) \approx 0.315\), the arrival rate is approximately \(4 \times 0.315 = 1.26\) jumps per year.

Problem 3.3: In numerical Fourier inversion of a Lévy characteristic function, explain why high jump activity (large \(\nu(\R)\)) accelerates decay of \(|\phi(u)|\) as \(u \to \infty\), whereas jump-diffusions with \(\sigma = 0\) and low jump activity decay slowly.

Solution

The modulus of the characteristic function is \(|\phi_{X_t}(u)| = \exp(t \Re(\psi(u)))\). The real part of the characteristic exponent is \(\Re(\psi(u)) = -\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 + \int (\cos(u x) - 1) \nu(dx)\). If \(\sigma > 0\), the Gaussian quadratic term \(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2\) guarantees super-exponential decay of order \(e^{-O(u^2)}\). If \(\sigma = 0\), decay is dictated exclusively by the jump integral \(\int (\cos(u x) - 1) \nu(dx) \le 0\). For infinite activity models with a singularity at the origin like CGMY with \(Y > 0\), \(\Re(\psi(u)) \sim - C |u|^Y\) as \(|u| \to \infty\), producing polynomial or sub-exponential decay. For finite activity compound Poisson models without Brownian diffusion, the jump integral is bounded by \(-2 \lambda\), so \(|\phi(u)|\) does not decay to zero as \(u \to \infty\), necessitating analytical damping for Fourier inversion.

Tier 4: Research & Extensions

Problem 4.1: In quantitative credit risk and sovereign default modeling, structural models often replace Brownian motion with a spectrally negative Lévy process (a Lévy process with no positive jumps, so \(\nu((0, \infty)) = 0\)). What mathematical advantage does spectral negativity offer for first-passage time and default probability calculations?

Solution

Spectrally negative Lévy processes possess the fundamental property of creeping: because the process can only jump downwards, it can only cross an upper barrier continuously without jumping over it. By spatial inversion, the first passage time below a lower barrier (default) possesses an explicit analytical Laplace transform governed by the scale functions \(W^{(q)}(x)\) and \(Z^{(q)}(x)\) (the fluctuation theory of Lévy processes). Unlike two-sided jump models where the asset price can overshoot the default boundary by an unknown random deficit, spectrally negative processes yield exact closed-form expressions for ruin probabilities and debt valuation.

Problem 4.2: Contrast the Lévy–Khintchine representation of an autonomous Lévy process with the time-inhomogeneous additive process framework of Sato. When calibrating across multiple option maturities simultaneously, why are autonomous Lévy processes often inadequate?

Solution

An autonomous Lévy process has stationary increments, requiring that the characteristic exponent satisfies \(\psi_t(u) = t \psi(u)\). Consequently, its cumulants scale strictly linearly with time: \(\kappa_n(t) = t \kappa_n(1)\). This enforces that skewness decays as \(O(1/\sqrt{t})\) and excess kurtosis decays as \(O(1/t)\) as tenor increases. Empirical market volatility surfaces frequently exhibit term structures of skewness and kurtosis that decay much more slowly or exhibit term-dependent humps. Sato processes generalize Lévy processes by preserving independent increments but allowing time-inhomogeneous characteristic triplets \((b(t), \sigma^2(t), \nu_t)\), providing the parametric flexibility required to calibrate across all option expiries simultaneously.

Problem 4.3: Discuss the concept of subordination in the sense of Bochner. If \(W_t\) is standard Brownian motion and \(T_t\) is an independent non-decreasing Lévy process (a subordinator) with Laplace exponent \(\ell(u) = -\ln \E[e^{-u T_1}]\), derive the characteristic function of the subordinated process \(X_t = W_{T_t}\).

Solution

Subordination evaluates a base process along a stochastic time clock \(T_t\). By the law of total expectation conditioning on the filtration generated by \(T_t\): \(\E[e^{i u X_t}] = \E\left[\E[e^{i u W_{T_t}} \mid T_t]\right]\). Because \(W\) is standard Brownian motion independent of \(T\), \(\E[e^{i u W_\tau} \mid T_t = \tau] = e^{-\frac{1}{2} u^2 \tau}\). Therefore: \(\E[e^{i u X_t}] = \E\left[e^{-\frac{1}{2} u^2 T_t}\right]\). By the definition of the subordinator Laplace exponent \(\E[e^{-s T_t}] = e^{-t \ell(s)}\), substituting \(s = \frac{1}{2} u^2\) yields: \(\E[e^{i u X_t}] = \exp\left(- t \, \ell\left(\tfrac{1}{2} u^2\right)\right)\). This demonstrates that subordinated Brownian motions are themselves Lévy processes whose characteristic exponent is given by \(\psi(u) = -\ell(\frac{1}{2} u^2)\).

11Formative Quiz

  1. Which of the following conditions is required for a measure \(\nu\) to be a valid Lévy measure on \(\R\)?

    Options

    By Definition 3.1, a Lévy measure need not be finite (\(\nu(\R) = \infty\) for infinite activity) nor integrate \(|x|\), but it must satisfy \(\int \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) < \infty\).

  2. What is the primary role of the linear term \(- i u x \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) in the Lévy–Khintchine representation?

    Options

    Near 0, \(e^{i u x} - 1 - i u x \approx -\frac{1}{2} u^2 x^2\), which is integrable against \(x^2 \nu(dx)\), ensuring convergence without requiring \(\int |x| \nu(dx) < \infty\).

  3. Under the Lévy–Khintchine theorem, what is the exact theoretical ratio of \(\ln \phi_{X_{1.0}}(u)\) to \(\ln \phi_{X_{0.5}}(u)\)?

    Because \(\ln \phi_{X_t}(u) = t \psi(u)\), the ratio is \(\frac{t_2 \psi(u)}{t_1 \psi(u)} = \frac{1.0}{0.5} = 2.0\).

  4. A Lévy process has \(\sigma = 0\) and \(\nu(dx) = \frac{3}{|x|^{1.5}} \mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}} dx\). What are its jump activity and path variation?

    Options

    \(\int_0^1 x^{-1.5} dx = [-2 x^{-0.5}]_0^1 = \infty\) (infinite activity). But \(\int_0^1 x \cdot x^{-1.5} dx = \int_0^1 x^{-0.5} dx = [2 x^{0.5}]_0^1 = 2 < \infty\) (finite variation).

  5. Why does every Lévy process admit an orthogonal decomposition into independent parts (drift, diffusion, large jumps, compensated small jumps)?

    Options

    Theorem 4.1 (Lévy–Itô decomposition) proves that any Lévy process is the sum of four mutually independent stochastic processes.

  6. What is the continuous quadratic variation \([X^c, X^c]_1\) of a pure-jump Lévy process with \(\sigma = 0\)?

    By the Lévy–Itô decomposition, when the Gaussian component \(\sigma = 0\), the continuous martingale part \(X^c\) is identically zero, so \([X^c, X^c]_t = 0\).

  7. If \(X_t\) is a Lévy process with \(\sigma = 0.20\), what is the path variation of \(X_t\)?

    Options

    Standard Brownian motion has unbounded 1-variation almost surely on any compact interval; thus any process with \(\sigma > 0\) has infinite total variation.

  8. Under what condition can the truncation indicator \(\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}\) be entirely removed from the Lévy–Khintchine formula?

    Options

    By Theorem 5.1, when the jump component has finite variation, \(\int_{|x| \le 1} x \nu(dx)\) converges absolutely, allowing the linear centering term to be absorbed into the drift \(b_0\).
