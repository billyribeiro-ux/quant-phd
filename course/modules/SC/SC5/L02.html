Merton's Jump-Diffusion Model

  • Uses: geometric Brownian motion, Itô's lemma, continuous-time martingale, normal distribution, Compound Poisson process

Standard geometric Brownian motion predicts that short-maturity implied volatility smiles flatten out to horizontal lines. In real options markets, short-dated options display steep asymmetric smiles. Robert C. Merton resolved this divergence in 1976 by augmenting continuous Brownian diffusion with compound Poisson jump shocks. Deriving the compensated drift and analytical series expansion provides the foundation for jump modeling in derivatives pricing.

1Model Formulation and Stochastic Differential Dynamics

In standard Black–Scholes theory, the underlying asset price \(S = (S_t)_{t \ge 0}\) follows geometric Brownian motion with continuous sample paths. Merton (1976) observed that financial markets experience two fundamentally distinct categories of information arrivals: continuous day-to-day balance of buy and sell orders, modeled by standard Brownian diffusion, and discrete, large news events (earnings surprises, geopolitical shocks, regulatory decisions), modeled by discrete Poisson jumps.

Definition 1.1 (Merton Jump-Diffusion Model)

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \Prob)\) be a filtered probability space carrying a standard Brownian motion \(B = (B_t)_{t \ge 0}\) and an independent Poisson process \(N = (N_t)_{t \ge 0}\) with constant arrival intensity \(\lambda > 0\). Let \((Y_k)_{k \ge 1}\) be a sequence of independent and identically distributed lognormal random variables, independent of \(B\) and \(N\), with \(\ln Y_k \sim \Normal(\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\). The Merton jump-diffusion model specifies asset price dynamics under the real-world measure \(\Prob\) as the stochastic differential equation:

\[ \frac{dS_t}{S_{t-}} = \mu dt + \sigma dB_t + (Y_t - 1) dN_t, \]

where \(S_{t-} = \lim_{s \uparrow t} S_s\) is the left limit, \(\mu \in \R\) is the instantaneous expected return, \(\sigma > 0\) is the continuous diffusion volatility, and when a jump occurs at time \(t\) (\(\Delta N_t = 1\)), the asset price jumps from \(S_{t-}\) to \(S_t = S_{t-} Y_t\).

Definition 1.2 (Expected Percentage Jump and Drift Compensator)

Because \(\ln Y \sim \Normal(\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\), the expectation of the jump amplitude \(Y\) is given by the lognormal moment generating function:

\[ \E[Y] = \exp\left( \mu_j + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_j^2 \right). \]

The expected relative price change upon a jump is defined as \(\kappa \coloneqq \E[Y - 1] = \exp(\mu_j + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_j^2) - 1\). The expected jump return per unit time is therefore \(\lambda \kappa\).

2Exact Analytical Solution and Risk-Neutral Measure

Theorem 2.1 (Exact Pathwise Solution to the Merton SDE)

Assumptions: (A1) \(S_t\) satisfies Definition 1.1 with initial price \(S_0 > 0\); (A2) \(B_t\) and \(N_t\) are independent; (A3) \(J_k \coloneqq \ln Y_k \iid \Normal(\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\).

Statement: The unique strong solution to the Merton jump-diffusion SDE is given explicitly by:

\[ S_t = S_0 \exp\left( \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma B_t \right) \prod_{k=1}^{N_t} Y_k = S_0 \exp\left( \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma B_t + \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k \right). \]
Proof

Apply Itô's formula for jump semimartingales to \(f(x) = \ln x\). Between jump arrivals \(T_{k-1} < t < T_k\), the path is continuous and driven by \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dB_t\). By standard Itô calculus:

\[ d(\ln S_t) = \frac{1}{S_t} dS_t^c - \frac{1}{2S_t^2} (dS_t^c)^2 = \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)dt + \sigma dB_t. \]

At jump arrival time \(T_k\), the path jumps discontinuously from \(S_{T_k-}\) to \(S_{T_k} = S_{T_k-} Y_k\). The jump in log price is:

\[ \Delta \ln S_{T_k} = \ln S_{T_k} - \ln S_{T_k-} = \ln(S_{T_k-} Y_k) - \ln S_{T_k-} = \ln Y_k = J_k. \]

Integrating from \(0\) to \(t\) sums the continuous diffusion integral and the discrete jump increments:

\[ \ln S_t - \ln S_0 = \int_0^t \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)ds + \int_0^t \sigma dB_s + \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k = \left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma B_t + \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k. \]

Exponentiating both sides yields the stated formula.

Theorem 2.2 (Risk-Neutral Pricing Dynamics and Martingale Compensation)

Assumptions: (A1) The riskless borrowing rate is constant \(r \ge 0\) and continuous dividend yield is \(q \ge 0\); (A2) \(\mathbb{Q}\) is an equivalent martingale pricing measure under which \(B_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\) is standard Brownian motion and \(N_t\) is a Poisson process with intensity \(\lambda\); (A3) Jump sizes under \(\mathbb{Q}\) remain lognormal with parameters \((\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\).

Statement: To ensure that the discounted total return \(e^{-(r - q)t} S_t\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale, the drift of the underlying price must satisfy \(\E_{\mathbb{Q}}[dS_t / S_{t-}] = (r - q)dt\), requiring the risk-neutral SDE:

\[ \frac{dS_t}{S_{t-}} = (r - q - \lambda \kappa) dt + \sigma dB_t^{\mathbb{Q}} + (Y_t - 1) dN_t. \]

Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the exact log-price dynamic is:

\[ S_t = S_0 \exp\left( \left(r - q - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 - \lambda \kappa\right)t + \sigma B_t^{\mathbb{Q}} + \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k \right). \]
Proof

Compute the expectation \(\E_{\mathbb{Q}}[S_t]\) from the exact path solution. Because \(B_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\), \(N_t\), and \((J_k)\) are mutually independent:

\[ \E_{\mathbb{Q}}[S_t] = S_0 e^{(r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 - \lambda \kappa)t} \E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{\sigma B_t^{\mathbb{Q}}}\right] \E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k\right)\right]. \]

For standard Brownian motion, \(\E[e^{\sigma B_t}] = e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t}\). For the compound Poisson jump factor, by Theorem 3.2 of Lesson 1:

\[ \E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{N_t} Y_k\right] = \exp\left(\lambda t (\E_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y] - 1)\right) = \exp(\lambda t \kappa). \]

Multiplying the terms:

\[ \E_{\mathbb{Q}}[S_t] = S_0 e^{(r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 - \lambda \kappa)t} e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t} e^{\lambda \kappa t} = S_0 e^{(r - q)t}. \]

Discounting by \(e^{-rt}\) yields \(\E_{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{-rt} S_t] = S_0 e^{-qt}\), which establishes that \(e^{-(r-q)t} S_t\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale.

3Merton's Analytical Series Option Pricing Formula

Theorem 3.1 (Merton Poisson Series Formula)

Assumptions: (A1) The risk-neutral price \(S_\tau\) follows Theorem 2.2 over tenor \(\tau > 0\); (A2) \(C_{BS}(S, K, \tau, \sigma_{\text{vol}}, r_{\text{rate}})\) denotes the standard Black–Scholes European call formula with spot \(S\), strike \(K\), tenor \(\tau\), volatility \(\sigma_{\text{vol}}\), and risk-free rate \(r_{\text{rate}}\).

Statement: The European call option price \(C(S_0, K, \tau)\) under Merton's jump-diffusion model is given by the rapidly convergent series of Black–Scholes prices:

\[ C(S_0, K, \tau) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{e^{-\lambda \tau} (\lambda \tau)^n}{n!} C_{BS}\left(S_n, K, \tau, \sigma_n, r\right), \]

where for each jump count \(n \in \N_0\):

\[ S_n \coloneqq S_0 (1 + \kappa)^n e^{-\lambda \kappa \tau}, \quad \text{and} \quad \sigma_n \coloneqq \sqrt{\sigma^2 + \frac{n \sigma_j^2}{\tau}}. \]
Proof

Condition on the event \(\{N_\tau = n\}\) that exactly \(n\) jumps occur over \([0, \tau]\). By law of total expectation:

\[ C(S_0, K, \tau) = e^{-r \tau} \E_{\mathbb{Q}}[(S_\tau - K)^+] = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \Prob(N_\tau = n) e^{-r \tau} \E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[(S_\tau - K)^+ \;\middle|\; N_\tau = n\right]. \]

Conditional on \(N_\tau = n\), the sum of independent Gaussian jump returns is \(\sum_{k=1}^n J_k \sim \Normal(n \mu_j, n \sigma_j^2)\). Combining this with the independent Brownian diffusion \(\sigma B_\tau \sim \Normal(0, \sigma^2 \tau)\), the conditional log-return has conditional variance \(v_n^2 = \sigma^2 \tau + n \sigma_j^2 = \sigma_n^2 \tau\), and conditional mean:

\[ m_n = \left(r - q - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 - \lambda \kappa\right)\tau + n \mu_j. \]

The conditional terminal price is lognormally distributed with mean \(\E_{\mathbb{Q}}[S_\tau \mid N_\tau = n] = S_0 e^{m_n + v_n^2 / 2}\). Expanding the exponent:

\[ m_n + \tfrac{1}{2}v_n^2 = (r - q - \lambda \kappa)\tau + n\left(\mu_j + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_j^2\right) = (r - q - \lambda \kappa)\tau + n \ln(1 + \kappa). \]

Hence \(S_0 e^{m_n + v_n^2/2} = S_0 (1 + \kappa)^n e^{-\lambda \kappa \tau} e^{(r - q)\tau} = S_n e^{(r - q)\tau}\). Evaluating the standard lognormal expectation for a European call with adjusted spot \(S_n\) and annualized volatility \(\sigma_n\) yields the Black–Scholes formula \(C_{BS}(S_n, K, \tau, \sigma_n, r)\). Multiplying by the Poisson probability \(p_n = e^{-\lambda \tau}(\lambda \tau)^n / n!\) and summing over \(n\) completes the proof.

4Where the canon breaks

The foundational insight of the Black–Scholes–Merton continuous-time framework is that options are redundant assets: dynamic continuous rebalancing of a delta-neutral portfolio \(\Pi = V - \Delta S\) completely eliminates risk, yielding a unique, preference-free arbitrage price. When stock prices exhibit jumps, this continuous replication canon collapses. At any jump instant \(T_k\), the asset price discontinues by random percentage \(Y_k - 1\). The change in portfolio value is \(\Delta \Pi = [V(S_{t-} Y_k) - V(S_{t-})] - \Delta S_{t-} (Y_k - 1)\). Because option value \(V\) is a nonlinear function of stock price, no single choice of delta \(\Delta\) can set this jump shortfall to zero across all possible realization values of \(Y_k\). Consequently, markets driven by jump processes are fundamentally incomplete (Cont & Tankov 2004). Option pricing requires selecting an equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), which introduces a non-zero jump-risk premium determined by investor risk preferences.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Merton's jump-diffusion model is complete because the jump times follow a Poisson process.”

    Even though the arrival times are governed by a single intensity parameter \(\lambda\), the jump magnitude \(Y\) is continuous. To dynamically hedge both the continuous Brownian risk and every potential jump amplitude would require an infinite continuum of simultaneously traded liquid vanilla options, which do not exist in practice.

  2. “The risk-neutral drift of \(\ln S_t\) under Merton's model is simply \(r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\).”

    This ignores the expected jump increment \(\lambda \kappa = \lambda (\E[Y] - 1)\). If jumps are not compensated by subtracting \(\lambda \kappa\), the expected discounted stock price \(\E_{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{-rt} S_t]\) will equal \(S_0 e^{\lambda \kappa t} \ne S_0\), violating the fundamental theorem of asset pricing.

  3. “Merton's series formula is an approximation that only works for small jump intensities.”

    The series expansion is mathematically exact: it is the rigorous conditioning of the expected payoff on the disjoint partition of all possible integer jump counts \(\{N_\tau = n\}\). Because the Poisson distribution has factorial decay, summing fewer than 30 terms evaluates the true theoretical price to floating-point precision.

Mechanics

M1 · Risk-neutral jump drift compensation

A stock price follows Merton's jump-diffusion model with risk-free rate \(r = 0.05\), continuous dividend yield \(q = 0.01\), volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), jump intensity \(\lambda = 0.80\), mean log-jump \(\mu_j = -0.15\), and log-jump volatility \(\sigma_j = 0.10\). Calculate the expected percentage jump \(\kappa\) and the risk-neutral drift of the log price.

Solution

Compute the expected jump amplitude \(\E[Y]\):

\[ \E[Y] = \exp\left(\mu_j + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_j^2\right) = \exp(-0.15 + 0.5 \times 0.01) = \exp(-0.145) \approx 0.865021. \]

The expected relative jump is \(\kappa = \E[Y] - 1 \approx 0.865021 - 1 = -0.134979\) (average downward jump of 13.5%). The jump drift compensator is \(-\lambda \kappa = -0.80 \times (-0.134979) = +0.107983\). The risk-neutral drift of \(\ln S_t\) is:

\[ \alpha_{\mathbb{Q}} = r - q - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 - \lambda \kappa = 0.05 - 0.01 - 0.5(0.04) + 0.107983 = 0.02 + 0.107983 = 0.127983. \]
M2 · Conditional volatility across jump states

Using the parameters from M1, compute the effective conditional volatility \(\sigma_n\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 5\) jumps over an investment horizon of \(\tau = 0.25\) years (3 months).

Solution

The conditional volatility formula from Theorem 3.1 is \(\sigma_n = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + \frac{n \sigma_j^2}{\tau}}\). With \(\sigma^2 = 0.04\), \(\sigma_j^2 = 0.01\), and \(\tau = 0.25\), the incremental variance per jump is \(\sigma_j^2 / \tau = 0.01 / 0.25 = 0.04\). Thus:

  • \(n = 0\): \(\sigma_0 = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.2000\) (20.0%).
  • \(n = 1\): \(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.04} = \sqrt{0.08} \approx 0.2828\) (28.3%).
  • \(n = 2\): \(\sigma_2 = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.08} = \sqrt{0.12} \approx 0.3464\) (34.6%).
  • \(n = 5\): \(\sigma_5 = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.20} = \sqrt{0.24} \approx 0.4899\) (49.0%).
M3 · Leading-order Poisson series approximation

For short maturities (\(\tau \ll 1\)), the Poisson series is dominated by the zero-jump and one-jump terms. For \(\lambda = 0.5\) and \(\tau = 0.10\), evaluate the Poisson probability weights \(p_0, p_1, p_2\) and the residual probability of observing 3 or more jumps.

Solution

The Poisson intensity parameter is \(\mu = \lambda \tau = 0.5 \times 0.10 = 0.05\). The weights are:

\[ p_0 = e^{-0.05} \approx 0.951229, \quad p_1 = 0.05 e^{-0.05} \approx 0.047561, \quad p_2 = \frac{0.05^2}{2} e^{-0.05} \approx 0.001189. \]

The sum of the first three terms is \(p_0 + p_1 + p_2 \approx 0.999979\). The residual probability of 3 or more jumps is \(1 - (p_0 + p_1 + p_2) \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-5}\) (approx 0.002%), confirming that two terms account for 99.88% of option value.

P1 · Merton characteristic function derivation

Prove that the risk-neutral characteristic function of log price \(s_t \coloneqq \ln S_t\) under Merton's jump-diffusion model is given by \(\phi_{s_t}(u) = \E_{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{i u s_t}] = e^{i u \ln S_0} \exp(t \psi(u))\), where \(\psi(u) = i u (r - q - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 - \lambda \kappa) - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 + \lambda (e^{i u \mu_j - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_j^2 u^2} - 1)\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.2, the log price is \(s_t = \ln S_0 + \omega t + \sigma B_t^{\mathbb{Q}} + \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k\), where \(\omega = r - q - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 - \lambda \kappa\). By mutual independence of Brownian motion and compound Poisson jumps:

\[ \E_{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{i u s_t}] = e^{i u \ln S_0} e^{i u \omega t} \E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{i u \sigma B_t^{\mathbb{Q}}}\right] \E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\exp\left(i u \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k\right)\right]. \]

For standard Brownian motion, \(\E[e^{i u \sigma B_t}] = \exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 t)\). For the compound Poisson sum, each \(J_k \sim \Normal(\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\) has characteristic function \(\phi_J(u) = \exp(i u \mu_j - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_j^2 u^2)\). By Theorem 3.2 of Lesson 1:

\[ \E\left[\exp\left(i u \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k\right)\right] = \exp\left( \lambda t (\phi_J(u) - 1) \right) = \exp\left( \lambda t (e^{i u \mu_j - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_j^2 u^2} - 1) \right). \]

Combining all exponential arguments yields \(e^{i u \ln S_0} \exp(t \psi(u))\), with \(\psi(u) = i u \omega - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 + \lambda (e^{i u \mu_j - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_j^2 u^2} - 1)\).

P2 · Non-negativity of the asset price path

Prove that under Merton's jump-diffusion model with lognormal jump amplitude \(Y = e^J > 0\), the asset price \(S_t\) remains strictly positive for all \(t \ge 0\) almost surely whenever \(S_0 > 0\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.1, the pathwise solution is \(S_t = S_0 \exp\left( (\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t + \sigma B_t + \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k \right)\). On any finite interval \([0, t]\), \(N_t < \infty\) almost surely, and each \(J_k = \ln Y_k \in (-\infty, \infty)\) is real-valued. The argument inside the exponential is the sum of finite terms, hence strictly real. Because the exponential function \(\exp(x) > 0\) for all \(x \in \R\) and \(S_0 > 0\), \(S_t > 0\) for all \(t \ge 0\) almost surely.

P3 · Put-call parity preservation under Merton's model

Prove that the European option prices defined by Merton's Poisson series formula satisfy standard European put-call parity: \(C(S_0, K, \tau) - P(S_0, K, \tau) = S_0 e^{-q \tau} - K e^{-r \tau}\).

Solution

For each jump state \(n \in \N_0\), the conditional Black–Scholes call and put prices satisfy standard European put-call parity:

\[ C_{BS}(S_n, K, \tau, \sigma_n, r) - P_{BS}(S_n, K, \tau, \sigma_n, r) = S_n e^{-q \tau} - K e^{-r \tau}. \]

Weighting by the Poisson probabilities \(p_n = e^{-\lambda \tau}(\lambda \tau)^n / n!\) and summing over all \(n \ge 0\):

\[ C - P = \sum_{n=0}^\infty p_n (S_n e^{-q \tau} - K e^{-r \tau}) = e^{-q \tau} \sum_{n=0}^\infty p_n S_n - K e^{-r \tau} \sum_{n=0}^\infty p_n. \]

Since \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty p_n = 1\), the second term is \(K e^{-r \tau}\). For the first term, recall \(S_n = S_0 (1 + \kappa)^n e^{-\lambda \kappa \tau}\):

\[ \sum_{n=0}^\infty p_n S_n = S_0 e^{-\lambda \kappa \tau} \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{e^{-\lambda \tau}(\lambda \tau)^n}{n!} (1 + \kappa)^n = S_0 e^{-\lambda \kappa \tau} e^{-\lambda \tau} \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(\lambda(1 + \kappa)\tau)^n}{n!} = S_0 e^{-\lambda(1+\kappa)\tau} e^{\lambda(1+\kappa)\tau} = S_0. \]

Substituting this back gives \(C - P = S_0 e^{-q \tau} - K e^{-r \tau}\), confirming exact put-call parity.

Empirical

E1 · Implied volatility smile steepness at short tenors

Empirical index options at \(\tau = 1\) month exhibit an implied volatility skew of 8 percentage points between 90% and 100% moneyness, whereas at \(\tau = 1\) year the skew is only 2.5 percentage points. Explain how Merton's jump-diffusion model reproduces this term structure of skewness, citing the central limit theorem.

Solution

Under Merton's model, the total return over \([0, \tau]\) combines a continuous Gaussian diffusion \(\sigma B_\tau \sim \Normal(0, \sigma^2 \tau)\) and a compound Poisson jump sum \(\sum_{k=1}^{N_\tau} J_k\). At short maturities \(\tau \to 0\), the continuous diffusion variance scales as \(\sigma^2 \tau\), while the jump probability scales as \(\lambda \tau\). However, when a jump occurs, its magnitude is \(O(1)\), dominating the tails and generating extreme skewness \(\propto 1/\sqrt{\tau}\). At long maturities \(\tau \to \infty\), by the central limit theorem for independent increments, the normalized sum of jumps converges to Gaussianity, causing skewness and excess kurtosis to decay as \(O(1/\sqrt{\tau})\) and \(O(1/\tau)\), naturally flattening the implied volatility smile at distant horizons.

E2 · Calibrating Merton parameters from S&P 500 options

Nonlinear least-squares calibration of Merton's model to liquid S&P 500 index options across strikes and tenors yields parameter estimates \(\sigma = 0.12\), \(\lambda = 0.55\), \(\mu_j = -0.12\), and \(\sigma_j = 0.14\). Interpret the economic meaning of each parameter for an equity portfolio manager.

Solution

Economic interpretation:

  • \(\sigma = 12.0\%\): The continuous baseline volatility represents regular market noise in the absence of discrete shocks.
  • \(\lambda = 0.55\): An average of 0.55 market crash events per year, corresponding to roughly one significant jump every 1.8 years.
  • \(\mu_j = -12.0\%\): Jump events are systematically negative, representing an average market drop of 12% upon arrival (crashophobia / downside asymmetry).
  • \(\sigma_j = 14.0\%\): High uncertainty in jump magnitude, with realized drops ranging from mild (-2%) to severe (-40%).
E3 · Delta hedging error under unhedged jump risk

A trader holds a short position in 1,000 European call options and delta-hedges using standard Black–Scholes delta \(\Delta_{BS} = 0.52\) shares per option on stock price \(S = 100\). A sudden jump occurs: \(S\) drops by 15% to \(85\). If the option price falls from \(6.40\) to \(1.10\), compute the instantaneous P&L of the delta-neutral portfolio.

Solution

Portfolio consists of \(-1,000\) options and \(+520\) shares of stock. Prior to the jump, portfolio value is \(\Pi_{0} = 520 \times 100 - 1000 \times 6.40 = 52,000 - 6,400 = 45,600\). After the jump:

\[ \Pi_{\text{after}} = 520 \times 85 - 1000 \times 1.10 = 44,200 - 1,100 = 43,100. \]

The instantaneous P&L is \(\Delta \Pi = 43,100 - 45,600 = -1,500\). Despite being delta-neutral, the trader incurs a $1,500 loss because the discrete jump violated the continuous Taylor expansion \(\Delta S \to 0\), demonstrating that discrete jumps expose delta-hedged books to unhedged gamma and jump shortfalls.

Research

R1 · Partial integro-differential equation (PIDE) for Merton options

Derive the partial integro-differential equation (PIDE) satisfied by the European option pricing function \(V(S, t)\) under Merton's jump-diffusion model by applying Feynman–Kac for jump processes.

Solution

By risk-neutral valuation, the discounted option price \(e^{-rt} V(S_t, t)\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale. Applying Itô's formula for jump processes to \(e^{-rt} V(S_t, t)\) and setting the drift of the differential to zero gives:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + (r - q - \lambda \kappa) S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + \lambda \int_0^\infty \left[ V(S y, t) - V(S, t) \right] f_Y(y) dy - r V = 0, \]

subject to terminal boundary condition \(V(S, T) = (S - K)^+\). The non-local integral term represents the expected jump in option value across the lognormal density \(f_Y(y)\), replacing the standard local Black–Scholes PDE with an integro-differential operator.

R2 · Kou's double exponential jump-diffusion versus Merton lognormal jumps

Contrast Merton's lognormal jump distribution with Kou's (2002) double exponential jump-diffusion model, where jump size \(Y = e^J\) has density \(f_J(y) = p \eta_1 e^{-\eta_1 y} \indic_{y \ge 0} + (1-p) \eta_2 e^{\eta_2 y} \indic_{y < 0}\). Discuss analytical tractability for path-dependent options (such as barrier and American options).

Solution

In Merton's model, the lognormal density has smooth tails and allows an infinite series solution for vanilla European options, but solving boundary-crossing problems (first-passage times for barrier and American options) is analytically intractable because overshoot distributions across barriers are complex. In Kou's model, the double exponential distribution possesses the memoryless property of exponential tails. Consequently, the overshoot over a barrier is also exponentially distributed, enabling exact closed-form Laplace transform solutions for barrier, lookback, and American options without requiring numerical PIDE solvers.

R3 · Quadratic hedging and market incompleteness in jump models

Formulate the local risk-minimization (Föllmer–Sondermann) hedging problem for an option payoff \(H\) in Merton's incomplete jump market. Derive the optimal stock holding \(\Delta_t^*\) and explain why the residual hedging risk cannot vanish.

Solution

In an incomplete market, a contingent claim \(H\) cannot be perfectly replicated. Quadratic hedging minimizes expected squared tracking error \(\E_{\mathbb{P}}[(H - V_T)^2]\). By the Kunita–Watanabe decomposition, the optimal stock delta is the orthogonal projection of the option increment onto the stock increment:

\[ \Delta_t^* = \frac{d\langle V, S \rangle_t}{d\langle S, S \rangle_t} = \frac{\sigma^2 S_{t-} \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \lambda \E[(V(S_{t-}Y) - V(S_{t-}))(Y - 1) S_{t-}]}{\sigma^2 S_{t-}^2 + \lambda \E[(Y - 1)^2] S_{t-}^2}. \]

The optimal delta is a weighted compromise between the continuous Black–Scholes delta \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\) and the discrete jump difference ratio. The residual variance \(\langle V - \Delta^* S \rangle_T > 0\) represents intrinsic market incompleteness risk that can only be mitigated by trading additional options.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. What probability distribution does Merton's (1976) model assume for the jump amplitude \(Y\)?

    Options

    By Definition 1.1, Merton assumed \(\ln Y \sim \Normal(\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\), ensuring that the asset price remains strictly positive after any jump.

  2. Why must the risk-neutral drift of the Merton jump-diffusion SDE include the compensation term \(-\lambda \kappa\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 2.2, jumps add expected return \(\lambda \kappa t\). Subtracting \(\lambda \kappa t\) restores the risk-neutral expected growth rate to \(r - q\).

  3. If \(\mu_j = 0.08\) and \(\sigma_j = 0.20\), what is the expected relative price jump \(\kappa = \E[Y - 1]\)?

    \(\E[Y] = \exp(0.08 + 0.5 \times 0.04) = \exp(0.10) \approx 1.10517\). Thus \(\kappa = 1.10517 - 1 = 0.10517\).

  4. How does Merton's analytical option pricing formula evaluate the price of a European call option?

    Options

    By Theorem 3.1, conditioning on the number of jumps \(n\) yields a Black–Scholes price for each state, weighted by the Poisson probability \(p_n\).

  5. If continuous volatility \(\sigma = 0.15\), jump volatility \(\sigma_j = 0.10\), and tenor \(\tau = 0.25\), what is the conditional volatility \(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_j^2 / \tau}\) for \(n = 1\) jump?

    \(\sigma^2 = 0.0225\), \(\sigma_j^2 / \tau = 0.01 / 0.25 = 0.04\). \(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{0.0225 + 0.04} = \sqrt{0.0625} = 0.25\).

  6. Why does delta hedging fail to eliminate all risk in a jump-diffusion market?

    Options

    A jump creates an \(O(1)\) price change where nonlinear option payoff curvature produces an unhedgeable jump shortfall.

  7. What empirical feature of options surfaces does Merton's jump-diffusion model capture that continuous geometric Brownian motion cannot?

    Options

    At short horizons, jumps dominate the probability distribution, producing steep out-of-the-money put skews.

  8. What is the probability that asset price \(S_t\) becomes negative at any finite time \(t > 0\) in Merton's model?

    Because \(S_t = S_0 \exp(\dots)\) and the exponential function is strictly positive, \(\Prob(S_t \le 0) = 0\).
