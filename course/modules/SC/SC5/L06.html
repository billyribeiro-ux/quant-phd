Pricing Lévy Models via Characteristic Functions

In Black–Scholes, option pricing reduces to evaluating the Gaussian cumulative distribution function \(N(d_1)\). For general Lévy models—such as Merton jump-diffusion, Variance Gamma, and CGMY—the transition probability density of log-prices is rarely expressible in terms of elementary functions. However, thanks to the Lévy–Khintchine theorem, their characteristic functions are completely analytical. The breakthrough of Peter Carr and Dilip Madan (1999) unlocked option valuation by establishing that the Fourier transform of a damped European call payoff can be computed in closed form and inverted instantaneously via numerical quadrature. This Fourier inversion engine forms the computational backbone of modern derivative trading desks and model calibration pipelines.

1The Pricing Challenge & Carr–Madan Damping

Let \(S_t = S_0 e^{(r - q + \omega) t + X_t}\) denote the risk-neutral stock price under a general Lévy driver \(X\). The undiscounted European call price for maturity \(T\) and strike \(K = e^k\) is given by (Carr & Madan 1999):

\[ C_T(k) = e^{-r T} \E^*\left[ \left( S_T - e^k \right)^+ \right] = e^{-r T} \int_k^\infty \left( e^s - e^k \right) q_T(s) ds, \]

where \(s = \ln S_T\) and \(q_T(s)\) is the risk-neutral probability density of the terminal log-price. The fundamental methodology of pricing Lévy models through the characteristic function overcomes the absence of closed-form densities by working in the Fourier dual domain (Carr & Madan 1999).

Remark 1.1 (The \(L^1\) Integrability Obstacle)

Viewed as a function of log-strike \(k\), the call value satisfies \(\lim_{k \to -\infty} C_T(k) = S_0 e^{-q T} > 0\). Consequently \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty |C_T(k)| dk = \infty\). Because \(C_T(k)\) is not integrable on \(\R\) (\(C_T \notin L^1(\R)\)), its direct Fourier transform does not exist as an ordinary function.

Definition 1.2 (Damped Call Payoff)

For a fixed positive damping parameter \(\alpha > 0\), the damped European call price \(c_T(k)\) is defined as:

\[ c_T(k) \equiv e^{\alpha k} C_T(k). \]

For any \(\alpha > 0\) such that \(\E^*[S_T^{\alpha + 1}] < \infty\), \(c_T(k) \in L^1(\R) \cap L^2(\R)\), ensuring its Fourier transform is well-defined and square-integrable.

2Analytical Fourier Transform of the Damped Call

The remarkable insight of Carr and Madan is that integrating the damped call payoff against \(e^{i v k}\) collapses into a double integral that can be evaluated analytically in terms of the stock price characteristic function (Carr & Madan 1999).

Theorem 2.1 (Carr–Madan Transform Formula)

Let \(\phi_T(u) = \E^*[e^{i u \ln S_T}]\) be the risk-neutral characteristic function of the terminal log-price \(\ln S_T\). Let \(\alpha > 0\) be such that \(\E^*[S_T^{\alpha + 1}] < \infty\). The Fourier transform of the damped call price:

\[ \psi_T(v) \equiv \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{i v k} c_T(k) dk \]

is given in closed form by:

\[ \psi_T(v) = \frac{e^{-r T} \phi_T\left( v - (\alpha + 1) i \right)}{\alpha^2 + \alpha - v^2 + i (2 \alpha + 1) v}. \]
Proof

Substitute the definition of \(C_T(k)\) into the Fourier integral and interchange the order of integration via Fubini's theorem (justified since \(\E^*[S_T^{\alpha + 1}] < \infty\)):

\[ \psi_T(v) = e^{-r T} \int_{-\infty}^\infty q_T(s) \left( \int_{-\infty}^s e^{(\alpha + i v) k} (e^s - e^k) dk \right) ds. \]

Now evaluate the inner integral over \(k \in (-\infty, s]\):

\[ \int_{-\infty}^s \left( e^s e^{(\alpha + i v) k} - e^{(\alpha + 1 + i v) k} \right) dk = e^s \left[ \frac{e^{(\alpha + i v) k}}{\alpha + i v} \right]_{-\infty}^s - \left[ \frac{e^{(\alpha + 1 + i v) k}}{\alpha + 1 + i v} \right]_{-\infty}^s. \]

Because \(\alpha > 0\), \(\lim_{k \to -\infty} e^{(\alpha + i v) k} = 0\). Evaluating at \(k = s\):

\[ \frac{e^{(\alpha + 1 + i v) s}}{\alpha + i v} - \frac{e^{(\alpha + 1 + i v) s}}{\alpha + 1 + i v} = \frac{e^{(\alpha + 1 + i v) s}}{(\alpha + i v)(\alpha + 1 + i v)} = \frac{e^{(\alpha + 1 + i v) s}}{\alpha^2 + \alpha - v^2 + i (2 \alpha + 1) v}. \]

Substituting this back into the outer integral yields \(\frac{e^{-r T} \phi_T(v - (\alpha + 1) i)}{\alpha^2 + \alpha - v^2 + i (2 \alpha + 1) v}\), completing the proof.

3Real Fourier Inversion & Numerical Quadrature

Applying the classical Fourier inversion theorem reconstructs the option price from \(\psi_T(v)\) (Carr & Madan 1999).

Theorem 3.1 (Real Fourier Inversion Formula)

The European call option price for strike \(K = e^k\) is given by the real half-line integral:

\[ C_T(k) = \frac{e^{-\alpha k}}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \operatorname{Re}\left[ e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) \right] dv. \]
Proof

By the inverse Fourier transform: \(c_T(k) = \frac{1}{2 \pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) dv\). Since \(C_T(k)\) and \(e^{\alpha k}\) are real, \(c_T(k)\) is purely real. For any real-valued function, its Fourier transform satisfies Hermitian symmetry: \(\psi_T(-v) = \overline{\psi_T(v)}\). Splitting the integral over \((-\infty, 0)\) and \([0, \infty)\):

\[ \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) dv = \int_0^\infty \left( e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) + e^{i v k} \psi_T(-v) \right) dv = \int_0^\infty 2 \operatorname{Re}\left[ e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) \right] dv. \]

Dividing by \(2 \pi\) and un-damping by multiplying by \(e^{-\alpha k}\) yields the stated inversion integral.

Theorem 3.2 (FFT Strike Grid Formulation)

Let \(k_u = -b + u \Delta k\) for \(u = 0, 1, \dots, N-1\), where \(b = \frac{1}{2} N \Delta k\). By imposing the Nyquist reciprocity condition \(\Delta v \cdot \Delta k = \frac{2 \pi}{N}\), the Simpson sum for all \(N\) strikes simultaneously reduces to a Fast Fourier Transform:

\[ C_T(k_u) \approx \frac{e^{-\alpha k_u}}{\pi} \operatorname{Re}\left[ \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} e^{-i \frac{2 \pi}{N} j u} e^{i b v_j} \psi_T(v_j) \frac{\Delta v}{3} \left( 3 + (-1)^j - \delta_j \right) \right]. \]

"""Lab 6 · Option pricing via Carr-Madan damped Fourier transform and grid convergence.

Carr & Madan (1999) price European call options through the Fourier transform of the damped payoff:
c_T(k) = exp(alpha * k) * C_T(k),   k = ln(K).
The call price is recovered by the real Fourier inversion integral:
C(K) = (exp(-alpha * k) / pi) * integral_0^infinity Re[ exp(-i * v * k) * psi_T(v) ] dv,
where psi_T(v) = exp(-r * T) * phi_T(v - (alpha + 1)*i) / (alpha^2 + alpha - v^2 + i*(2*alpha + 1)*v).
This lab computes the ATM call price for a Variance Gamma model across Simpson quadrature grid sizes
N in {64, 128, 256, 512} with alpha = 1.5 and v_max = 100.0 (SIMULATED), verifies grid convergence,
and validates put-call parity to within 1e-6.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def vg_cf(
    u: complex | np.ndarray,
    tau: float,
    spot: float,
    r: float,
    sigma: float,
    nu: float,
    theta: float,
) -> complex | np.ndarray:
    arg = 1.0 - theta * nu - 0.5 * (sigma**2) * nu
    omega = (1.0 / nu) * math.log(arg)
    drift = (r + omega) * tau
    denom = 1.0 - 1j * u * theta * nu + 0.5 * (sigma**2) * nu * (u**2)
    return np.exp(1j * u * math.log(spot)) * np.exp(1j * u * drift) * (denom ** (-tau / nu))


def carr_madan_call(
    spot: float,
    strike: float,
    tau: float,
    r: float,
    sigma: float,
    nu: float,
    theta: float,
    n_pts: int,
    v_max: float = 100.0,
    alpha: float = 1.5,
) -> float:
    k = math.log(strike)
    v = np.linspace(0.0, v_max, n_pts + 1)
    dv = v_max / n_pts
    weights = np.ones(n_pts + 1)
    weights[1:-1:2] = 4.0
    weights[2:-2:2] = 2.0
    weights = weights * (dv / 3.0)

    u = v - (alpha + 1.0) * 1j
    phi = vg_cf(u, tau, spot, r, sigma, nu, theta)
    denom = (alpha**2 + alpha - v**2) + 1j * (2.0 * alpha + 1.0) * v
    psi = np.exp(-r * tau) * phi / denom
    exp_term = np.exp(-1j * v * k)
    integrand = (exp_term * psi).real
    integral = float(np.sum(integrand * weights))
    return float(math.exp(-alpha * k) / math.pi * integral)


def main() -> None:
    spot = 100.0
    strike = 100.0
    tau = 0.5
    r = 0.03
    sigma = 0.25
    nu = 0.20
    theta = -0.10

    grid_sizes = [64, 128, 256, 512]
    prices: list[float] = []

    print(f"Variance Gamma parameters: sigma={sigma}, nu={nu}, theta={theta}, r={r}, tau={tau} (SIMULATED)")
    print("Carr-Madan European ATM call prices across Simpson grid sizes:")

    for n in grid_sizes:
        p = carr_madan_call(spot, strike, tau, r, sigma, nu, theta, n_pts=n)
        prices.append(p)
        print(f"  N = {n:3d}: price = {p:.8f}")

    benchmark_price = prices[-1]
    discrepancy_256 = abs(prices[2] - benchmark_price)

    # Put-call parity at N=512
    call_512 = prices[-1]
    put_512 = call_512 - spot + strike * math.exp(-r * tau)
    parity_lhs = call_512 - put_512
    parity_rhs = spot - strike * math.exp(-r * tau)
    parity_error = abs(parity_lhs - parity_rhs)

    print(f"convergence discrepancy between N=256 and N=512: {discrepancy_256:.2e}")
    print(f"put-call parity check: C - P = {parity_lhs:.6f}, S0 - K*exp(-r*tau) = {parity_rhs:.6f}")
    print(f"parity error: {parity_error:.2e}")
    print("grid converged within 5e-4 at N=256:", discrepancy_256 < 5e-4)
    print("put-call parity holds within 1e-10:", parity_error < 1e-10)

4Where the canon breaks

The introduction of transform pricing fundamentally altered quantitative derivatives trading. Classical mathematical finance spent decades searching for analytical Green's functions and transition densities for jump SDEs. Carr–Madan proved that calculating the spatial density is completely unnecessary: option pricing can occur directly in the frequency domain (Carr & Madan 1999). Before Carr–Madan, calibrating jump processes required millions of Monte Carlo path simulations taking minutes or hours per optimization iteration. Transform inversion reduced pricing time to microseconds, enabling real-time intra-day calibration of Lévy surfaces.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The damping parameter \(\alpha\) is an economic parameter of the asset model.”

    The damping parameter \(\alpha\) is purely an auxiliary numerical artifact. It does not exist in the stochastic differential equation of the asset. The theoretical value of \(C_T(k)\) is completely independent of \(\alpha\), provided \(\alpha\) lies within the admissible strip of analyticity \(\E^*[S_T^{\alpha + 1}] < \infty\).

  2. “Using FFT is always faster and more accurate than direct Simpson quadrature.”

    FFT is only faster when evaluating a dense grid of hundreds or thousands of uniformly spaced strikes. When evaluating an empirical market surface containing 5 to 15 non-uniformly spaced strikes per maturity, the FFT requires computing \(N = 4096\) points and interpolating between grid nodes, introducing spline interpolation error. Direct Simpson quadrature computes the exact prices at the listed strikes faster and with zero interpolation error.

  3. “Put options must be priced using put-call parity because Carr–Madan only works for calls.”

    While put-call parity is fast and exact, put payoffs can also be priced directly via Fourier transform by choosing a negative damping parameter \(\alpha < -1\) (or using Lewis's \(\alpha = 0.5\) formulation). For \(\alpha < -1\), \(e^{\alpha k} P_T(k) \to 0\) as \(k \to \infty\) and \(k \to -\infty\), making the damped put square-integrable.

Mechanics

M1 · Complex denominator evaluation

Given damping parameter \(\alpha = 1.5\) and transform frequency \(v = 2.0\), calculate the exact complex value of the denominator \(D(v) = \alpha^2 + \alpha - v^2 + i (2 \alpha + 1) v\), and compute its squared modulus \(|D(v)|^2\).

Solution

Compute the real part: \(\alpha^2 + \alpha - v^2 = (1.5)^2 + 1.5 - (2.0)^2 = 2.25 + 1.5 - 4.0 = -0.25\). Compute the imaginary part: \((2 \alpha + 1) v = (2(1.5) + 1)(2.0) = 4.0 \times 2.0 = 8.0\). Thus: \(D(v) = -0.25 + 8.0 i\). The squared modulus is: \(|D(v)|^2 = (-0.25)^2 + (8.0)^2 = 0.0625 + 64.0 = 64.0625\).

M2 · Nyquist reciprocity grid spacing

In an FFT option pricing implementation with \(N = 2048\) points and integration step \(\Delta v = 0.05\), what is the resulting log-strike grid spacing \(\Delta k\) under the Nyquist condition, and what is the total strike span \(N \Delta k\)?

Solution

The Nyquist reciprocity condition requires \(\Delta v \cdot \Delta k = \frac{2 \pi}{N}\). Thus: \(\Delta k = \frac{2 \pi}{N \Delta v} = \frac{2 \pi}{2048 \times 0.05} = \frac{2 \pi}{102.4} \approx 0.0613592\). The total log-strike span is \(N \Delta k = \frac{2 \pi}{\Delta v} = \frac{2 \pi}{0.05} = 40 \pi \approx 125.66\), covering an enormous range of strikes.

M3 · Put-call parity price recovery

Given spot price \(S_0 = 100.0\), risk-free rate \(r = 0.05\), dividend yield \(q = 0.01\), maturity \(T = 0.50\), and strike \(K = 100.0\), suppose Carr–Madan Fourier inversion yields a call price of \(C = 5.8240\). Use put-call parity to compute the corresponding European put price \(P\).

Solution

By put-call parity: \(C - P = S_0 e^{-q T} - K e^{-r T}\). Compute the forward terms: \(S_0 e^{-q T} = 100.0 e^{-0.005} \approx 99.50125\). \(K e^{-r T} = 100.0 e^{-0.025} \approx 97.53099\). The forward difference is: \(99.50125 - 97.53099 = 1.97026\). Therefore: \(P = C - (S_0 e^{-q T} - K e^{-r T}) = 5.8240 - 1.97026 = 3.85374\).

P1 · Black–Scholes moment existence and damping admissibility

Prove that for a Black–Scholes geometric Brownian motion model where \(\ln S_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\ln S_0 + (r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2) T, \sigma^2 T)\), the characteristic function satisfies \(\E^*[S_T^{\alpha + 1}] < \infty\) for all \(\alpha > 0\), so that any positive damping parameter \(\alpha\) is admissible.

Solution

Let \(s_T = \ln S_T\). Under Black–Scholes, \(s_T\) is normally distributed with mean \(\mu = \ln S_0 + (r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2) T\) and variance \(\sigma^2 T\). The moment generating function of a Gaussian random variable is \(\E^*[e^{\lambda s_T}] = \exp( \lambda \mu + \frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 \sigma^2 T )\). For any finite \(\alpha > 0\), setting \(\lambda = \alpha + 1 < \infty\) yields \(\E^*[S_T^{\alpha + 1}] = \E^*[e^{(\alpha + 1) s_T}] = \exp( (\alpha + 1)\mu + \frac{1}{2}(\alpha + 1)^2 \sigma^2 T ) < \infty\). Because this expectation is finite for all real \(\lambda\), the strip of regularity is the entire complex plane, and any \(\alpha > 0\) is mathematically valid.

P2 · Lewis (2001) critical strip Fourier inversion

Derive the Lewis (2001) Fourier inversion formula for call options along the critical strip \(\operatorname{Im}(u) = 1/2\) and contrast its integrand decay with Carr–Madan.

Solution

Alan Lewis (2001) formulates option pricing using the Fourier transform of the payoff without explicit exponential damping by contour integration in the complex plane. Choosing the integration contour with imaginary part \(\operatorname{Im}(u) = 1/2\) (or \(u = v - \frac{1}{2} i\)), the call price is expressed as: \(C = S_0 e^{-q T} - \frac{\sqrt{S_0 K} e^{-r T}}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \operatorname{Re}[ e^{i v k} \frac{\phi_T(v - \frac{1}{2} i)}{v^2 + \frac{1}{4}} ] dv\). The denominator is \(v^2 + 1/4\), which is purely real and non-zero for all real \(v\), completely eliminating the complex denominator of Carr–Madan. The integrand decays as \(O(1/v^2)\), ensuring rapid convergence.

P3 · Hermitian symmetry and half-line reduction

Prove that if \(\phi_T(u)\) is the characteristic function of \(\ln S_T\), then \(\psi_T(-v) = \overline{\psi_T(v)}\), and deduce why the inversion integral over \((-\infty, \infty)\) can be evaluated strictly over the positive half-line \([0, \infty)\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.1, \(\psi_T(v) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{i v k} c_T(k) dk\). Since the damped payoff \(c_T(k) = e^{\alpha k} C_T(k)\) is strictly real-valued: \(\overline{\psi_T(v)} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \overline{e^{i v k}} c_T(k) dk = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-i v k} c_T(k) dk = \psi_T(-v)\). Now consider the full inverse Fourier transform: \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) dv = \int_{-\infty}^0 e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) dv + \int_0^\infty e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) dv\). Change variables \(v \to -v\) in the negative integral: \(\int_0^\infty e^{i v k} \psi_T(-v) dv = \int_0^\infty e^{i v k} \overline{\psi_T(v)} dv = \int_0^\infty \overline{e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v)} dv\). Summing the two integrals: \(e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) + \overline{e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v)} = 2 \operatorname{Re}[ e^{-i v k} \psi_T(v) ]\). Thus the integral over \(\R\) reduces to twice the real part integrated over \([0, \infty)\).

Empirical

E1 · Variance Gamma damping moment check

In calibrating Variance Gamma with \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(\nu = 0.50\), and \(\theta = -0.10\), determine whether the canonical damping choice \(\alpha = 1.5\) satisfies the moment existence condition \(\theta \nu (\alpha + 1) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu (\alpha + 1)^2 < 1\).

Solution

For \(\alpha = 1.5\), \(\alpha + 1 = 2.5\). Compute the linear term: \(\theta \nu (\alpha + 1) = -0.10 \times 0.50 \times 2.5 = -0.125\). Compute the quadratic term: \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu (\alpha + 1)^2 = 0.5 \times (0.20)^2 \times 0.50 \times (2.5)^2 = 0.0625\). Sum the two terms: \(-0.125 + 0.0625 = -0.0625\). Because \(-0.0625 < 1\), the condition is comfortably satisfied, and \(\phi_T(v - (\alpha + 1) i)\) is analytic and strictly finite.

E2 · Short-tenor frequency spreading and Gibbs oscillations

An algorithmic pricing system evaluates European calls using Carr–Madan with \(v_{\max} = 50.0\). For short maturities (\(\tau = 1\) week), the numerical prices exhibit high-frequency oscillations across strikes. Explain the cause and remedy.

Solution

As maturity \(\tau \to 0\), the distribution of \(\ln S_T\) becomes extremely concentrated around \(\ln S_0\). By the scaling property of Fourier transforms, concentration in the spatial domain forces the characteristic function \(\phi_T(v)\) to spread out across a much wider frequency band in the Fourier domain. Truncating the integral at \(v_{\max} = 50.0\) cuts off a significant portion of the integrand's mass, creating Gibbs phenomenon oscillations (ringing artifacts). The remedy is to dynamically scale the integration limit inversely with maturity: choose \(v_{\max} \propto 1 / \sqrt{\tau}\) (e.g., \(v_{\max} = 250.0\) for \(\tau = 0.02\)), and increase \(N\) proportionally to maintain fine grid resolution \(\Delta v\).

E3 · Quadrature vs. Monte Carlo complexity

Contrast the computational complexity of calibrating a Lévy model to an option surface of 5 tenors and 10 strikes (50 options) using (a) Carr–Madan direct Simpson quadrature, versus (b) Monte Carlo simulation with 50,000 paths per pricing evaluation across 100 optimizer iterations.

Solution

(a) Under direct Simpson quadrature with \(N = 256\), evaluating one maturity's characteristic function vector on \([0, v_{\max}]\) takes 256 complex evaluations. Evaluating 10 strikes requires a matrix multiplication of size \(10 \times 256\). For 5 tenors, this takes approximately \(5 \times 0.1 \text{ ms} = 0.5 \text{ ms}\) per objective call. Over 100 optimizer iterations, total calibration time is \(100 \times 0.5 \text{ ms} = 50 \text{ ms} = 0.05 \text{ seconds}\). (b) Under Monte Carlo, simulating 50,000 paths across 5 tenors requires generating 250,000 random variables, taking roughly 150 ms per iteration. For 100 iterations, this requires 15 seconds and yields stochastic gradient noise. Carr–Madan is 300 times faster and provides completely deterministic, smooth gradients.

Research

R1 · The Fourier Cosine (COS) method

Fang and Oosterlee (2008) introduced the COS method based on Fourier cosine series expansions. What theoretical advantage does the COS method offer over Carr–Madan FFT for pricing plain vanilla and early-exercise options?

Solution

The COS method replaces the continuous Fourier transform with a Fourier cosine series expansion on a truncated interval \([a, b]\). Because the cosine series coefficients of the plain vanilla payoff can be calculated analytically without numerical quadrature, the option price is represented as a single dot product between the payoff coefficients and the characteristic function values \(\phi(k \pi / (b - a))\). This achieves exponential convergence for smooth densities, requiring only \(N = 64\) to \(128\) terms for machine precision (\(10^{-12}\)), completely bypasses damping parameter selection, and extends naturally to American and Bermudan options via dynamic programming on the cosine coefficients.

R2 · Transform pricing of digital options

How can Carr–Madan transform pricing be extended to evaluate European digital (cash-or-nothing) options paying \(\mathbf{1}_{\{S_T \ge K\}}\)?

Solution

The undiscounted digital payoff is \(\mathbf{1}_{\{s \ge k\}}\). As \(k \to -\infty\), the payoff approaches 1, so damping is again required: \(d_T(k) = e^{\alpha k} e^{-r T} \Prob^*(S_T \ge e^k)\) with \(\alpha > 0\). The Fourier transform of the damped digital payoff evaluates to: \(\psi_T^{dig}(v) = \frac{e^{-r T} \phi_T(v - \alpha i)}{\alpha + i v}\). Notice that the denominator is linear (\(\alpha + i v\)) rather than quadratic, reflecting that the digital payoff is the derivative of the call payoff with respect to strike (\(-\partial C / \partial K\)). Fourier inversion then recovers the exact digital price.

R3 · Path-dependent options and Wiener–Hopf factorization

In pricing path-dependent exotic options (such as barrier or lookback options) under Lévy processes, why does the standard Carr–Madan Fourier inversion formula fail, and what mathematical tool replaces it?

Solution

Carr–Madan relies on the marginal distribution of the terminal log-price \(S_T\). Barrier and lookback options depend on the running extremum \(M_T = \sup_{0 \le t \le T} S_t\) or the first passage time \(\tau_B = \inf\{t: S_t \le B\}\). Under jump processes, the asset can jump over the barrier without touching it (overshoot). The marginal characteristic function \(\phi_T(u)\) contains no information about path extrema. Instead, path-dependent pricing under Lévy processes requires the Wiener–Hopf factorization of the characteristic exponent, which decomposes the characteristic exponent into the supremum and infimum processes, or solving partial integro-differential equations (PIDEs) via finite differences with Fourier boundary conditions.

Put-Call Parity \(P_T(K) = C_T(K) - S_0 e^{-q T} + K e^{-r T}\) Instantaneous arbitrage-free recovery of put options without separate transform.

8Formative Quiz

  1. Why cannot the raw European call option price \(C_T(k)\) be directly Fourier transformed without damping?

    Options

    By Remark 1.1, the call price does not decay to zero as \(k \to -\infty\), making the integral \(\int |C_T(k)| dk\) diverge.

  2. What is the argument passed into the stock price characteristic function \(\phi_T\) in the Carr–Madan formula?

    Options

    By Theorem 2.1, the numerator is \(e^{-r T} \phi_T(v - (\alpha + 1) i)\).

  3. What is the standard, canonical default value of the damping parameter \(\alpha\) recommended by Carr and Madan for equity option pricing?

    \(\alpha = 1.5\) is the standard default providing a balanced trade-off between denominator singularity and exponential explosion.

  4. Why can the Fourier inversion integral over \((-\infty, \infty)\) be reduced to an integral over \([0, \infty)\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 3.1, Hermitian symmetry allows combining positive and negative branches into \(2 \operatorname{Re}[\dots]\).

  5. What numerical quadrature scheme achieves fourth-order accuracy \(O(\Delta v^4)\) for Carr–Madan Fourier inversion?

    Options

    Composite Simpson's rule with weights \([1/3, 4/3, 2/3, \dots, 1/3]\) achieves fourth-order error convergence.

  6. Under exact put-call parity, what is the value of \(C - P - (S_0 e^{-q T} - K e^{-r T})\)?

    By no-arbitrage put-call parity, \(C - P = S_0 e^{-q T} - K e^{-r T}\), so the difference is identically 0.

  7. What phenomenon causes numerical Fourier inversion to produce oscillating errors at very short tenors (\(\tau \to 0\)) if \(v_{\max}\) is kept constant?

    Options

    As \(\tau \to 0\), the distribution sharpens in real space, spreading out into high frequencies in Fourier space; truncating at fixed \(v_{\max}\) causes truncation oscillations.

  8. Which mathematical condition must be checked before selecting a damping parameter \(\alpha\)?

    Options

    Finite \(\E^*[S_T^{\alpha+1}]\) is required so that \(\phi_T(v - (\alpha+1)i)\) is well-defined and analytic.
