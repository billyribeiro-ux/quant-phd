The Variance Gamma Model

Merton's jump-diffusion models price discontinuities as rare, large cataclysms superimposed on continuous Gaussian noise. In reality, financial order flow generates an infinite cascade of small price revisions alongside occasional substantial adjustments. Introduced by Dilip Madan, Peter Carr, and Eric Chang (1998), the Variance Gamma (VG) model discards continuous diffusion altogether, replacing it with a pure-jump Lévy process of infinite activity and finite variation. By evaluating Brownian motion along an independent stochastic Gamma time clock, VG provides an analytically tractable four-parameter framework that captures heavy tails, negative skewness, and short-dated implied volatility smiles without requiring a continuous Brownian driver.

1Subordination of Brownian Motion

The economic intuition behind the Variance Gamma model rests on the concept of business time versus calendar time. Rather than information arriving continuously and uniformly across calendar days, market activity accelerates during corporate announcements and slows during quiet trading sessions (Madan, Carr & Chang 1998).

Definition 1.1 (The Gamma Process)

A non-decreasing Lévy process \(G = (G_t)_{t \ge 0}\) with \(G_0 = 0\) is called a Gamma process with mean rate \(1\) and variance rate \(\nu > 0\) if for each \(t > 0\), \(G_t\) follows a Gamma distribution with shape parameter \(a = t / \nu\) and scale parameter \(b = \nu\):

\[ f_{G_t}(g) = \frac{g^{\frac{t}{\nu} - 1} e^{-\frac{g}{\nu}}}{\nu^{\frac{t}{\nu}} \Gamma\left(\frac{t}{\nu}\right)}, \quad g > 0. \]

The expectation and variance of the stochastic time clock are \(\E[G_t] = t\) and \(\Var(G_t) = \nu t\). The parameter \(\nu\) measures the variance of the economic time clock: as \(\nu \to 0\), \(G_t\) degenerates to deterministic calendar time \(t\).

Definition 1.2 (Variance Gamma via Subordination)

Let \(B = (B_s)_{s \ge 0}\) be a standard Brownian motion with drift \(\theta \in \R\) and volatility \(\sigma > 0\). Let \(G = (G_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be an independent Gamma process with mean rate 1 and variance rate \(\nu > 0\). The Variance Gamma process \(X = (X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is defined by evaluating arithmetic Brownian motion at the random time \(G_t\):

\[ X_t = \theta G_t + \sigma B(G_t). \]
Theorem 1.3 (Lévy Property of Subordinated Brownian Motion)

The subordinated process \(X_t = \theta G_t + \sigma B(G_t)\) is an autonomous Lévy process with stationary and independent increments.

Proof

Since \(G_0 = 0\), \(X_0 = \theta G_0 + \sigma B(0) = 0\). Let \(s < t\). Conditioning on the independent increment \(G_t - G_s = \Delta G\), the increment of the subordinated process is \(X_t - X_s = \theta (G_t - G_s) + \sigma (B(G_t) - B(G_s))\). By the strong Markov property and stationary increments of Brownian motion, \((B(G_t) - B(G_s)) \mid (G_t - G_s = \Delta G) \stackrel{d}{=} B(\Delta G)\). Because the increments of \(G\) are stationary and independent, \(X_t - X_s\) depends only on \(t - s\) and is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_s\). Stochastic continuity follows directly from the stochastic continuity of both \(B\) and \(G\).

2Characteristic Function & Moments

Because the conditional distribution of \(X_t\) given the time clock \(G_t\) is Gaussian, the unconditional characteristic function admits a simple closed-form algebraic expression (Madan, Carr & Chang 1998).

Theorem 2.1 (Variance Gamma Characteristic Function)

For any \(u \in \R\) such that \(1 - i \theta \nu u + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \nu u^2 \ne 0\), the characteristic function of the Variance Gamma process \(X_t\) is given by:

\[ \phi_{X_t}(u) = \E\left[e^{i u X_t}\right] = \left( 1 - i \theta \nu u + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \nu u^2 \right)^{-\frac{t}{\nu}}. \]

Equivalently, the characteristic exponent \(\psi_{VG}(u) = \frac{1}{t} \ln \phi_{X_t}(u)\) is:

\[ \psi_{VG}(u) = -\frac{1}{\nu} \ln\left( 1 - i \theta \nu u + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu u^2 \right). \]
Proof

Condition on the random time variable \(G_t = g\). Because \(B\) is standard Brownian motion independent of \(G\):

\[ \E\left[e^{i u X_t} \mid G_t = g\right] = \E\left[e^{i u (\theta g + \sigma B_g)}\right] = e^{i u \theta g} e^{-\frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 u^2 g} = \exp\left( \left( i \theta u - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 u^2 \right) g \right). \]

By the law of total expectation, \(\phi_{X_t}(u) = \E\left[\E\left[e^{i u X_t} \mid G_t\right]\right] = \E[ e^{- z G_t} ]\), where \(z = - i \theta u + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 u^2\). For a Gamma random variable with shape \(a = t / \nu\) and scale \(b = \nu\), its Laplace transform is \(\E[e^{- z G_t}] = (1 + \nu z)^{-t / \nu}\). Substituting \(z\) yields:

\[ \phi_{X_t}(u) = \left( 1 + \nu \left( -i \theta u + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2 \right) \right)^{-\frac{t}{\nu}} = \left( 1 - i \theta \nu u + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \nu u^2 \right)^{-\frac{t}{\nu}}. \]
Theorem 2.2 (Analytical Moments of Variance Gamma)

The mean, variance, skewness, and kurtosis of \(X_t\) are given in closed form by:

\[ \E[X_t] = \theta t, \qquad \Var(X_t) = \left( \sigma^2 + \nu \theta^2 \right) t, \] \[ \operatorname{Skew}(X_t) = \frac{\theta \nu \left( 3 \sigma^2 + 2 \nu \theta^2 \right)}{\left( \sigma^2 + \nu \theta^2 \right)^{3/2} \sqrt{t}}, \qquad \operatorname{Kurt}(X_t) = 3 + \frac{3 \nu \left( \sigma^4 + 2 \nu \theta^2 \sigma^2 + \nu^2 \theta^4 \right)}{\left( \sigma^2 + \nu \theta^2 \right)^2 t}. \]

In particular, when \(\theta < 0\), the distribution is negatively skewed. When \(\theta = 0\), skewness is exactly 0 and excess kurtosis simplifies to \(\frac{3 \nu}{t}\).

3Difference of Gammas & Lévy Measure

A remarkable algebraic feature of the Variance Gamma model is that any subordinated Brownian motion can be uniquely decomposed into the difference of two independent, monotonic Gamma processes representing upward and downward price adjustments (Madan, Carr & Chang 1998).

Theorem 3.1 (Difference of Gammas Decomposition)

Let \(X_t\) be a Variance Gamma process with parameters \((\sigma, \nu, \theta)\). There exist two independent standard Gamma processes \(\Gamma_t^{(p)}\) (positive jumps) and \(\Gamma_t^{(n)}\) (negative jumps) such that:

\[ X_t \stackrel{d}{=} \Gamma_t^{(p)} - \Gamma_t^{(n)}, \]

where the marginal densities have common shape parameter \(t / \nu\) and distinct scale parameters \(\eta_p\) and \(\eta_n\) given by:

\[ \eta_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\theta^2 \nu^2 + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu} + \frac{1}{2}\theta \nu, \qquad \eta_n = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\theta^2 \nu^2 + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu} - \frac{1}{2}\theta \nu. \]
Proof

Notice that \(\eta_p \eta_n = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu\) and \(\eta_p - \eta_n = \theta \nu\). Multiplying the linear binomials:

\[ (1 - i u \eta_p)(1 + i u \eta_n) = 1 - i u (\eta_p - \eta_n) + u^2 \eta_p \eta_n = 1 - i \theta \nu u + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu u^2. \]

Raising both sides to the power \(-t / \nu\) decomposes \(\phi_{X_t}(u)\) into the product of two characteristic functions of independent Gamma random variables with parameters \((t/\nu, \eta_p)\) and \(-(t/\nu, \eta_n)\).

Theorem 3.2 (Lévy Density and Martingale Correction)

The Variance Gamma process has Gaussian component \(\sigma_{diff} = 0\) and Lévy density:

\[ k_{VG}(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{\nu x} e^{- M x}, & x > 0 \\ \dfrac{1}{\nu |x|} e^{- G |x|}, & x < 0 \end{cases} \]

where \(M = 1 / \eta_p\) and \(G = 1 / \eta_n\). The discounted stock price \(e^{-(r - q) t} S_t\) under \(S_t = S_0 e^{(r - q + \omega) t + X_t}\) is a martingale if and only if:

\[ \omega = \frac{1}{\nu} \ln\left( 1 - \theta \nu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu \right), \quad \text{requiring } \theta \nu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu < 1. \]

Lab 4 · Variance Gamma path simulation via Gamma subordination versus analytical moments

Under Madan, Carr & Chang (1998), the Variance Gamma process \(X_t\) evaluated at horizon \(H = 0.5\) with parameters \(\sigma = 0.20, \nu = 0.25, \theta = -0.10\) has theoretical mean \(\E[X_H] = \theta H = -0.05\) and variance \(\Var(X_H) = (\sigma^2 + \nu \theta^2) H = (0.04 + 0.0025) \times 0.5 = 0.02125\). Simulating 10,000 realization paths (SIMULATED) produces sample moments matching theoretical values within 3 standard errors.

"""Lab 4 · Variance Gamma path simulation via Gamma subordination versus analytical moments.

Under Madan, Carr & Chang (1998), the Variance Gamma process X_t is obtained by evaluating Brownian motion with
drift theta and volatility sigma at a random time given by an independent Gamma process G_t with mean t and
variance nu * t:
X_t = theta * G_t + sigma * B(G_t),   where G_t ~ Gamma(shape=t/nu, scale=nu).
The analytical moments of X_t are:
E[X_t] = theta * t,
Var(X_t) = (sigma^2 + nu * theta^2) * t.
This lab simulates 10,000 realizations of X_H at horizon H = 0.5 (SIMULATED) using seed 42,
evaluates sample mean, sample variance, and sample skewness, and compares them against theoretical moments
within 3 standard errors.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    horizon = 0.5
    sigma = 0.25
    nu = 0.30
    theta = -0.15

    # Theoretical moments
    theo_mean = theta * horizon
    theo_var = (sigma**2 + nu * theta**2) * horizon

    n_paths = 10000
    x_vals: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(n_paths):
        # Sample G ~ Gamma(shape = horizon / nu, scale = nu)
        g = rng.gammavariate(horizon / nu, nu)
        z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        x = theta * g + sigma * math.sqrt(g) * z
        x_vals.append(x)

    sample_mean = sum(x_vals) / n_paths
    sample_var = sum((x - sample_mean) ** 2 for x in x_vals) / (n_paths - 1)
    sample_skew = sum((x - sample_mean) ** 3 for x in x_vals) / ((n_paths - 1) * (sample_var**1.5))

    se_mean = math.sqrt(sample_var / n_paths)
    se_var = math.sqrt(2.0 * (sample_var**2) / (n_paths - 1))

    print(f"simulated paths (SIMULATED): {n_paths}, horizon H: {horizon:.2f}")
    print(f"parameters: sigma = {sigma:.2f}, nu = {nu:.2f}, theta = {theta:.2f}")
    print(f"analytical moments: mean = {theo_mean:.6f}, variance = {theo_var:.6f}")
    print(f"sample moments: mean = {sample_mean:.6f} (se {se_mean:.6f})")
    print(f"sample variance = {sample_var:.6f} (se {se_var:.6f})")
    print(f"sample skewness = {sample_skew:.6f}")
    print("mean inside 3*se band:", abs(sample_mean - theo_mean) <= 3.0 * se_mean)
    print("variance inside 3*se band:", abs(sample_var - theo_var) <= 3.0 * se_var)

4Where the canon breaks

The transition from Merton jump-diffusion to Variance Gamma overturns established modeling paradigms. Classical models assume that Brownian motion is an indispensable base component of asset dynamics. Variance Gamma demonstrates that setting \(\sigma_{diff} = 0\) produces an exceptionally rich market model with infinite micro-jumps that emulate continuous diffusion at coarse scales while correctly pricing heavy tails (Madan, Carr & Chang 1998). Furthermore, the difference-of-Gammas representation reveals that any Variance Gamma path can be viewed as the net balance of two accumulated order-flow streams: buying volume (\(\Gamma^{(p)}\)) and selling volume (\(\Gamma^{(n)}\)).

5Misconceptions

  1. “Because Variance Gamma has infinite jump activity, simulating its sample paths requires drawing an infinite number of random numbers.”

    While the sample path exhibits infinitely many jumps in any non-empty time window, the marginal distribution over any discrete interval \(\Delta t\) is exactly subordinated: \(X_{t+\Delta t} - X_t = \theta \Delta G + \sigma \sqrt{\Delta G} Z\), where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and \(\Delta G \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\Delta t / \nu, \nu)\). Exact path simulation at discrete observation dates requires exactly two random draws per time step.

  2. “Variance Gamma is a stochastic volatility model like Heston.”

    Variance Gamma is a Lévy process with stationary and independent increments. In contrast, Heston (1993) is a continuous-path diffusion with a mean-reverting instantaneous variance process that has memory and dependent increments. VG generates volatility clustering across business time, but its increments over disjoint calendar intervals remain statistically independent.

  3. “The parameter \(\theta\) represents the expected annual return of the underlying stock.”

    The parameter \(\theta\) governs the drift of the subordinated Brownian motion, controlling the skewness of returns. In derivative pricing under \(\Prob^*\), the expected return of the stock is strictly constrained to the risk-free rate \(r - q\) by the martingale correction \(\omega\); \(\theta\) does not affect the risk-neutral mean return of the stock.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Risk-neutral martingale correction calculation

Given Variance Gamma parameters \(\sigma = 0.25\), \(\nu = 0.40\), and \(\theta = -0.15\), calculate the exact numerical value of the risk-neutral martingale correction \(\omega\).

Solution

From Theorem 3.2, \(\omega = \frac{1}{\nu} \ln(1 - \theta \nu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu)\). First compute the inner terms: \(\theta \nu = -0.15 \times 0.40 = -0.06\). \(\sigma^2 = (0.25)^2 = 0.0625\), so \(\frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \nu = 0.5 \times 0.0625 \times 0.40 = 0.0125\). Thus: \(1 - (-0.06) - 0.0125 = 1 + 0.06 - 0.0125 = 1.0475\). Evaluating the natural logarithm: \(\ln(1.0475) \approx 0.0464115\). Dividing by \(\nu = 0.40\): \(\omega = \frac{0.0464115}{0.40} \approx 0.116029\).

M2 · Symmetric Variance Gamma moments

For a symmetric Variance Gamma process with \(\theta = 0\), \(\sigma = 0.20\), and \(\nu = 0.50\), compute the annualized variance \(\Var(X_1)\) and the excess kurtosis \(\operatorname{Kurt}(X_1) - 3\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.2 with \(\theta = 0\) and \(t = 1\): \(\Var(X_1) = (\sigma^2 + \nu \theta^2) = \sigma^2 = (0.20)^2 = 0.040\). The excess kurtosis formula simplifies to: \(\operatorname{Kurt}(X_1) - 3 = \frac{3 \nu}{t} = \frac{3 \times 0.50}{1} = 1.50\).

M3 · Difference of Gammas scale parameters

Compute the parameters \(\eta_p\) and \(\eta_n\) of the difference-of-Gammas representation for \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(\nu = 0.20\), and \(\theta = -0.10\).

Solution

From Theorem 3.1: \(\theta \nu = -0.10 \times 0.20 = -0.02\). \(\frac{1}{2}\theta \nu = -0.01\). \(\frac{1}{4}\theta^2 \nu^2 = 0.0001\). \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu = \frac{1}{2}(0.04)(0.20) = 0.0040\). The radical term is \(\sqrt{0.0001 + 0.0040} = \sqrt{0.0041} \approx 0.064031\). Then: \(\eta_p = 0.064031 - 0.01 = 0.054031\), and \(\eta_n = 0.064031 - (-0.01) = 0.074031\).

P1 · Brownian motion limit as time variance vanishes

Prove that in the limit as the time variance parameter \(\nu \downarrow 0\), the Variance Gamma characteristic exponent \(\psi_{VG}(u)\) converges pointwise to the characteristic exponent of an arithmetic Brownian motion with drift \(\theta\) and volatility \(\sigma\).

Solution

Recall that \(\psi_{VG}(u) = - \frac{1}{\nu} \ln(1 - i \theta \nu u + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu u^2)\). Let \(z = - i \theta u + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2\), so the expression becomes \(- \frac{\ln(1 + \nu z)}{\nu}\). By L'Hôpital's rule or the first-order Taylor expansion \(\ln(1 + \nu z) = \nu z + O(\nu^2)\) as \(\nu \to 0\): \(\lim_{\nu \to 0} \left( - \frac{\ln(1 + \nu z)}{\nu} \right) = - z = - (- i \theta u + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2) = i \theta u - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 u^2\). This is precisely the characteristic exponent of Brownian motion with drift \(\theta\) and volatility \(\sigma\).

P2 · Third central moment derivation via conditioning

Derive the third central moment \(\mu_3 = \E[(X_t - \E[X_t])^3]\) of the Variance Gamma process using conditioning on the subordinator \(G_t\).

Solution

Recall \(X_t = \theta G_t + \sigma B(G_t)\) and \(\E[X_t] = \theta t\). Thus \(X_t - \E[X_t] = \theta (G_t - t) + \sigma B(G_t)\). Cubing both sides and conditioning on \(G_t\): odd powers of \(B\) vanish (\(\E[B(G_t) \mid G_t] = 0\) and \(\E[B(G_t)^3 \mid G_t] = 0\)). For the even power, \(\E[B(G_t)^2 \mid G_t] = G_t\). Thus: \(\E[(X_t - \E[X_t])^3 \mid G_t] = \theta^3 (G_t - t)^3 + 3 \theta \sigma^2 (G_t - t) G_t\). Now take unconditional expectation: \(\E[(G_t - t)^3] = 2 \nu^2 t\) and \(\E[(G_t - t) G_t] = \Var(G_t) = \nu t\). Hence: \(\E[(X_t - \E[X_t])^3] = \theta^3 (2 \nu^2 t) + 3 \theta \sigma^2 (\nu t) = \theta \nu t (3 \sigma^2 + 2 \nu \theta^2)\).

P3 · Finite total variation proof

Prove that the Variance Gamma process has finite total variation almost surely by evaluating the integral \(\int_{-1}^1 |x| k_{VG}(x) dx\).

Solution

From Theorem 3.2, the Lévy density on \([-1, 1] \setminus \{0\}\) is \(k_{VG}(x) = \frac{C}{x} e^{-M x}\) for \(x > 0\) and \(k_{VG}(x) = \frac{C}{|x|} e^{-G |x|}\) for \(x < 0\), where \(C = 1/\nu\). Evaluating the integral: \(\int_{-1}^1 |x| k_{VG}(x) dx = C \int_0^1 e^{-M x} dx + C \int_{-1}^0 e^{G x} dx = C [ \frac{1 - e^{-M}}{M} + \frac{1 - e^{-G}}{G} ]\). Since \(M > 0\) and \(G > 0\), this quantity is strictly positive and finite. Combined with \(\sigma_{diff} = 0\), this proves that paths of Variance Gamma have bounded variation on any finite interval almost surely.

Empirical

E1 · Skewness and kurtosis term structure decay

In calibrating Variance Gamma to equity index options, an analyst observes that the implied volatility skew is steepest at short tenors (\(\tau = 1\) month) and flattens out at long tenors (\(\tau = 2\) years). Explain this phenomenon using the analytical skewness and excess kurtosis decay formulas.

Solution

By Theorem 2.2, the skewness of Variance Gamma decays as \(\operatorname{Skew}(X_\tau) \propto 1 / \sqrt{\tau}\), and excess kurtosis decays as \(\operatorname{Kurt}(X_\tau) - 3 \propto 1 / \tau\). In option pricing, implied volatility skew is directly proportional to skewness of the underlying return distribution, while implied volatility smile curvature (wings) is driven by excess kurtosis. At short maturities (\(\tau \to 0\)), \(1 / \sqrt{\tau}\) and \(1 / \tau\) diverge, creating an extremely pronounced, steep skew and sharp wings. As maturity increases (\(\tau \to \infty\)), the central limit theorem takes over, skewness and kurtosis vanish, and the implied volatility surface flattens toward Black–Scholes constant volatility.

E2 · Parameter admissibility bound calculation

Consider the parameter admissibility constraint \(\theta \nu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu < 1\). If \(\sigma = 0.30\) and \(\nu = 0.50\), determine the maximum permissible value of the drift parameter \(\theta\) for a valid risk-neutral Variance Gamma model.

Solution

First compute \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu = 0.5 \times (0.30)^2 \times 0.50 = 0.5 \times 0.09 \times 0.50 = 0.0225\). The constraint requires \(\theta \nu < 1 - 0.0225 = 0.9775\). Dividing by \(\nu = 0.50\): \(\theta < \frac{0.9775}{0.50} = 1.955\). Any value of \(\theta \ge 1.955\) produces infinite stock price expectations (\(\E^*[S_T] = \infty\)), violating absence of arbitrage.

E3 · Monte Carlo efficiency via subordination

In a Monte Carlo pricing simulation of European call options under Variance Gamma, why does Gamma subordination yield orders of magnitude lower execution time than Euler discretization of a jump SDE?

Solution

Euler discretization of a jump SDE requires discretizing time into small steps \(\Delta t\) to capture jump arrivals, requiring \(M = T / \Delta t\) evaluations per path and suffering from discretization bias. In contrast, under Gamma subordination, the increment \(X_T\) over the entire maturity \(T\) can be sampled exactly in just two random draws: draw \(\Delta G \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(T/\nu, \nu)\), then draw \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), and set \(X_T = \theta \Delta G + \sigma \sqrt{\Delta G} Z\). This provides zero time-discretization error and evaluates each path in \(O(1)\) computational operations.

Research

R1 · Variance Gamma with Stochastic Arrival (VGSA)

Dilip Madan and Marti Subrahmanyam (2000) introduced the Variance Gamma with Stochastic Arrival (VGSA) model. How does VGSA generalize the Gamma subordinator to capture persistent volatility clustering?

Solution

In standard Variance Gamma, the time clock \(G_t\) has independent increments, precluding volatility clustering across time. In VGSA, the business time clock is modeled as the integral of a mean-reverting square-root (CIR / Heston-type) process \(y_t\): \(T_t = \int_0^t y_s ds\), where \(d y_t = \kappa (\eta - y_t) dt + \lambda \sqrt{y_t} d W_t\). Evaluating the base Variance Gamma process along this continuous rate clock creates intertemporal dependency in volatility: periods of high trading intensity persist over time, matching empirical autoregressive volatility clustering while preserving analytical characteristic functions via the affine transform method.

R2 · Normal Inverse Gaussian vs. Variance Gamma

Contrast the Variance Gamma model with the Normal Inverse Gaussian (NIG) model of Barndorff-Nielsen (1997). What subordinator replaces the Gamma process in NIG, and how does the small-jump behavior differ?

Solution

In the Normal Inverse Gaussian (NIG) model, Brownian motion is subordinated by an Inverse Gaussian process \(I_t\) rather than a Gamma process. The Inverse Gaussian subordinator has Lévy density \(k_{IG}(x) \propto x^{-3/2} e^{-\gamma x}\), which has a higher singularity at the origin than the Gamma subordinator's \(k_{Gamma}(x) \propto x^{-1} e^{-\lambda x}\). Consequently, the NIG process has Lévy density \(k_{NIG}(x) \sim |x|^{-2}\) near zero, which is a process of infinite activity and infinite variation, whereas Variance Gamma has finite variation. NIG paths are thus significantly rougher at microscopic scales.

R3 · Limit order book queue dynamics and difference of Gammas

In high-frequency electronic trading, order flow is often modeled as two competing Poisson or Hawkes processes. How does the difference-of-Gammas representation provide a continuous-time fluid limit for limit order book queue dynamics?

Solution

Consider limit order book queues where buy market orders arrive with intensity \(\lambda_b\) and sell market orders arrive with intensity \(\lambda_s\). Under high trading volume and infinitesimal tick sizes, scaling the arrivals via functional central limit theorems leads to subordinated jump processes. Since \(\Gamma_t^{(p)}\) and \(\Gamma_t^{(n)}\) are pure-jump subordinators with dense small jumps, they represent the continuous fluid accumulation of buying pressure and selling pressure, respectively. The price \(X_t = \Gamma_t^{(p)} - \Gamma_t^{(n)}\) reflects the net inventory imbalance, providing an analytical bridge between microscopic order matching and macroscopic heavy-tailed asset pricing.

8Formative Quiz

  1. What type of stochastic process is the Variance Gamma model?

    Options

    Variance Gamma has \(\sigma_{diff} = 0\), \(\int k(x) dx = \infty\) (infinite activity), and \(\int_{-1}^1 |x| k(x) dx < \infty\) (finite variation).

  2. For a Variance Gamma process with \(\sigma = 0.20\), \(\nu = 0.60\), and \(\theta = -0.10\), what is the total annualized variance \(\Var(X_1)\)?

    \(\Var(X_1) = \sigma^2 + \nu \theta^2 = (0.20)^2 + 0.60 \times (-0.10)^2 = 0.040 + 0.60 \times 0.010 = 0.046\).

  3. What role does the drift parameter \(\theta\) play in the shape of the Variance Gamma return distribution?

    Options

    By Theorem 2.2, the skewness is proportional to \(\theta\); negative \(\theta\) tilts probability density to the left tail.

  4. Under what mathematical condition is the risk-neutral martingale correction \(\omega = \frac{1}{\nu}\ln(1 - \theta \nu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu)\) well-defined and real?

    Options

    The argument of the natural logarithm must be strictly positive to ensure finite real expectations \(\E^*[e^{X_t}]\).

  5. What is the excess kurtosis \(\operatorname{Kurt}(X_1) - 3\) of a symmetric Variance Gamma process (\(\theta = 0\)) with \(\nu = 1.0\) at horizon \(t = 1\)?

    For \(\theta = 0\), excess kurtosis is \(\frac{3 \nu}{t} = \frac{3 \times 1.0}{1} = 3.0\).

  6. How does Variance Gamma decompose into the difference of two independent Gamma processes \(\Gamma_t^{(p)} - \Gamma_t^{(n)}\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 3.1, \(1 - i \theta \nu u + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \nu u^2 = (1 - i u \eta_p)(1 + i u \eta_n)\), which corresponds to the difference of two independent Gammas.

  7. Why can an increment of a Variance Gamma process be simulated exactly in a single time step without time-discretization error?

    Options

    By Definition 1.2, \(X_t \mid G_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta G_t, \sigma^2 G_t)\), allowing exact two-stage sampling regardless of step size.

  8. What is the continuous Brownian diffusion volatility \(\sigma_{diff}\) of the Variance Gamma model in its characteristic triplet?

    Variance Gamma is a pure-jump process: its continuous Gaussian diffusion parameter is identically zero (\(\sigma_{diff} = 0\)); all variance originates from jumps.
