The CGMY Model

  • Introduces: CGMY model

The Variance Gamma process models asset returns via infinite jump arrivals, but its singularity at zero jump size is strictly logarithmic (\(|x|^{-1}\)). Is financial order flow limited to this specific small-jump intensity, or do markets exhibit stronger or weaker jump clustering? Introduced by Peter Carr, Hélyette Geman, Dilip Madan, and Marc Yor (2002), the CGMY model provides an elegant four-parameter generalization that nests Variance Gamma while decoupling overall jump intensity, right-tail decay, left-tail decay, and small-jump path roughness. The activity index \(Y\) governs whether the process has finite activity, infinite activity with finite variation, or infinite variation approaching Brownian motion, making CGMY the benchmark laboratory for non-Gaussian jump dynamics.

1The CGMY Lévy Density

The CGMY model specifies a pure-jump Lévy process on \(\R\) through a parametric Lévy density exhibiting power-law behavior near the origin and asymmetric exponential damping in the tails [cont2003financial].

Definition 1.1 (CGMY Lévy Density) A pure-jump Lévy process \(X = (X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) follows a CGMY model with parameter vector \((C, G, M, Y)\) if its Gaussian diffusion parameter is zero (\(\sigma = 0\)) and its Lévy measure has density \(k_{CGMY}(x) = \frac{\nu(dx)}{dx}\) given by:

\[ k_{CGMY}(x) = \begin{cases} C \, \dfrac{e^{- M x}}{x^{1 + Y}}, & x > 0, \\[8pt] C \, \dfrac{e^{- G |x|}}{|x|^{1 + Y}}, & x < 0, \end{cases} \]

subject to the parameter constraints:

  • \(C > 0\): the overall jump activity level,
  • \(G > 0\): the exponential decay rate for negative jumps (controlling the left crash tail),
  • \(M > 0\): the exponential decay rate for positive jumps (controlling the right rally tail), and
  • \(Y < 2\): the small-jump activity and variation index.

Remark 1.1 (Relation to Variance Gamma) When \(Y = 0\), the CGMY Lévy density simplifies to:

\[ k(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{C}{x} e^{-M x}, & x > 0 \\ \frac{C}{|x|} e^{-G |x|}, & x < 0 \end{cases} \]

which is identical to the Variance Gamma Lévy density derived in Lesson 4 with \(C = 1 / \nu\). Thus Variance Gamma is precisely CGMY with \(Y = 0\).

2The Role of \(Y\): Activity & Variation Regimes

The parameter \(Y\) is the defining structural innovation of the CGMY framework. By tuning the power of the denominator \(|x|^{1 + Y}\), \(Y\) completely governs the path roughness and arrival dynamics of small jumps [cont2003financial].

Theorem 2.1 (Classification of CGMY Regimes) The analytical properties of the CGMY process partition into four distinct regimes depending on \(Y\):

Regime Jump Activity \(\nu(\R)\) Total Path Variation Small-Jump Asymptotics (\(\epsilon \downarrow 0\)) Economic Interpretation
\(Y < 0\) Finite (\(\nu(\R) < \infty\)) Finite variation \(\int_\epsilon^\infty k(x) dx \to \Lambda(0) < \infty\) Compound Poisson process; isolated macro news shocks.
\(Y = 0\) Infinite (\(\nu(\R) = \infty\)) Finite variation \(\int_\epsilon^\infty k(x) dx \sim C \ln(1 / \epsilon)\) Variance Gamma; logarithmic explosion of micro-jumps.
\(0 < Y < 1\) Infinite (\(\nu(\R) = \infty\)) Finite variation \(\int_\epsilon^\infty k(x) dx \sim \frac{C}{Y} \epsilon^{-Y}\) Power-law infinite activity; dense micro-revisions, finite 1-variation.
\(1 \le Y < 2\) Infinite (\(\nu(\R) = \infty\)) Infinite variation \(\int_{-1}^1 |x| k(x) dx = \infty\), but \(\int_{-1}^1 x^2 k(x) dx < \infty\) Ultra-high jump intensity; sample paths mimic Brownian motion with heavy tails.

Proof Near \(x = 0\), the exponential factors \(e^{-M x} \to 1\) and \(e^{-G |x|} \to 1\). Thus \(k_{CGMY}(x) \sim C |x|^{-(1+Y)}\). For jump activity, integrate on \((0, 1]\):

\[ \int_0^1 x^{-(1+Y)} dx = \left[ -\frac{x^{-Y}}{Y} \right]_0^1 = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{|Y|}, & Y < 0 \text{ (converges)}, \\[6pt] \infty, & Y \ge 0 \text{ (diverges)}. \end{cases} \]

For total variation, evaluate the integral of \(|x|\) on \([-1, 1]\):

\[ \int_0^1 x \cdot x^{-(1+Y)} dx = \int_0^1 x^{-Y} dx = \left[ \frac{x^{1 - Y}}{1 - Y} \right]_0^1 = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{1 - Y} < \infty, & Y < 1, \\[6pt] \infty, & Y \ge 1. \end{cases} \]

Finally, for the Lévy measure integrability condition \(\int_{|x| \le 1} x^2 k(x) dx < \infty\):

\[ \int_0^1 x^2 \cdot x^{-(1+Y)} dx = \int_0^1 x^{1 - Y} dx = \left[ \frac{x^{2 - Y}}{2 - Y} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{2 - Y} < \infty \iff Y < 2. \]

Thus \(Y < 2\) is the necessary and sufficient condition for \(\nu\) to be a valid Lévy measure.

3The Analytical Characteristic Function

A major strength of the CGMY model is that despite its flexibility across activity regimes, its characteristic exponent admits an exact representation in terms of Euler's Gamma function \(\Gamma(z)\) [cont2003financial].

Theorem 3.1 (CGMY Characteristic Exponent) Let \(X = (X_t)_{t \ge 0}\) be a CGMY process with parameters \((C, G, M, Y)\). For \(Y \notin \{0, 1\}\), the characteristic exponent \(\psi(u) = \frac{1}{t}\ln \E[e^{i u X_t}]\) in uncompensated form (for \(Y < 1\)) or compensated form (for \(1 \le Y < 2\)) is given by:

\[ \psi_{CGMY}(u) = C \, \Gamma(-Y) \left[ (M - i u)^Y - M^Y + (G + i u)^Y - G^Y \right]. \]

For \(Y = 0\), taking the continuous limit \(Y \to 0\) yields the Variance Gamma characteristic exponent:

\[ \lim_{Y \to 0} \psi_{CGMY}(u) = - C \left[ \ln\left(1 - \frac{i u}{M}\right) + \ln\left(1 + \frac{i u}{G}\right) \right]. \]

Proof Outline For \(Y < 1\), the process has finite variation, so \(\psi(u) = \int_0^\infty (e^{i u x} - 1) k(x) dx + \int_{-\infty}^0 (e^{i u x} - 1) k(x) dx\). Consider the positive jump integral \(I_+(u) = C \int_0^\infty (e^{i u x} - 1) x^{-(1+Y)} e^{-M x} dx\). Using the integral definition of the Gamma function \(\Gamma(-Y) = \int_0^\infty z^{-(1+Y)} e^{-z} dz\) for \(Y < 0\), and continuing analytically to \(Y \in (0, 1)\) via integration by parts, the integral evaluates to \(C \Gamma(-Y) ((M - i u)^Y - M^Y)\). Applying the identical derivation to the negative jumps with conjugate factor \((G + i u)^Y - G^Y\) produces the stated formula [cont2003financial].

4Risk-Neutral Martingale Dynamics

Under a risk-neutral pricing measure \(\Prob^*\), the stock price is modeled as:

\[ S_t = S_0 \exp\left( (r - q + \omega) t + X_t \right), \]

where the convexity correction \(\omega\) must satisfy \(\E^*[e^{X_t}] = e^{-\omega t}\) to ensure \(e^{-(r-q)t} S_t\) is a martingale [cont2003financial].

Theorem 4.1 (CGMY Convexity Drift Correction) Let \(M > 1\). The drift correction \(\omega\) that ensures the discounted asset price is a \(\Prob^*\)-martingale under CGMY dynamics with \(Y \ne 0\) is:

\[ \omega = - C \, \Gamma(-Y) \left[ (M - 1)^Y - M^Y + (G + 1)^Y - G^Y \right]. \]

The requirement \(M > 1\) is strictly necessary: if \(M \le 1\), the positive tail of the jump density decays slower than \(e^x\), causing the expectation \(\E^*[e^{X_t}] = \int_0^\infty e^x k(x) dx\) to diverge to \(+\infty\), which violates the no-arbitrage condition.

Proof The martingale condition requires \(\E^*[e^{X_t}] = \phi_{X_t}(-i) = e^{-\omega t}\). Evaluating the characteristic exponent at \(u = -i\):

\[ \psi_{CGMY}(-i) = C \, \Gamma(-Y) \left[ (M - i(-i))^Y - M^Y + (G + i(-i))^Y - G^Y \right] = C \, \Gamma(-Y) \left[ (M - 1)^Y - M^Y + (G + 1)^Y - G^Y \right]. \]

Because \(\E^*[e^{X_t}] = e^{t \psi_{CGMY}(-i)}\), equating this to \(e^{-\omega t}\) yields \(\omega = - \psi_{CGMY}(-i)\). For \((M - 1)^Y\) to be well-defined on the positive real line, we must have \(M > 1\).

6Canon Breaks & Market Realities

The CGMY framework challenges several foundational assumptions inherited from classical diffusion theory:

  • Brownian Motion as a Limiting Case, Not an Axiom: In CGMY, Brownian motion is not a prerequisite foundation; it is merely the boundary limit as \(Y \uparrow 2\). Empirical calibrations to financial option surfaces routinely find \(Y \in [0.4, 0.8]\) for equities, proving that markets operate in an intermediate regime of infinite activity and finite variation far removed from continuous diffusion.
  • Decoupling Tails from Center: Classical models like Cauchy or Pareto tie small-jump activity directly to tail thickness. CGMY completely decouples the two: \(Y\) controls small-jump clustering, while \(G\) and \(M\) independently govern the steepness of left crash tails and right rally tails.
  • Power-Law Liquidity Consumption: In high-frequency market microstructure, limit order book resilience exhibits power-law recovery. The CGMY small-jump intensity \(\Lambda(\epsilon) \sim C \epsilon^{-Y}\) matches empirical high-frequency order cancellation and fill statistics.

7Common Pitfalls & Misconceptions

Misconception 1: "Because \(Y < 2\), we can freely choose any \(Y \in \R\) including \(Y \ge 2\)."

Correction: If \(Y \ge 2\), the integral \(\int_{-1}^1 x^2 k_{CGMY}(x) dx \sim \int_0^1 x^{1-Y} dx\) diverges at \(x = 0\). This violates the fundamental Lévy measure condition \(\int \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) < \infty\), meaning that for \(Y \ge 2\) the density does not define any stochastic process.

Misconception 2: "The parameter \(M\) must be greater than zero for the model to exist, so \(M = 0.5\) is valid for risk-neutral option pricing."

Correction: While \(M > 0\) defines a valid statistical process for log-returns, derivative pricing under \(\Prob^*\) requires the underlying asset price \(S_t = S_0 e^{X_t}\) to have finite expectation. Because \(\E[e^{X_t}] < \infty \iff M > 1\), setting \(M \le 1\) creates an infinite stock price expectation, rendering option pricing mathematically impossible.

Misconception 3: "The CGMY model is difficult to calibrate because it lacks closed-form option prices."

Correction: While CGMY lacks closed-form cumulative distribution functions, its characteristic function is completely analytical and evaluates in microseconds. Combined with Fourier inversion (Carr–Madan or Lewis formulas), European call and put option prices across hundreds of strikes can be computed in a fraction of a second.

8Comprehensive Problem Set

Tier 1: Mechanics

Problem 1.1: Given CGMY parameters \(C = 0.5\), \(G = 12.0\), \(M = 18.0\), and \(Y = 0.5\), verify whether \(M\) satisfies the risk-neutral pricing admissibility condition, and evaluate the exponent \(1 + Y\) governing the small-jump singularity.

Solution

Risk-neutral admissibility requires \(M > 1\) so that \(\E^*[e^{X_t}] < \infty\). Here \(M = 18.0 > 1\), which satisfies the condition. The small-jump denominator power is \(1 + Y = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5\). Thus near the origin, \(k_{CGMY}(x) \sim C |x|^{-1.5}\), confirming the process is in the infinite activity, finite variation regime.

Problem 1.2: For a symmetric CGMY model with \(G = M = 10.0\), \(C = 1.0\), and \(Y = 0.5\), compute the annualized variance \(\Var(X_1) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x^2 k_{CGMY}(x) dx\).

Solution

By symmetry, \(\Var(X_1) = 2 C \int_0^\infty x^2 x^{-(1+Y)} e^{-M x} dx = 2 C \int_0^\infty x^{1 - Y} e^{-M x} dx\). Substituting \(z = M x\): \(dx = \frac{dz}{M}\) and \(x^{1-Y} = M^{-(1-Y)} z^{1-Y}\). The integral becomes: \(2 C M^{-(2 - Y)} \int_0^\infty z^{1 - Y} e^{-z} dz = 2 C M^{-(2 - Y)} \Gamma(2 - Y)\). For \(Y = 0.5\), \(\Gamma(2 - 0.5) = \Gamma(1.5) = \frac{1}{2}\Gamma(0.5) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \approx 0.886227\). Then \(M^{-(2 - Y)} = 10^{-1.5} = 10^{-3/2} = \frac{1}{10 \sqrt{10}} \approx 0.0316228\). Thus: \(\Var(X_1) = 2 \times 1.0 \times 0.0316228 \times 0.886227 \approx 0.056046\).

Problem 1.3: In the compound Poisson regime where \(Y = -0.5\), \(C = 2.0\), and \(M = 5.0\), evaluate the total arrival rate of positive jumps \(\Lambda_+ = \int_0^\infty k(x) dx\).

Solution

For \(Y = -0.5\), the exponent is \(1 + Y = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5\). The arrival rate is \(\Lambda_+ = C \int_0^\infty x^{-0.5} e^{-M x} dx\). Substituting \(z = M x\), we have \(dx = dz / M\) and \(x^{-0.5} = M^{0.5} z^{-0.5}\). Thus: \(\Lambda_+ = C M^{-0.5} \int_0^\infty z^{-0.5} e^{-z} dz = C M^{-1/2} \Gamma(0.5) = \frac{C \sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{M}}\). Substituting \(C = 2.0\) and \(M = 5.0\): \(\Lambda_+ = \frac{2.0 \times \sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{5.0}} = \frac{2.0 \times 1.77245}{2.23607} \approx 1.58533\) positive jumps per year.

Tier 2: Proof & Theory

Problem 2.1: Prove that as \(Y \to 0\), the CGMY term \(C \Gamma(-Y) ((M - i u)^Y - M^Y)\) converges to \(- C \ln(1 - \frac{i u}{M})\).

Solution

Using the identity \(\Gamma(1 - Y) = - Y \Gamma(-Y)\), we rewrite the expression as \(- C \Gamma(1 - Y) \frac{(M - i u)^Y - M^Y}{Y}\). As \(Y \to 0\), \(\Gamma(1 - Y) \to \Gamma(1) = 1\). By the definition of the derivative with respect to \(Y\) at \(Y = 0\): \(\lim_{Y \to 0} \frac{A^Y - B^Y}{Y} = \ln A - \ln B = \ln(A / B)\). Here \(A = M - i u\) and \(B = M\). Therefore: \(\lim_{Y \to 0} \left( - C \Gamma(1 - Y) \frac{(M - i u)^Y - M^Y}{Y} \right) = - C \left( \ln(M - i u) - \ln M \right) = - C \ln\left( \frac{M - i u}{M} \right) = - C \ln\left( 1 - \frac{i u}{M} \right)\). This proves that the CGMY characteristic function continuously nests Variance Gamma at \(Y = 0\).

Problem 2.2: Show that for \(1 \le Y < 2\), the sample paths of the CGMY process have infinite total variation almost surely.

Solution

A pure-jump Lévy process has finite total variation if and only if \(\int_{|x| \le 1} |x| \nu(dx) < \infty\). For the CGMY density, consider the positive jump integral on \((0, 1]\): \(\int_0^1 x k_{CGMY}(x) dx = C \int_0^1 x \cdot x^{-(1+Y)} e^{-M x} dx = C \int_0^1 x^{-Y} e^{-M x} dx\). Since \(e^{-M} \le e^{-M x} \le 1\) for \(x \in [0, 1]\), this integral is bounded below by \(C e^{-M} \int_0^1 x^{-Y} dx\). For \(Y \ge 1\), \(\int_0^1 x^{-Y} dx = \lim_{\epsilon \downarrow 0} \left[ \frac{x^{1-Y}}{1-Y} \right]_\epsilon^1 = \infty\) (diverging logarithmically when \(Y = 1\) and as \(\epsilon^{-(Y-1)}\) when \(Y > 1\)). Because the integral of jump sizes diverges at the origin, the sum of jump magnitudes \(\sum_{s \le t} |\Delta X_s|\) diverges almost surely, establishing infinite total variation.

Problem 2.3: Derive the third cumulant \(\kappa_3\) of the CGMY process for \(Y < 1\) and explain how \(G\) and \(M\) control return asymmetry.

Solution

The \(n\)-th cumulant of a pure-jump Lévy process is given by \(\kappa_n = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x^n \nu(dx)\). For \(n = 3\): \(\kappa_3 = \int_0^\infty x^3 \left( C x^{-(1+Y)} e^{-M x} \right) dx + \int_{-\infty}^0 x^3 \left( C |x|^{-(1+Y)} e^{-G |x|} \right) dx = C \int_0^\infty x^{2 - Y} e^{-M x} dx - C \int_0^\infty x^{2 - Y} e^{-G x} dx\). Evaluating the Gamma integrals: \(\kappa_3 = C \Gamma(3 - Y) \left( M^{-(3 - Y)} - G^{-(3 - Y)} \right)\). For financial equity indices, negative jumps dominate positive jumps, meaning the left tail decays slower than the right tail: \(G < M\). Because \(3 - Y > 0\), \(G < M \implies G^{-(3 - Y)} > M^{-(3 - Y)}\), which guarantees \(\kappa_3 < 0\) (negative skewness). If \(G = M\), \(\kappa_3 = 0\) (perfect symmetry).

Tier 3: Empirical & Numerical

Problem 3.1: Calibrating CGMY to S&P 500 index options across maturities yields estimates: \(C = 0.024\), \(G = 4.2\), \(M = 9.8\), and \(Y = 0.55\). Interpret each calibrated parameter economically.

Solution

1. \(C = 0.024\) represents the baseline scale of jump arrivals across the entire spectrum. 2. \(G = 4.2\) is significantly smaller than \(M = 9.8\), indicating that negative jumps decay much more slowly (\(e^{-4.2 |x|}\)) than positive jumps (\(e^{-9.8 x}\)), producing substantial negative skewness and pricing deep out-of-the-money put options (crash fear). 3. \(Y = 0.55 \in (0, 1)\) proves that equity returns operate in an infinite activity, finite variation regime: the asset price does not diffuse continuously, but rather moves through dense micro-jumps of order \(|x|^{-1.55}\) near zero.

Problem 3.2: In high-frequency foreign exchange markets, typical calibrated values of \(Y\) are near \(1.2\) to \(1.4\). Why is \(Y > 1\) more prevalent in FX markets than in equity index markets?

Solution

Major currency pairs (e.g., EUR/USD, USD/JPY) represent exchange rates between sovereign economies with continuous, two-sided institutional order flow. Unlike equity indices that experience catastrophic one-sided earnings crashes and corporate defaults, FX markets are dominated by an immense volume of continuous two-way quote updates. A value of \(Y \in (1, 2)\) produces infinite total variation: the trajectory exhibits extreme micro-roughness indistinguishable from continuous diffusion at macro scales, while retaining heavy tail shocks at microsecond frequencies.

Problem 3.3: When evaluating \(\psi_{CGMY}(u)\) numerically for \(Y\) very close to 0 (e.g., \(Y = 10^{-6}\)), direct evaluation suffers from catastrophic cancellation error in \((M - i u)^Y - M^Y\). How should this be implemented robustly in software?

Solution

For small \(|Y| < 10^{-4}\), rewrite the bracketed term using the exact exponential representation: \((M - i u)^Y - M^Y = M^Y \left( (1 - i u / M)^Y - 1 \right) = M^Y \left( \exp(Y \ln(1 - i u / M)) - 1 \right)\). Using the standard numerical function \(\operatorname{expm1}(z) = e^z - 1\), this avoids subtraction of nearly equal floating-point numbers. Furthermore, as \(Y \to 0\), \(\Gamma(-Y) \operatorname{expm1}(Y \ln(1 - i u / M)) = - \Gamma(1 - Y) \frac{\operatorname{expm1}(Y \ln(1 - i u / M))}{Y} \to - \ln(1 - i u / M)\), maintaining 16 digits of numerical precision across all regimes.

Tier 4: Research & Extensions

Problem 4.1: Carr, Geman, Madan, and Yor (2003) extended CGMY to the CGMYe model (with stochastic volatility). Why is adding a stochastic clock or stochastic volatility to CGMY necessary to calibrate the term structure of implied volatility?

Solution

Because CGMY is a time-homogeneous Lévy process, its cumulants scale strictly linearly with maturity: \(\kappa_n(t) = t \kappa_n(1)\). Consequently, its implied volatility skew must decay at the rigid rate of \(O(1/\sqrt{\tau})\) as maturity \(\tau\) increases. Empirical option surfaces frequently exhibit term structures of skewness that persist across multiple years. Subordinating CGMY with an integrated CIR or Ornstein–Uhlenbeck rate process introduces stochastic volatility, allowing the model to decouple short-tenor jump skew from long-tenor persistent variance clustering.

Problem 4.2: Contrast the CGMY model with the bilateral Gamma process of Küchler and Tappe (2008). Under what parameter restriction does CGMY reduce to bilateral Gamma?

Solution

The bilateral Gamma process is defined as the difference of two independent Gamma processes with independent shape and scale parameters: \(X_t = \Gamma_1(t; \alpha_1, \beta_1) - \Gamma_2(t; \alpha_2, \beta_2)\). Its Lévy density is \(k(x) = \frac{\alpha_1}{x} e^{-\beta_1 x}\) for \(x > 0\) and \(k(x) = \frac{\alpha_2}{|x|} e^{-\beta_2 |x|}\) for \(x < 0\). CGMY generalizes bilateral Gamma by replacing the \(1/|x|\) singularity with \(|x|^{-(1+Y)}\). When \(Y = 0\) and \(C_+ = C_- = C\), CGMY reduces to Variance Gamma (a symmetric-shape bilateral Gamma). When \(Y = 0\) and distinct amplitudes \(C_+\) and \(C_-\) are permitted, CGMY exactly coincides with the general bilateral Gamma process.

Problem 4.3: In the boundary case where \(Y \uparrow 2\), show that the CGMY process converges weakly to a continuous Brownian motion after appropriate rescaling of \(C\).

Solution

Let \(C = \sigma^2 \frac{2 - Y}{2 \Gamma(2 - Y)}\). Consider the variance of the truncated jumps: as \(Y \uparrow 2\), the singularity \(x^{-(1+Y)} = x^{-3}\) diverges, but the scaling factor \(2 - Y\) normalizes the second moment to \(\sigma^2\). For any \(\epsilon > 0\), the jump arrival rate of jumps larger than \(\epsilon\) is \(\int_\epsilon^\infty C x^{-(1+Y)} e^{-M x} dx \le C \epsilon^{-Y} / Y \to 0\) as \(C \to 0\) (since \(2 - Y \to 0\)). By the functional central limit theorem for triangular arrays of Lévy processes, all jumps of size \(|\Delta X| > \epsilon\) vanish almost surely, while the continuous quadratic variation converges to \(\sigma^2 t\). Hence the scaled CGMY process converges weakly in the Skorokhod topology to standard Brownian motion \(\sigma W_t\).

10Formative Quiz

  1. What structural property distinguishes the CGMY model from the Variance Gamma model?

    Options

    Variance Gamma restricts the small-jump singularity to \(|x|^{-1}\) (\(Y = 0\)), whereas CGMY allows any power \(|x|^{-(1+Y)}\) with \(Y < 2\).

  2. Under which condition on \(Y\) does the CGMY process have paths of finite total variation?

    Options

    By Theorem 2.1, \(\int_{-1}^1 |x| k(x) dx < \infty \iff \int_0^1 x^{-Y} dx < \infty \iff Y < 1\).

  3. Why is the condition \(M > 1\) strictly mandatory for risk-neutral derivative pricing under CGMY?

    Options

    By Theorem 4.1, \(\int_0^\infty e^x k(x) dx \sim \int^\infty e^{(1 - M) x} dx\), which converges if and only if \(1 - M < 0 \iff M > 1\).

  4. What numerical value of \(Y\) in the CGMY model recovers the classical Variance Gamma model?

    When \(Y = 0\), the CGMY Lévy density reduces to \(C |x|^{-1} e^{-M x \mathbf{1}_{x>0} - G |x| \mathbf{1}_{x<0}}\), which is identical to Variance Gamma.

  5. If a calibrated CGMY model on equity index options has \(G = 5.0\) and \(M = 14.0\), what is the direction of return skewness?

    Options

    The decay rate of negative jumps is \(e^{-G |x|}\). Smaller \(G\) means heavier left tail, producing negative skewness.

  6. What happens to the CGMY Lévy measure if the index parameter \(Y \ge 2\)?

    Options

    A Lévy measure must satisfy \(\int \min(1, x^2) \nu(dx) < \infty\). For \(Y \ge 2\), the integral near 0 behaves as \(\int_0^1 x^{1-Y} dx = \infty\).

  7. Which regime of \(Y\) corresponds to a compound Poisson process with a finite total arrival rate of jumps?

    Options

    When \(Y < 0\), \(\int_0^1 x^{-(1+Y)} dx < \infty\), ensuring the total jump intensity \(\nu(\R)\) is strictly finite.

  8. What is the continuous Gaussian diffusion parameter \(\sigma_{diff}\) in the canonical CGMY model?

    The CGMY model is a pure-jump Lévy process; its continuous diffusion component is identically zero (\(\sigma_{diff} = 0\)).
