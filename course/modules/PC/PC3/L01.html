Fermi Estimation and Mental Math

  • Uses: the exponential and logarithm functions (M1) and the Taylor theorem with remainder (M2)
  • Introduces: Fermi estimation, defined in Section 1
  • Ttheorem, proved on the page
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A Fermi estimate answers a quantity question from a few factors, each stated as a range, when no table is at hand. The product of the ranges gives bounds that can be defended one factor at a time. This lesson defines the estimate, proves the bounds of a product of positive ranges, proves that the log-error widths of the factors add, and derives a bound on the error of the mental estimate \(1 + nx\) for \((1 + x)^n\). Every range in this lesson is assumed for the example. None is a data value, and the lesson makes no claim about any market.

1Fermi estimates and product bounds

Real numbers are written \(\R\). A quantity that is hard to know is often easy to bound. Write it as a product of factors, state each factor as a range, and multiply the ends of the ranges. The lesson uses only the ordinary order and arithmetic of \(\R\).

Definition 1.1 (Fermi estimate)

A Fermi estimate of a quantity \(Q\) is an identity \(Q = x_1 x_2 \cdots x_k\) together with a factor range \([l_i, u_i]\) for each factor \(x_i\). The estimate is the set of products that the ranges allow. Its ends are the bounds of Theorem 1.2.

Assumption A1 (positive ranges)

Each factor satisfies \(0 < l_i \le x_i \le u_i\) for stated numbers \(l_i\) and \(u_i\). The estimator chooses the ranges, and the theorems below use them as given. No theorem tests whether a range is correct.

Theorem 1.2 (product bounds)
  1. (H1) \(k \ge 1\) is an integer.
  2. (H2) Assuming A1, \(x_1, \dots, x_k\) are real numbers with \(0 < l_i \le x_i \le u_i\) for each \(i\).

Conclusion: \(\prod_{i=1}^{k} l_i \le \prod_{i=1}^{k} x_i \le \prod_{i=1}^{k} u_i\). Both bounds are attained. The lower bound is the product when every \(x_i = l_i\), and the upper bound is the product when every \(x_i = u_i\).

Proof

First, a lemma. Let \(a, a', b, b'\) be real numbers with \(0 < a' \le a\) and \(0 < b' \le b\). Then \(a'b' \le ab\), because \(ab - a'b' = a(b - b') + b'(a - a')\), and both terms are nonnegative: \(a > 0\) with \(b \ge b'\), and \(b' > 0\) with \(a \ge a'\). Likewise, if \(0 < a \le A\) and \(0 < b \le B\), then \(ab \le AB\), because \(AB - ab = A(B - b) + b(A - a)\), and both terms are nonnegative.

Now induct on \(k\). For \(k = 1\) the claim is (H2). Assume the claim for \(k - 1\) factors and write \(P = \prod_{i < k} x_i\), \(L = \prod_{i < k} l_i\) and \(U = \prod_{i < k} u_i\). The induction hypothesis gives \(0 < L \le P \le U\). The lemma with \(a = P\), \(a' = L\), \(b = x_k\) and \(b' = l_k\) gives \(P x_k \ge L l_k\). The lemma with \(A = U\), \(B = u_k\), \(a = P\) and \(b = x_k\) gives \(P x_k \le U u_k\). This is the claim for \(k\) factors. Attainment: the point \(x_i = l_i\) for every \(i\) lies in the box of (H2), and its product is \(\prod_i l_i\). The same holds for \(u_i\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 1.2 is a worst-case statement. It covers every combination of factor values that the ranges allow, and it assigns no probability to any of them. Its proof uses only the ranges, so it holds whatever the factors are assumed to depend on. Section 4 returns to this point.

Proposition 1.3 (log-error widths add)
  1. (H1) and (H2) of Theorem 1.2, so that A1 holds.

Conclusion: \(\ln\!\big(\prod_i u_i \big/ \prod_i l_i\big) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \ln(u_i / l_i)\). The left side is the log-error width of the product interval, and each term on the right is the log-error width of a factor range.

Proof

All ends are positive. Finite products of positive numbers rearrange freely, so \(\prod_i u_i / \prod_i l_i = \prod_i (u_i / l_i)\), and every factor \(u_i / l_i\) is positive. The logarithm turns products into sums: \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\) for positive \(a\) and \(b\), which is the defining property of the logarithm (M1, exponential and logarithm functions). Induction on \(k\) then gives the sum. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.4 (quote updates in one day)

Take three assumed ranges. Symbols quoted lie in \([20, 60]\). Quote updates per symbol per minute lie in \([2, 10]\). Minutes of quoting per day lie in \([300, 390]\). These are assumptions of the example. By Theorem 1.2 the product, which is the number of quote updates in a day under these assumptions, lies in \([20 \cdot 2 \cdot 300,\ 60 \cdot 10 \cdot 390] = [12\,000,\ 234\,000]\). The ratio of the ends is \(234\,000 / 12\,000 = 19.5\), so the log-error width is \(\ln 19.5\). The factor widths are \(\ln 3\), \(\ln 5\) and \(\ln 1.3\), and by Proposition 1.3 they sum to \(\ln 19.5\).

The midpoint of the log interval is its geometric mean, \(\sqrt{12\,000 \cdot 234\,000}\), which is 52 990.57 when displayed to two decimals. Lab 1, Part 1 prints the bounds, the width and the factor widths, and checks the sum.

2Which factor to refine

Proposition 1.3 turns the question of refinement into arithmetic. Narrowing one factor's range by a smaller subrange lowers the total log-error width by exactly the drop in that factor's own width. The total does not depend on the order in which factors are refined. A factor whose own width is already small can lower the total by at most that small width, whatever the other factors are.

Lab 1, Part 2 narrows one factor at a time in Example 1.4. Symbols quoted from \([20, 60]\) to \([20, 40]\) lowers the total width from \(\ln 19.5 = 2.9704\) to \(2.5649\), a reduction of \(0.4055\). Quote updates from \([2, 10]\) to \([4, 8]\) lowers it to \(2.0541\), a reduction of \(0.9163\), which is 30.8 percent of the baseline total. Minutes from \([300, 390]\) to \([330, 360]\) lowers it to \(2.7951\), a reduction of \(0.1754\), which is 5.9 percent of the baseline. In this finite comparison the largest reduction comes from the widest factor. That is a statement about these three narrowings. It is not a general rule, and Research problem R3 gives a case where the widest factor does not give the largest reduction.

3Mental bounds for powers

A mental estimate of \((1 + x)^n\) for small \(x\) keeps the first-order part of the expansion, \(1 + nx\). The bound below says how large the error of that estimate can be, for \(x \ge 0\) and integer \(n \ge 2\).

Assumption A2 (Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem)

For \(f\) twice differentiable on an interval containing \(0\) and \(x\), there is \(\xi\) strictly between \(0\) and \(x\) with \(f(x) = f(0) + f'(0)\,x + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\,x^2\). This is the Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder of M2 (Taylor's theorem with remainder). It is used here as a known result, and the lesson does not reprove it.

Theorem 1.5 (second-order remainder of \((1+x)^n\))
  1. (H1) \(n \ge 2\) is an integer.
  2. (H2) \(x \ge 0\) is real.
  3. (H3) Assuming A2, applied to \(f(t) = (1 + t)^n\) on \([0, x]\).

Conclusion: \(0 \le (1 + x)^n - 1 - nx \le \dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\,(1 + x)^{n-2}\,x^2\).

Proof

If \(x = 0\), both sides of the first inequality are 0 and the second is 0. Let \(x > 0\). The function \(f(t) = (1 + t)^n\) is defined and twice differentiable for \(t > -1\), with \(f'(t) = n(1 + t)^{n-1}\) and \(f''(t) = n(n-1)(1 + t)^{n-2}\). Assuming A2, there is \(\xi\) with \(0 < \xi < x\) such that \((1 + x)^n = 1 + nx + \tfrac12 n(n-1)(1 + \xi)^{n-2} x^2\), because \(f(0) = 1\) and \(f'(0) = n\). Hence \((1 + x)^n - 1 - nx = \tfrac12 n(n-1)(1 + \xi)^{n-2} x^2\). Since \(\xi > 0\) and \(n - 2 \ge 0\), the power satisfies \(0 < 1 \le (1 + \xi)^{n-2} \le (1 + x)^{n-2}\), which gives both inequalities. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.6

For \(n = 2\) the exponent \(n - 2\) is 0. The bound equals \(x^2\), and the remainder is exactly \((1 + x)^2 - 1 - 2x = x^2\), so the upper inequality holds with equality for every \(x \ge 0\). For \(n \ge 3\) and \(x > 0\) the proof gives a strict upper inequality, because \(\xi < x\) and \((1 + \xi)^{n-2} < (1 + x)^{n-2}\); Lab 1, Part 3 prints strict cases for \(n = 5\) and \(n = 10\). The identity \((1 + x)^n - 1 - nx = \sum_{j=2}^{n} \binom{n}{j} x^j\), which follows from the binomial theorem, gives the same remainder and is checked exactly in the lab.

Example 1.7 (ten compounding steps)

Take \(n = 10\) and \(x = 1/100\). The first-order estimate is \(1 + nx = 1.1\). Lab 1, Part 3 prints the exact value \((1.01)^{10} = 1.1046221254\), the remainder \(0.0046221254\) and the bound \(0.0048728552\). The remainder is below the bound, as Theorem 1.5 requires. The ratio of the remainder to the bound is \(0.0046221254 / 0.0048728552 = 0.9485\) to four places, so the bound is \(0.0048728552 / 0.0046221254 - 1 = 0.054\), that is 5.4 percent, larger than the remainder in this case.

At \(x = 1/20\) the same comparison is less favourable. The first-order estimate is \(1.5\), and the exact value of \((1.05)^{10}\) is \(1.6289\) to four places. The remainder \(0.1289\) is still below the bound \(0.1662\), but the estimate misses by \(0.1289 / 1.6289 = 0.079\), that is 7.9 percent, of the exact value. The bound grows with \(x\), and the lesson does not claim the estimate for large \(x\).

Lab 1 · Product bounds, narrowing and the Taylor bound

The lab computes Example 1.4 with fractions and prints the product bounds, 12000 and 234000, the ratio 19.5 and the log-error width 2.9704144656, which equals the sum of the three factor widths. Part 1 also checks 125 products on a grid of factor values against the bounds, which is a finite check of Theorem 1.2. Part 2 prints the total width after narrowing each factor alone. Part 3 prints, for n = 2, 5 and 10 and x = 0.01 and 0.05, the exact value, the first-order estimate, the remainder and the bound, and checks 0 ≤ remainder ≤ bound and the binomial-tail identity with exact fractions. Decimal values print to ten places, and the comparison tolerance is 1e-9.

"""PC3 lab L01: bounds on a Fermi product, its log-error width, and a second-order bound for (1 + x)^n.

Standard library only. No randomness and no network. Every factor range below is an assumed range chosen for
this example. It is not an estimate of any market, and the lab reads no data file. Bounds and products are
fractions.Fraction, so every printed bound and every checked inequality is exact. Decimal columns print to ten
places and are used only for the log-error widths and for the displayed values of the Taylor bound.

The product bounds rest on the monotonicity argument of Theorem 1.2 on the page. The lab checks that every
product over a regular grid of factor values lies inside them. A finite grid is a check, not a proof.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
import math
from fractions import Fraction

Factor = tuple[str, Fraction, Fraction]

# Assumed ranges (lower end, upper end) for this example. They are not estimates of any market.
FACTORS: tuple[Factor, ...] = (
    ("symbols quoted", Fraction(20), Fraction(60)),
    ("quote updates per symbol per minute", Fraction(2), Fraction(10)),
    ("minutes of quoting per day", Fraction(300), Fraction(390)),
)

# Narrowed ranges, one factor at a time, for Part 2.
NARROWED: dict[str, tuple[Fraction, Fraction]] = {
    "symbols quoted": (Fraction(20), Fraction(40)),
    "quote updates per symbol per minute": (Fraction(4), Fraction(8)),
    "minutes of quoting per day": (Fraction(330), Fraction(360)),
}

GRID_POINTS = 5


def product_bounds(factors: tuple[Factor, ...]) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Lower and upper bounds of the product of the factor ranges. Every lower end must be positive."""
    lower = Fraction(1)
    upper = Fraction(1)
    for name, lo, hi in factors:
        if lo <= 0 or hi < lo:
            raise ValueError(f"factor {name!r} needs 0 < lower end <= upper end")
        lower *= lo
        upper *= hi
    return lower, upper


def log_width(lower: Fraction, upper: Fraction) -> float:
    """The log-error width ln(upper / lower) of an interval with positive ends, in natural-log units."""
    return math.log(float(upper / lower))


def grid_products(factors: tuple[Factor, ...]) -> list[Fraction]:
    """Products over a regular grid of GRID_POINTS values in each range, both ends included."""
    axes = [[lo + (hi - lo) * Fraction(k, GRID_POINTS - 1) for k in range(GRID_POINTS)] for _, lo, hi in factors]
    products: list[Fraction] = []
    for combo in itertools.product(*axes):
        value = Fraction(1)
        for x in combo:
            value *= x
        products.append(value)
    return products


def remainder(n: int, x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """(1 + x)^n - 1 - n x: the part of the expansion of (1 + x)^n after its first-order term."""
    return (1 + x) ** n - 1 - n * x


def binomial_tail(n: int, x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The sum of C(n, j) x^j for j from 2 to n, computed term by term."""
    return sum((math.comb(n, j) * x**j for j in range(2, n + 1)), Fraction(0))


def lagrange_bound(n: int, x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """n(n - 1)/2 times (1 + x)^(n - 2) times x^2, the bound on the remainder for 0 <= x."""
    return Fraction(n * (n - 1), 2) * (1 + x) ** (n - 2) * x * x


def part_one() -> None:
    print("Part 1 · product of assumed factor ranges")
    lower, upper = product_bounds(FACTORS)
    print(f"product lower bound = {int(lower)}")
    print(f"product upper bound = {int(upper)}")
    print(f"upper / lower = {float(upper / lower):.10f}")
    width = log_width(lower, upper)
    print(f"log-error width ln(upper / lower) = {width:.10f}")
    print(f"geometric midpoint sqrt(lower * upper) = {math.sqrt(float(lower * upper)):.10f}")
    parts = [log_width(lo, hi) for _, lo, hi in FACTORS]
    for (name, lo, hi), part in zip(FACTORS, parts, strict=True):
        print(f"  factor log-width, {name} ({lo} to {hi}) = {part:.10f}")
    print(f"sum of factor log-widths = {sum(parts):.10f}")
    print(f"sum equals product log-width: {abs(sum(parts) - width) < 1e-12}")
    grid = grid_products(FACTORS)
    inside = sum(lower <= p <= upper for p in grid)
    print(f"grid products inside the bounds: {inside} of {len(grid)}")


def part_two() -> None:
    print("Part 2 · narrowing one factor at a time (total log-error width)")
    base = log_width(*product_bounds(FACTORS))
    print(f"baseline total log-error width = {base:.10f}")
    for name, lo_hi in NARROWED.items():
        changed = tuple((n, lo_hi[0], lo_hi[1]) if n == name else (n, lo, hi) for n, lo, hi in FACTORS)
        total = log_width(*product_bounds(changed))
        print(f"narrow {name} to {lo_hi[0]} to {lo_hi[1]}: total {total:.10f}, reduction {base - total:.10f}")


def part_three() -> None:
    print("Part 3 · second-order remainder of (1 + x)^n for 0 <= x")
    ok_bound = True
    ok_tail = True
    for n in (2, 5, 10):
        for x in (Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(1, 20)):
            exact = (1 + x) ** n
            rem = remainder(n, x)
            bound = lagrange_bound(n, x)
            ok_bound = ok_bound and 0 <= rem <= bound
            ok_tail = ok_tail and rem == binomial_tail(n, x)
            print(
                f"n={n} x={float(x):.2f}: first-order 1 + n x = {float(1 + n * x):.10f}, "
                f"exact = {float(exact):.10f}, remainder = {float(rem):.10f}, bound = {float(bound):.10f}"
            )
    print(f"0 <= remainder <= bound in every case: {ok_bound}")
    print(f"remainder equals the binomial tail in every case: {ok_tail}")


def main() -> None:
    part_one()
    part_two()
    part_three()

4Where the canon breaks

The product bounds are attained (Theorem 1.2), and each claim in this lesson has a limit that the lesson states.

  • A product interval is not a probability statement. Theorem 1.2 holds for every combination of factor values. It gives no probability to any of them, so a width of \(\ln 19.5\) does not say where the product is likely to lie inside \([12\,000, 234\,000]\). A statement about likelihood needs a model of the joint behaviour of the factors, and this lesson does not supply one.
  • Correlated errors. Theorem 1.2 needs no assumption about how the factors depend on one another, so it holds when they are correlated. What it says about the joint behaviour is nothing. If several factors share a common cause, a model that treats them as separate ranges can misdescribe the product, and the lesson makes no claim about the size of that effect.
  • The Taylor bound is for small, nonnegative \(x\). Theorem 1.5 covers \(x \ge 0\) and integer \(n \ge 2\). The lesson does not state a bound for negative \(x\), and the bound grows with \(x\), as Example 1.7 shows at \(x = 1/20\).

5Misconceptions

  1. “A more precise factor makes the estimate more accurate.”

    Under Proposition 1.3 the total log-error width is the sum of the factor widths, so refining one factor changes the total only by that factor's drop. In Example 1.4 narrowing minutes from \([300, 390]\) to \([330, 360]\) removes 0.1754 of the total width, which is 5.9 percent of 2.9704. Narrowing quote updates removes 0.9163, which is 30.8 percent. Lab 1, Part 2 prints both reductions. A precise factor does not help much while another factor is rough.

  2. “A Fermi interval is a confidence interval.”

    Theorem 1.2 is a worst-case statement over every combination of values in the ranges, and it assigns no probability to any combination (Section 4). The proof uses only the ranges, so no probability can come out of it. A confidence statement needs a model that Theorem 1.2 does not contain.

  3. “The mental estimate \(1 + nx\) is accurate enough without a bound.”

    The error is \((1 + x)^n - 1 - nx\), and it depends on \(n\) and \(x\). At \(n = 10\) and \(x = 1/20\) the estimate is 1.5 and the exact value is 1.6289 to four places, so the miss is 0.1289. Theorem 1.5 gives 0.1662 as an upper bound on that miss, and Lab 1, Part 3 prints both numbers. Without the bound the size of the miss is unknown.

Mechanics

M1 · Product bounds for three ranges

Let \(x_1 \in [2, 3]\), \(x_2 \in [5, 7]\) and \(x_3 \in [1/2, 1]\). Give the bounds of Theorem 1.2 for \(x_1 x_2 x_3\), and the log-error width of the product interval in closed form.

Solution

The lower bound is \(2 \cdot 5 \cdot \tfrac12 = 5\) and the upper bound is \(3 \cdot 7 \cdot 1 = 21\), so the product lies in \([5, 21]\). The log-error width is \(\ln(21/5) = \ln 4.2\). The factor widths are \(\ln(3/2)\), \(\ln(7/5)\) and \(\ln 2\), and their sum is \(\ln(1.5 \cdot 1.4 \cdot 2) = \ln 4.2\), which agrees with Proposition 1.3.

M2 · A two-factor width

For \(x_1 \in [1, 4]\) and \(x_2 \in [2, 8]\), give the product interval and its log-error width, and check the width against the sum of the factor widths.

Solution

The product interval is \([1 \cdot 2, 4 \cdot 8] = [2, 32]\). Its ratio is \(32/2 = 16\), so the log-error width is \(\ln 16\), which is 2.7726 to four places. The factor widths are \(\ln(4/1) = \ln 4\) and \(\ln(8/2) = \ln 4\), and \(\ln 4 + \ln 4 = \ln 16\), as Proposition 1.3 requires.

M3 · The Taylor bound by hand

For \(n = 3\) and \(x = 1/10\), compute \((1 + x)^3 - 1 - 3x\) exactly, and compute the bound of Theorem 1.5. Check the inequality.

Solution

Here \((11/10)^3 = 1331/1000\), so the remainder is \(1331/1000 - 1 - 3/10 = 31/1000\), which is 0.031. The bound is \(\tfrac{3 \cdot 2}{2} \cdot (11/10)^{1} \cdot (1/100) = 3 \cdot \tfrac{11}{10} \cdot \tfrac{1}{100} = 33/1000\), which is 0.033. Since \(31/1000 \le 33/1000\), the inequality of Theorem 1.5 holds.

Proof

P1 · Reciprocal bounds and quotients

Let \(0 < l \le x \le u\). Prove that \(1/u \le 1/x \le 1/l\). Then let \(a \in [a_l, a_u]\) and \(b \in [b_l, b_u]\) with all ends positive, and use Theorem 1.2 to bound \(a/b\). State every hypothesis you use.

Solution

Hypotheses used: \(0 < l \le x \le u\), and positivity of the ends for \(a\) and \(b\). First, \(1/l - 1/x = (x - l)/(lx) \ge 0\) because \(x \ge l > 0\), so \(1/x \le 1/l\). Similarly \(1/x - 1/u = (u - x)/(xu) \ge 0\), so \(1/u \le 1/x\). For the quotient, \(1/b\) is a factor with range \([1/b_u, 1/b_l]\), and its ends are positive. Theorem 1.2 applied to the two factors \(a\) and \(1/b\) gives \(a_l / b_u \le a/b \le a_u / b_l\), with both ends attained.

P2 · Bernoulli's inequality for nonnegative x

Prove that \((1 + x)^n \ge 1 + nx\) for every integer \(n \ge 1\) and every real \(x \ge 0\). List the hypotheses that the induction uses.

Solution

Hypotheses used: \(n\) is a positive integer and \(x \ge 0\), so \(1 + x > 0\). For \(n = 1\) the two sides are equal. Assume \((1 + x)^n \ge 1 + nx\). Multiplying both sides by \(1 + x > 0\) gives \((1 + x)^{n+1} \ge (1 + nx)(1 + x) = 1 + (n+1)x + nx^2\). Since \(nx^2 \ge 0\), the right side is at least \(1 + (n+1)x\). This completes the induction.

P3 · The binomial-tail identity

For an integer \(n \ge 2\) and real \(x\), prove that \((1 + x)^n - 1 - nx = \sum_{j=2}^{n} \binom{n}{j} x^j\). Use the binomial theorem \((1 + x)^n = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \binom{n}{j} x^j\) as a known fact.

Solution

Hypotheses used: \(n \ge 2\) is an integer, and the binomial theorem holds for real \(x\). In the binomial expansion the \(j = 0\) term is \(\binom{n}{0} x^0 = 1\) and the \(j = 1\) term is \(\binom{n}{1} x = nx\). Subtracting these two terms and 1 from both sides leaves exactly the sum from \(j = 2\) to \(n\).

Empirical

E1 · The remainder-to-bound ratio from the printed output

From Lab 1, Part 3, take the remainder and the bound for \(n = 10\) and \(x = 0.01\). Compute their ratio to four places. Then say whether Theorem 1.5 is consistent with the ratio.

Solution

The printed remainder is 0.0046221254 and the printed bound is 0.0048728552. Their ratio is \(0.0046221254 / 0.0048728552 = 0.94854\ldots\), which is 0.9485 to four places. The ratio is below 1, which is consistent with the upper inequality of Theorem 1.5. The ratio is a computation from two printed values, and the lab does not print it.

E2 · Shares of the total width

From Lab 1, Part 2, find the share of the baseline total width of 2.9704 that each of two narrowings removes: the one for quote updates and the one for minutes. Give each share as a percentage to one decimal place.

Solution

The printed reduction for quote updates is 0.9163. Dividing by the baseline 2.9704 gives a share of 0.3085 to four places, which is 30.8 percent to one decimal place. The printed reduction for minutes is 0.1754, and dividing by 2.9704 gives 0.0590, which is 5.9 percent. The shares are stated from the four-place values. Computed from the full-precision widths of Lab 1, the shares are 30.8 percent and 5.9 percent, the same to one decimal place.

E3 · A changed range (assumed, not data)

Replace the minutes range of Example 1.4 by \([300, 330]\) and keep the other two ranges. Give the new product bounds, the new log-error width, and whether the width increased or decreased, with the change computed from full-precision values.

Solution

The new lower bound is \(20 \cdot 2 \cdot 300 = 12\,000\) and the new upper bound is \(60 \cdot 10 \cdot 330 = 198\,000\). Their ratio is \(16.5\), so the log-error width is \(\ln 16.5\), which is 2.8034 to four places. The full-precision change from \(\ln 19.5\) is \(\ln 19.5 - \ln 16.5 = 0.167054\ldots\), so the width decreased. This range is an assumption of this problem, not an estimate of any quantity.

E4 · Two equal ranges

Find two factor ranges, each with lower end 1, whose product interval has ratio 4. State the sum rule of Proposition 1.3 for them.

Solution

Take \([1, 2]\) for both factors. The product interval is \([1, 4]\), with ratio 4. Each factor has log-error width \(\ln 2\), and \(\ln 2 + \ln 2 = \ln 4\), which is the log-error width of the product interval, as Proposition 1.3 states.

Research

R1 · A stated threshold for refinement

Open design question. A Fermi estimate is refined only when a factor's range is wide enough to matter. Write a criterion that a presenter could state before refining a factor. The criterion must (a) give a numerical threshold on the factor's log-error width, (b) justify the threshold from Proposition 1.3, and (c) say what changes in the total when the threshold is crossed. Apply it to the three ranges of Example 1.4.

Solution

Criterion: refine a factor when its log-error width is more than half of the total width. Justification: by Proposition 1.3 the total is the sum of the factor widths. If factor \(i\) has width \(w_i > \tfrac12 \sum_j w_j\), then \(w_i > \sum_{j \ne i} w_j\). Narrowing factor \(i\) can remove at most \(w_i\) from the total, and narrowing all the other factors together can remove at most \(\sum_{j \ne i} w_j\), so the single factor has the larger possible effect. Application to Example 1.4: the total width is \(\ln 19.5 = 2.9704\), and half of it is 1.4852. The quote-updates width \(\ln 5 = 1.6094\) exceeds that half, so the criterion selects that factor. The symbols width \(\ln 3 = 1.0986\) and the minutes width \(\ln 1.3 = 0.2624\) do not. A presenter who uses a different threshold must justify it in the same way.

R2 · Which hypotheses the product bound uses

Open question. Does Theorem 1.2 need the factors to be independent, or to have any joint distribution? List every hypothesis the proof uses, and decide the question. The answer must name each hypothesis the proof uses and say which of them is an assumption about the joint behaviour of the factors.

Solution

The proof uses only (H1), the integer \(k \ge 1\), and (H2), the bounds \(0 < l_i \le x_i \le u_i\) for each \(i\). The lemma uses only inequalities between positive reals, and the induction applies the lemma to the partial product and the next factor. No independence, no joint distribution and no probability appears. So the answer is no: the bound needs no assumption about joint behaviour, and it also says nothing about joint behaviour. A claim that depends on the joint distribution requires a separate assumption, which this theorem does not supply.

R3 · Does the widest factor always give the largest reduction?

Open question with a stated criterion. Decide whether, for every pair of factors and every narrowing of each, the factor with the larger width gives the larger reduction in the total. Criterion: give a proof, or a pair of factors and narrowings that shows the claim false, with each log-error width computed.

Solution

The claim is false. Let factor A have range \([1, 3]\), with width \(\ln 3 = 1.0986\), which is the larger width. Narrow A to \([1, 2]\), a reduction of \(\ln(3/2) = 0.4055\). Let factor B have range \([1, 2.5]\), with width \(\ln 2.5 = 0.9163\), which is smaller than A's width. Narrow B to \([1, 1.01]\), a reduction of \(\ln(2.5/1.01) = 0.9063\). The narrower factor B gives the larger reduction, so the claim does not hold for every narrowing. Lab 1, Part 2 compares only three narrowings, and its result is a finite comparison.

  1. Factors lie in \([2, 3]\) and \([5, 7]\). What is the lower bound of their product under Theorem 1.2?

  2. A factor lies in \([2, 8]\). What is its log-error width \(\ln(8/2)\), to six decimal places?

    \(\ln(8/2) = \ln 4 = 1.386294\) to six decimal places. Proposition 1.3 gives the width of a single factor as \(\ln(u/l)\).

  3. Does Theorem 1.2 assign a probability to each combination of factor values?

    Options

    The proof uses only the ranges, and it assigns no probability to any combination (the paragraph after Theorem 1.2, and Section 4).

  4. In Example 1.4, what is the upper bound of the product of the three ranges?

    The upper bound is \(60 \cdot 10 \cdot 390 = 234\,000\), the product of the upper ends.

  5. In Lab 1, Part 2, which narrowing removes the largest share of the baseline total width?

    Options

    The reductions are 0.4055, 0.9163 and 0.1754. The quote-updates reduction is the largest, and it is 30.8 percent of the baseline total.

  6. For \(n = 10\) and \(x = 1/100\), what is \((1.01)^{10} - 1 - 10 \cdot 0.01\), to six decimal places?

    The remainder is 0.0046221254 to ten places, which is 0.004622 to six decimal places (Lab 1, Part 3).

  7. For \(n = 2\) and \(x \ge 0\), how do the remainder and the bound of Theorem 1.5 compare?

    Options

    With \(n = 2\) the exponent \(n - 2\) is 0, and \((1 + x)^2 - 1 - 2x = x^2\). The bound is \(\tfrac{2 \cdot 1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot x^2 = x^2\) (Remark 1.6).

  8. For \(x \ge 0\), how does \(1 + nx\) compare with \((1 + x)^n\)?

    Options

    Problem P2 proves \((1 + x)^n \ge 1 + nx\) for \(x \ge 0\). The estimate \(1 + nx\) is therefore a lower value for the power.

  9. The product interval of Example 1.4 is \([12\,000, 234\,000]\). What is the geometric mean \(\sqrt{12\,000 \cdot 234\,000}\), to four decimal places?

    \(\sqrt{2\,808\,000\,000} = 52\,990.5652\) to four decimal places. It is the midpoint of the log interval, displayed to two decimals as 52 990.57 in Example 1.4.

  10. Which statement about a Fermi estimate is supported by Theorem 1.2?

    Options

    Theorem 1.2 is conditional on the ranges (Assumption A1). It says nothing about likelihood, and Section 2 shows that the reduction from refining a factor depends on which factor it is.
