This lesson works two kinds of question: short calculations on a few identities, and a case that asks for a recommendation from a business question with numbers. This lesson proves the no-arbitrage forward price and the payoff identity behind put–call parity, derives the net present value's break-even, and works one case from start to recommendation. The case answer depends on assumptions the candidate states, so the lesson reports how the answer moves when one assumption moves.

1Technical questions and their prerequisites

This lesson uses the concepts of its prerequisite modules, and the Fermi estimate of Lesson 1: forward contracts and their no-arbitrage price, put–call parity, the net present value, and the articulation of the three statements. Each is linked to the module that defines it. Questions that need a risk model or a valuation of a whole firm are not in this lesson.

Proposition 1.1 (the payoff identity for calls and puts)

Hypotheses: \(s\) and \(K\) are real numbers, and \(x^{+}=\max(x,0)\). Conclusion: \((s-K)^{+}-(K-s)^{+}=s-K\).

Proof

If \(s\ge K\), then \((s-K)^{+}=s-K\) and \((K-s)^{+}=0\), so the left side is \(s-K\). If \(s<K\), then \((s-K)^{+}=0\) and \((K-s)^{+}=K-s\), so the left side is \(0-(K-s)=s-K\). \(\square\)

Remark 1.2 (put–call parity)

The identity of Proposition 1.1 is the payoff form of put–call parity. Its price form, \(\text{call}-\text{put}=S-DK\), where \(D\) is the discount factor of assumption A2 (Section 2 of this lesson), is stated in the linked put–call parity concept of the prerequisite module, under that module's assumptions. This lesson uses the price form only through that link and does not re-derive it.

For cash flows \(C_0,\dots,C_n\) and a rate \(r>-1\), the net present value is \(\operatorname{NPV}(r)=\sum_{t=0}^{n}C_t(1+r)^{-t}\), with \(C_0\) at \(t=0\). The concept is defined in its linked module, and this lesson uses that formula without defining it again.

The articulation of the three statements, linked above, gives one identity this lesson uses in a question about cash: the cash-flow statement ends at the balance-sheet cash. The lesson uses that identity as the prerequisite states it and does not state further accounting rules.

2The no-arbitrage forward price

Standing assumptions (A1 to A5)
  • A1 (no income). The asset pays no income between date 0 and the expiry date \(\tau\).
  • A2 (one rate). Lending \(S\) at date 0 returns \(S/D\) at \(\tau\), and borrowing \(S\) at date 0 is repaid as \(S/D\) at \(\tau\). Here \(D\in(0,1]\) is the discount factor for \(\tau\), a given number. In the convention of the linked forward concept, \(D=e^{-r\tau}\).
  • A3 (no costs). There are no transaction costs, and a forward is entered at date 0 for no payment.
  • A4 (no arbitrage). No portfolio with zero cost at date 0 has a payoff at \(\tau\) that is nonnegative in every state and positive in some state.
  • A5 (short sale). The asset can be sold short at date 0 at its price \(S\), and the short seller delivers the asset at \(\tau\) without other cost.
Proposition 2.1 (the state-free portfolio and the forward price)

Hypotheses: A1, A2 and A3; the asset has price \(S>0\) at date 0 and price \(S_\tau\) at \(\tau\); a forward on the asset with expiry \(\tau\) is quoted at \(F'\). Conclusions: (i) the portfolio of one long unit of the asset, a loan of \(S\) at date 0 repaid as \(S/D\), and a short forward at \(F'\) has zero cost at date 0 and pays \(F'-S/D\) at \(\tau\) in every state. (ii) Assuming also A4 and A5, \(F'=S/D\).

Proof

(i) At date 0 the long asset costs \(-S\), and the loan provides \(+S\), so the cost is zero. At \(\tau\) the asset is worth \(S_\tau\) and is delivered against the forward, and the short forward receives \(F'\), so the forward and the asset together pay \(F'\). The loan is repaid as \(S/D\), so the total payoff is \(F'-S/D\). This does not depend on \(S_\tau\).

(ii) If \(F'>S/D\), the portfolio of (i) costs zero and pays \(F'-S/D>0\) in every state, which A4 forbids. If \(F'<S/D\), take the reverse portfolio: sell the asset short at \(S\) (A5), lend \(S\) at date 0 to receive \(S/D\) at \(\tau\), and enter a long forward at \(F'\). Its cost is zero, and at \(\tau\) the forward delivers the asset that covers the short sale, so the payoff is \(S/D-F'>0\) in every state, which A4 also forbids. Hence \(F'=S/D\). \(\square\)

Remark 2.2 (the same price in the linked convention)

With \(D=e^{-r\tau}\), the no-arbitrage price \(S/D\) equals \(Se^{r\tau}\), the cost-of-carry forward price of the linked forward concept. The lab takes \(D\) as an input, as a rational number, because \(e^{-r\tau}\) is not a rational number in general, and exact arithmetic needs a rational input.

3The net present value and its break-even

A case can reduce to the sign of a net present value. The next proposition describes that sign completely for a machine that earns a constant contribution per unit.

Proposition 3.1 (linear net present value and its break-even)

Hypotheses: (H1) the rate \(r>-1\) is fixed; (H2) \(C_0=-I\) with \(I>0\), and \(C_t=um\) for \(t=1,\dots,n\), with a margin \(m>0\) and units \(u\ge 0\); (H3) \(n\ge 1\). Let \(A=\sum_{t=1}^{n}(1+r)^{-t}\) (the annuity factor). Conclusions: (i) \(\operatorname{NPV}(u)=-I+mAu\); (ii) \(\operatorname{NPV}\) is strictly increasing in \(u\); (iii) the unique value with \(\operatorname{NPV}=0\), called the break-even, is \(u^{*}=I/(mA)\).

Proof

(i) By H2, \(\operatorname{NPV}(u)=-I+\sum_{t=1}^{n}um(1+r)^{-t}=-I+mAu\). (ii) \(A>0\) because each term is positive by H1 and H3, and \(m>0\), so \(mA>0\) and the function is strictly increasing. (iii) The equation \(-I+mAu=0\) has the unique solution \(u=I/(mA)\), which is the break-even. \(\square\)

Proposition 3.2 (bounds on a Fermi product)

Hypotheses: (H1) there are \(k\ge 1\) factors \(x_1,\dots,x_k\), with \(x_j\in[\ell_j,h_j]\) and \(0<\ell_j\le h_j\); (H2) the estimate is the product \(P=x_1\cdots x_k\). Conclusion: \(\prod_j\ell_j\le P\le\prod_j h_j\), and both ends are attained.

Proof

Induction on \(k\). For \(k=1\) the claim is the hypothesis. Assume it for \(k-1\) factors, with product \(P_{k-1}\in[L_{k-1},H_{k-1}]\), where \(L_{k-1}=\prod_{j<k}\ell_j\) and \(H_{k-1}=\prod_{j<k}h_j\). Since \(x_k\in[\ell_k,h_k]\) and all bounds are positive, multiplication preserves order: \(P_{k-1}x_k\ge L_{k-1}\ell_k\) and \(P_{k-1}x_k\le H_{k-1}h_k\). The ends are attained by choosing each factor at its lower or upper end. \(\square\)

Corollary 3.3 (a range that straddles the break-even does not fix the sign)

Assume Proposition 3.2 and Proposition 3.1. Write \(\ell=\prod_j\ell_j\) and \(h=\prod_j h_j\) for the product bounds of Proposition 3.2. If \(\ell<u^{*}<h\), then the value \(u=\ell\) gives a loss and the value \(u=h\) gives a gain, so the bounds alone do not fix the sign of \(\operatorname{NPV}\). The sign is fixed when the whole interval \([\ell,h]\) lies on one side of \(u^{*}\). This is a consequence of the two propositions, not an extra assumption.

Proof

By Proposition 3.2, the products \(\ell\) and \(h\) are attained by admissible factor values, so both are admissible values of \(u\). By Proposition 3.1, \(\operatorname{NPV}(u)=mA(u-u^{*})\), which is negative for \(u<u^{*}\) and positive for \(u>u^{*}\), because \(mA>0\). Since \(\ell<u^{*}<h\), the value \(u=\ell\) has a loss and \(u=h\) has a gain. If \(h<u^{*}\) or \(\ell>u^{*}\), every \(u\) in \([\ell,h]\) lies on one side of \(u^{*}\), so every unit count in \([\ell,h]\) has the same sign. \(\square\)

4The case: a machine and its units

Definition 4.1 (case interview)

A case interview is a business question with numbers, answered by five steps in order. Restate the question. State every assumption that the answer uses. Size each unknown quantity with a Fermi estimate, as a product of factor ranges. Compute the answer under the stated assumptions. Recommend a course of action and report how the answer changes when an assumption changes.

Example 4.2 (the machine)

Question. Should the firm buy a machine for 15000000 cents that earns a contribution of 5000 cents for each unit sold, over three years? Assumptions, chosen for this example and not taken from any firm: the rate is 10 percent; there is no salvage value and no tax; the contribution is the same in each year. Sizing. Units per year are customers times orders per customer times the share of orders won. The ranges are 2000 to 4000 customers, 1 to 2 orders per customer per year, and a share from one tenth to one fifth. By Proposition 3.2 the units lie in \([200,\,1600]\) per year. Compute. By Proposition 3.1, the annuity factor is \(A=3310/1331\), the break-even is \(u^{*}=399300/331\), about 1206.34 units per year, and the net present value is negative at 200 units and positive at 1600 units. Recommend. The range straddles the break-even, so by Corollary 3.3 the range does not decide the case. The recommendation is conditional: the firm should buy only if its evidence puts units above 1206.34 per year at this rate, and it should measure units before committing.

Remark 4.3 (sensitivity to the rate)

The break-even moves with the rate. At 8 percent it is about 1164.10 units, and at 12 percent it is about 1249.05 units. Measured from the unrounded values, 1164.1005, 1206.3444 and 1249.0469 units at 8, 10 and 12 percent, the break-even is 42.24 units lower at 8 percent and 42.70 units higher at 12 percent than at 10 percent. The net present value at 200 units is negative, and at 1600 units positive, at all three rates. Lab 1, Part 2 prints the rounded values.

Lab 1 · The forward check and the case, in exact fractions

Before running: Part 1 takes spot 9700 cents and the discount factor 97/100 as an assumed input. It expects the no-arbitrage price 10000 cents, a zero value at inception, and a payoff that is 0 cents at a quote of 10000 and 100 cents at a quote of 10100, at each of five spot values from 8000 to 12000 cents (8000, 9000, 10000, 11000 and 12000). Part 2 uses the Fermi bounds of Example 4.2 and the 10 percent rate. It expects the annuity factor \(3310/1331\), the break-even \(399300/331\), an exact zero NPV at the break-even, an increasing NPV on the grid, and the NPV values at the bounds and at 8, 10 and 12 percent. Standard library only, with no randomness and no network access; every value is an exact fraction, and only the displayed values are taken half up to the whole cent or to two decimals.

"""PC3 lab L06: one forward-price check and one case cash-flow calculation, in exact fractions.

Every input is an assumption made for this example. None is a market quote, a market rate or a company's figure, and the
lab reads no data file. Standard library only, with no randomness and no network access.

Money is an integer number of cents. Every value is held as a fractions.Fraction, so every identity is exact. Display
is the only place a value is changed: a displayed value is the exact value taken half up, toward plus infinity, to the
stated number of decimal places, applied once to the exact value.

Part 1 is a forward-price check. A discount factor D = 97/100 for the expiry is an input, with D = exp(-r tau) in the
convention of the lesson. The no-arbitrage forward price is F = S / D. The portfolio of a long asset, a loan of S repaid
as S / D, and a short forward at a quoted price F' pays F' - S / D at expiry in every spot state.

Part 2 is a case. A machine costs 15000000 cents. It earns a contribution of 5000 cents per unit sold for three
years, and units per year are sized by a Fermi product of three assumed factor ranges. The net present value is the
sum of C_t / (1 + r)^t at the stated rate, with the outlay at t = 0.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
import math
from collections.abc import Sequence
from fractions import Fraction

SPOT_CENTS = 9_700  # spot price today, 9700 cents
DISCOUNT = Fraction(97, 100)  # assumed discount factor for the expiry
QUOTED_CENTS = 10_100  # a forward quoted above the no-arbitrage price

OUTLAY_CENTS = 15_000_000  # 15000000 cents at t = 0
MARGIN_CENTS = 5_000  # contribution per unit: 5000 cents
YEARS = 3
RATE = Fraction(10, 100)  # assumed rate for the case


def half_up(x: Fraction, places: int) -> str:
    """The exact value x shown to the given number of decimal places, taken half up once, toward plus infinity."""
    scale = 10**places
    n = math.floor(x * scale + Fraction(1, 2))
    sign = "-" if n < 0 else ""
    n = abs(n)
    if places == 0:
        return f"{sign}{n}"
    whole, frac = divmod(n, scale)
    return f"{sign}{whole}.{frac:0{places}d}"


def npv(cash_flows: Sequence[Fraction], rate: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Net present value: the sum over t of C_t / (1 + rate)^t, with cash_flows[0] at t = 0. Exact."""
    return sum((c / (1 + rate) ** t for t, c in enumerate(cash_flows)), Fraction(0))


def part_one() -> None:
    print("Part 1 · forward price check, cents, discount factor 97/100 (assumed)")
    spot = Fraction(SPOT_CENTS)
    no_arb = spot / DISCOUNT
    print(f"no-arbitrage forward price F = S / D = {no_arb} cents")
    print(f"value at inception of a forward struck at F, S - D F = {spot - DISCOUNT * no_arb} cents")
    for label, quoted in (("no-arbitrage price", no_arb), ("quoted above it", Fraction(QUOTED_CENTS))):
        payoffs: list[Fraction] = []
        for spot_t in (8_000, 9_000, 10_000, 11_000, 12_000):
            asset = Fraction(spot_t)  # the long asset, worth S_T at expiry
            forward = quoted - Fraction(spot_t)  # short forward: receive F, deliver the asset worth S_T
            loan = -spot / DISCOUNT  # the loan of S today is repaid as S / D
            payoffs.append(asset + forward + loan)
        constant = len(set(payoffs)) == 1
        print(f"{label}, F = {quoted}: payoff at expiry for S_T = 8000..12000 is {[str(p) for p in payoffs]}")
        equals_formula = payoffs[0] == quoted - spot / DISCOUNT
        print(f"  payoff the same at the five spot values: {constant}; equals F - S / D: {equals_formula}")
    initial = -spot + spot  # buy the asset for S, lend nothing; borrow S today
    print(f"initial outlay of the portfolio: -S for the asset plus S from the loan = {initial} cents")


def fermi_units() -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Lower and upper bounds on units per year, as the product of customers, orders per customer and share."""
    customers = (Fraction(2_000), Fraction(4_000))
    orders = (Fraction(1), Fraction(2))
    share = (Fraction(1, 10), Fraction(1, 5))
    lower = customers[0] * orders[0] * share[0]
    upper = customers[1] * orders[1] * share[1]
    return lower, upper


def cash_flows(units: Fraction) -> list[Fraction]:
    """C_0 = -outlay, and C_1 = C_2 = C_3 = units x margin, all in cents."""
    return [Fraction(-OUTLAY_CENTS)] + [units * MARGIN_CENTS] * YEARS


def part_two() -> None:
    print("Part 2 · case: machine cost 15000000 cents, margin 5000 cents per unit, 3 years, rate 10% (assumed)")
    lower, upper = fermi_units()
    print(f"Fermi bounds on units per year: lower {lower}, upper {upper}")
    annuity = sum((1 / (1 + RATE) ** t for t in range(1, YEARS + 1)), Fraction(0))
    print(f"annuity factor sum of (1 + r)^-t for t = 1..3 = {annuity}")
    break_even = Fraction(OUTLAY_CENTS, MARGIN_CENTS) / annuity
    print(f"break-even units per year = {break_even} = {half_up(break_even, 2)} to two decimals")
    npv_break_even = npv(cash_flows(break_even), RATE)
    print(f"NPV at the break-even units is exactly zero: {npv_break_even == 0}")
    grid = [Fraction(u) for u in range(200, 1601, 100)]
    values = [npv(cash_flows(u), RATE) for u in grid]
    increasing = all(a < b for a, b in itertools.pairwise(values))
    print(f"NPV increases with units on the grid 200 to 1600 in steps of 100: {increasing}")
    lower_npv = npv(cash_flows(lower), RATE)
    print(f"NPV at the lower bound = {half_up(lower_npv, 0)} cents (exact {lower_npv})")
    print(f"NPV at the upper bound = {half_up(npv(cash_flows(upper), RATE), 0)} cents")
    print(f"the Fermi range contains the break-even: {lower <= break_even <= upper}")
    print("sensitivity to the rate, cents, displayed to the whole cent:")
    for rate in (Fraction(8, 100), Fraction(10, 100), Fraction(12, 100)):
        low_npv = npv(cash_flows(lower), rate)
        high_npv = npv(cash_flows(upper), rate)
        be = Fraction(OUTLAY_CENTS, MARGIN_CENTS) / sum((1 / (1 + rate) ** t for t in range(1, YEARS + 1)), Fraction(0))
        print(
            f"  rate {half_up(rate * 100, 0)}%: NPV at lower {half_up(low_npv, 0)}, at upper {half_up(high_npv, 0)}, "
            f"break-even units {half_up(be, 2)}"
        )


def main() -> None:
    part_one()
    part_two()

Part 1 shows that the payoff of the portfolio is the same at each of the five spot values it tests, which is the state-free property of Proposition 2.1; the proof, not the five values, covers every spot value. 5Where the canon breaks

The forward price of Proposition 2.1 relies on A1 to A5. Real assets pay income, real borrowing and lending rates differ, and short sales have costs, so the price is a model result. The rate convention matters: the linked net present value is discrete, and the linked forward concept is continuous. The lab uses a given discount factor, and it does not convert between the two conventions.

The case answer is only as good as its assumptions. The model holds the margin fixed at 5000 cents and the rate fixed over the three years. It ignores tax, depreciation, working capital, salvage and any option to stop. Each omission can move the break-even, and the lesson does not measure any of them.

Proposition 3.2 gives the bounds of a product, and it does not give a probability. A Fermi range is an assumed range of plausible values, and the lesson does not treat it as evidence that the values are typical. The range of units is wide, by a factor of 8, because each of three factors is assumed to vary by a factor of 2.

6Misconceptions

  1. “The forward price is the expected future spot price.”

    The no-arbitrage price \(S/D\) uses no expectation. Proposition 2.1 gives a portfolio whose payoff is the same in every spot state. Lab 1, Part 1 prints a payoff that is constant over spot values from 8000 to 12000 cents: 0 cents at the no-arbitrage quote of 10000 cents, and 100 cents at the quote of 10100 cents.

  2. “A case answer is a number, and its assumptions are a footnote.”

    The break-even depends on the rate. Lab 1, Part 2 prints about 1164.10 units at 8 percent, about 1206.34 at 10 percent and about 1249.05 at 12 percent. On average over 8 to 12 percent, the break-even moves by 21.24 units per point of rate, which is \((1249.0469-1164.1005)/4\) from the unrounded values (Problem E3), so the assumptions are part of the answer.

  3. “The Fermi bounds decide the case.”

    In Example 4.2 the bounds of Proposition 3.2 bracket the break-even. At 200 units the NPV is negative, and at 1600 units it is positive (Lab 1, Part 2). Corollary 3.3 says the bounds alone do not fix the sign when the break-even lies between them.

  4. “One net present value at one rate settles the case.”

    The sign at the bounds is the same at all three rates in Lab 1, but the break-even units move with the rate. A recommendation that holds at one rate and fails at another needs the sensitivity of Remark 4.3.

7Problem set

Each problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. A problem that quotes a lab value uses the value the lab prints. A solution that needs a value the lab does not print computes it by hand from the closed form of Proposition 3.1 and says so. No problem uses a market quote.

Mechanics

M1 · A forward and an arbitrage

The spot is 4900 cents and the discount factor is 0.98. Find the no-arbitrage forward price. If a forward is quoted at 5050 cents, find the payoff per unit at expiry of the portfolio of Proposition 2.1.

Solution

The no-arbitrage price is \(S/D=4900/0.98=5000\) cents. The portfolio payoff is \(F'-S/D=5050-5000=50\) cents per unit, in every spot state, and its cost at date 0 is zero.

M2 · A small net present value

Find the net present value at 10 percent of cash flows \(-100,\ 60,\ 60\) at \(t=0,1,2\), exactly.

Solution

Discount each flow by its factor \((1.1)^{-t}\) and add. The two later flows give \(60/1.1=600/11\) and \(60/1.21=6000/121\), so \(\operatorname{NPV}=-100+600/11+6000/121\). Over the denominator 121 this is \(-12100/121+6600/121+6000/121=500/121\), about 4.13.

M3 · A break-even in units

An outlay of 100000 cents earns a margin of 400 cents per unit in each of three years, at a rate of 10 percent. Find the break-even units per year, exactly.

Solution

By Proposition 3.1, \(u^{*}=I/(mA)\) with \(I=100000\), \(m=400\) and \(A=3310/1331\). So \(u^{*}=100000\cdot 1331/(400\cdot 3310)=33275/331\), about 100.53 units per year.

Proof

P1 · The reverse portfolio

Write out the reverse portfolio of Proposition 2.1(ii) for \(F'<S/D\), and show that its payoff at \(\tau\) equals \(S/D-F'\) in every state, with zero cost at date 0.

Solution

Sell the asset short for \(S\) at date 0, which is allowed by A5. Lend \(S\) at date 0, which returns \(S/D\) at \(\tau\) by A2. Enter a long forward at \(F'\). The cost at date 0 is \(+S-S=0\), since the forward is entered for no payment by A3. At \(\tau\) the forward delivers the asset to the long party, which covers the short sale, and the long party pays \(F'\) and receives the asset. The net from the forward is the asset received minus \(F'\), which covers the short sale and leaves \(-F'\). Adding the loan's \(S/D\) gives \(S/D-F'\) in every state, which does not depend on \(S_\tau\).

P2 · The unique break-even

Prove that \(\operatorname{NPV}(u)=-I+mAu\) has exactly one zero, and that it is positive above that zero and negative below it.

Solution

By Proposition 3.1(i) the function is affine in \(u\) with slope \(mA>0\). An affine function with positive slope has one zero, at \(u^{*}=I/(mA)\). For \(u>u^{*}\), \(mA(u-u^{*})>0\), so \(\operatorname{NPV}(u)=mA(u-u^{*})>0\). For \(u<u^{*}\) the same identity gives a negative value.

P3 · Three Fermi factors

Let \(x_1\in[2000,4000]\), \(x_2\in[1,2]\) and \(x_3\in[1/10,1/5]\). State the product bounds from Proposition 3.2 and show that each end is attained.

Solution

The lower bound is \(2000\cdot 1\cdot(1/10)=200\) and the upper bound is \(4000\cdot 2\cdot(1/5)=1600\). By the induction in Proposition 3.2, the product of the three factors lies in \([200,1600]\). The lower end is attained at \((2000,1,1/10)\) and the upper end at \((4000,2,1/5)\), since each factor is taken at one of its ends.

Empirical

E1 · The break-even at 12 percent

Compute the annuity factor at 12 percent and the break-even units for the machine of Example 4.2. Compare with the value Lab 1, Part 2 prints.

Solution

The annuity factor is \(1/1.12+1/1.2544+1/1.404928\). Its value is about 2.40183. The break-even is \(3000/2.40183\), about 1249.05 units per year, which matches the value Lab 1 prints. The factor 3000 is the outlay divided by the margin, \(15000000/5000\).

E2 · How many grid points are profitable

Lab 1 evaluates the NPV on the units grid 200, 300, \(\dots\), 1600 at 10 percent. Count the grid points with a positive NPV, and identify the largest grid point with a negative NPV.

Solution

The break-even is about 1206.34 units, so the grid points above it are 1300, 1400, 1500 and 1600, which is four points. The point 1200 lies below the break-even. Lab 1, Part 2 checks that the NPV increases on the grid but prints only the bound values, so the two NPVs below are hand computations from Proposition 3.1 with \(A = 3310/1331\), margin 5000 cents and outlay 15000000 cents. At 1200 units the NPV is \(5000 \cdot 1200 \cdot 3310/1331 - 15000000 = -105000000/1331\), which is \(-78888.05\) cents to two places and negative. At 1300 units it is \(1550000000/1331\), which is about \(1164537.94\) cents and positive. So 1200 is the largest negative grid point, and four grid points are positive.

E3 · The spread of break-even units

From Lab 1, Part 2, the break-even units are about 1164.10 at 8 percent and about 1249.05 at 12 percent. Compute the width of the range that these two rates produce, and the change per point of rate.

Solution

Differences are taken from the unrounded break-evens, which Proposition 3.1 gives at each rate with its own factor \(A\), namely \(1164.1005\) at 8 percent, \(1206.3444\) at 10 percent and \(1249.0469\) at 12 percent, and each difference is displayed to two decimals after it is taken. The width is \(1249.0469-1164.1005=84.9464\), which displays as 84.95 units. The span from 8 to 12 percent is four percentage points, so the average change is \(84.9464/4=21.2366\), which displays as 21.24 units per point. The change from 8 to 10 percent is \(1206.3444-1164.1005=42.2439\) units, and from 10 to 12 percent it is \(1249.0469-1206.3444=42.7025\), which displays as 42.70. Subtracting the displayed values, 1249.05 minus 1206.34, would give 42.71 and is not the answer. Each two-point step moves the break-even by about 42 units, which is about 21 units per point on average over 8 to 12 percent.

R1 · Which factor to narrow first

Criterion: among the three factors of Example 4.2, name the one whose narrowing reduces the log-width of the units product most. The log-width of a factor range \([\ell,h]\) is \(\ln(h/\ell)\). State what would decide the case, and whether the log-width criterion is enough.

Solution

The log-width of a product of positive ranges is the sum of the factor log-widths, \(\ln(h_j/\ell_j)\). Each factor is a range with a ratio of 2: customers \(4000/2000\), orders \(2/1\) and share \((1/5)/(1/10)\). So each log-width is \(\ln 2\), and the three factors tie on this criterion. The log-width criterion does not choose one. An open question remains: a criterion based on the decision, such as the factor whose narrowing moves the break-even out of the range, is not settled by this lesson, and the lesson does not compute it.

R2 · A margin that is not flat

Criterion: suppose the margin in year \(t\) is \(m_t\) instead of a constant. Find the break-even scale of units in closed form, and state what the model no longer determines.

Solution

If the units are a common scale \(u\) in every year, then the cash flow in year \(t\) is \(um_t\), and \(\operatorname{NPV}(u)=-I+u\sum_{t}m_t(1+r)^{-t}\). This is affine in \(u\), so the break-even is \(u^{*}=I/\sum_{t}m_t(1+r)^{-t}\), by the argument of Proposition 3.1 with \(mA\) replaced by the weighted sum. The model does not determine the margin path itself, which is an assumption the case must state.

R3 · The margin move that reaches the upper bound

Criterion: find the proportional fall in the margin of 5000 cents that moves the break-even to 1600 units, the upper end of the units range, at 10 percent. Express the answer as a percentage to one decimal place.

Solution

The break-even is \(I/(mA)\). Setting it equal to 1600 gives the margin \(m^{*}=I/(1600A)\). In cents, \(I=15000000\) and \(A=3310/1331\), so \(m^{*}=15000000\cdot 1331/(1600\cdot 3310)\), about 3769.8 cents, which is 0.7540 of the assumed 5000 cents. The fall is about 24.6 percent. Below that margin the break-even exceeds the top of the range, so the whole range would be unprofitable.
