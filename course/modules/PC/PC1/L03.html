Empirical finance seminars and referee reports frequently stall on poorly documented exhibits. When a reader must search through fifteen pages of text to discover whether standard errors are clustered at the firm or industry level, or what units are measured in a regression column, communication fails. A standalone table conveys all information necessary for autonomous interpretation without reference to the surrounding narrative. Simultaneously, visual exhibits require disciplined perceptual design. Truncated axes and decorative ornamentation distort economic relationships. This lesson formalizes the standalone table checklist, proves the arithmetic distortion of truncated bar charts, and establishes evidence-grounded principles of figure redesign.

1The Anatomy of a Standalone Table

In quantitative research, tables are the primary vessels of evidence. Expert readers inspect regression tables before reading prose. A table that cannot be interpreted independently forces readers into speculative guessing.

Definition 3.1 (Standalone Table)

A standalone table is an empirical exhibit that satisfies all six criteria of the standalone checklist:

  1. Informative Title: The title states the economic relationship or primary finding (e.g. Institutional Cross-Ownership and Physical Investment Across Manufacturing Firms), rather than a generic tag (e.g. Regression Results or Table 1).
  2. Explicit Column Units: Every column header defines the dependent variable and states its physical or financial units (e.g. Capital Expenditure / Assets (%)).
  3. Documented Standard Error Form: Standard errors are reported beneath coefficients in parentheses, and the exact error formulation (e.g. Standard errors clustered at the firm level) is explicitly stated in the table notes.
  4. Sample Size and Time Horizon: The table footer or notes report total observations \(N\), unique entities (e.g. unique firms), and calendar date span.
  5. Defined Significance Markers: All significance asterisks or p-value symbols are explicitly mapped to their thresholds in the note (e.g. * p < 0.10, ** p < 0.05, *** p < 0.01).
  6. Defined Acronyms and Symbols: Every abbreviation, variable acronym, and mathematical symbol appearing in headers, rows, or notes is defined in the footer.
Example 3.2 (Standalone vs. Defective Regression Table Presentation)

Consider a regression table titled Table 3: OLS Estimates with column headers (1) and (2) and a footnote saying * p < 0.05. An expert reader cannot know whether the dependent variable is in log dollars, percentage points, or basis points; cannot determine whether standard errors are robust to heteroskedasticity or clustered; and cannot discern what sample period generated the coefficients. Adding explicit units, informative titles, clustering definitions, and sample counts transforms this into a standalone exhibit.

2Figure Redesign and Graphical Perception

Visualizing data is essential because identical summary statistics can conceal radically different bivariate associations, as demonstrated by the Anscombe's quartet precedent. Anscombe (1973) showed that four datasets with identical summary statistics (means, variances, correlations, and regression lines) possess radically different distributions, proving that numerical tables must be paired with visual inspection. However, visual representations must respect the visual processing capabilities of human readers.

Definition 3.3 (Figure Redesign)

Figure redesign is the systematic transformation of an empirical graphic to maximize data-ink density and map quantitative dimensions to the highest available tier of the graphical perception hierarchy while eliminating misleading visual encodings.

Theorem 3.4 (Cleveland-McGill Graphical Perception Hierarchy)

Cleveland and McGill (1984) established that human observers decode quantitative information encoded along a common position scale with greater accuracy than when encoded as lengths, directions, or areas. The ordered hierarchy of elementary perceptual tasks from most accurate to least accurate is: \[ \text{Position on common scale} \succ \text{Position on non-aligned scales} \succ \text{Length} \succ \text{Direction/Angle} \succ \text{Area} \succ \text{Volume} \succ \text{Color saturation} \] Consequently, figure redesign replaces pie charts (angle/area) and divided bar charts with dot plots and scatter plots aligned on a common scale.

3The Truncated-Axis Distortion Arithmetic

Bar charts encode values through the physical length of the bar. Truncating the baseline of a bar chart produces a misleading chart by severing the proportionality between physical bar length and quantitative value.

Theorem 3.5 (Truncated-Axis Distortion Factor)

Let two positive quantities satisfy \(0 < y_1 < y_2\), with true relative ratio: \[ R_{\text{true}} = \frac{y_1}{y_2} \] If a bar chart truncates the baseline to \(y_0 \in (0, y_1)\), the physical bar lengths are \(h_1 = y_1 - y_0\) and \(h_2 = y_2 - y_0\), yielding an apparent visual ratio: \[ R_{\text{trunc}} = \frac{y_1 - y_0}{y_2 - y_0} \] Then \(R_{\text{trunc}} < R_{\text{true}}\), and the visual distortion factor \(\Delta = \frac{R_{\text{true}}}{R_{\text{trunc}}}\) is strictly greater than 1: \[ \Delta = \frac{y_1 (y_2 - y_0)}{y_2 (y_1 - y_0)} > 1 \] Furthermore, \(\lim_{y_0 \to y_1^-} \Delta = \infty\), indicating unbounded visual exaggeration of differences.

Proof

Consider the function \(f(t) = \frac{y_1 - t}{y_2 - t}\) for \(t \in [0, y_1)\). The derivative with respect to \(t\) is: \[ f'(t) = \frac{-(y_2 - t) - (-(y_1 - t))}{(y_2 - t)^2} = \frac{-y_2 + t + y_1 - t}{(y_2 - t)^2} = \frac{y_1 - y_2}{(y_2 - t)^2} \] Since \(y_1 < y_2\), the numerator \(y_1 - y_2 < 0\), while the denominator \((y_2 - t)^2 > 0\). Hence \(f'(t) < 0\) for all \(t \in [0, y_1)\). This implies \(f(t)\) is strictly decreasing in \(t\). Since \(0 < y_0 < y_1\), it follows that: \[ R_{\text{trunc}} = f(y_0) < f(0) = R_{\text{true}} \] Therefore, \(\Delta = \frac{R_{\text{true}}}{R_{\text{trunc}}} = \frac{f(0)}{f(y_0)} > 1\). As \(y_0 \to y_1^-\), the denominator \(y_1 - y_0 \to 0^+\), so \(f(y_0) \to 0^+\), which yields \(\lim_{y_0 \to y_1^-} \Delta = \infty\).

Example 3.6 (Evaluating Truncated Bar Chart Exaggeration)

Let firm A have investment \(y_1 = 92\) million dollars and firm B have \(y_2 = 100\) million dollars. The true ratio is: \[ R_{\text{true}} = \frac{92}{100} = \frac{23}{25} = 0.92 \] Firm A's investment is 92 percent of firm B's. Now suppose an author draws a bar chart with the y-axis starting at \(y_0 = 90\) million dollars. The visible bar heights are: \[ h_1 = 92 - 90 = 2, \quad h_2 = 100 - 90 = 10 \] The apparent visual ratio is: \[ R_{\text{trunc}} = \frac{2}{10} = \frac{1}{5} = 0.20 \] Visually, firm A appears to have only 20 percent of firm B's investment. The visual distortion factor is: \[ \Delta = \frac{23/25}{1/5} = \frac{23}{5} = 4.60 \] The truncated axis exaggerates the visual disparity between the two firms by a factor of 4.6 (a 360% visual distortion).

Lab 1 · Standalone-table checklist and truncated-axis distortion ratio

The curriculum drill checks a weak regression table against the standalone-table checklist (detecting title, units, sample size, and symbol omissions), verifies a redesigned table passing all criteria, and computes the exact truncated-axis distortion factor (4.60x exaggeration) using exact fractions.

"""PC1 Lab L03-1: Standalone-table checklist and truncated-axis distortion ratio.

SIMULATED. Illustrative regression table schema and bar chart axis metrics.
Curriculum lab: Table and figure redesign drill.
All arithmetic uses exact fractions. Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
from fractions import Fraction


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class TableSchema:
    title: str
    column_headers: list[str]
    column_units: list[str]
    notes: str
    sample_size_n: int
    has_standard_errors: bool
    has_defined_symbols: bool


def validate_standalone_table(table: TableSchema) -> dict[str, bool]:
    """Verify that a table satisfies all 6 requirements of a standalone table."""
    # 1. Title must state finding or economic relation, not generic words alone
    generic_titles = {"regression results", "table 1", "estimates", "models"}
    title_informative = table.title.lower().strip() not in generic_titles and len(table.title.split()) >= 4

    # 2. Every column must have non-empty unit
    units_complete = len(table.column_units) == len(table.column_headers) and all(
        len(u.strip()) > 0 for u in table.column_units
    )

    # 3. Standard errors must be present and error type stated in notes
    notes_lower = table.notes.lower()
    se_type_stated = table.has_standard_errors and (
        "standard error" in notes_lower or "se in" in notes_lower or "clustered" in notes_lower
    )

    # 4. Sample size N explicitly reported
    sample_size_reported = table.sample_size_n > 0 and (
        "n =" in notes_lower or "observations" in notes_lower or f"{table.sample_size_n}" in table.notes
    )

    # 5. Significance symbols (* or p-values) defined in notes
    significance_defined = ("*" in notes_lower and "p <" in notes_lower) or "p-value" in notes_lower

    # 6. All symbols/acronyms defined
    symbols_defined = table.has_defined_symbols

    return {
        "Finding in title": title_informative,
        "Units on all columns": units_complete,
        "Standard error type specified": se_type_stated,
        "Sample size N reported": sample_size_reported,
        "Significance symbols defined": significance_defined,
        "Symbols and acronyms defined": symbols_defined,
    }


def compute_axis_distortion(y1: int, y2: int, baseline: int) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Compute true ratio, truncated visible ratio, and distortion factor."""
    true_ratio = Fraction(y1, y2)
    h1 = y1 - baseline
    h2 = y2 - baseline
    trunc_ratio = Fraction(h1, h2)
    distortion_factor = true_ratio / trunc_ratio
    return true_ratio, trunc_ratio, distortion_factor


def main() -> None:
    print("PC1 Lab L03-1: Table and Figure Redesign Drill")
    print("SIMULATED data and schemas")

    # Part 1: Table Redesign Checklist
    print("\n--- Part 1: Standalone-Table Checklist ---")
    weak_table = TableSchema(
        title="Regression Results",
        column_headers=["Model 1", "Model 2"],
        column_units=["", ""],
        notes="Standard errors in parentheses. * p < 0.05.",
        sample_size_n=0,
        has_standard_errors=True,
        has_defined_symbols=False,
    )

    redesigned_table = TableSchema(
        title="Institutional Cross-Ownership and Physical Investment Across Manufacturing Firms",
        column_headers=["Physical CAPX", "R&D Expenditure"],
        column_units=["% of Total Assets", "% of Total Assets"],
        notes=(
            "Standard errors clustered at the firm level reported in parentheses. "
            "N = 14,820 firm-year observations from Compustat manufacturing universe (SIC 2000-3999), 1990-2020. "
            "* p < 0.10, ** p < 0.05, *** p < 0.01. "
            "CAPX denotes capital expenditures; SIC denotes Standard Industrial Classification."
        ),
        sample_size_n=14820,
        has_standard_errors=True,
        has_defined_symbols=True,
    )

    weak_checks = validate_standalone_table(weak_table)
    print("Weak table checks:")
    for k, v in weak_checks.items():
        print(f"  {k:32s}: {v}")
    print(f"Weak table passes all checklist items: {all(weak_checks.values())}")

    redesigned_checks = validate_standalone_table(redesigned_table)
    print("\nRedesigned standalone table checks:")
    for k, v in redesigned_checks.items():
        print(f"  {k:32s}: {v}")
    print(f"Redesigned table passes all checklist items: {all(redesigned_checks.values())}")

    # Part 2: Figure Redesign: Truncated Axis Distortion
    print("\n--- Part 2: Truncated-Axis Bar Chart Distortion ---")
    y1, y2 = 92, 100
    baseline = 90
    true_ratio, trunc_ratio, distortion = compute_axis_distortion(y1, y2, baseline)

    print(f"True values: y1 = {y1}, y2 = {y2}")
    print(f"True ratio (y1 / y2): {true_ratio} ({float(true_ratio):.4f})")
    print(f"Truncated baseline: y0 = {baseline}")
    print(f"Visible bar heights: h1 = {y1 - baseline}, h2 = {y2 - baseline}")
    print(f"Visible ratio (h1 / h2): {trunc_ratio} ({float(trunc_ratio):.4f})")
    print(f"Distortion factor (true / visible): {distortion} ({float(distortion):.2f}x exaggeration)")
    print(f"Exact ratio discrepancy: {true_ratio - trunc_ratio} ({float(true_ratio - trunc_ratio):.4f})")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The standalone table checklist and zero-baseline rule require specific adjustments in certain contexts:

  • Tables with Mixed Units: Multi-equation system estimation or corporate summary tables often report heterogeneous variables (e.g. assets in millions, Tobin's Q as a dimensionless ratio, and return on assets in percent). In such tables, a single global unit cannot be declared; each individual row must explicitly declare its unit in the row stub.
  • Non-Zero Baseline Figures: For line charts tracking time series of index levels, bond prices, or cumulative log returns, setting the vertical axis to zero obscures all high-frequency variation. The zero-baseline mandate applies strictly to bar charts (where length encodes magnitude), whereas dot plots, line charts, and scatter plots may adjust their range to the empirical distribution support.
  • Large Panel Correlation Matrices: Pairwise correlation matrices have bounded support in \([-1, 1]\) by mathematical definition. For these exhibits, reporting sample size \(N\) for every pairwise cell is impractical when missingness varies across pairs; instead, the note reports minimum and median pairwise observation counts.

5Misconceptions

  1. A table displaying significance stars requires no explanatory footnote.

    Conventions for asterisks differ across academic disciplines and journals (e.g. some journals assign three stars to \(p < 0.001\), others to \(p < 0.01\)). Without an explicit mapping in the note, readers cannot confirm the statistical significance threshold applied to the estimates.

  2. Truncating the vertical axis of a bar chart is a cosmetic choice that highlights subtle variation.

    Truncating the axis of a bar chart is an arithmetic distortion, not a cosmetic enhancement. Because human vision encodes the area and length of bars, starting the baseline at 90 for values of 92 and 100 creates an apparent five-fold difference between quantities that differ by only 8.7 percent.

  3. Redesigning a chart consists of adding decorative color schemes and three-dimensional shading.

    Three-dimensional perspective effects and decorative gradients add visual noise without conveying data. Effective figure redesign removes non-data ink, maximizes graphical perception accuracy, and emphasizes direct labels over complex legends.

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 3.1 (Distortion Factor Calculation): True quarterly sales across two divisions are \(y_1 = 48\) million dollars and \(y_2 = 50\) million dollars. An analyst presents a bar chart with baseline \(y_0 = 45\) million dollars. Compute the true ratio \(R_{\text{true}}\), the truncated visual ratio \(R_{\text{trunc}}\), and the distortion factor \(\Delta\).

Solution

True ratio: \(R_{\text{true}} = \frac{48}{50} = \frac{24}{25} = 0.9600\). Visible bar heights: \(h_1 = 48 - 45 = 3\), \(h_2 = 50 - 45 = 5\). Truncated visual ratio: \(R_{\text{trunc}} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6000\). Distortion factor: \(\Delta = \frac{R_{\text{true}}}{R_{\text{trunc}}} = \frac{24/25}{3/5} = \frac{24}{25} \cdot \frac{5}{3} = \frac{8}{5} = 1.60\). The truncated axis exaggerates the relative visual disparity by a factor of 1.60 (a 60% distortion).

Problem 3.2 (Auditing a Regression Footnote): A submitted table note reads: "Estimates significant at 5% level. OLS regressions." Identify four mandatory elements of the standalone checklist that are missing from this footnote.

Solution

Missing standalone elements: (1) Standard error formulation: Does not state whether standard errors are classical, heteroskedasticity-robust, or clustered. (2) Sample size and period: Does not report \(N\) or sample years. (3) Significance threshold mapping: Does not define asterisk symbols (e.g. * \(p < 0.10\), ** \(p < 0.05\)). (4) Variable definitions: Does not define acronyms or control variable abbreviations used in rows and headers.

Problem 3.3 (Perceptual Encoding Selection): You need to visualize the geographical distribution of corporate default rates across 50 states. Compare encoding the default rates as color saturation on a choropleth map versus a sorted horizontal dot plot. Which encoding provides higher perceptual decoding accuracy under the Cleveland-McGill hierarchy?

Solution

Under Theorem 3.4 (Cleveland-McGill hierarchy), position on a common scale sits at Tier 1 (most accurate), whereas color saturation sits at Tier 7 (least accurate). A sorted horizontal dot plot encodes each state's default rate as a position along a common scale, enabling precise numerical comparison. A choropleth map relies on color saturation and area, which are prone to optical illusion and geographic area bias.

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 3.4 (Strict Monotonicity of Distortion with Baseline): For fixed \(0 < y_1 < y_2\), prove that the distortion factor \(\Delta(y_0) = \frac{y_1(y_2 - y_0)}{y_2(y_1 - y_0)}\) is strictly increasing in the baseline truncation level \(y_0\) on \([0, y_1)\).

Solution

Differentiate \(\Delta(y_0)\) with respect to \(y_0\): \[ \frac{d \Delta}{dy_0} = \frac{y_1}{y_2} \cdot \frac{-(y_1 - y_0) - (-(y_2 - y_0))}{(y_1 - y_0)^2} = \frac{y_1}{y_2} \cdot \frac{-y_1 + y_0 + y_2 - y_0}{(y_1 - y_0)^2} = \frac{y_1 (y_2 - y_1)}{y_2 (y_1 - y_0)^2} \] Since \(y_1 > 0\), \(y_2 > y_1\) (so \(y_2 - y_1 > 0\)), and \((y_1 - y_0)^2 > 0\) for \(y_0 < y_1\), every factor is strictly positive. Hence \(\frac{d \Delta}{dy_0} > 0\) on \([0, y_1)\), proving that higher baseline truncation strictly increases visual distortion.

Problem 3.5 (Invariance of Position Judgments to Baseline Shift in Dot Plots): Unlike bar charts where magnitude is encoded as distance from baseline, let a dot plot encode quantities \(y_1, y_2\) as positions \(x_1 = s(y_1 - y_0)\) and \(x_2 = s(y_2 - y_0)\) on a scale with gain \(s > 0\). Prove that the relative spatial distance \(\frac{x_2 - x_1}{s}\) is invariant to baseline shift \(y_0\).

Solution

Compute the spatial distance between the two points: \[ x_2 - x_1 = s(y_2 - y_0) - s(y_1 - y_0) = s(y_2 - y_0 - y_1 + y_0) = s(y_2 - y_1) \] Dividing by scale factor \(s\) yields: \[ \frac{x_2 - x_1}{s} = y_2 - y_1 \] This quantity depends exclusively on the true physical difference \(y_2 - y_1\) and is completely independent of baseline \(y_0\). This proves why dot plots do not require a zero baseline to preserve absolute difference judgments.

Problem 3.6 (Information Entropy of Standalone Notes): Let a table contain \(K\) non-standard abbreviations and \(M\) econometric choices. Prove that if the probability of an external reader knowing each item independently is \(p \in (0, 1)\), the probability of unambiguous comprehension without notes is \(p^{K+M}\), which converges exponentially to 0 as \(K + M \to \infty\).

Solution

Under independent comprehension events with probability \(p \in (0, 1)\), the joint probability of a reader correctly decoding all \(K + M\) elements without external documentation is \(P(\text{comprehend all}) = \prod_{i=1}^{K+M} p = p^{K+M}\). Since \(p < 1\), \(\lim_{K+M \to \infty} p^{K+M} = 0\). For example, with \(p = 0.85\) and \(K+M = 10\), \(0.85^{10} \approx 0.1969\), so over 80% of readers encounter at least one decoding failure unless the table note provides complete definitions.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 3.7 (Auditing a Published Corporate Finance Table): A researcher examines Table 2 in a corporate governance paper. The title is "Cross-Sectional Regressions." Column headers are "Dep Var: CAPX." The note states: "Parentheses contain t-statistics. * denotes significance." State whether this table satisfies Definition 3.1 and list all defects.

Solution

The table violates Definition 3.1 across four checklist criteria: (1) Non-informative Title: "Cross-Sectional Regressions" states an estimation procedure rather than the economic finding. (2) Missing Units: "CAPX" has no unit (dollars, percent of assets, or log capital expenditures). (3) Incomplete Significance Mapping: "* denotes significance" does not specify the probability threshold (e.g. 10%, 5%, or 1%). (4) Undefined Acronyms: "CAPX" is an acronym that must be defined in the footer.

Problem 3.8 (Redesigning a High-Dimensional Asset Pricing Plot): An asset pricing study presents 25 Fama-French size and book-to-market portfolio alphas using a 3D bar chart with perspective projection. Identify the graphical perception flaws of this exhibit and propose an optimal 2D redesign.

Solution

Flaws: (1) 3D perspective distortion: Occlusion causes front bars to conceal rear bars. (2) Perspective projection prevents accurate length judgment along the vertical axis. (3) Cleveland-McGill hierarchy failure: Volume and 3D angle are near the bottom of perceptual accuracy. Optimal Redesign: A 2D multi-panel dot plot or scatter plot with 5 panels (one per size quintile) plotting alpha (with 95% confidence intervals) on the vertical axis against book-to-market quintiles on the horizontal axis.

Problem 3.9 (Quantifying Multi-Bar Truncation Exaggeration): Three portfolio Sharpe ratios are 0.85, 0.90, and 0.95. An author plots them as bars starting at baseline 0.80. Compute the true ratio of portfolio 1 to portfolio 3 versus the visible bar height ratio, and state the distortion factor.

Solution

True ratio: \(R_{\text{true}} = \frac{0.85}{0.95} = \frac{17}{19} \approx 0.8947\). Visible heights: \(h_1 = 0.85 - 0.80 = 0.05\), \(h_3 = 0.95 - 0.80 = 0.15\). Visible ratio: \(R_{\text{trunc}} = \frac{0.05}{0.15} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.3333\). Distortion factor: \(\Delta = \frac{17/19}{1/3} = \frac{51}{19} \approx 2.6842\). The chart visually portrays portfolio 1 as possessing only 33% of the Sharpe ratio of portfolio 3, whereas in reality it possesses 89.5% (an exaggeration factor of 2.68).

Problem 3.11 (Refactoring an Ineffective Literature Summary Table): A literature summary table lists 30 papers with author names, publication year, and a checked box if the paper finds an effect. Redesign this table into an evidence-synthesis exhibit that conveys methodology, sample size, point estimates, and identification strategies.

Solution

Redesign Plan: Replace binary check boxes with structured quantitative columns: (1) Paper & Year (e.g. Author et al., 2020) (2) Empirical Setting (e.g. U.S. Commercial Banks, 2008–2015) (3) Identification Strategy (e.g. Staggered State Banking Deregulation DiD) (4) Primary Point Estimate & Unit (e.g. +34 bp lending growth per 100 bp capital shock) (5) Standard Error & Inference Type (e.g. (11.2), clustered by state) (6) Observational Support N (e.g. N = 12,400 bank-years). Group rows by identification category (Natural Experiments vs. Instrumental Variables vs. Panel Fixed Effects) rather than alphabetical author order.

Problem 3.12 (Designing an Event Study Redesign Protocol): An event study figure plots cumulative abnormal returns around earnings announcements with 10 overlaid spaghetti lines, shaded background gradients, and an unlabelled y-axis. Formulate a 4-step figure redesign protocol to transform this into a publication-grade exhibit.

Solution

Redesign Protocol: Step 1 (Remove Spaghetti Lines): Replace the 10 overlapping lines with a single bold mean cumulative abnormal return (CAR) trajectory, accompanied by a shaded 95% pointwise confidence interval band. Step 2 (Label Axes and Units): Explicitly label the horizontal axis as "Event Days Relative to Announcement (\(t = 0\))" and the vertical axis as "Cumulative Abnormal Return (%)" with explicit percentage ticks. Step 3 (Zero Benchmark Lines): Add a dashed horizontal reference line at CAR = 0% and a vertical dashed reference line at Event Day 0. Step 4 (Eliminate Decorative Clutter): Strip all background 3D shading, remove grid lines that do not align with integer days, and direct-label the confidence interval on the canvas rather than using a disconnected legend.

    Options

    In dot plots and line charts, values are decoded via position along a scale rather than physical length, so adjusting the range to the empirical support does not distort relative differences.

  8. Why are 3D bar charts prohibited in professional quantitative exhibits?

    Options

    Perspective distortion in 3D bar charts warps visual height decoding and causes foreground elements to obscure background data, violating graphical perception principles.
