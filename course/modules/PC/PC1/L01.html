Structure of a Finance Paper

An empirical finance paper succeeds when an expert reader extracts its core quantitative finding, identification strategy, and institutional mechanism within five minutes. Readers do not read papers linearly from start to finish. They read the introduction, inspect primary tables, and examine robustness checks. A fragmented or unconventional section structure forces readers to search for essential econometric choices. This lesson establishes the canonical six-section architecture of an empirical finance paper, defines exact word budgets across sections, and specifies the first-number position rule for empirical introductions.

1The Canonical Six-Section Architecture

In quantitative and empirical finance, papers communicate causal relationships, asset pricing anomalies, or corporate financial decisions using structured observational data. The architecture of a research manuscript reflects the underlying paper anatomy introduced in foundation research craft.

Definition 1.1 (Research Paper Structure)

The research paper structure of an empirical finance manuscript is the ordered sequence of six functional sections: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{S} = (&\text{Introduction}, \text{Data \& Sample}, \text{Empirical Framework}, \\ &\text{Core Results}, \text{Mechanisms \& Robustness}, \text{Conclusion}) \end{aligned} \] where each section satisfies an exclusive functional assignment:

  1. Introduction: States the central research question, headline quantitative estimate with explicit units, identification strategy, institutional mechanism, and document roadmap.
  2. Data & Sample Selection: Details primary data sources, sample filters, variable definitions, corporate action adjustments, and winsorization thresholds.
  3. Empirical Framework: Specifies the structural econometric regression model, identifying variation, instrumental variables, and clustering choices for identification.
  4. Core Results: Reports baseline regression tables, point estimates, economic magnitudes, and primary hypothesis tests.
  5. Mechanisms & Robustness: Conducts battery of robustness checks, falsification tests, sub-sample splits, and channel diagnostics supporting causal interpretation.
  6. Conclusion: Summarizes the economic takeaway, policy consequences, and explicit boundary conditions without introducing new empirical estimates.
Remark 1.2 (Convention vs. Empirical Claim)

The six-section sequence is a professional communication convention designed to facilitate verification, replication, and referee scrutiny. It is not an empirical law of nature. Manuscripts deviating from this functional partition risk referee friction because readers must hunt for identification assumptions across descriptive prose.

2Section Word Budget Allocation

A standard empirical finance paper comprises approximately \(N_w = 10\,000\) words of main text, excluding appendices and bibliographic entries. Unbalanced word budgets distort paper quality: an overextended literature review crowds out identification discussion, while an abbreviated data section impedes independent replication.

Definition 1.3 (Canonical Word Budget Allocation)

Let \(N_w \in \mathbb{N}\) denote total main text word count. The canonical fractional budget vector \(\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2, w_3, w_4, w_5, w_6)\) assigns word shares to the six sections in \(\mathcal{S}\) according to: \[ \mathbf{w} = \left(\frac{1}{10},\, \frac{3}{20},\, \frac{1}{5},\, \frac{3}{10},\, \frac{1}{5},\, \frac{1}{20}\right) \] satisfying the budget exhaustion identity \(\sum_{i=1}^6 w_i = \frac{2}{20} + \frac{3}{20} + \frac{4}{20} + \frac{6}{20} + \frac{4}{20} + \frac{1}{20} = 1\).

Example 1.4 (Target Budget for a 10,000-Word Manuscript)

For a baseline manuscript with \(N_w = 10\,000\) words, the target section word counts evaluate to:

  • Introduction: \(N_w \cdot w_1 = 10\,000 \cdot \frac{1}{10} = 1\,000\) words (10.0%).
  • Data & Sample: \(N_w \cdot w_2 = 10\,000 \cdot \frac{3}{20} = 1\,500\) words (15.0%).
  • Empirical Framework: \(N_w \cdot w_3 = 10\,000 \cdot \frac{1}{5} = 2\,000\) words (20.0%).
  • Core Results: \(N_w \cdot w_4 = 10\,000 \cdot \frac{3}{10} = 3\,000\) words (30.0%).
  • Mechanisms & Robustness: \(N_w \cdot w_5 = 10\,000 \cdot \frac{1}{5} = 2\,000\) words (20.0%).
  • Conclusion: \(N_w \cdot w_6 = 10\,000 \cdot \frac{1}{20} = 500\) words (5.0%).

3First-Number Position Rule

An introduction that defers quantitative findings to page 3 or paragraph 4 generates ambiguity regarding the paper's contribution. Readers cannot assess the economic magnitude without an immediate empirical benchmark.

Definition 1.5 (First-Number Position Metric)

Let an introduction comprise an ordered sequence of sentences \((s_1, s_2, \dots, s_K)\). The first-number position \(j^*\) is the minimal sentence index containing an empirical quantitative estimate: \[ j^* = \min \{j \in \{1, \dots, K\} : s_j \text{ contains a quantitative empirical estimate}\} \] The first-sentence result rule requires \(j^* = 1\), with an allowable upper bound of \(j^* \le 2\).

Theorem 1.6 (Information Preservation under Early Quantitative Anchoring)

Under the hypothesis that a reader's probability of reading past sentence \(k\) decays monotonically according to \(P(\text{continue} \ge k) = \rho^{k-1}\) with continuation parameter \(\rho \in (0, 1)\), the expected probability that a reader observes the paper's headline empirical magnitude is maximized if and only if \(j^* = 1\).

Proof

The probability of observing the headline magnitude is \(P(\text{observe}) = P(\text{continue} \ge j^*) = \rho^{j^* - 1}\). Since \(\rho \in (0, 1)\), the function \(f(j) = \rho^{j-1}\) is strictly decreasing in \(j\) for \(j \ge 1\). The maximum over \(j \in \{1, 2, \dots, K\}\) occurs uniquely at \(j^* = 1\), yielding \(P(\text{observe}) = \rho^0 = 1\). If the headline number is delayed to sentence \(j^* = 8\) with \(\rho = 0.8\), the probability drops to \(0.8^7 = \frac{2097152}{10000000} \approx 0.2097\), an approximate 79 percent information loss.

Lab 1 · Section budget and first-number position check

The lab checks a simulated 10,000-word empirical finance outline against the canonical budget vector (1/10, 3/20, 1/5, 3/10, 1/5, 1/20) and verifies that the introduction's first empirical number occurs in sentence 1.

"""PC1 Lab L01-1: Section budget and first-number position check on a simulated paper outline.

SIMULATED. Outline and paragraph text are constructed models of an empirical finance paper.
All numbers are verified with exact fractions and integer counts. Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import re
from dataclasses import dataclass
from fractions import Fraction


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class SectionEntry:
    title: str
    words: int
    target_fraction: Fraction


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class PaperOutline:
    sections: list[SectionEntry]

    @property
    def total_words(self) -> int:
        return sum(s.words for s in self.sections)


def check_section_budget(outline: PaperOutline) -> list[tuple[str, int, Fraction, Fraction]]:
    """Return each section with words, actual fraction, and target fraction."""
    total = outline.total_words
    results: list[tuple[str, int, Fraction, Fraction]] = []
    for s in outline.sections:
        actual = Fraction(s.words, total)
        results.append((s.title, s.words, actual, s.target_fraction))
    return results


def find_first_number_sentence(intro_text: str) -> tuple[int, str]:
    """Find the 1-based index and text of the first sentence containing an empirical digit."""
    # Split sentences on periods followed by whitespace
    sentences = [s.strip() for s in re.split(r"\.\s+", intro_text.strip()) if s.strip()]
    for idx, sentence in enumerate(sentences, start=1):
        if re.search(r"\d", sentence):
            return idx, sentence
    return 0, ""


def main() -> None:
    print("PC1 Lab L01-1: Section Budget and First-Number Position Check")
    print("SIMULATED outline and introduction text")

    # Canonical 6-section empirical finance paper structure (10,000 words total)
    sections = [
        SectionEntry("Introduction", 1000, Fraction(1, 10)),
        SectionEntry("Data and Sample Selection", 1500, Fraction(3, 20)),
        SectionEntry("Identification and Model", 2000, Fraction(1, 5)),
        SectionEntry("Core Empirical Results", 3000, Fraction(3, 10)),
        SectionEntry("Robustness and Mechanisms", 2000, Fraction(1, 5)),
        SectionEntry("Conclusion", 500, Fraction(1, 20)),
    ]
    outline = PaperOutline(sections)

    print(f"Total word count: {outline.total_words}")
    budget_checks = check_section_budget(outline)
    all_matched = True
    for title, words, actual, target in budget_checks:
        pct_actual = float(actual) * 100
        pct_target = float(target) * 100
        matched = actual == target
        if not matched:
            all_matched = False
        print(
            f"  {title:30s} | Words: {words:5d} | Actual: {pct_actual:5.1f}% "
            f"({actual}) | Target: {pct_target:5.1f}% ({target}) | Match: {matched}"
        )

    print(f"All section budget shares match targets: {all_matched}")

    # Simulated introduction text
    simulated_intro = (
        "We show that institutional cross-ownership increases five-year physical investment by 14 basis points\n"
        "per standard deviation increase in common ownership across United States manufacturing firms from 1990 to "
        "2020.\n"
        "This effect is concentrated in industries with high product market overlap and persists after instrumenting\n"
        "for ownership using financial institution mergers.\n"
        "In contrast to theories of unilateral market power, the investment response reflects coordinated capital\n"
        "expenditure reductions in duplicate facilities.\n"
        "The remainder of this paper details the sample selection, empirical identification, primary regression "
        "results,\n"
        "battery of robustness checks, and concluding implications for antitrust regulation."
    )

    sent_idx, sent_text = find_first_number_sentence(simulated_intro)
    # Normalize newline to single space for clean display
    clean_sent = " ".join(sent_text.split())
    print(f"First empirical number occurs in sentence: {sent_idx}")
    print(f"First number sentence: {clean_sent}")
    first_sentence_rule = sent_idx == 1
    print(f"First-sentence result requirement satisfied: {first_sentence_rule}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The canonical six-section architecture applies specifically to empirical archival finance papers. It breaks in the following settings:

  • Pure Theoretical Asset Pricing: Continuous-time equilibrium models and derivative pricing papers have no observational data section. Their structure replaces Data & Sample with an Economic Environment and Asset Menu section, followed by Equilibrium Characterization and Numerical Comparative Statics.
  • Methodological & Econometric Contributions: Papers developing new estimators or inference procedures allocate their primary space to Estimator Formulation, Asymptotic Properties, and Monte Carlo Simulation Experiments before presenting an Empirical Illustration.
  • Short Research Notes: Journal letters (such as Finance Research Letters) operate under strict length constraints (2,500 to 3,500 words). They collapse the model, results, and robustness into a single consolidated empirical section without a formal literature discussion.

5Misconceptions

  1. The literature review must precede the paper's results to establish context.

    Placing three pages of historical literature review before the paper's findings forces readers to evaluate claims before understanding what the paper accomplishes. Expert readers require the paper's own findings in Section 1 before positioning those findings against prior work in Section 2 or later.

  2. The introduction should outline the paper's administrative plan rather than substantive findings.

    Generic roadmap sentences such as "Section 2 describes data; Section 3 describes methods" convey zero economic information. An informative roadmap names the content of each section, detailing the identification mechanism and specific battery of robustness tests.

  3. The six-section paper structure is an arbitrary bureaucratic requirement rather than an identification scaffold.

    The six-section architecture isolates identification choices from descriptive statistics and baseline results from sensitivity checks. Conflating baseline results with robustness checks hides the vulnerability of the primary causal claim.

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 1.1 (Word Budget Shares): An empirical manuscript contains 12,500 total words. Using the canonical budget vector \(\mathbf{w} = (1/10, 3/20, 1/5, 3/10, 1/5, 1/20)\), compute the exact target word budget for each of the six canonical sections.

Solution

Multiply total word count \(N_w = 12\,500\) by each fractional component: Introduction: \(12\,500 \cdot \frac{1}{10} = 1\,250\) words. Data & Sample Selection: \(12\,500 \cdot \frac{3}{20} = 1\,875\) words. Empirical Framework: \(12\,500 \cdot \frac{1}{5} = 2\,500\) words. Core Results: \(12\,500 \cdot \frac{3}{10} = 3\,750\) words. Mechanisms & Robustness: \(12\,500 \cdot \frac{1}{5} = 2\,500\) words. Conclusion: \(12\,500 \cdot \frac{1}{20} = 625\) words. Check sum: \(1250 + 1875 + 2500 + 3750 + 2500 + 625 = 12\,500\) words.

Problem 1.2 (First-Number Indexing): An introduction contains 12 sentences. Quantitative numbers appear in sentences 4, 7, and 11. Determine the first-number position \(j^*\) and evaluate whether this introduction satisfies the first-sentence result rule.

Solution

By Definition 1.5, \(j^* = \min \{j : s_j \text{ contains an empirical number}\} = \min\{4, 7, 11\} = 4\). The first-sentence result rule requires \(j^* = 1\) (with an allowable threshold \(j^* \le 2\)). Because \(j^* = 4 > 2\), the introduction fails the first-sentence result requirement and must be rewritten to place the headline finding in sentence 1.

Problem 1.3 (Introduction Budget Check): A working paper draft has an introduction of 2,400 words inside an 8,000-word manuscript. Compute the actual budget share and the discrepancy relative to the canonical 10% share.

Solution

The actual budget share is \(\frac{2400}{8000} = \frac{3}{10} = 30.0\%\). The canonical allocation is \(\frac{1}{10} = 10.0\%\). The excess share is \(30.0\% - 10.0\% = 20.0\%\) of the total paper, representing an overallocation of \(2\,400 - 800 = 1\,600\) words. This indicates extensive literature review or preliminary results that should be shifted to later sections.

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 1.4 (Monotonicity of Reader Attention): Let reader survival probability follow \(S(k) = \exp(-\lambda(k-1))\) for hazard rate \(\lambda > 0\) across sentences \(k \ge 1\). Prove that delaying the first empirical result from sentence 1 to sentence \(m > 1\) incurs a strictly positive relative loss of reader reach equal to \(1 - \exp(-\lambda(m-1))\).

Solution

At \(k = 1\), survival probability is \(S(1) = \exp(-\lambda(0)) = 1\). At sentence \(k = m\), survival probability is \(S(m) = \exp(-\lambda(m-1))\). The relative loss is \(\frac{S(1) - S(m)}{S(1)} = \frac{1 - \exp(-\lambda(m-1))}{1} = 1 - \exp(-\lambda(m-1))\). Since \(\lambda > 0\) and \(m > 1\), the argument \(-\lambda(m-1) < 0\), which implies \(\exp(-\lambda(m-1)) < 1\). Hence \(1 - \exp(-\lambda(m-1)) > 0\), proving a strictly positive loss.

Problem 1.5 (Functional Orthogonality of Sections): Suppose a paper partitions all information content into set \(\Omega\). If the six canonical sections \((S_1, \dots, S_6)\) satisfy \(S_i \cap S_j = \emptyset\) for all \(i \ne j\) and \(\bigcup_{i=1}^6 S_i = \Omega\), prove that duplicating an empirical table in both Section 4 (Results) and Section 1 (Introduction) violates partition efficiency.

Solution

Let table information be \(T \subset \Omega\) with measure \(\mu(T) > 0\). If \(T \subset S_1\) and \(T \subset S_4\), then \(T \subset (S_1 \cap S_4)\), so \(S_1 \cap S_4 \ne \emptyset\), contradicting pairwise disjointness. The total paper length \(\sum_{i=1}^6 \mu(S_i) = \mu(\Omega) + \mu(T) > \mu(\Omega)\), which incurs deadweight reading cost without increasing total information content.

Problem 1.6 (Uniqueness of Section Sequences): Prove that under the requirement that identification must precede baseline estimation and baseline estimation must precede robustness testing, the sequence (Empirical Framework, Core Results, Robustness) is the unique valid ordering of these three components.

Solution

There are \(3! = 6\) permutations of the three components: Framework (F), Results (R), Robustness (B). The requirements establish two strict partial orders: \(F \prec R\) and \(R \prec B\). By transitivity of strict partial orders, \(F \prec B\). Among all 6 permutations, the only ordering satisfying \(F \prec R \prec B\) is \((F, R, B)\). Any other permutation violates at least one precedence constraint.

Problem 1.8 (Sentence Position vs. Referee Sentiment): In a simulated sample of 50 referee evaluations, papers with \(j^* = 1\) received an average clarity score of 4.6/5.0, whereas papers with \(j^* \ge 5\) received an average score of 2.8/5.0. Compute the percentage difference in clarity score relative to the \(j^* \ge 5\) baseline.

Solution

SIMULATED evaluation metrics. The percentage increase in clarity score is computed from exact values: \(\frac{4.6 - 2.8}{2.8} = \frac{1.8}{2.8} = \frac{18}{28} = \frac{9}{14} \approx 64.29\%\). Placing the result in sentence 1 is associated with a 64.3 percent increase in subjective clarity score relative to papers deferring results to sentence 5 or later.

Problem 1.9 (Sample Selection Transparency Check): A draft data section reports an initial sample of 45,000 observations and a final regression sample of 18,200 observations without listing intermediate filters. Specify the exact checklist items missing from this section.

Solution

A transparent Data and Sample Selection section requires a step-by-step sample attrition table. Missing items include: (1) Initial universe retrieval query and date; (2) Missing data filter exclusions (e.g. dropped due to missing Compustat assets or CRSP stock returns); (3) Financial and utility firm exclusions (SIC 6000-6999, 4900-4949); (4) Minimum price and market equity thresholds (e.g. price \(\ge \$5\)); (5) Attrition counts and remaining sample sizes at each sequential filter step.

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 1.10 (Restructuring a Narrative Paper): You are handed a draft that opens with a 6-page historical review of sovereign banking crises before stating the research question on page 7. Construct a 3-step refactoring plan to reorganize the draft into canonical research paper structure.

Solution

Refactoring plan: Step 1: Extract the central empirical estimate, unit, and identification strategy from page 7 and formulate Sentence 1 of a newly condensed 1,000-word Introduction. Step 2: Relocate the 6-page historical crisis review into an Institutional Setting subsection within Section 2 (Data & Sample Selection) or Section 3 (Empirical Framework), compressing narrative prose by 50%. Step 3: Establish a dedicated Section 5 (Mechanisms & Robustness) to house subsequent channel diagnostics, ensuring the baseline regression table stands alone in Section 4.

Problem 1.11 (Drafting a Functional Roadmap): Transform the following weak roadmap: "Section 2 presents data. Section 3 discusses methodology. Section 4 presents empirical results. Section 5 concludes" into an informative, content-descriptive roadmap for a paper on algorithmic trading and market liquidity.

Solution

Informative roadmap rewrite: "The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the NASDAQ millisecond limit order book dataset and message traffic filtering. Section 3 outlines the high-frequency difference-in-differences identification strategy exploiting colocation server upgrades. Section 4 reports core regression estimates of algorithmic order cancelation on quoted and effective bid-ask spreads. Section 5 evaluates alternative liquidity metrics, volatility regimes, and execution latency mechanisms. Section 6 concludes with policy implications for exchange fee structures."

Problem 1.12 (Designing a Mechanism Section): An author finds that CEO tenure correlates positively with corporate green bond issuance. Referees suspect managerial career horizon bias. Design a 3-part empirical test battery for Section 5 (Mechanisms & Robustness) to evaluate this mechanism.

Solution

Section 5 test battery: Part 1 (Subsample Split by Retirement Proximity): Partition the sample into CEOs within 2 years of mandatory retirement versus younger CEOs; career horizon bias predicts the green bond premium concentrates exclusively among near-retirement executives seeking legacy status. Part 2 (Compensation Sensitivity): Regress green bond issuance on tenure while controlling for the interaction between tenure and ESG-linked executive compensation incentives. Part 3 (Falsification Placebo): Examine conventional bond issuance timing; if career horizon bias is specific to environmental signaling, tenure should not predict conventional debt timing.
