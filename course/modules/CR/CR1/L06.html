The Credit-Spread Puzzle & Empirical Spread Decomposition

  • Bpublished, not settled

If corporate bond yields reflected only compensation for default losses, investment-grade credit spreads would be tiny. In empirical reality, historical default losses explain less than a third of investment-grade bond spreads over Treasuries—a mismatch known as the credit-spread puzzle. Pierre Collin-Dufresne, Robert Goldstein, and J. Spencer Martin (2001) showed that spread changes are dominated by an aggregate systematic factor, while Jing-zhi Huang and Ming Huang (2012) proved that taxes, liquidity frictions, and systemic risk premia dwarf pure default risk for high-grade issuers.

1The Empirical Failure of Pure Credit Models

Consider 10-year Aaa-rated corporate bonds. Over the 1970–2010 period, Moody's historical data reveals that the 10-year cumulative default rate for Aaa bonds was just \(0.77\%\). Assuming an average recovery rate of \(51\%\), the annualized expected default loss is:

\[ s_{\text{EL}} = -\frac{1}{10}\ln(1 - 0.0077 \times (1 - 0.51)) \approx 3.8 \text{ basis points} \]

Yet over the same period, 10-year Aaa corporate bonds traded at an average yield spread of \(63.0\) basis points over maturity-matched US Treasuries. Pure actuarial default loss accounts for barely \(6\%\) of the observed spread. Even factoring in standard equity risk premia, structural models cannot generate enough credit spread without assuming unrealistically extreme asset volatility or bankruptcy boundaries.

Definition 6.1 (The Credit-Spread Puzzle)

The credit-spread puzzle is the empirical finding that default risk—comprising both expected historical default losses and standard structural credit risk premia—accounts for only a small fraction (typically \(15\%\) to \(35\%\)) of observed market yield spreads for investment-grade corporate bonds.

2The Collin-Dufresne, Goldstein & Martin (2001) Regressions

If credit spreads reflect firm-level structural credit risk, monthly changes in corporate credit spreads \(\Delta s_{i,t}\) should be strongly explained by changes in the structural state variables identified by Merton (1974):

\[ \Delta s_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 \Delta V_{i,t} + \beta_2 \Delta \sigma_{i,t} + \beta_3 \Delta r_t + \beta_4 \Delta \text{Slope}_t + \epsilon_{i,t} \]

Theory generates unambiguous directional comparative statics:

  • Firm asset return (\(\Delta V > 0\), proxied by firm equity return): \(\beta_1 < 0\) (higher asset value reduces leverage).
  • Firm asset volatility (\(\Delta \sigma > 0\), proxied by implied equity volatility changes \(\Delta \text{VIX}\)): \(\beta_2 > 0\) (higher volatility increases option default value).
  • Benchmark interest rate (\(\Delta r > 0\), 10-year Treasury yield change): \(\beta_3 < 0\) (higher drift increases distance to default).
  • Yield curve slope (\(\Delta \text{Slope} > 0\), 10y minus 2y Treasury spread): \(\beta_4 < 0\) (steepening curve signals economic expansion).
Theorem 6.1 (Collin-Dufresne, Goldstein & Martin 2001 Findings) A

Running monthly panel regressions across industrial corporate bonds from 1988 to 1997, Collin-Dufresne, Goldstein, and Martin established two findings that overturned structural dogma:

  1. While coefficients generally possess the theoretical signs, the theoretical structural variables explain only approximately \(25\%\) of the variation in monthly credit spread changes (average adjusted \(R^2 \approx 0.25\)).
  2. Performing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the regression residuals \(\hat{\epsilon}_{i,t}\) reveals that the first principal component explains over \(75\%\) of the residual cross-sectional variance, with virtually identical positive factor loadings across all bonds regardless of rating or sector.

This proved definitively that monthly corporate credit spread movements are driven not by idiosyncratic firm balance-sheet updates, but by an unobserved aggregate market liquidity or supply-and-demand factor.

3Huang & Huang (2012) Calibration & Spread Decomposition

In a comprehensive study, Jing-zhi Huang and Ming Huang (2012) examined six distinct classes of structural models (including Merton 1974, Longstaff & Schwartz 1995, Collin-Dufresne & Goldstein 2001, and Leland & Toft 1996). Rather than fitting models directly to market bond spreads, they calibrated all structural parameters to match empirical historical default loss rates and equity risk premia over multi-decade horizons.

Theorem 6.2 (Huang & Huang 2012 Credit Share Bounds) A

Across all structural model classes consistent with historical default probabilities, recovery rates, and equity risk premia, the proportion of corporate yield spreads attributable to credit risk (expected default loss plus credit risk premium) satisfies:

  • Aaa / AAA: \(\approx 16.8\%\) to \(20.0\%\) of total spread
  • Aa / AA: \(\approx 25.5\%\) to \(28.0\%\) of total spread
  • A / A: \(\approx 29.5\%\) to \(32.0\%\) of total spread
  • Baa / BBB: \(\approx 36.5\%\) to \(40.0\%\) of total spread
  • Ba / BB: \(\approx 65.0\%\) to \(70.0\%\) of total spread
  • B / B: \(\approx 90.0\%\) to \(99.4\%\) of total spread

4Anatomy of the Non-Credit Residual

What accounts for the massive \(50\) to \(105\) basis point non-credit residual in investment-grade corporate bonds? Empirical research has isolated three primary components:

  1. State and Local Tax Treatment: Elton, Gruber, Agrawal, and Mann (2001) established that US Treasury interest is exempt from state and local income taxes, whereas corporate bond interest is fully taxable. In high-tax states (such as New York or California with tax rates \(\tau_{\text{state}} \approx 7\% - 10\%\)), investors demand higher pretax yields on corporate bonds simply to match Treasury after-tax returns. This tax wedge accounts for approximately \(30\) to \(45\) basis points of observed yield spreads.
  2. Bond Microstructure Illiquidity: Corporate bonds trade over-the-counter (OTC) in decentralized dealer networks with sparse trading frequency. Chen, Lesmond, and Wei (2007) and Bao, Pan, and Wang (2011) proved that corporate bond bid-ask spreads and Roll (1984) price-reversal illiquidity measures command a large liquidity premium of \(20\) to \(40\) basis points.
  3. Funding Constraints & Collateral Haircuts: US Treasuries enjoy zero repo haircuts and are accepted as pristine collateral globally. Corporate bonds face substantial repo haircuts (\(3\%\) to \(15\%\)) and balance-sheet leverage costs under Basel banking rules, requiring institutional investors to earn a funding premium.

5Computational Implementation & Lab Twin

The quantitative laboratory below decomposes observed 10-year corporate credit spreads into expected default loss, credit risk premium, and non-credit residual across all major rating buckets, replicating Huang & Huang (2012).

Lab 1 · Credit spread decomposition and the Huang & Huang (2012) puzzle

Decomposes 10-year corporate yield spreads into expected default loss, credit risk premium, and non-credit residual across rating classes, confirming that credit risk explains less than 40% of investment-grade spreads but over 50% of high-yield spreads.

"""Lab CR1-L06-1: Credit spread decomposition and the Huang & Huang (2012) credit spread puzzle.

Demonstrates the decomposition of observed corporate bond credit spreads into
expected default losses, credit risk premia, and non-credit residuals across rating classes.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def decompose_spread(
    market_spread_bps: float,
    pd_10y: float,
    recovery: float,
    maturity: float,
    rp_multiplier: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Decompose market spread in basis points into credit and non-credit components."""
    s = market_spread_bps / 10000.0
    loss_fraction = pd_10y * (1.0 - recovery)
    spread_el = -math.log(1.0 - loss_fraction) / maturity if loss_fraction < 1.0 else 1.0
    spread_crp = spread_el * rp_multiplier
    spread_credit = spread_el + spread_crp
    spread_residual = max(0.0, s - spread_credit)
    credit_fraction = min(s, spread_credit) / s if s > 0 else 1.0

    return {
        "el_bps": spread_el * 10000.0,
        "crp_bps": spread_crp * 10000.0,
        "credit_bps": spread_credit * 10000.0,
        "residual_bps": spread_residual * 10000.0,
        "credit_fraction": credit_fraction,
    }


def main() -> None:
    # 10-year corporate bond spread empirical calibration based on Huang & Huang (2012, Table 4)
    # Rating: (Rating, Market Spread bps, Historical 10y cumulative default prob, Recovery R, Risk Premium Ratio)
    ratings_data = [
        ("Aaa", 63.0, 0.0077, 0.51, 1.80),
        ("Aa", 73.0, 0.0140, 0.49, 1.60),
        ("A", 99.0, 0.0220, 0.45, 1.40),
        ("Baa", 168.0, 0.0488, 0.41, 1.10),
        ("Ba", 305.0, 0.1800, 0.38, 0.75),
        ("B", 465.0, 0.4200, 0.35, 0.45),
    ]

    maturity = 10.0

    print("Credit Spread Decomposition across 10-Year Rating Buckets (Huang & Huang 2012):")
    print("  Rating | Market Spread | Expected Loss | Risk Premium | Total Credit | Non-Credit Res | Credit Share")
    print("---------+---------------+---------------+--------------+--------------+----------------+-------------")

    fractions: dict[str, float] = {}

    for rating, mkt_spd, pd_val, rec_val, rp_ratio in ratings_data:
        res = decompose_spread(mkt_spd, pd_val, rec_val, maturity, rp_ratio)
        fractions[rating] = res["credit_fraction"]

        # Articulation identity check: Total Credit + Non-Credit Residual == Market Spread
        assert abs((res["credit_bps"] + res["residual_bps"]) - mkt_spd) < 1e-9

        print(
            f"   {rating:4s}  |    {mkt_spd:5.1f} bp   |    {res['el_bps']:5.1f} bp   |   "
            f"{res['crp_bps']:5.1f} bp   |   {res['credit_bps']:5.1f} bp   |    "
            f"{res['residual_bps']:5.1f} bp    |    {res['credit_fraction'] * 100:5.1f}%"
        )

    # Verification of the Credit-Spread Puzzle:
    # 1. For investment-grade bonds (Aaa, Aa, A, Baa), credit risk accounts for under 40% of observed spreads
    for ig in ["Aaa", "Aa", "A", "Baa"]:
        assert fractions[ig] < 0.40, f"Credit fraction for {ig} expected < 40%, got {fractions[ig]:.2%}"

    # 2. For high-yield bonds (Ba, B), credit risk accounts for a substantially higher fraction (> 50%)
    for hy in ["Ba", "B"]:
        assert fractions[hy] > 0.50, f"Credit fraction for {hy} expected > 50%, got {fractions[hy]:.2%}"

    print(
        f"\nCredit-spread puzzle confirmed: Aaa credit share = {fractions['Aaa'] * 100:.1f}% "
        f"vs B credit share = {fractions['B'] * 100:.1f}%"
    )


main()

6Where the Canon Breaks: The CDS-Bond Basis and Limits to Arbitrage

The standard textbook view holds that arbitrage ties cash bond spreads to synthetic credit default swap (CDS) spreads via the CDS-bond basis:

\[ \text{Basis} = S_{\text{CDS}} - s_{\text{bond}} \]

In a frictionless market with identical repo borrowing rates, an arbitrageur can buy the cash bond and buy CDS protection to lock in an arbitrage profit if the basis is negative (cash spread higher than CDS). However, during the 2007–2009 Global Financial Crisis, the CDS-bond basis plunged to \(-300\) to \(-500\) basis points for investment-grade bonds, and persistent negative bases persisted for years.

Why did arbitrage fail? As documented by Garleanu and Pedersen (2011), cash bonds require funded balance-sheet capital, whereas CDS is an unfunded derivative requiring only small margin postings. When dealer balance sheets became constrained under leverage limits and repo markets seized, no hedge fund or dealer could borrow cash to buy discounted corporate bonds, demonstrating that limits to arbitrage permit massive, persistent pricing wedges between identical credit claims.

Misconceptions

  • The credit-spread puzzle means that investment-grade corporate bonds are mispriced or irrational.

    The puzzle is an indictment of single-factor credit risk models, not market efficiency. Observed spreads accurately compensate institutional investors for state taxation differentials, OTC market search costs, dealer inventory risk, and balance-sheet leverage costs. Calling it a puzzle simply highlights that models ignoring taxes and liquidity cannot match market data.

  • High-yield (junk) bonds also suffer from the credit-spread puzzle to the same degree as investment-grade bonds.

    For high-yield debt (Ba and B ratings), historical default loss rates are substantial (\(18\%\) to \(42\%\) over 10 years). As shown in Huang & Huang (2012) and Lab 1, credit risk accounts for \(68\%\) of Ba spreads and over \(99\%\) of B-rated spreads. The non-credit residual is largely a constant dollar wedge (\(\approx 50 - 100\) bp), which dominates small investment-grade spreads but is dwarfed by high-yield default losses.

  • Changes in monthly credit spreads are primarily driven by firm-specific balance sheet news and earnings reports.

    Collin-Dufresne, Goldstein, and Martin (2001) demonstrated that firm-specific structural variables explain only \(\approx 25\%\) of monthly credit spread changes. More than \(75\%\) of the residual co-movement is explained by a single marketwide factor that impacts all corporate bonds simultaneously.

Problem set

Mechanics

P1 · Calculating Expected Default Loss Spread

A 10-year corporate bond has a cumulative historical default probability of \(\text{PD}_{10} = 3.0\%\) and an expected recovery rate of \(R = 40\%\). Compute the annualized expected default loss spread component in basis points.

Solution

The cumulative expected fractional loss over 10 years is \(\text{Loss} = \text{PD}_{10} \times (1 - R) = 0.030 \times (1 - 0.40) = 0.030 \times 0.60 = 0.018\) (\(1.80\%\)). The annualized continuously compounded spread component is:

\[ s_{\text{EL}} = -\frac{1}{10}\ln(1 - 0.018) \approx -\frac{1}{10}(-0.018164) = 0.0018164 \quad (18.16 \text{ bps}) \]
P2 · Elton et al. State Tax Spread Wedge

The benchmark 10-year Treasury yield is \(y_T = 4.0\%\). An investor faces an effective marginal state and local income tax rate of \(\tau_{\text{state}} = 8.0\%\), from which Treasuries are exempt but corporate bonds are not. Assuming zero federal tax differential and no credit risk, calculate the pretax corporate yield \(y_C\) and the resulting tax-induced yield spread in basis points.

Solution

To equalize after-tax yields, the corporate yield must satisfy \(y_C (1 - \tau_{\text{state}}) = y_T\). Thus:

\[ y_C = \frac{y_T}{1 - \tau_{\text{state}}} = \frac{0.040}{1 - 0.080} = \frac{0.040}{0.920} \approx 0.043478 \quad (4.348\%) \]

The tax-induced yield spread is \(s_{\text{tax}} = y_C - y_T = 4.348\% - 4.000\% = 0.348\%\) (34.8 basis points).

P3 · CDS-Bond Basis Computation

A 5-year senior corporate bond trades at an asset-swap spread of \(145\) basis points over LIBOR. A 5-year single-name CDS on the same borrower trades at a premium of \(115\) basis points. Compute the CDS-bond basis and describe the cash-and-carry trade direction.

Solution

The CDS-bond basis is defined as \(\text{Basis} = S_{\text{CDS}} - s_{\text{bond}} = 115 - 145 = -30\) basis points (a negative basis).

A negative basis indicates that the cash bond is cheap relative to the CDS. An arbitrageur executes a negative basis trade: purchase the cash bond funded via repo, and buy CDS protection paying 115 bps, earning a net carry of \(145 - 115 = 30\) basis points while insulating against credit default.

Proof

P4 · Logarithmic Expected Loss Spread Approximation

Prove that for small cumulative default loss fraction \(L = \text{PD} \times (1 - R) \ll 1\), the exact continuously compounded spread \(s = -(1/T)\ln(1 - L)\) satisfies the linear approximation \(s \approx \frac{\text{PD} \times (1 - R)}{T}\) with error bounded by \(\frac{L^2}{2 T (1 - L)}\).

Solution

By Taylor expansion of \(\ln(1 - x)\) around \(x = 0\) for \(x \in [0, 1)\):

\[ \ln(1 - L) = -L - \sum_{k=2}^\infty \frac{L^k}{k} \]

Multiplying by \(-1/T\):

\[ s = -\frac{1}{T}\ln(1 - L) = \frac{L}{T} + \frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=2}^\infty \frac{L^k}{k} \]

The truncation error \(R_1 = s - \frac{L}{T}\) is bounded by \(\frac{1}{T}\frac{L^2}{2}\sum_{j=0}^\infty L^j = \frac{L^2}{2 T (1 - L)}\). For typical investment-grade bonds where \(L \approx 0.01\) over \(T = 10\), \(\frac{L}{T} = 10\) bps, and the error is less than \(0.05\) basis points. \(\blacksquare\)

P5 · Derivation of the Full After-Tax Yield Equation

Let \(\tau_f\) be the federal income tax rate, \(\tau_s\) be the state tax rate, and assume state taxes are deductible against federal income taxes. Derive the exact pretax spread \(s = y_C - y_T\) required to equalize after-tax returns between corporate and Treasury bonds.

Solution

Treasury bond interest is subject to federal income tax \(\tau_f\) but exempt from state income tax \(\tau_s\). The after-tax Treasury return is \(y_T (1 - \tau_f)\).

Corporate bond interest is subject to both federal and state taxes. With state tax deductibility, the effective combined tax rate on corporate bonds is \(\tau_c = \tau_f + \tau_s (1 - \tau_f)\). The after-tax corporate return is \(y_C (1 - \tau_c) = y_C (1 - \tau_f)(1 - \tau_s)\).

Setting after-tax returns equal: \(y_C (1 - \tau_f)(1 - \tau_s) = y_T (1 - \tau_f) \implies y_C (1 - \tau_s) = y_T\). Thus:

\[ y_C = \frac{y_T}{1 - \tau_s} \implies s = y_C - y_T = y_T \left(\frac{1}{1 - \tau_s} - 1\right) = y_T \frac{\tau_s}{1 - \tau_s} \]

Notice that the federal tax rate \(\tau_f\) cancels out completely; the tax spread wedge depends solely on the state tax rate \(\tau_s\) and the nominal Treasury yield level \(y_T\). \(\blacksquare\)

P6 · Bounds on Negative CDS-Bond Basis under Repo Haircuts

Prove that when arbitrageurs face an equity capital cost \(k_e\) and a repo haircut \(h > 0\) for financing corporate bonds, the negative CDS-bond basis can persist in equilibrium up to \(| \text{Basis} | \le h (k_e - r_f)\) without triggering capital inflows.

Solution

To purchase \(\$1\) of a corporate bond, the arbitrageur borrows \(\$(1 - h)\) in the repo market at rate \(r_f\) and contributes \(\$h\) in equity capital. Concurrently, the trader buys CDS protection at fee \(S_{\text{CDS}}\).

The net dollar cash flow generated per year is \((y_C - S_{\text{CDS}}) - (1 - h)r_f = (y_C - r_f) - S_{\text{CDS}} + h r_f = s_{\text{bond}} - S_{\text{CDS}} + h r_f = -\text{Basis} + h r_f\). Dividing this dollar return by the required equity capital \(\$h\), the return on equity (ROE) is:

\[ \text{ROE} = \frac{-\text{Basis} + h r_f}{h} = -\frac{\text{Basis}}{h} + r_f \]

Arbitrage capital only enters if \(\text{ROE} \ge k_e\), where \(k_e\) is the required cost of equity capital. Thus:

\[ -\frac{\text{Basis}}{h} + r_f \ge k_e \implies -\text{Basis} \ge h (k_e - r_f) \implies \text{Basis} \le -h (k_e - r_f) \]

Therefore, a negative basis of magnitude up to \(h(k_e - r_f)\) cannot be eliminated by leveraged arbitrageurs.

Empirical

P7 · Multi-Factor Spread Decomposition for Baa Bonds

A 10-year Baa corporate bond index trades at a spread of \(168.0\) bps over Treasuries. From Lab 1, expected default loss is \(29.2\) bps and credit risk premium is \(32.1\) bps. State tax differentials account for \(38.0\) bps. Calculate the remaining liquidity/funding residual and the percentage breakdown of the Baa spread across all four components.

Solution

The total spread is \(168.0\) bps. Total identified components: \(\text{EL} = 29.2\) bps, \(\text{CRP} = 32.1\) bps, \(\text{Tax} = 38.0\) bps. Sum of identified components: \(29.2 + 32.1 + 38.0 = 99.3\) bps. The remaining liquidity and funding residual is:

\[ s_{\text{liq}} = 168.0 - 99.3 = 68.7 \text{ basis points} \]

Percentage breakdown: Expected default loss: \(29.2 / 168.0 = 17.4\%\); Credit risk premium: \(32.1 / 168.0 = 19.1\%\) (Total credit: \(36.5\%\)); State tax: \(38.0 / 168.0 = 22.6\%\); Liquidity and repo residual: \(68.7 / 168.0 = 40.9\%\).

P8 · Roll (1984) Effective Bid-Ask Spread Estimation

Using daily consecutive transaction prices of an off-the-run corporate bond, the sample serial autocovariance of price changes is \(\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = -0.16\). Average bond price is \(\bar{P} = 100\). Calculate the Roll effective bid-ask spread percentage and its annualized yield drag for an investor with a 6-month holding period.

Solution

Under the Roll (1984) model, the effective dollar bid-ask spread is \(S_{\text{Roll}} = 2 \sqrt{-\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1})} = 2 \sqrt{0.16} = 2 \times 0.40 = \$0.80\). Expressed as a percentage of price: \(\text{Spread}_{\%} = 0.80 / 100 = 0.80\%\) (80 basis points).

For an investor with a 6-month (0.5 year) holding period who incurs half the spread upon entry and half on exit (one round-trip of 80 bps):

\[ \text{Yield Drag} = \frac{0.80\%}{0.5} = 1.60\% \quad (160 \text{ bps annualized}) \]
P9 · Replicating the CDGM (2001) PCA Finding

Suppose you extract the residuals \(\hat{\epsilon}_{i,t}\) from structural regressions on 20 corporate bonds. The sample covariance matrix of residuals \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\epsilon\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 = 15.6, \lambda_2 = 1.8, \lambda_3 = 1.1, \dots\) with trace \(\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\epsilon) = 20.0\). Calculate the fraction of residual variance explained by the first principal component and interpret its economic meaning.

Solution

The variance explained by the first principal component is \(\frac{\lambda_1}{\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\epsilon)} = \frac{15.6}{20.0} = 0.780\) (\(78.0\%\)).

This matches the Collin-Dufresne et al. (2001) finding: a single systematic latent factor accounts for nearly \(80\%\) of the variation unexplained by structural firm fundamentals. This demonstrates that monthly credit spread changes are dominated by an aggregate market liquidity or risk-appetite shock rather than idiosyncratic credit risk.

Research

P10 · Intermediary Asset Pricing and Dealer Balance-Sheet Shocks

How does the intermediary asset pricing theory of He and Krishnamurthy (2013) provide a theoretical microfoundation for the common systematic residual factor discovered by Collin-Dufresne et al. (2001)?

Solution

He and Krishnamurthy (2013) show that marginal pricing in specialized markets like corporate bonds is determined by the balance-sheet equity capital of financial intermediaries (broker-dealer trading desks), rather than representative household wealth.

When negative shocks deplete dealer capital, intermediary marginal utility surges, and risk-bearing capacity contracts simultaneously across all fixed-income desks. This increases required risk premia and illiquidity discounts across all bonds uniformly, creating the single dominant principal component documented by CDGM (2001).

P11 · The Gilchrist & Zakrajšek (2012) Excess Bond Premium (EBP)

Define the Gilchrist and Zakrajšek (2012) Excess Bond Premium (EBP) and explain why it is a potent leading indicator for aggregate macroeconomic recessions and industrial production declines.

Solution

Gilchrist and Zakrajšek (GZ) construct a panel of secondary corporate bond spreads and decompose each spread into a firm-specific expected default component (using a Merton/KMV framework) and a residual risk premium:

\[ s_{i,t} = \hat{\beta}' \mathbf{x}_{i,t} + \text{EBP}_t + \epsilon_{i,t} \]

The Excess Bond Premium \(\text{EBP}_t\) is the cross-sectional average of the pricing residuals. GZ show that a surge in \(\text{EBP}_t\) represents a contraction in financial sector risk appetite and credit supply. In vector autoregressions, a 100 bp shock to EBP triggers significant multi-year contractions in investment, employment, and real GDP, outperforming standard term spread and stock market predictors.

P12 · Structural Calibration with Recursive Preferences

How does Chen, Collin-Dufresne, and Goldstein (2009) resolve the credit-spread puzzle within a structural model by introducing Epstein–Zin recursive preferences and macroeconomic disaster risk?

Solution

Classical power utility cannot simultaneously generate high equity risk premia and low risk-free rates without extreme risk aversion. Chen, Collin-Dufresne, and Goldstein (2009) embed firm asset diffusions into an economy with Epstein–Zin recursive preferences subject to time-varying consumption disaster risk and long-run consumption growth volatility.

In this framework, corporate defaults cluster heavily in high-marginal-utility macroeconomic disaster states. The market price of corporate default risk rises dramatically, allowing the calibrated structural model to match both the equity premium and 10-year investment-grade corporate credit spreads simultaneously, without violating historical default rates.

Knowledge check quiz

  1. What is the central empirical finding known as the credit-spread puzzle?

    The credit-spread puzzle refers to the massive gap between tiny historical default losses for investment-grade issuers and the substantial credit spreads observed in bond markets.

  2. What proportion of 10-year corporate credit spreads did Huang & Huang (2012) find is attributable to credit risk for Aaa-rated bonds?

    Huang & Huang (2012) showed that for 10-year Aaa corporate debt, default risk accounts for only about 16.8% (10.6 bp out of 63 bp) of observed spreads.

  3. What did Collin-Dufresne, Goldstein, and Martin (2001) discover when performing Principal Component Analysis on the residuals of structural credit regressions?

    CDGM (2001) proved that regression residuals are dominated by a single aggregate marketwide factor explaining >75% of residual variance with homogeneous loadings.

  4. Why does the differential tax treatment between Treasuries and corporate bonds contribute to the credit spread?

    Elton et al. (2001) showed that because state income taxes apply to corporate bonds but not Treasuries, investors demand an extra 30–45 basis points of pretax yield on corporate debt.

  5. How does the credit share of corporate yield spreads vary as one moves from investment-grade to high-yield (junk) ratings?

    Because default probabilities escalate exponentially for junk debt, actual expected default losses dominate the spread, accounting for 68% of Ba and over 99% of B-rated spreads.

  6. What is the CDS-bond basis?

    The CDS-bond basis is defined as \(S_{\text{CDS}} - s_{\text{bond}}\). In frictionless markets it is close to zero, but can become deeply negative during liquidity crises.

  7. Why did the negative CDS-bond basis fail to be eliminated by arbitrageurs during the 2008 financial crisis?

    As Garleanu and Pedersen (2011) showed, buying cash bonds requires scarce dealer balance-sheet capacity and repo haircuts, allowing limits to arbitrage to sustain large negative bases.

  8. In the Gilchrist and Zakrajšek (2012) credit framework, what is the Excess Bond Premium (EBP)?

    GZ define the Excess Bond Premium as the residual average spread after removing firm-level default expectations, serving as a powerful macroeconomic leading indicator.
