Empirical Default Forecasting & Distance to Default

Robert C. Merton's 1974 model was initially a theoretical pricing breakthrough. Stephen Kealhofer, John McQuown, and Oldrich Vasicek (KMV) transformed it into the world's most widely adopted commercial credit monitoring framework by inverting equity market prices to recover unobservable firm asset values and volatilities. Computing Distance to Default (DD) bridges risk-neutral derivatives pricing with physical default prediction, while Sreedhar Bharath and Tyler Shumway (2008) uncovered surprising empirical truths about how structural models actually forecast corporate bankruptcies.

1From Theory to Industry: The KMV Implementation

In academic finance, the Merton structural credit model assumes total firm asset value \(V_t\) and asset volatility \(\sigma_V\) are directly known. In corporate reality, total asset value is unobservable: corporate loans, private debt, and non-traded physical machinery lack continuous market prices.

Only equity market capitalization \(E = P_{\text{stock}} \times N_{\text{shares}}\) and high-frequency equity price volatility \(\sigma_E\) are directly observable in liquid secondary markets. To operationalize the Merton model, Moody's KMV introduced two crucial practical conventions:

  • The Default Point (\(D\)): Because corporate debt matures across heterogeneous schedules, setting maturity to a single fixed date is impossible. KMV found empirically that corporations do not default when asset value drops below total liabilities; they default when assets fall below short-term commitments plus half of long-term liabilities: \[ D = \text{Short-Term Debt} + 0.5 \times \text{Long-Term Debt} \]
  • One-Year Horizon (\(T = 1.0\)): Corporate credit risk surveillance standardizes default prediction over an annual horizon.

2The Simultaneous Non-Linear System & Inversion

Because equity is modeled as a European call option on total assets \(V\) with strike \(D\), the observed market value of equity satisfies the Black–Scholes–Merton call option formula:

\[ E = V \Phi(d_1) - D e^{-r T} \Phi(d_2) \]

where

\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(V / D) + (r + 0.5\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma_V \sqrt{T} \]

A single equation in two unobservables \((V, \sigma_V)\) has infinite solutions. To close the system, KMV applies Itô's Lemma to the equity value function \(E(V, t)\). Because \(E\) is driven by the single Brownian motion driving \(V\), equity volatility satisfies:

\[ \sigma_E E = \frac{\partial E}{\partial V} \sigma_V V = \Phi(d_1) \sigma_V V \]

Rearranging yields the second fundamental equation:

\[ \sigma_V = \sigma_E \frac{E}{V \Phi(d_1)} \]
Definition 5.1 (The Merton–KMV Inversion System)

Given observable equity value \(E\), equity return volatility \(\sigma_E\), debt default point \(D\), risk-free rate \(r\), and horizon \(T\), the unobservable firm asset value \(V\) and asset volatility \(\sigma_V\) solve the simultaneous non-linear system:

\[ \begin{cases} F_1(V, \sigma_V) = V \Phi(d_1) - D e^{-r T} \Phi(d_2) - E = 0 \\ F_2(V, \sigma_V) = \Phi(d_1) \sigma_V V - \sigma_E E = 0 \end{cases} \]

This system cannot be solved in closed form. In numerical practice, fixed-point iteration or two-dimensional Newton–Raphson is employed. Starting from the naive initial guess \(V^{(0)} = E + D\) and \(\sigma_V^{(0)} = \sigma_E \frac{E}{E + D}\), the solver updates \(V\) via the Black–Scholes inversion and updates \(\sigma_V\) via the volatility link until convergence (typically within 10 to 20 iterations).

3Physical Measure vs. Risk-Neutral Measure: Distance to Default

A vital distinction must be maintained between credit derivatives pricing and physical default prediction. Pricing bonds or CDS occurs under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), where firm assets drift at the risk-free rate \(r\). However, default forecasting in risk management occurs under the physical objective measure \(\mathbb{P}\), where firm assets drift at the expected physical return on assets \(\mu_V\):

\[ d\ln V_t = \left(\mu_V - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\right)dt + \sigma_V dW_t^\mathbb{P} \]

Integrating forward over horizon \(T\), the expected log asset value under \(\mathbb{P}\) is \(\ln V_0 + (\mu_V - 0.5\sigma_V^2)T\), and its standard deviation is \(\sigma_V \sqrt{T}\).

Definition 5.2 (Distance to Default)

The Distance to default (DD) is the standardized number of standard deviations the expected log asset value at horizon \(T\) lies above the debt default point \(\ln D\) under the physical probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\):

\[ \text{DD} = \frac{\ln(V / D) + \left(\mu_V - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\right)T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} \]

Under the geometric Brownian motion assumption, the theoretical physical probability of default is \(\pi_{\text{Merton}} = \Phi(-\text{DD})\).

Notice that for high-grade firms, \(\text{DD}\) typically ranges from 4.0 to 10.0 standard deviations. Evaluating the Gaussian cumulative normal \(\Phi(-8.0) \approx 6 \times 10^{-16}\) yields a default probability that is absurdly small and completely unrepresentative of real default frequencies. Moody's KMV therefore abandoned the Gaussian \(\Phi(-\text{DD})\) in favor of an empirical lookup table: they map a firm's calculated \(\text{DD}\) to historical default records, defining the Expected Default Frequency (EDF) as the fraction of historical firms with that same \(\text{DD}\) that actually defaulted within 12 months.

4The Bharath & Shumway (2008) Challenge: The "Naive" Model

In a landmark empirical paper, Sreedhar Bharath and Tyler Shumway (2008) asked a provocative question: does the computational complexity of solving the non-linear Merton system actually improve default forecasting over simple balance-sheet heuristics?

Bharath and Shumway formulated an explicitly non-iterative, closed-form proxy called the naive distance to default model:

Definition 5.3 (Bharath–Shumway Naive Distance to Default)

The Bharath & Shumway (2008) naive model estimates total firm assets and volatility without solving any implicit equations:

\[ V_{\text{naive}} = E + D \] \[ \sigma_{D,\text{naive}} = 0.05 + 0.25 \sigma_E \] \[ \sigma_{V,\text{naive}} = \frac{E}{E + D}\sigma_E + \frac{D}{E + D}\sigma_{D,\text{naive}} \] \[ \text{DD}_{\text{naive}} = \frac{\ln(V_{\text{naive}} / D) + \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_{V,\text{naive}}^2\right)T}{\sigma_{V,\text{naive}} \sqrt{T}} \]
Theorem 5.1 (Bharath & Shumway Empirical Sufficiency Rejection) A

In dynamic hazard regressions predicting corporate default across CRSP/Compustat firms from 1980 to 2003, Bharath and Shumway established two critical empirical results:

  1. The naive distance to default \(\text{DD}_{\text{naive}}\) achieves pseudo-\(R^2\) and hazard discrimination matching or slightly exceeding the iteratively solved Merton/KMV \(\text{DD}\). Across cross-sections, \(\operatorname{Corr}(\text{DD}_{\text{Merton}}, \text{DD}_{\text{naive}}) > 0.99\).
  2. When Merton \(\text{DD}\) is included in a multivariable hazard regression alongside naive DD, reduced-form accounting variables (leverage, return on assets, equity volatility), the Merton DD coefficient is statistically insignificant or economically negligible. Thus, the Merton model is not a sufficient statistic for default forecasting.

5Computational Implementation & Lab Twin

The laboratory below executes the numerical inversion of the simultaneous non-linear Merton system across a stylized cross-section of corporate issuers, evaluates the Bharath–Shumway naive proxy, and computes their empirical cross-sectional correlation.

Lab 1 · Merton/KMV system inversion and Bharath-Shumway naive comparison

Implements the iterative solution to the simultaneous Merton non-linear equations for asset value and volatility, evaluates the closed-form Bharath-Shumway naive distance to default, and verifies that the correlation between Merton DD and naive DD exceeds 0.98 across corporate issuers.

"""Lab CR1-L05-1: Distance to default and Bharath-Shumway (2008) empirical comparison.

Demonstrates the numerical inversion of the simultaneous Merton/KMV system for
unobservable firm asset value and volatility, compares Merton DD with Bharath-Shumway
naive DD across a cross-section of firms, and verifies their near-perfect correlation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def solve_merton_system(
    e: float,
    d: float,
    t: float,
    r: float,
    sigma_e: float,
    mu: float,
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Solve Merton/KMV system for (V, sigma_V) and compute distance to default."""
    v = e + d
    sigma_v = sigma_e * (e / v)

    for _ in range(150):
        vol_sqrt = max(1e-8, sigma_v * math.sqrt(t))
        d1 = (math.log(v / d) + (r + 0.5 * sigma_v * sigma_v) * t) / vol_sqrt
        d2 = d1 - vol_sqrt
        n_d1 = norm_cdf(d1)
        n_d2 = norm_cdf(d2)

        f_val = v * n_d1 - d * math.exp(-r * t) * n_d2 - e
        if abs(f_val) < 1e-11:
            break
        v = max(e * 0.8, v - f_val / max(1e-5, n_d1))

        vol_sqrt = max(1e-8, sigma_v * math.sqrt(t))
        d1 = (math.log(v / d) + (r + 0.5 * sigma_v * sigma_v) * t) / vol_sqrt
        sigma_v = max(1e-6, sigma_e * e / (v * max(1e-5, norm_cdf(d1))))

    vol_sqrt = max(1e-8, sigma_v * math.sqrt(t))
    dd = (math.log(v / d) + (mu - 0.5 * sigma_v * sigma_v) * t) / vol_sqrt
    pd = norm_cdf(-dd)
    return v, sigma_v, dd, pd


def bharath_shumway_naive(
    e: float,
    d: float,
    t: float,
    sigma_e: float,
    mu: float,
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Compute Bharath & Shumway (2008) naive distance to default."""
    v_naive = e + d
    sigma_d = 0.05 + 0.25 * sigma_e
    sigma_v_naive = (e / v_naive) * sigma_e + (d / v_naive) * sigma_d
    vol_sqrt = max(1e-8, sigma_v_naive * math.sqrt(t))
    dd_naive = (math.log(v_naive / d) + (mu - 0.5 * sigma_v_naive * sigma_v_naive) * t) / vol_sqrt
    pd_naive = norm_cdf(-dd_naive)
    return v_naive, sigma_v_naive, dd_naive, pd_naive


def main() -> None:
    # Stylized cross-section of 5 firms varying from high credit quality to distress
    firms = [
        ("Firm A (AAA)", 200.0, 50.0, 0.20, 0.08),
        ("Firm B (A)", 120.0, 60.0, 0.25, 0.08),
        ("Firm C (BBB)", 80.0, 70.0, 0.32, 0.09),
        ("Firm D (BB)", 45.0, 65.0, 0.42, 0.10),
        ("Firm E (CCC)", 15.0, 60.0, 0.65, 0.12),
    ]

    t_horizon = 1.0
    r_rf = 0.04

    print("Distance to Default Comparison: Merton KMV vs Bharath-Shumway (2008) Naive:")
    print("  Firm         | Merton V | Merton sigV | Merton DD | Merton PD | Naive DD | Naive PD")
    print("---------------+----------+-------------+-----------+-----------+----------+---------")

    merton_dds: list[float] = []
    naive_dds: list[float] = []

    for name, e_val, d_val, s_e, mu_val in firms:
        v_m, sv_m, dd_m, pd_m = solve_merton_system(e_val, d_val, t_horizon, r_rf, s_e, mu_val)
        _v_n, _sv_n, dd_n, pd_n = bharath_shumway_naive(e_val, d_val, t_horizon, s_e, mu_val)

        merton_dds.append(dd_m)
        naive_dds.append(dd_n)

        # Inversion precision check
        vol_sqrt = sv_m * math.sqrt(t_horizon)
        d1 = (math.log(v_m / d_val) + (r_rf + 0.5 * sv_m**2) * t_horizon) / vol_sqrt
        d2 = d1 - vol_sqrt
        reconstructed_e = v_m * norm_cdf(d1) - d_val * math.exp(-r_rf * t_horizon) * norm_cdf(d2)
        assert abs(reconstructed_e - e_val) < 1e-5

        print(
            f"  {name:12s} |  {v_m:7.2f} |    {sv_m:6.4f}   |   {dd_m:6.2f}  |   {pd_m:6.4f}  |  "
            f"{dd_n:6.2f}  |  {pd_n:6.4f}"
        )

    # Compute correlation between Merton DD and Naive DD across the cross-section
    n = len(merton_dds)
    mean_m = sum(merton_dds) / n
    mean_n = sum(naive_dds) / n
    cov = sum((merton_dds[i] - mean_m) * (naive_dds[i] - mean_n) for i in range(n))
    var_m = sum((merton_dds[i] - mean_m) ** 2 for i in range(n))
    var_n = sum((naive_dds[i] - mean_n) ** 2 for i in range(n))
    corr = cov / math.sqrt(var_m * var_n)

    print(f"\nCorrelation between Merton DD and Bharath-Shumway Naive DD: {corr:6.4f}")
    assert corr > 0.98


main()

6Where the Canon Breaks: Fat Tails, Jumps, and Off-Balance Sheet Debt

While Distance to Default is a foundational risk measure, the classical KMV implementation suffers from three severe structural breakdowns:

  • Gaussian tail collapse: Assuming asset log returns follow a normal distribution underestimates the probability of large 5-sigma drops by many orders of magnitude. In market downturns, asset returns exhibit excess kurtosis and jump risk, making \(\Phi(-\text{DD})\) dangerous without empirical lookup adjustments.
  • Off-balance sheet and contingent liabilities: Setting the default point \(D = \text{Short-Term Debt} + 0.5 \times \text{Long-Term Debt}\) ignores operating leases, derivatives counterparty exposures, unfunded pension obligations, and debt covenants. For financial institutions in 2008 (such as Lehman Brothers or Bear Stearns), nominal accounting debt severely understated run-on-the-bank liquidity commitments.
  • Sufficiency failure in multivariable hazard models: As proven by Campbell, Hilscher, and Szilagyi (2008), Merton DD alone fails to incorporate accounting profitability (net income to assets), market-to-book ratios, and stock price momentum, all of which contain independent default predictive power.

Misconceptions

  • Distance to Default uses the risk-free rate r as the asset drift.

    Distance to Default is a physical-measure forecasting statistic, not a risk-neutral derivatives price. It requires the physical expected return on assets \(\mu_V\), typically estimated via CAPM (\(\mu_V = r + \beta_V \lambda_{\text{market}}\)) or historical asset appreciation. Using the risk-free rate \(r\) calculates \(d_2\), which is the risk-neutral default probability under \(\mathbb{Q}\), not physical default risk under \(\mathbb{P}\).

  • The iterative numerical solution of the non-linear Merton system provides dramatically superior default forecasts over simple accounting heuristics.

    Bharath and Shumway (2008) demonstrated empirically that the naive closed-form proxy \(\text{DD}_{\text{naive}}\) achieves equal or superior out-of-sample default classification. The predictive power of the structural model originates in combining equity market value, debt book value, and equity volatility, rather than in the non-linear Black–Scholes option mapping.

  • A firm with Distance to Default of 5.0 standard deviations has an annual default probability of \(\Phi(-5.0) \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-7}\).

    Real corporate asset returns are non-Gaussian and subject to discrete jump events. In historical data compiled by Moody's KMV, firms with \(\text{DD} = 5.0\) exhibit realized default frequencies closer to 5 to 10 basis points (\(0.05\% - 0.10\%\)), which is hundreds of times higher than \(\Phi(-5.0)\). Practitioners must map DD through empirical lookup tables rather than cumulative normal distributions.

Problem set

Mechanics

P1 · Calculating Distance to Default

A corporation has estimated firm asset value \(V = \$500\text{M}\), debt default point \(D = \$350\text{M}\), asset volatility \(\sigma_V = 22\%\), and expected return on assets \(\mu_V = 9.0\%\). Compute the 1-year Distance to Default (\(\text{DD}\)) and the theoretical normal default probability \(\Phi(-\text{DD})\).

Solution

Compute \(\ln(V / D) = \ln(500 / 350) = \ln(1.42857) \approx 0.35667\). The drift term is \(\mu_V - 0.5\sigma_V^2 = 0.090 - 0.5 \times (0.22)^2 = 0.090 - 0.0242 = 0.0658\). For \(T = 1.0\), the numerator is \(0.35667 + 0.0658 = 0.42247\). The denominator is \(\sigma_V \sqrt{T} = 0.22\). Distance to Default is:

\[ \text{DD} = \frac{0.42247}{0.22} \approx 1.9203 \]

The theoretical default probability is \(\Phi(-1.9203) \approx 0.0274\) (\(2.74\%\)).

P2 · Bharath–Shumway Naive Asset Volatility

A firm has market equity \(E = \$150\text{M}\), debt default point \(D = \$100\text{M}\), and equity return volatility \(\sigma_E = 40\%\). Calculate the naive debt volatility \(\sigma_{D,\text{naive}}\) and the naive firm asset volatility \(\sigma_{V,\text{naive}}\).

Solution

Under the Bharath–Shumway specification:

\[ \sigma_{D,\text{naive}} = 0.05 + 0.25 \sigma_E = 0.05 + 0.25 \times 0.40 = 0.05 + 0.10 = 0.15 \quad (15\%) \]

Total naive assets: \(V_{\text{naive}} = E + D = 150 + 100 = \$250\text{M}\). Equity weight: \(w_E = 150 / 250 = 0.60\); debt weight: \(w_D = 100 / 250 = 0.40\). Naive asset volatility is:

\[ \sigma_{V,\text{naive}} = 0.60 \times 0.40 + 0.40 \times 0.15 = 0.24 + 0.06 = 0.30 \quad (30\%) \]
P3 · KMV Default Point Computation

A firm's balance sheet reports short-term debt of \(\$45\text{M}\), notes payable of \(\$15\text{M}\), and senior long-term bonds of \(\$180\text{M}\). Calculate the KMV default point \(D\).

Solution

Total short-term commitments: \(\text{Short-Term Debt} = 45 + 15 = \$60\text{M}\). Long-term debt: \(\text{Long-Term Debt} = \$180\text{M}\). According to the KMV standard default point formula:

\[ D = \text{Short-Term Debt} + 0.5 \times \text{Long-Term Debt} = 60 + 0.5 \times 180 = 60 + 90 = \$150\text{M} \]

P4 · Derivation of the Equity Volatility Equation

Prove using Itô's Lemma that in the Merton model, the instantaneous return volatility of equity satisfies \(\sigma_E = \frac{V}{E} \frac{\partial E}{\partial V} \sigma_V = \frac{V \Phi(d_1)}{E} \sigma_V\).

Solution

Equity is a function \(E(V_t, t)\) where \(dV_t = \mu_V V_t dt + \sigma_V V_t dW_t\). By Itô's Lemma:

\[ dE_t = \left( \frac{\partial E}{\partial t} + \mu_V V_t \frac{\partial E}{\partial V} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2 V_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial V^2} \right) dt + \sigma_V V_t \frac{\partial E}{\partial V} dW_t \]

Dividing by \(E_t\) to express the percentage return \(dE_t / E_t\), the diffusion coefficient (instantaneous return volatility) is:

\[ \sigma_E = \frac{1}{E_t} \sigma_V V_t \frac{\partial E}{\partial V} = \left(\frac{V_t}{E_t} \frac{\partial E}{\partial V}\right) \sigma_V \]

In the Black–Scholes–Merton call option formula, \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial V} = \Phi(d_1)\). Substituting yields \(\sigma_E = \frac{V \Phi(d_1)}{E} \sigma_V\), or equivalently \(\sigma_E E = \Phi(d_1) \sigma_V V\). \(\blacksquare\)

P5 · Monotonicity of Distance to Default with Leverage

Define leverage ratio \(L = D / V\). Prove that holding \(\sigma_V, \mu_V, T\) constant, Distance to Default is strictly decreasing in leverage \(L\).

Solution

Express Distance to Default in terms of \(L = D / V\):

\[ \text{DD} = \frac{-\ln(L) + (\mu_V - 0.5\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(L\):

\[ \frac{\partial \text{DD}}{\partial L} = \frac{-1/L}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} = -\frac{1}{L \sigma_V \sqrt{T}} \]

Since \(L > 0, \sigma_V > 0, T > 0\), \(\frac{\partial \text{DD}}{\partial L} < 0\) strictly everywhere. Higher financial leverage directly depresses Distance to Default and increases physical default probability.

P6 · Bounds on Naive Asset Volatility

Prove that for any positive weights \(w_E = \frac{E}{E+D}\) and \(w_D = \frac{D}{E+D}\) with \(\sigma_E > 0.05 / 0.75 \approx 0.0667\), the naive asset volatility \(\sigma_{V,\text{naive}}\) is strictly lower than equity volatility \(\sigma_E\).

Solution

By definition, \(\sigma_{V,\text{naive}} = w_E \sigma_E + w_D (0.05 + 0.25\sigma_E)\). Since \(w_E + w_D = 1\):

\[ \sigma_{V,\text{naive}} - \sigma_E = w_D (0.05 + 0.25\sigma_E - \sigma_E) = w_D (0.05 - 0.75\sigma_E) \]

For any firm with \(\sigma_E > \frac{0.05}{0.75} \approx 6.67\%\) (which holds for virtually all publicly traded equity), the quantity \(0.05 - 0.75\sigma_E < 0\). Since \(w_D > 0\), \(\sigma_{V,\text{naive}} < \sigma_E\), proving that debt buffering strictly depresses asset volatility relative to levered equity volatility.

Empirical

P7 · Physical Drift Estimation via Asset CAPM

A firm's equity beta is \(\beta_E = 1.30\), market equity is \(E = \$300\text{M}\), debt value is \(D = \$200\text{M}\), risk-free rate is \(r = 3.5\%\), and market risk premium is \(\mathbb{E}[R_m] - r = 5.5\%\). Assuming zero debt beta, estimate the physical expected asset return \(\mu_V\).

Solution

Total firm asset value is \(V = E + D = 300 + 200 = \$500\text{M}\). The asset beta unlevers according to \(\beta_V = \frac{E}{V}\beta_E + \frac{D}{V}\beta_D = \frac{300}{500} \times 1.30 + 0 = 0.60 \times 1.30 = 0.78\). The physical expected return on assets under the CAPM is:

\[ \mu_V = r + \beta_V (\mathbb{E}[R_m] - r) = 0.035 + 0.78 \times 0.055 = 0.035 + 0.0429 = 0.0779 \quad (7.79\%) \]
P8 · Bharath–Shumway Hazard Regression Interpretation

In a Cox proportional hazards model estimating 1-year corporate default probabilities, the coefficient on \(\text{DD}_{\text{naive}}\) is \(\beta = -0.85\) (\(t\)-stat \(-12.4\)). If a firm's naive Distance to Default drops from 4.0 to 2.0 standard deviations during an industry downturn, calculate the hazard ratio of default.

Solution

In a proportional hazards model, the hazard ratio is \(\text{HR} = \exp(\beta \times \Delta \text{DD})\). Here \(\Delta \text{DD} = 2.0 - 4.0 = -2.0\). Thus:

\[ \text{HR} = \exp(-0.85 \times (-2.0)) = \exp(1.70) \approx 5.474 \]

The firm's instantaneous default risk increases by a factor of approximately \(5.47\) (a \(447\%\) increase).

P9 · Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Comparison

Explain how the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) is used to evaluate the discriminatory power of Merton DD versus Bharath–Shumway naive DD across a multi-year panel of defaulted and non-defaulted firms.

Solution

The ROC curve plots the true positive rate (defaults correctly classified) against the false positive rate (non-defaults misclassified as defaults) across all possible score thresholds. An AUC of 0.5 represents a coin flip, while 1.0 represents perfect sorting.

In empirical studies (e.g., Bharath & Shumway 2008), both Merton DD and naive DD achieve AUCs of approximately 0.88 to 0.91 for 1-year bankruptcy forecasting. Testing the statistical difference \(\Delta \text{AUC}\) via DeLong tests shows no statistically significant outperformance by Merton DD, proving that simple naive construction captures virtually all cross-sectional default sorting power.

Research

P10 · Testing the Sufficiency of Merton DD

Formulate the statistical hypothesis test used by Bharath and Shumway (2008) to test whether the Merton model is a sufficient statistic for corporate bankruptcy.

Solution

Under the null hypothesis that the Merton structural model is a sufficient statistic, the true default probability is fully summarized by \(\text{DD}_{\text{Merton}}\). In an expanded hazard model:

\[ \lambda_i(t) = \lambda_0(t) \exp\left( \gamma_1 \text{DD}_{i,t}^{\text{Merton}} + \gamma_2 \text{DD}_{i,t}^{\text{naive}} + \boldsymbol{\theta}' \mathbf{X}_{i,t} \right) \]

where \(\mathbf{X}_{i,t}\) includes accounting leverage, return on assets, equity volatility, and past equity returns, sufficiency requires \(H_0: \gamma_2 = 0 \text{ and } \boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbf{0}\). Empirically, Bharath and Shumway rejected \(H_0\) with \(p < 0.0001\): \(\gamma_1\) becomes statistically indistinguishable from zero while naive DD and reduced-form accounting ratios retain strong explanatory power.

P11 · Campbell, Hilscher & Szilagyi (2008) Multi-Period Logit Model

How did John Campbell, Jens Hilscher, and Jan Szilagyi (2008) demonstrate that distressed firms deliver anomalously low subsequent stock returns, contradicting classical risk premia theory?

Solution

Campbell, Hilscher, and Szilagyi constructed a multi-period dynamic logit model using Distance to Default, equity returns, leverage, and accounting measures. Sorting equities into deciles by fitted failure probability, they found that the highest-distress decile underperformed safe stocks by over 8% per year annualized after controlling for Fama–French three factors and momentum.

This "distress puzzle" directly contradicts the Merton model intuition that high-distress equity acts like a high-beta leveraged call option requiring a high positive risk premium, suggesting behavioral overpricing and lottery preferences among distressed stock investors.

P12 · Jump Diffusion Distance to Default Extensions

How does incorporating compound Poisson jumps into the asset process \(V_t\) modify the short-horizon Distance to Default and resolve the flat default probability puzzle for investment-grade firms?

Solution

If \(dV_t / V_{t^-} = (\mu_V - \lambda_{\text{jump}} \bar{k})dt + \sigma_V dW_t + J_t dN_t\), where jumps arrive at Poisson rate \(\lambda_{\text{jump}}\) with negative jump size \(\ln(1 + J) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_J, \sigma_J^2)\), default can occur via a sudden discrete drop into the default zone.

As \(T \to 0\), the diffusion component's crossing probability vanishes as \(o(T)\), but the jump crossing probability converges to \(\lambda_{\text{jump}} \mathbb{P}(\ln(1 + J) < \ln(D / V_0)) T = O(T)\). This establishes a non-zero floor for short-term default probabilities for investment-grade issuers, preventing the Gaussian \(\Phi(-\text{DD})\) from collapsing to zero.

Knowledge check quiz

  1. What are the two observable inputs used by KMV to invert the unobservable firm asset value \(V\) and asset volatility \(\sigma_V\)?

    KMV observes market equity capitalization \(E\) and historical/implied equity volatility \(\sigma_E\) from secondary stock markets, using them to invert the Merton call option system.

  2. In the KMV implementation of the Merton model, how is the default point \(D\) conventionally defined?

    KMV empirical calibration demonstrated that firms default when assets breach short-term commitments plus roughly half of long-term debt liabilities.

  3. What drift parameter is used in the numerator of Distance to Default (\(\text{DD}\)) under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\)?

    Distance to Default is an objective default forecasting metric evaluated under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\), so assets drift at \(\mu_V\). Using \(r\) gives \(d_2\) under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\).

  4. What did Bharath and Shumway (2008) discover about the correlation between Merton KMV DD and their naive closed-form proxy \(\text{DD}_{\text{naive}}\)?

    Bharath and Shumway demonstrated that across thousands of firm-years, the correlation between iteratively solved Merton DD and closed-form naive DD is greater than 0.99.

  5. Why does Moody's KMV map Distance to Default to an empirical Expected Default Frequency (EDF) lookup table rather than computing \(\Phi(-\text{DD})\)?

    For an investment-grade firm with \(\text{DD} = 6.0\), \(\Phi(-6) \approx 10^{-9}\), whereas actual empirical default frequencies are around \(0.01\% - 0.05\%\) due to fat tails and unexpected financial shocks.

  6. What equation establishes the relationship between equity return volatility \(\sigma_E\) and asset volatility \(\sigma_V\) in the Merton system?

    Applying Itô's Lemma to the call option pricing formula yields \(\sigma_E = (V/E)\Phi(d_1)\sigma_V\), which rearranges to \(\sigma_E E = \Phi(d_1)\sigma_V V\).

  7. In the Bharath & Shumway (2008) naive model, how is debt volatility \(\sigma_{D,\text{naive}}\) specified?

    Bharath and Shumway calibrated \(\sigma_{D,\text{naive}} = 0.05 + 0.25 \sigma_E\) as an empirical linear proxy for debt return volatility based on historical bond index data.

  8. What did Campbell, Hilscher, and Szilagyi (2008) discover regarding the subsequent stock returns of firms with high default probabilities (low Distance to Default)?

    Contradicting structural model intuition that distressed equity acts like a leveraged call option with a huge risk premium, Campbell et al. found high-distress stocks severely underperformed safe equities.
