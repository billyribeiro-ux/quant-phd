The Merton (1974) Structural Model

Robert C. Merton (1974) revolutionized credit analysis by demonstrating that corporate liabilities are contingent claims on total firm asset value. By mapping corporate equity to a European call option and risky debt to a riskless bond minus a put option, the model establishes the foundational structural link between asset volatility, balance-sheet leverage, debt yields, and default probabilities.

1Firm Asset Dynamics and the Structural Framework

Consider a levered corporation whose total enterprise market value \(V_t\) evolves continuously in frictionless financial markets. The capital structure consists of two classes of securities: equity \(E_t\) and a single homogeneous class of zero-coupon debt with contractual face value \(D\) maturing at a fixed future date \(T\).

Definition 1.1 (Merton Structural Credit Model)

The Merton structural credit model specifies that total firm asset value \(V_t\) follows geometric Brownian motion under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\):

\[ dV_t = \mu V_t dt + \sigma_V V_t dW_t^\mathbb{P} \]

where \(\mu\) is the expected rate of return on firm assets, \(\sigma_V > 0\) is constant asset return volatility, and \(W_t^\mathbb{P}\) is a standard Brownian motion. Default occurs at maturity \(T\) if and only if total asset value is insufficient to cover promised face value:

\[ \text{Default event: } V_T < D \]

The firm cannot issue senior debt, makes no dividend or coupon payouts prior to \(T\), and debt covenants cannot force liquidation before \(T\).

Under the equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) defined via Girsanov's theorem with constant risk-free short rate \(r\), the discounted asset price \(e^{-r t} V_t\) is a martingale:

\[ dV_t = r V_t dt + \sigma_V V_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q} \]

where \(dW_t^\mathbb{Q} = dW_t^\mathbb{P} + \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma_V} dt\). Under Modigliani–Miller conditions, the market value of the firm is independent of its capital structure: \(V_t = E_t + B_t\) at all \(t \in [0, T]\).

2Equity as a Call Option on Firm Assets

At debt maturity \(T\), the contractual priorities of corporate law dictate terminal payoffs under limited liability:

  • If \(V_T \ge D\), the firm is fully solvent. Debt holders receive the promised face value \(D\), and equityholders receive the residual surplus \(V_T - D\).
  • If \(V_T < D\), the firm is bankrupt. Debt holders take ownership of all remaining firm assets \(V_T\), and equityholders walk away with zero under limited liability.
Theorem 1.1 (Merton Option Isomorphism)

In the Merton structural credit model, equity is an isomorphic European call option on firm assets with exercise price \(D\) and expiration \(T\):

\[ E_T = \max(V_T - D, 0) \]

At any time \(t < T\), the market value of equity equals the Black–Scholes call price on an underlying asset with price \(V_t\) and volatility \(\sigma_V\):

\[ E(V_t, t) = V_t \Phi(d_1) - D e^{-r(T - t)} \Phi(d_2) \]

where \(\Phi\) denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function and

\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(V_t / D) + \left(r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\right)(T - t)}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T - t}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma_V \sqrt{T - t} \]
Proof

By standard risk-neutral valuation under measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the price of any European claim with terminal payoff \(E_T\) is given by the discounted conditional expectation:

\[ E_t = e^{-r(T - t)} \mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{Q}\left[\max(V_T - D, 0)\right] \]

Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), asset dynamics follow \(d\ln V_s = \left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\right) ds + \sigma_V dW_s^\mathbb{Q}\). Integrating from \(t\) to \(T\):

\[ \ln V_T = \ln V_t + \left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\right)(T - t) + \sigma_V \sqrt{T - t} Z, \quad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \]

The payoff condition \(V_T \ge D\) is equivalent to \(Z \ge -d_2\). Evaluating the expectation across the survival region:

\[ E_t = e^{-r(T - t)} \int_{-d_2}^\infty \left(V_t e^{\left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\right)(T - t) + \sigma_V \sqrt{T - t} z} - D\right) \frac{e^{-z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} dz \]

Splitting into two integrals and completing the square for the first term yields \(V_t \Phi(d_1)\), while the second term gives \(D e^{-r(T - t)} \Phi(d_2)\), completing the proof.

3Risky Debt Valuation and Credit Spreads

Applying the fundamental balance-sheet identity \(B_t = V_t - E_t\), the market value of risky corporate debt decomposes into a default-free bond minus an embedded put option.

Theorem 1.2 (Merton Debt Valuation and Credit Spread)

The market value of defaultable zero-coupon debt \(B_t\) at time \(t < T\) is:

\[ B(V_t, t) = V_t - E(V_t, t) = D e^{-r(T - t)} \Phi(d_2) + V_t \Phi(-d_1) = D e^{-r(T - t)} - P(V_t, D, T - t) \]

where \(P(V_t, D, T - t) = D e^{-r(T - t)} \Phi(-d_2) - V_t \Phi(-d_1)\) is the European put option on firm assets. The continuously compounded yield to maturity \(y(t, T)\) and credit spread \(s(t, T)\) are:

\[ y(t, T) = -\frac{1}{T - t} \ln\left(\frac{B_t}{D}\right), \quad s(t, T) = y(t, T) - r = -\frac{1}{T - t} \ln\left(\frac{B_t}{D e^{-r(T - t)}}\right) \]

The risk-neutral default probability equals \(\mathrm{PD}^\mathbb{Q} = \Phi(-d_2)\).

Proof

From Theorem 1.1, \(B_t = V_t - E_t = V_t - [V_t \Phi(d_1) - D e^{-r(T - t)} \Phi(d_2)] = V_t(1 - \Phi(d_1)) + D e^{-r(T - t)} \Phi(d_2)\). Since \(1 - \Phi(d_1) = \Phi(-d_1)\) by symmetry of the Gaussian distribution, \(B_t = D e^{-r(T - t)} \Phi(d_2) + V_t \Phi(-d_1)\).

By standard put-call parity on the underlying firm assets, \(E_t + D e^{-r(T - t)} = V_t + P_t\). Rearranging gives \(B_t = V_t - E_t = D e^{-r(T - t)} - P_t\). Substituting \(B_t\) into the yield definition \(e^{-y(T - t)} = B_t / D\) and subtracting \(r\) yields the credit spread formula.

Example 1.1 (Pricing a Stylized Corporate Bond)

Consider a firm with total asset value \(V = \$100\), single zero-coupon debt maturing in \(T = 2\) years with face value \(D = \$70\), risk-free rate \(r = 4.0\%\), and asset volatility \(\sigma_V = 25\%\). Let us calculate the equity value, debt price, yield, and credit spread:

  1. Volatility scale: \(\sigma_V \sqrt{T} = 0.25 \times \sqrt{2} \approx 0.353553\).
  2. Log moneyness: \(\ln(V / D) = \ln(100 / 70) \approx 0.356675\).
  3. Option factors: \[ d_1 = \frac{0.356675 + (0.04 + 0.5 \times 0.25^2) \times 2}{0.353553} = \frac{0.356675 + 0.1425}{0.353553} \approx 1.41188 \] \[ d_2 = 1.41188 - 0.353553 \approx 1.05833 \]
  4. Normal probabilities: \(\Phi(d_1) \approx 0.92099\), \(\Phi(d_2) \approx 0.85505\).
  5. Equity value: \(E = 100 \times 0.92099 - 70 \times e^{-0.08} \times 0.85505 \approx 92.099 - 55.249 = \$36.85\).
  6. Debt value: \(B = 100 - 36.85 = \$63.15\).
  7. Debt yield: \(y = -\frac{1}{2} \ln(63.15 / 70) \approx 5.15\%\).
  8. Credit spread: \(s = 5.15\% - 4.00\% = 1.15\%\) (114.8 basis points).
  9. Risk-neutral default probability: \(\mathrm{PD}^\mathbb{Q} = \Phi(-1.05833) \approx 14.50\%\).

4Comparative Statics and Term Structures of Credit Spreads

The comparative statics of the Merton model shed deep light on debt-equity conflict and the shape of corporate credit curves:

  • Volatility effect (\(\partial E / \partial \sigma_V > 0\), \(\partial B / \partial \sigma_V < 0\)): Because equity is a call option, equityholders benefit from increased business risk. Higher volatility expands the upper tail while limited liability truncates downside risk at 0, transferring wealth directly from bondholders to shareholders (asset substitution / risk-shifting).
  • Leverage effect (\(\partial s / \partial (D/V) > 0\)): Increasing leverage increases the default probability \(\Phi(-d_2)\), strictly expanding the credit spread.
  • Term structure shapes: As proven by Merton (1974), the term structure of credit spreads is non-monotonic:
    • For safe, low-leverage firms (\(V \gg D\)), the spread curve starts at 0 at \(T=0\), rises to a moderate peak as diffusive uncertainty accumulates over time, and gradually levels off (hump-shaped).
    • For distressed firms (\(V < D\)), bankruptcy is imminent in the short term, producing an inverted, downward-sloping spread curve where short spreads reach distressed levels before decaying as time allows potential recovery.

Lab 1 · Merton pricing and spread term structures

Verifies balance-sheet articulation \(E + B = V\) across maturities, demonstrates that short spreads vanish to zero for solvent firms, and shows that distressed credit spread curves are strictly downward-sloping.

"""Lab CR1-L01-1: Merton (1974) structural credit model pricing and spread term structures.

Demonstrates closed-form valuation of equity as a call option on firm assets,
risky zero-coupon debt, credit spreads, and the term structure of credit spreads
for solvent versus distressed firms.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def merton_pricing(
    v: float,
    d: float,
    t: float,
    r: float,
    sigma: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate Merton model components."""
    vol_sqrt = sigma * math.sqrt(t)
    d1 = (math.log(v / d) + (r + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * t) / vol_sqrt
    d2 = d1 - vol_sqrt

    equity = v * norm_cdf(d1) - d * math.exp(-r * t) * norm_cdf(d2)
    debt = v - equity
    debt_yield = -math.log(debt / d) / t
    spread_bps = (debt_yield - r) * 10000.0
    pd = norm_cdf(-d2)

    return {
        "equity": equity,
        "debt": debt,
        "yield": debt_yield,
        "spread_bps": spread_bps,
        "default_prob": pd,
    }


def main() -> None:
    # Stylized solvent firm: V = 100, D = 70, r = 0.04, sigma = 0.25
    v_solvent = 100.0
    d_face = 70.0
    r_rf = 0.04
    sigma_v = 0.25

    maturities = [0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 10.0]

    print("Merton (1974) Credit Spread Term Structure (Solvent Firm: V=100, D=70):")
    print("  T (yr) | Equity ($) |  Debt ($)  | Yield (%) | Spread (bp) | Risk-Neutral PD")
    print("---------+------------+------------+-----------+-------------+----------------")

    spreads_solvent: list[float] = []
    for t in maturities:
        res = merton_pricing(v_solvent, d_face, t, r_rf, sigma_v)
        spreads_solvent.append(res["spread_bps"])
        # Invariant check: Equity + Debt == Asset Value
        assert abs(res["equity"] + res["debt"] - v_solvent) < 1e-11
        print(
            f"  {t:5.1f}  |   {res['equity']:6.2f}   |   {res['debt']:6.2f}   |   "
            f"{res['yield'] * 100:5.2f}%  |    {res['spread_bps']:6.1f}   |     {res['default_prob']:6.4f}"
        )

    # Distressed firm: V = 60, D = 70 (in distress, leverage D/V > 1)
    v_distressed = 60.0
    print("\nMerton (1974) Credit Spread Term Structure (Distressed Firm: V=60, D=70):")
    print("  T (yr) | Equity ($) |  Debt ($)  | Yield (%) | Spread (bp) | Risk-Neutral PD")
    print("---------+------------+------------+-----------+-------------+----------------")

    spreads_distressed: list[float] = []
    for t in maturities:
        res = merton_pricing(v_distressed, d_face, t, r_rf, sigma_v)
        spreads_distressed.append(res["spread_bps"])
        assert abs(res["equity"] + res["debt"] - v_distressed) < 1e-11
        print(
            f"  {t:5.1f}  |   {res['equity']:6.2f}   |   {res['debt']:6.2f}   |   "
            f"{res['yield'] * 100:5.2f}%  |    {res['spread_bps']:6.1f}   |     {res['default_prob']:6.4f}"
        )

    # Confirm key qualitative properties of Merton term structure
    # For solvent firm, short spread approaches zero as T -> 0
    short_spread = merton_pricing(v_solvent, d_face, 0.05, r_rf, sigma_v)["spread_bps"]
    print(f"\nSolvent firm short-maturity spread (T=0.05y): {short_spread:6.2f} bp (approaches 0)")
    assert short_spread < 1.0

    # For distressed firm, spread is monotonically downward sloping
    is_downward = all(spreads_distressed[i] >= spreads_distressed[i + 1] for i in range(len(spreads_distressed) - 1))
    print(f"Distressed firm term structure is monotonically downward sloping: {is_downward}")
    assert is_downward


main()

5Where the Canon Breaks: The Zero Short-Spread Puzzle

The central empirical defect of the Merton model is its prediction that credit spreads for solvent firms approach zero as maturity approaches zero:

\[ \lim_{T \to 0} s(t, T) = 0 \quad \text{for any firm with } V_t > D \]

Because Brownian motion sample paths are almost surely continuous, the probability that the diffusion path crosses the finite distance \(\ln(V_t / D) > 0\) in an infinitesimal interval \(dt\) decays at rate \(\exp(-c / dt) / \sqrt{dt}\), which vanishes faster than any polynomial in \(dt\). Consequently, the conditional default probability over short horizons is virtually zero.

In real corporate bond markets, even ultra-safe AAA/AA corporate issuers pay strictly positive credit spreads of 30 to 80 basis points on 1-month and 3-month commercial paper and bonds. This breakdown demonstrates that default risk in reality cannot be modeled purely as a smooth, predictable continuous diffusion with default only at maturity; real markets exhibit sudden jump risk, incomplete accounting visibility into assets, and early default covenants.

Misconceptions

  • Default can occur at any time prior to debt maturity in the Merton (1974) model if firm asset value dips below debt face value.

    In Merton's original 1974 specification, default can happen only at maturity \(T\). Even if firm assets plunge to 1% of debt face value at \(T/2\), the firm cannot be liquidated or reorganized early. Debt holders have no legal covenant to seize assets before maturity, meaning the firm continues operating and may recover before \(T\). Continuous default monitoring requires first-passage models such as Black & Cox (1976).

  • Higher asset volatility harms both bondholders and equityholders because the probability of bankruptcy increases.

    Higher asset volatility \(\sigma_V\) strictly increases equity value \(E\) while strictly decreasing debt value \(B\). Because equity is a convex call option, equityholders capture all upside gains while limited liability caps downside losses at zero. This structural conflict creates the classic agency problem of asset substitution (risk-shifting), where managers acting for shareholders have incentives to adopt excessively risky, negative-NPV projects.

  • The Merton model credit spread represents the physical expected annual default loss of the firm.

    The Merton credit spread \(s\) is computed under the equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), not the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\). The spread contains both the physical expected loss rate and a substantial credit risk premium generated by the market price of asset risk \(\frac{\mu - r}{\sigma_V}\). Risk-neutral default probabilities \(\Phi(-d_2)\) systematically exceed physical default probabilities whenever assets carry an equity risk premium (\(\mu > r\)).

P1 · Merton Equity and Debt Pricing

A firm has total asset value \(V = \$120\text{M}\), debt face value \(D = \$90\text{M}\) due in \(T = 1\) year, risk-free rate \(r = 5\%\), and asset volatility \(\sigma_V = 20\%\). Compute \(d_1\), \(d_2\), equity value \(E\), risky debt value \(B\), and the credit spread \(s\) in basis points.

Solution

Compute option factors: \(\ln(V/D) = \ln(120/90) \approx 0.287682\). Drift term: \((0.05 + 0.5 \times 0.20^2) \times 1 = 0.070\). Volatility scale: \(\sigma_V \sqrt{T} = 0.20\). Hence \(d_1 = (0.287682 + 0.070) / 0.20 = 1.7884\), and \(d_2 = 1.7884 - 0.20 = 1.5884\). Standard normal probabilities: \(\Phi(d_1) = 0.9631\), \(\Phi(d_2) = 0.9439\). Discount factor: \(e^{-0.05} \approx 0.951229\). Equity: \(E = 120 \times 0.9631 - 90 \times 0.951229 \times 0.9439 = 115.57 - 80.81 = \$34.76\text{M}\). Risky debt: \(B = V - E = 120 - 34.76 = \$85.24\text{M}\). Yield: \(y = -\ln(85.24 / 90) = 0.05432\) (5.43%). Credit spread: \(s = 5.43\% - 5.00\% = 0.432\%\) (43.2 basis points).

P2 · Risk-Neutral Default Probability

Using the parameters from Problem P1, calculate the risk-neutral default probability \(\mathrm{PD}^\mathbb{Q}\). If the physical expected rate of return on assets is \(\mu = 10\%\), calculate the physical default probability \(\mathrm{PD}^\mathbb{P}\) and compare.

Solution

The risk-neutral default probability is \(\mathrm{PD}^\mathbb{Q} = \Phi(-d_2) = \Phi(-1.5884) \approx 0.0561\) (5.61%). Under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\), asset value drifts at \(\mu = 10\%\). The physical distance parameter is \(d_2^\mathbb{P} = \frac{\ln(V/D) + (\mu - 0.5\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} = \frac{0.287682 + (0.10 - 0.02) \times 1}{0.20} = \frac{0.367682}{0.20} = 1.8384\). The physical default probability is \(\mathrm{PD}^\mathbb{P} = \Phi(-1.8384) \approx 0.0330\) (3.30%). The risk-neutral default probability is 70% higher than the physical default probability because asset cash flows are evaluated under a measure that discounts risk at the risk-free rate rather than allowing for the asset risk premium.

P3 · Put-Call Parity Decomposition

A firm's debt has face value \(D = \$100\), maturity \(T = 2\) years, and risk-free rate \(r = 4\%\). If the market price of risky debt is \(B = \$85.00\), determine the exact market price of the put option on the firm's assets.

Solution

From Theorem 1.2, risky debt satisfies \(B = D e^{-r T} - P\), where \(P\) is the value of the European put option on firm assets with strike \(D\) and maturity \(T\). The present value of default-free debt is \(D e^{-r T} = 100 \times e^{-0.04 \times 2} = 100 \times e^{-0.08} \approx \$92.31\). Substituting into the identity gives \(85.00 = 92.31 - P\), which implies \(P = 92.31 - 85.00 = \$7.31\). The put option represents the value of the limited liability default put granted to equityholders at the expense of debtholders.

P4 · Proof of the Zero Short-Spread Limit

Prove that for any solvent firm with initial asset value strictly exceeding debt face value (\(V_0 > D\)), the credit spread in the Merton model converges to zero as maturity \(T \to 0\): \(\lim_{T \to 0} s(0, T) = 0\).

Solution

Let \(k = \ln(V_0 / D) > 0\). As \(T \to 0\), the term \(d_2 = \frac{k + (r - 0.5\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} \approx \frac{k}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} \to +\infty\). Using Mill's ratio for the standard normal tail, \(\Phi(-d_2) = \int_{d_2}^\infty \phi(z) dz \sim \frac{\phi(d_2)}{d_2} = \frac{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}}{k \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{k^2}{2\sigma_V^2 T}\right)\). Similarly, \(\Phi(-d_1) \sim \frac{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}}{k \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{k^2}{2\sigma_V^2 T}\right)\). The put option value \(P = D e^{-r T} \Phi(-d_2) - V_0 \Phi(-d_1)\) decays as \(O(\sqrt{T} e^{-c/T})\) for \(c > 0\). The credit spread is \(s = -\frac{1}{T} \ln(1 - P / (D e^{-r T})) \approx \frac{P}{T D e^{-r T}} \sim \frac{O(\sqrt{T} e^{-c/T})}{T} \to 0\) exponentially fast as \(T \to 0\).

P5 · Monotonicity of Debt with Respect to Asset Volatility

Prove that \(\frac{\partial B}{\partial \sigma_V} < 0\) for any strictly positive maturity \(T > 0\) and positive asset volatility \(\sigma_V > 0\).

Solution

By balance sheet articulation, \(B = V - E\). Differentiating with respect to \(\sigma_V\) gives \(\frac{\partial B}{\partial \sigma_V} = -\frac{\partial E}{\partial \sigma_V}\). Since equity \(E\) is a European call option on asset value \(V\) with strike \(D\), its derivative with respect to volatility is the Black–Scholes vega: \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial \sigma_V} = V \sqrt{T} \phi(d_1)\), where \(\phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}\). For \(V > 0\), \(T > 0\), and finite \(d_1\), \(\phi(d_1) > 0\), which implies \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial \sigma_V} > 0\). Therefore, \(\frac{\partial B}{\partial \sigma_V} = -V \sqrt{T} \phi(d_1) < 0\). Risky debt value is strictly decreasing in asset volatility.

P6 · Debt Beta and Asset Beta Relationship

Let \(\beta_V\) denote the CAPM beta of total firm assets. Prove that the systematic risk of corporate debt satisfies \(\beta_B = \left(1 - \frac{E}{B}\left(\frac{V \Phi(d_1)}{E} - 1\right)\right) \beta_V = \left(\frac{V - V\Phi(d_1)}{B}\right) \beta_V\).

Solution

Under continuous trading and Itô's lemma, the instantaneous return on equity satisfies \(dE = \frac{\partial E}{\partial V} dV + \dots = \Phi(d_1) dV + \dots\). Expressed in percentage returns, \(\frac{dE}{E} = \frac{V \Phi(d_1)}{E} \frac{dV}{V} + \dots\), so equity beta is \(\beta_E = \frac{V \Phi(d_1)}{E} \beta_V\). By balance sheet articulation, total asset beta is the value-weighted average of debt and equity betas: \(\beta_V = \frac{E}{V} \beta_E + \frac{B}{V} \beta_B\). Substituting \(\beta_E\) yields \(\beta_V = \frac{E}{V} \left(\frac{V \Phi(d_1)}{E}\right) \beta_V + \frac{B}{V} \beta_B = \Phi(d_1) \beta_V + \frac{B}{V} \beta_B\). Rearranging gives \(\frac{B}{V} \beta_B = (1 - \Phi(d_1)) \beta_V\), which leads directly to \(\beta_B = \left(\frac{V(1 - \Phi(d_1))}{B}\right) \beta_V = \left(\frac{V \Phi(-d_1)}{B}\right) \beta_V\).

Empirical

P7 · Numerical Inversion for Distressed Yield Curve

A distressed company has asset value \(V = \$50\text{M}\), debt face value \(D = \$80\text{M}\), asset volatility \(\sigma_V = 30\%\), and risk-free rate \(r = 4\%\). Compute the credit spread at maturities \(T = 0.5\), \(1.0\), and \(5.0\) years, and verify that the spread curve is inverted.

Solution

For \(T = 0.5\): \(\sigma_V \sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.21213\), \(\ln(50/80) \approx -0.47000\), \(d_1 \approx \frac{-0.47000 + 0.0425}{0.21213} \approx -2.0152\), \(d_2 \approx -2.2274\). \(\Phi(d_1) \approx 0.0219\), \(\Phi(d_2) \approx 0.0129\). Equity: \(E \approx 50 \times 0.0219 - 80 e^{-0.02} \times 0.0129 \approx 1.10 - 1.01 = \$0.09\text{M}\). Debt \(B \approx 50 - 0.09 = \$49.91\text{M}\). Yield \(y = -\frac{1}{0.5} \ln(49.91 / 80) \approx 94.4\%\). Spread \(s \approx 90.4\%\) (\(9040\) bp). For \(T = 1.0\): \(s \approx 4760\) bp. For \(T = 5.0\): \(s \approx 950\) bp. The spread decreases monotonically from 9040 bp to 950 bp, confirming the inverted credit spread term structure characteristic of distressed firms.

P8 · Asset Volatility Estimation from Equity Volatility

An analyst observes a stock price \(S = \$40\) with 10M shares outstanding (\(E = \$400\text{M}\)), annualized equity volatility \(\sigma_E = 45\%\), total debt face value \(D = \$600\text{M}\), maturity \(T = 1\) year, and \(r = 3\%\). In the Merton framework, estimate whether asset volatility \(\sigma_V\) is greater than, equal to, or less than \(45\%\), and explain why.

Solution

In the Merton model, equity volatility satisfies \(\sigma_E = \frac{V \Phi(d_1)}{E} \sigma_V\). Because the firm has substantial debt (\(D = \$600\text{M}\)), total asset value \(V = E + B > E\). Specifically, \(V \approx \$400\text{M} + \$550\text{M} = \$950\text{M}\). The leverage multiplier \(\frac{V \Phi(d_1)}{E} \approx \frac{950 \times 0.90}{400} \approx 2.14 > 1\). Therefore, \(\sigma_V = \frac{\sigma_E}{(V \Phi(d_1) / E)} \approx \frac{45\%}{2.14} \approx 21.0\%\). Asset volatility must be strictly less than equity volatility because financial leverage mechanically amplifies underlying asset volatility into equity return volatility.

P9 · Calibration of Hump-Shaped Spread Peak

Using the solvent firm parameters from Example 1.1 (\(V = 100, D = 70, r = 0.04, \sigma_V = 0.25\)), evaluate the credit spread at \(T = 2\), \(3\), \(5\), and \(7\) years to identify the maturity of maximum credit spread.

Solution

From Lab 1: at \(T = 2.0\), spread is \(114.8\) bp; at \(T = 3.0\), spread is \(125.9\) bp; at \(T = 5.0\), spread is \(124.8\) bp; at \(T = 7.0\), spread is \(116.9\) bp. The credit spread curve achieves its maximum around \(T^* \approx 3.0\) years at \(125.9\) basis points. This confirms the theoretical hump shape: at short horizons, low cumulative diffusive variance keeps default probability negligible, while at long horizons, the drift of asset value under positive risk premia and discounting mitigates default risk, causing intermediate maturities to bear the highest credit spread.

P10 · Incorporating Continuous Payouts

Suppose the firm pays a continuous dividend / coupon payout yield \(\delta > 0\) to claimholders, such that \(dV_t = (r - \delta) V_t dt + \sigma_V V_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q}\). Derive the modified formulas for \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) and explain the impact on credit spreads.

Solution

When the firm pays a continuous dividend rate \(\delta\), the risk-neutral drift of assets is \(r - \delta\). The forward asset value at maturity is \(\mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{Q}[V_T] = V_t e^{(r - \delta)(T - t)}\). Applying the Black–Scholes dividend-adjusted call formula: \(E_t = V_t e^{-\delta(T - t)} \Phi(d_1) - D e^{-r(T - t)} \Phi(d_2)\), where \(d_1 = \frac{\ln(V_t / D) + (r - \delta + 0.5\sigma_V^2)(T - t)}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T - t}}\) and \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma_V \sqrt{T - t}\). Because \(\delta > 0\) drains assets from the firm over time, the risk-neutral asset value at maturity is lower, increasing the default probability \(\Phi(-d_2)\) and widening the credit spread for all maturities.

P11 · Endogenous Liquidation vs Terminal Default

Critically assess why institutional bondholders negotiate debt covenants (e.g. debt-to-equity ceilings, minimum net worth thresholds) in light of the Merton model's terminal default assumption.

Solution

In the Merton model, equityholders retain exclusive control until maturity \(T\). If the firm experiences distress at \(T/2\), equityholders have every incentive to pursue risk-shifting strategies or asset stripping, knowing that debtholders bear almost all downside losses while equity retains upside optionality. Debt covenants introduce an absorbing barrier that grants debtholders the legal right to accelerate debt repayment, block dividends, or force corporate reorganization the moment firm health deteriorates, protecting recovery values before assets are depleted.

P12 · Jump Diffusion and Short-Term Spreads

Propose a mathematical modification to Merton's asset diffusion process that resolves the zero short-spread puzzle, and explain why it generates non-zero spreads as \(T \to 0\).

Solution

Following Merton (1976), introduce compound Poisson jumps into the asset process: \(dV_t / V_{t-} = (r - \lambda k) dt + \sigma_V dW_t^\mathbb{Q} + (Y - 1) dN_t\), where \(N_t\) is a Poisson process with arrival intensity \(\lambda\) and jump size distribution \(Y > 0\). Over an infinitesimal interval \(dt\), there is a strictly positive probability \(\lambda dt > 0\) that a catastrophic negative jump (\(Y < D/V\)) instantly moves asset value below \(D\). The short-term credit spread satisfies \(\lim_{T \to 0} s(0, T) = \lambda \mathbb{E}[1 - Y \mid Y < D/V] > 0\), resolving the zero short-spread puzzle.

Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Merton (1974) model, what is the economic interpretation of the strike price in the equity call option?

    Options
    Equityholders pay debt face value \(D\) at maturity \(T\) to buy out debtholders and retain full ownership of assets \(V_T\); hence \(D\) acts as the exercise price.

  2. What happens to the market value of risky debt \(B\) if firm asset volatility \(\sigma_V\) increases, holding asset value \(V\), debt face \(D\), and maturity \(T\) constant?

    Options
    Since \(B = D e^{-r T} - P\) and option vega is strictly positive (\(\partial P / \partial \sigma_V > 0\)), higher asset volatility increases the put option held by equityholders, strictly lowering risky debt value.

  3. Why does the Merton model predict credit spreads of zero as maturity \(T \to 0\) for solvent firms?

    Options
    Continuity of Brownian motion sample paths guarantees that the probability of a solvent firm diffusing from \(V_0\) to \(D\) in an infinitesimal interval \(dt\) decays as \(e^{-c/dt}\), causing spreads to vanish.

  4. In the Merton formula \(E = V \Phi(d_1) - D e^{-r T} \Phi(d_2)\), what is the term \(\Phi(d_2)\)?

    Options
    \(\Phi(d_2)\) is the probability under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) that \(V_T \ge D\) (the option expires in the money). Its complement \(\Phi(-d_2) = 1 - \Phi(d_2)\) is the risk-neutral default probability.

  5. How does the credit spread curve behave as a function of maturity for a severely distressed firm (\(V_0 < D\))?

    Options
    When \(V_0 < D\), default is almost certain in the immediate future, resulting in astronomical short spreads. Over longer horizons, there is some probability that assets recover, lowering the annualized yield spread.

  6. What constitutes the asset substitution (risk-shifting) problem in structural corporate finance?

    Options
    Because equity is a call option on assets, its value increases with asset volatility (\(\partial E / \partial \sigma_V > 0\)), incentivizing equityholders to increase firm risk even if projects have negative net present value.

  7. Under the Merton model, what is the delta of equity with respect to firm asset value?

    Options
    By standard Black-Scholes calculus, the delta of a European call option with respect to the underlying asset is \(\Phi(d_1)\).

  8. Which of the following is an explicit assumption of the original Merton (1974) structural model?

    Options
    Merton (1974) explicitly assumes a single zero-coupon debt issue maturing at \(T\) with no intermediate payouts or senior debt issuance.

  9. In Example 1.1 with \(V = 100\), \(D = 70\), \(T = 2\), \(r = 4\%\), and \(\sigma_V = 25\%\), what is the credit spread in basis points (to one decimal place)?

    From Example 1.1, the debt price is \(B = \$63.15\), yield is \(y = -\frac{1}{2}\ln(63.15/70) = 0.05148\) (5.148%), and credit spread is \(s = 5.148\% - 4.00\% = 1.148\%\) or 114.8 basis points.

  10. What is the theoretical limiting value (in basis points) of the Merton credit spread for a solvent firm with \(V_0 = 100\) and \(D = 70\) as maturity \(T \to 0\)?

    As proven in Theorem 1.2 and Problem P4, the continuous diffusion of assets prevents immediate default for \(V_0 > D\), so the credit spread vanishes to exactly 0 basis points as \(T \to 0\).
