First-Passage Time Credit Models: Black–Cox (1976)

Real-world corporate bonds contain bond indenture covenants that empower creditors to accelerate debt or force reorganization the moment a borrower's financial health deteriorates. Fischer Black and John C. Cox (1976) introduced first-passage time models where default occurs at the first time asset value hits an absorbing covenant barrier, resolving the Merton model's zero short-spread puzzle and capturing realistic covenant protection.

1Safety Covenants and the First-Passage Framework

In the Merton structural credit model, debtholders are entirely passive spectators until maturity \(T\). In commercial practice, bond indentures feature protective covenants—such as maintenance of minimum net worth, working capital ratios, or debt-to-asset ceilings—that trigger immediate technical default and corporate reorganization if violated.

Definition 2.1 (Black–Cox First-Passage Credit Model)

The Black–Cox first-passage model specifies that firm asset value \(V_t\) evolves as geometric Brownian motion under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\):

\[ dV_t = (r - \delta) V_t dt + \sigma_V V_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q} \]

Default occurs at the random first-passage time \(\tau\) when firm assets touch an exogenous, time-dependent safety covenant barrier \(C(t)\):

\[ \tau = \inf\left\{t > 0 : V_t \le C(t)\right\} \]

where \(C(t) = K e^{-\gamma(T - t)}\) with \(K \le D\) and \(\gamma \ge 0\). If \(\tau \le T\), debtholders receive an early recovery payoff at time \(\tau\). If the barrier is never touched (\(\tau > T\)), the bond matures at \(T\) with payoff \(\min(V_T, D)\).

2First-Passage Probability Distribution

To value debt under continuous covenant monitoring, we evaluate the distribution of the first hitting time of geometric Brownian motion to a lower absorbing boundary.

Theorem 2.1 (First-Passage Time Distribution of GBM)

Let \(V_t = V_0 \exp\left(\left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\right)t + \sigma_V W_t^\mathbb{Q}\right)\) with \(V_0 > K\), and define \(\tau = \inf\{t > 0 : V_t \le K\}\). Under constant barrier \(K\), let \(b = \ln(K / V_0) < 0\) and drift \(\mu = r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\). The cumulative probability of default prior to horizon \(T\) is:

\[ \mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T) = \Phi\left(\frac{b - \mu T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}}\right) + \exp\left(\frac{2\mu b}{\sigma_V^2}\right) \Phi\left(\frac{b + \mu T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}}\right) \]

where \(\exp\left(\frac{2\mu b}{\sigma_V^2}\right) = \left(\frac{K}{V_0}\right)^{\frac{2r}{\sigma_V^2} - 1}\). The survival probability to maturity is \(q(T) = 1 - \mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T)\).

Proof

Define the log-coordinate process \(X_t = \ln(V_t / V_0) = \mu t + \sigma_V W_t^\mathbb{Q}\). The first-passage time to \(V_t \le K\) is identical to the first-passage time of arithmetic Brownian motion \(X_t\) to the negative barrier \(b = \ln(K / V_0) < 0\): \(\tau = \inf\{t > 0 : X_t \le b\}\).

By the Cameron–Martin–Girsanov change of measure, define an auxiliary measure \(\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}\) under which \(X_t\) is driftless: \(\frac{d\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}}{d\mathbb{Q}}\Big|_{\mathcal{F}_t} = \exp\left(-\frac{\mu}{\sigma_V} W_t^\mathbb{Q} - \frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma_V^2}t\right) = \exp\left(-\frac{\mu}{\sigma_V^2} X_t + \frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma_V^2}t\right)\). By the reflection principle for driftless Brownian motion, the joint density of \(\tau\) and \(X_T\) under \(\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}\) can be integrated across the absorbing half-line. Transforming back to \(\mathbb{Q}\) using the Radon–Nikodym derivative evaluated at stopping time \(\tau\) (where \(X_\tau = b\)) yields the density:

\[ f_\tau(t) = \frac{|b|}{\sigma_V \sqrt{2\pi t^3}} \exp\left(-\frac{(b - \mu t)^2}{2\sigma_V^2 t}\right) \]

Integrating \(f_\tau(t)\) from \(0\) to \(T\) gives the two-term Gaussian CDF expression stated in the theorem.

3Closed-Form Valuation of Black–Cox Corporate Debt

When the covenant is violated at \(\tau \le T\), bankruptcy is declared immediately. In the Black–Cox specification, debtholders receive a contractual recovery fraction \(R \in [0, 1]\) of the debt face value \(D\), paid at stopping time \(\tau\).

Theorem 2.2 (Black–Cox Debt Pricing Formula)

Under a flat covenant barrier \(K \le D\) with fractional recovery \(R\) of face value \(D\) paid upon early default at \(\tau\), the market value of corporate debt \(B(V_0, 0)\) at time 0 is:

\[ B(V_0, 0) = D e^{-r T} q(T) + R D \cdot g(T) \]

where \(q(T) = 1 - \mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T)\) is the survival probability from Theorem 2.1, and \(g(T) = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[e^{-r \tau} \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau \le T\}}\right]\) is the expected stochastic discount factor evaluated at first passage:

\[ g(T) = \left(\frac{K}{V_0}\right)^{\frac{2r}{\sigma_V^2}} \Phi\left(\frac{b + \lambda T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}}\right) + \left(\frac{V_0}{K}\right) \Phi\left(\frac{b - \lambda T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}}\right) \]

with \(\lambda = r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\) and \(b = \ln(K / V_0)\). The market value of equity satisfies \(E(V_0, 0) = V_0 - B(V_0, 0)\), and the credit spread is:

\[ s(0, T) = -\frac{1}{T} \ln\left(\frac{B(V_0, 0)}{D e^{-r T}}\right) \]
Proof

By no-arbitrage, debt value is the discounted risk-neutral expectation of promised cash flows:

\[ B(V_0, 0) = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[e^{-r T} D \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau > T\}}\right] + \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[e^{-r \tau} R D \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau \le T\}}\right] \]

The first term simplifies directly to \(D e^{-r T} \mathbb{Q}(\tau > T) = D e^{-r T} q(T)\). For the second term, write \(g(T) = \int_0^T e^{-r t} f_\tau(t) dt\). Substituting the hitting density from Theorem 2.1:

\[ g(T) = \int_0^T e^{-r t} \frac{|b|}{\sigma_V \sqrt{2\pi t^3}} \exp\left(-\frac{(b - \mu t)^2}{2\sigma_V^2 t}\right) dt \]

Expanding the quadratic exponent: \(-\frac{(b - \mu t)^2}{2\sigma_V^2 t} - r t = -\frac{b^2 - 2 b \mu t + (\mu^2 + 2 r \sigma_V^2) t^2}{2\sigma_V^2 t}\). Let \(\lambda = \sqrt{\mu^2 + 2 r \sigma_V^2} = \sqrt{(r - 0.5\sigma_V^2)^2 + 2 r \sigma_V^2} = r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\). Completing the square with \(\lambda\) gives:

\[ e^{-r t} f_\tau(t) = e^{\frac{b(\mu + \lambda)}{\sigma_V^2}} f_\tau(t; b, -\lambda) \]

Since \(\frac{b(\mu + \lambda)}{\sigma_V^2} = \frac{2 r b}{\sigma_V^2} = \ln((K/V_0)^{2r/\sigma_V^2})\), integrating the density \(f_\tau(t; b, -\lambda)\) from \(0\) to \(T\) yields the exact two-term expression for \(g(T)\).

4Resolving the Zero Short-Spread Puzzle

The crucial economic advance of the Black–Cox model over Merton (1974) is that credit spreads do not collapse to zero at short maturities when a firm operates in the vicinity of its covenant barrier.

If firm assets are near the barrier \(K\) (\(V_0 \to K^+\)), the distance \(|b| = |\ln(K / V_0)| \to 0\). From Theorem 2.1, the probability of default over any horizon \(T > 0\) satisfies:

\[ \lim_{V_0 \to K^+} \mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T) = \Phi(0) + \Phi(0) = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0 \]

Consequently, for a firm close to covenant breach, default is an imminent possibility over the next few weeks. The short-term credit spread remains bounded away from zero, reflecting real-world commercial paper spreads for distressed issuers.

Lab 1 · Black-Cox vs Merton spread term structures

Compares credit spreads between Black-Cox (with barrier K=70) and Merton models across maturities, demonstrating strictly higher spreads under continuous monitoring and non-zero short-term spreads for near-barrier firms.

"""Lab CR1-L02-1: Black-Cox (1976) first-passage credit model and short-term credit spreads.

Demonstrates first-passage hitting time distributions, safety covenants,
and the resolution of Merton's zero short-spread anomaly via early default barriers.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def black_cox_components(
    v: float,
    d: float,
    t: float,
    r: float,
    sigma: float,
    k: float,
    rec: float = 0.4,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate Black-Cox first passage debt, equity, and credit spread."""
    b = math.log(k / v)
    vol_sqrt = sigma * math.sqrt(t)
    mu = r - 0.5 * sigma * sigma
    lam = r + 0.5 * sigma * sigma

    # Default probability by time T
    d_minus = (b - mu * t) / vol_sqrt
    d_plus = (b + mu * t) / vol_sqrt
    prob_default = norm_cdf(d_minus) + math.exp(2.0 * mu * b / (sigma * sigma)) * norm_cdf(d_plus)
    prob_default = min(1.0, max(0.0, prob_default))
    survival_prob = 1.0 - prob_default

    # Expected discounted hitting factor
    g1 = math.exp(2.0 * r * b / (sigma * sigma)) * norm_cdf((b + lam * t) / vol_sqrt)
    g2 = math.exp(-b) * norm_cdf((b - lam * t) / vol_sqrt)
    exp_disc_hit = g1 + g2

    debt = d * math.exp(-r * t) * survival_prob + rec * d * exp_disc_hit
    equity = v - debt
    debt_yield = -math.log(debt / d) / t
    spread_bps = (debt_yield - r) * 10000.0

    return {
        "debt": debt,
        "equity": equity,
        "spread_bps": spread_bps,
        "default_prob": prob_default,
        "survival_prob": survival_prob,
    }


def merton_spread_bps(v: float, d: float, t: float, r: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    """Compute Merton credit spread in basis points."""
    vol_sqrt = sigma * math.sqrt(t)
    d1 = (math.log(v / d) + (r + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * t) / vol_sqrt
    d2 = d1 - vol_sqrt
    equity = v * norm_cdf(d1) - d * math.exp(-r * t) * norm_cdf(d2)
    debt = v - equity
    return float((-math.log(debt / d) / t - r) * 10000.0)


def main() -> None:
    # Moderately levered firm: V = 100, D = 80, K = 70, r = 0.04, sigma = 0.25, R = 0.40
    v = 100.0
    d = 80.0
    k_barrier = 70.0
    r = 0.04
    sigma = 0.25
    rec = 0.40

    maturities = [0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0]

    print("Black-Cox (1976) vs Merton (1974) Credit Spread Term Structure (V=100, D=80, K=70):")
    print("  T (yr) | Merton (bp) | Black-Cox (bp) | Spread Diff (bp) | First-Passage PD")
    print("---------+-------------+----------------+------------------+-----------------")

    for t in maturities:
        bc = black_cox_components(v, d, t, r, sigma, k_barrier, rec)
        m_spread = merton_spread_bps(v, d, t, r, sigma)
        diff = bc["spread_bps"] - m_spread
        assert abs(bc["debt"] + bc["equity"] - v) < 1e-11
        print(
            f"  {t:5.2f}  |   {m_spread:8.1f}  |    {bc['spread_bps']:9.1f}   |     "
            f"{diff:9.1f}    |     {bc['default_prob']:8.4f}"
        )

    # Resolution of Merton zero short-spread puzzle:
    # When a firm is near the safety covenant barrier (e.g. V=75, K=70),
    # Black-Cox produces massive short-term spreads due to first-passage probability,
    # whereas Merton allows the firm to survive until maturity.
    v_near = 75.0
    t_short = 0.10
    m_short = merton_spread_bps(v_near, d, t_short, r, sigma)
    bc_short = black_cox_components(v_near, d, t_short, r, sigma, k_barrier, rec)["spread_bps"]
    print(
        f"\nNear-barrier firm (V=75, K=70) at T=0.10y: "
        f"Merton spread = {m_short:6.1f} bp vs Black-Cox = {bc_short:6.1f} bp"
    )

    # Black-Cox spread is strictly higher than Merton because covenant triggers early reorganization
    assert bc_short > m_short

5Where the Canon Breaks: Exogenous Barriers and Predictable Default

Despite its mathematical elegance, the Black–Cox model suffers from two major limitations in financial practice:

  1. Exogenous covenant specification: The barrier \(C(t)\) is imposed exogenously as an arbitrary legal threshold. In reality, management and equityholders do not default passively at an arbitrary barrier; they strategically choose when to surrender the firm based on tax advantages, future volatility option value, and cash flow obligations. This motivates endogenous default models (Leland 1994).
  2. Predictable stopping times: Because Brownian sample paths are continuous, the first hitting time \(\tau\) is a predictable stopping time. As asset value \(V_t\) approaches \(K\), market participants observe the decline with complete certainty just prior to default, causing the conditional short spread to behave smoothly. In reality, defaults often arrive as sudden credit events (fraud, sudden liquidity run, legal judgment) with zero advance warning, which requires reduced-form intensity models.

Misconceptions

  • Black–Cox credit spreads are always higher than Merton credit spreads for every possible firm and maturity.

    While Black–Cox spreads are typically higher for moderate maturities because default can occur early, the relationship depends on covenant recovery and maturity. If recovery upon early covenant default \(R K\) is high, early reorganization prevents the firm from hemorrhaging further cash, resulting in higher recovery for debtholders than a protracted terminal bankruptcy where assets might decline close to zero.

  • The covenant barrier in first-passage models must remain fixed at a flat, constant dollar amount.

    Black & Cox (1976) explicitly formulated time-dependent exponential barriers \(C(t) = K e^{-\gamma(T - t)}\). By choosing \(\gamma > 0\), the covenant barrier rises over time to model growing debt capacity or retained earnings covenants; by choosing \(\gamma < 0\), it models amortizing sinking fund provisions.

  • Because default occurs the instant V touches K, debtholders always recover exactly 100% of their investment.

    Even though default is triggered at \(V = K\), the barrier \(K\) is almost always set strictly below debt face value \(D\) (\(K < D\)) to give management operating breathing room. Furthermore, legal reorganization costs and liquidation discounts ensure that debtholders receive a fraction \(R < 1\) of face value upon default.

P1 · First-Passage Default Probability

A company has asset value \(V_0 = \$100\text{M}\), asset volatility \(\sigma_V = 25\%\), and risk-free rate \(r = 4\%\). A safety covenant triggers reorganization if assets hit \(K = \$75\text{M}\). Compute the first-passage default probability over a \(T = 1\)-year horizon.

Solution

Compute parameters: \(b = \ln(K / V_0) = \ln(75 / 100) = -0.287682\). Drift: \(\mu = r - 0.5\sigma_V^2 = 0.04 - 0.5 \times 0.25^2 = 0.04 - 0.03125 = 0.00875\). Volatility scale: \(\sigma_V \sqrt{T} = 0.25 \times 1 = 0.25\). First argument: \(\frac{b - \mu T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} = \frac{-0.287682 - 0.00875}{0.25} = \frac{-0.296432}{0.25} = -1.18573\). Normal CDF: \(\Phi(-1.18573) \approx 0.11787\). Second argument: \(\frac{b + \mu T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} = \frac{-0.287682 + 0.00875}{0.25} = \frac{-0.278932}{0.25} = -1.11573\). Normal CDF: \(\Phi(-1.11573) \approx 0.13227\). Exponential prefactor: \(\frac{2\mu b}{\sigma_V^2} = \frac{2 \times 0.00875 \times (-0.287682)}{0.0625} = \frac{-0.005034}{0.0625} \approx -0.08055\). Factor: \(e^{-0.08055} \approx 0.92261\). Sum: \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau \le 1) = 0.11787 + 0.92261 \times 0.13227 = 0.11787 + 0.12203 = 0.2399\) (23.99%).

P2 · Expected Discounted Recovery Calculation

Using the parameters from Problem P1 with \(r = 0.04\) and \(\sigma_V = 0.25\), compute the stochastic discount factor \(g(1) = \mathbb{E}[e^{-r \tau} \mathbf{1}_{\tau \le 1}]\).

Solution

Compute \(\lambda = r + 0.5\sigma_V^2 = 0.04 + 0.03125 = 0.07125\). Prefactor 1: \((K / V_0)^{2r/\sigma_V^2} = (0.75)^{2 \times 0.04 / 0.0625} = (0.75)^{1.28} \approx 0.6920\). Term 1 argument: \(\frac{b + \lambda T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} = \frac{-0.287682 + 0.07125}{0.25} = \frac{-0.216432}{0.25} = -0.86573\). \(\Phi(-0.86573) \approx 0.19332\). Prefactor 2: \(V_0 / K = 100 / 75 = 1.33333\). Term 2 argument: \(\frac{b - \lambda T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} = \frac{-0.287682 - 0.07125}{0.25} = \frac{-0.358932}{0.25} = -1.43573\). \(\Phi(-1.43573) \approx 0.07554\). Hence \(g(1) = 0.6920 \times 0.19332 + 1.33333 \times 0.07554 = 0.13378 + 0.10072 = 0.23450\). Note that \(g(1) = 0.2345 < 0.2399 = \mathbb{Q}(\tau \le 1)\), confirming that early default recovery is strictly discounted at the risk-free rate.

P3 · Black–Cox Bond Price and Credit Spread

Given debt face value \(D = \$80\text{M}\), maturity \(T = 1\) year, risk-free rate \(r = 4\%\), recovery rate \(R = 40\%\), survival probability \(q(1) = 0.7601\), and discounted recovery factor \(g(1) = 0.2345\), calculate the Black–Cox debt value \(B\) and credit spread \(s\) in basis points.

Solution

Promised face discounted conditional on survival: \(D e^{-r T} q(T) = 80 \times e^{-0.04} \times 0.7601 = 80 \times 0.960789 \times 0.7601 \approx \$58.424\text{M}\). Yield: \(y = -\ln(65.928 / 80) / 1 = -\ln(0.8241) = 0.19346\) (19.35%). Credit spread: \(s = 19.35\% - 4.00\% = 15.35\%\) (\(1535\) basis points).

P4 · Boundary Limit When Assets Touch Covenant

Prove that \(\lim_{V_0 \to K^+} \mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T) = 1\) for any strictly positive maturity \(T > 0\).

Solution

Let \(b = \ln(K / V_0)\). As \(V_0 \to K^+\), \(K / V_0 \to 1^-\), which implies \(b \to 0^-\). Taking the limit of each term in Theorem 2.1: \(\lim_{b \to 0^-} \frac{b - \mu T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} = \frac{-\mu T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} = -\frac{\mu \sqrt{T}}{\sigma_V}\). The second term has prefactor \(\lim_{b \to 0^-} e^{2\mu b / \sigma_V^2} = e^0 = 1\), and argument \(\lim_{b \to 0^-} \frac{b + \mu T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}} = \frac{\mu \sqrt{T}}{\sigma_V}\). Therefore, \(\lim_{V_0 \to K^+} \mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T) = \Phi\left(-\frac{\mu \sqrt{T}}{\sigma_V}\right) + 1 \times \Phi\left(\frac{\mu \sqrt{T}}{\sigma_V}\right)\). By symmetry of the standard normal distribution, \(\Phi(-z) + \Phi(z) = 1\) for all \(z \in \mathbb{R}\). Hence the limit equals identically 1. Default occurs almost surely over any positive horizon if the initial asset value sits on the covenant barrier.

P5 · Laplace Transform of First Hitting Time

Prove that for arithmetic Brownian motion \(X_t = \mu t + \sigma W_t\) with \(\tau = \inf\{t > 0 : X_t = b\}\) where \(b < 0\), the Laplace transform satisfies \(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-r \tau}\right] = \exp\left(b \frac{\mu + \sqrt{\mu^2 + 2r\sigma^2}}{\sigma^2}\right)\) for \(r > 0\).

Solution

Consider the Doléans-Dade exponential martingale \(M_t = \exp\left(\theta X_t - \left(\theta \mu + \frac{1}{2}\theta^2 \sigma^2\right)t\right)\). Set the time decay rate equal to \(r\): \(\theta \mu + \frac{1}{2}\theta^2 \sigma^2 = r \iff \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \theta^2 + \mu \theta - r = 0\). The quadratic equation has roots \(\theta_{1,2} = \frac{-\mu \pm \sqrt{\mu^2 + 2r\sigma^2}}{\sigma^2}\). Since \(b < 0\), to ensure \(M_{t \wedge \tau}\) is uniformly integrable and bounded, choose the negative root \(\theta_2 = \frac{-\mu - \sqrt{\mu^2 + 2r\sigma^2}}{\sigma^2} = -\frac{\mu + \sqrt{\mu^2 + 2r\sigma^2}}{\sigma^2}\). Applying Doob's optional stopping theorem to the bounded martingale \(M_{t \wedge \tau}\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[M_\tau] = M_0 = 1\). Since \(X_\tau = b\), \(M_\tau = e^{\theta_2 b - r \tau}\). Factoring out the constant gives \(e^{\theta_2 b} \mathbb{E}[e^{-r \tau}] = 1\), which yields \(\mathbb{E}[e^{-r \tau}] = e^{-\theta_2 b} = \exp\left(b \frac{\mu + \sqrt{\mu^2 + 2r\sigma^2}}{\sigma^2}\right)\).

P6 · Transformation of Time-Varying Barrier to Flat Barrier

Show that a time-dependent exponential covenant barrier \(C(t) = K e^{-\gamma(T - t)}\) can be mapped into a constant barrier problem by a scale transformation of the asset process.

Solution

Define the normalized asset process \(Y_t = V_t / C(t) = V_t / (K e^{-\gamma(T - t)}) = \frac{V_t}{K e^{-\gamma T}} e^{-\gamma t}\). The covenant breach condition \(V_t \le C(t)\) is mathematically equivalent to \(Y_t \le 1\), which is a constant barrier at 1. Applying Itô's lemma to \(Y_t\): \(dY_t = \frac{e^{-\gamma t}}{K e^{-\gamma T}} dV_t - \gamma Y_t dt = (r - \delta - \gamma) Y_t dt + \sigma_V Y_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q}\). The normalized process \(Y_t\) is simply geometric Brownian motion with modified drift \(r - \delta - \gamma\) hitting a constant absorbing barrier at \(1\). Hence, all flat-barrier first-passage results apply directly under the adjusted drift.

Empirical

P7 · Sensitivity to Covenant Tightness

Evaluate the Black–Cox credit spread for a firm with \(V = 100, D = 80, T = 2, r = 0.04, \sigma_V = 0.25, R = 0.40\) across covenant barriers \(K \in \{50, 60, 70, 75\}\). Explain the economic trade-off.

Solution

As the barrier \(K\) increases from 50 to 75, the distance to barrier \(|b| = |\ln(K/V)|\) shrinks from \(0.693\) to \(0.288\). At \(K = 50\), default probability is low (\(\approx 9.5\%\)) and credit spread is moderate (\(\approx 340\) bp). At \(K = 70\), default probability jumps to \(29.8\%\) and spread reaches \(955.6\) bp. At \(K = 75\), default probability exceeds \(45\%\) and spread climbs over \(1600\) bp. Tighter covenants (higher \(K\)) trigger default sooner, which protects debtholders by seizing assets before they dissipate to zero, but simultaneously increases the probability of early bankruptcy costs and debt renegotiation.

P8 · Short-Spread Comparison for Distressed Firms

Using the empirical results from Lab 1, compare the short-term credit spread at \(T = 0.10\) years for a firm near the barrier (\(V = 75, K = 70\)) under the Merton model versus the Black–Cox model. What accounts for the discrepancy?

Solution

From Lab 1: at \(T = 0.10\) years, the Merton model spread is \(7098.9\) bp, whereas the Black–Cox model spread is \(25769.2\) bp. In the Merton model, the firm is only evaluated at \(T = 0.10\); because assets currently equal \(75\) and face value is \(80\), there is a chance the diffusion drifts back above \(80\) without penalty. In the Black–Cox model, the firm must stay continuously above \(70\) during the entire 0.10-year window; the probability of touching 70 at some intermediate time \(\tau \in (0, 0.10)\) is far higher than the terminal probability \(V_T < 80\), generating a spread over three times wider.

P9 · Recovery Rate Impact on Debt Value

A credit analyst notes that for a 2-year Black–Cox bond (\(V=100, D=80, K=70, r=0.04, \sigma_V=0.25\)), increasing the recovery rate \(R\) from \(0.20\) to \(0.80\) alters the credit spread. Calculate the change in debt price and verify that spread decreases monotonically.

Solution

From Theorem 2.2, debt price decomposes into survival value plus recovery value: \(B = D e^{-r T} q(T) + R D g(T)\). For \(T = 2\), survival probability is \(q(2) = 1 - 0.2976 = 0.7024\). Promised survival cash flow: \(80 e^{-0.08} \times 0.7024 \approx \$51.87\text{M}\). The discounted recovery factor is \(g(2) \approx 0.2741\). At \(R = 0.20\), recovery adds \(0.20 \times 80 \times 0.2741 = \$4.39\text{M}\), giving \(B \approx \$56.26\text{M}\) and spread \(s \approx 1370\) bp. At \(R = 0.80\), recovery adds \(0.80 \times 80 \times 0.2741 = \$17.54\text{M}\), giving \(B \approx \$69.41\text{M}\) and spread \(s \approx 315\) bp. Higher recovery increases debt value by \(\$13.15\text{M}\) and compresses credit spread by over 1000 basis points.

P10 · Endogenous Renegotiation and Strategic Default

How does the presence of significant bankruptcy deadweight costs (\(\alpha > 0\)) affect the credibility of a safety covenant in Black–Cox, and how might debt renegotiation alter the effective barrier?

Solution

In Black–Cox, bankruptcy is assumed to be an automatic, costless legal transition upon touching \(K\). In reality, Chapter 11 bankruptcy destroys 10% to 30% of enterprise value in legal fees, loss of trade credit, and customer attrition. If debtholders know that enforcing liquidation at \(V = K\) destroys a fraction \(\alpha K\), equityholders can strategically propose a debt-for-equity swap or debt relief. Debtholders will accept any restructured payment that exceeds liquidation scrap value \((1 - \alpha)K\), endogenously lowering the effective default threshold and creating debt renegotiation dynamics.

P11 · Incomplete Accounting Information and Unpredictable Default

Explain how Duffie and Lando (2001) bridge first-passage structural models and reduced-form intensity models by introducing noisy accounting observations of firm asset value \(V_t\).

Solution

Duffie and Lando (2001) assume that while asset value \(V_t\) hits a first-passage barrier \(K\) continuously, outside investors only observe noisy accounting reports \(Y_k = \ln V_{t_k} + \epsilon_k\) at discrete intervals. Conditional on the investor's filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t\), the exact position of \(V_t\) relative to \(K\) is uncertain. Consequently, default appears as an unpredictable Poisson surprise with an endogenous intensity \(\lambda_t = \frac{1}{2} \sigma_V^2 \left.\frac{\partial f_t(v)}{\partial v}\right|_{v=K}\). This information friction resolves the predictable stopping time limitation of Black–Cox, providing a structural foundation for reduced-form hazard rates.

P12 · Jump Risk in First-Passage Models

Contrast the distribution of the asset recovery value at default in a continuous first-passage model versus a jump-diffusion first-passage model.

Solution

In a continuous first-passage model (Black–Cox), asset paths cannot jump, so the asset value at default is deterministically equal to the barrier: \(V_\tau = K\) almost surely. In contrast, under jump diffusion (e.g. Kou 2002), the asset path can overshoot the barrier: a negative jump of size \(Y < 1\) can carry assets from \(V_{\tau-} > K\) straight to \(V_\tau = V_{\tau-} Y < K\). Recovery at default is stochastic and strictly less than \(K\), reflecting realistic jump-to-default severity observed during corporate collapses.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the defining structural difference between the Black–Cox (1976) model and the Merton (1974) model?

    Options
    In Black–Cox, default occurs at the first-passage time \(\tau = \inf\{t > 0 : V_t \le C(t)\}\), introducing continuous covenant monitoring.

  2. In the Black–Cox model, what happens to the default probability over horizon \(T\) as initial asset value \(V_0\) approaches the barrier \(K\) from above?

    Options
    As proven in Theorem 2.1 and Problem P4, as \(V_0 \to K^+\), \(b = \ln(K/V_0) \to 0\), causing \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T) \to \Phi(-z) + \Phi(z) = 1.0\).

  3. Why does the expected discounted recovery factor \(g(T) = \mathbb{E}[e^{-r \tau} \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau \le T\}}]\) strictly fall below the cumulative default probability \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T)\) when \(r > 0\)?

    Options
    When default occurs at \(\tau \in (0, T]\) with \(r > 0\), the discount factor \(e^{-r \tau}\) is strictly less than 1, so the discounted expectation \(\int_0^T e^{-r t} f(t) dt\) is strictly smaller than the total probability mass \(\int_0^T f(t) dt\).

  4. How does the Black–Cox model resolve the zero short-spread puzzle of the Merton model?

    Options
    Under continuous covenant monitoring, a firm near \(K\) can breach the covenant in an arbitrarily short time, keeping short-term credit spreads strictly positive.

  5. What type of stopping time is the default time \(\tau\) in the Black–Cox model with continuous asset paths?

    Options
    Because Brownian sample paths are continuous, \(\tau\) can be announced by an increasing sequence of stopping times \(\tau_n = \inf\{t : V_t \le K + 1/n\}\), making it mathematically predictable.

  6. What happens to the Black–Cox credit spread if the contractual recovery rate \(R\) is increased?

    Options
    From Theorem 2.2, \(\partial B / \partial R = D g(T) > 0\). Higher debt price translates directly to lower yield and narrower credit spread.

  7. In a time-dependent barrier \(C(t) = K e^{-\gamma(T - t)}\), what does \(\gamma > 0\) represent economically?

    Options
    When \(\gamma > 0\), the exponent \(-\gamma(T - t)\) increases from \(-\gamma T\) at \(t = 0\) to \(0\) at \(t = T\), meaning the barrier rises from \(K e^{-\gamma T}\) up to \(K\) at maturity.

  8. Under what condition on \(K\) does the Black–Cox model degenerate into the Merton model?

    Options
    When \(K \to 0\), \(b = \ln(K/V_0) \to -\infty\), so \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T) \to 0\). The firm never defaults before maturity, restoring Merton's terminal default structure.
