Structural credit models require filtering unobservable firm asset values and predict zero short-term spreads for solvent issuers. Robert Jarrow, Stuart Turnbull (1995), and Darrell Duffie and Kenneth Singleton (1999) bypassed unobservable balance-sheet dynamics by modeling corporate default as an unpredictable, exogenous jump arrival governed by a stochastic intensity. This reduced-form paradigm powers modern credit default swap (CDS) calibration, basket default swaps, and multi-name credit risk management.

1Information Filtration and Unpredictable Default

The distinction between structural and reduced-form models centers on the information filtration \(\mathbb{F} = (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}\) accessible to market participants. In continuous-path diffusion structural models with observable assets, default is a predictable stopping time: creditors watch the asset trajectory approach the covenant barrier, so the probability of default over an infinitesimal horizon \(dt\) is \(o(dt)\), forcing the short credit spread \(s(0)\) to zero.

In market reality, investors observe delayed and noisy accounting disclosures. Duffie and Lando (2001) demonstrated that adding observational noise to asset values transforms a predictable barrier hitting time into an unpredictable stopping time possessing an intensity. Consequently, reduced-form modeling directly posits an intensity process governing unexpected arrival.

Definition 4.1 (Reduced-Form Credit Model)

A reduced-form credit model is an arbitrage-free pricing framework where corporate default \(\tau\) is modeled directly as an exogenous random stopping time on a filtered probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{G}, (\mathcal{G}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \mathbb{Q})\), whose jump arrival is directed by a non-negative stochastic intensity process \(\lambda_t\), without reference to an unobservable balance-sheet diffusion process.

2Point Processes, Hazard Rates, and Cox Processes

Let \(\tau\) denote the default stopping time and \(N_t = \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau \le t\}}\) be the associated single-jump counting process. Let \(\mathbb{F} = (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}\) represent the background market filtration excluding default status, and \(\mathcal{G}_t = \mathcal{F}_t \vee \sigma(\{\tau \le s\} : s \le t)\) be the progressively enlarged filtration.

Definition 4.2 (Default Intensity / Hazard Rate)

The default intensity (hazard rate) under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is an \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-progressively measurable non-negative process \(\lambda_t \ge 0\) such that the compensated jump process

\[ M_t = N_t - \int_0^{t \wedge \tau} \lambda_s \, ds \]

is a \((\mathcal{G}_t, \mathbb{Q})\)-martingale. Equivalently, conditional on survival to time \(t\),

\[ \mathbb{Q}(t < \tau \le t + dt \mid \tau > t, \mathcal{F}_t) = \lambda_t \, dt + o(dt) \]

When \(\lambda_t\) is stochastic, the default process is a Cox process (or doubly stochastic Poisson process). Conditional on the realized intensity path \(\{\lambda_s : 0 \le s \le T\}\), default arrivals follow an inhomogeneous Poisson process.

Theorem 4.1 (Cox Survival Probability) T

Let \(\tau\) be the first jump of a Cox process directed by the intensity process \((\lambda_t)_{t \ge 0}\). The risk-neutral survival probability over \([t, T]\) conditional on \(\mathcal{G}_t\) and survival \(\{\tau > t\}\) is:

\[ \mathbb{Q}(\tau > T \mid \mathcal{G}_t) = \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau > t\}} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[ \exp\left( -\int_t^T \lambda_s \, ds \right) \,\Bigg|\, \mathcal{F}_t \right] \]

Proof. Under the immersion property (Hypothesis H), conditioning on the background sigma-algebra \(\mathcal{F}_\infty\) yields the conditional survival probability \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau > T \mid \mathcal{F}_\infty, \tau > t) = \exp(-\int_t^T \lambda_s ds)\). Taking conditional expectation with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_t\) by the tower property yields the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

3Recovery Conventions and the Duffie–Singleton Theorem

Default pricing requires specifying what debtholders receive upon default. Three conventions prevail:

  1. Recovery of Market Value (RMV): Debtholders receive a fraction \(R_t \in [0, 1)\) of the pre-default market value \(P(\tau^-, T)\) immediately at default \(\tau\) (Duffie & Singleton 1999).
  2. Recovery of Face Value (RFV): Debtholders receive a fraction \(R \in [0, 1)\) of the bond nominal face value \(D\) at default time \(\tau\) (Jarrow & Turnbull 1995).
  3. Recovery of Treasury (RT): Debtholders receive a fraction \(R\) of an otherwise identical default-free government bond.
Theorem 4.2 (Duffie–Singleton 1999 Pricing Theorem) T

Under Recovery of Market Value (RMV) with fractional loss \(L_t = 1 - R_t\), the pre-default price \(P(t, T)\) of a defaultable zero-coupon claim paying \(X\) at maturity \(T\) satisfies:

\[ P(t, T) = \mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{Q}\left[ \exp\left( -\int_t^T \left( r_s + \lambda_s L_s \right) ds \right) X \right] \]

where discounting occurs at the default-adjusted discount rate \(\mathcal{R}_s = r_s + \lambda_s(1 - R_s)\).

Proof Sketch. Prior to default, holding the defaultable bond yields capital gains and jump losses: \(dP_t = r_t P_t dt + dM_t^{\text{claim}} - (1 - R_t) P_{t^-} dN_t\). Compensating \(dN_t\) by \(\lambda_t dt\) and setting the drift equal to \(r_t P_t\) under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the pre-default drift satisfies \(dP_t = (r_t + \lambda_t(1 - R_t)) P_t dt + dM_t\). Applying the Feynman–Kac formula produces effective discounting at \(r_s + \lambda_s(1 - R_s)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Definition 4.3 (Analytical Comparison: RMV vs. RFV)

For a defaultable zero-coupon bond with face value 1 under constant risk-free rate \(r\) and constant intensity \(\lambda\):

\[ P_{\text{RMV}}(0, T) = e^{-(r + \lambda(1 - R))T}, \qquad s_{\text{RMV}}(T) = \lambda(1 - R) \] \[ P_{\text{RFV}}(0, T) = e^{-(r + \lambda)T} + \frac{R\lambda}{r + \lambda}\left(1 - e^{-(r + \lambda)T}\right), \qquad s_{\text{RFV}}(T) = -\frac{1}{T}\ln P_{\text{RFV}}(0, T) - r \]

While \(s_{\text{RMV}}\) is perfectly flat across maturities, \(s_{\text{RFV}}\) slopes downward as maturity increases because receiving \(R\) of par provides an immediate payout floor.

4Affine Term Structures: CIR Default Intensity

To capture term structure variations in credit spreads, default intensity \(\lambda_t\) is modeled as a mean-reverting Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) square-root diffusion:

\[ d\lambda_t = \kappa(\theta - \lambda_t)\,dt + \sigma_\lambda \sqrt{\lambda_t}\,dW_t^\mathbb{Q} \]

where \(2\kappa\theta \ge \sigma_\lambda^2\) guarantees that \(\lambda_t > 0\) almost surely. Assuming zero correlation with the short rate \(r\), the RMV bond price factorizes as \(P(0, T) = e^{-r T} A(T) e^{-B(T) \lambda_0}\), with functions \(A(T)\) and \(B(T)\) solving the Riccati ODEs with effective loss \(L = 1 - R\):

\[ \gamma = \sqrt{\kappa^2 + 2 \sigma_\lambda^2 L}, \qquad B(T) = \frac{2 L (e^{\gamma T} - 1)}{(\gamma + \kappa)(e^{\gamma T} - 1) + 2\gamma} \] \[ A(T) = \left[ \frac{2\gamma \exp\left(\frac{\kappa + \gamma}{2}T\right)}{(\gamma + \kappa)(e^{\gamma T} - 1) + 2\gamma} \right]^{\frac{2\kappa\theta}{\sigma_\lambda^2}} \]

Lab 1 · Reduced-form pricing: RMV vs RFV and CIR stochastic intensity

Compares bond prices, yields, and credit spreads between RMV and RFV recovery conventions under constant hazard rate, and demonstrates that mean-reverting CIR default intensity generates upward-sloping credit spread term structures when initial intensity is below the long-term mean.

"""Lab CR1-L04-1: Reduced-form credit models and Duffie-Singleton intensity pricing.

Demonstrates defaultable zero-coupon bond pricing under constant hazard rates,
compares Recovery of Market Value (RMV) vs Recovery of Face Value (RFV),
and prices affine term structures under Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) stochastic default intensity.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def reduced_form_pricing(
    maturity: float,
    r: float,
    lam: float,
    rec: float,
    convention: str = "rmv",
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Price defaultable bond under constant intensity."""
    t = maturity
    if convention == "rmv":
        disc_rate = r + lam * (1.0 - rec)
        price = math.exp(-disc_rate * t)
    else:  # 'rfv'
        tot = r + lam
        p_surv = math.exp(-tot * t)
        p_rec = (rec * lam / tot) * (1.0 - p_surv)
        price = p_surv + p_rec

    bond_yield = -math.log(price) / t
    spread_bps = (bond_yield - r) * 10000.0
    survival_prob = math.exp(-lam * t)

    return {
        "price": price,
        "yield": bond_yield,
        "spread_bps": spread_bps,
        "survival_prob": survival_prob,
    }


def cir_intensity_bond_price(
    t: float,
    r: float,
    lam0: float,
    kappa: float,
    theta: float,
    sigma_lam: float,
    rec: float = 0.4,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Price defaultable bond under CIR stochastic default intensity with RMV.

    dlambda_t = kappa * (theta - lambda_t) dt + sigma_lam * sqrt(lambda_t) dW_t
    Effective loss rate L = 1 - R.
    Discount rate R_t = r + L * lambda_t.
    """
    loss = 1.0 - rec
    # Rescaled CIR parameters for effective discount factor
    # S_t = loss * lambda_t
    # Alternatively solve standard Riccati ODE for E[exp(-L int lambda_s ds)]:
    # gamma = sqrt(kappa^2 + 2 * sigma_lam^2 * L)
    gamma = math.sqrt(kappa * kappa + 2.0 * sigma_lam * sigma_lam * loss)
    exp_gt = math.exp(gamma * t)
    denom = (gamma + kappa) * (exp_gt - 1.0) + 2.0 * gamma

    b_t = 2.0 * loss * (exp_gt - 1.0) / denom
    a_t = math.pow(2.0 * gamma * math.exp(0.5 * (kappa + gamma) * t) / denom, 2.0 * kappa * theta / (sigma_lam**2))

    credit_discount = a_t * math.exp(-b_t * lam0)
    riskfree_discount = math.exp(-r * t)
    total_price = riskfree_discount * credit_discount

    bond_yield = -math.log(total_price) / t
    spread_bps = (bond_yield - r) * 10000.0
    return total_price, spread_bps


def main() -> None:
    # Baseline parameters: r = 0.03, lambda = 0.02 (200 bp hazard), R = 0.40
    r = 0.03
    lam_const = 0.02
    rec = 0.40
    maturities = [1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 10.0]

    print("Reduced-Form Pricing: RMV vs RFV Recovery Conventions (r=3%, lambda=200bp, R=40%):")
    print("  T (yr) | RMV Price | RMV Spread | RFV Price | RFV Spread | Survival Prob")
    print("---------+-----------+------------+-----------+------------+--------------")

    for t in maturities:
        rmv = reduced_form_pricing(t, r, lam_const, rec, convention="rmv")
        rfv = reduced_form_pricing(t, r, lam_const, rec, convention="rfv")
        # Under constant intensity RMV, spread is constant = lambda * (1 - R) = 0.02 * 0.60 = 120 bps
        assert abs(rmv["spread_bps"] - 120.0) < 1e-9
        print(
            f"  {t:5.1f}  |  {rmv['price']:8.4f} |  {rmv['spread_bps']:6.1f} bp |  "
            f"{rfv['price']:8.4f} |  {rfv['spread_bps']:6.1f} bp |   {rmv['survival_prob']:8.4f}"
        )

    # CIR stochastic intensity: kappa = 0.3, theta = 0.03, sigma_lam = 0.08, lambda_0 = 0.01 (100 bp)
    kappa = 0.3
    theta = 0.03
    sigma_lam = 0.08
    lam0 = 0.01

    print("\nAffine Term Structure under CIR Default Intensity (lam0=100bp, theta=300bp):")
    print("  T (yr) | Total Bond Price | Yield (%) | Credit Spread (bp)")
    print("---------+------------------+-----------+-------------------")

    cir_spreads: list[float] = []
    for t in maturities:
        p_cir, s_cir = cir_intensity_bond_price(t, r, lam0, kappa, theta, sigma_lam, rec)
        cir_spreads.append(s_cir)
        print(f"  {t:5.1f}  |      {p_cir:8.4f}    |   {-math.log(p_cir) / t * 100:5.2f}%  |      {s_cir:6.1f}")

    # Because lam0 < theta, mean reversion causes credit spread to slope upward over time
    is_upward = all(cir_spreads[i] <= cir_spreads[i + 1] for i in range(len(cir_spreads) - 1))
    print(f"\nCIR credit spread curve is upward-sloping from mean reversion: {is_upward}")
    assert is_upward


main()

5Where the Canon Breaks: Identifiability and Bankruptcy Law

Reduced-form intensity models possess compelling analytical tractability, but face two severe structural critiques in professional practice:

  • Credit spread identifiability problem: Under RMV, cash bond prices depend solely on the product \(\lambda(1 - R)\). A credit spread of 120 bps can arise from \(\lambda = 2\%\) with \(R = 0.40\), or \(\lambda = 4\%\) with \(R = 0.70\), or \(\lambda = 1.33\%\) with \(R = 0.10\). Bond prices alone cannot disentangle default frequency from loss severity without auxiliary data (such as recovery swaps or distinct senior/subordinated tranches).
  • The bankruptcy law disconnect of RMV: Mathematical convenience makes RMV ubiquitous, but bankruptcy courts do not honor RMV. Under US Chapter 11, debtholders claim par value plus accrued interest, meaning real-world recovery follows RFV. Pricing distressed bonds under RMV systematically underprices short-dated debt trading at deep discounts.

Misconceptions

  • Reduced-form models assume the firm has no balance sheet or economic fundamentals.

    Reduced-form models specify default intensity \(\lambda_t\) as a direct function of macroeconomic and balance-sheet state variables (such as leverage, cash flow coverage, and rating). The distinction is that default is modeled as an unpredictable jump conditioned on those variables rather than an exact deterministic barrier crossing of an unobservable asset diffusion.

  • RMV and RFV yield identical credit spreads across all maturities when the hazard rate is constant.

    While RMV produces a constant spread \(s = \lambda(1 - R)\) across all maturities, RFV yields a downward-sloping spread curve. Under RFV, receiving a fixed dollar fraction of par value provides an early cash payout that becomes more valuable relative to discounted par as maturity lengthens, compressing long-term spreads.

  • Under stochastic intensity, the credit spread equals the expected hazard rate multiplied by loss given default.

    Because the exponential discounting function \(\exp(-x)\) is strictly convex, Jensen's inequality implies \(\mathbb{E}[\exp(-\int \lambda_s L ds)] > \exp(-\int \mathbb{E}[\lambda_s] L ds)\). Intensity volatility \(\sigma_\lambda\) strictly increases expected bond prices and lowers credit spreads, generating an intensity convexity adjustment.

P1 · Survival Probability from Hazard Rate

A corporate borrower has a constant risk-neutral default intensity \(\lambda = 0.025\) (250 bps per year). Calculate the 1-year, 5-year, and 10-year cumulative survival probabilities \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau > T)\) and default probabilities \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T)\).

Solution

Under constant intensity \(\lambda\), \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau > T) = \exp(-\lambda T)\) and \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau \le T) = 1 - \exp(-\lambda T)\):

  • \(T = 1\): \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau > 1) = e^{-0.025 \times 1} \approx 0.97531\) (Default: \(2.47\%\)).
  • \(T = 5\): \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau > 5) = e^{-0.025 \times 5} = e^{-0.125} \approx 0.88250\) (Default: \(11.75\%\)).
  • \(T = 10\): \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau > 10) = e^{-0.025 \times 10} = e^{-0.250} \approx 0.77880\) (Default: \(22.12\%\)).
P2 · Duffie–Singleton RMV Price and Spread

Consider a 5-year defaultable zero-coupon bond. The risk-free rate is \(r = 4.0\%\), default intensity is \(\lambda = 300\) bps (\(0.03\)), and Recovery of Market Value is \(R = 40\%\) (\(L = 0.60\)). Compute the default-adjusted discount rate, the bond price, and the credit spread.

Solution

The default-adjusted discount rate is \(\mathcal{R} = r + \lambda(1 - R) = 0.040 + 0.03 \times 0.60 = 0.040 + 0.018 = 0.058\) (\(5.80\%\)). The bond price is \(P(0, 5) = \exp(-0.058 \times 5) = \exp(-0.29) \approx 0.74826\). The credit spread is \(s = \mathcal{R} - r = 0.018\) (\(180\) basis points).

P3 · Implied Hazard Rate Extraction

A 1-year defaultable zero-coupon bond trades at \(P(0, 1) = 0.9300\) and the 1-year risk-free zero-coupon bond trades at \(P_0(0, 1) = 0.9600\). Assuming RMV with \(R = 0.35\), calculate the implied risk-neutral hazard rate \(\lambda\).

Solution

From Duffie–Singleton, \(P(0, 1) = P_0(0, 1) \exp(-\lambda(1 - R) \times 1)\):

\[ \exp(-\lambda \times 0.65) = \frac{0.9300}{0.9600} \approx 0.96875 \implies -0.65 \lambda = \ln(0.96875) \approx -0.031749 \]

Thus \(\lambda = \frac{0.031749}{0.65} \approx 0.04884\) (488.4 basis points).

P4 · Short-Term Spread Limit under RMV

Prove that as maturity \(T \to 0\), the credit spread in a reduced-form model with RMV approaches \(\lambda_0 (1 - R)\), contrasting with the Merton model limit of zero for solvent issuers.

Solution

The zero-coupon credit spread is defined as \(s(T) = -\frac{1}{T}\ln\left(\frac{P(0, T)}{P_0(0, T)}\right)\). Under RMV:

\[ \frac{P(0, T)}{P_0(0, T)} = \mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\int_0^T \lambda_s(1 - R) ds\right)\right] = 1 - \lambda_0(1 - R)T + o(T) \]

Taking logarithms: \(\ln(1 - \lambda_0(1 - R)T + o(T)) = -\lambda_0(1 - R)T + o(T)\). Dividing by \(-T\) and taking the limit as \(T \to 0\):

\[ \lim_{T \to 0} s(T) = \lim_{T \to 0} \frac{\lambda_0(1 - R)T + o(T)}{T} = \lambda_0(1 - R) > 0 \]

In contrast, in the Merton model with \(V_0 > D\), \(\Phi(-d_2) = o(T)\) as \(T \to 0\), forcing \(\lim_{T \to 0} s(T) = 0\). The unpredictable jump arrival in reduced-form models preserves strictly positive short spreads.

P5 · Derivation of the CIR Intensity Riccati ODE

Let \(d\lambda_t = \kappa(\theta - \lambda_t)dt + \sigma_\lambda \sqrt{\lambda_t}dW_t^\mathbb{Q}\). Use the Feynman–Kac theorem to derive the Riccati ordinary differential equations for \(A(\tau)\) and \(B(\tau)\) in the pricing ansatz \(f(\tau, \lambda) = \exp(A(\tau) - B(\tau)\lambda)\) where \(\tau = T - t\).

Solution

The pricing PDE for \(f(t, \lambda) = \mathbb{E}_t[\exp(-L\int_t^T \lambda_s ds)]\) is:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \kappa(\theta - \lambda)\frac{\partial f}{\partial \lambda} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_\lambda^2 \lambda \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial \lambda^2} - L\lambda f = 0 \]

Substituting \(f = \exp(A(\tau) - B(\tau)\lambda)\) with \(\tau = T - t\), we have \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = (-A' + B'\lambda)f\), \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \lambda} = -Bf\), and \(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial \lambda^2} = B^2 f\). Dividing by \(f\):

\[ -A'(\tau) + B'(\tau)\lambda - \kappa(\theta - \lambda)B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_\lambda^2 \lambda B^2(\tau) - L\lambda = 0 \]

Equating coefficients of \(\lambda\) and constants independently yields:

\[ B'(\tau) + \kappa B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_\lambda^2 B^2(\tau) - L = 0, \qquad B(0) = 0 \] \[ A'(\tau) = -\kappa\theta B(\tau), \qquad A(0) = 0 \]

Solving the Riccati equation for \(B(\tau)\) yields the hyperbolic functions given in Section 4.

P6 · Unbiasedness of Compensated Poisson Martingale

Prove that if \(N_t\) is a counting process with intensity \(\lambda_t\), the compensated process \(M_t = N_t - \int_0^{t \wedge \tau} \lambda_s ds\) is a local martingale with respect to the filtration \(\mathcal{G}_t\).

Solution

For any \(0 \le s \le t\), consider \(\mathbb{E}[M_t - M_s \mid \mathcal{G}_s]\). Partitioning the interval \([s, t]\) into small subintervals of width \(\Delta t = (t - s)/n\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[N_{u+\Delta t} - N_u \mid \mathcal{G}_u] = \mathbb{Q}(u < \tau \le u + \Delta t \mid \mathcal{G}_u) = \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau > u\}} \lambda_u \Delta t + o(\Delta t) \]

Summing across subintervals and taking \(n \to \infty\), the sum of conditional expectations converges in \(L^1\) to \(\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{s \wedge \tau}^{t \wedge \tau} \lambda_u du \,\Bigg|\, \mathcal{G}_s\right]\). Subtracting the compensator integral cancels the drift exactly, confirming that \(\mathbb{E}[M_t - M_s \mid \mathcal{G}_s] = 0\). Thus \(M_t\) is a \((\mathcal{G}_t, \mathbb{Q})\)-martingale.

Empirical

P7 · Piecewise Constant Hazard Bootstrapping

Given market zero-coupon bond survival probabilities \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau > 1) = 0.9700\) and \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau > 2) = 0.9300\), calculate the forward hazard rate \(\lambda_{[1, 2]}\) for the second year.

Solution

The survival probability over \([0, 2]\) factorizes as \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau > 2) = \mathbb{Q}(\tau > 1) \exp\left(-\lambda_{[1, 2]} \times 1\right)\). Thus:

\[ \exp(-\lambda_{[1, 2]}) = \frac{0.9300}{0.9700} \approx 0.95876 \implies \lambda_{[1, 2]} = -\ln(0.95876) \approx 0.04212 \quad (421.2 \text{ bps}) \]

The forward default intensity increases from \(304.6\) bps in year 1 to \(421.2\) bps in year 2.

P8 · Numerical Comparison of RMV vs RFV Across Tenors

Let \(r = 0.05\), \(\lambda = 0.04\), and \(R = 0.50\). Calculate the bond price and annualized credit spread under both RMV and RFV for maturities \(T = 1\) and \(T = 10\).

Solution

1. RMV: Spread is independent of \(T\): \(s = \lambda(1 - R) = 0.04 \times 0.50 = 0.0200\) (200 bps). Price at \(T=1\) is \(\exp(-0.07) \approx 0.93239\); price at \(T=10\) is \(\exp(-0.70) \approx 0.49659\).

2. RFV: Here \(r+\lambda = 0.09\), and \(\frac{R\lambda}{r+\lambda} = \frac{0.50 \times 0.04}{0.09} = \frac{0.02}{0.09} \approx 0.22222\).

  • \(T = 1\): \(e^{-0.09} \approx 0.91393\). Price is \(0.91393 + 0.22222 \times (1 - 0.91393) \approx 0.93306\). Yield is \(-\ln(0.93306)/1 = 0.06927\), spread is \(1.93\%\) (193 bps).
  • \(T = 10\): \(e^{-0.90} \approx 0.40657\). Price is \(0.40657 + 0.22222 \times (1 - 0.40657) \approx 0.53844\). Yield is \(-\ln(0.53844)/10 = 0.06191\), spread is \(1.19\%\) (119 bps).
P9 · Negative Correlation Between Interest Rates and Hazard Rate

Empirically, corporate default intensity surges during recessions when the central bank slashes interest rates, causing \(\operatorname{Cov}(r_t, \lambda_t) < 0\). Show how negative correlation between the short rate and hazard rate affects corporate bond prices relative to the uncorrelated case.

Solution

Under RMV, the bond price is \(P(0, T) = \mathbb{E}[\exp(-\int_0^T r_s ds - L \int_0^T \lambda_s ds)]\). For lognormally distributed discount factors, \(\mathbb{E}[\exp(-I_r - I_\lambda)] = \exp(-\mathbb{E}[I_r] - \mathbb{E}[I_\lambda] + \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Var}(I_r) + \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Var}(I_\lambda) + \operatorname{Cov}(I_r, I_\lambda))\).

When \(\operatorname{Cov}(I_r, I_\lambda) < 0\), the covariance term reduces the bond price \(P(0, T)\) relative to the independent case. Because the risk-free price \(P_0(0, T)\) is unchanged, the credit spread \(s(T) = -\frac{1}{T}\ln(P/P_0)\) widens, demonstrating that negative correlation creates an additional systematic risk premium.

P10 · Hypothesis (H) and Filtration Enlargement

Define the immersion property (Hypothesis H) in filtration enlargement theory \(\mathcal{G}_t = \mathcal{F}_t \vee \sigma(\tau \wedge t)\) and describe the consequences for credit derivative pricing if Hypothesis (H) is violated.

Solution

Hypothesis (H) states that every \((\mathbb{F}, \mathbb{Q})\)-martingale remains a \((\mathbb{G}, \mathbb{Q})\)-martingale. Equivalently, \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[X \mid \mathcal{G}_t] = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[X \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) for every bounded \(\mathcal{F}_\infty\)-measurable variable \(X\).

If Hypothesis (H) fails, observing that default has not occurred prior to \(t\) leaks predictive information regarding future paths of background processes like benchmark interest rates or equity indexes. This would introduce arbitrage or invalidate the standard decomposition where default-free term structure calibration is insulated from credit risk parameters.

P11 · Duffie–Lando (2001) Boundary Flux Density

Explain how Duffie and Lando (2001) express the default intensity \(\lambda_t\) of an unobserved asset diffusion with barrier \(V_B\) in terms of the conditional density gradient \(\left.\frac{\partial g_t(v)}{\partial v}\right|_{v = \ln V_B}\).

Solution

In Duffie and Lando (2001), investors receive periodic noisy accounting reports of log assets \(v_t = \ln V_t\). Let \(g_t(v)\) denote the conditional density of \(v_t\) given the information filtration \(\mathcal{G}_t\). Since default occurs upon hitting \(v_B = \ln V_B\), the absorbing boundary requires \(g_t(v_B) = 0\).

Over an instant \(dt\), Brownian paths crossing the boundary escape at a rate determined by the boundary flux in the Fokker–Planck PDE. Applying Fick's law of diffusion, the conditional probability rate of crossing is:

\[ \lambda_t = \lim_{dt \to 0} \frac{\mathbb{Q}(t < \tau \le t + dt \mid \tau > t, \mathcal{G}_t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2 \left. \frac{\partial g_t(v)}{\partial v} \right|_{v = \ln V_B} \]

Because the conditional density profile has a strictly positive slope at the boundary, this proves that imperfect information creates a strictly positive, continuous hazard rate from a continuous diffusion.

P12 · Resolving the Identifiability Problem via CDS Tranches

Propose a quantitative method using senior and subordinated CDS contracts to disentangle the risk-neutral hazard rate \(\lambda_t\) from the recovery rate \(R\).

Solution

Assume the firm has both Senior Unsecured and Subordinated debt sharing the same default stopping time \(\tau\) (and hence identical default intensity \(\lambda_t\)), but differing legal recovery rates \(R_{\text{sen}} > R_{\text{sub}}\).

Under RMV, the senior spread is \(s_{\text{sen}} \approx \lambda(1 - R_{\text{sen}})\) and the subordinated spread is \(s_{\text{sub}} \approx \lambda(1 - R_{\text{sub}})\). Subtracting the two gives \(\Delta s = s_{\text{sub}} - s_{\text{sen}} = \lambda(R_{\text{sen}} - R_{\text{sub}})\). Combining this with historical recovery differential data or equity cross-sectional constraints allows simultaneous identification of both \(\lambda\) and \(R_{\text{sen}}\).

Knowledge check quiz

  1. What is the fundamental difference in stopping time properties between structural diffusion models and reduced-form credit models?

    In continuous-path diffusion structural models, the asset value approaches the barrier continuously, making default predictable. In reduced-form models, default is the first jump of a point process, making it totally unpredictable conditional on the background filtration.

  2. Under the Duffie–Singleton (1999) Recovery of Market Value (RMV) assumption with constant hazard rate \(\lambda\), interest rate \(r\), and recovery \(R\), what is the credit spread of a zero-coupon bond?

    Under constant RMV parameters, the default-adjusted discount rate is \(r + \lambda(1 - R)\), so the yield is \(r + \lambda(1 - R)\) and the credit spread is exactly \(\lambda(1 - R)\).

  3. What does the credit spread identifiability problem state regarding single-name bond prices under RMV?

    Because RMV bond prices depend strictly on the product \(\lambda(1 - R)\), infinitely many pairs of \((\lambda, R)\) yield identical bond prices unless recovery is fixed or identified via distinct seniority tranches.

  4. Why does the Recovery of Face Value (RFV) convention produce a maturity-dependent spread even under flat hazard and risk-free rates?

    Under RFV, recovering \(R\) of par value at default provides a payoff whose present value depends on the time of default, creating an embedded floor that flattens or depresses long-term credit yields.

  5. In a CIR default intensity model \(d\lambda_t = \kappa(\theta - \lambda_t)dt + \sigma_\lambda \sqrt{\lambda_t}dW_t\), if spot intensity \(\lambda_0\) is less than the long-term mean \(\theta\), how does the credit spread curve behave?

    Because \(\lambda_0 < \theta\), the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[\lambda_t]\) rises over time toward \(\theta\), causing longer-term default probabilities and spreads to be higher than short-term spreads.

  6. How did Duffie and Lando (2001) bridge structural first-passage models with reduced-form intensity models?

    Duffie and Lando proved that when investors observe firm assets with measurement noise, the probability distribution of asset value smooths out at the barrier, generating a finite hazard rate identical to a reduced-form intensity.

  7. What is the effect of intensity volatility \(\sigma_\lambda\) on defaultable zero-coupon bond prices under stochastic intensity?

    Because \(\exp(-x)\) is a convex function, higher variance of integrated intensity increases \(\mathbb{E}[\exp(-\int \lambda_s ds)]\), increasing bond prices and lowering long-term credit yields.

  8. Under the Immersion Property (Hypothesis H), what occurs when the reference filtration \(\mathbb{F}\) is enlarged to include the default time \(\tau\)?

    Hypothesis H ensures that observing whether default has occurred does not introduce arbitrage or alter the martingale dynamics of reference processes like risk-free term structures.

  9. In Lab 1 under RMV with hazard rate \(\lambda = 200\) bps (\(0.02\)) and recovery rate \(R = 0.40\), what is the credit spread in basis points?

    From Lab 1: spread is \(\lambda(1 - R) = 0.02 \times (1 - 0.40) \times 10000 = 120.0\) bps across all maturities.

  10. In Lab 1 under CIR stochastic intensity (\(\lambda_0 = 100\) bp, \(\theta = 300\) bp), what is the credit spread in basis points at maturity \(T = 10.0\) years?

    From Lab 1 table at \(T=10.0\text{y}\), the CIR credit spread is \(140.6\) basis points.
