The CDS–Bond Basis

No-arbitrage equivalence between synthetic and cash corporate credit, repo financing mechanics, negative basis carry economics, and limits to capital structure arbitrage during systemic liquidity contractions.

Under frictionless no-arbitrage, the price of insuring a bond in the credit default swap market must equal the yield spread of the cash bond over the risk-free benchmark. In real markets, the CDS–bond basis frequently diverges from zero, generating major capital structure arbitrage opportunities for hedge funds and bank credit desks. Analyzing the drivers of the basis reveals how funding haircuts, counterparty risk, and short-sale frictions govern price formation.

1. Definition of the CDS–Bond Basis

The CDS–bond basis is the difference between the par-equivalent credit default swap spread on a reference entity and the cash credit spread on a bond issued by that same entity of matching maturity:

\[ \text{Basis} = S_{\text{CDS}} - S_{\text{bond}} \]
Definition 3.1 (Bond Cash Spread Metrics)

To compute the basis accurately, the cash bond spread \(S_{\text{bond}}\) must be matched to the floating reference curve used in swap discounting. Practitioners utilize two primary measures:

  • Asset Swap Spread (ASW): The fixed spread over Libor/SOFR received by an investor who buys the cash bond at market price and enters an interest rate swap to convert fixed bond coupons into floating cash flows.
  • Zero-Volatility Spread (Z-spread): The constant spread that, when added to the benchmark zero-coupon curve, discounts the contractual bond cash flows to its market price.

When \(S_{\text{CDS}} > S_{\text{bond}}\), the basis is positive: synthetic credit insurance is more expensive than cash bond default risk. When \(S_{\text{CDS}} < S_{\text{bond}}\), the basis is negative: cash bonds trade at a wider spread than synthetic default protection.

2. The Par Equivalence Theorem

The theoretical benchmark for credit pricing equivalence was established by Duffie & Singleton (1999).

Theorem 3.1 (Duffie's Par-CDS Par-Bond Equivalence)

Assume a frictionless market without taxes, transaction costs, or counterparty default risk. Suppose:

  1. The risk-free reference rate is flat and deterministic at rate \(r\).
  2. A defaultable corporate bond pays coupon \(c_{\text{par}}\) maturing at \(T\) and trades at par value (\$100).
  3. Upon default at \(\tau \le T\), the bond recovers a fraction \(R\) of its par face value.
  4. A CDS on the obligor matures at \(T\) with par spread \(S_{\text{par}}\) and identical recovery \(R\).

Then absence of arbitrage requires:

\[ S_{\text{par}} = c_{\text{par}} - r \]

that is, the par CDS spread exactly equals the credit spread on a par corporate bond.

Proof

Consider a portfolio formed at \(t = 0\):

  • Long 1 defaultable par bond paying coupon \(c_{\text{par}}\), financed by borrowing \$1 at risk-free rate \(r\).
  • Long 1 CDS protection contract paying spread \(S_{\text{par}}\) until default or maturity \(T\).

Before default or maturity, net ongoing cash flow per period is:

\[ \text{Cash Flow}(t) = c_{\text{par}} - r - S_{\text{par}} \]

If default occurs at \(\tau \le T\):

  • The bond delivers recovery value \(R\).
  • The CDS protection leg pays \((1 - R)\).
  • Total asset recovery is \(R + (1 - R) = 1.00\) (par face value).
  • The borrowed principal of \$1 is repaid exactly in full with zero shortfall or surplus.

If no default occurs by \(T\):

  • The bond matures and repays \$1 principal, which repays the \$1 borrowed funds.

The terminal payoff is identically zero in all states of the world. Because the position requires zero initial capital outlay and bears zero default or interest rate risk, absence of arbitrage requires that ongoing intermediate cash flows be zero: \(c_{\text{par}} - r - S_{\text{par}} = 0\), establishing \(S_{\text{par}} = c_{\text{par}} - r\).

3. Arbitrage Trading Mechanics: Negative & Positive Basis

Definition 3.2 (Negative Basis Trade)

When \(\text{Basis} < 0\) (\(S_{\text{bond}} > S_{\text{CDS}}\)), a basis trader executes the negative basis trade:

  1. Buy the cash corporate bond earning coupon \(y = r + S_{\text{bond}}\).
  2. Buy CDS protection on the same obligor paying spread \(S_{\text{CDS}}\).
  3. Finance the bond in the repo market, pledging the bond as collateral at repo rate \(r_{\text{repo}} = r + s_{\text{repo}}\), subject to haircut \(h\).
  4. Fund the haircut equity portion \(h\) using internal equity capital.

The gross carry of the trade is \(- \text{Basis} = S_{\text{bond}} - S_{\text{CDS}} > 0\). The net carry deducts the repo funding spread over the benchmark rate:

\[ \text{Net Carry} = (S_{\text{bond}} - S_{\text{CDS}}) - (r_{\text{repo}} - r) \]
Example 3.1 (Leveraged Return on Equity for a Negative Basis Trade)

Consider a 5-year corporate bond trading at \(S_{\text{bond}} = 135\text{ bp}\) over benchmark rate \(r = 3.00\%\). The 5-year CDS trades at \(S_{\text{CDS}} = 95\text{ bp}\), giving a negative basis of \(-40\text{ bp}\). Term repo financing costs \(r_{\text{repo}} = 3.15\%\) (\(s_{\text{repo}} = 15\text{ bp}\)) with a \(2\%\) haircut.

On a \$10,000,000 face value position:

  • Equity capital required (2% haircut): \(\$200,000\).
  • Repo debt borrowed (98%): \(\$9,800,000\).
  • Bond income: \(\$10,000,000 \times 4.35\% = \$435,000\).
  • CDS protection cost: \(\$10,000,000 \times 0.95\% = \$95,000\).
  • Repo financing cost: \(\$9,800,000 \times 3.15\% = \$308,700\).
  • Net annual dollar profit: \(\$435,000 - \$95,000 - \$308,700 = \$31,300\).

The return on invested equity capital is:

\[ \text{ROE} = \frac{\$31,300}{\$200,000} = 15.65\% \]

If repo haircuts widen from 2% to 10% during market turbulence, the required equity increases to \$1,000,000, depressing the ROE to \(5.65\%\).

4. Structural Determinants of Non-Zero Basis

In practice, the basis oscillates around zero due to distinct market frictions (O'Kane 2008):

Market Friction Impact on Basis Economic Mechanism
Counterparty Credit Risk Negative (\(S_{\text{CDS}} < S_{\text{bond}}\)) Protection buyers demand a discount because the CDS seller may default, leaving synthetic protection less valuable than holding cash collateral.
Cheapest-to-Deliver (CTD) Option Positive (\(S_{\text{CDS}} > S_{\text{bond}}\)) In physical settlement, protection buyers hold an option to deliver the lowest-priced qualifying bond, increasing CDS value above cash spread.
Bond Repo Specialness Positive (\(S_{\text{CDS}} > S_{\text{bond}}\)) If a bond is hard to borrow, its repo rate drops below benchmark rates, making long cash positions cheaper to fund and depressing bond cash spreads.
Funding Constraints & Haircuts Negative (\(S_{\text{CDS}} < S_{\text{bond}}\)) Purchasing cash bonds requires leverage and balance sheet capacity; when funding freezes, forced selling widens bond spreads far past synthetic CDS.
Short-Sale Constraints Positive (\(S_{\text{CDS}} > S_{\text{bond}}\)) Shorting cash corporate bonds is difficult and costly; bearish investors buy CDS instead, pushing CDS spreads up relative to bonds.

Lab 3 · CDS–Bond Basis Measurement and Negative Basis Carry

We hypothesize that for a 5Y corporate bond trading at 135 bp cash spread versus a 95 bp CDS quote, the basis is \(-40\text{ bp}\). Under a 2% repo haircut and 15 bp repo spread, the net carry is \(+25\text{ bp}\) on notional, producing an annualized ROE of \(15.65\%\). Increasing the haircut to 25% compresses ROE to \(4.15\%\). 

"""Lab CR2-L03-1: CDS-bond basis measurement and negative basis trade arbitrage economics.

Demonstrates basis calculation, repo funding mechanics, haircut leverage drag,
and returns on equity across financial crisis friction regimes.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def main() -> None:
    # 5-Year benchmark and instrument parameters
    risk_free_rate = 0.0300  # 3.00% benchmark yield
    cds_spread_bps = 95.0  # 95 bp par CDS spread
    bond_cash_spread_bps = 135.0  # 135 bp par bond Z-spread / ASW

    # Repo and funding market observables
    repo_spread_bps = 15.0  # Repo rate = r + 15 bp
    haircuts = [0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.25]  # Haircuts from normal to crisis

    # 1. Basis definition
    basis_bps = cds_spread_bps - bond_cash_spread_bps
    gross_carry_bps = -basis_bps  # Cash spread - CDS spread

    print("CDS-Bond Basis Economics (5Y Tenor, Notional = 10000000):")
    print(f"  Par CDS Spread:       {cds_spread_bps:6.1f} bp")
    print(f"  Bond Cash Spread:     {bond_cash_spread_bps:6.1f} bp")
    print(f"  CDS-Bond Basis:       {basis_bps:6.1f} bp (Negative Basis Regime)")
    print(f"  Gross Carry:          {gross_carry_bps:6.1f} bp")

    print("\nLeveraged Negative Basis Trade Structure:")
    print("  1. Long 10M Face Value 5Y Corporate Bond (earns r + 135 bp).")
    print("  2. Long 10M 5Y CDS Protection (pays 95 bp).")
    print("  3. Finance (1 - Haircut) in term repo market at repo rate (r + 15 bp).")
    print("  4. Fund Haircut with firm equity capital.")

    print("\nHaircut Sensitivity & Return on Regulatory Capital (ROE):")
    print("  Haircut (%) | Equity Capital |   Repo Debt    |   Net Carry    | Return on Equity (%)")
    print("--------------+----------------+----------------+----------------+----------------------")

    for h in haircuts:
        equity_cap = 10_000_000.0 * h
        repo_debt = 10_000_000.0 * (1.0 - h)

        # Revenue: Bond coupon (r + 135 bp)
        bond_income = 10_000_000.0 * (risk_free_rate + bond_cash_spread_bps / 10000.0)
        # Cost 1: CDS protection
        cds_cost = 10_000_000.0 * (cds_spread_bps / 10000.0)
        # Cost 2: Repo interest
        repo_rate = risk_free_rate + repo_spread_bps / 10000.0
        repo_cost = repo_debt * repo_rate

        net_income = bond_income - cds_cost - repo_cost
        roe = (net_income / equity_cap) * 100.0

        print(
            f"    {h * 100:4.1f}%    |   {equity_cap:10.0f}   |   {repo_debt:10.0f}   |   "
            f"{net_income:10.0f}   |        {roe:5.2f}%"
        )

    # Invariants
    assert basis_bps < 0.0
    assert gross_carry_bps > 0.0


main()

5. Where the Canon Breaks: Limits to Arbitrage in 2008

The textbook no-arbitrage foundation assumes that basis deviations are self-correcting: whenever \(\text{Basis} < 0\), arbitrageurs step in to buy bonds and buy CDS until the spread gap closes. In Autumn 2008, the investment-grade corporate CDS–bond basis collapsed to unprecedented negative extremes, reaching \(-300\text{ bp}\) to \(-500\text{ bp}\) (Mian & Sufi 2009).

Arbitrage failed to operate because the negative basis trade is not a pure arbitrage; it is an asset-liability funding mismatch. When investment banks increased repo haircuts from 2% to 20% overnight, hedge funds received massive margin calls. Because funds could not finance their bond inventories, they were forced to dump cash bonds into an illiquid market, exacerbating the negative basis blowout and causing severe losses on hedged arbitrage books.

Misconceptions

  • A negative basis trade is risk-free because buying CDS protection perfectly eliminates corporate default risk.

    The trade eliminates fundamental credit risk of the reference entity, but leaves the trader exposed to funding liquidity risk (repo haircut hikes), counterparty default risk on the CDS contract, and mark-to-market basis widening risk prior to maturity.

  • A zero CDS–bond basis implies that cash bonds and synthetic CDS are priced by identical investor clienteles.

    The cash bond market is dominated by real-money institutional investors (pension funds, insurance companies) seeking yield and restricted from using derivatives. The CDS market is dominated by banks and hedge funds managing macro risk. A zero basis occurs when opposing institutional flows coincidentally balance, not because clienteles are identical.

  • The CDS–bond basis is identical whether measured against Treasury yield spreads, Libor swap spreads, or SOFR OIS spreads.

    The basis is strictly defined relative to the funding benchmark of the marginal investor. Measuring against Treasury yields embeds sovereign credit risk and repo convenience premia, whereas measuring against swap rates isolates pure corporate credit risk.

Problem set

A 5-year senior unsecured bond issued by Apex Logistics trades at an ASW spread of 160 bp. The 5-year CDS spread on Apex Logistics is 125 bp. Calculate the CDS–bond basis and state whether the market presents a positive or negative basis arbitrage opportunity.

Worked solution

The CDS–bond basis is \(\text{Basis} = S_{\text{CDS}} - S_{\text{bond}} = 125\text{ bp} - 160\text{ bp} = -35\text{ bp}\). Because the basis is negative, it presents a negative basis trade opportunity: buy the cash bond at 160 bp, buy CDS protection at 125 bp, and capture 35 bp of gross annual carry.

A hedge fund enters a negative basis trade on $50,000,000 of par bonds with gross carry of 45 bp. The broker charges a repo rate of \(r + 20\text{ bp}\) with a 4% haircut. Calculate the fund's net dollar carry per year and its return on equity.

Worked solution

Equity capital required is \(4\% \times \$50,000,000 = \$2,000,000\). Repo debt is \(96\% \times \$50,000,000 = \$48,000,000\). Gross carry earns \(45\text{ bp} \times \$50,000,000 = \$225,000\). Repo funding drag over benchmark is \(20\text{ bp} \times \$48,000,000 = \$96,000\). Net annual dollar carry is \(\$225,000 - \$96,000 = \$129,000\). Return on equity is \(\text{ROE} = \frac{\$129,000}{\$2,000,000} = 6.45\%\).

In a positive basis trade, a desk shorts $20,000,000 of cash bonds at 70 bp cash spread and sells CDS protection at 95 bp. If the desk must pay a 15 bp borrow fee to short the bond, calculate the net annual basis carry in basis points and dollars.

Worked solution

Gross carry is \(\text{Basis} = S_{\text{CDS}} - S_{\text{bond}} = 95 - 70 = +25\text{ bp}\). Deducting the bond borrow fee of 15 bp gives a net carry of \(25 - 15 = 10\text{ bp}\). In dollars on $20,000,000 notional: \(\text{Net Dollar Carry} = \$20,000,000 \times 0.0010 = \$20,000\) per annum.

Prove that if a defaultable bond trades at a discount to par (\(P < 100\)), the cash-synthetic replication portfolio in Theorem 3.1 fails to guarantee zero terminal default risk.

Worked solution

If the bond is purchased at price \(P < 1\), borrowing \(P\) finances the purchase. At default \(\tau \le T\), the bond delivers recovery \(R\) on par (not on purchase price \(P\)). The CDS pays \((1 - R)\). Total cash received is \(R + (1 - R) = 1.00\). However, the debt owed is \(P\). The investor receives a windfall gain of \(1.00 - P > 0\) upon default. Because default produces a non-zero terminal cash flow, the bond cannot be perfectly replicated by a linear combination of par CDS and riskless debt without introducing jump-to-default basis risk.

Prove that introducing a positive repo haircut \(h > 0\) funded by unhedged equity capital strictly lowers the break-even negative basis threshold.

Worked solution

Let \(r_e\) be the cost of equity and \(r_d\) be the repo borrowing rate. Total funding cost per dollar of bond is \(C = h r_e + (1 - h) r_d\). For the negative basis trade to generate economic value, net bond return \(y - S_{\text{CDS}}\) must exceed \(C\). Expressing this relative to benchmark \(r\): \((r + S_{\text{bond}}) - S_{\text{CDS}} > h r_e + (1 - h) r_d\), which rearranges to \(S_{\text{bond}} - S_{\text{CDS}} > h(r_e - r) + (1 - h)(r_d - r)\). Since \(r_e > r_d > r\), increasing \(h\) strictly raises the required gross spread gap \(S_{\text{bond}} - S_{\text{CDS}}\) necessary to achieve profitability.

Show that under recovery of market value (RMV), the par equivalence theorem holds exactly for bonds trading away from par.

Worked solution

Under RMV, the fractional loss upon default is \((1 - R) P(\tau^-)\). If a CDS pays \((1 - R) P(\tau^-)\) upon default, the recovery payment plus bond value equals \(R P(\tau^-) + (1 - R) P(\tau^-) = P(\tau^-)\). By discounting at default-adjusted rate \(r + \lambda(1 - R)\), the bond yield spread over \(r\) is exactly \(\lambda(1 - R)\), identical to the continuous CDS spread. Thus, under RMV, par equivalence generalizes to discount and premium bonds.

Empirical research demonstrates that during the 2008 Lehman collapse, negative basis levels were strongly correlated with dealer bank CDS spreads. Formulate an empirical model testing the hypothesis that counterparty risk explained basis widening.

Worked solution

Estimate the time-series regression \(\text{Basis}_t = \alpha + \beta_1 S_{\text{Dealer},t} + \beta_2 \text{TED}_t + \beta_3 \text{Haircut}_t + \epsilon_t\), where \(S_{\text{Dealer},t}\) is the average CDS spread of primary broker-dealers, \(\text{TED}_t\) is the 3-month TED spread (funding liquidity), and \(\text{Haircut}_t\) is corporate repo haircut data. Empirical results show a large negative \(\beta_1\), confirming that higher dealer counterparty risk directly compressed CDS values relative to bonds, driving the basis deeply negative.

Analyze why the CDS–bond basis for high-yield issuers is systematically more negative on average than for investment-grade issuers.

Worked solution

High-yield cash bonds suffer from severe illiquidity, higher repo haircuts (typically 10% to 25%), and wider bid-ask spreads than investment-grade bonds. Investors require a significant liquidity premium in cash bond yields to compensate for turnover friction. In contrast, synthetic CDS trading operates with standardized contracts and lower capital charges, leaving cash spreads wider and the basis more negative.

Examine how the Federal Reserve's Secondary Market Corporate Credit Facility (SMCCF) in March 2020 impacted the CDS–bond basis across eligible corporate bonds.

Worked solution

When the Fed announced direct purchases of cash corporate bonds and bond ETFs in March 2020, cash bond prices rallied sharply, compressing bond cash spreads. Because the facility did not purchase single-name CDS, CDS spreads tightened more slowly. This central bank intervention reversed the negative basis spike, causing the basis to rapidly rebound toward zero.

Connect the CDS–bond basis to the credit-spread puzzle introduced in CR1. How does separating the cash bond spread into synthetic CDS spread and the basis isolate pure default risk from funding/liquidity components?

Worked solution

The total corporate bond spread can be partitioned as \(S_{\text{bond}} = S_{\text{CDS}} - \text{Basis}\). Since \(S_{\text{CDS}}\) captures pure credit risk (expected default loss plus systematic credit risk premium) without requiring cash bond financing, the negative of the basis (\(-\text{Basis} = S_{\text{bond}} - S_{\text{CDS}}\)) directly isolates the non-credit component (bond market illiquidity, repo funding costs, and tax frictions). This decomposition demonstrates that non-credit factors account for the majority of the residual in the credit-spread puzzle.

Model the dynamic feedback loop between repo haircut spirals and mark-to-market basis unwinds using the Brunnermeier & Pedersen (2009) market liquidity and funding liquidity framework.

Worked solution

Let leverage be \(L = 1/h\). A negative shock to bond prices triggers margin calls, forcing funds to liquidate bonds. Bond fire sales depress market liquidity, widening the bid-ask spread and price volatility. Risk models at dealer banks respond to higher volatility by raising haircuts \(h\), further contracting funding liquidity. This feedback loop causes negative basis widening to become self-reinforcing until external central bank liquidity backstops the repo market.

Evaluate how the transition from Libor to SOFR affected historical CDS–bond basis measurements. Why did the absence of a bank credit risk premium in SOFR distort legacy basis series?

Worked solution

Libor contained an embedded interbank credit risk premium, whereas SOFR is a secured, risk-free overnight repo rate. Measuring bond Z-spreads over SOFR produces a wider cash spread than measuring over Libor. Unless an explicit credit adjustment spread (CAS) is added to the SOFR benchmark, legacy CDS–bond basis calculations appear systematically more negative by approximately 15 to 25 basis points.

Retrieval quiz

  1. How is the CDS–bond basis algebraically defined?

    Options
    The standard market convention defines the basis as the CDS spread minus the cash bond spread (Z-spread or ASW).

  2. What positions constitute a standard negative basis trade?

    Options
    When the basis is negative (\(S_{\text{bond}} > S_{\text{CDS}}\)), buying the higher-yielding bond and buying the cheaper CDS protection locks in positive gross carry.

  3. What core assumption in Duffie's par equivalence theorem is violated when corporate bonds trade at deep discounts to par?

    Options
    Duffie's proof relies on the par bond assumption. For a discount bond, borrowing the purchase price leaves an unhedged surplus upon default because recovery is established on full face value.

  4. How does a sudden increase in repo haircuts impact hedge funds engaged in leveraged negative basis trades?

    Options
    A higher haircut directly reduces leverage, forcing the trader to post more equity capital or liquidate bond positions, triggering forced-selling spirals.

  5. Why does the presence of counterparty credit risk on the CDS protection seller exert downward pressure on the CDS spread?

    Options
    If the protection seller carries default risk, the buyer receives defaultable insurance; the market discounts the CDS premium, driving the basis negative.

  6. What is the "cheapest-to-deliver" (CTD) option in physical CDS settlement?

    Options
    In physical delivery, the protection buyer chooses which qualifying bond to deliver at par; selecting the lowest-market-value bond maximizes the option value to the buyer.

  7. If a corporate bond is on "special" in the repo market, how does this affect the CDS–bond basis?

    Options
    A bond on special trades at a low repo rate (cheap financing), which increases the bond's cash price and lowers its cash spread \(S_{\text{bond}}\), making \(S_{\text{CDS}} - S_{\text{bond}}\) more positive.

  8. During the peak of the 2008 financial crisis, why did negative basis trades fail to close the spread gap?

    Options
    Limits to arbitrage, driven by liquidity freezes, spiking repo haircuts, and dealer balance sheet contraction, prevented capital from entering to exploit the negative basis.
