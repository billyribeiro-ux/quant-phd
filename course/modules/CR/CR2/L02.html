Bootstrapping Hazard Curves

Constructing the risk-neutral default probability term structure from market quotes through sequential root-finding, survival curve extraction, and recovery rate sensitivity analysis.

Market participants do not directly quote survival probabilities; they quote par credit default swap spreads across standard tenors. Bootstrapping hazard curves extracts the underlying term structure of default intensity required to price off-market contracts, compute forward credit spreads, and calibrate multi-name correlation models. A flawed bootstrapper produces arbitrage opportunities and misprices portfolio tranches.

1. Piecewise-Constant Hazard Rate Model

Market liquid credit default swap quotes exist across discrete benchmark maturities \(T_1 < T_2 < \dots < T_M\) (typically 1Y, 2Y, 3Y, 5Y, 7Y, and 10Y). To interpolate between observed tenors without assuming an ad-hoc functional form, quantitative finance parameterizes the default intensity (hazard rate) \(\lambda(t)\) as a piecewise-constant function.

Definition 2.1 (Hazard Curve Bootstrapping)

Bootstrapping hazard curves from CDS quotes is the sequential, recursive numerical procedure that determines the piecewise-constant hazard rates \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_M\) defined on intervals \((T_{k-1}, T_k]\) with \(T_0 = 0\):

\[ \lambda(t) = \lambda_k \quad \text{for } t \in (T_{k-1}, T_k] \]

such that each corresponding theoretical model par CDS spread \(S_{\text{model}}(T_k)\) reproduces the observed market par quote \(S_k^{\text{mkt}}\) within machine precision.

Under this piecewise-constant intensity parameterization, the cumulative survival probability \(Q(t) = \mathbb{Q}(\tau > t) = \exp(-\int_0^t \lambda(u) du)\) possesses a closed analytical representation across any horizon \(t \in (T_{k-1}, T_k]\):

\[ Q(t) = \exp\left( -\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \lambda_j (T_j - T_{j-1}) - \lambda_k (t - T_{k-1}) \right) = Q(T_{k-1}) \cdot \exp(-\lambda_k (t - T_{k-1})) \]

2. Recursive Bootstrapping Algorithm

The bootstrapping algorithm proceeds forward in maturity, solving for exactly one unknown hazard rate \(\lambda_k\) at step \(k\), taking the previously calibrated intensities \(\{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{k-1}\}\) as fixed constants (O'Kane 2008).

Theorem 2.1 (Existence and Uniqueness of Bootstrapped Hazard Rate)

Let \(S_k^{\text{mkt}} > 0\) be the observed market par spread at tenor \(T_k\). For a fixed recovery rate \(R \in [0, 1)\) and previously determined intensities \(\{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{k-1}\}\), the objective function:

\[ f(\lambda_k) = \text{ProtPV}(T_k; \lambda_k) - S_k^{\text{mkt}} \cdot \text{PremPV}(T_k; \lambda_k) \]

satisfies \(f(0) < 0\) and \(\lim_{\lambda_k \to \infty} f(\lambda_k) > 0\). Furthermore, \(f(\lambda_k)\) is strictly monotonically increasing on \([0, \infty)\), guaranteeing a unique positive root \(\lambda_k^* > 0\).

Proof

At \(\lambda_k = 0\), the reference entity cannot default in the interval \((T_{k-1}, T_k]\). Thus, \(Q(t) = Q(T_{k-1})\) for all \(t \in (T_{k-1}, T_k]\). The incremental protection payment over \((T_{k-1}, T_k]\) is zero, while the premium leg receives coupon cash flows weighted by the positive constant survival probability \(Q(T_{k-1}) > 0\). If \(S_k^{\text{mkt}} > 0\), the premium leg strictly exceeds the protection leg, so \(f(0) < 0\).

As \(\lambda_k \to \infty\), \(Q(t) \to 0\) instantaneously for all \(t > T_{k-1}\). The entity defaults immediately after \(T_{k-1}\) with probability \(Q(T_{k-1})\). The protection leg pays \((1 - R) D(T_{k-1}) Q(T_{k-1}) > 0\), while all premium payments for \(t > T_{k-1}\) vanish. Hence, \(\lim_{\lambda_k \to \infty} f(\lambda_k) = \text{ProtPV}(T_{k-1}) + (1 - R) D(T_{k-1}) Q(T_{k-1}) - S_k^{\text{mkt}} \text{PremPV}(T_{k-1}) > 0\).

Monotonicity follows because \(\frac{\partial Q(t)}{\partial \lambda_k} = -(t - T_{k-1}) Q(t) < 0\) for all \(t \in (T_{k-1}, T_k]\). Differentiating \(f(\lambda_k)\) shows that the protection leg increases with default probability while the premium leg decreases, ensuring \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \lambda_k} > 0\). By the intermediate value theorem, a unique root \(\lambda_k^*\) exists.

Example 2.1 (First Step Bootstrap Analytical Solution)

For a 1-year contract with annual payment (\(\Delta t = 1.0\)), observed spread \(S_1 = 60\text{ bp}\), recovery \(R = 40\%\), and risk-free rate \(r = 3\%\):

\[ \text{ProtPV} = (1 - 0.40) e^{-0.03 \times 0.5} (1 - e^{-\lambda_1 \times 1.0}) = 0.60 \times 0.98511 \times (1 - e^{-\lambda_1}) \] \[ \text{PremPV} = 0.0060 \times \left[ 1.0 \times e^{-0.03 \times 1.0} e^{-\lambda_1} + 0.5 \times e^{-0.03 \times 0.5} (1 - e^{-\lambda_1}) \right] \]

Equating \(\text{ProtPV} = \text{PremPV}\) and solving for \(e^{-\lambda_1}\) yields \(e^{-\lambda_1} \approx 0.9899\), which gives \(\lambda_1 \approx -\ln(0.9899) = 0.01015\) (101.5 bp). The linear approximation \(S_1 / (1 - R) = 60 / 0.60 = 100\text{ bp}\) approximates this within 1.5 bp.

3. Recovery Rate Ambiguity and the Spread-Hazard Coupling

A central structural feature of credit derivatives bootstrapping is the impossibility of disentangling default intensity \(\lambda\) from loss given default \((1 - R)\) using single-name CDS par quotes alone (O'Kane 2008).

Remark 2.1 (The Recovery Rate Under-identification Problem)

Market standard CDS pricing quotes assume an exogenous fixed recovery rate (typically \(R = 40\%\) for senior unsecured North American and European corporate debt). The continuous-time relationship \(S_{\text{par}} \approx \lambda (1 - R)\) demonstrates that an infinity of pairs \((\lambda, R)\) match the identical market quote. If actual recovery is 20% instead of 40%, the true hazard rate \(\lambda\) is lower:

\[ \lambda(R = 0.20) \approx \frac{S_{\text{par}}}{0.80} = 0.75 \times \lambda(R = 0.40) \]

Conversely, assuming higher recovery (\(R = 60\%\)) requires a higher implied hazard rate \(\lambda \approx S_{\text{par}} / 0.40 = 1.50 \times \lambda(R = 0.40)\). Recovery rate assumptions must therefore be held consistent when pricing cross-sectional derivatives or measuring capital structure arbitrage.

Lab 2 · CDS Hazard Rate Curve Bootstrapping

We hypothesize that bootstrapping a 6-tenor CDS curve (45 bp to 130 bp) with recovery \(R = 40\%\) generates strictly positive, increasing hazard rates from 74.7 bp at 1Y to 292.9 bp at 10Y. Lowering recovery to \(R = 20\%\) scales hazard rates by \((1 - 0.40)/(1 - 0.20) = 0.75\), while raising recovery to \(R = 60\%\) scales hazard rates by \((1 - 0.40)/(1 - 0.60) = 1.50\). 

"""Lab CR2-L02-1: Bootstrapping hazard rate curves from market CDS quotes.

Implements sequential root-finding for piecewise constant default intensities
and evaluates recovery rate sensitivity across market standard conventions.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

from scipy import optimize


def get_survival_prob(t: float, tenors: list[float], rates: list[float]) -> float:
    """Evaluate survival probability under piecewise constant hazard rates."""
    if t <= 0.0:
        return 1.0
    accum = 0.0
    prev_t = 0.0
    for j, tj in enumerate(tenors):
        if t <= prev_t:
            break
        duration = min(t, tj) - prev_t
        accum += rates[j] * duration
        prev_t = tj
        if t <= tj:
            break
    return math.exp(-accum)


def bootstrap_curve(
    tenors: list[float],
    spreads_bps: list[float],
    recovery: float,
    r: float = 0.03,
    coupon_freq: int = 4,
) -> tuple[list[float], list[float]]:
    """Bootstrap piecewise constant hazard rates from par spreads."""
    dt = 1.0 / coupon_freq
    hazard_rates: list[float] = []
    survival_probs: list[float] = []

    for i, tenor_val in enumerate(tenors):
        s_target = spreads_bps[i] / 10000.0
        known = list(hazard_rates)
        sub_tenors = tenors[: i + 1]
        num_periods = round(tenor_val * coupon_freq)
        times = [(k + 1) * dt for k in range(num_periods)]

        def error_fn(
            lam: float,
            k_rates: list[float] = known,
            t_steps: list[float] = times,
            a_tenors: list[float] = sub_tenors,
            s_tgt: float = s_target,
        ) -> float:
            c_rates = [*k_rates, lam]
            prem = 0.0
            prot = 0.0
            for k, tk in enumerate(t_steps):
                t_prev = t_steps[k - 1] if k > 0 else 0.0
                df_k = math.exp(-r * tk)
                df_mid = math.exp(-r * 0.5 * (t_prev + tk))
                q_prev = get_survival_prob(t_prev, a_tenors, c_rates)
                q_k = get_survival_prob(tk, a_tenors, c_rates)
                prem += dt * df_k * q_k + 0.5 * dt * df_mid * (q_prev - q_k)
                prot += (1.0 - recovery) * df_mid * (q_prev - q_k)
            return prot - s_tgt * prem

        sol = float(optimize.brentq(error_fn, 1e-6, 3.0))
        hazard_rates.append(sol)
        survival_probs.append(get_survival_prob(tenor_val, sub_tenors, hazard_rates))

    return hazard_rates, survival_probs


def main() -> None:
    tenors = [1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 10.0]
    market_quotes = [45.0, 60.0, 75.0, 95.0, 115.0, 130.0]  # basis points
    r = 0.03

    rates_40, surv_40 = bootstrap_curve(tenors, market_quotes, recovery=0.40, r=r)
    rates_20, _surv_20 = bootstrap_curve(tenors, market_quotes, recovery=0.20, r=r)
    rates_60, _surv_60 = bootstrap_curve(tenors, market_quotes, recovery=0.60, r=r)

    print("CDS Hazard Rate Bootstrapping (Base Recovery R = 40%, r = 3%):")
    print("  Tenor (yr) | Par Quote (bp) | Hazard Rate lambda (bp) | Survival Q(T) | 5Y Fwd Intensity")
    print("-------------+----------------+-------------------------+---------------+------------------")

    for i, t in enumerate(tenors):
        print(
            f"    {t:4.1f}     |     {market_quotes[i]:6.1f}     |         {rates_40[i] * 10000:6.1f}          |"
            f"    {surv_40[i]:6.4f}   |      {rates_40[i] * 10000:6.1f} bp"
        )

    print("\nRecovery Rate Sensitivity Analysis (lambda ~ s / (1 - R)):")
    print("  Tenor (yr) | lambda (R=20%) | lambda (R=40%) | lambda (R=60%) | Ratio (R=60% / R=20%)")
    print("-------------+----------------+----------------+----------------+----------------------")
    for i, t in enumerate(tenors):
        r20 = rates_20[i] * 10000
        r40 = rates_40[i] * 10000
        r60 = rates_60[i] * 10000
        ratio = r60 / r20
        print(f"    {t:4.1f}     |    {r20:6.1f} bp   |    {r40:6.1f} bp   |    {r60:6.1f} bp   |        {ratio:4.2f}x")

    # Invariants
    assert all(r > 0 for r in rates_40)
    for i in range(len(surv_40) - 1):
        assert surv_40[i] > surv_40[i + 1]


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks

The piecewise-constant hazard rate model produces non-smooth, saw-toothed forward intensity curves. When the market CDS spread curve inverts sharply—as observed during distressed episodes when short-term protection spikes above long-term spreads—the bootstrapped forward intensity \(\lambda_k\) on subsequent intervals can become negative. A negative hazard rate implies that survival probability increases over time (\(Q(T_k) > Q(T_{k-1})\)), violating no-arbitrage.

In practice, practitioners address inverted curves by using constrained optimization or smooth spline interpolations (such as monotonic cubic Hermite splines on cumulative hazard rates) to enforce \(\lambda(t) \ge 0\) across the entire horizon.

Misconceptions

  • A flat CDS term structure implies that the probability of default is constant in each year.

    A flat CDS spread term structure implies a constant hazard rate (default intensity) \(\lambda\). Because the cumulative survival probability decays exponentially (\(Q(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\)), the marginal probability of default in year \(k\), given by \(Q(k-1) - Q(k) = e^{-\lambda(k-1)}(1 - e^{-\lambda})\), strictly decreases over time because fewer surviving entities remain at risk.

  • Bootstrapped hazard rates are model-independent empirical observations.

    Bootstrapped intensities depend fundamentally on the assumed recovery rate convention, the risk-free discount curve, the payment frequency, and the accrual interpolation scheme. A change in the benchmark interest rate curve directly shifts the bootstrapped hazard rates even when market CDS quotes remain unchanged.

  • A steeper CDS curve always implies increasing distress for the reference entity.

    Healthy investment-grade issuers typically exhibit upward-sloping CDS curves because long-term credit uncertainty is naturally higher than near-term default risk. In contrast, extreme distress is characterized by an inverted CDS curve, where near-term default risk outweighs long-term uncertainty.

Problem set

Given bootstrapped hazard rates \(\lambda_1 = 0.0120\) on \([0, 1]\), \(\lambda_2 = 0.0180\) on \((1, 3]\), and \(\lambda_3 = 0.0240\) on \((3, 5]\), calculate the cumulative survival probability \(Q(5)\) and the 5-year cumulative default probability.

Worked solution

The cumulative integrated hazard is \(\int_0^5 \lambda(s) ds = \lambda_1 (1 - 0) + \lambda_2 (3 - 1) + \lambda_3 (5 - 3) = 0.0120 \times 1 + 0.0180 \times 2 + 0.0240 \times 2 = 0.0120 + 0.0360 + 0.0480 = 0.0960\). The survival probability is \(Q(5) = \exp(-0.0960) \approx 0.90846\) (90.85%). The cumulative default probability is \(\mathbb{Q}(\tau \le 5) = 1 - Q(5) \approx 0.09154\) (9.15%).

A 1-year CDS trades at 80 bp and a 2-year CDS trades at 110 bp on the same obligor. Assuming annual coupon payment, zero interest rates (\(r = 0\)), and recovery \(R = 40\%\), calculate the bootstrapped hazard rates \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\).

Worked solution

With \(r = 0\), \(\text{ProtPV}_1 = (1 - R)(1 - Q_1) = 0.60(1 - Q_1)\) and \(\text{PremPV}_1 = S_1 [Q_1 + 0.5(1 - Q_1)] = 0.0080 [0.5 + 0.5 Q_1]\). Equating gives \(0.60 - 0.60 Q_1 = 0.0040 + 0.0040 Q_1 \implies 0.6040 Q_1 = 0.5960 \implies Q_1 = 0.98675\). Thus \(\lambda_1 = -\ln(0.98675) \approx 0.01333\) (133.3 bp). For 2-year, \(\text{ProtPV}_2 = 0.60(1 - Q_2)\) and \(\text{PremPV}_2 = 0.0110 [Q_1 + Q_2 + 0.5(1 - Q_2)]\). Solving for \(Q_2\) and using \(Q_2 = Q_1 e^{-\lambda_2}\) yields \(\lambda_2 \approx 0.0238\) (238 bp).

Suppose market consensus for senior unsecured recovery increases from 40% to 50% following a restructuring reform. By what percentage does the bootstrapped hazard rate change for a firm whose CDS spread remains fixed at 150 bp?

Worked solution

Under the continuous approximation \(S \approx \lambda (1 - R)\), \(\lambda_1 = \frac{S}{1 - 0.40} = \frac{S}{0.60}\) and \(\lambda_2 = \frac{S}{1 - 0.50} = \frac{S}{0.50}\). The ratio is \(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} = \frac{0.60}{0.50} = 1.20\). Therefore, the implied hazard rate increases by exactly \(+20\%\) to offset the higher recovery assumption.

Prove that a downward-sloping CDS curve (\(S_1 > S_2\)) can produce a negative forward hazard rate \(\lambda_2 < 0\) if the term structure drop exceeds a threshold determined by the recovery rate.

Worked solution

For two annual tenors with \(r = 0\), the 2-year par spread is a weighted average of the first year par spread and the forward spread: \(S_2 \approx \frac{S_1 Q_1 + S_{1,2} Q_1 e^{-\lambda_2}}{Q_1 + Q_1 e^{-\lambda_2}}\). If \(S_2 < S_1\), the forward spread \(S_{1,2}\) must be lower than \(S_2\). If \(S_2\) is sufficiently below \(S_1\) such that the required premium leg exceeds the maximum possible default protection in period 2, the required forward default intensity satisfies \(\lambda_2 = \frac{S_{1,2}}{1 - R} < 0\). Survival probability would then satisfy \(Q_2 > Q_1\), violating the monotonicity of probability measures.

Prove that under deterministic interest rates, the order in which tenors are bootstrapped (from shortest to longest) is strictly necessary and sufficient for finding a unique piecewise-constant hazard curve.

Worked solution

At tenor \(T_k\), the survival curve \(Q(t)\) for \(t \le T_{k-1}\) depends exclusively on \(\{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{k-1}\}\). The pricing equation at \(T_k\) contains only one unknown variable: \(\lambda_k\). The system of equations is strictly lower triangular with respect to \(\{\lambda_k\}_{k=1}^M\). Because Theorem 2.1 proves that each diagonal entry has a unique strictly monotonic root, forward substitution (sequential bootstrapping) is both necessary (due to dependency on history) and sufficient to uniquely determine the full hazard curve.

Show that the sensitivity of the bootstrapped survival probability \(Q(T_k)\) with respect to an earlier hazard rate \(\lambda_j\) (\(j < k\)) is proportional to the duration of interval \(j\).

Worked solution

From \(Q(T_k) = \exp(-\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i \Delta T_i)\), taking the partial derivative with respect to \(\lambda_j\) gives \(\frac{\partial Q(T_k)}{\partial \lambda_j} = -\Delta T_j \exp(-\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i \Delta T_i) = -\Delta T_j Q(T_k)\). Dividing by \(Q(T_k)\) yields the log-derivative \(\frac{\partial \ln Q(T_k)}{\partial \lambda_j} = -\Delta T_j\), confirming that the sensitivity is exactly equal to the length of interval \(j\).

During the European sovereign debt crisis in 2011, Greek 1-year sovereign CDS traded above 3,500 bp while 5-year CDS traded near 1,800 bp. Explain how this extreme curve inversion affected bootstrapping algorithms and how dealers quoted contracts.

Worked solution

An inverted curve of this magnitude caused standard piecewise-constant bootstrapping algorithms to fail because forward survival probabilities exceeded 1.0 (negative forward hazard rates). Dealers responded by quoting contracts entirely on points upfront with fixed 100 bp or 500 bp running coupons, shifting from par-spread models to cash-dollar settlement models based on estimated time-to-restructuring.

Contrast the hazard curves bootstrapped from corporate CDS against hazard curves implied by corporate bond yield spreads (Duffie & Singleton 1999). What explains the systemic discrepancy between synthetic and cash credit curves?

Worked solution

Cash corporate bond yield spreads incorporate tax considerations, repo financing costs, and bond market illiquidity premia. Consequently, hazard curves bootstrapped directly from bond yield spreads typically imply higher default intensities than CDS hazard curves during normal periods, but lower intensities during credit crunches when repo financing freezes. This spread difference is the CDS–bond basis.

Evaluate the impact of the standard ISDA 40% recovery assumption on distressed credit names. When a firm's equity drops toward zero and restructuring is imminent, why does the 40% assumption break down empirically?

Worked solution

For distressed entities, secondary bond market prices frequently trade at 15 to 25 cents on the dollar, indicating expected recoveries well below 40%. Imposing a 40% recovery in the bootstrapper forces the implied hazard rate to artificially high levels (sometimes exceeding 100% per annum). Market participants replace standard 40% assumptions with bespoke recovery estimates derived from deliverable bond quotes.

Formulate a continuous-time spline bootstrapping model for cumulative hazard rates. How does penalizing curvature (\(\int (\lambda''(t))^2 dt\)) resolve the forward rate discontinuities of piecewise-constant bootstrapping?

Worked solution

Parameterize cumulative hazard \(\Lambda(t) = \int_0^t \lambda(s) ds\) as a cubic spline with knots at market tenors, subject to monotonicity constraints \(\Lambda'(t) \ge 0\). The objective minimizes pricing error plus a smoothing penalty \(\alpha \int_0^T (\Lambda'''(t))^2 dt\). This guarantees that the instantaneous forward hazard rate \(\lambda(t) = \Lambda'(t)\) is strictly continuous and non-negative, eliminating artificial jump shocks at contract maturity boundaries.

Investigate the interaction between stochastic interest rates and hazard rate curve bootstrapping. Under what conditions is the assumption of independence between default intensity and interest rates empirically invalidated?

Worked solution

Empirical macroeconomic data shows a pronounced negative correlation between risk-free interest rates and corporate default intensities: during recessions, central banks cut benchmark policy rates while corporate defaults surge. As derived by Brigo & Mercurio (2007), neglecting this negative correlation leads to overestimation of protection leg present values and distorts long-dated hazard curve bootstrapping.

Design a dual-calibration framework that simultaneously bootstraps the survival curve \(Q(t)\) and the expected recovery rate \(R(t)\) by incorporating digital default swaps and recovery locks.

Worked solution

A digital default swap pays a fixed unit payout upon default regardless of recovery, isolating \(Q(t)\). Standard CDS pays \((1 - R)\). Combining digital swap quotes (which pin down \(\text{DigitalPV} = \int_0^T D(u) dQ(u)\)) with standard CDS quotes allows the model to uniquely identify the survival curve \(Q(t)\) and the implied recovery rate \(R(t)\) simultaneously without exogenous recovery assumptions.

Retrieval quiz

  1. Why is hazard curve bootstrapping solved sequentially from the shortest tenor to the longest tenor?

    Options
    The cash flows of a \(T_k\) CDS contract up to \(T_{k-1}\) depend only on prior intensities. Fixing prior intensities isolates \(\lambda_k\) as the unique single-variable root.

  2. Under a piecewise-constant hazard rate \(\lambda(t) = \lambda_k\) for \(t \in (T_{k-1}, T_k]\), how does survival probability evolve?

    Options
    Cumulative survival probability is \(Q(t) = Q(T_{k-1}) e^{-\lambda_k (t - T_{k-1})}\), exhibiting exponential decay on each piecewise segment.

  3. If the market recovery convention for a corporate CDS is changed from 40% to 20%, what happens to the bootstrapped hazard rate for a fixed spread?

    Options
    From \(S \approx \lambda (1 - R)\), \(\lambda \approx S / (1 - R)\). When recovery drops from 40% to 20%, loss given default rises from 0.60 to 0.80. The implied intensity becomes \(S / 0.80 = 0.75 \times (S / 0.60)\), a 25% decrease.

  4. What numerical phenomenon occurs when a market CDS term structure is severely inverted?

    Options
    A sharp downward drop in CDS quotes across consecutive tenors forces the forward intensity to turn negative to pull down cumulative protection payments, violating the axiom of non-decreasing default risk.

  5. What is the standard market recovery assumption for senior unsecured debt of North American and European corporate entities?

    Options
    ISDA and Markit standard models fix senior corporate recovery at 40% (and subordinated financial debt typically at 20%).

  6. If \(\lambda_1 = 0.0100\) on \([0, 1]\) and \(\lambda_2 = 0.0200\) on \((1, 2]\), what is the survival probability \(Q(2)\)?

    Options
    Survival probability is the exponential of the negative sum of integrated hazard rates: \(\exp(-(0.0100 \times 1 + 0.0200 \times 1)) = \exp(-0.0300) \approx 0.9704\).

  7. Which root-finding algorithm is standard for single-dimensional CDS hazard rate bootstrapping?

    Options
    Brent's method guarantees robust bracketing convergence with superlinear speed for 1D monotonic continuous root-finding.

  8. What is the shape of the default intensity curve for a typical solvent investment-grade corporate obligor?

    Options
    Solvent investment-grade firms possess ample near-term liquidity and cash flows; their credit curves slope upward due to long-term business cycle risks and leverage drift.
