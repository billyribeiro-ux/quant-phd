One-Factor Gaussian Copula & Portfolio Credit Loss

Latent asset return modeling, conditional default probability factorization, Vasicek large homogeneous portfolio limit distribution, analytical credit Value-at-Risk, and tail dependence analysis.

Portfolios of corporate credit risk exhibit joint default clustering that linear correlation cannot capture. The one-factor Gaussian copula proposed by David X. Li (2000) revolutionized credit risk management by mapping multi-dimensional default times to standard normal distributions driven by a single systematic market factor. This formulation yields closed-form loss distributions for large homogeneous portfolios (Vasicek 1987) and powers regulatory capital allocation under Basel II/III. Understanding its mechanics and mathematical limitations is fundamental to synthetic CDO pricing and modern risk management.

1. The Latent Variable Framework and Sklar's Theorem

When managing a credit portfolio of \(N\) reference entities, single-name marginal default probabilities \(p_i(t) = \mathbb{P}(\tau_i \le t)\) can be bootstrapped with high precision from single-name credit default swap spreads and hazard rate curves. However, calculating portfolio loss distributions requires the joint distribution of default times:

\[ F(\tau_1, \tau_2, \dots, \tau_N) = \mathbb{P}(\tau_1 \le t_1, \tau_2 \le t_2, \dots, \tau_N \le t_N) \]

By Sklar's Theorem (1959), any multivariate distribution function \(F\) can be decomposed into its marginal distribution functions \(F_i(t_i) = \mathbb{P}(\tau_i \le t_i)\) and a copula function \(C:[0,1]^N \to [0,1]\):

\[ F(t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N) = C\left(F_1(t_1), F_2(t_2), \dots, F_N(t_N)\right) \]
Definition 4.1 (One-Factor Gaussian Copula)

The one-factor Gaussian copula specifies that the default timing of obligor \(i\) (\(i = 1, \dots, N\)) is governed by a continuous latent variable \(X_i\) representing the standardized return on the firm's assets, driven by a common systematic market factor \(M\) and an idiosyncratic firm factor \(Z_i\):

\[ X_i = \sqrt{\rho_i}\, M + \sqrt{1 - \rho_i}\, Z_i \]

where:

  • \(M \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) is the systematic market factor common to all obligors in the economy.
  • \(Z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) is the idiosyncratic factor specific to firm \(i\), independent across obligors (\(Z_i \perp Z_j\) for \(i \ne j\)) and independent of \(M\) (\(Z_i \perp M\)).
  • \(\rho_i \in [0, 1)\) represents the asset correlation of firm \(i\) with the systematic factor.

Obligor \(i\) defaults before horizon \(T\) if and only if its latent asset variable falls below a calibrated default barrier \(K_i(T)\):

\[ \tau_i \le T \iff X_i \le K_i(T) \quad \text{where} \quad K_i(T) = \Phi^{-1}\left(p_i(T)\right) \]

where \(\Phi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and \(\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)\) is its inverse (the probit quantile function).

Under this linear factor model, the marginal distribution of each latent asset return is standard normal:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_i] = 0, \quad \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \rho_i \operatorname{Var}(M) + (1 - \rho_i)\operatorname{Var}(Z_i) = \rho_i + (1 - \rho_i) = 1 \]

The pairwise linear correlation between the latent asset returns of distinct obligors \(i\) and \(j\) is:

\[ \operatorname{Corr}(X_i, X_j) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_i)\operatorname{Var}(X_j)}} = \mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sqrt{\rho_i} M + \sqrt{1-\rho_i} Z_i\right)\left(\sqrt{\rho_j} M + \sqrt{1-\rho_j} Z_j\right)\right] = \sqrt{\rho_i \rho_j} \]

When all obligors have equal sensitivity \(\rho_i = \rho\), the pairwise asset correlation is uniform: \(\operatorname{Corr}(X_i, X_j) = \rho\) for all \(i \ne j\).

2. Conditional Default Probability and Independence

The pivotal mathematical feature of the one-factor copula is conditional independence: conditional on a realization of the systematic market factor \(M = m\), the latent variables \(X_i\) and \(X_j\) are statistically independent.

Theorem 4.1 (Conditional Default Probability)

Conditional on the systematic factor realizing a value \(M = m\), the probability of default for obligor \(i\) prior to horizon \(T\) is given analytically by:

\[ p_i(m; T) = \mathbb{P}\left(\tau_i \le T \mid M = m\right) = \Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}\left(p_i(T)\right) - \sqrt{\rho_i}\, m}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_i}}\right) \]
Proof

Evaluating the event \(X_i \le K_i\) conditional on \(M = m\):

\[ \sqrt{\rho_i}\, m + \sqrt{1 - \rho_i}\, Z_i \le K_i \iff \sqrt{1 - \rho_i}\, Z_i \le K_i - \sqrt{\rho_i}\, m \iff Z_i \le \frac{K_i - \sqrt{\rho_i}\, m}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_i}} \]

Because \(Z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) is independent of \(M\), its conditional distribution given \(M = m\) remains standard normal. Applying the standard normal cumulative distribution function \(\Phi\) and substituting \(K_i = \Phi^{-1}(p_i)\) yields the result:

\[ p_i(m; T) = \mathbb{P}\left(Z_i \le \frac{\Phi^{-1}(p_i) - \sqrt{\rho_i}\, m}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_i}}\right) = \Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(p_i) - \sqrt{\rho_i}\, m}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_i}}\right) \]

3. Unconditional Expected Loss Invariance (The Tower Property)

A fundamental identity of credit risk modeling is that the unconditional expected loss of a credit portfolio is strictly invariant to the asset correlation parameter \(\rho\).

Theorem 4.2 (Correlation Invariance of Expected Loss)

Let a portfolio consist of \(N\) obligors with notional weights \(w_i \ge 0\) (\(\sum w_i = 1\)), marginal default probabilities \(p_i\), and deterministic recovery rates \(R_i\). The total fractional portfolio loss is:

\[ L = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i (1 - R_i) \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_i \le T\}} \]

Then for any correlation parameter \(\rho \in [0, 1)\), the unconditional expected loss satisfies:

\[ \mathbb{E}[L] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \mathbb{E}[L \mid M = m] \, \phi(m) \, dm = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i (1 - R_i) p_i \]

where \(\phi(m) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-m^2/2}\) is the standard normal probability density function.

Proof

By the law of total expectation (Tower Property) and linearity of expectations:

\[ \mathbb{E}[L] = \mathbb{E}_M\left[ \mathbb{E}[L \mid M] \right] = \mathbb{E}_M\left[ \sum_{i=1}^N w_i (1 - R_i) \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_i \le T\}} \mid M\right] \right] = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i (1 - R_i) \mathbb{E}_M\left[ p_i(M) \right] \]

Evaluating \(\mathbb{E}_M[p_i(M)]\):

\[ \mathbb{E}_M[p_i(M)] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(p_i) - \sqrt{\rho_i}\, m}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_i}}\right) \phi(m) \, dm \]

Let \(M \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) be independent. The integrand is \(\mathbb{P}(Z \le \frac{K_i - \sqrt{\rho_i} M}{\sqrt{1-\rho_i}} \mid M)\). The integral is the unconditional probability:

\[ \mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{1 - \rho_i}\, Z + \sqrt{\rho_i}\, M \le K_i\right) = \mathbb{P}(X_i \le K_i) = \Phi(K_i) = p_i \]

Hence \(\mathbb{E}_M[p_i(M)] = p_i\) identically, irrespective of \(\rho_i\).

4. The Large Homogeneous Portfolio (LHP) Limit & Vasicek Distribution

In a landmark paper, Vasicek (1987, 2002) demonstrated that as the number of portfolio obligors grows large (\(N \to \infty\)), idiosyncratic risk is completely diversified away conditional on \(M\), yielding an analytical closed-form loss distribution.

Theorem 4.3 (Vasicek Large Homogeneous Portfolio Distribution)

Assume a portfolio of \(N\) obligors with equal weights \(w_i = 1/N\), common marginal default probability \(p\), identical recovery rate \(R\), and uniform pairwise asset correlation \(\rho \in (0, 1)\). As \(N \to \infty\), the cumulative distribution function of fractional portfolio loss \(L \in [0, 1 - R]\) converges almost surely to:

\[ F_L(x) = \mathbb{P}(L \le x) = \Phi\left(\frac{\sqrt{1 - \rho}\, \Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{1 - R}\right) - \Phi^{-1}(p)}{\sqrt{\rho}}\right), \quad 0 < x < 1 - R \]

Inverting the distribution function yields the analytical Value-at-Risk at confidence level \(\alpha\):

\[ \operatorname{VaR}_\alpha(L) = (1 - R) \, \Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(p) + \sqrt{\rho}\, \Phi^{-1}(\alpha)}{\sqrt{1 - \rho}}\right) \]
Regulatory Heritage: The Basel II/III Capital Accord

The regulatory capital requirement for credit risk under the Basel Internal Ratings-Based (IRB) approach is derived directly from Theorem 4.3. Regulators set the capital requirement \(K_{\text{reg}}\) equal to the 99.9% Value-at-Risk minus the expected loss:

\[ K_{\text{reg}} = \operatorname{VaR}_{0.999}(L) - \mathbb{E}[L] = (1 - R)\left[ \Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(p) + \sqrt{\rho}\, \Phi^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1 - \rho}}\right) - p \right] \]

5. Computational Lab: One-Factor Gaussian Copula and LHP VaR

The following production lab evaluates a 125-name credit portfolio under the one-factor Gaussian copula using Gauss-Legendre quadrature. It numerically tests the correlation invariance of expected loss and calculates the Vasicek 99.0% and 99.9% VaR quantiles across increasing asset correlations:

Lab 4: One-Factor Gaussian Copula Loss Distribution & LHP VaR

Hypothesis: Systematic asset correlation redistributes portfolio loss dispersion into extreme tails without changing unconditional expected loss, which remains exactly equal to (1 - R) * p.

"""Lab CR2-L04-1: One-factor Gaussian copula loss distribution and Large Homogeneous Portfolio (LHP) VaR.

Demonstrates Li (2000) latent factor conditioning, verifies unconditional expected loss invariance,
and quantifies tail risk expansion under increasing asset correlation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats


def main() -> None:
    # Portfolio parameters
    num_names = 125
    default_prob = 0.05  # 5.0% 5Y cumulative default probability
    recovery = 0.40  # 40% recovery
    correlations = [0.01, 0.10, 0.20, 0.35, 0.50]

    # Gauss-Legendre quadrature over systematic market factor M
    num_points = 80
    nodes, weights = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(num_points)
    m_nodes = nodes * 5.0
    w_nodes = weights * 5.0

    inv_p = float(stats.norm.ppf(default_prob))
    inv_99 = float(stats.norm.ppf(0.99))
    inv_999 = float(stats.norm.ppf(0.999))

    print(f"One-Factor Gaussian Copula Loss Distribution (N={num_names}, p=5.0%, R=40%):")
    print("  Correlation rho | Expected Loss | Loss Vol (std) |  99.0% LHP VaR | 99.9% LHP VaR")
    print("------------------+---------------+----------------+----------------+---------------")

    for rho in correlations:
        sigma_idio = math.sqrt(max(1e-10, 1.0 - rho))
        cond_p = stats.norm.cdf((inv_p - math.sqrt(rho) * m_nodes) / sigma_idio)
        loss_m = (1.0 - recovery) * cond_p
        phi_m = stats.norm.pdf(m_nodes)

        exp_loss = float(np.sum(loss_m * phi_m * w_nodes))
        loss_sq = float(np.sum((loss_m**2) * phi_m * w_nodes))
        loss_std = math.sqrt(max(0.0, loss_sq - exp_loss**2))

        # Analytical Vasicek / LHP quantiles
        p_99 = float(stats.norm.cdf((inv_p + math.sqrt(rho) * inv_99) / sigma_idio))
        var_99 = (1.0 - recovery) * p_99

        p_999 = float(stats.norm.cdf((inv_p + math.sqrt(rho) * inv_999) / sigma_idio))
        var_999 = (1.0 - recovery) * p_999

        print(
            f"       {rho:4.2f}       |     {exp_loss * 100:5.2f}%     |     "
            f"{loss_std * 100:5.2f}%      |     {var_99 * 100:5.2f}%     |    {var_999 * 100:5.2f}%"
        )

        # Invariant: Expected loss is invariant to correlation rho
        assert abs(exp_loss - (1.0 - recovery) * default_prob) < 1e-4

    print("\nTower Property Verification:")
    print("  E[Loss] = (1 - R) * p = (1 - 0.40) * 0.05 = 3.00% across all correlation values.")
    print("  Correlation governs the dispersion and tail shape of portfolio losses, not the mean.")


main()

The numerical results verify that unconditional expected loss is exactly 3.00% across all correlation levels (from rho=0.01 to rho=0.50), confirming the Tower Property. As asset correlation increases from 0.01 to 0.50, loss volatility expands nearly tenfold (from 0.62% to 5.91%) and the 99.9% LHP VaR quantile increases from 5.38% to 46.66%, illustrating tail risk concentration in correlated credit portfolios.

6. Where the Canon Breaks: Tail Independence and Correlation Conflation

Canon Break 4.1 (Asymptotic Tail Independence of the Gaussian Copula)

The most catastrophic theoretical flaw of the Gaussian copula is that for any correlation parameter \(\rho < 1\), the bivariate Gaussian copula possesses zero tail dependence:

\[ \lambda_L = \lim_{u \to 0^+} \mathbb{P}(U_1 \le u \mid U_2 \le u) = \lim_{u \to 0^+} \frac{C_{\text{Gauss}}(u, u; \rho)}{u} = 0, \quad \forall \rho \in [0, 1) \]

In words: no matter how high the calibrated asset correlation \(\rho\), the probability that firm 2 defaults in an extreme tail quantile given that firm 1 has defaulted vanishes asymptotically to zero!

In financial markets, systemic crises are defined by non-zero tail clustering: when Lehman Brothers defaulted, the conditional default probability of other investment banks skyrocketed. The Gaussian copula treats such joint crises as mathematically impossible extreme draws from thin Gaussian tails.

Canon Break 4.2 (Asset Correlation vs Default Correlation vs Spread Correlation)

Traders and analysts often conflate three completely distinct correlation metrics:

  1. Asset Correlation (\(\rho\)): The latent Gaussian variable correlation. Unobservable, inferred from equity returns or credit tranches. Typical investment-grade range: \(0.15\) to \(0.35\).
  2. Default Indicator Correlation (\(\rho_D\)): The Pearson correlation between discrete default events \(\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_1 \le T\}}\) and \(\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_2 \le T\}}\): \[ \rho_D = \frac{\mathbb{P}(\tau_1 \le T, \tau_2 \le T) - p^2}{p(1 - p)} = \frac{C_{\text{Gauss}}(p, p; \rho) - p^2}{p(1 - p)} \] Because default is a rare event (\(p \ll 1\)), \(\rho_D\) is dramatically smaller than \(\rho\). For \(p = 0.05\) and \(\rho = 0.30\), \(\rho_D \approx 0.048\)—an order of magnitude smaller than \(\rho\)!
  3. Spread Correlation (\(\rho_S\)): The empirical correlation of daily mark-to-market CDS spread changes. Often exceeds \(0.60\)–\(0.80\) during risk-off episodes, yet has no direct 1-to-1 equivalence with asset correlation \(\rho\).

Misconceptions

  • Higher asset correlation increases expected portfolio loss.

    By the Tower Property (Theorem 4.2), expected loss is strictly constant across all values of \(\rho\). Correlation alters the variance and skewness of the loss distribution, not the mean.

  • Asset correlation equals joint default probability.

    An asset correlation of \(\rho = 0.30\) does not mean there is a 30% chance of joint default. The joint default probability for \(p = 0.05\) at \(\rho = 0.30\) is less than 0.5%.

  • Calibrating marginal single-name CDS spreads guarantees accurate portfolio joint tail risk.

    By Sklar's theorem, marginals can be coupled with any copula. An analyst can fit marginal CDS curves with zero error, yet if the copula has zero tail dependence (as the Gaussian copula does), the probability of catastrophic simultaneous defaults is severely understated.

Problem set

An obligor has a 5-year marginal default probability \(p = 0.05\) and asset correlation \(\rho = 0.20\). Compute the conditional default probability \(p(m)\) when the macroeconomic factor takes value \(m = -2.0\) (recession) and \(m = +2.0\) (expansion).

Worked solution

Default barrier is \(K = \Phi^{-1}(0.05) \approx -1.64485\). Sensitivity parameters: \(\sqrt{\rho} = \sqrt{0.20} \approx 0.44721\), \(\sqrt{1 - \rho} = \sqrt{0.80} \approx 0.89443\). For \(m = -2.0\): \(p(-2.0) = \Phi\left(\frac{-1.64485 - 0.44721(-2.0)}{0.89443}\right) = \Phi\left(\frac{-0.75042}{0.89443}\right) = \Phi(-0.83899) \approx 0.2007\) (20.07%). For \(m = +2.0\): \(p(+2.0) = \Phi\left(\frac{-1.64485 - 0.89443}{0.89443}\right) = \Phi(-2.83899) \approx 0.0023\) (0.23%). In a severe downturn, default probability increases fourfold from 5% to 20.07%.

For two obligors with identical marginal default probability \(p = 0.05\) and asset correlation \(\rho = 0.20\), the bivariate normal probability is \(C(0.05, 0.05; 0.20) \approx 0.00624\). Compute the Pearson default indicator correlation \(\rho_D\).

Worked solution

Default indicator variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(D_i) = p(1 - p) = 0.05(0.95) = 0.0475\). Covariance is \(\operatorname{Cov}(D_1, D_2) = \mathbb{P}(D_1 = 1, D_2 = 1) - p^2 = 0.00624 - 0.00250 = 0.00374\). Default indicator correlation is \(\rho_D = \frac{0.00374}{0.0475} \approx 0.0787\) (7.87%). Discretizing continuous asset returns reduces the apparent correlation from 20.0% to 7.87%.

For a portfolio with \(N = 100\), \(p = 0.04\), and recovery \(R = 0.40\), calculate the unconditional expected loss under \(\rho = 0.05\) and \(\rho = 0.40\).

Worked solution

By Theorem 4.2 (Tower Property), \(\mathbb{E}[L] = (1 - R) \cdot p = (1 - 0.40) \cdot 0.04 = 0.0240\) (2.40%). Because \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty p(m; \rho) \phi(m) dm = p\) for any valid correlation \(\rho\), the expected loss is exactly 2.40% under both \(\rho = 0.05\) and \(\rho = 0.40\).

Prove that the lower tail dependence coefficient \(\lambda_L = \lim_{u \to 0^+} \frac{C_{\text{Gauss}}(u, u; \rho)}{u}\) of a bivariate Gaussian copula with correlation \(\rho \in [0, 1)\) is identically zero.

Worked solution

Conditioning on \(X_2 = z\), \(X_1 \mid (X_2 = z) \sim \mathcal{N}(\rho z, 1 - \rho^2)\). The joint probability is \(\int_{-\infty}^x \Phi\left(\frac{x - \rho z}{\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}}\right) \phi(z) dz\). Differentiating numerator and denominator by L'Hôpital's rule with respect to \(x\) yields the leading term \(\Phi\left(x \sqrt{\frac{1 - \rho}{1 + \rho}}\right)\). Because \(\rho < 1\), the coefficient \(\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}} > 0\). As \(x \to -\infty\), this term converges to \(\Phi(-\infty) = 0\), establishing that \(\lambda_L = 0\).

Derive the asymptotic variance of fractional portfolio loss \(\lim_{N \to \infty} \operatorname{Var}(L)\) under uniform pairwise default correlation \(\rho_D\) and explain why non-zero default correlation prevents complete risk diversification.

Worked solution

The variance of fractional loss is \(\operatorname{Var}(L) = \frac{(1 - R)^2}{N^2} \left[ N p(1 - p) + N(N - 1) \rho_D p(1 - p) \right] = (1 - R)^2 p(1 - p) \left[ \frac{1}{N} + \frac{N - 1}{N} \rho_D \right]\). Taking the limit as \(N \to \infty\): \(\lim_{N \to \infty} \operatorname{Var}(L) = (1 - R)^2 p(1 - p) \rho_D\). Idiosyncratic risk \(\frac{1}{N}\) diversifies to zero, but systematic variance remains strictly positive whenever \(\rho_D > 0\).

Derive the Vasicek 99.9% Credit Value-at-Risk formula by inverting the cumulative distribution function \(F_L(\operatorname{VaR}_{0.999}) = 0.999\).

Worked solution

Setting \(\Phi\left(\frac{\sqrt{1 - \rho}\,\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\operatorname{VaR}}{1 - R}\right) - \Phi^{-1}(p)}{\sqrt{\rho}}\right) = 0.999\) and applying \(\Phi^{-1}\) to both sides yields \(\frac{\sqrt{1 - \rho}\,\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\operatorname{VaR}}{1 - R}\right) - \Phi^{-1}(p)}{\sqrt{\rho}} = \Phi^{-1}(0.999)\). Multiplying by \(\sqrt{\rho}\) and isolating \(\operatorname{VaR}\) gives \(\frac{\operatorname{VaR}}{1 - R} = \Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(p) + \sqrt{\rho}\,\Phi^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1 - \rho}}\right)\), so \(\operatorname{VaR}_{0.999} = (1 - R)\Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(p) + \sqrt{\rho}\,\Phi^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1 - \rho}}\right)\).

Calculate the analytical Vasicek 99.9% VaR for a portfolio with \(p = 0.03\), \(R = 0.40\), and \(\rho = 0.15\), and compare it with the portfolio's expected loss.

Worked solution

Normal quantiles: \(\Phi^{-1}(0.03) \approx -1.88079\), \(\Phi^{-1}(0.999) \approx 3.09023\). Standardized quantile argument: \(z = \frac{-1.88079 + \sqrt{0.15}(3.09023)}{\sqrt{0.85}} = \frac{-1.88079 + 1.19685}{0.92195} = \frac{-0.68394}{0.92195} \approx -0.74184\). Conditional default rate is \(\Phi(-0.74184) \approx 0.22909\). \(\operatorname{VaR}_{0.999} = (1 - 0.40) \times 0.22909 \approx 0.13745\) (13.75%). Expected loss is \((1 - 0.40) \times 0.03 = 1.80\%\). The 99.9% tail risk exceeds expected loss by a factor of 7.6x.

Under the Basel corporate IRB formula, an obligor with \(p = 0.001\) receives \(\rho = 23.41\%\) whereas an obligor with \(p = 0.05\) receives \(\rho = 12.98\%\). What empirical economic rationale justifies assigning lower asset correlation to higher-risk obligors?

Worked solution

Empirical corporate finance research indicates that distress in high-quality firms is overwhelmingly driven by systemic macroeconomic downturns (high systematic correlation), whereas distress in speculative-grade firms is heavily driven by idiosyncratic firm-specific managerial, operational, and financial errors. Regulators assign lower asset correlation to distressed credits to reflect this higher idiosyncratic variance share.

Explain why Gauss-Legendre quadrature with 60 nodes evaluates the unconditional tranche expectation \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty g(m) \phi(m) dm\) with machine precision (\(10^{-14}\)) while Monte Carlo simulation with 100,000 paths produces standard errors around 0.10%.

Worked solution

Gauss-Legendre quadrature integrates smooth, analytic functions with exponential spectral convergence \(O(e^{-c K})\). For \(K = 60\), error drops below \(10^{-14}\). In contrast, Monte Carlo relies on pseudo-random sampling with root-N convergence \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\). For \(N = 100,000\), sampling noise is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{100,000}} \approx 0.00316\) (32 bp), creating significant stochastic pricing noise.

Connect the one-factor Gaussian copula to the credit-spread puzzle introduced in CR1. How does the systematic latent factor \(M\) provide a microfoundation for the market-wide risk premium observed in single-name CDS spreads?

Worked solution

Under risk-neutral pricing, the systematic factor \(M\) shifts by the market price of credit risk \(\gamma\): \(M^{\mathbb{Q}} = M^{\mathbb{P}} - \gamma\). This drift shift scales up all conditional default probabilities simultaneously: \(p^{\mathbb{Q}}(m) = \Phi\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(p) - \sqrt{\rho}(m - \gamma)}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right) > p^{\mathbb{P}}(m)\). In the unconditional expectation, \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[L] > \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[L]\). This gap between risk-neutral and physical default losses is precisely the systematic credit risk premium that explains the credit-spread puzzle.

Evaluate the systemic risk implications of the Gaussian copula's zero lower tail dependence (\(\lambda_L = 0\)). Why did reliance on this copula lead risk managers to underestimate portfolio capital requirements prior to the 2008 crash?

Worked solution

Because \(\lambda_L = 0\), the Gaussian copula implies that the probability of simultaneous extreme distress across multiple institutions vanishes as the quantile horizon becomes extreme. In real financial panics, counterparty exposures, common asset holdings, and fire-sale contagion generate heavy lower-tail clustering (\(\lambda_L > 0\)). Risk managers relying on Gaussian copula VaR models held insufficient equity capital to absorb joint catastrophic defaults, resulting in systemic insolvency when correlated defaults occurred.

Formulate a regime-switching Gaussian copula where the economy transitions between a low-correlation regime (\(\rho_{\text{low}} = 0.15\)) and a crisis regime (\(\rho_{\text{high}} = 0.50\)). Show how this mixture model introduces pseudo-tail dependence while preserving analytical tractability.

Worked solution

Let the regime variable \(S \in \{0, 1\}\) have stationary probabilities \(\pi_0\) and \(\pi_1 = 1 - \pi_0\). The mixture copula is \(C(u, v) = \pi_0 C_{\text{Gauss}}(u, v; \rho_{\text{low}}) + \pi_1 C_{\text{Gauss}}(u, v; \rho_{\text{high}})\). Although each component copula has \(\lambda_L = 0\) at the mathematical limit \(u \to 0\), for finite stress quantiles \(u \in [0.001, 0.05]\), the high-correlation regime \(\rho_{\text{high}}\) generates substantial joint default mass \(\pi_1 C_{\text{Gauss}}(u, u; \rho_{\text{high}})\). This mixture structure allows practitioners to price senior tranches and compute stressed VaR without discarding the computational speed of 1D Gaussian quadrature.

Retrieval quiz

  1. In the one-factor Gaussian copula \(X_i = \sqrt{\rho} M + \sqrt{1-\rho} Z_i\), what is the pairwise correlation \(\operatorname{Corr}(X_i, X_j)\) between distinct obligors \(i \ne j\)?

    Options
    \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \mathbb{E}[(\sqrt{\rho} M)(\sqrt{\rho} M)] = \rho \operatorname{Var}(M) = \rho\). Since \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = 1\), correlation is \(\rho\).

  2. When the systematic market factor \(M\) realizes a severe downturn value \(m \to -\infty\), what happens to the conditional default probability \(p(m)\)?

    Options
    Because \(-\sqrt{\rho} m \to +\infty\), the argument inside \(\Phi(\cdot)\) approaches \(+\infty\), so \(p(m) \to 1\).

  3. What is the effect of increasing asset correlation \(\rho\) on the unconditional expected loss \(\mathbb{E}[L]\) of a credit portfolio?

    Options
    By the Tower Property \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[L \mid M]] = (1 - R) p\), expected loss is strictly constant regardless of \(\rho\).

  4. In the Vasicek Large Homogeneous Portfolio limit, what allows the loss distribution to become deterministic conditional on the systematic factor \(M\)?

    Options
    Conditional on \(M\), defaults are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables, so the sample average converges almost surely to the conditional mean \(p(m)\) as \(N \to \infty\).

  5. What is the lower tail dependence coefficient \(\lambda_L\) of the bivariate Gaussian copula for any correlation \(\rho < 1\)?

    Options
    The Gaussian copula has zero asymptotic tail dependence (\(\lambda_L = 0\)) for any \(\rho < 1\).

  6. Why is default indicator correlation \(\rho_D\) substantially lower than asset correlation \(\rho\) for investment-grade credits?

    Options
    Discretizing continuous latent variables into extreme-tail binary events (\(p = 0.01\) to \(0.05\)) severely shrinks the Pearson correlation coefficient.

  7. The Basel II/III Internal Ratings-Based (IRB) capital requirement formula is derived from which mathematical quantile of the Vasicek model?

    Options
    Basel IRB sets regulatory capital to the 99.9% 1-year Vasicek loss quantile minus unconditional expected loss.

  8. When using numerical quadrature to price derivatives dependent on the one-factor Gaussian copula, why is Gauss-Legendre quadrature preferred over standard Monte Carlo simulation?

    Options
    1D integration over the systematic factor is smooth and converges exponentially with Gauss-Legendre quadrature, avoiding Monte Carlo sampling noise.
