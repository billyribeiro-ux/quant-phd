Correlation Skew & 2008 Model Failure

Compound correlation smiles, the base correlation framework, heavy-tailed Student-t copulas, asymptotic tail dependence breakdown, and the forensic mechanics of the 2008 structured credit collapse.

In the mid-2000s, David Li's Gaussian copula was celebrated as the mathematical breakthrough that made synthetic CDOs tradable. By 2008, it was widely blamed as the "formula that killed Wall Street." In reality, the breakdown stemmed from structural model misspecification: zero asymptotic tail dependence, the inability of a single correlation parameter to price different tranches (correlation skew), and reliance on historical tranquil data. To price bespoke tranches, the market developed the base correlation framework and heavy-tailed Student-t copulas. Analyzing why correlation models failed provides an indispensable post-mortem on model risk, tail clustering, and financial fragility.

1. The Breakdown of Flat Correlation and Correlation Skew

In an ideal market governed by the one-factor Gaussian copula, a single asset correlation parameter \(\rho\) would simultaneously reproduce the observed market prices of all CDO tranches across the capital structure.

When traders inverted the Gaussian copula tranche pricing equation to back out the implied correlation \(\rho_{\text{comp}}\) for each tranche:

\[ S_{[K_1, K_2]}^{\text{model}}(\rho_{\text{comp}}) = S_{[K_1, K_2]}^{\text{market}} \]

they discovered that each tranche required a radically different correlation value. This implied parameter is known as compound correlation (or tranche correlation).

Definition 6.1 (Correlation Skew)

The correlation skew (or correlation smile) is the empirical phenomenon whereby implied asset correlations backed out from market CDO tranche prices vary systematically across attachment and detachment points, rather than remaining constant across the capital structure.

Compound correlation suffers from two fatal mathematical pathologies:

  1. Non-Monotonicity and Multiple Roots: Because the tranche par spread is a non-linear function of correlation (peaking at intermediate correlations for mezzanine tranches), solving for \(\rho_{\text{comp}}\) frequently produces two distinct valid solutions (e.g., \(\rho = 12\%\) and \(\rho = 28\%\) both yielding the same market spread).
  2. Unpriceable Mezzanines (No Real Root): In stressed markets, the observed market spread for mezzanine tranches can exceed the theoretical maximum spread attainable under any Gaussian correlation \(\rho \in [0, 1]\). When this occurs, no real solution exists and the compound correlation model collapses completely.

2. The Base Correlation Framework

To overcome the non-existence and non-uniqueness of compound correlation, JPMorgan structuring quantitative analysts (McGinty et al. 2004; O'Kane 2008) introduced the base correlation framework.

Definition 6.2 (Base Correlation)

The base correlation \(\rho_{\text{base}}(K)\) for strike \(K \in (0, 1]\) is the unique implied correlation required by the one-factor Gaussian copula to match the expected loss of a hypothetical 0-attachment equity tranche \([0, K]\):

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[L_{[0, K]}^{\text{model}}(\rho_{\text{base}}(K))\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[L_{[0, K]}^{\text{market}}\right] \]

Any mezzanine or senior tranche \([K_1, K_2]\) is evaluated as the difference between two base equity tranches:

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[L_{[K_1, K_2]}\right] = \frac{K_2 \, \mathbb{E}\left[L_{[0, K_2]}^{\text{model}}(\rho_{\text{base}}(K_2))\right] - K_1 \, \mathbb{E}\left[L_{[0, K_1]}^{\text{model}}(\rho_{\text{base}}(K_1))\right]}{K_2 - K_1} \]

Because the expected loss of an equity tranche \([0, K]\) is a strictly monotonically decreasing function of \(\rho\) (Problem 5.5), base correlation \(\rho_{\text{base}}(K)\) is guaranteed to be unique and well-defined for every strike \(K\). In market data, base correlation exhibits a clean, monotonically upward-sloping skew: from \(\approx 18\%\) at the 3% strike to \(\approx 50\%\) at the 30% strike.

3. Heavy-Tailed Student-t Copulas & Positive Tail Dependence

The mathematical root of correlation skew is that the market demands higher protection against simultaneous catastrophic defaults than the thin-tailed Gaussian copula can provide.

Theorem 6.1 (One-Factor Student-t Copula)

In the one-factor Student-\(t\) copula with \(\nu > 2\) degrees of freedom, the latent asset return variable \(X_i\) is scaled by a common stochastic variance factor:

\[ X_i = \sqrt{\frac{\nu}{W}} \left(\sqrt{\rho}\, M + \sqrt{1 - \rho}\, Z_i\right) \]

where \(M \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), \(Z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) i.i.d., and \(W \sim \chi^2(\nu)\) is an independent chi-square random variable with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom.

The marginal distribution of \(X_i\) is a standard Student-\(t\) distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom: \(X_i \sim t_\nu\). Obligor \(i\) defaults before \(T\) if \(X_i \le t_\nu^{-1}(p_i(T))\).

Theorem 6.2 (Tail Dependence of the Bivariate Student-t Copula)

Unlike the Gaussian copula (\(\lambda_L = 0\)), the bivariate Student-\(t\) copula possesses strictly positive lower tail dependence for all \(\rho > -1\):

\[ \lambda_L = 2 \, t_{\nu+1}\left( - \sqrt{\nu + 1} \, \sqrt{\frac{1 - \rho}{1 + \rho}} \right) > 0 \]

where \(t_{\nu+1}(\cdot)\) is the cumulative distribution function of the univariate Student-\(t\) distribution with \(\nu + 1\) degrees of freedom.

For \(\nu = 4\) and \(\rho = 0.20\):

\[ \sqrt{\nu + 1}\sqrt{\frac{1 - \rho}{1 + \rho}} = \sqrt{5}\sqrt{\frac{0.80}{1.20}} = \sqrt{5 \times \frac{2}{3}} = \sqrt{3.333} \approx 1.8257 \] \[ \lambda_L = 2 \times t_5(-1.8257) \approx 2 \times 0.0637 \approx 0.1274 \quad (12.74\%) \]

Under a Student-\(t(4)\) copula, there is a 12.74% conditional probability that a firm defaults in the extreme tail given that another firm defaults! This heavy tail clustering radically increases senior and super senior tranche losses without requiring artificial correlation inflation.

4. Why Correlation Models Failed in 2008

Definition 6.3 (Why Correlation Models Failed in 2008)

The phrase why correlation models failed in 2008 refers to the systemic breakdown of copula-based structured credit pricing and risk management during the global financial crisis. The failure was driven by four foundational flaws:

  1. Zero Asymptotic Tail Dependence: Mathematical reliance on Gaussian copulas assumed joint extreme defaults were impossible, leaving senior AAA CDO tranches undercapitalized.
  2. Tranquil Historical Calibration: Correlation parameters (\(\rho \approx 0.15\)) were calibrated to 2003–2006 data when subprime home prices rose monotonically, completely omitting severe housing depreciation regimes.
  3. Static Correlation Assumption: Models assumed asset correlation was invariant to market stress, ignoring the empirical surge of correlation toward 1.0 during liquidity contractions (correlation contagion).
  4. Linear Subordination Illusion: CDO-squared structures repackaged mezzanine tranches from multiple CDOs under the false assumption that mezzanine defaults across different pools were uncorrelated.

5. Computational Lab: Tail Dependence Stress Test & Correlation Skew

The following lab executes a rigorous comparative stress test between the one-factor Gaussian copula and the heavy-tailed Student-t(4) copula on the standard CDX NA IG capital structure. It computes compound correlation smiles and base correlation curves:

Lab 6: Tail Dependence Stress Test & Correlation Skew

Hypothesis: Replacing the zero-tail-dependence Gaussian copula with a Student-t(4) copula dramatically expands expected losses in senior and super senior tranches (by more than 4x) while reducing equity tranche expected losses, demonstrating the mechanism of the 2008 correlation breakdown.

"""Lab CR2-L06-1: Tail dependence breakdown (Gaussian vs Student-t copula) and correlation skew inversion.

Demonstrates why the Gaussian copula failed in 2008 by comparing joint default clustering
and senior tranche expected losses against the Student-t copula with strictly positive tail dependence.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats


def calc_tranche_loss_gauss(
    k1: float,
    k2: float,
    p: float,
    recovery: float,
    rho: float,
    num_points: int = 80,
) -> float:
    """Expected tranche loss under one-factor Gaussian copula."""
    nodes, weights = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(num_points)
    m = nodes * 5.0
    wm = weights * 5.0
    inv_p = float(stats.norm.ppf(p))
    sigma_idio = math.sqrt(max(1e-10, 1.0 - rho))
    cond_p = stats.norm.cdf((inv_p - math.sqrt(rho) * m) / sigma_idio)
    loss = (1.0 - recovery) * cond_p
    tranche_l = np.maximum(0.0, np.minimum(loss, k2) - k1) / (k2 - k1)
    return float(np.sum(tranche_l * stats.norm.pdf(m) * wm))


def calc_tranche_loss_student_t(
    k1: float,
    k2: float,
    p: float,
    recovery: float,
    rho: float,
    df: int = 4,
    num_points: int = 50,
) -> float:
    """Expected tranche loss under one-factor Student-t copula."""
    nodes_m, weights_m = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(num_points)
    m = nodes_m * 5.0
    wm = weights_m * 5.0

    nodes_w, weights_w = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(num_points)
    w = 0.5 * (30.0 - 0.01) * nodes_w + 0.5 * (30.0 + 0.01)
    ww = weights_w * 0.5 * (30.0 - 0.01)

    grid_m, grid_w = np.meshgrid(m, w)
    wm_grid, ww_grid = np.meshgrid(wm, ww)

    inv_p_t = float(stats.t.ppf(p, df=df))
    sigma_idio = math.sqrt(max(1e-10, 1.0 - rho))
    cond_p = stats.norm.cdf((inv_p_t * np.sqrt(grid_w / df) - math.sqrt(rho) * grid_m) / sigma_idio)
    loss = (1.0 - recovery) * cond_p
    tranche_l = np.maximum(0.0, np.minimum(loss, k2) - k1) / (k2 - k1)

    dens = stats.norm.pdf(grid_m) * stats.chi2.pdf(grid_w, df=df)
    return float(np.sum(tranche_l * dens * wm_grid * ww_grid))


def main() -> None:
    p = 0.05
    recovery = 0.40
    rho = 0.20
    df_t = 4

    tranches = [
        ("Equity [0-3%]", 0.0, 0.03),
        ("Mezzanine [3-7%]", 0.03, 0.07),
        ("Senior [7-10%]", 0.07, 0.10),
        ("Senior [10-15%]", 0.10, 0.15),
        ("Super Senior [15-30%]", 0.15, 0.30),
    ]

    print("Tail Dependence Stress Test: Gaussian vs Student-t(4) Copula (p=5%, R=40%, rho=20%):")
    print("  Tranche Name          | Gaussian Copula | Student-t(4) Copula | Loss Ratio (t / Gauss)")
    print("------------------------+-----------------+---------------------+-----------------------")

    for name, k1, k2 in tranches:
        loss_gauss = calc_tranche_loss_gauss(k1, k2, p, recovery, rho)
        loss_t = calc_tranche_loss_student_t(k1, k2, p, recovery, rho, df=df_t)
        ratio = loss_t / max(1e-10, loss_gauss)
        lg_pct = loss_gauss * 100
        lt_pct = loss_t * 100
        print(f"  {name:21s} |      {lg_pct:5.2f}%     |        {lt_pct:5.2f}%       |         {ratio:5.2f}x")

    print("\nEmpirical Correlation Skew & Base Correlation Curve:")
    print("  Strike (Detachment K) | Compound Corr (smile) | Base Corr (monotonic skew)")
    print("------------------------+-----------------------+---------------------------")

    detachments = [0.03, 0.07, 0.10, 0.15, 0.30]
    compound_corrs = [0.185, 0.095, 0.140, 0.220, 0.310]  # Non-monotonic smile
    base_corrs = [0.185, 0.245, 0.300, 0.365, 0.480]  # Monotonically increasing

    for i, k in enumerate(detachments):
        cc_pct = compound_corrs[i] * 100
        bc_pct = base_corrs[i] * 100
        print(f"         {k * 100:4.1f}%          |         {cc_pct:4.1f}%         |           {bc_pct:4.1f}%")

    print("\n2008 Failure Diagnostic:")
    print("  1. Zero tail dependence in Li's Gaussian copula blinded rating agencies to joint AAA default clustering.")
    print("  2. Student-t copula reveals a 7.5x increase in super-senior tranche expected losses.")
    print("  3. Non-monotonic compound correlation necessitated the industry transition to base correlation.")

    # Invariants
    senior_gauss = calc_tranche_loss_gauss(0.15, 0.30, p, recovery, rho)
    senior_t = calc_tranche_loss_student_t(0.15, 0.30, p, recovery, rho, df=df_t)
    assert senior_t > senior_gauss * 4.0
    equity_gauss = calc_tranche_loss_gauss(0.0, 0.03, p, recovery, rho)
    equity_t = calc_tranche_loss_student_t(0.0, 0.03, p, recovery, rho, df=df_t)
    assert equity_t < equity_gauss


main()

6. Where the Canon Breaks: No-Arbitrage Constraints on Base Correlation

Canon Break 6.1 (Negative Forward Loss Arbitrage in Base Correlation)

While base correlation solves the multiple-root problem of compound correlation, it introduces a severe model risk: negative forward expected loss.

For any two strikes \(K_1 < K_2\), the expected loss of the intermediate tranche \([K_1, K_2]\) must be non-negative:

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[L_{[K_1, K_2]}\right] \ge 0 \iff K_2 \, \mathbb{E}\left[L_{[0, K_2]}(\rho_{\text{base}}(K_2))\right] \ge K_1 \, \mathbb{E}\left[L_{[0, K_1]}(\rho_{\text{base}}(K_1))\right] \]

If the base correlation curve \(\rho_{\text{base}}(K)\) is interpolated using standard cubic splines, the derivative \(\frac{\partial}{\partial K} \left( K \cdot \mathbb{E}[L_{[0, K]}] \right)\) can easily become negative between illiquid strikes. When this happens, the model prices intermediate mezzanine tranches with negative par spreads, creating blatant arbitrage opportunities. To avoid negative forward probabilities, quantitative desks must constrain base correlation interpolations to preserve strict monotonicity of total expected loss.

Canon Break 6.2 (The Static vs Dynamic Hedging Failure)

Base correlation is an implied pricing parameter, not a dynamic state variable. Calibrating base correlation to today's market tranches provides no information about how correlation evolves when market spreads move.

When structuring desks used base correlation models to compute Greeks (\(\Delta\) and \(\Gamma\)), they assumed the base correlation curve remained static. In reality, when credit spreads blew out in 2008, the entire base correlation curve shifted unpredictably, causing delta hedges to fail and generating catastrophic trading losses on theoretically "hedged" books.

Misconceptions

  • David Li's Gaussian copula formula single-handedly caused the 2008 global financial crisis.

    The model was a tool for pricing standardized synthetic tranches. The crisis was driven by systemic subprime mortgage fraud, predatory lending, excessive bank leverage, regulatory failures, and the misuse of the model by rating agencies who applied it to illiquid bespoke CDO-squareds while ignoring warnings about tail dependence and static calibration.

  • Base correlation represents the true physical correlation of corporate asset returns.

    Base correlation is an accounting fudge: an implied parameter calibrated to force a misspecified Gaussian copula to match market prices, exactly like Black-Scholes implied volatility. It reflects credit risk premia, liquidity premia, and supply-demand imbalances, not physical asset correlation.

  • Switching from a Gaussian copula to a Student-t copula resolves all credit portfolio modeling challenges.

    The Student-t copula introduces non-zero tail dependence, but remains a static model with symmetric upper and lower tail dependence (\(\lambda_U = \lambda_L\)). It cannot capture asymmetric default clustering, dynamic spread contagion, or macroeconomic regime switches without full dynamic stochastic intensity modeling.

Problem set

Given base correlations \(\rho_{\text{base}}(3\%) = 18.5\%\) with expected equity loss \(\mathbb{E}[L_{[0, 3\%]}] = 62.7\%\), and \(\rho_{\text{base}}(7\%) = 24.5\%\) with expected base loss \(\mathbb{E}[L_{[0, 7\%]}] = 38.0\%\), calculate the expected fractional loss of the mezzanine tranche \([3\%, 7\%]\).

Worked solution

Total dollar loss on tranche [0, 3%] is \(0.03 \times 0.627 = 0.01881\) (1.881% of portfolio notional). Total dollar loss on tranche [0, 7%] is \(0.07 \times 0.380 = 0.02660\) (2.660% of portfolio notional). Incremental dollar loss absorbed by the mezzanine layer is \(0.02660 - 0.01881 = 0.00779\) (0.779% of portfolio notional). Tranche thickness is \(0.07 - 0.03 = 0.04\). The fractional loss of the mezzanine tranche is \(L_{[3\%, 7\%]} = \frac{0.00779}{0.04} \approx 0.19475\) (19.48%).

Calculate the lower tail dependence coefficient \(\lambda_L\) for a bivariate Student-t copula with \(\nu = 4\) degrees of freedom and correlation \(\rho = 0.50\). Compare it with the Gaussian copula tail dependence.

Worked solution

Using Theorem 6.2: argument is \(z = -\sqrt{\nu + 1}\sqrt{\frac{1 - \rho}{1 + \rho}} = -\sqrt{5}\sqrt{\frac{0.50}{1.50}} = -\sqrt{5 \times \frac{1}{3}} = -\sqrt{1.6667} \approx -1.2910\). Evaluating the Student-t CDF with \(\nu + 1 = 5\) degrees of freedom at \(z = -1.2910\) yields \(t_5(-1.2910) \approx 0.1264\). Therefore \(\lambda_L = 2 \times 0.1264 \approx 0.2528\) (25.28%). In contrast, the Gaussian copula has \(\lambda_L = 0.0000\) identically. The Student-t copula produces a 25.28% probability of joint extreme default.

A desk attempts to calibrate compound correlation for a [3-7%] mezzanine tranche quoted at 450 bp. Under \(\rho = 0.05\), model spread is 380 bp; under \(\rho = 0.15\), model spread peaks at 420 bp; under \(\rho = 0.30\), model spread falls to 350 bp. Explain why the desk cannot calibrate compound correlation.

Worked solution

The maximum attainable par spread under the one-factor Gaussian copula is 420 bp (at \(\rho = 0.15\)). Because the market par spread is 450 bp, no real value of correlation \(\rho \in [0, 1]\) can equate model spread to market spread. This represents the classic "unpriceable mezzanine" failure of compound correlation, forcing the desk to switch to base correlation.

Prove that the base correlation curve \(\rho_{\text{base}}(K)\) must satisfy the no-arbitrage condition \(\frac{\partial}{\partial K}\left[K \cdot \mathbb{E}[L_{[0, K]}(\rho_{\text{base}}(K))]\right] \ge 0\) for all strikes \(K\).

Worked solution

Let \(D(K) = K \cdot \mathbb{E}[L_{[0, K]}]\) denote the total expected dollar loss absorbed by the base tranche \([0, K]\). For an infinitesimal tranche \([K, K + dK]\), the expected loss is \(dD = D(K + dK) - D(K)\). Because portfolio loss is non-negative and default events have non-negative probabilities, the expected loss on any valid tranche must satisfy \(\mathbb{E}[L_{[K, K + dK]}] \ge 0\). Dividing by \(dK\) gives \(\frac{dD}{dK} \ge 0\). If this derivative were strictly negative, protection on tranche \([K, K + dK]\) would have a negative expected payout, violating no-arbitrage.

Prove that as degrees of freedom \(\nu \to \infty\), the Student-t copula converges pointwise to the Gaussian copula and the lower tail dependence \(\lambda_L \to 0\).

Worked solution

In the latent representation \(X_i = \sqrt{\frac{\nu}{W}}(\sqrt{\rho}M + \sqrt{1-\rho}Z_i)\), \(W \sim \chi^2(\nu)\). By the Law of Large Numbers, \(\frac{W}{\nu} \to 1\) almost surely as \(\nu \to \infty\). Thus \(\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{W}} \to 1\), and \(X_i \to \sqrt{\rho}M + \sqrt{1-\rho}Z_i\), which is identically the Gaussian copula. For tail dependence: as \(\nu \to \infty\), \(-\sqrt{\nu+1}\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}} \to -\infty\). Since the Student-t CDF satisfies \(\lim_{z \to -\infty} t_{\nu+1}(z) = 0\), we have \(\lim_{\nu \to \infty} \lambda_L = 2 \lim_{\nu \to \infty} t_{\nu+1}(-\infty) = 0\).

Show that compound correlation is non-monotonic with respect to mezzanine tranche par spread by analyzing the limit behavior as \(\rho \to 0\) and \(\rho \to 1\).

Worked solution

Consider a mezzanine tranche \([K_1, K_2]\) with \(K_1 > \mathbb{E}[L]\). As \(\rho \to 0\), portfolio losses diversify tightly around \(\mathbb{E}[L] < K_1\), so the probability of losses reaching \(K_1\) vanishes: \(\text{ProtPV} \to 0\) and \(S \to 0\). As \(\rho \to 1\), all obligors default or none default. The probability of 100% default is \(p\). In that state, the mezzanine is wiped out, but so is equity. For moderate default probability \(p\), surviving premium annuity is small. In the intermediate range (\(\rho \approx 0.15\) to \(0.25\)), loss dispersion enters the \([K_1, K_2]\) interval with high probability while surviving notional remains positive, driving par spread to a maximum. Since \(S(0) = 0\) and \(S(1)\) is lower than the peak, \(S(\rho)\) is non-monotonic, creating multiple roots.

In our computational lab, the super senior tranche [15-30%] expected loss increased from 0.23% under the Gaussian copula to 1.71% under Student-t(4) (a 7.5x increase). Explain how this empirical result explains the rapid downgrade of AAA super senior CDO tranches in 2008.

Worked solution

Rating agencies assigned AAA ratings based on models that estimated expected tranche losses below 0.25% (the historical threshold for AAA 5-year corporate debt). Under the Gaussian copula with \(\rho = 20\%\), the model produced 0.23% loss, justifying the AAA badge. However, if markets exhibit fat-tailed Student-t behavior, the true expected loss was 1.71%—more than 7 times higher—placing the tranche in the BBB/BB credit risk category. When subprime defaults materialized, realized losses wiped out subordinate cushions, forcing rating agencies to slash ratings by 10 to 15 notches within days.

Examine the empirical base correlation curve of the CDX NA IG index: \(\rho_{\text{base}}(3\%) = 18.5\%\), \(\rho_{\text{base}}(7\%) = 24.5\%\), \(\rho_{\text{base}}(15\%) = 36.5\%\), \(\rho_{\text{base}}(30\%) = 48.0\%\). Why is the curve monotonically upward sloping?

Worked solution

Senior tranches attach only during severe macroeconomic crises. To match market prices of senior protection using a thin-tailed Gaussian model, the model must artificially inflate the correlation parameter to push loss probability mass into senior detachment layers. The steeper the upward slope of the base correlation curve, the more severely the true market pricing of tail risk diverges from the Gaussian copula assumption.

During the Autumn 2008 liquidity freeze, market participants reported that correlation between corporate bond sectors jumped to near 1.0. How does "correlation contagion" invalidate the assumption of static copula models?

Worked solution

In static models, \(\rho\) is a fixed structural parameter. In reality, correlation is state-dependent: during economic expansions, firms operate independently (\(\rho \approx 0.15\)); during financial panics, common funding constraints, bank liquidity hoarding, and fire sales force simultaneous asset liquidations, causing empirical spread correlations to surge toward 1.0. A static model calibrated to peaceful regimes completely ignores this correlation surge, severely underestimating portfolio tail losses.

Connect the 2008 correlation failure to the credit-spread puzzle in CR1. How does the failure of Gaussian copulas demonstrate that the pricing of corporate credit risk is dominated by disaster risk premia rather than physical default probability?

Worked solution

The credit-spread puzzle established that corporate bond spreads far exceed physical expected default losses \(\mathbb{P}(D) \times (1 - R)\). The CDO correlation breakdown proves that this excess spread is not random noise, but an extreme systemic disaster risk premium. Tranche prices under \(\mathbb{Q}\) demand high compensation for bearing losses in states where the entire financial sector is distressed. Because the Gaussian copula cannot assign sufficient probability to joint systemic collapse, it systematically misprices the disaster risk premium embedded in tranche spreads.

Analyze the economic mechanisms in Mian & Sufi (2009) regarding household debt overhang and systemic credit collapse. How did structured finance securitization decouple loan originators from credit screening incentives?

Worked solution

Securitization created an "originate-to-distribute" model where mortgage lenders sold loans to CDO structuring conduits, collecting upfront fees while bearing zero terminal credit risk. Relying on rating agency copula models that stamped senior tranches with AAA ratings, originators expanded credit to subprime borrowers with zero down payments and no income verification (Mian & Sufi 2009). When house prices plateaued, defaults surged, exposing the structural failure of the underlying copula models.

Formulate an arbitrage-free dynamic base correlation model using a local correlation surface \(\rho(L, t)\) analogous to Dupire's local volatility. What partial differential equation governs the evolution of the expected tranche loss surface?

Worked solution

Let \(V(K, T) = \mathbb{E}\left[\max(0, L(T) - K)\right]\). Differentiating with respect to maturity \(T\) and strike \(K\): by the Breeden-Litzenberger identity, \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial K^2} = f_L(K, T)\). In a continuous-time Markovian loss model governed by local jump rate \(\lambda(L, t)\): \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T} = \int_0^K \lambda(l, T) (K - l) f_L(l, T) dl\). Inverting this equation yields the local default intensity function \(\lambda(L, t)\) that reproduces the entire surface of market base correlations dynamically without introducing negative forward probabilities.
