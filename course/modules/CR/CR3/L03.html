The Excess Bond Premium as an Economic Leading Indicator

Why do corporate bond yield spreads widen before macroeconomic downturns? Gilchrist and Zakrajšek (2012) resolved this puzzle by showing that the predictive power of credit spreads does not stem from expected default losses. Instead, virtually all forecasting power resides in the Excess Bond Premium (EBP)—a non-default spread residual reflecting the risk tolerance and capital health of financial intermediaries.

1Decomposing the GZ Credit Spread

Building on the micro-level synthetic Treasury matching developed in Lesson 2, the aggregate GZ spread at quarter \(t\) is decomposed additively into a predicted default-risk component and an orthogonal residual premium.

Definition 3.1 (Excess Bond Premium)

The excess-bond-premium (\(\text{EBP}_t\)) is the component of the aggregate Gilchrist–Zakrajšek credit spread \(S_t^{\text{GZ}}\) that remains after subtracting the component \(\hat{S}_t\) explained by firm-level distance-to-default and bond indenture characteristics:

\[ S_t^{\text{GZ}} = \hat{S}_t + \text{EBP}_t \]

where the predicted default-risk component is the cross-sectional average of expected bond-level spreads:

\[ \hat{S}_t = \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} \hat{s}_{it} = \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} \exp\left(\hat{\beta}' x_{it} + \hat{\gamma} \text{EDF}_{it} + \frac{1}{2}\hat{\sigma}_\epsilon^2\right) \]

and \(\text{EBP}_t = S_t^{\text{GZ}} - \hat{S}_t\).

Theorem 3.1 (Lognormal Retransformation and EBP Identification)

Let the micro bond pricing specification be \(\ln s_{it} = \beta' x_{it} + \gamma \text{EDF}_{it} + \epsilon_{it}\), where \(\epsilon_{it} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2)\). The conditional expectation of the micro spread in levels satisfies:

\[ \E[s_{it} \mid x_{it}, \text{EDF}_{it}] = \exp\left(\beta' x_{it} + \gamma \text{EDF}_{it} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_\epsilon^2\right) \]

The Excess Bond Premium \(\text{EBP}_t = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t} (s_{it} - \hat{s}_{it})\) is the sample cross-sectional mean of idiosyncratic pricing residuals transformed back to spread levels.

Proof

Because \(\ln s_{it} = z_{it} + \epsilon_{it}\) with \(z_{it} = \beta' x_{it} + \gamma \text{EDF}_{it}\) and \(\epsilon_{it}\) normally distributed, \(s_{it} = \exp(z_{it})\exp(\epsilon_{it})\) follows a lognormal distribution conditional on covariates. By the moment-generating function of the normal distribution, \(\E[\exp(\epsilon_{it})] = \exp(\frac{1}{2}\sigma_\epsilon^2)\). Therefore,

\[ \E[s_{it} \mid z_{it}] = \exp(z_{it}) \E[\exp(\epsilon_{it})] = \exp\left(z_{it} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_\epsilon^2\right) \]

Subtracting this expected level from observed spread \(s_{it}\) yields the micro-level pricing residual. Averaging across the \(N_t\) bonds traded in quarter \(t\) yields \(\text{EBP}_t = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t} s_{it} - \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t}\hat{s}_{it} = S_t^{\text{GZ}} - \hat{S}_t\), proving exact additive balance.

2Economic Interpretation of EBP: Intermediary Capital Constraints

Why does \(\text{EBP}_t\) fluctuate over time? In frictionless asset pricing, bond prices reflect the discounted value of promised cash flows adjusted for expected default. In modern credit markets, however, corporate bonds are held predominantly by specialized institutional investors and broker-dealer balance sheets (Gertler and Karadi 2015; Huang and Huang 2012, citing huang2012much).

When financial intermediaries experience capital erosion, regulatory leverage constraints tighten. As balance-sheet capacity contracts, dealers demand elevated compensation for bearing inventory and credit risk, widening credit spreads without any change in the actual default risk of underlying corporations. Thus, \(\text{EBP}_t\) acts as a direct market proxy for the shadow price of financial intermediary capital.

3Macroeconomic Forecasting Horse Race

Gilchrist and Zakrajšek (2012) evaluate the predictive power of \(\hat{S}_t\) versus \(\text{EBP}_t\) for future economic activity using multi-period forecasting regressions:

\[ \Delta^h Y_{t+h} = \alpha + \sum_{j=1}^p \phi_j \Delta Y_{t-j+1} + \beta_1 \hat{S}_t + \beta_2 \text{EBP}_t + \beta_3 \text{TermSpread}_t + u_{t+h} \]

where \(Y_t\) represents log real GDP or log industrial production, \(h\) is the forecast horizon (e.g., 4 quarters), and \(\text{TermSpread}_t = y_t(10) - y_t(3\text{m})\) is the benchmark Estrella and Mishkin (1998, citing estrella1998predicting) yield curve spread.

Example 3.1 (Empirical Horse Race Regression Results)

In a 4-quarter ahead GDP growth regression (\(h=4\)), the estimated coefficients and t-statistics reported by Gilchrist & Zakrajšek are:

PredictorCoefficientRobust t-statisticp-value

A 100-basis-point increase in EBP forecasts a \(0.85\%\) contraction in real GDP over the subsequent year with overwhelming statistical significance, whereas the default component \(\hat{S}_t\) is economically tiny and statistically indistinguishable from zero.

Lab 1 · EBP vs default component macro forecasting

Verifies that the Excess Bond Premium possesses strong predictive power for subsequent GDP growth, while the default-risk component demonstrates negligible explanatory power.

"""Lab CR3-L03-1: Excess Bond Premium Decomposition and Macroeconomic Forecasting.

SIMULATED: decomposes aggregate credit spread into default-risk compensation (S_hat)
and the Excess Bond Premium (EBP), and compares their out-of-sample predictive power
for future economic activity (Gilchrist & Zakrajšek 2012, AER).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import NamedTuple


class MacroQuarter(NamedTuple):
    q: str
    ebp: float
    s_hat: float
    term_spread: float
    growth: float


def main() -> None:
    # Stylized quarterly time series: 12 quarters covering an expansion and credit crunch
    data = [
        MacroQuarter("Q1", -35.0, 110.0, 2.2, 3.8),
        MacroQuarter("Q2", -40.0, 105.0, 2.0, 3.5),
        MacroQuarter("Q3", -25.0, 115.0, 1.6, 3.2),
        MacroQuarter("Q4", -10.0, 120.0, 1.2, 2.7),
        MacroQuarter("Q5", 15.0, 135.0, 0.5, 1.5),
        MacroQuarter("Q6", 60.0, 160.0, -0.2, -0.8),
        MacroQuarter("Q7", 110.0, 190.0, -0.6, -2.6),
        MacroQuarter("Q8", 145.0, 220.0, 0.1, -3.5),
        MacroQuarter("Q9", 90.0, 210.0, 1.4, -1.2),
        MacroQuarter("Q10", 30.0, 170.0, 2.1, 1.0),
        MacroQuarter("Q11", -15.0, 140.0, 2.4, 2.6),
        MacroQuarter("Q12", -30.0, 125.0, 2.3, 3.4),
    ]

    ebp_vals = [d.ebp for d in data]
    shat_vals = [d.s_hat for d in data]
    growth_vals = [d.growth for d in data]

    def simple_regression(x: list[float], y: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
        mean_x = sum(x) / len(x)
        mean_y = sum(y) / len(y)
        ss_xx = sum((xi - mean_x) ** 2 for xi in x)
        ss_xy = sum((xi - mean_x) * (yi - mean_y) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=False))
        ss_yy = sum((yi - mean_y) ** 2 for yi in y)
        beta = ss_xy / ss_xx if ss_xx > 0 else 0.0
        alpha = mean_y - beta * mean_x
        r2 = (ss_xy**2) / (ss_xx * ss_yy) if (ss_xx * ss_yy) > 0 else 0.0
        return alpha, beta, r2

    # Model 1: Growth on EBP
    _alpha_ebp, beta_ebp, r2_ebp = simple_regression(ebp_vals, growth_vals)

    # Model 2: Growth on S_hat (default component)
    _alpha_shat, beta_shat, r2_shat = simple_regression(shat_vals, growth_vals)

    print("Macroeconomic Forecasting: Excess Bond Premium vs Expected Default Component:")
    print("  Quarter | EBP (bp) | S_hat (bp) | Total GZ (bp) | Future 4Q GDP Growth (%)")
    print("----------+----------+------------+---------------+-------------------------")
    for d in data:
        gz_tot = d.ebp + d.s_hat
        print(
            f"    {d.q:3s}   |   {d.ebp:5.1f}  |    {d.s_hat:5.1f}   |     "
            f"{gz_tot:5.1f}     |          {d.growth:4.1f}%"
        )

    print("\nPredictive Regressions (Forward 4-Quarter Economic Growth):")
    print(f"  Model 1 (EBP only):    beta = {beta_ebp:7.4f}, R^2 = {r2_ebp * 100:4.1f}%")
    print(f"  Model 2 (S_hat only):  beta = {beta_shat:7.4f}, R^2 = {r2_shat * 100:4.1f}%")

    # In Gilchrist & Zakrajsek (2012), EBP carries substantially higher forecasting power than S_hat
    assert r2_ebp > r2_shat
    assert beta_ebp < 0.0


main()

4Where the Canon Breaks: The Invariance of Default Probabilities

Standard credit risk theory presumes that corporate yield spreads reflect forward-looking default expectations. If spreads were primarily default compensation, \(\hat{S}_t\) should dominate \(\text{EBP}_t\) in forecasting real activity. The total collapse of \(\hat{S}_t\)'s predictive power demonstrates that physical default risk adjusts contemporaneously or with a lag during recessions, whereas intermediary risk pricing shifts pre-emptively. Conflating total credit spreads with physical insolvency risk causes analysts to misjudge the timing and severity of turning points.

Misconceptions

  • A widening credit spread indicates that corporate borrowers are becoming more insolvent.

    Empirical evidence demonstrates that the majority of sudden credit spread surges reflect contractions in intermediary risk appetite (rising EBP) rather than immediate increases in borrower default rates. Actual corporate bankruptcies typically surge several quarters after credit spreads have peaked.

  • The Excess Bond Premium can be calculated simply by subtracting default rates from bond yields.

    EBP is not a simple yield minus default rate. It requires estimating a cross-sectional micro-level regression across thousands of individual bond issues, explicitly controlling for firm distance-to-default, bond duration, coupon structure, liquidity, and embedded call options.

  • The yield curve term spread makes the Excess Bond Premium redundant in macroeconomic forecasting.

    Horse race regressions prove that EBP and the Treasury term spread provide orthogonal macroeconomic information. The term spread reflects monetary policy expectations and term premia, while EBP captures financial intermediary balance-sheet capacity and corporate credit supply frictions. Both variables retain independent statistical significance when included together.

Problem set

Mechanics

P1 · Additive EBP Extraction

In a given quarter, the aggregate GZ credit spread is observed at \(S_t^{\text{GZ}} = 3.20\%\) (320 basis points). The micro-level regression predicted default-risk component is \(\hat{S}_t = 1.35\%\) (135 basis points). Compute the Excess Bond Premium in basis points.

Solution

By the fundamental additive decomposition \(S_t^{\text{GZ}} = \hat{S}_t + \text{EBP}_t\), we have \(\text{EBP}_t = S_t^{\text{GZ}} - \hat{S}_t = 3.20\% - 1.35\% = 1.85\%\), which equals exactly \(185\) basis points.

P2 · Retransformation Bias Correction

A regression models log credit spreads: \(\ln s_{it} = 0.50 + \epsilon_{it}\), where \(\epsilon_{it} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.16)\). Calculate the naive predicted spread \(\exp(0.50)\) and the corrected expected spread in levels \(\E[s_{it}]\).

Solution

The naive predicted spread is \(\exp(0.50) \approx 1.6487\). Because \(\epsilon_{it} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma^2 = 0.16\), the Jensen's inequality correction factor is \(\exp(0.5 \sigma^2) = \exp(0.08) \approx 1.0833\). The corrected expected spread in levels is \(\E[s_{it}] = \exp(0.50 + 0.08) = \exp(0.58) \approx 1.7860\). Omitting the variance adjustment underestimates the spread by approximately \(7.7\%\).

P3 · Forecasted GDP Impact

Using the forecasting equation \(\Delta Y_{t+4} = 0.025 - 0.85 \text{EBP}_t + 0.40 \text{TermSpread}_t\), compute the forecasted 1-year GDP growth when \(\text{EBP}_t = 0.020\) (200 basis points) and \(\text{TermSpread}_t = 0.010\) (100 basis points).

Solution

Substituting the values into the regression: \(\Delta Y_{t+4} = 0.025 - 0.85(0.020) + 0.40(0.010) = 0.025 - 0.0170 + 0.0040 = 0.0120\), or \(1.20\%\) annual GDP growth.

P4 · Proof of Orthogonality of EBP and Fitted Default Risk

Prove that under OLS estimation of the linear regression \(s_{it} = \beta' x_{it} + \epsilon_{it}\) with an intercept, the sample covariance between the predicted values \(\hat{s}_{it}\) and the residuals \(\hat{\epsilon}_{it}\) is identically zero: \(\sum_{i=1}^N \hat{s}_{it} \hat{\epsilon}_{it} = 0\).

Solution

Let \(\mathbf{X}\) denote the \(N \times K\) regressor matrix and \(\mathbf{s}\) the spread vector. The OLS normal equations require \(\mathbf{X}' \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = \mathbf{0}\). The fitted values vector is \(\hat{\mathbf{s}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\). Premultiplying residuals by transposed fitted values yields \(\hat{\mathbf{s}}' \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = (\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})' \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}' (\mathbf{X}' \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}) = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}' \mathbf{0} = 0\). Thus, the fitted default-risk component and the pricing residuals are strictly orthogonal in the cross-section.

P5 · Lognormal Mean Formula via Characteristic Function

Prove from first principles using Gaussian integration that if \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), then \(\E[e^Z] = \exp(\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\).

Solution

By definition of expectation, \(\E[e^Z] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^z \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(z - \mu)^2 / (2\sigma^2)} dz\). Let \(u = (z - \mu)/\sigma\), so \(z = \mu + \sigma u\) and \(dz = \sigma du\):

\[ \E[e^Z] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{\mu + \sigma u - u^2/2} du = e^\mu \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-(u^2 - 2\sigma u)/2} du \]

Completing the square in the exponent: \(u^2 - 2\sigma u = (u - \sigma)^2 - \sigma^2\). Substituting this gives:

\[ \E[e^Z] = e^{\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-(u - \sigma)^2/2} du = e^{\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2} \times 1 = \exp\left(\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) \]
P6 · Consistency of Aggregate EBP Under Cross-Sectional Dependence

Suppose pricing residuals satisfy \(\epsilon_{it} = \gamma_t + \nu_{it}\), where \(\gamma_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\gamma^2)\) is a common macroeconomic intermediary shock and \(\nu_{it}\) are i.i.d. idiosyncratic shocks. Prove that as the number of bonds \(N_t \to \infty\), the cross-sectional average \(\bar{\epsilon}_t\) converges to the systematic intermediary shock \(\gamma_t\).

Solution

The cross-sectional average is \(\bar{\epsilon}_t = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t}(\gamma_t + \nu_{it}) = \gamma_t + \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t}\nu_{it}\). Because \(\nu_{it}\) are independent across firms with mean zero and finite variance \(\sigma_\nu^2\), by Kolmogorov's Strong Law of Large Numbers, \(\lim_{N_t \to \infty} \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t}\nu_{it} = 0\) almost surely. Therefore, \(\lim_{N_t \to \infty} \bar{\epsilon}_t = \gamma_t\) almost surely. This proves that aggregating micro residuals completely diversifies idiosyncratic firm noise and isolates the systematic intermediary shock.

Empirical

P7 · Historical Behavior During the 2008 Financial Crisis

In October 2008 following the collapse of Lehman Brothers, the GZ spread spiked to \(580\text{ bp}\) while the fitted default component was \(220\text{ bp}\). Calculate the EBP and compare its percentage share of the total spread to its tranquil baseline of \(20\%\).

Solution

At the peak, \(\text{EBP} = 580 - 220 = 360\) basis points. The EBP share of the total spread was \(360 / 580 \approx 62.1\%\). Compared to its tranquil baseline share of \(20\%\), the share of non-default risk premia more than tripled, confirming that the Great Financial Crisis was primarily an intermediary liquidity and risk-bearing capacity crisis.

P8 · Evaluating Forecast Encompassing

Consider two models for 1-year GDP growth: Model A uses only \(\text{TermSpread}_t\) (\(R^2 = 0.18\)), while Model B includes both \(\text{TermSpread}_t\) and \(\text{EBP}_t\) (\(R^2 = 0.34\)). Conduct an F-test for the incremental explanatory power of \(\text{EBP}_t\) with \(T = 160\) quarterly observations.

Solution

The incremental F-statistic for 1 restriction is \(F = \frac{(R_B^2 - R_A^2) / 1}{(1 - R_B^2) / (T - K)} = \frac{(0.34 - 0.18) / 1}{(1 - 0.34) / (160 - 3)} = \frac{0.16}{0.66 / 157} = \frac{0.16}{0.004204} \approx 38.06\). The critical value for \(F(1, 157)\) at \(\alpha = 0.001\) is approximately \(11.2\). Because \(38.06 \gg 11.2\), we reject the null hypothesis that EBP has no incremental forecasting power at \(p < 10^{-8}\).

P9 · Lead-Lag Dynamics Across Business Cycle Peaks

Empirical lead-lag correlations show that \(\text{Corr}(\text{EBP}_t, \Delta Y_{t+4}) = -0.52\), whereas \(\text{Corr}(\hat{S}_t, \Delta Y_{t+4}) = -0.11\) and \(\text{Corr}(\hat{S}_{t+4}, \Delta Y_t) = -0.48\). Interpret the timing relationship between EBP, default risk, and output.

Solution

The strong correlation between current EBP and GDP growth 4 quarters forward (\(-0.52\)) indicates that EBP is a powerful leading indicator. In contrast, the fitted default risk component \(\hat{S}_t\) has little correlation with future output (\(-0.11\)), but future default risk \(\hat{S}_{t+4}\) is strongly correlated with past output contractions (\(-0.48\)). This proves that default risk is a lagging indicator that responds after real economic activity contracts.

Research

P10 · Intermediary Asset Pricing: He and Krishnamurthy (2013)

Connect the empirical behavior of EBP to the theoretical intermediary asset pricing model of He and Krishnamurthy (2013).

Solution

In He and Krishnamurthy (2013), marginal investors in risky assets are specialized financial intermediaries rather than representative households. Intermediaries face equity capital constraints. When intermediary equity is high, risk premia are low and stable. When an adverse shock impairs intermediary net worth, their effective risk aversion spikes nonlinearly, driving up risk premia on all intermediated assets. EBP directly measures this time-varying marginal value of intermediary wealth, explaining why it surges during financial sector distress.

P11 · Monetary Policy Surprises and EBP Response

Using the Gertler and Karadi (2015) high-frequency identification methodology, explain how an unexpected 25 bp monetary policy tightening affects EBP.

Solution

Gertler and Karadi (2015) show that high-frequency policy rate surprises (measured within 30-minute windows around FOMC announcements) generate an immediate, statistically significant jump in EBP of approximately 10 to 15 basis points. Because corporate default probabilities cannot change within a 30-minute window, the immediate widening of credit spreads proves that monetary policy transmits rapidly through financial intermediary risk tolerance and balance-sheet capacity rather than through immediate changes in fundamental default risk.

P12 · Real-Time Nowcasting vs Out-of-Sample Forecasting

Discuss the practical econometric challenges of using EBP in real-time macroeconomic nowcasting compared to revised historical backtests.

Solution

In historical backtests, the cross-sectional regression coefficients (\(\hat{\beta}, \hat{\gamma}\)) are estimated using the full sample. In real-time nowcasting, economists must re-estimate the Svensson yield curve and the cross-sectional panel regression using only data available up to quarter \(t\). Furthermore, secondary bond trade prices can become sparse during crises, requiring robust filtering to avoid illiquidity contamination. Nonetheless, out-of-sample studies verify that recursively estimated EBP preserves its robust leading indicator properties.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What does the Excess Bond Premium (\(\text{EBP}_t\)) capture economically?

    Options
    EBP isolates the non-default spread residual, which reflects intermediary risk aversion, leverage constraints, and credit supply conditions.

  2. In the Gilchrist & Zakrajšek (2012) forecasting horse race for future GDP growth, what was the primary empirical finding regarding \(\hat{S}_t\) and \(\text{EBP}_t\)?

    Options
    GZ demonstrated that the non-default risk premium (EBP) accounts for almost all forecasting power, while expected default risk has no incremental forecasting power.

  3. Why is the term \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma_\epsilon^2\) added when calculating expected micro credit spreads from log-linear regression estimates?

    Options
    For a lognormal variable \(Y = \exp(X)\) with \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), \(\E[Y] = \exp(\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\); omitting \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\) creates severe downward bias.

  4. How does the Estrella and Mishkin (1998) term spread compare with EBP in predicting economic recessions?

    Options
    Term spreads and EBP capture different financial channels (monetary policy stance vs private credit risk tolerance) and remain jointly significant in multivariate regressions.

  5. According to intermediary asset pricing models (e.g. He & Krishnamurthy 2013), what causes EBP to surge during a crisis?

    Options
    Losses erode intermediary equity capital, tightening regulatory and market leverage constraints and driving up required excess risk premia.

  6. What is the timing relationship between EBP and actual corporate bankruptcies across the business cycle?

    Options
    EBP reacts immediately to financial conditions, providing a 2 to 4 quarter leading signal, whereas corporate bankruptcies lag output contractions.

  7. If an economy enters a period with a flat yield curve and a sharply elevated EBP, what does the empirical evidence forecast for real GDP growth over the next year?

    Options
    Both a flat/inverted term spread and an elevated EBP are strong contractionary leading indicators, signalling significant recession risk.

  8. In a given quarter, the aggregate GZ spread is \(3.20\%\) and the fitted default component is \(1.35\%\). What is the Excess Bond Premium in basis points?

    By subtraction, \(\text{EBP} = 3.20\% - 1.35\% = 1.85\%\) or 185 basis points.

  9. Under the forecasting equation \(\Delta Y_{t+4} = 0.025 - 0.85 \text{EBP}_t + 0.40 \text{TermSpread}_t\), what is the forecasted GDP growth (in percent) when \(\text{EBP}_t = 0.020\) and \(\text{TermSpread}_t = 0.010\)?

    \(\Delta Y = 0.025 - 0.85(0.020) + 0.40(0.010) = 0.025 - 0.017 + 0.004 = 0.012\) or 1.20%.
