Credit Market Sentiment and Predictable Reversals

Under efficient markets, asset prices follow martingales: credit spread changes should be unpredictable noise. López-Salido, Stein, and Zakrajšek (2017) overturned this view by demonstrating that credit-market sentiment induces predictable mean-reversion. Elevated sentiment today systematically forecasts credit spread widening two to three years forward, which in turn acts as an endogenous contractionary shock that precipitates macroeconomic recessions.

1Quantifying Credit-Market Sentiment

Credit expansions frequently coincide with aggressive investor risk-taking and competitive erosion of lending standards. Rather than relying on subjective surveys, modern quantitative macro-credit constructs empirical sentiment proxies from market volume and pricing.

Definition 4.1 (Credit-Market Sentiment)

The credit-market-sentiment index \(\text{Sent}_t\) is an empirical metric capturing the degree to which credit risk is underpriced and credit standards are relaxed relative to historical fundamentals. Following López-Salido, Stein, and Zakrajšek (2017), a primary proxy is the high-yield issuance share \(\text{ISS}_t\):

\[ \text{ISS}_t = \frac{\text{HY}_t}{\text{HY}_t + \text{IG}_t} \]

where \(\text{HY}_t\) is the gross issuance volume of non-financial speculative-grade corporate bonds and \(\text{IG}_t\) is investment-grade issuance during quarter \(t\). An elevated \(\text{ISS}_t\) indicates buoyant sentiment, as low-credit-quality borrowers successfully tap public markets at low premia.

Composite sentiment measures combine the high-yield issuance share with compressed credit spreads and depressed Excess Bond Premium levels:

\[ \text{Sent}_t = w_1 z(\text{ISS}_t) - w_2 z(S_t) - w_3 z(\text{EBP}_t) \]

where \(z(\cdot)\) denotes cross-sectional or rolling time-series standardization, ensuring that elevated sentiment reflects both high speculative debt issuance and compressed risk premia.

2The Two-Stage Predictive Reversal Hypothesis

Under the Rational Expectations Hypothesis (REH), the change in credit spreads over any horizon \(h\), \(\Delta^h S_{t+h} = S_{t+h} - S_t\), should be orthogonal to all information known at time \(t\): \(\E[\Delta^h S_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\). The sentiment model directly violates this proposition through a two-stage mechanism.

Theorem 4.1 (Two-Stage Predictive Spread Reversal)

Empirical credit-market sentiment operates through a two-stage dynamic relationship:

  1. Stage 1 (Predictable Spread Widening): Elevated sentiment at time \(t\) predictably forecasts credit spread widening over multi-year horizons (\(h = 8\) to \(12\) quarters): \[ S_{t+h} - S_t = \alpha_h + \beta_h^{\text{rev}} \text{Sent}_t + \epsilon_{t+h}, \quad \text{with } \beta_h^{\text{rev}} > 0 \]
  2. Stage 2 (Subsequent Real Activity Contraction): Predictable spread widening drives subsequent contractions in real economic activity: \[ \Delta^k Y_{t+k} = \theta_k + \gamma_k^{\text{sent}} \text{Sent}_t + \sum_{j=1}^p \rho_j \Delta Y_{t-j+1} + u_{t+k}, \quad \text{with } \gamma_k^{\text{sent}} < 0 \] where \(Y_t\) is log real GDP and \(k \ge 8\) quarters.
Proof Outline (Behavioral Extrapolation Model)

Let credit investors form beliefs over future default intensities \(\lambda_{t+1}\) using diagnostic or extrapolative expectations (Bordalo, Gennaioli, and Shleifer 2018):

\[ \E_t^B[\lambda_{t+1}] = \E_t[\lambda_{t+1}] - \theta (\E_t[\lambda_{t+1}] - \E_{t-1}[\lambda_t]), \quad \theta > 0 \]

Following a sequence of low defaults, investors under-estimate future default risk: \(\E_t^B[\lambda_{t+1}] < \E_t[\lambda_{t+1}]\). Competitive bond pricing under risk neutrality implies compressed credit spread \(S_t \approx \E_t^B[\lambda_{t+1}] (1 - R)\). High-yield issuers rush to issue debt, raising \(\text{ISS}_t\). As future defaults materialize according to their true physical distribution \(\lambda_{t+h}\), beliefs must rationally regress to the mean. Therefore, future spread change satisfies \(\E_t[S_{t+h} - S_t] \approx (1 - R)[\E_t[\lambda_{t+h}] - \E_t^B[\lambda_{t+1}]] > 0\). The inevitable repricing of credit tightens financial accelerator constraints (Lesson 1), forcing disinvestment and depressing real output \(\Delta^k Y_{t+k} < 0\).

Lab 1 · Sentiment spread reversals and real activity contraction

Verifies that elevated credit sentiment forecasts statistically significant spread widening over an 8-quarter horizon and subsequent contraction in real GDP growth.

"""Lab CR3-L04-1: Credit Sentiment and Two-Stage Predictable Reversals.

SIMULATED: evaluates López-Salido, Stein & Zakrajšek (2017) hypothesis that elevated
credit-market sentiment (high HY issuance share, compressed spreads) predictably
forecasts credit spread widening and subsequent macroeconomic contractions.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import NamedTuple


class SentimentPeriod(NamedTuple):
    t: str
    hy_share: float
    spread: float
    fwd_spread_widening: float
    fwd_gdp: float


def main() -> None:
    # Stylized semi-annual credit cycle observations (10 periods, 5 years)
    periods = [
        SentimentPeriod("T1", 0.38, 120.0, 160.0, -1.5),
        SentimentPeriod("T2", 0.42, 105.0, 195.0, -2.8),
        SentimentPeriod("T3", 0.35, 135.0, 140.0, -0.8),
        SentimentPeriod("T4", 0.24, 210.0, 20.0, 1.4),
        SentimentPeriod("T5", 0.12, 340.0, -130.0, 4.5),
        SentimentPeriod("T6", 0.10, 360.0, -150.0, 4.8),
        SentimentPeriod("T7", 0.18, 260.0, -60.0, 3.6),
        SentimentPeriod("T8", 0.28, 175.0, 50.0, 2.2),
        SentimentPeriod("T9", 0.34, 130.0, 135.0, 0.2),
        SentimentPeriod("T10", 0.40, 110.0, 180.0, -2.0),
    ]

    hy_vals = [p.hy_share for p in periods]
    spr_widening = [p.fwd_spread_widening for p in periods]
    fwd_gdp = [p.fwd_gdp for p in periods]

    def ols(x: list[float], y: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
        mx = sum(x) / len(x)
        my = sum(y) / len(y)
        s_xx = sum((xi - mx) ** 2 for xi in x)
        s_xy = sum((xi - mx) * (yi - my) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=False))
        s_yy = sum((yi - my) ** 2 for yi in y)
        b = s_xy / s_xx if s_xx > 0 else 0.0
        a = my - b * mx
        r2 = (s_xy**2) / (s_xx * s_yy) if (s_xx * s_yy) > 0 else 0.0
        return a, b, r2

    # Stage 1: Predictable spread widening from high-yield issuance share
    _a1, b_stage1, r2_stage1 = ols(hy_vals, spr_widening)

    # Stage 2: Real GDP contraction from sentiment
    _a2, b_stage2, r2_stage2 = ols(hy_vals, fwd_gdp)

    print("Two-Stage Credit Sentiment Predictive Dynamics (López-Salido et al. 2017):")
    print("  Period | HY Issuance Share | Spread (bp) | Fwd Spread Change (bp) | Fwd GDP Growth (%)")
    print("---------+-------------------+-------------+------------------------+-------------------")
    for p in periods:
        hy_pct = p.hy_share * 100
        spr = p.spread
        spr_chg = p.fwd_spread_widening
        gdp = p.fwd_gdp
        print(
            f"   {p.t:4s}  |       {hy_pct:4.1f}%       |    {spr:5.1f}    |         "
            f"{spr_chg:+5.1f}          |       {gdp:+4.1f}%"
        )

    print("\nTwo-Stage Regression Results:")
    print(
        f"  Stage 1 (Spread Reversal):    beta = {b_stage1:+6.1f} bp per 100% HY share, R^2 = {r2_stage1 * 100:4.1f}%"
    )
    print(
        f"  Stage 2 (Output Contraction): beta = {b_stage2:+6.2f} % GDP per 100% HY share, "
        f"R^2 = {r2_stage2 * 100:4.1f}%"
    )

    # High sentiment positively predicts future spread widening and negatively predicts future GDP growth
    assert b_stage1 > 0.0
    assert b_stage2 < 0.0
    assert r2_stage1 > 0.60


main()

3Where the Canon Breaks: The Random Walk Hypothesis for Credit Spreads

Under the orthodox Efficient Market Hypothesis (Fama 1970), bond market yields incorporate all publicly available information instantly. A predictive regression of the form \(S_{t+h} - S_t = \alpha + \beta \text{Sent}_t + \epsilon_{t+h}\) should yield \(\beta = 0\). The robust empirical rejection (\(t > 3.5\)) demonstrates that credit markets are subject to systematic over-extrapolation, institutional reach-for-yield, and slow-moving capital. Treating corporate credit spreads as martingales blinds risk models to predictable multi-year credit busts.

Misconceptions

  • Narrow credit spreads always indicate that the macroeconomy is fundamentally sound and credit risk is low.

    Unusually tight credit spreads often reflect excessive market sentiment and aggressive search-for-yield rather than pristine fundamentals. Historically, the periods with the narrowest credit spreads (such as 2006–2007) preceded the sharpest economic collapses, as low spreads encouraged reckless debt accumulation.

  • Credit spread reversals can be reliably traded with short-term 3-month options.

    Sentiment-driven reversals operate over long, multi-year horizons (typically 2 to 3 years). Over 1 to 4 quarters, sentiment displays substantial momentum. Attempting to time spread widening using short-dated derivative contracts incurs severe negative carry and roll decay before the reversal materializes.

  • High-yield issuance surges because risky firms suddenly experience positive productivity shocks.

    Micro-level data shows that speculative-grade issuance surges during credit booms are driven by market demand (investor willingness to accept low yields and weak covenants) rather than sudden improvements in issuer productivity (Mian and Sufi 2009).

Problem set

Mechanics

P1 · High-Yield Issuance Share Calculation

In a given quarter, US corporations issue \(\$45\text{B}\) in high-yield debt and \(\$105\text{B}\) in investment-grade debt. Calculate the high-yield issuance share \(\text{ISS}_t\). If the historical average is \(18\%\) with standard deviation \(6\%\), compute the standardized z-score.

Solution

Total corporate debt issuance is \(45 + 105 = \$150\text{B}\). The high-yield issuance share is \(\text{ISS}_t = 45 / 150 = 0.30\) (30.0%). The standardized z-score is \(z = (0.30 - 0.18) / 0.06 = 0.12 / 0.06 = +2.00\), indicating that speculative issuance is two standard deviations above its long-term average.

P2 · Composite Sentiment Index Construction

Let the standardized sentiment variables be \(z(\text{ISS}) = +1.80\), \(z(S) = -1.50\), and \(z(\text{EBP}) = -1.20\). Compute the composite sentiment index using equal weights \(w_1 = w_2 = w_3 = 1/3\).

Solution

The composite sentiment index is \(\text{Sent}_t = \frac{1}{3} z(\text{ISS}) - \frac{1}{3} z(S) - \frac{1}{3} z(\text{EBP}) = \frac{1}{3}(1.80) - \frac{1}{3}(-1.50) - \frac{1}{3}(-1.20) = 0.60 + 0.50 + 0.40 = +1.50\). The positive value of \(+1.50\) indicates buoyant credit-market sentiment.

P3 · Forward Spread Widening Forecast

Using the estimated reversal equation \(S_{t+8} - S_t = 0.10 + 0.65 \text{Sent}_t\) (in percentage points), calculate the predicted change in credit spreads over the next 8 quarters when current sentiment is \(\text{Sent}_t = +1.50\).

Solution

Substituting into the predictive model: \(S_{t+8} - S_t = 0.10 + 0.65(1.50) = 0.10 + 0.975 = 1.075\) percentage points, or approximately \(107.5\) basis points of spread widening.

Proof

P4 · Rejection of the Martingale Property for Spreads

Let \(S_t\) be a stochastic process adapted to filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t\). Prove that if \(\beta_h^{\text{rev}} \ne 0\) in the linear regression \(\E[S_{t+h} - S_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \alpha_h + \beta_h^{\text{rev}} \text{Sent}_t\), where \(\text{Sent}_t \in \mathcal{F}_t\), then \(S_t\) cannot be a martingale with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_t\).

Solution

By definition, a process \(S_t\) is an \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-martingale if and only if \(\E[S_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = S_t\) almost surely for all \(h > 0\), which is equivalent to \(\E[S_{t+h} - S_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\) almost surely. If \(\beta_h^{\text{rev}} \ne 0\) and \(\text{Sent}_t\) has non-degenerate variance, \(\E[S_{t+h} - S_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \alpha_h + \beta_h^{\text{rev}} \text{Sent}_t \ne 0\) with positive probability. Thus, \(S_t\) violates the conditional expectation condition, proving that credit spreads are not martingales under the physical measure.

P5 · Unbiasedness of Two-Stage Least Squares Under Orthogonality

Consider the two-stage model: (1) \(\Delta^h S_{t+h} = \beta \text{Sent}_t + u_{t+h}\), and (2) \(\Delta^h Y_{t+h} = \gamma \widehat{\Delta^h S}_{t+h} + v_{t+h}\). Prove that if \(\E[\text{Sent}_t u_{t+h}] = 0\) and \(\E[\text{Sent}_t v_{t+h}] = 0\), the 2SLS estimator \(\hat{\gamma}_{\text{2SLS}}\) is a consistent estimator of the causal impact of predictable spread changes on output.

Solution

In matrix notation, \(\hat{\gamma}_{\text{2SLS}} = (\hat{\mathbf{x}}' \hat{\mathbf{x}})^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{x}}' \mathbf{y}\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{Z}\) is the sentiment instrument. Substituting \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}\gamma + \mathbf{v}\) gives \(\hat{\gamma}_{\text{2SLS}} = \gamma + (\hat{\mathbf{x}}' \hat{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{x}}'\mathbf{v} = \gamma + (\mathbf{x}' \mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{x})^{-1} \mathbf{x}' \mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{v}\). By the Law of Large Numbers, \(\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{v} \xrightarrow{p} \E[\text{Sent}_t v_{t+h}] = 0\), and \(\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{x} \xrightarrow{p} \beta \text{Var}(\text{Sent}_t) \ne 0\). Therefore, the second term vanishes in probability, establishing consistency \(\hat{\gamma}_{\text{2SLS}} \xrightarrow{p} \gamma\).

P6 · Belief Regression Dynamics

Suppose belief errors follow an AR(1) process \(e_t = \rho e_{t-1} + \xi_t\) with \(0 < \rho < 1\). If credit spreads are set as \(S_t = S^* - e_t\), prove that the multi-period forward spread change satisfies \(S_{t+h} - S_t = (1 - \rho^h) e_t + \eta_{t, h}\), and show that the reversal coefficient increases monotonically toward \(1\) as horizon \(h \to \infty\).

Solution

At horizon \(h\), \(e_{t+h} = \rho^h e_t + \sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\rho^j \xi_{t+h-j}\). The spread change is \(S_{t+h} - S_t = (S^* - e_{t+h}) - (S^* - e_t) = e_t - e_{t+h} = e_t - (\rho^h e_t + \eta_{t, h}) = (1 - \rho^h) e_t - \eta_{t, h}\). The reversal coefficient on initial sentiment error \(e_t\) is \(\beta(h) = 1 - \rho^h\). Differentiating with respect to \(h\) yields \(\frac{d\beta}{dh} = -\rho^h \ln(\rho) > 0\) since \(\ln(\rho) < 0\) for \(\rho \in (0, 1)\). As \(h \to \infty\), \(\rho^h \to 0\), so \(\lim_{h \to \infty} \beta(h) = 1\), confirming monotonic convergence to full mean-reversion.

Empirical

P7 · Analyzing High-Yield Issuance Peaks

In 1998, 2007, and 2021, the US high-yield issuance share exceeded \(35\%\). Describe the subsequent 24-month trajectory of the Baa–Aaa and GZ credit spreads following each of these three historical peaks.

Solution

In all three episodes, extreme high-yield issuance shares were followed by dramatic credit spread widening 18 to 24 months later: (1) following the 1998 peak, the telecom/tech bust and Enron/WorldCom defaults widened spreads sharply through 2001–2002; (2) following the 2007 peak, the Great Financial Crisis triggered record spread widening in 2008–2009; (3) following the 2021 speculative issuance peak, central bank tightening and regional bank failures widened spreads through 2022–2023. These episodes demonstrate the predictive reliability of \(\text{ISS}_t\).

P8 · Newey–West Standard Errors for Overlapping Horizons

An econometrician estimates an 8-quarter ahead regression \(\Delta^8 S_{t+8} = \alpha + \beta \text{Sent}_t + u_{t+8}\) on quarterly data. Explain why OLS standard errors are biased downward and specify the minimum Newey–West lag truncation parameter required.

Solution

Because the dependent variable spans 8 quarters of future data, the error term \(u_{t+8}\) contains an MA(7) moving average structure: adjacent quarterly observations share 7 overlapping quarters of common shocks. Under standard OLS, positive autocorrelation in residuals biases standard errors severely downward, inflating t-statistics. The minimum lag truncation parameter for the Newey–West (1987) HAC covariance estimator is \(J = h - 1 = 7\) quarters (or typically \(\lfloor 1.5 \times h \rfloor = 12\) quarters) to guarantee consistent standard errors.

P9 · Out-of-Sample R-squared in Two-Stage Models

In an out-of-sample forecast of 2-year GDP growth across 1990–2020, an AR(4) benchmark delivers root mean squared error \(\text{RMSE}_{\text{bench}} = 2.10\%\). Adding credit sentiment \(\text{Sent}_t\) lowers the forecast error to \(\text{RMSE}_{\text{sent}} = 1.82\%\). Calculate the out-of-sample \(R^2\) (\(R_{\text{OOS}}^2\)).

Solution

The out-of-sample \(R^2\) is defined as \(R_{\text{OOS}}^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{MSE}_{\text{sent}}}{\text{MSE}_{\text{bench}}} = 1 - \frac{1.82^2}{2.10^2} = 1 - \frac{3.3124}{4.4100} = 1 - 0.7511 = +0.2489\) or \(24.9\%\). An out-of-sample \(R^2\) of approximately \(25\%\) indicates substantial economic and statistical gains over standard autoregressive macro forecasts.

Research

P10 · Diagnostic Expectations in Credit Markets

Contrast the Bordalo, Gennaioli, and Shleifer (2018) diagnostic expectations model with the rational expectations benchmark in explaining credit cycle reversals.

Solution

Under rational expectations, investors update beliefs using Bayes' rule with correct probabilities, ruling out predictable forecasting errors. Diagnostic expectations incorporate the Kahneman–Tversky representativeness heuristic: agents overweight states of nature whose likelihood has increased in recent data. Following benign macro shocks, low default realizations become representative, causing investors to neglect downside tail risk and underprice debt contracts. When subsequent macro shocks arrive, expectations correct sharply, generating the endogenous boom-bust cycle.

P11 · Search for Yield and Regulatory Arbitrage

How do institutional constraints (such as insurance company risk-based capital rules and pension return targets) amplify credit sentiment dynamics?

Solution

Many institutional investors face fixed nominal return liabilities (e.g. 7% pension actuarial hurdles) alongside strict rating-based capital penalties. During prolonged low-interest-rate environments, institutions engage in "reach-for-yield," systematically crowding into BBB-rated corporate bonds and leveraged loans that offer yield pickups while barely qualifying for favorable capital treatment. This concentrated institutional demand compresses spreads and enables low-quality firms to issue debt at unsustainable valuations.

P12 · Macroprudential Policy and Sentiment Leaning

Critically assess whether central banks should use countercyclical capital buffers (CCyB) to lean against elevated credit sentiment.

Solution

Proponents argue that raising CCyB requirements when high-yield issuance is elevated and spreads are compressed curbs excessive credit supply and builds loss-absorbing capital before the predictable downturn occurs. Opponents note that macroprudential tightening in the regulated banking sector frequently drives corporate borrowing into unregulated shadow banking and private credit channels (regulatory leakage), potentially increasing systemic opacity without eliminating credit cycle vulnerabilities.
