Default Prediction & The Altman Z-Score Model

Linear discriminant analysis, financial ratio profiles, bankruptcy classification boundaries, model revisions, and structural distance-to-default benchmarking.

Predicting corporate insolvency is the foundational task of credit risk management and distressed debt investing. Before negotiating a restructuring, valuing a distressed claim, or constructing a recovery waterfall, analysts must distinguish transient operational underperformance from impending economic default. Traditional univariate financial ratio analysis fails because individual metrics emit conflicting signals. Edward Altman's (1968) multivariate discriminant framework synthesized liquidity, cumulative profitability, operating efficiency, leverage cushion, and asset turnover into a single predictive score. Understanding discriminant mathematics, classification boundaries, private-firm adjustments, and their empirical relationship with structural market-based default predictors establishes the quantitative baseline for distressed capital structure analysis.

1. Statistical Foundations of Default Prediction

Default prediction is the statistical and econometric estimation of the probability or propensity that an obligor will fail to service its contractual debt obligations. Corporate bankruptcy modeling began historically with univariate ratio comparisons, where liquid ratios (such as current assets to current liabilities) were compared across surviving and failing firms.

In his seminal contribution, Altman (1968) demonstrated that univariate ratio analysis produces ambiguous or contradictory classifications when a firm exhibits strong sales turnover but severe balance sheet leverage. To resolve this, Altman applied Ronald Fisher's technique of Multiple Discriminant Analysis (MDA).

Definition 1.1 (Fisher Linear Discriminant Analysis). Let \(\mathbf{x} \in \R^p\) be a \(p\)-dimensional vector of financial ratios. Suppose an obligor belongs to one of two mutually exclusive populations: \(\Pi_1\) (firms that default within a specified forecast horizon) or \(\Pi_2\) (surviving, non-defaulting firms). Let \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 \in \R^p\) and \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_2 \in \R^p\) denote the population mean vectors, and assume equal covariance matrices \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_1 = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_2 = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\).

Fisher's linear discriminant function seeks a linear projection vector \(\mathbf{w} \in \R^p\) that maximizes the between-group variance relative to the within-group variance:

\[ J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{S}_B \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{S}_W \mathbf{w}} \]

where \(\mathbf{S}_B = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^T\) is the between-class scatter matrix, and \(\mathbf{S}_W\) is the pooled within-class covariance matrix.

Theorem 1.1 (Optimal Linear Discriminant Vector). The vector \(\mathbf{w}^*\) that maximizes the Fisher Rayleigh quotient \(J(\mathbf{w})\) satisfies:

\[ \mathbf{w}^* = \mathbf{S}_W^{-1} (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2) \]

yielding the scalar discriminant score \(Z(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^{*T} \mathbf{x}\).

Proof. The objective is to maximize \(J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^T (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^T \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{S}_W \mathbf{w}}\). Notice that \((\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^T \mathbf{w}\) is a scalar, so \(\mathbf{S}_B \mathbf{w} = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2) \cdot c\), where \(c = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^T \mathbf{w}\).

Setting the gradient of \(J(\mathbf{w})\) with respect to \(\mathbf{w}\) to zero:

\[ \nabla_\mathbf{w} J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{2 \mathbf{S}_B \mathbf{w} (\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{S}_W \mathbf{w}) - 2 \mathbf{S}_W \mathbf{w} (\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{S}_B \mathbf{w})}{(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{S}_W \mathbf{w})^2} = \mathbf{0} \]

This implies \(\mathbf{S}_B \mathbf{w} = J(\mathbf{w}) \mathbf{S}_W \mathbf{w}\). Substituting \(\mathbf{S}_B \mathbf{w} = c (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)\), we obtain:

\[ c (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2) = J(\mathbf{w}) \mathbf{S}_W \mathbf{w} \implies \mathbf{w} \propto \mathbf{S}_W^{-1} (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2) \]

Because multiplying \(\mathbf{w}\) by a positive scalar does not alter the ratio of projections, setting the scale factor to unity yields \(\mathbf{w}^* = \mathbf{S}_W^{-1} (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.1 (Comparison with Structural Distance to Default). While Linear Discriminant Analysis computes a linear projection of accounting metrics, structural credit models (CR1) such as the Merton structural credit model derive default propensity from the market value and volatility of firm assets. In Merton's model, the distance to default measures the number of standard deviations between expected future asset value and contractual debt principal. Bharath & Shumway (2008) empirically established that structural distance-to-default does not act as a sufficient statistic for default prediction, and that accounting variables add substantial incremental explanatory power.

2. The Original Altman (1968) Manufacturing Model

Altman's initial empirical sample consisted of 66 publicly traded manufacturing corporations, divided into 33 bankrupt firms that filed Chapter X or XI between 1946 and 1965, and 33 healthy manufacturing firms matched by industry asset size.

From an initial set of 22 balance sheet and income statement ratios, Altman selected five variables that maximized inter-group discrimination while minimizing collinearity:

Definition 1.2 (Altman 1968 Z-Score Ratios). The five standardized financial ratios are defined as follows:

  • \(X_1 = \frac{\text{Working Capital}}{\text{Total Assets}}\): Measures net liquid assets relative to firm capitalization. A firm experiencing recurring operating losses suffers shrinkage in current assets relative to current liabilities.
  • \(X_2 = \frac{\text{Retained Earnings}}{\text{Total Assets}}\): Measures cumulative profitability over the operating history of the firm. Young firms naturally exhibit lower \(X_2\), reflecting higher structural vulnerability.
  • \(X_3 = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Total Assets}}\): Measures the operating earning power of the firm's assets independent of tax shields and capital structure leverage.
  • \(X_4 = \frac{\text{Market Value of Equity}}{\text{Book Value of Total Liabilities}}\): Measures the cushion by which the market value of the firm's equity can decline before balance-sheet liabilities exceed asset value.
  • \(X_5 = \frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Total Assets}}\): Measures asset turnover and capital utilization capacity in generating commercial revenues.

Equation 1.1 (Altman 1968 Discriminant Function). The empirical linear discriminant function for publicly traded manufacturing firms is:

\[ Z = 1.2 X_1 + 1.4 X_2 + 3.3 X_3 + 0.6 X_4 + 0.999 X_5 \]

where ratios \(X_1\) through \(X_5\) are expressed in absolute decimal format (for example, \(X_1 = 0.20\) for \(20\%\)).

Altman established empirical classification cutoffs based on minimizing misclassification costs:

Z-Score Range Classification Zone Empirical Default Likelihood
\(Z < 1.81\) Distress Zone Very high probability of bankruptcy within 1–2 years
\(1.81 \le Z \le 2.99\) Grey Zone Indeterminate default propensity; requires granular operational audit
\(Z > 2.99\) Safe Zone Solvent firm with low short-term bankruptcy probability

Example 1.1 (Z-Score Calculation for a Distressed Manufacturer). Consider an industrial firm with Total Assets \(\$500\text{M}\), Current Assets \(\$150\text{M}\), Current Liabilities \(\$200\text{M}\), Cumulative Retained Earnings \(-\$50\text{M}\), EBIT \(\$25\text{M}\), Total Liabilities \(\$350\text{M}\), Market Capitalization \(\$140\text{M}\), and Annual Sales \(\$450\text{M}\).

The discriminant ratios are:

  • \(X_1 = (150 - 200) / 500 = -50 / 500 = -0.10\)
  • \(X_2 = -50 / 500 = -0.10\)
  • \(X_3 = 25 / 500 = 0.05\)
  • \(X_4 = 140 / 350 = 0.40\)
  • \(X_5 = 450 / 500 = 0.90\)

Evaluating the Z-score:

\[ Z = 1.2(-0.10) + 1.4(-0.10) + 3.3(0.05) + 0.6(0.40) + 0.999(0.90) = -0.12 - 0.14 + 0.165 + 0.24 + 0.8991 = 1.0441 \]

Because \(Z = 1.044 < 1.81\), the firm sits deep in the Distress Zone.

3. Revisions for Private Firms (\(Z'\)) and Non-Manufacturing (\(Z''\))

The original 1968 formulation suffers from two structural limitations: it requires market equity valuations in \(X_4\), rendering it inapplicable to privately held enterprises, and variable \(X_5\) (Sales / Total Assets) penalizes firms in industries with lower asset turnover, such as real estate, utilities, and financial services.

Definition 1.3 (Altman Private Firm Z'-Score). For privately held manufacturing firms, market equity in \(X_4\) is replaced by the book value of equity, and coefficients are re-estimated:

\[ Z' = 0.717 X_1 + 0.847 X_2 + 3.107 X_3 + 0.420 X_4^{\text{book}} + 0.998 X_5 \]

Classification zones: \(Z' < 1.23\) (Distress Zone), \(1.23 \le Z' \le 2.90\) (Grey Zone), and \(Z' > 2.90\) (Safe Zone).

Definition 1.4 (Altman Four-Variable Z''-Score). To eliminate the asset turnover bias of \(X_5\) and enable application to non-manufacturing corporations and emerging market issuers, variable \(X_5\) is excluded:

\[ Z'' = 6.56 X_1 + 3.26 X_2 + 6.72 X_3 + 1.05 X_4^{\text{book}} \]

Classification zones: \(Z'' < 1.10\) (Distress Zone), \(1.10 \le Z'' \le 2.60\) (Grey Zone), and \(Z'' > 2.60\) (Safe Zone).

Lab 1 · Altman Z-Score vs. Distance to Default Classification

Demonstrates calculation of Altman Z, Z', and Z'' across healthy, grey, and distressed firms, verifying classification consistency against structural Merton distance to default.

"""Lab CR4-L01-1: Altman Z-Score Model and Distance to Default Comparison.

Demonstrates calculation of Altman (1968) Z-score for manufacturing firms,
the revised Z' and Z'' models, classification thresholds, and empirical comparison
against structural Merton distance-to-default predictors.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from typing import NamedTuple


class FirmProfile(NamedTuple):
    name: str
    wc: float
    re: float
    ebit: float
    mve: float
    sales: float
    ta: float
    tl: float
    asset_val: float
    debt_face: float
    asset_vol: float


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def altman_z_score(
    wc: float,
    re: float,
    ebit: float,
    mve: float,
    sales: float,
    ta: float,
    tl: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Compute Altman (1968) Z-score and discriminant ratios.

    X1 = Working Capital / Total Assets
    X2 = Retained Earnings / Total Assets
    X3 = EBIT / Total Assets
    X4 = Market Value Equity / Total Liabilities
    X5 = Sales / Total Assets
    """
    x1 = wc / ta
    x2 = re / ta
    x3 = ebit / ta
    x4 = mve / tl
    x5 = sales / ta

    z = 1.2 * x1 + 1.4 * x2 + 3.3 * x3 + 0.6 * x4 + 0.999 * x5
    z_prime = 0.717 * x1 + 0.847 * x2 + 3.107 * x3 + 0.420 * x4 + 0.998 * x5
    z_double_prime = 6.56 * x1 + 3.26 * x2 + 6.72 * x3 + 1.05 * x4

    return {
        "x1": x1,
        "x2": x2,
        "x3": x3,
        "x4": x4,
        "x5": x5,
        "z": z,
        "z_prime": z_prime,
        "z_double_prime": z_double_prime,
    }


def merton_distance_to_default(
    v: float,
    d: float,
    mu: float,
    sigma: float,
    t: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute Merton distance to default (DD)."""
    return (math.log(v / d) + (mu - 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * t) / (sigma * math.sqrt(t))


def classify_z(z: float) -> str:
    """Classify firm into Altman distress zones."""
    if z < 1.81:
        return "Distress"
    elif z <= 2.99:
        return "Grey"
    else:
        return "Safe"


def main() -> None:
    firms = [
        FirmProfile(
            name="Healthy Manufacturing Corp",
            wc=120.0,
            re=250.0,
            ebit=95.0,
            mve=600.0,
            sales=800.0,
            ta=500.0,
            tl=200.0,
            asset_val=800.0,
            debt_face=200.0,
            asset_vol=0.20,
        ),
        FirmProfile(
            name="Borderline Capital Inc",
            wc=30.0,
            re=50.0,
            ebit=35.0,
            mve=180.0,
            sales=400.0,
            ta=400.0,
            tl=250.0,
            asset_val=430.0,
            debt_face=250.0,
            asset_vol=0.30,
        ),
        FirmProfile(
            name="Distressed Industrial Ltd",
            wc=-40.0,
            re=-80.0,
            ebit=-15.0,
            mve=45.0,
            sales=300.0,
            ta=350.0,
            tl=320.0,
            asset_val=365.0,
            debt_face=320.0,
            asset_vol=0.45,
        ),
    ]

    print("Altman (1968) Z-Score & Merton Distance-to-Default Analysis:")
    print("Firm Name                    |   Z   | Zone      |   Z'  |  Z''  | Merton DD | Merton PD")
    print("-" * 88)

    for f in firms:
        res = altman_z_score(f.wc, f.re, f.ebit, f.mve, f.sales, f.ta, f.tl)
        zone = classify_z(res["z"])
        dd = merton_distance_to_default(f.asset_val, f.debt_face, 0.05, f.asset_vol, 1.0)
        pd = norm_cdf(-dd)

        z_str = f"{res['z']:5.2f}"
        zp_str = f"{res['z_prime']:5.2f}"
        zpp_str = f"{res['z_double_prime']:5.2f}"
        print(f"{f.name:28} | {z_str} | {zone:9} | {zp_str} | {zpp_str} |   {dd:5.2f}   |   {pd:6.4f}")

    # Invariant verification:
    # Distress firm must have Z < 1.81 and DD substantially lower than Healthy firm
    h_f = firms[0]
    d_f = firms[2]
    healthy_res = altman_z_score(h_f.wc, h_f.re, h_f.ebit, h_f.mve, h_f.sales, h_f.ta, h_f.tl)
    distressed_res = altman_z_score(d_f.wc, d_f.re, d_f.ebit, d_f.mve, d_f.sales, d_f.ta, d_f.tl)

    assert healthy_res["z"] > 2.99
    assert distressed_res["z"] < 1.81
    print("\nVerified discriminant thresholds: Healthy Z > 2.99 and Distressed Z < 1.81.")


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks

The Altman discriminant model breaks down in modern corporate credit under specific structural regimes:

  • Intangible Assets and Asset-Light Capital Structures: Modern software, pharmaceutical, and technology firms hold valuable proprietary IP, software codebases, and brand equity that are expensed rather than capitalized under GAAP. As a result, book Total Assets are artificially depressed, inflating ratios \(X_1, X_2, X_3\) and distorting \(Z\)-score comparability.
  • Lease Accounting and Off-Balance Sheet Obligations: Prior to IFRS 16 and ASC 842, operating leases did not appear in Total Liabilities, artificially suppressing the denominator of \(X_4\) and overstating credit solvency.
  • Sample Selection and Survivor Bias: The original Altman sample utilized a paired 1:1 match of bankrupt to surviving firms. In actual commercial economies, the unconditional default rate is between \(1\%\) and \(3\%\), so naive application of sample discriminant cutoffs yields excessive Type I false alarms.
  • Dynamic Liquidity Runs vs. Static Balance Sheets: Discriminant analysis evaluates static quarterly snapshots. A solvent firm with a high \(Z\)-score can rapidly collapse into distress within days if wholesale funding or repo facilities are withdrawn.

5. Misconceptions

  • The Altman Z-score outputs an exact probability of default on [0, 1].

    Multiple Discriminant Analysis calculates a relative discriminant score on an unbounded real line \((-\infty, +\infty)\), not a bounded probability measure in \([0, 1]\). Converting \(Z\)-scores to cumulative default probabilities requires fitting a logistic mapping function or calibrating empirical mortality tables against historical default cohorts.

  • Original 1968 manufacturing coefficients apply directly to financial institutions.

    Commercial banks operate with financial leverage exceeding 10:1 to 20:1, and their assets consist primarily of loans and securities rather than inventory or physical plant. The concepts of working capital and sales turnover are economically meaningless for depository institutions.

  • Any firm entering the distress zone (\(Z < 1.81\)) inevitably files for Chapter 11.

    Altman documented an in-sample Type II error rate of 28% at two years prior to default. Distressed corporations frequently execute out-of-court debt-for-equity exchanges, sponsor cash injections, or operational asset sales that rehabilitate balance sheet solvency without formal judicial proceedings.

Mechanics

Problem M1. A publicly traded manufacturer reports: Current Assets \(\$220\text{M}\), Current Liabilities \(\$140\text{M}\), Total Assets \(\$800\text{M}\), Retained Earnings \(\$160\text{M}\), Operating Profit (EBIT) \(\$72\text{M}\), Total Book Liabilities \(\$480\text{M}\), Market Capitalization \(\$360\text{M}\), and Annual Revenues \(\$960\text{M}\). Calculate all five Altman ratios and compute the original 1968 \(Z\)-score. State its classification zone.

Solution

1. Working Capital \(= 220 - 140 = 80\text{M}\). Hence \(X_1 = 80 / 800 = 0.100\).

2. Retained Earnings / Total Assets: \(X_2 = 160 / 800 = 0.200\).

3. EBIT / Total Assets: \(X_3 = 72 / 800 = 0.090\).

4. Market Value Equity / Total Liabilities: \(X_4 = 360 / 480 = 0.750\).

5. Sales / Total Assets: \(X_5 = 960 / 800 = 1.200\).

Evaluating the discriminant equation:

\[ Z = 1.2(0.10) + 1.4(0.20) + 3.3(0.09) + 0.6(0.75) + 0.999(1.20) \] \[ Z = 0.120 + 0.280 + 0.297 + 0.450 + 1.1988 = 2.3458 \]

Since \(1.81 \le 2.346 \le 2.99\), the firm sits in the Grey Zone.

Problem M2. A private services firm has \(X_1 = 0.05\), \(X_2 = 0.08\), \(X_3 = 0.04\), and Book Equity / Total Liabilities \(= 0.30\). Compute the four-variable \(Z''\)-score and determine whether the firm is in the distress zone.

Solution

The four-variable \(Z''\) formula is:

\[ Z'' = 6.56 X_1 + 3.26 X_2 + 6.72 X_3 + 1.05 X_4 \] \[ Z'' = 6.56(0.05) + 3.26(0.08) + 6.72(0.04) + 1.05(0.30) = 0.328 + 0.2608 + 0.2688 + 0.315 = 1.1726 \]

For \(Z''\), the distress threshold is \(Z'' < 1.10\) and the grey zone is \([1.10, 2.60]\). Since \(Z'' = 1.173\), the firm is narrowly in the Grey Zone, just above the distress boundary.

Problem M3. For the private manufacturer in M1, suppose the stock is delisted and Book Equity is \(\$320\text{M}\). Compute the private firm \(Z'\)-score and compare it with the \(Z'\) distress threshold of \(1.23\).

Solution

\(X_4^{\text{book}} = 320 / 480 = 0.667\). Ratios \(X_1 = 0.10, X_2 = 0.20, X_3 = 0.09, X_5 = 1.20\).

\[ Z' = 0.717(0.10) + 0.847(0.20) + 3.107(0.09) + 0.420(0.667) + 0.998(1.20) \] \[ Z' = 0.0717 + 0.1694 + 0.2796 + 0.2801 + 1.1976 = 1.9984 \]

Because \(Z' = 1.998\) is well above \(1.23\) (and below \(2.90\)), the firm is classified in the private-firm Grey Zone.

Proof

Problem P1. Prove that in a two-group Linear Discriminant Analysis with equal prior probabilities \(p_1 = p_2 = 0.5\) and equal misclassification costs \(C(1|2) = C(2|1)\), the optimal Bayes classification cutoff \(Z_c\) for score \(Z(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^{*T} \mathbf{x}\) is the midpoint of the projected class means: \(Z_c = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{w}^{*T} \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \mathbf{w}^{*T} \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)\).

Solution

Under normality \(\mathbf{x} \sim \Normal(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), the log-likelihood ratio for assigning to \(\Pi_1\) over \(\Pi_2\) is:

\[ \ln \frac{f_1(\mathbf{x})}{f_2(\mathbf{x})} = -\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1)^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1) + \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2) \]

Expanding the quadratic forms:

\[ \ln \frac{f_1(\mathbf{x})}{f_2(\mathbf{x})} = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{x} - \frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\mu}_1^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\mu}_2^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 \]

Setting \(\mathbf{w}^* = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)\), the classification rule assigns to \(\Pi_1\) if \(\ln \frac{f_1(\mathbf{x})}{f_2(\mathbf{x})} > 0\):

\[ \mathbf{w}^{*T} \mathbf{x} > \frac{1}{2} (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2) = \frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{w}^{*T} \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \mathbf{w}^{*T} \boldsymbol{\mu}_2) \]

Thus, the optimal Bayes threshold is exactly the midpoint \(Z_c = \frac{1}{2}(\bar{Z}_1 + \bar{Z}_2)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P2. Derive the shift in the optimal cutoff \(\Delta Z_c\) when misclassification costs are asymmetric: the cost of classifying a defaulting firm as healthy is \(C(2|1) = \$100\text{K}\), while the cost of a false alarm is \(C(1|2) = \$10\text{K}\), with prior default rate \(p_1 = 0.05\).

Solution

The Bayes optimal decision rule assigns an obligor to the default class \(\Pi_1\) if:

\[ \frac{f_1(\mathbf{x})}{f_2(\mathbf{x})} > \frac{C(1|2) p_2}{C(2|1) p_1} = \theta \]

Taking logarithms:

\[ Z(\mathbf{x}) - Z_c > \ln \theta \implies Z(\mathbf{x}) > Z_c + \ln \left( \frac{C(1|2) (1 - p_1)}{C(2|1) p_1} \right) \]

Substituting the parameters: \(C(1|2) = 10, C(2|1) = 100, p_1 = 0.05, p_2 = 0.95\):

\[ \theta = \frac{10 \times 0.95}{100 \times 0.05} = \frac{9.5}{5.0} = 1.90 \]

Thus \(\Delta Z_c = \ln(1.90) = 0.6419\). The cutoff shifts upward by \(0.642\) to account for the relative prior rarity of default despite its higher per-instance severity.

Problem P3. Show that two-group Linear Discriminant Analysis is algebraically equivalent to Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression where the dependent variable is a scaled binary indicator \(y_i \in \{ -N/N_1, N/N_2 \}\).

Solution

Let \(N = N_1 + N_2\). Define the indicator \(y_i = -N/N_1\) for \(i \in \Pi_1\) and \(y_i = N/N_2\) for \(i \in \Pi_2\). Notice that \(\sum_{i=1}^N y_i = -N_1(N/N_1) + N_2(N/N_2) = -N + N = 0\), so \(y\) has sample mean zero.

The OLS slope vector is \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}\). Centering \(\mathbf{X}\) around its grand mean, the sample covariance \(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}\) satisfies:

\[ \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y} = \sum_{i \in \Pi_1} \left(-\frac{N}{N_1}\right) \mathbf{x}_i + \sum_{i \in \Pi_2} \left(\frac{N}{N_2}\right) \mathbf{x}_i = N (\bar{\mathbf{x}}_2 - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_1) \]

Furthermore, total sample sum of squares decomposes as \(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{S}_W + \frac{N_1 N_2}{N} (\bar{\mathbf{x}}_1 - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_2)(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_1 - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_2)^T\). By the Sherman–Morrison inversion formula, the projection \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is directly proportional to \(\mathbf{S}_W^{-1} (\bar{\mathbf{x}}_2 - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_1)\), which equals the Fisher discriminant vector \(\mathbf{w}^*\). \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem E1. In a holdout validation sample of 1,000 corporate bond issuers, 30 firms default over a 12-month horizon. A \(Z\)-score model with cutoff \(Z_c = 1.81\) correctly predicts 27 of the defaults, but falsely classifies 58 solvent firms into the distress zone. Construct the complete confusion matrix and calculate the Type I error rate, Type II error rate, sensitivity, and specificity.

Solution

Confusion matrix entries:

  • True Positives (Default predicted as Default): \(TP = 27\)
  • False Negatives (Default predicted as Solvent, Type I error): \(FN = 30 - 27 = 3\)
  • False Positives (Solvent predicted as Default, Type II error): \(FP = 58\)
  • True Negatives (Solvent predicted as Solvent): \(TN = 970 - 58 = 912\)

Performance metrics:

  • Type I Error Rate: \(FN / (TP + FN) = 3 / 30 = 10.0\%\)
  • Sensitivity (Recall): \(TP / (TP + FN) = 27 / 30 = 90.0\%\)
  • Type II Error Rate: \(FP / (FP + TN) = 58 / 970 = 5.98\%\)
  • Specificity: \(TN / (FP + TN) = 912 / 970 = 94.02\%\)

Problem E2. Explain why the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and Area Under the Curve (AUC) provide an evaluation metric superior to raw classification accuracy for default prediction models.

Solution

In credit risk, defaults represent a highly imbalanced class (typically \(1\%\) to \(2\%\) of obligors annually). A trivial model predicting "no default" for every borrower achieves \(98\%\) raw accuracy while providing zero economic utility. The ROC curve plots Sensitivity (True Positive Rate) against \(1 - \text{Specificity}\) (False Positive Rate) across all possible threshold cutoffs \(Z_c \in (-\infty, +\infty)\). The AUC integrates this curve, providing a threshold-invariant measure of ranking ability: an AUC of \(0.50\) corresponds to random guessing, while an AUC of \(1.00\) indicates perfect rank discrimination between defaulting and surviving borrowers regardless of class prevalence.

Problem E3. A credit hedge fund compares two models: Model A (Altman Z-score) with \(\text{AUC} = 0.82\), and Model B (Merton structural distance-to-default) with \(\text{AUC} = 0.86\). When combined in a bivariate logistic regression, the joint \(\text{AUC}\) rises to \(0.91\). What does this empirical super-additivity imply about the information sets of accounting metrics versus market option prices?

Solution

The improvement from \(0.86\) to \(0.91\) confirms that accounting ratios and market-implied asset volatilities provide orthogonal, complementary information sets. Structural distance-to-default incorporates forward-looking market equity valuations and instantaneous stock volatility, capturing market sentiment and rapid capitalization shifts. In contrast, Altman accounting ratios capture fundamental balance-sheet liquidity, cumulative retained earnings, and operating efficiency that may be temporarily masked by equity market momentum or speculative bubbles.

Problem R1. Propose an econometric specification that combines Altman's static ratio vector \(\mathbf{x}_{i,t}\) with a time-varying macroeconomic credit cycle indicator \(S_t\) in a multi-period discrete hazard rate framework.

Solution

Specify a conditional logit / discrete-time hazard model for obligor \(i\) in period \(t\):

\[ \Prob(Y_{i,t} = 1 \mid Y_{i,t-1} = 0, \mathbf{x}_{i,t-1}, S_{t-1}) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(\alpha + \boldsymbol{\beta}^T \mathbf{x}_{i,t-1} + \gamma S_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\delta}^T (\mathbf{x}_{i,t-1} \cdot S_{t-1})))} \]

Here, \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) captures baseline firm-specific balance sheet vulnerability, \(\gamma\) captures systemic credit contraction shocks, and the interaction term \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) captures non-linear fragility: a firm with low liquidity (\(X_1\)) faces exponentially higher default risk when aggregate credit markets freeze (\(S_{t-1}\) is elevated).

Problem R2. Modern credit machine learning models (gradient boosted trees, neural networks) frequently achieve higher in-sample AUC than linear discriminant analysis. Identify two structural regulatory or institutional constraints that prevent institutional risk managers from replacing transparent LDA scoring with deep black-box models.

Solution

1. Regulatory Explainability and Fair Lending Audits: Under Basel internal ratings-based (IRB) guidelines and consumer credit regulations, financial institutions must provide adverse action notices and explainable causal drivers for credit downgrades or loan rejections. Complex deep networks lack monotonic attribution and can violate regulatory interpretability mandates.

2. Stress Testing and Counterfactual Coherence: Regulators require banks to run macroeconomic stress tests (such as CCAR/DFAST). A linear score \(Z\) reacts predictably to specified accounting shocks (\(\partial Z / \partial X_3 = 3.3\)). In contrast, unconstrained neural networks exhibit non-monotonic behavior and out-of-distribution hallucinations under severe macro stress, generating unstable capital reserve requirements.

Problem R3. Formulate a research hypothesis on how the widespread adoption of credit default swaps (CDS) on public debt issuers since 2000 has impacted the out-of-sample predictive accuracy of the original 1968 Altman \(Z\)-score.

Solution

Hypothesis: The presence of active CDS markets diminishes the lead-time accuracy of static accounting \(Z\)-scores because debt holders holding CDS protection face the "empty creditor" problem. Insured bondholders have reduced incentive to grant out-of-court covenant waivers or debt concessions, leading to accelerated formal Chapter 11 filings for firms whose balance sheet ratios might otherwise place them in the borderline grey zone rather than immediate default.

8. Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Altman (1968) Z-score model for manufacturing firms, what does ratio \(X_1\) measure?

    Options
    Altman defined \(X_1 = \text{Working Capital} / \text{Total Assets} = (\text{Current Assets} - \text{Current Liabilities}) / \text{Total Assets}\) to measure net liquid asset cushion.

  2. Why did Altman eliminate the sales-to-assets ratio (\(X_5\)) in the revised four-variable \(Z''\) model?

    Options
    Capital-intensive manufacturers have much lower sales-to-assets turnover than retail or services. Excluding \(X_5\) allows the \(Z''\) model to be applied across diverse industries and emerging markets without industry bias.

  3. What are the classification zone thresholds for the original 1968 Altman Z-score?

    Options
    Altman (1968) identified \(1.81\) and \(2.99\) as the critical cutoffs minimizing classification error for public manufacturing corporations.

  4. How is the equity cushion measured in the original Altman 1968 model (\(X_4\))?

    Options
    Altman specified \(X_4 = \text{MVE} / \text{Book Total Liabilities}\) to measure how far the firm's assets could decline in market value before liabilities exceeded assets.

  5. In Fisher Linear Discriminant Analysis, what does the pooled within-class covariance matrix \(\mathbf{S}_W\) represent?

    Options
    \(\mathbf{S}_W\) aggregates the scatter of observations within each population around its own mean: \(\mathbf{S}_W = \sum_{k=1}^2 \sum_{i \in \Pi_k} (\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_k)(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_k)^T\).

  6. According to Bharath & Shumway (2008), what is the empirical relationship between the Merton distance to default and accounting default models?

    Options
    Bharath & Shumway (2008) showed that while Merton's model contains signal, it is not a sufficient statistic: accounting metrics provide orthogonal explanatory power that significantly improves out-of-sample default forecasting.

  7. What modification is introduced in Altman's private firm model (\(Z'\)) for variable \(X_4\)?

    Options
    In \(Z'\), book equity replaces market equity because private firms lack observable stock prices, and the coefficient is recalibrated to \(0.420\).

  8. What happens to the Altman Z-score of an asset-light software corporation that expenses substantial internal research and software development?

    Options
    Under GAAP, expensing R&D suppresses capitalized balance sheet assets, artificially reducing Total Assets and inflating ratios scaled by assets, illustrating where the canon breaks for modern tech firms.
