Distressed Debt Investing & Fulcrum Security Analysis

Distressed debt investing transforms corporate crisis into asymmetric investment opportunities. By acquiring senior loans and discounted bonds of distressed corporations, investors navigate legal bankruptcy procedures to capture excess returns or convert debt into controlling equity ownership. Identifying the fulcrum security across enterprise value scenarios is the essential analytical core of the strategy: the fulcrum tranche determines which creditors take operational control of the reorganized corporation and receive the upside of post-emergence equity.

1Distressed Debt Investment Styles

Institutional investors in distressed corporate debt operate across three primary investment styles distinguished by liquidity horizon, activist involvement, and intended post-reorganization claim format:

Definition 1.1 (Distressed Debt Investing)

Distressed debt investing is the acquisition of debt securities (bank loans, trade claims, senior notes, or subordinated debentures) of companies experiencing severe financial distress, operational distress, or formal bankruptcy proceedings, trading at significant discounts to par value or yielding distressed spread premia (typically spreads exceeding 1,000 basis points over benchmark Treasuries).

The three dominant strategic styles comprise:

  1. Passive / Yield-to-Liquidation: Investors purchase senior secured debt at discounts (e.g., 75 cents on the dollar) backed by high-quality collateral, anticipating rapid full par recovery upon refinancing, asset sale, or court-supervised liquidation without seeking active control.
  2. Active / Blocking Position: Under Bankruptcy Code § 1126(c), plan acceptance by an impaired class requires at least two-thirds (66.67%) in dollar amount of voting claims. By accumulating more than 33.34% of an impaired class, an investor acquires a statutory negative blocking veto. This position is leveraged to force structural concessions, higher cash distributions, or board seats.
  3. Loan-to-Own: The investor identifies and buys the fulcrum security at a deep secondary discount, intending to sponsor the Chapter 11 Plan of Reorganization, equitize the debt tranche, and emerge as the controlling common equity owner of the deleveraged business.

2The Fulcrum Security Concept

The allocation of reorganization consideration is governed by where the debtor's estimated enterprise value falls relative to its cumulative debt obligations.

Definition 1.2 (Fulcrum Security)

In a capital structure recovery waterfall with total enterprise reorganization value \(V\), the fulcrum security is the most senior tranche of debt that is not fully covered by reorganization enterprise value. It is the tranche that receives a partial recovery (\(0 < R_{\text{fulcrum}} < 1.0\)) under strict priority. In Chapter 11 practice, the fulcrum tranche is typically equitized under the Plan of Reorganization, receiving the majority or entirety of the reorganized common equity.

Theorem 1.1 (Fulcrum Existence and Seniority Migration)

Let a distressed firm have \(K\) debt tranches with claims \(D_1, \dots, D_K\) sorted in strict descending seniority, and cumulative claims \(C_k = \sum_{j=1}^k D_j\) with \(C_0 = 0\). For any realized enterprise value \(V \in (0, C_K)\):

  1. Existence and Uniqueness: There exists a unique tranche \(k^* \in \{1, \dots, K\}\) such that \(C_{k^*-1} < V \le C_{k^*}\). Tranche \(k^*\) is the unique fulcrum security.
  2. Consideration Allocation: All senior tranches \(j < k^*\) are fully covered (\(R_j = 1.0\)) and receive cash or reinstated take-back debt. The fulcrum tranche \(k^*\) receives all residual enterprise value \(V - C_{k^*-1} \le D_{k^*}\), typically distributed as reorganized equity. All junior tranches \(j > k^*\) receive zero recovery (\(R_j = 0.0\)).
  3. Seniority Migration: As enterprise value \(V\) increases continuously, the fulcrum tranche index \(k^*(V)\) is a monotonically non-decreasing step function of \(V\). Upside valuation surprises migrate control down the seniority ladder.
Proof

1. The cumulative claims sequence \(0 = C_0 < C_1 < C_2 < \dots < C_K\) forms a strictly increasing partition of the interval \([0, C_K]\). For any \(V \in (0, C_K)\), the half-open intervals \((C_{k-1}, C_k]\) are disjoint and their union is \((0, C_K]\). Hence \(V\) belongs to exactly one interval \((C_{k^*-1}, C_{k^*}]\), establishing unique existence of \(k^*\).

2. By strict priority, tranche \(j\) receives \(V_j = \max(0, \min(D_j, V - C_{j-1}))\). For \(j < k^*\), \(V \ge C_{k^*-1} \ge C_j\), so \(V_j = D_j\) and \(R_j = 1.0\). For \(j = k^*\), \(V_{k^*} = V - C_{k^*-1} \le D_{k^*}\), so \(R_{k^*} = (V - C_{k^*-1})/D_{k^*} \in (0, 1]\). For \(j > k^*\), \(V \le C_{k^*} \le C_{j-1}\), so \(V_j = 0\) and \(R_j = 0.0\).

3. If \(V_A < V_B\), then \(C_{k^*(V_A)-1} < V_A < V_B \le C_{k^*(V_B)}\). This implies \(C_{k^*(V_A)-1} < C_{k^*(V_B)}\), so \(k^*(V_A) - 1 < k^*(V_B)\), which guarantees \(k^*(V_A) \le k^*(V_B)\). Control migrates down the capital structure as \(V\) expands. \(\blacksquare\)

3Distressed Investor Return Analytics: MOIC and IRR

Distressed debt investors evaluate investment opportunities across scenario-weighted recovery distributions, calculating cash-on-cash multiples and annualized internal rates of return.

Definition 1.3 (Multiple on Invested Capital and Annualized IRR)

Let an investor purchase face claim \(D_k\) of tranche \(k\) at secondary market purchase price \(P_k \in (0, 1)\) (dollars paid per dollar of face value), incurring total acquisition cost \(I_k = P_k D_k\). Over an investment holding horizon of \(T\) years, the expected dollar recovery is \(\bar{V}_k = \sum_{s=1}^S p_s V_k(s)\), where \(p_s\) is the probability of scenario \(s\). The Multiple on Invested Capital (MOIC) and annualized Internal Rate of Return (IRR) are:

\[ \text{MOIC}_k = \frac{\bar{V}_k}{I_k} = \frac{\bar{R}_k}{P_k}, \qquad \text{IRR}_k = (\text{MOIC}_k)^{1/T} - 1 = \left(\frac{\bar{R}_k}{P_k}\right)^{1/T} - 1 \]

where \(\bar{R}_k = \bar{V}_k / D_k\) is the expected recovery rate per dollar of face claim.

Example 1.1 (Evaluating a Senior Unsecured Fulcrum Purchase)

An investor evaluates \(\$80\text{M}\) face value of Senior Unsecured Notes trading at \(P = 0.35\) (\(\$28\text{M}\) total purchase cost). The holding period to Chapter 11 emergence is \(T = 1.5\) years. Enterprise value scenarios are:

  • Liquidation (\(\text{EV} = \$70\text{M}\), prob \(0.20\)): Senior notes recover \(\$0\text{M}\).
  • Low Reorg (\(\text{EV} = \$130\text{M}\), prob \(0.30\)): Senior notes recover \(\$0\text{M}\).
  • Base Reorg (\(\text{EV} = \$200\text{M}\), prob \(0.35\)): Senior notes act as fulcrum, recovering \(\$40\text{M}\) (50% recovery).
  • High Reorg (\(\text{EV} = \$260\text{M}\), prob \(0.15\)): Senior notes fully covered, recovering \(\$80\text{M}\) (100% recovery).

1. Expected recovery: \(\bar{V} = 0.20(0) + 0.30(0) + 0.35(40) + 0.15(80) = 0 + 0 + 14.0 + 12.0 = \$26.0\text{M}\).

2. Expected recovery rate: \(\bar{R} = 26.0 / 80.0 = 32.5\%\).

3. Expected MOIC: \(\frac{26.0}{28.0} = 0.9286\text{x}\). Notice that expected MOIC is below 1.0x due to the 50% probability of total wipeout in low/liquidation scenarios.

4. Conditional Base Reorg Return: If the Base scenario materializes, payoff is \(\$40\text{M}\), yielding \(\text{MOIC} = 40 / 28 = 1.4286\text{x}\) and \(\text{IRR} = (1.4286)^{1/1.5} - 1 = (1.4286)^{0.6667} - 1 = 26.8\%\).

Lab 1 · Capital Structure Recovery Waterfall & Fulcrum Security Migration

As enterprise value expands across reorganization scenarios, the fulcrum security migrates strictly down the capital structure seniority ladder, shifting equity upside and governance control from senior secured lenders to junior debtholders.

"""Lab CR4-L05-1: Capital Structure Recovery Waterfall & Fulcrum Security Analysis.

Demonstrates multi-tranche recovery waterfall allocation under strict APR,
fulcrum security migration across enterprise value scenarios,
and investor MOIC / IRR profiles for distressed debt acquisitions.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def allocate_waterfall(
    claims: list[tuple[str, float]],
    ev: float,
) -> list[dict[str, float | str | bool]]:
    """Allocate EV across prioritized claims and identify fulcrum tranche."""
    rem = ev
    results: list[dict[str, float | str | bool]] = []
    total_claims = sum(c[1] for c in claims)

    fulcrum_found = False
    for name, claim in claims:
        alloc = min(rem, claim)
        rem -= alloc
        rec_pct = (alloc / claim) if claim > 0 else 0.0

        is_fulcrum = False
        is_part = 0.0 < alloc < claim
        is_bound = abs(alloc - claim) < 1e-6 and abs(ev - (ev - rem)) < 1e-6 and rem == 0.0
        if not fulcrum_found and ev < total_claims and (is_part or is_bound):
            is_fulcrum = True
            fulcrum_found = True

        results.append(
            {
                "name": name,
                "claim": claim,
                "allocated": alloc,
                "recovery_pct": rec_pct,
                "is_fulcrum": is_fulcrum,
            }
        )

    return results


def main() -> None:
    # Capital structure claims (in $M)
    claims = [
        ("1st Lien Term Loan", 100.0),
        ("2nd Lien Notes", 60.0),
        ("Senior Unsecured Notes", 80.0),
        ("Subordinated Debentures", 40.0),
    ]
    total_debt = sum(c[1] for c in claims)  # 280.0

    ev_scenarios = [70.0, 130.0, 200.0, 260.0, 320.0]

    print("Capital Structure Recovery Waterfall Across Enterprise Value Scenarios:")
    print("Claims: 1st Lien=$100M, 2nd Lien=$60M, Sr Unsec=$80M, Sub Debt=$40M (Total Debt = $280M)")
    print("-" * 88)
    print("  EV ($M)  | 1st Lien Rec (%) | 2nd Lien Rec (%) | Sr Unsec Rec (%) | Sub Debt Rec (%) | Fulcrum Tranche")
    print("-" * 88)

    for ev in ev_scenarios:
        res = allocate_waterfall(claims, ev)
        fl_pct = float(res[0]["recovery_pct"]) * 100.0
        sl_pct = float(res[1]["recovery_pct"]) * 100.0
        unsec_pct = float(res[2]["recovery_pct"]) * 100.0
        sub_pct = float(res[3]["recovery_pct"]) * 100.0

        fulcrum_name = "None (Fully Solvent)"
        for r in res:
            if r["is_fulcrum"]:
                fulcrum_name = str(r["name"])
                break

        fl_s = f"{fl_pct:5.1f}%"
        sl_s = f"{sl_pct:5.1f}%"
        un_s = f"{unsec_pct:5.1f}%"
        sb_s = f"{sub_pct:5.1f}%"
        row = (
            f"   {ev:5.1f}   |      {fl_s}      |      {sl_s}      |"
            f"      {un_s}      |      {sb_s}      | {fulcrum_name}"
        )
        print(row)

    # Distressed Investor Analysis: Buying Senior Unsecured Notes at 35 cents on the dollar
    purchase_price = 0.35  # 35c
    holding_period = 1.5  # years
    unsec_claim = 80.0
    cost = purchase_price * unsec_claim

    print("\nDistressed Investor Return Profile: Senior Unsecured Notes ($80M claim, Bought @ 35c = $28M cost):")
    print("  EV ($M)  | Unsecured Recovery ($M) | Recovery (%) |   MOIC   | Annualized IRR")
    print("-" * 80)

    for ev in ev_scenarios:
        res = allocate_waterfall(claims, ev)
        unsec_rec = float(res[2]["allocated"])
        rec_pct = float(res[2]["recovery_pct"]) * 100.0
        moic = unsec_rec / cost if cost > 0 else 0.0
        irr = (moic ** (1.0 / holding_period) - 1.0) * 100.0 if moic > 0 else -100.0

        u_s = f"{unsec_rec:5.1f}"
        r_s = f"{rec_pct:5.1f}%"
        m_s = f"{moic:5.2f}x"
        i_s = f"{irr:5.1f}%"
        print(f"   {ev:5.1f}   |          {u_s}          |    {r_s}    |  {m_s}  |     {i_s}")

    # Invariant checks:
    # 1. Total allocated equals min(EV, Total Debt)
    for ev in ev_scenarios:
        res = allocate_waterfall(claims, ev)
        tot_alloc = sum(float(r["allocated"]) for r in res)
        assert abs(tot_alloc - min(ev, total_debt)) < 1e-9

    # 2. At EV=200, Senior Unsecured is the fulcrum security (recovers 40 out of 80 = 50%)
    res_200 = allocate_waterfall(claims, 200.0)
    assert res_200[2]["is_fulcrum"] is True
    assert abs(float(res_200[2]["recovery_pct"]) - 0.50) < 1e-9

    print("\nVerified waterfall invariants:")
    print("1. Enterprise value conservation holds exactly across all EV scenarios.")
    print("2. Fulcrum security migrates strictly down the seniority ladder as EV expands.")

Where the canon breaks

Waterfall and fulcrum security modeling breaks down under real-world restructuring complexities:

  • Intercreditor Agreement (ICA) Turnover Provisions: Second lien lenders routinely execute ICAs waiving voting rights and agreeing to "turn over" any reorganization distributions to first lien lenders until first lien claims are paid in full in cash. Even if the waterfall shows value breaking in the second lien tranche, turnover provisions can divert 100% of second lien equity to first lien lenders.
  • Rights Offerings and Backstop Fees: Reorganized equity is rarely distributed purely pro-rata to the fulcrum tranche. Instead, the fulcrum class is granted non-transferable subscription rights to purchase reorganized equity at a deep discount (e.g., 30% discount to plan value) to fund exit cash, with anchor hedge funds collecting 5% to 10% backstop commitment fees that heavily dilute passive tranche holders.
  • Valuation Fights and the "Battle of the Experts": Because the fulcrum tranche captures the reorganized equity, the debtor's enterprise valuation is fiercely litigated. Senior lenders submit low DCF valuations to cram down junior creditors; junior creditors submit high EV valuations to push the fulcrum security down to their tranche.

Misconceptions

  • The fulcrum security is always the lowest-ranking subordinated debt tranche.

    The fulcrum security is entirely determined by enterprise value relative to cumulative debt. If enterprise value is low, senior secured bank debt can be the fulcrum tranche, wiping out all junior notes and equity completely.

  • Purchasing a blocking position guarantees that an investor can dictate plan terms.

    Holding > 33.34% of an impaired class grants negative veto power to block consensual class acceptance under § 1126(c), but does not give affirmative power to force adoption of the investor's own plan. If the debtor satisfies the requirements of § 1129(b), the court can confirm the plan via cramdown over the investor's objection.

  • A 2.0x MOIC over two years represents a 100% annualized IRR.

    Annualized IRR accounts for compounding: \(\text{IRR} = (2.0)^{1/2} - 1 = \sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 41.42\%\), not 50% or 100%. Simple annualized return drastically overstates compound investment yield.

Problem set

Mechanics

Problem M1. A distressed company has: \(\$50\text{M}\) First Lien Term Loan, \(\$40\text{M}\) Second Lien Notes, and \(\$60\text{M}\) Senior Unsecured Bonds. Identify the fulcrum tranche and calculate the exact dollar recovery and recovery rate percentage for all three tranches under three enterprise value scenarios: (a) \(\text{EV} = \$40\text{M}\); (b) \(\text{EV} = \$70\text{M}\); (c) \(\text{EV} = \$120\text{M}\).

Solution

Cumulative claim thresholds: 1st Lien \(= \$50\text{M}\); 2nd Lien \(= 50 + 40 = \$90\text{M}\); Senior Unsecured \(= 90 + 60 = \$150\text{M}\).

1. Scenario A (\(\text{EV} = \$40\text{M}\)):

  • 1st Lien: receives \(\$40\text{M}\) (\(40/50 = 80.0\%\) recovery). Fulcrum = 1st Lien.
  • 2nd Lien: \(\$0\text{M}\) (0.0%).
  • Senior Unsecured: \(\$0\text{M}\) (0.0%).

2. Scenario B (\(\text{EV} = \$70\text{M}\)):

  • 1st Lien: receives \(\$50\text{M}\) (\(100.0\%\) recovery).
  • 2nd Lien: receives \(70 - 50 = \$20\text{M}\) (\(20/40 = 50.0\%\) recovery). Fulcrum = 2nd Lien.
  • Senior Unsecured: \(\$0\text{M}\) (0.0%).

3. Scenario C (\(\text{EV} = \$120\text{M}\)):

  • 1st Lien: receives \(\$50\text{M}\) (\(100.0\%\)).
  • 2nd Lien: receives \(\$40\text{M}\) (\(100.0\%\)).
  • Senior Unsecured: receives \(120 - 90 = \$30\text{M}\) (\(30/60 = 50.0\%\) recovery). Fulcrum = Senior Unsecured.

Problem M2. A distressed debt fund acquires \(\$25\text{M}\) par value of Second Lien Notes at 40 cents on the dollar (\(\$10\text{M}\) invested capital). The Chapter 11 reorganization concludes after 2.0 years. Under the confirmed Plan of Reorganization, the Second Lien Notes receive a 72% recovery paid as \(\$10\text{M}\) in new take-back debt and \(\$8\text{M}\) in reorganized common equity. Calculate the fund's total dollar recovery, MOIC, and annualized IRR.

Solution

1. Total dollar recovery: \(\$25\text{M} \times 0.72 = \$18.0\text{M}\) (\(\$10\text{M}\) debt \(+ \$8\text{M}\) equity).

2. Multiple on Invested Capital: \(\text{MOIC} = \frac{\$18.0\text{M}}{\$10.0\text{M}} = 1.80\text{x}\).

3. Annualized IRR over \(T = 2.0\) years:

\[ \text{IRR} = (1.80)^{1/2.0} - 1 = \sqrt{1.80} - 1 \approx 1.3416 - 1 = 34.16\% \]

Problem M3. An impaired class of unsecured bonds has \(\$150\text{M}\) in total allowed claims. A distressed hedge fund wishes to establish a blocking position to prevent a non-consensual plan from being accepted by the class under § 1126(c). If voter turnout is anticipated to be 80% of total claims, what is the minimum dollar amount of claims the fund must vote against the plan to block class acceptance?

Solution

1. Voting claims: \(80\% \times \$150\text{M} = \$120\text{M}\).

2. Acceptance requires at least two-thirds (66.67%) of voting claims to vote in favor. To block acceptance, negative votes must exceed one-third (33.33%):

\[ \text{Blocking threshold} > \frac{1}{3} \times \$120\text{M} = \$40.0\text{M} \]

The fund must vote strictly more than \(\$40\text{M}\) in claims (e.g., \(\$40,000,001\)) to block class acceptance.

Proof

Problem P1. Prove that the investor's payoff function \(\Pi_k(V) = \max(0, \min(D_k, V - C_{k-1}))\) for tranche \(k\) as a function of enterprise value \(V\) is isomorphic to a bull call spread on enterprise value, and express \(\Pi_k(V)\) as a portfolio of European call options.

Solution

Let \((V - K)^+ = \max(0, V - K)\) denote the payoff of a call option on \(V\) with strike \(K\). For tranche \(k\) with claim \(D_k\) and prior cumulative claims \(C_{k-1}\):

\[ \Pi_k(V) = \begin{cases} 0 & V \le C_{k-1} \\ V - C_{k-1} & C_{k-1} < V < C_{k-1} + D_k \\ D_k & V \ge C_{k-1} + D_k \end{cases} \]

Consider the bull call spread portfolio consisting of long one call with strike \(K_1 = C_{k-1}\) and short one call with strike \(K_2 = C_{k-1} + D_k = C_k\):

\[ \text{Payoff}(V) = (V - C_{k-1})^+ - (V - C_k)^+ \]
  1. If \(V \le C_{k-1}\): both calls expire worthless, payoff \(= 0 - 0 = 0 = \Pi_k(V)\).
  2. If \(C_{k-1} < V \le C_k\): long call is in the money, short call expires worthless, payoff \(= (V - C_{k-1}) - 0 = V - C_{k-1} = \Pi_k(V)\).
  3. If \(V > C_k\): both calls are in the money, payoff \(= (V - C_{k-1}) - (V - C_k) = C_k - C_{k-1} = D_k = \Pi_k(V)\).

Thus, \(\Pi_k(V) \equiv (V - C_{k-1})^+ - (V - C_k)^+\) identically. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P2. Prove that if an investor holds the fulcrum security, the marginal sensitivity of their dollar recovery to an increase in reorganization enterprise value is exactly unity: \(\frac{\partial V_{\text{fulcrum}}}{\partial V} = 1\), whereas for all senior and junior tranches, \(\frac{\partial V_j}{\partial V} = 0\).

Solution

From Theorem 1.1, for enterprise value \(V\) strictly in the interior of the fulcrum interval \((C_{k^*-1}, C_{k^*})\):

  1. For any senior tranche \(j < k^*\), \(V_j = D_j\) (constant), so \(\frac{\partial V_j}{\partial V} = 0\).
  2. For any junior tranche \(j > k^*\), \(V_j = 0\) (constant), so \(\frac{\partial V_j}{\partial V} = 0\).
  3. For the fulcrum tranche \(k^*\), \(V_{k^*} = V - C_{k^*-1}\). Differentiating with respect to \(V\) yields \(\frac{\partial V_{k^*}}{\partial V} = \frac{d}{dV}(V - C_{k^*-1}) = 1\).

The fulcrum security absorbs 100% of marginal valuation changes at the boundary, functioning as synthetic equity. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P3. Prove that under any concave utility function \(u(\cdot)\) with \(u'' < 0\), a risk-averse investor strictly prefers holding senior secured debt over junior fulcrum debt when both have identical expected monetary returns \(\mathbb{E}[\Pi]\).

Solution

Let \(\Pi_{\text{sec}}(V)\) and \(\Pi_{\text{ful}}(V)\) be the payoffs of senior and fulcrum debt with \(\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{\text{sec}}] = \mathbb{E}[\Pi_{\text{ful}}]\). From Problem P1, senior debt has lower strike prices \(K_1, K_2\) and reaches its maximum payoff at a lower enterprise value, truncating upside but providing a higher downside floor. Fulcrum debt has higher variance and suffers complete losses on low EV realizations. By Rothschild and Stiglitz's theorem on second-order stochastic dominance, senior debt second-order stochastically dominates fulcrum debt: \(\Pi_{\text{sec}} \succeq_{\text{SSD}} \Pi_{\text{ful}}\). Therefore, for every strictly concave utility function \(u\), \(\mathbb{E}[u(\Pi_{\text{sec}})] > \mathbb{E}[u(\Pi_{\text{ful}})]\). \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem E1. A distressed hedge fund establishes a loan-to-own position in the Senior Subordinated Notes of an auto parts supplier. Contractual debt is \(\$120\text{M}\) First Lien and \(\$80\text{M}\) Senior Subordinated. The fund purchases \(\$60\text{M}\) of the Subordinated Notes at 20 cents on the dollar (\(\$12\text{M}\) cost). The debtor reorganizes via a debt-for-equity swap where First Lien is reinstated at par and Subordinated Notes receive 100% of new equity. If the company achieves post-emergence EBITDA of \(\$25\text{M}\) and trades at an exit multiple of 6.0x EV/EBITDA, calculate the fund's post-emergence equity value and MOIC.

Solution

1. Emergence Enterprise Value: \(25 \times 6.0 = \$150.0\text{M}\).

2. Reinstated First Lien Debt: \(\$120.0\text{M}\).

3. Reorganized Equity Value: \(\text{EV} - \text{Debt} = 150.0 - 120.0 = \$30.0\text{M}\).

4. Fund's ownership share: The fund owns \(\$60\text{M}\) out of \(\$80\text{M}\) subordinated notes, or \(60 / 80 = 75.0\%\) of the class.

5. Fund's equity value: \(75.0\% \times \$30.0\text{M} = \$22.5\text{M}\).

6. Fund's MOIC: \(\frac{\$22.5\text{M}}{\$12.0\text{M}} = 1.875\text{x}\).

Problem E2. An analyst estimates the annualized return on a distressed debt trade under two exit timing scenarios: (a) Quick 363 sale closing in 6 months with 1.30x MOIC; (b) Contested Chapter 11 plan confirmation requiring 24 months with 1.65x MOIC. Which strategy generates a higher annualized IRR?

Solution

1. Quick 363 sale (\(T = 0.5\) years):

\[ \text{IRR}_{363} = (1.30)^{1/0.5} - 1 = (1.30)^2 - 1 = 1.69 - 1 = 69.0\% \]

2. Contested Chapter 11 (\(T = 2.0\) years):

\[ \text{IRR}_{Ch11} = (1.65)^{1/2.0} - 1 = \sqrt{1.65} - 1 \approx 1.2845 - 1 = 28.45\% \]

The quick 363 sale generates a significantly higher annualized IRR (69.0% vs. 28.45%) despite a lower absolute dollar multiple, illustrating the high time-value penalty of extended Chapter 11 proceedings.

Problem E3. A distressed investor purchases \(\$40\text{M}\) face value of unsecured notes at 15 cents (\(\$6\text{M}\) cost). In an equity rights offering, noteholders can subscribe for new equity at a 30% discount to plan enterprise value. If the investor fully subscribes for their \(\$4\text{M}\) rights allocation, calculate their total cash invested and blended breakeven valuation.

Solution

1. Secondary debt purchase cost: \(\$6.0\text{M}\).

2. Rights offering cash subscription: \(\$4.0\text{M}\).

3. Total cash invested: \(6.0 + 4.0 = \$10.0\text{M}\).

4. The \(\$4\text{M}\) rights subscription purchases equity valued at plan value of \(\$4.0\text{M} / (1 - 0.30) = \$5.71\text{M}\). The investor's total portfolio breaks even when combined distributions (debt recovery plus equity rights value) equal \(\$10.0\text{M}\).

Research

Problem R1. Analyze the governance conflicts that arise when a single distressed hedge fund holds cross-tranche positions (e.g., both First Lien Term Loans and Fulcrum Senior Unsecured Notes). How can such an investor utilize its senior voting power to expropriate non-cross-holding junior creditors?

Solution

A cross-holding fund faces asymmetric payoff incentives. As a first lien lender, the fund can advocate for a low reorganization valuation, asserting that enterprise value breaks in the first lien tranche and proposing a Section 363 credit bid or strict cramdown that wipes out junior debt. Concurrently, the fund can structure a Plan of Reorganization where first lien debt receives 100% of new equity, effectively buying the company at a trough valuation and capturing future operational upside while destroying the claims of pure junior noteholders who lack first lien protection.

Problem R2. Explain the mechanics and legal enforceability of "silent second" intercreditor agreements. Under what circumstances can a bankruptcy court refuse to enforce a junior creditor's contractual waiver of plan voting rights?

Solution

In a "silent second" agreement, second lien lenders contractually agree that first lien lenders may vote their claims or that second lien lenders will vote in favor of any plan supported by first lien lenders. While Delaware and New York bankruptcy courts generally enforce contractual subordination under Bankruptcy Code § 510(a), several bankruptcy decisions (e.g., In re 203 North LaSalle and In re Boston Generating) held that pre-petition waivers of statutory voting rights under § 1126(a) violate federal bankruptcy public policy, allowing junior creditors to cast dissenting ballots despite the intercreditor agreement.

Problem R3. Formulate a structural asset valuation model where post-reorganization enterprise value follows a jump-diffusion process. How does post-emergence operational execution risk affect the discount rate applied to equitized fulcrum debt relative to reinstated fixed-income paper?

Solution

Reinstated take-back debt has fixed coupon cash flows with downside protection provided by the new equity cushion. In contrast, equitized fulcrum paper absorbs 100% of the operational beta, customer recovery volatility, and jump risks associated with post-bankruptcy emergence. Consequently, investors apply a significantly higher discount rate (typically 15%–25% cost of equity vs. 8%–12% for take-back debt), leading to deep market discounts on fulcrum securities during plan negotiation.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What defines the "fulcrum security" in a corporate capital structure recovery waterfall?

    Options
    The fulcrum security is the tranche where enterprise value runs out; tranches above are paid in full, while the fulcrum tranche absorbs the partial loss and typically receives the reorganized equity.

  2. What percentage of allowed claims must an investor acquire to possess a blocking veto in Chapter 11 class voting?

    Options
    Because class acceptance requires at least two-thirds (66.67%) in dollar amount, holding strictly more than one-third (>33.34%) gives the investor an absolute veto to block consensual acceptance.

  3. In a "loan-to-own" distressed strategy, what is the investor's primary objective?

    Options
    Loan-to-own investors target the fulcrum security at a discount, aiming to convert their debt claims into controlling equity of the deleveraged post-emergence company.

  4. What happens to the fulcrum security when the debtor's estimated enterprise value increases?

    Options
    As enterprise value expands, senior tranches become fully covered, shifting the partial impairment point (and reorganized equity allocation) down to more junior tranches.

  5. How is the payoff of an intermediate debt tranche mathematically isomorphic to financial option contracts on enterprise value?

    Options
    Tranche payoff is \(\Pi_k(V) = (V - C_{k-1})^+ - (V - C_k)^+\), which is mathematically identical to a bull call spread on enterprise value.

  6. If an investor achieves a Multiple on Invested Capital (MOIC) of 1.44x over a 2.0-year holding period, what is the annualized IRR?

    Options
    \(\text{IRR} = (1.44)^{1/2} - 1 = \sqrt{1.44} - 1 = 1.20 - 1 = 20.0\%\).

  7. In the lab simulation (lab-CR4-L05-1), which tranche was identified as the fulcrum security when enterprise value was $200M?

    Options
    At $200M EV, 1st Lien ($100M) and 2nd Lien ($60M) were paid in full ($160M cumulative); Senior Unsecured ($80M claim) absorbed the remaining $40M EV (50% recovery), acting as fulcrum.

  8. What purpose do "backstop fees" serve in a Chapter 11 rights offering sponsored by distressed funds?

    Options
    Backstop parties commit to fund 100% of the rights offering if other creditors decline to participate, earning valuable commitment fees (typically 5%–10% in discounted stock).
