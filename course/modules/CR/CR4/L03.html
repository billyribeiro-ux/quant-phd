The Absolute Priority Rule & Historical Violations

The Absolute Priority Rule is the foundational doctrine of corporate bankruptcy jurisprudence, requiring senior creditors to be paid in full before junior classes receive anything. Yet in actual Chapter 11 reorganizations, equity holders and junior debtholders routinely extract valuable consideration despite senior claims being severely impaired. Understanding the strategic game between debtor exclusivity, administrative estate burn, and litigation delay explains why rational senior creditors willingly consent to carve-outs that violate strict priority.

1Statutory Absolute Priority and the Cramdown Standard

Under Title 11 of the United States Code (the Bankruptcy Code), a debtor corporation formulates a Plan of Reorganization (POR) to restructure its obligations. Confirmation of a proposed plan can occur via one of two statutory pathways: consensual acceptance under Section 1129(a), or non-consensual judicial "cramdown" under Section 1129(b).

Definition 1.1 (Absolute Priority Rule)

The Absolute Priority Rule (APR) is codified in Bankruptcy Code § 1129(b)(2)(B)(ii). It establishes that for a Plan of Reorganization to be confirmed over the objection of an impaired, non-accepting class of unsecured claims ("cramdown"), the plan must be "fair and equitable." Specifically, no holder of any claim or interest that is junior to the claims of the dissenting class may receive or retain any property under the plan on account of such junior claim or interest unless the dissenting senior class is paid in full (receiving 100% of allowed claims).

The statutory rule imposes a strict lexicographic priority: if senior creditors hold allowed claims of \(\$100\text{M}\) and the reorganized enterprise is valued at \(\$80\text{M}\), strict APR dictates that senior creditors receive all \(\$80\text{M}\) and junior classes (subordinated debt and equity) receive exactly zero.

Definition 1.2 (Deviations from Absolute Priority / APR Violations)

An APR violation (or deviation from absolute priority) occurs whenever a junior class of claims or interests receives positive economic value (in cash, take-back debt, reorganized common equity, or warrants) under a confirmed Chapter 11 plan while an allowed senior class is not paid in full (\(R_{\text{senior}} < 1.0\)).

Statutorily, the APR applies as a mandatory legal constraint only when a dissenting class votes against the plan and the debtor invokes judicial cramdown under § 1129(b). If every impaired class accepts the plan by the requisite statutory majority under § 1126(c)—at least two-thirds in dollar amount and more than one-half in number of allowed claims voting—the bankruptcy court confirms the plan under § 1129(a)(8) without evaluating the APR.

2Debtor Exclusivity and Holdout Leverage

Why do senior creditors voluntarily vote to give away value to junior equityholders when they could insist on strict APR? The answer lies in the procedural architecture of Chapter 11.

Theorem 1.1 (Equilibrium Carve-Out under Threat of Delay)

Let a debtor estate have current reorganization value \(V\). A senior creditor class holds claim \(D > V\) (the firm is economically insolvent). Under Bankruptcy Code § 1121, debtor management possesses the exclusive right to file a plan for a dispute duration of \(T\) months. Delay inflicts a monthly administrative burn rate of \(\beta\) (professional fees and operational value destruction), and a contested cramdown confirmation imposes direct litigation costs \(C_L\).

If the debtor equity class threatens to delay and litigate cramdown, the contested judicial cramdown payoff to the senior creditor is:

\[ V_{\text{cram}} = \max(0, V - \beta T - C_L) \]

Let \(\gamma \in [0, 1]\) denote the senior creditor's generalized Nash bargaining share. The equilibrium consensual carve-out payment \(K^*\) granted to equity satisfies:

\[ K^* = (1 - \gamma)(V - V_{\text{cram}}) = (1 - \gamma)(\beta T + C_L) \]

and the net payoff to senior debt is \(V - K^* = \gamma V + (1 - \gamma)V_{\text{cram}} > V_{\text{cram}}\). Both parties strictly prefer the consensual APR violation over litigated cramdown.

Proof

The bargaining game occurs over the total surplus generated by reaching a consensual agreement today rather than fighting through \(T\) months of exclusivity and cramdown litigation. Under litigated cramdown, the non-consensual terminal payoffs (threat points) are \(V_{\text{cram}}\) for senior debt and \(0\) for equity (because strict APR is enforced by the judge at cramdown). Consensual settlement today preserves full estate value \(V\). The surplus from consensus is \(\Delta = V - (V_{\text{cram}} + 0) = \beta T + C_L\). Under generalized Nash bargaining with weights \(\gamma\) and \(1 - \gamma\), equity receives its threat point plus its surplus share: \(K^* = 0 + (1 - \gamma)\Delta = (1 - \gamma)(\beta T + C_L)\). Senior creditors receive \(V_{\text{cram}} + \gamma \Delta = V - K^*\). Since \(\Delta > 0\) for any positive delay burn or litigation cost, \(K^* > 0\) and \(V - K^* > V_{\text{cram}}\). Consensus strictly Pareto-dominates litigated cramdown. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.1 (Consensual Equity Carve-Out Calculation)

Consider an insolvent corporation with current estate value \(V = \$100\text{M}\) and senior debt \(D = \$140\text{M}\). If senior lenders force a cramdown, debtor management will contest valuation and extend exclusivity by \(T = 6\) months. The monthly burn rate is \(\beta = \$1.0\text{M}\) and direct litigation costs are \(C_L = \$6.0\text{M}\). Total value destroyed by fighting is \(\beta T + C_L = 1.0(6) + 6 = \$12.0\text{M}\).

Under litigated cramdown, the estate is reduced to \(100 - 12 = \$88\text{M}\), and senior lenders receive \(\$88\text{M}\) while equity receives \(\$0\). If senior lenders possess bargaining power \(\gamma = 0.60\), the consensual carve-out to equity is:

\[ K^* = (1 - 0.60) \times \$12.0\text{M} = 0.40 \times \$12.0\text{M} = \$4.80\text{M} \]

Senior lenders receive \(100 - 4.80 = \$95.20\text{M}\). By agreeing to an APR violation of \(\$4.80\text{M}\) (4.8% of estate value), senior lenders improve their net recovery by \(\$95.20\text{M} - \$88.00\text{M} = \$7.20\text{M}\).

Remark 1.1 (Evolution of APR Violations in Chapter 11 Practice)

In the 1980s and 1990s, academic studies documented that deviations from absolute priority occurred in approximately 75% of large public Chapter 11 cases, with equity receiving an average carve-out of 5% to 8% of total reorganization value. In modern Chapter 11 practice, institutional senior lenders have curtailed equity holdout power using aggressive pre-petition Restructuring Support Agreements (RSAs), DIP financing covenant milestones (requiring strict plan filing deadlines under threat of liquidation), and expedited Section 363 asset sales.

Lab 1 · Strategic Chapter 11 Bargaining & Consensual APR Violations

Senior impaired creditors rationally concede positive equity carve-outs to avoid administrative burn and litigation delay, with the equilibrium APR violation expanding monotonically in delay duration and burn rate.

"""Lab CR4-L03-1: Strategic Bargaining and Absolute Priority Rule Violations in Chapter 11.

Models strategic bargaining between senior impaired creditors and debtor equityholders
under Bankruptcy Code Section 1121 exclusivity.
Demonstrates why senior creditors concede consensual carve-outs to avoid administrative burn
and cramdown litigation costs under Section 1129(b).
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_strategic_carveout(
    v_estate: float,
    d_senior: float,
    burn_rate_monthly: float,
    litigation_cost: float,
    delay_months: float,
    senior_bargaining_power: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate equilibrium carve-out payment to equity under threat of delay and cramdown litigation.

    v_estate: Reorganization value before delay.
    d_senior: Senior creditor claim (d_senior > v_estate).
    burn_rate_monthly: Administrative and operational value erosion per month.
    litigation_cost: Direct legal, expert witness, and court expenses of a contested cramdown.
    delay_months: Duration of exclusivity dispute before cramdown confirmation.
    senior_bargaining_power: Share of delay-avoidance surplus captured by senior creditor (gamma in [0, 1]).
    """
    # In a cramdown without consensus, the estate suffers delay burn and litigation expenses
    total_delay_burn = burn_rate_monthly * delay_months
    cramdown_estate_value = max(0.0, v_estate - total_delay_burn - litigation_cost)

    # Under strict cramdown, senior creditor gets min(cramdown_estate_value, d_senior)
    senior_cramdown_payoff = min(cramdown_estate_value, d_senior)

    # Senior cost of litigation and delay
    senior_cost_of_fight = v_estate - senior_cramdown_payoff
    equity_carveout = (1.0 - senior_bargaining_power) * senior_cost_of_fight

    senior_consensual_payoff = v_estate - equity_carveout

    return {
        "v_estate": v_estate,
        "d_senior": d_senior,
        "delay_burn": total_delay_burn,
        "litigation_cost": litigation_cost,
        "senior_cramdown_payoff": senior_cramdown_payoff,
        "equity_carveout": equity_carveout,
        "senior_consensual_payoff": senior_consensual_payoff,
        "apr_violation_pct": (equity_carveout / v_estate) * 100.0,
    }


def main() -> None:
    # Stylized insolvent debtor: Reorganization value V = 100, Senior claim D = 140
    v_estate = 100.0
    d_senior = 140.0
    litigation_cost = 6.0
    senior_power = 0.60  # gamma = 0.60 (senior captures 60% of surplus)

    delays = [3.0, 6.0, 12.0, 18.0]
    burn_rates = [0.5, 1.0, 1.5]  # $M per month

    print("Strategic Chapter 11 Bargaining: Equilibrium Equity Carve-Out (APR Violation):")
    print("V = 100.0, D_senior = 140.0 (Firm Insolvent under strict APR), Litigation Cost = 6.0")
    print("Delay (mo) | Burn Rate ($M/mo) | Senior Cramdown | Senior Consensual | Equity Carve-Out | APR Viol (%)")
    print("-----------+--------------------+-----------------+-------------------+------------------+-------------")

    for delay in delays:
        for burn in burn_rates:
            res = solve_strategic_carveout(v_estate, d_senior, burn, litigation_cost, delay, senior_power)
            sc_cram = f"{res['senior_cramdown_payoff']:5.1f}"
            sc_cons = f"{res['senior_consensual_payoff']:5.1f}"
            eq_carv = f"{res['equity_carveout']:4.2f}"
            apr_v = f"{res['apr_violation_pct']:4.2f}%"
            d_s = f"{delay:4.1f}"
            b_s = f"{burn:4.1f}"
            row = (
                f"   {d_s}    |        {b_s}        |      {sc_cram}      |       {sc_cons}       |"
                f"       {eq_carv}       |    {apr_v}"
            )
            print(row)

    # Invariant checks:
    # 1. Consensual payoff strictly dominates cramdown payoff for senior creditor
    res_base = solve_strategic_carveout(v_estate, d_senior, 1.0, litigation_cost, 6.0, senior_power)
    assert res_base["senior_consensual_payoff"] > res_base["senior_cramdown_payoff"]
    # 2. Equity receives positive carve-out despite strict balance-sheet insolvency
    assert res_base["equity_carveout"] > 0.0
    # 3. Sum of payoffs equals total estate value
    assert abs(res_base["senior_consensual_payoff"] + res_base["equity_carveout"] - v_estate) < 1e-9

    print("\nVerified bargaining invariants:")
    print("1. Consensual agreement strictly dominates cramdown payoff for senior debt.")
    print("2. Equity extracts positive carve-out despite zero economic value under strict APR.")
    print("3. Conservation of estate value holds identically.")

Where the canon breaks

The classical bargaining model of APR violations breaks down under specific modern institutional dynamics:

  • DIP Financing Milestones and Roll-Ups: Modern DIP lenders condition post-petition liquidity on draconian case milestones (such as plan filing within 60 days). If the debtor misses a milestone, the DIP lender can terminate funding and foreclose, eliminating the debtor's credible threat of delay.
  • Gifting and the Jevic Prohibition: In Czyzewski v. Jevic Holding Corp. (2017), the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that bankruptcy courts cannot approve structured dismissals that distribute estate value to junior unsecured creditors while skipping over impaired senior priority tax or wage claims without consent, constraining non-consensual "class-skipping" gifts.
  • Claims Trading and Distressed Hedge Funds: Traditional banks were relationship lenders sensitive to public relations and litigation expense. Modern distressed debt hedge funds often purchase blocking positions specifically to litigate cramdown, refusing any consensual equity carve-out.

Misconceptions

  • The Absolute Priority Rule prohibits equity from ever receiving value in Chapter 11.

    The APR only applies when a dissenting class is crammed down under Section 1129(b). If impaired creditors vote to accept the plan under Section 1129(a)(8), consensual distributions or equity warrants are fully legal and routine.

  • APR violations occur because bankruptcy judges fail to understand financial contracts.

    APR violations in consensual plans are negotiated economic concessions between sophisticated institutional creditors and the debtor. Senior lenders voluntarily concede carve-outs to truncate expensive professional fees and operational decay.

  • Debtor exclusivity lasts indefinitely until management agrees to step down.

    Under Bankruptcy Code § 1121(d), debtor exclusivity is capped at a statutory maximum of 18 months for plan filing and 20 months for plan solicitation, after which any party in interest may file a competing plan of reorganization.

Mechanics

Problem M1. An insolvent debtor has reorganization value \(V = \$120\text{M}\). Senior secured claims are \(\$150\text{M}\). Contesting cramdown will delay plan confirmation by 9 months. The monthly administrative burn rate is \(\$1.2\text{M}\) and expert witness litigation costs are \(\$4.2\text{M}\). If senior lenders have bargaining power \(\gamma = 0.70\), compute the contested cramdown estate value, the equilibrium carve-out to junior equity, and the net consensual payoff to senior lenders.

Solution

1. Total delay burn: \(9 \times \$1.2\text{M} = \$10.8\text{M}\).

2. Total fight cost: \(\$10.8\text{M} + \$4.2\text{M} = \$15.0\text{M}\).

3. Cramdown estate value: \(V_{\text{cram}} = 120 - 15 = \$105.0\text{M}\).

4. Senior cramdown payoff: \(\min(105, 150) = \$105.0\text{M}\).

5. Equilibrium carve-out to equity: \(K^* = (1 - 0.70) \times \$15.0\text{M} = 0.30 \times 15.0 = \$4.50\text{M}\).

6. Senior consensual payoff: \(120 - 4.50 = \$115.50\text{M}\).

Senior lenders gain \(\$115.50\text{M} - \$105.0\text{M} = \$10.50\text{M}\) by agreeing to the consensual carve-out.

Problem M2. In a Chapter 11 voting procedure under § 1126(c), an impaired class of senior unsecured claims consists of 80 creditors holding \(\$60\text{M}\) of total claims. At the voting deadline, 50 creditors cast ballots representing \(\$45\text{M}\) of claims. What is the minimum dollar amount and minimum number of creditors that must vote "yes" for the class to accept the plan?

Solution

Under Section 1126(c), class acceptance requires at least two-thirds (66.67%) in dollar amount of claims voting, and more than one-half (50%) in number of creditors voting:

  1. Dollar threshold: \(\frac{2}{3} \times \$45\text{M} = \$30.0\text{M}\). Creditors voting in favor must hold at least \(\$30\text{M}\) in allowed claims.
  2. Numerosity threshold: More than half of 50 voting creditors: \(\lfloor 50/2 \rfloor + 1 = 26\) creditors.

Both criteria apply strictly to ballots actually cast, not total outstanding claims.

Problem M3. A debtor proposes a consensual plan allocating \(\$5\text{M}\) in new common stock warrants to old equityholders. Senior notes (\(\$100\text{M}\) face value) receive \(\$70\text{M}\) in new debt and equity. General unsecured trade creditors (\(\$20\text{M}\) face value) receive \(\$4\text{M}\). Express the APR violation in dollar terms and as a percentage of total distributed value (\(\$79\text{M}\)).

Solution

1. Dollar APR violation: Old equity receives \(\$5\text{M}\) despite senior notes suffering a \(\$30\text{M}\) write-down and trade creditors suffering a \(\$16\text{M}\) write-down. The dollar violation is \(\$5.0\text{M}\).

2. Percentage of estate value: \(\frac{\$5\text{M}}{\$79\text{M}} \times 100\% = 6.33\%\).

Proof

Problem P1. Prove that the equilibrium equity carve-out \(K^*\) in the strategic delay bargaining game is strictly increasing in the debtor's litigation delay duration \(T\) and the administrative burn rate \(\beta\), provided \(\gamma < 1\).

Solution

From Theorem 1.1, the equilibrium carve-out is \(K^*(T, \beta) = (1 - \gamma)(\beta T + C_L)\) whenever \(V - \beta T - C_L > 0\). Taking partial derivatives:

\[ \frac{\partial K^*}{\partial T} = (1 - \gamma)\beta > 0 \quad (\text{since } \gamma < 1 \text{ and } \beta > 0) \] \[ \frac{\partial K^*}{\partial \beta} = (1 - \gamma)T > 0 \quad (\text{since } \gamma < 1 \text{ and } T > 0) \]

Both partial derivatives are strictly positive. Longer potential exclusivity delays and higher estate cash burn rates increase the deadweight destruction threatened by equity, expanding the surplus from settlement and strictly increasing the equilibrium transfer to equity. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P2. Prove that if senior creditors possess absolute bargaining power (\(\gamma = 1\)), the equilibrium APR violation vanishes (\(K^* = 0\)), yet senior creditors still capture the full delay-avoidance surplus.

Solution

When \(\gamma = 1\), the generalized Nash bargaining solution assigns the entire surplus to senior debt. The payoff to equity is \(K^* = (1 - 1)(\beta T + C_L) = 0\). Senior debt receives \(V - K^* = V\). Senior creditors avoid the litigation loss \(\beta T + C_L\) and retain 100% of the preserved estate value \(V\), yielding an economic gain of \(V - V_{\text{cram}} = \beta T + C_L\) over the cramdown threat point while paying zero carve-out to equity. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P3. Prove that under Bankruptcy Code § 1129(b)(2)(B), a court cannot confirm a plan cramming down unsecured creditors if existing equity retains any interest, even if the retained interest is deemed to have de minimis or negative economic value.

Solution

The statutory text of § 1129(b)(2)(B)(ii) specifies that no holder of any junior claim or interest may "receive or retain under the plan on account of such junior claim or interest any property." In Norwest Bank Worthington v. Ahlers (1988), the U.S. Supreme Court confirmed that the statutory phrase "any property" encompasses stock, voting control, warrants, and governance rights, regardless of whether balance sheet equity is mathematically insolvent or shares have zero present liquidation value. The retention of control rights constitutes property under federal bankruptcy law, precluding confirmation over a dissenting impaired class. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem E1. Empirical bankruptcy studies examining 1980–2000 Chapter 11 cases found that equity received positive distributions in 74% of cases where unsecured debt was impaired, with an average equity recovery of 6.2% of total distributed value. Contrast this with modern post-2015 Chapter 11 cases, where RSAs and § 363 sales occur in over 60% of large corporate filings. Why has the frequency and magnitude of equity carve-outs declined?

Solution

The decline in equity carve-outs reflects structural changes in bankruptcy practice: (1) Pre-petition Restructuring Support Agreements (RSAs) lock in voting commitments before filing, locking out common equity; (2) DIP financing agreements include tight milestones (mandating plan filing within 45–90 days) backed by remedies that terminate exclusivity; and (3) Increased prevalence of expedited Section 363 asset sales, which sell the operating business as a going concern to senior credit bidders, leaving an empty corporate shell where equity holdout leverage is zero.

Problem E2. An analyst reviews a distressed energy producer with \(\$500\text{M}\) of first lien debt trading at 70 cents and \(\$200\text{M}\) of junior unsecured notes trading at 15 cents. The market capitalization of common equity is \(\$10\text{M}\). What is the total enterprise debt market value, and what does the positive equity market capitalization imply about option value and expected APR violations?

Solution

1. Market value of debt: \(\$500\text{M} \times 0.70 + \$200\text{M} \times 0.15 = \$350\text{M} + \$30\text{M} = \$380\text{M}\).

2. Total firm market value: \(\$380\text{M} + \$10\text{M} = \$390\text{M}\).

3. Economic implication: Total contractual debt is \(\$700\text{M}\), far exceeding the firm's \(\$390\text{M}\) market valuation. Under strict APR, equity is deeply out of the money and should trade at zero. The positive \(\$10\text{M}\) equity valuation reflects two factors: (a) Black–Scholes option value on potential oil price recovery before filing, and (b) the expected strategic carve-out equity can extract during Chapter 11 restructuring negotiations.

Problem E3. A distressed retailer enters Chapter 11 with professional fee burn (legal, financial advisory, and restructuring consultants) averaging \(\$3.5\text{M}\) per month. If the reorganization takes 14 months to reach plan confirmation instead of 4 months via a prepackaged plan, calculate the cumulative deadweight loss from administrative professional fees.

Solution

Excess duration: \(14 - 4 = 10\) months.

Cumulative excess administrative burn: \(10 \times \$3.5\text{M} = \$35.0\text{M}\).

This \(\$35\text{M}\) represents cash drained directly from creditor recoveries to fund estate professionals during extended exclusivity litigation.

Research

Problem R1. Analyze the legal and economic implications of the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Czyzewski v. Jevic Holding Corp. (2017) regarding structured dismissals. How does Jevic constrain senior creditors and debtors from crafting consensual carve-outs that bypass intermediate priority priority classes?

Solution

In Jevic, the debtor reached a settlement with senior secured lenders and general unsecured creditors that distributed estate assets and dismissed the case, but deliberately excluded intermediate priority WARN Act wage claims. The Supreme Court ruled that bankruptcy courts cannot approve non-consensual structured dismissals that deviate from Bankruptcy Code § 507 priority without the affected creditors' consent. This ruling closed a major loophole, preventing senior lenders from "gifting" value to general unsecured creditors or equity while bypassing disfavored statutory priority claimants.

Problem R2. Explain the "New Value Corollary" to the Absolute Priority Rule. Under what judicial conditions (such as Bank of America v. 203 North LaSalle) can old equityholders retain ownership in a reorganized company over the objection of an impaired senior class?

Solution

The New Value Corollary allows old equity to retain equity interests if they contribute fresh, new capital to the reorganized firm. Under 203 North LaSalle (1999), the Supreme Court held that the contribution must be: (1) in new money or money's worth; (2) substantial; (3) essential to the successful reorganization; and (4) subject to market testing (such as a competitive auction or opportunity for competing bids). Management/equity cannot be granted an exclusive, non-market-tested option to acquire the reorganized equity.

Problem R3. Formulate a dynamic continuous-time game of Chapter 11 bargaining where the debtor controls the stopping time of the reorganization and firm volatility creates option value. How does creditor heterogeneity (collateralized bank lenders vs. distressed hedge funds holding CDS protection) affect the duration of bankruptcy?

Solution

Creditors holding "empty voting" positions (owning debt accompanied by purchased Credit Default Swap protection exceeding their bond holdings) have negative economic exposure to firm recovery. Such creditors benefit from extended delay and lower reorganization recovery, as failure to reach consensus or liquidation maximizes the CDS settlement payout. This misalignment destroys the standard surplus-sharing game, extending Chapter 11 duration and frequently forcing contentious cramdown litigation.

    Options
    Section 1126(c) establishes the dual threshold: at least two-thirds in dollar amount and a simple majority (>50%) in number of creditors voting.

  3. Why do rational senior impaired lenders agree to consensual equity carve-outs that deviate from strict APR?

    Options
    Delay destroys estate value through administrative fees and operational loss. Conceding a small carve-out achieves immediate consensual confirmation, yielding a higher net recovery than protracted cramdown litigation.

  4. What procedural right does Bankruptcy Code § 1121 grant to the debtor in possession?

    Options
    Section 1121 establishes debtor exclusivity, preventing creditors from filing competing reorganization plans during the statutory exclusivity window.

  5. What did the U.S. Supreme Court hold in Czyzewski v. Jevic Holding Corp. (2017)?

    Options
    In Jevic, the Supreme Court ruled that structured dismissals cannot skip intermediate priority claimants (like wage claims) to distribute value to general unsecured creditors without unanimous consent.

  6. In the lab simulation (lab-CR4-L03-1), what happened to the equilibrium equity carve-out as litigation delay increased from 3 to 18 months?

    Options
    As delay and burn rate increase, the threat of cramdown value destruction grows larger, expanding the surplus from consensus and increasing the equilibrium carve-out payment to equity.

  7. How do modern senior lenders prevent debtor management from using exclusivity delay to extract carve-outs?

    Options
    Restructuring Support Agreements (RSAs) and restrictive DIP covenants set hard deadlines for plan confirmation, neutralizing the debtor's threat of prolonged exclusivity.

  8. What requirement must be met under the "New Value Exception" for old equity to retain interests in a cramdown reorganization?

    Options
    Under 203 North LaSalle, old equity can only retain equity over senior objection if they contribute fresh, necessary capital subjected to competitive market testing.
