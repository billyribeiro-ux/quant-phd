Chapter 11 Reorganization vs. Out-of-Court Workouts

When corporate debt becomes unsustainable, a firm faces a critical strategic choice: restructure liabilities privately out of court or file for judicial reorganization under Chapter 11. While out-of-court workouts avoid the millions of dollars in professional fees and public operational disruption of bankruptcy, Section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act makes non-consensual debt write-downs impossible outside court. Understanding the game-theoretic holdout problem, exit consent mechanics, and institutional determinants of workout success is essential for restructuring advisory and distressed trading.

1The Restructuring Choice Spectrum

Corporate debt restructurings fall along a continuum defined by the degree of judicial intervention and pre-petition consensus:

Definition 1.1 (Out-of-Court Restructuring)

An out-of-court restructuring (or private workout) is a consensual modification of a debtor's capital structure negotiated directly between the company and its major financial creditors without filing a petition under the Bankruptcy Code. It typically involves debt-for-equity exchanges, debt-for-debt swaps with reduced principal or extended maturities, equity cash injections, or covenant waivers.

The restructuring spectrum encompasses four distinct procedural mechanisms:

  1. Consensual Private Workout: Purely contractual exchange offers and loan amendments executed entirely outside of court. Avoids statutory bankruptcy stigma and court costs, but requires voluntary creditor participation.
  2. Prepackaged Chapter 11 ("Prepack"): The debtor solicits votes on a complete Plan of Reorganization and disclosure statement before filing for bankruptcy. Once the required voting thresholds under § 1126(c) are locked in, the firm files Chapter 11 solely to bind dissenting holdouts, emerging within 30 to 60 days.
  3. Pre-arranged Chapter 11: The debtor negotiates a Restructuring Support Agreement (RSA) with core creditor groups prior to filing, but conducts formal plan voting post-petition under court supervision. Emergence typically requires 3 to 6 months.
  4. Free-Fall Chapter 11: The debtor files an immediate bankruptcy petition without an agreed plan, typically triggered by an acute liquidity crisis, acceleration of debt, or impending foreclosure. Emergence typically requires 12 to 24 months and incurs massive professional fee burn.

2The Trust Indenture Act and the Holdout Problem

The central obstacle to completing an out-of-court debt exchange is the legal protection afforded to public bondholders under federal securities law.

Definition 1.2 (Section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act of 1939)

Section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act (15 U.S.C. § 77ppp(b)) mandates that the contractual right of any holder of an indenture security to receive payment of the principal of and interest on such security, on or after the respective due dates expressed in such security, shall not be impaired or affected without the consent of such holder.

Because Section 316(b) requires 100% unanimous consent to modify core payment terms (face value, coupon rate, or maturity date) outside of bankruptcy, an individual public bondholder has a strong incentive to "free ride" on concessions made by other creditors.

Theorem 1.1 (The Public Bondholder Holdout Dilemma)

Consider an indebted corporation with \(N\) identical public bondholders, each holding a bond with face value \(D\). The firm offers an out-of-court exchange package of new securities with value \(V_E < D\). If all or a substantial fraction of bondholders tender, the firm avoids default and attains solvent enterprise value \(V_S \ge N D\). If the exchange fails, the firm files for Chapter 11, suffering deadweight administrative and business disruption costs \(C_{\text{Ch11}}\), leaving total recovery \(V_{\text{Ch11}} = V_S - C_{\text{Ch11}}\) distributed pro-rata across all claims.

Without restrictive contractual mechanisms, individual bondholder payoffs satisfy:

\[ \Pi(\text{Hold Out} \mid \text{Others Tender}) = D > V_E = \Pi(\text{Tender} \mid \text{Others Tender}) \]

Holding out is a strictly dominant strategy for each atomistic bondholder. Consequently, the unique symmetric Nash equilibrium is for all bondholders to hold out, forcing the firm into Chapter 11 and yielding the Pareto-inferior payoff \(\Pi(\text{All Hold Out}) = \frac{V_S - C_{\text{Ch11}}}{N} < V_E\).

Proof

Under Section 316(b), a non-tendering bondholder retains an unaltered legal claim against the operating firm. If sufficiently many other bondholders tender into the exchange package \(V_E\), the firm's aggregate debt burden is reduced, ensuring solvency. Because the firm is now solvent, it must service the non-tendered old bonds at 100% of par: the holdout captures full contractual value \(D\). A tendering bondholder receives only \(V_E < D\). Therefore, conditional on the workout succeeding, holding out yields \(D > V_E\). If the workout fails because others hold out, filing Chapter 11 occurs regardless of the individual's choice, yielding identical pro-rata liquidation recovery. Holding out weakly dominates tendering in all states and strictly dominates whenever the firm avoids bankruptcy. By iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, all atomistic bondholders hold out. The out-of-court workout collapses, destroying deadweight value \(C_{\text{Ch11}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Exit Consents and Holdout Neutralization

To overcome the Section 316(b) holdout problem, distressed corporations developed the legal technology of the exit consent.

Definition 1.3 (Exit Consent Mechanics)

An exit consent is a transaction structure in which tendering bondholders are required, as a condition of receiving the new exchange package, to execute a consent ballot voting to strip non-core protective covenants (such as negative pledge, cross-default, financial maintenance tests, and reporting requirements) from the old indenture immediately prior to exchanging their bonds.

While Section 316(b) protects core payment terms from majority modification, non-core covenants can typically be amended by a simple majority (50.1%) or super-majority (66.7%) of outstanding bondholders. Tendering bondholders, who are exiting the old security, have zero incentive to protect the old indenture. Once stripped of covenants, the non-tendered old bonds become illiquid, deeply subordinated "stub" paper.

Theorem 1.2 (Restoration of Workout Equilibrium via Exit Consents)

Let an exit consent impose a liquidity and subordination penalty \(\delta \in (0, 1)\) on non-tendered stub bonds, such that a holdout's secondary market recovery is reduced to \(V_{\text{stub}} = (1 - \delta) V_E\). If the covenant-stripping penalty satisfies \(\delta > 0\), then:

\[ \Pi(\text{Tender} \mid \text{Others Tender}) = V_E > (1 - \delta) V_E = \Pi(\text{Hold Out} \mid \text{Others Tender}) \]

Tendering becomes the strictly dominant strategy for all bondholders. The Pareto-efficient workout equilibrium is restored, and the deadweight bankruptcy cost \(C_{\text{Ch11}}\) is eliminated.

Proof

Comparing the payoffs with exit consents: if the required majority tenders and strips covenants, any non-tendering bondholder is left holding an unhedged stub bond without covenant protection, trading at \(V_{\text{stub}} = (1 - \delta) V_E\). Because \(\delta > 0\), \(V_{\text{stub}} < V_E\). An individual bondholder who tenders secures \(V_E\). Hence, conditional on others tendering, tendering yields strictly higher payoff than holding out (\(V_E > (1 - \delta) V_E\)). If others do not tender, the exchange fails and all receive bankruptcy recovery. Thus, tendering weakly dominates holding out in all states and strictly dominates in the success state. All rational bondholders tender, confirming the successful private workout. \(\blacksquare\)

4Empirical Evidence: Gilson, John & Lang (1990)

In their classic empirical investigation of financially distressed corporations, Gilson, John & Lang (1990) examined 169 publicly traded U.S. corporations that experienced severe financial distress between 1978 and 1987.

Key Empirical Findings of Gilson, John & Lang (1990)
  1. Resolution Split: Out of 169 distressed firms, approximately half (89 firms, or 52.7%) successfully restructured their debt out of court, while the remaining 80 firms (47.3%) filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy petitions.
  2. Bank Debt Concentration: Firms that successfully restructured out of court owed a significantly higher proportion of their total debt to commercial banks (mean of 49.0% vs. 37.5% for Chapter 11 filers). Because bank lenders are concentrated, sophisticated, and can form small steering committees, they resolve information asymmetry and coordination frictions far more efficiently than dispersed public bondholders.
  3. Public Debt Dispersal: Firms with more distinct public debt issues and more complex capital structures were significantly more likely to fail in private workouts and file Chapter 11, directly confirming the holdout friction imposed by Section 316(b).
  4. Asset Specificity and Liquidity: Firms with higher fractions of tangible, liquid assets that could be pledged or sold were significantly more likely to succeed in out-of-court workouts without judicial protection.

Lab 1 · Out-of-Court Exchange Offer Game & Exit Consents under TIA 316(b)

Without exit consents, Section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act makes holding out the dominant strategy for public bondholders, causing workout failure and inefficient Chapter 11 bankruptcy; exit consents invert the payoff differential, making tendering dominant and restoring the successful out-of-court workout.

"""Lab CR4-L04-1: Out-of-Court Debt Exchange Offers and Exit Consents under TIA 316(b).

Models the public bondholder coordination game under Section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act.
Demonstrates the free-rider holdout problem that causes workout failure,
and shows how exit consents alter payoffs to restore the successful out-of-court workout equilibrium
as documented by Gilson, John & Lang (1990).
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def evaluate_exchange_game(
    face_value_old: float,
    package_value_new: float,
    v_solvent: float,
    v_ch11: float,
    ch11_deadweight_cost: float,
    n_bondholders: int,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Evaluate payoff matrix for a representative bondholder facing an exchange offer.

    face_value_old: Contractual claim of old bond ($100).
    package_value_new: Value of exchange package (new debt + equity) offered ($65).
    v_solvent: Unlevered firm value if workout succeeds ($120).
    v_ch11: Firm value in Chapter 11 after deadweight costs ($75 = v_solvent - ch11_deadweight_cost).
    ch11_deadweight_cost: Administrative and business disruption costs in court ($45).
    n_bondholders: Total symmetric public bondholders (e.g. 100).
    """
    total_old_debt = face_value_old * n_bondholders

    # Case A: Without Exit Consents
    # If all others tender, firm survives. A holdout retains full old bond claim: min(face_value_old, v_solvent)
    holdout_payoff_if_others_tender = min(face_value_old, 100.0)
    # If bondholder tenders, they receive package_value_new
    tender_payoff_if_others_tender = package_value_new

    # If others hold out, workout fails and firm files Chapter 11
    # Recovery in Chapter 11 is distributed pro-rata across all claims: v_ch11 / total_old_debt
    ch11_recovery_rate = v_ch11 / total_old_debt
    payoff_if_all_holdout = face_value_old * ch11_recovery_rate

    # Case B: With Exit Consents
    # Tendering bondholders simultaneously vote to strip all protective covenants
    # (cross-default, negative pledge, financial covenants) from non-tendered old bonds.
    # Non-tendered bonds become subordinated, illiquid 'stub' paper trading at deep discount.
    stub_discount_penalty = 0.50  # old stripped bond trades at 50% discount
    holdout_payoff_with_exit_consent = package_value_new * (1.0 - stub_discount_penalty)

    return {
        "tender_when_others_tender": tender_payoff_if_others_tender,
        "holdout_when_others_tender_no_consent": holdout_payoff_if_others_tender,
        "ch11_failure_payoff": payoff_if_all_holdout,
        "holdout_with_exit_consent": holdout_payoff_with_exit_consent,
        "ch11_deadweight_cost": ch11_deadweight_cost,
    }


def main() -> None:
    face_old = 100.0
    package_new = 65.0
    v_solvent = 120.0
    v_ch11 = 75.0  # $45M destroyed in Chapter 11
    ch11_cost = 45.0
    n_holders = 100

    res = evaluate_exchange_game(face_old, package_new, v_solvent, v_ch11, ch11_cost, n_holders)

    print("Out-of-Court Exchange Offer Game (TIA 316(b) vs Exit Consents):")
    print("Old Face Value = 100.0, Exchange Offer Package = 65.0, Chapter 11 Deadweight Loss = 45.0")
    print("---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    print("Action Profile                                | Individual Payoff | Game State Outcome")
    print("----------------------------------------------+-------------------+-------------------")
    t_p = f"{res['tender_when_others_tender']:5.1f}"
    h_p = f"{res['holdout_when_others_tender_no_consent']:5.1f}"
    c_p = f"{res['ch11_failure_payoff']:5.1f}"
    e_p = f"{res['holdout_with_exit_consent']:5.1f}"

    print(f"1. No Exit Consent: Tender (others tender)  |       {t_p}       | Voluntary Concession")
    print(f"2. No Exit Consent: Hold Out (others tender)|       {h_p}       | Free-Rider Holdout (Dominant)")
    print(f"3. All Hold Out -> Workout Fails (Ch 11)   |       {c_p}       | Pareto-Inferior Nash Eq")
    print("-" * 75)
    print(f"4. With Exit Consents: Tender (Consensual) |       {t_p}       | Successful Workout")
    print(f"5. With Exit Consents: Hold Out (Stub)     |       {e_p}       | Punished Non-Tender")

    # Invariant checks:
    # 1. Without exit consent, holding out strictly dominates tendering: 100 > 65
    assert res["holdout_when_others_tender_no_consent"] > res["tender_when_others_tender"]
    # 2. Both tendering (65) Pareto-dominates the all-holdout Chapter 11 outcome (48.75)
    assert res["tender_when_others_tender"] > res["ch11_failure_payoff"]
    # 3. Exit consent penalizes holdouts below the tender package, making tendering the dominant strategy
    assert res["tender_when_others_tender"] > res["holdout_with_exit_consent"]

    print("\nVerified coordination game invariants (Gilson et al. 1990):")
    print("1. Without exit consent, individual free-riding forces inefficient Chapter 11.")
    print("2. Exit consent eliminates holdout surplus, restoring Pareto-optimal workout.")

Where the canon breaks

The classical theory of out-of-court debt workouts encounters boundaries in modern debt capital markets:

  • The Marblegate Interpretation of TIA 316(b): In Marblegate Asset Management v. Education Management Corp. (2d Cir. 2017), the Second Circuit held that Section 316(b) prohibits only formal, non-consensual amendments to an indenture's core legal payment terms, not out-of-court restructuring transactions that impair a bondholder's practical ability to receive payment (such as parent guarantees releases). This decision significantly broadened borrower freedom to execute aggressive coercive exchange offers.
  • Cooperation Agreements and Creditor Group Fracturing: Modern distressed ad hoc creditor groups frequently enter into confidential "cooperation agreements" that prohibit members from breaking ranks or negotiating bilateral deals with the debtor, disabling exit consent coercion.
  • Tax Disadvantages of Out-of-Court Workouts: Under Internal Revenue Code § 108, Cancellation of Debt (COD) income is taxable unless the debtor is in formal Title 11 bankruptcy. An out-of-court debt forgiveness can trigger immediate corporate tax liabilities, whereas Chapter 11 debtors can exclude COD income from taxable earnings.

Misconceptions

  • A 75% supermajority of public bondholders can vote to reduce the principal face value of all bonds outside court.

    Under Section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act, core payment terms (principal amount, coupon rate, and maturity date) cannot be impaired without 100% unanimous consent of every individual holder outside of a formal Chapter 11 plan confirmation.

  • Filing Chapter 11 is always inferior to an out-of-court restructuring.

    While Chapter 11 incurs direct administrative expenses, it provides powerful statutory tools unavailable out of court: the automatic stay (§ 362), the ability to reject burdensome executory contracts and leases (§ 365), super-priority DIP financing (§ 364), and the statutory power to bind holdout creditors via class voting (§ 1126(c)).

  • Commercial banks prefer to force distressed borrowers into Chapter 11 bankruptcy immediately.

    Gilson, John & Lang (1990) demonstrated that firms with higher bank debt concentration are significantly more likely to restructure out of court. Banks prefer private workouts because they avoid public bankruptcy disclosure, preserve enterprise going-concern value, and allow lenders to negotiate equity warrants and enhanced governance oversight.

Problem set

Mechanics

Problem M1. A corporation has \(\$200\text{M}\) par value of unsecured 8% bonds outstanding. It offers an exchange package: for each \(\$1,000\) bond, investors receive \(\$600\) face value of new 10% senior secured notes plus 20 shares of common stock valued at \(\$5\) per share. Calculate the total dollar value of the exchange package per \(\$1,000\) bond, the percentage haircut accepted by tendering bondholders, and the aggregate debt reduction if 90% of bonds are tendered.

Solution

1. Value of package per bond: \(\$600\) (new debt) \(+ 20 \times \$5 = \$600 + \$100 = \$700\).

2. Percentage haircut: \(\frac{1,000 - 700}{1,000} = 30.0\%\).

3. Aggregate debt reduction: 90% of \(\$200\text{M} = \$180\text{M}\) face value tendered. New debt issued is \(\$180\text{M} \times 0.60 = \$108\text{M}\). Non-tendered old debt is \(\$20\text{M}\). Total new debt \(= 108 + 20 = \$128\text{M}\). Aggregate contractual debt reduction is \(\$200\text{M} - \$128\text{M} = \$72\text{M}\).

Problem M2. A distressed firm with \(\$100\text{M}\) in bank debt and \(\$100\text{M}\) in public bonds attempts an out-of-court workout. Bank debt is held by 3 commercial banks; public bonds are held by 1,200 retail and institutional investors. Based on the empirical findings of Gilson, John & Lang (1990), explain which creditor class presents the primary obstacle to completing the workout and quantify the voting coordination challenge.

Solution

The 3 commercial banks can easily form a steering committee and negotiate unanimously to amend loan terms. In contrast, modifying the public bonds outside of bankruptcy requires navigating Section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act, which requires 100% individual consent to alter payment terms across 1,200 dispersed bondholders. Any single bondholder can hold out for full par recovery if others make concessions, creating an insurmountable coordination failure without an exit consent structure.

Problem M3. An exchange offer includes an exit consent requiring 51% approval to strip covenants. A distressed hedge fund acquires 42% of the outstanding bond issue and public retail investors hold 58%. If retail investors are passive and only 40% of retail holders tender their ballots, will the exit consent succeed?

Solution

1. Total outstanding bonds = 100%. Hedge fund holds 42% and opposes the exchange.

2. Retail holds 58%. If only 40% of retail holders tender, the affirmative votes equal \(0.40 \times 58\% = 23.2\%\) of total outstanding bonds.

3. Because 23.2% < 51.0%, the exit consent fails to reach the required majority threshold. The old covenants remain intact, and the hedge fund successfully blocks the exchange.

Proof

Problem P1. Prove that in an out-of-court exchange offer without exit consents, as the number of symmetric public bondholders \(N \to \infty\), the probability of voluntary consensual debt reduction approaches zero under any positive individual holdout surplus.

Solution

Let each bondholder have strategy \(\sigma_i \in \{\text{Tender}, \text{Hold Out}\}\). The firm avoids bankruptcy if the aggregate participation rate satisfies \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma_i = \text{Tender}\}} \ge \theta\), where \(\theta \in (0, 1)\) is the critical solvency threshold. Because each atomistic creditor's influence on the aggregate sum is \(1/N \to 0\), the individual's probability of being pivotal is zero in the limit. If the threshold is met, holding out yields \(D > V_E\); if the threshold is not met, filing occurs regardless of individual action. Because holding out strictly dominates tendering in the non-pivotal success state, the dominant strategy equilibrium is \(\sigma_i^* = \text{Hold Out}\) for all \(i\). The probability of voluntary consensual participation is \(\lim_{N \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(\text{Workout Success}) = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P2. Prove that the introduction of an exit consent creates a coordination game with strategic complementarities, and identify the threshold condition on the stub discount penalty \(\delta\) that eliminates the holdout equilibrium.

Solution

Let \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) be the fraction of other bondholders tendering. With an exit consent requiring threshold \(\bar{\alpha}\), the payoff to holding out is \(D\) if \(\alpha \ge \theta\) and \(\alpha < \bar{\alpha}\), but collapses to \((1 - \delta)V_E\) if \(\alpha \ge \bar{\alpha}\). The payoff to tendering is \(V_E\) whenever \(\alpha \ge \theta\). If \(\bar{\alpha} = \theta\) (the covenant-stripping threshold coincides with the solvency threshold), then conditional on the workout succeeding, an individual faces payoff \(V_E\) from tendering versus \((1 - \delta)V_E\) from holding out. If \(\delta > 0\), then \(V_E > (1 - \delta)V_E\). Holding out is strictly dominated once \(\alpha \ge \bar{\alpha}\), transforming the game into a coordination structure where tendering is the unique Pareto-dominant equilibrium. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P3. Prove that under U.S. federal bankruptcy jurisprudence, a prepackaged Plan of Reorganization accepted by the requisite § 1126(c) majorities prior to the petition date satisfies statutory due process and binds 100% of dissenting holdouts upon confirmation under § 1129(a).

Solution

Under Bankruptcy Code § 1126(b), pre-petition acceptance or rejection of a plan is legally valid if the solicitation was in compliance with applicable non-bankruptcy law (such as SEC proxy rules) or if the disclosure provided adequate information under § 1125(a). Once the bankruptcy court confirms the plan under § 1129, Section 1141(a) establishes that the provisions of a confirmed plan bind the debtor and any creditor, whether or not the claim of such creditor is impaired and whether or not such creditor has accepted the plan. This statutory discharge and replacement of claims overrides Section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act, binding 100% of dissenting holdouts. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem E1. In the Gilson, John & Lang (1990) sample, 52.7% of troubled firms restructured successfully out of court, while 47.3% filed Chapter 11. Compute the empirical odds ratio of restructuring success, and explain how the composition of assets (tangible plant vs. specialized intangible assets) influenced this outcome.

Solution

1. Empirical odds of success: \(\frac{89}{80} = 1.1125\) (or an odds ratio of approximately 1.11 to 1).

2. Asset composition: Gilson, John & Lang documented that firms holding higher fractions of intangible, specialized assets suffered larger enterprise value destruction in bankruptcy due to employee departures, lost trade relationships, and court delays. Consequently, firms with specialized assets had greater incentives to avoid Chapter 11 and were more willing to grant equity warrants or bank concessions to complete out-of-court workouts.

Problem E2. An advisor evaluates a troubled borrower with \(\$400\text{M}\) in debt facing impending maturity default. Direct Chapter 11 professional costs are estimated at 4.5% of total debt, and operational customer churn during a 14-month court proceeding is estimated to destroy an additional \(\$30\text{M}\) of enterprise value. Compare the deadweight loss of Chapter 11 against an out-of-court exchange offer that incurs \(\$8\text{M}\) in advisory fees and consent solicitation expenses.

Solution

1. Chapter 11 direct costs: \(4.5\% \times \$400\text{M} = \$18.0\text{M}\).

2. Chapter 11 operational loss: \(\$30.0\text{M}\).

3. Total Chapter 11 deadweight cost: \(18.0 + 30.0 = \$48.0\text{M}\).

4. Out-of-court restructuring cost: \(\$8.0\text{M}\).

5. Net deadweight savings from an out-of-court workout: \(\$48.0\text{M} - \$8.0\text{M} = \$40.0\text{M}\). This \(\$40\text{M}\) represents the net restructuring surplus available to divide among creditors and equity.

Problem E3. A distressed corporation with \(\$300\text{M}\) in public bonds launches an exchange offer with a 90% minimum tender condition. If the firm receives tenders from 86% of bondholders at the final deadline, what strategic dilemma does the board face, and what risks arise from waiving the minimum condition?

Solution

The board faces two choices: (1) Declare the exchange failed and immediately file Chapter 11, incurring substantial court costs; or (2) Waive the 90% condition and close the exchange with 86% participation. The primary risk of waiving the condition is the "holdout burden": the 14% of non-tendering bondholders (\(\$42\text{M}\) face value) retain full contractual claims with original maturities and high coupons. If the restructured company cannot service this remaining unreduced debt, it will suffer a second default ("Chapter 22"), having wasted restructuring transaction costs without achieving true balance sheet sustainability.

Research

Problem R1. Evaluate the judicial reasoning in Marblegate Asset Management v. Education Management Corp. (2017) regarding the scope of Section 316(b). How did the Second Circuit's interpretation alter the bargaining power between distressed borrowers, private equity sponsors, and non-consenting minority noteholders?

Solution

In Marblegate, the Second Circuit examined whether Section 316(b) protects only formal legal amendments to core payment terms or also protects noteholders against practical impairment (such as an out-of-court foreclosure and parent guarantee release that leaves non-consenting noteholders with recourse against an empty shell company). The court held that the legislative history of the 1939 Act demonstrates that Congress intended only to prohibit non-consensual alterations to formal contractual payment terms. This ruling substantially weakened minority holdout power, giving borrowers and majority creditor steering committees legal sanction to structure coercive intercompany asset transfers and foreclosure workouts outside Chapter 11.

Problem R2. Explain the mechanism of a "pre-packaged Chapter 11" and analyze how it resolves both the Section 316(b) holdout problem and the administrative fee burn of free-fall bankruptcy.

Solution

A prepack combines the speed and low cost of an out-of-court workout with the binding cramdown power of Chapter 11. Prior to filing, the debtor prepares a comprehensive disclosure statement and Plan of Reorganization, soliciting votes from creditor classes. Once the statutory threshold of § 1126(c) (two-thirds dollar amount and majority in numerosity) is obtained for all impaired classes, the debtor files its Chapter 11 petition. The bankruptcy court evaluates the pre-petition voting record and enters a confirmation order within 30 to 45 days. By using § 1141(a), the confirmed plan binds 100% of holdouts, eliminating the TIA 316(b) barrier while avoiding months of costly in-court litigation.

Problem R3. Contrast the institutional determinants of corporate debt restructuring under U.S. Chapter 11 versus the UK Part 26A Restructuring Plan (introduced in the Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020). How does the UK "cross-class cramdown" mechanism alter restructuring incentives?

Solution

Under the UK Part 26A Restructuring Plan, the English High Court can confirm a restructuring plan over the objection of a dissenting creditor class (cross-class cramdown) if: (1) none of the members of the dissenting class would be worse off than under the "relevant alternative" (typically immediate liquidation), and (2) the plan has been approved by a 75% majority in value of at least one impaired in-the-money class. Unlike Chapter 11 § 1129(b), the UK mechanism does not strictly enforce the Absolute Priority Rule, giving courts broader discretion to approve plans that allow junior creditors or equity to retain value provided senior creditors achieve a recovery higher than liquidation value.

  1. What does Section 316(b) of the Trust Indenture Act of 1939 prohibit without unanimous consent?

    Options
    Section 316(b) establishes that core contractual payment terms (principal, interest, and maturity) cannot be impaired outside of bankruptcy without the consent of 100% of individual holders.

  2. Why does a public bondholder have an incentive to "hold out" during an out-of-court debt exchange offer?

    Options
    If other bondholders accept haircuts, the firm survives; the holdout free-rides on their concessions and captures full contractual par recovery, creating a dominant strategy to hold out.

  3. How does an "exit consent" overcome the public bondholder holdout problem?

    Options
    Exit consents require tendering bondholders to vote to eliminate protective covenants, leaving holdouts with deeply subordinated stub bonds, making tendering the dominant strategy.

  4. According to the empirical findings of Gilson, John & Lang (1990), which corporate characteristic significantly increases the likelihood of a successful out-of-court restructuring?

    Options
    Commercial banks are concentrated, sophisticated repeat players who can negotiate private modifications far more efficiently than dispersed public bondholders, leading to higher workout success.

  5. What distinguishes a "prepackaged Chapter 11" from a traditional "free-fall" Chapter 11?

    Options
    In a prepack, the debtor locks in required class acceptances under § 1126(c) before filing, using the bankruptcy court solely to confirm the plan and legally bind dissenting holdouts.

  6. What did the Second Circuit rule in Marblegate Asset Management v. Education Management Corp. (2017)?

    Options
    In Marblegate, the Second Circuit limited Section 316(b) strictly to formal, legal modifications of core payment terms, confirming borrower flexibility to execute aggressive exchange offers.

  7. In the lab simulation (lab-CR4-L04-1), why did bondholders achieve only a $0.80 recovery in the game without exit consents?

    Options
    Because holding out was the individually dominant strategy, all bondholders held out, resulting in a Pareto-inferior Nash equilibrium where the workout failed and bankruptcy destruction ensued.

  8. What major tax advantage does formal Chapter 11 offer over an out-of-court debt forgiveness transaction?

    Options
    IRC § 108 provides an absolute exclusion from gross income for Cancellation of Debt (COD) income realized in a Title 11 bankruptcy case, whereas private workouts can trigger immediate tax liability.
