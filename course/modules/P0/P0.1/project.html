Paper Teardown

A quantitative researcher must be able to dissect a published finance paper in a single sitting: extracting the core economic mechanism, identifying the exact source of identifying variation, auditing the sample filters and data locators, pinpointing the single weakest assumption, and assigning a rigorous evidence grade backed by counter-evidence. You will deconstruct Banz (1981) using our structured one-page teardown protocol, evaluated against a six-criterion rubric with annotated model exemplars.

The ladder

The four-rung project ladder structures your progression from guided deconstruction to independent research evaluation. If you passed the placement diagnostic on the module hub page (scoring at least 80 percent and answering both skill checks correctly), you may skip directly to rung 3.

  1. Rung 1 · Worked example

    A complete, professionally annotated teardown of Ball & Brown (1968) detailing each of the six evaluation dimensions with explicit analytical rationales.

  2. Rung 2 · Completion problem

    A completion template with identification strategy, sample locators, and weakest-assumption fields blanked. Graded instantly with diagnostic feedback.

  3. Rung 3 · Independent project

    An independent one-page structured teardown of Banz (1981) evaluated by rubric against six criteria with four performance levels.

  4. Rung 4 · Stretch

    An open-ended evidence audit of a modern contested asset-pricing or econometrics paper from your target research domain, defending an evidence grade against published counter-evidence.

Specification

Context
Institutional investors and quantitative trading groups constantly evaluate published academic papers claiming novel return anomalies or risk factors. Academic presentation often obscures endogeneity, sample selection biases, and transaction cost realities. Quantitative analysts must produce concise, hostile-expert-proof teardowns that establish whether an empirical finding is a genuine economic phenomenon or a fragile statistical artifact.
Inputs
The primary input is the published journal article by Rolf W. Banz (1981), "The relationship between return and market value of common stocks", published in the Journal of Financial Economics, volume 9, issue 1, pages 3–18, available under citation registry key banz1981relationship. Supporting counter-evidence is provided by Shumway (1997) regarding CRSP delisting return bias.
Required outputs
A single-page structured markdown or text teardown document (teardown.md) adhering strictly to six standardized sections: (1) Research Question & Mechanism, (2) Identification Strategy & Exogenous Variation, (3) Data, Sample Universe & Exact Locators, (4) Main Empirical Results & Effect Magnitudes, (5) Weakest Assumption & Robustness Boundary, and (6) Evidence Grade & Counter-Evidence Defense.
Constraints
The teardown must not exceed 600 words of prose. Every quoted empirical number, coefficient, or sample count must carry an exact page, table, or panel locator from Banz (1981). The evidence grade must be chosen from {T, A, B, C, D, X} per program standards. For Grade B or C, published counter-evidence must be cited using verified registry keys.
Grading
This project is evaluated via a formal rubric comprising six criteria across four observable performance levels. Passing requires achieving Level 3 (Proficient) or Level 4 (Exemplary) on every criterion, calibrated against the two provided exemplars.

Rung 1 · Worked example: Ball & Brown (1968)

A complete, professional-grade teardown of Ball and Brown (1968). Study the analytical notes explaining the rationale for each section.

Teardown: Ball & Brown (1968), Journal of Accounting Research

Citation: Ball, R., & Brown, P. (1968). An Empirical Evaluation of Accounting Income Numbers. Journal of Accounting Research, 6(2), 159–178. Key: ball1968empirical.

1. Research Question & Mechanism

Does the annual net income figure reported by corporations convey valuable information to equity markets regarding firm economic value, or is accounting net income a meaningless construct superseded by other market signals? The economic mechanism is that unexpected accounting earnings signal revisions to expected future corporate cash flows, inducing rational investors to reprice shares around the announcement.

Why: The question is stated as a clear, falsifiable proposition addressing the economic role of accounting numbers, separating cash flow signaling from price formation.

2. Identification Strategy

Identification rests on an event study leveraging the precise calendar timing of annual earnings report announcements. By computing the sign of unexpected earnings using an empirical forecasting model (regression of firm income changes on market-wide income changes), the authors separate firms into positive and negative unexpected earnings portfolios. Aligning stock returns in event time (months -11 to +6 relative to report month 0) isolates return drift associated with the specific fiscal announcement.

Why: Causal identification requires isolating the temporal arrival of news. Event-time alignment relative to announcement month 0 ensures that general macroeconomic shocks occurring in calendar time do not bias the unexpected earnings partition.

3. Data, Sample & Exact Locators

Sample consists of 261 firms listed on the New York Stock Exchange across the 1946–1965 period, yielding 2,340 annual earnings announcements (p. 165). Return data are obtained from the CRSP monthly file. Announcement dates are gathered from The Wall Street Journal. Financial and utility firms are excluded due to regulatory reporting differences.

Why: Precise sample constraints (261 NYSE firms, 1946–1965) prevent over-generalizing findings to OTC or international markets.

4. Main Empirical Results

Approximately 85 percent to 90 percent of the information contained in the annual earnings announcement is anticipated by the market prior to the announcement month (Figure 1, p. 169). Positive unexpected earnings firms experience cumulative abnormal returns of approximately +7 percent over the 12 months preceding the announcement, while negative firms experience -9 percent (Table 4, p. 172). Post-announcement drift persists for several months following month 0.

Why: Quantitative figures are accompanied by exact locators (Figure 1, p. 169; Table 4, p. 172). Results report both economic magnitudes (percent abnormal return) and temporal structure.

5. Weakest Assumption

The weakest assumption is that the announcement month recorded in The Wall Street Journal represents the earliest date on which earnings news leaked into the public domain, and that the single-factor market model accurately purges expected returns without omitted risk factor contamination.

Why: If earnings leaked earlier through private broker channels or dividend announcements, the pre-announcement drift reflects informal disclosures rather than market anticipation of the audited report.

6. Evidence Grade & Justification

Grade A. The finding that earnings announcements carry substantial information that is largely anticipated prior to formal disclosure, followed by post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD), has been replicated across dozens of international equity markets, modern sample periods (1970–2025), and alternative econometric specifications by hundreds of independent academic teams.

Why: Meets all criteria for Grade A: robust multi-decade independent replication out of sample across global equity universes.

Rung 2 · Completion problem

Complete the partial teardown below by selecting the correct identification and assumption terms. Check each answer to verify your diagnostic mastery before beginning the independent Banz (1981) project.

  1. In a teardown of Banz (1981), what is the primary econometric identification strategy employed to isolate the relationship between firm size and equity returns?

    Options

    Banz (1981) estimated a generalized CAPM cross-sectional regression adding a non-linear market equity term (\(\phi_i\)) to test if size explained average returns incremental to beta.

  2. What is the critical data construction flaw in Banz (1981) identified by subsequent financial econometricians as its weakest assumption?

    Options

    As Shumway (1997) proved, CRSP historically lacked returns for distressed delisted firms. Because microcaps delist at high rates, omitting delisting returns creates an artificial small-firm premium.

  3. Under the program's evidence standard, what evidence grade should Banz (1981) receive, and why?

    Options

    Banz (1981) is a classic peer-reviewed paper that faces active counter-evidence (Shumway 1997; post-1980 disappearance), classifying it as Grade B.

Rung 3 · Rubric

The teardown of Banz (1981) is evaluated against six criteria with four observable performance levels each. Level 3 (Proficient) or Level 4 (Exemplary) on all criteria is required to pass.

Paper teardown rubric: six criteria, four observable levels each.
Criterion 4 · Exemplary 3 · Proficient 2 · Developing 1 · Beginning
C1 · Question & Mechanism States the research question and economic mechanism in one sharp sentence each, checkable against the introduction. Explicitly identifies the economic transmission channel linking variables. States the research question and mechanism in two or three sentences, but mechanism is described informally without specifying the exact economic channel. States the general topic rather than a falsifiable research question, or conflates the question with the empirical finding. Vague or absent research question; no economic mechanism identified.
C2 · Identification Strategy Names the precise variation that isolates the estimate (e.g. cross-sectional size sorting relative to beta), explains why it is credible, and explicitly specifies the exclusion restriction. Identifies the empirical technique and independent variables, but does not isolate the exact identifying variation or address potential confounders. Confuses statistical estimation technique (such as OLS) with identification, or asserts that controls eliminate all bias. Fails to address identification; treats observed empirical correlation as direct causal evidence.
C3 · Data, Sample & Locators Specifies exact sample universe, date range, frequency, and filtering criteria. Every quoted figure or estimate carries an exact table, panel, or page locator checkable against the text. Names data sources and sample period, but omits specific filtering criteria or omits locators for some quoted figures. Names data sources vaguely with incorrect sample boundaries; quotes figures without specific page or table locators. Data sources unstated or generic; numbers quoted without sources.
C4 · Statistical Inference & Results Accurately reports point estimates, standard errors / t-statistics, and economic effect sizes with exact locators. Interprets results strictly within the bounded sample without over-generalizing. Reports main point estimates and significance correctly, but frames conclusions too broadly or neglects economic effect size in basis points or dollars. Reports statistical significance without reporting economic magnitudes, or misinterprets standard error adjustments. Misstates main empirical findings or misreports quantitative results.
C5 · Weakest Assumption Identifies the single most fragile assumption in identification or data construction, details the failure mechanism, and notes whether subsequent literature addressed it. Identifies a valid limitation of the study, but focuses on minor sample restrictions rather than the core identifying assumption. Mentions generic caveats without analyzing the economic or econometric assumptions of the model. Asserts the paper has no weaknesses, or repeats the authors' conclusions uncritically.
C6 · Evidence Grade & Defense Assigns an evidence grade strictly adhering to program standards, justifies it from replication provenance, and for Grade B or C cites published counter-evidence with verified registry keys. Assigns an evidence grade consistent with program definitions and justifies it, but cites counter-evidence in passing without analyzing the methodological critique. Assigns an incorrect evidence grade or fails to provide balanced counter-evidence for a contested claim. Grade unassigned, or justified merely by personal opinion rather than program evidentiary standards.

Annotated exemplars

Examine the two model teardowns below evaluating Banz (1981). Exemplar A demonstrates Level 4 performance across all criteria. Exemplar B illustrates common Level 2 deficiencies.

Exemplar A · Exemplary Teardown (Level 4)

Official scoring: C1 4 · C2 4 · C3 4 · C4 4 · C5 4 · C6 4. Flawless execution with exact locators, rigorous identification analysis, identification of delisting bias as the weakest assumption, and Grade B rating citing Shumway (1997).

1. Research Question & Mechanism

Does firm size, measured by the market value of common equity, have incremental explanatory power for expected stock returns beyond systematic risk measured by the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) beta? The economic mechanism is that if the standard Sharpe-Lintner-Black CAPM is correctly specified, expected returns should depend linearly on market beta alone; any persistent residual return premium correlated with market capitalization indicates either model misspecification (omitted risk factors correlated with size) or market segmentation.

2. Identification Strategy

Identification rests on cross-sectional generalized asset pricing regressions of the form \(R_i - R_f = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \beta_i + \gamma_2 \left(\frac{\phi_i - \phi_m}{\phi_m}\right) + \epsilon_i\), where \(\phi_i\) is the market value of firm \(i\), \(\phi_m\) is average market value, and \(\gamma_2\) captures the incremental size effect. To overcome errors-in-variables in individual firm beta estimates, Banz employs a two-stage portfolio grouping procedure: sorting stocks into 25 portfolios based on historical beta and market equity, estimating portfolio betas, and running cross-sectional regressions across rolling subperiods.

3. Data, Sample & Exact Locators

The sample comprises common stocks listed on the New York Stock Exchange for at least five continuous years between 1926 and 1975, retrieved from the CRSP monthly master file (p. 8). Primary empirical tests focus on the 40-year window 1936–1975 to ensure robust pre-ranking estimation periods. Non-NYSE equities and OTC securities are excluded.

4. Main Empirical Results

Banz documents a statistically significant negative relationship between market value and average return incremental to beta (Table 1, p. 9). In the 1936–1975 overall period, the average size coefficient \(\hat{\gamma}_2\) is \(-0.00052\) per month with a t-statistic of \(-2.92\). The return difference between the smallest quintile and largest quintile of NYSE stocks averages approximately 0.40 percent per month, or 4.8 percent annualized, holding beta constant. However, Banz notes that the effect is non-linear: the size premium is concentrated entirely in the smallest 20 percent of firms, with little difference between medium and large firms (Table 3, p. 12).

5. Weakest Assumption

The critical identifying assumption is that CRSP monthly stock returns accurately reflect the true realized holding-period returns of microcap equities without systematic survivorship or delisting biases. This assumption fails: as Shumway (1997) demonstrated, CRSP routinely omitted final delisting returns for bankrupt or distressed firms during the 1926–1975 period. Because microcap stocks delisted for performance failure at substantially higher rates than large firms, omitting these large negative returns (-30 percent average on NYSE) generates an artificial upward bias in small-stock returns.

6. Evidence Grade & Justification

Grade B. Banz (1981) is a seminal peer-reviewed contribution that established the size anomaly. However, the finding is graded B because it has failed robust out-of-sample replication: following its publication, the US small-firm premium disappeared from 1980 to 2020. Furthermore, correcting for delisting survivorship bias (Shumway, 1997) and subtracting realistic bid-ask execution costs eliminates the economically exploitable net alpha.

Exemplar B · Developing Teardown (Level 2)

Official scoring: C1 2 · C2 2 · C3 2 · C4 2 · C5 2 · C6 2. Vague research question, confuses regression mechanics with identification, missing page locators, generic caveat, and uncritically assigns Grade A based on prestige alone.

1. Research Question & Mechanism

The paper studies whether small stocks have higher returns than big stocks on the stock exchange. The mechanism is that investors like big companies more so small companies have to pay higher returns.

2. Identification Strategy

The author identifies the effect by running ordinary least squares regressions in Python or Fortran on stock returns, controlling for beta so that market risk is fully eliminated.

3. Data, Sample & Exact Locators

The paper uses US stock market data from CRSP over a long historical time period from the 1920s to the 1970s.

4. Main Empirical Results

Small stocks outperform large stocks by around 5 percent per year and the t-statistic is very significant at around 3, proving that the CAPM is wrong.

5. Weakest Assumption

The weakest assumption is that Banz only looked at American stocks and did not test European stocks, so the sample could have been larger.

6. Evidence Grade & Justification

Grade A. This paper is published in the Journal of Financial Economics, one of the top three finance journals in the world, and has thousands of citations, so it is a proven fact.
