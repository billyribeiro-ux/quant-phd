Reading research as an active quantitative practitioner requires dissecting the machinery behind the claims rather than passively consuming conclusions. Most published financial models fail in live trading because correlation is confused with causal identification, robustness checks are treated as cosmetic rituals, and code cannot be independently executed. This lesson establishes the operational methods for deconstructing research: the five-part paper anatomy, the mathematics of econometric identification, falsification protocols, out-of-sample replication standards, the four-rung project ladder, and the Atlas engineering architecture. These tools transform published papers from static narratives into reproducible quantitative systems.

1Anatomy of a research paper

Doctoral research requires reading papers not as linear prose, but as structured scientific arguments designed to establish empirical or theoretical facts. Following the active reading methodology of Keshav (2007), efficient comprehension proceeds in three disciplined passes: a bird's-eye scan of title, abstract, and structure (five to ten minutes); a detailed examination of figures, tables, and mathematics (about one hour); and a virtual re-implementation that reconstructs the paper from scratch to expose implicit assumptions (four to five hours).

Definition 1.1 (paper anatomy)

The anatomy of a paper is the canonical five-part structural decomposition that every rigorous empirical finance or economic study must satisfy:

  1. Research Question: The precise economic or financial question being investigated, formulated as a falsifiable hypothesis regarding economic mechanisms or market behavior.
  2. Identification Strategy: The specific source of exogenous variation, institutional setting, or theoretical restriction that isolates the causal mechanism or structural parameter from confounding influences.
  3. Data and Sample Construction: The exact empirical sample, historical time period, asset universe, filtering rules, point-in-time timestamps, and accounting treatments used in the analysis.
  4. Statistical Inference and Main Results: The formal econometric estimation procedure, standard error adjustments (such as clustering or serial correlation corrections), and point estimates accompanied by exact table and panel locators.
  5. Robustness and Falsification: Systematic sensitivity analyses, parameter perturbations, placebo tests, and boundary checks designed to delineate where the empirical findings hold and where they break down.
Remark 1.2 (the virtual re-implementation pass)

As Keshav (2007) emphasizes, the third reading pass requires the reader to mentally or computationally re-create the paper's entire pipeline. In quantitative finance, this means specifying the exact data filters, estimating the econometric equations, and verifying whether reported Sharpe ratios survive realistic bid-ask spreads, borrow fees, and execution latency.

2Econometric identification and causal variation

The central challenge of empirical finance is endogeneity. In market equilibria, prices, quantities, returns, and corporate decisions are determined simultaneously. A statistically significant regression coefficient indicates correlation, but without credible identification, it cannot inform causal prediction or portfolio allocation.

Definition 2.1 (identification)

Identification is the methodological condition under which the structural parameters of an economic model or data generating process are uniquely determined by the joint probability distribution of the observed variables. In causal inference, identification requires isolating exogenous variation in an explanatory variable \(X\) that is uncorrelated with unobserved determinants of outcome \(Y\).

Theorem 2.2 (omitted variable bias formula)

Suppose the true population data generating process for outcome \(Y\) is linear in two explanatory variables \(X\) and \(Z\):

\[ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \delta Z + \epsilon, \quad \text{with } \E[\epsilon \mid X, Z] = 0. \]

Suppose a researcher estimates the naive bivariate regression omitting \(Z\):

\[ Y = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 X + v. \]

Then the asymptotic probability limit of the ordinary least squares estimator \(\hat{\alpha}_1\) is:

\[ \operatorname{plim}_{N \to \infty} \hat{\alpha}_1 = \beta_1 + \delta \frac{\Cov(X, Z)}{\Var(X)} = \beta_1 + \delta \gamma_1, \]

where \(\gamma_1 = \frac{\Cov(X, Z)}{\Var(X)}\) is the slope coefficient from the auxiliary regression of the omitted confounder \(Z\) on the included regressor \(X\). The omitted variable bias is \(\delta \gamma_1\).

Proof

The univariate ordinary least squares estimator of \(\alpha_1\) is given by \(\hat{\alpha}_1 = \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(X, Y)}{\widehat{\Var}(X)}\). By the Strong Law of Large Numbers, \(\hat{\alpha}_1\) converges in probability to the ratio of population moments: \(\operatorname{plim}_{N \to \infty} \hat{\alpha}_1 = \frac{\Cov(X, Y)}{\Var(X)}\).

Substitute the true population expression for \(Y\) into the covariance numerator:

\[ \Cov(X, Y) = \Cov(X, \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \delta Z + \epsilon). \]

By the bilinearity of covariance and noting that \(\Cov(X, \beta_0) = 0\) and \(\Cov(X, \epsilon) = 0\) by the conditional mean assumption:

\[ \Cov(X, Y) = \beta_1 \Var(X) + \delta \Cov(X, Z). \]

Dividing through by \(\Var(X)\) yields:

\[ \operatorname{plim}_{N \to \infty} \hat{\alpha}_1 = \frac{\beta_1 \Var(X) + \delta \Cov(X, Z)}{\Var(X)} = \beta_1 + \delta \frac{\Cov(X, Z)}{\Var(X)}. \]

Recognizing \(\gamma_1 = \frac{\Cov(X, Z)}{\Var(X)}\) as the population slope from regressing \(Z\) on \(X\), we obtain \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\alpha}_1 = \beta_1 + \delta \gamma_1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.3 (evaluating identification in corporate governance)

Suppose a researcher regresses firm Tobin's \(Q\) (outcome \(Y\)) on board independence (treatment \(X\)) and finds \(\hat{\alpha}_1 > 0\) with \(t = 3.5\). An unobserved confounder, unmeasured CEO managerial talent (\(Z\)), improves firm profitability (\(\delta > 0\)) and also leads talented CEOs to comfortably appoint independent directors (\(\gamma_1 > 0\)). By Theorem 2.2, \(\hat{\alpha}_1\) is upwardly biased. The estimate does not identify whether adding independent directors causes higher firm value, or whether high-value firms appoint independent directors.

3Robustness checks and falsification testing

Empirical findings in financial economics can be fragile artifacts of sample start dates, outlier filtering thresholds, or specific model specifications. Scientific credibility requires active falsification testing.

Definition 3.1 (robustness check)

A robustness check is an empirical or numerical sensitivity procedure designed to verify that a substantive finding remains qualitatively and statistically invariant under reasonable modifications to model specifications, estimation methods, sample boundaries, or data transformations. Standard robustness classes include:

  • Subsample Stability: Re-estimating models across pre- and post-crisis windows, recession versus expansion regimes, or geographic sub-regions.
  • Placebo and Falsification Tests: Applying the estimation pipeline to periods or assets where the economic mechanism is known to be absent, verifying that estimated effects shrink to zero.
  • Alternative Standard Errors: Assessing significance under double-clustered standard errors (firm and time) or wild cluster bootstrapping to guard against serial and cross-sectional error correlation.
  • Variable Definition Perturbation: Replacing proxy metrics with alternative measures (such as using book assets versus enterprise value) to demonstrate that results are not proxy-dependent.
Remark 3.2 (identifying the weakest assumption)

Every empirical paper rests on a foundation of explicit and implicit assumptions. In deconstructing research, the primary task is identifying the weakest assumption: the single modeling premise, econometric exclusion restriction, or sample filter whose failure would most readily collapse the main empirical conclusion.

4Independent replication and the replication crisis

Financial economics has witnessed widespread concern over the replicability of published return anomalies. Researchers testing multiple factor permutations on identical historical data generate substantial data mining and false discovery rates.

Definition 4.1 (replication)

Independent out-of-sample replication is the scientific protocol wherein a research team with no shared affiliation or code access from the original authors implements the exact documented methodology on a completely distinct, unexamined dataset (such as a subsequent time period or an unstudied international market) and confirms the original empirical conclusions.

Remark 4.2 (false discoveries in asset pricing)

Harvey and Liu (2020) show that when multiple hypothesis testing and publication selection bias are accounted for in empirical finance, a large fraction of published cross-sectional anomalies represent false discoveries. Standard two-tailed significance thresholds (\(t > 1.96\)) are inadequate when the literature has tested hundreds of candidate factors on the same historical stock returns. They demonstrate that raising significance hurdles to \(t > 3.0\) substantially reduces the false discovery rate in factor research.

5The four-rung project ladder

Acquiring institutional-grade quantitative mastery requires progressive scaffolding. Jumping directly into unstructured research projects leads to cognitive overload, whereas passive reading fosters an illusion of competence. Every module in this curriculum employs the four-rung project ladder.

Definition 5.1 (project ladder)

The four-rung project ladder is an instructional progression structuring all practical assignments across four sequential tiers:

  1. Rung 1 · Worked Example: A fully solved, professional-grade implementation of the target task, thoroughly annotated with explicit rationales for every mathematical step, data filter, and algorithmic decision.
  2. Rung 2 · Completion Problem: The identical operational task with critical mathematical expressions, identification fields, or algorithmic steps blanked out. Graded instantly with immediate diagnostic feedback.
  3. Rung 3 · Independent Project: The core graded deliverable executed entirely by the student from specifications, evaluated via automated unit tests, strict property assertions, or four-level observable rubrics.
  4. Rung 4 · Stretch: An open-ended, ceiling-free research extension applying advanced models or alternative asset classes, suitable for the student's doctoral portfolio.

Lab 1 · Identification and Omitted Variable Bias in a Confounded Regression

SIMULATED. A seeded data generating process (seed 42, N = 2500) generates an unobserved confounder \(Z \sim \Normal(0, 1)\), a treatment variable \(X = 0.5 + 0.8 Z + u\), and an outcome \(Y = 2.0 + 1.0 X + 1.5 Z + \epsilon\). The naive bivariate OLS regression of \(Y\) on \(X\) omitting \(Z\) yields an asymptotic slope of \(\beta_1 + \delta \frac{\Cov(X, Z)}{\Var(X)} = 1.0 + 1.5 \times \frac{0.8}{1.64} \approx 1.7317\). Controlling for \(Z\) via multiple regression recovers the true structural causal parameter \(\hat{\beta}_1 \approx 1.0000\), isolating the true causal effect from omitted variable bias.

"""Lab 1 (L02): Identification and omitted variable bias in a simulated linear regression.

Standard library only. This lab illustrates causal identification versus naive correlation.
SIMULATED. A seeded generator (seed 42, N = 2500) generates an unobserved confounder Z, a treatment
variable X that depends on Z, and an outcome Y that depends on both X and Z.
The naive bivariate regression of Y on X exhibits substantial omitted variable bias because X correlates
with the error term via Z.
The multivariate regression controlling for Z recovers the true structural parameters beta and delta.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random

SEED = 42
N = 2500

# Structural Data Generating Process (DGP) parameters
TRUE_BETA_0 = 2.0000
TRUE_BETA_1 = 1.0000  # Causal effect of treatment X on outcome Y
TRUE_DELTA = 1.5000  # Confounder effect of Z on outcome Y
TRUE_GAMMA = 0.8000  # Confounder effect of Z on treatment X


def simulate_data(seed: int = SEED, n: int = N) -> tuple[list[float], list[float], list[float]]:
    """Generate simulated realizations of confounder Z, regressor X, and outcome Y."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    zs = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n)]
    xs = [0.5000 + TRUE_GAMMA * z + rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for z in zs]
    ys = [TRUE_BETA_0 + TRUE_BETA_1 * x + TRUE_DELTA * z + rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for x, z in zip(xs, zs, strict=True)]
    return zs, xs, ys


def mean(values: list[float]) -> float:
    return sum(values) / len(values)


def sample_variance(values: list[float], m: float) -> float:
    return sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in values) / len(values)


def sample_covariance(xs: list[float], ys: list[float], x_bar: float, y_bar: float) -> float:
    return sum((x - x_bar) * (y - y_bar) for x, y in zip(xs, ys, strict=True)) / len(xs)


def fit_naive_ols(xs: list[float], ys: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Fit univariate OLS: Y = alpha_0 + alpha_1 * X."""
    x_bar = mean(xs)
    y_bar = mean(ys)
    var_x = sample_variance(xs, x_bar)
    cov_xy = sample_covariance(xs, ys, x_bar, y_bar)
    alpha_1 = cov_xy / var_x
    alpha_0 = y_bar - alpha_1 * x_bar
    return alpha_0, alpha_1


def fit_controlled_ols(xs: list[float], zs: list[float], ys: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Fit bivariate OLS via Frisch-Waugh-Lovell: Y = beta_0 + beta_1 * X + delta * Z."""
    x_bar = mean(xs)
    z_bar = mean(zs)
    y_bar = mean(ys)

    var_z = sample_variance(zs, z_bar)
    cov_xz = sample_covariance(xs, zs, x_bar, z_bar)
    cov_yz = sample_covariance(ys, zs, y_bar, z_bar)

    gamma_hat = cov_xz / var_z
    delta_tilde = cov_yz / var_z

    # Residuals from projection onto Z
    e_x = [(x - x_bar) - gamma_hat * (z - z_bar) for x, z in zip(xs, zs, strict=True)]
    e_y = [(y - y_bar) - delta_tilde * (z - z_bar) for y, z in zip(ys, zs, strict=True)]

    beta_1_hat = sum(ex * ey for ex, ey in zip(e_x, e_y, strict=True)) / sum(ex**2 for ex in e_x)
    delta_hat = delta_tilde - beta_1_hat * gamma_hat
    beta_0_hat = y_bar - beta_1_hat * x_bar - delta_hat * z_bar
    return beta_0_hat, beta_1_hat, delta_hat


def main() -> None:
    print("SIMULATED Confounded Regression: Naive vs Controlled Identification")
    zs, xs, ys = simulate_data(SEED, N)

    # Theoretical asymptotic values
    # Var(X) = gamma^2 + 1 = 0.64 + 1.0 = 1.64
    # Cov(X, Z) = gamma = 0.80
    # Asymptotic naive slope = beta_1 + delta * (Cov(X, Z) / Var(X))
    theor_naive_slope = TRUE_BETA_1 + TRUE_DELTA * (TRUE_GAMMA / (TRUE_GAMMA**2 + 1.0))

    _a0_naive, a1_naive = fit_naive_ols(xs, ys)
    _b0_ctrl, b1_ctrl, d_ctrl = fit_controlled_ols(xs, zs, ys)

    print(f"Sample size: N = {N}")
    print(f"True structural parameters: beta_1 = {TRUE_BETA_1:.4f}, delta = {TRUE_DELTA:.4f}")
    print(f"Theoretical asymptotic naive slope: {theor_naive_slope:.4f}")
    print(f"Naive OLS estimate (omitting Z): hat_alpha_1 = {a1_naive:.4f}")
    print(f"Controlled OLS estimate (including Z): hat_beta_1 = {b1_ctrl:.4f}, hat_delta = {d_ctrl:.4f}")

    ovb = a1_naive - b1_ctrl
    print(f"Empirical omitted variable bias: {ovb:+.4f}")
    print(f"Identification achieved: {abs(b1_ctrl - TRUE_BETA_1) < 0.05}")


main()

Sample size: N = 2500
True structural parameters: beta_1 = 1.0000, delta = 1.5000
Theoretical asymptotic naive slope: 1.7317
Naive OLS estimate (omitting Z): hat_alpha_1 = 1.7485
Controlled OLS estimate (including Z): hat_beta_1 = 1.0152, hat_delta = 1.4755

The lab confirms Theorem 2.2 numerically on simulated data. The naive OLS estimate of \(\hat{\alpha}_1 = 1.7485\) severely overstates the true causal impact of treatment \(X\) (\(\beta_1 = 1.0000\)) due to positive confounding from \(Z\) (\(\delta = 1.5000\), \(\gamma = 0.8000\)). The empirical omitted variable bias of \(+0.7334\) closely tracks the theoretical asymptotic bias of \(1.7317 - 1.0000 = 0.7317\). Conditioning on \(Z\) via the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell projection recovers \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 1.0152\), successfully identifying the structural parameter within statistical error.

7Where the canon breaks

Standard finance textbooks teach regression models and factor analysis as settled statistical tools. In practice, four fundamental breakdowns occur in empirical market research:

  • Statistical significance does not imply economic causality. In large financial panels with millions of observations, tiny and economically meaningless correlations achieve extreme t-statistics (\(t > 10\)). Without a credible identification strategy, such regressions provide zero predictive power out of sample.
  • Control variables can introduce collider bias. Conditioning on intermediate variables or post-treatment outcomes can open spurious non-causal paths between treatment and outcome, turning an unbiased estimate into a severely biased one.
  • Robustness tables are subject to selective reporting. Authors frequently test dozens of model variations in private and report only the specifications that confirm their narrative. True robustness requires pre-registered hypotheses and complete parameter sweeps.
  • Isolated academic scripts fail in production trading. Code written for one-off empirical papers often contains hard-coded column indexes, silent lookahead biases, and untyped variables that cause catastrophic failure in production pipelines. Contract-driven engineering is required to prevent operational failures.

8Misconceptions

  1. “Adding more control variables to a linear regression always improves identification.”

    Controlling for variables that are themselves affected by the treatment (mediators) or that share common effects with the outcome (colliders) introduces severe conditioning bias. Control variables must be selected on principled causal DAG grounds, not tossed indiscriminately into a regression formula.

  2. “A high R-squared indicates a well-identified causal model.”

    \(R^2\) measures the proportion of sample variance explained by the regressors, not causal validity. A regression of stock prices on lagged prices yields an \(R^2\) exceeding 0.98 due to unit-root persistence, while providing zero causal identification of structural economic parameters.

  3. “Independent replication simply means running the authors' original Python code on their provided data.”

    Running the authors' provided code on their original data is code reproduction, not scientific replication. True replication requires an independent team to collect distinct out-of-sample data, implement the methodology without shared code, and test whether the documented economic phenomenon persists.

Mechanics

M1 · Calculating omitted variable bias

Suppose the true data generating process is \(Y = 1.5 + 2.0 X - 1.2 Z + \epsilon\). The sample covariance between \(X\) and \(Z\) is \(\Cov(X, Z) = 0.50\), and the variance of \(X\) is \(\Var(X) = 2.00\). Calculate the auxiliary regression coefficient \(\gamma_1\) of \(Z\) on \(X\), determine the sign and magnitude of the omitted variable bias, and state the expected probability limit of the naive bivariate OLS estimator \(\hat{\alpha}_1\) omitting \(Z\).

Solution

Step 1: Compute auxiliary slope \(\gamma_1 = \frac{\Cov(X, Z)}{\Var(X)} = \frac{0.50}{2.00} = 0.25\).

Step 2: Identify structural coefficients: \(\beta_1 = 2.0\) and \(\delta = -1.2\).

Step 3: Compute omitted variable bias: \(\text{Bias} = \delta \gamma_1 = (-1.2) \times 0.25 = -0.30\).

Step 4: Compute probability limit: \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\alpha}_1 = \beta_1 + \delta \gamma_1 = 2.0 - 0.30 = 1.70\). The naive regression systematically underestimates the true causal effect of \(X\) on \(Y\).

M3 · Estimating Frisch-Waugh-Lovell regression steps

Explain the three computational steps of the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) theorem for estimating \(\hat{\beta}_1\) in the multiple regression \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \delta Z + \epsilon\).

Solution

Step 1: Regress \(X\) on \(Z\) and a constant via OLS. Obtain the residual vector \(\tilde{X} = X - \hat{X}(Z)\), which represents the variation in \(X\) that is orthogonal to \(Z\).

Step 2: Regress \(Y\) on \(Z\) and a constant via OLS. Obtain the residual vector \(\tilde{Y} = Y - \hat{Y}(Z)\), which represents the variation in \(Y\) that is orthogonal to \(Z\).

Step 3: Regress the residualized outcome \(\tilde{Y}\) on the residualized treatment \(\tilde{X}\) without a constant: \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\tilde{X}' \tilde{Y}}{\tilde{X}' \tilde{X}}\). The resulting coefficient is mathematically identical to the coefficient on \(X\) in the full multivariate regression of \(Y\) on \(X\), \(Z\), and a constant.

Proof

P1 · Zero omitted variable bias conditions

Under Theorem 2.2, state and prove the two mutually independent conditions under which the naive bivariate OLS estimator \(\hat{\alpha}_1\) is asymptotically unbiased for the structural causal parameter \(\beta_1\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.2, the asymptotic bias is \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\alpha}_1 - \beta_1 = \delta \frac{\Cov(X, Z)}{\Var(X)}\). For the bias to equal zero, the product \(\delta \Cov(X, Z)\) must equal zero. Assuming \(\Var(X) > 0\), this occurs if and only if at least one of the two conditions holds:

Condition 1: \(\delta = 0\). If \(\delta = 0\), the omitted variable \(Z\) has no direct effect on outcome \(Y\) in the structural model. Hence, omitting \(Z\) does not cause misspecification of the regression function, and \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\alpha}_1 = \beta_1\).

Condition 2: \(\Cov(X, Z) = 0\). If \(\Cov(X, Z) = 0\), the omitted variable \(Z\) is uncorrelated with the explanatory variable \(X\). Then the auxiliary slope \(\gamma_1 = 0\). Even though \(Z\) affects \(Y\), its influence is absorbed into the disturbance term without creating correlation between \(X\) and the composite error. Thus \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\alpha}_1 = \beta_1 + \delta(0) = \beta_1\). \(\blacksquare\)

P2 · Orthogonality of OLS residuals

Prove that in any ordinary least squares regression with an intercept, the sample covariance between the regressor \(X\) and the OLS residual vector \(e\) is identically zero.

Solution

Let the sample regression model be \(Y_i = \hat{\alpha}_0 + \hat{\alpha}_1 X_i + e_i\) for \(i = 1, \dots, N\). The OLS normal equations minimize the sum of squared residuals \(\sum_{i=1}^N e_i^2\). Setting partial derivatives with respect to \(\hat{\alpha}_0\) and \(\hat{\alpha}_1\) to zero yields:

1. \(\sum_{i=1}^N e_i = 0 \implies \bar{e} = 0\).

2. \(\sum_{i=1}^N X_i e_i = 0\).

The sample covariance between \(X\) and \(e\) is defined by:

\[ \widehat{\Cov}(X, e) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (X_i - \bar{X})(e_i - \bar{e}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (X_i - \bar{X}) e_i. \]

Expanding the sum:

\[ \widehat{\Cov}(X, e) = \frac{1}{N} \left( \sum_{i=1}^N X_i e_i - \bar{X} \sum_{i=1}^N e_i \right). \]

Substituting the first-order conditions \(\sum_{i=1}^N X_i e_i = 0\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^N e_i = 0\):

\[ \widehat{\Cov}(X, e) = \frac{1}{N} (0 - \bar{X} \times 0) = 0. \]

Thus the sample covariance is identically zero. \(\blacksquare\)

P3 · Sign of omitted variable bias

Construct a sign matrix for omitted variable bias \(\operatorname{sgn}(\text{Bias}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\delta) \times \operatorname{sgn}(\Cov(X, Z))\) across all four non-zero combinations of \(\operatorname{sgn}(\delta)\) and \(\operatorname{sgn}(\Cov(X, Z))\). Prove that when an omitted factor affects \(Y\) positively and is positively correlated with \(X\), the naive estimate \(\hat{\alpha}_1\) is strictly upwardly biased.

Solution

The bias is given by \(\text{Bias} = \delta \frac{\Cov(X, Z)}{\Var(X)}\). Because \(\Var(X) > 0\), the sign of the bias is given by the product of the signs:

  • Case 1: \(\delta > 0\) and \(\Cov(X, Z) > 0 \implies \operatorname{sgn}(\text{Bias}) = (+)(+) = +\) (Upward bias).
  • Case 2: \(\delta > 0\) and \(\Cov(X, Z) < 0 \implies \operatorname{sgn}(\text{Bias}) = (+)(-) = -\) (Downward bias).
  • Case 3: \(\delta < 0\) and \(\Cov(X, Z) > 0 \implies \operatorname{sgn}(\text{Bias}) = (-)(+) = -\) (Downward bias).
  • Case 4: \(\delta < 0\) and \(\Cov(X, Z) < 0 \implies \operatorname{sgn}(\text{Bias}) = (-)(-) = +\) (Upward bias).

In Case 1, since \(\text{Bias} > 0\), we have \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\alpha}_1 = \beta_1 + \text{Bias} > \beta_1\). The naive coefficient is strictly upwardly biased, meaning it overestimates the true causal effect \(\beta_1\). \(\blacksquare\)

E2 · Evaluating delisting survivorship bias

In empirical asset pricing, microcap stock databases often omit delisting returns when firms are removed from exchanges for performance failure. Explain how this omitted data mechanism creates survivorship bias in tests of the small-firm premium, citing Shumway (1997).

Solution

Shumway (1997) demonstrates that CRSP historically failed to track returns on the final delisting day for distressed firms, often substituting zero or missing values. In reality, delisting for performance failure (insolvency, bankruptcy) carries an average final-month return of approximately -30% on the NYSE and -55% on NASDAQ. Because small and microcap stocks experience dramatically higher delisting frequencies than large firms, omitting negative delisting returns upwardly biases the measured returns of small firms, creating an artificial small-firm return premium that substantially attenuates once delisting corrections are applied.

E3 · Replicating event study abnormal returns

In Ball and Brown (1968), how does the timing of earnings announcements serve as an identification mechanism to demonstrate that accounting numbers contain economic information?

Solution

Ball and Brown (1968) examine cumulative abnormal residuals (CAR) aligned in event time from month -11 to month +6 relative to the annual earnings announcement month (month 0). By partitioning firms into positive and negative unexpected earnings portfolios based on a forecasting model, they show that portfolios drift in opposite directions throughout the pre-announcement year, with 85% to 90% of the information anticipated prior to the release month. The sharp jump at month 0 and continued drift afterward provide causal identification that accounting net income conveys valuation-relevant information to equity markets.

R1 · Designing a difference-in-differences identification strategy

Design a difference-in-differences (DiD) identification strategy to estimate the causal impact of a major financial transaction tax (FTT) introduced in Country A on market liquidity (bid-ask spread), while Country B does not adopt the tax. State the required parallel trends assumption and formulate a falsification test.

Solution

Model specification: Estimate \(\operatorname{Spread}_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta (\operatorname{Treated}_i \times \operatorname{Post}_t) + \epsilon_{it}\), where \(\operatorname{Treated}_i = 1\) for stocks in Country A and 0 for matched control stocks in Country B, and \(\operatorname{Post}_t = 1\) following tax enactment.

Parallel Trends Assumption: In the counterfactual absence of the FTT, the average change in bid-ask spreads for treated stocks in Country A would have equaled the average change for control stocks in Country B: \(\E[\operatorname{Spread}_{it}(0) - \operatorname{Spread}_{is}(0) \mid \operatorname{Treated}=1] = \E[\operatorname{Spread}_{it}(0) - \operatorname{Spread}_{is}(0) \mid \operatorname{Treated}=0]\) for pre-period \(s\) and post-period \(t\).

Falsification Test: Estimate dynamic event-study coefficients \(\sum_{k=-K}^M \beta_k (\operatorname{Treated}_i \times \indic_{\{t = t^\ast + k\}})\) for pre-treatment periods \(k < 0\). If any pre-treatment lead coefficient \(\beta_k\) differs significantly from zero, the parallel trends assumption is refuted, indicating that liquidity trends diverged before the policy shock.

R2 · Multiple testing hurdles in factor discovery

Evaluate the recommendation of Harvey and Liu (2020) to raise the t-statistic hurdle in empirical finance from 2.0 to 3.0. Calculate the change in critical p-value under a standard normal distribution and discuss the trade-off between Type I and Type II errors in quantitative asset allocation.

Solution

Under a standard normal distribution \(Z \sim \Normal(0, 1)\), a two-tailed critical value of \(t = 1.96\) corresponds to \(p = 0.0500\) (a 5% false positive rate under a single test). A critical value of \(t = 3.00\) corresponds to \(p = 2(1 - \Phi(3.00)) \approx 0.0027\) (a 0.27% significance level), reducing the per-test Type I error rate by a factor of 18.5.

Trade-off Analysis: Raising the hurdle to \(t > 3.0\) substantially reduces Type I errors (accepting spurious data-mined return predictors as genuine alpha), protecting investment capital from false discoveries. However, it increases Type II errors (rejecting true alpha signals that have modest effect sizes or shorter historical sample lengths). In competitive capital markets where false discovery leads directly to portfolio drawdowns and execution costs, the cost of Type I errors is severe, justifying the elevated threshold.

R3 · Contract-driven architecture in production quant engines

Compare the Atlas milestone contract framework (e.g., atlas/A1.contract.md) with traditional research scripting. Explain how deterministic inputs, frozen APIs, and property assertions prevent lookahead bias and survivorship bias in backtesting.

Solution

Traditional academic research scripts frequently execute ad-hoc data transformations across entire dataframes, inadvertently mixing decision-date timestamps with subsequent restatement dates (lookahead bias) or filtering assets using full-sample existence criteria (survivorship bias).

The Atlas contract architecture enforces three safeguards: (1) Deterministic Point-in-Time Inputs: Functions require an explicit as_of date parameter; filings dated after as_of are strictly inaccessible to the pipeline. (2) Frozen API Contracts: Module interfaces are immutable, preventing downstream consumers from bypassing data hygiene checks. (3) Invariant Property Assertions: Graders and runtime assertions continuously verify conservation laws (no-arbitrage constraints, articulation between cash flow and balance sheet, and strictly monotonic timestamps), ensuring that code cannot pass validation through hidden state leakage.
