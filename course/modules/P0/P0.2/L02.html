Quantitative finance requires permanent retention of hundreds of mathematical theorems, econometric estimators, and pricing formulas. Most students rely on passive rereading, highlighted lecture slides, and massed cramming—strategies cognitive science has proven to produce an illusion of competence followed by rapid forgetting. This lesson establishes the empirical foundations of durable learning: the testing effect, spaced retrieval practice, and interleaved problem solving. We formalize the Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler (FSRS), proving the mathematical relationship between memory stability and optimal review intervals, enabling you to maintain high retention across your doctoral curriculum with minimal study time.

1The testing effect and active retrieval practice

The default study habit of most university students is passive review: re-reading textbook chapters, skimming highlighted lecture notes, and watching recorded lectures. While re-reading creates a subjective sensation of fluency, cognitive psychology demonstrates that fluency during study is an unreliable predictor of delayed recall. Real, durable long-term retention is forged by the effortful act of retrieving information from memory.

Definition 1.1 (retrieval practice)

Retrieval practice (the testing effect) is a learning strategy wherein a student actively reconstructs knowledge from memory in response to a prompt or problem, rather than passively re-studying or reading the source material.

The landmark empirical demonstration of this phenomenon was conducted by Roediger & Karpicke (2006). In their first experiment, university students studied prose passages under two conditions: repeated study (Study-Study, STST) versus study followed by a testing recall session without feedback (Study-Test, ST). On an immediate test administered 5 minutes later, repeated study produced slightly higher recall (81% versus 75%). However, on a delayed test administered one week later, active retrieval practice produced dramatically superior retention: students in the testing condition retained 61% of the material compared to only 40% for students in the repeated study condition, an effect size of \(d = 1.05\).

In their second experiment, Roediger & Karpicke (2006) compared four study sessions (SSSS) against one study session followed by three consecutive retrieval tests (STTT). After one week, the repeated study group forgot more than half of the material, recalling only 40%, whereas the retrieval practice group retained 61%. In a comprehensive review of educational techniques, Dunlosky et al. (2013) awarded practice testing the highest utility rating among all evaluated learning methods, noting that its benefits generalize across age groups, educational domains, test formats, and retention intervals.

Remark 1.2 (cognitive mechanisms of the testing effect)

Why does testing beat re-reading? Cognitive psychologists identify two primary mechanisms: (1) Elaborative retrieval: Activating a concept from memory forces the brain to search through associative mental networks, creating multiple alternative retrieval pathways and semantic retrieval cues. (2) Reconsolidation: Effortful recall renders the memory trace labile, inducing synaptic consolidation processes that strengthen neural connections and render the trace resistant to interference and decay.

2Distributed practice and spaced retrieval

Active retrieval is powerful, but its timing is equally critical. If a student performs five retrieval trials in immediate succession on a single afternoon (massed practice, commonly known as cramming), performance collapses over multi-week horizons. Distributing retrieval opportunities across expanding time intervals—spaced retrieval—produces exponentially more durable memory traces.

Definition 2.1 (spaced retrieval)

Spaced retrieval practice (distributed practice) is an instructional schedule in which retrieval trials for a given concept are separated by temporal delays, with review intervals expanding as the concept's memory stability increases.

The quantitative relationship between review intervals and long-term memory was synthesized by Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006). In a meta-analytic review of 271 studies spanning 317 experiments in verbal recall tasks, the authors investigated how the inter-study interval (ISI) interacts with the retention interval (RI). Their quantitative synthesis demonstrated two fundamental principles:

  1. For any given retention interval, the relationship between ISI and final test performance is non-monotonic: recall initially rises as spacing increases, reaches a clear peak, and then gradually declines.
  2. As the desired retention interval expands from days to months, the optimal inter-study interval expands proportionally. For long retention intervals (one month to one year), the optimal spacing interval typically lies between 10% and 30% of the retention interval (Cepeda et al. 2006).
Remark 2.2 (scope boundary of verbal recall synthesis)

The quantitative estimates of Cepeda et al. (2006) were derived specifically from verbal recall and paired-associate tasks. While the qualitative benefit of spacing holds universally across mathematical and procedural skills, exact optimal intervals in quantitative finance depend heavily on mathematical schema complexity and problem difficulty.

3Shuffled problem solving and interleaved practice

Standard textbook curricula organize problem sets into uniform blocks: all problems at the end of Chapter 3 require applying the formula introduced in Chapter 3; all problems at the end of Chapter 4 require the Chapter 4 formula. This organization, known as blocked practice, creates a subtle but dangerous failure mode in quantitative education.

Definition 3.1 (interleaved practice)

Interleaved practice is a problem-solving schedule in which exercises requiring different mathematical techniques, models, or solution algorithms are mixed together in shuffled sequences, rather than grouped in homogeneous topical blocks.

The definitive study of interleaving in mathematical problem solving was conducted by Rohrer & Taylor (2007). Students learned to calculate the volume of four unfamiliar geometric solids (wedges, spherical cones, spheroids, and half cones). One cohort practiced in blocked fashion (all wedge problems grouped together, then all cone problems), while the other cohort solved an identical set of problems in shuffled (interleaved) order.

During the practice session, blocked practice appeared superior: blocked students scored 89% accuracy versus 60% for interleaved students. However, on the unannounced delayed test administered one week later, the results dramatically inverted: the interleaved cohort scored 63% accuracy, while the blocked cohort collapsed to 20% accuracy—a massive effect size of \(d = 1.34\).

Remark 3.2 (discriminative facility mechanism)

Rohrer and Taylor identified the underlying cognitive mechanism: blocked practice strips away the critical task of problem diagnosis. When a student knows all problems in a block require a specific bond duration formula, they do not need to read the problem to determine which model applies; they merely execute arithmetic. In real quantitative research and financial markets, problems do not arrive labeled with the chapter that solves them. Interleaved practice trains discriminative facility—the ability to look at an unstructured financial problem, recognize its structural features, and choose the correct mathematical framework.

4The FSRS scheduler and stochastic memory trajectories

To implement spaced retrieval at the scale of an entire doctoral curriculum, we need a mathematical model of memory dynamics that can calculate exact, individual review dates for thousands of concepts. While historical spaced repetition systems (such as Leitner boxes or SuperMemo SM-2) relied on heuristic linear multipliers, modern cognitive engineering relies on parameterized retrievability decay models.

Definition 4.1 (FSRS-family scheduler)

An FSRS-family spaced-repetition scheduler (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler) is an adaptive memory scheduling algorithm derived from the stochastic shortest path framework of Ye, Su & Cao (2022). It models card memory through a three-dimensional state vector of stability \(S \in \R^+\), difficulty \(D \in [1, 10]\), and retrievability \(R \in [0, 1]\), scheduling review intervals to maintain a target retention probability while minimizing total review effort.

In the open FSRS-4.5 specification implemented throughout the program's review deck (course/review.html), memory retrievability \(R(t, S)\) represents the probability that a concept can be successfully recalled at elapsed time \(t \ge 0\) days after its last review, given current memory stability \(S > 0\). The decay follows a power forgetting curve:

\[ R(t, S) = \left( 1 + F \cdot \frac{t}{S} \right)^C \]

where \(C = -0.5\) is the memory decay exponent and \(F = \frac{19}{81}\) is the scaling factor.

Theorem 4.2 (FSRS retrievability identity and optimal review interval)

Let retrievability follow the FSRS-4.5 power-decay model \(R(t, S) = \left( 1 + \frac{19}{81}\frac{t}{S} \right)^{-0.5}\). Then:

  1. At elapsed time \(t = S\), retrievability equals 90% exactly: \(R(S, S) = 0.90\).
  2. For any target retention probability \(r \in (0, 1)\), the unique optimal review interval \(I(S, r)\) that schedules review when \(R(t, S) = r\) is: \[ I(S, r) = \frac{S}{F} \left( r^{1/C} - 1 \right) = \frac{81 S}{19} \left( r^{-2} - 1 \right) \] In particular, when \(r = 0.90\), \(I(S, 0.90) = S\).
Proof

Part 1: Evaluate \(R(t, S)\) at \(t = S\):

\[ R(S, S) = \left( 1 + \frac{19}{81} \cdot \frac{S}{S} \right)^{-0.5} = \left( 1 + \frac{19}{81} \right)^{-0.5} = \left( \frac{81 + 19}{81} \right)^{-0.5} = \left( \frac{100}{81} \right)^{-0.5} \] Taking the reciprocal square root: \[ R(S, S) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{100}{81}}} = \frac{1}{\frac{10}{9}} = \frac{9}{10} = 0.90 \] This establishes the exact identity.

Part 2: To find the interval \(t = I(S, r)\) such that \(R(t, S) = r\), solve:

\[ \left( 1 + F \frac{t}{S} \right)^C = r \] Raising both sides to the power \(1/C\): \[ 1 + F \frac{t}{S} = r^{1/C} \implies F \frac{t}{S} = r^{1/C} - 1 \implies t = \frac{S}{F} \left( r^{1/C} - 1 \right) \] Substituting \(C = -0.5\) and \(F = 19/81\): \[ r^{1/(-0.5)} = r^{-2}, \quad \frac{1}{F} = \frac{81}{19} \implies I(S, r) = \frac{81 S}{19} \left( r^{-2} - 1 \right) \] Evaluating at \(r = 0.90 = 9/10\): \[ r^{-2} = \left( \frac{10}{9} \right)^2 = \frac{100}{81} \implies r^{-2} - 1 = \frac{100}{81} - 1 = \frac{19}{81} \] Multiplying by \(81 S / 19\): \[ I(S, 0.90) = \frac{81 S}{19} \cdot \frac{19}{81} = S \] proving that stability \(S\) is identically the elapsed time in days required for retrievability to decline from 1.0 to 0.90.

Example 4.3 (evaluating interval sensitivity to target retention)

Suppose a mathematical finance concept has current stability \(S = 20.0\text{ days}\). If the learner targets 90% retention (\(r = 0.90\)), the next review occurs at \(I(20, 0.90) = 20.0\text{ days}\). If the learner demands 95% retention (\(r = 0.95\)), the review interval contracts sharply:

\[ I(20, 0.95) = \frac{81(20)}{19} \left( (0.95)^{-2} - 1 \right) \approx 85.263 \times (1.10803 - 1) \approx 85.263 \times 0.10803 \approx 9.21\text{ days} \] Demanding 95% instead of 90% retention more than doubles review frequency, demonstrating the severe non-linear cost of extreme retention targets.

"""Lab 1 (L02): FSRS-family spaced retrieval scheduling and memory dynamics.

Standard library only. Implements the Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler (FSRS-4.5) memory model
derived from Ye, Su & Cao (2022). Verifies the exact mathematical identity R(S, S) = 0.90, calculates
optimal review intervals as a function of target retention, and simulates longitudinal memory retention
over a 180-day horizon comparing massed cramming, fixed linear spacing, and adaptive FSRS spaced retrieval.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

# FSRS-4.5 power-forgetting curve constants
DECAY: float = -0.5
FACTOR: float = 19.0 / 81.0


def fsrs_retrievability(elapsed_days: float, stability: float) -> float:
    """Retrievability R(t, S) = (1 + (19/81) * (t / S))^(-0.5)."""
    if stability <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return math.pow(1.0 + FACTOR * (elapsed_days / stability), DECAY)


def fsrs_interval(stability: float, target_retention: float = 0.90) -> float:
    """Optimal review interval I(S, r) = (S / (19/81)) * (r^(1 / -0.5) - 1) = (81/19) * S * (r^(-2) - 1)."""
    if target_retention <= 0.0 or target_retention >= 1.0:
        raise ValueError("Target retention r must lie strictly in (0, 1)")
    return (stability / FACTOR) * (math.pow(target_retention, 1.0 / DECAY) - 1.0)


def update_stability_on_recall(stability: float, difficulty: float, retrievability: float) -> float:
    """Update stability after successful recall under standard FSRS parameters."""
    # Simplified standard FSRS-4.5 recall multiplier:
    # S' = S * (1 + exp(w8) * (11 - D) * S^(-w9) * (exp(w10 * (1 - R)) - 1))
    # Using default parameter magnitudes: w8 = 1.6474, w9 = 0.1367, w10 = 1.0461
    w8, w9, w10 = 1.6474, 0.1367, 1.0461
    factor = math.exp(w8) * (11.0 - difficulty) * math.pow(stability, -w9)
    gain = factor * (math.exp(w10 * (1.0 - retrievability)) - 1.0)
    return stability * (1.0 + max(gain, 0.1))


def simulate_massed_practice(initial_stability: float, horizon_days: int) -> tuple[int, float]:
    """Massed practice: reviews on Day 0 and Day 1, then no subsequent reviews."""
    # Day 0: card introduced
    s = initial_stability
    # Day 1: cramming review after 1 day
    r1 = fsrs_retrievability(1.0, s)
    s = update_stability_on_recall(s, difficulty=5.0, retrievability=r1)
    reviews_count = 2

    # Retrievability at Day horizon_days (elapsed = horizon_days - 1)
    final_r = fsrs_retrievability(float(horizon_days - 1), s)
    return reviews_count, final_r


def simulate_fixed_spacing(initial_stability: float, horizon_days: int, step_days: int = 7) -> tuple[int, float]:
    """Fixed spacing: reviews every fixed interval (e.g. 7 days)."""
    s = initial_stability
    reviews_count = 1  # Day 0 initial study
    current_day = 0

    while current_day + step_days <= horizon_days:
        current_day += step_days
        r = fsrs_retrievability(float(step_days), s)
        s = update_stability_on_recall(s, difficulty=5.0, retrievability=r)
        reviews_count += 1

    remaining = float(horizon_days - current_day)
    final_r = fsrs_retrievability(remaining, s) if remaining > 0 else 1.0
    return reviews_count, final_r


def simulate_fsrs_spacing(
    initial_stability: float,
    horizon_days: int,
    target_retention: float = 0.90,
) -> tuple[int, float]:
    """Adaptive FSRS spacing: review dynamically scheduled when R drops to target retention."""
    s = initial_stability
    reviews_count = 1  # Day 0 initial study
    current_day = 0.0

    while True:
        interval = max(1.0, round(fsrs_interval(s, target_retention)))
        if current_day + interval > horizon_days:
            break
        current_day += interval
        r = fsrs_retrievability(interval, s)
        s = update_stability_on_recall(s, difficulty=5.0, retrievability=r)
        reviews_count += 1

    remaining = float(horizon_days) - current_day
    final_r = fsrs_retrievability(remaining, s) if remaining > 0 else 1.0
    return reviews_count, final_r


def main() -> None:
    print("SIMULATED FSRS Memory Model Verification & Longitudinal Simulation")

    # 1. Closed-form identity checks
    test_stability = 24.5
    r_identity = fsrs_retrievability(elapsed_days=test_stability, stability=test_stability)
    i_identity = fsrs_interval(stability=test_stability, target_retention=0.90)

    print("\n--- Theoretical Identity Verifications ---")
    print(f"Test stability S:                        {test_stability:.1f} days")
    print(f"R(S, S) calculated:                      {r_identity:.12f}")
    print("Theoretical target R(S, S):              0.900000000000")
    print(f"Absolute residual |R(S, S) - 0.9|:       {abs(r_identity - 0.9):.2e}")
    print(f"I(S, 0.90) calculated:                   {i_identity:.12f} days")
    print(f"Absolute residual |I(S, 0.90) - S|:     {abs(i_identity - test_stability):.2e}")

    # 2. Sensitivity of optimal interval to target retention
    print("\n--- Optimal Interval Sensitivity vs Target Retention (S = 10.0 days) ---")
    print(f"{'Target Retention (r)':>20} | {'Optimal Interval I(S, r)':>25} | {'Interval / S Ratio':>20}")
    print("-" * 72)
    s_base = 10.0
    for r_target in [0.70, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90, 0.95]:
        interval = fsrs_interval(s_base, r_target)
        ratio = interval / s_base
        print(f"{r_target:20.2f} | {interval:23.2f} d | {ratio:20.2f}x")

    # 3. 180-Day longitudinal simulation across learning paradigms
    horizon = 180
    init_s = 2.0  # Initial stability of 2 days after first encounter

    reviews_massed, ret_massed = simulate_massed_practice(init_s, horizon)
    reviews_fixed, ret_fixed = simulate_fixed_spacing(init_s, horizon, step_days=7)
    reviews_fsrs, ret_fsrs = simulate_fsrs_spacing(init_s, horizon, target_retention=0.90)

    print(f"\n--- 180-Day Longitudinal Retention Simulation (S_0 = {init_s:.1f} days) ---")
    print(f"{'Strategy':>25} | {'Total Reviews':>15} | {'Day 180 Retention':>20} | {'Efficiency (Ret/Rev)':>22}")
    print("-" * 90)
    for name, revs, ret in [
        ("Massed Practice (Cramming)", reviews_massed, ret_massed),
        ("Fixed Spacing (7-Day)", reviews_fixed, ret_fixed),
        ("Adaptive FSRS-4.5 (r=0.90)", reviews_fsrs, ret_fsrs),
    ]:
        eff = ret / revs
        print(f"{name:>25} | {revs:15d} | {ret:19.1%} | {eff:22.4f}")


main()

5Where the canon breaks

Standard higher education practices reinforce intuitive study habits that contradict the cognitive science of memory consolidation. In traditional university environments, four widespread practices fail:

  • The illusion of competence from re-reading. Students mistake perceptual fluency (the ease of re-reading familiar text) for durable storage strength. Under Roediger & Karpicke (2006), re-reading feels effective to students but produces rapid forgetting over delayed intervals.
  • The cramming trap. Massed cramming before semester midterms can produce acceptable short-term scores on an exam the next morning. However, as synthesized by Cepeda et al. (2006), memory traces formed through massed review decay exponentially, leaving students with zero residual retention when encountering prerequisite material six months later.
  • Topical blocking in quantitative problem sets. Grouping problems by chapter eliminates the need for problem diagnosis. When students enter trading desks or research environments, they fail because market anomalies do not arrive labeled with the econometric technique required to test them (Rohrer & Taylor 2007).
  • Fixed-interval flashcard reviews. Reviewing cards on fixed calendar intervals (e.g. once every week) wastes enormous study effort on highly stable cards while failing to review rapidly decaying cards before they are forgotten (Ye, Su & Cao 2022).

6Misconceptions

  1. “Re-reading textbook chapters and highlighting key formulas is an effective strategy for long-term retention.”

    Empirical evidence conclusively refutes this belief. Roediger & Karpicke (2006) proved that while repeated reading provides a temporary boost on 5-minute tests, testing produces 61% one-week retention versus 40% for repeated study (\(d = 1.05\)). Dunlosky et al. (2013) ranked re-reading and highlighting as low-utility techniques.

  2. “Massed cramming over a weekend is an efficient study strategy for mastering doctoral coursework.”

    Massed cramming produces rapid memory decay. Cepeda et al. (2006) showed that long-term retention requires distributing study sessions across intervals proportional to the retention horizon. In Lab 1, massed practice resulted in retention collapsing to 33.5% over 180 days, whereas spaced retrieval maintained 97.9% retention.

  3. “Practicing 20 identical Black-Scholes option pricing problems in a single block produces superior pricing mastery than solving mixed problems.”

    Blocked practice creates an illusion of mastery by eliminating problem diagnosis. Rohrer & Taylor (2007) demonstrated that while blocked practice scores higher during initial training (89% vs 60%), interleaved practice triples delayed test performance (63% vs 20%, \(d = 1.34\)) by training learners to identify which formula applies to an unlabelled problem.

7Problem set

Mechanics

Problem M.1. Under the FSRS-4.5 decay model, compute the retrievability \(R(t, S)\) of an asset pricing formula after \(t = 7\text{ days}\) if its current stability is \(S = 14\text{ days}\).

Worked solution

Under the FSRS-4.5 model, \(R(t, S) = (1 + \frac{19}{81}\frac{t}{S})^{-0.5}\). Substituting \(t = 7\) and \(S = 14\): \(\frac{t}{S} = \frac{7}{14} = 0.5\). The argument is \(1 + \frac{19}{81}(0.5) = 1 + \frac{19}{162} = \frac{181}{162} \approx 1.117284\). Taking the inverse square root: \(R(7, 14) = (1.117284)^{-0.5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.117284}} \approx \frac{1}{1.057017} \approx 0.94606\) (94.61%).

Problem M.2. A quantitative finance card has stability \(S = 30\text{ days}\). Compute the review interval \(I(S, r)\) required to maintain target retention \(r = 0.85\).

Worked solution

Under Theorem 4.2, \(I(S, r) = \frac{81 S}{19} (r^{-2} - 1)\). For \(r = 0.85\), \(r^{-2} = (0.85)^{-2} = \frac{1}{0.7225} \approx 1.384083\). Thus \(r^{-2} - 1 \approx 0.384083\). Multiplying by \(\frac{81 \times 30}{19} = \frac{2430}{19} \approx 127.8947\): \(I(30, 0.85) = 127.8947 \times 0.384083 \approx 49.12\text{ days}\).

Problem M.3. In an experimental test of the testing effect, a student studies 40 finance terms under repeated study (STST) and 40 terms under retrieval practice (ST). Based on Roediger & Karpicke (2006) 1-week retention rates (40% vs 61%), how many terms are expected to be recalled in each condition?

Worked solution

For repeated study: \(\E[\text{Recall}] = 40 \times 0.40 = 16\text{ terms}\). For retrieval practice: \(\E[\text{Recall}] = 40 \times 0.61 = 24.4\text{ terms}\). Retrieval practice yields an expected gain of \(24.4 - 16 = 8.4\) additional retained concepts, representing a 52.5% relative increase in retained knowledge.

Proof

Problem P.1. Prove that the FSRS-4.5 retrievability decay function \(R(t, S) = (1 + F \frac{t}{S})^C\) with \(F > 0\) and \(C < 0\) is strictly decreasing and strictly convex with respect to elapsed time \(t > 0\).

Worked solution

Compute the first derivative with respect to \(t\): \(\frac{\partial R}{\partial t} = C \frac{F}{S} \left(1 + F \frac{t}{S}\right)^{C - 1}\). Because \(F > 0\), \(S > 0\), and \(1 + F \frac{t}{S} > 0\) for \(t > 0\), the sign is determined entirely by \(C\). Since \(C = -0.5 < 0\), \(\frac{\partial R}{\partial t} < 0\) for all \(t > 0\), proving \(R\) is strictly decreasing. Compute the second derivative: \(\frac{\partial^2 R}{\partial t^2} = C(C - 1) \left(\frac{F}{S}\right)^2 \left(1 + F \frac{t}{S}\right)^{C - 2}\). Since \(C = -0.5\), \(C(C - 1) = (-0.5)(-1.5) = +0.75 > 0\). Since all other factors are squared or positive, \(\frac{\partial^2 R}{\partial t^2} > 0\) for all \(t > 0\), proving strict convexity.

Problem P.2. Prove that the interval elasticity with respect to target retention, defined as \(\epsilon(r) = \frac{\partial \ln I(S, r)}{\partial \ln r}\), satisfies \(\epsilon(r) = \frac{-2}{1 - r^2}\) for \(r \in (0, 1)\), and evaluate the elasticity at \(r = 0.90\).

Worked solution

From Theorem 4.2, \(I(S, r) = K \cdot S \cdot (r^{-2} - 1)\) where \(K = 81/19\). Taking natural logs: \(\ln I = \ln(K \cdot S) + \ln(r^{-2} - 1)\). Differentiating with respect to \(\ln r\) using \(\frac{d \ln I}{d \ln r} = r \frac{d \ln I}{d r}\): \(\frac{d \ln I}{d r} = \frac{-2 r^{-3}}{r^{-2} - 1} = \frac{-2 r^{-3}}{\frac{1 - r^2}{r^2}} = \frac{-2}{r(1 - r^2)}\). Multiplying by \(r\): \(\epsilon(r) = r \cdot \frac{-2}{r(1 - r^2)} = \frac{-2}{1 - r^2}\). At \(r = 0.90\): \(1 - r^2 = 1 - 0.81 = 0.19\). Thus \(\epsilon(0.90) = \frac{-2}{0.19} \approx -10.53\). This proves extreme elasticity: a 1% increase in target retention requires a 10.53% reduction in the review interval.

Problem P.3. Under a continuous-time memory model where forgetting rate is \(h(t) = -\frac{1}{R(t)} \frac{d R}{d t}\), prove that the FSRS power-law decay exhibits a decreasing hazard rate over elapsed time \(t\), in contrast to exponential decay \(R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\) which exhibits a constant hazard rate.

Worked solution

For exponential decay \(R(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\), \(\frac{dR}{dt} = -\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\), so \(h_{\text{exp}}(t) = -\frac{-\lambda e^{-\lambda t}}{e^{-\lambda t}} = \lambda\), which is constant for all \(t\). For the FSRS power-law model \(R(t) = (1 + F \frac{t}{S})^C\) with \(C = -0.5\): \(\frac{dR}{dt} = C \frac{F}{S} (1 + F \frac{t}{S})^{C - 1}\). The hazard rate is \(h_{\text{fsrs}}(t) = -\frac{C \frac{F}{S} (1 + F \frac{t}{S})^{C - 1}}{(1 + F \frac{t}{S})^C} = -C \frac{F}{S} \left(1 + F \frac{t}{S}\right)^{-1} = \frac{0.5 F / S}{1 + F \frac{t}{S}}\). Differentiating with respect to \(t\): \(\frac{d h_{\text{fsrs}}}{dt} = -\frac{0.5 (F/S)^2}{(1 + F \frac{t}{S})^2} < 0\). This proves that the instantaneous conditional forgetting rate strictly decreases with elapsed time: memories that survive initial decay become progressively more resilient per unit of additional time.

Empirical

Problem E.1. In the meta-analysis by Cepeda et al. (2006), the optimal inter-study interval (ISI) for a 35-day retention interval (RI) was found to be approximately 5.7 days. Compute the optimal ISI-to-RI ratio and determine the predicted optimal review date for a student preparing for a comprehensive doctoral exam scheduled 70 days after initial study.

Worked solution

The ratio is \(\text{Ratio} = \frac{\text{ISI}}{\text{RI}} = \frac{5.7}{35.0} \approx 0.1629\) (16.29%). Applying this 16.3% optimal ratio to an exam horizon of \(\text{RI} = 70\text{ days}\): \(\text{Optimal ISI} = 70 \times 0.1629 \approx 11.4\text{ days}\). Thus, the student should schedule their primary retrieval review session approximately 11 to 12 days after initial study to maximize retention on Day 70.

Problem E.2. In Rohrer & Taylor (2007), blocked students scored 20% on the final delayed test while interleaved students scored 63%, with pooled standard deviation \(s = 32\%\). Calculate the empirical Cohen's \(d\) effect size and state whether it matches the published value.

Worked solution

Cohen's \(d\) is calculated as \(d = \frac{\bar{X}_{\text{interleaved}} - \bar{X}_{\text{blocked}}}{s_{\text{pooled}}} = \frac{63\% - 20\%}{32\%} = \frac{43\%}{32\%} \approx 1.34375\). Rounded to two decimal places, this equals \(d = 1.34\), exactly matching the reported effect size in Rohrer & Taylor (2007, p. 490).

Problem E.3. In Lab 1, over a 180-day longitudinal simulation, adaptive FSRS-4.5 scheduling achieved 97.9% final retention using 5 reviews, while fixed 7-day spacing achieved 99.9% retention using 26 reviews. Calculate the marginal review efficiency (marginal retention gained per additional review session) of fixed spacing relative to FSRS.

Worked solution

The additional reviews required by fixed spacing is \(\Delta \text{Reviews} = 26 - 5 = 21\text{ reviews}\). The marginal retention gained is \(\Delta \text{Retention} = 99.9\% - 97.9\% = +2.0\%\) (0.0200). The marginal efficiency is \(\frac{\Delta \text{Retention}}{\Delta \text{Reviews}} = \frac{0.0200}{21} \approx 0.000952\) (less than 0.10 percentage points of retention per review session). Fixed spacing requires 420% more study effort for a negligible 2.0% retention gain, confirming the dramatic efficiency of adaptive FSRS scheduling.

Problem R.1. How should an institutional quantitative research desk structure daily code and paper reviews to maximize analyst retention of mathematical formulas while preventing task-switching productivity loss?

Worked solution

A research desk should operationalize retrieval and interleaving through a structured 15-minute daily morning micro-deck: (1) Deploy an automated FSRS queue embedded directly into the developer environment (e.g. CLI prompt or editor startup) containing 5 to 10 cards due that morning. (2) Cards should be interleaved across diverse topics (derivatives pricing, econometric estimators, market microstructure mechanics) rather than blocked by the analyst's current project. (3) Completing the retrieval deck at the start of the trading day takes under 10 minutes, eliminating midday task switching while ensuring permanent retention of the firm's algorithmic IP.

Problem R.2. In high-frequency market microstructure where market conditions evolve non-stationarily, how can the FSRS difficulty parameter \(D\) be dynamically updated to account for concept shifts (e.g. regime changes in bid-ask spread dynamics)?

Worked solution

In non-stationary environments, concept difficulty is not constant across calendar time. The scheduler can incorporate a Kalman-filter or Bayesian state-space update on difficulty: \(D_{t+1} = \lambda D_t + (1 - \lambda) \hat{D}_{\text{obs}}\), where \(\hat{D}_{\text{obs}}\) is inferred from recent recall latencies and error rates across the analyst cohort. When market volatility regimes shift, the variance of the observation noise expands, accelerating the adaptation rate of \(D\), which contracts intervals and triggers re-learning before critical models fail in live execution.

Problem R.3. Formulate the stochastic shortest path problem of Ye, Su & Cao (2022) for scheduling card reviews, and explain why the optimal policy exhibits expanding review intervals under power-law memory dynamics.

Worked solution

In Ye, Su & Cao (2022), spaced repetition is modeled as a Markov decision process over states \(x = (S, D, R)\). An action is the chosen review interval \(\Delta t\). The transition probability depends on whether the student successfully recalls the card (\(Y = 1\), with probability \(R(\Delta t, S)\)) or fails (\(Y = 0\)). The objective is to minimize total discounted review cost \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty \gamma^k c(x_k, a_k)\) subject to keeping terminal retrievability above threshold \(r\). When recall succeeds, stability jumps to \(S' > S\). Because \(I(S', r) \propto S'\), the subsequent optimal interval \(\Delta t'\) is strictly larger than \(\Delta t\). Thus, successful recall monotonically expands intervals, generating the characteristic expanding cadence of optimal spaced repetition.

9Retrieval quiz

  1. In the landmark experiment by Roediger & Karpicke (2006), what were the one-week delayed recall rates for the study-test (ST) versus repeated-study (STST) groups?

    Options

    Roediger & Karpicke (2006) showed that testing produced 61% recall after one week compared to only 40% for repeated study, reversing the immediate test pattern.

  2. What utility rating did Dunlosky et al. (2013) award to practice testing and distributed practice in their comprehensive educational review?

    Options

    Dunlosky et al. (2013) evaluated ten learning techniques and awarded only practice testing and distributed practice their highest utility rating.

  3. According to the quantitative synthesis by Cepeda et al. (2006), what proportion of the retention interval does the optimal inter-study interval typically occupy for long delays?

    Options

    Cepeda et al. (2006) showed that for long retention intervals, the peak of the non-monotonic recall curve occurs when the inter-study interval is roughly 10% to 30% of the retention interval.

  4. In Rohrer & Taylor (2007), what was the delayed test score difference between interleaved and blocked mathematics practice cohorts?

    Options

    Rohrer & Taylor (2007) found that interleaved practice tripled delayed test scores (63% vs 20%), despite blocked practice looking superior during initial training.

  5. Why does blocked practice produce an illusion of competence during initial practice sessions?

    Options

    Blocked practice removes the necessity of problem identification. Because all problems in a chapter use the same model, learners execute arithmetic without learning to diagnose model applicability.

  6. Under the FSRS-4.5 retrievability decay formula \(R(t, S) = (1 + \frac{19}{81}\frac{t}{S})^{-0.5}\), what is the value of \(R(S, S)\)?

    Options

    By mathematical construction, \(R(S, S) = (1 + 19/81)^{-0.5} = (100/81)^{-0.5} = 9/10 = 0.900\) exactly.

  7. Under Theorem 4.2, what is the optimal review interval \(I(S, r)\) when stability is \(S = 10\text{ days}\) and target retention is \(r = 0.90\)?

    Options

    When target retention is 0.90, the optimal interval \(I(S, 0.90) = S\), so for \(S = 10\text{ days}\), the interval is exactly 10.0 days.

  8. What happens to the optimal review interval \(I(S, r)\) as a student demands higher target retention \(r\) (e.g. increasing from 0.90 to 0.95)?

    Options

    Because \(I(S, r) \propto (r^{-2} - 1)\), increasing target retention \(r\) reduces \((r^{-2} - 1)\), contracting the interval and increasing the required review frequency.

  9. In Lab 1, what was the 180-day retention rate for massed practice (cramming)?

    Options

    In Lab 1, massed practice (reviewing only on Day 0 and Day 1) suffered severe memory decay, leaving only 33.5% retention by Day 180.

  10. What mathematical formulation underlies the FSRS family of spaced-repetition schedulers introduced by Ye, Su & Cao (2022)?

    Options

    Ye, Su & Cao (2022) formulated spaced repetition as a stochastic shortest path (SSP) problem over memory state space to minimize total review cost subject to target retention.
