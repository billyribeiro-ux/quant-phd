Model Comparison, Horse Races & The Tangency Frontier

The proliferation of hundreds of candidate factors has created an empirical crisis known as the 'factor zoo.' Evaluating competing asset pricing models requires moving beyond ad-hoc test assets to the geometric foundations of the mean-variance frontier. Mastering the finite-sample Gibbons–Ross–Shanken (GRS) exact test, the Barillas–Shanken test-asset invariance theorem, the maximum squared Sharpe ratio metric, and multiple testing discipline provides the doctoral toolkit necessary to conduct rigorous, definitive factor horse races.

1The Gibbons–Ross–Shanken (1989) Exact Test

In time-series asset pricing regressions, an econometrician tests whether a \(K\)-factor model perfectly prices a set of \(N\) test assets by examining the joint hypothesis that all pricing errors (\(\alpha_i\)) are simultaneously zero:

\[\begin{aligned} H_0: \boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_N)^\top = \mathbf{0}. \end{aligned}\]

Gibbons, Ross & Shanken (1989) derived the exact, finite-sample \(F\)-distribution for this joint hypothesis under normally distributed residuals.

Theorem 6.1 · Gibbons–Ross–Shanken (GRS) Exact F-Statistic
Let \(R_t\) denote the \(N \times 1\) vector of excess returns on \(N\) test assets, and let \(f_t\) denote the \(K \times 1\) vector of traded factor returns across \(T\) time periods: \[\begin{aligned} R_t &= \boldsymbol{\alpha} + \boldsymbol{\beta} f_t + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \sim \text{i.i.d. } \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\epsilon). \end{aligned}\] Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\) be the OLS intercept vector, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon = \frac{1}{T - K - 1}\sum_{t=1}^T \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_t \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_t^\top\) be the unbiased residual covariance matrix, \(\bar{f} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T f_t\), and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_f = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^T (f_t - \bar{f})(f_t - \bar{f})^\top\). Under the null hypothesis \(H_0: \boldsymbol{\alpha} = \mathbf{0}\), the statistic: \[\begin{aligned} GRS &= \left(\frac{T - N - K}{N}\right) \left[ 1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_f^{-1} \bar{f} \right]^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \end{aligned}\] is distributed exactly as an \(F\)-distribution with \(N\) numerator degrees of freedom and \(T - N - K\) denominator degrees of freedom: \[\begin{aligned} GRS \sim F(N, T - N - K). \end{aligned}\]

Theorem 6.2 · Geometric Equivalence to Tangency Sharpe Expansion
The GRS quadratic form is algebraically identical to the relative expansion in the maximum squared Sharpe ratio achieved by augmenting the \(K\) factors with the \(N\) test assets: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{Sh^2(f, R) - Sh^2(f)}{1 + Sh^2(f)} &= \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}, \end{aligned}\] where \(Sh^2(f) = \bar{f}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_f^{-1} \bar{f}\) is the maximum squared Sharpe ratio of the factor portfolio, and \(Sh^2(f, R)\) is the maximum squared Sharpe ratio of the combined portfolio of factors and test assets.

Proof
By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell orthogonal decomposition, any portfolio of \((f, R)\) can be uniquely mapped to a portfolio of the factors \(f\) and the orthogonalized residuals \(\tilde{R} = R - \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} f = \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\). Because \(\Cov(f, \tilde{R}) = \mathbf{0}\), the joint covariance matrix is block-diagonal with blocks \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_f\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon\). The maximum squared Sharpe ratio of the augmented space is: \[\begin{aligned} Sh^2(f, R) &= Sh^2(f) + Sh^2(\tilde{R}) = Sh^2(f) + \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \left( 1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_f^{-1} \bar{f} \right) \cdot \dots \end{aligned}\] Recognizing that the sample mean of \(\tilde{R}\) in finite sample incorporates the factor estimation uncertainty \(\bar{f}\), exact matrix inversion of the partitioned sample second-moment matrix yields: \[\begin{aligned} 1 + Sh^2(f, R) &= \left( 1 + Sh^2(f) \right) \left( 1 + \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \right). \end{aligned}\] Dividing both sides by \(1 + Sh^2(f)\) and subtracting 1: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{Sh^2(f, R) - Sh^2(f)}{1 + Sh^2(f)} &= \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}. \end{aligned}\] Therefore, testing \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \mathbf{0}\) is mathematically equivalent to testing whether the factor tangency portfolio already achieves the maximum Sharpe ratio across all assets.

2Barillas & Shanken (2017) Test-Asset Invariance

In empirical finance literature, researchers traditionally ran 'horse races' by selecting an arbitrary set of test assets (such as 25 Size-BM portfolios, 25 Size-Momentum portfolios, or 30 Industry portfolios) and comparing models by their GRS statistics or mean absolute pricing errors. Barillas & Shanken (2017) proved that when factor models consist of traded portfolios, this practice is econometrically flawed.

Definition 6.1 · Model Comparison by Maximum Squared Sharpe Ratio
The practice of comparing factor models by maximum squared Sharpe ratio ranks candidate traded factor models \(M_a\) and \(M_b\) strictly by the maximum squared Sharpe ratio achievable by their constituent factor portfolios: \[\begin{aligned} Sh^2(f) &= \boldsymbol{\mu}_f^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_f. \end{aligned}\] Model \(M_a\) dominates Model \(M_b\) if and only if \(Sh^2(f^{(a)}) > Sh^2(f^{(b)})\).

Theorem 6.3 · Barillas–Shanken Test-Asset Invariance Theorem
Let \(M_a\) and \(M_b\) be two competing models composed of traded factor portfolios \(f^{(a)}\) and \(f^{(b)}\). Let \(R\) be any set of test assets that includes both sets of factors (\(R \supseteq \{f^{(a)}, f^{(b)}\}\)). The difference between the GRS statistics (or quadratic pricing error metrics) of the two models on test assets \(R\) is strictly determined by their ability to price each other's factors, and is completely independent of any additional test assets in \(R\). Specifically, Model \(M_a\) achieves a smaller GRS statistic than Model \(M_b\) on any common spanning test set \(R\) if and only if: \[\begin{aligned} Sh^2(f^{(a)}) > Sh^2(f^{(b)}). \end{aligned}\]

The Barillas–Shanken theorem delivers a profound simplification: researchers no longer need to argue over which test assets to use (Size-BM vs Size-Momentum vs Profitability-Investment grids). For traded factor models, the model that spans the highest tangency Sharpe ratio automatically dominates all competing models across all possible test assets.

Lab 1 · Factor Model Horse Race, GRS Test, and Max-Sharpe Metric

Benchmarking CAPM, Fama-French 3, Carhart 4, Fama-French 5, and the Hou-Xue-Zhang q-factor model on 25 simulated test assets will demonstrate that multi-factor models expand the tangency frontier and achieve monotonically lower GRS rejection statistics.

"""Lab F2-L06-1: Factor Model Horse Race, GRS Test, and Max-Sharpe Metric.

Compares CAPM, Fama-French 3, Carhart 4, Fama-French 5, and the Hou-Xue-Zhang
q-factor model on 25 test assets using the Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (1989) test
and the Barillas-Shanken (2017) maximum squared Sharpe ratio metric Sh^2(f).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
import scipy.stats


def run_factor_model_comparison(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Execute GRS tests and compute max squared Sharpe ratios across 5 models."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_months = 360  # 30 years
    n_assets = 25  # 25 test portfolios (e.g. 5x5 Size-BM)

    # Underlying factors
    mkt = rng.normal(0.065 / 12, 0.155 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    smb = rng.normal(0.025 / 12, 0.100 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    hml = rng.normal(0.035 / 12, 0.110 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    wml = 0.075 / 12 + rng.normal(0.0, 0.130 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    rmw = rng.normal(0.038 / 12, 0.080 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    cma = rng.normal(0.032 / 12, 0.070 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)

    # q-factor components: MKT, ME (size), IA (investment), ROE (profitability)
    # q-factor IA and ROE track CMA and RMW with slight noise / quarterly timing
    q_me = smb + rng.normal(0, 0.02 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    q_ia = cma + rng.normal(0, 0.02 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    q_roe = rmw + rng.normal(0, 0.02 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)

    # Model dictionaries
    models = {
        "CAPM": np.column_stack([mkt]),
        "FF3": np.column_stack([mkt, smb, hml]),
        "Carhart": np.column_stack([mkt, smb, hml, wml]),
        "FF5": np.column_stack([mkt, smb, hml, rmw, cma]),
        "q-factor": np.column_stack([mkt, q_me, q_ia, q_roe]),
    }

    # Simulate 25 test assets with realistic factor structure + slight mispricing
    b_mkt = rng.uniform(0.7, 1.3, size=n_assets)
    b_smb = rng.uniform(-0.4, 0.8, size=n_assets)
    b_hml = rng.uniform(-0.5, 0.7, size=n_assets)
    # True pricing errors (CAPM has largest alphas, FF5 / q-factor smallest)
    true_alpha = rng.normal(0.0, 0.015 / 12, size=n_assets)

    test_returns = np.zeros((n_months, n_assets))
    for t in range(n_months):
        idio = rng.normal(0, 0.18 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_assets)
        test_returns[t, :] = true_alpha + b_mkt * mkt[t] + b_smb * smb[t] + b_hml * hml[t] + idio

    # Helper: GRS test and Max-Sharpe computation
    def evaluate_model(f_mat: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
        t_obs = len(test_returns)
        k_factors = f_mat.shape[1]

        # 1. Barillas-Shanken Max Squared Sharpe ratio (annualized)
        mu_f = np.mean(f_mat, axis=0)
        sigma_f = np.cov(f_mat, rowvar=False, ddof=1)
        if k_factors == 1:
            sigma_f_inv = 1.0 / sigma_f
            sh2_ann = float((mu_f[0] ** 2) * sigma_f_inv * 12.0)
            sh2_monthly = float((mu_f[0] ** 2) * sigma_f_inv)
        else:
            sigma_f_inv = np.linalg.inv(sigma_f)
            sh2_ann = float((mu_f @ sigma_f_inv @ mu_f) * 12.0)
            sh2_monthly = float(mu_f @ sigma_f_inv @ mu_f)

        # 2. Time-series regressions of 25 assets on factors
        x_des = np.column_stack([np.ones(t_obs), f_mat])
        betas = np.linalg.lstsq(x_des, test_returns, rcond=None)[0]
        alphas = betas[0, :]
        resids = test_returns - x_des @ betas

        # Residual covariance matrix (unbiased: T - K - 1)
        sigma_eps = (resids.T @ resids) / (t_obs - k_factors - 1)
        sigma_eps_inv = np.linalg.inv(sigma_eps)

        # GRS F-statistic
        # F = (T - N - K)/N * (1 + f_bar' * Sigma_f^-1 * f_bar)^-1 * alpha' * Sigma_eps^-1 * alpha
        grs_scale = (t_obs - n_assets - k_factors) / n_assets
        alpha_quad = alphas @ sigma_eps_inv @ alphas
        grs_stat = float(grs_scale * (1.0 / (1.0 + sh2_monthly)) * alpha_quad)
        p_val = float(1.0 - scipy.stats.f.cdf(grs_stat, n_assets, t_obs - n_assets - k_factors))

        return sh2_ann, grs_stat, p_val

    results = {}
    for name, f_mat in models.items():
        sh2, grs, p = evaluate_model(f_mat)
        results[f"sh2_{name}"] = round(sh2, 4)
        results[f"grs_{name}"] = round(grs, 2)
        results[f"p_{name}"] = round(p, 4)

    return results


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F2-L06-1 | Factor Model Horse Race (GRS & Barillas-Shanken)")
    print("----------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = run_factor_model_comparison(seed=42)
    models = ["CAPM", "FF3", "Carhart", "FF5", "q-factor"]
    print(f"{'Model':<10} | {'Sh^2(f) (Ann)':<14} | {'GRS Stat':<10} | {'p-value':<8}")
    print("-" * 50)
    for m in models:
        print(f"{m:<10} | {res[f'sh2_{m}']:<14.4f} | {res[f'grs_{m}']:<10.2f} | {res[f'p_{m}']:<8.4f}")
    print("-" * 50)
    print("Model ranking by max squared Sharpe ratio expands monotonically with unspanned factors.")


main()

3The Factor Zoo and Multiple Testing Hurdles

Over the past four decades, academic literature has documented over 400 equity anomalies and factor candidates. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) demonstrated that applying standard single-hypothesis testing rules (\(t > 2.0\), corresponding to a two-sided \(5\%\) significance level) to hundreds of mined anomalies produces an epidemic of false discoveries.

Theorem 6.4 · The Multiple Testing Hurdle
Suppose \(M\) independent candidate factors are tested under the global null hypothesis that no true predictability exists. The probability of discovering at least one false anomaly at single-test significance level \(\alpha = 0.05\) is: \[\begin{aligned} \alpha_{\text{family}} &= 1 - (1 - \alpha)^M. \end{aligned}\] For \(M = 400\) candidate factors, \(\alpha_{\text{family}} = 1 - (0.95)^{400} \approx 1 - 1.8 \times 10^{-9} \approx 99.9999998\%\). To control the Family-Wise Error Rate (\(\text{FWER}\)) at 5%, the Bonferroni critical threshold is \(\alpha / M = 0.05 / 400 = 0.000125\), requiring a critical \(t\)-statistic hurdle of: \[\begin{aligned} t_{\text{critical}} &= \Phi^{-1}\left(1 - \frac{0.05}{2 \times 400}\right) \approx 3.84. \end{aligned}\] Under less conservative Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (\(\text{FDR}\)) controls, Harvey, Liu & Zhu recommend establishing a minimum hurdle of \(t \ge 3.0\) for any newly proposed asset pricing factor.

Where the Canon Breaks

While the maximum squared Sharpe ratio \(Sh^2(f) = \boldsymbol{\mu}_f^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_f\) is mathematically invariant for traded models, in finite samples it suffers from severe in-sample estimation optimism. Inverting a sample covariance matrix \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_f\) across \(K\) factors with modest sample length \(T\) causes sample tangency portfolio weights to aggressively exploit in-sample estimation noise, dramatically exaggerating the estimated squared Sharpe ratio. When models are tested out-of-sample or cross-validated, high-dimensional factor models frequently experience severe parameter degradation, producing worse out-of-sample root mean squared pricing errors (RMSPE) than parsimonious 3-factor models.

Misconceptions

  1. A factor model that achieves the lowest pricing errors on 25 Size-BM portfolios is the universally best pricing model.

    Testing on specific portfolios induces severe test-asset bias. A model containing HML will mechanically price 25 Size-BM portfolios well because its factors are formed from the same sort. Barillas & Shanken prove that traded factor models must be judged by their ability to span each other's factor portfolios and maximize tangency Sharpe ratios, not on arbitrary test assets.

  2. The GRS test is an asymptotic large-sample test that requires thousands of monthly observations to be valid.

    The GRS test is an exact finite-sample test. Under the assumption of multivariate normal regression errors, the GRS statistic follows an exact \(F(N, T - N - K)\) distribution for any sample size \(T > N + K\).

  3. A high average time-series regression \(R^2\) proves that a factor model successfully explains expected stock returns.

    Time-series \(R^2\) measures the percentage of common variance explained by factors, not the explanation of expected returns. A factor with zero expected return (such as an industry noise factor) can generate an \(R^2\) of 90% while providing zero explanation of the cross-section of expected returns.

Practice Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An empirical factor model is tested on \(N = 25\) portfolios using \(K = 5\) factors over \(T = 360\) monthly observations. What are the exact numerator and denominator degrees of freedom for the GRS \(F\)-test?

Solution

The numerator degrees of freedom is the number of test assets: \(df_1 = N = 25\). The denominator degrees of freedom is \(df_2 = T - N - K = 360 - 25 - 5 = 330\). The test statistic is evaluated against an \(F(25, 330)\) distribution.

If a factor model's monthly factor Sharpe ratio is \(Sh(f) = 0.20\) and the alpha quadratic form across 25 assets is \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} = 0.15\), compute the GRS \(F\)-statistic for \(T = 360, N = 25, K = 3\).

Solution

The monthly squared Sharpe ratio is \(Sh^2(f) = (0.20)^2 = 0.04\). The denominator degrees of freedom is \(T - N - K = 360 - 25 - 3 = 332\). The GRS scaling factor is \(\frac{T - N - K}{N} = \frac{332}{25} = 13.28\). The GRS statistic is \(GRS = 13.28 \times \frac{1}{1 + 0.04} \times 0.15 = 13.28 \times \frac{0.15}{1.04} \approx 13.28 \times 0.14423 \approx 1.915\).

Model A achieves an annualized maximum squared Sharpe ratio of \(Sh^2(f^{(A)}) = 0.55\), while Model B achieves \(Sh^2(f^{(B)}) = 0.42\). According to the Barillas & Shanken (2017) theorem, which model dominates in factor pricing?

Solution

Under the Barillas & Shanken theorem, Model A strictly dominates Model B because \(Sh^2(f^{(A)}) = 0.55 > 0.42 = Sh^2(f^{(B)})\). Model A's factor portfolio spans a more efficient tangency portfolio and will generate lower aggregate pricing errors on any common test set spanning both models.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that if a set of \(K\) factors spans the mean-variance tangency portfolio of all available assets, every test asset \(i\) must have an expected pricing error \(\alpha_i = 0\).

Solution

If factors \(f\) span the tangency portfolio, the tangency portfolio can be written as \(R_p = w^\top f\). In mean-variance mathematics, the tangency portfolio is the unique portfolio satisfying \(\E[R_i] - R_f = \beta_{i, p} (\E[R_p] - R_f)\) for every asset \(i\). Since \(R_p\) is a linear combination of factors \(f\), projecting \(R_i\) on \(f\) completely absorbs the expected return: \(\E[R_i] - R_f = \boldsymbol{\beta}_i^\top \E[f]\). Consequently, the regression intercept is identically \(\alpha_i = \E[R_i - R_f] - \boldsymbol{\beta}_i^\top \E[f] = 0\).

Prove that the GRS statistic is strictly invariant to any full-rank linear transformation of the test assets (i.e. replacing test asset returns \(R_t\) with \(Z_t = \mathbf{A} R_t\) for any invertible \(N \times N\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\)).

Solution

Under the linear transformation \(Z_t = \mathbf{A} R_t\), the OLS intercepts transform as \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_Z = \mathbf{A} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\), and the residual covariance matrix transforms as \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\epsilon, Z} = \mathbf{A} \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon \mathbf{A}^\top\). The quadratic form in the GRS statistic is: \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_Z^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\epsilon, Z}^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_Z = (\mathbf{A} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^\top (\mathbf{A} \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon \mathbf{A}^\top)^{-1} (\mathbf{A} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}) = \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^\top \mathbf{A}^\top (\mathbf{A}^\top)^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon^{-1} \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{A} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_\epsilon^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\). Because the quadratic form is unchanged, the GRS statistic is invariant to \(\mathbf{A}\).

Prove that the Bonferroni correction controls the Family-Wise Error Rate at level \(\alpha\) across \(M\) dependent or independent hypothesis tests.

Solution

Let \(E_m\) denote the event that null hypothesis \(m\) is falsely rejected under single-test threshold \(\alpha / M\). The Family-Wise Error Rate is \(\Pr(\bigcup_{m=1}^M E_m)\). By Boole's inequality (union bound): \(\Pr(\bigcup_{m=1}^M E_m) \le \sum_{m=1}^M \Pr(E_m) = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{\alpha}{M} = M \times \frac{\alpha}{M} = \alpha\). Because the union bound holds for arbitrary correlation structures, the Bonferroni correction guarantees FWER control at level \(\alpha\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

In empirical tests of the CAPM on 25 Fama–French Size-BM portfolios, why does the GRS test overwhelmingly reject the CAPM with p-values below 0.001?

Solution

The CAPM fails because small-growth stocks deliver large negative CAPM alphas (\(\alpha < -0.4\%\) monthly) while small-value stocks deliver large positive CAPM alphas (\(\alpha > +0.4\%\) monthly). These 25 portfolios exhibit strong residual cross-correlations, so the inverted residual covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\epsilon^{-1}\) severely penalizes these systematic pricing errors, producing a massive GRS \(F\)-statistic that decisively rejects the null.

How does adding operating profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA) in the Fama–French 5-factor model improve the GRS test statistic relative to the 3-factor model on Size-BM-OP-Inv test grids?

Solution

The FF3 model produces severe pricing errors on portfolios sorted by profitability and investment (e.g. unprofitable high-investment firms have large negative FF3 alphas). In FF5, RMW and CMA directly absorb these return spreads, shrinking the alpha vector \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\) and reducing the GRS statistic, although formal statistical rejection of exact pricing often remains due to large sample sizes.

Why does out-of-sample cross-validation frequently favor more parsimonious factor models (like Carhart 4 or FF3) over models with 6 to 10 factors?

Solution

In-sample factor regressions overfit sample-specific noise. When a model includes 8 or 10 factors, estimation error in factor risk premia and covariances compounds out-of-sample. Out-of-sample RMSPE penalizes factor parameter variance, favoring parsimonious models whose in-sample explanatory power translates into reliable out-of-sample forecasts.

Tier 4 · Research

Explain the Bayesian factor model comparison methodology developed by Chib, Zeng & Zhao (2020) and how it handles factor model selection under diffuse priors.

Solution

Chib, Zeng & Zhao compute marginal likelihoods for candidate factor models by integrating the likelihood function over parameter priors. By comparing Bayes factors and posterior model probabilities across models, the methodology penalizes model complexity (Occam's razor) and accounts for parameter estimation uncertainty without relying on asymptotic distributions or arbitrary test-asset choices.

How does machine-learning shrinkage (e.g. Kozak, Nagel & Santosh 2020) discipline factor model selection across hundreds of candidate anomaly characteristics?

Solution

Kozak, Nagel & Santosh apply ridge (L2) and lasso (L1) penalties to the Stochastic Discount Factor coefficient vector \(b\) across hundreds of characteristic-managed portfolios. They prove that an SDF with a small number of principal components captures nearly all Sharpe ratio expansion, showing that the factor zoo can be summarized by a low-dimensional factor representation.

Design an empirical protocol to evaluate whether a newly proposed anomaly factor survives the publication hurdle according to Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) standards.

Solution

The protocol requires: (1) Out-of-sample holdout validation on data not used during discovery; (2) Raising the minimum spanning regression \(t\)-statistic to \(t \ge 3.0\) relative to FF5 + WML; (3) Evaluating transaction-cost drag using realistic market impact and borrow fee models; (4) Applying Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate corrections across all tested variations; and (5) Confirming the premium across independent international equity markets.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What does the Gibbons–Ross–Shanken (1989) GRS statistic test in time-series factor models?

    Options

    The GRS test is an exact finite-sample F-test evaluating the null hypothesis \(H_0: \alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \dots = \alpha_N = 0\).

  2. What is the central finding of the Barillas & Shanken (2017) theorem regarding traded factor model comparison?

    Options

    Barillas & Shanken prove test-asset invariance: traded factor models are ranked solely by their factor tangency portfolio's maximum squared Sharpe ratio.

  3. In a GRS test with \(T = 360\) months, \(N = 25\) test portfolios, and \(K = 5\) factors, what is the denominator degrees of freedom \(T - N - K\)?

    \(T - N - K = 360 - 25 - 5 = 330\).

  4. How does the GRS statistic relate geometrically to the mean-variance tangency portfolio?

    Options

    The GRS statistic is proportional to (Sh^2(f, R) - Sh^2(f)) / (1 + Sh^2(f)), measuring Sharpe ratio expansion.

  5. Why does Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) advocate raising the factor discovery threshold from \(t > 2.0\) to \(t \ge 3.0\)?

    Options

    Multiple testing across hundreds of mined factors inflates family-wise error rates; controlling false discoveries requires raising the significance threshold to t >= 3.0.

  6. What is the approximate two-sided critical \(t\)-statistic threshold under a Bonferroni correction for \(M = 400\) independent hypothesis tests at family error rate \(\alpha = 0.05\)?

    \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - 0.05 / 800) = \Phi^{-1}(0.9999375) \approx 3.84\).

  7. Why can a model with a high time-series regression \(R^2\) fail as an asset pricing model?

    Options

    A factor can explain high variance (high R^2) while having a zero risk premium and leaving large unexplained pricing alphas.

  8. What finite-sample problem plagues the maximum squared Sharpe ratio metric \(Sh^2(f) = \boldsymbol{\mu}_f^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_f\) when the factor dimension \(K\) is large?

    Options

    Sample covariance inversion exploits estimation noise, leading to substantial in-sample upward bias in tangency Sharpe ratios.
