Size & Value: Economic Rationale & Portfolio Construction

The discovery of the size effect and value premium fundamentally dismantled the empirical standing of the single-factor CAPM. Understanding how market equity and book-to-market multiples relate to discount rates, mastering the canonical Fama–French \(2 \times 3\) sorting protocol, and resolving the debate between systematic distress risk and behavioral overreaction are essential foundations for all subsequent empirical factor pricing and quantitative portfolio engineering.

1The Size Effect: Discovery and Mechanism

In the earliest systematic challenge to the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), Banz (1981) demonstrated that common stocks of small market capitalization firms earned substantially higher risk-adjusted returns than large firms over the 1936–1975 sample period.

Definition 1.1 · Size Effect
The size effect is the empirical cross-sectional regularity wherein assets with lower total market equity (\(ME = |P| \times \text{SHROUT}\)) generate average returns exceeding the predictions of the Sharpe–Lintner CAPM, producing a statistically significant positive cross-sectional intercept (\(\alpha > 0\)) when sorted into capitalization deciles.

Banz estimated the generalized empirical regression: \[\begin{aligned} R_{i,t} - R_{f,t} &= \gamma_{0,t} + \gamma_{1,t} \beta_i + \gamma_{2,t} \left(\frac{ME_i - \overline{ME}}{\sigma(ME)}\right) + \eta_{i,t}, \end{aligned}\] and found \(\hat{\gamma}_{2} < 0\) with a \(t\)-statistic exceeding \(-3.0\), demonstrating that market equity contains explanatory power for expected returns orthogonal to market beta.

Theorem 1.1 · Berk's Identity: Market Value as a Discount Rate Mirror
Let \(C_{i,t+\tau}\) denote the stochastic cash flows generated by asset \(i\), and let \(r_i\) be its expected rate of return (discount rate). The market value of equity satisfies: \[\begin{aligned} ME_{i,t} &= \sum_{\tau=1}^\infty \frac{\E_t[C_{i,t+\tau}]}{(1 + r_i)^\tau}. \end{aligned}\] Holding expected future cash flows \(\{\E_t[C_{i,t+\tau}]\}\) constant, market capitalization \(ME_{i,t}\) is a strictly monotonically decreasing function of the discount rate \(r_i\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial ME_{i,t}}{\partial r_i} < 0. \end{aligned}\] Consequently, any cross-sectional variation in true expected returns not perfectly captured by an empirical asset pricing model will appear mechanically correlated with firm size.

Proof
Differentiating the discounted present value with respect to the discount rate \(r_i\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial ME_{i,t}}{\partial r_i} &= \sum_{\tau=1}^\infty \frac{-\tau \E_t[C_{i,t+\tau}]}{(1 + r_i)^{\tau + 1}}. \end{aligned}\] Because corporate operating assets generate positive aggregate expected cash flows \(\E_t[C_{i,t+\tau}] > 0\) and \(r_i > -1\), every term in the summation is strictly negative. Therefore, \(\frac{\partial ME_{i,t}}{\partial r_i} < 0\). If an econometrician models expected returns with an incomplete proxy \(\hat{r}_i\), the omitted risk premium component \(\epsilon_i = r_i - \hat{r}_i\) depresses \(ME_i\), inducing a mechanical negative correlation between firm market capitalization and realized average returns.

2Book-to-Market Equity and the Value Premium

While the size anomaly established that market capitalization contains information about expected returns, Fama & French (1992) showed that the ratio of book equity to market equity (\(BE/ME\)) exhibits an even stronger cross-sectional relationship with average stock returns.

Definition 1.2 · Value Premium (HML)
The value premium (HML) (High Minus Low) is the expected return difference between high book-to-market equities (value firms, whose accounting book equity is large relative to market capitalization) and low book-to-market equities (growth firms, whose market equity reflects substantial anticipated growth options).

In their seminal methodology, Fama & French (1993) constructed benchmark factor portfolios using independent \(2 \times 3\) bivariate sorts on Size (\(ME\)) and Book-to-Market (\(BE/ME\)) to isolate size and value premia while controlling for their interaction.

Remark 1.1 · The Fama–French 2x3 Portfolio Formation Protocol

At the end of June of each calendar year \(t\):

  • Size Breakpoint: The median NYSE market equity (\(ME\)) at the close of June divides stocks into two size groups: Small (\(S\)) and Big (\(B\)).
  • Valuation Breakpoints: The 30th and 70th percentiles of NYSE \(BE/ME\) (using December \(t-1\) book and market equity) divide stocks into three valuation groups: Growth (\(L\), bottom 30%), Neutral (\(M\), middle 40%), and Value (\(H\), top 30%).
  • Six Portfolios: The intersection of the two sorts produces six value-weighted portfolios: \(S/L\), \(S/M\), \(S/H\), \(B/L\), \(B/M\), and \(B/H\).

The Small-Minus-Big (\(SMB\)) and High-Minus-Low (\(HML\)) factor returns are defined as: \[\begin{aligned} SMB_t &= \frac{1}{3}\left(R_{S/L, t} + R_{S/M, t} + R_{S/H, t}\right) - \frac{1}{3}\left(R_{B/L, t} + R_{B/M, t} + R_{B/H, t}\right), \\ HML_t &= \frac{1}{2}\left(R_{S/H, t} + R_{B/H, t}\right) - \frac{1}{2}\left(R_{S/L, t} + R_{B/L, t}\right). \end{aligned}\]

Lab 1 · Size and Value Portfolio Sorts (Fama–French 1993)

Simulating 2x3 size-value sorts across 300 equities over 120 months will reproduce the canonical Fama-French SMB and HML factor premia and verify their empirical return distribution.

"""Lab F2-L01-1: Size and Value Portfolio Sorts and Factor Construction.

Simulates monthly equity returns and fundamental characteristics (Market Equity ME
and Book-to-Market BE/ME) to construct canonical Fama-French (1993) 2x3 size
and value portfolios, computing SMB, HML, and empirical cross-sectional diagnostics.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_size_and_value(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float | int]:
    """Simulate 2x3 size-value sorting and factor return generation."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_stocks = 300
    n_months = 120

    # Simulate market equity (log-normal, size variation across 3 orders of magnitude)
    log_me = rng.normal(loc=6.0, scale=1.5, size=n_stocks)
    me = np.exp(log_me)

    # Simulate book-to-market (correlated negatively with size, log-normal)
    log_bm = -0.3 * (log_me - 6.0) + rng.normal(loc=-0.5, scale=0.6, size=n_stocks)
    bm = np.exp(log_bm)

    # NYSE-like median breakpoint for size (50th percentile)
    # 30th and 70th percentile breakpoints for book-to-market
    size_median = float(np.median(me))
    bm_p30 = float(np.percentile(bm, 30))
    bm_p70 = float(np.percentile(bm, 70))

    # Portfolio masks
    small = me <= size_median
    big = me > size_median

    value = bm >= bm_p70
    neutral = (bm >= bm_p30) & (bm < bm_p70)
    growth = bm < bm_p30

    # True factor underlying structure (monthly annualized excess returns)
    # Market factor: 6% mean, 16% vol
    # Size factor (SMB): 2.5% mean, 10% vol
    # Value factor (HML): 4.0% mean, 12% vol
    mkt_ret = rng.normal(0.06 / 12, 0.16 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    smb_true = rng.normal(0.025 / 12, 0.10 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    hml_true = rng.normal(0.040 / 12, 0.12 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)

    # Stock betas to factors
    beta_mkt = 0.8 + 0.4 * rng.uniform(0, 1, size=n_stocks)
    beta_smb = np.where(
        small,
        0.8 + 0.2 * rng.normal(0, 0.2, size=n_stocks),
        -0.3 + 0.2 * rng.normal(0, 0.2, size=n_stocks),
    )
    beta_hml = np.where(value, 0.7, np.where(growth, -0.6, 0.05)) + rng.normal(0, 0.15, size=n_stocks)

    # Generate monthly stock returns (T x N)
    stock_returns = np.zeros((n_months, n_stocks))
    for t in range(n_months):
        idio = rng.normal(0, 0.25 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_stocks)
        stock_returns[t, :] = beta_mkt * mkt_ret[t] + beta_smb * smb_true[t] + beta_hml * hml_true[t] + idio

    # Value-weighted 2x3 portfolio returns
    # Portfolios: S/V, S/N, S/G, B/V, B/N, B/G
    def calc_vw_ret(mask: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        w = me[mask] / np.sum(me[mask])
        return np.asarray(stock_returns[:, mask] @ w)

    r_sv = calc_vw_ret(small & value)
    r_sn = calc_vw_ret(small & neutral)
    r_sg = calc_vw_ret(small & growth)
    r_bv = calc_vw_ret(big & value)
    r_bn = calc_vw_ret(big & neutral)
    r_bg = calc_vw_ret(big & growth)

    # Fama-French (1993) SMB and HML formulas:
    # SMB = 1/3 (S/V + S/N + S/G) - 1/3 (B/V + B/N + B/G)
    # HML = 1/2 (S/V + B/V) - 1/2 (S/G + B/G)
    smb = (r_sv + r_sn + r_sg) / 3.0 - (r_bv + r_bn + r_bg) / 3.0
    hml = (r_sv + r_bv) / 2.0 - (r_sg + r_bg) / 2.0

    mean_smb_ann = float(np.mean(smb) * 12 * 100)
    std_smb_ann = float(np.std(smb, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12) * 100)
    t_smb = float(np.mean(smb) / (np.std(smb, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_months)))

    mean_hml_ann = float(np.mean(hml) * 12 * 100)
    std_hml_ann = float(np.std(hml, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12) * 100)
    t_hml = float(np.mean(hml) / (np.std(hml, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_months)))

    corr_smb_hml = float(np.corrcoef(smb, hml)[0, 1])

    return {
        "n_stocks": n_stocks,
        "n_months": n_months,
        "mean_smb_ann": round(mean_smb_ann, 2),
        "std_smb_ann": round(std_smb_ann, 2),
        "t_smb": round(t_smb, 2),
        "mean_hml_ann": round(mean_hml_ann, 2),
        "std_hml_ann": round(std_hml_ann, 2),
        "t_hml": round(t_hml, 2),
        "corr_smb_hml": round(corr_smb_hml, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F2-L01-1 | Size and Value Portfolio Sorts (Fama-French 1993)")
    print("----------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = simulate_size_and_value(seed=42)
    print(f"Sample size: {res['n_stocks']} equities over {res['n_months']} months")
    print(
        f"SMB Annualized Premium:   {res['mean_smb_ann']:+.2f}% (vol {res['std_smb_ann']:.2f}%, t = {res['t_smb']:.2f})"
    )
    print(
        f"HML Annualized Premium:   {res['mean_hml_ann']:+.2f}% (vol {res['std_hml_ann']:.2f}%, t = {res['t_hml']:.2f})"
    )
    print(f"SMB-HML Correlation:      {res['corr_smb_hml']:+.4f}")


main()

3Economic Interpretation: Distress Risk versus Expectational Errors

The academic literature presents two competing paradigms explaining the value premium:

  • Systematic Distress Risk (Fama & French 1993, 1996): High \(BE/ME\) firms are persistently distressed operations characterized by low earnings, high debt leverage, and elevated bankruptcy hazard. Value firms suffer disproportionately during economic downturns and credit crunches because their capital is inflexible and irreversible. The value premium represents rational compensation for systematic distress risk.
  • Expectational Errors & Extrapolation: In contrast, Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny (1994) argue that the value premium reflects cognitive biases. Investors naively extrapolate past growth trends: they overprice glamorous growth companies with recent earnings expansion and underprice unglamorous value companies experiencing temporary operational difficulties. Subsequent earnings surprise reversals generate high returns for value stocks.

Where the Canon Breaks

The standalone size effect documented by Banz (1981) has largely disappeared in post-1980 US equity markets (McLean & Pontiff 2016). Once microcaps (stocks below the 20th percentile of NYSE market capitalization) and the intense January seasonal effect are excluded, the unconditional return spread between large-cap and small-cap stocks is statistically indistinguishable from zero (\(t < 1.0\)). Furthermore, the value premium suffered severe multi-year drawdowns between 2007 and 2020, challenging whether static book-to-market ratios accurately capture modern intellectual property and intangible corporate assets.

Misconceptions

  1. Misconception: The size effect is a linear premium across all market capitalization deciles, ensuring that mid-cap stocks consistently outperform mega-cap stocks.

    Correction: The historical size anomaly is highly non-linear and overwhelmingly concentrated in the smallest microcaps. For mid-cap and large-cap stocks, the size premium is negligible and statistically insignificant over multi-decade post-1980 samples.

  2. Misconception: Book-to-market equity measures the physical liquidation value of a corporation's tangible property.

    Correction: Book equity is an accounting construct (assets minus liabilities plus deferred taxes minus preferred stock) governed by historical cost accounting rules; it frequently diverges dramatically from replacement cost or liquidation value, particularly for firms with heavy intangible R&D investments.

  3. Misconception: The Fama–French HML factor is constructed using simple unweighted averages of high and low book-to-market stocks.

    Correction: HML is formed as the spread between value-weighted subportfolios: \(HML = \frac{1}{2}(S/V + B/V) - \frac{1}{2}(S/G + B/G)\). Value-weighting within each of the six cells is essential to prevent illiquid microcaps from dominating the factor's return.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A researcher constructs six Fama–French \(2 \times 3\) portfolios in month \(t\). The realized returns are: \(R_{S/L} = 1.2\%\), \(R_{S/M} = 1.8\%\), \(R_{S/H} = 2.6\%\), \(R_{B/L} = 0.8\%\), \(R_{B/M} = 1.1\%\), and \(R_{B/H} = 1.5\%\). Calculate the realized monthly returns of \(SMB_t\) and \(HML_t\).

Solution

Apply the Fama–French (1993) formulas: \[\begin{aligned} SMB_t &= \frac{1.2\% + 1.8\% + 2.6\%}{3} - \frac{0.8\% + 1.1\% + 1.5\%}{3} \\ &= 1.867\% - 1.133\% = 0.733\%. \\ HML_t &= \frac{2.6\% + 1.5\%}{2} - \frac{1.2\% + 0.8\%}{2} \\ &= 2.05\% - 1.00\% = 1.05\%. \end{aligned}\] Thus, \(SMB_t = +0.733\%\) and \(HML_t = +1.050\%\).

A firm has 50 million common shares outstanding trading at \(\$40.00\) per share at the close of December of year \(t-1\). In Compustat, its stockholders' equity is \(\$1.2\) billion, balance sheet deferred taxes are \(\$200\) million, and preferred stock carrying value is \(\$100\) million. Compute the Book-to-Market ratio used in June of year \(t\).

Solution

First, calculate Book Equity (\(BE\)): \[\begin{aligned} BE &= \text{Stockholders' Equity} + \text{Deferred Taxes} - \text{Preferred Stock} \\ &= \$1,200\text{M} + \$200\text{M} - \$100\text{M} = \$1,300\text{M}. \end{aligned}\] Next, calculate Market Equity (\(ME_{\text{Dec}}\)): \[\begin{aligned} ME_{\text{Dec}} &= 50\text{M shares} \times \$40.00 = \$2,000\text{M}. \end{aligned}\] The Book-to-Market ratio is: \[\begin{aligned} BE/ME &= \frac{\$1,300\text{M}}{\$2,000\text{M}} = 0.650. \end{aligned}\]

Under annual June rebalancing, if portfolio \(S/H\) contains two stocks with December \(t-1\) market caps of \(\$100\)M and \(\$300\)M, and June \(t\) market caps of \(\$120\)M and \(\$280\)M, what weights are used to form the portfolio return in July of year \(t\)?

Solution

Under Fama–French methodology, portfolio weights in month \(t\) are proportional to market equity at the formation date (June of year \(t\)): \[\begin{aligned} w_1 &= \frac{\$120\text{M}}{\$120\text{M} + \$280\text{M}} = \frac{120}{400} = 0.30 \quad (30\%), \\ w_2 &= \frac{\$280\text{M}}{\$400\text{M}} = 0.70 \quad (70\%). \end{aligned}\] December market equity is used only to calculate the \(BE/ME\) sorting ratio, while June market equity determines portfolio investment weights.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that the Fama–French \(SMB\) factor has an exact zero theoretical weighting on the High-Minus-Low valuation spread.

Solution

Expand \(SMB\) by grouping components by valuation level: \[\begin{aligned} SMB &= \frac{1}{3}\left(R_{S/L} - R_{B/L}\right) + \frac{1}{3}\left(R_{S/M} - R_{B/M}\right) + \frac{1}{3}\left(R_{S/H} - R_{B/H}\right). \end{aligned}\] The valuation premium within small stocks is \(R_{S/H} - R_{S/L}\), and within big stocks is \(R_{B/H} - R_{B/L}\). The weight assigned to Value (\(H\)) is \(+\frac{1}{3}\) for small and \(-\frac{1}{3}\) for big; the weight assigned to Growth (\(L\)) is \(+\frac{1}{3}\) for small and \(-\frac{1}{3}\) for big. The aggregate loading on the valuation dimension across all small stocks is \(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = 0\), and across big stocks is \(-\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = 0\). Thus, by construction, \(SMB\) is orthogonal in expectations to the book-to-market spread.

Let asset returns be generated by a two-factor model \(R_{i} = \beta_{i,1} f_1 + \beta_{i,2} f_2 + \epsilon_i\). Prove that omitting \(f_2\) from a single-factor regression on \(f_1\) biases the estimated alpha if and only if \(\beta_{i,2} \neq 0\) and \(\E[f_2] \neq 0\).

Solution

Consider the univariate regression of excess return on factor 1: \[\begin{aligned} R_{i} &= \alpha_{i,1} + \beta_{i,1}^* f_1 + u_i. \end{aligned}\] Taking expectations: \(\E[R_i] = \alpha_{i,1} + \beta_{i,1}^* \E[f_1]\). Under the true two-factor model: \(\E[R_i] = \beta_{i,1} \E[f_1] + \beta_{i,2} \E[f_2]\). Assuming \(\Cov(f_1, f_2) = 0\) for clarity, \(\beta_{i,1}^* = \beta_{i,1}\). Substituting: \[\begin{aligned} \alpha_{i,1} &= \E[R_i] - \beta_{i,1} \E[f_1] = \beta_{i,2} \E[f_2]. \end{aligned}\] Therefore, \(\alpha_{i,1} = 0\) if and only if \(\beta_{i,2} = 0\) or \(\E[f_2] = 0\). If factor 2 has a non-zero expected risk premium (\(\E[f_2] \neq 0\)) and the asset has non-zero exposure (\(\beta_{i,2} \neq 0\)), omitting factor 2 induces an apparent pricing anomaly (\(\alpha_{i,1} \neq 0\)).

Prove that Berk's identity holds under the Gordon Growth Model with constant growth \(g\) and discount rate \(r > g\).

Solution

Under the Gordon Growth Model, price is: \[\begin{aligned} P &= \frac{D_1}{r - g}. \end{aligned}\] Differentiating with respect to the expected return \(r\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial P}{\partial r} &= -\frac{D_1}{(r - g)^2}. \end{aligned}\] Since dividends \(D_1 > 0\) and \((r - g)^2 > 0\), the derivative \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial r} < 0\) strictly. Market capitalization \(ME = P \times \text{Shares}\) is therefore strictly decreasing in the discount rate \(r\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

An analyst sorts US equities into 10 deciles by market equity using pooled exchange breakpoints versus NYSE breakpoints. Explain why pooled breakpoints generate an apparent size premium driven by illiquid OTC and NASDAQ penny stocks.

Solution

NASDAQ and OTC contain thousands of tiny microcap stocks representing less than 2% of total market capitalization but over 60% of listed firm count. Using pooled breakpoints forces nearly all lower deciles (Deciles 1 through 4) to be composed entirely of microcaps afflicted by severe bid-ask bounce, zero trading volume, and high delisting attrition. Using NYSE breakpoints ensures that Decile 1 represents true small-cap operating firms rather than microstructure noise.

Between 2007 and 2020, the Fama–French HML factor delivered an annualized return of approximately \(-2.8\%\). What structural changes in modern corporate balance sheets contributed to this decade-long value drawdown?

Solution

Modern corporate capital formation shifted heavily from physical property, plant, and equipment (PP&E) to intangible investments (software, R&D, patents, customer networks, and brand equity). Under US GAAP, internally developed intangible investments are expensed immediately rather than capitalized onto balance sheets. Consequently, traditional book value severely underestimates true corporate equity for modern technology companies, misclassifying high-productivity firms as expensive growth stocks.

In empirical factor regressions, why do researchers use value-weighted rather than equal-weighted factor portfolios to test asset pricing models?

Solution

Equal-weighted portfolios allocate 90% of their capital to tiny microcaps that are impossible to trade at institutional scale due to extreme market impact and short-sale borrowing fees. Value-weighted portfolios represent the actual aggregate wealth held by investors in equilibrium, ensuring that empirical asset pricing tests evaluate economically feasible investment strategies.

Tier 4 · Research

Design an empirical test to distinguish whether the value premium reflects systematic distress risk (Fama–French) or behavioral mispricing (LSV). What state variable or conditional conditioning test would discriminate between them?

Solution

An econometrician can condition the value premium on aggregate macroeconomic distress states: credit spread widenings, consumption growth declines, and NBER recession dates. If value is systematic distress risk, high \(BE/ME\) stocks must have higher cash flow covariances with aggregate distress and perform worst during severe market drawdowns. If value is behavioral overreaction, value stock earnings announcements will exhibit positive abnormal announcement-day returns regardless of the macroeconomic state.

Evaluate the impact of post-publication decay (McLean & Pontiff 2016) on the size effect. Why did academic publication lead to a 58% average decay in anomaly returns?

Solution

Publication publicizes previously undiscovered or unexploited price patterns. Quantitative arbitrageurs, hedge funds, and factor ETFs deploy institutional capital to exploit the published anomaly, bidding up undervalued assets and shorting overvalued assets until the return spread shrinks to the margin of transaction costs and short-sale constraints.

How does the rise of share repurchases over cash dividends since 1980 affect the timing and measurement of book equity in the Fama–French value premium?

Solution

Share repurchases reduce cash and treasury stock, directly depleting accounting book equity while leaving market equity relatively intact. Extensive share repurchases can push book equity negative for highly profitable, mature corporations (e.g. McDonald's, Home Depot), causing standard Fama–French filters (which exclude negative book equity stocks) to discard profitable large-cap firms from the value universe.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What did Banz (1981) discover regarding the relationship between market equity and stock returns?

    Options

    Banz (1981) documented the size anomaly, showing that small market equity stocks produced positive risk-adjusted returns unexplained by the CAPM.

  2. How does Berk (1995) explain the universal empirical emergence of size effects in financial data?

    Options

    Berk showed that price is an inverse mirror of the discount rate: holding cash flows fixed, high expected return firms have lower market equity.

  3. In the Fama–French (1993) \(2 \times 3\) sorting protocol, what percentile cutoffs are used for Book-to-Market equity?

    Options

    Fama and French use the 30th and 70th percentiles of NYSE book-to-market ratios to divide stocks into Growth (bottom 30%), Neutral (middle 40%), and Value (top 30%).

  4. Which argument forms the foundation of Fama and French's risk-based defense of the value premium?

    Options

    Fama and French argue that high book-to-market reflects systematic vulnerability to distress, prolonged earnings depressions, and credit tightening.

  5. If the average return of the small-value portfolio is \(3.0\%\), big-value is \(2.0\%\), small-growth is \(1.0\%\), and big-growth is \(0.5\%\), what is the Fama–French HML return in percent?

    \(HML = \frac{1}{2}(3.0 + 2.0) - \frac{1}{2}(1.0 + 0.5) = 2.5 - 0.75 = 1.75 - 0.25 = 1.50\%\).

  6. Why does McLean & Pontiff (2016) find that asset pricing anomalies decay post-publication?

    Options

    Academic publication diffuses knowledge to arbitrageurs whose collective trading diminishes cross-sectional predictability by ~58%.

  7. Why did the traditional book-to-market value factor experience prolonged underperformance during the 2010s?

    Options

    Intangible investments (R&D, software, brand capital) are expensed under GAAP rather than capitalized, impairing book value as a measure of corporate wealth.

  8. According to McLean & Pontiff (2016), by what percentage does the average anomaly return decay following academic publication?

    McLean and Pontiff (2016) find an average post-publication return decay of approximately 58%.
