Investment & Profitability: Production-Based Asset Pricing

For two decades, empirical factor models were dominated by size and book-to-market. The realization that corporate investment and operating profitability provide direct structural anchors for discount rates revolutionized asset pricing. Grounded in the dividend discount identity and production-based \(q\)-theory, high profitability commands high expected returns while aggressive capital expenditures signal low hurdle rates. Mastering profitability and investment factors clarifies why value suffered prolonged drawdowns, exposes the power of quality-growth hedging, and establishes the modern five-factor benchmark.

1Theoretical Foundations: The Valuation Identity

The theoretical motivation for profitability and investment factors originates directly in the clean surplus accounting relation and the dividend discount model, formalized by Fama & French (2015).

Theorem 3.1 · The Dividend Discount Valuation Identity
Let \(M_t\) denote the market value of equity at time \(t\), \(B_t\) denote book equity, \(Y_{t+\tau}\) denote total operating equity earnings in period \(t+\tau\), and \(dB_{t+\tau} = B_{t+\tau} - B_{t+\tau-1}\) denote net additions to book equity (corporate investment). By clean surplus accounting, dividends satisfy \(D_{t+\tau} = Y_{t+\tau} - dB_{t+\tau}\). Dividing both sides of the present value formula by current book equity \(B_t\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{M_t}{B_t} &= \sum_{\tau=1}^\infty \frac{\E_t[Y_{t+\tau} - dB_{t+\tau}]}{(1 + r)^\tau B_t}, \end{aligned}\] where \(r\) is the internal rate of return (expected stock return). From this algebraic identity, holding all other variables constant:

  1. The Value Relation: Holding future profitability \(\{Y_{t+\tau}\}\) and investment \(\{dB_{t+\tau}\}\) fixed, a lower market-to-book ratio \(M_t/B_t\) (higher book-to-market \(B_t/M_t\)) implies a higher expected return \(r\).
  2. The Profitability Relation: Holding valuation \(M_t/B_t\) and investment \(\{dB_{t+\tau}\}\) fixed, higher expected future profitability \(\{Y_{t+\tau}\}\) implies a higher expected return \(r\).
  3. The Investment Relation: Holding valuation \(M_t/B_t\) and profitability \(\{Y_{t+\tau}\}\) fixed, higher expected future investment \(\{dB_{t+\tau}\}\) implies a lower expected return \(r\).

Proof
Define the discounted present value function \(g(r) \equiv \sum_{\tau=1}^\infty \frac{\E_t[Y_{t+\tau} - dB_{t+\tau}]}{(1 + r)^\tau B_t} - \frac{M_t}{B_t} = 0\). The partial derivative with respect to the discount rate \(r\) is: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial g}{\partial r} &= -\sum_{\tau=1}^\infty \frac{\tau \E_t[Y_{t+\tau} - dB_{t+\tau}]}{(1 + r)^{\tau + 1} B_t} < 0, \end{aligned}\] since aggregate corporate free cash flows are strictly positive in expectation. By the Implicit Function Theorem: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial r}{\partial Y_{t+\tau}} &= -\frac{\partial g / \partial Y_{t+\tau}}{\partial g / \partial r} = -\frac{(1+r)^{-\tau} / B_t}{\partial g / \partial r} > 0, \\ \frac{\partial r}{\partial dB_{t+\tau}} &= -\frac{\partial g / \partial dB_{t+\tau}}{\partial g / \partial r} = -\frac{-(1+r)^{-\tau} / B_t}{\partial g / \partial r} < 0, \\ \frac{\partial r}{\partial (M_t / B_t)} &= -\frac{\partial g / \partial (M_t / B_t)}{\partial g / \partial r} = -\frac{-1}{\partial g / \partial r} < 0. \end{aligned}\] Thus expected return \(r\) increases monotonically with future profitability and decreases monotonically with valuation multiples and investment.

2Profitability: Gross Profitability vs. Operating Profitability

Historically, researchers examined bottom-line accounting earnings (such as earnings-to-price or return on equity) but found mixed empirical power. Novy-Marx (2013) established that moving higher up the income statement produces a dramatically more powerful predictor of average returns.

Definition 3.1 · Profitability Factor
A profitability factor is a zero-investment portfolio that goes long equities with high fundamental operating productivity and short equities with low fundamental operating productivity.

  • Gross Profitability (Novy-Marx 2013): Defined as gross profit scaled by total book assets: \[\begin{aligned} GP/A_i &= \frac{\text{REVT}_i - \text{COGS}_i}{\text{AT}_i}. \end{aligned}\]
  • Operating Profitability RMW (Fama & French 2015): Defined as revenue minus cost of goods sold, selling, general, and administrative expenses, and interest expense, scaled by book equity: \[\begin{aligned} OP_i &= \frac{\text{REVT}_i - \text{COGS}_i - \text{XSGA}_i - \text{XINT}_i}{BE_i}. \end{aligned}\] The Robust Minus Weak (\(RMW\)) factor return is formed via \(2 \times 3\) sorts: \[\begin{aligned} RMW_t &= \frac{1}{2}\left(R_{S/\text{Robust}, t} + R_{B/\text{Robust}, t}\right) - \frac{1}{2}\left(R_{S/\text{Weak}, t} + R_{B/\text{Weak}, t}\right). \end{aligned}\]

Novy-Marx demonstrated that gross profitability generates a premium comparable in magnitude to the value premium (\(\sim 0.33\%\) per month, \(t > 3.0\)), yet is negatively correlated with value (\(\rho \approx -0.30\)). Highly profitable firms tend to be expensive growth companies, while deeply discounted value firms tend to suffer from depressed operational productivity. Combining value and profitability creates a potent diversification barbell.

3The Investment Factor: Conservative Minus Aggressive

A parallel stream in corporate finance and production economics demonstrates that aggressive corporate investment foretells lower subsequent equity returns (Fama & French 2015; Hou, Xue & Zhang 2015).

Definition 3.2 · Investment Factor
An investment factor is a zero-investment portfolio going long firms with conservative asset growth and short firms with aggressive capital expansion. In Fama & French (2015), investment is measured as the annual percentage change in total assets: \[\begin{aligned} \text{Inv}_{i, t} &= \frac{\text{AT}_{i, t-1} - \text{AT}_{i, t-2}}{\text{AT}_{i, t-2}}. \end{aligned}\] Using independent \(2 \times 3\) sorts on Size (NYSE median) and Investment (30th and 70th percentiles), the Conservative Minus Aggressive (\(CMA\)) factor is constructed as: \[\begin{aligned} CMA_t &= \frac{1}{2}\left(R_{S/\text{Conservative}, t} + R_{B/\text{Conservative}, t}\right) - \frac{1}{2}\left(R_{S/\text{Aggressive}, t} + R_{B/\text{Aggressive}, t}\right). \end{aligned}\]

Theorem 3.2 · Production-Based Asset Pricing and Investment Hurdles
In a dynamic firm optimizing capital accumulation under convex installation costs \(c(I/K)\), the first-order condition for optimal corporate investment equates the marginal cost of investing to marginal \(q\) (the shadow value of installed capital): \[\begin{aligned} 1 + c'\left(\frac{I_t}{K_t}\right) &= q_t = \E_t\left[ \sum_{\tau=1}^\infty M_{t,\tau} \Pi'_{t+\tau} \right], \end{aligned}\] where \(M_{t,\tau}\) is the stochastic discount factor and \(\Pi'\) is the marginal productivity of capital. Holding marginal productivity \(\Pi'\) constant, high investment \(I_t/K_t\) requires a high marginal \(q_t\), which occurs when the discount rate \(r_t\) applied by financiers is low. Therefore, aggressive corporate investment directly reveals that the firm faces a low cost of capital (low expected stock return).

Lab 1 · Operating Profitability (RMW) and Investment (CMA) Factor Construction

Simulating 120 months across 300 equities with realistic cross-sectional distributions of market equity, operating profitability, and asset growth will construct canonical RMW and CMA factors, verifying their statistical significance and factor cross-correlations.

"""Lab F2-L03-1: Operating Profitability (RMW) and Investment (CMA) Factor Construction.

Simulates corporate fundamentals (Market Equity ME, Operating Profitability OP,
and Asset Growth Investment Inv) to construct canonical Fama-French (2015)
RMW and CMA factors via 2x3 sorts, verifying the negative correlation between
investment and profitability and testing their diversification benefits with HML.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_profitability_and_investment(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float | int]:
    """Simulate 2x3 sorts for RMW and CMA and measure factor cross-correlations."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_stocks = 300
    n_months = 120

    # Cross-sectional characteristics
    log_me = rng.normal(loc=6.0, scale=1.4, size=n_stocks)
    me = np.exp(log_me)

    # Book-to-market
    log_bm = -0.25 * (log_me - 6.0) + rng.normal(loc=-0.4, scale=0.5, size=n_stocks)
    bm = np.exp(log_bm)

    # Operating profitability: negatively correlated with distressed high B/M stocks
    op = -0.15 * log_bm + rng.normal(loc=0.14, scale=0.10, size=n_stocks)

    # Investment (Asset Growth): high-growth firms invest aggressively (low B/M, high investment)
    inv = -0.20 * log_bm + rng.normal(loc=0.08, scale=0.12, size=n_stocks)

    # Breakpoints (median for size, 30/70 for OP and Inv)
    size_median = float(np.median(me))
    small = me <= size_median
    big = me > size_median

    op_p30 = float(np.percentile(op, 30))
    op_p70 = float(np.percentile(op, 70))
    robust = op >= op_p70
    weak = op < op_p30
    (op >= op_p30) & (op < op_p70)

    inv_p30 = float(np.percentile(inv, 30))
    inv_p70 = float(np.percentile(inv, 70))
    conservative = inv < inv_p30
    aggressive = inv >= inv_p70
    (inv >= inv_p30) & (inv < inv_p70)

    # Monthly factor returns simulation
    rmw_true = rng.normal(0.035 / 12, 0.08 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    cma_true = rng.normal(0.030 / 12, 0.07 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    hml_true = rng.normal(0.040 / 12, 0.11 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)

    # Betas
    beta_op = np.where(robust, 0.7, np.where(weak, -0.7, 0.0)) + rng.normal(0, 0.1, size=n_stocks)
    beta_inv = np.where(conservative, 0.6, np.where(aggressive, -0.6, 0.0)) + rng.normal(0, 0.1, size=n_stocks)
    beta_bm = np.where(bm > np.median(bm), 0.5, -0.4) + rng.normal(0, 0.1, size=n_stocks)

    stock_rets = np.zeros((n_months, n_stocks))
    for t in range(n_months):
        idio = rng.normal(0, 0.20 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_stocks)
        stock_rets[t, :] = beta_op * rmw_true[t] + beta_inv * cma_true[t] + beta_bm * hml_true[t] + idio

    def vw_ret(mask: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        w = me[mask] / np.sum(me[mask])
        return np.asarray(stock_rets[:, mask] @ w)

    # RMW: Robust Minus Weak
    # RMW = 1/2 (Small Robust + Big Robust) - 1/2 (Small Weak + Big Weak)
    r_sr = vw_ret(small & robust)
    r_br = vw_ret(big & robust)
    r_sw = vw_ret(small & weak)
    r_bw = vw_ret(big & weak)
    rmw = 0.5 * (r_sr + r_br) - 0.5 * (r_sw + r_bw)

    # CMA: Conservative Minus Aggressive
    # CMA = 1/2 (Small Conservative + Big Conservative) - 1/2 (Small Aggressive + Big Aggressive)
    r_sc = vw_ret(small & conservative)
    r_bc = vw_ret(big & conservative)
    r_sa = vw_ret(small & aggressive)
    r_ba = vw_ret(big & aggressive)
    cma = 0.5 * (r_sc + r_bc) - 0.5 * (r_sa + r_ba)

    mean_rmw = float(np.mean(rmw) * 12 * 100)
    std_rmw = float(np.std(rmw, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12) * 100)
    t_rmw = float(np.mean(rmw) / (np.std(rmw, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_months)))

    mean_cma = float(np.mean(cma) * 12 * 100)
    std_cma = float(np.std(cma, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12) * 100)
    t_cma = float(np.mean(cma) / (np.std(cma, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_months)))

    corr_rmw_cma = float(np.corrcoef(rmw, cma)[0, 1])

    return {
        "n_stocks": n_stocks,
        "n_months": n_months,
        "mean_rmw": round(mean_rmw, 2),
        "std_rmw": round(std_rmw, 2),
        "t_rmw": round(t_rmw, 2),
        "mean_cma": round(mean_cma, 2),
        "std_cma": round(std_cma, 2),
        "t_cma": round(t_cma, 2),
        "corr_rmw_cma": round(corr_rmw_cma, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F2-L03-1 | Profitability (RMW) and Investment (CMA) Factor Sorts")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = simulate_profitability_and_investment(seed=42)
    print(f"Sample size: {res['n_stocks']} equities over {res['n_months']} months")
    print(f"RMW Annualized Premium: {res['mean_rmw']:+.2f}% (vol {res['std_rmw']:.2f}%, t = {res['t_rmw']:.2f})")
    print(f"CMA Annualized Premium: {res['mean_cma']:+.2f}% (vol {res['std_cma']:.2f}%, t = {res['t_cma']:.2f})")
    print(f"RMW-CMA Correlation:    {res['corr_rmw_cma']:+.4f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The standard Fama–French (2015) formulation of operating profitability and investment relies on physical balance sheet measures that have become severely degraded in the modern knowledge-based economy. Modern corporate investment is dominated by intangible expenditures—such as software development, research and development (R&D), patent filings, and customer acquisition costs. Under US GAAP, intangible investments are fully expensed immediately on the income statement rather than capitalized on the balance sheet. Consequently, high-investment technology companies appear artificially to have low capital investment (measured by physical asset growth) and depressed operating profitability (depressed by R&D expensing), misclassifying innovative leaders as low-profitability firms.

Misconceptions

  1. Value, profitability, and investment are mutually redundant expressions of corporate distress.

    Empirically, value and profitability have a negative cross-sectional correlation (\(\rho \approx -0.30\)). Distressed value stocks typically suffer from poor operating profitability, whereas highly profitable firms trade at premium multiples. Far from redundant, combining value and profitability creates substantial diversification gains.

  2. Bottom-line net income is the most accurate accounting metric to measure corporate profitability for asset pricing.

    Bottom-line net income is heavily contaminated by discretionary accruals, one-off extraordinary charges, tax structuring, and interest expense. Novy-Marx (2013) demonstrated that gross profit (\(\text{REVT} - \text{COGS}\)) scaled by assets has dramatically stronger predictive power for future expected returns than bottom-line earnings.

  3. Firms with aggressive asset growth deliver superior stock returns because investment signals high economic growth.

    Empirically, the opposite is true: aggressive asset expansion predicts substantial underperformance. From \(q\)-theory, firms invest heavily when their discount rate (cost of equity) is lowest. Furthermore, corporate managers frequently over-invest due to empire-building agency incentives, reducing subsequent shareholder returns.

Practice Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Firm A reports Total Revenue of \(\$1,000\text{M}\), Cost of Goods Sold of \(\$600\text{M}\), SG&A of \(\$150\text{M}\), Interest Expense of \(\$50\text{M}\), Total Assets of \(\$2,000\text{M}\), and Book Equity of \(\$800\text{M}\). Compute Firm A's Novy-Marx Gross Profitability (GP/A) and Fama–French Operating Profitability (OP).

Solution

Gross Profit is \(\text{Revenue} - \text{COGS} = 1000 - 600 = \$400\text{M}\). Novy-Marx Gross Profitability is \(GP/A = \frac{400}{2000} = 0.20\) (\(20\%\)). Fama–French Operating Profit is \(\text{Revenue} - \text{COGS} - \text{SG\&A} - \text{Interest} = 1000 - 600 - 150 - 50 = \$200\text{M}\). Operating Profitability is \(OP = \frac{200}{800} = 0.25\) (\(25\%\)).

A firm has total assets of \(\$1,200\text{M}\) at fiscal year-end \(t-2\) and \(\$1,380\text{M}\) at fiscal year-end \(t-1\). Calculate its asset growth investment metric for Fama–French CMA factor sorting.

Solution

The asset growth rate is \(\text{Inv} = \frac{\text{AT}_{t-1} - \text{AT}_{t-2}}{\text{AT}_{t-2}} = \frac{1380 - 1200}{1200} = \frac{180}{1200} = 0.150\) or \(15.0\%\).

In a Fama–French \(2 \times 3\) sort on size and investment, the monthly returns are: Small Conservative \(1.8\%\), Big Conservative \(1.2\%\), Small Aggressive \(0.6\%\), and Big Aggressive \(0.4\%\). Compute the CMA factor return.

Solution

The CMA factor return is \(CMA = \frac{1}{2}(1.8 + 1.2) - \frac{1}{2}(0.6 + 0.4) = 1.5\% - 0.5\% = 1.0\%\) per month (\(12.0\%\) annualized).

Tier 2 · Proof

Using the Gordon Growth Model with payout ratio \((1 - b)\), return on equity \(\text{ROE}\), growth \(g = b \times \text{ROE}\), and discount rate \(r > g\), prove that the market-to-book ratio is an increasing function of profitability (\(\text{ROE}\)) and derive its sensitivity.

Solution

Under clean surplus, \(P = \frac{E_1 (1 - b)}{r - g} = \frac{B_0 \text{ROE} (1 - b)}{r - b \text{ROE}}\). Dividing by \(B_0\), we have \(\frac{P}{B} = \frac{\text{ROE} (1 - b)}{r - b \text{ROE}}\). Differentiating with respect to \(\text{ROE}\): \(\frac{\partial (P/B)}{\partial \text{ROE}} = \frac{(1 - b)(r - b \text{ROE}) - \text{ROE}(1 - b)(-b)}{(r - b \text{ROE})^2} = \frac{(1 - b)r}{(r - b \text{ROE})^2}\). Because \(0 < b < 1\) and \(r > 0\), this derivative is strictly positive, proving that higher profitability raises market-to-book valuation.

Prove that in the Fama–French valuation identity, holding expected profitability \(\{Y_{t+\tau}\}\) and valuation \(M_t/B_t\) strictly constant, any increase in expected future capital additions \(dB_{t+\tau}\) mathematically requires a reduction in the internal discount rate \(r\).

Solution

Consider \(\frac{M_t}{B_t} = \sum_{\tau=1}^\infty \frac{\E_t[Y_{t+\tau}] - \E_t[dB_{t+\tau}]}{(1 + r)^\tau B_t}\). If \(dB_{t+\tau}\) increases for any \(\tau\), the numerator in period \(\tau\) decreases. Since \(M_t/B_t\) is held fixed on the left-hand side, the denominator \((1 + r)^\tau\) must decrease to offset the smaller cash flow. Therefore, \(1 + r\) must decrease, which proves that \(\frac{\partial r}{\partial dB} < 0\).

Prove that if profitability \(Y\) and investment \(dB\) are positively correlated across firms, a univariate sort on investment that fails to control for profitability underestimates the true ceteris-paribus investment discount rate penalty.

Solution

Let expected return be governed by \(r_i = \gamma_0 + \gamma_Y Y_i - \gamma_I I_i + \epsilon_i\) with \(\gamma_Y > 0, \gamma_I > 0\). In an omitted variable regression of \(r_i\) on \(I_i\) alone, the univariate slope coefficient satisfies \(\hat{b}_I = -\gamma_I + \gamma_Y \frac{\Cov(Y, I)}{\Var(I)}\). Because profitable firms invest more (\(\Cov(Y, I) > 0\)) and \(\gamma_Y > 0\), the term \(\gamma_Y \frac{\Cov(Y, I)}{\Var(I)} > 0\). This pulls \(\hat{b}_I\) toward zero, attenuating the measured investment penalty unless profitability is held constant.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Between 2010 and 2020, why did the Fama–French operating profitability factor (RMW) deliver strong positive returns while the value factor (HML) experienced severe losses?

Solution

During the 2010s, high-margin, cash-generative technology giants (Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet) generated massive profits but traded at elevated valuation multiples (low book-to-market). HML shorted these companies as 'expensive growth' and bought distressed traditional companies, suffering severe drawdowns. RMW, however, was long these profitable companies and short unprofitable small-caps, delivering robust positive alpha.

Why does Novy-Marx (2013) find that scaling gross profit by total assets (GP/A) produces stronger asset pricing predictability than scaling gross profit by book equity (GP/BE)?

Solution

Scaling by book equity introduces leverage and valuation noise. Book equity can be depressed or made negative by accumulated debt, historical losses, and share repurchases, distorting ratios. Scaling by total book assets measures the fundamental productive capacity of the entire firm's asset base independent of capital structure financing choices.

How does Sloan's (1996) accrual anomaly relate to the difference between cash profitability and accounting operating profitability?

Solution

Accounting operating earnings consist of cash flows plus accounting accruals (\(\text{Earnings} = \text{CFO} + \text{Accruals}\)). Sloan (1996) demonstrated that accruals exhibit low persistence because they reverse in subsequent years. Firms with high accruals appear profitable today but suffer earnings declines tomorrow. Cash-based operating profitability removes accruals, producing an even stronger and more persistent asset pricing premium.

Tier 4 · Research

How do Peters & Taylor (2017) modify balance sheet investment metrics to account for corporate intangible capital? What is the impact on factor pricing?

Solution

Peters & Taylor capitalize past R&D expenditures using a perpetual inventory method with industry-specific depreciation rates, and capitalize a fraction of SG&A as organizational brand capital. Adding intangible capital to physical PP&E restores the explanatory power of investment and book-to-market factors in the post-2000 universe, reducing pricing errors for technology firms.

Contrast the economic philosophies behind the Fama–French (2015) 5-factor model and the Hou–Xue–Zhang (2015) q-factor model.

Solution

Fama & French motivate RMW and CMA from consumer valuation identities (dividend discount model and clean surplus accounting), viewing factors as empirical state variable proxies. Hou, Xue & Zhang derive their model directly from the firm's first-order condition in neoclassical production economics (\(q\)-theory), arguing that expected returns are determined by corporate supply-side optimization rather than consumer Euler equations.

Why does Fama & French (2015) find that the value factor (HML) becomes statistically redundant in spanning regressions once RMW and CMA are included in the model?

Solution

In 5-factor spanning regressions, HML has a high positive loading on CMA (conservative investment) and a negative loading on RMW (weak profitability). Because value stocks are predominantly low-investment, low-profitability firms, their expected return is fully captured by the combination of CMA and RMW, driving HML's standalone intercept to zero (\(t < 1.0\)).

Retrieval Quiz

  1. According to the valuation identity in Fama & French (2015), what is the relation between expected investment and expected stock return, holding valuation and profitability fixed?

    Options

    From the clean surplus dividend discount equation, higher capital investment reduces current net cash flows, requiring a lower discount rate to match current market price.

  2. How does Novy-Marx (2013) define gross profitability (GP/A)?

    Options

    Novy-Marx defines gross profitability as (REVT - COGS) / AT, measuring operating productivity uncontaminated by leverage or discretionary expenses.

  3. How is the Fama–French (2015) CMA factor constructed?

    Options

    CMA (Conservative Minus Aggressive) buys firms with low percentage asset growth and shorts firms with high percentage asset growth.

  4. If a firm has revenue of \(\$500\text{M}\), COGS of \(\$300\text{M}\), and total book assets of \(\$1,000\text{M}\), what is its Novy-Marx Gross Profitability (GP/A) in percent?

    \(GP/A = \frac{500 - 300}{1000} = \frac{200}{1000} = 20.0\%\).

  5. What empirical correlation is typically observed between the value factor (HML) and the gross profitability factor?

    Options

    Value and profitability exhibit negative correlation because growth stocks tend to be highly profitable whereas distressed value stocks have depressed margins.

  6. According to production-based \(q\)-theory, why do aggressive investing firms have lower expected stock returns?

    Options

    In production economics, investment increases with marginal q. When the cost of equity is low, firms face low hurdle rates and invest aggressively.

  7. If a firm's total assets increase from \(\$2,500\text{M}\) at \(t-2\) to \(\$2,800\text{M}\) at \(t-1\), what is its percentage asset growth rate?

    \(\frac{2800 - 2500}{2500} = \frac{300}{2500} = 0.120 = 12.0\%\).

  8. Why does GAAP accounting treatment of intangible investments (R&D, software) distort physical balance sheet factors?

    Options

    Under GAAP, R&D and intangible investments are expensed rather than capitalized, causing book equity and physical asset growth to miss modern corporate capital formation.
