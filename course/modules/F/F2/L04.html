Low-Risk Anomalies, IVOL & Quality: Leverage & Preferences

The foundational premise of financial economics asserts that bearing higher systematic risk commands higher expected returns. Yet empirical reality presents a stark paradox: the Security Market Line is stubbornly flat, low-beta assets deliver superior risk-adjusted alphas, and stocks with high idiosyncratic volatility suffer catastrophic underperformance. Exploring Black's borrowing constraints, Frazzini & Pedersen's Betting Against Beta factor, the Ang et al. IVOL puzzle, and Asness et al.'s Quality Minus Junk factor reveals how leverage frictions and behavioral lottery preferences reshape the pricing of equity risk.

1Leverage Constraints and the Flat Security Market Line

In the standard Sharpe–Lintner CAPM, all investors can lend and borrow without limit at the risk-free rate \(R_f\). Black (1972) relaxed this assumption by modeling an economy in which investors face borrowing restrictions or cannot borrow at the risk-free rate.

Theorem 4.1 · Black's Zero-Beta CAPM and Leverage Constraints
When investors face borrowing constraints (or borrow at rate \(R_b > R_f\)), investors desiring expected returns above the tangency portfolio cannot simply apply leverage. Instead, they overweight high-beta securities to amplify portfolio expected returns. In equilibrium: \[\begin{aligned} \E[R_i] - R_f &= \alpha_0 (1 - \beta_i) + \beta_i (\E[R_M] - R_f), \end{aligned}\] where \(\alpha_0 = R_z - R_f > 0\) represents the premium of the zero-beta portfolio \(R_z\) over cash. Consequently:

  1. The empirical Security Market Line (SML) is flatter than predicted by the Sharpe–Lintner CAPM: the slope with respect to \(\beta_i\) is strictly less than \(\E[R_M] - R_f\).
  2. Low-beta assets (\(\beta_i < 1\)) earn positive Jensen's alpha: \(\alpha_i = \alpha_0 (1 - \beta_i) > 0\).
  3. High-beta assets (\(\beta_i > 1\)) earn negative Jensen's alpha: \(\alpha_i = \alpha_0 (1 - \beta_i) < 0\).

Proof
Consider an agent maximizing expected utility subject to a leverage limit \(w^\top \mathbf{1} \le L\) with borrowing rate \(R_b = R_f + \psi\), where \(\psi \ge 0\) is the Lagrange multiplier on the leverage constraint. The agent's first-order condition for asset allocation yields: \[\begin{aligned} \E[R_i] - R_f &= \psi + \beta_i (\E[R_M] - R_f - \psi). \end{aligned}\] Rearranging terms: \[\begin{aligned} \E[R_i] - R_f &= \psi(1 - \beta_i) + \beta_i (\E[R_M] - R_f). \end{aligned}\] When the constraint binds (\(\psi > 0\)), the intercept relative to beta is \(\psi > 0\) and the slope is \(\E[R_M] - R_f - \psi < \E[R_M] - R_f\). Evaluating Jensen's alpha under the standard CAPM: \[\begin{aligned} \alpha_i &= \E[R_i] - R_f - \beta_i (\E[R_M] - R_f) = \psi(1 - \beta_i). \end{aligned}\] Because \(\psi > 0\), \(\alpha_i > 0\) for all \(\beta_i < 1\), and \(\alpha_i < 0\) for all \(\beta_i > 1\).

2Betting Against Beta (BAB): Frazzini & Pedersen (2014)

Building on Black's theory, Frazzini & Pedersen (2014) constructed an explicit, factor-neutral trading strategy to harvest the premium generated by leverage-constrained investors.

Definition 4.1 · Low-Risk Anomaly and Betting Against Beta (BAB)
The low-risk anomaly and betting against beta factor is a zero-beta, dollar-neutral portfolio that takes a leveraged long position in low-beta assets and a deleveraged short position in high-beta assets.

The construction proceeds in three precise mathematical steps:

  1. Beta Estimation & Shrinkage: Historical betas are estimated using trailing 1-year daily returns (or 5-year monthly returns) and shrunk toward unity using Vasicek empirical Bayes shrinkage: \[\begin{aligned} \beta_i^{\text{shrunk}} &= 0.60 \times \hat{\beta}_i + 0.40 \times 1.0. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Rank-Based Weighting: Securities are ranked by \(\beta_i^{\text{shrunk}}\) around the cross-sectional median \(\beta_z\). Assets with \(\beta_i < \beta_z\) are assigned to the low-beta portfolio \(L\), and those with \(\beta_i > \beta_z\) to the high-beta portfolio \(H\), weighted proportionally to their distance from the median: \[\begin{aligned} w_i^L &= k_L (\beta_z - \beta_i)^+, \quad w_i^H = k_H (\beta_i - \beta_z)^+, \end{aligned}\] normalized such that \(\sum w_i^L = 1\) and \(\sum w_i^H = 1\). Let \(\beta_L = \sum w_i^L \beta_i\) and \(\beta_H = \sum w_i^H \beta_i\) be the resulting portfolio betas.
  3. Ex-Ante Beta Neutralization: The BAB factor scales the low-beta portfolio by \(1/\beta_L\) (applying leverage) and the high-beta portfolio by \(1/\beta_H\) (deleveraging): \[\begin{aligned} R_{t+1}^{\text{BAB}} &= \frac{1}{\beta_L}(R_{t+1}^L - R_f) - \frac{1}{\beta_H}(R_{t+1}^H - R_f). \end{aligned}\]

Theorem 4.2 · Ex-Ante Beta Neutrality of the BAB Factor
Under the estimated portfolio loadings \(\beta_L\) and \(\beta_H\), the ex-ante market beta of the BAB factor is identically zero: \[\begin{aligned} \beta_{\text{BAB}} &= \Cov\left( \frac{1}{\beta_L}(R^L - R_f) - \frac{1}{\beta_H}(R^H - R_f), R_M - R_f \right) / \sigma_M^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{\beta_L}\beta_L - \frac{1}{\beta_H}\beta_H = 1 - 1 = 0. \end{aligned}\] Because \(\beta_{\text{BAB}} = 0\), its expected return reflects pure alpha: \[\begin{aligned} \E[R^{\text{BAB}}] &= \frac{\alpha_L}{\beta_L} - \frac{\alpha_H}{\beta_H} = \frac{\psi(1 - \beta_L)}{\beta_L} - \frac{\psi(1 - \beta_H)}{\beta_H} = \psi \left(\frac{1}{\beta_L} - \frac{1}{\beta_H}\right) > 0. \end{aligned}\]

3The Idiosyncratic Volatility Puzzle

In standard financial theory, idiosyncratic risk is diversifiable and should command zero premium. Under incomplete information models (Merton 1987), undiversified investors require a positive premium for holding stocks with high idiosyncratic volatility (\(\text{IVOL}\)). In striking contrast, Ang, Hodrick, Xing & Zhang (2006) uncovered a profound empirical puzzle.

Definition 4.2 · Idiosyncratic-Volatility Puzzle
The idiosyncratic-volatility puzzle is the empirical finding that equities with high idiosyncratic volatility relative to standard factor models (such as the Fama–French three-factor model) deliver abnormally low subsequent average returns, underperforming low-IVOL stocks by more than \(1.0\%\) per month.

Ang et al. measured idiosyncratic volatility for each stock \(i\) in month \(t\) as the sample standard deviation of daily residuals from the Fama–French three-factor regression: \[\begin{aligned} R_{i,\tau} - R_{f,\tau} &= \alpha_i + \beta_i (R_{M,\tau} - R_{f,\tau}) + s_i SMB_\tau + h_i HML_\tau + \epsilon_{i,\tau}, \quad \tau \in \text{month } t. \\ \text{IVOL}_{i,t} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{D_t - 1}\sum_{\tau=1}^{D_t} \epsilon_{i,\tau}^2}. \end{aligned}\] Sorting stocks into quintiles by \(\text{IVOL}\), the highest-volatility quintile (Q5) underperformed the lowest-volatility quintile (Q1) by \(-1.06\%\) per month (\(t = -3.10\)). This anomaly cannot be explained by size, value, momentum, liquidity, or volume.

Leading behavioral explanations identify retail lottery preferences: retail investors disproportionately purchase high-IVOL, low-priced stocks possessing extreme positive skewness (lottery-ticket payoffs), driving prices above fundamental value. Because short-sale constraints prevent rational arbitrageurs from shorting these overpriced microcaps, the negative expected returns persist.

Lab 1 · Betting Against Beta (BAB) and the IVOL Puzzle

Simulating 15 years across 250 equities with realistic dispersion in betas and idiosyncratic volatilities will construct the BAB factor, verify ex-ante beta neutrality, and demonstrate the negative IVOL return spread documented by Ang et al. (2006).

"""Lab F2-L04-1: Betting Against Beta (BAB) and Idiosyncratic Volatility (IVOL).

Constructs the Frazzini-Pedersen (2014) Betting Against Beta factor using empirical
beta shrinking, leverage scaling, and ex-ante beta neutrality, and tests the Ang et al.
(2006) idiosyncratic volatility puzzle across quintile portfolios.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_bab_and_ivol(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float | int]:
    """Construct BAB portfolio and evaluate IVOL quintile returns."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_stocks = 250
    n_months = 180

    # True market factor: 6% mean, 15% vol
    mkt = rng.normal(0.06 / 12, 0.15 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)

    # True dispersion in market betas: [0.4, 1.8]
    true_betas = rng.uniform(0.4, 1.8, size=n_stocks)

    # High-beta stocks have lottery/distress characteristics and higher IVOL
    ivol_true = 0.10 + 0.15 * (true_betas - 0.4) + rng.uniform(0.05, 0.20, size=n_stocks)

    # Security returns: Black (1972) flat security market line
    # High-beta stocks earn flat or lower risk-adjusted returns (alpha penalty on high beta / high IVOL)
    alpha_penalty = -0.04 * (true_betas - 1.0) / 12.0  # low beta earns positive alpha, high beta negative

    stock_rets = np.zeros((n_months, n_stocks))
    for t in range(n_months):
        idio = rng.normal(0, ivol_true / np.sqrt(12), size=n_stocks)
        stock_rets[t, :] = alpha_penalty + true_betas * mkt[t] + idio

    # Vasicek / Bayesian shrinkage of historical betas: beta_shrunk = 0.6 * beta_sample + 0.4 * 1.0
    sample_betas = np.zeros(n_stocks)
    for i in range(n_stocks):
        cov = np.cov(stock_rets[:, i], mkt)[0, 1]
        sample_betas[i] = cov / np.var(mkt)

    shrunk_betas = 0.6 * sample_betas + 0.4 * 1.0

    # Sort into Low Beta (bottom 50%) and High Beta (top 50%)
    median_beta = float(np.median(shrunk_betas))
    low_mask = shrunk_betas <= median_beta
    high_mask = shrunk_betas > median_beta

    # Value/equal weights within legs normalized by beta distance
    # Frazzini & Pedersen rank weights:
    beta_low = float(np.mean(shrunk_betas[low_mask]))
    beta_high = float(np.mean(shrunk_betas[high_mask]))

    r_low = np.mean(stock_rets[:, low_mask], axis=1)
    r_high = np.mean(stock_rets[:, high_mask], axis=1)

    # BAB factor: 1/beta_low * r_low - 1/beta_high * r_high
    r_bab = (1.0 / beta_low) * r_low - (1.0 / beta_high) * r_high

    # Verify BAB beta against market
    bab_beta = float(np.cov(r_bab, mkt)[0, 1] / np.var(mkt))

    mean_bab_ann = float(np.mean(r_bab) * 12 * 100)
    vol_bab_ann = float(np.std(r_bab, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12) * 100)
    sharpe_bab = float(mean_bab_ann / vol_bab_ann)

    # Idiosyncratic volatility sort (quintiles Q1 low IVOL to Q5 high IVOL)
    ivol_sample = np.std(stock_rets - np.outer(mkt, sample_betas), axis=0, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12)
    q_bounds = np.percentile(ivol_sample, [20, 40, 60, 80])
    q1_ret = np.mean(stock_rets[:, ivol_sample <= q_bounds[0]], axis=1)
    q5_ret = np.mean(stock_rets[:, ivol_sample >= q_bounds[3]], axis=1)

    q1_mean_ann = float(np.mean(q1_ret) * 12 * 100)
    q5_mean_ann = float(np.mean(q5_ret) * 12 * 100)
    ivol_spread = q5_mean_ann - q1_mean_ann  # Ang et al. puzzle: Q5 - Q1 is negative!

    return {
        "n_stocks": n_stocks,
        "beta_low": round(beta_low, 2),
        "beta_high": round(beta_high, 2),
        "bab_beta_to_mkt": round(bab_beta, 3),
        "mean_bab_ann": round(mean_bab_ann, 2),
        "vol_bab_ann": round(vol_bab_ann, 2),
        "sharpe_bab": round(sharpe_bab, 2),
        "q1_low_ivol_ann": round(q1_mean_ann, 2),
        "q5_high_ivol_ann": round(q5_mean_ann, 2),
        "ivol_spread_ann": round(ivol_spread, 2),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F2-L04-1 | Betting Against Beta (BAB) & IVOL Puzzle (Ang et al. 2006)")
    print("------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = simulate_bab_and_ivol(seed=42)
    print(f"Sample: {res['n_stocks']} equities; Shrunk Beta Low: {res['beta_low']:.2f}, High: {res['beta_high']:.2f}")
    print(f"BAB Realized Market Beta:    {res['bab_beta_to_mkt']:+.3f} (ex-ante beta neutral)")
    print(
        f"BAB Annualized Premium:      {res['mean_bab_ann']:+.2f}% "
        f"(vol {res['vol_bab_ann']:.2f}%, SR = {res['sharpe_bab']:.2f})"
    )
    print(f"Q1 (Low IVOL) Ann Return:    {res['q1_low_ivol_ann']:+.2f}%")
    print(f"Q5 (High IVOL) Ann Return:   {res['q5_high_ivol_ann']:+.2f}%")
    print(f"IVOL Spread (Q5 - Q1):       {res['ivol_spread_ann']:+.2f}% (IVOL Puzzle anomaly)")


main()

4Quality Minus Junk (QMJ): Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen (2019)

Connecting profitability, low beta, and safety into a unified framework, Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen (2019) formalized the definition of high-quality corporate equity.

Definition 4.3 · Quality Minus Junk (QMJ)
The quality minus junk factor is a zero-investment portfolio that goes long high-quality companies (characterized by high profitability, strong growth, high safety/low risk, and high shareholder payout) and short low-quality (junk) companies.

QMJ defines quality along four distinct economic dimensions, converting each metric into a standardized cross-sectional \(z\)-score:

  • Profitability: High return on equity (\(\text{ROE}\)), return on assets (\(\text{ROA}\)), gross margin (\(\text{GMAR}\)), and cash flow from operations over assets.
  • Growth: Consistent 5-year growth in operating profitability, measuring persistent fundamental expansion.
  • Safety: Low market beta (\(\beta\)), low idiosyncratic volatility (\(\text{IVOL}\)), low financial leverage (\(\text{Debt}/\text{Assets}\)), and low bankruptcy distress (Altman \(Z\)-score).
  • Payout: High net equity issuance payout (dividends plus share repurchases minus stock issuances).

Asness et al. showed that high-quality assets trade at higher valuation multiples, but their price premium is surprisingly modest compared to their superior subsequent performance, enabling the QMJ factor to generate high risk-adjusted Sharpe ratios (\(\text{SR} > 0.70\)) across global markets.

Where the Canon Breaks

While BAB and QMJ show stellar backtested performance, their empirical implementation suffers from severe institutional frictions. First, BAB relies critically on borrowing cash at the risk-free rate \(R_f\) to lever up the low-beta portfolio (\(1/\beta_L \approx 1.5\) to \(2.0\times\)). Real-world institutional investors borrow at prime broker margin rates or repo spreads (\(R_f + 50\) to \(150\text{ bps}\)), substantially eroding net excess returns. Second, Novy-Marx (2014) showed that BAB is not beta-neutral with respect to the Fama–French three-factor model; its apparent alpha is heavily driven by small-cap, low-liquidity stock dynamics and large industry bets. Third, the IVOL puzzle is concentrated almost entirely in tiny, illiquid microcaps; among large-cap US equities, the IVOL spread is statistically negligible.

Misconceptions

  1. The low-risk anomaly implies that low-beta stocks have higher total nominal returns than high-beta stocks.

    Unleveraged low-beta stocks typically have lower nominal volatility and modest total returns. The anomaly is that their risk-adjusted alpha (Sharpe ratio) is substantially higher than predicted by the CAPM, while high-beta stocks deliver negative alphas. Harvesting the anomaly requires applying leverage to low-beta assets.

  2. High idiosyncratic risk stocks must offer a positive risk premium to compensate undiversified retail investors.

    Empirically, high-IVOL stocks experience severe subsequent underperformance (\(\sim -1\%\) per month). Retail investors treat high-IVOL equities as lottery tickets, paying a substantial speculative premium that drives expected future returns negative.

  3. Quality and Value are mutually incompatible investment philosophies.

    Quality and Value evaluate complementary sides of the investment equation: Quality measures what fundamental productivity you receive, while Value measures what market price you pay. Combining QMJ and HML isolates high-quality companies trading at discounted multiples, producing the classic 'Buffett alpha'.

Practice Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

In a BAB strategy, the low-beta portfolio has an estimated beta of \(\beta_L = 0.60\) and the high-beta portfolio has \(\beta_H = 1.50\). What leverage multipliers \(k_L\) and \(k_H\) must be applied to the low and high portfolios to ensure ex-ante beta neutrality?

Solution

To achieve ex-ante beta neutrality, the leverage multipliers are the inverses of the portfolio betas: \(k_L = \frac{1}{\beta_L} = \frac{1}{0.60} \approx 1.667\) and \(k_H = \frac{1}{\beta_H} = \frac{1}{1.50} \approx 0.667\). The net beta is \(k_L \beta_L - k_H \beta_H = 1.667(0.60) - 0.667(1.50) = 1.0 - 1.0 = 0\).

Suppose the realized monthly excess return of the low-beta portfolio is \(0.90\%\) and the high-beta portfolio is \(0.75\%\). Using \(\beta_L = 0.60\) and \(\beta_H = 1.50\), compute the realized BAB return for the month.

Solution

The BAB factor return is \(R^{\text{BAB}} = \frac{1}{\beta_L} R^L - \frac{1}{\beta_H} R^H = \frac{0.90\%}{0.60} - \frac{0.75\%}{1.50} = 1.50\% - 0.50\% = 1.00\%\) for the month (\(12.0\%\) annualized).

An analyst measures trailing sample beta as \(\hat{\beta}_i = 1.80\). Compute the Vasicek-shrunk beta using the standard Frazzini–Pedersen formula: \(\beta^{\text{shrunk}} = 0.6 \hat{\beta} + 0.4(1.0)\).

Solution

\(\beta^{\text{shrunk}} = 0.6(1.80) + 0.4(1.0) = 1.08 + 0.40 = 1.48\). Empirical Bayes shrinkage dampens estimation error by pulling extreme sample estimates toward the cross-sectional mean of 1.0.

Tier 2 · Proof

Under Black's zero-beta CAPM, prove that the cross-sectional Security Market Line slope is strictly less than the expected market risk premium \(\E[R_M] - R_f\) whenever borrowing constraints bind (\(R_z > R_f\)).

Solution

In Black's equilibrium, \(\E[R_i] = R_z + \beta_i (\E[R_M] - R_z)\). Expressing this as excess returns over cash \(R_f\): \(\E[R_i] - R_f = (R_z - R_f) + \beta_i (\E[R_M] - R_f - (R_z - R_f))\). The slope with respect to \(\beta_i\) is \(\gamma_1 = \E[R_M] - R_f - (R_z - R_f)\). When borrowing constraints bind, \(R_z > R_f\), so \(\gamma_1 < \E[R_M] - R_f\). The empirical SML is therefore strictly flatter than the Sharpe–Lintner benchmark.

Prove that if financing costs for borrowing exceed the risk-free rate by spread \(s > 0\) (so the leveraged long leg borrows at \(R_f + s\)), the net expected return of the BAB factor decreases by exactly \(s / \beta_L\).

Solution

In the unleveraged specification, the long leg receives \(R^L - R_f\). When borrowing costs are \(R_f + s\), the leveraged long leg return becomes \(\frac{1}{\beta_L}(R^L - R_f - s)\). Subtracting the deleveraged short leg: \(R_{\text{net}}^{\text{BAB}} = \frac{1}{\beta_L}(R^L - R_f) - \frac{s}{\beta_L} - \frac{1}{\beta_H}(R^H - R_f) = R^{\text{BAB}} - \frac{s}{\beta_L}\). Because \(\beta_L < 1\), the leverage multiplier \(\frac{1}{\beta_L} > 1\), amplifying the financing spread drag on net alpha.

Prove that under the Cumulative Prospect Theory value function with probability weighting (Tversky & Kahneman 1992), retail investors with lottery preferences will accept negative expected returns for securities with high positive skewness.

Solution

Cumulative prospect theory overweights small probabilities of extreme positive payoffs via the non-linear probability weighting function \(w(p) \gg p\) as \(p \to 0\). For a lottery asset with a small probability \(p\) of a large gain \(G\) and a high probability \(1-p\) of loss \(L\), the subjective valuation is \(V = w(p) v(G) + w(1-p) v(-L)\). Because \(w(p) / p > 1\), the agent values the asset above its objective expected value \(\E[X] = p G - (1-p) L\). In competitive equilibrium with retail dominance, price is bid up until objective expected return becomes negative.

Tier 3 · Empirical

In Ang et al. (2006), how does the performance of the highest idiosyncratic volatility quintile (Q5) compare to the lowest (Q1) across size, value, and momentum controls?

Solution

Ang et al. found that the Q5 minus Q1 return spread was approximately \(-1.06\%\) per month (\(t = -3.10\)). Controlling for market, size, and value exposures in a Fama–French three-factor regression expanded the negative alpha to \(-1.38\%\) per month (\(t = -4.56\)), and adding momentum did not eliminate the puzzle, proving that high IVOL assets systematically destroy shareholder wealth.

How does Novy-Marx (2014) critique the claimed market-neutrality and factor robustness of Frazzini and Pedersen's BAB factor?

Solution

Novy-Marx demonstrated that BAB is not beta-neutral in multi-factor models. Because high-beta stocks load heavily on small size and growth, BAB implicitly holds a large short position in small growth stocks and a large long position in large-cap utilities. Once multi-factor benchmarks and industry controls are applied, much of BAB's apparent standalone alpha is subsumed by standard factor exposures.

How does the performance of the Quality Minus Junk (QMJ) factor behave during market panics and recessions?

Solution

Unlike momentum, which crashes violently during panic turnaround transitions, QMJ acts as a powerful flight-to-safety asset. During severe recessions and market panics (e.g. 2008), junk companies suffer extreme default risk while high-quality firms with pristine balance sheets and high profitability maintain operations, causing QMJ to deliver strong positive returns during broad market drawdowns.

Tier 4 · Research

Explain how Baker, Bradley & Wurgler (2011) attribute the low-risk anomaly to institutional benchmarking mandates (the 'limits to arbitrage' of delegated asset management).

Solution

Institutional portfolio managers are evaluated against equity benchmarks (e.g. S&P 500) and penalize tracking error risk. An institutional manager cannot exploit the flat SML by levering low-beta stocks because mandates strictly forbid leverage and cash borrowing. To beat the benchmark, managers who need outperformance must overweight high-beta stocks, creating excess demand for high-beta equities that flattens the SML.

How does Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw (2011) resolve part of the IVOL puzzle using the maximum daily return over the previous month (MAX)?

Solution

Bali et al. showed that the negative relationship between IVOL and stock returns is largely driven by extreme positive daily returns (MAX). High-IVOL stocks that experienced extreme positive lottery-like days in month \(t-1\) suffer severe underperformance in month \(t\). Once MAX is controlled for in cross-sectional regressions, the IVOL effect is significantly attenuated, proving that investor demand for lottery payoffs drives the anomaly.

Propose an econometric specification to test whether Quality Minus Junk (QMJ) subsumes or is subsumed by the Fama–French (2015) 5-factor model.

Solution

Run bidirectional spanning regressions: first, regress \(QMJ_t\) on the five Fama–French factors (\(MKT, SMB, HML, RMW, CMA\)) and test whether the intercept \(\alpha_{QMJ} = 0\); second, regress each FF5 factor individually on \(MKT, SMB, HML,\) and \(QMJ\). Empirically, QMJ retains a statistically significant positive alpha relative to FF5 because its Safety and Payout dimensions capture distress and leverage dynamics not fully spanned by RMW and CMA.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. Why does Black (1972) argue that borrowing constraints flatten the empirical Security Market Line?

    Options

    When borrowing constraints bind, investors seeking high expected returns bid up high-beta equities, suppressing high-beta alpha and flattening the SML.

  2. How does Frazzini & Pedersen (2014) ensure ex-ante beta neutrality in the Betting Against Beta (BAB) factor?

    Options

    By scaling each portfolio by the inverse of its beta, both legs have a unit beta, ensuring their difference has exactly zero ex-ante market beta.

  3. What is the central empirical finding of Ang, Hodrick, Xing & Zhang (2006) regarding idiosyncratic volatility?

    Options

    Ang et al. documented the IVOL puzzle: high-IVOL stocks underperform low-IVOL stocks by over 1% per month, contradicting standard asset pricing theory.

  4. Using the Vasicek shrinkage formula \(\beta^{\text{shrunk}} = 0.6 \hat{\beta} + 0.4(1.0)\), what is the shrunk beta for an asset with a sample beta of \(\hat{\beta} = 1.80\)?

    \(0.6(1.80) + 0.4(1.0) = 1.08 + 0.40 = 1.48\).

  5. Which four fundamental dimensions constitute the Quality Minus Junk (QMJ) factor of Asness, Frazzini & Pedersen (2019)?

    Options

    QMJ measures quality across four dimensions: Profitability, Growth (profitability growth), Safety (low risk and low leverage), and Payout.

  6. Why do retail investors' lottery preferences contribute to the idiosyncratic volatility puzzle?

    Options

    Demand for lottery-like payoffs bids up high-IVOL stock prices, leading to low or negative subsequent realized risk-adjusted returns.

  7. If the low-beta portfolio has \(\beta_L = 0.50\), what is the leverage multiplier applied to its returns in the BAB factor?

    \(k_L = \frac{1}{\beta_L} = \frac{1}{0.50} = 2.0\).

  8. What real-world institutional friction impairs real-world implementation of the theoretical BAB factor?

    Options

    BAB requires leveraging low-beta assets at the risk-free rate. Real-world borrowing spreads and margin requirements reduce practical profits.
