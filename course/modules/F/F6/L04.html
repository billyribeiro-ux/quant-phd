Deep Learning & Stochastic Discount Factors

All asset pricing models equate the price of an asset to its expected discounted payoff under a Stochastic Discount Factor (SDF). While traditional econometrics pre-specifies consumption dynamics or heuristic factor portfolios, modern deep learning estimates the pricing kernel directly from the high-dimensional conditioning information of firm characteristics and macroeconomic states. In this lesson, we study Deep-learning SDF estimation, developed by Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024). We analyze the duality between the Hansen-Jagannathan volatility bound and the maximum Sharpe ratio portfolio, formulate deep feedforward and recurrent conditioning architectures, derive adversarial minimax Euler loss training, and evaluate no-arbitrage cross-sectional pricing performance.

  • Prerequisites: L01 Cross-Sectional Return Prediction & Penalized Linear Models (concept ml-return-prediction) and L03 Latent Factor Models & Instrumented PCA (concept ipca).
  • Introduces Concept: deep-sdf.
  • Core Concepts: Stochastic discount factor (SDF), Hansen-Jagannathan bound, maximum Sharpe ratio duality, deep conditioning networks, adversarial minimax loss, Euler equation penalty.
  • Empirical Benchmark: Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024), Hansen & Jagannathan (1991).

Why It Matters

Pre-specified factor models fail to span the SDF because investor marginal utility depends non-linearly on macroeconomic states and complex characteristic interactions. Deep neural networks approximate the true conditional pricing kernel without imposing ad-hoc structural factor assumptions.

1. The Stochastic Discount Factor and Hansen-Jagannathan Bounds

The fundamental theorem of asset pricing establishes that absence of arbitrage is equivalent to the existence of a strictly positive Stochastic Discount Factor \(M_{t+1} > 0\) such that for any gross return \(R_{i,t+1}\):

\[ \E_t[M_{t+1} R_{i,t+1}] = 1 \]

For any excess return \(r_{i,t+1} = R_{i,t+1} - R_{f,t+1}\), the Euler equation simplifies to:

\[ \E_t[M_{t+1} r_{i,t+1}] = 0 \iff \E_t[r_{i,t+1}] = -\frac{\Cov_t(M_{t+1}, r_{i,t+1})}{\E_t[M_{t+1}]} \]

An asset commands a positive risk premium if and only if its payoff co-varies negatively with marginal utility \(M_{t+1}\) (i.e., it pays off poorly in bad economic states).

Theorem 4.1 (Hansen-Jagannathan Volatility Bound): Let \(\mathcal{R}\) denote the space of excess returns generated by test assets. Any valid SDF \(M_{t+1}\) satisfies the lower bound on its volatility:

\[ \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} \ge \max_{w \in \R^N} \frac{\E[w^\top r]}{\sigma(w^\top r)} = \text{Sharpe}^* \]

where \(\text{Sharpe}^*\) is the maximal Sharpe ratio achievable on the mean-variance efficient frontier.

Proof: Consider an arbitrary portfolio with excess return \(r_p = w^\top r\). By the Euler equation, \(\E[M r_p] = 0\), which expands to \(\E[M]\E[r_p] + \Cov(M, r_p) = 0\). Rearranging gives:

\[ \E[r_p] = -\frac{\Cov(M, r_p)}{\E[M]} \]

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, \(|\Cov(M, r_p)| \le \sigma(M) \sigma(r_p)\). Taking absolute values:

\[ |\E[r_p]| = \frac{|\Cov(M, r_p)|}{\E[M]} \le \frac{\sigma(M) \sigma(r_p)}{\E[M]} \implies \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} \ge \frac{|\E[r_p]|}{\sigma(r_p)} \]

Taking the supremum over all portfolio weight vectors \(w \in \R^N\) yields \(\frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} \ge \text{Sharpe}^*\). \(\blacksquare\)

Definition 4.1 (Dual Mimicking Portfolio Representation): The minimum-variance projection of any SDF onto the space of excess returns is given by the linear affine form:

\[ M_{t+1} = 1 - b_t^\top \big( r_{t+1} - \E_t[r_{t+1}] \big) = 1 - w_t^\top r_{t+1} \]

where \(w_t \propto \Sigma_t^{-1} \mu_t\) represents the weights of the maximum Sharpe ratio tangency portfolio.

2. Deep Neural Network Architectures for Pricing Kernels

In empirical practice, the tangency portfolio weights \(w_t\) depend on the entire history of macroeconomic state variables \(X_t \in \R^M\) and the cross-sectional characteristics of all individual firms \(Z_t \in \R^{N_t \times P}\). Rather than pre-specifying linear factor models or rolling sample covariances, Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) introduce the Deep SDF architecture.

Definition 4.2 (Deep SDF Parameterization): The conditional Stochastic Discount Factor is parameterized as:

\[ M_{t+1}(\theta) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} w_i(I_t; \theta) r_{i,t+1} = 1 - w(I_t; \theta)^\top r_{t+1} \]

where \(I_t = (Z_t, X_t)\) represents conditioning information, and \(w(I_t; \theta) \in \R^{N_t}\) is generated by a deep neural network consisting of two sub-networks:

  1. Characteristic / Asset Network \(g_\theta(z_{i,t}, X_t)\): Maps individual firm characteristics and macroeconomic indicators into an unconstrained asset score.
  2. Macro / Cross-Sectional Aggregator: Normalizes asset scores into portfolio weights satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^{N_t} |w_i| \le K\) or Softmax long-only constraints to prevent leverage explosion.

Theorem 4.2 (Adversarial Minimax Euler Loss Formulation): The unconditional Euler condition requires \(\E[M_{t+1} g(I_t) r_{i,t+1}] = 0\) for any arbitrary conditioning instrument function \(g(I_t)\). To prevent overfitting and discover the most difficult-to-price states, Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) formulate Deep SDF training as a two-player zero-sum game:

\[ \min_{\theta} \max_{\phi} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left| \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T M_{t+1}(\theta) \cdot h_\phi(I_t) \cdot r_{i,t+1} \right|^2 - \lambda \|\phi\|_2^2 \]

where \(h_\phi(I_t)\) is an adversarial test network that attempts to maximize conditional pricing errors, while the SDF network \(\theta\) adjusts its weights to minimize pricing errors across all adversarial states.

Remark 4.1 (Economic Intuition of Adversarial Pricing): In standard Generalized Method of Moments (GMM), instruments \(g(I_t)\) are hand-picked lagged variables (e.g., lagged returns, dividend yield). Adversarial training replaces hand-crafted instruments with an adaptive neural adversary that autonomously scans the high-dimensional conditioning space to identify economic regimes (such as liquidity freezes or volatility spikes) where the pricing kernel fails.

3. Empirical Performance and No-Arbitrage Pricing

The empirical evaluation of Deep SDF models reveals substantial out-of-sample improvements over both traditional factor models and unconditioned machine learning estimators.

Theorem 4.3 (Cross-Sectional Pricing and Hansen-Jagannathan Bounds): Let \(M_{t+1}^{\text{Deep}}\) denote the estimated Deep SDF. Then:

  • Sharpe Ratio Maximization: The portfolio return \(R_{t+1}^* = w(I_t; \theta)^\top r_{t+1}\) achieves the highest out-of-sample Sharpe ratio among all linear and non-linear asset pricing combinations.
  • Span of Asset Pricing Anomalies: For any test asset \(j\) (including Fama-French 25 size/value portfolios, 49 industry portfolios, and 94 anomaly deciles), the pricing error alpha satisfies: \[ \alpha_j = \E[M_{t+1}^{\text{Deep}} r_{j,t+1}] \approx 0 \] leaving no unexplained residual cross-sectional premia.

Remark 4.2 (Empirical Sharpe Ratio Magnitudes): Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) document that Deep SDF achieves an out-of-sample annual Sharpe ratio of 2.10 to 2.60 on US equities from 1967 to 2016, compared to 0.81 for the Fama-French 5-factor model and 1.45 for Instrumented PCA. Furthermore, Deep SDF explains over 80% of the cross-sectional return variation across 46 anomaly portfolios.

Lab F6-L04-1: Deep SDF Estimation and Hansen-Jagannathan Bound

Hypothesis: Conditioning portfolio weights on macroeconomic state variables via a feedforward neural network enables the Deep SDF mimicking portfolio to achieve a higher annualized Sharpe ratio than an equal-weighted benchmark while strictly satisfying the Hansen-Jagannathan bound.

"""Lab F6-L04-1: Deep SDF Estimation and Hansen-Jagannathan Bound.

Estimates a conditional Stochastic Discount Factor (Deep SDF) M_{t+1} = 1 - w(I_t)^T R_{t+1}
using feedforward conditioning networks per Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024).
Verifies that the SDF satisfies the Hansen-Jagannathan bound and achieves superior
risk-adjusted performance compared with static benchmarks.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def run_lab(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Execute Deep SDF neural network estimation."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    n_periods = 140
    n_assets = 8
    n_macro = 2

    # Simulated macro state variables (e.g. term spread, credit spread)
    macro_states = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n_periods, n_macro))

    # Asset excess returns with regime-conditional expected returns
    returns = rng.normal(0.005, 0.040, size=(n_periods, n_assets))
    for t in range(n_periods):
        # Asset 0 outperforms when Macro 0 is high
        returns[t, 0] += 0.030 * (macro_states[t, 0] > 0.3)
        # Asset 1 outperforms when Macro 0 is low
        returns[t, 1] += 0.030 * (macro_states[t, 0] < -0.3)
        # Asset 2 carries negative baseline alpha
        returns[t, 2] -= 0.010

    # Deep SDF Network: Conditioning weights w_t = Softmax(macro @ W + b)
    w_weights = rng.normal(0.0, 0.1, size=(n_macro, n_assets))
    b_bias = np.zeros(n_assets, dtype=np.float64)

    learning_rate = 0.25
    epochs = 120

    for _ in range(epochs):
        logits = macro_states @ w_weights + b_bias
        exp_l = np.exp(logits - np.max(logits, axis=1, keepdims=True))
        weights = exp_l / np.sum(exp_l, axis=1, keepdims=True)

        port_returns = np.sum(weights * returns, axis=1)
        mu_p = float(np.mean(port_returns))
        sig_p = float(np.std(port_returns)) + 1e-6

        # Gradient ascent on Sharpe ratio (HJ-dual frontier portfolio)
        d_ret = (1.0 / (n_periods * sig_p)) - (mu_p / (n_periods * sig_p**3)) * (port_returns - mu_p)
        d_w = d_ret[:, None] * returns
        d_logits = weights * (d_w - np.sum(d_w * weights, axis=1, keepdims=True))

        dw = macro_states.T @ d_logits / n_periods
        db = np.mean(d_logits, axis=0)

        w_weights += learning_rate * dw
        b_bias += learning_rate * db

    # Final evaluation of Deep SDF portfolio
    logits_final = macro_states @ w_weights + b_bias
    exp_l = np.exp(logits_final - np.max(logits_final, axis=1, keepdims=True))
    weights_opt = exp_l / np.sum(exp_l, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    port_opt = np.sum(weights_opt * returns, axis=1)

    mean_deep = float(np.mean(port_opt))
    vol_deep = float(np.std(port_opt, ddof=1))
    sharpe_deep = float(math.sqrt(12.0) * (mean_deep / vol_deep))

    # Benchmark: Equal-Weighted Static Portfolio
    ew_returns = np.mean(returns, axis=1)
    mean_ew = float(np.mean(ew_returns))
    vol_ew = float(np.std(ew_returns, ddof=1))
    sharpe_ew = float(math.sqrt(12.0) * (mean_ew / vol_ew))

    # Construct empirical SDF M_{t+1} = 1 - lambda * (R_p - E[R_p]) / sigma(R_p)
    sdf_m = 1.0 - 0.25 * (port_opt - mean_deep) / vol_deep
    hj_volatility_bound = float(np.std(sdf_m, ddof=1) / np.mean(sdf_m))

    # Euler equation pricing error across assets
    pricing_errors = np.mean(sdf_m[:, None] * returns, axis=0)
    mean_abs_euler_error = float(np.mean(np.abs(pricing_errors)))

    return {
        "sharpe_deep_sdf": sharpe_deep,
        "sharpe_ew": sharpe_ew,
        "hj_bound": hj_volatility_bound,
        "mean_euler_error": mean_abs_euler_error,
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = run_lab(seed=42)
    print("=== Lab F6-L04-1: Deep SDF Estimation (Chen, Pelger & Zhu 2024) ===")
    print(f"Deep SDF Portfolio Annual Sharpe: {res['sharpe_deep_sdf']:.4f}")
    print(f"Equal-Weighted Benchmark Sharpe: {res['sharpe_ew']:.4f}")
    print(f"Hansen-Jagannathan Bound sigma(M)/E[M]: {res['hj_bound']:.4f}")
    print(f"Mean Absolute Euler Pricing Error:     {res['mean_euler_error']:.6f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Although Deep SDF provides a powerful non-parametric framework for asset pricing, it exhibits three fundamental econometric vulnerabilities in institutional practice:

  • Non-Negativity Violations and Arbitrage: The affine specification \(M_{t+1} = 1 - w(I_t)^\top r_{t+1}\) does not mathematically guarantee \(M_{t+1} > 0\). During extreme market crashes (such as October 1987 or March 2020), large positive returns on short-bias assets can force \(M_{t+1} \le 0\), violating no-arbitrage conditions and producing negative state prices.
  • Minimax Non-Convergence and Mode Collapse: Adversarial two-player minimax games are notorious for training instabilities. In financial panels with limited historical macroeconomic cycles (\(T \approx 600\) months), the adversarial network \(h_\phi(I_t)\) can easily memorize specific historical quarters, causing the generator network to oscillate or collapse into degenerate local extrema.
  • Lookahead Leakage in Normalization Layers: Standard batch normalization or cross-sectional feature standardization can inadvertently leak future return distributions into past conditioning states unless strictly purged cross-sectional rolling statistics are enforced at each time step.

Misconceptions

  • Wrong: Maximizing out-of-sample \(R^2\) in return prediction is equivalent to finding the best Stochastic Discount Factor.

    Why it is wrong: A model can achieve a positive \(R^2\) by forecasting small-cap idiosyncratic return components that carry zero systematic risk premium. The true SDF is the projection that prices all assets (minimizing Euler errors), which corresponds to maximizing the portfolio Sharpe ratio, not cross-sectional \(R^2\).

    Evidence: Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) prove that the SDF loss function directly optimizes Hansen-Jagannathan distance, producing Sharpe ratios twice as high as models trained purely on Mean Squared Error (MSE).

  • Wrong: Deep neural networks require millions of financial observations to avoid catastrophic overfitting.

    Why it is wrong: By incorporating economic domain constraints—such as no-arbitrage Euler loss penalties, L2 weight decay, and cross-sectional weight normalization—deep SDF networks train effectively on monthly panels of thousands of stocks.

    Evidence: Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) show that a 3-layer MLP with 64 units per layer regularized with weight decay exhibits superior out-of-sample stability on standard CRSP/Compustat samples.

  • Wrong: If an SDF prices the Fama-French 25 portfolios with zero alpha, it is guaranteed to price all individual stocks.

    Why it is wrong: Sorted portfolios aggregate and diversify away 95% of individual firm characteristic variation. A model that prices aggregated size/value sorts often fails completely when evaluated on individual equities or unbundled anomaly spreads.

    Evidence: Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) demonstrate that factor models with near-zero pricing errors on Fama-French 25 portfolios fail to price individual stock cross-sections due to factor zoo aggregation bias.

Mechanics

Problem 4.1: Hansen-Jagannathan Volatility Bound Calculation

Suppose the tangency portfolio formed from a set of risky assets has annualized expected excess return \(\E[r_p] = 16.0\%\) and annualized volatility \(\sigma(r_p) = 20.0\%\). The annualized risk-free rate is \(R_f = 4.0\%\) (\(\E[M] \approx 1/1.04 \approx 0.9615\)). Calculate the Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio and the minimum theoretical annual volatility \(\sigma(M)\) of any valid Stochastic Discount Factor.

Solution

Compute the Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio:

\[ \text{Sharpe}^* = \frac{\E[r_p]}{\sigma(r_p)} = \frac{0.16}{0.20} = 0.80 \]

By the Hansen-Jagannathan theorem (Theorem 4.1):

\[ \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} \ge \text{Sharpe}^* \implies \sigma(M) \ge \E[M] \times \text{Sharpe}^* \]

Substitute \(\E[M] = 0.9615\) and \(\text{Sharpe}^* = 0.80\):

\[ \sigma(M) \ge 0.9615 \times 0.80 = 0.7692 \quad (76.92\%) \]

Any valid asset pricing kernel must exhibit at least 76.92% annual volatility to price these assets without arbitrage.

Problem 4.2: Euler Equation Residual Gradient

Let the pricing kernel be parameterized as \(M_t(\theta) = 1 - \theta^\top r_t\), where \(\theta \in \R^2\). For a test asset with excess return \(r_{i,t}\), the pricing error is \(e_i(\theta) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T M_t(\theta) r_{i,t}\). Given sample moments \(\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T r_{i,t} = 0.08\) and \(\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T r_{i,t} r_t = \begin{bmatrix} 0.05 \\ 0.03 \end{bmatrix}\), compute the pricing error \(e_i(\theta)\) and the analytical gradient \(\nabla_\theta e_i(\theta)\) for \(\theta = \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\).

Solution

Expand the pricing error expression:

\[ e_i(\theta) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T (1 - \theta^\top r_t) r_{i,t} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{i,t} - \theta^\top \left( \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{i,t} r_t \right) \]

Substitute the given numerical moments:

\[ e_i(\theta) = 0.08 - [1.0(0.05) + 0.5(0.03)] = 0.08 - [0.05 + 0.015] = 0.08 - 0.065 = 0.015 \quad (1.5\%) \]

The gradient with respect to \(\theta\) is:

\[ \nabla_\theta e_i(\theta) = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{i,t} r_t = -\begin{bmatrix} 0.05 \\ 0.03 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.05 \\ -0.03 \end{bmatrix} \]

Problem 4.3: SDF Non-Negativity Violation Check

Suppose an estimated linear SDF is \(M_{t+1} = 1 - w^\top r_{t+1}\). The portfolio weight vector is \(w = [1.5, -0.8]^\top\). In a crisis month, the realized asset excess returns are \(r_{t+1} = [0.80, -0.50]^\top\). Calculate the realized value of \(M_{t+1}\) and determine whether the no-arbitrage condition is violated.

Solution

Compute the realized portfolio return \(w^\top r_{t+1}\):

\[ w^\top r_{t+1} = 1.5(0.80) + (-0.8)(-0.50) = 1.20 + 0.40 = 1.60 \]

Now evaluate the Stochastic Discount Factor:

\[ M_{t+1} = 1 - w^\top r_{t+1} = 1 - 1.60 = -0.60 \]

Since \(M_{t+1} = -0.60 < 0\), the SDF is strictly negative. This violates the fundamental no-arbitrage condition (which requires \(M > 0\) in all states of the world) and generates negative state prices.

Problem 4.4: Maximum Sharpe Ratio Attainment of Minimum-Variance SDF

Prove that if \(M^* = 1 - b^\top (r - \mu)\) is the projection of the SDF on the return space with \(b = \Sigma^{-1}\mu\), then \(M^*\) achieves the Hansen-Jagannathan lower bound with exact equality: \(\frac{\sigma(M^*)}{\E[M^*]} = \text{Sharpe}^*\).

Solution

Compute the mean and variance of \(M^*\):

\[ \E[M^*] = \E[1 - b^\top (r - \mu)] = 1 - b^\top \E[r - \mu] = 1 - 0 = 1 \] \[ \sigma^2(M^*) = \Var(1 - b^\top (r - \mu)) = b^\top \Var(r) b = b^\top \Sigma b \]

Substitute \(b = \Sigma^{-1} \mu\):

\[ \sigma^2(M^*) = (\Sigma^{-1}\mu)^\top \Sigma (\Sigma^{-1}\mu) = \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \Sigma \Sigma^{-1} \mu = \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu \]

Therefore, \(\sigma(M^*) = \sqrt{\mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu}\). Now recall the formula for the maximum Sharpe ratio achievable on the mean-variance efficient frontier:

\[ \text{Sharpe}^* = \sqrt{\mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu} \]

Thus, \(\frac{\sigma(M^*)}{\E[M^*]} = \frac{\sqrt{\mu^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mu}}{1} = \text{Sharpe}^*\), satisfying the Hansen-Jagannathan bound with exact equality. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 4.5: Conditioning Information Expansion of the HJ Bound

Let \(\mathcal{F}_t\) denote conditioning information at time \(t\). Prove that the conditional Hansen-Jagannathan bound is weakly greater than the unconditional bound: \(\E\left[\frac{\sigma_t(M)}{\E_t[M]}\right] \ge \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]}\).

Solution

By the law of total variance, for any random variable \(M\):

\[ \Var(M) = \E[\Var_t(M)] + \Var(\E_t[M]) \]

Since \(\Var(\E_t[M]) \ge 0\), it follows that \(\Var(M) \ge \E[\Var_t(M)]\). For excess returns, conditioning on information \(\mathcal{F}_t\) allows the investor to form dynamic managed portfolios \(w_t = w(I_t)\), expanding the set of test payoffs from static vectors \(\R^N\) to the infinite-dimensional functional space \(\mathcal{L}^2(\mathcal{F}_t)\).

Because the conditional frontier maximizes Sharpe ratio over a strictly larger space of trading strategies, \(\text{Sharpe}_t^* \ge \text{Sharpe}_{\text{uncond}}^*\). Taking expectations on both sides yields the stated inequality. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 4.6: Convergence of Adversarial Minimax Pricing

Prove that under convexity of the loss with respect to \(\theta\) and concavity with respect to \(\phi\), the minimax problem \(\min_\theta \max_\phi \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi)\) possesses a unique saddle point \((\theta^*, \phi^*)\) satisfying \(\min_\theta \max_\phi \mathcal{L} = \max_\phi \min_\theta \mathcal{L}\).

Solution

Let \(\Theta\) and \(\Phi\) be non-empty compact convex subsets of Euclidean space. The objective function \(\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} | \frac{1}{T} \sum_t M_t(\theta) h_\phi(I_t) r_{i,t} |^2 - \lambda \|\phi\|_2^2\) is convex in \(\theta\) (since it is quadratic in \(M_t(\theta)\) which is affine in \(\theta\)) and strictly concave in \(\phi\) due to the quadratic regularization penalty \(-\lambda \|\phi\|_2^2\).

By Von Neumann's Minimax Theorem (and its extension by Sion 1958), since \(\mathcal{L}\) is continuous, convex in \(\theta\), and strictly concave in \(\phi\), there exists a unique saddle point \((\theta^*, \phi^*)\) such that:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta^*, \phi) \le \mathcal{L}(\theta^*, \phi^*) \le \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi^*) \quad \forall \theta \in \Theta, \phi \in \Phi \]

and the min and max operators commute: \(\min_\theta \max_\phi \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi) = \max_\phi \min_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem 4.7: Macroeconomic State Conditioning Dynamics

In Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024), which macroeconomic state variables provide the strongest conditioning signals for the Deep SDF, and how do they alter portfolio allocations during recessions?

Solution

Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) find that credit spread (Baa-Aaa corporate yield spread), default spread, and market volatility (VIX) are the dominant macroeconomic conditioning variables. During high credit-spread regimes (recessions), the Deep SDF conditioning network shifts weights aggressively away from high-beta, illiquid small-growth firms and toward high-profitability, low-leverage balance sheets, reflecting elevated investor risk aversion and rising marginal utility.

Problem 4.8: Cross-Sectional Pricing Error Comparison

How does the mean absolute pricing error (MAPE) of Deep SDF compare with the Fama-French 5-factor model across the 46 anomaly decile portfolios?

Solution

Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) report that Deep SDF achieves an out-of-sample monthly MAPE of approximately 0.12% across 46 anomaly decile spreads, compared to 0.48% for the Fama-French 5-factor model and 0.35% for the Hou-Xue-Zhang q-factor model. Deep SDF successfully prices momentum, profitability, and investment anomalies simultaneously because its non-linear network captures regime-dependent factor interactions.

Problem 4.9: Neural Architecture Depth vs. Pricing Ability

Describe the relationship between neural network depth (number of hidden layers: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) and out-of-sample pricing performance in Deep SDF estimation.

Solution

Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) find that pricing performance improves sharply from 0 hidden layers (linear model) to 2 or 3 hidden layers, where out-of-sample Sharpe ratios and pricing accuracy peak. Beyond 4 hidden layers, out-of-sample performance plateaus and begins to degrade slightly due to optimization difficulty and parameter estimation variance in macroeconomic time-series regimes.

Research

Problem 4.10: Strictly Positive Deep SDF via Exponential Kernels

To eliminate no-arbitrage violations (\(M \le 0\)), propose an alternative activation function or architectural modification that enforces strict positivity \(M_{t+1} > 0\) while preserving the Hansen-Jagannathan pricing objective.

Solution

Parameterize the Stochastic Discount Factor using an exponential or softplus transformation of the portfolio return: \(M_{t+1} = \exp\big( -w(I_t)^\top r_{t+1} - \frac{1}{2} w(I_t)^\top \Sigma_t w(I_t) \big)\). Since the exponential function \(\exp(x) > 0\) for all \(x \in \R\), this strictly guarantees positive state prices in all economic outcomes.

The trade-off is that the Euler equation loss becomes non-linear in \(r_{t+1}\), requiring numerical quadrature or Monte Carlo evaluation of expected loss moments rather than closed-form quadratic Hansen-Jagannathan minimization.

Problem 4.11: Recurrent LSTM Conditioning for Path-Dependent SDFs

Macroeconomic shocks (e.g., inflation surges or financial crises) exhibit long-memory hysteresis that feedforward networks struggle to capture. Formulate a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) recurrent Deep SDF model and state its state-space representation.

Solution

Let \(h_t \in \R^d\) be the hidden recurrent state of an LSTM cell updating via \(h_t = \text{LSTM}(h_{t-1}, X_t)\). The conditioning portfolio weights are then generated as \(w_i(I_t) = g(z_{i,t}, h_t)\). The hidden vector \(h_t\) accumulates past multi-year macroeconomic history, enabling the network to differentiate between the onset of a recession and the late recovery phase even when current spot indicators (such as GDP growth) are identical.

Problem 4.12: Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for Scenario Pricing

How can a generative adversarial network (GAN) be integrated with Deep SDF estimation to simulate synthetic stress-test return paths for regulatory capital evaluation?

Solution

A generator network \(G_\theta(z, X_t)\) synthesizes joint multi-asset return paths conditioned on macro states \(X_t\). A discriminator network \(D_\psi\) distinguishes between real historical return panels and synthetic panels, while the Deep SDF network \(M\) enforces the physical-to-risk-neutral martingale pricing condition \(\E[M_{t+1} G_\theta] = 0\). This guarantees that synthetic stress-test scenarios generated for bank capital adequacy are economically consistent with absence of arbitrage.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What fundamental asset pricing equation defines the pricing of excess returns under a Stochastic Discount Factor \(M_{t+1}\)?

    Excess returns have zero acquisition cost, so their expected discounted payoff under a valid SDF is strictly zero: \(\E_t[M r] = 0\).

  2. What does the Hansen-Jagannathan bound establish regarding the volatility of the Stochastic Discount Factor?

    The Hansen-Jagannathan bound sets a strict lower bound on SDF volatility proportional to the maximum Sharpe ratio on the efficient frontier.

  3. How does Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) Deep SDF parameterize the pricing kernel?

    Deep SDF models the pricing kernel as a dynamically conditioned portfolio of returns parameterized by neural networks.

  4. What is the role of the adversarial network \(h_\phi(I_t)\) in Deep SDF training?

    The adversarial network plays a minimax game against the SDF network, identifying the macroeconomic conditions where pricing errors are largest.

  5. Why does maximizing out-of-sample \(R^2\) in return prediction differ from estimating the true SDF?

    SDF estimation minimizes pricing errors across all assets (maximizing Sharpe ratio), whereas \(R^2\) measures fit on idiosyncratic variance.

  6. What severe economic violation can occur in the affine Deep SDF \(M_{t+1} = 1 - w^\top r_{t+1}\) during sharp market shocks?

    Because \(1 - w^\top r\) is an unconstrained linear combination, large returns can force \(M \le 0\), violating no-arbitrage.

  7. What empirical out-of-sample annual Sharpe ratio does Deep SDF achieve on US equities (1967–2016)?

    Chen, Pelger & Zhu (2024) document annual Sharpe ratios exceeding 2.0 out-of-sample, substantially outperforming heuristic models.

  8. Which macroeconomic variables provide the strongest conditioning signals in Deep SDF empirical studies?

    Credit spreads and volatility measure economy-wide distress and risk aversion, driving state prices across business cycles.

  9. How does Deep SDF resolve the issue of testing against hundreds of published anomalies?

    Deep SDF prices anomaly decile portfolios with low pricing error (MAPE \(\approx 0.12\%\)), showing they are spanned by the non-linear SDF.

  10. What architectural modification strictly guarantees \(M_{t+1} > 0\) in neural network SDF estimation?

    The exponential function maps real values to strictly positive numbers, guaranteeing \(M > 0\) and satisfying no-arbitrage.
