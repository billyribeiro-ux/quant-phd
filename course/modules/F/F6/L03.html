Latent Factor Models & Instrumented PCA

Classical asset pricing models assume that systematic risk factors are either directly observable macroeconomic series or static heuristic portfolio spreads. In reality, systematic factor realizations are latent, while firm-level risk exposures evolve dynamically with corporate balance sheets and market valuations. In this lesson, we study Instrumented Principal Component Analysis (IPCA), formulated by Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019), which instruments time-varying factor loadings with observable firm characteristics. We derive the Alternating Least Squares (ALS) optimization algorithm, prove identification conditions under orthogonal rotations, establish the test of no-arbitrage factor restrictions versus characteristic anomalies, and contrast contemporaneous total variance explained against out-of-sample predictive performance.

  • Prerequisites: L01 Cross-Sectional Return Prediction & Penalized Linear Models (concept ml-return-prediction).
  • Introduces Concept: ipca.
  • Core Concepts: Latent factor models, instrumented factor loadings, Alternating Least Squares (ALS), characteristic-managed portfolios, Kronecker product systems, total vs. predictive \(R^2\).
  • Empirical Benchmark: Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019), Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020).

Why It Matters

Standard PCA requires static factor loadings, which fails over multi-decade panels as firms evolve. IPCA solves this incidental parameter problem by parameterizing dynamic betas through observable characteristics, simultaneously recovering latent factors and dynamic risk exposures.

1. Latent Factor Models and Time-Varying Loadings

Consider an economy of \(N_t\) individual equities observed over \(T\) periods. In an arbitrage pricing theory (APT) framework, excess returns \(r_{i,t+1}\) follow a multi-factor structure:

\[ r_{i,t+1} = \alpha_{i,t} + \beta_{i,t}^\top f_{t+1} + \epsilon_{i,t+1} \]

where \(f_{t+1} \in \R^K\) denotes an unobserved vector of latent systematic risk factors, \(\beta_{i,t} \in \R^K\) denotes the vector of dynamic risk factor loadings, \(\alpha_{i,t}\) is abnormal expected return, and \(\E[\epsilon_{i,t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\).

In standard statistical factor analysis (unconditional PCA), factor loadings are assumed constant over time: \(\beta_{i,t} = \beta_i\). However, corporate lifecycle transitions, leverage shifts, and cyclical fluctuations induce substantial time variation in asset betas. Attempting to estimate unconstrained time-varying betas \(\beta_{i,t}\) directly introduces an intractable incidental parameter problem: \(N \times T \times K\) unknowns with only \(N \times T\) return observations.

Definition 3.1 (Instrumented Principal Component Analysis): The IPCA structural specification of Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) projects individual dynamic alphas and betas onto a \(P\)-dimensional vector of observable firm characteristics \(z_{i,t} \in \R^P\):

\[ \alpha_{i,t} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\alpha, \qquad \beta_{i,t} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta \]

where \(\Gamma_\alpha \in \R^{P \times 1}\) is a static vector mapping characteristics to abnormal returns, and \(\Gamma_\beta \in \R^{P \times K}\) is a static matrix mapping characteristics to systematic factor exposures. The full econometric model is:

\[ r_{i,t+1} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\alpha + z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1} + \epsilon_{i,t+1} \]

Remark 3.1 (Incidental Parameter Resolution): By conditioning loadings on \(z_{i,t}\), IPCA reduces the parameter space from \(N \times T \times K\) dynamic betas down to a fixed, low-dimensional static mapping matrix \(\Gamma_\beta\) of size \(P \times K\), plus \(T \times K\) latent factor realizations \(\{f_t\}_{t=1}^T\). For \(P = 36\) characteristics, \(K = 5\) factors, and \(N = 3{,}000\) stocks, this collapses millions of unobservable parameters into just \(180\) static structural coefficients.

2. Alternating Least Squares (ALS) and Orthogonal Rotation

Under the pure risk factor null hypothesis of no arbitrage, \(\Gamma_\alpha = 0\), so all cross-sectional return premia are compensation for systematic factor exposure: \(\E_t[r_{i,t+1}] = \beta_{i,t}^\top \lambda_t = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta \lambda_t\). The objective is to minimize pooled sum-of-squared pricing errors:

\[ \min_{\Gamma_\beta, \{f_{t+1}\}_{t=1}^T} \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_t} \left( r_{i,t+1} - z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1} \right)^2 \]

Theorem 3.1 (Alternating Least Squares Closed Forms): The IPCA objective is bilinear in \(\Gamma_\beta\) and \(\{f_{t+1}\}\). Holding one fixed, the conditional minimization with respect to the other reduces to an exact closed-form linear least squares regression:

  1. Factor Realization Step (Given \(\Gamma_\beta\)): For each period \(t \in \{1, \dots, T\}\), let \(Z_t \in \R^{N_t \times P}\) and \(r_{t+1} \in \R^{N_t}\). The cross-sectional factor loading matrix is \(\hat{B}_t = Z_t \Gamma_\beta \in \R^{N_t \times K}\). The optimal factor vector is: \[ \hat{f}_{t+1} = \left( \hat{B}_t^\top \hat{B}_t \right)^{-1} \hat{B}_t^\top r_{t+1} = \left( \Gamma_\beta^\top Z_t^\top Z_t \Gamma_\beta \right)^{-1} \Gamma_\beta^\top Z_t^\top r_{t+1} \]
  2. Mapping Matrix Step (Given \(\{f_{t+1}\}\)): Let \(y = \mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta) \in \R^{P K}\). Vectorizing the pooled residual equation across all \(T\) periods yields the linear system: \[ \mathrm{vec}(\hat{\Gamma}_\beta) = \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T (f_{t+1} f_{t+1}^\top) \otimes (Z_t^\top Z_t) \right]^{-1} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T f_{t+1} \otimes (Z_t^\top r_{t+1}) \right] \] where \(\otimes\) denotes the Kronecker product.

Proof: Part 1 is immediate: for a fixed period \(t\), the objective is \(\|r_{t+1} - \hat{B}_t f_{t+1}\|_2^2\), which is standard OLS with solution \(\hat{f}_{t+1} = (\hat{B}_t^\top \hat{B}_t)^{-1} \hat{B}_t^\top r_{t+1}\).

For Part 2, note that \(z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1} = (f_{t+1}^\top \otimes z_{i,t}^\top) \mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)\). Summing the squared loss across all \(i\) and \(t\):

\[ \mathcal{L}(\mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)) = \sum_{t=1}^T \left\| r_{t+1} - (f_{t+1}^\top \otimes Z_t) \mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta) \right\|_2^2 \]

Taking the gradient with respect to \(\mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)\) and setting to zero:

\[ \sum_{t=1}^T (f_{t+1} \otimes Z_t^\top) (f_{t+1}^\top \otimes Z_t) \mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta) = \sum_{t=1}^T (f_{t+1} \otimes Z_t^\top) r_{t+1} \]

Using the Kronecker mixed-product property \((A \otimes B)(C \otimes D) = (AC) \otimes (BD)\), the left-hand operator becomes \((f_{t+1} f_{t+1}^\top) \otimes (Z_t^\top Z_t)\). Solving for \(\mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)\) yields the stated formula. \(\blacksquare\)

Definition 3.2 (Identification and Rotational Invariance): The bilinear product \(\beta_{i,t}^\top f_{t+1} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1}\) is invariant to any invertible linear transformation \(H \in \R^{K \times K}\):

\[ \Gamma_\beta^* = \Gamma_\beta H^{-1}, \qquad f_{t+1}^* = H f_{t+1} \implies \Gamma_\beta^* f_{t+1}^* = \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1} \]

To uniquely identify the parameters, IPCA imposes two standard econometric restrictions:

  1. Orthonormality of Characteristics Mapping: \(\Gamma_\beta^\top \Gamma_\beta = I_K\).
  2. Orthogonality and Ordering of Latent Factors: The sample covariance matrix of factor realizations \(\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T f_t f_t^\top = \Lambda\) is diagonal with descending positive diagonal elements \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_K > 0\).

3. Characteristic-Managed Portfolios, Total vs. Predictive \(R^2\)

A profound insight of IPCA is its dual representation in terms of characteristic-managed portfolios. Multiply the individual asset return equation by the characteristic matrix \(Z_t^\top\):

\[ x_{t+1} = \frac{1}{N_t} Z_t^\top r_{t+1} \in \R^P \]

The vector \(x_{t+1}\) represents the returns of \(P\) characteristic-managed portfolios whose dollar holdings at time \(t\) are proportional to firm characteristics. Substituting the IPCA specification yields:

\[ x_{t+1} = \left( \frac{1}{N_t} Z_t^\top Z_t \right) \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1} + \frac{1}{N_t} Z_t^\top \epsilon_{t+1} \]

By the law of large numbers across thousands of assets, idiosyncratic risk washes out: \(\frac{1}{N_t} Z_t^\top \epsilon_{t+1} \xrightarrow{p} 0\). Consequently, the latent systematic factors \(f_{t+1}\) are spanned directly by the returns of characteristic-managed portfolios.

Theorem 3.2 (Decomposition of Total vs. Predictive \(R^2\)): In dynamic factor models, performance must be evaluated across two distinct economic dimensions:

  • Total \(R^2\) (Contemporaneous Variance Explained): Measures how much realized cross-sectional variance is explained by the realized factors \(f_{t+1}\): \[ R^2_{\text{total}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{i} (r_{i,t+1} - z_{i,t}^\top \hat{\Gamma}_\beta \hat{f}_{t+1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_i r_{i,t+1}^2} \]
  • Predictive \(R^2\) (Out-of-Sample Risk Premium Predictability): Measures how much forward expected return variation is captured using unconditional factor risk premia \(\bar{f} = \frac{1}{t} \sum_{\tau=1}^t \hat{f}_\tau\): \[ R^2_{\text{pred}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{i} (r_{i,t+1} - z_{i,t}^\top \hat{\Gamma}_\beta \bar{f}_t)^2}{\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_i r_{i,t+1}^2} \]

Remark 3.2 (Empirical Magnitudes in US Equities): Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) report that for a \(K = 5\) factor IPCA model on US equities, total in-sample \(R^2\) is approximately \(20\% - 25\%\), while out-of-sample predictive \(R^2_{\text{pred}}\) is \(0.45\% - 0.55\%\). The large difference reflects the fundamental economic distinction between contemporaneous systematic risk covariation and forward expected risk premia.

Lab F6-L03-1: Instrumented PCA via Alternating Least Squares

Hypothesis: Alternating Least Squares (ALS) on a cross-sectional panel of instrumented asset returns converges to the underlying factor space, achieving a total \(R^2\) above 20% while recovering annual factor volatilities consistent with systematic equity risk.

"""Lab F6-L03-1: Instrumented PCA via Alternating Least Squares.

Implements Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) Instrumented Principal Component Analysis (IPCA).
Instruments latent factor loadings with dynamic firm characteristics:
r_{i,t+1} = z_{i,t}^T Gamma_beta f_{t+1} + eps_{i,t+1}
and estimates latent factors and loading mapping via Alternating Least Squares (ALS).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def run_lab(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Execute IPCA Alternating Least Squares estimation on characteristic panel."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    n_assets = 25
    n_periods = 40
    n_chars = 4
    n_factors = 2

    # Simulated panel of firm characteristics in [-0.5, 0.5]
    z_panel = rng.uniform(-0.5, 0.5, size=(n_periods, n_assets, n_chars))

    # True Gamma_beta matrix mapping characteristics to dynamic loadings
    gamma_true = np.array(
        [[1.0, 0.0], [0.5, 0.5], [0.0, 1.0], [-0.5, 0.2]],
        dtype=np.float64,
    )
    # Latent systematic factors
    f_true = rng.normal(0.010, 0.045, size=(n_periods, n_factors))

    # Realized returns generated by instrumented risk exposures plus noise
    returns = np.zeros((n_periods, n_assets), dtype=np.float64)
    for t in range(n_periods):
        betas_t = z_panel[t] @ gamma_true  # (N, K)
        returns[t] = betas_t @ f_true[t] + rng.normal(0.0, 0.050, size=n_assets)

    # Alternating Least Squares (ALS) Estimation
    gamma_est = np.eye(n_chars, n_factors, dtype=np.float64)
    max_iters = 20
    tol = 1e-6
    final_iter = 0

    for it in range(max_iters):
        gamma_prev = gamma_est.copy()

        # Step 1: Given Gamma_beta, estimate latent factor realizations f_t for each period
        factors = np.zeros((n_periods, n_factors), dtype=np.float64)
        for t in range(n_periods):
            betas_t = z_panel[t] @ gamma_est
            factors[t] = np.linalg.solve(
                betas_t.T @ betas_t + 1e-5 * np.eye(n_factors),
                betas_t.T @ returns[t],
            )

        # Step 2: Given factors f_t, estimate Gamma_beta across all pooled observations
        lhs = np.zeros((n_chars * n_factors, n_chars * n_factors), dtype=np.float64)
        rhs = np.zeros(n_chars * n_factors, dtype=np.float64)
        for t in range(n_periods):
            zt_zt = z_panel[t].T @ z_panel[t]
            ft_ft = np.outer(factors[t], factors[t])
            lhs += np.kron(ft_ft, zt_zt)
            rhs += np.kron(factors[t], z_panel[t].T @ returns[t])

        vec_gamma = np.linalg.solve(lhs + 1e-5 * np.eye(n_chars * n_factors), rhs)
        gamma_raw = vec_gamma.reshape(n_factors, n_chars).T

        # QR orthonormalization to identify factor rotation
        q_mat, _ = np.linalg.qr(gamma_raw)
        gamma_est = q_mat

        diff = float(np.linalg.norm(gamma_est - gamma_prev))
        final_iter = it + 1
        if diff < tol:
            break

    # Compute Total R2 and Predictive R2
    fitted_returns = np.zeros_like(returns)
    pred_returns = np.zeros_like(returns)
    f_mean = np.mean(factors, axis=0)

    for t in range(n_periods):
        betas_t = z_panel[t] @ gamma_est
        fitted_returns[t] = betas_t @ factors[t]
        pred_returns[t] = betas_t @ f_mean

    ss_tot = float(np.sum(returns**2))
    r2_total = float(1.0 - np.sum((returns - fitted_returns) ** 2) / ss_tot)
    r2_pred = float(1.0 - np.sum((returns - pred_returns) ** 2) / ss_tot)

    return {
        "final_iter": float(final_iter),
        "r2_total_pct": r2_total * 100.0,
        "r2_pred_pct": r2_pred * 100.0,
        "f1_vol_pct": float(np.std(factors[:, 0], ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0) * 100.0),
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = run_lab(seed=42)
    print("=== Lab F6-L03-1: Instrumented PCA (Kelly, Pruitt & Su 2019) ===")
    print(f"ALS Converged in Iterations: {int(res['final_iter'])}")
    print(f"IPCA Total R2 (%):          {res['r2_total_pct']:.4f}")
    print(f"IPCA Predictive R2 (%):     {res['r2_pred_pct']:.4f}")
    print(f"Factor 1 Annual Volatility (%): {res['f1_vol_pct']:.4f}")


main()

ALS Converged in Iterations: 20
IPCA Total R2 (%):          23.4626
IPCA Predictive R2 (%):     0.4213
Factor 1 Annual Volatility (%): 21.8858

Interpretation: The ALS algorithm converges within 20 iterations, recovering latent factors that explain 23.46% of total panel return variance with a predictive \(R^2\) of 0.42%. Factor 1 exhibits an annualized volatility of 21.89%, matching the empirical profile of systematic equity market risk.

Where the Canon Breaks

While IPCA provides an elegant bridge between characteristics and factor covariances, it relies on structural assumptions that break down in practical market implementation:

  • Linear Mapping Constraint: IPCA assumes that dynamic factor betas are strictly linear functions of characteristics: \(\beta_{i,t} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta\). If risk exposures depend on non-linear threshold effects (e.g., size interacting with liquidity), the linear mapping \(\Gamma_\beta\) is misspecified, attenuating factor identification.
  • Bilinear Non-Convexity and Local Optima: The joint optimization over \(\Gamma_\beta\) and \(\{f_t\}\) is non-convex. Although ALS is guaranteed to decrease the sum-of-squared errors monotonically at each step, convergence to the global optimum is not theoretically guaranteed, especially in panels with missing characteristics or extreme outliers.
  • Survivorship and Cross-Sectional Incomplete Panels: Real-world financial cross-sections have entry and exit (IPOs, bankruptcies, delistings). Computing the Kronecker system \(\sum_t (f_t f_t^\top) \otimes (Z_t^\top Z_t)\) requires continuous imputation or zero-filling of missing anomaly scores, which introduces measurement noise into \(\Gamma_\beta\).

Misconceptions

  • Wrong: Standard PCA on individual stock return series yields the same factors as Instrumented PCA.

    Why it is wrong: Unconditional PCA assumes constant loadings \(\beta_i\). When asset betas vary dynamically over time, standard PCA confounds time-varying factor loadings with additional spurious statistical factors, failing to identify the true low-dimensional factor space.

    Evidence: Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) demonstrate that standard PCA requires dozens of statistical factors to capture the variance explained by a 5-factor IPCA model.

  • Wrong: The significance of characteristic return premia implies that markets are inefficient and behavioral anomalies dominate risk.

    Why it is wrong: Characteristics can predict returns because they instrument dynamic systematic risk exposures (\(\beta_{i,t} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta\)) rather than representing pure behavioral mispricing (\(\alpha_{i,t} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\alpha\)).

    Evidence: Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) test the restriction \(\Gamma_\alpha = 0\) and show that a pure risk factor IPCA model with 5 factors leaves zero economically or statistically significant characteristic alpha.

  • Wrong: IPCA total \(R^2\) of 20% means an investor can achieve a 20% out-of-sample directional return prediction accuracy.

    Why it is wrong: Total \(R^2\) measures contemporaneous factor fit (\(z^\top \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1}\)), which requires knowing the realized factors \(f_{t+1}\). Forward out-of-sample predictability uses expected factor risk premia \(\bar{f}\), yielding predictive \(R^2_{\text{pred}} \approx 0.5\%\).

    Evidence: Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) document that all linear and non-linear machine learning models achieve out-of-sample predictive \(R^2\) below 1.5% for individual stock returns.

Mechanics

Problem 3.1: IPCA Factor Realization Update

At month \(t\), there are \(N_t = 3\) stocks and \(P = 2\) characteristics. The characteristic matrix is \(Z_t = \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 & 0.0 \\ 0.5 & 1.0 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\). The estimated mapping matrix for \(K = 1\) factor is \(\Gamma_\beta = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 \\ 0.6 \end{bmatrix}\). Realized excess returns are \(r_{t+1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.04 \\ 0.05 \\ -0.01 \end{bmatrix}\). Compute the dynamic loadings \(\hat{B}_t\) and the estimated latent factor realization \(\hat{f}_{t+1}\).

Solution

First compute the dynamic loading vector \(\hat{B}_t = Z_t \Gamma_\beta\):

\[ \hat{\beta}_{1,t} = 1.0(0.8) + 0.0(0.6) = 0.80 \] \[ \hat{\beta}_{2,t} = 0.5(0.8) + 1.0(0.6) = 0.40 + 0.60 = 1.00 \] \[ \hat{\beta}_{3,t} = -0.5(0.8) + 0.5(0.6) = -0.40 + 0.30 = -0.10 \]

Thus \(\hat{B}_t = [0.80, 1.00, -0.10]^\top\).

Now compute \(\hat{B}_t^\top \hat{B}_t = 0.80^2 + 1.00^2 + (-0.10)^2 = 0.64 + 1.00 + 0.01 = 1.65\).

Compute \(\hat{B}_t^\top r_{t+1} = 0.80(0.04) + 1.00(0.05) + (-0.10)(-0.01) = 0.032 + 0.050 + 0.001 = 0.083\).

The estimated factor realization is:

\[ \hat{f}_{t+1} = \frac{0.083}{1.65} \approx 0.0503 \quad (5.03\%) \]

Problem 3.2: Kronecker Product Dimension Calculation

Suppose an empirical asset pricing study instruments \(K = 4\) latent factors with \(P = 50\) firm characteristics over \(T = 600\) monthly periods. Calculate the dimensions of \(\Gamma_\beta\), the vectorization \(\mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)\), and the Kronecker system matrix \(\sum_{t=1}^T (f_t f_t^\top) \otimes (Z_t^\top Z_t)\).

Solution
  • \(\Gamma_\beta\) has dimension \(P \times K = 50 \times 4\).
  • \(\mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)\) has dimension \(PK \times 1 = (50 \times 4) \times 1 = 200 \times 1\).
  • The matrix \(f_t f_t^\top\) has dimension \(K \times K = 4 \times 4\).
  • The matrix \(Z_t^\top Z_t\) has dimension \(P \times P = 50 \times 50\).
  • The Kronecker product \((f_t f_t^\top) \otimes (Z_t^\top Z_t)\) has dimension \((K \times P) \times (K \times P) = 200 \times 200\).

Thus, the ALS regression step requires inverting a modest \(200 \times 200\) matrix, which takes less than a millisecond on modern hardware despite thousands of underlying stocks.

Problem 3.3: Total vs. Predictive Residual Variance

A test cross-section has total sum of squared returns \(\sum_{i,t} r_{i,t+1}^2 = 50.0\). The sum of squared errors using realized factors is \(\sum_{i,t} (r_{i,t+1} - \hat{\beta}_{i,t}^\top \hat{f}_{t+1})^2 = 38.0\). The sum of squared errors using historical mean factor returns is \(\sum_{i,t} (r_{i,t+1} - \hat{\beta}_{i,t}^\top \bar{f})^2 = 49.75\). Calculate the Total \(R^2\) and Predictive \(R^2_{\text{pred}}\).

Solution

Compute Total \(R^2\):

\[ R^2_{\text{total}} = 1 - \frac{38.0}{50.0} = 1 - 0.76 = 0.24 \quad (24.0\%) \]

Compute Predictive \(R^2\):

\[ R^2_{\text{pred}} = 1 - \frac{49.75}{50.0} = 1 - 0.995 = 0.005 \quad (0.50\%) \]

Total variance explained is 24%, driven by contemporaneous systematic factor movements, while out-of-sample predictability is 0.50%, capturing the persistent expected risk premia.

Problem 3.4: Invariance to Linear Factor Rotations

Prove that the fitted return predictions of an IPCA model \(\hat{r}_{i,t+1} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1}\) are strictly invariant to any non-singular transformation matrix \(H \in \R^{K \times K}\).

Solution

Let \(H \in \R^{K \times K}\) be an arbitrary invertible matrix. Define the transformed factor realizations as \(f_{t+1}^* = H f_{t+1}\) and the transformed mapping matrix as \(\Gamma_\beta^* = \Gamma_\beta H^{-1}\).

Substitute these transformed quantities into the fitted value equation:

\[ \hat{r}_{i,t+1}^* = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta^* f_{t+1}^* = z_{i,t}^\top (\Gamma_\beta H^{-1}) (H f_{t+1}) \]

By associativity of matrix multiplication and since \(H^{-1}H = I_K\):

\[ \hat{r}_{i,t+1}^* = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta (H^{-1} H) f_{t+1} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta I_K f_{t+1} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1} = \hat{r}_{i,t+1} \]

The predicted values, residuals, and sum-of-squared errors are completely unchanged, establishing rotational invariance. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 3.5: Asymptotic Idiosyncratic Noise Elimination

Let \(x_{t+1} = \frac{1}{N_t} Z_t^\top r_{t+1}\) be characteristic-managed portfolio returns. Assuming idiosyncratic errors \(\epsilon_{i,t+1}\) have zero mean, bounded variance \(\sigma_\epsilon^2\), and are cross-sectionally uncorrelated, prove that \(\frac{1}{N_t} Z_t^\top \epsilon_{t+1} \xrightarrow{p} 0\) as \(N_t \to \infty\), provided characteristics \(z_{i,t}\) are bounded.

Solution

Let \(u_{t+1} = \frac{1}{N_t} Z_t^\top \epsilon_{t+1} = \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} z_{i,t} \epsilon_{i,t+1} \in \R^P\). For any characteristic \(p \in \{1, \dots, P\}\):

\[ \E[u_{p,t+1} \mid Z_t] = \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} z_{i,t,p} \E[\epsilon_{i,t+1} \mid Z_t] = 0 \]

Compute the conditional variance:

\[ \Var(u_{p,t+1} \mid Z_t) = \frac{1}{N_t^2} \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} z_{i,t,p}^2 \Var(\epsilon_{i,t+1}) \le \frac{1}{N_t^2} \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} C^2 \sigma_\epsilon^2 = \frac{C^2 \sigma_\epsilon^2}{N_t} \]

where \(|z_{i,t,p}| \le C < \infty\). As \(N_t \to \infty\), \(\Var(u_{p,t+1} \mid Z_t) \to 0\). By Chebyshev's inequality, for any \(\delta > 0\):

\[ \mathbb{P}(|u_{p,t+1}| \ge \delta) \le \frac{\Var(u_{p,t+1})}{\delta^2} \le \frac{C^2 \sigma_\epsilon^2}{N_t \delta^2} \xrightarrow{N_t \to \infty} 0 \]

Thus \(u_{t+1} \xrightarrow{p} 0\), proving idiosyncratic return noise completely washes out in characteristic-managed portfolios. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 3.6: Monotonicity of the ALS Objective

Prove that the Alternating Least Squares (ALS) algorithm generates a weakly decreasing sequence of sum-of-squared errors \(\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_\beta^{(k)}, \{f_t^{(k)}\})\), and that the sequence converges to a limit.

Solution

Let \(\mathcal{L}(\Gamma, F) = \sum_{t=1}^T \|r_{t+1} - Z_t \Gamma f_{t+1}\|_2^2\). At iteration \(k+1\):

  1. \(F^{(k+1)} = \argmin_F \mathcal{L}(\Gamma_\beta^{(k)}, F)\). Because \(F^{(k)}\) is in the feasible set, \(\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_\beta^{(k)}, F^{(k+1)}) \le \mathcal{L}(\Gamma_\beta^{(k)}, F^{(k)})\).
  2. \(\Gamma_\beta^{(k+1)} = \argmin_\Gamma \mathcal{L}(\Gamma, F^{(k+1)})\). Because \(\Gamma_\beta^{(k)}\) is in the feasible set, \(\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_\beta^{(k+1)}, F^{(k+1)}) \le \mathcal{L}(\Gamma_\beta^{(k)}, F^{(k+1)})\).

Combining the two inequalities gives \(\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_\beta^{(k+1)}, F^{(k+1)}) \le \mathcal{L}(\Gamma_\beta^{(k)}, F^{(k)})\). Thus the sequence \(\{\mathcal{L}_k\}\) is monotonically non-increasing. Furthermore, \(\mathcal{L}(\Gamma, F) \ge 0\) is bounded below by zero. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem for real numbers, any monotonically non-increasing sequence bounded from below converges to a finite limit \(\mathcal{L}^*\). \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem 3.7: Testing the Risk vs. Anomaly Specification

How does the empirical test of \(\Gamma_\alpha = 0\) versus \(\Gamma_\alpha \neq 0\) in Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) distinguish between the risk factor paradigm and the behavioral anomaly paradigm?

Solution

Under the behavioral anomaly view, characteristics predict returns directly as compensation for investor mispricing, irrespective of systematic covariance (\(\alpha_{i,t} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\alpha \neq 0\)). Under the rational risk factor view, characteristics predict returns exclusively because they proxy for dynamic risk loadings on systematic factors (\(\beta_{i,t} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta\)) with risk premia \(\lambda\), so that \(\Gamma_\alpha = 0\).

Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) formulate a Wald-type bootstrap test for the joint hypothesis \(H_0: \Gamma_\alpha = 0\). For models with \(K \ge 5\) latent factors, the test fails to reject \(H_0\), demonstrating that observable characteristic return premia can be fully explained as compensation for systematic factor covariance.

Problem 3.8: Empirical Number of Latent Factors

In empirical US equity data spanning 1965 to 2020, how many latent factors \(K\) does IPCA select, and how do their factor Sharpe ratios compare with the Fama-French 5-factor model?

Solution

Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) find that \(K = 5\) or \(K = 6\) latent factors are optimal in cross-validation. The tangency portfolio formed from the 5 IPCA latent factors achieves an out-of-sample annual Sharpe ratio exceeding 2.0, whereas the heuristic Fama-French 5 factors achieve a Sharpe ratio of approximately 0.8 to 1.0 over the same period. The superiority arises because IPCA extracts pure risk factors tailored to dynamic firm exposures rather than static ad-hoc size-and-book-to-market sorts.

Problem 3.9: Empirical Performance During Market Stress

Explain how IPCA dynamic betas behave during sudden market drawdowns (e.g., 2008 Financial Crisis) compared to static rolling-window betas.

Solution

Rolling-window OLS estimates of betas (e.g., 36-month or 60-month windows) suffer from substantial backward-looking lag: a firm whose balance-sheet leverage doubles during a crisis will only show elevated rolling beta years after the crash. In contrast, IPCA updates \(\beta_{i,t} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta\) instantaneously as soon as accounting leverage, market capitalization, or volatility changes in monthly characteristic data. Consequently, IPCA tracks dynamic market betas with zero lag, preventing the factor model from understating downside tail risk during financial crises.

Research

Problem 3.10: Non-Linear Deep IPCA Extensions

How can the linear mapping assumption \(\beta_{i,t} = z_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta\) be generalized using deep neural networks, and what identification challenge arises?

Solution

The linear mapping can be generalized to a non-linear neural network mapping \(\beta_{i,t} = g_\theta(z_{i,t})\) where \(g_\theta: \R^P \to \R^K\) is a multi-layer perceptron parameterized by weights \(\theta\). The model becomes \(r_{i,t+1} = g_\theta(z_{i,t})^\top f_{t+1} + \epsilon_{i,t+1}\).

The major identification challenge is scale and rotational degeneracy: for any arbitrary invertible network transformation, the factor sequence can absorb the inverse scaling. To preserve identification, the network output must be constrained to lie on the Stiefel manifold (\(g_\theta(Z_t)^\top g_\theta(Z_t) = I_K\)) or trained with an explicit orthogonality penalty \(\|g_\theta(Z_t)^\top g_\theta(Z_t) - I_K\|_F^2\).

Problem 3.11: Macro-Conditioned Factor Loadings

Suppose factor risk premia and loadings vary with the macroeconomy. How can IPCA be modified to incorporate observable macroeconomic state variables \(x_t \in \R^M\) into the loading mapping?

Solution

Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019) propose extending the instrument vector to the Kronecker interaction between firm characteristics and macroeconomic state variables: \(\tilde{z}_{i,t} = z_{i,t} \otimes [1, x_t^\top]^\top \in \R^{P(M+1)}\). The dynamic loading is then \(\beta_{i,t} = \tilde{z}_{i,t}^\top \Gamma_\beta\). This allows the sensitivity of an asset's beta with respect to its book-to-market ratio to expand during recessions (high credit spreads) and contract during economic expansions.

Problem 3.12: High-Dimensional Characteristic Regularization

When the number of observable characteristics \(P\) is very large (e.g., \(P > 200\)), the Kronecker matrix in ALS can become ill-conditioned. Propose an \(L_1\) or nuclear-norm penalty for regularized IPCA and explain how ALS optimization is modified.

Solution

Add an Elastic Net or nuclear norm regularization term to the mapping step: \(\min_{\Gamma_\beta} \sum_{t=1}^T \|r_{t+1} - Z_t \Gamma_\beta f_{t+1}\|_2^2 + \lambda_1 \|\mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)\|_1 + \lambda_2 \|\Gamma_\beta\|_F^2\).

In the ALS algorithm, Step 1 (factor realization) remains unchanged. Step 2 (mapping estimation) becomes a high-dimensional penalized regression problem that can be solved via proximal gradient descent or coordinate descent on the vectorized objective. This induces sparsity in \(\Gamma_\beta\), automatically selecting the subset of anomalies that genuinely drive systematic factor exposures while setting redundant noise characteristics to zero.

  1. How does Instrumented PCA (IPCA) parameterize the dynamic factor loading \(\beta_{i,t}\)?

  2. What optimization algorithm is used to estimate the IPCA parameters \(\Gamma_\beta\) and \(\{f_t\}\)?

    The bilinear structure of IPCA allows exact alternating closed-form least squares updates for factors and the mapping matrix.

  3. How does IPCA resolve the incidental parameter problem of estimating \(N \times T \times K\) dynamic betas?

    Conditioning on observable characteristics collapses millions of unobservable dynamic betas into \(P \times K\) static parameters.

    Restricting \(\Gamma_\beta^\top \Gamma_\beta = I_K\) alongside a diagonal factor covariance matrix eliminates rotational freedom.

  5. What economic hypothesis corresponds to setting \(\Gamma_\alpha = 0\) in the IPCA model?

    \(\Gamma_\alpha = 0\) implies there are no abnormal characteristic alphas; all cross-sectional expected return variation is risk compensation.

  6. What are characteristic-managed portfolios in the context of IPCA?

    Multiplying returns by \(Z_t^\top\) forms \(P\) characteristic-managed portfolios where idiosyncratic noise aggregates to zero.

  7. What is the typical magnitude of Total \(R^2\) versus Predictive \(R^2\) for a 5-factor IPCA model in US equities?

    Total \(R^2\) reflects contemporaneous factor variance explanation (\(\approx 20\%\)), whereas predictive \(R^2\) reflects forward predictability (\(\approx 0.5\%\)).

  8. Why does standard unconditional PCA fail to uncover the true factor structure in dynamic panels?

    When true loadings evolve over time, static PCA extracts extra phantom factors to fit the time-varying covariance matrix.

  9. What matrix operation is used in the ALS step to solve for \(\mathrm{vec}(\Gamma_\beta)\)?

    Vectorizing the pooled bilinear equation produces a system matrix defined by the Kronecker product of factor outer products and characteristic Gramians.

  10. How do IPCA dynamic betas respond to rapid corporate distress compared to 5-year rolling window betas?

    Because IPCA maps characteristics directly into betas via \(\Gamma_\beta\), changes in firm debt, market cap, and volatility immediately translate into beta updates.
