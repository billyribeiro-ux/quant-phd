Overparameterization, Double Descent & the Complexity Debate

Classical econometric doctrine mandates parsimony: models with more parameters than observations inevitably suffer from severe overfitting, exploded sampling variance, and catastrophic out-of-sample breakdown. Modern statistical learning theory, however, has unveiled the phenomenon of 'double descent,' wherein heavily overparameterized models (\(P \gg N\)) that perfectly interpolate noisy training data achieve superior out-of-sample generalization via implicit \(L_2\) norm shrinkage. In this lesson, we analyze the virtue-of-complexity claim, formalized by Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024). We derive the minimum-norm interpolator in dual Hilbert space, study the spectral mechanics of random feature expansions, and examine the vigorous debate ignited by the Nagel (2025) critique regarding whether complexity is truly virtuous or merely a substitute for explicit regularization.

  • Prerequisites: L01 Cross-Sectional Return Prediction & Penalized Linear Models (concept ml-return-prediction).
  • Introduces Concept: virtue-of-complexity.
  • Debate: Does complexity help return prediction?
  • Core Concepts: Double descent, interpolation threshold, minimum-norm interpolators, dual ridge regression, implicit shrinkage, random feature models, the Nagel critique.
  • Empirical Benchmark: Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024), Nagel (2025), Hastie et al. (2019).

Why It Matters

For decades, financial economists restricted models to a handful of factors to avoid overfitting. The discovery of double descent reveals that massive neural networks and random feature models can achieve superior prediction risk through implicit shrinkage, challenging classical parsimony.

1. The Classical U-Curve vs. Modern Double Descent

In classical statistical learning, model complexity is governed by the bias-variance tradeoff. Let \(M\) denote sample size and \(P\) denote the number of parameters. In the underparameterized regime (\(P < M\)), increasing \(P\) decreases approximation bias but inflates parameter estimation variance, producing the familiar U-shaped out-of-sample test error curve that reaches a minimum at an intermediate capacity \(P^* \ll M\).

As the complexity ratio \(c = P / M \to 1^-\), the empirical Gram matrix \(Z^\top Z\) approaches singularity. The smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\min}(Z^\top Z) \to 0\), causing estimation variance \(\sigma^2 \tr((Z^\top Z)^{-1})\) to explode asymptotically toward infinity at the interpolation threshold \(P = M\).

Definition 5.1 (Minimum \(L_2\) Norm Interpolator): In the overparameterized regime (\(P > M\)), the linear system \(Z \beta = y\) is underdetermined with infinitely many exact solutions satisfying zero training loss. The ridgeless minimum-norm interpolator selects the unique coefficient vector with minimal Euclidean norm:

\[ \hat{\beta}_{\text{MN}} = \argmin_{\beta \in \R^P} \|\beta\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad Z \beta = y \]

Using the method of Lagrange multipliers, the solution is given in dual coordinates by:

\[ \hat{\beta}_{\text{MN}} = Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-1} y \]

where \(Z Z^\top \in \R^{M \times M}\) is invertible almost surely when the features are continuously distributed.

Theorem 5.1 (Double Descent Risk Profile): Let \(y = z^\top \beta^* + \epsilon\) with noise variance \(\sigma_\epsilon^2\). For ridgeless least squares across complexity ratio \(\gamma = P / M\):

  • As \(\gamma \to 1^-\), prediction risk explodes: \(\lim_{\gamma \to 1^-} \E[(\hat{y} - y)^2] = \infty\).
  • For \(\gamma > 1\), as complexity expands further (\(\gamma \to \infty\)), the out-of-sample prediction risk monotonically descends, converging to an asymptotic limit that can be strictly lower than the classical underparameterized optimal risk: \[ \lim_{\gamma \to \infty} \E[(\hat{y} - y)^2] < \min_{P < M} \E[(\hat{y}_{\text{OLS}} - y)^2] \]

Remark 5.1 (The Virtue of Complexity Formulation): This empirical and theoretical phenomenon forms the core of Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024): in financial return prediction, models with millions of parameters (such as random Fourier feature expansions or deep neural networks) outperform parsimonious linear models because overparameterization provides the capacity to capture non-linear market interactions while the minimum-norm constraint provides implicit shrinkage.

2. Mathematical Mechanics of Overparameterized Shrinkage

To understand why out-of-sample risk descends when \(P \gg M\), consider a Random Feature Model. Let \(x_{i} \in \R^K\) be a base vector of firm characteristics. We generate a large projection of \(P\) random non-linear features:

\[ z_{i} = \sigma(W x_{i}) \in \R^P, \qquad W_{j,k} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1/K) \]

where \(\sigma(\cdot)\) is a non-linear activation function (such as ReLU or GeLU).

Theorem 5.2 (Spectral Mechanics of Implicit Regularization): Consider the dual Gram matrix \(K = \frac{1}{P} Z Z^\top \in \R^{M \times M}\). By the Marchenko-Pastur law from random matrix theory, as \(P, M \to \infty\) with \(\gamma = P / M > 1\), the empirical eigenvalue distribution of \(K\) has support:

\[ \lambda \in \left[ (1 - \gamma^{-1/2})^2, \, (1 + \gamma^{-1/2})^2 \right] \]

In particular, the minimum eigenvalue satisfies \(\lambda_{\min}(K) \to (1 - 1/\sqrt{\gamma})^2 > 0\). As complexity \(\gamma \to \infty\):

\[ \lambda_{\min}(K) \to 1, \qquad \lambda_{\max}(K) \to 1, \qquad K \xrightarrow{p} I_M \]

Proof: In the dual formulation, \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{MN}} = Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-1} y = \frac{1}{P} Z^\top K^{-1} y\). The estimation variance of the prediction on a new test point \(z_{\text{test}}\) is:

\[ \Var(\hat{f}(z_{\text{test}})) = \sigma_\epsilon^2 z_{\text{test}}^\top \E[Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-2} Z] z_{\text{test}} = \frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{P} z_{\text{test}}^\top \E[Z^\top K^{-2} Z] z_{\text{test}} \]

When \(\gamma \approx 1\), \(\lambda_{\min}(K) = (1 - 1/\sqrt{\gamma})^2 \to 0\), causing \(K^{-2}\) to explode with eigenvalues scaling as \(\mathcal{O}((1 - \sqrt{\gamma})^{-4})\). However, when \(\gamma \to \infty\), \(K \to I_M\), and the condition number \(\kappa(K) \to 1\). The matrix inverse \(K^{-1}\) becomes perfectly conditioned, and the factor \(1/P\) shrinks the variance of individual parameter estimates toward zero. Thus, massive overparameterization acts as an implicit shrinkage operator that tames estimation variance without explicitly setting a ridge penalty. \(\blacksquare\)

3. The Asset Pricing Complexity Debate: Kelly et al. vs. Nagel

The virtue of complexity claim has triggered one of the most intense methodological debates in modern quantitative finance. The central disagreement centers on whether the superior performance of overparameterized models is a genuine structural advantage or an artifact of flawed benchmarking.

Definition 5.2 (The Nagel (2025) Equivalence Theorem): Nagel (2025) proves that any ridgeless minimum-norm interpolator in an overparameterized random feature space is asymptotically equivalent to an explicitly regularized Ridge regression in a lower-dimensional feature space:

\[ \hat{f}_{\text{MN}}(x) \xrightarrow{P \to \infty} \hat{f}_{\text{Ridge}}(x; \lambda^*) \]

where the implicit regularization parameter \(\lambda^* > 0\) is determined solely by the activation function and the complexity ratio \(\gamma\).

Remark 5.2 (Nagel's Empirical Critique): Nagel (2025) demonstrates that the dramatic 'descent' observed in overparameterized models occurs only when compared to unregularized OLS (which explodes at \(P = M\)). When an econometrician applies standard, optimal \(L_2\) Ridge shrinkage (\(\lambda > 0\)) directly to the base characteristics, the double descent peak completely disappears. In fact, a low-dimensional Ridge regression with 8 to 15 features matches or outperforms million-parameter random feature interpolators, while retaining full economic interpretability and dramatically lower execution turnover.

Lab F6-L05-1: Double Descent and the Virtue of Complexity

Hypothesis: In an overparameterized random feature regression, ridgeless minimum-norm out-of-sample MSE exhibits double descent—exploding at the interpolation threshold \(\gamma = P/N = 1.0\) and descending as \(\gamma\) increases to 4.0—whereas an explicitly regularized Ridge estimator eliminates the peak entirely.

"""Lab F6-L05-1: Double Descent and the Virtue of Complexity in Asset Pricing.

Simulates random feature regressions across complexity ratios gamma = P / N in [0.25, 4.0].
Demonstrates the Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024) double descent phenomenon for ridgeless
minimum-norm interpolators and tests the Nagel (2025) critique on explicit regularization.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def run_lab(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, list[float]]:
    """Execute double-descent complexity sweep across parameter ratios."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    n_samples_train = 300
    n_samples_test = 300
    n_features_base = 8

    # Generate underlying predictive signals
    x_train = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n_samples_train, n_features_base))
    x_test = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n_samples_test, n_features_base))

    beta_sparse = np.zeros(n_features_base, dtype=np.float64)
    beta_sparse[:3] = np.array([0.030, -0.020, 0.020], dtype=np.float64)

    y_train = x_train @ beta_sparse + rng.normal(0.0, 0.050, size=n_samples_train)
    y_test = x_test @ beta_sparse + rng.normal(0.0, 0.050, size=n_samples_test)

    gammas = [0.25, 0.50, 0.90, 1.00, 1.50, 2.50, 4.00]
    ridgeless_mse: list[float] = []
    regularized_mse: list[float] = []

    for gamma in gammas:
        d_proj = max(2, int(gamma * n_samples_train))
        w_mat = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0 / math.sqrt(n_features_base), size=(n_features_base, d_proj))

        z_train = np.maximum(0.0, x_train @ w_mat)
        z_test = np.maximum(0.0, x_test @ w_mat)

        # 1. Ridgeless minimum-norm interpolator
        if d_proj <= n_samples_train:
            gram = z_train.T @ z_train + 1e-5 * np.eye(d_proj)
            beta_rl = np.linalg.solve(gram, z_train.T @ y_train)
        else:
            dual = z_train @ z_train.T + 1e-5 * np.eye(n_samples_train)
            alpha_dual = np.linalg.solve(dual, y_train)
            beta_rl = z_train.T @ alpha_dual

        pred_rl = z_test @ beta_rl
        mse_rl = float(np.mean((y_test - pred_rl) ** 2))
        ridgeless_mse.append(mse_rl)

        # 2. Explicitly regularized ridge estimator (optimal shrinkage per Nagel 2025)
        reg_penalty = 0.05 * n_samples_train
        gram_reg = z_train.T @ z_train + reg_penalty * np.eye(d_proj)
        beta_reg = np.linalg.solve(gram_reg, z_train.T @ y_train)
        pred_reg = z_test @ beta_reg
        mse_reg = float(np.mean((y_test - pred_reg) ** 2))
        regularized_mse.append(mse_reg)

    return {
        "gammas": gammas,
        "ridgeless_mse": ridgeless_mse,
        "regularized_mse": regularized_mse,
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = run_lab(seed=42)
    print("=== Lab F6-L05-1: Double Descent & Virtue of Complexity ===")
    for g, rl, reg in zip(res["gammas"], res["ridgeless_mse"], res["regularized_mse"], strict=True):
        print(f"gamma = {g:4.2f} | Ridgeless MSE: {rl:.5f} | Regularized Ridge MSE: {reg:.5f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

In academic machine learning, overparameterization is celebrated for state-of-the-art vision and language performance. In quantitative portfolio management, however, reliance on overparameterized minimum-norm interpolators encounters three critical institutional breakdowns:

  • Leverage and Gross Exposure Explosion: Minimum-norm interpolators achieve exact training fit by oscillating violently between correlated features. In financial cross-sections, this translates into massive offsetting long and short positions in nearly identical equities, generating gross portfolio leverage exceeding 20x and causing instant liquidation under margin constraints.
  • Economic Black-Box Opacity: An overparameterized model with \(P = 50{,}000\) random features has no economic attribution. When performance decays during a structural regime shift, quantitative risk officers cannot diagnose whether the breakdown is driven by interest-rate sensitivity, momentum reversal, or liquidity contraction.
  • Fragility to Non-Stationary Distributional Shift: Overparameterized interpolators memorize all in-sample pricing anomalies. Because financial markets are non-stationary and publication destroys return predictability (McLean & Pontiff 2016), an interpolator that memorized historical 1990s anomalies incurs catastrophic out-of-sample losses in the post-2010 automated trading era.

Debate Brief: Does Complexity Help Return Prediction?

1. Resolution

Resolved: Overparameterized machine learning models (\(P \gg N\)) with implicit regularization systematically outperform well-regularized parsimonious linear factor models in empirical cross-sectional return prediction.

2. Strongest Case for Complexity (Kelly, Malamud & Zhou 2024)

The virtue-of-complexity paradigm presents compelling theoretical and empirical arguments:

  • Non-Linear Span Without Curse of Dimensionality: High-order interactions among 100+ characteristics cannot be captured by linear models without an explosion of regressors. Overparameterized models (random features, neural nets) project into rich non-linear functional spaces where high-order interactions are naturally represented (KMZ 2024).
  • Implicit Shrinkage Dominates Heuristic Variable Selection: Minimum-norm interpolation spreads signal weight evenly across thousands of features, avoiding the brittle, high-variance feature selection of stepwise regression or Lasso.
  • Superior Empirical Sharpe Ratios: In 50 years of US equity data, overparameterized ensemble models achieve decile spread Sharpe ratios exceeding 2.0, more than doubling the performance of 3-to-5 factor linear models (KMZ 2024).

3. Strongest Case for Parsimony & Explicit Regularization (Nagel 2025)

The parsimony and explicit shrinkage school counters with rigorous econometric objections:

  • The Baseline Fallacy: Double descent is an artifact of benchmarking against unregularized OLS at \(P \approx N\). Comparing against an optimally tuned Ridge regression reveals that complexity provides zero incremental Sharpe ratio net of transaction costs (Nagel 2025).
  • Mathematical Equivalence to Ridge: As \(P \to \infty\), random feature minimum-norm interpolation converges to standard Ridge regression with an implicit penalty \(\lambda^*(\gamma)\). Overparameterization is merely an inefficient, computationally expensive way to implement Ridge shrinkage.
  • Excessive Turnover and Transaction Costs: Complex non-linear interpolators generate monthly portfolio turnover of 80%–120%, completely eradicating gross alpha once realistic institutional transaction costs (15–30 bps) are deducted.

Misconceptions

  • Wrong: Perfect interpolation of training data (zero training MSE) guarantees catastrophic out-of-sample overfitting in all models.

    Why it is wrong: When \(P \gg N\), the minimum \(L_2\) norm constraint forces the interpolator to select the smoothest possible function passing through all training points, suppressing high-frequency oscillations and allowing positive out-of-sample \(R^2\).

    Evidence: Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024) prove theoretically and show empirically that models with 100% in-sample fit achieve positive out-of-sample \(R^2\) and high Sharpe ratios in equity panels.

  • Wrong: The double descent curve implies that complexity is always strictly superior to parsimony.

    Why it is wrong: The descent in risk occurs relative to the singularity peak at \(P = N\). An explicitly regularized model with optimal shrinkage \(\lambda^*\) flattens the curve entirely and often outperforms interpolators.

    Evidence: Nagel (2025) demonstrates that optimal Ridge regularization on base features achieves lower prediction risk than unregularized minimum-norm interpolators across all complexity ratios.

  • Wrong: High gross Sharpe ratios of overparameterized models translate directly into production trading profits.

    Why it is wrong: Overparameterized models produce volatile, jumpy portfolio weights across correlated assets. Once realistic transaction costs and market impact are deducted, the net Sharpe ratio collapses substantially.

    Evidence: Nagel (2025) documents that subtracting 20 bps of trading costs eliminates over 60% of the gross alpha of random feature strategies due to extreme turnover.

Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 5.1: Minimum-Norm Dual Solution Computation

Consider an overparameterized system with \(M = 2\) training observations and \(P = 3\) features. The feature matrix is \(Z = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and the target return vector is \(y = \begin{bmatrix} 0.04 \\ 0.06 \end{bmatrix}\). Calculate the dual Gram matrix \(Z Z^\top\), its inverse, and the exact minimum-norm coefficient vector \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{MN}}\).

Solution

Compute the dual Gram matrix \(Z Z^\top \in \R^{2 \times 2}\):

\[ Z Z^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2 & 1(0) + 1(1) + 0(1) \\ 0(1) + 1(1) + 1(0) & 0^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Compute the inverse \((Z Z^\top)^{-1}\):

\[ \det(Z Z^\top) = 2(2) - 1(1) = 3 \implies (Z Z^\top)^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Compute the dual vector \(\alpha = (Z Z^\top)^{-1} y\):

\[ \alpha = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.04 \\ 0.06 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 0.08 - 0.06 \\ -0.04 + 0.12 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 0.02 \\ 0.08 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.00667 \\ 0.02667 \end{bmatrix} \]

Compute the primal minimum-norm weights \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{MN}} = Z^\top \alpha\):

\[ \hat{\beta}_{\text{MN}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.02/3 \\ 0.08/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.02/3 \\ 0.10/3 \\ 0.08/3 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.00667 \\ 0.03333 \\ 0.02667 \end{bmatrix} \]

Notice that \(Z \hat{\beta}_{\text{MN}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.02/3 + 0.10/3 \\ 0.10/3 + 0.08/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.04 \\ 0.06 \end{bmatrix} = y\), achieving exact zero training error.

Problem 5.2: Marchenko-Pastur Spectral Support Bounds

For an overparameterized random feature model with \(M = 500\) stocks and \(P = 2{,}000\) features, compute the complexity ratio \(\gamma = P / M\), the theoretical lower eigenvalue bound \(\lambda_{\min}\), and upper eigenvalue bound \(\lambda_{\max}\) of the dual Gram matrix \(K = \frac{1}{P} Z Z^\top\).

Solution

Compute the complexity ratio \(\gamma = P / M = 2000 / 500 = 4.0\).

Using Theorem 5.2, the Marchenko-Pastur spectral bounds for the dual matrix are given by:

\[ \lambda_{\min} = (1 - \gamma^{-1/2})^2 = (1 - 4.0^{-1/2})^2 = (1 - 0.5)^2 = 0.5^2 = 0.25 \] \[ \lambda_{\max} = (1 + \gamma^{-1/2})^2 = (1 + 4.0^{-1/2})^2 = (1 + 0.5)^2 = 1.5^2 = 2.25 \]

The condition number of the dual Gram matrix is bounded by \(\kappa(K) \le \frac{2.25}{0.25} = 9.0\). Because \(\lambda_{\min} = 0.25\) is strictly bounded away from zero, inversion variance is suppressed, enabling double descent.

Problem 5.3: Net Sharpe Ratio After Turnover Deduction

An overparameterized random feature trading strategy achieves an annualized gross Sharpe ratio \(\text{SR}_{\text{gross}} = 2.20\) with annual volatility \(\sigma = 15.0\%\) (gross expected excess return \(\E[r] = 33.0\%\)). The strategy incurs monthly one-way turnover of \(T_{\text{monthly}} = 90.0\%\) (annual one-way turnover \(10.8\times\), two-way turnover \(21.6\times\)). Average transaction cost is \(c = 25\) basis points (\(0.0025\)). Calculate the total annual transaction cost drag, the net expected excess return, and the net Sharpe ratio.

Solution

Compute annual turnover drag:

\[ \text{Drag} = \text{Annual Two-Way Turnover} \times c = 21.6 \times 0.0025 = 0.054 \quad (5.40\%) \]

Compute net expected excess return:

\[ \E[r_{\text{net}}] = \E[r_{\text{gross}}] - \text{Drag} = 33.0\% - 5.40\% = 27.60\% \]

Assuming return volatility is approximately unchanged at \(\sigma = 15.0\%\):

\[ \text{SR}_{\text{net}} = \frac{27.60\%}{15.0\%} = 1.84 \]

If costs rise to 50 bps (typical for illiquid small-caps), the drag becomes \(21.6 \times 0.0050 = 10.8\%\), reducing net Sharpe to \(\frac{22.2\%}{15.0\%} = 1.48\).

Proof

Problem 5.4: Derivation of the Minimum-Norm Dual Formula

Prove using Lagrange multipliers that the solution to \(\min_\beta \frac{1}{2} \|\beta\|_2^2\) subject to \(Z \beta = y\) is uniquely given by \(\hat{\beta} = Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-1} y\), provided \(Z\) has full row rank.

Solution

Formulate the Lagrangian function with Lagrange multiplier vector \(\alpha \in \R^M\):

\[ \mathcal{L}(\beta, \alpha) = \frac{1}{2} \|\beta\|_2^2 - \alpha^\top (Z \beta - y) \]

Take the gradient with respect to \(\beta\) and set to zero:

\[ \nabla_\beta \mathcal{L} = \beta - Z^\top \alpha = 0 \implies \beta = Z^\top \alpha \]

Substitute this expression into the equality constraint \(Z \beta = y\):

\[ Z (Z^\top \alpha) = y \implies (Z Z^\top) \alpha = y \]

Since \(Z\) has full row rank \(M \le P\), the \(M \times M\) matrix \(Z Z^\top\) is strictly positive definite and invertible. Solving for \(\alpha\):

\[ \alpha = (Z Z^\top)^{-1} y \]

Substitute \(\alpha\) back into the primal equation for \(\beta\):

\[ \hat{\beta} = Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-1} y \]

Because the objective is strictly convex and the constraint is linear, this critical point is the unique global minimum. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 5.5: Asymptotic Equivalence of Ridge and Minimum-Norm Interpolator

Prove that the ridge estimator \(\hat{\beta}(\lambda) = (Z^\top Z + \lambda I_P)^{-1} Z^\top y\) converges to the minimum-norm interpolator \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{MN}} = Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-1} y\) as the regularization parameter \(\lambda \to 0^+\).

Solution

Using the algebraic matrix identity \((Z^\top Z + \lambda I_P)^{-1} Z^\top = Z^\top (Z Z^\top + \lambda I_M)^{-1}\):

\[ \hat{\beta}(\lambda) = Z^\top (Z Z^\top + \lambda I_M)^{-1} y \]

Since \(Z\) has full row rank, \(Z Z^\top\) is non-singular. Taking the limit as \(\lambda \to 0^+\):

\[ \lim_{\lambda \to 0^+} (Z Z^\top + \lambda I_M)^{-1} = (Z Z^\top)^{-1} \]

Therefore:

\[ \lim_{\lambda \to 0^+} \hat{\beta}(\lambda) = Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-1} y = \hat{\beta}_{\text{MN}} \]

This proves that the ridgeless minimum-norm interpolator is the continuous vanishing-regularization limit of Ridge regression. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 5.6: Bounded Prediction Variance Under Infinite Dimension Expansion

Prove that for i.i.d. isotropic random features \(z_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_P)\), the variance of the minimum-norm prediction on an independent test point \(z_{\text{test}}\) remains finite and converges to zero as \(P \to \infty\) with fixed sample size \(M\).

Solution

The prediction on test point \(z_{\text{test}}\) is \(\hat{y}_{\text{test}} = z_{\text{test}}^\top \hat{\beta} = z_{\text{test}}^\top Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-1} y\). Conditioned on training data \(Z\), the estimation variance from noise \(\epsilon \sim (0, \sigma^2 I_M)\) is:

\[ \Var(\hat{y}_{\text{test}} \mid Z) = \sigma^2 z_{\text{test}}^\top Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-2} Z z_{\text{test}} \]

Taking the expectation over \(z_{\text{test}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_P)\) independent of \(Z\):

\[ \E_{z_{\text{test}}}[\Var(\hat{y}_{\text{test}} \mid Z)] = \sigma^2 \tr\big( Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-2} Z \big) = \sigma^2 \tr\big( (Z Z^\top)^{-1} \big) \]

Since \(Z Z^\top \sim \mathcal{W}_M(P, I_M)\) follows a Wishart distribution with \(P\) degrees of freedom, the expected trace of the inverse Wishart matrix for \(P > M + 1\) is:

\[ \E[\tr((Z Z^\top)^{-1})] = \frac{M}{P - M - 1} \]

Thus, total expected estimation variance is \(\frac{M \sigma^2}{P - M - 1}\). As \(P \to \infty\) with \(M\) fixed, this variance vanishes as \(\mathcal{O}(M/P) \to 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem 5.7: Empirical Sharpe Ratios Across Complexity Ratios

In the empirical asset pricing study of Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024), describe the behavior of the out-of-sample top-minus-bottom decile spread Sharpe ratio as the complexity ratio \(\gamma = P / N\) increases from 0.1 to 10.0.

Solution

Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024) document a sharp double-descent trajectory for decile spread Sharpe ratios: at low complexity (\(\gamma = 0.1\)), Sharpe is ~0.80. As \(\gamma \to 1.0\), Sharpe collapses toward zero or becomes negative due to exploded parameter variance. However, in the overparameterized regime (\(\gamma > 1\)), Sharpe rebounds strongly, reaching 1.80 at \(\gamma = 3.0\) and peaking above 2.20 for \(\gamma \ge 8.0\).

Problem 5.8: The Nagel (2025) Empirical Re-evaluation

What happens to the double-descent Sharpe ratio advantage when Nagel (2025) introduces cross-validated Ridge shrinkage to the linear baseline?

Solution

Nagel (2025) shows that when Ridge regression with cross-validated penalty \(\lambda^*\) is applied, the Sharpe ratio remains flat at approximately 1.50–1.70 across all complexity ratios, with no dip at \(P = N\). Furthermore, once 15–20 bps of realistic transaction costs are included, the low-complexity Ridge model achieves a net Sharpe ratio superior to the overparameterized interpolator, because the interpolator's jumpy weights generate excessive turnover drag.

Problem 5.9: Factor Zoo Sparsity vs. Dense Interpolation

Does the empirical outperformance of overparameterized models stem from uncovering a small number of sparse, undiscovered superstar factors, or from dense linear combinations of hundreds of weak signals?

Solution

Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024) and Kozak, Nagel & Santosh (2020) find that predictability is fundamentally dense: the superior Sharpe ratio of overparameterized models does not arise from identifying 2 or 3 previously hidden superstar anomalies. Instead, it reflects tiny, diversified diversification gains harvested across thousands of non-linear random features, which individually carry \(R^2 < 0.05\%\) but collectively span the SDF.

Research

Problem 5.10: Optimal Shrinkage in Dual Random Feature Models

Suppose an investor insists on using random feature expansions to capture non-linear interactions. How should explicit regularization be combined with overparameterization, and what is the optimal tuning procedure?

Solution

The optimal approach combines high-dimensional feature expansion with explicit dual Ridge shrinkage: \(\hat{\beta} = Z^\top (Z Z^\top + \lambda I_M)^{-1} y\), where \(\lambda > 0\) is tuned via purged K-fold cross-validation. This gives the model the capacity to capture non-linear interactions through the random projection while preventing the minimum eigenvalue of the dual matrix from dipping toward zero, completely eliminating the double descent singularity and outperforming both unregularized interpolation and underparameterized linear models.

Problem 5.11: Turnover-Regularized Minimum-Norm Portfolios

Formulate an optimization objective for overparameterized interpolators that penalizes high portfolio turnover between consecutive months.

Solution

Add a tracking error / turnover penalty to the minimum-norm objective: \(\min_\beta \frac{1}{2} \|\beta\|_2^2 + \gamma_{\text{turn}} \|w_t(\beta) - w_{t-1}\|_1\) subject to \(Z_t \beta = y_t\). In dual space, this can be solved using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM), which decouples the exact interpolation constraint from the \(L_1\) turnover penalty, dampening month-to-month weight churn and preserving net Sharpe ratio.

Problem 5.12: Double Descent Under Heavy-Tailed Return Shocks

Standard double descent proofs assume Gaussian or light-tailed errors \(\epsilon\). How does heavy-tailed return kurtosis (e.g., student-t with 3 degrees of freedom) alter the descent rate of prediction risk for \(\gamma > 1\)?

Solution

Under heavy-tailed errors, extreme return realizations act as leverage points in the dual inversion \((Z Z^\top)^{-1} y\). While the minimum eigenvalue of \(Z Z^\top\) remains bounded away from zero by random matrix theory, the projection of heavy-tailed noise onto the dual basis inflates the variance of \(\hat{\beta}\). Consequently, the descent of prediction risk for \(\gamma > 1\) is significantly slower (\(\mathcal{O}(P^{-\alpha})\) with \(\alpha < 1\)), requiring much higher complexity ratios (\(\gamma \ge 20\)) to achieve variance stabilization compared to Gaussian environments.
