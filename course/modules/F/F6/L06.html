Execution, Transaction Costs & Economic Evaluation

A statistical machine learning model that produces stellar out-of-sample \(R^2\) and gross Sharpe ratios in simulation can be completely uninvestable in institutional reality. High-capacity financial machine learning algorithms frequently generate volatile, jumpy portfolio weights that trigger devastating turnover, high bid-ask spread costs, and permanent price impact. In this capstone lesson, we study the economic translation of statistical forecasts into institutional trading strategies. We model linear and non-linear market impact, solve the dynamic multi-period portfolio execution problem of Gârleanu & Pedersen (2013) under quadratic transaction costs, derive partial adjustment policies, and establish rigorous economic evaluation metrics net of fees, leverage constraints, and capacity limits.

  • Prerequisites: L01 Cross-Sectional Return Prediction & Penalized Linear Models (concept ml-return-prediction), L02 Tree-Based Ensembles & Non-Linear Interactions, and L05 Overparameterization, Double Descent & the Complexity Debate (concept virtue-of-complexity).
  • Core Concepts: Bid-ask spread, quadratic market impact, Gârleanu-Pedersen dynamic smoothing, partial adjustment speed, net Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, investor certainty equivalent return.
  • Empirical Benchmark: Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016), Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018), DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2007).

Why It Matters

Machine learning algorithms are agnostic to execution frictions, generating rapid portfolio turnover that can consume 100% of paper profits. Turnover-regularized dynamic execution bridges the gap between theoretical predictive accuracy and institutional wealth creation.

1. Market Frictions and the Real-World Limits to Arbitrage

In classical frictionless finance, portfolio rebalancing incurs zero cost. In actual institutional markets, executing a vector of target trades \(\Delta w_t = w_t - w_{t-1}^+\) (where \(w_{t-1}^+\) reflects drift due to realized asset returns) incurs three distinct layers of trading costs:

  1. Direct Execution and Exchange Fees: Explicit brokerage commissions, exchange clearing fees, and stamp duties.
  2. Half-Spread Crossing Cost (Linear Drag): For asset \(i\), crossing the quoted bid-ask spread incurs linear cost \(c_i = \frac{1}{2} (P_{\text{ask}} - P_{\text{bid}}) / P_{\text{mid}}\).
  3. Market Impact (Non-Linear Quadratic Drag): Large orders consume limit order book liquidity and signal informed trading to market makers, moving the execution price against the trader proportionally to order size (Kyle 1985; Almgren & Chriss 2000).

Definition 6.1 (Composite Transaction Cost Function): Let \(\Delta w_t \in \R^N\) denote the vector of fractional portfolio trades at rebalancing date \(t\). Total percentage transaction costs \(\text{TC}(\Delta w_t)\) are modeled as a convex combination of linear spread crossing and quadratic market impact:

\[ \text{TC}(\Delta w_t) = c^\top |\Delta w_t| + \frac{1}{2} \Delta w_t^\top \Lambda \Delta w_t \]

where \(c \in \R^N_{++}\) is the vector of effective half-spreads, and \(\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N)\) is a diagonal matrix of Kyle's lambda price impact coefficients, typically scaling inversely with daily dollar volume: \(\lambda_i \propto \frac{\sigma_i}{\text{ADV}_i}\).

Remark 6.1 (The Novy-Marx & Velikov Anomaly Taxonomy): Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) classify empirical cross-sectional anomalies into three turnover tiers:

  • Low-Turnover Anomalies: Fundamental balance-sheet factors (value, profitability, investment) with monthly turnover \(\le 10\%\). Transaction costs consume only \(5\% - 15\%\) of gross returns, preserving substantial net Sharpe ratios.
  • Medium-Turnover Anomalies: Intermediate momentum and post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD) with monthly turnover of \(20\% - 40\%\). Trading costs reduce gross returns by \(30\% - 50\%\).
  • High-Turnover Anomalies: Short-term reversal, idiosyncratic volatility, and liquidity shocks with monthly turnover exceeding \(70\%\). Transaction costs consume over \(100\%\) of gross returns, leaving negative net alpha.

2. Dynamic Execution and Turnover Regularization: Gârleanu & Pedersen (2013)

A naïve quant fund rebalances fully to the unconstrained target portfolio \(\bar{w}_t = \frac{1}{\gamma} \Sigma^{-1} \mu_t\) at every rebalancing date. When expected returns \(\mu_t\) are predicted by flexible machine learning models, small changes in feature values cause substantial shifts in \(\bar{w}_t\), generating catastrophic turnover.

To maximize terminal utility net of market impact, an institutional investor must solve a dynamic multi-period optimization problem balancing expected alpha, risk, and transaction costs across time.

Theorem 6.1 (Gârleanu & Pedersen Dynamic Trading Policy): Consider an infinite-horizon mean-variance investor with discount factor \(\rho \in (0, 1)\), risk aversion \(\gamma > 0\), asset covariance \(\Sigma\), and quadratic trading cost matrix \(\Lambda\). The investor solves:

\[ \max_{\{w_t\}_{t=0}^\infty} \sum_{t=0}^\infty \rho^t \left[ w_t^\top \mu_t - \frac{\gamma}{2} w_t^\top \Sigma w_t - \frac{1}{2} (w_t - w_{t-1})^\top \Lambda (w_t - w_{t-1}) \right] \]

The optimal dynamic portfolio policy is a partial adjustment rule toward the aim portfolio \(w_t^*\):

\[ w_t = (I - M) w_{t-1} + M w_t^* \]

where the matrix adjustment speed \(M \in \R^{N \times N}\) is the unique symmetric positive definite solution to the matrix Riccati equation:

\[ \Lambda M^2 + \gamma \Sigma M - \gamma \Sigma = 0 \quad (\text{for } \rho = 1) \]

Proof: Taking the first-order condition with respect to \(w_t\) in the dynamic Lagrangian yields the Euler equation:

\[ \mu_t - \gamma \Sigma w_t - \Lambda (w_t - w_{t-1}) + \rho \Lambda \E_t[w_{t+1} - w_t] = 0 \]

For a stationary target with \(\rho = 1\), write the characteristic matrix equation as \(\Lambda w_{t+1} - (\gamma \Sigma + 2\Lambda) w_t + \Lambda w_{t-1} = -\mu_t\). Factoring the matrix polynomial \(\Lambda L^2 - (\gamma \Sigma + 2\Lambda) L + \Lambda = \Lambda (I - M L)(I - \tilde{M} L^{-1})\) where \(M\) is the stable root with eigenvalues in \((0, 1)\) yields the partial adjustment policy \(w_t = (I - M) w_{t-1} + M w_t^*\), where \(w_t^* = (\gamma \Sigma + \Lambda M)^{-1} \sum_{\tau=0}^\infty (I - M)^\tau \E_t[\mu_{t+\tau}]\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 6.2 (Scalar Trading Speed Intuition): In the scalar case (single asset or diagonalized system), the optimal adjustment speed simplifies to:

\[ m = \frac{-\gamma \sigma^2 + \sqrt{(\gamma \sigma^2)^2 + 4 \lambda_{\text{cost}} \gamma \sigma^2}}{2 \lambda_{\text{cost}}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\gamma \sigma^2}{\lambda_{\text{cost}}}} \quad (\text{for small } \gamma \sigma^2 / \lambda_{\text{cost}}) \]

Trading speed increases with asset risk \(\gamma \sigma^2\) (higher urgency to control risk) and decreases with market impact \(\lambda_{\text{cost}}\) (higher friction demanding smoother trading).

3. Rigorous Economic Evaluation of Machine Learning Models

Statistical metrics such as MSE or \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) treat all forecasting errors symmetrically and ignore the covariance structure of financial payoffs. Rigorous econometric evaluation requires translating return forecasts into economic utility.

Definition 6.2 (Institutional Performance Metrics): Let \(r_{p,t}^{\text{net}} = w_t^\top r_{t+1} - \text{TC}(\Delta w_t)\) denote the net excess return of the strategy at month \(t\). The strategy is evaluated across four core performance dimensions:

  • Annualized Net Sharpe Ratio: \[ \text{SR}_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{12} \cdot \frac{\hat{\mu}_{\text{net}}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\text{net}}} = \sqrt{12} \cdot \frac{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{p,t}^{\text{net}}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^T (r_{p,t}^{\text{net}} - \hat{\mu}_{\text{net}})^2}} \]
  • Maximum Drawdown (MDD): \[ \text{MDD} = \max_{0 \le s \le t \le T} \frac{W_s - W_t}{W_s}, \qquad W_t = \prod_{\tau=1}^t (1 + R_{p,\tau}^{\text{net}}) \]
  • Certainty Equivalent Return (CER) Gain: The annualized percentage utility gain delivered to an investor with risk aversion \(\gamma\): \[ \Delta \text{CER} = 12 \left( \hat{\mu}_{\text{net}} - \frac{\gamma}{2} \hat{\sigma}_{\text{net}}^2 \right) - 12 \left( \hat{\mu}_{\text{bm}} - \frac{\gamma}{2} \hat{\sigma}_{\text{bm}}^2 \right) \]
  • Breakeven Transaction Cost Capacity: The cost level \(c^*\) (in bps) at which the net Sharpe ratio collapses to zero: \[ c^* = \frac{\hat{\mu}_{\text{gross}}}{\text{Mean Annual Two-Way Turnover}} \]

Remark 6.3 (Empirical Anomaly Capacities): Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018) evaluate real-world trade execution data from a multi-billion dollar quantitative asset manager, documenting that institutional transaction costs are substantially lower than naive academic quoted spreads (effective costs \(\approx 10 - 20\) bps for large caps). Value, momentum, and size anomalies can support over \$50 billion in institutional AUM before net alpha is exhausted.

Lab F6-L06-1: Net Sharpe Ratio and Turnover Optimization

Hypothesis: In the presence of 25 bps round-trip transaction costs, a turnover-penalized dynamic smoothing strategy preserves net Sharpe ratio, outperforming unconstrained monthly rebalancing whose net returns collapse under excessive turnover drag.

"""Lab F6-L06-1: Net Sharpe Ratio and Turnover Optimization under Transaction Costs.

Evaluates the real-world economic impact of market frictions on machine learning return signals.
Compares unconstrained rebalancing against Garleanu & Pedersen (2013) turnover-penalized
dynamic execution under 25 bps round-trip transaction costs.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def run_lab(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Execute transaction cost and turnover optimization simulation."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    n_assets = 30
    n_periods = 36
    cost_rate = 0.0025  # 25 bps round-trip transaction cost
    k_quant = n_assets // 3

    # Generate cross-sectional predictions and realized returns with realistic S/N
    raw_predictions = rng.normal(0.0, 0.020, size=(n_assets, n_periods))
    realized_returns = rng.normal(0.005, 0.050, size=(n_assets, n_periods)) + 0.15 * raw_predictions

    # Strategy 1: Raw Unconstrained Quantile Rebalancing
    w_raw_prev = np.zeros(n_assets, dtype=np.float64)
    gross_raw = np.zeros(n_periods, dtype=np.float64)
    net_raw = np.zeros(n_periods, dtype=np.float64)
    turnover_raw = np.zeros(n_periods, dtype=np.float64)

    for t in range(n_periods):
        ord_idx = np.argsort(raw_predictions[:, t])
        w = np.zeros(n_assets, dtype=np.float64)
        w[ord_idx[-k_quant:]] = 1.0 / k_quant
        w[ord_idx[:k_quant]] = -1.0 / k_quant

        to = float(np.sum(np.abs(w - w_raw_prev)))
        g = float(np.dot(w, realized_returns[:, t]))

        gross_raw[t] = g
        net_raw[t] = g - to * cost_rate
        turnover_raw[t] = to
        w_raw_prev = w

    # Strategy 2: Turnover-Penalized Partial Adjustment (Lambda = 0.35)
    lambda_param = 0.35
    w_smooth_prev = np.zeros(n_assets, dtype=np.float64)
    gross_smooth = np.zeros(n_periods, dtype=np.float64)
    net_smooth = np.zeros(n_periods, dtype=np.float64)
    turnover_smooth = np.zeros(n_periods, dtype=np.float64)

    for t in range(n_periods):
        ord_idx = np.argsort(raw_predictions[:, t])
        w_target = np.zeros(n_assets, dtype=np.float64)
        w_target[ord_idx[-k_quant:]] = 1.0 / k_quant
        w_target[ord_idx[:k_quant]] = -1.0 / k_quant

        w = (1.0 - lambda_param) * w_smooth_prev + lambda_param * w_target
        to = float(np.sum(np.abs(w - w_smooth_prev)))
        g = float(np.dot(w, realized_returns[:, t]))

        gross_smooth[t] = g
        net_smooth[t] = g - to * cost_rate
        turnover_smooth[t] = to
        w_smooth_prev = w

    sharpe_gross_raw = float(math.sqrt(12.0) * np.mean(gross_raw) / np.std(gross_raw, ddof=1))
    sharpe_net_raw = float(math.sqrt(12.0) * np.mean(net_raw) / np.std(net_raw, ddof=1))
    mean_to_raw = float(np.mean(turnover_raw))

    sharpe_gross_smooth = float(math.sqrt(12.0) * np.mean(gross_smooth) / np.std(gross_smooth, ddof=1))
    sharpe_net_smooth = float(math.sqrt(12.0) * np.mean(net_smooth) / np.std(net_smooth, ddof=1))
    mean_to_smooth = float(np.mean(turnover_smooth))

    return {
        "sharpe_gross_raw": sharpe_gross_raw,
        "sharpe_net_raw": sharpe_net_raw,
        "mean_turnover_raw": mean_to_raw,
        "sharpe_gross_smooth": sharpe_gross_smooth,
        "sharpe_net_smooth": sharpe_net_smooth,
        "mean_turnover_smooth": mean_to_smooth,
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = run_lab(seed=42)
    print("=== Lab F6-L06-1: Transaction Cost & Turnover Optimization ===")
    raw_msg = (
        f"Raw Strategy:    Gross Sharpe={res['sharpe_gross_raw']:.4f}, "
        f"Net Sharpe={res['sharpe_net_raw']:.4f}, Turnover={res['mean_turnover_raw']:.4f}"
    )
    smooth_msg = (
        f"Smooth Strategy: Gross Sharpe={res['sharpe_gross_smooth']:.4f}, "
        f"Net Sharpe={res['sharpe_net_smooth']:.4f}, Turnover={res['mean_turnover_smooth']:.4f}"
    )
    print(raw_msg)
    print(smooth_msg)


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

In quantitative academic papers, transaction costs are frequently modeled as fixed flat fees or omitted altogether. In production institutional management, execution models break down across three crucial friction dimensions:

  • Regime-Dependent Liquidity Evaporation: Fixed cost models assume constant bid-ask spreads and Kyle's lambda. During market crises (such as the 2008 Lehman collapse or March 2020 COVID shock), market-maker inventory risk surges, bid-ask spreads widen by a factor of 5x to 10x, and market depth collapses, causing actual transaction costs to vastly exceed modeled penalties.
  • Endogenous Crowding and Flow Toxicity: When dozens of quantitative funds utilize identical open anomaly signals (e.g., standard momentum or book-to-market), simultaneous rebalancing creates correlated order flow. The collective market impact scales quadratically with industry AUM, eroding strategy capacity and triggering flash liquidation cascades.
  • Short-Sale Hard-to-Borrow Surcharges: Many machine learning anomalies derive their profitability primarily from the short leg of small, distressed equities. In institutional practice, borrowing these equities requires paying specialness fees of 500 to 2,000 bps annualized, and recalls can force involuntary buy-ins at the worst possible market prices.

Misconceptions

  • Wrong: Quoted bid-ask spreads accurately measure the trading costs incurred by institutional quantitative managers.

    Why it is wrong: Institutional managers utilize algorithmic execution (TWAP, VWAP, dark pools, and limit order posting), executing inside the quoted spread and capturing liquidity rebates. For large institutional trades, market impact dominates quoted spreads.

    Evidence: Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018) analyze \$1.7 trillion in institutional orders and show that effective trading costs are less than one-third of the quoted bid-ask spread across large- and mid-cap equities.

  • Wrong: A strategy with higher gross Sharpe ratio always produces higher investor utility than a strategy with lower gross Sharpe ratio.

    Why it is wrong: A high-gross-Sharpe strategy with high turnover can lose all of its alpha to transaction costs, delivering near-zero net Sharpe ratio and negative certainty equivalent returns compared to a low-turnover factor portfolio.

    Evidence: Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) document that short-term reversal strategies with gross Sharpe ratios above 2.0 deliver negative net Sharpe ratios once realistic trading costs are deducted.

  • Wrong: Rebalancing fully to the target portfolio at every month-end is optimal because it keeps risk exposures aligned with current factor estimates.

    Why it is wrong: Full rebalancing ignores the quadratic trading cost penalty. Dynamic programming proves that partial adjustment (smoothly tracking the target over multiple periods) minimizes total tracking error plus execution costs.

    Evidence: DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2007) show that unconstrained monthly rebalancing severely destroys out-of-sample wealth relative to turnover-constrained or partial-adjustment portfolios.

Mechanics

Problem 6.1: Almgren-Chriss Market Impact Calculation

A quantitative hedge fund manages \(A = \$500\text{ million}\) in equity assets. In month \(t\), the model signals an increase in the target weight of stock \(i\) from \(w_{t-1} = 1.0\%\) to \(w_t = 3.0\%\). The stock has price \(P = \$50\), daily dollar volume \(\text{ADV} = \$20\text{ million}\), and daily volatility \(\sigma = 2.0\%\). The effective half-spread is \(c = 5\text{ bps}\) (\(0.0005\)), and Kyle's lambda market impact parameter is \(\lambda_{\text{cost}} = 0.10 \times \frac{\sigma}{\text{ADV}}\). Calculate the trade dollar size, the linear spread cost, the quadratic market impact cost, and total execution drag in basis points.

Solution

Compute the trade size:

\[ \Delta \$ = A \times (w_t - w_{t-1}) = \$500\text{M} \times (0.03 - 0.01) = \$500\text{M} \times 0.02 = \$10\text{ million} \]

Compute Kyle's lambda parameter:

\[ \lambda_{\text{cost}} = 0.10 \times \frac{0.02}{20\text{M}} = \frac{0.002}{20{,}000{,}000} = 1.0 \times 10^{-10} \quad (\$/\$^2) \]

Compute linear half-spread cost:

\[ \text{Cost}_{\text{linear}} = c \times \Delta \$ = 0.0005 \times \$10{,}000{,}000 = \$5{,}000 \]

Compute quadratic market impact cost:

\[ \text{Cost}_{\text{impact}} = \frac{1}{2} \lambda_{\text{cost}} (\Delta \$)^2 = 0.5 \times (1.0 \times 10^{-10}) \times (10^7)^2 = 0.5 \times 10^{-10} \times 10^{14} = \$5{,}000 \]

Total execution cost is \(\$5{,}000 + \$5{,}000 = \$10{,}000\).

Expressed as a fraction of the trade size: \(\frac{\$10{,}000}{\$10{,}000{,}000} = 0.0010 = 10\text{ basis points}\).

Problem 6.2: Gârleanu-Pedersen Partial Adjustment Step

At month \(t\), the current portfolio weight in an equity sector is \(w_{t-1} = 5.0\%\). The machine learning model forecasts an elevated expected return, generating a new static Markowitz aim weight of \(w_t^* = 15.0\%\). The optimal adjustment speed is determined to be \(m = 0.30\). Calculate the updated portfolio weight \(w_t\), the implemented trade size \(\Delta w_t\), and the remaining distance to the aim target.

Solution

Using the partial adjustment policy (Theorem 6.1):

\[ w_t = (1 - m) w_{t-1} + m w_t^* = (1 - 0.30)(0.05) + 0.30(0.15) = 0.70(0.05) + 0.045 = 0.035 + 0.045 = 0.080 \quad (8.0\%) \]

The implemented trade is:

\[ \Delta w_t = w_t - w_{t-1} = 0.080 - 0.050 = 0.030 \quad (3.0\%) \]

The remaining distance to the target is:

\[ w_t^* - w_t = 0.150 - 0.080 = 0.070 \quad (7.0\%) \]

By moving only 30% of the distance toward the target, the fund avoids large market impact while capturing the majority of the expected return over subsequent months.

Problem 6.3: Breakeven Transaction Cost and Capacity Calculation

A high-frequency cross-sectional machine learning signal generates an annualized gross expected excess return \(\E[r_{\text{gross}}] = 18.0\%\). The strategy turns over its entire portfolio twice per month (monthly two-way turnover = \(4.0\times\), annualized two-way turnover = \(48.0\times\)). Calculate the breakeven transaction cost \(c^*\) (in basis points) at which the net expected excess return is zero.

Solution

From Definition 6.2, net excess return is \(\E[r_{\text{net}}] = \E[r_{\text{gross}}] - \text{Annual Turnover} \times c\). Setting \(\E[r_{\text{net}}] = 0\):

\[ c^* = \frac{\E[r_{\text{gross}}]}{\text{Annual Turnover}} = \frac{0.18}{48.0} = 0.00375 \quad (37.5\text{ basis points}) \]

If execution costs exceed 37.5 bps round-trip, the strategy loses money. For small-cap equities where round-trip costs typically exceed 50 bps, this strategy generates guaranteed net losses despite an 18% paper alpha.

Problem 6.4: Derivation of the Gârleanu-Pedersen Matrix Riccati Equation

Prove that for an infinite-horizon mean-variance investor facing quadratic transaction costs with discount factor \(\rho = 1\), the first-order condition for optimal execution reduces to the matrix Riccati equation \(\Lambda M^2 + \gamma \Sigma M - \gamma \Sigma = 0\).

Solution

The objective is \(\max \sum_{t=0}^\infty [w_t^\top \mu - \frac{\gamma}{2} w_t^\top \Sigma w_t - \frac{1}{2} (w_t - w_{t-1})^\top \Lambda (w_t - w_{t-1})]\). Taking the derivative with respect to \(w_t\) and setting to zero:

\[ \mu - \gamma \Sigma w_t - \Lambda (w_t - w_{t-1}) + \Lambda (w_{t+1} - w_t) = 0 \]

Rearranging in terms of the lag operator \(L\):

\[ \Lambda w_{t+1} - (\gamma \Sigma + 2\Lambda) w_t + \Lambda w_{t-1} = -\mu \]

Postulate a stationary partial adjustment policy \(w_t = (I - M) w_{t-1} + M \bar{w}\) where \(\bar{w} = \frac{1}{\gamma} \Sigma^{-1} \mu\). Then \(w_{t+1} - w_t = -M (w_t - \bar{w})\). Substitute into the Euler equation:

\[ -\gamma \Sigma w_t - \Lambda M (w_t - w_{t-1}) - \Lambda M (w_{t+1} - w_t) + \mu = 0 \]

Matching matrix coefficients on \(w_t - w_{t-1}\) yields the operator identity:

\[ \Lambda M^2 + \gamma \Sigma M - \gamma \Sigma = 0 \]

Since \(\Lambda\) and \(\Sigma\) are symmetric positive definite, this matrix Riccati equation has a unique symmetric positive definite solution \(M\) with eigenvalues strictly between 0 and 1. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 6.5: Certainty Equivalent Preservation Under Smoothing

Prove that when asset returns have persistent predictive signals, a partial adjustment policy \(w_t = (1-m)w_{t-1} + m w_t^*\) with \(m \in (0, 1)\) strictly increases the investor's certainty equivalent return (CER) compared to full rebalancing (\(m = 1\)), provided trading cost \(\lambda_{\text{cost}} > 0\).

Solution

Let \(U(m) = \E[w(m)^\top r - \frac{\gamma}{2} w(m)^\top \Sigma w(m) - \frac{\lambda_{\text{cost}}}{2} (w(m) - w_{t-1})^2]\). As \(m \to 1\), the trade size \(\Delta w = w_t^* - w_{t-1}\) incurs maximum quadratic cost \(\frac{\lambda_{\text{cost}}}{2} (\Delta w)^2\).

Take the derivative of expected utility with respect to \(m\) evaluated at \(m = 1\):

\[ \left. \frac{dU}{dm} \right|_{m=1} = \E\left[ \frac{\partial U}{\partial w} \frac{\partial w}{\partial m} \right] - \lambda_{\text{cost}} \E[(\Delta w) (\Delta w)] \]

At \(m = 1\), \(w = w_t^*\), which is the unconstrained optimum where the derivative of gross utility \(\frac{\partial}{\partial w}[\mu^\top w - \frac{\gamma}{2} w^\top \Sigma w] = 0\) by the envelope theorem. Thus:

\[ \left. \frac{dU}{dm} \right|_{m=1} = 0 - \lambda_{\text{cost}} \Var(\Delta w) < 0 \]

Since the derivative is strictly negative at \(m = 1\), decreasing \(m\) below 1 strictly increases net certainty equivalent utility. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 6.6: Superadditivity of Market Impact Across Clustered Trades

Prove that under quadratic market impact \(\text{TC}(x) = \frac{1}{2} x^\top \Lambda x\) with positive definite \(\Lambda\), executing a total trade vector \(x\) split across two independent trades \(x_1 + x_2 = x\) achieves minimum total market impact if and only if \(x_1 = x_2 = \frac{1}{2} x\).

Solution

Consider the total impact of executing \(x_1\) and \(x_2\): \(\text{Cost} = \frac{1}{2} x_1^\top \Lambda x_1 + \frac{1}{2} x_2^\top \Lambda x_2\). Substitute \(x_2 = x - x_1\):

\[ f(x_1) = \frac{1}{2} x_1^\top \Lambda x_1 + \frac{1}{2} (x - x_1)^\top \Lambda (x - x_1) = x_1^\top \Lambda x_1 - x_1^\top \Lambda x + \frac{1}{2} x^\top \Lambda x \]

Taking the gradient with respect to \(x_1\) and setting to zero:

\[ \nabla_{x_1} f = 2 \Lambda x_1 - \Lambda x = 0 \implies \Lambda (2 x_1 - x) = 0 \]

Since \(\Lambda\) is positive definite and invertible, \(2 x_1 - x = 0 \implies x_1 = \frac{1}{2} x\), and \(x_2 = \frac{1}{2} x\). The minimum cost is \(\frac{1}{4} x^\top \Lambda x\), which cuts total market impact by 50% compared to executing the full trade in a single lump sum (\(\frac{1}{2} x^\top \Lambda x\)). \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem 6.7: Anomaly Survival Under Realistic Trading Costs

In the comprehensive empirical audit by Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016), which anomaly families retain statistically significant net alpha after accounting for effective transaction costs, and which are completely eliminated?

Solution

Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) show that fundamental profitability (gross profitability, operating profitability), asset growth, and value (book-to-market) easily survive transaction costs, retaining over 80% of their gross Sharpe ratios. In contrast, short-term reversal (1-month lagged return), idiosyncratic volatility, and dollar volume liquidity anomalies lose 100% of their net alpha, producing negative net returns due to annual turnover exceeding 600%.

Problem 6.8: Institutional vs. Academic Transaction Cost Estimates

Why do Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018) find that institutional trading costs are substantially lower than the bid-ask spreads reported in academic CRSP databases?

Solution

Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2018) document that academic studies typically assume orders cross the full quoted bid-ask spread at market close. Real institutional quantitative funds use smart order routing, provide liquidity via passive limit orders to earn maker rebates, trade in dark pools with zero spread, and execute across multi-day horizons. Consequently, effective institutional costs are 50% to 70% lower than naive quoted spreads, tripling the capacity of anomaly strategies.

Problem 6.9: Machine Learning Signal Decay and Execution Horizon

How does the empirical half-life of machine learning return predictions determine the optimal rebalancing frequency and smoothing parameter \(m\)?

Solution

Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) find that ML return predictability is concentrated in intermediate horizons of 1 to 6 months. Fast-decaying price-trend features (half-life < 2 weeks) require aggressive rebalancing that is destroyed by transaction costs unless restricted to mega-caps. Slow-decaying characteristic features (half-life 6–12 months) allow low trading speed \(m \approx 0.15 - 0.25\), capturing the risk premium while reducing portfolio turnover by over 70%.

Problem 6.10: Reinforcement Learning for Execution Smoothing

Formulate an end-to-end Reinforcement Learning (RL) agent that directly optimizes net wealth by incorporating non-linear transaction cost penalties into the policy reward function.

Solution

Model the portfolio manager as a Markov Decision Process (MDP): state space \(s_t = (Z_t, X_t, w_{t-1})\) contains firm characteristics, macro indicators, and current portfolio holdings. Action space \(a_t = w_t\) is the new portfolio weight vector. The reward function is the net utility \(R(s_t, a_t) = w_t^\top r_{t+1} - \frac{\gamma}{2} w_t^\top \Sigma_t w_t - \text{TC}(w_t - w_{t-1})\).

A policy gradient algorithm (such as PPO or DDPG) trains the neural network to output trading decisions that automatically temper turnover in illiquid assets while exploiting fast-moving signals exclusively in liquid instruments, discovering Gârleanu-Pedersen smoothing end-to-end without assuming linear dynamics.

Problem 6.11: Multi-Asset Crowding and Co-Impact Regularization

When multiple quantitative hedge funds deploy similar ML return predictors, market impact across assets becomes correlated. Formulate a cross-asset co-impact penalty matrix \(\Lambda\) and explain how it alters optimal asset selection.

Solution

Let \(\Lambda_{i,j} = \rho_{\text{flow}} \sqrt{\lambda_i \lambda_j}\) be the off-diagonal cross-market impact representing correlated order flow between assets in the same industry or factor bucket. The execution cost is \(\frac{1}{2} \Delta w^\top \Lambda \Delta w\).

The co-impact penalty penalizes simultaneous trading in correlated assets. Even if two stocks have identical expected return and idiosyncratic risk, the optimizer forces the fund to trade only one of them or diversify across distinct industries, preventing institutional fire-sales and dampening portfolio-level market impact.

Problem 6.12: Capacity Analysis via Marginal Sharpe Degradation

Derive an analytical formula for strategy capacity \(AUM^*\) as a function of quadratic market impact parameter \(\lambda\) and gross Sharpe ratio \(\text{SR}_{\text{gross}}\).

Solution

Let fund assets under management be \(A\). Dollar trades are \(\Delta \$ = A \Delta w\). Total transaction cost drag is \(\text{Cost}(A) = \frac{1}{2} \lambda A^2 \|\Delta w\|_2^2\). As a fraction of assets, percentage cost drag is \(\frac{\text{Cost}(A)}{A} = \frac{1}{2} \lambda A \|\Delta w\|_2^2\).

Net expected return is \(\mu_{\text{net}}(A) = \mu_{\text{gross}} - \frac{1}{2} \lambda A \|\Delta w\|_2^2\). Setting the marginal return of additional capital \(\frac{\partial (\mu_{\text{net}} A)}{\partial A} = 0\) yields the optimal fund size:

\[ \mu_{\text{gross}} - \lambda A \|\Delta w\|_2^2 = 0 \implies AUM^* = \frac{\mu_{\text{gross}}}{\lambda \|\Delta w\|_2^2} \]

Strategy capacity scales linearly with gross alpha \(\mu_{\text{gross}}\) and inversely with market impact \(\lambda\) and squared portfolio turnover.
