Delisting Returns & Delisting Bias Correction

When an exchange-listed company delists due to insolvency, distress, or failure to satisfy exchange minimum listing criteria, its equity does not simply vanish. The final trading payoff is captured by the delisting return. In his landmark study, Shumway (1997) uncovered a severe delisting bias in CRSP: thousands of performance-related delistings had missing delisting returns. Researchers who ignored missing delistings or coded them as zero dramatically overstated historical returns, particularly for distressed, small-cap, and high-beta stocks. Correcting delisting bias via empirical replacement penalties is an essential data integrity protocol required for every valid empirical asset pricing model and institutional factor backtest.

1CRSP Delisting Return Architecture and Event Codes

In the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) database, when a security ceases trading on an exchange, CRSP records its final event in the delisting history table with two critical fields: the delisting status code (DLSTCD) and the delisting return (DLRET).

Definition 3.1 · Delisting Bias
Delisting bias is the systematic overstatement of equity returns and factor premia caused by missing terminal delisting returns for companies dropped from exchanges due to bankruptcy, liquidation, or regulatory delisting.

CRSP classifies delisting events into distinct three-digit series according to their underlying economic cause:

Code SeriesCategoryEconomic NatureTypical Payoff
100ActiveSecurity remains actively traded on exchangeOngoing trading

300-399ExchangesStock exchanged for other issues or recapitalizedNeutral to modest gain

Code 500 designates a general exchange delisting due to halted trading or capital impairment. Codes 520 through 584 specify bankruptcy, insufficient shareholders, failure to maintain minimum bid price (the NASDAQ $1 rule), or regulatory non-compliance.

2Compounding Monthly Returns with Delisting Payoffs

When an equity delists mid-month, CRSP records two separate return values: the ordinary monthly return RET (which captures trading from the beginning of the month up to the final trading day on the exchange), and the delisting return DLRET (which captures the value realized from the final closing price on the exchange to the post-delisting over-the-counter or bankruptcy payoff).

Theorem 3.1 · Total Delisting Compounding Formula
Let \(R_{i,t}^{\text{CRSP}}\) be the regular monthly return recorded in CRSP, and let \(R_{i,t}^{\text{delist}}\) be the terminal delisting return. The total gross return realized by an investor holding the security through the delisting event is: \[\begin{aligned} 1 + R_{i,t}^{\text{total}} &= (1 + R_{i,t}^{\text{CRSP}}) \times (1 + R_{i,t}^{\text{delist}}). \end{aligned}\] Equivalently, the net total monthly return is: \[\begin{aligned} R_{i,t}^{\text{total}} &= R_{i,t}^{\text{CRSP}} + R_{i,t}^{\text{delist}} + R_{i,t}^{\text{CRSP}} \times R_{i,t}^{\text{delist}}. \end{aligned}\]

Proof
Let \(P_{t-1}\) be the month-beginning price, \(P_{\text{last}}\) be the final exchange transaction price, and \(P_{\text{final}}\) be the post-delisting terminal value. By definition, \(1 + R_{i,t}^{\text{CRSP}} = \frac{P_{\text{last}} + D_t}{P_{t-1}}\) and \(1 + R_{i,t}^{\text{delist}} = \frac{P_{\text{final}}}{P_{\text{last}}}\). Assuming dividends \(D_t\) are paid before exchange cessation: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{P_{\text{final}} + D_t}{P_{t-1}} &= \frac{P_{\text{last}} + D_t}{P_{t-1}} \times \frac{P_{\text{final}}}{P_{\text{last}}} = (1 + R_{i,t}^{\text{CRSP}})(1 + R_{i,t}^{\text{delist}}). \end{aligned}\] Subtracting 1 yields the net total return.

3The Shumway (1997) Correction Protocol

In a groundbreaking paper in the Journal of Finance, Shumway (1997) investigated why CRSP recorded missing delisting returns for a large proportion of delisted stocks. For NYSE and AMEX securities delisted between 1962 and 1993, Shumway tracked post-delisting over-the-counter quotes from the National Quotation Bureau (Pink Sheets).

Theorem 3.2 · Shumway Empirical Delisting Replacement Rules
For any security delisted in month \(t\) where the recorded CRSP delisting return \(R_{i,t}^{\text{delist}}\) is missing (NaN), the unbiased replacement return \(R_{i,t}^{\text{adj}}\) is given by: \[\begin{aligned} R_{i,t}^{\text{adj}} &= \begin{cases} -0.30 & \text{if } \text{DLSTCD} \in \{500, 520\text{--}584\} \text{ and } \text{HEXCD} \in \{1, 2\} \text{ (NYSE/AMEX)}, \\ -0.55 & \text{if } \text{DLSTCD} \in \{500, 520\text{--}584\} \text{ and } \text{HEXCD} = 3 \text{ (NASDAQ)}, \\ 0.00 & \text{if } \text{DLSTCD} < 500 \text{ or } \text{DLSTCD} > 584 \text{ (Mergers and non-performance)}. \end{cases} \end{aligned}\]

The \(-30\%\) penalty reflects Shumway's finding that the average post-delisting price of NYSE and AMEX performance delistings collapsed by 30%. In subsequent work on NASDAQ securities, Shumway & Sloan (1999) documented that NASDAQ performance delistings suffered even more catastrophic illiquidity, documenting an average post-delisting decline of \(-55\%\).

Lab 1 · Shumway (1997) Delisting Bias Simulator

Simulating a cross-section with missing performance delistings will confirm that omitting delisting returns inflates cross-sectional returns by over 20% annualized in distressed deciles, and that applying Shumway corrections fully captures terminal distress payoffs.

"""Lab F1-L03-1: Delisting Returns & Delisting Bias Correction (Shumway 1997).

Simulates terminal delisting events and evaluates the return distortion
when missing performance delisting returns are omitted versus corrected.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_delisting_bias(seed: int = 77) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Demonstrate the impact of missing delisting returns and Shumway correction."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_stocks = 300

    # 1. Base monthly returns before delisting
    raw_returns = rng.normal(loc=0.008, scale=0.06, size=n_stocks)

    # 2. Assign exchanges: 40% NYSE (1), 10% AMEX (2), 50% NASDAQ (3)
    exchange_codes = rng.choice([1, 2, 3], size=n_stocks, p=[0.40, 0.10, 0.50])

    # 3. Delisting events: ~10% of stocks delist this month
    # 70% of delistings are performance-related (codes 500, 520, 560, 584)
    # 30% are mergers (code 200)
    delist_codes = np.full(n_stocks, 100, dtype=np.int32)
    delist_returns = np.full(n_stocks, np.nan, dtype=np.float64)

    delist_idx = rng.choice(n_stocks, size=30, replace=False)
    for idx in delist_idx:
        is_perf = rng.uniform() < 0.70
        if is_perf:
            delist_codes[idx] = int(rng.choice([500, 520, 560, 584]))
            # In CRSP, roughly half of performance delistings historically had missing DLRET
            has_crsp_dlret = rng.uniform() < 0.40
            if has_crsp_dlret:
                # Realized delisting loss
                delist_returns[idx] = rng.uniform(-0.60, -0.20)
            else:
                delist_returns[idx] = np.nan  # MISSING in database!
        else:
            delist_codes[idx] = 200  # merger
            delist_returns[idx] = rng.uniform(0.05, 0.25)  # takeover premium

    # Case A: Naive approach (missing delisting returns treated as 0.0)
    ret_naive = np.copy(raw_returns)
    for i in range(n_stocks):
        if np.isfinite(delist_returns[i]):
            ret_naive[i] = (1.0 + raw_returns[i]) * (1.0 + delist_returns[i]) - 1.0
        # If delist_returns[i] is NaN, naive leaves raw_returns[i] unchanged (implicit 0% delisting return)

    # Case B: Shumway (1997) / Shumway & Sloan (1999) correction
    ret_shumway = np.copy(raw_returns)
    for i in range(n_stocks):
        if np.isfinite(delist_returns[i]):
            ret_shumway[i] = (1.0 + raw_returns[i]) * (1.0 + delist_returns[i]) - 1.0
        elif delist_codes[i] >= 200:
            is_perf = delist_codes[i] == 500 or (520 <= delist_codes[i] <= 584)
            penalty = (-0.55 if exchange_codes[i] == 3 else -0.30) if is_perf else 0.0
            ret_shumway[i] = (1.0 + raw_returns[i]) * (1.0 + penalty) - 1.0

    mean_naive = float(np.mean(ret_naive))
    mean_shumway = float(np.mean(ret_shumway))
    upward_bias_annual = float((mean_naive - mean_shumway) * 12.0 * 100.0)

    # Distress portfolio bias: only stocks that delisted
    delist_naive = float(np.mean(ret_naive[delist_idx]))
    delist_shumway = float(np.mean(ret_shumway[delist_idx]))
    distress_bias_monthly = float((delist_naive - delist_shumway) * 100.0)

    return {
        "n_stocks": n_stocks,
        "n_delistings": len(delist_idx),
        "mean_naive_pct": round(mean_naive * 100.0, 3),
        "mean_shumway_pct": round(mean_shumway * 100.0, 3),
        "annual_bias_pct": round(upward_bias_annual, 3),
        "distress_naive_pct": round(delist_naive * 100.0, 2),
        "distress_shumway_pct": round(delist_shumway * 100.0, 2),
        "distress_bias_pct": round(distress_bias_monthly, 2),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F1-L03-1 | Shumway (1997) Delisting Bias Simulator")
    print("------------------------------------------------------")
    res = simulate_delisting_bias(seed=77)
    print(f"Total cross-section size:               {res['n_stocks']} stocks")
    print(f"Delisting events in month:              {res['n_delistings']} events")
    print(f"Naive cross-sectional mean return:      {res['mean_naive_pct']:.3f}% / month")
    print(f"Shumway-corrected mean return:          {res['mean_shumway_pct']:.3f}% / month")
    print(f"Annualized upward bias from omission:   +{res['annual_bias_pct']:.3f}% p.a.")
    print(f"Delisted group return (naive):          {res['distress_naive_pct']:.2f}%")
    print(f"Delisted group return (corrected):      {res['distress_shumway_pct']:.2f}%")
    print(f"Delisting return understatement:        +{res['distress_bias_pct']:.2f}%")


main()

4Where the Canon Breaks: OTC Trading Halts and Modern Bankruptcy Emergence

While the \(-30\%\) and \(-55\%\) constant replacement returns are the universal academic standard (French Data Library; Chen & Zimmermann 2022), modern market microstructure introduces substantial cross-sectional heterogeneity in delisting resolutions.

In contemporary markets governed by FINRA Rule 6432 and SEC Rule 15c2-11, dark unquoted OTC stocks can suffer total 100% loss of trade liquidity. Conversely, in high-profile Chapter 11 reorganizations, distressed equity options and meme-stock retail trading occasionally generate sudden speculative rallies that cause realized delisting returns to deviate from Shumway's historical constants.

5Misconceptions

  1. "If CRSP reports a missing delisting return, the stock return for that month should be coded as 0.0%."

    Treating missing delisting returns as 0.0% implicitly assumes that an investor holding a company that went bankrupt and was kicked off the exchange suffered zero capital loss upon expulsion. In reality, performance delistings suffer average losses of -30% to -55%.

  2. "Delisting bias only affects individual stock return series, not aggregated factor portfolios."

    Because distressed, low-market-cap, and high-book-to-market companies experience dramatically higher delisting frequencies, delisting bias severely overstates small-cap and value factor premia if left uncorrected.

  3. "All delistings represent financial failure and should receive negative penalties."

    Merger delistings (DLSTCD 200-series) represent corporate acquisitions where target shareholders receive cash or shares at a substantial takeover premium (averaging +15% to +30%). Penalizing mergers would introduce severe downward bias into small-cap returns.

6Problem Set

Mechanics

An active NASDAQ common stock earns a raw monthly return of \(R_{\text{CRSP}} = -10.0\%\) before delisting for failing exchange minimum bid requirements (DLSTCD = 560). The CRSP delisting return is missing (NaN). Applying Shumway & Sloan's replacement penalty, what is the total compounded return for the month?

Solution

For NASDAQ (HEXCD = 3) with performance delisting (DLSTCD = 560), the replacement penalty is \(R_{\text{delist}} = -0.55\). Compounding: \(1 + R_{\text{total}} = (1 - 0.10) \times (1 - 0.55) = 0.90 \times 0.45 = 0.405\). Net total return: \(R_{\text{total}} = 0.405 - 1 = -0.595\) (\(-59.5\%\)).

An NYSE stock earns a raw monthly return of \(+2.0\%\) and delists due to a cash merger (DLSTCD = 211) with a recorded delisting return of \(+18.0\%\). What is the total compounded return for the month?

Solution

Because an explicit delisting return is recorded, it is used directly: \(1 + R_{\text{total}} = (1 + 0.02) \times (1 + 0.18) = 1.02 \times 1.18 = 1.2036\). Net total return: \(R_{\text{total}} = +20.36\%\).

An AMEX stock delists with DLSTCD = 520 and a missing delisting return. What replacement return must be assigned under Shumway's convention?

Solution

For AMEX (HEXCD = 2) and performance delisting (DLSTCD = 520), the replacement return is \(-30\%\) (\(-0.30\)).

Proof

Prove that omitting delisting returns causes the equal-weighted portfolio return to be strictly more biased than the value-weighted portfolio return.

Solution

Let \(\delta_i \equiv R_{i}^{\text{naive}} - R_{i}^{\text{total}} \ge 0\) be the upward return bias for stock \(i\), which is strictly positive for performance delistings. The equal-weighted bias is \(\Delta_{EW} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \delta_i\). The value-weighted bias is \(\Delta_{VW} = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \delta_i\), where \(w_i = ME_i / \sum_j ME_j\). Because performance delistings are overwhelmingly concentrated in microcaps and small caps with tiny market capitalizations (\(ME_i \ll \overline{ME}\)), the value-weights on delisting stocks satisfy \(w_i \ll \frac{1}{N}\). Consequently, \(\Delta_{VW} \ll \Delta_{EW}\), proving that equal-weighting suffers substantially greater delisting bias.

Prove that failing to compound partial-month returns with delisting returns (\(R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{CRSP}} + R_{\text{delist}}\)) violates no-arbitrage portfolio accounting.

Solution

Additively summing returns assumes simple interest across consecutive non-overlapping sub-periods. If an investor starts with \(\$1.00\), after the partial month they have \(1 + R_{\text{CRSP}}\). The delisting return is realized on this remaining capital, yielding terminal wealth \((1 + R_{\text{CRSP}})(1 + R_{\text{delist}})\). Additive summation yields \(1 + R_{\text{CRSP}} + R_{\text{delist}}\), which overstates wealth by \(R_{\text{CRSP}} \times R_{\text{delist}}\) when both returns are negative, violating cash flow conservation.

Prove that if missing delisting returns occur randomly across all stocks, the cross-sectional mean return remains biased upward.

Solution

Even if missingness is missing-at-random (MAR) with respect to firm characteristics, the conditional expectation of performance delisting returns is \(\E[R_{\text{delist}} \mid \text{delist}] \approx -0.35 < 0\). Replacing missing values with 0.0 imposes \(\E[R_{\text{delist}}^{\text{naive}}] = 0 > -0.35\), shifting the cross-sectional expectation strictly upward regardless of missingness independence.

Empirical

According to Shumway (1997), by how many percentage points per annum is the size premium (small minus big) overstated if delisting returns are ignored?

Solution

Shumway (1997, Table IV) documented that omitting delisting returns overstates the historical size premium on NYSE/AMEX by approximately 1.8% to 2.5% per annum, because small-cap firms experience substantially higher rates of distress delisting.

Why did Shumway & Sloan (1999) find that NASDAQ stocks required a -55% delisting replacement penalty compared to -30% for NYSE?

Solution

NASDAQ listing standards are substantially lower than NYSE standards, permitting smaller, unprofitable growth firms to trade publicly. When dropped from NASDAQ, these firms face severe market illiquidity and insolvency, suffering far greater capital destruction than larger NYSE firms.

In modern CRSP files, CRSP has retroactively populated many missing delisting returns. Does this eliminate the need for the Shumway algorithm?

Solution

No. While CRSP has conducted extensive historical audits, hundreds of performance delistings still possess missing returns. Modern quantitative pipelines must continue to check for missing values and apply Shumway defaults to guarantee data completeness.

Research

Design an empirical test to evaluate whether machine-learning models predicting individual OTC recovery values outperform Shumway's constant -30%/-55% defaults.

Solution

Train an ensemble regression on securities with verified OTC Pink Sheet recovery quotes using pre-delisting leverage, cash burn rate, bid-ask spread, and market equity as features. Evaluate out-of-sample RMSE against the constant baseline. While ML slightly reduces MSE, constant defaults prevent model overfitting in factor backtests.

How does delisting bias affect short-side profitability in long-short equity factor strategies?

Solution

In a long-short strategy, the short leg holds distressed securities. If delisting returns are omitted (recorded as 0%), the short leg appears to earn 0% instead of capturing the +30% to +55% short profit from catastrophic decline, causing researchers to understate short-side strategy profits.

Evaluate the legal and settlement constraints of realizing the -55% delisting return in an actual institutional short portfolio.

Solution

When a stock delists to the OTC Pink Sheets, prime brokers often recall stock borrow, forcing short sellers to buy back shares at high spreads before post-delisting liquidation occurs, preventing real-world traders from harvesting theoretical delisting profits.

  1. What empirical replacement penalty did Shumway (1997) establish for missing performance delisting returns on NYSE and AMEX?

    Options

  2. What empirical replacement penalty did Shumway & Sloan (1999) establish for missing performance delistings on NASDAQ?

    Options

    NASDAQ performance delistings suffer substantially greater losses than NYSE stocks, averaging -55% post-delisting decline.

  3. An active NYSE stock earns raw monthly return \(R_{\text{CRSP}} = -1.0\%\) before delisting for capital impairment (DLSTCD 500) with a missing delisting return. Applying Shumway's -30% penalty, what is the total compounded return in percent?

    \(R_{\text{total}} = (1 - 0.01) \times (1 - 0.30) - 1 = 0.99 \times 0.70 - 1 = 0.693 - 1 = -0.307\) (\(-30.7\%\)).

  4. Which CRSP delisting code range designates performance-related delisting events?

    Options

    500-series codes (specifically 500 and 520 to 584) identify liquidation, bankruptcy, insolvency, and failure to satisfy exchange minimum listing requirements.

  5. What replacement return should be assigned if a stock delists due to a merger (DLSTCD = 200) and the delisting return is missing?

    Options

    Mergers represent acquisitions where target shareholders receive a takeover premium; assigning a bankruptcy penalty would severely distort merger returns.

  6. Why does omitting delisting returns introduce greater bias into small-cap portfolios than large-cap portfolios?

    Options

    Performance delistings are heavily concentrated in small-cap and microcap equities, so omitting their terminal negative payoffs severely inflates small-firm returns.

  7. A stock earns raw return \(R_{\text{CRSP}} = 0.0\%\) and has recorded delisting return \(R_{\text{delist}} = +12.0\%\). What is the total compounded monthly return in percent?

    \(R_{\text{total}} = (1 + 0.0) \times (1 + 0.12) - 1 = +12.0\%\).

  8. How does delisting bias distort backtests of short-selling strategies?

    Options

    When a shorted stock collapses to zero upon delisting, the short seller earns a maximum gain. Omitting delisting returns makes the stock look like it earned 0%, missing the short profit.
