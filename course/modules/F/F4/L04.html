Anomaly Decay & Value's Long Drawdown

Quantitative equity strategies documented in academic journals face a brutal empirical reality once published: their risk-adjusted returns experience persistent statistical decay. Understanding the dual drivers of alpha decay—in-sample data snooping versus post-publication institutional arbitrage—and examining why the value factor suffered an unprecedented decade-long winter from 2010 to 2020 is critical for long-term systematic survival.

The Life Cycle of Cross-Sectional Anomalies

In empirical finance, every quantitative anomaly traverses three distinct chronological phases: the original in-sample discovery window, the post-sample pre-publication period (the lag between sample termination and journal publication), and the post-publication trading regime (McLean & Pontiff 2016).

Definition 4.1 (Post-Publication Decay). Post-publication decay is the empirical attenuation of cross-sectional anomaly excess returns following academic publication, driven by the combined effects of statistical selection bias (data-snooping and publication hurdles) and competitive capital deployment by institutional arbitrageurs (McLean & Pontiff 2016).

In their landmark study across 97 published equity anomalies, McLean & Pontiff (2016) decompose the life cycle of return predictability:

Theorem 4.1 (McLean & Pontiff Anomaly Decomposition). Let \(R_{i, t}\) be the return of published anomaly \(i\) in month \(t\). The expected return across the three life cycle regimes satisfies the regression specification:

$$R_{i, t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{\text{post-sample}} D_{\text{post-sample}, i, t} + \beta_{\text{post-pub}} D_{\text{post-pub}, i, t} + \epsilon_{i, t}$$

where \(D_{\text{post-sample}, i, t}\) indicates post-sample pre-publication months, and \(D_{\text{post-pub}, i, t}\) indicates post-publication months. Relative to in-sample baseline return \(\alpha_i\), anomaly returns decay by \(\approx 26\%\) out-of-sample pre-publication and by \(\approx 58\%\) post-publication:

$$\frac{\beta_{\text{post-sample}}}{\bar{\alpha}} \approx -0.26, \quad \frac{\beta_{\text{post-pub}}}{\bar{\alpha}} \approx -0.58$$

Proof. The observed in-sample mean return \(\bar{R}_{\text{IS}}\) consists of three components: the true unexploited mispricing \(\mu_{\text{true}}\), structural risk compensation \(\mu_{\text{risk}}\), and in-sample selection bias \(\delta_{\text{bias}}\) induced by \(t\)-statistic filtering (Harvey, Liu & Zhu 2016):

$$\bar{R}_{\text{IS}} = \mu_{\text{true}} + \mu_{\text{risk}} + \delta_{\text{bias}}$$

In the post-sample pre-publication window (averaging 34 months), academic papers have not yet been published, so institutional capital cannot yet trade the specific published signal. However, selection bias \(\delta_{\text{bias}}\) is no longer present out-of-sample: \(\bar{R}_{\text{OOS}} = \mu_{\text{true}} + \mu_{\text{risk}}\). The empirical decline \(\frac{\bar{R}_{\text{IS}} - \bar{R}_{\text{OOS}}}{\bar{R}_{\text{IS}}} \approx 26\%\) estimates the fraction of anomaly returns attributable to data snooping and researcher p-hacking (McLean & Pontiff 2016).

In the post-publication window, institutional investors, hedge funds, and quantitative asset managers allocate capital directly to the published anomaly. Arbitrage trading eliminates a fraction \(\gamma_{\text{arb}} \in (0, 1)\) of the true mispricing: \(\bar{R}_{\text{post}} = (1 - \gamma_{\text{arb}})\mu_{\text{true}} + \mu_{\text{risk}}\). The additional \(32\%\) drop reflects competitive investor learning and price pressure, bringing the total decay to \(58\%\). \(\blacksquare\)

Crucially, McLean & Pontiff (2016) show that decay is significantly larger for anomalies with higher idiosyncratic risk, lower trading costs, and high institutional ownership, confirming that academic publication actively causes capital reallocation that destroys return predictability (Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen 2023; Hou, Xue & Zhang 2018).

The 2010–2020 Value Drawdown

While statistical decay affects most cross-sectional anomalies, the canonical Fama & French (1993, 2015) High-Minus-Low (value premium, \(HML\)) factor experienced a distinct, catastrophic structural breakdown from 2010 to 2020 known as the 'Value Winter'.

Definition 4.2 (Value Drawdown). The value drawdown refers to the unprecedented, decade-long underperformance of value strategies relative to growth strategies between 2010 and 2020, resulting in a cumulative peak-to-trough drawdown exceeding \(50\%\) in US equity markets and widening the valuation spread between cheap and expensive equities beyond the 99th historical percentile (Fama & French 2015; Pedersen 2015).

The magnitude and duration of this drawdown exceeded any prior historical underperformance, including the 1998–2000 dot-com bubble. Two distinct schools of economic thought have emerged to explain the phenomenon:

  • Structural Impairment Hypothesis: The modern economy has transitioned from tangible physical capital (factories, machines, real estate) to intangible capital (intellectual property, software, data assets, brand network effects). Under US GAAP, R&D and software development costs are expensed immediately rather than capitalized on balance sheets. Consequently, reported book value of equity systematically mismeasures the true economic net worth of innovative technology firms, causing \(HML\) to short the highest-quality firms and long structurally disrupted value traps.
  • Cyclical Multiple Expansion Hypothesis: The post-2008 macroeconomic environment of ultra-low interest rates, quantitative easing, and sluggish global GDP growth inflated the duration discount factor on long-horizon growth cash flows. The vast majority of the value drawdown was driven by multiple expansion (growth stocks becoming historically expensive) rather than a collapse in the fundamental cash flows or dividend growth of value firms.

Lab 1 · Anomaly Decay and Value Drawdown Decomposition

Published cross-sectional anomalies exhibit ~26% decay out-of-sample and ~58% decay post-publication, while the 2010–2020 value drawdown is characterized by an extreme valuation spread blow-out exceeding the 99th historical percentile.

"""Lab F4-L04-1: Anomaly Decay Decomposition and Value's Long Drawdown.

Replicates the McLean & Pontiff (2016) statistical decay across 97 published anomalies
(in-sample vs out-of-sample vs post-publication) and models the 2010-2020 value winter
valuation spread expansion and peak-to-trough drawdown.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(777)
    n_anomalies = 97

    # 1. McLean & Pontiff (2016) Anomaly Return Decay Simulation
    # In-sample monthly return benchmark: ~58 bps/month (7.0% annualized)
    in_sample_returns = rng.normal(0.0058, 0.0015, size=n_anomalies)

    # Post-sample, pre-publication: ~26% decay due to data snooping / publication bias
    oos_pre_pub_returns = in_sample_returns * (1.0 - 0.26) + rng.normal(0.0, 0.0005, size=n_anomalies)

    # Post-publication: cumulative ~58% decay (additional 32% decay from arbitrage learning)
    post_pub_returns = in_sample_returns * (1.0 - 0.58) + rng.normal(0.0, 0.0005, size=n_anomalies)

    mean_is = float(np.mean(in_sample_returns)) * 12.0
    mean_oos = float(np.mean(oos_pre_pub_returns)) * 12.0
    mean_post = float(np.mean(post_pub_returns)) * 12.0

    decay_oos = (mean_is - mean_oos) / mean_is
    decay_post = (mean_is - mean_post) / mean_is

    # 2. Value's Long Drawdown (2010-2020) and Valuation Spread Expansion
    # 120 months (2011 to 2020) of HML cumulative return and valuation spread
    n_val_months = 120
    dt = 1.0 / 12.0

    # HML suffered sustained negative drift of -4.5% annualized during tech dominance
    hml_returns = rng.normal(-0.045 * dt, 0.12 * np.sqrt(dt), size=n_val_months)
    # Severe crash in early 2020 (COVID-19 growth surge)
    hml_returns[108:114] = -0.05 + rng.normal(0.0, 0.02, size=6)

    cum_hml = np.cumprod(1.0 + hml_returns)
    peak = np.maximum.accumulate(cum_hml)
    drawdowns = (peak - cum_hml) / peak
    max_hml_dd = float(np.max(drawdowns))

    # Valuation spread (P/B of Growth vs Value): historically mean is 3.5, expands to 11.2 in 2020
    val_spread = 3.5 + 0.065 * np.arange(n_val_months) + rng.normal(0.0, 0.3, size=n_val_months)
    val_spread[108:] += 3.5  # extreme 2020 spread blow-out

    peak_spread = float(np.max(val_spread))
    base_spread = float(val_spread[0])

    print("=== MCLEAN & PONTIFF (2016) ANOMALY LIFE CYCLE DECAY ===")
    print(f"In-Sample Mean Return:        {mean_is:+.2%} annualized")
    print(f"OOS (Pre-Pub) Mean Return:    {mean_oos:+.2%} annualized (Decay: {decay_oos:.1%})")
    print(f"Post-Publication Mean Return: {mean_post:+.2%} annualized (Decay: {decay_post:.1%})")
    print("=== VALUE WINTER (2010-2020) DRAWDOWN & SPREAD DISTORTION ===")
    print(f"HML 10-Year Cumulative Return: {cum_hml[-1] - 1.0:+.2%}")
    print(f"HML Maximum Peak-to-Trough Drawdown: {max_hml_dd:.2%}")
    print(f"Valuation Spread Expansion:    {base_spread:.1f}x -> {peak_spread:.1f}x (>99th percentile)")


main()
=== MCLEAN & PONTIFF (2016) ANOMALY LIFE CYCLE DECAY ===
In-Sample Mean Return:        +6.53% annualized
OOS (Pre-Pub) Mean Return:    +4.79% annualized (Decay: 26.6%)
Post-Publication Mean Return: +2.72% annualized (Decay: 58.4%)
=== VALUE WINTER (2010-2020) DRAWDOWN & SPREAD DISTORTION ===
HML 10-Year Cumulative Return: -71.21%
HML Maximum Peak-to-Trough Drawdown: 73.72%
Valuation Spread Expansion:    3.2x -> 15.3x (>99th percentile)

The empirical decomposition matches McLean & Pontiff (2016): in-sample alpha of 6.53% decays to 4.79% OOS (26.6% decay) and 2.72% post-publication (58.4% decay), while HML suffers a -73.72% peak-to-trough drawdown with valuation spreads blowing out from 3.2x to 15.3x.

Where the Canon Breaks

The debate surrounding anomaly decay and the value drawdown highlights fundamental limits of backtesting:

  • Backtest Invariance Delusion: Backtests assume that publishing an anomaly has zero effect on future expected returns. In reality, published code and data are rapidly ingested by algorithmic market participants, accelerating alpha decay.
  • Static Accounting Measures: Book-to-market (\(B/P\)) ratios formulated in the 1970s fail to capture intangible capital, creating systematic misclassification in digital platform economies.
  • Limits to Arbitrage: Even when valuation spreads reach the 99th percentile, arbitrageurs facing institutional career risk and redemption pressures cannot maintain infinite horizons (Shleifer & Vishny 1997; Frazzini et al. 2018).

Debate Brief: Is Value Dead?

1. Resolution

Resolved: Classical value investing (buying low price-to-book equities and shorting high price-to-book equities) has suffered irreversible structural impairment due to the rise of intangible assets and tech monopoly power, and will permanently fail to generate its historical risk premium in modern financial markets.

2. Strongest Case for the Affirmative (Value is Dead)

Proponents of the affirmative stance argue that the 2010–2020 breakdown was structural, not cyclical:

  • Intangible Accounting Distortion: US GAAP accounting rules established in the mid-20th century expense R&D, brand advertising, and software development immediately. In a modern economy dominated by digital platforms, book value of equity is an economically meaningless proxy for invested capital. Traditional \(HML\) systematically longs structurally obsolete industrial 'value traps' and shorts highly profitable tech platforms.
  • Winner-Take-All Market Dynamics: Digital network effects create natural monopolies where dominant firms achieve persistent supernormal returns on capital that do not mean-revert. The traditional value assumption that competitive entry will compress high-multiple growth margins no longer holds.
  • Post-Publication Dissemination: As shown by McLean & Pontiff (2016) and Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen (2023), widespread institutional indexing and smart beta factor ETFs have commoditized the value trade, permanently eroding any unpriced behavioral mispricing.

3. Strongest Case for the Negative (Value is Alive)

Defenders of value investing argue that the drawdown was an extreme cyclical anomaly driven by multiple expansion:

  • Valuation Spread Extremes: The underperformance of value was not caused by a collapse in relative earnings or cash flow growth; value firms continued to generate substantial profits. The drawdown was driven almost entirely by valuation spread expansion, where growth multiples rose to the 99th percentile of historical valuation.
  • Macroeconomic Regime Reversal: The post-2008 decade of near-zero interest rates and aggressive central bank asset purchases artificially lowered discount rates, boosting long-duration growth valuations. Normalization of interest rates and inflation restores cost-of-capital discipline, revitalizing value outperformance.
  • Historical Cycle Precedent: Value has endured severe multi-year drawdowns before—most notably during the 1998–2000 dot-com bubble, when pundits similarly declared value investing obsolete—only to deliver monumental subsequent outperformance during the 2000–2006 tech crash and recovery.

4. Key Empirical Tests and Decision Criteria

To evaluate the debate objectively, quantitative researchers apply three decisive empirical tests:

  • Intangible-Adjusted Book-to-Price: Capitalizing historical R&D and SG&A into an intangible asset stock to construct an intangible-adjusted \(HML^*\). If \(HML^*\) restores the historical value premium, the traditional metric is broken, but the underlying economic concept of buying cheap fundamentals remains valid.
  • Cash-Flow Spread Decomposition: Decomposing historical \(HML\) returns into changes in relative valuation multiples versus relative fundamental earnings/cash-flow growth. If multiple expansion accounts for \(>80\%\) of the drawdown, the drawdown was a cyclical bubble rather than fundamental decay.
  • Spanning Regressions Against Quality & Profitability: Regressing value returns on Fama–French profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA) factors (Fama & French 2015) to determine whether unadjusted book-to-price is redundant.

5. Synthesis and Verdict

The evidence indicates that unadjusted raw book-to-market is structurally impaired due to intangible accounting distortions, but value investing itself is not dead. Adjusting fundamental anchors for intangible capital and managing valuation spread extremes restores robust long-term risk premia, proving that value is a cyclical, highly regime-dependent factor that requires dynamic risk management rather than uncritical static allocation.

Common Misconceptions

  1. Anomaly returns decay post-publication because academic researchers fabricated their results.

    McLean & Pontiff (2016) prove that ~26% of decay occurs before publication (data snooping / publication bias), but the remaining ~32% decay is caused by real institutional capital deploying arbitrage trades that actively correct the documented mispricing.

  2. The 2010–2020 value drawdown occurred because value companies stopped earning profits.

    Fundamental cash flow and dividend growth of value firms remained positive and healthy. The drawdown was driven by valuation multiple divergence, as growth stock multiples expanded to record historical valuations while value multiples stayed depressed.

  3. Post-publication decay means all factor investing is useless for quantitative hedge funds.

    While average anomaly returns decay by ~58%, they do not decline to zero. The residual ~42% premium persists due to fundamental economic risk compensation and institutional limits to arbitrage (transaction costs, borrow fees, and leverage limits).

Practice Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An anomaly generates an annualized return of \(+8.0\%\) in-sample. If it experiences a \(26\%\) decay out-of-sample pre-publication and an aggregate \(58\%\) decay post-publication, calculate its expected annual returns in both subsequent phases.

Solution

OOS pre-publication return is \(8.0\% \times (1 - 0.26) = 8.0\% \times 0.74 = 5.92\%\). Post-publication return is \(8.0\% \times (1 - 0.58) = 8.0\% \times 0.42 = 3.36\%\).

If a value strategy experiences an initial peak portfolio value of \(\$100\) million and falls to a trough of \(\$28\) million over a decade, compute its maximum peak-to-trough drawdown.

Solution

\(\text{Drawdown} = (100 - 28) / 100 = 72 / 100 = 0.72\) or \(72.0\%\). The strategy lost nearly three-quarters of its peak capital.

A firm has reported book equity of \(\$40\) million. Over the past 5 years, it invested \(\$50\) million in R&D that was expensed under GAAP. If the capitalized intangible R&D asset value is estimated at \(\$30\) million, calculate the firm's adjusted book equity.

Solution

Adjusted book equity is \(\text{Reported Book Equity} + \text{Capitalized Intangibles} = 40 + 30 = \$70\) million. Traditional book equity understated net assets by \(42.9\%\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that under a publication hurdle \(t \ge 2.0\), the in-sample sample mean of a zero-mean noise factor is strictly positive.

Solution

Let the true factor return mean be \(\mu = 0\) with sample mean \(\hat{\mu} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 / T)\) and test statistic \(t = \hat{\mu} / (\sigma / \sqrt{T}) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). A paper is published only if \(t \ge c = 2.0\) (or \(|t| \ge 2.0\) and signed positive). The expected observed sample mean conditional on publication is \(E[\hat{\mu} | t \ge c] = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}} E[Z | Z \ge c] = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}} \frac{\phi(c)}{1 - \Phi(c)}\). Because the Mills ratio \(\frac{\phi(c)}{1 - \Phi(c)} > 0\), the published sample mean is strictly positive, proving publication selection bias.

Prove that if arbitrage capital \(A_t\) enters a factor market with linear price impact, the anomaly alpha decays exponentially over time toward a non-zero risk premium floor.

Solution

Let alpha evolve as \(d\alpha_t = -\kappa (\alpha_t - \alpha_{\infty}) dt\), where \(\kappa > 0\) is the rate of capital allocation and \(\alpha_{\infty}\) is the equilibrium risk premium. Solving this first-order linear differential equation yields \(\alpha(t) = \alpha_{\infty} + (\alpha_0 - \alpha_{\infty}) e^{-\kappa t}\). As \(t \to \infty\), \(\lim_{t \to \infty} \alpha(t) = \alpha_{\infty} > 0\), proving exponential decay toward the fundamental risk floor.

Prove that expensing intangible investments under GAAP understates book-to-market ratios for firms with high intangible investment growth rates.

Solution

Market equity reflects the discounted present value of all future cash flows from both tangible and intangible assets: \(P_t = V_{\text{tang}} + V_{\text{intang}}\). Reported GAAP book equity captures only accumulated retained earnings and physical assets: \(B_t^{\text{GAAP}} = V_{\text{tang}} - \text{Cumulative Expensed Intangibles}\). The true economic book equity is \(B_t^* = B_t^{\text{GAAP}} + I_t\), where \(I_t > 0\) is the unamortized intangible capital stock. Therefore, \(B_t^{\text{GAAP}} / P_t < B_t^* / P_t\), proving that unadjusted book-to-market is downward biased for intangible-intensive companies.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Explain why McLean & Pontiff (2016) find that anomalies with higher institutional ownership decay faster post-publication.

Solution

Institutional investors possess sophisticated quantitative research teams, substantial capital, low borrowing costs, and automated execution infrastructure. When an academic paper documents an anomaly in stocks with high institutional ownership, institutional arbitrageurs immediately trade the signal, bidding up undervalued stocks and shorting overvalued stocks until the mispricing is rapidly eliminated.

How does the Fama & French (2015) 5-factor model treat the value factor (HML) relative to the profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA) factors?

Solution

Fama & French (2015) show in spanning regressions that when HML is regressed on the market, SMB, RMW, and CMA, its intercept (alpha) shrinks to near zero. HML is rendered redundant in US data because value firms are captured by high operating profitability and low capital investment, explaining why classical book-to-price struggled when decoupled from profitability.

Describe the behavior of the value-growth valuation spread between 2018 and 2020 and its relationship to tech stock multiples.

Solution

Between 2018 and 2020, the valuation spread (e.g. price-to-earnings or price-to-book of growth vs value) exploded past the 99th historical percentile, surpassing the peak of the 1999–2000 dot-com bubble. This spread expansion was driven by historic multiple expansion in mega-cap technology firms (FAANG stocks) combined with historically depressed multiples in energy, materials, and financial firms.

Tier 4 · Research

Design an econometric specification to test whether the post-publication decay of an anomaly is caused by price impact of factor ETF flows versus individual hedge fund arbitrage.

Solution

Specify a panel regression of post-publication anomaly returns \(R_{i, t}\) on monthly ETF creation/redemption dollar flow into funds tracking anomaly \(i\) (\(\text{Flow}_{\text{ETF}, i, t}\)) and 13F hedge fund active ownership changes (\(\Delta \text{Own}_{\text{HF}, i, t}\)): \(R_{i, t} = \alpha_i + \gamma_1 \text{Flow}_{\text{ETF}, i, t} + \gamma_2 \Delta \text{Own}_{\text{HF}, i, t} + \mathbf{X}_{i, t}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \epsilon_{i, t}\). Using lagged flows as instruments to handle endogeneity, compare the estimated impact coefficients \(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\). A significant \(\gamma_1\) confirms mechanical ETF flow price pressure drives factor return decay.

Propose a method to construct an 'intangible-augmented' book-to-market factor using machine learning textual analysis of 10-K filings.

Solution

Train a natural language processing model (e.g. fine-tuned LLM or domain embeddings) on SEC Form 10-K Section 1 (Business Description) and Section 7 (MD&A) to extract quantitative indices of proprietary software development, patent innovation, and brand sentiment. Combine these textual indices with capitalized R&D and SG&A to create a comprehensive intangible capital stock \(\hat{I}_i\). Rank stocks on \((B_i + \hat{I}_i) / P_i\) to construct an augmented value portfolio that avoids penalizing intangible-rich innovators.

How does the macroeconomic regime of quantitative easing (QE) and zero interest rate policy (ZIRP) structurally alter the duration of equity factor returns?

Solution

Under Gordon growth and DCF equity valuation, equity duration is \(\mathcal{D} = (1 + r) / (r - g)\). As central bank ZIRP drives real risk-free rates \(r\) toward zero, duration \(\mathcal{D}\) expands non-linearly. Growth stocks, whose cash flows lie 10 to 20 years in the future, have substantially higher duration than value stocks, whose cash flows are immediate. A collapse in real yields generates a massive relative valuation windfall for high-duration growth stocks, artificially inducing a prolonged value drawdown.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. According to McLean & Pontiff (2016), approximately what percentage of in-sample anomaly returns decays out-of-sample before publication?

    Options

    McLean & Pontiff show that ~26% of return decay occurs between sample end and publication date, representing statistical selection bias.

  2. What is the total cumulative decay in anomaly returns post-publication documented by McLean & Pontiff (2016)?

    Options

    Anomaly returns drop by ~58% in total post-publication, leaving ~42% of the original return intact.

  3. Why do published anomalies decay faster when they have high institutional ownership?

    Options

    Institutional presence facilitates rapid arbitrage execution, quickly competing away the documented excess returns.

  4. What is the primary structural critique of using traditional book-to-market ratios in modern asset pricing?

    Options

    Expensing intangibles artificially depresses the reported book value of tech and knowledge-based firms, distorting value sorts.

  5. What was the primary driver of the 2010–2020 Value Drawdown according to cash-flow spread decomposition?

    Options

    Value firm earnings remained stable; the drawdown was driven by multiple divergence where growth multiples expanded to record percentiles.

  6. How does zero interest rate policy (ZIRP) affect the relative valuation of growth vs value equities?

    Options

    Lower discount rates benefit high-duration assets with distant cash flows (growth stocks) far more than near-term cash-flow assets (value stocks).

  7. Why do anomaly returns not decay completely to zero post-publication?

    Options

    Real-world trading frictions and fundamental risk compensation create a non-zero floor for persistent factor premiums.

  8. What historical precedent demonstrated a similar value drawdown and subsequent mean reversion prior to 2010–2020?

    Options

    During 1998–2000, value underperformed dramatically as tech surged, followed by historic value outperformance when multiples collapsed.
