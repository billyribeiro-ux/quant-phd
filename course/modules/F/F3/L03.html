Time-Series Momentum

Cross-sectional momentum compares assets relative to one another, buying relative outperformers and selling relative underperformers. In contrast, time-series momentum evaluates each asset strictly against its own past return history. Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) established that an asset's past 12-month return positively forecasts its future excess return across 58 liquid futures contracts spanning equity indices, sovereign bonds, currencies, and commodities over a 25-year history. Crucially, time-series momentum dynamically adjusts its directional market exposure, delivering uncorrelated 'crisis alpha' during prolonged market collapses.

1. Formulating Time-Series Momentum

In classical quantitative finance, cross-sectional momentum (CSMOM, Jegadeesh & Titman 1993) ranks an \(N\)-asset universe cross-sectionally, constructing a zero-net-investment portfolio by going long the top decile and short the bottom decile. Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) introduced time-series momentum (TSMOM), an absolute trend-following rule where the position in asset \(i\) depends solely on the sign and magnitude of asset \(i\)'s own past return.

Definition 3.1 · Time-Series Momentum (TSMOM)

Time-series momentum (TSMOM) is an absolute trend-following trading strategy in which the directional portfolio allocation to each instrument \(i\) at time \(t\) is governed strictly by the sign of its own cumulative excess return over a formation lookback horizon \(k\), scaled inversely by its conditional trailing volatility \(\sigma_t^i\):

\[ w_t^i = \text{sign}\left(r_{t-k:t}^i\right) \cdot \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\hat{\sigma}_t^i} \]

where \(r_{t-k:t}^i = \sum_{s=0}^{k-1} r_{t-s}^i\) represents the past \(k\)-month cumulative return, \(\hat{\sigma}_t^i\) is the ex-ante annualized conditional volatility estimate of instrument \(i\), and \(\sigma_{\text{target}}\) is a constant portfolio annualized target volatility per instrument (typically \(10\%\) to \(40\%\)).

The total portfolio return of a multi-asset TSMOM book containing \(N_t\) liquid instruments at month \(t+1\) is:

\[ r_{t+1}^{\text{TSMOM}} = \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} w_t^i r_{t+1}^i = \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} \text{sign}\left(r_{t-k:t}^i\right) \cdot \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\hat{\sigma}_t^i} r_{t+1}^i \]

Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) documented that across 58 liquid futures contracts covering 24 commodities, 12 equity indices, 13 sovereign bond contracts, and 9 currency pairs from 1985 to 2009, past 12-month excess returns predict next-month returns with positive coefficients for every single contract. A diversified TSMOM portfolio generated an annualized Sharpe ratio of \(1.10\), outperforming standard equity benchmarks while exhibiting near-zero unconditional correlation to global equities and bonds.

2. Time-Series vs. Cross-Sectional Momentum

Although TSMOM and CSMOM both exploit return persistence, their portfolio mechanics and macroeconomic risk factor exposures are fundamentally distinct:

  • Dollar Neutrality: CSMOM is strictly dollar-neutral at every time step (\(\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^i = 0\)). TSMOM is unconstrained: its net portfolio weight \(\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^i\) varies dynamically across time, ranging from \(-N\) (when all assets are trending down) to \(+N\) (when all assets are trending up).
  • Market Beta Variation: In broad bull markets, TSMOM holds long positions across almost all asset classes, generating a large positive market beta (\(\beta > 0\)). In broad bear markets, TSMOM systematically flips to short positions across asset classes, generating a negative market beta (\(\beta < 0\)).

Theorem 3.1 · Orthogonal Decomposition of TSMOM into CSMOM and Market Timing

Let \(w_t^i\) denote the position weight in asset \(i\) and \(r_{t+1}^i\) denote its return. The portfolio return of TSMOM can be decomposed exactly into three components: a dollar-neutral cross-sectional momentum strategy, an aggregate market-timing return, and an interaction term:

\[ r_{t+1}^{\text{TSMOM}} = \sum_{i=1}^N \left(w_t^i - \bar{w}_t\right) \left(r_{t+1}^i - \bar{r}_{t+1}\right) + \bar{w}_t \cdot \bar{r}_{t+1} + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^i \bar{r}_{t+1} \]

where \(\bar{w}_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^i\) is the net market directional exposure and \(\bar{r}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N r_{t+1}^i\) is the cross-sectional average market return.

Proof of Theorem 3.1

Express the inner product \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^i r_{t+1}^i\) by centering both weights and returns around their cross-sectional means:

\[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^i r_{t+1}^i = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left( (w_t^i - \bar{w}_t) + \bar{w}_t \right) \left( (r_{t+1}^i - \bar{r}_{t+1}) + \bar{r}_{t+1} \right) \]

Expanding the product:

\[ = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (w_t^i - \bar{w}_t)(r_{t+1}^i - \bar{r}_{t+1}) + \bar{w}_t \left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (r_{t+1}^i - \bar{r}_{t+1})\right) + \bar{r}_{t+1} \left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (w_t^i - \bar{w}_t)\right) + \bar{w}_t \bar{r}_{t+1} \]

By definition of the arithmetic mean, \(\sum_{i=1}^N (r_{t+1}^i - \bar{r}_{t+1}) = 0\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^N (w_t^i - \bar{w}_t) = 0\). The second and third terms vanish identically, leaving:

\[ r_{t+1}^{\text{TSMOM}} = \Cov_i(w_t^i, r_{t+1}^i) + \bar{w}_t \bar{r}_{t+1} \]

where \(\Cov_i(w_t^i, r_{t+1}^i)\) is the dollar-neutral cross-sectional selection return (CSMOM) and \(\bar{w}_t \bar{r}_{t+1}\) is the dynamic market-timing return.

Example 3.1 · Crisis Alpha Decomposition

Suppose an investor trades 4 futures contracts. During a severe macro crisis, all 4 assets have negative past returns (\(r_{t-12:t}^i < 0\)), so \(w_t^i = -1\) for all \(i\), giving net exposure \(\bar{w}_t = -1.0\). In month \(t+1\), the market crashes further: returns are \([-8\%, -10\%, -12\%, -14\%]\), with average return \(\bar{r}_{t+1} = -11.0\%\). The cross-sectional selection return is zero (\(w_t^i - \bar{w}_t = 0\)). The market timing component is \(\bar{w}_t \bar{r}_{t+1} = (-1.0) \times (-0.11) = +11.0\%\). TSMOM delivers an \(11.0\%\) gain during a catastrophic crash, demonstrating why absolute trend following produces 'crisis alpha' that pure dollar-neutral CSMOM cannot provide.

3. Volatility Scaling and Equal Risk Contribution

A central pillar of the Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) framework is ex-ante inverse volatility scaling. Futures contracts exhibit dramatically disparate volatility profiles: 10-year Treasury bond futures have annualized volatilities around \(6\%\), whereas crude oil, natural gas, or equity indices exhibit volatilities of \(25\%\) to \(50\%\).

If positions were unscaled (\(w_t^i = \text{sign}(r_{t-k:t}^i)\)), high-volatility commodity and equity contracts would completely dominate portfolio variance, rendering bond and currency signals economically irrelevant. Scaling each contract by \(\sigma_{\text{target}} / \hat{\sigma}_t^i\) equalizes the ex-ante risk contribution of each asset.

Moskowitz et al. (2012) estimate conditional volatility using an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) or trailing rolling standard deviation over 60 to 120 trading days:

\[ (\hat{\sigma}_t^i)^2 = 261 \sum_{s=0}^\infty (1 - \delta) \delta^s \left(r_{t-1-s}^i - \bar{r}_t^i\right)^2 \]

with decay parameter \(\delta = 0.98\). This inverse-volatility scaling dampens portfolio positions when market volatility spikes, cutting exposure during turbulence and increasing exposure in placid, low-volatility regimes.

4. Crisis Alpha and Asymmetric Tail Protection

The primary institutional rationale for allocating to time-series momentum (commonly managed by Commodity Trading Advisors, or CTAs) is crisis alpha: positive performance during prolonged bear markets.

Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) analyzed the performance of TSMOM during the worst historical drawdowns of global equity markets (including the 2000–2002 dot-com bust and the 2007–2009 global financial crisis). While equity markets collapsed by \(40\%\) to \(55\%\), diversified TSMOM generated positive cumulative returns exceeding \(25\%\), acting as a synthetic long put option on global growth.

Unlike equity momentum (Daniel & Moskowitz 2016), which suffers catastrophic 'momentum crashes' when shorted distressed stocks suddenly rally following a bear market trough, cross-asset TSMOM hedges equity crashes through multi-asset breadth: long positions in flight-to-safety sovereign bonds and short positions in cyclical commodities and equities.

Lab 1 · Time-Series Momentum vs Cross-Sectional Momentum

Simulating TSMOM and CSMOM across 12 futures assets will reveal that TSMOM provides crisis alpha and negative market beta during severe crashes, whereas CSMOM remains market-neutral.

"""Lab F3-L03-1: Time-Series Momentum (TSMOM) vs Cross-Sectional Momentum (CSMOM).

Compares time-series momentum (Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen 2012) with cross-sectional
momentum (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993) across 12 diversified liquid futures assets,
highlighting dynamic net market exposure, volatility scaling, and crisis alpha.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_tsmom_vs_csmom(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate and compare TSMOM and CSMOM across market regimes."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_months = 240
    n_assets = 12
    target_vol_ann = 0.10

    # 1. Simulate asset returns with shared macro factor + idiosyncratic components
    # Include an explicit market crisis period (months 100 to 112)
    macro_factor = rng.normal(0.06 / 12, 0.16 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
    # Severe crisis: 12-month crash of -45% cumulative in macro factor
    macro_factor[100:112] = rng.normal(-0.45 / 12, 0.25 / np.sqrt(12), size=12)

    betas = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, size=n_assets)
    returns = np.zeros((n_months, n_assets))
    for i in range(n_assets):
        idio = rng.normal(0.0, 0.20 / np.sqrt(12), size=n_months)
        returns[:, i] = betas[i] * macro_factor + idio

    # 2. Compute TSMOM and CSMOM strategies
    lookback = 12
    t_start = lookback

    tsmom_returns = np.zeros(n_months - t_start)
    csmom_returns = np.zeros(n_months - t_start)
    market_returns = macro_factor[t_start:]

    for idx, t in enumerate(range(t_start, n_months)):
        past_12m = np.sum(returns[t - lookback : t, :], axis=0)
        # Trailing volatility estimate (annualized)
        hist_vol = np.std(returns[max(0, t - 24) : t, :], axis=0, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12)
        hist_vol = np.maximum(hist_vol, 0.05)  # clamp

        # TSMOM positions: sign(past_12m) scaled by target_vol / hist_vol
        ts_pos = np.sign(past_12m) * (target_vol_ann / hist_vol) / n_assets
        tsmom_returns[idx] = float(np.sum(ts_pos * returns[t, :]))

        # CSMOM positions: demeaned rank weights (dollar-neutral)
        ranks = np.argsort(np.argsort(past_12m))
        cs_weights = (ranks - np.mean(ranks)) / np.sum(np.abs(ranks - np.mean(ranks)))
        csmom_returns[idx] = float(np.sum(cs_weights * returns[t, :]))

    # Performance metrics
    def calc_stats(r: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
        mean_ann = float(np.mean(r) * 12 * 100)
        vol_ann = float(np.std(r, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12) * 100)
        sr = float(mean_ann / vol_ann) if vol_ann > 0 else 0.0
        # Performance during crash (months 100 to 112, offset by lookback)
        crash_slice = r[100 - t_start : 112 - t_start]
        crash_ret = float(np.sum(crash_slice) * 100)
        return round(mean_ann, 2), round(vol_ann, 2), round(sr, 2), round(crash_ret, 2)

    mkt_m, mkt_v, mkt_sr, mkt_cr = calc_stats(market_returns)
    ts_m, ts_v, ts_sr, ts_cr = calc_stats(tsmom_returns)
    cs_m, cs_v, cs_sr, cs_cr = calc_stats(csmom_returns)

    # Beta to market
    cov_ts_mkt = float(np.cov(tsmom_returns, market_returns)[0, 1])
    cov_cs_mkt = float(np.cov(csmom_returns, market_returns)[0, 1])
    var_mkt = float(np.var(market_returns, ddof=1))
    beta_ts = round(cov_ts_mkt / var_mkt, 2)
    beta_cs = round(cov_cs_mkt / var_mkt, 2)

    return {
        "mkt_mean": mkt_m,
        "mkt_vol": mkt_v,
        "mkt_sr": mkt_sr,
        "mkt_crash": mkt_cr,
        "ts_mean": ts_m,
        "ts_vol": ts_v,
        "ts_sr": ts_sr,
        "ts_crash": ts_cr,
        "ts_beta": beta_ts,
        "cs_mean": cs_m,
        "cs_vol": cs_v,
        "cs_sr": cs_sr,
        "cs_crash": cs_cr,
        "cs_beta": beta_cs,
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F3-L03-1 | Time-Series Momentum vs Cross-Sectional Momentum")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = simulate_tsmom_vs_csmom(seed=42)
    header = (
        f"{'Strategy':<10} | {'Mean %':<8} | {'Vol %':<8} | {'Sharpe':<8} | "
        f"{'Crash Return %':<16} | {'Market Beta':<12}"
    )
    print(header)
    print("-" * 72)
    mm, mv, msr = res["mkt_mean"], res["mkt_vol"], res["mkt_sr"]
    mcr = res["mkt_crash"]
    print(f"{'Market':<10} | {mm:<8.2f} | {mv:<8.2f} | {msr:<8.2f} | {mcr:<16.2f} | {1.00:<12.2f}")

    tm, tv, tsr = res["ts_mean"], res["ts_vol"], res["ts_sr"]
    tcr, tb = res["ts_crash"], res["ts_beta"]
    print(f"{'TSMOM':<10} | {tm:<8.2f} | {tv:<8.2f} | {tsr:<8.2f} | {tcr:<16.2f} | {tb:<12.2f}")

    cm, cv, csr = res["cs_mean"], res["cs_vol"], res["cs_sr"]
    ccr, cb = res["cs_crash"], res["cs_beta"]
    print(f"{'CSMOM':<10} | {cm:<8.2f} | {cv:<8.2f} | {csr:<8.2f} | {ccr:<16.2f} | {cb:<12.2f}")
    print("-" * 72)
    print(f"TSMOM delivers crisis alpha ({res['ts_crash']:+.2f}% during market crash) via dynamic short positioning.")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Despite its theoretical elegance, time-series momentum faces severe real-world operational challenges:

  • Whipsaw Losses in Range-Bound Markets: In choppy, sideways markets without persistent directional trends, trend signals trigger repeated false breakouts. An investor buys near local tops and sells near local bottoms, accumulating severe whipsaw drag.
  • V-Shaped Market Reversals: When markets experience sharp V-shaped rebounds (such as the March–April 2020 pandemic rebound or the November 2020 vaccine announcement), TSMOM holds massive short positions built up during the selloff. The violent rally crushes short positions before the 12-month formation window can flip positive.
  • Rolling Futures and Financing Frictions: Maintaining continuous futures exposure across 50+ contracts requires active calendar rolling. In contango markets, roll decay eats into trading gains, and margin maintenance during multi-asset volatility spikes demands large cash reserves.

Misconceptions

  1. Time-series momentum and cross-sectional momentum are mathematically equivalent strategies with identical risk profiles.

    Evidence: By Theorem 3.1, TSMOM contains an active market-timing exposure (\(\bar{w}_t \bar{r}_{t+1}\)) that dynamically fluctuates between net short and net long; CSMOM is strictly dollar-neutral with zero net market investment at all times.

  2. Inverse-volatility scaling is merely an arbitrary leverage tweak that does not alter risk-adjusted Sharpe ratios.

    Evidence: Moskowitz et al. (2012) prove that volatility scaling substantially expands portfolio Sharpe ratios by dampening exposures during turbulent, high-volatility regimes and re-leveraging during calm, persistent trends.

  3. Time-series momentum achieves its highest returns during quiet, low-volatility bull markets.

    Evidence: TSMOM generates its largest outperformance ('crisis alpha') during extreme bear markets and persistent macro disruptions, whereas quiet range-bound markets lead to frequent whipsaw losses.

Problem Set

Mechanics

An asset's past 12-month return is \(+18.5\%\). Its annualized trailing conditional volatility is estimated as \(\hat{\sigma}_t = 25.0\%\). If the target volatility per instrument is \(\sigma_{\text{target}} = 10.0\%\), calculate the TSMOM position weight \(w_t^i\).

Solution

Applying the Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) position sizing formula:

\[ w_t^i = \text{sign}\left(r_{t-12:t}^i\right) \cdot \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\hat{\sigma}_t^i} \]

Since the past return \(+18.5\%\) is positive, \(\text{sign}(+0.185) = +1\). With target volatility \(0.10\) and conditional volatility \(0.25\):

\[ w_t^i = +1 \cdot \frac{0.10}{0.25} = +0.40 \]

The investor allocates \(+40\%\) of target capital to this instrument.

A 10-year Treasury bond futures contract has past 12-month return \(-4.2\%\) and trailing volatility \(\hat{\sigma}_t = 5.0\%\). For \(\sigma_{\text{target}} = 10.0\%\), compute the position weight.

Solution

Because the past return is negative (\(-4.2\% < 0\)), \(\text{sign}(-0.042) = -1\). The position weight is:

\[ w_t^i = -1 \cdot \frac{0.10}{0.05} = -2.00 \]

The investor holds a leveraged short position of \(200\%\) of target capital in the bond futures contract.

A portfolio trades 5 assets. In month \(t\), their directional position weights are \([+0.8, +0.6, -0.4, -0.6, -0.8]\). In month \(t+1\), the realized returns are \([+2.0\%, +1.0\%, -3.0\%, -2.0\%, -4.0\%]\). Calculate the net market exposure \(\bar{w}_t\) and total portfolio return.

Solution

The net market directional exposure is the average weight:

\[ \bar{w}_t = \frac{0.8 + 0.6 - 0.4 - 0.6 - 0.8}{5} = \frac{-0.4}{5} = -0.08 \]

The total portfolio return is the dot product divided by \(N = 5\):

\[ r_{t+1} = \frac{1}{5} [0.8(0.02) + 0.6(0.01) + (-0.4)(-0.03) + (-0.6)(-0.02) + (-0.8)(-0.04)] \] \[ r_{t+1} = \frac{1}{5} [0.016 + 0.006 + 0.012 + 0.012 + 0.032] = \frac{0.078}{5} = 0.0156 \quad (1.56\%) \]

The portfolio earns \(+1.56\%\) across the period.

Proof

Prove that if all assets have identical trailing returns and volatilities, cross-sectional momentum (CSMOM) delivers exactly zero return, while time-series momentum (TSMOM) earns non-zero return whenever realized returns are non-zero.

Solution

Let past returns satisfy \(r_{t-k:t}^i = c\) for all \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). In CSMOM, ranking all assets yields identical ranks, so demeaned weights are identically zero: \(w_{t,i}^{\text{CSMOM}} = (c - \bar{c}) / \sum |c - \bar{c}| = 0 / 0 \to 0\), yielding portfolio return \(r_{t+1}^{\text{CSMOM}} = 0\). In TSMOM, with identical volatilities \(\hat{\sigma}_t^i = \sigma\), the position weight for each asset is \(w_t^i = \text{sign}(c) \cdot (\sigma_{\text{target}} / \sigma)\). The TSMOM portfolio return is \(r_{t+1}^{\text{TSMOM}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \text{sign}(c) \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\sigma} r_{t+1}^i = \text{sign}(c) \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\sigma} \bar{r}_{t+1}\). Whenever \(\bar{r}_{t+1} \neq 0\), the TSMOM return is strictly non-zero, proving that TSMOM captures broad market trends that dollar-neutral CSMOM cannot exploit.

Prove that when an asset follows an AR(1) process \(r_t = \mu + \phi (r_{t-1} - \mu) + \epsilon_t\) with \(\phi \in (0, 1)\), the expected sign of the trailing 1-period return \(\E[\text{sign}(r_t - \mu) \cdot (r_{t+1} - \mu)]\) is strictly positive.

Solution

Define demeaned return \(x_t \equiv r_t - \mu\). The AR(1) process is \(x_{t+1} = \phi x_t + \epsilon_{t+1}\), where \(\epsilon_{t+1}\) is independent white noise with mean zero. Taking conditional expectation given \(x_t\): \(\E[x_{t+1} \mid x_t] = \phi x_t\). Now evaluate the expected product: \(\E[\text{sign}(x_t) x_{t+1}] = \E\left[\E[\text{sign}(x_t) x_{t+1} \mid x_t]\right] = \E[\text{sign}(x_t) \phi x_t] = \phi \E[\text{sign}(x_t) x_t]\). Recalling that \(\text{sign}(x) \cdot x = |x|\), we have \(\E[\text{sign}(x_t) x_{t+1}] = \phi \E[|x_t|]\). Since \(\phi > 0\) and \(x_t\) has non-degenerate distribution with \(\E[|x_t|] > 0\), the expected product is strictly positive: \(\phi \E[|x_t|] > 0\). This proves that positive serial autocorrelation guarantees positive expected TSMOM profits.

Prove that scaling portfolio positions by inverse volatility \(\sigma_{\text{target}} / \hat{\sigma}_t\) eliminates volatility clustering in individual position dollar risk contributions under a GARCH(1,1) return specification.

Solution

Let an asset return be \(r_{t+1} = \sigma_{t+1} z_{t+1}\) with \(z_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and conditional variance \(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + \alpha r_t^2 + \beta \sigma_t^2\). Under ex-ante volatility scaling, the position return is \(y_{t+1} = w_t r_{t+1}\), where \(w_t = \sigma_{\text{target}} / \hat{\sigma}_{t+1}\). If the conditional volatility forecast is exact (\(\hat{\sigma}_{t+1} = \sigma_{t+1}\)), the scaled return is \(y_{t+1} = \left(\frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\sigma_{t+1}}\right) (\sigma_{t+1} z_{t+1}) = \sigma_{\text{target}} z_{t+1}\). The conditional variance of the position return is \(\Var_t(y_{t+1}) = \Var_t(\sigma_{\text{target}} z_{t+1}) = \sigma_{\text{target}}^2 \Var(z_{t+1}) = \sigma_{\text{target}}^2\), which is constant across all time periods, proving that exact volatility scaling completely purges volatility clustering from the trading position.

Empirical

In an empirical study of 58 futures contracts from 1985 to 2010, Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) test formation lookback windows \(k \in \{1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, 36, 48\}\) months. At what horizon does return predictability peak, and what happens to predictability at 36 to 48 months?

Solution

Predictability peaks at intermediate horizons between 9 and 12 months, where \(t\)-statistics for past sign predictability exceed \(10\) in pooled regressions. At horizons beyond 24 months (36 to 48 months), the regression coefficient reverses sign and becomes negative, reflecting long-horizon macroeconomic mean reversion (the value effect). This confirms that momentum is an intermediate-horizon phenomenon (1 to 12 months), after which prices mean-revert toward fundamental replacement costs.

During the 2008 global financial crisis (January to December 2008), the S&P 500 fell \(-37.0\%\). Why did diversified CTA funds and TSMOM strategies deliver positive returns (+15% to +25%) over the same period?

Solution

TSMOM delivered positive returns in 2008 due to two primary macro channels: (1) As equity markets trended downward through 2007–2008, TSMOM flipped into systematic short positions across global equity indices (S&P 500, DAX, Nikkei) and cyclical commodities (crude oil, industrial metals), profiting from the collapse; (2) In flight-to-safety assets, central bank rate cuts created massive upward trends in sovereign bond futures (US Treasuries, German Bunds), where TSMOM held leveraged long positions. The combination of short equity/commodity exposure and long sovereign bond exposure created massive positive returns.

Analyze the correlation between a diversified TSMOM portfolio and a diversified cross-asset value portfolio. Why does their combination yield a Sharpe ratio greater than the sum of individual components?

Solution

TSMOM and value display a strong negative correlation (typically \(\rho \approx -0.40\) to \(-0.55\)). When an asset experiences a multi-year downturn, value classifies it as cheap (long signal), while TSMOM identifies a persistent downward trend (short signal). By Theorem 1.1, combining two factors with negative correlation dramatically reduces portfolio variance (\(\sigma_{\text{combo}} = \sigma \sqrt{(1+\rho)/2} \approx 0.50 \sigma\) when \(\rho = -0.50\)), effectively doubling the combined Sharpe ratio while smoothing out drawdown cycles.

Research

How does market segmentation between institutional CTA trend followers and fundamental discretionary investors explain the persistence of 12-month time-series momentum?

Solution

Behavioral and institutional frictions combine to generate 12-month persistence: (1) Under-reaction: fundamental investors under-react to complex macroeconomic news (e.g. monetary policy cycles, supply shocks), causing prices to drift gradually toward equilibrium; (2) Disposition effect: investors sell winners prematurely and hold losers, creating price resistance that slows price discovery; (3) Capital constraints: arbitrage capital is slow-moving, allowing trends to persist until CTAs and momentum capital push prices into over-extension, triggering long-horizon mean reversion.

Contrast the crash vulnerability of pure equity cross-sectional momentum (Daniel & Moskowitz 2016) with multi-asset time-series momentum. Why is TSMOM more resilient to momentum crashes?

Solution

Equity CSMOM crashes occur when shorted stocks (distressed, high-beta equities) surge during sudden post-crisis market rebounds, creating an unhedged short call option profile. In contrast, multi-asset TSMOM is protected by cross-asset breadth: during a recovery, while short equity positions may lose money, long positions in recovering commodities or currencies and diversified bond positions offset equity losses. Furthermore, TSMOM de-leverages rapidly via inverse-volatility scaling as crisis volatility spikes, reducing gross exposure before the rebound strikes.

Design an adaptive volatility-scaling mechanism that replaces trailing standard deviation with real-time implied volatility (VIX / options-implied vol) to mitigate lag in position resizing.

Solution

Trailing standard deviations suffer from lookback inertia: when a crisis strikes, realized volatility takes days or weeks to rise, leaving positions over-leveraged during the initial gap shock. An adaptive mechanism sizes positions using forward-looking options-implied volatility: \(w_t^i = \text{sign}(r_{t-k:t}^i) \cdot \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\max(\hat{\sigma}_{\text{realized}}^i, \sigma_{\text{implied}}^i)}\). When implied volatility spikes instantly (e.g. VIX leaping from 15 to 40), the denominator surges immediately, cutting gross portfolio leverage within hours and preventing gap-risk losses before trailing historical volatility reacts.

Comprehension Check

  1. What is the defining distinction between Time-Series Momentum (TSMOM) and Cross-Sectional Momentum (CSMOM)?

    Options

    TSMOM is an absolute trend-following strategy that takes long or short positions based on each asset's own past return sign, allowing net portfolio market exposure to vary.

  2. An equity index futures contract has a positive past 12-month return and a trailing annualized volatility of \(20.0\%\). If the target volatility per instrument is \(10.0\%\), what is the position weight \(w_t^i\)?

    Position weight is \(w_t^i = \text{sign}(+) \cdot (\sigma_{\text{target}} / \hat{\sigma}_t) = 1.0 \times (0.10 / 0.20) = 0.50\).

  3. Why is inverse-volatility scaling (\(\sigma_{\text{target}} / \hat{\sigma}_t^i\)) crucial in a multi-asset TSMOM portfolio?

    Options

    Without volatility scaling, volatile contracts like commodities would dwarf bond signals, destroying cross-asset risk balance.

  4. What term describes the positive returns generated by TSMOM during severe multi-month equity market drawdowns?

    Options

    Crisis alpha refers to the substantial positive returns delivered by trend-following strategies during prolonged macro market crises by going short equities and long flight-to-safety bonds.

  5. According to Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012), at what formation lookback horizon does return predictability peak?

    Options

    TSMOM predictability peaks at the 12-month lookback horizon before mean reversion sets in at 24 to 48 months.

  6. If all 10 assets in a multi-asset universe have negative past 12-month returns, and each is allocated unit normalized weight (\(w_t^i = -1\)), what is the aggregate directional exposure \(\bar{w}_t\)?

    Average exposure is \(\bar{w}_t = \frac{1}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10} (-1) = -1.00\).

  7. What market regime causes the most severe drawdowns and whipsaw losses for time-series momentum?

    Options

    In range-bound markets, trend strategies repeatedly buy at resistance and sell at support, suffering continuous whipsaw losses.

  8. Why does combining TSMOM with a cross-asset Value factor produce a superior combined Sharpe ratio?

    Options

    Negative correlation between 12-month trend continuation and 3-to-5 year valuation mean reversion generates powerful Sharpe ratio expansion.
