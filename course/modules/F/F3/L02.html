Carry Across Asset Classes

Carry is frequently caricatured as a currency trade—borrowing low-yielding yen to purchase high-yielding Australian dollars. Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen & Vrugt (2018) revolutionized empirical asset pricing by unifying carry across currencies, government bonds, commodities, and equities under a single mathematical principle: carry is the expected return assuming market prices and conditions remain unchanged. Across all asset classes, carry earns robust risk premia that cannot be explained by standard factor models, but universally exhibits negative skewness and severe crash risk during global liquidity crunches.

1. The Unified Theory of Carry

In historical market lore, fixed income investors focused on bond yield roll-down, commodity traders analyzed backwardation and convenience yields, and foreign exchange desks traded the forward premium trade. Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen & Vrugt (2018) demonstrated that these seemingly disparate market mechanisms are manifestations of a single, universal pricing principle: general carry.

Definition 2.1 · General Carry and the Carry Factor

For any financial asset with price \(P_t\) at time \(t\) and cash flow or dividend payout \(D_{t+1}\) at time \(t+1\), the carry \(C_t\) is defined as the expected return on the asset assuming that market prices and market conditions remain unchanged over the holding interval:

\[ C_t \equiv \frac{\E_t^{\text{static}}[P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}] - P_t}{P_t} \]

where \(\E_t^{\text{static}}[\cdot]\) denotes the expectation operator under the static assumption that the term structure, spot prices, and volatility surface at \(t+1\) are identical to those observed at time \(t\).

The carry factor is the systematic cross-asset risk factor constructed by ranking assets within or across asset classes by their static carry \(C_t\), taking dollar-neutral long positions in high-carry assets and short positions in low-carry assets, scaled by inverse volatility to equalize risk contributions.

The actual realized excess return of an asset can be decomposed identically into its carry component and the return resulting from dynamic price changes:

\[ R_{t+1} \equiv \frac{P_{t+1} + D_{t+1} - P_t}{P_t} = C_t + \frac{P_{t+1} - \E_t^{\text{static}}[P_{t+1}]}{P_t} \]

Taking conditional expectations gives:

\[ \E_t[R_{t+1}] = C_t + \E_t\left[\frac{\Delta P_{t+1}^{\text{dynamic}}}{P_t}\right] \]

If markets were semi-strong form efficient under risk neutrality and rational expectations, the expected dynamic price change would exactly offset carry (\(\E_t[\Delta P_{t+1}^{\text{dynamic}} / P_t] = -C_t\)), causing expected excess returns to equal zero across all assets. Empirically, Koijen et al. (2018) find that expected dynamic price changes do not offset carry; in fact, high-carry assets deliver large, persistent positive excess returns.

2. Asset-Specific Formulations of Carry

The operational formulation of \(C_t\) depends directly on the contractual mechanics of each asset class:

Foreign Exchange (FX)

In currency forward markets, by Covered Interest Parity (CIP), the forward exchange rate \(F_{t,T}\) (expressed as domestic currency per unit of foreign currency) satisfies \(F_{t,T} / S_t = (1 + r_t) / (1 + r_t^*)\), where \(S_t\) is the spot exchange rate, \(r_t\) is the domestic risk-free rate, and \(r_t^*\) is the foreign risk-free rate. Under the static assumption that the spot exchange rate remains unchanged (\(\E_t^{\text{static}}[S_{t+1}] = S_t\)), carry on a long foreign currency forward contract is:

\[ C_t^{\text{FX}} = \frac{S_t - F_{t,T}}{F_{t,T}} \approx r_t^* - r_t \]

Under Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP), high-interest-rate currencies should depreciate by exactly \(r_t^* - r_t\), setting expected currency excess returns to zero. The empirical failure of UIP—the famous forward premium puzzle (Fama 1984)—is the FX manifestation of the universal carry premium.

Fixed Income (Government Bonds)

For a zero-coupon bond with maturity \(n\) years and yield to maturity \(y_t^{(n)}\), the price is \(P_t^{(n)} = \exp(-n y_t^{(n)})\). Over holding period \(\Delta t\), if the yield curve remains static (\(y_{t+\Delta t}^{(n - \Delta t)} = y_t^{(n - \Delta t)}\)), the bond rolls down the yield curve. Carry consists of the initial yield plus the roll-down return minus the short-term financing rate \(r_t\):

\[ C_t^{\text{Bond}} = y_t^{(n)} + n \left(y_t^{(n)} - y_t^{(n - \Delta t)}\right) - r_t \]

On an upward-sloping yield curve, longer-duration bonds enjoy positive roll-down gains as their remaining maturity shortens toward lower-yielding points on the curve.

Commodities

For commodity futures with expiration \(T\), carry is the roll yield generated as the futures price converges to the spot price. Under a static spot price \(\E_t^{\text{static}}[S_T] = S_t\), carry is:

\[ C_t^{\text{Comm}} = \frac{S_t - F_{t,T}}{F_{t,T}} \]

When the commodity market is in backwardation (\(F_{t,T} < S_t\)), the futures curve is downward-sloping, generating positive carry as the long futures contract rolls up the curve to converge with spot. Conversely, in contango (\(F_{t,T} > S_t\)), carry is negative.

Equities

For an equity index with dividend yield \(D_t / P_t\) and continuous borrowing rate \(r_t\), assuming static prices and earnings yields, carry is simply the dividend yield net of financing costs:

\[ C_t^{\text{Equity}} = \frac{D_t}{P_t} - r_t \]

3. Crash Risk, Skewness, and Downside Asymmetry

Why do carry strategies across global markets offer Sharpe ratios exceeding \(0.60\)? Neoclassical asset pricing dictates that excess returns must represent compensation for systemic risk. The universal vulnerability of carry is negative return skewness: carry strategies harvest steady, low-volatility gains during calm market expansions, but suffer sudden, catastrophic drawdowns during global liquidity squeezes.

Theorem 2.1 · Carry Skewness and Liquidity Spirals

Let an asset return follow a jump-diffusion process with drift \(\mu_c\), diffusion volatility \(\sigma_c\), and rare Poisson negative jumps of intensity \(\lambda\) and jump size distribution \(J \sim \mathcal{N}(-\theta_J, \delta_J^2)\) with \(\theta_J > 0\). The third standardized moment (skewness) satisfies:

\[ \text{Skew}(R) = \frac{\lambda \left(-3 \theta_J \delta_J^2 - \theta_J^3\right)}{\left(\sigma_c^2 + \lambda(\theta_J^2 + \delta_J^2)\right)^{3/2}} < 0 \]

Whenever high-carry assets are financed by leveraged financial intermediaries subject to margin constraints, an initial funding shock triggers margin calls, forcing liquidations of high-carry assets and exacerbating price drops in a self-reinforcing liquidity spiral (Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen 2008).

Proof of Theorem 2.1

The total return over interval \(\Delta t\) is \(R = \mu_c \Delta t + \sigma_c \sqrt{\Delta t} Z + \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\Delta t}} J_k\), where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and \(N_{\Delta t} \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda \Delta t)\). By the law of total cumulance, the variance of the compound Poisson process is \(\Var(R) = \sigma_c^2 \Delta t + \lambda \Delta t \E[J^2] = \left[\sigma_c^2 + \lambda (\theta_J^2 + \delta_J^2)\right] \Delta t\). The third central moment is \(\kappa_3(R) = \lambda \Delta t \E[J^3]\). Since \(J \sim \mathcal{N}(-\theta_J, \delta_J^2)\), we compute \(\E[J^3] = (-\theta_J)^3 + 3(-\theta_J)\delta_J^2 = -(\theta_J^3 + 3 \theta_J \delta_J^2)\). Dividing \(\kappa_3\) by \([\Var(R)]^{3/2}\) eliminates \(\Delta t\) and produces the negative skewness expression. Since \(\lambda > 0\) and \(\theta_J > 0\), the numerator is strictly negative, proving that \(\text{Skew}(R) < 0\).

Example 2.1 · FX Carry Drawdown Under Liquidity Crunch

Consider an FX carry strategy earning a static interest rate spread of \(C_t = 6.0\%\) per annum with diffusion volatility \(\sigma = 8.0\%\). Over 5 consecutive calm years, the strategy accumulates an annualized return of \(6.0\%\) with steady monthly gains. In year 6, a global liquidity shock strikes (\(\lambda = 1\)): high-yield currencies collapse by \(24\%\) in two weeks as leveraged carry trades are liquidated. The single crash wipes out 4 years of accumulated carry income, demonstrating why unhedged carry produces pronounced negative skewness (\(\text{Skew} \approx -1.2\)).

Remark 2.1 · Peso Problems vs. Disaster Risk

Burnside, Eichenbaum, Kleshchelski & Rebelo (2010) examine whether carry returns can be explained by rare disaster "peso problems." While disaster risk models explain a portion of the spread, hedged carry strategies that purchase out-of-the-money put options to eliminate crash risk still earn statistically significant positive alphas, confirming that crash risk alone does not fully exhaust the carry premium.

4. Cross-Asset Diversification and Risk-Parity Allocation

While carry strategies within a single asset class (e.g. FX alone) suffer from pronounced crash skewness, Koijen et al. (2018) demonstrate that combining carry across disparate asset classes—currencies, fixed income, and commodities—significantly mitigates crash risk. Because liquidity panics and supply-demand imbalances rarely hit commodity curves, sovereign bond curves, and currency pairs at identical moments, a cross-asset carry factor delivers a Sharpe ratio of \(0.89\), compared to standalone Sharpe ratios of \(\sim 0.50\).

To implement cross-asset carry, factor returns are formed via dollar-neutral demeaned weights:

\[ w_{t,i} = \frac{C_{t,i} - \bar{C}_t}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_t} |C_{t,j} - \bar{C}_t|} \]

where \(\bar{C}_t = \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_j C_{t,j}\). This specification guarantees zero net capital commitment (\(\sum w_{t,i} = 0\)) and unit gross exposure (\(\sum |w_{t,i}| = 1\)).

Lab 1 · Cross-Asset Carry Factor & Crash Risk

Simulating carry factor returns across Currencies, Fixed Income, and Commodities will reveal consistently positive Sharpe ratios accompanied by negative skewness.

"""Lab F3-L02-1: Cross-Asset Carry Factor Construction & Crash Skewness.

Constructs cross-asset carry factors across Currencies, Fixed Income, and
Commodities following Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen & Vrugt (2018). Computes
annualized excess returns, Sharpe ratios, and higher-order moments, verifying
that carry premia are accompanied by pronounced negative return skewness.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
import scipy.stats


def simulate_cross_asset_carry(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, dict[str, float]]:
    """Simulate carry factor returns and crash distributions across asset classes."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_months = 360  # 30 years
    asset_classes = ["Currencies_FX", "Fixed_Income", "Commodities"]

    results: dict[str, dict[str, float]] = {}

    for ac in asset_classes:
        # Carry returns: positive drift, negative jumps / crash risk
        # Normal diffusion + rare Poisson negative jumps
        drift = 0.055 / 12.0  # 5.5% annual drift
        diffusion_vol = 0.085 / np.sqrt(12.0)  # 8.5% annual diffusion

        # Jump parameters: lambda = 0.35 jumps/year (prob ~0.03/month), jump mean -8%, jump std 3%
        jump_intensity = 0.35 / 12.0
        n_jumps = rng.poisson(jump_intensity, size=n_months)
        jump_sizes = np.zeros(n_months)
        for i in range(n_months):
            if n_jumps[i] > 0:
                jump_sizes[i] = np.sum(rng.normal(-0.08, 0.03, size=n_jumps[i]))

        monthly_returns = rng.normal(drift, diffusion_vol, size=n_months) + jump_sizes

        ann_mean = float(np.mean(monthly_returns) * 12.0 * 100.0)
        ann_vol = float(np.std(monthly_returns, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0) * 100.0)
        sharpe = float(ann_mean / ann_vol)
        skew = float(scipy.stats.skew(monthly_returns, bias=False))
        kurt = float(scipy.stats.kurtosis(monthly_returns, bias=False))
        worst_mth = float(np.min(monthly_returns) * 100.0)

        results[ac] = {
            "mean": round(ann_mean, 2),
            "vol": round(ann_vol, 2),
            "sharpe": round(sharpe, 2),
            "skewness": round(skew, 2),
            "kurtosis": round(kurt, 2),
            "worst_month": round(worst_mth, 2),
        }

    return results


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F3-L02-1 | Cross-Asset Carry Factor & Crash Risk (KMPV 2018)")
    print("----------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = simulate_cross_asset_carry(seed=42)
    print(f"{'Asset Class':<15} | {'Mean %':<8} | {'Vol %':<8} | {'Sharpe':<8} | {'Skewness':<9} | {'Worst Mth %':<11}")
    print("-" * 70)
    for ac, st in res.items():
        m, v, s = st["mean"], st["vol"], st["sharpe"]
        sk, wm = st["skewness"], st["worst_month"]
        print(f"{ac:<15} | {m:<8.2f} | {v:<8.2f} | {s:<8.2f} | {sk:<9.2f} | {wm:<11.2f}")
    print("-" * 70)
    print("Every carry factor exhibits positive Sharpe ratios paired with negative skewness.")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical carry canon encounters severe real-world frictions:

  • Cross-Currency Basis and CIP Breakdown: Following the 2008 global financial crisis, Covered Interest Parity broke down systematically. The cross-currency basis (e.g. EUR/USD and JPY/USD basis swaps) widened dramatically due to bank balance sheet constraints, regulatory leverage caps, and asymmetrical dollar funding demand, disrupting traditional FX carry arbitrage.
  • Zero and Negative Lower Bound Distortions: Post-2010 European and Japanese central bank policy rates pushed sovereign yields into negative territory. In negative rate regimes, the static roll-down mechanism in government bonds became distorted as yield curves flattened or inverted.
  • Unwind Crowding Spirals: When large quantitative funds and macro hedge funds hold overlapping carry positions, a volatility spike forces mechanical de-leveraging. The resulting simultaneous fire sales trigger market illiquidity and sharp gap-risk, breaching standard Value-at-Risk limits.

Misconceptions

  1. Carry is exclusively a foreign exchange currency strategy that has no counterpart in fixed income or commodities.

    Evidence: Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen & Vrugt (2018) prove that carry is a general property of all financial assets with term structures, showing that bond roll-down, commodity basis, and equity dividend yields are economically identical to FX interest rate differentials.

  2. Carry delivers a 'free lunch' with positive returns and zero systematic risk because forward prices mechanically converge to spot prices.

    Evidence: Carry strategies universally exhibit severe negative skewness; during global liquidity contractions, high-carry assets suffer violent drawdowns driven by margin call liquidations.

  3. Rare disaster risk and peso problems completely explain the carry premium, leaving zero risk-adjusted alpha for active investors.

    Evidence: Burnside et al. (2010) show that hedged carry strategies that eliminate disaster risk using deep out-of-the-money put options still generate statistically significant positive Sharpe ratios.

Problem Set

Mechanics

In the foreign exchange market, the 1-year spot exchange rate is \(S_0 = 1.3000\) USD per GBP. The 1-year forward rate is \(F_{0,1} = 1.2740\) USD per GBP. Calculate the 1-year static carry \(C_0^{\text{FX}}\) for an investor holding a long position in GBP financed in USD.

Solution

Under the static expectation that the spot rate remains unchanged at \(S_1 = S_0 = 1.3000\), the carry return from holding the forward contract to settlement is:

\[ C_0^{\text{FX}} = \frac{S_0 - F_{0,1}}{F_{0,1}} = \frac{1.3000 - 1.2740}{1.2740} = \frac{0.0260}{1.2740} \approx 0.020408 \quad (2.04\%) \]

Alternatively, by Covered Interest Parity, \(F/S = (1+r_{\text{USD}})/(1+r_{\text{GBP}}) = 1.2740 / 1.3000 = 0.9800\), indicating that the British pound carries an interest rate premium of approximately \(2.04\%\) over the US dollar.

A 10-year Treasury bond currently yields \(y^{(10)} = 4.50\%\). The 9-year Treasury bond yields \(y^{(9)} = 4.20\%\). The 1-year risk-free financing rate is \(r = 3.50\%\). Calculate the 1-year static bond carry assuming the yield curve remains constant.

Solution

The static bond carry consists of the coupon yield plus the roll-down capital gain minus the financing rate. With maturity \(n = 10\) and holding period \(\Delta t = 1\):

\[ C^{\text{Bond}} = y^{(10)} + n \left(y^{(10)} - y^{(9)}\right) - r \] \[ C^{\text{Bond}} = 0.0450 + 10 \times (0.0450 - 0.0420) - 0.0350 = 0.0450 + 10 \times (0.0030) - 0.0350 = 0.0450 + 0.0300 - 0.0350 = 0.0400 \quad (4.00\%) \]

The 1-year static bond carry is \(4.00\%\), comprising \(1.00\%\) net yield spread and \(3.00\%\) roll-down capital appreciation.

Crude oil spot price is \(S_0 = \$80.00\) per barrel. The 6-month futures contract trades at \(F_{0,0.5} = \$76.00\) per barrel. State whether the curve is in backwardation or contango, and compute the annualized roll yield carry.

Solution

Because \(F_{0,0.5} = \$76.00 < S_0 = \$80.00\), the futures curve is downward-sloping, which defines backwardation. The 6-month carry is:

\[ C_{6\text{m}} = \frac{S_0 - F_{0,0.5}}{F_{0,0.5}} = \frac{80 - 76}{76} = \frac{4}{76} \approx 0.052632 \quad (5.26\%) \]

Annualizing by multiplying by 2 (or compounding \((1+0.052632)^2 - 1 = 10.80\%\)) gives an annualized simple roll yield carry of \(10.53\%\).

Proof

Prove that if Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) holds strictly, the expected excess return on an FX carry trade is identically zero.

Solution

Let \(S_t\) be the spot exchange rate (domestic currency per foreign currency) and \(F_{t,t+1}\) be the 1-period forward exchange rate. By Covered Interest Parity (CIP), \(F_{t,t+1} = S_t \frac{1 + r_t}{1 + r_t^*}\). The excess return on a long foreign currency forward is \(R_{t+1}^{\text{FX}} = \frac{S_{t+1} - F_{t,t+1}}{S_t}\). Uncovered Interest Parity states that the expected change in spot exchange rates equals the interest rate differential: \(\E_t[S_{t+1}] = S_t \frac{1+r_t}{1+r_t^*}\). Substituting CIP into UIP gives \(\E_t[S_{t+1}] = F_{t,t+1}\). Taking conditional expectations of excess return yields \(\E_t[R_{t+1}^{\text{FX}}] = \frac{\E_t[S_{t+1}] - F_{t,t+1}}{S_t} = \frac{F_{t,t+1} - F_{t,t+1}}{S_t} = 0\). Thus, strict UIP implies that expected excess returns are zero and the carry trade offers no systematic compensation.

Prove that the dollar-neutral demeaning weighting scheme \(w_{t,i} = (C_{t,i} - \bar{C}_t) / \sum_j |C_{t,j} - \bar{C}_t|\) satisfies both zero net dollar exposure and unit gross leverage.

Solution

Let \(x_i \equiv C_{t,i} - \bar{C}_t\). By definition of the cross-sectional mean \(\bar{C}_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N C_{t,j}\), the sum of demeaned signals is identically zero: \(\sum_{i=1}^N x_i = \sum_{i=1}^N C_{t,i} - N \left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N C_{t,j}\right) = 0\). Therefore, the sum of portfolio weights is:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^N w_{t,i} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N x_i}{\sum_{j=1}^N |x_j|} = \frac{0}{\sum_{j=1}^N |x_j|} = 0 \]

proving dollar neutrality. Next, evaluating gross leverage:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^N |w_{t,i}| = \sum_{i=1}^N \left|\frac{x_i}{\sum_{j=1}^N |x_j|}\right| = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N |x_i|}{\sum_{j=1}^N |x_j|} = 1 \]

which establishes that total gross exposure is normalized to exactly unity.

Prove that for an upward-sloping yield curve where forward rates exceed spot rates (\(f_t(n, n+\Delta t) > y_t(n)\)), static bond carry strictly exceeds the contemporaneous yield to maturity.

Solution

The static roll-down return is defined as \(\lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{P_{t+\Delta t}^{(n - \Delta t)} - P_t^{(n)}}{P_t^{(n)} \Delta t}\). Recalling \(P_t^{(n)} = \exp(-n y_t(n))\), the instantaneous holding period return under static yields is \(y_t(n) + n y_t'(n)\), where \(y_t'(n) = \frac{\partial y_t(n)}{\partial n}\). By definition of the instantaneous forward rate, \(f_t(n) = y_t(n) + n y_t'(n)\). Thus, static bond carry is \(C_t = f_t(n) - r_t\). If the yield curve is upward-sloping, \(y_t'(n) > 0\), which implies \(f_t(n) > y_t(n)\). Therefore, \(C_t + r_t = f_t(n) > y_t(n)\), proving that total static return strictly exceeds the yield to maturity.

Empirical

In an empirical sample of 20 currency pairs over 360 months, the top carry quintile earns an annualized excess return of \(7.2\%\) with annual volatility \(10.0\%\), while the bottom carry quintile earns \(-1.8\%\) with annual volatility \(10.0\%\). The sample skewness of the top quintile is \(-0.95\), while the bottom quintile has skewness \(+0.42\). Explain the economic source of this skewness divergence.

Solution

The divergence in skewness reflects asymmetric liquidity risk and funding spirals (Brunnermeier et al. 2008). High-carry currencies are held by leveraged investors. During liquidity shocks, margin calls force rapid unwinding of long high-carry positions, causing severe, rapid price drops (negative skewness \(-0.95\)). Conversely, funding currencies (low-carry) are shorted; when carry trades unwind, investors rush to buy back funding currencies, causing sudden upward price spikes for funding currencies (positive skewness \(+0.42\)).

Evaluate an empirical regression of monthly currency excess returns on carry: \(R_{t+1}^i = \alpha + \beta C_t^i + \epsilon_{t+1}^i\). If UIP held, what would be the expected values of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\)? What does the empirical literature (Koijen et al. 2018) find for \(\beta\)?

Solution

Under UIP, \(\E_t[R_{t+1}^i] = 0\), so \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\beta = 0\). In the Fama (1984) formulation using spot changes \(\Delta s_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta_F (f_t - s_t) + \epsilon\), UIP requires \(\beta_F = 1\). In the excess return formulation \(R_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta C_t\), Koijen et al. (2018) find \(\beta > 0\) (typically around \(0.8\) to \(1.2\)) with high statistical significance, confirming that carry predicts future returns rather than being wiped out by spot depreciation.

A quantitative macro fund constructs a 3-asset carry portfolio spanning FX, Sovereign Bonds, and Commodities. Standalone Sharpe ratios are \(0.52\) (FX), \(0.61\) (Bonds), and \(0.48\) (Commodities). Pairwise correlations are \(\rho_{\text{FX, Bonds}} = 0.12\), \(\rho_{\text{FX, Comm}} = 0.18\), and \(\rho_{\text{Bonds, Comm}} = -0.05\). Explain why the multi-asset carry composite achieves a Sharpe ratio near \(0.90\).

Solution

Because the pairwise correlations between carry strategies across distinct asset classes are close to zero (averaging \(\sim 0.08\)), the cross-asset portfolio diversifies away asset-specific idiosyncratic risks. Under equal-risk weighting with average standalone Sharpe \(\overline{\SR} \approx 0.54\) and average correlation \(\bar{\rho} \approx 0.083\), the composite Sharpe ratio scales as \(\SR_{\text{port}} \approx \overline{\SR} \sqrt{\frac{K}{1 + (K-1)\bar{\rho}}} = 0.54 \sqrt{\frac{3}{1 + 2(0.083)}} = 0.54 \sqrt{\frac{3}{1.166}} = 0.54 \times 1.604 = 0.866\), nearly matching the observed \(0.89\) in Koijen et al. (2018).

Research

How does the post-2008 emergence of a persistent non-zero cross-currency basis challenge neoclassical asset pricing models of frictionless carry?

Solution

In frictionless markets, Covered Interest Parity (CIP) is an exact no-arbitrage condition enforced by simultaneous spot and forward transactions. The post-2008 persistence of a non-zero cross-currency basis (where synthetic dollar borrowing costs via FX swaps diverge from direct cash market yields) proves that arbitrage is constrained by intermediary balance sheet capacity. Stricter post-crisis banking regulations (Basel III leverage ratios, liquidity coverage requirements) impose a shadow cost on bank balance sheets, preventing arbitrageurs from fully exploiting carry spreads and causing carry risk premia to reflect institutional funding constraints.

Propose an empirical test to distinguish whether carry crash risk is driven by sudden macroeconomic fundamental shocks or by endogenous intermediary leverage liquidations.

Solution

To distinguish between fundamental shocks and endogenous deleveraging, one can regress high-frequency carry returns on: (1) macro news surprise indexes (e.g. non-farm payrolls, GDP revisions), and (2) intermediary funding constraint proxies, such as changes in the TED spread, broker-dealer equity leverage, and the VIX (following Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009). If crashes are driven by endogenous spirals, carry drawdowns will correlate strongly with funding liquidity contractions even on days with zero macroeconomic news surprises, and will exhibit volume spikes followed by persistent post-crash price rebounds as liquidity normalizes.

Why might a systematic value factor and a carry factor be positively correlated in commodities and bonds, but display distinct dynamic drawdown properties?

Solution

In commodities and fixed income, an asset with depressed prices often exhibits high current yield or backwardation (e.g. high bond yield or tight current physical inventory), producing positive cross-sectional correlation between value (5-year price reversal) and carry (current roll yield). However, their dynamic properties diverge: carry is a short-horizon static yield harvesting strategy that suffers immediate negative skewness when credit or funding spreads widen, whereas value is a long-horizon mean-reversion anchor that underperforms gradually during extended structural shocks but avoids sudden margin-call-induced gap risk.

    Options

    Carry is defined as the expected return assuming static market conditions: \(C_t = (\E_t^{\text{static}}[P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}] - P_t) / P_t\).

  2. A foreign currency forward trades at a discount of \(3.0\%\) relative to spot (\((S_0 - F_{0,1})/F_{0,1} = 0.03\)). Assuming the spot exchange rate remains unchanged, what is the annualized carry return in percent?

    The static carry return is \((S_0 - F_{0,1})/F_{0,1} = 3.0\%\).

  3. In commodity futures markets, what term structure shape produces positive carry for a long futures position?

    Options

    In backwardation, futures trade below spot, generating a positive roll yield as the futures price converges upward toward spot at expiration.

  4. What higher-order moment universally characterizes carry factor returns across diverse asset classes?

    Options

    Carry strategies harvest steady, low-volatility gains during expansions but suffer severe negative return skewness during market liquidity spirals.

  5. According to Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen (2008), what mechanism amplifies carry trade crashes?

    Options

    When leveraged carry traders suffer adverse shocks, margin constraints force sales, producing a self-fulfilling downward spiral.

  6. Under the strict validity of Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP), what is the theoretical expected excess return of an unhedged FX carry position?

    UIP dictates that exchange rate movements exactly offset interest rate differentials, yielding \(0.00\%\) expected excess return.

  7. What empirical finding did Burnside et al. (2010) report regarding crash-hedged carry trades?

    Options

    Burnside et al. (2010) show that hedged carry trades remain profitable, proving peso/disaster risk does not fully explain carry premia.

  8. Why does combining carry across FX, government bonds, and commodities expand the overall Sharpe ratio?

    Options

    Low cross-asset correlation allows diversification of localized crash events, elevating the multi-asset carry Sharpe ratio to ~0.89.
