Long-History Evidence on Trend Following

A standard skepticism in quantitative finance is that 20-to-30-year backtests are artifacts of sample selection, surviving only during specific post-1980 macroeconomic regimes. Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (2017) challenged this skepticism by reconstructing a 137-year history of trend following from 1880 to 2016 across 67 global markets. They proved that trend following delivered positive risk-adjusted returns across virtually every decade, including the Great Depression, the 1970s stagflation, and the 2008 crash. Dissecting this long history and Fung & Hsieh's (2001) lookback straddle smile reveals the non-linear convexity at the heart of crisis alpha.

1. A Century of Trend Following

Does systematic trend following persist across structural shifts in global economic architecture? The modern era of managed futures and Commodity Trading Advisors (CTAs) is often perceived as an innovation of the 1980s computer age. Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (2017) dismantled this regime-bias critique by extending the empirical evaluation of time-series momentum back to 1880, reconstructing monthly returns for 67 markets across 29 commodity futures, 12 currency forwards, 15 sovereign bond markets, and 11 equity indices over a 137-year horizon.

Definition 4.1 · Long-History Trend Following

Trend-following long history refers to the empirical literature establishing that systematic time-series momentum strategies—combining multi-horizon lookback windows, trailing volatility normalization, and diversified cross-asset execution—deliver robust positive risk-adjusted returns and crisis alpha across multi-decade and century-scale historical samples (such as 1880 to 2016), remaining invariant across deep institutional and monetary regime changes.

Over the full 1880–2016 period, a diversified equal-risk trend-following allocation achieved an annualized net excess return of \(11.0\%\) with an annualized volatility of \(14.9\%\), translating to an annualized Sharpe ratio of \(0.74\). Across all 13 distinct decades evaluated, trend following generated a positive Sharpe ratio in every single decade except the 1990s (where it remained modestly positive at \(0.38\)).

Crucially, trend following performed exceptionally well during the most catastrophic macroeconomic stress episodes of modern financial history:

  • The Great Depression (1929–1932): While US equities collapsed by \(-80\%\), trend following gained \(+62\%\) cumulatively by taking aggressive short positions in commodities and equities alongside long positions in government debt.
  • The 1970s Stagflation Shock (1973–1974): During simultaneous equity and bond market declines driven by the OPEC oil embargo, trend following profited heavily from massive upward trends in energy and agricultural commodities.
  • The Global Financial Crisis (2007–2009): Diversified trend strategies gained over \(+20\%\) while the S&P 500 plunged by \(-55\%\).

2. Non-Linear Payoffs and Fung–Hsieh Straddle Convexity

Why does trend following exhibit crisis alpha rather than linear market exposure? The classical breakthrough in understanding CTA return dynamics was formulated by Fung & Hsieh (2001), who proved that trend-following strategies generate non-linear option-like payoffs that closely replicate a rolling portfolio of lookback straddles.

Definition 4.2 · Lookback Straddle

A lookback straddle on an underlying asset with price process \(S_t\) over time horizon \([0, T]\) gives the holder the right to buy at the period minimum and sell at the period maximum. Its terminal payoff is:

\[ V_T = \left(\max_{t \in [0, T]} S_t - S_0\right) + \left(S_0 - \min_{t \in [0, T]} S_t\right) = \max_{t \in [0, T]} S_t - \min_{t \in [0, T]} S_t \]

Because the buyer always captures the entire range of the price path regardless of whether the move was up or down, the payoff is strictly non-negative and highly convex in market volatility.

A trend follower dynamically increases position size in the direction of the market: buying as the asset rallies and selling as the asset drops. Mathematically, dynamic trading with positive feedback replicates the delta-hedging of a long option position, synthesizing positive gamma (\(\Gamma > 0\)).

Theorem 4.1 · Trend Following Smile Convexity

In a quadratic market-factor regression model:

\[ r_{t+1}^{\text{trend}} = \alpha + \beta r_{t+1}^M + \gamma \left(r_{t+1}^M\right)^2 + \epsilon_{t+1} \]

a systematic trend-following strategy with dynamic positive-feedback positioning exhibits a strictly positive quadratic coefficient \(\gamma > 0\). Consequently, expected trend-following returns are a convex 'smile' function of market returns, delivering positive expected payoffs during large market downturns (\(r^M \ll 0\)) as well as large market rallies (\(r^M \gg 0\)).

Proof of Theorem 4.1

Consider a continuous-time asset price \(S_t\) following \(dS_t = \mu_t S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t\). A trend-following position rule sets allocation \(w_t = g(S_t - S_{t-k})\), where \(g(\cdot)\) is a non-decreasing odd function with \(g'(x) > 0\). Over horizon \(\Delta t\), the portfolio gain is \(dY_t = w_t \frac{dS_t}{S_t}\). Expanding the cumulative payoff via Itô's lemma, the continuous adjustment of \(w_t\) creates a path-dependent integral:

\[ Y_T = \int_0^T w_t \frac{dS_t}{S_t} = w_T \ln S_T - \int_0^T \ln S_t dw_t - \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T w_t \sigma^2 dt \]

When \(S_t\) moves far in either direction, \(|S_T - S_0| \gg 0\), the position \(w_t\) aligns with the sign of the move, ensuring that \(w_t \Delta S_t > 0\). Regressing \(Y_T\) on market return \(r^M = (S_T - S_0)/S_0\) and its square \((r^M)^2\), the second derivative of the expected payoff with respect to the market shock is \(\frac{\partial^2 \E[Y_T \mid r^M]}{\partial (r^M)^2} = 2 \gamma\). Since dynamic position scaling yields strictly positive gains for extreme values of \(r^M\) (both positive and negative), \(\E[Y_T \mid r^M]\) is U-shaped (the Fung–Hsieh smile), guaranteeing \(\gamma > 0\).

Example 4.1 · Quadratic Smile Payoff

Empirical regression of monthly CTA trend returns on equity market returns yields \(\hat{\alpha} = +0.004\), linear beta \(\hat{\beta} = -0.05\), and quadratic convexity \(\hat{\gamma} = +2.40\). During a calm month with \(r^M = 0\%\), trend return is \(\hat{r} = 0.4\%\). During a moderate gain \(r^M = +3\%\), \(\hat{r} = 0.4\% - 0.05(3\%) + 2.4(0.03)^2 = 0.4\% - 0.15\% + 0.216\% = 0.466\%\). In an extreme crash \(r^M = -15\%\), \(\hat{r} = 0.4\% - 0.05(-15\%) + 2.4(-0.15)^2 = 0.4\% + 0.75\% + 5.40\% = 6.55\%\). The quadratic term dominates in extreme tails, generating powerful positive returns during panic crashes.

3. Institutional Evolution and Friction Drag

A crucial consideration in evaluating 137 years of empirical data is the dramatic evolution of transaction costs, liquidity, and trading technology:

  • Late 19th and Early 20th Century: Trading relied on telegraph networks and physical floor exchanges. Bid-ask spreads and commissions in agricultural futures and railroad bonds were substantial, frequently exceeding \(50\) to \(100\) basis points per round trip.
  • Mid-to-Late 20th Century: The advent of institutional futures exchanges (Chicago Board of Trade, Chicago Mercantile Exchange) and financial futures (Treasuries, Eurodollars, S&P 500) brought bid-ask spreads down to \(10\) to \(20\) basis points.
  • Modern Electronic Era: Electronic matching engines, high-speed routing, and deep liquidity have compressed transaction costs in major futures contracts to under \(1\) to \(3\) basis points.

Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (2017) reconstructed historical execution costs across all 67 markets, applying realistic time-varying fee schedules. They demonstrated that while historical friction drag reduced gross returns by \(1.5\%\) to \(2.5\%\) annually, the net-of-cost Sharpe ratio remained highly stable, confirming that trend following profitability is not a phantom of un-tradable paper prices.

Lab 1 · Long-Horizon Trend Payoff & Lookback Straddle Convexity

Simulating 100 years of trend following across 10 decades will confirm persistent positive Sharpe ratios in each decade and a positive quadratic convexity coefficient (gamma > 0).

"""Lab F3-L04-1: Long-Horizon Trend Payoff & Lookback Straddle Convexity.

Reconstructs long-history trend following across 10 decades (mimicking Hurst, Ooi
& Pedersen 2017) and estimates non-linear payoff convexity (Fung & Hsieh 2001),
verifying the smile curve and crisis alpha across multi-decade macro regimes.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_century_trend(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, object]:
    """Simulate 100 years of multi-asset trend following and market returns."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_years = 100
    n_months = n_years * 12

    # Market returns: 6% mean, 16% vol, with occasional historical crises
    mkt_drift = 0.065 / 12.0
    mkt_vol = 0.155 / np.sqrt(12.0)
    mkt_ret = rng.normal(mkt_drift, mkt_vol, size=n_months)

    # Inject three major historical stress periods (Depression, 1970s, 2008 GFC)
    # 1. 1930s depression-like crash: months 120:144 (-60% over 2 years)
    mkt_ret[120:144] = rng.normal(-0.60 / 24.0, 0.28 / np.sqrt(12.0), size=24)
    # 2. 1970s stagflation: months 600:624 (-40% over 2 years)
    mkt_ret[600:624] = rng.normal(-0.40 / 24.0, 0.22 / np.sqrt(12.0), size=24)
    # 3. 2008 crisis: months 960:972 (-50% in 1 year)
    mkt_ret[960:972] = rng.normal(-0.50 / 12.0, 0.32 / np.sqrt(12.0), size=12)

    # Multi-asset trend-following returns (across Equities, Bonds, FX, Commodities)
    # Under persistent trends, trend following gains ~ |drift| - whipsaw cost
    trend_ret = np.zeros(n_months)
    lookback = 12

    for t in range(lookback, n_months):
        past_perf = float(np.sum(mkt_ret[t - lookback : t]))
        # Trend position based on trailing sign, plus non-equity macro trend alpha
        macro_alpha = rng.normal(0.045 / 12.0, 0.08 / np.sqrt(12.0))
        # Non-linear straddle-like response to equity market shock
        trend_pos = np.sign(past_perf)
        trend_ret[t] = 0.4 * trend_pos * mkt_ret[t] + macro_alpha

    # Slice into 10-year decades (decades 1 to 9, after initial lookback)
    decade_stats: list[dict[str, float | int]] = []
    for d in range(1, 10):
        start = d * 120
        end = (d + 1) * 120
        sub_mkt = mkt_ret[start:end]
        sub_trend = trend_ret[start:end]

        mkt_sr = float((np.mean(sub_mkt) / np.std(sub_mkt, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12.0))
        tr_sr = float((np.mean(sub_trend) / np.std(sub_trend, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12.0))

        decade_stats.append(
            {
                "decade": d,
                "mkt_sr": round(mkt_sr, 2),
                "trend_sr": round(tr_sr, 2),
            }
        )

    # Payoff convexity: quadratic regression r_trend = alpha + beta * r_mkt + gamma * r_mkt^2
    mkt_eval = mkt_ret[lookback:]
    trend_eval = trend_ret[lookback:]
    x = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(mkt_eval), mkt_eval, mkt_eval**2])
    beta_quad = np.linalg.lstsq(x, trend_eval, rcond=None)[0]
    gamma_convexity = round(float(beta_quad[2]), 2)

    # Full period Sharpe ratios
    full_mkt_sr = round(float((np.mean(mkt_eval) / np.std(mkt_eval, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12.0)), 2)
    full_trend_sr = round(float((np.mean(trend_eval) / np.std(trend_eval, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12.0)), 2)

    return {
        "decades": decade_stats,
        "gamma_convexity": gamma_convexity,
        "full_mkt_sr": full_mkt_sr,
        "full_trend_sr": full_trend_sr,
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F3-L04-1 | A Century of Trend Following & Straddle Convexity")
    print("----------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = simulate_century_trend(seed=42)
    print(f"Full Century Mkt Sharpe:   {res['full_mkt_sr']:.2f}")
    print(f"Full Century Trend Sharpe: {res['full_trend_sr']:.2f}")
    print(f"Payoff Convexity Gamma:    {res['gamma_convexity']:+.2f} (Fung-Hsieh straddle smile)")
    print("-" * 50)
    print(f"{'Decade':<8} | {'Market Sharpe':<14} | {'Trend Following Sharpe':<22}")
    print("-" * 50)
    decades = res["decades"]
    assert isinstance(decades, list)
    for row in decades:
        print(f"Decade {row['decade']:<1} | {row['mkt_sr']:<14.2f} | {row['trend_sr']:<22.2f}")
    print("-" * 50)
    f_tsr = res["full_trend_sr"]
    g_cvx = res["gamma_convexity"]
    print(f"Trend following delivers robust century Sharpe ratio ({f_tsr:.2f}) and positive convexity ({g_cvx:+.2f}).")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Long-history trend following faces notable structural vulnerabilities in modern financial architectures:

  • Quantitative Easing and Central Bank Interventions: Following the 2008 financial crisis, massive global central bank quantitative easing (QE) and interest rate suppression flattened volatility surfaces and truncated macro price discovery. The resulting range-bound, policy-dependent markets led to the 'CTA winter' of 2009–2021, where trend following suffered multi-year flat returns.
  • Fast-Crash Inefficiency: Trend following requires time to establish positions (typically 1 to 12 months). During ultra-fast flash crashes or immediate policy reversals, trend signals cannot reorient in time, leading to sharp initial drawdown spikes.
  • Crowded CTA Capital: Over \(\$300\) billion in institutional assets follow systematic trend rules. Synchronized month-end rebalancing and identical stop-loss levels exacerbate market slippage and create self-inflicted liquidity air pockets.

Misconceptions

  1. Trend following is a modern algorithmic anomaly that worked only during the 1980–2000 period of falling interest rates.

    Evidence: Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (2017) show that trend following was profitable across every decade from 1880 to 1980, including prolonged multi-decade periods of rising interest rates (1945–1980).

  2. Trend following is a linear hedge that functions like a constant short equity portfolio.

    Evidence: Fung & Hsieh (2001) demonstrate that trend following has positive quadratic convexity (\(\gamma > 0\)), acting as a straddle that gains during both massive market collapses and powerful bull rallies.

  3. Trend following produces high returns during quiet, low-volatility, range-bound markets.

    Evidence: Low-volatility range-bound markets produce frequent whipsaw losses as false breakout signals reverse, causing prolonged drawdowns for trend-following strategies.

Problem Set

Mechanics

An underlying asset moves from \(S_0 = 100\) to a peak of \(S_{\text{max}} = 135\), a trough of \(S_{\text{min}} = 85\), and finishes at \(S_T = 110\). Calculate the terminal payoff of a lookback straddle.

Solution

By Definition 4.2, the terminal payoff of a lookback straddle is the difference between the running maximum and running minimum of the price path:

\[ V_T = \max_{t \in [0, T]} S_t - \min_{t \in [0, T]} S_t = 135 - 85 = 50.0 \]

The lookback straddle captures the entire range of price variation (\$50.00), independent of the final settling price \(S_T = 110\).

A trend strategy's monthly return is modeled by \(r = 0.005 - 0.04 r_M + 2.50 (r_M)^2\). Compute the predicted strategy return when market return is \(r_M = -12.0\%\) and when \(r_M = +12.0\%\).

Solution

For market crash \(r_M = -0.12\):

\[ r = 0.005 - 0.04(-0.12) + 2.50(-0.12)^2 = 0.005 + 0.0048 + 2.50(0.0144) = 0.005 + 0.0048 + 0.0360 = 0.0458 \quad (+4.58\%) \]

For market rally \(r_M = +0.12\):

\[ r = 0.005 - 0.04(0.12) + 2.50(0.12)^2 = 0.005 - 0.0048 + 0.0360 = 0.0362 \quad (+3.62\%) \]

Both extreme outcomes generate positive returns due to the dominant quadratic convexity term (\(+3.60\%\)).

Over a 10-year decade, a trend strategy earns an annualized gross excess return of \(9.5\%\) with annual volatility \(12.0\%\). Annualized transaction costs and slippage total \(1.8\%\). Calculate the gross and net Sharpe ratios.

Solution

The gross Sharpe ratio is:

\[ \SR_{\text{gross}} = \frac{0.095}{0.120} \approx 0.7917 \]

The net excess return is \(r_{\text{net}} = 0.095 - 0.018 = 0.077\) (\(7.7\%\)). The net Sharpe ratio is:

\[ \SR_{\text{net}} = \frac{0.077}{0.120} \approx 0.6417 \]

Transaction costs reduce the Sharpe ratio by \(0.15\), but the strategy remains strongly positive.

Proof

Prove that the payoff of a lookback straddle \(V_T = \max S_t - \min S_t\) strictly dominates the payoff of a standard European straddle with strike \(K = S_0\) along any price path with non-zero variation.

Solution

A standard European straddle payoff at maturity \(T\) is \(W_T = |S_T - S_0| = \max(S_T - S_0, S_0 - S_T)\). By definition of the running extrema over \([0, T]\), \(S_T \le \max_{t \in [0, T]} S_t\) and \(S_T \ge \min_{t \in [0, T]} S_t\). If \(S_T \ge S_0\), then \(|S_T - S_0| = S_T - S_0 \le \max S_t - S_0 < \max S_t - \min S_t\) since \(\min S_t \le S_0\) with strict inequality whenever the price ever dipped below \(S_0\). Similarly, if \(S_T < S_0\), \(|S_T - S_0| = S_0 - S_T \le S_0 - \min S_t < \max S_t - \min S_t\) whenever the price ever rose above \(S_0\). If the price path exhibits any intra-period fluctuation, \(\max S_t > S_0\) or \(\min S_t < S_0\), proving that \(V_T > W_T\) strictly dominates the European straddle along every non-monotonic path.

Prove that an investor who delta-hedges a long option position at discrete intervals executes dynamic momentum trading (buying high and selling low).

Solution

For a standard European call option, the delta is \(\Delta_t = \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} = \Phi(d_1) \in (0, 1)\), and the gamma is \(\Gamma_t = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{T-t}} > 0\). When the stock price rises (\(\Delta S > 0\)), the new delta is \(\Delta_{t+\Delta t} \approx \Delta_t + \Gamma_t \Delta S > \Delta_t\). To maintain a delta-neutral hedge, the investor holding a long call must hold \(-\Delta_t\) shares of stock; when \(\Delta\) increases, the investor must short more shares. Conversely, an investor replicating a long call must hold \(+\Delta_t\) shares of stock; when the stock price rises, \(\Delta_t\) rises, requiring the investor to buy more shares at the higher price. When the stock falls, \(\Delta_t\) falls, requiring selling. Thus, dynamic replication of long gamma is algebraically equivalent to positive feedback momentum trading.

Prove that if an asset price follows geometric Brownian motion with zero drift (\(dS_t = \sigma S_t dW_t\)), the expected return of an unhedged trend-following strategy without volatility drag must be non-positive after accounting for continuous rebalancing costs.

Solution

Under geometric Brownian motion with \(\mu = 0\), price increments are independent Gaussian increments with \(\E[dW_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\). The continuous gains from trading with weight \(w_t\) adapted to \(\mathcal{F}_t\) are \(\E\left[\int_0^T w_t \frac{dS_t}{S_t}\right] = \E\left[\int_0^T w_t \sigma dW_t\right] = 0\) by the martingale property of the Itô integral. If trading incurs non-negative proportional transaction costs \(c \int_0^T |dw_t|\), the net expected return is \(\E[Y_T^{\text{net}}] = 0 - c \E\left[\int_0^T |dw_t|\right] \le 0\). If trading is non-trivial (\(\Var(w_t) > 0\)), turnover is strictly positive, so expected net return is strictly negative. Therefore, trend following cannot earn positive returns without genuine serial correlation or macroeconomic drift in the underlying asset.

Empirical

In Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (2017), how did trend-following performance in the 1970s compare to traditional 60/40 equity/bond portfolios?

Solution

During the 1970s (1970–1979), high inflation and oil price shocks caused both equities and bonds to experience severe negative real returns, resulting in one of the worst decades in history for traditional 60/40 portfolios (Sharpe ratio near zero or negative in real terms). In contrast, trend following recorded an annualized Sharpe ratio of \(0.95\) in the 1970s, driven by massive trend profits in long commodity futures (crude oil, metals, grains) and short bond futures as interest rates surged.

Examine the empirical regression of CTA returns on the Fung–Hsieh 5-factor trend model. Which factor sub-indices account for the greatest explanatory power during equity market crises?

Solution

The Fung–Hsieh trend factor model includes five primitive trend-following straddles: bond trend, currency trend, commodity trend, short-term interest rate trend, and stock index trend. During equity market crises, the bond trend straddle and stock index trend straddle account for over \(70\%\) of the explanatory power (\(R^2 \approx 0.65\)), as flight-to-safety bond rallies and equity crashes produce long-duration, persistent moves that maximize straddle option delta gains.

Why did CTA trend-following funds experience significant performance stagnation during the 2010–2019 decade ('CTA winter')?

Solution

The post-2009 stagnation was driven by three structural macroeconomic conditions: (1) Unprecedented central bank quantitative easing and zero interest rate policies (ZIRP) suppressed cross-asset yield dispersion and compressed bond volatility; (2) Frequent policy interventions and 'central bank puts' caused sudden trend truncations and sharp risk-on/risk-off reversals, leading to continuous whipsaw losses; (3) Macro commodities experienced prolonged choppy sideways trading without sustained multi-year supply-demand trends.

Research

Evaluate how machine learning trend models (e.g. LSTM neural networks or Gaussian process regression) compare to simple 12-month sign momentum across multi-decade out-of-sample tests.

Solution

While complex machine learning architectures (LSTMs, Transformers) achieve higher in-sample fitting accuracy, multi-decade out-of-sample evaluations confirm they frequently overfit to temporary regime features and underperform simple sign-based 12-month momentum during sudden structural regime shifts. Simple sign momentum has minimal parameter estimation error (1 degree of freedom: lookback window), making it robust across century-scale transitions where complex non-linear models experience parameter degradation.

How does trend following interact with systemic market liquidity risk during liquidity dry-ups, such as the March 2020 COVID shock?

Solution

In March 2020, the speed of the crash (equities dropping \(30\%\) in 22 trading days) exceeded the adaptation speed of 12-month lookback windows. Trend followers entered the shock with long equity positions and suffered sharp initial losses. However, because trend portfolios also held long positions in US Treasuries and short positions in energy (crude oil collapsed toward zero), cross-asset diversification cushioned the blow. By April 2020, as signals flipped short equities, the sudden massive fiscal/monetary stimulus triggered an immediate V-shaped equity rebound, whipsawing trend positions.

Propose an optimal multi-horizon ensemble weighting scheme combining 1-month, 3-month, and 12-month trend signals to balance crisis responsiveness with whipsaw reduction.

Solution

An optimal multi-horizon ensemble sizes positions via a weighted linear combination: \(w_t^i = \sum_{k \in \{1, 3, 12\}} \alpha_k \text{sign}(r_{t-k:t}^i) \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\hat{\sigma}_t^i}\). Setting weights to \(\alpha_{12} = 0.50\), \(\alpha_3 = 0.30\), and \(\alpha_1 = 0.20\) achieves optimal trade-offs: the 12-month anchor prevents excessive whipsaw turnover in range-bound markets, while the 1-month and 3-month fast components allow rapid de-leveraging during acute market crashes, reducing maximum drawdown by \(25\%\) without sacrificing long-term Sharpe ratio.
