Value and Momentum Everywhere

For decades, value and momentum were studied as isolated equity anomalies driven by retail behavioral biases or stock-specific distress. Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013) shattered this segmentation by establishing that value and momentum exist across four major asset classes and eight diverse markets, exhibiting strong negative within-asset correlations and common global factor exposures. Mastering this cross-asset framework reveals why combining value and momentum nearly doubles standalone Sharpe ratios and rejects market-segmented pricing models.

1. Empirical Breadth Across Asset Classes

In classical empirical finance, the value premium and cross-sectional momentum were treated as idiosyncratic features of equity markets. Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013) examined four distinct non-equity asset classes alongside four equity markets: individual equities across the United States, United Kingdom, Continental Europe, and Japan; country equity index futures; government bond futures; foreign exchange currencies; and commodity futures over the period 1972 to 2011. Across all eight markets, both value and momentum yield statistically significant positive risk-adjusted excess returns.

Definition 1.1 · Value and Momentum Everywhere

Value and momentum everywhere denotes the empirical law that buying cheap assets relative to fundamental anchors (value) and buying recent outperforming assets (momentum) delivers positive, statistically significant Sharpe ratios across all major asset classes, exhibits strong negative within-market return correlations, and shares common global risk factor dynamics.

To implement value and momentum uniformly across disparate financial structures, signals are formulated relative to asset-class-specific anchors:

  • Individual Equities: Value is trailing book-to-market ratio \(B/M\); momentum is trailing 12-month return skipping the most recent month (\(t-12\) to \(t-2\)) to purge short-term reversal.
  • Equity Index Futures: Value is aggregate book-to-market or dividend yield; momentum is 12-month return.
  • Government Bonds: Value is the 5-year change in 10-year sovereign bond yield (\(-(y_t - y_{t-60})\)); momentum is the 12-month bond future return.
  • Foreign Exchange: Value is the 5-year real exchange rate deviation from Purchasing Power Parity (PPP); momentum is the 12-month currency forward return.
  • Commodities: Value is the negative 5-year spot price change (\(-\ln(S_t / \bar{S}_{t-60:t})\)); momentum is the 12-month futures return.

2. The Negative Correlation Phenomenon

Within every single asset class studied by Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013), the correlation between the value strategy return \(r^V\) and the momentum strategy return \(r^M\) is strongly negative, averaging \(-0.47\) across all asset classes and reaching \(-0.64\) in individual US stocks.

Remark 1.1 · Economic Mechanism of Negative Correlation

The negative correlation arises from opposing horizon dynamics. Value buys assets whose prices have declined over multi-year horizons (3 to 5 years), betting on fundamental mean reversion. Momentum buys assets that have rallied over the past year (12 months), betting on intermediate continuation. Consequently, when an asset experiences a sustained rally, it moves from the value bucket into the expensive bucket while simultaneously entering the high-momentum bucket. When market sentiment shifts, these opposing exposures provide an intrinsic structural hedge.

3. The Sharpe Ratio Doubling Theorem

The practical implication of negative correlation between two positive-expectation factors is dramatic diversification expansion. We formalize this property analytically:

Theorem 1.1 · Sharpe Ratio Expansion Under Negative Correlation

Let two factor strategies have identical positive expected excess return \(\mu > 0\), identical return variance \(\sigma^2 > 0\), identical standalone Sharpe ratio \(\SR = \mu / \sigma\), and correlation \(\rho \in (-1, 1)\). The Sharpe ratio of an equal-risk combination \(r^C = \frac{1}{2} r^V + \frac{1}{2} r^M\) satisfies:

\[ \SR_{\text{combo}} = \frac{\SR}{\sqrt{\frac{1 + \rho}{2}}} \]

In particular, if \(\rho = -0.50\), the combined Sharpe ratio is exactly twice the standalone Sharpe ratio: \(\SR_{\text{combo}} = 2 \cdot \SR\).

Proof of Theorem 1.1

The expected return of the 50/50 combination is \(\E[r^C] = \frac{1}{2}\mu + \frac{1}{2}\mu = \mu\). The variance of the combination is:

\[ \Var(r^C) = \frac{1}{4} \Var(r^V) + \frac{1}{4} \Var(r^M) + \frac{1}{2} \Cov(r^V, r^M) = \frac{1}{4}\sigma^2 + \frac{1}{4}\sigma^2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho \sigma^2 = \frac{1 + \rho}{2} \sigma^2 \]

Taking the square root yields standard deviation \(\sigma_C = \sigma \sqrt{\frac{1+\rho}{2}}\). Dividing expected return by standard deviation gives \(\SR_{\text{combo}} = \frac{\mu}{\sigma \sqrt{(1+\rho)/2}} = \frac{\SR}{\sqrt{(1+\rho)/2}}\). Setting \(\rho = -0.50\) gives \(\sqrt{(1 - 0.50)/2} = \sqrt{0.25} = 0.50\), yielding \(\SR_{\text{combo}} = \SR / 0.50 = 2 \SR\).

Example 1.1 · Worked Value-Momentum Combination

Consider an equity index value factor with annualized mean return \(\mu_V = 4.0\%\) and volatility \(\sigma_V = 10.0\%\) (\(\SR_V = 0.40\)), and a momentum factor with \(\mu_M = 4.0\%\) and \(\sigma_M = 10.0\%\) (\(\SR_M = 0.40\)). If their correlation is \(\rho = -0.50\), the 50/50 combination has expected return \(\mu_C = 4.0\%\) and volatility \(\sigma_C = 10.0\% \times \sqrt{0.25} = 5.0\%\). The combined Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_{\text{combo}} = 4.0\% / 5.0\% = 0.80\), achieving double the risk-adjusted efficiency.

4. Global Factor Structure and Market Integration

Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013) tested whether value and momentum returns across asset classes are driven by market-specific frictions or common global systematic risk. They specify a two-factor pricing model:

\[ r_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i, V}^{\text{global}} f_t^{\text{Val, global}} + \beta_{i, M}^{\text{global}} f_t^{\text{Mom, global}} + \epsilon_{i,t} \]

The global two-factor model successfully prices the cross-section of value and momentum portfolios across all eight diverse markets, rejecting the null hypothesis of segmented asset markets. Furthermore, global value loads positively on global liquidity risk, while momentum loads positively on consumption growth and negatively on liquidity risk.

Momentum crash behavior (Daniel & Moskowitz 2016) further illuminates this asymmetry: during panic states when the market drops precipitously, the short side of momentum accumulates high-beta distressed options that rally violently when market panic subsides, causing catastrophic momentum drawdowns unless hedged by value.

Lab 1 · Cross-Asset Value and Momentum Sorts

Simulating value and momentum across Equities, Bonds, Currencies, and Commodities will exhibit consistent negative within-asset-class return correlations and approximately double the standalone Sharpe ratios under equal-risk combination.

"""Lab F3-L01-1: Cross-Asset Value and Momentum Sorts & 50/50 Combo Diversification.

Simulates monthly returns and signals across four asset classes (Equities, Bonds,
Currencies, Commodities) to construct value and momentum factor portfolios,
measure within-asset negative correlations, and verify Sharpe ratio expansion
under equal-risk combination following Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_cross_asset_valmom(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, object]:
    """Simulate value and momentum factor returns across four asset classes."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_months = 240
    asset_classes = ["Equities", "Bonds", "Currencies", "Commodities"]

    # Target annualized annualized statistics:
    # Individual factors: ~4% to 5% excess return, ~10% annual vol (Sharpe ~0.40 - 0.50)
    # Negative correlation within each asset class ~ -0.45 to -0.55
    dt = 1.0 / 12.0
    vol_mth = 0.10 * np.sqrt(dt)

    class_stats: dict[str, dict[str, float]] = {}
    all_val: list[np.ndarray] = []
    all_mom: list[np.ndarray] = []

    for ac in asset_classes:
        # Generate correlated bivariate normal returns for Value and Momentum
        # Base correlation -0.50
        rho = -0.50 + rng.uniform(-0.05, 0.05)
        cov = np.array([[vol_mth**2, rho * vol_mth**2], [rho * vol_mth**2, vol_mth**2]])
        mean = np.array([0.045 * dt, 0.048 * dt])

        ret = rng.multivariate_normal(mean, cov, size=n_months)
        val_ret = ret[:, 0]
        mom_ret = ret[:, 1]

        all_val.append(val_ret)
        all_mom.append(mom_ret)

        # 50/50 combo
        combo_ret = 0.5 * val_ret + 0.5 * mom_ret

        # Annualized statistics
        sr_val = float((np.mean(val_ret) / np.std(val_ret, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12))
        sr_mom = float((np.mean(mom_ret) / np.std(mom_ret, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12))
        sr_combo = float((np.mean(combo_ret) / np.std(combo_ret, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12))
        corr_vm = float(np.corrcoef(val_ret, mom_ret)[0, 1])

        class_stats[ac] = {
            "sr_val": round(sr_val, 2),
            "sr_mom": round(sr_mom, 2),
            "sr_combo": round(sr_combo, 2),
            "corr": round(corr_vm, 2),
        }

    # Global cross-asset diversified factor portfolios
    global_val = np.mean(all_val, axis=0)
    global_mom = np.mean(all_mom, axis=0)
    global_combo = 0.5 * global_val + 0.5 * global_mom

    sr_g_val = float((np.mean(global_val) / np.std(global_val, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12))
    sr_g_mom = float((np.mean(global_mom) / np.std(global_mom, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12))
    sr_g_combo = float((np.mean(global_combo) / np.std(global_combo, ddof=1)) * np.sqrt(12))
    corr_g = float(np.corrcoef(global_val, global_mom)[0, 1])

    return {
        "classes": class_stats,
        "global_sr_val": round(sr_g_val, 2),
        "global_sr_mom": round(sr_g_mom, 2),
        "global_sr_combo": round(sr_g_combo, 2),
        "global_corr": round(corr_g, 2),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F3-L01-1 | Cross-Asset Value & Momentum Everywhere (AMP 2013)")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = simulate_cross_asset_valmom(seed=42)
    classes = res["classes"]
    assert isinstance(classes, dict)

    print(f"{'Asset Class':<14} | {'SR(Val)':<8} | {'SR(Mom)':<8} | {'Corr(V,M)':<10} | {'SR(Combo)':<8}")
    print("-" * 60)
    for ac, st in classes.items():
        assert isinstance(st, dict)
        sr_v, sr_m = st["sr_val"], st["sr_mom"]
        cvm, src = st["corr"], st["sr_combo"]
        print(f"{ac:<14} | {sr_v:<8.2f} | {sr_m:<8.2f} | {cvm:<10.2f} | {src:<8.2f}")
    print("-" * 60)
    gsr_v, gsr_m = res["global_sr_val"], res["global_sr_mom"]
    g_corr, gsr_c = res["global_corr"], res["global_sr_combo"]
    print(f"{'Global All':<14} | {gsr_v:<8.2f} | {gsr_m:<8.2f} | {g_corr:<10.2f} | {gsr_c:<8.2f}")
    print("Negative correlation within every asset class doubles the combined Sharpe ratio.")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical canon of value and momentum everywhere faces significant institutional boundaries:

  • Structural Value Traps: Long-term technological disruption (e.g. legacy energy and retail during digital transformation) can render book-value metrics obsolete, resulting in multi-decade value underperformance where mean reversion fails.
  • Momentum Tail Crashes: As demonstrated by Daniel & Moskowitz (2016), cross-sectional momentum exhibits severe negative skewness during macroeconomic turnarounds, requiring dynamic volatility scaling or explicit call-option hedges.
  • Capacity Constraints: While liquid index futures and currency forwards accommodate large capital, individual stock value and momentum strategies encounter severe transaction cost drag in microcaps and illiquid international markets (microcap problem).

Misconceptions

  1. Value and momentum are retail equity anomalies that do not exist in institutional fixed income or foreign exchange markets.

    Evidence: Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013) demonstrate robust value and momentum premia in government bond futures, currency forwards, and commodity contracts where retail investors have negligible presence.

  2. Combining two factor strategies produces a Sharpe ratio equal to the average of their individual Sharpe ratios.

    Evidence: By Theorem 1.1, when two factors possess negative correlation (\(\rho < 0\)), their combination achieves a Sharpe ratio strictly greater than either individual component, doubling standalone Sharpe efficiency when \(\rho = -0.50\).

  3. Value and momentum crash at the same time during financial crises.

    Evidence: Value tends to suffer during liquidity crunches, whereas momentum suffers during sudden market rebounds following prolonged panic; their negative correlation holds even in crisis states.

Problem Set

Mechanics

Two factors \(A\) and \(B\) have identical annual standard deviations \(\sigma = 0.12\), identical annual expected excess returns \(\mu = 0.06\), and return correlation \(\rho = -0.60\). Calculate the volatility and Sharpe ratio of an equal-weighted 50/50 combination.

Solution

The expected return of the 50/50 combo is \(\mu_C = 0.06\). By Theorem 1.1, the combined portfolio standard deviation is:

\[ \sigma_C = \sigma \sqrt{\frac{1 + \rho}{2}} = 0.12 \times \sqrt{\frac{1 - 0.60}{2}} = 0.12 \times \sqrt{0.20} = 0.12 \times 0.447214 = 0.053666 \]

The standalone Sharpe ratio is \(\SR = 0.06 / 0.12 = 0.50\). The combination Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_{\text{combo}} = 0.06 / 0.053666 = 1.1180\), representing an expansion factor of \(1.118 / 0.50 = 2.236\).

In an equity index futures universe of 4 contracts, trailing 12-month returns are \(+15\%\), \(+8\%\), \(-2\%\), and \(-11\%\). Compute the demeaned dollar-neutral momentum weights normalized such that gross leverage \(\sum |w_i| = 1.0\).

Solution

Mean return is \(\bar{r} = (15 + 8 - 2 - 11)/4 = 10/4 = 2.5\%\). The demeaned returns are \([12.5, 5.5, -4.5, -13.5]\%\). The sum of absolute demeaned returns is \(12.5 + 5.5 + 4.5 + 13.5 = 36.0\%\). The normalized weights are \(w = [12.5/36, 5.5/36, -4.5/36, -13.5/36] = [+0.3472, +0.1528, -0.1250, -0.3750]\). The weights sum to zero (\(\sum w_i = 0\)) and sum of absolute values equals \(1.0\).

A currency value factor has annualized volatility of \(8\%\) and annualized mean return of \(3.2\%\). A currency momentum factor has annualized volatility of \(10\%\) and annualized mean return of \(4.0\%\). Their correlation is \(\rho = -0.40\). Determine the optimal risk-parity weights and resulting Sharpe ratio.

Solution

Both factors have identical standalone Sharpe ratio \(\SR = 3.2/8.0 = 4.0/10.0 = 0.40\). Under risk parity, weights are inversely proportional to volatility: \(w_V \propto 1/8 = 0.125\), \(w_M \propto 1/10 = 0.100\). Normalizing yields \(w_V = 0.125 / 0.225 = 0.5556\) and \(w_M = 0.100 / 0.225 = 0.4444\). The combined expected return is \(\mu_C = 0.5556(0.032) + 0.4444(0.040) = 0.03556\). The combined variance is \(w_V^2 \sigma_V^2 + w_M^2 \sigma_M^2 + 2 w_V w_M \rho \sigma_V \sigma_M = (0.5556)^2 (0.0064) + (0.4444)^2 (0.0100) + 2(0.5556)(0.4444)(-0.40)(0.08)(0.10) = 0.001975 + 0.001975 - 0.001579 = 0.002371\). Thus \(\sigma_C = \sqrt{0.002371} = 0.04870\), and the combination Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_C = 0.03556 / 0.04870 = 0.7302\).

Proof

Prove that for any correlation \(\rho \in (-1, 1)\), the Sharpe ratio expansion factor \(\kappa(\rho) = 1 / \sqrt{(1+\rho)/2}\) is strictly decreasing in \(\rho\), and find its limit as \(\rho \to -1^+\).

Solution

Let \(g(\rho) = (1+\rho)/2\). Since \(g'(\rho) = 1/2 > 0\), \(g(\rho)\) is strictly increasing on \((-1, 1)\). The expansion factor is \(\kappa(\rho) = [g(\rho)]^{-1/2}\). Differentiating with respect to \(\rho\) using the chain rule yields \(\kappa'(\rho) = -\frac{1}{2} [g(\rho)]^{-3/2} g'(\rho) = -\frac{1}{4} [(1+\rho)/2]^{-3/2} < 0\) for all \(\rho \in (-1, 1)\). Hence \(\kappa(\rho)\) is strictly decreasing in \(\rho\). As \(\rho \to -1^+\), \(g(\rho) \to 0^+\), so \(\lim_{\rho \to -1^+} \kappa(\rho) = +\infty\). As correlation approaches perfect negative correlation, risk is eliminated and Sharpe ratio approaches infinity.

Let \(K\) independent asset classes each have a value factor \(V_k\) and momentum factor \(M_k\) with correlation \(\rho \in (-1, 1)\) within each asset class, and zero correlation across different asset classes. Prove that the Sharpe ratio of the global combination \(\frac{1}{2K} \sum_{k=1}^K (V_k + M_k)\) scales as \(\sqrt{K} \cdot \SR_{\text{combo}}\).

Solution

Let \(C_k = \frac{1}{2}(V_k + M_k)\) be the within-class combo portfolio for asset class \(k\). By Theorem 1.1, each \(C_k\) has expected return \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma_C^2 = \frac{1+\rho}{2} \sigma^2\). Since asset classes are mutually uncorrelated, the global combo \(G = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K C_k\) has expected return \(\E[G] = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \mu = \mu\). The variance is \(\Var(G) = \frac{1}{K^2} \sum_{k=1}^K \Var(C_k) = \frac{1}{K^2} (K \sigma_C^2) = \frac{1}{K} \sigma_C^2\). Thus the standard deviation of \(G\) is \(\sigma_G = \sigma_C / \sqrt{K}\). The Sharpe ratio of the global portfolio is \(\SR_G = \E[G] / \sigma_G = \mu / (\sigma_C / \sqrt{K}) = \sqrt{K} (\mu / \sigma_C) = \sqrt{K} \cdot \SR_{\text{combo}}\).

Prove that if two factors have unequal Sharpe ratios \(\SR_1 > \SR_2 > 0\) and correlation \(\rho\), a positive-weight combination strictly improves upon \(\SR_1\) if and only if \(\rho < \SR_2 / \SR_1\).

Solution

Without loss of generality, scale both assets to unit variance \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1\), so \(\mu_1 = \SR_1\) and \(\mu_2 = \SR_2\). Consider a portfolio with weight \((1-w)\) on asset 1 and \(w\) on asset 2. The Sharpe ratio is \(f(w) = \frac{(1-w)\mu_1 + w\mu_2}{\sqrt{(1-w)^2 + w^2 + 2w(1-w)\rho}}\). Differentiating with respect to \(w\) and evaluating at \(w = 0\) yields \(f'(0) = \mu_2 - \rho \mu_1 = \SR_2 - \rho \SR_1\). The derivative is strictly positive if and only if \(\SR_2 - \rho \SR_1 > 0\), which is equivalent to \(\rho < \SR_2 / \SR_1\). When this condition holds, shifting marginal weight from asset 1 to asset 2 strictly increases the portfolio Sharpe ratio beyond \(\SR_1\).

Empirical

In empirical studies of currency value strategies using Purchasing Power Parity (PPP), what is the typical half-life of real exchange rate deviations, and what does this imply for strategy turnover?

Solution

Empirical studies (e.g. Rogoff 1996, Asness et al. 2013) find that real exchange rate deviations from PPP have a half-life of 3 to 5 years (36 to 60 months). This extremely slow mean reversion implies that currency value strategies have very low portfolio turnover (typically under 20% annualized), resulting in minimal transaction cost drag compared to high-turnover 12-month momentum strategies.

Why do Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013) use 5-year changes in 10-year yields rather than yield curve slope as their primary bond value indicator?

Solution

The yield curve slope (10-year yield minus 3-month yield) measures carry and term premium rather than fundamental valuation relative to historical baseline. The 5-year change in yield measures whether current yields are unusually high or low relative to their multi-year medium-term average, isolating secular price cheapness (higher yields imply cheaper bond prices) from static carry roll-down.

During the 2007–2008 Global Financial Crisis, how did value and momentum behave respectively in global equity and currency markets?

Solution

During the onset of the crisis in late 2007 through early 2008, momentum performed strongly by shorting distressed financials and homebuilders while going long commodities and defensive equities. Value suffered massive drawdowns as cheap financials became value traps and liquidity contracted. In the sudden market turnaround of March–April 2009, momentum experienced a violent crash while value recovered, confirming their complementary tail properties.

Research

Evaluate the macroeconomic risk explanation for the global value premium: what state variables explain why value assets across equities, bonds, currencies, and commodities demand an expected return premium?

Solution

Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013) demonstrate that global value factors load systematically on market liquidity risk (Pastor & Stambaugh 2003, Acharya & Pedersen 2005). Value assets represent firms or economies with high operating leverage, elevated debt refinancing burdens, or funding constraints that face severe distress when global liquidity dries up. In equilibrium, risk-averse investors require an expected return premium to hold assets that perform poorly during aggregate credit and liquidity crunches.

Why does the presence of cross-asset value and momentum factors reject market segmentation theories of asset pricing?

Solution

Market segmentation theories hypothesize that asset pricing anomalies are caused by specialized investor clienteles and regulatory barriers unique to specific exchanges (e.g. mutual fund retail flows in equities or central bank mandates in bond markets). If segmentation held, value and momentum in commodities would be uncorrelated with value and momentum in equities. The fact that a global two-factor model jointly prices returns across eight distinct markets rejects segmentation in favor of globally integrated factor risk.

Design an institutional implementation of a combined 50/50 value-momentum book that dynamically manages momentum crash risk without sacrificing the negative correlation benefit.

Solution

A sophisticated implementation utilizes Daniel & Moskowitz (2016) volatility-timed momentum scaling alongside explicit out-of-the-money call option overlays on the short momentum basket. When trailing market volatility exceeds historical thresholds and the market is in a distressed drawdown, momentum gross exposure is dynamically scaled down by \(\sigma_{\text{target}} / \sigma_{\text{forecast}}\), preventing the leverage explosion that causes momentum crashes during sudden market turnarounds while preserving negative correlation during normal market regimes.
