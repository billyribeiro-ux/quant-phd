Publication Bias & Anomaly Alpha Decay

Academic journals act as non-linear selection filters: candidate factor strategies that fail to achieve statistical significance are relegated to researchers' file drawers, while those crossing significance thresholds are celebrated. This publication hurdle truncates the distribution of published effects, guaranteeing upward-biased in-sample returns. When published anomalies are traded out-of-sample, their return predictability decays sharply due to the unwinding of selection bias and the arrival of arbitrage capital. Decomposing statistical bias from real arbitrage decay and diagnosing funnel asymmetry are critical competencies for quantitative portfolio managers evaluating backtested returns.

1The Mechanism of Publication Selection

In academic finance and empirical economics, editorial decisions and peer review impose an implicit or explicit hurdle: a candidate factor anomaly must produce a test statistic \(|t| \ge 1.96\) (or \(p \le 0.05\)) to warrant publication. This conditioning mechanism fundamentally alters the sampling distribution of published discoveries.

Definition 2.1 · Publication Bias
Publication bias (or file-drawer bias) is the systematic conditioning of published empirical findings on statistical significance or directionality, such that the observed distribution of published test statistics \(\hat{\theta}_{\text{pub}}\) differs systematically from the true unconditioned population distribution \(\theta \sim F_{\theta}\).

Suppose an underlying universe of potential characteristics contains both true pricing signals and uninformative noise factors within the factor zoo. Let \(\hat{\alpha}_i\) denote the estimated in-sample annualized alpha of candidate factor \(i\), and let \(\se(\hat{\alpha}_i)\) denote its standard error. If journals only publish studies where \(t_i = \hat{\alpha}_i / \se(\hat{\alpha}_i) \ge c\), then even if the true population alpha \(\alpha_i = 0\), the expected published alpha is strictly positive:

\[ \E\bigl[\hat{\alpha}_i \bigm| t_i \ge c\bigr] = \se(\hat{\alpha}_i) \cdot \E\bigl[Z \bigm| Z \ge c\bigr] > 0 \]

Theorem 2.1 · Expected Truncation Bias Under the Null
Suppose candidate alphas satisfy \(\hat{\alpha}_i \sim \Normal(0, \sigma^2)\). If publication requires \(\hat{\alpha}_i / \sigma \ge c\), the conditional expectation of published alphas follows the inverse Mills ratio for the standard normal distribution:

\[ \E\bigl[\hat{\alpha}_i \bigm| \hat{\alpha}_i \ge c\sigma\bigr] = \sigma \, \lambda(c) = \sigma \, \frac{\phi(c)}{1 - \Phi(c)} \]

For the classical two-sided 5% significance hurdle where \(c = 1.96\), \(\phi(1.96) \approx 0.05844\) and \(1 - \Phi(1.96) = 0.025\), yielding:

\[ \lambda(1.96) = \frac{0.05844}{0.025} \approx 2.338 \]

Consequently, every published pure-noise factor exhibits an expected in-sample \(t\)-statistic of at least \(2.34\), despite possessing exactly zero predictive capability.

2The McLean & Pontiff (2016) Anomaly Decay Decomposition

In a landmark empirical investigation, McLean & Pontiff (2016) studied 97 published equity return anomalies across three distinct chronological regimes:

  1. In-Sample Period: The historical date range utilized in the original published paper.
  2. Out-of-Sample (Post-Sample, Pre-Publication) Period: The time window between the end of the paper's data sample and the journal publication date.
  3. Post-Publication Period: The time window after the research paper has appeared in print and becomes widely known to the market.

By comparing the cross-sectional average return spreads across these three intervals, McLean & Pontiff decomposed total observed anomaly decay into two structural components:

Definition 2.2 · Two-Stage Anomaly Alpha Decay
Total post-publication decay \(\Delta_{\text{total}}\) is defined as the proportional reduction in mean monthly return spread from the in-sample baseline:

\[ \Delta_{\text{total}} = 1 - \frac{\bar{R}_{\text{post}}}{\bar{R}_{\text{in}}} \]

This decomposes into:

  1. Statistical Selection Bias (p-hacking / file-drawer): \[ \Delta_{\text{stat}} = 1 - \frac{\bar{R}_{\text{oos}}}{\bar{R}_{\text{in}}} \]
  2. Arbitrage / Price Impact Decay: \[ \Delta_{\text{arb}} = \frac{\bar{R}_{\text{oos}} - \bar{R}_{\text{post}}}{\bar{R}_{\text{in}}} = \Delta_{\text{total}} - \Delta_{\text{stat}} \]

McLean & Pontiff (2016) document that portfolio returns decline by an average of 26% out-of-sample (post-sample but pre-publication), representing statistical bias from data mining and sample selection. Post-publication, portfolio returns decline by a cumulative average of 58%. The incremental 32% decline following public dissemination represents the impact of institutional arbitrage capital competing away return predictability.

Remark 2.1 · Institutional Arbitrage vs. Data Snooping

The fact that \(\bar{R}_{\text{post}}\) remains strictly positive (averaging 42% of original in-sample returns) implies that cross-sectional anomalies are not purely statistical artifacts. True mispricings exist in the cross-section, but publication accelerates capital allocation by quantitative hedge funds, bidding up underpriced stocks and shorting overpriced stocks until trading frictions prevent further decay.

3Funnel Plot Asymmetry and Egger Regressions

In meta-analysis, the standard diagnostic tool for detecting publication selection is the funnel plot, which charts estimated effect sizes against sample precision \(\text{Prec}_i = 1 / \se(\hat{\alpha}_i)\).

In the absence of publication bias, point estimates \(\hat{\alpha}_i\) should scatter symmetrically around the true mean effect, with scatter width narrowing symmetrically as precision increases (resembling an inverted funnel). However, when researchers only report results exceeding a critical \(t\)-statistic hurdle \(|t| \ge c\), studies with small samples (low precision, high standard error) must report massive point estimates to clear the hurdle:

\[ |\hat{\alpha}_i| \ge c \cdot \se(\hat{\alpha}_i) \]

Studies with small standard errors can clear the hurdle with modest alphas. This produces severe asymmetry in the funnel plot: a hollow region in the lower-left where small, statistically insignificant alphas should reside.

Theorem 2.2 · Egger Regression Test for Publication Bias
Let \((\hat{\alpha}_i, \se_i)\) denote the estimated alpha and standard error of published factor \(i = 1, \dots, N\). The Egger regression standardizes the effect size by its standard error:

\[ t_i \equiv \frac{\hat{\alpha}_i}{\se_i} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \left(\frac{1}{\se_i}\right) + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \sim \Normal(0, \sigma_\varepsilon^2) \]

Under the null hypothesis of no publication selection bias, the intercept satisfies \(H_0: \beta_0 = 0\), and the slope \(\beta_1\) captures the true underlying effect size. A statistically significant intercept \(\beta_0 > 0\) (\(p < 0.05\)) provides direct formal evidence of publication selection asymmetry.

4Empirical Lab: Auditing Publication Decay and Funnel Asymmetry

We execute an empirical simulation replicating the McLean & Pontiff (2016) lifecycle and testing funnel plot asymmetry via the Egger specification.

Lab F5-L02-1 · Publication Bias and Anomaly Alpha Decay

Simulating 300 candidate anomalies subject to a publication filter of \(t \ge 2.00\) will produce an in-sample return substantially higher than true latent alpha, with post-sample decay of ~25% and post-publication decay exceeding 50%, while the Egger regression intercept will reject \(\beta_0 = 0\) with \(p < 0.01\).

"""Lab F5-L02-1: Publication Bias and Anomaly Alpha Decay.

Models the publication filter in empirical finance, measures in-sample vs.
post-publication alpha decay (McLean & Pontiff 2016), and detects funnel plot
asymmetry via Egger regressions.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats


def evaluate_publication_bias(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float | int]:
    """Simulate anomaly publication selection and post-publication decay."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_candidates = 300
    t1_is_months = 180
    t2_oos_months = 120

    # True underlying latent annualized alphas drawn from Half-Normal/Exponential
    # with mean ~ 2.4% (0.2% / mo) and vol ~ 15% (4.33% / mo)
    true_alpha_mo = rng.exponential(scale=0.0020, size=n_candidates)
    vol_mo = rng.uniform(0.030, 0.060, size=n_candidates)

    # In-sample realized returns
    is_returns = np.zeros(n_candidates)
    is_se = vol_mo / np.sqrt(t1_is_months)
    for i in range(n_candidates):
        sample = rng.normal(true_alpha_mo[i], vol_mo[i], size=t1_is_months)
        is_returns[i] = float(np.mean(sample))

    is_t_stats = is_returns / is_se

    # Publication filter: only publish if in-sample t >= 2.00
    pub_mask = is_t_stats >= 2.00
    n_published = int(np.sum(pub_mask))

    pub_is_returns = is_returns[pub_mask]
    pub_is_t = is_t_stats[pub_mask]
    pub_is_se = is_se[pub_mask]
    pub_true_alpha = true_alpha_mo[pub_mask]
    pub_vol = vol_mo[pub_mask]

    # Post-publication returns: subject to both statistical mean-reversion
    # AND arbitrage capital inflow decaying true alpha by ~30%
    arbitrage_decay_factor = 0.70
    oos_returns = np.zeros(n_published)
    for j in range(n_published):
        post_alpha = pub_true_alpha[j] * arbitrage_decay_factor
        sample_oos = rng.normal(post_alpha, pub_vol[j], size=t2_oos_months)
        oos_returns[j] = float(np.mean(sample_oos))

    # Annualized return percentages
    mean_is_ann = float(np.mean(pub_is_returns) * 12 * 100)
    mean_oos_ann = float(np.mean(oos_returns) * 12 * 100)
    mean_true_ann = float(np.mean(pub_true_alpha) * 12 * 100)

    # Anomaly decay fraction: 1 - R_oos / R_is
    decay_pct = float((1.0 - mean_oos_ann / mean_is_ann) * 100)

    # Statistical bias component: (R_is - True) / R_is
    stat_bias_pct = float(((mean_is_ann - mean_true_ann) / mean_is_ann) * 100)

    # Egger regression test for funnel asymmetry on published set:
    # t_i = beta_0 + beta_1 * (1 / SE_i) + e_i
    precision = 1.0 / pub_is_se
    x_mat = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(precision), precision])
    beta, _res, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(x_mat, pub_is_t, rcond=None)
    egger_intercept = float(beta[0])
    egger_slope = float(beta[1])

    # Residual standard error and intercept t-stat
    res_var = float(np.sum((pub_is_t - x_mat @ beta) ** 2) / (n_published - 2))
    cov_beta = res_var * np.linalg.inv(x_mat.T @ x_mat)
    se_intercept = float(np.sqrt(cov_beta[0, 0]))
    t_egger = egger_intercept / se_intercept
    p_egger = float(2.0 * (1.0 - stats.t.cdf(np.abs(t_egger), df=n_published - 2)))

    return {
        "n_candidates": n_candidates,
        "n_published": n_published,
        "mean_is_ann": round(mean_is_ann, 2),
        "mean_oos_ann": round(mean_oos_ann, 2),
        "mean_true_ann": round(mean_true_ann, 2),
        "decay_pct": round(decay_pct, 2),
        "stat_bias_pct": round(stat_bias_pct, 2),
        "egger_intercept": round(egger_intercept, 3),
        "egger_slope": round(egger_slope, 4),
        "t_egger": round(t_egger, 2),
        "p_egger": round(p_egger, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F5-L02-1 | Publication Bias and Anomaly Alpha Decay")
    print("------------------------------------------------------")
    res = evaluate_publication_bias(seed=42)
    print(f"Candidates tested: {res['n_candidates']} | Published (t >= 2.0): {res['n_published']}")
    print(f"In-sample published alpha:     {res['mean_is_ann']:.2f}% per year")
    print(f"Post-publication alpha:        {res['mean_oos_ann']:.2f}% per year (True: {res['mean_true_ann']:.2f}%)")
    print(f"Total post-publication decay:  {res['decay_pct']:.2f}%")
    print(f"In-sample selection bias:      {res['stat_bias_pct']:.2f}% of in-sample alpha")
    print(
        f"Egger funnel asymmetry:        intercept = {res['egger_intercept']:+.3f} "
        f"| t = {res['t_egger']:.2f} | p = {res['p_egger']:.4f}"
    )


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Traditional corporate finance and investments textbooks teach the CAPM and empirical factor models as stable, immutable physical laws: once a risk premium is uncovered, it is presumed to represent permanent compensation for non-diversifiable systematic risk. This consensus breaks down completely under the weight of empirical replication research:

  • Premia are non-stationary: When academic papers reveal a predictable mispricing, institutional capital flows into long-short anomaly portfolios, directly depressing future expected returns. The act of publishing an anomaly alters the data-generating process.
  • In-sample Sharpe ratios cannot be annualized blindly: Backtested Sharpe ratios published in top journals overstate achievable out-of-sample performance by a factor of two to three due to the joint impact of publication truncation and competitive arbitrage crowding.

Common Misconceptions

  1. Anomaly alpha decay implies that the original researchers committed academic fraud or fabricated data.

    Post-publication decay occurs naturally through two benign mechanisms: standard statistical selection (conditioning on \(t \ge 2\) selects from the right tail of realization noise) and competitive arbitrage (smart capital trades against the published anomaly, eliminating the mispricing). Neither requires dishonest manipulation.

  2. If an anomaly's alpha drops by 50% post-publication, it has become worthless to quantitative investors.

    A factor retaining 40% to 50% of its initial in-sample alpha out-of-sample often remains highly valuable in a multi-factor portfolio context. What matters is whether the net risk-adjusted return exceeds turnover-induced transaction costs and idiosyncratic carry risk.

  3. Publication bias can be eliminated simply by running out-of-sample tests on the next 3 years of post-publication market data.

    Three years of monthly data provides only \(T = 36\) observations. For an anomaly with an annualized volatility of 15%, the standard error over 3 years is \(\se = 15\% / \sqrt{3} \approx 8.66\%\). Statistical power to reject the null of zero alpha over 36 months is near zero; decades of out-of-sample data or global multi-country panels are required for definitive power.

Practice Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An empirical researcher backtests an anomaly across 180 months. The annualized sample mean return is \(\bar{\alpha} = 4.50\%\) and the annualized standard deviation is \(\sigma = 18.0\%\). Calculate the estimated \(t\)-statistic and determine whether it clears the conventional 5% two-sided hurdle (\(|t| \ge 1.96\)).

Solution

The sample size in years is \(T = 180 / 12 = 15\) years. The standard error of the annualized alpha estimate is:

\[ \se(\bar{\alpha}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T}} = \frac{18.0\%}{\sqrt{15}} = \frac{18.0\%}{3.873} \approx 4.648\% \]

The resulting \(t\)-statistic is:

\[ t = \frac{\bar{\alpha}}{\se(\bar{\alpha})} = \frac{4.50\%}{4.648\%} \approx 0.968 \]

Since \(|t| = 0.968 < 1.96\), the candidate anomaly fails to clear the conventional 5% significance hurdle and would be consigned to the file drawer.

A published anomaly generates an in-sample annualized return of \(\bar{R}_{\text{in}} = 8.0\%\), an out-of-sample (pre-publication) return of \(\bar{R}_{\text{oos}} = 6.0\%\), and a post-publication return of \(\bar{R}_{\text{post}} = 3.6\%\). Calculate the total decay percentage, the statistical bias percentage, and the arbitrage decay percentage.

Solution

Following Definition 2.2:

  1. Total post-publication decay: \[ \Delta_{\text{total}} = 1 - \frac{\bar{R}_{\text{post}}}{\bar{R}_{\text{in}}} = 1 - \frac{3.6}{8.0} = 1 - 0.45 = 55.0\% \]
  2. Statistical bias decay: \[ \Delta_{\text{stat}} = 1 - \frac{\bar{R}_{\text{oos}}}{\bar{R}_{\text{in}}} = 1 - \frac{6.0}{8.0} = 1 - 0.75 = 25.0\% \]
  3. Arbitrage decay: \[ \Delta_{\text{arb}} = \Delta_{\text{total}} - \Delta_{\text{stat}} = 55.0\% - 25.0\% = 30.0\% \]

These values closely match the empirical averages reported in McLean & Pontiff (2016).

An Egger regression on a set of 50 published accounting anomalies yields an intercept of \(\hat{\beta}_0 = 1.45\) with a standard error of \(\se(\hat{\beta}_0) = 0.35\). Compute the \(t\)-statistic and state whether funnel asymmetry is statistically significant at the 1% level.

Solution

The \(t\)-statistic for the intercept under the null \(H_0: \beta_0 = 0\) is:

\[ t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_0}{\se(\hat{\beta}_0)} = \frac{1.45}{0.35} \approx 4.14 \]

With \(df = 50 - 2 = 48\), the two-tailed critical value for \(\alpha = 0.01\) is approximately \(t_{0.005, 48} \approx 2.68\). Since \(4.14 > 2.68\) (\(p < 0.0002\)), we decisively reject the null of no publication bias, confirming severe funnel asymmetry.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for any standard normal variable \(Z \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) and truncation cutoff \(c\), the conditional expectation satisfies \(\E[Z \mid Z \ge c] = \frac{\phi(c)}{1 - \Phi(c)}\), where \(\phi(z)\) is the standard normal PDF and \(\Phi(z)\) is the CDF.

Solution

By definition of conditional expectation for a continuous random variable:

\[ \E[Z \mid Z \ge c] = \frac{\int_c^\infty z \phi(z) \, dz}{\Prob(Z \ge c)} = \frac{1}{1 - \Phi(c)} \int_c^\infty z \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2} \, dz \]

Notice that \(\frac{d}{dz} \left(-e^{-z^2/2}\right) = z e^{-z^2/2}\). Therefore:

\[ \int_c^\infty z e^{-z^2/2} \, dz = \left[ -e^{-z^2/2} \right]_c^\infty = 0 - (-e^{-c^2/2}) = e^{-c^2/2} \]

Dividing by \(\sqrt{2\pi}\), we obtain \(\int_c^\infty z \phi(z) \, dz = \phi(c)\). Substituting this back into the conditional expectation yields:

\[ \E[Z \mid Z \ge c] = \frac{\phi(c)}{1 - \Phi(c)} \equiv \lambda(c) \]

This completes the proof. The ratio \(\lambda(c)\) is known as the inverse Mills ratio.

Prove that the inverse Mills ratio \(\lambda(c) = \frac{\phi(c)}{1 - \Phi(c)}\) is strictly increasing in \(c\) for all \(c \in \R\).

Solution

Differentiating \(\lambda(c)\) with respect to \(c\):

\[ \lambda'(c) = \frac{\phi'(c)(1 - \Phi(c)) - \phi(c)(-\phi(c))}{(1 - \Phi(c))^2} \]

Using the identity \(\phi'(c) = -c \phi(c)\):

\[ \lambda'(c) = \frac{-c \phi(c)(1 - \Phi(c)) + \phi(c)^2}{(1 - \Phi(c))^2} = \lambda(c) \left[\lambda(c) - c\right] \]

Recall that \(\lambda(c) = \E[Z \mid Z \ge c]\). Since the conditional distribution of \(Z\) given \(Z \ge c\) is supported on \([c, \infty)\) with strictly positive probability on \((c, \infty)\), the expectation must be strictly greater than the lower bound:

\[ \E[Z \mid Z \ge c] > c \implies \lambda(c) - c > 0 \]

Since \(\phi(c) > 0\) and \(1 - \Phi(c) > 0\), \(\lambda(c) > 0\). Therefore \(\lambda'(c) = \lambda(c)(\lambda(c) - c) > 0\) for all \(c \in \R\). This proves that truncation bias strictly increases as journals adopt more stringent publication cutoffs.

Derive the conditional variance \(\Var(Z \mid Z \ge c)\) for \(Z \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) and show that truncation reduces variance relative to the unconstrained standard normal distribution.

Solution

Using \(\Var(Z \mid Z \ge c) = \E[Z^2 \mid Z \ge c] - (\E[Z \mid Z \ge c])^2\), we first compute \(\E[Z^2 \mid Z \ge c]\):

\[ \int_c^\infty z^2 \phi(z) \, dz = \int_c^\infty z (z \phi(z)) \, dz \]

Integrating by parts with \(u = z\) and \(dv = z \phi(z) \, dz = -d\phi(z)\):

\[ [-z \phi(z)]_c^\infty + \int_c^\infty \phi(z) \, dz = c \phi(c) + (1 - \Phi(c)) \]

Dividing by \(1 - \Phi(c)\) gives:

\[ \E[Z^2 \mid Z \ge c] = 1 + c \, \lambda(c) \]

Subtracting \(\lambda(c)^2\), we obtain:

\[ \Var(Z \mid Z \ge c) = 1 + c \, \lambda(c) - \lambda(c)^2 = 1 - \lambda(c)[\lambda(c) - c] \]

As established in Problem P2, \(\lambda(c)[\lambda(c) - c] = \lambda'(c) > 0\). Therefore:

\[ \Var(Z \mid Z \ge c) = 1 - \lambda'(c) < 1 \]

Thus, conditioning on exceeding a hurdle strictly contracts the conditional dispersion of the test statistic.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Suppose an asset manager reviews a proposed factor backtest that achieved an in-sample Sharpe ratio of 0.90 over a 15-year backtest. Based on McLean & Pontiff's decomposition, estimate the expected post-publication out-of-sample Sharpe ratio if the factor exhibits average statistical bias (26%) and average arbitrage decay (32%).

Solution

Total expected decay is \(\Delta_{\text{total}} = 26\% + 32\% = 58\%\). The expected retained proportion of return spread is:

\[ 1 - \Delta_{\text{total}} = 1 - 0.58 = 0.42 \]

Assuming factor return volatility remains approximately constant out-of-sample, the expected post-publication Sharpe ratio is:

\[ \SR_{\text{oos}} = 0.42 \times \SR_{\text{in}} = 0.42 \times 0.90 = 0.378 \approx 0.38 \]

An institutional allocator should haircut the backtested Sharpe ratio from 0.90 down to ~0.38 before factoring in transaction costs and execution slippage.

Why does Egger regression require regressing \(t_i\) on \(1 / \se_i\) rather than directly regressing point estimates \(\hat{\alpha}_i\) on \(\se_i\)?

Solution

Under the null hypothesis of no publication bias, the relationship between effect size and standard error is \(\hat{\alpha}_i = \alpha_0 + \varepsilon_i\), where \(\Var(\varepsilon_i) = \se_i^2\). This specification exhibits severe heteroskedasticity because studies with large standard errors have much higher residual variance.

Dividing both sides by \(\se_i\) yields weighted least squares (WLS):

\[ \frac{\hat{\alpha}_i}{\se_i} = \frac{\alpha_0}{\se_i} + \frac{\varepsilon_i}{\se_i} \implies t_i = \alpha_0 \left(\frac{1}{\se_i}\right) + u_i \]

where \(\Var(u_i) = \Var(\varepsilon_i / \se_i) = 1\) is homoskedastic. Adding an intercept \(\beta_0\) tests whether small-sample studies deviate from this proportional relationship due to publication truncation.

Chen & Zimmermann (2020) examine publication bias bounds across 200+ anomalies. If an anomaly's in-sample return was 0.60% per month and its post-publication return was 0.45% per month, determine whether the observed decay is smaller or larger than the literature benchmark.

Solution

The observed decay rate is:

\[ \Delta = 1 - \frac{0.45}{0.60} = 1 - 0.75 = 25.0\% \]

The McLean & Pontiff benchmark for total post-publication decay is approximately 58%. A decay of only 25% indicates that this specific anomaly suffered substantially less attenuation than average, retaining 75% of its original predictive alpha (compared to the typical 42%).

Tier 4 · Research

How does the presence of short-sale constraints and trading frictions affect the McLean–Pontiff arbitrage decay channel across large-cap versus micro-cap stocks?

Solution

Arbitrage capital flows preferentially into liquid, low-friction assets where borrowing fees are low and capacity is high (large-cap equities). Consequently, for large-cap anomalies, post-publication decay is dominated by the arbitrage channel (\(\Delta_{\text{arb}}\)), as institutional capital rapidly eliminates mispricings.

In contrast, microcaps face severe limits to arbitrage, including high borrow fees, bid-ask spreads, and scarce loan supply. In microcaps, post-publication decay is primarily driven by statistical mean reversion (\(\Delta_{\text{stat}}\)), while the true underlying mispricing persists because arbitrage capital cannot economically execute against it.

Explain how Andrews and Kasy (2019) identify the publication probability function \(p(t)\) non-parametrically without making strong functional form assumptions about the distribution of true latent effects.

Solution

Andrews and Kasy observe that in the absence of publication bias, the distribution of estimated \(z\)-scores conditional on the standard error \(\se\) would be continuous and smooth across the significance threshold \(t = 1.96\). Under publication selection, there is a discontinuous jump or cliff at \(t = 1.96\).

By comparing the density of observed estimates just to the right of the cutoff (\(t = 1.96^+\)) with the density just to the left (\(t = 1.96^-\)), the ratio of publication probabilities \(p(t \ge 1.96) / p(t < 1.96)\) can be identified non-parametrically, providing an exact weighting function to reweight published estimates back to the true unselected population.

Propose an empirical protocol for a quantitative hedge fund to stress-test backtested trading alphas against publication and selection bias prior to live capital deployment.

Solution

A rigorous institutional protocol incorporates four sequential validation hurdles:

  1. Strict Out-of-Sample Holdout: Enforce an immutable out-of-sample data freeze (at least 3 to 5 years of recent data) that the researcher cannot query during model specification or hyperparameter tuning.
  2. Multiple-Testing Haircut: Apply Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) or Holm step-down adjustments tracking the total number of trial models evaluated by the researcher.
  3. Standard 50% Alpha Haircut: Discount historical in-sample alpha by at least 50% to account for McLean & Pontiff style selection bias and market crowding.
  4. Transaction Cost & Liquidity Audit: Re-evaluate returns under conservative execution models, excluding microcaps (below NYSE 20th percentile) and imposing realistic short-borrow borrow rates.

  3. Under the null hypothesis of zero true alpha, what is the expected t-statistic of a candidate factor conditioned on clearing the conventional t >= 1.96 hurdle?

    By the properties of the truncated normal distribution (inverse Mills ratio), \(\E[Z \mid Z \ge 1.96] \approx 2.338\).

  4. What does a statistically significant positive intercept (\(\beta_0 > 0\)) in an Egger regression indicate?

    Options

    An Egger intercept significantly different from zero indicates that low-precision studies produce inflated \(t\)-statistics to clear publication hurdles.

  5. Why do published anomalies retain a substantial fraction (~42%) of their original alpha post-publication?

    Options

    Real-world frictions (short-borrow costs, liquidity limits, capacity constraints) keep arbitrage capital from completely bidding away mispricings.

  6. In a funnel plot without publication bias, what geometric shape should the scatter of point estimates resemble?

    Options

    As sample precision increases, sampling variance decreases, creating a symmetric inverted funnel narrowing at high precision.

  7. Why is evaluating out-of-sample performance over just 2 to 3 years statistically insufficient to disprove an anomaly?

    Options

    With annual vol of 15% to 20%, standard errors over 36 months are 5% to 8%, preventing statistically significant conclusions.

  8. What did Chen & Zimmermann (2020) find regarding anomaly post-publication decay?

    Options

    Chen & Zimmermann confirm significant attenuation while demonstrating that anomaly predictability does not vanish entirely.
