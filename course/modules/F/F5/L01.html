The Factor Zoo & Multiple Testing Haircuts

Over five decades of empirical asset pricing research, scholars and practitioners have identified hundreds of firm characteristics claiming to predict stock returns. Applying the conventional statistical hurdle of \(|t| \ge 1.96\) across hundreds of sequentially tested factors produces an unsustainable deluge of false discoveries. Understanding how data mining and multiple testing invalidate single-hypothesis inference, mastering family-wise error rate and false discovery rate corrections, and evaluating the Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) raised hurdle of \(t > 3.0\) are fundamental prerequisites for modern quantitative factor research.

1The Emergence of the Factor Zoo

In his 2011 American Finance Association Presidential Address, John Cochrane surveyed the state of cross-sectional asset pricing and delivered a stark assessment: "We used to have one model, the CAPM… Now we have a zoo of new factors."

Definition 1.1 · The Factor Zoo
The factor zoo refers to the vast, uncoordinated proliferation of published cross-sectional equity return predictors—exceeding 400 distinct firm characteristics, accounting ratios, market signals, and macro-state variables—discovered primarily through repeated historical backtesting on identical underlying CRSP and Compustat databases.

Following the formulation of the Fama–French three-factor model in 1993, the pace of factor discovery accelerated exponentially. As documented by Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016), financial economists published an average of 1 new factor per year in the 1970s, expanding to 5 per year in the 1990s, and exceeding 18 new factors per year by the 2010s. Over 315 factor papers in premier finance and economics journals documented statistically significant cross-sectional premia.

Remark 1.1 · The Shared Historical Data Trap

Unlike medical trials or experimental physics, where each scientific inquiry gathers fresh experimental observations, empirical finance relies on a single realized historical path of US public equity prices from 1926 to the present. When thousands of researchers fit regressions on the identical sample, standard sampling theory collapses. Independent statistical tests performed sequentially across the discipline constitute a massive joint hypothesis search.

2Why Classical Significance Fails Under Sequential Search

In classical frequentist statistics, a researcher tests a single hypothesis \(H_0: \alpha = 0\) against \(H_1: \alpha \ne 0\) using an estimated test statistic \(\hat{t} = \hat{\alpha} / \se(\hat{\alpha})\). Under standard regularity conditions, \(\hat{t} \dto \Normal(0, 1)\), and the two-sided significance hurdle corresponding to a size of \(\alpha_{\text{size}} = 0.05\) is \(|t| \ge 1.96\).

Theorem 1.1 · FWER Expansion Under Independent Searches
Suppose an empirical discipline evaluates \(M\) independent candidate factor anomalies, all of which are true null hypotheses (\(\alpha_m = 0\) for \(m = 1, \dots, M\)). If each factor is tested at nominal significance level \(\alpha_{\text{size}}\), the family-wise error rate (FWER)—the probability of making at least one false discovery—is: \[\begin{aligned} \FWER = 1 - (1 - \alpha_{\text{size}})^M. \end{aligned}\] As \(M \to \infty\), \(\FWER \to 1\).

Proof
Under the global null hypothesis that all \(M\) factors are uninformative noise, let \(D_m = \indic\{|t_m| \ge z_{1 - \alpha_{\text{size}}/2}\}\) be the indicator that candidate \(m\) is rejected. Because the tests are mutually independent, the probability that candidate \(m\) is not rejected is \(\Prob(D_m = 0) = 1 - \alpha_{\text{size}}\). The probability that zero discoveries are declared across the entire family is \(\prod_{m=1}^M \Prob(D_m = 0) = (1 - \alpha_{\text{size}})^M\). Therefore, the probability of at least one false rejection is \(\Prob\left(\sum_{m=1}^M D_m \ge 1\right) = 1 - (1 - \alpha_{\text{size}})^M\). For \(\alpha_{\text{size}} = 0.05\) and \(M = 200\), \(\FWER = 1 - (0.95)^{200} \approx 0.999965\).

Example 1.1 · False Discovery Proportion Under Moderate Prevalence

Suppose an investment committee tests \(M = 200\) trading strategies. Exactly \(M_0 = 180\) are pure noise (\(\alpha = 0\)) and \(M_1 = 20\) are true signals. The statistical tests have size \(\alpha_{\text{size}} = 0.05\) and power \(1 - \beta = 0.60\). The expected number of false discoveries is: \[\begin{aligned} \E[V] = M_0 \times \alpha_{\text{size}} = 180 \times 0.05 = 9.0. \end{aligned}\] The expected number of true discoveries is: \[\begin{aligned} \E[S] = M_1 \times (1 - \beta) = 20 \times 0.60 = 12.0. \end{aligned}\] The expected total number of discoveries is \(\E[R] = \E[V] + \E[S] = 9.0 + 12.0 = 21.0\). The false discovery rate (FDR) is: \[\begin{aligned} \FDR = \frac{\E[V]}{\E[R]} = \frac{9.0}{21.0} \approx 42.86\%. \end{aligned}\] Even with 60% power on genuine factors, over 42% of all statistically "significant" factors discovered at \(|t| \ge 1.96\) are false rejections.

3Multiple Testing Haircuts: FWER and FDR Control

To control false discoveries when screening multiple candidates, statisticians have established two principal error paradigms: Family-Wise Error Rate control and False Discovery Rate control.

Definition 1.2 · FWER Haircuts: Bonferroni and Holm
Let \(p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \dots \le p_{(M)}\) denote the ordered, two-sided \(p\)-values of \(M\) candidate factors:

  • Bonferroni Correction: Reject hypothesis \(H_{(m)}\) if \(p_{(m)} \le \frac{\alpha}{M}\). The equivalent two-sided critical t-statistic hurdle is \(z_{\text{bonf}} = \Phi^{-1}\left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{2M}\right)\).
  • Holm Step-Down Procedure: Let \(k\) be the smallest index such that \(p_{(k)} > \frac{\alpha}{M - k + 1}\). Reject all \(H_{(m)}\) for \(m < k\); retain all \(H_{(m)}\) for \(m \ge k\).

While Bonferroni and Holm guarantee \(\FWER \le \alpha\) under arbitrary dependence, FWER control becomes excessively conservative when \(M\) is large. For \(M = 400\) and \(\alpha = 0.05\), the Bonferroni hurdle requires \(p \le 0.000125\), corresponding to \(|t| \ge 3.84\), severely diminishing statistical power against true factors.

Definition 1.3 · Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate
The Benjamini–Hochberg (1995) procedure bounds the expected proportion of false discoveries at target level \(q \in (0, 1)\): \[\begin{aligned} k &= \max\left\{m : p_{(m)} \le \frac{m}{M} q\right\}. \end{aligned}\] If such a \(k\) exists, reject all hypotheses \(H_{(1)}, \dots, H_{(k)}\); otherwise, reject no hypotheses.

Under positive regression dependency on subsets (PRDS), the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure satisfies \(\E[\FDP] \le \frac{M_0}{M} q \le q\), providing substantially higher power than Bonferroni while bounding the proportion of spurious factors.

Theorem 1.2 · The Harvey–Liu–Zhu Raised Hurdle
Accounting for the historical trajectory of approximately 400 candidate factors tested over the past half-century and the correlation structure among asset characteristics, the minimum two-sided \(t\)-statistic hurdle required to claim an empirically undiscovered factor at a 5% false discovery rate is: \[\begin{aligned} |t| > 3.00, \end{aligned}\] corresponding to a two-sided \(p\)-value haircut of \(p < 0.0027\). For newly proposed factors evaluated today against expanded libraries exceeding \(M = 500\), the required hurdle rises to \(t > 3.40\).

Lab 1 · Multiple Testing Haircuts in the Factor Zoo

Simulating 200 candidate factor returns with 25 true positive-alpha factors and 175 noise factors will demonstrate that the unadjusted threshold (|t| >= 1.96) produces a high false discovery rate (>50%), whereas Bonferroni, Holm, Benjamini-Hochberg, and Harvey-Liu-Zhu (|t| >= 3.00) hurdles effectively eliminate false rejections.

"""Lab F5-L01-1: Multiple Testing Haircuts in the Factor Zoo.

Evaluates multiple-testing adjustments across candidate equity factors, comparing
unadjusted t-statistics with Bonferroni, Holm step-down, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR,
and Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) raised hurdles.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats


def evaluate_factor_hypotheses(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float | int]:
    """Simulate candidate factor returns and compute multiple-testing metrics."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_factors = 200
    n_true = 25
    n_noise = n_factors - n_true
    n_months = 240

    # True factors have positive annualized alpha: mean 3.6% (0.3% / mo), vol 12% (3.46% / mo)
    true_alpha_mo = 0.0030
    vol_mo = 0.0346

    true_returns = rng.normal(true_alpha_mo, vol_mo, size=(n_true, n_months))
    # Noise factors have zero alpha: mean 0.0%, vol 12%
    noise_returns = rng.normal(0.0, vol_mo, size=(n_noise, n_months))

    all_returns = np.vstack([true_returns, noise_returns])
    is_true = np.zeros(n_factors, dtype=bool)
    is_true[:n_true] = True

    # Compute sample mean, sample standard error, t-statistic, and two-sided p-value
    sample_means = np.mean(all_returns, axis=1)
    sample_stds = np.std(all_returns, axis=1, ddof=1)
    se = sample_stds / np.sqrt(n_months)
    t_stats = sample_means / se
    p_values = 2.0 * (1.0 - stats.norm.cdf(np.abs(t_stats)))

    # 1. Classical unadjusted test at alpha = 0.05 (|t| >= 1.96)
    crit_classical = 1.96
    disc_classical = np.abs(t_stats) >= crit_classical
    n_disc_classical = int(np.sum(disc_classical))
    n_false_classical = int(np.sum(disc_classical & ~is_true))
    fdr_classical = float(n_false_classical / max(n_disc_classical, 1))

    # 2. Bonferroni correction: FWER <= 0.05 -> alpha_bonf = 0.05 / M
    alpha_bonf = 0.05 / n_factors
    crit_bonf = float(stats.norm.ppf(1.0 - alpha_bonf / 2.0))
    disc_bonf = p_values <= alpha_bonf
    n_disc_bonf = int(np.sum(disc_bonf))
    n_false_bonf = int(np.sum(disc_bonf & ~is_true))

    # 3. Holm step-down procedure (FWER <= 0.05)
    sort_idx = np.argsort(p_values)
    sorted_p = p_values[sort_idx]
    holm_thresholds = 0.05 / (n_factors - np.arange(n_factors))
    holm_passed = sorted_p <= holm_thresholds
    first_fail = np.argmin(holm_passed) if not np.all(holm_passed) else n_factors
    disc_holm = np.zeros(n_factors, dtype=bool)
    if np.any(holm_passed):
        disc_holm[sort_idx[:first_fail]] = True
    n_disc_holm = int(np.sum(disc_holm))
    n_false_holm = int(np.sum(disc_holm & ~is_true))

    # 4. Benjamini-Hochberg (1995) FDR control at q = 0.05
    q_fdr = 0.05
    bh_thresholds = (np.arange(1, n_factors + 1) / n_factors) * q_fdr
    bh_passed = sorted_p <= bh_thresholds
    disc_bh = np.zeros(n_factors, dtype=bool)
    if np.any(bh_passed):
        max_idx = int(np.max(np.where(bh_passed)[0]))
        disc_bh[sort_idx[: max_idx + 1]] = True
    n_disc_bh = int(np.sum(disc_bh))
    n_false_bh = int(np.sum(disc_bh & ~is_true))
    fdr_bh = float(n_false_bh / max(n_disc_bh, 1))

    # 5. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) raised hurdle: |t| >= 3.00
    crit_hlz = 3.00
    disc_hlz = np.abs(t_stats) >= crit_hlz
    n_disc_hlz = int(np.sum(disc_hlz))
    n_false_hlz = int(np.sum(disc_hlz & ~is_true))
    fdr_hlz = float(n_false_hlz / max(n_disc_hlz, 1))

    return {
        "n_factors": n_factors,
        "n_true": n_true,
        "n_noise": n_noise,
        "n_disc_classical": n_disc_classical,
        "n_false_classical": n_false_classical,
        "fdr_classical": round(fdr_classical, 4),
        "crit_bonf": round(crit_bonf, 3),
        "n_disc_bonf": n_disc_bonf,
        "n_false_bonf": n_false_bonf,
        "n_disc_holm": n_disc_holm,
        "n_false_holm": n_false_holm,
        "n_disc_bh": n_disc_bh,
        "n_false_bh": n_false_bh,
        "fdr_bh": round(fdr_bh, 4),
        "n_disc_hlz": n_disc_hlz,
        "n_false_hlz": n_false_hlz,
        "fdr_hlz": round(fdr_hlz, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F5-L01-1 | Multiple Testing Haircuts in the Factor Zoo")
    print("---------------------------------------------------------")
    res = evaluate_factor_hypotheses(seed=42)
    print(f"Total candidate factors: {res['n_factors']} ({res['n_true']} true, {res['n_noise']} noise)")
    print(
        f"Classical unadjusted (|t| >= 1.96): {res['n_disc_classical']} discoveries "
        f"({res['n_false_classical']} false, FDR {res['fdr_classical']:.2%})"
    )
    print(
        f"Bonferroni FWER (|t| >= {res['crit_bonf']}): {res['n_disc_bonf']} discoveries ({res['n_false_bonf']} false)"
    )
    print(f"Holm step-down FWER:                {res['n_disc_holm']} discoveries ({res['n_false_holm']} false)")
    print(
        f"Benjamini-Hochberg FDR (q = 5%):    {res['n_disc_bh']} discoveries "
        f"({res['n_false_bh']} false, FDR {res['fdr_bh']:.2%})"
    )
    print(
        f"Harvey-Liu-Zhu hurdle (|t| >= 3.00): {res['n_disc_hlz']} discoveries "
        f"({res['n_false_hlz']} false, FDR {res['fdr_hlz']:.2%})"
    )


main()

4Where the Canon Breaks

The classical canon of cross-sectional asset pricing held that any factor producing a \(t\)-statistic above \(2.0\) in a Fama–MacBeth regression represented a genuine systematic risk factor or persistent market inefficiency. This canon breaks on multiple fronts:

  • Publication Incentives and the File-Drawer Problem: Standard multiple-testing corrections assume the econometrician knows the total number of tested hypotheses \(M\). In reality, uncounted thousands of non-significant backtests remain unpublished in academic and hedge fund file drawers. Therefore, any observable \(M\) is a lower bound, understating true multi-testing severity.
  • Cross-Sectional Correlation Among Factors: The 400+ published factors are not independent random variables; they are heavily clustered around several common themes (value, momentum, profitability, investment, distress). Simple Bonferroni adjustments overpenalize correlated tests, while unadjusted tests underpenalize them.
  • Time-Varying Hurdles: A factor discovered in 1980 faced far fewer preceding tests than a factor proposed in 2024. Imposing a fixed historical hurdle ignores the sequential expansion of the factor zoo.

5Misconceptions

  1. A t-statistic of 2.50 in a 30-year backtest confirms the discovery of a genuine anomaly.

    Why it is wrong: When evaluated in isolation, \(t = 2.50\) corresponds to \(p \approx 0.012\). However, in a discipline where hundreds of candidate variables have been searched across the same data sample, the expected maximum \(t\)-statistic from pure noise exceeds \(3.0\). Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) show that with 400 prior tests, \(t = 2.50\) yields an adjusted \(p\)-value well above conventional significance.

  2. Bonferroni correction is always the optimal multiple testing adjustment for equity factor research.

    Why it is wrong: Bonferroni controls the Family-Wise Error Rate (the probability of even one false discovery), which makes it intensely conservative under large \(M\). In financial economics and quantitative portfolio management, an investor seeks to maximize risk-adjusted Sharpe ratio and can tolerate a small fraction of false signals; hence, False Discovery Rate (FDR) control via Benjamini–Hochberg is far more economically appropriate.

  3. Raising the t-statistic hurdle to 3.0 solves all data snooping problems.

    Why it is wrong: Raising the hurdle reduces false positives but simultaneously increases false negatives (Type II error), rejecting genuine economic factors that have modest statistical power or short sample histories. Furthermore, if researchers p-hack aggressively enough by combining multiple filtering choices, even noise factors can cross \(t > 3.0\).

6Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Calculate the Bonferroni two-sided critical \(t\)-statistic hurdle for testing \(M = 500\) candidate factor anomalies at overall family-wise error rate \(\alpha = 0.05\), assuming asymptotic standard normality.

Solution

Under the Bonferroni correction, the per-hypothesis significance level is: \[\begin{aligned} \alpha_{\text{bonf}} = \frac{\alpha}{M} = \frac{0.05}{500} = 0.00010. \end{aligned}\] For a two-sided test under the standard normal distribution \(\Normal(0, 1)\), the critical cutoff is: \[\begin{aligned} z_{\text{crit}} = \Phi^{-1}\left(1 - \frac{\alpha_{\text{bonf}}}{2}\right) = \Phi^{-1}(1 - 0.00005) = \Phi^{-1}(0.99995) \approx 3.891. \end{aligned}\] Thus, a candidate factor must achieve \(|t| \ge 3.89\) to be declared statistically significant under Bonferroni FWER control across 500 tests.

Consider 5 candidate factors with ordered two-sided \(p\)-values: \(p_{(1)} = 0.004\), \(p_{(2)} = 0.009\), \(p_{(3)} = 0.015\), \(p_{(4)} = 0.025\), and \(p_{(5)} = 0.040\). Determine which hypotheses are rejected under the Holm step-down procedure at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Solution

The Holm step-down thresholds are \(\frac{\alpha}{M - m + 1}\) for \(m = 1, \dots, 5\):

  • \(m = 1\): Threshold is \(\frac{0.05}{5} = 0.0100\). Since \(p_{(1)} = 0.004 \le 0.0100\), reject \(H_{(1)}\).
  • \(m = 2\): Threshold is \(\frac{0.05}{4} = 0.0125\). Since \(p_{(2)} = 0.009 \le 0.0125\), reject \(H_{(2)}\).
  • \(m = 3\): Threshold is \(\frac{0.05}{3} \approx 0.0167\). Since \(p_{(3)} = 0.015 \le 0.0167\), reject \(H_{(3)}\).
  • \(m = 4\): Threshold is \(\frac{0.05}{2} = 0.0250\). Since \(p_{(4)} = 0.025 \le 0.0250\), reject \(H_{(4)}\).
  • \(m = 5\): Threshold is \(\frac{0.05}{1} = 0.0500\). Since \(p_{(5)} = 0.040 \le 0.0500\), reject \(H_{(5)}\).
All 5 hypotheses are rejected under the Holm step-down procedure.

Using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure with false discovery rate parameter \(q = 0.10\) on 4 tests with ordered \(p\)-values \([0.012, 0.024, 0.048, 0.095]\), identify the maximum index \(k\) and state the rejected hypotheses.

Solution

The Benjamini–Hochberg thresholds are \(\frac{m}{M} q = \frac{m}{4}(0.10)\):

  • \(m = 1\): Threshold \(\frac{1}{4}(0.10) = 0.025\). \(p_{(1)} = 0.012 \le 0.025\) (True).
  • \(m = 2\): Threshold \(\frac{2}{4}(0.10) = 0.050\). \(p_{(2)} = 0.024 \le 0.050\) (True).
  • \(m = 3\): Threshold \(\frac{3}{4}(0.10) = 0.075\). \(p_{(3)} = 0.048 \le 0.075\) (True).
  • \(m = 4\): Threshold \(\frac{4}{4}(0.10) = 0.100\). \(p_{(4)} = 0.095 \le 0.100\) (True).
The largest index satisfying \(p_{(m)} \le \frac{m}{M} q\) is \(k = 4\). Therefore, all 4 hypotheses are rejected at \(\FDR \le 0.10\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that the Holm step-down procedure uniformly dominates the standard Bonferroni procedure in statistical power while strictly guaranteeing \(\FWER \le \alpha\).

Solution

Let \(H_1, \dots, H_M\) be hypotheses with ordered \(p\)-values \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(M)}\). The Bonferroni procedure uses a constant threshold \(\tau_{\text{bonf}} = \alpha / M\). The Holm procedure uses threshold \(\tau_{\text{holm}}(m) = \frac{\alpha}{M - m + 1}\). For any step \(m \ge 1\), \(M - m + 1 \le M\), so: \[\begin{aligned} \tau_{\text{holm}}(m) = \frac{\alpha}{M - m + 1} \ge \frac{\alpha}{M} = \tau_{\text{bonf}}. \end{aligned}\] For \(m = 1\), the threshold equals Bonferroni (\(\alpha / M\)). For all subsequent steps \(m > 1\), the Holm threshold is strictly higher: \(\frac{\alpha}{M - m + 1} > \frac{\alpha}{M}\). Therefore, every hypothesis rejected by Bonferroni is also rejected by Holm, and any hypothesis where \(p_{(m)} \in (\frac{\alpha}{M}, \frac{\alpha}{M - m + 1}]\) is rejected by Holm but missed by Bonferroni. To establish FWER control, let \(I_0 \subseteq \{1, \dots, M\}\) be the set of true null hypotheses with cardinality \(M_0 \le M\). Let \(k^*\) be the first index in the ordered list corresponding to a true null hypothesis. At step \(k^*\), at most \(M - M_0\) false nulls have been rejected, so \(k^* \le M - M_0 + 1\), which implies \(M - k^* + 1 \ge M_0\). Thus: \[\begin{aligned} \Prob(\text{False Rejection}) &\le \Prob\left(p_{(k^*)} \le \frac{\alpha}{M - k^* + 1}\right) \le \Prob\left(\min_{j \in I_0} p_j \le \frac{\alpha}{M_0}\right) \le \sum_{j \in I_0} \Prob\left(p_j \le \frac{\alpha}{M_0}\right) = M_0 \frac{\alpha}{M_0} = \alpha. \end{aligned}\] Hence, Holm strictly controls \(\FWER \le \alpha\) while providing strictly greater statistical power than Bonferroni.

Prove that under independent test statistics, the expected maximum t-statistic among \(M\) true null factors scales asymptotically as \(\E[\max_{1 \le m \le M} t_m] \sim \sqrt{2 \ln M}\).

Solution

Let \(t_1, \dots, t_M \iid \Normal(0, 1)\). By standard extreme value theory for Gaussian sequences, the cumulative distribution of \(M_M = \max_{1 \le m \le M} t_m\) satisfies: \[\begin{aligned} \Prob(M_M \le x) = [\Phi(x)]^M. \end{aligned}\] Using the Mill's ratio bound for the standard normal tail: \[\begin{aligned} 1 - \Phi(x) = \frac{\phi(x)}{x}\left(1 - O(x^{-2})\right) = \frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{x \sqrt{2\pi}}(1 - O(x^{-2})). \end{aligned}\] Setting \(1 - \Phi(b_M) = \frac{1}{M}\) yields: \[\begin{aligned} \ln M \approx \frac{b_M^2}{2} + \ln b_M + \frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi) \implies b_M = \sqrt{2 \ln M} - \frac{\ln(4\pi \ln M)}{2\sqrt{2\ln M}} + o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ln M}}\right). \end{aligned}\] The normalized maximum \(a_M(M_M - b_M)\) where \(a_M = \sqrt{2 \ln M}\) converges weakly to the Gumbel distribution \(\Lambda(x) = \exp(-e^{-x})\), whose mean is the Euler–Mascheroni constant \(\gamma \approx 0.5772\). Therefore: \[\begin{aligned} \E[M_M] = b_M + \frac{\gamma}{a_M} + o(a_M^{-1}) = \sqrt{2 \ln M} + O\left(\frac{\ln \ln M}{\sqrt{\ln M}}\right). \end{aligned}\] For \(M = 400\), \(\sqrt{2 \ln 400} = \sqrt{2 \times 5.9915} = \sqrt{11.983} \approx 3.46\). Thus, merely testing 400 noise factors yields an expected maximum t-statistic exceeding 3.0.

Let \(p \sim U(0, 1)\) under the null hypothesis. Derive the distribution of the Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value (q-value) for a single hypothesis test evaluated against \(M\) independent nulls.

Solution

For a given hypothesis with raw \(p\)-value \(p_{(m)}\), the Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted \(p\)-value (or minimal FDR threshold \(q_{(m)}\)) is defined recursively as: \[\begin{aligned} q_{(m)} = \min_{j \ge m} \left\{ \frac{M}{j} p_{(j)} \right\}. \end{aligned}\] When all \(M\) hypotheses are null, the ordered \(p\)-values \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(M)}\) are order statistics from an independent Uniform\((0, 1)\) distribution. The marginal distribution of the \(j\)-th order statistic is \(\text{Beta}(j, M - j + 1)\), with expected value \(\E[p_{(j)}] = \frac{j}{M + 1}\). Consequently: \[\begin{aligned} \E\left[\frac{M}{j} p_{(j)}\right] = \frac{M}{j} \frac{j}{M + 1} = \frac{M}{M + 1} \approx 1. \end{aligned}\] Thus, under the global null, the adjusted \(q\)-values are concentrated near 1, guaranteeing that the probability of declaring a false discovery at any nominal FDR \(q < 0.10\) remains bounded by \(q\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

In a library of \(M = 315\) factors compiled from published papers between 1970 and 2012, Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) find that when evaluated against a hurdle of \(t > 3.0\), approximately what fraction of published factors fail to clear the raised significance threshold?

Solution

Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016, Table 2 and Table 4) document that approximately 50% of published empirical factors fail to satisfy a multiple-testing hurdle of \(t > 3.00\). While virtually all published factors cleared the unadjusted threshold of \(t \ge 1.96\) at publication, roughly half fail once adjusted for the sequential testing of hundreds of cross-sectional predictors.

Suppose a factor has an in-sample annualized Sharpe ratio of \(\SR = 0.50\) over \(T = 20\) years (\(240\) months) of monthly returns. Compute its \(t\)-statistic. Does it survive the classical hurdle? Does it survive the Harvey–Liu–Zhu hurdle?

Solution

The \(t\)-statistic for a sample Sharpe ratio over \(T\) years is: \[\begin{aligned} t = \SR \times \sqrt{T} = 0.50 \times \sqrt{20} = 0.50 \times 4.4721 \approx 2.236. \end{aligned}\] Under the classical hurdle: \(|t| = 2.24 \ge 1.96\), so the factor survives and is declared statistically significant. Under the Harvey–Liu–Zhu hurdle: \(|t| = 2.24 < 3.00\), so the factor fails to clear the hurdle and is classified as potentially spurious data snooping.

How does the correlation among candidate factors affect the effective number of tests \(M_{\text{eff}}\) relative to the nominal count \(M\)? If 100 factors have pairwise correlation \(\rho = 0.80\), explain qualitatively whether the hurdle should be higher or lower than for independent tests.

Solution

When candidate factors are positively correlated, their test statistics co-vary and contain redundant information. Consequently, the effective number of independent hypothesis tests \(M_{\text{eff}}\) is strictly less than the nominal count \(M\) (\(M_{\text{eff}} \ll M\)). Under high correlation (\(\rho = 0.80\)), the independent information content corresponds to only a few latent principal factors. Therefore, the required multiple-testing hurdle for correlated factors is lower than the Bonferroni hurdle for independent factors, though still substantially higher than the classical 1.96 cutoff.

Tier 4 · Research

Critically evaluate the argument by Chen (2025) in "Do t-Statistic Hurdles Need to Be Raised?" that raising hurdles to \(t > 3.0\) imposes an excessive welfare loss on investors by rejecting profitable true factors.

Solution

Chen (2025) demonstrates that raising significance hurdles involves an asymmetric trade-off: while it suppresses false discoveries (Type I errors), it severely increases missed discoveries (Type II errors). In an investment setting, missing a genuine factor with a true Sharpe ratio of 0.35 that produces \(t \approx 2.4\) imposes a real opportunity cost on portfolio expected returns. If the cost of including a zero-alpha factor is merely holding an uncompensated variance risk, while the cost of omitting a true factor is permanent alpha forfeiture, the Bayesian expected utility-maximizing threshold may be closer to \(t \approx 2.2\) rather than \(t = 3.0\), depending on investor risk aversion and diversification capacity.

Design an empirical protocol to test whether a newly discovered corporate governance factor adds genuine pricing power to an existing zoo of 200 published factors without committing data snooping bias.

Solution

A rigorous protocol comprises four steps: 1. Pre-registration / Protocol Freeze: Freeze the characteristic definition, rebalancing rules, and breakpoint screens before executing tests. 2. Spanning Regression Against Multi-Factor Benchmark: Regress the candidate factor returns on canonical models (FF5, \(q\)-factor, and Momentum) to obtain residual alpha \(\alpha_{\text{orth}}\). 3. High-Dimensional Double-Selection Test: Apply the Feng, Giglio & Xiu (2020) double-selection estimator against all 200 existing control factors to select relevant controls and compute asymptotically valid standard errors. 4. Out-of-Sample / International Validation: Test the frozen specification on out-of-sample time periods (post-2015) and independent international equity markets (Europe, Japan, Asia-Pacific) using hierarchical Bayesian shrinkage.

Why does evaluating factor anomalies using tradeable factor portfolios (e.g. long-short quintiles) introduce different multiple-testing challenges compared to Fama–MacBeth cross-sectional firm-level regressions?

Solution

Tradeable factor portfolios require explicit sorting choices (number of quantiles, weighting scheme, breakpoint exchange universe, rebalancing frequency, holding period), multiplying the degrees of researcher freedom by a factor of 10 to 50 for a single characteristic. In contrast, Fama–MacBeth regressions use continuous characteristics across the entire firm panel. However, Fama–MacBeth regressions are sensitive to outliers and leverage linear functional form assumptions, whereas portfolio sorts are non-parametric but heavily influenced by microcap pricing in the extreme buckets. Consequently, portfolio sorting creates a vastly expanded "shadow zoo" of specification variants.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What term did John Cochrane coin in his 2011 AFA Presidential Address to describe the proliferation of cross-sectional return predictors?

    Options

    Cochrane (2011) coined "the zoo of new factors" to characterize the uncoordinated publication of hundreds of empirical characteristics.

  2. Why does testing 200 independent noise factors at \(\alpha = 0.05\) guarantee false discoveries?

    Options

    Under 200 independent tests, the probability of at least one false positive is \(1 - (1 - 0.05)^{200} \approx 0.999965\).

  3. What raised two-sided t-statistic hurdle do Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) propose for declaring a new factor discovery?

    Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) propose raising the hurdle to \(|t| \ge 3.00\) (or \(p < 0.0027\)).

  4. How does Bonferroni correction adjust the nominal significance level \(\alpha\) across \(M\) tests?

    Options

    Bonferroni divides the family error rate equally across hypotheses: \(\alpha_{\text{bonf}} = \alpha / M\).

  5. What error rate does the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure control?

    Options

    Benjamini–Hochberg controls the False Discovery Rate \(\E[V / \max(R, 1)]\).

  6. Why is the Holm step-down procedure strictly preferred over the standard Bonferroni correction?

    Options

    Holm uses step-down thresholds \(\alpha / (M - m + 1) \ge \alpha / M\), rejecting all Bonferroni rejections plus additional borderlines.

  7. What did Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) find when applying a t > 3.0 hurdle to 315 published factors?

    Options

    HLZ (2016) document that roughly 50% of published factors fail once multiple testing haircuts are enforced.

  8. Why is historical financial market data uniquely vulnerable to data-snooping bias?

    Options

    Financial researchers repeatedly mine the same CRSP/Compustat dataset, turning uncoordinated research into a massive collective search.
