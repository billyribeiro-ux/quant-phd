Econometric Return Unsmoothing

Institutional allocators frequently cite low reported volatilities and high Sharpe ratios to justify heavy capital allocations to private equity, real estate, and hedge funds. However, when returns are smoothed by appraisal accounting and illiquidity, these reported metrics are econometric artifacts that mask catastrophic tail risk, understate true asset volatility by 30% to 50%, and artificially depress measured beta exposures to zero. Econometric return unsmoothing provides the quantitative toolkit to invert appraisal lag processes, deconvolve true underlying economic returns from reported accounting marks, and reconstruct unbiased factor exposures and risk-adjusted performance measures. Without unsmoothing, risk budgeting algorithms misallocate capital, risk parity portfolios overleverage illiquid holdings, and enterprise stress testing remains dangerously blind.

Reported track records of illiquid funds are distorted by appraisal lags and discretionary mark-to-model smoothing. To assess true investment skill, calibrate factor exposures, and prevent catastrophic risk misallocation, financial econometricians must invert the smoothing filter. In this lesson, we study classical Geltner AR(1) inversion, derive the Getmansky, Lo & Makarov (2004) constrained maximum likelihood estimator on Toeplitz covariance matrices, reconstruct true latent volatility and Sharpe ratios, and examine modern state-space extensions.

1. The Econometrics of Unsmoothing

In Lesson 3, we established that illiquidity and appraisal accounting transform latent economic returns \(R_t^*\) into smoothed reported returns \(R_t\) through an autoregressive or moving-average filter. Return unsmoothing is the inverse econometric operation.

Definition 4.1 (Return Unsmoothing) Return unsmoothing is the econometric process of inverting the structural lag filter in reported asset returns to deconvolve the unobserved, true latent economic return series \(R_t^*\), recover unbiased second moments and risk factor loadings, and eliminate artificial volatility suppression.

Return unsmoothing is required across institutional risk management for three reasons:

  1. True Volatility Recovery: By Theorem 3.2, reported volatility satisfies \(\sigma_R = \sigma^* \sqrt{\xi}\), where \(\xi = \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j^2 \le 1.0\). Unsmoothing recovers \(\sigma^* = \sigma_R / \sqrt{\xi}\), exposing true unhedged dispersion.
  2. True Factor Beta Recovery: A standard contemporaneous regression of reported returns on market factors yields \(\hat{\beta}_0 = \theta_0 \beta^*\). Unsmoothing restores the true underlying beta \(\beta^* = \sum_{j=0}^k \beta_j\).
  3. Covariance and Portfolio Optimization: Smoothed assets exhibit artificially low contemporaneous correlations with public equities and Treasuries. Plugging smoothed covariances into mean-variance optimizers produces massive over-allocations to illiquid assets. Unsmoothing restores genuine economic correlations.

2. First-Generation Unsmoothing: Geltner AR(1) Inversion

The earliest systematic approach to unsmoothing was developed by Geltner (1991, 1993) in commercial real estate appraisal. Consider the first-order partial adjustment process where the reported return \(R_t\) is a convex combination of current latent economic return \(R_t^*\) and the prior reported return \(R_{t-1}\):

\[ R_t = \theta_0 R_t^* + (1 - \theta_0) R_{t-1}, \quad 0 < \theta_0 \le 1 \]

Here \(\theta_0\) represents the speed of valuation adjustment. Inverting this relation yields the deterministic Geltner AR(1) unsmoothing formula:

\[ R_t^* = \frac{R_t - (1 - \theta_0) R_{t-1}}{\theta_0} \]

Econometric Pathology of Geltner AR(1) Inversion

While intuitive, direct AR(1) inversion suffers from severe structural flaws in real-world finance:

  • Denominator Explosion: As \(\theta_0 \to 0\) (extreme illiquidity or slow appraisals), the scaling factor \(1/\theta_0\) explodes, amplifying sample estimation noise exponentially.
  • Artificial Negative Autocorrelation: If \(\theta_0\) is misestimated even slightly, the resulting series \(R_t^*\) exhibits spurious negative first-order serial correlation (\(\rho_1 < 0\)), introducing non-existent mean reversion.
  • Measurement Error Magnification: Any reporting error \(\epsilon_t\) in \(R_t\) is amplified by \(1/\theta_0\) in \(R_t^*\), corrupting higher moments.

3. The Getmansky, Lo & Makarov (2004) Moving-Average Likelihood

To overcome the fragility of AR(1) inversion, Getmansky, Lo & Makarov (2004) formulated a structural moving-average framework estimated via constrained maximum likelihood.

Recall the \(k\)-th order GLM specification:

\[ R_t = \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j R_{t-j}^*, \quad \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j = 1, \quad \theta_j \ge 0, \quad \forall j \in \{0, \dots, k\} \]

where latent returns are independently and identically distributed: \(R_t^* \sim \text{i.i.d.} \, \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^{*2})\).

Autocovariance Structure of the MA(\(k\)) Process

Let \(\gamma_s = \Cov(R_t, R_{t-s})\) denote the autocovariance of reported returns at lag \(s\). For \(s \in \{0, 1, \dots, k\}\):

\[ \gamma_s = \sigma^{*2} \sum_{j=0}^{k-s} \theta_j \theta_{j+s}, \quad \text{and} \quad \gamma_s = 0 \quad \forall s > k \]

For an MA(2) model (\(k=2\)), the non-zero autocovariances are explicitly:

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma_0 &= \sigma^{*2} \left( \theta_0^2 + \theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2 \right) = \xi \sigma^{*2} \\ \gamma_1 &= \sigma^{*2} \left( \theta_0 \theta_1 + \theta_1 \theta_2 \right) \\ \gamma_2 &= \sigma^{*2} \left( \theta_0 \theta_2 \right) \end{aligned} \]

Toeplitz Covariance Matrix and Gaussian Likelihood

Given a sample of \(T\) demeaned reported returns \(\mathbf{y} = [R_1 - \bar{R}, R_2 - \bar{R}, \dots, R_T - \bar{R}]^\top\), the \(T \times T\) covariance matrix \(\mathbf{\Gamma}\) is symmetric and Toeplitz:

\[ \mathbf{\Gamma} = \begin{bmatrix} \gamma_0 & \gamma_1 & \gamma_2 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \gamma_1 & \gamma_0 & \gamma_1 & \gamma_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \gamma_2 & \gamma_1 & \gamma_0 & \gamma_1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & \gamma_2 & \gamma_1 & \gamma_0 & \gamma_1 \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & \gamma_2 & \gamma_1 & \gamma_0 \end{bmatrix}_{T \times T} \]

Assuming Gaussian innovations, the exact log-likelihood function of the reported return vector \(\mathbf{y}\) is:

\[ \ln \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\theta}, \sigma^*; \mathbf{y}) = -\frac{T}{2} \ln(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2} \ln \det(\mathbf{\Gamma}) - \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{y}^\top \mathbf{\Gamma}^{-1} \mathbf{y} \]

Theorem 4.1 (Constrained Maximum Likelihood Estimation of GLM Weights) The parameters \(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}} = [\hat{\theta}_0, \dots, \hat{\theta}_k]^\top\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^*\) solve the constrained nonlinear program:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{\theta} \in \R^{k+1}, \, \sigma^* > 0} \frac{1}{2} \left[ \ln \det(\mathbf{\Gamma}(\mathbf{\theta}, \sigma^*)) + \mathbf{y}^\top \mathbf{\Gamma}(\mathbf{\theta}, \sigma^*)^{-1} \mathbf{y} \right] \]

subject to:

\[ \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \theta_j \ge 0 \quad \forall j \in \{0, \dots, k\} \]

Numerical Implementation via Cholesky Decomposition: Direct inversion of the \(T \times T\) matrix \(\mathbf{\Gamma}\) is numerically unstable. Instead, compute the lower-triangular Cholesky factor \(\mathbf{L}\) such that \(\mathbf{\Gamma} = \mathbf{L} \mathbf{L}^\top\). The log-determinant simplifies to:

\[ \ln \det(\mathbf{\Gamma}) = 2 \sum_{i=1}^T \ln L_{ii} \]

and the quadratic form evaluates via forward substitution \(\mathbf{L} \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y}\):

\[ \mathbf{y}^\top \mathbf{\Gamma}^{-1} \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{z}^\top \mathbf{z} = \sum_{i=1}^T z_i^2 \]

This reduces objective evaluation complexity from \(\mathcal{O}(T^3)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(T^2)\) and guarantees positive-definiteness throughout sequential quadratic programming (SLSQP).

4. Reconstructing True Volatility, Sharpe Ratios, and Factor Betas

Once the constrained MLE estimates \(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^*\) are obtained, all primary performance metrics are reconstructed.

1. The Illiquidity Concentration Index \(\xi\)

The parameter \(\xi = \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j^2\) serves as a direct metric of illiquidity and smoothing concentration:

  • If \(\xi = 1.0\), \(\theta_0 = 1\) and \(\theta_{j>0} = 0\): perfectly liquid, unsmoothed reporting.
  • If \(\xi \to 1/(k+1)\): maximum smoothing, equal weighting across all \(k+1\) lags.

2. Unbiased Volatility and Sharpe Ratio

The true latent annual standard deviation is directly recovered:

\[ \sigma_{\text{ann}}^* = \hat{\sigma}^* \sqrt{12} = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_R}{\sqrt{\hat{\xi}}} \sqrt{12} \]

and the true, unsmoothed economic Sharpe ratio is:

\[ SR^* = \frac{\bar{R} - R_f}{\sigma_{\text{ann}}^*} = SR_{\text{reported}} \cdot \sqrt{\hat{\xi}} \]

Because \(\sqrt{\hat{\xi}} < 1\), the unsmoothed Sharpe ratio is strictly lower than the reported Sharpe ratio.

3. Factor Beta Deconvolution

When an illiquid fund invests in systematic risk factors \(F_t\), the reported return reflects both contemporaneous and lagged factor moves:

\[ R_t = \alpha + \beta_0 F_t + \beta_1 F_{t-1} + \dots + \beta_k F_{t-k} + \epsilon_t \]

Following Dimson (1979) and Getmansky et al. (2004), the true total systematic beta is the sum of all contemporaneous and lagged regression coefficients:

\[ \beta_{\text{total}} = \sum_{j=0}^k \beta_j \]

Under pure structural smoothing with weights \(\theta_j\), the individual lagged coefficients satisfy \(\beta_j = \theta_j \beta^*\), so that \(\sum_{j=0}^k \beta_j = \beta^* \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j = \beta^*\). A risk manager who runs only a single-factor contemporaneous regression captures only \(\beta_0 = \theta_0 \beta^*\), missing \((1 - \theta_0)\) of the portfolio's systematic market exposure.

4. Modern State-Space Extensions

In recent literature, Couts, Welk & Zych (2024) extend the GLM framework using Kalman filtering and state-space models. While classical GLM assumes constant smoothing parameters \(\theta\), commercial real estate and private debt funds exhibit time-varying illiquidity: appraisal marks adjust more rapidly during severe liquidity crises (when transaction comps are forced) than during tranquil bull regimes. State-space models accommodate time-varying \(\theta_t\) and cross-sectional Bayesian shrinkage across peer fund categories.

Lab 4 · Constrained Maximum Likelihood Return Unsmoothing

Estimating GLM MA(2) parameters via constrained maximum likelihood on the Toeplitz covariance matrix will recover unbiased true economic volatility and deflate inflated reported Sharpe ratios.

"""Lab F7-L04-1: GLM (2004) Constrained Maximum Likelihood Return Unsmoothing.

Implements the Getmansky, Lo & Makarov (2004) econometric moving-average
return unsmoothing framework. Estimates smoothing coefficients theta_0, ..., theta_k
via constrained maximum likelihood, evaluates the illiquidity score xi, and recovers
unbiased true volatility and unsmoothed Sharpe ratios.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
import scipy.linalg
import scipy.optimize


def run_glm_unsmoothing(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Estimate GLM (2004) MA(2) smoothing parameters via constrained MLE."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_months = 180  # 15 years

    # 1. Ground truth parameters:
    # theta = [0.60, 0.25, 0.15], sum(theta) = 1.0
    theta_true = np.array([0.60, 0.25, 0.15])
    true_sigma_mo = 0.045
    true_mu_mo = 0.0075
    xi_true = float(np.sum(theta_true**2))

    # 2. Simulate true latent returns and smoothed reported returns
    r_star = rng.normal(true_mu_mo, true_sigma_mo, size=n_months + 2)
    r_obs = np.zeros(n_months)
    for t in range(n_months):
        r_obs[t] = theta_true[0] * r_star[t + 2] + theta_true[1] * r_star[t + 1] + theta_true[2] * r_star[t]

    # 3. Reported statistics
    sample_mean = float(np.mean(r_obs))
    sample_vol_obs = float(np.std(r_obs, ddof=1))
    demeaned = r_obs - sample_mean

    # 4. Constrained Maximum Likelihood Estimation of MA(2):
    # theta_0, theta_1, sigma (with theta_2 = 1 - theta_0 - theta_1 >= 0)
    def neg_log_lik(params: np.ndarray) -> float:
        t0, t1, sig = float(params[0]), float(params[1]), float(params[2])
        t2 = 1.0 - t0 - t1
        if t2 < -1e-7 or t0 < 0.0 or t1 < 0.0 or sig <= 1e-6:
            return 1e10
        t2 = max(0.0, t2)
        th = np.array([t0, t1, t2])

        # Autocovariances for MA(2)
        g0 = (sig**2) * float(np.sum(th**2))
        g1 = (sig**2) * float(th[0] * th[1] + th[1] * th[2])
        g2 = (sig**2) * float(th[0] * th[2])

        c = np.zeros(n_months)
        c[0], c[1], c[2] = g0, g1, g2
        try:
            cov_mat = scipy.linalg.toeplitz(c) + np.eye(n_months) * 1e-8
            chol_l = scipy.linalg.cholesky(cov_mat, lower=True)
            log_det = 2.0 * float(np.sum(np.log(np.diag(chol_l))))
            y_vec = scipy.linalg.solve_triangular(chol_l, demeaned, lower=True)
            quad_form = float(np.sum(y_vec**2))
            return float(0.5 * (float(n_months) * np.log(2.0 * np.pi) + log_det + quad_form))
        except Exception:
            return 1e10

    # Optimization with multi-start search
    best_res = None
    best_nll = 1e10
    bounds = [(0.05, 0.95), (0.00, 0.95), (1e-4, 1.0)]
    cons = ({"type": "ineq", "fun": lambda p: 1.0 - p[0] - p[1]},)

    for init_t0 in [0.75, 0.55, 0.40]:
        init_t1 = (1.0 - init_t0) * 0.5
        init_sig = sample_vol_obs / np.sqrt(init_t0**2 + init_t1**2 + (1.0 - init_t0 - init_t1) ** 2)
        res = scipy.optimize.minimize(
            neg_log_lik,
            np.array([init_t0, init_t1, init_sig]),
            bounds=bounds,
            constraints=cons,
            method="SLSQP",
        )
        if res.success and res.fun < best_nll:
            best_nll = float(res.fun)
            best_res = res

    assert best_res is not None
    theta_0_hat = float(best_res.x[0])
    theta_1_hat = float(best_res.x[1])
    theta_2_hat = max(0.0, 1.0 - theta_0_hat - theta_1_hat)
    sigma_star_hat = float(best_res.x[2])

    # 5. Illiquidity concentration index xi = sum theta_j^2
    xi_hat = float(theta_0_hat**2 + theta_1_hat**2 + theta_2_hat**2)

    # Annualized metrics
    vol_reported_ann = float(sample_vol_obs * np.sqrt(12.0) * 100.0)
    vol_true_ann = float(true_sigma_mo * np.sqrt(12.0) * 100.0)
    vol_unsmoothed_ann = float(sigma_star_hat * np.sqrt(12.0) * 100.0)

    sr_reported = float((sample_mean / sample_vol_obs) * np.sqrt(12.0))
    sr_unsmoothed = float((sample_mean / sigma_star_hat) * np.sqrt(12.0))

    return {
        "theta_0_true": round(float(theta_true[0]), 3),
        "theta_1_true": round(float(theta_true[1]), 3),
        "theta_2_true": round(float(theta_true[2]), 3),
        "theta_0_hat": round(theta_0_hat, 3),
        "theta_1_hat": round(theta_1_hat, 3),
        "theta_2_hat": round(theta_2_hat, 3),
        "xi_true": round(xi_true, 4),
        "xi_hat": round(xi_hat, 4),
        "vol_reported_ann": round(vol_reported_ann, 2),
        "vol_true_ann": round(vol_true_ann, 2),
        "vol_unsmoothed_ann": round(vol_unsmoothed_ann, 2),
        "sr_reported": round(sr_reported, 4),
        "sr_unsmoothed": round(sr_unsmoothed, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F7-L04-1 | GLM (2004) Constrained Maximum Likelihood Unsmoothing")
    print("--------------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = run_glm_unsmoothing(seed=42)
    print(
        f"True Smoothing Weights:      theta = [{res['theta_0_true']}, "
        f"{res['theta_1_true']}, {res['theta_2_true']}] (xi = {res['xi_true']:.4f})"
    )
    print(
        f"GLM Estimated Weights:       theta = [{res['theta_0_hat']}, "
        f"{res['theta_1_hat']}, {res['theta_2_hat']}] (xi = {res['xi_hat']:.4f})"
    )
    print(f"Reported Annualized Vol:     {res['vol_reported_ann']:.2f}% (Sharpe: {res['sr_reported']:.4f})")
    print(f"True Latent Volatility:      {res['vol_true_ann']:.2f}%")
    print(f"GLM Unsmoothed Volatility:   {res['vol_unsmoothed_ann']:.2f}% (Sharpe: {res['sr_unsmoothed']:.4f})")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Canon Break 4.1: Unconstrained AR Inversion Generates Ghost Volatility

Naive practitioners often estimate an unrestricted AR(p) model via OLS on reported returns: \(R_t = c + \phi_1 R_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p R_{t-p} + u_t\), and define unsmoothed returns as the fitted residuals plus mean: \(R_t^* = \bar{R} + u_t / (1 - \sum \phi_i)\). Because OLS does not impose the theoretical GLM constraints \(\theta_j \ge 0\) and \(\sum \theta_j = 1\), the inverted series frequently displays artificial variance spikes, negative autocovariances, and wild over-adjustments. Strict constrained MLE or Kalman filtering is mathematically required.

Canon Break 4.2: Unsmoothing Does Not Create Liquidity

An unsmoothed return series represents the hypothetical economic valuation if underlying assets were continuously marked to market. It does not mean the investor could have transacted at those prices! Liquidation in illiquid secondary markets entails massive market impact, bid-ask spreads, and lockups. Unsmoothing corrects risk accounting; it does not eliminate liquidity risk.

Misconceptions

  1. Misconception: Unsmoothing changes the fund's historical cumulative wealth and alters long-term compounded investor gains.

    Correction: False. Because \(\sum \theta_j = 1\), the expected return is invariant (\(\E[R_t] = \E[R_t^*] = \mu\)). Unsmoothing rearranges return timing across periods to restore true volatility; it neither creates nor destroys long-term capital.

  2. Misconception: A low contemporaneous adjustment speed \(\theta_0 < 0.50\) implies intentional fund manager fraud or deceptive reporting.

    Correction: False. In direct real estate, private equity, and private credit, assets trade infrequently. Even a completely honest valuation committee adhering to GAAP/IFRS must rely on periodic appraisals that lag market clearing prices.

  3. Misconception: Returns can be unsmoothed simply by multiplying reported periodic returns by a scalar inflation factor \(1/\sqrt{\xi}\).

    Correction: False. While standard deviation scales by \(1/\sqrt{\xi}\), periodic returns cannot be scaled by a scalar multiplier. Unsmoothing is a dynamic deconvolution problem that shifts return mass across adjacent quarters according to the Toeplitz covariance structure.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 4.1 (Geltner AR(1) Point Inversion) An illiquid direct real estate fund reports the following three consecutive monthly returns: \(R_{t-2} = +1.0\%\), \(R_{t-1} = +0.5\%\), and \(R_t = -2.0\%\). The appraiser's update parameter is estimated at \(\theta_0 = 0.50\). Calculate the unsmoothed economic returns \(R_{t-1}^*\) and \(R_t^*\) under Geltner AR(1) inversion.

Solution

Using the Geltner AR(1) formula \(R_s^* = [R_s - (1 - \theta_0) R_{s-1}] / \theta_0\):

  1. For period \(t-1\): \[ R_{t-1}^* = \frac{0.005 - (1 - 0.50)(0.010)}{0.50} = \frac{0.005 - 0.005}{0.50} = 0.000 = 0.0\% \]
  2. For period \(t\): \[ R_t^* = \frac{-0.020 - (1 - 0.50)(0.005)}{0.50} = \frac{-0.020 - 0.0025}{0.50} = \frac{-0.0225}{0.50} = -0.045 = -4.5\% \]

Notice that while the reported decline in month \(t\) was \(-2.0\%\), the true unsmoothed economic decline was \(-4.5\%\)—more than twice as severe.

Problem 4.2 (GLM Autocovariance Evaluation) A private credit fund follows a GLM MA(2) model with parameters \(\theta_0 = 0.50\), \(\theta_1 = 0.35\), and \(\theta_2 = 0.15\). The true monthly economic return standard deviation is \(\sigma^* = 3.0\%\). Compute the first three theoretical autocovariances \(\gamma_0, \gamma_1, \gamma_2\) and the first two autocorrelations \(\rho_1, \rho_2\) of reported monthly returns.

Solution

First calculate \(\sigma^{*2} = 0.03^2 = 0.0009\).

Now evaluate the moving-average autocovariance formulas:

\[ \begin{aligned} \xi &= \theta_0^2 + \theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2 = 0.50^2 + 0.35^2 + 0.15^2 = 0.2500 + 0.1225 + 0.0225 = 0.3950 \\ \gamma_0 &= \sigma^{*2} \xi = 0.0009 \times 0.3950 = 0.0003555 \quad (\sigma_R = \sqrt{\gamma_0} \approx 1.885\%) \\ \gamma_1 &= \sigma^{*2} (\theta_0 \theta_1 + \theta_1 \theta_2) = 0.0009 \times (0.50 \times 0.35 + 0.35 \times 0.15) \\ &= 0.0009 \times (0.1750 + 0.0525) = 0.0009 \times 0.2275 = 0.00020475 \\ \gamma_2 &= \sigma^{*2} (\theta_0 \theta_2) = 0.0009 \times (0.50 \times 0.15) = 0.0009 \times 0.0750 = 0.00006750 \end{aligned} \]

The resulting autocorrelations are:

\[ \rho_1 = \frac{\gamma_1}{\gamma_0} = \frac{0.2275}{0.3950} \approx 0.5759, \qquad \rho_2 = \frac{\gamma_2}{\gamma_0} = \frac{0.0750}{0.3950} \approx 0.1899 \]

Problem 4.3 (Unsmoothed Sharpe Ratio Calibration) An infrastructure fund reports an annualized mean excess return of \(9.0\%\) with reported annualized volatility of \(6.0\%\), yielding an apparent reported Sharpe ratio of \(SR = 1.50\). Econometric unsmoothing reveals \(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}} = [0.45, 0.35, 0.20]\). Calculate the illiquidity concentration index \(\hat{\xi}\), the true unsmoothed volatility \(\sigma^*\), and the true unsmoothed Sharpe ratio \(SR^*\).

Solution
  1. Illiquidity index: \[ \hat{\xi} = 0.45^2 + 0.35^2 + 0.20^2 = 0.2025 + 0.1225 + 0.0400 = 0.3650 \]
  2. Square root factor: \(\sqrt{\hat{\xi}} = \sqrt{0.3650} \approx 0.60415\)
  3. True unsmoothed annualized volatility: \[ \sigma^* = \frac{\sigma_R}{\sqrt{\hat{\xi}}} = \frac{6.0\%}{0.60415} \approx 9.93\% \]
  4. True unsmoothed Sharpe ratio: \[ SR^* = \frac{9.0\%}{9.93\%} \approx 0.906 \quad (\text{or } 1.50 \times 0.60415 = 0.906) \]

The reported Sharpe ratio of 1.50 was inflated by \((1.50 - 0.906)/0.906 = 65.5\%\) due to return smoothing.

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 4.4 (Minimum Variance Reduction Ratio Bound) Prove that for any GLM smoothing process of order \(k\) satisfying \(\sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j = 1\) with \(\theta_j \ge 0\), the variance reduction ratio satisfies the lower bound \(\xi \ge 1/(k+1)\), with equality if and only if \(\theta_j = 1/(k+1)\) for all \(j \in \{0, \dots, k\}\).

Solution

Consider the optimization problem:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{\theta} \in \R^{k+1}} \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j = 1, \quad \theta_j \ge 0 \]

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on vectors \(\mathbf{\theta} = (\theta_0, \dots, \theta_k)\) and \(\mathbf{1} = (1, \dots, 1)\):

\[ \left( \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j \cdot 1 \right)^2 \le \left( \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j^2 \right) \left( \sum_{j=0}^k 1^2 \right) \]

Substituting \(\sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j = 1\) and \(\sum_{j=0}^k 1 = k+1\):

\[ 1^2 \le \xi \cdot (k+1) \implies \xi \ge \frac{1}{k+1} \]

Equality in Cauchy-Schwarz holds if and only if \(\mathbf{\theta}\) is proportional to \(\mathbf{1}\), which gives \(\theta_j = c\). Since \(\sum \theta_j = (k+1)c = 1\), we must have \(\theta_j = 1/(k+1)\) for all \(j\). This establishes that reported variance can be reduced by at most a factor of \(k+1\).

Problem 4.5 (Invariance of Long-Run Cumulative Return) Prove that the long-run expected geometric growth rate of an illiquid asset under GLM smoothing equals the growth rate of the unobserved latent asset, up to an \(\mathcal{O}(1/T)\) boundary condition.

Solution

Let \(R_t\) be defined by \(R_t = \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j R_{t-j}^*\) with \(\sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j = 1\). Consider the cumulative sum of returns over \(T\) periods:

\[ \sum_{t=1}^T R_t = \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j R_{t-j}^* = \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j \left( \sum_{t=1}^T R_{t-j}^* \right) \]

Partition the inner sum relative to \(\sum_{t=1}^T R_t^*\):

\[ \sum_{t=1}^T R_{t-j}^* = \sum_{t=1}^T R_t^* + \sum_{s=1-j}^0 R_s^* - \sum_{s=T-j+1}^T R_s^* \]

Substituting back and using \(\sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j = 1\):

\[ \sum_{t=1}^T R_t = \sum_{t=1}^T R_t^* + \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j \left( \sum_{s=1-j}^0 R_s^* - \sum_{s=T-j+1}^T R_s^* \right) \]

Dividing by \(T\):

\[ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T R_t - \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T R_t^* = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{j=0}^k \theta_j \left( \sum_{s=1-j}^0 R_s^* - \sum_{s=T-j+1}^T R_s^* \right) \]

The boundary term contains at most \(k\) terms and has expectation bounded by \(2k\mu\). As \(T \to \infty\), \(\frac{1}{T} \to 0\), establishing that the sample mean return of reported returns converges almost surely to the sample mean of true returns with discrepancy \(\mathcal{O}(1/T)\).

Problem 4.6 (Positive Definiteness of the Toeplitz Covariance Matrix) Let \(\mathbf{\Gamma}\) be the \(T \times T\) autocovariance matrix generated by an MA(\(k\)) process with \(\sigma^* > 0\) and \(\theta_0 > 0\). Prove that \(\mathbf{\Gamma}\) is strictly positive definite.

Solution

Let \(\mathbf{a} = [a_1, \dots, a_T]^\top \in \R^T\) be any non-zero vector. We must show \(\mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{\Gamma} \mathbf{a} > 0\).

By definition, the reported return vector can be written as a linear filter of latent white noise innovations: \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{\Theta} \mathbf{R}^*\), where \(\mathbf{R}^* \sim (0, \sigma^{*2} \mathbf{I}_{T+k})\) and \(\mathbf{\Theta}\) is a \(T \times (T+k)\) banded matrix of smoothing weights:

\[ \mathbf{\Theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \theta_k & \cdots & \theta_0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \theta_k & \cdots & \theta_0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & \theta_k & \cdots & \theta_0 \end{bmatrix} \]

The covariance matrix is \(\mathbf{\Gamma} = \E[\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^\top] = \sigma^{*2} \mathbf{\Theta} \mathbf{\Theta}^\top\). Therefore:

\[ \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{\Gamma} \mathbf{a} = \sigma^{*2} \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{\Theta} \mathbf{\Theta}^\top \mathbf{a} = \sigma^{*2} \|\mathbf{\Theta}^\top \mathbf{a}\|_2^2 \]

Because \(\sigma^{*2} > 0\), this quadratic form is zero if and only if \(\mathbf{\Theta}^\top \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\). Since \(\theta_0 > 0\), the rows of \(\mathbf{\Theta}\) are linearly independent (the matrix has full row rank \(T\)). Hence \(\mathbf{\Theta}^\top \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\) implies \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\). Thus for all \(\mathbf{a} \neq \mathbf{0}\), \(\mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{\Gamma} \mathbf{a} > 0\), proving that \(\mathbf{\Gamma}\) is strictly positive definite.

Problem 4.8 (Dimson Beta Summation on Private Equity) A private equity buyout index is regressed on public market excess returns with 2 quarterly lags: \[ R_t^{\text{PE}} = 0.015 + 0.38 R_t^m + 0.42 R_{t-1}^m + 0.24 R_{t-2}^m + e_t \] Explain how an allocator who looks only at the contemporaneous beta (\(0.38\)) misjudges the market exposure of the PE allocation, and compute the true Dimson beta.

Solution
  1. The contemporaneous beta is \(\hat{\beta}_0 = 0.38\). An allocator looking only at this figure would conclude that private equity provides defensive equity exposure with less than half the systematic market risk of the S&P 500 (\(\beta = 0.38 \ll 1.0\)).
  2. The true systematic market exposure is given by the Dimson (1979) sum of betas: \[ \beta_{\text{total}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2 = 0.38 + 0.42 + 0.24 = 1.04 \]
  3. In reality, the private equity portfolio has slightly higher systematic market risk than the public equity market (\(\beta = 1.04 > 1.0\)), which is consistent with the substantial leverage utilized in leveraged buyouts. The apparent "low beta" was purely an illusion produced by valuation lags.

Problem 4.9 (Multi-Start MLE Sensitivity) Why is multi-start numerical optimization necessary when solving the GLM likelihood problem, and what risk arises from starting solely at \(\mathbf{\theta} = [1, 0, 0]\)?

Solution

The MA(\(k\)) Gaussian log-likelihood on Toeplitz covariance matrices is non-convex with respect to \(\mathbf{\theta}\), featuring saddle points and local minima near the boundaries of the simplex \(\sum \theta_j = 1, \theta_j \ge 0\). If the optimizer is initiated at \(\theta = [1, 0, 0]\) (the unsmoothed boundary point), the gradient of the autocovariances \(\nabla \gamma_s\) with respect to lagged \(\theta_j\) can vanish or point into infeasible negative regions, trapping standard gradient algorithms (such as L-BFGS-B or SLSQP) at the boundary. Initializing across multiple interior points (e.g., \(\theta_0 \in \{0.75, 0.55, 0.40\}\)) guarantees exploration of both moderate and heavy smoothing basins of attraction, identifying the global maximum likelihood.

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 4.10 (State-Space Unsmoothing: Couts, Welk & Zych 2024) In modern institutional finance, why does Couts, Welk & Zych (2024) argue that state-space Kalman filtering outperforms classical constant-parameter GLM for commercial real estate and direct lending funds?

Solution

Classical GLM assumes time-invariant smoothing weights \(\mathbf{\theta}\). However, in actual illiquid markets:

  1. Regime-Dependent Appraisals: During acute financial crises (e.g., 2008 GFC or 2020 COVID shock), distressed property sales and forced loan renegotiations provide immediate transaction comps, causing appraisers to update valuations rapidly (\(\theta_{0,t}\) increases). During tranquil periods, appraisal updates are sluggish (\(\theta_{0,t}\) decreases).
  2. Cross-Sectional Hierarchical Information: Individual fund track records have finite sample lengths (\(T \approx 60\) to \(120\) months). A state-space framework allows empirical Bayes cross-sectional shrinkage, borrowing strength across peer funds in the same vintage or property sector to prevent overfitting idiosyncratic fund noise.

Problem 4.11 (Risk Parity Allocation Failure under Smoothed Covariances) Consider a Risk Parity multi-asset portfolio allocating across public equities (\(\sigma_{\text{eq}} = 16\%\)) and private equity (\(\sigma_{\text{pe}} = 10\%\) reported, \(\sigma^*_{\text{pe}} = 20\%\) true economic). If the risk parity algorithm sizes weights inversely proportional to volatility (\(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\)), quantify the capital and risk distortion caused by failing to unsmooth.

Solution

1. Under naive reported volatility:

\[ \frac{w_{\text{pe}}^{\text{naive}}}{w_{\text{eq}}^{\text{naive}}} = \frac{1/10\%}{1/16\%} = 1.60 \]

The optimizer allocates \(1.6\times\) more capital to private equity than to public equities, assuming it is significantly safer.

2. Under true unsmoothed volatility:

\[ \frac{w_{\text{pe}}^*}{w_{\text{eq}}^*} = \frac{1/20\%}{1/16\%} = 0.80 \]

True risk parity dictates that private equity should receive only \(0.80\times\) the capital of public equities.

3. Risk Contribution Impact: In the naive portfolio, the true risk contributed by private equity is \(w_{\text{pe}}^2 \sigma^{*2}_{\text{pe}} = (1.60)^2 \times 20^2 = 2.56 \times 400 = 1024\), while public equities contribute \((1.00)^2 \times 16^2 = 256\). Private equity actually drives \(\frac{1024}{1280} = 80\%\) of total portfolio risk! The allocator believes the portfolio is risk-balanced, but it is dangerously overconcentrated in illiquid leveraged private equity.

Problem 4.12 (Discretionary vs. Structural Smoothing Diagnostic) How can an institutional allocator quantitatively distinguish whether serial correlation in an illiquid fund is driven by legitimate appraisal frictions (structural) versus deliberate manager manipulation (opportunistic)?

Solution

An allocator applies a two-pronged econometric test:

  1. Asymmetry Test (Bollen & Pool 2008, 2009): Estimate GLM smoothing parameters separately for positive market periods (\(\Delta M_t > 0\)) and negative market periods (\(\Delta M_t < 0\)). Under pure structural friction, \(\theta_j\) should be symmetric across market regimes. If \(\theta_{0, \text{down}} \ll \theta_{0, \text{up}}\) (the manager delays reporting losses far more than gains), opportunistic manipulation is present.
  2. Discontinuity at Zero Test: Run McCrary density tests and binomial sign tests on small returns in the interval \([-\epsilon, +\epsilon]\). A sharp discontinuity with an excess of small positive returns (\(0.0\% < R_t < 0.2\%\)) and a deficit of small negative returns indicates active NAV management to preserve unbroken positive track records.
