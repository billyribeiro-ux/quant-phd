Classical Performance Attribution & Alpha Inference

Active portfolio management promises excess risk-adjusted returns above passive market benchmarks. Yet across six decades since Jensen (1968), econometricians have demonstrated that separating true investment skill from luck is profoundly challenging. Classical OLS standard errors fail when residuals exhibit volatility clustering or serial persistence, necessitating Newey–West HAC inference. Moreover, an apparent single-factor alpha often vanishes when evaluated against multi-factor benchmarks such as Carhart (1997) or Fama–French (2015). In a cross-section of thousands of competing managers, multiple hypothesis testing guarantees that hundreds of unskilled managers appear gifted by pure chance. Mastery of alpha inference, robust covariance estimation, and false discovery control is essential for allocating fiduciary capital.

From the seminal mutual fund study of Jensen (1968) to modern multi-factor benchmarking, separating true manager skill from luck requires rigorous econometric inference. In this lesson, we formalize the asymptotic distribution of alpha, contrast homoskedastic OLS with Newey–West HAC standard errors, dissect multi-factor benchmark models, and apply false discovery rate methods to cross-sections of active managers.

1. The Jensen (1968) Paradigm & Single-Factor Alpha

In his landmark empirical study, Jensen (1968) introduced the first formal risk-adjusted metric for evaluating mutual fund performance by anchoring evaluation to the Capital Asset Pricing Model.

Definition 1.1 (Jensen's Alpha) Let \(R_{i, t}\) denote the return of portfolio \(i\) at time \(t\), let \(R_{f, t}\) denote the risk-free rate, and let \(R_{m, t}\) denote the return of the market portfolio. Under the single-factor market model, excess return follows the time-series regression:

\[R_{i, t} - R_{f, t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i (R_{m, t} - R_{f, t}) + \epsilon_{i, t}, \quad \E[\epsilon_{i, t}] = 0, \quad \Cov(\epsilon_{i, t}, R_{m, t} - R_{f, t}) = 0\]

The parameter \(\alpha_i\) is Jensen's alpha, defined as the expected excess return of the portfolio above the return required for its systematic market covariance: \(\alpha_i = \E[R_{i, t} - R_{f, t}] - \beta_i \E[R_{m, t} - R_{f, t}]\).

Theorem 1.1 (Zero Alpha Under Benchmark Efficiency) If the benchmark market portfolio \(m\) is mean-variance efficient with respect to the universe of investable assets, then for every passive or uninformative portfolio \(i\), \(\alpha_i = 0\).

Proof. By definition of mean-variance efficiency, the benchmark portfolio \(m\) satisfies the first-order conditions of the portfolio optimization problem. The expected return of any asset or portfolio \(i\) is linearly spanned by its covariance with \(m\): \(\E[R_i - R_f] = \frac{\Cov(R_i, R_m)}{\Var(R_m)} \E[R_m - R_f] = \beta_i \E[R_m - R_f]\). Substituting this expectation into the definition of alpha yields \(\alpha_i = \E[R_i - R_f] - \beta_i \E[R_m - R_f] = 0\). Conversely, a non-zero alpha \(\alpha_i \ne 0\) implies that adding asset \(i\) to the benchmark expands the tangency portfolio Sharpe ratio.

Evaluating 115 open-end mutual funds over 1945–1964, Jensen found that net of management expenses, funds generated an average annual alpha of -1.1% (\(t = -3.2\)), and before expenses an average alpha of -0.4%, demonstrating that on average active managers did not outperform passive index buy-and-hold strategies.

2. Asymptotic Distribution of Alpha & Robust Inference

Testing whether an estimated alpha represents genuine economic skill rather than sampling noise requires alpha inference: formal statistical hypothesis testing and confidence interval construction on \(\alpha_i\) under non-spherical error structures.

Let \(Y \in \R^T\) denote portfolio excess returns, and let \(X = [\mathbf{1}_T, F] \in \R^{T \times (K+1)}\) denote the regression design matrix containing an intercept and \(K\) systematic benchmark factors. The ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator is:

\[\hat{\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\alpha} \\ \hat{\beta} \end{bmatrix} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top Y\]

Theorem 1.2 (Asymptotic Distribution of Alpha under HAC Conditions) Assume the factor series and regression errors satisfy stationarity, ergodicity, \(\E[X_t \epsilon_t] = 0\), and finite fourth moments. As sample length \(T \to \infty\):

\[\sqrt{T} (\hat{\theta} - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal\left(\mathbf{0}, Q^{-1} S Q^{-1}\right)\]

where \(Q = \operatorname{plim} \frac{1}{T} X^\top X\), and \(S\) is the spectral density matrix of \(X_t \epsilon_t\) at frequency zero:

\[S = \lim_{T \to \infty} \Var\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \sum_{t=1}^T X_t \epsilon_t\right) = \Gamma_0 + \sum_{l=1}^\infty (\Gamma_l + \Gamma_l^\top), \quad \Gamma_l = \E[(X_t \epsilon_t) (X_{t-l} \epsilon_{t-l})^\top]\]

Proof sketch. Decompose the estimation error as \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta) = \left(\frac{1}{T} X^\top X\right)^{-1} \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \sum_{t=1}^T X_t \epsilon_t\right)\). By the uniform law of large numbers, \(\frac{1}{T} X^\top X \xrightarrow{p} Q\). By the Gourieroux–Monfort central limit theorem for stationary mixing processes, \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \sum_{t=1}^T X_t \epsilon_t \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(\mathbf{0}, S)\). Slutsky's theorem directly gives the sandwich covariance \(Q^{-1} S Q^{-1}\). The asymptotic variance of alpha is the \((0, 0)\) diagonal entry of this matrix.

In empirical finance, three covariance estimators are commonly compared:

  1. Classical Homoskedastic OLS: Assumes \(\E[\epsilon_t^2 | X_t] = \sigma^2\) and \(\E[\epsilon_t \epsilon_{t-l}] = 0\), so \(\hat{V}_{\text{OLS}} = s^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\). Under conditional heteroskedasticity, this estimator is biased.
  2. White (1980) Heteroskedasticity-Consistent (HC0): Sets \(\hat{S} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \hat{\epsilon}_t^2 X_t X_t^\top\), correcting for time-varying residual volatility.
  3. Newey–West (1987) HAC: Weights autocovariances with a Bartlett triangular kernel: \(\hat{S} = \hat{\Gamma}_0 + \sum_{l=1}^L \left(1 - \frac{l}{L+1}\right) (\hat{\Gamma}_l + \hat{\Gamma}_l^\top)\), with bandwidth \(L = \lfloor 4 (T/100)^{2/9} \rfloor\).

3. Multi-Factor Benchmarking & Omitted Factor Biases

A positive single-factor CAPM alpha may simply reflect uncompensated exposure to known systematic anomaly factors rather than manager skill. This necessitates factor-adjusted alpha: the intercept extracted from a multi-factor regression that explicitly controls for systematic asset pricing factors.

Carhart (1997) demonstrated that the apparent one-year persistence in mutual fund performance documented by Hendricks, Patel & Zeckhauser (1993) is almost entirely explained by common exposure to the 12-2 momentum factor. The Carhart four-factor specification is:

\[R_{i, t} - R_{f, t} = \alpha_i^{\text{C4}} + \beta_{i, m} (R_{m, t} - R_{f, t}) + \beta_{i, \text{SMB}} \text{SMB}_t + \beta_{i, \text{HML}} \text{HML}_t + \beta_{i, \text{MOM}} \text{MOM}_t + \epsilon_{i, t}\]

Theorem 1.3 (Omitted Factor Bias in Alpha) Let the true return-generating model include an omitted systematic factor \(F_2\) with true risk premium \(\lambda_2 = \E[F_2]\): \(R_t = \alpha + \beta_1 F_{1, t} + \beta_2 F_{2, t} + \epsilon_t\). If the econometrician estimates the misspecified single-factor regression on \(F_{1, t}\), the estimated intercept satisfies:

\[\operatorname{plim} \hat{\alpha}_{\text{miss}} = \alpha + \beta_2 \left(\E[F_2] - \frac{\Cov(F_1, F_2)}{\Var(F_1)} \E[F_1]\right) = \alpha + \beta_2 \alpha_{2|1}\]

where \(\alpha_{2|1}\) is the alpha of factor 2 when regressed on factor 1.

Proof. The univariate regression slope converges to \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{b}_1 = \frac{\Cov(R, F_1)}{\Var(F_1)} = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \frac{\Cov(F_1, F_2)}{\Var(F_1)}\). The estimated intercept is \(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{miss}} = \bar{R} - \hat{b}_1 \bar{F}_1\). Taking probability limits: \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\alpha}_{\text{miss}} = (\alpha + \beta_1 \E[F_1] + \beta_2 \E[F_2]) - \left(\beta_1 + \beta_2 \frac{\Cov(F_1, F_2)}{\Var(F_1)}\right) \E[F_1] = \alpha + \beta_2 (\E[F_2] - \beta_{2|1} \E[F_1]) = \alpha + \beta_2 \alpha_{2|1}\).

If the omitted factor generates a positive unspanned premium (\(\alpha_{2|1} > 0\)) and the manager carries positive factor exposure (\(\beta_2 > 0\)), the single-factor model erroneously credits systematic factor harvesting as manager skill.

4. Cross-Sectional Luck vs. Skill: False Discovery Rates

When an endowment screens a universe of \(N = 2{,}000\) managers at the classical significance threshold \(t_{\hat{\alpha}} > 2.0\) (\(p < 0.05\)), standard individual hypothesis tests fail to account for the multiple testing multiplicity.

Fama & French (2010) constructed a non-parametric cross-sectional bootstrap to separate lucky managers from skilled managers across 3,156 US mutual funds over 1984–2006. By subtracting each fund's estimated alpha from its return series to enforce the null hypothesis \(\alpha_i = 0\), they generated empirical distributions of t-statistics produced entirely by luck.

Barras, Scaillet & Wermers (2010) formalized this cross-sectional identification using the False Discovery Rate (FDR). The fund universe is partitioned into three latent proportions: skilled managers (\(\pi^+\)), unskilled managers (\(\pi^-\)), and zero-alpha managers (\(\pi_0\)), where \(\pi^+ + \pi^- + \pi_0 = 1\).

Definition 1.2 (False Discovery Rate in Alpha Screening) For a chosen significance threshold \(\gamma\) (e.g. \(p < 0.05\)), let \(S^+(\gamma)\) denote the total number of significant positive alpha discoveries. The False Discovery Rate is the expected ratio of lucky zero-alpha funds to total discoveries:

\[\text{FDR}^+(\gamma) = \E\left[\frac{F^+(\gamma)}{S^+(\gamma)}\right] \approx \frac{\hat{\pi}_0 \cdot (\gamma / 2)}{S^+(\gamma) / N}\]

where \(\hat{\pi}_0\) is estimated by the density of p-values in the uninformative region \([\lambda^*, 1]\): \(\hat{\pi}_0 = \frac{\#\{p_i > \lambda^*\}}{N(1 - \lambda^*)}\).

Across 2,076 US equity funds over 1975–2006, Barras et al. found that \(\hat{\pi}_0 = 75.4\%\), unskilled funds comprised \(\hat{\pi}^- = 24.0\%\), and skilled funds comprised only \(\hat{\pi}^+ = 0.6\%\). Among the funds with significant positive net alphas at \(t > 2.0\), over 75% were false discoveries driven by luck.

Lab 1 · Multi-Factor Alpha Inference and HAC Standard Errors

Under conditional heteroskedasticity and residual autocorrelation, Newey–West (1987) HAC standard errors will be materially larger than classical OLS standard errors, deflating the apparent t-statistic and preventing spurious skill attribution.

"""Lab F7-L01-1: Alpha Inference and HAC Standard Errors in Multi-Factor Benchmarking.

Evaluates manager alpha under single-factor (CAPM) and multi-factor (Carhart 4-factor)
benchmarks, comparing classical OLS standard errors with White (1980) heteroskedasticity-robust
and Newey-West (1987) heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors,
and validating inference via residual bootstrap.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
import scipy.stats


def run_alpha_inference(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Estimate alpha and compute robust standard errors for a candidate fund manager."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_months = 180  # 15 years

    # 1. Simulate 4 systematic factors: Mkt-RF, SMB, HML, MOM
    mkt = rng.normal(0.0065, 0.045, size=n_months)
    smb = rng.normal(0.0020, 0.030, size=n_months)
    hml = rng.normal(0.0030, 0.032, size=n_months)
    mom = rng.normal(0.0055, 0.042, size=n_months)
    factors = np.column_stack([mkt, smb, hml, mom])

    # 2. True data generating process: zero true alpha, systematic factor exposures,
    # plus AR(1) heteroskedastic residuals (simulating time-varying liquidity risk)
    beta_true = np.array([0.90, 0.35, -0.20, 0.40])
    alpha_true = 0.0000

    u = np.zeros(n_months)
    sigma_base = 0.020
    for t in range(n_months):
        vol_t = sigma_base * (1.0 + 0.8 * (mkt[t] < -0.04))  # volatility expansion in market drawdowns
        innov = rng.normal(0.0, vol_t)
        u[t] = 0.30 * u[t - 1] + innov if t > 0 else innov

    # Manager excess return: r_t = alpha + beta * F_t + u_t
    r_manager = alpha_true + factors @ beta_true + u

    # 3. Multi-factor regression matrix X = [1, F]
    ones = np.ones((n_months, 1))
    x_mat = np.column_stack([ones, factors])
    k_params = x_mat.shape[1]

    # OLS estimation: b = (X'X)^{-1} X' y
    xtx_inv = np.linalg.inv(x_mat.T @ x_mat)
    beta_hat = xtx_inv @ (x_mat.T @ r_manager)
    alpha_hat = float(beta_hat[0])
    resid = r_manager - x_mat @ beta_hat

    # 4. Standard error estimators
    # (a) Classical homoskedastic OLS
    s2_ols = float(np.sum(resid**2) / (n_months - k_params))
    v_ols = s2_ols * xtx_inv
    se_alpha_ols = float(np.sqrt(v_ols[0, 0]))

    # (b) White (1980) heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC0)
    diag_e2 = np.diag(resid**2)
    s_white = (x_mat.T @ diag_e2 @ x_mat) / float(n_months)
    v_white = float(n_months) * (xtx_inv @ s_white @ xtx_inv)
    se_alpha_white = float(np.sqrt(v_white[0, 0]))

    # (c) Newey-West (1987) HAC with Bartlett kernel (bandwidth L = 4)
    bandwidth = 4
    gamma_0 = (x_mat.T @ diag_e2 @ x_mat) / float(n_months)
    gamma_sum = np.zeros_like(gamma_0)
    for lag in range(1, bandwidth + 1):
        weight = 1.0 - (lag / (bandwidth + 1.0))
        gamma_l = np.zeros_like(gamma_0)
        for t in range(lag, n_months):
            x_t = x_mat[t : t + 1, :].T
            x_t_lag = x_mat[t - lag : t - lag + 1, :].T
            gamma_l += resid[t] * resid[t - lag] * (x_t @ x_t_lag.T + x_t_lag @ x_t.T)
        gamma_sum += weight * (gamma_l / float(n_months))

    s_hac = gamma_0 + gamma_sum
    v_hac = float(n_months) * (xtx_inv @ s_hac @ xtx_inv)
    se_alpha_hac = float(np.sqrt(v_hac[0, 0]))

    # t-statistics and p-values
    t_stat_ols = alpha_hat / se_alpha_ols
    t_stat_hac = alpha_hat / se_alpha_hac
    p_val_ols = float(2.0 * (1.0 - scipy.stats.t.cdf(np.abs(t_stat_ols), df=n_months - k_params)))
    p_val_hac = float(2.0 * (1.0 - scipy.stats.t.cdf(np.abs(t_stat_hac), df=n_months - k_params)))

    # Annualized alpha in percent
    alpha_ann_pct = alpha_hat * 12.0 * 100.0

    return {
        "alpha_monthly": alpha_hat,
        "alpha_ann_pct": round(alpha_ann_pct, 4),
        "se_alpha_ols": round(se_alpha_ols, 6),
        "se_alpha_white": round(se_alpha_white, 6),
        "se_alpha_hac": round(se_alpha_hac, 6),
        "t_stat_ols": round(t_stat_ols, 3),
        "t_stat_hac": round(t_stat_hac, 3),
        "p_val_ols": round(p_val_ols, 4),
        "p_val_hac": round(p_val_hac, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F7-L01-1 | Alpha Inference and HAC Standard Errors")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------")
    res = run_alpha_inference(seed=42)
    print(f"Annualized Alpha:              {res['alpha_ann_pct']:+.4f}%")
    print(
        f"Classical OLS Standard Error:  {res['se_alpha_ols']:.6f} "
        f"(t = {res['t_stat_ols']:+.3f}, p = {res['p_val_ols']:.4f})"
    )
    print(f"White (1980) HC0 Std Error:    {res['se_alpha_white']:.6f}")
    print(
        f"Newey-West (1987) HAC Std Err: {res['se_alpha_hac']:.6f} "
        f"(t = {res['t_stat_hac']:+.3f}, p = {res['p_val_hac']:.4f})"
    )


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical performance evaluation canon assumes that fund alpha is a static, time-invariant parameter reflecting constant stock-picking talent. In equilibrium, this assumption breaks down through two well-documented economic mechanisms:

  1. Berk & Green (2004) Rational Flow Equilibrium: In competitive capital markets, rational investors allocate capital to managers with past positive performance until decreasing returns to scale erode net alpha to zero. Skilled managers extract economic rents through expanding assets under management (AUM) and percentage fees, so net performance does not persist even when managers possess genuine skill.
  2. The Arithmetic of Active Management: As proved by Sharpe (1991), before costs the return on the average actively managed dollar equals the return on the average passively managed dollar; after costs, the average active dollar must underperform the average passive dollar as a mathematical identity.

Misconceptions

  1. Misconception: A t-statistic above 2.0 proves that a manager is skilled

    Correction: In an institutional database of \(N = 2{,}000\) managers, multiple hypothesis testing guarantees that approximately 50 completely unskilled managers will produce \(t > 1.96\) purely by chance under the null hypothesis of zero alpha. Evaluating an individual t-statistic without accounting for the false discovery rate (\(\text{FDR}\)) leads to massive capital misallocation to lucky noise traders. Fama & French (2010) show via cross-sectional bootstrap that 97% of active fund alphas in the right tail are consistent with pure chance.

  2. Misconception: High R-squared indicates superior portfolio management

    Correction: In performance attribution, a high \(R^2\) (e.g. \(R^2 > 0.95\)) indicates that portfolio returns are almost entirely spanned by passive benchmark factors, indicating a closet indexer charging active fees for beta replication. Genuine stock selection skill produces idiosyncratic risk that reduces \(R^2\). Carhart (1997) shows that high-fee closet indexers consistently underperform net of expenses.

  3. Misconception: Classical OLS standard errors are sufficient if the sample size is large

    Correction: Increasing sample length \(T\) does not eliminate the bias of classical OLS standard errors when errors violate conditional homoskedasticity or independence. If volatility clusters or residuals exhibit positive serial correlation, classical standard errors remain inconsistent regardless of \(T\). Theorem 1.2 proves that asymptotic variance requires the sandwich matrix \(Q^{-1}SQ^{-1}\); omitting off-diagonal autocovariances underestimates variance systematically.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A portfolio generates an average monthly return of 1.10% over a 120-month sample during which the risk-free rate averaged 0.25% per month. Regression against the market factor (which had an average excess return of 0.60% per month) yields an estimated beta \(\hat{\beta}_m = 1.25\). Calculate the estimated monthly Jensen's alpha and its annualized percentage value.

Solution

The monthly excess return of the portfolio is \(\bar{R}_p - \bar{R}_f = 1.10\% - 0.25\% = 0.85\%\). The expected excess return predicted by the CAPM beta is \(\hat{\beta}_m (\bar{R}_m - \bar{R}_f) = 1.25 \times 0.60\% = 0.75\%\). The monthly Jensen's alpha is \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.85\% - 0.75\% = 0.10\%\) per month (0.0010 in decimals). The annualized alpha is \(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{ann}} = 0.10\% \times 12 = 1.20\%\) per year.

In a Carhart four-factor regression over \(T = 180\) months, a manager's monthly alpha is estimated as \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.0025\) with Newey–West HAC standard error \(\operatorname{se}(\hat{\alpha}) = 0.0010\). Compute the t-statistic and the corresponding two-sided p-value against the null hypothesis \(H_0: \alpha = 0\).

Solution

The t-statistic is \(t = \frac{\hat{\alpha}}{\operatorname{se}(\hat{\alpha})} = \frac{0.0025}{0.0010} = 2.50\). With degrees of freedom \(df = T - K - 1 = 180 - 4 - 1 = 175\), the two-sided p-value is \(2 \times (1 - F_t(2.50, 175)) \approx 2 \times (1 - 0.9933) = 0.0134\). Because \(p < 0.05\), the null hypothesis is rejected at the 5% significance level.

An endowment screens \(N = 1{,}000\) managers at significance level \(\gamma = 0.05\). The estimated proportion of zero-alpha managers is \(\hat{\pi}_0 = 0.80\). If the test yields 60 positive alpha discoveries (\(S^+ = 60\)), calculate the estimated False Discovery Rate (\(\text{FDR}^+\)).

Solution

Under the null hypothesis, zero-alpha managers generate positive p-values below \(\gamma\) with probability \(\gamma / 2 = 0.05 / 2 = 0.025\). The expected number of false discoveries from the \(N \hat{\pi}_0 = 1{,}000 \times 0.80 = 800\) unskilled funds is \(F^+ = 800 \times 0.025 = 20\). The False Discovery Rate is \(\text{FDR}^+ = \frac{F^+}{S^+} = \frac{20}{60} = 0.3333\) or 33.3%. One third of all significant discoveries are expected to be pure luck.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that in an orthogonal multi-factor model where all factors have pairwise zero sample covariances (\(X_j^\top X_k = 0\) for \(j \ne k\)), the estimated factor-adjusted alpha \(\hat{\alpha}\) is identical to the alpha from separate univariate regressions minus the sum of omitted factor premia.

Solution

When factors are orthogonal, the design cross-product matrix \(X^\top X\) is block diagonal. The inverse \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) is diagonal with diagonal elements \(1/T\) and \(1/(X_k^\top X_k)\). The OLS normal equations uncouple completely, yielding \(\hat{\beta}_k = (X_k^\top X_k)^{-1} X_k^\top Y\) for every \(k\). The intercept estimator satisfies \(\hat{\alpha} = \bar{Y} - \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{\beta}_k \bar{F}_k\). In a univariate regression on factor 1, the slope is \(\hat{b}_1 = \hat{\beta}_1\), and the univariate intercept is \(\hat{\alpha}_1 = \bar{Y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{F}_1\). Substituting yields \(\hat{\alpha} = \hat{\alpha}_1 - \sum_{k=2}^K \hat{\beta}_k \bar{F}_k\), proving that the multi-factor alpha subtracts the exact systematic risk premia contributed by all additional factors.

Prove that under positive residual autocorrelation (\(\E[\epsilon_t \epsilon_{t-1}] > 0\)) and positive factor persistence (\(\E[F_t F_{t-1}] > 0\)), the classical homoskedastic OLS variance estimator strictly underestimates the true asymptotic variance of \(\hat{\beta}\).

Solution

The true variance of the numerator \(\sum X_t \epsilon_t\) in the OLS slope estimator is \(\Var(\sum X_t \epsilon_t) = \sum_t \E[X_t^2 \epsilon_t^2] + 2 \sum_{l=1}^{T-1} \sum_{t=l+1}^T \E[X_t X_{t-l} \epsilon_t \epsilon_{t-l}]\). Under homoskedasticity and independence, the cross-terms vanish, leaving \(\sigma^2 \sum X_t^2\). Under positive autocorrelation, \(\E[\epsilon_t \epsilon_{t-l}] > 0\) and \(\E[X_t X_{t-l}] > 0\), so the cross-product expectations are strictly positive. The true variance exceeds the homoskedastic formula by \(2 \sum_{l=1}^\infty \Gamma_l > 0\). The OLS variance estimator omits these positive cross-terms, proving that it is strictly downward biased.

Prove that if a fund's beta is stochastic and positively correlated with market returns (\(\Cov(\beta_t, R_{m, t}) > 0\), e.g. successful market timing), a standard fixed-beta OLS regression produces a positive bias in estimated beta and a downward bias in estimated alpha.

Solution

Let \(R_t - R_f = \alpha + \beta_t (R_{m, t} - R_f) + \epsilon_t\), where \(\beta_t = \bar{\beta} + u_t\) with \(\E[u_t] = 0\) and \(\Cov(u_t, R_{m, t}) > 0\). The covariance between portfolio excess return and market excess return is \(\Cov(R_t - R_f, R_{m, t} - R_f) = \bar{\beta} \Var(R_m) + \Cov(u_t (R_{m, t} - R_f), R_{m, t} - R_f)\). For positive timing (\(u_t > 0\) when \(R_m\) is high), this higher-order moment is strictly positive. The estimated OLS beta is \(\hat{\beta} = \frac{\Cov(R_p, R_m)}{\Var(R_m)} = \bar{\beta} + \frac{\E[u_t (R_{m, t} - R_f)^2]}{\Var(R_m)} > \bar{\beta}\). The estimated alpha is \(\hat{\alpha} = \bar{R}_p - \hat{\beta} \bar{R}_m = (\alpha + \bar{\beta} \bar{R}_m + \Cov(u, R_m)) - \hat{\beta} \bar{R}_m = \alpha - (\hat{\beta} - \bar{\beta}) \bar{R}_m + \Cov(u, R_m)\). When market risk premium \(\bar{R}_m > 0\), the upward-biased beta absorbs timing gains, deflating the intercept.

Tier 3 · Empirical

An active small-cap value manager claims to generate 4.0% annualized alpha relative to the S&P 500 index. When regressed against the Fama–French three-factor model, their factor loadings are \(\hat{\beta}_m = 1.05\), \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{SMB}} = 0.70\), and \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{HML}} = 0.60\). If historical SMB and HML premia average 2.5% and 4.0% per year respectively, calculate the true factor-adjusted three-factor alpha.

Solution

The single-factor CAPM alpha reflects unadjusted factor tilts. The contribution from the small-cap tilt is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{SMB}} \times \E[\text{SMB}] = 0.70 \times 2.5\% = 1.75\%\). The contribution from the value tilt is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{HML}} \times \E[\text{HML}] = 0.60 \times 4.0\% = 2.40\%\). The total systematic factor return masquerading as alpha is \(1.75\% + 2.40\% = 4.15\%\). The true factor-adjusted alpha is \(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{FF3}} = \hat{\alpha}_{\text{CAPM}} - 4.15\% = 4.00\% - 4.15\% = -0.15\%\) per year. The manager generates negative risk-adjusted alpha once systematic factor tilts are controlled.

In a hedge fund return panel, monthly residual autocorrelations are estimated as \(\hat{\rho}_1 = 0.30\), \(\hat{\rho}_2 = 0.15\), and \(\hat{\rho}_3 = 0.05\). For bandwidth \(L = 3\), compute the Newey–West Bartlett kernel weights \(w(l, 3) = 1 - \frac{l}{4}\) and calculate the factor by which the sum of autocovariances inflates the long-run residual variance over the sample variance \(\hat{\gamma}_0\).

Solution

The Bartlett weights are \(w(1, 3) = 1 - 1/4 = 0.75\), \(w(2, 3) = 1 - 2/4 = 0.50\), and \(w(3, 3) = 1 - 3/4 = 0.25\). The variance inflation factor is \(1 + 2 \sum_{l=1}^3 w(l, 3) \hat{\rho}_l = 1 + 2 [0.75(0.30) + 0.50(0.15) + 0.25(0.05)] = 1 + 2 [0.225 + 0.075 + 0.0125] = 1 + 2(0.3125) = 1 + 0.625 = 1.625\). The long-run residual variance is 62.5% larger than the simple sample variance, expanding standard errors by \(\sqrt{1.625} \approx 1.275\) (+27.5%).

In an institutional evaluation of 500 hedge funds, 50 funds generate p-values below 0.05. Using the Storey (2002) estimator with tuning parameter \(\lambda^* = 0.50\), 210 funds have p-values exceeding 0.50. Estimate \(\hat{\pi}_0\) and the False Discovery Rate among the 50 discoveries.

Solution

The estimated proportion of true nulls is \(\hat{\pi}_0 = \frac{\#\{p_i > 0.50\}}{N (1 - \lambda^*)} = \frac{210}{500 \times (1 - 0.50)} = \frac{210}{250} = 0.8400\) (84% of funds have zero true alpha). The expected number of false discoveries at \(\gamma = 0.05\) is \(F = N \times \hat{\pi}_0 \times \gamma = 500 \times 0.84 \times 0.05 = 21.0\). The False Discovery Rate is \(\text{FDR} = \frac{21.0}{50} = 0.4200\) (42.0%). Out of 50 significant managers, approximately 21 are false positive noise traders.

Tier 4 · Research

How does the rise of multi-factor smart beta exchange-traded funds (ETFs) affect the economic rents that active equity managers can extract under the Berk & Green (2004) framework?

Solution

In Berk & Green (2004), managers extract surplus by charging active management fees on assets under management up to the point where net expected alpha is zero. When systematic factor premia (size, value, momentum, quality) become liquidly securitized as low-cost ETFs charging 10 to 20 basis points, sophisticated allocators unbundle factor returns from idiosyncratic skill. Investors refuse to pay 100 to 200 basis points for factor tilts, compressing the fee base and forcing active managers to prove pure unspanned idiosyncratic alpha.

Why does the Fama–French (2010) bootstrap methodology preserve cross-sectional return correlation across funds while randomizing time-series order, and what would happen if individual fund residuals were bootstrapped independently?

Solution

Active funds hold overlapping equity portfolios, creating strong cross-sectional residual correlation \(\Cov(\epsilon_{i, t}, \epsilon_{j, t}) \ne 0\). The Fama–French protocol resamples entire monthly cross-sectional vectors \(t \in \{1, \dots, T\}\) simultaneously, perfectly preserving cross-fund contemporaneous correlation. If funds were bootstrapped independently, cross-sectional covariance would be destroyed, artificially depressing the cross-sectional dispersion of lucky alphas and underestimating the frequency of false discoveries.

Critique the use of Carhart four-factor alpha for evaluating fixed income or global macro hedge funds. What structural asset pricing failures arise?

Solution

The Carhart four-factor model was derived specifically for US common equities. Fixed income and global macro hedge funds trade interest rate duration, term premia, credit default spreads, foreign exchange carry, and sovereign yield curve curvature. Regressing fixed income returns on equity SMB and HML factors induces severe omitted variable bias: bond yield changes will be forced into the residual, while market beta will misclassify duration exposure during flight-to-safety episodes. Multi-asset benchmarking requires tailored asset-class factors.
