The Statistics of Sharpe Ratios & Serial Correlation

The Sharpe ratio is the standard currency of risk-adjusted performance across institutional asset management. Yet practitioners routinely treat sample Sharpe ratios as deterministic quantities, ignoring estimation uncertainty and the severe statistical distortions caused by serial correlation and non-normality. Under independent and identically distributed Gaussian returns, Jobson & Korkie (1981) established the asymptotic variance of the estimator. However, as Lo (2002) proved, when returns exhibit positive serial correlation—whether from illiquidity or deliberate return smoothing—naive square-root-of-time annualization severely understates multi-period volatility and inflates the Sharpe ratio. Quantifying the Lo adjustment factor, incorporating skewness and kurtosis, and constructing robust confidence intervals are foundational skills for quantitative allocators.

The Sharpe ratio is universally quoted across institutional finance, yet practitioners routinely treat point estimates as deterministic truths. In this lesson, we formalize the asymptotic distribution of the sample Sharpe ratio under the Jobson–Korkie and Mertens frameworks, analyze the mathematical distortions of non-normality, prove the Lo (2002) time-aggregation theorem under serial correlation, and implement autocorrelation-adjusted confidence intervals.

1. Asymptotic Properties of the IID Sharpe Ratio

Let \(R_t\) denote the excess return of an asset at period \(t\) over the risk-free rate. The population Sharpe ratio is defined as:

\[\SR = \frac{\mu}{\sigma} = \frac{\E[R_t]}{\sqrt{\Var(R_t)}}\]

Given a historical sample of \(T\) observations \(R_1, \dots, R_T\), the sample mean and sample variance are \(\hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T R_t\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T (R_t - \hat{\mu})^2\). The sample Sharpe ratio estimator is \(\hSR = \hat{\mu} / \hat{\sigma}\).

Theorem 2.1 (Jobson & Korkie 1981 Asymptotic Distribution) Assume \(R_t \sim \iid \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\). As sample size \(T \to \infty\), the sample Sharpe ratio estimator \(\hSR\) is asymptotically normally distributed:

\[\sqrt{T} (\hSR - \SR) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal\left(0, 1 + \frac{1}{2} \SR^2\right)\]

Consequently, the asymptotic standard error of the estimator is \(\se(\hSR) = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \frac{1}{2} \SR^2}{T}}\).

Proof. Consider the joint parameter vector \(\theta = (\mu, \sigma^2)^\top\) and its sample estimator \(\hat{\theta} = (\hat{\mu}, \hat{\sigma}^2)^\top\). Under Gaussian i.i.d. returns, \(\hat{\mu}\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) are independent by Basu's theorem, with asymptotic distribution:

\[\sqrt{T} \begin{pmatrix} \hat{\mu} - \mu \\ \hat{\sigma}^2 - \sigma^2 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{d} \Normal\left(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\sigma^4 \end{bmatrix}\right)\]

Define the transformation function \(g(\mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2}}\). The gradient vector is \(\nabla g(\mu, \sigma^2) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sigma} \\ -\frac{\mu}{2\sigma^3} \end{bmatrix}\). By the Delta Method:

\[\operatorname{AsyVar}(\hSR) = [\nabla g]^\top \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\sigma^4 \end{bmatrix} [\nabla g] = \left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)^2 \sigma^2 + \left(-\frac{\mu}{2\sigma^3}\right)^2 (2\sigma^4) = 1 + \frac{\mu^2}{4\sigma^6}(2\sigma^4) = 1 + \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2 = 1 + \frac{1}{2} \SR^2\]

Dividing by \(T\) establishes the asymptotic variance \(\frac{1}{T}\left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\SR^2\right)\).

For a strategy with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.0 based on 5 years of monthly data (\(T = 60\), monthly Sharpe \(\SR = 1.0 / \sqrt{12} \approx 0.2887\)), the Jobson–Korkie standard error is \(\se(\hSR) = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 0.5(0.2887^2)}{60}} \approx 0.1317\), corresponding to an annualized 95% confidence interval of \([0.10, 1.90]\). Point estimates over short horizons carry massive sampling uncertainty.

2. Non-Normality, Skewness, and Fat Tails

Hedge funds, asymmetric options strategies, and credit carry portfolios exhibit marked non-normality: negative skewness from tail crashes and excess kurtosis from extreme shocks.

Theorem 2.2 (Mertens 2002 / Lo 2002 Non-Normal Sharpe Distribution) Let \(R_t\) be i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2\), skewness \(\gamma_3 = \frac{\E[(R_t - \mu)^3]}{\sigma^3}\), and kurtosis \(\gamma_4 = \frac{\E[(R_t - \mu)^4]}{\sigma^4}\). As \(T \to \infty\):

\[\sqrt{T} (\hSR - \SR) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(0, V_{\text{non-norm}}), \quad V_{\text{non-norm}} = 1 + \frac{1}{2} \SR^2 - \gamma_3 \SR + \frac{\gamma_4 - 3}{4} \SR^2\]

Proof sketch. The joint covariance matrix of \((\hat{\mu}, \hat{\sigma}^2)\) under arbitrary non-Gaussian distributions includes cross-terms and higher moments: \(\Cov(\hat{\mu}, \hat{\sigma}^2) = \frac{\E[(R_t - \mu)^3]}{T} = \frac{\sigma^3 \gamma_3}{T}\) and \(\Var(\hat{\sigma}^2) = \frac{\sigma^4 (\gamma_4 - 1)}{T}\). Applying the Delta Method with gradient \(\nabla g = [1/\sigma, -\mu/(2\sigma^3)]^\top\) yields:

\[V_{\text{non-norm}} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}(\sigma^2) + 2\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)\left(-\frac{\mu}{2\sigma^3}\right)(\sigma^3 \gamma_3) + \left(-\frac{\mu}{2\sigma^3}\right)^2 (\sigma^4 (\gamma_4 - 1)) = 1 - \frac{\mu}{\sigma} \gamma_3 + \frac{\mu^2}{4\sigma^2}(\gamma_4 - 1) = 1 - \gamma_3 \SR + \frac{1}{2} \SR^2 + \frac{\gamma_4 - 3}{4} \SR^2\]

When a strategy exhibits negative skewness (\(\gamma_3 < 0\)) or fat tails (\(\gamma_4 > 3\)), both terms strictly increase \(V_{\text{non-norm}}\), widening the confidence intervals and reducing statistical power.

3. The Statistics of Serial Correlation: The Lo (2002) Haircut

The standard industry convention for annualizing a monthly Sharpe ratio is multiplying by \(\sqrt{12}\): \(\SR_{\text{naive}} = \sqrt{12} \hSR\). As Lo (2002) proved, this convention is valid if and only if returns are serially independent. When returns exhibit serial persistence, \(\SR_{\text{naive}}\) is severely biased.

Let \(R_t\) denote a stationary return process with mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2\), and autocorrelation function \(\rho_k = \Corr(R_t, R_{t-k})\). Consider the \(q\)-period compounded return \(R_t(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{q-1} R_{t-k}\). The mean of the aggregated return is \(\E[R_t(q)] = q \mu\). The variance is:

\[\Var(R_t(q)) = \Var\left(\sum_{k=0}^{q-1} R_{t-k}\right) = q \sigma^2 + 2 \sigma^2 \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q - k) \rho_k = q \sigma^2 \left[1 + \frac{2}{q} \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q - k) \rho_k\right]\]

Definition 2.1 (Lo 2002 Time-Aggregation Factor) The time-aggregation adjustment factor \(\eta(q)\) for compounding across \(q\) periods is defined as:

\[\eta(q) \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{2}{q} \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q - k) \rho_k}} = \sqrt{\frac{q}{q + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q - k) \rho_k}}\]

Theorem 2.3 (Autocorrelation-Adjusted Sharpe Ratio) The true population \(q\)-period autocorrelation-adjusted Sharpe ratio \(\SR(q)\) satisfies:

\[\SR(q) = \frac{\E[R_t(q)]}{\sqrt{\Var(R_t(q))}} = \frac{q \mu}{\sqrt{q} \sigma \sqrt{1 + \frac{2}{q} \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q - k) \rho_k}} = \eta(q) \cdot \sqrt{q} \SR\]

Proof. Directly divide the expected \(q\)-period return \(q \mu\) by the square root of the multi-period variance \(\sigma(q) = \sqrt{q \sigma^2 [1 + \frac{2}{q} \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q-k)\rho_k]}\):

\[\SR(q) = \frac{q \mu}{\sqrt{q} \sigma \sqrt{1 + \frac{2}{q} \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q - k) \rho_k}} = \sqrt{q} \left(\frac{\mu}{\sigma}\right) \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{2}{q} \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q - k) \rho_k}} = \eta(q) \sqrt{q} \SR\]

When returns exhibit positive autocorrelation (\(\rho_k > 0\)), the cross-terms in the denominator are strictly positive, forcing \(\eta(q) < 1\). Multiplying by \(\sqrt{q}\) without the \(\eta(q)\) correction overstates the true Sharpe ratio by \(\frac{1}{\eta(q)} - 1\).

For an illiquid hedge fund with first-order autocorrelation \(\rho_1 = 0.50\) and geometric decay \(\rho_k = \rho_1^k\), \(\eta(12) = 0.6124\); a naive annualized Sharpe ratio of 2.0 corresponds to a true economic Sharpe ratio of only \(2.0 \times 0.6124 = 1.22\), an overstatement of 63.3%.

4. Robust Hypothesis Testing for Equal Sharpe Ratios

Allocators often compare two managers to decide whether Manager A's Sharpe ratio is statistically significantly superior to Manager B's. Let \(d = \SR_A - \SR_B\). Under the null hypothesis \(H_0: d = 0\), the classical Jobson & Korkie (1981) test assumes normality and independence, but as Ledoit & Wolf (2008) demonstrate, it suffers from severe size distortions when returns are fat-tailed or serially correlated.

Ledoit & Wolf introduce a studentized circular block bootstrap that constructs confidence intervals for \(\Delta = \mu_A/\sigma_A - \mu_B/\sigma_B\) without imposing parametric assumptions on marginal distributions or temporal dependence, providing asymptotically valid inference in institutional fund comparisons.

Lab 2 · Lo (2002) Autocorrelation Adjustment & Sharpe Asymptotics

Simulating an AR(1) return series with positive persistence (\(\phi = 0.40\)) will produce first-order autocorrelation \(\hat{\rho}_1 \approx 0.45\), resulting in \(\eta(12) \approx 0.81\) and deflating the naive annualized Sharpe ratio by over 20%.

"""Lab F7-L02-1: Lo (2002) Autocorrelation-Adjusted Sharpe Ratio and Asymptotics.

Demonstrates the distortion of naive sqrt(12) Sharpe ratio annualization under
positive return autocorrelation, computes sample autocorrelations rho_k, evaluates
the Lo (2002) time-aggregation adjustment factor eta(q), and derives asymptotic
confidence intervals for the Sharpe ratio.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
import scipy.stats


def run_lo_sharpe_analysis(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Compute naive and Lo (2002) autocorrelation-adjusted Sharpe ratios."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    burn_in = 60
    n_months = 240  # 20 years
    total_len = n_months + burn_in
    q_horizon = 12  # annualization horizon (12 months)

    # 1. Simulate stationary AR(1) return series with positive serial persistence:
    # r_t = c + phi * r_{t-1} + e_t
    phi_ar1 = 0.40
    innov_vol = 0.030
    target_mean = 0.008

    c_const = target_mean * (1.0 - phi_ar1)
    raw_returns = np.zeros(total_len)
    r_prev = target_mean
    for t in range(total_len):
        r_t = c_const + phi_ar1 * r_prev + rng.normal(0.0, innov_vol)
        raw_returns[t] = r_t
        r_prev = r_t

    returns = raw_returns[burn_in:]

    # 2. Sample statistics
    sample_mean = float(np.mean(returns))
    sample_std = float(np.std(returns, ddof=1))
    sr_monthly = sample_mean / sample_std

    # Naive annualization: SR_naive = sqrt(12) * SR_monthly
    sr_naive_ann = float(np.sqrt(q_horizon) * sr_monthly)

    # 3. Autocorrelation estimation: rho_k for k = 1, ..., q-1
    demeaned = returns - sample_mean
    var_denom = float(np.sum(demeaned**2))
    rho_estimates = np.zeros(q_horizon - 1)
    for k in range(1, q_horizon):
        cov_k = float(np.sum(demeaned[k:] * demeaned[:-k]))
        rho_estimates[k - 1] = cov_k / var_denom

    # 4. Lo (2002) time-aggregation correction factor eta(q):
    # eta(q) = sqrt( q / (q + 2 * sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q - k) * rho_k) )
    cross_terms_sum = 0.0
    for k in range(1, q_horizon):
        cross_terms_sum += 2.0 * (q_horizon - k) * rho_estimates[k - 1]

    denom_var_ratio = float(q_horizon + cross_terms_sum)
    eta_q = float(np.sqrt(float(q_horizon) / denom_var_ratio))

    # Autocorrelation-adjusted annualized Sharpe ratio
    sr_adjusted_ann = eta_q * sr_naive_ann

    # 5. Asymptotic standard error of monthly Sharpe ratio under Lo (2002)
    rho_sum_kernel = float(np.sum(rho_estimates[:4]))
    hac_inflation = float(np.sqrt(max(1.0 + 2.0 * rho_sum_kernel, 0.1)))
    se_sr_iid = float(np.sqrt((1.0 + 0.5 * (sr_monthly**2)) / float(n_months)))
    se_sr_hac = se_sr_iid * hac_inflation

    # 95% Confidence interval for adjusted annualized Sharpe ratio
    z_crit = float(scipy.stats.norm.ppf(0.975))
    ci_lower = float(sr_adjusted_ann - z_crit * se_sr_hac * np.sqrt(q_horizon) * eta_q)
    ci_upper = float(sr_adjusted_ann + z_crit * se_sr_hac * np.sqrt(q_horizon) * eta_q)

    pct_overstatement = float(((sr_naive_ann / sr_adjusted_ann) - 1.0) * 100.0)

    return {
        "sample_mean_ann": round(sample_mean * 12.0 * 100.0, 2),
        "sample_vol_ann": round(sample_std * np.sqrt(12.0) * 100.0, 2),
        "rho_1": round(float(rho_estimates[0]), 4),
        "rho_2": round(float(rho_estimates[1]), 4),
        "eta_q": round(eta_q, 4),
        "sr_naive_ann": round(sr_naive_ann, 4),
        "sr_adjusted_ann": round(sr_adjusted_ann, 4),
        "pct_overstatement": round(pct_overstatement, 2),
        "ci_lower": round(ci_lower, 4),
        "ci_upper": round(ci_upper, 4),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F7-L02-1 | Lo (2002) Autocorrelation-Adjusted Sharpe Ratio")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------")
    res = run_lo_sharpe_analysis(seed=42)
    print(f"Sample Annualized Mean:      {res['sample_mean_ann']:.2f}% (vol {res['sample_vol_ann']:.2f}%)")
    print(f"First-Order Autocorr (rho1): {res['rho_1']:+.4f} (rho2: {res['rho_2']:+.4f})")
    print(f"Lo (2002) eta(12) Factor:    {res['eta_q']:.4f}")
    print(f"Naive Annualized Sharpe:     {res['sr_naive_ann']:.4f}")
    print(f"Lo-Adjusted Annualized SR:   {res['sr_adjusted_ann']:.4f}")
    print(f"Naive Sharpe Overstatement:  +{res['pct_overstatement']:.2f}%")
    print(f"Adjusted 95% Conf Interval:  [{res['ci_lower']:.4f}, {res['ci_upper']:.4f}]")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The Sharpe ratio evaluates risk exclusively through the second central moment (variance). In hedge funds and derivatives strategies, this framework fails fundamentally:

  1. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio & Selection Bias: Bailey & Lopez de Prado (2014) show that when researchers test multiple strategy variations, the maximum observed Sharpe ratio grows with the number of trials even under pure noise. Evaluating a fund's Sharpe ratio without deflating for track record selection and backtest overfitting produces catastrophic out-of-sample disappointment.
  2. The Volatility Inversion Paradox: If a strategy intentionally sells deep out-of-the-money options, its sample variance over short periods can be artificially tiny, causing the sample Sharpe ratio (\(\hat{\mu}/\hat{\sigma}\)) to explode toward infinity right before a ruinous drawdown.

Misconceptions

  1. Misconception: Monthly Sharpe ratios always scale by the square root of 12

    Correction: The \(\sqrt{12}\) rule is an algebraic consequence of the i.i.d. assumption. When returns are serially correlated, multi-period variance contains covariance cross-terms, requiring multiplication by \(\eta(12) \sqrt{12}\). Ignoring \(\eta(12)\) distorts hedge fund rankings in favor of smoothed illiquid assets. Theorem 2.3 proves that \(\SR(q) = \eta(q) \sqrt{q} \SR\), with \(\eta(q) < 1\) whenever autocorrelation is positive.

  2. Misconception: A 3-year track record is sufficient to establish a fund's Sharpe ratio

    Correction: Over \(T = 36\) monthly observations, the Jobson–Korkie standard error for an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.0 is \(\se(\hSR) \approx \sqrt{\frac{1 + 0.5(1/12)}{36}} \times \sqrt{12} \approx 0.59\). A 95% confidence interval spans \([1.0 - 1.96(0.59), 1.0 + 1.96(0.59)] = [-0.16, 2.16]\), spanning negative returns to elite performance. Lo (2002) shows that standard errors remain wide even over 5 to 10 year samples.

  3. Misconception: Negative skewness has no effect on Sharpe ratio confidence intervals

    Correction: Negative skewness (\(\gamma_3 < 0\)) directly enters the asymptotic variance with a negative sign (\(-\gamma_3 \SR\)), strictly inflating the asymptotic sampling variance whenever the Sharpe ratio is positive. Theorem 2.2 proves that \(V_{\text{non-norm}} = 1 + \frac{1}{2}\SR^2 - \gamma_3 \SR + \frac{\gamma_4-3}{4}\SR^2\).

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A hedge fund reports a monthly sample mean excess return of 0.60% and a monthly sample standard deviation of 1.50% based on \(T = 120\) months of data. Under the assumption of i.i.d. Gaussian returns, compute the monthly Sharpe ratio, the naive annualized Sharpe ratio, and the Jobson–Korkie 95% confidence interval for the annualized Sharpe ratio.

Solution

The monthly Sharpe ratio is \(\hSR_m = 0.0060 / 0.0150 = 0.4000\). The naive annualized Sharpe ratio is \(\hSR_{\text{ann}} = \sqrt{12} \times 0.4000 \approx 3.4641 \times 0.4000 = 1.3856\). The asymptotic standard error of the monthly Sharpe ratio is \(\se(\hSR_m) = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 0.5(0.4000^2)}{120}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.08}{120}} = \sqrt{0.0090} \approx 0.09487\). The annualized standard error is \(\sqrt{12} \times 0.09487 \approx 0.3286\). The 95% confidence interval is \(1.3856 \pm 1.96 \times 0.3286 = [1.3856 - 0.6441, 1.3856 + 0.6441] = [0.7415, 2.0297]\).

An illiquid fund has monthly autocorrelation coefficients \(\hat{\rho}_1 = 0.40\), \(\hat{\rho}_2 = 0.20\), and \(\hat{\rho}_k = 0\) for \(k \ge 3\). Calculate the Lo (2002) time-aggregation correction factor \(\eta(12)\) for annual compounding (\(q = 12\)).

Solution

The formula for the cross-product sum is \(2 \sum_{k=1}^{11} (12 - k) \hat{\rho}_k\). For \(k = 1\), the term is \(2 \times (12 - 1) \times 0.40 = 2 \times 11 \times 0.40 = 8.80\). For \(k = 2\), the term is \(2 \times (12 - 2) \times 0.20 = 2 \times 10 \times 0.20 = 4.00\). For \(k \ge 3\), terms are 0. The sum of cross-terms is \(8.80 + 4.00 = 12.80\). The denominator is \(q + 12.80 = 12 + 12.80 = 24.80\). The Lo correction factor is \(\eta(12) = \sqrt{\frac{12}{24.80}} = \sqrt{0.48387} \approx 0.6956\).

If a manager's naive annualized Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_{\text{naive}} = 1.80\) and their Lo (2002) correction factor is \(\eta(12) = 0.6956\), compute the true autocorrelation-adjusted annualized Sharpe ratio and the percentage by which the naive figure overstates performance.

Solution

The true adjusted annualized Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_{\text{adj}} = \eta(12) \times \SR_{\text{naive}} = 0.6956 \times 1.80 = 1.2521\). The percentage overstatement is \(\frac{\SR_{\text{naive}} - \SR_{\text{adj}}}{\SR_{\text{adj}}} = \frac{1.80 - 1.2521}{1.2521} = \frac{0.5479}{1.2521} \approx 43.76\%\). The naive marketing metric exaggerates true risk-adjusted return by 43.8%.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for an AR(1) process with autoregressive parameter \(\phi \in (0, 1)\), the multi-period variance ratio \(\frac{\Var(R_t(q))}{q \sigma^2}\) simplifies in closed form to \(1 + \frac{2\phi}{1-\phi} \left(1 - \frac{1-\phi^q}{q(1-\phi)}\right)\), and establish that \(\lim_{q \to \infty} \eta(q) \sqrt{q} = \sqrt{\frac{1-\phi}{1+\phi}}\).

Solution

For AR(1), \(\rho_k = \phi^k\). The cross-product sum is \(S_q = \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} (q - k) \phi^k = q \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} \phi^k - \sum_{k=1}^{q-1} k \phi^k\). Using the geometric series identities \(\sum_{k=1}^{q-1} \phi^k = \frac{\phi(1 - \phi^{q-1})}{1 - \phi}\) and \(\sum_{k=1}^{q-1} k \phi^k = \frac{\phi - q \phi^q + (q - 1)\phi^{q+1}}{(1 - \phi)^2}\), straightforward algebraic reduction yields \(\frac{2}{q} S_q = \frac{2\phi}{1-\phi} \left(1 - \frac{1 - \phi^q}{q(1-\phi)}\right)\). As \(q \to \infty\), the term \(\frac{1 - \phi^q}{q(1-\phi)} \to 0\), so the variance ratio converges to \(1 + \frac{2\phi}{1-\phi} = \frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}\). Therefore, \(\eta(q) \to \sqrt{\frac{1-\phi}{1+\phi}}\), proving that the long-run annualized Sharpe ratio is scaled by the asymptotic factor \(\sqrt{\frac{1-\phi}{1+\phi}}\).

Prove that under negative skewness (\(\gamma_3 < 0\)) and positive population Sharpe ratio (\(\SR > 0\)), the Jobson–Korkie standard error strictly underestimates the true asymptotic standard error derived under Theorem 2.2.

Solution

From Theorem 2.2, \(V_{\text{non-norm}} = 1 + \frac{1}{2}\SR^2 - \gamma_3 \SR + \frac{\gamma_4 - 3}{4}\SR^2\). The Jobson–Korkie asymptotic variance is \(V_{\text{JK}} = 1 + \frac{1}{2}\SR^2\). The difference is \(V_{\text{non-norm}} - V_{\text{JK}} = -\gamma_3 \SR + \frac{\gamma_4 - 3}{4}\SR^2\). If the return distribution has negative skewness (\(\gamma_3 < 0\)) and positive excess return (\(\SR > 0\)), the product \(-\gamma_3 \SR\) is strictly positive (\(-\gamma_3 \SR > 0\)). Furthermore, financial return distributions typically exhibit excess kurtosis (\(\gamma_4 > 3\)), so \(\frac{\gamma_4 - 3}{4}\SR^2 \ge 0\). Consequently, \(V_{\text{non-norm}} > V_{\text{JK}}\). The Jobson–Korkie variance strictly underestimates the true sampling variance.

Prove that the sample Sharpe ratio \(\hSR\) is a biased estimator of \(\SR\) in finite samples under normality, and derive the exact finite-sample bias correction factor \(C(T) = \frac{\sqrt{2} \Gamma(T/2)}{\sqrt{T-1} \Gamma((T-1)/2)}\).

Solution

Let \(R_t \sim \iid \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\). The sample mean \(\hat{\mu} \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2/T)\) is independent of the sample variance \(s^2 = \frac{1}{T-1} \sum (R_t - \bar{R})^2\). The statistic \(\frac{(T-1) s^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2(T-1)\). Thus \(\hSR = \frac{\hat{\mu}}{s} = \frac{\hat{\mu}}{\sigma} \times \frac{\sigma}{s}\). Taking expectations: \(\E[\hSR] = \E\left[\frac{\hat{\mu}}{\sigma}\right] \times \E\left[\frac{\sigma}{s}\right] = \SR \times \E\left[\left(\frac{\chi^2(T-1)}{T-1}\right)^{-1/2}\right]\). For a chi-square variable \(W \sim \chi^2(\nu)\), \(\E[W^{-1/2}] = \frac{\sqrt{2} \Gamma((\nu-1)/2)}{2 \Gamma(\nu/2)}\). Setting \(\nu = T-1\) and simplifying yields \(\E[\hSR] = \SR \times \frac{\sqrt{T-1} \Gamma((T-2)/2)}{\sqrt{2} \Gamma((T-1)/2)} > \SR\). Inverting this expectation yields the exact finite-sample unbiasing multiplier \(C(T)\), which shrinks the sample Sharpe ratio downward toward the population value.

Tier 3 · Empirical

A quantitative market-making fund trades daily with mean daily return 0.08% and daily standard deviation 0.40%. Daily returns exhibit negative serial correlation \(\hat{\rho}_1 = -0.15\) due to bid-ask bounce. For a 21-day monthly horizon (\(q = 21\)), calculate the Lo time-aggregation factor \(\eta(21)\) assuming \(\hat{\rho}_k = 0\) for \(k \ge 2\). Does naive scaling understate or overstate monthly risk?

Solution

For \(q = 21\) with \(\hat{\rho}_1 = -0.15\), the cross-term is \(2 \times (21 - 1) \times (-0.15) = 2 \times 20 \times (-0.15) = -6.00\). The denominator is \(q + (-6.00) = 21 - 6.00 = 15.00\). The Lo factor is \(\eta(21) = \sqrt{\frac{21}{15.00}} = \sqrt{1.40} \approx 1.1832\). Because \(\eta(21) > 1\), negative serial correlation means daily variances mean-revert, reducing 21-day multi-period variance below \(21 \sigma^2\). Naive \(\sqrt{21}\) scaling overstates multi-period variance and understates the monthly Sharpe ratio by approximately 15.5%.

Two hedge funds are compared over \(T = 120\) months. Fund A has \(\hSR_A = 0.35\) (\(\SR_{\text{ann}} = 1.21\)) and Fund B has \(\hSR_B = 0.25\) (\(\SR_{\text{ann}} = 0.87\)). The correlation between their monthly returns is \(r = 0.60\). Under the Jobson–Korkie test, compute the test statistic \(z = \frac{\hSR_A - \hSR_B}{\sqrt{\operatorname{AsyVar}(\hSR_A - \hSR_B)}}\), where:

\[\operatorname{AsyVar}(\hSR_A - \hSR_B) = \frac{1}{T} \left[2(1 - r) + \frac{1}{2}(\SR_A^2 + \SR_B^2 - 2 r^2 \SR_A \SR_B)\right]\]
Solution

The difference in monthly Sharpe ratios is \(d = 0.35 - 0.25 = 0.10\). In the asymptotic variance formula, \(2(1 - r) = 2(1 - 0.60) = 0.80\). The second term evaluates to:

\[\frac{1}{2} [0.35^2 + 0.25^2 - 2(0.60^2)(0.35)(0.25)] = \frac{1}{2} [0.1850 - 0.0630] = 0.0610\]

The bracketed sum is \(0.80 + 0.0610 = 0.8610\). The asymptotic variance is \(\frac{0.8610}{120} \approx 0.007175\), giving standard error \(\sqrt{0.007175} \approx 0.0847\). The test statistic is \(z = \frac{0.10}{0.0847} \approx 1.18\). With \(p \approx 0.238\), the 0.34 difference in annualized Sharpe ratios is not statistically significant.

A short-volatility strategy has monthly sample skewness \(\hat{\gamma}_3 = -2.5\) and kurtosis \(\hat{\gamma}_4 = 12.0\). Its monthly Sharpe ratio is \(\hSR = 0.30\) over \(T = 180\) months. Calculate the non-normal asymptotic variance factor \(V_{\text{non-norm}}\) and compare the resulting standard error to the Gaussian Jobson–Korkie standard error.

Solution

Under normality, \(V_{\text{JK}} = 1 + 0.5(0.30^2) = 1 + 0.045 = 1.045\), yielding standard error \(\se_{\text{JK}} = \sqrt{1.045 / 180} \approx 0.0762\). Under the non-normal formula: \(V_{\text{non-norm}} = 1 + 0.5(0.30^2) - (-2.5)(0.30) + \frac{12.0 - 3}{4}(0.30^2) = 1.045 + 0.750 + \frac{9.0}{4}(0.090) = 1.045 + 0.750 + 0.2025 = 1.9975\). The non-normal standard error is \(\se_{\text{non-norm}} = \sqrt{1.9975 / 180} \approx 0.1053\). Accounting for skewness and fat tails inflates the standard error by \(\frac{0.1053}{0.0762} - 1 \approx 38.2\%\).

Tier 4 · Research

How does Bailey & Lopez de Prado's (2014) Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) adjust for backtest overfitting when an algorithmic fund selects the best performing strategy out of \(N\) independent parameter trials?

Solution

When selecting the best strategy among \(N\) trials, extreme value theory dictates that the expected maximum Sharpe ratio under the null hypothesis of zero true skill is \(\E[\max_n \hSR_n] \approx \sqrt{2 \ln N} + \frac{\gamma^*}{\sqrt{2 \ln N}}\), where \(\gamma^* \approx 0.5772\) is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio computes the probability that the observed maximum Sharpe ratio exceeds this selection-inflated threshold while adjusting for track record length, skewness, and kurtosis. If an observed Sharpe ratio fails to clear the selection hurdle, it is rejected as a statistical artifact of backtest data snooping.

Why does the presence of illiquidity and appraisal smoothing in private equity funds invalidate standard mean-variance portfolio optimization when naive Sharpe ratios are inputted?

Solution

Mean-variance optimization allocates capital proportional to Sharpe ratios and inversely proportional to covariances. Appraisal smoothing artificially suppresses reported volatility and dampens reported correlations with public markets. Inputting unadjusted metrics causes the optimizer to treat private equity as a near-riskless asset with high Sharpe ratio and zero correlation, allocating excessive capital to private illiquid assets. When true multi-period variance and covariance are restored via Lo adjustments or unsmoothing, optimal private market weights drop significantly.

Critique the use of the Sortino ratio as an alternative to the Sharpe ratio for evaluating hedge funds with asymmetric option payoffs. What estimation hazards arise?

Solution

The Sortino ratio replaces total standard deviation with downside semi-deviation below a minimum acceptable return. While theoretically appealing for asymmetric payoffs, downside semi-deviation discards all observations above the target, severely reducing the effective sample size available for estimating risk. In a sample of 60 monthly returns, a strategy may have only 10 to 15 downside observations, leading to massive sampling variance in the denominator. A single large tail loss can cause the Sortino ratio to collapse, while an unrepresentative calm window produces an artificially explosive ratio.
