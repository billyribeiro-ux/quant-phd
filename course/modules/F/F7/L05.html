Nonlinear Payoffs, Option Exposures & Manager Due Diligence

Classical performance evaluation presumes linear relationships between portfolio returns and asset pricing factors. However, modern hedge fund strategies dynamically trade options, execute trend-following rules, or harvest volatility risk premia, creating fundamentally nonlinear payoff profiles. As Fung & Hsieh (2001) established, trend-following CTAs behave like lookback straddles, while carry and short volatility strategies exhibit concave negative gamma masquerading as high linear alpha. Evaluating nonlinear managers with linear models leads to severe misattribution of skill. Furthermore, superior investment returns are worthless if operational risk, fraud, or survivorship bias corrupts the track record. Rigorous quantitative due diligence bridges nonlinear financial econometrics with forensic audit verification to identify genuine, sustainable investment skill.

Linear factor models fail when evaluating active hedge funds that dynamically alter market exposure or sell volatility. In this capstone lesson, we formalize nonlinear payoff exposures, derive the Fung & Hsieh (2001) lookback straddle and Treynor-Mazuy timing models, expose pseudo-alpha generated by short volatility carry traps, and establish comprehensive quantitative and operational manager due diligence protocols.

1. Nonlinear Payoff Exposures and Due Diligence

Traditional asset pricing models (CAPM, Fama-French, Carhart) assume fund returns are linear combinations of systematic factor returns. However, dynamic trading strategies inherently generate curved, asymmetric return distributions.

Definition 5.1 (Nonlinear Payoff Exposure) A nonlinear payoff exposure is an empirical or structural relationship between fund excess returns \(R_t\) and underlying market risk factors \(M_t\) exhibiting nonzero higher-order derivatives (\(\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 \E[R|M]}{\mathrm{d} M^2} \neq 0\)), asymmetric state-dependent betas, or option-like payoff profiles.

Nonlinear exposures fall into two polar archetypes:

  • Convex Strategies (\(\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 R}{\mathrm{d} M^2} > 0\), Positive Gamma): Commodity Trading Advisors (CTAs) and trend followers that purchase options or deploy dynamic stop-loss rules. They gain during large directional market moves (rallies or crashes) while bleeding time decay (theta) during range-bound regimes.
  • Concave Strategies (\(\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 R}{\mathrm{d} M^2} < 0\), Negative Gamma): Short volatility funds, credit carry strategies, and merger arbitrage. They collect steady positive premiums during quiet regimes but experience severe left-tail drawdowns when market volatility spikes.

Definition 5.2 (Manager Due Diligence) Manager due diligence is the systematic quantitative, forensic, and operational investigation of an investment manager's track record, risk architecture, fee structures, counterparty relationships, and governance to confirm that past performance is attributable to genuine skill rather than unmodeled tail risk, database biases, or fraud.

2. Fung & Hsieh (2001) Trend-Following Lookback Straddles

In their seminal study, Fung & Hsieh (2001) demonstrated that trend-following hedge funds do not generate standard equity or bond factor exposures. Instead, trend-following CTAs exhibit payoff profiles closely matching portfolios of lookback straddles.

The Lookback Straddle Payoff

A lookback call option grants the holder the right to buy at the period minimum price \(S_{\min}\), and a lookback put grants the right to sell at the period maximum price \(S_{\max}\). A lookback straddle delivers the payoff:

\[ \Pi_T = (S_T - S_{\min}) + (S_{\max} - S_T) = S_{\max} - S_{\min} \]

The lookback straddle captures the entire high-to-low range of the market over the investment horizon, guaranteeing large gains whenever the underlying asset experiences a sustained trend in either direction.

Fung & Hsieh constructed five primitive trend-following factor portfolios (PTFS) across:

  1. Stock index lookback straddles
  2. Bond lookback straddles
  3. Short-term interest rate lookback straddles
  4. Currency lookback straddles
  5. Commodity lookback straddles

Regressing trend-following CTA returns on these five lookback straddle factors explains up to \(50\%\) of monthly return variation (\(R^2 \approx 0.45\text{--}0.50\)), whereas standard linear equity/bond benchmarks produce an \(R^2\) under \(0.05\). When lookback straddle factors are included, the apparent linear "alpha" of CTAs vanishes, proving that their returns represent systematic compensation for providing market convexity.

3. Market Timing & Curvature Models

When evaluating discretionary or tactical asset allocators who adjust market beta dynamically, econometricians deploy curvature models.

1. The Treynor & Mazuy (1966) Quadratic Model

Suppose a manager possesses market timing ability, systematically increasing beta \(\beta_t\) ahead of bull markets and reducing beta ahead of bear markets: \(\beta_t = \beta + \gamma M_t\), where \(M_t = R_{m,t} - R_{f,t}\). Substituting into the CAPM specification yields:

\[ R_t - R_{f,t} = \alpha + \beta M_t + \gamma M_t^2 + \epsilon_t \]

Theorem 5.1 (Treynor-Mazuy Timing Curvature) In the quadratic regression \(R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i M_t + \gamma_i M_t^2 + \epsilon_{i,t}\):

  • \(\gamma_i > 0\) indicates positive market timing skill (convex payoff / positive gamma).
  • \(\gamma_i = 0\) indicates no market timing ability (linear factor exposure).
  • \(\gamma_i < 0\) indicates market timing failure or unhedged short option / carry exposures (concave payoff / negative gamma).
  • The intercept \(\alpha_i\) represents option-adjusted selectivity alpha after controlling for systematic market convexity.

2. The Henriksson & Merton (1981) Segmented Beta Model

Henriksson and Merton model a market timer who takes one of two discrete beta postures depending on whether the market excess return is positive or negative:

\[ \beta_t = \begin{cases} \beta_1 & \text{if } M_t > 0 \\ \beta_2 & \text{if } M_t \le 0 \end{cases} \]

This is equivalent to holding a base portfolio with beta \(\beta_1\) plus a synthetic put option on the market:

\[ R_t - R_{f,t} = \alpha + \beta_1 M_t + \gamma \max(0, -M_t) + \epsilon_t \]

A successful market timer exhibits \(\gamma > 0\), providing downside protection while participating in upside rallies.

4. The Short Volatility Carry Trap

A classic pitfall in quantitative performance evaluation is the pseudo-alpha generated by short volatility and carry strategies.

Consider a manager who systematically sells out-of-the-money index put options each month. Under typical market conditions (volatility is low and equity markets drift upward), the options expire worthless. The manager books steady monthly income:

\[ R_t \approx +1.0\% \text{ to } +1.5\% \text{ per month}, \quad \sigma_R \approx 3\% \text{ to } 5\% \text{ annualized} \]

Because market crashes occur rarely (once every 5 to 10 years), a standard linear CAPM regression over a 3-year or 5-year sample window produces startling metrics:

  • Apparent CAPM \(\hat{\alpha} = +10\%\) to \(+15\%\) annualized (\(t\text{-stat} > 3.0\)).
  • Apparent market beta \(\hat{\beta} \approx 0.20\).
  • Apparent Sharpe ratio \(SR > 1.80\).

The Pseudo-Alpha Anatomy

This "alpha" is completely spurious. The manager is not generating abnormal risk-adjusted returns; rather, the manager is acting as an unhedged catastrophe insurance underwriter. When evaluated under the Treynor-Mazuy model, the manager's curvature coefficient is deeply negative (\(\gamma \ll 0\), \(t_\gamma < -5.0\)), exposing severe negative gamma. When an extreme market shock hits (e.g., 1987, 2008, or March 2020), the portfolio suffers an instantaneous \(50\%\) to \(100\%\) loss, wiping out years of accumulated premiums.

5. Quantitative Operational Due Diligence (ODD) & Forensic Screening

Institutional allocators must conduct two distinct diligence tracks: Investment Due Diligence (IDD) (evaluating investment thesis, process, and econometrics) and Operational Due Diligence (ODD) (evaluating governance, custody, accounting, and fraud risk).

1. Commercial Database Biases

Hedge fund databases (e.g., Lipper TASS, HFR, Morningstar) suffer from severe survivorship and reporting biases:

  • Survivorship Bias: Defunct or liquidated funds are dropped from active databases. Fung & Hsieh (2001) document that survivorship bias inflates average database returns by \(1.5\%\) to \(3.0\%\) per year.
  • Backfill / Instant-History Bias: When a manager joins a database, commercial vendors backfill the fund's historical track record prior to listing. Managers only choose to list after an exceptional incubation run, inflating backfilled returns by \(2.0\%\) to \(4.0\%\) per year.
  • Selection Bias: Reporting to commercial databases is voluntary. Top-tier funds closed to outside capital do not report, while underperforming funds stop reporting before formal closure.

2. Forensic Red Flags for Discretionary Misreporting & Fraud

Quantitative forensic screening uncovers warning signs before allocators suffer operational losses:

  1. Discontinuity at Zero: Bollen & Pool (2009) discovered that fraudulent and misreporting funds exhibit an abnormal spike in small positive returns (\(0.0\% < R_t < 0.2\%\)) and an unnatural deficit of small negative returns (\(-0.2\% < R_t < 0.0\%\)), indicating deliberate loss concealment.
  2. Discretionary NAV Padding vs. Illiquidity: Jorion & Schwarz (2014) developed econometric tests to differentiate between structural illiquidity and deliberate return smoothing, confirming that serial correlation alone does not prove fraud unless combined with cross-sectional price discrepancies.
  3. Service Provider Turnover: Abrupt changes in independent prime brokers, custodians, or auditing firms (or using an obscure, single-partner local accounting firm) are prime operational red flags (e.g., Madoff, Bayou).
  4. Custody and Related-Party Conflicts: Prime broker assets held without third-party custodian segregation, or affiliated broker-dealers executing fund transactions.

3. Capacity Constraints and Style Drift

Following Berk & Green (2004) and Berk & van Binsbergen (2015), competitive capital markets drive net investor alpha toward zero as successful managers attract excessive Assets Under Management (AUM). Beyond strategy capacity, managers experience diminishing returns, higher transaction costs, and "style drift"—migrating into more liquid, crowded macro trades that dilute original edge.

Lab 5 · Fung-Hsieh Nonlinear Payoff Detection & Option-Adjusted Alpha

Evaluating trend-following and short-volatility strategies under classical linear CAPM will erroneously credit option-like market exposures as managerial skill, whereas quadratic Treynor-Mazuy regressions will isolate true curvature and option-adjusted alpha.

"""Lab F7-L05-1: Fung-Hsieh Nonlinear Payoff Detection and Option-Adjusted Alpha.

Demonstrates how nonlinear option-like strategies (trend-following straddles vs.
systematic put writing) distort classical linear CAPM alpha. Implements the
Treynor-Mazuy / Fung-Hsieh quadratic payoff model to extract option convexity
gamma and recover option-adjusted alpha.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def run_nonlinear_payoff_analysis(seed: int = 42) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Evaluate linear vs nonlinear option-like payoffs for two alternative managers."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_months = 180  # 15 years

    # 1. Market excess return (mean = 0.70% / mo, vol = 4.5% / mo)
    mkt = rng.normal(0.0070, 0.0450, size=n_months)
    mkt_demeaned_sq = (mkt - np.mean(mkt)) ** 2

    # 2. Simulate Manager A: Trend Follower / CTA (Convex Payoff / Long Straddle)
    # Generates convex gains in extreme market moves, true alpha = 0.20% / mo
    gamma_cta_true = 4.50
    alpha_cta_true = 0.0020
    r_cta = alpha_cta_true + 0.10 * mkt + gamma_cta_true * mkt_demeaned_sq + rng.normal(0.0, 0.020, size=n_months)

    # 3. Simulate Manager B: Systematic Put Seller / Carry (Concave Payoff / Short Volatility)
    # Collects regular option premium (1.10% / mo) but suffers in large market moves (-5.50 * mkt^2).
    # True skill alpha is zero!
    gamma_put_true = -5.50
    premium_put = 0.0110
    alpha_put_true = 0.0000
    r_put = (
        alpha_put_true
        + premium_put
        + 0.40 * mkt
        + gamma_put_true * mkt_demeaned_sq
        + rng.normal(0.0, 0.015, size=n_months)
    )

    # Regression helper
    def estimate_regressions(
        r_series: np.ndarray,
    ) -> tuple[float, float, float, float, float]:
        ones = np.ones((n_months, 1))

        # (a) Linear CAPM regression: r = alpha + beta * mkt
        x_lin = np.column_stack([ones, mkt])
        b_lin = np.linalg.lstsq(x_lin, r_series, rcond=None)[0]
        e_lin = r_series - x_lin @ b_lin
        s2_lin = float(np.sum(e_lin**2) / (n_months - 2))
        se_lin = np.sqrt(np.diag(np.linalg.inv(x_lin.T @ x_lin) * s2_lin))
        alpha_lin_ann = float(b_lin[0] * 12.0 * 100.0)
        t_alpha_lin = float(b_lin[0] / se_lin[0])

        # (b) Quadratic Treynor-Mazuy / Fung-Hsieh: r = alpha* + beta * mkt + gamma * mkt^2
        x_quad = np.column_stack([ones, mkt, mkt_demeaned_sq])
        b_quad = np.linalg.lstsq(x_quad, r_series, rcond=None)[0]
        e_quad = r_series - x_quad @ b_quad
        s2_quad = float(np.sum(e_quad**2) / (n_months - 3))
        se_quad = np.sqrt(np.diag(np.linalg.inv(x_quad.T @ x_quad) * s2_quad))
        alpha_quad_ann = float(b_quad[0] * 12.0 * 100.0)
        gamma_hat = float(b_quad[2])
        t_gamma = float(b_quad[2] / se_quad[2])

        return alpha_lin_ann, t_alpha_lin, alpha_quad_ann, gamma_hat, t_gamma

    (
        cta_a_lin,
        cta_t_lin,
        cta_a_quad,
        cta_gamma,
        cta_t_gamma,
    ) = estimate_regressions(r_cta)

    (
        put_a_lin,
        put_t_lin,
        put_a_quad,
        put_gamma,
        put_t_gamma,
    ) = estimate_regressions(r_put)

    return {
        "cta_alpha_lin": round(cta_a_lin, 2),
        "cta_t_lin": round(cta_t_lin, 2),
        "cta_alpha_quad": round(cta_a_quad, 2),
        "cta_gamma": round(cta_gamma, 2),
        "cta_t_gamma": round(cta_t_gamma, 2),
        "put_alpha_lin": round(put_a_lin, 2),
        "put_t_lin": round(put_t_lin, 2),
        "put_alpha_quad": round(put_a_quad, 2),
        "put_gamma": round(put_gamma, 2),
        "put_t_gamma": round(put_t_gamma, 2),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("Lab F7-L05-1 | Fung-Hsieh Nonlinear Payoff Detection")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------")
    res = run_nonlinear_payoff_analysis(seed=42)
    print("Manager A (Trend Follower / CTA):")
    print(f"  Linear CAPM Alpha:         {res['cta_alpha_lin']:+.2f}% / yr (t = {res['cta_t_lin']:+.2f})")
    print(f"  Quadratic Convexity gamma: {res['cta_gamma']:+.2f} (t = {res['cta_t_gamma']:+.2f}) -> Convexity")
    print(f"  Option-Adjusted Alpha:     {res['cta_alpha_quad']:+.2f}% / yr")
    print("Manager B (Put Seller / Short Volatility):")
    print(f"  Linear CAPM Alpha:         {res['put_alpha_lin']:+.2f}% / yr (t = {res['put_t_lin']:+.2f}) -> 'Alpha'")
    print(f"  Quadratic Concavity gamma: {res['put_gamma']:+.2f} (t = {res['put_t_gamma']:+.2f}) -> Negative Gamma")
    print(f"  Zero-Vol Regime Intercept: {res['put_alpha_quad']:+.2f}% / yr (Unhedged option premium)")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Canon Break 5.1: High Linear Alpha Can Be 100% Option Carry

A common institutional belief is that a fund with \(t(\hat{\alpha}) > 3.0\) and \(R^2 < 0.20\) possesses undisputed stock-picking skill. In reality, an unhedged out-of-the-money put-writing strategy routinely produces \(t\)-statistics exceeding 3.0 in ordinary market years while holding zero genuine skill. Without testing for quadratic curvature (\(\gamma\)) or regressing on option straddle factors, linear alpha is economically meaningless for derivative and carry strategies.

Canon Break 5.2: Top Track Records Are Systematically Corrupted by Incubation Bias

Hedge fund management companies typically launch 10 to 20 small internal "incubation" strategies simultaneously with proprietary seed capital. After two years, the 18 strategies that underperformed are quietly shut down without public record, while the 2 top-performing strategies are marketed to institutional investors with backfilled historical track records. An allocator viewing the surviving track record is observing an extreme right-tail order statistic, not representative managerial skill.

Misconceptions

  1. Misconception: A zero or near-zero correlation with the S&P 500 proves that a hedge fund is truly market neutral.

    Correction: False. A dynamic straddle or trend-following strategy has approximately zero linear correlation because it profits during both steep rallies and severe crashes. Similarly, a short volatility fund has near-zero linear correlation in calm months. Both harbor massive nonlinear market exposures that manifest during volatility spikes.

  2. Misconception: Operational due diligence is merely a qualitative legal and compliance check separate from risk management.

    Correction: False. Historical empirical audits establish that over 50% of institutional capital losses in hedge funds stem from operational failures (fraud, unauthorized trading, misrepresentation of NAV, commingling of assets) rather than investment strategy underperformance.

  3. Misconception: Fung & Hsieh (2001) lookback straddles require the fund manager to actively trade exotic lookback options.

    Correction: False. Lookback options are illiquid and rarely traded directly. Fung & Hsieh (2001) proved that a simple dynamic trend-following trading rule (buying as prices rise, shorting as prices fall) mathematically replicates the convex payoff profile of a lookback straddle.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 5.1 (Treynor-Mazuy Timing Curvature) An equity long/short fund is evaluated over 120 months using the Treynor-Mazuy model: \[ R_{i,t} - R_{f,t} = 0.0025 + 0.45 (R_{m,t} - R_{f,t}) + 3.20 (R_{m,t} - R_{f,t})^2 + e_t \] Calculate the fund's effective market beta when the market excess return is (a) \(-8.0\%\), (b) \(0.0\%\), and (c) \(+8.0\%\). Does this manager exhibit positive or negative timing skill?

Solution

The effective marginal beta is the derivative of expected fund return with respect to market excess return \(M_t\):

\[ \beta_{\text{eff}}(M_t) = \frac{\mathrm{d} \E[R_i|M]}{\mathrm{d} M} = \beta + 2 \gamma M_t = 0.45 + 2(3.20) M_t = 0.45 + 6.40 M_t \]
  1. When \(M_t = -0.08\): \(\beta_{\text{eff}} = 0.45 + 6.40(-0.08) = 0.45 - 0.512 = -0.062\). In a severe bear market, the fund effectively becomes net short.
  2. When \(M_t = 0.00\): \(\beta_{\text{eff}} = 0.45 + 6.40(0.00) = 0.45\). In a neutral market, the fund holds a base beta of 0.45.
  3. When \(M_t = +0.08\): \(\beta_{\text{eff}} = 0.45 + 6.40(+0.08) = 0.45 + 0.512 = +0.962\). In a strong bull market, the fund nearly doubles its market exposure to participate in the rally.

Because \(\gamma = +3.20 > 0\), the manager demonstrates positive market timing ability (convex payoff profile).

Problem 5.2 (Henriksson-Merton Segmented Beta Evaluation) An absolute return manager exhibits the following Henriksson-Merton regression estimates: \[ R_{i,t} - R_{f,t} = 0.0010 + 0.70 M_t - 0.40 \max(0, -M_t) + e_t \] Determine the fund's beta in up-markets versus down-markets. What type of trading strategy produces this profile?

Solution
  1. In up-markets (\(M_t > 0\)): \(\max(0, -M_t) = 0\), so \(\beta_{\text{up}} = 0.70\).
  2. In down-markets (\(M_t < 0\)): \(\max(0, -M_t) = -M_t\). Substituting gives: \[ \E[R_i - R_f] = 0.0010 + 0.70 M_t - 0.40(-M_t) = 0.0010 + 1.10 M_t \] Hence \(\beta_{\text{down}} = 1.10\).

The fund has a beta of \(0.70\) when the market rises, but its beta expands to \(1.10\) when the market falls! This is a concave, short option / short volatility profile where downside losses are magnified relative to upside participation.

Problem 5.3 (Lookback Straddle Payoff Calculation) Over a 3-month quarter, the price path of 10-year Treasury bond futures is \(S_0 = 100\), \(S_1 = 94\), \(S_2 = 106\), \(S_3 = 102\). Calculate the terminal payoff of a primitive trend-following lookback straddle established at \(t=0\) and expiring at \(t=3\).

Solution

From the price series \(\{100, 94, 106, 102\}\):

  • The minimum observed price is \(S_{\min} = 94\).
  • The maximum observed price is \(S_{\max} = 106\).
  • The terminal price is \(S_3 = 102\).

Using the Fung & Hsieh (2001) lookback straddle payoff formula:

\[ \Pi = (S_3 - S_{\min}) + (S_{\max} - S_3) = S_{\max} - S_{\min} = 106 - 94 = 12 \text{ points} \]

The lookback straddle captures the entire 12-point peak-to-trough range of the bond futures market, regardless of where the price ended at terminal date \(t=3\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 5.4 (Spurious Alpha from Omitted Option Convexity) Suppose a fund's true return generating process is quadratic: \(R_t = \alpha + \beta M_t + \gamma (M_t^2 - \sigma_m^2) + \epsilon_t\), where \(\E[M_t] = \mu_m\), \(\Var(M_t) = \sigma_m^2\), and \(\operatorname{Skew}(M_t) = s_m = \E[(M_t - \mu_m)^3]/\sigma_m^3\). Derive the analytical expression for the omitted variable bias in the estimated linear CAPM alpha \(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{linear}}\) when an econometrician omits the quadratic term.

Solution

Let the true model be \(\mathbf{y} = \alpha \mathbf{1} + \beta \mathbf{x} + \gamma \mathbf{z} + \mathbf{\epsilon}\), where \(x_t = M_t\) and \(z_t = M_t^2 - \sigma_m^2\). By standard omitted variable theory, the estimated coefficient vector in a regression of \(\mathbf{y}\) on \([\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x}]\) has expectation:

\[ \E\begin{bmatrix} \hat{\alpha}_{\text{lin}} \\ \hat{\beta}_{\text{lin}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{bmatrix} + \gamma (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{z} \]

For large \(T\), the sample moments converge to population moments. The regression of \(z_t = M_t^2 - \sigma_m^2\) on \(M_t\) yields slope:

\[ b_{z,x} = \frac{\Cov(M_t^2, M_t)}{\Var(M_t)} = \frac{\E[(M_t - \mu_m)^3] + 2\mu_m \sigma_m^2}{\sigma_m^2} = \sigma_m s_m + 2\mu_m \]

The omitted bias in the intercept \(\alpha\) is:

\[ \text{Bias}(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{lin}}) = \gamma \left( \E[z_t] - b_{z,x} \E[M_t] \right) = \gamma \left( 0 - ( \sigma_m s_m + 2\mu_m ) \mu_m \right) = -\gamma \mu_m (\sigma_m s_m + 2\mu_m) \]

In addition, if market returns are symmetric (\(s_m \approx 0\)), \(\text{Bias}(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{lin}}) = -2\gamma \mu_m^2\). If the true strategy is concave (selling options, \(\gamma < 0\)), the omitted term contributes a positive bias \(-2(-|\gamma|)\mu_m^2 > 0\), creating an artificial positive alpha.

Problem 5.5 (Equivalence of Dynamic Trend-Following to Synthetic Straddle) Using Ito's lemma and continuous delta-hedging arguments, prove that an automated momentum rule maintaining equity exposure proportional to cumulative past price changes generates positive option gamma (\(\Gamma > 0\)).

Solution

Let the underlying asset price follow \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t\). Consider an automated momentum rule where the portfolio's dollar holding in the asset is \(\Delta(S_t)\), with \(\Delta'(S) = \frac{\mathrm{d} \Delta}{\mathrm{d} S} > 0\) (exposure increases as prices rise, decreases as prices fall).

The instantaneous portfolio wealth change is \(dW_t = \Delta(S_t) dS_t + (W_t - \Delta(S_t) S_t) r dt\). By Taylor expansion / Ito's formula for a nonlinear payoff \(V(S_t)\) whose delta is \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = \Delta(S_t)\):

\[ dV(S_t) = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} dS_t + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} (dS_t)^2 = \Delta(S_t) dS_t + \frac{1}{2} \Delta'(S_t) \sigma^2 S_t^2 dt \]

Because \(\Delta'(S_t) > 0\), the second derivative (gamma) is strictly positive:

\[ \Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = \Delta'(S_t) > 0 \]

The term \(\frac{1}{2} \Gamma \sigma^2 S^2 dt > 0\) is the continuous convexity harvest: every time the asset fluctuates, the dynamic adjustment captures positive returns. This proves that continuous trend following is mathematically equivalent to holding a long gamma option straddle position.

Problem 5.6 (Survivorship Bias Derivation) Suppose the universe of hedge funds at \(t=0\) consists of \(N\) funds with true returns \(R_{i} \sim \text{i.i.d.} \, \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\). A fund liquidates and disappears from the database at \(t=1\) if its return falls below threshold \(c\). Prove that the observed sample mean return \(\bar{R}_{\text{obs}}\) of surviving funds is strictly upward biased and derive the exact bias in terms of the Mills ratio.

Solution

The observed sample contains only funds satisfying \(R_i \ge c\). The conditional expectation of a truncated normal distribution is:

\[ \E[R_i \mid R_i \ge c] = \mu + \sigma \lambda(\alpha_c), \quad \text{where } \alpha_c = \frac{c - \mu}{\sigma}, \quad \lambda(\alpha_c) = \frac{\phi(\alpha_c)}{1 - \Phi(\alpha_c)} \]

where \(\phi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal PDF, \(\Phi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal CDF, and \(\lambda(\alpha_c)\) is the inverse Mills ratio.

Because \(\phi(\alpha_c) > 0\) and \(1 - \Phi(\alpha_c) > 0\) for all finite \(c\), the inverse Mills ratio \(\lambda(\alpha_c)\) is strictly positive. Since \(\sigma > 0\):

\[ \text{Bias} = \E[R_i \mid R_i \ge c] - \mu = \sigma \frac{\phi((c-\mu)/\sigma)}{1 - \Phi((c-\mu)/\sigma)} > 0 \]

This proves that the observed mean return in commercial databases that exclude liquidated funds is strictly upward biased.

Problem 5.8 (Bollen & Pool Discontinuity Test) An allocator audits the monthly returns of a multi-strategy fund over 120 months. In the narrow bandwidth \([-0.25\%, +0.25\%]\), the allocator observes 28 returns in \([0.00\%, +0.25\%]\) and only 4 returns in \([-0.25\%, 0.00\%)\). Conduct a binomial sign test of the null hypothesis that small returns are symmetrically distributed around zero.

Solution

Total small returns in bandwidth: \(n = 28 + 4 = 32\). Under the null hypothesis of unmanipulated, symmetric return distributions, the probability of a positive return given that \(|R_t| \le 0.25\%\) is \(p = 0.50\).

Let \(K \sim \text{Binomial}(32, 0.50)\). The observed number of successes is \(k = 28\).

Using the normal approximation to the binomial distribution:

\[ \E[K] = np = 32 \times 0.50 = 16, \quad \Var(K) = np(1-p) = 32 \times 0.25 = 8 \implies \sigma_K = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.828 \]

The continuity-corrected \(z\)-score is:

\[ z = \frac{k - 0.5 - \E[K]}{\sigma_K} = \frac{28 - 0.5 - 16}{2.828} = \frac{11.5}{2.828} \approx 4.07 \]

The two-tailed \(p\)-value is \(p < 0.00005\). The null hypothesis of unmanipulated returns is rejected at the \(0.01\%\) significance level. This 7:1 asymmetry indicates opportunistic return smoothing or active loss concealment around the zero threshold (Bollen & Pool 2009).

Problem 5.9 (Berk & Green Capacity and Fee Extraction) A top quantitative equity market neutral fund charges a \(2\%\) management fee and \(20\%\) incentive fee. It generated \(+8.0\%\) gross alpha on \(\$500\text{M}\) AUM in year 1. Over the next 5 years, AUM swells to \(\$5\text{B}\). Due to market impact and strategy capacity limits, gross alpha declines to \(+2.5\%\). Analyze investor net alpha across the two periods following Berk & Green (2004).

Solution
  1. Year 1 (\(\$500\text{M}\) AUM):
    • Gross Alpha: \(+8.0\%\)
    • Management Fee: \(2.0\%\)
    • Performance Fee: \(0.20 \times (8.0\% - 2.0\%) = 1.2\%\)
    • Total Fees: \(3.2\%\)
    • Investor Net Alpha: \(8.0\% - 3.2\% = +4.8\%\) (Investors earn substantial excess return).
  2. Year 5 (\(\$5\text{B}\) AUM):
    • Gross Alpha: \(+2.5\%\)
    • Management Fee: \(2.0\%\)
    • Performance Fee: \(0.20 \times (2.5\% - 2.0\%) = 0.10\%\)
    • Total Fees: \(2.1\%\)
    • Investor Net Alpha: \(2.5\% - 2.1\% = +0.4\%\) (Net alpha effectively driven to zero).
    • Manager Total Dollar Fee Extraction: \(\$5\text{B} \times 2.1\% = \$105\text{M}\) per year (vs \(\$500\text{M} \times 3.2\% = \$16\text{M}\) in Year 1).

Consistent with Berk & Green (2004), the manager extracts all economic rents through AUM expansion, while investors' risk-adjusted net return converges to zero.

Problem 5.10 (Designing an Operational Due Diligence Audit Matrix) Formulate a 5-pillar institutional Operational Due Diligence (ODD) audit matrix for an alternative investment allocator, specifying for each pillar: (a) primary verification documents, and (b) critical disqualification flags.

Problem 5.11 (Jorion & Schwarz 2014 Misreporting vs. Illiquidity Frontier) In their 2014 study, how do Jorion & Schwarz demonstrate that hedge fund serial correlation is predominantly an unavoidable consequence of holding illiquid assets rather than deliberate fraudulent misreporting?

Solution

Jorion & Schwarz (2014) exploit cross-sectional asset composition data:

  1. They separate hedge funds by the verifiable market liquidity of their underlying portfolio holdings (e.g., large-cap equity L/S vs convertible arbitrage, distressed debt, and private loans).
  2. If serial correlation was driven by opportunistic manager manipulation, it should appear across all fund styles whenever managers experience drawdowns.
  3. Empirically, serial correlation is virtually zero in liquid styles (e.g., managed futures, long/short large cap equities) and highly concentrated in genuinely illiquid asset categories (distressed debt, emerging market credit).
  4. Furthermore, when comparing reported fund returns against the returns of the actual underlying illiquid bond indices, the lag structures match closely, proving that serial correlation reflects structural asset illiquidity rather than systematic fraud.

Problem 5.12 (Synthesis: The Complete Quantitative Due Diligence Protocol) Synthesize Lessons 1 through 5 into an integrated, 6-step quantitative due diligence protocol for an institutional allocator vetting a prospective hedge fund track record.

Solution

The integrated 6-step quantitative due diligence protocol consists of:

  1. Step 1: Track Record Sanitization & Bias Filtering (L05): Remove backfilled incubation periods prior to the fund's official launch; adjust for survivorship bias in peer benchmarks.
  2. Step 2: Serial Correlation & Lo (2002) Sharpe Adjustment (L02, L03): Compute sample autocorrelations \(\hat{\rho}_1, \dots, \hat{\rho}_q\). If \(\hat{\rho}_1 > 0.15\), compute Lo's adjustment factor \(\eta(q)\) to deflate reported Sharpe ratios.
  3. Step 3: GLM Structural Return Unsmoothing (L04): Fit the Getmansky-Lo-Makarov MA(\(k\)) model via constrained MLE to estimate smoothing weights \(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}\) and illiquidity index \(\hat{\xi}\). Reconstruct true latent economic volatility \(\sigma^* = \sigma_R / \sqrt{\hat{\xi}}\) and true Dimson betas \(\sum \beta_j\).
  4. Step 4: Multi-Factor HAC Alpha Inference (L01): Run Fama-French-Carhart multi-factor regressions with Newey-West HAC standard errors (lag \(L \approx 4(T/100)^{2/9}\)). Test whether unsmoothed alpha \(\alpha^*\) is statistically significant (\(t > 2.0\)).
  5. Step 5: Nonlinear Curvature & Option Payoff Detection (L05): Estimate Treynor-Mazuy quadratic curvature (\(\gamma\)) and Fung-Hsieh lookback straddle regressions. Verify that apparent alpha is not driven by concave negative gamma (\(\gamma < 0\)) or short volatility carry.
  6. Step 6: Forensic Audit & Zero Discontinuity Screening (L03, L05): Apply Bollen-Pool discontinuity tests around zero to detect loss suppression; cross-check third-party administrator and custodian statements for operational integrity.
