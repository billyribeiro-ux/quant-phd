The Probability of Backtest Overfitting

Lessons 1–7 asked whether one reported number survives the search that produced it. This lesson asks a different question: does the search itself work? A research process that tries \(N\) configurations and keeps the in-sample winner can be judged by how that winner ranks out of sample, across many ways of splitting the history. If the winner tends to land in the bottom half, the process has selected noise, and no single statistic of the winner can rescue it. The probability of backtest overfitting turns that question into a number you can compute from the trial matrix your backtester already holds.

1Overfitting is a property of the selection process

Collect the per-period returns of all \(N\) configurations a researcher tried into a matrix \(M\) with \(T\) rows (periods) and \(N\) columns (trials). Let \(J\) be a set of rows used to choose a configuration and \(\bar J\) the remaining rows. Write \(R_n(J)\) for the Sharpe ratio of column \(n\) on the rows \(J\). The researcher's rule selects \[ n^\ast(J) = \argmax_{n} R_n(J), \] and the question is where \(n^\ast(J)\) ranks among the \(N\) configurations on \(\bar J\).

Definition 1.1 (backtest overfitting)

A strategy-selection process exhibits backtest overfitting when the configuration it selects as optimal in sample has an expected out-of-sample rank below the median of the \(N\) configurations. The definition follows .

Two features set this definition apart from everything earlier in the module. It judges the process (try \(N\) things, keep the best), not one statistic of one strategy. And it is non-parametric: it uses only ranks, so it needs no distributional assumption about returns and no estimate of the number of independent trials. Compare data-snooping bias (Lesson 1). That is the inflation of the selected statistic. Backtest overfitting is the failure of the selection to carry any information forward.

2The probability of backtest overfitting

Ranks are made comparable across different \(N\) by mapping them into the unit interval. If the selected configuration has out-of-sample rank \(r\) among \(N\) (rank 1 is the worst, rank \(N\) the best), its relative rank is \[ \omega = \frac{r}{N+1} \in (0,1), \qquad \lambda = \ln\frac{\omega}{1-\omega}. \tag{1}\] The logit \(\lambda\) is positive when the in-sample winner beats the out-of-sample median and negative when it falls below. Dividing by \(N + 1\) rather than \(N\) keeps \(\omega\) strictly inside \((0,1)\), so \(\lambda\) is always finite.

Definition 2.1 (probability of backtest overfitting)

Over a distribution of in-sample/out-of-sample splits \((J, \bar J)\) of the history, the probability of backtest overfitting is \[ \PBO = \Prob\big(\lambda(J) \le 0\big), \tag{2}\] the probability that the configuration selected in sample ranks at or below the out-of-sample median. The non-strict inequality is the convention this module's project uses: a winner that lands exactly on the median counts as overfit.

Definition 2.1 needs a distribution of splits. With one history there is no population of independent samples to draw from, so the splits are generated combinatorially from the data itself.

3Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation

Definition 3.1 (CSCV)

Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV) estimates \(\PBO\) from a \(T \times N\) matrix \(M\) and an even number of blocks \(S\), with \(2 \le S \le T\):

  1. If \(S\) does not divide \(T\), drop the first \(T \bmod S\) rows (the oldest). Cut the remaining rows into \(S\) consecutive blocks of equal length.
  2. For each of the \(\binom{S}{S/2}\) ways to choose \(S/2\) blocks, form the in-sample set \(J\) from the chosen blocks and the out-of-sample set \(\bar J\) from the rest, each in time order.
  3. On \(J\), select \(n^\ast = \argmax_n R_n(J)\), taking the lowest index on ties.
  4. On \(\bar J\), rank \(n^\ast\) among the \(N\) configurations by \(R_n(\bar J)\) with average ranks for ties: \(r = 1 + \#\{n : R_n(\bar J) \lt R_{n^\ast}(\bar J)\} + \tfrac12\,\#\{n \ne n^\ast : R_n(\bar J) = R_{n^\ast}(\bar J)\}\). Compute \(\omega\) and \(\lambda\) by (1).
  5. Report \(\widehat{\PBO}\), the share of the \(\binom{S}{S/2}\) logits with \(\lambda \le 0\).

Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu introduce CSCV as one way to estimate PBO and find, in their own test cases, that it produces reasonable estimates. No independent study in the registry has yet evaluated its accuracy, so the grade is C. Section 4 proves what it estimates under the null, and Lab 2 measures how noisy one estimate is.

The word "symmetric" has a precise content, stated in Proposition 3.2. Every block is used in sample equally often, and every split is matched by its mirror image with the roles of in-sample and out-of-sample swapped.

Proposition 3.2 (the symmetry of CSCV)

Let \(S \ge 2\) be even and let \(\mathcal{C}\) be the family of \(S/2\)-element subsets of \(\{1, \dots, S\}\). Then (a) each block belongs to exactly \(\binom{S-1}{S/2-1} = \tfrac12\binom{S}{S/2}\) members of \(\mathcal{C}\); (b) the complement of every member of \(\mathcal{C}\) is another member of \(\mathcal{C}\), so the splits come in pairs \((J, \bar J)\) and \((\bar J, J)\).

Proof

(a) Fix block \(s\). A member of \(\mathcal{C}\) containing \(s\) is determined by its other \(S/2 - 1\) elements, chosen from the remaining \(S - 1\) blocks, so there are \(\binom{S-1}{S/2-1}\) of them. Since \(\binom{S}{S/2} = \frac{S}{S/2}\binom{S-1}{S/2-1} = 2\binom{S-1}{S/2-1}\), this count is half of \(|\mathcal{C}|\). (b) The complement of an \(S/2\)-element subset of an \(S\)-element set has \(S - S/2 = S/2\) elements, so it lies in \(\mathcal{C}\). It differs from the original set, which is non-empty. Complementation is an involution, so \(\mathcal{C}\) splits into \(\tfrac12\binom{S}{S/2}\) disjoint pairs.

Lab 1 · Every split of Example 3.3

The split with in-sample AB should reproduce \(\lambda = -1.0986\) from the hand calculation. Configuration s2 is steady in every block, so it should win most other splits and rank above the median out of sample. In the mirror split, in-sample CD, s1's early run should push s2 down to the median rank, giving \(\lambda = 0\), which counts as overfit. The estimate should then be \(2/6\).

4What PBO measures when nothing works

To read a PBO you need its value in the world where no configuration has skill. The next theorem gives it exactly, for any exchangeable dependence between the configurations (any common correlation).

Theorem 4.1 (PBO under an exchangeable null)

Suppose the rows of \(M\) are independent and identically distributed across periods; the columns are exchangeable (their joint distribution is invariant to permutations of the configurations, so any common correlation is allowed); and the out-of-sample metrics \(R_1(\bar J), \dots, R_N(\bar J)\) are almost surely distinct. Then for every split, \(\Prob(\lambda \le 0) = \lfloor (N+1)/2 \rfloor / N\), and therefore \[ \E\big[\widehat{\PBO}\big] = \frac{\lfloor (N+1)/2 \rfloor}{N} = \begin{cases} 1/2, & N \text{ even}, \\ (N+1)/(2N), & N \text{ odd}. \end{cases} \tag{3}\]

Proof

Fix a split \((J, \bar J)\). Its row sets are disjoint and the rows are independent, so the in-sample data are independent of the out-of-sample data. The selected index \(n^\ast\) is a function of the in-sample rows only, hence independent of the out-of-sample metric vector. By exchangeability, the out-of-sample rank of any fixed column is uniform on \(\{1, \dots, N\}\), because the metrics are almost surely distinct and every ordering is equally likely. Conditioning on \(n^\ast = k\) does not change the distribution of the out-of-sample metrics, so the rank \(r\) of \(n^\ast\) is uniform on \(\{1, \dots, N\}\). Next, \(\lambda \le 0\) if and only if \(\omega \le \tfrac12\), if and only if \(r \le (N+1)/2\). Exactly \(\lfloor (N+1)/2 \rfloor\) of the \(N\) equally likely ranks satisfy this. \(\widehat{\PBO}\) is the average of the indicators \(\indic\{\lambda(J) \le 0\}\) over the splits, so linearity of expectation gives (3). The splits share data and are therefore dependent, but linearity needs no independence.

Remark 4.2 (the null value depends on the convention)

Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu's formal definition counts a selected configuration as overfit when its out-of-sample rank satisfies \(r \lt N/2\), while their estimator integrates the density of the logits up to zero. The two differ. Under the assumptions of Theorem 4.1, \(r\) is uniform on \(\{1, \dots, N\}\), so the formal definition has null value \(\#\{r : r \lt N/2\}/N = (\lceil N/2 \rceil - 1)/N\): 0.45 at \(N = 20\), against 1/2 for Definition 2.1. For even \(N\) the rank \(N/2\) has a negative logit, so the integral counts it and the formal definition does not. For odd \(N\) the median rank has \(\lambda = 0\) exactly, and the convention at zero decides whether it counts; Definition 2.1 counts it. Ties move the null value too. With \(N = 2\), if the two out-of-sample metrics tie with probability \(q \gt 0\), the winner's average rank is 1.5, so \(\lambda = 0\) and the split counts, and \(\Prob(\lambda \le 0) = q + (1-q)/2 \gt 1/2\). Returns recorded on a coarse grid can therefore push a null PBO above (3). Before comparing a PBO with its null value, check which convention produced it.

Two consequences matter in practice. First, under the null the expected value is one half (slightly more for odd \(N\), because the median rank is counted as overfit). A process that selects noise is not detected by a PBO "near one", but by a PBO that is not well below one half. Second, Theorem 4.1 is silent about the variance of a single estimate. The splits overlap heavily, so one estimate can land far from its expectation. Lab 2 measures how far.

Lab 2 · Pure noise versus one genuine configuration

For 20 SIMULATED skill-free configurations, Theorem 4.1 predicts a mean PBO of 0.5 across samples, with individual estimates scattered widely because the 252 splits share their data. Adding a true daily Sharpe ratio of 0.15 to one configuration should pull the mean PBO far below one half.

import itertools
import math

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray


def sharpe(x: NDArray[np.float64]) -> NDArray[np.float64]:
    """Per-column Sharpe ratio of per-period returns."""
    return np.asarray(x.mean(axis=0) / x.std(axis=0, ddof=1))


def pbo(m: NDArray[np.float64], n_blocks: int) -> float:
    """CSCV estimate of the probability of backtest overfitting (Sharpe ratio as the metric)."""
    m = m[m.shape[0] % n_blocks :]
    blocks = np.split(m, n_blocks)
    n = m.shape[1]
    logits = []
    for combo in itertools.combinations(range(n_blocks), n_blocks // 2):
        train = np.vstack([blocks[i] for i in combo])
        test = np.vstack([blocks[i] for i in range(n_blocks) if i not in combo])
        best = int(np.argmax(sharpe(train)))
        oos = sharpe(test)
        rank = 1 + np.sum(oos < oos[best]) + 0.5 * (np.sum(oos == oos[best]) - 1)
        omega = rank / (n + 1)
        logits.append(math.log(omega / (1 - omega)))
    return float(np.mean(np.array(logits) <= 0))


# SIMULATED daily returns: 1000 days, 20 configurations, 10 blocks (252 splits each).
rng = np.random.default_rng(808)
null_pbo = np.array([pbo(rng.standard_normal((1000, 20)) * 0.01, 10) for _ in range(20)])
spread = null_pbo.std(ddof=1)
print(f"20 skill-free configurations, 20 samples: mean PBO {null_pbo.mean():.4f}")
print(f"  standard deviation {spread:.4f}, standard error of the mean {spread / np.sqrt(20):.4f}")
print(f"  smallest {null_pbo.min():.4f}, largest {null_pbo.max():.4f}")

genuine_pbo = []
for _ in range(20):
    m = rng.standard_normal((1000, 20)) * 0.01
    m[:, 0] += 0.0015  # configuration 1 has a true daily Sharpe ratio of 0.15
    genuine_pbo.append(pbo(m, 10))
print(f"one genuine configuration among 20, 20 samples: mean PBO {np.mean(genuine_pbo):.4f}")
20 skill-free configurations, 20 samples: mean PBO 0.4569
  standard deviation 0.2040, standard error of the mean 0.0456
  smallest 0.0317, largest 0.7698
one genuine configuration among 20, 20 samples: mean PBO 0.0736

The null mean sits about one standard error (0.046) below the theorem's one half, which is within sampling error. The spread is the lesson. Single estimates have a standard deviation of about 0.20, and one pure-noise sample produced a PBO near 0.03, which a reader would take as proof of a robust process. A low PBO from one history is therefore weak evidence on its own. It should be read together with the deflated Sharpe ratio of the winner and a count of the trials. With a genuine configuration present, the selection usually picks it and it ranks near the top out of sample, so the mean PBO falls to below a tenth.

5Dilution: every extra trial makes the search worse

A genuine edge competes in sample against the luckiest of the null configurations, and the luck, the expected maximum of the nulls, rises with \(N\) (Lesson 1 bounds it by \(\sqrt{2\ln N}\), Theorem 2.1). The probability that the genuine configuration wins a split, \(\Prob(Z + \delta \gt Q_{N-1})\), where \(\delta\) is its standardized edge, \(Z\) its standardized noise and \(Q_{N-1}\) the largest of the \(N-1\) null statistics, cannot rise when a null is added, whatever the joint law of the in-sample statistics: adding a null can only raise the maximum, so the winning event with one more null is contained in the event without it. As \(N\) grows, the null configurations win the in-sample contest more often. Each such win selects a configuration whose out-of-sample rank is uniform among the nulls, which pushes the PBO back toward one half.

Lab 3 · One genuine edge in a growing crowd

With one SIMULATED configuration earning a true daily Sharpe ratio of 0.10 and the rest skill-free, the mean PBO should rise as the number of trials grows from 2 to 200, from about 0.11 toward the null value of one half. At two trials it equals \(2p(1-p)\), where \(p = \Phi(0.10\sqrt{500}/\sqrt{2}) = \Phi(1.58) = 0.943\) is the probability that the genuine configuration wins a 500-day half: the split counts when the winner is the worse of the two out of sample. Each mean comes from 16 samples, so the lab prints its standard error.

The edge is the same in every row; only the size of the search changed. The two-trial mean, 0.12, agrees with \(2p(1-p) = 0.107\) within its standard error. From 2 to 200 trials the mean rose by 0.22, about 3.2 times the standard error of the difference, \(\sqrt{0.0537^2 + 0.0407^2} = 0.067\). The step from 2 to 20 trials is about one standard error, which 16 samples cannot resolve. A rough calculation shows why the rise happens. With 500 in-sample days the genuine configuration's standardized edge is \(0.10\sqrt{500} = 2.24\), while the maximum of 199 standard normals has mean 2.744 and standard deviation 0.401 (numerical integration). Treating their difference as normal, the genuine configuration wins the in-sample contest in only about \(\Phi\big((2.24 - 2.744)/\sqrt{1 + 0.401^2}\big) = \Phi(-0.47) = 0.32\) of splits. This is the selection-process view of Lesson 1's warning. Testing more variants of an idea does not just inflate the winner's statistic; it lowers the probability that the winner is the thing you were looking for. The remedy is to restrict the search before running it, not to correct for it afterwards.

Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu pair PBO with companion statistics computed from the same splits: performance degradation, the relation between in-sample and out-of-sample performance of the selected configurations across splits; the probability of loss, the share of splits in which the selected configuration loses money out of sample; and a comparison of the whole out-of-sample performance distribution of the selected configurations with that of a configuration picked at random. Performance degradation and the probability of loss answer the questions a capital allocator asks next: how much of the in-sample performance should I expect, and how often does the selection lose outright?

6Three different questions: PBO, DSR and Romano–Wolf

The module now has three tools that are easily confused. Each answers a different question, and they can disagree without contradiction.

The tools are complements, not rivals. deflates the reported statistic. controls the error rate of the decisions. CSCV audits the rule that produced them. A configuration can be a genuine, StepM-significant discovery with a DSR well above one half and still come from a process whose PBO is far from zero: one real edge among many tries, which the process finds in some splits only. Problem R3 builds such a case.

7Where the canon breaks

Theorem 4.1 tells you what CSCV measures when its assumptions hold. Each assumption fails in some practical setting.

  • Blocks are not exchangeable in time. CSCV pairs any \(S/2\) blocks with any other \(S/2\), so the "out-of-sample" data often precede the in-sample data. That is a strength for estimating a population quantity from one history. But it means CSCV does not simulate deployment, in which a configuration is always selected on earlier data and run on later data. A strategy that worked in the first half of the sample and died in the second can still be selected and ranked well in the splits that mix the halves. Example 3.3 shows the regime effect in miniature.
  • Serial correlation and overlapping returns leak across block boundaries. If returns are overlapping (for example, monthly returns sampled daily) or strongly autocorrelated, adjacent in-sample and out-of-sample blocks share information. The independence used in the proof of Theorem 4.1 then fails at every boundary. CSCV as defined here does nothing about it; QR2 develops treatments of the observations near block boundaries.
  • Small \(S\) and small \(N\) make the estimate coarse. With \(S = 4\) there are six splits, so \(\widehat{\PBO}\) moves in steps of \(1/6\). With small \(N\), one median finish changes it materially (Lab 1). Large \(S\) means short blocks, in which the Sharpe ratio is itself noisy.
  • The estimate is noisy even when the assumptions hold. Lab 2 found PBO estimates from 0.03 to 0.77 for the same null process. A single PBO is one draw of a heavily dependent average.
  • The trial matrix must be complete. Like every tool in this module, CSCV only sees the trials it is given (Lesson 1, Problem R1). Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu warn that hiding trials leads to an underestimate of the overfitting. The direction is not fixed. If the trials that vanish from a log are the ones that did worst over the full sample, dropping them raises the estimate: in Problem E2(b), keeping only the 20 best of 100 pure-noise configurations moves the mean PBO from about one half to about 0.8. Either way, a PBO computed from an incomplete log describes a search that was not the one run.

8Misconceptions

  1. “A PBO of 30% means a 30% chance that my strategy will lose money.”

    PBO is the probability that the selected configuration ranks at or below the out-of-sample median of the configurations tried. If all configurations are profitable, the selected one can rank below the median and still make money. If all lose, it can rank first and still lose. The probability of loss is a separate companion statistic (Section 5).

  2. “A pure-noise search gives a PBO near 100%.”

    Theorem 4.1 gives one half for even \(N\) (and \((N+1)/(2N)\) for odd \(N\)), not one. A selection rule that picks at random ranks above the median as often as below. Evidence of a working process is a PBO well below one half. Evidence against it is a PBO near or above one half.

  3. “A low PBO proves the strategy has skill.”

    PBO audits the selection process, and a single estimate is noisy: Lab 2 produced 0.03 from pure noise. Whether the selected strategy has skill is the question the deflated Sharpe ratio (Lesson 7) and Romano–Wolf (Lesson 5) answer. They should agree before capital moves.

  4. “CSCV is a chronological out-of-sample test, repeated over more splits.”

    A chronological test always selects on earlier data and evaluates on later data. CSCV uses every half of the blocks as in-sample, regardless of time order, so it estimates how informative the selection rule is across the history, not how it would have fared deployed in sequence.

For \(S \in \{4, 6, 8, 10, 16\}\), compute the number of CSCV splits and the number of splits in which a given block is in sample.

Solution

The number of splits is \(\binom{S}{S/2}\): 6, 20, 70, 252 and 12,870. By Proposition 3.2, each block is in sample in \(\binom{S-1}{S/2-1}\) splits, exactly half: 3, 10, 35, 126 and 6,435. The growth is why the labs use \(S \le 10\). At \(S = 16\) every evaluation is repeated 12,870 times.

M2 · Logits

With \(N = 9\) configurations, the in-sample winner ranks 4th out of sample (rank 1 is the worst). Compute \(\omega\) and \(\lambda\), and say whether the split counts toward the PBO. Repeat for ranks 5 and 7.

Solution

\(\omega = r/(N+1) = r/10\). For rank 4, \(\omega = 0.4\) and \(\lambda = \ln(0.4/0.6) = -0.4055\), so the split counts. For rank 5, the middle of ranks 1–9, \(\omega = 0.5\) and \(\lambda = 0\). Under the convention \(\lambda \le 0\) it counts. For rank 7, \(\omega = 0.7\) and \(\lambda = \ln(0.7/0.3) = 0.8473\), so it does not count.

M3 · From logits to PBO

A CSCV run with \(S = 4\) returns the logits \(1.2, -0.3, 0.0, 2.1, -1.4, 0.7\). Compute \(\widehat{\PBO}\). Which pairs of splits are mirror images of one another, and what does Proposition 3.2 say about them?

Solution

Three logits are at or below zero (\(-0.3\), \(0.0\), \(-1.4\)), so \(\widehat{\PBO} = 3/6 = 0.5\). In itertools order the splits are AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, CD. Their mirror pairs are (AB, CD), (AC, BD) and (AD, BC), that is, positions (1, 6), (2, 5) and (3, 4). Proposition 3.2 says the mirror of each split is also a split. It does not say their logits agree: each mirror swaps which half selects and which half judges. Here the pairs give (1.2, 0.7), (−0.3, −1.4) and (0.0, 2.1).

Proof

P1 · Odd N

Under the assumptions of Theorem 4.1, compute \(\E[\widehat{\PBO}]\) for \(N \in \{2, 3, 4, 5, 21\}\). Prove that for odd \(N\) the excess over one half is \(1/(2N)\), and explain where it comes from.

Solution

By (3): \(N = 2\): \(1/2\); \(N = 3\): \(2/3\); \(N = 4\): \(1/2\); \(N = 5\): \(3/5\); \(N = 21\): \(11/21 = 0.5238\). For odd \(N\), \(\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor = (N+1)/2\), so \(\E[\widehat{\PBO}] = (N+1)/(2N) = 1/2 + 1/(2N)\). The \((N-1)/2\) ranks strictly below the middle contribute \((N-1)/(2N) = 1/2 - 1/(2N)\). The middle rank \((N+1)/2\), where \(\omega = 1/2\) and \(\lambda = 0\), has probability \(1/N\) and counts in full under the convention \(\lambda \le 0\). A symmetric convention that counted the middle rank as half overfit would give exactly \(1/2\). The excess \(1/(2N)\) is therefore the price of the conservative convention, and it vanishes as \(N\) grows.

P2 · Leverage does not change PBO

Let \(M'\) be \(M\) with column \(n\) multiplied by a constant \(a_n > 0\) for every \(n\) (each configuration levered differently). Prove that CSCV returns identical logits for \(M\) and \(M'\). Then show by example that adding a constant to one column can change them.

Solution

On any row set \(J\), the mean and the standard deviation of column \(n\) both scale by \(a_n > 0\), so \(R_n(J)\) is unchanged. Every in-sample argmax, every out-of-sample rank, and therefore every logit is the same. Adding a constant \(c\) to column \(n\) shifts its mean by \(c\) and leaves its standard deviation unchanged, so \(R_n(J)\) changes by \(c/s_n(J)\), which differs across row sets. In Example 3.3, add 0.02 to every return of s3. On AB its returns \(-0.01, 0.02, 0.01, -0.02\) have mean 0 and standard deviation 0.018257, so its Sharpe ratio rises from 0 to \(0.02/0.018257 = 1.095\). On AC (mean 0.01, the same standard deviation) it rises from 0.548 to \(0.03/0.018257 = 1.643\). Recomputing all six splits, s3 now wins in sample in three of them, and in every split the in-sample winner lands at or below the out-of-sample median: the estimate jumps from \(2/6\) to \(6/6\). A financing cost or a different risk-free rate is a constant shift of this kind, so PBO comparisons must use excess returns computed consistently.

P3 · A dominant configuration

Suppose column 1 of \(M\) has the highest Sharpe ratio on every union of \(S/2\) blocks, and also on every complementary union. Prove that \(\widehat{\PBO} = 0\) whenever \(N \ge 2\). Then explain why "highest Sharpe ratio in every block" is not enough for the conclusion.

Solution

In every split, column 1 wins in sample by hypothesis (lowest index on ties, so it wins even when tied). Out of sample it again has the highest Sharpe ratio, so its rank is \(N\) or, with ties, \(N - \tfrac12 k\) for \(k\) tied rivals. Without ties, \(\omega = N/(N+1) \gt 1/2\) and \(\lambda \gt 0\) for \(N \ge 2\), so no split counts and \(\widehat{\PBO} = 0\). If column 1 ties for the top with \(k\) others, its average rank is \(N - k/2\), which exceeds \((N+1)/2\) unless \(k = N - 1\), that is, unless all \(N\) configurations tie (then \(\lambda = 0\) and the split counts). Read "the highest" as strictly highest, and the conclusion holds. Block-wise dominance is not enough because a Sharpe ratio on a union of blocks is not a function of the block-wise Sharpe ratios. It depends on the block means and the pooled dispersion, so a configuration that wins each block by a little but has very different means across blocks can lose on a union. The hypothesis must be stated on the unions CSCV actually evaluates.

Empirical

E1 · How strong must an edge be?

Using the setting of Lab 2 (one genuine configuration among 20, 1,000 days, so 500 in-sample days per split), estimate the daily Sharpe ratio at which the mean PBO falls to about 0.05. First derive an approximation, then check it by modifying Lab 2.

Solution

Approximate the process by two cases. With probability \(\pi\) the genuine configuration wins in sample, and then it almost always ranks near the top out of sample. With probability \(1 - \pi\) a null wins, and then its out-of-sample rank is roughly uniform among the 19 nulls below the genuine one. That rank is at or below the median of 20 with probability about \(10/19 = 0.526\). So \(\PBO \approx 0.526\,(1 - \pi)\), and a PBO of 0.05 needs \(\pi \approx 0.905\).

The genuine configuration wins when \(Z + \delta \gt Q_{19}\), where \(Z\) is its standardized noise, \(\delta = \mu\sqrt{500}\) its standardized edge, and \(Q_{19}\) the maximum of 19 standard normals. Numerical integration of the maximum's density gives \(\E[Q_{19}] = 1.844\) with standard deviation 0.529. Treating \(Z - Q_{19}\) as normal with mean \(-1.844\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{1 + 0.529^2} = 1.131\), \(\pi = 0.9\) needs \(\delta = 1.844 + 1.2816 \times 1.131 = 3.294\), so \(\mu \approx 3.294/\sqrt{500} = 0.147\) per day, about 2.3 annualized. Lab 2's daily 0.15 gave a mean PBO below a tenth, the same order. The normal treatment of the maximum and the dependence between splits make this a rough guide. Modify Lab 2 by sweeping the added drift over 0.0010–0.0020 in steps of 0.00025, and you should see the mean cross 0.05 near the derived value. The economic message is that in a 20-trial search over two years of daily data, only an edge of about 2.3 annualized makes the selection reliable.

E2 · Correlated and censored null configurations

(a) Change Lab 2's null universe so that the 20 configurations share a common factor with pairwise correlation 0.8. Predict the mean PBO and its dispersion before running, then compare. (b) Return to independent nulls. Run 100 pure-noise configurations, then keep only the 20 with the best full-sample Sharpe ratios, as a log that loses its failures would. Predict whether the PBO of the kept 20 sits above or below one half, then compare with keeping 20 at random.

Solution

A common factor keeps the columns exchangeable, and the rows stay independent in time, so Theorem 4.1 still applies: the expected PBO is 0.5, whatever the correlation. The theorem says nothing about dispersion, so measure it rather than guess. Construct the returns as 0.01 * (np.sqrt(0.8) * w + np.sqrt(0.2) * e), with w of shape (1000, 1) and e of shape (1000, 20), and report the mean, standard deviation and range over 20 samples as Lab 2 does. Your mean should land within about two standard errors of 0.5. The contrast with Lesson 1, Problem P3 is the point: correlation shrinks the inflation of the winner's statistic, but it does not make a null selection informative.

(b) The kept configurations all had good full-sample results. A configuration whose full-sample result is good and whose in-sample half is especially strong must have a weaker out-of-sample half, so the in-sample winner tends to rank low among the kept configurations out of sample, and the PBO rises above one half. Keeping 20 at random changes nothing: the columns stay exchangeable. The simulation below reuses Lab 2's pbo function:

import itertools
import math

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray


def sharpe(x: NDArray[np.float64]) -> NDArray[np.float64]:
    """Per-column Sharpe ratio of per-period returns."""
    return np.asarray(x.mean(axis=0) / x.std(axis=0, ddof=1))


def pbo(m: NDArray[np.float64], n_blocks: int) -> float:
    """CSCV estimate of the probability of backtest overfitting (Sharpe ratio as the metric)."""
    m = m[m.shape[0] % n_blocks :]
    blocks = np.split(m, n_blocks)
    n = m.shape[1]
    logits = []
    for combo in itertools.combinations(range(n_blocks), n_blocks // 2):
        train = np.vstack([blocks[i] for i in combo])
        test = np.vstack([blocks[i] for i in range(n_blocks) if i not in combo])
        best = int(np.argmax(sharpe(train)))
        oos = sharpe(test)
        rank = 1 + np.sum(oos < oos[best]) + 0.5 * (np.sum(oos == oos[best]) - 1)
        omega = rank / (n + 1)
        logits.append(math.log(omega / (1 - omega)))
    return float(np.mean(np.array(logits) <= 0))


# SIMULATED pure noise: 100 configurations, 1000 days, 10 blocks. A log that loses its failures
# keeps only the 20 best full-sample results; compare it with the full log and with a random 20.
rng = np.random.default_rng(1001)
full_log, best_20, random_20 = [], [], []
for _ in range(50):
    m = rng.standard_normal((1000, 100)) * 0.01
    full_sharpe = m.mean(axis=0) / m.std(axis=0, ddof=1)
    full_log.append(pbo(m, 10))
    best_20.append(pbo(m[:, np.argsort(full_sharpe)[-20:]], 10))
    random_20.append(pbo(m[:, rng.choice(100, 20, replace=False)], 10))
for name, v in (("all 100 logged", full_log), ("20 best kept", best_20), ("random 20 kept", random_20)):
    print(f"{name}: mean PBO {np.mean(v):.3f}, standard error {np.std(v, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(v)):.3f}")
all 100 logged: mean PBO 0.473, standard error 0.020
20 best kept: mean PBO 0.816, standard error 0.022
random 20 kept: mean PBO 0.463, standard error 0.027

The mean PBO is 0.473 with all 100 logged, 0.816 with the 20 best kept and 0.463 with a random 20 kept. A log that loses its failures does not make a search look better under CSCV; it makes the estimate describe a different search.

E3 · A strategy that died

Build a SIMULATED universe of 20 null configurations plus one that earns a daily Sharpe ratio of 0.2 in the first 500 days and \(-0.2\) in the last 500. Predict whether CSCV with \(S = 10\) flags the process, then run it. What does a single chronological split (select on days 1–500, evaluate the selected configuration on days 501–1000) say instead?

Solution

Classify the 252 splits by the number \(k\) of early blocks (days 1–500) in the in-sample half. There are \(\binom{5}{k}\binom{5}{5-k}\) splits with \(k\) early blocks: 1, 25, 100, 100, 25 and 1 for \(k = 0, \dots, 5\). The dying configuration's in-sample daily Sharpe ratio is \(0.2(2k-5)/5\), a standardized edge \(\delta(k) = 0.2(2k-5)\sqrt{500}/5\): 4.47, 2.68 and 0.89 for \(k = 5, 4, 3\), and the negatives for \(k = 0, 1, 2\). The best of the 20 nulls has mean 1.868 and standard deviation 0.525 (numerical integration), so the dying configuration wins a split with probability about \(\Phi\big((\delta(k) - 1.868)/\sqrt{1 + 0.525^2}\big)\). With \(N = 21\), a split counts when the winner's out-of-sample rank is at most 11.

  • \(k \ge 4\) (26 splits). The dying configuration wins with probability near 1 at \(k = 5\) and 0.77 at \(k = 4\), and its out-of-sample edge \(-\delta(k)\) puts it at the bottom, so the split counts. When a null wins instead, the dying configuration sits near the bottom out of sample and the null's rank is roughly uniform on 2–21, so the split counts with probability \(10/20\). For \(k = 4\): \(0.77 + 0.23 \times 0.5 = 0.88\).
  • \(k = 3\) (100 splits). It wins with probability 0.19 and then still ranks below the median out of sample, where its edge is \(-0.89\). Otherwise a null wins and the split counts about half the time: \(0.19 + 0.81 \times 0.5 = 0.60\).
  • \(k \le 2\) (126 splits). A null almost always wins. The dying configuration's out-of-sample edge is positive, so it usually ranks above the null winner, whose rank is then roughly uniform on 1–20 and counts with probability \(11/20 = 0.55\); when it ranks below, the probability is \(10/20\). Call it 0.54.

The expected PBO is therefore about \((1 + 25 \times 0.88 + 100 \times 0.60 + 126 \times 0.54)/252 = 0.60\), above the null value \(11/21 = 0.524\) of Theorem 4.1. A simulation with Lab 2's pbo function checks it:

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

    m = rng.standard_normal((1000, 21)) * 0.01

    m[500:, 20] -= 0.002

The mean PBO is 0.579, close to the derivation, and 66% of single estimates are at or above one half. By the reading rule of Misconception 2, CSCV usually flags this process: it returns the verdict of a pure-noise search, that the selection is uninformative. It cannot see that the selected strategy died. The chronological split can: it selects the dying configuration in 98.5% of the simulated histories, and the selected configuration's daily Sharpe ratio over days 501–1000 averages \(-0.193\). CSCV averages over regime timing, while deployment experiences it in order. Report both.

Research

R1 · A reporting standard for an investment committee

Draft the section of a strategy-approval memo that reports backtest overfitting. Specify the inputs, the parameters, the companion statistics and the decision rule, and justify each choice.

Solution
  1. The trial matrix. Every configuration evaluated during development, drawn from the trial log (Lesson 1, Problem R1), with the count \(N\) and the share of configurations logged automatically.
  2. Parameters. State \(S\) and justify it. The block length must be long enough for a meaningful Sharpe ratio (at least a year of daily data per block is a defensible floor) and \(\binom{S}{S/2}\) must be computable. Report the rows dropped to make \(T\) divisible by \(S\).
  3. Statistics. Report PBO with its null benchmark from Theorem 4.1, plus performance degradation and the probability of loss. Report the DSR of the selected configuration (Lesson 7) and the Romano–Wolf decisions (Lesson 5) alongside, because each answers a different question (Section 6).
  4. Robustness. Recompute PBO for at least two values of \(S\) and over a stationary bootstrap of the trial matrix's rows (Lesson 4), so the committee sees the estimate's dispersion. Lab 2 showed it can be large.
  5. Decision rule. Approve only if PBO is well below one half under both values of \(S\), the DSR clears the committee's threshold, and a chronological split (select on the earlier part of the history, evaluate on the later part) agrees in sign. Disagreements are escalated with the specific statistic that failed.
  6. Limitations, stated in the memo. Regime timing (Problem E3), overlapping returns, and trials missing from the log.
R2 · Time order

CSCV ignores time order. A single chronological split, which selects on the earlier part of the history and evaluates on the later part, respects it. Construct an argument for each as the primary evidence in a strategy review, and state the conditions under which each argument wins.

Solution

For CSCV. One history gives one chronological split, so its outcome is a single draw with large variance. CSCV reuses the data symmetrically, every block serving as in-sample and as out-of-sample equally often (Proposition 3.2), and so averages over many splits. If the return-generating process is stationary across blocks, the time order carries no information about selection quality, and CSCV's average is the more efficient estimate.

For the chronological split. A strategy is deployed forward, not in a shuffled order. If the return-generating process changes over calendar time, a strategy's performance depends on when it is measured. McLean and Pontiff find that the portfolio returns of 97 predictors of cross-sectional stock returns are 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication; Jacobs and Müller, studying 241 anomalies in 39 stock markets, find that the United States is the only country with a reliable post-publication decline in long-short returns. Where such decay is present, the chronological split measures the quantity an allocator experiences, and CSCV can credit a process for selecting configurations that worked only in the past (Problem E3).

When each wins. CSCV wins when stationarity is credible and the sample is short relative to the number of trials. The chronological split wins when there is evidence of time variation, such as a structural break or a known regime change in the sample. A rigorous review runs both and treats disagreement as information about non-stationarity. QR2 extends the toolkit for overlapping labels.

R3 · When the tools disagree

Construct, in words and with the parameters you would simulate, a scenario in which the selected strategy survives Romano–Wolf and has a deflated Sharpe ratio well above one half, yet the process has a PBO far from zero (about one third). Explain which conclusion should govern a decision to allocate capital to the selected strategy, and to the research process that found it.

Solution

Take one configuration with a true daily Sharpe ratio of 0.14 and 1,999 skill-free configurations, over 1,000 days of normal returns. The calculations below treat the maximum of the nulls as normal, so they are approximations to check by simulation.

  • Full sample. The genuine configuration's standardized edge is \(0.14\sqrt{1000} = 4.43\). The maximum of 1,999 standard normals has mean 3.435 and standard deviation 0.335 (numerical integration), so the genuine configuration is the full-sample winner with probability about \(\Phi\big((4.43 - 3.435)/\sqrt{1 + 0.335^2}\big) = 0.83\).
  • DSR and StepM. When it is the winner, its DSR is about \(\Phi(4.43 - 3.447) = 0.84\), using the approximation (3) of Lesson 1 to the expected maximum, which gives 3.447 for \(N = 2000\). That is well above one half but below the conventional 0.95 of Lesson 7. It also clears the Bonferroni-type one-sided threshold \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - 0.05/2000) = 4.06\). By the union bound of Lesson 2, the exact critical value of the maximum statistic that StepM targets never exceeds that threshold, whatever the dependence.
  • CSCV. On 500-day halves the edge is only \(0.14\sqrt{500} = 3.13\), below the typical null maximum. The genuine configuration wins only about \(\Phi\big((3.13 - 3.435)/\sqrt{1.112}\big) = 0.39\) of the splits, and the estimated PBO is roughly \(0.5 \times (1 - 0.39) = 0.31\).

The decisions separate. Allocating to the strategy rests on its full-sample evidence, which DSR and StepM assess, and should wait for independent confirmation such as new data or a later period of the history. The process deserves less trust: in about a third of the ways of splitting its own history, it picks a configuration that is median or worse. A firm that funds the strategy and keeps running the same 2,000-configuration search should expect its next pick to be noise far more often than this one.
