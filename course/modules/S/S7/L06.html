Hundreds of return predictors have been proposed, and 296 of the 316 that Harvey, Liu and Zhu catalogue are significant at the 5% benchmark of a t-statistic of 1.96. Lessons 2–5 built the machinery for testing many hypotheses at once; this lesson applies it to the literature itself. You will derive the t-statistic a new predictor must clear when the field has already tried many, measure how a specification search inflates the false-positive rate even when every individual test is honest, and convert a t-statistic into a bound on the probability that the effect is not there at all.

1The literature as one large multiple test

For this lesson a predictor is any variable proposed to explain why some assets earn higher average returns than others: a firm characteristic, a portfolio return, a macroeconomic series. All that matters here is that each proposal is certified by a significance test of one coefficient, reported as a t-statistic. Harvey, Liu and Zhu note that one of the first cross-sectional tests in this literature reported a t-statistic of 2.57 for its predictor, comfortably above the usual cutoff of 2.0. Decades later the same cutoff was still in use, while the number of proposals had grown into the hundreds: restricting attention to top journals and a selected set of working papers, they catalogue 316 different factors, a count they say likely understates the population of factors. Whether most of that zoo is false is contested: Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen find that the majority of factors replicate, while Hou, Xue and Zhang find that 82% of 452 anomalies fail a multiple-test hurdle of 2.78. Section 5 sets the two side by side.

A t-statistic judged against the single-test cutoff is treated as if it were the only test ever run. Viewed from the literature's side, the \(M\) published tests are treated as \(M\) hypotheses tested on overlapping data, exactly the setting of Lessons 2 and 3. Harvey, Liu and Zhu therefore apply three procedures from those lessons to the whole catalogue: the Bonferroni correction and the Holm step-down procedure, which control the family-wise error rate under any dependence, and the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure, which controls the false discovery rate under any dependence . The question changes from “is this predictor significant?” to “what t-statistic must the next predictor clear, given everything already tried?”

2The t-statistic hurdle

Definition 2.1 (t-statistic hurdle)

Let a multiple-testing procedure at level \(\alpha\) be applied to \(M\) two-sided tests whose statistics are approximately standard normal under their nulls. Its t-statistic hurdle is the critical p-value the procedure applies at the step where it stops, converted to a statistic: a p-value threshold \(\bar p\) corresponds to the hurdle \(t^\ast = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \bar p/2)\). For Bonferroni the threshold is \(\alpha/M\) at every step, so \[ t^\ast_M = \Phi^{-1}\!\Big(1 - \frac{\alpha}{2M}\Big). \tag{1}\] For Holm and Benjamini–Yekutieli the threshold depends on the whole set of p-values, so their hurdles are random.

Equation (1) starts at 1.96 for a single test at \(\alpha = 5\%\) and grows with \(M\) at the rate of Lesson 1's luck hurdle \(\sqrt{2\ln N}\) (compare L02 Proposition 4.1), as the next two results prove.

Lemma 2.2 (Mills-ratio bounds)

For every \(x > 0\), with \(\varphi\) the standard normal density, \[ \frac{x\,\varphi(x)}{1 + x^2} \le 1 - \Phi(x) \le \frac{\varphi(x)}{x}. \tag{2}\]

Proof

Upper bound: \(\varphi'(u) = -u\varphi(u)\), so for \(u \ge x\), \(\varphi(u) \le (u/x)\varphi(u)\), and \(1 - \Phi(x) = \int_x^\infty \varphi(u)\,du \le \frac1x\int_x^\infty u\varphi(u)\,du = \varphi(x)/x\). Lower bound: \(\frac{d}{du}\big(\varphi(u)/u\big) = -\varphi(u)\,(1 + 1/u^2)\), hence \(\varphi(x)/x = \int_x^\infty \varphi(u)(1 + 1/u^2)\,du \le (1 + 1/x^2)\,(1 - \Phi(x))\), because \(1/u^2 \le 1/x^2\) on the range of integration. Rearranging gives the left inequality.

Theorem 2.3 (growth of the Bonferroni hurdle)

Fix \(\alpha \in (0,1)\) and let \(t^\ast_M\) be the hurdle (1). Whenever \(t^\ast_M \ge 1\), \[ 2\ln\frac{2M}{\alpha} - 2\ln\!\Big(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\big(t^\ast_M + 1/t^\ast_M\big)\Big) \;\le\; (t^\ast_M)^2 \;\le\; 2\ln\frac{2M}{\alpha} - 2\ln\!\big(\sqrt{2\pi}\,t^\ast_M\big), \tag{3}\] so \(t^\ast_M \le \sqrt{2\ln(2M/\alpha)}\) and \(t^\ast_M / \sqrt{2\ln M} \to 1\) as \(M \to \infty\).

Proof

By definition \(1 - \Phi(t^\ast_M) = \alpha/(2M)\). Insert \(x = t^\ast_M\) into (2) and use \(\varphi(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\). The right inequality of (2) gives \(\alpha/(2M) \le e^{-x^2/2}/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,x)\); taking logarithms yields the right inequality of (3). The left inequality of (2) gives \(\alpha/(2M) \ge e^{-x^2/2}/\big(\sqrt{2\pi}(x + 1/x)\big)\), which yields the left inequality of (3). When \(x \ge 1\), \(\sqrt{2\pi}\,x > 1\), so the last term on the right of (3) is negative and \((t^\ast_M)^2 \le 2\ln(2M/\alpha)\). Both correction terms are \(O(\ln t^\ast_M) = O(\ln\ln M)\), while \(2\ln(2M/\alpha) = 2\ln M + O(1)\); dividing by \(2\ln M\) gives the limit.

The Bonferroni hurdle is therefore Lesson 1's luck hurdle \(\sqrt{2\ln N}\) in disguise: the largest of \(M\) null statistics and the critical value that controls the chance of any false rejection grow at the same rate. Bounds (3) are tight in practice. At \(M = 316\) and \(\alpha = 5\%\), (1) gives \(t^\ast = 3.7778\), and solving each side of (3) for \(t^\ast\) brackets it between 3.7760 and 3.7928. Harvey, Liu and Zhu report that their Bonferroni benchmark starts at 1.96 and reaches 3.78 by 2012, the value (1) produces at the size of their catalogue. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen find that the majority of factors replicate, and Section 5 sets such benchmarks against that evidence.

Holm's hurdle sits slightly below Bonferroni's, because Holm relaxes the threshold after each rejection. The false-discovery-rate procedure behaves differently. Its cutoff \(u = k^\ast q/(M\,c(M))\) is the largest grid point at which the share of p-values at or below \(u\) is at least \(u\,c(M)/q\), with \(c(M) = \sum_{j \le M} 1/j \approx \ln M + 0.577\). The share of p-values below a level is set by the composition of the literature: how many effects are real and how strong they are. So, for a literature of fixed composition, \(M\) moves the cutoff mainly through the slowly growing \(c(M)\), and the hurdle creeps up far more slowly than Bonferroni's \(\sqrt{2\ln M}\); its exact rate depends on the strength of the real effects. The next lab measures all three on simulated literatures of increasing size.

Lab 1 · Three hurdles as the literature grows

In SIMULATED literatures where 20% of tested predictors are real (with t-statistics drawn around 3) and the rest are null, the Bonferroni hurdle should follow (1) exactly and Holm should sit just below it, both rising with \(M\). The Benjamini–Yekutieli hurdle at a 5% false discovery rate should be nearly flat once \(M\) is in the hundreds, because the share of real effects sets it and \(M\) enters mainly through \(c(M)\).

from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED literatures: M two-sided tests, 20% of them true effects with t ~ N(3, 1).
# A procedure's t-statistic hurdle is the critical value it applies at the step where it stops,
# converted to |t| with the normal approximation.
rng = np.random.default_rng(62)
phi = NormalDist()
alpha, q, reps = 0.05, 0.05, 40


def to_t(p: float) -> float:
    """|t| whose two-sided normal p-value equals p."""
    return phi.inv_cdf(1.0 - p / 2.0)


def hurdles(t: NDArray[np.float64]) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Bonferroni and Holm hurdles at FWER alpha, and the BHY hurdle at FDR q, for one set of tests."""
    m = t.size
    p = np.sort([2.0 * (1.0 - phi.cdf(abs(float(x)))) for x in t])
    ranks = np.arange(1, m + 1)
    holm_fail = np.nonzero(p > alpha / (m - ranks + 1))[0]
    k = int(holm_fail[0]) + 1 if holm_fail.size else m  # Holm stops at its first failure
    c_m = float(np.sum(1.0 / ranks))
    bhy_pass = np.nonzero(p <= ranks * q / (m * c_m))[0]
    k_star = int(bhy_pass[-1]) + 1 if bhy_pass.size else 1  # BHY's cutoff sits at its last rejection
    return to_t(alpha / m), to_t(alpha / (m - k + 1)), to_t(k_star * q / (m * c_m))


print("M  bonferroni  holm  bhy")
for m in (10, 100, 316, 1000, 10000):
    rows = []
    for _ in range(reps):
        effect = rng.random(m) < 0.2
        t = rng.standard_normal(m) + np.where(effect, 3.0, 0.0)
        rows.append(hurdles(t))
    mean = np.mean(np.array(rows), axis=0)
    print(f"{m} {mean[0]:.2f} {mean[1]:.2f} {mean[2]:.2f}")
M  bonferroni  holm  bhy
10 2.81 2.76 3.04
100 3.48 3.46 3.44
316 3.78 3.77 3.51
1000 4.06 4.05 3.54
10000 4.56 4.56 3.64

FWER control demands a hurdle that rises without bound, slowly, as the literature grows: 4.56 at ten thousand tests. FDR control demands a hurdle set mainly by the composition of the literature: in this simulation it moves only from 3.51 to 3.64 between 316 and ten thousand tests, the slow creep coming from \(c(M)\). With a larger share of real or stronger effects it would sit lower (Problem E2), which is why the FDR hurdle reported for real data can differ from this simulation's. The lab shows the mechanism, not the hurdle appropriate for any actual literature.

On the real catalogue, Harvey, Liu and Zhu find that the Benjamini–Yekutieli benchmark stabilizes at 3.39 at a 1% false discovery rate and is 2.78 at 5% in 2012, and conclude that the minimum threshold for 5% significance is about 2.8; Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen dispute the argument that multiple testing of too many factors has produced a replication crisis (Section 5). Their headline recommendation is rounder: a new factor needs to clear a t-statistic greater than 3.0. They qualify it themselves: a factor derived from first principles can justify a lower threshold than one found by a purely empirical search, but a t-statistic of 2.0 is no longer appropriate even for factors derived from theory. Section 4 turns that qualification into arithmetic.

3p-hacking and the garden of forking paths

The hurdle in Section 2 corrects for the tests a field has run and published. A second multiplicity hides inside each paper: the choices made before any test is reported.

Definition 3.1 (p-hacking)

p-hacking is choosing among analyses of the same question after seeing their results, through the sample, the variables, how they are aggregated, the estimator, the standard errors or the exclusions, and reporting a p-value as though the chosen analysis had been fixed in advance. The garden of forking paths is the special case in which only one analysis is ever run, but which one depends on the data. The reported test then still selects among the analyses that would have been run had the data looked different.

Harvey lists forms of p-hacking, some more egregious than others: running thousands of correlations and reporting only the most significant, which he calls academic fraud; studying correlations among eleven variables and reporting ten, noting that not reporting all variables examined is commonplace in financial economics; trying aggregations of variables until one passes; and trying statistical approaches (linear probability versus logit or probit, panel regression versus Fama–MacBeth, Newey–West lag 1 versus lag 4, different clustering) and reporting the most significant. Outside finance, Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn show, with computer simulations and two experiments, that flexibility in data collection, analysis and reporting dramatically increases actual false-positive rates above the nominal 5%. In American Scientist, Gelman and Loken argue that data-contingent analysis creates this multiplicity even when a researcher runs a single, theoretically motivated analysis and never consciously fishes; Definition 3.1 and the forking-paths misconception below state the mechanism precisely. Both claims are contested. Rubin argues that questionable research practices, p-hacking and forking paths among them, do not usually inflate the relevant Type I error rates above their nominal level. Ulrich and Miller, in a statistical model of replication that includes such practices, find that the base rate of true effects is the major factor that determines how often results replicate, a point they say is under-appreciated by accounts that blame poor replicability mainly on the practices. Kravitz and Mitrof, applying three common questionable practices (retaining pilot data, adding data after checking for significance, and not publishing null findings) to a large data set randomized to a true null, find false discovery rates far above 5%, which they argue can explain the lion's share of replication failures in psychological science.

Proposition 3.2 (the cost of reporting the best of \(K\) analyses)

Let \(P_1, \dots, P_K\) be p-values of \(K\) analyses of a true null, each uniform on \((0,1)\). If they are independent, the reported minimum satisfies \(\Prob\big(\min_k P_k \le \alpha\big) = 1 - (1-\alpha)^K\), and \(\min_k P_k\) has the Beta\((1, K)\) distribution with mean \(1/(K+1)\). For dependent p-values, \(\alpha \le \Prob(\min_k P_k \le \alpha) \le \min(1, K\alpha)\).

Proof

Independence gives \(\Prob(\min_k P_k > u) = \prod_k \Prob(P_k > u) = (1-u)^K\) for \(u \in [0,1]\), so the distribution function of the minimum is \(1 - (1-u)^K\). That is the Beta\((1,K)\) distribution function, whose mean is \(1/(1+K)\). Setting \(u = \alpha\) gives the first claim. Without independence, \(\{\min_k P_k \le \alpha\} \supseteq \{P_1 \le \alpha\}\) gives the lower bound, and the union bound \(\Prob(\cup_k\{P_k \le \alpha\}) \le \sum_k \Prob(P_k \le \alpha) = K\alpha\) gives the upper bound.

Real specification searches sit between the two bounds, because the analyses share data. The next lab measures where.

Lab 2 · The p-hacking engine

In a SIMULATED market where no asset is predictable, a researcher tests a rule that buys assets with high past returns and sells those with low ones, in 36 variants: three universes, four lookback windows and three choices of how many recent months to skip. Each test is valid on its own, so a single pre-specified variant should be significant about 5% of the time. Reporting the best of the 36 should be significant far more often. Because the variants share data, the rate should land well below the independent-test value \(1 - 0.95^{36} = 0.842\) of Proposition 3.2.

from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED null world: 60 assets with iid monthly returns and no predictability.
# A researcher searches 36 cross-sectional momentum specifications in each world:
# 3 universes x 4 lookbacks x 3 choices of how many recent months to skip.
rng = np.random.default_rng(606)
n_assets, n_months, n_worlds = 60, 240, 300
lookbacks, skips = (3, 6, 9, 12), (0, 1, 2)
universes = (slice(0, 30), slice(30, 60), slice(0, 60))
start = max(lookbacks) + max(skips)  # common first month, so every specification uses the same sample
phi = NormalDist()


def strategy(r: NDArray[np.float64], c: NDArray[np.float64], lookback: int, skip: int) -> NDArray[np.float64]:
    """Monthly returns of long-short momentum held one month: weights rank each asset's return over
    months t-lookback-skip .. t-1-skip against the cross-sectional mean, one dollar per side.
    c holds cumulative returns with a leading row of zeros, so c[t] sums months 0..t-1."""
    t = np.arange(start, r.shape[0])
    past = c[t - skip] - c[t - skip - lookback]
    w = past - past.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    w = w / np.abs(w).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) * 2.0
    return np.asarray(np.sum(w * r[start:], axis=1))


def two_sided_p(x: NDArray[np.float64]) -> float:
    """p-value of the mean monthly return (non-overlapping holdings, so the iid t-test applies)."""
    t = x.mean() / (x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(x.size))
    return 2.0 * (1.0 - phi.cdf(abs(float(t))))


smallest = np.empty(n_worlds)
single = np.empty(n_worlds)
for world in range(n_worlds):
    r = rng.standard_normal((n_months, n_assets)) * 0.05
    c = np.vstack([np.zeros((1, n_assets)), np.cumsum(r, axis=0)])
    pvals = [two_sided_p(strategy(r[:, u], c[:, u], k, s)) for u in universes for k in lookbacks for s in skips]
    smallest[world] = min(pvals)
    single[world] = pvals[-2]  # the one pre-specified test: all assets, 12-month lookback, skip 1 month

share = float(np.mean(smallest < 0.05))
print(f"specifications searched: {len(universes) * len(lookbacks) * len(skips)}")
print(f"false-positive rate of one pre-specified test: {np.mean(single < 0.05):.3f}")
print(f"false-positive rate of the best of the search: {share:.3f}")
print(f"median smallest p-value: {np.median(smallest):.4f}")
print(f"effective number of independent tests: {np.log(1 - share) / np.log(0.95):.1f}")
specifications searched: 36
false-positive rate of one pre-specified test: 0.060
false-positive rate of the best of the search: 0.493
median smallest p-value: 0.0517
effective number of independent tests: 13.3

The pre-specified test rejects at close to its nominal 5%; with 300 simulated worlds the Monte Carlo standard error of that rate is about 0.013. The best of the search is “significant” in about half of the worlds, a rate the 36 correlated variants deliver as if they were 13 independent tests. The median smallest p-value is close to 0.05: a researcher who searches this grid in a market with nothing to find typically ends at the edge of publishability. No individual test in this lab is wrong. The error is in reporting one of them as if it were the only one.

4The Bayesian view: what a t-statistic says about the null

A p-value is a probability computed under the null. A reader of a factor paper wants the probability of the null. Bayes' theorem connects the two only through two further quantities: the prior odds of the null, and the Bayes factor \(B = f(\text{data} \mid H_0)/f(\text{data} \mid H_1)\), with posterior odds \(= B \times\) prior odds. The Bayes factor needs a prior over alternatives, and different researchers can reasonably choose different priors. Its smallest possible value does not.

Definition 4.1 (minimum Bayes factor)

Let \(Z \sim \Normal(\theta, 1)\) be observed at \(z\), with \(H_0: \theta = 0\). For a prior \(\pi_A\) on \(\theta\) under the alternative, the Bayes factor is \(B(\pi_A) = \varphi(z) \big/ \int \varphi(z - \theta)\,\pi_A(d\theta)\). The minimum Bayes factor is \(\mathrm{MBF}(z) = \inf_{\pi_A} B(\pi_A)\). With prior probability \(\pi_0\) of the null and prior odds \(\mathrm{PO} = \pi_0/(1-\pi_0)\), the Bayesianized p-value is \(\mathrm{PO}\cdot\mathrm{MBF}/(1 + \mathrm{PO}\cdot\mathrm{MBF})\).

Theorem 4.2 (the minimum Bayes factor of a normal test)

Under Definition 4.1, \(\mathrm{MBF}(z) = e^{-z^2/2}\), attained by the point mass \(\pi_A = \delta_z\). For every prior \(\pi_A\), the posterior probability of the null satisfies \[ \Prob(H_0 \mid z) \;\ge\; \frac{\mathrm{PO}\, e^{-z^2/2}}{1 + \mathrm{PO}\, e^{-z^2/2}}. \tag{4}\]

Proof

For every \(\theta\), \(\varphi(z - \theta) \le \varphi(0) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}\), so \(\int \varphi(z-\theta)\,\pi_A(d\theta) \le \varphi(0)\) for every probability measure \(\pi_A\), and \(B(\pi_A) \ge \varphi(z)/\varphi(0) = e^{-z^2/2}\). The point mass at \(\theta = z\) gives \(\int\varphi(z-\theta)\,\delta_z(d\theta) = \varphi(0)\), so the bound is attained and is the infimum. For (4), the posterior odds of the null are \(\mathrm{PO}\cdot B(\pi_A) \ge \mathrm{PO}\cdot e^{-z^2/2}\), and \(x \mapsto x/(1+x)\) is increasing on \([0, \infty)\), so the posterior probability is at least the stated bound.

The minimum is the Bayes factor of the alternative most favourable to the researcher: all prior mass placed exactly at the estimate. Every real prior gives a larger Bayes factor, so (4) is the most generous verdict the data can support. proposes reporting this bound, and the resulting Bayesianized p-value, alongside the usual p-value, and credits the minimum Bayes factor to Edwards, Lindman and Savage . For priors on the alternative that are symmetric and descending around the null, Harvey reports a less extreme bound, the SD-MBF \(= -e\cdot p\ln p\) for a p-value \(p\), and states that the MBF is always smaller, because the MBF is the minimum over all priors and the SD-MBF over a class of them. The formula itself has a domain, which Proposition 4.3 makes precise.

Proposition 4.3 (the domain of the SD-MBF formula)

Let \(p = 2(1 - \Phi(z))\) be the two-sided p-value of \(z \gt 0\). For \(0 \lt p \le 1/e\), \(-e\,p\ln p \ge e^{-z^2/2}\). The inequality can fail above \(1/e\): at \(p = 0.6\), \(z = 0.5244\), the formula gives \(2.71828 \times 0.6 \times 0.5108 = 0.833\) and the MBF \(e^{-0.1375} = 0.872\). A value below the MBF cannot be a minimum over any class of priors, so there the formula is not the SD-MBF.

Proof

Write \(Q = 1 - \Phi\) and \(c = \sqrt{\pi/2}/e = 0.46107\). The lower bound in Lemma 2.2 gives \(\varphi(z) \le Q(z)\,(z + 1/z)\), so \(e^{-z^2/2} = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\varphi(z) \le \sqrt{2\pi}\,Q(z)\,(z + 1/z) = \sqrt{\pi/2}\;p\,(z + 1/z)\). It therefore suffices that \(-e\,p\ln p \ge \sqrt{\pi/2}\;p\,(z + 1/z)\), that is, \(u(z) = -\ln p - c\,(z + 1/z) \ge 0\). Since \(-\ln p = -\ln 2 - \ln Q(z)\) and \(Q' = -\varphi\), \(u'(z) = \varphi(z)/Q(z) - c\,(1 - 1/z^2)\). The upper bound in Lemma 2.2 gives \(\varphi(z)/Q(z) \ge z\), and \(z \gt c\,(1 - 1/z^2)\) for every \(z \gt 0\): for \(z \lt 1\) the right side is negative, and for \(z \ge 1\) it is below \(c \lt 1 \le z\). So \(u\) is increasing. At \(p = 1/e\), \(z^\ast = 0.9005\) and \(u(z^\ast) = 1 - c\,(z^\ast + 1/z^\ast) = 1 - 0.46107 \times 2.01101 = 0.0728 \gt 0\). A smaller \(p\) means a larger \(z\), so \(u(z) \ge u(z^\ast) \gt 0\) for \(0 \lt p \le 1/e\). For \(p = 0.6\), \(z = \Phi^{-1}(0.7) = 0.5244\), \(z^2/2 = 0.1375\) and \(-\ln 0.6 = 0.5108\). Finally, the MBF minimizes the Bayes factor over all priors (Theorem 4.2), so it is at most the minimum over any class of them.

Lab 3 · From t-statistics to the probability of the null

At \(t = 1.96\) the minimum Bayes factor should be \(e^{-1.96^2/2} \approx 0.15\), so even with even prior odds the null keeps a posterior probability above 10%. As the prior probability of the null rises toward 0.99, which is plausible for a mechanically mined predictor, even \(t = 3\) should leave the null more likely than not.

import math
from statistics import NormalDist

# Minimum Bayes factor exp(-t^2/2) and the implied lower bound on the probability
# that the null is true, for several prior probabilities of the null.
phi = NormalDist()
priors = (0.5, 0.8, 0.95, 0.99)

print("t  p_two_sided  MBF  " + "  ".join(f"pi0={pi:.2f}" for pi in priors))
for t in (1.96, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0):
    p = 2.0 * (1.0 - phi.cdf(t))
    mbf = math.exp(-t * t / 2.0)
    bounds = []
    for pi0 in priors:
        odds = pi0 / (1.0 - pi0) * mbf  # posterior odds of the null, at their smallest
        bounds.append(odds / (1.0 + odds))
    print(f"{t:.2f} {p:.4f} {mbf:.4f} " + " ".join(f"{b:.4f}" for b in bounds))
t  p_two_sided  MBF  pi0=0.50  pi0=0.80  pi0=0.95  pi0=0.99
1.96 0.0500 0.1465 0.1278 0.3695 0.7357 0.9355
2.50 0.0124 0.0439 0.0421 0.1495 0.4550 0.8131
3.00 0.0027 0.0111 0.0110 0.0425 0.1743 0.5238
3.50 0.0005 0.0022 0.0022 0.0087 0.0399 0.1780
4.00 0.0001 0.0003 0.0003 0.0013 0.0063 0.0321

Each entry is a lower bound on the probability that the null is true. With a 50% prior, \(t = 1.96\) leaves at least a 13% chance of no effect, far from the 5% a careless reading of the p-value suggests. With a 99% prior, \(t = 3\) leaves at least 52%. This is Harvey's point that if the effect being studied is rare, even \(t > 3\) will produce a large number of false positives. Whether true effects are that rare is contested: Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen find that the majority of factors replicate (Section 5). The table cannot say which prior is right. It makes the prior explicit, which a bare t-statistic hides.

5Is most of the zoo false? The evidence on both sides

Sections 2–4 show that the conventional cutoff is too low for a literature this large. Whether most published predictors are therefore false is a separate, empirical question, and the evidence is divided. Module F5 adjudicates it with the full replication data. Here are the positions and what each actually measures.

  • Many are likely false. Harvey, Liu and Zhu count, among 296 published significant factors, 158 that would be false discoveries under Bonferroni, 142 under Holm, 132 under BHY at 1% and 80 under BHY at 5%, and conclude that many are likely false; Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen reach different conclusions (below). Hou, Xue and Zhang find that, with microcaps mitigated through NYSE breakpoints and value weighting, 65% of 452 anomalies fail a single-test hurdle of 1.96 and 82% fail a multiple-test hurdle of 2.78; Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen dispute that this failure means the factors are false. Chordia, Goyal and Saretto, using over 2 million randomly generated strategies, put the multiple-testing thresholds at 3.8 for time-series and 3.4 for cross-sectional regressions and estimate that about 45% of rejections would be false if multiple testing were ignored; Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen contest the inference from such hurdles.
  • Most replicate. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen estimate a Bayesian model of factor replication and find that the majority of factors replicate, cluster into 13 themes, work out of sample in a new data set covering 93 countries, and have evidence that is strengthened, not weakened, by the large number of factors; Hou, Xue and Zhang's failure rates cut the other way. Chen and Zimmermann estimate publication bias in 156 published long-short portfolios from in-sample data alone and find bias-adjusted returns only 12.3% smaller than in-sample returns, with a standard error of 1.7 percentage points, because the dispersion of returns across predictors is too large to be data-mined noise; they offer this as a different perspective on recent papers that find most published predictors likely false.
  • Somewhere between. McLean and Pontiff find that the returns of 97 predictors are 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication, and they treat the out-of-sample decline as an upper bound on data-mining effects, which describes a decline rather than a disappearance. Jacobs and Müller, extending their question to 241 anomalies in 39 stock markets, find that the United States is the only country with a reliable post-publication decline in long-short returns, and read the evidence as mispricing rather than data mining. Dickerson, Julliard and Mueller, analysing models for the joint pricing of corporate bonds and stocks, find that only a handful of factors are likely robust sources of priced risk, yet the true stochastic discount factor is dense in the observable factors, so a Bayesian average over dozens of them prices assets better than any low-dimensional model, in and out of sample; few factors stand alone, while many carry information jointly. Harvey and Liu warn that hurdles also cost missed discoveries, propose calibrating Type I and Type II errors together, and find that current methods lack power to detect outperforming managers. One study, by Hounyo and Lin, proposes a wild bootstrap that keeps each fund's entry and exit and has near-optimal size and more power than the standard bootstrap tests, and with it finds, in mutual fund data, that a measurable fraction of funds outperform the market. Against it, Barras, Scaillet and Wermers, controlling for false discoveries, find that 75% of funds exhibit zero alpha net of expenses and that almost no skilled funds remain by 2006; Andrikogiannopoulou and Papakonstantinou contest that method as conservative, underestimating the proportion of funds with nonzero alpha (Lesson 3, Section 8).

The disagreement is not about arithmetic. Hou, Xue and Zhang's abstract states their construction: microcaps mitigated with NYSE breakpoints and value-weighted returns, judged against a single-test hurdle of 1.96 and a multiple-test hurdle of 2.78. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen's abstract describes a Bayesian model of factor replication that clusters factors into 13 themes and finds the evidence strengthened, not weakened, by the large number of observed factors; it does not state how they construct each factor. On their abstracts the two positions differ in how multiplicity enters: a frequentist hurdle charges for the number of predictors, while a Bayesian model lets that number add evidence. Theorem 4.2 shows how much the prior matters. Lu makes the same point about the replication debate itself: in an empirical illustration, the pessimistic and optimistic conclusions reflect different points along a spectrum of prior assumptions, rather than contradictory evidence. Which object each study tests, and how it builds each factor, needs the papers themselves; F5 reads them and isolates which choice drives the opposite conclusions. F5 also owns the models of replicability and of publication bias: its concepts include publication bias, the anomaly replication rate and hierarchical Bayesian shrinkage of factor alphas. This lesson quotes estimates from such models in the papers above and does not develop the models.

6Where the canon breaks

  • The hurdle needs the unobservable number of tests. Equation (1) uses \(M\), but the relevant \(M\) counts every test tried, published or not. Harvey, Liu and Zhu argue that their 3.0 cutoff is likely too low, because they count only factors published in prominent journals and many tried factors were never published; they note that an incomplete count makes the benchmark a lower bound always for Bonferroni, but not always for Holm and BHY. Chen argues that such calls to raise hurdles may be difficult to justify empirically: the unpublished results must be extrapolated, which can identify a revised hurdle only weakly, while statistics that target published findings alone, such as the false discovery rate and empirical Bayes shrinkage, can be strongly identified. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen contest the premise that a large number of factors weakens the evidence.
  • Exchangeability is assumed, not justified. Bonferroni, Holm and BHY treat every hypothesis alike. A predictor with a prior economic rationale and one found by mining accounting ratios receive the same hurdle. The Bayesian bound of Section 4 is the honest way to let them differ: through the prior odds, stated in the open.
  • How much to correct is itself a modelling choice. Viviano, Wüthrich and Niehaus derive multiple-testing adjustments from an economic model of research: adjustment is appropriate to the extent that research costs do not vary with the number of hypotheses, a Bonferroni-type correction emerges when all costs are fixed, no correction is needed when costs scale with the hypothesis count, and in their drug-approval and program-evaluation applications some adjustment is warranted, but less than standard practice implies. Their applications are outside finance; whether a field-wide hurdle fits a factor literature depends on what testing one more predictor costs.
  • The normal approximation is used twice. Hurdles convert p-values to t-statistics through \(\Phi\), and the MBF formula assumes a normal test statistic. Neither holds exactly for small samples, fat tails or serially correlated predictor returns, where the standard errors themselves are a forking path.
  • A significance hurdle is not a decision rule. A predictor can clear 3.0 and still be useless after costs, or fail it and still be worth a small allocation. Hurdles control error rates. Whether to trade is a separate decision, which Lessons 7 and 8 approach through the deflated Sharpe ratio and the probability of backtest overfitting.

7Misconceptions

  1. “A t-statistic above 3 proves the predictor is real.”

    A hurdle controls an error rate over a family of tests; it says nothing certain about one predictor. Lab 3 shows that with a 99% prior probability of the null, \(t = 3\) still leaves the null more likely than not. Lab 1 shows that the hurdle appropriate for a literature depends on how many predictors were tried and how many are real.

  2. “p-hacking requires dishonesty.”

    Every test in Lab 2 is individually valid, and the researcher it models never lies. The inflation comes from choosing among valid analyses after seeing results. The forking-paths argument extends this to a researcher who runs only one analysis, chosen in light of the data. Rubin contests it, arguing that forking paths do not usually inflate the relevant Type I error rate.

  3. “Bayes factors are useless because nobody agrees on the prior.”

    The minimum Bayes factor needs no prior on the alternative: Theorem 4.2 takes the infimum over all of them. What remains is the prior probability of the null, and Lab 3 shows the conclusion for every value of it, so a disagreement becomes explicit rather than hidden inside a 5% convention.

  4. “The Bonferroni hurdle makes discovery impossible in a large literature.”

    By Theorem 2.3 it grows only like \(\sqrt{2\ln M}\): from 3.78 at 316 tests to 4.56 at ten thousand (Lab 1). Strong effects clear it. What it rules out is certifying weak effects one at a time.

  5. “The replication question is settled.”

    Section 5 shows careful studies reaching opposite headlines. On their abstracts they differ in how multiplicity enters: a frequentist hurdle charges for the number of predictors, while a Bayesian model lets that number add evidence. Citing either side alone is a one-sided treatment of a contested claim.

8Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · Bonferroni hurdles

Compute the two-sided Bonferroni hurdle (1) at \(\alpha = 5\%\) for \(M = 1, 10, 100, 316, 1000\) and \(10^4\), and compare each with the upper bound \(\sqrt{2\ln(2M/\alpha)}\) from Theorem 2.3.

Solution

\(t^\ast_M = \Phi^{-1}(1 - 0.025/M)\): 1.9600, 2.8070, 3.4808, 3.7778, 4.0556 and 4.5648. With \(\alpha = 0.05\), \(2M/\alpha = 40M\), and the bound \(\sqrt{2\ln(40M)}\) gives 2.7162, 3.4616, 4.0728, 4.3462, 4.6036 and 5.0792. The bound applies because \(t^\ast_M \ge 1\) for every \(M\) here. It overstates the hurdle by between 0.51 and 0.76, the effect of the \(-2\ln(\sqrt{2\pi}\,t^\ast)\) term it drops. The ratio of hurdle to bound rises from 0.72 to 0.90 across the table, slowly toward 1, as the theorem's limit requires.

M2 · A Bayesianized p-value

A predictive regression reports \(t = 2.8\). Treating it as normal, compute the two-sided p-value, the minimum Bayes factor, and the lower bound (4) on the probability of the null for prior odds of 1:1 and 2:1 in favour of the null.

Solution

The p-value is \(2(1 - \Phi(2.8)) = 0.0051\). The MBF is \(e^{-2.8^2/2} = e^{-3.92} = 0.01984\). With \(\mathrm{PO} = 1\), the bound is \(0.01984/1.01984 = 0.0195\). With \(\mathrm{PO} = 2\), it is \(0.03968/1.03968 = 0.0382\). Even at the most favourable alternative, a 2:1 prior against the effect leaves about a 4% probability that it is absent: small, but seven times the p-value.

M3 · Two bounds at \(p = 0.01\)

For a two-sided p-value of 0.01, compute the SD-MBF \(-e\,p\ln p\), then the MBF of the corresponding normal statistic. Which is smaller, and why must it be?

Solution

SD-MBF \(= -e \times 0.01 \times \ln 0.01 = 2.71828 \times 0.01 \times 4.60517 = 0.1252\). The statistic with two-sided p-value 0.01 is \(z = \Phi^{-1}(0.995) = 2.5758\), so MBF \(= e^{-2.5758^2/2} = 0.0362\). The MBF is smaller because it is the infimum over all priors on the alternative, while the SD-MBF is the infimum over the smaller class of priors symmetric and descending around the null. An infimum over a larger set can only be smaller.

Proof

P1 · One-sided alternatives

Restrict the alternative to \(\theta > 0\) in Definition 4.1. Show that the minimum Bayes factor is \(e^{-z^2/2}\) when \(z > 0\) and 1 when \(z \le 0\).

Solution

The Bayes factor is \(\varphi(z)/\int_{(0,\infty)}\varphi(z-\theta)\,\pi_A(d\theta)\), and the infimum over \(\pi_A\) supported on \((0,\infty)\) is \(\varphi(z)/\sup_{\theta > 0}\varphi(z - \theta)\): the integral is at most the supremum, and point masses approach it. If \(z > 0\), the supremum is \(\varphi(0)\), attained at \(\theta = z\), so the MBF is \(e^{-z^2/2}\) as before. If \(z \le 0\), then \(|z - \theta| > |z|\) for every \(\theta > 0\) and \(|z - \theta| \to |z|\) as \(\theta \downarrow 0\), so the supremum is \(\varphi(z)\) (not attained) and the MBF is 1. Data pointing the wrong way cannot count as any evidence against the null.

P2 · The hurdle a Bayesian would set

Find the smallest \(|t|\) for which the bound (4) falls to 5% at prior probability \(\pi_0\) of the null, and evaluate it for \(\pi_0 = 0.5, 0.9, 0.99\).

Solution

The bound \(x/(1+x)\) with \(x = \mathrm{PO}\,e^{-t^2/2}\) equals 0.05 when \(x = 0.05/0.95 = 1/19\). So \(e^{-t^2/2} = 1/(19\,\mathrm{PO})\) and \(t = \sqrt{2\ln(19\,\mathrm{PO})}\). For \(\pi_0 = 0.5\) (\(\mathrm{PO} = 1\)): \(\sqrt{2\ln 19} = 2.427\). For \(\pi_0 = 0.9\) (\(\mathrm{PO} = 9\)): \(\sqrt{2\ln 171} = 3.207\). For \(\pi_0 = 0.99\) (\(\mathrm{PO} = 99\)): \(\sqrt{2\ln 1881} = 3.883\). This is the formal version of Harvey, Liu and Zhu's remark that a theory-backed factor (lower \(\pi_0\)) can justify a lower hurdle than a mined one . Because the MBF is the most favourable Bayes factor, these hurdles are minimal.

P3 · Selecting among analyses that share data

Let \(Z_1, Z_2\) be bivariate normal test statistics with unit variances and correlation \(\rho \in [0,1)\), both with mean zero under the null. The researcher reports \(\max(|Z_1|,|Z_2|)\) against the two-sided 5% critical value \(\kappa = 1.96\). Show that the false-positive rate is non-increasing in \(\rho\) between its endpoints \(1 - 0.95^2 = 0.0975\) at \(\rho = 0\) and \(0.05\) as \(\rho \to 1\), by expressing it with the bivariate normal distribution function.

Solution

The false-positive rate is \(1 - \Prob(|Z_1| \le \kappa, |Z_2| \le \kappa)\). At \(\rho = 0\), independence gives \(1 - (0.95)^2 = 0.0975\). As \(\rho \to 1\), \(Z_2 \to Z_1\) in probability, so the rate tends to \(\Prob(|Z_1| > \kappa) = 0.05\). For monotonicity, write \(F_\rho = \Prob(|Z_1| \le \kappa, |Z_2| \le \kappa)\) and let \(\Phi_2, \varphi_2\) be the bivariate normal distribution function and density with correlation \(\rho\). Differentiating the density directly verifies \(\partial\varphi_2/\partial\rho = \partial^2\varphi_2/\partial x\,\partial y\); integrating over \((-\infty, x] \times (-\infty, y]\) gives \(\partial\Phi_2(x,y;\rho)/\partial\rho = \varphi_2(x,y;\rho)\). Since \(F_\rho = \Phi_2(\kappa,\kappa) - \Phi_2(\kappa,-\kappa) - \Phi_2(-\kappa,\kappa) + \Phi_2(-\kappa,-\kappa)\), symmetry of \(\varphi_2\) gives \(\partial F_\rho/\partial\rho = 2\varphi_2(\kappa,\kappa;\rho) - 2\varphi_2(\kappa,-\kappa;\rho) \ge 0\), because \(\varphi_2(\kappa,\kappa;\rho) \ge \varphi_2(\kappa,-\kappa;\rho)\) when \(\rho \ge 0\): the exponent \(-(\kappa^2 \mp 2\rho \kappa^2 + \kappa^2)/(2(1-\rho^2))\) is larger with the minus sign. So \(F_\rho\) is non-decreasing and the false-positive rate non-increasing in \(\rho\). Shared data reduces, but never removes, the inflation, which is what Lab 2 measures with 36 correlated analyses.

Empirical

E1 · Pruning the garden

In Lab 2, drop the universe choice (use all 60 assets only) and rerun. Predict, before running, whether the false-positive rate of the search and the effective number of independent tests fall by more or less than the factor-of-three reduction in the number of variants.

Solution

The grid falls from 36 to 12 variants. The two half-universes are independent of each other, and each is correlated with the full universe, so the universe dimension contributed nearly independent tests. Lookback and skip variants are highly correlated within a universe. Dropping the universe choice should therefore remove at least the proportional two-thirds of the effective tests. A reasonable prediction is an effective number near 4–5 and a false-positive rate near \(1 - 0.95^{4.5} \approx 0.21\). Running the change (universes = (slice(0, 60),); pvals[-2] is still the same pre-specified test) gives a rate of 0.240, an effective number of 5.4 tests and a median smallest p-value of 0.111. The reduction is roughly proportional, slightly less than predicted, because the full universe already shares information with each half. Monte Carlo error with 300 worlds is about ±0.025 on the rate.

E2 · What sets the FDR hurdle

In Lab 1, change the share of real effects from 20% to 5% and then to 50%, keeping \(M = 1000\). Predict the direction in which the Benjamini–Yekutieli hurdle moves, then verify.

Solution

The step-up rule rejects up to the largest rank \(k\) with \(p_{(k)} \le kq/(M\,c(M))\). More real effects mean more small p-values, so the largest passing rank \(k^\ast\) grows and the threshold \(k^\ast q/(M\,c(M))\) rises: the hurdle in t-units falls. Running it at \(M = 1000\): with 5% real effects the BHY hurdle is 4.08, essentially the Bonferroni level of 4.06; with 50% it falls to 3.19, against 3.54 for 20%. Only the Bonferroni and Holm hurdles are anchored by \(M\); the FDR hurdle is anchored by the composition of the literature. This is why an FDR hurdle estimated from one literature does not transfer to another.

E3 · A simulated literature with a publication filter

Simulate 400 tested predictors, a share \(\pi_1\) real with \(t \sim \Normal(3,1)\) and the rest null with \(t \sim \Normal(0,1)\). “Publish” those with \(|t| > 1.96\). Among the published, compute the share that are truly null, and the share that Holm at 5% (applied to all 400 tests) would leave unrejected. Repeat for \(\pi_1 = 0.1, 0.3, 0.6\).

Solution

The truly null share among the published is \((1-\pi_1)\cdot 0.05 \,/\, \big((1-\pi_1)\cdot 0.05 + \pi_1\cdot\Prob(|\Normal(3,1)| > 1.96)\big)\). With \(\Prob(|\Normal(3,1)| > 1.96) = \Phi(1.04) + \Phi(-4.96) \approx 0.851\), this gives 0.346, 0.121 and 0.038 for \(\pi_1 = 0.1, 0.3, 0.6\). The simulation below (seeded) checks them and adds Holm:

from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np

# SIMULATED literature: 400 tested predictors, a share pi1 real with t ~ N(3, 1), the rest null.
# "Published" means |t| > 1.96. Holm at 5% is applied to all 400 two-sided tests.
rng = np.random.default_rng(64)
phi = NormalDist()
m, reps, alpha = 400, 400, 0.05
holm_cut = np.array([alpha / (m - j) for j in range(m)])  # alpha / (m + 1 - k) for ranks k = 1..m

print("pi1  null_share_of_published  holm_unrejected_share_of_published  mean_holm_hurdle")
for pi1 in (0.1, 0.3, 0.6):
    null_share, unrejected, hurdle = [], [], []
    for _ in range(reps):
        real = rng.random(m) < pi1
        t = rng.standard_normal(m) + np.where(real, 3.0, 0.0)
        p = np.array([2.0 * (1.0 - phi.cdf(abs(float(x)))) for x in t])
        order = np.argsort(p)
        fail = np.nonzero(p[order] > holm_cut)[0]
        stop = int(fail[0]) if fail.size else m  # Holm rejects the `stop` smallest p-values
        rejected = np.zeros(m, dtype=bool)
        rejected[order[:stop]] = True
        published = np.abs(t) > 1.96
        null_share.append(np.mean(~real[published]))
        unrejected.append(np.mean(~rejected[published]))
        hurdle.append(phi.inv_cdf(1.0 - holm_cut[min(stop, m - 1)] / 2.0))
    print(f"{pi1} {np.mean(null_share):.3f} {np.mean(unrejected):.3f} {np.mean(hurdle):.2f}")
pi1  null_share_of_published  holm_unrejected_share_of_published  mean_holm_hurdle
0.1 0.344 0.844 3.83
0.3 0.119 0.784 3.82
0.6 0.037 0.763 3.80

The simulated null shares agree with the derived values within Monte Carlo error. The share of published findings that Holm leaves unrejected is far larger: 0.844, 0.784 and 0.763. Holm's hurdle averages 3.80 to 3.83, just below Bonferroni's \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - 0.05/800) = 3.836\) at \(M = 400\), so it fails many real effects whose estimates land between 1.96 and 3.8. The exercise separates two numbers that are easy to conflate: the fraction of published findings that are false, which depends on \(\pi_1\), and the fraction that fail a hurdle, which also counts real but weakly estimated effects. HLZ's counts in Section 5 are of the second kind.

Research

R1 · A pre-analysis plan for a predictor paper

Write the pre-analysis plan you would register before testing a new cross-sectional return predictor, so that a referee could compute its multiplicity. Name every forking path from Harvey's list that the plan closes.

Solution

A complete plan covers eight points.

  1. The predictor: its exact construction and the economic argument for it, which fixes the prior side of P2.
  2. The sample: universe, period, exclusions (price floors, microcaps), and data vintages. This closes data-set and exclusion choices.
  3. The test: sorts or regressions, weighting, breakpoints, the standard-error method and lag length. This closes the estimator and the Newey–West lag choice.
  4. The primary statistic and decision rule: the hurdle to be applied, justified by M and by the prior.
  5. A fixed list of robustness checks, every one of which is reported.
  6. A rule for aggregating variables declared in advance, which closes aggregation search.
  7. A commitment to report all variants examined, with the trial log of L01 Problem R1.
  8. Holdout data reserved and opened once.

The plan cannot close what is invisible to it: ideas the author abandoned before writing the plan. That residual multiplicity is why a reader should still apply a hurdle above 1.96.

R2 · Opposite headlines from overlapping data

Hou, Xue and Zhang conclude that most anomalies fail; Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen conclude that most factors replicate . Using only what their abstracts state, list the methodological differences that could produce both conclusions without either being wrong, and design one test that would discriminate between them.

Solution

The abstracts already reveal three margins.

  1. Criterion. Hou, Xue and Zhang count failures to clear a single-test hurdle of 1.96 or a multiple-test hurdle of 2.78 . Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen estimate a Bayesian model of factor replication, which their abstract says leads to different conclusions; whether they also apply a hurdle to each factor is not in the abstract, so that needs the paper itself .
  2. Construction. Hou, Xue and Zhang mitigate microcaps with NYSE breakpoints and value-weighted returns; their abstract adds that even replicated anomalies have much smaller economic magnitudes than originally reported .
  3. Pooling. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen cluster factors into themes and use the large number of factors as evidence, which a hurdle treats as a penalty .

A discriminating test is to fix one construction (breakpoints, weighting, sample), run both criteria on the same factors, and decompose the difference in replication rates into the part due to construction and the part due to the criterion. Then, on post-sample data unseen by either study, check which criterion better predicts which factors keep earning returns. That is the design F5's project implements.

R3 · A reporting standard

Propose what a factor paper should report so that a reader can compute both the multiple-testing hurdle and the Bayesianized p-value without trusting the author. Defend each item against the objection that it is burdensome.

Solution
  1. The number of variants examined, with their t-statistics. This lets a reader apply Holm or BHY within the paper (Lessons 2–3) and see the search. The burden is a table the author already has.
  2. The test statistic and its distribution (normal approximation, degrees of freedom, standard-error method), so that the conversion to p-values and to the MBF is checkable.
  3. The MBF \(e^{-t^2/2}\) and the bound (4) for a stated range of prior probabilities, as Lab 3 tabulates. One line, and it makes the author's implicit prior explicit.
  4. Where the predictor came from (theory or search), which bears on a reader's prior.
  5. Data and code, so the other items can be verified.

The objection that the prior is subjective is answered by reporting the whole range. The objection that variants are many is answered by the fact that the hurdle needs only their count and dependence. A paper that cannot report the number of variants it examined is asking for a 1.96 hurdle it has not earned.

Answer from memory, then check. Finishing the quiz adds this lesson's cards to your review deck.

  1. What is the two-sided Bonferroni t-statistic hurdle at \(\alpha = 5\%\) for \(M = 316\) tests? (two decimals)

    \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - 0.05/632) = 3.78\), the value Harvey, Liu and Zhu's Bonferroni benchmark reaches by 2012 (Section 2).

  2. As the number of tests \(M\) grows, the Bonferroni hurdle grows at what rate?

    Options

    Theorem 2.3, proved from the Mills-ratio bounds. It is the same rate as Lesson 1's luck hurdle.

  3. Why does the Benjamini–Yekutieli hurdle barely rise with \(M\) in Lab 1?

    Options

    The step-up cutoff \(k^\ast q/(M c(M))\) depends on how many hypotheses are rejected, which the share and strength of real effects drive. For a literature of fixed composition, \(M\) moves it mainly through \(c(M) \approx \ln M\), so in Lab 1 the hurdle creeps up far more slowly than Bonferroni's.

  4. A researcher reports the best of 36 independent analyses of a true null at the 5% level. What is the false-positive rate? (three decimals)

    Proposition 3.2: \(1 - 0.95^{36} = 0.842\). Lab 2's correlated analyses gave 0.493, \(0.493/0.842 = 0.59\) of that.

  5. Which statement describes the garden of forking paths?

    Options

    The multiplicity is in the analyses that could have been run, not in the number actually run.

  6. What is the minimum Bayes factor for a normal statistic \(z = 2\)? (four decimals)

    \(e^{-z^2/2} = e^{-2} = 0.1353\).

  7. The minimum Bayes factor is attained by which prior on the alternative?

    Options

    Theorem 4.2: \(\varphi(z-\theta) \le \varphi(0)\), with equality only at \(\theta = z\). It is the alternative most favourable to the researcher.

  8. With a prior probability of the null of 0.99, what is the lower bound on the probability of the null after \(t = 3\)? (four decimals)

    \(\mathrm{PO} = 99\), \(\mathrm{MBF} = e^{-4.5} = 0.0111\), \(x = 1.0998\), and \(x/(1+x) = 0.5238\) (Lab 3).

  9. Why is a Bonferroni hurdle computed from the published factor literature a lower bound?

    Options

    The true number of tests is unobservable and larger, and the Bonferroni hurdle rises with it. Harvey, Liu and Zhu note that the same argument does not always hold for Holm and BHY, and Chen argues that extrapolating the unpublished tests can identify a revised hurdle only weakly (§6).

  10. Which pair reaches opposite conclusions on whether most published factors replicate?

    Options

    Significance hurdles with microcap-robust construction versus a Bayesian replication model. F5 adjudicates the difference.
