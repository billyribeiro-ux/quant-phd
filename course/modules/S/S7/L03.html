A desk that screens five hundred signals does not need a guarantee that none of its discoveries is false. It needs most of them to be real. Family-wise control buys the first guarantee at a price that grows with the size of the search, until almost nothing survives. Controlling the false discovery rate buys the second guarantee instead, keeps power as the search grows, and remains valid under the positive dependence that shared risk factors create. This lesson proves all of that, and then shows the catch: the rate is an average across studies, and the share of false discoveries in your study can be several times the target.

1From the family-wise error rate to the false discovery rate

Keep the setting of Lesson 2. There are \(m\) hypotheses \(H_1, \dots, H_m\) with p-values \(p_1, \dots, p_m\). An unknown set \(\mathcal{H}_0\) of \(m_0\) hypotheses is true, and the remaining \(m_1 = m - m_0\) are false. A procedure rejects \(R\) hypotheses, \(V\) of them true nulls. The family-wise error rate \(\FWER = \Prob(V \ge 1)\) charges the same penalty for one false discovery among three rejections as for one among three hundred.

That price grows with \(m\). Bonferroni at \(\alpha = 0.05\) with \(m = 1000\) one-sided z-tests rejects only when \(z > \Phi^{-1}(1 - 0.05/1000) = 3.891\). A real effect whose z-statistic has mean 3 is then found with probability \(1 - \Phi(3.891 - 3) = 0.187\), against \(1 - \Phi(1.645 - 3) = 0.912\) for the same effect tested alone. Benjamini and Hochberg's answer was to change the error being controlled.

Definition 1.1 (false discovery proportion and rate)

The false discovery proportion is \(\FDP = V / \max(R, 1)\), the share of rejections that are false, set to 0 when nothing is rejected. The false discovery rate is its expectation, \(\FDR = \E[\FDP]\).

Proposition 1.2 (FDR and FWER)

For any procedure, \(\FDR \le \FWER\). When every hypothesis is true (\(m_0 = m\)), \(\FDR = \FWER\).

Proof

If \(V = 0\) then \(\FDP = 0\). If \(V \ge 1\) then \(R \ge V \ge 1\) and \(\FDP = V/R \le 1\). Hence \(\FDP \le \indic\{V \ge 1\}\) pointwise, and taking expectations gives \(\FDR \le \Prob(V \ge 1)\). When \(m_0 = m\) every rejection is false, so \(V = R\) and \(\FDP = \indic\{R \ge 1\} = \indic\{V \ge 1\}\).

So any procedure that controls the FWER at \(\alpha\) also controls the FDR at \(\alpha\), and the two coincide when nothing is real. Benjamini and Hochberg propose controlling the FDR instead, and argue that the gain in power can be substantial where control of the expected proportion of false rejections is what the problem calls for. Section 4 measures that gain.

2The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure

Definition 2.1 (Benjamini–Hochberg)

Order the p-values \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}\) and fix a level \(q \in (0,1)\). The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure computes \[ k^\ast = \max\Big\{ k \in \{1,\dots,m\} : p_{(k)} \le \frac{k q}{m} \Big\}, \tag{1}\] with \(k^\ast = 0\) if no \(k\) qualifies, and rejects the hypotheses with the \(k^\ast\) smallest p-values.

The procedure compares the \(k\)-th smallest p-value with a line that rises linearly in \(k\), and scans from the top: it finds the largest \(k\) whose p-value lies under the line. One p-value under its line at rank \(k\) carries every smaller p-value with it, even those above their own lines. That is what “step-up” means, and it is the source of BH's power.

Example 2.2 (ten signals)

Ten p-values, already sorted: 0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.021, 0.024, 0.046, 0.060, 0.074, 0.205, 0.212, with \(q = 0.05\). The BH lines are \(0.005k\): 0.005, 0.010, 0.015, 0.020, 0.025, 0.030, 0.035, 0.040, 0.045, 0.050. The fifth p-value, 0.024, lies under its line 0.025, and no larger rank qualifies (0.046 > 0.030, 0.060 > 0.035, 0.074 > 0.040, 0.205 > 0.045, 0.212 > 0.050). So \(k^\ast = 5\) and BH rejects five hypotheses, including the fourth, whose p-value 0.021 exceeds its own line 0.020. Holm's lines at the same level are \(0.05/(m-k+1)\), which are 0.0050, 0.0056 and 0.0063 for the first three ranks. Holm rejects the first hypothesis (0.001 ≤ 0.0050) and stops at the second (0.008 > 0.0056). Bonferroni also rejects one.

Two equivalent descriptions of the BH rejection set make the proofs short. Write \(N(k) = \#\{j : p_j \le kq/m\}\) for the number of p-values under the \(k\)-th line.

Lemma 2.3 (counting form)

(a) \(p_{(k)} \le kq/m\) if and only if \(N(k) \ge k\). Hence \(R := k^\ast = \max\{k : N(k) \ge k\}\), with \(R = 0\) if the set is empty.
(b) BH rejects \(H_i\) if and only if \(p_i \le Rq/m\).
(c) \(R\) is non-increasing in each \(p_j\): raising any p-value cannot add rejections.

Proof

(a) At least \(k\) p-values lie at or below \(kq/m\) exactly when the \(k\)-th smallest does.

(b) If \(R = 0\), nothing is rejected, and no p-value equals 0: a zero p-value would give \(N(1) \ge 1\) and hence \(R \ge 1\) by (a). Suppose \(R \ge 1\). A rejected \(H_i\) has \(p_i \le p_{(R)} \le Rq/m\). Conversely, suppose \(p_i \le Rq/m\) but \(H_i\) is not rejected. Then \(p_i\) is \(p_{(j)}\) for some \(j > R\), and \(p_{(j)} \le Rq/m \le jq/m\) contradicts the maximality of \(R\).

(c) Raising one p-value can only lower each count \(N(k)\), so the largest \(k\) with \(N(k) \ge k\) cannot increase.

3BH controls the false discovery rate

The proof turns on one device: for a true null \(H_i\), replace \(p_i\) by 0 and look at how many rejections the remaining p-values support. Write \(p^{(i)}\) for the vector \(p\) with its \(i\)-th entry set to 0, and \(R_i\) for the number of BH rejections it produces. \(R_i\) is a function of \((p_j)_{j \ne i}\) alone, and \(R_i \ge 1\), because \(N(1) \ge 1\) when one p-value is 0.

Lemma 3.1 (leave one out)

For every \(i\) and every \(k \in \{1, \dots, m\}\), \[ \{p_i \le kq/m,\; R = k\} = \{p_i \le kq/m,\; R_i = k\}. \]

Proof

Fix \(k\) and suppose \(p_i \le kq/m\). For every \(j \ge k\) we have \(p_i \le kq/m \le jq/m\), so \(p_i\) is counted in \(N(j)\), and so is the 0 that replaces it in \(p^{(i)}\). All other entries agree. Hence the counts under \(p\) and \(p^{(i)}\) agree at every \(j \ge k\): \(N(j) = N^{(i)}(j)\).

If \(R = k\), then \(N^{(i)}(k) = N(k) \ge k\), so \(R_i \ge k\). For \(j > k\), \(N^{(i)}(j) = N(j) \lt j\) by the maximality of \(R\), so \(R_i \le k\). Hence \(R_i = k\). The converse uses the same equalities with the roles of \(p\) and \(p^{(i)}\) exchanged.

Theorem 3.2 (Benjamini–Hochberg, independent tests)

Assume that each true-null p-value is super-uniform, \(\Prob(p_i \le u) \le u\) for all \(u \in [0,1]\), and that for each \(i \in \mathcal{H}_0\), \(p_i\) is independent of \((p_j)_{j \ne i}\). Then the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure at level \(q\) satisfies \[ \FDR \le \frac{m_0}{m}\, q \le q, \tag{2}\] with equality in the first inequality when the true-null p-values are exactly uniform.

Proof

By Lemma 2.3(b), \(H_i\) is rejected and \(R = k\) exactly when \(p_i \le kq/m\) and \(R = k\). Splitting \(\FDP\) by the value of \(R\), \[ \FDP = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{H}_0} \frac{\indic\{H_i \text{ rejected}\}}{\max(R,1)} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{H}_0} \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac1k\, \indic\{p_i \le kq/m,\; R = k\}. \] Take expectations and apply Lemma 3.1, then the independence of \(p_i\) and \(R_i\) (a function of the other p-values), then super-uniformity: \[ \FDR = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{H}_0} \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac1k\, \Prob(p_i \le kq/m)\, \Prob(R_i = k) \le \sum_{i \in \mathcal{H}_0} \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac1k \cdot \frac{kq}{m}\, \Prob(R_i = k) = \frac{q}{m} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{H}_0} \sum_{k=1}^{m} \Prob(R_i = k). \] Since \(R_i \in \{1, \dots, m\}\), the inner sum is 1, and the bound is \(m_0 q/m\). Every inequality is an equality when \(\Prob(p_i \le u) = u\).

Two features of (2) matter in practice. The bound is \(m_0 q/m\), not \(q\): when most hypotheses are false, BH is conservative by the factor \(\pi_0 = m_0/m\), which Section 6 exploits. And when nothing is real, BH's FDR equals its FWER (Proposition 1.2), so it is exactly a level-\(q\) test of the complete null.

Lab 1 · Checking Theorem 3.2 by simulation

With 200 independent one-sided z-tests at \(q = 0.10\), the realized FDR should equal \(q = 0.10\) when all hypotheses are null and \(m_0 q / m = 0.08\) when 40 of them are real effects with mean 3, up to Monte Carlo error. Under the complete null, the share of replications with no rejection should be \(1 - q = 0.90\). The data are SIMULATED.

import math

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED one-sided z-tests: nulls have mean 0, true effects have mean 3.
rng = np.random.default_rng(303)
m, q, effect = 200, 0.10, 3.0
upper_tail = np.frompyfunc(lambda z: 0.5 * math.erfc(z / math.sqrt(2.0)), 1, 1)


def bh_count(p: NDArray[np.float64], level: float) -> NDArray[np.int64]:
    """Number of BH rejections in each row of p (rows are replications)."""
    ordered = np.sort(p, axis=1)
    lines = level * np.arange(1, p.shape[1] + 1) / p.shape[1]
    passing = ordered <= lines
    last = p.shape[1] - np.argmax(passing[:, ::-1], axis=1)
    return np.where(passing.any(axis=1), last, 0)


print("m1  realized_FDR  bound_m0q/m  power  P(R=0)")
for m1, n_reps in ((0, 10000), (40, 2000)):
    mu = np.zeros(m)
    mu[:m1] = effect
    z = rng.standard_normal((n_reps, m)) + mu
    p = upper_tail(z).astype(float)
    r = bh_count(p, q)
    threshold = q * r / m
    rejected = p <= threshold[:, None]
    false = rejected[:, m1:].sum(axis=1)
    fdp = false / np.maximum(r, 1)
    power = rejected[:, :m1].sum(axis=1).mean() / m1 if m1 else 0.0
    print(f"{m1} {fdp.mean():.4f} {(m - m1) * q / m:.4f} {power:.4f} {np.mean(r == 0):.4f}")

Both realized rates sit on the theorem's values within Monte Carlo error. For the complete null that error is about \(\sqrt{0.1 \times 0.9/10000} = 0.003\) per standard error, so 0.098 is within one standard error of 0.10. Under the complete null, the share of runs with at least one rejection equals the FDR, as Proposition 1.2 requires. With real effects present, BH found about four-fifths of them while holding the false share near 8%. The lab tests independent statistics only; Lab 3 relaxes that.

4BH against the family-wise procedures

Proposition 4.1 (BH rejects everything Holm rejects)

At a common level \(\alpha = q\), every hypothesis rejected by Holm's step-down procedure, and therefore every hypothesis rejected by Bonferroni, is rejected by Benjamini–Hochberg.

Proof

Holm's line at rank \(k\) is \(\alpha/(m-k+1)\) and BH's is \(k\alpha/m\). Since \(k(m-k+1) - m = (k-1)(m-k) \ge 0\) for \(1 \le k \le m\), Holm's line lies on or below BH's at every rank. Let Holm reject \(h \ge 1\) hypotheses: \(p_{(1)}, \dots, p_{(h)}\) lie under Holm's lines, so in particular \(p_{(h)} \le \alpha/(m-h+1) \le h\alpha/m\). By (1), \(k^\ast \ge h\), and BH rejects the \(h\) smallest p-values and possibly more. Holm's rejections include Bonferroni's (Lesson 2).

The proof also locates the source of the power gap. Holm's lines start at \(\alpha/m\) and grow only at the very end of the ranking; BH's lines grow linearly from the start. The more false hypotheses there are, the further BH's step-up reaches.

Lab 2 · Power of four procedures as the number of real effects grows

With \(m = 500\) independent one-sided z-tests at level 0.05 and true effects of mean 3, Bonferroni's power should be near \(1 - \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(1 - 0.05/500) - 3) = 1 - \Phi(3.719 - 3) = 0.236\) whatever the number of real effects \(m_1\), and Holm's should barely exceed it. BH's power should rise with \(m_1\). Benjamini–Yekutieli (Section 5) should fall below Bonferroni when real effects are few, because its first line \(q/(m\,c(m))\) is \(c(500) = 6.79\) times stricter than Bonferroni's \(q/m\), and rise above Bonferroni, staying below BH, as \(m_1\) grows. Holm's FWER and BH's FDR should stay at or below 0.05 up to Monte Carlo error. The data are SIMULATED.

import math

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED independent one-sided z-tests; true effects have mean 3. Level 0.05 for every procedure.
rng = np.random.default_rng(305)
m, level, n_reps, effect = 500, 0.05, 2000, 3.0
upper_tail = np.frompyfunc(lambda z: 0.5 * math.erfc(z / math.sqrt(2.0)), 1, 1)
k = np.arange(1, m + 1)
c_m = float(np.sum(1.0 / k))


def step_up(ordered: NDArray[np.float64], lines: NDArray[np.float64]) -> NDArray[np.int64]:
    """Rejections of a step-up procedure: the largest k with p_(k) under its line."""
    passing = ordered <= lines
    return np.where(passing.any(axis=1), m - np.argmax(passing[:, ::-1], axis=1), 0)


def step_down(ordered: NDArray[np.float64], lines: NDArray[np.float64]) -> NDArray[np.int64]:
    """Rejections of a step-down procedure: stop at the first p_(k) above its line."""
    failing = ordered > lines
    return np.where(failing.any(axis=1), np.argmax(failing, axis=1), m)


print("m1  power_Bonferroni  power_Holm  power_BH  power_BY  FWER_Holm  FDR_BH")
for m1 in (10, 50, 150):
    mu = np.zeros(m)
    mu[:m1] = effect
    p = upper_tail(rng.standard_normal((n_reps, m)) + mu).astype(float)
    ordered = np.sort(p, axis=1)
    counts = {
        "bonferroni": np.sum(p <= level / m, axis=1),
        "holm": step_down(ordered, level / (m - k + 1)),
        "bh": step_up(ordered, level * k / m),
        "by": step_up(ordered, level * k / (m * c_m)),
    }
    power, errors = {}, {}
    for name, r in counts.items():
        cutoff = ordered[np.arange(n_reps), np.maximum(r, 1) - 1]
        rejected = (p <= cutoff[:, None]) & (r[:, None] > 0)
        power[name] = rejected[:, :m1].sum(axis=1).mean() / m1
        false = rejected[:, m1:].sum(axis=1)
        errors[name] = (np.mean(false > 0), np.mean(false / np.maximum(r, 1)))
    print(
        f"{m1} {power['bonferroni']:.4f} {power['holm']:.4f} {power['bh']:.4f} {power['by']:.4f} "
        f"{errors['holm'][0]:.4f} {errors['bh'][1]:.4f}"
    )

Family-wise control holds power flat near the Bonferroni value. BH's power rises steeply with the number of real effects, because each real discovery lifts the line for the next. At \(m_1 = 10\), Benjamini–Yekutieli's power (0.1653) is below Bonferroni's (0.2354): with few real effects the step-up barely lifts the line, and BY pays the full factor \(c(m)\); by \(m_1 = 50\) it is above Bonferroni and still below BH. No error rate exceeds 0.05 by more than Monte Carlo error: with 2,000 replications the standard error of an estimated FWER near 0.05 is \(\sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95 / 2000} = 0.0049\), so 0.0535 is within one standard error of the bound, not a violation, and at \(m_1 = 150\) both error rates sit below it, at 0.0380 and 0.0351. Power here is the average share of real effects found. It says nothing about which procedure a desk should use; that depends on what a false discovery costs (Problem R1).

5Dependence: positive regression dependence and the Benjamini–Yekutieli correction

Strategies and factors share risk exposures, so their test statistics are correlated, and Theorem 3.2's independence assumption fails. Two results restore control. The first shows that BH needs no change under the most common kind of dependence. The second gives a correction that works under any dependence at all, at a price in power.

Definition 5.1 (PRDS)

A set \(D \subseteq [0,1]^m\) is increasing if \(x \in D\) and \(y \ge x\) componentwise imply \(y \in D\). The p-values are positively regression dependent on the subset of true nulls (PRDS) if, for every increasing set \(D\) and every \(i \in \mathcal{H}_0\), the function \(u \mapsto \Prob(p \in D \mid p_i = u)\) is non-decreasing.

In words: learning that a true-null p-value is larger makes every “large p-values” event weakly more likely. Proposition 5.3 proves the property for one-sided tests in the equicorrelated Gaussian model that Lab 3 simulates. Benjamini and Yekutieli prove that BH controls the FDR under positive regression dependency on the test statistics of the true nulls, and state that the condition covers, among other cases, multivariate normal test statistics with positive correlation matrix.

Theorem 5.2 (BH under PRDS)

If the p-values are PRDS on the true nulls, each true-null p-value is super-uniform, and \(\Prob(p_i \le u) > 0\) for \(u > 0\), then BH at level \(q\) has \(\FDR \le m_0 q/m\).

Proof

Fix a true null \(i\) and write \(u_k = kq/m\). As in Theorem 3.2, \(\E[\indic\{H_i \text{ rejected}\}/\max(R,1)] = \sum_k \frac1k \Prob(p_i \le u_k, R = k)\). Write the joint probability as \(\Prob(p_i \le u_k)\big[A_k - B_k\big]\), where \(A_k = \Prob(R \le k \mid p_i \le u_k)\) and \(B_k = \Prob(R \le k-1 \mid p_i \le u_k)\). The bracket is a conditional probability of \(\{R = k\}\), so it is non-negative, and \(\Prob(p_i \le u_k) \le u_k = kq/m\) by super-uniformity. Hence the contribution of \(H_i\) is at most \(\frac{q}{m}\sum_{k=1}^m (A_k - B_k)\).

Two facts give \(\sum_k (A_k - B_k) \le 1\). First, by Lemma 2.3(c) the event \(\{R \le k-1\}\) is an increasing set of p-value vectors. Second, for an increasing set \(D\), PRDS makes \(h(v) = \Prob(p \in D \mid p_i = v)\) non-decreasing, and then \(u \mapsto \Prob(p \in D \mid p_i \le u) = \E[h(p_i) \mid p_i \le u]\) is non-decreasing too. To see this, take \(u \lt u'\). Then \(\E[h(p_i) \mid p_i \le u']\) is a weighted average of \(\E[h(p_i) \mid p_i \le u]\) and \(\E[h(p_i) \mid u \lt p_i \le u']\). The second term is at least \(h(u)\), which is at least the first, since \(h\) is non-decreasing. Applying this with \(D = \{R \le k-1\}\) and \(u_{k-1} \lt u_k\) gives \(B_k \ge A_{k-1}\) for \(k \ge 2\). Therefore \[ \sum_{k=1}^m (A_k - B_k) \le \sum_{k=1}^m A_k - B_1 - \sum_{k=2}^{m} A_{k-1} = A_m - B_1 = 1 - 0 = 1, \] because \(R \le m\) always gives \(A_m = 1\), and \(p_i \le u_1\) forces \(N(1) \ge 1\), hence \(R \ge 1\) and \(B_1 = 0\). Summing the bound \(q/m\) over the \(m_0\) true nulls completes the proof.

Proposition 5.3 (equicorrelated one-sided tests are PRDS)

Let \(z_j = \sqrt{\rho}\, W + \sqrt{1-\rho}\, \varepsilon_j + \mu_j\) with \(\rho \in [0,1)\) and \(W, \varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_m\) independent standard normals, and \(p_j = 1 - \Phi(z_j)\). Then the p-values are PRDS on every subset.

Proof

Each \(p_j\) is a decreasing function of \(z_j\), so an increasing set of p-values is a decreasing set \(D'\) of z-vectors, and \(p_i = u\) corresponds to \(z_i = t(u)\) with \(t\) decreasing in \(u\). It suffices to show that \(t \mapsto \Prob(z \in D' \mid z_i = t)\) is non-increasing for every decreasing \(D'\). Raising \(t\) raises the \(i\)-th coordinate itself, which can only move the vector out of a decreasing set; it remains to treat the other coordinates. Given \(z_i = t\), the variable \(W\) is normal with mean \(\sqrt{\rho}\,(t - \mu_i)\) and variance \(1 - \rho\), which is stochastically increasing in \(t\). The other coordinates are \(z_j = \sqrt{\rho}\,W + \sqrt{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_j + \mu_j\), with the \(\varepsilon_j\) independent of \(W\) and of \(z_i\). So \(\Prob(z \in D' \mid z_i = t) = \E[g(W) \mid z_i = t]\), where \(g(w)\) is the probability that the vector with \(W = w\) and \(z_i = t\) lies in \(D'\). Since \(D'\) is decreasing, \(g\) is non-increasing in \(w\). The expectation of a non-increasing function under a stochastically increasing law is non-increasing in \(t\).

Under arbitrary dependence (two-sided tests on correlated statistics, statistics that move in opposite directions) PRDS can fail. Benjamini and Yekutieli's second result covers every case by deflating the level.

Definition 5.4 (Benjamini–Yekutieli)

Let \(c(m) = \sum_{j=1}^m 1/j\). The Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure is BH run at level \(q / c(m)\): it rejects the \(k^\ast\) smallest p-values, with \(k^\ast = \max\{k : p_{(k)} \le kq/(m\, c(m))\}\).

Theorem 5.5 (arbitrary dependence)

If each true-null p-value is uniform on \([0,1]\), then under any joint distribution of the p-values the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure has \(\FDR \le m_0 q/m\). (Problem P3 extends the result to super-uniform nulls.)

Proof

Write \(u_k = kq/(m\,c(m))\), \(u_0 = 0\) and \(I_j = (u_{j-1}, u_j]\). For a true null \(i\), Lemma 2.3(b) (applied at level \(q/c(m)\)) gives a contribution to the FDR of \(\sum_k \frac1k \Prob(p_i \le u_k, R = k)\). A uniform \(p_i\) equals 0 with probability 0, so split \(\{p_i \le u_k\}\) into the disjoint pieces \(\{p_i \in I_j\}\) for \(j \le k\), and use \(1/k \le 1/j\) for \(j \le k\): \[ \sum_{k=1}^m \frac1k \sum_{j=1}^k \Prob(p_i \in I_j, R = k) \le \sum_{j=1}^m \frac1j \sum_{k=j}^m \Prob(p_i \in I_j, R = k) \le \sum_{j=1}^m \frac1j\, \Prob(p_i \in I_j). \] For a uniform \(p_i\), \(\Prob(p_i \in I_j) = u_j - u_{j-1} = q/(m\,c(m))\), so the right side is \(\frac{q}{m\,c(m)} \sum_{j=1}^m \frac1j = q/m\). No step used any property of the joint distribution. Summing over the \(m_0\) true nulls gives \(m_0 q/m\).

The price is the factor \(c(m)\), which grows like \(\ln m\). Exactly, \(c(10) = 2.929\), \(c(100) = 5.187\) and \(c(1000) = 7.485\), against \(\ln m + \emgamma\) = 2.880, 5.182 and 7.485. Lab 2 showed what that costs: at \(m = 500\), BY's power was between 44% and 61% of BH's (0.1653/0.3735 = 0.44, 0.3136/0.6076 = 0.52, 0.4679/0.7715 = 0.61). Harvey, Liu and Zhu adopt this conservative version as their false-discovery benchmark for the factor zoo, setting \(c(M) = \sum_{j=1}^{M} 1/j\) because it allows the procedure to work under arbitrary dependence among the test statistics. Lesson 6 turns their choice into a t-statistic threshold.

Lab 3 · The realized false discovery proportion under dependence

6Estimating the share of true nulls

Theorem 3.2 shows that BH spends only \(\pi_0 q\) of its error budget, where \(\pi_0 = m_0/m\). If \(\pi_0\) were known, running BH at \(q/\pi_0\) would restore control at exactly \(q\) under independence and gain power. Storey reverses the logic of sequential procedures: he fixes the rejection region, estimates the error rate it implies, argues that the positive FDR, \(\E[V/R \mid R > 0]\), is probably the more relevant quantity, defines the q-value as its analogue of the p-value, and reports numerical examples in which the approach yields over eight times the power of the Benjamini–Hochberg method. The power figure comes from examples chosen to show the method, not from a general bound; the gain depends on how many hypotheses are false.

Remark 6.1 (estimating \(\pi_0\))

True-null p-values are uniform, so a fraction \(1 - \lambda\) of them should exceed any \(\lambda \in (0,1)\), while false-null p-values rarely do. Hence \(\hat\pi_0(\lambda) = \#\{j : p_j > \lambda\} / ((1-\lambda)\, m)\). Its expectation is \(\pi_0 + (m_1/m)\,\Prob(p_j > \lambda \mid H_j \text{ false})/(1-\lambda) \ge \pi_0\), so it errs on the conservative side. For one-sided z-tests with effect mean 3 and \(\lambda = 0.5\), a false null exceeds 0.5 only if its statistic falls below 0, with probability \(\Phi(-3) = 0.00135\). With \(m = 1000\) and \(m_0 = 800\), \(\E[\hat\pi_0(0.5)] = (800 \times 0.5 + 200 \times 0.00135)/500 = 0.8005\). Problem E3 tests the adaptive procedure by simulation.

7Local false discovery rates and empirical Bayes

BH and Storey's method describe a list of rejections. A desk deciding how much risk to give one signal asks two questions about that signal: how likely is it to be a null, and how large is its true Sharpe ratio once the selection is accounted for? One model answers both. It treats the hypotheses as a sample from a population, which is reasonable when there are hundreds of them, and lets the data estimate that population.

Definition 7.1 (two-group model)

In the two-group model, the pairs \((H_n, Z_n)\), \(n = 1, \dots, N\), are independent and identically distributed. Each hypothesis is null (\(H_n = 0\)) with probability \(\pi_0 \gt 0\) and non-null (\(H_n = 1\)) with probability \(\pi_1 = 1 - \pi_0\). Given \(H_n = 0\) the statistic \(Z_n\) has density \(f_0\), and given \(H_n = 1\) it has density \(f_1\). The statistics therefore have the mixture density \(f = \pi_0 f_0 + \pi_1 f_1\), with upper-tail probabilities \(S_0(z) = \int_z^\infty f_0\), \(S_1(z) = \int_z^\infty f_1\) and \(S = \pi_0 S_0 + \pi_1 S_1\).

Here \(\pi_0\) is the share of true nulls that Remark 6.1 estimates. The model adds one assumption to Section 1: each hypothesis is null at random, independently of the others. Efron describes the two-groups model as a simple Bayesian construction that facilitates empirical Bayes analysis, and studies the interplay of Bayesian and frequentist ideas in it, with particular attention to Benjamini and Hochberg's FDR method.

Definition 7.2 (local and tail-area false discovery rates)

For \(z\) with \(f(z) \gt 0\), the local false discovery rate is \[ \mathrm{lfdr}(z) = \frac{\pi_0 f_0(z)}{f(z)} = \Prob(H = 0 \mid Z = z), \] and for \(z\) with \(S(z) \gt 0\) the tail-area false discovery rate is \[ \mathrm{Fdr}(z) = \frac{\pi_0 S_0(z)}{S(z)} = \Prob(H = 0 \mid Z \ge z). \] Both equalities are Bayes' rule.

Efron develops an empirical Bayes analysis plan for testing hundreds or thousands of cases at once that uses a local version of the false discovery rate to examine the inference issues. The local rate speaks about one statistic, the tail rate about everything beyond it. They are linked exactly.

Proposition 7.3 (the tail rate averages the local rate)

In the two-group model, \(\mathrm{Fdr}(z) = \E[\mathrm{lfdr}(Z) \mid Z \ge z]\) for every \(z\) with \(S(z) \gt 0\). If \(\mathrm{lfdr}\) is non-increasing on \([z, \infty)\), then \(\mathrm{lfdr}(z) \ge \mathrm{Fdr}(z)\).

Proof

Since \(\pi_0 f_0 \le f\), the null density vanishes wherever \(f\) does, so \[ \E\big[\mathrm{lfdr}(Z)\,\indic\{Z \ge z\}\big] = \int_{\{u \ge z,\ f(u) \gt 0\}} \frac{\pi_0 f_0(u)}{f(u)}\, f(u)\, du = \pi_0 \int_z^\infty f_0(u)\, du = \pi_0 S_0(z). \] Dividing by \(\Prob(Z \ge z) = S(z)\) gives the identity. If \(\mathrm{lfdr}(u) \le \mathrm{lfdr}(z)\) for every \(u \ge z\), the conditional average over \(\{Z \ge z\}\) is at most \(\mathrm{lfdr}(z)\).

The monotonicity condition is a property of the alternative: \(\mathrm{lfdr} = \pi_0/(\pi_0 + \pi_1 f_1/f_0)\) is non-increasing exactly when the likelihood ratio \(f_1/f_0\) is non-decreasing. For \(f_0 = \Normal(0,1)\) and \(f_1 = \Normal(a, 1)\) with \(a \gt 0\), the ratio \(e^{az - a^2/2}\) increases, so the hypothesis at the edge of a tail region is more likely null than the region's average. The next result shows that BH is a rule about tail regions.

Proposition 7.4 (tail rules, BH and the q-value)

In the two-group model with \(f_0\) positive on the whole real line, reject \(H_n\) when \(Z_n \ge z\), and let \(R(z)\) and \(V(z)\) be the numbers of rejections and false rejections.

  1. \(\E[V(z)/R(z) \mid R(z) \gt 0] = \mathrm{Fdr}(z)\), and \(\E[V(z)] = \mathrm{Fdr}(z)\,\E[R(z)]\), for every \(N\).
  2. Let \(p_n = S_0(Z_n)\) be the one-sided p-values, \(Z_{(k)}\) the \(k\)-th largest statistic and \(p_{(k)} = S_0(Z_{(k)})\). Estimate \(S(z)\) by the empirical tail share \(R(z)/N\) and \(\pi_0\) by 1. The estimate \(\widehat{\mathrm{Fdr}}(z) = N S_0(z)/R(z)\) then satisfies \(\widehat{\mathrm{Fdr}}(Z_{(k)}) = N p_{(k)}/k\), so \(\widehat{\mathrm{Fdr}}(Z_{(k)}) \le q\) if and only if \(p_{(k)} \le kq/N\). BH (Definition 2.1) rejects the largest tail region whose estimated Fdr is at most \(q\).
Proof

(a) Condition on the set of indices with \(Z_n \ge z\). The pairs are independent, so given that this set has \(r \ge 1\) members, each of them is a draw from the conditional law of \((H, Z)\) given \(Z \ge z\), and each is null with probability \(\mathrm{Fdr}(z)\). Hence \(\E[V \mid R = r] = r\,\mathrm{Fdr}(z)\). Dividing by \(r\) and averaging over \(r \ge 1\) gives the first identity. Summing \(\E[V \mid R = r]\,\Prob(R = r)\) over \(r\) gives the second.

(b) \(S_0\) is strictly decreasing because \(f_0 \gt 0\), so \(Z_n \ge Z_{(k)}\) if and only if \(p_n \le p_{(k)}\). The statistics have a density, so ties have probability zero and \(R(Z_{(k)}) = k\) almost surely. Then \(\widehat{\mathrm{Fdr}}(Z_{(k)}) = N S_0(Z_{(k)})/k = N p_{(k)}/k\), and the equivalence is a rearrangement. BH's \(k^\ast = \max\{k : p_{(k)} \le kq/N\}\) is the largest such \(k\).

Part (a) makes the positive FDR of Section 6 exact in this model. Storey's q-value, his analogue of the p-value, reads naturally here: the p-value of an observed \(z\) is the smallest level of a region \(\{Z \ge z'\}\) that contains it, and its q-value is the smallest positive FDR of such a region, \(\inf_{z' \le z} \mathrm{Fdr}(z')\). When \(\mathrm{lfdr}\) is non-increasing, so is \(\mathrm{Fdr}\), and the q-value is \(\mathrm{Fdr}(z)\) itself. Part (b) shows BH estimating that tail rate with \(\pi_0\) set to 1, which is one way to see the factor \(\pi_0\) in Theorem 3.2; the adaptive procedure of Problem E3 puts \(\hat\pi_0\) back. Proposition 7.3 adds what neither BH nor a q-value says: when the likelihood ratio is monotone, the last hypotheses on a rejection list are more likely null than the list's average.

Remark 7.5 (empirical Bayes)

The ingredients \(\pi_0\), \(f_0\) and \(f\) are unknown, but with hundreds of statistics the data can estimate them. The mixture \(f\) is the density of the observed statistics, so it can be estimated directly, for example by a kernel density estimate \(\hat f\). The null density \(f_0\) comes from theory, here \(\Normal(0,1)\), and \(\pi_0\) from Remark 6.1. The estimate \(\widehat{\mathrm{lfdr}}(z) = \min\{1, \hat\pi_0 f_0(z)/\hat f(z)\}\) is a Bayes formula whose prior is estimated from the same data, which is what empirical Bayes means. Efron, Tibshirani, Storey and Tusher introduce a simple nonparametric empirical Bayes model for a microarray experiment, use it to reduce the data to a single summary statistic per gene and to make simultaneous inferences, and note that the empirical Bayes inferences are closely related to the frequentist FDR criterion. Efron also argues that many simultaneous tests allow the null hypothesis itself to be estimated, and that this empirical null may be considerably more dispersed than the theoretical null that would be used for any one case considered separately. Roquain and Verzelen show that an estimated null cannot always stand in for the true one: when the nulls are Gaussian with unknown mean and variance, a procedure that asymptotically matches Benjamini–Hochberg run with the true null exists if and only if the number of false nulls is of order less than \(N/\log N\), where \(N\) is the number of tests. Section 9 returns to what that means for a backtest universe.

The same population view corrects the selected Sharpe ratio. Lesson 1 measured the problem: the best of \(N\) trials overstates its own true Sharpe ratio by the data-snooping bias. Write \(X_n = \hSR_n\) for trial \(n\) and \(\theta_n = \SR_n\) for its true per-period Sharpe ratio. As in Lesson 1, take \(X_n \mid \theta_n \approx \Normal(\theta_n, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma^2 = 1/T\), the iid standard error of a Sharpe ratio near zero.

Proposition 7.6 (the normal posterior)

If \(\theta \sim \Normal(\mu, \tau^2)\) and \(X \mid \theta \sim \Normal(\theta, \sigma^2)\) with \(\tau^2, \sigma^2 \gt 0\), then \(X \sim \Normal(\mu, \tau^2 + \sigma^2)\) and \[ \theta \mid X \sim \Normal\big(\mu + B(X - \mu),\; B\sigma^2\big), \qquad B = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2}. \tag{3}\]

Proof

Write \(X = \theta + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \Normal(0, \sigma^2)\) independent of \(\theta\); a sum of independent normals is normal with the summed means and variances. For the posterior, the joint density as a function of \(\theta\) is proportional to \(\exp\{-(x - \theta)^2/(2\sigma^2) - (\theta - \mu)^2/(2\tau^2)\}\). The exponent is quadratic in \(\theta\) with leading coefficient \(-\tfrac12(1/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2)\), so the posterior is normal with variance \((1/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2)^{-1} = \sigma^2\tau^2/(\sigma^2 + \tau^2) = B\sigma^2\). Its mean maximizes the exponent: \((x - \theta)/\sigma^2 = (\theta - \mu)/\tau^2\) gives \(\theta = (\tau^2 x + \sigma^2 \mu)/(\tau^2 + \sigma^2) = \mu + B(x - \mu)\).

Definition 7.7 (empirical Bayes shrinkage of Sharpe ratios)

Empirical Bayes shrinkage of \(N\) Sharpe ratios fits the normal population of Proposition 7.6 by moments and reports its posterior means. Marginally \(X_n \sim \Normal(\mu, \tau^2 + \sigma^2)\), so with \(\bar X\) the cross-sectional mean and \(s_X^2\) the cross-sectional sample variance (divisor \(N - 1\)), \[ \hat\mu = \bar X, \qquad \hat\tau^2 = \max\{0,\, s_X^2 - \sigma^2\}, \qquad \hat B = \frac{\hat\tau^2}{\hat\tau^2 + \sigma^2}, \qquad \tilde\theta_n = \hat\mu + \hat B\,(X_n - \hat\mu). \]

Each estimate moves toward the cross-sectional mean by the share \(1 - \hat B\) of its distance, and that share is the fraction of the observed dispersion that sampling noise alone explains: when \(\hat\tau^2 \gt 0\), \(1 - \hat B = \sigma^2/s_X^2\). Efron and Morris recall that in 1961 James and Stein exhibited an estimator of the mean of a multivariate normal distribution with uniformly lower mean squared error than the sample mean, review it in an empirical Bayes context, and report applications in which the mean squared error of Stein's rule and its generalizations is less than half that of the sample mean. In earlier work they show that the positive-part version of Stein's estimator, which dominates the maximum likelihood estimator of \(k \ge 3\) normal means, is one member of a class of rules that have Bayesian properties and also dominate it. A Stein-type rule that shrinks toward the grand mean uses \(1 - (N - 3)\sigma^2/\sum_n (X_n - \bar X)^2\), floored at zero, in place of \(\hat B\); the moment version is the same expression with \(N - 1\). The constant \(N - 3\) makes the estimated complement unbiased. Under the model \(\sum_n (X_n - \bar X)^2 = (\tau^2 + \sigma^2)\,W\), with \(W\) chi-square with \(\nu = N - 1\) degrees of freedom, and for \(\nu \gt 2\) \[ \E[1/W] = \int_0^\infty \frac{x^{\nu/2 - 2} e^{-x/2}}{2^{\nu/2}\,\Gamma(\nu/2)}\, dx = \frac{2^{\nu/2 - 1}\,\Gamma(\nu/2 - 1)}{2^{\nu/2}\,\Gamma(\nu/2)} = \frac{1}{\nu - 2}, \] using \(\Gamma(a + 1) = a\,\Gamma(a)\). Hence \(\E\big[(N-3)\sigma^2/\sum_n (X_n - \bar X)^2\big] = \sigma^2/(\tau^2 + \sigma^2) = 1 - B\). At \(N = 500\) the two constants differ by the factor \(497/499 = 0.996\).

Proposition 7.8 (selection does not bias a posterior mean)

Let \((\theta_n, X_n)\), \(n = 1, \dots, N\), be independent pairs with \(\E|\theta_n| \lt \infty\), and let \(\tilde\theta_n = \E[\theta_n \mid X_n]\). Let \(n^\ast\) be any index chosen as a function of \(X = (X_1, \dots, X_N)\), for example \(n^\ast = \argmax_n X_n\). Then \(\E[\tilde\theta_{n^\ast}] = \E[\theta_{n^\ast}]\), while the raw estimate of the selected trial has the data-snooping bias \(\E[X_{n^\ast}] - \E[\theta_{n^\ast}]\) of Lesson 1.

Proof

The indicator \(\indic\{n^\ast = n\}\) is a function of \(X\), so \(\E[\theta_{n^\ast} \mid X] = \sum_n \indic\{n^\ast = n\}\,\E[\theta_n \mid X]\). The pairs are independent, so \(\E[\theta_n \mid X] = \E[\theta_n \mid X_n] = \tilde\theta_n\). Hence \(\E[\theta_{n^\ast} \mid X] = \tilde\theta_{n^\ast}\), and taking expectations gives the claim.

The proposition needs the true prior. Empirical Bayes plugs in an estimated one, which is close only if the population really has the assumed shape and \(N\) is large. Efron describes the overestimation of the largest few of many estimates as selection bias, or regression to the mean, and investigates the merits and limitations of Tweedie's formula, first reported by Robbins in 1956, as a simple empirical Bayes approach for correcting it; he notes a close connection between applications of the formula and James–Stein estimation. The formula drops the normal prior.

Proposition 7.9 (Tweedie's formula)

Let \(X \mid \theta \sim \Normal(\theta, \sigma^2)\), where \(\theta\) has any distribution \(G\), and let \(f(x) = \int \varphi_\sigma(x - \theta)\, dG(\theta)\) be the marginal density of \(X\), with \(\varphi_\sigma\) the \(\Normal(0, \sigma^2)\) density. Then \[ \E[\theta \mid X = x] = x + \sigma^2\, \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \tag{4}\] For \(G = \Normal(\mu, \tau^2)\), (4) is the posterior mean \(\mu + B(x - \mu)\) of Proposition 7.6.

Proof

The normal density satisfies \(\varphi_\sigma'(u) = -(u/\sigma^2)\,\varphi_\sigma(u)\), and \(|u\,\varphi_\sigma(u)|\) is bounded, so differentiation under the integral is justified by dominated convergence and \[ f'(x) = \int \frac{\theta - x}{\sigma^2}\, \varphi_\sigma(x - \theta)\, dG(\theta). \] Dividing by \(f(x)\), \(\sigma^2 f'(x)/f(x) = \int \theta\, \varphi_\sigma(x - \theta)\, dG(\theta)/f(x) - x = \E[\theta \mid X = x] - x\), since \(\varphi_\sigma(x - \theta)\, dG(\theta)/f(x)\) is the posterior of \(\theta\). For \(G = \Normal(\mu, \tau^2)\), \(f\) is the \(\Normal(\mu, \tau^2 + \sigma^2)\) density, \(f'(x)/f(x) = -(x - \mu)/(\tau^2 + \sigma^2)\), and (4) becomes \(x - (1 - B)(x - \mu) = \mu + B(x - \mu)\).

Formula (4) needs only the marginal density, which the observed Sharpe ratios estimate directly, the same \(\hat f\) that the local false discovery rate uses. The lab compares the two corrections on one design where the normal prior is right and one where it is wrong.

Lab 4 · Local false discovery rates and shrunk Sharpe ratios for 500 strategies

Each SIMULATED universe holds 500 strategies with five years of daily returns (\(T = 1260\)). In the two-group design, 450 strategies have true Sharpe ratio zero and 50 have annualized true Sharpe ratios drawn from \(\Normal(1.0, 0.25^2)\); in the normal design, all 500 are drawn from \(\Normal(0, 0.5^2)\). import math

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED universes of N strategies, each with T daily returns of unit volatility. For iid normal returns the
# sample mean and variance are independent (normal, and chi-square over T - 1), so each Sharpe ratio is drawn
# exactly from those two instead of from T returns.
rng = np.random.default_rng(306)
N, T, P, q = 500, 1260, 252, 0.10
se = 1.0 / math.sqrt(T)  # per-period standard error of a Sharpe ratio near zero
annual = math.sqrt(P)
Vec = NDArray[np.float64]


def draw_truth(design: str) -> Vec:
    """True per-period Sharpe ratios: annualized N(0, 0.5^2), or 450 zeros and 50 skilled at N(1.0, 0.25^2)."""
    if design == "normal":
        return rng.normal(0.0, 0.5, N) / annual
    theta = np.zeros(N)
    theta[:50] = rng.normal(1.0, 0.25, 50) / annual
    return theta


def sample_sharpe(theta: Vec) -> Vec:
    mean = theta + rng.standard_normal(N) * se
    var = rng.chisquare(T - 1, N) / (T - 1)
    return np.asarray(mean / np.sqrt(var), dtype=np.float64)


def normal_pdf(x: Vec) -> Vec:
    return np.exp(-0.5 * x * x) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)


def upper_tail(x: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 * math.erfc(x / math.sqrt(2.0))


def analyse(x: Vec) -> dict[str, Vec]:
    """Empirical Bayes in one universe: estimated lfdr, normal-prior (linear) shrinkage and Tweedie's formula."""
    z = x / se
    pi0_hat = min(1.0, float(np.mean(z < 0.0)) / 0.5)  # Storey's estimator at lambda = 0.5: p > 0.5 iff z < 0
    h = 1.06 * float(z.std(ddof=1)) * N**-0.2  # Gaussian kernel density estimate of f, Silverman's bandwidth
    u = (z[:, None] - z[None, :]) / h
    f = normal_pdf(u).mean(axis=1) / h
    f_prime = (-u * normal_pdf(u)).mean(axis=1) / h**2
    mu, tau2 = float(x.mean()), max(0.0, float(x.var(ddof=1)) - se**2)
    b = tau2 / (tau2 + se**2)
    return {
        "lfdr": np.minimum(1.0, pi0_hat * normal_pdf(z) / f),
        "linear": mu + b * (x - mu),
        "tweedie": (z + f_prime / f) * se,
        "pi0, B": np.array([pi0_hat, b]),
    }


# The two-group design's true z-densities: nulls N(0, 1), skilled N(m1, 1 + v1), with z = SR_hat / se.
m1, v1 = math.sqrt(T / P) * 1.0, (T / P) * 0.25**2


def true_lfdr(z: Vec) -> Vec:
    f1 = normal_pdf((z - m1) / math.sqrt(1.0 + v1)) / math.sqrt(1.0 + v1)
    return 0.9 * normal_pdf(z) / (0.9 * normal_pdf(z) + 0.1 * f1)

est = analyse(x)

print(f"BH at q = {q:.2f}: {k_bh} rejections, {int(np.sum(theta[order[:k_bh]] == 0.0))} of them null")

print("rank  raw_SR  linear_SR  tweedie_SR  true_SR  lfdr_hat  lfdr_true  (annualized Sharpe ratios)")
for rank, n in enumerate(order[:3], start=1):
    sharpes = " ".join(f"{v * annual:.3f}" for v in (x[n], est["linear"][n], est["tweedie"][n], theta[n]))
    print(rank, sharpes, f"{est['lfdr'][n]:.3f} {lfdr_true[n]:.3f}")

# 200 universes per design: the top-ranked strategy's bias, and (two-group) the pooled tails behind Proposition 7.3.
n_reps = 200
bias: dict[str, list[float]] = {}
pooled_z, pooled_null = [], []
for design in ("normal", "two-group"):
    for _ in range(n_reps):
        theta = draw_truth(design)
        x = sample_sharpe(theta)
        est = analyse(x)
        top = int(np.argmax(x))
        for name, value in (("raw", x[top]), ("linear", est["linear"][top]), ("tweedie", est["tweedie"][top])):
            bias.setdefault(f"{design} {name}", []).append(float(value - theta[top]) * annual)
        if design == "two-group":
            pooled_z.append(x / se)
            pooled_null.append(theta == 0.0)

8False discoveries in finance

The FDR is the natural error rate wherever many candidate signals or managers are screened at once. Chordia, Goyal and Saretto generate over 2 million trading strategies from real data, infer the properties of the strategies researchers could have studied, compute multiple-testing t-statistic thresholds of 3.8 for time-series and 3.4 for cross-sectional regressions, and estimate that researchers who ignored multiple testing would produce about 45% false rejections. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen reach different conclusions with a Bayesian model of factor replication: the majority of asset pricing factors can be replicated, work out of sample in a large data set covering 93 countries, and have evidence that is strengthened, not weakened, by the large number of factors observed. The two estimates answer different questions: the first concerns the space of possible strategies, the second the factors that were published. The full debate belongs to F5. Here the point is narrower: an FDR estimate is only as good as its model of the null population, and here that model is contested.

Mutual fund evaluation uses the same machinery. separate funds into unskilled, zero-alpha and skilled groups while allowing for dependence in estimated alphas across funds. Andrikogiannopoulou and Papakonstantinou challenge the method's use in this setting. In simulations with parameters informed by the data, they find it conservative: it underestimates the proportion of funds with nonzero alpha and classifies many funds with economically large alphas as zero-alpha, because fund returns have a low signal-to-noise ratio and the tests have low power. Barras, Scaillet and Wermers reply that reasonable adjustments to the parameter choices sizeably reduce the bias over the original 1975–2006 sample, and that over an updated 1975–2018 sample the probability of misclassifying a fund with a true alpha of 2% per year is 32% with the procedure they propose and 37% with the procedure of Barras, Scaillet and Wermers and of Andrikogiannopoulou and Papakonstantinou, against 65% in Andrikogiannopoulou and Papakonstantinou, who study 1975–2006. They conclude, from their results together with the critics', that using the FDR in finance should be accompanied by a careful evaluation of the underlying data-generating process, especially when the sample is small. FDR control bounds the share of false discoveries among the rejections. It says nothing about the true effects a low-power test fails to reject.

9Where the canon breaks

  • An expectation is not a guarantee about your study. FDR control bounds the average FDP over hypothetical repetitions. Lab 3 shows that under common-factor dependence the FDP of one study can exceed the target several times over, while the average stays below it. Controlling the probability that the FDP exceeds a bound (false discovery exceedance) is a different and stronger target, and none of this lesson's procedures provides it.
  • The family is a choice. FDR procedures depend on which hypotheses are put together. Adding hypotheses that are certain to be rejected raises the lines for everything else (Problem M3), so a family padded with sure discoveries makes marginal ones pass. Define the family before seeing the data, and report it.
  • The dependence conditions are rarely checkable. PRDS covers one-sided tests on positively correlated Gaussian statistics. Two-sided tests, long-short pairs and hedged strategies with opposing exposures can violate it. The only general fallback in this lesson, Benjamini–Yekutieli, has only 44–61% of BH's power in Lab 2. Lessons 4–5 build resampling methods that estimate the dependence instead of assuming it.
  • The null distribution is an assumption too. Every lfdr and every shrinkage factor in Section 7 takes the theoretical null \(\Normal(0, 1)\) for the z-statistics, and Remark 7.5 records Efron's argument that an empirical null may be considerably more dispersed, and when an estimated null can stand in for the true one. In a backtest universe, positively autocorrelated or overlapping returns (Lesson 4) make the true sampling variance of a Sharpe ratio exceed \(1/T\), which widens the null. A theoretical null then makes local false discovery rates too small and shrinkage too weak. Compare the spread of the central z-values with 1 before trusting either.
  • The unobserved search still matters. Every count \(m\) in this lesson is the number of hypotheses tested. As in Lesson 1, the hypotheses tried and not reported are invisible, and BH run on the reported family alone understates the problem.

10Misconceptions

  1. “With BH at q = 5%, five percent of my discoveries are false.”

    The FDR is an expectation over repeated studies, and Theorem 3.2 bounds it by \(\pi_0 q\), not \(q\). In a single study the realized proportion can be zero or far above \(q\). Lab 3 finds it above twice the target in one study in ten under moderate correlation.

  2. “BH requires independent test statistics, so it cannot be used for correlated strategies.”

    Independence is sufficient, not necessary. Under positive regression dependence, which one-sided tests on positively correlated Gaussian statistics satisfy (Proposition 5.3 and Benjamini–Yekutieli), BH controls the FDR unchanged (Theorem 5.2). Outside PRDS (two-sided tests, even on positively correlated statistics, or dependence of mixed sign) Benjamini–Yekutieli is the only correction of BH in this lesson with a proven FDR guarantee (Theorem 5.5); FWER procedures such as Holm also bound the FDR there (Proposition 1.2). Neither dominates on power: in Lab 2, Holm is less powerful than BY when many effects are real (0.2406 against 0.4679 at \(m_1 = 150\)) and more powerful when few are (0.2356 against 0.1653 at \(m_1 = 10\)).

  3. “Benjamini–Yekutieli is always valid, so it is the safe default.”

    Valid, but expensive: it divides the level by \(c(m) \approx \ln m + 0.577\), which is 7.485 at \(m = 1000\). In Lab 2 its power is only 44–61% of BH's. When the statistics are one-sided and positively correlated, the extra protection is bought with no matching reduction in error.

  4. “FDR control is a weaker version of FWER control, so it is just a more lenient p-value threshold.”

    BH has no fixed threshold: its cutoff \(Rq/m\) depends on how many hypotheses look significant (Lemma 2.3). The same p-value can be rejected in a family rich in real effects and kept in a family of nulls. That data-dependence is the whole mechanism, and it is why padding a family changes the answer (Problem M3).

  5. “Every discovery on a BH list at q = 10% has at most a 10% chance of being a null.”

    The level bounds the expected share of nulls on the list, an average over its members (Proposition 7.4). When the likelihood ratio is monotone, the members near the cutoff are more likely null than that average (Proposition 7.3). In Lab 4 the last of 26 BH rejections at \(q = 0.10\) has a true local false discovery rate of 0.287. Size discoveries by their local rate, not by their membership of the list.

M1 · Four procedures on eight signals

Eight signals have sorted p-values 0.0004, 0.0021, 0.0064, 0.0102, 0.0181, 0.0279, 0.0412, 0.3310. At level 0.05, how many hypotheses do Bonferroni, Holm, Benjamini–Hochberg and Benjamini–Yekutieli reject?

Solution

Bonferroni: the threshold is \(0.05/8 = 0.00625\). Two p-values pass (0.0004 and 0.0021); 0.0064 does not.

Holm: the first four lines \(0.05/(9-k)\) are 0.00625, 0.00714, 0.00833 and 0.0100. The first three p-values pass (0.0004, 0.0021, 0.0064), and the fourth, 0.0102, exceeds 0.0100, so Holm stops at three.

BH: the lines \(0.05k/8\) are 0.00625, 0.0125, 0.01875, 0.025, 0.03125, 0.0375, 0.04375, 0.05. The seventh p-value, 0.0412, lies under 0.04375, and the eighth, 0.3310, exceeds 0.05, so \(k^\ast = 7\).

BY: \(c(8) = 1 + 1/2 + \dots + 1/8 = 2.7179\), so the lines are \(0.05k/(8 \times 2.7179) = 0.0023k\): 0.0023, 0.0046, 0.0069, 0.0092, 0.0115, 0.0138, 0.0161, 0.0184. The third p-value, 0.0064, passes 0.0069, and none of the later ones passes its line (0.0102 > 0.0092, 0.0181 > 0.0115, 0.0279 > 0.0138, 0.0412 > 0.0161, 0.3310 > 0.0184), so BY rejects three.

The counts are 2, 3, 7 and 3. BH finds more than twice as many discoveries as Holm, and BY matches Holm here.

M2 · BH-adjusted p-values

The BH-adjusted p-value of the hypothesis ranked \(i\) is \(\tilde p_{(i)} = \min_{j \ge i} \min(1,\, m\, p_{(j)}/j)\). Compute it for the eight p-values of M1, and check that rejecting every hypothesis with \(\tilde p \le 0.05\) reproduces BH's answer.

Solution

The ratios \(8 p_{(j)}/j\) are 0.0032, 0.0084, 0.01707, 0.0204, 0.02896, 0.0372, 0.04709 and 0.3310. They already increase with \(j\), so taking the minimum over \(j \ge i\) changes nothing, and the adjusted values equal the ratios. Seven of them are at most 0.05, the same seven that BH rejects in M1. The adjusted p-value is the smallest level \(q\) at which BH would reject the hypothesis, which is how it is usually reported (P2 proves the equivalence).

M3 · Padding the family

Two signals have p-values 0.026 and 0.051. (a) What does BH reject at \(q = 0.05\)? (b) A third hypothesis with p-value 0.001 is added to the family. What does BH reject now? (c) What does this imply for how families are chosen?

Solution

(a) With \(m = 2\) the lines are 0.025 and 0.05. Since 0.026 > 0.025 and 0.051 > 0.05, BH rejects nothing.

(b) With \(m = 3\) the lines are 0.0167, 0.0333 and 0.05. The sorted p-values are 0.001, 0.026 and 0.051. Then 0.026 ≤ 0.0333, while 0.051 > 0.05, so \(k^\ast = 2\): BH now rejects the hypothesis with p-value 0.026, which it kept in (a).

(c) Adding a sure discovery made another hypothesis significant without any new evidence about it. The FDR guarantee still holds for the new family, because one more true discovery dilutes the expected share of false ones. But a researcher who chooses the family after seeing the data can manufacture discoveries this way. The family must be fixed in advance and reported.

M4 · Local and tail-area rates by hand

In a two-group model, \(\pi_0 = 0.9\), \(f_0 = \Normal(0,1)\) and \(f_1 = \Normal(3,1)\). For \(z = 2.5\) compute \(\mathrm{lfdr}(z)\), \(\mathrm{Fdr}(z)\), and the large-sample limit of BH's estimate \(N S_0(z)/R(z)\) from Proposition 7.4(b). P1 · The complete null and Simes' equality

Let all \(m\) hypotheses be true, with independent uniform p-values. Prove that \(\Prob\big(\exists\, k : p_{(k)} \le k\alpha/m\big) = \alpha\).

Solution

The event \(\{\exists\, k : p_{(k)} \le k\alpha/m\}\) is exactly \(\{R \ge 1\}\) for BH at level \(q = \alpha\), by Lemma 2.3(a). Under the complete null, Proposition 1.2 gives \(\Prob(R \ge 1) = \FWER = \FDR\). Theorem 3.2 holds with equality for independent uniform null p-values, so \(\FDR = m_0\alpha/m = \alpha\). Hence the probability is exactly \(\alpha\). This is the equality behind Simes' global test, which rejects the complete null when some \(p_{(k)} \le k\alpha/m\). Lab 1's complete-null row checks it numerically: the share of runs with a rejection is \(1 - 0.9016 = 0.0984\) against \(\alpha = 0.10\).

P2 · Adjusted p-values

With \(\tilde p_{(i)}\) defined as in M2, prove that for every \(q \in (0,1)\), BH at level \(q\) rejects \(H_{(i)}\) if and only if \(\tilde p_{(i)} \le q\).

Solution

Since \(q \lt 1\), the cap at 1 never binds when comparing with \(q\), so \(\tilde p_{(i)} \le q\) if and only if \(m p_{(j)}/j \le q\) for some \(j \ge i\), that is, \(p_{(j)} \le jq/m\) for some \(j \ge i\).

If BH rejects \(H_{(i)}\), then \(i \le k^\ast\) and \(p_{(k^\ast)} \le k^\ast q/m\), so \(j = k^\ast\) works. Conversely, if \(p_{(j)} \le jq/m\) for some \(j \ge i\), then \(k^\ast \ge j \ge i\) by (1), so \(H_{(i)}\) is among the \(k^\ast\) rejected.

P3 · Benjamini–Yekutieli with super-uniform nulls

In the proof of Theorem 5.5, replace "uniform" by super-uniform: \(F(u) = \Prob(p_i \le u) \le u\). Prove that \(\sum_{j=1}^m \frac1j \big(F(u_j) - F(u_{j-1})\big) \le q/m\), so the theorem still holds.

Solution

Write \(a_j = 1/j\) and \(F_j = F(u_j)\). Super-uniformity gives \(F_0 = F(0) \le 0\), so \(F_0 = 0\), and \(\Prob(p_i \in I_j) = F_j - F_{j-1}\) covers every value of \(p_i\) up to \(u_m\). Summation by parts gives \[ \sum_{j=1}^m a_j (F_j - F_{j-1}) = a_m F_m + \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} (a_j - a_{j+1}) F_j - a_1 F_0 \le \frac{F_m}{m} + \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{F_j}{j(j+1)}, \] using \(F_0 = 0\). The weights \(1/m\) and \(1/(j(j+1))\) are positive, so super-uniformity, \(F_j \le u_j = jq/(m\,c(m))\), bounds the right side by \[ \frac{q}{m\,c(m)}\Big(1 + \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} \frac{1}{j+1}\Big) = \frac{q}{m\,c(m)}\Big(1 + c(m) - 1\Big) = \frac{q}{m}. \] The rest of the proof of Theorem 5.5 is unchanged.

P4 · Shrinkage and the deflated Sharpe ratio

Lesson 7 deflates a selected Sharpe ratio. It tests the winner against the benchmark \(\SR_0 = s_X\, c_N\), where \(s_X\) is the cross-sectional standard deviation of the \(N\) estimated Sharpe ratios and \(c_N = (1 - \emgamma)\,\Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/N) + \emgamma\,\Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/(Ne))\) is the bracket of approximation (3) of Lesson 1. For normal returns its statistic is \(\DSR = \Phi\big((X_{n^\ast} - \SR_0)\sqrt{T - 1}/\sqrt{1 + X_{n^\ast}^2/2}\big)\). (a) In the normal-means model of Proposition 7.6 with independent trials, show that \(\E[s_X^2] = \tau^2 + \sigma^2\), so that \(\SR_0 \approx \sigma c_N/\sqrt{1 - B}\). (b) How do shrinkage and the deflation benchmark each respond as the true dispersion \(\tau\) grows? (c) Evaluate both for Lab 4's single universe: \(N = 500\), \(T = 1260\), \(\hat B = 0.337\) and a winner at 2.261 annualized. (d) Which question does each answer?

(a) By Proposition 7.6 the \(X_n\) are independent \(\Normal(\mu, \tau^2 + \sigma^2)\) draws, and the sample variance with divisor \(N - 1\) is unbiased for their variance, so \(\E[s_X^2] = \tau^2 + \sigma^2\). Since \(1 - B = \sigma^2/(\tau^2 + \sigma^2)\), \(\sqrt{\tau^2 + \sigma^2} = \sigma/\sqrt{1 - B}\), and replacing \(s_X\) by its target gives \(\SR_0 \approx \sigma c_N/\sqrt{1 - B}\).

(b) As \(\tau\) grows, \(B \to 1\): shrinkage moves the winner less, because more of the observed dispersion is real. For variants of one idea that is the intended behaviour: under equicorrelation, Lesson 7 (Proposition 4.2) shows that the cross-sectional variance correctly prices the correlation among null trials; with clustered trials it can miss in either direction. Across genuinely different strategies it raises the bar exactly when skill is present.

(c) Per day, \(\sigma = 1/\sqrt{1260} = 0.028172\) and \(s_X = \sigma/\sqrt{1 - 0.337} = 0.028172/0.81425 = 0.034599\), or 0.549 annualized. (d) Deflation answers a testing question: could the best of \(N\) skill-free trials, dispersed as these are, look this good? It is calibrated for the null that nothing works and is conservative when real skill spreads the trials. Shrinkage answers an estimation question: given the population the trials come from, what is the winner's true Sharpe ratio? It is unbiased after selection when the population model is right (Proposition 7.8) and misleading when it is wrong, as the two-group design shows. Research

R1 · FWER or FDR for a signal desk?

A systematic desk screens 2,000 candidate signals each quarter and allocates risk to those that survive. Argue whether it should control the FWER or the FDR, and at what level. Specify what a false discovery costs and how the portfolio construction changes the answer.

Solution

The answer turns on the loss function.

  1. What a false discovery costs. A false signal in a diversified book earns zero expected return, draws its share of risk budget and transaction costs, and dilutes the book's Sharpe ratio. A book of \(R\) signals in which a share \(\FDP\) are false loses roughly that share of its expected alpha per unit of risk. This loss is proportional, which is exactly what the FDR measures. FWER control would protect against any single false signal, which matters only if one bad signal is catastrophic, for example a large, levered, concentrated position.
  2. Power. With 2,000 tests, Bonferroni-type lines sit at \(0.05/2000 = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}\), so most real but modest signals are lost (Section 1). FDR control keeps power as the number of real signals grows (Lab 2).
  3. Level. Choose \(q\) from the economics. If a false signal costs its risk allocation and earns nothing, the book's expected Sharpe ratio scales roughly with \((1 - \FDP)\), so the tolerable FDR is a policy choice that trades the cost of false signals against the cost of missed true ones.
  4. Dependence and variability. Signals built from the same data share factor exposure. Use BH when the tests are one-sided on positively correlated statistics (Theorem 5.2), and recognise that the realized FDP of any one quarter can far exceed \(q\) (Lab 3). Diversifying across uncorrelated signal families reduces that variance.
  5. Process. Fix the family before screening (Problem M3), and count every signal tested, including those abandoned (Lesson 1).
R2 · Reconciling 45% with "most factors replicate"

Chordia, Goyal and Saretto estimate about 45% false rejections among strategies that would pass unadjusted tests. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen find that most published factors replicate. Design a study that would tell you whether these findings conflict.

Solution

First establish whether they measure the same thing.

  • The first estimate is an FDP for the population of all strategies a researcher could have formed from the data: millions of mostly arbitrary combinations, among which real effects are rare (\(\pi_0\) close to 1).
  • The second concerns the population of published factors, selected by economic reasoning, peer review and prior evidence, where \(\pi_0\) may be much lower.

By Theorem 3.2's logic, the false discovery proportion of an unadjusted 5% test is about \(\pi_0 \alpha / (\pi_0 \alpha + (1-\pi_0)\,\text{power})\). It can be large in the first population and small in the second without contradiction.

A study that tests for conflict would:

  1. Estimate \(\pi_0\) and the effect-size distribution separately in each population, with a method whose assumptions are stated (Remark 6.1, or the two-group model of Section 7 with an estimated density).
  2. Apply one common procedure, such as BH at a fixed \(q\) with the same dependence treatment, to both.
  3. Check whether published factors look like draws from the high-signal tail of the generated population.

Conflict exists only if the populations are comparable and the estimates still disagree. The full adjudication is F5's project.

R3 · Choosing a procedure under unknown dependence

Factor returns are strongly cross-correlated and their t-statistics have both signs. You must choose among BH, BY and a resampling approach. State the criteria and a decision rule, and say what each choice risks.

Solution

Use three criteria:

  • whether the guarantee holds under the dependence actually present
  • power
  • how variable the realized FDP is

Each choice has a different profile.

  • BH is guaranteed under independence or PRDS. With two-sided statistics and mixed signs, PRDS is not assured, so BH's guarantee is not established, although simulations of specific designs can show its behaviour (E2).
  • BY is guaranteed under any dependence, but in Lab 2, with \(m = 500\), it has only 44–61% of BH's power.
  • Resampling methods estimate the joint distribution of the test statistics from the data: bootstrap the time series of factor returns jointly, as Lessons 4–5 do for FWER. Their guarantees are asymptotic and depend on the bootstrap capturing the dependence.

A defensible rule is:

  1. Use one-sided hypotheses wherever economics fixes the sign (a value premium is predicted to be positive). When the statistics are also positively correlated and approximately Gaussian, that restores the PRDS structure, and with it BH.
  2. For the remaining two-sided tests, report BY as the guaranteed baseline, and a resampling-based estimate as the efficient alternative.
  3. In every case, report the dispersion of the FDP from a simulation calibrated to the estimated correlation matrix, because the average alone hides the risk Lab 3 exposes.
