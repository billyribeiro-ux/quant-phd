Every strategy you will ever be shown is the survivor of a search. If a hundred variants were tried and the best one is reported, its Sharpe ratio is the maximum of a hundred noisy estimates, and a maximum of noise is not zero. This lesson measures how far that maximum drifts above the truth, proves a bound that holds whatever the dependence between variants, and shows why the usual defence, a holdout sample, fails as soon as it is consulted more than once. A live track record checked daily is a search over looks too, and Section 6 builds a monitor that stays valid however often it is checked. Lessons 2–8 build the corrections on this foundation.

1Many strategies, one history

A researcher studies one idea in \(N\) variants, called trials: lookback windows, holding periods, universes, stop rules. Trial \(n\) produces \(T\) per-period excess returns \(r_{n,1}, \dots, r_{n,T}\) and a sample Sharpe ratio \[ \hSR_n = \frac{\bar r_n}{s_n}, \qquad \bar r_n = \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T r_{n,t}, \qquad s_n^2 = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^T (r_{n,t} - \bar r_n)^2 . \] All of them are computed on the same history. White's paper on the problem opens with a precise definition:

“Data snooping occurs when a given set of data is used more than once for purposes of inference or model selection.”

—

From S1 you know the sampling distribution of one Sharpe ratio. For iid normal returns with true Sharpe ratio \(\SR\), \(\sqrt{T}\,(\hSR - \SR) \dto \Normal(0,\, 1 + \SR^2/2)\), so under the null \(\SR = 0\) the estimate is approximately \(\Normal(0, 1/T)\) (S1). What S1 did not ask is what happens when the researcher reports the largest of the \(N\) estimates.

Definition 1.1 (data-snooping bias)

Let \(n^\ast = \argmax_n \hSR_n\) be the trial a researcher selects. The data-snooping bias (or selection bias) of the reported statistic is \[ \E\big[\hSR_{n^\ast}\big] - \E\big[\SR_{n^\ast}\big], \] the amount by which the selected estimate exceeds, on average, the true Sharpe ratio of the trial it selects.

When every trial is null (\(\SR_n = 0\) for all \(n\)), the bias is simply \(\E[\max_n \hSR_n]\), which is strictly positive for \(N \ge 2\) whenever the estimates are not perfectly correlated (Problem P2 gives the two-trial value exactly). The bias is not confined to strategy backtests. Lo and MacKinlay show, analytically and by simulation, that tests of asset pricing models built on portfolios sorted by a characteristic chosen because it is correlated with returns in the same data can reject far too often, and conclude that the effects of this kind of data snooping can be substantial. The mechanism is identical: a choice informed by the data, followed by inference that ignores the choice.

2A bound on the maximum under any dependence

Standardize each null estimate, \(Z_n = \sqrt{T}\,\hSR_n\), so that each \(Z_n\) is approximately standard normal. The trials share one history, so the \(Z_n\) may be correlated, in a pattern the researcher does not observe. The following bound needs no assumption about that pattern.

Theorem 2.1 (maximum of normals, any dependence)

Let \(Z_1, \dots, Z_N\) each have the standard normal distribution, with any joint distribution, and \(N \ge 2\). Then \[ \E\Big[\max_{1 \le n \le N} Z_n\Big] \le \sqrt{2 \ln N}. \tag{1}\]

Proof

Let \(M = \max_n Z_n\) and fix \(\lambda > 0\). The function \(x \mapsto e^{\lambda x}\) is convex, so Jensen's inequality gives \(e^{\lambda \E[M]} \le \E[e^{\lambda M}]\). Since \(e^{\lambda M} = \max_n e^{\lambda Z_n} \le \sum_n e^{\lambda Z_n}\) pointwise, and the moment generating function of a standard normal is \(\E[e^{\lambda Z_n}] = e^{\lambda^2/2}\), \[ e^{\lambda \E[M]} \le \sum_{n=1}^N \E\big[e^{\lambda Z_n}\big] = N e^{\lambda^2/2}. \] Taking logarithms, \(\E[M] \le \frac{\ln N}{\lambda} + \frac{\lambda}{2}\) for every \(\lambda > 0\). The right-hand side is minimized at \(\lambda = \sqrt{2 \ln N}\), where it equals \(\sqrt{2 \ln N}\). No step used independence: the sum bound holds for any joint distribution, and linearity of expectation needs none.

Corollary 2.2 (the luck hurdle)

If \(N\) trials all have true Sharpe ratio zero and \(\hSR_n \approx \Normal(0, 1/T)\), then \[ \E\Big[\max_n \hSR_n\Big] \lesssim \sqrt{\frac{2 \ln N}{T}} \quad \text{per period}, \qquad \sqrt{\frac{2 P \ln N}{T}} \quad \text{annualized with } P \text{ periods per year}. \tag{2}\]

Example 2.3 (five years of daily data, one hundred variants)

Take \(N = 100\), \(T = 1260\) daily observations and \(P = 252\). Then \(2 \ln 100 = 9.2103\), and \[ \sqrt{\frac{2 P \ln N}{T}} = \sqrt{\frac{252 \times 9.2103}{1260}} = \sqrt{1.8421} = 1.357. \] On average, the best of one hundred skill-free variants can show an annualized Sharpe ratio of up to 1.36 over five years. Bound (2) is an upper bound; Section 3 gives an estimate of where the expectation actually sits.

Remark 2.4 (slow growth is not small growth)

The hurdle grows like \(\sqrt{\ln N}\) and falls like \(1/\sqrt{T}\). Growth in \(N\) is slow: a hundredfold larger search, from \(N = 10\) to \(N = 1000\), raises the bound only from 2.15 to 3.72 standard errors. Slow is not small, though. At \(T = 1260\) and \(P = 252\) the difference of 1.57 standard errors is \(1.57 \times \sqrt{252/1260} = 0.70\) in annualized Sharpe ratio (Example 2.3, Problem M1).

3Where the expectation sits: the extreme-value approximation

Bound (1) holds for any dependence, which is also why it is loose. For independent trials the exact expectation can be computed, and a closed-form approximation tracks it closely.

Definition 3.1 (expected maximum Sharpe ratio)

For \(N\) independent trials whose estimated Sharpe ratios are normal with mean \(\E[\hSR]\) and variance \(\Var[\hSR]\), the expected maximum Sharpe ratio is \(\E[\max_n \hSR_n]\). Bailey and López de Prado use the extreme-value approximation \[ \E\Big[\max_n \hSR_n\Big] \approx \E[\hSR] + \sqrt{\Var[\hSR]}\,\Big( (1 - \emgamma)\, \Phi^{-1}\!\big(1 - \tfrac1N\big) + \emgamma\, \Phi^{-1}\!\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{N e}\big) \Big), \tag{3}\] with \(\emgamma \approx 0.5772\) the Euler–Mascheroni constant and \(e\) Euler's number, a result they attribute to Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu.

Approximation (3) is a large-\(N\) result. Whether it is accurate at the values of \(N\) a researcher actually uses is an empirical question about a formula, so we test it directly rather than trust it.

Lab 1 · Testing the approximation against simulation

For independent standard normals, the simulated mean maximum should agree with the exact expectation, computed by numerical integration, up to Monte Carlo error. Both should sit below the bound \(\sqrt{2\ln N}\) for every \(N\), and approximation (3) should overstate the exact value by a few percent at \(N = 10\), with the error shrinking as \(N\) grows.

import math
from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np

phi = NormalDist()
gamma_e = 0.5772156649015329

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

grid = np.linspace(-9.0, 10.0, 190001)
pdf = np.exp(-0.5 * grid**2) / math.sqrt(2 * math.pi)

4The bias in a full research loop

Sections 2 and 3 concern the reported in-sample number. The next lab follows the selected strategy forward. It splits five years (1,260 days) of simulated daily returns into an in-sample half used for selection and an out-of-sample half that is looked at once.

Lab 2 · The random-strategy overfitting engine

With 100 skill-free strategies and 630 in-sample days, the selected strategy's in-sample annualized Sharpe ratio should average close to the prediction of (3), \(2.531 \times \sqrt{252/630} = 1.60\). Its out-of-sample Sharpe ratio should average close to zero, and it should lose money out of sample about half the time.

import numpy as np

# SIMULATED pure-noise strategies: no strategy has any skill.
rng = np.random.default_rng(20261017)
n_strategies, n_is, n_oos, n_reps = 100, 630, 630, 100
annualize = np.sqrt(252)

best_is = np.empty(n_reps)
best_oos = np.empty(n_reps)
for rep in range(n_reps):
    r = rng.standard_normal((n_is + n_oos, n_strategies)) * 0.01  # daily excess returns
    is_part, oos_part = r[:n_is], r[n_is:]
    sr_is = is_part.mean(axis=0) / is_part.std(axis=0, ddof=1)
    sr_oos = oos_part.mean(axis=0) / oos_part.std(axis=0, ddof=1)
    k = int(np.argmax(sr_is))  # the researcher keeps the in-sample winner
    best_is[rep] = sr_is[k] * annualize
    best_oos[rep] = sr_oos[k] * annualize

print(f"mean in-sample Sharpe of the selected strategy: {best_is.mean():.3f}")
print(f"mean out-of-sample Sharpe of the same strategy: {best_oos.mean():.3f}")
print(f"share of selections that lose money out of sample: {np.mean(best_oos < 0):.2f}")
mean in-sample Sharpe of the selected strategy: 1.612
mean out-of-sample Sharpe of the same strategy: 0.034
share of selections that lose money out of sample: 0.52

Selection alone manufactured an in-sample Sharpe ratio of about 1.6. All of it vanished out of sample, because none of it was skill. A single honest holdout told the truth here. This does not show that holdouts protect a research process in general; Section 5 shows how they stop doing so.

5Why a reused holdout stops protecting you

A holdout protects only the first time it is used. In practice a researcher selects in-sample, checks the holdout, is disappointed, revises the idea, and checks again. Each round is another trial evaluated on the same holdout, and the number finally reported is the best holdout result across rounds: a maximum again. White argues that the problem is practically unavoidable with time-series data, because typically only a single history of the phenomenon is available.

Lab 3 · Holdout reuse

Under the null, and assuming each round's configuration is unrelated to the earlier ones so that its holdout Sharpe ratio is an independent draw, the best of \(K\) rounds should behave like the expected maximum of \(K\) independent null statistics: near zero for \(K = 1\), and close to approximation (3) for \(K = 5\) and \(K = 20\).

import math
from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np

# SIMULATED null research loop. Each round tests a new configuration on the same one-year
# holdout, and that configuration's holdout returns are independent of every earlier
# round's (an assumption: unrelated revisions); keeping the best round reports a maximum.
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n_oos, n_reps = 252, 2000
phi, gamma_e = NormalDist(), 0.5772156649015329

print("rounds  mean_reported_holdout_sharpe  expected_max_approximation")
for rounds in (1, 5, 20):
    holdout = rng.standard_normal((n_reps, rounds, n_oos))
    sharpe = holdout.mean(axis=2) / holdout.std(axis=2, ddof=1) * math.sqrt(252)
    reported = sharpe.max(axis=1).mean()
    if rounds == 1:
        approx = 0.0
    else:
        z = (1 - gamma_e) * phi.inv_cdf(1 - 1 / rounds) + gamma_e * phi.inv_cdf(1 - 1 / (rounds * math.e))
        approx = z * math.sqrt(252 / n_oos)
    print(f"{rounds} {reported:.3f} {approx:.3f}")

Five peeks at a one-year holdout produce a reported out-of-sample Sharpe ratio above 1 from pure noise, and twenty peeks produce almost 2. The holdout has become part of the search. Its protection is restored only by counting the rounds as trials, which is what the corrections in Lessons 2–8 do. This assumes independent rounds; if each revision is correlated with the last, the inflation shrinks (by \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\) under equicorrelation, Problem P3).

6Repeated looks: inference valid at any time

Section 5 counted looks across candidates. A live strategy adds looks across time. A paper-trading record, or a strategy newly given capital, is reviewed as its returns arrive, daily on a dashboard or monthly at a risk committee, and every review asks, on a longer sample, whether the edge is real yet. To make that question exact, let the daily excess returns be \(r_t = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon_t\) with \(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2, \dots \iid \Normal(0, 1)\) and the volatility \(\sigma\) known in advance. The standardized returns \(Y_t = r_t/\sigma\) are then iid \(\Normal(\SR, 1)\), where \(\SR = \mu/\sigma\) is the daily Sharpe ratio, and after \(t\) days the z-statistic for \(H_0\colon \SR = 0\) is \[ Z_t = \frac{S_t}{\sqrt{t}}, \qquad S_t = Y_1 + \dots + Y_t, \qquad S_0 = 0. \] A two-sided test at level \(\alpha\) rejects on day \(t\) when \(|Z_t| \ge c\), where \(c = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)\), which is 1.960 at \(\alpha = 0.05\). If \(t\) is fixed in advance, its type I error is exactly \(\alpha\). Call each review that computes \(Z_t\) and acts on it a look. Repeated significance testing applies the fixed-level test at every look and stops at the first rejection.

Proposition 6.1 (repeated looks inflate the type I error)

Under \(H_0\), let \(p_n = \Prob\big(|Z_t| \ge c \text{ for some } t \in \{1, \dots, n\}\big)\) be the probability that repeated significance testing rejects within its first \(n\) looks. Then (a) \(p_1 = \alpha\); (b) \(p_n \lt p_{n+1}\) for every \(n \ge 1\); (c) \(p_n \to 1\) as \(n \to \infty\).

Proof

(a) Under \(H_0\), \(Z_1 = Y_1 \sim \Normal(0, 1)\), so \(p_1 = 2\big(1 - \Phi(c)\big) = \alpha\).

(b) Let \(A_n\) be the event that some look among the first \(n\) rejects. Then \(A_n \subseteq A_{n+1}\), so \(p_{n+1} - p_n = \Prob(A_{n+1} \setminus A_n)\), and \(A_{n+1} \setminus A_n\) contains the event that \((Y_1, \dots, Y_{n+1})\) lies in \[ G = \Big\{ y \in \R^{n+1} : |y_1 + \dots + y_t| \lt c\sqrt{t} \text{ for } t \le n, \ \ |y_1 + \dots + y_{n+1}| \gt c\sqrt{n+1} \Big\}. \] The set \(G\) is open, as a finite intersection of sets defined by strict inequalities between continuous functions, and it is not empty: since \(c \gt 0\), the point with \(y_1 = \dots = y_n = 0\) and \(y_{n+1} = 2c\sqrt{n+1}\) belongs to it. The vector \((Y_1, \dots, Y_{n+1})\) has the density \(\prod_i \varphi(y_i)\), which is positive everywhere, and a positive continuous function has a positive integral over a nonempty open set. Hence \(p_{n+1} - p_n \ge \Prob\big((Y_1, \dots, Y_{n+1}) \in G\big) \gt 0\).

(c) The events \(A_n\) increase to the event that some look ever rejects, so \(p_n\) converges to the probability of that event. Armitage, McPherson and Rowe point out that, by the law of the iterated logarithm, a standardized cumulative sum of deviations from expectation, such as \(Z_t\), eventually reaches any preassigned value with probability one. So the event has probability 1, and \(p_n \to 1\).

Remark 6.2 (only the number of looks matters)

If the looks fall on days \(d, 2d, \dots, nd\) instead of \(1, 2, \dots, n\), nothing changes. The look statistics \((Z_d, Z_{2d}, \dots, Z_{nd})\) are linear in independent normals, hence jointly normal with mean zero and unit variances, and for \(i \le j\) \[ \Cov(Z_{id}, Z_{jd}) = \frac{\Cov(S_{id}, S_{jd})}{\sqrt{id}\,\sqrt{jd}} = \frac{id}{d\sqrt{ij}} = \sqrt{i/j}, \] which does not depend on \(d\). A jointly normal vector's law is fixed by its means and covariances, so \(p_n\) depends only on the number of looks: twelve monthly reviews of one year's returns carry the same \(p_{12}\) as twelve quarterly reviews of three years' returns. Lab 4, after Example 6.7, estimates \(p_n\) at eight values of \(n\) from 1 to 2,520.

No fixed threshold survives unlimited looks, so a valid monitor needs a threshold that rises with \(t\). The way to find one is to control the whole path of a single statistic, and the statistic that makes this possible is a gambler's wealth.

Definition 6.3 (e-value, test supermartingale)

Fix a null hypothesis \(H_0\) about the distribution of \(Y_1, Y_2, \dots\), and write \(\Prob_0\) and \(\E_0\) for probability and expectation under a distribution in \(H_0\).

  1. An e-value for \(H_0\) is a random variable \(E \ge 0\) with \(\E_0[E] \le 1\) under every distribution in \(H_0\).
  2. A test supermartingale for \(H_0\) is a sequence \(M_0, M_1, M_2, \dots\) of random variables with \(M_0 = 1\) and \(M_t \ge 0\), in which each \(M_t\) is a function of \(Y_1, \dots, Y_t\) and, under every distribution in \(H_0\), \[ \E_0[M_t \mid Y_1, \dots, Y_{t-1}] \le M_{t-1}, \qquad t \ge 1. \] It is a test martingale when equality holds.

These are the definitions of Ramdas, Grünwald, Vovk and Shafer, who read a test martingale as the wealth process of a gambler betting against the null, and who call a nonnegative process an e-process when, for every distribution in the null, it is bounded by a test martingale for that distribution. Think of \(M_t\) as the wealth after \(t\) days of a gambler who starts with one unit, bets each day at odds that are fair or worse under \(H_0\), and never borrows. Taking expectations of the defining inequality, by the tower property of conditional expectation, gives \(\E_0[M_t] \le \E_0[M_{t-1}] \le \dots \le M_0 = 1\), so each \(M_t\) is an e-value. A large e-value is evidence against \(H_0\): by Markov's inequality, \(\Prob_0(E \ge 1/\alpha) \le \alpha\,\E_0[E] \le \alpha\). That bound concerns one statistic, or one day fixed in advance. The bound that covers every day at once is Ville's inequality.

Theorem 6.4 (Ville's inequality)

If \((M_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a test supermartingale for \(H_0\) and \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\), then under every distribution in \(H_0\) \[ \Prob_0\Big(\sup_{t \ge 0} M_t \ge \frac{1}{\alpha}\Big) \le \alpha. \]

Proof

Fix a level \(h \gt 0\) and a horizon \(n\). For \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n\) let \(B_k = \{M_j \lt h \text{ for all } j \le k\}\) be the event that the wealth has not reached \(h\) by day \(k\), let \(B_{-1}\) be the whole sample space, and let \(A_k = B_{k-1} \setminus B_k\) be the event that the wealth reaches \(h\) for the first time on day \(k\). The events \(A_0, \dots, A_n\) are disjoint, their union is \(\{\max_{k \le n} M_k \ge h\}\), and \(A_k\) and \(B_k\) are determined by \(Y_1, \dots, Y_k\). Consider the wealth of a gambler who copies \(M\) but stops betting the first time it reaches \(h\): \[ W_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} M_k \indic_{A_k} + M_n \indic_{B_n}. \] Since \(A_n\) and \(B_n\) split \(B_{n-1}\) into two disjoint parts, \(W_n - W_{n-1} = (M_n - M_{n-1})\,\indic_{B_{n-1}}\). The indicator is a function of \(Y_1, \dots, Y_{n-1}\), so it can be taken out of the conditional expectation, and each \(M_t\) is integrable because \(\E_0[M_t] \le 1\): \[ \E_0[W_n - W_{n-1}] = \E_0\Big[\indic_{B_{n-1}}\big(\E_0[M_n \mid Y_1, \dots, Y_{n-1}] - M_{n-1}\big)\Big] \le 0. \] Hence \(\E_0[W_n] \le \E_0[W_0] = M_0 = 1\), because \(A_0\) and \(B_0\) split the sample space. Every term of \(W_n\) is nonnegative and \(M_k \ge h\) on \(A_k\), so \(W_n \ge h \sum_{k \le n} \indic_{A_k} = h\,\indic\{\max_{k \le n} M_k \ge h\}\). Taking expectations, \[ \Prob_0\Big(\max_{k \le n} M_k \ge h\Big) \le \frac{1}{h}. \] The events on the left increase with \(n\) to \(\{M_k \ge h \text{ for some } k\}\), so that event also has probability at most \(1/h\). Finally, \(\sup_k M_k \ge h\) implies \(M_k \ge h'\) for some \(k\), for every \(h' \lt h\), so \(\Prob_0(\sup_k M_k \ge h) \le 1/h'\) for every \(h' \lt h\), and therefore \(\Prob_0(\sup_k M_k \ge h) \le 1/h\). Setting \(h = 1/\alpha\) proves the theorem.

Ville stated the inequality for test martingales and called it the theorem of gamblers' ruin: a gambler who begins with unit capital and keeps betting until winning \(1/\alpha\) has little chance of succeeding. The proof shows why repeated looks cost the wealth nothing. Stopping at the first crossing is itself a betting strategy, and no betting strategy turns odds that are fair or worse into an expected gain.

Corollary 6.5 (anytime validity)

Let \((M_t)\) be a test supermartingale for \(H_0\) and \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\).

  1. The monitor that rejects \(H_0\) on the first day with \(M_t \ge 1/\alpha\) ever rejects with probability at most \(\alpha\) under \(H_0\), however long it runs.
  2. For every random time \(\tau\) with values in \(\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\), however it is chosen (fixed in advance, set by the data seen so far, or picked with hindsight from the whole path), \(\Prob_0(M_\tau \ge 1/\alpha) \le \alpha\).
Proof

(1) The monitor ever rejects exactly when \(M_t \ge 1/\alpha\) for some \(t\), an event contained in \(\{\sup_t M_t \ge 1/\alpha\}\). (2) Whatever \(\tau\) is, \(\{M_\tau \ge 1/\alpha\} \subseteq \{\sup_t M_t \ge 1/\alpha\}\). Both bounds follow from Theorem 6.4.

Howard, Ramdas, McAuliffe and Sekhon prove that the three guarantees are equivalent: error at most \(\alpha\) uniformly over time, at every random time, and at every time chosen by a rule that sees only the data so far. Anytime-valid inference is inference whose error guarantee holds in this sense, so that the analyst may look as often as they like and stop or continue for any reason. Ramdas et al. describe the field's measures of evidence, e-processes for testing and their counterparts for estimation, as remaining valid whenever the analysis stops, which accommodates continuous monitoring and stopping or continuing for any reason, and they state that these measures rely crucially on test martingales. Repeated significance testing fails exactly here: each event \(\{|Z_t| \ge c\}\) has probability \(\alpha\), but nothing bounds their union, which Proposition 6.1 shows has probability 1. Johari, Koomen, Pekelis and Walsh make the same point for online A/B tests: fixed-sample p-values and confidence intervals are wholly unreliable when users choose sample sizes by continuously monitoring them, and the authors define always-valid p-values and confidence intervals that remain valid whenever the user decides. They report that their methodology runs on a large commercial A/B-testing platform and has analysed hundreds of thousands of experiments.

Ville's inequality needs a test supermartingale that grows when the strategy has an edge. A likelihood ratio does, and averaging likelihood ratios over the possible edges removes the need to guess the edge in advance.

Proposition 6.6 (the normal-mixture monitor)

Let \(Y_1, Y_2, \dots \iid \Normal(\SR, 1)\) and fix \(\kappa \gt 0\). For \(t \ge 0\) define \[ M_t = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa + t}}\;\exp\!\Big(\frac{S_t^2}{2(\kappa + t)}\Big). \tag{4}\]

  1. \(M_t = \int_\R L_t(\lambda)\,\varphi_\kappa(\lambda)\,d\lambda\), where \(L_t(\lambda) = \exp(\lambda S_t - \lambda^2 t/2)\) is the likelihood ratio of \(\SR = \lambda\) against \(\SR = 0\) for \(Y_1, \dots, Y_t\), and \(\varphi_\kappa\) is the \(\Normal(0, 1/\kappa)\) density.
  2. Under \(H_0\colon \SR = 0\), \((M_t)\) is a test martingale.
  3. \(M_t \ge 1/\alpha\) if and only if \(|S_t| \ge b_t\), where \[ b_t = \sqrt{(\kappa + t)\Big(2\ln\frac{1}{\alpha} + \ln\Big(1 + \frac{t}{\kappa}\Big)\Big)}. \tag{5}\]
  4. Whatever the true \(\SR\), with probability at least \(1 - \alpha\) every one of the intervals \[ C_t = \Big[\frac{S_t - b_t}{t}, \ \frac{S_t + b_t}{t}\Big], \qquad t = 1, 2, \dots \tag{6}\] contains \(\SR\).
Proof

(1) For one day, the ratio of the \(\Normal(\lambda, 1)\) and \(\Normal(0, 1)\) densities at \(Y_i\) is \(\exp(\lambda Y_i - \lambda^2/2)\); the product over \(i \le t\) is \(L_t(\lambda)\). Completing the square in \(\lambda\) and using \(\int_\R e^{-a v^2/2}\,dv = \sqrt{2\pi/a}\) for \(a \gt 0\), \[ \int_\R e^{\lambda S_t - \lambda^2 t/2}\,\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}}\,e^{-\kappa\lambda^2/2}\,d\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}}\;e^{S_t^2/(2(\kappa + t))}\int_\R e^{-(\kappa + t)\,(\lambda - S_t/(\kappa + t))^2/2}\,d\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa + t}}\;e^{S_t^2/(2(\kappa + t))}. \]

(2) \(M_t \gt 0\), \(M_0 = 1\), and \(M_t\) is a function of \(Y_1, \dots, Y_t\). Fix \(t \ge 1\) and condition on \(Y_1, \dots, Y_{t-1}\): then \(s = S_{t-1}\) is known and \(S_t = s + Y_t\), where under \(H_0\) the return \(Y_t \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) is independent of the past. For \(a \lt 1/2\) and \(Y \sim \Normal(0, 1)\), completing the square in \(y\) gives \[ \E\big[e^{a(s + Y)^2}\big] = \int_\R e^{a(s + y)^2}\varphi(y)\,dy = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2a}}\,\exp\Big(\frac{a s^2}{1 - 2a}\Big). \] Take \(a = 1/(2(\kappa + t))\), which is below \(1/2\) because \(\kappa + t \gt 1\). Then \(1 - 2a = (\kappa + t - 1)/(\kappa + t)\) and \(a/(1 - 2a) = 1/(2(\kappa + t - 1))\), so \[ \E_0[M_t \mid Y_1, \dots, Y_{t-1}] = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa + t}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\kappa + t}{\kappa + t - 1}}\;\exp\Big(\frac{S_{t-1}^2}{2(\kappa + t - 1)}\Big) = M_{t-1}. \]

(3) Taking logarithms, \(M_t \ge 1/\alpha\) if and only if \(S_t^2/(2(\kappa + t)) \ge \ln(1/\alpha) + \tfrac12\ln\big((\kappa + t)/\kappa\big)\), that is, if and only if \(S_t^2 \ge b_t^2\).

(4) Under any \(\SR\), the centred returns \(Y_t - \SR\) are iid \(\Normal(0, 1)\), and their partial sums are \(S_t - t\,\SR\). By parts (2) and (3) applied to them, equation (4) with \(S_t\) replaced by \(S_t - t\,\SR\) is a test martingale that reaches \(1/\alpha\) exactly when \(|S_t - t\,\SR| \ge b_t\). By Theorem 6.4 this happens on some day with probability at most \(\alpha\), and \(|S_t - t\,\SR| \lt b_t\) for every \(t\) says that every \(C_t\) contains \(\SR\).

A sequence of intervals with the simultaneous coverage of part 4 is a confidence sequence. A confidence interval is required to cover the truth with probability \(1 - \alpha\) only at one sample size fixed in advance; a confidence sequence covers it at all times at once, and so remains valid under continuous monitoring. The construction is the method of mixtures: Robbins extended an inequality of Ville and Wald into a method for probability statements about the whole sequence of observations, and Ramdas et al. trace mixtures of test martingales to Ville, Wald and Robbins. Three features of the boundary (5) matter in practice.

  • It rises faster than the fixed test's \(c\sqrt{t}\), but slower than \(t\). \(b_t^2/(t\ln t) \to 1\) as \(t \to \infty\), so \(b_t/t \to 0\). Since \(S_t/t \to \SR\) almost surely by the strong law of large numbers, \(|S_t| - b_t = t\big(|S_t/t| - b_t/t\big) \to \infty\) whenever \(\SR \ne 0\): the monitor eventually rejects every real edge. Howard et al. note that a confidence sequence whose radius grows more slowly than \(t\) typically yields such a test of power one.
  • \(\kappa\) is the precision of the bet. The mixture spreads its stake over daily Sharpe ratios \(\lambda \sim \Normal(0, 1/\kappa)\), so the annualized Sharpe ratio \(\sqrt{252}\,\lambda\) it bets on has standard deviation \(\sqrt{252/\kappa}\), which is 1 at \(\kappa = 252\). Howard et al. show that the two-sided normal-mixture boundary divided by \(\sqrt{t}\) is smallest at a time proportional to the mixing parameter, and that at \(\alpha = 0.05\) the normalized boundary is about 3.0 at that time and only about 3.6 at one hundred times that time. Problem M5 derives the optimal time, \(t = 8.21\kappa\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\).
  • Validity has a price in width. The fixed test compares \(|S_t|\) with \(c\sqrt{t}\); the monitor compares it with \(b_t\), which is larger at every \(t\) (Problem M5 shows \(b_t/\sqrt{t} \ge 3.035\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\)). Example 6.7 measures the gap.
Example 6.7 (a three-year review)

Take \(\alpha = 0.05\), \(\kappa = 252\) and a review after three years, \(t = 756\). Then \(2\ln 20 = 5.9915\) and \(\ln(1 + 756/252) = \ln 4 = 1.3863\), so \[ b_{756} = \sqrt{1008 \times 7.3778} = \sqrt{7436.82} = 86.237. \] The monitor rejects only if \(|Z_{756}| = |S_{756}|/\sqrt{756} \ge 86.237/27.495 = 3.136\), against 1.960 for one test planned for day 756. With known volatility the annualized Sharpe estimate is \(\sqrt{252}\,S_t/t\), so \(Z_t\) is that estimate times \(\sqrt{t/252}\), here \(\sqrt{3}\). The monitor therefore needs an annualized Sharpe estimate of at least \(3.136/\sqrt{3} = 1.811\) after three years, where the single planned test needs \(1.960/\sqrt{3} = 1.132\). In the same units, the half-width of \(C_{756}\) is 1.811, which is \(3.136/1.960 = 1.60\) times that of the fixed-sample 95% interval.

Lab 4 · Monitoring a live strategy every day

For skill-free strategies, the share that the daily two-sided 5% z-test rejects at least once in its first \(n\) looks should start at 0.05 for \(n = 1\) and rise with every look (Proposition 6.1); by Remark 6.2 the row for \(n\) looks also covers \(n\) equally spaced reviews. The monitor (5), with \(\alpha = 0.05\) and \(\kappa = 252\), should stay at or below 0.05 at every horizon (Corollary 6.5). With 10,000 null paths each rate has a standard error of at most \(\sqrt{0.25/10000} = 0.005\). Under a real edge the monitor should eventually reject, but later than one z-test planned in advance for day \(T = (c/\SR)^2\), at which its power is \(\Phi(0) + \Phi(-2c) = 0.5000\) to four decimals: even odds.

import math

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# volatility known in advance, so the standardized return is N(SR, 1) with SR the daily Sharpe ratio.
alpha, kappa, horizon, batch = 0.05, 252.0, 2520, 500
z_crit = 1.959963984540054  # two-sided 5% critical value of the standard normal
days = np.arange(1, horizon + 1)
naive_line = z_crit * np.sqrt(days)  # the fixed-level z-test rejects on day t when |S_t| >= this line
boundary = np.sqrt((days + kappa) * (2 * math.log(1 / alpha) + np.log1p(days / kappa)))  # b_t, equation (5)


def first_rejection_days(
    rng: np.random.Generator, daily_sharpe: float, n_paths: int
) -> tuple[NDArray[np.int64], NDArray[np.int64]]:
    """Trading day of the first rejection by the naive daily z-test and by the monitor (horizon + 1: never)."""
    naive = np.empty(n_paths, dtype=np.int64)
    monitor = np.empty(n_paths, dtype=np.int64)
    for start in range(0, n_paths, batch):
        size = min(batch, n_paths - start)
        cum = np.abs(np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal((size, horizon)) + daily_sharpe, axis=1))  # |S_t|
        for out, line in ((naive, naive_line), (monitor, boundary)):
            crossed = cum >= line
            out[start : start + size] = np.where(crossed.any(axis=1), crossed.argmax(axis=1) + 1, horizon + 1)
    return naive, monitor


rng = np.random.default_rng(20261018)
naive, monitor = first_rejection_days(rng, 0.0, 10000)
print("skill-free strategies: share rejected at least once in the first n daily looks")
print("looks  naive_z_test  anytime_valid_monitor")
for looks in (1, 2, 5, 12, 36, 252, 756, 2520):
    print(f"{looks} {np.mean(naive <= looks):.3f} {np.mean(monitor <= looks):.4f}")

    print(

        f"{np.mean(detect <= horizon):.3f} {half_power_days / 252:.2f}"
    )

Daily 5% z-tests rejected 50.6% of skill-free strategies within three years and 57.8% within ten. The climb toward 1 that Proposition 6.1 guarantees is slow, but every look adds to it. The monitor rejected 0.64% within three years and 1.79% within ten, below 0.05 as Ville's inequality requires. The rest of \(\alpha\) is not wasted: the bound covers an unlimited horizon, and a ten-year window sees only part of it. In discrete time a further gap comes from overshoot: at its first crossing a test martingale is typically strictly above \(1/\alpha\). For the monitor (4) the overshoot is almost sure, because \(M_k = 1/\alpha\) exactly when \(|S_k| = b_k\), an event of probability zero. The proof of Theorem 6.4 prices it: with \(h = 1/\alpha\), the same steps give \(h\,\Prob_0(\max_{k \le n} M_k \ge h) + \E_0\big[\sum_{k \le n} (M_k - h)\,\indic_{A_k}\big] \le 1\). The expected overshoot is positive from the first day \(n_0\) on which a crossing has positive probability, and it can only grow with \(n\), so the probability of ever crossing is at most \(\alpha\) times one minus the expected overshoot up to \(n_0\): strictly below \(\alpha\). With \(\kappa = 252\) the monitor almost never fires in the first weeks, because \(b_t/\sqrt{t}\) falls until \(t = 8.21\kappa\) (Problem M5) and \(b_{36}/\sqrt{36} = 7.0\). For a monitor that must stay valid at every horizon, with no last day fixed in advance, the price is time. Koning and van Meer show that when a maximum number of observations \(N\) is fixed in advance, any valid test of a simple null hypothesis based on \(N\) observations can be turned into an anytime valid sequential test that matches it after \(N\) observations. A true annualized Sharpe ratio of 1 took the monitor a median 7.20 years to confirm, against 3.84 years for the single planned test to reach even odds; a Sharpe ratio of 2 took 2.25 years against 0.96, so the monitor needs \(7.20/3.84 = 1.9\) and \(2.25/0.96 = 2.3\) times as long. These numbers assume normal returns with known volatility (Remark 6.8), and they move with \(\kappa\) (Problem M5).

Remark 6.8 (beyond normal returns with known volatility)

Proposition 6.6 assumes iid normal returns and a volatility known in advance. Neither is safe for strategy returns, which can have fat tails and serial correlation (Section 7), so it matters which parts of the construction survive.

  • Light tails, known scale. Part (2) of the proof computed a normal integral, but part (1) shows what the construction needs: \(M_t\) averages the likelihood ratios \(L_t(\lambda)\), and each \(L_t(\lambda)\) is a product of factors \(e^{\lambda Y_t - \lambda^2/2}\), which form a test supermartingale whenever the conditional moment generating function of \(Y_t\) is at most \(e^{\lambda^2/2}\) (Problem P4(b)). If, given the past, each observation is sub-Gaussian with a known parameter, the same normal mixture is a test supermartingale and (6) remains a confidence sequence; when the conditional means vary over time, it covers their running average. In standardized units the condition reads \(\E[e^{\lambda(Y_t - \mu_t)} \mid Y_1, \dots, Y_{t-1}] \le e^{\lambda^2/2}\) for every real \(\lambda\), with \(\mu_t = \E[Y_t \mid Y_1, \dots, Y_{t-1}]\); Problem P1 uses the unconditional version.
  • Heavy tails, known variance bound. Ramdas et al. report that Wang and Ramdas prove a test supermartingale property assuming only a known upper bound on the conditional variance, and derive extensions for a finite moment of order \(p \gt 1\); their paper builds confidence sequences for the mean under the sole assumption of a known upper bound on the variance, and extends them to data with infinite variance whose central moment of some order \(p \gt 1\) is finite. The resulting confidence sequences appear, visually, almost identical to the sub-Gaussian ones; that is a comparison of plotted intervals, not a theorem.
  • The scale is not learned. In both cases the sub-Gaussian parameter or the variance bound must be supplied in advance, because it is provably impossible to learn it from the data; for observations in a bounded range, variance-adaptive confidence sequences do exist. Replacing \(\sigma\) by a running estimate in (5) is therefore not covered by Ville's inequality, and under volatility clustering the constant supplied must bound the conditional scale on every day, the most volatile included. The assumption is moved into the choice of scale, not removed.
  • Normal returns, unknown volatility. For iid normal data the standardized mean \(\mu/\sigma\), which here is the Sharpe ratio itself, can be tested with \(\sigma\) unknown: reducing the data to scale-free ratios makes the null simple and yields a test martingale, which Ramdas et al. trace to Lai's anytime-valid use and his closed-form confidence sequence for a normal mean with unknown variance. That validity rests on exact normality.
  • Asymptotic validity. Ramdas et al. report a universality result of Waudby-Smith and coauthors: when the data have more than two moments, the sub-Gaussian confidence sequence with \(\sigma\) replaced by an empirical estimate is an asymptotic confidence sequence, a time-uniform analogue of the central limit theorem. The guarantee then holds only in the limit, as for an ordinary interval built on an estimated standard error.
Remark 6.9 (group-sequential designs and alpha spending)

Group-sequential methods for clinical trials handle repeated looks through the design of the trial. Pocock divides patient entry into equal-sized groups and bases the decision to stop or continue on repeated significance tests after each group, with exact results for a normal response with known variance, and treats the size and number of groups as part of the design. For two treatments with an immediate success-or-failure response, O'Brien and Fleming fix the maximum number of tests and the number of observations between them in advance. They report that the overall size is controlled with virtually the same accuracy as by the single test on all the observations, and that the power is virtually the same. Lan and DeMets note that these methods require the total number of decision times to be specified in advance, and replace it with a function \(\alpha^\ast(t)\) giving the rate at which the error level \(\alpha\) is spent, so that the boundary at a look depends on past and current decision times but not on future ones or on their total number. Dividing a fixed total error \(\alpha\) among the looks in this way is alpha spending.

The comparison with the monitor of Proposition 6.6 is a trade of flexibility for power. Within its budget a group-sequential design with a fixed number of looks can be cheap, as O'Brien and Fleming's power finding above illustrates. A spending design's guarantee also ends with its budget, though. A look the budget does not cover can only add rejections, since the events are nested as in the proof of Proposition 6.1, and for normal data it adds them with positive probability by the argument of part (b) there; a design that has spent all of \(\alpha\) therefore exceeds \(\alpha\) once that look is added. The anytime-valid monitor needs no budget, no count of looks and no end date, and the analyst may stop or continue for any reason. Its price is the wider boundary: a z-hurdle of 3.136 instead of 1.960 at three years (Example 6.7), and 1.9 to 2.3 times as long to an even chance of detection in Lab 4. Howard et al. find the cost of their confidence sequences to be less than double the width of the fixed-sample interval, their discrete-mixture bound staying within a factor of two of it over five orders of magnitude in time. A paper-trading trial with a fixed review date suits a spending design; a live track record with no end date needs a monitor that is valid at any time.

7Where the canon breaks

The textbook significance test in S1 assumes that one hypothesis was fixed before the data were seen. Applied to a reported strategy, it answers a question nobody asked: how surprising this statistic would be had it been the only one computed. Four facts break the textbook frame in practice.

  • The count of trials is unobserved. Harvey, Liu and Zhu stress that what matters is the number of factors tested, not the number published, and that the former is unobservable because tests that fail are not published. Every correction in this module needs \(N\), so the research process must record it (Problem R1).
  • Trials are dependent. Theorem 2.1 survives any dependence, but approximation (3) assumes independence, and real variants (a 50-day versus a 55-day lookback, which share 50 of their 55 days) can be highly correlated. Problem P3 shows that equicorrelation \(\rho \ge 0\) shrinks the bias by the factor \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\). Estimating the number of effectively independent trials is a modelling choice taken up in Lesson 7.
  • Normality is an approximation. \(\hSR \approx \Normal(0, 1/T)\) ignores skewness, fat tails and serial correlation, which strategy returns can have. Lesson 7 replaces the variance with one that accounts for skewness and kurtosis.
  • The sample size is not fixed. The textbook test also assumes that the number of observations was chosen before the data were seen. A live record is judged when someone decides it has proved itself, a choice that depends on the data. Section 6 shows how the fixed-level test then fails and gives a monitor that does not; that monitor's own assumptions, a known scale and light tails, are the next to break (Remark 6.8).

8Misconceptions

  1. “A t-statistic of 2 means there is only a 5% chance my strategy is a fluke.”

    This is wrong twice. First, a p-value is the probability of a statistic at least this extreme given the null hypothesis, not the probability that the null is true given the statistic. Converting one into the other needs prior odds (Lesson 6). Second, after a search the reported statistic is a maximum, and the maximum of \(N\) null statistics exceeds the single-test 5% critical value far more often than 5% of the time. For \(N\) independent null tests that probability is \(1 - 0.95^N\), already 0.64 at \(N = 20\); Problem M3 works the same calculation for a Sharpe threshold.

  2. “Out-of-sample testing makes data snooping impossible.”

    A holdout consulted once is a valid test of one hypothesis. Consulted repeatedly, it becomes in-sample data for the selection of the final strategy, and Lab 3 shows that the reported out-of-sample Sharpe ratio then inherits the selection bias of a maximum over the rounds.

  3. “With a long enough sample, selection bias disappears.”

    By (2) the hurdle falls like \(1/\sqrt{T}\) but rises like \(\sqrt{\ln N}\). Longer samples invite larger searches. Holding \(N\) fixed, quadrupling the sample only halves the hurdle. The bias disappears only if \(T\) grows much faster than \(\ln N\), and nothing in a research process forces that.

  4. “My variants are just small tweaks of one idea, so they do not count as separate trials.”

    Correlated trials count partially, not zero times. Problem P3 shows that equicorrelated trials scale the expected maximum by \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\). At a pairwise correlation of 0.5 that removes only 29% of the bias.

  5. “I check my strategy's t-statistic every day and will stop the trial the first time it is significant at 5%. The 5% error rate still applies.”

    The 5% belongs to one look fixed in advance. Stopping at the first crossing reports the most favourable of many looks, so for a skill-free strategy the chance of crossing at some look rises with every look and tends to one (Proposition 6.1); Lab 4 puts it at 0.51 for a z-test with known volatility checked daily for three years. A rule that stops when the evidence looks good needs a statistic valid at any time, such as the e-value (4) compared with \(1/\alpha\) (Corollary 6.5).

M1 · Three years, twenty variants

A researcher tests \(N = 20\) independent variants on three years of daily data (\(T = 756\), \(P = 252\)) and reports the best annualized Sharpe ratio, 1.05. Assume every variant has a true Sharpe ratio of zero and \(\hSR_n \approx \Normal(0, 1/T)\). (a) Bound the expected maximum annualized Sharpe ratio with (2). (b) Estimate it with (3). (c) Is 1.05 evidence of skill?

Solution

(a) \(\sqrt{2\ln 20} = \sqrt{5.991465} = 2.4477\). The per-period standard deviation is \(1/\sqrt{756} = 0.03637\). Annualizing multiplies by \(\sqrt{252} = 15.875\), so the bound is \(2.4477 \times 0.03637 \times 15.875 = 1.413\).

(b) \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/20) = \Phi^{-1}(0.95) = 1.6449\) and \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/(20e)) = \Phi^{-1}(0.981606) = 2.0881\). The bracket is \(0.4228 \times 1.6449 + 0.5772 \times 2.0881 = 0.6955 + 1.2052 = 1.9007\). Annualized, the estimate is \(1.9007 \times 0.03637 \times 15.875 = 1.097\).

(c) No. The reported 1.05 is below what the best of twenty skill-free variants delivers on average: Lab 1's second table gives the exact expectation of the maximum of 20 standard normals, 1.8675, so \(1.8675 \times 0.03637 \times 15.875 = 1.078\) annualized, and approximation (3) gives 1.10. Nothing in the number distinguishes it from luck.

M2 · Monthly data

A fund reports an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.0 from five years of monthly returns (\(T = 60\), \(P = 12\)), selected from \(N = 50\) configurations. Compute the bound (2) and the estimate (3) of the expected maximum under the null.

Solution

The per-period standard deviation is \(1/\sqrt{60} = 0.12910\), and annualizing multiplies by \(\sqrt{12} = 3.4641\). The bound is \(\sqrt{2\ln 50} = 2.7971\), giving \(2.7971 \times 0.12910 \times 3.4641 = 1.251\). For the estimate, the bracket at \(N = 50\) is \((1-\emgamma)\Phi^{-1}(0.98) + \emgamma\Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/(50e)) = 0.4228 \times 2.0537 + 0.5772 \times 2.4393 = 2.2763\), so the estimate is \(2.2763 \times 0.12910 \times 3.4641 = 1.018\). The reported 1.0 is about what luck alone produces on average: estimate (3) gives 1.018. Short monthly track records cannot carry a search over fifty configurations.

M3 · The probability that some null strategy looks good

Each of \(N = 20\) independent null strategies has \(T = 1000\) daily observations. (a) What is the standard error of \(\hSR\) under the null? (b) What is the probability that one given strategy shows \(\hSR > 0.05\) per day? (c) What is the probability that at least one of the twenty does?

Solution

(a) Under the null the iid-normal variance is \((1 + 0^2/2)/T = 1/1000\), so the standard error is \(1/\sqrt{1000} = 0.03162\).

(b) \(\Prob(\hSR > 0.05) = 1 - \Phi(0.05 \times \sqrt{1000}) = 1 - \Phi(1.5811) = 0.0569\).

(c) By independence, \(1 - (1 - 0.0569)^{20} = 1 - 0.9431^{20} = 1 - 0.3099 = 0.690\). An event that is unusual for one strategy is the expected outcome across twenty.

M4 · Reading the monitor on day 504

A strategy with known daily volatility has run live for \(t = 504\) trading days, and its standardized cumulative return is \(S_{504} = 60\). Use \(\alpha = 0.05\) and \(\kappa = 252\). (a) Would a single two-sided 5% z-test planned for day 504 reject? (b) Compute the e-value \(M_{504}\) from (4) and the boundary \(b_{504}\) from (5). Does the monitor reject? (c) Give the interval \(C_{504}\) of (6) in annualized Sharpe-ratio units and compare it with the fixed-sample 95% interval.

Solution

(a) \(Z_{504} = 60/\sqrt{504} = 60/22.450 = 2.673 \gt 1.960\), so a single test planned for day 504 rejects.

(b) \(M_{504} = \sqrt{252/756}\,\exp\big(60^2/(2 \times 756)\big) = 0.57735 \times e^{2.38095} = 0.57735 \times 10.8152 = 6.244\). That is below \(1/\alpha = 20\), so the monitor does not reject. Equivalently, \(b_{504} = \sqrt{756 \times (5.9915 + \ln 3)} = \sqrt{756 \times 7.0901} = \sqrt{5360.1} = 73.21\), and \(|S_{504}| = 60 \lt 73.21\).

(c) Per day, \(C_{504} = [(60 - 73.21)/504,\ (60 + 73.21)/504] = [-0.02621,\ 0.26431]\). Multiplying by \(\sqrt{252} = 15.875\) gives \([-0.42,\ 4.20]\) annualized, around the point estimate \(15.875 \times 60/504 = 1.89\). The fixed-sample interval is \(60/504 \pm 1.959964/\sqrt{504} = 0.11905 \pm 0.08730\) per day, or \([0.50,\ 3.28]\) annualized. Each answer is right for its own question. The fixed interval is valid if day 504 was the only look planned. The e-value says that a gambler betting against zero skill has multiplied capital 6.2-fold, short of the 20-fold that makes the evidence decisive at 5% whatever the rule for stopping.

M5 · Tuning the monitor to a review date

Write \(u = t/\kappa\) and \(\ell = 2\ln(1/\alpha)\). (a) Show that, for fixed \(\kappa\), the normalized boundary \(b_t/\sqrt{t}\) of (5) is smallest at \(t = u^\ast\kappa\), where \(u^\ast\) is the unique positive solution of \(u = \ell + \ln(1 + u)\), and that its smallest value is \(\sqrt{1 + u^\ast}\), whatever \(\kappa\). (b) Find \(u^\ast\) and the minimum for \(\alpha = 0.05\) by iterating the equation from \(u = 8\). (c) Which \(\kappa\) makes the monitor tightest at a three-year review, and how much does it gain over \(\kappa = 252\) (Example 6.7)?

Solution

(a) \(b_t^2/t = (1 + 1/u)\big(\ell + \ln(1 + u)\big) =: f(u)\). Differentiating, \[ f'(u) = -\frac{\ell + \ln(1 + u)}{u^2} + \Big(1 + \frac{1}{u}\Big)\frac{1}{1 + u} = \frac{u - \ell - \ln(1 + u)}{u^2}. \] The numerator \(g(u) = u - \ell - \ln(1 + u)\) has \(g(0) = -\ell \lt 0\), \(g'(u) = u/(1 + u) \gt 0\) for \(u \gt 0\), and \(g(u) \to \infty\), so it has exactly one positive root \(u^\ast\), with \(f\) decreasing before it and increasing after. At the root \(\ell + \ln(1 + u^\ast) = u^\ast\), so \(f(u^\ast) = (1 + 1/u^\ast)\,u^\ast = 1 + u^\ast\), which does not involve \(\kappa\).

(b) \(\ell = 2\ln 20 = 5.99146\). Iterating \(u \mapsto \ell + \ln(1 + u)\) from 8 gives 8.18869, 8.20944, 8.21169, 8.21194, 8.21196, so \(u^\ast = 8.212\) and the minimum is \(\sqrt{9.212} = 3.035\). The iteration settles quickly because the map's derivative, \(1/(1 + u)\), is about 0.11 near the root. Howard et al. give the same optimum in closed form through the Lambert W function and quote about 3.0 for the minimum at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

(c) Put the minimum at \(t = 756\): \(\kappa = 756/8.212 = 92.1\), which gives \(b_{756}/\sqrt{756} = 3.035\) against 3.136 at \(\kappa = 252\), a hurdle 3.2% lower. The cost appears at other horizons: at ten years (\(t = 2520\)) the normalized boundary is 3.111 with \(\kappa = 92.1\) and 3.038 with \(\kappa = 252\). Neither choice is as much as 3.5% above the best possible value, 3.035, at either horizon, so a rough choice of \(\kappa\) costs little.

Proof

P1 · Sub-Gaussian trials

A random variable \(X\) is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy \(\sigma^2\) if \(\E[e^{\lambda X}] \le e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2}\) for all real \(\lambda\). Prove that if \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) are each sub-Gaussian with proxy \(\sigma^2\), with any joint distribution, then \(\E[\max_n X_n] \le \sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}\) for \(N \ge 2\).

Solution

Repeat the proof of Theorem 2.1 with the weaker moment bound. For \(\lambda > 0\), Jensen's inequality and \(e^{\lambda \max_n X_n} \le \sum_n e^{\lambda X_n}\) give \(e^{\lambda\E[\max X_n]} \le \sum_n \E[e^{\lambda X_n}] \le N e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2}\). Taking logarithms, \(\E[\max X_n] \le \ln N/\lambda + \lambda\sigma^2/2\). Minimizing over \(\lambda\) gives \(\lambda = \sqrt{2\ln N}/\sigma\) and the bound \(\sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}\). Theorem 2.1 is the case \(\sigma = 1\), since a standard normal satisfies the moment condition with equality. The result matters because centred bounded statistics (sub-Gaussian with proxy \((b-a)^2/4\) by Hoeffding's lemma) and any other centred sub-Gaussian statistic also obey it; merely light-tailed statistics need not (the expected maximum of \(N\) centred exponentials grows like \(\ln N\)).

P2 · Two trials, exactly

Let \((Z_1, Z_2)\) be bivariate normal with standard normal margins and correlation \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\). Prove that \(\E[\max(Z_1, Z_2)] = \sqrt{(1-\rho)/\pi}\), and evaluate it at \(\rho = 0\) and \(\rho = 1\).

Solution

For any reals, \(\max(a,b) = \tfrac{a+b}{2} + \tfrac{|a-b|}{2}\). Taking expectations, \(\E[(Z_1+Z_2)/2] = 0\). The difference \(W = Z_1 - Z_2\) is normal with mean 0 and variance \(1 + 1 - 2\rho = 2(1-\rho)\). For \(W \sim \Normal(0, s^2)\), \(\E|W| = s\sqrt{2/\pi}\). Hence \(\E|Z_1 - Z_2| = \sqrt{2(1-\rho)}\sqrt{2/\pi} = 2\sqrt{(1-\rho)/\pi}\), and \(\E[\max(Z_1,Z_2)] = \sqrt{(1-\rho)/\pi}\). At \(\rho = 0\) this is \(1/\sqrt{\pi} = 0.5642\). At \(\rho = 1\) it is 0: two identical trials are one trial, and choosing between them creates no bias.

P3 · Equicorrelated trials

Let \(Z_n = \sqrt{\rho}\,W + \sqrt{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_n\) for \(n = 1, \dots, N\), where \(\rho \in [0,1]\) and \(W, \varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_N\) are independent standard normals. Show that each \(Z_n\) is standard normal, that \(\Corr(Z_m, Z_n) = \rho\) for \(m \ne n\), and that \(\E[\max_n Z_n] = \sqrt{1-\rho}\;\E[\max_n \varepsilon_n]\).

Solution

\(Z_n\) is a linear combination of independent normals, so it is normal with mean 0 and variance \(\rho + (1-\rho) = 1\). For \(m \ne n\), \(\Cov(Z_m, Z_n) = \rho\Var(W) = \rho\), which is the correlation because the variances are 1. Since \(\sqrt{\rho}\,W\) does not depend on \(n\), \(\max_n Z_n = \sqrt{\rho}\,W + \sqrt{1-\rho}\max_n\varepsilon_n\). Taking expectations and using \(\E[W] = 0\) gives the result. Correlation among trials therefore scales the selection bias by \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\): by 0.71 at \(\rho = 0.5\), by 0.32 at \(\rho = 0.9\). It does not divide the number of trials by any fixed factor.

P4 · Multiplying evidence

(a) Prove that the product of independent e-values for \(H_0\) is an e-value for \(H_0\). (b) Let \(B_1, B_2, \dots\) be nonnegative, with each \(B_t\) a function of \(Y_1, \dots, Y_t\) and \(\E_0[B_t \mid Y_1, \dots, Y_{t-1}] \le 1\). Prove that \(M_0 = 1\), \(M_t = B_1 B_2 \cdots B_t\) is a test supermartingale. (c) Show by example that the product of two dependent e-values need not be an e-value. (d) What does (b) allow a desk that reviews a strategy each quarter to do?

Solution

(a) If \(E_1, \dots, E_k\) are independent e-values, their product is nonnegative and, by independence, \(\E_0[E_1 \cdots E_k] = \E_0[E_1] \cdots \E_0[E_k] \le 1\).

(b) \(M_t \ge 0\), \(M_0 = 1\), and \(M_t\) is a function of \(Y_1, \dots, Y_t\). Given \(Y_1, \dots, Y_{t-1}\), the factor \(M_{t-1}\) is known and nonnegative, so \(\E_0[M_t \mid Y_1, \dots, Y_{t-1}] = M_{t-1}\,\E_0[B_t \mid Y_1, \dots, Y_{t-1}] \le M_{t-1}\). The likelihood ratio \(L_t(\lambda)\) of Proposition 6.6 is the case \(B_t = e^{\lambda Y_t - \lambda^2/2}\), whose conditional mean under \(H_0\) is exactly 1 by the normal moment generating function.

(c) Let \(U\) be uniform on \((0, 1)\) and \(E_1 = E_2 = 2\,\indic\{U \le 1/2\}\). Each has mean 1, but \(E_1 E_2 = 4\,\indic\{U \le 1/2\}\) has mean 2.

(d) Suppose each quarter's e-value is valid given the earlier quarters, for example the normal mixture (4) restarted at the start of the quarter and computed from that quarter's returns only, with returns iid across days. By (b), read in quarterly time, the running product over quarters is a test supermartingale, so by Theorem 6.4 the desk may keep multiplying and act in the first quarter in which the product reaches \(1/\alpha\), with type I error at most \(\alpha\) however many quarters that takes. Ramdas et al. state both rules used in this problem: independent e-values, or e-values each valid given the earlier ones, may be multiplied, and an average of e-values with weights fixed in advance is an e-value under any dependence.

Empirical

E1 · Scaling with the number of trials

Modify Lab 2 to run with \(N \in \{10, 100, 1000\}\) strategies, keeping 630 in-sample days. Before running, predict the mean in-sample annualized Sharpe ratio of the selected strategy from approximation (3) and from the exact expectation. Then run and compare.

Solution

Scale a standard-normal maximum to an annualized Sharpe ratio by multiplying by \(\sqrt{252/630} = 0.632456\). From (3): \(1.5746 \times 0.632456 = 0.996\), \(2.5306 \times 0.632456 = 1.600\) and \(3.2551 \times 0.632456 = 2.059\). The exact expectations, which Lab 1 computes by numerical integration, are 1.5388, 2.5076 and 3.2414, giving 0.973, 1.586 and 2.050. With 100 replications the simulated means carry Monte Carlo error. The maximum of \(N\) normals has standard deviation 0.5868, 0.4294 and 0.3514 for \(N = 10, 100, 1000\) (Lab 1's second table), so the standard error is at most \(0.5868 \times 0.632456/\sqrt{100} = 0.037\). Your results should therefore sit within about ±0.07 (two standard errors) of the exact values. At 100 replications the Monte Carlo error (up to 0.037) is larger than the gap between (3) and the exact value (0.009–0.023 in annualized units), so the run cannot tell them apart. The sketch: wrap the lab's loop in for n_strategies in (10, 100, 1000): and print the mean of best_is.

E2 · Correlated variants

Change Lab 2 so that the strategies share a common component, r = 0.01 * (np.sqrt(rho) * w + np.sqrt(1 - rho) * e) with w of shape (T, 1) and e of shape (T, N), and set \(\rho = 0.5\). Predict the mean in-sample Sharpe ratio of the selected strategy using P3, then verify.

Solution

By P3 the expected maximum of the standardized statistics scales by \(\sqrt{1-\rho} = 0.7071\). The exact independent value for \(N = 100\) is 2.5076 (Lab 1), so the prediction is \(0.7071 \times 2.5076 \times 0.632456 = 1.121\) annualized, against 1.586 for independent strategies. One subtlety: each column's sample mean contains the common term \(\sqrt{\rho}\,\bar w\), divided by a standard deviation that is nearly the same for every column. That term hardly changes which trial wins, and because \(\E[\bar w] = 0\) it contributes nothing on average across replications. Your simulated mean should therefore be close to 1.12, with Monte Carlo error of a few hundredths.

E3 · A momentum grid

A researcher evaluates a time-series momentum rule on twenty years of monthly data (\(T = 240\), \(P = 12\)) over a grid of lookbacks \(J \in \{3,6,9,12\}\) and holding periods \(K \in \{3,6,9,12\}\). Compute \(N\), the estimate (3) and the bound (2) for the expected maximum annualized Sharpe ratio under the null. Then argue in which direction the grid's structure moves the true expectation.

Solution

\(N = 16\). The per-period standard deviation is \(1/\sqrt{240} = 0.06455\), and annualizing multiplies by \(\sqrt{12} = 3.4641\). The bracket in (3) is 1.8005, giving \(1.8005 \times 0.06455 \times 3.4641 = 0.403\). The bound is \(\sqrt{2\ln 16} = 2.3548\), giving \(2.3548 \times 0.06455 \times 3.4641 = 0.527\). Neighbouring cells of the grid share most of their positions (a 9-month and a 12-month lookback overlap for nine months), so the trials are strongly positively correlated. By P3 that lowers the expectation below the independent value of 0.40. Approximation (3) with \(N = 16\) is therefore conservative for this grid. The exact amount depends on the correlation matrix, which Lesson 7 estimates.

Research

R1 · Making N observable

Design a trial log for a research team so that every correction in this module can be computed from it. Specify what is recorded, when, by whom, and how variants of one idea are grouped.

Solution

A complete answer covers six points.

  1. An append-only record per evaluation. Each time any performance statistic is computed on any data, record a timestamp, the code commit, the full parameter vector, the data vintage and sample window, the statistic, and whether the result was viewed. The log is written by the backtester itself (Atlas A9 passes every trial's returns to A8's integrity_report, per atlas/A8.contract.md; the other fields are yours to add), never by hand, so no evaluation escapes it.
  2. A holdout register. Every access to reserved data is a logged event, and each access counts as a trial against that holdout (Lab 3).
  3. Grouping. Variants of one idea share an idea identifier, so analysts can report both the raw count and an effective count estimated from the correlation of the variants' return series (Lesson 7).
  4. Pre-registration. Before touching data, record the primary hypothesis, the statistic, the sample and the decision rule. Departures are allowed but flagged.
  5. Reporting. Every strategy proposal carries N, the dispersion of the trial Sharpe ratios and the deflated statistics of Lessons 7–8.
  6. Limitations. Ideas that were considered and rejected without running any code are invisible to any log, and grouping is a judgement call. The log reduces the unobserved part of N but does not eliminate it.
R2 · Interrogating a pitch

A manager says: “We tested 400 variants in-sample, and the chosen one has an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.5 over three years.” Write the questions you would ask, and explain how each answer would change your assessment.

Solution

Start from the best case. If the out-of-sample period was used exactly once, for this one strategy, then it is a valid single test. An annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.5 over three years has a t-statistic of about \(1.5\sqrt{3} = 2.60\) (one-sided p-value 0.005), which is genuine evidence. The questions decide whether that best case holds.

  1. How many times was the out-of-sample period consulted, and for how many candidates? If \(K\) rounds peeked at it, the reported number is a maximum over \(K\) (Lab 3). For instance, \(K = 5\) moves the null expectation from 0 to about 1.19 for a one-year holdout, or \(1.19/\sqrt{3} = 0.69\) for three years.
  2. Was the out-of-sample period chosen after seeing results, for example by starting it after a drawdown? If so, its boundaries are themselves a trial.
  3. How correlated were the 400 variants? This matters for the in-sample statistics (P3), though not for a single clean holdout.
  4. What are the skewness and kurtosis of the returns? Strategies that sell volatility can show high Sharpe ratios over three calm years, and Lesson 7 adjusts for this.
  5. Can the trial log be audited (R1)?

A manager who cannot answer the first question has not shown out-of-sample evidence.

R3 · A single history

White argues that data snooping is practically unavoidable with time-series data because only a single history is available. Propose three partial remedies, and state the cost or failure mode of each.

Solution
  1. Correct for the search inside the sample. Use the FWER, FDR and bootstrap methods of Lessons 2–5, or deflate the reported statistic (Lessons 7–8). The cost is power, and every method needs N and the dependence between trials, which (Section 7) are imperfectly known.
  2. Find genuinely new data. Look to other markets, other asset classes, earlier periods recovered from archives, or the future (paper trading). This is the strongest remedy, because the new data played no part in the search. Its failure modes are that markets may differ for real economic reasons, so failure abroad is not proof of luck at home, and that waiting for the future is slow.
  3. Restrict the search in advance. Use theory and pre-registration to cut N before looking. This lowers the hurdle legitimately, but it can exclude true discoveries, and it works only if the restriction is credible to an outside reader, which requires the trial log of R1.

No remedy removes the single-history problem. Each trades power, speed or scope for credibility.

R4 · Monitoring a book of strategies

A desk runs \(K = 20\) live strategies and monitors each daily with its own normal-mixture e-value \(M^{(k)}_t\), computed from that strategy's standardized returns alone. (a) Show that raising an alarm on strategy \(k\) when \(M^{(k)}_t \ge K/\alpha\) keeps the probability that any skill-free strategy ever raises one at or below \(\alpha\), whatever the dependence between the strategies. (b) What z-hurdle does this impose at three years for \(\alpha = 0.05\) and \(\kappa = 252\)? (c) If the desk only needs to know whether any strategy has skill, when is the average \(\bar M_t = K^{-1}\sum_k M^{(k)}_t\) a valid monitor, and what does it gain and lose? (d) How does this connect to Lessons 2 and 3?

Solution

(a) For each skill-free strategy \(k\), \(M^{(k)}\) is a test martingale for its own data, so Theorem 6.4 at level \(\alpha/K\) gives \(\Prob\big(\sup_t M^{(k)}_t \ge K/\alpha\big) \le \alpha/K\). The probability that at least one of the \(K_0 \le K\) skill-free strategies ever alarms is at most the sum of these bounds, \(K_0\alpha/K \le \alpha\). Each bound uses only strategy \(k\)'s own returns, so no assumption about the dependence between strategies enters.

(b) Use (5) with \(\alpha/K = 0.0025\): \(2\ln 400 = 11.9829\) and \(\ln 4 = 1.3863\), so \(b_{756} = \sqrt{1008 \times 13.3692} = \sqrt{13476.2} = 116.09\), a z-hurdle of \(116.09/27.495 = 4.222\), against 3.136 for a single strategy. Monitoring twenty strategies raises the bar by 35%.

(c) Suppose the vectors of the \(K\) standardized returns are iid across days, with any correlation within a day. Then, given the joint past of all strategies, each skill-free strategy's next return is still \(\Normal(0, 1)\), so part (2) of the proof of Proposition 6.6 goes through with the joint past in place of the strategy's own, and each \(M^{(k)}\) is a test martingale with respect to it. An average of test martingales with respect to the same past is a test martingale, by linearity of conditional expectation, so if all \(K\) strategies are skill-free, \(\Prob(\sup_t \bar M_t \ge 1/\alpha) \le \alpha\). When several strategies carry modest evidence their e-values add, and \(\bar M\) can cross \(1/\alpha\) while no single \(M^{(k)}\) reaches \(K/\alpha\). When the evidence sits in one strategy, \(\bar M\) crosses only when that strategy's e-value is near \(K/\alpha\), so nothing is gained. And rejecting this null says that some strategy has skill, not which one. Without the iid assumption, each \(M^{(k)}\) is known to be a test martingale only with respect to its own strategy's past, and the argument for the average no longer applies.

(d) The guarantee in (a) is the family-wise error rate of Lesson 2, controlled by Bonferroni's argument applied to Ville's bound, and here valid at every look. Lesson 3's false discovery rate has an e-value counterpart: Ramdas et al. describe the e-BH procedure of Wang and Ramdas, which rejects the hypotheses with the \(k^\ast\) largest of \(K\) e-values, \(k^\ast = \max\{k : e_{[k]} \ge K/(k\alpha)\}\) with \(e_{[k]}\) the \(k\)-th largest, and controls the false discovery rate at \(\alpha\) under arbitrary dependence between the e-values. The desk must supply the same ingredient as in Problem R1: a complete list of the strategies being monitored, because \(K\) enters every threshold.
