Bonferroni and Holm protect a family of tests by assuming the worst about how the tests depend on one another. Strategies mined from one history are strongly dependent, so those bounds throw away power exactly where a desk needs it. The bootstrap answers the question a portfolio manager actually asks, whether the best of everything tried beats the benchmark, while respecting the dependence across strategies and over time. This lesson builds that test and its stationary-bootstrap engine, proves what each part does, and shows the one design flaw that Hansen's refinement removes.

1One benchmark, many challengers

A researcher compares \(K\) strategies with a benchmark (cash, the market, the strategy already running) on the same \(T\) periods. Write \(r_{k,t}\) for strategy \(k\)'s return and \(r_{0,t}\) for the benchmark's.

Definition 1.1 (performance differentials)

The performance differential of strategy \(k\) in period \(t\) is \(d_{k,t} = r_{k,t} - r_{0,t}\), with mean \(\mu_k = \E[d_{k,t}]\) and sample mean \(\bar d_k = T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^T d_{k,t}\). Any criterion that is an average over periods (a loss differential, a log-return differential, a utility differential) fits the same frame.

Start with one challenger, \(K = 1\), and write \(d_t = d_{1,t}\) and \(\mu = \mu_1\). The question is whether \(\mu = 0\). The variance that governs \(\bar d\) is the long-run variance \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} = \sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty} \gamma(h)\), where \(\gamma(h) = \Cov(d_t, d_{t+h})\), not \(\gamma(0)\) alone: forecast errors at overlapping horizons and returns on stale or smoothed prices make the differentials serially correlated, and Section 3 shows by how much that changes the variance of an average.

Definition 1.2 (Diebold–Mariano statistic)

For a bandwidth \(\ell \ge 0\), let \(\hat\gamma(j) = T^{-1}\sum_{t=j+1}^{T} (d_t - \bar d)(d_{t-j} - \bar d)\) and \[ \hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} = \hat\gamma(0) + 2\sum_{j=1}^{\ell} \Big(1 - \frac{j}{\ell + 1}\Big)\hat\gamma(j), \] a Bartlett-weighted estimate of the long-run variance of the kind behind Newey–West standard errors; the weights make it non-negative in every sample (Problem P5). The Diebold–Mariano test of equal predictive accuracy, \(H_0\colon \mu = 0\), uses \[ \mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{LR}}/\sqrt{T}} \] and rejects at level \(\alpha\) when \(|\mathrm{DM}| \gt \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)\), or, against the one-sided alternative that the challenger is better, when \(\mathrm{DM} \gt \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha)\).

Diebold and Mariano propose tests of the null of no difference in the accuracy of two competing forecasts in which the loss function need not be quadratic, or even symmetric, and the forecast errors can be non-Gaussian, nonzero-mean, serially correlated and contemporaneously correlated; they propose and evaluate both asymptotic and exact finite-sample tests. The asymptotic test rests on one property of the loss differential.

Proposition 1.3 (the Diebold–Mariano limit)

Suppose that (i) \((d_t)\) is covariance stationary with mean \(\mu\) and absolutely summable autocovariances, (ii) \(\sqrt{T}(\bar d - \mu) \dto \Normal(0, \sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}})\) with \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} \gt 0\), and (iii) \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} \pto \sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}}\). Then \(T\Var(\bar d) \to \sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}}\). Under \(H_0\colon \mu = 0\), \(\mathrm{DM} \dto \Normal(0, 1)\), so both versions of the test have asymptotic level \(\alpha\). If \(\mu \gt 0\), \(\mathrm{DM} \to \infty\) in probability.

Proof

By stationarity, \(T\Var(\bar d) = T^{-1}\sum_{s,t=1}^{T} \gamma(t - s) = \sum_{|h| \lt T} (1 - |h|/T)\,\gamma(h)\). Each term tends to \(\gamma(h)\) and is bounded by \(|\gamma(h)|\), which is summable, so by dominated convergence the sum tends to \(\sum_h \gamma(h) = \sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}}\). Under \(H_0\), \(\mathrm{DM} = \big(\sqrt{T}\,\bar d/\sigma_{\mathrm{LR}}\big)\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{LR}}/\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{LR}}\big)\). The first factor converges in distribution to \(\Normal(0,1)\) by (ii). The second converges in probability to 1 by (iii), because \(x \mapsto \sqrt{\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}}/x}\) is continuous at \(x = \sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} \gt 0\). Slutsky's theorem gives the limit. If \(\mu \gt 0\), \(\mathrm{DM} = \sqrt{T}\mu/\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{LR}} + \sqrt{T}(\bar d - \mu)/\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{LR}}\). The second term is bounded in probability by (ii) and (iii), and the first tends to infinity in probability.

Assumption (i) concerns the loss differential, not the forecasts: biased, fat-tailed or correlated forecast errors are allowed as long as \(d_t\) is covariance stationary. Assumptions (ii) and (iii) are where dependence enters. (ii) is a central limit theorem for dependent data, of the kind Theorem 5.1 imports. (iii) needs the bandwidth \(\ell\) to grow with \(T\), but more slowly. Newey and West give a simple method of calculating a heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent covariance matrix that is positive semi-definite by construction, and establish its consistency under fairly general conditions. Problem M4 computes the statistic by hand, and Problem E4 measures its size.

Remark 1.4 (nested models)

Proposition 1.3 needs \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} \gt 0\), and a common use violates it. Suppose forecast 0 comes from a regression and forecast 1 from the same regression with extra predictors, and the null is that the extra coefficients are zero. At the population coefficients the two forecasts are then identical, so the population loss differential is zero in every period and \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} = 0\). The sample differential is not zero, because both models are estimated, but it consists entirely of estimation error, and the normal limit of Proposition 1.3 is not available. Clark and McCracken study tests of equal forecast accuracy and encompassing for nested models. Giacomini and White propose tests of predictive ability for possibly misspecified forecasting models, derived in an environment where the finite-sample properties of the estimators on which the forecasts depend are preserved asymptotically, so that the tests capture the effect of estimation uncertainty on relative forecast performance and can handle forecasts from both nested and nonnested models. Diebold later stresses that the DM test was intended for comparing forecasts, not models, and that for comparing models in pseudo-out-of-sample environments simpler and more compelling full-sample procedures exist. Theorem 5.1 likewise assumes differentials that involve no estimated parameters: the challengers in this module are fixed rules, so their differentials compare forecasts in Diebold's sense.

Remark 1.5 (from one challenger to many)

With \(K = 1\) the Reality Check statistic of Section 2 is \(\sqrt{T}\,\bar d\), and by Theorem 5.1 its bootstrap null law approximates \(\Normal(0, \sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}})\). Its p-value therefore approaches the one-sided DM p-value \(1 - \Phi(\mathrm{DM})\): for one challenger the two tests coincide asymptotically, with different estimates of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{LR}}\). With \(K \gt 1\), running one DM test per strategy and reporting the best is the data snooping of Lesson 1. The Reality Check replaces the \(K\) separate nulls by the law of their maximum, and the SPA test of Section 6 studentizes each average, so its statistic is the largest of \(K\) DM-type statistics.

Remark 1.6 (encompassing and combination)

Equal accuracy is not the only question two forecasts raise. With errors \(e_{0,t}\) and \(e_{1,t}\), the combination with weight \(\lambda\) on forecast 1 has error \(e_{0,t} - \lambda(e_{0,t} - e_{1,t})\) and mean squared error \(\E[e_0^2] - 2\lambda\E[e_0(e_0 - e_1)] + \lambda^2\E[(e_0 - e_1)^2]\), which is minimized at \(\lambda^\ast = \E[e_0(e_0 - e_1)]/\E[(e_0 - e_1)^2]\). Forecast 0 encompasses forecast 1 when \(\lambda^\ast = 0\), that is, when \(\E[e_{0,t}(e_{0,t} - e_{1,t})] = 0\): forecast 1 adds nothing to a combination. That is again a null about the mean of a series, \(e_{0,t}(e_{0,t} - e_{1,t})\), and Proposition 1.3 applies to it under the same conditions. Harvey, Leybourne and Newbold note that the natural regression-based test for forecast encompassing has a null distribution that is not robust to nonnormality in the forecast errors, and discuss alternative tests that are robust. Equal accuracy does not imply encompassing. If both forecasts are unbiased, with mean squared error \(s^2\) and error correlation \(\rho \lt 1\), then \(\E[e_0(e_0 - e_1)] = s^2(1 - \rho) \gt 0\), \(\lambda^\ast = 1/2\), and the equal-weight combination has mean squared error \(s^2(1 + \rho)/2 \lt s^2\). A DM test would find nothing to choose between them, and the combination beats both. Bates and Granger combine two sets of forecasts of airline passenger data, conclude that the composite can yield lower mean-square error than either original, and examine ways of deriving the weights from past errors.

With \(K \gt 1\), each \(\bar d_k\) is the kind of statistic Lesson 1 warned about: reporting the best of \(K\) of them carries data-snooping bias. White's formulation tests the selection itself. The null hypothesis is that the best model is no better than the benchmark, \(H_0\colon \max_{k=1,\dots,K} \mu_k \le 0\), an intersection of the \(K\) one-sided hypotheses \(\mu_k \le 0\). Rejecting \(H_0\) says that at least one strategy beats the benchmark; Lesson 5 turns this into decisions about individual strategies.

Lesson 2 controlled the family-wise error rate with bounds that hold for any dependence. White states the alternative goal directly: a method that does not rely on bounds such as Bonferroni's but delivers, at least asymptotically, appropriate p-values. The price of that precision is a model of the joint distribution of \((\bar d_1, \dots, \bar d_K)\), which the bootstrap supplies.

2The least favourable configuration

\(H_0\) is composite: every vector \(\mu\) with all coordinates \(\le 0\) satisfies it. A test needs one null distribution, and the natural statistic, \[ V_K = \max_{k} \sqrt{T}\,\bar d_k, \tag{1}\] is largest when every \(\mu_k\) sits at the boundary.

Theorem 2.1 (the boundary is least favourable)

Suppose \(\bar d = \mu + \bar\varepsilon\), where the distribution of the noise vector \(\bar\varepsilon \in \R^K\) does not depend on \(\mu\). Write \(V(\mu) = \max_k \sqrt{T}(\mu_k + \bar\varepsilon_k)\). Then for every \(\mu\) with \(\max_k \mu_k \le 0\) and every \(c \in \R\), \[ \Prob\big(V(\mu) > c\big) \le \Prob\big(V(0) > c\big). \]

Proof

Fix an outcome of \(\bar\varepsilon\). Since every \(\mu_k \le 0\), each coordinate satisfies \(\sqrt{T}(\mu_k + \bar\varepsilon_k) \le \sqrt{T}\,\bar\varepsilon_k\), so the maximum satisfies \(V(\mu) \le V(0)\) on every outcome. Hence \(\{V(\mu) > c\} \subseteq \{V(0) > c\}\), and the probabilities are ordered the same way. The law of \(V(0)\) is the same whatever the true \(\mu\), because the law of \(\bar\varepsilon\) is.

A critical value computed at \(\mu = 0\) therefore controls the rejection probability at every point of the null. White enforces the null in exactly this way, using the element of the null least favourable to the alternative, \(\mu_k = 0\) for all \(k\). The location-family assumption holds in the limit, where \(\sqrt{T}(\bar d - \mu)\) is approximately normal with a covariance that does not depend on \(\mu\). In finite samples it is an approximation: a strategy's variance can move with its mean.

One problem remains. The null distribution of \(V(0)\) is the law of the maximum of \(K\) correlated, serially dependent averages. White notes that this distribution, the extreme value of a vector of correlated normals, is not known in closed form for the general case, so an analytic test is not feasible. It has to be simulated from the data.

3Resampling dependent data: the stationary bootstrap

The bootstrap of S1 resamples observations independently. That destroys serial dependence, and serial dependence changes the variance of an average. Take the stationary AR(1) process (an ARMA model of S3) \(x_t = \phi x_{t-1} + e_t\) with \(|\phi| \lt 1\) and \(e_t\) iid with variance \(\sigma^2\). Its autocovariances are \(\gamma(h) = \sigma^2 \phi^{|h|}/(1-\phi^2)\), and the variance of the sample mean satisfies \[ T\,\Var(\bar x) \to \sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty} \gamma(h) = \frac{\sigma^2}{1-\phi^2}\cdot\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi} = \frac{\sigma^2}{(1-\phi)^2}, \] the long-run variance that HAC standard errors estimate. An iid resample sees only \(\gamma(0) = \sigma^2/(1-\phi^2)\). The ratio of the two is \((1+\phi)/(1-\phi)\), which equals 3 at \(\phi = 0.5\), so the iid bootstrap understates the standard error of the mean by a factor \(\sqrt{3} = 1.73\). Strategy differentials measured over overlapping horizons (for example 20-day returns sampled daily), or computed from stale or smoothed prices, are serially correlated, so this is not a curiosity.

Resampling blocks of consecutive observations keeps the dependence inside each block. The stationary bootstrap chooses block lengths at random so that the resampled series is itself stationary. The procedure, due to Politis and Romano, uses blocks whose lengths are geometrically distributed with mean block length \(b\).

Definition 3.1 (stationary bootstrap)

Given observations \(x_0, \dots, x_{n-1}\) and a mean block length \(b \ge 1\), set \(p = 1/b\). The stationary bootstrap draws indices \(I_1, \dots, I_n\) as follows: \(I_1\) is uniform on \(\{0, \dots, n-1\}\); for \(t \ge 2\), with probability \(p\) a new block starts and \(I_t\) is a fresh uniform draw, and otherwise \(I_t = (I_{t-1} + 1) \bmod n\). The resample is \(X^\ast_t = x_{I_t}\). With several series observed on the same dates, one index sequence is applied to every series, so each resampled period keeps its cross-section intact.

Theorem 3.2 (what the index chain guarantees)

Conditional on the data, (a) every \(I_t\) is uniform on \(\{0, \dots, n-1\}\), and the sequence \((I_t)\) is a strictly stationary Markov chain; (b) the number of periods from the start of one block to the start of the next is geometric, \(\Prob(L = \ell) = (1-p)^{\ell - 1} p\) for \(\ell \ge 1\), with mean \(1/p = b\); (c) the resample \((X^\ast_t)\) is strictly stationary; (d) \(\E^\ast[\bar X^\ast] = \bar x\), where \(\E^\ast\) is the expectation over the resampling.

Proof

(a) The indices form a Markov chain on \(\{0, \dots, n-1\}\) with transition matrix \(P = p\,\tfrac1n \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top} + (1-p) S\), where \(S\) is the cyclic shift sending \(i\) to \((i+1) \bmod n\). Let \(u = \tfrac1n \mathbf{1}^{\top}\) be the uniform row vector. Then \(u\,\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top} = \tfrac1n (u\mathbf{1})\mathbf{1}^{\top} = u\), and \(uS = u\) because \(S\) permutes the states. Hence \(uP = pu + (1-p)u = u\): the uniform distribution is invariant. The chain starts from it, so every \(I_t\) is uniform and the chain is strictly stationary.

(b) Each step after the first starts a new block with probability \(p\), independently of everything else. The gap between consecutive block starts is the waiting time for the first success in independent trials with success probability \(p\), which is geometric with mean \(1/p\). Blocks cut off by the end of the resample are shorter; this truncation does not affect (a), (c) or (d).

(c) \(X^\ast_t = x_{I_t}\) applies one fixed function to each term of a strictly stationary sequence, so the joint law of \((X^\ast_{t+1}, \dots, X^\ast_{t+m})\) does not depend on \(t\).

(d) By (a), \(\E^\ast[X^\ast_t] = \tfrac1n \sum_{i} x_i = \bar x\) for every \(t\). Averaging over \(t\) gives \(\E^\ast[\bar X^\ast] = \bar x\).

Part (d) is what makes recentring work in Section 4: \(\sqrt{T}(\bar d^\ast_k - \bar d_k)\) has bootstrap mean zero, so the resampled statistics automatically sit at the least favourable point. The next result measures how much dependence the resample keeps.

Theorem 3.3 (exact bootstrap variance of the mean)

Let \(\hat c(h) = \tfrac1n \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (x_i - \bar x)(x_{(i+h) \bmod n} - \bar x)\) be the circular sample autocovariance. For a stationary-bootstrap resample of length \(n\), \[ \Var^\ast\big(\bar X^\ast\big) = \frac1n\Big[\hat c(0) + 2\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}\Big(1 - \frac hn\Big)(1-p)^h\,\hat c(h)\Big]. \tag{2}\]

Proof

Fix \(t\) and \(h \ge 1\). If no new block starts at any of the steps \(t+1, \dots, t+h\), an event of probability \((1-p)^h\) independent of \(I_t\), then \(I_{t+h} = (I_t + h) \bmod n\). Since \(I_t\) is uniform, the bootstrap covariance on this event is \(\hat c(h)\). If a new block starts at some step \(s \in (t, t+h]\), then \(I_s\) is a fresh uniform draw, and \(I_{t+h} = (I_s + t + h - s) \bmod n\) is uniform and independent of \(I_t\). On that event the covariance is \(\E^\ast[X^\ast_t - \bar x]\,\E^\ast[X^\ast_{t+h} - \bar x] = 0\) by Theorem 3.2(d). Hence \(\Cov^\ast(X^\ast_t, X^\ast_{t+h}) = (1-p)^h \hat c(h)\), and \(\Var^\ast(X^\ast_t) = \hat c(0)\). Summing over the \(n^2\) pairs, there are \(n - h\) pairs at each distance \(h\): \[ \Var^\ast(\bar X^\ast) = \frac1{n^2}\Big[n\hat c(0) + 2\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}(n-h)(1-p)^h\hat c(h)\Big], \] which is (2).

Remark 3.4 (the block length is a bias–degeneracy trade-off)

Formula (2) is a lag-window estimate of the long-run variance with geometrically decaying weights \((1-p)^h\). At \(p = 1\), the iid bootstrap, it collapses to \(\hat c(0)/n\) and ignores all autocorrelation. Shorter blocks shrink the weights on true autocovariances and pull the variance toward the iid value \(\hat c(0)/n\): downward when the autocovariances are positive, as for the AR(1) of Lab 1, upward when they are negative. The opposite extreme also fails. As \(p \to 0\) the resample becomes a single block, a rotation of the sample whose mean equals \(\bar x\) exactly, and (2) tends to zero. The block length must therefore grow with the sample, but more slowly than it. For means of α-mixing processes the procedure delivers valid bootstrap approximations provided the block length grows appropriately with the sample size, in White's statement \(q_n = 1/b \to 0\) with \(n q_n \to \infty\).

Lab 1 · Iid versus stationary bootstrap for a dependent series

For a SIMULATED series \(x_t = 0.5\,x_{t-1} + e_t\) of length 500, the true standard deviation of the sample mean is \(1/((1-0.5)\sqrt{500}) = 0.0894\). The iid bootstrap should report about \(0.0894/\sqrt3 = 0.0516\). Stationary-bootstrap estimates should recover most of the gap, should not increase without limit as blocks lengthen (Remark 3.4), and formula (2) should agree with brute-force resampling on a single series.

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED AR(1) series: x_t = phi * x_{t-1} + e_t with unit-variance shocks.
rng = np.random.default_rng(404)
phi, n_obs, n_series, n_boot = 0.5, 500, 40, 400


def ar1(count: int, n: int) -> NDArray[np.float64]:
    """count independent AR(1) paths of length n, one per row."""
    e = rng.standard_normal((count, n + 200))
    x = np.empty_like(e)
    x[:, 0] = e[:, 0]
    for t in range(1, e.shape[1]):
        x[:, t] = phi * x[:, t - 1] + e[:, t]
    return x[:, 200:]  # drop the burn-in so each path starts near stationarity


def stationary_indices(n: int, mean_block: float, draws: int) -> NDArray[np.intp]:
    """Stationary-bootstrap index matrix (draws x n): new block w.p. 1/mean_block, else step +1 mod n."""
    starts = rng.integers(0, n, size=(draws, n))
    new_block = rng.random((draws, n)) < 1.0 / mean_block
    new_block[:, 0] = True
    pos = np.arange(n)
    block_start = np.maximum.accumulate(np.where(new_block, pos, 0), axis=1)
    rows = np.arange(draws)[:, None]
    idx: NDArray[np.intp] = (starts[rows, block_start] + pos - block_start) % n
    return idx


def exact_bootstrap_sd(x: NDArray[np.float64], mean_block: float) -> float:
    """Theorem 3.3: Var*(mean) = (1/n)[c(0) + 2 sum_h (1 - h/n)(1 - p)^h c(h)], c = circular autocovariance."""
    n, p = x.size, 1.0 / mean_block
    dev = x - x.mean()
    c = np.array([np.mean(dev * np.roll(dev, -h)) for h in range(n)])
    h = np.arange(1, n)
    return float(np.sqrt((c[0] + 2.0 * np.sum((1 - h / n) * (1 - p) ** h * c[1:])) / n))


long_run_sd = 1.0 / (1.0 - phi) / np.sqrt(n_obs)  # sd of the sample mean implied by the long-run variance
monte_carlo_sd = np.std(ar1(4000, n_obs).mean(axis=1), ddof=1)
series = ar1(n_series, n_obs)

print("method  sd_of_sample_mean")
print(f"long-run_theory {long_run_sd:.4f}")
print(f"monte_carlo {monte_carlo_sd:.4f}")
iid = np.mean([np.std(s[rng.integers(0, n_obs, size=(n_boot, n_obs))].mean(axis=1), ddof=1) for s in series])
print(f"iid_bootstrap {iid:.4f}")
for block in (5.0, 20.0, 50.0):
    sb = np.mean([np.std(s[stationary_indices(n_obs, block, n_boot)].mean(axis=1), ddof=1) for s in series])
    print(f"stationary_bootstrap_block_{block:.0f} {sb:.4f}")

first = series[0]
draws = first[stationary_indices(n_obs, 20.0, 4000)].mean(axis=1)
print(f"one_series_block_20_formula {exact_bootstrap_sd(first, 20.0):.4f}")
print(f"one_series_block_20_resampled {np.std(draws, ddof=1):.4f}")

long-run_theory 0.0894

iid_bootstrap 0.0520
stationary_bootstrap_block_5 0.0800

The iid bootstrap misses about 42% of the standard error, close to the factor \(\sqrt3\) the algebra predicts. The stationary bootstrap recovers most of it. At this sample size no block length recovers all of it: the estimate rises from block length 5 to 20 and falls back at 50, as the weights in (2) trade bias against the collapse toward a single rotated block. Formula (2) matches 4,000 resamples of the same series to within their Monte Carlo error. The lab shows one dependence structure and one sample size. It does not show which block length is best in general, a separate estimation problem (Problem R2).

Remark 3.5 (why permutation tests fail for autocorrelated differentials)

A tempting exact test of \(H_0\colon \mu = 0\) for one challenger swaps the two forecasts' losses at random within each period, which flips the sign of \(d_t\), and compares \(|\sum_t d_t|\) with its distribution over random sign patterns. The test is exact when the \(d_t\) are independent and each is symmetric about zero, because every sign pattern is then equally likely under \(H_0\). Serial correlation breaks that symmetry. With independent random signs \(\varepsilon_t\), the series \((\varepsilon_t d_t)\) has the same marginal distributions as \((d_t)\) but zero autocovariances. Conditional on the data, \(T^{-1/2}\sum_t \varepsilon_t d_t\) has variance \(T^{-1}\sum_t d_t^2\), which tends to \(\gamma(0)\) under \(H_0\): the iid variance, not \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}}\). For an AR(1) differential with \(\phi \gt 0\) the reference distribution is therefore too narrow by the factor \(\sqrt{(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)}\) of this section, and a nominal two-sided 5% test rejects a true null with probability \(2\big(1 - \Phi(1.96/\sqrt{3})\big) = 2(1 - \Phi(1.132)) = 0.258\) at \(\phi = 0.5\), and \(2(1 - \Phi(1.96/\sqrt{1.857})) = 2(1 - \Phi(1.438)) = 0.150\) at \(\phi = 0.3\). The stationary bootstrap resamples blocks of consecutive periods, so by Theorem 3.3 it keeps the autocovariances with weights \((1-p)^h\), and as the block length grows with \(T\) its variance approaches \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}}\) (Remark 3.4). Lab 1 shows it recovering most of the standard error that the iid resample misses. Problem E4 measures the size of all four tests of equal accuracy in this lesson.

4White's Reality Check

Combine the least favourable null of Section 2 with the stationary bootstrap of Section 3, applied to whole periods so that the dependence across strategies survives resampling.

Definition 4.1 (the Reality Check)

A Reality Check is a test of \(H_0\colon \max_k \mu_k \le 0\) that accounts for the specification search by comparing the statistic \(V_K\) of (1) with an estimate of its null distribution at the least favourable point. White gives the name to any method that delivers a consistent estimate of the p-value of \(H_0\) in the context of a specification search. The bootstrap version used throughout this module is the following.

  1. Compute \(\bar d_k\) for every \(k\) and \(V_K = \max_k \sqrt{T}\,\bar d_k\).
  2. For \(j = 1, \dots, B\), draw one stationary-bootstrap index sequence of length \(T\), resample every strategy's differentials with it, and compute \(V^\ast_{K,j} = \max_k \sqrt{T}\,(\bar d^\ast_{k,j} - \bar d_k)\).
  3. Report \(\hat p = B^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^B \indic\{V^\ast_{K,j} \ge V_K\}\) and reject at level \(\alpha\) when \(\hat p \le \alpha\).

Step 2 subtracts \(\bar d_k\), and by Theorem 3.2(d) that centres every resampled average at zero, which is the least favourable point of Theorem 2.1. This is White's bootstrap statistic, the maximum over models of \(n^{1/2}(\bar f^\ast_k - \bar f_k)\), computed with Politis and Romano's stationary bootstrap.

Lab 2 · A Reality Check on a simulated universe

Among 50 SIMULATED skill-free strategies with pairwise correlation 0.3, the single-test p-value of the best strategy should be small, because it is the minimum of 50 p-values, while the Reality Check p-value should be large. When strategy 0 is given a true mean worth a t-statistic of about 4, the Reality Check should reject.

from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED universe: 50 strategies' daily performance differentials against a benchmark,
# sharing a common component. "null": no strategy beats the benchmark; "alpha": strategy 0 does.
rng = np.random.default_rng(2000)
n_obs, n_strategies, n_boot, mean_block = 1000, 50, 1000, 10.0

    """How often each period appears in each stationary-bootstrap resample (draws x n)."""

    np.add.at(counts, (np.repeat(np.arange(draws), n), idx.ravel()), 1.0)
    return counts

    return 1.0 - NormalDist().cdf(z)

Remark 4.2 (an application to trading rules)

Sullivan, Timmermann and White apply the Reality Check to simple technical trading rules. They take the 26 rules of an earlier study as a starting point, expand them into a full universe of rules, and evaluate that universe on 100 years of daily Dow Jones Industrial Average data to measure the effect of data snooping. The design point generalizes. The universe must contain every rule that could have been chosen, not only the rules that were reported.

Remark 4.3 (a maximum is built for sparse alternatives)

The statistic \(V_K\) of (1) is a maximum. It is built for a sparse alternative, a few strategies that beat the benchmark by a wide margin, and spends no power on many strategies that each beat it slightly. A sum-type statistic, such as the normalized sum \(K^{-1/2}\sum_k \sqrt{T}\,\bar d_k/\omega_k\) or Fisher's combination \(-2\sum_k \ln p_k\) of one-sided p-values, accumulates weak evidence across the universe and is built for the dense alternative. Neither dominates. With \(K = 100\) independent standardized averages at level 0.05, Problem P4 finds that one strategy with standardized mean 4 is detected with probability 0.775 by the maximum and 0.107 by the normalized sum (0.245 by Fisher's combination), while 100 strategies each with standardized mean 0.3 are detected with probability 0.133 by the maximum and 0.912 by the sum (0.859 by Fisher's). Fan, Liao and Yao note that, when a high-dimensional parameter vector is tested, tests based on quadratic forms such as the Wald statistic often have low power against sparse alternatives, in which the null is violated by only a few components, because the errors in estimating many parameters accumulate; more powerful tests for sparse alternatives, such as thresholding and extreme-value tests, require stringent conditions or the bootstrap to derive the null distribution and often suffer size distortions from slow convergence; they add a screening-based power enhancement component to an asymptotically pivotal statistic and apply it to testing factor pricing models. Donoho and Jin show that the higher criticism statistic, which maximizes a standardized comparison of the observed and expected fractions of significant tests over a range of levels, is effective for testing whether many normal means are all zero against the alternative that a small fraction is nonzero, and is in a sense optimally adaptive to the unknown sparsity and size of the nonnull effects. A desk asking whether its best strategy beats the benchmark asks a sparse question, which is why this module uses maxima. A desk asking whether a family of signals carries any edge in aggregate asks a dense one, and for that question the Reality Check is the wrong tool.

5Why the Reality Check works

Theorem 5.1 (asymptotic validity, White 2000)

Suppose the differentials involve no estimated parameters and satisfy White's Assumptions A and B: strict stationarity, α-mixing (dependence that dies out with distance at a polynomial rate) and moment conditions under which \(\sqrt{T}(\bar d - \mu)\) converges in distribution to \(\Normal(0, \Omega)\) with \(\Omega\) positive definite. Suppose also that the stationary bootstrap uses \(p_T \to 0\) with \(T p_T \to \infty\). Then the conditional law of \(\max_k \sqrt{T}(\bar d^\ast_k - \bar d_k)\) converges in probability to the law of \(\max_k Z_k\), \(Z \sim \Normal(0, \Omega)\). Under the alternative, when \(\max_k \mu_k > 0\), \(V_K\) diverges at rate \(\sqrt{T}\).

Proof
  1. CLT for the averages (skipped). Under the mixing and moment conditions, \(\sqrt{T}(\bar d - \mu) \dto \Normal(0, \Omega)\), the multivariate central limit theorem for dependent data that White imports.
  2. Bootstrap CLT (skipped). The conditional law of \(\sqrt{T}(\bar d^\ast - \bar d)\) converges in probability to the same \(\Normal(0, \Omega)\). This is the consistency theorem for the stationary bootstrap of the mean, and the conditions on \(p_T\) are where Remark 3.4's trade-off enters.
  3. Continuous mapping. The map \(z \mapsto \max_k z_k\) is continuous on \(\R^K\), so step 2 carries over to \(V^\ast_K\), and step 1 gives \(V_K(0) \dto \max_k Z_k\) at the least favourable point.
  4. Size. At \(\mu = 0\), the bootstrap quantiles converge to the quantiles of \(\max_k Z_k\) wherever its distribution function is continuous and strictly increasing at the quantile, so the rejection probability tends to \(\alpha\). Elsewhere in the null the limiting version of Theorem 2.1 bounds it by \(\alpha\). The continuity of the limit law is assumed rather than shown here; White treats a singular \(\Omega\) explicitly.
  5. Power. If \(\mu_{k^\ast} > 0\), then \(V_K \ge \sqrt{T}\,\bar d_{k^\ast} = \sqrt{T}\mu_{k^\ast} + O_p(1) \to \infty\), while the bootstrap quantiles stay bounded in probability. The test rejects with probability tending to one.

Two conditions in this theorem rarely appear in backtest reports. The theorem is about the maximum over the whole universe searched. Its approximation also improves only as the block length grows with the sample, so a 250-day sample of highly persistent differentials gives the asymptotics little room.

6Hansen's critique and the SPA test

Theorem 2.1 makes the Reality Check valid by evaluating every strategy at the boundary \(\mu_k = 0\). That protects size, and it has a cost. Hansen shows that the Reality Check is sensitive to the inclusion of poor and irrelevant alternatives. Strategies whose true means are far below zero are still recentred at zero in the bootstrap, so they raise the critical value of the maximum without any chance of being the reason for a rejection. In addition, the non-studentized statistic (1) gives erratic forecasts undue influence on the maximum, which a studentized statistic reduces.

Definition 6.1 (test for superior predictive ability)

Hansen's test for superior predictive ability (SPA) tests the same null as the Reality Check with two modifications: a studentized test statistic that reduces the influence of erratic forecasts, and a sample-dependent null distribution. Studentizing divides each average by its own standard error, so variance alone cannot win the maximum. This module implements the sample-dependent null in one specific way, an SPA-style recentring: when building the null distribution, strategy \(k\) is recentred at its own estimated mean \(\bar d_k\), rather than at zero, if \(\bar d_k\) lies below a threshold \(-A_k\) that shrinks to zero as \(T\) grows, but more slowly than the standard error of \(\bar d_k\). Strategies with means far below zero then drop out of the bootstrap maximum, while borderline ones are still treated as possibly null.

The lab below uses the threshold \(A_k = \hat\omega_k\sqrt{2\ln\ln T / T}\) and recentres strategy \(k\) at \(\bar d_k\) when \(\bar d_k \le -A_k\). Here \(\hat\omega_k\) is a consistent estimator of the long-run standard deviation \(\sqrt{\Omega_{kk}}\), the standard deviation of \(\sqrt T\,\bar d_k\): under serial dependence a HAC estimate, or the stationary-bootstrap standard deviation of \(\sqrt T\,\bar d^\ast_k\) (Theorem 3.3); for serially independent differentials, as in Lab 3, the sample standard deviation. The following sketch shows why recentring does not cost asymptotic validity. It skips two steps: that the bootstrap law of the studentized, recentred maximum converges to the law of \(\max_{k : \mu_k = 0} Z_k/\sqrt{\Omega_{kk}}\) (steps 2–3 of the proof of Theorem 5.1, with a consistent \(\hat\omega_k\)), and that this is also the limit law of the observed statistic at that \(\mu\). The guarantee is pointwise in \(\mu\), not uniform. If the strategy's true mean is zero, \(\bar d_k\) has standard deviation about \(\hat\omega_k/\sqrt T\), and \(A_k\) exceeds that by the factor \(\sqrt{2\ln\ln T}\), which grows without bound, so the probability of wrongly recentring a null strategy tends to zero. If its true mean is negative, \(\sqrt T\,\bar d_k/\hat\omega_k\) diverges to \(-\infty\) faster than \(\sqrt{2\ln\ln T}\), so it is recentred with probability tending to one and its bootstrap statistics drop out of the maximum. This choice vanishes as \(T\) grows but more slowly than \(\hat\omega_k/\sqrt T\), which is the property the argument needs. In finite samples recentring does cost something. At \(T = 1000\) a true-null strategy is recentred with probability about \(\Phi(-\sqrt{2\ln\ln 1000}) = \Phi(-1.966) = 0.025\), and in Lab 3's base universe one of the nine null strategies is recentred (strategy 2, with \(t = -2.65\); the lab prints it). Together with studentization, that is why the SPA-style p-value with no poor strategies (0.040) is below the Reality Check's (0.050). We call the result SPA-style because it does not claim to reproduce the details of Hansen's construction.

7Where the canon breaks

  • The universe is never complete. Theorem 5.1 concerns the maximum over everything searched. White notes that accounting for the data-snooping efforts of others requires their matrix of performance differentials as well, and that matrix almost never exists. A Reality Check on the strategies a team happens to report is a check on that subset only.
  • One answer, not a list. Rejecting \(H_0\) establishes that some strategy beats the benchmark. It does not say which strategies, or how many, while keeping the family-wise error rate. Lesson 5's step-down procedure does.
  • Tuning that matters. The block length is a free parameter. Remark 3.4 shows that both extremes fail, and the asymptotic guarantee says nothing about the right choice at a given \(T\). Politis and White propose automatic, data-driven block-length selection for the dependent bootstrap, and Patton, Politis and White later published a correction to that paper. Use an implementation that incorporates the correction, and report the block length next to the p-value, since the p-value depends on it.
  • Recentring is one answer to the power problem, not the last. Hsu, Hsu and Kuan build a stepwise test of predictive ability without data-snooping bias and apply it to technical analysis; stepwise is meant in the sense of Lesson 5, which names the strategies that beat the benchmark. Song generalizes Hansen's observation that tests built on a one-sided sup functional suffer from asymptotic bias, and robustifies their power by coupling the one-sided sup test with a complementary test. Kim shows that this hybrid test may not control size: in a simple example it rejects more than 11% of the time at a nominal 5% level, and under reasonable conditions it is pointwise asymptotically invalid. Bouamara, Laurent and Shi propose a stepwise Cauchy combination test for dependent statistics and sparse signals that pinpoints which p-values drive rejections while maintaining strong family-wise error control, and report that it is less conservative under dependence and more powerful than conventional multiple-testing corrections, including in tests for nonzero alphas.
  • Averages, not Sharpe ratios. The theory above is stated for means of differentials. White extends it to smooth functions of vectors of averages, naming the prediction-sample Sharpe ratio for evaluating investment strategies as an example. That extension relies on a delta-method argument and more conditions, which Problem R1 asks you to work through.

8Misconceptions

  1. “A small Reality Check p-value certifies the strategy I selected.”

    The test rejects the global null that no strategy beats the benchmark. It licenses the statement that at least one does. White shows that, when one model is strictly best, it has the best estimated performance with probability tending to one. In a finite sample, though, the selected strategy and the one driving the rejection can differ, and nothing in the p-value measures how much better the selection is. Strategy-level decisions need the step-down procedure of Lesson 5.

  2. “The iid bootstrap is fine for strategy returns because daily returns are nearly uncorrelated.”

    Differentials measured over overlapping horizons, or computed from stale or smoothed prices, are serially correlated, and Lab 1 shows an iid bootstrap missing 42% of the standard error at an autocorrelation of 0.5. Volatility clustering without autocorrelation leaves the variance of the mean at \(\gamma(0)/T\), so the danger is autocorrelation specifically. Resampling whole periods, as in Definition 3.1, keeps the cross-strategy dependence whatever block length is used.

  3. “Putting every strategy I tried into the Reality Check is free: it can only make the test more honest.”

    It is more honest, and with the Reality Check it is not free. Lab 3 shows strategies that could never win raising the p-value of a real edge from 0.05 to 0.37. The fix is the SPA's recentring, which keeps the full universe and still restores power. Trimming the universe is not a fix.

  4. “Longer bootstrap blocks are always the conservative choice.”

    As \(p \to 0\) the resample becomes a single block, a rotation of the data, and the bootstrap variance of the mean tends to zero (Remark 3.4); once the blocks are long enough to capture the dependence, it usually falls as they lengthen further. Long before that limit, at \(b = T\), the default sample in the figure after Remark 3.4 recovers less than half of the true standard error. Over-long blocks therefore make the test anti-conservative. Lab 1 shows the estimate already falling between block lengths 20 and 50 at \(T = 500\).

  5. “A permutation test is exact, so it is the safe way to compare two strategies' returns.”

    Its exactness rests on exchangeability: every swap of the two strategies' returns within a period must be equally likely under the null, which holds when the differentials are independent and symmetric. Autocorrelated differentials break it. The swaps destroy the autocovariances, so the reference distribution has the iid variance \(\gamma(0)/T\) instead of the long-run variance (Remark 3.5). In Problem E4 the sign-flip test rejects a true null 26.1% of the time at a nominal 5% when \(\phi = 0.5\), as often as the naive t-test.

Mechanics

M1 · Reading off a p-value

A Reality Check uses \(B = 1000\) resamples. The observed statistic is \(V_K = 3.1\), and 37 of the bootstrap statistics \(V^\ast_{K,j}\) are at least 3.1. (a) Compute \(\hat p\) as in Definition 4.1. (b) Many authors use \((1 + \#\{V^\ast \ge V\})/(B+1)\) instead. Compute it, and explain when the difference matters. (c) Decide at the 5% level.

Solution

(a) \(\hat p = 37/1000 = 0.037\).

(b) \((1 + 37)/(1000 + 1) = 38/1001 = 0.038\). The corrected version counts the observed statistic as one of the draws, so it can never return zero. It is exactly valid for a Monte Carlo test when the draws are exchangeable with the statistic under the null. The difference is \(O(1/B)\), so it matters only when \(B\) is small or the p-value is near zero, where reporting \(\hat p = 0\) would overstate the evidence.

(c) Both are below 0.05, so the null that no strategy beats the benchmark is rejected at 5%.

M2 · Blocks by the numbers

A stationary bootstrap uses mean block length \(b = 10\) on \(n = 500\) observations. (a) What is \(p\)? (b) What is the expected number of block starts in one resample? (c) What is the probability that a block lasts at least 20 periods (ignoring truncation at the end)? (d) What is the probability that \(I_{t+1} = (I_t + 1) \bmod n\)?

Solution

(a) \(p = 1/b = 0.1\).

(b) The first index always starts a block, and each of the remaining 499 starts one with probability 0.1: \(1 + 499 \times 0.1 = 50.9\).

(c) A block lasts at least 20 periods when the 19 following steps all continue it: \((1-p)^{19} = 0.9^{19} = 0.1351\).

(d) Either no new block starts (probability 0.9), or a new block starts and the fresh draw happens to land on the next index (probability \(0.1 \times 1/500\)). The total is \(0.9 + 0.0002 = 0.9002\). Counting "breaks" in a resample therefore estimates \(p(1 - 1/n)\), not \(p\).

M3 · How wrong is the iid bootstrap?

For \(x_t = \phi x_{t-1} + e_t\), compute the factor by which the iid bootstrap understates the standard error of the mean, for \(\phi \in \{0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.6\}\).

Solution

The variance ratio is \((1+\phi)/(1-\phi)\) (Section 3), so the standard-error factor is its square root. At \(\phi = 0\) the factor is 1. At 0.3 it is \(\sqrt{1.3/0.7} = \sqrt{1.857} = 1.363\). At 0.5 it is \(\sqrt{3} = 1.732\). At 0.6 it is \(\sqrt{4} = 2\). Any t-statistic built on the iid standard error is inflated by the same factor, so a t-statistic of 2 at \(\phi = 0.6\) is worth 1 after correction.

M4 · Diebold–Mariano by hand

Over \(T = 250\) days a challenger's loss differential against the benchmark has \(\bar d = 0.15\), \(\hat\gamma(0) = 1.0\), \(\hat\gamma(1) = 0.40\) and \(\hat\gamma(2) = 0.15\), with negligible autocovariance beyond lag 2. (c) What does the comparison say, and what would flat weights have given?

(b) The Bartlett weights \(1 - j/3\) are \(2/3\) and \(1/3\), so \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} = 1 + 2(\tfrac23 \times 0.40 + \tfrac13 \times 0.15) = 1 + 2(0.26667 + 0.05) = 1.6333\). Proof

P1 · The Reality Check beats Bonferroni under independence

Suppose the \(K\) standardized averages are independent standard normals at the least favourable point. Show that the exact level-\(\alpha\) critical value for their maximum is \(c_K = \Phi^{-1}\big((1-\alpha)^{1/K}\big)\). Show that \(c_K \le \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/K)\), the Bonferroni critical value of Lesson 2, and evaluate both for \(K = 50\), \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Solution

By independence, \(\Prob(\max_k Z_k \le c) = \Phi(c)^K\). Setting this equal to \(1 - \alpha\) gives \(\Phi(c_K) = (1-\alpha)^{1/K}\). Bernoulli's inequality gives \((1 - \alpha/K)^K \ge 1 - \alpha\) for \(0 \le \alpha \le 1\). Taking \(K\)-th roots, \(1 - \alpha/K \ge (1-\alpha)^{1/K}\), and since \(\Phi^{-1}\) is increasing, \(c_K \le \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/K)\). For \(K = 50\): \(c_{50} = \Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/50}) = 3.0828\) and \(\Phi^{-1}(0.999) = 3.0902\). Under independence the gain is tiny. The Reality Check's real advantage appears under positive dependence, where the maximum of correlated statistics is smaller (L01, Problem P3) and the bootstrap quantile falls with it, while Bonferroni's does not.

P2 · Recentring imposes the null

Show that under the stationary bootstrap \(\E^\ast[\sqrt T(\bar d^\ast_k - \bar d_k)] = 0\) for every \(k\). Then show that if one skipped the recentring and used \(\max_k \sqrt T\,\bar d^\ast_k\) as the null statistic, a strategy with \(\bar d_k > 0\) would push the bootstrap distribution up and make the test reject less often than it should.

Solution

Theorem 3.2(d) applies to each column, since one index sequence resamples all of them: \(\E^\ast[\bar d^\ast_k] = \bar d_k\). Multiplying by \(\sqrt T\) and subtracting gives zero. Without recentring, \(\sqrt T\,\bar d^\ast_k = \sqrt T(\bar d^\ast_k - \bar d_k) + \sqrt T\,\bar d_k\). Each resampled statistic is shifted by \(\sqrt T\,\bar d_k\), which for a genuinely good strategy grows without bound. The bootstrap distribution would then be centred at the observed performance, not at the null. Its upper quantiles would sit near \(V_K\) itself, so \(\hat p\) would hover near one half however strong the evidence. Recentring is what makes the resamples a picture of the null rather than of the data.

P3 · The two limits of formula (2)

Using (2), show that at \(p = 1\) the stationary bootstrap reproduces the iid bootstrap variance \(\hat c(0)/n\). Then show directly, without (2), that when the resample is a single block (\(p = 0\)) its mean equals \(\bar x\) exactly, and deduce that \(\hat c(0) + 2\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}(1 - h/n)\,\hat c(h) = 0\) for every sample.

Solution

At \(p = 1\) every term with \(h \ge 1\) carries the factor \((1-p)^h = 0\), leaving \(\hat c(0)/n\). At \(p = 0\) the resample is \(x_{I_1}, x_{I_1+1}, \dots\) taken mod \(n\) for \(n\) steps, which visits every index exactly once. Its mean is therefore \(\bar x\) whatever \(I_1\) is, so \(\Var^\ast(\bar X^\ast) = 0\). Substituting \(p = 0\) into (2), which holds for every \(p\) including 0 because its proof never used \(p > 0\), gives \(\frac1n[\hat c(0) + 2\sum_{h}(1 - h/n)\hat c(h)] = 0\). The identity is the circular-autocovariance version of the fact that the deviations from the mean sum to zero.

P4 · Maxima against sums, sparse against dense

Let \(Z_k = \theta_k + \varepsilon_k\), \(k = 1, \dots, K\), with independent standard normal \(\varepsilon_k\), and the global null \(\theta = 0\). The max test rejects when \(\max_k Z_k \gt c_K = \Phi^{-1}\big((1-\alpha)^{1/K}\big)\) (Problem P1); the sum test rejects when \(S = K^{-1/2}\sum_k Z_k \gt z_{1-\alpha} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha)\). (a) Show that both have level \(\alpha\), and give the power of each against any \(\theta\). (b) Evaluate both at \(K = 100\), \(\alpha = 0.05\), against the sparse \(\theta = (4, 0, \dots, 0)\) and the dense \(\theta = (0.3, \dots, 0.3)\). (c) Against \(\theta = (a, 0, \dots, 0)\), show that the sum test reaches power 1/2 only at \(a = \sqrt{K}\,z_{1-\alpha}\), while the max test has power at least 1/2 at \(a = c_K \le \sqrt{2\ln(K/\alpha)}\). (d) Against \(\theta = (b/\sqrt{K}, \dots, b/\sqrt{K})\) with \(b \gt 0\) fixed, show that the sum test's power is \(1 - \Phi(z_{1-\alpha} - b)\) for every \(K\), while the max test's power tends to \(\alpha\).

Solution

(a) Under \(\theta = 0\), independence gives \(\Prob(\max_k Z_k \le c_K) = \Phi(c_K)^K = 1 - \alpha\), and \(S \sim \Normal(0, 1)\). In general \(\Prob(\max_k Z_k \le c_K) = \prod_k \Phi(c_K - \theta_k)\), so the max test has power \(1 - \prod_k \Phi(c_K - \theta_k)\). \(S \sim \Normal(K^{-1/2}\sum_k \theta_k, 1)\), so the sum test has power \(1 - \Phi(z_{1-\alpha} - K^{-1/2}\sum_k \theta_k)\).

P5 · Why Bartlett weights

Show that \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}}\) of Definition 1.2 is non-negative for every sample and every bandwidth \(\ell\). Then show that the flat-weight estimate \(\hat\gamma(0) + 2\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\hat\gamma(j)\) can be negative, using \(\hat\gamma(0) = 1\), \(\hat\gamma(1) = -0.6\) and \(\ell = 1\).

Solution

Let \(u_t = d_t - \bar d\) for \(1 \le t \le T\) and \(u_t = 0\) for every other integer \(t\). Then \(\hat\gamma(j) = T^{-1}\sum_t u_t u_{t+j}\) for \(j \ge 0\), with the sum over all integers. Define the moving sums \(S_s = \sum_{i=0}^{\ell} u_{s+i}\). Expanding the squares, \[ \sum_s S_s^2 = \sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\sum_{i'=0}^{\ell} \sum_s u_{s+i}\,u_{s+i'} = \sum_{h=-\ell}^{\ell} (\ell + 1 - |h|)\; T\,\hat\gamma(|h|), \] because for each lag \(h = i' - i\) there are \(\ell + 1 - |h|\) pairs \((i, i')\), and each inner sum is \(T\hat\gamma(|h|)\). Dividing by \(T(\ell + 1)\), \[ \hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} = \sum_{h=-\ell}^{\ell}\Big(1 - \frac{|h|}{\ell+1}\Big)\hat\gamma(|h|) = \frac{1}{T(\ell+1)}\sum_s S_s^2 \ge 0. \] With flat weights the example gives \(1 + 2(-0.6) = -0.2\), a negative variance, while the Bartlett estimate is \(1 + 2 \times \tfrac12 \times (-0.6) = 0.4\). Any sample whose lag-1 autocorrelation is below \(-0.5\) makes the flat estimate negative, for example a long AR(1) path with coefficient \(-0.6\), while at \(\ell = 1\) the identity guarantees \(\hat\gamma(0) + \hat\gamma(1) \ge 0\) for the Bartlett estimate. This is the one-series case of the positive semi-definiteness by construction that Newey and West establish for their covariance matrix.

E3 · The variance trap

To Lab 3's base universe (no poor strategies), add one strategy with mean zero and standard deviation 5. Predict the effect on the Reality Check and on the SPA-style p-value, then verify.

Solution

The new strategy's centred bootstrap statistic \(\sqrt T(\bar d^\ast - \bar d)\) has standard deviation about 5, against 1 for every other strategy. E4 · Four tests of equal accuracy under autocorrelation

Solution

At \(\phi = 0\) every test should reject about 5% of the time. At \(\phi = 0.5\), tests (i) and (ii) use a variance near \(\gamma(0)\), so Remark 3.5 predicts 0.258. Test (iii) keeps the first seven autocovariances with Bartlett weights. Replacing each sample autocovariance by \(\gamma(j)\) gives \(\E[\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}}] \approx \gamma(0)\big[1 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{7}(1 - j/8)\,0.5^j\big] = 2.502\,\gamma(0)\), against \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} = 3\gamma(0)\), which predicts \(2\big(1 - \Phi(1.96\sqrt{2.502/3})\big) = 0.073\). For the circular autocovariances of Theorem 3.3, expanding the product and using \(\sum_i d_i = T\bar d\) gives \(\hat c(h) = T^{-1}\sum_i d_i\, d_{(i+h) \bmod T} - \bar d^2\). Under \(H_0\), \(T - h\) of the products have mean \(\gamma(h)\) and the \(h\) that wrap around have mean \(\gamma(T - h)\), so \[ \E[\hat c(h)] = \Big(1 - \frac hT\Big)\gamma(h) + \frac hT\,\gamma(T - h) - \Var(\bar d). \] Every lag loses \(\Var(\bar d)\), so a variance estimate loses \(\Var(\bar d)\) times the sum of its weights; the Bartlett autocovariances lose approximately the same per lag. The script computes every mean exactly as well, as a quadratic form in the demeaned series. The simulation, with 2,000 series per row:

import math
from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED null loss differentials: AR(1) with unit-variance shocks and mean zero. Four two-sided 5% tests of
# equal predictive accuracy, each on the same series.
rng = np.random.default_rng(4041)
T, n_reps, n_flips, lag, mean_block, z_crit = 500, 2000, 499, 7, 20.0, 1.959964
Vec = NDArray[np.float64]


def ar1(phi: float) -> Vec:
    e = rng.standard_normal(T + 200)
    x = np.empty_like(e)
    x[0] = e[0]
    for t in range(1, e.size):
        x[t] = phi * x[t - 1] + e[t]
    return x[200:]  # drop the burn-in so the path starts near stationarity


def newey_west(d: Vec) -> float:
    """Bartlett-weighted long-run variance: gamma(0) + 2 sum_j (1 - j/(lag + 1)) gamma(j), divisor T."""
    u = d - d.mean()
    gamma = [float(u[j:] @ u[: T - j]) / T for j in range(lag + 1)]
    return gamma[0] + 2.0 * sum((1.0 - j / (lag + 1)) * gamma[j] for j in range(1, lag + 1))


def stationary_bootstrap_var(d: Vec) -> float:
    """Theorem 3.3: the exact stationary-bootstrap variance of the mean, from circular autocovariances."""
    u = d - d.mean()
    c = np.fft.irfft(np.abs(np.fft.rfft(u)) ** 2, n=T) / T  # c[h] = (1/T) sum_i u_i u_{(i+h) mod T}
    h = np.arange(1, T)
    return float(c[0] + 2.0 * np.sum((1.0 - h / T) * (1.0 - 1.0 / mean_block) ** h * c[1:])) / T


def means(phi: float) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Means, in units of gamma(0), of T Var(mean) and of both long-run variance estimates.

    Each estimate is a quadratic form u'Ku / T in the demeaned series u = Mx, so its exact mean is
    sum_ij K_ij (M R M)_ij / T, with R the AR(1) autocorrelation matrix and M = I - 11'/T. The
    "plain" means are the page's first approximations: every sample autocovariance replaced by gamma(h).
    """
    gap = np.abs(np.subtract.outer(np.arange(T), np.arange(T)))
    r = phi**gap
    m = np.eye(T) - 1.0 / T
    mrm = m @ r @ m
    k_nw = np.where(gap <= lag, 1.0 - gap / (lag + 1), 0.0)  # Bartlett weights on both sides of the diagonal
    lead = np.subtract.outer(np.arange(T), np.arange(T)) % T  # circular lag (j - i) mod T, as k and T - k
    h = np.arange(1, T)
    w = np.concatenate([[0.5], (1.0 - h / T) * (1.0 - 1.0 / mean_block) ** h])
    k_sb = w[lead] + w[(T - lead) % T]  # 1 on the diagonal, w_k + w_{T-k} off it
    j = np.arange(1, lag + 1)
    return {
        "target": float(r.sum()) / T,
        "nw weights": float(k_nw[0].sum() + k_nw[0, 1:].sum()),
        "sb weights": float(k_sb[0].sum()),
        "nw plain": 1.0 + 2.0 * float(np.sum((1.0 - j / (lag + 1)) * phi**j)),
        "sb plain": 1.0 + 2.0 * float(np.sum(w[1:] * phi**h)),
        "nw exact": float(np.sum(k_nw * mrm)) / T,
        "sb exact": float(np.sum(k_sb * mrm)) / T,
    }

    return 2.0 * (1.0 - NormalDist().cdf(z_crit * math.sqrt(mean_ratio)))

R1 · A Reality Check on Sharpe ratios

Your desk ranks strategies by Sharpe ratio, not by mean differential. Design a Reality Check for \(H_0\colon \max_k (\SR_k - \SR_0) \le 0\), state what must be resampled, and identify the assumptions beyond those of Theorem 5.1.

Solution

Each Sharpe ratio is a smooth function \(g(m_1, m_2) = m_1/\sqrt{m_2 - m_1^2}\) of two averages, \(m_1\) of returns and \(m_2\) of squared returns. White's extension to functions of averages applies.

  1. The statistic is \(V = \max_k \sqrt T(\hSR_k - \hSR_0)\).
  2. Each resample uses one stationary-bootstrap index sequence for the benchmark and all strategies together, and recomputes every Sharpe ratio from the resampled returns.
  3. Recentre at the sample values: \(V^\ast = \max_k \sqrt T[(\hSR^\ast_k - \hSR^\ast_0) - (\hSR_k - \hSR_0)]\).

The extra assumptions are four. The map \(g\) must be differentiable at the true moments with a non-degenerate gradient, which requires positive variance. Fourth moments must exist and be finite, since the delta-method variance involves them, which is exactly the skewness and kurtosis adjustment of Lesson 7. The CLT must hold jointly for \((m_1, m_2)\) under the mixing conditions. And \(T\) must be large enough that the delta-method linearization is accurate. With fat-tailed returns this last condition is demanding, which is a reason to check the bootstrap distribution's shape rather than trust it.

R2 · Choosing the block length

Propose a procedure for choosing the mean block length of a Reality Check on daily strategy differentials, and a sensitivity analysis to report with it.

Solution

Start from Theorem 3.3. The block length controls how much autocovariance formula (2) keeps.

  1. Estimate the dependence. Compute the autocorrelations of the differentials up to a generous lag. Take the lag beyond which they are indistinguishable from zero as a rough memory \(m\).
  2. Choose \(b\). Pick \(b\) as a few multiples of \(m\), subject to \(b \ll T\). The geometric weights \((1-1/b)^h\) are near one for \(h \ll b\), so lags up to \(m\) keep most of their weight. For \(b\) far below \(T\) the rotation collapse is negligible.
  3. Fix the rule before testing. If you use a published data-driven rule for the block length, name it and apply it before looking at any p-value, so that the choice cannot be tuned to the result.
  4. Report a sensitivity table. Give the p-value for \(b \in \{m, 2m, 4m, 8m\}\), with the bootstrap standard deviation of the mean for the best strategy in each case.

A conclusion that flips within that range is not robust. Report it as such rather than choosing the block length that rejects, since choosing it after seeing the p-values is itself data snooping.

R3 · Reconstructing a universe

You inherit a strategy from a team that kept no trial log. Its documentation mentions lookbacks of 20 to 250 days and three rebalancing frequencies. How do you build a defensible Reality Check universe, and what can you not recover?

Solution

Rebuild the search space, not the reported subset.

  1. Enumerate the plausible grid implied by the documentation: every lookback on the grid a researcher would have used (for example, steps of 10 days from 20 to 250) times the three frequencies. Generate the differentials of all of them on the same sample.
  2. Run the SPA-style test, not the plain Reality Check, since a reconstructed grid contains many poor variants (Lab 3).
  3. Report the result with two universes, the narrow documented grid and a wider grid that includes neighbouring design choices (signal transforms, universes, stop rules). If the conclusion survives only the narrow grid, say so.

What cannot be recovered is the other ideas the team abandoned without documenting them, and the choice of sample period if it was itself selected. The Reality Check then answers a narrower question than the one you care about, and the trial log of Lesson 1, Problem R1, is the only remedy going forward.

Answer from memory, then check. Finishing the quiz adds this lesson's cards to your review deck.

  1. What null hypothesis does the Reality Check test?

    Options

    The null is the composite intersection of the one-sided hypotheses \(\mu_k \le 0\). The point \(\mu = 0\) is only its least favourable element.

  2. Why are the bootstrap statistics computed as \(\sqrt T(\bar d^\ast_k - \bar d_k)\) rather than \(\sqrt T\,\bar d^\ast_k\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 3.2(d), \(\E^\ast[\bar d^\ast_k] = \bar d_k\), so subtracting \(\bar d_k\) puts every resampled average at mean zero (Problem P2).

  3. By what factor does the iid bootstrap understate the standard error of the mean of an AR(1) series with \(\phi = 0.5\)? (three decimals)

    \(\sqrt{(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)} = \sqrt{3} = 1.732\).

  4. A stationary bootstrap starts a new block with probability \(p = 0.1\) at each step. What is the mean block length?

    Block lengths are geometric with mean \(1/p = 10\) (Theorem 3.2(b)).

  5. What does the stationary bootstrap's variance of the mean do as the mean block length grows without bound (\(p \to 0\))?

    Options

    One block visits every observation once, so the resampled mean equals \(\bar x\) exactly (Problem P3).

  6. When resampling \(K\) strategies' differentials, which of the following is required?

    Options

    Shared indices keep the cross-strategy dependence that makes the Reality Check sharper than Bonferroni.

  7. Adding strategies with very negative mean differentials to the universe…

    Options

    The Reality Check recentres them at zero, so they inflate the bootstrap maximum. The SPA recentres them at their own negative means (Lab 3).

  8. What does a rejection by the Reality Check establish?

    Options

    It is a test of the global null. Strategy-level decisions with FWER control come from the step-down procedure of Lesson 5.

  9. With mean block length 10, what is the probability that a block lasts at least 20 periods? (three decimals)

    \((1 - 0.1)^{19} = 0.135\) (Problem M2).

  10. Which two changes does Hansen's SPA make to the Reality Check?

    Options

    Studentization stops variance from winning the maximum. The sample-dependent null, which this module implements as SPA-style recentring (Definition 6.1), stops poor alternatives from inflating the null distribution.

  11. Over \(T = 250\) periods a loss differential has \(\bar d = 0.15\) and long-run variance estimate \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} = 1.6333\). What is the Diebold–Mariano statistic? (three decimals)

    \(0.15/\sqrt{1.6333/250} = 0.15/0.080829 = 1.856\) (Problem M4). The naive statistic with \(\hat\gamma(0) = 1\) would be 2.372.

  12. Why can the Diebold–Mariano normal limit fail when forecast 1 comes from a model that nests forecast 0's model?

    Options

    Remark 1.4: Proposition 1.3 needs \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{LR}} \gt 0\). With nested models and zero extra coefficients the sample differential is pure estimation error.

  13. A sign-flip test of equal accuracy is run at a nominal 5% on an AR(1) loss differential with \(\phi = 0.5\). It…

    Options

    Remark 3.5: random signs keep the marginal distributions and remove the autocovariances, so the reference variance is \(\gamma(0)\). The rejection rate is \(2(1 - \Phi(1.96/\sqrt3)) = 0.258\); Problem E4 measures 0.261.

  14. A hundred strategies each beat the benchmark by a small, equal margin. Which global statistic is more likely to detect it?

    Options

    Problem P4: with standardized means of 0.3 the sum detects the alternative with probability 0.912 and Fisher's combination with 0.859, against 0.133 for the maximum. One strong strategy reverses the order.
