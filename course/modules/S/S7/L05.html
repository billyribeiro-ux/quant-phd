The Reality Check of Lesson 4 answers one question: does the best of \(K\) strategies beat the benchmark? A research desk needs a different answer: which of the \(K\) strategies beat it, with a guarantee that the probability of even one false claim stays below \(\alpha\). Applying the single-step critical value to every strategy is valid but wasteful, because it keeps charging each strategy for a search over hypotheses that are already rejected. The Romano–Wolf step-down recomputes the critical value over the strategies still in doubt. It adapts to their dependence through a joint bootstrap, and it never rejects less than the single-step test. It is the procedure you will build into Atlas.

1One critical value is one too many

Strategy \(k \in \{1, \dots, K\}\) produces per-period performance differentials \(d_{k,1}, \dots, d_{k,T}\) against a benchmark, as in Lesson 4. Write \(\mu_k = \E[d_{k,t}]\) and test the \(K\) one-sided hypotheses \[ H_k : \mu_k \le 0, \qquad k = 1, \dots, K, \] with the studentized t-statistics \[ t_k = \frac{\bar d_k}{s_k/\sqrt{T}}, \qquad \bar d_k = \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T d_{k,t}, \qquad s_k^2 = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^T (d_{k,t}-\bar d_k)^2 . \] The target is the family-wise error rate: the probability of rejecting at least one true \(H_k\).

A single-step procedure compares every \(t_k\) with one critical value, the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of \(\max_{k} t_k\) under the null. It controls the family-wise error rate, but the price is high. Suppose the \(K = 20\) statistics are independent standard normals under the null. The quantile of their maximum solves \(\Phi(c)^{20} = 0.95\), so \(c = \Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/20}) = 2.799\). Once the five strongest strategies have been rejected, only fifteen hypotheses remain in doubt. The quantile of a maximum of fifteen is \(\Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/15}) = 2.705\), and with ten remaining it is \(\Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/10}) = 2.568\). A single-step test keeps demanding 2.799 from every survivor, as if the five rejected strategies could still produce the false rejection. The step-down idea is to stop paying for them.

Definition 1.1 (joint studentized bootstrap)

Draw \(B\) stationary-bootstrap index vectors \(\iota_b = (\iota_{b,1}, \dots, \iota_{b,T})\), one per draw \(b\), each shared by all \(K\) strategies: draw \(b\) uses the rows \(d_{\cdot,\iota_{b,1}}, \dots, d_{\cdot,\iota_{b,T}}\) of the \(T \times K\) matrix. From the resampled matrix compute \(\bar d^{\ast}_{b,k}\) and \(s^{\ast}_{b,k}\), and the bootstrap statistics centred at the sample mean, \[ t^{\ast}_{b,k} = \frac{\bar d^{\ast}_{b,k} - \bar d_k}{s^{\ast}_{b,k}/\sqrt{T}} . \] For a set \(A\) of strategies and a level \(\alpha\), let \(c_\alpha(A)\) be the \(\lceil (1-\alpha)B \rceil\)-th smallest of the \(B\) maxima \(\max_{k \in A} t^{\ast}_{b,k}\), with \(c_\alpha(\varnothing) = -\infty\).

Centring at \(\bar d_k\) makes the bootstrap world one in which every hypothesis is exactly at its boundary \(\mu_k = 0\). The bootstrap therefore mimics the sampling distribution of the centred statistics \(\tau_k = (\bar d_k - \mu_k)/(s_k/\sqrt{T})\), whatever the true means are.

Definition 1.2 (StepM)

The Romano–Wolf StepM procedure at level \(\alpha\) runs as follows.

  1. Start with the active set \(A_1 = \{1, \dots, K\}\) and no rejections.
  2. At step \(j\), compute \(c_\alpha(A_j)\) and reject every \(k \in A_j\) with \(t_k > c_\alpha(A_j)\) (strict inequality).
  3. Remove the hypotheses just rejected: \(A_{j+1} = A_j \setminus \{k : t_k > c_\alpha(A_j)\}\).
  4. Stop when a step rejects nothing or the active set is empty. The rejected set \(R\) is the union of all steps' rejections.

Step 1 alone is the single-step studentized max-t test, the studentized relative of White's Reality Check.

2Three properties you can prove

Everything this section proves holds for a fixed set of bootstrap draws, in finite samples. It concerns the algorithm, not the asymptotics.

Lemma 2.1 (monotone critical values)

For fixed bootstrap draws: if \(A \subseteq A'\) then \(c_\alpha(A) \le c_\alpha(A')\); and if \(\alpha \lt \alpha'\) then \(c_{\alpha'}(A) \le c_\alpha(A)\).

Proof

If \(A \subseteq A'\), then \(\max_{k \in A} t^{\ast}_{b,k} \le \max_{k \in A'} t^{\ast}_{b,k}\) for every draw \(b\). Order statistics preserve pointwise inequalities: the \(r\)-th smallest of a list that is elementwise no larger is no larger. Taking \(r = \lceil (1-\alpha)B \rceil\) gives the first claim. For the second, \(\alpha \lt \alpha'\) gives \(\lceil (1-\alpha')B \rceil \le \lceil (1-\alpha)B \rceil\). A lower-ranked order statistic of the same list is no larger.

Proposition 2.2 (never worse than single-step)

With the same bootstrap draws, StepM rejects every hypothesis the single-step test rejects, and possibly more.

Proof

The single-step test rejects \(\{k : t_k > c_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\})\}\), which is exactly the rejection set of StepM's first step. Later steps only add rejections.

The extra rejections are not free power bought with a higher error rate. Section 3 shows that the family-wise error rate is controlled by the same argument as for the single-step test. The next lab measures both effects with oracle critical values, drawn from the known null distribution, so that only the step-down logic differs between the two procedures.

Lab 1 · Step-down against single-step

With 20 SIMULATED strategies whose statistics have pairwise correlation 0.3 and eight genuine alphas, StepM should find more of the eight per study than the single-step test, because its critical value falls as true alphas are removed. Both family-wise error rates should stay at or below 5%, up to Monte Carlo error.

import math

import numpy as np
import numpy.typing as npt

# SIMULATED universe of 20 strategies whose t-statistics share a common shock
# (pairwise correlation 0.3). Eight have genuine positive drift. Critical values
# come from the known null distribution (an oracle), so this lab isolates the
# step-down logic; the bootstrap that replaces the oracle comes in Lab 2.
rng = np.random.default_rng(505)
n_strat, rho, alpha, n_draws, n_reps = 20, 0.3, 0.05, 10000, 4000
drift = np.zeros(n_strat)
drift[:8] = [4.5, 4.0, 3.6, 3.3, 3.1, 2.9, 2.8, 2.7]  # expected t-statistics of the eight true alphas
true_alpha = drift > 0

null_draws = math.sqrt(rho) * rng.standard_normal((n_draws, 1)) + math.sqrt(1 - rho) * rng.standard_normal(
    (n_draws, n_strat)
)
rank = math.ceil((1 - alpha) * n_draws)


cache: dict[bytes, float] = {}


def critical(active: npt.NDArray[np.bool_]) -> float:
    """The ceil((1 - alpha) B)-th smallest maximum of the null draws over the active strategies."""
    key = active.tobytes()
    if key not in cache:
        cache[key] = float(np.sort(null_draws[:, active].max(axis=1))[rank - 1])
    return cache[key]


c_all = critical(np.ones(n_strat, dtype=bool))
found_single = found_step = 0
fwer_single = fwer_step = 0
for _ in range(n_reps):
    t = drift + math.sqrt(rho) * rng.standard_normal() + math.sqrt(1 - rho) * rng.standard_normal(n_strat)
    single = t > c_all
    rejected = np.zeros(n_strat, dtype=bool)
    while not rejected.all():
        active = ~rejected
        new = active & (t > critical(active))
        if not new.any():
            break
        rejected |= new
    found_single += int(single[true_alpha].sum())
    found_step += int(rejected[true_alpha].sum())
    fwer_single += int(single[~true_alpha].any())
    fwer_step += int(rejected[~true_alpha].any())

print(f"first-step critical value (all 20 active): {c_all:.3f}")
print(f"true alphas found per study, single-step: {found_single / n_reps:.3f}")
print(f"true alphas found per study, step-down: {found_step / n_reps:.3f}")
print(f"FWER, single-step: {fwer_single / n_reps:.3f}")
print(f"FWER, step-down: {fwer_step / n_reps:.3f}")
first-step critical value (all 20 active): 2.743
true alphas found per study, single-step: 5.596
true alphas found per study, step-down: 5.798
FWER, single-step: 0.031
FWER, step-down: 0.045

StepM finds about 0.2 more true alphas per study, out of eight, and its family-wise error rate rises toward the nominal 5% without crossing it. The single-step test's 3.1% is the cost of its fixed critical value: the true alphas it already caught keep inflating the bar for everyone else. The Monte Carlo standard error of each rate is about 0.003 with 4,000 replications. The size of the gain depends on how many true alphas there are and how strong they are. With none, the two procedures coincide (Proposition 3.2).

Theorem 2.3 (the output is the smallest closed set, and it is nested in α)

Fix the bootstrap draws. Call a set \(R \subseteq \{1, \dots, K\}\) closed at level \(\alpha\) if \(t_k \le c_\alpha(R^{c})\) for every \(k \notin R\), where \(R^c\) is the complement. Then the StepM output \(R_\alpha\) is closed at level \(\alpha\), and \(R_\alpha \subseteq S\) for every set \(S\) closed at level \(\alpha\). Consequently \(R_\alpha \subseteq R_{\alpha'}\) whenever \(\alpha \lt \alpha'\).

Proof

Closed. StepM stops either because the active set is empty (so \(R_\alpha\) is everything and the condition is vacuous), or because step \(j\) rejects nothing, which says exactly that \(t_k \le c_\alpha(A_j)\) for every \(k \in A_j = R_\alpha^c\).

Smallest. Let \(S\) be closed at level \(\alpha\), and let \(R_j\) be the set rejected in steps \(1, \dots, j\), with \(R_0 = \varnothing\). We show \(R_j \subseteq S\) by induction. The case \(j = 0\) is trivial. Suppose \(R_{j-1} \subseteq S\) and that step \(j\) rejects some \(k \notin S\). Then \(t_k > c_\alpha(A_j)\) with \(A_j = R_{j-1}^c\). Since \(R_{j-1} \subseteq S\), we have \(S^c \subseteq A_j\), and Lemma 2.1 gives \(c_\alpha(S^c) \le c_\alpha(A_j) \lt t_k\). That contradicts closedness of \(S\), because \(k \notin S\). Hence \(R_j \subseteq S\) for every \(j\), and \(R_\alpha \subseteq S\).

Nested. Let \(\alpha \lt \alpha'\). The set \(R_{\alpha'}\) is closed at level \(\alpha'\): \(t_k \le c_{\alpha'}(R_{\alpha'}^c)\) for \(k \notin R_{\alpha'}\). By Lemma 2.1, \(c_{\alpha'}(R_{\alpha'}^c) \le c_\alpha(R_{\alpha'}^c)\), so \(R_{\alpha'}\) is also closed at level \(\alpha\), and minimality gives \(R_\alpha \subseteq R_{\alpha'}\).

Remark 2.4 (why the characterization matters)

Theorem 2.3 describes the output without reference to the order of the steps. Rejecting all exceedances at once, or only the largest one per step, leads to the same set (Problem P1). An implementation that differs from the definition only in bookkeeping must return identical rejections. That is why the A8 autograder can test your StepM against hand-built bootstrap statistics.

3Family-wise error control

The procedure is due to Romano and Wolf , who prove that it asymptotically controls the familywise error rate under conditions on the data-generating process. Theorem 3.1 below states two assumptions that suffice and proves the conclusion from them.

Theorem 3.1 (asymptotic FWER control)

Let \(I_0 = \{k : \mu_k \le 0\}\) be the set of true hypotheses, let \(G_T\) be the distribution function of \(M_T = \max_{k \in I_0} \tau_k\), and let \(G^\ast_T\) be the bootstrap distribution function of \(\max_{k \in I_0} t^{\ast}_{k}\), given the data. Assume

  1. there is a distribution function \(G\) such that \(G_T(x) \to G(x)\) and \(G^\ast_T(x) \to G(x)\) in probability at every point \(x\) where \(G\) is continuous;
  2. \(G\) is continuous at its \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile \(q\), and strictly increasing there: \(G(q - \eta) \lt 1 - \alpha \lt G(q + \eta)\) for every \(\eta > 0\).

Then \[ \limsup_{T,\,B \to \infty} \Prob\big(\text{StepM rejects some } H_k \text{ with } k \in I_0\big) \le \alpha . \]

Proof

Monotonicity. Suppose some true hypothesis is rejected, and let \(j\) be the first step at which that happens. Before step \(j\) no true hypothesis was removed, so \(I_0 \subseteq A_j\), and Lemma 2.1 gives \(c_\alpha(A_j) \ge c_\alpha(I_0)\). The rejected \(k \in I_0\) has \(t_k > c_\alpha(A_j) \ge c_\alpha(I_0)\). For \(k \in I_0\), \(\mu_k \le 0\) implies \(t_k \le \tau_k\). Hence \[ \Prob(\text{some true } H_k \text{ rejected}) \le \Prob\big(M_T > c_\alpha(I_0)\big). \]

The limit. Let \(\hat G_B\) be the empirical distribution function of the \(B\) bootstrap maxima \(\max_{k \in I_0} t^{\ast}_{b,k}\). Then \(c_\alpha(I_0)\), the \(\lceil (1-\alpha)B \rceil\)-th smallest of them, equals \(\inf\{x : \hat G_B(x) \ge 1-\alpha\}\). A monotone function has at most countably many discontinuities, so we can fix an arbitrarily small \(\eta > 0\) with \(G\) continuous at \(q - \eta\) and \(q + \eta\). Given the data, \(B\,\hat G_B(x)\) is binomial with \(B\) trials and success probability \(G^\ast_T(x)\), so Chebyshev's inequality gives \(\Prob\big(|\hat G_B(x) - G^\ast_T(x)| > \delta\big) \le 1/(4B\delta^2)\). With assumption 1, \(\hat G_B(q \pm \eta) \to G(q \pm \eta)\) in probability as \(T, B \to \infty\). By assumption 2, \(G(q - \eta) \lt 1 - \alpha \lt G(q + \eta)\), so with probability tending to one \(\hat G_B(q - \eta) \lt 1 - \alpha \le \hat G_B(q + \eta)\), which places \(c_\alpha(I_0)\) in \((q - \eta,\, q + \eta]\). Therefore \[ \Prob\big(M_T > c_\alpha(I_0)\big) \le \Prob(M_T > q - \eta) + \Prob\big(c_\alpha(I_0) \le q - \eta\big) \to 1 - G(q - \eta), \] using assumption 1 for \(G_T(q - \eta)\). Let \(\eta \downarrow 0\) through such points. Continuity at \(q\) and assumption 2 give \(G(q) = 1 - \alpha\), so the bound tends to \(\alpha\).

The monotonicity step is why step-down methods cost nothing in error control. The first false rejection must happen while all true hypotheses are still active. At that moment the critical value is at least as large as the one a test of the true hypotheses alone would use.

Proposition 3.2 (the global null)

If every \(H_k\) is true, then with the same bootstrap draws StepM rejects some hypothesis exactly when the single-step test does. Their family-wise error rates are equal.

Proof

StepM rejects anything only if its first step rejects something, and its first step is the single-step test. Conversely, every single-step rejection is a StepM rejection (Proposition 2.2). When every hypothesis is true, every rejection is false, so the two events "some false rejection" coincide.

The next lab therefore measures StepM's family-wise error rate under the global null by running only its first step. It replaces Lab 1's oracle with the joint studentized bootstrap. The SIMULATED returns are serially independent, so resampling single dates (mean block length 1) is enough.

Lab 2 · FWER under the global null

With eight SIMULATED null strategies correlated through a common factor, rejecting each one at its own 5% level should produce at least one false rejection far more often than 5% of the time. The bootstrap max-t critical value should bring that rate back to about 5%.

import math
from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np

# SIMULATED global null: 8 strategies with zero mean differentials, correlated
# through a common factor. Compare a per-test 5% rule with the studentized
# bootstrap max-t critical value (whole dates resampled jointly, block length 1
# because these SIMULATED returns are serially independent).
rng = np.random.default_rng(55)
n_obs, n_strat, n_boot, n_reps, alpha = 200, 8, 199, 400, 0.05
per_test = NormalDist().inv_cdf(1 - alpha)
rank = math.ceil((1 - alpha) * n_boot)

naive_false = boot_false = 0
for _ in range(n_reps):
    d = 0.6 * rng.standard_normal((n_obs, 1)) + 0.8 * rng.standard_normal((n_obs, n_strat))
    means = d.mean(axis=0)
    t = means / (d.std(axis=0, ddof=1) / math.sqrt(n_obs))
    sample = d[rng.integers(0, n_obs, size=(n_boot, n_obs))]  # (B, T, K): one date vector per draw
    t_star = (sample.mean(axis=1) - means) / (sample.std(axis=1, ddof=1) / math.sqrt(n_obs))
    critical = np.sort(t_star.max(axis=1))[rank - 1]
    naive_false += int((t > per_test).any())
    boot_false += int((t > critical).any())

print(f"per-test critical value: {per_test:.3f}")
print(f"FWER with per-test 5% rule: {naive_false / n_reps:.3f}")
print(f"FWER with bootstrap max-t (first step of StepM): {boot_false / n_reps:.3f}")
per-test critical value: 1.645
FWER with per-test 5% rule: 0.223
FWER with bootstrap max-t (first step of StepM): 0.035

Testing eight strategies separately produced a false discovery in 22% of the studies. Under independence the rate would be \(1 - 0.95^8 = 0.337\). The common factor makes the strategies' statistics move together, which lowers it. The bootstrap max-t rate of 3.5% is within about 1.4 Monte Carlo standard errors of 5% (the standard error is about 0.011 with 400 replications). Treat it as consistent with the nominal level rather than as evidence of conservatism. With only 200 periods, asymptotic control is a statement about where the rate converges, not a finite-sample guarantee.

4Why whole dates are resampled

Definition 1.1 resamples rows of the \(T \times K\) matrix: one draw of dates, applied to every strategy. A tempting shortcut is to resample each strategy's history on its own. It is wrong, and the cleanest way to see why is a strategy that appears twice.

Proposition 4.1 (duplicates add no multiplicity under joint resampling)

Suppose columns \(k\) and \(l\) are identical, \(d_{k,t} = d_{l,t}\) for all \(t\). Under joint resampling, \(t_k = t_l\) and \(t^{\ast}_{b,k} = t^{\ast}_{b,l}\) for every draw. Hence \(c_\alpha(A \cup \{l\}) = c_\alpha(A)\) for every \(A \ni k\), and StepM makes the same decision on \(k\) and \(l\) as it makes on \(k\) in the problem without the copy.

Under independent resampling of each column, suppose the bootstrap statistics of the copies were exactly independent draws from a common continuous distribution \(F\). Then the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of their maximum over \(m\) copies would be \(F^{-1}\big((1-\alpha)^{1/m}\big)\), strictly larger than \(F^{-1}(1-\alpha)\) for \(m \ge 2\).

Proof

Under joint resampling, the resampled columns \(k\) and \(l\) are the same rows of identical columns, so their means and standard deviations agree draw by draw. Thus \(t^{\ast}_{b,k} = t^{\ast}_{b,l}\), and \(\max_{i \in A\cup\{l\}} t^{\ast}_{b,i} = \max_{i\in A} t^{\ast}_{b,i}\) whenever \(k \in A\). The critical values coincide, and since \(t_k = t_l\) every comparison does too. For the independent case, \(\Prob(\max_{i \le m} X_i \le c) = F(c)^m\), and setting this equal to \(1-\alpha\) gives \(c = F^{-1}((1-\alpha)^{1/m})\). Because \((1-\alpha)^{1/m} > 1-\alpha\) for \(\alpha \in (0,1)\) and \(m \ge 2\), and \(F^{-1}\) is increasing, the inequality is strict.

The duplicate is the extreme case of a general principle. Joint resampling reproduces the cross-sectional dependence of the strategies, so the critical value reflects how many genuinely different bets were made. Independent resampling treats correlated variants as independent ones, which overstates the multiplicity and throws away power. Lesson 1 made the same point for the expected maximum: correlation shrinks it by \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\) (L01, Problem P3).

Lab 3 · Ten copies of one strategy

A SIMULATED series with a t-statistic of 2.3, copied into ten columns, should be rejected at 5% under joint resampling, where the critical value stays near the one-test value 1.645. Under independent resampling the critical value should rise to near the ten-test value \(\Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/10}) = 2.568\), and the copies should not be rejected.

import math
from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np

# One SIMULATED differential series with a t-statistic of exactly 2.3, copied
# into 10 identical columns. Resample whole dates (joint) or each column on its
# own (independent) and compare the first-step critical values.
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n_obs, n_copies, n_boot, alpha = 400, 10, 999, 0.05
z = rng.standard_normal(n_obs)
x = (z - z.mean()) / z.std(ddof=1) + 2.3 / math.sqrt(n_obs)
t_obs = x.mean() / (x.std(ddof=1) / math.sqrt(n_obs))
rank = math.ceil((1 - alpha) * n_boot)

joint = x[rng.integers(0, n_obs, size=(n_boot, n_obs))]  # every copy sees the same dates
t_joint = (joint.mean(axis=1) - x.mean()) / (joint.std(axis=1, ddof=1) / math.sqrt(n_obs))
c_joint = np.sort(t_joint)[rank - 1]  # the max over identical copies is the single statistic

apart = x[rng.integers(0, n_obs, size=(n_boot, n_copies, n_obs))]  # each copy draws its own dates
t_apart = (apart.mean(axis=2) - x.mean()) / (apart.std(axis=2, ddof=1) / math.sqrt(n_obs))
c_apart = np.sort(t_apart.max(axis=1))[rank - 1]

phi = NormalDist()
print(f"observed t-statistic of every copy: {t_obs:.3f}")
print(f"critical value, joint resampling: {c_joint:.3f}  rejects all copies: {t_obs > c_joint}")
print(f"critical value, independent resampling: {c_apart:.3f}  rejects all copies: {t_obs > c_apart}")
print(f"normal benchmarks: one statistic {phi.inv_cdf(0.95):.3f}, max of 10 independent {phi.inv_cdf(0.95**0.1):.3f}")
observed t-statistic of every copy: 2.300
critical value, joint resampling: 1.601  rejects all copies: True
critical value, independent resampling: 2.566  rejects all copies: False
normal benchmarks: one statistic 1.645, max of 10 independent 2.568

Joint resampling recognizes the ten columns as one bet and rejects at the one-test critical value. Independent resampling charges for ten independent bets that were never made: its critical value of 2.566 nearly equals the independent-normal benchmark, and a genuine t-statistic of 2.3 goes undetected. In a real strategy book the variants are usually positively correlated rather than identical; then the error lies between these extremes and never favours the researcher. Negatively correlated strategies, such as a signal and its reversal, reverse the direction: independent resampling then understates the critical value.

5Studentization, and Holm with a bootstrap

Studentize so that no strategy owns the maximum. Suppose two independent null strategies have per-period standard deviations 1 and 5, and the test used the unstudentized statistics \(\sqrt{T}\,\bar d_k\), which are approximately \(\Normal(0, 1)\) and \(\Normal(0, 25)\). The 95% quantile of their maximum solves \(\Phi(c)\,\Phi(c/5) = 0.95\), which gives \(c = 8.224\). The noisy strategy sets the bar alone. A low-volatility strategy with a true t-statistic of 3 has \(\sqrt{T}\,\bar d_k \approx \Normal(3, 1)\), and it clears 8.224 with probability \(1 - \Phi(5.224) \approx 9 \times 10^{-8}\). After studentizing, both statistics are approximately standard normal, the critical value is \(\Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/2}) = 1.955\), and the same strategy is rejected with probability \(\Phi(3 - 1.955) = 0.852\). Romano and Wolf recommend studentized statistics where studentization is feasible. The arithmetic above shows what is at stake: without it, the critical value is a property of the noisiest strategy in the book.

Holm is StepM with a Bonferroni critical value. Holm's step-down is also a step-down procedure: it tests the smallest remaining p-value at level \(\alpha/a\), where \(a\) is the number of hypotheses still active. For one-sided z-statistics, that is the critical value \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/a)\). StepM replaces this dependence-blind bound by an estimate of the actual quantile of the maximum over the active set.

Proposition 5.1 (StepM is never stricter than Holm for independent normal statistics)

If the \(a\) active statistics are independent standard normals under the null, the exact quantile of their maximum is \(\Phi^{-1}\big((1-\alpha)^{1/a}\big)\). This is at most Holm's \(\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/a)\), for every \(a \ge 1\) and \(\alpha \in (0,1)\).

Proof

The quantile follows from \(\Prob(\max \le c) = \Phi(c)^a\). By Bernoulli's inequality, \((1 - \alpha/a)^a \ge 1 - \alpha\) whenever \(\alpha/a \le 1\). Taking \(a\)-th roots gives \(1 - \alpha/a \ge (1-\alpha)^{1/a}\), and \(\Phi^{-1}\) is increasing.

Under independence the gap is small: at \(a = 10\), StepM's 2.568 against Holm's 2.576, and at \(a = 20\), 2.799 against 2.807. Under positive dependence it widens, because StepM's critical values fall toward the one-test value 1.645 as correlation approaches one, while Holm's do not move. Lab 1's first-step critical value of 2.743 at correlation 0.3 sits below the independent value of 2.799 for twenty strategies. Holm remains usable when only p-values are available, as for published factors without a shared return panel. StepM needs the joint data.

6From StepM to simultaneous bands

A rejection says that \(\mu_k > 0\). Allocating capital also needs a range for \(\mu_k\), for every strategy at once. A confidence interval for each mean gives a range one strategy at a time: its coverage probability concerns that strategy alone, and with \(K\) intervals the chance that at least one misses can be large. This section builds intervals with a joint guarantee from the bootstrap draws StepM already uses, and shows exactly how they relate to StepM's rejections.

Definition 6.1 (simultaneous confidence band)

Random intervals \(C_1, \dots, C_K\), computed from the data, form a simultaneous confidence band for \((\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K)\) at level \(1-\alpha\) if their simultaneous coverage \[ \Prob\big(\mu_k \in C_k \text{ for every } k = 1, \dots, K\big) \] is at least \(1-\alpha\). The band is asymptotically valid if the \(\liminf\) of its simultaneous coverage is at least \(1-\alpha\). The probability \(\Prob(\mu_k \in C_k)\) is the pointwise coverage of the single interval \(C_k\).

Every band in this section has the form \(C_k = [\bar d_k - c\, s_k/\sqrt{T},\ \bar d_k + c\, s_k/\sqrt{T}]\) for some critical value \(c\). With the centred statistics \(\tau_k = (\bar d_k - \mu_k)/(s_k/\sqrt{T})\) of Section 1, \(\mu_k \in C_k\) exactly when \(|\tau_k| \le c\), so \[ \{\mu_k \in C_k \text{ for every } k\} = \Big\{ \max_{1 \le k \le K} |\tau_k| \le c \Big\}. \tag{1}\] Three choices of \(c\) are in use. The pointwise band takes \(c = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)\), which is 1.960 at \(\alpha = 0.05\): each interval has asymptotic coverage \(1-\alpha\), and the band has no joint guarantee. The Bonferroni band takes \(c = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/(2K))\), which is 3.023 for \(K = 20\). If each \(\tau_k\) is asymptotically standard normal, Boole's inequality bounds the asymptotic probability of a miss by \(K \cdot \alpha/K = \alpha\), whatever the dependence. The third choice reads the quantile of the maximum in (1) off the bootstrap.

Definition 6.2 (bootstrap sup-t band)

With the draws of Definition 1.1, let \(c^{\pm}_\alpha\) be the \(\lceil (1-\alpha)B \rceil\)-th smallest of the \(B\) two-sided maxima \(\max_{1 \le k \le K} |t^{\ast}_{b,k}|\). The bootstrap sup-t band is \[ C_k = \Big[\bar d_k - c^{\pm}_\alpha\, \frac{s_k}{\sqrt{T}},\ \ \bar d_k + c^{\pm}_\alpha\, \frac{s_k}{\sqrt{T}}\Big], \qquad k = 1, \dots, K. \]

Theorem 6.3 (the sup-t band is asymptotically valid)

Let \(H_T\) be the distribution function of \(M_T = \max_{1 \le k \le K} |\tau_k|\), the maximum over all \(K\) strategies, and let \(H^\ast_T\) be the bootstrap distribution function of \(\max_{1 \le k \le K} |t^{\ast}_{k}|\), given the data. Assume

  1. there is a distribution function \(H\) such that \(H_T(x) \to H(x)\) and \(H^\ast_T(x) \to H(x)\) in probability at every point \(x\) where \(H\) is continuous;
  2. \(H\) is continuous at its \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile \(h\), and strictly increasing there: \(H(h - \eta) \lt 1 - \alpha \lt H(h + \eta)\) for every \(\eta > 0\).

Then \[ \lim_{T,\,B \to \infty} \Prob\big(\mu_k \in C_k \text{ for every } k\big) = 1 - \alpha . \]

Proof

By (1), the simultaneous coverage is \(\Prob(M_T \le c^{\pm}_\alpha)\). The limit step of Theorem 3.1's proof applies with \(G\), \(q\), \(G^\ast_T\) and \(c_\alpha(I_0)\) replaced by \(H\), \(h\), \(H^\ast_T\) and \(c^{\pm}_\alpha\): for every \(\eta > 0\) at which \(H\) is continuous at \(h - \eta\) and \(h + \eta\), the critical value \(c^{\pm}_\alpha\) lies in \((h - \eta,\, h + \eta]\) with probability tending to one. Since \(\{M_T > h + \eta\} \subseteq \{M_T > c^{\pm}_\alpha\} \cup \{c^{\pm}_\alpha > h + \eta\}\) and \(\{M_T > c^{\pm}_\alpha\} \subseteq \{M_T > h - \eta\} \cup \{c^{\pm}_\alpha \le h - \eta\}\), \[ \Prob(M_T > h + \eta) - \Prob(c^{\pm}_\alpha > h + \eta) \;\le\; \Prob(M_T > c^{\pm}_\alpha) \;\le\; \Prob(M_T > h - \eta) + \Prob(c^{\pm}_\alpha \le h - \eta). \] By assumption 1 the lower bound tends to \(1 - H(h + \eta)\) and the upper bound to \(1 - H(h - \eta)\). Let \(\eta \downarrow 0\) through such points. Continuity of \(H\) at \(h\) and assumption 2 give \(H(h) = 1 - \alpha\), so both bounds tend to \(\alpha\), and the coverage tends to \(1 - \alpha\).

Three things differ from Theorem 3.1. The maximum runs over all \(K\) strategies rather than the true hypotheses, because coverage is a claim about every \(\mu_k\), so the proof needs no monotonicity step and no inequality \(t_k \le \tau_k\). The statistics enter in absolute value. And the conclusion is a limit, not an upper bound, because (1) is exactly the event whose probability the bootstrap estimates. The assumptions are Theorem 3.1's, restated for the two-sided maximum over all strategies.

Proposition 6.4 (duality, and what the band gives up against StepM)

Fix the bootstrap draws.

  1. \(0 \notin C_k\) if and only if \(|t_k| > c^{\pm}_\alpha\). The band excludes zero exactly for the hypotheses rejected by the single-step two-sided max-\(|t|\) test of \(H^{0}_k : \mu_k = 0\). Under the assumptions of Theorem 6.3, that test's probability of some false rejection is at most one minus the simultaneous coverage, which tends to \(\alpha\).
  2. \(c_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\}) \le c^{\pm}_\alpha\). Hence the strategies whose interval lies entirely above zero, \(\{k : \bar d_k - c^{\pm}_\alpha s_k/\sqrt{T} > 0\} = \{k : t_k > c^{\pm}_\alpha\}\), are among StepM's first-step rejections, and so among its rejections.
  3. The inclusion in (b) can be strict.
Proof

(a) \(0 \in C_k\) exactly when \(|\bar d_k| \le c^{\pm}_\alpha s_k/\sqrt{T}\), that is, \(|t_k| \le c^{\pm}_\alpha\). If \(H^{0}_k\) is true, then \(\tau_k = t_k\), so a false rejection means \(|\tau_k| > c^{\pm}_\alpha\) for some \(k\), and by (1) that is a failure of simultaneous coverage.

(b) For every draw, \(\max_k t^{\ast}_{b,k} \le \max_k |t^{\ast}_{b,k}|\). Order statistics preserve pointwise inequalities (the argument of Lemma 2.1), so the \(\lceil (1-\alpha)B \rceil\)-th smallest one-sided maximum, \(c_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\})\), is at most \(c^{\pm}_\alpha\). The lower end of \(C_k\) exceeds zero exactly when \(t_k > c^{\pm}_\alpha\), which implies \(t_k > c_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\})\): a rejection at StepM's first step (Definition 1.2).

(c) In Problem M1, StepM rejects strategy 3, with \(t_3 = 2.2\), at its second step. By (b), \(c^{\pm}_\alpha \ge c_\alpha(\{1,2,3,4\}) = 2.5 > 2.2\), so the band's interval for strategy 3 contains zero.

The band pays twice for its joint guarantee: for two-sidedness, since \(|t^\ast| \ge t^\ast\), and for covering every mean, including those of strategies StepM has already rejected. StepM may drop a rejected strategy from the maximum because a rejection is final. A band may not, because its statement about a rejected strategy's mean can still be wrong. The one-sided duality is exact. The lower bounds \(\bar d_k - c_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\})\, s_k/\sqrt{T}\) hold for every \(k\) at once exactly when \(\max_k \tau_k \le c_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\})\), and they exceed zero exactly where StepM's first step rejects. Romano and Wolf build their bootstrap critical values on this duality between a one-sided joint confidence region and the single-step test .

Remark 6.5 (how much narrower than Bonferroni)

If the limit \(H\) is the distribution of \(\max_k |Z_k|\) for standard normal \(Z_k\), the sup-t band is never wider than Bonferroni's in the limit. At \(z_B = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/(2K))\), Boole's inequality gives \(1 - H(z_B) \le \sum_k \Prob(|Z_k| > z_B) = \alpha\), so \(H(z_B) \ge 1 - \alpha\) and \(h \le z_B\). Apart from a small gain that exists even under independence, the gain comes from dependence. Under independence \(H(x) = (2\Phi(x) - 1)^K\), so \(h = \Phi^{-1}\big((1 + (1-\alpha)^{1/K})/2\big)\). For \(K = 20\) and \(\alpha = 0.05\) that is \(\Phi^{-1}(0.998719) = 3.016\), against Bonferroni's 3.023.

Montiel Olea and Plagborg-Møller show that the ranking of the sup-t band below the Bonferroni and projection bands, known in linear models, holds asymptotically in general nonlinear models such as vector autoregressions, and show that the sup-t band is the optimal default when the researcher does not know the audience's preferences . In their application to structural VAR impulse responses, the sup-t band is at least 35% narrower than other off-the-shelf simultaneous bands. The next lab measures the gain at two correlations.

Lab 4 · Three bands for twenty strategies

In a SIMULATED version of Lab 1's universe with returns (20 strategies over 250 periods, eight true alphas), each 95% pointwise interval should cover its own mean about 95% of the time, while all twenty should be covered together far less often. The Bonferroni band and the bootstrap sup-t band should both cover all twenty in about 95% of studies or more. By Remark 6.5, at Lab 1's correlation of 0.3 the two should have nearly the same width, and at correlation 0.9 the sup-t band should be much narrower. StepM's one-sided first-step critical value should sit below the band's (Proposition 6.4(b)).

cells = n_obs * np.arange(n_boot)[:, None]  # offsets that place draw b's dates in row b of a (B, T) table

The pointwise band fails. On average across the twenty, an interval covers its own mean 95.0% of the time at \(\rho = 0.3\), yet all twenty are covered together in only 45.5% of studies; at \(\rho = 0.9\) the intervals move together and the figure rises to 84.8%, still short. The other two hold within Monte Carlo error: Bonferroni at 96.3% and 98.8%, the sup-t band at 95.0% and 94.3%, against a standard error of \(\sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95/400} = 0.011\). At \(\rho = 0.3\) the sup-t band is not narrower: its mean critical value of 3.031 is 0.3% above Bonferroni's 3.023. Two effects nearly cancel there. Even under independence the limiting sup-t value is only 0.2% below Bonferroni's (Remark 6.5: 3.016 against 3.023), and a correlation of 0.3 does little to the far tail of the two-sided maximum. At \(\rho = 0.9\) the sup-t band is 17% narrower (\(2.508/3.023 = 0.830\)) and still covers, while Bonferroni spends the difference on over-coverage. The advantage is a property of the dependence, so it is largest in books of closely related variants. StepM's first-step critical values, 2.785 and 2.189, sit below the band's, as Proposition 6.4(b) requires: a one-sided test at the boundary asks less than a two-sided statement about every mean.

Intervals for the selected few

A band covers every mean at once. A desk that reports intervals only for the strategies it selects needs less, but a conventional interval for each selected strategy is not enough.

“We show that such selected intervals fail to provide the assumed coverage probability.”

—

Definition 6.6 (false coverage-statement rate)

A procedure selects a set \(S\) of strategies from the data, with \(R = |S|\), and reports an interval \(C_k\) for each \(k \in S\). Let \(V_{\mathrm{CI}}\) be the number of selected strategies whose interval misses \(\mu_k\). The false coverage-statement rate is \(\mathrm{FCR} = \E\big[V_{\mathrm{CI}} / \max(R, 1)\big]\).

Since \(V_{\mathrm{CI}}/\max(R,1) \le \indic\{V_{\mathrm{CI}} \ge 1\}\), any simultaneous band at level \(1-\alpha\), restricted to any selection, has \(\mathrm{FCR} \le \alpha\). The FCR stands to simultaneous coverage as the false discovery rate stands to the family-wise error rate, and Benjamini and Yekutieli give a procedure for it.

“A general procedure is then introduced, offering FCR control at level q under any selection rule. The procedure constructs a marginal CI for each selected parameter, but instead of the confidence level 1 − q being used marginally, q is divided by the number of parameters considered and multiplied by the number selected.”

—

With \(K\) strategies and \(R\) selected, each selected interval is built at marginal level \(1 - Rq/K\). When the selection is Benjamini–Hochberg at level \(q\), applied to the p-values of tests dual to the intervals, these intervals exclude zero exactly for the selected strategies (Problem M4). Problem P4 proves FCR control at level \(q\) under any selection rule and any dependence once \(q\) is further divided by \(c(K) = \sum_{j=1}^{K} 1/j\), the constant of Lesson 3's Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure. Selecting the single strategy with the largest t-statistic is the extreme case, the winner's curse, and Problem R4 treats it.

The bands and the FCR procedure of this section concern selection among \(K\) strategy means. When the selection is of regressors in a regression, such as which factors to include in a pricing model, inference after a data-driven selection is a separate problem, and this lesson gives no tool for it. Module F5 takes it up, under factor selection and a double-selection test for new factors.

7Tolerating a few false claims: the k-FWER and FDP exceedance

With hundreds or thousands of candidates, demanding that not one claim be false sacrifices most true discoveries (§9), while false-discovery-rate control bounds only an average. Two targets sit between them. This section works with p-values for a family of \(m\) hypotheses \(H_1, \dots, H_m\), as Lessons 2 and 3 do; for the strategy book \(m = K\). Here \(i\) indexes hypotheses and \(k\) is the number of false rejections that counts as a failure, following Lehmann and Romano. As before, \(I_0\) is the set of true hypotheses, \(m_0 = |I_0|\), \(V\) counts false rejections and \(R\) all rejections, and every true-null p-value is valid: \(\Prob(p_i \le u) \le u\) for \(u \in (0,1)\).

Definition 7.1 (k-FWER and false discovery exceedance)

For an integer \(k \ge 1\), the k-familywise error rate is \(\Prob(V \ge k)\), the probability of \(k\) or more false rejections; \(k = 1\) gives the \(\FWER\). For \(\gamma \in [0, 1)\), the false discovery exceedance at \(\gamma\) is \(\Prob(\FDP > \gamma)\), with \(\FDP = V/\max(R,1)\) as in Lesson 3. A procedure controls either one at level \(\alpha\) if it is at most \(\alpha\) for every configuration of true and false hypotheses.

Lemma 7.2 (exceedance and the false discovery rate)

For any procedure and \(\gamma \in [0,1)\): (a) \(\FDR \le \gamma + (1 - \gamma)\,\Prob(\FDP > \gamma)\); (b) if \(\gamma > 0\), \(\Prob(\FDP > \gamma) \le \FDR/\gamma\); (c) at \(\gamma = 0\) the exceedance is the \(\FWER\).

Proof

(a) Because \(0 \le \FDP \le 1\), \(\FDP \le \gamma\, \indic\{\FDP \le \gamma\} + \indic\{\FDP > \gamma\}\). Taking expectations gives \(\FDR \le \gamma\,(1 - P) + P = \gamma + (1-\gamma)P\) with \(P = \Prob(\FDP > \gamma)\). (b) is Markov's inequality. (c) \(\FDP > 0\) exactly when \(V \ge 1\). Lehmann and Romano note both bounds .

Bound (b) is crude. FDR control at \(q = 0.10\) guarantees only \(\Prob(\FDP > 0.2) \le 0.10/0.2 = 0.5\). Lesson 3's Lab 3 measured 10.6% for BH at \(\rho = 0.5\): far inside the bound, but more than twice the 5% a risk limit would set. Controlling the exceedance requires procedures built for it.

Lemma 7.3 (the counting bound)

Let \(N(u) = \#\{i \in I_0 : p_i \le u\}\). For any joint distribution of the p-values, \(\Prob(N(u) \ge k) \le m_0 u/k\). In particular, rejecting every \(H_i\) with \(p_i \le k\alpha/m\) controls the k-FWER at level \(\alpha\).

Proof

Markov's inequality and validity give \(\Prob(N(u) \ge k) \le \E[N(u)]/k = \sum_{i \in I_0} \Prob(p_i \le u)/k \le m_0 u/k\). The single-step rule makes \(V = N(k\alpha/m)\), and \(m_0 (k\alpha/m)/k = \alpha m_0/m \le \alpha\). This is Lehmann and Romano's Theorem 2.1(i) ; for \(k = 1\) it is Bonferroni.

Theorem 7.4 (Lehmann–Romano: a step-down for the k-FWER)

Order the p-values \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}\), with hypotheses \(H_{(1)}, \dots, H_{(m)}\), and set \[ \alpha_i = \begin{cases} \dfrac{k\alpha}{m}, & i \le k, \\[2mm] \dfrac{k\alpha}{m + k - i}, & i > k. \end{cases} \tag{2}\] (These are Lehmann and Romano's constants (12), with their \(s\) written \(m\).) The step-down procedure rejects \(H_{(1)}, \dots, H_{(r)}\) for the largest \(r\) with \(p_{(i)} \le \alpha_i\) for every \(i \le r\), and rejects nothing if \(p_{(1)} > \alpha_1\). If every true-null p-value is valid, it controls the k-FWER at level \(\alpha\) under any dependence. For \(k = 1\) it is Holm's step-down.

Proof

If \(m_0 \lt k\) there is nothing to prove, so let \(m_0 \ge k\). The constants are nondecreasing in \(i\): \(k\alpha/(m+k-i)\) grows with \(i\) and equals \(k\alpha/m\) at \(i = k\). Let \(q_{(1)} \le \dots \le q_{(m_0)}\) be the ordered true-null p-values, and let \(j\) be the position in the ordering of the \(k\)-th true null, so that \(p_{(j)} = q_{(k)}\). At most \(m - m_0\) false nulls precede it, so \(k \le j \le m - m_0 + k\).

The procedure rejects an initial segment of the ordering. If it rejects \(k\) or more true nulls, it rejects the one at position \(j\), which requires \(p_{(j)} \le \alpha_j\). Because the constants are nondecreasing and \(j \le m - m_0 + k\), \[ q_{(k)} = p_{(j)} \le \alpha_j \le \alpha_{m - m_0 + k} = \frac{k\alpha}{m_0}, \] where the last equality reads off (2) at \(i = m - m_0 + k\) (it is the case \(i = k\) when \(m_0 = m\)). So \(\{V \ge k\} \subseteq \{q_{(k)} \le k\alpha/m_0\} = \{N(k\alpha/m_0) \ge k\}\), and Lemma 7.3 bounds its probability by \(m_0 (k\alpha/m_0)/k = \alpha\). For \(k = 1\), (2) is \(\alpha/(m - i + 1)\), Holm's constants. As in Lesson 2's proof for Holm, nothing assumed independence: the false nulls can only push the \(k\)-th true null later in the ordering, where its threshold is looser by at most the factor \(m/m_0\).

Tolerating a few false claims buys a looser bar at once. With \(m = 500\), \(k = 5\) and \(\alpha = 0.05\), Holm's first threshold is \(0.05/500 = 0.0001\), and (2) starts at \(5 \times 0.05/500 = 0.0005\), five times higher. Lehmann and Romano also prove that the constants cannot be improved: for every \(i \ge k\), raising \(\alpha_i\) alone violates k-FWER control for some joint distribution of valid p-values. The first \(k - 1\) constants could be raised to one without losing control, but that rejects \(k - 1\) hypotheses whatever the data, which they decline to do .

Exceedance. Having rejected \(i\) hypotheses, \(\FDP \le \gamma\) allows \(\lfloor \gamma i \rfloor\) false ones, so the procedure must make \(\lfloor \gamma i \rfloor + 1\) false rejections unlikely. Putting that \(k\) into (2) gives Lehmann and Romano's exceedance constants \[ \alpha_i = \frac{(\lfloor \gamma i \rfloor + 1)\,\alpha}{m + \lfloor \gamma i \rfloor + 1 - i}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, m, \tag{3}\] their Eq. (26). They are nondecreasing: from \(i\) to \(i+1\), \(\lfloor \gamma i \rfloor\) rises by zero or one, so the numerator does not fall and the denominator does not rise.

Theorem 7.5 (where an exceedance must come from)

Run the step-down procedure with constants (3), let \(q_{(1)} \le \dots \le q_{(m_0)}\) be the ordered true-null p-values, and let \(M = \min(\lfloor \gamma m \rfloor + 1,\ m_0)\). For any joint distribution of the p-values, \[ \{\FDP > \gamma\} \subseteq \bigcup_{l=1}^{M} \Big\{ q_{(l)} \le \frac{l\,\alpha}{m_0} \Big\}. \]

Proof

Suppose \(\FDP > \gamma\), and let the procedure reject \(H_{(1)}, \dots, H_{(r)}\). Let \(V_i\) be the number of true nulls among \(H_{(1)}, \dots, H_{(i)}\), with \(V_0 = 0\). Since \(V_r > \gamma r\), the index \(j = \min\{i \le r : V_i > \gamma i\}\) exists, and minimality gives \(V_{j-1} \le \gamma(j-1)\). Since \(V_j \le V_{j-1} + 1\), \[ \lfloor \gamma j \rfloor \le \gamma j \lt V_j \le \gamma (j - 1) + 1 \lt \lfloor \gamma j \rfloor + 2, \] so \(V_j = V_{j-1} + 1\) and \(V_j = \lfloor \gamma j \rfloor + 1 =: l\). Thus \(H_{(j)}\) is the \(l\)-th true null in the ordering, it was rejected, and \(q_{(l)} = p_{(j)} \le \alpha_j\). The first \(j\) positions hold \(l\) true nulls and \(j - l\) false ones, so \(m_0 \le m - (j - l)\). By (3), \[ q_{(l)} \le \frac{l\,\alpha}{m + l - j} \le \frac{l\,\alpha}{m_0}. \] Finally \(l \le m_0\), and \(l = \lfloor \gamma j \rfloor + 1 \le \lfloor \gamma m \rfloor + 1\) because \(j \le m\); so \(l \le M\).

Lemma 7.6 (a union bound for ordered p-values)

Let \(p_1, \dots, p_t\) be valid p-values with ordered values \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(t)}\), and let \(M \le t\) and \(0 = \beta_0 \le \beta_1 \le \dots \le \beta_M \le 1\). For any joint distribution, \[ \Prob\Big( \bigcup_{l=1}^{M} \{p_{(l)} \le \beta_l\} \Big) \le t \sum_{l=1}^{M} \frac{\beta_l - \beta_{l-1}}{l}. \]

Proof

Let \(J\) be the smallest \(l \le M\) with \(p_{(l)} \le \beta_l\) (\(J = \infty\) if there is none) and \(\theta_l = \Prob(J = l)\). The events \(\{J = l\}\) are disjoint, so the left side is \(\sum_{l=1}^{M} \theta_l\). For \(j \le M\), let \(S_j = \#\{i : p_i \le \beta_j\}\). If \(J = l \le j\), then \(p_{(l)} \le \beta_l \le \beta_j\), so at least \(l\) p-values lie at or below \(\beta_j\): \(J\, \indic\{J \le j\} \le S_j\). Taking expectations and using validity, \[ \sum_{l=1}^{j} l\,\theta_l \le \E[S_j] \le t \beta_j, \qquad j = 1, \dots, M. \] Multiply the \(j\)-th inequality by \(w_j = 1/(j(j+1))\) for \(j \lt M\) and by \(w_M = 1/M\), and add. The weights are positive and telescope, \(\sum_{j=l}^{M} w_j = (1/l - 1/M) + 1/M = 1/l\), so the left sides add to \(\sum_l l\,\theta_l \cdot (1/l) = \sum_l \theta_l\). The right sides add to \(t\big(\sum_{j \lt M} \beta_j/(j(j+1)) + \beta_M/M\big)\), which equals \(t \sum_{l=1}^{M} (\beta_l - \beta_{l-1})/l\) by summation by parts, since \(\sum_{l=1}^{M} (\beta_l - \beta_{l-1})/l = \sum_{l \lt M} \beta_l \big(1/l - 1/(l+1)\big) + \beta_M/M\). For \(\beta_l = l\alpha/t\) and \(M = t\) this is Hommel's bound \(\alpha \sum_{l \le t} 1/l\), which Lehmann and Romano generalize.

Theorem 7.7 (exceedance control under any dependence)

Let \(c(j) = \sum_{l=1}^{j} 1/l\), Lesson 3's constant. The step-down procedure with constants \(\alpha_i / c(\lfloor \gamma m \rfloor + 1)\), with \(\alpha_i\) from (3), satisfies \(\Prob(\FDP > \gamma) \le \alpha\) for any joint distribution of valid p-values.

Proof

Dividing every constant by \(c(\lfloor \gamma m \rfloor + 1)\) is (3) at level \(\alpha' = \alpha / c(\lfloor \gamma m \rfloor + 1)\). Theorem 7.5 at level \(\alpha'\) gives \(\Prob(\FDP > \gamma) \le \Prob\big(\bigcup_{l \le M} \{q_{(l)} \le l\alpha'/m_0\}\big)\). Apply Lemma 7.6 to the \(t = m_0\) true-null p-values with \(\beta_l = l\alpha'/m_0\), which is nondecreasing, at most \(\alpha' \le 1\) for \(l \le M \le m_0\), and has increments \(\alpha'/m_0\). The bound is \(m_0 \sum_{l=1}^{M} \alpha'/(m_0 l) = \alpha'\, c(M) \le \alpha'\, c(\lfloor \gamma m \rfloor + 1) = \alpha\), because \(M \le \lfloor \gamma m \rfloor + 1\).

The factor \(c(\lfloor \gamma m \rfloor + 1)\) is much smaller than the Benjamini–Yekutieli factor \(c(m)\): at \(m = 500\) and \(\gamma = 0.2\) it is \(c(101) = 5.197\) (Lab 5 prints it), against \(c(500) = 6.793\). Lehmann and Romano prove that the uncorrected constants (3) already control the exceedance in two cases: when each true-null p-value remains valid conditional on the false-null p-values (their condition (27), which holds, for example, when the true-null p-values are independent of the false-null ones and arbitrarily dependent among themselves), and, by Theorem 7.5, when the true-null p-values satisfy the Simes inequality \(\Prob\big(\bigcup_{l \le m_0} \{q_{(l)} \le l\alpha/m_0\}\big) \le \alpha\). They note that Sarkar and Chang, and Sarkar, have shown that the Simes inequality holds for distributions that are multivariate totally positive of order 2 (MTP2).

Equicorrelated normal statistics with correlation \(0 \le \rho \lt 1\), the design of Lab 5, are MTP2. Their covariance matrix \((1-\rho)I + \rho\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\) of order \(m_0\) has inverse \(Q\) with off-diagonal entries \(-\rho/\big((1-\rho)(1-\rho+m_0\rho)\big) \le 0\). For a normal density \(f\) with such a \(Q\), take any \(x\) and \(y\), and let \(u = x \vee y\) and \(v = x \wedge y\) coordinatewise. The diagonal terms of the quadratic forms cancel, because \(u_i^2 + v_i^2 = x_i^2 + y_i^2\), so \[ \log f(u) + \log f(v) - \log f(x) - \log f(y) = \tfrac12 \sum_{i \ne j} Q_{ij}\,\big(x_i x_j + y_i y_j - u_i u_j - v_i v_j\big). \] Each bracket is zero when \(x_i - y_i\) and \(x_j - y_j\) have the same sign, and equals \((x_i - y_i)(x_j - y_j) \le 0\) otherwise. Every term is therefore nonnegative, and \(f(u) f(v) \ge f(x) f(y)\), which is the MTP2 inequality. One-sided p-values are decreasing functions of the statistics, applied coordinate by coordinate; that swaps \(\vee\) and \(\wedge\), and the Jacobian is a product of one-coordinate factors, so the true-null p-values are MTP2 too. They then satisfy the Simes inequality, and by Theorem 7.5 the uncorrected constants (3) control the exceedance in that design at every \(\rho \in [0, 1)\).

Proposition 7.8 (augmenting a family-wise procedure)

Let a procedure control the \(\FWER\) at level \(\alpha\), and let it reject \(R_0\) hypotheses. Reject, in addition, the \(D = \lfloor \gamma R_0/(1-\gamma) \rfloor\) most significant of the remaining hypotheses (all of them, if fewer remain). The augmented procedure satisfies \(\Prob(\FDP > \gamma) \le \alpha\), under whatever conditions the original procedure controls the \(\FWER\): for Holm, any dependence.

Proof

Suppose the original procedure makes no false rejection. The augmented procedure rejects \(R = R_0 + D'\) hypotheses, with \(D' = \min(D, m - R_0) \le D\), and at most the \(D'\) added ones are false. If \(R = 0\), then \(\FDP = 0\). Otherwise \(\FDP \le D'/(R_0 + D') \le D/(R_0 + D)\), because \(x \mapsto x/(R_0 + x)\) is increasing. And \(D \le \gamma R_0/(1-\gamma)\) is equivalent to \(D \le \gamma(R_0 + D)\), so \(\FDP \le \gamma\). Hence \(\{\FDP > \gamma\}\) requires a false rejection by the original procedure, which has probability at most \(\alpha\).

The construction is van der Laan, Dudoit and Pollard's , who give the same augmentation for tail probabilities of the number of false positives. Romano and Wolf argue that augmentation for the k-FWER seems suboptimal: it assumes the worst case, that the \(k - 1\) added hypotheses are all true, it rejects them even when their p-values show no sign of significance, and it rejects more than \(k - 1\) hypotheses only if the family-wise procedure rejects something. Against this, van der Laan, Dudoit and Pollard had shown, three years earlier, that augmenting step-down procedures that control the FWER asymptotically exactly at level \(\alpha\) yields exact asymptotic control of these error rates. Lab 5 compares the two approaches on one design.

The bootstrap version: k-StepM. Theorem 7.4, like Holm, ignores the dependence between tests, and the bootstrap repairs that for the k-FWER the way StepM repairs Holm. In what follows \(i\) indexes strategies, with the statistics of Section 1, and \(\max^{(k)}_{i \in A}\) denotes the \(k\)-th largest value over \(A\) (\(-\infty\) if \(|A| \lt k\)).

Definition 7.9 (k-StepM)

With the draws of Definition 1.1, let \(c^{(k)}_\alpha(A)\) be the \(\lceil (1-\alpha)B \rceil\)-th smallest of the \(B\) values \(\max^{(k)}_{i \in A} t^{\ast}_{b,i}\). Step 1 rejects every \(i\) with \(t_i > c^{(k)}_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\})\). At step \(j \ge 2\), with \(R_{j-1}\) the set rejected so far and \(A_j\) its complement, the critical value is \[ d_j = \max\big\{ c^{(k)}_\alpha(A_j \cup I) : I \subseteq R_{j-1},\ |I| \le k - 1 \big\}, \] and the step rejects every \(i \in A_j\) with \(t_i > d_j\). The procedure stops when a step rejects nothing. For \(k = 1\), only \(I = \varnothing\) qualifies, and this is StepM.

This is Romano and Wolf's generic step-down for the k-FWER (Algorithm 2.1) with bootstrap critical values (Eq. (21)) , studentized as in Definition 1.1. They maximize over subsets of size exactly \(k - 1\); the \(k\)-th largest over a larger set is no smaller, so the maximum above is attained at \(|I| = \min(k-1, |R_{j-1}|)\), and the two definitions agree. Including up to \(k - 1\) rejected strategies in the set that sets the critical value is the price of tolerating false claims: the first \(k - 1\) false rejections may already have removed true hypotheses from the active set.

Theorem 7.10 (k-StepM controls the k-FWER asymptotically)

Let \(I_0 = \{i : \mu_i \le 0\}\) with \(|I_0| \ge k\), let \(G^{(k)}_T\) be the distribution function of \(\max^{(k)}_{i \in I_0} \tau_i\), and let \(G^{(k)\ast}_T\) be the bootstrap distribution function of \(\max^{(k)}_{i \in I_0} t^{\ast}_i\), given the data. If assumptions 1–2 of Theorem 3.1 hold with \(G_T\) and \(G^\ast_T\) replaced by \(G^{(k)}_T\) and \(G^{(k)\ast}_T\), then \[ \limsup_{T,\,B \to \infty} \Prob\big(\text{k-StepM rejects at least } k \text{ true hypotheses}\big) \le \alpha . \]

Proof

Suppose at least \(k\) true hypotheses are rejected, and let \(j\) be the step at which the \(k\)-th of them is rejected. At every step \(j' \le j\), fewer than \(k\) true hypotheses had been rejected before it, so \(I' = R_{j'-1} \cap I_0\) has \(|I'| \le k - 1\), and every true hypothesis is still active or in \(I'\): \(I_0 \subseteq A_{j'} \cup I'\). The \(k\)-th largest over a larger set is no smaller, draw by draw, and order statistics keep the inequality, as in Lemma 2.1. So \(d_{j'} \ge c^{(k)}_\alpha(A_{j'} \cup I') \ge c^{(k)}_\alpha(I_0)\), and step 1's critical value \(c^{(k)}_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\})\) is at least \(c^{(k)}_\alpha(I_0)\) too. Each of the at least \(k\) rejected true hypotheses was rejected at some step \(j' \le j\), so it has \(\tau_i \ge t_i > c^{(k)}_\alpha(I_0)\), using \(\mu_i \le 0\). Hence \[ \Prob(\text{at least } k \text{ false rejections}) \le \Prob\Big(\max^{(k)}_{i \in I_0} \tau_i > c^{(k)}_\alpha(I_0)\Big), \] and the limit step of Theorem 3.1, with \(G^{(k)}_T\) and \(G^{(k)\ast}_T\) in place of \(G_T\) and \(G^\ast_T\), bounds the right side by \(\alpha\) in the limit.

The maximum over \(I\) has up to \(\binom{|R_{j-1}|}{k-1}\) candidates. Romano and Wolf prove that a streamlined algorithm, which adds back only the \(k - 1\) least significant hypotheses rejected so far, also controls the k-FWER asymptotically; it rejects at least as much as the generic algorithm, but unlike the generic one it would not control the k-FWER in finite samples even with exact critical values. As a compromise they suggest an operative method that maximizes only over subsets of the \(M\) least significant rejections, with \(M\) the largest number whose subsets of size \(k - 1\) number at most a cap \(N_{\max}\), such as 50 . For the exceedance, they run the k-FWER step-down for \(k = 1, 2, \dots\), stop at the first \(k\) whose number of rejections \(N_k\) satisfies \(N_k \lt k/\gamma - 1\), report that step-down's rejections, and prove asymptotic control of \(\Prob(\FDP > \gamma)\). Romano, Shaikh and Wolf review these generalized error rates for econometrics, starting from the observation that with many hypotheses family-wise control can become so demanding that few or no false hypotheses are rejected, and illustrate them with a simulation study and two empirical applications .

Every lab so far simulated its data, so it knew which hypotheses were true. On market data nothing is known in advance, and the procedures of this lesson and Lesson 4 are the only judges. The universe below is a small version of the technical-rule universes of (Lesson 4, Remark 4.2): moving-average rules on six exchange rates against the dollar. That remark's design point applies to it at once. The universe must contain every rule that could have been chosen, and a reader of this page can check only that it contains every rule the page ran.

The data are spot rates, and a spot return is not what a currency position earns. Quote the rate \(S_t\) in dollars per unit of foreign currency. A dollar investor who goes long for one period converts one dollar into \(1/S_{t-1}\) units, deposits them at the foreign interest rate \(i^\ast\) for the period, converts back at \(S_t\), and repays the dollar, borrowed at the dollar rate \(i\). The excess return per dollar is \[ \frac{(1+i^\ast)\,S_t}{S_{t-1}} - (1+i) = r_t + (i^\ast - i) + i^\ast r_t, \qquad r_t = \frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}} - 1, \tag{4}\] as expanding \((1+i^\ast)(1+r_t) - (1+i)\) shows, and a short position earns the negative. The files hold \(r_t\) only. A rule with position \(w_t \in \{-1, 0, 1\}\) earns \(w_t r_t\) in the lab, and its tradable excess return differs by three terms. The carry \(w_t(i^\ast - i)\) raises the rule's mean when it tends to be long the currency with the higher interest rate and lowers it otherwise. The cross term \(w_t i^\ast r_t\) is a product of two per-period rates. Trading costs lower every rule's return, and lower most the returns of the rules that trade most. Every conclusion below is therefore a conclusion about spot returns.

def stationary_indices(mean_block: float) -> NDArray[np.int64]:

    return boot_mean, boot_se


def stepm(t_star: NDArray[np.float64]) -> tuple[NDArray[np.bool_], float, int]:
    """StepM (Definition 1.2): rejections, the first step's critical value, and the number of steps run."""
    rejected = np.zeros(n_rules, dtype=bool)
    critical: list[float] = []
    while not rejected.all():
        active = ~rejected
        critical.append(float(np.sort(t_star[:, active].max(axis=1))[rank - 1]))
        new = active & (t_obs > critical[-1])
        if not new.any():
            break
        rejected |= new
    return rejected, critical[0], len(critical)


def reality_check(boot_mean: NDArray[np.float64]) -> float:
    """White's Reality Check p-value (Lesson 4, Definition 4.1)."""
    v_star = math.sqrt(n_obs) * (boot_mean - means).max(axis=1)
    return float(np.mean(v_star >= math.sqrt(n_obs) * means.max()))


def spa_style(boot_mean: NDArray[np.float64]) -> tuple[float, int]:
    """SPA-style p-value (Lesson 4, Definition 6.1), with omega_k the bootstrap sd of sqrt(T) * mean*_k."""
    omega = math.sqrt(n_obs) * boot_mean.std(axis=0, ddof=1)
    centre = np.where(means <= -omega * math.sqrt(2.0 * math.log(math.log(n_obs)) / n_obs), means, 0.0)
    observed = max(float((math.sqrt(n_obs) * means / omega).max()), 0.0)
    v_star = np.maximum((math.sqrt(n_obs) * (boot_mean - means + centre) / omega).max(axis=1), 0.0)
    return float(np.mean(v_star >= observed)), int((centre < 0).sum())

9Where the canon breaks

  • Asymptotic, not exact. Theorem 3.1 bounds a limit. With a few hundred periods, the realized family-wise error rate depends on \(T\), on \(B\) and on the bootstrap's mean block length, and it can sit on either side of \(\alpha\). Lab 2 is one data point. A credible application reports a simulation of its own size at the sample length it actually has.
  • The studentizing scale is wrong under serial correlation. The statistic \(t_k\) uses the iid standard error \(s_k/\sqrt{T}\). With autocorrelated differentials its null distribution is not standard normal: its variance is the ratio of the long-run variance of \(d_{k,t}\) to its marginal variance (Problem E2). The stationary bootstrap reproduces the same distortion in \(t^{\ast}\), which is what keeps the procedure valid. But strategies with different autocorrelation are then no longer on a common scale, which partly undoes the purpose of studentizing. Studentizing with HAC standard errors, in the data and in every bootstrap draw, restores the common scale at the cost of a bandwidth choice.
  • The mean block length is a tuning parameter without a universal rule. Too short and the bootstrap ignores the serial dependence. Too long and few distinct blocks are drawn, so the critical values become noisy. Report results across a range of block lengths, as Lab 6 does.
  • The maximum is one way to use the dependence, not the only one. Bouamara, Laurent and Shi propose a stepwise Cauchy combination test for dependent statistics and sparse signals that keeps strong family-wise error control, pinpoints which p-values drive the rejections, is less conservative under dependence, and in their simulations and applications, including tests for nonzero alphas, has more power than conventional corrections. Which procedure is more powerful depends on how many effects are real and how strongly the statistics are correlated, so compare candidates by simulation on your own design.
  • Family-wise control is the wrong target when \(K\) runs into the thousands. Demanding that not even one of thousands of claims is false sacrifices most true discoveries. There, control of the false discovery rate (Lesson 3) is the more useful contract, and the k-FWER and exceedance control of Section 7 sit between the two when the realized share of false claims must be bounded, not only its average.
  • The tolerance is a choice made before the data. Theorems 7.4, 7.7 and 7.10 hold for a \(k\), \(\gamma\) and \(\alpha\) fixed in advance. Raising \(k\) or \(\gamma\) after seeing how many strategies would be rejected turns the tolerance into one more researcher degree of freedom, and the guarantee no longer applies.
  • Rejection is not estimation. StepM says which hypotheses to reject, not how large the rejected strategies' effects are. Their sample means were selected for being large, and they inherit the data-snooping bias of Lesson 1. The sup-t band of Section 6 gives intervals valid for every strategy at once, and so for any strategy the data single out, but it is two-sided and asks more of the data than StepM's test (Proposition 6.4). Intervals for the selected few (Definition 6.6) and corrections for the winner's curse (Problem R4) trade that generality for width.

10Misconceptions

  1. “Step-down procedures buy power by tolerating more false rejections.”

    They control the same family-wise error rate as the single-step test. The proof of Theorem 3.1 shows that any first false rejection happens while every true hypothesis is still active, so the critical value is then at least as large as for the true hypotheses alone. Lab 1's StepM error rate rose toward 5%, not past it. The gain comes from no longer paying for hypotheses already rejected.

  2. “Resampling each strategy's history separately is the same as resampling dates.”

    It destroys the dependence between strategies and treats correlated variants as independent bets. Proposition 4.1 and Lab 3 show the effect on duplicates: the critical value jumps from about 1.6 to about 2.6, and a genuine signal is missed.

  3. “Studentizing only changes units; the same strategies get rejected either way.”

    It changes which strategies get rejected. Without studentization the maximum is dominated by the noisiest strategy. Section 5's example puts the critical value at 8.224 instead of 1.955, which turns a 0.85 probability of detecting a genuine t-statistic of 3 into less than one in ten million.

  4. “Once StepM rejects a strategy, its backtested mean is a fair estimate of its edge.”

    The rejected strategies are the ones whose sample means came out large. Their estimates remain biased upward, which is the selection bias of Lesson 1. StepM controls the probability of a false claim of skill, not the size of the claimed skill.

  5. “Twenty 95% confidence intervals give 95% confidence that all twenty contain the truth.”

    Each interval covers its own mean 95% of the time; together they cover all twenty far less often. In Lab 4, at correlation 0.3, the pointwise intervals covered their own means 95.0% of the time on average, and all twenty together covered in 45.5% of studies. A joint statement needs a simultaneous band (Definition 6.1), such as Bonferroni's or the bootstrap sup-t band of Theorem 6.3.

  6. “If StepM rejects a strategy's null, its sup-t interval must lie above zero.”

    The band answers a harder question with a larger critical value, \(c^{\pm}_\alpha \ge c_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\})\) (Proposition 6.4). It is two-sided, and it never drops rejected strategies from its maximum. The strategies whose interval lies above zero are among StepM's rejections, but StepM can reject more: in Problem M1 it rejects strategy 3 at its second step, while the band's interval for strategy 3 contains zero.

M1 · StepM by hand

Four strategies have \(t = (4.1,\ 2.9,\ 2.2,\ 1.0)\). From the bootstrap, \(c_\alpha(\{1,2,3,4\}) = 2.5\), \(c_\alpha(\{2,3,4\}) = 2.3\), \(c_\alpha(\{3,4\}) = 2.1\) and \(c_\alpha(\{4\}) = 1.7\). Which hypotheses does the single-step test reject? Which does StepM reject, and in how many steps? What changes if \(t_3 = 2.0\)?

Solution

Single-step: compare everything with \(c_\alpha(\{1,2,3,4\}) = 2.5\). Strategies 1 (4.1) and 2 (2.9) exceed it, so the single-step test rejects \(\{1, 2\}\).

StepM: step 1 is the same comparison and rejects \(\{1, 2\}\). Step 2 uses the active set \(\{3, 4\}\) with \(c_\alpha(\{3,4\}) = 2.1\): strategy 3 (2.2) exceeds it and is rejected. Step 3 uses \(\{4\}\) with critical value 1.7, and 1.0 does not exceed it, so StepM stops. It rejects \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) in three steps; the third step rejects nothing and ends the procedure. The value \(c_\alpha(\{2,3,4\})\) is never used, because strategies 1 and 2 leave the active set together.

With \(t_3 = 2.0\), step 2 rejects nothing (2.0 does not exceed 2.1), and StepM stops at \(\{1, 2\}\), the single-step answer.

M2 · Which order statistic?

Compute the rank \(\lceil (1-\alpha)B \rceil\) of the critical value among the \(B\) bootstrap maxima for (a) \(B = 999\), \(\alpha = 0.05\); (b) \(B = 1000\), \(\alpha = 0.05\); (c) \(B = 499\), \(\alpha = 0.10\); (d) \(B = 19\), \(\alpha = 0.05\). What does (d) imply?

Solution

(a) \(\lceil 0.95 \times 999 \rceil = \lceil 949.05 \rceil = 950\). (b) \(\lceil 950 \rceil = 950\). (c) \(\lceil 0.9 \times 499 \rceil = \lceil 449.1 \rceil = 450\). (d) \(\lceil 0.95 \times 19 \rceil = \lceil 18.05 \rceil = 19\): the critical value is the largest of the 19 maxima. With so few draws the critical value is as noisy as a single extreme observation can make it. The bootstrap quantile is a sample quantile, and \(B\) should be large enough that its sampling error is small relative to the gaps between the \(t_k\).

M3 · StepM, Holm and perfect correlation

For \(a = 20\), \(15\) and \(10\) active one-sided tests at \(\alpha = 0.05\), compute (a) StepM's limiting critical value when the statistics are independent standard normals, (b) Holm's \(\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/a)\), and (c) StepM's limiting critical value when the statistics are perfectly correlated.

Solution

(a) \(\Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/a})\): \(2.799\) for \(a = 20\), \(2.705\) for \(a = 15\) and \(2.568\) for \(a = 10\).

(b) \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - 0.05/a)\): \(\Phi^{-1}(0.9975) = 2.807\), \(\Phi^{-1}(0.99667) = 2.713\) and \(\Phi^{-1}(0.995) = 2.576\). Each is slightly above (a), as Proposition 5.1 requires.

(d) Compare the critical value with those of the unadjusted 90% intervals and of the Bonferroni band for all ten at 90%.

(a) The statistics are \(z = 3.4, 3.0, 2.7, 2.5, 2.2, 1.6, 1.2, 0.8, 0.4, -0.3\), and the two-sided p-values, already in increasing order, are 0.00067, 0.0027, 0.0069, 0.0124, 0.0278, 0.1096, 0.2301, 0.4237, 0.6892 and 0.7642. Proof

P1 · Order does not matter

Consider a variant of StepM that, at each step, rejects only the single largest \(t_k\) exceeding \(c_\alpha(A_j)\) (ties broken by index), and otherwise follows Definition 1.2. Prove that it returns the same set as StepM.

Solution

Let \(R'\) be the variant's output. \(R'\) is closed: the variant stops only when the active set is empty or no active statistic exceeds \(c_\alpha(A)\) for the current active set \(A = R'^{c}\). That is the closedness condition of Theorem 2.3.

\(R'\) is contained in every closed set \(S\): repeat the induction of Theorem 2.3. If all hypotheses rejected so far lie in \(S\) and the variant now rejects some \(k \notin S\), then \(t_k > c_\alpha(A) \ge c_\alpha(S^c)\) by Lemma 2.1, since \(S^c \subseteq A\). That contradicts closedness.

So \(R'\) is the smallest closed set. By Theorem 2.3 so is StepM's output, and the smallest closed set is unique: two sets each contained in the other are equal. The same argument covers any rule that rejects at least one exceeding hypothesis per step.

P2 · The cost of independent resampling

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_m\) be independent with a common continuous, strictly increasing distribution function \(F\). (a) Show that the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of \(\max_i X_i\) is \(F^{-1}\big((1-\alpha)^{1/m}\big)\). (b) Show it exceeds \(F^{-1}(1-\alpha)\) for \(m \ge 2\). (c) Evaluate both for \(F = \Phi\), \(m = 10\), \(\alpha = 0.05\). (d) What is the quantile of \(\max_i X_i\) if instead \(X_1 = \dots = X_m\)?

Solution

(a) \(\Prob(\max_i X_i \le c) = \prod_i \Prob(X_i \le c) = F(c)^m\). Setting \(F(c)^m = 1-\alpha\) gives \(c = F^{-1}((1-\alpha)^{1/m})\), which is unique because \(F\) is continuous and strictly increasing.

(b) For \(0 \lt 1-\alpha \lt 1\) and \(m \ge 2\), \((1-\alpha)^{1/m} > 1-\alpha\), and \(F^{-1}\) is strictly increasing.

(c) \(\Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/10}) = \Phi^{-1}(0.99488) = 2.568\), against \(\Phi^{-1}(0.95) = 1.645\).

(d) The maximum is \(X_1\), so the quantile is \(F^{-1}(1-\alpha)\), which is 1.645 for the normal. Lab 3's two critical values, 1.601 and 2.566, are bootstrap estimates of these two numbers.

P3 · Leverage is not a new strategy

Strategy \(l\) is strategy \(k\) at leverage \(a > 0\): \(d_{l,t} = a\, d_{k,t}\) for all \(t\). Show that, under joint resampling, \(t_l = t_k\) and \(t^{\ast}_{b,l} = t^{\ast}_{b,k}\), so StepM treats \(l\) as a duplicate of \(k\). Show that a version based on the unstudentized statistics \(\sqrt{T}\,\bar d_k\) does not.

Solution

Scaling a series by \(a > 0\) scales its sample mean and sample standard deviation by \(a\), so \(t_l = a\bar d_k / (a s_k/\sqrt{T}) = t_k\). Under joint resampling the resampled column \(l\) is \(a\) times the resampled column \(k\) in every draw, so the same cancellation gives \(t^{\ast}_{b,l} = t^{\ast}_{b,k}\), and Proposition 4.1 applies.

Unstudentized, \(\sqrt{T}\,\bar d_l = a\sqrt{T}\,\bar d_k\), and the bootstrap statistics scale the same way. For \(a > 1\) the leveraged copy dominates the bootstrap maximum, raises the critical value, and can push the unleveraged version, or any third strategy, below the bar. Studentization makes StepM invariant to leverage, a property any sensible test of skill should have.

P4 · False coverage under any dependence

For each strategy \(k\) and level \(u \in (0,1)\), let \(C_k(u)\) be an interval with \(\Prob(\mu_k \notin C_k(u)) \le u\), nested so that \(C_k(u') \subseteq C_k(u)\) whenever \(u \le u'\). A rule selects a set \(S\) from the data, with \(R = |S|\), and reports \(C_k(Rq'/K)\) for each \(k \in S\), where \(q' = q/c(K)\) and \(c(K) = \sum_{j=1}^{K} 1/j\). Prove that \(\mathrm{FCR} \le q\) for every selection rule and every joint distribution of the data.

Solution

Let \(E_{k,j} = \{\mu_k \notin C_k(jq'/K)\}\) for \(j = 1, \dots, K\), with \(E_{k,0} = \varnothing\). Nesting makes \(E_{k,j}\) increasing in \(j\), and \(\Prob(E_{k,j}) \le jq'/K\). A selected strategy contributes to \(V_{\mathrm{CI}}\) when \(R = j\) only on \(E_{k,j}\), so \[ \mathrm{FCR} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{j=1}^{K} \frac1j\, \Prob\big(k \in S,\ R = j,\ E_{k,j}\big) \le \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{j=1}^{K} \frac1j\, \Prob\big(R = j,\ E_{k,j}\big). \] Split \(E_{k,j}\) into the disjoint pieces \(D_{k,l} = E_{k,l} \setminus E_{k,l-1}\), \(l \le j\). Since \(1/j \le 1/l\) for \(j \ge l\), \[ \sum_{j=1}^{K} \frac1j\, \Prob(R = j,\ E_{k,j}) = \sum_{j=1}^{K} \sum_{l=1}^{j} \frac1j\, \Prob(R = j,\ D_{k,l}) \le \sum_{l=1}^{K} \frac1l \sum_{j \ge l} \Prob(R = j,\ D_{k,l}) \le \sum_{l=1}^{K} \frac{e_l - e_{l-1}}{l}, \] with \(e_l = \Prob(E_{k,l})\). Summation by parts, as in Lemma 7.6, gives \[ \sum_{l=1}^{K} \frac{e_l - e_{l-1}}{l} = \sum_{l=1}^{K-1} e_l \Big(\frac1l - \frac{1}{l+1}\Big) + \frac{e_K}{K} \le \frac{q'}{K}\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{K-1} \frac{1}{l+1} + 1\Big) = \frac{q'}{K}\, c(K), \] because every coefficient is positive and \(e_l \le lq'/K\). Summing over the \(K\) strategies, \(\mathrm{FCR} \le q'\, c(K) = q\). The argument is the one behind Lesson 3's Theorem 5.5, and it uses nothing about the selection rule or the dependence. Dropping the factor \(c(K)\) needs conditions on the dependence; Benjamini and Yekutieli's abstract mentions results under positive regression dependency and under general dependency .

Let a single null differential follow the AR(1) process \(d_t = \phi d_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) with \(\phi = 0.3\). (a) Derive the limiting null variance of the iid-studentized \(t = \bar d/(s/\sqrt{T})\). (b) A bootstrap that resamples single dates produces \(t^{\ast}\) that is approximately standard normal. What rejection rate does it deliver at a nominal 5%? (c) Write the simulation that measures the rejection rate for mean block lengths 1, 5 and 20, and predict the pattern.

Solution

(a) \(\sqrt{T}\,\bar d\) has limiting variance equal to the long-run variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1-\phi)^2\), while \(s^2\) converges to the marginal variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1-\phi^2)\). Their ratio is \((1-\phi^2)/(1-\phi)^2 = (1+\phi)/(1-\phi) = 1.3/0.7 = 1.857\), so \(t\) has a limiting standard deviation of \(\sqrt{1.857} = 1.363\).

(b) With a critical value of about 1.645, the rejection rate is \(\Prob(\Normal(0, 1.857) > 1.645) = 1 - \Phi(1.645/1.363) = 1 - \Phi(1.207) = 0.114\): more than twice the nominal level.

(c) Prediction: mean block length 1 is the case in (b), so expect roughly 11%. Longer blocks let \(t^{\ast}\) inherit the long-run variance, and the rate should fall toward 5%. At block length 20 the bootstrap may become noisier, with only about 25 distinct blocks per draw. The simulation:

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED null AR(1) differentials, phi = 0.3: how often does a one-sided 5% bootstrap
# test of H0: mean <= 0 reject, as a function of the stationary bootstrap's mean block length?
rng = np.random.default_rng(52)
phi, n_obs, n_boot, n_reps, alpha = 0.3, 500, 299, 300, 0.05


def stationary_indices(n: int, mean_block: float, draws: int) -> NDArray[np.int64]:
    """draws x n stationary-bootstrap index vectors (Lesson 4, Definition 3.1)."""
    starts = rng.integers(0, n, size=(draws, n))
    new_block = rng.random((draws, n)) < 1.0 / mean_block
    new_block[:, 0] = True
    pos = np.arange(n)
    block_start = np.maximum.accumulate(np.where(new_block, pos, 0), axis=1)
    idx = (starts[np.arange(draws)[:, None], block_start] + pos - block_start) % n
    return np.asarray(idx, dtype=np.int64)


print("mean_block  rejection_rate")
for mean_block in (1.0, 5.0, 20.0):
    rejections = 0
    for _ in range(n_reps):
        e = rng.standard_normal(n_obs + 100)
        d = np.empty_like(e)
        d[0] = e[0]
        for t in range(1, e.size):
            d[t] = phi * d[t - 1] + e[t]
        d = d[100:]  # burn-in
        t_obs = d.mean() / (d.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_obs))
        sample = d[stationary_indices(n_obs, mean_block, n_boot)]
        t_star = (sample.mean(axis=1) - d.mean()) / (sample.std(axis=1, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_obs))
        critical = np.sort(t_star)[int(np.ceil((1 - alpha) * n_boot)) - 1]
        rejections += int(t_obs > critical)
    print(f"{mean_block:.0f} {rejections / n_reps:.3f}")

E3 · The noisiest strategy owns the maximum

Simulate Section 5's example: two independent strategies with per-period standard deviations 1 and 5, \(T = 250\), where the low-volatility strategy has a true t-statistic of 3 and the other has mean zero. Using oracle critical values, estimate how often the low-volatility strategy is rejected with and without studentization, and compare with the derived values.

Solution

Derived values: unstudentized, the critical value solves \(\Phi(c)\Phi(c/5) = 0.95\), giving \(c = 8.224\). The low-volatility strategy's statistic is approximately \(\Normal(3, 1)\), so it is rejected with probability \(1 - \Phi(5.224) \approx 9 \times 10^{-8}\). Studentized, \(c = \Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/2}) = 1.955\) and the probability is \(\Phi(1.045) = 0.852\).

Simulation: draw \(d_1 = 3/\sqrt{T} + \varepsilon_1\) and \(d_2 = 5\varepsilon_2\) over \(T\) periods with independent standard normal \(\varepsilon\), and form both statistics. The oracle critical value of the pair is the \(\lceil 0.95\,n \rceil\)-th smallest of their maximum over \(n = 200{,}000\) null draws. Count rejections of strategy 1 over 20,000 replications, at the first step and under StepM, whose second step tests strategy 1 alone (against its own oracle value) when strategy 2 is rejected first.

import math

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED: two independent strategies over T = 250 periods. Strategy 1 has per-period sd 1 and a true
# t-statistic of 3; strategy 2 has sd 5 and mean zero. Oracle critical values come from null draws of the pair.
rng = np.random.default_rng(503)
n_obs, n_null, n_alt, chunk, alpha = 250, 200_000, 20_000, 20_000, 0.05
root = math.sqrt(n_obs)


def statistics(n: int, edge: float) -> tuple[NDArray[np.float64], NDArray[np.float64]]:
    """n replications of the pair: unstudentized sqrt(T) * mean and studentized t, one column per strategy."""
    d1 = edge / root + rng.standard_normal((n, n_obs))
    d2 = 5.0 * rng.standard_normal((n, n_obs))
    raw = root * np.column_stack([d1.mean(axis=1), d2.mean(axis=1)])
    sd = np.column_stack([d1.std(axis=1, ddof=1), d2.std(axis=1, ddof=1)])
    return raw, raw / sd


def order_statistic(values: NDArray[np.float64]) -> float:
    """The ceil((1 - alpha) n)-th smallest value: the module's critical-value convention."""
    return float(np.sort(values)[math.ceil((1 - alpha) * values.size) - 1])


null = [statistics(chunk, 0.0) for _ in range(n_null // chunk)]
raw_alt, t_alt = statistics(n_alt, 3.0)
print("statistic  pair_critical_value  first_step_rate  stepm_rate")
for name, index, alt in (("unstudentized", 0, raw_alt), ("studentized", 1, t_alt)):
    pair = np.concatenate([s[index] for s in null])
    c_pair = order_statistic(pair.max(axis=1))
    c_single = order_statistic(pair[:, 0])  # step 2 when only strategy 1 is still active
    first = alt[:, 0] > c_pair
    stepm = first | ((alt[:, 1] > c_pair) & (alt[:, 0] > c_single))
    print(f"{name} {c_pair:.3f} {first.mean():.4f} {stepm.mean():.4f}")

Unstudentized, the first step rejects strategy 1 in none of the 20,000 replications, as the derived \(9 \times 10^{-8}\) predicts. Studentized, it rejects in 84.9% of them against the derived 0.852; the Monte Carlo standard error is about 0.0025, and the derivation's normal approximation to the t-statistic (critical value 1.955 against the simulated 1.959) accounts for part of the gap. StepM's later step adds little to the studentized test: 85.0% against a derived \(0.8521 + 0.0253 \times 0.0602 = 0.8536\), where 0.0253 is the chance that the null strategy 2 clears the pair's critical value and 0.0602 the chance that strategy 1 then lies between 1.645 and 1.955. It gives the unstudentized test its only rejections, 4.5% (derived \(0.050 \times 0.912 = 0.046\)). They occur only when the noisy null strategy is falsely rejected first and strategy 1 is then tested alone, so that power comes from a false discovery.

R1 · StepM for a multi-strategy book

A multi-strategy fund wants to know which of its 40 strategy sleeves have beaten their benchmarks over five years of daily returns, with family-wise error control at 10%. Specify the analysis completely.

Solution

A complete specification fixes each choice before any results are seen.

  1. Differentials. Each sleeve's net-of-cost return minus its own pre-declared benchmark, aligned on a common calendar. Missing dates are handled identically across sleeves (the joint resampling of Definition 1.1 requires a rectangular panel).
  2. Hypotheses. One-sided \(H_k : \mu_k \le 0\) for all 40 sleeves, including sleeves that were later closed: dropping losers after the fact is selection.
  3. Statistics. Studentized, ideally with a HAC standard error in both the data and every bootstrap draw, because daily strategy returns are autocorrelated and sleeves differ in their autocorrelation (§9).
  4. Bootstrap. Stationary bootstrap of whole dates, \(B \ge 1{,}999\), seeded, with results reported for several mean block lengths. A simulation at \(T \approx 1{,}260\) checks the procedure's size under a null calibrated to the data.
  5. Reporting. The rejected set, the step at which each sleeve was rejected, and the single-step comparison. For every sleeve, report the bootstrap sup-t band from the same draws (§6), which stays valid for whichever sleeves the analysis singles out; the point estimates of rejected sleeves carry the selection bias of §9. Accepted hypotheses are not proofs of zero skill.
R2 · “The Reality Check found nothing”

A paper applies White's Reality Check to 2,000 technical trading-rule variants and fails to reject. It concludes that none of the rules has predictive ability. Evaluate the inference.

Solution

Four problems, in decreasing order of importance.

  1. Failure to reject is not evidence of no skill. The Reality Check tests whether the best rule beats the benchmark, at a critical value inflated by 2,000 alternatives. Its power against a few modestly skilled rules among 2,000 can be low, so the conclusion needs a power calculation under plausible alternatives.
  2. The unstudentized maximum can be dominated by noisy rules. Section 5 shows how high-volatility rules set the bar. Studentizing, as Hansen's test for superior predictive ability and StepM do, can change the answer.
  3. A global test answers a global question. Even a rejection would say only that some rule has skill. A step-down procedure identifies which rules have it. With 2,000 hypotheses, false-discovery-rate control (Lesson 3) is often the more useful target.
  4. Dependence matters. The 2,000 variants are highly correlated, so joint resampling is essential (§4). An implementation that resampled rules independently would have overstated the multiplicity.

The defensible conclusion is narrower: at this sample length and level, the data do not establish that the best rule beats the benchmark.

R3 · Making k-StepM computable

Definition 7.9's critical value \(d_j\) maximizes a bootstrap quantile over up to \(\binom{|R_{j-1}|}{k-1}\) subsets, which cannot be enumerated once hundreds of strategies have been rejected. Propose a critical value that needs one pass over the bootstrap draws per step, prove that k-StepM with it still controls the k-FWER asymptotically, compare it with Romano and Wolf's streamlined and operative methods, and design a study that measures what the shortcut costs.

Solution

Proposal. Move the maximum over subsets inside the draws. For draw \(b\), let \(J_b\) be the \(\min(k-1, |R_{j-1}|)\) strategies in \(R_{j-1}\) with the largest \(t^{\ast}_{b,i}\), and let \(e_j\) be the \(\lceil (1-\alpha)B \rceil\)-th smallest over draws of \(\max^{(k)}_{i \in A_j \cup J_b} t^{\ast}_{b,i}\). Each draw needs the top \(k\) values of \(A_j\) and the top \(k-1\) of \(R_{j-1}\), one partial sort; Romano and Wolf's three algorithms do not include this construction.

It bounds \(d_j\) from above. Fix a draw, an admissible \(I \subseteq R_{j-1}\) with \(|I| \le k-1\), and let \(x\) be the \(k\)-th largest of the draw's statistics over \(A_j \cup I\). At least \(k\) of them are at least \(x\): say \(n_A\) in \(A_j\) and \(n_I\) in \(I\). Since \(I \subseteq R_{j-1}\), at least \(n_I\) strategies of \(R_{j-1}\) have statistics at least \(x\), and \(J_b\), the top \(|J_b| \ge |I| \ge n_I\) of \(R_{j-1}\), contains at least \(n_I\) of them. So \(A_j \cup J_b\) has at least \(n_A + n_I \ge k\) statistics at least \(x\), and its \(k\)-th largest is at least \(x\). The inequality holds draw by draw, and order statistics keep it (Lemma 2.1), so \(c^{(k)}_\alpha(A_j \cup I) \le e_j\) for every admissible \(I\), and \(d_j \le e_j\).

Control. The proof of Theorem 7.10 uses the critical values only through \(d_{j'} \ge c^{(k)}_\alpha(I_0)\) at the steps up to the \(k\)-th false rejection, with \(I' = R_{j'-1} \cap I_0\) admissible. Since \(e_{j'} \ge d_{j'}\), the same chain gives \(e_{j'} \ge c^{(k)}_\alpha(I_0)\), and the conclusion of Theorem 7.10 holds under the same assumptions. The argument is pathwise, so with exact rather than bootstrap quantiles it would give finite-sample control, as the generic algorithm does.

Comparison. The generic algorithm is the benchmark: at any given rejected set, \(e_j \ge d_j\), so the shortcut is the more conservative of the two. The streamlined algorithm adds back only the \(k-1\) least significant rejections; it rejects at least as much as the generic one and is justified asymptotically, but it would not control the k-FWER in finite samples with exact critical values . The operative method enumerates subsets of a capped number of the least significant rejections . The shortcut therefore keeps the generic algorithm's finite-sample logic at a price in power, while the streamlined algorithm buys power with a weaker argument.

Where the price comes from. The bootstrap statistics are centred, so a rejected strategy's \(t^{\ast}_{b,i}\) is noise, however strong its alpha. \(J_b\) picks the \(k-1\) largest of \(|R_{j-1}|\) noise values in each draw, and their size grows with \(|R_{j-1}|\), as the expected maximum of Lesson 1 does. The generic algorithm maximizes quantiles over fixed subsets, not draws, and pays less. The loss should therefore grow with the number of strategies rejected before the step in question.

Study. Simulate Lab 4's universe with returns at \(K = 40\), so that the generic algorithm is still enumerable for \(k \le 3\), with 5, 10 and 20 true alphas and correlations 0.3 and 0.9. Run the generic, streamlined, operative (\(N_{\max} = 50\)) and shortcut versions on the same bootstrap draws. Report the realized k-FWER with its Monte Carlo standard error (all four should be near or below \(\alpha\)), the mean number of true alphas found, and run time. Prediction: the shortcut matches the generic algorithm when few are rejected and falls behind as the number of true alphas grows; the streamlined algorithm leads on power throughout. A shortcut that loses little at the sizes a desk actually meets is worth its finite-sample logic; one that loses much is not.

R4 · The in-sample winner

A desk backtests 20 strategies, picks the one with the largest t-statistic, and reports its mean differential with the conventional 95% interval \(\bar d \pm 1.96\, s/\sqrt{T}\). (a) Show that this interval's coverage of the winner's true mean can be far below 95%. (b) Give an interval for the winner with a valid coverage guarantee, whatever rule picked it. (c) Say what else the literature offers. (d) Specify what the desk should report.

Solution

(a) Take the global null, \(\mu_k = 0\) for all \(k\), with independent and approximately standard normal statistics. The winner \(\hat k\) maximizes \(t_k\), and its interval covers \(\mu_{\hat k} = 0\) exactly when \(|t_{\hat k}| \le 1.96\), that is, when \(-1.96 \le \max_k t_k \le 1.96\). That has probability \(\Phi(1.96)^{20} - \Phi(-1.96)^{20} = 0.975^{20} - 0.025^{20} = 0.603\). The nominal 95% interval covers 60% of the time. The estimate is biased upward too: under this null, \(\bar d_{\hat k} - \mu_{\hat k}\) is \(s_{\hat k}/\sqrt{T}\) times \(\max_k \tau_k\), whose mean is the positive expected maximum of Lesson 1. Correlation between the strategies raises the coverage toward 95%, because the 20 become fewer independent bets. With genuine alphas in the book, the size of the bias depends on how far the winner's true mean stands above the others', and a simulation at the desk's own design measures it.

(b) The sup-t band. Whatever rule picks \(\hat k\), the event that every interval covers its mean implies that the winner's does, so \(\Prob(\mu_{\hat k} \in C_{\hat k}) \ge \Prob(\mu_k \in C_k \text{ for every } k)\), which tends to \(1-\alpha\) by Theorem 6.3. In Lab 4's universe at correlation 0.3 its critical value was 3.031, so the interval is \(3.031/1.960 = 1.55\) times as wide as the conventional one. It guards against every selection rule, not only the maximum, and that generality is its cost.

(c) Two alternatives aim narrower. Sample splitting selects on one part of the sample and estimates on another; it is valid when the parts are independent, which for serially dependent returns needs a gap between them, and it costs half the data for each task. Andrews, Kitagawa and McCloskey develop estimators and confidence intervals that eliminate the winner's curse; applied to job-training programs selected on estimated earnings effects and to neighborhoods selected on estimated economic opportunity, their corrections can make an economically significant difference to conclusions but still allow informative inference. Their abstract states the problem as this solution's part (a) does: the curse biases estimates for selected options upward and can invalidate conventional confidence intervals.

(d) Report the number of strategies tried and the selection rule; the winner's estimate with its sup-t interval from the joint bootstrap, and a sample-split or winner's-curse-corrected interval if one was computed; and the StepM decision on the winner. Never report the conventional interval alone. A simulation at the desk's own \(K\), \(T\) and estimated correlation shows how far below 95% the conventional interval falls in its case.

  1. After StepM rejects some hypotheses, over which strategies is the next critical value computed?

    Options

    Each step recomputes \(c_\alpha(A_j)\) over the active set, so the rejected strategies stop inflating the bar.

  2. With the same bootstrap draws, how do StepM's rejections compare with the single-step test's?

    Options

    StepM's first step is the single-step test, and later steps only add rejections (Proposition 2.2).

  3. With \(B = 999\) bootstrap draws and \(\alpha = 0.05\), which order statistic of the bootstrap maxima is the critical value? (an integer rank)

    \(\lceil 0.95 \times 999 \rceil = \lceil 949.05 \rceil = 950\).

  4. Why does StepM resample whole dates, applying one index vector to every strategy?

    Options

    Proposition 4.1: under joint resampling duplicates add no multiplicity; under independent resampling the critical value is inflated.

  5. What is the 95% quantile of the maximum of 10 independent standard normals? (three decimals)

    \(\Phi^{-1}(0.95^{1/10}) = \Phi^{-1}(0.99488) = 2.568\).

  6. If every hypothesis is true, how do the family-wise error rates of StepM and the single-step test compare (same draws)?

    Options

    Any StepM rejection requires a first-step rejection, which is the single-step test (Proposition 3.2).

  7. What problem does studentizing the statistics solve?

    Options

    Section 5: unstudentized, the noisy strategy pushes the critical value to 8.224, while studentized it is 1.955.

  8. Under strong positive correlation between strategies, how do StepM's critical values compare with Holm's?

    Options

    Holm's \(\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/a)\) ignores dependence. StepM's bootstrap quantile falls toward 1.645 as the strategies become one bet.

  9. What kind of family-wise error control does StepM provide?

    Options

    Theorem 3.1 bounds a limit. Finite-sample size depends on \(T\), \(B\) and the block length (§9).

  10. StepM rejects a strategy's null. Is its backtested mean an unbiased estimate of its edge?

    Options

    StepM controls false claims of skill, not the size of the claimed skill. Selection bias (Lesson 1) remains.

  11. With the same bootstrap draws, how does the sup-t band's critical value \(c^{\pm}_\alpha\) compare with StepM's first-step critical value \(c_\alpha(\{1, \dots, K\})\)?

    Options

    Draw by draw, \(\max_k t^{\ast}_{b,k} \le \max_k |t^{\ast}_{b,k}|\), and order statistics keep the inequality (Proposition 6.4(b)). The band is two-sided and must cover every mean, including those StepM has rejected.

  12. Lehmann and Romano's k-FWER step-down with \(m = 100\) hypotheses, \(k = 3\) and \(\alpha = 0.05\): what is the constant \(\alpha_{10}\)? (four significant digits)

    Since \(10 > k\), \(\alpha_{10} = k\alpha/(m + k - 10) = 0.15/93 = 0.001613\) (Theorem 7.4).

  13. What does controlling the false discovery exceedance at \((\gamma, \alpha)\) guarantee?

    Options

  14. A currency rule's return is computed from spot rates, as in Lab 6. What does it leave out?

    Options
