A Sharpe ratio is two estimates divided into each other, and its standard error depends on the shape of the return distribution. A short option position collects a small premium in most periods and pays a large loss in a few, so its return distribution has a long left tail: negative skewness and high kurtosis. For returns of that shape the textbook standard error flatters the strategy. This lesson derives the correct standard error and turns it into the probabilistic Sharpe ratio and a minimum track-record length. It raises the bar to the luck hurdle of Lesson 1, giving the deflated Sharpe ratio, confronts an estimated input, the number of independent trials, and tests whether two Sharpe ratios differ.

1The Sharpe ratio under skewness and kurtosis

Let \(r_1, \dots, r_T\) be per-period excess returns with mean \(\mu\), standard deviation \(\sigma > 0\), skewness \(\gamma_3 = \E[(r-\mu)^3]/\sigma^3\) and kurtosis \(\gamma_4 = \E[(r-\mu)^4]/\sigma^4\) (not excess: 3 for a normal). The population Sharpe ratio is \(\SR = \mu/\sigma\), and its estimate is \(\hSR = \bar r / s\) with \(s\) the sample standard deviation. In S1 you derived the iid normal standard error. Here we drop normality but keep independence.

Definition 1.1 (non-normal Sharpe-ratio variance)

The non-normal Sharpe-ratio variance is the asymptotic variance of \(\sqrt{T}(\hSR - \SR)\) for iid returns with skewness \(\gamma_3\) and kurtosis \(\gamma_4\): \[ V(\SR, \gamma_3, \gamma_4) = 1 - \gamma_3\, \SR + \frac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\, \SR^2. \tag{1}\]

Theorem 1.2 (asymptotic distribution of the Sharpe ratio)

Let \(r_1, r_2, \dots\) be iid with \(\E[r^4] \lt \infty\) and \(\sigma > 0\). Then \[ \sqrt{T}\,\big(\hSR - \SR\big) \dto \Normal\big(0,\; V(\SR, \gamma_3, \gamma_4)\big). \]

Proof

Step 1: a joint central limit theorem. Write \(\nu_k = \E[(r-\mu)^k]\), so \(\nu_2 = \sigma^2\), \(\nu_3 = \gamma_3\sigma^3\), \(\nu_4 = \gamma_4\sigma^4\). The vectors \(X_t = \big(r_t - \mu,\; (r_t-\mu)^2 - \sigma^2\big)\) are iid with mean zero and, because \(\E[r^4] \lt \infty\), finite covariance matrix \[ \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2 & \nu_3 \\ \nu_3 & \nu_4 - \sigma^4 \end{pmatrix}. \] The multivariate central limit theorem gives \(\sqrt{T}\,\bar X_T \dto \Normal(0, \Sigma)\).

Step 2: the sample variance behaves like its leading term. Expanding around \(\mu\), \[ s^2 = \frac{T}{T-1}\Big(\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T (r_t-\mu)^2 - (\bar r - \mu)^2\Big). \] Since \(\sqrt{T}(\bar r - \mu)\) converges in distribution, \(\sqrt{T}(\bar r - \mu)^2 \pto 0\), and \(T/(T-1) \to 1\). Hence \(\sqrt{T}\big((\bar r, s^2) - (\mu, \sigma^2)\big) = \sqrt{T}\,\bar X_T + o_p(1)\), and by Slutsky's lemma it has the same limit \(\Normal(0, \Sigma)\).

Step 3: the delta method. \(\hSR = g(\bar r, s^2)\) with \(g(y, v) = y/\sqrt{v}\), differentiable at \((\mu, \sigma^2)\) because \(\sigma > 0\), with gradient \(\nabla g = \big(1/\sigma,\; -\mu/(2\sigma^3)\big)\). Therefore \(\sqrt{T}(\hSR - \SR) \dto \Normal(0, \nabla g^\top \Sigma\, \nabla g)\), and \[ \nabla g^\top \Sigma\, \nabla g = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2} - 2\,\frac{1}{\sigma}\,\frac{\mu}{2\sigma^3}\,\nu_3 + \frac{\mu^2}{4\sigma^6}\big(\nu_4 - \sigma^4\big) = 1 - \SR\,\gamma_3 + \SR^2\,\frac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}. \]

Corollary 1.3 (the normal case)

For normal returns \(\gamma_3 = 0\) and \(\gamma_4 = 3\), so \(V = 1 + \SR^2/2\): the iid normal variance from S1 is the special case of (1), not a separate result.

Example 1.4 (a short-volatility profile)

Take a daily Sharpe ratio of 0.1 (annualized \(0.1\sqrt{252} = 1.59\)), skewness \(-2\) and kurtosis 9, the moments of a mirrored exponential shock. Then \[ V = 1 - (-2)(0.1) + \tfrac{9-1}{4}(0.1)^2 = 1 + 0.2 + 0.02 = 1.22, \] against \(1 + 0.1^2/2 = 1.005\) under normality. With \(T = 1000\) the standard errors are \(\sqrt{1.22/999} = 0.0349\) and \(\sqrt{1.005/999} = 0.0317\): the normal formula understates the uncertainty by a factor of \(\sqrt{1.22/1.005} = 1.10\), almost entirely through the skewness term \(-\gamma_3\SR\).

Lab 1 · Checking the non-normal variance by simulation

For SIMULATED returns with daily Sharpe ratio 0.1 and kurtosis 9, the simulated value of \(T\cdot\Var(\hSR)\) should match formula (1): 0.82 with skewness \(+2\) and 1.22 with skewness \(-2\). The normal formula, 1.005, should miss both.

import numpy as np

# SIMULATED returns r = mu + sigma * Z with Z standardized exponential (skewness 2,
# kurtosis 9) or its mirror image (skewness -2, kurtosis 9), daily Sharpe ratio 0.1.
rng = np.random.default_rng(707)
n_obs, n_reps, sr = 1000, 10000, 0.1


def simulated_scaled_variance(sign: float) -> float:
    """Monte Carlo estimate of T * Var(estimated Sharpe ratio), per-period units."""
    z = sign * (rng.exponential(1.0, (n_reps, n_obs)) - 1.0)
    r = sr + z  # mu / sigma = sr with sigma = 1; the Sharpe ratio is scale-free
    estimates = r.mean(axis=1) / r.std(axis=1, ddof=1)
    return float(n_obs * estimates.var(ddof=1))


print("skewness  kurtosis  normal_formula  nonnormal_formula  simulated")

Formula (1) lands within Monte Carlo error of the simulation in both directions. With 10,000 replications the standard error of a simulated variance is about \(V\sqrt{2/9999}\), roughly 0.012 at \(V = 0.82\) and 0.017 at \(V = 1.22\). Positive skewness earns a tighter interval and negative skewness a wider one, at the same Sharpe ratio. The lab says nothing about serially correlated returns, which Theorem 1.2 excludes by assumption.

Remark 1.5 (the assumptions, and who relaxes them)

Theorem 1.2 needs independence. Lo derives the Sharpe ratio's distribution under iid and under stationary returns, and shows that monthly Sharpe ratios cannot be annualized by multiplying by \(\sqrt{12}\) except in special circumstances, because serial correlation can overstate an annual Sharpe ratio substantially; F7 builds that correction. Opdyke presents a formula for the Sharpe ratio's asymptotic distribution that he states is valid for stationary and ergodic returns, permitting time-varying volatility and serial correlation, and Bailey and López de Prado read that result as showing that formula (1) itself holds beyond iid returns. That reading conflicts with Lo's, and a short calculation shows why it cannot hold with ordinary moments. For a small Sharpe ratio the leading term of \(T\cdot\Var(\hSR)\) is \(T\cdot\Var(\bar r)/\sigma^2\), because every term involving the estimated standard deviation is of order \(\SR\) or smaller. For AR(1) returns with coefficient \(\phi\), Lesson 4 (Section 3) showed that \(T\cdot\Var(\bar r)/\sigma^2 \to (1+\phi)/(1-\phi)\), where \(\sigma^2\) is the marginal variance. At \(\phi = 0.3\) that is \(1.3/0.7 = 1.86\), against a value near 1 from formula (1): the variance depends on the autocorrelations, which (1) does not contain. López de Prado, Lipton and Zoonekynd propose a closed-form approximation to the Sharpe ratio's sampling distribution for returns that are jointly non-normal and serially correlated, a problem they describe as long-standing and open; Lo's derivation for stationary returns, above, is the earlier answer that claim must be weighed against. Formula (1) is the iid result proved here, and it is the case the deflated Sharpe ratio below uses.

2The probabilistic Sharpe ratio and the minimum track record

Theorem 1.2 turns a Sharpe ratio into a test statistic. Plugging in estimates, and following the conventions of the A8 contract (moment estimators for \(\hat\gamma_3, \hat\gamma_4\), and \(T-1\) in the standard error), gives the probabilistic Sharpe ratio .

Definition 2.1 (probabilistic Sharpe ratio)

For a benchmark Sharpe ratio \(\SR^\ast\) in the same per-period units, the probabilistic Sharpe ratio is \[ \PSR(\SR^\ast) = \Phi\!\left( \frac{(\hSR - \SR^\ast)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1 - \hat\gamma_3\,\hSR + \frac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\,\hSR^2}} \right), \tag{2}\] with \(\hat\gamma_3 = m_3/m_2^{3/2}\), \(\hat\gamma_4 = m_4/m_2^{2}\) and \(m_k = \frac1T\sum_t (r_t - \bar r)^k\).

Proposition 2.2 (what PSR is)

\(\PSR(\SR^\ast) = 1 - p\), where \(p\) is the one-sided asymptotic p-value of the null \(\SR \le \SR^\ast\) based on Theorem 1.2. In particular \(\PSR(\hSR) = 1/2\), and \(\PSR\) is strictly decreasing in \(\SR^\ast\).

Proof

Under the boundary null \(\SR = \SR^\ast\), Theorem 1.2 makes \(Z = (\hSR - \SR^\ast)/\widehat{\se}\) approximately standard normal, with \(\widehat{\se}\) the plug-in standard error in the denominator of (2). The one-sided p-value is \(p = \Prob(Z' \ge z) = 1 - \Phi(z)\) at the observed \(z\), so \(\PSR = \Phi(z) = 1 - p\). At \(\SR^\ast = \hSR\), \(z = 0\) and \(\Phi(0) = 1/2\). The argument of \(\Phi\) is affine in \(\SR^\ast\) with negative slope \(-1/\widehat{\se}\), and \(\Phi\) is strictly increasing.

Example 2.3 (three years of daily returns)

A strategy shows \(\hSR = 0.09\) per day (annualized \(0.09\sqrt{252} = 1.43\)) over \(T = 756\) days, with \(\hat\gamma_3 = -1.5\) and \(\hat\gamma_4 = 8\). The bracket is \(1 + 1.5 \times 0.09 + \tfrac{7}{4}(0.0081) = 1.1492\), so \(\widehat{\se} = \sqrt{1.1492/755} = 0.0390\) and \(\PSR(0) = \Phi(0.09/0.0390) = \Phi(2.307) = 0.989\). Ignoring the skewness and kurtosis gives \(\widehat{\se} = \sqrt{1.00405/755} = 0.0365\) and \(\Phi(2.468) = 0.993\). The difference looks small at this level of evidence, but it compounds once the benchmark is raised (Section 3).

Remark 2.4 (the name is not a posterior)

By Proposition 2.2, \(\PSR\) is one minus a p-value: a statement about how often a statistic this large would arise if the true Sharpe ratio were \(\SR^\ast\). It is not the probability that the strategy is good given the data. That needs prior odds, which Lesson 6 supplies through the minimum Bayes factor.

Proposition 2.2 fixes the record and measures the evidence. An allocator often needs the reverse calculation: the record length at which statistics like these would clear the benchmark.

Definition 2.5 (minimum track-record length)

Fix a benchmark \(\SR^\ast\), a level \(\alpha \in (0, \tfrac12)\) and estimates \(\hSR \gt \SR^\ast\), \(\hat\gamma_3\), \(\hat\gamma_4\), and read (2) as a function \(\PSR_T(\SR^\ast)\) of the number of observations \(T\), with the estimates held fixed. The minimum track-record length is \[ \mathrm{MinTRL} = \inf\big\{T \ge 1 : \PSR_T(\SR^\ast) \ge 1 - \alpha\big\}, \] with \(T\) treated as a real number. A record needs at least \(\lceil \mathrm{MinTRL} \rceil\) observations.

Proposition 2.6 (MinTRL in closed form)

Let \(z_{1-\alpha} = \Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)\), and let \(\hat b = 1 - \hat\gamma_3\hSR + \tfrac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\hSR^2\) be the bracket of (2), assumed positive. (i) \[ \mathrm{MinTRL} = 1 + \hat b\,\Big(\frac{z_{1-\alpha}}{\hSR - \SR^\ast}\Big)^2, \] and \(\PSR_T(\SR^\ast) \ge 1 - \alpha\) if and only if \(T \ge \mathrm{MinTRL}\). (ii) With \(\hSR\) and the moments fixed, \(\mathrm{MinTRL} - 1\) is proportional to \((\hSR - \SR^\ast)^{-2}\): halving the margin over the benchmark quadruples it. (iii) \(\partial\,\mathrm{MinTRL}/\partial\hat\gamma_3 = -\hSR\,z_{1-\alpha}^2/(\hSR - \SR^\ast)^2\) and \(\partial\,\mathrm{MinTRL}/\partial\hat\gamma_4 = \hSR^2 z_{1-\alpha}^2/\big(4(\hSR - \SR^\ast)^2\big)\). For \(\hSR \gt 0\), more negative skewness and fatter tails both lengthen the required record. (iv) As \(\hSR \downarrow \SR^\ast\) with the moments fixed, \(\mathrm{MinTRL} \to \infty\) at the rate \((\hSR - \SR^\ast)^{-2}\), provided the bracket evaluated at \(\SR^\ast\) is positive. If \(\hSR \le \SR^\ast\), no record length gives \(\PSR_T(\SR^\ast) \ge 1 - \alpha\).

Proof

Write \(\Delta = \hSR - \SR^\ast\) and \(z = z_{1-\alpha}\), which is positive because \(\alpha \lt \tfrac12\). (i) By (2), \(\PSR_T(\SR^\ast) = \Phi\big(\Delta\sqrt{T-1}/\sqrt{\hat b}\big)\). For \(\Delta \gt 0\) this is continuous and strictly increasing in \(T \ge 1\), because \(\Phi\) is. Hence \(\PSR_T(\SR^\ast) \ge 1 - \alpha\) if and only if \(\Delta\sqrt{T-1}/\sqrt{\hat b} \ge z\). Both sides are non-negative, so squaring preserves the inequality: \(T - 1 \ge \hat b z^2/\Delta^2\). The smallest such \(T\) is \(1 + \hat b z^2/\Delta^2\), and every larger \(T\) qualifies. (ii) \(\SR^\ast\) enters (i) only through \(\Delta^{-2}\); \(\hat b\) does not depend on it. (iii) \(\hat b\) is affine in \(\hat\gamma_3\) with slope \(-\hSR\) and in \(\hat\gamma_4\) with slope \(\hSR^2/4\); multiply each slope by \(z^2/\Delta^2\). (iv) \(\hat b\) is continuous in \(\hSR\), so \((\mathrm{MinTRL} - 1)\,\Delta^2 = \hat b z^2\) tends to \(z^2\) times the bracket at \(\SR^\ast\), which is positive by assumption. If \(\Delta \le 0\), the argument of \(\Phi\) is at most 0 for every \(T\), so \(\PSR_T(\SR^\ast) \le \tfrac12 \lt 1 - \alpha\).

Remark 2.7 (the source's formula, and its convention)

Bailey and López de Prado define the minimum track-record length through the set \(\{n \mid \widehat{\PSR}(\SR^\ast) \gt 1 - \alpha\}\) of record lengths \(n\), with the PSR of their Eq. (11), which standardizes by \(\sqrt{n-1}\), and give \(\mathrm{MinTRL} = n^\ast = 1 + \big[1 - \hat\gamma_3\hSR + \tfrac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\hSR^2\big]\big(Z_\alpha/(\hSR - \SR^\ast)\big)^2\). Definition 2.1 uses the same \(T - 1\) convention (the A8 contract's), so Proposition 2.6(i) is their Eq. (13) term for term. The leading 1 is the footprint of that convention: a PSR standardized by \(\sqrt{T}\), the scaling of Theorem 1.2, would give \(\hat b\,\big(z_{1-\alpha}/(\hSR - \SR^\ast)\big)^2\), one observation fewer. Two smaller differences remain. Their set uses a strict inequality, which changes the whole number of observations required only when MinTRL is itself a whole number. And their \(Z_\alpha\) enters squared, so its sign convention does not matter. They stress that MinTRL counts observations, not calendar time, and that it rests on an asymptotic distribution. Their worked numbers follow from Proposition 2.6(i). With normal returns (\(\hat\gamma_3 = 0\), \(\hat\gamma_4 = 3\)), showing that an annualized Sharpe ratio of 2 beats a benchmark of 1 at 95% needs 688.2 daily, 147.1 weekly or 38.88 monthly observations: 2.73, 2.83 and 3.24 years at 252, 52 and 12 periods a year. With the monthly skewness \(-0.72\) and kurtosis 5.78 they take for a hedge-fund index, it needs 59.90 months, or 4.99 years. These are the values they report: 2.73, 2.83, 3.24 and 4.99 years, and 59.895 months in their appendix.

Example 2.8 (how long Example 2.3's record had to be)

For the strategy of Example 2.3, \(\hat b = 1.1492\) and \(z_{0.95} = 1.6449\). Against zero, \((1.6449/0.09)^2 = 334.04\), so \[ \mathrm{MinTRL} = 1 + 1.1492 \times 334.04 = 384.9: \] 385 days, or 1.53 years. The record's 756 days clear it, as \(\PSR(0) = 0.989 \ge 0.95\) already said (Proposition 2.6(i)). With the normal bracket 1.00405 the answer is \(1 + 1.00405 \times 334.04 = 336.4\), or 337 days, so the skewness and kurtosis cost 48 days, 14% more. Against an annualized benchmark of 0.5, \(\SR^\ast = 0.5/\sqrt{252} = 0.031497\) per day, the margin is 0.058503, \((1.6449/0.058503)^2 = 790.5\), and \(\mathrm{MinTRL} = 1 + 1.1492 \times 790.5 = 909.4\): 910 days, or 3.61 years. That is longer than the record, which matches \(\PSR(0.0315) = \Phi\big((0.09 - 0.0315)/0.0390\big) = \Phi(1.50) = 0.933 \lt 0.95\).

MinTRL counts periods. With \(q\) periods a year and annualized Sharpe ratios \(\SR_{\text{ann}} = \sqrt{q}\,\hSR\) and \(\SR^\ast_{\text{ann}} = \sqrt{q}\,\SR^\ast\), Proposition 2.6(i) becomes \[ \frac{\mathrm{MinTRL} - 1}{q} = \hat b\,\Big(\frac{z_{1-\alpha}}{\SR_{\text{ann}} - \SR^\ast_{\text{ann}}}\Big)^2 \ \text{years}. \] For daily data \(\hSR\) is small, so \(\hat b\) is close to \(1 - \hat\gamma_3\hSR\), and close to 1 unless the skewness is large. The number of years is then roughly the square of \(z_{1-\alpha}\) divided by the square of the annualized margin. At 95%, \(z_{0.95}^2 = 2.706\). With near-normal daily returns, showing that an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1 is positive takes about 2.7 years; for 0.5 it takes about 10.8 years, and for 2 about 0.68 years. These lengths assume iid returns and ignore any search, which Section 3 prices.

Remark 2.9 (a break-even length, not a power calculation)

MinTRL answers a question about a record already in hand. Used to plan a new record from a believed true Sharpe ratio \(\SR\) and true moments, with \(b\) the bracket at those values, it is a break-even length, not a power calculation. By Theorem 1.2, the statistic \(Z_T = (\hSR - \SR^\ast)\sqrt{T-1}/\sqrt{\hat b}\) inside (2) is approximately normal with mean \((\SR - \SR^\ast)\sqrt{T-1}/\sqrt{b}\) and, when the margin \(\SR - \SR^\ast\) is small, standard deviation close to 1. At \(T = \mathrm{MinTRL}\) computed from the true values the mean is exactly \(z_{1-\alpha}\), so the test passes with probability about \(\tfrac12\) and the median PSR is about \(1 - \alpha\). A pass probability of \(1 - \beta\) needs the mean at \(z_{1-\alpha} + z_{1-\beta}\): \[ T_\beta = 1 + b\,\Big(\frac{z_{1-\alpha} + z_{1-\beta}}{\SR - \SR^\ast}\Big)^2. \] At \(\alpha = 0.05\) and 80% power, \(T_\beta - 1\) is \((1.6449 + 0.8416)^2/1.6449^2 = 2.29\) times \(\mathrm{MinTRL} - 1\). Lab 4 (Section 5) checks both statements by simulation. López de Prado, Lipton and Zoonekynd list ignoring test power and minimum sample-length requirements among five recurring pitfalls of Sharpe-ratio inference.

Remark 2.10 (MinTRL inherits the iid assumption)

Proposition 2.6 solves (2) for \(T\), so MinTRL inherits every assumption of Theorem 1.2, independence first. Bailey and López de Prado state that their results hold beyond iid returns, for stationary and ergodic returns, on the strength of Opdyke's result. Remark 1.5 rejects that reading, and Ledoit and Wolf reject its basis: they state that Opdyke's formulae for the time-series case are incorrect because they are equivalent to his formulae for iid data, against Opdyke's own statement (Remark 1.5). The calculation of Remark 1.5 shows what changes. \(\mathrm{MinTRL} - 1\) is proportional to the variance of \(\hSR\). For AR(1) returns with coefficient \(\phi\) and a small Sharpe ratio, the leading term of that variance is \((1+\phi)/(1-\phi)\) times its iid value, so the iid formula understates the required record by roughly that factor. At \(\phi = 0.3\) the factor is 1.86, which turns Example 2.8's 385 days into about \(1 + 1.86 \times 383.9 = 715\). Negative autocorrelation works the other way. For smoothed or illiquid returns (Section 6), the bracket \(\hat b\) must be replaced by a dependence-robust variance: Lo's correction, built in F7, or a stationary-bootstrap estimate (Problem R3).

3The deflated Sharpe ratio

PSR against zero answers the single-test question of S1. After a search over \(N\) trials, zero is the wrong benchmark. Lesson 1 showed that the best of \(N\) skill-free trials has an expected maximum Sharpe ratio well above zero. The deflated Sharpe ratio raises the benchmark to that hurdle.

Definition 3.1 (deflated Sharpe ratio)

Let \(\{\hSR_n\}_{n=1}^N\) be the estimated Sharpe ratios of all \(N\) trials, including the selected one, and \(\widehat{\Var}[\{\hSR_n\}]\) their sample variance across trials (divisor \(N-1\)). The deflated Sharpe ratio of the selected strategy is \(\DSR = \PSR(\SR_0)\) with \[ \SR_0 = \sqrt{\widehat{\Var}[\{\hSR_n\}]}\,\Big((1-\emgamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\!\big(1 - \tfrac1N\big) + \emgamma\,\Phi^{-1}\!\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{Ne}\big)\Big), \tag{3}\] the approximation (3) of Lesson 1 to the expected maximum of \(N\) independent null trials. Bailey and López de Prado propose this statistic to correct a selected Sharpe ratio for selection bias under multiple testing and for non-normal returns. They define \(N\) as the number of independent trials and, for \(M\) correlated trials with average correlation \(\hat\rho\), propose the implied count \(\hat N = \hat\rho + (1-\hat\rho)M\), used with the cross-sectional variance of the trials' Sharpe ratios. This lesson, like the A8 contract, uses the raw count \(N\) of all trials with that variance. Section 4 shows why: under equicorrelation the source's pairing counts the correlation twice (Proposition 4.2).

Every ingredient of DSR is now derived: the standard error from Theorem 1.2, the hurdle from Lesson 1. What the definition adds is the decision to set the null at the expected maximum. That benchmark is the average luck of the best null trial, not an upper quantile, so \(\DSR \gt 0.95\) asks that the selected Sharpe ratio exceed the average best-of-\(N\) luck by about 1.64 standard errors. López de Prado and Porcu describe this location benchmark as the original form of DSR, and propose a variant, DSR-LS, that adds the dispersion of the selected maximum while keeping a location-and-scale form compatible with PSR and MinTRL; Problem R4 builds the location-benchmark version of that MinTRL.

Example 3.2 (the same strategy, chosen from forty trials)

Suppose the strategy of Example 2.3 was the best of \(N = 40\) trials whose Sharpe ratios have a cross-sectional standard deviation of 0.03 per day. The bracket in (3) at \(N = 40\) is 2.1895, so \(\SR_0 = 0.03 \times 2.1895 = 0.0657\) per day, or 1.04 annualized. Then \[ \DSR = \Phi\!\Big(\frac{0.09 - 0.0657}{0.0390}\Big) = \Phi(0.623) = 0.733. \] A track record that cleared zero with \(\PSR = 0.989\) does not clear the luck of forty trials.

Lab 2 · PSR and DSR as the search grows

A SIMULATED strategy with a true daily Sharpe ratio of 0.06 and skewness \(-2\) should clear zero with a PSR close to 1. Its DSR should fall steadily as the same record is presented as the winner of 2, 10, 100 and 1000 trials, because the hurdle \(\SR_0\) grows with \(N\).

import math
from statistics import NormalDist

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED: one proposed strategy (true daily Sharpe ratio 0.06, skewness -2) chosen
# from N trials; the other N - 1 trials are skill-free. Five years of daily data.
rng = np.random.default_rng(2707)
phi, gamma_e, n_obs = NormalDist(), 0.5772156649015329, 1260


def moments(r: NDArray[np.float64]) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Sharpe ratio (ddof=1), skewness and kurtosis (moment estimators), per period."""
    c = r - r.mean()
    m2, m3, m4 = (float(np.mean(c**k)) for k in (2, 3, 4))
    return float(r.mean() / r.std(ddof=1)), m3 / m2**1.5, m4 / m2**2


def psr(r: NDArray[np.float64], benchmark: float) -> float:
    """Probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds `benchmark` (per-period units)."""
    sr, g3, g4 = moments(r)
    se = math.sqrt((1.0 - g3 * sr + (g4 - 1.0) / 4.0 * sr**2) / (r.size - 1))
    return phi.cdf((sr - benchmark) / se)


def expected_max_z(n: int) -> float:
    return (1 - gamma_e) * phi.inv_cdf(1 - 1 / n) + gamma_e * phi.inv_cdf(1 - 1 / (n * math.e))


selected = 0.01 * (0.06 - (rng.exponential(1.0, n_obs) - 1.0))

4How many trials? The effective number

Formula (3) treats the \(N\) trials as independent. Trials that are variants of one idea share much of their signal and are therefore correlated (Lab 3 uses \(\rho = 0.5\)). Lesson 1, Problem P3, showed that equicorrelation \(\rho \ge 0\) scales the expected maximum by \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\). Correcting for this is unavoidable, and it can be done twice by mistake.

Definition 4.1 (effective number of trials)

The effective number of independent trials is a number \(N_{\text{eff}}\) chosen so that a formula built for independent trials gives the right answer for correlated ones. It is a modelling choice, not an observable: different estimators answer different questions, and the right one depends on which variance it is paired with.

Proposition 4.2 (cross-sectional dispersion already prices correlation)

Let \(\hSR_n = C + \sqrt{(1-\rho)/T}\;\varepsilon_n\) for \(n = 1, \dots, N\), where \(C \sim \Normal(0, \rho/T)\), \(\rho \in [0,1)\), and \(C, \varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_N\) are independent with \(\varepsilon_n \sim \Normal(0,1)\). This is the equicorrelated null, in which each \(\hSR_n\) has variance \(1/T\). Then (i) \(\E\big[\widehat{\Var}[\{\hSR_n\}]\big] = (1-\rho)/T\), and (ii) \(\E[\max_n \hSR_n] = \sqrt{(1-\rho)/T}\;\E[\max_n \varepsilon_n]\). So the raw count \(N\), paired with the expected cross-sectional variance and the exact expected maximum of \(N\) standard normals, reproduces the expected maximum of the correlated trials exactly.

Proof

(i) Deviations from the cross-sectional mean remove the common term: \(\hSR_n - \overline{\hSR} = \sqrt{(1-\rho)/T}\,(\varepsilon_n - \bar\varepsilon)\). The sample variance of \(N\) iid standard normals has expectation 1, so the cross-sectional variance has expectation \((1-\rho)/T\). (ii) \(C\) does not depend on \(n\), so \(\max_n \hSR_n = C + \sqrt{(1-\rho)/T}\,\max_n \varepsilon_n\). Take expectations and use \(\E[C] = 0\). The final sentence combines (i) and (ii).

The consequence is practical. If a researcher computes the cross-sectional variance of the trials' Sharpe ratios, as Definition 3.1 does, the correlation is already in the variance. Replacing \(N\) by a smaller effective number as well counts the correlation twice and understates the hurdle. An effective \(N\) belongs with the single-trial variance \(1/T\), not with the cross-sectional one. This is where the lesson departs from the source of Definition 3.1, which pairs its implied count \(\hat N\) with the cross-sectional variance: under equicorrelation that pairing understates the hurdle. Two common estimators show how far apart the candidates can be:

  • Participation ratio. \(N_{\text{PR}} = (\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2\), with \(\lambda_i\) the eigenvalues of the trials' correlation matrix (eigendecomposition). It counts the dimensions that carry the variance. For the equicorrelation matrix the eigenvalues are \(1 + (N-1)\rho\) once and \(1-\rho\) with multiplicity \(N-1\), so \(N_{\text{PR}} = N^2/\big[(1+(N-1)\rho)^2 + (N-1)(1-\rho)^2\big]\). At \(N = 100\), \(\rho = 0.5\) this is \(10000/2575 = 3.88\).
  • Matching. Choose \(N_{\text{eff}}\) so that the independent-trials hurdle at variance \(1/T\) equals the correlated one: the bracket of (3) at \(N_{\text{eff}}\) equals \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\) times the bracket at \(N\). This answers the question DSR asks.

Remark 4.3 (when equicorrelation fails)

Proposition 4.2 is exact only under equicorrelation. With clusters of near-duplicate variants and little correlation between clusters, the cross-sectional variance mixes within-cluster and between-cluster dispersion, and the raw-\(N\) hurdle can miss the truth in either direction. Problem P3 derives the cross-sectional variance for any structure, and Problem E3 measures the miss for two clusters. Clustering the trials and counting clusters is a third estimator with the same status: a modelling choice to state and stress-test, not a fact to look up.

5Comparing two Sharpe ratios

PSR compares one estimated Sharpe ratio with a fixed number. A desk more often compares two estimates: a new variant against the strategy already running, or a manager against an index fund, over the same \(T\) periods. Both estimates are noisy, and when the strategies share a signal or a market exposure their errors move together. Write \(r_{1,t}, r_{2,t}\) for the two excess returns, with means \(\mu_i\), standard deviations \(\sigma_i \gt 0\), Sharpe ratios \(\SR_i = \mu_i/\sigma_i\) and standardized returns \(u_{i,t} = (r_{i,t} - \mu_i)/\sigma_i\). Their correlation is \(\rho = \E[u_1u_2]\), and the mixed moments are \(\kappa_{ab} = \E[u_1^a u_2^b]\). Strategy \(i\) has skewness \(\gamma_{3,i}\), kurtosis \(\gamma_{4,i}\) and the variance \(V_i = V(\SR_i, \gamma_{3,i}, \gamma_{4,i})\) of (1).

Theorem 5.1 (the difference of two Sharpe ratios)

Let the pairs \((r_{1,t}, r_{2,t})\) be iid over \(t\), with finite fourth moments and \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2 \gt 0\). Then \[ \sqrt{T}\,\big((\hSR_1 - \hSR_2) - (\SR_1 - \SR_2)\big) \dto \Normal(0, \theta), \] \[ \theta = V_1 + V_2 - 2\rho + \SR_1\,\kappa_{21} + \SR_2\,\kappa_{12} - \tfrac12\,\SR_1\SR_2\,(\kappa_{22} - 1). \tag{4}\] Equivalently, \(\theta = \Var(\psi_1 - \psi_2)\) with \(\psi_i = u_i - \tfrac{\SR_i}{2}\,(u_i^2 - 1)\).

Proof

Step 1: a joint central limit theorem. The vectors \(X_t = \big(r_{1,t} - \mu_1,\; r_{2,t} - \mu_2,\; (r_{1,t}-\mu_1)^2 - \sigma_1^2,\; (r_{2,t}-\mu_2)^2 - \sigma_2^2\big)\) are iid with mean zero and, by the finite fourth moments, a finite covariance matrix \(\Cov(X_t)\). The multivariate central limit theorem gives \(\sqrt{T}\,\bar X_T \dto \Normal\big(0, \Cov(X_t)\big)\).

Step 2: the sample variances. Step 2 of Theorem 1.2, applied to each strategy, gives \(\sqrt{T}\big((\bar r_1, \bar r_2, s_1^2, s_2^2) - (\mu_1, \mu_2, \sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2)\big) = \sqrt{T}\,\bar X_T + o_p(1)\).

Step 3: the delta method. \(\hSR_1 - \hSR_2 = g(\bar r_1, \bar r_2, s_1^2, s_2^2)\) with \(g(y_1, y_2, v_1, v_2) = y_1/\sqrt{v_1} - y_2/\sqrt{v_2}\), differentiable at the true values with gradient \(\nabla g = \big(1/\sigma_1,\; -1/\sigma_2,\; -\mu_1/(2\sigma_1^3),\; \mu_2/(2\sigma_2^3)\big)\). The limit is \(\Normal\big(0, \nabla g^\top \Cov(X_t)\,\nabla g\big)\), and \[ \nabla g^\top X_t = u_{1,t} - \tfrac{\SR_1}{2}\big(u_{1,t}^2 - 1\big) - u_{2,t} + \tfrac{\SR_2}{2}\big(u_{2,t}^2 - 1\big) = \psi_{1,t} - \psi_{2,t}, \] so \(\theta = \Var(\psi_1 - \psi_2)\).

Step 4: the moments. \(\Var(\psi_i) = V_i\) is the computation in Step 3 of Theorem 1.2. The \(\psi_i\) have mean zero, so expanding the product gives \[ \Cov(\psi_1, \psi_2) = \E[u_1u_2] - \tfrac{\SR_2}{2}\E\big[u_1(u_2^2 - 1)\big] - \tfrac{\SR_1}{2}\E\big[(u_1^2 - 1)u_2\big] + \tfrac{\SR_1\SR_2}{4}\E\big[(u_1^2-1)(u_2^2-1)\big]. \] Because \(\E u_i = 0\) and \(\E u_i^2 = 1\), the three expectations are \(\kappa_{12}\), \(\kappa_{21}\) and \(\kappa_{22} - 1 - 1 + 1 = \kappa_{22} - 1\), so \[ \Cov(\psi_1, \psi_2) = \rho - \tfrac{\SR_2}{2}\kappa_{12} - \tfrac{\SR_1}{2}\kappa_{21} + \tfrac{\SR_1\SR_2}{4}(\kappa_{22} - 1). \] Then \(\theta = V_1 + V_2 - 2\Cov(\psi_1, \psi_2)\), which is (4).

Corollary 5.2 (jointly normal returns)

If the pairs are iid bivariate normal with correlation \(\rho\), then \[ \theta_{\mathrm{N}} = 2(1 - \rho) + \tfrac12\big(\SR_1^2 + \SR_2^2\big) - \SR_1\SR_2\,\rho^2. \tag{5}\]

Proof

Odd moments of a centred normal vector vanish, so \(\gamma_{3,i} = \kappa_{12} = \kappa_{21} = 0\), and \(\gamma_{4,i} = 3\) gives \(V_i = 1 + \SR_i^2/2\) (Corollary 1.3). Write \(u_2 = \rho u_1 + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,w\) with \(w\) standard normal and independent of \(u_1\). Then \(\kappa_{22} = \rho^2\E u_1^4 + 2\rho\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\E[u_1^3]\,\E[w] + (1-\rho^2)\,\E[u_1^2]\,\E[w^2] = 3\rho^2 + 1 - \rho^2 = 1 + 2\rho^2\). Substituting in (4) gives \(\theta = 2 + \tfrac12(\SR_1^2 + \SR_2^2) - 2\rho - \SR_1\SR_2\rho^2\), which is (5).

In the corrected form that Ledoit and Wolf describe, the test of Jobson and Korkie computes the standard error of \(\hSR_1 - \hSR_2\) by the delta method, from an asymptotic covariance of \(\sqrt{T}\) times the errors in \((\hat\mu_1, \hat\mu_2, \hat\sigma_1^2, \hat\sigma_2^2)\) that assumes iid bivariate-normal returns: \(\sigma_i^2\) and \(\sigma_{12}\) for the means, \(2\sigma_i^4\) and \(2\sigma_{12}^2\) for the variances, and zero between means and variances, where \(\sigma_{12} = \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\) is the covariance. With that covariance the delta method of Step 3 gives exactly (5) (Problem P5): (5) is the corrected Jobson–Korkie variance, and Corollary 5.2 shows it is the normal special case of (4). The general formula differs in three places: each strategy's skewness and kurtosis, through \(V_i\); the co-skewness terms \(\kappa_{12}, \kappa_{21}\); and the co-kurtosis \(\kappa_{22}\), which equals \(1 + 2\rho^2\) under joint normality but not in general (Problem P5(b) gives 1.25 at \(\rho = 0\)). A shared crash exposure moves all three at once (Lab 4). Opdyke derives a two-sample statistic without assuming normal returns, and studies it by simulation under fat tails, asymmetry and strong positive correlation between funds; his claim that it also holds for serially dependent returns is the one Ledoit and Wolf dispute (Remark 2.10). In simulations with 120 observations, Ledoit and Wolf find that the corrected Jobson–Korkie test rejects a true null too often when returns have heavy tails or serial dependence, and that HAC standard errors are often liberal at that sample size; they propose a studentized time-series bootstrap interval for \(\SR_1 - \SR_2\), which they find works well for both iid and time-series data in their designs.

Definition 5.3 (paired test of two Sharpe ratios)

The paired test of two Sharpe ratios rejects \(H_0\colon \SR_1 = \SR_2\) at two-sided level \(\alpha\) when \(|Z| \gt z_{1-\alpha/2}\), where \[ Z = \frac{(\hSR_1 - \hSR_2)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{\hat\theta}} \] and \(\hat\theta\) is (4) with every moment replaced by its sample counterpart, or (5) with \(\hat\rho\) when the returns are close to normal. The one-sided test of \(H_0\colon \SR_1 \le \SR_2\) rejects when \(Z \gt z_{1-\alpha}\). The \(T - 1\) follows (2): if the second Sharpe ratio is a known number \(\SR^\ast\) rather than an estimate, only strategy 1's estimation error remains, the variance becomes \(V_1\), and \(\Phi(Z)\) is \(\PSR(\SR^\ast)\). The naive test treats the two estimates as independent and uses \(\hat V_1 + \hat V_2\) in place of \(\hat\theta\): (4) with \(\rho = \kappa_{12} = \kappa_{21} = 0\) and \(\kappa_{22} = 1\), the values for independent strategies.

Proposition 5.4 (the naive test has the wrong size)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 5.1, let \(\SR_1 = \SR_2\), \(\theta \gt 0\), and \(\hat V_i \pto V_i\). The naive test at two-sided level \(\alpha\) rejects with probability tending to \[ 2\Big(1 - \Phi\big(z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{(V_1 + V_2)/\theta}\,\big)\Big), \] which is below \(\alpha\) when \(\theta \lt V_1 + V_2\) and above \(\alpha\) when \(\theta \gt V_1 + V_2\). For jointly normal returns with a common Sharpe ratio \(\SR\) and \(\SR^2 \lt 2\), positive correlation makes the naive test undersized and negative correlation makes it oversized.

Proof

By Theorem 5.1 and \(\sqrt{(T-1)/T} \to 1\), \((\hSR_1 - \hSR_2)\sqrt{T-1} \dto \Normal(0, \theta)\) under \(\SR_1 = \SR_2\). Since \(\hat V_1 + \hat V_2 \pto V_1 + V_2 \gt 0\), Slutsky's lemma gives the naive statistic the limit \(\Normal\big(0, \theta/(V_1 + V_2)\big)\), so it exceeds \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) in absolute value with probability tending to the display. That expression decreases strictly in \((V_1 + V_2)/\theta\) and equals \(\alpha\) when the ratio is 1. For jointly normal returns with \(\SR_1 = \SR_2 = \SR\), (5) gives \(\theta = 2(1-\rho) + \SR^2(1-\rho^2)\), while \(V_1 + V_2 = 2 + \SR^2\). Their difference is \((V_1 + V_2) - \theta = \rho\,(2 + \SR^2\rho)\). When \(\SR^2 \lt 2\), the factor \(2 + \SR^2\rho\) is positive for every \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\), so the difference has the sign of \(\rho\).

Example 5.5 (two variants of one signal)

Two daily strategies built on one signal show \(\hSR_1 = 0.06\) and \(\hSR_2 = 0.04\) (annualized 0.95 and 0.63) over \(T = 1260\) days, with \(\hat\rho = 0.95\) and returns close enough to normal to use (5). Then \[ \hat\theta = 2(0.05) + \tfrac12(0.0036 + 0.0016) - 0.0024 \times 0.9025 = 0.10043, \] the standard error is \(\sqrt{0.10043/1259} = 0.008932\), and \(Z = 0.02/0.008932 = 2.239\), a two-sided p-value of 0.025. The naive test uses \(2 + 0.0026 = 2.0026\), a standard error of 0.03988, and \(Z = 0.50\), a p-value of 0.62. The paired standard error is 4.5 times smaller. Each Sharpe ratio on its own carries an annualized standard error of about 0.45, \(\sqrt{252 \times 1.0018/1259} = 0.448\), yet the difference of 0.32 annualized is detected, because the two estimates share most of their noise.

Remark 5.6 (one pair, then many: the Reality Check)

The paired test is the \(K = 1\) case of the problem Lesson 4 solved for many strategies. With one challenger and a benchmark, the Reality Check on Sharpe ratios of Lesson 4, Problem R1, has statistic \(\sqrt{T}(\hSR_1 - \hSR_0)\). Its stationary bootstrap resamples whole dates for both series and so estimates the distribution whose variance Theorem 5.1 gives in closed form for iid returns. The naive test is the analytic twin of resampling each strategy on its own dates, which Lesson 5 (Section 4) showed throws away power; here it also misstates the size, in a direction that Proposition 5.4 gives: for jointly normal returns with a common Sharpe ratio whose square is below 2 it is set by the sign of the correlation. Two cautions carry over. First, Theorem 5.1 needs iid returns. For stationary returns, Ledoit and Wolf keep the delta-method gradient and replace the iid covariance of the moment vector by its long-run covariance, estimated by a HAC kernel; in the notation of Theorem 5.1 that is the long-run variance of \(\psi_{1,t} - \psi_{2,t}\). It is the Diebold–Mariano construction of Lesson 4 (Definition 1.2) applied to the linearized differential \(\psi_{1,t} - \psi_{2,t}\): Lesson 4's one-challenger test on mean differentials and the paired test here answer the same \(K = 1\) question for two criteria. Second, the test is valid for a pair fixed in advance. Choosing the best of \(K\) strategies by its Sharpe ratio and then testing it against the benchmark with Definition 5.3 is the data-snooping error of Lesson 1. The Reality Check, with statistic \(\max_k \sqrt{T}(\hSR_k - \hSR_0)\), is the test that prices the search, as DSR does when the selected strategy is compared with zero rather than with a benchmark strategy.

6What deflation does not protect against

DSR is a single test of one null at one hurdle. It inherits every assumption used to build it:

  • Unrecorded trials. \(N\) and the cross-sectional variance come from the trials the researcher reports. Trials that were run and forgotten, or rejected without code, do not enter. DSR computed from an incomplete log overstates the evidence (Lesson 1, Problem R1).
  • Serial correlation. Theorem 1.2 assumes independent returns. Getmansky, Lo and Makarov find hedge-fund returns often highly serially correlated, attribute this mainly to illiquidity exposure, and show that smoothed reported returns understate volatility and raise the Sharpe ratio. The attribution is contested: Bollen and Pool show that a manager who reports gains fully but delays reporting losses produces conditional serial correlation even when true returns are independent, and find it in hedge-fund returns related to investor cash flows, consistent with smoothing in response to the risk of capital flight, and they find far more small monthly gains than small losses in pooled hedge-fund returns, a discontinuity that disappears in the three months before an audit and that they attribute at least in part to temporarily overstated returns; Jorion and Schwarz show that incentive fees, asset illiquidity and yields bounded at zero can each create such a discontinuity without manipulation, and that fees account for almost all of it in the large, liquid long-short equity style. Positive autocorrelation makes the iid standard error too small (Remark 1.5).
  • Non-stationarity. Moments estimated over one regime are assumed to hold over the sample and beyond. A strategy whose skewness appears only in crises can show benign moments over a calm sample.
  • Estimation error in the moments. \(\hat\gamma_4\) is consistent whenever \(\E[r^4] \lt \infty\) (by the law of large numbers), but its sampling error shrinks like \(T^{-1/2}\) only if \(\E[r^8] \lt \infty\). For heavy-tailed returns the plug-in standard error is itself unreliable.
  • The shape of the cross-section. Formula (3) treats the trial Sharpe ratios as a normal sample. Selection on a criterion other than the Sharpe ratio, or a researcher who refines one variant while abandoning others early, breaks that model.

7Where the canon breaks

The textbook Sharpe-ratio test is a z-test on a normal sample. It fails along three axes, and this lesson repairs only one of them.

  • Shape (repaired). Theorem 1.2 replaces \(1 + \SR^2/2\) with \(1 - \gamma_3\SR + \tfrac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\SR^2\). The normal formula is wrong in a known direction for a known class of strategies: it flatters negative skewness.
  • Dependence over time (not repaired here). Formula (1) assumes independence, and under serial correlation the variance of \(\hSR\) depends on the autocorrelations (Remark 1.5). Lo's results and F7 address it; the stationary bootstrap of Lesson 4 is a nonparametric alternative (Problem R3). MinTRL and the paired test inherit the same assumption (Remarks 2.10 and 5.6).
  • Search (repaired, conditionally). DSR prices the search only through \(N\) and the trials' dispersion, which must be recorded honestly and modelled carefully (Section 4). A different repair, built on multiple-testing hurdles for the t-statistic, is the subject of Lesson 6 (t-statistic hurdle). The two answer related but different questions.

8Misconceptions

  1. “The standard error of a Sharpe ratio is \(1/\sqrt{T}\), whatever the strategy.”

    That is the null, normal special case. Theorem 1.2 shows the variance depends on the Sharpe ratio, the skewness and the kurtosis. For the short-volatility profile of Example 1.4 the normal formula understates the variance by 18% (Lab 1).

  2. “A PSR of 0.97 means a 97% chance that the strategy's true Sharpe ratio beats the benchmark.”

    Proposition 2.2: PSR is one minus a one-sided p-value. It conditions on the null and says nothing about the probability of the null without prior odds (Remark 2.4).

  3. “My trials are correlated, so I should use a small effective N in the DSR.”

    Not if the hurdle uses the cross-sectional variance of the trials' Sharpe ratios. Under equicorrelation that variance already shrinks by \(1-\rho\), and shrinking \(N\) too counts the correlation twice. In Lab 3 that cut the hurdle to less than half its correct value (Proposition 4.2). The implied count \(\hat N\) that Bailey and López de Prado pair with the cross-sectional variance makes the same double count (Definition 3.1).

  4. “Positive skewness is always good news for a Sharpe-ratio test.”

    Positive skewness lowers the variance only through \(-\gamma_3\SR\), which helps when \(\SR \gt 0\). The kurtosis term \(\tfrac{\gamma_4-1}{4}\SR^2\) always adds. For a negative Sharpe ratio, positive skewness widens the interval. Problem E1 finds where the two effects cancel.

  5. “A skilled manager needs MinTRL periods to prove it, and a record longer than MinTRL proves skill.”

    Both halves are wrong. Computed from a record's own estimates, MinTRL restates the PSR test (Proposition 2.6(i)): a longer record rejects \(\SR \le \SR^\ast\) at level \(\alpha\), which is not a probability of skill (Remark 2.4). Computed from a true Sharpe ratio, it is a break-even length at which the test passes about half the time; in Lab 4, 49% of records of exactly MinTRL days passed. Planning needs \(T_\beta\) (Remark 2.9).

  6. “Two Sharpe ratios whose separate confidence intervals overlap cannot be told apart.”

    The standard error of a difference is not built from the two separate standard errors when the strategies are correlated. In Example 5.5 each Sharpe ratio carries an annualized standard error of about 0.45, yet the paired test detects a difference of 0.32 at a p-value of 0.025, because \(\hat\rho = 0.95\) makes the paired standard error 4.5 times smaller than the naive one (Theorem 5.1). For jointly normal returns with a common per-period Sharpe ratio whose square is below 2, the error runs the other way under negative correlation: the naive test rejects a true null too often (Proposition 5.4, Lab 4).

M1 · A ten-year record

A strategy shows a daily Sharpe ratio of 0.05 over \(T = 2520\) days with \(\hat\gamma_3 = -1\) and \(\hat\gamma_4 = 6\). Compute the bracket in (2), the standard error, the normal-theory standard error, and \(\PSR(0)\).

Solution

The bracket is \(1 - (-1)(0.05) + \tfrac{6-1}{4}(0.05)^2 = 1 + 0.05 + 0.003125 = 1.053125\). The standard error is \(\sqrt{1.053125/2519} = 0.02045\). Under normality the bracket is \(1 + 0.05^2/2 = 1.00125\) and the standard error \(\sqrt{1.00125/2519} = 0.01994\). Then \(\PSR(0) = \Phi(0.05/0.02045) = \Phi(2.445) = 0.9928\). The annualized Sharpe ratio is \(0.05\sqrt{252} = 0.794\): ten years of a modest record clear zero comfortably.

M2 · Raising the benchmark

For the strategy of M1, compute \(\PSR(\SR^\ast)\) against an annualized benchmark of 0.5.

Solution

Convert the benchmark to daily units: \(0.5/\sqrt{252} = 0.03150\). Then \(\PSR = \Phi\big((0.05 - 0.03150)/0.02045\big) = \Phi(0.905) = 0.817\). The record is strong evidence against a zero Sharpe ratio and weak evidence against an annualized 0.5. Units matter: \(\SR^\ast\) must be in the same per-period units as \(\hSR\).

M3 · How many trials can it survive?

The strategy of M1 was selected from \(N = 25\) trials whose Sharpe ratios have a cross-sectional standard deviation of 0.02 per day. Compute \(\SR_0\) and the DSR. Then find the largest \(N\) for which \(\DSR \ge 0.95\), holding the cross-sectional standard deviation fixed.

Solution

The bracket of (3) at \(N = 25\) is 1.9971, so \(\SR_0 = 0.02 \times 1.9971 = 0.03994\), and \(\DSR = \Phi\big((0.05 - 0.03994)/0.02045\big) = \Phi(0.492) = 0.689\). For \(\DSR \ge 0.95\) we need \((0.05 - 0.02\,z_N)/0.02045 \ge \Phi^{-1}(0.95) = 1.6449\), that is \(z_N \le (0.05 - 0.03363)/0.02 = 0.818\), where \(z_N\) is the bracket of (3). The bracket is 0.520 at \(N = 2\) and 0.853 at \(N = 3\), so the record survives at most two trials. Ten years of data with a daily Sharpe ratio of 0.05 is not enough to support any real search.

M4 · A minimum track record

For the strategy of M1, compute MinTRL at \(\alpha = 0.05\) against zero and against an annualized benchmark of 0.5, in days and in years of 252 days. Check each answer against the PSR values of M1 and M2.

Solution

The bracket is \(\hat b = 1.053125\) (M1) and \(z_{0.95} = 1.6449\). Against zero, \((1.6449/0.05)^2 = 1082.3\), so \(\mathrm{MinTRL} = 1 + 1.053125 \times 1082.3 = 1140.8\): 1141 days, or 4.53 years. The record's 2520 days exceed it, consistent with \(\PSR(0) = 0.9928 \ge 0.95\). Against 0.5 annualized, \(\SR^\ast = 0.031497\) and the margin is \(0.05 - 0.031497 = 0.018503\), so \((1.6449/0.018503)^2 = 7903\) and \(\mathrm{MinTRL} = 1 + 1.053125 \times 7903 = 8324\) days, or 33.0 years. The record falls far short, consistent with \(\PSR = 0.817 \lt 0.95\) in M2. The margin shrank by a factor of \(0.05/0.018503 = 2.70\), and the requirement grew by \(2.70^2 = 7.3\), as Proposition 2.6(ii) predicts.

M5 · Paired or naive

Two funds report 120 monthly excess returns with Sharpe ratios 0.30 and 0.15 per month and return correlation 0.7. Treat the returns as normal. Test \(\SR_1 = \SR_2\) at 5%, two-sided, with the paired test using (5), then with the naive test.

Solution

Paired: \(\hat\theta = 2(1 - 0.7) + \tfrac12(0.09 + 0.0225) - 0.30 \times 0.15 \times 0.49 = 0.6 + 0.05625 - 0.02205 = 0.6342\). The standard error is \(\sqrt{0.6342/119} = 0.07300\), so \(Z = 0.15/0.07300 = 2.055\) and the two-sided p-value is 0.040: reject at 5%. Naive: \(V_1 + V_2 = 2 + 0.05625 = 2.05625\), a standard error of \(\sqrt{2.05625/119} = 0.1315\), \(Z = 1.141\), p-value 0.25: no rejection. The annualized Sharpe ratios are \(0.30\sqrt{12} = 1.04\) and \(0.15\sqrt{12} = 0.52\). The paired verdict rests on normality and on 120 observations, the sample size at which Ledoit and Wolf's simulations found the corrected Jobson–Korkie test liberal under heavy tails or serial dependence (Section 5). Proof

P1 · The bracket is never negative

Prove Pearson's inequality \(\gamma_4 \ge 1 + \gamma_3^2\) for any distribution with finite fourth moment and positive variance. Deduce that \(V(\SR, \gamma_3, \gamma_4) \ge 0\) for every real \(\SR\), and that \(V\) can vanish only if \(\gamma_4 = 1 + \gamma_3^2\).

Solution

Let \(U = (r-\mu)/\sigma\), so \(\E U = 0\), \(\E U^2 = 1\), \(\E U^3 = \gamma_3\), \(\E U^4 = \gamma_4\). For \(a = \gamma_3\), \(0 \le \Var(U^2 - aU) = \E[(U^2 - aU)^2] - (\E[U^2 - aU])^2 = \gamma_4 - 2a\gamma_3 + a^2 - 1 = \gamma_4 - \gamma_3^2 - 1\). Now view \(V = 1 - \gamma_3 x + \tfrac{\gamma_4-1}{4}x^2\) as a quadratic in \(x = \SR\). Its leading coefficient is \((\gamma_4 - 1)/4 \ge \gamma_3^2/4 \ge 0\). Its discriminant is \(\gamma_3^2 - (\gamma_4 - 1) \le 0\) by Pearson's inequality, so \(V \ge 0\) everywhere, and a zero requires a zero discriminant. The reason is structural: in the proof of Theorem 1.2, \(V\) is the variance of the linear combination \(\nabla g^\top X_t\). Equality \(\gamma_4 = 1 + \gamma_3^2\) holds only for two-point distributions, so the A8 rule that rejects a non-positive bracket fires only on degenerate samples.

P2 · Sample versus population moments

A8 computes \(\hSR\) with \(s\) (divisor \(T-1\)) but \(\hat\gamma_3, \hat\gamma_4\) with moment estimators (divisor \(T\)). Show that the plug-in bracket \(1 - \hat\gamma_3\hSR + \tfrac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\hSR^2\) is non-negative for every sample, using P1 applied to the empirical distribution.

Solution

The moment estimators \(\hat\gamma_3, \hat\gamma_4\) are exactly the skewness and kurtosis of the empirical distribution, which puts mass \(1/T\) on each observation and has finite fourth moment and positive variance whenever the sample is not constant. P1 applied to that distribution gives \(\hat\gamma_4 \ge 1 + \hat\gamma_3^2\). The bracket is the same quadratic in \(x = \hSR\), and P1 shows it is non-negative for every real \(x\), whatever divisor was used to compute \(\hSR\). A zero needs \(\hat\gamma_4 = 1 + \hat\gamma_3^2\), which happens only when the sample takes exactly two distinct values.

P3 · Cross-sectional dispersion under any correlation

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) have common mean, common variance \(\sigma^2\) and average pairwise covariance \(\bar c = \frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{m \ne n}\Cov(X_m, X_n)\). Show that the cross-sectional sample variance \(S^2 = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_n (X_n - \bar X)^2\) satisfies \(\E[S^2] = \sigma^2 - \bar c\). Apply it to (a) equicorrelation \(\rho\) and (b) two clusters of \(h\) trials with within-cluster correlation \(\rho\) and zero correlation between clusters.

Solution

\(\sum_n (X_n - \bar X)^2 = \sum_n (X_n - \mu)^2 - N(\bar X - \mu)^2\). Taking expectations, \(\E\sum_n (X_n-\mu)^2 = N\sigma^2\), and \(N\,\Var(\bar X) = \frac1N\big(N\sigma^2 + N(N-1)\bar c\big) = \sigma^2 + (N-1)\bar c\). So \(\E\sum_n (X_n - \bar X)^2 = (N-1)\sigma^2 - (N-1)\bar c\), and dividing by \(N-1\) gives \(\sigma^2 - \bar c\).

(a) Equicorrelation: \(\bar c = \rho\sigma^2\), so \(\E[S^2] = (1-\rho)\sigma^2\). With \(\sigma^2 = 1/T\) this is Proposition 4.2 (i).

(b) Two clusters: there are \(2h(h-1)\) ordered within-cluster pairs, each with covariance \(\rho\sigma^2\), out of \(N(N-1) = 2h(2h-1)\). So \(\bar c = \rho\sigma^2 (h-1)/(2h-1)\) and \(\E[S^2] = \sigma^2\big(1 - \rho(h-1)/(2h-1)\big)\). At \(h = 50\), \(\rho = 0.9\) this is \(0.5545\,\sigma^2\). The correlation is diluted across the cross-section even though half of every trial's neighbours are near-duplicates. That is why the raw-\(N\) rule misprices clustered trials (Problem E3).

P4 · MinTRL across sampling frequencies

Fix the annualized Sharpe ratio \(\SR_{\text{ann}}\) and benchmark \(\SR^\ast_{\text{ann}} \lt \SR_{\text{ann}}\), and suppose returns are normal at every frequency, with \(q\) periods a year. Show that MinTRL in years is \[ \frac{\mathrm{MinTRL}}{q} = \frac1q + \Big(\frac{z_{1-\alpha}}{\SR_{\text{ann}} - \SR^\ast_{\text{ann}}}\Big)^2\Big(1 + \frac{\SR_{\text{ann}}^2}{2q}\Big), \] that it decreases strictly in \(q\) towards \(\big(z_{1-\alpha}/(\SR_{\text{ann}} - \SR^\ast_{\text{ann}})\big)^2\), and use it to reproduce the 2.73, 2.83 and 3.24 years of Remark 2.7.

Solution

Per period, \(\hSR = \SR_{\text{ann}}/\sqrt q\) and the margin is \((\SR_{\text{ann}} - \SR^\ast_{\text{ann}})/\sqrt q\). Normal returns have \(\hat\gamma_3 = 0\) and \(\hat\gamma_4 = 3\), so the bracket is \(1 + \hSR^2/2 = 1 + \SR_{\text{ann}}^2/(2q)\). Proposition 2.6(i) gives \(\mathrm{MinTRL} = 1 + \big(1 + \SR_{\text{ann}}^2/(2q)\big)\,q\,z_{1-\alpha}^2/(\SR_{\text{ann}} - \SR^\ast_{\text{ann}})^2\); dividing by \(q\) gives the display. Both terms that depend on \(q\), namely \(1/q\) and \(\SR_{\text{ann}}^2/(2q)\), are positive and strictly decreasing in \(q\), and both vanish as \(q \to \infty\), which leaves the limit. With \(\SR_{\text{ann}} = 2\), \(\SR^\ast_{\text{ann}} = 1\) and \(z_{0.95}^2 = 2.7055\): at \(q = 252\), \(0.00397 + 2.7055 \times 1.00794 = 2.731\); at \(q = 52\), \(0.01923 + 2.7055 \times 1.03846 = 2.829\); at \(q = 12\), \(0.08333 + 2.7055 \times 1.16667 = 3.240\). These round to 2.73, 2.83 and 3.24. Under normality, then, sampling more often shortens the record in years, but only through the two small terms; the limit, 2.71 years here, is set by the annualized margin alone. The comparison holds the moments fixed across frequencies, which is exact only for normal iid returns: aggregating iid returns over \(m\) periods divides the skewness by \(\sqrt m\) and the excess kurtosis by \(m\), and serial dependence changes the variance itself (Remark 2.10).

P5 · The paired variance in special cases

(a) Show that \(\theta = 0\) when \(r_{2,t} = r_{1,t}\). (b) Show that \(\theta = V_1 + V_2\) when the two strategies are independent, and give an example with \(\rho = 0\) in which \(\theta \ne V_1 + V_2\). (c) Show that the delta method of Step 3 of Theorem 5.1, with the bivariate-normal covariance described after Corollary 5.2, gives (5).

Solution

(a) Identical returns give \(u_1 = u_2\) and \(\SR_1 = \SR_2\), so \(\psi_1 = \psi_2\) and \(\theta = \Var(\psi_1 - \psi_2) = 0\). Formula (4) agrees: with \(\rho = 1\), \(\kappa_{12} = \kappa_{21} = \gamma_3\), \(\kappa_{22} = \gamma_4\) and \(V_1 = V_2 = V\), it gives \(2V - 2 + 2\gamma_3\SR - \tfrac12\SR^2(\gamma_4 - 1) = 0\).

(b) Independence gives \(\rho = \E u_1\,\E u_2 = 0\), \(\kappa_{12} = \E u_1\,\E u_2^2 = 0\), \(\kappa_{21} = 0\) and \(\kappa_{22} = \E u_1^2\,\E u_2^2 = 1\), so (4) reduces to \(V_1 + V_2\). Zero correlation is not enough. Let \(u_i = s\,\varepsilon_i\), with \(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2\) standard normal, \(s \gt 0\) a common volatility independent of them, and \(s^2\) equal to 0.5 or 1.5 with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, so \(\E s^2 = 1\) and \(\E s^4 = 1.25\). Then \(\rho = \E[s^2]\,\E[\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2] = 0\) and \(\kappa_{12} = \kappa_{21} = 0\), but \(\kappa_{22} = \E[s^4]\,\E[\varepsilon_1^2]\,\E[\varepsilon_2^2] = 1.25\), so \(\theta = V_1 + V_2 - \SR_1\SR_2/8\). Shared volatility, which makes squared returns correlated, enters \(\theta\) even when returns are uncorrelated.

(c) With \(\sigma_{12} = \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\) and the gradient \(\nabla g = \big(1/\sigma_1, -1/\sigma_2, -\mu_1/(2\sigma_1^3), \mu_2/(2\sigma_2^3)\big)\), the mean block contributes \(1 + 1 - 2\sigma_{12}/(\sigma_1\sigma_2) = 2 - 2\rho\). The variance block contributes \(\tfrac{\mu_1^2}{4\sigma_1^6}\,2\sigma_1^4 + \tfrac{\mu_2^2}{4\sigma_2^6}\,2\sigma_2^4 - 2\,\tfrac{\mu_1\mu_2}{4\sigma_1^3\sigma_2^3}\,2\sigma_{12}^2 = \tfrac12\SR_1^2 + \tfrac12\SR_2^2 - \SR_1\SR_2\rho^2\). The cross block is zero. The sum is (5).

E1 · Where skewness and kurtosis cancel

Find the Sharpe ratio at which the non-normal variance (1) equals the normal variance \(1 + \SR^2/2\), as a function of \(\gamma_3\) and \(\gamma_4\). Evaluate it for the exponential shocks of Lab 1, then modify Lab 1 to run at that Sharpe ratio and confirm.

Solution

Set \(1 - \gamma_3\SR + \tfrac{\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\SR^2 = 1 + \tfrac{\SR^2}{2}\). This gives \(\SR\big(\tfrac{\gamma_4 - 3}{4}\SR - \gamma_3\big) = 0\), so \(\SR = 0\) or \(\SR = 4\gamma_3/(\gamma_4 - 3)\). For \(\gamma_3 = 2\), \(\gamma_4 = 9\) the second root is \(8/6 = 1.33\) per period, absurdly high for daily data. For realistic Sharpe ratios the skewness term therefore dominates: positive skewness shrinks and negative skewness inflates the variance. In Lab 1, set sr = 4 / 3. Both formulas then give \(1 + (4/3)^2/2 = 1.889\), and with positive skewness the simulation should agree up to Monte Carlo error. With negative skewness the non-normal value is \(1 + 2(4/3) + 2(16/9) = 7.22\).

E2 · Skill in the cross-section

In Lab 2, give the \(N-1\) other trials true daily Sharpe ratios drawn from \(\Normal(0, 0.02^2)\) instead of zero. Predict the effect on \(\SR_0\) before running, and explain why the change makes DSR conservative rather than anti-conservative.

Solution

The cross-sectional variance of the estimated Sharpe ratios becomes sampling noise plus true dispersion: about \(1/T + 0.02^2 = 0.000794 + 0.0004\) per day squared at \(T = 1260\). Its square root rises from 0.0282 to 0.0345, by a factor of 1.226, and \(\SR_0\) rises by the same factor at every \(N\). DSR falls. The hurdle is built for skill-free trials, so genuine dispersion in skill is read as extra luck, which raises the bar for the selected strategy. That errs on the safe side for the investor and against the researcher. A sharper hurdle would need an estimate of how much of the dispersion is skill, from a hierarchical model that splits the cross-sectional dispersion into skill and noise.

E3 · Two clusters

Change Lab 3 to two clusters of 50 trials with within-cluster correlation 0.9 and no correlation between clusters. Using P3, predict the raw-\(N\) hurdle in units of \(1/\sqrt{T}\). Then estimate the true expected maximum and compare.

By P3(b) the expected cross-sectional variance is \(0.5545/T\), so the raw-\(N\) hurdle is \(\sqrt{0.5545} \times 2.5306/\sqrt{T} = 1.884/\sqrt{T}\). Within cluster \(k\), \(\sqrt{T}\,\hSR_n = \sqrt{0.9}\,W_k + \sqrt{0.1}\,\varepsilon_n\), so the cluster maximum is \(Y_k = \sqrt{0.9}\,W_k + \sqrt{0.1}\,Q_k\), where \(Q_k\) is the maximum of 50 standard normals. \(Q_k\) has mean 2.249 (the exact value from the Lesson 1 figure) and a variance of about 0.22 by simulation. Approximating \(Y_1, Y_2\) as independent normals with mean \(\sqrt{0.1}\times 2.249 = 0.711\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{0.9 + 0.1 \times 0.22} = 0.960\), Problem P2 of Lesson 1 gives \(\E[\max(Y_1, Y_2)] \approx 0.711 + 0.960/\sqrt{\pi} = 1.25\). The raw-\(N\) rule therefore overstates the hurdle by about 50% here. It is conservative for this structure, but no general sign is guaranteed, which is why Remark 4.3 asks you to stress-test the choice.

E4 · What an undersized test costs

Change Part 2 of Lab 4 to the setting of Example 5.5: true daily Sharpe ratios 0.06 and 0.04, \(\rho = 0.95\), \(T = 1260\), jointly normal returns. Before running, predict how often each test rejects at 5%. Then run it and compare.

Solution

Set n_obs = 1260, loop over rho = 0.95 only, and generate the returns as 0.06 + x[0] and 0.04 + rho * x[0] + math.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * x[1]. By Theorem 5.1 the paired statistic is approximately normal with standard deviation 1 and mean \(0.02\sqrt{1259}/\sqrt{0.10043} = 2.239\) (Example 5.5), so it rejects with probability about \(\Phi(2.239 - 1.960) + \Phi(-2.239 - 1.960) = \Phi(0.279) = 0.61\). The naive statistic divides the same difference by \(\sqrt{2.0026/1259}\) instead of \(\sqrt{0.10043/1259}\). Its mean is 0.50 and its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.10043/2.0026} = 0.224\), so reaching 1.960 takes a draw \((1.960 - 0.50)/0.224 = 6.5\) standard deviations above the mean: its power is essentially zero. With 4000 replications the paired rate should land within about \(\sqrt{0.61 \times 0.39/4000} = 0.008\) of 0.61, and the naive test should reject in none of them. An undersized test is not a safe test. It trades false positives for missed discoveries, here every one of them.

Research

R1 · An approval policy when N is uncertain

Design a capital-allocation rule based on DSR for a fund whose trial logs are incomplete. Specify what is reported, which threshold applies, and how the uncertainty in \(N\) enters.

Solution

DSR is strictly decreasing in \(N\) (Lab 2), so report it as a curve, not a number. The proposal shows \(\DSR(N)\) over a range running from the logged count to a stated multiple of it, together with the break-even \(N^\ast\) at which DSR crosses the threshold (M3 computes one). The committee approves only if \(N^\ast\) exceeds a conservative bound on the true number of trials, for instance twice the logged count when logs are known to be incomplete. Both choices, the threshold (0.95 is a convention, not a law) and the multiple, are policy, set once and applied to every proposal. The proposal also states which effective-\(N\) estimator was used and why (Section 4), and shows the moments \(\hat\gamma_3, \hat\gamma_4\) with a bootstrap interval (R3), so that fragile tail estimates are visible. The rule cannot detect trials that left no trace. Its value is that it makes the unverifiable assumption explicit and priced.

R2 · Translating PSR for an investment committee

A committee member reads “PSR = 0.97” as “97% chance the strategy is real.” Write the two-paragraph correction you would give, including what additional input would allow a statement of that form.

Solution

First paragraph: PSR is one minus a one-sided p-value (Proposition 2.2). It says that if the true Sharpe ratio were exactly at the benchmark, a record at least this good would arise about 3% of the time, under the iid and large-sample assumptions of Theorem 1.2. It is computed as if this were the only strategy tested, so after a search it must be replaced by DSR.

Second paragraph: a probability that the strategy is real needs prior odds that a strategy of this kind has an edge, which depend on the research area's track record. The minimum Bayes factor of Lesson 6 converts a test statistic into a bound on how far the data can move those odds. With even prior odds and a z-statistic of about 1.88 (the one behind PSR = 0.97), the bound \(e^{-z^2/2} = 0.17\) leaves the null with at least \(0.17/1.17 = 15\%\) posterior probability, five times the 3% the committee member had in mind.

R3 · Beyond iid

Propose a procedure that tests \(\SR \le \SR_0\) without assuming independent returns. State what it costs and what it still assumes.

Solution

Resample the selected strategy's returns with the stationary bootstrap of Lesson 4, which preserves short-range dependence through random-length blocks, and compute \(\hSR^\ast_b\) for each resample. The spread of \(\hSR^\ast_b - \hSR\) estimates the sampling distribution of \(\hSR\) under the data's own dependence and tail shape, so the null distribution of \(\hSR\) at \(\SR = \SR_0\) is approximated by \(\SR_0 + (\hSR^\ast_b - \hSR)\). The bootstrap p-value of \(\SR \le \SR_0\) is the share of those values at or above \(\hSR\), that is, the share of resamples with \(\hSR^\ast_b \ge 2\hSR - \SR_0\). It costs computation and a block-length choice to which the answer can be sensitive. It still assumes stationarity, so it cannot see a regime that is absent from the sample, and the hurdle \(\SR_0\) still comes from (3) with all its assumptions about the trials. For serially correlated returns, the parametric alternative is Lo's correction (Remark 1.5), developed in F7.

R4 · MinTRL after a search

The strategy of Example 2.3 was the best of \(N = 40\) trials, as in Example 3.2. Suppose the trials were skill-free and independent. How long must its record be before \(\DSR \ge 0.95\)? Model the hurdle's dependence on \(T\), give a number, and state what the number assumes.

Solution

The hurdle is not fixed. For skill-free trials the cross-sectional variance of the trial Sharpe ratios is sampling noise, about \(1/(T-1)\) under the convention of (2) at \(\SR = 0\), so \(\SR_0 = z_N/\sqrt{T-1}\), where \(z_N\) is the bracket of (3): 2.1895 at \(N = 40\). Then \(\DSR \ge 0.95\) if and only if \((\hSR - z_N/\sqrt{T-1})\sqrt{T-1}/\sqrt{\hat b} \ge z_{0.95}\), that is \(\hSR\sqrt{T-1} \ge z_{0.95}\sqrt{\hat b} + z_N\), or \[ T - 1 \ge \Big(\frac{z_{0.95}\sqrt{\hat b} + z_N}{\hSR}\Big)^2 = \Big(\frac{1.6449 \times 1.0720 + 2.1895}{0.09}\Big)^2 = 43.92^2 = 1929. \] About 1930 days, or 7.7 years, against 385 days without the search (Example 2.8): the search multiplies \(\mathrm{MinTRL} - 1\) by 5.0. Holding Example 3.2's hurdle 0.0657 fixed instead would give \(1 + 1.1492\,(1.6449/0.0243)^2 = 5267\) days, about 21 years. That answer is wrong, because the hurdle falls like \((T-1)^{-1/2}\) as the record lengthens. Example 3.2's cross-sectional standard deviation of 0.03 is itself below the \(1/\sqrt{755} = 0.0364\) that independent skill-free trials would show at \(T = 756\); under equicorrelation it corresponds to \(\rho = 1 - 755 \times 0.03^2 = 0.32\). Correlated trials lower the requirement: under equicorrelation \(\rho\) the cross-sectional variance is \((1-\rho)/(T-1)\) (Proposition 4.2), \(z_N\) becomes \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\,z_N\), and at \(\rho = 0.5\) the requirement falls to \(\big((1.7633 + 1.5482)/0.09\big)^2 = 1354\), about 1355 days or 5.4 years.

The number assumes four things. (1) \(\hSR\) is the true Sharpe ratio. The winner of a search overstates it (Lesson 1), so plugging it in understates the required length; a planner should use a shrunk estimate. (2) The trials are skill-free. If their true Sharpe ratios are dispersed (Problem E2), the cross-sectional variance tends to that dispersion \(\tau^2\) rather than to zero, the hurdle tends to \(\tau z_N\), and no record length suffices when \(\SR \le \tau z_N\). (3) Returns are iid with stable moments (Remark 2.10). (4) The trial count is complete (Section 6). The calculation uses the location benchmark of Definition 3.1 and ignores the randomness of the hurdle itself, which the DSR-LS variant mentioned in Section 3 is designed to price. A desk can report the required length as a curve in \(N\), as R1 does for DSR itself.
