Weak Instruments & Identification Diagnostics

Finite-Sample Bias Toward OLS, First-Stage F-Statistics, and Heavy-Tailed Failure Modes

Why It Matters

An instrument that satisfies the exclusion restriction can still fail catastrophically if its correlation with the endogenous treatment is weak. Under weak instruments, Two-Stage Least Squares is not merely noisy; it is systematically biased toward the contaminated ordinary least squares estimate, while standard error formulas severely underestimate sampling uncertainty. Empirical finance literature abounds with spuriously significant instrumental variables findings that collapse once weak-identification diagnostics are applied.

1. The Weak Instrument Problem

In Lesson 2, we showed that the instrumental variables estimator requires relevance: \(\Cov(Z_i, D_i) \ne 0\). However, in finite samples, a non-zero population covariance does not guarantee reliable inference if the correlation is small relative to sample noise.

Definition 1.1 · Weak Instruments

Weak instruments arise in instrumental variables regression when the correlation between the instruments \(\boldsymbol{Z}\) and the endogenous regressor \(D\) is close to zero, causing the concentration parameter of the first stage to remain bounded as sample size grows. Under weak instruments, standard asymptotic approximations fail: 2SLS point estimates are biased toward OLS, and conventional Wald hypothesis tests over-reject the null hypothesis.

Theorem 1.1 · Finite-Sample Relative Bias of 2SLS

Assumptions:

  1. Linear structural model \(Y_i = \beta D_i + \varepsilon_i\) and first-stage equation \(D_i = \boldsymbol{Z}_i \boldsymbol{\pi} + v_i\).
  2. Disturbances \((\varepsilon_i, v_i)\) are jointly normal with covariance \(\sigma_{\varepsilon v} \ne 0\).
  3. Number of instruments \(K \ge 2\).

Conclusion: To a first-order Taylor approximation, the expected finite-sample bias of 2SLS relative to OLS bias satisfies:

\[ \frac{\E[\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} - \beta]}{\operatorname{plim}\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} - \beta} \approx \frac{1}{\E[F] - 1}, \]

where \(F\) is the first-stage \(F\)-statistic testing \(H_0: \boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{0}\). When \(F\) is small, the 2SLS bias approaches the full OLS omitted-variable bias.

Proof

The 2SLS estimator estimation error is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} - \beta = (\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\). Decomposing \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon v}}{\sigma_v^2} \boldsymbol{v} + \boldsymbol{w}\), where \(\boldsymbol{w}\) is orthogonal to \(\boldsymbol{v}\):

\[ \E[\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} - \beta] = \frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon v}}{\sigma_v^2} \E[(\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{v}]. \]

Substituting \(\boldsymbol{D} = \boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{\pi} + \boldsymbol{v}\), the quadratic form in the denominator follows a non-central Wishart distribution with non-centrality parameter \(\mu^2 = \boldsymbol{\pi}^\top \boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z} \boldsymbol{\pi} / \sigma_v^2\). Expanding the inverse expectation around \(\mu^2\) yields \(\E[(\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{v}] \approx \frac{K}{\mu^2 + K}\). Recognizing that the expected first-stage \(F\)-statistic is \(\E[F] = 1 + \mu^2 / K\), the ratio simplifies to \(1 / (\E[F] - 1)\).

Remark 1.1 · Diagnostic Thresholds

Econometric practice screens for weak instruments by examining the first-stage F-statistic. When the first-stage F-statistic is small, 2SLS standard errors are severely underestimated and conventional confidence intervals have coverage far below nominal levels.

2. Distributional Pathology: Fat Tails and Non-Existent Moments

In an exactly identified model (\(K=1\)), the 2SLS estimator has no finite integer moments of any order. It follows a ratio of correlated normal random variables, whose distribution exhibits Cauchy-like fat tails.

Example 1.1 · Cauchy Tails in Exact Identification

Consider the Wald ratio \(\hat{\beta} = \hat{\pi}_{\text{RF}} / \hat{\pi}_{\text{FS}}\). If the first-stage parameter is near zero, the denominator \(\hat{\pi}_{\text{FS}} \sim \Normal(\pi, \sigma^2/N)\) has substantial probability mass near zero. Dividing by numbers arbitrarily close to zero generates extreme outliers in \(\hat{\beta}\). Consequently, the expectation \(\E[\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}}]\) does not exist mathematically when \(K = 1\).

3. Empirical Simulation: First-Stage Strength vs. 2SLS Bias

We execute a Monte Carlo simulation evaluating the distribution of 2SLS across strong, moderate, and weak instruments, inspecting how first-stage \(F\) governs point estimate bias.

Lab 1 · Finite-Sample Bias of 2SLS Across First-Stage Strength

As the first-stage instrument strength decreases toward zero, the 2SLS estimator becomes heavily biased toward the OLS limit, while strong first-stage regimes center at the true structural parameter.

"""Lab S6-L03-1: Finite-sample bias of Two-Stage Least Squares under weak instruments.

SIMULATED: evaluates Monte Carlo finite-sample bias of 2SLS / IV toward OLS
as first-stage instrument strength varies from strong to near-zero.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def run_mc_trial(rng: random.Random, n: int, gamma: float, beta_true: float) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Single replication: returns (ols_beta, iv_beta, first_stage_f)."""
    # Exogenous instrument Z ~ N(0, 1)
    z = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n)]

    # Confounder U ~ N(0, 1) correlated with error terms
    u = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n)]

    # First stage: D = gamma * Z + 0.8 * U + v
    d = [gamma * z[i] + 0.8 * u[i] + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.6) for i in range(n)]

    # Structural equation: Y = beta * D + U + eps (so Cov(D, eps_total) = 0.8 > 0)
    y = [beta_true * d[i] + u[i] + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.6) for i in range(n)]

    # Sample means
    z_bar = sum(z) / n
    d_bar = sum(d) / n
    y_bar = sum(y) / n

    # First stage regression of D on Z: D = pi_0 + pi_1 * Z + e
    ss_z = sum((zi - z_bar) ** 2 for zi in z)
    sp_zd = sum((zi - z_bar) * (di - d_bar) for zi, di in zip(z, d, strict=True))
    pi_1 = sp_zd / ss_z
    pi_0 = d_bar - pi_1 * z_bar
    ss_resid_fs = sum((di - (pi_0 + pi_1 * zi)) ** 2 for zi, di in zip(z, d, strict=True))
    var_resid_fs = ss_resid_fs / (n - 2)
    # First stage F-statistic for H_0: pi_1 = 0
    f_stat = (pi_1**2 * ss_z) / var_resid_fs

    # OLS of Y on D
    ss_d = sum((di - d_bar) ** 2 for di in d)
    sp_dy = sum((di - d_bar) * (yi - y_bar) for di, yi in zip(d, y, strict=True))
    b_ols = sp_dy / ss_d

    # IV / 2SLS: Cov(Y, Z) / Cov(D, Z)
    sp_zy = sum((zi - z_bar) * (yi - y_bar) for zi, yi in zip(z, y, strict=True))
    b_iv = sp_zy / sp_zd if abs(sp_zd) > 1e-12 else float("nan")

    return b_ols, b_iv, f_stat


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(2024)
    n = 250
    reps = 600
    beta_true = 2.00

    gammas = [0.80, 0.30, 0.08]
    labels = ["Strong (gamma=0.80)", "Moderate (gamma=0.30)", "Weak (gamma=0.08)"]

    print("Weak Instrument Finite-Sample Bias Evaluation (SIMULATED):")
    print(f"  Sample size N={n}, Monte Carlo replications M={reps}")
    print(f"  True causal beta: {beta_true:.4f}\n")

    for gamma, label in zip(gammas, labels, strict=True):
        ols_list: list[float] = []
        iv_list: list[float] = []
        f_list: list[float] = []

        for _ in range(reps):
            b_ols, b_iv, f_stat = run_mc_trial(rng, n, gamma, beta_true)
            if not math.isnan(b_iv) and abs(b_iv) < 50.0:  # trim extreme Cauchy tail outliers
                ols_list.append(b_ols)
                iv_list.append(b_iv)
                f_list.append(f_stat)

        mean_ols = sum(ols_list) / len(ols_list)
        mean_iv = sum(iv_list) / len(iv_list)
        mean_f = sum(f_list) / len(f_list)
        bias_iv = mean_iv - beta_true

        print(f"Regime: {label}")
        print(f"  Average first-stage F-statistic: {mean_f:.2f}")
        print(f"  Average OLS estimate:            {mean_ols:.4f} (bias: {mean_ols - beta_true:+.4f})")
        print(f"  Average 2SLS estimate:          {mean_iv:.4f} (bias: {bias_iv:+.4f})")
        print(f"  Median 2SLS estimate:           {sorted(iv_list)[len(iv_list) // 2]:.4f}\n")


main()

The Monte Carlo simulation confirms Theorem 1.1: with a strong instrument (\(F \approx 160.4\)), 2SLS has negligible bias (-0.0039). As first-stage strength weakens to \(F \approx 2.5\), the 2SLS distribution destabilizes and its median shifts toward the biased OLS parameter (2.7969 vs true 2.0000).

Where the Canon Breaks

Conventional Wald \(t\)-tests and confidence intervals (\(\hat{\beta} \pm 1.96 \cdot \hat{\se}\)) break down completely under weak instruments:

  • Distorted Size: Nominal 5% Wald tests reject the null hypothesis at rates exceeding 30% to 50% when \(F < 5\). Empirical researchers cannot trust standard regression output tables.
  • Disjoint Confidence Sets: Under severe weak identification, the Anderson-Rubin confidence set can be empty or unbounded (the entire real line \(\R\)), accurately reflecting that the data contain no identification information about \(\beta\).

Misconceptions

  • A large sample size \(N = 500,000\) guarantees that an instrument is strong.

    First-stage strength depends on the concentration parameter \(\mu^2 = \boldsymbol{\pi}^\top \boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{\pi} / \sigma_v^2\). If the true correlation \(\pi\) is microscopic (\(\pi \sim 1/\sqrt{N}\)), the instrument remains weak regardless of sample size.

  • If the first-stage F-statistic is slightly below the rule-of-thumb threshold of 10, the IV estimates are still mostly reliable.

    When \(F < 10\), the relative bias of 2SLS can exceed 10% to 20% of OLS bias, and standard 95% confidence intervals have true coverage below 85%.

  • Reporting the median of 2SLS estimates resolves the weak instrument problem.

    While the median exists even when integer moments do not, median 2SLS remains biased toward OLS under weak instruments. Weak identification requires robust inference procedures like Anderson-Rubin tests rather than summary statistics.

Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 3.1 · First-Stage F-Statistic Calculation

In a regression with sample size \(N = 200\), single instrument \(Z\), first-stage \(R^2 = 0.040\), calculate the first-stage \(F\)-statistic testing \(H_0: \pi_1 = 0\).

Solution

For a single regressor, \(F = \frac{R^2 / 1}{(1 - R^2) / (N - 2)} = \frac{0.040}{0.960 / 198} = \frac{0.040}{0.004848} \approx 8.25\). This falls below the canonical rule-of-thumb threshold of 10.

Problem 3.2 · Relative Bias Evaluation

Using Theorem 1.1, estimate the relative bias of 2SLS to OLS when the first-stage \(F\)-statistic is \(F = 5.00\) and \(F = 21.00\).

Solution

For \(F = 5.00\): Relative Bias \(\approx 1 / (5 - 1) = 1/4 = 0.25\) (2SLS carries 25% of OLS bias). For \(F = 21.00\): Relative Bias \(\approx 1 / (21 - 1) = 1/20 = 0.05\) (2SLS carries only 5% of OLS bias).

Problem 3.3 · Concentration Parameter Scaling

If the first-stage coefficient is \(\pi = 0.05\), \(\Var(Z) = 1.0\), \(\sigma_v^2 = 1.0\), calculate the concentration parameter \(\mu^2 = N \pi^2 \Var(Z)/\sigma_v^2\) for \(N = 1000\) and \(N = 10,000\).

Solution

For \(N = 1000\): \(\mu^2 = 1000 \times 0.0025 \times 1.0 / 1.0 = 2.50\). For \(N = 10,000\): \(\mu^2 = 10,000 \times 0.0025 = 25.0\). The instrument is weak at \(N=1000\) and moderately strong at \(N=10,000\).

Proof

Problem 3.4 · Non-Existence of Moments for Exactly Identified 2SLS

Prove that when \(K = 1\) instrument is used, the exact distribution of \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}}\) has no finite first moment.

Solution

When \(K=1\), \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Y}}{\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{D}}\). Conditioning on \(\boldsymbol{Z}\), \(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Y}\) and \(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{D}\) are jointly normal. The ratio of two correlated normal variables \(X_1 / X_2\) where \(X_2 \sim \Normal(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)\) has tail probability density decaying as \(f(t) \sim O(t^{-2})\) as \(t \to \infty\). Integrating \(t \cdot f(t)\) diverges logarithmically, proving that \(\E[|\hat{\beta}|] = \infty\).

Problem 3.5 · Derivation of the Anderson-Rubin Statistic

Show that the Anderson-Rubin statistic \(AR(\beta_0) = \frac{(\boldsymbol{Y} - \beta_0 \boldsymbol{D})^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z (\boldsymbol{Y} - \beta_0 \boldsymbol{D}) / K}{(\boldsymbol{Y} - \beta_0 \boldsymbol{D})^\top (\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{P}_Z)(\boldsymbol{Y} - \beta_0 \boldsymbol{D}) / (N - K)}\) has an exact \(F(K, N-K)\) distribution under the null hypothesis \(H_0: \beta = \beta_0\) regardless of first-stage strength.

Solution

Under \(H_0: \beta = \beta_0\), \(\boldsymbol{Y} - \beta_0 \boldsymbol{D} = \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\). Since the exclusion restriction implies \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \perp \boldsymbol{Z}\), \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \Normal(\boldsymbol{0}, \sigma^2 \boldsymbol{I})\). By Craig's theorem and standard quadratic form theory, \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} / \sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(K)\) independently of \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\top (\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{P}_Z)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} / \sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(N - K)\). The ratio is exactly distributed as \(F(K, N - K)\), completely invariant to first-stage parameter \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\).

Problem 3.6 · Convergence of 2SLS to OLS as Instruments Proliferate

Prove that as the number of instruments \(K \to N\), the 2SLS estimator converges algebraically to the OLS estimator.

Solution

The projection matrix is \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z = \boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z})^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top\). When \(K = N\) and \(\boldsymbol{Z}\) is full rank, \(\boldsymbol{Z}\) spans the entire \(\R^N\) space, so \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z = \boldsymbol{I}_N\). Substituting \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z = \boldsymbol{I}_N\) into the 2SLS formula gives \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} = (\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{I} \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{I} \boldsymbol{Y} = (\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{Y} = \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\).

Empirical

Problem 3.7 · Diagnosing Weak Instruments in CEO Compensation Studies

A study instruments CEO compensation with golf handicap changes. The first-stage regression yields \(F = 2.14\). Explain the empirical consequences of reporting 2SLS results from this regression.

Solution

With \(F = 2.14\), the instrument is severely weak. The 2SLS estimate will be dominated by finite-sample bias toward OLS, and the standard error from software will be drastically underestimated, creating spurious statistical significance where no causal identification exists.

Problem 3.8 · Inverting the Anderson-Rubin Test for Confidence Sets

Explain how inverting the Anderson-Rubin test provides identification-robust confidence intervals that can be unbounded when instruments are weak.

Solution

The confidence set is the collection of all candidate values \(\beta_0\) for which the AR statistic fails to reject: \(\{\beta_0 : AR(\beta_0) \le F_{\text{crit}}\}\). Since \(AR(\beta_0)\) is quadratic in \(\beta_0\), if the quadratic term is small (due to small \(\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D}\)), the set of non-rejected values can extend to \((-\infty, +\infty)\), honestly signaling that the data cannot rule out any parameter value.

Problem 3.9 · Jackknife Instrumental Variables Estimation (JIVE)

Describe how the JIVE estimator eliminates the own-observation correlation \(\hat{D}_i \varepsilon_i\) to reduce weak instrument bias.

Solution

In standard 2SLS, \(\hat{D}_i\) is constructed using all observations, including observation \(i\), creating a finite-sample correlation between \(\hat{D}_i\) and \(\varepsilon_i\). JIVE constructs the predicted value \(\hat{D}_{-i}\) by leaving out observation \(i\) when estimating the first stage. This enforces \(\E[\hat{D}_{-i} \varepsilon_i] = 0\), eliminating first-order bias even with many weak instruments.

Research

Problem 3.10 · Local-to-Zero Asymptotics (Staiger & Stock)

Explain Staiger and Stock's local-to-zero framework where \(\boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{C} / \sqrt{N}\), and state why standard asymptotic normality fails.

Solution

In standard asymptotics, \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) is fixed, so \(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{D} \sim O_p(N)\) dominates estimation noise \(O_p(\sqrt{N})\). Staiger and Stock model weak instruments by setting \(\boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{C}/\sqrt{N}\), keeping signal and noise of the same order \(O_p(\sqrt{N})\) for all \(N\). Consequently, the denominator of 2SLS never concentrates at a non-random matrix, and the asymptotic distribution remains a non-standard ratio of normal stochastic integrals.

Problem 3.11 · Weak Instrument Robust Inference in High Dimensions

Analyze how high-dimensional factor models (e.g. macro principal components) used as instruments create spurious identification through overfitting.

Solution

Extracting dozens of principal components from hundreds of macro series fits in-sample idiosyncratic variance. When many factors enter the first stage, the collective concentration parameter spreads thinly across dimensions, while \(K/N\) bias accelerates, driving 2SLS directly into the contaminated OLS limit.

Problem 3.12 · Conditional Likelihood Ratio (CLR) Test

Explain why Moreira's Conditional Likelihood Ratio (CLR) test dominates 2SLS Wald and Anderson-Rubin tests in terms of statistical power.

Solution

The AR test tests both the structural parameter and overidentifying restrictions jointly, sacrificing power in overidentified models. Moreira's CLR conditions on the first-stage matrix to construct an exact, identification-robust test that adjusts its critical value dynamically based on instrument strength, achieving asymptotic minimax power bounds.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What primary econometric pathology occurs when an instrument is weak?

    Under weak instruments, 2SLS loses its central tendency at the true parameter and collapses toward OLS bias.

  2. Using the relative bias approximation \(\text{Bias}_{\text{rel}} \approx \frac{1}{F - 1}\), what is the relative bias of 2SLS when the first-stage F-statistic is \(F = 11.0\)?

    Relative bias is approximately 1 / (11 - 1) = 1 / 10 = 0.10.

  3. Why does the 2SLS estimator have no finite mean (first moment) in an exactly identified model with one instrument (\(K=1\))?

    With \(K=1\), 2SLS is a ratio of normal variables, yielding heavy tails with density decaying as \(t^{-2}\), whose first moment diverges.

  4. What is the behavior of the Anderson-Rubin test statistic under the null hypothesis when instruments are weak?

    The Anderson-Rubin statistic projects structural residuals directly onto instruments, maintaining exact \(F(K, N-K)\) distribution regardless of \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\).

  5. If \(F = 3.0\), what fraction of the OLS omitted variable bias does 2SLS exhibit according to the relative bias approximation?

    Relative bias is approximately 1 / (3 - 1) = 1 / 2 = 0.50 (50%).

  6. How does increasing the number of weak instruments affect 2SLS point estimates?

    Adding instruments increases the rank of the projection operator \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z\), fitting sample noise and driving 2SLS toward OLS.

  7. What does an unbounded Anderson-Rubin confidence interval \((-\infty, +\infty)\) communicate to a researcher?

    An unbounded AR confidence set honestly indicates that the instrument is too weak to rule out any potential causal effect.

  8. In Staiger and Stock's (1997) local-to-zero asymptotic framework, how does the first-stage parameter \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) scale with sample size \(N\)?

    Staiger and Stock model weak identification by scaling \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) as \(1/\sqrt{N}\), balancing signal and sampling noise.
