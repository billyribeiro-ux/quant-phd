Instrumental Variables & Two-Stage Least Squares

Relevance, Exclusion Restrictions, the Wald Ratio, and Projection Geometry

Why It Matters

In financial economics, regressors are rarely exogenous. Capital structure, executive compensation, corporate governance, and central bank policy rates are chosen deliberately by optimizing agents in response to unobserved future prospects. Ordinary least squares estimates of such relationships suffer from omitted variable bias and reverse causality, yielding severely misleading parameter signs. The instrumental variables method provides a mathematical mechanism to isolate exogenous variation in endogenous treatments, recovering true structural elasticities.

1. The Endogeneity Problem and Instrumental Variables

Consider the linear structural equation relating response \(Y_i\) to treatment \(D_i\):

\[ Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \varepsilon_i. \]

When treatment is endogenous, \(\Cov(D_i, \varepsilon_i) \ne 0\). The OLS estimator converges in probability to:

\[ \operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = \beta + \frac{\Cov(D_i, \varepsilon_i)}{\Var(D_i)} \ne \beta. \]

To identify \(\beta\), we introduce an auxiliary variable \(Z_i\) that shifts \(D_i\) without directly affecting \(Y_i\).

Definition 1.1 · Instrumental Variables

An instrumental variables design identifies the causal effect of an endogenous regressor \(D_i\) on outcome \(Y_i\) using an external instrument \(Z_i\) satisfying two core conditions:

  1. Instrument Relevance: \(Z_i\) is correlated with the endogenous regressor: \[ \Cov(Z_i, D_i) \ne 0. \]
  2. Instrument Exogeneity (Exclusion Restriction): \(Z_i\) is uncorrelated with the structural disturbance \(\varepsilon_i\): \[ \Cov(Z_i, \varepsilon_i) = 0. \]

Under these conditions, \(Z_i\) affects \(Y_i\) exclusively through its impact on \(D_i\).

Theorem 1.1 · The Wald Estimator Identity

Assumptions:

  1. Linear structural equation: \(Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \varepsilon_i\).
  2. Instrument relevance: \(\Cov(Z_i, D_i) \ne 0\).
  3. Exclusion restriction: \(\Cov(Z_i, \varepsilon_i) = 0\).

Conclusion: The causal parameter \(\beta\) is uniquely identified by the ratio of population covariances:

\[ \beta = \frac{\Cov(Y_i, Z_i)}{\Cov(D_i, Z_i)}. \]

When the instrument \(Z_i \in \{0, 1\}\) is binary, this equals the Wald ratio of differences in conditional expectations:

\[ \beta = \frac{\E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]}{\E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]}. \]
Proof

Compute the covariance between \(Y_i\) and \(Z_i\) using the structural equation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Cov(Y_i, Z_i) &= \Cov(\alpha + \beta D_i + \varepsilon_i, Z_i) \\ &= \beta \Cov(D_i, Z_i) + \Cov(\varepsilon_i, Z_i). \end{aligned} \]

Applying the exclusion restriction \(\Cov(\varepsilon_i, Z_i) = 0\):

\[ \Cov(Y_i, Z_i) = \beta \Cov(D_i, Z_i). \]

Dividing by \(\Cov(D_i, Z_i)\) (valid since \(\Cov(D_i, Z_i) \ne 0\) by relevance):

\[ \beta = \frac{\Cov(Y_i, Z_i)}{\Cov(D_i, Z_i)}. \]

For a binary indicator \(Z_i \in \{0, 1\}\), by definition of covariance with a Bernoulli variable, \(\Cov(W_i, Z_i) = \Prob(Z_i = 1)\Prob(Z_i = 0)[\E[W_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \E[W_i \mid Z_i = 0]]\). Taking the ratio cancels the probability scaling, establishing the Wald difference-in-means ratio.

Remark 1.1 · Reduced Form and First Stage

The Wald estimator expresses the structural causal parameter as the ratio of the reduced-form effect of the instrument on the outcome divided by the first-stage effect of the instrument on the treatment.

2. Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) and Projection Geometry

When multiple instruments \(\boldsymbol{Z}_i\) are available, Two-Stage Least Squares generalises the Wald ratio through least-squares projection.

Definition 1.2 · Two-Stage Least Squares Estimator

The Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) estimator operates in two sequential projection steps:

  1. First Stage: Regress endogenous regressor \(\boldsymbol{D}\) on instrument matrix \(\boldsymbol{Z}\) by OLS to compute predicted values \(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}} = \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D}\), where \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z = \boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z})^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top\) is the orthogonal projection operator.
  2. Second Stage: Regress outcome \(\boldsymbol{Y}\) on predicted values \(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\) by OLS: \[ \hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} = (\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{D}})^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol{D}}^\top \boldsymbol{Y} = (\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D})^{-1} \boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{Y}. \]
Theorem 1.2 · Equivalence of 2SLS and Wald with Exactly Identified Models

Assumptions:

  1. Single scalar endogenous regressor \(D\) and single scalar instrument \(Z\).
  2. Sample covariance \(\widehat{\Cov}(D, Z) \ne 0\) and \(\widehat{\Var}(Z) > 0\).

Conclusion: The 2SLS sample slope coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}}\) is algebraically identical to the sample covariance ratio:

\[ \hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} = \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(Y, Z)}{\widehat{\Cov}(D, Z)} = \hat{\beta}_{\text{Wald}}. \]
Proof

In the first stage, \(\hat{D}_i = \hat{\pi}_0 + \hat{\pi}_1 Z_i\), where \(\hat{\pi}_1 = \widehat{\Cov}(D, Z) / \widehat{\Var}(Z)\). In the second stage, regressing \(Y_i\) on \(\hat{D}_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} &= \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(Y, \hat{D})}{\widehat{\Var}(\hat{D})} \\ &= \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(Y, \hat{\pi}_1 Z)}{\widehat{\Var}(\hat{\pi}_1 Z)} \\ &= \frac{\hat{\pi}_1 \widehat{\Cov}(Y, Z)}{\hat{\pi}_1^2 \widehat{\Var}(Z)} \\ &= \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(Y, Z)}{\hat{\pi}_1 \widehat{\Var}(Z)} \\ &= \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(Y, Z)}{[\widehat{\Cov}(D, Z)/\widehat{\Var}(Z)] \widehat{\Var}(Z)} \\ &= \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(Y, Z)}{\widehat{\Cov}(D, Z)}. \end{aligned} \]

This confirms algebraic equality.

3. Empirical Simulation: The Wald Identity in Practice

We verify this numerical identity on a simulated sample with severe endogeneity (\(\Cov(D, \varepsilon) > 0\)), confirming that OLS is biased while the Wald ratio and 2SLS recover the true structural parameter.

Lab 1 · Wald Ratio and Covariance Equivalence

The sample Wald ratio of differences in means identically equals the ratio of sample covariances within floating-point precision, and eliminates the endogeneity bias exhibited by naive OLS.

"""Lab S6-L02-1: Instrumental variables and the Wald estimator identity.

SIMULATED: verifies that the Wald ratio of differences in means identically equals
the ratio of sample covariances, and demonstrates that 2SLS / IV recovers the true
causal parameter under endogeneity where OLS fails.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(101)
    n = 12000

    # True parameters: structural equation Y = alpha + beta * D + eps
    true_alpha = 1.00
    true_beta = 3.00

    # Binary instrument Z in {0, 1}
    z = [1 if rng.random() < 0.5 else 0 for _ in range(n)]

    # Confounder U affects both D and Y (creating endogeneity)
    u = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.2) for _ in range(n)]

    # First stage: D is endogenous, positively correlated with Z and U
    # D = gamma_0 + gamma_1 * Z + pi * U + v
    gamma_1 = 0.65
    d = [1 if (0.2 + gamma_1 * z[i] + 0.8 * u[i] + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.5)) > 0.8 else 0 for i in range(n)]

    # Structural outcome Y: affected by D, U (unobserved), and independent noise
    eps = [u[i] + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.8) for i in range(n)]  # Cov(D, eps) > 0
    y = [true_alpha + true_beta * d[i] + eps[i] for i in range(n)]

    # 1. Compute Wald estimator from subgroup sample means
    n_z1 = sum(z)
    n_z0 = n - n_z1

    y_bar_z1 = sum(y[i] for i in range(n) if z[i] == 1) / n_z1
    y_bar_z0 = sum(y[i] for i in range(n) if z[i] == 0) / n_z0

    d_bar_z1 = sum(d[i] for i in range(n) if z[i] == 1) / n_z1
    d_bar_z0 = sum(d[i] for i in range(n) if z[i] == 0) / n_z0

    wald_beta = (y_bar_z1 - y_bar_z0) / (d_bar_z1 - d_bar_z0)

    # 2. Compute covariance ratio: Cov(Y, Z) / Cov(D, Z)
    z_bar = sum(z) / n
    y_bar = sum(y) / n
    d_bar = sum(d) / n

    cov_yz = sum((y[i] - y_bar) * (z[i] - z_bar) for i in range(n)) / n
    cov_dz = sum((d[i] - d_bar) * (z[i] - z_bar) for i in range(n)) / n
    cov_ratio_beta = cov_yz / cov_dz

    # 3. Naive OLS estimator: Cov(Y, D) / Var(D)
    cov_yd = sum((y[i] - y_bar) * (d[i] - d_bar) for i in range(n)) / n
    var_d = sum((d[i] - d_bar) ** 2 for i in range(n)) / n
    ols_beta = cov_yd / var_d

    discrepancy = abs(wald_beta - cov_ratio_beta)

    print("Instrumental Variables Identification & Wald Equivalence (SIMULATED):")
    print(f"  Sample size N: {n}")
    print(f"  True causal beta: {true_beta:.4f}")
    print(f"  Naive OLS estimate: {ols_beta:.4f} (biased by endogeneity)")
    print("\nWald Estimator Components:")
    print(f"  E[Y|Z=1] - E[Y|Z=0] (Reduced Form): {y_bar_z1 - y_bar_z0:.4f}")
    print(f"  E[D|Z=1] - E[D|Z=0] (First Stage):   {d_bar_z1 - d_bar_z0:.4f}")
    print(f"  Wald ratio: {wald_beta:.6f}")
    print(f"  Sample covariance ratio: {cov_ratio_beta:.6f}")
    print(f"  Numerical discrepancy: {discrepancy:.2e}")
    print(f"  Wald recovery error: {abs(wald_beta - true_beta):.4f}")


main()

  Sample size N: 12000
  True causal beta: 3.0000

Wald Estimator Components:

The empirical results confirm Theorem 1.2: the numerical discrepancy between the Wald ratio and the covariance ratio is exactly 0.00e+00. While naive OLS estimates beta as 4.6696 (biased upwards by 55.7%), the IV estimator recovers 2.9290 (within 2.4% of true parameter 3.0000).

Where the Canon Breaks

The instrumental variables estimator is fragile because the exclusion restriction is untestable in exactly identified models:

  • Direct Causal Paths: In financial studies, proposed instruments often violate exclusion by directly influencing asset pricing. For example, geographic distance to bank headquarters (used as an instrument for credit access) directly correlates with local real estate prices, labor markets, and infrastructure.
  • Omitted General Shocks: Regulatory shocks (e.g. state-level corporate law changes) simultaneously alter corporate governance, tax exposure, and local competition. Attributing the entire reduced-form movement to the governance channel alone overstates the structural parameter.

Misconceptions

  • A high correlation between instrument \(Z\) and outcome \(Y\) proves that \(Z\) is a valid instrument.

    Correlation with the outcome is the reduced-form product of the first stage and the structural effect. Exogeneity requires \(\Cov(Z, \varepsilon) = 0\), which is an assumption about the unobservable error term that cannot be confirmed by inspecting \(\Cov(Z, Y)\).

  • Running 2SLS manually by substituting first-stage fitted values into an OLS software package produces correct standard errors.

    Manual OLS in the second stage calculates residuals as \(Y_i - \hat{\beta}\hat{D}_i\) rather than the true structural residuals \(Y_i - \hat{\beta}D_i\). This systematically underestimates residual variance and produces invalid test statistics. Production IV software re-evaluates residuals using actual \(D_i\).

  • Instrumental variables always produce less biased estimates than ordinary least squares in finite samples.

    IV is asymptotically consistent but biased in finite samples. When instrument relevance is weak, 2SLS is heavily biased toward OLS and can have larger mean squared error than OLS.

Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 2.1 · Evaluating the Wald Ratio from Summary Statistics

In an exogenous monetary policy shock study, \(\E[Y \mid Z=1] = 0.045\), \(\E[Y \mid Z=0] = 0.015\), \(\E[D \mid Z=1] = 0.060\), and \(\E[D \mid Z=0] = 0.020\). Calculate the reduced-form difference, first-stage difference, and the Wald estimate of \(\beta\).

Solution

Reduced-form effect: \(\Delta Y = 0.045 - 0.015 = 0.030\). First-stage effect: \(\Delta D = 0.060 - 0.020 = 0.040\). The Wald estimator is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{Wald}} = \Delta Y / \Delta D = 0.030 / 0.040 = 0.750\).

Problem 2.2 · OLS Asymptotic Bias Calculation

Suppose true \(\beta = 1.50\), \(\Var(D) = 2.00\), and \(\Cov(D, \varepsilon) = 0.60\). Compute the probability limit of the OLS estimator and the asymptotic bias percentage.

Solution

The probability limit of OLS is \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = \beta + \Cov(D, \varepsilon)/\Var(D) = 1.50 + 0.60 / 2.00 = 1.50 + 0.30 = 1.80\). The asymptotic bias is \(+0.30\), representing a \(+20.0\%\) distortion.

Problem 2.3 · 2SLS Projection Matrix Idempotence

Verify that the projection operator \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z = \boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z})^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top\) satisfies idempotence: \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{P}_Z = \boldsymbol{P}_Z\).

Solution

Multiplying \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z\) by itself: \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{P}_Z = [\boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z})^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top][\boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z})^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top] = \boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z})^{-1}[(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z})(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z})^{-1}]\boldsymbol{Z}^\top = \boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{Z})^{-1}\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top = \boldsymbol{P}_Z\).

Problem 2.4 · Consistency of the IV Estimator

Prove that under assumptions \(\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \xrightarrow{\;p\;} \boldsymbol{0}\) and \(\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{D} \xrightarrow{\;p\;} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ZD} \ne \boldsymbol{0}\), the IV estimator is consistent: \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}} \xrightarrow{\;p\;} \beta\).

Solution

Substitute \(\boldsymbol{Y} = \boldsymbol{D}\beta + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) into the IV formula: \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}} = (\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top(\boldsymbol{D}\beta + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = \beta + (\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}(\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{Z}^\top \boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\). By Slutsky's theorem and the continuous mapping theorem, \(\operatorname{plim}\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}} = \beta + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ZD}^{-1}\boldsymbol{0} = \beta\).

Problem 2.5 · Asymptotic Variance of 2SLS

Derive the asymptotic variance of the 2SLS estimator under conditional homoskedasticity \(\E[\varepsilon_i^2 \mid \boldsymbol{Z}_i] = \sigma^2\).

Solution

Expanding the estimation error \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}} - \beta = (\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\). Under homoskedasticity, \(\Var(\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \mid \boldsymbol{Z}) = \sigma^2 \boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D} = \sigma^2 (\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D})\). Thus, \(\operatorname{Avar}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}}) = \sigma^2 (\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\).

Problem 2.6 · Efficiency Ranking: OLS vs IV Under Exogeneity

Prove that when \(D\) is exogenous (\(\Cov(D, \varepsilon) = 0\)), the asymptotic variance of the OLS estimator is strictly less than or equal to that of the IV estimator.

Solution

Under exogeneity, \(\operatorname{Avar}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}) = \sigma^2 (\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\) and \(\operatorname{Avar}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}}) = \sigma^2 (\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D})^{-1}\). Since \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z\) is an orthogonal projection matrix, \(\boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{D} - \boldsymbol{D}^\top \boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D} = \boldsymbol{D}^\top (\boldsymbol{I} - \boldsymbol{P}_Z)\boldsymbol{D} \ge 0\) in the positive semi-definite order. Inverting reverses the order, proving \(\operatorname{Avar}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}) \le \operatorname{Avar}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}})\).

Empirical

Problem 2.7 · Weather Instruments in Commodity Futures

Analyze the validity of rainfall anomalies as an instrument for agricultural supply in estimating price elasticity of demand. Identify one potential exclusion violation.

Solution

Rainfall strongly affects crop yields (relevance). However, extreme weather simultaneously disrupts transport infrastructure (barge traffic on rivers), which directly impacts shipping costs and consumer demand, violating the exclusion restriction unless transport costs are explicitly controlled.

Problem 2.8 · Peer Firm Stock Returns as Instruments

Researchers often instrument a firm's investment with peer firms' average investment. Discuss why correlated industry demand shocks invalidate this instrument.

Solution

If unobserved industry-wide technology or consumer demand shifts occur, they enter the structural error term \(\varepsilon_i\) of all firms simultaneously. Peer investment correlates with \(\varepsilon_i\), violating \(\Cov(Z, \varepsilon) = 0\).

Problem 2.9 · Instrumental Variables with Binary Endogenous Treatment

Explain the Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) interpretation of the Wald estimator when treatment effects are heterogeneous and units include compliers, defiers, and always-takers.

Solution

Under monotonicity (no defiers), the Wald estimator identifies the average treatment effect specifically for compliers—the subpopulation induced to adopt treatment by the instrument: \(\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D(1) > D(0)]\). It does not identify the ATE for always-takers or never-takers.

Research

Problem 2.10 · High-Frequency Identification of Monetary Policy Shocks

Evaluate how 30-minute Federal Funds futures rate surprises around FOMC announcements satisfy relevance and exclusion for macroeconomic VAR identification.

Solution

The narrow 30-minute window ensures that macroeconomic fundamentals do not change during the window, satisfying exclusion \(\Cov(Z, \varepsilon) = 0\). Future surprises directly reflect unexpected policy revisions, satisfying relevance \(\Cov(Z, D) \ne 0\). However, central bank information effects can confound the instrument if market participants view rate surprises as disclosures of private macroeconomic forecasts.

Problem 2.11 · Many Instruments and 2SLS Overfitting

Explain why adding dozens of instruments causes 2SLS to converge toward the biased OLS estimator in finite samples.

Solution

As the number of instruments \(K\) grows relative to sample size \(N\), the projection operator \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z\) fits in-sample noise, driving \(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}} \to \boldsymbol{D}\). In the limit as \(K \to N\), \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z \to \boldsymbol{I}\), causing \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{2SLS}}\) to reproduce \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\) exactly, restoring the full omitted variable bias.

Problem 2.12 · Limited Information Maximum Likelihood (LIML)

Contrast LIML with 2SLS in the presence of many instruments, explaining why LIML is median-unbiased.

Solution

LIML minimizes the ratio of structural residual variance to reduced-form error variance. Geometrically, LIML treats the endogenous regressor and response symmetrically rather than projecting in an asymmetric two-stage sequence. This symmetry removes the first-order asymptotic bias that plagues 2SLS when \(K/N\) is non-negligible.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What are the two mandatory conditions for an instrumental variable to be valid?

    A valid instrument must shift the treatment (relevance) and affect the outcome only through treatment (exclusion).

  2. How is the Wald estimator defined for a binary instrument \(Z \in \{0, 1\}\)?

    The Wald estimator is the reduced-form difference in outcomes divided by the first-stage difference in treatment.

  3. If \(\Cov(Y, Z) = 0.050\) and \(\Cov(D, Z) = 0.020\), what is the instrumental variables estimate of \(\beta\)?

    The IV estimator is the ratio of covariances: 0.050 / 0.020 = 2.5.

  4. Why is the exclusion restriction \(\Cov(Z, \varepsilon) = 0\) untestable in an exactly identified model?

    With one instrument and one endogenous variable, the single moment condition sets sample covariance to zero by construction.

  5. What role does the projection matrix \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z\) play in Two-Stage Least Squares?

    \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z\) replaces endogenous \(\boldsymbol{D}\) with its linear projection onto the exogenous instruments \(\boldsymbol{Z}\).

  6. Given reduced-form effect \(\Delta Y = 0.030\) and first-stage effect \(\Delta D = 0.020\), evaluate the Wald ratio.

    Wald ratio = 0.030 / 0.020 = 1.5.

  7. What happens to the 2SLS standard error if instrument relevance \(\Cov(D, Z)\) is very close to zero?

    The asymptotic variance of IV has \(\Cov(D, Z)^2\) in the denominator; as relevance vanishes, variance explodes.

  8. Under what circumstance does 2SLS equal OLS?

    If \(Z = D\), \(\boldsymbol{P}_Z \boldsymbol{D} = \boldsymbol{D}\), making 2SLS collapse to the standard OLS formula.
