Difference-in-Differences & Parallel Trends

The Canonical 2x2 Design, Counterfactual Trajectories, and Fixed Effects Equivalence

Why It Matters

Financial policy changes—such as bank capital surcharges, trading fee revisions, margin rules, and disclosure mandates—rarely occur in randomized laboratories. Instead, regulators introduce rules for specific institutions while leaving others exempt. Difference-in-differences allows quantitative researchers to exploit these quasi-experiments. By comparing the trajectory of treated entities before and after the reform against an untreated control group, researchers purge both static unobserved firm heterogeneity and common time-series macro shocks.

1. The Canonical 2x2 Difference-in-Differences Design

In the canonical 2x2 design, units \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) are observed across two time periods \(t \in \{0, 1\}\), representing pre-treatment and post-treatment intervals. A subset of units adopts treatment at \(t=1\) (\(D_i = 1\)), while control units remain untreated (\(D_i = 0\)).

Definition 1.1 · Difference-in-Differences

The difference-in-differences estimand is the difference between the average outcome change over time for the treated group and the average outcome change over time for the control group:

\[ \tau_{\text{DiD}} = [\E[Y_{i1} \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_{i0} \mid D_i = 1]] - [\E[Y_{i1} \mid D_i = 0] - \E[Y_{i0} \mid D_i = 0]], \]

where \(Y_{it}\) is the observed outcome of unit \(i\) in period \(t\).

Definition 1.2 · The Parallel Trends Assumption

The core identification condition for difference-in-differences is the Parallel Trends Assumption: in the counterfactual state of no treatment, the average outcome of the treated group would have evolved along the same trajectory as the control group:

\[ \E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 1] = \E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 0]. \]
Definition 1.3 · The No-Anticipation Assumption

The No-Anticipation Assumption requires that treatment has no causal impact prior to its formal implementation date:

\[ Y_{i0}(1) = Y_{i0}(0), \quad \forall i. \]

Units do not alter their pre-treatment baseline in anticipation of future policy changes.

Theorem 1.1 · Causal Identification Under Parallel Trends

Assumptions:

  1. Parallel trends: \(\E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 1] = \E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 0]\).
  2. No anticipation: \(Y_{i0} = Y_{i0}(0)\) for all \(i\).
  3. SUTVA holds across units and time.

Conclusion: The difference-in-differences estimand uniquely identifies the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) in period \(t=1\):

\[ \tau_{\text{DiD}} = \E[Y_{i1}(1) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 1] = \tau_{\text{ATT}, 1}. \]
Proof

Under the switching equation and no anticipation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[Y_{i0} \mid D_i = 1] &= \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 1], \\ \E[Y_{i0} \mid D_i = 0] &= \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 0], \\ \E[Y_{i1} \mid D_i = 0] &= \E[Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 0], \\ \E[Y_{i1} \mid D_i = 1] &= \E[Y_{i1}(1) \mid D_i = 1]. \end{aligned} \]

Substitute these into the difference-in-differences formula:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_{\text{DiD}} &= [\E[Y_{i1}(1) \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 1]] \\ &\quad - [\E[Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 0] - \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 0]]. \end{aligned} \]

By the parallel trends assumption, replace the control trend with the counterfactual treated trend:

\[ \E[Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 0] - \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 0] = \E[Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 1]. \]

Substituting this equality:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_{\text{DiD}} &= [\E[Y_{i1}(1) \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 1]] \\ &\quad - [\E[Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 1]] \\ &= \E[Y_{i1}(1) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 1] \\ &= \tau_{\text{ATT}, 1}. \end{aligned} \]

This proves that DiD identifies the causal treatment effect on treated units.

Remark 1.1 · The Difference-in-Differences Canon

As Angrist & Pischke (2009) establish, difference-in-differences resolves selection on time-invariant unobservables by using pre-treatment differences to establish baseline counterfactual levels.

2. Equivalence to Two-Way Fixed Effects Regression

In empirical work, difference-in-differences is implemented via linear regression with unit and time fixed effects.

Theorem 1.2 · TWFE Regression Equivalence in 2x2 Panels

Assumptions:

  1. Balanced 2x2 panel: units \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), periods \(t \in \{0, 1\}\).
  2. Two-way fixed effects (TWFE) OLS specification: \[ Y_{it} = \mu + \gamma \cdot \operatorname{Treat}_i + \lambda \cdot \operatorname{Post}_t + \beta \cdot (\operatorname{Treat}_i \times \operatorname{Post}_t) + \varepsilon_{it}. \]

Conclusion: The ordinary least squares interaction coefficient \(\hat{\beta}\) is algebraically identical to the sample difference-in-differences estimand \(\hat{\tau}_{\text{DiD}}\):

\[ \hat{\beta}_{\text{TWFE}} = (\bar{Y}_{T,1} - \bar{Y}_{T,0}) - (\bar{Y}_{C,1} - \bar{Y}_{C,0}) = \hat{\tau}_{\text{DiD}}. \]
Proof

By the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, \(\hat{\beta}\) is obtained by regressing the residual of \((\operatorname{Treat}_i \times \operatorname{Post}_t)\) on \(Y_{it}\) after projecting out the intercept, \(\operatorname{Treat}_i\), and \(\operatorname{Post}_t\). The expected values of the 4 group cells are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[Y \mid \operatorname{Treat}=0, \operatorname{Post}=0] &= \mu, \\ \E[Y \mid \operatorname{Treat}=0, \operatorname{Post}=1] &= \mu + \lambda, \\ \E[Y \mid \operatorname{Treat}=1, \operatorname{Post}=0] &= \mu + \gamma, \\ \E[Y \mid \operatorname{Treat}=1, \operatorname{Post}=1] &= \mu + \gamma + \lambda + \beta. \end{aligned} \]

Evaluating the double difference:

\[ \begin{aligned} &(\E[Y \mid T=1, P=1] - \E[Y \mid T=1, P=0]) - (\E[Y \mid T=0, P=1] - \E[Y \mid T=0, P=0]) \\ &= [(\mu + \gamma + \lambda + \beta) - (\mu + \gamma)] - [(\mu + \lambda) - \mu] \\ &= (\lambda + \beta) - \lambda = \beta. \end{aligned} \]

Because OLS reproduces sample group means in saturated indicator models, \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{TWFE}} = \hat{\tau}_{\text{DiD}}\) exactly.

Remark 1.2 · Standard Error Clustering

In difference-in-differences panel models, repeated observations on the same cross-sectional unit induce severe serial correlation. Standard OLS standard errors underestimate uncertainty by 50% or more. Empirical inference must use clustered standard errors at the unit level to preserve valid test sizes.

3. Empirical Demonstration: Exact 2x2 Panel Equivalence

We test this algebraic equivalence on a constructed 2x2 panel using exact rational arithmetic (fractions.Fraction) to verify that \(\hat{\tau}_{\text{DiD}} - \hat{\beta}_{\text{TWFE}} = 0\).

Lab 1 · Exact Equivalence of 2x2 DiD and TWFE

Evaluating canonical 2x2 DiD and TWFE interaction regression on a constructed balanced panel in exact rational arithmetic confirms algebraic identity with zero numerical discrepancy.

"""Lab S6-L04-1: Difference-in-Differences and Two-Way Fixed Effects equivalence.

Constructed example: verifies using exact rational arithmetic (fractions.Fraction)
that the canonical 2x2 DiD estimand identically equals the interaction coefficient
from a two-way fixed-effects OLS regression.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def solve_ols(x_mat: list[list[Fraction]], y_vec: list[Fraction]) -> list[Fraction]:
    """Solve (X^T X) beta = X^T y using exact Gauss-Jordan elimination."""
    p = len(x_mat[0])
    n = len(x_mat)

    # Compute X^T X (p x p) and X^T y (p x 1)
    xtx: list[list[Fraction]] = [
        [sum((x_mat[i][j] * x_mat[i][k] for i in range(n)), Fraction(0)) for k in range(p)] for j in range(p)
    ]
    xty: list[Fraction] = [sum((x_mat[i][j] * y_vec[i] for i in range(n)), Fraction(0)) for j in range(p)]

    # Augmented matrix [XTX | XTy]
    aug: list[list[Fraction]] = [xtx[i] + [xty[i]] for i in range(p)]

    # Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting
    for col in range(p):
        pivot = col
        while pivot < p and aug[pivot][col] == Fraction(0):
            pivot += 1
        if pivot == p:
            raise ValueError("matrix is singular")
        aug[col], aug[pivot] = aug[pivot], aug[col]

        piv_val = aug[col][col]
        for c in range(col, p + 1):
            aug[col][c] = aug[col][c] / piv_val

        for r in range(p):
            if r != col and aug[r][col] != Fraction(0):
                factor = aug[r][col]
                for c in range(col, p + 1):
                    aug[r][c] = aug[r][c] - factor * aug[col][c]

    return [aug[r][p] for r in range(p)]


def main() -> None:
    # 2x2 balanced panel: 2 units in Control, 2 units in Treated, observed at Pre (t=0) and Post (t=1)
    # Unit 1: Control, pre=10, post=14
    # Unit 2: Control, pre=12, post=16
    # Unit 3: Treated, pre=20, post=31
    # Unit 4: Treated, pre=22, post=33

    data = [
        # (unit, treat, post, y)
        (1, Fraction(0), Fraction(0), Fraction(10)),
        (1, Fraction(0), Fraction(1), Fraction(14)),
        (2, Fraction(0), Fraction(0), Fraction(12)),
        (2, Fraction(0), Fraction(1), Fraction(16)),
        (3, Fraction(1), Fraction(0), Fraction(20)),
        (3, Fraction(1), Fraction(1), Fraction(31)),
        (4, Fraction(1), Fraction(0), Fraction(22)),
        (4, Fraction(1), Fraction(1), Fraction(33)),
    ]

    # 1. Compute canonical 2x2 DiD estimand from group sample means
    y_ctrl_pre = sum(row[3] for row in data if row[1] == 0 and row[2] == 0) / 2
    y_ctrl_post = sum(row[3] for row in data if row[1] == 0 and row[2] == 1) / 2
    y_treat_pre = sum(row[3] for row in data if row[1] == 1 and row[2] == 0) / 2
    y_treat_post = sum(row[3] for row in data if row[1] == 1 and row[2] == 1) / 2

    ctrl_trend = y_ctrl_post - y_ctrl_pre
    treat_change = y_treat_post - y_treat_pre
    did_estimand = treat_change - ctrl_trend

    # 2. Build regression design matrix: Y = beta_0 + beta_treat * Treat + beta_post * Post + beta_did * (Treat * Post)
    x_mat: list[list[Fraction]] = []
    y_vec: list[Fraction] = []

    for _, treat, post, y in data:
        interact = treat * post
        x_mat.append([Fraction(1), treat, post, interact])
        y_vec.append(y)

    beta_hat = solve_ols(x_mat, y_vec)

    b0, b_treat, b_post, b_did = beta_hat

    print("Canonical 2x2 Difference-in-Differences Equivalence (Constructed Panel):")
    print(f"  Control Pre Mean:   {y_ctrl_pre} ({float(y_ctrl_pre):.2f})")
    print(f"  Control Post Mean:  {y_ctrl_post} ({float(y_ctrl_post):.2f})")
    print(f"  Control Trend:      {ctrl_trend} ({float(ctrl_trend):.2f})")
    print(f"  Treated Pre Mean:   {y_treat_pre} ({float(y_treat_pre):.2f})")
    print(f"  Treated Post Mean:  {y_treat_post} ({float(y_treat_post):.2f})")
    print(f"  Treated Change:     {treat_change} ({float(treat_change):.2f})")
    print(f"  DiD Estimand:       {did_estimand} ({float(did_estimand):.2f})\n")

    print("Two-Way Fixed Effects OLS Estimates (Exact Fractions):")
    print(f"  Intercept (beta_0):       {b0}")
    print(f"  Treated Effect (beta_T):  {b_treat}")
    print(f"  Time Trend (beta_Post):   {b_post}")
    print(f"  Interaction (beta_DiD):   {b_did}")
    print(f"  Exact Equivalence Check:  {did_estimand == b_did}")
    print(f"  Algebraic Difference:     {did_estimand - b_did}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The validity of difference-in-differences hinges entirely on parallel trends:

  • Differential Pre-Trends: If treated firms were already growing faster or deteriorating faster prior to the event, the control group trajectory does not represent the counterfactual. The parallel trends assumption fails, and DiD attributes pre-existing divergence to the policy shock.
  • Functional Form Sensitivity: Parallel trends in levels (\(Y_{it}\)) does not imply parallel trends in logarithms (\(\ln Y_{it}\)) or percentage changes. A policy evaluated in levels may appear positive while in logs it appears zero or negative.

Misconceptions

  • Passing a statistical test of equal pre-treatment trends proves that the parallel trends assumption holds.

    Testing pre-trends tests the past, not the counterfactual future. Parallel trends is an untestable assumption about what would have happened post-treatment in the absence of treatment. A shock that occurs simultaneously with treatment can break parallel trends even if pre-trends were identical.

  • Difference-in-differences requires that the treated and control groups have identical baseline levels before treatment.

    DiD permits substantial baseline differences in levels. Fixed unit effects \(\gamma_i\) explicitly absorb time-invariant level disparities. DiD requires only that the counterfactual changes (slopes) over time be identical.

  • Standard OLS t-statistics in DiD regressions are reliable as long as the sample size of firms is large.

    As Bertrand, Duflo & Mullainathan (2004) proved, uncorrected serial correlation in multi-year panels inflates t-statistics drastically, causing nominal 5% tests to reject the null at rates of 45% or higher. Standard errors must be clustered at the entity level.

Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 4.1 · Computing 2x2 DiD from Cell Means

In a financial transaction tax study, average daily trading volumes are: Control Pre = 120, Control Post = 110, Treated Pre = 250, Treated Post = 210. Calculate the control trend, the treated change, and the DiD estimate.

Solution

Control trend: \(\Delta Y_C = 110 - 120 = -10\). Treated change: \(\Delta Y_T = 210 - 250 = -40\). The DiD estimate is \(\hat{\tau}_{\text{DiD}} = \Delta Y_T - \Delta Y_C = -40 - (-10) = -30\). The transaction tax caused a 30-unit reduction in volume.

Problem 4.2 · Saturated OLS Coefficient Recovery

From the cell means in Problem 4.1, write down the 4 regression coefficients (\(\mu, \gamma, \lambda, \beta\)) for the TWFE specification \(Y_{it} = \mu + \gamma \operatorname{Treat} + \lambda \operatorname{Post} + \beta (\operatorname{Treat} \times \operatorname{Post})\).

Solution

\(\mu = \bar{Y}_{C,0} = 120\); \(\gamma = \bar{Y}_{T,0} - \bar{Y}_{C,0} = 250 - 120 = 130\); \(\lambda = \bar{Y}_{C,1} - \bar{Y}_{C,0} = 110 - 120 = -10\); \(\beta = \hat{\tau}_{\text{DiD}} = -30\).

Problem 4.3 · Testing Pre-Trends in a 3-Period Design

In a panel observed at \(t=-1, 0, 1\), average outcomes are: Control: [-1: 10, 0: 12, 1: 15]; Treated: [-1: 20, 0: 22, 1: 28]. Test whether pre-trends were parallel, and compute the post-treatment DiD effect.

Solution

Pre-treatment trend for Control: \(12 - 10 = +2\). Pre-treatment trend for Treated: \(22 - 20 = +2\). Pre-trends are identical (\(\Delta = 0\)), supporting parallel trends. Post-treatment Control change: \(15 - 12 = +3\). Post-treatment Treated change: \(28 - 22 = +6\). The DiD estimate is \(+6 - (+3) = +3\).

Problem 4.4 · Derivation of Bias Under Parallel Trends Violation

Let \(\delta = \E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i=0]\) be the non-parallel trend divergence. Prove that \(\tau_{\text{DiD}} = \tau_{\text{ATT}, 1} + \delta\).

Solution

Expanding \(\tau_{\text{DiD}} = [\E[Y_{i1}(1) \mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i=1]] - [\E[Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i=0] - \E[Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i=0]]\). Adding and subtracting \(\E[Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i=1]\): \(\tau_{\text{DiD}} = \{\E[Y_{i1}(1) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i=1]\} + \{[\E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i=1]] - [\E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i=0]]\} = \tau_{\text{ATT}, 1} + \delta\). If \(\delta \ne 0\), DiD is biased by exactly the differential trend.

Problem 4.5 · Frisch-Waugh-Lovell Proof of TWFE Interaction Coefficient

Formally apply the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell projection theorem to prove that in a balanced 2x2 panel with cell sizes \(n\), the regression slope on \(D_{it} = \operatorname{Treat}_i \times \operatorname{Post}_t\) equals \(\bar{Y}_{T,1} - \bar{Y}_{T,0} - (\bar{Y}_{C,1} - \bar{Y}_{C,0})\).

Solution

Project \(D_{it}\) onto the space spanned by unit indicators and time indicators. The residual \(\tilde{D}_{it}\) takes values \(+1/4\) for \((T=1, P=1)\), \(-1/4\) for \((T=1, P=0)\), \(-1/4\) for \((T=0, P=1)\), and \(+1/4\) for \((T=0, P=0)\). Regressing \(Y_{it}\) on \(\tilde{D}_{it}\) yields \(\sum Y_{it}\tilde{D}_{it} / \sum \tilde{D}_{it}^2 = [n/4 (\bar{Y}_{T,1} - \bar{Y}_{T,0} - \bar{Y}_{C,1} + \bar{Y}_{C,0})] / [4n (1/16)] = \bar{Y}_{T,1} - \bar{Y}_{T,0} - (\bar{Y}_{C,1} - \bar{Y}_{C,0})\).

Problem 4.6 · Failure of Invariance Under Nonlinear Transformations

Show by counterexample that if parallel trends holds in levels (\(\E[Y_1(0) - Y_0(0) \mid T] = \E[Y_1(0) - Y_0(0) \mid C]\)), it fails in logarithms (\(\ln Y\)) unless baseline levels are identical.

Solution

Let Control baseline be 10 rising to 20 (\(\Delta = 10\)). Let Treated baseline be 100 rising to 110 (\(\Delta = 10\)). Parallel trends in levels holds perfectly (\(20 - 10 = 110 - 100 = 10\)). In logs, Control change is \(\ln(20) - \ln(10) = \ln(2) \approx 0.693\). Treated change is \(\ln(110) - \ln(100) = \ln(1.10) \approx 0.095\). Since \(0.693 \ne 0.095\), parallel trends in logs fails.

Empirical

Problem 4.7 · Bank Capital Surcharges and Lending Contraction

Global Systemically Important Banks (G-SIBs) faced Basel III capital surcharges starting in 2016. Smaller regional banks did not. Describe a 2x2 DiD design to measure the impact on corporate lending spreads, stating the identification assumptions.

Solution

Treated: G-SIBs; Control: Large non-G-SIB regional banks. Pre-period: 2013-2015; Post-period: 2016-2018. Outcome: Loan spread over SOFR/LIBOR. Identification assumes that absent surcharges, G-SIB loan spreads would have evolved parallel to regional banks, with no anticipation in 2015 and no confounding regulatory interventions.

Problem 4.8 · Event-Study Pre-Trend Coefficients

In a dynamic DiD regression with event-time leads and lags \(\sum_{k=-4}^4 \beta_k D_{it}^k\), explain how the leads (\(k < 0\)) serve as a diagnostic check for parallel trends.

Solution

Coefficients \(\beta_k\) for \(k < 0\) measure outcome differences between treated and control units \(k\) periods before treatment relative to reference period \(k=-1\). If parallel trends holds, \(\beta_k \approx 0\) for all \(k < 0\). Non-zero leads indicate pre-existing divergence or anticipation effects, invalidating post-event causal claims.

Problem 4.9 · Triple Differences (DDD) for Macro Shocks

Explain how a Triple Differences (Difference-in-Difference-in-Differences) design adds an unaffected industry sector to control for local state-level macro shocks.

Solution

If state-level economic booms confound treated banks in treated states, DDD introduces an untreated sector (e.g. agricultural lending vs commercial lending). By subtracting the DiD of the unaffected sector from the DiD of the affected sector, state-level macro shocks common to all local sectors are purged, relaxing the parallel trends assumption.

Research

Problem 4.10 · Bertrand-Duflo-Mullainathan Serial Correlation Simulation

Describe Bertrand, Duflo, and Mullainathan's (2004) empirical simulation showing that OLS standard errors in DiD reject a true null hypothesis at rates up to 45%.

Solution

BDM placed placebo random laws on state-level employment data where no true effect existed. Because state laws and employment outcomes are both highly positively autocorrelated, standard OLS standard errors that assume independent errors underestimate standard errors by a factor of 2 to 3, causing t-statistics to exceed 1.96 in nearly 45% of simulations.

Problem 4.11 · Synthetic Difference-in-Differences (Arkhangelsky et al.)

Explain how Synthetic DiD combines synthetic control weighting with two-way fixed effects to reweight control units and achieve parallel trends.

Solution

Standard DiD weights all control units equally. Synthetic DiD finds non-negative unit weights \(w_i\) that match pre-treatment trends between treated units and the weighted control group, and time weights \(\lambda_t\) that balance pre-treatment periods. It then estimates a weighted TWFE regression, eliminating bias when unweighted parallel trends fails.

Problem 4.12 · Honest Parallel Trends Bounds (Rambachan & Roth)

Explain how Rambachan and Roth (2023) construct sensitivity bounds on post-treatment treatment effects allowing for smooth post-treatment deviations from parallel trends bounded by pre-treatment fluctuations.

Solution

Rambachan and Roth formalize that post-treatment trend divergence \(\delta\) is unlikely to exceed the maximum curvature observed in pre-treatment leads. By restricting the second differences of post-treatment trends to be bounded by \(M\) times the maximum pre-treatment violation, they construct robust confidence sets showing how large a violation is required to overturn statistical significance.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the essential identifying assumption of the difference-in-differences estimator?

    DiD does not require identical baseline levels; it requires that the counterfactual time trends (changes) be identical between groups.

  2. If Control changes from 10 to 14, and Treated changes from 20 to 29, what is the estimated DiD treatment effect?

    Control trend is 14 - 10 = 4. Treated change is 29 - 20 = 9. DiD = 9 - 4 = 5.0.

  3. In a 2x2 panel regression \(Y_{it} = \mu + \gamma \operatorname{Treat}_i + \lambda \operatorname{Post}_t + \beta (\operatorname{Treat}_i \times \operatorname{Post}_t) + \varepsilon_{it}\), which coefficient represents the DiD estimand?

    The coefficient \(\beta\) on the interaction term \(\operatorname{Treat} \times \operatorname{Post}\) captures the differential post-treatment change.

  4. Why is clustering standard errors at the unit level essential in difference-in-differences regressions?

    Serial correlation within units inflates naive test statistics; clustering accounts for arbitrary intra-unit correlation over time.

  5. What does the no-anticipation condition require?

    No-anticipation requires that \(Y_{i0}(1) = Y_{i0}(0)\); pre-treatment outcomes must not reflect future treatment status.

  6. If Control changes by -2.0, and DiD estimate is +14.0, what was the observed change for the Treated group?

    DiD = Treated Change - Control Change, so 14.0 = Treated Change - (-2.0), meaning Treated Change = 14.0 - 2.0 = 12.0.

  7. Does parallel trends in levels guarantee parallel trends in log outcomes?

    Parallel trends is functional-form dependent; equal level additions imply different growth rates when initial levels differ.

  8. In an event-study plot with event-time leads and lags, what pattern supports the parallel trends assumption?

    Zero pre-treatment leads confirm that treated and control units did not diverge prior to the intervention.
