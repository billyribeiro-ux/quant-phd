Staggered Adoption & Two-Way Fixed Effects Bias

Differential Rollout Timing, the Goodman-Bacon Decomposition, and Bad Control Dynamics

Why It Matters

For decades, empirical finance relied on linear regressions with firm and time fixed effects to evaluate staggered corporate and regulatory reforms. A major econometrics revolution spearheaded by Goodman-Bacon proved that whenever treatment timing varies across units and causal effects evolve dynamically over time, canonical two-way fixed effects regressions use previously treated units as controls for later adopters. This practice subtracts treatment effect growth from newly treated gains, generating negative implicit weights that can make an unambiguously positive policy appear negative and statistically significant.

1. The Staggered Adoption Setting

In real-world policy rollouts, units adopt treatment at different calendar dates. Let \(G_i\) denote the adoption cohort of unit \(i\), where \(G_i = k\) indicates that unit \(i\) received treatment starting in period \(t = k\). Units with \(G_i = \infty\) are never treated (\(U\)). The treatment indicator is \(D_{it} = \indic\{t \ge G_i\}\).

Definition 1.1 · Staggered Adoption and TWFE Bias

Staggered adoption and two-way fixed-effects bias refers to the systematic failure of the standard static Two-Way Fixed Effects (TWFE) regression model:

\[ Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta^{\text{TWFE}} D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

to identify a convex, weighted average of causal treatment effects when treatment adoption is staggered across cohorts and treatment effects vary over time. The regression coefficient \(\beta^{\text{TWFE}}\) implicitly weights 2x2 subgroup comparisons using already-treated units as controls, contaminating the estimand with past dynamic treatment changes.

Theorem 1.1 · The Goodman-Bacon Decomposition Theorem

Assumptions:

  1. Balanced panel of units observed over \(T\) periods with staggered adoption cohorts.
  2. Static TWFE regression: \(Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta^{\text{TWFE}} D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}\).
  3. Standard linear OLS identification.

Conclusion: The TWFE coefficient decomposes identically into a weighted average of all potential 2x2 difference-in-differences estimators across cohort pairs:

\[ \beta^{\text{TWFE}} = \sum_{k \ne U} s_{kU} \hat{\beta}_{kU}^{2 \times 2} + \sum_{k < l} s_{kl} \left[ \mu_{kl} \hat{\beta}_{kl}^{\text{pre}} + (1 - \mu_{kl}) \hat{\beta}_{kl}^{\text{post}} \right], \]

where weights sum to one (\(\sum s = 1\)), and each weight is proportional to the subsample size and the variance of treatment in that sub-comparison:

\[ s_{kl} \propto (n_k + n_l)^2 \Var(D_{it} \mid i \in \{k, l\}). \]
Proof

By the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, \(\beta^{\text{TWFE}} = \sum_{i,t} \tilde{D}_{it} Y_{it} / \sum_{i,t} \tilde{D}_{it}^2\), where \(\tilde{D}_{it}\) is the double-demeaned treatment dummy. Partitioning the sample sum across cohorts \(k\) and \(l\), the linear projection of \(D_{it}\) on unit and time fixed effects within each pair equals the pairwise difference-in-means indicator. Summing across all pairs yields the exact weighted decomposition stated above.

2. The "Already-Treated as Controls" Pathology

The critical flaw in TWFE stems from the fourth term in the decomposition: \(\hat{\beta}_{kl}^{\text{post}}\), where late adopters \(l\) are compared against early adopters \(k\) who are already treated.

Remark 1.1 · Why Already-Treated Units Contaminate Inference

In a comparison between late treated \(L\) (treated at \(t=3\)) and early treated \(E\) (treated at \(t=2\)) across periods \(t=2\) and \(t=3\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\beta}_{LE}^{\text{post}} &= (Y_{L,3} - Y_{L,2}) - (Y_{E,3} - Y_{E,2}) \\ &= [\Delta Y_L(0) + \tau_{L,3}] - [\Delta Y_E(0) + (\tau_{E,3} - \tau_{E,2})]. \end{aligned} \]

Under parallel trends, \(\Delta Y_L(0) = \Delta Y_E(0)\), so:

\[ \hat{\beta}_{LE}^{\text{post}} = \tau_{L,3} - (\tau_{E,3} - \tau_{E,2}). \]

If the treatment effect grows over time (\(\tau_{E,3} > \tau_{E,2}\)), the growth in early treatment effect \((\tau_{E,3} - \tau_{E,2})\) is subtracted from the newly treated effect \(\tau_{L,3}\). If treatment effects mature gradually, this subtraction turns \(\hat{\beta}_{LE}^{\text{post}}\) negative, contaminating the entire TWFE regression.

3. Empirical Demonstration: Goodman-Bacon 3-Cohort Panel

We implement a constructed 3-cohort balanced panel in exact rational arithmetic (fractions.Fraction) with positive but dynamic treatment effects, proving that the Goodman-Bacon weights sum to 1.0 and confirming how already-treated units introduce negative bias.

Lab 1 · Goodman-Bacon Decomposition and Dynamic Bias

In a 3-cohort staggered panel with dynamic treatment effects (+2 in initial period, +8 in mature period), TWFE decomposes into four 2x2 comparisons whose weights sum to 1, with the late-versus-already-treated comparison generating an estimate of -4.00.

"""Lab S6-L05-1: Goodman-Bacon decomposition and negative weights under staggered adoption.

Constructed example: demonstrates the Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposition of two-way fixed effects (TWFE)
in a 3-cohort staggered adoption panel with dynamic treatment effects, showing how already-treated
units act as bad controls and receive negative contributions.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    # 3 cohorts, 1 unit each, observed over 3 time periods t=1, 2, 3 (balanced panel)
    # Baseline counterfactual trend is 0 for all units
    # Cohort U (Never-treated): D = [0, 0, 0], Y = [10, 10, 10]
    # Cohort E (Early treated at t=2): D = [0, 1, 1]
    #   Treatment effect is dynamic: +2 at t=2, +8 at t=3
    #   Y = [10, 12, 18]
    # Cohort L (Late treated at t=3): D = [0, 0, 1]
    #   Treatment effect: +2 at t=3
    #   Y = [10, 10, 12]

    panel = [
        # (unit, time, y, d)
        (0, 1, Fraction(10), Fraction(0)),
        (0, 2, Fraction(10), Fraction(0)),
        (0, 3, Fraction(10), Fraction(0)),
        (1, 1, Fraction(10), Fraction(0)),
        (1, 2, Fraction(12), Fraction(1)),
        (1, 3, Fraction(18), Fraction(1)),
        (2, 1, Fraction(10), Fraction(0)),
        (2, 2, Fraction(10), Fraction(0)),
        (2, 3, Fraction(12), Fraction(1)),
    ]

    # 1. Full TWFE regression: Y_it = alpha_i + lambda_t + beta_TWFE * D_it + e_it via FWL
    n_units = Fraction(3)
    n_times = Fraction(3)
    n_obs = Fraction(len(panel))

    y_mean = sum((r[2] for r in panel), Fraction(0)) / n_obs
    d_mean = sum((r[3] for r in panel), Fraction(0)) / n_obs

    y_unit = [sum((r[2] for r in panel if r[0] == i), Fraction(0)) / n_times for i in range(3)]
    d_unit = [sum((r[3] for r in panel if r[0] == i), Fraction(0)) / n_times for i in range(3)]

    y_time = [sum((r[2] for r in panel if r[1] == t), Fraction(0)) / n_units for t in range(1, 4)]
    d_time = [sum((r[3] for r in panel if r[1] == t), Fraction(0)) / n_units for t in range(1, 4)]

    d_tilde: list[Fraction] = []
    y_tilde: list[Fraction] = []
    for u, t, y, d in panel:
        t_idx = t - 1
        d_res = d - d_unit[u] - d_time[t_idx] + d_mean
        y_res = y - y_unit[u] - y_time[t_idx] + y_mean
        d_tilde.append(d_res)
        y_tilde.append(y_res)

    var_d_tilde = sum(dt**2 for dt in d_tilde)
    cov_dy_tilde = sum(dt * yt for dt, yt in zip(d_tilde, y_tilde, strict=True))
    beta_twfe = cov_dy_tilde / var_d_tilde

    # 2. Goodman-Bacon (2021) 2x2 Sub-Estimates:
    # 2a. Early vs Never (over t=1,2,3; post is t=2,3)
    # Early change: (12 + 18)/2 - 10 = 15 - 10 = 5; Never change = 0
    beta_eu = Fraction(5, 1)

    # 2b. Late vs Never (over t=1,2,3; post is t=3)
    # Late change: 12 - (10 + 10)/2 = 12 - 10 = 2; Never change = 0
    beta_lu = Fraction(2, 1)

    # 2c. Early vs Late as clean control (over t=1,2; post is t=2)
    # Early change: 12 - 10 = 2; Late change: 10 - 10 = 0
    beta_el = Fraction(2, 1)

    # 2d. Late vs Already-Treated Early as control (over t=2,3; post is t=3)
    # Late change: 12 - 10 = 2; Early change: 18 - 12 = 6
    # beta_LE = 2 - 6 = -4 (severely negative due to growth in dynamic effect!)
    beta_le = Fraction(-4, 1)

    # 3. Goodman-Bacon exact analytical weights from Eq. (5) & (6):
    # s_EU = 1/3, s_LU = 1/3, s_EL = 1/6, s_LE = 1/6
    w_eu = Fraction(1, 3)
    w_lu = Fraction(1, 3)
    w_el = Fraction(1, 6)
    w_le = Fraction(1, 6)

    bacon_sum = w_eu * beta_eu + w_lu * beta_lu + w_el * beta_el + w_le * beta_le

    print("Goodman-Bacon TWFE Staggered Adoption Decomposition (Constructed Panel):")
    print(f"  Overall TWFE Coefficient (beta_TWFE): {beta_twfe} ({float(beta_twfe):.4f})")
    print(f"  Bacon Decomposed Weighted Sum:       {bacon_sum} ({float(bacon_sum):.4f})")
    print(f"  Exact Equivalence Check:             {beta_twfe == bacon_sum}\n")

    print("2x2 Component Decomposition:")
    print(
        f"  1. Early vs Never (clean):           estimate = {float(beta_eu):+.2f}, weight = {float(w_eu):.4f} ({w_eu})"
    )
    print(
        f"  2. Late vs Never (clean):            estimate = {float(beta_lu):+.2f}, weight = {float(w_lu):.4f} ({w_lu})"
    )
    print(
        f"  3. Early vs Late (timing pre):       estimate = {float(beta_el):+.2f}, weight = {float(w_el):.4f} ({w_el})"
    )
    le_str = f"estimate = {float(beta_le):+.2f}, weight = {float(w_le):.4f} ({w_le})"
    print(f"  4. Late vs Already-Treated (dirty):  {le_str}  <-- Dynamic bias!")
    print(f"  Sum of Weights: {w_eu + w_lu + w_el + w_le}")


main()
Goodman-Bacon TWFE Staggered Adoption Decomposition (Constructed Panel):
  Overall TWFE Coefficient (beta_TWFE): 2 (2.0000)
  Bacon Decomposed Weighted Sum:       2 (2.0000)
  Exact Equivalence Check:             True

2x2 Component Decomposition:
  1. Early vs Never (clean):           estimate = +5.00, weight = 0.3333 (1/3)
  2. Late vs Never (clean):            estimate = +2.00, weight = 0.3333 (1/3)
  3. Early vs Late (timing pre):       estimate = +2.00, weight = 0.1667 (1/6)
  4. Late vs Already-Treated (dirty):  estimate = -4.00, weight = 0.1667 (1/6)  <-- Dynamic bias!
  Sum of Weights: 1

The exact calculation confirms Theorem 1.1: the overall TWFE coefficient is 2.0000, matching the Bacon decomposed sum identically. Although true treatment effects are everywhere positive (+2 and +8), the fourth comparison evaluates to -4.00 with weight 1/6, dragging down the estimate.

Where the Canon Breaks

Canonical static TWFE fails whenever treatment effects are heterogeneous across time or across cohorts:

  • Sign Reversal: If treatment effects grow sufficiently fast among early adopters, the negative contribution of \(\tau_{L} - \Delta \tau_E\) can outweigh the positive clean comparisons, causing the overall regression coefficient \(\beta^{\text{TWFE}}\) to be strictly negative even when every single treated firm benefited from the policy.
  • Weights Do Not Reflect Population Shares: Goodman-Bacon weights are governed by treatment variance, which peaks for units treated near the middle of the sample period. Units treated very early or very late receive minimal weight, distorting representative policy evaluation.

Misconceptions

  • Two-way fixed effects regressions naturally estimate a variance-weighted average of true treatment effects with strictly positive weights.

    As Goodman-Bacon proved, when treatment timing varies, already-treated units act as controls. In the presence of treatment effect dynamics, the effective weight on some underlying causal parameters is strictly negative.

  • Staggered adoption bias only occurs when parallel trends fails.

    Staggered adoption TWFE bias occurs even when parallel trends holds perfectly for all units. The bias is caused by treatment effect heterogeneity over time, which turns past treated units into bad counterfactual controls.

  • Adding unit-specific linear time trends to the TWFE specification fixes staggered adoption bias.

    Unit-specific linear trends absorb dynamic treatment effects and extrapolate post-treatment growth into the trend, worsening bias and often exaggerating spurious sign reversals.

Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 5.1 · Calculating Goodman-Bacon Weights for Timing Groups

In a 2-cohort timing panel without an untreated group, Cohort A is treated at \(t=0.25\) of the timeline, and Cohort B is treated at \(t=0.75\). Compute the treatment variance \(\Var(D_A) = 0.25 \times 0.75\) and \(\Var(D_B) = 0.75 \times 0.25\), and the relative timing weights.

Solution

\(\Var(D_A) = 0.25 \times 0.75 = 0.1875\). \(\Var(D_B) = 0.75 \times 0.25 = 0.1875\). The timing difference is \(D_A - D_B\), which has variance \(\Delta = 0.75 - 0.25 = 0.50\), with variance \(0.50 \times (1 - 0.50) = 0.2500\). The weights between the two timing comparisons are split equally at 0.50 each.

Problem 5.2 · Detecting Negative Comparison Sign

Suppose Early adopter change is \(Y_{E,3} - Y_{E,2} = +10.0\). Late adopter change is \(Y_{L,3} - Y_{L,2} = +4.0\). Calculate the 2x2 DiD estimate when Late is compared against already-treated Early.

Solution

The 2x2 estimate is \(\hat{\beta}_{LE}^{\text{post}} = (Y_{L,3} - Y_{L,2}) - (Y_{E,3} - Y_{E,2}) = +4.0 - (+10.0) = -6.0\). Although both cohorts grew, Early's growth of +10 was subtracted from Late's growth of +4, generating a negative estimate of -6.0.

Problem 5.3 · Bacon Decomposed Weighted Average Calculation

A decomposition yields 3 comparisons: Clean comparison 1: \(\hat{\beta}_1 = +6.0\), weight \(w_1 = 0.50\); Clean comparison 2: \(\hat{\beta}_2 = +4.0\), weight \(w_2 = 0.30\); Dirty timing comparison: \(\hat{\beta}_3 = -5.0\), weight \(w_3 = 0.20\). Compute the overall TWFE estimate.

Solution

\(\beta^{\text{TWFE}} = 0.50 \times 6.0 + 0.30 \times 4.0 + 0.20 \times (-5.0) = 3.0 + 1.2 - 1.0 = +3.20\). The dirty comparison shrinks the true effect from ~5.0 down to 3.2.

Proof

Problem 5.4 · Algebraic Derivation of the Bad Control Bias Expression

Prove that \(\hat{\beta}_{LE}^{\text{post}} = \tau_{L, post} - \Delta \tau_{E, post}\) under parallel counterfactual trends, where \(\Delta \tau_{E, post} = \tau_{E, post} - \tau_{E, pre}\).

Solution

Write observed changes: \(Y_{L, post} - Y_{L, pre} = [Y_{L, post}(0) - Y_{L, pre}(0)] + \tau_{L, post}\). For early treated units, treatment was already active in both periods, so \(Y_{E, post} - Y_{E, pre} = [Y_{E, post}(0) - Y_{E, pre}(0)] + [\tau_{E, post} - \tau_{E, pre}]\). Subtracting: \(\hat{\beta}_{LE}^{\text{post}} = \{[Y_{L, post}(0) - Y_{L, pre}(0)] - [Y_{E, post}(0) - Y_{E, pre}(0)]\} + \tau_{L, post} - (\tau_{E, post} - \tau_{E, pre})\). Under parallel trends, the first bracket vanishes, leaving \(\tau_{L, post} - \Delta \tau_E\).

Problem 5.5 · Proof of Sign Reversal Condition

State and prove the mathematical condition under which \(\beta^{\text{TWFE}} < 0\) even when all true cohort treatment effects \(\tau_{it} > 0\) for all \(i, t\).

Solution

Let \(\beta^{\text{TWFE}} = w_{\text{clean}}\beta_{\text{clean}} + w_{\text{dirty}}\beta_{\text{dirty}}\), where \(\beta_{\text{dirty}} = \tau_L - \Delta \tau_E\). If \(\Delta \tau_E > \tau_L + \frac{w_{\text{clean}}}{w_{\text{dirty}}} \beta_{\text{clean}}\), then \(w_{\text{dirty}}\beta_{\text{dirty}} < -w_{\text{clean}}\beta_{\text{clean}}\), forcing \(\beta^{\text{TWFE}} < 0\). This proves that sufficiently rapid treatment effect growth among early adopters forces a sign reversal.

Problem 5.6 · Invariance of TWFE to Level Shifts but Not Growth Shifts

Prove that adding a constant level shift \(c\) to unit \(i\)'s potential outcomes has zero impact on \(\beta^{\text{TWFE}}\), whereas adding a time-dependent shift \(c \cdot t\) alters \(\beta^{\text{TWFE}}\).

Solution

A static level shift \(Y_{it} \to Y_{it} + c_i\) is completely absorbed by unit fixed effects \(\alpha_i\) via the FWL projection matrix \(\boldsymbol{M}_{\alpha}\), leaving residuals \(\tilde{Y}_{it}\) unchanged. A time-dependent growth shift \(Y_{it} \to Y_{it} + c_i t\) cannot be spanned by \(\alpha_i + \lambda_t\), leaving non-zero residuals that alter the numerator covariance \(\sum \tilde{D}_{it}\tilde{Y}_{it}\).

Empirical

Problem 5.7 · Staggered Adoption of Interstate Banking Deregulation

The Riegle-Neal Interstate Banking Act was preceded by staggered state-level banking deregulation from 1978 to 1994. Explain why bank liquidity studies using static TWFE suffered from Bacon decomposition bias.

Solution

States deregulated at different dates over 16 years. Banks took years to establish interstate branch networks, meaning efficiency and liquidity gains matured dynamically over decades. Early deregulating states acted as controls for late adopters, subtracting early bank branch expansion from late adopter gains and understating the true benefits of deregulation.

Problem 5.8 · Visualizing the Bacon Decomposition Plot

Describe how an empirical researcher constructs and interprets a Goodman-Bacon decomposition scatter plot.

Solution

The plot displays each 2x2 comparison estimate on the vertical axis against its Bacon weight on the horizontal axis, classified into comparison types (Treated vs Untreated, Earlier vs Later, Later vs Earlier). A horizontal dashed line marks the overall TWFE estimate. Clustering of Later vs Earlier comparisons with negative estimates visually diagnoses bad-control bias.

Problem 5.9 · Comparing Callaway-Sant'Anna with Static TWFE

Explain how Callaway and Sant'Anna's (2021) group-time average treatment effect estimator \(ATT(g, t)\) prevents staggered adoption bias.

Solution

Callaway and Sant'Anna estimate treatment effects cohort by cohort: \(ATT(g, t)\) compares cohort \(g\) at time \(t\) exclusively against units that are never treated or not yet treated. By explicitly forbidding already-treated units from serving as controls, it ensures that all weights are strictly non-negative, eliminating negative weighting bias.

Research

Problem 5.10 · Heterogeneity-Robust Estimators (Sun & Abraham)

Explain how Sun and Abraham's (2021) interaction-weighted estimator resolves dynamic treatment effect contamination in event-study leads and lags.

Solution

In standard event studies with staggered adoption, lead and lag coefficients on event-time dummies are contaminated by effects from other relative periods. Sun and Abraham estimate cohort-specific event-study trajectories and aggregate them using cohort sample shares, ensuring clean non-parametric weights that isolate specific dynamic horizons.

Problem 5.11 · de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfoeuille Diagnostic Weights

Explain de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille's (2020) test for negative weights in linear regressions with multiple treatment intensities.

Solution

de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille compute the exact analytical weights \(\sigma_{g,t}\) attached to each unit-time treatment effect in arbitrary fixed effects regressions. If any weights are negative, they compute the ratio of the sum of negative weights to positive weights and calculate the minimum treatment effect heterogeneity required to reverse the regression sign.

Problem 5.12 · Re-Evaluating Seminal Corporate Finance Papers

Discuss the findings of recent replication studies re-estimating seminal corporate governance staggered DiD papers using heterogeneity-robust methods.

Solution

Re-estimations of staggered antitakeover law adoptions (e.g. Business Combination laws) and universal demand laws found that widely cited negative impacts on firm value (Tobin's Q) or innovation collapsed to statistical insignificance once already-treated states were removed as controls, demonstrating that the original canon was an artifact of negative TWFE weighting.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What does the Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposition establish about two-way fixed effects regressions with staggered adoption?

    Goodman-Bacon proved that TWFE averages clean comparisons with timing comparisons where already-treated units serve as controls.

  2. Why is comparing newly treated units to already-treated units problematic in the presence of dynamic treatment effects?

    The already-treated unit's outcome changes by \(\Delta Y(0) + \Delta \tau_E\); subtracting this change subtracts \(\Delta \tau_E\) from the treatment effect.

  3. If newly treated units grow by +4.0, while already-treated control units grow by +7.0 over the same post-period, what is the resulting 2x2 comparison estimate?

    The 2x2 comparison is 4.0 - 7.0 = -3.0.

  4. Can a static TWFE regression estimate a negative treatment effect when the true causal effect is positive for all units at all times?

    Negative implicit weights allow the regression coefficient to lie completely outside the convex hull of true treatment effects.

  5. Which cohort receives the highest Goodman-Bacon weight in a staggered adoption panel?

    The variance of a binary indicator \(D \in \{0, 1\}\) is \(p(1-p)\), which reaches its maximum of 0.25 at \(p = 0.50\).

  6. What must the sum of all Goodman-Bacon decomposition weights equal across all 2x2 comparisons?

    By Theorem 1.1, the Goodman-Bacon decomposition weights sum to exactly 1.0.

  7. How do modern heterogeneity-robust estimators (such as Callaway-Sant'Anna) solve the Goodman-Bacon problem?

    By restricting the control pool to clean (never-treated or not-yet-treated) units, all weights remain non-negative.

  8. Does the presence of never-treated units completely eliminate staggered adoption bias in TWFE?

    TWFE weights all possible comparisons; even with a never-treated group, the timing comparisons still enter with positive weight and bad-control bias.
