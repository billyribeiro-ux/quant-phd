Regression Discontinuity Designs

Cutoff Thresholds, Conditional Continuity, and Local Linear Estimation

Why It Matters

In quantitative finance and corporate governance, institutional rules frequently assign treatments using strict numeric cutoffs, such as index inclusion thresholds (Russell 1000 versus Russell 2000), credit score boundaries for loan origination, or majority voting margins on shareholder proxy ballots. A regression discontinuity design exploits these arbitrary institutional thresholds as quasi-experiments. Firms just above the cutoff receive treatment while firms just below do not, yet both groups possess virtually identical underlying economic fundamentals. By comparing local limits around the threshold, researchers estimate causal treatment effects free from unobserved confounders.

1. The Sharp Regression Discontinuity Design

In a regression discontinuity design, treatment assignment is governed by an observed continuous score \(X_i\), termed the running variable or forcing variable.

Definition 1.1 · Regression Discontinuity Design

A regression discontinuity design (RDD) is a quasi-experimental identification strategy in which treatment status \(D_i \in \{0, 1\}\) changes discontinuously as an observed continuous running variable \(X_i\) crosses an exogenous threshold \(c\).

In a sharp RDD, treatment assignment is a deterministic step function at cutoff \(c\):

\[ D_i = \indic\{X_i \ge c\}. \]

Units with \(X_i \ge c\) are assigned to the treated condition (\(D_i = 1\)), while units with \(X_i < c\) remain in the control condition (\(D_i = 0\)).

Angrist and Pischke (2009) formalize the core identification logic: even when the running variable \(X_i\) is strongly correlated with potential outcomes, treatment status is conditionally independent of potential outcomes in the immediate neighborhood of the cutoff.

Theorem 1.1 · Sharp RDD Identification via Conditional Continuity

Assumptions:

  1. Assignment Rule (A1): \(D_i = \indic\{X_i \ge c\}\) with known cutoff \(c \in \R\).
  2. Potential Outcome Continuity (A2): The conditional expectation functions of potential outcomes, \(\mu_0(x) \equiv \E[Y_i(0) \mid X_i = x]\) and \(\mu_1(x) \equiv \E[Y_i(1) \mid X_i = x]\), are continuous functions of \(x\) at the threshold \(x = c\).
  3. Positive Density (A3): The marginal probability density function \(f_X(x)\) is continuous and strictly positive in an open neighborhood around \(c\).

Conclusion: The average causal treatment effect at the threshold \(c\) is identified as the jump in the observed regression conditional expectation function:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_{\text{SRD}}(c) &\equiv \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid X_i = c] \\ &= \lim_{x \downarrow c} \E[Y_i \mid X_i = x] \\ &\quad - \lim_{x \uparrow c} \E[Y_i \mid X_i = x]. \end{aligned} \]
Proof

By the switching equation, observed outcome \(Y_i = D_i Y_i(1) + (1 - D_i) Y_i(0)\). For any \(x \ge c\), \(D_i = 1\), so \(\E[Y_i \mid X_i = x] = \E[Y_i(1) \mid X_i = x] = \mu_1(x)\). Taking the right-hand limit as \(x \downarrow c\):

\[ \lim_{x \downarrow c} \E[Y_i \mid X_i = x] = \lim_{x \downarrow c} \mu_1(x) = \mu_1(c), \]

where the final equality follows from assumption A2 (continuity of \(\mu_1\) at \(c\)).

Similarly, for any \(x < c\), \(D_i = 0\), so \(\E[Y_i \mid X_i = x] = \E[Y_i(0) \mid X_i = x] = \mu_0(x)\). Taking the left-hand limit as \(x \uparrow c\):

\[ \lim_{x \uparrow c} \E[Y_i \mid X_i = x] = \lim_{x \uparrow c} \mu_0(x) = \mu_0(c), \]

which follows from continuity of \(\mu_0\) at \(c\). Subtracting the two one-sided limits yields:

\[ \lim_{x \downarrow c} \E[Y \mid X=x] - \lim_{x \uparrow c} \E[Y \mid X=x] = \mu_1(c) - \mu_0(c) = \tau_{\text{SRD}}(c). \]

The discontinuity in observed outcomes at \(c\) equals the treatment effect at \(c\) exactly.

2. Local Linear Regression & Bandwidth Trade-Offs

In empirical practice, we do not observe infinite data at the point \(X_i = c\). We must approximate the limits using observations within a symmetric window \(X_i \in [c - h, c + h]\), where \(h > 0\) is the bandwidth.

Definition 1.2 · Local Linear Estimator

The local linear RDD estimator fits separate linear regressions on either side of the threshold within bandwidth \(h\). Defining centered running variable \(\tilde{X}_i \equiv X_i - c\), we solve the ordinary least squares problem on the subsample with \(|\tilde{X}_i| \le h\):

\[ Y_i = \alpha + \tau D_i + \beta_1 \tilde{X}_i + \beta_2 (D_i \cdot \tilde{X}_i) + \varepsilon_i. \]

The coefficient \(\hat{\tau}\) on the treatment indicator \(D_i\) estimates the vertical jump at the threshold \(\tilde{X}_i = 0\).

Remark 1.1 · The Bandwidth Bias-Variance Trade-Off

The choice of bandwidth \(h\) governs an unavoidable bias-variance trade-off:

  • Variance: The effective sample size inside the window is \(N_h \approx 2 h f_X(c) N\). The sampling variance of \(\hat{\tau}\) scales as \(O(1 / (N h))\). Shrinking \(h\) reduces sample size and inflates variance.
  • Bias: If the true conditional expectation \(\mu_0(x)\) has non-zero curvature (second derivative \(\mu_0''(c) \ne 0\)), approximating it with a linear slope induces an asymptotic bias of order \(O(h^2)\). Expanding \(h\) increases curvature bias.

3. Empirical Demonstration: Local Linear Bandwidth Sensitivity

We evaluate local linear RDD estimation on a simulated dataset of \(N = 6{,}000\) observations. The true data generating process features a known causal jump of \(\tau = 3.50\) at cutoff \(c = 0.0\), with underlying nonlinear curvature \(\mu_0(x) = 2.0 + 1.2 x + 0.8 x^2\). We estimate \(\hat{\tau}\) across bandwidths \(h \in \{0.40, 0.75, 1.25, 2.00\}\).

Lab 1 · Sharp RDD Bandwidth Trade-Off Analysis (SIMULATED)

Across four bandwidths h in {0.40, 0.75, 1.25, 2.00} on N = 6,000 observations with true jump tau = 3.50, local linear regression recovers tau within 0.03 for all bandwidths while window sample size increases from 962 to 4,819.

"""Lab S6-L06-1: Sharp regression discontinuity and local linear bandwidth trade-offs.

SIMULATED: evaluates local linear RDD estimation of a known treatment jump at cutoff c=0,
demonstrating the bias-variance trade-off across varying bandwidths h.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random


def fit_local_linear(x: list[float], y: list[float], d: list[int]) -> float:
    """Fit local linear regression: Y = alpha + tau * D + beta_1 * X + beta_2 * (D * X).

    Returns estimated jump tau at the cutoff X=0.
    """
    n = len(x)
    # Design matrix columns: [1, D, X, D*X]
    # Solve 4x4 normal equations using Gauss-Jordan
    cols = [[1.0] * n, [float(di) for di in d], list(x), [float(di) * xi for di, xi in zip(d, x, strict=True)]]
    p = 4
    xtx = [[sum(cols[j][i] * cols[k][i] for i in range(n)) for k in range(p)] for j in range(p)]
    xty = [sum(cols[j][i] * y[i] for i in range(n)) for j in range(p)]

    aug = [xtx[i] + [xty[i]] for i in range(p)]
    for col in range(p):
        pivot = col
        while pivot < p and abs(aug[pivot][col]) < 1e-12:
            pivot += 1
        aug[col], aug[pivot] = aug[pivot], aug[col]

        piv_val = aug[col][col]
        for c in range(col, p + 1):
            aug[col][c] /= piv_val
        for r in range(p):
            if r != col and abs(aug[r][col]) > 1e-12:
                f = aug[r][col]
                for c in range(col, p + 1):
                    aug[r][c] -= f * aug[col][c]

    return aug[1][p]  # tau is coefficient on D (column 1)


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(888)
    n = 6000

    # True parameters
    cutoff = 0.0
    true_tau = 3.50

    # Running variable X ~ Uniform[-2.5, 2.5]
    x = [rng.uniform(-2.5, 2.5) for _ in range(n)]

    # Potential outcomes with nonlinear curvature: Y(0) = 2.0 + 1.2*X + 0.8*X^2 + noise
    # At cutoff X=0, E[Y(0)|X=0] = 2.0, E[Y(1)|X=0] = 2.0 + 3.5 = 5.5
    y: list[float] = []
    d: list[int] = []
    for xi in x:
        di = 1 if xi >= cutoff else 0
        eps = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.6)
        baseline = 2.0 + 1.2 * xi + 0.8 * (xi**2) + eps
        val = baseline + true_tau * di
        y.append(val)
        d.append(di)

    bandwidths = [0.40, 0.75, 1.25, 2.00]

    print("Sharp Regression Discontinuity Local Linear Estimation (SIMULATED):")
    print(f"  Total observations N: {n}")
    print(f"  Cutoff threshold c:   {cutoff:.1f}")
    print(f"  True causal jump tau: {true_tau:.4f}\n")
    print("Bandwidth Sensitivity & Bias Analysis:")
    print("  Bandwidth (h) | Window Obs (N_h) | Estimated Jump (tau_hat) | Bias vs True Jump")
    print("  --------------+------------------+--------------------------+------------------")

    for h in bandwidths:
        # Filter observations in [cutoff - h, cutoff + h]
        idx = [i for i in range(n) if abs(x[i] - cutoff) <= h]
        sub_x = [x[i] for i in idx]
        sub_y = [y[i] for i in idx]
        sub_d = [d[i] for i in idx]

        tau_hat = fit_local_linear(sub_x, sub_y, sub_d)
        bias = tau_hat - true_tau

        print(f"      {h:4.2f}      |       {len(idx):4d}       |          {tau_hat:6.4f}          |     {bias:+6.4f}")


main()

The numerical simulation confirms that local linear regression on either side of the cutoff controls for the linear trend. Where the Canon Breaks

The validity of a regression discontinuity design hinges on assumptions that fail in institutional settings:

  • Manipulation and Precise Sorting: If economic agents have the knowledge and ability to manipulate their running variable to fall on the desirable side of cutoff \(c\) (for example, corporate treasurers managing earnings to beat analyst consensus by one cent, or firms manipulating market cap around index rebalancing dates), the continuity assumption A2 fails. Units just above the threshold are systematically different from units just below. The McCrary density test inspects whether \(f_X(x)\) exhibits a discontinuous spike at \(c\).
  • High-Order Polynomial Pitfalls: Early RDD papers fit 3rd-, 4th-, or 5th-order global polynomials over the entire running variable range. Gelman and Imbens showed that global polynomials place excessive weight on observations far from the cutoff and produce noisy, biased boundary predictions. Modern practice mandates local linear or local quadratic estimation within an explicit data-driven bandwidth.
  • Local Validity: The estimated parameter \(\tau_{\text{SRD}}(c)\) identifies the treatment effect solely for units with running variable \(X_i = c\). It carries no necessary external validity for units far from the threshold.

Misconceptions

  • RDD requires the running variable \(X_i\) to be randomly assigned across units.

    The running variable \(X_i\) can be completely non-random and heavily correlated with outcomes (e.g., firm size or credit rating). Identification only requires that the conditional expectation functions \(\mu_0(x)\) and \(\mu_1(x)\) are continuous across the cutoff \(c\), meaning agents cannot manipulate their score with infinite precision.

  • A wider bandwidth is always preferable because it increases sample size and reduces statistical standard errors.

    Widening bandwidth \(h\) increases sample size but introduces systematic asymptotic bias of order \(O(h^2)\) whenever the true underlying expectation function has curvature. An optimal bandwidth balances variance against squared bias.

  • Fitting a high-order global polynomial (e.g., 4th-order) across all data points eliminates the need for choosing a local bandwidth.

    Global polynomials suffer from Runge's phenomenon, where polynomial oscillations at the boundary of the domain distort predictions precisely at the cutoff. Local linear estimation within a bounded neighborhood is vastly superior and standard practice.

Problem 6.1 · Bandwidth Filtering and Treatment Classification

In a sharp RDD with cutoff \(c = 50\), a researcher specifies bandwidth \(h = 4\). Which observations are retained in the local estimation sample? If the running variable values are \(\{44.2, 46.1, 48.0, 50.0, 53.9, 54.5\}\), list the retained values and their corresponding treatment indicator \(D_i\).

Solution

The local window is \([c - h, c + h] = [50 - 4, 50 + 4] = [46.0, 54.0]\). We test each value:

  • \(44.2 < 46.0\): excluded.
  • \(46.1 \in [46, 54]\): retained, \(D = 0\) (since \(46.1 < 50\)).
  • \(48.0 \in [46, 54]\): retained, \(D = 0\).
  • \(50.0 \in [46, 54]\): retained, \(D = 1\) (since \(50.0 \ge 50\)).
  • \(53.9 \in [46, 54]\): retained, \(D = 1\).
  • \(54.5 > 54.0\): excluded.

The retained sample contains four observations: \(\{46.1, 48.0\}\) with \(D=0\) and \(\{50.0, 53.9\}\) with \(D=1\).

Problem 6.2 · Calculating Treatment Jump from Local Linear Estimates

A local linear regression within bandwidth \(h=2\) yields estimated regression equation: \[ \hat{Y}_i = 10.50 + 3.20 D_i + 0.45 (X_i - c) - 0.15 D_i (X_i - c). \] Compute the predicted limit of \(Y\) approaching \(c\) from below, the predicted limit approaching \(c\) from above, and the estimated treatment jump \(\hat{\tau}\).

Solution

At the cutoff, centered variable \(\tilde{X}_i = X_i - c = 0\).

Approaching from below (\(D_i = 0\), \(\tilde{X}_i \to 0^-\)):

\[ \lim_{x \uparrow c} \hat{Y}(x) = 10.50 + 3.20(0) + 0.45(0) - 0.15(0)(0) = 10.50. \]

Approaching from above (\(D_i = 1\), \(\tilde{X}_i \to 0^+\)):

\[ \lim_{x \downarrow c} \hat{Y}(x) = 10.50 + 3.20(1) + 0.45(0) - 0.15(1)(0) = 13.70. \]

The estimated treatment jump is the difference \(\hat{\tau} = 13.70 - 10.50 = 3.20\), which equals the coefficient on \(D_i\) identically.

Problem 6.3 · Running Variable Centering Mechanics

Explain why centered running variable \(\tilde{X}_i = X_i - c\) is used in the regression rather than uncentered \(X_i\). What does the intercept represent if \(X_i\) is not centered?

Solution

When \(\tilde{X}_i = X_i - c\) is used, evaluating the equation at the threshold corresponds to \(\tilde{X}_i = 0\), making the coefficient on \(D_i\) directly equal to the treatment jump at the cutoff.

If uncentered \(X_i\) were used, the model is \(Y_i = \alpha + \tau D_i + \beta_1 X_i + \beta_2 D_i X_i\). At \(X_i = 0\), the jump would be \(\tau\). However, the actual cutoff is at \(X_i = c\), where the jump would be \(\tau + \beta_2 c\). Centering ensures that the coefficient on \(D_i\) directly delivers the treatment effect at cutoff \(c\).

Problem 6.4 · Nonparametric Non-Identification on Discrete Grids

Prove that if the running variable \(X_i\) has a discrete distribution with mass points separated by distance \(\Delta > 0\), the continuous limits \(\lim_{x \downarrow c}\) and \(\lim_{x \uparrow c}\) are not nonparametrically identified, requiring parametric extrapolation.

Solution

Let \(X_i\) take discrete values in support \(\mathcal{X} = \{\dots, c - \Delta, c, c + \Delta, \dots\}\). Nonparametric identification of the limit \(\lim_{x \downarrow c} \E[Y \mid X=x]\) requires observing data points in an arbitrarily small punctured neighborhood \((c, c + \epsilon)\) for every \(\epsilon > 0\).

For any \(\epsilon \in (0, \Delta)\), the set \((c, c + \epsilon) \cap \mathcal{X} = \emptyset\). No observations exist closer to \(c\) than \(c + \Delta\). Consequently, evaluating \(\lim_{x \downarrow c} \mu_1(x)\) requires extrapolating across the gap of size \(\Delta\) using a parametric functional form. The estimator is therefore dependent on the assumed functional specification and not nonparametrically identified.

Problem 6.5 · Equivalence of LATE and TOT at the Sharp Cutoff

Show that in a sharp RDD under continuity of \(\mu_0(x)\) and \(\mu_1(x)\), the local average treatment effect at the cutoff \(\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X=c]\) equals the treatment on the treated \(\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X=c, D=1]\).

Solution

In a sharp RDD, conditioning on \(X = c\) and approaching from above yields \(D = 1\) deterministically. Specifically, for units at the cutoff \(X = c\), assignment is \(D = \indic\{c \ge c\} = 1\). Therefore, the subpopulation of units at \(X = c\) that are treated is the entire subpopulation at \(X = c\): \(\Prob(D = 1 \mid X = c) = 1\).

Hence, conditioning on \(\{X = c, D = 1\}\) is identical to conditioning on \(\{X = c\}\):

\[ \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = c, D = 1] = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = c]. \]

Both quantities coincide exactly at the boundary.

Problem 6.6 · Derivation of Asymptotic Bias Order in Local Linear RDD

Let \(\mu_0(x)\) be twice continuously differentiable. Prove that the leading bias term of a local linear estimator within bandwidth \(h\) is proportional to \(h^2\).

Solution

Expand \(\mu_0(x)\) in a Taylor series around \(c\) for \(x \in [c - h, c)\):

\[ \mu_0(x) = \mu_0(c) + \mu_0'(c)(x - c) + \frac{1}{2} \mu_0''(c)(x - c)^2 + o((x - c)^2). \]

A local linear model fits the affine approximation \(\alpha_0 + \beta_0 (x - c)\). The residual of this linear fit on the quadratic term is \(\frac{1}{2} \mu_0''(c) (x - c)^2\). Taking expectations over the symmetric interval with kernel weighting of width \(h\), the average quadratic discrepancy scales as:

\[ \int_{c - h}^c (x - c)^2 dx = \left[ \frac{(x - c)^3}{3} \right]_{c - h}^c = \frac{h^3}{3}. \]

Normalizing by interval length \(h\), the bias in the estimated intercept at \(x = c\) scales as \(O(h^2)\), proportional to the second derivative \(\mu_0''(c)\).

Empirical

Problem 6.7 · Detecting Manipulation via McCrary Density Discontinuity

A quantitative researcher studies corporate governance outcomes using shareholder voting data. Treatment is a governance proposal passing, which requires vote share \(V_i \ge 50.0\%\). The McCrary density test on vote shares reveals a significant discontinuity at \(50.0\%\): density just above \(50.0\%\) is three times higher than density just below \(49.9\%\). Interpret this result. Is RDD identification valid?

Solution

A statistically significant discontinuity in the density of the running variable at the cutoff violates assumption A3 and provides strong evidence of non-random manipulation or sorting around the threshold. In corporate shareholder votes, management or activist investors may engage in strategic vote-buying, horse-trading, or recount pressure to ensure favorable proposals narrowly clear the 50.0% hurdle.

Because units just above 50.0% have actively selected into treatment, their potential outcomes \(\E[Y(0) \mid V = 50.0^+]\) are no longer equal to those just below \(\E[Y(0) \mid V = 50.0^-]\). The continuity assumption A2 is violated, rendering the standard RDD causal estimate biased and invalid.

Problem 6.8 · Evaluating Curvature Bias Absorption in Local Linear Models

In Lab 1, the true treatment jump is \(\tau = 3.50\). The estimated jump \(\hat{\tau}\) changes from \(3.5263\) at \(h = 0.40\) to \(3.5108\) at \(h = 2.00\). Explain why the bias remains relatively small even at bandwidth \(h = 2.00\) despite the underlying quadratic curvature \(\mu_0(x) = 2.0 + 1.2x + 0.8x^2\).

Solution

In local linear regression, separate slopes \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_1 + \beta_2\) are estimated on either side of the cutoff. The quadratic curvature \(0.8 x^2\) affects both sides of \(c=0\) identically because \(x^2\) is symmetric around 0.

When fitting local linear regressions on symmetric intervals \([-h, 0)\) and \([0, h]\), the linear approximations on both sides absorb the slope and the symmetric portion of the curvature, causing much of the quadratic bias to cancel when taking the difference between the two intercepts. Consequently, the bias at \(h=2.00\) is only \(+0.0108\), demonstrating the robustness of local linear modeling relative to local constant estimation.

Problem 6.9 · Baseline Placebo Balance at the Russell 1000/2000 Threshold

A hedge fund analyst proposes running an RDD around index inclusion for the Russell 1000/2000 cutoff. The market capitalization ranking on May 31 determines whether a firm enters the Russell 1000 (ranks 1–1000) or Russell 2000 (ranks 1001–3000). Why is rank 1000 a suitable RDD cutoff, and what placebo check on pre-event firm fundamentals should the analyst run?

Solution

Rank 1000 serves as an institutional sharp cutoff where treatment (Russell 2000 inclusion, which attracts massive benchmark-tracking passive inflows relative to small Russell 1000 weights) changes discontinuously based on market cap rank on May 31.

To validate the design, the analyst must conduct placebo checks on predetermined baseline covariates measured prior to the reconstitution date (e.g., prior-year sales, book-to-market ratio, return on equity, and historical beta). Running the RDD specification using these baseline covariates as placebo outcomes should produce estimated jumps that are statistically indistinguishable from zero, confirming that firms just above and below rank 1000 were economically comparable prior to treatment.

Research

Problem 6.10 · Formalizing the Fuzzy RDD Local Instrumental Variable Estimand

Contrast a sharp RDD with a fuzzy RDD. How does fuzzy RDD combine regression discontinuity with instrumental variables? State the fuzzy RDD estimand.

Solution

In a sharp RDD, treatment status changes deterministically from 0 to 1 at cutoff \(c\): \(\Prob(D_i = 1 \mid X_i \ge c) = 1\) and \(\Prob(D_i = 1 \mid X_i < c) = 0\).

In a fuzzy RDD, crossing the threshold changes the probability or intensity of treatment discontinuously, but imperfect compliance exists: \(\lim_{x \downarrow c} \Prob(D_i = 1 \mid X_i = x) \ne \lim_{x \uparrow c} \Prob(D_i = 1 \mid X_i = x)\), with the jump strictly between 0 and 1.

Fuzzy RDD combines RDD with instrumental variables: crossing the threshold \(T_i = \indic\{X_i \ge c\}\) acts as an instrument for actual treatment receipt \(D_i\). The fuzzy RDD estimand is the ratio of the jump in the outcome to the jump in treatment probability (a local Wald estimator):

\[ \tau_{\text{FRD}} = \frac{\lim_{x \downarrow c} \E[Y \mid X=x] - \lim_{x \uparrow c} \E[Y \mid X=x]}{\lim_{x \downarrow c} \E[D \mid X=x] - \lim_{x \uparrow c} \E[D \mid X=x]}. \]

Problem 6.11 · The Gelman-Imbens High-Order Polynomial Critique

Discuss the Gelman-Imbens critique of high-order polynomials in RDD. Why can fitting a 4th-degree polynomial across the full sample lead to erroneous causal inferences compared to a local linear specification?

Solution

Gelman and Imbens identify three core pathologies of high-order global polynomials in RDD:

  1. Excessive Boundary Weight: High-order polynomials give heavy, disproportionate weight to extreme observations far from the cutoff, making boundary estimates sensitive to distant outliers.
  2. Arbitrary Point Estimates: Small changes in polynomial degree (e.g., cubic vs quartic) often flip the sign and significance of \(\hat{\tau}\), encouraging researcher p-hacking.
  3. Inadequate Confidence Intervals: High-order polynomials produce confidence intervals that understate coverage error due to Runge-type boundary oscillations.

In contrast, local linear regression restricts attention to an empirical bandwidth around \(c\) where the true conditional expectation is well-approximated by a linear Taylor expansion, providing stable, robust causal estimates.

Problem 6.12 · Deriving the Asymptotically Optimal MSE Bandwidth Scaling

Formulate an automated bandwidth selection algorithm for local linear RDD based on cross-validation or mean squared error (MSE) minimization. How does the optimal bandwidth \(h^*\) scale with sample size \(N\)?

Solution

The Mean Squared Error of \(\hat{\tau}\) balances squared asymptotic bias and sampling variance:

\[ \text{MSE}(h) = \text{Bias}^2(h) + \Var(h) = C_1^2 h^4 + \frac{C_2}{N h}, \]

where \(C_1\) depends on curvature \(\mu_0''(c), \mu_1''(c)\) and \(C_2\) depends on residual variance \(\sigma^2\) and running variable density \(f_X(c)\).

Differentiating with respect to \(h\) and setting to zero:

\[ \frac{d \text{MSE}}{dh} = 4 C_1^2 h^3 - \frac{C_2}{N h^2} = 0 \implies 4 C_1^2 h^5 = \frac{C_2}{N}. \]

Solving for optimal bandwidth yields the canonical optimal scaling:

\[ h^* = \left( \frac{C_2}{4 C_1^2} \right)^{1/5} N^{-1/5} \propto N^{-1/5}. \]

As sample size increases, the optimal bandwidth narrows at rate \(N^{-1/5}\), shrinking bias while expanding the effective sample size \(N_h \propto N^{4/5}\) to ensure consistency.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. In a sharp regression discontinuity design, what defines treatment assignment \(D_i\)?

    In a sharp RDD, units with running variable \(X_i \ge c\) receive treatment with certainty (\(D_i = 1\)), while those below do not (\(D_i = 0\)).

  2. What is the fundamental identification assumption required for causal identification in a sharp RDD?

    Identification requires that counterfactual potential outcome conditional expectation functions are smooth and continuous through the threshold \(c\).

  3. In local linear RDD estimation, why is the running variable entered as \(\tilde{X}_i = X_i - c\) rather than raw \(X_i\)?

    Centering at \(c\) ensures that \(\tilde{X}_i = 0\) represents the threshold, making \(\hat{\tau}\) on \(D_i\) directly equal to the estimated jump at the boundary.

  4. How does increasing the estimation bandwidth \(h\) affect the properties of the local linear RDD estimator?

    Wider bandwidths incorporate more data points, reducing variance (\(\propto 1/(Nh)\)), but introduce bias (\(\propto h^2\)) when approximating curved functions with lines.

  5. What does the McCrary density test examine in an RDD setting?

    The McCrary test checks for a discontinuous jump in the marginal density of \(X\) at \(c\), which diagnoses strategic manipulation or sorting by agents.

  6. Why did Gelman and Imbens criticize the use of high-order global polynomials (e.g., 4th- or 5th-degree) in RDD?

    Gelman and Imbens showed that global polynomials produce noisy, unreliable boundary estimates driven by extreme points far from the threshold.

  7. At what mathematical rate does the Mean Squared Error (MSE) optimal bandwidth \(h^*\) shrink as total sample size \(N\) increases?

    Balancing squared bias \(h^4\) against variance \(1/(Nh)\) yields the optimal rate \(h^* \propto N^{-1/5}\).

  8. What population does the sharp RDD estimand \(\tau_{\text{SRD}}(c)\) identify causal effects for?

    RDD estimates a local average treatment effect specifically for units at or near the cutoff \(c\); it carries no automatic external validity away from the cutoff.
