Potential Outcomes & The Selection Problem

Counterfactual Reasoning, Average Treatment Effects, and Selection Bias Decomposition

Why It Matters

Every investment strategy, regulatory intervention, and corporate policy decision rests on a counterfactual claim: what would asset returns, volatility, or funding costs have been had the action not occurred? In observational financial data, comparing treated entities to untreated peers conflates the true causal treatment effect with baseline selection bias. Confounded comparisons lead quantitative researchers to mistake sorting for performance, misallocate capital to endogenous signals, and misjudge regulatory shocks.

1. The Neyman-Rubin Potential Outcomes Framework

The foundation of modern causal identification is the counterfactual framework formalizing potential states of nature. For each economic unit \(i\), let \(D_i \in \{0, 1\}\) denote a binary treatment assignment indicator, where \(D_i = 1\) indicates treatment adoption and \(D_i = 0\) indicates the control state.

Definition 1.1 · Potential Outcomes Framework

Under the potential outcomes framework, every unit \(i\) possesses two hypothetical outcome values:

  • \(Y_i(1)\): the outcome realized if unit \(i\) receives treatment (\(D_i = 1\)).
  • \(Y_i(0)\): the outcome realized if unit \(i\) does not receive treatment (\(D_i = 0\)).

The realized, observable outcome \(Y_i\) is linked to potential outcomes via the switching equation:

\[ Y_i = D_i Y_i(1) + (1 - D_i) Y_i(0). \]

The unit-level causal treatment effect is defined as \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\).

Remark 1.1 · The Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference

Because each unit either receives treatment or does not, researchers observe at most one potential outcome for any individual entity: \(Y_i(1)\) when \(D_i = 1\), and \(Y_i(0)\) when \(D_i = 0\). The counterfactual outcome is fundamentally missing. Consequently, individual treatment effects \(\tau_i\) can never be observed directly; identification focuses on population average counterfactual quantities.

Definition 1.2 · Average Treatment Effect (ATE) and ATT

The Average Treatment Effect (ATE) is the expected causal effect across the entire population:

\[ \tau_{\text{ATE}} = \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)]. \]

The Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) measures the expected effect among units that actually received treatment:

\[ \tau_{\text{ATT}} = \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1]. \]
Theorem 1.1 · Selection Bias Decomposition of Observational Differences

Assumptions:

  1. Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA): no interference between units and a single version of treatment.
  2. Finite first moments: \(\E[|Y_i(1)|] < \infty\) and \(\E[|Y_i(0)|] < \infty\).
  3. Non-degenerate treatment probability: \(0 < \Prob(D_i = 1) < 1\).

Conclusion: The difference in observational group means decomposes algebraically into the ATT plus baseline selection bias:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0] &= \tau_{\text{ATT}} + \text{Selection Bias}, \end{aligned} \]

where the selection bias term is defined as:

\[ \text{Selection Bias} = \E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]. \]
Proof

By definition of realized outcomes under the switching equation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] &= \E[Y_i(1) \mid D_i = 1], \\ \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0] &= \E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]. \end{aligned} \]

Subtracting the two expectations and adding and subtracting \(\E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1]\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0] \\ &= \E[Y_i(1) \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0] \\ &= \{\E[Y_i(1) \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1]\} \\ &\quad + \{\E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]\} \\ &= \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] + \text{Selection Bias} \\ &= \tau_{\text{ATT}} + \text{Selection Bias}. \end{aligned} \]

This completes the algebraic decomposition.

Theorem 1.2 · Identification Under Physical Randomization

Assumptions:

  1. Random assignment: treatment \(D_i\) is statistically independent of potential outcomes: \(\{Y_i(1), Y_i(0)\} \perp D_i\).
  2. SUTVA holds and moments are finite.

Conclusion: Selection bias vanishes identically, and the naive observational difference in means equals both the ATT and the ATE:

\[ \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0] = \tau_{\text{ATE}} = \tau_{\text{ATT}}. \]
Proof

Under statistical independence \(\{Y_i(1), Y_i(0)\} \perp D_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] &= \E[Y_i(0)], \\ \E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0] &= \E[Y_i(0)]. \end{aligned} \]

Consequently, the selection bias term evaluates to:

\[ \text{Selection Bias} = \E[Y_i(0)] - \E[Y_i(0)] = 0. \]

Furthermore, \(\E[Y_i(1) \mid D_i = 1] = \E[Y_i(1)]\), so:

\[ \tau_{\text{ATT}} = \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] = \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)] = \tau_{\text{ATE}}. \]

The observational difference identifies the causal effect without confounding.

2. Empirical Simulation: Selection Bias vs. Randomization

To inspect this mechanism quantitatively, we simulate an observational population where treatment adoption is endogenous (correlated with baseline quality \(U_i\)) and contrast it with randomized assignment.

Lab 1 · Observational Selection vs. Randomized Assignment

Under observational selection with confounder-correlated adoption, the naive difference in sample means equals the true ATE plus baseline selection bias; under randomized assignment, the naive difference converges directly to the true ATE.

"""Lab S6-L01-1: Potential outcomes and the selection problem.

SIMULATED: compares naive difference in means against the true Average Treatment Effect (ATE)
under observational selection (confounded) versus randomized treatment assignment.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n = 10000

    # True structural parameters
    mu_0 = 10.0
    true_tau = 2.50

    # Generate potential outcomes for population
    # U_i is an unobserved confounder (e.g. manager skill, firm quality)
    u = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.5) for _ in range(n)]
    y0 = [mu_0 + u[i] + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.5) for i in range(n)]
    y1 = [y0[i] + true_tau for i in range(n)]

    true_ate = sum(y1[i] - y0[i] for i in range(n)) / n

    # 1. Observational Selection: treatment assignment correlated with confounder U_i
    # Higher U_i -> higher probability of selecting into treatment D_obs = 1
    d_obs = [1 if u[i] + rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) > 0.0 else 0 for i in range(n)]
    y_obs = [y1[i] if d_obs[i] == 1 else y0[i] for i in range(n)]

    n_treat_obs = sum(d_obs)
    n_ctrl_obs = n - n_treat_obs

    mean_treat_obs = sum(y_obs[i] for i in range(n) if d_obs[i] == 1) / n_treat_obs
    mean_ctrl_obs = sum(y_obs[i] for i in range(n) if d_obs[i] == 0) / n_ctrl_obs
    naive_diff_obs = mean_treat_obs - mean_ctrl_obs

    # Actual baseline difference in potential outcome Y(0) between groups (Selection Bias)
    e_y0_treat = sum(y0[i] for i in range(n) if d_obs[i] == 1) / n_treat_obs
    e_y0_ctrl = sum(y0[i] for i in range(n) if d_obs[i] == 0) / n_ctrl_obs
    selection_bias = e_y0_treat - e_y0_ctrl

    # 2. Randomized Assignment: treatment assigned independently with probability 0.5
    d_rand = [1 if rng.random() < 0.5 else 0 for _ in range(n)]
    y_rand = [y1[i] if d_rand[i] == 1 else y0[i] for i in range(n)]

    n_treat_rnd = sum(d_rand)
    n_ctrl_rnd = n - n_treat_rnd

    mean_treat_rnd = sum(y_rand[i] for i in range(n) if d_rand[i] == 1) / n_treat_rnd
    mean_ctrl_rnd = sum(y_rand[i] for i in range(n) if d_rand[i] == 0) / n_ctrl_rnd
    naive_diff_rnd = mean_treat_rnd - mean_ctrl_rnd

    print("Potential Outcomes Identification Analysis (SIMULATED):")
    print(f"  Population size N: {n}")
    print(f"  True ATE: {true_ate:.4f}")
    print("\nObservational Sample (Confounded Selection):")
    print(f"  Treated mean Y: {mean_treat_obs:.4f} (N={n_treat_obs})")
    print(f"  Control mean Y: {mean_ctrl_obs:.4f} (N={n_ctrl_obs})")
    print(f"  Naive Difference: {naive_diff_obs:.4f}")
    print(f"  Baseline Y(0) Bias: {selection_bias:.4f}")
    print(f"  Decomposition Check (ATE + Bias): {true_ate + selection_bias:.4f}")
    print("\nRandomized Assignment Sample:")
    print(f"  Treated mean Y: {mean_treat_rnd:.4f} (N={n_treat_rnd})")
    print(f"  Control mean Y: {mean_ctrl_rnd:.4f} (N={n_ctrl_rnd})")
    print(f"  Randomized Difference: {naive_diff_rnd:.4f}")
    print(f"  Absolute Error vs ATE: {abs(naive_diff_rnd - true_ate):.4f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The potential outcomes framework rests on the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA). SUTVA breaks down in real financial markets in two primary ways:

  • Interference & General Equilibrium Spillovers: In financial networks, treating a systemically important bank (e.g., providing emergency liquidity) directly impacts the potential solvency outcomes of untreated counterparties through fire-sale externalities and funding channels. The outcome of unit \(i\) depends on the treatment vector of all other units \(\boldsymbol{D}_{-i}\).
  • Endogenous Price Formation: An algorithmic execution policy that changes trading speed alters market depth and bid-ask spreads for all participants. The clean isolation of unit-level treatment effects fails when the market clearing mechanism endogenously couples all agents.

Misconceptions

  • A statistically significant positive correlation between an executive incentive scheme and subsequent stock performance proves the incentive caused the outperformance.

    Correlation reflects both treatment effects and selection bias. High-performing firms with superior governance or unobserved manager talent are more likely to adopt aggressive incentive schemes. The baseline difference \(\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid D=0]\) is non-zero, making naive regressions severely biased.

  • Controlling for all observable firm characteristics in a multivariable OLS regression fully eliminates selection bias.

    Regression controls for observables only (the conditional independence assumption). If unobserved confounders (e.g., management quality, risk appetite, private bank relationships) correlate with treatment choice, selection on unobservables persists and OLS remains biased.

  • The Average Treatment Effect (ATE) and the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) are always identical in observational studies.

    ATE equals ATT only when treatment effects are strictly homogeneous across the population or when treatment is assigned completely at random. Under heterogeneous treatment effects, entities that anticipate the largest private benefits self-select into treatment, causing \(\tau_{\text{ATT}} > \tau_{\text{ATE}}\).

Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 1.1 · Computing Selection Bias from Potential Outcome Means

Suppose in a corporate finance study, \(\E[Y(1) \mid D=1] = 18.0\), \(\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] = 12.0\), and \(\E[Y(0) \mid D=0] = 9.0\). Calculate the true ATT, the selection bias, and the naive observational difference in means.

Solution

The ATT is \(\tau_{\text{ATT}} = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D=1] = 18.0 - 12.0 = 6.0\). Selection bias is \(\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid D=0] = 12.0 - 9.0 = 3.0\). The naive observational difference is \(\E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0] = 18.0 - 9.0 = 9.0\), which exactly equals \(\tau_{\text{ATT}} + \text{Selection Bias} = 6.0 + 3.0 = 9.0\).

Problem 1.2 · Switching Equation Outcome Calculation

For a sample of 4 firms, potential outcomes and treatments are: Firm 1: \(Y_1(0)=5, Y_1(1)=8, D_1=1\); Firm 2: \(Y_2(0)=4, Y_2(1)=6, D_2=0\); Firm 3: \(Y_3(0)=7, Y_3(1)=11, D_3=1\); Firm 4: \(Y_4(0)=6, Y_4(1)=8, D_4=0\). Calculate the observed outcome \(Y_i\) for each firm and the sample ATE.

Solution

Using the switching equation \(Y_i = D_i Y_i(1) + (1-D_i)Y_i(0)\): Firm 1: \(Y_1 = 8\); Firm 2: \(Y_2 = 4\); Firm 3: \(Y_3 = 11\); Firm 4: \(Y_4 = 6\). Individual treatment effects are \(\tau_1 = 3, \tau_2 = 2, \tau_3 = 4, \tau_4 = 2\). The true sample ATE is \((3 + 2 + 4 + 2) / 4 = 11 / 4 = 2.75\).

Problem 1.3 · Negative Selection Bias Identification

In a distressed debt restructuring study, treated firms exhibit observed post-event earnings of \(\E[Y \mid D=1] = 2.0\), while untreated solvent peers exhibit \(\E[Y \mid D=0] = 8.0\). If the true causal restructuring effect is \(\tau_{\text{ATT}} = +4.0\), what was the magnitude and sign of selection bias?

Solution

By Theorem 1.1, \(\E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0] = \tau_{\text{ATT}} + \text{Selection Bias}\). Substituting values: \(2.0 - 8.0 = 4.0 + \text{Selection Bias}\), so \(-6.0 = 4.0 + \text{Selection Bias}\), yielding \(\text{Selection Bias} = -10.0\). The untreated counterfactual baseline of distressed firms was severely negative relative to solvent firms.

Problem 1.4 · Derivation of ATE Decomposition Under Selection on Observables

Assume the Conditional Independence Assumption (CIA) holds: \(\{Y(1), Y(0)\} \perp D \mid X\). Prove that \(\tau_{\text{ATE}}\) is identified by the law of iterated expectations over covariate distribution \(F_X\).

Solution

Under CIA, for any \(X=x\), \(\E[Y(1) \mid X=x, D=1] = \E[Y(1) \mid X=x]\) and \(\E[Y(0) \mid X=x, D=0] = \E[Y(0) \mid X=x]\). The conditional average treatment effect is \(\tau(x) = \E[Y \mid X=x, D=1] - \E[Y \mid X=x, D=0]\). By the law of iterated expectations, \(\tau_{\text{ATE}} = \E_X[\tau(X)] = \int [\E[Y \mid X=x, D=1] - \E[Y \mid X=x, D=0]] \, dF_X(x)\), which depends exclusively on observable joint distributions.

Problem 1.5 · Proof of Equality Between ATE and ATT Under Complete Randomization

Formally prove that if treatment assignment \(D\) is statistically independent of \(\{Y(1), Y(0)\}\), then \(\tau_{\text{ATE}} = \tau_{\text{ATT}}\) even when individual treatment effects \(\tau_i\) are heterogeneous.

Solution

Let individual effect be \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\). By definition, \(\tau_{\text{ATT}} = \E[\tau_i \mid D_i = 1]\). Under complete independence, the joint distribution of \(\tau_i\) and \(D_i\) factorizes into marginals, implying \(\E[\tau_i \mid D_i = 1] = \E[\tau_i]\). Since \(\E[\tau_i] = \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)] = \tau_{\text{ATE}}\), it follows directly that \(\tau_{\text{ATT}} = \tau_{\text{ATE}}\).

Problem 1.6 · Non-Identification of the Correlation Between Potential Outcomes

Prove that the joint distribution of \((Y(1), Y(0))\) is not non-parametrically identified from observational data without additional structural restrictions.

Solution

Observational data identifies only the marginal distributions \(F_{Y(1) \mid D=1}\) and \(F_{Y(0) \mid D=0}\). By Fréchet-Hoeffding bounds, any copula connecting two marginals produces a valid joint distribution with identical observables. Since the covariance \(\Cov(Y(1), Y(0))\) involves the unobservable joint expectation \(\E[Y(1)Y(0)]\), no data can identify the correlation between counterfactuals without unverifiable structural assumptions.

Empirical

Problem 1.7 · Evaluating Selection Bias in Volatility Targeting Funds

A hedge fund database shows volatility-targeting funds had average Sharpe ratios of 0.85, while static funds had 0.60. An exogenous regulatory mandate forced a random sub-sample of static funds to adopt volatility targeting; their Sharpe ratios rose from 0.60 to 0.70. Estimate the causal ATE and the original selection bias.

Solution

The randomized mandate identifies the causal effect \(\tau = 0.70 - 0.60 = 0.10\). The naive observational difference was \(0.85 - 0.60 = 0.25\). By Theorem 1.1, Naive Difference = Causal Effect + Selection Bias, so \(0.25 = 0.10 + \text{Selection Bias}\), meaning \(\text{Selection Bias} = 0.15\). Superior managers originally chose volatility targeting.

Problem 1.8 · Propensity Score Common Support Diagnostics

Explain why a failure of the common support condition (\(0 < \Prob(D=1 \mid X) < 1\)) prevents identification of the ATE, and describe how empirical researchers diagnose support overlap.

Solution

If for some covariate region \(\Prob(D=1 \mid X=x) = 1\), there are no control units available to estimate counterfactual \(\E[Y(0) \mid X=x]\). Researchers diagnose this by estimating propensity scores \(\hat{e}(X)\) via logit and plotting overlapping kernel densities. Observations in non-overlapping regions are trimmed to ensure common support.

Problem 1.9 · Selection on Unobservables in Credit Rating Upgrades

Firms paying unsolicited rating agencies show higher subsequent bond yields than unrated peers. Discuss the unobserved confounders that generate positive selection bias in credit spreads.

Solution

Firms seeking unsolicited ratings frequently suffer unobserved liquidity strains or private banking friction, compelling them to seek alternative bond market access. This unobserved credit deterioration elevates baseline spreads \(\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] > \E[Y(0) \mid D=0]\), creating severe positive selection bias that masks any certification benefit.

Research

Problem 1.10 · SUTVA Violations in High-Frequency Market Making

Analyze how peer-order routing causes SUTVA violations when testing the causal impact of maker-taker fee changes at a single exchange venue.

Solution

When one exchange adjusts rebates, algorithmic routers dynamically redirect order flow across competing venues. The liquidity outcome at untreated exchanges is directly affected by the fee changes at the treated exchange. This market-wide order displacement violates SUTVA's no-interference assumption, requiring structural industrial organization models of multi-venue routing.

Problem 1.11 · Bounding Treatment Effects Under Non-Random Selection

Describe the Manski worst-case bounds for the ATE when outcomes are bounded in \([a, b]\) and treatment assignment is completely arbitrary.

Solution

Manski bounds evaluate extreme counterfactual scenarios. Since unobserved \(Y(0)\) for treated units must lie in \([a, b]\), the lowest possible counterfactual mean is \(a\) and the highest is \(b\). The resulting bound on \(\tau_{\text{ATE}}\) has width \(b - a\), which is always informative if outcomes are naturally bounded (e.g. default probabilities in \([0, 1]\)), even without unconfoundedness.

Problem 1.12 · Machine Learning and Double Machine Learning for Causal Estimation

Explain how Chernozhukov's Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML) resolves regularization bias when estimating causal effects with high-dimensional confounders.

Solution

Standard regularized machine learning (e.g. Lasso, random forests) introduces shrinkage bias that contaminates causal estimates. DML uses orthogonal Neyman-score moment equations and cross-fitting (sample splitting) so that errors in estimating high-dimensional nuisance functions have zero first-order impact on the structural parameter, restoring \(\sqrt{N}\) asymptotic normality.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the fundamental reason individual causal effects \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\) cannot be measured directly?

    The fundamental problem of causal inference states that we observe at most one potential outcome for each unit; the counterfactual is missing.

  2. In the decomposition \(\E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0] = \tau_{\text{ATT}} + \text{Bias}\), what is the exact formula for selection bias?

    Selection bias is the difference in untreated potential outcomes between treated and control groups: \(\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid D=0]\).

  3. If \(\E[Y \mid D=1] = 15.0\), \(\E[Y \mid D=0] = 9.0\), and true \(\tau_{\text{ATT}} = 2.0\), what is the value of selection bias?

    Naive Difference (15 - 9 = 6) equals ATT (2) plus Selection Bias, so Selection Bias = 6 - 2 = 4.0.

  4. Under what condition does selection bias equal zero?

    When \(D \perp Y(0)\), \(\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] = \E[Y(0) \mid D=0]\), causing selection bias to vanish.

  5. What does the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) require?

    SUTVA requires that one unit's potential outcomes are unaffected by other units' treatment status (no interference) and treatment is homogeneous.

  6. In a completely randomized experiment, \(\E[Y \mid D=1] = 12.0\) and \(\E[Y \mid D=0] = 9.0\). What is the estimated ATE?

    Under randomization, selection bias is zero, so ATE equals \(\E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0] = 12.0 - 9.0 = 3.0\).

  7. When is \(\tau_{\text{ATT}}\) strictly greater than \(\tau_{\text{ATE}}\)?

    If entities self-select based on heterogeneous gains, \(\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D=1] > \E[Y(1) - Y(0)]\), making ATT exceed ATE.

  8. How does SUTVA fail in central bank quantitative easing programs?

    Large-scale asset purchases generate market-wide general equilibrium yield shifts, violating the no-interference assumption across securities.
