Large-sample OLS asymptotics

In real-world quantitative finance, asset returns and macroeconomic variables are never normally distributed. Returns exhibit heavy tails, skewness, and volatility clustering. If classical regression inferences depended strictly on finite-sample Gaussian disturbances, linear models would be virtually useless in empirical finance. Large-sample asymptotic theory provides the rigorous mathematical justification for linear regression: as sample size grows, the central limit theorem guarantees that standardized OLS estimators converge in distribution to standard multivariate Gaussian vectors, even when errors are asymmetric, discrete, or moderately heavy-tailed.

The asymptotic framework

In finite samples, establishing exact distributions of \(\hat\beta\) requires strong, unverifiable distributional assumptions such as \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_n)\). Under OLS asymptotics, we evaluate the behavior of the estimator as the sample size \(n \to \infty\), replacing exact finite-sample distributions with limiting probability statements.

Consider the linear model in vector form for observations \(i = 1, \dots, n\):

\[ y_i = x_i' \beta + \varepsilon_i, \quad y = X\beta + \varepsilon \]

where \(x_i\) is a \(k \times 1\) column vector of regressors and \(\beta \in \R^k\). The OLS estimator is:

\[ \hat\beta_n = (X'X)^{-1} X'y = \beta + \left(\frac{1}{n} X'X\right)^{-1} \left(\frac{1}{n} X'\varepsilon\right) \]

where \(X'X = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i'\) and \(X'\varepsilon = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \varepsilon_i\).

Consistency via the Law of Large Numbers

Assumption 7.1 (Regularity Conditions for Consistency)

  1. Data Generation: \(\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) is an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sequence of random vectors with finite fourth moments.
  2. Identification / Non-singularity: The matrix \(\E[x_i x_i'] = Q\) exists, is finite, and is positive definite (hence invertible): \(\det(Q) > 0\).
  3. Orthogonality: \(\E[x_i \varepsilon_i] = 0\).

Theorem 7.1 (Consistency of the OLS Estimator)

Under Assumption 7.1, the OLS estimator \(\hat\beta_n\) converges in probability to the true parameter vector \(\beta\):

\[ \hat\beta_n \xrightarrow{p} \beta \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \]

Proof: By Khinchin's Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN), because \(\{x_i x_i'\}\) is an i.i.d. sequence with finite expectation \(\E[x_i x_i'] = Q\):

\[ \frac{1}{n} X'X = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i' \xrightarrow{p} Q \]

Because matrix inversion is continuous at non-singular matrices, the Continuous Mapping Theorem (CMT) implies:

\[ \left(\frac{1}{n} X'X\right)^{-1} \xrightarrow{p} Q^{-1} \]

Similarly, the cross-product sequence \(\{x_i \varepsilon_i\}\) is i.i.d. with mean \(\E[x_i \varepsilon_i] = 0\) by Assumption 7.1(3). Applying Khinchin's WLLN:

\[ \frac{1}{n} X'\varepsilon = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \varepsilon_i \xrightarrow{p} \E[x_i \varepsilon_i] = 0 \]

By Slutsky's theorem, the product of a sequence converging in probability to a constant matrix and a sequence converging in probability to zero converges to zero:

\[ \hat\beta_n - \beta = \left(\frac{1}{n} X'X\right)^{-1} \left(\frac{1}{n} X'\varepsilon\right) \xrightarrow{p} Q^{-1} \cdot 0 = 0 \]

Hence \(\hat\beta_n \xrightarrow{p} \beta\). ■

Asymptotic normality and the sandwich covariance

Consistency tells us that \(\hat\beta_n\) collapses onto \(\beta\), but it does not specify the rate of convergence or the shape of the estimation error distribution. To study fluctuations around \(\beta\), we scale the estimation error by \(\sqrt{n}\):

\[ \sqrt{n}(\hat\beta_n - \beta) = \left(\frac{1}{n} X'X\right)^{-1} \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} X'\varepsilon\right) \]

Theorem 7.2 (Asymptotic Normality of OLS)

Under Assumption 7.1, let \(\Omega = \E[\varepsilon_i^2 x_i x_i']\) be finite and positive definite. Then:

\[ \sqrt{n}(\hat\beta_n - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(0, Q^{-1} \Omega Q^{-1}\right) \]

Proof: The score vector component \(s_i = x_i \varepsilon_i\) has mean \(\E[s_i] = \E[x_i \varepsilon_i] = 0\) and variance-covariance matrix:

\[ \Var(s_i) = \E[s_i s_i'] = \E[\varepsilon_i^2 x_i x_i'] = \Omega \]

Because \(\{s_i\}\) is an i.i.d. sequence with mean zero and finite covariance matrix \(\Omega\), the multivariate Lindeberg-Lévy Central Limit Theorem implies:

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} X'\varepsilon = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^n s_i \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Omega) \]

From Theorem 7.1, \(\left(\frac{1}{n} X'X\right)^{-1} \xrightarrow{p} Q^{-1}\). Applying Slutsky's theorem for the product of a matrix converging in probability and a vector converging in distribution:

\[ \sqrt{n}(\hat\beta_n - \beta) = \left(\frac{1}{n} X'X\right)^{-1} \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} X'\varepsilon\right) \xrightarrow{d} Q^{-1} \cdot \mathcal{N}(0, \Omega) = \mathcal{N}\left(0, Q^{-1} \Omega Q^{-1}\right) \]

which establishes the result. ■

The matrix \(V_{\text{asym}} = Q^{-1} \Omega Q^{-1}\) is called the sandwich covariance matrix:

  • The bread is \(Q^{-1} = (\E[x_i x_i'])^{-1}\), dictated by the second moment of the regressors.
  • The meat is \(\Omega = \E[\varepsilon_i^2 x_i x_i']\), the variance of the regression score.

Under conditional homoskedasticity, where \(\E[\varepsilon_i^2 | x_i] = \sigma^2\) is constant, the law of iterated expectations simplifies the meat:

\[ \Omega = \E[\varepsilon_i^2 x_i x_i'] = \E\left[\E[\varepsilon_i^2 | x_i] x_i x_i'\right] = \sigma^2 \E[x_i x_i'] = \sigma^2 Q \]

In this special homoskedastic case, the bread cancels one layer of the meat:

\[ V_{\text{homo}} = Q^{-1} (\sigma^2 Q) Q^{-1} = \sigma^2 Q^{-1} \]

This confirms that the classical OLS covariance formula is asymptotically valid when homoskedasticity holds, even if the error distribution is non-Gaussian.

The delta method for nonlinear functions

Quantitative researchers frequently require standard errors and confidence intervals for nonlinear functions of estimated parameters, such as the Sharpe ratio of an optimal portfolio \(\mu / \sigma\), the half-life of mean reversion \(-\ln(2)/\ln(\phi)\), or long-run multipliers \(\sum_{j=0}^\infty \beta_j\).

Theorem 7.3 (Multivariate Delta Method)

Let \(\hat\theta_n\) be a sequence of estimators such that \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V)\). Let \(g: \R^k \to \R^m\) be continuously differentiable at \(\theta_0\), with Jacobian matrix \(J(\theta_0) = \left. \frac{\partial g(\theta)}{\partial \theta'} \right|_{\theta=\theta_0} \in \R^{m \times k}\). Then:

\[ \sqrt{n}\left(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta_0)\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(0, J(\theta_0) V J(\theta_0)'\right) \]

Proof: By Taylor expansion of \(g\) around \(\theta_0\):

\[ g(\hat\theta_n) = g(\theta_0) + J(\tilde\theta_n)(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) \]

where \(\tilde\theta_n\) lies on the line segment connecting \(\hat\theta_n\) and \(\theta_0\). Multiplying by \(\sqrt{n}\):

\[ \sqrt{n}(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta_0)) = J(\tilde\theta_n) \sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) \]

Because \(\hat\theta_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\), we have \(\tilde\theta_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\). By continuity of the gradient, \(J(\tilde\theta_n) \xrightarrow{p} J(\theta_0)\). Applying Slutsky's theorem yields the asymptotic Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix \(J(\theta_0) V J(\theta_0)'\). ■

Lab 1 · OLS asymptotic coverage under non-normal errors

Hypothesis: For centered exponential disturbances with skewness 2 and kurtosis 9, asymptotic nominal 95% confidence intervals achieve empirical coverage within 2 standard errors of 0.95 across sample sizes \(n \in \{20, 80, 320\}\), confirming the central limit theorem rescue of linear regression inference.

"""Lab S2-L07-1: Asymptotic distribution and coverage of OLS under non-normal errors.

Evaluates coverage of asymptotic t-intervals across sample sizes n = 20, 80, 320
under centered exponential errors. Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    sample_sizes = [20, 80, 320]
    replications = 3000

    beta_0 = 1.00
    beta_1 = 2.00

    # Exponential parameter: scale = 1.0, mean = 1.0, var = 1.0
    # Centered exponential error: e = Exp(1) - 1.0 (mean = 0, var = 1, skewness = 2, kurtosis = 9)

    print("Sample Size n | Nominal | Empirical Coverage | Binomial SE")
    print("-" * 55)

    for n in sample_sizes:
        covered = 0
        df = n - 2
        # Student-t critical value approximation for df degrees of freedom
        # For df >= 18, t_0.975 is close to 1.96 + 2.4 / df
        # Let's compute accurate t critical value using continued fraction / standard series or known values:
        if n == 20:
            t_crit = 2.1009  # df = 18
        elif n == 80:
            t_crit = 1.9908  # df = 78
        else:
            t_crit = 1.9675  # df = 318

        for _ in range(replications):
            # Uniform regressor x in [1, 5]
            x = [rng.uniform(1.0, 5.0) for _ in range(n)]
            # Centered exponential disturbance: -log(U) - 1.0
            errors = [-math.log(rng.random()) - 1.0 for _ in range(n)]
            y = [beta_0 + beta_1 * xi + ei for xi, ei in zip(x, errors, strict=True)]

            x_bar = sum(x) / n
            y_bar = sum(y) / n
            sxx = sum((xi - x_bar) ** 2 for xi in x)
            sxy = sum((xi - x_bar) * (yi - y_bar) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True))

            b1_hat = sxy / sxx
            resids = [yi - (y_bar + b1_hat * (xi - x_bar)) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True)]
            rss = sum(ri**2 for ri in resids)

            sigma2_hat = rss / df
            se_b1 = math.sqrt(sigma2_hat / sxx)

            margin = t_crit * se_b1
            if b1_hat - margin <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + margin:
                covered += 1

        coverage_rate = covered / replications
        binom_se = math.sqrt(coverage_rate * (1.0 - coverage_rate) / replications)

        print(f"      n = {n:3d} |  0.9500 |             {coverage_rate:.4f} |      {binom_se:.4f}")


main()
Sample Size n | Nominal | Empirical Coverage | Binomial SE
-------------------------------------------------------
      n =  20 |  0.9500 |             0.9520 |      0.0039
      n =  80 |  0.9500 |             0.9513 |      0.0039
      n = 320 |  0.9500 |             0.9463 |      0.0041

Even with strongly skewed and leptokurtic errors (kurtosis 9), OLS t-based confidence intervals exhibit empirical coverage within 0.005 of the nominal 95% level across all sample sizes. This verifies that asymptotic normality takes hold rapidly in linear regression models.

Where the canon breaks

Asymptotic normality is a cornerstone of econometric analysis, but it fails under specific structural violations common in financial engineering:

  • Heavy tails and infinite variance: If disturbances or regressors follow power-law distributions with tail index \(\alpha \le 2\) (such as Pareto or Cauchy distributions), \(\E[\varepsilon_i^2] = \infty\). The classical Lindeberg-Lévy CLT collapses, and the normalized estimator converges to an \(\alpha\)-stable Lévy process rather than a Gaussian law.
  • Unit roots and non-stationarity: When regressors follow random walks or integrated processes (\(x_t = x_{t-1} + v_t\)), \(\frac{1}{n} X'X\) does not converge to a deterministic matrix \(Q\). Instead, \(\frac{1}{n^2} X'X\) converges to an integral of Brownian motion, and the estimator converges at rate \(n\) rather than \(\sqrt{n}\) (super-consistency) with non-standard Dickey-Fuller distributions.
  • Weak identification / weak instruments: When explanatory variation is vanishingly small relative to sample size, the denominator \(\frac{1}{n} X'X\) is close to singular, producing Cauchy-like bimodality and severe size distortions in asymptotic tests.
  • Parameters on the boundary: If the true parameter lies on the boundary of the allowable parameter space (e.g., non-negative volatility or variance components \(\sigma^2 = 0\)), the asymptotic distribution is a mixture of distributions rather than a standard normal law.

Misconceptions

  1. OLS t-tests are invalid unless disturbances are normally distributed.

    The Gauss-Markov theorem requires only mean zero, constant variance, and uncorrelated errors for OLS to be BLUE; it never requires normality. As shown in Theorem 7.2, the central limit theorem guarantees that t-statistics are asymptotically standard normal for any disturbance distribution with finite fourth moments.

  2. Gathering more data solves omitted variable bias or endogeneity.

    Consistency requires \(\E[x_i \varepsilon_i] = 0\). If \(\E[x_i \varepsilon_i] = c \ne 0\), then \(\frac{1}{n} X'\varepsilon \xrightarrow{p} c\), and \(\hat\beta_n \xrightarrow{p} \beta + Q^{-1} c \ne \beta\). Increasing sample size \(n\) simply causes \(\hat\beta_n\) to converge with zero variance to the wrong parameter value.

  3. The delta method provides exact standard errors for nonlinear functions.

    The delta method is a first-order linear approximation. When the function \(g(\cdot)\) exhibits high local curvature (e.g., ratios where the denominator is close to zero), the remainder terms in the Taylor expansion are non-trivial, causing substantial finite-sample bias in confidence intervals.

Practice problems

Mechanics

State Khinchin's Weak Law of Large Numbers and explain how it applies to the sample covariance matrix \(\frac{1}{n} X'X\).

Solution

Khinchin's WLLN states that if \(\{Z_i\}_{i=1}^n\) is an i.i.d. sequence of random variables with finite expectation \(\E[Z_i] = \mu\), then the sample average \(\bar{Z}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i \xrightarrow{p} \mu\). For \(\frac{1}{n} X'X = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i'\), each element is an average of i.i.d. variables with finite expectation \(Q_{jk} = \E[x_{ij} x_{ik}]\), so element-by-element convergence in probability guarantees \(\frac{1}{n} X'X \xrightarrow{p} Q\).

Define Slutsky's theorem for vector operations.

Solution

Let \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) and \(Y_n \xrightarrow{p} c\), where \(c\) is a constant vector or matrix. Slutsky's theorem establishes that \(X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X + c\), \(Y_n X_n \xrightarrow{d} c X\), and \(X_n / Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X / c\) provided \(c \ne 0\).

In the asymptotic sandwich covariance matrix \(V = Q^{-1} \Omega Q^{-1}\), identify which term represents the "meat" and define it mathematically.

Solution

The "meat" is \(\Omega = \E[\varepsilon_i^2 x_i x_i']\), which represents the variance-covariance matrix of the regression score vector \(s_i = x_i \varepsilon_i\).

Proof

Show how the sandwich covariance matrix \(V = Q^{-1} \Omega Q^{-1}\) collapses to \(\sigma^2 Q^{-1}\) when errors are conditionally homoskedastic.

Solution

Under conditional homoskedasticity, \(\E[\varepsilon_i^2 | x_i] = \sigma^2\). By the Law of Iterated Expectations, \(\Omega = \E[\varepsilon_i^2 x_i x_i'] = \E[\E[\varepsilon_i^2 | x_i] x_i x_i'] = \sigma^2 \E[x_i x_i'] = \sigma^2 Q\). Substituting this into the sandwich formula yields \(V = Q^{-1} (\sigma^2 Q) Q^{-1} = \sigma^2 Q^{-1} Q Q^{-1} = \sigma^2 Q^{-1}\).

Derive the asymptotic variance of the transformed parameter \(\theta = \beta_1^2\) in a simple univariate regression using the delta method, given \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\beta_1 - \beta_1) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V_1)\).

Solution

Let \(g(\beta_1) = \beta_1^2\). The derivative is \(g'(\beta_1) = 2\beta_1\). By the delta method, \(\sqrt{n}(g(\hat\beta_1) - g(\beta_1)) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, [g'(\beta_1)]^2 V_1) = \mathcal{N}(0, 4\beta_1^2 V_1)\). The asymptotic variance is \(4\beta_1^2 V_1\).

Explain why the delta method fails when testing \(\theta = \beta_1^2\) if the true value is \(\beta_1 = 0\).

Solution

When \(\beta_1 = 0\), the first derivative is \(g'(0) = 0\). The first-order Taylor expansion yields degenerate zero variance. A second-order expansion is required: \(n g(\hat\beta_1) = (\sqrt{n}\hat\beta_1)^2 \xrightarrow{d} V_1 \cdot \chi^2(1)\). The limiting distribution is non-normal (scaled chi-square), invalidating normal critical values.

Empirical

In asset pricing regressions, market returns often exhibit excess kurtosis (leptokurtosis) around 7 to 10. How does this non-normality affect OLS beta estimates and standard errors in sample sizes of \(T = 60\) monthly observations?

Solution

Under finite fourth moments, the OLS slope estimator remains consistent and asymptotically normal. With \(T = 60\) observations, the central limit theorem provides adequate approximation for point estimates, but excess kurtosis increases the sampling variance of standard error estimators. Robust standard errors or bootstrap adjustments are recommended to prevent underestimating parameter uncertainty.

Suppose an empirical researcher estimates a predictive regression with a sample of \(T = 200\) daily returns. Why does the delta method sometimes yield confidence intervals for the annualized Sharpe ratio that include negative values even when the estimated Sharpe ratio is positive?

Solution

The delta method produces symmetric asymptotic Gaussian confidence intervals \(\hat{\text{SR}} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \text{SE}(\hat{\text{SR}})\). When the sampling variability of the estimated mean return is large relative to the mean itself (common in high-frequency financial returns), the margin of error exceeds \(\hat{\text{SR}}\), causing the lower symmetric bound to extend into negative territory.

In an AR(1) model of real interest rates \(r_t = \rho r_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) with \(\rho = 0.95\) and \(T = 100\), evaluate the adequacy of the asymptotic normal distribution for testing \(H_0: \rho = 1\).

Solution

When \(\rho\) is close to unity, the standard asymptotic normal distribution is a poor approximation in sample sizes of \(T = 100\). The distribution of \(\hat\rho\) is skewed toward zero with a negative Kendall bias of approximately \(-2\rho/T = -0.019\). For testing \(\rho = 1\), Dickey-Fuller unit-root distribution critical values must be used rather than standard normal critical values.

Research

Contrast the convergence rate of OLS under stationary regressors versus a unit-root regressor \(x_t = x_{t-1} + v_t\) where \(\rho = 1\).

Solution

For stationary regressors, \(\sum x_t^2 = \mathcal{O}_p(T)\), yielding the standard \(\sqrt{T}\) rate of convergence with a Gaussian limit. For a unit root, \(\sum x_t^2 = \mathcal{O}_p(T^2)\). Consequently, \(T(\hat\rho - 1) \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\int_0^1 W(r) dW(r)}{\int_0^1 W(r)^2 dr}\), where \(W(r)\) is standard Brownian motion. The estimator is super-consistent (rate \(T\)) and has an asymmetric, non-Gaussian Dickey-Fuller distribution.

Let \(\hat\beta_n\) be the OLS estimator in \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\). Prove that the residual variance estimator \(s^2 = \frac{e'e}{n-k}\) is a consistent estimator of \(\sigma^2 = \E[\varepsilon_i^2]\) under homoskedasticity.

Solution

Residuals satisfy \(e = M\varepsilon = \varepsilon - X(X'X)^{-1} X'\varepsilon\). Then \(e'e = \varepsilon'\varepsilon - \varepsilon'X(X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon\). Dividing by \(n\): \(\frac{e'e}{n} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \varepsilon_i^2 - \left(\frac{1}{n} \varepsilon'X\right) \left(\frac{1}{n} X'X\right)^{-1} \left(\frac{1}{n} X'\varepsilon\right)\). By Khinchin's WLLN, \(\frac{1}{n} \sum \varepsilon_i^2 \xrightarrow{p} \sigma^2\). By consistency, \(\frac{1}{n} X'\varepsilon \xrightarrow{p} 0\), so the second quadratic term vanishes in probability to \(0 \cdot Q^{-1} \cdot 0 = 0\). Since \(\frac{n}{n-k} \to 1\), \(s^2 = \frac{n}{n-k} \frac{e'e}{n} \xrightarrow{p} \sigma^2\).

Formulate the asymptotic Wald test for the linear hypothesis \(H_0: R\beta = r\) where \(R \in \R^{q \times k}\) has full row rank \(q\). State its asymptotic distribution under \(H_0\).

Solution

Under \(H_0\), \(\sqrt{n}(R\hat\beta_n - r) = R \sqrt{n}(\hat\beta_n - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, R V_{\text{asym}} R')\). Let \(\hat{V}_n\) be a consistent estimator of \(V_{\text{asym}}\). The Wald statistic is \(W_n = n (R\hat\beta_n - r)' [R \hat{V} R']^{-1} (R\hat\beta_n - r)\). Because it is a quadratic form in a standard Gaussian vector of dimension \(q\), \(W_n \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(q)\) under \(H_0\).

Retrieval quiz

  1. Which condition is necessary and sufficient for the OLS estimator to be consistent?

    Options

    Consistency requires only non-singular regressor second moments and asymptotic orthogonality of regressors and disturbances (Theorem 7.1).

  2. In the sandwich covariance matrix \(V = Q^{-1} \Omega Q^{-1}\), what represents \(\Omega\)?

    Options

    \(\Omega\) is the asymptotic covariance of the score vector \(s_i = x_i \varepsilon_i\) (Theorem 7.2).

  3. Why do nominal 95% confidence intervals based on the standard normal distribution achieve asymptotically correct coverage even when errors are skewed?

    Options

    By the Lindeberg-Lévy CLT, the standardized score converges to a Gaussian vector regardless of error skewness (Theorem 7.2 and Lab 1).

  4. What is the convergence rate of the OLS estimator \(\hat\beta_n\) toward \(\beta\) under stationary regressors?

    Options

    \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\beta_n - \beta) = \mathcal{O}_p(1)\), meaning \(\hat\beta_n - \beta = \mathcal{O}_p(n^{-1/2})\).

  5. When estimating \(\gamma = g(\beta) = \beta_1 / \beta_2\), what mathematical tool provides the asymptotic distribution of \(g(\hat\beta)\)?

    Options

    The delta method uses a first-order Taylor expansion around \(\beta\) to establish the asymptotic normality of continuously differentiable transformations (Theorem 7.3).

  6. If \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\beta - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V)\), what is the asymptotic distribution of \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\beta^2 - \beta^2)\) when \(\beta \ne 0\)?

    Options

    With \(g(\beta) = \beta^2\), \(g'(\beta) = 2\beta\). The delta method asymptotic variance is \([g'(\beta)]^2 V = 4\beta^2 V\) (Problem P2).

  7. Under what disturbance condition does the Lindeberg-Lévy CLT fail to apply to OLS estimation?

    Options

    Lindeberg-Lévy requires finite second moments; if \(\E[\varepsilon_i^2] = \infty\), estimators converge to \(\alpha\)-stable distributions (Section Canon breaks).

  8. What is the asymptotic distribution of the Wald statistic \(W = n(R\hat\beta - r)' [R \hat{V} R']^{-1} (R\hat\beta - r)\) under \(H_0: R\beta = r\) where \(R\) has \(q\) linearly independent rows?

    Options

    The Wald statistic is an asymptotic quadratic form in a standard normal vector of dimension \(q\), yielding \(\chi^2(q)\) (Problem E3).
