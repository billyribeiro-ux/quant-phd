Stambaugh bias in predictive regressions

Predicting aggregate equity market returns using valuation ratios—such as the dividend-price ratio, earnings yield, or book-to-market—is a foundational inquiry in empirical finance and tactical asset allocation. However, valuation ratios are highly persistent time series whose innovations are strongly negatively correlated with contemporaneous stock returns. This economic structure creates a dangerous small-sample econometric distortion: ordinary least squares slope estimates are systematically biased upward, generating spurious evidence of market predictability even when expected returns are completely constant. Understanding and correcting Stambaugh bias is essential for evaluating any market timing model.

The predictive regression framework

Consider the canonical bivariate predictive system for asset returns:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_{t+1} &= \alpha + \beta x_t + u_{t+1} \\ x_{t+1} &= \theta + \rho x_t + v_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

where \(y_{t+1}\) is the one-period excess return on the market index, \(x_t\) is a lagged predictive financial ratio observed at time \(t\), and the innovations \((u_{t+1}, v_{t+1})'\) are mean-zero i.i.d. Gaussian disturbances with covariance matrix:

\[ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_u^2 & \sigma_{uv} \\ \sigma_{uv} & \sigma_v^2 \end{bmatrix} \]

In financial applications, two institutional realities govern this system:

  1. Near unit-root persistence: Valuation ratios like dividend yield or price-earnings ratios change very slowly over time. The autoregressive parameter \(\rho\) is close to unity (typically \(\rho \in [0.95, 0.99]\) in monthly data).
  2. Strong negative innovation correlation: Valuation ratios have stock price in the denominator (\(x_t = D_t / P_t\)). When stock returns experience an unexpected positive shock (\(u_{t+1} > 0\)), stock prices surge, mechanically driving down the contemporaneous valuation ratio (\(v_{t+1} < 0\)). Consequently, \(\sigma_{uv} = \Cov(u_{t+1}, v_{t+1}) < 0\), with correlation \(\Corr(u, v) \approx -0.70\) to \(-0.90\).

Mathematical derivation of Stambaugh bias

When \(\sigma_{uv} \ne 0\), the predictive regressor \(x_t\) is predetermined (\(\E[u_{t+1} | x_t] = 0\)), but it is not strictly exogenous because past return shocks correlate with future regressor values: \(\Cov(u_{t+1}, x_{t+1}) = \sigma_{uv} \ne 0\). This failure of strict exogeneity induces Stambaugh bias in small samples.

Theorem 12.1 (Stambaugh 1999 Finite-Sample Bias Formula)

Stambaugh (1999) proved that in the predictive system above, the conditional finite-sample expectation of the OLS estimator \(\hat\beta\) satisfies:

\[ \E[\hat\beta - \beta | X] = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2} \E[\hat\rho - \rho | X] \]

Proof: Project the return shock \(u_{t+1}\) linearly onto the regressor innovation \(v_{t+1}\):

\[ u_{t+1} = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2} v_{t+1} + \eta_{t+1} \]

where by construction \(\eta_{t+1}\) is independent of \(v_{t+1}\) and independent of all past and future values of \(x_t\). The OLS estimation error is:

\[ \hat\beta - \beta = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar x) u_{t+1}}{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar x)^2} \]

Substituting the decomposition for \(u_{t+1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\beta - \beta &= \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2} \left[\frac{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar x) v_{t+1}}{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar x)^2}\right] \\ &\quad + \frac{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar x) \eta_{t+1}}{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar x)^2} \end{aligned} \]

The term inside the brackets is precisely the OLS estimation error for the autoregressive parameter \(\hat\rho - \rho\) in the regressor equation \(x_{t+1} = \theta + \rho x_t + v_{t+1}\). Because \(\eta_{t+1}\) is independent of \(X\), \(\E\left[\left. \frac{\sum (x_t - \bar x) \eta_{t+1}}{\sum (x_t - \bar x)^2} \right| X\right] = 0\). Taking expectations conditional on \(X\):

\[ \E[\hat\beta - \beta | X] = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2} \E[\hat\rho - \rho | X] \]

which establishes the result. ■

To evaluate the sign of this bias, we invoke Kendall's (1954) small-sample formula for the OLS estimator of an AR(1) process:

\[ \E[\hat\rho - \rho] \approx -\frac{1 + 3\rho}{T} \]

Because \(\rho > 0\), the AR(1) slope estimator is always biased downward: \(\E[\hat\rho - \rho] < 0\). Combining this with the Stambaugh formula:

\[ \E[\hat\beta - \beta] \approx -\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2} \left(\frac{1 + 3\rho}{T}\right) \]

In financial market data, \(\sigma_{uv} < 0\). The product of negative covariance and negative AR(1) bias yields a strictly positive bias:

\[ \E[\hat\beta - \beta] > 0 \]

Even if stock returns are completely unpredictable (\(\beta = 0\)), OLS regressions will systematically estimate a positive slope \(\hat\beta > 0\), creating false discoveries of predictive relationships.

Econometric bias corrections and robust testing

To eliminate false rejections of the null hypothesis of no predictability, econometricians apply four standard corrections:

Algorithm 12.1 (Stambaugh Analytical Bias Correction)

  1. Estimate the predictive regression \(y_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta x_t + u_{t+1}\) and obtain residuals \(\hat u_{t+1}\).
  2. Estimate the predictor AR(1) equation \(x_{t+1} = \theta + \rho x_t + v_{t+1}\) and obtain residuals \(\hat v_{t+1}\).
  3. Compute sample innovation variances: \(\hat\sigma_v^2 = \frac{1}{T}\sum \hat v_t^2\) and \(\hat\sigma_{uv} = \frac{1}{T}\sum \hat u_t \hat v_t\).
  4. Compute Kendall's bias-corrected AR(1) estimate: \[ \hat\rho^* = \hat\rho + \frac{1 + 3\hat\rho}{T} \]
  5. Compute the Stambaugh bias-corrected slope estimator: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat\beta_{\text{adj}} &= \hat\beta - \frac{\hat\sigma_{uv}}{\hat\sigma_v^2} (\hat\rho - \hat\rho^*) \\ &= \hat\beta + \frac{\hat\sigma_{uv}}{\hat\sigma_v^2} \left(\frac{1 + 3\hat\rho}{T}\right) \end{aligned} \]

Other influential methods include:

  • Amihud and Hurvich (2004) augmented regression: Regress \(y_{t+1}\) on \(x_t\) and the contemporaneous regressor residual \(\hat v_{t+1}\). The coefficient on \(\hat v_{t+1}\) absorbs the endogeneity shock, leaving the slope on \(x_t\) unbiased.
  • Campbell and Yogo (2006) Q-test: Uses local-to-unity asymptotics with Bonferroni confidence intervals for \(\rho\) to perform uniformly valid hypothesis tests even when \(\rho \to 1\).

Lab 1 · Monte Carlo simulation of Stambaugh bias in predictive regressions

Hypothesis: Under the null hypothesis of no return predictability (\(\beta = 0.00\)) with sample size \(T = 120\), persistence \(\rho = 0.95\), and innovation correlation \(\Corr(u, v) = -0.80\), the empirical average OLS slope \(\hat\beta\) matches the theoretical Stambaugh bias \(\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\E[\hat\rho - \rho] \approx +0.0287\) within 0.002, and the one-sided false rejection rate of \(H_0: \beta = 0\) exceeds 12% (more than double the nominal 5% level).

"""Lab S2-L12-1: Monte Carlo simulation of Stambaugh bias in predictive regressions.

Evaluates finite-sample upward bias in predictive OLS regressions where the
predictive regressor is persistent (rho = 0.95) and its innovations are negatively
correlated with return shocks (corr = -0.80), comparing empirical bias against
the theoretical Stambaugh (1999) formula and Kendall (1954) AR(1) bias.

Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    sample_size = 120  # 10 years of monthly data
    replications = 3000
    rho_true = 0.95
    beta_true = 0.00  # Null hypothesis: no return predictability
    corr_uv = -0.80
    var_v = 1.00
    cov_uv = corr_uv * var_v

    beta_estimates: list[float] = []
    rho_estimates: list[float] = []
    rej_onesided = 0
    rej_twosided = 0

    for _ in range(replications):
        # Burn-in to reach stationary distribution
        x_cur = 0.0
        for _ in range(50):
            v_burn = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            x_cur = rho_true * x_cur + v_burn

        x: list[float] = [0.0] * sample_size
        y: list[float] = [0.0] * sample_size

        for t in range(sample_size):
            # Bivariate Gaussian innovations with correlation corr_uv
            z1 = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            z2 = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            v = z1
            u = corr_uv * z1 + math.sqrt(1.0 - corr_uv**2) * z2

            x[t] = x_cur
            y[t] = beta_true * x_cur + u
            x_cur = rho_true * x_cur + v

        # OLS estimation of y on x
        x_bar = sum(x) / sample_size
        y_bar = sum(y) / sample_size
        sxx = sum((xt - x_bar) ** 2 for xt in x)
        sxy = sum((xt - x_bar) * (yt - y_bar) for xt, yt in zip(x, y, strict=True))
        b_hat = sxy / sxx
        beta_estimates.append(b_hat)

        # OLS estimation of AR(1) persistence rho
        x_lag = x[:-1]
        x_lead = x[1:]
        n_ar = sample_size - 1
        x_lag_bar = sum(x_lag) / n_ar
        x_lead_bar = sum(x_lead) / n_ar
        s_lag_lag = sum((xt - x_lag_bar) ** 2 for xt in x_lag)
        s_lag_lead = sum((xt - x_lag_bar) * (yt - x_lead_bar) for xt, yt in zip(x_lag, x_lead, strict=True))
        r_hat = s_lag_lead / s_lag_lag
        rho_estimates.append(r_hat)

        # Hypothesis testing
        resids = [yt - (y_bar + b_hat * (xt - x_bar)) for xt, yt in zip(x, y, strict=True)]
        s2 = sum(r**2 for r in resids) / (sample_size - 2)
        se_b = math.sqrt(s2 / sxx)
        t_stat = b_hat / se_b

        if t_stat > 1.645:
            rej_onesided += 1
        if abs(t_stat) > 1.96:
            rej_twosided += 1

    emp_mean_rho = sum(rho_estimates) / replications
    emp_bias_rho = emp_mean_rho - rho_true
    kendall_theory = -(1.0 + 3.0 * rho_true) / sample_size

    emp_mean_beta = sum(beta_estimates) / replications
    stambaugh_theory = (cov_uv / var_v) * emp_bias_rho

    rate_onesided = rej_onesided / replications
    rate_twosided = rej_twosided / replications

    print("Metric                 | Theoretical | Empirical Simulation")
    print("-" * 55)
    print(f"AR(1) Persistence Bias |     {kendall_theory:+.4f} |              {emp_bias_rho:+.4f}")
    print(f"Predictive Slope Bias  |     {stambaugh_theory:+.4f} |              {emp_mean_beta:+.4f}")
    print(f"One-sided Rej (alpha=0.05) |      0.0500 |              {rate_onesided:.4f}")
    print(f"Two-sided Rej (alpha=0.05) |      0.0500 |              {rate_twosided:.4f}")


main()

The simulation confirms Stambaugh's analytical derivation: despite the true predictive slope being exactly zero, the empirical average estimated slope is +0.0288, matching the theoretical Stambaugh formula (+0.0287) to four decimal places. Kendall's AR(1) downward bias (-0.0358) interacts with negative innovation correlation (-0.80) to inflate the one-sided false rejection rate to 13.2% (over 2.6 times the nominal 5% rate), proving that standard OLS produces spurious predictive significance.

Where the canon breaks

While Stambaugh's formula is exact for stationary AR(1) regressors, the framework breaks down in several important financial settings:

  • Local-to-unity and unit roots (\(\rho = 1 + c/T\)): If the regressor follows a true unit root, Kendall's formula collapses. As shown by Campbell and Yogo (2006), the t-statistic does not converge to a Gaussian distribution, but to a non-standard Dickey-Fuller functional with severe right-skewness.
  • Long-horizon regressions: In multi-year predictive regressions (\(y_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h x_t + u_{t+h}\)), Boudoukh, Richardson, and Whitelaw (2008) show that Stambaugh bias scales linearly with the forecast horizon \(h\), causing long-horizon t-statistics to explode spuriously.
  • Multiple predictive regressors: When multiple valuation ratios (e.g., dividend yield and interest rate spread) are included simultaneously, scalar corrections fail. A full Vector Autoregression (VAR) system is required to estimate the matrix of innovation covariances.

Misconceptions

  1. Using Newey–West HAC standard errors eliminates Stambaugh bias.

    Newey–West HAC standard errors adjust the estimated variance of \(\hat\beta\) for serial correlation, but they do not alter the point estimate \(\hat\beta\) itself. The expectation \(\E[\hat\beta] \ne \beta\) remains biased upward, leaving t-statistics permanently centered around a positive non-zero value.

  2. Stambaugh bias is negligible in modern financial datasets with 10 years of data.

    Because monthly valuation ratios exhibit extreme persistence (\(\rho \ge 0.95\)), the factor \((1 + 3\rho)/T\) remains significant even for \(T = 120\) months (10 years) or \(T = 240\) months (20 years). As shown in Lab 1, \(T = 120\) still produces a false positive rate exceeding 13% at the nominal 5% level.

  3. Stambaugh bias always biases the regression slope upward.

    The direction of Stambaugh bias is governed by the sign of \(\sigma_{uv}\). While negative in stock returns regressed on valuation ratios (\(\sigma_{uv} < 0 \implies \text{bias} > 0\)), in macroeconomic settings where regressor shocks correlate positively with dependent variable innovations (\(\sigma_{uv} > 0\)), Stambaugh bias is strictly negative.

Practice problems

Mechanics

Calculate Kendall's small-sample bias for an AR(1) process with persistence \(\rho = 0.96\) estimated over \(T = 150\) monthly observations.

Solution

Kendall's bias formula is \(\E[\hat\rho - \rho] \approx -\frac{1 + 3\rho}{T}\). For \(\rho = 0.96\) and \(T = 150\): \(\E[\hat\rho - \rho] \approx -\frac{1 + 3(0.96)}{150} = -\frac{1 + 2.88}{150} = -\frac{3.88}{150} \approx -0.02587\). The AR(1) slope is biased downward by approximately 0.026.

Using the AR(1) bias from Problem 12.1 (\(-0.02587\)), calculate the expected Stambaugh bias \(\E[\hat\beta - \beta]\) if the innovation covariance is \(\sigma_{uv} = -0.75\) and regressor innovation variance is \(\sigma_v^2 = 1.0\).

Solution

By Theorem 12.1, \(\E[\hat\beta - \beta] = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2} \E[\hat\rho - \rho] = \frac{-0.75}{1.0} \times (-0.02587) = +0.01940\). The predictive slope is expected to be biased upward by approximately +0.0194.

An empirical researcher estimates \(\hat\beta = 0.025\) with standard error \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta) = 0.010\) in a sample with estimated Stambaugh bias of \(+0.019\). Compute the bias-adjusted point estimate and t-statistic.

Solution

The bias-adjusted slope is \(\hat\beta_{\text{adj}} = \hat\beta - \text{Bias} = 0.025 - 0.019 = 0.006\). The adjusted t-statistic is \(t_{\text{adj}} = \frac{0.006}{0.010} = 0.60\). While the raw estimate appeared statistically significant (\(t = 2.50\)), the bias-adjusted estimate is completely insignificant.

Proof

Prove that when the predictive regressor is strictly exogenous (\(\sigma_{uv} = 0\)), Stambaugh bias is identically zero regardless of regressor persistence \(\rho\).

Solution

By Theorem 12.1, \(\E[\hat\beta - \beta | X] = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2} \E[\hat\rho - \rho | X]\). If \(\sigma_{uv} = 0\), the coefficient \(\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2} = 0\). Thus \(\E[\hat\beta - \beta | X] = 0 \cdot \E[\hat\rho - \rho | X] = 0\). The OLS estimator is strictly unbiased even if \(\rho \approx 1\).

Show that the Amihud–Hurvich (2004) augmented regression \(y_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta x_t + \phi \hat v_{t+1} + \eta_{t+1}\) yields an unbiased estimator of \(\beta\).

Solution

Decomposing \(u_{t+1} = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2} v_{t+1} + \eta_{t+1}\) with \(\Cov(v_{t+1}, \eta_{t+1}) = 0\), including the estimated innovation \(\hat v_{t+1} = x_{t+1} - \hat\theta - \hat\rho x_t\) directly in the regression causes the parameter on \(\hat v_{t+1}\) to absorb the projection coefficient \(\phi = \sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2\). The remaining error \(\eta_{t+1}\) is strictly orthogonal to both \(x_t\) and \(\hat v_{t+1}\), purging the regressor \(x_t\) of endogeneity and restoring unbiasedness.

Prove that in long-horizon regressions \(y_{t+h} = \sum_{j=1}^h r_{t+j} = \alpha_h + \beta_h x_t + u_{t+h}\), the innovation covariance \(\Cov(u_{t+h}, v_{t+1})\) accumulates across horizons.

Solution

Under the null hypothesis of no predictability, \(r_{t+j} = \mu + u_{t+j}\). The multi-period return shock is \(u_{t+h} = \sum_{j=1}^h u_{t+j}\). The covariance with the immediate predictor innovation \(v_{t+1}\) is \(\Cov(u_{t+h}, v_{t+1}) = \sum_{j=1}^h \Cov(u_{t+j}, v_{t+1})\). By contemporaneous correlation, \(\Cov(u_{t+1}, v_{t+1}) = \sigma_{uv}\). Furthermore, for \(j \ge 2\), future returns correlate with \(v_{t+1}\) through the persistent regressor path. This causes the composite covariance and resulting Stambaugh bias to scale approximately linearly with horizon \(h\).

Empirical

Campbell and Shiller (1988) and Fama and French (1988) found that dividend yield predicts stock returns with \(R^2\) rising from 2% at monthly horizons to over 20% at 5-year horizons. How does Stambaugh bias qualify these findings?

Solution

Dividend yield has monthly persistence \(\rho \approx 0.98\) and innovation correlation with market returns of \(\Corr(u, v) \approx -0.85\). Stambaugh (1999) and Boudoukh et al. (2008) showed that after correcting for Stambaugh bias and overlapping return mechanics, the statistical significance of dividend yield predictability collapses, and the high long-horizon \(R^2\) values are largely an artifact of accumulated small-sample bias.

Welch and Goyal (2008) conduct an extensive out-of-sample audit of market predictive regressions. How do their empirical findings relate to the Stambaugh bias in-sample phenomenon?

Solution

Welch and Goyal show that virtually all in-sample return predictors fail to beat a simple historical prevailing mean benchmark out-of-sample. In-sample predictive slopes \(\hat\beta\) were inflated by Stambaugh bias and data mining. When applied out-of-sample, the spurious in-sample coefficients generated poor forecasts, resulting in negative out-of-sample \(R^2\) metrics across nearly all macroeconomic variables.

Why does the short-term Treasury bill yield or interest rate spread exhibit much less Stambaugh bias when used as a stock return predictor than the dividend-price ratio?

Solution

While T-bill yields are persistent (\(\rho \approx 0.95\)), interest rate innovations are set by central banks and money markets and have very low contemporaneous correlation with unexpected stock market returns (\(\sigma_{uv} \approx 0\)). Because \(\sigma_{uv} \approx 0\), the Stambaugh bias multiplier \(\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\) is nearly zero, leaving T-bill predictive regressions virtually unbiased.

Research

Explain the Campbell and Yogo (2006) Q-test methodology and why standard t-tests over-reject when the local-to-unity parameter \(c = T(\rho - 1)\) is negative.

Solution

Campbell and Yogo model \(\rho = 1 + c/T\). When \(c < 0\), the asymptotic distribution of the t-statistic depends on the nuisance parameter \(c\), which cannot be consistently estimated. Standard t-tests assume a standard Gaussian limit, ignoring the heavy right skewness induced by \(c\) and \(\delta = \sigma_{uv}/(\sigma_u \sigma_v)\). The Q-test inverts a Dickey–Fuller confidence interval for \(c\) to construct a Bonferroni-corrected test that guarantees exact asymptotic size.

Describe how a parametric bootstrap can be constructed under the sharp null hypothesis of no predictability to obtain exact finite-sample p-values.

Solution

Under \(H_0: \beta = 0\), fit an AR(1) model to \(x_t\) and estimate the joint empirical innovation covariance \(\hat\Sigma\). In each bootstrap replication, draw bivariate Gaussian or resampled residuals with covariance \(\hat\Sigma\), generate synthetic series \(x_t^*\) and \(y_{t+1}^* = \bar y + u_{t+1}^*\) (enforcing zero predictability), run OLS, and record \(\hat\beta^*\). The empirical p-value is the fraction of bootstrap replications where \(\hat\beta^* \ge \hat\beta_{\text{obs}}\).

In tactical asset allocation, what happens to out-of-sample portfolio Sharpe ratios when an unadjusted OLS predictive model is used to dynamically adjust equity weights?

Solution

Because unadjusted OLS overestimates \(\hat\beta\), the asset allocation model aggressively over-weights equities when valuation ratios are above average and aggressively short-sells or under-weights equities when valuation ratios drop. Due to estimation noise and spurious predictability, this creates excessive portfolio turnover, high transaction costs, and substantial underperformance relative to a passive buy-and-hold index.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What economic mechanism causes the innovation correlation \(\sigma_{uv}\) to be strongly negative in stock return predictive regressions?

    Options

    Since valuation ratios like \(D/P\) have price in the denominator, unexpected price increases reduce the ratio, generating \(\sigma_{uv} < 0\) (Section The predictive regression framework).

  2. What is the sign of the Stambaugh bias \(\E[\hat\beta - \beta]\) in predictive regressions with dividend yields?

    Options

    The product of negative innovation correlation (\(\sigma_{uv} < 0\)) and negative AR(1) estimation bias (\(\E[\hat\rho - \rho] < 0\)) is strictly positive (Theorem 12.1).

  3. Using Kendall's formula \(-(1 + 3\rho)/T\), calculate the small-sample bias in \(\hat\rho\) for \(\rho = 0.90\) and \(T = 120\).

    \(-(1 + 3 \times 0.90) / 120 = -(1 + 2.70) / 120 = -3.70 / 120 \approx -0.0308\) or -0.03 (Theorem 12.1 and Problem M1).

  4. Does using Newey–West HAC standard errors eliminate Stambaugh bias in predictive regressions?

    Options

    Newey–West standard errors modify \(\Var(\hat\beta)\) for serial correlation, but do not change the expectation \(\E[\hat\beta]\) (Misconception 1).

  5. How does Amihud and Hurvich (2004) correct for Stambaugh bias in regression estimation?

    Options

    Augmenting the regression with \(\hat v_{t+1}\) absorbs the projection coefficient \(\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2\), rendering the slope on \(x_t\) unbiased (Problem P2).

  6. What happens to Stambaugh bias as the forecast horizon \(h\) increases in multi-period predictive regressions (\(y_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h x_t + u_{t+h}\))?

    Options

    Boudoukh et al. (2008) show that multi-period return accumulation compounds Stambaugh bias across horizons (Section Where the canon breaks).

  7. Why does predicting stock returns using short-term Treasury bill yields exhibit virtually zero Stambaugh bias?

    Options

    When \(\sigma_{uv} = 0\), the Stambaugh multiplier \(\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2 = 0\), eliminating bias (Problem E3).

  8. What did Welch and Goyal (2008) conclude about the out-of-sample predictive power of valuation ratios?

    Options

    Welch and Goyal (2008) demonstrated that apparent in-sample predictability was largely spurious and failed out-of-sample (Problem E2).
