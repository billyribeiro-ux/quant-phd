Partialling out: the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem

Multivariate regressions often control for nuisance factors such as industry fixed effects, macroeconomic trends, or market momentum. The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem proves that the coefficient on any variable of interest equals the slope from a bivariate regression of residualized outcomes on residualized regressors. This algebraic equivalence underpins fixed effects estimation, visual added-variable diagnostics, and the economic interpretation of "holding other variables constant".

Partitioned regression and the annihilator operator

Consider the linear regression model with the design matrix partitioned into two blocks of regressors:

\[ y = X_1\beta_1 + X_2\beta_2 + \varepsilon \]

where \(X_1 \in \R^{n \times k_1}\), \(X_2 \in \R^{n \times k_2}\), and \(k = k_1 + k_2\). In financial applications, \(X_1\) typically contains baseline controls or fixed effect indicators, while \(X_2\) represents the primary policy variable, signal, or characteristic whose incremental impact is under investigation.

Assumption 2.1 (Partitioned Full Column Rank)

The joint \(n \times (k_1 + k_2)\) design matrix \(X = [X_1 \;\; X_2]\) has full column rank, \(\mathrm{rank}([X_1 \;\; X_2]) = k_1 + k_2\), with \(n > k_1 + k_2\). Consequently, neither \(X_1\) nor \(X_2\) is rank deficient, and no linear combination of columns of \(X_1\) can replicate any column of \(X_2\).

From Lesson 1, the ordinary least squares estimator of the joint parameter vector \(\hat\beta = (\hat\beta_1', \hat\beta_2')'\) solves the joint normal equations:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} X_1'X_1 & X_1'X_2 \\ X_2'X_1 & X_2'X_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \hat\beta_1 \\ \hat\beta_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} X_1'y \\ X_2'y \end{bmatrix} \]

Recall from orthogonal projection theory that the annihilator matrix associated with \(X_1\) is defined as:

\[ M_1 = I_n - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1' \]

By Theorem 1.1, \(M_1\) is symmetric (\(M_1' = M_1\)) and idempotent (\(M_1^2 = M_1\)). Crucially, \(M_1 X_1 = X_1 - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_1 = X_1 - X_1 = 0\), meaning \(M_1\) projects any vector onto the orthogonal complement of the column space of \(X_1\).

The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem

The fundamental insight of partialling out is that premultiplying the entire regression system by \(M_1\) purges all linear dependence on \(X_1\).

Theorem 2.1 (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL) theorem)

Under Assumption 2.1, the OLS estimator \(\hat\beta_2\) from the full multivariate regression of \(y\) on \([X_1 \;\; X_2]\) satisfies the following exact algebraic identities:

  1. Residual-on-residual regression: \(\hat\beta_2\) is identical to the OLS slope vector obtained by regressing the residual vector \(\tilde y = M_1 y\) on the residualized regressor matrix \(\tilde X_2 = M_1 X_2\): \[ \hat\beta_2 = (X_2' M_1 X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1 y = (\tilde X_2' \tilde X_2)^{-1} \tilde X_2' \tilde y \]
  2. Raw outcome on residualized regressor: \(\hat\beta_2\) is identical to the OLS slope vector obtained by regressing the raw response vector \(y\) on \(\tilde X_2\): \[ \hat\beta_2 = (\tilde X_2' \tilde X_2)^{-1} \tilde X_2' y \]
  3. Identical residuals: The residual vector \(e\) from the full regression of \(y\) on \([X_1 \;\; X_2]\) equals the residual vector from regressing \(\tilde y\) on \(\tilde X_2\): \[ e = \tilde y - \tilde X_2 \hat\beta_2 \]

Proof of Theorem 2.1

The partitioned normal equations expand into two coupled matrix equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} (1)\quad X_1'X_1 \hat\beta_1 + X_1'X_2 \hat\beta_2 &= X_1'y \\ (2)\quad X_2'X_1 \hat\beta_1 + X_2'X_2 \hat\beta_2 &= X_2'y \end{aligned} \]

Because \(X_1\) has full column rank, \(X_1'X_1\) is invertible. Solving equation (1) for \(\hat\beta_1\) yields:

\[ \hat\beta_1 = (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'y - (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2 \hat\beta_2 \]

Substitute this expression for \(\hat\beta_1\) directly into equation (2):

\[ X_2'X_1 \left[ (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'y - (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2 \hat\beta_2 \right] + X_2'X_2 \hat\beta_2 = X_2'y \]

Rearranging terms by grouping the coefficients on \(\hat\beta_2\) and collecting terms involving \(y\):

\[ \left[ X_2'X_2 - X_2'X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'X_2 \right] \hat\beta_2 = X_2'y - X_2'X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'y \]

Factoring \(X_2'\) on the left and factoring \(X_2\) and \(y\) on the right:

\[ X_2' \left[ I_n - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1' \right] X_2 \hat\beta_2 = X_2' \left[ I_n - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1' \right] y \]

Recognizing the annihilator matrix \(M_1 = I_n - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\), this simplifies directly to:

\[ (X_2' M_1 X_2) \hat\beta_2 = X_2' M_1 y \]

Because \([X_1 \;\; X_2]\) has full column rank, \(M_1 X_2\) has full column rank \(k_2\), ensuring that \(X_2' M_1 X_2 = (M_1 X_2)'(M_1 X_2) = \tilde X_2' \tilde X_2\) is non-singular. Inverting yields Part 1:

\[ \hat\beta_2 = (X_2' M_1 X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1 y \]

For Part 2, since \(M_1\) is idempotent, \(M_1 y = M_1^2 y = M_1 \tilde y\). Therefore, \(X_2' M_1 y = X_2' M_1 M_1 y = (M_1 X_2)'(M_1 y) = \tilde X_2' \tilde y = \tilde X_2' y\), confirming Part 2.

For Part 3, the full regression residual is \(e = y - X_1\hat\beta_1 - X_2\hat\beta_2\). Substituting the solution for \(\hat\beta_1\):

\[ e = y - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'(y - X_2\hat\beta_2) - X_2\hat\beta_2 = M_1(y - X_2\hat\beta_2) = M_1 y - M_1 X_2\hat\beta_2 = \tilde y - \tilde X_2\hat\beta_2 \]

This completes the proof.

Lab 1 · Exact Frisch–Waugh–Lovell partialling out identity

Hypothesis: For a multivariate regression with an intercept and two correlated regressors, the coefficient on the second regressor \(\hat\beta_2\) obtained from the full 3x3 normal equations equals the slope from regressing the residualized outcome \(\tilde y\) on the residualized regressor \(\tilde x_2\) in exact rational arithmetic, with difference identically zero.

"""Lab S2-L02-1: Exact Frisch-Waugh-Lovell partialling out identity.

Evaluates the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem using exact rational arithmetic
(fractions.Fraction). Verifies that the partialled-out slope equals the
multivariate regression coefficient identically. Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def solve_3x3(mat: list[list[Fraction]], rhs: list[Fraction]) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Solve 3x3 linear system A * b = rhs using Gaussian elimination in exact rational arithmetic."""
    a = [[Fraction(val.numerator, val.denominator) for val in row] for row in mat]
    b = [Fraction(val.numerator, val.denominator) for val in rhs]

    # Forward elimination with partial pivoting
    for i in range(3):
        # Pivot selection
        pivot = i
        for r in range(i + 1, 3):
            if abs(a[r][i]) > abs(a[pivot][i]):
                pivot = r
        if a[pivot][i] == Fraction(0):
            raise ValueError("Singular matrix")
        a[i], a[pivot] = a[pivot], a[i]
        b[i], b[pivot] = b[pivot], b[i]

        for r in range(i + 1, 3):
            factor = a[r][i] / a[i][i]
            for c in range(i, 3):
                a[r][c] -= factor * a[i][c]
            b[r] -= factor * b[i]

    # Back substitution
    ans = [Fraction(0), Fraction(0), Fraction(0)]
    for i in range(2, -1, -1):
        s = sum((a[i][j] * ans[j] for j in range(i + 1, 3)), Fraction(0))
        ans[i] = (b[i] - s) / a[i][i]

    return ans[0], ans[1], ans[2]


def solve_2x2(
    a: Fraction, b: Fraction, c: Fraction, d: Fraction, y1: Fraction, y2: Fraction
) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Solve 2x2 linear system in exact rational arithmetic."""
    det = a * d - b * c
    if det == 0:
        raise ValueError("Singular matrix")
    return (d * y1 - b * y2) / det, (-c * y1 + a * y2) / det


def main() -> None:
    # Seeded integer design: n = 6 observations, k = 3 (intercept 1, regressor x1, regressor x2)
    # y = response
    x1 = [Fraction(1), Fraction(2), Fraction(3), Fraction(4), Fraction(5), Fraction(6)]
    x2 = [Fraction(2), Fraction(1), Fraction(4), Fraction(3), Fraction(7), Fraction(5)]
    y = [Fraction(3), Fraction(5), Fraction(8), Fraction(7), Fraction(13), Fraction(11)]
    n = Fraction(len(y))

    # --- Step 1: Full multivariate OLS regression: y = b0 + b1*x1 + b2*x2 ---
    # Construct normal equations (X'X) b = X'y
    s_x1 = sum(x1, Fraction(0))
    s_x2 = sum(x2, Fraction(0))
    s_y = sum(y, Fraction(0))

    s_x1x1 = sum((xi * xi for xi in x1), Fraction(0))
    s_x2x2 = sum((xi * xi for xi in x2), Fraction(0))
    s_x1x2 = sum((xi * xj for xi, xj in zip(x1, x2, strict=True)), Fraction(0))

    s_x1y = sum((xi * yi for xi, yi in zip(x1, y, strict=True)), Fraction(0))
    s_x2y = sum((xi * yi for xi, yi in zip(x2, y, strict=True)), Fraction(0))

    gram = [
        [n, s_x1, s_x2],
        [s_x1, s_x1x1, s_x1x2],
        [s_x2, s_x1x2, s_x2x2],
    ]
    rhs = [s_y, s_x1y, s_x2y]

    b0, b1, b2 = solve_3x3(gram, rhs)

    # --- Step 2: Partialling out via Frisch-Waugh-Lovell ---
    # Auxiliary regression A: Regress y on [1, x1] -> residuals y_tilde = M1 * y
    gamma_y0, gamma_y1 = solve_2x2(n, s_x1, s_x1, s_x1x1, s_y, s_x1y)
    y_tilde = [yi - (gamma_y0 + gamma_y1 * xi) for xi, yi in zip(x1, y, strict=True)]

    # Auxiliary regression B: Regress x2 on [1, x1] -> residuals x2_tilde = M1 * x2
    gamma_x0, gamma_x1 = solve_2x2(n, s_x1, s_x1, s_x1x1, s_x2, s_x1x2)
    x2_tilde = [xi2 - (gamma_x0 + gamma_x1 * xi1) for xi1, xi2 in zip(x1, x2, strict=True)]

    # Bivariate regression of y_tilde on x2_tilde without intercept (since M1 is mean-zero projection)
    num = sum((xi * yi for xi, yi in zip(x2_tilde, y_tilde, strict=True)), Fraction(0))
    den = sum((xi * xi for xi in x2_tilde), Fraction(0))
    b2_partial = num / den

    # Also regress raw y on x2_tilde
    num_raw = sum((xi * yi for xi, yi in zip(x2_tilde, y, strict=True)), Fraction(0))
    b2_raw = num_raw / den

    # Exact difference check
    diff = b2 - b2_partial

    print(f"Full regression b0: {b0} ({float(b0):.6f}), b1: {b1} ({float(b1):.6f})")
    print(f"Full regression b2: {b2} ({float(b2):.6f})")
    print(f"Partialled-out residual regression b2: {b2_partial} ({float(b2_partial):.6f})")
    print(f"Raw y on x2_tilde regression: {b2_raw} ({float(b2_raw):.6f})")
    print(f"Exact difference (b2 - b2_partial): {diff}")

Interpretation: The difference between the joint OLS coefficient and the residual-on-residual slope is exactly zero. The FWL theorem is a mathematical identity that holds sample by sample, demonstrating that multivariate regression coefficients isolate variation unique to each regressor.

Where the canon breaks

The FWL theorem guarantees identical point estimates \(\hat\beta_2\) and identical residual vectors \(e\). However, the naive standard error reported by standard software when estimating the bivariate regression of \(\tilde y\) on \(\tilde X_2\) is incorrectly small. The univariate regression divides the sum of squared residuals by \(n - k_2\) degrees of freedom, failing to account for the \(k_1\) degrees of freedom consumed during the initial projection by \(M_1\). The true degrees of freedom in the full model is \(n - k_1 - k_2\). In panel datasets with hundreds of fixed-effect dummy variables (\(k_1 \gg 1\)), this degree-of-freedom discrepancy creates severe downward bias in naive standard errors unless explicitly corrected.

Misconceptions

  1. Partialling out controls alters the effective sample or changes the estimated coefficient on \(X_2\).

    The FWL theorem proves that \(\hat\beta_2\) from the partialled-out regression is algebraically identical to the multivariate OLS coefficient. No observations are lost, and the numerical value is unchanged down to machine precision.

  2. To partial out controls \(X_1\), one only needs to regress \(y\) on \(X_1\) and regress the resulting residuals on raw \(X_2\).

    Regressing \(\tilde y = M_1 y\) on raw \(X_2\) yields \((\tilde X_2' X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1 y\), which generally does not equal \(\hat\beta_2\) unless \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are mutually orthogonal. Both the dependent variable and the independent variable must be residualized, or raw \(y\) must be regressed on residualized \(\tilde X_2\).

  3. Standard errors from the second-stage residual regression match the full multivariate OLS standard errors automatically.

    Second-stage software uses \(n - k_2\) degrees of freedom instead of \(n - k_1 - k_2\). The estimated residual variance \(\hat\sigma^2\) is underestimated by the factor \((n - k_1 - k_2)/(n - k_2)\), requiring degrees-of-freedom rescaling for valid inference.

Practice problems

Mechanics

Let \(y = (4, 6, 8)'\), \(x_1 = (1, 1, 1)'\), and \(x_2 = (1, 2, 3)'\). Compute the residualized vectors \(\tilde y = M_1 y\) and \(\tilde x_2 = M_1 x_2\) with respect to the constant vector \(x_1\).

Solution

The projection operator onto the constant vector \(x_1 = \mathbf{1}\) computes the sample mean: \(H_1 y = \bar y \mathbf{1}\). Here \(\bar y = (4 + 6 + 8)/3 = 6\), so \(\tilde y = y - 6 \cdot \mathbf{1} = (-2, 0, 2)'\). For \(x_2\), \(\bar x_2 = (1 + 2 + 3)/3 = 2\), so \(\tilde x_2 = x_2 - 2 \cdot \mathbf{1} = (-1, 0, 1)'\). Both residual vectors are demeaned versions of the original vectors, having zero sample mean.

Using the demeaned vectors from Problem M1, compute the bivariate slope of \(\tilde y\) on \(\tilde x_2\). Compare it to the regression of raw \(y\) on \(\tilde x_2\).

Solution

The inner product \(\tilde x_2' \tilde y = (-1)(-2) + (0)(0) + (1)(2) = 2 + 0 + 2 = 4\). The sum of squares \(\tilde x_2' \tilde x_2 = (-1)^2 + 0^2 + 1^2 = 2\). The slope is \(\hat\beta_2 = 4 / 2 = 2.0\). For the regression of raw \(y\) on \(\tilde x_2\): \(\tilde x_2' y = (-1)(4) + (0)(6) + (1)(8) = -4 + 8 = 4\). The denominator remains \(\tilde x_2' \tilde x_2 = 2\), yielding \(\hat\beta_2 = 4 / 2 = 2.0\). Both formulas yield identical slopes, verifying the FWL identity.

In a panel regression with \(n = 1000\) observations, a researcher partials out \(k_1 = 200\) fixed effect indicators and runs a second-stage regression on \(k_2 = 5\) continuous regressors. If the second-stage software reports a residual variance estimate of \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{naive}} = 1.60\), what is the corrected unbiased residual variance estimate?

Solution

The naive estimate divides the residual sum of squares by \(n - k_2 = 1000 - 5 = 995\): \(\mathrm{RSS} = 1.60 \times 995 = 1592\). The true degrees of freedom in the full model is \(n - k_1 - k_2 = 1000 - 200 - 5 = 795\). The corrected unbiased residual variance is \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{corrected}} = \mathrm{RSS} / 795 = 1592 / 795 \approx 2.0025\). The naive variance was understated by over 20%.

Proof

Prove that \(M_1 X_2\) is orthogonal to \(X_1\), and deduce that the columns of \(M_1 X_2\) lie in \(\mathrm{col}(X_1)^\perp\).

Solution

Compute the inner product of \(X_1\) with \(M_1 X_2\): \(X_1' (M_1 X_2) = (X_1' M_1) X_2\). From the definition of \(M_1 = I_n - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\), we have \(X_1' M_1 = X_1' (I_n - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1') = X_1' - (X_1'X_1)(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1' = X_1' - X_1' = 0\). Thus \(X_1' M_1 X_2 = 0 \cdot X_2 = 0\). Because every column of \(M_1 X_2\) has zero inner product with every column of \(X_1\), all columns of \(M_1 X_2\) belong to the orthogonal complement \(\mathrm{col}(X_1)^\perp\).

Prove that the full projection matrix \(H = X(X'X)^{-1}X'\) can be decomposed as \(H = H_1 + M_1 X_2 (X_2' M_1 X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1\), where \(H_1 = X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'\).

Solution

Any fitted vector can be written \(\hat y = X_1\hat\beta_1 + X_2\hat\beta_2\). Substituting \(\hat\beta_1 = (X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1'(y - X_2\hat\beta_2)\) gives \(\hat y = X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1' y - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1' X_2\hat\beta_2 + X_2\hat\beta_2 = H_1 y + (I - H_1)X_2\hat\beta_2 = H_1 y + M_1 X_2\hat\beta_2\). By the FWL theorem, \(\hat\beta_2 = (X_2' M_1 X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1 y\). Substituting this into \(\hat y\) yields \(\hat y = \left[ H_1 + M_1 X_2 (X_2' M_1 X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1 \right] y\). Since \(\hat y = Hy\) holds for all \(y \in \R^n\), the projection operator decomposes into two mutually orthogonal projection operators: \(H = H_1 + P_{\tilde X_2}\).

Show that if \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are mutually orthogonal (\(X_1' X_2 = 0\)), the multivariate estimator \(\hat\beta_2\) equals the simple regression estimator of \(y\) on \(X_2\).

Solution

If \(X_1' X_2 = 0\), then \(M_1 X_2 = (I - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1') X_2 = X_2 - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}(X_1'X_2) = X_2 - 0 = X_2\). The FWL formula for \(\hat\beta_2\) becomes \(\hat\beta_2 = (X_2' M_1 X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1 y = (X_2' X_2)^{-1} X_2' M_1 y\). Since \(M_1 = I - H_1\), \(X_2' M_1 y = X_2'(I - H_1)y = X_2' y - (X_2' X_1)(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1' y = X_2' y - 0 = X_2' y\). Therefore, \(\hat\beta_2 = (X_2' X_2)^{-1} X_2' y\), which is exactly the simple univariate regression slope of \(y\) on \(X_2\).

Empirical

Explain how the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem enables two-way fixed effects (firm and year) estimation via demeaning without creating thousands of explicit indicator dummy columns.

Solution

By the FWL theorem, regressing returns on firm and year indicator matrices \(X_1\) alongside economic signals \(X_2\) is mathematically equivalent to regressing within-firm and within-year demeaned returns \(\tilde y\) on demeaned signals \(\tilde X_2\). Demeaning subtracts firm-specific means and time-specific means directly, eliminating the need to store or invert an \((N + T) \times (N + T)\) dummy matrix.

What is an added-variable plot (partial regression plot), and how does the FWL theorem guarantee its slope matches the multivariate regression coefficient?

Solution

An added-variable plot graphs the residuals \(\tilde y = M_1 y\) on the vertical axis against the residuals \(\tilde x_j = M_1 x_j\) on the horizontal axis, where \(X_1\) contains all regressors except \(x_j\). By the FWL theorem, the bivariate OLS slope through these scatter points equals the multivariate coefficient \(\hat\beta_j\) exactly, allowing visual inspection of leverage points and non-linearities controlling for other factors.

In estimating momentum factor returns controlling for size and value, a hedge fund researcher observes that partialling out size and value dramatically increases the variance of the momentum regressor. What does this indicate?

Solution

The variance of the residualized regressor is \(\tilde x_2' \tilde x_2 = x_2' M_1 x_2 = x_2' x_2 (1 - R_{2.1}^2)\), where \(R_{2.1}^2\) is the \(R^2\) from regressing momentum on size and value. If momentum is collinear with size and value, \(\tilde x_2' \tilde x_2\) becomes very small, which inflates the variance of \(\hat\beta_2\) via \(\Var(\hat\beta_2) = \sigma^2 (\tilde X_2' \tilde X_2)^{-1}\). The finding indicates multicollinearity between the style factors.

Research

In modern machine learning applications, high-dimensional controls (such as text embeddings or large factor sets) are frequently partialled out via Double/Debiased Machine Learning (Chernozhukov et al.). How does this generalize the FWL logic?

Solution

The FWL theorem establishes that OLS partialling out orthogonalizes the outcome and regressor of interest with respect to linear projections of \(X_1\). Double Machine Learning replaces the linear projection matrix \(H_1\) with non-linear machine learning estimators (random forests, neural networks, or lasso) to estimate \(\E[y|X_1]\) and \(\E[X_2|X_1]\), forming orthogonal Neyman scores that ensure \(\sqrt{n}\)-consistent inference on \(\beta_2\) despite regularization bias.

Explain why the FWL theorem does not apply directly to non-linear models such as logistic regression or Probit without modification.

Solution

The FWL theorem relies on the linearity and idempotence of projection operators in Hilbert space (\(M_1^2 = M_1\)). Non-linear models like Logit maximize a non-quadratic log-likelihood where conditional expectations are non-linear link functions \(G(X_1\beta_1 + X_2\beta_2)\). Regressing residualized Logit pseudo-outcomes on residualized regressors does not recover the maximum likelihood parameter vector because the link function couples the parameters non-linearly across the likelihood Hessian.

Evaluate the implications of the FWL theorem for collinearity diagnostics: how does the tolerance statistic \(\mathrm{Tol}_j = 1 - R_j^2\) relate to the length of \(\tilde X_j\)?

Solution

When regressing \(x_j\) on all other regressors \(X_{-j}\), the residual vector is \(\tilde x_j = M_{-j} x_j\). The sum of squared residuals is \(\|\tilde x_j\|^2 = \|x_j\|^2 (1 - R_j^2)\). The tolerance statistic is exactly the ratio \(\|\tilde x_j\|^2 / \|x_j\|^2\). If tolerance approaches zero, \(\|\tilde x_j\| \to 0\), meaning \(x_j\) has almost no variation independent of the other controls, driving the variance inflation factor \(\mathrm{VIF}_j = 1/\mathrm{Tol}_j\) to infinity.

    \(M_1 X_1 = (I - X_1(X_1'X_1)^{-1}X_1')X_1 = X_1 - X_1 = 0\) (Theorem 2.1).

  3. If \(X_1\) contains only the constant intercept vector \(\mathbf{1}\), what operation does \(M_1\) perform on any vector \(z\)?

    Options

    \(M_1 z = z - \mathbf{1}(\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{1})^{-1}\mathbf{1}'z = z - \bar z \mathbf{1}\), which demeans the vector (Problem M1).

  4. What is the algebraic difference between the OLS estimate \(\hat\beta_2\) from the full regression and the slope from regressing \(M_1 y\) on \(M_1 X_2\)? Enter as a number.

    By the FWL theorem, the difference is identically 0.0 in exact arithmetic (Theorem 2.1 and Lab 1).

  5. Can the full regression slope \(\hat\beta_2\) be obtained by regressing raw \(y\) on the residualized regressor \(\tilde X_2 = M_1 X_2\)?

    Options

    Because \(M_1\) is idempotent, \(\tilde X_2' y = (M_1 X_2)' y = X_2' M_1 y = \tilde X_2' \tilde y\) (Theorem 2.1, Part 2).

  6. In a partitioned regression with \(n = 100\), \(k_1 = 30\) fixed effects, and \(k_2 = 10\) policy regressors, what is the degrees-of-freedom divisor consumed by the policy regressors alone in the second stage (\(k_2\))?

    \(k_2 = 10\). Naive second-stage software uses \(n - k_2 = 90\) instead of \(n - k_1 - k_2 = 60\) (Problem M3).

  7. What is the true degrees of freedom in the full model for the previous problem (\(n - k_1 - k_2\)) with \(n=100, k_1=30, k_2=10\)?

    \(n - k_1 - k_2 = 100 - 30 - 10 = 60\) (Section Canon breaks).

  8. Under what condition on the regressors does the multivariate coefficient \(\hat\beta_2\) equal the univariate slope from regressing \(y\) on raw \(X_2\)?

    Options

    When \(X_1' X_2 = 0\), \(M_1 X_2 = X_2\), collapsing the FWL formula to univariate regression (Problem P3).
