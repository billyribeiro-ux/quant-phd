Fit and the coefficient of determination

The coefficient of determination \(R^2\) measures the proportion of total sample variation in an asset or portfolio return explained by linear factors. While widely quoted as a summary of goodness-of-fit, \(R^2\) is neither a test of causal identification nor a guarantee of parameter unbiasedness. In financial time series, low \(R^2\) values are standard due to market efficiency, whereas high in-sample \(R^2\) frequently signals overfitting or lookahead bias.

The sum of squares decomposition

Let \(y \in \R^n\) be an observed response vector with sample mean \(\bar y = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n y_i\). The total sample variation about the mean is measured by the total sum of squares (TSS):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{TSS} &= \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)^2 \\ &= (y - \bar y \mathbf{1})'(y - \bar y \mathbf{1}) = y' M_0 y \end{aligned} \]

where \(M_0 = I_n - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}'\) is the centering matrix that subtracts the sample mean. Consider the ordinary least squares estimator \(\hat\beta\) from the linear model \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\), yielding fitted values \(\hat y = X\hat\beta\) and residuals \(e = y - \hat y\).

Assumption 3.1 (Intercept Inclusion)

The design matrix \(X \in \R^{n \times k}\) contains an explicit constant column \(\mathbf{1} = (1, 1, \dots, 1)'\), or the columns of \(X\) linearly span the constant vector. Consequently, the sample mean of the residuals is identically zero: \(\bar e = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0\), ensuring \(\bar{\hat y} = \bar y\).

Under Assumption 3.1, the explained sum of squares (ESS) and residual sum of squares (RSS) are defined as:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{ESS} &= \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 \\ &= (\hat y - \bar y \mathbf{1})'(\hat y - \bar y \mathbf{1}) \\ \mathrm{RSS} &= \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = e'e \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 3.1 (Orthogonal Sum of Squares Decomposition)

Under Assumption 3.1, the total sum of squares decomposes additively into the explained sum of squares and the residual sum of squares:

\[ \mathrm{TSS} = \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS} \]

Proof of Theorem 3.1

Express the deviation of \(y_i\) from its sample mean as:

\[ y_i - \bar y = (\hat y_i - \bar y) + (y_i - \hat y_i) = (\hat y_i - \bar y) + e_i \]

Squaring both sides and summing over \(i = 1, \dots, n\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 \\ &\quad + 2\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - \bar y)e_i \end{aligned} \]

The cross-product term expands to:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - \bar y)e_i &= \sum_{i=1}^n \hat y_i e_i - \bar y \sum_{i=1}^n e_i \\ &= \hat y'e - \bar y (\mathbf{1}'e) \end{aligned} \]

By Theorem 1.2, fitted values are orthogonal to residuals, \(\hat y'e = 0\). By Assumption 3.1, the residuals sum to zero, \(\mathbf{1}'e = 0\). Therefore, the cross-product vanishes identically, leaving \(\mathrm{TSS} = \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}\).

The coefficient of determination and squared correlation

Definition 3.1 (Coefficient of determination)

In a linear regression satisfying Assumption 3.1 with \(\mathrm{TSS} > 0\), the coefficient of determination \(R^2\) is the fraction of total sample variance explained by the model:

\[ R^2 = \frac{\mathrm{ESS}}{\mathrm{TSS}} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}} \]

Theorem 3.2 (\(R^2\) as Squared Sample Correlation)

Under Assumption 3.1, \(R^2\) equals the squared sample correlation coefficient between the observed values \(y\) and the OLS fitted values \(\hat y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} R^2 &= r_{y, \hat y}^2 \\ &= \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)(\hat y_i - \bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)^2 \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2}} \right)^2 \end{aligned} \]

Proof of Theorem 3.2

Consider the sample covariance numerator between \(y\) and \(\hat y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)(\hat y_i - \bar y) &= \sum_{i=1}^n [(\hat y_i - \bar y) + e_i](\hat y_i - \bar y) \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^n e_i (\hat y_i - \bar y) \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\hat y'e = 0\) and \(\mathbf{1}'e = 0\), the cross-term vanishes, showing that \(\Cov_{\mathrm{sample}}(y, \hat y) = \mathrm{ESS}\). Substituting into the correlation definition:

\[ r_{y, \hat y} = \frac{\mathrm{ESS}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{TSS} \cdot \mathrm{ESS}}} = \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{ESS}}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{TSS}}} \]

Squaring both sides yields \(r_{y, \hat y}^2 = \mathrm{ESS}/\mathrm{TSS} = R^2\).

Theorem 3.3 (Monotonicity Under Regressor Addition)

Adding regressors to an OLS specification never decreases \(R^2\). If \(X_1 \subset X_2\) in the sense that \(\mathrm{col}(X_1) \subseteq \mathrm{col}(X_2)\), then:

\[ R^2(X_2) \ge R^2(X_1) \]

Proof of Theorem 3.3

The OLS criterion minimizes the Euclidean distance \(\mathrm{RSS} = \min_{b \in \mathrm{col}(X)} \|y - Xb\|^2\). Expanding the column space from \(\mathrm{col}(X_1)\) to \(\mathrm{col}(X_2)\) enlarges the set of feasible linear combinations. Because the candidate vector minimizing distance over the superspace can always choose zero coefficients on the additional columns, \(\mathrm{RSS}(X_2) \le \mathrm{RSS}(X_1)\). Since \(\mathrm{TSS}\) depends only on \(y\) and remains fixed, \(R^2(X_2) = 1 - \mathrm{RSS}(X_2)/\mathrm{TSS} \ge 1 - \mathrm{RSS}(X_1)/\mathrm{TSS} = R^2(X_1)\).

Remark 3.1 (Adjusted \(R^2\))

Because \(R^2\) increases mechanically whenever any regressor is added, empirical researchers use the adjusted coefficient of determination \(\bar R^2\), which penalizes parameter proliferation by dividing each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom:

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar R^2 &= 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}/(n - k)}{\mathrm{TSS}/(n - 1)} \\ &= 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{n - 1}{n - k} \end{aligned} \]

Unlike \(R^2\), \(\bar R^2\) increases upon adding a regressor if and only if the absolute \(t\)-statistic on that regressor exceeds 1 in magnitude.

Lab 1 · Exact coefficient of determination and squared correlation identity

Hypothesis: For an OLS regression with an intercept, the total sum of squares equals the exact sum of ESS and RSS in rational arithmetic, and \(1 - \mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS}\) matches the squared sample correlation \(r(y, \hat y)^2\) with zero difference.

"""Lab S2-L03-1: Exact coefficient of determination R^2 and squared correlation identity.

Evaluates TSS, ESS, RSS decomposition and verifies that R^2 = 1 - RSS/TSS
equals the squared sample correlation of y and y_hat in exact rational arithmetic.
Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def solve_2x2(
    a: Fraction, b: Fraction, c: Fraction, d: Fraction, y1: Fraction, y2: Fraction
) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Solve 2x2 linear system in exact rational arithmetic."""
    det = a * d - b * c
    if det == 0:
        raise ValueError("Singular matrix")
    return (d * y1 - b * y2) / det, (-c * y1 + a * y2) / det


def main() -> None:
    # Seeded integer design: n = 6 observations, k = 2 (intercept and regressor x)
    x = [Fraction(1), Fraction(2), Fraction(3), Fraction(4), Fraction(5), Fraction(6)]
    y = [Fraction(2), Fraction(4), Fraction(5), Fraction(4), Fraction(7), Fraction(8)]
    n = Fraction(len(x))

    # OLS estimation
    s_x = sum(x, Fraction(0))
    s_y = sum(y, Fraction(0))
    s_xx = sum((xi * xi for xi in x), Fraction(0))
    s_xy = sum((xi * yi for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True)), Fraction(0))

    b0, b1 = solve_2x2(n, s_x, s_x, s_xx, s_y, s_xy)

    # Fitted values and residuals
    y_hat = [b0 + b1 * xi for xi in x]
    e = [yi - yhat_i for yi, yhat_i in zip(y, y_hat, strict=True)]

    # Means
    y_bar = s_y / n
    yhat_bar = sum(y_hat, Fraction(0)) / n

    # Sum of squares
    tss = sum(((yi - y_bar) ** 2 for yi in y), Fraction(0))
    ess = sum(((yhat_i - y_bar) ** 2 for yhat_i in y_hat), Fraction(0))
    rss = sum((ei * ei for ei in e), Fraction(0))

    # Pythagorean check: TSS = ESS + RSS
    orthog_sum = ess + rss
    tss_diff = tss - orthog_sum

    # R-squared definition: 1 - RSS / TSS
    r2_def = Fraction(1) - (rss / tss)

    # Sample correlation r(y, y_hat):
    # Cov(y, y_hat) = sum((y - y_bar)(y_hat - yhat_bar))
    # Var(y) = sum((y - y_bar)^2) = TSS
    # Var(y_hat) = sum((y_hat - yhat_bar)^2) = ESS
    # Cov(y, y_hat) = sum(y * y_hat) - n * y_bar^2
    # In OLS with intercept, Cov(y, y_hat) = sum(y_hat^2) - n * y_bar^2 = ESS!
    cov_y_yhat = sum(((yi - y_bar) * (yhat_i - yhat_bar) for yi, yhat_i in zip(y, y_hat, strict=True)), Fraction(0))
    # Squared correlation: Cov^2 / (Var(y) * Var(y_hat)) = ESS^2 / (TSS * ESS) = ESS / TSS = r2_def
    sq_corr = (cov_y_yhat * cov_y_yhat) / (tss * ess)

    r2_diff = r2_def - sq_corr

    print(f"OLS coefficients: b0 = {b0} ({float(b0):.4f}), b1 = {b1} ({float(b1):.4f})")
    print(f"TSS: {tss} ({float(tss):.4f})")
    print(f"ESS: {ess} ({float(ess):.4f})")
    print(f"RSS: {rss} ({float(rss):.4f})")
    print(f"TSS - (ESS + RSS): {tss_diff}")
    print(f"R^2 (1 - RSS/TSS): {r2_def} ({float(r2_def):.6f})")
    print(f"Squared correlation r(y, y_hat)^2: {sq_corr} ({float(sq_corr):.6f})")
    print(f"Exact difference (R^2 - sq_corr): {r2_diff}")


main()

Interpretation: The exact algebraic identity \(\mathrm{TSS} = \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}\) and \(R^2 = r(y, \hat y)^2\) confirms that goodness-of-fit in OLS is equivalent to the squared cosine of the angle between the centered response vector and the projection space.

Where the canon breaks

\(R^2\) is widely abused in quantitative finance. A high \(R^2\) does not imply that estimated coefficients are unbiased, consistent, or causally identified; an omitted variable can simultaneously inflate \(R^2\) and severely bias parameters. In regressions estimated without an intercept, the cross-product term \(\sum \hat y_i e_i - \bar y \sum e_i \ne 0\), causing \(\mathrm{TSS} \ne \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}\); under the standard formula \(1 - \mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS}\), \(R^2\) can be strictly negative. In predictive regressions of monthly asset returns, market efficiency dictates that the true \(R^2\) is rarely above 1% to 3%; an empirical \(R^2\) of 20% in a stock return regression almost invariably signals lookahead bias or data snooping.

Misconceptions

  1. A high \(R^2\) means the econometric specification is correctly identified and unbiased.

    \(R^2\) measures in-sample linear correlation between \(y\) and \(\hat y\). It contains zero information regarding the exogeneity condition \(\E[\varepsilon|X] = 0\). Adding endogenous variables or lagged dependent variables often inflates \(R^2\) while destroying the causal validity of the estimates.

  2. A low \(R^2\) implies that a trading strategy or factor model has no economic value.

    In quantitative trading, a monthly predictive \(R^2\) of just 1% on broad market returns can generate an annualized Sharpe ratio exceeding 0.70 when combined with dynamic leverage. In efficient markets, predictable variation is necessarily small.

  3. \(R^2\) is always bounded between 0 and 1.

    While \(0 \le R^2 \le 1\) is guaranteed for models with an intercept evaluated on their in-sample training data, out-of-sample \(R^2\) or in-sample \(R^2\) without an intercept can be negative whenever the model forecasts worse than the simple sample mean \(\bar y\).

Practice problems

Mechanics

In a regression with \(n = 50\) observations and an intercept, \(\mathrm{TSS} = 200.0\) and \(\mathrm{RSS} = 50.0\). Calculate \(R^2\), \(\mathrm{ESS}\), and adjusted \(\bar R^2\) for \(k = 5\).

Solution

The explained sum of squares is \(\mathrm{ESS} = \mathrm{TSS} - \mathrm{RSS} = 200.0 - 50.0 = 150.0\). The coefficient of determination is \(R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS} = 1 - 50.0 / 200.0 = 0.75\). The adjusted \(R^2\) is \(\bar R^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{n - 1}{n - k} = 1 - (1 - 0.75)\frac{49}{45} = 1 - 0.25 \times 1.0889 = 1 - 0.2722 = 0.7278\).

A univariate regression of \(y\) on \(x\) with an intercept produces a sample correlation \(r_{x, y} = -0.60\). What is the \(R^2\) of the regression?

Solution

In a simple bivariate linear regression with an intercept, the fitted values are a linear affine transformation of \(x\): \(\hat y_i = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_i\). The correlation between \(y\) and \(\hat y\) satisfies \(r_{y, \hat y} = |r_{x, y}|\). Therefore, \(R^2 = r_{y, \hat y}^2 = r_{x, y}^2 = (-0.60)^2 = 0.36\). The model explains 36% of the variance of \(y\).

Suppose a researcher adds one additional regressor to an existing model with \(n = 101\) and \(k = 5\). The \(t\)-statistic on the new regressor is \(t = 0.85\). Does adjusted \(\bar R^2\) increase or decrease?

Solution

Adjusted \(\bar R^2\) increases upon adding a single regressor if and only if the absolute \(t\)-statistic exceeds 1.0 (or equivalently, \(F = t^2 > 1\)). Because \(|t| = 0.85 < 1.0\), the reduction in \(\mathrm{RSS}\) is insufficient to offset the loss of one degree of freedom in the denominator. Consequently, \(\bar R^2\) strictly decreases.

Proof

Prove that in an OLS regression without an intercept, the identity \(\mathrm{TSS} = \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}\) fails in general, and show why \(1 - \mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS}\) can be negative.

Solution

In the proof of Theorem 3.1, the cross-product term is \(\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)e_i = \hat y'e - \bar y \sum e_i\). While \(\hat y'e = 0\) holds by orthogonality to the fitted plane, \(\sum e_i = \mathbf{1}'e\) is zero if and only if \(\mathbf{1} \in \mathrm{col}(X)\). Without an intercept, \(\sum e_i \ne 0\), so the cross-term \(-2\bar y \sum e_i \ne 0\), violating \(\mathrm{TSS} = \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}\). Furthermore, if the fitted model predicts poorly compared to the constant \(\bar y\), \(\mathrm{RSS} = \sum (y_i - X_i b)^2 > \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2 = \mathrm{TSS}\), which directly causes \(1 - \mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS} < 0\).

Prove that \(\bar R^2 \le R^2\) whenever \(k > 1\), with equality if and only if \(R^2 = 1\).

Solution

Write \(\bar R^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{n - 1}{n - k}\). Note that for \(k > 1\), \(n - k < n - 1\), so \(\frac{n - 1}{n - k} > 1\). Therefore, \((1 - R^2)\frac{n - 1}{n - k} \ge 1 - R^2\), with strict inequality if \(1 - R^2 > 0\). Subtracting both sides from 1 yields \(\bar R^2 \le R^2\). Equality occurs if and only if \(1 - R^2 = 0\), which requires \(R^2 = 1\) (a perfect fit).

Show that the coefficient of determination can be expressed in terms of the projection matrix as \(R^2 = \frac{y' (H - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}') y}{y' (I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}') y}\).

Solution

The total sum of squares is \(\mathrm{TSS} = y' M_0 y = y' (I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}') y\). The fitted values are \(\hat y = Hy\). Because \(X\) contains an intercept, \(H\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}\), so \(\bar{\hat y} = \bar y = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}'y\). The explained sum of squares is \(\mathrm{ESS} = (\hat y - \bar y\mathbf{1})'(\hat y - \bar y\mathbf{1}) = \hat y' M_0 \hat y = (Hy)' M_0 (Hy) = y' H M_0 H y\). Note that \(H M_0 H = H(I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}')H = H^2 - \frac{1}{n}(H\mathbf{1})(H\mathbf{1})' = H - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}'\). Thus \(\mathrm{ESS} = y' (H - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}') y\). Dividing \(\mathrm{ESS}\) by \(\mathrm{TSS}\) gives the stated projection formula.

Empirical

A quantitative analyst fits a Fama–French 3-factor model to monthly Apple (AAPL) excess returns, finding \(R^2 = 0.45\). What economic proportion of Apple's return volatility represents idiosyncratic (diversifiable) risk?

Solution

The proportion of total return variance explained by systematic factor variation is \(R^2 = 0.45\). By the Pythagorean decomposition \(\mathrm{TSS} = \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}\), the fraction attributable to idiosyncratic, non-factor residual variance is \(1 - R^2 = 1 - 0.45 = 0.55\), or 55%. More than half of Apple's monthly return variability is diversifiable idiosyncratic risk.

Campbell and Shiller (1988) report that dividend-yield predictive regressions for annual aggregate stock market returns have \(R^2 \approx 0.10\) at a 1-year horizon and \(R^2 \approx 0.35\) at a 5-year horizon. Why does \(R^2\) scale with the forecast horizon?

Solution

Stock returns are the sum of persistent expected returns and transitory unexpected cash flow and discount rate shocks. At short horizons (1 month), transitory noise dominates total variance, depressing \(R^2\). Over multi-year horizons, transitory return shocks average out through central tendency while the persistent expected return component compounds, allowing the persistent dividend yield to explain a substantially larger share of cumulative return variance.

In an algorithmic trading backtest, adding 50 technical indicators increases in-sample \(R^2\) from 0.02 to 0.28, but out-of-sample \(R^2\) on the test set is \(-0.15\). Interpret this divergence.

Solution

The sharp in-sample increase demonstrates the monotonicity theorem: adding 50 regressors mechanically reduces in-sample \(\mathrm{RSS}\) by fitting random in-sample noise (overfitting). The negative out-of-sample \(R^2\) of \(-0.15\) indicates that the overfitted parameter weights generate mean squared prediction errors worse than simply predicting the historical sample mean \(\bar y\), confirming severe parameter instability.

Research

Campbell and Thompson (2008) analyze out-of-sample \(R^2\) for market predictability. Define \(R_{\mathrm{OOS}}^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t} (y_t - \hat y_t)^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \bar y_t)^2}\) and explain why even \(R_{\mathrm{OOS}}^2 = 0.005\) produces substantial utility gains for a mean-variance investor.

Solution

For a mean-variance investor with risk aversion \(\gamma\), Campbell and Thompson show that the proportional increase in expected utility from timing the market relative to holding a constant portfolio is approximately \(\Delta U \approx \frac{R_{\mathrm{OOS}}^2}{\gamma} \frac{1 + \mathrm{SR}^2}{\mathrm{SR}^2}\), where \(\mathrm{SR}\) is the unconditional Sharpe ratio. Because market return variance is large, an out-of-sample \(R^2\) of 0.5% translates into a meaningful annual economic gain (often 50–100 basis points of risk-adjusted return).

In cross-sectional asset pricing, Roll (1977) critiques the use of \(R^2\) to evaluate the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM). Summarize Roll's critique regarding the mean-variance efficiency of the market proxy.

Solution

Roll showed that the cross-sectional \(R^2\) between average asset returns and estimated market betas is a mathematical tautology depending solely on whether the chosen index proxy lies on the sample mean-variance frontier. If the proxy is mean-variance efficient, the cross-sectional \(R^2\) is 1.0 identically; if not, \(R^2\) can be low even if the true unobserved market portfolio satisfies the CAPM perfectly. Thus \(R^2\) tests the efficiency of the proxy, not the theoretical validity of the CAPM.

Explain the relationship between the \(F\)-test of overall regression significance and \(R^2\): \(F = \frac{R^2 / (k - 1)}{(1 - R^2) / (n - k)}\).

Solution

The \(F\)-test evaluates the joint null hypothesis \(H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_{k-1} = 0\) against the alternative that at least one slope is non-zero. The test statistic is \(F = \frac{(\mathrm{TSS} - \mathrm{RSS}) / (k - 1)}{\mathrm{RSS} / (n - k)} = \frac{\mathrm{ESS} / (k - 1)}{\mathrm{RSS} / (n - k)}\). Dividing both numerator and denominator by \(\mathrm{TSS}\) directly yields \(F = \frac{R^2 / (k - 1)}{(1 - R^2) / (n - k)}\). Thus, the overall \(F\)-statistic is a strictly monotonic transformation of \(R^2\).

Retrieval quiz

  1. Under what condition does the exact decomposition \(\mathrm{TSS} = \mathrm{ESS} + \mathrm{RSS}\) hold?

    Options

    An intercept ensures that the residuals sum to zero (\(\mathbf{1}'e = 0\)), which eliminates the cross-product term in the sum of squares expansion (Theorem 3.1).

  2. What does \(R^2\) equal in a linear regression with an intercept?

    Options

    \(R^2 = r_{y, \hat y}^2\) holds algebraically in any OLS regression containing an intercept (Theorem 3.2).

  3. If a regression has \(\mathrm{TSS} = 500\) and \(\mathrm{ESS} = 400\), what is the value of \(R^2\)?

    \(R^2 = \mathrm{ESS} / \mathrm{TSS} = 400 / 500 = 0.80\) (Definition 3.1).

  4. For the same regression (\(\mathrm{TSS} = 500, \mathrm{ESS} = 400\)), what is the residual sum of squares \(\mathrm{RSS}\)?

    \(\mathrm{RSS} = \mathrm{TSS} - \mathrm{ESS} = 500 - 400 = 100\) (Theorem 3.1).

  5. What happens to the in-sample \(R^2\) when an arbitrary new regressor is added to an existing OLS regression?

    Options

    Expanding the projection column space cannot increase the minimum residual norm, so \(\mathrm{RSS}\) weakly decreases and \(R^2\) weakly increases (Theorem 3.3).

  6. Under what condition does adding a single regressor cause adjusted \(\bar R^2\) to increase?

    Options

    \(\bar R^2\) increases if and only if the \(F\)-statistic for the added variable exceeds 1, corresponding to \(|t| > 1\) (Remark 3.1).

  7. In a simple regression of \(y\) on \(x\) with an intercept, the correlation between \(x\) and \(y\) is \(r_{x, y} = 0.80\). What is the \(R^2\) of the regression?

    In simple regression with an intercept, \(R^2 = r_{x, y}^2 = (0.80)^2 = 0.64\) (Problem M2).

  8. Can the coefficient of determination \(R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS}\) ever be strictly negative?

    Options

    Without an intercept, or out of sample, \(\mathrm{RSS}\) can exceed \(\mathrm{TSS}\), producing \(R^2 < 0\) (Section Canon breaks).
