Errors-in-variables bias

Financial regressors are rarely observed without measurement noise. Corporate fundamental ratios, implied volatilities, macroeconomic indicators, and preliminary asset pricing betas are proxies containing estimation error. Classical measurement error in independent variables attenuates OLS slope estimates toward zero, causing systematic underestimation of factor loadings and false rejection of true economic relationships. Understanding the mathematics of errors-in-variables is vital for interpreting empirical beta-pricing tests and applying structural corrections.

The classical measurement error model

Consider a univariate data-generating process where the response \(y \in \R^n\) is linearly related to an unobserved, latent true regressor \(x^* \in \R^n\):

\[ y = \alpha + \beta x^* + \varepsilon \]

where \(\beta\) is the structural parameter of economic interest and \(\varepsilon\) is a disturbance term satisfying \(\E[\varepsilon | x^*] = 0\).

Assumption 6.1 (Classical Measurement Error)

The econometrician does not observe \(x^*\). Instead, an imperfect proxy \(x \in \R^n\) is observed:

\[ x = x^* + u \]

where \(u \in \R^n\) represents classical measurement error satisfying:

  1. Zero mean: \(\E[u] = 0\).
  2. Independence from the latent regressor: \(\Cov(u, x^*) = 0\).
  3. Independence from the equation disturbance: \(\Cov(u, \varepsilon) = 0\).

The latent regressor has finite positive variance \(\sigma_{x^*}^2 = \Var(x^*)\), and the measurement error has finite variance \(\sigma_u^2 = \Var(u)\). Consequently, the observed regressor has variance \(\Var(x) = \sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2\).

Attenuation bias and the reliability ratio

When the researcher estimates the regression of \(y\) on the observed mismeasured regressor \(x\) using the ordinary least squares estimator:

\[ y = a + b x + w \]

the OLS slope converges to the ratio of the sample covariance to sample variance.

Theorem 6.1 (Errors-in-variables bias and Attenuation)

Under Assumption 6.1, the probability limit of the OLS slope estimator \(\hat\beta\) as sample size \(n \to \infty\) is strictly shrunk toward zero by the reliability ratio \(\lambda \in (0, 1]\):

\[ \mathrm{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat\beta = \beta \cdot \lambda = \beta \left( \frac{\sigma_{x^*}^2}{\sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2} \right) \]

Because \(\sigma_u^2 > 0\), the reliability ratio satisfies \(\lambda < 1\), producing errors-in-variables attenuation bias:

\[ |\mathrm{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat\beta| < |\beta| \]

Proof of Theorem 6.1

The simple OLS slope estimator is \(\hat\beta = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2}\). By the Law of Large Numbers, \(\hat\beta \pto \frac{\Cov(x, y)}{\Var(x)}\). Express \(x\) and \(y\) in terms of their primitive components:

\[ x = x^* + u, \qquad y = \alpha + \beta x^* + \varepsilon \]

Compute the covariance between observed \(x\) and \(y\):

\[ \Cov(x, y) = \Cov(x^* + u, \alpha + \beta x^* + \varepsilon) = \beta \Cov(x^*, x^*) + \Cov(x^*, \varepsilon) + \beta \Cov(u, x^*) + \Cov(u, \varepsilon) \]

By Assumption 6.1, \(\Cov(x^*, \varepsilon) = 0\), \(\Cov(u, x^*) = 0\), and \(\Cov(u, \varepsilon) = 0\). Therefore, the covariance simplifies to:

\[ \Cov(x, y) = \beta \Var(x^*) = \beta \sigma_{x^*}^2 \]

Now evaluate the variance of the observed regressor:

\[ \Var(x) = \Var(x^* + u) = \Var(x^*) + \Var(u) + 2\Cov(x^*, u) = \sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2 \]

Taking the ratio:

\[ \mathrm{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat\beta = \frac{\beta \sigma_{x^*}^2}{\sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2} = \beta \left( \frac{\sigma_{x^*}^2}{\sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2} \right) = \beta \lambda \]

Since \(\sigma_u^2 > 0\), the denominator exceeds the numerator, proving \(0 < \lambda < 1\) and \(|\mathrm{plim} \hat\beta| < |\beta|\).

Theorem 6.2 (Measurement Error in the Dependent Variable)

Suppose the true regressor \(x\) is observed without error, but the dependent variable is measured with noise: \(y = y^* + v\), where \(\E[v] = 0\) and \(\Cov(v, x) = 0\). The OLS slope estimator \(\hat\beta\) remains strictly unbiased and consistent:

\[ \E[\hat\beta | x] = \beta, \qquad \mathrm{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat\beta = \beta \]

Measurement error in \(y\) inflates the disturbance variance from \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) to \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \sigma_v^2\), which increases the standard error of \(\hat\beta\) and depresses \(R^2\), but induces zero parameter bias.

Remark 6.1 (Financial Asset Pricing and the Shanken Correction)

In two-pass cross-sectional asset pricing regressions (Fama & MacBeth), individual asset returns are first regressed on factors in time series to estimate betas \(\hat\beta_i\). In the second pass, average returns \(\bar r_i\) are regressed on estimated betas \(\hat\beta_i\) to estimate factor risk premia \(\gamma\). Because the explanatory variables in the second pass are generated regressors containing estimation noise \(\hat\beta_i = \beta_i + u_i\), the estimated risk premium \(\hat\gamma\) suffers from errors-in-variables attenuation. Shanken (1992) derives an asymptotic adjustment factor \(1 + \gamma'\Sigma_f^{-1}\gamma\) that corrects the second-pass standard errors for first-pass estimation noise.

Lab 1 · Errors-in-variables attenuation bias simulation

Hypothesis: For a linear model with true slope \(\beta = 2.50\), regressor variance \(\sigma_{x^*}^2 = 4.0\), and measurement noise variance \(\sigma_u^2 = 1.0\), the reliability ratio is \(\lambda = 4.0/(4.0+1.0) = 0.80\). The OLS slope using mismeasured regressors across \(n = 10,000\) observations attenuates to \(\beta \lambda = 2.00\), whereas measurement noise in \(y\) preserves an unbiased slope of 2.50.

"""Lab S2-L06-1: Errors-in-variables attenuation bias simulation.

Evaluates attenuation bias in OLS regression under classical measurement error
against the theoretical reliability ratio lambda. Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n = 10000

    # True parameter and variances
    beta_true = 2.50
    alpha_true = 0.50
    var_xstar = 4.00
    var_u = 1.00  # Measurement error variance in regressor
    var_eps = 1.00  # Equation disturbance variance
    var_v = 1.50  # Measurement error variance in response y

    # Theoretical reliability ratio: lambda = Var(x*) / (Var(x*) + Var(u))
    lambda_ratio = var_xstar / (var_xstar + var_u)
    expected_attenuated_beta = beta_true * lambda_ratio

    # Generate data
    x_star = [rng.gauss(0.0, var_xstar**0.5) for _ in range(n)]
    u_meas = [rng.gauss(0.0, var_u**0.5) for _ in range(n)]
    eps = [rng.gauss(0.0, var_eps**0.5) for _ in range(n)]
    v_meas = [rng.gauss(0.0, var_v**0.5) for _ in range(n)]

    # Observed mismeasured regressor x = x* + u
    x_obs = [xs + ui for xs, ui in zip(x_star, u_meas, strict=True)]

    # True response y* = alpha + beta * x* + eps
    y_star = [alpha_true + beta_true * xs + ei for xs, ei in zip(x_star, eps, strict=True)]

    # Observed mismeasured response y = y* + v
    y_obs = [ys + vi for ys, vi in zip(y_star, v_meas, strict=True)]

    # Helper for simple bivariate OLS slope
    def bivariate_slope(x_in: list[float], y_in: list[float]) -> float:
        x_m = sum(x_in) / len(x_in)
        y_m = sum(y_in) / len(y_in)
        sxx = sum((xi - x_m) ** 2 for xi in x_in)
        sxy = sum((xi - x_m) * (yi - y_m) for xi, yi in zip(x_in, y_in, strict=True))
        return sxy / sxx

    # 1. Oracle regression: true y* on true x*
    b_oracle = bivariate_slope(x_star, y_star)

    # 2. Measurement error in regressor: true y* on mismeasured x_obs
    b_meas_x = bivariate_slope(x_obs, y_star)

    # 3. Measurement error in response: mismeasured y_obs on true x*
    b_meas_y = bivariate_slope(x_star, y_obs)

    # 4. Measurement error in both: mismeasured y_obs on mismeasured x_obs
    b_meas_both = bivariate_slope(x_obs, y_obs)

    # Compute empirical attenuation factor
    empirical_lambda = b_meas_x / b_oracle

    print(f"True beta: {beta_true:.4f}")
    print(f"Reliability ratio lambda: {lambda_ratio:.4f}")
    print(f"Theoretical attenuated beta: {expected_attenuated_beta:.4f}")
    print(f"Oracle OLS slope (true x*, true y*): {b_oracle:.4f}")
    print(f"Mismeasured x slope (observed x, true y*): {b_meas_x:.4f}")
    print(f"Mismeasured y slope (true x*, observed y): {b_meas_y:.4f}")
    print(f"Mismeasured both slope (observed x, observed y): {b_meas_both:.4f}")
    print(f"Empirical attenuation ratio: {empirical_lambda:.4f}")


main()

Theoretical attenuated beta: 2.0000
Oracle OLS slope (true x*, true y*): 2.5040
Mismeasured x slope (observed x, true y*): 2.0085
Mismeasured y slope (true x*, observed y): 2.5114

Interpretation: The simulation demonstrates the asymmetry between regressor noise and outcome noise: measurement error in \(x\) shrinks the slope by exactly the reliability ratio 0.80, while measurement error in \(y\) leaves the slope estimate unbiased near 2.50.

Where the canon breaks: non-classical error and multivariate attenuation

Under classical measurement error, attenuation bias is strictly toward zero. However, when measurement error is non-classical—such as mean-reverting error where \(\Cov(u, x^*) < 0\), or when measurement error is correlated with the equation disturbance \(\Cov(u, \varepsilon) \ne 0\)—the bias can push estimates away from zero or reverse sign entirely. In multivariate regressions where multiple regressors are measured with noise, measurement error in one variable can bias coefficients on accurately measured variables in arbitrary directions. The primary econometric remedy for errors-in-variables is an instrumental variable, developed later in the econometrics sequence.

Misconceptions

  1. Measurement error in the dependent variable biases the OLS slope estimator.

    Classical measurement error in \(y\) adds noise to the disturbance term without inducing correlation between the regressor and the error. The OLS slope remains strictly unbiased and consistent; only the residual variance increases and estimation precision decreases.

  2. Increasing the sample size eliminates attenuation bias from errors-in-variables.

    Attenuation bias is an asymptotic identification defect, not a small-sample bias. As \(n \to \infty\), \(\hat\beta\) converges in probability to \(\beta \lambda \ne \beta\). The inconsistency persists in infinite samples unless structural corrections or instrumental variables are used.

  3. In multivariate regression, measurement error in one regressor only attenuates that specific regressor's coefficient.

    Through the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell partialling-out operator, measurement error in \(x_1\) corrupts the residualized regressor \(\tilde x_2\), spilling over to bias the estimated coefficients on perfectly measured control variables in unpredictable directions.

Practice problems

Mechanics

A true regressor has variance \(\sigma_{x^*}^2 = 6.0\). It is contaminated by classical noise with variance \(\sigma_u^2 = 2.0\). If the true structural slope is \(\beta = 1.60\), calculate the reliability ratio \(\lambda\) and the asymptotic limit of the OLS estimator.

Solution

The reliability ratio is \(\lambda = \frac{\sigma_{x^*}^2}{\sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2} = \frac{6.0}{6.0 + 2.0} = \frac{6.0}{8.0} = 0.75\). The asymptotic limit of the OLS estimator is \(\mathrm{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat\beta = \beta \lambda = 1.60 \times 0.75 = 1.20\). The estimated slope is attenuated toward zero by 25%.

Suppose a researcher estimates a regression slope of \(\hat\beta = 0.85\) using a noisy proxy. An independent audit reveals that the noise-to-signal ratio is \(\sigma_u^2 / \sigma_{x^*}^2 = 0.25\). What is the implied true slope \(\beta\)?

Solution

The reliability ratio can be written in terms of the noise-to-signal ratio \(\theta = \sigma_u^2 / \sigma_{x^*}^2\) as \(\lambda = \frac{1}{1 + \theta} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.25} = \frac{1}{1.25} = 0.80\). Since \(\mathrm{plim} \hat\beta = \beta \lambda\), the implied true structural coefficient is \(\beta = \frac{\hat\beta}{\lambda} = \frac{0.85}{0.80} = 1.0625\).

If the true \(R^2\) of a clean regression \(y^* = \beta x^* + \varepsilon\) is 0.50 with \(\Var(y^*) = 10.0\), what is the observed \(R^2\) when \(y\) is contaminated by independent noise with variance \(\sigma_v^2 = 2.5\)?

Solution

The explained sum of squares depends only on \(\beta x^*\) and remains unchanged: \(\mathrm{ESS} = R^2 \Var(y^*) = 0.50 \times 10.0 = 5.0\). The observed total variance becomes \(\Var(y) = \Var(y^*) + \Var(v) = 10.0 + 2.5 = 12.5\). The observed \(R^2\) is \(R_{\mathrm{obs}}^2 = \frac{\mathrm{ESS}}{\Var(y)} = \frac{5.0}{12.5} = 0.40\). Measurement error in \(y\) lowers \(R^2\) from 0.50 to 0.40.

Proof

Prove that under non-classical measurement error where the error is negatively correlated with the true variable (\(\Cov(u, x^*) = -\theta < 0\)), the OLS estimator can exhibit either attenuation or expansion.

Solution

Evaluate \(\Cov(x, y) = \Cov(x^* + u, \beta x^* + \varepsilon) = \beta \sigma_{x^*}^2 + \beta \Cov(u, x^*) = \beta (\sigma_{x^*}^2 - \theta)\). The observed variance is \(\Var(x) = \sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2 + 2\Cov(u, x^*) = \sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2 - 2\theta\). The probability limit is \(\mathrm{plim} \hat\beta = \beta \frac{\sigma_{x^*}^2 - \theta}{\sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2 - 2\theta}\). If \(\sigma_u^2 < \theta\), the denominator shrinks faster than the numerator, which causes \(\frac{\sigma_{x^*}^2 - \theta}{\sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2 - 2\theta} > 1\), creating parameter expansion rather than attenuation.

Show that the difference between the inverse of the OLS estimator of \(y\) on \(x\) and the reverse OLS estimator of \(x\) on \(y\) bounds the true slope under classical measurement error (Frisch bounds).

Solution

Direct OLS of \(y\) on \(x\) yields \(\hat\beta \pto \beta \frac{\sigma_{x^*}^2}{\sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2} = \beta \lambda \le \beta\) (attenuated from below). Reverse regression of \(x\) on \(y\) yields slope \(\hat\delta = \frac{\Cov(x, y)}{\Var(y)} \pto \frac{\beta \sigma_{x^*}^2}{\beta^2 \sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2}\). Inverting \(\hat\delta\) gives \(1/\hat\delta \pto \beta + \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\beta \sigma_{x^*}^2} \ge \beta\) (for \(\beta > 0\)). Therefore, \(\mathrm{plim} \hat\beta \le \beta \le \mathrm{plim}(1/\hat\delta)\). The direct and reverse regression slopes bound the true structural parameter from below and above.

Prove that in a multivariate regression where \(x_1\) has classical measurement error and \(x_2\) is measured perfectly, the coefficient on the perfectly measured variable \(\hat\beta_2\) is biased unless \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are uncorrelated.

Solution

By the FWL theorem, \(\hat\beta_2\) is obtained by regressing \(y\) on the residualized regressor \(\tilde x_2 = M_1 x_2\). Because \(x_1 = x_1^* + u\) contains measurement noise, the projection matrix \(M_1\) fails to fully purge \(x_1^*\). The residual \(\tilde x_2\) remains correlated with \(x_1^*\), allowing the measurement error in \(x_1\) to contaminate \(\hat\beta_2\). Specifically, \(\mathrm{plim}\hat\beta_2 = \beta_2 + \beta_1 \frac{\Cov(x_1, x_2)}{\Var(x_2)} (1 - \lambda)\). If \(\Cov(x_1, x_2) \ne 0\) and \(\lambda < 1\), \(\hat\beta_2\) suffers from transmission bias even though \(x_2\) is measured with zero error.

Empirical

In empirical tests of the Capital Asset Pricing Model, Black, Jensen, and Scholes (1972) group individual stocks into portfolios based on historical beta before estimating second-pass risk premia. How does portfolio grouping mitigate errors-in-variables attenuation?

Solution

Individual stock beta estimates \(\hat\beta_i = \beta_i + u_i\) contain substantial idiosyncratic estimation noise, depressing the second-pass security market line slope via attenuation. Grouping \(N\) stocks into diversified portfolios averages out individual estimation noise: \(\Var(\bar u_p) = \frac{1}{N}\sigma_u^2\). This drives the portfolio reliability ratio \(\lambda_p = \frac{\sigma_{\beta_p}^2}{\sigma_{\beta_p}^2 + \sigma_u^2/N}\) close to 1.0, eliminating almost all attenuation bias.

A corporate finance researcher measures firm marginal q using average Tobin's q calculated from financial statements. If accounting conventions introduce classical noise into measured q, what is the impact on the estimated sensitivity of corporate investment to market opportunities?

Solution

Measured average q is an imperfect proxy for unobserved marginal q: \(q_{\mathrm{measured}} = q_{\mathrm{marginal}} + u\). By the attenuation theorem, the regression slope of investment on measured q is attenuated toward zero by the reliability ratio \(\lambda < 1\). This leads researchers to falsely conclude that managers ignore market valuation signals when making physical capital expenditures.

When estimating consumption-based asset pricing models, quarterly aggregate consumption data is constructed via time-aggregation and survey extrapolation. How does time-averaging violate the classical measurement error assumptions?

Solution

Time-averaging continuous consumption expenditures over a calendar quarter introduces mechanical positive first-order serial correlation into the measurement error \(u_t = \frac{1}{2}(u_{t}^* + u_{t-1}^*)\), violating the classical assumption of mutually independent errors. Furthermore, time-averaging causes measured consumption growth to lag true innovation shocks, creating artificial correlation between lagged asset returns and contemporaneous consumption errors.

Research

Klepper and Leamer (1984) establish diagnostic bounds for multiple regressors measured with classical noise. Summarize how their bounds determine whether the signs of all coefficients are identified.

Solution

Klepper and Leamer demonstrate that when all regressors are potentially measured with error, evaluating all \(k+1\) regressions (direct regression of \(y\) on \(X\) and the \(k\) reverse regressions of each \(x_j\) on \(y\) and \(X_{-j}\)) forms a convex polytope of consistent parameter vectors. If all \(k+1\) regressions yield identical signs for all coefficients, the signs of the true parameters are bounded and identified. If any reverse regression changes sign, no coefficient's sign can be identified without prior bounds on measurement error variances.

Explain how high-frequency realized volatility estimates constructed from 5-minute intraday returns suffer from microstructure noise (bid-ask bounce) and how this errors-in-variables problem is modeled.

Solution

At ultra-high frequencies (1-second), bid-ask bounce introduces negative serial correlation in tick returns, causing standard realized variance \(\mathrm{RV} = \sum r_i^2\) to explode as sampling frequency increases due to the accumulation of squared noise \(\sum u_i^2\). Econometricians model observed price as latent efficient price plus microstructure noise: \(p_t = p_t^* + \eta_t\). Two-scale realized volatility or pre-averaging filters are applied to isolate the latent integrated volatility.

In structural corporate finance, researchers use instrumental variables to address measurement error in investment equations. Explain why lagged investment or lagged cash flow is often an invalid instrument for Tobin's q.

Solution

A valid instrument must satisfy relevance (\(\Cov(z, q^*) \ne 0\)) and exclusion (\(\Cov(z, \varepsilon) = 0\)). If firms face dynamic financing frictions or adjustment costs, unobserved capital productivity shocks \(\varepsilon_t\) exhibit persistent serial correlation. Lagged investment or cash flow is correlated with past productivity shocks, which by persistence correlates with current \(\varepsilon_t\), violating the exclusion restriction and rendering the instrumental variable estimator inconsistent.

Retrieval quiz

  1. Under classical measurement error in the independent variable \(x = x^* + u\), what is the asymptotic effect on the OLS slope estimator \(\hat\beta\)?

    Options

    Theorem 6.1 proves that \(\mathrm{plim} \hat\beta = \beta \lambda\), where \(\lambda = \frac{\sigma_{x^*}^2}{\sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_u^2} < 1\).

  2. If the latent regressor variance is \(\sigma_{x^*}^2 = 8.0\) and the measurement error variance is \(\sigma_u^2 = 2.0\), what is the reliability ratio \(\lambda\)?

    \(\lambda = 8.0 / (8.0 + 2.0) = 8.0 / 10.0 = 0.80\) (Theorem 6.1).

  3. For the same problem (\(\lambda = 0.80\)), if the true structural slope is \(\beta = 2.50\), what is the asymptotic limit of the OLS estimator \(\mathrm{plim} \hat\beta\)?

    \(\mathrm{plim} \hat\beta = \beta \lambda = 2.50 \times 0.80 = 2.00\) (Theorem 6.1 and Lab 1).

  4. What is the effect of classical measurement error in the dependent variable \(y = y^* + v\)?

    Options

    Theorem 6.2 proves that measurement error in \(y\) only inflates residual variance without biasing the slope parameter.

  5. Does increasing the sample size \(n\) from 100 to 100,000 eliminate attenuation bias in OLS?

    Options

    Attenuation bias reflects non-zero covariance between observed \(x\) and the composite error, rendering OLS permanently inconsistent (Section Canon breaks).

  6. Why does portfolio grouping (such as Black, Jensen, and Scholes 1972) mitigate errors-in-variables in beta-pricing regressions?

    Options

    Portfolio aggregation averages out independent first-pass estimation errors, driving the portfolio reliability ratio to near unity (Problem E1).

  7. In a multivariate regression where \(x_1\) has measurement error and \(x_2\) is measured perfectly, what happens to the coefficient on \(x_2\)?

    Options

    Measurement error in \(x_1\) spills over through the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell residualization operator, biasing coefficients on correlated controls (Problem P3).

  8. If the noise variance equals the true signal variance (\(\sigma_u^2 = \sigma_{x^*}^2\)), what is the reliability ratio \(\lambda\)?

    \(\lambda = \sigma_{x^*}^2 / (\sigma_{x^*}^2 + \sigma_{x^*}^2) = 1/2 = 0.50\) (Theorem 6.1).
