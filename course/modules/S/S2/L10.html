Clustered standard errors and the Moulton design effect

Modern quantitative finance relies heavily on panel data: multiple firms tracked over hundreds of monthly or quarterly intervals. In these datasets, observations are rarely independent. A firm's capital expenditures, dividend policies, and leverage choices exhibit persistent corporate culture, while executive compensation and analyst forecasts cluster by industry. When disturbances share unobserved group components, both classical OLS and White heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors fail. Cluster-robust covariance estimators account for arbitrary within-group correlation, providing the foundational inference framework for corporate finance and cross-sectional asset pricing.

Clustered data in financial econometrics

Consider a panel or grouped dataset with \(G\) independent clusters indexed by \(g = 1, \dots, G\), where cluster \(g\) contains \(N_g\) observations indexed by \(i = 1, \dots, N_g\). The total number of observations is \(N = \sum_{g=1}^G N_g\). The linear regression model is:

\[ y_{ig} = x_{ig}'\beta + \varepsilon_{ig} \]

where \(x_{ig} \in \R^k\) and \(\beta \in \R^k\). Grouping all observations within cluster \(g\) into vector and matrix blocks:

\[ y_g = X_g \beta + \varepsilon_g \]

where \(y_g \in \R^{N_g}\), \(X_g \in \R^{N_g \times k}\), and \(\varepsilon_g \in \R^{N_g}\).

Assumption 10.1 (Cluster Sampling Structure)

  1. Independence across clusters: \(\E[\varepsilon_{ig} \varepsilon_{jh} | X] = 0\) for all \(g \ne h\), and any \(i, j\).
  2. Unrestricted within-cluster correlation: Within cluster \(g\), disturbances have an arbitrary conditional covariance matrix \(\Sigma_g = \Var(\varepsilon_g | X_g) = \E[\varepsilon_g \varepsilon_g' | X_g] \in \R^{N_g \times N_g}\).
  3. Exogeneity: \(\E[\varepsilon_g | X_g] = 0\) for all \(g = 1, \dots, G\).

Under Assumption 10.1, the full disturbance covariance matrix \(\Sigma = \Var(\varepsilon | X)\) is block-diagonal:

\[ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & \Sigma_2 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & \Sigma_G \end{bmatrix} \]

The cluster-robust CR0 sandwich estimator

The OLS estimator of \(\beta\) across the entire sample is \(\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\). The estimation error satisfies:

\[ \hat\beta - \beta = (X'X)^{-1} X'\varepsilon = (X'X)^{-1} \sum_{g=1}^G X_g'\varepsilon_g = (X'X)^{-1} \sum_{g=1}^G s_g \]

where \(s_g = X_g'\varepsilon_g = \sum_{i=1}^{N_g} x_{ig} \varepsilon_{ig} \in \R^k\) is the total score contribution of cluster \(g\).

Because clusters are mutually independent, the variance of the score sum is the sum of cluster score variances:

\[ \Var\left(\sum_{g=1}^G s_g \middle| X\right) = \sum_{g=1}^G \Var(s_g | X_g) = \sum_{g=1}^G X_g' \Sigma_g X_g \]

To estimate this quantity without imposing parametric structure on \(\Sigma_g\), we use clustered standard errors (the Liang–Zeger 1986 and Arellano 1987 cluster-robust variance estimator, known as CR0).

Definition 10.1 (Cluster-Robust CR0 Estimator)

Let \(e_g = y_g - X_g\hat\beta\) denote the \(N_g \times 1\) vector of OLS residuals for cluster \(g\). The cluster score estimator is \(\hat s_g = X_g' e_g = \sum_{i=1}^{N_g} x_{ig} e_{ig}\). The CR0 variance-covariance estimator is:

\[ \hat V_{\text{CR0}} = (X'X)^{-1} \left(\sum_{g=1}^G \hat s_g \hat s_g'\right) (X'X)^{-1} = (X'X)^{-1} \left(\sum_{g=1}^G X_g' e_g e_g' X_g\right) (X'X)^{-1} \]

Theorem 10.1 (Collapse of CR0 to White HC0)

If each cluster contains exactly one observation (\(N_g = 1\) for all \(g = 1, \dots, G\), so \(G = N\)), the cluster-robust estimator \(\hat V_{\text{CR0}}\) is identically the White (1980) HC0 heteroskedasticity-consistent estimator:

\[ \hat V_{\text{CR0}} \equiv \hat V_{\text{HC0}} \]

Proof: When \(N_g = 1\), the cluster index \(g\) equals the observation index \(i\), and \(X_g' e_g = x_i e_i\). The meat matrix simplifies to:

\[ \sum_{g=1}^G \hat s_g \hat s_g' = \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i e_i)(x_i e_i)' = \sum_{i=1}^N e_i^2 x_i x_i' \]

Pre- and post-multiplying by \((X'X)^{-1}\) gives \(\hat V_{\text{HC0}}\). ■

The Moulton design effect and bias of classical OLS

Why does ignoring cluster correlation cause catastrophic under-coverage? In a landmark econometric paper, Moulton (1986) derived the relationship between the true cluster-robust variance and the classical homoskedastic variance.

Theorem 10.2 (Moulton Factor for Cluster Variance)

Suppose clusters have equal size \(m = N_g\). Let the disturbances have equicorrelated intra-cluster correlation \(\rho_\varepsilon = \Corr(\varepsilon_{ig}, \varepsilon_{jg})\) for \(i \ne j\), and let the regressor have intra-cluster correlation \(\rho_x = \Corr(x_{ig}, x_{jg})\). The ratio of the true variance of \(\hat\beta\) to the classical homoskedastic variance is given by the Moulton design effect:

\[ \frac{\Var_{\text{cluster}}(\hat\beta)}{\Var_{\text{classical}}(\hat\beta)} \approx 1 + (m - 1) \rho_x \rho_\varepsilon \]

Petersen (2008) examined empirical finance literature and demonstrated that ignoring firm clustering inflated t-statistics by a factor of 2 to 4, generating hundreds of false anomaly discoveries. For example, in a panel of 500 firms observed over \(m = 20\) quarters with modest residual persistence \(\rho_\varepsilon = 0.20\) and regressor persistence \(\rho_x = 0.80\), the Moulton factor is:

\[ 1 + (20 - 1) \times 0.80 \times 0.20 = 1 + 19 \times 0.16 = 1 + 3.04 = 4.04 \]

The true standard error is \(\sqrt{4.04} \approx 2.01\) times larger than the classical standard error. A reported t-statistic of 3.00 is in reality only \(3.00 / 2.01 = 1.49\), completely failing statistical significance at the 5% level.

Finite-sample adjustments and the few-clusters problem

Because \(\hat s_g = X_g' e_g\) uses OLS residuals rather than true disturbances, \(\hat V_{\text{CR0}}\) is biased downward in finite samples. To improve test size, econometric packages implement the CR1 degrees-of-freedom correction:

\[ \hat V_{\text{CR1}} = \left(\frac{G}{G - 1}\right) \left(\frac{N - 1}{N - k}\right) \hat V_{\text{CR0}} \]

When conducting hypothesis tests with clustered standard errors, test statistics follow a Student-t distribution with \(G - 1\) degrees of freedom, where \(G\) is the number of clusters (not \(N - k\)!).

The Few-Clusters Problem: Consistency of CR0 and CR1 relies on the number of clusters \(G \to \infty\), not the total number of observations \(N \to \infty\). If a researcher has \(N = 100,000\) observations grouped into only \(G = 15\) industries, asymptotic normality fails. Test sizes are severely distorted (rejection rates of 15%–20% at nominal 5%). When \(G < 30\), Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller (2008) recommend the wild cluster bootstrap.

Lab 1 · Clustered standard errors and the Moulton design effect

Hypothesis: For a panel of \(G = 50\) clusters of size \(m = 10\) (\(N = 500\)) with intra-cluster correlation \(\rho_x = 0.50\) and \(\rho_\varepsilon = 0.50\) (theoretical Moulton factor \(1 + 9 \times 0.25 = 3.25\)), classical OLS and White HC0 standard errors exhibit severe under-coverage (~73%), whereas cluster-robust CR0 standard errors achieve nominal 95% coverage within 2 binomial standard errors.

"""Lab S2-L10-1: Clustered standard errors and the Moulton design effect.

Simulates a panel dataset with unobserved cluster random effects across G = 50 clusters
and cluster size m = 10 (N = 500). Compares coverage of classical OLS, White HC0,
and cluster-robust CR0 standard errors against nominal 95% confidence intervals.

Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    num_clusters = 50
    cluster_size = 10
    total_obs = num_clusters * cluster_size
    replications = 2500
    beta_0 = 1.00
    beta_1 = 2.00
    z_crit = 1.96

    cov_classical = 0
    cov_white = 0
    cov_cr0 = 0

    for _ in range(replications):
        x: list[float] = []
        errors: list[float] = []
        cluster_ids: list[int] = []

        for g in range(num_clusters):
            # Cluster-level random components
            mu_g = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            alpha_g = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)

            for _ in range(cluster_size):
                # Regressor with intra-cluster correlation rho_x = 0.50
                xi = mu_g + rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
                # Error with intra-cluster correlation rho_eps = 0.50
                ei = alpha_g + rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)

                x.append(xi)
                errors.append(ei)
                cluster_ids.append(g)

        # Dependent variable
        y = [beta_0 + beta_1 * xi + ei for xi, ei in zip(x, errors, strict=True)]

        x_bar = sum(x) / total_obs
        y_bar = sum(y) / total_obs
        sxx = sum((xi - x_bar) ** 2 for xi in x)
        sxy = sum((xi - x_bar) * (yi - y_bar) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True))
        b1_hat = sxy / sxx
        resids = [yi - (y_bar + b1_hat * (xi - x_bar)) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True)]

        # Classical OLS standard error
        s2 = sum(r**2 for r in resids) / (total_obs - 2)
        se_classical = math.sqrt(s2 / sxx)

        # White HC0 standard error (ignores clustering)
        meat_white = sum(((xi - x_bar) ** 2) * (r**2) for xi, r in zip(x, resids, strict=True))
        se_white = math.sqrt(meat_white) / sxx

        # Cluster-robust CR0 standard error
        cluster_scores = [0.0] * num_clusters
        for xi, r, g in zip(x, resids, cluster_ids, strict=True):
            cluster_scores[g] += (xi - x_bar) * r
        meat_cr0 = sum(sg**2 for sg in cluster_scores)
        se_cr0 = math.sqrt(meat_cr0) / sxx

        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_classical <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_classical:
            cov_classical += 1
        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_white <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_white:
            cov_white += 1
        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_cr0 <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_cr0:
            cov_cr0 += 1

    rate_classical = cov_classical / replications
    rate_white = cov_white / replications
    rate_cr0 = cov_cr0 / replications

    print("Estimator     | Nominal | Empirical Coverage | Under-coverage")
    print("-" * 59)
    print(f"Classical OLS |  0.9500 |             {rate_classical:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_classical:+.4f}")
    print(f"White HC0     |  0.9500 |             {rate_white:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_white:+.4f}")
    print(f"Clustered CR0 |  0.9500 |             {rate_cr0:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_cr0:+.4f}")


main()

-----------------------------------------------------------
Classical OLS |  0.9500 |             0.7344 |        +0.2156
White HC0     |  0.9500 |             0.7312 |        +0.2188
Clustered CR0 |  0.9500 |             0.9328 |        +0.0172

The experiment confirms the catastrophic failure of classical and White HC0 estimators under grouped error dependence: both achieve only ~73% coverage for a nominal 95% confidence interval. White HC0 provides zero protection against cluster correlation because it assumes observation independence. In contrast, clustered CR0 aggregates scores at the cluster level, restoring empirical coverage to 93.3%.

Where the canon breaks

Cluster-robust inference is robust to arbitrary within-cluster correlation, but breaks down under the following conditions:

  • Few clusters (\(G < 30\)): Asymptotics depend on \(G \to \infty\). With small \(G\), the score covariance estimator has high sampling noise, causing over-rejection of the null hypothesis unless wild cluster bootstrapping is used.
  • Massively unbalanced cluster sizes: If one cluster contains 40% of all observations while the remaining 60% are split across small clusters, the effective number of clusters is much smaller than \(G\), invalidating standard CR0 inference.
  • Two-way clustering: In panel regressions with both persistent firm effects and macroeconomic time shocks, clustering along only one dimension (firm or time) leaves the second dimension's correlation unaddressed. Two-way clustering (Lesson 11) is required.
  • Cross-cluster spillovers: If clusters interact through network links or spatial proximity, the assumption of zero cross-cluster correlation (\(\E[\varepsilon_{ig}\varepsilon_{jh}] = 0\)) fails. Spatial HAC estimators (Conley 1999) must be employed.

Misconceptions

  1. Clustering standard errors changes the OLS slope estimates.

    Clustering modifies only the variance-covariance matrix of the parameter estimator. The point estimates \(\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\) remain completely identical to ordinary least squares estimates.

  2. White HC0 standard errors protect against cluster correlation.

    White HC0 is robust only to heteroskedasticity; it strictly assumes that disturbances across different observations are mutually uncorrelated. As shown in Lab 1, White HC0 produces the exact same under-coverage as classical OLS when cluster correlation is present.

  3. Including cluster fixed effects eliminates the need for clustered standard errors.

    Cluster fixed effects remove only time-invariant cluster-level mean shifts. They do not remove autocorrelation across time within a firm, nor do they eliminate time-varying intra-cluster heteroskedasticity.

Practice problems

Mechanics

Calculate the Moulton variance inflation factor for a panel regression where the average cluster size is \(m = 25\), the regressor intra-cluster correlation is \(\rho_x = 0.60\), and the disturbance intra-cluster correlation is \(\rho_\varepsilon = 0.15\).

Solution

Apply the Moulton design effect formula: \(\text{Factor} = 1 + (m - 1) \rho_x \rho_\varepsilon = 1 + (25 - 1) \times 0.60 \times 0.15 = 1 + 24 \times 0.09 = 1 + 2.16 = 3.16\). The true variance is 3.16 times larger than classical variance, and true standard errors are \(\sqrt{3.16} \approx 1.778\) times larger.

In a panel dataset with \(G = 40\) clusters, \(N = 2,000\) observations, and \(k = 4\) regressors, calculate the CR1 finite-sample degrees-of-freedom scaling factor.

Solution

The CR1 scaling factor is \(\left(\frac{G}{G - 1}\right) \left(\frac{N - 1}{N - k}\right) = \left(\frac{40}{39}\right) \left(\frac{1999}{1996}\right) \approx 1.02564 \times 1.00150 \approx 1.02718\). The CR0 covariance matrix is multiplied by approximately 1.027.

What degrees of freedom should be used when looking up critical values for a t-statistic computed with CR0 standard errors across \(G = 35\) firms and \(N = 7,000\) quarterly observations?

Solution

The reference distribution is a Student-t with \(G - 1\) degrees of freedom, where \(G\) is the number of independent clusters. Here, \(G - 1 = 35 - 1 = 34\) degrees of freedom (not \(N - k = 6996\)).

Proof

Prove that the cluster score vector \(s_g = X_g'\varepsilon_g\) satisfies \(\E[s_g] = 0\) and \(\Var\left(\sum_{g=1}^G s_g\right) = \sum_{g=1}^G X_g'\Sigma_g X_g\) under Assumption 10.1.

Solution

By the law of iterated expectations, \(\E[s_g] = \E[X_g'\E[\varepsilon_g | X_g]] = \E[X_g' \cdot 0] = 0\). For the variance of the sum, \(\Var\left(\sum_{g=1}^G s_g \middle| X\right) = \sum_{g=1}^G \Var(s_g | X_g) + \sum_{g \ne h} \Cov(s_g, s_h | X)\). By independence across clusters, \(\Cov(s_g, s_h | X) = X_g'\E[\varepsilon_g \varepsilon_h' | X] X_h = 0\). For each cluster, \(\Var(s_g | X_g) = X_g'\E[\varepsilon_g \varepsilon_g' | X_g] X_g = X_g'\Sigma_g X_g\). Summing yields \(\sum_{g=1}^G X_g'\Sigma_g X_g\).

Prove that if a regressor is purely idiosyncratic across observations within a cluster (\(\rho_x = 0\)), the Moulton factor equals 1 even if disturbances are highly correlated within clusters (\(\rho_\varepsilon > 0\)).

Solution

The cross-product of scores across observations \(i \ne j\) within cluster \(g\) has expectation \(\E[x_{ig} x_{jg}' \varepsilon_{ig} \varepsilon_{jg}] = \E[x_{ig} x_{jg}'] \E[\varepsilon_{ig} \varepsilon_{jg}]\) by regressor-error exogeneity. If \(\rho_x = 0\), \(\E[x_{ig} x_{jg}'] = 0\) for \(i \ne j\). Thus all off-diagonal cross-products in \(X_g' \Sigma_g X_g\) vanish, collapsing the cluster score variance to \(\sum_{i=1}^{N_g} \sigma_i^2 x_{ig} x_{ig}'\). The Moulton factor simplifies to \(1 + (m-1)(0)\rho_\varepsilon = 1.0\).

Show that the cluster-robust variance estimator \(\hat V_{\text{CR0}}\) can be expressed as a linear combination of cluster-level projection operators.

Solution

Define the cluster selection matrix \(D_g \in \R^{N \times N_g}\) such that \(X_g = D_g' X\) and \(e_g = D_g' e\). Then the cluster score is \(\hat s_g = X_g' e_g = X' D_g D_g' e\). Let \(P_g = D_g D_g' \in \R^{N \times N}\) be the block-diagonal selection projector. The meat matrix is \(\sum_{g=1}^G \hat s_g \hat s_g' = X' \left(\sum_{g=1}^G P_g e e' P_g\right) X\). Because \(\sum P_g e e' P_g\) isolates the diagonal blocks of \(e e'\), \(\hat V_{\text{CR0}} = (X'X)^{-1} X' [e e' \odot B] X (X'X)^{-1}\), where \(B\) is a block-diagonal indicator matrix of cluster co-membership.

Empirical

In executive compensation research, why does regressing CEO salary on firm performance require clustering by firm rather than by year?

Solution

Executive compensation structures and firm governance practices are highly persistent over time for a given firm (\(\rho_\varepsilon > 0.60\)), while firm size and industry characteristics are also slow-moving (\(\rho_x > 0.80\)). As shown by Petersen (2008), this generates a massive firm-level Moulton design effect. Clustering by firm accounts for unobserved firm-specific culture, whereas clustering by year ignores all intra-firm serial correlation.

Explain why the Fama–MacBeth (1973) two-step procedure produces standard errors that are biased downward in the presence of firm fixed effects or persistent firm shocks.

Solution

Fama–MacBeth runs cross-sectional regressions in each time period and calculates standard errors from the time-series variance of the cross-sectional estimates \(\hat\beta_t\). While this perfectly accounts for cross-sectional correlation at each point in time, it assumes that the time series of estimates \(\hat\beta_t\) is independent over time. When firm shocks are persistent, \(\hat\beta_t\) exhibits serial correlation, causing Fama–MacBeth standard errors to severely understate true variance.

A researcher studies loan defaults across 50 US states over 20 years. State banking regulations vary at the state level. Should standard errors be clustered at the state level or the county level?

Solution

Standard errors must be clustered at the level of the policy variation: the state level. Because state banking regulations are identical for all counties within a state (\(\rho_x = 1.0\)), clustering at the county level would fail to account for unobserved statewide macro shocks and state policy persistence, severely violating independence.

Research

Explain the mechanics of the wild cluster bootstrap (Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller 2008) and why it outperforms CR0 when the number of clusters is small (\(G < 20\)).

Solution

The wild cluster bootstrap resamples entire cluster residual vectors \(\tilde e_g = v_g e_g\) using Rademacher weights \(v_g \in \{-1, +1\}\) with probability 0.5. By flipping the sign of the entire cluster residual vector simultaneously, it preserves the exact unconstrained intra-cluster correlation structure \(\Sigma_g\) while imposing the null hypothesis. It generates a bootstrap t-distribution that accurately captures finite-\(G\) tail thickness without asymptotic normality assumptions.

How does the effective number of clusters \(G^*\) defined by Carter, Schnepel, and Steigerwald (2017) diagnose asymptotic breakdown in unbalanced panels?

Solution

Carter et al. show that when cluster sizes or cluster leverages are heterogeneous, asymptotic variance depends on the effective number of clusters \(G^* = G / (1 + \mathrm{CV}_w^2)\), where \(\mathrm{CV}_w\) is the coefficient of variation of cluster weights. If a few large clusters dominate the sample, \(G^*\) can be 5 even if the nominal number of clusters is \(G = 50\), alerting researchers that standard cluster asymptotics will fail.

Contrast one-way clustering by firm with random effects generalized least squares (GLS). When is OLS with clustered standard errors preferable to RE-GLS?

Solution

RE-GLS assumes an equicorrelated error structure \(\varepsilon_{ig} = \alpha_g + \eta_{ig}\) with constant variance and homoskedastic idiosyncratic noise. If the true correlation structure involves decaying AR(1) persistence, dynamic heteroskedasticity, or arbitrary within-cluster covariance, RE-GLS standard errors are biased. OLS with CR0 clustered standard errors makes zero assumptions about the functional form of \(\Sigma_g\), providing robust inference.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What does the cluster-robust CR0 estimator assume about observations within the same cluster?

    Options

    CR0 allows any arbitrary within-cluster covariance matrix \(\Sigma_g\) while assuming independence across clusters (Definition 10.1).

  2. If average cluster size is \(m = 10\), regressor intra-cluster correlation is \(\rho_x = 0.50\), and error correlation is \(\rho_\varepsilon = 0.50\), what is the Moulton variance inflation factor?

    \(1 + (10 - 1) \times 0.50 \times 0.50 = 1 + 9 \times 0.25 = 1 + 2.25 = 3.25\) (Theorem 10.2).

  3. When does the cluster-robust CR0 estimator collapse identically to the White (1980) HC0 estimator?

    Options

    When \(N_g = 1\), each cluster score is simply \(x_i e_i\), yielding White HC0 (Theorem 10.1).

  4. What degrees of freedom should be used for t-tests when standard errors are clustered across \(G\) groups?

    Options

    Asymptotics are driven by the number of independent clusters \(G\), making \(G - 1\) the appropriate degrees of freedom (Section Finite-sample adjustments).

  5. Why does Fama–MacBeth (1973) fail to produce correct standard errors when panel data exhibit persistent firm effects?

    Options

    Petersen (2008) demonstrated that Fama-MacBeth ignores serial correlation in cross-sectional estimates induced by persistent firm effects (Problem E2).

  6. Does clustering standard errors change the estimated OLS slope coefficients \(\hat\beta\)?

    Options

    Clustering affects only the estimated variance of \(\hat\beta\), not the point estimates themselves (Misconception 1).

  7. What procedure is recommended when the number of clusters is small (\(G < 20\) or 30)?

    Options

    With small \(G\), CR0 over-rejects; the wild cluster bootstrap provides accurate test sizes (Section Where the canon breaks and Problem R1).

  8. If the regressor \(x_{ig}\) is purely idiosyncratic within each cluster (\(\rho_x = 0\)), how does intra-cluster error correlation (\(\rho_\varepsilon > 0\)) affect OLS standard errors?

    Options

    When \(\rho_x = 0\), the product \(\rho_x \rho_\varepsilon = 0\), so the Moulton inflation factor is exactly 1.0 (Problem P2).
