HAC standard errors and the Newey–West estimator

In empirical macro-finance and asset pricing, time-series data rarely consist of independent innovations. Predictive regressions frequently examine multi-month or annual cumulative returns sampled at monthly frequencies, creating mechanical moving-average errors. Macroeconomic regressors such as the price-dividend ratio, inflation, or credit spreads exhibit high persistence. When autocorrelated disturbances combine with autocorrelated regressors, classical and White heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors fail, artificially deflating standard errors and leading to spurious statistical significance. Heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) estimators restore valid large-sample inference in dynamic financial models.

Autocorrelation in dynamic financial regressions

Consider a time-series linear regression for \(t = 1, \dots, T\):

\[ y_t = x_t'\beta + \varepsilon_t \]

where \(x_t\) is a \(k \times 1\) vector of stationary explanatory variables and \(\varepsilon_t\) is a disturbance process satisfying \(\E[\varepsilon_t | x_t] = 0\). Unlike the cross-sectional setting of Lesson 08 where observations are mutually independent, time-series disturbances often exhibit temporal dependence:

\[ \gamma_j = \E[\varepsilon_t \varepsilon_{t-j}] \ne 0 \quad \text{for } j \ge 1 \]

A classic financial example is overlapping return regressions. If monthly returns \(r_{t+1}\) are compounded over a horizon of \(h\) months to predict long-term performance, \(y_{t+h} = \sum_{j=1}^h r_{t+j}\), adjacent horizons overlap by \(h-1\) months, inducing a mechanical \(\mathrm{MA}(h-1)\) disturbance process.

The OLS score vector at period \(t\) is \(s_t = x_t \varepsilon_t\). The sampling variance of the normalized OLS estimator \(\sqrt{T}(\hat\beta - \beta) = \left(\frac{1}{T}\sum x_t x_t'\right)^{-1} \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum s_t\right)\) depends on the variance of the sample score sum:

\[ \Var\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \sum_{t=1}^T s_t\right) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{\tau=1}^T \E[s_t s_\tau'] = \Gamma_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{T-1} \left(1 - \frac{j}{T}\right) (\Gamma_j + \Gamma_j') \]

where \(\Gamma_j = \E[s_t s_{t-j}']\) is the autocovariance of the score at lag \(j\). If \(\{s_t\}\) is serially uncorrelated (e.g., when \(x_t\) is i.i.d.), all \(\Gamma_j = 0\) for \(j \ge 1\), and White HC0 standard errors remain asymptotically valid. However, if both \(x_t\) and \(\varepsilon_t\) are positively autocorrelated, \(\Gamma_j\) is positive definite, and omitting these lag autocovariances severely biases standard errors downward.

Long-run covariance and the Newey–West estimator

Under suitable mixing and stationarity conditions, as \(T \to \infty\), the score sum variance converges to the long-run covariance matrix:

\[ \Omega = \lim_{T \to \infty} \Var\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \sum_{t=1}^T s_t\right) = \Gamma_0 + \sum_{j=1}^\infty (\Gamma_j + \Gamma_j') \]

where \(\Gamma_0 = \E[s_t s_t']\). The asymptotic covariance of the OLS estimator is the sandwich form \(V_{\text{HAC}} = Q^{-1} \Omega Q^{-1}\), where \(Q = \E[x_t x_t']\).

A naive sample estimator of \(\Omega\) would replace expectations with sample averages up to some truncation lag \(L\):

\[ \hat\Omega_{\text{naive}} = \hat\Gamma_0 + \sum_{j=1}^L (\hat\Gamma_j + \hat\Gamma_j'), \quad \text{where } \hat\Gamma_j = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=j+1}^T e_t e_{t-j} x_t x_{t-j}' \]

However, this unweighted sample estimator has a fatal flaw: in finite samples, \(\hat\Omega_{\text{naive}}\) is not guaranteed to be positive semi-definite. Researchers frequently encounter negative variance estimates or imaginary standard errors.

The Bartlett kernel and positive semi-definiteness

To solve the non-positive definiteness of unweighted estimators, heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) standard errors apply kernel weighting to the sample autocovariances. Newey and West (1987) proved that weighting autocovariances by the Bartlett (triangular) kernel guarantees that the resulting covariance matrix estimator is strictly positive semi-definite in any finite sample.

Definition 9.1 (Newey–West HAC Estimator)

Let \(e_t = y_t - x_t'\hat\beta\) denote the OLS residuals, and let \(\hat s_t = x_t e_t\). The Newey–West HAC estimator of the long-run covariance matrix with truncation lag \(L\) is:

\[ \hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(L) = \hat\Gamma_0 + \sum_{j=1}^L \left(1 - \frac{j}{L + 1}\right) (\hat\Gamma_j + \hat\Gamma_j') \]

where the sample autocovariance matrices are \(\hat\Gamma_j = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=j+1}^T \hat s_t \hat s_{t-j}'\). The Newey–West covariance matrix of \(\hat\beta\) is:

\[ \hat V_{\text{NW}} = (X'X)^{-1} \left(T \hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(L)\right) (X'X)^{-1} \]

Theorem 9.1 (Positive Semi-Definiteness of the Newey–West Estimator)

For any choice of data \((X, y)\) and any non-negative integer lag \(L \ge 0\), the Newey–West matrix \(\hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(L)\) is positive semi-definite:

\[ c' \hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(L) c \ge 0 \quad \forall c \in \R^k \]

Proof: For any vector \(c \in \R^k\), define the scalar process \(z_t = c'\hat s_t = c'x_t e_t\). The quadratic form is:

\[ c' \hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(L) c = \hat\gamma_0 + 2 \sum_{j=1}^L \left(1 - \frac{j}{L + 1}\right) \hat\gamma_j \]

where \(\hat\gamma_j = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=j+1}^T z_t z_{t-j}\). In spectral analysis, the Bartlett-weighted autocovariance sum corresponds to the spectral density of \(\{z_t\}\) evaluated at frequency zero using Fejér's kernel. Specifically, define the running block sum \(S_{t, L} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L+1}} \sum_{l=0}^L z_{t-l}\) (with \(z_t = 0\) for \(t \le 0\)). Then the quadratic form can be written as an exact sum of squares:

\[ c' \hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(L) c = \frac{1}{T (L + 1)} \sum_{t=1}^{T + L} \left(\sum_{l=0}^L z_{t-l}\right)^2 \ge 0 \]

Because it is a sum of squared real terms, it is non-negative for any vector \(c\). ■

Theorem 9.2 (Lag 0 Equivalence to White HC0)

When the truncation lag is set to \(L = 0\), the Newey–West HAC covariance matrix collapses identically to the White (1980) HC0 heteroskedasticity-consistent covariance matrix:

\[ \hat V_{\text{NW}}(0) \equiv \hat V_{\text{HC0}} \]

Proof: At \(L = 0\), the summation over lags \(j = 1, \dots, L\) contains zero terms. Thus \(\hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(0) = \hat\Gamma_0 = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T e_t^2 x_t x_t'\). Substituting into the sandwich formula:

\[ \hat V_{\text{NW}}(0) = (X'X)^{-1} \left(T \cdot \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T e_t^2 x_t x_t'\right) (X'X)^{-1} = (X'X)^{-1} \left(\sum_{t=1}^T e_t^2 x_t x_t'\right) (X'X)^{-1} \equiv \hat V_{\text{HC0}} \]

which establishes the result to exact machine precision. ■

Lag truncation and bandwidth selection

The choice of truncation lag \(L\) involves a fundamental bias-variance trade-off:

  • Too small \(L\): Omitted autocovariances for lags \(j > L\) introduce downward bias if serial correlation is persistent, leading to under-coverage.
  • Too large \(L\): Estimating higher-order sample autocovariances with few observations increases the sampling variance of \(\hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}\), degrading test size in finite samples.

In overlapping return regressions of horizon \(h\), the theoretical error process is \(\mathrm{MA}(h-1)\), suggesting the fixed rule \(L = h - 1\). In general time-series settings, Newey and West (1994) propose the heuristic rule of thumb:

\[ L = \left\lfloor 4 \left(\frac{T}{100}\right)^{2/9} \right\rfloor \]

which ensures that \(L \to \infty\) as \(T \to \infty\) while \(L / T \to 0\), guaranteeing asymptotic consistency.

Lab 1 · Newey-West HAC standard errors and Bartlett lag truncation

Hypothesis: (1) At truncation lag \(L = 0\), the Newey-West HAC standard error matches the White HC0 standard error to exact machine precision. (2) When regressor and disturbance both follow AR(1) processes with persistence 0.60 and 0.50, classical and White HC0 standard errors exhibit severe under-coverage (~84%), whereas Newey-West HAC with Bartlett weights restores empirical coverage toward the nominal 95% level.

"""Lab S2-L09-1: Newey-West HAC standard errors and Bartlett kernel lag truncation.

Validates:
1. Exact mathematical identity between Newey-West with lag L=0 and White (1980) HC0.
2. Coverage breakdown of classical and White standard errors under persistent AR(1) errors.
3. Coverage restoration via Newey-West HAC standard errors with Bartlett weights.

Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def compute_nw_se(
    lag: int,
    x: list[float],
    x_bar: float,
    e: list[float],
    sxx: float,
    meat_white: float,
    n: int,
) -> float:
    gamma_0 = meat_white
    sum_g = 0.0
    for j in range(1, lag + 1):
        weight = 1.0 - j / (lag + 1)
        gamma_j = sum((x[t] - x_bar) * (x[t - j] - x_bar) * e[t] * e[t - j] for t in range(j, n))
        sum_g += 2.0 * weight * gamma_j
    long_run_var = max(1e-12, gamma_0 + sum_g)
    return math.sqrt(long_run_var) / sxx


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    sample_size = 250
    replications = 2500
    beta_0 = 1.00
    beta_1 = 1.50
    rho_x = 0.60
    rho_u = 0.50
    z_crit = 1.96

    max_diff_l0 = 0.0
    cov_classical = 0
    cov_white = 0
    cov_nw0 = 0
    cov_nw4 = 0
    cov_nw8 = 0

    for _ in range(replications):
        # Generate persistent regressor x_t
        x: list[float] = [0.0] * sample_size
        cur_x = 0.0
        for t in range(sample_size):
            cur_x = rho_x * cur_x + rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            x[t] = cur_x

        # Generate persistent disturbance u_t
        u: list[float] = [0.0] * sample_size
        cur_u = 0.0
        for t in range(sample_size):
            cur_u = rho_u * cur_u + rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
            u[t] = cur_u

        # Dependent variable
        y = [beta_0 + beta_1 * xt + ut for xt, ut in zip(x, u, strict=True)]

        x_bar = sum(x) / sample_size
        y_bar = sum(y) / sample_size
        sxx = sum((xt - x_bar) ** 2 for xt in x)
        sxy = sum((xt - x_bar) * (yt - y_bar) for xt, yt in zip(x, y, strict=True))
        b1_hat = sxy / sxx
        e = [yt - (y_bar + b1_hat * (xt - x_bar)) for xt, yt in zip(x, y, strict=True)]

        # Classical OLS standard error
        s2 = sum(et**2 for et in e) / (sample_size - 2)
        se_classical = math.sqrt(s2 / sxx)

        # White HC0 standard error
        meat_white = sum(((xt - x_bar) ** 2) * (et**2) for xt, et in zip(x, e, strict=True))
        se_white = math.sqrt(meat_white) / sxx

        se_nw0 = compute_nw_se(0, x, x_bar, e, sxx, meat_white, sample_size)
        se_nw4 = compute_nw_se(4, x, x_bar, e, sxx, meat_white, sample_size)
        se_nw8 = compute_nw_se(8, x, x_bar, e, sxx, meat_white, sample_size)

        diff = abs(se_nw0 - se_white)
        if diff > max_diff_l0:
            max_diff_l0 = diff

        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_classical <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_classical:
            cov_classical += 1
        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_white <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_white:
            cov_white += 1
        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_nw0 <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_nw0:
            cov_nw0 += 1
        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_nw4 <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_nw4:
            cov_nw4 += 1
        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_nw8 <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_nw8:
            cov_nw8 += 1

    rate_classical = cov_classical / replications
    rate_white = cov_white / replications
    rate_nw0 = cov_nw0 / replications
    rate_nw4 = cov_nw4 / replications
    rate_nw8 = cov_nw8 / replications

    print(f"Max abs diff |NW(L=0) - White HC0|: {max_diff_l0:.2e}")
    print("Estimator            | Nominal | Empirical Coverage | Under-coverage")
    print("-" * 68)
    print(f"Classical OLS        |  0.9500 |             {rate_classical:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_classical:+.4f}")
    print(f"White HC0            |  0.9500 |             {rate_white:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_white:+.4f}")
    print(f"Newey-West HAC (L=0) |  0.9500 |             {rate_nw0:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_nw0:+.4f}")
    print(f"Newey-West HAC (L=4) |  0.9500 |             {rate_nw4:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_nw4:+.4f}")
    print(f"Newey-West HAC (L=8) |  0.9500 |             {rate_nw8:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_nw8:+.4f}")


main()

Where the canon breaks

Newey–West HAC standard errors are widely used, but they break down under specific time-series dynamics:

  • Unit roots and non-stationarity: If regressors or disturbances follow integrated \(I(1)\) processes, the autocovariance sequence does not decay, and the long-run covariance \(\Omega\) does not exist. Regressions become spurious (Granger & Newbold 1974), and HAC t-statistics diverge at rate \(\sqrt{T}\).
  • Strong persistence and small sample sizes: When AR(1) persistence is close to unity (\(\rho \ge 0.95\)), the Bartlett kernel requires very large \(L\) to capture long-range dependence. In finite samples (\(T \le 200\)), HAC estimators remain severely biased downward.
  • Fixed-\(b\) asymptotic distortion: When \(L\) is chosen as a fixed fraction of sample size (\(b = L/T \in (0, 1]\)), standard Gaussian asymptotic critical values fail. Kiefer and Vogelsang (2005) show that the limiting distribution of the HAC t-statistic depends nonlinearly on the kernel shape and requires non-standard fixed-\(b\) critical values.
  • Cross-sectional clustering in panels: In panel datasets, Newey–West corrects for serial correlation along the time dimension for a single unit, but does not account for cross-sectional correlation across multiple firms at the same time period. Panel cluster-robust estimators are required.

Misconceptions

  1. Autocorrelation in regression errors causes OLS point estimates to be biased.

    If the orthogonality condition \(\E[\varepsilon_t | X] = 0\) holds across all time periods (strict exogeneity), OLS point estimates remain strictly unbiased and consistent. Autocorrelation compromises only estimator efficiency and the validity of classical standard errors.

  2. Serial correlation in disturbances always invalidates White HC0 standard errors.

    White HC0 is invalid only if the score vector \(s_t = x_t \varepsilon_t\) is serially correlated. If the regressor \(x_t\) is an i.i.d. sequence, \(\E[s_t s_{t-j}'] = \E[x_t x_{t-j}'] \E[\varepsilon_t \varepsilon_{t-j}] = 0\) for \(j \ne 0\). In this case, White HC0 standard errors remain asymptotically consistent even if disturbances \(\varepsilon_t\) follow an AR(1) process.

  3. Increasing the lag truncation \(L\) always improves Newey–West standard error estimation.

    While increasing \(L\) reduces truncation bias from omitted higher-order autocovariances, it increases estimation variance because higher-order sample autocovariances \(\hat\Gamma_j\) are estimated with fewer data pairs and higher noise. Optimal bandwidth rules balance this bias-variance trade-off.

Practice problems

Mechanics

Calculate the Bartlett kernel weights \(w_j = 1 - \frac{j}{L+1}\) for lags \(j = 1, 2, 3, 4\) when the truncation lag is set to \(L = 4\).

Solution

For \(L = 4\), the denominator is \(L + 1 = 5\). The Bartlett weights are: \(w_1 = 1 - 1/5 = 4/5 = 0.80\); \(w_2 = 1 - 2/5 = 3/5 = 0.60\); \(w_3 = 1 - 3/5 = 2/5 = 0.40\); \(w_4 = 1 - 4/5 = 1/5 = 0.20\). At \(j \ge 5\), the weights are zero.

For a sample of \(T = 300\) monthly observations, compute the suggested Newey–West rule-of-thumb truncation lag \(L = \lfloor 4 (T/100)^{2/9} \rfloor\).

Solution

Compute \(T / 100 = 300 / 100 = 3.0\). Then \(3.0^{2/9} = 3.0^{0.2222} \approx 1.2765\). Multiplying by 4 yields \(4 \times 1.2765 \approx 5.106\). Taking the floor gives \(L = 5\).

Verify mathematically that for \(L = 0\), the Newey–West long-run variance \(\hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(0)\) contains only \(\hat\Gamma_0\).

Solution

The definition of the Newey–West estimator is \(\hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(L) = \hat\Gamma_0 + \sum_{j=1}^L \left(1 - \frac{j}{L+1}\right) (\hat\Gamma_j + \hat\Gamma_j')\). When \(L = 0\), the upper index of the summation is smaller than the lower index (\(1 > 0\)), so the summation is empty and evaluates to zero. Therefore, \(\hat\Omega_{\text{NW}}(0) = \hat\Gamma_0 = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T e_t^2 x_t x_t'\).

Proof

Prove that for an AR(1) process \(z_t = \rho z_{t-1} + \eta_t\) with \(|\rho| < 1\) and \(\Var(\eta_t) = \sigma_\eta^2\), the theoretical long-run variance is \(\frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{(1 - \rho)^2}\).

Solution

The autocovariance of an AR(1) process is \(\gamma_j = \frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{1 - \rho^2} \rho^j\). The long-run variance is \(\Omega = \gamma_0 + 2 \sum_{j=1}^\infty \gamma_j = \frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{1 - \rho^2} \left[1 + 2 \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^j\right]\). The geometric series evaluates to \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^j = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}\). Thus, \(\Omega = \frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{1 - \rho^2} \left[1 + \frac{2\rho}{1 - \rho}\right] = \frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{1 - \rho^2} \left[\frac{1 + \rho}{1 - \rho}\right] = \frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{(1 - \rho)(1 + \rho)} \frac{1 + \rho}{1 - \rho} = \frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{(1 - \rho)^2}\).

Prove that the unweighted sample autocovariance estimator \(\hat\Omega = \hat\Gamma_0 + \hat\Gamma_1 + \hat\Gamma_1'\) can have negative eigenvalues in finite samples.

Solution

Consider a univariate sample where \(\hat\gamma_0 = 1\) and \(\hat\gamma_1 = -0.60\). The unweighted estimator is \(\hat\Omega = \hat\gamma_0 + 2 \hat\gamma_1 = 1 + 2(-0.60) = -0.20 < 0\). Because variance cannot be negative, this unweighted estimator fails. Under Bartlett weights with \(L = 1\), \(w_1 = 1 - 1/2 = 0.50\), yielding \(\hat\Omega_{\text{NW}} = 1 + 2(0.50)(-0.60) = 1 - 0.60 = 0.40 > 0\).

Show that if the regressor \(x_t\) is i.i.d. with mean zero, the score autocovariances \(\Gamma_j = \E[x_t x_{t-j}' \varepsilon_t \varepsilon_{t-j}]\) are identically zero for all \(j \ne 0\), even if \(\varepsilon_t\) is serially correlated.

Solution

Assume \(x_t\) is strictly independent across time. For any \(j \ne 0\), \(x_t\) is independent of \(x_{t-j}\) and \(\varepsilon_{t-j}\). By the law of iterated expectations, \(\E[x_t x_{t-j}' \varepsilon_t \varepsilon_{t-j}] = \E[x_t \varepsilon_t] \E[x_{t-j}' \varepsilon_{t-j}] = 0 \cdot 0 = 0\). Because all cross-lag score covariances vanish, the long-run score covariance collapses to \(\Omega = \Gamma_0\), rendering White HC0 asymptotically valid.

Empirical

In long-horizon predictive regressions of annual stock returns on the dividend-price ratio using monthly data (\(h = 12\)), what truncation lag \(L\) is required by the Hansen-Hodrick / Newey-West framework?

Solution

Because annual returns compounded from monthly observations overlap by 11 months, the forecast error induces an \(\mathrm{MA}(11)\) autocorrelation structure under the null hypothesis of market efficiency. Researchers set the Newey–West truncation lag to at least \(L = h - 1 = 12 - 1 = 11\) (often \(L = 12\) to \(18\) to allow for residual persistence in the dividend-price ratio).

A researcher regresses 10-year Treasury bond yields on inflation. The OLS t-statistic with classical standard errors is 6.50, but with Newey–West HAC standard errors (\(L = 12\)) it drops to 2.10. Explain the economic and econometric reasons for this dramatic reduction.

Solution

Bond yields and inflation are both highly persistent macroeconomic time series with first-order autocorrelation exceeding 0.90. This creates massive positive score autocorrelation (\(\Gamma_j > 0\) for dozens of lags). Classical OLS treats all \(T\) observations as independent innovations, underestimating the variance of \(\hat\beta\) by a factor of \((6.50 / 2.10)^2 \approx 9.6\). HAC standard errors correct for this effective sample size reduction.

Why do empirical researchers often use the Parzen or Quadratic Spectral (QS) kernel instead of the Bartlett kernel in spectral GMM estimation?

Solution

Andrews (1991) proved that under an asymptotic mean squared error criterion, the Quadratic Spectral kernel has an asymptotic optimal rate of convergence that strictly dominates the Bartlett kernel. The Bartlett kernel has an asymptotic MSE rate of \(\mathcal{O}(T^{-2/3})\), whereas the QS kernel achieves \(\mathcal{O}(T^{-4/5})\) due to smoother spectral tapering.

Research

Explain the "fixed-\(b\)" asymptotic framework of Kiefer and Vogelsang (2005) and contrast it with traditional small-\(b\) asymptotics where \(L/T \to 0\).

Solution

Traditional asymptotics assume \(L \to \infty\) while \(b = L/T \to 0\), yielding standard Gaussian limits but severe finite-sample size distortions. Kiefer and Vogelsang model \(b = L/T \in (0, 1]\) as a fixed constant as \(T \to \infty\). Under fixed-\(b\), the HAC covariance estimator converges to a random matrix functional of Brownian motion rather than a constant, generating non-standard test distributions with heavier tails that accurately reflect estimation variability in \(\hat\Omega\).

In return predictability regressions, how does Newey–West standard error adjustment interact with the Stambaugh (1999) small-sample bias?

Solution

Newey–West HAC standard errors address only the variance of the estimator under serial correlation; they do not remove the finite-sample expectation bias of the point estimate \(\hat\beta\). When predictive regressors are persistent and endogenous (\(\Cov(u_t, v_t) < 0\)), \(\hat\beta\) is biased upward by Stambaugh bias regardless of whether Newey–West standard errors are applied, requiring simulation-based or analytical bias corrections.

Describe Andrews' (1991) automatic bandwidth selection procedure for HAC covariance estimation using univariate AR(1) plug-in models.

Solution

Andrews models each component score \(s_{ti}\) as an auxiliary AR(1) process \(s_{ti} = \rho_i s_{t-1, i} + \eta_{ti}\). Estimating \(\hat\rho_i\) and innovation variance \(\hat\sigma_i^2\) provides parametric estimates of the spectral density at zero and its normalized second derivative. These plug-in estimates determine the optimal asymptotic bandwidth parameter \(\hat\alpha(q)\), eliminating arbitrary manual selection of the truncation lag \(L\).

Retrieval quiz

  1. Why cannot sample autocovariances \(\hat\Gamma_j\) be summed without weights to estimate the long-run covariance matrix?

    Options

    Unweighted autocovariance sums can yield negative eigenvalues and negative estimated variances (Section Long-run covariance).

  2. What kernel weighting does the Newey–West (1987) estimator use to guarantee positive semi-definiteness?

    Options

    Newey and West (1987) proved that Bartlett triangular weights ensure positive semi-definiteness for any sample data (Theorem 9.1).

  3. What is the relationship between the Newey–West HAC estimator at lag \(L = 0\) and the White (1980) HC0 estimator?

    Options

    At \(L = 0\), all cross-lag terms vanish, leaving only \(\hat\Gamma_0\), which is identical to White HC0 (Theorem 9.2 and Lab 1).

  4. In a Newey–West estimator with truncation lag \(L = 3\), what weight is assigned to the first lag autocovariance \(\hat\Gamma_1\)?

    \(w_1 = 1 - 1 / (3 + 1) = 1 - 1/4 = 3/4 = 0.75\) (Definition 9.1).

  5. When estimating a predictive regression with 12-month overlapping returns, what is the natural truncation lag \(L\)?

    Options

    12-month overlapping returns induce an \(\mathrm{MA}(11)\) error process, making \(L = h - 1 = 11\) the natural lag truncation (Problem E1).

  6. Under what condition do serially correlated disturbances NOT cause White HC0 standard errors to fail?

    Options

    If regressors are serially uncorrelated, score autocovariances \(\E[x_t x_{t-j}' \varepsilon_t \varepsilon_{t-j}]\) are zero for all \(j \ne 0\), preserving White HC0 validity (Misconception 2).

  7. What happens to OLS regressions when variables follow integrated \(I(1)\) unit-root processes?

    Options

    Unit roots violate stationarity, causing the long-run covariance to fail to converge and creating spurious regressions (Section Where the canon breaks).

  8. In the Kiefer–Vogelsang fixed-\(b\) asymptotic framework, what is held fixed as \(T \to \infty\)?

    Options

    Fixed-\(b\) asymptotics fix \(b = L/T \in (0, 1]\), leading to non-standard asymptotic distributions that reflect estimation error in \(\hat\Omega\) (Problem R1).
