Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors

Cross-sectional asset returns and corporate financial metrics rarely exhibit constant conditional variance. Mega-cap technology equities experience fundamentally different return volatility than micro-cap biotech firms, and distress risk scales nonlinearly with debt ratios. When conditional heteroskedasticity is present, ordinary least squares parameter estimates remain unbiased and consistent, but classical homoskedastic standard errors collapse. In empirical asset pricing, ignoring heteroskedasticity inflates t-statistics and leads to spurious discoveries of non-existent trading anomalies. Heteroskedasticity-consistent covariance estimators restore asymptotically exact inference without requiring researchers to specify an arbitrary parametric model for variance.

The heteroskedasticity problem in linear regression

Consider the classical linear regression model in matrix form:

\[ y = X\beta + \varepsilon, \quad \E[\varepsilon | X] = 0 \]

where \(X \in \R^{n \times k}\) has full column rank \(k\). In Lesson 04, the Gauss-Markov theorem assumed spherical disturbances, \(\Var(\varepsilon | X) = \sigma^2 I_n\). Under conditional heteroskedasticity, the disturbance covariance matrix is diagonal but non-scalar:

\[ \Var(\varepsilon | X) = \Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2, \dots, \sigma_n^2) \]

where \(\sigma_i^2 = \Var(\varepsilon_i | x_i) = \E[\varepsilon_i^2 | x_i]\) varies across observations as an unknown function of the regressors.

Because \(\E[\varepsilon | X] = 0\), the OLS estimator \(\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\) remains strictly unbiased and consistent:

\[ \E[\hat\beta | X] = \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X' \E[\varepsilon | X] = \beta \]

However, the true finite-sample conditional covariance matrix of \(\hat\beta\) becomes:

\[ \Var(\hat\beta | X) = (X'X)^{-1} X' \Var(\varepsilon | X) X (X'X)^{-1} = (X'X)^{-1} \left(\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2 x_i x_i'\right) (X'X)^{-1} \]

The classical variance estimator \(s^2 (X'X)^{-1}\), where \(s^2 = \frac{e'e}{n-k}\), estimates \(\sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}\). If \(\sigma_i^2\) correlates positively with the squared regressors \(x_i^2\) (common in financial data where volatile assets have extreme characteristic loadings), \(s^2 (X'X)^{-1}\) systematically underestimates the true sampling variability. As demonstrated in computational simulations, nominal 95% confidence intervals based on classical standard errors can achieve less than 70% empirical coverage.

The White (1980) HC0 sandwich estimator

To conduct valid inference without knowing the functional form of \(\sigma_i^2 = \Var(\varepsilon_i | x_i)\), Halbert White proposed heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors (also known as Eicker–Huber–White or HC0 standard errors). White (1980) proved that consistent estimation of the asymptotic covariance matrix does not require consistent estimation of each individual variance \(\sigma_i^2\); it requires only consistent estimation of the average score covariance matrix \(\Omega = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2 x_i x_i'\).

Definition 8.1 (White HC0 Covariance Matrix Estimator)

Let \(e_i = y_i - x_i'\hat\beta\) denote the OLS residuals. The White HC0 covariance matrix estimator is:

\[ \hat V_{\text{HC0}} = (X'X)^{-1} \left(\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 x_i x_i'\right) (X'X)^{-1} \]

In matrix notation, defining \(\hat\Sigma_0 = \operatorname{diag}(e_1^2, e_2^2, \dots, e_n^2)\):

\[ \hat V_{\text{HC0}} = (X'X)^{-1} X' \hat\Sigma_0 X (X'X)^{-1} \]

Theorem 8.1 (Consistency of White HC0 Estimator)

Under Assumption 7.1 with finite eighth moments for \((x_i, \varepsilon_i)\),

\[ n \hat V_{\text{HC0}} = \left(\frac{1}{n} X'X\right)^{-1} \left(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 x_i x_i'\right) \left(\frac{1}{n} X'X\right)^{-1} \xrightarrow{p} Q^{-1} \Omega Q^{-1} \]

Proof: We already have \(\frac{1}{n} X'X \xrightarrow{p} Q\). It remains to establish that \(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 x_i x_i' - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \varepsilon_i^2 x_i x_i' \xrightarrow{p} 0\). Notice that \(e_i = \varepsilon_i - x_i'(\hat\beta - \beta)\). Squaring both sides:

\[ e_i^2 = \varepsilon_i^2 - 2 \varepsilon_i x_i'(\hat\beta - \beta) + (\hat\beta - \beta)' x_i x_i' (\hat\beta - \beta) \]

Multiplying by \(x_i x_i'\) and averaging over \(i = 1, \dots, n\):

\[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 x_i x_i' = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \varepsilon_i^2 x_i x_i' - 2 \sum_{j=1}^k (\hat\beta_j - \beta_j) \left[\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \varepsilon_i x_{ij} x_i x_i'\right] + \sum_{j=1}^k \sum_{l=1}^k (\hat\beta_j - \beta_j)(\hat\beta_l - \beta_l) \left[\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_{ij} x_{il} x_i x_i'\right] \]

By Khinchin's WLLN, \(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \varepsilon_i^2 x_i x_i' \xrightarrow{p} \Omega\). By OLS consistency, \(\hat\beta - \beta \xrightarrow{p} 0\). The terms inside the brackets converge in probability to finite moment expectations by the law of large numbers. Applying Slutsky's theorem, the second and third terms vanish in probability to zero:

\[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 x_i x_i' \xrightarrow{p} \Omega \]

By continuity of matrix products and inversion, \(n \hat V_{\text{HC0}} \xrightarrow{p} Q^{-1} \Omega Q^{-1}\). ■

Finite-sample corrections: HC1, HC2, and HC3

Although HC0 is asymptotically consistent, it exhibits substantial downward bias in small samples (\(n < 250\)), especially in the presence of high-leverage observations. Because OLS residuals minimize \(\sum e_i^2\), they are systematically smaller than the true disturbances \(\varepsilon_i\): \(\E[e_i^2 | X] = \sigma_i^2 (1 - h_{ii})\) under homoskedasticity, where \(h_{ii}\) is the \(i\)-th diagonal element of the hat matrix \(H = X(X'X)^{-1}X'\).

To remediate small-sample under-coverage, three standard finite-sample variants are used:

Estimator Formula for Diagonal Weight \(\hat\sigma_i^2\) Properties and Motivation
HC0 \(e_i^2\) Original White (1980) asymptotic estimator; downward-biased in small samples.
HC1 \(\frac{n}{n-k} e_i^2\) Degrees-of-freedom scaling; standard Stata default (vce(robust)).
HC2 \(\frac{e_i^2}{1 - h_{ii}}\) Exactly unbiased for \(\Var(\hat\beta | X)\) when disturbances are homoskedastic.
HC3 \(\frac{e_i^2}{(1 - h_{ii})^2}\) Jackknife covariance approximation; recommended by MacKinnon and White (1985) for \(n < 250\).

The leverage score satisfies \(0 \le h_{ii} \le 1\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^n h_{ii} = k\). For high-leverage observations where \(h_{ii}\) is large, HC2 and HC3 scale up the squared residual aggressively, preventing outlying points in design space from artificially compressing standard errors.

The White specification test for heteroskedasticity

Before applying robust standard errors, empirical researchers often test whether heteroskedasticity is present. The White (1980) test evaluates the null hypothesis of homoskedasticity:

\[ H_0: \E[\varepsilon_i^2 | x_i] = \sigma^2 \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_1: \E[\varepsilon_i^2 | x_i] = g(x_i) \]

Under \(H_0\), the squared residuals \(e_i^2\) should be uncorrelated with the regressors, their squares, and their mutual cross-products.

Algorithm 8.1 (White Specification Test)

  1. Estimate the primary regression \(y_i = x_i'\beta + \varepsilon_i\) by OLS and compute residuals \(e_i\).
  2. Run an auxiliary OLS regression of squared residuals \(e_i^2\) on a constant, all distinct regressors \(x_{ij}\), their squares \(x_{ij}^2\), and cross-products \(x_{ij} x_{il}\) (\(j < l\)): \[ e_i^2 = \alpha_0 + \sum_{j=1}^k \alpha_j x_{ij} + \sum_{j=1}^k \gamma_j x_{ij}^2 + \sum_{j < l} \delta_{jl} x_{ij} x_{il} + u_i \]
  3. Calculate the coefficient of determination \(R_{\text{aux}}^2\) from this auxiliary regression.
  4. Compute the Lagrange Multiplier test statistic: \[ \mathrm{LM} = n R_{\text{aux}}^2 \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(P) \] where \(P\) is the number of non-redundant regressors in the auxiliary regression (excluding the intercept).
  5. Reject \(H_0\) if \(\mathrm{LM} > \chi_{1-\alpha}^2(P)\).

Lab 1 · Classical versus White HC0 coverage under heteroskedasticity

Hypothesis: When disturbance variance scales quadratically with regressor magnitude (\(\sigma_i = 1 + 2 x_i^2\)), classical OLS standard errors produce severe under-coverage (empirical coverage under 70%), whereas White (1980) HC0 standard errors achieve nominal 95% coverage within 2 binomial standard errors.

"""Lab S2-L08-1: Classical versus White HC0 coverage under heteroskedasticity.

Demonstrates the breakdown of homoskedastic OLS standard errors when error variance
increases with regressor magnitude, and validates the nominal 95% coverage of
White (1980) heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n = 200
    replications = 3000
    beta_0 = 1.00
    beta_1 = 2.00
    z_crit = 1.96

    cov_classical = 0
    cov_hc0 = 0

    for _ in range(replications):
        x = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n)]
        x_bar = sum(x) / n
        sxx = sum((xi - x_bar) ** 2 for xi in x)

        # Strongly heteroskedastic error: sigma_i = 1.0 + 2.0 * x_i^2
        errors = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0 + 2.0 * xi**2) for xi in x]
        y = [beta_0 + beta_1 * xi + ei for xi, ei in zip(x, errors, strict=True)]
        y_bar = sum(y) / n
        sxy = sum((xi - x_bar) * (yi - y_bar) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True))
        b1_hat = sxy / sxx
        e = [yi - (y_bar + b1_hat * (xi - x_bar)) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True)]

        # Classical homoskedastic standard error
        s2 = sum(ei**2 for ei in e) / (n - 2)
        se_classical = math.sqrt(s2 / sxx)

        # White (1980) HC0 heteroskedasticity-robust standard error
        meat = sum(((xi - x_bar) ** 2) * (ei**2) for xi, ei in zip(x, e, strict=True))
        se_hc0 = math.sqrt(meat) / sxx

        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_classical <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_classical:
            cov_classical += 1
        if b1_hat - z_crit * se_hc0 <= beta_1 <= b1_hat + z_crit * se_hc0:
            cov_hc0 += 1

    rate_classical = cov_classical / replications
    rate_hc0 = cov_hc0 / replications

    print("Estimator     | Nominal | Empirical Coverage | Under-coverage")
    print("-" * 59)
    print(f"Classical OLS |  0.9500 |             {rate_classical:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_classical:+.4f}")
    print(f"White HC0     |  0.9500 |             {rate_hc0:.4f} |        {0.95 - rate_hc0:+.4f}")


main()
Estimator     | Nominal | Empirical Coverage | Under-coverage
-----------------------------------------------------------
Classical OLS |  0.9500 |             0.6577 |        +0.2923
White HC0     |  0.9500 |             0.9420 |        +0.0080

Under severe heteroskedasticity, classical homoskedastic standard errors suffer catastrophic breakdown, capturing the true parameter in only 65.8% of replications for a nominal 95% interval. In contrast, White HC0 standard errors restore asymptotically valid coverage to 94.2%, confirming the necessity of sandwich covariance estimators in financial data.

Where the canon breaks

While White standard errors are robust to arbitrary conditional heteroskedasticity, they break down in specific structural environments:

  • Serial correlation: White HC0 assumes that disturbances across observations are mutually uncorrelated (\(\E[\varepsilon_i \varepsilon_j | X] = 0\) for \(i \ne j\)). In time-series return data with persistent volatility or overlapping returns, the off-diagonal elements of \(\Sigma\) are non-zero. White standard errors are inconsistent and typically underestimate standard errors; Newey–West HAC standard errors must be used instead.
  • Cluster correlation: In panel datasets where observations are grouped by firm, industry, or country, errors within clusters share unobserved shocks. White HC0 treats all observations as independent, failing to account for intra-cluster correlation. Clustered standard errors (CR0) are required.
  • High leverage points: When design matrix leverage points satisfy \(h_{ii} \to 1\), the sample score fails to satisfy the Lindeberg condition. HC0 standard errors exhibit extreme downward bias; HC3 or jackknife resampling is strictly required.
  • High-dimensional regressors (\(k/n \to c > 0\)): In modern factor pricing models where the number of test assets or factors \(k\) is a non-trivial fraction of sample size \(n\), Cattaneo, Jansson, and Newey (2018) prove that White HC0 is inconsistent, requiring many-regressor corrections.

Misconceptions

  1. Heteroskedasticity causes OLS parameter estimates to be biased.

    Unbiasedness of the OLS estimator depends solely on the orthogonality condition \(\E[\varepsilon | X] = 0\). Non-constant error variance affects only the variance-covariance matrix of \(\hat\beta\) and the efficiency of the estimator, leaving point estimates completely unbiased.

  2. White HC0 standard errors are always larger than classical homoskedastic standard errors.

    White standard errors can be smaller than classical standard errors. If the conditional variance \(\sigma_i^2\) is negatively correlated with \(x_i^2\) (observations with large regressor values have smaller error variance), classical OLS overestimates parameter variance, and White standard errors are strictly smaller.

  3. Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) is always preferred over OLS with White standard errors.

    FGLS achieves Gauss-Markov efficiency only if the skedastic model for \(\sigma_i^2 = \Var(\varepsilon_i | x_i)\) is correctly specified. If the variance function is misspecified, FGLS estimates can be less efficient than OLS, and under minor endogeneity, FGLS becomes inconsistent while OLS remains consistent.

Practice problems

Mechanics

In a univariate regression through the origin \(y_i = \beta x_i + \varepsilon_i\), express the White HC0 variance estimator for \(\hat\beta\) in terms of scalar summations.

Solution

For univariate regression through the origin, \(X'X = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\). The meat of the sandwich is \(\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 x_i^2\). The White HC0 variance estimator is \(\hat V_{\text{HC0}}(\hat\beta) = (X'X)^{-1} \left(\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 x_i^2\right) (X'X)^{-1} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2 e_i^2}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\right)^2}\).

Given a sample of \(n = 100\) observations and \(k = 5\) regressors, calculate the multiplicative factor that converts an HC0 covariance matrix into an HC1 covariance matrix.

Solution

The HC1 adjustment factor is \(\frac{n}{n-k}\). For \(n = 100\) and \(k = 5\), the factor is \(\frac{100}{100 - 5} = \frac{100}{95} \approx 1.05263\). All HC0 standard errors are multiplied by \(\sqrt{1.05263} \approx 1.026\).

An observation has leverage score \(h_{ii} = 0.20\) and residual \(e_i = 1.6\). Calculate the effective squared error contribution to the meat matrix under HC0, HC2, and HC3.

Solution

For HC0, \(\hat\sigma_i^2 = e_i^2 = 1.6^2 = 2.56\). For HC2, \(\hat\sigma_i^2 = \frac{e_i^2}{1 - h_{ii}} = \frac{2.56}{0.80} = 3.20\). For HC3, \(\hat\sigma_i^2 = \frac{e_i^2}{(1 - h_{ii})^2} = \frac{2.56}{0.80^2} = \frac{2.56}{0.64} = 4.00\).

Proof

Prove that under homoskedasticity (\(\sigma_i^2 = \sigma^2\) for all \(i\)), the expectation of the HC2 residual variance is exactly \(\sigma^2\).

Solution

Residuals satisfy \(e = (I - H)\varepsilon\), where \(H = X(X'X)^{-1}X'\) is symmetric and idempotent. Under homoskedasticity, \(\Var(e | X) = (I - H)\sigma^2(I - H)' = (I - H)\sigma^2\). For the \(i\)-th diagonal element, \(\E[e_i^2 | X] = \sigma^2 (1 - h_{ii})\). Therefore, \(\E\left[\left. \frac{e_i^2}{1 - h_{ii}} \right| X\right] = \frac{\sigma^2(1 - h_{ii})}{1 - h_{ii}} = \sigma^2\), establishing exact unbiasedness.

Show that the difference between the true asymptotic variance \(Q^{-1}\Omega Q^{-1}\) and the classical homoskedastic variance \(\sigma^2 Q^{-1}\) depends on the covariance between \(\sigma_i^2\) and \(x_i x_i'\).

Solution

By definition, \(\Omega = \E[\sigma_i^2 x_i x_i'] = \E[(\sigma_i^2 - \sigma^2 + \sigma^2) x_i x_i'] = \sigma^2 \E[x_i x_i'] + \E[(\sigma_i^2 - \sigma^2) x_i x_i'] = \sigma^2 Q + \Cov(\sigma_i^2, x_i x_i')\). Pre- and post-multiplying by \(Q^{-1}\) yields \(Q^{-1}\Omega Q^{-1} - \sigma^2 Q^{-1} = Q^{-1} \Cov(\sigma_i^2, x_i x_i') Q^{-1}\). Thus, the classical formula is asymptotically valid if and only if \(\Cov(\sigma_i^2, x_i x_i') = 0\).

Prove that the auxiliary regression in the White test does not require including the third or fourth powers of regressors to test for general heteroskedasticity.

Solution

Under the null hypothesis of homoskedasticity, \(\Var(\varepsilon_i | x_i) = \sigma^2\) is constant. A second-order Taylor expansion of any smooth conditional variance function \(\sigma^2(x_i)\) around \(\E[x_i]\) yields \(\sigma^2(x) \approx \alpha_0 + \sum \alpha_j x_j + \sum \gamma_j x_j^2 + \sum_{j < l} \delta_{jl} x_j x_l\). Testing that all linear, quadratic, and interaction coefficients are zero tests for any second-order curvature in conditional variance without requiring higher-order moment assumptions.

Empirical

In a cross-sectional regression of stock returns on market capitalization and book-to-market ratios, why do small-cap stocks induce heteroskedasticity?

Solution

Small-cap firms have higher idiosyncratic volatility, lower liquidity, and more volatile fundamentals than large-cap firms. Consequently, residual return variance \(\sigma_i^2\) is substantially higher for small firms than large firms. Because firm size is directly included in the regression, \(\sigma_i^2\) is systematically related to the regressor \(x_i\), violating homoskedasticity.

A quant researcher finds that a trading strategy's alpha has a classical t-statistic of 2.45, but the White HC0 t-statistic is 1.78. Interpret this discrepancy and state the appropriate investment conclusion.

Solution

The substantial drop in t-statistic indicates that high strategy returns coincided with high-volatility periods or extreme factor loadings, leading classical OLS to underestimate standard errors by approximately 27%. Under White HC0 standard errors, the alpha is not statistically significant at the 5% level (\(t = 1.78 < 1.96\)), suggesting that the apparent anomaly is likely spurious noise.

Why does MacKinnon and White (1985) recommend HC3 over HC0 and HC1 when analyzing cross-sectional corporate datasets with sample sizes around \(n = 100\)?

Solution

In corporate financial datasets of moderate size (\(n \approx 100\)), corporate actions or superstar firms create severe design matrix leverage (\(h_{ii} > 0.10\)). MacKinnon and White (1985) show through extensive Monte Carlo simulations that HC0 and HC1 suffer from severe downward size distortion (rejection rates of 10%–15% at nominal 5%), whereas HC3 effectively mimics the jackknife estimator, maintaining empirical test size close to 5%.

Research

Explain the incidental parameter problem in HC0 estimation when the number of regressors grows proportionally with sample size (\(k/n \to c \in (0, 1)\)).

Solution

When \(k/n \to c > 0\), the average leverage \(\bar h = k/n\) does not vanish. Cattaneo, Jansson, and Newey (2018) prove that residuals \(e_i\) remain systematically smaller than true disturbances \(\varepsilon_i\), causing White HC0 and HC1 covariance estimators to be inconsistent and severely underestimate asymptotic variance. A modified degrees-of-freedom and leverage adjustment is required to achieve consistency under many regressors.

How does WLS (Weighted Least Squares) compare with OLS with White standard errors when the conditional variance model \(\sigma_i^2 = \exp(z_i'\gamma)\) is slightly misspecified?

Solution

If the skedastic function is misspecified, WLS minimizes a weighted sum of squares with suboptimal weights. While WLS remains consistent if \(\E[\varepsilon_i | x_i] = 0\), its standard errors will be biased unless robust sandwich standard errors are applied to the WLS estimator. Furthermore, misspecified WLS can have higher asymptotic variance than unweighted OLS, making OLS with HC3 standard errors more robust.

In modern asset pricing, researchers often estimate regressions using 10,000 stocks with 50 industry dummy variables. What computational algorithm efficiently computes \(\hat V_{\text{HC0}}\) without forming dense \(n \times n\) matrices?

Solution

Instead of forming the \(n \times n\) diagonal matrix \(\hat\Sigma\), the algorithm computes the meat matrix by scaling regressor rows directly: let \(\tilde X = \operatorname{diag}(e) X \in \R^{n \times k}\), so \(\tilde X_{ij} = e_i X_{ij}\). The meat is simply the matrix product \(\tilde X' \tilde X = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 x_i x_i'\). Pre- and post-multiplying by \((X'X)^{-1}\) requires \(\mathcal{O}(n k^2 + k^3)\) floating point operations and \(\mathcal{O}(n k)\) memory.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the effect of conditional heteroskedasticity on the OLS estimator \(\hat\beta\)?

    Options

    Heteroskedasticity does not violate \(\E[\varepsilon | X] = 0\), so unbiasedness and consistency hold, but efficiency fails (Section The heteroskedasticity problem).

  2. In the White HC0 covariance estimator, what replaces the unknown conditional variances \(\sigma_i^2\)?

    Options

    White (1980) proved that replacing \(\sigma_i^2\) with squared residuals \(e_i^2\) yields a consistent estimator of the average score variance \(\Omega\) (Theorem 8.1).

  3. Why does White HC0 tend to underestimate standard errors in small samples?

    Options

    Because OLS fits the sample data, residuals are mechanically compressed by the factor \(1 - h_{ii}\), inducing downward bias in HC0 (Section Finite-sample corrections).

  4. Which finite-sample robust covariance variant divides squared residuals by \((1 - h_{ii})^2\)?

    Options

    HC3 scales residuals by \((1 - h_{ii})^2\), approximating the jackknife covariance estimator (Section Finite-sample corrections).

  5. For a sample of \(n = 100\) observations and \(k = 10\) parameters, what is the degrees-of-freedom scaling factor \(n / (n-k)\) used in HC1?

    \(n / (n-k) = 100 / (100 - 10) = 100 / 90 \approx 1.111\) or 1.11 (Problem M2).

  6. What is the asymptotic distribution of the White test statistic \(\mathrm{LM} = n R_{\text{aux}}^2\) under the null hypothesis of homoskedasticity?

    Options

    The White test statistic is an asymptotic Lagrange Multiplier test distributed as \(\chi^2(P)\) (Algorithm 8.1).

  7. Can White heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors be smaller than classical homoskedastic standard errors?

    Options

    When extreme regressors have smaller error variance, classical standard errors overestimate sampling variance, so White standard errors are smaller (Misconception 2).

  8. If financial time series disturbances exhibit serial correlation, why are White HC0 standard errors invalid?

    Options

    White HC0 is diagonal, ignoring off-diagonal autocovariance terms. Autocorrelation requires Newey–West HAC standard errors (Section Where the canon breaks).
