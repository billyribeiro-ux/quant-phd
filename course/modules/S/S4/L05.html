Realized Volatility & Microstructure Noise

Continuous-Time Quadratic Variation, High-Frequency Sampling, and Volatility Signature Plots

Why It Matters

Parametric discrete-time models like GARCH treat daily variance as an unobservable latent variable inferred from low-frequency returns. High-frequency intraday prices make volatility virtually observable by summing squared intraday returns over discrete increments. However, physical trading frictions—bid-ask bounce, discrete ticks, and order processing—introduce microstructure noise that causes naive high-frequency volatility estimates to explode. Mastering quadratic variation and signature plots unlocks non-parametric volatility measurement, providing high-precision variance targets for derivatives pricing desks, statistical arbitrage engines, and intraday risk controllers.

1. Continuous-Time Foundations and Quadratic Variation

Let the frictionless log price \(p_t^* = \ln S_t^*\) follow a continuous-time jump-diffusion semimartingale defined on a filtered probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \mathbb{P})\):

\[ \begin{aligned} dp_t^* &= \mu_t \, dt + \sigma_t \, dW_t + J_t \, dq_t, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\mu_t\) is a continuous, locally bounded drift process, \(\sigma_t > 0\) is the spot volatility process, \(W_t\) is standard Brownian motion, \(dq_t\) is a counting measure with stochastic intensity \(\lambda_t\), and \(J_t\) is jump size. In the absence of jumps (\(dq_t = 0\)), the continuous-time target is the integrated variance (IV) across day \(t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} IV_t &= \int_{t-1}^t \sigma_s^2 \, ds. \end{aligned} \]

Under semimartingale theory (Andersen et al., 2003), the quadratic variation of the price path equals the integrated variance. Partition the trading day \([t-1, t]\) into \(M\) equally spaced intervals of length \(\Delta = 1/M\), recording prices at \(t_{i} = t - 1 + i\Delta\) for \(i = 0, 1, \dots, M\).

Intraday log returns are defined by \(r_{t, i} = p_{t_i}^* - p_{t_{i-1}}^*\).

Definition 5.1 · Realized Volatility and Realized Variance

The realized volatility (or realized variance, \(RV_t\)) is the non-parametric estimator defined by the sum of squared intraday increments:

\[ \begin{aligned} RV_t(M) &= \sum_{i=1}^M r_{t, i}^2. \end{aligned} \]

The realized volatility in annualized standard deviation units is given by the square root \(\sqrt{RV_t(M)}\).

Theorem 5.1 · Consistency of Realized Variance

Assumptions:

  1. Let the frictionless price path \(p_t^*\) be a continuous semimartingale with drift \(\mu_t\) and spot variance \(\sigma_t^2\).
  2. Let the partition mesh size \(\Delta = \max_i |t_i - t_{i-1}| \to 0\) as \(M \to \infty\).
  3. Observations are recorded without microstructure noise or market friction.

Conclusion: The realized variance converges in probability to the integrated variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} RV_t(M) &\xrightarrow{\;p\;} \int_{t-1}^t \sigma_s^2 \, ds = IV_t. \end{aligned} \]

Furthermore, by the Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard central limit theorem, under suitable regularity conditions:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\sqrt{M}\big(RV_t(M) - IV_t\big)}{\sqrt{2 \int_{t-1}^t \sigma_s^4 \, ds}} &\xrightarrow{\;d\;} \Normal(0, 1). \end{aligned} \]

This theorem is fundamental: it converts volatility from a latent state filtered from daily returns—as in GARCH models—into an observable quantity computed directly from trade tape data.

2. Market Microstructure Noise and Volatility Explosion

In real trading venues, recorded trade prices \(\tilde{p}_{t_i}\) do not equal frictionless equilibrium values \(p_{t_i}^*\). Discrete tick sizes, bid-ask bounce, order routing delays, and dealer inventory effects contaminate prices with microstructure noise:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{p}_{t_i} &= p_{t_i}^* + \varepsilon_{t_i}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\varepsilon_{t_i}\) is zero-mean noise with \(\Var(\varepsilon_{t_i}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2\), independent of the latent price path \(p^*\).

Theorem 5.2 · Microstructure Noise Explosion

Assumptions:

  1. The observed price is \(\tilde{p}_{t_i} = p_{t_i}^* + \varepsilon_{t_i}\).
  2. The noise innovations satisfy \(\varepsilon_{t_i} \iid (0, \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\) independent of \(p^*\).
  3. The latent increments \(r_{t, i}^* = p_{t_i}^* - p_{t_{i-1}}^*\) have \(\sum_{i=1}^M (r_{t, i}^*)^2 \approx IV_t\).

Conclusion: The observed intraday return \(\tilde{r}_{t, i} = \tilde{p}_{t_i} - \tilde{p}_{t_{i-1}} = r_{t, i}^* + (\varepsilon_{t_i} - \varepsilon_{t_{i-1}})\) satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E\big[\tilde{RV}_t(M) \mid p^*\big] &= \sum_{i=1}^M (r_{t, i}^*)^2 + 2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \approx IV_t + 2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2. \end{aligned} \]

As the sampling frequency approaches infinity (\(M \to \infty\)), the noise accumulation term \(2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \to \infty\), causing naive realized variance to diverge to infinity.

3. Volatility Signature Plots and Empirical Verification

To inspect the noise threshold empirically, Andersen et al. (2003) introduced the volatility signature plot. The signature plot charts the sample average of realized variance \(\overline{RV}(\Delta)\) against the sampling interval \(\Delta\), typically spanning from 1 second to 60 minutes.

At ultra-fine sampling intervals (\(\Delta < 30\) seconds), the signature curve shoots upward due to the \(2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2\) bid-ask bounce bias. As \(\Delta\) increases to between 5 and 15 minutes, the curve flattens into an invariant plateau where microstructure noise has decayed to negligible levels. At intervals beyond 30 minutes, the curve becomes erratic due to finite-sample discretization error. Hence, the 5-minute sampling frequency has served as the canonical rule-of-thumb across equity and foreign exchange markets.

Lab 1 · High-Frequency Realized Variance Signature

Simulating high-frequency diffusion paths with additive microstructure noise demonstrates that 1-second realized variance exhibits massive upward bias, whereas 5-minute to 15-minute sampling stabilizes on a plateau tracking true integrated variance.

"""Lab S4-L05-1: Realized volatility and microstructure noise signature plot.

SIMULATED: simulates high-frequency intraday prices with additive microstructure noise
and computes realized variance at sampling intervals from 5 seconds to 30 minutes.
Demonstrates the volatility signature plot bias: ultra-high frequency sampling is
dominated by noise variance 2*M*sigma_eta^2.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def simulate_day_path(
    n_seconds: int = 23400, sigma_day: float = 0.015, noise_std: float = 0.0002, seed: int = 42
) -> tuple[list[float], list[float]]:
    """Simulate 1 second diffusion path with additive microstructure noise."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    dt = 1.0 / n_seconds
    sigma_step = sigma_day * math.sqrt(dt)

    p_star = [0.0]
    for _ in range(n_seconds):
        p_star.append(p_star[-1] + rng.gauss(0.0, sigma_step))

    p_obs = [p + rng.gauss(0.0, noise_std) for p in p_star]
    return p_star, p_obs


def main() -> None:
    p_star, p_obs = simulate_day_path(seed=42)
    true_iv = sum((p_star[t] - p_star[t - 1]) ** 2 for t in range(1, len(p_star)))

    sampling_intervals = [5, 30, 60, 300, 900, 1800]
    print(f"True Integrated Variance (IV): {true_iv:.6e}")
    print("Interval (s) | Subsamples M | Realized Variance | Relative to IV | Noise Bias")
    print("-" * 75)

    for delta in sampling_intervals:
        sub_prices = p_obs[::delta]
        rv = sum((sub_prices[k] - sub_prices[k - 1]) ** 2 for k in range(1, len(sub_prices)))
        ratio = rv / true_iv
        bias = rv - true_iv
        print(
            f"    {delta:5d}    |     {len(sub_prices) - 1:5d}    |   {rv:.6e}    |     {ratio:.3f}x    | {bias:+.4e}"
        )


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

While realized volatility provides a model-free proxy for integrated variance, standard realized variance faces critical breakdown points in real-world trading:

  • Microstructure noise dominance at ultra-high frequencies: At sampling intervals finer than 1 minute, bid-ask bounce and discrete order ticks inject massive upward bias, invalidating naive sum-of-squares estimators and requiring two-scale or kernel corrections.
  • Overnight non-trading gaps and weekend jumps: Realized variance computed over trading hours excludes market-moving price jumps occurring during overnight hours, systematically underestimating full 24-hour portfolio return variance.
  • Non-synchronous trading in multivariate settings: When computing realized covariance across multiple assets, asynchronous trade timestamps induce the Epps effect, causing estimated covariances to artificially shrink toward zero at fine sampling intervals.

Misconceptions

  • Wrong belief: More high-frequency ticks always produce a more accurate variance estimate.

    Why it is wrong: In textbook statistics, larger sample sizes monotonically shrink estimation error. But in financial markets, increasing frequency beyond 5 minutes introduces physical market microstructure noise (bid-ask bounce). At the 1-second scale, more than 90% of observed realized variance is pure microstructure noise, not fundamental asset volatility.

  • Wrong belief: Realized variance requires normally distributed log returns.

    Why it is wrong: Realized variance is a non-parametric estimator derived from continuous semimartingale theory. It does not require normal returns, finite moments beyond the quadratic variation, or any parametric GARCH specification. It holds for general jump-diffusions with stochastic volatility.

  • Wrong belief: Microstructure noise generates positive return autocorrelation.

    Why it is wrong: Independent microstructure noise generates negative first-order return autocorrelation (\(\Cov(\tilde{r}_{t, i}, \tilde{r}_{t, i-1}) = -\sigma_\varepsilon^2 < 0\)). The mechanical bouncing of observed transactions between the bid and ask price produces alternating positive and negative price changes.

Practice Problems

Mechanics Tier

An asset has daily integrated variance \(IV = 0.000225\) (1.5% daily volatility). The market has independent microstructure noise with standard deviation \(\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.0003\). Calculate the theoretical expected realized variance \(\E[\tilde{RV}(M)]\) when sampling every second (\(M = 23,400\)) versus every 5 minutes (\(M = 78\)).

Worked Solution

The analytical expectation under independent additive noise is: \[ \begin{aligned} \E[\tilde{RV}(M)] &= IV + 2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2. \end{aligned} \] The noise variance is \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = (0.0003)^2 = 9.0 \times 10^{-8}\). For 1-second sampling (\(M = 23,400\)): \[ 2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 2 \times 23,400 \times 9.0 \times 10^{-8} = 46,800 \times 9.0 \times 10^{-8} = 0.004212. \] Hence, \(\E[\tilde{RV}(23,400)] = 0.000225 + 0.004212 = 0.004437\). The noise inflates the true variance by a factor of \(0.004437 / 0.000225 \approx 19.72\), an upward bias exceeding 1,800%! For 5-minute sampling (\(M = 78\)): \[ 2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 2 \times 78 \times 9.0 \times 10^{-8} = 156 \times 9.0 \times 10^{-8} = 0.00001404. \] Hence, \(\E[\tilde{RV}(78)] = 0.000225 + 0.000014 = 0.00023904\), which is an upward bias of only 6.2%.

Given a 6.5-hour trading day with 78 five-minute returns, the computed 5-minute realized variance is \(RV = 0.000400\). Annualize this realized variance and compute the annualized realized volatility in percent (assume 252 trading days per year).

Worked Solution

The annualized realized variance is obtained by scaling daily realized variance by the annual factor: \[ RV_{\text{annual}} = 252 \times RV_{\text{daily}} = 252 \times 0.000400 = 0.1008. \] Annualized realized volatility is the square root: \[ \sigma_{\text{annual}} = \sqrt{0.1008} \approx 0.31749 = 31.75\%. \] The asset exhibits an annualized realized volatility of 31.75%.

If the true integrated variance is \(IV = 0.000100\) and the market noise standard deviation is \(\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.0001\), find the sampling interval \(M\) at which the expected noise bias equals exactly 10% of the true integrated variance.

Worked Solution

The noise bias is \(2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2\). We require: \[ 2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 0.10 \times IV = 0.10 \times 0.000100 = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}. \] Since \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = (0.0001)^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-8}\): \[ 2 M \times 1.0 \times 10^{-8} = 1.0 \times 10^{-5} \implies 2 M = 1000 \implies M = 500. \] In a 23,400-second trading day, \(M = 500\) corresponds to a sampling interval of \(\Delta = 23,400 / 500 = 46.8\) seconds. Sampling slower than 46.8 seconds keeps noise bias below 10%.

Proof Tier

Prove that if the latent frictionless returns \(r_{t, i}^*\) are serially uncorrelated and the microstructure noise \(\varepsilon_{t, i} \iid (0, \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\) is independent of \(p^*\), the first-order autocovariance of observed returns \(\tilde{r}_{t, i}\) is exactly equal to \(-\sigma_\varepsilon^2\).

Worked Solution

The observed intraday return at step \(i\) is: \[ \tilde{r}_{t, i} = \tilde{p}_{t_i} - \tilde{p}_{t_{i-1}} = r_{t, i}^* + \varepsilon_{t_i} - \varepsilon_{t_{i-1}}. \] The adjacent lagged return is: \[ \tilde{r}_{t, i-1} = r_{t, i-1}^* + \varepsilon_{t_{i-1}} - \varepsilon_{t_{i-2}}. \] Taking the covariance between \(\tilde{r}_{t, i}\) and \(\tilde{r}_{t, i-1}\): \[ \Cov(\tilde{r}_{t, i}, \tilde{r}_{t, i-1}) = \Cov(r_{t, i}^* + \varepsilon_{t_i} - \varepsilon_{t_{i-1}}, \, r_{t, i-1}^* + \varepsilon_{t_{i-1}} - \varepsilon_{t_{i-2}}). \] Because \(r^*\) is independent of \(\varepsilon\), and \(\varepsilon\) is serially \(\iid\), all cross-terms vanish except the covariance between \(-\varepsilon_{t_{i-1}}\) and \(+\varepsilon_{t_{i-1}}\): \[ \Cov(-\varepsilon_{t_{i-1}}, \, \varepsilon_{t_{i-1}}) = -\Var(\varepsilon_{t_{i-1}}) = -\sigma_\varepsilon^2. \] For all higher lags \(h \ge 2\), the returns share no overlapping noise increments, yielding \(\Cov(\tilde{r}_{t, i}, \tilde{r}_{t, i-h}) = 0\). This proves that microstructure noise acts as an MA(1) error process with negative lag-1 autocovariance.

Prove that the expectation of observed realized variance under additive \(\iid\) noise satisfies \(\E[\tilde{RV}(M) \mid p^*] = \sum_{i=1}^M (r_{t, i}^*)^2 + 2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2\).

Worked Solution

By definition, \(\tilde{RV}(M) = \sum_{i=1}^M \tilde{r}_{t, i}^2\), where \(\tilde{r}_{t, i} = r_{t, i}^* + \Delta \varepsilon_i\) with \(\Delta \varepsilon_i = \varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i-1}\). Squaring each term: \[ \tilde{r}_{t, i}^2 = (r_{t, i}^*)^2 + 2 r_{t, i}^* \Delta \varepsilon_i + (\Delta \varepsilon_i)^2. \] Taking expectation conditional on the latent path \(p^*\): \[ \E[\tilde{r}_{t, i}^2 \mid p^*] = (r_{t, i}^*)^2 + 2 r_{t, i}^* \E[\Delta \varepsilon_i] + \E[(\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i-1})^2]. \] Since \(\E[\varepsilon_i] = 0\), \(\E[\Delta \varepsilon_i] = 0\). Since \(\varepsilon_i \iid (0, \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\), \(\E[(\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i-1})^2] = \Var(\varepsilon_i) + \Var(\varepsilon_{i-1}) = 2\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Summing over all \(i = 1, \dots, M\): \[ \E[\tilde{RV}(M) \mid p^*] = \sum_{i=1}^M (r_{t, i}^*)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^M 2\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = \sum_{i=1}^M (r_{t, i}^*)^2 + 2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2. \] This completes the proof.

Derive the first-order condition for the optimal sampling frequency \(M^*\) minimizing \(MSE(M) = 4 M^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^4 + 2 IQ M^{-1}\), where \(IQ = \int_{t-1}^t \sigma_s^4 \, ds\).

Worked Solution

Express the objective function explicitly in terms of \(M\): \[ MSE(M) = 4 M^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^4 + 2 IQ M^{-1}. \] Differentiating with respect to \(M\) and setting the derivative to zero: \[ \frac{d MSE}{d M} = 8 M \sigma_\varepsilon^4 - 2 IQ M^{-2} = 0 \implies 8 M^3 \sigma_\varepsilon^4 = 2 IQ \implies M^3 = \frac{IQ}{4 \sigma_\varepsilon^4}. \] Solving for \(M^*\): \[ M^* = \left(\frac{IQ}{4 \sigma_\varepsilon^4}\right)^{1/3}. \] The second derivative \(\frac{d^2 MSE}{d M^2} = 8 \sigma_\varepsilon^4 + 4 IQ M^{-3} > 0\) confirms a strict minimum. Thus, optimal sampling scales as \(O(\sigma_\varepsilon^{-4/3})\).

Empirical Tier

An analyst computes realized variance for Apple Inc. across various frequencies: 10-second RV is 0.001250, 1-minute RV is 0.000450, 5-minute RV is 0.000280, 15-minute RV is 0.000275, and 30-minute RV is 0.000282. What sampling frequency should be selected for model estimation and why?

Worked Solution

The signature plot values show steep inflation at 10-second (0.001250) and 1-minute (0.000450) intervals due to bid-ask bounce. Between 5 minutes (0.000280), 15 minutes (0.000275), and 30 minutes (0.000282), the estimates stabilize on a flat plateau around \(\approx 0.000280\). The 5-minute frequency should be selected because it lies at the onset of the stable plateau, filtering out microstructure noise while preserving 78 intraday observations to minimize discretization variance.

How does an econometrician test whether a time series of high-frequency price quotes exhibits significant microstructure noise without directly estimating \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\)?

Worked Solution

The econometrician computes the sample autocorrelation of high-frequency returns at lag 1, \(\hat{\rho}(1)\). Under pure diffusion without noise, frictionless returns are serially uncorrelated (\(\rho(1) = 0\)). Under additive microstructure noise, \(\rho(1) = -\sigma_\varepsilon^2 / \Var(\tilde{r}) < 0\). A statistically significant negative lag-1 sample autocorrelation confirms the presence of bid-ask bounce noise.

In foreign exchange markets that trade 24 hours per day, how does the intraday diurnal pattern (the U-shaped volatility smile throughout the day) affect the optimal choice of sampling interval?

Worked Solution

Intraday trading volume and liquidity vary drastically between market opens (e.g. London-New York overlap) and quiet periods (e.g. late Asian session). During illiquid hours, bid-ask spreads widen and tick arrivals slow down, elevating noise variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) relative to spot volatility \(\sigma_t^2\). Econometricians often adopt business-time or tick-time sampling (sampling every \(N\) transactions) rather than fixed calendar-time intervals, ensuring equal information content per interval.

Research Tier

Contrast the two-scale realized volatility (TSRV) estimator of Zhang, Mykland, and Aït-Sahalia (2005) with the standard 5-minute realized variance estimator.

Worked Solution

Standard 5-minute RV discards ~98% of tick data to avoid noise bias, resulting in a slow convergence rate of \(n^{-1/4}\). In contrast, the Two-Scale Realized Volatility (TSRV) estimator computes realized variance on two timescales: a slow subsampled scale \(\overline{RV}_{\text{slow}}\) (e.g. across \(K\) grids) and the ultra-fast tick-by-tick scale \(RV_{\text{fast}}\). By subtracting a scaled noise estimate \(\hat{\sigma}_\varepsilon^2 = RV_{\text{fast}} / (2M)\), TSRV removes noise bias completely while using all tick observations, achieving the optimal parametric convergence rate of \(n^{-1/6}\).

Explain how Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard's (2004) Bipower Variation separates continuous diffusion volatility from discrete jump variation in high-frequency price paths.

Worked Solution

Total quadratic variation equals integrated variance plus jump variation: \(QV = IV + \sum J^2\). Standard realized variance \(RV\) converges to \(QV\). Bipower variation is defined as \(BV = \frac{\pi}{2} \sum_{i=2}^M |r_{t, i}| |r_{t, i-1}|\). Because jumps are isolated discrete events, adjacent intervals \([i-1, i]\) rarely both contain a jump. As \(\Delta \to 0\), the product \(|r_{t, i}| |r_{t, i-1}|\) extinguishes the jump contribution, converging purely to integrated variance \(IV\). Hence, \(\max(RV - BV, 0)\) consistently isolates jump variance.

Analyze how the Epps effect distorts realized covariance estimation across two non-synchronously traded assets and describe how Hayashi-Yoshida (2005) resolves this issue.

Worked Solution

When two assets trade asynchronously, prices recorded on a fixed calendar grid do not reflect simultaneous quotes. At high frequencies, price innovations in one asset are paired with stale quotes in the other, biasing sample covariance towards zero (the Epps effect). Hayashi and Yoshida (2005) developed an estimator that sums products of overlapping tick intervals without calendar-time imputation: \(\sum_i \sum_j (p_{1, t_i} - p_{1, t_{i-1}})(p_{2, s_j} - p_{2, s_{j-1}}) \mathbf{1}_{\{(t_{i-1}, t_i] \cap (s_{j-1}, s_j] \ne \emptyset\}}\), which remains unbiased even as sampling frequency reaches tick level.

Diagnostic Quiz

  1. In an idealized frictionless diffusion market without jumps or noise, what is the asymptotic limit of realized variance as sampling frequency \(\Delta \to 0\)?

    By continuous semimartingale theory, realized variance converges in probability to the integrated variance (quadratic variation) of the continuous price path.

  2. What is the primary cause of the explosive upward bias observed in ultra-high-frequency realized variance estimates?

    Under additive noise \(\tilde{p} = p^* + \varepsilon\), observed squared return expectations include \(2\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Summed across \(M\) intervals, the noise bias \(2M\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) grows linearly with \(M\).

  3. On a volatility signature plot, what feature indicates an appropriate sampling frequency?

    The flat plateau indicates that the microstructure noise bias has dissipated while discretization error remains controlled.

  4. What sign does the first-order autocovariance \(\Cov(\tilde{r}_{t, i}, \tilde{r}_{t, i-1})\) exhibit under pure additive \(\iid\) microstructure noise?

    Because \(\tilde{r}_{t, i} = r_i^* + \varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i-1}\), the shared term between adjacent returns is \(-\varepsilon_{i-1}\) and \(+\varepsilon_{i-1}\), creating covariance \(-\sigma_\varepsilon^2\).

  5. Why is 5-minute sampling widely adopted as an empirical standard rather than 1-second sampling?

    5-minute sampling offers an empirical balance where the noise bias \(2 M \sigma_\varepsilon^2\) is small and discretization variance \(2IQ/M\) is manageable.

  6. How does subsampling over multiple offset grids improve realized variance estimation?

    Averaging across all shifted subgrids uses all price points without increasing the sampling frequency within each subgrid, reducing variance by approximately 33%.

  7. If a high-frequency trading desk uses 1-second realized variance directly without noise correction to calibrate its risk limit, what is the operational consequence?

    Because 1-second RV is dominated by noise variance (often 10x-20x true volatility), risk models will perceive immense phantom risk and shut down normal trading.

  8. Under Andersen et al. (2003), how does realized variance compare to parametric daily GARCH models for out-of-sample volatility forecasting?

    By summing intraday squared returns, realized variance reduces measurement error by orders of magnitude compared to squared daily returns, vastly improving out-of-sample forecast precision.
