Forecast Evaluation & QLIKE Loss

Quasi-Likelihood Scoring, Scale Invariance, and Asymmetric Penalization of Risk Underestimation

Why It Matters

Evaluating volatility models is fundamentally distinct from evaluating return forecasts because true conditional variance is an unobservable latent variable. When evaluating forecasts against noisy empirical proxies like squared returns or realized variance, standard loss metrics like Mean Squared Error (MSE) fail catastrophically: they are distorted by crisis outliers, scale-dependent, and penalize risk overestimation and underestimation symmetrically. In financial risk management, underestimating volatility causes margin blowouts and catastrophic liquidations. The Gaussian Quasi-Likelihood (QLIKE) loss function provides a mathematically rigorous, scale-invariant scoring rule that heavily penalizes variance underestimation and guarantees consistent model rankings.

1. The Latent Target Problem in Volatility Forecasting

In standard regression forecasting, the realized target \(Y_t\) is directly observed (e.g. GDP, stock price, or default rate). In volatility econometrics, the true conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2 = \Var(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})\) is never directly observed. Instead, model forecasts \(h_t\) must be evaluated against an observable proxy \(\hat{\sigma}_t^2\), such as daily squared returns \(r_t^2\) or high-frequency realized volatility \(RV_t\).

Let \(y_t\) denote the realized proxy and \(h_t\) denote the volatility forecast generated by a model at time \(t-1\). A standard statistical loss function is Mean Squared Error (MSE):

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{MSE}}(y, h) &= (y - h)^2. \end{aligned} \]

While MSE is familiar, it possesses two severe drawbacks in volatility forecasting:

  1. Extreme sensitivity to volatility levels: Because errors are squared in levels, a 50% forecast error during a market crisis (\(y=10.0, h=5.0\), error \(= 25.0\)) dominates thousands of normal trading days (\(y=0.04, h=0.02\), error \(= 0.0004\)). MSE model selection is almost entirely dictated by a handful of turbulent days.
  2. Symmetric penalization: MSE treats under-forecasting (\(h < y\)) and over-forecasting (\(h > y\)) identically in absolute terms. In quantitative finance, underestimating volatility is far more dangerous than overestimating it.

2. The Gaussian Quasi-Likelihood (QLIKE) Loss Function

To establish a loss metric rooted in probability theory, econometricians derive the loss function implied by the Gaussian conditional likelihood. If \(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \sim \Normal(0, h_t)\), the conditional log-likelihood contribution is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell_t(h_t) &= -\frac{1}{2} \ln(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2} \ln h_t - \frac{r_t^2}{2 h_t}. \end{aligned} \]

Dropping constants and multiplying by \(-2\) yields the quasi-likelihood objective. Formalized by Patton (2011), the normalized QLIKE loss function is standardized to have a minimum value of zero when the forecast is perfect.

Definition 7.1 · The QLIKE Loss Function

The QLIKE loss between a realized variance proxy \(y > 0\) and a strictly positive volatility forecast \(h > 0\) is defined as:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h) &= \frac{y}{h} - \ln\left(\frac{y}{h}\right) - 1. \end{aligned} \]

Equivalently, expressing the terms individually:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h) &= \ln h + \frac{y}{h} - (\ln y + 1). \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 7.1 · Fundamental Properties of QLIKE Loss

Assumptions:

  1. Let the target proxy \(y > 0\) and forecast \(h > 0\).
  2. Define the forecast ratio \(z = y / h > 0\).

Conclusions:

  1. Non-negativity: \(L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h) \ge 0\) for all \(y, h > 0\), with \(L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h) = 0\) if and only if \(h = y\).
  2. Scale Invariance (0-Homogeneity): For any positive scaling constant \(c > 0\): \[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{QLIKE}}(c y, c h) &= L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h). \end{aligned} \] QLIKE depends exclusively on the relative forecast ratio \(y/h\), ensuring that tranquil and crisis regimes receive balanced weight.
  3. Asymmetric Penalization: As \(h \to 0^+\) (extreme under-forecasting of volatility), \(L_{\text{QLIKE}} \to \infty\) at a linear rate \(y/h\). In contrast, as \(h \to \infty\) (extreme over-forecasting), \(L_{\text{QLIKE}}\) grows only logarithmically as \(\ln(h/y)\).
Proof of Non-negativity

Let \(g(z) = z - \ln z - 1\) for \(z \in (0, \infty)\). Taking the first derivative:

\[ \begin{aligned} g'(z) &= 1 - \frac{1}{z} = \frac{z - 1}{z}. \end{aligned} \]

For \(z < 1\), \(g'(z) < 0\) (strictly decreasing); for \(z > 1\), \(g'(z) > 0\) (strictly increasing). The unique global minimum occurs at \(z = 1\), where \(g(1) = 1 - 0 - 1 = 0\). The second derivative \(g''(z) = 1/z^2 > 0\) confirms strict convexity. Thus, \(L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h) \ge 0\) with equality if and only if \(y = h\).

3. Empirical Lab: Asymmetry and Scale Properties of QLIKE vs MSE

The following standalone Python lab evaluates QLIKE loss against MSE loss across a spectrum of forecast ratios from severe under-forecasting (\(h = 0.25y\)) to severe over-forecasting (\(h = 2.0y\)). It demonstrates the asymmetric penalty ratio between under-forecasting and over-forecasting risk.

Lab 1 · QLIKE vs MSE Loss Asymmetry Demonstration

Evaluating loss profiles shows that while MSE penalizes 50% under-forecasting and 50% over-forecasting identically, QLIKE penalizes a 50% under-forecast more than two times more severely than an equivalent over-forecast.

"""Lab S4-L07-1: Asymmetry and scale properties of QLIKE vs MSE loss.

Evaluates quasi-likelihood loss L(y, h) = y/h - ln(y/h) - 1 against MSE loss
across a spectrum of volatility forecast ratios y/h.
Demonstrates the severe penalty QLIKE imposes on under-predicting volatility.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def qlike_loss(realized: float, forecast: float) -> float:
    """Compute QLIKE loss."""
    ratio = realized / forecast
    return float(ratio - math.log(ratio) - 1.0)


def mse_loss(realized: float, forecast: float) -> float:
    """Compute MSE loss."""
    diff = realized - forecast
    return float(diff * diff)


def main() -> None:
    y = 1.00
    forecasts = [0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.25, 1.50, 2.00]

    print(f"Loss Evaluation for Target Realized Variance y = {y:.2f}:")
    print("Forecast h | Ratio y/h | QLIKE Loss | MSE Loss | QLIKE/MSE Ratio")
    print("-" * 65)

    for h in forecasts:
        q = qlike_loss(y, h)
        m = mse_loss(y, h)
        ratio_str = f"{q / m:.3f}" if m > 0.0 else "N/A (min)"
        print(f"   {h:4.2f}    |    {y / h:4.2f}   |   {q:.4f}   |  {m:.4f}  |    {ratio_str}")

    print("\nAsymmetry Demonstration:")
    q_under = qlike_loss(1.0, 0.50)  # 50% under-forecast
    q_over = qlike_loss(1.0, 1.50)  # 50% over-forecast
    m_under = mse_loss(1.0, 0.50)
    m_over = mse_loss(1.0, 1.50)
    print(f"50% Under-forecast (h=0.5): QLIKE = {q_under:.4f}, MSE = {m_under:.4f}")
    print(f"50% Over-forecast  (h=1.5): QLIKE = {q_over:.4f}, MSE = {m_over:.4f}")
    print(f"QLIKE Under/Over penalty ratio: {q_under / q_over:.2f}x (MSE ratio: {m_under / m_over:.2f}x)")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

While QLIKE is mathematically robust and economically sensible, practitioners must navigate several boundary limitations:

  • Sensitivity to zero or negative forecasts: The term \(\ln h\) and \(y/h\) require strictly positive forecasts \(h > 0\). If an unconstrained linear model (like standard OLS HAR) produces a negative or zero forecast, QLIKE evaluates to undefined (\(\ln(\le 0)\) or division by zero).
  • Sensitivity to zero realized proxies: When using coarse proxies like squared intraday returns, zero price movements (\(r_t = 0\)) can yield \(y = 0\). While \(\lim_{y \to 0} L(y, h) = \ln h + \text{const}\), empirical implementations must floor proxies above machine precision to prevent numerical log domain errors.
  • Dependence on proxy unbiasedness: Although QLIKE is robust to noise in the proxy, it strictly assumes that the proxy is conditionally unbiased (\(\E[y_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \sigma_t^2\)). If the proxy is contaminated by microstructure noise bias, QLIKE rankings become distorted.

Misconceptions

  • Wrong belief: MSE is always the most objective, model-free metric for ranking volatility forecasts.

    Why it is wrong: MSE is dominated by high-volatility outlier periods. In a sample of 2,000 days, a single market panic (such as March 2020) can account for over 60% of total MSE loss, causing an inferior model that fits crashes well to outrank a superior model across the other 1,999 days. QLIKE normalizes by forecast scale, preventing outlier dominance.

  • Wrong belief: QLIKE is only valid if underlying return innovations are exactly Gaussian.

    Why it is wrong: QLIKE is a quasi-likelihood scoring rule. Patton (2011) proved that QLIKE generates consistent parameter estimates and identical model rankings under general non-Gaussian distributions, so long as the conditional variance is correctly specified.

  • Wrong belief: Mean Absolute Error (MAE) \(|y - h|\) is a suitable robust alternative to QLIKE.

    Why it is wrong: MAE is not a robust volatility loss function. Minimizing expected MAE leads to forecasting the conditional median of the proxy distribution rather than its conditional mean (variance), generating a systematically downward-biased volatility forecast.

Practice Problems

Mechanics Tier

Given a target realized variance of \(y = 0.0400\) (20% annualized volatility), compute the QLIKE loss for an under-forecast of \(h_1 = 0.0200\) and an over-forecast of \(h_2 = 0.0800\). Determine which forecast incurs larger loss.

Worked Solution

For the under-forecast \(h_1 = 0.0200\): \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{y}{h_1} &= \frac{0.0400}{0.0200} = 2.0. \\ L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h_1) &= 2.0 - \ln(2.0) - 1.0 \approx 2.0 - 0.69315 - 1.0 = 0.30685. \end{aligned} \] For the over-forecast \(h_2 = 0.0800\): \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{y}{h_2} &= \frac{0.0400}{0.0800} = 0.50. \\ L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h_2) &= 0.50 - \ln(0.50) - 1.0 = 0.50 - (-0.69315) - 1.0 = 0.19315. \end{aligned} \] The under-forecast \(h_1\) incurs a loss of 0.3069, whereas the over-forecast \(h_2\) incurs a loss of only 0.1932. Even though both represent a factor-of-two deviation, under-predicting volatility is penalized 1.59 times more severely.

Demonstrate the scale invariance of QLIKE: evaluate \(L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h)\) for \(y = 2.0, h = 1.0\), and for \(y = 200.0, h = 100.0\). Compare this behavior with MSE.

Worked Solution

For \(y=2.0, h=1.0\): \[ L_{\text{QLIKE}}(2.0, 1.0) = \frac{2.0}{1.0} - \ln\left(\frac{2.0}{1.0}\right) - 1.0 = 2.0 - \ln 2 - 1.0 \approx 0.30685. \] For \(y=200.0, h=100.0\): \[ L_{\text{QLIKE}}(200.0, 100.0) = \frac{200.0}{100.0} - \ln\left(\frac{200.0}{100.0}\right) - 1.0 = 2.0 - \ln 2 - 1.0 \approx 0.30685. \] The QLIKE loss is identical (0.30685) because both pairs share the ratio \(y/h = 2\). In contrast, MSE loss scales quadratically: \(L_{\text{MSE}}(2, 1) = (2-1)^2 = 1.0\), whereas \(L_{\text{MSE}}(200, 100) = (200-100)^2 = 10,000.0\), inflating by a factor of 10,000.

Calculate the first-order condition derivative \(\frac{\partial L_{\text{QLIKE}}}{\partial h}\) and verify that setting it to zero yields \(h = y\).

Worked Solution

Write QLIKE as: \[ L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h) = \ln h + \frac{y}{h} - (\ln y + 1). \] Differentiating with respect to the forecast \(h\): \[ \frac{\partial L_{\text{QLIKE}}}{\partial h} = \frac{1}{h} - \frac{y}{h^2} = \frac{h - y}{h^2}. \] Setting the derivative equal to zero: \[ \frac{h - y}{h^2} = 0 \implies h - y = 0 \implies h = y. \] The second derivative \(\frac{\partial^2 L_{\text{QLIKE}}}{\partial h^2} = -\frac{1}{h^2} + \frac{2y}{h^3}\). Evaluated at \(h = y\): \(-1/y^2 + 2/y^2 = 1/y^2 > 0\). Thus, \(h = y\) is the unique global minimum.

Proof Tier

Prove that if the forecast \(h\) is chosen to minimize expected loss \(\E[L(Y, h)]\) under QLIKE, the optimal forecast is the conditional expectation \(\E[Y]\).

Worked Solution

The expected QLIKE loss with respect to random variable \(Y\) is: \[ \E[L_{\text{QLIKE}}(Y, h)] = \E\left[\ln h + \frac{Y}{h} - \ln Y - 1\right] = \ln h + \frac{\E[Y]}{h} - \E[\ln Y] - 1. \] Differentiating with respect to \(h\) and setting the derivative to zero: \[ \frac{d}{dh} \E[L_{\text{QLIKE}}(Y, h)] = \frac{1}{h} - \frac{\E[Y]}{h^2} = 0 \implies h^* = \E[Y]. \] Since \(\frac{d^2}{dh^2} \E[L] = -1/h^2 + 2\E[Y]/h^3\), at \(h = \E[Y]\) the second derivative is \(1/(\E[Y])^2 > 0\). This proves that QLIKE strictly elicits the true conditional mean \(\E[Y]\).

Prove that as the forecast \(h \to 0^+\) for a fixed realized target \(y > 0\), the ratio of QLIKE loss to MSE loss diverges to infinity.

Worked Solution

Consider the asymptotic behavior as \(h \to 0^+\): \[ L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h) = \frac{y}{h} - \ln\left(\frac{y}{h}\right) - 1 = O\left(\frac{1}{h}\right). \] Meanwhile, the MSE loss is: \[ L_{\text{MSE}}(y, h) = (y - h)^2 \to y^2 = O(1). \] Taking the ratio: \[ \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y, h)}{L_{\text{MSE}}(y, h)} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{y/h - \ln(y/h) - 1}{(y - h)^2} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{y/h}{y^2} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{1}{y h} = \infty. \] This proves that QLIKE penalizes severe under-forecasting infinitely more harshly than MSE, preventing models that collapse toward zero variance.

Prove that the normalized QLIKE loss function \(L(y, h) = y/h - \ln(y/h) - 1\) is convex in the forecast \(h\) on the domain \(h \in (0, 2y)\).

Worked Solution

Compute the second derivative of \(L(y, h)\) with respect to \(h\): \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial h} = \frac{1}{h} - \frac{y}{h^2}. \] \[ \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial h^2} = -\frac{1}{h^2} + \frac{2y}{h^3} = \frac{2y - h}{h^3}. \] For convexity, we require \(\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial h^2} \ge 0\). Since \(h > 0\), the denominator \(h^3 > 0\). The numerator is non-negative if and only if \(2y - h \ge 0 \iff h \le 2y\). Thus, QLIKE is strictly convex on the interval \((0, 2y)\), encompassing all under-forecasts and over-forecasts up to a factor of two.

Empirical Tier

In an out-of-sample volatility forecasting tournament between Model A (a GARCH model) and Model B (a HAR model), Model A has MSE = 0.0018 and QLIKE = 0.42, while Model B has MSE = 0.0022 and QLIKE = 0.35. Explain this divergence in model ranking.

Worked Solution

This divergence arises because MSE is dominated by a few high-volatility outlier days, whereas QLIKE evaluates relative proportional accuracy across the entire distribution. Model A likely produced very aggressive spikes during a single market crash, minimizing level errors on those extreme days and winning the MSE contest. However, Model B maintained superior relative accuracy throughout the remaining 99% of trading days and avoided dangerously under-predicting volatility in quieter periods, earning the superior (lower) QLIKE score.

How does replacing daily squared returns \(r_t^2\) with 5-minute realized variance \(RV_t\) as the proxy \(y_t\) affect the statistical power of the Diebold-Mariano test under QLIKE loss?

Worked Solution

Squared daily returns \(r_t^2 = \sigma_t^2 z_t^2\) are an extremely noisy proxy with variance \(\Var(r_t^2 \mid \sigma_t^2) = 2\sigma_t^4\). By contrast, 5-minute realized variance reduces measurement noise by over 90%. Using \(RV_t\) dramatically shrinks the variance of the loss differential \(\bar{d}_t = L(y_t, h_{1t}) - L(y_t, h_{2t})\). Consequently, the denominator in the Diebold-Mariano \(t\)-statistic decreases substantially, vastly increasing test power and allowing researchers to distinguish competing forecasting models with statistical confidence.

An algorithmic market maker sets bid-ask spread quotes proportional to predicted volatility \(\hat{\sigma}_t\). If the trading desk uses MSE loss to tune its volatility forecaster, what operational risk does it face compared to tuning under QLIKE?

Worked Solution

Tuning under MSE places low penalty on moderate under-forecasting during tranquil markets. If predicted volatility is too low, the desk posts spreads that are too tight, incurring adverse selection from informed traders and accumulating inventory at loss-making levels during sudden market transitions. Tuning under QLIKE severely penalizes under-forecasting risk, ensuring that the model errs on the side of caution and sets wider spreads when volatility is ambiguous, protecting the desk from inventory blowouts.

Research Tier

Summarize Patton's (2011) necessary and sufficient conditions for a loss function \(L(y, h)\) to yield consistent volatility model rankings when evaluating against a noisy proxy.

Worked Solution

Patton (2011) proved that a loss function \(L(y, h)\) is robust to noise in proxy \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) if and only if the ranking between any two forecasts \(h_1, h_2\) is identical whether evaluated under the unobserved true variance \(\sigma^2\) or an unbiased proxy \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) (\(\E[\hat{\sigma}^2 \mid \mathcal{F}] = \sigma^2\)). The necessary and sufficient condition is that \(\frac{\partial^2 L(y, h)}{\partial y \partial h}\) must not depend on the proxy \(y\). The entire family of robust loss functions takes the form \(L(y, h) = \tilde{C}(h) + C(h) y + f(y)\), which contains MSE and QLIKE as the leading special cases.

Analyze why QLIKE is the unique homogeneous loss function of degree zero in Patton's robust family.

Worked Solution

A loss function is homogeneous of degree \(k\) if \(L(c y, c h) = c^k L(y, h)\). For degree zero (\(k = 0\)), loss must be completely scale-invariant: multiplying all prices or volatility levels by a constant factor does not alter loss. In Patton's robust family \(L(y, h) = \frac{h^{k+1} - y^{k+1}}{k(k+1)} - \frac{y^k}{k}(h - y)\), taking the mathematical limit as \(k \to -1\) yields: \[ \lim_{k \to -1} L_k(y, h) = \frac{y}{h} - \ln\left(\frac{y}{h}\right) - 1. \] Thus, QLIKE is the unique member of the robust family that exhibits scale invariance of degree zero.

Explain how the Model Confidence Set (MCS) procedure of Hansen, Lunde, and Nason (2011) uses QLIKE loss differentials to isolate superior volatility models.

Worked Solution

Rather than choosing a single "best" model that may be subject to sample data mining, the Model Confidence Set (MCS) determines a subset of models \(\mathcal{M}^*\) that contains the best model with a pre-specified confidence level \(1 - \alpha\) (e.g. 90%). At each step, MCS tests the null hypothesis of equal predictive ability: \(H_0: \E[d_{ij, t}] = 0\) for all \(i, j \in \mathcal{M}\), where \(d_{ij, t} = L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y_t, h_{it}) - L_{\text{QLIKE}}(y_t, h_{jt})\). An elimination rule removes the worst-performing model based on the maximum standardized loss differential until the null cannot be rejected.

Diagnostic Quiz

  1. Why is volatility forecast evaluation fundamentally different from return forecast evaluation?

    True conditional volatility is never directly observed on market tapes; econometricians must evaluate forecasts against proxies like squared returns or realized variance.

  2. What is the formula for normalized Gaussian quasi-likelihood (QLIKE) loss between proxy \(y\) and forecast \(h\)?

    The normalized QLIKE loss is \(y/h - \ln(y/h) - 1\), derived from Gaussian likelihood and reaching its minimum of zero at \(h = y\).

  3. What degree of homogeneity does QLIKE loss possess with respect to \((y, h)\)?

    Because \(L(c y, c h) = L(y, h)\), QLIKE is homogeneous of degree zero, meaning a 20% forecast error is penalized identically regardless of whether variance is 0.01 or 1.0.

  4. How does QLIKE penalize under-forecasting volatility (\(h < y\)) compared to over-forecasting (\(h > y\))?

    As \(h \to 0\), QLIKE explodes linearly with \(y/h\), whereas as \(h \to \infty\), loss grows only logarithmically with \(\ln(h/y)\).

  5. What major empirical flaw affects MSE when ranking volatility forecasting models over a multi-year sample?

    Because level errors are squared, crisis days with high volatility produce errors thousands of times larger than tranquil days, overwhelming overall model rankings.

  6. Under what condition on the volatility proxy does QLIKE guarantee consistent model rankings?

    Patton (2011) showed that robustness requires only that the proxy be an conditionally unbiased estimator of true conditional variance.

  7. Why is Mean Absolute Error (MAE) unsuitable for volatility forecast evaluation?

    Because squared return distributions are heavily right-skewed, their median is strictly smaller than their mean. Tuning under MAE produces systematically underestimated volatility.

  8. What operational requirement does QLIKE impose on candidate volatility models?

    Because QLIKE contains terms \(\ln h_t\) and \(y_t / h_t\), non-positive forecasts (\(h_t \le 0\)) cause mathematical failure (undefined logarithms or division by zero).
