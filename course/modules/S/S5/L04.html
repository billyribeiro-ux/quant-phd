The Fama–MacBeth Two-Pass Regression

Time-Series Factor Loadings, Monthly Cross-Sections, and Robust Standard Error Calculation

Why It Matters

Testing multi-factor asset pricing models across hundreds of test assets presents a severe econometric challenge: asset returns are strongly correlated across firms at any given point in time. A naive pooled ordinary least squares regression across assets and time produces standard errors that drastically understate true sampling uncertainty due to cross-sectional residual correlation. The two-pass methodology of Fama and MacBeth (1973) bypasses this problem by running separate cross-sectional regressions in each time period and evaluating the time-series variance of the resulting slope estimates. This elegant procedure forms the empirical benchmark for testing factor premia across modern equity, fixed income, and currency markets.

1. The Two-Pass Econometric Methodology

Let \(R_{it}\) denote the excess return of asset \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) in period \(t \in \{1, \dots, T\}\), and let \(f_t \in \R^K\) denote a vector of \(K\) candidate pricing factors with covariance matrix \(\Sigma_f\). Multi-factor pricing models assert that expected excess returns are linear in asset factor loadings.

Definition 4.1 · Fama–MacBeth Two-Pass Regression

The Fama–MacBeth two-pass regression estimates factor risk premia through a sequential two-stage ordinary least squares procedure:

  1. Pass 1 (Time-Series Loadings): For each asset \(i = 1, \dots, N\), run a time-series OLS regression of excess returns on a constant and the \(K\) factor returns over an initial window \(t = 1, \dots, T_1\): \[ \begin{aligned} R_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i^\top f_t + \varepsilon_{it}, \quad t = 1, \dots, T_1. \end{aligned} \] This yields the estimated factor loading vector \(\hat{\beta}_i \in \R^K\) and intercept \(\hat{\alpha}_i\).
  2. Pass 2 (Monthly Cross-Sectional Regressions): For each month \(t = T_1 + 1, \dots, T\) (totaling \(T_2 = T - T_1\) test periods), run a cross-sectional regression of the \(N\) asset returns on an intercept and the estimated loadings: \[ \begin{aligned} R_{it} = \lambda_{0,t} + \hat{\beta}_i^\top \lambda_t + \eta_{it}, \quad i = 1, \dots, N. \end{aligned} \] This produces a time series of estimated zero-beta rates \(\hat{\lambda}_{0,t}\) and risk premia slopes \(\hat{\lambda}_t \in \R^K\) for each month \(t\).
  3. Aggregation: The Fama–MacBeth factor risk premium estimator is the time-series mean of the monthly slopes: \[ \begin{aligned} \bar{\lambda} = \frac{1}{T_2} \sum_{t=1}^{T_2} \hat{\lambda}_t. \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 4.1 · Naive Fama–MacBeth Standard Errors

Assumptions:

  1. A1: The monthly slope vector sequence \(\{\hat{\lambda}_t\}_{t=1}^{T_2}\) is independent and identically distributed with finite variance \(\Var(\hat{\lambda}_t) = \Sigma_\lambda\).
  2. A2: First-pass loadings \(\beta_i\) are known without measurement error (\(\hat{\beta}_i = \beta_i\)).

Conclusion: Under Assumptions A1–A2, the sample mean \(\bar{\lambda}\) has asymptotic covariance matrix:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Var(\bar{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{T_2} \Sigma_\lambda. \end{aligned} \]

The sample estimator of this covariance matrix is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\Var}(\bar{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{T_2(T_2 - 1)} \sum_{t=1}^{T_2} (\hat{\lambda}_t - \bar{\lambda})(\hat{\lambda}_t - \bar{\lambda})^\top, \end{aligned} \]

yielding the naive Fama–MacBeth standard error for factor \(k\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \se_{\text{naive}}(\bar{\lambda}_k) = \frac{s(\hat{\lambda}_{k,t})}{\sqrt{T_2}}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(s(\hat{\lambda}_{k,t}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T_2 - 1} \sum_{t=1}^{T_2} (\hat{\lambda}_{k,t} - \bar{\lambda}_k)^2}\).

Remark 4.1 · Automatic Absorption of Cross-Sectional Correlation

Consider the second-pass OLS slope: \(\hat{\lambda}_t = (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top R_t\), where \(\hat{Z} = [\mathbf{1}_N, \hat{\beta}]\). If the cross-sectional disturbances \(\eta_t\) have arbitrary contemporaneous covariance \(\Cov(\eta_t) = \Sigma_\eta\) (an \(N \times N\) matrix), the variance of \(\hat{\lambda}_t\) in month \(t\) is \((\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top \Sigma_\eta \hat{Z} (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1}\). Because the Fama–MacBeth standard error computes the sample variance across time of the realized slopes \(\hat{\lambda}_t\), it automatically captures the full impact of \(\Sigma_\eta\) without ever estimating the high-dimensional \(N \times N\) matrix \(\Sigma_\eta\).

2. Autocorrelated Monthly Slopes and HAC Inference

When macroeconomic state variables or time-varying risk aversion induce serial persistence in risk premia, the sequence of monthly slopes \(\hat{\lambda}_t\) violates the i.i.d. assumption (Assumption A1). If \(\Cov(\hat{\lambda}_t, \hat{\lambda}_{t-j}) \ne \mathbf{0}\), naive standard errors are downward-biased.

Newey and West (1987) established that the long-run covariance matrix of autocorrelated time series can be consistently estimated using a Bartlett kernel:

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\Var}_{\text{HAC}}(\bar{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{T_2} \left[ \hat{\Gamma}_0 + \sum_{j=1}^m \left(1 - \frac{j}{m + 1}\right) (\hat{\Gamma}_j + \hat{\Gamma}_j^\top) \right], \end{aligned} \]

where \(\hat{\Gamma}_j = \frac{1}{T_2} \sum_{t=j+1}^{T_2} (\hat{\lambda}_t - \bar{\lambda})(\hat{\lambda}_{t-j} - \bar{\lambda})^\top\) and \(m\) is the truncation lag. This provides the HAC standard errors required when monthly pricing slopes exhibit persistence.

Lab 1 · Two-Pass Fama–MacBeth Regression Execution

The Fama–MacBeth factor risk premium equals the sample mean of monthly cross-sectional slopes exactly, and the standard error of the mean correctly reflects the time-series dispersion of the monthly estimates.

"""Lab S5-L04-1: Fama-MacBeth two-pass cross-sectional regression.

SIMULATED: generates synthetic asset returns and factor returns for N=20 assets across T=180 months,
executes time-series regressions to obtain factor loadings, runs monthly cross-sectional regressions,
and computes Fama-MacBeth risk premia, naive standard errors, and t-statistics.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    seed = 303
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_assets = 20
    t_months = 180
    k_factors = 2

    # Factor excess returns (T, K)
    f_mean = np.array([0.006, 0.004])  # 60 bps and 40 bps monthly
    f_cov = np.array([[0.0016, 0.0004], [0.0004, 0.0009]])
    factors = rng.multivariate_normal(f_mean, f_cov, size=t_months)

    # True asset loadings (N, K) and pricing errors
    true_betas = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, size=(n_assets, k_factors))
    true_alpha = np.zeros(n_assets)
    resid_sd = rng.uniform(0.02, 0.04, size=n_assets)

    # Generate asset excess returns: R_it = alpha_i + beta_i' f_t + e_it
    excess_returns = np.empty((t_months, n_assets))
    for t in range(t_months):
        e_t = rng.normal(0.0, resid_sd, size=n_assets)
        excess_returns[t] = true_alpha + true_betas @ factors[t] + e_t

    # Step 1: First-pass time-series regressions for each asset
    # Design: [1, factors] (T, K+1)
    x_ts = np.column_stack([np.ones(t_months), factors])
    betas_est = np.empty((n_assets, k_factors + 1))
    for i in range(n_assets):
        y_i = excess_returns[:, i]
        coef = np.linalg.solve(x_ts.T @ x_ts, x_ts.T @ y_i)
        betas_est[i] = coef

    # Step 2: Second-pass monthly cross-sectional regressions
    # For each month t, regress R_t (N,) on [1, beta_est[:, 1:]] (N, K+1)
    z_cs = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_assets), betas_est[:, 1:]])
    monthly_slopes = np.empty((t_months, k_factors + 1))
    for t in range(t_months):
        r_t = excess_returns[t]
        gamma_t = np.linalg.solve(z_cs.T @ z_cs, z_cs.T @ r_t)
        monthly_slopes[t] = gamma_t

    # Fama-MacBeth estimates: mean and standard error of monthly slopes
    fm_premia = np.mean(monthly_slopes, axis=0)
    slope_sd = np.std(monthly_slopes, axis=0, ddof=1)
    fm_naive_se = slope_sd / np.sqrt(t_months)
    fm_t_stats = fm_premia / fm_naive_se

    # Check that premium equals average of monthly slopes exactly
    diff_premia = np.max(np.abs(fm_premia - np.sum(monthly_slopes, axis=0) / t_months))

    print("Lab S5-L04-1: Fama-MacBeth Two-Pass Cross-Sectional Regression")
    print(f"  Test assets N: {n_assets}, Months T: {t_months}, Factors K: {k_factors} (SIMULATED, seed {seed})")
    print(f"  Max absolute difference between mean and manual sum/T: {diff_premia:.2e}")
    print("  Parameter | Estimated Premium | Naive SE    | t-statistic | Significant (|t| > 1.96)?")
    print("  " + "-" * 72)
    names = ["Zero-Beta Intercept", "Factor 1 Premium", "Factor 2 Premium"]
    for idx, name in enumerate(names):
        sig = "Yes" if abs(fm_t_stats[idx]) > 1.96 else "No"
        p_str = f"{fm_premia[idx]:+.6f}"
        se_str = f"{fm_naive_se[idx]:.6f}"
        t_str = f"{fm_t_stats[idx]:+7.3f}"
        print(f"  {name:<20} | {p_str}        | {se_str}   | {t_str}     | {sig}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Theorem 4.1 crucially relies on Assumption A2: true factor loadings \(\beta_i\) must be known without error. In real financial applications, \(\hat{\beta}_i\) is estimated from finite historical return samples (\(T_1 \in [60, 120]\) months). The estimation error \(\hat{\beta}_i = \beta_i + v_i\) represents classical measurement error in the second-pass regressor matrix. Because the naive Fama–MacBeth formula ignores this generated regressor noise, naive standard errors are systematically too small, inflating t-statistics and leading researchers to falsely declare unpriced factors to be statistically significant.

Misconceptions

  1. Fama–MacBeth standard errors account for sampling noise in the first-pass estimated factor loadings.

    Fama–MacBeth standard errors account only for cross-sectional correlation in the second-pass residuals \(\eta_t\). They treat the estimated factor loadings \(\hat{\beta}_i\) as fixed, non-stochastic constants. Any sampling variability from estimating \(\hat{\beta}_i\) in the first pass is completely ignored, requiring Shanken's (1992) correction to restore valid inference.

  2. Running a pooled OLS regression across all N x T observations yields more efficient estimates than Fama–MacBeth.

    Because the second-pass regressors \(\hat{\beta}_i\) are time-invariant, the pooled OLS point estimate is mathematically identical to the unweighted time-series mean of the monthly Fama–MacBeth slopes. However, pooled OLS standard errors assume spherical errors across assets, severely understating uncertainty by ignoring contemporaneous cross-sectional correlation.

  3. The Fama–MacBeth procedure requires estimating an N x N covariance matrix of asset returns.

    The brilliance of Fama–MacBeth is that it computes \(T_2\) separate cross-sectional OLS regressions, each requiring inversion of only a small \((K + 1) \times (K + 1)\) matrix \(\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z}\). The \(N \times N\) asset covariance matrix \(\Sigma_\eta\) is never formed or inverted, making the method numerically robust even when \(N \gg T\).

Practice Problems

Over \(T_2 = 120\) monthly cross-sections, the monthly slope estimates for a market factor have sample mean \(\bar{\lambda} = 0.0065\) (65 bps monthly) and sample standard deviation \(s = 0.0450\). Compute the naive Fama–MacBeth standard error and the resulting t-statistic.

Worked Solution

The naive standard error divides the sample standard deviation by \(\sqrt{T_2}\): \(\se_{\text{naive}}(\bar{\lambda}) = \frac{0.0450}{\sqrt{120}} = \frac{0.0450}{10.9545} \approx 0.004108\). The t-statistic is \(t = \frac{\bar{\lambda}}{\se_{\text{naive}}} = \frac{0.0065}{0.004108} \approx 1.582\). Because \(|t| \le 1.96\), the premium is not statistically significant at the 5% level.

In a cross-sectional regression for month \(t\) with \(N = 3\) assets and \(K = 1\) factor, the estimated loadings are \(\hat{\beta} = [0.8, 1.0, 1.2]^\top\). The excess returns in month \(t\) are \(R_t = [0.010, 0.015, 0.020]^\top\). Compute the design matrix \(\hat{Z} = [\mathbf{1}_3, \hat{\beta}]\), evaluate \(\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z}\), and compute the monthly slope vector \(\hat{\lambda}_t\).

Worked Solution

The design matrix is \(\hat{Z} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.8 \\ 1 & 1.0 \\ 1 & 1.2 \end{pmatrix}\). Computing cross-products: \(\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3.0 \\ 3.0 & 3.08 \end{pmatrix}\). The determinant is \(3(3.08) - (3.0)^2 = 9.24 - 9.00 = 0.24\). The inverse is \((\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.24} \begin{pmatrix} 3.08 & -3.0 \\ -3.0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Vector \(\hat{Z}^\top R_t = \begin{pmatrix} 0.045 \\ 0.047 \end{pmatrix}\). Solving: \(\hat{\lambda}_t = \frac{1}{0.24} \begin{pmatrix} 3.08(0.045) - 3.0(0.047) \\ -3.0(0.045) + 3(0.047) \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{0.24} \begin{pmatrix} 0.1386 - 0.1410 \\ -0.1350 + 0.1410 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{0.24} \begin{pmatrix} -0.0024 \\ 0.0060 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.0100 \\ 0.0250 \end{pmatrix}\). The zero-beta intercept is \(-1.0\%\) and factor slope is \(+2.5\%\).

If monthly slopes \(\hat{\lambda}_t\) have sample variance \(\hat{\Gamma}_0 = 0.0016\) and first-order sample autocovariance \(\hat{\Gamma}_1 = 0.0004\), compute the Newey–West standard error adjustment with lag truncation \(m = 1\) on \(T_2 = 100\) months.

Worked Solution

With \(m = 1\), the Bartlett weight is \(w_1 = 1 - 1 / (1 + 1) = 0.50\). The long-run variance estimate is \(\hat{S}_{\text{HAC}} = \hat{\Gamma}_0 + 2 \times 0.50 \times \hat{\Gamma}_1 = 0.0016 + 1.0 \times 0.0004 = 0.0020\). The naive variance is \(\hat{\Gamma}_0 = 0.0016\). The ratio of HAC to naive standard error is \(\sqrt{0.0020 / 0.0016} = \sqrt{1.25} \approx 1.1180\). The HAC standard error is \(11.8\%\) larger than the naive standard error due to positive serial correlation.

Prove that the pooled OLS estimator of risk premia \(\hat{\lambda}_{\text{pooled}}\) from stacking all \(N \times T_2\) observations is algebraically identical to the unweighted time-series mean of the monthly cross-sectional slopes \(\bar{\lambda} = \frac{1}{T_2}\sum_{t=1}^{T_2} \hat{\lambda}_t\) when factor loadings \(\hat{\beta}_i\) are time-invariant.

Worked Solution

Let \(\hat{Z} = [\mathbf{1}_N, \hat{\beta}] \in \R^{N \times (K+1)}\). In pooled regression, the design matrix is \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{1}_{T_2} \otimes \hat{Z} \in \R^{(N T_2) \times (K+1)}\) and the response vector is \(\mathbf{Y} = [R_1^\top, \dots, R_{T_2}^\top]^\top\). The normal equations are \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} \hat{\lambda}_{\text{pooled}} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{Y}\). Evaluating the block products: \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{1}_{T_2}^\top \otimes \hat{Z}^\top)(\mathbf{1}_{T_2} \otimes \hat{Z}) = (\mathbf{1}_{T_2}^\top \mathbf{1}_{T_2}) \otimes (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z}) = T_2 (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})\). Similarly, \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{Y} = \sum_{t=1}^{T_2} \hat{Z}^\top R_t\). Thus: \(\hat{\lambda}_{\text{pooled}} = \left[ T_2 (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z}) \right]^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{T_2} \hat{Z}^\top R_t = \frac{1}{T_2} \sum_{t=1}^{T_2} (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top R_t = \frac{1}{T_2} \sum_{t=1}^{T_2} \hat{\lambda}_t = \bar{\lambda}\). The pooled OLS point estimate strictly equals the Fama–MacBeth slope average.

Show that if cross-sectional errors satisfy \(\Cov(\eta_t) = \Sigma_\eta\) and are independent across time, the true variance of \(\bar{\lambda}\) conditional on loadings \(\hat{Z}\) is \(\frac{1}{T_2} (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top \Sigma_\eta \hat{Z} (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1}\).

Worked Solution

For each month \(t\), \(\hat{\lambda}_t = (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top R_t = \lambda + (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top \eta_t\). The error term has variance \(\Var(\hat{\lambda}_t \mid \hat{Z}) = (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top \Var(\eta_t) \hat{Z} (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} = (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top \Sigma_\eta \hat{Z} (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1}\). Since \(\eta_t\) is independent across months \(t\), the variance of the sample average \(\bar{\lambda} = \frac{1}{T_2}\sum_{t=1}^{T_2} \hat{\lambda}_t\) divides by \(T_2\): \(\Var(\bar{\lambda} \mid \hat{Z}) = \frac{1}{T_2^2} \sum_{t=1}^{T_2} \Var(\hat{\lambda}_t \mid \hat{Z}) = \frac{1}{T_2} (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top \Sigma_\eta \hat{Z} (\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z})^{-1}\).

Formulate the Fama–MacBeth two-pass regression as a stacked moment condition system in the GMM framework.

Worked Solution

Stack the first-pass time-series moments and the second-pass cross-sectional moments together. For asset \(i\), the time-series moments are \(\E[(R_{it} - \alpha_i - \beta_i^\top f_t) \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ f_t \end{pmatrix}] = \mathbf{0}_{(K+1) \times 1}\) for \(i = 1, \dots, N\), totaling \(N(K + 1)\) moments. The second-pass pricing moments are \(\E[R_t - \mathbf{1}_N \lambda_0 - \beta \lambda_f] = \mathbf{0}_{N \times 1}\). The complete parameter vector is \(\theta = [\alpha_1, \beta_1^\top, \dots, \alpha_N, \beta_N^\top, \lambda_0, \lambda_f^\top]^\top\). The system has \(N(K + 2)\) moments and \(N(K + 1) + (K + 1)\) parameters. This GMM representation yields identical point estimates and provides the theoretical foundation for errors-in-variables corrections.

In an empirical application of Fama–MacBeth to 100 industry portfolios, a researcher observes that the zero-beta rate estimate is \(\bar{\lambda}_0 = 0.0080\) (80 bps monthly) with standard error \(0.0015\). Discuss the economic implication of finding \(\bar{\lambda}_0 > 0\) when testing excess returns.

Worked Solution

In a fully specified asset pricing model written in excess returns (\(R_{it} = R_{i,raw} - R_f\)), the theoretical zero-beta rate should be zero (\(\lambda_0 = 0\)). Finding \(\bar{\lambda}_0 = 0.0080\) with \(t = 0.0080 / 0.0015 \approx 5.33 > 1.96\) strongly rejects the hypothesis that the factor set prices the cross-section without residual intercept. A positive intercept implies that a zero-beta asset earns returns exceeding the risk-free rate, indicating either borrowing constraints (Black CAPM) or omitted common risk factors.

Why did Fama and French (1993) sort stocks into 25 size and book-to-market portfolios rather than running Fama–MacBeth regressions directly on thousands of individual stocks?

Worked Solution

Individual stock returns contain large idiosyncratic return variance (high \(\sigma_e^2\)), causing first-pass factor loadings \(\hat{\beta}_i\) to be estimated with substantial measurement error. Grouping stocks into diversified portfolios averages out idiosyncratic noise, drastically shrinking \(\Var(\hat{\beta}_i - \beta_i)\). Furthermore, sorting on characteristics creates a wide cross-sectional spread in loadings, maximizing the rank and conditioning of \(\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z}\) in the second pass.

A quant models equity returns using Fama–MacBeth with rolling 60-month windows for Pass 1 versus a single full-sample Pass 1. Compare the trade-off between parameter time-variation and look-ahead bias.

Worked Solution

Rolling 60-month windows allow factor loadings \(\beta_{it}\) to adapt to corporate changes, shifts in financial leverage, and evolving business models, eliminating look-ahead bias if estimation uses only strictly historical data. In contrast, full-sample loading estimation uses future return realizations to estimate historical betas (severe look-ahead bias). However, rolling windows estimate loadings from only 60 observations, increasing measurement error and inflating errors-in-variables bias in the second pass.

Examine the asymptotic behavior of Fama–MacBeth estimators when the number of assets N and the number of time periods T grow large simultaneously (large-N, large-T panel asymptotics).

Worked Solution

When \(N, T \to \infty\), Bai and Ng (2002) and Giglio and Xiu (2021) showed that the errors-in-variables bias in second-pass slopes decays at rate \(O_p(1/T_1)\). If \(T_1 / \sqrt{T_2} \to \infty\), the first-pass measurement error is asymptotically negligible, and \(\sqrt{T_2}(\bar{\lambda} - \lambda) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma_f)\). However, if \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) grow at the same rate, the first-pass error leaves a non-vanishing asymptotic bias that requires explicit analytical correction (such as Shanken or three-pass PCA).

Discuss how the 'useless factor' problem analyzed by Kan and Zhang (1999) distorts Fama–MacBeth t-statistics.

Worked Solution

Kan and Zhang (1999) proved that if a candidate factor has zero true loading across all assets (\(\beta_i = 0\) for all \(i\)), the true cross-sectional rank of the factor loadings is zero. In finite samples, OLS estimates non-zero noisy loadings \(\hat{\beta}_i\). In the second pass, regressing returns on this pure noise regressor causes the t-statistic of the useless factor to diverge at rate \(\sqrt{T}\) rather than converging to a standard normal distribution. Consequently, the Fama–MacBeth procedure severely over-detects spurious factors when loadings are weak.

Evaluate the weighted least squares (WLS) variant of Fama–MacBeth regressions and its connection to Generalized Least Squares (GLS) cross-sectional asset pricing.

Worked Solution

In WLS Fama–MacBeth, the second-pass cross-sectional regression weights each asset by the inverse of its residual variance \(\hat{\sigma}_{ei}^{-2}\) or portfolio market capitalization: \(\hat{\lambda}_{t,\text{WLS}} = (\hat{Z}^\top \Omega^{-1} \hat{Z})^{-1} \hat{Z}^\top \Omega^{-1} R_t\). This improves statistical efficiency when idiosyncratic variances vary widely across assets. In full GLS, \(\Omega = \Sigma_\eta\) includes cross-asset covariances. Shanken (1992) showed that GLS Fama–MacBeth achieves the maximum Sharpe ratio frontier of test assets, providing an exact link between statistical efficiency and mean-variance portfolio theory.

Retrieval Practice

  1. What are the regressors in the second pass of a Fama–MacBeth two-pass regression?

    In the second pass, monthly asset returns are regressed across assets onto the estimated factor loadings \(\hat{\beta}_i\) from the first pass.

  2. How does Fama–MacBeth address cross-sectional correlation of asset returns?

    Evaluating the sample variance of the time series of cross-sectional slopes automatically captures contemporaneous cross-sectional correlation.

  3. What is the divisor in the formula for the naive Fama–MacBeth standard error of the mean slope?

    The naive standard error is the standard error of the time-series mean: \(s(\hat{\lambda}_t) / \sqrt{T_2}\).

  4. What major source of sampling uncertainty is ignored by naive Fama–MacBeth standard errors?

    Naive Fama–MacBeth standard errors treat \(\hat{\beta}_i\) as known non-stochastic numbers, ignoring errors-in-variables from the first pass.

  5. When factor loadings are time-invariant, what is the mathematical relationship between the pooled OLS slope and the Fama–MacBeth slope average?

    Linear algebra proves that pooled OLS on time-invariant regressors collapses exactly to the unweighted time-series mean of the monthly slopes.

  6. Why should a Newey–West adjustment be applied to Fama–MacBeth standard errors in certain applications?

    If monthly slopes are serially correlated, naive standard errors underestimate variance; Newey–West HAC standard errors correct for autocorrelation.

  7. In an empirical test on excess returns, what does a statistically significant non-zero intercept \(\bar{\lambda}_0 \ne 0\) imply?

    A valid asset pricing model in excess returns implies \(\lambda_0 = 0\); a significant non-zero intercept rejects the model's pricing ability.

  8. Why is Fama–MacBeth computationally advantageous over full GLS in large panels with thousands of assets?

    Second-pass OLS inverts \(\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z}\) of dimension \((K+1) \times (K+1)\), avoiding ill-conditioned \(N \times N\) matrix inversions.
