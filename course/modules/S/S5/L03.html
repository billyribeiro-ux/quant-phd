Hansen's J-Test of Overidentifying Restrictions

Model Specification Testing, Degrees of Freedom, and Asymptotic Chi-Squared Null Distribution

Why It Matters

In an overidentified econometric model, estimating parameters requires more moment conditions than unknowns. If the economic model is fundamentally misspecified—for example, if a multi-factor asset pricing model omits a priced risk dimension or if an instrumental variable is correlated with structural disturbances—no parameter vector can satisfy all population restrictions simultaneously. Hansen's (1982) J-test exploits this overidentification by evaluating the minimized quadratic distance of sample moments from zero. Because the minimized criterion converges to an exact chi-squared distribution under the null hypothesis, the J-test provides an omnibus diagnostic to reject flawed pricing models and invalid instruments before interpreting risk premia.

1. The J-Statistic and Specification Testing

Consider the GMM model introduced in Lesson 1 with \(q\) moment conditions \(\E[g(w_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}\) and \(k\) parameters with \(q > k\). When estimated using the efficient GMM weighting matrix \(W_T = \hat{S}_T^{-1}\) established in Lesson 2, the minimized criterion value measures overall model fit.

Definition 3.1 · Hansen's J-Test of Overidentifying Restrictions

Let \(\hat{\theta}_T\) be the two-step or iterated GMM estimator computed with a consistent estimator \(\hat{S}_T\) of the long-run moment covariance matrix. Hansen's J-test of overidentifying restrictions tests the null hypothesis that all \(q\) population moment conditions are valid:

\[ \begin{aligned} H_0: \E[g(w_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}_{q \times 1} \quad \text{for some } \theta_0 \in \Theta. \end{aligned} \]

The test statistic, denoted \(J_T\) (or the Sargan–Hansen statistic), is sample size \(T\) times the minimized quadratic objective function:

\[ \begin{aligned} J_T = T \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top \hat{S}_T^{-1} \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T). \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 3.1 · Asymptotic Distribution of the J-Statistic

Assumptions:

  1. A1: The data sequence \(\{w_t\}_{t=1}^T\) is strictly stationary and ergodic.
  2. A2 (Null Hypothesis): \(\E[g(w_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}\) for some \(\theta_0 \in \operatorname{int}(\Theta)\).
  3. A3 (Jacobian Rank): \(D = \E[\nabla_\theta g(w_t, \theta_0)] \in \R^{q \times k}\) has full column rank \(k < q\).
  4. A4 (Moment CLT): \(\sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(\mathbf{0}, S)\) with strictly positive definite covariance \(S\).
  5. A5 (Optimal Weighting): \(\hat{S}_T \xrightarrow{p} S\).

Conclusion: Under Assumptions A1–A5, as \(T \to \infty\), Hansen's J-statistic converges in distribution to a chi-squared distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of overidentifying restrictions:

\[ \begin{aligned} J_T = T \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top \hat{S}_T^{-1} \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T) \xrightarrow{\;d\;} \chi^2(q - k). \end{aligned} \]
Proof

Expanding the sample moments \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T)\) around the true parameter \(\theta_0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = \bar{g}_T(\theta_0) + D \cdot (\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) + o_p(T^{-1/2}). \end{aligned} \]

From Theorem 1.1 under optimal weighting \(W = S^{-1}\), the estimator satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) = - (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} \sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\theta_0) + o_p(1). \end{aligned} \]

Substituting this asymptotic expansion into the normalized sample moment expression:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T) &= \left[ I_q - D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} \right] \sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\theta_0) + o_p(1) \\ &= \left[ I_q - P_D \right] \sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\theta_0) + o_p(1), \end{aligned} \]

where \(P_D = D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1}\) is an oblique projection matrix onto the column space of \(D\). Note that \(P_D\) is idempotent (\(P_D^2 = P_D\)) and has rank \(k\).

Pre-multiply by \(S^{-1/2}\) to standardize the asymptotic variance of the raw moments: let \(\xi_T = S^{-1/2} \sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(\mathbf{0}, I_q)\). Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} S^{-1/2} \sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = \left[ I_q - S^{-1/2} D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1/2} \right] \xi_T + o_p(1). \end{aligned} \]

Let \(M = I_q - S^{-1/2} D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1/2}\). The matrix \(M\) is symmetric (\(M = M^\top\)) and idempotent (\(M^2 = M\)). Its trace and rank are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(M) &= \operatorname{tr}(I_q) - \operatorname{tr}\left( (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1/2} S^{-1/2} D \right) \\ &= q - \operatorname{tr}(I_k) = q - k. \end{aligned} \]

The J-statistic can be written as the squared Euclidean norm of \(S^{-1/2} \sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} J_T &= \xi_T^\top M^\top M \xi_T + o_p(1) = \xi_T^\top M \xi_T + o_p(1). \end{aligned} \]

By the classical Cochran theorem for quadratic forms of standard Gaussian vectors, \(\xi_T^\top M \xi_T \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(\operatorname{rank}(M)) = \chi^2(q - k)\).

2. Geometric Intuition of Degrees of Freedom

Why is the degrees of freedom equal to \(q - k\) rather than the total number of moments \(q\)? The answer lies in the first-order condition of efficient GMM:

\[ \begin{aligned} D_T^\top \hat{S}_T^{-1} \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = \mathbf{0}_{k \times 1}. \end{aligned} \]

The estimator \(\hat{\theta}_T\) chooses \(k\) parameters to force exactly \(k\) linear combinations of the sample moments to vanish identically. This consumes \(k\) degrees of freedom from the \(q\)-dimensional moment space. The remaining \(q - k\) orthogonal directions represent genuine overidentifying restrictions that cannot be set to zero by parameter selection. Under \(H_0\), deviations in these \(q - k\) directions arise purely from sampling noise, generating a \(\chi^2(q - k)\) limit.

Lab 1 · Hansen's J-Test Under the Null Hypothesis

Under the true null hypothesis of valid moment restrictions, the empirical rejection rate of Hansen's J-test at the nominal 5% level matches 0.05 within two Monte Carlo standard errors across 1000 independent replications.

"""Lab S5-L03-1: Hansen's J-test of overidentifying restrictions.

SIMULATED: generates synthetic samples for an over-identified GMM system (q=4, k=1, df=3),
evaluates Hansen's J-statistic on a primary sample, and computes the empirical rejection rate
at the nominal 5% significance level across 1000 Monte Carlo replications under the null hypothesis.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
import scipy.stats


def compute_j_stat(z: np.ndarray, x: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Compute two-step GMM estimator, Hansen J-statistic, and p-value."""
    n = len(y)
    q = z.shape[1]
    s_zx = (z.T @ x) / n  # (q,)
    s_zy = (z.T @ y) / n  # (q,)

    # Step 1: Identity weight
    theta1 = float((s_zx.T @ s_zy) / (s_zx.T @ s_zx))

    # Step 2: Efficient weight S_inv
    resid1 = y - x * theta1
    g_mat = z * resid1[:, np.newaxis]
    s_hat = (g_mat.T @ g_mat) / n
    s_inv = np.linalg.inv(s_hat)

    denom2 = float(s_zx.T @ s_inv @ s_zx)
    theta2 = float((s_zx.T @ s_inv @ s_zy) / denom2)

    # Hansen J-statistic: n * g_bar(theta2)' * S_inv * g_bar(theta2)
    resid2 = y - x * theta2
    g_bar = (z.T @ resid2) / n  # (q,)
    j_stat = float(n * (g_bar.T @ s_inv @ g_bar))

    df = q - 1
    p_val = float(scipy.stats.chi2.sf(j_stat, df=df))
    return theta2, j_stat, p_val


def main() -> None:
    seed = 202
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = 500
    q = 4
    k = 1
    df = q - k
    theta_true = 1.60
    gamma = np.array([0.7, -0.9, 1.1, 0.5])
    reps = 1000

    # Primary realization
    z0 = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n, q))
    u0 = rng.normal(0.0, 0.5, size=n)
    eps0 = 0.4 * u0 + rng.normal(0.0, 0.7, size=n)
    x0 = z0 @ gamma + u0
    y0 = theta_true * x0 + eps0

    th0, j0, p0 = compute_j_stat(z0, x0, y0)
    crit_95 = float(scipy.stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, df=df))

    # Monte Carlo simulation of size under null
    rejections = 0
    for _ in range(reps):
        z_r = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n, q))
        u_r = rng.normal(0.0, 0.5, size=n)
        eps_r = 0.4 * u_r + rng.normal(0.0, 0.7, size=n)
        x_r = z_r @ gamma + u_r
        y_r = theta_true * x_r + eps_r

        _, j_r, _ = compute_j_stat(z_r, x_r, y_r)
        if j_r > crit_95:
            rejections += 1

    emp_size = rejections / reps
    mc_se = float(np.sqrt(emp_size * (1.0 - emp_size) / reps))

    print("Lab S5-L03-1: Hansen's J-Test Under the Null Hypothesis")
    print(f"  Sample size T: {n}, Moments q: {q}, Regressors k: {k}, df: {df} (SIMULATED, seed {seed})")
    print(f"  Primary sample GMM estimate: {th0:.6f}")
    print(f"  Primary sample J-statistic: {j0:.4f} (p-value: {p0:.4f})")
    print(f"  Chi-squared(3) 95% critical threshold: {crit_95:.4f}")
    print(f"  Primary sample null accepted (J <= crit): {j0 <= crit_95}")
    print(f"  Monte Carlo empirical size (R={reps}): {emp_size:.4f} (MC SE: {mc_se:.4f})")
    print(f"  Nominal 5% coverage within 2 SEs: {abs(emp_size - 0.05) <= 2.0 * mc_se}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The asymptotic \(\chi^2(q - k)\) distribution of the J-test requires that the weighting matrix is strictly optimal: \(W_T = \hat{S}_T^{-1}\). If any other weighting matrix is used—such as the identity \(W = I\)—the test statistic \(T \bar{g}_T^\top W \bar{g}_T\) does not follow a chi-squared distribution, but rather a weighted sum of independent 1-degree-of-freedom chi-squared variables with unequal eigenvalues. Furthermore, in finite samples with many moments (large \(q\)), the J-test suffers substantial over-rejection under the null, falsely diagnosing model failure when the economic theory is correct.

Misconceptions

  1. A statistically insignificant J-test (p-value > 0.05) proves that the underlying economic model is correctly specified.

    Failing to reject the null hypothesis does not prove the model is true. The J-test evaluates only whether the overidentifying moments are mutually consistent with each other. If all instruments are correlated with structural disturbances in the exact same direction, or if instruments are weak, the J-test suffers from very low statistical power and will fail to reject even when every individual moment condition is false.

  2. The J-test can be performed in just-identified models where q = k.

    In a just-identified model, \(q = k\), so the degrees of freedom are \(q - k = 0\). As established in Lesson 1, the sample moments satisfy \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}\) exactly, which forces \(J_T = T \cdot \mathbf{0}^\top \hat{S}^{-1} \mathbf{0} = 0\) identically. No overidentifying restrictions exist to test, and the J-statistic provides zero degrees of freedom for hypothesis testing.

  3. A rejection by the J-test indicates exactly which moment condition is invalid.

    The J-test is an omnibus test of all \(q\) moment conditions simultaneously. A rejection signifies that the system of \(q\) restrictions cannot be satisfied jointly by any parameter vector \(\theta \in \Theta\). It cannot isolate which specific instrument or moment equation is contaminated without further subset testing (such as C-tests / difference-in-Sargan tests).

Practice Problems

An overidentified asset pricing model specifies \(q = 6\) test assets to estimate \(k = 2\) factor risk premia. Using a sample of \(T = 300\) months, the minimized GMM criterion value under optimal weighting is \(Q_T(\hat{\theta}) = 0.035\). Compute the J-statistic, state its degrees of freedom, and compare it against the 5% critical value of \(\chi^2(4) = 9.488\).

Worked Solution

The J-statistic is \(J_T = T \cdot Q_T(\hat{\theta}) = 300 \times 0.035 = 10.500\). The degrees of freedom are \(df = q - k = 6 - 2 = 4\). Comparing against the critical threshold: \(J_T = 10.500 > 9.488\). The null hypothesis of correct model specification is rejected at the 5% significance level (p-value \(\approx 0.0328\)).

In an instrumental variables regression with \(q = 3\) instruments and \(k = 1\) endogenous regressor on \(T = 400\) observations, the J-statistic evaluates to \(J_T = 3.20\). State the null hypothesis, determine the degrees of freedom, and state whether the instrument orthogonality conditions are accepted at the 5% significance level (\(\chi^2_{0.95}(2) = 5.991\)).

Worked Solution

The null hypothesis is \(H_0: \E[z_t \varepsilon_t] = \mathbf{0}\) (all 3 instruments are exogenous). The degrees of freedom are \(df = q - k = 3 - 1 = 2\). The 5% critical value for \(\chi^2(2)\) is 5.991. Because \(J_T = 3.20 \le 5.991\), the null hypothesis cannot be rejected at the 5% level (p-value \(\approx 0.2019\)). The instruments are accepted as mutually consistent with exogeneity.

Suppose \(q = 5\) moments are used to estimate \(k = 5\) parameters. An analyst computes \(J_T = 0.000\). Explain why this outcome is guaranteed mathematically regardless of whether the economic model is true.

Worked Solution

When \(q = k = 5\), the model is just-identified. With 5 equations in 5 unknowns, the sample moment equations \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}\) are solved exactly. Substituting into the J-statistic yields \(J_T = T \cdot \mathbf{0}^\top \hat{S}^{-1} \mathbf{0} = 0.000\). The test has 0 degrees of freedom and cannot test model specification because the sample moments vanish mechanically.

Prove that if an arbitrary weighting matrix \(W \ne S^{-1}\) is used in the GMM criterion, the statistic \(T \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta})^\top W \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta})\) does not follow a \(\chi^2(q - k)\) distribution.

Worked Solution

Under an arbitrary weight \(W\), \(\sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = [I - D (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W] \sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\theta_0)\). Let \(A = I - D (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W\). The quadratic form is \(J_W = \xi^\top S^{1/2} A^\top W A S^{1/2} \xi\), where \(\xi \sim \Normal(\mathbf{0}, I)\). The matrix in this quadratic form is \(\Omega = S^{1/2} A^\top W A S^{1/2}\). By Craig's theorem on quadratic forms, \(J_W\) follows a chi-squared distribution if and only if \(\Omega\) is idempotent (\(\Omega^2 = \Omega\)). A direct calculation shows that \(\Omega^2 = \Omega\) if and only if \(W = c S^{-1}\). For any \(W \ne c S^{-1}\), \(\Omega\) is not idempotent, and \(J_W\) is distributed as a sum of weighted chi-squares \(\sum_{i=1}^q \lambda_i z_i^2\) with unequal eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\).

Show that the projection matrix \(P_D = D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1}\) is idempotent and has rank \(k\).

Worked Solution

Evaluate the square: \(P_D^2 = \left[ D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} \right] \left[ D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} \right] = D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} \left[ D^\top S^{-1} D \right] (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1}\). The bracketed product cancels with the following inverse: \(P_D^2 = D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} I \cdot D^\top S^{-1} = P_D\). Thus, \(P_D\) is idempotent. For an idempotent matrix, its rank equals its trace: \(\operatorname{rank}(P_D) = \operatorname{tr}(P_D) = \operatorname{tr}\left( D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} \right) = \operatorname{tr}\left( (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} (D^\top S^{-1} D) \right) = \operatorname{tr}(I_k) = k\).

Derive the degrees of freedom of the difference-in-Sargan (C-test) statistic \(C_T = J_{\text{full}} - J_{\text{restricted}}\) for testing the exogeneity of a subset of \(p\) suspect instruments.

Worked Solution

Let the full model have \(q\) instruments and \(k\) parameters, so \(J_{\text{full}} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(q - k)\). The restricted model drops \(p\) suspect instruments, using only \(q - p\) instruments, so \(J_{\text{restricted}} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(q - p - k)\). When estimated using the optimal block-diagonal weight matrix, the difference statistic \(C_T = J_{\text{full}} - J_{\text{restricted}}\) isolates the contribution of the \(p\) suspect instruments. The difference in degrees of freedom is \((q - k) - (q - p - k) = q - k - q + p + k = p\). Under the null hypothesis that the \(p\) suspect instruments are valid, \(C_T \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(p)\).

In an empirical evaluation of the CAPM on 25 size and value portfolios (\(q = 25\)) with market factor (\(k = 1\)), the GMM J-statistic is \(J_T = 52.4\) on \(T = 600\) monthly periods. Calculate the degrees of freedom, the approximate 5% critical value (using \(\chi^2_{0.95}(24) \approx 36.42\)), and formulate the economic conclusion.

Worked Solution

The degrees of freedom are \(df = q - k = 25 - 1 = 24\). The 5% critical threshold is 36.42. Because \(J_T = 52.4 > 36.42\) (p-value \(\approx 0.0007\)), the null hypothesis that the CAPM correctly prices the 25 portfolios is rejected with overwhelming statistical significance. The pricing errors across the size and value dimensions are too large to be explained by random sampling error under the single market factor.

A macroeconomic panel has \(T = 60\) observations. A researcher tests 12 moment conditions to estimate 2 parameters, obtaining \(J_T = 18.3\) with p-value 0.051. Explain why finite-sample size distortions suggest this p-value should not be trusted.

Worked Solution

With \(T = 60\) and \(q = 12\), the sample covariance matrix \(\hat{S}_T\) of dimension \(12 \times 12\) is estimated with substantial noise. Monte Carlo studies (e.g., Hansen, Heaton, and Yaron 1996) prove that when \(q/T\) is large (here \(12/60 = 0.20\)), the empirical distribution of the J-statistic is heavily shifted to the right relative to the asymptotic \(\chi^2(10)\) distribution. The true finite-sample 5% critical value is considerably larger than the asymptotic critical value of 18.31. An asymptotic p-value of 0.051 reflects size distortion, and bootstrap or Bartlett-corrected critical values are necessary.

An analyst adds an irrelevant noise instrument (pure Gaussian white noise uncorrelated with all variables) to an overidentified system. Describe how this affects \(q\), the degrees of freedom, and the behavior of the J-statistic.

Worked Solution

Adding a pure noise instrument increases the number of moment conditions from \(q\) to \(q + 1\), increasing the degrees of freedom from \(q - k\) to \(q - k + 1\). Because the instrument is genuinely uncorrelated with errors, its population moment is zero, so the null hypothesis remains true. However, because it contains no information about \(\theta\), it contributes random sampling variance to \(\hat{S}_T\), inflating the finite-sample bias of the GMM estimator and diluting the statistical power of the J-test against other misspecified moments.

Analyze the local power properties of Hansen's J-test against drifting local alternatives of the form \(\E[g(w_t, \theta_0)] = \delta / \sqrt{T}\).

Worked Solution

Under local alternatives where moment conditions are violated by a Pitman drift \(\E[g(w_t, \theta_0)] = \delta / \sqrt{T}\), the normalized sample moments have expectation \(\sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(\delta, S)\). The asymptotic distribution of the J-statistic becomes a non-central chi-squared distribution: \(J_T \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(q - k, \lambda)\), where the non-centrality parameter is \(\lambda = \delta^\top S^{-1/2} M S^{-1/2} \delta\). The test has power to detect violations in directions that lie in the null space of the Jacobian \(D\) (\(M \delta \ne \mathbf{0}\)). If the misspecification \(\delta\) lies entirely in the range space of \(D\) (\(\delta = D \gamma\)), then \(M \delta = \mathbf{0}\), \(\lambda = 0\), and the J-test has zero power, as the parameter estimates absorb the misspecification completely.

Why is the J-test often described as having poor power against 'factor zoo' proliferation in empirical cross-sectional asset pricing?

Worked Solution

As researchers add more candidate factors and test assets, the number of moment conditions \(q\) proliferates rapidly. The non-centrality parameter \(\lambda\) under misspecification grows at most linearly, but the degrees of freedom \(q - k\) grow proportionally to \(q\). The mean of the chi-squared distribution shifts right by \(q - k\) and its standard deviation grows as \(\sqrt{2(q - k)}\). The signal-to-noise ratio \(\lambda / \sqrt{2(q - k)}\) degrades when many uninformative moments are included, causing the power of the omnibus J-test to collapse, failing to reject models with economically severe pricing distortions.

Evaluate how windmeijer (2005) finite-sample corrections address standard error underestimation in two-step GMM and their relation to J-test size.

Worked Solution

Windmeijer (2005) recognized that standard two-step GMM standard errors treat the weighting matrix \(\hat{W}_T = \hat{S}_T(\hat{\theta}_1)^{-1}\) as fixed, ignoring the sampling variability of the first-step estimator \(\hat{\theta}_1\). Windmeijer developed a Taylor series expansion of the second-step estimator that incorporates the derivative of \(\hat{W}_T\) with respect to \(\hat{\theta}_1\), producing a robust finite-sample variance correction that inflates standard errors appropriately. While the Windmeijer correction resolves downward bias in Wald t-statistics, the J-test statistic itself requires bootstrap or Bartlett adjustments because it depends directly on the eigenvalues of \(\hat{S}_T\).

Key Results

Test Component Mathematical Form Statistical Role
Hansen's J-Statistic \(J_T = T \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta})^\top \hat{S}_T^{-1} \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta})\) Omnibus quadratic distance metric measuring mutual consistency of all moment restrictions.
Null Distribution \(J_T \xrightarrow{\;d\;} \chi^2(q - k)\) Exact asymptotic chi-squared limit under correct specification with efficient weighting.
Degrees of Freedom \(df = q - k\) Number of moment equations minus the number of parameters estimated.
Decision Rule Reject \(H_0\) if \(J_T > \chi^2_{1-\alpha}(q - k)\) Rejection provides evidence of structural model misspecification or instrument invalidity.
Non-Central Limit \(J_T \xrightarrow{\;d\;} \chi^2(q - k, \lambda)\) Asymptotic distribution under local Pitman alternatives with drift \(\delta / \sqrt{T}\).

Retrieval Practice

  1. What are the degrees of freedom for Hansen's J-test in an overidentified GMM model with \(q\) moments and \(k\) parameters?

    The degrees of freedom equal \(q - k\), because the GMM first-order conditions set \(k\) linear combinations of the sample moments to zero.

  2. Under what essential condition does Hansen's J-statistic follow a chi-squared distribution asymptotically?

    Chi-squared convergence requires optimal weighting: with any arbitrary weight \(W \ne S^{-1}\), the statistic is a sum of weighted chi-squares with unequal weights.

  3. What is the value of Hansen's J-statistic in a just-identified GMM model (\(q = k\))?

    When \(q = k\), the sample moments can be set to zero exactly: \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}\), making \(J_T = T \cdot \mathbf{0}^\top \hat{S}^{-1} \mathbf{0} = 0\).

  4. What does a rejection of the null hypothesis in Hansen's J-test indicate?

    A rejection implies that the \(q\) population moment conditions are mutually incompatible, signifying model misspecification or invalid instruments.

  5. Why can Hansen's J-test fail to reject a severely misspecified model when instruments are weak?

    Weak instruments reduce the curvature of the GMM criterion and inflate variance, eroding the statistical power of the test to detect misspecification.

  6. What is the difference-in-Sargan (C-test) used for in GMM?

    The C-test subtracts the J-statistic of a restricted model from that of the full model to test whether a subset of \(p\) suspect instruments is valid.

  7. How does increasing the number of test assets \(q\) in a finite sample of size \(T\) typically distort the J-test?

    Noise in estimating high-dimensional covariance matrices \(\hat{S}\) shifts the finite-sample distribution of \(J_T\) to the right, causing severe over-rejection.

  8. What is the relationship between Hansen's J-test in linear IV and the classical Sargan (1958) test?

    Sargan's test assumes homoskedastic errors (\(S = \sigma^2 Z^\top Z / T\)); Hansen's J-test robustifies the test to arbitrary conditional heteroskedasticity and serial correlation.
