The Gibbons–Ross–Shanken (GRS) Test

Mean-Variance Portfolio Efficiency, Maximum Sharpe Ratios, and Exact Finite-Sample F Statistics

Why It Matters

A central tenet of modern portfolio theory is that benchmark factors span the mean-variance efficient frontier if and only if all test assets have zero abnormal returns (alphas). Testing zero intercepts asset by asset using separate univariate t-tests generates severe data-snooping distortions by ignoring cross-asset residual correlation. Gibbons, Ross, and Shanken (1989) formulated an exact finite-sample test that simultaneously evaluates the joint hypothesis that all N alphas are zero. By framing the test statistic as the percentage expansion in the squared Sharpe ratio, the GRS test connects linear regression algebra directly to portfolio geometry, providing the canonical hurdle every candidate factor model must overcome.

1. Mean-Variance Efficiency and the GRS Statistic

Let \(R_{it}\) denote the excess return on test portfolio \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) and let \(f_t \in \R^K\) denote a vector of \(K\) benchmark factor excess returns over \(t = 1, \dots, T\). We estimate the multivariate time-series regression for all \(N\) test assets simultaneously:

\[ \begin{aligned} R_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i^\top f_t + \varepsilon_{it}, \quad i = 1, \dots, N. \end{aligned} \]

Stacking across assets yields \(R_t = \alpha + B f_t + \varepsilon_t\), where \(\alpha = [\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_N]^\top \in \R^N\) and \(B = [\beta_1, \dots, \beta_N]^\top \in \R^{N \times K}\). The benchmark factors are mean-variance efficient with respect to the universe of test assets if and only if the joint hypothesis \(H_0: \alpha = \mathbf{0}_{N \times 1}\) holds.

Definition 6.1 · Gibbons–Ross–Shanken (GRS) Test

Let \(\hat{\alpha} \in \R^N\) denote the vector of ordinary least squares intercepts. Let \(\hat{\Sigma}\) denote the unbiased residual covariance matrix with divisor \(T - K - 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{T - K - 1} \sum_{t=1}^T \hat{\varepsilon}_t \hat{\varepsilon}_t^\top. \end{aligned} \]

Let \(\bar{f} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T f_t\) and \(\hat{\Sigma}_f = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^T (f_t - \bar{f})(f_t - \bar{f})^\top\). The factor Sharpe ratio squared is \(\SR_f^2 = \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}\). The Gibbons–Ross–Shanken (GRS) test statistic is:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{\text{GRS}} = \frac{T - N - K}{N} \left(1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}\right)^{-1} \hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \hat{\alpha}. \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 6.1 · Exact Finite-Sample Distribution of the GRS Statistic

Assumptions:

  1. A1 (Normality): The error vector \(\varepsilon_t\) is independently and identically distributed across time according to a multivariate normal distribution \(\Normal(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)\).
  2. A2 (Exogeneity): \(\varepsilon_t\) is independent of factor realizations \(f_s\) for all \(t, s\).
  3. A3 (Degrees of Freedom): Sample size satisfies \(T > N + K\), ensuring that \(\hat{\Sigma}\) is non-singular with probability 1.
  4. A4 (Null Hypothesis): The true intercept vector vanishes: \(\alpha = \mathbf{0}_{N \times 1}\).

Conclusion: Under Assumptions A1–A4, the test statistic follows an exact central F distribution with \(N\) numerator and \(T - N - K\) denominator degrees of freedom in finite samples:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{\text{GRS}} \sim F(N, T - N - K). \end{aligned} \]
Proof Geometric Derivation

Under the normality assumption conditional on factors \(\{f_t\}\), OLS theory guarantees that \(\hat{\alpha}\) is normally distributed:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\alpha} \mid \{f_t\} \sim \Normal\left(\mathbf{0}, \left(1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}\right) \frac{\Sigma}{T}\right). \end{aligned} \]

Simultaneously, the residual sum-of-squares matrix \((T - K - 1)\hat{\Sigma}\) follows an independent Wishart distribution with \(T - K - 1\) degrees of freedom: \((T - K - 1)\hat{\Sigma} \sim \mathcal{W}_N(T - K - 1, \Sigma)\). By the Hotelling \(T^2\) theorem, quadratic forms combining a normal vector with an independent inverted Wishart matrix follow Hotelling's \(T^2\) distribution:

\[ \begin{aligned} T^2 = \hat{\alpha}^\top \left[ \left(1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}\right) \frac{\hat{\Sigma}}{T} \right]^{-1} \hat{\alpha} \sim \frac{N (T - K - 1)}{T - N - K} F(N, T - N - K). \end{aligned} \]

Scaling \(T^2\) by \(\frac{T - N - K}{N (T - K - 1)}\) yields the exact finite-sample F-distributed statistic \(F_{\text{GRS}}\).

2. Portfolio Geometry: Maximum Sharpe Ratio Expansion

Gibbons, Ross, and Shanken proved that the quadratic form \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \hat{\alpha}\) has an extraordinarily transparent economic interpretation in terms of Sharpe ratio expansion.

Remark 6.1 · Geometric Frontier Expansion

Let \(\SR_f = \sqrt{\bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f}}\) be the maximum Sharpe ratio attainable using the \(K\) benchmark factors alone. Let \(\SR_q\) denote the maximum Sharpe ratio attainable by investing across the enlarged universe combining both the \(K\) factors and the \(N\) test assets. Gibbons, Ross, and Shanken showed that:

\[ \begin{aligned} \SR_q^2 - \SR_f^2 = \hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \hat{\alpha}. \end{aligned} \]

Substituting this geometric relationship into the GRS statistic yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{\text{GRS}} = \frac{T - N - K}{N} \left[ \frac{\SR_q^2 - \SR_f^2}{1 + \SR_f^2} \right]. \end{aligned} \]

The GRS test is a direct test of whether adding the \(N\) test assets produces a statistically significant expansion of the tangency portfolio's Sharpe ratio beyond what the benchmark factors already achieve.

Lab 1 · Gibbons–Ross–Shanken (GRS) Test Execution

Under the null hypothesis that all N test assets have zero abnormal alpha, the GRS test statistic computed on simulated test portfolios follows an exact F(N, T - N - K) distribution with empirical size matching the 5% nominal level within Monte Carlo tolerance.

"""Lab S5-L06-1: Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS) test of portfolio efficiency.

SIMULATED: generates synthetic excess returns for N=10 test portfolios across T=120 months
under a K=1 factor asset pricing model, evaluates the exact finite-sample GRS F-statistic,
and verifies nominal size via 1000 Monte Carlo replications under the null hypothesis.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
import scipy.stats


def compute_grs(returns: np.ndarray, factors: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Compute GRS F-statistic, factor Sharpe ratio, p-value, and critical value."""
    t_months, n_assets = returns.shape
    k_factors = factors.shape[1]

    # Time-series regressions
    x = np.column_stack([np.ones(t_months), factors])  # (T, K+1)
    # (X'X)^(-1) X' Y
    coefs = np.linalg.solve(x.T @ x, x.T @ returns)  # (K+1, N)
    alphas = coefs[0, :]  # (N,)
    resids = returns - x @ coefs  # (T, N)

    # Residual covariance matrix Sigma_hat (unbiased divisor T - K - 1)
    df_resid = t_months - k_factors - 1
    sigma_hat = (resids.T @ resids) / df_resid

    # Factor statistics
    f_bar = np.mean(factors, axis=0)  # (K,)
    f_cov = np.cov(factors, rowvar=False, ddof=1).reshape((k_factors, k_factors))
    sr2_f = float(f_bar.T @ np.linalg.solve(f_cov, f_bar))

    # GRS F-statistic:
    # F = [(T - N - K) / N] * [1 / (1 + SR_f^2)] * alpha' Sigma_hat^(-1) alpha
    d1 = n_assets
    d2 = t_months - n_assets - k_factors
    quad_alpha = float(alphas.T @ np.linalg.solve(sigma_hat, alphas))
    grs_stat = (float(d2) / float(d1)) * (1.0 / (1.0 + sr2_f)) * quad_alpha

    p_val = float(scipy.stats.f.sf(grs_stat, dfn=d1, dfd=d2))
    crit_val = float(scipy.stats.f.ppf(0.95, dfn=d1, dfd=d2))
    return grs_stat, sr2_f, p_val, crit_val


def main() -> None:
    seed = 505
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_assets = 10
    t_months = 120
    k_factors = 1
    d1 = n_assets
    d2 = t_months - n_assets - k_factors
    reps = 1000

    f_mean = 0.007
    f_sd = 0.045
    betas_true = rng.uniform(0.6, 1.4, size=n_assets)
    sigma_true = 0.04

    # Primary sample under the null: alpha_i = 0 for all i
    f_0 = rng.normal(f_mean, f_sd, size=(t_months, k_factors))
    e_0 = rng.normal(0.0, sigma_true, size=(t_months, n_assets))
    r_0 = f_0 @ betas_true.reshape(1, n_assets) + e_0

    grs_stat, sr2_f, p_val, crit_val = compute_grs(r_0, f_0)

    # Monte Carlo simulation of exact F test under null
    rejections = 0
    for _ in range(reps):
        f_r = rng.normal(f_mean, f_sd, size=(t_months, k_factors))
        e_r = rng.normal(0.0, sigma_true, size=(t_months, n_assets))
        r_r = f_r @ betas_true.reshape(1, n_assets) + e_r
        f_stat, _, _, _ = compute_grs(r_r, f_r)
        if f_stat > crit_val:
            rejections += 1

    emp_size = rejections / reps
    mc_se = float(np.sqrt(emp_size * (1.0 - emp_size) / reps))

    print("Lab S5-L06-1: Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS) Portfolio Efficiency Test")
    print(f"  Test portfolios N: {n_assets}, Months T: {t_months}, Factors K: {k_factors} (SIMULATED, seed {seed})")
    print(f"  Degrees of freedom: df1={d1}, df2={d2}")
    print(f"  Factor Sharpe ratio squared (SR_f^2): {sr2_f:.4f}")
    print(f"  Primary sample GRS F-statistic: {grs_stat:.4f} (p-value: {p_val:.4f})")
    print(f"  F({d1}, {d2}) 95% critical threshold: {crit_val:.4f}")
    print(f"  Primary sample null accepted (F <= crit): {grs_stat <= crit_val}")
    print(f"  Monte Carlo empirical size under H_0 (R={reps}): {emp_size:.4f} (MC SE: {mc_se:.4f})")
    print(f"  Nominal 5% coverage within 2 SEs: {abs(emp_size - 0.05) <= 2.0 * mc_se}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The exact finite-sample F-distribution of Theorem 6.1 is conditioned strictly on Assumption A1: returns must be multivariate Gaussian and serially independent. Financial returns exhibit substantial excess kurtosis (fat tails) and volatility clustering (GARCH effects). Under leptokurtic distributions, the finite-sample distribution of \(F_{\text{GRS}}\) shifts, leading to significant over-rejection of the null hypothesis in moderate samples. Furthermore, when the number of test assets \(N\) approaches \(T - K\), the residual covariance matrix \(\hat{\Sigma}\) becomes ill-conditioned, blowing up \(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\) and invalidating the test without shrinkage or high-dimensional regularizations.

Misconceptions

  1. A factor model is satisfactory if most individual portfolio alphas have t-statistics below 1.96.

    Testing individual alphas one by one commits the multiple testing fallacy. Even if every single t-statistic satisfies \(|t_i| < 1.96\), the joint quadratic form \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \hat{\alpha}\) can easily reject \(H_0\) with overwhelming significance by exploiting the positive cross-sectional correlation of pricing errors. The GRS test evaluates the joint hypothesis \(\alpha_1 = \dots = \alpha_N = 0\) properly.

  2. The GRS test is valid when the number of test portfolios N exceeds the number of time periods T.

    The GRS statistic requires inverting the \(N \times N\) sample residual covariance matrix \(\hat{\Sigma}\). The rank of \(\hat{\Sigma}\) is at most \(T - K - 1\). If \(N \ge T - K\), the sample covariance matrix is singular and cannot be inverted, causing the GRS test statistic to be undefined.

  3. The GRS test and the asymptotic Wald chi-squared test are identical in small samples.

    The asymptotic Wald chi-squared test multiplies the quadratic form by \(T\) and uses a \(\chi^2(N)\) critical value. In finite samples, the Wald test severely over-rejects the null hypothesis because it ignores the degrees-of-freedom penalty for estimating \(\Sigma\). The GRS test provides the exact finite-sample degrees-of-freedom correction through the factor \(\frac{T - N - K}{N (T - K - 1)}\).

Practice Problems

In an asset pricing test with \(N = 10\) test portfolios, \(K = 1\) factor, and \(T = 120\) months, compute the numerator and denominator degrees of freedom \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) for the GRS test.

Worked Solution

The numerator degrees of freedom equal the number of test assets: \(d_1 = N = 10\). The denominator degrees of freedom equal sample size minus assets minus factors: \(d_2 = T - N - K = 120 - 10 - 1 = 109\). The exact null distribution is \(F(10, 109)\).

Suppose \(T = 180\), \(N = 5\), and \(K = 1\). The estimated factor Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_f = 0.20\). The quadratic form of estimated alphas is \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \hat{\alpha} = 0.1300\). Compute the GRS F-statistic.

Worked Solution

First compute the squared factor Sharpe ratio: \(\SR_f^2 = (0.20)^2 = 0.0400\). The scaling term is \((1 + \SR_f^2)^{-1} = 1 / 1.0400 \approx 0.9615\). The degrees-of-freedom scaling is \(\frac{T - N - K}{N} = \frac{180 - 5 - 1}{5} = \frac{174}{5} = 34.8\). Multiplying terms: \(F_{\text{GRS}} = 34.8 \times \frac{0.1300}{1.0400} = 34.8 \times 0.1250 = 4.350\). With \(F_{0.95}(5, 174) \approx 2.266\), the null hypothesis is rejected.

An analyst tests \(N = 2\) independent test assets against a single factor. Estimated alphas are \(\hat{\alpha} = [0.003, -0.004]^\top\) with diagonal residual covariance \(\hat{\Sigma} = \operatorname{diag}(0.0009, 0.0016)\). Compute the quadratic form \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \hat{\alpha}\).

Worked Solution

Because \(\hat{\Sigma}\) is diagonal, the quadratic form is the sum of squared standardized intercepts: \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \hat{\alpha} = \frac{(0.003)^2}{0.0009} + \frac{(-0.004)^2}{0.0016} = \frac{0.000009}{0.0009} + \frac{0.000016}{0.0016} = 0.0100 + 0.0100 = 0.0200\).

Prove that as \(T \to \infty\) with \(N\) and \(K\) fixed, the product \(N \cdot F_{\text{GRS}}\) converges in distribution to a chi-squared random variable with \(N\) degrees of freedom.

Worked Solution

Recall that \(F_{\text{GRS}} \sim F(N, T - N - K)\). By classical distribution theory, if a random variable \(X\) follows \(F(d_1, d_2)\), then as the denominator degrees of freedom \(d_2 \to \infty\), the product \(d_1 X\) converges in distribution to a \(\chi^2(d_1)\) random variable: \(d_1 X \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(d_1)\). Here, \(d_1 = N\) and \(d_2 = T - N - K\). As \(T \to \infty\), \(d_2 \to \infty\). Therefore, \(N \cdot F_{\text{GRS}} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(N)\). This demonstrates that the GRS test is the exact finite-sample counterpart to the asymptotic Wald test.

Derive the identity \(\SR_q^2 - \SR_f^2 = \hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \hat{\alpha}\) using the partitioned matrix inverse of the combined test asset and factor return covariance matrix.

Worked Solution

Let \(Y = [f^\top, R^\top]^\top\) denote the combined \((K + N)\)-vector of excess returns. The expected return vector is \(\mu_Y = [\mu_f^\top, \alpha^\top + \mu_f^\top B^\top]^\top\) and its covariance matrix is partitioned as \(\Omega = \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_f & \Sigma_f B^\top \\ B \Sigma_f & B \Sigma_f B^\top + \Sigma \end{pmatrix}\). The squared maximum Sharpe ratio of the combined universe is \(\SR_q^2 = \mu_Y^\top \Omega^{-1} \mu_Y\). Applying the block matrix inversion theorem (Schur complement), the inverse \(\Omega^{-1}\) can be decomposed into a block for the factors alone and a projection onto the residuals. Multiplying out the quadratic form, the factor terms reduce to \(\mu_f^\top \Sigma_f^{-1} \mu_f = \SR_f^2\), and the remaining residual component simplifies exactly to \(\alpha^\top \Sigma^{-1} \alpha\). Hence, \(\SR_q^2 = \SR_f^2 + \alpha^\top \Sigma^{-1} \alpha\).

Explain why the term \((1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f})^{-1}\) appears in the denominator of the GRS statistic.

Worked Solution

In the time-series regression \(R_t = \alpha + B f_t + \varepsilon_t\), the intercept estimator is \(\hat{\alpha} = \bar{R} - \hat{B} \bar{f}\). The variance of \(\hat{\alpha}\) conditional on the factor sample realizations depends on the distance of the factor mean \(\bar{f}\) from zero. Standard regression theory establishes that \(\Var(\hat{\alpha} \mid \{f_t\}) = \frac{1}{T} \left[ 1 + \bar{f}^\top \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \bar{f} \right] \Sigma\). Because the sampling variance of \(\hat{\alpha}\) is inflated by the factor \(1 + \SR_f^2\), standardizing \(\hat{\alpha}\) to form an F-statistic requires dividing by this term.

A quant tests the CAPM on 25 sorted portfolios using 30 years of monthly data (\(T = 360\)). The market Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_m = 0.12\) per month. The tangency portfolio of the combined 25 portfolios and market achieves \(\SR_q = 0.28\) per month. Compute the GRS F-statistic and test whether the CAPM is rejected (\(F_{0.95}(25, 334) \approx 1.545\)).

Worked Solution

Compute squared Sharpe ratios: \(\SR_m^2 = 0.12^2 = 0.0144\) and \(\SR_q^2 = 0.28^2 = 0.0784\). The difference is \(\SR_q^2 - \SR_m^2 = 0.0784 - 0.0144 = 0.0640\). The degrees of freedom are \(N = 25\) and \(T - N - K = 360 - 25 - 1 = 334\). The GRS statistic is \(F_{\text{GRS}} = \frac{334}{25} \left[ \frac{0.0640}{1 + 0.0144} \right] = 13.36 \times \frac{0.0640}{1.0144} = 13.36 \times 0.06309 \approx 0.8429\). Because \(F_{\text{GRS}} = 0.8429 \le 1.545\) (p-value \(\approx 0.686\)), the CAPM cannot be rejected on this sample.

Explain why portfolio sorting can artificially cause the GRS test to reject valid factor models when test assets are sorted on characteristics known to be correlated with in-sample historical returns (data-snooping bias).

Worked Solution

When portfolios are formed by sorting stocks on anomalies discovered from the same historical sample, the sort deliberately selects assets that experienced large positive or negative return realizations. This sorting mechanism mechanically maximizes in-sample \(\hat{\alpha}\). In the GRS formula, sorting inflates the numerator \(\SR_q^2\), producing an artificially large F-statistic that rejects the factor model even if the true population alpha is zero outside the discovery window.

A researcher evaluates a 5-factor model on \(N = 40\) portfolios with \(T = 60\) monthly observations. What numerical and statistical obstacles prevent running the GRS test?

Worked Solution

The denominator degrees of freedom are \(d_2 = T - N - K = 60 - 40 - 5 = 15\). While \(15 > 0\) allows mathematical computation, estimating a \(40 \times 40\) covariance matrix (820 distinct parameters) from only 15 effective degrees of freedom produces extreme estimation noise. The smallest eigenvalues of \(\hat{\Sigma}\) will be severely downward-biased, causing \(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\) to explode and generating catastrophic finite-sample over-rejection.

Evaluate how MacKinlay and Richardson (1991) modified the GRS test to be robust against generalized non-normal and conditionally heteroskedastic return distributions.

Worked Solution

MacKinlay and Richardson (1991) used GMM to derive robust Wald statistics for the joint hypothesis \(\alpha = \mathbf{0}\) without assuming Gaussian errors. They showed that when asset returns have fat tails, the fourth moments of returns inflate the asymptotic variance of \(\hat{\alpha}\). In the GMM framework, the covariance of \(\hat{\alpha}\) is estimated directly from the sample average of \(\hat{\varepsilon}_{it} \hat{\varepsilon}_{jt}\), which automatically incorporates excess kurtosis and conditional heteroskedasticity, eliminating the spurious over-rejection of the classical GRS test in financial markets.

Discuss how Ledoit and Wolf (2004) shrinkage covariance estimators can be integrated into high-dimensional GRS testing when N is comparable to T.

Worked Solution

When \(N/T\) is not small, the sample covariance matrix \(\hat{\Sigma}\) is ill-conditioned. Ledoit and Wolf (2004) proposed shrinking \(\hat{\Sigma}\) toward a structured target \(F\) (such as an identity matrix or single-index model): \(\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{shrink}} = \delta F + (1 - \delta) \hat{\Sigma}\), where the optimal shrinkage intensity \(\delta \in (0, 1)\) is chosen to minimize Frobenius loss. Substituting \(\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{shrink}}^{-1}\) into the GRS quadratic form prevents eigenvalue collapse and restores well-conditioned test statistics in large-cross-section asset pricing.

Analyze the relationship between the GRS test of portfolio efficiency and the spanning tests of Huberman and Kandel (1987).

Worked Solution

The GRS test evaluates mean-variance efficiency with a risk-free rate, testing whether \(\alpha = \mathbf{0}\). Huberman and Kandel (1987) developed mean-variance spanning tests when there is no riskless asset, testing whether the benchmark factors span the entire efficient frontier of the test assets. Spanning requires two joint conditions: \(\alpha = \mathbf{0}\) and \(B \mathbf{1}_K = \mathbf{1}_N\) (factor loadings sum to one). Spanning tests use a bivariate F-distribution or likelihood ratio test that nests the GRS alpha test as a sub-hypothesis.

Retrieval Practice

  1. What null hypothesis is tested by the Gibbons–Ross–Shanken (GRS) test?

    The GRS test evaluates whether all \(N\) test assets have zero abnormal pricing intercepts simultaneously.

  2. Under what distributional assumption does the GRS statistic follow an exact finite-sample F-distribution?

    Exact F-distribution derivation requires multivariate Gaussian disturbances independent of factor realizations.

  3. What are the numerator and denominator degrees of freedom for the GRS test?

    The numerator degrees of freedom is the number of assets \(N\); the denominator is \(T - N - K\).

  4. What economic quantity is algebraically equal to \(\hat{\alpha}^\top \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} \hat{\alpha}\)?

    Gibbons, Ross, and Shanken proved that the quadratic form measures the gain in the squared Sharpe ratio from adding the test assets.

  5. Why is running individual t-tests on each alpha invalid for testing factor model efficiency?

    Individual t-tests ignore contemporaneous residual covariances, missing joint departures that are significant in the metric of \(\Sigma^{-1}\).

  6. What occurs mathematically if the number of test assets \(N\) satisfies \(N \ge T - K\)?

    Because \(\hat{\Sigma}\) has rank at most \(T - K - 1\), when \(N \ge T - K\) it is rank-deficient and non-invertible.

  7. How does excess kurtosis (fat tails) in asset returns affect the finite-sample performance of the GRS test?

    Fat tails violate the normal Wishart assumption, inflating the finite-sample distribution of the quadratic form and causing over-rejection.

  8. What is the asymptotic relationship between the GRS F-statistic and the Wald chi-squared statistic?

    As denominator degrees of freedom \(T - N - K \to \infty\), \(N \cdot F(N, T - N - K) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(N)\).
