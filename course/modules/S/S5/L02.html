Efficient Weighting & the Sandwich Covariance

Optimal Weighting Matrices, Gauss–Markov Analogs, and Two-Step Feasible Estimation

Why It Matters

In an overidentified system, an infinity of distinct weighting matrices yields consistent parameter estimates, but each choice produces a different asymptotic sampling variance. Selecting an arbitrary matrix, such as the identity, weights noisy moments equally with highly informative moments, squandering precision. Hansen's (1982) efficient weighting theorem proves that choosing the inverse of the long-run moment covariance matrix achieves the lower bound on asymptotic variance within the class of GMM estimators. Implementing two-step feasible efficient GMM tightens parameter confidence bounds, improves the statistical power of asset pricing factor tests, and provides the foundation for specification testing.

1. The Sandwich Covariance and Weighting Efficiency

In Lesson 1, we established that for any symmetric positive semi-definite matrix \(W_T \xrightarrow{p} W\), the generalized method of moments estimator \(\hat{\theta}_T\) satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{\;d\;} \Normal(\mathbf{0}, V(W)), \end{aligned} \]

where the asymptotic variance is given by the sandwich formula:

\[ \begin{aligned} V(W) = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}. \end{aligned} \]

Here, \(D = \E[\nabla_\theta g(w_t, \theta_0)] \in \R^{q \times k}\) is the expected Jacobian of moments and \(S = \lim_{T \to \infty} \Var(\sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\theta_0)) \in \R^{q \times q}\) is the spectral density of the moment conditions at zero frequency.

Definition 2.1 · Efficient GMM Weighting Matrix

An efficient GMM weighting matrix \(W^*\) is any symmetric positive definite matrix satisfying:

\[ \begin{aligned} W^* \propto S^{-1}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(S = \lim_{T \to \infty} \Var(\sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\theta_0))\). The resulting asymptotic variance attains the efficiency lower bound:

\[ \begin{aligned} V^* = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}. \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 2.1 · Hansen's Optimal Weighting Theorem

Assumptions:

  1. A1: The moment conditions \(\E[g(w_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}\) identify \(\theta_0\) with full-rank Jacobian \(D \in \R^{q \times k}\) (\(\operatorname{Rank}(D) = k\)).
  2. A2: The long-run covariance matrix \(S = \lim_{T \to \infty} \Var(\sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\theta_0))\) is finite, symmetric, and strictly positive definite.
  3. A3: \(W\) is any symmetric positive definite \(q \times q\) matrix.

Conclusion: The asymptotic covariance matrix under \(W^* = S^{-1}\) is weakly smaller than the asymptotic covariance under any alternative weighting matrix \(W\):

\[ \begin{aligned} V(W) - V(S^{-1}) \ge \mathbf{0}, \end{aligned} \]

in the matrix Loewner sense (the difference is positive semi-definite).

Proof

Let \(A = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W\) and \(A^* = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1}\). Notice that both \(A\) and \(A^*\) are \(k \times q\) left-inverse projection matrices for \(D\): \(A D = I_k\) and \(A^* D = I_k\). The sandwich variance is \(V(W) = A S A^\top\), and the efficient variance is \(V(S^{-1}) = A^* S (A^*)^\top = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\).

Consider the difference \((A - A^*) S (A - A^*)^\top\). Because \(S\) is positive definite, this quadratic form is necessarily positive semi-definite:

\[ \begin{aligned} (A - A^*) S (A - A^*)^\top \ge \mathbf{0}. \end{aligned} \]

Expanding the product:

\[ \begin{aligned} (A - A^*) S (A - A^*)^\top &= A S A^\top - A S (A^*)^\top - A^* S A^\top + A^* S (A^*)^\top. \end{aligned} \]

Evaluating the cross-terms: \(A S (A^*)^\top = A S \left[ S^{-1} D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} \right] = (A D) (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = I_k (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = V(S^{-1})\). Similarly, \(A^* S A^\top = V(S^{-1})\). Substituting these equalities:

\[ \begin{aligned} (A - A^*) S (A - A^*)^\top &= V(W) - V(S^{-1}) - V(S^{-1}) + V(S^{-1}) \\ &= V(W) - V(S^{-1}). \end{aligned} \]

Since the left-hand side is positive semi-definite, \(V(W) - V(S^{-1}) \ge \mathbf{0}\).

2. Two-Step Feasible and Iterated GMM

Because the true parameter \(\theta_0\) is unknown, the optimal weighting matrix \(S^{-1}\) cannot be evaluated directly from theory. In practice, estimation proceeds via a two-step feasible procedure.

Definition 2.2 · Two-Step Feasible GMM

The two-step feasible GMM algorithm proceeds as follows:

  1. Step 1 (Initial Consistent Estimation): Choose a convenient positive definite initial weight matrix \(W_T^{(1)}\), typically the identity matrix \(W_T^{(1)} = I_q\) or a diagonal matrix scaling moments by their empirical variances. Compute the initial GMM estimate: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\theta}_T^{(1)} = \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} \bar{g}_T(\theta)^\top W_T^{(1)} \bar{g}_T(\theta). \end{aligned} \]
  2. Covariance Estimation: Using the first-step residuals \(g(w_t, \hat{\theta}_T^{(1)})\), compute a consistent estimator \(\hat{S}_T\) of the long-run covariance matrix. For conditionally heteroskedastic but serially independent moments: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{S}_T = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T g(w_t, \hat{\theta}_T^{(1)}) g(w_t, \hat{\theta}_T^{(1)})^\top. \end{aligned} \]
  3. Step 2 (Efficient Estimation): Invert the covariance estimate to form the optimal weight matrix \(W_T^{(2)} = \hat{S}_T^{-1}\). Compute the efficient GMM estimate: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\theta}_T^{(2)} = \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} \bar{g}_T(\theta)^\top \hat{S}_T^{-1} \bar{g}_T(\theta). \end{aligned} \]
Remark 2.1 · Iterated GMM

Iterated GMM continues the cycle: the second-step estimate \(\hat{\theta}_T^{(2)}\) is used to re-estimate \(\hat{S}_T\), yielding a third-step estimate \(\hat{\theta}_T^{(3)}\), iterating until convergence \(\|\hat{\theta}_T^{(m+1)} - \hat{\theta}_T^{(m)}\| < \epsilon\). While iterated GMM has the same asymptotic distribution as the two-step estimator, it eliminates dependence on the arbitrary choice of initial weight matrix \(W_T^{(1)}\) in finite samples.

Lab 1 · Variance Reduction Under Efficient Weighting

Under conditional heteroskedasticity, two-step GMM with optimal weighting W = S^(-1) achieves strictly lower analytical asymptotic variance and empirical Monte Carlo variance than one-step identity weighting.

"""Lab S5-L02-1: Identity versus efficient weighting in over-identified GMM.

SIMULATED: generates synthetic samples for an over-identified linear model (q=3, k=1)
with conditional heteroskedasticity, computes two-step GMM with identity weighting and
efficient weighting W = S^(-1), and verifies variance reduction under optimal weighting.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def estimate_two_step(z: np.ndarray, x: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Perform two-step GMM estimation and return estimates and asymptotic variances."""
    n = len(y)
    s_zx = (z.T @ x) / n  # (q,)
    s_zy = (z.T @ y) / n  # (q,)

    # Step 1: Identity weight W1 = I
    denom1 = float(s_zx.T @ s_zx)
    theta1 = float(s_zx.T @ s_zy) / denom1

    # Residuals and moment covariance estimator S_hat
    resid1 = y - x * theta1
    g_mat = z * resid1[:, np.newaxis]  # (n, q)
    s_hat = (g_mat.T @ g_mat) / n  # (q, q)
    s_inv = np.linalg.inv(s_hat)

    # Asymptotic variance for Step 1 (sandwich formula)
    # D = -s_zx => (D' W D)^(-1) D' W S W D (D' W D)^(-1)
    avar1 = float((s_zx.T @ s_hat @ s_zx) / (denom1**2))

    # Step 2: Efficient weight W2 = S_inv
    denom2 = float(s_zx.T @ s_inv @ s_zx)
    theta2 = float(s_zx.T @ s_inv @ s_zy) / denom2
    avar2 = float(1.0 / denom2)

    return theta1, theta2, avar1, avar2


def main() -> None:
    seed = 101
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = 600
    q = 3
    theta_true = 2.50

    # True instrument coefficients
    gamma = np.array([0.5, 1.0, 1.5])
    reps = 800

    estimates_step1 = np.empty(reps)
    estimates_step2 = np.empty(reps)

    # Run Monte Carlo replications with conditional heteroskedasticity
    for r in range(reps):
        z_r = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n, q))
        u_r = rng.normal(0.0, 0.4, size=n)
        # Conditional heteroskedasticity: variance scales with z_1
        vol_r = 0.4 + 1.2 * np.abs(z_r[:, 0])
        eps_r = vol_r * rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n)
        x_r = z_r @ gamma + u_r
        y_r = theta_true * x_r + eps_r

        t1, t2, _, _ = estimate_two_step(z_r, x_r, y_r)
        estimates_step1[r] = t1
        estimates_step2[r] = t2

    # Point estimate and asymptotic variances on the primary sample (rep 0)
    z0 = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n, q))
    u0 = rng.normal(0.0, 0.4, size=n)
    vol0 = 0.4 + 1.2 * np.abs(z0[:, 0])
    eps0 = vol0 * rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n)
    x0 = z0 @ gamma + u0
    y0 = theta_true * x0 + eps0

    th1, th2, avar1, avar2 = estimate_two_step(z0, x0, y0)

    mc_var1 = float(np.var(estimates_step1, ddof=1))
    mc_var2 = float(np.var(estimates_step2, ddof=1))
    mc_se_diff = float(np.sqrt(2.0 / reps) * mc_var2)

    efficiency_gain_pct = (1.0 - avar2 / avar1) * 100.0
    empirical_gain_pct = (1.0 - mc_var2 / mc_var1) * 100.0

    print("Lab S5-L02-1: Efficient GMM Weighting vs Identity Weighting")
    print(f"  Sample size T: {n}, Moments q: {q}, Parameter k: 1 (SIMULATED, seed {seed})")
    print(f"  Step 1 (Identity W=I) estimate: {th1:.6f}, Asymptotic Var: {avar1:.6f}")
    print(f"  Step 2 (Efficient W=S^-1) estimate: {th2:.6f}, Asymptotic Var: {avar2:.6f}")
    print(f"  Analytical asymptotic variance reduction: {efficiency_gain_pct:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Monte Carlo empirical Var(theta_1): {mc_var1:.6f}")
    print(f"  Monte Carlo empirical Var(theta_2): {mc_var2:.6f} (MC SE: {mc_se_diff:.6f})")
    print(f"  Monte Carlo empirical variance reduction: {empirical_gain_pct:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Efficiency condition Var(theta_2) < Var(theta_1): {avar2 < avar1 and mc_var2 < mc_var1}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The optimality of \(W^* = S^{-1}\) is strictly an asymptotic property (\(T \to \infty\)). In finite samples with large numbers of moments \(q\) relative to sample size \(T\), the estimated covariance matrix \(\hat{S}_T\) contains considerable estimation error. Inverting \(\hat{S}_T\) downweights linear combinations of moments that appear to have low variance purely due to in-sample sampling noise. This phenomenon, known as the 'many-instruments bias' or 'small-sample GMM distortion', leads to severely biased point estimates, distorted overidentification test sizes, and standard errors that underestimate true finite-sample variability.

Misconceptions

  1. Iterating GMM until numerical convergence improves the asymptotic efficiency of the parameter estimates.

    Iterated GMM and two-step feasible GMM have identically the same asymptotic distribution: \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(\mathbf{0}, (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1})\). Because the first-step estimator is already \(\sqrt{T}\)-consistent, \(\hat{S}_T(\hat{\theta}^{(1)})\) is a consistent estimator of \(S\). Updating \(\hat{S}\) further does not change the asymptotic covariance; its benefits are purely finite-sample invariance to the initial weighting matrix.

  2. The optimal weighting matrix is always the inverse of the sample covariance of observable data w_t.

    The matrix \(S\) is the covariance matrix of the moment functions \(g(w_t, \theta_0)\), not the data \(w_t\). Furthermore, when moment conditions are serially correlated (as in multi-period forecasting or overlapping returns), \(S\) must include autocovariances: \(S = \Gamma_0 + \sum_{j=1}^\infty (\Gamma_j + \Gamma_j^\top)\). Inverting only the contemporaneous variance \(\Gamma_0\) produces an inefficient estimator and invalid test statistics.

  3. Using the efficient weighting matrix ensures that the GMM estimator is unbiased in finite samples.

    Efficiency in GMM is an asymptotic variance concept, not an unbiasedness property. In fact, two-step feasible GMM is generally biased in finite samples because the second-step weight matrix \(\hat{S}_T^{-1}\) is correlated with the sample moments \(\bar{g}_T(\theta)\). In severe cases with many overidentifying moments, the finite-sample bias of two-step GMM can be substantially worse than that of one-step identity-weighted GMM.

Practice Problems

In an overidentified model with \(q = 2\) and \(k = 1\), the Jacobian is \(D = [1.0, 1.0]^\top\). The moment covariance matrix is \(S = \begin{pmatrix} 4.0 & 0.0 \\ 0.0 & 1.0 \end{pmatrix}\). Compute the asymptotic variance of the GMM estimator under the identity weight \(W = I_2\) and under the optimal weight \(W^* = S^{-1}\).

Worked Solution

Under \(W = I_2\): \(D^\top W D = D^\top D = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2.0\). \(D^\top W S W D = D^\top S D = 4.0 \times 1^2 + 1.0 \times 1^2 = 5.0\). The sandwich variance is \(V(I) = (2.0)^{-1} (5.0) (2.0)^{-1} = 5.0 / 4.0 = 1.250\). Under optimal weight \(W^* = S^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.25 & 0.0 \\ 0.0 & 1.0 \end{pmatrix}\): \(D^\top S^{-1} D = 1.0 \times 0.25 + 1.0 \times 1.0 = 1.25\). The efficient variance is \(V^* = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = 1 / 1.25 = 0.800\). The efficient variance is 36% lower than the identity-weighted variance.

Given \(S = \begin{pmatrix} 2.0 & 1.0 \\ 1.0 & 2.0 \end{pmatrix}\), compute the analytical inverse matrix \(S^{-1}\) to be used as the optimal weighting matrix.

Worked Solution

The determinant of the \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(S\) is \(\det(S) = (2.0)(2.0) - (1.0)(1.0) = 4.0 - 1.0 = 3.0\). The inverse matrix formula swaps diagonal elements and negates off-diagonal elements divided by the determinant: \(S^{-1} = \frac{1}{3.0} \begin{pmatrix} 2.0 & -1.0 \\ -1.0 & 2.0 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.6667 & -0.3333 \\ -0.3333 & 0.6667 \end{pmatrix}\).

In a two-step GMM estimation on \(T = 500\) observations with \(k = 1\) parameter, the efficient asymptotic variance is estimated as \(\hat{V}^* = 1.80\). Compute the estimated standard error \(\se(\hat{\theta})\) for the second-step parameter estimate.

Worked Solution

The asymptotic distribution is \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(0, V^*)\). The finite-sample variance of \(\hat{\theta}\) is approximated by \(\widehat{\Var}(\hat{\theta}) = \hat{V}^* / T = 1.80 / 500 = 0.0036\). Taking the square root yields the standard error: \(\se(\hat{\theta}) = \sqrt{0.0036} = 0.0600\).

Prove that if \(W_1 = c W_2\) for any non-zero positive scalar \(c > 0\), the GMM estimator \(\hat{\theta}(W_1)\) and its sandwich asymptotic variance \(V(W_1)\) are identical to \(\hat{\theta}(W_2)\) and \(V(W_2)\).

Worked Solution

The objective function with \(W_1\) is \(Q_T(\theta; W_1) = \bar{g}_T(\theta)^\top (c W_2) \bar{g}_T(\theta) = c \cdot Q_T(\theta; W_2)\). Because \(c > 0\) is a constant scaling factor, the location of the minimizer is invariant: \(\arg\min_\theta c Q_T(\theta; W_2) = \arg\min_\theta Q_T(\theta; W_2)\). For the sandwich variance: \(V(c W_2) = (D^\top c W_2 D)^{-1} D^\top (c W_2) S (c W_2) D (D^\top c W_2 D)^{-1}\). Factoring out scalars: \((c)^{-1} (D^\top W_2 D)^{-1} \cdot c^2 [D^\top W_2 S W_2 D] \cdot (c)^{-1} (D^\top W_2 D)^{-1} = \frac{c^2}{c^2} V(W_2) = V(W_2)\). Thus, GMM is invariant to positive scalar re-scaling of the weighting matrix.

Show that when \(W = S^{-1}\), the first-order condition \(D^\top S^{-1} \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}\) implies that the asymptotic covariance between the normalized estimator \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0)\) and the normalized sample moments \(\sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta})\) is zero.

Worked Solution

Asymptotically, \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) = - (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} \sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\theta_0)\). Expanding the sample moments at the optimum: \(\sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) \approx \sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\theta_0) + D \sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) = [I - D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1}] \sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\theta_0)\). Let \(P = D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1}\). Notice that \(P\) is an idempotent projection matrix in the metric of \(S^{-1}\). Computing the covariance: \(\Cov(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0), \sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta})) = - (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} \Var(\sqrt{T}\bar{g}_T(\theta_0)) [I - P]^\top = - (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} S [I - S^{-1} D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top] = - (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} [D^\top - D^\top] = \mathbf{0}\). Under optimal weighting, parameter estimation noise is asymptotically uncorrelated with pricing residuals.

Demonstrate why the sandwich variance formula reduces to \((D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\) when substituting \(W = S^{-1}\).

Worked Solution

Substitute \(W = S^{-1}\) directly into \(V(W) = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}\). The central meat product is \(D^\top W S W D = D^\top S^{-1} S S^{-1} D = D^\top (S^{-1} S) S^{-1} D = D^\top I S^{-1} D = D^\top S^{-1} D\). The sandwich formula becomes \(V(S^{-1}) = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} [D^\top S^{-1} D] (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\). Because \((D^\top S^{-1} D)\) is a non-singular \(k \times k\) matrix, multiplying it by its inverse yields the identity: \([(D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} (D^\top S^{-1} D)] (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = I \cdot (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\).

An empirical asset pricing test uses \(N = 25\) portfolios to estimate \(K = 3\) factor risk premia on \(T = 180\) months. Calculate the number of non-redundant parameters in the unconstrained \(S\) matrix, and assess the degree of estimation risk in computing \(S^{-1}\).

Worked Solution

With \(N = 25\) moment conditions, the symmetric covariance matrix \(S\) has dimension \(25 \times 25\). The number of non-redundant parameters on and above the diagonal is \(N(N + 1) / 2 = 25 \times 26 / 2 = 325\). With \(T = 180\) time periods, the researcher attempts to estimate 325 covariance parameters from only 180 vector observations. The sample covariance matrix is mathematically singular (rank at most 180 < 325 if uncentered, or 179 centered) and ill-conditioned, meaning unconstrained inversion is impossible without factor structure shrinkage or diagonal regularization.

A researcher finds that the condition number (ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue) of \(\hat{S}_T\) is \(1.4 \times 10^7\). Explain what this implies for the numerical stability of two-step GMM estimates.

Worked Solution

A condition number of \(1.4 \times 10^7\) indicates that \(\hat{S}_T\) is nearly singular. Small numerical perturbations or rounding in the sample moments will be magnified by a factor of up to \(10^7\) when multiplying by \(\hat{S}_T^{-1}\). The second-step GMM criterion will assign gigantic weights to linear combinations of moments that align with the near-zero eigenvalues, producing wildly erratic parameter estimates that reflect numerical roundoff rather than true economic relationships.

In an empirical study, the first-step GMM estimate with \(W_1 = I\) yields \(\hat{\theta}_1 = 0.45\), while the second-step estimate with \(W_2 = \hat{S}^{-1}\) yields \(\hat{\theta}_2 = -1.20\). Explain why a massive divergence between step 1 and step 2 suggests potential model misspecification.

Worked Solution

Under correct model specification (\(\E[g(w_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}\)), any positive definite weighting matrix yields a consistent estimator of \(\theta_0\). As \(T \to \infty\), \(\hat{\theta}_1\) and \(\hat{\theta}_2\) must both converge in probability to \(\theta_0\). A radical divergence in sign and magnitude indicates that the moment conditions cannot be satisfied simultaneously (the model is misspecified). Step 1 minimizes unweighted Euclidean distance, while Step 2 rotates the metric toward a completely different compromise point, signaling severe structural conflict across moments.

Why did Hansen and Jagannathan (1997) advocate for using the second-moment matrix of asset returns \(W = \E[R_t R_t^\top]^{-1}\) rather than the statistically efficient GMM weighting matrix \(S^{-1}\) when evaluating asset pricing models?

Worked Solution

Hansen and Jagannathan (1997) noted that efficient GMM weighting \(W = S^{-1}\) rewards a candidate model for having high idiosyncratic pricing error variance: if a model produces highly volatile pricing errors for an asset, \(S\) has a large diagonal element, so \(S^{-1}\) assigns that asset a tiny weight, mechanically lowering the criterion. By contrast, fixing \(W = \E[R_t R_t^\top]^{-1}\) provides an invariant economic metric—the distance between the model's stochastic discount factor and the true mean-variance efficient proxy—allowing objective comparison across competing non-nested models.

Analyze the trade-off between the Newey–West (1987) Bartlett kernel and the quadratic spectral kernel of Andrews (1991) when estimating the long-run covariance matrix S.

Worked Solution

The Newey–West estimator uses a triangular Bartlett kernel with a fixed truncation lag \(m\). It guarantees positive semi-definiteness by construction and is straightforward to implement, but its truncation imposes an abrupt boundary in lag space. Andrews (1991) proved that the quadratic spectral (QS) kernel is asymptotically optimal in minimizing asymptotic mean squared error for spectral density estimation. However, the QS kernel does not truncate to zero at finite lags and requires data-driven automatic bandwidth selection, creating slightly greater finite-sample complexity in small macro-financial panels.

Examine how pre-whitening procedures proposed by Andrews and Monahan (1992) affect the performance of HAC covariance estimators in highly persistent financial time series.

Worked Solution

When moment conditions exhibit strong autoregressive persistence (e.g., near-unit-root valuation ratios), standard kernel estimators with small bandwidths severely underestimate the zero-frequency spectral density \(S\). Pre-whitening fits a low-order vector autoregression (VAR(1)) to the moment vector \(\hat{g}_t\), computes the kernel estimator on the filtered innovations \(\tilde{g}_t\) (which are closer to white noise), and re-colors the resulting spectral matrix using the estimated VAR transfer function. This procedure significantly reduces finite-sample downward bias in standard errors for persistent processes.

Retrieval Practice

  1. What matrix is the optimal GMM weighting matrix according to Hansen's (1982) theorem?

    Hansen (1982) proved that weighting moments by \(S^{-1}\) minimizes the asymptotic variance of \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0)\).

  2. What does the matrix inequality \(V(W) - V(S^{-1}) \ge \mathbf{0}\) mean in practice?

    The Loewner order \(A \ge B\) means \(A - B\) is positive semi-definite, ensuring \(c^\top A c \ge c^\top B c\) for every non-zero vector \(c\).

  3. What is the simplified form of the asymptotic covariance matrix \(V\) when the optimal weight \(W = S^{-1}\) is used?

    Substituting \(W = S^{-1}\) into the sandwich formula causes the central term to collapse with the outer inverses, leaving \((D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\).

  4. Why is a two-step estimation procedure required to implement feasible efficient GMM in practice?

    The true parameter \(\theta_0\) is unknown, so a first-step consistent estimate \(\hat{\theta}_1\) is necessary to evaluate the residuals \(g(w_t, \hat{\theta}_1)\) and estimate \(S\).

  5. What happens to the GMM parameter estimate and standard error if the weighting matrix is multiplied by 100?

    GMM is invariant to positive scalar re-scaling: multiplying \(W\) by \(c > 0\) scales the objective function proportionally without altering its minimizer or sandwich variance.

  6. How does iterated GMM differ from two-step GMM asymptotically?

    Because the first-step estimator is already \(\sqrt{T}\)-consistent, the asymptotic distribution is unchanged by further iterations, though finite-sample properties may differ.

  7. What major numerical risk arises when the number of moments \(q\) is large relative to sample size \(T\)?

    Estimating \(q(q+1)/2\) covariance elements from few observations makes \(\hat{S}\) nearly singular, corrupting the inverted weights.

  8. In an overidentified asset pricing model with \(N\) test assets, why might an identity weighting matrix \(W = I\) be preferred over \(S^{-1}\) for model comparison?

    Hansen and Jagannathan (1997) showed that \(S^{-1}\) weighting distorts model comparison by downweighting assets with noisy pricing residuals.
