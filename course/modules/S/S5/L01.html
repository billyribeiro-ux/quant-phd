Moment Conditions & Generalized Method of Moments

Semiparametric Identification, Quadratic Criterion Functions, and Asymptotic Distribution

Why It Matters

Classical maximum likelihood estimation requires asserting the exact joint probability density of all observable variables. In dynamic macro-finance and empirical asset pricing, economic theory delivers Euler equations and orthogonality restrictions, but rarely fully specified probability densities. The Generalized Method of Moments, pioneered by Hansen (1982), liberates empirical analysis from full distribution assumptions. By requiring only that specific expectation functions vanish at the true parameter, GMM provides a unified, semiparametric estimation framework that encompasses ordinary least squares, two-stage least squares, and nonlinear instrumental variables as special cases, enabling robust estimation without assuming conditional Gaussianity.

1. Population Moment Conditions and Semiparametric Identification

Econometric models often imply that a vector of observable variables \(w_t \in \R^d\) and an unknown parameter vector \(\theta_0 \in \Theta \subset \R^k\) satisfy theoretical orthogonality conditions. Rather than modeling the full conditional distribution of \(w_t\), the econometrician specifies \(q\) distinct expectation equations.

Definition 1.1 · Generalized Method of Moments (GMM)

Let \(\{w_t\}_{t=1}^T\) be a stationary and ergodic stochastic process taking values in \(\R^d\). Let \(\theta \in \Theta \subset \R^k\) denote the parameter vector, and let \(g(w_t, \theta): \R^d \times \Theta \to \R^q\) be a vector of continuous moment functions with \(q \ge k\). A moment condition states that at the true parameter value \(\theta_0 \in \Theta\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[g(w_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}_{q \times 1}. \end{aligned} \]

The sample average of these moment conditions over a sample of size \(T\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{g}_T(\theta) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T g(w_t, \theta). \end{aligned} \]

The Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) estimator \(\hat{\theta}_T\) is the argument minimizing the quadratic criterion:

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_T(\theta) = \bar{g}_T(\theta)^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\theta), \end{aligned} \]

where \(W_T\) is a \(q \times q\) symmetric positive semi-definite matrix converging in probability to a positive definite limit \(W\).

Definition 1.2 · Identification Structures

The system of moment conditions is classified according to the relation between the number of moment equations \(q\) and the number of parameters \(k\):

  • Just-identified (\(q = k\)): The classical method of moments. The number of moment equations equals the number of parameters. Provided the Jacobian has full rank, there exists an estimator \(\hat{\theta}_T\) satisfying \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = \mathbf{0}\) exactly, and the choice of positive definite weighting matrix \(W_T\) does not affect the point estimate.
  • Over-identified (\(q > k\)): The system has more moment equations than unknown parameters. In finite samples, no single parameter vector can set all \(q\) sample moments to zero simultaneously. The criterion \(Q_T(\theta)\) trades off deviations across moments according to the weighting matrix \(W_T\).
  • Under-identified (\(q < k\)): The system has fewer moments than parameters. Unique point identification is impossible without additional structural restrictions.
Example 1.1 · Linear Instrumental Variables as GMM

Consider the linear regression model with endogenous regressor \(x_t\) and structural error \(\varepsilon_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t = x_t \theta_0 + \varepsilon_t, \quad \E[x_t \varepsilon_t] \ne 0. \end{aligned} \]

Suppose an instrument vector \(z_t \in \R^q\) satisfies exogeneity \(\E[z_t \varepsilon_t] = \mathbf{0}\) and relevance \(\operatorname{Rank}(\E[z_t x_t]) = k\). The moment function is \(g(w_t, \theta) = z_t (y_t - x_t \theta)\). The sample moment is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{g}_T(\theta) &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T z_t y_t - \left(\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T z_t x_t\right) \theta \\ &= S_{zy} - S_{zx} \theta. \end{aligned} \]

In the just-identified case (\(q = k = 1\)), setting \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = 0\) yields the closed-form instrumental variables estimator \(\hat{\theta} = S_{zy} / S_{zx}\).

2. Asymptotic Properties of the GMM Estimator

The asymptotic distribution of the GMM estimator depends on regularity conditions ensuring uniform convergence of the sample objective function and asymptotic normality of the sample moments.

Theorem 1.1 · Consistency and Asymptotic Normality of GMM

Assumptions:

  1. A1 (Stationarity & Ergodicity): \(\{w_t\}_{t=1}^T\) is strictly stationary and ergodic.
  2. A2 (Identification): \(\E[g(w_t, \theta)] = \mathbf{0}\) if and only if \(\theta = \theta_0\), and the parameter space \(\Theta\) is compact with \(\theta_0 \in \operatorname{int}(\Theta)\).
  3. A3 (Regularity): \(g(w_t, \theta)\) is continuously differentiable in \(\theta\). The expected Jacobian \(D = \E[\nabla_\theta g(w_t, \theta_0)] \in \R^{q \times k}\) has full column rank \(k\).
  4. A4 (Moment CLT): The sample moments at \(\theta_0\) satisfy a central limit theorem: \[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\theta_0) \xrightarrow{\;d\;} \Normal(\mathbf{0}, S), \end{aligned} \] where \(S = \lim_{T \to \infty} \Var(\sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\theta_0))\) is positive definite.
  5. A5 (Weighting): \(W_T \xrightarrow{\;p\;} W\), where \(W\) is a symmetric positive definite matrix.

Conclusion: Under Assumptions A1–A5, the GMM estimator \(\hat{\theta}_T\) is consistent and asymptotically normal:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\theta}_T &\xrightarrow{\;p\;} \theta_0, \\ \sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) &\xrightarrow{\;d\;} \Normal\left(\mathbf{0}, V\right), \end{aligned} \]

where the asymptotic covariance matrix \(V\) has the sandwich form:

\[ \begin{aligned} V = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}. \end{aligned} \]
Proof Sketch

The first-order condition for minimizing \(Q_T(\theta) = \bar{g}_T(\theta)^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\theta)\) requires the gradient with respect to \(\theta\) to vanish:

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta Q_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = 2 D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = \mathbf{0}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(D_T(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \bar{g}_T(\theta) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta g(w_t, \theta)\). Taking a first-order Taylor expansion of \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T)\) around \(\theta_0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = \bar{g}_T(\theta_0) + D_T(\bar{\theta}) (\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0), \end{aligned} \]

where \(\bar{\theta}\) lies on the segment between \(\hat{\theta}_T\) and \(\theta_0\). Substituting into the first-order condition and rearranging:

\[ \begin{aligned} D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T \left[ \bar{g}_T(\theta_0) + D_T(\bar{\theta}) (\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) \right] = \mathbf{0}. \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying by \(\sqrt{T}\) and solving for \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0) = - \left[ D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T D_T(\bar{\theta}) \right]^{-1} D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T \sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\theta_0). \end{aligned} \]

By the law of large numbers, \(D_T(\hat{\theta}_T) \xrightarrow{p} D\) and \(D_T(\bar{\theta}) \xrightarrow{p} D\). By Slutsky's theorem, \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0)\) is a linear transformation of \(\sqrt{T} \bar{g}_T(\theta_0)\). The variance of this linear combination yields \(V = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}\).

Lab 1 · Just-Identified Sample Moment Optimization

For a just-identified linear instrumental variables model, the numerical GMM root obtained by minimizing the quadratic criterion equals the closed-form IV ratio to within machine precision, and the sample moment evaluates to zero.

"""Lab S5-L01-1: Just-identified sample moment conditions in GMM.

SIMULATED: generates synthetic instrumental variable series with known true parameter,
computes the closed-form method of moments estimator and numerical GMM root,
and verifies exact equality and zero sample moment condition.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = 500
    theta_true = 1.75

    # Instrument z, endogenous regressor x correlated with structural error eps
    z = rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=1.0, size=n)
    v = rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=0.5, size=n)
    eps = 0.6 * v + rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=0.8, size=n)
    x = 1.2 * z + v
    y = theta_true * x + eps

    # Closed-form IV / Method of Moments estimator
    # Sample moment: g_n(theta) = (1/n) * sum(z * (y - x * theta)) = 0
    # => theta_hat = sum(z * y) / sum(z * x)
    zy = float(np.sum(z * y))
    zx = float(np.sum(z * x))
    theta_closed = zy / zx

    # GMM numerical criterion: Q(theta) = (g_n(theta))^2
    # Analytical gradient: d/dtheta g_n(theta) = - (1/n) * sum(z * x)
    # Newton step from initial guess theta_0 = 0.0
    theta_guess = 0.0
    for _ in range(5):
        g_val = float(np.mean(z * (y - x * theta_guess)))
        dg_val = -float(np.mean(z * x))
        theta_guess = theta_guess - g_val / dg_val

    theta_gmm = theta_guess
    sample_moment = float(np.mean(z * (y - x * theta_gmm)))
    abs_diff = abs(theta_gmm - theta_closed)

    print("Lab S5-L01-1: Just-Identified GMM Estimation")
    print(f"  Sample size T: {n} (SIMULATED, seed {seed})")
    print(f"  True parameter theta_0: {theta_true:.4f}")
    print(f"  Closed-form IV estimator: {theta_closed:.6f}")
    print(f"  Numerical GMM root: {theta_gmm:.6f}")
    print(f"  Absolute difference: {abs_diff:.2e}")
    print(f"  Evaluated sample moment g_T(theta_hat): {sample_moment:.2e}")
    print(f"  Exact root property (|g_T| < 1e-12): {abs(sample_moment) < 1e-12}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The asymptotic normality theorem requires Assumption A3: the Jacobian matrix \(D = \E[\nabla_\theta g(w_t, \theta_0)]\) must have full column rank \(k\). In financial applications where candidate instruments are weakly correlated with endogenous regressors—or where asset return cross-sections contain uninformative factors—the Jacobian \(D\) is nearly rank-deficient. When \(D^\top W D \approx \mathbf{0}\), the inverse \((D^\top W D)^{-1}\) explodes. Standard GMM asymptotic standard errors severely underestimate true sampling uncertainty, t-statistics fail to converge to Gaussian distributions, and confidence intervals derived from the sandwich formula suffer catastrophic undercoverage.

Misconceptions

  1. In just-identified GMM models, different choices of the weighting matrix W alter the parameter point estimate.

    In a just-identified system with \(q = k\), the number of equations equals the number of unknowns. Provided the Jacobian is invertible, there exists a unique parameter value \(\hat{\theta}\) setting all sample moments \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}\) simultaneously. Because the vector \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta})\) is zero, the quadratic form \(\bar{g}_T^\top W \bar{g}_T = 0\) reaches its global minimum for any symmetric positive definite matrix \(W\). Weighting affects point estimates only in overidentified models (\(q > k\)).

  2. GMM is a non-parametric estimator because it does not assume Gaussian errors.

    GMM is a semiparametric estimator, not a non-parametric one. It estimates a finite-dimensional parameter vector \(\theta \in \R^k\) using a finite set of parametric moment conditions \(g(w_t, \theta)\). The semiparametric nature arises because no functional form is imposed on the distribution of \(w_t\) beyond the moment conditions themselves.

  3. Sample moments bar{g}_T(theta_hat) are identically zero at the GMM optimum in all models.

    In overidentified models (\(q > k\)), \(q\) equations in \(k\) unknowns cannot generally be solved simultaneously. The GMM estimator sets a linear combination of sample moments to zero: \(D_T^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}\), where the matrix \(D_T^\top W_T\) has dimension \(k \times q\). The individual sample moments \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta})\) are non-zero residuals representing pricing errors or orthogonality deviations.

Practice Problems

In a just-identified scalar GMM model \(y_t = x_t \theta + \varepsilon_t\) with instrument \(z_t\), sample cross-products are \(\frac{1}{T}\sum z_t y_t = 3.60\) and \(\frac{1}{T}\sum z_t x_t = 1.20\). Compute the GMM parameter estimate \(\hat{\theta}\) and verify the value of the criterion function \(Q_T(\hat{\theta})\) with weight \(W = 5.0\).

Worked Solution

The sample moment condition is \(\bar{g}_T(\theta) = \frac{1}{T}\sum z_t (y_t - x_t \theta) = 3.60 - 1.20 \theta\). Setting \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = 0\) yields \(\hat{\theta} = 3.60 / 1.20 = 3.00\). Evaluating the GMM criterion function at \(\hat{\theta}\): \(Q_T(3.00) = (3.60 - 1.20 \times 3.00)^2 \times 5.0 = 0.0^2 \times 5.0 = 0.0\). The minimized criterion is identically zero regardless of the weight \(W\).

Suppose an overidentified model has \(q = 3\) moment conditions and \(k = 1\) parameter with Jacobian \(D = [1.0, 2.0, 3.0]^\top\). If the weighting matrix is the identity \(W = I_3\), compute the scalar \(D^\top W D\) and the projection vector \((D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W\).

Worked Solution

With \(W = I_3\), \(D^\top W D = D^\top D = 1.0^2 + 2.0^2 + 3.0^2 = 1.0 + 4.0 + 9.0 = 14.0\). The scalar inverse is \((D^\top W D)^{-1} = 1 / 14.0\). The projection row vector is \(\frac{1}{14.0} [1.0, 2.0, 3.0] \approx [0.0714, 0.1429, 0.2143]\), giving the linear weights assigned to each sample moment in the first-order condition.

Given \(T = 200\), \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = [0.02, -0.01]^\top\), and weighting matrix \(W = \begin{pmatrix} 2.0 & 0.0 \\ 0.0 & 1.0 \end{pmatrix}\), compute the numerical value of the minimized objective function \(Q_T(\hat{\theta})\) and \(T Q_T(\hat{\theta})\).

Worked Solution

Evaluate the quadratic form: \(Q_T(\hat{\theta}) = [0.02, -0.01] \begin{pmatrix} 2.0 & 0.0 \\ 0.0 & 1.0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0.02 \\ -0.01 \end{pmatrix} = 2.0 \times (0.02)^2 + 1.0 \times (-0.01)^2 = 2.0 \times 0.0004 + 1.0 \times 0.0001 = 0.0008 + 0.0001 = 0.0009\). Multiplying by sample size \(T = 200\): \(T Q_T(\hat{\theta}) = 200 \times 0.0009 = 0.1800\).

Prove that when \(q = k\) and the expected Jacobian \(D\) is non-singular, the sandwich asymptotic variance formula \(V = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}\) reduces to \(V = D^{-1} S (D^\top)^{-1}\), which is completely independent of the weighting matrix \(W\).

Worked Solution

When \(q = k\), the Jacobian \(D\) is a square \(k \times k\) matrix. Because \(D\) is invertible by assumption, \((D^\top W D)^{-1} = D^{-1} W^{-1} (D^\top)^{-1}\). Substituting into the sandwich expression: \(V = \left[ D^{-1} W^{-1} (D^\top)^{-1} \right] D^\top W S W D \left[ D^{-1} W^{-1} (D^\top)^{-1} \right]\). Noting that \((D^\top)^{-1} D^\top = I\) and \(D D^{-1} = I\), this simplifies to \(V = D^{-1} W^{-1} (I) W S W (I) W^{-1} (D^\top)^{-1} = D^{-1} (W^{-1} W) S (W W^{-1}) (D^\top)^{-1} = D^{-1} S (D^\top)^{-1}\). The weighting matrix cancels completely.

Show that the linear regression ordinary least squares estimator \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y\) is the GMM estimator under the population moment condition \(\E[x_t (y_t - x_t^\top \beta)] = \mathbf{0}\) with identity weighting.

Worked Solution

The sample moment is \(\bar{g}_T(\beta) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T x_t (y_t - x_t^\top \beta) = \frac{1}{T} (X^\top y - X^\top X \beta)\). Because \(q = k\) (each regressor serves as its own instrument), setting \(\bar{g}_T(\hat{\beta}) = \mathbf{0}\) yields \(\frac{1}{T} X^\top y - \frac{1}{T} X^\top X \hat{\beta} = \mathbf{0}\), which implies \(X^\top X \hat{\beta} = X^\top y\). Inverting \(X^\top X\) yields \(\hat{\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y\). Thus, OLS is the just-identified GMM estimator associated with regressor exogeneity.

Derive the first-order condition for minimizing the GMM objective function \(Q_T(\theta) = \bar{g}_T(\theta)^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\theta)\) with respect to a vector parameter \(\theta \in \R^k\).

Worked Solution

Applying the matrix chain rule to the scalar quadratic form \(Q_T(\theta)\), let \(D_T(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \bar{g}_T(\theta) = \frac{\partial \bar{g}_T(\theta)}{\partial \theta^\top} \in \R^{q \times k}\). The total derivative with respect to \(\theta\) is \(\frac{\partial Q_T(\theta)}{\partial \theta} = 2 \left( \frac{\partial \bar{g}_T(\theta)}{\partial \theta^\top} \right)^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\theta) = 2 D_T(\theta)^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\theta)\). Setting this \(k \times 1\) vector to zero yields the foundational first-order condition \(D_T(\hat{\theta}_T)^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = \mathbf{0}\).

An analyst estimates a consumption Euler equation using quarterly data (\(T = 160\)) with \(q = 4\) instruments and \(k = 2\) parameters. If the minimized sample moment norm is \(\|\bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta})\| = 0.045\), explain why this norm is non-zero and identify what determines the trade-off across moments.

Worked Solution

Because the model is overidentified (\(q = 4 > 2 = k\)), there are 4 equations in only 2 unknown parameters. In any finite empirical sample, random sampling noise prevents all 4 sample averages from vanishing simultaneously. The trade-off is governed by the weighting matrix \(W_T\): the estimator minimizes the weighted distance \(\bar{g}_T^\top W_T \bar{g}_T\), placing larger penalty weights on moments that the researcher or theory deems more informative or more precisely measured.

In an instrumental variables regression of stock returns on dividend yield using two lagged macroeconomic variables as instruments (\(q = 2, k = 1\)), the sample Jacobian is \(D_T = [-0.15, -0.40]^\top\). If \(W_T = I_2\), determine which instrument exerts greater influence on the resulting parameter estimate.

Worked Solution

The first-order condition weights moments by \(D_T^\top W_T = [-0.15, -0.40]\). The magnitude of the weight on the second instrument is \(|-0.40| = 0.40\), which is \(0.40 / 0.15 \approx 2.67\) times larger than the weight on the first instrument (\(0.15\)). The second instrument has a stronger correlation with the regressor (larger Jacobian derivative), and therefore exerts dominant influence in determining \(\hat{\theta}\).

A researcher specifies 10 moment conditions to estimate 2 parameters on \(T = 50\) observations. Explain the numerical risks of using an arbitrary empirical weight matrix in this setting.

Worked Solution

With \(T = 50\) and \(q = 10\), the sample covariance matrix of moments has dimension \(10 \times 10\). Estimating a \(10 \times 10\) matrix requires estimating \(10 \times 11 / 2 = 55\) distinct covariance parameters from only 50 observations, exceeding the sample size. The estimated covariance matrix is nearly singular or ill-conditioned, causing its inverse to have wildly unstable eigenvalues, which injects severe finite-sample distortion into the GMM criterion and point estimates.

Discuss how the GMM approach to linear factor models differs fundamentally from maximum likelihood factor analysis under conditional Gaussianity.

Worked Solution

Maximum likelihood assumes that asset returns conditional on factors follow a joint multivariate normal distribution. In contrast, GMM requires only the pricing orthogonality condition \(\E[R_t - \beta f_t] = \mathbf{0}\) (or stochastic discount factor moment conditions \(\E[M_t R_t - 1] = 0\)). GMM does not require returns to be normal, homoskedastic, or serially independent. Heteroskedasticity and fat tails are accommodated semiparametrically by estimating the moment covariance matrix \(S\) via robust sandwich formulas without changing the point identification equations.

Evaluate the consequences for GMM estimation when the parameter \(\theta_0\) lies on the boundary of the parameter space \(\Theta\).

Worked Solution

Standard GMM asymptotic normality relies on the first-order condition \(\nabla_\theta Q_T(\hat{\theta}_T) = \mathbf{0}\), which requires \(\theta_0\) to lie in the interior of \(\Theta\). If \(\theta_0\) lies on the boundary (e.g., a variance or risk aversion parameter constrained to be non-negative), the estimator cannot take symmetric perturbations around \(\theta_0\). The asymptotic distribution of \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta}_T - \theta_0)\) becomes a truncated or folded normal distribution rather than a standard Gaussian distribution, invalidating standard symmetric Wald confidence intervals and t-tests.

Contrast the continuous updating GMM estimator (CUE) of Hansen, Heaton, and Yaron (1996) with the standard two-step GMM estimator regarding weighting matrix dependence.

Worked Solution

In standard two-step GMM, the weighting matrix \(W_T = S_T(\hat{\theta}_1)^{-1}\) is fixed after the first step and treated as a constant matrix during the second-step minimization over \(\theta\). In contrast, the continuous updating estimator simultaneously optimizes over \(\theta\) while recalculating the weighting matrix at every trial value: \(Q_{\text{CUE}}(\theta) = \bar{g}_T(\theta)^\top S_T(\theta)^{-1} \bar{g}_T(\theta)\). While CUE eliminates the dependence on an arbitrary initial weight matrix and possesses superior median-unbiasedness properties in finite samples, its criterion function is non-quadratic and numerically difficult to optimize.

Key Results

Concept / Formula Mathematical Form Econometric Significance
Moment Condition \(\E[g(w_t, \theta_0)] = \mathbf{0}_{q \times 1}\) Semiparametric orthogonality restriction defining parameter identification without distribution assumptions.
Sample Moments \(\bar{g}_T(\theta) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T g(w_t, \theta)\) Empirical sample analogue of theoretical expectation vanishing asymptotically at \(\theta_0\).
GMM Criterion \(Q_T(\theta) = \bar{g}_T(\theta)^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\theta)\) Quadratic loss function minimized to balance overidentified moment discrepancies.
First-Order Condition \(D_T(\hat{\theta})^\top W_T \bar{g}_T(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}_{k \times 1}\) Orthogonality system requiring sample moments to be orthogonal to the weighted Jacobian column space.
Sandwich Covariance \(V = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}\) Asymptotic variance of \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0)\) valid for any positive definite weighting matrix \(W\).

Retrieval Practice

  1. What condition distinguishes an overidentified GMM model from a just-identified GMM model?

    Overidentification occurs when \(q > k\), providing more estimating equations than unknown parameters and enabling specification testing.

  2. Under what condition on the moment Jacobian matrix \(D = \E[\nabla_\theta g(w_t, \theta_0)]\) is the parameter vector \(\theta_0\) locally identified?

    Local identification requires the \(q \times k\) Jacobian matrix \(D\) to have full column rank \(k\), ensuring that \(D^\top W D\) is strictly positive definite and invertible.

  3. In a just-identified GMM estimation (\(q = k\)), what is the value of the minimized objective function \(Q_T(\hat{\theta}_T)\)?

    With \(q = k\) equations in \(k\) unknowns, there exists a parameter vector setting all sample moments to zero, yielding \(Q_T(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}^\top W \mathbf{0} = 0\).

  4. What role does the weighting matrix \(W_T\) play in overidentified GMM estimation?

    In overidentified systems, \(W_T\) determines the relative cost of violating individual moment conditions in the quadratic distance metric.

  5. Why is the asymptotic covariance matrix of the GMM estimator called a 'sandwich' covariance matrix?

    The algebraic structure \(A \cdot B \cdot A^\top\) places the 'meat' matrix \(S\) between the 'bread' projection matrices \((D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W\).

  6. Which classical regression estimator is identical to a just-identified linear GMM estimator where each regressor is used as its own instrument?

    The OLS normal equations \(X^\top(y - X\beta) = \mathbf{0}\) are the exact sample moments \(\frac{1}{T}\sum x_t (y_t - x_t^\top \beta) = \mathbf{0}\).

  7. What occurs when the moment Jacobian matrix \(D\) is near rank-deficient (weak identification)?

    Weak identification causes the quadratic curvature around the minimum to flatten, making \((D^\top W D)\) nearly singular and invalidating Gaussian limit theory.

  8. How does GMM accommodate serial correlation and conditional heteroskedasticity in the underlying error terms?

    GMM isolates temporal dependence and heteroskedasticity inside the spectral density at frequency zero (matrix \(S\)), leaving the population moment condition \(\E[g_t] = \mathbf{0}\) unchanged.
