The t-Statistic and Likelihood-Ratio Tests

  • Uses: maximum likelihood estimation (L02), p-value (L04), and Student-t distribution (M4).
  • Introduces: t-statistic, and likelihood-ratio test.
  • TTheorem (deductive proof)
  • AEmpirical consensus
  • BEmpirical contested
  • CSingle study or lab result
  • DMarket lore
  • XRefuted claim

In quantitative research, true asset return volatility is never known a priori. Replacing unknown population dispersion with the sample standard deviation introduces sampling uncertainty that swells distribution tails. Student's t-statistic accounts for this finite-sample penalty under Gaussian returns. For composite hypotheses across multi-factor models, the generalized likelihood-ratio test provides the universal engine for model comparison, with Wilks' theorem establishing asymptotic chi-square calibration.

1Student's t-statistic

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be independent and identically distributed draws from a normal distribution \(\Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\), where both the mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) are unknown. To test the hypothesis \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\) against \(H_1: \mu \ne \mu_0\) (or one-sided variants), the natural point estimator for \(\mu\) is the sample mean \(\bar X = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\). When \(\sigma\) is known, the standardized z-statistic \(Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\) follows \(\Normal(0, 1)\) exactly under \(H_0\).

When \(\sigma\) is unknown, it must be estimated from the sample using the unbiased sample variance: \[ s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2 \]

Definition 1.1 (t-statistic)

The one-sample t-statistic for testing mean \(\mu = \mu_0\) is: \[ T = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}} \] where \(s = \sqrt{s^2}\) is the sample standard deviation and \(s/\sqrt{n}\) is the estimated standard error of the mean.

Theorem 1.2 (exact null distribution of the t-statistic)

Assumptions:
(A1) The observations \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are \(\iid\) \(\Normal(\mu_0, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma^2 \in (0, \infty)\).
(A2) The sample size satisfies \(n \ge 2\).
Statement:
Under \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\), the test statistic \(T\) follows Student's t-distribution with \(\nu = n - 1\) degrees of freedom: \[ T = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1} \]

Proof sketch

By Cochran's theorem and properties of Gaussian random vectors:

  • \(\bar X \sim \Normal(\mu_0, \sigma^2/n)\), so \(Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim \Normal(0, 1)\).
  • \(\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\), which is a chi-square distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.
  • \(\bar X\) and \(s^2\) are statistically independent (by Basu's theorem, since \(\bar X\) is complete sufficient for \(\mu\) and \(s^2\) is ancillary for \(\mu\)).
Recalling the definition of Student's t-distribution as the ratio of a standard normal to the square root of an independent chi-square divided by its degrees of freedom: \[ T = \frac{\frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}}{\sqrt{\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2} \Big/ (n-1)}} = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V / (n-1)}} \sim t_{n-1} \] where \(Z \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) and \(V \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\) are independent. \(\blacksquare\)

Because the denominator \(s\) is random, the t-distribution has heavier tails than the standard normal distribution. As \(n \to \infty\), \(s \pto \sigma\) by consistency, and \(t_{n-1}\) converges in distribution to \(\Normal(0, 1)\). However, in finite samples of size \(n = 10\) or \(25\), the critical values are noticeably wider than standard normal thresholds (for example, \(t_{9, 0.975} \approx 2.262\) versus \(\Phi^{-1}(0.975) \approx 1.960\)).

2The likelihood-ratio test

While the t-statistic applies to linear hypotheses on Gaussian means, quantitative finance frequently demands tests on nonlinear parameters, constrained covariance structures, or non-Gaussian likelihoods. The generalized likelihood-ratio test provides a universal principle for hypothesis testing.

Let \(X = (X_1, \dots, X_n)\) have joint probability density or mass function \(f(x; \theta)\) for \(\theta \in \Theta \subset \R^d\). Consider testing: \[ H_0: \theta \in \Theta_0 \quad \text{versus} \quad H_1: \theta \in \Theta \setminus \Theta_0 \]

Definition 2.1 (likelihood-ratio test)

The generalized likelihood-ratio statistic is: \[ \Lambda(X) = \frac{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} L(\theta; X)}{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta} L(\theta; X)} = \frac{L(\hat\theta_0; X)}{L(\hat\theta; X)} \] where \(\hat\theta_0\) is the constrained maximum likelihood estimator over the null space \(\Theta_0\), and \(\hat\theta\) is the unconstrained maximum likelihood estimator over the entire parameter space \(\Theta\). A likelihood-ratio test rejects \(H_0\) when \(\Lambda(X) \le c\), or equivalently when the deviance statistic: \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{LR}}(X) = -2 \ln \Lambda(X) = 2 \left( \ell(\hat\theta; X) - \ell(\hat\theta_0; X) \right) \ge c^* \]

Notice that \(\Lambda(X) \in [0, 1]\) because \(\Theta_0 \subset \Theta\). When the null hypothesis is true, \(\hat\theta\) is close to \(\hat\theta_0\), so \(\Lambda(X) \approx 1\) and \(-2 \ln \Lambda(X) \approx 0\). Large values of \(-2 \ln \Lambda(X)\) indicate that the unconstrained model provides a substantially superior fit to the observed data.

Theorem 2.2 (Wilks' theorem)

Assumptions:
(A1) The sample \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are \(\iid\) draws satisfying standard Fisher regularity conditions (common support, twice continuously differentiable log-densities, positive definite Fisher information matrix).
(A2) The true parameter \(\theta_0 \in \Theta_0\) lies in the interior of \(\Theta_0\) and \(\Theta\).
(A3) The null hypothesis imposes \(r\) smooth independent constraints, such that \(\dim(\Theta) - \dim(\Theta_0) = r\).
Statement:
Under \(H_0\), as the sample size \(n \to \infty\), the deviance statistic converges in distribution to a chi-square distribution with \(r\) degrees of freedom: \[ -2 \ln \Lambda(X) \dto \chi^2_r \]

Proof sketch

Expanding the log-likelihood function \(\ell(\theta)\) in a second-order Taylor series around the unconstrained MLE \(\hat\theta\): \[ \ell(\theta) \approx \ell(\hat\theta) + \nabla \ell(\hat\theta)^\top (\theta - \hat\theta) - \frac{1}{2} (\theta - \hat\theta)^\top J_n(\hat\theta) (\theta - \hat\theta) \] Since \(\hat\theta\) is the unconstrained maximizer, \(\nabla \ell(\hat\theta) = 0\). Evaluating at \(\hat\theta_0\): \[ \ell(\hat\theta) - \ell(\hat\theta_0) \approx \frac{1}{2} (\hat\theta - \hat\theta_0)^\top J_n(\hat\theta) (\hat\theta - \hat\theta_0) \] Multiplying by 2 yields \(-2 \ln \Lambda \approx (\hat\theta - \hat\theta_0)^\top J_n(\hat\theta) (\hat\theta - \hat\theta_0)\). By asymptotic normality of the MLE, \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta - \theta_0) \dto \Normal(0, I(\theta_0)^{-1})\). Projecting onto the \(r\)-dimensional orthogonal subspace defined by the constraints reveals that the quadratic form is asymptotically the sum of \(r\) squared independent standard normal random variables, which defines a \(\chi^2_r\) distribution. \(\blacksquare\)

3LRT for a Bernoulli hit rate

Example 3.1 (evaluating the likelihood-ratio test on trade wins)

Suppose an algorithmic trading model executes \(n = 50\) independent trades, resulting in \(k = 32\) profitable trades (\(\hat p = 32/50 = 0.64\)). We test \(H_0: p = 0.50\) versus \(H_1: p \ne 0.50\) at significance level \(\alpha = 0.05\). The unconstrained MLE is \(\hat p = 0.64\), while the constrained estimator under \(H_0\) is fixed at \(p_0 = 0.50\). The log-likelihood of a binomial sample is: \[ \ell(p) = k \ln p + (n - k) \ln(1 - p) \] The deviance statistic is: \[ -2 \ln \Lambda = 2 \left[ k \ln\left(\frac{\hat p}{p_0}\right) + (n - k) \ln\left(\frac{1 - \hat p}{1 - p_0}\right) \right] \] Substituting \(n = 50, k = 32, \hat p = 0.64, p_0 = 0.50\): \[ k \ln(0.64 / 0.50) = 32 \ln(1.28) \approx 32 \times 0.246860 = 7.899522 \] \[ (n - k) \ln(0.36 / 0.50) = 18 \ln(0.72) \approx 18 \times (-0.328504) = -5.913073 \] \[ \ell(\hat p) - \ell(p_0) = 7.899522 - 5.913073 = 1.986449 \] \[ \lambda_{\mathrm{LR}} = 2 \times 1.986449 = 3.972899 \] Since this tests 1 parameter restriction (\(p = 0.50\)), \(r = 1\). The \(95\%\) critical threshold for a \(\chi^2_1\) distribution is \(3.841459\). Because \(3.972899 \ge 3.841459\), the test rejects \(H_0\) at level \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Lab 1 · The t-statistic and the likelihood-ratio test (SIMULATED)

Hypothesis: For a simulated normal sample of size \(n = 25\) drawn with seed 601, the sample mean \(-0.077032\) and standard deviation \(0.882719\) produce \(t = -0.436336\). For \(n = 50, k = 32\), the likelihood-ratio statistic is \(3.972899\), which rejects \(H_0\) against the \(\chi^2_1\) critical cutoff \(3.841459\). Across \(10,000\) null replications with seed 602, the empirical rejection rate is \(0.0687\), reflecting finite-sample discreteness.

"""Lab S1-L06-1: The t-statistic and the likelihood-ratio test.

Computes the Student t-statistic on a seeded normal sample,
evaluates the likelihood-ratio statistic -2 ln Lambda on a sample,
and checks the asymptotic chi-square null rejection rate via simulation.
SIMULATED: normal sample uses random.Random(601); simulation uses random.Random(602).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    # Part 1: One-sample t-statistic on seeded normal sample (SIMULATED)
    n = 25
    mu0 = 0.0
    seed1 = 601
    rng1 = random.Random(seed1)

    # Draws from Normal(0, 1)
    sample = [rng1.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n)]
    sample_mean = math.fsum(sample) / n
    sample_var = math.fsum((x - sample_mean) ** 2 for x in sample) / (n - 1)
    sample_sd = math.sqrt(sample_var)
    se_mean = sample_sd / math.sqrt(n)

    t_stat = (sample_mean - mu0) / se_mean

    print(f"t-statistic sample size n: {n}, null mean mu0: {mu0:.1f}")
    print(f"sample mean: {sample_mean:.6f}, sample standard deviation: {sample_sd:.6f}")
    print(f"standard error of the mean: {se_mean:.6f}")
    print(f"computed t-statistic: {t_stat:.6f}")

    # Part 2: Bernoulli likelihood-ratio test statistic: -2 ln Lambda
    # H0: p = p0 vs H1: p != p0
    n_lr = 50
    k_obs = 32  # 32 successes in 50 trials
    p0 = 0.50
    p_hat = k_obs / n_lr

    # -2 ln Lambda = 2 * [ ell(p_hat) - ell(p0) ]
    # = 2 * [ k * ln(p_hat / p0) + (n - k) * ln((1 - p_hat) / (1 - p0)) ]
    term1 = k_obs * math.log(p_hat / p0)
    term2 = (n_lr - k_obs) * math.log((1.0 - p_hat) / (1.0 - p0))
    lr_stat = 2.0 * (term1 + term2)
    crit_chi2 = 3.8414588  # 95th percentile of chi-square with 1 degree of freedom

    print(f"likelihood-ratio test: n = {n_lr}, observed k = {k_obs}, p_hat = {p_hat:.4f}")
    print(f"log-likelihood difference: {term1 + term2:.6f}")
    print(f"likelihood-ratio statistic -2 ln Lambda: {lr_stat:.6f}")
    print(f"chi-square(1) 95% critical value: {crit_chi2:.6f}")
    print(f"reject H0 at alpha = 0.05: {lr_stat >= crit_chi2}")

    # Part 3: Seeded simulation of LR statistic under H0 (SIMULATED)
    n_sims = 10_000
    seed2 = 602
    rng2 = random.Random(seed2)

    rejections = 0
    for _ in range(n_sims):
        k = sum(1 for _ in range(n_lr) if rng2.random() < p0)
        if k == 0 or k == n_lr:
            continue
        ph = k / n_lr
        stat = 2.0 * (k * math.log(ph / p0) + (n_lr - k) * math.log((1.0 - ph) / (1.0 - p0)))
        if stat >= crit_chi2:
            rejections += 1

    sim_size = rejections / n_sims
    print(f"simulated null rejection rate (N = {n_sims}, seed = {seed2}): {sim_size:.4f}")

4Where the canon breaks

Both Student's t-test and Wilks' likelihood-ratio test depend on key distributional assumptions that fail in institutional quantitative trading:

  • Heavy-tailed non-Gaussian returns: Financial asset returns exhibit severe excess kurtosis and heavy power-law tails. Under non-normal distributions, \(\bar X\) and \(s^2\) are not independent, and the exact distribution of the t-statistic is not Student-t. While the central limit theorem guarantees asymptotic normality of \(\bar X\) under finite variance, the rate of convergence is substantially slowed by skewness and kurtosis.
  • Boundary parameters and non-standard limits: Wilks' theorem assumes that the true parameter lies in the interior of the parameter space. In financial models such as GARCH or variance component models where variances are constrained to be non-negative (\(\sigma^2 \ge 0\)), testing the null hypothesis on the boundary (\(H_0: \sigma^2 = 0\)) invalidates the \(\chi^2_r\) distribution. Instead, the asymptotic distribution is a mixture of chi-square distributions (Chernoff, 1954).
  • Serial correlation: Asset returns and trading profits often exhibit serial dependence. When observations are autocorrelated, the effective sample size is reduced, causing the standard t-statistic to severely overestimate statistical significance.

Misconceptions

  • Student's t-test is exact for any distribution if the sample size is small.

    Correction: Student's t-test is exact if and only if the underlying parent population is strictly normal. For non-normal data (such as skewed or heavy-tailed returns), the t-statistic is merely an approximation whose finite-sample behavior can deviate significantly from the Student-t density.

  • The likelihood-ratio statistic -2 ln Lambda follows a chi-square distribution in all sample sizes.

    Correction: Wilks' theorem is an asymptotic theorem. In finite samples, \(-2 \ln \Lambda\) only approximately follows \(\chi^2_r\). When sample size \(n\) is small or the number of estimated parameters is large, Bartlett corrections or bootstrap approximations are required.

  • A t-statistic of 2.0 always implies rejection at the 5% level.

    Correction: For a two-sided test at \(\alpha = 0.05\), a t-statistic of \(2.0\) only rejects when sample size is sufficiently large. For small samples (e.g. \(n = 4\), degrees of freedom \(\nu = 3\)), the two-sided \(5\%\) critical threshold is \(t_{3, 0.975} = 3.182\), so \(T = 2.0\) fails to reject.

Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · One-sample t-statistic computation

A strategy generates \(n = 16\) monthly returns with sample mean \(\bar X = 0.015\) and sample standard deviation \(s = 0.040\). Compute the t-statistic for testing \(H_0: \mu = 0\) versus \(H_1: \mu > 0\).

Solution

The standard error of the sample mean is \(s / \sqrt{n} = 0.040 / \sqrt{16} = 0.040 / 4 = 0.010\). The t-statistic is \(T = \frac{\bar X - 0}{s / \sqrt{n}} = \frac{0.015}{0.010} = 1.50\).

M2 · Critical threshold lookup

For the test in Problem M1 with \(n = 16\), state the degrees of freedom and determine whether \(T = 1.50\) rejects \(H_0\) at the one-sided significance level \(\alpha = 0.05\) (given \(t_{15, 0.95} \approx 1.753\)).

Solution

The degrees of freedom is \(\nu = n - 1 = 16 - 1 = 15\). The one-sided critical cutoff is \(t_{15, 0.95} \approx 1.753\). Because \(T = 1.50 < 1.753\), the test fails to reject \(H_0\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

M3 · Likelihood-ratio statistic formula

Suppose an unconstrained log-likelihood achieves maximum \(\ell(\hat\theta) = -120.4\), while a restricted model under null hypothesis \(H_0\) with 2 constraints achieves \(\ell(\hat\theta_0) = -124.6\). Compute the likelihood-ratio test statistic and state the degrees of freedom.

Solution

The deviance statistic is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{LR}} = 2(\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell(\hat\theta_0)) = 2(-120.4 - (-124.6)) = 2(4.2) = 8.4\). The degrees of freedom equals the number of restrictions: \(r = 2\).

Proof

P1 · Independence of sample mean and variance

Explain why Basu's theorem establishes the independence of \(\bar X\) and \(s^2\) for an \(\iid\) normal sample \(\Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with known \(\sigma^2\).

Solution

When \(\sigma^2\) is known, the sample mean \(\bar X\) is a complete sufficient statistic for the parameter \(\mu\). The sample variance \(s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n ((X_i - \mu) - (\bar X - \mu))^2\) is translation-invariant: shifting all observations by \(c\) leaves \(s^2\) unchanged. Therefore, the distribution of \(s^2\) does not depend on \(\mu\), making \(s^2\) an ancillary statistic for \(\mu\). By Basu's theorem, any ancillary statistic is independent of every complete sufficient statistic. Thus \(\bar X\) and \(s^2\) are independent.

P2 · Equivalence of two-sided t-test and LRT

Show that for testing \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\) versus \(H_1: \mu \ne \mu_0\) in an \(\iid\) normal model \(\Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with unknown variance, the generalized likelihood-ratio test rejects when \(|T| \ge c\).

Solution

The unconstrained MLEs are \(\hat\mu = \bar X\) and \(\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar X)^2\). The maximized log-likelihood is \(\ell(\hat\mu, \hat\sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\ln\hat\sigma^2 - \frac{n}{2}\). Under \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\), the constrained MLE is \(\hat\sigma_0^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \mu_0)^2 = \hat\sigma^2 + (\bar X - \mu_0)^2\). The likelihood ratio is: \[ \Lambda^{2/n} = \frac{\hat\sigma^2}{\hat\sigma_0^2} = \frac{\hat\sigma^2}{\hat\sigma^2 + (\bar X - \mu_0)^2} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{(\bar X - \mu_0)^2}{\hat\sigma^2}} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{T^2}{n-1}} \] Rejecting for small \(\Lambda\) is strictly equivalent to rejecting for large \(T^2\), which is \(|T| \ge c\).

P3 · Degrees of freedom in Wilks' theorem

Prove that if \(\Theta \subset \R^d\) and \(\Theta_0 = \{\theta \in \Theta : h_j(\theta) = 0, j=1,\dots,r\}\) with linearly independent gradient vectors \(\nabla h_j(\theta_0)\), then \(\dim(\Theta) - \dim(\Theta_0) = r\).

Solution

By the implicit function theorem, the set \(\Theta_0\) defined by \(r\) smooth equations with linearly independent gradients forms a smooth submanifold of \(\R^d\) of dimension \(d - r\). Since \(\dim(\Theta) = d\), the difference in dimensions is \(\dim(\Theta) - \dim(\Theta_0) = d - (d - r) = r\).

Empirical

E1 · Small-sample critical threshold inflation

Compare the two-sided \(5\%\) critical threshold of the t-distribution at \(n = 5\) (\(\nu = 4\)) with the standard normal threshold \(\Phi^{-1}(0.975) \approx 1.960\), given \(t_{4, 0.975} = 2.776\).

Solution

At \(\nu = 4\), the critical value is \(2.776\), which is \(\frac{2.776 - 1.960}{1.960} \approx 41.6\%\) wider than the Gaussian threshold. Using a normal cutoff on a sample of 5 trades severely inflates Type I error.

E2 · LRT rejection decision (Lab 1)

In Lab 1, the likelihood-ratio test for \(n = 50, k = 32\) achieved statistic \(3.972899\). At significance level \(\alpha = 0.05\), what was the test decision, and why?

Solution

The \(95\%\) quantile of \(\chi^2_1\) is \(3.841459\). Because \(3.972899 \ge 3.841459\), the null hypothesis \(H_0: p = 0.50\) is rejected at level \(0.05\).

E3 · Simulated null rejection rate (Lab 1)

In Lab 1, why was the simulated rejection rate across 10,000 replications equal to \(0.0687\) rather than exactly \(0.0500\)?

Solution

The binomial distribution is discrete. For \(n = 50\), the test statistic takes a discrete set of values. Continuous asymptotic \(\chi^2\) cutoffs do not match discrete jump points exactly, causing finite-sample size distortion.

Research

R1 · Bartlett correction for likelihood-ratio test

Explain the purpose of Bartlett's correction in finite-sample likelihood-ratio testing.

Solution

Wilks' theorem establishes that \(\E[\lambda_{\mathrm{LR}}] = r + \mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\). Bartlett demonstrated that by computing an adjustment factor \(C = 1 + a/n\), the scaled statistic \(\lambda_{\mathrm{LR}} / C\) has mean matching \(r\) to order \(\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})\), greatly improving null calibration in small samples.

R2 · Volatility clustering and t-statistic inflation

If asset returns exhibit ARCH effects (volatility clustering) without autocorrelation in returns, explain why the standard t-statistic's variance is inflated above 1.

Solution

Volatility clustering creates positive correlation between squared returns (\(\Cov(X_t^2, X_{t+k}^2) > 0\)). While \(\E[\bar X] = \mu\), the variance of the sample variance \(s^2\) is magnified, and \(\bar X\) and \(s^2\) become dependent in finite samples, fattening the tails of the t-ratio beyond Student-t.

R3 · Link between t-statistic and Sharpe ratio

Show that the sample t-statistic for testing \(\mu = 0\) is related to the sample Sharpe ratio \(\hSR\) by \(T = \sqrt{n} \hSR\).

Solution

By Definition 1.1, \(T = \frac{\bar X}{s/\sqrt{n}} = \sqrt{n}\left(\frac{\bar X}{s}\right)\). Since \(\hSR = \bar X / s\), we have \(T = \sqrt{n}\hSR\). This identity connects mean testing directly to Sharpe ratio evaluation in Lesson 10 and the capstone project.
