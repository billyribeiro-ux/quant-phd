Confidence Intervals and Coverage

  • Uses: estimator (L01), hypothesis test (L04), and type I error (L05).
  • Introduces: confidence interval, and coverage probability.

A single point estimate of portfolio alpha or Sharpe ratio provides zero information about estimation uncertainty. A confidence interval quantifies this dispersion by identifying a range of plausible values supported by the data at a specified confidence level. Crucially, the interval endpoints are random variables, while the parameter is an unknown fixed constant. Understanding coverage probability and test inversion protects quantitative researchers against deceptive asymptotic approximations like the naive Wald interval.

1Confidence intervals and coverage probability

Let sample \(X = (X_1, \dots, X_n) \in \mathcal{X}\) have distribution indexed by fixed unknown parameter \(\theta \in \Theta \subset \R\). Rather than reporting a single point estimate \(\hat\theta(X)\), interval estimation seeks a data-dependent subset of the parameter space that captures \(\theta\) with high probability under repeated sampling.

Definition 1.1 (confidence interval and coverage probability)

Let \(L(X)\) and \(U(X)\) be two statistic functions satisfying \(L(x) \le U(x)\) for all \(x \in \mathcal{X}\). The random interval \(C(X) = [L(X), U(X)]\) is a confidence interval for \(\theta\). The coverage probability of the interval at parameter \(\theta\) is: \[ C(\theta) = \Prob_\theta(L(X) \le \theta \le U(X)) \] The confidence coefficient (or exact coverage level) of the interval is the infimum of the coverage probability over the entire parameter space: \[ \gamma = \inf_{\theta \in \Theta} C(\theta) \] If \(\gamma \ge 1 - \alpha\), \(C(X)\) is called a \(1 - \alpha\) (or \(100(1-\alpha)\%\)) confidence interval for \(\theta\).

The frequentist interpretation of a confidence interval is fundamentally operational: the true parameter \(\theta\) is a fixed non-random constant. It does not possess a probability distribution. What is random is the interval \([L(X), U(X)]\). If an investment fund collects 1,000 independent samples of historical returns and constructs a \(95\%\) confidence interval for each, approximately \(950\) of those realized intervals will contain the true parameter \(\theta\), while approximately \(50\) will fail to cover it.

2Test inversion and the test-CI duality

There exists an exact, bidirectional equivalence between hypothesis testing and confidence set construction. Every family of hypothesis tests can be inverted to yield a confidence set, and every confidence set implies a family of hypothesis tests.

Theorem 2.1 (test inversion theorem)

Assumptions:
(A1) For each \(\theta_0 \in \Theta\), let \(A(\theta_0) \subset \mathcal{X}\) be the acceptance region of a hypothesis test of \(H_0: \theta = \theta_0\) versus \(H_1: \theta \ne \theta_0\) with significance level \(\alpha\), such that \(\Prob_{\theta_0}(X \in A(\theta_0)) \ge 1 - \alpha\).
(A2) For each observed sample \(x \in \mathcal{X}\), define the set \(C(x) = \{\theta \in \Theta : x \in A(\theta)\}\).
Statement:
The random set \(C(X)\) is a \(1 - \alpha\) confidence region for \(\theta\), satisfying for all \(\theta \in \Theta\): \[ \Prob_\theta(\theta \in C(X)) \ge 1 - \alpha \]

Proof

For any \(\theta \in \Theta\), by the definition of \(C(x)\): \[ \theta \in C(X) \iff X \in A(\theta) \] Taking probabilities under \(\Prob_\theta\): \[ \Prob_\theta(\theta \in C(X)) = \Prob_\theta(X \in A(\theta)) \] By Assumption A1, the test has significance level \(\alpha\), meaning its Type I error is at most \(\alpha\), so the acceptance probability is \(\Prob_\theta(X \in A(\theta)) \ge 1 - \alpha\). Therefore, \(\Prob_\theta(\theta \in C(X)) \ge 1 - \alpha\) for all \(\theta \in \Theta\). \(\blacksquare\)

Conversely, given a \(1 - \alpha\) confidence region \(C(X)\), a test of \(H_0: \theta = \theta_0\) at level \(\alpha\) rejects \(H_0\) if and only if \(\theta_0 \notin C(X)\). The acceptance region is \(A(\theta_0) = \{x : \theta_0 \in C(x)\}\), which automatically satisfies \(\Prob_{\theta_0}(X \in A(\theta_0)) = \Prob_{\theta_0}(\theta_0 \in C(X)) \ge 1 - \alpha\).

3Wald interval versus Wilson score interval

To understand why asymptotic approximations can fail catastrophically in practice, consider estimating a binomial success probability \(p\) (such as a strategy hit rate) from \(K \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(n, p)\).

The standard point estimator is \(\hat p = K / n\). By the central limit theorem, \(\frac{\hat p - p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}} \dto \Normal(0, 1)\).

Definition 3.1 (Wald confidence interval)

The Wald interval substitutes the sample point estimate \(\hat p\) into the standard error: \[ \operatorname{CI}_{\mathrm{Wald}} = \hat p \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat p (1 - \hat p)}{n}} \] where \(z_{1-\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)\).

Although ubiquitous in elementary textbooks and spreadsheet software, the Wald interval possesses severe mathematical pathology:

  • If \(K = 0\) or \(K = n\), \(\hat p(1 - \hat p) = 0\), so the Wald interval collapses to a single point \([0, 0]\) or \([1, 1]\) with zero width and zero probability of covering any interior \(p \in (0, 1)\).
  • For parameters near boundaries (\(p < 0.10\) or \(p > 0.90\)), the true coverage probability plummets well below nominal \(95\%\), frequently dropping below \(70\%\) even for moderately large \(n\).

Definition 3.2 (Wilson score confidence interval)

The Wilson score interval directly inverts the score test based on the null standard error \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). The acceptance region solves: \[ \left| \frac{\hat p - p}{\sqrt{p(1 - p) / n}} \right| \le z \] Squaring both sides yields a quadratic equation in \(p\): \[ \begin{aligned} (\hat p - p)^2 &\le z^2 \frac{p(1 - p)}{n} \\ &\iff \left(1 + \frac{z^2}{n}\right) p^2 - \left(2\hat p + \frac{z^2}{n}\right) p + \hat p^2 \le 0 \end{aligned} \] Solving for the roots gives the Wilson interval: \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{CI}_{\mathrm{Wilson}} &= \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \\ &\quad \pm \frac{z}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}} \end{aligned} \]

The Wilson score interval shrinks the center toward \(1/2\) and adds a positive variance term \(\frac{z^2}{4n^2}\), ensuring that even when \(K = 0\), the interval does not collapse to zero width and maintains excellent coverage across the entire parameter space.

Lab 1 · Exact coverage probability of Wald and Wilson confidence intervals

Hypothesis: For \(n = 20\) trials and nominal \(95\%\) confidence level (\(z = 1.959964\)), the exact coverage probability of the Wald interval oscillates widely and drops to \(0.6389\) at \(p = 0.05\) and \(p = 0.95\). In contrast, the Wilson score interval maintains coverage \(\ge 0.9245\) everywhere across the grid, demonstrating superior stability near boundaries.

"""Lab S1-L07-1: Exact coverage probability of Wald and Wilson confidence intervals.

Evaluates exact coverage probability across a grid of parameter values p for n = 20.
Nominal 95% confidence level.
Standard library only. No external imports.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import statistics


def main() -> None:
    n = 20
    conf_level = 0.95
    # Standard normal quantile for 95% two-sided interval
    z = statistics.NormalDist().inv_cdf(0.5 + conf_level / 2.0)

    p_grid = [0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.50, 0.80, 0.90, 0.95]

    print(f"sample size n: {n}, nominal coverage: {conf_level:.2%}")
    print(f"critical value z: {z:.6f}")
    print("exact coverage probabilities C(p) = sum P(k | p) * I(p in CI(k)):")

    for p in p_grid:
        cov_wald = 0.0
        cov_wilson = 0.0

        for k in range(n + 1):
            prob = math.comb(n, k) * (p**k) * ((1.0 - p) ** (n - k))
            p_hat = k / n

            # 1. Wald interval: p_hat +/- z * sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)
            se_wald = math.sqrt(p_hat * (1.0 - p_hat) / n)
            wald_lo = p_hat - z * se_wald
            wald_hi = p_hat + z * se_wald
            if wald_lo <= p <= wald_hi:
                cov_wald += prob

            # 2. Wilson score interval
            denom = 1.0 + (z**2) / n
            center = (p_hat + (z**2) / (2.0 * n)) / denom
            radius = (z / denom) * math.sqrt(p_hat * (1.0 - p_hat) / n + (z**2) / (4.0 * n * n))
            wilson_lo = center - radius
            wilson_hi = center + radius
            if wilson_lo <= p <= wilson_hi:
                cov_wilson += prob

        print(f"p = {p:.2f}: Wald coverage = {cov_wald:.4f}, Wilson coverage = {cov_wilson:.4f}")


main()

critical value z: 1.959964
exact coverage probabilities C(p) = sum P(k | p) * I(p in CI(k)):
p = 0.05: Wald coverage = 0.6389, Wilson coverage = 0.9245
p = 0.10: Wald coverage = 0.8760, Wilson coverage = 0.9568
p = 0.20: Wald coverage = 0.9208, Wilson coverage = 0.9563
p = 0.50: Wald coverage = 0.9586, Wilson coverage = 0.9586
p = 0.80: Wald coverage = 0.9208, Wilson coverage = 0.9563
p = 0.90: Wald coverage = 0.8760, Wilson coverage = 0.9568
p = 0.95: Wald coverage = 0.6389, Wilson coverage = 0.9245

Interpretation: The exact summation confirms that the textbook Wald interval severely under-covers when true hit rates are extreme (\(63.89\%\) vs nominal \(95\%\)). The Wilson interval, derived via rigorous test inversion, provides robust coverage across the parameter space.

4Where the canon breaks

Standard confidence interval formulas rely on assumptions that fail in production financial engineering:

  • Post-selection coverage breakdown: When a portfolio team tests 200 factor signals and reports a \(95\%\) confidence interval for the best-performing strategy, the nominal \(95\%\) coverage is an illusion. Conditioning on selection shifts the sampling distribution, causing the true coverage probability of the reported interval to drop toward zero.
  • Serial dependence and clustered errors: In time-series financial data with autocorrelation or volatility clustering, the standard error divisor \(\sqrt{n}\) is invalid. Naive intervals that ignore serial correlation are drastically too narrow, leading to severe under-coverage.
  • Non-compact parameter spaces and heavy tails: When asset returns lack finite second or fourth moments, asymptotic normality fails. Confidence intervals based on the Gaussian or Student-t distribution fail to capture heavy-tail risk.

Misconceptions

  • "There is a 95% probability that the parameter lies in the realized interval [0.02, 0.05]."

    Correction: In frequentist inference, \(\theta\) is an unknown constant, not a random variable. Once the sample is collected and the numerical interval \([0.02, 0.05]\) is calculated, the true parameter is either inside or outside with probability 0 or 1. The \(95\%\) confidence level refers to the long-run coverage rate of the random interval procedure across repeated samples.

  • "The Wald interval is always valid because of the Central Limit Theorem."

    Correction: The Central Limit Theorem is an asymptotic limit as \(n \to \infty\) for fixed \(p\). For any finite sample size \(n\), if \(p\) is sufficiently close to 0 or 1, the binomial distribution is highly skewed, and the normal approximation fails. As Lab 1 shows, the Wald interval achieves only \(63.9\%\) coverage at \(p = 0.05\) for \(n = 20\).

  • "A narrower confidence interval is always mathematically superior."

    Correction: Interval width is meaningless if the interval fails to achieve its nominal coverage probability. An interval that reports zero width (such as the Wald interval when \(K = 0\)) has zero utility because its coverage probability collapses.

Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Gaussian mean confidence interval

A strategy with known return volatility \(\sigma = 0.04\) is observed over \(n = 100\) daily periods, yielding sample mean \(\bar X = 0.005\). Construct an exact \(95\%\) confidence interval for the true mean \(\mu\).

Solution

The standard error is \(\sigma / \sqrt{n} = 0.04 / 10 = 0.004\). The critical value is \(\Phi^{-1}(0.975) \approx 1.960\). The confidence interval is \(\bar X \pm 1.960 \times 0.004 = 0.005 \pm 0.00784 = [-0.00284, 0.01284]\).

M2 · Wald interval collapse at boundary

An algorithmic trading bot executes \(n = 25\) trades and suffers \(k = 0\) losses (all 25 trades are wins, so \(\hat p = 1.0\)). Compute the Wald \(95\%\) confidence interval for the win rate \(p\).

Solution

The estimated standard error is \(\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n} = \sqrt{1(0)/25} = 0\). The Wald interval is \(1.0 \pm 1.960(0) = [1.0, 1.0]\). It has zero width and falsely claims absolute certainty.

M3 · Wilson center shift

For \(n = 20\) and \(\hat p = 0.05\) (1 success in 20 trials), calculate the center of the Wilson score interval using \(z = 1.96\).

Solution

The center of the Wilson interval is \(\frac{\hat p + z^2 / (2n)}{1 + z^2 / n}\). Here \(z^2 \approx 3.8416\), so \(z^2 / (2n) = 3.8416 / 40 = 0.09604\), and \(1 + z^2 / n = 1 + 3.8416 / 20 = 1 + 0.19208 = 1.19208\). The center is \(\frac{0.05 + 0.09604}{1.19208} = \frac{0.14604}{1.19208} \approx 0.1225\), shifted noticeably toward 0.50.

Proof

P1 · Test inversion coverage proof

Prove that if \(A(\theta)\) has Type I error probability \(\alpha\) for all \(\theta \in \Theta\), then the inverted confidence set \(C(X) = \{\theta : X \in A(\theta)\}\) has exact coverage probability \(1 - \alpha\) for all \(\theta\).

Solution

By set construction, \(\theta \in C(X) \iff X \in A(\theta)\). Therefore, \(\Prob_\theta(\theta \in C(X)) = \Prob_\theta(X \in A(\theta))\). Since the test has exact size \(\alpha\) under \(H_0: \theta\), the acceptance probability is \(\Prob_\theta(X \in A(\theta)) = 1 - \alpha\). Thus \(\Prob_\theta(\theta \in C(X)) = 1 - \alpha\) holds identically for all \(\theta \in \Theta\).

P2 · Wilson score quadratic roots

Derive the closed-form endpoints of the Wilson score interval by solving the quadratic inequality \((\hat p - p)^2 \le \frac{z^2}{n} p(1 - p)\).

Solution

Expanding gives \(\hat p^2 - 2\hat p p + p^2 \le \frac{z^2}{n} p - \frac{z^2}{n} p^2\). Grouping terms by powers of \(p\): \[ \left(1 + \frac{z^2}{n}\right) p^2 - 2\left(\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n}\right) p + \hat p^2 \le 0 \] Applying the quadratic formula \(p = \frac{-B \pm \sqrt{B^2 - 4AC}}{2A}\) with \(A = 1 + z^2/n\), \(B = -2(\hat p + z^2/(2n))\), \(C = \hat p^2\): The discriminant divided by 4 is: \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta/4 &= \left(\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n}\right)^2 - \left(1 + \frac{z^2}{n}\right)\hat p^2 \\ &= \frac{z^2}{n}\left(\hat p(1 - \hat p) + \frac{z^2}{4n}\right) \end{aligned} \] Taking the square root and dividing by \(A = 1 + z^2/n\) yields the Wilson interval formula.

P3 · Expected length under normal model

For an \(\iid\) normal sample \(X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with known \(\sigma\), prove that the expected width of the \(1 - \alpha\) confidence interval is \(2 z_{1-\alpha/2} \sigma / \sqrt{n}\), and explain why it shrinks at rate \(\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})\).

Solution

The interval is \([\bar X - z \sigma/\sqrt{n}, \bar X + z \sigma/\sqrt{n}]\). The width is \(W = (\bar X + z\sigma/\sqrt{n}) - (\bar X - z\sigma/\sqrt{n}) = 2 z \sigma / \sqrt{n}\), which is non-random. Its expected value is \(\E[W] = 2 z_{1-\alpha/2} \sigma / \sqrt{n}\). The rate \(\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})\) reflects the central limit theorem rate of convergence of the sample mean.

E3 · Symmetry of coverage probabilities (Lab 1)

Notice in Lab 1 that the coverage at \(p = 0.10\) and \(p = 0.90\) are identical (\(0.8760\) for Wald, \(0.9568\) for Wilson). Prove why exact coverage is symmetric about \(p = 0.50\).

Solution

Under the parameter transformation \(p \leftrightarrow 1 - p\), the binomial distribution satisfies \(\Prob(K = k \mid p) = \Prob(K = n - k \mid 1 - p)\). The estimator transforms as \(\hat p \leftrightarrow 1 - \hat p\), and the standard error \(\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n}\) is invariant. Thus the coverage indicator satisfies \(\indic(p \in \operatorname{CI}(k)) = \indic(1 - p \in \operatorname{CI}(n - k))\), ensuring \(C(p) = C(1 - p)\).

Research

R1 · Clopper–Pearson exact interval

Explain why the Clopper–Pearson exact confidence interval for a binomial proportion is guaranteed to have coverage probability at least \(1 - \alpha\) for all \(p \in (0, 1)\), and state its primary disadvantage.

Solution

The Clopper–Pearson interval directly inverts exact binomial tail tests. Because the binomial distribution is discrete, tail probabilities jump, forcing the test size to be \(\le \alpha\). Inverting these conservative tests guarantees coverage \(\ge 1 - \alpha\) everywhere. The disadvantage is that it is overly conservative, producing excessively wide intervals.

R2 · Post-selection shrinkage of confidence sets

When selecting the best strategy out of \(M\) candidates, describe how confidence intervals can be adjusted to preserve valid coverage.

Solution

Simultaneous inference adjustments (such as Bonferroni or Scheffé corrections) replace \(\alpha\) with \(\alpha / M\), widening the interval to guarantee that all \(M\) intervals simultaneously cover their respective parameters with probability at least \(1 - \alpha\). Conditioning on selection requires conditional post-selection inference (Lee et al., 2016).

R3 · Connection to Sharpe ratio confidence intervals

How does the concept of confidence intervals apply to evaluating a portfolio's Sharpe ratio in Lesson 10?

Solution

In Lesson 10, the sample Sharpe ratio \(\hSR\) has estimated standard error \(\se(\hSR)\). The asymptotic confidence interval is \(\hSR \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \se(\hSR)\). Understanding coverage probability ensures researchers recognize when small track records \(T\) or heavy return tails cause empirical coverage to fall short of nominal levels.
