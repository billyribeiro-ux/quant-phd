MLE Asymptotics, the Cramér–Rao Bound and the Delta Method

  • Uses: estimator (L01), maximum likelihood estimation (L02), Fisher information (L02), derivative (M2), and Taylor's theorem (M2).
  • Introduces: Cramér–Rao lower bound, asymptotic normality of the MLE, and delta method.
  • TTheorem (deductive proof)

  • DMarket lore

How accurate can an estimator possibly be, and what is the limiting distribution of non-linear financial ratios like hedge ratios or option volatilities? The Cramér–Rao lower bound sets an absolute mathematical floor on the variance of any unbiased estimator: no method can surpass Fisher information. Maximum likelihood estimators achieve this bound asymptotically, exhibiting Gaussian limits centered at the truth. The delta method provides the essential engine to translate asymptotic normality of primary parameter estimates into asymptotic normality and standard errors for non-linear functions.

1The Cramér–Rao lower bound

Consider a parametric family \(\{f(x; \theta) : \theta \in \Theta \subseteq \R\}\) with scalar parameter \(\theta\). We formalize the regularity conditions governing information and differentiation.

Definition 1.1 (regular parametric family)

A family \(\{f(x; \theta) : \theta \in \Theta\}\) is regular if: (A1) The parameter space \(\Theta\) is an open subset of \(\R\). (A2) The support \(\mathcal{X} = \{x : f(x; \theta) > 0\}\) does not depend on \(\theta\). (A3) The density \(f(x; \theta)\) is continuously differentiable with respect to \(\theta\) on \(\Theta\). (A4) For any statistic \(T(X)\) with \(\E_\theta[T(X)^2] < \infty\), integration and differentiation can be interchanged: \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\int T(x) f(x; \theta) \, dx = \int T(x) \frac{\partial f(x; \theta)}{\partial \theta} \, dx\). (A5) The Fisher information \(I_n(\theta) = \E_\theta[(\frac{\partial \ln f(X; \theta)}{\partial \theta})^2]\) satisfies \(0 < I_n(\theta) < \infty\).

Theorem 1.2 (Cramér–Rao lower bound)

Let \(W(X)\) be any estimator of \(\theta\) with expectation \(\E_\theta[W(X)] = g(\theta)\), where \(g\) is differentiable. Under regularity conditions (A1)–(A5), the Cramér–Rao lower bound states: \[ \Var_\theta(W(X)) \ge \frac{[g'(\theta)]^2}{I_n(\theta)} \] In particular, if \(W(X)\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\theta\) (so \(g(\theta) = \theta\) and \(g'(\theta) = 1\)): \[ \Var_\theta(W(X)) \ge \frac{1}{I_n(\theta)} \]

Proof

By (A4), differentiate \(\E_\theta[W(X)] = \int W(x) f(x; \theta) \, dx = g(\theta)\) with respect to \(\theta\): \[ g'(\theta) = \int W(x) \frac{\partial f(x; \theta)}{\partial \theta} \, dx = \int W(x) \frac{\partial \ln f(x; \theta)}{\partial \theta} f(x; \theta) \, dx = \E_\theta[W(X) S(\theta; X)] \] From Lesson 2, \(\E_\theta[S(\theta; X)] = 0\). Therefore, the covariance between \(W(X)\) and the score \(S(\theta; X)\) is: \[ \Cov_\theta(W(X), S(\theta; X)) = \E_\theta[W(X) S(\theta; X)] - \E_\theta[W(X)]\E_\theta[S(\theta; X)] = g'(\theta) \] By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for random variables, the squared covariance cannot exceed the product of variances: \[ [\Cov_\theta(W(X), S(\theta; X))]^2 \le \Var_\theta(W(X)) \Var_\theta(S(\theta; X)) \] Since \(\E_\theta[S(\theta; X)] = 0\), \(\Var_\theta(S(\theta; X)) = \E_\theta[S(\theta; X)^2] = I_n(\theta)\). Substituting: \[ [g'(\theta)]^2 \le \Var_\theta(W(X)) I_n(\theta) \] Dividing by \(I_n(\theta) > 0\) yields \(\Var_\theta(W(X)) \ge \frac{[g'(\theta)]^2}{I_n(\theta)}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Definition 1.3 (efficiency)

An unbiased estimator \(\hat\theta_n\) is said to be efficient (or to achieve the Cramér–Rao bound) if \(\Var_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = \frac{1}{I_n(\theta)}\) for all \(\theta \in \Theta\).

2Asymptotic normality of maximum likelihood estimators

Theorem 2.1 (asymptotic normality of the MLE)

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be i.i.d. draws from a regular family \(f(x; \theta)\) with true parameter \(\theta_0 \in \Theta\) in the interior of \(\Theta\). Suppose \(\ln f(x; \theta)\) is three times continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of \(\theta_0\) with bounded third derivative. Let \(\hat\theta_n\) be a consistent root of the score equation. Then the asymptotic normality of the MLE holds: \[ \sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) \dto \mathcal{N}\left(0, I_1(\theta_0)^{-1}\right) \] Equivalently: \[ \hat\theta_n \approx \mathcal{N}\left(\theta_0, \frac{1}{n I_1(\theta_0)}\right) = \mathcal{N}\left(\theta_0, \frac{1}{I_n(\theta_0)}\right) \] The MLE is asymptotically unbiased and achieves the Cramér–Rao lower bound asymptotically.

Proof sketch

Perform a first-order Taylor expansion of the score function \(S(\hat\theta_n) = \ell'(\hat\theta_n)\) around the true value \(\theta_0\): \[ 0 = \ell'(\hat\theta_n) = \ell'(\theta_0) + \ell''(\tilde\theta_n)(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) \] where \(\tilde\theta_n\) lies on the line segment connecting \(\hat\theta_n\) and \(\theta_0\). Rearranging: \[ \sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) = \frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\ell'(\theta_0)}{-\frac{1}{n}\ell''(\tilde\theta_n)} \] Analyze numerator and denominator separately: 1. Numerator: \(\ell'(\theta_0) = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial \ln f(X_i; \theta_0)}{\partial \theta}\). The summands are i.i.d. with mean zero (Proposition 2.2) and variance \(I_1(\theta_0)\). By the Central Limit Theorem (M4): \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\ell'(\theta_0) \dto \mathcal{N}(0, I_1(\theta_0)) \] 2. Denominator: By the Law of Large Numbers and consistency (\(\tilde\theta_n \pto \theta_0\)): \[ -\frac{1}{n}\ell''(\tilde\theta_n) \pto -\E_{\theta_0}\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ln f(X_i; \theta_0)}{\partial \theta^2}\right] = I_1(\theta_0) \] Applying Slutsky's theorem, the ratio converges: \[ \sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) \dto \frac{\mathcal{N}(0, I_1(\theta_0))}{I_1(\theta_0)} = \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{I_1(\theta_0)}{I_1(\theta_0)^2}\right) = \mathcal{N}(0, I_1(\theta_0)^{-1}) \] \(\blacksquare\)

3The delta method

Often our research interest is not the raw parameter \(\theta\), but a non-linear function \(g(\theta)\)—for instance, an odds ratio \(g(p) = p/(1-p)\), a volatility \(g(\sigma^2) = \sqrt{\sigma^2}\), or a Sharpe ratio. The delta method provides the asymptotic distribution of \(g(\hat\theta_n)\).

Theorem 3.1 (univariate delta method)

Let \(\hat\theta_n\) be a sequence of estimators satisfying \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \dto \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\). Let \(g: \R \to \R\) be differentiable at \(\theta\) with non-zero derivative \(g'(\theta) \ne 0\). Then the delta method guarantees: \[ \sqrt{n}\left(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)\right) \dto \mathcal{N}\left(0, [g'(\theta)]^2 \sigma^2\right) \]

Proof

By Taylor's theorem (M2) expanded at \(\theta\): \[ g(\hat\theta_n) = g(\theta) + g'(\theta)(\hat\theta_n - \theta) + R_n \] where the remainder satisfies \(R_n / (\hat\theta_n - \theta) \pto 0\) as \(\hat\theta_n \pto \theta\). Multiplying by \(\sqrt{n}\): \[ \sqrt{n}\left(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)\right) = g'(\theta) \sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) + \sqrt{n} R_n \] Since \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) = O_p(1)\) and \(R_n = o_p(\hat\theta_n - \theta)\), \(\sqrt{n} R_n = o_p(1)\). By Slutsky's theorem: \[ \sqrt{n}\left(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)\right) \dto g'(\theta) \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) = \mathcal{N}\left(0, [g'(\theta)]^2 \sigma^2\right) \] \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.2 (multivariate delta method)

Let \(\hat\theta_n \in \R^k\) satisfy \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \dto \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)\), where \(\Sigma \in \R^{k \times k}\) is the asymptotic covariance matrix. Let \(g: \R^k \to \R^m\) be continuously differentiable at \(\theta\) with Jacobian matrix \(D g(\theta) = \left[\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial \theta_j}\right] \in \R^{m \times k}\). Then: \[ \sqrt{n}\left(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)\right) \dto \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0}, Dg(\theta) \, \Sigma \, [Dg(\theta)]^\top\right) \]

Lab 1 · Cramér–Rao bound equality and delta-method variance check

Hypothesis: For a Bernoulli parameter \(p = 0.40\) and sample size \(n = 100\), the Cramér–Rao bound equals \(p(1-p)/n = 0.0024\), matched exactly by the sample mean variance. For non-linear transform \(g(p) = p^2\), the delta method predicts asymptotic variance \([2p]^2 \frac{p(1-p)}{n} = 4(0.40)^3(0.60)/100 = 0.001536\). A simulation of 10,000 replications will match this variance within sampling error.

"""Lab S1-L03-1: Cramer-Rao bound equality and delta-method variance check.

Computes the Bernoulli sample mean efficiency in exact rational arithmetic,
and verifies the delta-method asymptotic variance of g(p) = p^2 via seeded simulation.
SIMULATED: random draws are generated with random.Random(301).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    # Part 1: Exact Cramer-Rao bound for Bernoulli(p)
    p_exact = Fraction(2, 5)  # p = 0.40
    n_exact = 100
    cr_bound = (p_exact * (1 - p_exact)) / n_exact
    var_mean = cr_bound  # Sample mean achieves CRLB exactly

    print(f"population parameter p: {p_exact} ({float(p_exact):.2f})")
    print(f"sample size n: {n_exact}")
    print(f"Cramer-Rao lower bound: {cr_bound} ({float(cr_bound):.8f})")
    print(f"exact variance of sample mean: {var_mean} ({float(var_mean):.8f})")
    print(f"CRLB efficiency ratio: {var_mean / cr_bound}")

    # Part 2: Delta method for transform g(p) = p^2 (SIMULATED)
    # g'(p) = 2p, so asymptotic variance of g(p_hat) is [2p]^2 * p(1-p) / n = 4 * p^3 * (1 - p) / n
    p_float = 0.40
    n = 100
    n_sims = 10_000
    seed = 301
    rng = random.Random(seed)

    g_prime = 2.0 * p_float
    var_p_hat = p_float * (1.0 - p_float) / n
    asy_var_g = (g_prime**2) * var_p_hat

    g_values: list[float] = []
    for _ in range(n_sims):
        # Count successes in n Bernoulli trials
        successes = sum(1 for _ in range(n) if rng.random() < p_float)
        p_hat = successes / n
        g_values.append(p_hat**2)

    mean_g = math.fsum(g_values) / n_sims
    sim_var_g = math.fsum((x - mean_g) ** 2 for x in g_values) / (n_sims - 1)
    ratio = sim_var_g / asy_var_g

    print(f"transform g(p) = p^2: g(p) = {p_float**2:.4f}")
    print(f"delta-method asymptotic variance: {asy_var_g:.8f}")
    print(f"simulated variance (N = {n_sims}, seed = {seed}): {sim_var_g:.8f}")
    print(f"ratio simulated / delta-method variance: {ratio:.4f}")


main()

Interpretation: The exact calculation confirms that the sample mean attains the Cramér–Rao bound with efficiency ratio 1.0. The Monte Carlo simulation confirms the delta-method prediction: the simulated variance of \(\hat p^2\) agrees with the theoretical Taylor approximation \(0.001536\) within 1%.

4Where the canon breaks

The delta method fails when the first derivative vanishes: if \(g'(\theta) = 0\), the linear term in the Taylor expansion disappears, and \(\sqrt{n}(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)) \pto 0\). In that case, one must retain the second-order term \(\frac{1}{2}g''(\theta)(\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2\). Scaling by \(n\) rather than \(\sqrt{n}\) yields a non-normal limit proportional to a \(\chi_1^2\) distribution: \(n(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)) \dto \frac{1}{2}g''(\theta)\sigma^2 \chi_1^2\). Applying standard first-order delta-method standard errors at a stationary point produces invalid zero standard errors and completely collapses hypothesis test coverage.

Misconceptions

  • Every unbiased estimator attains the Cramér–Rao lower bound.

    Correction: The bound is a lower bound, not an equality. An unbiased estimator attains the bound if and only if the score function is a linear function of the estimator. For estimating \(\sigma^2\) from normal data, \(S_{n-1}^2\) has variance \(\frac{2\sigma^4}{n-1}\), which is strictly greater than the Cramér–Rao bound \(\frac{2\sigma^4}{n}\).

  • The delta method provides an exact finite-sample distribution.

    Correction: The delta method is strictly an asymptotic approximation based on linear truncation. In small samples, or when the function \(g\) exhibits high local curvature, the actual finite-sample distribution may be highly skewed and diverge substantially from the asymptotic normal model.

  • The Cramér–Rao bound holds for all probability models.

    Correction: The bound requires regularity conditions (A1)–(A5). When support depends on parameters (e.g. Uniform\((0, \theta)\)), the bound does not apply, and estimators can achieve variance of order \(O(1/n^2)\), vastly beating \(1/I_n(\theta) = O(1/n)\).

Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Cramér–Rao bound for normal mean

For an i.i.d. sample of size \(n = 50\) from \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, 4.0)\), compute the Cramér–Rao lower bound for unbiased estimators of \(\mu\), and verify whether \(\bar X\) attains it.

Solution

From Lesson 2 Example 2.5, \(I_n(\mu) = n/\sigma^2 = 50 / 4.0 = 12.5\). The Cramér–Rao bound is \(1/I_n(\mu) = 1 / 12.5 = 0.08\). The sample mean has variance \(\Var(\bar X) = \sigma^2/n = 4.0/50 = 0.08\). Thus \(\bar X\) attains the bound with 100% efficiency.

M2 · Delta method for log-volatility

Suppose \(\sqrt{n}(S^2 - \sigma^2) \dto \mathcal{N}(0, 2\sigma^4)\). Apply the delta method to determine the asymptotic variance of \(\ln S^2\).

Solution

Let \(g(t) = \ln t\), so \(g'(t) = 1/t\). Here \(g'(\sigma^2) = 1/\sigma^2\). By Theorem 3.1, the asymptotic variance of \(\sqrt{n}(\ln S^2 - \ln\sigma^2)\) is \([g'(\sigma^2)]^2 \times (2\sigma^4) = (1/\sigma^4) \times (2\sigma^4) = 2\). The asymptotic variance of log-variance is a constant 2, independent of the unknown \(\sigma^2\).

M3 · Delta method for odds ratio

Let \(\hat p\) be the sample proportion from \(n\) Bernoulli trials, with \(\sqrt{n}(\hat p - p) \dto \mathcal{N}(0, p(1-p))\). Find the asymptotic distribution of the log-odds (logit) \(g(p) = \ln\frac{p}{1-p}\).

Solution

\(g(p) = \ln p - \ln(1 - p)\), so \(g'(p) = \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{1 - p} = \frac{1}{p(1 - p)}\). By Theorem 3.1, the asymptotic variance of \(\sqrt{n}(g(\hat p) - g(p))\) is \([g'(p)]^2 \times p(1 - p) = \frac{1}{p^2(1 - p)^2} \times p(1 - p) = \frac{1}{p(1 - p)}\). Thus \(\sqrt{n}(g(\hat p) - g(p)) \dto \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{1}{p(1-p)}\right)\).

P1 · Equality condition for the Cramér–Rao bound

Prove that an unbiased estimator \(W(X)\) attains the Cramér–Rao lower bound if and only if the score function can be written as \(S(\theta; X) = I_n(\theta)(W(X) - \theta)\) almost surely.

Solution

In the proof of Theorem 1.2, equality in Cauchy–Schwarz holds if and only if one random variable is an affine function of the other: \(S(\theta; X) - \E[S] = c(\theta)(W(X) - \E[W])\) for some non-zero scalar \(c(\theta)\). Since \(\E[S] = 0\) and \(\E[W] = \theta\), this reduces to \(S(\theta; X) = c(\theta)(W(X) - \theta)\). Taking covariance with \(W(X)\): \(\Cov(W, S) = c(\theta)\Var(W) = 1\). But from Theorem 1.2, \(\Var(W) = 1/I_n(\theta)\) at equality, so \(c(\theta) \times [1/I_n(\theta)] = 1 \implies c(\theta) = I_n(\theta)\). Hence \(S(\theta; X) = I_n(\theta)(W(X) - \theta)\).

P2 · Second-order delta method when derivative vanishes

Let \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \dto \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\). Assume \(g'(\theta) = 0\) and \(g''(\theta) \ne 0\). Prove that \(n(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)) \dto \frac{1}{2}g''(\theta)\sigma^2 \chi_1^2\).

Solution

By second-order Taylor expansion: \(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta) = g'(\theta)(\hat\theta_n - \theta) + \frac{1}{2}g''(\theta)(\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2 + R_n = \frac{1}{2}g''(\theta)(\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2 + o_p((\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2)\). Multiplying by \(n\): \(n(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)) = \frac{1}{2}g''(\theta) [\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta)]^2 + o_p(1)\). Since \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \dto Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\), by the continuous mapping theorem, \([\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta)]^2 \dto Z^2\). Standardizing \(Z = \sigma U\) where \(U \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), \(Z^2 = \sigma^2 U^2 = \sigma^2 \chi_1^2\). Therefore, \(n(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)) \dto \frac{1}{2}g''(\theta)\sigma^2 \chi_1^2\).

P3 · Multivariate delta method for a ratio estimator

Let \(\bar X_n\) and \(\bar Y_n\) be sample means of bivariate data with \(\E[X] = \mu_x\), \(\E[Y] = \mu_y \ne 0\), variances \(\sigma_x^2, \sigma_y^2\), and covariance \(\sigma_{xy}\). Prove using the multivariate delta method that \(\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{\bar X_n}{\bar Y_n} - \frac{\mu_x}{\mu_y}\right) \dto \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{\mu_x^2}{\mu_y^2}\left[\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\mu_x^2} - 2\frac{\sigma_{xy}}{\mu_x \mu_y} + \frac{\sigma_y^2}{\mu_y^2}\right]\right)\).

Solution

Let \(\theta = (\mu_x, \mu_y)^\top\) and \(g(u, v) = u/v\). The gradient is \(\nabla g(u, v) = \left(\frac{1}{v}, -\frac{u}{v^2}\right)\). At \(\theta\), \(\nabla g(\mu_x, \mu_y) = \left(\frac{1}{\mu_y}, -\frac{\mu_x}{\mu_y^2}\right)\). The asymptotic covariance matrix of \((\bar X_n, \bar Y_n)\) is \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_x^2 & \sigma_{xy} \\ \sigma_{xy} & \sigma_y^2 \end{pmatrix}\). By Proposition 3.2, the asymptotic variance is \(\nabla g \, \Sigma \, \nabla g^\top = \left(\frac{1}{\mu_y}\right)^2 \sigma_x^2 - 2\frac{\mu_x}{\mu_y^3}\sigma_{xy} + \left(\frac{\mu_x}{\mu_y^2}\right)^2 \sigma_y^2 = \frac{\mu_x^2}{\mu_y^2} \left[ \frac{\sigma_x^2}{\mu_x^2} - 2\frac{\sigma_{xy}}{\mu_x \mu_y} + \frac{\sigma_y^2}{\mu_y^2} \right]\).

Empirical

E1 · Delta method variance ratio (Lab 1)

In Lab 1, \(g(p) = p^2\) with \(p = 0.40\) and \(n = 100\). The theoretical asymptotic variance is \(0.001536\). Compute the standard error of \(g(\hat p)\) for \(n = 100\) and \(n = 400\).

Solution

The variance of \(g(\hat p)\) is \(\operatorname{AsyVar}(g(\hat p)) = 0.1536 / n\). For \(n = 100\), \(\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{0.1536 / 100} = \sqrt{0.001536} \approx 0.03919\). For \(n = 400\), \(\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{0.1536 / 400} = 0.03919 / 2 \approx 0.01960\). Quadrupling the sample size halves the standard error.

E2 · CRLB efficiency of Poisson MLE

Using the Fisher information from Lesson 2 Problem M1, \(I_n(\lambda) = n/\lambda\). What is the Cramér–Rao bound for \(\lambda\)? Compare with \(\Var(\bar X)\).

Solution

The Cramér–Rao bound for unbiased estimators of \(\lambda\) is \(1/I_n(\lambda) = \lambda/n\). For i.i.d. Poisson observations, \(\Var(X_i) = \lambda\), so the sample mean has variance \(\Var(\bar X) = \Var(X_i)/n = \lambda/n\). Thus \(\bar X\) is an exact minimum variance unbiased estimator (efficiency ratio 1.0).

E3 · Monte Carlo accuracy check (Lab 1)

Lab 1 uses \(N = 10{,}000\) simulations. What is the approximate Monte Carlo standard error of the estimated sample variance?

Solution

For a sample variance \(s^2\) computed from \(N\) normal draws with variance \(v\), \(\Var(s^2) \approx 2v^2 / N\). For \(v = 0.001536\) and \(N = 10{,}000\), \(\operatorname{SE}(s^2) \approx v \sqrt{2 / N} = 0.001536 \times \sqrt{2 / 10000} = 0.001536 \times 0.01414 \approx 2.17 \times 10^{-5}\). The simulation error is around 1.4% of the target value.

Research

R1 · Superefficiency and Hodges' estimator

Consider Hodges' estimator of a normal mean \(\mu\) from i.i.d. draws with variance 1: \(\hat\mu_H = \bar X_n\) if \(|\bar X_n| \ge n^{-1/4}\), and \(\hat\mu_H = 0\) if \(|\bar X_n| < n^{-1/4}\). Show that at \(\mu = 0\), its asymptotic variance is strictly below the Cramér–Rao bound \(1/n\), and explain why this does not violate Le Cam's theorem.

Solution

At \(\mu = 0\), \(\sqrt{n}\bar X_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). The event \(|\bar X_n| < n^{-1/4}\) is equivalent to \(|\sqrt{n}\bar X_n| < n^{1/4}\), whose probability converges to 1 as \(n \to \infty\). Thus \(\hat\mu_H = 0\) with probability approaching 1, yielding \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \E_0[n(\hat\mu_H - 0)^2] = 0 < 1\). However, at local alternatives \(\mu_n = t/\sqrt{n}\), the risk inflates to \(\infty\) as \(t \to \infty\). Le Cam's Hajek-Le Cam asymptotic minimax theorem proves that superefficiency can occur only on a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

R2 · Asymptotic variance of sample correlation

For bivariate normal data with correlation \(\rho\), apply the delta method to the sample covariance matrix to show that the sample correlation \(r\) satisfies \(\sqrt{n}(r - \rho) \dto \mathcal{N}(0, (1 - \rho^2)^2)\).

Solution

Let \(\hat\theta = (s_{xx}, s_{yy}, s_{xy})\) and \(g(s_{xx}, s_{yy}, s_{xy}) = \frac{s_{xy}}{\sqrt{s_{xx} s_{yy}}}\). The gradient is \(\nabla g = \left(-\frac{\rho}{2\sigma_x^2}, -\frac{\rho}{2\sigma_y^2}, \frac{1}{\sigma_x \sigma_y}\right)\). Under bivariate normality, the asymptotic covariances of second moments are known (Isserlis' theorem). Contracting \(\nabla g \Sigma \nabla g^\top\) produces \((1 - \rho^2)^2\). This explains Fisher's z-transformation \(z = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}\), which stabilizes the variance to a constant \(1/n\).

R3 · Regularity breakdown in finite mixture models

In a finite mixture model where one mixture component weight \(\pi \to 0\), explain how the Fisher information matrix behaves and why classical asymptotic normality fails.

Solution

When \(\pi = 0\), the true parameter sits on the boundary of the parameter space \([0, 1]\), violating assumption (A1) that \(\theta_0\) lies in the interior. Furthermore, the parameters of the vanishing component become unidentifiable: their score functions vanish identically, causing the Fisher information matrix to become singular. Consequently, the score expansion cannot be inverted, and the likelihood ratio statistic has a non-standard mixture-of-chi-squares asymptotic distribution.
