Estimators and Their Sampling Distributions

Every empirical parameter in finance—from expected returns and volatilities to factor betas and Sharpe ratios—is calculated from finite samples. An estimator is a random variable, not a fixed constant. Knowing only the point estimate is useless without understanding its sampling distribution: does it systematically overstate profitability, what is its sampling dispersion, and does it converge to the true parameter as sample size expands? This lesson establishes the mathematical foundations of point estimation, the bias-variance decomposition of mean squared error, and conditions for consistency.

1Estimators and sampling distributions

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be a collection of observable real-valued random variables defined on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \Prob_\theta)\), where \(\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \R^k\) is an unknown parameter indexing the family of probability measures. The sample vector is denoted by \(X = (X_1, \dots, X_n)\).

Definition 1.1 (estimator and sampling distribution)

A statistic is any measurable function \(T: \R^n \to \R^k\) of the sample that does not depend on the unknown parameter \(\theta\). An estimator of \(\theta\), denoted \(\hat\theta_n = T(X_1, \dots, X_n)\), is a statistic used to infer the value of \(\theta\). Because \(X\) is random under \(\Prob_\theta\), the estimator \(\hat\theta_n\) is a random variable; the probability law of \(\hat\theta_n\) induced by \(\Prob_\theta\) is termed its sampling distribution.

Example 1.2 (sample mean and sample variance)

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) draws from a distribution with finite mean \(\mu = \E[X_i]\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2 = \Var(X_i)\). The sample mean is: \[ \bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \] The sample variance with Bessel's correction (divisor \(n - 1\)) is: \[ S_{n-1}^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X_n)^2 \] The naive sample variance with divisor \(n\) is: \[ S_n^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X_n)^2 = \frac{n-1}{n} S_{n-1}^2 \]

2Bias, variance, and mean squared error

Definition 2.1 (bias, variance, and mean squared error)

Let \(\hat\theta_n\) be an estimator of a scalar parameter \(\theta \in \Theta\). The bias, variance and mean squared error of an estimator are defined respectively as: \[ \operatorname{Bias}_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = \E_\theta[\hat\theta_n] - \theta \] \[ \Var_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = \E_\theta\left[(\hat\theta_n - \E_\theta[\hat\theta_n])^2\right] \] \[ \operatorname{MSE}_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = \E_\theta\left[(\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2\right] \] An estimator is said to be unbiased if \(\operatorname{Bias}_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = 0\) for all \(\theta \in \Theta\).

Proposition 2.2 (bias-variance decomposition of MSE)

Let \(\hat\theta_n\) have finite second moment \(\E_\theta[\hat\theta_n^2] < \infty\). Then: \[ \operatorname{MSE}_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = \Var_\theta(\hat\theta_n) + \left[\operatorname{Bias}_\theta(\hat\theta_n)\right]^2 \]

Proof

Write \(\hat\theta_n - \theta = (\hat\theta_n - \E_\theta[\hat\theta_n]) + (\E_\theta[\hat\theta_n] - \theta)\). Squaring both sides: \[ (\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2 = (\hat\theta_n - \E_\theta[\hat\theta_n])^2 + 2(\hat\theta_n - \E_\theta[\hat\theta_n])(\E_\theta[\hat\theta_n] - \theta) + (\E_\theta[\hat\theta_n] - \theta)^2 \] Taking expectations on both sides under \(\Prob_\theta\): \[ \E_\theta\left[(\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2\right] = \Var_\theta(\hat\theta_n) + 2(\E_\theta[\hat\theta_n] - \theta)\E_\theta[\hat\theta_n - \E_\theta[\hat\theta_n]] + [\operatorname{Bias}_\theta(\hat\theta_n)]^2 \] Since \(\E_\theta[\hat\theta_n - \E_\theta[\hat\theta_n]] = 0\), the cross-product term vanishes identically. Thus \(\operatorname{MSE}_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = \Var_\theta(\hat\theta_n) + [\operatorname{Bias}_\theta(\hat\theta_n)]^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.3 (unbiasedness of sample mean and variance)

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be i.i.d. draws with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\). Then:

(a) \(\E[\bar X_n] = \mu\) (the sample mean is unbiased).

(b) \(\Var(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2 / n\).

(c) \(\E[S_{n-1}^2] = \sigma^2\) (Bessel's corrected sample variance is unbiased).

(d) \(\E[S_n^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\), so \(\operatorname{Bias}(S_n^2) = -\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\) (the divisor \(n\) estimator is downward biased).

Proof

For (a), by linearity of expectation: \(\E[\bar X_n] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \E[X_i] = \frac{1}{n}(n\mu) = \mu\). For (b), by independence: \(\Var(\bar X_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \Var(X_i) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\).

For (c) and (d), expand the sum of squared deviations: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X_n)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 - n\bar X_n^2 \] Taking expectations: \(\E[X_i^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2\) and \(\E[\bar X_n^2] = \Var(\bar X_n) + (\E[\bar X_n])^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2\). Therefore: \[ \begin{aligned} \E\left[\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X_n)^2\right] &= n(\sigma^2 + \mu^2) - n\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2\right) \\ &= n\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (n - 1)\sigma^2 \end{aligned} \] Dividing by \(n - 1\) yields \(\E[S_{n-1}^2] = \sigma^2\). Dividing by \(n\) yields \(\E[S_n^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\), giving bias \(-\sigma^2/n\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Consistency in probability

Definition 3.1 (consistent estimator)

A sequence of estimators \(\hat\theta_n\) is a consistent estimator of \(\theta\) (or weakly consistent) if \(\hat\theta_n\) converges in probability to \(\theta\) as \(n \to \infty\), written \(\hat\theta_n \pto \theta\). That is, for every \(\epsilon > 0\): \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \Prob_\theta\left(|\hat\theta_n - \theta| \ge \epsilon\right) = 0 \]

Theorem 3.2 (MSE consistency criterion)

If \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \operatorname{MSE}_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = 0\), then \(\hat\theta_n\) is consistent for \(\theta\). Equivalently, if \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \operatorname{Bias}_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = 0\) and \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \Var_\theta(\hat\theta_n) = 0\), then \(\hat\theta_n \pto \theta\).

Proof

By Markov's inequality applied to the non-negative random variable \((\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2\): \[ \Prob_\theta(|\hat\theta_n - \theta| \ge \epsilon) = \Prob_\theta\left((\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2 \ge \epsilon^2\right) \le \frac{\E_\theta[(\hat\theta_n - \theta)^2]}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\operatorname{MSE}_\theta(\hat\theta_n)}{\epsilon^2} \] If \(\operatorname{MSE}_\theta(\hat\theta_n) \to 0\), the right-hand side vanishes for any fixed \(\epsilon > 0\), establishing \(\hat\theta_n \pto \theta\). By Proposition 2.2, \(\operatorname{MSE} = \Var + \operatorname{Bias}^2\), so vanishing variance and vanishing bias together imply vanishing MSE. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.3 (consistency of biased estimator S_n^2)

Although \(S_n^2 = \frac{n-1}{n}S_{n-1}^2\) is biased with \(\operatorname{Bias}(S_n^2) = -\sigma^2/n\), this bias converges to 0 as \(n \to \infty\). If the fourth population moment \(\E[X^4] < \infty\), then \(\Var(S_{n-1}^2) = O(1/n) \to 0\), so \(\Var(S_n^2) = (\frac{n-1}{n})^2 \Var(S_{n-1}^2) \to 0\). Hence \(\operatorname{MSE}(S_n^2) \to 0\) and \(S_n^2 \pto \sigma^2\). A biased estimator can be fully consistent.

Lab 1 · Exact bias of variance estimators on a three-point law

Hypothesis: For all 9 possible samples of size \(n = 2\) drawn from a three-point discrete law, exact rational calculation will confirm that the expectation of \(S_{n-1}^2\) equals the true population variance \(\sigma^2 = 41/36\), while the expectation of \(S_n^2\) equals \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 = 41/72\), producing an exact bias of \(-41/72\).

"""Lab S1-L01-1: Exact bias of sample variance estimators with divisor n and n - 1.

Enumerates all samples of size n = 2 from a three-point discrete law using fractions.Fraction.
Standard library only. No external imports.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
from fractions import Fraction

# Three-point distribution: values and exact rational probabilities
OUTCOMES = [
    (Fraction(0), Fraction(1, 2)),
    (Fraction(1), Fraction(1, 3)),
    (Fraction(3), Fraction(1, 6)),
]


def main() -> None:
    # Population moments
    mu = sum(x * p for x, p in OUTCOMES)
    sigma2 = sum((x - mu) ** 2 * p for x, p in OUTCOMES)

    print(f"population mean: {mu} ({float(mu):.6f})")
    print(f"population variance: {sigma2} ({float(sigma2):.6f})")

    # Enumerate all 9 pairs of samples of size n = 2
    n = 2
    e_s_biased = Fraction(0)
    e_s_unbiased = Fraction(0)

    for (x1, p1), (x2, p2) in itertools.product(OUTCOMES, repeat=n):
        prob = p1 * p2
        mean = (x1 + x2) / n
        s_biased = ((x1 - mean) ** 2 + (x2 - mean) ** 2) / n
        s_unbiased = ((x1 - mean) ** 2 + (x2 - mean) ** 2) / (n - 1)

        e_s_biased += s_biased * prob
        e_s_unbiased += s_unbiased * prob

    bias_n = e_s_biased - sigma2
    bias_n_minus_1 = e_s_unbiased - sigma2

    print(f"E[S_n^2] (divisor n): {e_s_biased} ({float(e_s_biased):.6f})")
    print(f"bias of S_n^2: {bias_n} ({float(bias_n):.6f})")
    print(f"E[S_{{n-1}}^2] (divisor n - 1): {e_s_unbiased} ({float(e_s_unbiased):.6f})")
    print(f"bias of S_{{n-1}}^2: {bias_n_minus_1} ({float(bias_n_minus_1):.6f})")
    print(f"ratio E[S_n^2] / sigma^2: {e_s_biased / sigma2} (expected (n-1)/n = 1/2)")

population variance: 41/36 (1.138889)

bias of S_n^2: -41/72 (-0.569444)

Interpretation: The exact enumeration demonstrates that Bessel's correction \(n - 1\) restores unbiasedness in finite samples by compensating for the degree of freedom consumed in estimating the sample mean. The divisor \(n\) estimator underestimates population variance by factor \((n-1)/n = 1/2\) at \(n = 2\).

4Where the canon breaks

The classical bias-variance framework assumes independent and identically distributed draws from a stationary distribution. In quantitative finance, return series exhibit temporal dependence (heteroskedasticity and volatility clustering), heavy tails (where variance or fourth moments may be infinite), and structural regime shifts. When \(\E[X_i^2] = \infty\) (such as Pareto returns with tail index \(\alpha \le 2\)), the population variance \(\sigma^2\) does not exist, sample variance fails to converge to any constant, and Chebyshev consistency collapses.

Misconceptions

  • A biased estimator cannot be consistent.

    Correction: Consistency requires only that the bias vanishes asymptotically as \(n \to \infty\) along with the sampling variance. The naive sample variance \(S_n^2 = \frac{n-1}{n}S_{n-1}^2\) has bias \(-\sigma^2/n \ne 0\) in every finite sample, but \(\lim_{n \to \infty} (-\sigma^2/n) = 0\), rendering \(S_n^2\) weakly consistent.

  • An unbiased estimator always has smaller mean squared error than a biased estimator.

    Correction: Mean squared error equals variance plus bias squared. An estimator that introduces a slight bias may dramatically reduce sampling variance, yielding lower total MSE. In normal samples, the estimator \(\tilde S^2 = \frac{1}{n+1}\sum (X_i - \bar X)^2\) has strictly lower MSE than the unbiased estimator \(S_{n-1}^2\).

  • An estimator with zero variance is optimal.

    Correction: The constant estimator \(\hat\theta = 0\) has zero variance, but its bias is \(-\theta\). Its mean squared error is \(\theta^2\), which diverges as the true parameter deviates from zero. Meaningful estimation balances bias against variance across the parameter space.

Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Bias of divisor n variance estimator

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_{10}\) be i.i.d. draws with variance \(\sigma^2 = 4.0\). Compute the exact expectation and bias of the naive sample variance \(S_{10}^2 = \frac{1}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}(X_i - \bar X)^2\).

Solution

By Proposition 2.3(d), \(\E[S_n^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\). For \(n = 10\) and \(\sigma^2 = 4.0\), \(\E[S_{10}^2] = \frac{9}{10} \times 4.0 = 3.60\). The bias is \(\operatorname{Bias}(S_{10}^2) = \E[S_{10}^2] - \sigma^2 = 3.60 - 4.00 = -0.40\).

M2 · MSE calculation from bias and variance

An estimator \(\hat\theta\) has bias \(\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta) = 0.20\) and variance \(\Var(\hat\theta) = 0.05\). Compute its mean squared error \(\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta)\).

Solution

By the bias-variance decomposition (Proposition 2.2), \(\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \Var(\hat\theta) + \operatorname{Bias}^2 = 0.05 + 0.04 = 0.09\).

M3 · Variance of scaled sample mean

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be i.i.d. returns with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). For constant \(c > 0\), determine the bias, variance, and MSE of the scaled estimator \(\hat\mu_c = c \bar X_n\) as an estimator of \(\mu\).

Solution

\(\E[\hat\mu_c] = c\E[\bar X_n] = c\mu\), so \(\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\mu_c) = (c - 1)\mu\). The variance is \(\Var(\hat\mu_c) = c^2 \Var(\bar X_n) = c^2 \sigma^2 / n\). By Proposition 2.2, \(\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\mu_c) = c^2 \sigma^2 / n + (c - 1)^2 \mu^2\).

Proof

P1 · Unbiasedness of sample covariance

Let \((X_1, Y_1), \dots, (X_n, Y_n)\) be i.i.d. bivariate draws with covariance \(\sigma_{xy} = \Cov(X_i, Y_i)\). Prove that the sample covariance \(S_{xy} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)(Y_i - \bar Y)\) is unbiased: \(\E[S_{xy}] = \sigma_{xy}\).

Solution

Expand the sum of cross-deviations: \(\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)(Y_i - \bar Y) = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i Y_i - n \bar X \bar Y\). Now \(\E[X_i Y_i] = \sigma_{xy} + \mu_x \mu_y\).

Furthermore: \[ \begin{aligned} \E[\bar X \bar Y] &= \E\left[\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n X_i Y_j\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{n^2}\left[n\E[X_i Y_i] + n(n-1)\E[X_i]\E[Y_j]\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{n}(\sigma_{xy} + \mu_x \mu_y) + \frac{n-1}{n}\mu_x \mu_y \\ &= \frac{\sigma_{xy}}{n} + \mu_x \mu_y \end{aligned} \] Subtracting: \(\E\left[\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)(Y_i - \bar Y)\right] = n(\sigma_{xy} + \mu_x \mu_y) - (\sigma_{xy} + n\mu_x \mu_y) = (n - 1)\sigma_{xy}\). Dividing by \(n - 1\) yields \(\E[S_{xy}] = \sigma_{xy}\).

P2 · Minimum MSE linear shrinkage of the sample mean

Consider the estimator \(\hat\mu_c = c \bar X_n\) for \(\mu \ne 0\). Prove that the value of \(c\) minimizing \(\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\mu_c)\) is \(c^* = \frac{\mu^2}{\mu^2 + \sigma^2/n} \in (0, 1)\), and show that \(\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\mu_{c^*}) < \operatorname{MSE}(\bar X_n)\).

Solution

From M3, \(\operatorname{MSE}(c) = c^2 \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + (c - 1)^2 \mu^2\). Differentiating with respect to \(c\): \(\frac{d}{dc}\operatorname{MSE}(c) = 2c \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + 2(c - 1)\mu^2 = 0\), which yields \(c\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2\right) = \mu^2\), hence \(c^* = \frac{\mu^2}{\mu^2 + \sigma^2/n}\). Since \(\sigma^2/n > 0\), \(c^* < 1\). The second derivative is \(2(\sigma^2/n + \mu^2) > 0\), confirming a global minimum. At \(c = 1\) (the sample mean), \(\operatorname{MSE}(1) = \sigma^2/n\). At \(c^*\), \(\operatorname{MSE}(c^*) = c^* \sigma^2/n < \sigma^2/n\), proving that shrinking the unbiased sample mean strictly reduces MSE.

P3 · Consistency via Chebyshev under finite variance

Let \(\hat\theta_n\) be an estimator with \(\E[\hat\theta_n] = \theta + b_n\) and \(\Var(\hat\theta_n) = v_n\). Prove using Chebyshev's inequality that if \(b_n \to 0\) and \(v_n \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\), then \(\hat\theta_n \pto \theta\).

Solution

For any \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(N\) such that for all \(n \ge N\), \(|b_n| < \epsilon/2\). For such \(n\), if \(|\hat\theta_n - \theta| \ge \epsilon\), by triangle inequality \(|\hat\theta_n - \E[\hat\theta_n]| \ge |\hat\theta_n - \theta| - |b_n| \ge \epsilon - \epsilon/2 = \epsilon/2\). Therefore, \(\Prob(|\hat\theta_n - \theta| \ge \epsilon) \le \Prob(|\hat\theta_n - \E[\hat\theta_n]| \ge \epsilon/2)\). Applying Chebyshev's inequality to \(\hat\theta_n\): \(\Prob(|\hat\theta_n - \E[\hat\theta_n]| \ge \epsilon/2) \le \frac{v_n}{(\epsilon/2)^2} = \frac{4v_n}{\epsilon^2}\). As \(n \to \infty\), \(v_n \to 0\), so the upper bound vanishes, establishing \(\lim_{n \to \infty}\Prob(|\hat\theta_n - \theta| \ge \epsilon) = 0\).

Empirical

E1 · Ratio of sample variance expectations (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints \(\E[S_n^2] / \sigma^2 = 1/2\) for \(n = 2\). Verify this ratio analytically for general \(n\) and compute the numerical ratio for \(n = 12\) (one year of monthly data).

Solution

By Proposition 2.3(d), \(\E[S_n^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\). The ratio is \(\frac{n-1}{n} = 1 - \frac{1}{n}\). For \(n = 2\), \(1 - 1/2 = 1/2 = 0.50\). For \(n = 12\), the ratio is \(\frac{11}{12} \approx 0.9167\). The naive sample variance underestimates true annual volatility by approximately 8.33% of the variance.

E2 · Three-point population variance (Lab 1)

In Lab 1, outcomes \(\{0, 1, 3\}\) have probabilities \(\{1/2, 1/3, 1/6\}\). Verify by hand the exact calculation of \(\mu = 5/6\) and \(\sigma^2 = 41/36\).

Solution

\(\mu = 0(1/2) + 1(1/3) + 3(1/6) = 1/3 + 1/2 = 5/6\). Next, \(\E[X^2] = 0^2(1/2) + 1^2(1/3) + 3^2(1/6) = 1/3 + 9/6 = 1/3 + 3/2 = 11/6\). Then \(\sigma^2 = \E[X^2] - \mu^2 = 11/6 - (5/6)^2 = 66/36 - 25/36 = 41/36\). As a decimal, \(41/36 \approx 1.138889\).

E3 · Pairwise probability sum (Lab 1)

In the enumeration of all 9 sample pairs in Lab 1, compute the probability of drawing the pair \((X_1, X_2) = (1, 3)\) and its contribution to \(\E[S_{n-1}^2]\).

Solution

By independence, \(\Prob(X_1=1, X_2=3) = p(1)p(3) = (1/3)(1/6) = 1/18\). The sample mean is \(\bar X = (1 + 3)/2 = 2\). The sample variance with \(n - 1 = 1\) is \((1 - 2)^2 + (3 - 2)^2 = 1 + 1 = 2\). The contribution to \(\E[S_{n-1}^2]\) is \(2 \times (1/18) = 2/18 = 1/9\).

Research

R1 · Shrinkage estimators under normal sampling

For i.i.d. normal observations \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), the sample variance \(S^2 = S_{n-1}^2\) satisfies \((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi_{n-1}^2\). Prove that among all estimators of the form \(c \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2\), the choice \(c = \frac{1}{n+1}\) minimizes MSE.

Solution

Let \(Q = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2 = (n-1)S^2\). Since \(Q/\sigma^2 \sim \chi_{n-1}^2\), \(\E[Q] = (n-1)\sigma^2\) and \(\Var(Q) = 2(n-1)\sigma^4\). For estimator \(\hat\sigma^2(c) = cQ\), the bias is \(\E[cQ] - \sigma^2 = (c(n-1) - 1)\sigma^2\), and the variance is \(c^2 \Var(Q) = 2c^2(n-1)\sigma^4\). Therefore, \(\operatorname{MSE}(c) = \sigma^4 \left[ 2c^2(n-1) + (c(n-1) - 1)^2 \right]\). Differentiating with respect to \(c\): \(4c(n-1) + 2(c(n-1) - 1)(n-1) = 0\). Dividing by \(2(n-1)\): \(2c + c(n-1) - 1 = 0 \implies c(n + 1) = 1 \implies c = \frac{1}{n+1}\). Thus, dividing by \(n + 1\) yields lower MSE than dividing by \(n - 1\) or \(n\).

R2 · Bias induced by Jensen's inequality for sample volatility

Even though \(S_{n-1}^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\), prove using Jensen's inequality that the sample standard deviation \(S_{n-1} = \sqrt{S_{n-1}^2}\) is strictly downward biased for \(\sigma\): \(\E[S_{n-1}] < \sigma\).

Solution

The square root function \(g(t) = \sqrt{t}\) is strictly concave on \((0, \infty)\), meaning \(g''(t) = -\frac{1}{4}t^{-3/2} < 0\). By Jensen's inequality for strictly concave functions, for any non-degenerate random variable \(Y > 0\), \(\E[g(Y)] < g(\E[Y])\). Applying this to \(Y = S_{n-1}^2\): \(\E[S_{n-1}] = \E[\sqrt{S_{n-1}^2}] < \sqrt{\E[S_{n-1}^2]} = \sqrt{\sigma^2} = \sigma\). Hence, sample volatility systematically underestimates true population volatility in finite samples.

R3 · Consistency failure under Cauchy sampling

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be i.i.d. standard Cauchy random variables with density \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}\). Explain why the sample mean \(\bar X_n\) fails to be a consistent estimator of the median.

Solution

The standard Cauchy distribution has characteristic function \(\phi_X(t) = \exp(-|t|)\). The characteristic function of the sample mean is \(\phi_{\bar X_n}(t) = [\phi_X(t/n)]^n = \exp(-|t|)\). Hence, \(\bar X_n\) has exactly the same standard Cauchy distribution as a single observation for all \(n \ge 1\). Its dispersion does not contract as \(n \to \infty\), violating the definition of consistency: \(\Prob(|\bar X_n| \ge \epsilon) = \Prob(|X_1| \ge \epsilon) > 0\), so \(\bar X_n \not\pto 0\).

    Proposition 2.2 proves that the cross-product expectation vanishes, leaving MSE equal to variance plus bias squared.

  2. For an i.i.d. sample of size \(n = 5\) from a distribution with variance \(\sigma^2 = 1.0\), what is the finite-sample bias of the divisor \(n\) variance estimator \(S_5^2\)? Enter as decimal.

    \(\operatorname{Bias}(S_n^2) = -\sigma^2/n = -1.0 / 5 = -0.20\) (Proposition 2.3).

  3. Which mathematical property defines weak consistency of an estimator \(\hat\theta_n\) for \(\theta\)?

    Options

    Definition 3.1: consistency is defined as convergence in probability of the estimator sequence to the true parameter.

  4. Can a biased estimator be consistent?

    Options

    Theorem 3.2: if bias vanishes and variance vanishes, MSE vanishes, guaranteeing consistency in probability.

  5. Let \(X_1, \dots, X_{20}\) be i.i.d. draws with variance \(\sigma^2 = 2.0\). What is the variance of the sample mean \(\bar X_{20}\)? Enter as decimal.

    \(\Var(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2 / n = 2.0 / 20 = 0.10\) (Proposition 2.3).

  6. By Jensen's inequality, what is the finite-sample bias of the sample standard deviation \(S_{n-1} = \sqrt{S_{n-1}^2}\) as an estimator of \(\sigma\)?

    Options

    Since the square root is strictly concave, \(\E[\sqrt{S^2}] < \sqrt{\E[S^2]} = \sigma\) (Research problem R2).

  7. An estimator has \(\Var(\hat\theta) = 0.04\) and \(\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta) = -0.10\). What is its mean squared error \(\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta)\)?

    \(\operatorname{MSE} = \Var + \operatorname{Bias}^2 = 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.05\) (Proposition 2.2).

  8. Why does Bessel's correction divide by \(n - 1\) instead of \(n\) in the sample variance?

    Options

    Estimating \(\mu\) with \(\bar X\) minimizes \(\sum (X_i - c)^2\), making \(\sum (X_i - \bar X)^2\) strictly smaller than \(\sum (X_i - \mu)^2\) on average by \(\sigma^2\).
