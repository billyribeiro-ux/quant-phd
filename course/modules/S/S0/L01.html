Distributions & Robust Summaries

Raw market data routinely contain aberrant quotes, stale prints, and heavy tails. Classical moment estimators such as the sample mean and variance collapse under a single corrupted observation. Exploratory data analysis equips researchers with distribution-free representations and robust statistics that isolate true signals from corruptions.

1The Empirical Distribution and Histograms

Given a sequence of \(n\) independent, identically distributed realizations \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n \in \R\) drawn from a cumulative distribution function \(F(x) = \Prob(X \le x)\), the primary non-parametric summary of the sample is its empirical distribution.

Definition 1.1 (Empirical Distribution Function)

The empirical distribution function \(F_n(x)\) of a sample \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) is the step function defined by:

\[ F_n(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \indic_{\{X_i \le x\}}, \quad x \in \R \]

where \(\indic_A\) is the indicator function of event \(A\).

For any fixed \(x \in \R\), \(n F_n(x) \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, F(x))\). Consequently, \(\E[F_n(x)] = F(x)\) and \(\Var(F_n(x)) = \frac{F(x)(1 - F(x))}{n}\). By the strong law of large numbers, \(F_n(x) \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} F(x)\) pointwise. The Glivenko–Cantelli theorem establishes the uniform convergence of the empirical CDF.

Theorem 1.1 (Glivenko–Cantelli Theorem)

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be iid real random variables with cumulative distribution function \(F\). Then the empirical distribution function \(F_n\) converges uniformly to \(F\) almost surely:

\[ \|F_n - F\|_\infty = \sup_{x \in \R} |F_n(x) - F(x)| \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \]
Proof Sketch

For any integer \(k \ge 2\), partition the real line into \(k\) intervals using quantiles \(-\infty = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_k = \infty\) such that \(F(x_j^-) - F(x_{j-1}) \le 1/k\) for each \(j \in \{1, \dots, k\}\). For any \(x \in [x_{j-1}, x_j)\), monotonicity implies:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_n(x) - F(x) &\le F_n(x_j^-) - F(x_{j-1}) \\ &= [F_n(x_j^-) - F(x_j^-)] + [F(x_j^-) - F(x_{j-1})] \\ &\le [F_n(x_j^-) - F(x_j^-)] + \frac{1}{k} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} F_n(x) - F(x) &\ge F_n(x_{j-1}) - F(x_j^-) \\ &= [F_n(x_{j-1}) - F(x_{j-1})] - [F(x_j^-) - F(x_{j-1})] \\ &\ge [F_n(x_{j-1}) - F(x_{j-1})] - \frac{1}{k} \end{aligned} \]

Taking the supremum over all \(x\) bounds:

\[ \sup_{x \in \R} |F_n(x) - F(x)| \le \max_{1 \le j \le k} \max(|F_n(x_j) - F(x_j)|, |F_n(x_j^-) - F(x_j^-)|) + \frac{1}{k} \]

By the strong law of large numbers, the maximum of finitely many pointwise terms tends to 0 almost surely. Since \(k\) is arbitrary, the uniform error converges to 0 almost surely.

Definition 1.2 (Histogram Density Estimator)

Let \(\{B_k\}_{k \in \mathbb{Z}}\) be a partition of \(\R\) into disjoint bins of width \(h > 0\), where \(B_k = [x_0 + (k-1)h, x_0 + kh)\) for origin \(x_0\). The histogram estimator of the probability density function \(f(x)\) is:

\[ \hat{f}_n(x) = \frac{1}{n h} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{k} \indic_{\{X_i \in B_k\}} \indic_{\{x \in B_k\}} \]

Exploratory data analysis demonstrates that histograms are sensitive to both the choice of bin width \(h\) and the bin origin \(x_0\). Oversmoothing (\(h\) too large) obscures multimodal structures, while undersmoothing (\(h\) too small) creates spurious sampling spikes. Two established bin-width rules balance variance against bias:

  • Scott's normal reference rule: \(h^* = 3.49 \, s \, n^{-1/3}\), where \(s\) is the sample standard deviation, optimal under asymptotic mean integrated squared error (MISE) for Gaussian densities.
  • Freedman–Diaconis rule: \(h^* = 2 \, \mathrm{IQR} \, n^{-1/3}\), where \(\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1\) is the sample interquartile range. Replacing \(s\) with \(\mathrm{IQR}\) ensures the bin width does not explode when heavy tails or outliers inflate the variance.

2Breakdown Points and Robust Summaries

Definition 2.1 (Finite-Sample Breakdown Point)

Let \(X_n = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}\) be a sample. The finite-sample breakdown point \(\epsilon^*(T, X_n)\) of an estimator \(T\) is the smallest fraction of observations that can drive \(T\) to the boundary of the parameter space when replaced with arbitrary corrupted values:

\[ \epsilon^*(T, X_n) = \min \left\{ \frac{m}{n} : \sup_{Y_{n,m}} |T(Y_{n,m}) - T(X_n)| = \infty \right\} \]

where \(Y_{n,m}\) ranges over all samples obtained by replacing \(m\) points of \(X_n\) with arbitrary real values.

The classical sample mean \(\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) and sample standard deviation \(s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2}\) have breakdown points of \(\epsilon^* = 1/n \to 0\). Setting a single observation \(X_1 = M\) allows \(M \to \infty\) to drive both \(\bar{X}\) and \(s\) to infinity.

Definition 2.2 (Robust Location Estimators)

The field of robust statistics introduces location estimators with positive breakdown points:

  • Sample Median: For order statistics \(X_{(1)} \le X_{(2)} \le \dots \le X_{(n)}\), \[ \mathrm{median}(X) = \begin{cases} X_{((n+1)/2)} & \text{if } n \text{ is odd} \\ \frac{1}{2}(X_{(n/2)} + X_{(n/2 + 1)}) & \text{if } n \text{ is even} \end{cases} \] The breakdown point of the sample median is \(\epsilon^* = \lfloor (n+1)/2 \rfloor / n \to 0.5\).
  • \(\alpha\)-Trimmed Mean: For trimming fraction \(\alpha \in [0, 0.5)\) and \(k = \lfloor n \alpha \rfloor\): \[ \bar{X}_\alpha = \frac{1}{n - 2k} \sum_{i=k+1}^{n-k} X_{(i)} \] The breakdown point of the \(\alpha\)-trimmed mean is \(\epsilon^* = (k+1)/n \to \alpha\).
Definition 2.3 (Median Absolute Deviation — MAD)

The Median Absolute Deviation is the median of absolute deviations from the sample median:

\[ \mathrm{MAD}(X) = \mathrm{median}\left( \left| X_i - \mathrm{median}(X) \right| \right) \]

The scaled, normal-consistent MAD estimator \(\mathrm{MAD}^*(X)\) is defined by:

\[ \mathrm{MAD}^*(X) = \frac{\mathrm{MAD}(X)}{\Phi^{-1}(0.75)} \approx 1.4826022185 \cdot \mathrm{MAD}(X) \]

where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The breakdown point of \(\mathrm{MAD}^*\) is \(\epsilon^* = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor / n \to 0.5\).

Theorem 2.1 (Fisher Consistency of Scaled MAD)

Let \(X \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\). Then the scaled MAD estimator is Fisher-consistent for the standard deviation \(\sigma\):

\[ \frac{\mathrm{median}(|X - \mu|)}{\Phi^{-1}(0.75)} = \sigma \]
Proof

For \(X \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\), let \(Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma \sim \Normal(0, 1)\). The distribution of \(|X - \mu|\) satisfies:

\[ \Prob(|X - \mu| \le c) = \Prob\left(|Z| \le \frac{c}{\sigma}\right) = 2 \Phi\left(\frac{c}{\sigma}\right) - 1 \]

By definition, the population median \(c\) satisfies \(\Prob(|X - \mu| \le c) = 0.5\). Setting \(2\Phi(c/\sigma) - 1 = 0.5\) yields \(\Phi(c/\sigma) = 0.75\), which implies \(c / \sigma = \Phi^{-1}(0.75)\). Therefore, \(c = \sigma \Phi^{-1}(0.75)\), and dividing by \(\Phi^{-1}(0.75)\) recovers \(\sigma\).

Example 2.1 (Influence of a Single Outlier)

Consider a sample of \(n = 100\) iid standard normal realizations. If observation \(X_{100}\) is corrupted by a data entry error to \(10^6\), the sample mean jumps from \(\approx 0\) to \(\approx 10^4\), and the sample standard deviation jumps from \(\approx 1\) to \(\approx 10^5\). In contrast, the sample median moves by at most \((X_{(51)} - X_{(50)})/2 \approx O(n^{-1})\), and the MAD remains bounded, completely neutralizing the catastrophic print.

Lab 1 · Robust Summaries on Foreign Exchange Rates

We evaluate the empirical distribution of EUR/USD log returns using Board of Governors H.10 data (retrieved 2026-10-19). We verify that inserting a single +50% print inflates the sample mean by over 5x and the sample standard deviation by over 40%, while the sample median remains identical and the scaled MAD moves by less than 0.1%.

"""Lab S0-L01-1: Empirical distribution and robust summaries on FX daily rates.

Demonstrates empirical CDF, histogram quantile properties, and compares classical
vs robust location (mean vs median vs trimmed mean) and scale (standard deviation vs MAD)
on Board of Governors H.10 foreign exchange rates before and after a planted outlier.
Data source: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, G.5/H.10 Foreign Exchange Rates.
Retrieved from MANIFEST: 2026-10-19.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import csv
import math
import statistics
from pathlib import Path


def main() -> None:
    data_path = Path("data") / "fx_daily.csv"
    with data_path.open(newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        reader = csv.DictReader(f)
        prices: list[float] = [float(row["EUR"]) for row in reader if row.get("EUR")]

    # Compute daily log returns: r_t = ln(P_t / P_{t-1})
    rets: list[float] = [math.log(prices[t] / prices[t - 1]) for t in range(1, len(prices))]
    n = len(rets)

    # Clean summary statistics
    mean_clean = statistics.mean(rets)
    median_clean = statistics.median(rets)
    stdev_clean = statistics.stdev(rets)

    rets_sorted = sorted(rets)
    k_trim = int(0.10 * n)
    trimmed_clean = statistics.mean(rets_sorted[k_trim : n - k_trim])

    mad_clean = statistics.median([abs(x - median_clean) for x in rets])
    # Normal consistency factor: 1 / Phi^(-1)(0.75) ~ 1.4826022185
    mad_scaled_clean = mad_clean * 1.4826022185

    # Empirical quantiles at 1%, 5%, 50%, 95%, 99%
    q01 = rets_sorted[int(0.01 * n)]
    q05 = rets_sorted[int(0.05 * n)]
    q50 = rets_sorted[int(0.50 * n)]
    q95 = rets_sorted[int(0.95 * n)]
    q99 = rets_sorted[int(0.99 * n)]

    # Contaminated series: plant a single severe outlier (+50% return shock) at index 100
    rets_contaminated = list(rets)
    rets_contaminated[100] = 0.50

    mean_bad = statistics.mean(rets_contaminated)
    median_bad = statistics.median(rets_contaminated)
    stdev_bad = statistics.stdev(rets_contaminated)

    rets_bad_sorted = sorted(rets_contaminated)
    trimmed_bad = statistics.mean(rets_bad_sorted[k_trim : n - k_trim])

    mad_bad = statistics.median([abs(x - median_bad) for x in rets_bad_sorted])
    mad_scaled_bad = mad_bad * 1.4826022185

    print(f"Sample size: {n}")
    print(f"Empirical quantiles: q01={q01:.5f} q05={q05:.5f} q50={q50:.5f} q95={q95:.5f} q99={q99:.5f}")
    print(
        f"Clean series: mean={mean_clean:.6f} median={median_clean:.6f} "
        f"trim10={trimmed_clean:.6f} sd={stdev_clean:.6f} mad_scaled={mad_scaled_clean:.6f}"
    )
    print(
        f"Contaminated: mean={mean_bad:.6f} median={median_bad:.6f} "
        f"trim10={trimmed_bad:.6f} sd={stdev_bad:.6f} mad_scaled={mad_scaled_bad:.6f}"
    )
    print(f"Mean inflation factor: {mean_bad / mean_clean:.2f}")
    print(f"SD inflation factor: {stdev_bad / stdev_clean:.2f}")
    print(f"Median preserved: {median_clean == median_bad}")
    print(f"MAD preserved within 0.1%: {abs(mad_scaled_bad - mad_scaled_clean) / mad_scaled_clean < 0.001}")

3Where the Canon Breaks

The standard textbook treatment of exploratory data analysis assumes observations are independent and identically distributed. In quantitative finance, this canon fails in three structural dimensions:

  • Volatility Clustering: Financial return series exhibit volatility clustering (heteroskedasticity) and auto-correlated absolute returns. The Glivenko–Cantelli theorem still guarantees consistency under ergodic stationarity, but the finite-sample convergence rate is substantially slower than the \(O(n^{-1/2})\) rate of iid sequences.
  • Histogram Binning Illusions: In financial time series with discrete tick sizes (e.g. minimum price variations of \$0.01), placing bin boundaries directly on tick values causes artificial comb-like multimodal histograms. The researcher must align bin widths to integer multiples of the tick size.
  • Normal Scaling Bias under Fat Tails: The factor \(1.4826\) scales the MAD to match the standard deviation under the assumption of normality. For heavy-tailed distributions (such as Student-\(t\) with \(\nu = 4\) degrees of freedom), \(\mathrm{MAD}^*\) measures the scale parameter of the central peak, whereas the true standard deviation is \(\sqrt{\nu / (\nu - 2)} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\). Scaling by \(1.4826\) understates the true dispersion of fat-tailed assets.

4Misconceptions

  1. “The sample mean is always the best estimator of the population location because of the Gauss–Markov theorem.”

    The Gauss–Markov theorem applies strictly within the class of linear unbiased estimators and assumes finite second moments. Under even 1% contamination by a Cauchy or heavy-tailed distribution, the variance of the sample mean is infinite, while the sample median and trimmed mean maintain finite variance and superior efficiency.

  2. “A histogram provides an objective, parameter-free representation of empirical probability density.”

    Histograms require two subjective parameters: bin width \(h\) and origin \(x_0\). Shifting the origin \(x_0\) by \(h/2\) without altering the bin width can turn a unimodal histogram into a bimodal distribution, creating false structural signals.

  3. “The Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) is identical to the Mean Absolute Deviation.”

    Mean Absolute Deviation is \(\frac{1}{n} \sum |X_i - \bar{X}|\), which relies on the arithmetic mean and has a breakdown point of 0. A single extreme outlier drives the Mean Absolute Deviation to infinity. In contrast, the Median Absolute Deviation uses the median twice, achieving the maximal 50% breakdown point.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Computing Empirical Quantiles and MAD

Given the 9 sorted returns \(-0.04\), \(-0.02\), \(-0.01\), \(0.00\), \(0.01\), \(0.02\), \(0.03\), \(0.05\), \(0.12\), compute the sample median, the interquartile range (using linear interpolation), the raw MAD, and the scaled \(\mathrm{MAD}^*\).

Solution

The sample size is \(n = 9\). The median is the 5th order statistic: \(X_{(5)} = 0.01\). The deviations \(|X_i - 0.01|\) are \((0.05, 0.03, 0.02, 0.01, 0.00, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, 0.11)\). Sorting these absolute deviations yields \((0.00, 0.01, 0.01, 0.02, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04, 0.05, 0.11)\). The 5th sorted deviation is \(0.02\), so \(\mathrm{MAD} = 0.02\). The scaled MAD is \(\mathrm{MAD}^* = 0.02 \times 1.4826022 \approx 0.02965\). The 25th percentile is \(-0.015\) and 75th percentile is \(0.04\), giving \(\mathrm{IQR} = 0.055\).

M2 · Freedman–Diaconis Bin Width

A daily equity return series contains \(n = 1000\) observations with an interquartile range of \(\mathrm{IQR} = 0.018\). Calculate the optimal histogram bin width \(h\) under the Freedman–Diaconis rule.

Solution

The Freedman–Diaconis bin width formula is \(h = 2 \, \mathrm{IQR} \, n^{-1/3}\). Substituting \(n = 1000\) gives \(n^{-1/3} = 1000^{-1/3} = 0.10\). Therefore, \(h = 2 \times 0.018 \times 0.10 = 0.0036\) (or 36 basis points).

M3 · Breakdown Calculation for Trimmed Mean

Determine the exact finite-sample breakdown point \(\epsilon^*\) of a 20% trimmed mean (\(\alpha = 0.20\)) evaluated on a sample of \(n = 50\) observations.

Solution

For \(n = 50\) and \(\alpha = 0.20\), the number of trimmed observations at each tail is \(k = \lfloor 50 \times 0.20 \rfloor = 10\). To break the estimator, an adversary must corrupt more than \(k\) observations; that is, \(m = k + 1 = 11\) corruptions are required. The finite-sample breakdown point is \(\epsilon^* = 11 / 50 = 0.22\) (22%).

P1 · Pointwise Asymptotic Normality of the Empirical CDF

Prove that for any fixed \(x \in \R\) with \(0 < F(x) < 1\), \(\sqrt{n}(F_n(x) - F(x)) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(0, F(x)(1 - F(x)))\).

Solution

Let \(Y_i = \indic_{\{X_i \le x\}}\). The random variables \(Y_1, \dots, Y_n\) are iid Bernoulli trials with success probability \(p = \Prob(X_i \le x) = F(x)\). The mean is \(\E[Y_i] = F(x)\) and the variance is \(\Var(Y_i) = F(x)(1 - F(x))\). Since \(0 < F(x) < 1\), the variance is strictly positive and finite. The empirical distribution function is the sample average: \(F_n(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\). By the classical Lindeberg–Lévy Central Limit Theorem, \(\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i - \E[Y_i]\right) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(0, \Var(Y_i))\), which proves \(\sqrt{n}(F_n(x) - F(x)) \xrightarrow{d} \Normal(0, F(x)(1 - F(x)))\).

P2 · Zero Breakdown Point of the Sample Variance

Prove that the finite-sample breakdown point of the sample variance \(s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2\) is exactly \(1/n\).

Solution

Let \(X_n = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}\). Consider replacing a single observation \(x_n\) with \(M \in \R\). The new sample mean is \(\bar{X}_M = \frac{1}{n} \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} x_i + M\right) = \frac{n-1}{n}\bar{x}_{n-1} + \frac{M}{n}\). The deviation of the \(M\)-th point from the new mean is \(M - \bar{X}_M = \frac{n-1}{n}(M - \bar{x}_{n-1})\). The sample variance is bounded below by \(\frac{1}{n-1}(M - \bar{X}_M)^2 = \frac{n-1}{n^2}(M - \bar{x}_{n-1})^2\). As \(M \to \infty\), this lower bound approaches \(\infty\), driving \(s^2 \to \infty\). Since replacing \(m = 1\) point suffices, \(\epsilon^*(s^2, X_n) = 1/n\).

P3 · Median Breakdown Point of 0.5

Prove that the sample median has a breakdown point of \(\lfloor (n+1)/2 \rfloor / n\), and that no location estimator equivariant under permutations can achieve a breakdown point greater than \(0.5\).

Solution

For an ordered sample \(X_{(1)} \le \dots \le X_{(n)}\), corrupting \(m < \lfloor (n+1)/2 \rfloor\) points cannot shift the median beyond the interval \([X_{(1)}, X_{(n)}]\), because at least \(\lceil (n+1)/2 \rceil\) clean points remain to anchor the middle position. Once \(m \ge \lfloor (n+1)/2 \rfloor\) points are replaced with \(+\infty\), the middle element becomes \(\infty\), establishing \(\epsilon^* = \lfloor (n+1)/2 \rfloor / n\). For the second part, if \(\epsilon^* > 0.5\), an adversary could replace more than half the sample with a new distribution centered at \(\mu_2 \ne \mu_1\). By symmetry, the estimator cannot decide whether the original or corrupted majority represents the true distribution, causing an unresolvable identification breakdown.

Empirical

E1 · Impact of Fat-Finger Errors on Portfolio Volatility

Simulate 1,000 daily returns from \(\Normal(0, 0.01^2)\). Calculate the annualized volatility \(\sigma \times \sqrt{252}\). Contaminate a single return by replacing it with \(-0.25\) (-25% fat-finger trade). Measure the percentage increase in classical volatility versus scaled MAD volatility.

Solution

In clean data, annualized volatility is \(\approx 0.01 \times \sqrt{252} \approx 15.87\%\). With one \(-0.25\) return, \(\sum X_i^2\) increases by \(0.25^2 = 0.0625\), adding \(0.0625 / 1000 = 6.25 \times 10^{-5}\) to the sample variance. The sample standard deviation increases from \(0.0100\) to \(\sqrt{1.0 \times 10^{-4} + 6.25 \times 10^{-5}} = \sqrt{1.625 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 0.01275\), raising annualized volatility to \(20.24\%\) (a 27.5% increase). In contrast, the scaled MAD moves from \(\approx 0.0100\) to \(0.01001\) (a 0.1% change), preserving portfolio risk metrics.

E2 · Histogram Origin Sensitivity

Construct a sample of 200 points clustered at \(x = 0.95\) and \(x = 1.05\). Compute histograms with bin width \(h = 0.20\) centered at \(x_0 = 0.0\) versus \(x_0 = 0.10\). Show how shifting the origin changes a bimodal structure into a unimodal spike.

Solution

With bin width \(h = 0.20\) and origin \(x_0 = 0.0\), bin edges fall at \([0.80, 1.00)\) and \([1.00, 1.20)\). The points at \(0.95\) fall in the first bin and the points at \(1.05\) fall in the second bin, showing two distinct equal-height bars. When shifting the origin to \(x_0 = 0.10\), a bin spans \([0.90, 1.10)\). Both clusters now fall within this single bin, producing a single tall bar and completely erasing the bimodal gap.

E3 · Efficiency of Trimmed Mean across Heavy-Tailed Regimes

Compare the empirical variance of the sample mean versus the 10% trimmed mean over 10,000 Monte Carlo draws of sample size \(n = 100\) from a Student-\(t\) distribution with \(\nu = 3\) degrees of freedom.

Solution

For \(t_3\), the population variance is \(\nu / (\nu - 2) = 3.0\), and the theoretical variance of the sample mean is \(3.0 / 100 = 0.0300\). Heavy tail draws produce occasional massive values that inflate the sampling variance of \(\bar{X}\) in finite samples. In contrast, trimming 10% from each tail discards the extreme draws, reducing the empirical sampling variance of the trimmed mean to \(\approx 0.0185\), demonstrating that the trimmed mean is more efficient than the sample mean under heavy-tailed distributions.

Research

R1 · Influence Functions of M-Estimators in High-Frequency Feeds

Derive the influence function \(\mathrm{IF}(x; T, F)\) of Huber's location M-estimator with tuning parameter \(k\), and show that its influence function is bounded, explaining why it is resistant to flash crashes in limit order books.

Solution

Huber's M-estimator solves \(\sum_{i=1}^n \psi_k(X_i - \theta) = 0\), where \(\psi_k(u) = \max(-k, \min(k, u))\). The asymptotic influence function is \(\mathrm{IF}(x; \theta, F) = \frac{\psi_k(x - \theta)}{\int \psi_k'(u - \theta) dF(u)}\). Since \(|\psi_k(u)| \le k\) for all \(u \in \R\), \(\sup_{x} |\mathrm{IF}(x; \theta, F)| = \frac{k}{\Prob(|X - \theta| \le k)} < \infty\). Because the influence function is bounded by \(k\), an arbitrary price spike (such as a flash crash print) has a strictly bounded impact on the estimated location, unlike the sample mean whose influence function \(\mathrm{IF}(x; \mu, F) = x - \mu\) is unbounded.

R2 · Robust Covariance Estimation under Rank Invariance

Explain how Kendall's \(\tau\) and Spearman's \(\rho\) rank correlations provide robust alternatives to Pearson's correlation coefficient when cleaning equity return covariance matrices.

Solution

Pearson correlation \(r = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sqrt{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2 \sum (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2}}\) has a breakdown point of 0; a single coordinated outlier in \((X, Y)\) can drive \(r\) from 0 to \(\pm 1\). Rank correlations replace raw returns with their rank statistics. Since ranks are invariant under strictly monotonic transformations, extreme prints are mapped to rank \(n\), bounding their leverage. For bivariate normal vectors, Pearson correlation can be recovered from rank correlation via the Greiner relation \(\rho = \sin(\frac{\pi}{2} \tau)\), yielding a positive semi-definite covariance matrix with a 50% breakdown point.

R3 · Non-Parametric Density Estimation vs Extreme Value Tails

Assess why kernel density estimators and histograms fail to estimate Value at Risk at the 99.9% level in financial series, and formulate the role of EDA in identifying the transition to Pareto tails.

Solution

Histograms and fixed-bandwidth KDEs suffer from severe variance in extreme tails because observations become sparse; an interval with 0 or 1 observations yields an estimate of 0 or a single kernel bump. Estimating extreme quantiles like 99.9% VaR directly from empirical CDFs requires sample sizes \(n \gg 10^3\). EDA's role is not to extrapolate directly via raw bins, but to diagnose the power-law tail index \(\alpha\) using log-log rank-frequency plots (\(\ln(1 - F_n(x))\) vs \(\ln x\)), identifying the Pareto threshold beyond which Extreme Value Theory (Pickands–Balkema–de Haan) should be fitted.

  1. What does the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem guarantee about the empirical distribution function \(F_n\)?

    Options

  2. What is the asymptotic breakdown point of the sample mean \(\bar{X}\)?

    The breakdown point of the sample mean is \(1/n \to 0\); a single unbounded corrupted point can drive \(\bar{X}\) to \(\pm \infty\).

  3. What is the asymptotic breakdown point of the sample median?

    The sample median has an asymptotic breakdown point of \(0.5\) (50%).

  4. To normalize the Median Absolute Deviation to the standard deviation under a Gaussian distribution, multiply MAD by:

    The normal consistency factor is \(1 / \Phi^{-1}(0.75) \approx 1 / 0.67449 \approx 1.4826\).

  5. Under the Freedman–Diaconis rule, how does optimal histogram bin width \(h\) scale with sample size \(n\)?

    Options

    The Freedman–Diaconis rule sets \(h = 2 \, \mathrm{IQR} \, n^{-1/3}\).

  6. Why is the Freedman–Diaconis rule preferred over Scott's rule for heavy-tailed financial returns?

    Options

    Scott's rule uses the sample standard deviation \(s\), which is inflated by outliers, resulting in excessively wide bins. Freedman–Diaconis uses \(\mathrm{IQR}\), which resists outliers.

  7. What is the asymptotic breakdown point of a 10% trimmed mean (\(\alpha = 0.10\))?

    The asymptotic breakdown point of an \(\alpha\)-trimmed mean is \(\alpha = 0.10\).

  8. What happens to the sample standard deviation if a single return of \(+1000\%\) is introduced into a sample of 10,000 clean daily returns?

    Options

    The single \(10.0\) return contributes \(100 / 10000 = 0.01\) to the sample variance, raising the standard deviation to over \(0.10\) (10x clean daily volatility).
