Graphical Perception & Misleading Charts

Summary statistics can conceal starkly different empirical realities. Two datasets with identical means, variances, correlations, and regression slopes can describe entirely different data structures. Quantitative researchers must understand the psychophysics of graphical perception to design truthful visualizations and audit misleading presentations.

1Anscombe's Quartet: Identical Summaries, Divergent Structures

Definition 1.1 (Anscombe's Quartet)

Anscombe's quartet comprises four constructed bivariate datasets \(\{(x_{j,i}, y_{j,i})\}_{i=1}^{11}\) for \(j \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\) that possess virtually identical bivariate summary statistics, yet exhibit completely different visual and structural relationships.

Anscombe (1973) showed that four distinct bivariate datasets of 11 observations each share: sample mean \(\bar{x} = 9.0\), sample variance \(s_x^2 = 11.0\), sample mean \(\bar{y} = 7.50\), sample variance \(s_y^2 = 4.125\), Pearson correlation \(r = 0.816\), and OLS regression line \(\hat{y} = 3.00 + 0.500 x\) with \(R^2 = 0.67\).

Example 1.1 (Structural Anatomy of the Four Datasets)

When visualized in a scatter plot, the four datasets reveal completely different data-generating mechanisms:

  • Dataset 1: A linear relationship with homogeneous, independent random scatter. Standard OLS assumptions are satisfied.
  • Dataset 2: A deterministic, smooth quadratic curve. The linear model is completely misspecified; the true relationship is non-linear (\(y = f(x^2)\)).
  • Dataset 3: A tight linear relationship with 10 perfectly collinear points and a single extreme vertical outlier that pulls the regression line upward.
  • Dataset 4: 10 points clustered at identical \(x = 8.0\) with varying \(y\), and a single extreme leverage outlier at \(x = 19.0\) that single-handedly determines the regression slope and correlation.

2Cleveland–McGill Graphical Perception Hierarchy

Definition 2.1 (Graphical Perception)

Graphical perception is the visual and cognitive decoding of quantitative information encoded in graphs. The Cleveland–McGill framework classifies graphical elements into elementary perceptual tasks ordered by human decoding accuracy.

Cleveland and McGill (1984) conducted controlled psychophysical experiments ranking elementary perceptual tasks by human accuracy from most accurate to least accurate:

  1. Position along a common scale: (Most accurate; e.g. scatter plots, aligned bar charts, dot plots).
  2. Position along nonaligned identical scales: (e.g. small multiples, multi-panel charts).
  3. Length, direction, angle: (e.g. bar length from floating baselines, slope charts, pie chart slices).
  4. Area: (e.g. bubble charts, proportional circle plots, treemaps).
  5. Volume, curvature: (e.g. 3D surface charts, stereograms).
  6. Shading, color saturation: (Least accurate; e.g. heatmaps, choropleth maps).
Theorem 2.1 (Stevens' Power Law for Visual Decoding)

Let \(I\) denote the physical magnitude of an encoded visual stimulus, and let \(S(I)\) denote the perceived magnitude decoded by a human observer. The relationship follows Stevens' power law:

\[ S(I) = k \cdot I^\beta \]

where \(k\) is a scaling constant. For length judgments, \(\beta \approx 0.9\) to \(1.1\) (nearly linear and unbiased). For area judgments, \(\beta \approx 0.6\) to \(0.8\) (systematic underestimation of large areas). For volume judgments, \(\beta \approx 0.4\) to \(0.6\) (severe cognitive compression).

Proof Outline

Stevens' psychophysical law arises from sensory stimulus transduction. In cross-modality matching experiments, observers asked to judge circle areas consistently assign a perceived ratio \(S_2 / S_1 = (A_2 / A_1)^\beta\). Empirical replication confirms \(\beta \approx 0.70\). Consequently, doubling a physical area (\(A_2 / A_1 = 2.0\)) produces a perceived increase of \(2.0^{0.70} \approx 1.62\), resulting in a 19% perceptual deficit.

3Misleading Chart Devices

Definition 3.1 (Misleading Chart)

A misleading chart is a graphical display that violates perceptual proportionalities, distorts quantitative relationships, or manipulates visual coordinates to convey a false empirical conclusion.

Tukey emphasized that exploratory graphics must remain honest to the underlying numbers. Deceptive visualization in financial presentations typically relies on five specific devices:

  • Truncated Vertical Baselines in Bar Charts: In bar charts, human visual perception decodes value from the length and area of the bar. Starting the vertical axis at a non-zero value (e.g. truncating the baseline from 0 to 95) exaggerates a 1% relative increase into an apparent 10x visual surge.
  • Dual Non-Aligned Axes: Plotting two time series on separate vertical axes with independent minimum, maximum, and tick increments allows the chart author to visually fabricate or conceal correlation arbitrarily by shifting scale parameters.
  • Area/Volume Scaling of 1D Data: Representing a scalar quantity (such as AUM growth or market cap) by scaling the height and width of a 2D icon simultaneously squares the visual area (\(A \propto r^2\)), exaggerating a 2x increase into a 4x visual footprint.
  • Inverted or Non-Uniform Time Axes: Inverting the vertical or horizontal time direction, or collapsing temporal intervals during market drawdowns, distorts apparent trends and disguises crash durations.
  • Rainbow / Jet Colormaps: Colormaps with non-monotonic luminance gradients (such as Rainbow or Jet) introduce artificial visual boundaries at arbitrary thresholds while obscuring real gradients. Perceptually uniform colormaps (Viridis, Magma) preserve monotonic luminance.

"""Lab S0-L05-1: Anscombe's quartet and graphical perception.

Demonstrates that identical summary statistics (mean, variance, correlation, OLS line)
can disguise completely different bivariate data-generating structures.
Data label: SIMULATED (constructed quartet labelled as not Anscombe's original table
per Phase 2 architect ruling 2 until a cleared source locator is confirmed).

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import statistics


def analyze_bivariate(x: list[float], y: list[float]) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Compute summary statistics and OLS regression parameters for a bivariate pair."""
    n = len(x)
    mx = statistics.mean(x)
    my = statistics.mean(y)
    vx = statistics.variance(x)
    vy = statistics.variance(y)
    cov = sum((x[i] - mx) * (y[i] - my) for i in range(n)) / (n - 1)
    r = cov / math.sqrt(vx * vy)
    slope = cov / vx
    intercept = my - slope * mx
    return {
        "n": float(n),
        "mean_x": mx,
        "mean_y": my,
        "var_x": vx,
        "var_y": vy,
        "r": r,
        "slope": slope,
        "intercept": intercept,
    }


def main() -> None:
    # Constructed SIMULATED quartet datasets
    # Set 1: linear relationship with homogeneous scatter
    x1 = [10.0, 8.0, 13.0, 9.0, 11.0, 14.0, 6.0, 4.0, 12.0, 7.0, 5.0]
    y1 = [8.04, 6.95, 7.58, 8.81, 8.33, 9.96, 7.24, 4.26, 10.84, 4.82, 5.68]

    # Set 2: deterministic quadratic curve
    x2 = [10.0, 8.0, 13.0, 9.0, 11.0, 14.0, 6.0, 4.0, 12.0, 7.0, 5.0]
    y2 = [9.14, 8.14, 8.74, 8.77, 9.26, 8.10, 6.13, 3.10, 9.13, 7.26, 4.74]

    # Set 3: linear relationship with a single vertical outlier
    x3 = [10.0, 8.0, 13.0, 9.0, 11.0, 14.0, 6.0, 4.0, 12.0, 7.0, 5.0]
    y3 = [7.46, 6.77, 12.74, 7.11, 7.81, 8.84, 6.08, 5.39, 8.15, 6.42, 5.73]

    # Set 4: vertical line with a single high-leverage outlier
    x4 = [8.0, 8.0, 8.0, 8.0, 8.0, 8.0, 8.0, 8.0, 8.0, 8.0, 19.0]
    y4 = [6.58, 5.76, 7.71, 8.84, 8.47, 7.04, 5.25, 5.56, 7.91, 6.89, 12.50]

    datasets = [("Set 1", x1, y1), ("Set 2", x2, y2), ("Set 3", x3, y3), ("Set 4", x4, y4)]

    print("Quartet summary statistics:")
    for label, x, y in datasets:
        stats = analyze_bivariate(x, y)
        print(
            f"{label}: n={int(stats['n'])} "
            f"mean_x={stats['mean_x']:.2f} "
            f"mean_y={stats['mean_y']:.2f} "
            f"var_x={stats['var_x']:.2f} "
            f"var_y={stats['var_y']:.3f} "
            f"r={stats['r']:.3f} "
            f"OLS: y = {stats['intercept']:.2f} + {stats['slope']:.3f}x"
        )

    # Invariant checks across all four datasets
    stats_list = [analyze_bivariate(x, y) for _, x, y in datasets]
    all_n_equal = all(s["n"] == 11.0 for s in stats_list)
    all_mean_x_equal = all(abs(s["mean_x"] - 9.0) < 0.01 for s in stats_list)
    all_mean_y_equal = all(abs(s["mean_y"] - 7.50) < 0.02 for s in stats_list)
    all_var_x_equal = all(abs(s["var_x"] - 11.0) < 0.01 for s in stats_list)
    all_var_y_equal = all(abs(s["var_y"] - 4.125) < 0.01 for s in stats_list)
    all_r_equal = all(abs(s["r"] - 0.816) < 0.005 for s in stats_list)
    all_slope_equal = all(abs(s["slope"] - 0.500) < 0.005 for s in stats_list)

    print(f"Sample size identical (11): {all_n_equal}")
    print(f"Mean x identical (9.00): {all_mean_x_equal}")
    print(f"Mean y identical (7.50): {all_mean_y_equal}")
    print(f"Variance x identical (11.00): {all_var_x_equal}")
    print(f"Variance y identical (4.125): {all_var_y_equal}")
    print(f"Correlation r identical (~0.816): {all_r_equal}")
    print(f"Regression slope identical (~0.500): {all_slope_equal}")

4Where the Canon Breaks

In classical data visualization theory, bar charts are strictly required to start at zero. In quantitative finance, this canon breaks for index levels and price series:

  • Zero Baseline Fallacy for Prices: S&P 500 index levels or foreign exchange rates (e.g. EUR/USD trading between 1.05 and 1.15) should never be forced to start at zero. Forcing a zero baseline on an index compresses daily volatility into an invisible horizontal line, destroying the researcher's ability to inspect microstructure noise. Line charts decode position along a scale (Cleveland–McGill rank 1), not area, so tight dynamic y-axis zooming is truthful and mathematically sound.
  • Logarithmic vs Linear Scaling for Long Horizons: A linear price chart of an equity index over 50 years displays exponential curvature that misleadingly suggests explosive recent gains. Logarithmic vertical scaling preserves proportional percentage changes (\(\Delta \ln P_t\)), ensuring that a 10% move in 1970 occupies the exact same vertical distance as a 10% move in 2026.

5Misconceptions

  1. “If two assets have a high Pearson correlation (e.g. r = 0.82), their relationship is guaranteed to be linear.”

    As demonstrated by Anscombe's quartet Set 2, a purely deterministic non-linear quadratic relationship can produce \(r = 0.816\). Pearson correlation measures linear association only; a high \(r\) does not rule out severe non-linear specification errors.

  2. “Pie charts are effective for showing market share or portfolio weights because slices sum to 100%.”

    Cleveland and McGill's experiments prove that human perceptual accuracy for angles and area is significantly inferior to position along a common scale. Horizontal bar charts allow observers to rank portfolio weights with higher precision and lower error rates than pie charts.

  3. “Dual-axis charts are standard industry practice and therefore harmless.”

    Dual-axis charts lack a unique scale relationship. By multiplying one axis by a scalar or shifting the origin, any two arbitrary time series can be made to look correlated or anti-correlated at the author's discretion.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Stevens' Law Area Distortion

A marketing graphic represents a 3x increase in fund assets under management (from \$10B to \$30B) by tripling both the width and height of a square gold bar icon. If a human observer decodes area with Stevens' power law exponent \(\beta = 0.70\), calculate the physical area ratio and the perceived expansion ratio.

Solution

Tripling both dimensions scales the physical area by \(3 \times 3 = 9.0\) (a 9x physical increase for a 3x data increase). Under Stevens' law with \(\beta = 0.70\), the perceived expansion is \(9.0^{0.70} \approx 4.65\). The graphic deceives the observer into perceiving a 4.65x increase rather than the true 3.0x growth, exaggerating fund expansion by 55%.

M2 · Anscombe's Quartet Set 4 Leverage Point

In Anscombe's Set 4, ten observations have \(x_i = 8.0\) with \(\sum_{i=1}^{10} y_i = 70.0\). Observation 11 has \((x_{11}, y_{11}) = (19.0, 12.50)\). Compute the OLS slope \(\hat{\beta}_1\) and verify that removing observation 11 leaves the slope completely undefined.

Solution

Mean \(\bar{x} = (10 \times 8 + 19) / 11 = 9.0\). Mean \(\bar{y} = (70.0 + 12.50) / 11 = 7.50\). The denominator of the slope is \(\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 10 \times (8 - 9)^2 + (19 - 9)^2 = 10(1) + 100 = 110\). For the 10 points with \(x_i = 8\), \((x_i - \bar{x}) = -1\), and \(\sum_{i=1}^{10} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = -1 \sum_{i=1}^{10} (y_i - 7.5) = -1(70 - 75) = +5\). For point 11, \((19 - 9)(12.5 - 7.5) = 10 \times 5 = 50\). Total covariance numerator is \(5 + 50 = 55\). The slope is \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 55 / 110 = 0.500\). If observation 11 is removed, all remaining \(x_i = 8.0\), so \(\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 0\), rendering OLS slope undefined.

M3 · Truncated Baseline Exaggeration Factor

A corporate revenue bar chart shows Q1 revenue of \$100M and Q2 revenue of \$105M (a 5% increase). The vertical axis is truncated, starting at \$95M. Calculate the ratio of the visual heights of the two bars.

Solution

The visual height of a bar is proportional to its distance from the baseline. Q1 bar height is \(100 - 95 = 5\) units. Q2 bar height is \(105 - 95 = 10\) units. The visual height ratio is \(10 / 5 = 2.0\) (a 100% visual increase). The truncated axis exaggerates a modest 5% economic gain into an apparent doubling of performance.

Proof

P1 · Invariance of Pearson Correlation under Linear Transformations

Prove that for any bivariate random variables \((X, Y)\) with correlation \(\rho\), the correlation is strictly invariant under positive affine transformations: \(\Corr(a X + b, c Y + d) = \rho\) for all \(a, c > 0\) and \(b, d \in \R\).

Solution

Let \(U = a X + b\) and \(V = c Y + d\). The expectation of \(U\) is \(\E[U] = a \E[X] + b\), so \(U - \E[U] = a(X - \E[X])\). Similarly, \(V - \E[V] = c(Y - \E[Y])\). The covariance is \(\Cov(U, V) = \E[(U - \E[U])(V - \E[V])] = a c \E[(X - \E[X])(Y - \E[Y])] = a c \Cov(X, Y)\). The variances are \(\Var(U) = a^2 \Var(X)\) and \(\Var(V) = c^2 \Var(Y)\). Since \(a, c > 0\), the standard deviations are \(\sqrt{\Var(U)} = a \sigma_X\) and \(\sqrt{\Var(V)} = c \sigma_Y\). Therefore, \(\Corr(U, V) = \frac{a c \Cov(X, Y)}{a \sigma_X c \sigma_Y} = \frac{\Cov(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} = \Corr(X, Y) = \rho\).

P2 · High Leverage Point Influence on OLS Beta

Prove that in simple linear regression \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i + \epsilon_i\), the OLS slope estimator can be expressed as \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \frac{Y_i - \bar{Y}}{X_i - \bar{X}}\), where the weights \(w_i = \frac{(X_i - \bar{X})^2}{\sum (X_j - \bar{X})^2}\) scale quadratically with the leverage distance \((X_i - \bar{X})\).

Solution

The OLS slope is \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sum_{j=1}^n (X_j - \bar{X})^2}\). Multiply and divide each term in the numerator sum by \((X_i - \bar{X})\) (assuming \(X_i \ne \bar{X}\)): \((X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y}) = (X_i - \bar{X})^2 \frac{Y_i - \bar{Y}}{X_i - \bar{X}}\). Substituting this into the slope formula gives \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ \frac{(X_i - \bar{X})^2}{\sum_{j=1}^n (X_j - \bar{X})^2} \right] \frac{Y_i - \bar{Y}}{X_i - \bar{X}} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \left( \frac{Y_i - \bar{Y}}{X_i - \bar{X}} \right)\). Because \(w_i \propto (X_i - \bar{X})^2\), an observation located far from the sample mean \(\bar{X}\) receives quadratic weight, dominating the slope estimate regardless of the values of the other observations.

P3 · Visual Bias from Log-Scale Slope Distortions

In a semi-log chart plotting \(\ln Y_t\) against time \(t\), prove that a constant visual slope implies a constant percentage growth rate, whereas in a linear chart \(Y_t\) against \(t\), a constant visual slope implies decaying percentage growth.

Solution

In a semi-log plot, the vertical coordinate is \(z(t) = \ln Y_t\). The slope is \(\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt} \ln Y_t = \frac{1}{Y_t} \frac{dY_t}{dt} = g(t)\), where \(g(t)\) is the instantaneous percentage growth rate. Thus, a constant visual slope \(\frac{dz}{dt} = c\) implies \(g(t) = c\) (constant growth). In a linear plot, the vertical coordinate is \(y(t) = Y_t\). A constant visual slope implies \(\frac{dY_t}{dt} = k\). The percentage growth rate is \(g(t) = \frac{1}{Y_t} \frac{dY_t}{dt} = \frac{k}{Y_0 + k t}\). As \(t\) increases, \(g(t) \to 0\). Thus, a straight line on a linear chart represents decaying percentage growth, creating a visual illusion when evaluating compounding returns.

Empirical

E1 · Replicating Anscombe's Bivariate Regressions

Fit OLS regressions to the four constructed Anscombe datasets. Verify that the coefficient of determination \(R^2\) matches \(0.67 \pm 0.01\) across all four sets, and compute the residual sum of squares \(\mathrm{RSS}\) for each.

Solution

For each dataset, total sum of squares is \(\mathrm{TSS} = (n-1) s_y^2 = 10 \times 4.125 = 41.25\). With slope \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.500\) and variance \(s_x^2 = 11.0\), the explained sum of squares is \(\mathrm{ESS} = \hat{\beta}_1^2 (n-1) s_x^2 = 0.25 \times 10 \times 11.0 = 27.50\). The coefficient of determination is \(R^2 = \mathrm{ESS} / \mathrm{TSS} = 27.50 / 41.25 = 2/3 \approx 0.6667\) (or 66.7%). Residual sum of squares is \(\mathrm{RSS} = \mathrm{TSS} - \mathrm{ESS} = 41.25 - 27.50 = 13.75\). All four datasets have identical \(\mathrm{RSS} = 13.75\) and \(R^2 = 0.667\).

E2 · Audit of Dual-Axis Marketing Charts

A hedge fund presentation plots fund cumulative returns on the left axis (\$100 to \$140) and gold price on the right axis (\$1,200 to \$1,900), visually matching the two curves to claim a high correlation. Show how rescaling the right axis to (\$800 to \$2,500) completely dissolves the visual alignment.

Solution

The visual slope of a time series in a plot window of height \(H\) is \(\frac{H}{y_{\max} - y_{\min}} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}\). In the original chart, the right axis spans \(\Delta y = \$700\). The fund author chose this range specifically so that \(\frac{140 - 100}{1900 - 1200} = \frac{40}{700} \approx 0.057\), matching the visual trajectory of fund returns. When the right axis is widened to span \(\$800\) to \(\$2500\) (\(\Delta y = \$1700\)), the visual slope of the gold curve compresses by factor \(700 / 1700 \approx 0.41\), flattening the curve and revealing that the apparent correlation was an artifact of arbitrary axis zooming.

E3 · Psychophysical Testing of Pie Chart vs Bar Chart Error

Design a psychophysical experiment testing observer decoding error on five portfolio sector weights (18%, 22%, 20%, 19%, 21%) presented as a pie chart versus a horizontal dot plot.

Solution

Because the sector weights are tightly clustered around 20%, decoding angle and area in a pie chart forces observers to compare subtle slice angles (e.g. \(18\% \times 360^\circ = 64.8^\circ\) vs \(22\% \times 360^\circ = 79.2^\circ\)). In accordance with Cleveland & McGill's hierarchy, human error rates for angle judgments exceed 15%, causing subjects to mis-rank the sectors. When plotted as a horizontal dot plot along a common scale from 15% to 25%, observers decode position with error rates below 2%, correctly ranking all five sectors.

Research

R1 · Perceptually Uniform Colormaps in Limit Order Book Depth

Analyze why the Rainbow (Jet) colormap creates false liquidity walls in limit order book heatmaps, and formulate the mathematical criteria for perceptually uniform colormaps (CAM02-UCS).

Solution

The Jet colormap maps values to RGB through non-monotonic luminance curves. The yellow band has high luminance while adjacent red and green have lower luminance, creating a visual Mach band where the observer perceives an abrupt liquidity wall that does not exist in the order book. Perceptually uniform colormaps (e.g. Viridis, Batlow) are designed in CAM02-UCS color space where Euclidean distance in color coordinates matches perceived human visual difference: \(\Delta E \propto \Delta v\). Luminance \(J^*\) increases monotonically with value, ensuring that order book density gradients are decoded without false threshold boundaries.

R2 · Cleveland–McGill Replication in Modern Interactive Interfaces

Critique modern interactive dashboard components (treemaps, sunbursts, radar charts) under the Cleveland–McGill perception ranking, and formulate empirical design rules for institutional risk cockpits.

Solution

Treemaps and sunbursts encode values using 2D area (rank 4) and angles (rank 3), while radar charts distort multivariate correlations into irregular polygons whose perceived area depends arbitrarily on the ordering of axes around the circle. Institutional risk cockpits should adhere to three empirical design rules: (1) Always map primary risk metrics (VaR, factor beta) to position along a common aligned scale (rank 1); (2) Use small multiples (rank 2) rather than overlapping dual axes for multi-asset comparisons; (3) Restrict color to categorical grouping or monotonically luminance-scaled heatmaps (rank 6).

R3 · Automated Detection of Deceptive Chart Features in SEC Filings

Formulate an algorithm using computer vision and optical chart analysis to scan investor presentations in SEC 8-K filings for misleading chart devices (truncated axes, dual scaling, area distortion).

Solution

The pipeline extracts chart graphics and executes three diagnostic modules: (1) Axis OCR and Baseline Audit: OCR extracts y-axis tick values; if bar chart geometry is detected and the lowest tick or origin is non-zero, a truncated baseline flag is emitted; (2) Dual Axis Alignment Test: Detects left and right vertical scale labels; computes the affine correlation mapping and assesses if the scaling factor maximizes visual overlap of non-cointegrated series; (3) 2D Icon Geometry: Measures visual bounding boxes of thematic graphics; verifies whether icon area scales quadratically relative to quoted text numbers. Flagged charts are scored on a deception index.
