Summary moments hide tail behavior. A portfolio with normal-looking mean and variance can harbor catastrophic tail risk. Quantile diagnostics reveal non-normality, skewness, and heavy tails directly, while robust outlier rules identify data corruptions without succumbing to masking or swamping.

1Quantile–Quantile Diagnostics

Definition 1.1 (Quantile–Quantile Plot)

Let \(X_{(1)} \le X_{(2)} \le \dots \le X_{(n)}\) denote the order statistics of a sample \(X_1, \dots, X_n\), and let \(F_0\) be a continuous theoretical reference distribution with quantile function \(Q_0(p) = F_0^{-1}(p)\). A quantile–quantile plot (Q-Q plot) is the scatter plot of points:

\[ \left( Q_0(p_i), X_{(i)} \right), \quad i = 1, \dots, n \]

where \(p_i \in (0, 1)\) are plotting positions given by Blom's formula \(p_i = \frac{i - 3/8}{n + 1/4}\) or the standard midpoint \(p_i = \frac{i - 0.5}{n}\).

Theorem 1.1 (Location-Scale Linearity of Q-Q Plots)

Suppose the true data-generating distribution \(F\) belongs to a location-scale family generated by reference distribution \(F_0\), such that \(F(x) = F_0\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)\) for location \(\mu \in \R\) and scale \(\sigma > 0\). Then the population quantiles satisfy:

\[ Q_F(p) = \mu + \sigma Q_0(p), \quad \forall p \in (0, 1) \]

Consequently, the points \((Q_0(p_i), X_{(i)})\) in a Q-Q plot scatter around a straight line with intercept \(\mu\) and slope \(\sigma\).

Proof

By definition of the quantile function, \(Q_F(p) = \inf\{x : F(x) \ge p\}\). Substituting \(F(x) = F_0\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)\):

\[ F_0\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right) \ge p \iff \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \ge F_0^{-1}(p) = Q_0(p) \iff x \ge \mu + \sigma Q_0(p) \]

Taking the infimum yields \(Q_F(p) = \mu + \sigma Q_0(p)\). Since the sample order statistics satisfy \(X_{(i)} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} Q_F(p_i)\) as \(n \to \infty\), the plot \((Q_0(p_i), X_{(i)})\) converges almost surely to the line \(y = \mu + \sigma x\).

Departures from linearity in a normal Q-Q plot (\(F_0 = \Phi\)) diagnose specific departures from normality:

  • Heavy Tails (Leptokurtosis): An S-shaped curve where sample quantiles are lower than the reference line in the left tail (\(X_{(i)} < \mu + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(p_i)\)) and higher than the reference line in the right tail (\(X_{(i)} > \mu + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(p_i)\)). Typical of financial asset returns.
  • Light Tails (Platykurtosis): An inverted S-curve where extreme sample quantiles are pulled toward the median relative to normal quantiles (e.g. uniform distributions).
  • Positive Skewness (Right-Skewed): A convex, upward-curving pattern where both tails sit above the reference line.
  • Negative Skewness (Left-Skewed): A concave, downward-curving pattern where both tails sit below the reference line.

2Outlier Detection: Masking and Swamping

Definition 2.1 (Outlier Detection)

Outlier detection is the systematic identification of observations that deviate so markedly from the remainder of the sample as to indicate a different data-generating mechanism, recording failure, or extreme structural shock.

A naive heuristic flags observations exceeding 3 standard deviations from the sample mean: \(|X_i - \bar{X}| > 3 s\). In multi-outlier settings, this classical rule suffers from two mathematical breakdown phenomena:

  • Masking: When a cluster of severe outliers is present, their values pull the sample mean \(\bar{X}\) toward themselves and inflate the sample standard deviation \(s\). Consequently, the standardized residuals \(\frac{|X_i - \bar{X}|}{s}\) shrink below the threshold \(3.0\), preventing the test from flagging any of the true outliers.
  • Swamping: When an extreme outlier inflates the sample variance, clean observations located in the shoulder of the distribution can be incorrectly flagged as outliers because the shifted mean increases their residual.
Definition 2.2 (Hampel's Identifier / Robust 3-MAD Rule)

Hampel's identifier uses the sample median and the scaled Median Absolute Deviation \(\mathrm{MAD}^*(X) = 1.4826 \cdot \mathrm{median}(|X_i - \mathrm{median}(X)|)\). Observation \(X_i\) is flagged as an outlier if:

\[ \frac{|X_i - \mathrm{median}(X)|}{\mathrm{MAD}^*(X)} > k \]

where \(k\) is a user-specified threshold (commonly \(k = 3.0\) for 99.7% Gaussian equivalence, or \(k = 5.0\) for conservative filtering).

Tukey proposed outlier fences based on order statistics. For first quartile \(Q_1\) and third quartile \(Q_3\), with \(\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1\), observations outside \([Q_1 - 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR}, Q_3 + 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR}]\) are classified as outliers, and points outside \([Q_1 - 3.0 \, \mathrm{IQR}, Q_3 + 3.0 \, \mathrm{IQR}]\) are classified as extreme outliers.

Lab 1 · Heavy Tails and Masking in Treasury Yields

We analyze daily changes in 10-year Treasury yields from Board of Governors H.15 data (retrieved 2026-10-20). We test whether real yield changes exhibit heavier tails than the normal reference at the 0.1% and 99.9% quantile levels. In a local window of N=100 observations, we plant 5 clustered outliers (+25 bps) and verify that classical 3-sigma fails to detect any outliers due to masking (0/5 detected), whereas robust 3-MAD detects all 5/5.

"""Lab S0-L02-1: Quantile diagnostics (Q-Q plot) and robust outlier detection on Treasury yields.

Demonstrates heavy-tailed quantile diagnostics against the standard normal reference,
and evaluates classical 3-sigma vs robust Hampel MAD outlier detection under masking.
Data source: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, H.15 Selected Interest Rates.
Retrieved from MANIFEST: 2026-10-20.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import csv
import statistics
from pathlib import Path
from statistics import NormalDist


def main() -> None:
    data_path = Path("data") / "ust_daily.csv"
    with data_path.open(newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        reader = csv.DictReader(f)
        yields: list[float] = [float(row["cmt_10y"]) for row in reader if row.get("cmt_10y")]

    # Daily changes in 10-year Treasury yield (percentage points)
    dy: list[float] = [yields[t] - yields[t - 1] for t in range(1, len(yields))]
    n = len(dy)

    phi = NormalDist()
    dy_sorted = sorted(dy)

    med = statistics.median(dy)
    stdev = statistics.stdev(dy)
    mad = statistics.median([abs(x - med) for x in dy])
    mad_scaled = mad * 1.4826022185

    # Empirical vs theoretical normal quantiles at 0.1%, 1%, 99%, 99.9%
    p_levels = (0.001, 0.01, 0.99, 0.999)
    emp_quantiles = [dy_sorted[int(p * n)] for p in p_levels]
    norm_quantiles = [med + phi.inv_cdf(p) * mad_scaled for p in p_levels]

    # Clean full-sample outlier counts: classical 3*sigma vs robust 3*MAD*
    clean_classical_outliers = [x for x in dy if abs(x - med) > 3.0 * stdev]
    clean_robust_outliers = [x for x in dy if abs(x - med) > 3.0 * mad_scaled]

    # Masking experiment on a local window of N=100 daily yield changes
    sub = dy[:100]
    sub_contaminated = list(sub)
    planted_indices = list(range(10, 15))
    planted_val = 0.25  # +25 bps jump
    for idx in planted_indices:
        sub_contaminated[idx] = planted_val

    mean_contam = statistics.mean(sub_contaminated)
    stdev_contam = statistics.stdev(sub_contaminated)
    med_contam = statistics.median(sub_contaminated)
    mad_contam = statistics.median([abs(x - med_contam) for x in sub_contaminated])
    mad_scaled_contam = mad_contam * 1.4826022185

    classical_detected = sum(
        1 for idx in planted_indices if abs(sub_contaminated[idx] - mean_contam) > 3.0 * stdev_contam
    )
    robust_detected = sum(
        1 for idx in planted_indices if abs(sub_contaminated[idx] - med_contam) > 3.0 * mad_scaled_contam
    )

    is_heavy = abs(emp_quantiles[0]) > abs(norm_quantiles[0]) and emp_quantiles[3] > norm_quantiles[3]

    print(f"Sample size: {n}")
    print(f"Location and scale: median={med:.4f} stdev={stdev:.4f} mad_scaled={mad_scaled:.4f}")
    print(f"Empirical 0.1% vs Normal 0.1%: {emp_quantiles[0]:.3f} vs {norm_quantiles[0]:.3f}")
    print(f"Empirical 99.9% vs Normal 99.9%: {emp_quantiles[3]:.3f} vs {norm_quantiles[3]:.3f}")
    print(f"Heavy tail confirmed: {is_heavy}")
    print(f"Clean outlier flags: classical={len(clean_classical_outliers)} robust={len(clean_robust_outliers)}")
    print(f"Masking experiment (N=100): planted {len(planted_indices)} clustered outliers")
    print(f"Classical 3-sigma detected: {classical_detected}/{len(planted_indices)}")
    print(f"Robust 3-MAD detected: {robust_detected}/{len(planted_indices)}")


main()

Empirical 0.1% vs Normal 0.1%: -0.240 vs -0.137
Empirical 99.9% vs Normal 99.9%: 0.240 vs 0.137
Heavy tail confirmed: True

The experiment confirms empirical fat tails in real Treasury yields (-24 bps empirical 0.1% quantile vs -13.7 bps normal reference). Furthermore, it proves that classical 3-sigma rules suffer from complete masking breakdown under clustered anomalies, while robust MAD filters identify every anomaly.

3Where the Canon Breaks

In standard data science workflows, outliers are viewed as noise to be scrubbed or imputed. In quantitative finance, this paradigm is dangerous:

  • Outliers as Economic Signal: Asset returns on October 19, 1987 (-20.5% S&P 500) or March 16, 2020 (-12.0%) are not data entry errors; they represent genuine market jumps. Discarding them eliminates the extreme risks that options, portfolio insurance, and risk models must price.
  • Zero MAD in Illiquid Markets: If an illiquid instrument experiences no trading across more than 50% of consecutive observations, its sample median return is \(0.0\) and more than half its absolute deviations are \(0.0\). Consequently, \(\mathrm{MAD} = 0.0\). Any subsequent nonzero print then results in a division-by-zero failure. An automated profiler must detect zero scale explicitly rather than guessing an arbitrary variance.

4Misconceptions

  1. “If a data point lies beyond 3 standard deviations from the mean, it is guaranteed to be flagged by a 3-sigma outlier rule.”

    If multiple outliers are present, they inflate the standard deviation \(s\) and pull \(\bar{X}\), so that none of the corrupted points lie beyond 3 inflated standard deviations from the corrupted mean. This is the masking phenomenon.

  2. “A straight Q-Q plot proves that the data follow a standard normal \(\Normal(0, 1)\) distribution.”

    A straight Q-Q plot against standard normal quantiles proves only that the data follow some normal distribution \(\Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\). The intercept equals \(\mu\) and the slope equals \(\sigma\). Only if the intercept is 0 and the slope is 1 does the data follow the standard normal distribution.

  3. “Tukey's IQR fences assume the data are normally distributed.”

    Tukey's fences (\([Q_1 - 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR}, Q_3 + 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR}]\)) are entirely non-parametric; they depend solely on sample quartiles and make no distributional assumptions. Under an exact normal distribution, the 1.5 IQR fence encompasses approximately 99.3% of the distribution (\(\pm 2.70 \sigma\)).

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Plotting Positions for Q-Q Diagnostics

Compute Blom's plotting positions \(p_i = \frac{i - 3/8}{n + 1/4}\) and standard normal theoretical quantiles \(\Phi^{-1}(p_i)\) for a sample of size \(n = 5\) at \(i = 1\) and \(i = 5\).

Solution

For \(n = 5\), the denominator is \(n + 1/4 = 5.25\). For \(i = 1\), the numerator is \(1 - 3/8 = 0.625\), giving \(p_1 = 0.625 / 5.25 = 5/42 \approx 0.11905\). The theoretical quantile is \(\Phi^{-1}(0.11905) \approx -1.1798\). By symmetry, for \(i = 5\), \(p_5 = 1 - p_1 = 37/42 \approx 0.88095\), and \(\Phi^{-1}(p_5) = -\Phi^{-1}(p_1) \approx +1.1798\).

M2 · Hampel Identifier on Contaminated Yields

A sample of yield changes has median \(m = 0.00\) and raw \(\mathrm{MAD} = 0.04\). Determine the cutoff threshold for an outlier under Hampel's identifier with tuning parameter \(k = 3.0\).

Solution

The scaled MAD is \(\mathrm{MAD}^* = 1.4826022 \times 0.04 = 0.059304\). The cutoff threshold under \(k = 3.0\) is \(k \times \mathrm{MAD}^* = 3.0 \times 0.059304 \approx 0.1779\) (or 17.79 basis points). Any yield change with \(|X_i| > 0.1779\) is flagged as an outlier.

M3 · Tukey Fences on Equity Returns

A return distribution has first quartile \(Q_1 = -0.012\) and third quartile \(Q_3 = 0.016\). Compute the inner and outer Tukey outlier fences.

Solution

The interquartile range is \(\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1 = 0.016 - (-0.012) = 0.028\). The inner fences are \(Q_1 - 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR} = -0.012 - 1.5(0.028) = -0.054\) and \(Q_3 + 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR} = 0.016 + 1.5(0.028) = 0.058\). The outer fences are \(Q_1 - 3.0 \, \mathrm{IQR} = -0.012 - 3.0(0.028) = -0.096\) and \(Q_3 + 3.0 \, \mathrm{IQR} = 0.016 + 3.0(0.028) = 0.100\).

Proof

P1 · Theoretical Coverage of Tukey Fences under Normality

Prove that for a standard normal distribution \(X \sim \Normal(0, 1)\), the probability of observing an outlier beyond Tukey's inner fences \([Q_1 - 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR}, Q_3 + 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR}]\) is approximately \(0.007\).

Solution

For \(\Normal(0, 1)\), \(Q_3 = \Phi^{-1}(0.75) \approx 0.67449\) and \(Q_1 = -\Phi^{-1}(0.75) \approx -0.67449\). The interquartile range is \(\mathrm{IQR} = 2 \times 0.67449 = 1.34898\). The upper fence is \(Q_3 + 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR} = 0.67449 + 1.5(1.34898) = 2.69796 \approx 2.70\). By symmetry, the lower fence is \(-2.69796\). The probability of falling outside these fences is \(2(1 - \Phi(2.69796)) = 2(1 - 0.99651) = 2 \times 0.00349 = 0.00698 \approx 0.007\) (or 0.70%).

P2 · Swamping Upper Bound

Prove that in any sample of size \(n\), the standardized residual of any observation cannot exceed \(\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{n}}\), and deduce why a single massive outlier forces all other observations to have residuals within \((-1/\sqrt{n}, 0)\).

Solution

By Cauchy–Schwarz, for any sample \(X_1, \dots, X_n\), \((X_i - \bar{X})^2 \le \frac{n-1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n (X_j - \bar{X})^2 = \frac{(n-1)^2}{n} s^2\). Taking the square root gives \(\frac{|X_i - \bar{X}|}{s} \le \frac{n-1}{\sqrt{n}}\). If observation \(X_n = M \to \infty\) while \(X_1, \dots, X_{n-1}\) remain fixed, \(\bar{X} \approx M/n\) and \(s \approx \frac{M}{\sqrt{n}}\). For each fixed \(i < n\), \(X_i - \bar{X} \approx -M/n\), so \(\frac{X_i - \bar{X}}{s} \approx \frac{-M/n}{M/\sqrt{n}} = -1/\sqrt{n}\). Thus, clean observations are swamped and clustered tightly around \(-1/\sqrt{n}\).

P3 · Q-Q Plot Curvature under Monotone Non-Linearity

Prove that if \(X = g(Z)\) where \(Z \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) and \(g\) is strictly increasing and strictly convex, the normal Q-Q plot of \(X\) is strictly convex.

Solution

The cumulative distribution function of \(X\) satisfies \(F_X(x) = \Prob(g(Z) \le x) = \Phi(g^{-1}(x))\). The quantile function of \(X\) is \(Q_X(p) = g(\Phi^{-1}(p))\). In a normal Q-Q plot, the horizontal coordinate is \(z = \Phi^{-1}(p)\) and the vertical coordinate is \(x = Q_X(p) = g(z)\). The curve plotted is precisely the function \(x = g(z)\). Since \(g\) is strictly convex by hypothesis, \(g''(z) > 0\) for all \(z\), which proves that the Q-Q curve is strictly convex (bending upwards in both tails, diagnosing right-skewness).

Empirical

E1 · Numerical Simulation of Masking Breakdown

Generate \(n = 50\) observations from \(\Normal(0, 1)\). Determine the minimum number \(m\) of clustered outliers at \(x = 5.0\) required to cause classical 3-sigma detection to flag 0 outliers.

Solution

With \(n = 50\) and \(m\) outliers at \(x = 5.0\), the contaminated mean is \(\bar{X} \approx 5m/50 = 0.1m\). The sum of squared deviations from the outliers is \(m(5 - 0.1m)^2\). For \(m = 4\), \(\bar{X} = 0.40\) and \(s \approx 1.54\), giving residual \(|5 - 0.40| / 1.54 = 2.98 < 3.0\). Thus, at \(m = 4\) (only 8% contamination), classical 3-sigma detection suffers complete masking breakdown, detecting 0 of the 4 outliers.

E2 · Diagnosing Student-t Tail Thickness

Simulate 2,000 draws from a Student-\(t\) distribution with \(\nu = 4\) degrees of freedom. Fit a normal Q-Q plot and measure the slope in the interquartile range versus the slope in the 1st–99th percentile range.

Solution

For \(t_4\), the theoretical variance is \(\nu / (\nu - 2) = 2.0\) (standard deviation \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\)), while \(\mathrm{IQR} \approx 1.48\). In the central region, the slope of the Q-Q plot matches \(\mathrm{MAD}^* \approx 1.09\). In the outer tails (\(p = 0.01\) to \(0.99\)), the empirical quantiles reach \(\pm 3.75\), whereas normal quantiles reach \(\pm 2.33 \times 1.09 \approx \pm 2.54\). The effective slope in the tails is \(3.75 / 2.33 \approx 1.61\), an increase of 48% over the central slope, demonstrating the diagnostic S-curve of fat tails.

E3 · Outlier Filtering on Overnight FX Spikes

In high-frequency foreign exchange quotes, brief network disconnections generate stale repeated bid-ask prices followed by jump prints. Formulate an algorithm that combines stale print filtering with Hampel's identifier to clean a 10,000-tick series.

Solution

The pipeline operates in two passes: First, identify and flag all consecutive quote sequences where bid and ask remain identical for \(\ge 5\) ticks, marking them as stale feeds. Second, compute tick-by-tick log returns on non-stale prints. Compute the rolling median and rolling MAD over a 500-tick window. Flag any return where \(|r_t - \mathrm{median}| > 5 \cdot \mathrm{MAD}^*\) as an aberrant jump print. This two-stage filter avoids contaminating the MAD estimate with stale zero returns while catching flash crashes.

Research

R1 · Multivariate Outliers and Minimum Covariance Determinant

Explain why univariate MAD filtering fails to detect multivariate outliers in cross-sectional equity returns, and formulate Rousseeuw's Minimum Covariance Determinant (MCD) estimator.

Solution

A multivariate outlier can possess unremarkable marginal values while violating the joint correlation structure (e.g., a high-beta technology stock rising +3% on a day the tech index falls -5%). Univariate MAD flags neither coordinate. Rousseeuw's MCD searches for the subset \(H\) of \(h\) observations (\(n/2 < h < n\)) whose sample covariance matrix has the minimum determinant \(\det(\Sigma_H)\). The robust location is the mean of \(H\), and the robust dispersion is \(\Sigma_H\). Mahalanobis distances computed with MCD covariance resist masking in up to 50% corrupted vectors.

R2 · Generalized Pareto Tails vs Normal Quantile Extrapolation

Critique the standard practice of fitting a Gaussian distribution to historical returns and extrapolating to 99.9% VaR. Show how Q-Q plots against a Generalized Pareto distribution reveal structural undercapitalization.

Solution

Gaussian extrapolation asserts that log-tail probabilities decay as \(e^{-x^2 / 2\sigma^2}\), whereas empirical financial returns exhibit power-law decay \(x^{-\alpha}\) with tail index \(\alpha \in [3, 5]\). At the 99.9% level (a 1-in-1,000 day event), a normal model predicts a loss of \(3.09 \sigma\), whereas financial returns regularly experience losses of \(6\sigma\) to \(10\sigma\). A Pareto Q-Q plot (plotting \(\ln(X_{(n-i+1)} / u)\) against exponential quantiles) displays a linear relationship above threshold \(u\), proving that capital reserves calibrated to Gaussian quantiles fail by an order of magnitude during crises.

R3 · Masking in High-Dimensional Factor Models

In a factor zoo of \(p = 200\) factors evaluated across \(n = 60\) monthly periods, prove that classical Mahalanobis distance cannot be computed, and assess how robust shrinkage estimators overcome singularity.

Solution

When \(p > n\), the sample covariance matrix \(S = \frac{1}{n-1} X^T X\) has rank at most \(n-1\), rendering it singular with zero determinant; classical Mahalanobis distance \(d_i^2 = (x_i - \bar{x})^T S^{-1} (x_i - \bar{x})\) is undefined. Robust shrinkage estimators (such as Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage applied to MCD or pairwise rank correlations) shrink the sample dispersion toward a well-conditioned target \(F = \frac{\mathrm{Tr}(S)}{p} I\): \(S^* = (1 - \lambda) S_{\text{MCD}} + \lambda F\). This guarantees invertibility while maintaining resistance against multi-factor outliers.

7Retrieval quiz

  1. In a normal Q-Q plot, an S-shaped curve where sample quantiles diverge upwards in the right tail and downwards in the left tail indicates:

    Options

    When sample quantiles in both tails are more extreme than theoretical normal quantiles, the distribution has heavier tails than the normal reference.

  2. What is the slope of the reference line in a Q-Q plot of \(X \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\) against standard normal quantiles \(\Phi^{-1}(p)\)?

    Options

    By the location-scale linearity theorem, \(Q_X(p) = \mu + \sigma \Phi^{-1}(p)\); the slope is \(\sigma\) and intercept is \(\mu\).

  3. Why does the classical 3-sigma outlier detection rule fail when multiple outliers are present?

    Options

    Outliers inflate the denominator \(s\) and shift the numerator \(\bar{X}\), so standardized residuals shrink below 3.0 (masking).

  4. Under Tukey's boxplot fence rule, what multiplier of \(\mathrm{IQR}\) is added to \(Q_3\) to establish the inner outlier boundary?

    Tukey's inner fences are defined as \([Q_1 - 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR}, Q_3 + 1.5 \, \mathrm{IQR}]\).

  5. What multiplier of \(\mathrm{IQR}\) is added to \(Q_3\) under Tukey's rule to establish the extreme outlier boundary?

    Tukey's outer fences are defined as \([Q_1 - 3.0 \, \mathrm{IQR}, Q_3 + 3.0 \, \mathrm{IQR}]\).

  6. What is the swamping phenomenon in outlier detection?

    Options

    Swamping occurs when good observations are incorrectly identified as outliers due to the distorting effect of other outliers.

  7. For an exact normal distribution, what fraction of observations lie outside Tukey's 1.5 IQR fences?

    Under \(\Normal(0, 1)\), the fences correspond to \(\pm 2.70 \sigma\), containing \(1 - 2(1 - \Phi(2.70)) \approx 0.993\) of the probability, so \(\approx 0.007\) (0.7%) fall outside.

  8. What does a convex upward-curving normal Q-Q plot signify about the empirical distribution?

    Options

    Convex curvature indicates that upper quantiles grow faster than expected and lower quantiles are compressed, diagnosing positive skewness.
