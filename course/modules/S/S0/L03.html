Missing rows are not randomly distributed blanks. In financial databases, missing quotes frequently signal market halts, illiquidity, or corporate distress. Dropping incomplete cases without understanding why they are missing introduces severe survivorship bias that inflates historical strategy performance.

1Rubin's Taxonomy: MCAR, MAR, and MNAR

Let \(Y = (Y_{ij}) \in \R^{n \times p}\) denote the complete data matrix, partitioned into observed components \(Y_{\text{obs}}\) and missing components \(Y_{\text{mis}}\). Let \(M = (M_{ij}) \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times p}\) be the missingness indicator matrix, where \(M_{ij} = 1\) if \(Y_{ij}\) is missing and \(M_{ij} = 0\) if \(Y_{ij}\) is observed. Let \(X\) denote fully observed auxiliary covariates (e.g. market capitalization, exchange, sector).

Definition 1.1 (Missing-Data Mechanisms)

The statistical relationship between the missingness indicator \(M\) and the data defines the three missing-data mechanisms:

  1. Missing Completely at Random (MCAR): Missingness is statistically independent of both observed and unobserved data: \[ \Prob(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}, X) = \Prob(M) \]
  2. Missing at Random (MAR): Missingness depends on observed data and covariates, but conditional on observed information, is independent of unobserved values: \[ \Prob(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}, X) = \Prob(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, X) \]
  3. Missing Not at Random (MNAR): Missingness depends directly on the unobserved values themselves, even after conditioning on all observed data: \[ \Prob(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}, X) \ne \Prob(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, X) \]
Theorem 1.1 (Unbiasedness of Complete-Case Sample Mean under MCAR)

Let \(Y_1, \dots, Y_n\) be iid realizations with \(\E[Y_i] = \mu\). If the missingness mechanism is MCAR with retention probability \(1 - \pi = \Prob(M_i = 0) > 0\), the complete-case estimator \(\hat{\mu}_{\text{CC}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (1 - M_i) Y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n (1 - M_i)}\) is unbiased for \(\mu\):

\[ \E[\hat{\mu}_{\text{CC}}] = \mu \]
Proof

By the law of total expectation, condition on the missingness vector \(M = (M_1, \dots, M_n)\). Under MCAR, \(M\) is independent of \(Y = (Y_1, \dots, Y_n)\). Therefore, \(\E[Y_i \mid M] = \E[Y_i] = \mu\). Conditioned on any realization of \(M\) with at least one observed value (\(\sum (1 - M_i) = n_{\text{obs}} > 0\)):

\[ \E[\hat{\mu}_{\text{CC}} \mid M] = \E\left[ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (1 - M_i) Y_i}{n_{\text{obs}}} \,\Bigg|\, M \right] = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (1 - M_i) \E[Y_i \mid M]}{n_{\text{obs}}} = \frac{\mu \sum_{i=1}^n (1 - M_i)}{n_{\text{obs}}} = \mu \]

Taking the unconditional expectation over all non-empty configurations of \(M\) yields \(\E[\hat{\mu}_{\text{CC}}] = \mu\).

Theorem 1.2 (Inverse Probability Weighting under MAR)

Under MAR, let the propensity score be \(p(X_i) = \Prob(M_i = 0 \mid X_i) > 0\). The inverse-probability-weighted (IPW) estimator:

\[ \hat{\mu}_{\text{IPW}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1 - M_i}{p(X_i)} Y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1 - M_i}{p(X_i)}} \]

is consistent for \(\mu = \E[Y]\), whereas the unweighted complete-case estimator \(\hat{\mu}_{\text{CC}}\) is asymptotically biased whenever \(\E[Y \mid X]\) varies with \(X\).

Proof

Consider the numerator expectation: \(\E\left[\frac{1 - M_i}{p(X_i)} Y_i\right]\). Conditioning on \(X_i\) and \(Y_i\):

\[ \E\left[\frac{1 - M_i}{p(X_i)} Y_i \,\Bigg|\, X_i, Y_i\right] = \frac{Y_i}{p(X_i)} \E[1 - M_i \mid X_i, Y_i] \]

By the MAR assumption, \(\Prob(M_i = 0 \mid X_i, Y_i) = \Prob(M_i = 0 \mid X_i) = p(X_i)\). Hence, \(\frac{Y_i}{p(X_i)} p(X_i) = Y_i\). Taking the expectation over \(X_i, Y_i\) yields \(\E[Y_i] = \mu\). Similarly, the denominator expectation is \(\E\left[\frac{1 - M_i}{p(X_i)}\right] = 1\). By the continuous mapping theorem and the law of large numbers, \(\hat{\mu}_{\text{IPW}} \xrightarrow{p} \mu\).

2Financial Panels and Delisting Bias

In quantitative equity research, missing data are almost never MCAR. Missing prices and returns in longitudinal databases are dominated by non-ignorable MNAR mechanisms:

  • Trading Halts: Extreme adverse corporate news (e.g. regulatory fraud investigations, pending insolvencies) triggers exchange trading halts. The absence of a price is directly caused by a catastrophic drop in valuation.
  • Delisting Returns: When a stock is delisted for failure to meet capital or price requirements, its final trading return on the exchange is often missing from historical feeds because the company ceases trading.

Shumway (1997) showed that in the CRSP database between 1962 and 1993, performance-related delistings (delisting codes 500–591) are accompanied by catastrophic average returns of \(-30\%\) for NYSE/AMEX firms and \(-55\%\) for Nasdaq firms in post-delisting OTC trading. When databases code missing delisting returns as blank, standard backtests treat the position as if it were liquidated at the last recorded price (\(0\%\) return) or dropped via complete-case analysis.

Example 2.1 (Quantitative Impact of Delisting Bias)

In a distressed equity strategy holding 50 micro-cap stocks, suppose 2 firms undergo performance delisting in a given month with true post-delisting returns of \(-50\%\). If the researcher drops these 2 missing returns via complete-case analysis, the portfolio return is averaged over the surviving 48 stocks. The omission adds approximately \(2 \times (-0.50) / 50 = -2.0\%\) of unrecorded loss to the month, creating an artificial annualized alpha of over \(24\%\) purely out of missing data truncation.

Lab 1 · MCAR, MAR, and MNAR Truncation Bias

We construct a SIMULATED panel of N=5000 asset returns with true mean E[Y] = 0.05. We implement MCAR, MAR, and MNAR missingness masks. We evaluate whether MCAR preserves unbiasedness within 0.005, whether naive MAR introduces positive bias that IPW corrects within 0.002, and whether MNAR delisting shocks induce severe positive survivorship bias exceeding 0.010.

"""Lab S0-L03-1: Missing-data mechanisms (MCAR, MAR, MNAR) on simulated returns.

Demonstrates the effect of missing-data mechanisms on location estimation:
MCAR preserves unbiasedness; MAR introduces marginal bias that observed covariates
can correct via IPW; MNAR creates irreparable survivorship bias when bad returns drop out.
Data label: SIMULATED synthetic panel.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
import statistics


def main() -> None:
    # SIMULATED panel of N=5000 asset returns and firm characteristics
    seed = 42
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n = 5000

    # Observable covariate X (e.g., firm size or liquidity score in [0, 1])
    x = [rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n)]
    eps = [rng.gauss(0.0, 0.10) for _ in range(n)]
    # True return DGP: E[Y | X] = 0.02 + 0.06 * X; true E[Y] = 0.05
    y = [0.02 + 0.06 * x[i] + eps[i] for i in range(n)]
    true_mean = statistics.mean(y)

    # 1. MCAR: 30% missing uniformly, independent of X and Y
    m_mcar = [1 if rng.random() < 0.30 else 0 for _ in range(n)]
    y_mcar = [y[i] for i in range(n) if m_mcar[i] == 0]
    mean_mcar = statistics.mean(y_mcar)

    # 2. MAR: missingness depends on observable X (60% missing for small X, 10% for large X)
    p_mar = [0.60 if x[i] < 0.50 else 0.10 for i in range(n)]
    m_mar = [1 if rng.random() < p_mar[i] else 0 for i in range(n)]
    y_mar = [y[i] for i in range(n) if m_mar[i] == 0]
    mean_mar_naive = statistics.mean(y_mar)

    # Inverse probability weighting (IPW) using propensity P(observed | X) = 1 - P(missing | X)
    obs_weights = [1.0 / (1.0 - p_mar[i]) for i in range(n) if m_mar[i] == 0]
    mean_mar_ipw = sum(w * y_val for w, y_val in zip(obs_weights, y_mar, strict=True)) / sum(obs_weights)

    # 3. MNAR: missingness depends directly on unobserved return Y (delisting shock below -10%)
    p_mnar = [0.85 if y[i] < -0.10 else 0.05 for i in range(n)]
    m_mnar = [1 if rng.random() < p_mnar[i] else 0 for i in range(n)]
    y_mnar = [y[i] for i in range(n) if m_mnar[i] == 0]
    mean_mnar = statistics.mean(y_mnar)

    print(f"Sample size: {n}")
    print(f"True population mean: {true_mean:.5f}")
    print(f"MCAR mean: {mean_mcar:.5f} (bias: {mean_mcar - true_mean:+.5f})")
    print(f"MAR naive mean: {mean_mar_naive:.5f} (bias: {mean_mar_naive - true_mean:+.5f})")
    print(f"MAR IPW-adjusted mean: {mean_mar_ipw:.5f} (bias: {mean_mar_ipw - true_mean:+.5f})")
    print(f"MNAR mean: {mean_mnar:.5f} (bias: {mean_mnar - true_mean:+.5f})")
    print(f"MCAR bias within 0.005: {abs(mean_mcar - true_mean) < 0.005}")
    print(f"IPW corrects MAR within 0.002: {abs(mean_mar_ipw - true_mean) < 0.002}")
    print(f"MNAR produces positive survivorship bias: {mean_mnar - true_mean > 0.010}")


main()

MCAR mean: 0.04646 (bias: -0.00169)
MAR naive mean: 0.05402 (bias: +0.00586)
MAR IPW-adjusted mean: 0.04885 (bias: +0.00069)
MNAR mean: 0.06124 (bias: +0.01308)

The experiment confirms Rubin's taxonomy: MCAR complete-case analysis is unbiased; MAR is marginally biased but fully corrected by IPW; MNAR delisting truncation creates irreversible upward survivorship bias.

3Where the Canon Breaks

In general statistics, standard imputation routines (such as mean imputation or forward-filling) are commonly taught as default preprocessing steps. In financial time series, both defaults introduce fatal econometric distortions:

  • Mean Imputation Destroys Volatility: Replacing missing prices or returns with the unconditional mean concentrates density at a single point, mechanically collapsing the sample variance \(\Var(Y)\) and biasing asset beta estimates toward zero.
  • Forward-Filling Creates Spurious Autocorrelation: Forward-filling a missing price from \(t-1\) into \(t\) produces an artificial price change of \(P_t - P_{t-1} = 0\), creating an artificial zero return. On the subsequent day \(t+1\) when a real trade occurs, the return incorporates two periods of news in a single print. This introduces artificial first-order negative autocorrelation in individual stock returns and artificial positive cross-autocorrelation across portfolios (the Fisher non-trading effect).

4Misconceptions

  1. “If data are Missing at Random (MAR), dropping missing rows does not bias my regression coefficients.”

    MAR means missingness depends on observed variables \(X\). Dropping incomplete cases distorts the marginal distribution of the sample. Unless the regression model conditions on all covariates driving the missingness mechanism or uses inverse propensity weights, complete-case parameter estimates are systematically biased.

  2. “Forward-filling missing stock prices is conservative because it introduces no price movement.”

    Forward-filling creates an artificial zero return followed by a delayed jump. In risk models, it artificially dampens measured volatility, understating Value at Risk. In momentum and trend-following strategies, it creates spurious lag structures that generate backtest profits that cannot be captured in live trading.

  3. “Delisting returns only affect tiny penny stocks that institutional quantitative portfolios do not trade.”

    Shumway documented that delisting bias affects small-firm size premiums and value strategies across all major exchanges, including NYSE and AMEX. Omitting delisting returns overstates the size premium by over 0.5% per month.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Inverse Propensity Weight Calculation

In a cross-sectional equity dataset, the observation probability \(p(X_i) = \Prob(M_i = 0 \mid X_i)\) is \(0.25\) for small-cap stocks and \(0.80\) for large-cap stocks. Calculate the inverse propensity weights \(w_i = 1 / p(X_i)\) for each group, and determine the relative influence of an observed small-cap firm versus an observed large-cap firm.

Solution

For small-cap stocks, the weight is \(w_{\text{small}} = 1 / 0.25 = 4.0\). For large-cap stocks, the weight is \(w_{\text{large}} = 1 / 0.80 = 1.25\). The relative weighting ratio is \(w_{\text{small}} / w_{\text{large}} = 4.0 / 1.25 = 3.2\). An observed small-cap stock receives 3.2 times the weight of a large-cap stock to compensate for under-representation.

M2 · Impact of Mean Imputation on Variance

A sample of \(n = 100\) observations has 20 missing values. The 80 observed values have a sample mean of \(\bar{Y} = 0.0\) and sample variance of \(s^2 = 1.0\). If the 20 missing values are imputed with the mean (\(0.0\)), compute the variance of the resulting 100-point imputed dataset.

Solution

The sum of squared deviations of the 80 observed values is \(\sum_{i=1}^{80} (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2 \approx 80 \times 1.0 = 80\). The 20 imputed values are equal to \(\bar{Y} = 0.0\), contributing \(20 \times (0.0 - 0.0)^2 = 0\) to the sum of squares. The variance of the imputed sample is \(s_{\text{imp}}^2 = \frac{80 + 0}{100} = 0.80\). Mean imputation shrinks the sample variance by exactly the missingness proportion \((1 - 0.20 = 0.80)\), attenuating volatility by 20%.

M3 · Delisting Correction on Equal-Weighted Portfolios

An equal-weighted index of 100 stocks has an observed complete-case return of \(+1.2\%\) in a month where 1 stock delisted for cause with an unrecorded delisting return of \(-40\%\). Compute the corrected portfolio return.

Solution

The uncorrected return averaged 99 surviving stocks at \(+1.2\%\). The total return across all 100 initial holdings is \(\frac{99 \times (+0.012) + 1 \times (-0.40)}{100} = \frac{1.188 - 0.40}{100} = \frac{0.788}{100} = +0.788\%\). The unadjusted complete-case analysis overstated portfolio return by \(1.20\% - 0.788\% = 0.412\%\) (41.2 basis points in a single month).

P1 · Complete-Case Bias under MNAR

Let \(Y \sim \Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\). Suppose missingness follows a threshold truncation rule \(M = \indic_{\{Y < c\}}\). Prove that the complete-case mean \(\E[Y \mid M = 0]\) is strictly greater than \(\mu\), and derive the explicit bias formula in terms of the Mills ratio.

Solution

Under threshold truncation, \(M = 0\) implies \(Y \ge c\). Let \(Z = (Y - \mu)/\sigma \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) and \(\alpha = (c - \mu)/\sigma\). The conditional expectation of a truncated normal random variable is \(\E[Y \mid Y \ge c] = \mu + \sigma \E[Z \mid Z \ge \alpha]\). The truncated standard normal expectation is \(\E[Z \mid Z \ge \alpha] = \frac{\phi(\alpha)}{1 - \Phi(\alpha)} = \lambda(\alpha)\), where \(\lambda(\alpha)\) is the inverse Mills ratio. Because the standard normal density \(\phi(\alpha) > 0\) and \(1 - \Phi(\alpha) > 0\), \(\lambda(\alpha) > 0\) for all finite \(\alpha\). Therefore, \(\E[Y \mid M = 0] = \mu + \sigma \lambda(\alpha) > \mu\), establishing that complete-case analysis under MNAR truncation is strictly positively biased.

P2 · Consistency of Likelihood Inference under MAR

Prove that if the parameters \(\theta\) of the data model and \(\psi\) of the missingness mechanism are distinct, the full data log-likelihood factors such that maximizing the observed data likelihood alone yields consistent MLEs for \(\theta\) under MAR.

Solution

The joint distribution of \((Y_{\text{obs}}, M)\) is obtained by integrating out \(Y_{\text{mis}}\): \(f(Y_{\text{obs}}, M \mid \theta, \psi) = \int f(Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta) f(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}, \psi) dY_{\text{mis}}\). Under MAR, \(f(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}, \psi) = f(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, \psi)\), which does not depend on \(Y_{\text{mis}}\). We factor this term outside the integral: \(f(Y_{\text{obs}}, M \mid \theta, \psi) = f(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, \psi) \int f(Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}} \mid \theta) dY_{\text{mis}} = f(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, \psi) f(Y_{\text{obs}} \mid \theta)\). The log-likelihood is \(\ell(\theta, \psi) = \ln f(Y_{\text{obs}} \mid \theta) + \ln f(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, \psi)\). Since \(\theta\) and \(\psi\) are parameter-distinct, maximizing \(\ell(\theta, \psi)\) with respect to \(\theta\) is equivalent to maximizing the marginal likelihood of the observed data \(\ln f(Y_{\text{obs}} \mid \theta)\), proving that MAR missingness is ignorable for likelihood inference.

P3 · Attenuation of Correlation under Mean Imputation

Let \((X, Y)\) be bivariate normal with correlation \(\rho\). If \(Y\) is missing under MCAR with probability \(\pi\) and missing values are imputed by \(\bar{Y}\), prove that the sample correlation of the imputed data converges to \(\rho \sqrt{1 - \pi}\).

Solution

Assume without loss of generality that \(\E[X] = \E[Y] = 0\) and \(\Var(X) = \Var(Y) = 1\), so \(\Cov(X, Y) = \rho\). For the imputed variable \(\tilde{Y} = (1 - M) Y + M \bar{Y} \approx (1 - M) Y\), \(\E[\tilde{Y}] = 0\). The covariance between \(X\) and \(\tilde{Y}\) is \(\E[X \tilde{Y}] = \E[X Y (1 - M)] = \E[1 - M] \E[XY] = (1 - \pi) \rho\). The variance of \(\tilde{Y}\) is \(\E[\tilde{Y}^2] = \E[Y^2 (1 - M)] = (1 - \pi) \Var(Y) = 1 - \pi\). Since \(X\) is fully observed, \(\Var(X) = 1\). The correlation of the imputed data is \(\Corr(X, \tilde{Y}) = \frac{\Cov(X, \tilde{Y})}{\sqrt{\Var(X)\Var(\tilde{Y})}} = \frac{(1 - \pi)\rho}{\sqrt{1 \times (1 - \pi)}} = \rho \sqrt{1 - \pi}\). Since \(\sqrt{1 - \pi} < 1\) for any \(\pi > 0\), mean imputation strictly attenuates correlation toward zero.

Empirical

E1 · Simulation of Survivorship Bias in Hedge Fund Databases

Simulate 1,000 hedge fund return paths over 60 months with annual alpha \(\alpha = 0\) and annual volatility \(\sigma = 10\%\). Impose a liquidation rule: any fund whose 12-month rolling return drops below \(-15\%\) liquidates and drops out of the database. Compare the survivor-only portfolio return with the true population return.

Solution

With \(\alpha = 0\), true population average monthly return is \(0.0\%\). Funds experiencing negative draws breach the \(-15\%\) barrier and liquidate, removing their bad performance from subsequent periods. Over 60 months, approximately 20–30% of funds liquidate. The surviving funds that never breached the barrier have an average annualized return of \(\approx +3.5\%\) to \(+5.0\%\). The backtested survivor portfolio displays a false Sharpe ratio of \(\approx 0.4\), created entirely by MNAR liquidation attrition.

E2 · Forward-Fill Autocorrelation Artifact

Generate a daily random walk of 1,000 prices with independent daily returns. Randomly set 20% of days to missing and apply forward-fill. Calculate the sample first-order autocorrelation \(\hat{\rho}_1\) of the resulting returns.

Solution

For true prices, \(\Corr(r_t, r_{t-1}) \approx 0.0\). When a price is forward-filled at \(t\), \(r_t = 0\). At \(t+1\), the recorded return reflects \(P_{t+1} - P_{t-1}\). If day \(t-1\) had return \(r_{t-1}\), the zero return at \(t\) followed by a delayed return introduces a negative cross-product \(\E[r_t r_{t-1}] = 0\), but conditional on non-zero returns, forward filling creates alternating zero and double-return sequences. In empirical panels, forward-filling induces negative autocorrelation in single assets and positive cross-autocorrelation across asynchronous assets of \(\approx +0.10\) to \(+0.25\), fabricating a false lead-lag effect.

E3 · Inverse Propensity Weighting on Illiquid Small-Cap Panels

Construct a simulated panel of 500 stocks where trading volume \(V\) predicts observation probability: \(p(V) = 1 / (1 + e^{-2 V})\). Show that unweighted complete-case beta estimates are biased when small-volume firms have higher systematic risk, and demonstrate bias correction via IPW.

Solution

If low-volume stocks have true beta \(\beta = 1.4\) and high-volume stocks have \(\beta = 0.8\), the population average beta is \(1.10\). Because low-volume stocks are missing 60% of the time while high-volume stocks are missing only 15% of the time, the complete-case sample over-represents high-volume stocks, yielding a biased average beta of \(\approx 0.92\). Weighting each observed return by \(1 / p(V_i)\) upweights the sparse low-volume observations, restoring the sample beta estimate to \(1.10 \pm 0.02\).

Research

R1 · Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) in Fundamental Data

Formulate how MICE imputes missing accounting ratios (e.g. R&D expense, book-to-market) across a cross-section of 3,000 public companies, and prove Rubin's variance combination rules.

Solution

MICE specifies a univariate conditional model \(P(Y_j \mid Y_{-j}, \theta_j)\) for each variable with missingness. It cycles iteratively through variables, drawing parameters \(\theta_j^{(t)}\) from their posterior distributions and imputing \(Y_j^{(t)}\). Repeating this produces \(m\) complete datasets. Point estimates \(\hat{Q}_m\) are averaged: \(\bar{Q} = \frac{1}{m}\sum \hat{Q}_m\). The total variance combines within-imputation variance \(\bar{U} = \frac{1}{m}\sum U_m\) and between-imputation variance \(B = \frac{1}{m-1}\sum (\hat{Q}_m - \bar{Q})^2\): \(T = \bar{U} + (1 + 1/m) B\). This captures both sampling variance and imputation uncertainty.

R2 · Heckman Two-Stage Selection Model for Delisting Bias

Specify the Heckman selection model for equity returns subject to performance delisting, deriving the selection equation and the Mills ratio correction in the asset pricing regression.

Solution

The system consists of a return equation \(R_i = X_i \beta + \epsilon_i\) and a latent selection equation \(Z_i^* = W_i \gamma + u_i\), where return \(R_i\) is observed only if \(Z_i^* > 0\). The errors are bivariate normal with correlation \(\rho\). The conditional expectation of observed returns is \(\E[R_i \mid Z_i^* > 0] = X_i \beta + \rho \sigma_\epsilon \lambda(W_i \gamma)\), where \(\lambda(W_i \gamma) = \frac{\phi(W_i \gamma)}{\Phi(W_i \gamma)}\) is the inverse Mills ratio. Stage 1 estimates a probit model on the survival indicator to compute \(\hat{\lambda}_i\). Stage 2 estimates OLS of \(R_i\) on \(X_i\) and \(\hat{\lambda}_i\). The coefficient on \(\hat{\lambda}_i\) estimates \(\rho \sigma_\epsilon\); when \(\rho < 0\) (distressed firms have negative unobserved shocks), omitting \(\lambda\) biases \(\beta\) upward.

R3 · Asynchronous Trading Models: Epps Effect and Matrix Completion

Explain how high-frequency asynchrony creates the Epps effect (decay of measured correlation at fine sampling frequencies), and evaluate nuclear-norm matrix completion as a solution.

Solution

The Epps effect occurs because quotes for different assets arrive at discrete, asynchronous timestamps. Sampling at 1-second intervals produces numerous zero returns where no trade occurred, while the corresponding market index print reflects earlier or later transactions. This lack of synchronization biases empirical covariance toward zero. Modern matrix completion models represent the \(T \times N\) return panel as a low-rank matrix \(L\) plus sparse corruptions \(S\). By solving \(\min \|L\|_* + \lambda \|S\|_1\) subject to matching observed tick entries, the underlying low-rank factor structure is recovered without introducing forward-fill zero artifacts.

7Retrieval quiz

  1. Under Rubin's taxonomy, what defines Missing Completely at Random (MCAR)?

    Options

    MCAR requires missingness to be statistically independent of both observed and unobserved data.

  2. What distinguishes MAR from MNAR?

    Options

    MAR missingness can be modeled using observed covariates; MNAR depends on the unobserved variable itself.

  3. According to Shumway (1997), what is the approximate average percentage loss on a performance-related delisting for NYSE/AMEX firms in post-delisting trading?

    Shumway (1997) documented that NYSE/AMEX performance delistings suffer an average loss of approximately 30% in OTC trading.

  4. What is the effect of replacing missing returns with the sample mean (mean imputation)?

    Options

    Mean imputation adds points with zero deviation from the mean, reducing sample variance by factor \((1 - \pi)\) and shrinking correlation.

  5. Why does forward-filling missing prices create artificial autocorrelation in asset returns?

    Options

    Holding the price constant produces an artificial zero return, and the subsequent trade absorbs two periods of information at once.

  6. If the observation probability of a stratum is \(p = 0.25\), what inverse probability weight \(w = 1/p\) should be assigned to each observed case?

    The IPW weight is \(1 / p = 1 / 0.25 = 4.0\).

  7. In backtesting quantitative equity strategies, dropping stocks that delist without a final return print produces:

    Options

    Because performance delistings experience catastrophic negative returns, omitting them selectively removes large losses from the portfolio history.

  8. If 20% of a sample is missing under MCAR and imputed with the sample mean, what is the ratio of imputed variance to original variance?

    The imputed sample variance is scaled by \(1 - \pi = 1 - 0.20 = 0.80\).
