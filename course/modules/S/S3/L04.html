Spurious Regression

The Granger–Newbold Phenomenon, Divergent Test Statistics, and Non-Stationary Diagnostics

Why It Matters

In cross-sectional regression, large t-statistics and high R-squared values provide strong evidence of an underlying linear relationship. In non-stationary time series, these exact same metrics routinely deceive the analyst. Regressing two completely independent random walks generates statistically significant t-ratios over 75% of the time. Understanding spurious regression is essential to avoid trading on imaginary historical relationships.

1. The Spurious Regression Phenomenon

Definition 4.1 · Spurious Regression

A spurious regression occurs when ordinary least squares regression between two or more independent non-stationary unit root processes yields apparent statistical significance, characterized by divergent t-statistics, inflated coefficients of determination \(R^2\), and severely autocorrelated residuals, despite the complete absence of any structural relationship.

Theorem 4.1 · The Granger–Newbold Asymptotic Properties

Assumptions:

  1. Let \(x_t = x_{t-1} + e_{1,t}\) and \(y_t = y_{t-1} + e_{2,t}\) be independent random walks with \(x_0 = y_0 = 0\).
  2. The innovation vectors satisfy \((e_{1,t}, e_{2,t})^\top \sim \operatorname{iid}\mathcal{N}(0, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2))\).
  3. The fitted model is \(y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + u_t\) estimated by OLS across \(T\) observations.

Conclusions: Granger and Newbold (1974) discovered through Monte Carlo experiments that the nominal t-statistic on \(\beta\) rejects the null \(H_0: \beta = 0\) with probability approaching 1 as \(T \to \infty\).

  1. The slope estimator \(\hat{\beta}\) does not converge in probability to 0: \(\hat{\beta} \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\int_0^1 W_1(r) W_2(r) dr - \int_0^1 W_1(r) dr \int_0^1 W_2(r) dr}{\int_0^1 [W_1(r)]^2 dr - (\int_0^1 W_1(r) dr)^2} \frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}\).
  2. The OLS t-statistic diverges at rate \(O_p(\sqrt{T})\): \(T^{-1/2} t_\beta = O_p(1)\).
  3. The coefficient of determination \(R^2\) has a non-degenerate limiting distribution on \([0, 1]\).
  4. The Durbin-Watson statistic converges in probability to zero at rate \(O_p(1/T)\): \(DW \xrightarrow{p} 0\).
Proof Sketch

Under the functional central limit theorem, normalized random walks converge to independent Brownian motions: \(T^{-1/2} x_{\lfloor rT \rfloor} \Rightarrow \sigma_1 W_1(r)\) and \(T^{-1/2} y_{\lfloor rT \rfloor} \Rightarrow \sigma_2 W_2(r)\). The OLS denominator scales as \(\sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2 = O_p(T^2)\), and the numerator scales as \(\sum (x_t - \bar{x})(y_t - \bar{y}) = O_p(T^2)\). Therefore \(\hat{\beta} = O_p(1)\) and does not vanish. The residual sum of squares satisfies \(\sum \hat{u}_t^2 = O_p(T^2)\), giving residual variance \(s^2 = \frac{1}{T-2} \sum \hat{u}_t^2 = O_p(T)\). The standard error is \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}) = \sqrt{s^2 / \sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2} = \sqrt{O_p(T) / O_p(T^2)} = O_p(T^{-1/2})\). Consequently, the t-ratio is \(t_\beta = \hat{\beta} / \operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}) = O_p(1) / O_p(T^{-1/2}) = O_p(\sqrt{T})\), diverging to \(\pm \infty\).

2. Detecting Spurious Regressions

Remark 4.1 · The Granger–Newbold Rule of Thumb

Granger and Newbold proposed the practical heuristic: if \(R^2 > DW\), suspect a spurious regression. In a stationary linear regression, the Durbin-Watson statistic is approximately \(DW \approx 2(1 - \hat{\rho}_u(1)) \approx 2.0\). In a spurious regression, the residual \(u_t = y_t - \alpha - \beta x_t\) remains an \(I(1)\) integrated process with \(\hat{\rho}_u(1) \approx 1 - c/T\), forcing \(DW \to 0\).

3. Numerical Verification: The Spurious-Regression Generator

We execute the curriculum laboratory (curriculum/01-foundations.yaml:730): generating independent random walks and evaluating the empirical rejection rate of nominal 5% tests.

Lab 1 · Spurious-Regression Generator on Independent Random Walks

Simulating 2000 pairs of independent random walks of length T=200 produces a nominal 5% t-test rejection rate exceeding 70%, mean R^2 > 0.20, and mean Durbin-Watson < 0.15.

"""Lab S3-L04-1: Spurious regression generator on independent random walks.

SIMULATED: generates independent random walks and measures the empirical
rejection rate of the nominal 5% t-test, average R^2, and Durbin-Watson statistic.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def run_spurious_trial(n: int, rng: random.Random) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Simulate one pair of independent random walks and regress y on x."""
    x = [0.0]
    y = [0.0]
    for _ in range(n - 1):
        x.append(x[-1] + rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0))
        y.append(y[-1] + rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0))

    mean_x = sum(x) / n
    mean_y = sum(y) / n
    cov = sum((x[i] - mean_x) * (y[i] - mean_y) for i in range(n))
    var_x = sum((x[i] - mean_x) ** 2 for i in range(n))
    var_y = sum((y[i] - mean_y) ** 2 for i in range(n))

    beta = cov / var_x
    alpha = mean_y - beta * mean_x
    res = [y[i] - alpha - beta * x[i] for i in range(n)]
    rss = sum(r * r for r in res)
    r2 = 1.0 - rss / var_y if var_y > 0.0 else 0.0

    s2 = rss / (n - 2)
    se_beta = math.sqrt(s2 / var_x)
    t_stat = beta / se_beta
    dw = sum((res[i] - res[i - 1]) ** 2 for i in range(1, n)) / rss if rss > 0.0 else 0.0
    return t_stat, r2, dw


def main() -> None:
    reps = 2000
    n = 200
    rng = random.Random(42)

    rejections = 0
    sum_r2 = 0.0
    sum_dw = 0.0

    for _ in range(reps):
        t_stat, r2, dw = run_spurious_trial(n, rng)
        if abs(t_stat) > 1.95996:
            rejections += 1
        sum_r2 += r2
        sum_dw += dw

    rej_rate = rejections / reps
    mean_r2 = sum_r2 / reps
    mean_dw = sum_dw / reps

    print(f"Spurious Regression Simulation (R={reps}, T={n}, seed=42)")
    print("Nominal test size: 0.0500 (two-sided critical value 1.96)")
    print(f"Empirical rejection rate: {rej_rate:.4f} ({rejections}/{reps} trials)")
    print(f"Mean R-squared: {mean_r2:.4f} (theoretical under independence: ~0.0)")
    print(f"Mean Durbin-Watson statistic: {mean_dw:.4f} (ideal stationary residuals: ~2.0)")


main()
Spurious Regression Simulation (R=2000, T=200, seed=42)
Nominal test size: 0.0500 (two-sided critical value 1.96)
Empirical rejection rate: 0.8190 (1638/2000 trials)
Mean R-squared: 0.2409 (theoretical under independence: ~0.0)
Mean Durbin-Watson statistic: 0.0907 (ideal stationary residuals: ~2.0)

The empirical laboratory demonstrates that over 80% of independent random walk trials spuriously reject the true null hypothesis at the nominal 5% level, with substantial artificial R-squared and collapsed Durbin-Watson statistics.

Where the Canon Breaks

Recognizing spurious regression is critical, but naive remediation creates new econometric failures:

  • Indiscriminate first-differencing: Differencing removes unit roots (\(\Delta y_t = \beta \Delta x_t + \Delta u_t\)) and eliminates spurious rejection. However, if \(y_t\) and \(x_t\) share a genuine long-run equilibrium relationship (cointegration, Lesson 5), differencing completely discards the low-frequency levels information, rendering long-horizon portfolio hedging impossible.
  • Over-reliance on Durbin-Watson: In financial equity returns, volatility clustering and GARCH errors can drive the Durbin-Watson statistic slightly below 2.0 without indicating non-stationarity. Conversely, dynamic regressions with lagged dependent variables bias DW toward 2.0 even when spurious persistence is present.

Misconceptions

  • Wrong belief: An R-squared above 0.70 combined with a t-statistic above 5.0 proves that two economic variables are related.

    Why it is wrong: Independent random walks easily produce \(R^2 > 0.70\) and \(|t| > 5.0\) as sample size increases. Goodness-of-fit metrics are entirely meaningless when regressors are non-stationary.

  • Wrong belief: The spurious regression problem disappears if sample size \(T\) is made very large.

    Why it is wrong: Increasing \(T\) makes spurious regression worse, not better. Because \(t_\beta\) scales as \(O_p(\sqrt{T})\), larger samples guarantee that the nominal test rejects the null of independence with probability approaching 100%.

  • Wrong belief: Including a deterministic linear time trend \(t\) eliminates the spurious correlation between two random walks.

    Why it is wrong: Detrending a pure random walk with a linear trend leaves residual stochastic trends intact. The regression between two linearly detrended random walks remains completely spurious.

Practice Problems

Mechanics Tier

An analyst regresses annual US corporate earnings on annual solar sunspot counts over \(T = 100\) years, obtaining \(\hat{\beta} = 2.4\), \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\beta}) = 0.60\), \(R^2 = 0.45\), and \(DW = 0.22\). Calculate the t-statistic on \(\beta\) and diagnose the regression using the Granger-Newbold rule of thumb.

Worked Solution

The t-statistic is \(t_\beta = 2.4 / 0.60 = 4.00\). Under standard normal tables, \(p < 0.0001\). However, comparing \(R^2 = 0.45\) to \(DW = 0.22\) reveals that \(R^2 > DW\). Residuals exhibit massive positive first-order autocorrelation, diagnosing the regression as spurious due to non-stationary persistence in both series.

Given an OLS regression with residuals \(\hat{u}_t\), if the estimated lag-1 residual autocorrelation is \(\hat{\rho}_1 = 0.94\), compute the approximate Durbin-Watson statistic.

Worked Solution

The Durbin-Watson statistic is related to the first-order residual autocorrelation by \(DW \approx 2(1 - \hat{\rho}_1)\). For \(\hat{\rho}_1 = 0.94\), \(DW \approx 2(1 - 0.94) = 2(0.06) = 0.12\), which is close to zero, reflecting non-stationary residuals.

Suppose two independent random walks have sample length \(T = 400\) and generate an empirical OLS t-statistic of \(t = 3.60\). Under the asymptotic scaling \(t_\beta \sim O_p(\sqrt{T})\), estimate the expected magnitude of the t-statistic if the sample length is expanded to \(T = 1600\).

Worked Solution

Because the spurious t-statistic scales proportionally to \(\sqrt{T}\), quadrupling the sample size from \(T = 400\) to \(T = 1600\) scales the expected t-statistic by \(\sqrt{1600 / 400} = \sqrt{4} = 2\). The expected t-statistic inflates to approximately \(3.60 \times 2 = 7.20\), demonstrating that larger samples exacerbate spurious significance.

Proof Tier

Prove that if \(u_t \sim I(1)\), the Durbin-Watson statistic \(DW = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^T (\hat{u}_t - \hat{u}_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^T \hat{u}_t^2}\) converges in probability to zero at rate \(O_p(1/T)\).

Worked Solution

Because \(u_t\) is integrated of order one, its first differences \(\Delta u_t\) are stationary \(I(0)\) with finite variance \(\sigma^2\). Therefore, the numerator satisfies \(\sum_{t=2}^T (\Delta \hat{u}_t)^2 = O_p(T)\). In contrast, the denominator accumulates a non-stationary random walk, satisfying \(\sum_{t=1}^T \hat{u}_t^2 = O_p(T^2)\). The ratio scales as \(DW = \frac{O_p(T)}{O_p(T^2)} = O_p(1/T)\). As \(T \to \infty\), \(DW \xrightarrow{p} 0\).

Prove that when two independent random walks \(y_t\) and \(x_t\) are differenced to stationary series \(\Delta y_t = e_{2,t}\) and \(\Delta x_t = e_{1,t}\), the OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}_{\Delta}\) in \(\Delta y_t = \beta \Delta x_t + v_t\) converges to 0 at rate \(\sqrt{T}\) and has standard normal limiting distribution.

Worked Solution

Because \(e_{1,t}\) and \(e_{2,t}\) are independent iid white noise sequences, \(\operatorname{Cov}(e_{1,t}, e_{2,t}) = 0\). The differenced regressor is stationary with \(\frac{1}{T}\sum (\Delta x_t)^2 \xrightarrow{p} \sigma_1^2\). By the classical central limit theorem, \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}} \sum \Delta x_t \Delta y_t \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_1^2 \sigma_2^2)\). Consequently, \(\sqrt{T}\hat{\beta}_{\Delta} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_2^2/\sigma_1^2)\), and the t-statistic converges to standard \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), eliminating spurious rejections.

Show why the standard OLS variance formula \(s^2 = \frac{1}{T-2}\sum \hat{u}_t^2\) fails to estimate the sampling variance of \(\hat{\beta}\) when the error \(u_t\) has long-range persistence.

Worked Solution

The true variance of the OLS estimator is \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}) = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \Omega X (X^\top X)^{-1}\), where \(\Omega = \mathbb{E}[u u^\top]\). The standard OLS formula assumes \(\Omega = \sigma^2 I\). When \(u_t \sim I(1)\), off-diagonal covariance entries \(\operatorname{Cov}(u_t, u_s) = \min(t, s)\sigma^2\) are massive and positive. Ignoring off-diagonal elements in \(\Omega\) underestimates the true sampling variance of the numerator cross-product by a factor of order \(O(T)\), artificially deflating standard errors.

Empirical Tier

In backtesting a statistical arbitrage strategy, a quant regresses the level of Bitcoin on the cumulative volume of crude oil futures, observing \(R^2 = 0.82\), \(t = 9.4\), and an in-sample equity curve with Sharpe ratio 2.5. Describe what happens when this strategy is deployed out-of-sample.

Worked Solution

Because both cumulative oil volume and Bitcoin price are integrated \(I(1)\) non-stationary series that do not share a structural economic relationship, the in-sample fit is entirely spurious. Out-of-sample, the two processes drift apart arbitrarily according to their independent Brownian components. The spread never mean-reverts, triggering catastrophic drawdowns and severe strategy liquidation.

Explain how Newey-West HAC standard errors behave when applied to a spurious regression with non-stationary residuals.

Worked Solution

Newey-West standard errors assume that the autocovariances of the regression errors are absolutely summable: \(\sum_{h=0}^\infty |\gamma_u(h)| < \infty\). For non-stationary \(I(1)\) residuals, autocovariances diverge with \(T\) and are not summable. Even with a bandwidth truncation parameter \(m = O(T^{1/3})\), the Newey-West estimator fails to correct the rate divergence of \(t_\beta\), which still diverges at rate \(O_p(T^{1/6})\).

Describe an empirical testing protocol to ensure that an observed high regression fit between two macro-financial series is genuine rather than spurious.

Worked Solution

Step 1: Test both series individually for unit roots using ADF and KPSS tests. If both are stationary \(I(0)\), standard OLS inference is valid. Step 2: If both are \(I(1)\), fit the levels regression and test the residuals \(\hat{u}_t\) for a unit root using the Engle-Granger cointegration test with appropriate critical values (Lesson 6). Step 3: If cointegration is rejected, re-estimate the specification in first differences.

Research Tier

Analyze the Yule (1926) "nonsense-correlation" theorem and its relationship to modern stochastic calculus and Brownian motion functionals.

Worked Solution

G. Udny Yule (1926) first observed that sample correlation between integrated series fails to cluster around zero and exhibits an M-shaped bimodal distribution toward \(\pm 1\). Phillips (1986) provided the rigorous mathematical foundation using functional central limit theorems, demonstrating that the sample correlation coefficient converges to the ratio of stochastic integrals of Brownian motions \(\int W_1 W_2 / (\int W_1^2 \int W_2^2)^{1/2}\), whose density is bimodal with high probability mass near the boundaries.

In panel data econometrics, what happens to spurious regression when the cross-sectional dimension \(N\) and time dimension \(T\) both grow large?

Worked Solution

As shown by Phillips and Moon (1999), in non-stationary panel regressions with independent cross-sectional units, averaging across \(N\) independent pairs invokes a cross-sectional central limit theorem that cancels out the spurious Brownian covariance. The pooled OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{pool}}\) converges in probability to 0 at rate \(\sqrt{N}\), providing a consistent estimate of zero long-run relationship despite individual time-series regressions remaining spurious.

Why do machine learning algorithms (such as deep neural networks and gradient-boosted trees) catastrophically overfit when fed non-stationary feature levels?

Worked Solution

Machine learning models partition the feature space into hyperplanes or non-linear manifolds. In non-stationary time series, future test data occupy regions of the feature space that were never visited during training (covariate shift / distribution drift). Tree splits fit spurious historical level coincidences, resulting in excellent in-sample training loss but catastrophic generalization error on un-snooped test regimes.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What did Granger and Newbold (1974) discover when regressing two completely independent random walks against each other?

    Granger and Newbold discovered spurious regression: independent random walks frequently produce statistically significant t-ratios and high \(R^2\), with collapsed Durbin-Watson statistics.

  2. What is the asymptotic behavior of the OLS t-statistic \(t_\beta\) in a spurious regression between independent random walks as \(T \to \infty\)?

    Phillips (1986) proved that because the standard error shrinks at rate \(O_p(T^{-1/2})\) while \(\hat{\beta} = O_p(1)\), the t-statistic diverges at rate \(O_p(\sqrt{T})\).

  3. According to the Granger-Newbold rule of thumb, what diagnostic signal indicates that a regression may be spurious?

    When the coefficient of determination exceeds the Durbin-Watson statistic (\(R^2 > DW\)), residual non-stationarity is likely generating an artificial regression fit.

  4. What happens to the Durbin-Watson statistic \(DW\) as the sample size \(T\) increases in a spurious regression?

    Because the numerator differences are \(O_p(T)\) while the denominator sum of squares is \(O_p(T^2)\), \(DW = O_p(1/T) \to 0\).

  5. Why does increasing the sample size \(T\) not resolve the spurious regression problem?

    Because the test statistic diverges at rate \(O_p(\sqrt{T})\), increasing sample size guarantees rejection of the true null hypothesis of no relationship.

  6. How does first-differencing solve the spurious regression problem between two non-cointegrated random walks?

    Differencing removes the stochastic trend, rendering regressors and errors \(I(0)\) stationary, which restores classical Gauss-Markov and central limit theorem assumptions.

  7. Why is first-differencing problematic if two time series are actually cointegrated?

    Differencing removes all long-run level information, eliminating the stationary spread that pairs-trading strategies seek to monetize.

  8. Does adding a linear deterministic time trend \(t\) to the regression of two independent random walks eliminate spurious correlation?

    A deterministic linear trend cannot remove a stochastic trend. Detrended random walks remain non-stationary and continue to produce spurious regression.
