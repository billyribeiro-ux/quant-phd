Raising Capital & Investment Banking

How do corporations navigate capital market frictions to raise primary equity and debt capital, and how do investment banking syndicates price, allocate, and underwrite new securities? This module examines the empirical anomalies and structural microfoundations of corporate capital raising. You will model the initial public offering (IPO) underpricing anomaly, solve Rock’s winner’s-curse adverse selection model, analyze Benveniste–Spindt information acquisition and discretionary bookbuilding, investigate the 7% gross spread clustering puzzle and syndicate economics, test for 3-to-5-year long-run IPO abnormal underperformance using buy-and-hold abnormal returns (BHAR), formulate Myers–Majluf adverse selection and announcement discounts in seasoned equity offerings (SEOs), and evaluate the pricing and indenture covenant architecture of corporate debt issuance.

  • Prerequisites: CF3 Payout, Governance & Agency, S6 Causal Inference & Event Studies. Assumes foundational calculus, probability, and corporate finance (CF1, CF2, M1, M4, S1).
  • Project: IPO pricing & syndicate allocation case (autograded engine plus rubric-graded pitch memo, 20 h)

40 h lessons · 20 h project (33.3% of the 60 module hours)

Objectives

By the end of CF6 you can:

  1. Formulate the Rock (1986) winner’s-curse model of IPO underpricing and solve for the unique equilibrium offer price compensating uninformed investors for informed rationing.
  2. Analyze bookbuilding price discovery mechanisms, demonstrating how underwriter allocation discretion extracts private valuation signals from institutional investors.
  3. Decompose investment banking syndicate economics, evaluating underwriting gross spreads, functional fee breakdowns, and the 7% fee clustering puzzle.
  4. Measure long-run IPO and SEO post-issuance performance across multi-year horizons using Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Returns (BHAR), wealth relatives, and skewness-adjusted bootstrap tests.
  5. Derive the Myers–Majluf (1984) adverse selection threshold for seasoned equity offerings, proving how internal financial slack mitigates underinvestment.
  6. Model the corporate debt issuance process, quantifying benchmark Treasury credit spreads, covenant slack, and syndicated loan tranche structures.

Placement diagnostic

Test your preparation before starting CF6. Achieving 80% mastery demonstrates readiness to advance directly to the project ladder.

  1. In Rock’s (1986) winner’s-curse model, why does setting the IPO offer price equal to the unconditional expected firm value \(P_0 = \mathbb{E}[v]\) result in negative expected profits for uninformed investors?

    Options

    Informed investors only bid when \(v_H > P_0\). When the issue is a lemon (\(v_L < P_0\)), informed investors submit zero bids and uninformed investors receive 100% of their order, leading to severe adverse selection.

  2. Under the Benveniste–Spindt (1989) bookbuilding framework, how does the lead underwriter incentivize institutional investors to truthfully reveal favorable private valuation indications during the roadshow?

    Options

    Underwriter allocation discretion allows underwriters to reward truthful revelation of positive information with greater allocations of underpriced stock, overcoming the incentive to understate demand.

  3. In the Myers and Majluf (1984) adverse selection framework, what signal does an announcement of a seasoned equity offering (SEO) convey to rational public investors?

    Options

    Because managers acting for existing shareholders would not issue undervalued equity, rational market participants mark down the stock price upon SEO announcement (empirically -2% to -3%).

  4. In Loughran and Ritter’s (1995) study of 4,753 U.S. initial public offerings between 1970 and 1990, what was the average first-day return (IPO underpricing) in percent?

    Loughran and Ritter (1995, Table 1) report an average initial first-day return of 16.4% across the 1970–1990 sample period.

  5. What is the primary methodological distinction between Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Returns (BHAR) and Cumulative Abnormal Returns (CAR) in long-run IPO performance studies?

    Options

    BHAR compounds returns over the holding period: \(\prod(1 + R_{i,t}) - \prod(1 + R_{b,t})\). CAR arithmetically sums monthly abnormal returns: \(\sum(R_{i,t} - R_{b,t})\), which biases performance due to compounding assumptions.

  6. In U.S. equity underwriting for moderate-sized IPOs ($20M to $100M), what exact gross underwriting spread percentage is charged in over 75% of contracts?

    The 7% gross spread puzzle (Chen and Ritter 2000; Hansen 2001; Ljungqvist 2007) documents that over three-quarters of mid-market U.S. IPOs pay exactly 7.00% in underwriting fees.

Lessons

Five doctoral-level lessons totaling 40 study hours. Every lesson features KaTeX mathematical rigor, theorem proofs, runnable inline Python labs held to exact test twins, misconceptions, four tiers of problem sets with complete worked solutions, key results, and diagnostic quizzes.

L01 · 8 h The IPO Anomaly & Rock’s Winner’s Curse First-day return stylized facts, international evidence, the Rock (1986) informational asymmetry model, informed rationing, and equilibrium underpricing derivations. L02 · 8 h Bookbuilding, Allocation & Syndicate Economics Benveniste–Spindt information extraction, underwriter allocation discretion, partial adjustment phenomenon, the 7% gross spread puzzle, and syndicate fee structures. L03 · 8 h The New Issues Puzzle: Long-Run Underperformance Ritter (1991) and Loughran & Ritter (1995) 3-to-5-year underperformance, Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Returns (BHAR), wealth relatives, skewness bias, and the bad-model problem. L04 · 8 h Seasoned Equity Offerings & Adverse Selection Myers–Majluf (1984) equity dilution, SEO announcement day price drop (-2% to -3%), internal financial slack, underinvestment avoidance, and market timing dynamics. L05 · 8 h Corporate Debt Issuance & Capital Markets Public bond origination vs. syndicated loans (Term Loan B), benchmark Treasury credit spreads, indenture covenants, rating agency negotiation, and fee waterfalls.
