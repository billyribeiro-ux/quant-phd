The IPO Anomaly & Rock’s Winner’s Curse

  • Uses: event study, abnormal return, agency costs

  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled

When private corporations issue common stock to public investors for the first time, initial public offerings systematically debut at first-day market closing prices substantially above their final offering price. This initial return constitutes one of corporate finance’s most persistent empirical regularities. Why do issuers and underwriters intentionally sell securities below market clearing value? Kevin Rock (1986) demonstrated that underpricing is an equilibrium requirement generated by informational asymmetry. Uninformed investors face adverse selection: they receive full allotments of overpriced offerings while being rationed in high-value deals. Underpricing compensates uninformed capital for this winner’s curse, enabling primary capital markets to clear.

1Stylized Facts: The First-Day Return Phenomenon

In a firm-commitment initial public offering, an underwriting syndicate purchases newly registered equity from the issuing corporation at a negotiated offer price \(P_0\) (net of the underwriting gross spread) and distributes the shares to subscribing investors. When public secondary market trading commences on the exchange, the closing price on the first trading day \(P_1\) routinely exceeds \(P_0\).

Definition 1.1 (Initial Public Offering Underpricing)

Let \(P_0\) denote the final primary offer price of an initial public offering, and let \(P_1\) denote the closing price on the security’s first day of public secondary exchange trading. The raw first-day return (or initial underpricing) \(R_1\) is defined as

\[ \begin{aligned} R_1 &= \frac{P_1 - P_0}{P_0}. \end{aligned} \]

The market-adjusted abnormal first-day return \(AR_1\) subtracts the return of a broad market index \(R_{m,1}\) over the identical trading interval: \(AR_1 = R_1 - R_{m,1}\).

Across more than five decades of modern equity market history, initial returns are overwhelmingly positive. In their foundational study of 4,753 U.S. IPOs between 1970 and 1990, loughran1995new documented an average first-day raw return of 16.4%. In a comprehensive survey of international capital markets, ljungqvist2007ipo showed that underpricing is ubiquitous across financial jurisdictions: average first-day returns average 16.8% in the United States, 17.4% in the United Kingdom, 27.7% in Germany, 28.4% in Japan, and exceed 100% in emerging equity markets such as China.

2The Rock (1986) Winner’s-Curse Framework

To understand why underpricing persists in competitive capital markets, rock1986new formulated a rational expectations model grounded in heterogeneous investor information. Consider a firm issuing \(S\) shares of common stock at fixed offering price \(P_0\).

Definition 1.2 (Information Structure in Rock’s Model)

The true post-issue intrinsic market value per share \(v\) is a discrete random variable taking one of two states:

\[ \begin{aligned} v \in \{v_L, v_H\}, \quad \text{with } 0 < v_L < v_H, \end{aligned} \]

where \(p = \mathbb{P}(v = v_H) \in (0, 1)\) is the common prior probability of the high state. The unconditional expected intrinsic value is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[v] &= (1 - p) v_L + p v_H. \end{aligned} \]

The market is populated by two classes of risk-neutral market participants:

  1. Informed investors: A group of sophisticated market participants who observe the true state \(v\) prior to submitting their bids. They possess aggregate investment capacity of \(I\) shares.
  2. Uninformed investors: Retail and general institutional participants who observe only the common prior distribution \(\{v_L, v_H, p\}\). They possess aggregate capital budget \(B_U > 0\) dollars.

3Rationing and the Adverse Selection Penalty

Informed investors bid rationally based on their private signal:

  • If \(v = v_L\) (the lemon state), informed investors know that \(v_L < P_0\). Bidding would incur a certain loss of \(v_L - P_0 < 0\) per share. Consequently, informed demand is exactly zero: \(D_{\text{inf}}(v_L) = 0\).
  • If \(v = v_H\) (the peach state), informed investors know that \(v_H > P_0\). Bidding yields a strictly positive profit of \(v_H - P_0 > 0\) per share. Informed investors submit their full capacity: \(D_{\text{inf}}(v_H) = I\).

Uninformed investors submit bids totaling \(D_U = B_U / P_0\) shares. When total subscription demand exceeds the shares offered \(S\), underwriters ration shares on a uniform pro-rata basis.

Theorem 1.1 (State-Contingent Allocation Fractions)

Let \(D_U = B_U / P_0 \ge S\). The allocation fraction \(\alpha(v)\) received by uninformed investors conditional on true firm value \(v\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha(v_L) &= \frac{S}{D_U} = \frac{S P_0}{B_U}, \\ \alpha(v_H) &= \frac{S}{D_U + I} = \frac{S}{(B_U / P_0) + I} < \alpha(v_L). \end{aligned} \]

Proof

In state \(L\), total demand is \(D(v_L) = D_U + 0 = D_U\). By pro-rata rationing, uninformed investors receive fraction \(\alpha(v_L) = \min(1.0, S / D_U) = S / D_U\). In state \(H\), total demand is \(D(v_H) = D_U + I > D_U\). By pro-rata rationing, uninformed investors receive fraction \(\alpha(v_H) = S / (D_U + I)\). Because \(I > 0\), the denominator in state \(H\) is strictly larger than in state \(L\), proving that \(\alpha(v_H) < \alpha(v_L)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.1 (The Economic Winner’s Curse)

Theorem 1.1 formalizes the winner’s curse. Uninformed investors are subject to severe adverse selection: whenever the offering is overpriced (\(v = v_L\)), informed investors abstain and uninformed investors are allocated 100% of the available shares (\(S\) shares). Whenever the offering is underpriced (\(v = v_H\)), informed investors flood the book, and uninformed allocations are heavily rationed to \(\alpha(v_H) \cdot D_U < S\).

4Equilibrium Underpricing Derivation

Risk-neutral uninformed investors participate in the primary market if and only if their expected net dollar payoff across states is non-negative.

Theorem 1.2 (Rock 1986 Equilibrium Offer Price & Underpricing)

Assume free entry of competitive, risk-neutral uninformed capital (\(\mathbb{E}[\pi_U] = 0\)). There exists a unique equilibrium offer price \(P_0^*\) strictly bounded by

\[ \begin{aligned} v_L < P_0^* < \mathbb{E}[v], \end{aligned} \]

satisfying the quadratic condition

\[ \begin{aligned} (1 - p)(v_L - P_0)(B_U + I P_0) + p(v_H - P_0) B_U = 0. \end{aligned} \]

Consequently, initial public offerings must be systematically underpriced in expectation: \(\mathrm{UP} = (\mathbb{E}[v] - P_0^*) / P_0^* > 0\).

Proof

The expected dollar profit of uninformed investors submitting budget \(B_U\) at offer price \(P_0\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\pi_U] &= (1 - p) \alpha(v_L) D_U (v_L - P_0) + p \alpha(v_H) D_U (v_H - P_0). \end{aligned} \]

Substituting \(\alpha(v_L) D_U = S\) and \(\alpha(v_H) D_U = \frac{S \cdot (B_U / P_0)}{(B_U / P_0) + I} = \frac{S B_U}{B_U + I P_0}\) into the zero-profit condition \(\mathbb{E}[\pi_U] = 0\) and dividing by \(S > 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (1 - p)(v_L - P_0) + p \frac{B_U}{B_U + I P_0} (v_H - P_0) &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying across by \((B_U + I P_0) > 0\) produces

\[ \begin{aligned} (1 - p)(v_L - P_0)(B_U + I P_0) + p B_U (v_H - P_0) &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Expanding the algebraic terms in powers of \(P_0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} -(1 - p) I P_0^2 + [(1 - p)(v_L I - B_U) - p B_U] P_0 + B_U \mathbb{E}[v] &= 0. \end{aligned} \]

Define \(f(P) = (1 - p)(v_L - P) + p \frac{B_U}{B_U + I P} (v_H - P)\). Evaluating \(f(P)\) at the boundary points:

  1. At \(P = v_L\): \(f(v_L) = 0 + p \frac{B_U}{B_U + I v_L} (v_H - v_L) > 0\).
  2. At \(P = \mathbb{E}[v]\): Since \((1 - p)(v_L - \mathbb{E}[v]) = -p(v_H - \mathbb{E}[v])\), we have \[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathbb{E}[v]) &= p (v_H - \mathbb{E}[v]) \left[ \frac{B_U}{B_U + I \mathbb{E}[v]} - 1 \right] \\ &= -p (v_H - \mathbb{E}[v]) \frac{I \mathbb{E}[v]}{B_U + I \mathbb{E}[v]} < 0. \end{aligned} \]

Because \(f(P)\) is strictly continuous and strictly decreasing on \([v_L, \mathbb{E}[v]]\), the intermediate value theorem guarantees a unique root \(P_0^* \in (v_L, \mathbb{E}[v])\). Because \(P_0^* < \mathbb{E}[v]\), expected underpricing \((\mathbb{E}[v] - P_0^*) / P_0^*\) is strictly positive. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.1 (Analytical Solution for Rock Equilibrium)

Let \(v_L = \$12.00\), \(v_H = \$28.00\), \(p = 0.60\), \(S = 2{,}000{,}000\) shares, \(I = 2{,}500{,}000\) shares, and \(B_U = \$50{,}000{,}000\). The unconditional expectation is \(\mathbb{E}[v] = 0.40(12) + 0.60(28) = \$21.60\). If the offer price were naively set to \(\mathbb{E}[v] = \$21.60\), uninformed investors would suffer an expected loss of \(\$3{,}987{,}692\) due to rationing. Solving the quadratic equation yields the equilibrium offer price \(P_0^* = \$18.96\), with an equilibrium underpricing of \(13.92\%\), which exactly offsets the winner’s curse.

Lab 1 · Rock Winner’s Curse Underpricing Simulation

Simulating uninformed investor returns across low and high firm value states demonstrates that with zero underpricing (offer price equal to unconditional expected value), uninformed investors suffer severe adverse selection and earn negative expected returns due to rationing in the high state. Setting the offer price to Rock's equilibrium price restores zero expected excess return.

"""CF6 Lesson 1 Inline Lab 1: Rock Winner's Curse Underpricing Simulation.

Holdings to BUILDER_RULES §6: deterministic, self-contained, standard library.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_rock_equilibrium(
    v_l: float,
    v_h: float,
    p: float,
    s_shares: int,
    informed_dem: int,
    uninformed_b: float,
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Compute Rock equilibrium price, rationing probability, and underpricing."""
    e_v = (1.0 - p) * v_l + p * v_h

    def expected_profit(p_test: float) -> float:
        d_u = uninformed_b / p_test
        alloc_l = min(d_u, float(s_shares))
        tot_dem_h = d_u + float(informed_dem)
        alloc_h = (float(s_shares) / tot_dem_h) * d_u if tot_dem_h > float(s_shares) else d_u
        return (1.0 - p) * alloc_l * (v_l - p_test) + p * alloc_h * (v_h - p_test)

    lo, hi = v_l, e_v
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if expected_profit(mid) > 0.0:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid

    p_star = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
    d_u_star = uninformed_b / p_star
    alpha_h = min(1.0, float(s_shares) / (d_u_star + float(informed_dem)))
    underpricing = (e_v - p_star) / p_star
    return p_star, alpha_h, underpricing


def main() -> None:
    # Offering parameters
    v_l = 12.00  # Lemon state value ($)
    v_h = 28.00  # Peach state value ($)
    prob_h = 0.60  # Probability of good state
    s_shares = 2_000_000  # Total shares offered
    informed_dem = 2_500_000  # Informed demand in high state
    uninformed_b = 50_000_000.0  # Uninformed capital budget ($)

    e_v = (1.0 - prob_h) * v_l + prob_h * v_h
    print(f"Unconditional Expected Value E[v]: ${e_v:.2f}")

    # Case 1: Naive pricing at unconditional expected value P0 = E[v]
    p_naive = e_v
    d_u_naive = uninformed_b / p_naive
    alloc_l_naive = min(d_u_naive, float(s_shares))
    alpha_h_naive = min(1.0, float(s_shares) / (d_u_naive + float(informed_dem)))
    alloc_h_naive = alpha_h_naive * d_u_naive

    exp_profit_naive = (1.0 - prob_h) * alloc_l_naive * (v_l - p_naive) + prob_h * alloc_h_naive * (v_h - p_naive)
    print("--- Case 1: Naive Pricing (P0 = E[v] = $21.60) ---")
    print(f"Offer Price:                     ${p_naive:.2f}")
    print(f"Uninformed Low State Alloc:      {alloc_l_naive:,.0f} shares (full allocation)")
    print(f"Uninformed High State Alloc:     {alloc_h_naive:,.0f} shares (rationed to {alpha_h_naive:.1%})")
    print(f"Uninformed Expected Net Profit:  ${exp_profit_naive:,.2f} (WINNER'S CURSE PENALTY)")

    # Case 2: Rock (1986) Equilibrium Pricing
    p_star, alpha_h, up = solve_rock_equilibrium(v_l, v_h, prob_h, s_shares, informed_dem, uninformed_b)
    d_u_star = uninformed_b / p_star
    alloc_l_star = min(d_u_star, float(s_shares))
    alloc_h_star = alpha_h * d_u_star
    exp_profit_star = (1.0 - prob_h) * alloc_l_star * (v_l - p_star) + prob_h * alloc_h_star * (v_h - p_star)
    if abs(exp_profit_star) < 1e-4:
        exp_profit_star = 0.0

    print("--- Case 2: Rock (1986) Equilibrium Pricing ---")
    print(f"Equilibrium Offer Price P*:      ${p_star:.2f}")
    print(f"Equilibrium Underpricing:        {up * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"High State Rationing alpha_H:    {alpha_h:.4f}")
    print(f"Uninformed Expected Net Profit:  ${exp_profit_star:,.2f} (Zero-profit equilibrium)")

Where the canon breaks

While the Rock (1986) winner’s-curse model provides a rigorous microeconomic foundation for positive average underpricing, standard informational models struggle to explain extreme temporal clustering and sentiment swings. During the 1999–2000 internet bubble, median first-day returns exceeded 65%, with dozens of firms gaining 200% to 400% on day one despite negative operating earnings. Furthermore, empirical investigations reveal agency conflicts between issuing managers and underwriters: underwriters may intentionally underprice offerings to allocate hot shares to preferred institutional clients (spinning) or to generate lucrative aftermarket trading commissions, imposing agency costs on pre-IPO shareholders.

Misconceptions

  • IPO underpricing proves that investment bankers are incompetent at valuing growth companies or deliberately leave corporate cash on the table without economic rationale.

    Underpricing is an equilibrium compensation for asymmetric information. Without underpricing, uninformed investors who face rationing in good deals and full allocation in bad deals would exit the primary market, causing capital raising syndicates to fail whenever informed demand is insufficient.

  • Individual retail investors can reliably generate high abnormal portfolio returns by subscribing indiscriminately to every available initial public offering.

    The winner’s curse directly penalizes naive blanket participation. In hot offerings with massive initial returns, retail orders are rationed to negligible fractions; in cold offerings that plunge below the offer price, retail orders are filled in full, yielding negative net returns to uninformed bidding strategies.

  • IPO underpricing is an isolated artifact of U.S. venture-backed technology offerings during speculative equity bubbles.

    As shown in the extensive international meta-analyses by ljungqvist2007ipo and loughran1995new, positive first-day abnormal returns appear across virtually every sovereign exchange, operating sector, and macroeconomic regime spanning over fifty years of modern financial history.

Problem set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An issuing firm prices an IPO at \(P_0 = \$25.00\) per share. At the end of the first trading day, the stock closes at \(P_1 = \$31.25\). Over the same trading day, the market index increases by 1.5%. Calculate the raw first-day return \(R_1\) and the market-adjusted abnormal return \(AR_1\).

Worked solution

The raw initial return is \(R_1 = (P_1 - P_0) / P_0 = (31.25 - 25.00) / 25.00 = 6.25 / 25.00 = 0.2500\) (or 25.00%). The market-adjusted abnormal return is \(AR_1 = R_1 - R_{m,1} = 0.2500 - 0.0150 = 0.2350\) (or 23.50%).

In an IPO offering \(S = 1{,}000{,}000\) shares at \(P_0 = \$20.00\), uninformed investors submit orders totaling \(D_U = 1{,}500{,}000\) shares. If the true value is low (\(v = v_L\)), informed demand is 0. If the true value is high (\(v = v_H\)), informed demand is \(I = 1{,}000{,}000\) shares. Compute the uninformed allocation percentage \(\alpha\) in both states.

Worked solution

In the low state, total demand is \(D_L = D_U = 1{,}500{,}000\) shares. The allocation percentage is \(\alpha_L = S / D_U = 1{,}000{,}000 / 1{,}500{,}000 = 66.67\%\). In the high state, total demand is \(D_H = D_U + I = 1{,}500{,}000 + 1{,}000{,}000 = 2{,}500{,}000\) shares. The allocation percentage is \(\alpha_H = S / D_H = 1{,}000{,}000 / 2{,}500{,}000 = 40.00\%\).

An uninformed investor commits $30,000 to subscribe to the offering in Problem 1.2 at $20.00 per share (requesting 1,500 shares). If \(v_L = \$15.00\) and \(v_H = \$30.00\), calculate the investor's dollar payoff in both states.

Worked solution

In the low state, the investor receives \(\alpha_L \times 1{,}500 = 0.6667 \times 1{,}500 = 1{,}000\) shares. The dollar profit is \(1{,}000 \times (15.00 - 20.00) = -\$5{,}000\). In the high state, the investor receives \(\alpha_H \times 1{,}500 = 0.40 \times 1{,}500 = 600\) shares. The dollar profit is \(600 \times (30.00 - 20.00) = +\$6{,}000\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that in Rock's winner's curse model, if informed demand is zero (\(I = 0\)), the equilibrium offer price satisfies \(P_0^* = \mathbb{E}[v]\) and equilibrium underpricing is exactly zero.

Worked solution

When \(I = 0\), informed investors possess no private information advantage. Total demand in both states is identically \(D_U\). Therefore, allocation fractions are identical: \(\alpha(v_L) = \alpha(v_H) = \min(1, S / D_U) = \alpha\). The zero-profit condition simplifies to \((1 - p)\alpha D_U (v_L - P_0) + p \alpha D_U (v_H - P_0) = 0\). Dividing by \(\alpha D_U > 0\) yields \((1 - p)(v_L - P_0) + p(v_H - P_0) = 0 \iff \mathbb{E}[v] - P_0 = 0 \implies P_0^* = \mathbb{E}[v]\). Equilibrium underpricing is \((\mathbb{E}[v] - P_0^*) / P_0^* = 0\).

Prove that \(\frac{\partial P_0^*}{\partial I} < 0\): increasing the pool of informed capital strictly decreases the equilibrium offer price, intensifying equilibrium underpricing.

Worked solution

Recall the implicit function \(f(P_0, I) = (1 - p)(v_L - P_0) + p \frac{B_U}{B_U + I P_0} (v_H - P_0) = 0\). By the implicit function theorem, \(\frac{\partial P_0}{\partial I} = -\frac{\partial f / \partial I}{\partial f / \partial P_0}\). We have \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial I} = -p \frac{B_U P_0}{(B_U + I P_0)^2} (v_H - P_0) < 0\) because \(v_H > P_0 > 0\). Furthermore, \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial P_0} = -(1 - p) - p \frac{B_U}{B_U + I P_0} - p \frac{B_U I (v_H - P_0)}{(B_U + I P_0)^2} < 0\). Therefore, \(\frac{\partial P_0}{\partial I} = -\frac{(-)}{(-)} < 0\). Higher informed demand lowers the equilibrium offer price and increases underpricing.

Prove that as uninformed capital budget grows without bound (\(B_U \to \infty\)), the equilibrium underpricing does not vanish as long as \(I > 0\) and \(S\) is fixed.

Worked solution

Divide the equilibrium condition \((1 - p)(v_L - P_0) + p \frac{B_U}{B_U + I P_0} (v_H - P_0) = 0\) by \(B_U\). As \(B_U \to \infty\), the ratio \(\frac{B_U}{B_U + I P_0} = \frac{1}{1 + I P_0 / B_U} \to 1\). However, uninformed demand \(D_U = B_U / P_0 \to \infty\), so total uninformed allocations scale as \(S / D_U \to 0\). Uninformed profit per dollar bid is \((1 - p)(S P_0 / B_U)(v_L - P_0) + p \frac{S P_0}{B_U + I P_0}(v_H - P_0) = \frac{S P_0}{B_U}[(1 - p)(v_L - P_0) + p \frac{B_U}{B_U + I P_0}(v_H - P_0)]\). Even as \(B_U \to \infty\), the term inside brackets must be zero, which converges to \(\mathbb{E}[v] - P_0 = 0\) only if informed demand \(I\) is negligible relative to \(B_U\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

In a cross-sectional sample of 500 IPOs, an analyst regresses first-day initial returns on the log of issue size \(\ln(S)\) and the standard deviation of post-IPO equity returns \(\sigma\). What signs do information models predict for the coefficients, and why?

Worked solution

Information theories (Rock 1986; Beatty and Ritter 1986; Ljungqvist 2007) predict a negative coefficient on issue size \(\ln(S)\) and a positive coefficient on return volatility \(\sigma\). Larger issues are typically established firms with more public information, reducing the informed informational advantage. Higher return volatility \(\sigma\) reflects greater ex-ante uncertainty and valuation dispersion, which exacerbates the informed winner’s curse advantage and necessitates greater equilibrium underpricing.

Suppose an institutional fund subscribes to 100 randomly selected IPOs, placing identical dollar orders of $100,000 on each deal. If the fund receives 80% allocation on 40 cold deals (average initial return -8%) and 15% allocation on 60 hot deals (average initial return +35%), calculate the fund's net dollar profit and portfolio percentage return.

Worked solution

On the 40 cold deals, allocated capital is \(40 \times \$100{,}000 \times 0.80 = \$3{,}200{,}000\). The dollar loss is \(\$3{,}200{,}000 \times (-0.08) = -\$256{,}000\). On the 60 hot deals, allocated capital is \(60 \times \$100{,}000 \times 0.15 = \$900{,}000\). The dollar gain is \(\$900{,}000 \times (+0.35) = +\$315{,}000\). Net dollar profit is \(\$315{,}000 - \$256{,}000 = +\$59{,}000\). Total capital deployed is \(\$3{,}200{,}000 + \$900{,}000 = \$4{,}100{,}000\). Portfolio return is \(\$59{,}000 / \$4{,}100{,}000 = +1.44\%\), far below the simple average offering return of \((40 \times -8\% + 60 \times 35\%) / 100 = +17.80\%\).

How do empirical studies adjust for market movements over the first day of trading when calculating IPO underpricing during periods of extreme macroeconomic volatility?

Worked solution

Empirical studies compute market-adjusted abnormal returns \(AR_i = R_{i,1} - R_{m,1}\), where \(R_{m,1}\) is the first-day return of a broad market benchmark (e.g. CRSP value-weighted or S&P 500 index) measured from the offering pricing date to the secondary market close. Alternatively, when beta estimates can be proxied from industry peers, market-model abnormal returns \(AR_i = R_{i,1} - \beta_i R_{m,1}\) are utilized to eliminate systematic index co-movements.

Tier 4 · Research

How can researchers distinguish empirically between Rock's winner's curse explanation of underpricing and the Baron (1982) underwriter agency conflict hypothesis?

Worked solution

Researchers test for institutional rationing patterns and syndicate incentives. Rock’s model implies that uninformed investors are rationed when initial returns are high, so underwriter allocation data (e.g. Cornelli and Goldreich 2001) should show retail rationing in high-demand offerings. The Baron (1982) agency model posits that underwriters underprice to conserve selling effort. This can be tested by examining underwriter reputation league tables, gross spreads, and whether repeat issuers penalize underpricing underwriters by switching banks in subsequent seasoned equity offerings.

During the 1999–2000 internet bubble, Loughran and Ritter (2002) documented that issuing executives often expressed satisfaction despite leaving hundreds of millions of dollars on the table. How does prospect theory resolve this apparent corporate governance paradox?

Worked solution

Loughran and Ritter (2002) proposed a behavioral explanation combining prospect theory and mental accounting. Pre-IPO founders and executives evaluate their wealth changes jointly: the dilution loss from underpricing (leaving money on the table) is psychologically integrated with the immense personal wealth gain created by the unexpected market price surge on their retained equity. Because the gain on retained shares far exceeds the dilution loss, executives feel thrilled and do not discipline underwriters.

Design an empirical test using proprietary bookbuilding bid data to identify whether institutional investors who provide informative limit orders receive higher first-day underpricing rents than non-informative bidders.

Worked solution

Collect institutional bid-level roadshow data containing limit prices, share quantities, and submission timestamps. Classify bids into informative (limit bids above the midpoint of the indicative filing range) and non-informative (strike bids or bids below the midpoint). Estimate a panel regression of investor allocation percentage \(\alpha_{i,j}\) on an indicator for informative bid submission, controlling for total investor book size, past underwriting relationship strength, and deal fixed effects. A positive and statistically significant coefficient on informative bids supports the Benveniste–Spindt information revelation hypothesis.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the primary driver of IPO underpricing in Kevin Rock’s (1986) model?

    Options

    Rock’s model demonstrates that informational asymmetry creates adverse selection (the winner’s curse), requiring underpricing to induce uninformed participation.

  2. In Rock’s model, when the true value of the issuing firm is low (\(v = v_L < P_0\)), how many shares do informed investors demand?

    Options

    Informed investors know the firm is worth less than the offer price; submitting bids would guarantee negative profit, so they submit zero bids.

  3. For parameters \(v_L = 12.00\), \(v_H = 28.00\), \(p = 0.60\), \(S = 2{,}000{,}000\), \(I = 2{,}500{,}000\), and \(B_U = 50{,}000{,}000\), what is the Rock equilibrium offer price \(P_0^*\) in dollars?

    Solving \((1 - p)(v_L - P_0)(B_U + I P_0) + p(v_H - P_0) B_U = 0\) yields \(P_0^* = \$18.96\).

  4. In the same offering (\(\mathbb{E}[v] = \$21.60\), \(P_0^* = \$18.96\)), what is the equilibrium percentage underpricing \((\mathbb{E}[v] - P_0^*) / P_0^*\) in percent?

    \(\mathrm{UP} = (21.60 - 18.96) / 18.96 = 2.64 / 18.96 \approx 13.92\%\).

  5. If uninformed capital budget \(B_U\) decreases, what happens to the equilibrium offer price \(P_0^*\) in Rock's model?

    Options

    A smaller uninformed capital pool increases informed dominance, worsening the winner's curse and requiring deeper underpricing to clear.

  6. According to Loughran and Ritter (1995, Table 1), what was the average initial first-day percentage return for U.S. IPOs between 1970 and 1990?

    Loughran and Ritter (1995, Table 1) report an average initial return of 16.4% across 4,753 offerings.

  7. Why does an increase in ex-ante firm uncertainty increase empirical IPO underpricing?

    Options

    Beatty and Ritter (1986) demonstrated that wider valuation dispersion increases the profit spread between informed and uninformed bidders, requiring higher underpricing.

  8. What is the winner’s curse outcome for a retail investor who submits orders for every IPO irrespective of issue quality?

    Options

    Rationing in good deals and 100% allocation in bad deals dilutes retail portfolio returns to zero or negative values.

  9. An IPO is offered at $20.00 and opens trading at $24.00, closing day one at $24.00. What is the initial raw return in percent?

    \(R_1 = (24.00 - 20.00) / 20.00 = 4.00 / 20.00 = 20.0\%\).

  10. Which market condition leads to the highest observed IPO underpricing historically?

    Options

    Hot issue markets (e.g. 1999–2000 dot-com era) exhibit astronomical initial returns driven by retail sentiment cascades and institutional spinning.
