The New Issues Puzzle: Long-Run Underperformance

  • Uses: IPO underpricing, abnormal return, event study
  • Introduces: long-run IPO underperformance
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or convention

While initial public offerings deliver substantial positive returns to subscribers on their first trading day, their multi-year post-issuance performance tells a starkly contrasting story. Jay Ritter (1991) and Tim Loughran and Jay Ritter (1995) documented that firms issuing equity substantially underperform non-issuing style-matched benchmark peers over the subsequent three to five years. This phenomenon—the new issues puzzle—poses a fundamental challenge to the efficient market hypothesis. Does long-run underperformance reflect behavioral fads and managerial market timing, or does it stem from econometric compounding biases, right-skewed return distributions, and the “bad-model” joint-hypothesis problem? This lesson develops rigorous measurement techniques for long-horizon abnormal performance.

1Empirical Evidence: The New Issues Puzzle

In a seminal paper examining 1,526 U.S. IPOs between 1975 and 1984, ritter1991longrun documented that an investor purchasing an equally weighted portfolio of IPOs at the close of the first day of trading and holding for 36 months earned an average cumulative return of 34.47%, compared to 61.86% for size-matched non-issuing firms.

Definition 3.1 (Long-Run IPO Underperformance)

Long-run IPO underperformance is the empirical regularity wherein equity securities of initial public offerings earn negative risk-adjusted abnormal returns over 36-to-60-month post-issuance holding periods relative to non-issuing benchmark firms matched on market capitalization and book-to-market characteristics.

Expanding the empirical sample to 4,753 IPOs between 1970 and 1990, loughran1995new found that the five-year buy-and-hold return for issuing firms was only 15.7%, compared to 66.4% for non-issuing firms matched on market capitalization. Issuing firms underperformed their benchmarks by an average of 5.1% per year over the five years following the offering.

2Econometric Methodologies: BHAR versus CAR

Measuring abnormal performance over long horizons (3 to 5 years) introduces severe econometric challenges absent in short-horizon event studies. Researchers deploy two primary event-time metrics: Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Returns (BHAR) and Cumulative Abnormal Returns (CAR).

Definition 3.2 (Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Return and Cumulative Abnormal Return)

Let \(R_{i,t}\) denote the return of issuing firm \(i\) in event month \(t \in \{1, \dots, T\}\), and let \(R_{b,t}\) denote the return of its matched benchmark portfolio. The Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Return over horizon \(T\) is defined as

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{BHAR}_{i,0,T} &= \prod_{t=1}^T (1 + R_{i,t}) - \prod_{t=1}^T (1 + R_{b,t}). \end{aligned} \]

The Cumulative Abnormal Return over the identical horizon is defined as

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CAR}_{i,0,T} &= \sum_{t=1}^T (R_{i,t} - R_{b,t}). \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 3.1 (Jensen Divergence between BHAR and CAR)

In general, \(\mathrm{BHAR}_{i,0,T} \neq \mathrm{CAR}_{i,0,T}\). Specifically, under geometric compounding with unequal period returns, \(\mathrm{CAR}\) systematically overstates investor compound returns due to implicit monthly rebalancing: \(\sum_{t=1}^T \ln(1 + R_{i,t}) \le \sum_{t=1}^T R_{i,t}\).

Proof

By mathematical definition, \(\prod_{t=1}^T (1 + R_{i,t}) = \exp\left( \sum_{t=1}^T \ln(1 + R_{i,t}) \right)\). By Taylor series expansion, \(\ln(1 + R) = R - \frac{1}{2} R^2 + \mathcal{O}(R^3)\). Summing across \(T\) periods:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{t=1}^T \ln(1 + R_{i,t}) &\approx \sum_{t=1}^T R_{i,t} - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{t=1}^T R_{i,t}^2. \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\frac{1}{2}\sum R_{i,t}^2 > 0\), the compound return \(\prod(1 + R_{i,t}) - 1\) is strictly reduced by return variance. Because individual stocks exhibit substantially higher return variance than diversified benchmark portfolios (\(\sigma_i^2 \gg \sigma_b^2\)), the variance drag depresses the compound return of individual IPOs relative to CAR, proving that BHAR and CAR diverge systematically over long horizons. \(\blacksquare\)

3The Ritter Wealth Relative Metric

To quantify the economic severity of multi-year underperformance, ritter1991longrun introduced the wealth relative.

Definition 3.3 (Ritter Wealth Relative)

The wealth relative \(WR\) over event horizon \(T\) is the ratio of terminal wealth generated by investing in issuing firms to terminal wealth generated by the matching benchmark:

\[ \begin{aligned} WR &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \prod_{t=1}^T (1 + R_{i,t})}{\sum_{i=1}^N \prod_{t=1}^T (1 + R_{b,t})}. \end{aligned} \]

A wealth relative of \(WR = 1.00\) indicates performance exactly matching the benchmark. A wealth relative \(WR < 1.00\) indicates abnormal underperformance. Ritter (1991) reported a 36-month wealth relative of \(WR = 0.831\) for U.S. IPOs, meaning that every dollar invested in IPOs generated only 83.1 cents compared to a matching non-issuing peer portfolio.

4The Bad-Model Problem and Skewness Bias

Does long-run IPO underperformance prove capital market inefficiency? In an influential critique, Eugene Fama (1998, as surveyed in ljungqvist2007ipo) emphasized that any test of long-horizon abnormal returns is fundamentally a joint test of market efficiency and the underlying asset pricing model (the “bad-model problem”).

Theorem 3.2 (The Joint-Hypothesis Limitation)

Let true expected returns be generated by asset pricing model \(M^*\), and let the researcher estimate abnormal returns using empirical benchmark model \(M\). The estimated abnormal return \(\hat{\alpha}_{0,T}\) satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\alpha}_{0,T} &= \alpha^* + [M^*(X) - M(X)], \end{aligned} \]

where \(\alpha^*\) is true market inefficiency. If \(M \neq M^*\), a finding that \(\hat{\alpha}_{0,T} < 0\) cannot distinguish between true economic underperformance (\(\alpha^* < 0\)) and benchmark mis-specification (\(M^* > M\)).

Furthermore, individual stock returns are severely right-skewed: a few outlier firms (e.g. Amazon, Microsoft) multiply dozens of times, while the majority underperform. Matching an individual stock against a diversified benchmark portfolio creates positive skewness bias, which inflates test statistics and generates spurious statistical rejections under asymptotic normal theory. Skewness-adjusted bootstrapped t-statistics are required for valid statistical inference.

Lab 1 · 36-Month BHAR, Wealth Relative & Bootstrap Significance

Evaluating post-IPO holding period returns over 36 months against style-matched benchmark portfolios reveals whether initial offerings exhibit long-run underperformance. Computing geometric Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Returns (BHAR) and the Ritter wealth relative demonstrates significant compound underperformance.

"""CF6 Lesson 3 Inline Lab 1: Long-Run IPO Performance (BHAR & Wealth Relative).

Holdings to BUILDER_RULES §6: deterministic, self-contained, standard library.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random


def compute_bhar(
    firm_rets: list[float],
    bench_rets: list[float],
) -> dict[str, float]:
    n = len(firm_rets)
    prod_f = 1.0
    prod_b = 1.0
    car = 0.0
    for rf, rb in zip(firm_rets, bench_rets, strict=True):
        prod_f *= 1.0 + rf
        prod_b *= 1.0 + rb
        car += rf - rb

    bhar = prod_f - prod_b
    wr = prod_f / prod_b if prod_b != 0.0 else 0.0
    ann_f = (prod_f ** (12.0 / float(n))) - 1.0
    ann_b = (prod_b ** (12.0 / float(n))) - 1.0

    return {
        "bhar": bhar,
        "wealth_relative": wr,
        "car": car,
        "firm_compounded": prod_f - 1.0,
        "benchmark_compounded": prod_b - 1.0,
        "annualized_abnormal": ann_f - ann_b,
    }


def main() -> None:
    # Seeded simulation matching Ritter (1991) and Loughran & Ritter (1995) empirical findings
    # 36 monthly returns for an IPO cohort vs matching style benchmark
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n_months = 36

    # Simulated IPO returns: initial year positive, subsequent 2 years underperforming
    # Ritter (1991) 3-year cumulative returns: ~34.5% IPO vs ~61.9% matching firm
    ipo_returns = [round(rng.gauss(0.008, 0.055), 4) for _ in range(n_months)]
    bench_returns = [round(rng.gauss(0.013, 0.038), 4) for _ in range(n_months)]

    res = compute_bhar(ipo_returns, bench_returns)

    print("=== 36-Month Long-Run Performance Evaluation ===")
    print(f"IPO 36-Month Compound Return:       {res['firm_compounded'] * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Benchmark 36-Month Compound Return: {res['benchmark_compounded'] * 100:.2f}%\n")
    print(f"Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Return (BHAR): {res['bhar'] * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Cumulative Abnormal Return (CAR):    {res['car'] * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Ritter Wealth Relative (WR):         {res['wealth_relative']:.4f}")
    print(f"Annualized Abnormal Geometric Return:{res['annualized_abnormal'] * 100:.2f}%/year\n")

    # Bootstrap test of H0: BHAR >= 0
    n_boot = 1_000
    boot_diffs = []
    diffs = [f - b for f, b in zip(ipo_returns, bench_returns, strict=True)]
    for _ in range(n_boot):
        sample = [rng.choice(diffs) for _ in range(n_months)]
        boot_diffs.append(sum(sample))

    p_value = sum(1 for d in boot_diffs if d >= 0) / float(n_boot)
    print(f"Bootstrap Test of Zero Abnormal Performance (p-value): {p_value:.3f}")
    if p_value < 0.05:
        print("Verdict: Reject null of market efficiency; significant long-run IPO underperformance.")
    else:
        print("Verdict: Cannot reject null of market efficiency at 5% level.")


main()

Where the canon breaks

When long-run IPO performance is evaluated using calendar-time factor regressions (such as the Fama–French three-factor or five-factor models) rather than event-time BHAR, measured abnormal underperformance often shrinks substantially or loses statistical significance. Brav and Gompers (1997, reviewed in ljungqvist2007ipo) showed that venture-capital-backed IPOs do not underperform when benchmarked against size-and-book-to-market portfolios. Instead, underperformance is concentrated in small, non-venture-backed growth firms with low book-to-market ratios—a characteristic shared by non-issuing small growth stocks. This suggests that long-run IPO underperformance may reflect the broader small-growth anomaly rather than an offering-specific pricing failure.

Misconceptions

  • A company with a massive first-day IPO surge (e.g. +100% underpricing) is fundamentally poised to outperform the market over the subsequent 3 to 5 years.

    Empirical evidence demonstrates the opposite: IPOs with the highest first-day returns often experience the worst subsequent 3-to-5-year stock performance. Extreme initial underpricing frequently reflects peak investor sentiment and retail fads that systematically reverse as post-issue earnings fail to meet inflated expectations.

  • Cumulative Abnormal Returns (CAR) and Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Returns (BHAR) yield identical conclusions in multi-year capital market studies.

    As shown in Theorem 3.1, CAR assumes monthly portfolio rebalancing, which introduces compounding bias and exaggerates performance. BHAR reflects the actual buy-and-hold wealth experience of real investors, and the two metrics frequently diverge in magnitude and statistical significance.

  • Long-run IPO underperformance is definitive proof that investors are irrational and secondary equity markets are fundamentally inefficient.

    Under Fama’s (1998) joint-hypothesis theorem, any multi-year test tests the pricing model alongside efficiency. When calendar-time regressions control for small-growth risk factor loadings, much of the apparent abnormal underperformance disappears.

Problem set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An investor purchases shares of an IPO at the first-day closing price. Over 36 months, the stock compounds to a total holding return of +28.0%. Over the identical 36 months, a style-matched benchmark portfolio compounds to +60.0%. Calculate the Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Return (BHAR) and the Ritter Wealth Relative (WR).

Worked solution

The Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Return is \(\mathrm{BHAR} = R_{\text{firm}} - R_{\text{bench}} = 0.280 - 0.600 = -0.320\) (or \(-32.0\%\)). The Ritter Wealth Relative is \(WR = (1 + R_{\text{firm}}) / (1 + R_{\text{bench}}) = 1.280 / 1.600 = 0.8000\). The IPO investor captured 80 cents for every dollar generated by the matching benchmark.

In a 3-month toy period, an IPO stock earns monthly returns of \(+20\%\), \(-10\%\), and \(+15\%\). Its matched benchmark earns \(+5\%\), \(+5\%\), and \(+5\%\). Calculate both the 3-month BHAR and the 3-month CAR.

Worked solution

For the IPO: \(\prod(1 + R) = (1.20)(0.90)(1.15) = 1.2420\) (return \(+24.20\%\)). For the benchmark: \(\prod(1 + R) = (1.05)^3 = 1.157625\) (return \(+15.76\%\)). Therefore, \(\mathrm{BHAR} = 0.2420 - 0.1576 = +8.44\%\). For CAR: \(\mathrm{CAR} = (0.20 - 0.05) + (-0.10 - 0.05) + (0.15 - 0.05) = 0.15 - 0.15 + 0.10 = +10.00\%\). CAR exceeds BHAR by 1.56%.

If a 60-month IPO portfolio earns a cumulative compounded return of \(+18.0\%\), calculate its annualized geometric compound return.

Worked solution

The annualized geometric return is \(R_{\text{ann}} = (1 + R_{\text{cum}})^{12/60} - 1 = (1.180)^{0.20} - 1 \approx 1.03367 - 1 = 3.37\%\) per year.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for any two positive numbers \(A\) and \(B\), the sign of the Buy-and-Hold Abnormal Return \(\mathrm{BHAR} = A - B\) is identical to the sign of \(\ln(WR)\), where \(WR = A / B\).

Worked solution

Let \(A = 1 + R_i > 0\) and \(B = 1 + R_b > 0\). Then \(\mathrm{BHAR} = A - B\). If \(A > B\), then \(A - B > 0\), and \(WR = A / B > 1\), which implies \(\ln(WR) > 0\). If \(A < B\), then \(A - B < 0\), and \(WR = A / B < 1\), which implies \(\ln(WR) < 0\). If \(A = B\), both \(\mathrm{BHAR} = 0\) and \(\ln(WR) = 0\). Thus \(\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{BHAR}) \equiv \mathrm{sign}(\ln(WR))\).

Prove that if individual stock returns follow a log-normal distribution \(\ln(1 + R_{i,t}) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), the expected compounded terminal wealth \(\mathbb{E}[\prod_{t=1}^T (1 + R_{i,t})]\) is strictly increasing in return volatility \(\sigma^2\).

Worked solution

Let \(X_T = \sum_{t=1}^T \ln(1 + R_{i,t})\). Under independent log-normality, \(X_T \sim \mathcal{N}(T\mu, T\sigma^2)\). The terminal wealth is \(W_T = \exp(X_T)\). By the moment-generating function of the normal distribution, \(\mathbb{E}[W_T] = \mathbb{E}[\exp(X_T)] = \exp(T\mu + \frac{1}{2} T \sigma^2)\). Differentiating with respect to \(\sigma^2\): \(\frac{\partial \mathbb{E}[W_T]}{\partial \sigma^2} = \frac{1}{2} T \exp(T\mu + \frac{1}{2}T\sigma^2) > 0\). Right-skewness from volatility increases mean compound wealth, creating a positive skewness wedge between individual stocks and diversified indices.

Prove that an equally weighted calendar-time portfolio regression eliminates cross-sectional correlation bias in statistical test statistics relative to event-time BHAR t-tests.

Worked solution

In event-time BHAR analysis, overlapping 36-month holding periods for IPOs issued in adjacent months create strong cross-sectional covariance among abnormal returns (\(\mathrm{Cov}(BHAR_i, BHAR_j) \neq 0\)). Standard t-tests assume independence, severely deflating standard errors and inflating t-statistics. In a calendar-time regression, all firms active in calendar month \(\tau\) form a single portfolio with monthly return \(R_{p,\tau}\). Regressing \(R_{p,\tau} - R_{f,\tau}\) on asset pricing factors produces residuals that are cross-sectionally aggregated by construction, completely eliminating cross-sectional correlation bias across firms.

Tier 3 · Empirical

In Loughran and Ritter (1995, Table 3), what was the 5-year buy-and-hold return for seasoned equity offerings (SEOs) compared to their matching non-issuing firms?

Worked solution

In Loughran and Ritter (1995, Table 3), 3,702 seasoned equity offerings between 1970 and 1990 earned an average 5-year buy-and-hold return of 33.4%, compared to 92.8% for non-issuing matching firms of similar size, yielding a wealth relative of \(WR = 1.334 / 1.928 \approx 0.69\). SEOs underperformed by an average of 7% per year over 5 years.

A sample of 250 IPOs has a sample mean 36-month BHAR of -18.5% with sample standard deviation \(s = 65.0\%\). Calculate the standard student t-statistic and discuss why positive return skewness invalidates standard p-values.

Worked solution

The standard error is \(SE = s / \sqrt{N} = 65.0\% / \sqrt{250} = 65.0\% / 15.811 \approx 4.11\%\). The t-statistic is \(t = -18.5\% / 4.11\% \approx -4.50\). Under standard normality, \(p < 0.0001\). However, because stock returns have high positive skewness, the sample mean BHAR distribution is shifted left relative to normal critical values, making negative outcomes appear falsely extreme and requiring a bootstrap or Johnson (1978) skewness correction.

How do Brav and Gompers (1997) demonstrate that venture capital backing mitigates long-run IPO underperformance?

Worked solution

Brav and Gompers (1997) partition IPOs into VC-backed and non-VC-backed cohorts. They find that VC-backed IPOs outperform non-VC-backed IPOs over 5 years. Furthermore, using Fama-French factor regressions, VC-backed IPOs exhibit zero abnormal underperformance (\(\alpha \approx 0\)), whereas non-VC-backed IPOs continue to exhibit negative alpha, demonstrating that reputational monitoring by venture capitalists curbs post-issue underperformance.

Tier 4 · Research

How does Miller's (1977) divergent expectations hypothesis explain both high first-day underpricing and subsequent long-run post-IPO underperformance?

Worked solution

Edward Miller (1977) showed that when investors have heterogeneous valuation beliefs and short-sales are restricted, the market clearing price reflects only the optimism of the most enthusiastic buyers. At the IPO date, valuation uncertainty is maximized, so the most optimistic investors bid prices up, causing high first-day returns. Over the subsequent 3 to 5 years, as public quarterly earnings reports reveal the firm’s true cash flows, valuation divergence collapses, and the stock price drifts downward toward the consensus mean, generating long-run underperformance.

How can an empirical researcher implement a calendar-time Fama-French five-factor regression to test whether post-IPO abnormal returns are driven by the investment and profitability factors?

Worked solution

Form a monthly rolling portfolio of all firms that completed an IPO within the prior 36 months. Compute the portfolio monthly excess return \(R_{p,\tau} - R_{f,\tau}\). Estimate the Fama-French five-factor regression: \(R_{p,\tau} - R_{f,\tau} = \alpha + \beta_{mkt} MKT_\tau + \beta_{smb} SMB_\tau + \beta_{hml} HML_\tau + \beta_{rmw} RMW_\tau + \beta_{cma} CMA_\tau + \varepsilon_\tau\). Because recent IPOs invest aggressively (negative CMA loading) and frequently have low current profitability (negative RMW loading), controlling for these factors tests whether the underperformance alpha \(\alpha\) survives modern multifactor controls.

Design a bootstrap resampling procedure to compute skewness-adjusted p-values for a 36-month BHAR event study on 400 sample firms.

Worked solution

Calculate the sample mean BHAR \(\overline{BHAR}\) and standard t-statistic \(t_{\text{stat}}\). From the non-issuing population, draw 400 matching control firms with replacement matching on size and book-to-market. Compute pseudo-abnormal return \(BHAR_i^b = R_{b1, i} - R_{b2, i}\) across 10,000 bootstrap replications. Construct the empirical distribution of bootstrap t-statistics \(t^b = (\overline{BHAR}^b - 0) / SE^b\). Calculate the empirical p-value as the proportion of bootstrap draws where \(t^b \le t_{\text{stat}}\). This non-parametric test accounts directly for cross-sectional skewness without distributional assumptions.
