Valuation Error Decomposition & Residual Income Synthesis

Valuation models frequently produce wide dispersions in estimated intrinsic value. Practitioners routinely attribute these discrepancies to unpredictable market noise. In reality, valuation dispersion originates from distinct, quantifiable sources: cash flow forecast volatility, cost of capital estimation error, unsustainable terminal growth assumptions, and structural accounting incoherence. Ohlson’s (1995) clean-surplus residual income model bridges financial accounting and neoclassical discounting, demonstrating that book value plus discounted abnormal earnings equals discounted cash flow value. This lesson decomposes valuation variance, proves the clean-surplus equivalence theorem, and equips analysts to stress-test DCF conclusions.

1Taxonomy of valuation error sources

Every discounted cash flow model is a composite function mapping cash flow forecasts, discount rates, and perpetual growth parameters into a scalar valuation estimate. Small perturbations in inputs can generate massive swings in enterprise value.

Definition 6.1 (Dominant Sources of Valuation Error)

The dominant sources of valuation error comprise four structural components that account for empirical dispersion and model misspecification in enterprise and equity valuation Damodaran (2012):

  1. Cash flow forecast error: Uncertainty in revenue projections, operating profit margins, working capital efficiency, and capital expenditure intensity.
  2. Discount rate error: Parameter estimation uncertainty in beta, equity risk premium (ERP), and credit spreads that dictate WACC and cost of equity.
  3. Terminal value bias: Overestimating perpetual growth rate \(g\) or assuming perpetual returns on capital that exceed cost of capital in a competitive economy.
  4. Articulation inconsistency: Failure to align balance sheet reinvestment, debt schedules, and clean-surplus equity growth with reported cash flows.

Consider the perpetual capitalization equation for enterprise or equity value under steady-state growth \(g\) and discount rate \(r > g\):

\[ \begin{aligned} V_0(CF, r, g) &= \frac{CF_1}{r - g}. \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 6.1 (First-Order Valuation Variance Decomposition)

Let cash flow \(CF_1\), discount rate \(r\), and terminal growth \(g\) be independent random variables with means \((\overline{CF}_1, \overline{r}, \overline{g})\) and variances \((\sigma_{CF}^2, \sigma_r^2, \sigma_g^2)\). The first-order Taylor expansion variance of valuation \(V_0\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(V_0) &\approx J_{CF}^2 \sigma_{CF}^2 \\ &\quad + J_r^2 \sigma_r^2 \\ &\quad + J_g^2 \sigma_g^2, \end{aligned} \]

where the Jacobians evaluated at the mean are:

\[ \begin{aligned} J_{CF} &= \frac{1}{\overline{r} - \overline{g}}, \\ J_r &= -\frac{\overline{CF}_1}{(\overline{r} - \overline{g})^2}, \\ J_g &= \frac{\overline{CF}_1}{(\overline{r} - \overline{g})^2}. \end{aligned} \]

Remark 6.1 (Duration and Error Amplification)

The modified duration of a perpetuity with growth \(g\) is \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{mod}} = 1 / (r - g)\). When \(r - g\) narrows (for instance, when \(r = 8.0\%\) and \(g = 3.0\%\), so \(r - g = 5.0\%\)), duration equals 20.0 years. A mere 50 basis point (0.50%) change in discount rate or terminal growth induces a \(\pm 10.0\%\) shift in intrinsic value. Consequently, terminal parameter errors dominate cash flow errors for high-growth and long-duration assets.

2Ohlson (1995) clean-surplus residual income valuation

Financial analysts often struggle with the disconnect between book equity reported under financial accounting rules and market equity value derived from discounted cash flow models. Ohlson (1995) resolved this dichotomy by establishing a formal link between accounting earnings, book value, and equity valuation.

Definition 6.2 (Clean Surplus Relation)

Accounting book equity satisfies the Clean Surplus Relation (CSR) if all changes in book equity between time \(t-1\) and time \(t\) equal comprehensive net income minus net dividends (distributions to equity holders less equity contributions) Ohlson (1995):

\[ \begin{aligned} B_t &= B_{t-1} + NI_t - Div_t, \end{aligned} \]

where \(B_t\) is ending book value of equity, \(NI_t\) is net income for period \(t\), and \(Div_t\) is total net dividends paid at period \(t\).

Definition 6.3 (Residual Income / Abnormal Earnings)

Residual income \(RI_t\) (also termed abnormal earnings) is the net income earned in period \(t\) in excess of the normal return on beginning book equity capital evaluated at the cost of equity \(r_e\):

\[ \begin{aligned} RI_t &= NI_t - r_e B_{t-1} \\ &= (ROE_t - r_e) B_{t-1}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(ROE_t = NI_t / B_{t-1}\) represents the accounting return on beginning book equity.

Theorem 6.2 (Ohlson Residual Income Valuation Formula)

Assuming the Clean Surplus Relation holds and provided the transversality condition \(\lim_{T \to \infty} (1+r_e)^{-T} B_T = 0\) is satisfied, the intrinsic equity value \(V_0\) equals current book equity plus the infinite present value of expected future residual income discounted at the cost of equity Ohlson (1995):

\[ \begin{aligned} V_0 &= B_0 \\ &\quad + \sum_{t=1}^\infty \frac{RI_t}{(1 + r_e)^t}. \end{aligned} \]

3Equivalence proof: DCF and Ohlson residual income

The mathematical identity between dividend discount (or articulated FCFE) DCF and Ohlson residual income is one of the most powerful results in modern corporate valuation. We present the direct telescopic sum proof.

Proof of Theorem 6.2 (Telescoping Equivalence)

From the Clean Surplus Relation (Definition 6.2), we isolate dividends for each period \(t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Div_t &= NI_t - (B_t - B_{t-1}). \end{aligned} \]

Substituting \(NI_t = RI_t + r_e B_{t-1}\) into the dividend expression yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} Div_t &= RI_t + r_e B_{t-1} \\ &\quad - B_t + B_{t-1} \\ &= RI_t + (1 + r_e)B_{t-1} - B_t. \end{aligned} \]

We now discount \(Div_t\) at \((1 + r_e)^t\) and sum from \(t = 1\) to finite horizon \(T\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{Div_t}{(1 + r_e)^t} &= \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{RI_t}{(1 + r_e)^t} \\ &\quad + \sum_{t=1}^T \left[ \frac{B_{t-1}}{(1 + r_e)^{t-1}} - \frac{B_t}{(1 + r_e)^t} \right]. \end{aligned} \]

Observe that the second summation on the right-hand side is a classic telescoping series:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{t=1}^T \left[ \frac{B_{t-1}}{(1 + r_e)^{t-1}} - \frac{B_t}{(1 + r_e)^t} \right] &= B_0 - \frac{B_T}{(1 + r_e)^T}. \end{aligned} \]

Hence, for any finite horizon \(T\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{Div_t}{(1 + r_e)^t} + \frac{B_T}{(1 + r_e)^T} &= B_0 + \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{RI_t}{(1 + r_e)^t}. \end{aligned} \]

Taking the limit as \(T \to \infty\), under the transversality condition \(\lim_{T \to \infty} (1+r_e)^{-T} B_T = 0\), the boundary term vanishes, yielding:

\[ \begin{aligned} V_{\text{DCF}} &= B_0 \\ &\quad + \sum_{t=1}^\infty \frac{RI_t}{(1 + r_e)^t} \\ &= V_{\text{Ohlson}}. \quad \blacksquare \end{aligned} \]

Warning 6.1 (Articulating Terminal Dividends and Book Equity)

In a finite-horizon DCF model with explicit horizon \(H\) and terminal growth \(g\), DCF and Ohlson will only match if terminal dividends reflect clean-surplus reinvestment: \(Div_{H+1} = NI_{H+1} - g B_H\). Setting terminal dividend arbitrarily to \(Div_H (1+g)\) violates clean-surplus balance sheet growth (\(B_{H+1} = B_H(1+g)\)) and breaks mathematical equivalence.

Lab 1 · Valuation error decomposition & Ohlson clean-surplus equivalence

Quantifies the dominant variance contributors to valuation error (demonstrating that discount rate and terminal growth parameter uncertainty together account for >60% of valuation variance) and numerically verifies the exact algebraic equivalence of Dividend DCF and Ohlson Residual Income to within 1e-12 machine precision.

"""Lab CF4-L06-1: Valuation Error Decomposition and Ohlson (1995) Clean-Surplus Equivalence.

Quantifies the dominant variance contributors to valuation error and demonstrates the
exact mathematical equivalence of DCF and Ohlson (1995) Residual Income valuation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def ohlson_vs_dcf_equivalence(
    b0: float,
    earnings: np.ndarray,
    dividends: np.ndarray,
    cost_of_equity: float,
    terminal_growth: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Demonstrate exact equivalence between Dividend Discount / FCFE and Ohlson RI."""
    r_e = cost_of_equity
    g = terminal_growth
    h = len(earnings)

    # 1. Clean surplus balance sheet progression: B_t = B_{t-1} + NI_t - Div_t
    b = np.zeros(h + 1, dtype=np.float64)
    b[0] = b0
    for t in range(h):
        b[t + 1] = b[t] + earnings[t] - dividends[t]

    # 2. Residual income sequence: RI_t = NI_t - r_e * B_{t-1}
    ri = np.zeros(h, dtype=np.float64)
    for t in range(h):
        ri[t] = earnings[t] - r_e * b[t]

    # 3. Discount explicit periods
    periods = np.arange(1, h + 1, dtype=np.float64)
    dfs = 1.0 / np.power(1.0 + r_e, periods)

    pv_divs_explicit = np.sum(dividends * dfs)
    pv_ri_explicit = np.sum(ri * dfs)

    # 4. Terminal values under clean-surplus long-run growth g:
    # In terminal state, book equity grows at g: B_{H+1} = B_H * (1 + g)
    # NI_{H+1} = NI_H * (1 + g)
    # Articulated terminal dividend: Div_{H+1} = NI_{H+1} - g * B_H
    # Articulated terminal residual income: RI_{H+1} = NI_{H+1} - r_e * B_H
    ni_next = earnings[-1] * (1.0 + g)
    div_next = ni_next - g * b[-1]
    ri_next = ni_next - r_e * b[-1]

    tv_div = div_next / (r_e - g)
    tv_ri = ri_next / (r_e - g)

    pv_div_terminal = tv_div / ((1.0 + r_e) ** h)
    pv_ri_terminal = tv_ri / ((1.0 + r_e) ** h)

    v_dividend_dcf = pv_divs_explicit + pv_div_terminal
    v_ohlson = b0 + pv_ri_explicit + pv_ri_terminal

    return {
        "v_dcf": float(v_dividend_dcf),
        "v_ohlson": float(v_ohlson),
        "b0": float(b0),
        "b_h": float(b[-1]),
        "pv_ri_explicit": float(pv_ri_explicit),
        "pv_ri_terminal": float(pv_ri_terminal),
        "absolute_difference": float(abs(v_ohlson - v_dividend_dcf)),
    }


def valuation_error_variance_breakdown(
    base_cf: float,
    base_wacc: float,
    base_g: float,
    sigma_cf_pct: float = 0.10,
    sigma_wacc_bps: float = 75.0,  # 75 bps
    sigma_g_bps: float = 40.0,  # 40 bps
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Decompose valuation variance into cash flow, WACC, and growth components."""
    # V = CF / (WACC - g)
    v0 = base_cf / (base_wacc - base_g)

    # Sensitivities (Jacobians)
    j_cf = 1.0 / (base_wacc - base_g)
    j_wacc = -base_cf / ((base_wacc - base_g) ** 2)
    j_g = base_cf / ((base_wacc - base_g) ** 2)

    var_cf = (base_cf * sigma_cf_pct) ** 2
    var_wacc = (sigma_wacc_bps / 10000.0) ** 2
    var_g = (sigma_g_bps / 10000.0) ** 2

    contr_cf = (j_cf**2) * var_cf
    contr_wacc = (j_wacc**2) * var_wacc
    contr_g = (j_g**2) * var_g
    total_var = contr_cf + contr_wacc + contr_g

    return {
        "v0": float(v0),
        "pct_cashflow_error": float(contr_cf / total_var * 100.0),
        "pct_wacc_error": float(contr_wacc / total_var * 100.0),
        "pct_growth_error": float(contr_g / total_var * 100.0),
        "total_standard_error": float(np.sqrt(total_var)),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("CF4 Lab L06-1: Valuation Error Sources & Ohlson Equivalence")
    print("==========================================================")

    # Part 1: Valuation Error Variance Breakdown
    print("--- Part 1: Dominant Sources of Valuation Error ---")
    err = valuation_error_variance_breakdown(
        base_cf=100.0,
        base_wacc=0.085,
        base_g=0.025,
        sigma_cf_pct=0.12,  # 12% cash flow uncertainty
        sigma_wacc_bps=80.0,  # 80 bps discount rate uncertainty
        sigma_g_bps=50.0,  # 50 bps terminal growth uncertainty
    )
    print(f"Base Capitalized Value:        ${err['v0']:.2f}M")
    print(f"Estimated Standard Error:      ${err['total_standard_error']:.2f}M")
    print(f"Contribution of Cash Flow Error:    {err['pct_cashflow_error']:.2f}%")
    print(f"Contribution of WACC Error:         {err['pct_wacc_error']:.2f}%")
    print(f"Contribution of Terminal Growth:    {err['pct_growth_error']:.2f}%\n")

    # Part 2: Ohlson (1995) Clean-Surplus Equivalence
    print("--- Part 2: Ohlson Clean-Surplus Residual Income Equivalence ---")
    b0 = 400.0
    earnings = np.array([60.0, 68.0, 76.0, 85.0, 95.0])
    dividends = np.array([20.0, 24.0, 28.0, 32.0, 38.0])
    r_e = 0.10
    g = 0.03

    res = ohlson_vs_dcf_equivalence(b0, earnings, dividends, r_e, g)
    print(f"Initial Book Equity (B0):      ${res['b0']:.2f}M")
    print(f"Terminal Book Equity (B5):     ${res['b_h']:.2f}M")
    print(f"PV of Explicit Residual Income:${res['pv_ri_explicit']:.2f}M")
    print(f"PV of Terminal Residual Income:${res['pv_ri_terminal']:.2f}M")
    print(f"Total Ohlson Equity Value:     ${res['v_ohlson']:.4f}M")
    print(f"Total Dividend DCF Value:      ${res['v_dcf']:.4f}M")
    print(f"Absolute Difference:           {res['absolute_difference']:.2e}")
    assert res["absolute_difference"] < 1e-10
    print("Exact algebraic and numerical equivalence verified: True")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

Textbook valuation treatments frequently present DCF and residual income as competing or disparate models, leading practitioners to pick whichever model produces a desired target price. In truth, under clean surplus, any valuation divergence is purely an artifact of mathematical inconsistency in terminal balance sheet articulation: assuming a dividend payout or reinvestment rate in terminal year \(H+1\) that fails to satisfy \(B_{H+1} = B_H (1+g)\). Furthermore, in practical accounting under US GAAP and IFRS, Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) items—such as foreign exchange adjustments and unrealized pension losses—bypass net income while altering book equity, requiring rigorous analyst adjustments to preserve clean surplus equivalence Ohlson (1995).

5Misconceptions

  • Residual income valuation produces higher equity value than DCF because it starts by adding current book equity \(B_0\).

    False. Future residual income \(RI_t = NI_t - r_e B_{t-1}\) is net of a capital charge on book value. For firms earning below their cost of equity (\(ROE < r_e\)), residual income is negative, pulling intrinsic value below book equity. When clean surplus holds, both models yield identical intrinsic value.

  • Terminal perpetual growth rate \(g\) can be chosen freely based on management's optimistic long-range projections.

    False. In economic equilibrium, no corporate entity can perpetually grow faster than the aggregate economy without eventually exceeding 100% of global GDP. Long-run growth \(g\) should strictly be capped at the long-run nominal GDP growth rate (2.0% to 3.5%).

  • Accounting conservatism (e.g. accelerated depreciation or immediate expensing of R&D) depresses intrinsic equity value.

    False. Conservative accounting reduces initial book equity \(B_0\) and short-term earnings, but smaller book equity proportionally lowers future equity capital charges \(r_e B_{t-1}\). The elevated future residual income exactly offsets the lower initial book value in present value terms.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Capitalized valuation sensitivity

A firm generates steady-state cash flow \(CF = \$100.0\text{M}\), with cost of capital \(WACC = 9.0\%\) and perpetual growth \(g = 3.0\%\). Compute the capitalized enterprise value and the Jacobian sensitivity \(\partial V / \partial WACC\).

Solution

Base enterprise value is \(V_0 = 100.0 / (0.09 - 0.03) = 100.0 / 0.06 = \$1{,}666.67\) million dollars. The Jacobian sensitivity is \(\partial V / \partial WACC = -CF / (WACC - g)^2 = -100.0 / (0.06)^2 = -27{,}777.78\) million dollars per unit of WACC, or \(-277.78\) million dollars per 100 bps.

M2 · Single-period residual income

A company starts with book equity \(B_0 = \$500.0\text{M}\). In year 1, it earns net income \(NI_1 = \$80.0\text{M}\). The cost of equity is \(r_e = 10.0\%\). Compute year 1 residual income \(RI_1\).

Solution

The normal equity capital charge is \(r_e \times B_0 = 0.10 \times 500.0 = \$50.0\) million dollars. Residual income is \(RI_1 = NI_1 - r_e B_0 = 80.0 - 50.0 = \$30.0\) million dollars.

M3 · Valuation variance breakdown

Using the parameters of Problem M1, suppose cash flow standard error is \(\sigma_{CF} = \$10.0\text{M}\) and WACC standard error is \(\sigma_{WACC} = 0.005\) (50 bps), with \(\sigma_g = 0\). Compute total variance and the percentage contribution of WACC uncertainty.

Solution

Cash flow variance contribution is \(J_{CF}^2 \sigma_{CF}^2 = (1 / 0.06)^2 \times (10.0)^2 = 277.78 \times 100.0 = 27{,}777.78\). WACC contribution is \(J_W^2 \sigma_W^2 = (-27{,}777.78)^2 \times (0.005)^2 = 7.716 \times 10^8 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-5} = 19{,}290.12\). Total variance is \(47{,}067.90\). The percentage contribution of WACC uncertainty is \(19{,}290.12 / 47{,}067.90 = 40.98\%\).

Proof

P1 · Transversality condition necessity

Prove that if the transversality condition \(\lim_{T \to \infty} (1+r_e)^{-T} B_T \ne 0\), the Ohlson residual income model diverges from the dividend discount model by exactly that asymptotic boundary value.

Solution

From the finite-horizon telescopic derivation, \(\sum_{t=1}^T (1+r_e)^{-t} Div_t + (1+r_e)^{-T} B_T = B_0 + \sum_{t=1}^T (1+r_e)^{-t} RI_t\). Rearranging gives \(V_{\text{DCF}} - V_{\text{Ohlson}} = -\lim_{T \to \infty} (1+r_e)^{-T} B_T\). If book value expands at a rate strictly exceeding the cost of equity, this terminal debt/equity bubble creates a non-zero divergence, proving transversality is both necessary and sufficient. \(\blacksquare\)

P2 · Accounting policy invariance

Prove that shifting accounting earnings between two periods under clean surplus leaves total Ohlson equity value strictly invariant.

Solution

Suppose an accounting change decreases period 1 net income by \(\Delta\) and increases period 2 net income by \(\Delta (1+r_e)\). Under clean surplus, \(B_1\) falls by \(\Delta\), which reduces the period 2 capital charge by \(r_e \Delta\). Hence \(RI_2\) changes by \(\Delta(1+r_e) - (-r_e \Delta) = \Delta(1+2r_e)\). Discounting these shifts yields \(-\Delta/(1+r_e) + \Delta/(1+r_e) = 0\), leaving intrinsic equity value completely invariant. \(\blacksquare\)

P3 · Ratio of sensitivities to terminal parameters

Prove that for any Gordon growth perpetuity \(V = CF / (r - g)\), the elasticity with respect to discount rate \(r\) equals the negative elasticity with respect to growth rate \(g\) when evaluated relative to the spread \(r - g\).

Solution

Let \(\varepsilon_r = (\partial V / \partial r)(r / V) = [-V / (r - g)](r / V) = -r / (r - g)\). Similarly, \(\varepsilon_g = (\partial V / \partial g)(g / V) = [V / (r - g)](g / V) = g / (r - g)\). Normalizing by the respective rates gives \(\frac{1}{V}\frac{\partial V}{\partial r} = -1 / (r - g)\) and \(\frac{1}{V}\frac{\partial V}{\partial g} = 1 / (r - g)\). The semi-elasticities are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

E1 · Analyst forecast bias in long-term earnings

Examine empirical literature on equity analyst forecast bias and its distorting impact on discounted cash flow target prices.

Solution

Empirical accounting studies consistently document that sell-side equity analysts exhibit optimistic bias in 3- to 5-year earnings growth projections, averaging 200 to 400 basis points above realized earnings. Incorporating unadjusted consensus forecasts directly into DCF models inflates explicit cash flows and leads to systemic equity overvaluation.

E2 · Cost of capital variation across economic cycles

Analyze how assuming a static time-invariant WACC across expansionary and recessionary regimes creates valuation errors.

Solution

In economic downturns, credit spreads widen dramatically and the equity risk premium elevates, raising instantaneous discount rates. Assuming a static cost of capital understates discount rates during distressed cyclical troughs and overstates them during liquidity-rich macro peaks, mispricing cyclical corporate equities.

E3 · Clean surplus violations under IFRS and US GAAP

Identify major accounting line items under modern GAAP/IFRS that bypass the income statement and violate clean surplus, and explain their valuation remedies.

Solution

Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) items—including foreign currency translation adjustments, unrealized gains/losses on available-for-sale securities, and defined-benefit pension actuarial adjustments—bypass net income while altering book equity. Analysts must use comprehensive income rather than net income to preserve clean surplus equivalence.

Research

R1 · Information dynamics in Ohlson (1995)

Describe Ohlson’s linear information dynamics \(v_t\) and explain how non-accounting other information governs the persistence of abnormal earnings.

Solution

Ohlson introduces non-accounting information \(v_t\) via autoregressive dynamics: \(RI_{t+1} = \omega RI_t + v_t + \varepsilon_{1,t+1}\) and \(v_{t+1} = \gamma v_t + \varepsilon_{2,t+1}\), with persistence parameters \(\omega, \gamma \in [0, 1)\). This allows market prices to incorporate forward-looking indicators (such as order backlogs and patent filings) before they appear in recognized financial statements.

R2 · Multi-factor discount rate uncertainty

Formulate a Bayesian framework for valuing equities when the true systematic risk premium and asset factor loadings are subject to parameter estimation error.

Solution

In a Bayesian valuation model, the discount rate is treated as a posterior distribution \(p(r \mid \mathcal{D})\). Because valuation is a convex function of \(r\), Jensen’s inequality dictates \(\mathbb{E}[V(r)] > V(\mathbb{E}[r])\). Point-estimate DCF using mean WACC understates expected valuation under parameter uncertainty, requiring Monte Carlo integration over the posterior parameter space.

R3 · Reverse DCF and implied expectations

Evaluate the methodology of reverse engineering market prices into implied consensus growth rates and return on incremental capital.

Solution

Rather than forecasting cash flows forward to derive value, reverse DCF inverts the pricing operator: given current market enterprise value \(EV_{\text{mkt}}\) and WACC, the analyst numerically solves for the implied growth rate \(g_{\text{implied}}\) or fade rate. This transforms subjective forecasting into an empirical test of market consensus plausibility.
