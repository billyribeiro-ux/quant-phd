The Cost of Capital: The WACC Identity

  • Uses: capm, security market line, equity risk premium, present value, annuity, discount factor
  • Introduces: weighted average cost of capital (WACC)
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or convention
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A corporation funds operating assets through a combination of equity and debt claims. Valuing the firm’s aggregate operating cash flows requires a hurdle rate reflecting the blended opportunity cost of all capital providers. The weighted average cost of capital blends required returns using current market weights while incorporating corporate tax deductions on interest. This lesson establishes the formal WACC identity, proves weight summation, derives required returns from market equilibrium, and identifies the constant-leverage boundaries required for consistent enterprise discounting.

1Capital claims and market weights

Consider a firm with productive assets financed entirely by two classes of financial securities: common equity with current market value \(E > 0\) and financial debt with current market value \(D \ge 0\). Total enterprise capital market value is

\[ V = E + D. \]

Corporate financial obligations require periodic debt interest payments. Let \(\tau_c \in [0, 1)\) denote the statutory marginal corporate income tax rate, and let \(r_D\) denote the pre-tax required return on debt. Because interest expenses are deductible from corporate taxable earnings, each dollar of interest paid generates a tax deduction of \(\tau_c\), producing an effective after-tax cost of debt of \(r_D (1 - \tau_c)\).

Definition 1.1 (weighted average cost of capital)

Assuming capital weights evaluated at market values (Assumption A1) and constant debt-to-equity leverage (Assumption A2), the weighted average cost of capital (\(r_{\mathrm{WACC}}\)) is

\[ \begin{aligned} r_{\mathrm{WACC}} &= w_E r_E + w_D r_D (1 - \tau_c) \\ &= \frac{E}{V} r_E + \frac{D}{V} r_D (1 - \tau_c), \end{aligned} \]

where \(w_E = E/V\) is the market-value equity weight, \(w_D = D/V\) is the market-value debt weight, \(r_E\) is the required cost of equity, and \(r_D\) is the pre-tax cost of debt.

Proposition 1.1 (convex combination of financing costs)

Let market weights be defined by \(w_E = E/V\) and \(w_D = D/V\) for \(E > 0, D \ge 0\). Then \(w_E + w_D = 1\), and \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}}\) is a convex combination of \(r_E\) and the after-tax cost of debt \(r_D(1 - \tau_c)\).

Proof

By definition, \(V = E + D\). Dividing both sides by \(V\) yields \(E/V + D/V = w_E + w_D = 1\). Since \(E > 0\) and \(D \ge 0\), we have \(w_E \in (0, 1]\) and \(w_D \in [0, 1)\). Hence \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}}\) is strictly a convex combination of \(r_E\) and \(r_D(1 - \tau_c)\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Component costs of capital

The cost of equity \(r_E\) represents the required rate of return demanded by common shareholders given the systematic risk of the firm’s equity cash flows. Under the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), equity cost is determined along the security market line:

\[ r_E = r_f + \beta_L \cdot \mathrm{ERP}, \]

where \(r_f\) is the risk-free rate, \(\beta_L\) is the levered equity beta, and \(\mathrm{ERP} = \E[r_m] - r_f\) is the equity risk premium.

The pre-tax cost of debt \(r_D\) is the effective market yield that equates the market value of the firm’s debt to the present value of promised contract interest and principal repayments:

\[ r_D = r_f + s, \]

where \(s\) is the corporate debt credit spread over the risk-free benchmark.

Example 1.2 (exact rational WACC calculation)

Consider a firm with market value of equity \(E = 60{,}000{,}000\) dollars and debt \(D = 40{,}000{,}000\) dollars, giving enterprise value \(V = 100{,}000{,}000\) dollars. Capital weights are \(w_E = 60/100 = 3/5\) and \(w_D = 40/100 = 2/5\). Suppose the cost of equity is \(r_E = 10\% = 1/10\), pre-tax debt yield is \(r_D = 6\% = 3/50\), and corporate tax rate is \(\tau_c = 25\% = 1/4\).

The after-tax cost of debt is

\[ r_D(1 - \tau_c) = \frac{3}{50} \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{3}{50} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{9}{200} = 4.50\%. \]

Exact WACC is

\[ r_{\mathrm{WACC}} = \frac{3}{5}\left(\frac{1}{10}\right) + \frac{2}{5}\left(\frac{9}{200}\right) = \frac{3}{50} + \frac{9}{500} = \frac{39}{500} = 7.80\%. \]

Lab 1 · The WACC identity and market-value weights

Verifies the exact rational WACC identity using fractions.Fraction, checks that weights sum strictly to one, and tabulates WACC sensitivity to capital structure weights.

"""CF2 lab L01-1: Exact weighted average cost of capital (WACC) identity.

Model: Exact closed-form capital structure valuation using fractions.Fraction.
No synthetic or simulated data is used; all parameters are exact rational numbers.
Demonstrates that market-value weights sum to unity and WACC equals the weighted
average of the cost of equity and after-tax cost of debt.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def calculate_wacc(
    equity: Fraction,
    debt: Fraction,
    cost_equity: Fraction,
    cost_debt: Fraction,
    tax_rate: Fraction,
) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction, Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Compute (weight_equity, weight_debt, after_tax_cost_debt, wacc) exactly."""
    total_val = equity + debt
    w_e = equity / total_val
    w_d = debt / total_val
    after_tax_rd = cost_debt * (Fraction(1, 1) - tax_rate)
    r_wacc = w_e * cost_equity + w_d * after_tax_rd
    return w_e, w_d, after_tax_rd, r_wacc


def main() -> None:
    print("CF2 Lab L01-1: The WACC Identity and Market-Value Weights")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------")

    # Stated capital structure in integer dollars
    equity = Fraction(60_000_000, 1)
    debt = Fraction(40_000_000, 1)
    r_e = Fraction(10, 100)  # 10.0%
    r_d = Fraction(6, 100)  # 6.0%
    tau_c = Fraction(25, 100)  # 25.0%

    w_e, w_d, after_tax_rd, r_wacc = calculate_wacc(equity, debt, r_e, r_d, tau_c)

    print(f"Equity market value: ${equity:,.2f}")
    print(f"Debt market value:   ${debt:,.2f}")
    print(f"Total value (V):     ${(equity + debt):,.2f}")
    print(f"Equity weight (w_E): {w_e} = {float(w_e):.4f}")
    print(f"Debt weight (w_D):   {w_d} = {float(w_d):.4f}")
    print(f"Weights sum to one:  {w_e + w_d == Fraction(1, 1)}")
    print(f"Pre-tax cost of debt:       {r_d} = {float(r_d):.4%}")
    print(f"After-tax cost of debt:     {after_tax_rd} = {float(after_tax_rd):.4%}")
    print(f"Cost of equity:             {r_e} = {float(r_e):.4%}")
    print(f"Exact WACC:                 {r_wacc} = {float(r_wacc):.4%}")

    print("\nWACC sensitivity to leverage (holding component costs constant):")
    for d_pct in [0, 20, 40, 50, 60, 80]:
        d_val = Fraction(d_pct * 1_000_000, 1)
        e_val = Fraction((100 - d_pct) * 1_000_000, 1)
        _, _, _, w = calculate_wacc(e_val, d_val, r_e, r_d, tau_c)
        print(f"  Debt share {d_pct:2d}%: WACC = {float(w):.4%} ({w})")


main()

3Where the canon breaks

The standard textbook WACC equation assumes constant leverage \(D/V\) over time (Assumption A2). When a firm issues lumpy debt or undertakes milestone-based project financing, the debt-to-value ratio varies over project life. Discounting variable-leverage cash flows with a static WACC introduces systematic valuation errors: it overstates tax shields when leverage is low and understates financial distress risk when debt spikes.

4Misconceptions

  • Book values from the corporate balance sheet should be used for capital weights because market prices fluctuate daily.

    False. Cost of capital measures the opportunity cost of capital in current market equilibrium. Historical accounting book equity reflects past retained earnings, not the current economic value of investor claims.

  • WACC is the simple sum of the cost of equity and cost of debt: \(r_E + r_D\).

    False. WACC is a weighted average of component costs, with weights equal to each claim’s proportional share of total market value, and debt cost reduced by the tax deduction factor \((1 - \tau_c)\).

  • Because debt is cheaper than equity, increasing leverage always reduces WACC toward the cost of debt.

    False. As leverage rises, financial risk to equity increases, driving up the required cost of equity \(r_E\). Beyond prudent thresholds, bankruptcy distress costs drive up debt yields as well.

5Problems

Mechanics

M1 · Hand calculation of WACC

A firm has 70 million dollars in equity and 30 million dollars in debt. Cost of equity is 11.0%, pre-tax debt yield is 5.0%, and corporate tax rate is 20.0%. Compute the market weights and WACC to two decimal places.

Solution

Total value \(V = 70 + 30 = 100\) million. Market weights are \(w_E = 70/100 = 0.70\) and \(w_D = 30/100 = 0.30\). After-tax debt cost is \(5.0\% \times (1 - 0.20) = 4.0\%\). WACC is \(0.70 \times 11.0\% + 0.30 \times 4.0\% = 7.70\% + 1.20\% = 8.90\%\).

M2 · Impact of tax rate changes

For the firm in M1, suppose the corporate tax rate rises from 20.0% to 35.0%. Find the new after-tax cost of debt and the resulting change in WACC in basis points.

Solution

At 35.0% tax rate, after-tax debt cost is \(5.0\% \times (1 - 0.35) = 3.25\%\). The new WACC is \(0.70 \times 11.0\% + 0.30 \times 3.25\% = 7.70\% + 0.975\% = 8.675\%\). The difference is \(8.675\% - 8.900\% = -0.225\%\), or a decline of 22.5 basis points due to the expanded tax shield.

M3 · Valuation of perpetual cash flows

A firm generates 8.90 million dollars in perpetual after-tax operating cash flow. Using the WACC from M1 (8.90%), compute the enterprise value in millions of dollars.

Solution

Using the perpetuity formula \(V = \mathrm{CF} / r_{\mathrm{WACC}}\), enterprise value is \(8.90 / 0.0890 = 100.00\) million dollars, exactly matching the initial total enterprise capital.

Proof

P1 · Boundedness of WACC

Prove that if \(r_D (1 - \tau_c) \le r_E\), then \(r_D (1 - \tau_c) \le r_{\mathrm{WACC}} \le r_E\).

Solution

By Proposition 1.1, \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}} = w_E r_E + w_D r_D (1 - \tau_c)\) with \(w_E + w_D = 1\) and \(w_E, w_D \ge 0\). Since \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}}\) is a convex combination of two quantities, it is bounded below by the minimum and above by the maximum: \(\min(r_E, r_D(1 - \tau_c)) \le r_{\mathrm{WACC}} \le \max(r_E, r_D(1 - \tau_c))\). When \(r_D(1 - \tau_c) \le r_E\), this yields \(r_D(1 - \tau_c) \le r_{\mathrm{WACC}} \le r_E\).

P2 · Derivative with respect to debt weight

Holding component costs \(r_E, r_D\) and \(\tau_c\) constant, derive the partial derivative \(\partial r_{\mathrm{WACC}} / \partial w_D\) and state its sign.

Solution

Substitute \(w_E = 1 - w_D\): \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}} = (1 - w_D) r_E + w_D r_D (1 - \tau_c) = r_E - w_D [r_E - r_D (1 - \tau_c)]\). Differentiating gives \(\partial r_{\mathrm{WACC}} / \partial w_D = -[r_E - r_D (1 - \tau_c)]\). Assuming \(r_E > r_D(1 - \tau_c)\), this derivative is strictly negative.

P3 · D/E representation of WACC

Express \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}}\) as a function of the debt-to-equity ratio \(L = D/E\).

Solution

Note that \(V = E + D = E(1 + L)\). Thus \(w_E = E/V = 1/(1 + L)\) and \(w_D = D/V = L/(1 + L)\). Substituting yields \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}} = \frac{r_E + L \cdot r_D (1 - \tau_c)}{1 + L}\).

Empirical

E1 · Reading Lab 1 debt sensitivity

From Lab 1 output, what is the exact WACC at 40% debt share compared to 0% debt share?

Solution

At 0% debt share, Lab 1 prints WACC = 10.0000% (39/500). At 40% debt share, Lab 1 prints WACC = 7.8000% (39/500), showing a 220 basis point reduction under constant component costs.

E2 · Effective tax rate vs marginal tax rate

Explain why corporate financial analysts use marginal tax rates rather than average effective accounting tax rates in WACC calculations.

Solution

Interest deductions generate cash savings on the marginal dollar of taxable income. Average effective tax rates reflect historical credits and foreign rate blending, whereas incremental investment financing decisions depend on statutory marginal rates.

E3 · Book vs market leverage discrepancy

If a firm has book equity of 20M and market equity of 80M with debt of 40M, calculate WACC under book weights versus market weights assuming \(r_E = 12\%, r_D = 6\%, \tau_c = 25\%\).

Solution

After-tax debt cost is \(6\% \times 0.75 = 4.5\%\). Book total is 60M: \(w_E = 20/60 = 1/3, w_D = 40/60 = 2/3\). Book WACC \(= (1/3)(12\%) + (2/3)(4.5\%) = 4\% + 3\% = 7.0\%\). Market total is 120M: \(w_E = 80/120 = 2/3, w_D = 40/120 = 1/3\). Market WACC \(= (2/3)(12\%) + (1/3)(4.5\%) = 8\% + 1.5\% = 9.5\%\). Book leverage understates WACC by 250 bps.

Research

R1 · Target leverage vs current leverage

Discuss the theoretical and practical justifications for using target long-term capital structure weights versus spot market weights in corporate budgeting.

Solution

Capital projects span multi-year horizons. If temporary market fluctuations distort current market weights, using target leverage aligns discount rates with the long-run financing policy used to fund perpetual operations, provided management actively rebalances toward the target.

R2 · Multi-division hurdle rates

Why is discounting all projects at company-wide WACC incorrect for a multi-divisional corporation with differing risk profiles?

Solution

Discount rates must reflect the specific systematic risk of project cash flows, not the corporate historical average. Company-wide WACC over-invests in high-risk divisions (understating their hurdle rate) and starves low-risk divisions (overstating their hurdle rate).

R3 · Circularity in market-value weights

Explain the circularity problem that arises when enterprise market value \(V\) depends on discounting cash flows by WACC, while WACC depends on \(V\).

Solution

To compute WACC, one needs weights \(D/V\) and \(E/V\). But to compute enterprise value \(V\), one discounts cash flows at WACC. For static firms with constant leverage, this circularity resolves analytically; for complex debt schedules, recursive simultaneous equations or APV must be used.

  1. What does \(w_E\) represent in the WACC formula?

    Options

    Capital weights must reflect current market values of claims: \(w_E = E / (E + D)\).

  2. A firm has 60% equity with cost 11.0% and 40% debt with pre-tax cost 6.0%. Corporate tax rate is 25.0%. What is its WACC in percent?

    After-tax cost of debt \(= 6.0\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 4.5\%\). WACC \(= 0.60 \times 11.0\% + 0.40 \times 4.5\% = 6.6\% + 1.8\% = 8.4\%\).

  3. Why is the cost of debt multiplied by \((1 - \tau_c)\) in the corporate WACC formula?

    Options

    Interest deductibility generates a tax shield, reducing the effective corporate cash cost of debt service.

  4. For any capital structure with positive market equity and non-negative debt, what is the exact sum of weights \(w_E + w_D\)?

    \(w_E + w_D = E/V + D/V = (E + D)/V = V/V = 1.0\).

  5. Which asset pricing model provides the standard benchmark for determining the cost of equity \(r_E\)?

    Options

    CAPM determines the expected cost of equity as \(r_f + \beta_L \cdot \mathrm{ERP}\).

  6. What is the exact WACC reported in Example 1.2 in percent?

    Example 1.2 derives \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}} = 39/500 = 7.80\%\).

  7. What key assumption underlies the use of a single constant WACC to discount multi-year project cash flows?

    Options

    Constant WACC requires constant capital weights (Assumption A2); time-varying leverage requires APV or dynamic discounting.

  8. If a firm has zero debt (\(D = 0\)), what does WACC equal?

    Options

    When \(D = 0\), \(w_E = 1\) and \(w_D = 0\), so \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}} = 1 \cdot r_E + 0 = r_E\).
