Modern corporate finance theory begins with the baseline irrelevance propositions of Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller. In a frictionless economic environment with no taxes, no transaction costs, and equal borrowing terms for individuals and corporations, the total market value of a firm is determined entirely by the earning power and risk of its real assets, not by how it slices cash flows into debt and equity. This lesson proves Proposition I by homemade leverage arbitrage, proves Proposition II for the linear response of equity cost, and establishes why leverage does not lower overall WACC.

1Frictionless capital market assumptions

The classical Modigliani–Miller propositions establish a rigorous no-arbitrage benchmark under the following explicit numbered assumptions:

  • Assumption A1 (Perfect Capital Markets): Securities trade in competitive, frictionless markets with zero transaction costs, no bid-ask spreads, complete information, and equal access: individual investors can borrow and lend at the same market riskless rate \(r_D\) available to corporations.
  • Assumption A4 (No Distress Costs): Default or financial reorganization incurs zero deadweight legal, administrative, or operational costs.
  • Assumption A5 (Financing-Independent Operating Cash Flows): Corporate investment strategy and operating earnings are completely independent of capital financing decisions.
  • Assumption A12 (No Corporate or Personal Taxes): All corporate earnings and investor payouts are untaxed (\(\tau_c = 0\)).

2Proposition I: Value irrelevance

Theorem 4.1 (Modigliani–Miller Proposition I)

Under Assumptions A1, A4, A5, and A12, the market value of any levered firm \(V_L = E_L + D\) is identical to the market value of an unlevered firm \(V_U\) generating identical operating cash flows:

\[ V_L = V_U. \]

Proof

The proof proceeds by classic homemade leverage arbitrage. Consider two firms, \(U\) (unlevered, equity value \(E_U = V_U\)) and \(L\) (levered, debt \(D\) at rate \(r_D\), equity value \(E_L\), total value \(V_L = E_L + D\)). Both firms produce identical perpetual operating cash flow \(X\).

Strategy 1: An investor purchases a fraction \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\) of levered equity \(E_L\). Investment cost is \(\alpha E_L\). Cash flow received is \(\alpha(X - r_D D)\).

Strategy 2: An investor creates “homemade leverage” by purchasing fraction \(\alpha\) of unlevered equity \(E_U\) and borrowing \(\alpha D\) on personal account at the market borrowing rate \(r_D\) (permitted under Assumption A1). Net investment cost is \(\alpha E_U - \alpha D = \alpha(V_U - D)\). Cash flow received is \(\alpha X - r_D(\alpha D) = \alpha(X - r_D D)\).

Strategies 1 and 2 deliver identical cash flows under all states of the world. In the absence of arbitrage (Assumption A1), two portfolios delivering identical cash flows must command identical purchase prices:

\[ \alpha E_L = \alpha (V_U - D). \]

Dividing by \(\alpha\) gives \(E_L = V_U - D\), which rearranges to \(E_L + D = V_U\). By definition \(V_L = E_L + D\), proving \(V_L = V_U\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Proposition II: The cost of equity

Theorem 4.2 (Modigliani–Miller Proposition II)

Under Assumptions A1, A4, A5, and A12, the expected cost of equity \(r_E\) is an increasing linear function of the firm’s debt-to-equity ratio \(D/E\):

\[ r_E = r_A + (r_A - r_D) \frac{D}{E}, \]

where \(r_A\) is the unlevered cost of capital (\(r_A = \E[X]/V_U\)). Furthermore, the overall WACC is invariant to capital structure and strictly equals \(r_A\):

\[ r_{\mathrm{WACC}} = r_A. \]

Proof

Without taxes (\(\tau_c = 0\)), definition 1.1 gives \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}} = \frac{E}{V} r_E + \frac{D}{V} r_D\). Total operating cash flow \(X\) is split between equity and debt: \(X = r_E E + r_D D\). Dividing by total enterprise value \(V\) yields

\[ \frac{X}{V} = \frac{E}{V} r_E + \frac{D}{V} r_D. \]

By Proposition I, \(V = V_U\), so \(X/V = X/V_U = r_A\). Therefore,

\[ r_A = \frac{E}{V} r_E + \frac{D}{V} r_D. \]

Multiplying both sides by \(V = E + D\) gives \(r_A (E + D) = r_E E + r_D D\), which expands to \(r_A E + r_A D = r_E E + r_D D\). Rearranging for \(r_E E\) gives \(r_E E = r_A E + (r_A - r_D) D\). Dividing by \(E\) yields

\[ r_E = r_A + (r_A - r_D) \frac{D}{E}. \]

Substituting \(r_E\) back into the WACC definition confirms \(r_{\mathrm{WACC}} = \frac{E}{V}[r_A + (r_A - r_D)\frac{D}{E}] + \frac{D}{V} r_D = r_A \frac{E}{V} + r_A \frac{D}{V} = r_A\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Modigliani–Miller propositions without taxes

Verifies Proposition I and Proposition II across a discrete D/E grid from 0.00 to 4.00 using exact rational fractions, demonstrating value invariance V_L = V_U and constant WACC = r_A.

"""CF2 lab L04-1: Modigliani-Miller propositions without corporate taxes.

Model: Exact closed-form capital structure calculations using fractions.Fraction.
No synthetic or simulated data is used; all quantities are exact rational numbers.
Demonstrates:
  Proposition I: Firm enterprise value V_L = V_U is invariant to leverage.
  Proposition II: Cost of equity r_E is strictly increasing and linear in D/E.
  Overall WACC remains constant and equal to the unlevered cost of capital r_A.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    print("CF2 Lab L04-1: Modigliani-Miller Propositions Without Taxes")
    print("---------------------------------------------------------")

    # Stated firm parameters
    ebit = Fraction(10_000_000, 1)  # $10M annual perpetual operating income
    r_a = Fraction(10, 100)  # 10% unlevered cost of capital
    r_d = Fraction(5, 100)  # 5% cost of debt (risk-free debt assumption)

    v_u = ebit / r_a
    print(f"Perpetual operating cash flow (EBIT): ${ebit:,.2f}")
    print(f"Unlevered cost of capital (r_A):       {r_a} = {float(r_a):.2%}")
    print(f"Pre-tax cost of debt (r_D):             {r_d} = {float(r_d):.2%}")
    print(f"Proposition I unlevered value (V_U):   ${v_u:,.2f}")

    print("\nLeverage Grid (Proposition I & II across D/E ratios):")
    print(
        f"{'D/E':>6} | {'Debt ($M)':>10} | {'Equity ($M)':>11} | {'Value ($M)':>10} | {'r_E (%)':>8} | {'WACC (%)':>8}"
    )
    print("-" * 65)

    de_ratios = [
        Fraction(0, 1),
        Fraction(1, 4),
        Fraction(1, 2),
        Fraction(1, 1),
        Fraction(3, 2),
        Fraction(2, 1),
        Fraction(4, 1),
    ]

    for de in de_ratios:
        # V = D + E = V_U
        # D/E = de => D = de * E => E * (1 + de) = V_U => E = V_U / (1 + de)
        e_val = v_u / (Fraction(1, 1) + de)
        d_val = v_u - e_val
        v_l = d_val + e_val

        # Proposition II: r_E = r_A + (r_A - r_D) * (D / E)
        r_e = r_a + (r_a - r_d) * de

        # WACC = (E/V)*r_E + (D/V)*r_D
        w_e = e_val / v_l
        w_d = d_val / v_l
        r_wacc = w_e * r_e + w_d * r_d

        d_m = float(d_val) / 1_000_000
        e_m = float(e_val) / 1_000_000
        v_m = float(v_l) / 1_000_000

        print(
            f"{float(de):6.2f} | {d_m:10.2f} | {e_m:11.2f} | {v_m:10.2f} | "
            f"{float(r_e) * 100:8.2f} | {float(r_wacc) * 100:8.2f}"
        )

        assert v_l == v_u, f"Proposition I violated at D/E = {de}"
        assert r_wacc == r_a, f"WACC invariance violated at D/E = {de}"

    print("-" * 65)
    print("Numerical confirmation: Across all leverage levels, V_L == V_U and WACC == r_A.")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

Modigliani–Miller Proposition I holds only in perfect, frictionless capital markets without taxes or distress costs. In real economies, governments grant corporate tax deductions for interest expenses (creating the interest tax shield examined in L05), while excessive leverage triggers direct and indirect costs of financial distress (examined in L06). Consequently, real-world firm value is not invariant to leverage.

5Misconceptions

  • Because debt is cheaper than equity (\(r_D < r_E\)), issuing debt always lowers WACC in perfect capital markets.

    False. In perfect markets, every dollar of cheap debt introduces financial risk to equity, raising \(r_E\) along Proposition II by the exact amount needed to leave total WACC unchanged.

  • Proposition I implies that capital structure does not matter for any company in the real world.

    False. Proposition I is a baseline benchmark theorem. It proves that capital structure affects firm value only through frictions: taxes, bankruptcy costs, agency conflicts, and information asymmetries.

  • Individual investors cannot replicate corporate leverage because corporations possess limited liability.

    False. Under Assumption A1, investors borrow against the portfolio shares with zero default risk, exactly reproducing the net equity payoff \(\alpha(X - r_D D)\).

6Problems

Mechanics

M1 · Proposition II cost of equity

An unlevered firm has cost of capital \(r_A = 10.0\%\). The firm issues debt yielding \(r_D = 4.0\%\) to achieve a debt-to-equity ratio \(D/E = 0.50\). Under MM without taxes, find the new cost of equity \(r_E\).

Solution

Proposition II: \(r_E = r_A + (r_A - r_D)(D/E) = 10.0\% + (10.0\% - 4.0\%) \times 0.50 = 10.0\% + 6.0\% \times 0.50 = 10.0\% + 3.0\% = 13.0\%\).

M2 · WACC verification

For the firm in M1, compute the market weights \(w_E, w_D\) and verify that \(w_E r_E + w_D r_D = r_A\).

Solution

Since \(D/E = 0.50 = 1/2\), we have \(E = 2D\) and \(V = 3D\). Thus \(w_E = 2/3\) and \(w_D = 1/3\). WACC \(= (2/3)(13.0\%) + (1/3)(4.0\%) = 26.0\% / 3 + 4.0\% / 3 = 30.0\% / 3 = 10.0\%\), exactly matching \(r_A\).

M3 · Equity value from enterprise value

A firm has unlevered operating cash flow of 12.0 million dollars perpetual, unlevered cost \(r_A = 8.0\%\), and debt \(D = 50.0\) million dollars. Find enterprise value \(V_L\) and equity value \(E_L\).

Solution

Under Proposition I, \(V_L = V_U = 12.0 / 0.08 = 150.0\) million dollars. Equity value is \(E_L = V_L - D = 150.0 - 50.0 = 100.0\) million dollars.

Proof

P1 · Arbitrage when levered firm is overvalued

Suppose \(V_L > V_U\). Construct an explicit arbitrage strategy showing that an investor can earn riskless profit by selling levered equity and buying unlevered equity with homemade leverage.

Solution

Let an investor own fraction \(\alpha\) of levered firm \(L\). Selling \(\alpha E_L\) generates cash \(\alpha(V_L - D)\). The investor buys \(\alpha E_U = \alpha V_U\) by borrowing \(\alpha D\) at rate \(r_D\), costing \(\alpha(V_U - D)\). Because \(V_L > V_U\), the investor pockets an immediate upfront arbitrage profit of \(\alpha(V_L - V_U) > 0\). The future cash flow is \(\alpha(X - r_D D)\), exactly matching the former payoff from holding levered equity.

P2 · Linear relationship between equity beta and leverage

Under MM without taxes and \(\beta_D = 0\), prove that equity beta satisfies \(\beta_L = \beta_U [1 + D/E]\).

Solution

In the absence of taxes (\(\tau_c = 0\)), the Hamada theorem from L02 simplifies directly by setting \(\tau_c = 0\): \(\beta_L = \beta_U [1 + (1 - 0) D/E] = \beta_U [1 + D/E]\). Alternatively, from CAPM: \(r_E = r_f + \beta_L \mathrm{ERP}\) and \(r_A = r_f + \beta_U \mathrm{ERP}\). Substituting into Proposition II gives \(r_f + \beta_L \mathrm{ERP} = r_f + \beta_U \mathrm{ERP} + (\beta_U \mathrm{ERP})(D/E)\). Canceling \(r_f\) and dividing by \(\mathrm{ERP}\) yields \(\beta_L = \beta_U [1 + D/E]\).

P3 · Proposition II with risky debt

Derive Proposition II when debt is risky with cost \(r_D(D/E)\).

Solution

Starting from \(r_A = (E/V) r_E + (D/V) r_D\), multiply by \(V/E = (E + D)/E = 1 + D/E\). Then \(r_A (1 + D/E) = r_E + r_D (D/E)\), which rearranges to \(r_E = r_A + (r_A - r_D)\frac{D}{E}\). As debt becomes risky, \(r_D\) rises, dampening the slope of \(r_E\) against \(D/E\), but WACC remains strictly constant at \(r_A\).

Empirical

E1 · Reading Lab 1 grid values

From Lab 1 output, report the cost of equity \(r_E\) at \(D/E = 1.00\) and \(D/E = 2.00\), and check that the difference matches Proposition II.

Solution

Lab 1 prints: at \(D/E = 1.00\), \(r_E = 15.00\%\); at \(D/E = 2.00\), \(r_E = 20.00\%\). The difference is \(5.00\%\), which equals \((r_A - r_D) \Delta(D/E) = (10.0\% - 5.0\%) \times (2.0 - 1.0) = 5.0\%\), confirming linear scaling.

E2 · Corporate borrowing vs individual borrowing

Why does higher individual borrowing costs relative to corporate borrowing costs invalidate the homemade leverage arbitrage proof?

Solution

If individuals must borrow at rate \(r_{D,\text{ind}} > r_{D,\text{corp}}\), replicating corporate leverage costs more for individuals. Corporations then possess a comparative advantage in debt issuance, enabling levered firms to trade at a premium (\(V_L > V_U\)) because retail investors cannot cheaply self-manufacture leverage.

E3 · Conglomerate diversification discount

Explain why Proposition I implies that pure financial diversification of uncorrelated business units creates zero corporate value in perfect capital markets.

Solution

Under Assumption A1, investors can diversify across separate single-segment firms on their own account without transaction costs. Value additivity implies \(V(A + B) = V(A) + V(B)\). Combining two firms into a conglomerate provides no risk-reduction service that investors cannot replicate costlessly.

Research

R1 · General equilibrium foundations of MM

How did Stiglitz (1969) generalize the Modigliani–Miller theorem to general equilibrium economies with bankruptcy and incomplete markets?

Solution

Stiglitz showed that MM irrelevance holds under general equilibrium provided firms do not span new payoff states not already replicable by existing securities, and that bankruptcy creates no real deadweight loss of economic output, confirming that financial structure is irrelevant unless it alters real production allocations.

R2 · Short-sale constraints

What happens to the MM arbitrage mechanism when short-selling of levered equity is legally restricted or subjected to costly borrow fees?

Solution

Arbitrage requires selling overvalued securities to buy undervalued substitutes. If short-sale constraints bind, optimistic investors can bid levered equity prices above fundamental replication parity, creating persistent deviations from \(V_L = V_U\).

R3 · Agency conflicts as violation of A5

Give an example where debt financing directly violates Assumption A5 by changing corporate investment policy.

Solution

In debt overhang (Myers 1977), an equity-controlled firm with heavy debt rejects positive-NPV projects because benefits accrue primarily to existing bondholders. Here financing choices alter real operating cash flows, violating Assumption A5.
