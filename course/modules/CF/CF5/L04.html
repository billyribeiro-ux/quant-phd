Debt Schedules, Interest Cascades & Mandatory Cash Sweeps

Following the closing of a leveraged buyout, the operational success of the transaction depends on dynamic cash generation and contractual debt service. Credit agreements enforce a strict priority liquidity cascade: operating cash must fund working capital, maintenance capital expenditures, contractual interest, and mandatory tranche amortization before excess cash is swept to prepay senior debt. We formalize Cash Flow Available for Debt Service (CFADS), model multi-tranche debt waterfalls, and audit financial covenants and technical defaults.

1. Cash Flow Available for Debt Service (CFADS)

In leveraged buyout mechanics, lenders do not evaluate debt capacity using accounting net income or unadjusted EBITDA. Credit agreements define a bespoke metric: Cash Flow Available for Debt Service (CFADS).

Definition 4.1 · Cash Flow Available for Debt Service (CFADS)

Cash Flow Available for Debt Service (CFADS) in period \(t\) is the organic cash generated by the business operations available to service contractual debt obligations (mandatory amortization and optional/mandatory prepayments), after deducting required capital expenditures, working capital investments, cash interest expense, and cash income taxes:

\[ \text{CFADS}_t = \text{EBITDA}_t - \text{CapEx}_t - \Delta \text{NWC}_t - \text{Interest}_t^{\text{cash}} - \text{Taxes}_t^{\text{cash}}. \]

CFADS differs fundamentally from Free Cash Flow to the Firm (FCFF) and Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE):

  • Versus FCFF: FCFF measures cash available to all capital providers before any interest payments (\(\text{NOPAT} + \text{Depr} - \text{CapEx} - \Delta \text{NWC}\)). CFADS is measured after cash interest expense and reflects the actual tax shield benefit generated by interest deductions.
  • Versus FCFE: FCFE measures residual cash flow available to equity holders after all mandatory debt principal repayments. CFADS measures cash available to service debt principal itself.
Remark 4.1 · Interest Expense Calculation Conventions

In professional financial modeling, interest expense in period \(t\) for debt tranche \(j\) is calculated on the beginning debt balance: \(\text{Interest}_{j,t} = D_{j,t-1} \times r_{d,j}\). While some practitioners use the average balance \(\frac{D_{j,t-1} + D_{j,t}}{2}\), doing so introduces a circular reference (because ending debt depends on the cash sweep, which depends on CFADS, which depends on interest expense). Beginning-balance interest avoids circularity and conforms to standard institutional credit modeling standards.

2. Mandatory Cash Sweeps and Priority Amortization

Lenders protect their capital by restricting the borrower's ability to retain cash on the balance sheet or distribute dividends to equity sponsors. This contractual enforcement is governed by a cash sweep.

Definition 4.2 · Mandatory Cash Sweep

A cash sweep (or mandatory excess cash flow sweep) is a contractual covenant in syndicated credit agreements requiring the borrower to apply a contractually specified percentage \(\omega_t \in [0.50, 1.00]\) of excess cash flow generated during fiscal period \(t\) above a permitted minimum operating cash buffer \(C_{\min}\) toward the mandatory prepayment of outstanding debt principal as specified in credit facilities.

Theorem 4.1 · Cash Sweep Liquidity Waterfall

Let \(C_{t-1}\) be opening balance sheet cash, \(\text{CFADS}_t\) the cash generated in period \(t\), and \(A_{j,t}^{\text{mand}}\) mandatory contractual amortization for tranche \(j\). The total mandatory principal debt service is \(A_t^{\text{mand}} = \sum_j A_{j,t}^{\text{mand}}\).

1. The Cash Available before Sweep is:

\[ C_t^{\text{pre}} = C_{t-1} + \text{CFADS}_t - A_t^{\text{mand}}. \]

2. The Excess Cash Sweep Pool is determined relative to the minimum cash buffer \(C_{\min}\):

\[ \text{Sweep Pool}_t = \omega_t \cdot \max\left( 0, \, C_t^{\text{pre}} - C_{\min} \right). \]

3. The sweep pool is applied across outstanding debt tranches in strict order of seniority. For senior tranche TLA and institutional tranche TLB with post-amortization balances \(D_{\text{TLA}, t}^{\text{post-mand}}\) and \(D_{\text{TLB}, t}^{\text{post-mand}}\):

\[ \text{Sweep}_{\text{TLA}, t} = \min\left( D_{\text{TLA}, t}^{\text{post-mand}}, \, \text{Sweep Pool}_t \right), \]

\[ \text{Sweep}_{\text{TLB}, t} = \min\left( D_{\text{TLB}, t}^{\text{post-mand}}, \, \text{Sweep Pool}_t - \text{Sweep}_{\text{TLA}, t} \right). \]

4. The Ending Cash Balance preserved on the balance sheet is:

\[ C_t = C_t^{\text{pre}} - \left( \text{Sweep}_{\text{TLA}, t} + \text{Sweep}_{\text{TLB}, t} \right) \ge C_{\min}. \]

Proof of Theorem 4.1

By construction, \(\text{Sweep Pool}_t \le C_t^{\text{pre}} - C_{\min}\) because \(\omega_t \le 1.0\). Total prepayments cannot exceed the sweep pool: \(\text{Sweep}_{\text{TLA}, t} + \text{Sweep}_{\text{TLB}, t} \le \text{Sweep Pool}_t \le C_t^{\text{pre}} - C_{\min}\). Subtracting total prepayments from \(C_t^{\text{pre}}\) guarantees \(C_t = C_t^{\text{pre}} - (\text{Sweep}_{\text{TLA}} + \text{Sweep}_{\text{TLB}}) \ge C_t^{\text{pre}} - (C_t^{\text{pre}} - C_{\min}) = C_{\min}\). Therefore, the contractual minimum operating liquidity buffer is strictly preserved in every period. \(\blacksquare\)

3. Debt Covenants, Technical Defaults & Private Workouts

Credit agreements contain affirmative, negative, and financial covenants to protect lenders against credit deterioration. Financial covenants are audited at the end of each fiscal quarter:

  • Maximum Leverage Ratio: Contractually caps total net debt (or senior net debt) as a multiple of trailing-twelve-month (TTM) EBITDA: \(\frac{\text{Net Debt}_t}{\text{EBITDA}_t} \le \lambda_t^{\max}\). Credit agreements often incorporate a "covenant step-down" requiring leverage to decline annually (e.g., from 5.5x in Year 1 to 3.5x in Year 5).
  • Minimum Interest Coverage Ratio: Requires operating earnings to exceed interest expense: \(\frac{\text{EBITDA}_t}{\text{Cash Interest}_t} \ge \theta^{\min}\) (typically set between 2.0x and 3.0x).

A covenant violation constitutes an Event of Default, giving lenders the legal right to accelerate debt maturity. However, empirical corporate finance demonstrates that technical defaults rarely precipitate immediate liquidation.

Empirical Finding 4.1 · Out-of-Court Workouts versus Bankruptcy

In an empirical study of financially distressed corporations, Gilson, John & Lang (1990) documented that approximately 50% of financially distressed firms successfully restructure their debt obligations privately out of court without filing for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. When private debt is held by a concentrated syndicate of commercial banks rather than dispersed public bondholders, banks negotiate covenant waivers, amendment fees, interest margin step-ups (50–100 bps), and sponsor equity cures.

4. Interactive Computational Lab

The Python lab below runs a 5-year debt waterfall for a buyout starting with $300.0M of Term Loan A (7.5% interest, 5% mandatory amortization) and $200.0M of Term Loan B (10.0% interest, 1% mandatory amortization), enforces a 100% cash sweep with a $25.0M minimum cash buffer, and audits leverage and interest coverage covenants across all 5 years.

Lab 1 · Multi-Tranche Debt Schedule and Priority Cash Sweep

Verifies that the multi-tranche debt schedule correctly calculates CFADS, applies mandatory amortization and priority cash sweeps to TLA before TLB, preserves the $25.0M cash buffer, and audits annual covenant compliance.

"""CF5 Lab L04-1: Multi-Tranche Debt Schedule, Mandatory Amortization, and Cash Sweeps.

Simulates a 5-year debt waterfall with priority cash sweep and evaluates leverage
and interest coverage covenants.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def run_debt_waterfall(
    ebitda: np.ndarray,
    capex: np.ndarray,
    delta_nwc: np.ndarray,
    tax_rate: float,
    tla_initial: float,
    tla_rate: float,
    tla_amort_pct: float,
    tlb_initial: float,
    tlb_rate: float,
    tlb_amort_pct: float,
    min_cash: float,
    sweep_pct: float,
) -> dict[str, list[float]]:
    """Execute multi-year debt waterfall with priority cash sweeps and ending cash balances."""
    n = len(ebitda)
    tla_bal = tla_initial
    tlb_bal = tlb_initial
    cash_bal = min_cash

    ending_debt: list[float] = []
    ending_cash: list[float] = []
    cfads_list: list[float] = []
    total_interest: list[float] = []
    tla_sweeps: list[float] = []
    tlb_sweeps: list[float] = []

    for i in range(n):
        eb = float(ebitda[i])
        cx = float(capex[i])
        nwc = float(delta_nwc[i])

        # Interest on beginning balances
        int_tla = tla_bal * tla_rate
        int_tlb = tlb_bal * tlb_rate
        tot_int = int_tla + int_tlb
        total_interest.append(tot_int)

        # Operating taxes and CFADS
        ebit = eb - (cx * 0.7)  # approximate D&A
        ebt = ebit - tot_int
        tax = max(0.0, ebt * tax_rate)
        cfads = eb - cx - nwc - tot_int - tax
        cfads_list.append(cfads)

        # Mandatory amortization
        mand_tla = min(tla_bal, tla_initial * tla_amort_pct)
        mand_tlb = min(tlb_bal, tlb_initial * tlb_amort_pct)
        tot_mand = mand_tla + mand_tlb

        # Cash before sweep
        cash_avail = cash_bal + cfads - tot_mand
        excess = max(0.0, cash_avail - min_cash)
        sweep_pool = excess * sweep_pct

        # Apply sweep: TLA first, then TLB
        rem_tla = tla_bal - mand_tla
        sw_tla = min(rem_tla, sweep_pool)
        rem_pool = sweep_pool - sw_tla

        rem_tlb = tlb_bal - mand_tlb
        sw_tlb = min(rem_tlb, rem_pool)

        tla_sweeps.append(sw_tla)
        tlb_sweeps.append(sw_tlb)

        tla_bal = rem_tla - sw_tla
        tlb_bal = rem_tlb - sw_tlb
        cash_bal = cash_avail - (sw_tla + sw_tlb)

        ending_debt.append(tla_bal + tlb_bal)
        ending_cash.append(cash_bal)

    return {
        "ending_debt": ending_debt,
        "ending_cash": ending_cash,
        "cfads": cfads_list,
        "total_interest": total_interest,
        "tla_sweeps": tla_sweeps,
        "tlb_sweeps": tlb_sweeps,
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("CF5 Lab L04-1: Multi-Tranche Debt Schedule and Priority Cash Sweep")
    print("==================================================================")

    # 5-year operating projections ($M)
    ebitda = np.array([100.0, 112.0, 125.0, 138.0, 150.0])
    capex = np.array([20.0, 22.0, 24.0, 26.0, 28.0])
    dnwc = np.array([5.0, 6.0, 6.0, 7.0, 7.0])
    tax_rate = 0.25

    tla_0 = 300.0  # $300M at 7.5%
    tlb_0 = 200.0  # $200M at 10.0%
    min_cash = 25.0

    res = run_debt_waterfall(
        ebitda=ebitda,
        capex=capex,
        delta_nwc=dnwc,
        tax_rate=tax_rate,
        tla_initial=tla_0,
        tla_rate=0.075,
        tla_amort_pct=0.05,  # $15M/yr
        tlb_initial=tlb_0,
        tlb_rate=0.100,
        tlb_amort_pct=0.01,  # $2M/yr
        min_cash=min_cash,
        sweep_pct=1.0,  # 100% sweep
    )

    print("Year | EBITDA | Interest | CFADS  | TLA Sweep | TLB Sweep | End Debt | End Cash")
    print("-----+--------+----------+--------+-----------+-----------+----------+---------")
    for yr in range(5):
        print(
            f"{yr + 1:4d} | ${ebitda[yr]:5.1f}M | ${res['total_interest'][yr]:7.2f}M |"
            f" ${res['cfads'][yr]:5.2f}M | ${res['tla_sweeps'][yr]:8.2f}M |"
            f" ${res['tlb_sweeps'][yr]:8.2f}M | ${res['ending_debt'][yr]:7.2f}M |"
            f" ${res['ending_cash'][yr]:6.2f}M"
        )

    # Covenant audit
    max_leverage_covenants = [5.5, 5.0, 4.5, 4.0, 3.5]
    print("\nFinancial Covenant Audit:")
    for yr in range(5):
        lev = res["ending_debt"][yr] / ebitda[yr]
        cov = ebitda[yr] / res["total_interest"][yr]
        max_lev = max_leverage_covenants[yr]
        status = "COMPLIANT" if lev <= max_lev and cov >= 2.5 else "BREACH"
        print(
            f"  Year {yr + 1}: Leverage = {lev:.2f}x (Max {max_lev:.1f}x), Coverage = {cov:.2f}x (Min 2.5x) -> {status}"
        )


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook debt modeling assumes static sweep percentages and frictionless cash allocation. In institutional credit markets, several contractual provisions alter the standard waterfall:

  1. Leverage-Based Sweep Ratchets: Credit agreements rarely maintain a static 100% or 50% sweep. Instead, the sweep percentage steps down based on senior secured leverage: 50% when leverage exceeds 4.0x, dropping to 25% when leverage is between 3.0x and 4.0x, and stepping down to 0% when leverage falls below 3.0x. This allows the sponsor to retain cash for growth investments once the company has substantially deleveraged.
  2. Restricted Payments Baskets: Credit agreements prohibit equity dividends or management fees under "restricted payment" covenants until a cumulative credit basket (often based on 50% of cumulative consolidated net income) is funded and leverage tests are satisfied.
  3. Equity Cure Rights: When a financial covenant is breached, modern sponsor-friendly agreements permit the private equity sponsor to inject additional equity (an "equity cure") within 10 to 15 business days. The injected equity is credited to EBITDA or used to prepay debt, retroactively curing the covenant breach and preventing default acceleration.

Misconceptions

  1. A financial covenant breach automatically triggers immediate corporate bankruptcy.

    A covenant violation creates a technical default, giving lenders the legal option to accelerate debt. However, acceleration destroys value for lenders. As documented by Gilson, John & Lang (1990), lenders overwhelmingly prefer out-of-court workouts, granting waivers in exchange for amendment fees, higher interest margins, or sponsor equity contributions.

  2. Cash sweeps are distributed pro rata across all outstanding debt tranches.

    Credit agreements enforce strict waterfall priority. Prepayments from cash sweeps must be applied to senior secured revolving credit and Term Loan A facilities until fully retired before any prepayments can be directed toward institutional Term Loan B or junior notes.

  3. Interest expense in an LBO model should be calculated on the ending debt balance.

    Calculating interest expense on ending debt balances understates borrowing costs because debt paydowns occur at period end (or mid-year). Interest accrues on the beginning balance outstanding during the period. Using ending debt also creates artificial circularities in financial models.

Problem Set

Mechanics

In Year 1, a buyout generates EBITDA of $60.0M, CapEx of $12.0M, and an increase in NWC of $4.0M. Beginning debt is $250.0M at an interest rate of 8.0%. Corporate tax rate is 25.0%, and D&A equals CapEx. Calculate cash interest expense, cash taxes, and CFADS in millions of dollars.

Solution

Interest expense = \(250.0 \times 0.08 = \$20.0\text{M}\). \(\text{EBIT} = \text{EBITDA} - \text{Depr} = 60.0 - 12.0 = \$48.0\text{M}\). \(\text{EBT} = \text{EBIT} - \text{Interest} = 48.0 - 20.0 = \$28.0\text{M}\). Taxes = \(28.0 \times 0.25 = \$7.0\text{M}\). \(\text{CFADS} = \text{EBITDA} - \text{CapEx} - \Delta \text{NWC} - \text{Interest} - \text{Taxes} = 60.0 - 12.0 - 4.0 - 20.0 - 7.0 = \$17.0\text{ million dollars}\).

At year-end, opening cash is $20.0M, CFADS is $35.0M, and mandatory debt amortization across all tranches is $10.0M. Contractual minimum operating cash is $15.0M. Under a 75% cash sweep policy, calculate cash available before sweep, the excess cash sweep pool, and ending cash on the balance sheet.

Solution

Cash available before sweep = \(C_{t-1} + \text{CFADS} - A^{\text{mand}} = 20.0 + 35.0 - 10.0 = \$45.0\text{M}\). Excess cash = \(45.0 - 15.0 = \$30.0\text{M}\). Sweep pool = \(30.0 \times 0.75 = \$22.5\text{M}\). Ending cash = \(45.0 - 22.5 = \$22.5\text{ million dollars}\) (which strictly exceeds the $15.0M minimum buffer).

A borrower has an outstanding balance of $12.0M on Term Loan A and $80.0M on Term Loan B. The cash sweep pool for the period is $25.0M. How much is swept to pay down Term Loan A and Term Loan B, and what are their ending balances?

Solution

Under seniority rules, TLA is paid first: \(\text{Sweep}_{\text{TLA}} = \min(12.0, 25.0) = \$12.0\text{M}\), fully retiring TLA. Remaining sweep pool = \(25.0 - 12.0 = \$13.0\text{M}\). TLB sweep = \(\min(80.0, 13.0) = \$13.0\text{M}\). Ending balances: \(\text{TLA} = 12.0 - 12.0 = \$0.0\text{M}\); \(\text{TLB} = 80.0 - 13.0 = \$67.0\text{ million dollars}\).

Proof

Prove that under a 100% cash sweep policy with fixed minimum operating cash \(C_{\min}\), the ending cash balance on the balance sheet is strictly invariant to increases in EBITDA whenever cash before sweep strictly exceeds \(C_{\min}\) and total debt outstanding exceeds the sweep pool.

Solution

Let \(C^{\text{pre}} = C_{t-1} + \text{CFADS} - A^{\text{mand}}\). If \(C^{\text{pre}} > C_{\min}\), excess cash is \(C^{\text{pre}} - C_{\min}\). Under 100% sweep (\(\omega = 1.0\)), the sweep pool is \(1.0 \times (C^{\text{pre}} - C_{\min})\). Since total debt \(D > \text{Sweep Pool}\), the entire sweep pool is applied to debt: \(\text{Total Sweep} = C^{\text{pre}} - C_{\min}\). Ending cash is \(C_t = C^{\text{pre}} - \text{Total Sweep} = C^{\text{pre}} - (C^{\text{pre}} - C_{\min}) = C_{\min}\). Any incremental increase in EBITDA increases CFADS and \(C^{\text{pre}}\) by \(\Delta\), but also increases the sweep by \(\Delta\), leaving ending cash identically equal to \(C_{\min}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that calculating interest expense on average debt balance \(\frac{D_{t-1} + D_t}{2}\) rather than beginning balance \(D_{t-1}\) introduces an algebraic circular dependency in financial model statements.

Solution

Notice the functional chain: Ending debt \(D_t = D_{t-1} - A_t^{\text{mand}} - \text{Sweep}_t\). The sweep depends on CFADS: \(\text{Sweep}_t = f(\text{CFADS}_t)\). CFADS depends linearly on interest: \(\text{CFADS}_t = g(\text{Interest}_t)\). If interest depends on ending debt: \(\text{Interest}_t = r_d \cdot \frac{D_{t-1} + D_t}{2}\), then \(D_t\) is a function of \(\text{Interest}_t\), and \(\text{Interest}_t\) is simultaneously a function of \(D_t\). In spreadsheet and algorithmic execution, this creates a simultaneous algebraic system \((I - A)X = B\) which requires matrix inversion or iterative convergence loops, whereas beginning-balance interest produces a lower-triangular recursive system that solves forward in a single pass. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that an equity cure of amount \(E_{\text{cure}}\) injected to prepay debt reduces ending leverage \(\frac{D_t - E_{\text{cure}}}{\text{EBITDA}_t}\) more effectively than if \(E_{\text{cure}}\) is retained as non-operating cash on the balance sheet whenever covenant definitions measure gross debt rather than net debt.

Solution

If the covenant test is formulated on Gross Debt: \(\text{Gross Leverage} = \frac{\text{Total Debt}}{\text{EBITDA}}\). Prepaying debt reduces gross debt by \(E_{\text{cure}}\), reducing the leverage ratio by \(\frac{E_{\text{cure}}}{\text{EBITDA}}\). Retaining \(E_{\text{cure}}\) as cash increases balance sheet cash but leaves gross debt unchanged, yielding zero reduction in gross leverage. If the covenant is formulated on Net Debt with a cap on cash netting (e.g., maximum allowable cash netting of $20M), retaining cash above the cap also fails to reduce net leverage, whereas debt prepayment guarantees dollar-for-dollar covenant relief. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

What empirical factors did Gilson, John & Lang (1990) identify as determining whether a financially distressed firm restructures out of court or files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy?

Solution

Gilson, John & Lang (1990, Table 3 & Table 4) demonstrated that successful out-of-court restructuring is positively correlated with: (1) higher concentration of debt held by commercial banks rather than dispersed public bondholders; (2) fewer distinct classes of debt claims; and (3) a higher ratio of tangible to intangible assets. When debt is dispersed across public bondholder classes, holdout problems and the Trust Indenture Act of 1939 (which prohibits non-consensual modification of core bond terms) force firms into Chapter 11.

In modern leveraged loans, what percentage of agreements feature leverage-based cash sweep step-downs? Summarize the typical ratchet thresholds.

Solution

Empirical market data shows that over 80% of syndicated Term Loan B agreements contain 3-tier leverage step-down ratchets. A typical structure mandates: 50% cash sweep when Total Net Leverage \(\ge 4.5\text{x}\); 25% cash sweep when \(3.75\text{x} \le \text{Leverage} < 4.5\text{x}\); and 0% cash sweep when \(\text{Leverage} < 3.75\text{x}\). This mechanism rewards successful deleveraging by freeing operating cash flow for corporate reinvestment.

How do credit rating agencies (Moody's and S&P) treat covenant-lite Term Loan B facilities during cyclical downturns?

Solution

Rating agencies observe that covenant-lite loans delay the timing of formal defaults because lenders cannot declare an Event of Default when financial performance deteriorates. However, this delay often allows distressed borrowers to burn liquidity and transfer assets, resulting in lower ultimate recovery rates upon default (50% to 60% recovery for modern cov-lite loans versus 70% to 80% for historically covenanted bank loans).

Research

Analyze the structural phenomenon of "liability management transactions" (LMTs), such as uptier priming exchanges and drop-down asset transfers, in distressed sponsor-backed companies.

Solution

In sponsor-backed buyouts facing financial distress, sponsors exploit loopholes in covenant-lite credit documentation to execute non-pro-rata liability management transactions. In a "drop-down" (e.g., J.Crew), valuable intellectual property is transferred to an unrestricted subsidiary to back new super-senior debt. In an "uptier priming" (e.g., Serta Simmons), a majority creditor coalition amends credit terms to issue super-priority priming debt, subordinating non-participating minority lenders. These transactions demonstrate how flexible debt covenants can be weaponized during distress.

How does the presence of second-lien debt or mezzanine financing complicate the intercreditor agreement during an out-of-court restructuring?

Solution

Intercreditor agreements govern rights between first-lien and second-lien lenders. They impose "standstill periods" (typically 90 to 180 days) during which junior lenders cannot exercise foreclosure remedies following a payment default. Junior lenders frequently block private workouts unless granted equity warrants or principal write-up protections, creating inter-creditor holdout friction that necessitates judicial resolution.

Evaluate how the transition from LIBOR to SOFR has affected interest rate risk and hedging strategies in multi-tranche LBO debt schedules.

Solution

LIBOR was a forward-looking unsecured bank borrowing rate that naturally incorporated credit risk premiums. SOFR is a backward-looking secured overnight rate derived from Treasury repo transactions. Because SOFR lacks a credit risk spread, credit agreements introduced credit spread adjustments (CSAs, typically 10 to 26 bps). Furthermore, because SOFR is compounded in arrears over the interest period, borrowers face uncertainty regarding exact debt service amounts until period close, necessitating more active interest rate swap and cap hedging.

  6. In the lesson lab debt waterfall, what contractual minimum operating cash buffer in millions of dollars was preserved on the balance sheet at all times?

    The lab enforced a constant contractual minimum cash buffer of $25.0M.

  7. What typically occurs immediately following a technical debt covenant default by an LBO portfolio company?

    Options

    Technical defaults trigger lender negotiations, leading to waivers, fee payments, and margin increases rather than liquidation.

  8. In Year 1 of the lesson lab, EBITDA was $100.0M and total interest expense was $42.50M. What was the interest coverage ratio, which resulted in a covenant breach against the 2.50x minimum?

    \(\text{Coverage Ratio} = 100.0 / 42.50 \approx 2.353\text{x}\).

  9. How does an equity cure provision function in private equity credit agreements?

    Options

    An equity cure allows the sponsor to contribute new cash to cure covenant breaches within a contractual cure window.

    \(\text{Leverage} = 478.38 / 100.0 = 4.7838 \approx 4.78\text{x}\).
