Sources & Uses of Funds, Transaction Structuring & LBO Mechanics

Leveraged buyouts fundamentally restructure corporate balance sheets by replacing public equity with multi-tiered debt packages and concentrated private equity ownership. Structuring an institutional buyout requires rigorous financial plumbing: reconciling the fundamental Sources and Uses accounting identity, determining purchase equity, debt sizing across commercial bank and institutional tranches, structuring management rollover equity, and allocating goodwill under purchase price accounting.

1. Institutional Leveraged Buyout Mechanics

In contrast to strategic corporate acquisitions, a private equity buyout relies heavily on borrowed capital to finance the acquisition of a target company. We define the foundational framework under leveraged buyout mechanics.

Definition 3.1 · Leveraged Buyout Mechanics

Leveraged buyout mechanics encompass the financial engineering, contractual structuring, and legal architecture whereby a financial sponsor acquires control of an enterprise primarily funded by debt capital (historically 50% to 70% of total enterprise value), pledging target operating cash flows and assets to service borrowings while sponsor equity captures leveraged residual equity appreciation as synthesized by modern private equity research.

An institutional buyout capital structure is segmented into distinct priority layers, balancing lender yield, security liens, and amortization schedules:

  1. Revolving Credit Facility (Revolver): A super-senior secured credit line provided by commercial banks to finance working capital volatility and seasonal operational swings. Usually undrawn at transaction close (except for letter-of-credit backing or minor liquidity buffers).
  2. Term Loan A (TLA): Senior secured debt held by commercial banks and depository institutions. TLA features lower interest margins (e.g., SOFR + 200–300 bps), tight financial maintenance covenants, and significant mandatory annual principal amortization (typically 5.0% to 10.0% of original principal per annum over a 5-year maturity) reflecting commercial bank regulatory reserve requirements.
  3. Term Loan B (TLB): Senior secured debt syndicated to non-bank institutional lenders, including collateralized loan obligations (CLOs), credit hedge funds, and private credit funds. TLB carries higher margins (e.g., SOFR + 350–500 bps), longer maturities (typically 7 years), covenant-lite documentation, and nominal mandatory amortization (strictly 1.0% per annum, with a 93% balloon at maturity).
  4. Subordinated / Mezzanine Debt: High-yield unsecured bonds or junior mezzanine facilities. These claims are contractually subordinated to senior credit facilities, pay higher fixed coupons (8.0% to 12.0%) or payment-in-kind (PIK) interest, and often carry detachable equity warrants or participation rights.
  5. Equity: The residual risk-bearing tranche, comprising management rollover equity and the financial sponsor's direct equity check.
Remark 3.1 · Agency Theory and the Discipline of High Debt

As established by Jensen & Meckling (1976), concentrated equity ownership aligns managerial incentives with shareholders, while contractual debt service eliminates discretionary cash flows that entrenched managers might otherwise misallocate to empire building.

2. The Sources and Uses of Funds Identity

Every buyout transaction model begins with the transaction funding schedule. We formalize this identity under sources and uses of funds.

Definition 3.2 · Sources and Uses of Funds

The sources and uses of funds schedule is the binding balance sheet bridge articulating every dollar expended to complete the acquisition against every dollar of debt, cash, and equity raised:

\[ \sum \text{Uses of Funds} \equiv \sum \text{Sources of Funds}. \]

The contractual components of the Uses and Sources schedules are formulated as follows:

Theorem 3.1 · Transaction Articulation Identity

Let \(\text{EV}_0 = \text{EBITDA}_0 \times M_0\) denote the agreed transaction Enterprise Value, \(D_{\text{target}}\) existing target debt to be refinanced, \(C_{\text{target}}\) target balance sheet cash, \(C_{\min}\) the minimum operating cash required on the pro forma opening balance sheet, and \(F = F_{\text{mna}} + F_{\text{debt}}\) total advisory and debt financing fees.

1. The Equity Purchase Price paid to selling shareholders is:

\[ E_{\text{purchase}} = \text{EV}_0 - D_{\text{target}} + C_{\text{target}}. \]

2. The Total Uses of Funds represents all closing cash obligations:

\[ \text{Total Uses} = E_{\text{purchase}} + D_{\text{target}} + F_{\text{mna}} + F_{\text{debt}} + C_{\min}. \]

3. The Total Sources of Funds equals total funded debt \(D_{\text{funded}}\), target cash contributed \(C_{\text{target}}\), rollover equity \(E_{\text{rollover}}\), and the sponsor equity check \(E_{\text{sponsor}}\):

\[ \text{Total Sources} = D_{\text{funded}} + C_{\text{target}} + E_{\text{rollover}} + E_{\text{sponsor}}. \]

4. Equating Total Sources to Total Uses isolates the required Sponsor Equity Check:

\[ E_{\text{sponsor}} = \text{Total Uses} - \left( D_{\text{funded}} + C_{\text{target}} + E_{\text{rollover}} \right). \]

Proof of Theorem 3.1

Substituting \(E_{\text{purchase}} = \text{EV}_0 - D_{\text{target}} + C_{\text{target}}\) into Total Uses yields:

\[ \text{Total Uses} = (\text{EV}_0 - D_{\text{target}} + C_{\text{target}}) + D_{\text{target}} + F + C_{\min} = \text{EV}_0 + C_{\text{target}} + F + C_{\min}. \]

Setting \(\text{Total Sources} = \text{Total Uses}\):

\[ D_{\text{funded}} + C_{\text{target}} + E_{\text{rollover}} + E_{\text{sponsor}} = \text{EV}_0 + C_{\text{target}} + F + C_{\min}. \]

Cancelling \(C_{\text{target}}\) from both sides demonstrates that the net cash funding requirement is independent of target cash: \(D_{\text{funded}} + E_{\text{rollover}} + E_{\text{sponsor}} = \text{EV}_0 + F + C_{\min}\). Rearranging isolates \(E_{\text{sponsor}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

3. Purchase Price Allocation, Asset Step-Ups & Goodwill

Under GAAP (ASC 805) and IFRS 3, an acquisition requires revaluing the target's balance sheet to fair market value on the closing date. The excess of purchase consideration over the fair market value of net identifiable assets is capitalized as goodwill.

Definition 3.3 · Purchase Price Goodwill Allocation

Let \(E_{\text{purchase}}\) be the equity purchase price, \(B_0\) the target's existing carrying book value of equity, and \(\Delta A_{\text{step-up}}\) the fair-value step-ups allocated to identifiable property, plant & equipment (PPE) and finite-lived intangibles (customer relationships, proprietary technology). If the transaction is structured as a stock purchase without a Section 338(h)(10) election, the asset step-ups generate a Deferred Tax Liability (DTL):

\[ \text{DTL} = \Delta A_{\text{step-up}} \times \tau_c. \]

The fair market value of net identifiable assets is \(A_{\text{net}} = B_0 + \Delta A_{\text{step-up}} - \text{DTL}\), and pro forma Goodwill created on the opening balance sheet is:

\[ \text{Goodwill} = E_{\text{purchase}} - A_{\text{net}} = E_{\text{purchase}} - \left( B_0 + \Delta A_{\text{step-up}} - \Delta A_{\text{step-up}} \times \tau_c \right). \]

In stock acquisitions, goodwill created is not deductible for corporate tax purposes and is subject to mandatory annual impairment tests under ASC 350 rather than straight-line amortization.

4. Historical Evolution of Buyout Leverage

The structural composition of LBO financing has evolved markedly over four decades of private equity practice, as documented in the survey by Kaplan & Strömberg (2009):

  • The 1980s Buyout Wave: Transactions in the 1980s were characterized by extreme leverage. Debt routinely funded 85% to 90% of total purchase price (often 6.0x to 8.0x EBITDA), with financial sponsors contributing a nominal equity check of just 10% to 15%. High interest rates and aggressive leverage caused elevated financial distress rates when macroeconomic conditions deteriorated.
  • Modern Buyouts (2000s–Present): Contemporary buyouts operate with substantial equity cushions. In the modern era, debt comprises approximately 50% to 65% of enterprise value (typically 4.5x to 6.0x EBITDA), with sponsors contributing 35% to 50% equity.
  • Shift toward Operational Value Creation: While 1980s buyouts generated returns predominantly through debt paydown and financial leverage, modern private equity firms rely heavily on operational improvement, margin expansion, and strategic buy-and-build add-on acquisitions to achieve target hurdles.

Lab 1 · LBO Sources & Uses and Purchase Price Allocation

Verifies that the Sources and Uses schedule articulates to zero residual discrepancy, determines the exact sponsor equity check, and reconciles fair value net identifiable assets with goodwill creation.

"""CF5 Lab L03-1: Sources and Uses of Funds and Purchase Price Accounting.

Constructs the transaction funding schedule, validates the Sources == Uses balance,
and calculates purchase price allocation, asset step-ups, and goodwill creation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def construct_sources_and_uses(
    target_ebitda: float,
    entry_multiple: float,
    existing_debt: float,
    target_cash: float,
    min_cash_buffer: float,
    revolver_drawn: float,
    tla_multiple: float,
    tlb_multiple: float,
    mezz_multiple: float,
    mna_fee_rate: float,
    debt_fee_rate: float,
    rollover_equity: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate institutional Sources & Uses schedule and sponsor equity check."""
    enterprise_value = target_ebitda * entry_multiple
    equity_purchase_price = enterprise_value - existing_debt + target_cash

    tla = target_ebitda * tla_multiple
    tlb = target_ebitda * tlb_multiple
    mezz = target_ebitda * mezz_multiple
    total_term_debt = tla + tlb + mezz + revolver_drawn

    mna_fees = enterprise_value * mna_fee_rate
    debt_fees = total_term_debt * debt_fee_rate
    total_fees = mna_fees + debt_fees

    total_uses = equity_purchase_price + existing_debt + total_fees + min_cash_buffer
    sponsor_equity = total_uses - (total_term_debt + target_cash + rollover_equity)
    total_sources = total_term_debt + target_cash + rollover_equity + sponsor_equity

    return {
        "enterprise_value": float(enterprise_value),
        "equity_purchase_price": float(equity_purchase_price),
        "refinance_debt": float(existing_debt),
        "total_fees": float(total_fees),
        "min_cash_buffer": float(min_cash_buffer),
        "total_uses": float(total_uses),
        "total_debt_funded": float(total_term_debt),
        "target_cash_used": float(target_cash),
        "rollover_equity": float(rollover_equity),
        "sponsor_equity": float(sponsor_equity),
        "total_sources": float(total_sources),
        "opening_debt": float(total_term_debt),
        "opening_cash": float(min_cash_buffer),
        "opening_net_debt": float(total_term_debt - min_cash_buffer),
    }


def purchase_price_allocation(
    equity_purchase_price: float,
    existing_book_equity: float,
    ppe_writeup: float,
    intangibles_writeup: float,
    deferred_tax_rate: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate goodwill created under GAAP/IFRS acquisition method."""
    book_value_step_up = ppe_writeup + intangibles_writeup
    deferred_tax_liability = book_value_step_up * deferred_tax_rate
    net_identifiable_fair_value = existing_book_equity + book_value_step_up - deferred_tax_liability
    goodwill = equity_purchase_price - net_identifiable_fair_value

    return {
        "equity_purchase_price": float(equity_purchase_price),
        "net_identifiable_fair_value": float(net_identifiable_fair_value),
        "deferred_tax_liability": float(deferred_tax_liability),
        "goodwill_created": float(goodwill),
    }

Where the Canon Breaks

In standard textbook modeling, transaction structuring is treated as a static one-period algebra exercise. In institutional practice, several legal and contractual complexities modify the canonical structure:

  1. Original Issue Discount (OID): Institutional loans and high-yield bonds are frequently issued at a discount to par (e.g., 98.0 cents on the dollar). If $500M par of TLB is issued with 2.0% OID, gross debt on the balance sheet is $500M, but cash proceeds available to fund Uses is only $490M. The $10M OID discount must be capitalized and amortized into interest expense over the loan life.
  2. Closing Working Capital Peg and True-Up: Purchase agreements define a target Net Working Capital "peg". If closing NWC delivered by the seller falls short of the peg, the equity purchase price is adjusted downward dollar-for-dollar; if delivered NWC exceeds the peg, the sponsor must fund additional cash at closing.
  3. Rollover Equity Tax Structuring: Management teams rolling over equity demand tax-free treatment under Internal Revenue Code Section 721 (partnership contribution) or Section 351 (corporate exchange). Structuring errors that trigger immediate capital gains recognition can derail executive team retention.

Misconceptions

  1. Maximizing initial leverage always maximizes financial sponsor equity returns.

    While leverage amplifies positive equity returns under favorable operational scenarios, excessive debt escalates interest expense, heightens financial distress risk, and imposes rigid maintenance covenants that restrict growth CapEx. Furthermore, modern private equity research confirms that overleveraged buyouts face prohibitive borrowing costs that erode net equity value creation during macroeconomic downturns.

  2. Transaction advisory and financing fees are negligible frictional costs in LBO modeling.

    In multi-billion-dollar transactions, M&A advisory, legal, accounting, and debt arrangement fees routinely aggregate to 3.0% to 5.0% of total transaction value ($40M to $100M+). Because fees represent cash outlays that do not acquire productive operating assets, they must be funded dollar-for-dollar through the equity check, directly increasing initial sponsor capital at risk.

  3. Goodwill created in an LBO can be amortized for corporate income tax deductions.

    In standard stock acquisitions, tax basis carries over under IRC Section 1060 rules; goodwill is non-amortizable for tax purposes and provides zero tax shields. Only when a transaction is legally structured as an asset purchase or accompanied by a joint Section 338(h)(10) election can step-ups and goodwill be amortized over 15 years under IRC Section 197.

Mechanics

Target company EBITDA is $80.0M. Sponsor acquires the company at an entry multiple of 10.0x EBITDA. Existing target debt of $150.0M is refinanced, and target cash of $20.0M is used to fund the transaction. Financing consists of 3.0x EBITDA in Term Loan A, 2.5x EBITDA in Term Loan B, and $15.0M drawn on a Revolver. Transaction fees are $18.0M and debt financing fees are $12.0M. Minimum cash buffer is $10.0M. Calculate the total Uses of funds and the required Sponsor Equity check in millions of dollars.

Solution

Enterprise Value = \(80.0 \times 10.0 = \$800.0\text{M}\). Equity Purchase Price = \(\text{EV} - D_{\text{target}} + C_{\text{target}} = 800.0 - 150.0 + 20.0 = \$670.0\text{M}\). Total Uses = \(E_{\text{purchase}} + D_{\text{refinance}} + F_{\text{total}} + C_{\min} = 670.0 + 150.0 + (18.0 + 12.0) + 10.0 = \$860.0\text{M}\). Funded Debt: \(\text{TLA} = 80 \times 3.0 = \$240.0\text{M}\), \(\text{TLB} = 80 \times 2.5 = \$200.0\text{M}\), Revolver = $15.0M. Total Funded Debt = \(240 + 200 + 15 = \$455.0\text{M}\). Sponsor Equity = \(\text{Total Uses} - (D_{\text{funded}} + C_{\text{target}}) = 860.0 - (455.0 + 20.0) = 860.0 - 475.0 = \$385.0\text{ million dollars}\).

In a buyout with equity purchase price of $500.0M, target existing book equity is $180.0M. Due diligence identifies $60.0M in PPE step-up and $40.0M in customer relationships intangible step-up. The corporate tax rate is 25.0%. Calculate the deferred tax liability created, net identifiable fair value, and goodwill recognized under purchase accounting.

Solution

Total asset step-ups = \(60.0 + 40.0 = \$100.0\text{M}\). Deferred Tax Liability (DTL) = \(100.0 \times 0.25 = \$25.0\text{M}\). Fair value of net identifiable assets = \(180.0 + 100.0 - 25.0 = \$255.0\text{M}\). Goodwill created = \(E_{\text{purchase}} - A_{\text{net}} = 500.0 - 255.0 = \$245.0\text{ million dollars}\).

A financial sponsor issues $300.0M in par value Term Loan B with a 2.0% Original Issue Discount (OID) and a 2.5% debt arrangement fee. How much net cash from the TLB facility is available to fund transaction Uses?

Solution

Gross proceeds net of OID = \(300.0 \times (1 - 0.02) = \$294.0\text{M}\). Debt financing fee = \(300.0 \times 0.025 = \$7.5\text{M}\). Net cash available to fund transaction uses = \(294.0 - 7.5 = \$286.5\text{ million dollars}\).

Proof

Prove that in any cash-free, debt-free transaction structuring where target cash is used dollar-for-dollar to offset existing debt, the net sponsor equity check required is strictly invariant to whether target cash is distributed to selling shareholders as a pre-closing dividend or swept into transaction Sources.

Solution

Case 1 (Target cash in Sources): Equity purchase price is \(E_1 = \text{EV} - D + C\). Total Uses = \(E_1 + D + F + C_{\min} = \text{EV} + C + F + C_{\min}\). Non-equity sources = \(D_{\text{funded}} + C\). Sponsor equity = \(\text{Total Uses} - (D_{\text{funded}} + C) = \text{EV} + F + C_{\min} - D_{\text{funded}}\). Case 2 (Target cash paid as dividend prior to close): Target cash becomes zero, so equity purchase price drops to \(E_2 = \text{EV} - D\). Total Uses = \(E_2 + D + F + C_{\min} = \text{EV} + F + C_{\min}\). Non-equity sources = \(D_{\text{funded}}\). Sponsor equity = \(\text{EV} + F + C_{\min} - D_{\text{funded}}\). The required sponsor equity check is identical in both cases, proving funding invariance. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that under ASC 805 purchase accounting, if asset step-ups are increased by \(\Delta S > 0\), the resulting goodwill recognized on the opening balance sheet decreases by exactly \(\Delta S(1 - \tau_c)\), where \(\tau_c\) is the marginal corporate tax rate.

Solution

Goodwill is defined as \(\text{GW} = E_{\text{purchase}} - A_{\text{net}}\). Net identifiable assets are \(A_{\text{net}} = B_0 + S - \tau_c S = B_0 + S(1 - \tau_c)\). Differentiating with respect to step-up \(S\): \(\frac{d A_{\text{net}}}{dS} = 1 - \tau_c > 0\). Since \(E_{\text{purchase}}\) is fixed by the merger agreement, \(\frac{d \text{GW}}{dS} = -\frac{d A_{\text{net}}}{dS} = -(1 - \tau_c)\). Thus, for any incremental step-up \(\Delta S\), goodwill decreases by exactly \(\Delta S(1 - \tau_c)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that management rollover equity \(\alpha = E_{\text{rollover}} / (E_{\text{rollover}} + E_{\text{sponsor}})\) does not alter opening Enterprise Value or pro forma net debt, but scales sponsor MOIC and IRR identically to an unlevered equity coinvestment.

Solution

Opening Enterprise Value is determined by purchase price \(\text{EV} = \text{EBITDA} \times M\), and pro forma net debt is \(D_{\text{funded}} - C_{\min}\), neither of which depends on the internal ownership split between rollover and sponsor equity. At exit, total equity proceeds are distributed pari passu: sponsor receives \((1 - \alpha) E_{\text{exit}}\) and rollover receives \(\alpha E_{\text{exit}}\). Sponsor MOIC is \(\frac{(1 - \alpha)E_{\text{exit}}}{(1 - \alpha)E_{\text{entry}}} = \frac{E_{\text{exit}}}{E_{\text{entry}}}\), which is mathematically identical to the MOIC achieved without rollover equity. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

According to Kaplan & Strömberg (2009), how did the median leverage ratio (Total Debt / EBITDA) in private equity buyouts change between the late 1980s and the 2000s boom? What structural factors caused this shift?

Solution

Kaplan & Strömberg (2009, Table 1) show that buyout debt levels declined from an average of ~85%–90% of purchase price (debt multiples often 7.0x–8.5x EBITDA) in the late 1980s to 60%–65% of purchase price (debt multiples around 5.0x–6.0x EBITDA) in the 2000s. Two structural drivers caused this transition: First, the collapse of the high-yield junk bond market in 1989–1990 following the failure of several highly levered 1980s mega-deals (e.g., Campeau / Federated). Second, institutional investors (limited partners) and bank syndicates demanded larger sponsor equity contributions (35% to 45%) to ensure alignment and absorb operational cash flow shocks.

What empirical evidence exists regarding the prevalence of "covenant-lite" loans in Term Loan B issuances over modern private equity cycles?

Solution

In the pre-2007 era, less than 25% of institutional leveraged loans were covenant-lite. By the 2018–2021 period, covenant-lite loans surged to over 85% of total Term Loan B volume. Covenant-lite facilities eliminate quarterly maintenance financial covenants (such as maximum debt/EBITDA or minimum interest coverage), replacing them with "incurrence-only" tests triggered only when the borrower issues additional debt or executes restricted cash distributions.

How does management equity rollover impact long-run operational performance in private equity buyouts according to empirical studies?

Solution

Empirical studies document that transactions with substantial management rollover equity (typically 10% to 20% of pro forma equity) exhibit significantly higher post-buyout EBITDA growth and lower default rates. Substantial rollover commits executive personal wealth to the company's leveraged turnaround, mitigating managerial moral hazard and reducing information asymmetry during post-closing integration.

Research

Analyze the structural rise of private credit direct lenders (e.g., Ares, Blackstone Credit, Blue Owl) relative to traditional syndicated Term Loan B markets in financing mid-market LBOs.

Solution

Direct lenders provide unitranche facilities—blending senior and subordinated debt into a single bilateral loan held by one or a small club of private funds. Unitranche structures offer certainty of execution, speed, confidentiality, and zero syndication flex risk, eliminating the need for public credit ratings. In exchange, private credit lenders charge a 50–150 bps illiquidity spread premium over syndicated TLB facilities.

How do Section 163(j) interest expense deductibility caps introduced under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) alter optimal LBO debt sizing?

Solution

Under IRC Section 163(j), business interest deductibility is capped at 30% of adjusted taxable income (which shifted from EBITDA to EBIT starting in 2022). For highly levered buyouts where interest expense exceeds 30% of EBIT, incremental debt yields zero current interest tax shield, transforming tax-deductible interest into non-deductible cash outflows. This tax friction has constrained optimal leverage multiples, encouraging larger equity checks.

Evaluate the economic function of equity commitment letters and reverse termination fees in public-to-private LBO transactions.

Solution

Private equity sponsors utilize shell acquisition vehicles (Merger Sub) with zero standalone balance sheet assets. To provide closing certainty to target shareholders, sponsors execute binding Equity Commitment Letters (ECLs) guaranteeing equity funding up to the sponsor equity check. Concurrently, merger agreements include Reverse Termination Fees (typically 5% to 7% of deal value) payable to the target if the sponsor's debt financing falls through, bounding the sponsor's liability if credit markets seize up.

  1. What is the fundamental accounting condition governing the Sources & Uses schedule of a buyout?

    Options

    Every transaction model must enforce \(\text{Total Sources} \equiv \text{Total Uses}\) to guarantee articulate balance sheet closing.

    Options

    TLA is held by commercial banks with higher mandatory amortization; TLB is syndicated to institutional investors with nominal 1% amort and higher margins.

  4. Equity purchase price is $600.0M. Target book equity is $250.0M. Asset step-ups total $200.0M with a 25.0% deferred tax rate. What is the goodwill recognized on the opening balance sheet in millions of dollars?

    \(\text{DTL} = 200.0 \times 0.25 = \$50.0\text{M}\). Net identifiable assets = \(250.0 + 200.0 - 50.0 = \$400.0\text{M}\). \(\text{Goodwill} = 600.0 - 400.0 = \$200.0\text{ million dollars}\).

  5. What did Kaplan & Strömberg (2009) find regarding the percentage of purchase price funded by debt in 1980s buyouts compared to modern buyouts?

    Options

    1980s buyouts relied on extreme debt (85-90%), whereas modern buyouts feature larger equity cushions (debt at 50-65%).

  6. Target PPE and intangible assets are stepped up by $200.0M in a stock purchase buyout. If the corporate tax rate is 25.0%, what is the deferred tax liability (DTL) recognized in millions of dollars?

    \(\text{DTL} = 200.0 \times 0.25 = \$50.0\text{ million dollars}\).

  7. Why does management rollover equity benefit financial sponsors in leveraged buyout transactions?

    Options

    Rollover equity commits incumbent executives financially alongside the sponsor while reducing the required sponsor cash equity outlay.

  8. In the lesson lab example, with $1,431.26M in Total Uses, $700.0M in funded debt, $40.0M in target cash, and $50.0M in rollover equity, what was the funded sponsor equity check in millions of dollars?

    \(E_{\text{sponsor}} = 1,431.26 - (700.0 + 40.0 + 50.0) = 1,431.26 - 790.0 = \$641.26\text{ million dollars}\).

  9. Under Jensen & Meckling (1976), how does substantial debt in an LBO structure discipline corporate executives?

    Options

    Debt creates a hard contractual commitment to pay cash flow to debtholders, disciplining managerial capital allocation.

  10. In the lesson lab example, the company generates $120.0M in EBITDA and issues $700.0M in total funded debt. What is the total debt-to-EBITDA leverage multiple?

    \(\text{Leverage Multiple} = 700.0 / 120.0 \approx 5.833\text{x}\).
