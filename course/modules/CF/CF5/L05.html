Returns Decomposition: MOIC, IRR & Value Creation Attribution

Financial sponsors evaluate buyouts through Multiple on Invested Capital (MOIC) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR). Yet headline performance numbers obscure how value was actually generated: did returns stem from financial leverage, valuation multiple expansion, or operational improvement? We formalize the mathematical relationship between MOIC and IRR, derive the exact Kaplan-Strömberg value creation attribution identity, and analyze the empirical transition from 1980s leverage-driven buyouts to modern operational turnarounds.

1. Private Equity Return Metrics: MOIC versus IRR

In institutional private equity, investment performance is evaluated using cash-on-cash multiples and annualized discount rates. We define the baseline metric under multiple on invested capital.

Definition 5.1 · Multiple on Invested Capital (MOIC)

Multiple on Invested Capital (MOIC) (also termed Cash-on-Cash multiple, TVPI, or Total Value to Paid-In) is the ratio of total cumulative cash distributions returned to equity sponsors plus residual net asset value (NAV) at exit to the total initial equity capital invested:

\[ \text{MOIC} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^T \text{Distributions}_t + \text{Residual NAV}_T}{\text{Invested Equity}_0} = \frac{E_{\text{exit}}}{E_{\text{entry}}}. \]

The relationship between MOIC and the annualized Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is direct when cash flows occur as a single entry check at \(t=0\) and single exit distribution at \(t=T\):

\[ \text{IRR} = \text{MOIC}^{1/T} - 1 \iff \text{MOIC} = (1 + \text{IRR})^T. \]

Remark 5.1 · The Economic Tension between MOIC and IRR

The time-discounted nature of IRR creates an agency tension between general partners (GPs) and institutional limited partners (LPs). A GP can maximize fund headline IRR by executing a rapid "dividend recapitalization" or flipping a portfolio company after 1.5 years at a \(1.40\times\) MOIC (generating an IRR of \((1.40)^{1/1.5} - 1 = 25.1\%\)). However, LPs cannot easily redeploy returned capital at 25% hurdle rates. LPs seek to compound substantial capital over extended durations: a 5-year hold delivering a \(2.50\times\) MOIC generates a lower IRR (\(20.1\%\)) but creates over 3.7 times more absolute dollar wealth.

2. The Kaplan-Strömberg Value Creation Attribution Identity

To determine whether private equity returns stem from financial engineering or genuine industrial value creation, economists and investment committees perform lbo-value-creation-attribution.

Definition 5.2 · LBO Value-Creation Attribution

LBO value-creation attribution is the exact mathematical decomposition of total dollar equity value created over the investment horizon \(\Delta E = E_T - E_0\) into three mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive drivers: operational EBITDA growth, valuation multiple expansion, and net debt paydown (deleveraging).

Under the balance sheet bridge established in sources and uses of funds, equity value at entry \(t=0\) and exit \(t=T\) satisfies:

\[ E_0 = \text{EV}_0 - \text{NetDebt}_0 = \text{EBITDA}_0 \cdot M_0 - \text{NetDebt}_0, \]

\[ E_T = \text{EV}_T - \text{NetDebt}_T = \text{EBITDA}_T \cdot M_T - \text{NetDebt}_T. \]

Theorem 5.1 · The Kaplan-Strömberg Attribution Identity

The total net equity value created \(\Delta E = E_T - E_0\) decomposes identically without residual error into operational EBITDA growth, multiple expansion, and deleveraging as formalized by Kaplan & Strömberg (2009):

\[ \Delta E = \underbrace{(\text{EBITDA}_T - \text{EBITDA}_0) \cdot M_0}_{\Delta E_{\text{EBITDA}} \; (\text{Operational Growth})} + \underbrace{\text{EBITDA}_T \cdot (M_T - M_0)}_{\Delta E_{\text{Multiple}} \; (\text{Multiple Expansion})} + \underbrace{(\text{NetDebt}_0 - \text{NetDebt}_T)}_{\Delta E_{\text{Deleverage}} \; (\text{Deleveraging})}. \]

Proof of Theorem 5.1

Total dollar equity creation is \(\Delta E = E_T - E_0\). Substituting the balance sheet bridges:

\[ \Delta E = (\text{EV}_T - \text{NetDebt}_T) - (\text{EV}_0 - \text{NetDebt}_0) = (\text{EV}_T - \text{EV}_0) + (\text{NetDebt}_0 - \text{NetDebt}_T). \]

Now consider the Enterprise Value difference \(\text{EV}_T - \text{EV}_0 = \text{EBITDA}_T \cdot M_T - \text{EBITDA}_0 \cdot M_0\). Add and subtract \(\text{EBITDA}_T \cdot M_0\):

\[ \text{EV}_T - \text{EV}_0 = (\text{EBITDA}_T \cdot M_0 - \text{EBITDA}_0 \cdot M_0) + (\text{EBITDA}_T \cdot M_T - \text{EBITDA}_T \cdot M_0) \]

\[ = (\text{EBITDA}_T - \text{EBITDA}_0) \cdot M_0 + \text{EBITDA}_T \cdot (M_T - M_0). \]

Substituting this identity back into the expression for \(\Delta E\) yields:

\[ \Delta E = (\text{EBITDA}_T - \text{EBITDA}_0) \cdot M_0 + \text{EBITDA}_T \cdot (M_T - M_0) + (\text{NetDebt}_0 - \text{NetDebt}_T). \]

The sum of the three terms identically equals \(\Delta E\), completing the proof with zero residual. \(\blacksquare\)

3. Empirical Evolution of Value Creation Sources

In their comprehensive review of the private equity industry, Kaplan & Strömberg (2009) documented a profound historical transformation in how financial sponsors generate equity returns:

  • The 1980s Financial Engineering Regime: In the first buyout wave, returns were overwhelmingly driven by financial leverage and debt paydown. Deleveraging and multiple expansion accounted for over 80% of total equity appreciation, while operational EBITDA improvements played a modest secondary role.
  • The Modern Operational Regime: In contemporary buyouts, extreme leverage is constrained by syndicated loan market standards and tax rules. Modern private equity firms derive almost half of their total equity value creation from operational improvements (organic revenue growth, gross margin optimization, operational restructuring, and strategic buy-and-build platform acquisitions).
  • Performance Persistence: Analyzing fund-level cash flows, Kaplan & Schoar (2005) discovered significant performance persistence across successive funds managed by the same private equity firm. General partners who outperform public markets in Fund \(N\) demonstrate a statistically significant probability of outperforming in Fund \(N+1\), demonstrating that operational value-add reflects durable organizational capabilities rather than luck.

Lab 1 · MOIC, IRR, and Value Creation Attribution

Verifies that the Kaplan-Strömberg attribution terms sum identically to total dollar equity gain with zero residual, and confirms that operational EBITDA growth and deleveraging can sustain positive equity returns even under severe valuation multiple contraction.

"""CF5 Lab L05-1: MOIC, IRR, and the Kaplan-Strömberg Value Creation Attribution.

Decomposes total LBO equity value creation into EBITDA growth, multiple expansion,
and deleveraging across alternative exit scenarios.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def decompose_lbo_returns(
    entry_ebitda: float,
    exit_ebitda: float,
    entry_multiple: float,
    exit_multiple: float,
    entry_net_debt: float,
    exit_net_debt: float,
    sponsor_equity_0: float,
    holding_period_years: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Decompose dollar and percentage equity creation via Kaplan-Strömberg (2009) framework."""
    entry_ev = entry_ebitda * entry_multiple
    exit_ev = exit_ebitda * exit_multiple

    entry_equity = entry_ev - entry_net_debt
    exit_equity = exit_ev - exit_net_debt
    total_gain = exit_equity - entry_equity

    # Attribution components
    delta_ebitda = (exit_ebitda - entry_ebitda) * entry_multiple
    delta_multiple = exit_ebitda * (exit_multiple - entry_multiple)
    delta_deleveraging = entry_net_debt - exit_net_debt

    moic = exit_equity / sponsor_equity_0
    irr = (moic ** (1.0 / holding_period_years)) - 1.0

    return {
        "entry_ev": float(entry_ev),
        "exit_ev": float(exit_ev),
        "entry_equity": float(entry_equity),
        "exit_equity": float(exit_equity),
        "total_gain": float(total_gain),
        "delta_ebitda": float(delta_ebitda),
        "delta_multiple": float(delta_multiple),
        "delta_deleveraging": float(delta_deleveraging),
        "pct_ebitda": float(delta_ebitda / total_gain) if total_gain else 0.0,
        "pct_multiple": float(delta_multiple / total_gain) if total_gain else 0.0,
        "pct_deleveraging": float(delta_deleveraging / total_gain) if total_gain else 0.0,
        "moic": float(moic),
        "irr": float(irr),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("CF5 Lab L05-1: MOIC, IRR, and Kaplan-Strömberg Attribution")
    print("=========================================================")

    # Baseline Case ($M)
    entry_eb = 100.0
    exit_eb = 145.0
    entry_m = 10.0
    exit_m = 11.5
    entry_nd = 475.0
    exit_nd = 120.0
    sponsor_eq = 525.0
    years = 5.0

    res = decompose_lbo_returns(
        entry_ebitda=entry_eb,
        exit_ebitda=exit_eb,
        entry_multiple=entry_m,
        exit_multiple=exit_m,
        entry_net_debt=entry_nd,
        exit_net_debt=exit_nd,
        sponsor_equity_0=sponsor_eq,
        holding_period_years=years,
    )

    print(f"Entry Enterprise Value:     ${res['entry_ev']:.2f}M")
    print(f"Exit Enterprise Value:      ${res['exit_ev']:.2f}M")
    print(f"Entry Common Equity:        ${res['entry_equity']:.2f}M")
    print(f"Exit Common Equity:         ${res['exit_equity']:.2f}M")
    print(f"Total Equity Created:       ${res['total_gain']:.2f}M")
    print(f"Sponsor MOIC:               {res['moic']:.3f}x")
    print(f"Sponsor Net IRR:            {res['irr']:.2%}")

    print("\nKaplan-Strömberg Value Creation Attribution:")
    print(f"  1. Operational EBITDA Gain: ${res['delta_ebitda']:.2f}M ({res['pct_ebitda']:.1%})")
    print(f"  2. Multiple Expansion:      ${res['delta_multiple']:.2f}M ({res['pct_multiple']:.1%})")
    print(f"  3. Deleveraging / Debt Pay: ${res['delta_deleveraging']:.2f}M ({res['pct_deleveraging']:.1%})")
    sum_attr = res["delta_ebitda"] + res["delta_multiple"] + res["delta_deleveraging"]
    diff_val = abs(sum_attr - res["total_gain"])
    print(f"  Sum of Components:          ${sum_attr:.2f}M (Identity Difference: ${diff_val:.4f}M)")

    # Multiple Contraction Downside Scenario (exit at 8.5x multiple)
    downside = decompose_lbo_returns(
        entry_ebitda=entry_eb,
        exit_ebitda=exit_eb,
        entry_multiple=entry_m,
        exit_multiple=8.5,
        entry_net_debt=entry_nd,
        exit_net_debt=exit_nd,
        sponsor_equity_0=sponsor_eq,
        holding_period_years=years,
    )
    print("\nDownside Scenario (Exit Multiple Contraction to 8.5x):")
    print(f"  Exit Equity:                ${downside['exit_equity']:.2f}M")
    print(f"  Total Equity Created:       ${downside['total_gain']:.2f}M")
    print(f"  Sponsor MOIC:               {downside['moic']:.3f}x")
    print(f"  Sponsor Net IRR:            {downside['irr']:.2%}")
    print(f"  Multiple Expansion Loss:    ${downside['delta_multiple']:.2f}M")
    print(f"  EBITDA + Deleveraging Gain: ${downside['delta_ebitda'] + downside['delta_deleveraging']:.2f}M")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The standard Kaplan-Strömberg identity assumes a simple single-entry, single-exit buyout without interim cash flows or add-on transactions. In real-world PE operations, three complexities alter the attribution mechanics:

  1. M&A Add-On Capital and Non-Organic EBITDA: When a portfolio platform company acquires smaller add-on competitors during the hold period, EBITDA growth is partially purchased rather than organically generated. Attributing non-organic EBITDA growth requires netting the capital invested in add-ons from operational gains.
  2. Interim Dividend Recapitalizations: When a portfolio company issues additional debt mid-hold to fund a special dividend distribution to the sponsor, debt increases while cash is returned early. Tracking attribution requires time-weighting intermediate distributions against ending NAV.
  3. GP Carried Interest Waterfall: The attribution identity operates at the portfolio company level (gross returns). Net returns delivered to LPs must reflect the private equity partnership waterfall: an 8% preferred return hurdle, 100% GP catch-up, and an 80/20 carried interest split on net gains.

Misconceptions

  1. An LBO investment delivering a 40% IRR always creates more economic dollar wealth than one delivering a 20% IRR.

    IRR measures the annualized discount rate, making it hyper-sensitive to holding period length. A 40% IRR achieved on a 1-year flip of a $100M equity check yields a profit of $40M (1.40x MOIC). In contrast, a 20% IRR achieved over a 5-year holding period yields an ending equity value of \(100 \times (1.20)^5 = \$248.8\text{M}\), generating $148.8M in profit (2.49x MOIC)—more than 3.7 times the absolute dollar wealth created.

  2. Valuation multiple expansion in an LBO reflects managerial skill by the private equity sponsor.

    Valuation multiples are largely determined by macro-level interest rates, equity market risk appetite, and sector-wide sentiment. Underwriting an LBO assuming exit multiple expansion introduces speculative market timing. Institutional investment committees require deals to meet return hurdles under flat or contracting exit multiples, ensuring returns are grounded in EBITDA growth and debt paydown.

  3. Private equity buyout returns can be evaluated purely using the S&P 500 index return over the same period.

    Direct comparisons against public indices ignore the timing, illiquidity, and leverage profile of private equity cash flows. Institutional LPs utilize Public Market Equivalent (PME) metrics (such as the Kaplan-Schoar PME), which invest and withdraw cash flows from the public benchmark on the exact dates of capital calls and distributions, adjusting for market beta and leverage.

Problem Set

Mechanics

A sponsor invests $200.0M of equity in a buyout. At the end of Year 4, the company is sold, generating total net equity proceeds to the sponsor of $518.4M. Calculate the sponsor's MOIC and annualized net IRR.

Solution

\(\text{MOIC} = \frac{518.4}{200.0} = 2.592\text{x}\). \(\text{IRR} = \text{MOIC}^{1/4} - 1 = (2.592)^{0.25} - 1 = 1.2687 - 1 = 26.87\%\).

Entry EBITDA is $50.0M at a 9.0x multiple with $300.0M in net debt. At Year 5 exit, EBITDA reaches $75.0M, exit multiple is 10.0x, and net debt has been reduced to $120.0M through cash sweeps. Calculate entry equity, exit equity, and decompose the equity value created into EBITDA growth, multiple expansion, and deleveraging.

Solution

Entry EV = \(50.0 \times 9.0 = \$450.0\text{M}\). Entry Equity = \(450.0 - 300.0 = \$150.0\text{M}\). Exit EV = \(75.0 \times 10.0 = \$750.0\text{M}\). Exit Equity = \(750.0 - 120.0 = \$630.0\text{M}\). Total Equity Created = \(630.0 - 150.0 = \$480.0\text{M}\). Kaplan-Strömberg decomposition: Operational EBITDA growth = \((75.0 - 50.0) \times 9.0 = \$225.0\text{M}\) (46.88%). Multiple expansion = \(75.0 \times (10.0 - 9.0) = \$75.0\text{M}\) (15.62%). Deleveraging = \(300.0 - 120.0 = \$180.0\text{M}\) (37.50%). Sum = \(225.0 + 75.0 + 180.0 = \$480.0\text{M}\).

In the previous problem, assume the exit multiple contracts from 9.0x to 7.5x while EBITDA and debt paydown remain identical. Calculate the new exit equity value, multiple expansion dollar loss, and new sponsor MOIC.

Solution

New Exit EV = \(75.0 \times 7.5 = \$562.5\text{M}\). Exit Equity = \(562.5 - 120.0 = \$442.5\text{M}\). Total Equity Created = \(442.5 - 150.0 = \$292.5\text{M}\). Multiple expansion component = \(75.0 \times (7.5 - 9.0) = -\$112.5\text{M}\). Operational growth ($225.0M) + Deleveraging ($180.0M) = $405.0M. Net gain = \(405.0 - 112.5 = \$292.5\text{M}\). New MOIC = \(442.5 / 150.0 = 2.950\text{x}\) (down from 4.20x).

Proof

Prove that in the continuous compounding limit where \(T \to 0\), the annualized IRR approaches infinity for any \(\text{MOIC} > 1\), demonstrating why IRR distortedly incentivizes short holding periods.

Solution

Let \(\text{MOIC} = M > 1\). Under discrete compounding, \(\text{IRR}(T) = M^{1/T} - 1 = \exp\left(\frac{\ln M}{T}\right) - 1\). Since \(M > 1\), \(\ln M > 0\). As \(T \to 0^+\), the exponent \(\frac{\ln M}{T} \to +\infty\), so \(\lim_{T \to 0^+} \text{IRR}(T) = +\infty\). Even a microscopic capital gain \(\Delta E > 0\) realized over a few weeks generates an astronomically large annualized IRR, confirming that IRR creates perverse incentives for quick exits that do not compound substantial wealth for LPs. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that an alternative decomposition grouping multiple expansion on entry EBITDA: \(\Delta E = (\text{EBITDA}_T - \text{EBITDA}_0) \cdot M_T + \text{EBITDA}_0 \cdot (M_T - M_0) + \Delta \text{Deleveraging}\) is mathematically identical to the Kaplan-Strömberg identity, and explain why Kaplan-Strömberg's weighting on \(M_0\) is preferred in corporate finance.

Solution

Notice that \((\text{EBITDA}_T - \text{EBITDA}_0) \cdot M_T + \text{EBITDA}_0 \cdot (M_T - M_0) = \text{EBITDA}_T M_T - \text{EBITDA}_0 M_T + \text{EBITDA}_0 M_T - \text{EBITDA}_0 M_0 = \text{EBITDA}_T M_T - \text{EBITDA}_0 M_0 = \text{EV}_T - \text{EV}_0\). Both decompositions sum to the exact Enterprise Value difference. Kaplan & Strömberg (2009) weight EBITDA growth by the entry multiple \(M_0\) because \(M_0\) represents the observable, contractually fixed valuation basis at the time the investment was underwritten. Weighting growth by \(M_T\) would contaminate the operational performance metric with post-acquisition macro multiple fluctuations. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that if a financial sponsor finances an LBO with debt-to-enterprise-value ratio \(L = D_0 / \text{EV}_0\), then under zero multiple expansion (\(M_T = M_0\)) and zero deleveraging (\(D_T = D_0\)), the sponsor's equity MOIC equals \(1 + \frac{g}{1 - L}\), where \(g = (\text{EBITDA}_T - \text{EBITDA}_0) / \text{EBITDA}_0\) is the percentage growth in EBITDA.

Solution

Entry Equity is \(E_0 = \text{EV}_0 - D_0 = \text{EV}_0(1 - L)\). Since \(M_T = M_0\), \(\text{EV}_T = \text{EBITDA}_T M_0 = \text{EBITDA}_0(1 + g)M_0 = \text{EV}_0(1 + g)\). Since net debt is unchanged, \(E_T = \text{EV}_T - D_0 = \text{EV}_0(1 + g) - L \cdot \text{EV}_0 = \text{EV}_0(1 + g - L)\). The equity MOIC is \(\frac{E_T}{E_0} = \frac{\text{EV}_0(1 + g - L)}{\text{EV}_0(1 - L)} = \frac{1 - L + g}{1 - L} = 1 + \frac{g}{1 - L}\). This proves that financial leverage scales the equity multiplier on operational growth by \(\frac{1}{1 - L}\). For example, with 60% leverage (\(L = 0.60\)), a 20% operational gain (\(g = 0.20\)) generates an equity gain of \(\frac{0.20}{0.40} = 50\%\) (\(\text{MOIC} = 1.50\text{x}\)). \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

According to Kaplan & Schoar (2005), how does persistence in private equity GP returns compare to mutual fund manager persistence?

Solution

In public mutual fund markets (such as Carhart 1997), performance persistence is minimal or negative after adjusting for fees and style factors. In stark contrast, Kaplan & Schoar (2005, Table 2 & Table 4) demonstrated strong, statistically significant performance persistence across consecutive private equity partnerships. GPs that outperformed in one fund vintage were significantly more likely to outperform in subsequent vintages, indicating that proprietary deal flow, governance structures, and operational playbooks provide enduring competitive advantages.

What did Kaplan & Strömberg (2009) document regarding the relative contribution of operational improvements versus financial leverage across buyout cohorts?

Solution

Kaplan & Strömberg (2009) documented that in the 1980s buyout wave, operational improvements accounted for less than 25% of equity returns, with debt paydown and leverage driving the rest. By the 2000s, operational improvement (measured by industry-adjusted EBITDA growth and operating margin expansion) accounted for roughly 45% to 50% of total returns, reflecting professionalization of sponsor operating partner teams and tighter credit market constraints.

How does the Kaplan-Schoar Public Market Equivalent (PME) metric evaluate whether a private equity fund outperformed the public equity market?

Solution

The Kaplan-Schoar PME discounts all fund distributions and capital calls at the realized return of a public benchmark (e.g., S&P 500). \(\text{PME} = \frac{\sum D_t / (I_t / I_0)}{\sum C_t / (I_t / I_0)}\), where \(I_t\) is the public index level. A PME strictly greater than 1.0 indicates that investing in the private equity fund generated more terminal wealth than investing identical cash flows into the public market index on the exact same dates.

Research

Analyze the structural growth of continuation funds (GP-led secondary transactions) and their effect on MOIC vs IRR realization.

Solution

Continuation funds allow a private equity sponsor to roll high-performing "trophy" assets out of an expiring fund (nearing its 10-year term) into a newly formed continuation vehicle backed by secondary investors. This eliminates forced exits, allowing the sponsor to crystallize a high MOIC for existing LPs while retaining operational control and carried interest participation for an additional 4–6 year hold.

How do subscription credit lines (capital call credit facilities) distort fund-level IRRs reported to limited partners?

Solution

Subscription credit lines allow GPs to borrow from commercial banks to fund initial portfolio investments rather than calling capital from LPs immediately. By delaying LP capital calls by 6 to 12 months, the time denominator in the fund IRR equation is artificially shortened, boosting reported net IRR by 200 to 500 basis points without increasing absolute dollar profit or MOIC.

Evaluate the economic rationale behind "buy-and-build" platform strategies in private equity value creation.

Solution

In a buy-and-build strategy, a sponsor acquires a large "platform" company at a high multiple (e.g., 12.0x EBITDA) and then executes multiple small bolt-on acquisitions of regional competitors at lower multiples (e.g., 6.0x–8.0x EBITDA). Integrating bolt-on cash flows into the platform captures immediate multiple arbitrage (the "multiple arbitrage step-up") alongside supply chain synergies, driving operational EBITDA expansion.
