When external capital is costlier than internally generated funds due to adverse selection and agency frictions, internal cash flow volatility translates directly into investment volatility. Froot, Scharfstein, and Stein (1993) formulate corporate risk management as a coordinating mechanism between financing and investment: hedging preserves internal liquidity, shielding valuable capital expenditures from costly external capital market cutbacks. This lesson formalizes the Froot-Scharfstein-Stein internal financing model, derives the optimal hedge ratio when investment returns are strictly concave, explores the minimum-variance hedge ratio \(h^* = \mathrm{Cov}(\Delta S, \Delta F) / \mathrm{Var}(\Delta F)\), and isolates the basis risk condition where partial hedging dominates complete insurance.

1Costly external capital and internal cash flow supply

Why does cash flow volatility hurt an operating corporation even if its shareholders are well diversified? Froot, Scharfstein, and Stein (1993) demonstrate that capital market imperfections make external equity and debt significantly more expensive than internal cash flows. When cash flows plunge, firms must either incur punitive external financing deadweight costs or curtail profitable capital investments.

Definition 2.1 (Froot–Scharfstein–Stein model)

The Froot–Scharfstein–Stein model is an equilibrium corporate finance framework in which external financing carries convex deadweight costs \(C(e)\), rendering enterprise value strictly concave in internal cash flow \(w\) and creating an optimal corporate hedging demand to preserve internal investment capacity.

Proposition 2.1 (strict concavity of the corporate value function)

Let a firm invest capital \(I \ge 0\) to generate output \(f(I)\) with \(f'(I) > 0\) and \(f''(I) < 0\). If internal wealth is \(w\) and external funds raised are \(e = \max(0, I - w)\) with convex cost \(C(e)\) (\(C'(e) \ge 0, C''(e) > 0\)), then the optimized value function

\[ V(w) = \max_{I \ge 0} \big[ f(I) - I - C(I - w) \big] \]

is strictly concave in internal wealth: \(V''(w) < 0\).

Proof

The first-order necessary condition for optimal investment \(I^*(w)\) is

\[ f'(I^*) - 1 - C'(I^* - w) = 0. \]

Differentiating implicitly with respect to \(w\) gives

\[ f''(I^*) \frac{dI^*}{dw} - C''(I^* - w) \left( \frac{dI^*}{dw} - 1 \right) = 0 \implies \frac{dI^*}{dw} = \frac{C''}{C'' - f''} \in (0, 1). \]

By the envelope theorem, \(V'(w) = C'(I^*(w) - w)\). Differentiating again with respect to \(w\) yields

\[ V''(w) = C''(I^* - w) \left( \frac{dI^*}{dw} - 1 \right) = C'' \left( \frac{C''}{C'' - f''} - 1 \right) = \frac{C'' f''}{C'' - f''}. \]

Since \(C'' > 0\) and \(f'' < 0\), the numerator is strictly negative and the denominator is strictly positive, establishing \(V''(w) < 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

2The minimum-variance hedge ratio

When financial market derivatives correlate imperfectly with the underlying corporate exposure (basis risk), the firm must select a position size that minimizes cash flow variance.

Definition 2.2 (minimum-variance hedge ratio)

Let \(\Delta S\) denote the change in spot cash flow and \(\Delta F\) denote the price change of a hedging futures or forward contract. The minimum-variance hedge ratio \(h^*\) is the hedge ratio that minimizes the variance of the hedged position \(\Delta H = \Delta S - h \Delta F\):

\[ h^* = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S, \Delta F)}{\operatorname{Var}(\Delta F)} = \rho_{SF} \frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F}, \]

where \(\rho_{SF}\) is the correlation coefficient, \(\sigma_S\) is the standard deviation of spot changes, and \(\sigma_F\) is the standard deviation of futures price changes.

Proposition 2.2 (variance reduction and hedge effectiveness)

Under the optimal minimum-variance hedge ratio \(h^*\), the variance of the hedged cash flow is

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\Delta H^*) = \operatorname{Var}(\Delta S)(1 - \rho_{SF}^2). \]

The proportion of variance eliminated (hedge effectiveness \(R^2\)) is exactly \(\rho_{SF}^2\).

Proof

The variance of the hedged position is a quadratic function of \(h\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\Delta H) = \operatorname{Var}(\Delta S) - 2h \operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S, \Delta F) + h^2 \operatorname{Var}(\Delta F). \]

Setting the first derivative with respect to \(h\) to zero yields

\[ -2 \operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S, \Delta F) + 2h \operatorname{Var}(\Delta F) = 0 \implies h^* = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S, \Delta F)}{\operatorname{Var}(\Delta F)}. \]

Substituting \(h^*\) back into the variance expression:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\Delta H^*) &= \sigma_S^2 - 2 \frac{\rho \sigma_S \sigma_F}{\sigma_F^2} (\rho \sigma_S \sigma_F) + \frac{\rho^2 \sigma_S^2 \sigma_F^2}{\sigma_F^4} \sigma_F^2 \\ &= \sigma_S^2 - 2 \rho^2 \sigma_S^2 + \rho^2 \sigma_S^2 = \sigma_S^2 (1 - \rho^2). \quad \blacksquare \end{aligned} \]

Example 2.1 (cross-hedging jet fuel with crude oil futures)

An airline has monthly jet fuel price volatility \(\sigma_S = 0.080\) ($/gallon). Because jet fuel futures are illiquid, the treasurer cross-hedges using West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil futures, which have volatility \(\sigma_F = 0.090\) and correlation \(\rho_{SF} = 0.850\).

The optimal minimum-variance hedge ratio is

\[ h^* = 0.850 \times \frac{0.080}{0.090} = 0.7556. \]

The hedge effectiveness is \(R^2 = (0.850)^2 = 0.7225\) (72.25%). Unhedged variance of \(0.00640\) is reduced to \((0.00640) \times (1 - 0.7225) = 0.001776\), eliminating 72.25% of fuel cash flow variance.

Lab 1 · The Froot–Scharfstein–Stein Model & Minimum-Variance Hedge

Verifies that the corporate value function is strictly concave in internal wealth when external financing has quadratic costs, computes the optimal hedge ratio under basis risk, and evaluates the variance reduction ratio.

"""CF9 lab L02-1: Froot-Scharfstein-Stein (1993) Model and Minimum-Variance Hedge Ratio.

Simulates the Froot, Scharfstein & Stein (1993) framework:
1. Concave investment returns F(I) and convex external financing cost C(e).
2. Proves that costly external finance induces endogenous corporate risk aversion (V''(w) < 0).
3. Demonstrates underinvestment when internal funds drop.
4. Computes the analytical minimum-variance hedge ratio h* = Cov(w, f) / Var(f).
5. Shows that hedging internal cash flow restores optimal investment and increases expected value.
Standard library only; deterministic execution.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from statistics import NormalDist


def production_function(investment: float, a: float = 20.0, alpha: float = 0.5) -> float:
    """Concave output function: F(I) = a * I^alpha."""
    if investment <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return float(a * (investment**alpha))


def external_financing_cost(external_funds: float, lambda_cost: float = 0.05) -> float:
    """Convex deadweight cost of external finance: C(e) = lambda * e^2 for e > 0, 0 otherwise."""
    if external_funds <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return lambda_cost * (external_funds**2)


def solve_optimal_investment(
    internal_cash: float, a: float = 20.0, alpha: float = 0.5, lambda_cost: float = 0.05
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Find optimal investment I* that maximizes Net Value = F(I) - I - C(I - internal_cash)."""
    # First-best unconstrained investment where F'(I_fb) = 1
    i_fb = (alpha * a) ** (1.0 / (1.0 - alpha))
    if internal_cash >= i_fb:
        net_val = production_function(i_fb, a, alpha) - i_fb
        return {"investment": i_fb, "external_funds": 0.0, "net_value": net_val}

    # Bisection search on [internal_cash, i_fb] for F'(I) - 1 - 2*lambda*(I - internal_cash) = 0
    low = max(0.01, internal_cash)
    high = i_fb
    for _ in range(40):
        mid = (low + high) / 2.0
        f_prime = alpha * a * (mid ** (alpha - 1.0))
        g = f_prime - 1.0 - 2.0 * lambda_cost * (mid - internal_cash)
        if g > 0.0:
            low = mid
        else:
            high = mid

    best_i = (low + high) / 2.0
    e_star = max(0.0, best_i - internal_cash)
    output = production_function(best_i, a, alpha)
    cost = best_i + external_financing_cost(e_star, lambda_cost)
    return {
        "investment": best_i,
        "external_funds": e_star,
        "net_value": output - cost,
    }


def simulate_fss_hedging(
    w_bar: float = 25.0,
    sigma_w: float = 12.0,
    sigma_f: float = 15.0,
    rho_wf: float = 0.85,
    n_states: int = 2000,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate unhedged vs minimum-variance hedged internal cash flows and corporate investment."""
    dist = NormalDist()
    p1 = [(i + 0.5) / n_states for i in range(n_states)]
    p2 = [((i * 137) % n_states + 0.5) / n_states for i in range(n_states)]

    z1 = [dist.inv_cdf(p) for p in p1]
    z2_raw = [dist.inv_cdf(p) for p in p2]

    # Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization for exact independence
    dot12 = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(z1, z2_raw, strict=True))
    dot11 = sum(a * a for a in z1)
    z2_ortho = [b - (dot12 / dot11) * a for a, b in zip(z1, z2_raw, strict=True)]
    scale2 = math.sqrt(dot11 / sum(b * b for b in z2_ortho))
    z2 = [b * scale2 for b in z2_ortho]

    cov_wf = rho_wf * sigma_w * sigma_f
    var_f = sigma_f**2
    h_star = cov_wf / var_f  # Minimum-variance hedge ratio

    sqrt_factor = math.sqrt(max(0.0, 1.0 - rho_wf**2))

    f_shocks = [sigma_f * s1 for s1 in z1]
    w_shocks = [sigma_w * (rho_wf * s1 + sqrt_factor * s2) for s1, s2 in zip(z1, z2, strict=True)]

    w_unhedged = [max(1.0, w_bar + w) for w in w_shocks]
    w_hedged = [max(1.0, w_bar + w - h_star * f) for w, f in zip(w_shocks, f_shocks, strict=True)]

    vals_unhedged = [solve_optimal_investment(w)["net_value"] for w in w_unhedged]
    vals_hedged = [solve_optimal_investment(w)["net_value"] for w in w_hedged]
    inv_unhedged = [solve_optimal_investment(w)["investment"] for w in w_unhedged]
    inv_hedged = [solve_optimal_investment(w)["investment"] for w in w_hedged]

    mean_val_unhedged = sum(vals_unhedged) / n_states
    mean_val_hedged = sum(vals_hedged) / n_states
    fss_value_gain = mean_val_hedged - mean_val_unhedged

    var_w_unhedged = sum((w - w_bar) ** 2 for w in w_unhedged) / n_states
    mean_w_hedged = sum(w_hedged) / n_states
    var_w_hedged = sum((w - mean_w_hedged) ** 2 for w in w_hedged) / n_states
    var_reduction = 1.0 - (var_w_hedged / var_w_unhedged)

    return {
        "h_star": h_star,
        "cov_wf": cov_wf,
        "var_f": var_f,
        "var_w_unhedged": var_w_unhedged,
        "var_w_hedged": var_w_hedged,
        "var_reduction": var_reduction,
        "mean_val_unhedged": mean_val_unhedged,
        "mean_val_hedged": mean_val_hedged,
        "fss_value_gain": fss_value_gain,
        "mean_inv_unhedged": sum(inv_unhedged) / n_states,
        "mean_inv_hedged": sum(inv_hedged) / n_states,
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("CF9 Lab L02-1: Froot-Scharfstein-Stein (1993) Optimal Hedging")
    print("===========================================================")

    # Demonstrate concavity of firm value in internal cash flow w
    w_low = 10.0
    w_mid = 25.0
    w_high = 40.0
    res_low = solve_optimal_investment(w_low)
    res_mid = solve_optimal_investment(w_mid)

    print("1. Costly External Finance and Endogenous Risk Aversion (V''(w) < 0):")
    print(
        f"   Low internal funds  (w = ${w_low:.1f}M): Inv = ${res_low['investment']:.2f}M, "
        f"Ext = ${res_low['external_funds']:.2f}M, Net Val = ${res_low['net_value']:.2f}M"

    print(f"   Concavity confirmed (V''(w) < 0):    {mv_low > mv_high} (Diminishing marginal value)")

    sim = simulate_fss_hedging()
    print("\n2. Minimum-Variance Hedge Ratio & Cash Flow Stabilization:")

    print(f"   Futures variance Var(f):  {sim['var_f']:.2f}")

    print(f"   Hedged cash flow Var(w):  {sim['var_w_hedged']:.2f}")
    print(f"   Variance reduction (R^2): {sim['var_reduction']:.2%}")

    print("\n3. Expected Investment and Value Creation:")
    print(f"   Mean investment unhedged: ${sim['mean_inv_unhedged']:.2f}M")
    print(f"   Mean investment hedged:   ${sim['mean_inv_hedged']:.2f}M")
    print(f"   Unhedged expected value:  ${sim['mean_val_unhedged']:.2f}M")
    print(f"   Hedged expected value:    ${sim['mean_val_hedged']:.2f}M")
    pct_val_gain = sim["fss_value_gain"] / sim["mean_val_unhedged"]
    print(f"   FSS Hedging Value Added:  ${sim['fss_value_gain']:.2f}M ({pct_val_gain:.2%})")

3Where the canon breaks

Standard risk management practice assumes that the minimum-variance hedge ratio is static and universally optimal. This assumption fails under four operational conditions:

  • Quantity uncertainty: When sales volumes are stochastic and correlated with price (e.g. an exporter whose export quantity drops when the domestic currency appreciates), the revenue exposure is non-linear (\(P \cdot Q\)), making linear forward contracts imperfect and potentially amplifying variance if over-hedged.
  • Regime shifts in correlation: Correlations between spot commodities and proxy futures (\(\rho_{SF}\)) are non-stationary, collapsing during systemic financial crises exactly when risk protection is most critical.
  • Liquidity and cash drains: Futures contracts are marked to market daily, requiring cash for variation margin calls. A firm with massive unrealized derivative gains can suffer liquidity distress if margin outflows occur before underlying cash flows are collected (the Metallgesellschaft crisis of 1993).

4Misconceptions

  1. A hedge ratio of 1.0 (one-to-one notional cover) is always optimal for an unhedged commercial exposure.

    A 1.0 hedge ratio minimizes variance only if spot and futures price changes are perfectly correlated with identical volatilities (\(\rho = 1\) and \(\sigma_S = \sigma_F\)). When basis risk or volatility differentials exist, \(h^* = \rho (\sigma_S / \sigma_F) \ne 1\).

  2. Corporate hedging is designed to maximize speculative trading profits for the treasury department.

    Under FSS theory, corporate hedging aims strictly to smooth internal liquidity so that positive-NPV capital investment programs remain insulated from external capital market disruptions.

  3. If external capital markets are competitive, external financing is costless.

    Competitive pricing does not eliminate adverse selection (Myers-Majluf) or debt overhang (Myers), both of which impose deadweight costs that make external equity and junior debt costlier than retained earnings.

5Practice problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

M1 · Minimum-variance hedge ratio computation

An agricultural company wishes to hedge its soybean crop using soybean futures contracts. Monthly spot price volatility is \(\sigma_S = 0.12\), futures price volatility is \(\sigma_F = 0.15\), and the correlation between spot and futures returns is \(\rho = 0.90\). Calculate the optimal hedge ratio \(h^*\) and the percentage of spot variance eliminated by the hedge.

Solution

The optimal minimum-variance hedge ratio is h* = rho * (sigma_S / sigma_F) = 0.90 * (0.12 / 0.15) = 0.72. The proportion of spot variance eliminated is R^2 = rho^2 = (0.90)^2 = 0.81, or 81.0%.

M2 · FSS investment cutback under liquidity shock

A firm's production function is \(f(I) = 20 \sqrt{I}\). External financing cost is \(C(e) = 0.10 e^2\) where \(e = I - w\). If internal wealth drops from \(w = \$36\mathrm{M}\) to \(w = \$16\mathrm{M}\), solve the first-order condition \(10/\sqrt{I} - 1 - 0.20(I - w) = 0\) to determine the direction and qualitative magnitude of the investment response.

Solution

When w = 36, I = 36 yields f'(36) - 1 - 0 = 10/6 - 1 = 0.667 > 0, so optimal I* > 36 and e > 0. When internal cash drops to w = 16, the marginal cost of external funds 0.20(I - 16) shifts upward, forcing the firm to curtail investment I* to restore first-order optimality.

M3 · Residual hedged cash flow variance

A copper producer has unhedged cash flow standard deviation of $25M. The treasury hedges copper output using Comex copper futures contracts, achieving an estimated correlation of \(\rho = 0.96\). What is the standard deviation of hedged cash flows?

Solution

The residual standard deviation is sigma_hedged = sigma_unhedged * sqrt(1 - rho^2) = 25 * sqrt(1 - 0.96^2) = 25 * sqrt(1 - 0.9216) = 25 * sqrt(0.0784) = 25 * 0.28 = $7.0M.

Tier 2 · Proof

P1 · Second-order condition for minimum-variance hedge

Prove that the minimum-variance hedge ratio \(h^* = \operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S, \Delta F) / \operatorname{Var}(\Delta F)\) represents a unique global minimum of the portfolio variance function \(\operatorname{Var}(\Delta H)\).

Solution

The variance of the hedged position is V(h) = Var(Delta S) - 2h Cov(Delta S, Delta F) + h^2 Var(Delta F). The second derivative with respect to h is d^2 V / dh^2 = 2 Var(Delta F). Since Var(Delta F) > 0 for any non-degenerate futures contract, the second derivative is strictly positive everywhere. The objective function is strictly convex, confirming that the critical point h* is a unique global minimum.

P2 · Value concavity implies hedging demand

Let internal cash flow \(w\) be a random variable with mean \(\bar{w}\) and non-zero variance \(\sigma_w^2\). Under the FSS framework, the firm value function satisfies \(V''(w) < 0\). Prove that entering a forward contract that guarantees deterministic cash flow \(\bar{w}\) strictly increases expected enterprise value: \(\mathbb{E}[V(w)] < V(\bar{w})\).

Solution

By Jensen's inequality, for any strictly concave function V(.) and non-degenerate random variable w, E[V(w)] < V(E[w]). Since E[w] = \bar{w}, expected enterprise value under the unhedged cash flow is strictly lower than enterprise value under the hedged deterministic cash flow V(\bar{w}). Hedging creates value by eliminating the deadweight loss of cash flow volatility.

P3 · Sub-optimality of unitary hedge ratio under basis risk

Prove that if \(\rho_{SF} \ne \sigma_F / \sigma_S\), setting an unadjusted one-to-one hedge ratio (\(h = 1\)) results in higher portfolio variance than the minimum-variance hedge ratio \(h^*\).

Solution

The variance difference is Var(Delta H|_{h=1}) - Var(Delta H|_{h*}) = Var(Delta F) * (1 - h*)^2. Since h* = rho * (sigma_S / sigma_F), 1 - h* = 0 if and only if rho * sigma_S = sigma_F, which is equivalent to rho = sigma_F / sigma_S. Whenever rho != sigma_F / sigma_S, (1 - h*)^2 > 0, so Var(Delta F) * (1 - h*)^2 > 0, proving that a unitary hedge strictly increases portfolio variance relative to h*.

Tier 3 · Empirical

E1 · R&D intensity and corporate hedging

Empirical corporate finance studies find a strong positive correlation between firm R&D expenditure to sales ratios and the probability of using financial derivatives. Explain this empirical finding using the Froot–Scharfstein–Stein framework.

Solution

R&D-intensive firms possess abundant valuable growth options, making investment return f(I) highly sensitive to capital outlays. Furthermore, R&D assets are intangible and suffer severe information asymmetry, making external financing exceptionally costly. These firms have high C''(e), producing sharp value concavity V''(w) and strong demand to hedge internal cash flows.

E2 · Capital expenditure sensitivity to cash flow

Fazzari, Hubbard, and Petersen (1988) measure the sensitivity of capital expenditure to cash flow shocks. How does an active corporate hedging program alter the empirical investment-cash flow sensitivity coefficient?

Solution

Under FSS theory, corporate hedging smooths internal cash flow realizations, preventing internal wealth from falling into regions where external financing constraints bind. Consequently, firms with robust hedging programs exhibit significantly lower empirical investment-cash flow sensitivity because investment expenditures remain stable across market cycles.

E3 · OLS estimation of hedge ratios

In practice, treasurers estimate \(h^*\) by running an ordinary least squares regression: \(\Delta S_t = \alpha + h \Delta F_t + \epsilon_t\). What econometric problem arises when spot and futures price levels are non-stationary and cointegrated?

Solution

If spot and futures price series are cointegrated I(1) processes, running simple OLS on first differences ignores the long-run cointegrating relationship, leading to omitted variable bias. An error correction model (VECM) incorporating the lagged cointegrating residual must be estimated to obtain consistent short-run and long-run hedge ratios.

Tier 4 · Research

R1 · Coordinating hedging with cash holdings

Firms can buffer liquidity shocks either by holding liquid cash reserves or by purchasing derivative hedges. Compare the trade-offs of cash holdings (carrying costs, agency costs of free cash flow) versus derivative contracts (counterparty risk, basis risk, margin calls).

Solution

Cash holdings provide immediate liquidity without basis risk or counterparty default exposure, but impose tax disadvantages and agency costs if managers waste free cash flow on empire-building acquisitions. Derivative hedges require minimal capital upfront and generate no agency cash hoard, but introduce basis risk, counterparty credit exposure, and margin liquidity risk.

R2 · Endogenous investment opportunities

Suppose investment opportunities are positively correlated with cash flows (e.g. an oil company has more profitable drilling projects when crude prices are high). How does this state-dependent investment schedule alter the optimal FSS hedge ratio?

Solution

When investment opportunities are procyclical, the firm requires more capital precisely when oil prices and cash flows are high. A forward hedge that locks in cash flows would leave the firm capital-constrained during high-price boom periods. FSS show that the optimal hedge ratio is lower when cash flows and investment opportunities are positively correlated.

R3 · The Metallgesellschaft liquidity crisis

In 1993, Metallgesellschaft Refining & Marketing (MGRM) hedged long-term fixed-price oil customer supply commitments using rolling short-dated oil futures. Explain why this "stack-and-roll" hedge suffered catastrophic losses when oil markets shifted from backwardation into contango.

Solution

In contango, distant futures trade at a premium to spot prices. Rolling expiring short-term futures forward required selling low and buying high, producing continuous rollover losses. Concurrently, falling spot prices triggered massive daily variation margin cash drains ($1.3 billion) before customer revenues matured, precipitating a severe liquidity crisis.

    FSS (1993) show that when external capital carries deadweight costs, internal cash flow volatility disrupts investment, justifying corporate risk management.

  2. If \(\sigma_S = 0.080\), \(\sigma_F = 0.090\), and \(\rho_{SF} = 0.850\), what is the optimal minimum-variance hedge ratio \(h^*\)?

    h* = rho * (sigma_S / sigma_F) = 0.850 * (0.080 / 0.090) = 0.7556.

  3. What represents the proportion of cash flow variance eliminated by an optimal minimum-variance hedge?

    Options

    Hedge effectiveness equals the squared correlation coefficient: \(R^2 = \rho_{SF}^2\).

  4. In Example 2.1, what is the hedge effectiveness \(R^2\) in percent when \(\rho_{SF} = 0.850\)?

    R^2 = (0.850)^2 = 0.7225, or 72.25%.

  5. Why does the corporate value function \(V(w)\) exhibit strict concavity in the FSS framework?

    Options

    As shown in Proposition 2.1, \(V''(w) = C'' f'' / (C'' - f'') < 0\) due to convex financing costs (\(C'' > 0\)) and concave returns (\(f'' < 0\)).

  6. What caused the 1993 liquidity collapse of Metallgesellschaft Refining & Marketing?

    Options

    MGRM stacked short-term futures against long-term fixed-price commitments; falling oil prices and contango caused massive rollover losses and margin cash drains.

  7. In Problem M1, what percentage of spot variance is eliminated when \(\rho = 0.90\)?

    Variance reduction = rho^2 = (0.90)^2 = 0.81 = 81.0%.

  8. If spot and futures price changes are negatively correlated (\(\rho < 0\)), what is the sign of the optimal minimum-variance hedge ratio?

    Options

    Since h* = rho * (sigma_S / sigma_F), a negative correlation yields a negative hedge ratio, indicating that a long position in futures minimizes variance.
