Executive Compensation & Governance Indices

  • Uses: agency costs, corporate governance
  • Introduces: executive compensation, shareholder rights indices
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or convention
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Corporate governance relies on two primary mechanisms to align managerial decisions with shareholder interests: executive compensation design and external takeover discipline. Executive compensation links managerial wealth to stock price performance and equity volatility, balancing risk-sharing against effort incentives. Simultaneously, the market for corporate control disciplines underperforming managers through hostile takeovers. However, entrenched executives deploy defensive provisions—such as poison pills and staggered boards—to insulate themselves from replacement. Researchers quantify managerial entrenchment using indices such as the 24-provision G-Index and the 6-provision E-Index. This lesson develops the economics of executive compensation, investigates how specific antitakeover provisions insulate managers, and demonstrates why focused entrenchment indices outperform noisy kitchen-sink governance metrics.

1Economics of Executive Compensation and Incentive Sensitivity

Jensen and Meckling (1976, citing jensen1976theory) showed that separation of ownership and control generates agency costs whenever managers hold less than 100% of corporate equity. An essential internal governance mechanism designed to counteract these agency frictions is executive compensation.

Definition 1.1 (Executive compensation contract)

An executive compensation contract is an incentive compensation structure awarded by the board of directors to senior corporate officers. In modern corporations, total executive pay consists of:

  1. Base salary: Fixed cash compensation independent of performance.
  2. Annual bonus: Short-term performance pay tied to annual accounting metrics (EBITDA, EPS, return on invested capital).
  3. Restricted stock: Long-term equity grants vesting over several years, aligning managerial wealth with stock price appreciation.
  4. Stock options: Call options on company equity providing asymmetric exposure to equity upside.
  5. Long-term incentive plans (LTIPs): Performance-contingent stock units tied to multi-year comparative benchmarks.

To quantify the managerial incentives generated by equity holdings, corporate finance models measure two portfolio sensitivities:

Theorem 6.1 (Managerial portfolio Delta and Vega)

Let \(W(P, \sigma)\) represent the total wealth of the CEO invested in company securities as a function of the stock price \(P\) and equity return volatility \(\sigma\).

  1. Pay-for-Performance Sensitivity (\(\delta\)): The dollar change in managerial wealth for a one-dollar (or one-percent) change in share price:

    \[ \delta = \frac{\partial W}{\partial P}. \]

    For a portfolio of \(N_s\) shares and \(N_o\) call options with Black-Scholes hedge ratio \(\Delta_{\mathrm{BS}} = \mathcal{N}(d_1)\), total portfolio delta is

    \[ \delta = N_s + N_o \cdot \Delta_{\mathrm{BS}}. \]

  2. Risk-Taking Incentive (\(\nu\) or Vega): The dollar change in managerial wealth for a 1% (or 0.01) increase in stock return volatility:

    \[ \nu = \frac{\partial W}{\partial \sigma} = N_o \cdot \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}} = N_o \cdot P \sqrt{T} \phi(d_1). \]

Why do boards award stock options rather than pure stock? Undiversified executives have the bulk of their human capital and financial wealth tied to a single firm. This makes them highly risk-averse, tempting them to reject risky, positive-NPV projects. Because option payoffs are convex, an increase in asset volatility \(\sigma\) increases option value (\(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}} > 0\)), counteracting risk-aversion and curbing managerial underinvestment.

Theorem 6.2 (Holmström–Milgrom linear contract under risk aversion)

Consider an agent choosing unobservable effort \(e\) with quadratic private cost \(c(e) = \frac{1}{2} k e^2\). Output is \(y = e + \varepsilon\) with noise \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\). The principal offers a linear contract \(w(y) = \alpha + \beta y\). The agent has constant absolute risk aversion (CARA) utility with risk-aversion coefficient \(r\). The optimal incentive slope \(\beta^*\) maximizing total certainty equivalent is

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta^* &= \frac{1}{1 + r k \sigma^2}. \end{aligned} \]

This closed-form solution demonstrates that as output volatility \(\sigma^2\) or managerial risk aversion \(r\) increases, the optimal incentive slope \(\beta^*\) must decline to avoid imposing excessive risk-bearing costs on the executive.

2The Market for Corporate Control and Antitakeover Defenses

While internal compensation aligns incentives, external market forces provide a disciplinary backstop. As surveyed by Shleifer and Vishny (1997, citing shleifer1997survey), the market for corporate control (hostile takeovers) disciplines entrenched, inefficient managers: if management destroys firm value, an outside corporate raider can purchase a controlling equity block, terminate incumbent executives, and capture the valuation gap.

To insulate themselves from hostile takeovers, corporate boards and executives install antitakeover provisions (ATPs) in corporate charters and bylaws:

Antitakeover Provision Structural Mechanism Governance Impact
Poison Pill (Shareholder Rights Plan) Dilutes hostile bidders by issuing discounted shares to all existing shareholders once an acquirer crosses a threshold (e.g., 15%). Precludes tender offers without prior board approval.
Staggered (Classified) Board Directors are divided into three classes with overlapping 3-year terms. Only 1/3 of the board is elected annually. Forces a hostile acquirer to win two successive annual proxy contests over two full years.
Golden Parachutes Lucrative severance compensation packages triggered upon a change in corporate control. Direct cash extraction upon takeover; can sometimes reduce managerial opposition to mergers.
Supermajority Merger Requirements Requires 66.7%, 75%, or 80% shareholder approval for mergers rather than a simple 50.1% majority. Enables a minority insider block to veto hostile acquisitions.
Bylaw Amendment Restrictions Restricts the ability of shareholders to amend corporate bylaws or call special shareholder meetings. Severely limits proxy contest actions outside scheduled annual general meetings.

3The Gompers, Ishii, and Metrick (2003) G-Index

In a seminal paper, Gompers, Ishii, and Metrick (2003, citing gompers2003corporate) constructed a comprehensive governance metric—the G-Index—to measure the balance of power between shareholders and managers across 1,500 large U.S. corporations tracked by the Investor Responsibility Research Center (IRRC).

Definition 3.1 (G-Index / Governance Index)

The G-Index is an unweighted integer index ranging from 0 to 24, constructed by adding one point for each antitakeover provision or managerial protection provision present in a company's charter, bylaws, or state antitakeover statutes:

\[ G = \sum_{j=1}^{24} I_j, \quad I_j \in \{0, 1\}. \]

Firms are classified into governance regimes:

  • Democracy Portfolio (\(G \le 5\)): Strong shareholder rights, minimal takeover defense barriers.
  • Dictatorship Portfolio (\(G \ge 14\)): Weak shareholder rights, heavily entrenched management.

Gompers et al. (2003, citing gompers2003corporate) documented striking empirical regularities during the 1990s:

  • Valuation discount: Higher G-index scores were associated with significantly lower Tobin's \(Q\) across the cross-section of firms.
  • Abnormal equity returns: A long-short trading strategy that purchased Democracy firms (\(G \le 5\)) and shorted Dictatorship firms (\(G \ge 14\)) generated an annualized risk-adjusted abnormal return (Fama-French alpha) of approximately 8.5% per year from 1990 to 1999.

4Bebchuk, Cohen, and Ferrell (2008): The Entrenchment Index (E-Index)

While the G-Index included 24 diverse provisions, Lucian Bebchuk, Alma Cohen, and Allen Ferrell (2008, citing bebchuk2008matters) investigated a fundamental question: Do all 24 provisions actually matter, or is governance driven by a focused subset of core entrenchment mechanisms?

Definition 4.1 (Entrenchment Index / E-Index)

The E-Index isolates 6 core provisions from the 24 provisions in the G-Index:

  1. Staggered (classified) board: Directors elected in classes across multiple years.
  2. Limits to shareholder bylaw amendments: Charter constraints on shareholder power to alter bylaws.
  3. Poison pills: Shareholder rights plans diluting hostile bidders.
  4. Golden parachutes: Change-in-control severance agreements for senior executives.
  5. Supermajority voting for mergers: Requiring supermajority shareholder approval for business combinations.
  6. Supermajority voting for charter amendments: Requiring supermajority shareholder approval to modify the corporate charter.

The index is an integer score:

\[ E = \sum_{k=1}^6 e_k, \quad e_k \in \{0, 1\}, \quad E \in \{0, 1, \dots, 6\}. \]

Bebchuk, Cohen, and Ferrell (2008, citing bebchuk2008matters) decomposed the 24-provision G-Index into two additive components: the 6-provision E-Index and the 18 other provisions (\(O = G - E\)). Running horse-race regressions of Tobin's \(Q\) and equity returns on both indices revealed a decisive empirical result:

Theorem 6.3 (Empirical sufficiency of the E-Index)

In cross-sectional and panel regressions of Tobin's \(Q\) on governance provisions:

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_i &= \alpha + \beta_E E_i + \beta_O O_i + \mathbf{\Gamma}' \mathbf{X}_i + \varepsilon_i \end{aligned} \]

  1. The slope on the 6 entrenchment provisions \(\beta_E\) is negative, economically large, and highly statistically significant (\(p < 0.001\)).
  2. The slope on the remaining 18 provisions \(\beta_O\) is statistically indistinguishable from zero (\(p > 0.40\)).
  3. The 6 entrenchment provisions fully account for the negative correlation between the G-Index and firm value, as well as the abnormal return anomaly of the 1990s.

5Econometrics of Governance Indices: Attenuation Bias and Endogeneity

Why is the 6-provision E-Index econometrically superior to the 24-provision G-Index? Suppose the true economic data-generating process (DGP) is governed exclusively by the 6 entrenchment provisions: \(Q_i = \alpha + \beta^* E_i + u_i\), with \(\beta^* < 0\). If a researcher instead uses the full G-Index \(G_i = E_i + \eta_i\), where \(\eta_i\) represents the 18 irrelevant provisions uncorrelated with \(u_i\):

Theorem 6.4 (Attenuation bias in kitchen-sink governance indices)

Let \(G_i = E_i + \eta_i\), where \(\operatorname{Cov}(E_i, \eta_i) \approx 0\). The OLS slope estimator from regressing Tobin's \(Q\) on \(G\) converges in probability to

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{plim} \widehat{\beta}_G &= \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Q, G)}{\operatorname{Var}(G)} \\ &= \beta^* \frac{\operatorname{Var}(E)}{\operatorname{Var}(E) + \operatorname{Var}(\eta)} \\ &= \beta^* \cdot \lambda < \beta^*, \end{aligned} \]

where the reliability ratio \(\lambda = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(E)}{\operatorname{Var}(E) + \operatorname{Var}(\eta)} \in (0, 1)\). The inclusion of 18 non-entrenchment provisions acts as classical measurement error, biasing the estimated governance coefficient toward zero and diluting statistical power.

Furthermore, quantitative researchers must confront serious endogeneity concerns in governance regressions:

  • Reverse Causality: Do antitakeover provisions cause low valuation, or do poorly performing firms adopt poison pills and staggered boards to protect management from takeover threats?
  • Omitted Variable Bias: Unobserved managerial talent, firm-specific human capital, and proprietary intellectual property may simultaneously drive lower Tobin's Q and the adoption of protective governance structures.

6Computational Lab: G-Index vs. E-Index Valuation Regressions

The following laboratory simulation implements the empirical horse-race between the 24-provision G-Index (Gompers et al. 2003) and the 6-provision E-Index (Bebchuk et al. 2008), demonstrating the attenuation bias and loss of statistical power caused by kitchen-sink governance indices.

Lab 1 · Shareholder rights indices: G-Index vs. E-Index

Simulates a cross-section of 60 corporate issuers to test whether the 6 entrenchment provisions in Bebchuk et al. (2008) drive valuation discounts on Tobin's Q while the remaining 18 provisions add econometric noise.

"""CF3 lab L06-1: Shareholder rights indices: G-Index vs E-Index (Gompers et al. 2003; Bebchuk et al. 2008).

Model: Cross-sectional empirical comparison of the 24-provision G-Index (Gompers, Ishii & Metrick 2003)
versus the 6-provision entrenchment E-Index (Bebchuk, Cohen & Ferrell 2008).
Simulates cross-section of corporate governance structures and Tobin's Q to test whether
the 6 entrenchment provisions drive valuation discounts while non-entrenchment provisions add noise.
Standard library only; deterministic cross-section.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def simulate_governance_panel(
    n_firms: int = 60,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> list[dict[str, float]]:
    """Simulate a cross-section of firms with 6 entrenchment and 18 non-entrenchment provisions."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    firms: list[dict[str, float]] = []

    # True DGP: Q = 2.40 - 0.12 * E_index + 0.0 * Other_index + noise
    true_alpha = 2.40
    true_beta_e = -0.12
    true_beta_o = 0.00  # Non-entrenchment provisions do not affect value

    for i in range(n_firms):
        # 6 core entrenchment provisions (prob ~ 0.50 each)
        e_provs = [1 if rng.random() < 0.50 else 0 for _ in range(6)]
        e_index = sum(e_provs)

        # 18 non-entrenchment provisions (prob ~ 0.40 each)
        o_provs = [1 if rng.random() < 0.40 else 0 for _ in range(18)]
        o_index = sum(o_provs)

        g_index = e_index + o_index

        noise = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.25)
        tobins_q = max(0.80, true_alpha + true_beta_e * e_index + true_beta_o * o_index + noise)

        firms.append(
            {
                "firm_id": float(i + 1),
                "e_index": float(e_index),
                "o_index": float(o_index),
                "g_index": float(g_index),
                "tobins_q": round(tobins_q, 4),
            }
        )

    return firms


def ols_bivariate(x: list[float], y: list[float]) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Bivariate OLS regression with n - 2 degrees of freedom."""
    n = len(x)
    mean_x = sum(x) / n
    mean_y = sum(y) / n
    s_xx = sum((xi - mean_x) ** 2 for xi in x)
    s_xy = sum((xi - mean_x) * (yi - mean_y) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True))
    s_yy = sum((yi - mean_y) ** 2 for yi in y)

    beta = s_xy / s_xx
    alpha = mean_y - beta * mean_x
    rss = sum((yi - (alpha + beta * xi)) ** 2 for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True))
    s2 = rss / (n - 2)
    se_beta = math.sqrt(s2 / s_xx)
    t_stat = beta / se_beta if se_beta > 0 else 0.0
    r2 = 1.0 - (rss / s_yy) if s_yy > 0 else 0.0

    return {
        "alpha": alpha,
        "beta": beta,
        "se_beta": se_beta,
        "t_stat": t_stat,
        "r2": r2,
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("CF3 Lab L06-1: Shareholder Rights Indices: G-Index vs. E-Index")
    print("=============================================================")

    n_firms = 60
    firms = simulate_governance_panel(n_firms=n_firms, seed=777)

    e_vals = [f["e_index"] for f in firms]
    g_vals = [f["g_index"] for f in firms]
    q_vals = [f["tobins_q"] for f in firms]

    # Model 1: Tobin's Q on Bebchuk et al. (2008) E-Index (6 provisions)
    res_e = ols_bivariate(e_vals, q_vals)

    # Model 2: Tobin's Q on Gompers et al. (2003) G-Index (24 provisions)
    res_g = ols_bivariate(g_vals, q_vals)

    print(f"Sample of {n_firms} corporate issuers:")
    print(f"  E-Index range: [{min(e_vals):.0f}, {max(e_vals):.0f}], mean: {sum(e_vals) / n_firms:.2f} / 6")
    print(f"  G-Index range: [{min(g_vals):.0f}, {max(g_vals):.0f}], mean: {sum(g_vals) / n_firms:.2f} / 24")
    print(f"  Tobin's Q range: [{min(q_vals):.2f}, {max(q_vals):.2f}], mean: {sum(q_vals) / n_firms:.2f}")

    print("\nModel 1: Regressing Tobin's Q on E-Index (Bebchuk et al. 2008):")
    print("  Q_i = alpha + beta_E * E_Index_i + e_i")
    print(f"  Intercept (alpha):   {res_e['alpha']:.4f}")
    print(f"  Slope (beta_E):      {res_e['beta']:.4f} (se: {res_e['se_beta']:.4f})")
    print(f"  t-statistic:         {res_e['t_stat']:.4f}")
    print(f"  R-squared:           {res_e['r2']:.4%}")

    print("\nModel 2: Regressing Tobin's Q on Full G-Index (Gompers et al. 2003):")
    print("  Q_i = alpha + beta_G * G_Index_i + e_i")
    print(f"  Intercept (alpha):   {res_g['alpha']:.4f}")
    print(f"  Slope (beta_G):      {res_g['beta']:.4f} (se: {res_g['se_beta']:.4f})")
    print(f"  t-statistic:         {res_g['t_stat']:.4f}")
    print(f"  R-squared:           {res_g['r2']:.4%}")

    print("\nEmpirical Takeaway (Bebchuk, Cohen & Ferrell 2008):")
    t_ratio = abs(res_e["t_stat"]) / abs(res_g["t_stat"])
    print(f"  E-Index t-statistic is {t_ratio:.2f}x stronger than G-Index.")
    print("  Adding the 18 non-entrenchment provisions dilutes statistical power and attenuates the slope.")

7Where the canon breaks

The empirical modeling of executive compensation and governance indices faces critical boundary conditions:

  • The "more provisions = better metric" fallacy: In econometric index construction, practitioners often assume that compiling 50 or 100 ESG check-boxes produces a more comprehensive measurement. In reality, adding provisions that do not constrain managerial opportunism creates severe measurement noise. Bebchuk et al. (2008) demonstrated that 18 out of 24 G-Index provisions add zero explanatory power and attenuate real entrenchment effects.
  • The disappearance of the Democracy alpha anomaly: While Gompers et al. (2003) documented an 8.5% annual abnormal return in the 1990s, subsequent research (Core et al. 2006; Bebchuk et al. 2008) proved that this return anomaly completely vanished after 2000 once markets learned the value implications of governance provisions.
  • Stock options as unalloyed agency alignment: Stock options solve managerial risk-aversion by introducing convex payoffs (Vega). However, option grants introduce severe secondary distortions: executives are incentivized to depress stock prices prior to grant dates, backdate options, withhold bad news until after vesting, and take excessively volatile negative-NPV gambles.

8Misconceptions

  • A governance index with 24 or 50 provisions is always statistically and economically superior to an index with only 6 provisions.

    False. Bebchuk, Cohen, and Ferrell (2008) proved that 18 of the 24 provisions in the G-Index are statistically uncorrelated with firm valuation and stock returns. Including irrelevant provisions adds econometric measurement error, inducing attenuation bias that weakens regression t-statistics and shrinks estimated governance slopes toward zero.

  • Awarding stock options eliminates agency conflicts by making executives behave exactly like equity shareholders.

    False. Stock options create asymmetric payoff profiles with zero downside exposure below the strike price. Unlike diversified shareholders who care about total risk-adjusted firm value, option-compensated managers are incentivized to increase asset volatility (excessive risk-taking) and suppress cash dividend payouts in favor of share repurchases to protect option strike value.

  • A staggered board is merely a minor procedural inconvenience that can be bypassed in a single hostile proxy fight.

    False. With a classified board, only one-third of the directors are elected each year. A hostile bidder must successfully win two separate proxy fights over two consecutive annual shareholder meetings to gain board control. In practice, maintaining financing and bid commitments across a 14-to-24 month siege is virtually impossible for most hostile acquirers, making staggered boards one of the most powerful anti-takeover deterrents.

  • Cross-sectional OLS regressions showing negative slopes between E-Index and Tobin's Q definitively prove that adopting antitakeover provisions directly destroys firm value.

    False. Governance structures are chosen endogenously by firms and boards. Distressed or low-growth firms with poor future prospects may deliberately adopt poison pills and staggered boards to insulate management from hostile takeovers. Without exogenous legal shocks or instrumental variables, cross-sectional OLS regressions capture correlation, not direct causality.

9Problems

Mechanics

M1 · Pay-for-performance portfolio Delta calculation

A CEO holds 250,000 shares of common stock and 500,000 stock options. The company's stock trades at \(P = \$50.00\). The Black-Scholes delta of the options is \(\Delta_{\mathrm{BS}} = 0.60\). Compute the total dollar pay-for-performance sensitivity (\(\delta\)) for a $1.00 increase in stock price, and compute the CEO's wealth change for a 5.0% increase in stock price.

Solution

Total portfolio delta is \(\delta = N_s + N_o \cdot \Delta_{\mathrm{BS}} = 250,000 + 500,000 \times 0.60 = 250,000 + 300,000 = 550,000\). For a $1.00 increase in stock price, the CEO's wealth increases by $550,000. A 5.0% increase in stock price is \(\Delta P = 0.05 \times 50.00 = \$2.50\). The resulting wealth change is \(\Delta W = 550,000 \times \$2.50 = \$1,375,000\).

M2 · Option Vega and risk-taking incentives

The CEO in M1 holds 500,000 options with Black-Scholes vega of \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{BS}} = \$18.50\) per 100% change in volatility (or $0.1850 per 1% change). Compute the CEO's portfolio Vega (\(\nu\)) for a 10 percentage point increase in annualized equity return volatility (e.g., from \(\sigma = 0.25\) to \(\sigma = 0.35\)).

Solution

Per-option change in value for a 10 percentage point (\(\Delta \sigma = 0.10\)) increase in volatility is \(\Delta V_{\mathrm{option}} = 0.10 \times \$18.50 = \$1.85\). For 500,000 options, the CEO's total portfolio vega gain is \(\Delta W = 500,000 \times \$1.85 = \$925,000\). This positive payoff illustrates why stock options induce managers to undertake riskier operational strategies.

M3 · E-Index calculation from corporate charter data

A corporate proxy statement discloses that the firm has: a classified board with 3-year director terms, a shareholder rights plan (poison pill), a golden parachute severance agreement for the CEO, and a 50.1% simple majority requirement for mergers and charter amendments. Compute the firm's E-Index score.

Solution

Evaluating the 6 E-Index provisions: (1) Staggered board: Present (= 1); (2) Limits to bylaw amendments: Absent (= 0); (3) Poison pill: Present (= 1); (4) Golden parachute: Present (= 1); (5) Supermajority for mergers: Absent (= 0, simple majority); (6) Supermajority for charter: Absent (= 0). Total E-Index score is \(E = 1 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 3\).

Proof

P1 · Derivation of Holmström–Milgrom optimal incentive slope

Derive the optimal linear incentive slope \(\beta^* = \frac{1}{1 + r k \sigma^2}\) for an agent with CARA utility \(-e^{-r(w - c(e))}\) where \(w = \alpha + \beta y\), \(y = e + \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\), and \(c(e) = \frac{1}{2} k e^2\).

Solution

The agent's wage is normally distributed with mean \(\alpha + \beta e\) and variance \(\beta^2 \sigma^2\). Under CARA-normal assumptions, certainty equivalent is \(\mathrm{CE} = \alpha + \beta e - \frac{1}{2} k e^2 - \frac{1}{2} r \beta^2 \sigma^2\). The agent chooses effort to maximize CE: \(\partial \mathrm{CE} / \partial e = \beta - k e = 0 \implies e^* = \beta / k\). The risk-neutral principal maximizes expected net profit subject to participation CE \(\ge \bar{u}\): \(\max_\beta [e^*(\beta) - \frac{1}{2} k (e^*(\beta))^2 - \frac{1}{2} r \beta^2 \sigma^2] = \max_\beta [\frac{\beta}{k} - \frac{\beta^2}{2k} - \frac{1}{2} r \beta^2 \sigma^2]\). First-order condition: \(\frac{1}{k} - \frac{\beta}{k} - r \beta \sigma^2 = 0 \implies \beta (1 + r k \sigma^2) = 1 \implies \beta^* = \frac{1}{1 + r k \sigma^2}\).

P2 · Proof of attenuation bias from noise provisions

Prove that when \(Q = \alpha + \beta_E E + u\) and a researcher regresses \(Q\) on \(G = E + \eta\) where \(\operatorname{Cov}(E, \eta) = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(u, \eta) = 0\), the OLS slope \(\widehat{\beta}_G\) converges to \(\beta_E \frac{\sigma_E^2}{\sigma_E^2 + \sigma_\eta^2}\).

Solution

The OLS estimator is \(\widehat{\beta}_G = \frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(Q, G)}{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(G)}\). Substituting \(Q = \alpha + \beta_E E + u\) and \(G = E + \eta\): \(\operatorname{Cov}(Q, G) = \operatorname{Cov}(\alpha + \beta_E E + u, E + \eta) = \beta_E \operatorname{Var}(E) + \beta_E \operatorname{Cov}(E, \eta) + \operatorname{Cov}(u, E) + \operatorname{Cov}(u, \eta) = \beta_E \sigma_E^2\). The denominator is \(\operatorname{Var}(G) = \operatorname{Var}(E + \eta) = \sigma_E^2 + \sigma_\eta^2 + 2\operatorname{Cov}(E, \eta) = \sigma_E^2 + \sigma_\eta^2\). Therefore, \(\operatorname{plim} \widehat{\beta}_G = \beta_E \frac{\sigma_E^2}{\sigma_E^2 + \sigma_\eta^2}\). Because \(\sigma_\eta^2 > 0\), the slope is strictly attenuated toward zero by the reliability ratio \(\lambda < 1\).

P3 · Option convexity and managerial asset substitution

Prove that for a call option on firm value \(C(V, K, \sigma)\), the second derivative with respect to firm volatility is strictly related to option gamma, and explain why high vega can incentivize managerial risk-shifting into negative-NPV projects.

Solution

By Black-Scholes formula, option Vega is \(\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} = V \sqrt{T} \phi(d_1) > 0\). Notice that \(\mathcal{V} = \sigma V^2 T \cdot \Gamma\), where \(\Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial V^2} = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{V \sigma \sqrt{T}} > 0\). Because \(\Gamma > 0\), option payoff is strictly convex. If an executive with high option holdings contemplates a project with negative expected return (\(\Delta V < 0\)) but massive variance increase (\(\Delta \sigma \gg 0\)), the change in executive wealth \(\Delta W \approx \delta \Delta V + \nu \Delta \sigma\) can be strictly positive even when shareholder value \(\Delta V\) is negative. This formalizes the asset substitution agency conflict created by excessive option vega.

Empirical

E2 · Gompers et al. (2003) Democracy vs Dictatorship spread

Describe the construction of the Democracy and Dictatorship portfolios in Gompers, Ishii, and Metrick (2003) and state their documented return spread over the 1990–1999 period.

Solution

Gompers et al. (2003) formed a Democracy portfolio comprising firms with \(G \le 5\) (strong shareholder rights) and a Dictatorship portfolio comprising firms with \(G \ge 14\) (heavily entrenched). Buying Democracy and shorting Dictatorship earned an annualized 4-factor Carhart alpha of 8.5% (\(t = 2.73\)) from September 1990 to December 1999. In addition, Democracy firms exhibited higher Tobin's Q, higher net profit margins, and faster sales growth.

E3 · Bebchuk et al. (2008) horse-race regression results

Summarize the empirical results when Bebchuk, Cohen, and Ferrell (2008) included both the 6-provision E-Index and the 18 other provisions (\(O\)) in the same Tobin's Q regression.

Solution

When Bebchuk et al. (2008) estimated \(Q_i = \alpha + \beta_E E_i + \beta_O O_i + \mathbf{\Gamma}' \mathbf{X}_i + \varepsilon_i\), the coefficient \(\beta_E\) remained negative, economically large (-0.05 to -0.08 per entrenchment provision), and statistically significant at the 1% level across all specifications. In contrast, the coefficient on the remaining 18 provisions \(\beta_O\) was statistically insignificant (often slightly positive, with \(p\)-values ranging from 0.40 to 0.85). This demonstrated that the 18 provisions added zero explanatory power.

Research

R1 · Disappearance of governance anomalies post-2000

Why did the 8.5% annual abnormal return to governance portfolios documented by Gompers et al. (2003) disappear during the 2000–2010 decade?

Solution

Core, Guay, and Rusticus (2006) and Bebchuk et al. (2008) showed that the abnormal return anomaly in the 1990s was driven by market surprise: investors had not yet anticipated the operating underperformance of entrenched firms. Once academic studies published these governance relationships and institutional investors actively tracked G-Index and E-Index scores, market prices incorporated governance differentials into valuations. Consequently, subsequent operating performance differentials were fully priced, and abnormal risk-adjusted returns fell to zero.

R2 · Endogeneity and instrumental variables in corporate governance

Explain why ordinary cross-sectional regressions of firm value on governance indices suffer from endogeneity, and describe an econometric strategy researchers use to establish causality.

Solution

Governance indices are not randomly assigned; firms choose charters endogenously based on unobserved characteristics (managerial talent, growth opportunities, threat of takeover). To establish causality, researchers exploit quasi-natural experiments, such as the staggered passage of state antitakeover laws (e.g., Business Combination Acts in the 1980s), mandatory listing standard changes (SOX 2002), or close-call regression discontinuity designs (RDD) around 50% shareholder voting thresholds for governance shareholder proposals.

R3 · Say-on-Pay and executive compensation governance

Describe the "Say-on-Pay" provisions enacted under the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 and evaluate their empirical impact on executive compensation structures.

Solution

Under Section 951 of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (2010), public companies are required to hold non-binding advisory shareholder votes on executive compensation at least once every three years. Empirical research (e.g., Larcker et al. 2011; Ferri & Maber 2013) shows that while negative votes are rare (< 3% of firms fail), boards actively restructure compensation programs (e.g., removing tax gross-ups, eliminating single-trigger golden parachutes, and raising performance-vesting thresholds) to avoid negative proxy advisory recommendations from ISS and Glass Lewis.
