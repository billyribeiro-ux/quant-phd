Internal Rate of Return and Its Pitfalls

  • Uses: net present value, present value and discounting, the discount factor
  • Introduces: the internal rate of return (IRR); the multiple-IRR problem and the NPV–IRR ranking conflict
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

The internal rate of return (IRR, Definition 1.2 below) is a rate, not an amount. It is a rate at which a project's NPV is zero. With an outlay at \(t = 0\) followed by non-negative inflows, not all zero, the IRR is unique and gives the same accept or reject decision as the NPV rule. With more sign changes it can have two values, and for mutually exclusive projects (only one can be chosen) it can rank them in the opposite order to the NPV. This lesson proves which case is which, and Lab 1 shows each one on SIMULATED cash flows.

1The NPV profile and the substitution that makes it a polynomial

Definition 1.1 (NPV profile)

For a cash-flow sequence \(C_0, C_1, \dots, C_N\), with \(N\) the last date, \(t = 0\) the date of the first flow and one period per year, the NPV profile is the function \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) = \sum_{t=0}^{N} C_t/(1+r)^t\) on \(r > -1\), with \(r\) the rate per year.

Definition 1.2 (internal rate of return)

An internal rate of return (IRR) of a cash-flow sequence is a rate \(r > -1\) with \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) = 0\). The set of IRRs is the set of roots of the NPV profile on \((-1, \infty)\).

Theorem 1.3 (substitution)

Let \(x = 1/(1+r)\). The map \(r \mapsto x\) is a strictly decreasing bijection of \((-1, \infty)\) onto \((0, \infty)\), with inverse \(r = 1/x - 1\). Put \(P(x) = \sum_{t=0}^{N} C_t x^t\). Then \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) = P(1/(1+r))\) for every \(r > -1\). Hence the IRRs are exactly the numbers \(1/x - 1\) for the roots \(x > 0\) of \(P\).

Proof

On \((-1, \infty)\), \(1 + r\) runs over \((0, \infty)\) strictly increasingly, so \(x = 1/(1+r)\) runs over \((0, \infty)\) strictly decreasingly, and \(r = 1/x - 1\) inverts it. Since \((1+r)^{-t} = x^t\), \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) = \sum_t C_t x^t = P(x)\). A root of \(\mathrm{NPV}\) is therefore a point where \(P\) vanishes, and the bijection carries roots to roots. \(\blacksquare\)

2One outlay, then non-negative inflows, not all zero: exactly one IRR

A project is conventional when its flows are an outlay \(C_0 < 0\) at \(t = 0\), followed by \(C_t \ge 0\) for \(t \ge 1\) with \(C_t > 0\) for at least one \(t \ge 1\). This is exactly the hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 below.

Theorem 2.1 (one sign change)

Let \(C_0 < 0\), let \(C_t \ge 0\) for \(t \ge 1\), and let \(C_t > 0\) for at least one \(t \ge 1\). Then the NPV profile is strictly decreasing on \((-1, \infty)\) and has exactly one IRR, which lies in \((-1, \infty)\). The IRR is non-negative when \(\sum_t C_t \ge 0\), and positive when \(\sum_t C_t > 0\).

Proof

Let \(P(x) = C_0 + \sum_{t \ge 1} C_t x^t\). For \(0 < x_1 < x_2\), \(P(x_2) - P(x_1) = \sum_{t \ge 1} C_t (x_2^t - x_1^t)\). Each term is non-negative, because \(C_t \ge 0\) and \(x_2^t > x_1^t\), and at least one term is strictly positive. So \(P\) is strictly increasing on \((0, \infty)\). As \(x \to 0^+\), \(P(x) \to C_0 < 0\). For \(x \ge 1\), \(x^t \ge x\) for \(t \ge 1\), so \(P(x) \ge C_0 + xS\) with \(S = \sum_{t \ge 1} C_t > 0\), which tends to infinity. The intermediate value theorem gives a root, and strict monotonicity gives only one. By Theorem 1.3 the root \(x^*\) gives the single IRR \(1/x^* - 1\). Since \(P\) is increasing, \(x^* \le 1\) exactly when \(P(1) = \sum_t C_t \ge 0\), and \(x^* < 1\) exactly when \(P(1) > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 2.2 (accept or reject at a given rate)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 2.1, \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) > 0\) if and only if \(r < \mathrm{IRR}\), and \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) < 0\) if and only if \(r > \mathrm{IRR}\).

Proof

The NPV profile is strictly decreasing and zero at the IRR, so it is positive to the left of the IRR and negative to the right. \(\blacksquare\) For such a project, comparing a given rate with the IRR gives the same accept-or-reject decision as the sign of the NPV at that rate.

3Two sign changes: an exact example with two IRRs

Definition 3.1 (multiple-IRR problem)

The multiple-IRR problem has two parts. The first is a cash-flow sequence with more than one IRR, so that the phrase "the IRR" no longer names a number. The second is a pair of mutually exclusive projects (choosing one excludes the other) whose IRR ranking and NPV ranking disagree at the rate that applies. Section 4 treats the second part.

Example 3.2 (a project with an outlay, an inflow and a cost)

Let \(P\) be the SIMULATED project with cash flows \(-100\,000\), \(230\,000\) and \(-132\,000\) USD at \(t = 0\), \(1\) and \(2\). The signs run \(-, +, -\), so there are two sign changes, and Theorem 2.1 does not apply. Its NPV profile is \[ \mathrm{NPV}(r) = -100\,000 + \frac{230\,000}{1+r} - \frac{132\,000}{(1+r)^2}. \] Lab 1 prints the profile at seven rates. Its values are negative at 0 per cent, 5 per cent, 25 per cent and 30 per cent, zero at 10 and 20 per cent, and positive at 15 per cent.

Proposition 3.3 (exact factorization)

For \(P\) in Example 3.2, with \(x = 1/(1+r)\), \[ \mathrm{NPV}(r) = -2\,000\,(11x - 10)(6x - 5) \quad \text{USD}. \] Its IRRs are exactly \(10\) per cent and \(20\) per cent, and \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) > 0\) exactly for \(0.10 < r < 0.20\).

Proof

Expand: \((11x - 10)(6x - 5) = 66x^2 - 55x - 60x + 50 = 66x^2 - 115x + 50\). Then \(-2\,000\,(66x^2 - 115x + 50) = -132\,000x^2 + 230\,000x - 100\,000\), which is \(\mathrm{NPV}\) by Theorem 1.3 with \(C_0 = -100\,000\), \(C_1 = 230\,000\) and \(C_2 = -132\,000\). The roots are \(x = 10/11\) and \(x = 5/6\). Theorem 1.3 gives \(r = 11/10 - 1 = 0.10\) and \(r = 6/5 - 1 = 0.20\). Between the roots, \(6x - 5 > 0\) and \(11x - 10 < 0\), so the product is negative and \(\mathrm{NPV} = -2\,000 \times (\text{negative}) > 0\). Outside them the signs of the factors agree and \(\mathrm{NPV} < 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.4 (what the example shows, and what it does not)

The example has two IRRs, so the phrase "the project's IRR" has no single referent. It does not show how often this happens. Proposition 3.3 is exact for this project. A grid scan of the profile (the method of Lab 1, which evaluates the profile at rates spaced 0.0001 apart) finds the same two sign changes, but a scan is evidence about a grid, and the factorization is the proof.

A three-term sequence has at most two IRRs. Write \(P(x) = C_0 + C_1 x + C_2 x^2\). If \(P\) is not identically zero, the quadratic formula gives at most two roots, and each IRR is a positive root by Theorem 1.3. The bound does not extend to longer sequences without an argument of its own, and this lesson does not give one.

4The IRR and the NPV can rank mutually exclusive projects differently

Theorem 4.1 (crossover of two projects)

Let project \(A\) have flows \(-K_A\) at \(t = 0\) and \(C_A\) at \(t = 1\), and project \(B\) have flows \(-K_B\) and \(C_B\), with all four amounts positive. Write \(\Delta K = K_B - K_A\) and \(\Delta C = C_B - C_A\). If \(\Delta K \ne 0\) and \(\Delta C/\Delta K > 0\), the NPVs of \(A\) and \(B\) are equal at exactly one rate, \(r^* = \Delta C/\Delta K - 1\). If \(\Delta K > 0\) and \(\Delta C > 0\), then \(B\) has the higher NPV for \(r < r^*\) and \(A\) has the higher NPV for \(r > r^*\).

Proof

The difference of the two NPVs is \(\mathrm{NPV}_B(r) - \mathrm{NPV}_A(r) = -\Delta K + \Delta C/(1+r)\). It is zero when \(1 + r = \Delta C/\Delta K\). That equation has a solution with \(r > -1\) exactly when \(\Delta C/\Delta K > 0\), and the solution is unique. If \(\Delta K > 0\) and \(\Delta C > 0\), the difference is positive when \(\Delta C/(1+r) > \Delta K\), that is, when \(1 + r < \Delta C/\Delta K\), which is \(r < r^*\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 4.2 (project A against project B)

Project \(A\) costs 100,000 USD and returns 130,000 USD after one year, so its IRR is 30 per cent. Project \(B\) costs 200,000 USD and returns 250,000 USD after one year, so its IRR is 25 per cent. The IRR ranks \(A\) first. At a 10 per cent cost of capital (the rate at which the flows are discounted) the NPVs are 18,181.82 USD for \(A\) and 27,272.73 USD for \(B\), so the NPV ranks \(B\) first. Here \(\Delta K = 100\,000\) and \(\Delta C = 120\,000\), so Theorem 4.1 gives \(r^* = 20\) per cent. Below 20 per cent the NPV prefers \(B\). At 20 per cent the two NPVs are equal, at 8,333.33 USD each. Above 20 per cent the NPV prefers \(A\), as does the IRR. The conflict is therefore present at every rate below 20 per cent and absent above it.

Remark 4.3 (scale)

\(B\) invests twice as much as \(A\). The IRR is a rate, so it does not register the extra 100,000 USD of outlay. The NPV is an amount, so it counts that outlay at the cost of capital. The ranking conflict is a conflict between a rate and an amount, and Theorem 4.1 locates it exactly.

Lab 1 · SIMULATED cash flows: the NPV profile of project P, and the ranking of A and B

Before running, the expected results are: the NPV profile of \(P\) has two sign changes, at 10 and 20 per cent; the grid scan and the factorization of Proposition 3.3 agree on both roots; the NPV of \(P\) at 5 per cent is negative, although both IRRs exceed 5 per cent (Corollary 2.2 does not apply to \(P\), so no positive NPV below the IRR can be inferred); project \(A\) has the higher IRR and project \(B\) the higher NPV at 10 per cent; and the crossover rate is 20 per cent (Theorem 4.1). The root search scans rates from \(-0.5\) to \(1.0\) in steps of 0.0001 and bisects every sign change, so it does not stop at the first root. The lab draws no random numbers.

"""CF1 L04 lab 1: the NPV profile of a project with two sign changes, and an IRR-versus-NPV ranking conflict.

SIMULATED: every cash flow is a fixed input written here. None is any firm's cash flow.
Units: money in cents, with t = 0 first and one period per year. Rates are decimals per year.
The root search scans a grid of rates from -0.5 to 1.0 in steps of 0.0001 for sign changes of the NPV profile,
then bisects each bracket, so it finds every sign change on the grid. The closed form uses x = 1 / (1 + r) and
the factorization of the two-sign-change project shown on the page. The lab draws no random numbers.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections.abc import Callable, Sequence

PROJECT_P: tuple[int, ...] = (-10_000_000, 23_000_000, -13_200_000)  # outlay, inflow, then a cost at t = 2
PROJECT_A: tuple[int, ...] = (-10_000_000, 13_000_000)  # mutually exclusive with B
PROJECT_B: tuple[int, ...] = (-20_000_000, 25_000_000)
COST_OF_CAPITAL = 0.10
GRID_LOW = -0.5
GRID_HIGH = 1.0
GRID_STEP = 0.0001


def npv(flows: Sequence[float], rate: float) -> float:
    """Net present value in cents: the sum of flows[t] / (1 + rate)**t; rate is per year and must exceed -1."""
    if rate <= -1.0:
        raise ValueError(f"rate {rate} must exceed -1")
    return sum(cf / (1.0 + rate) ** t for t, cf in enumerate(flows))


def bisect(f: Callable[[float], float], low: float, high: float) -> float:
    """A root of f in [low, high], given that f(low) and f(high) have opposite signs; the bracket narrows to 1e-12."""
    f_low = f(low)
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (low + high)
        f_mid = f(mid)
        if f_mid == 0.0 or high - low < 1e-12:
            return mid
        if f_low * f_mid < 0.0:
            high = mid
        else:
            low, f_low = mid, f_mid
    return 0.5 * (low + high)


def all_roots(flows: Sequence[float]) -> list[float]:
    """Every sign change of the NPV profile on the grid, each bisected; duplicates within 1e-7 are merged."""

    def f(rate: float) -> float:
        return npv(flows, rate)

    steps = round((GRID_HIGH - GRID_LOW) / GRID_STEP)
    grid = [GRID_LOW + i * GRID_STEP for i in range(steps + 1)]
    values = [f(r) for r in grid]
    roots: list[float] = []
    for i in range(steps + 1):
        if values[i] == 0.0:
            candidate = grid[i]
        elif i < steps and values[i] * values[i + 1] < 0.0:
            candidate = bisect(f, grid[i], grid[i + 1])
        else:
            continue
        if not roots or abs(candidate - roots[-1]) > 1e-7:
            roots.append(candidate)
    return roots


def sign_label(value: float) -> str:
    """Text sign of an NPV in cents: '+' above 1e-6, '-' below -1e-6, else '0' (a root, to float precision)."""
    return "+" if value > 1e-6 else "-" if value < -1e-6 else "0"


def usd(cents: float) -> str:
    """Cents shown as dollars with two decimals and no thousands separator; a value within 1e-6 cents is zero."""
    dollars = 0.0 if abs(cents) < 1e-6 else cents / 100
    return f"{dollars:.2f}"


def closed_form_p(rate: float) -> float:
    """NPV of PROJECT_P in cents from the factorization -200000 * (11x - 10) * (6x - 5), with x = 1 / (1 + rate)."""
    x = 1.0 / (1.0 + rate)
    return -200_000.0 * (11.0 * x - 10.0) * (6.0 * x - 5.0)


def main() -> None:
    print("PROJECT_P cash flows in cents, t = 0, 1, 2:", ", ".join(str(cf) for cf in PROJECT_P))
    print("NPV profile of PROJECT_P (USD), sign on the profile")
    for rate in (0.00, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25, 0.30):
        value = npv(PROJECT_P, rate)
        print(f"rate {rate:.2f}  NPV {usd(value)}  sign {sign_label(value)}")

    roots_p = all_roots(PROJECT_P)
    print(f"sign changes of the NPV profile on the grid: {len(roots_p)}")
    for i, root in enumerate(roots_p, start=1):
        print(f"IRR root {i} by grid scan and bisection: {root:.6f}")
    closed = [1.0 / x - 1.0 for x in (10.0 / 11.0, 5.0 / 6.0)]
    print(f"closed-form roots, r = 1/x - 1 at x = 10/11 and x = 5/6: {closed[0]:.6f}, {closed[1]:.6f}")
    gap = max(abs(npv(PROJECT_P, r) - closed_form_p(r)) for r in (-0.4, 0.0, 0.05, 0.15, 0.5, 2.0))
    print(f"largest absolute gap, factorization against direct NPV, cents: {gap:.6f}")
    print(f"NPV of PROJECT_P at the cost of capital {COST_OF_CAPITAL:.2f}: {usd(npv(PROJECT_P, COST_OF_CAPITAL))} USD")

    print("IRR ranking against NPV ranking for mutually exclusive projects")
    roots_a = all_roots(PROJECT_A)
    roots_b = all_roots(PROJECT_B)
    print(f"IRR of A: {roots_a[0]:.6f}; IRR of B: {roots_b[0]:.6f}")
    higher_irr = "A" if roots_a[0] > roots_b[0] else "B"
    print(f"project with the higher IRR: {higher_irr}")
    npv_a = npv(PROJECT_A, COST_OF_CAPITAL)
    npv_b = npv(PROJECT_B, COST_OF_CAPITAL)
    print(f"NPV of A at {COST_OF_CAPITAL:.2f}: {usd(npv_a)} USD; NPV of B: {usd(npv_b)} USD")
    higher_npv = "A" if npv_a > npv_b else "B"
    print(f"project with the higher NPV at {COST_OF_CAPITAL:.2f}: {higher_npv}")

    difference = tuple(b - a for a, b in zip(PROJECT_A, PROJECT_B, strict=True))
    crossover = all_roots(difference)
    print(f"difference B minus A in cents: {', '.join(str(cf) for cf in difference)}")
    print(f"crossover rate, the IRR of the difference: {crossover[0]:.6f}")
    print("rate  NPV A (USD)  NPV B (USD)  higher NPV")
    for rate in (0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25, 0.30):
        va = npv(PROJECT_A, rate)
        vb = npv(PROJECT_B, rate)
        leader = "tie" if abs(va - vb) <= 1e-6 else "A" if va > vb else "B"
        print(f"{rate:.2f}  {usd(va)}  {usd(vb)}  {leader}")


main()

5What the IRR root says about interim cash flows

Remark 5.1 (terminal value at a rate)

With \(N\) the last date, the terminal value at rate \(r\) is \(\mathrm{FV}(r) = \sum_{t} C_t(1+r)^{N-t} = (1+r)^N\,\mathrm{NPV}(r)\). At an IRR, \(\mathrm{FV} = 0\). For \(P\) at 20 per cent, with \(N = 2\), \(-100\,000 \times 1.2^2 + 230\,000 \times 1.2 - 132\,000 = -144\,000 + 276\,000 - 132\,000 = 0\). The zero follows from \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) = 0\) at an IRR and the identity \(\mathrm{FV}(r) = (1+r)^N\,\mathrm{NPV}(r)\), which holds at every rate. The interim inflow, compounded at the IRR, exactly offsets the outlay and the later cost. It does not show that the firm can earn the IRR on the interim flows. That is a reinvestment assumption, and the identity does not test it.

6Where the canon breaks

A natural reading treats the IRR as the return on the project. Theorem 2.1 gives that reading a basis for a conventional project, where the IRR is unique. Corollary 2.2 then makes it a valid accept-or-reject test at a given rate. Proposition 3.3 shows the reading fails for \(P\): both 10 and 20 per cent are IRRs, and neither is the project's return in any sense the theorems support.

The ranking conflict is not a defect of the NPV. Theorem 4.1 shows that the conflict depends on the rate: below the crossover the NPV prefers \(B\), and above it the NPV prefers \(A\). Choosing between a rate-based rule and an amount-based rule is a question about scale and about the cost of capital. A firm that ranks by IRR should say why the extra 100,000 USD of outlay in \(B\) does not count.

Remark 5.1 states what the IRR root establishes about the interim flows. Whether the firm can reinvest them at the IRR is an assumption, and no theorem on this page establishes it.

7Misconceptions

  1. “A project whose IRR exceeds the cost of capital has a positive NPV.”

    This holds for a conventional project (Corollary 2.2), and \(P\) is not one. Both of its IRRs, 10 and 20 per cent, exceed a 5 per cent cost of capital, and Lab 1 prints an NPV of \(-680.27\) USD at 5 per cent.

  2. “The project with the higher IRR is the better investment.”

    Project \(A\) has the higher IRR, 30 per cent against 25 per cent. At a 10 per cent cost of capital, \(B\) has the higher NPV, 27,272.73 USD against 18,181.82 USD (Example 4.2 and Lab 1). The IRR ranking ignores the extra outlay that \(B\) makes.

  3. “A project with two IRRs earns the average of the two.”

    The average of 10 and 20 per cent is 15 per cent. The NPV at 15 per cent is 189.04 USD, which is not zero, so 15 per cent is not an IRR of \(P\) (Proposition 3.3 and Lab 1). An IRR is a root of the NPV profile, and an average of roots is not a root in general.

  4. “A second IRR is a numerical artefact, and a better solver returns only the true one.”

    Proposition 3.3 gives both roots exactly, by factorization, so there is no second number to discard. A solver that stops at the first sign change, such as a bisection on a bracket it happens to choose, returns one of the two roots and misses the other. Lab 1 bisects every sign change on the grid for this reason.

Mechanics

M1 · The IRR of a single-period project

A project costs 100 USD at \(t = 0\) and returns 121 USD at \(t = 1\). Find its IRR.

Solution

The NPV is \(-100 + 121/(1+r)\). Setting it to zero gives \(1 + r = 121/100\), so the IRR is \(0.21\), or 21 per cent. There is one sign change, so by Theorem 2.1 this is the only IRR.

M2 · The IRR of a two-period project

A project costs 100 USD at \(t = 0\) and returns 60 USD at each of \(t = 1\) and \(t = 2\). Find its IRR to two decimals of a per cent.

Solution

Let \(x = 1/(1+r)\). The NPV is \(-100 + 60x + 60x^2\), and setting it to zero gives \(x^2 + x - 5/3 = 0\). The positive root is \(x = (-1 + \sqrt{23/3})/2 = 0.884437\). Then \(r = 1/x - 1 = 0.130662\), or 13.07 per cent. Theorem 2.1 applies, so this is the only IRR.

M3 · Two IRRs from a factorization

A project has flows \(-1\), \(+2.5\) and \(-1.5\) at \(t = 0\), \(1\) and \(2\), in any unit. Show that \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) = -(1.5x - 1)(x - 1)\) with \(x = 1/(1+r)\), and find both IRRs.

Solution

Expanding, \(-(1.5x - 1)(x - 1) = -(1.5x^2 - 2.5x + 1) = -1.5x^2 + 2.5x - 1\), which is \(-1 + 2.5x - 1.5x^2\), the NPV by Theorem 1.3. The roots are \(x = 2/3\) and \(x = 1\). Theorem 1.3 gives \(r = 1.5 - 1 = 0.5\) and \(r = 1 - 1 = 0\). The project has IRRs of 0 and 50 per cent.

Proof

P1 · The one-period case of Theorem 2.1

Let \(C_0 < 0\) and \(C_1 > 0\), with no later flows. Prove that the IRR exists, is unique, and equals \(C_1/(-C_0) - 1\).

Solution

The NPV is \(C_0 + C_1/(1+r)\), which is strictly decreasing in \(r\) on \((-1, \infty)\), because \(C_1 > 0\). Its limit as \(r \to -1^+\) is \(+\infty\), and as \(r \to \infty\) it tends to \(C_0 < 0\). So it has exactly one zero. Setting \(C_0 + C_1/(1+r) = 0\) gives \(1 + r = C_1/(-C_0)\), so the IRR is \(C_1/(-C_0) - 1\).

P2 · Equal inflows, different outlays

Two projects have the same inflow \(C > 0\) at \(t = 1\) and outlays \(K_A < K_B\) at \(t = 0\). Show that the IRR and the NPV rank them the same way at every rate.

Solution

The difference of NPVs is \(\mathrm{NPV}_B(r) - \mathrm{NPV}_A(r) = -(K_B - K_A) < 0\) for every \(r\), so \(A\) has the higher NPV at every rate. The IRRs are \(C/K_A - 1\) and \(C/K_B - 1\) by P1, and \(C/K_A > C/K_B\), so \(A\) also has the higher IRR. The two rules agree everywhere, because the outlay difference is a constant that does not depend on the rate.

P3 · Ranking by the ratio of inflow to outlay

Two projects each have one outlay and one inflow at \(t = 1\), with \(K_A\), \(C_A\), \(K_B\) and \(C_B\) all positive. Show that \(\mathrm{IRR}_B > \mathrm{IRR}_A\) if and only if \(C_B/K_B > C_A/K_A\).

Solution

By P1, \(\mathrm{IRR} = C/K - 1\) for each project. Then \(\mathrm{IRR}_B > \mathrm{IRR}_A\) exactly when \(C_B/K_B - 1 > C_A/K_A - 1\), which is \(C_B/K_B > C_A/K_A\). The IRR therefore ranks projects by the ratio of inflow to outlay, and it ignores the size of the outlay. That is the scale point of Remark 4.3, shown for the one-period case.

Empirical

E1 · Reading the ranking table

In Lab 1, at which rates does the NPV rank \(B\) above \(A\), at which is there a tie, and at which does it rank \(A\) above \(B\)? Give the rates from the printed table.

Solution

The table prints \(B\) for 0.05, 0.10 and 0.15, a tie at 0.20, and \(A\) for 0.25 and 0.30. These match Theorem 4.1: the crossover is 0.20, and below it the NPV prefers \(B\) because \(\Delta K = 100\,000 > 0\) and \(\Delta C = 120\,000 > 0\).

E2 · Changing project A in a copy

In a copy of Lab 1, change \(A\) to flows \(-100\,000\) and \(+120\,000\) USD, and keep \(B\). State the IRR of each project, the crossover rate, and the rates at which the IRR and NPV rankings disagree.

Solution

The IRR of \(A\) is 20 per cent and the IRR of \(B\) is 25 per cent, so the IRR ranks \(B\) first. Here \(\Delta K = 100\,000\) and \(\Delta C = 250\,000 - 120\,000 = 130\,000\), so the crossover is \(r^* = 1.3 - 1 = 0.30\). At 10 per cent both rules rank \(B\) first, with NPVs of 9,090.91 USD for \(A\) and 27,272.73 USD for \(B\). At 35 per cent the NPV of \(A\) is \(-11,111.11\) USD and that of \(B\) is \(-14,814.81\) USD, so the NPV ranks \(A\) first while the IRR ranks \(B\) first. The rankings disagree above 30 per cent, and agree below it.

E3 · The NPV of P below both IRRs

Using Proposition 3.3, compute the NPV of \(P\) at 5 per cent. Say which result of this lesson does and does not apply to \(P\) at that rate.

Solution

With \(x = 1/1.05\), \(\mathrm{NPV} = -2\,000\,(11/1.05 - 10)(6/1.05 - 5) = -680.27\) USD, which matches Lab 1. Corollary 2.2 does not apply, because \(P\) has two sign changes, so the rule that a positive NPV follows from a rate below the IRR is not available. The NPV is negative at 5 per cent even though both IRRs are above it.

Research

R1 · A project with a 400 per cent IRR

A project has flows \(-1\), \(+6\) and \(-5\) at \(t = 0\), \(1\) and \(2\). Find both IRRs, the set of rates at which the NPV is positive, and the NPV at a 10 per cent cost of capital.

Solution

The NPV in \(x = 1/(1+r)\) is \(-1 + 6x - 5x^2 = -(5x - 1)(x - 1)\). The roots are \(x = 1/5\) and \(x = 1\), which give \(r = 4\) and \(r = 0\), so the IRRs are 0 and 400 per cent. The NPV is positive between the roots, which is \(0 < r < 4\). At 10 per cent, \(\mathrm{NPV} = -1 + 6/1.1 - 5/1.21 = 0.322314\), which is positive. A high IRR is therefore not what makes this project acceptable: the NPV at the stated cost of capital is the test, and the IRR does not summarize the profile.

R2 · Equal outlays with one inflow at \(t = 1\) cannot produce a conflict

Two projects have the same outlay \(K > 0\) at \(t = 0\) and inflows \(C_A\) and \(C_B\) at \(t = 1\), with \(C_B > C_A\). Show that the NPV and the IRR rank them the same way at every rate. What does this imply about the size of the ranking conflict when the two projects differ in outlay?

Solution

The NPV difference is \(\mathrm{NPV}_B(r) - \mathrm{NPV}_A(r) = (C_B - C_A)/(1+r) > 0\) for every \(r > -1\), so \(B\) has the higher NPV at every rate. By P1 the IRRs are \(C_B/K - 1\) and \(C_A/K - 1\), and \(C_B/K > C_A/K\), so \(B\) also has the higher IRR. The rules agree everywhere. In Theorem 4.1 this is the case \(\Delta K = 0\), where the difference has no zero and no crossover. So for projects with one inflow at \(t = 1\), a conflict needs a difference in outlay. Equal outlays can still conflict when the inflows arrive at different dates. Take both outlays as 100 at \(t = 0\), with project \(A\) returning 300 at \(t = 2\) and project \(B\) returning 200 at \(t = 1\). Then \((1+r)^2 = 3\) gives \(\mathrm{IRR}_A = \sqrt{3} - 1 = 0.732051\), and \(\mathrm{IRR}_B = 1\). At 10 per cent, \(\mathrm{NPV}_A = 300/1.21 - 100 = 147.93\) and \(\mathrm{NPV}_B = 200/1.1 - 100 = 81.82\), so the NPV ranks \(A\) first while the IRR ranks \(B\) first. Whether real capital projects produce such conflicts is an empirical question, and this page does not answer it.

R3 · Terminal values of P at two rates

For \(P\) of Example 3.2, compute the terminal value at \(N = 2\) at 15 per cent and at 5 per cent, using \(\mathrm{FV}(r) = (1+r)^2\,\mathrm{NPV}(r)\).

Solution

At 15 per cent, \(\mathrm{NPV} = 189.036\) USD, so \(\mathrm{FV} = 1.3225 \times 189.036 = 250.00\) USD. At 5 per cent, \(\mathrm{NPV} = -680.272\) USD, so \(\mathrm{FV} = 1.1025 \times (-680.272) = -750.00\) USD. Both values follow from the identity in Remark 5.1, and the FV is zero at the IRRs of 10 and 20 per cent.

  1. Project P of Example 3.2 has flows \(-100\,000\), \(230\,000\) and \(-132\,000\) USD. Which pair of rates are its IRRs?

    Options

    Proposition 3.3 gives the factorization \(-2\,000(11x - 10)(6x - 5)\), with roots \(x = 10/11\) and \(x = 5/6\), which are \(r = 0.10\) and \(r = 0.20\).

  2. A project costs 100 USD at \(t = 0\) and returns 121 USD at \(t = 1\). What is its IRR as a decimal?

    \(121/100 - 1 = 0.21\) (Problem M1).

  3. For a conventional project with one sign change, which statement is correct?

    Options

    The NPV profile is strictly decreasing and zero at the IRR, so it is positive below the IRR (Corollary 2.2).

  4. What is the NPV of project P at 15 per cent, in USD to two decimals?

    \(-2\,000(11/1.15 - 10)(6/1.15 - 5) = 189.04\) USD (Lab 1 prints 189.04).

  5. What does the factorization of P establish that a grid scan of its NPV profile does not?

    Options

    A quadratic with its factorization has no other roots, which is a proof. A grid detects a root as a sign change between two neighbouring grid points, or as a grid point where the NPV is exactly zero. So it locates a root to within the grid step and cannot exclude a pair of roots that lie between the same two neighbouring points.

  6. Projects A (outlay 100,000, inflow 130,000) and B (outlay 200,000, inflow 250,000) cross in NPV at what rate, as a decimal?

    \(\Delta C/\Delta K - 1 = 120\,000/100\,000 - 1 = 0.20\) (Theorem 4.1).

  7. At a 10 per cent cost of capital, which project does the NPV rule prefer in Example 4.2?

    Options

    Example 4.2 gives the two NPVs. The ranking conflict is that the IRR ranks A first.

  8. A project costs 100 USD at \(t = 0\) and returns 60 USD at each of \(t = 1\) and \(t = 2\). What is its IRR in per cent, to two decimals?

    The positive root of \(x^2 + x - 5/3 = 0\) gives \(r = 0.130662\), or 13.07 per cent (Problem M2).

  9. Is the average of the two IRRs of project P an IRR of P?

    Options

    An IRR is a root of the NPV profile. The NPV at 15 per cent is positive, so 15 per cent is not a root (Lab 1).

  10. What is the terminal value at \(N = 2\) of project P at 20 per cent, in USD?

    \(-144\,000 + 276\,000 - 132\,000 = 0\). At an IRR the terminal value is zero (Remark 5.1).
