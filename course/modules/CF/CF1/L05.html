Payback and What Firms Actually Use

  • Uses: net present value, incremental project cash flows, present value, discount factors, evidence grades
  • Introduces: the payback period
  • Ttheorem

  • Bpublished, not settled

Payback answers one question: how long until the cash spent on a project has come back. That question is easy to ask and easy to answer. This lesson defines payback and its discounted form, and proves what each one ignores. The proofs settle which cash flows a payback rule never reads. Lab 1 compares payback with NPV on SIMULATED flows, and the evidence section quotes no passage from the registered paper and states no finding about use.

1Two payback rules

Definition 1.1 (simple payback)

Let \(C_0, C_1, \ldots, C_N\) be a project's cash flows, with \(C_0\) at time zero and \(C_t\) at the end of year \(t\). Let \(\mathrm{CUM}_t = \sum_{s=0}^{t} C_s\) be the cumulative undiscounted cash flow. The integer-year simple payback is \(\tau_S = \min\{t \ge 1 : \mathrm{CUM}_t \ge 0\}\), the first year-end at which the cumulative flow is zero or more. If \(\mathrm{CUM}_t \lt 0\) for every \(t \le N\), \(\tau_S\) is undefined. The payback period is the fractional year, computed by linear interpolation inside the year of \(\tau_S\): \[ \mathrm{PB} = (\tau_S - 1) + \frac{-\mathrm{CUM}_{\tau_S - 1}}{C_{\tau_S}}. \] The interpolation assumes that the flows of that year arrive evenly through the year. It is a convention, and it is not a finding.

Definition 1.2 (discounted payback)

Let \(r \ge 0\) be an annual discount rate. Replace each \(C_t\) by \(C_t/(1+r)^t\), and write \(D_t\) for the cumulative sum of the discounted flows. The integer-year discounted payback is \(\tau_D = \min\{t \ge 1 : D_t \ge 0\}\). Its fractional value is computed with the same interpolation as Definition 1.1, applied to the discounted flows.

The simple rule uses no discount rate. The discounted rule uses the rate the analyst chooses. When a payback year exists, neither rule reads a year after it: for the simple rule this is Proposition 2.1, and the discounted sums \(D_t\) depend on the same years, so the argument of Proposition 2.1 applies to them in the same way. The rest of the lesson shows what that restriction means for the decision.

2What the payback rules read

Proposition 2.1 (simple payback reads only its payback years)

Let two cash-flow vectors agree in every year \(t \le \tau_S\). Then they have the same simple payback \(\tau_S\) and the same fractional payback PB. Their later flows do not change either number.

Proof

The integer \(\tau_S\) is the first \(t \ge 1\) with \(\mathrm{CUM}_t \ge 0\). The value of \(\mathrm{CUM}_t\) for \(t \le \tau_S\) depends only on \(C_0, \ldots, C_{\tau_S}\). If the two vectors agree on those years, they have the same \(\mathrm{CUM}_t\) for every \(t \le \tau_S\), so the first \(t\) with \(\mathrm{CUM}_t \ge 0\) is the same for both. The fractional value uses \(\mathrm{CUM}_{\tau_S - 1}\) and \(C_{\tau_S}\), both of which lie within the agreed years. No year after \(\tau_S\) enters any step, so the statement follows.

Proposition 2.2 (discounting cannot shorten payback for conventional flows)

Flows with \(C_0 \lt 0\) and \(C_t \ge 0\) for every \(t \ge 1\) are called conventional. Let such flows be given, let \(r \ge 0\), and suppose \(\tau_S\) and \(\tau_D\) exist. Then \(\tau_D \ge \tau_S\).

Proof

Let \(v = 1/(1+r)\), so \(0 \lt v^t \le 1\) for \(t \ge 0\). For every \(t\), \(D_t = C_0 + \sum_{s=1}^{t} C_s v^s\), and \(\mathrm{CUM}_t = C_0 + \sum_{s=1}^{t} C_s\). Each term satisfies \(C_s v^s \le C_s\) because \(C_s \ge 0\). Hence \(D_t \le \mathrm{CUM}_t\) for every \(t\). Suppose \(\tau_D \lt \tau_S\). Then \(D_{\tau_D} \ge 0\), so \(\mathrm{CUM}_{\tau_D} \ge D_{\tau_D} \ge 0\), which makes \(\tau_D\) a year with a non-negative cumulative flow. Since \(\tau_S\) is the first such year and \(\tau_D \ge 1\), this gives \(\tau_S \le \tau_D\), a contradiction. Hence \(\tau_D \ge \tau_S\).

Remark 2.3 (what Proposition 2.1 does not say)

Proposition 2.1 says that payback is blind to later flows. It does not say that payback is wrong. A rule that reads only the first years is exactly what a payback test is. The defect appears when the decision depends on the later flows as well, which is the case for NPV, as Proposition 3.1 shows.

Lab 1 · Payback on SIMULATED project cash flows

The lab computes the simple and discounted payback of three SIMULATED projects at a 10% rate, and their NPVs. Project A pays back in 2.750 years on the simple rule and 3.469 years on the discounted rule. Project B has the same flows as A through year 3, so Proposition 2.1 predicts the same simple payback, 2.750, and Proposition 3.1 gives a lower NPV for B, because B's flows after year 3 are smaller than A's. Project D pays back in 2.000 years, and its NPV is negative. The lab then draws 1000 seeded variants of A, called tails, that change only the flows of years 5 and 6, and predicts one value of each payback across all 1000 draws, with a range of NPV values. All cash flows are in integer cents and are SIMULATED.

"""CF1 L05 lab 1: simple and discounted payback on SIMULATED project cash flows. It checks what payback uses.

SIMULATED: every cash flow in this lab is written into the code by the author or drawn from a seeded generator.
No company, filing or market series is read. The lab computes two rules on the same flows: the simple payback
(undiscounted cumulative cash flow) and the discounted payback (cumulative cash flow discounted at RATE), and the
net present value (NPV) of the same flows. It shows which of the three quantities changes when a cash flow after
the payback year changes, and which does not.

Units: every cash flow is an integer number of cents. Year 0 is the time of the investment; year t is the end of
year t. Payback is in years. The payback convention is linear interpolation inside the year in which the cumulative
flow first reaches zero or more, which assumes the flows of that year arrive evenly. A rule that never reaches
zero returns None.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
from collections.abc import Sequence

RATE = 0.10  # annual discount rate for the discounted payback and the NPV, a SIMULATED hurdle rate
SEED = 2026  # seed for the tail draws
N_TAILS = 1000  # number of seeded variants of the cash flows after year 4

PROJECT_A: tuple[int, ...] = (-1_000_000, 300_000, 400_000, 400_000, 300_000, 200_000, 100_000)
PROJECT_B: tuple[int, ...] = (-1_000_000, 300_000, 400_000, 400_000, 0, 0, 0)
PROJECT_D: tuple[int, ...] = (-1_000_000, 500_000, 500_000, 0, 0, 0, 0)


def payback(flows: Sequence[float]) -> float | None:
    """Payback in years: the first year whose end cumulative flow is zero or more, interpolated inside that year.

    flows[t] is the cash flow at the end of year t, in cents, with flows[0] at time zero. The result is None when the
    cumulative flow never reaches zero. Units: years.
    """
    if flows[0] >= 0:
        return 0.0
    total = float(flows[0])
    for t in range(1, len(flows)):
        previous = total
        total += flows[t]
        if total >= 0:
            # previous is negative and flows[t] is positive here, so the division is well defined
            return (t - 1) + (-previous) / flows[t]
    return None


def discount(flows: Sequence[float], rate: float) -> list[float]:
    """Each flow discounted to time zero at the given annual rate, in cents: flows[t] / (1 + rate) ** t."""
    return [f / (1.0 + rate) ** t for t, f in enumerate(flows)]


def npv(flows: Sequence[float], rate: float) -> float:
    """Net present value in cents: the sum of the discounted flows."""
    return math.fsum(discount(flows, rate))


def fmt(value: float | None) -> str:
    """A payback value to three decimals, or the word none when the rule never pays back."""
    return "none" if value is None else f"{value:.3f}"


def main() -> None:
    print(f"rate {RATE} seed {SEED} tails {N_TAILS} units cents")
    for name, flows in (("A", PROJECT_A), ("B", PROJECT_B), ("D", PROJECT_D)):
        print(
            f"project {name} simple_payback {fmt(payback(flows))} "
            f"discounted_payback {fmt(payback(discount(flows, RATE)))} npv_cents {npv(flows, RATE):.2f}"
        )
    gap = npv(PROJECT_A, RATE) - npv(PROJECT_B, RATE)
    print(f"npv_gap_A_minus_B_cents {gap:.2f}")
    print(f"simple_payback_equal_A_B {payback(PROJECT_A) == payback(PROJECT_B)}")

    # Vary only the cash flows of years 5 and 6, which come after both paybacks of project A.
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    simple: set[float | None] = set()
    discounted: set[float | None] = set()
    npvs: list[float] = []
    for _ in range(N_TAILS):
        tail = (*PROJECT_A[:5], rng.randint(0, 500_000), rng.randint(0, 500_000))
        simple.add(payback(tail))
        discounted.add(payback(discount(tail, RATE)))
        npvs.append(npv(tail, RATE))
    print(f"tail_draws {N_TAILS} distinct_simple_payback {len(simple)} simple_payback_value {fmt(next(iter(simple)))}")
    print(f"distinct_discounted_payback {len(discounted)} discounted_payback_value {fmt(next(iter(discounted)))}")
    print(f"tail_npv_cents_min {min(npvs):.2f} tail_npv_cents_max {max(npvs):.2f}")
    print(f"distinct_npv_values {len({f'{x:.6f}' for x in npvs})}")


main()

The output agrees with Proposition 2.1 on the simple rule: A and B agree through year 3, and both have simple payback 2.750. Project B's discounted cumulative flow stays negative after year 3 and never turns non-negative in any year of the lab's flows (years 0 to 6), so its discounted payback is none. Project A's discounted payback is 3.469 years. Across the 1000 tails the two payback values take one value each, while the NPV of the tails ranges from 121251.71 to 693692.91 cents. 3Payback and NPV on the same flows

Proposition 3.1 (the NPV gap is the present value of the later flows)

Let two cash-flow vectors \(C\) and \(C'\) agree in every year \(t \le \tau\), for some \(\tau \ge 0\). Their net present values at a rate \(r \ge 0\) differ by \(\mathrm{NPV}(C) - \mathrm{NPV}(C') = \sum_{t \gt \tau} (C_t - C'_t)/(1+r)^t\).

Proof

By definition \(\mathrm{NPV}(C) = \sum_{t=0}^{N} C_t/(1+r)^t\). The terms with \(t \le \tau\) are equal for \(C\) and \(C'\), so they cancel in the difference. What remains is the sum over \(t \gt \tau\) of \((C_t - C'_t)/(1+r)^t\).

Proposition 3.1 says that two projects that agree through year \(\tau\) differ in NPV by exactly the present value of their years after \(\tau\). Two projects with the same payback year need not agree through that year, so the payback year alone does not fix the NPV gap. If two projects agree through year \(\tau\) and one project's flows after \(\tau\) are at least the other's in every later year, its NPV is at least the other's, because each term of Proposition 3.1 is then non-negative for \(r \ge 0\). Counting positive later years does not order NPVs: at \(r = 0.10\), the flows \((-1000, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1)\) have two positive later years and NPV \(-1000 + 1/1.1^4 + 1/1.1^5 = -998.70\), while \((-1000, 0, 0, 0, 1000, 0)\) has one and NPV \(-1000 + 1000/1.1^4 = -316.99\). Payback does not measure that difference. The first vector has no simple payback, and the second pays back in 4.0 years, so the rule separates the two flows by timing and does not state either NPV.

Lab 1 is an instance. Projects A and B agree through year 3, so the gap in their NPVs is the present value of A's years 4 to 6. The lab prints the gap, 385535.69 cents, and that is the present value of 300000, 200000 and 100000 cents received in years 4, 5 and 6 at 10%. The gap is larger than B's NPV in absolute value, and it is 133.2% of A's NPV. Payback reads neither figure.

4Where the canon breaks, and what firms use

Payback has four defects that follow from its definition, and one that depends on evidence.

  • It ignores flows after its year. Proposition 2.1 proves it. A project with a large late receipt and a long payback can be rejected, and a project with a short payback and no later receipts can be accepted.
  • The simple rule ignores time value. The simple cumulative sum treats a cent in year 1 and a cent in year 4 as equal. The discounted rule fixes that for the years it reads.
  • The discounted rule still ignores later flows. The lab's tail draws change years 5 and 6, and the discounted payback of A stays at 3.469 in every draw. Proposition 2.2 adds that, for conventional flows at a rate of zero or more and when both paybacks exist, discounting never shortens the integer payback year.
  • The cut-off is a choice. The payback rule accepts a project when its payback is below a number of years that the firm sets. Nothing in the definition says what that number should be.

The fifth defect depends on evidence. Whether firms use payback, and how much weight they put on it, is an empirical question. Graham and Harvey (2001) is the registered source whose title names corporate finance practice, under graham2001theory. This lesson states no finding from it. It quotes no passage from that paper, so no share of firms, and no ranking of methods, is stated here.

The proofs above need no evidence. A reader who wants to know what practitioners do must read the survey, locate the passage, and then grade the claim it supports.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Payback measures how profitable a project is.”

    Payback measures how long the cash takes to come back. It does not read what the project earns after that. Projects A and B in Lab 1 both have a simple payback of 2.750 years, and their NPVs are 289367.40 and −96168.29 cents. The payback rule cannot tell them apart.

  2. “Discounting fixes payback.”

    Discounting corrects the time value of the years that payback reads. It leaves the years after payback unread. The argument of Proposition 2.1 applies to the discounted rule too, and the 1000 tails of Lab 1 keep the discounted payback of A at 3.469 years while the NPV changes.

  3. “A shorter payback always means a better project.”

    Project D in Lab 1 pays back in 2.000 years and has an NPV of −132231.40 cents. Project A pays back in 2.750 years and has an NPV of 289367.40 cents. A rule that prefers the shorter payback chooses D, which destroys value at the 10% rate. The NPV rule chooses A.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · Simple payback with a whole year

A project costs 1000 at time zero and returns 400 at the end of each of years 1, 2 and 3. Compute its simple payback.

Solution

The cumulative flows are \(-1000\), \(-600\), \(-200\), and then \(+200\) at the end of year 3. The first year-end with a non-negative cumulative flow is \(\tau_S = 3\). The payback is \((3 - 1) + 200/400 = 2.5\) years, because \(-\mathrm{CUM}_2 = 200\) and the flow of year 3 is 400.

M2 · Discounted payback at 10%

Take the project of M1, add a fourth receipt of 400 at the end of year 4, and discount at 10%. Compute the discounted payback. Then compute it again without the fourth receipt.

Solution

The discounted receipts are \(400/1.1 = 363.64\), \(400/1.21 = 330.58\), \(400/1.331 = 300.53\) and \(400/1.4641 = 273.21\). The cumulative discounted flow after year 3 is \(-1000 + 994.74 = -5.26\), where 994.74 is the sum of the unrounded terms (the three terms as displayed add to 994.75), and after year 4 it is \(-5.26 + 273.21 = +267.95\). The interpolated payback is \(3 + 5.26/273.21 = 3.019\) years, to three decimals. Without the fourth receipt the cumulative discounted flow stays negative at \(-5.26\), so the discounted payback is none, and that project never pays back on the discounted rule.

M3 · A fractional year

A project costs 500 at time zero and returns 100, 200 and 300 at the ends of years 1, 2 and 3. Compute its simple payback.

Solution

The cumulative flows are \(-500\), \(-400\), \(-200\), and \(+100\) at the end of year 3. So \(\tau_S = 3\), and \(\mathrm{PB} = 2 + 200/300 = 2.667\) years to three decimals. The exact value is \(2 + 2/3\), which is 2.667 to three decimals.

Proof

P1 · Payback at a zero rate

Show that at \(r = 0\) the integer-year discounted payback equals the integer-year simple payback for any flows.

Solution

At \(r = 0\), \(v = 1\), so each discounted flow equals the undiscounted flow. Then \(D_t = \mathrm{CUM}_t\) for every \(t\), and the two minimum definitions select the same first year \(t \ge 1\) with a non-negative cumulative flow. Hence \(\tau_D = \tau_S\). The fractional values also agree, because the same cumulative sums and flows enter the interpolation.

P2 · Two projects with one payback year

Let \(C\) and \(C'\) agree through year 3 and differ in year 4, with \(\delta = C_4 - C'_4\) and no other differences. Let both have a payback year at or before year 3. Compute the NPV difference at rate \(r\), and say what happens when \(\delta \lt 0\).

Solution

By Proposition 3.1 the difference is \(\delta/(1+r)^4\). If \(\delta \gt 0\), \(C\) has the higher NPV, and if \(\delta \lt 0\), \(C'\) has the higher NPV. Both payback years are at or before year 3, so by Proposition 2.1 they depend only on the agreed years and are equal. Payback is therefore indifferent between \(C\) and \(C'\) in both cases.

P3 · Payback at an exact zero

Suppose \(\mathrm{CUM}_{t} = 0\) exactly for some \(t \ge 1\), and \(\mathrm{CUM}_{s} \lt 0\) for \(s \lt t\). Show that the fractional payback equals \(t\).

Solution

Then \(\tau_S = t\), and \(\mathrm{CUM}_{t-1} = -C_t\), because \(\mathrm{CUM}_t = \mathrm{CUM}_{t-1} + C_t = 0\). The fractional formula gives \((t - 1) + (-\mathrm{CUM}_{t-1})/C_t = (t - 1) + C_t/C_t = t\). The interpolation therefore agrees with the integer rule at an exact zero.

Empirical

E1 · What the tails change

Lab 1 prints the NPV range over its 1000 tails as 121251.71 to 693692.91 cents, while both payback values take one value each. Compute the NPV range in currency units (the lab's cents divided by 100), and say what the range shows about payback.

Solution

The range is \(693692.91 - 121251.71 = 572441.20\) cents, which is 5724.41 currency units. Payback took the value 2.750 in all 1000 tails and the discounted value 3.469 in all of them, so its range is zero. The simple payback year is 3 and the discounted payback year is 4, so both paybacks read only years 0 to 4 (Proposition 2.1, and its discounted analogue in Section 1). Years 5 and 6 cannot move either payback value. The NPV does depend on years 5 and 6 (Proposition 3.1), and its spread is 5724.41 currency units. The contrast is a SIMULATED result, graded C, from Lab 1.

E2 · The gap relative to A

Project A has NPV 289367.40 cents and the NPV gap over B is 385535.69 cents, both from Lab 1. What share of A's NPV is the gap?

Solution

\(385535.69 / 289367.40 = 1.3323\), so the gap is 133.2% of A's NPV, to one decimal of a percent. The gap is larger than A's NPV because B's NPV is negative, \(-96168.29\) cents, and A minus B is \(289367.40 + 96168.29 = 385535.69\) cents. The example is a SIMULATED case and the figure is C.

E3 · Payback and NPV disagree on D and A

Project D has a simple payback of 2.000 years and an NPV of −132231.40 cents. Project A has a simple payback of 2.750 years and an NPV of 289367.40 cents. Say which project a firm that picks the shorter payback chooses, and which project the NPV rule chooses.

Solution

The shorter payback is D, at 2.000 years, so the payback rule chooses D. The NPV rule chooses A, because A's NPV is positive at 10% and D's is negative. The payback rule accepts D, which destroys value at this rate. Both numbers are SIMULATED output from Lab 1.

Research

R1 · Testing whether payback users invest differently

A researcher asks whether firms that report using payback invest less in long-lived projects than other firms. Specify the sample, the outcome, the comparison, the controls, and one robustness check. Say what evidence grade the question can reach before any data are read.

Solution

The sample is a set of firms with a stated answer on the methods they use, drawn from a survey or disclosure with a documented response rule. The outcome is a measure of long-lived investment, scaled by assets, and the comparison is between users and non-users of payback within size and industry groups. The controls include growth and leverage, which the design treats as possible confounders of both investment and the choice of method. The robustness check reruns the comparison with a different cut-off for what counts as long-lived. Before any data are read the question cannot exceed grade C, because a single survey sample is one study.

R2 · When discounted payback is none

Let the flows be conventional: \(C_0 \lt 0\), \(C_t \ge 0\) for \(t \ge 1\), over a horizon of \(N\) years, and let \(r \ge 0\). Prove that the discounted payback is reached within the horizon, \(\tau_D \le N\), if and only if \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) \ge 0\).

Solution

Write \(D_N = \mathrm{NPV}(r)\). If \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) \ge 0\), then \(D_N \ge 0\), so the cumulative discounted flow is non-negative at year \(N\), and \(\tau_D \le N\). Conversely, suppose \(\tau_D = \tau \le N\). Then \(D_\tau \ge 0\), and \(D_N = D_\tau + \sum_{t=\tau+1}^{N} C_t v^t \ge D_\tau \ge 0\), because each later term is non-negative. So \(\mathrm{NPV}(r) \ge 0\). The equivalence is a theorem under the stated conditions, and it says that a none on the discounted rule within the horizon is the same statement as a negative NPV.

R3 · Designing a SIMULATED comparison under a cut-off

Design a SIMULATED study that measures how much NPV a simple payback cut-off of three years forgoes, across random conventional projects. State the seed, the number of draws, the generator, and what the study cannot show.

Solution

Use random.Random(seed) with a fixed seed and draw 1000 projects, each with an investment at time zero and six years of non-negative receipts. For each project compute the simple payback and the NPV at a stated rate. Count the projects with a positive NPV and a payback above three years, and sum their NPVs. The study shows the NPV forgone under this generator and this cut-off. It cannot show the forgone NPV of real firms, because the generator sets the distribution of receipts, and the answer depends on that choice. A second seed and a second generator are the robustness checks that test this.
