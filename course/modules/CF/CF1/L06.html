A point NPV hides the uncertainty in the cash flows it sums. When the receipts are uncertain, the NPV is a random quantity, and a simulation can estimate its distribution. This lesson sets out what a simulated NPV estimates, how its error shrinks with the number of draws, and what the distribution cannot tell a decision-maker. The convergence check in Lab 1 uses a model whose expected NPV and standard deviation have closed forms, so the simulation can be compared with the exact answer. The lab's output is SIMULATED.

1The NPV as a random quantity

Definition 1.1 (the NPV of a draw)

Let the investment be \(I\) at time zero and let the receipt of year \(t\), \(X_t\), be a random variable for \(t = 1, \ldots, N\). The NPV of one draw of the receipts, at a rate \(r \gt -1\), is \(\mathrm{NPV} = -I + \sum_{t=1}^{N} X_t/(1+r)^t\). Plain words: the NPV of a draw is the investment subtracted from the discounted receipts of that draw.

Definition 1.2 (simulation-based NPV distribution)

The simulation-based NPV distribution is the set of NPV values from \(n\) independent draws of the receipts, summarized by its sample mean, its quantiles and the share of draws with a negative NPV. The sample mean is the average of the \(n\) values. Its standard error is the sample standard deviation divided by \(\sqrt{n}\). The \(p\) quantile, computed by the nearest-rank rule used in Lab 1, is the value at position \(\lceil pn \rceil\) of the values sorted in ascending order.

Estimating an expectation by averaging simulated draws in this way is a Monte Carlo estimate. Definition 1.2 defines the summaries that Lab 1 reports, in plain words. None of them is a decision rule. The decision rule remains the one of L01: accept a project whose expected NPV is positive, when the expectation is the right object for the decision.

2How the simulated mean converges

Proposition 2.1 (the standard error of a sample mean)

Let \(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n\) be independent and identically distributed with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\), and let \(\bar Y = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} Y_i\). Then \(\E[\bar Y] = \mu\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar Y) = \sigma^2/n\). The standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) falls in proportion to \(1/\sqrt{n}\) as the number of draws \(n\) grows.

Proof

By linearity, \(\E[\bar Y] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \E[Y_i] = \mu\). By independence, the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances, so \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar Y) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i \sigma^2 = \sigma^2/n\). Taking the square root gives the standard error.

Proposition 2.2 (variance of a discrete uniform receipt)

Let \(X\) be uniform on the \(m\) consecutive integers \(a, a+1, \ldots, a+m-1\), with \(m \ge 2\). Then \(\E[X] = a + (m-1)/2\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = (m^2-1)/12\).

Proof

Write \(X = a + K\), where \(K\) is uniform on \(\{0, \ldots, m-1\}\). Then \(\E[K] = (m-1)/2 = c\) by symmetry, and \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathrm{Var}(K) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(k-c)^2\). Expand the sum with \(\sum k = m(m-1)/2\) and \(\sum k^2 = (m-1)m(2m-1)/6\): \(\sum (k-c)^2 = \sum k^2 - 2c\sum k + mc^2 = m(m-1)(2m-1)/6 - m(m-1)^2/2 + m(m-1)^2/4\). Factor out \(m(m-1)\) to get \(m(m-1)\big[(2m-1)/6 - (m-1)/4\big] = m(m-1)(m+1)/12\). Dividing by \(m\) gives \((m^2-1)/12\).

Proposition 2.3 (the closed forms for the model of Lab 1)

Let the receipts \(X_1, \ldots, X_N\) be independent, with \(X_t\) having mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). At a rate \(r \gt -1\), write \(v_t = (1+r)^{-t}\) and \(A = \sum_{t=1}^{N} v_t\). Then \[ \E[\mathrm{NPV}] = -I + \mu A, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{NPV}) = \sigma^2 \sum_{t=1}^{N} v_t^2 . \] For the uniform receipts of Proposition 2.2, \(\mu = a + (m-1)/2\) and \(\sigma^2 = (m^2-1)/12\).

Proof

The expectation is linear, so \(\E[\mathrm{NPV}] = -I + \sum_t v_t \E[X_t] = -I + \mu\sum_t v_t\). Independence of the receipts gives \(\mathrm{Var}(\sum_t v_t X_t) = \sum_t v_t^2 \mathrm{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2 \sum_t v_t^2\), because the investment is a constant. The last sentence applies Proposition 2.2 with the stated \(a\) and \(m\).

Remark 2.4 (the normal approximation is not proved here)

Proposition 2.1 gives the scale of the error of the sample mean, not its shape. The claim that the standardized error, the sample mean minus its expectation divided by its standard error, is approximately normal for large \(n\) is not proved, cited or graded here. This lesson uses it only as a reading aid for the error in standard-error units. The registry records Glasserman (2003), Monte Carlo Methods in Financial Engineering, and this lesson states no result from it. Lab 1 does not test the normal reading. It reports the error in standard-error units and does not compare the shape of its distribution with a normal one.

Lab 1 · Convergence of a simulated NPV to its closed form

The lab draws 50000 seeded NPVs for the model of Proposition 2.3: an investment of 300000 cents, five years of receipts, each an integer number of cents uniform from 50000 to 150000, at a 10% rate. Proposition 2.3 gives the expected NPV as 79078.68 cents and the standard deviation as 49379.84 cents. The lab predicts that the sample mean at n = 100, 1000, 10000 and 50000 draws will fall within three standard errors of 79078.68 at each size, and that the sample standard deviation at 50000 draws will sit close to 49379.84 cents. It also predicts that the standard error falls roughly as the square root of n. All amounts are SIMULATED cents.

"""CF1 L06 lab 1: Monte Carlo NPV, checked for convergence against a case with a closed form. SIMULATED.

SIMULATED: every receipt is drawn from the seeded generator below. No company, filing or market series is read.

Model. An investment of INVESTMENT cents is made at time zero. Each year t = 1 to YEARS, the receipt is a whole
number of cents drawn uniformly from LOW to HIGH inclusive, independently across years. The net present value (NPV)
of one draw, in cents, is the investment's negative plus the receipts discounted at RATE.

Closed forms, for this model only. The mean receipt is (LOW + HIGH) / 2. The expected NPV is the negative of the
investment plus the mean receipt times the annuity factor (1 - (1 + RATE)**-YEARS) / RATE. The variance of one
discrete uniform receipt on m = HIGH - LOW + 1 values is (m**2 - 1) / 12, and the variance of the NPV is the sum over
years of that variance divided by (1 + RATE)**(2t). The Monte Carlo estimate of the mean has standard error
sd / sqrt(n), where sd is the sample standard deviation of n draws.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
import statistics

SEED = 6  # seed for every draw in this lab
RATE = 0.10  # annual discount rate, a SIMULATED hurdle rate
YEARS = 5  # receipts in years 1 to 5
INVESTMENT = 300_000  # cents paid at time zero
LOW = 50_000  # lowest possible receipt in a year, cents
HIGH = 150_000  # highest possible receipt in a year, cents
N_MAX = 50_000  # draws; the smaller samples are the first n draws of the same run
SAMPLE_SIZES = (100, 1_000, 10_000, 50_000)
QUANTILES = (0.10, 0.50, 0.90)


def annuity_factor(rate: float, years: int) -> float:
    """Present value of 1 per year for the given years at the given rate, a dimensionless number."""
    return (1.0 - (1.0 + rate) ** -years) / rate


def npv_of(receipts: list[int]) -> float:
    """NPV in cents of one draw: the investment is negative, and receipts[t - 1] arrives at the end of year t."""
    return -INVESTMENT + math.fsum(x / (1.0 + RATE) ** (t + 1) for t, x in enumerate(receipts))


def quantile(sorted_values: list[float], p: float) -> float:
    """Nearest-rank quantile of an ascending list: the value at position ceil(p * n) (1-based)."""
    rank = max(1, math.ceil(p * len(sorted_values)))
    return sorted_values[rank - 1]


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    npvs = [npv_of([rng.randint(LOW, HIGH) for _ in range(YEARS)]) for _ in range(N_MAX)]

    m = HIGH - LOW + 1
    receipt_var = (m * m - 1) / 12.0
    mean_receipt = (LOW + HIGH) / 2.0
    expected = -INVESTMENT + mean_receipt * annuity_factor(RATE, YEARS)
    sd_exact = math.sqrt(sum(receipt_var / (1.0 + RATE) ** (2 * t) for t in range(1, YEARS + 1)))

    print(f"seed {SEED} rate {RATE} years {YEARS} investment {INVESTMENT} low {LOW} high {HIGH} draws {N_MAX}")
    print(f"expected_npv_cents_closed_form {expected:.2f}")
    print(f"sd_npv_cents_closed_form {sd_exact:.2f}")
    for n in SAMPLE_SIZES:
        sample = npvs[:n]
        mean_n = math.fsum(sample) / n
        sd_n = statistics.stdev(sample)
        se_n = sd_n / math.sqrt(n)
        z = (mean_n - expected) / se_n
        print(
            f"n {n:>6} mean_npv_cents {mean_n:.2f} se_cents {se_n:.2f} error_in_se {z:+.3f} within_3se {abs(z) <= 3.0}"
        )
    sd_all = statistics.stdev(npvs)
    print(f"n {N_MAX} sd_npv_cents_sample {sd_all:.2f} sd_relative_to_closed_form {sd_all / sd_exact:.4f}")

    ordered = sorted(npvs)
    for p in QUANTILES:
        print(f"quantile_{int(p * 100):02d}_npv_cents {quantile(ordered, p):.2f}")
    negative = sum(1 for x in npvs if x < 0) / N_MAX
    se_negative = math.sqrt(negative * (1.0 - negative) / N_MAX)
    print(f"share_npv_below_zero {negative:.4f} se {se_negative:.4f}")


main()

At n = 100 the sample mean is 84543.40 cents, which is 1.242 standard errors above the closed form. At n = 50000 the mean is 79216.82 cents, 0.625 standard errors above it. All four sizes are within three standard errors, as predicted, so the sample means agree with the closed form at the stated error scale. The sample standard deviation at 50000 draws is 49419.62 cents, a ratio of 1.0008 to the closed form. The lab does not show the error of any model whose expectation has no closed form, and it does not show a result for any real project.

3What the distribution reports

Lab 1 prints three quantiles of the 50000 draws. The 10% quantile is 14896.62 cents, the median is 79457.94 cents and the 90% quantile is 143406.13 cents. The middle 80% of the simulated NPVs spans 128509.51 cents by the printed quantiles, which is the difference of the two printed figures, given the model. The lab computes the quantiles unrounded but prints them to two decimals. The difference of the unrounded quantiles is 128509.50 cents, 0.01 cents below the printed difference, and the lab does not print that unrounded difference. The lab also prints the share of draws with a negative NPV, 0.0552, with a standard error of 0.0010 from the binomial formula \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) at the estimated \(p\).

Each of these numbers is an estimate of a property of the model. None is a property of the project. The quantile band describes the simulated spread of NPV under the stated receipt distribution, and it is not a confidence interval for the NPV of a real project. The share below zero is conditional on the same model. Changing the receipt distribution can change each of them.

Remark 3.1 (the mean does not fix the share below zero)

Let \(X\) take the values \(-1\) and \(+1\) with probability one half each, and let \(Y = 0\). Both have mean zero, so they share an expected value. Then \(\Pr(X \lt 0) = 1/2\) and \(\Pr(Y \lt 0) = 0\). A project's expected NPV therefore does not determine the share of draws that lose money.

4Where the canon breaks

Propositions 2.1 and 2.3 hold for the model they are stated for. The first, second and fourth limits below concern the model and its inputs. The third concerns the sampling error of the quantiles of the estimator.

  • Independence across years. Proposition 2.3 uses independent receipts. Receipts within a project may be correlated, for example through a shared demand shock. This lesson treats independence as a model assumption, and Lab 1 does not test it. Correlation changes the variance (Problem P2), and the closed forms of this lesson do not cover it.
  • Sampling error is not model error. Proposition 2.1 shows that more draws shrink the standard error. It says nothing about inputs that are wrong. A simulation of the wrong distribution converges to the wrong answer with a small error bar.
  • The sampling error of tail quantiles is not measured here. The 10% and 90% quantiles of a finite sample are estimated from the draws near those ranks. Proposition 2.1 covers the mean and not the quantiles, so its standard error does not transfer to them. This lesson does not measure the sampling error of the quantiles of Lab 1.
  • The discount rate is an input. Every NPV in the lab uses 10%. A distribution at another rate is a different distribution, and the lab does not vary the rate.

Simulation converts assumptions about the cash flows into an NPV distribution. It does not validate those assumptions, and the robustness of a result to them is a separate check that this lesson does not perform.

5Misconceptions

  1. “More draws make the model more accurate.”

    More draws shrink the sampling error of the mean at the rate of Proposition 2.1. They do not change the model. Lab 1's four sample means lie near the closed form of its own model, and a model with the wrong receipt distribution would converge to its own wrong expectation with a smaller error bar.

  2. “The 10% to 90% band is a confidence interval for the project's NPV.”

    The band is the spread of simulated NPVs under the model. It is not an interval for the true NPV of a project, because the model is an assumption and the band does not include the error in that assumption. Lab 1's band, 128509.51 cents as the difference of its printed quantiles (128509.50 from the unrounded ones), describes its own generator.

  3. “A positive expected NPV means losses are unlikely.”

    Remark 3.1 gives two distributions with the same mean and different shares of losses. Lab 1 has a positive expected NPV of 79078.68 cents and a share of 0.0552 with negative NPV, which is a property of its distribution and not a consequence of the mean.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · A standard error from a sample

A simulation of 100 NPV draws has a sample standard deviation of 300 cents. Compute the standard error of the sample mean.

Solution

The standard error is \(s/\sqrt{n} = 300/\sqrt{100} = 300/10 = 30\) cents. If the sample standard deviation stayed at 300 cents, quadrupling the draws to 400 would halve the standard error to 15 cents, which is the square-root rule of Proposition 2.1.

M2 · The variance of a die

Let \(X\) be the face of a fair six-sided die, uniform on \(\{1, \ldots, 6\}\). Compute its mean and variance with Proposition 2.2.

Solution

Here \(m = 6\) and \(a = 1\). The mean is \(a + (m-1)/2 = 3.5\). The variance is \((m^2-1)/12 = 35/12 = 2.9167\), and the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{35/12} = 1.7078\), to four decimals.

M3 · The expected NPV of a two-year project

An investment of 100 is made at time zero. The receipt in each of years 1 and 2 is uniform on the integers 40 to 60, independently, at a rate of 10%. Compute the expected NPV with Proposition 2.3.

Solution

The mean receipt is \((40+60)/2 = 50\). The annuity factor \(A\) of Proposition 2.3 is \(1/1.1 + 1/1.21 = 0.909091 + 0.826446 = 1.735537\). So \(\E[\mathrm{NPV}] = -100 + 50 \times 1.735537 = -100 + 86.777 = -13.22\), in the same units as the investment.

Proof

P1 · Variance of the mean of uncorrelated draws

Let \(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n\) have a common variance \(\sigma^2\) and be pairwise uncorrelated, not necessarily independent. Show that \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar Y) = \sigma^2/n\).

Solution

\(\mathrm{Var}(\sum_i Y_i) = \sum_i \sum_j \mathrm{Cov}(Y_i, Y_j)\). Pairwise uncorrelated draws have zero covariance for \(i \ne j\), and the diagonal terms are \(\sigma^2\). So \(\mathrm{Var}(\sum_i Y_i) = n\sigma^2\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar Y) = n\sigma^2/n^2 = \sigma^2/n\). Independence was not needed, only uncorrelatedness.

P2 · Correlated receipts

Let \(N \ge 2\) and \(\sigma^2 \gt 0\). Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_N\) each have variance \(\sigma^2\), and let \(\mathrm{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \rho\sigma^2\) for \(s \ne t\). With discount factors \(v_t\), write the variance of the NPV in terms of \(\rho\), and state the sign of the change relative to independence when \(\rho \gt 0\).

Solution

\(\mathrm{Var}(\sum_t v_t X_t) = \sum_t v_t^2 \sigma^2 + \sum_{s \ne t} v_s v_t \rho \sigma^2\). For \(\rho = 0\) this is the formula of Proposition 2.3. Every added term has the sign of \(\rho\) because \(v_s v_t \gt 0\). For \(N \ge 2\) there is at least one added term, so for \(\rho \gt 0\) the variance is larger than under independence. The closed forms of this lesson therefore understate the spread when \(N \ge 2\) and receipts are positively correlated.

P3 · The median of an odd sample

Show that for an odd sample of size \(n\), the nearest-rank median of Definition 1.2 is the middle value of the sorted sample.

Solution

For \(p = 0.5\) and odd \(n\), the rank is \(\lceil n/2 \rceil = (n+1)/2\), because \(n/2\) is a half-integer and its ceiling is \((n+1)/2\). The value at that position in the ascending sort has \((n-1)/2\) values below it and \((n-1)/2\) above it, so it is the middle value.

Empirical

E1 · The error at 100 draws, in standard errors

Lab 1 prints a sample mean of 84543.40 cents at n = 100 with a standard error of 4400.22 cents. Compute the error in cents against the closed form, and the number of standard errors it represents.

Solution

The error is \(84543.40 - 79078.68 = 5464.72\) cents. Divided by the standard error, \(5464.72/4400.22 = 1.2419\), which the lab prints as +1.242 to three decimals. The error is well inside three standard errors. Under the normal reading of Remark 2.4, which this lesson does not test, the two-sided probability of a deviation of at least 1.2419 standard errors is \(2(1 - \Phi(1.2419)) = 0.2143\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function. So under that reading this result is unremarkable. The figure is SIMULATED, graded C, from Lab 1.

E2 · A sampling band for the share below zero

Lab 1 prints a share of 0.0552 of draws with negative NPV and a standard error of 0.0010. Compute an approximate 95% sampling band for the share, and say what the band measures.

Solution

The band is \(0.0552 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0010\), from the printed share and standard error, which is about 0.0532 to 0.0572. The 1.96 multiplier is the normal reading of Remark 2.4, which this lesson does not test. The band measures the sampling error of 50000 draws from this model. It does not measure the error in the receipt distribution, the rate or the independence assumption, and Section 4 lists those. The share is graded C because it comes from Lab 1 alone.

E3 · Width of the middle 80%

Lab 1 prints a 10% quantile of 14896.62 cents and a 90% quantile of 143406.13 cents. Compute the width of the middle 80% of simulated NPVs in currency units, and say whether the band is a confidence interval for the NPV of a real project.

Solution

The width is \(143406.13 - 14896.62 = 128509.51\) cents, which is 1285.10 currency units (cents divided by 100). The band is not a confidence interval for a real project's NPV. It describes the simulated spread under the model of Proposition 2.3, and the model is an assumption (Misconception 2).

Research

R1 · A convergence study for a model without a closed form

Design a convergence study for an NPV model whose expected value has no closed form. Specify the seeds, the sample sizes, the statistic reported, and how the study decides that the estimate has converged. State what the study cannot establish.

Solution

Use a fixed set of seeds, for example 20, generated by random.Random(seed), and sample sizes that double, for example 100 to 51200. Report the mean and its standard error at each size, for each seed. Convergence means that the standard error falls at the rate of Proposition 2.1 and that the means across seeds agree within their standard errors at the largest size. A second check computes two estimators from the same draws, so that they are compared on identical inputs. The study cannot establish that the model is right, and it cannot show convergence of any quantile without a separate error estimate.

R2 · Equal means, different outcomes

Give two random NPVs with the same expectation, one of which has a positive probability of a negative value and the other none. Prove that both properties hold.

Solution

Let \(X\) equal \(-1\) or \(+1\) with probability one half each, and let \(Y = 0\). Then \(\E[X] = 0 = \E[Y]\). \(\Pr(X \lt 0) = 1/2 \gt 0\), and \(\Pr(Y \lt 0) = 0\). The example is Remark 3.1, and it shows that a sample mean cannot stand in for the share of losing draws.

R3 · Which assumption to test first

Name the assumption of Proposition 2.3 that a single common demand shock violates. Explain how a SIMULATED study would test its effect on the NPV distribution, and the sign of the effect on the variance, from the algebra of Problem P2.

Solution

The assumption is independence across years. A common demand shock whose loadings are all non-zero and of one sign makes every pair of receipts positively correlated, so the variance of the NPV is larger than the independent formula. P2 states that result for one common pairwise covariance, which a shock with year-specific loadings does not give. The term-by-term argument of the P2 solution still applies, because each pairwise covariance is then positive. A study would generate receipts with one shared shock per simulated path, then compare the NPV spread with and without the shock at the same means and the same per-year variances. The expected result is a wider distribution with the same expected NPV, because the expectation does not depend on correlation.
