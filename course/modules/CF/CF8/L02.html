Trade Credit, Credit Policy and Supply-Chain Finance

  • Uses: working-capital days, cash conversion cycle, net present value, present value, discount factor, annuity
  • Introduces: trade credit and credit policy; supply-chain finance
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or convention
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A supplier that lets a customer pay later is lending it money, and the price of that loan is hidden in the terms. An early-payment discount states the price directly, so a firm can compare it with its other borrowing. A credit-term extension asks whether the extra sales repay the cash tied up in receivables. Supply-chain finance moves the payment to a financier. This lesson computes the implied annual cost of a discount, derives the break-even margin of an extension in closed form, and sets out what supply-chain finance changes in the buyer's and the supplier's day counts.

1Credit terms and the implied cost of a discount

The terms used here have three numbers. The invoice amount is \(S\). The seller offers a discount \(d\) with \(0 < d < 1\) if the buyer pays by day \(k\), and requires the full \(S\) by day \(N\), where \(0 \le k < N\). The lesson writes such terms as \(d/k\) net \(N\).

Definition 1.1 (trade credit and the credit policy)

Trade credit is the deferral of payment for goods already delivered. A credit policy is the rule a seller applies to that deferral: the credit period \(N\), the discount \(d\) and the early date \(k\).

The early-payment discount has two payment choices for the buyer. Pay \((1-d)S\) on day \(k\), or pay \(S\) on day \(N\). The difference between them is the subject of Proposition 1.3.

Convention 1.2 (simple annualization)

The annual cost of a period of \(N - k\) days is the simple rate that scales the period cost to a 365-day year, with no compounding: the period cost multiplied by \(365/(N-k)\). The lesson reports this figure as the simple annual cost. The compounded figure, used in Section 2, compounds the period cost over the same \(365/(N-k)\) periods: \((1/(1-d))^{365/(N-k)} - 1\).

Proposition 1.3 (the implied cost of giving up a discount)

Under Definition 1.1, paying \(S\) on day \(N\) instead of \((1-d)S\) on day \(k\) is the same as borrowing \((1-d)S\) from day \(k\) to day \(N\) at a cost of \(dS\). The cost of the period is \(d/(1-d)\) of the amount borrowed, and its simple annual cost, under Convention 1.2, is

\[ c(d, k, N) = \frac{d}{1-d}\cdot\frac{365}{N-k}. \]

Proof

The buyer who pays on day \(k\) pays \((1-d)S\). The buyer who waits to day \(N\) pays \(S\). Between the two dates the buyer keeps \((1-d)S\) for \(N-k\) days. The extra payment is \(S - (1-d)S = dS\), so the period cost is \(dS\) on an amount of \((1-d)S\). The ratio is \(dS/((1-d)S) = d/(1-d)\), which does not depend on \(S\). Scaling this period cost to a year by Convention 1.2 gives \(c(d,k,N)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.4 (terms 2/10 net 30)

Take \(d = 0.02\), \(k = 10\) and \(N = 30\). The period cost is \(0.02/0.98 = 0.020408\ldots\), over \(N - k = 20\) days. The simple annual cost is \[0.020408\ldots \cdot 365/20 = 0.37245\ldots\], which is 37.24 percent. The 2 percent discount costs 2.04 percent of the amount kept for those 20 days, not a cost for a year. Lab 2, Part A, prints the same 37.24 percent for these terms, and 44.59 percent compounded.

Proposition 1.5 (the cost rises with the discount and falls with the gap)

For fixed \(N - k\), \(c(d,k,N)\) is strictly increasing in \(d\) on \((0,1)\). For fixed \(d\), \(c(d,k,N)\) is strictly decreasing in \(N - k\) on \((0,\infty)\).

Proof

The map \(d \mapsto d/(1-d)\) has derivative \(1/(1-d)^2\), which is positive on \((0,1)\), so it is strictly increasing. Multiplying by the positive constant \(365/(N-k)\) keeps that property. For the second claim, \(d/(1-d)\) is fixed and positive, and \(N - k \mapsto 365/(N-k)\) is strictly decreasing on \((0,\infty)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.6 (the same algebra covers financing)

Proposition 1.3 is a statement about one payment and one later payment. It says nothing about why a seller offers the discount. Section 4 uses the same formula for a financier's discount on an invoice, because the algebra does not change when the party who pays early is a bank and not the buyer.

2Compounded and simple costs: a basis choice

Proposition 2.1 (compounding gives the larger cost)

Let \(N - k \le 365\), so that \(n = 365/(N-k) \ge 1\). The compounded annual cost \((1/(1-d))^{n} - 1\) is at least the simple annual cost \(c(d,k,N)\). The inequality is strict for every \(d \in (0,1)\) when \(N - k < 365\), and for \(N - k = 365\) the two costs are equal.

Proof

Let \(x = d/(1-d) > 0\) and let \(h(x) = (1+x)^{n} - 1 - nx\) for \(x \ge 0\), with \(n \ge 1\). Then \(h(0) = 0\), and \(h'(x) = n\,[(1+x)^{n-1} - 1]\). For \(n \ge 1\) and \(x \ge 0\) the base \(1 + x\) is at least 1, so \((1+x)^{n-1} \ge 1\) and \(h'(x) \ge 0\). Hence \(h(x) \ge 0\), with \(h(x) > 0\) for \(x > 0\) when \(n > 1\). Since \(c(d,k,N) = nx\) and \((1/(1-d))^n - 1 = (1+x)^n - 1\), the claim follows. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.2 (which annual figure to quote)

The simple figure is the one this lesson uses, and it is the smaller of the two. A reader who quotes an annual rate must name the convention. Lab 2, Part A, prints both figures for four sets of SIMULATED terms, and the compounded figure is larger in each of the four rows.

3Credit-term extensions and the break-even margin

A seller that lengthens its credit period from \(N_0\) to \(N_1\) days does two things at once. It ties up more cash in receivables on the sales it already makes, and it may win extra sales. The model below keeps both effects and fixes the rest.

Assumption 3.0 (the extension model)

(1) The seller's annual revenue before the change is \(\mathrm{Rev}_0\), collected on terms of \(N_0\) days. (2) The extension adds \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev} > 0\) of revenue each year for years \(1, \dots, n\), and that revenue earns a margin \(m\) per dollar, received at each year end. (3) The new terms apply to all revenue after the change, so the year-end receivable is revenue times the credit period over 365. (4) The extra receivable \(\Delta I\) is paid at period 0, and it is recovered at the end of year \(n\). (5) One rate \(r > 0\) discounts every period. (6) Nothing else changes: costs, bad debts and the revenue base are as they were.

Definition 3.1 (the extra investment)

Under Assumption 3.0, the extra investment in receivables is

\[ \Delta I = (\mathrm{Rev}_0 + \Delta\mathrm{Rev})\,\frac{N_1}{365} - \mathrm{Rev}_0\,\frac{N_0}{365}. \]

It is the year-end receivable on the new terms less the receivable on the old terms, on the same convention as Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1.

Proposition 3.2 (the break-even margin)

Under Assumption 3.0, the extension has a net present value of zero at the margin

\[ m^\ast = \frac{r\,\Delta I}{\Delta\mathrm{Rev}}, \]

and this margin does not depend on \(n\). The net present value at a margin \(m\) is

\[ \mathrm{NPV}(m) = -\Delta I + m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n) + \Delta I\,(1+r)^{-n}, \qquad A(r,n) = \sum_{t=1}^{n}(1+r)^{-t}. \]

Proof

The first claim is the expression for \(\mathrm{NPV}(m)\), which follows from Assumption 3.0: the investment is paid at period 0 and returned at the end of year \(n\), and the margin \(m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\) is received at the end of each year \(1, \dots, n\). Discounting each cash flow at \(r\) gives the sum. Summing the geometric series, \(A(r,n) = (1 - (1+r)^{-n})/r\), so \(1 - (1+r)^{-n} = r\,A(r,n)\). Setting \(\mathrm{NPV}(m) = 0\) and using this identity gives \[-\Delta I\,(1 - (1+r)^{-n}) + m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n) = 0\], that is \[-\Delta I\,r\,A(r,n) + m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n) = 0\]. Since \(A(r,n) > 0\), dividing by it gives \(m = r\,\Delta I/\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\). The right side has no \(n\). \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 3.3 (the decision rule)

Under Assumption 3.0, \(\mathrm{NPV}(m)\) is strictly increasing in \(m\), because \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n) > 0\). So the extension has a positive net present value exactly when the margin exceeds \(m^\ast\).

Proof

The derivative of \(\mathrm{NPV}(m)\) in \(m\) is \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n)\), which is positive because \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev} > 0\) and \(A(r,n) > 0\). Hence \(\mathrm{NPV}\) crosses zero once, at \(m^\ast\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.4 (a SIMULATED extension)

Let \(\mathrm{Rev}_0 = 12{,}000{,}000\) dollars, \(N_0 = 30\), \(N_1 = 45\), \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev} = 1{,}500{,}000\) dollars and \(r = 0.10\). Definition 3.1 gives \[\Delta I = (13{,}500{,}000 \cdot 45 - 12{,}000{,}000 \cdot 30)/365 = 678{,}082.19\] dollars. Proposition 3.2 gives \[m^\ast = 0.10 \cdot 678{,}082.19 / 1{,}500{,}000 = 0.0452\], which is 4.52 percent of extra revenue. Lab 2, Part B, prints the same investment and margin, and an NPV at a 5 percent margin of 27,262.51 dollars over five years. A margin of 5 percent is above the break-even, so the NPV is positive.

Remark 3.5 (where the result comes from)

Proposition 3.2 is an exact result under Assumption 3.0. Its simple form, \(r\,\Delta I/\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\), says that the break-even margin is the carrying charge on the extra receivable, one year's interest at \(r\), spread over the extra revenue. It does not say that the carrying charge is the right rate; \(r\) is an input that the analyst must justify.

4Supply-chain finance

Definition 4.1 (supply-chain finance)

Supply-chain finance is a payment arrangement in which a financier pays the supplier, before the invoice's due date, a sum \((1-\delta)S\) that is less than the invoice \(S\). The buyer then pays the financier the full \(S\) on the original due date \(N\). The discount \(\delta\), with \(0 < \delta < 1\), is the financier's price for the early payment.

Proposition 4.2 (the supplier's cost is the same algebra)

Let the financier pay \((1-\delta)S\) on day \(k\) and collect \(S\) from the buyer on day \(N\), with \(0 \le k < N\). The supplier's implied simple annual cost of receiving the early payment is \(c(\delta, k, N)\) of Proposition 1.3. It is positive for every \(\delta > 0\).

Proof

The supplier receives \((1-\delta)S\) on day \(k\) in place of \(S\) on day \(N\). This is the early-payment choice of Proposition 1.3 with \(d = \delta\), so the cost is the same expression. For \(\delta > 0\), \(d/(1-d) > 0\) and \(365/(N-k) > 0\), so the cost is positive. \(\blacksquare\)

Assumption 4.2 (the buyer's payable is unchanged by the program)

Under Definition 4.1, the buyer's trade payable stays at the invoice amount \(S\) until the due date \(N\), and the program changes neither that balance nor the buyer's purchases.

Remark 4.3 (what the program alone changes in the day counts)

Under Assumption 4.2 the program leaves the buyer's payable balance and its purchases unchanged, so the buyer's DPO, \(\mathrm{AP}\cdot D/\mathrm{Pur}\) in Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1, is unchanged. A change in the buyer's DPO can come from a change in its payable balance or in its purchases, and a change in the due date \(N\) can change the payable balance at a year end. The supplier receives cash on day \(k\) instead of day \(N\). Whether the supplier's balance sheet still shows the receivable is an accounting question that this lesson does not settle.

Lab 2 · Early-payment costs and credit-term break-evens (SIMULATED)

"""CF8 lab L02: early-payment discounts, credit-term break-evens and supply-chain financing, on SIMULATED terms.

SIMULATED: every term, balance, margin and rate below is an invented input for this lab. No company, supplier or
financier is real, and the lab reads no data file and makes no network request. Money is in dollars, not cents:
Part B uses exact dollar Fractions, and each printed dollar amount is rounded half up to the cent for display only.
The break-even margin and the NPV checks use fractions.Fraction and are exact. The compounded implied cost in Part A
is computed in floating point and printed to two decimals. Part D draws its cases from a seeded random number
generator. Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from decimal import ROUND_HALF_UP, Decimal
from fractions import Fraction

DAYS_PER_YEAR = 365
SEED = 20261029
RATE = Fraction(1, 10)


def pct_text(value: Fraction) -> str:
    """A rate as a percentage rounded half up to two decimals, for display only."""
    quotient = Decimal(value.numerator) * 100 / Decimal(value.denominator)
    return str(quotient.quantize(Decimal("0.01"), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP)) + "%"


def dollars_text(value: Fraction) -> str:
    """A dollar amount shown to two decimals, rounded half up for display only; the exact value is kept elsewhere."""
    quotient = Decimal(value.numerator) / Decimal(value.denominator)
    return str(quotient.quantize(Decimal("0.01"), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP))


def implied_simple_cost(discount: Fraction, days_early: int, days_late: int) -> Fraction:
    """Annual simple cost of giving up a discount d for paying N - k days later: d/(1-d) times 365/(N-k)."""
    if days_late <= days_early:
        raise ValueError("the full payment must fall after the discount date")
    return discount / (1 - discount) * Fraction(DAYS_PER_YEAR, days_late - days_early)


def implied_compound_cost(discount: Fraction, days_early: int, days_late: int) -> float:
    """Annual compounded cost in floating point: (1/(1-d)) raised to 365/(N-k), minus one."""
    factor = float(1 / (1 - discount))
    power = float(factor ** (DAYS_PER_YEAR / (days_late - days_early)))
    return power - 1.0


def npv_extension(margin: Fraction, extra_margin_base: Fraction, extra_invest: Fraction, years: int) -> Fraction:
    """NPV at RATE of an extension: pay the investment now, earn margin times the extra revenue each year, get the
    investment back at year n. The extra revenue is extra_margin_base; the investment is extra_invest."""
    annuity = sum((1 / (1 + RATE)) ** t for t in range(1, years + 1))
    return -extra_invest + margin * extra_margin_base * annuity + extra_invest / (1 + RATE) ** years


def break_even_margin(extra_revenue: Fraction, extra_invest: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Break-even margin m* = r times the investment over the extra revenue (the closed form of Section 3)."""
    return RATE * extra_invest / extra_revenue


def extension_investment(revenue: Fraction, days_old: int, days_new: int, extra_revenue: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Extra receivables from the new terms: (revenue + extra revenue) times the new days, less the old balance."""
    new_balance = (revenue + extra_revenue) * days_new / DAYS_PER_YEAR
    old_balance = revenue * days_old / DAYS_PER_YEAR
    return new_balance - old_balance


def main() -> None:
    print("A. Early-payment terms: implied annual cost of giving up the discount (SIMULATED terms)")
    terms = [
        ("2/10 net 30", Fraction(2, 100), 10, 30),
        ("1/10 net 30", Fraction(1, 100), 10, 30),
        ("2/10 net 60", Fraction(2, 100), 10, 60),
        ("3/15 net 45", Fraction(3, 100), 15, 45),
    ]
    for label, discount, early, late in terms:
        simple = implied_simple_cost(discount, early, late)
        compound = implied_compound_cost(discount, early, late)
        print(
            f"{label}: discount {pct_text(discount)}, days paid early {early}, days paid late {late}, "
            f"simple annual cost {pct_text(simple)}, compounded annual cost {compound * 100:.2f}%"
        )
    supplier_discount = Fraction(5, 1000)
    financed = implied_simple_cost(supplier_discount, 10, 60)
    print(
        f"financed payment (SIMULATED): discount {pct_text(supplier_discount)} for paying 50 days early, "
        f"simple annual cost {pct_text(financed)}"
    )

    print()
    print("B. Credit-term extension: break-even margin m*, equal to r times the investment over the extra revenue")
    revenue = Fraction(12_000_000)
    days_old, days_new = 30, 45
    extra_revenue = Fraction(1_500_000)
    years = 5
    invest = extension_investment(revenue, days_old, days_new, extra_revenue)
    print(f"revenue before the extension, in dollars: {dollars_text(revenue)}")
    print(f"extra revenue from the extension, in dollars: {dollars_text(extra_revenue)}")
    print(f"investment in receivables, in dollars, to the cent: {dollars_text(invest)}")
    m_star = break_even_margin(extra_revenue, invest)
    print(f"break-even margin m*, as a share of extra revenue: {pct_text(m_star)}")
    print(f"NPV at m* over {years} years, exact value: {npv_extension(m_star, extra_revenue, invest, years)}")
    below = npv_extension(m_star - Fraction(1, 100), extra_revenue, invest, years)
    above = npv_extension(m_star + Fraction(1, 100), extra_revenue, invest, years)
    print(f"NPV at m* minus one point is negative: {below < 0}")
    print(f"NPV at m* plus one point is positive: {above > 0}")
    print(
        "NPV at a 5 percent margin, in dollars: "
        + dollars_text(npv_extension(Fraction(5, 100), extra_revenue, invest, years))
    )
    same = all(
        npv_extension(break_even_margin(extra_revenue, invest), extra_revenue, invest, n) == 0 for n in range(1, 11)
    )
    print(f"m* gives an NPV of exactly zero for every horizon from 1 to 10 years: {same}")

    print()
    print("C. Closed form check")
    closed_form = RATE * invest / extra_revenue
    print(f"closed form r times investment over extra revenue equals m*: {closed_form == m_star}")

    print()
    print("D. Two hundred seeded SIMULATED extensions: the exact break-even is a zero of the NPV")
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    zeros = 0
    accepted_at_ten = 0
    for _ in range(200):
        base = Fraction(rng.randint(2_000_000, 40_000_000))
        old_days = rng.randint(20, 40)
        new_days = old_days + rng.randint(5, 30)
        extra = Fraction(rng.randint(100_000, 5_000_000))
        term = rng.randint(1, 8)
        inv = extension_investment(base, old_days, new_days, extra)
        m = break_even_margin(extra, inv)
        zeros += npv_extension(m, extra, inv, term) == 0
        accepted_at_ten += m < Fraction(10, 100)
    print(f"cases whose NPV at m* is exactly zero, out of 200: {zeros}")
    print(f"cases whose break-even margin is below ten percent, out of 200: {accepted_at_ten}")

5Where the canon breaks

The algebra is exact. The model that feeds it is not, and four assumptions carry the results:

  • The investment is recovered at year \(n\). Proposition 3.2 uses this recovery. If the receivable is never recovered, the break-even margin is higher, as Problem R1 shows. The horizon of the extension is a judgment the model does not supply.
  • The margin is a stated input. The model takes \(m\) as given, with the costs that vary with extra sales already removed. Estimating \(m\) is a separate task.
  • Default risk is outside the model. Assumption 3.0 item (6) keeps bad debts unchanged. A longer credit period could bring more non-payment, and the model does not price it.
  • Compounding is a convention. Section 2 shows the simple and compounded costs differ, and a comparison with a rate of borrowing needs both on one basis.

6Misconceptions

  1. “A 2 percent discount for paying early is a 2 percent cost.”

    The 2 percent discount costs 2.04 percent of the amount kept, over the days between the early date and the due date. Under the terms 2/10 net 30 the gap is 20 days, and the simple annual cost is 37.24 percent (Example 1.4 and Lab 2, Part A). The same 2 percent over a 50-day gap, as in 2/10 net 60, costs 14.90 percent a year, so the gap must appear next to the rate.

  2. “A credit extension pays if the extra sales are large enough to cover the costs.”

    The extra receivable on existing sales is a cost, and the test is the margin on the extra revenue against \(r\,\Delta I/\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\), which includes the carrying charge on the whole receivable increase. Corollary 3.3 says the extension has a positive net present value exactly when the margin exceeds that break-even.

  3. “A firm that stretches its payables pays nothing for the longer period.”

    If the supplier offers an early-payment discount, the stretch gives up that discount. Proposition 1.3 gives the cost of the period, and for 2/10 net 30 it is 37.24 percent a year on the simple convention. Section 3 does not model a payables stretch, so this lesson makes no claim about its cost beyond the discount forgone.

7Problem set

Attempt each problem before opening its solution. Every term and balance is SIMULATED.

Mechanics

M1 · The implied cost of 1/15 net 45

Terms of 1/15 net 45 give a discount of 1 percent for payment by day 15 and full payment by day 45. Find the simple annual cost of the early payment, to two decimals.

Solution

Here \(d = 0.01\), \(k = 15\) and \(N = 45\), so \(N - k = 30\). Proposition 1.3 gives \[c = (0.01/0.99)\cdot 365/30 = 0.010101\ldots \cdot 12.1667 = 0.122895\ldots\], which is 12.29 percent a year on the simple convention.

M2 · Break-even margin of an extension

A seller has revenue of 6,000,000 dollars a year on 30-day terms. It extends terms to 60 days and expects 600,000 dollars of extra revenue a year for four years. The rate is 8 percent. Find the extra receivable in dollars to the cent, and the break-even margin as a share of extra revenue, to two decimals.

Solution

By Definition 3.1, \[\Delta I = (6{,}600{,}000 \cdot 60 - 6{,}000{,}000 \cdot 30)/365 = (396{,}000{,}000 - 180{,}000{,}000)/365 = 216{,}000{,}000/365 = 591{,}780.8219\ldots\] dollars, which is 591,780.82 dollars to the cent. By Proposition 3.2, \[m^\ast = 0.08 \cdot 591{,}780.8219\ldots/600{,}000 = 0.078904\ldots\], which is 7.89 percent. The horizon of four years does not enter the answer.

M3 · NPV at a margin of 10 percent

For the extension of Problem M2, find the net present value at a margin of 10 percent of extra revenue, in dollars to the cent, using the closed form of Proposition 3.2.

Solution

The annuity factor is \[A(0.08, 4) = \sum_{t=1}^{4} 1.08^{-t} = (1 - 1.08^{-4})/0.08 = 3.3121268\ldots\]. Then \[\mathrm{NPV}(0.10) = -591{,}780.8219 + 0.10 \cdot 600{,}000 \cdot 3.3121268\ldots + 591{,}780.8219 \cdot 1.08^{-4}\]. The three terms are \(-591{,}780.82\), \(198{,}727.61\) and \(434{,}976.57\) dollars to the cent. Their sum is \(41{,}923.36\) dollars to the cent, which is the exact sum \(41{,}923.3589\ldots\) dollars rounded half up. The margin of 10 percent is above the break-even of 7.89 percent, so the sign is positive, as Corollary 3.3 requires.

Proof

P1 · The period cost is d over one minus d

Show that paying \((1-d)S\) on day \(k\) rather than \(S\) on day \(N\) earns a period return of \(d/(1-d)\) on the amount paid early, for every \(S > 0\).

Solution

Keeping \((1-d)S\) for the period means not paying \(S - (1-d)S = dS\) on day \(N\), which is the gain. The return on the amount paid early is the gain over the amount paid: \(dS/((1-d)S) = d/(1-d)\). The factor \(S\) cancels, so the return is the same for every invoice size. The argument uses only the two payment dates and the two amounts.

P2 · The cost increases with the discount

Prove Proposition 1.5's first claim without using the derivative of \(d/(1-d)\): show that for \(0 < d_1 < d_2 < 1\), \(d_1/(1-d_1) < d_2/(1-d_2)\).

Solution

Cross-multiply, which is valid because both denominators are positive. The claim \(d_1(1-d_2) < d_2(1-d_1)\) expands to \(d_1 - d_1 d_2 < d_2 - d_1 d_2\), that is \(d_1 < d_2\), which is true by hypothesis. So the function is strictly increasing on \((0,1)\), and multiplying by the positive constant \(365/(N-k)\) keeps the order.

P3 · Compounding never gives less than simple

Prove Proposition 2.1 for \(N - k = 30\) and any \(d \in (0,1)\), by showing \((1+x)^n - 1 \ge nx\) with \(x = d/(1-d)\) and \(n = 365/30\).

Solution

Here \(n = 365/30 = 12.1667 \ge 1\) and \(x > 0\). Let \(h(x) = (1+x)^n - 1 - nx\). Then \(h(0) = 0\) and \(h'(x) = n[(1+x)^{n-1} - 1]\). Because \(n - 1 > 0\) and \(1 + x > 1\), the power \((1+x)^{n-1} > 1\), so \(h' > 0\) on \((0, \infty)\). Hence \(h(x) > 0\) for \(x > 0\), which is the claim. The proof uses the exponent \(n \ge 1\), which holds because \(N - k \le 365\).

Empirical

E1 · Three offers against a 9 percent bank line

A SIMULATED buyer is offered 2/10 net 30, 1/15 net 45 and 2/10 net 60. Its bank line costs 9 percent a year on the simple convention. Find the simple annual cost of each offer, rank them, and state whether paying early beats the bank line for each.

Solution

The three costs are 37.24 percent for 2/10 net 30, 12.29 percent for 1/15 net 45 (Problem M1) and 14.90 percent for 2/10 net 60, which is \[(0.02/0.98)\cdot 365/50 = 0.148979\ldots\]. Ranked from the lowest cost up: 1/15 net 45, then 2/10 net 60, then 2/10 net 30. Each cost exceeds 9 percent, so for each of the three offers, paying early and borrowing from the bank would be the cheaper route for the buyer, under the simple convention and this bank rate.

E2 · A SIMULATED extension with a 6 percent margin

A seller has revenue of 20,000,000 dollars on 30-day terms and considers 45-day terms. Extra revenue is 2,000,000 dollars a year for three years, the margin is 6 percent of extra revenue, and the rate is 9 percent. Find the extra receivable to the cent, the break-even margin to two decimals, and whether the extension has a positive net present value.

Solution

\[\Delta I = (22{,}000{,}000 \cdot 45 - 20{,}000{,}000 \cdot 30)/365 = (990{,}000{,}000 - 600{,}000{,}000)/365 = 1{,}068{,}493.15\] dollars. The break-even margin is \[0.09 \cdot 1{,}068{,}493.15/2{,}000{,}000 = 0.048082\ldots\], which is 4.81 percent. The margin of 6 percent is above it, so by Corollary 3.3 the net present value is positive. Its value is 60,334.97 dollars, which comes from \[\mathrm{NPV}(0.06) = -\Delta I + 0.06 \cdot 2{,}000{,}000 \cdot A(0.09, 3) + \Delta I \cdot 1.09^{-3}\] with \(A(0.09, 3) = 2.531295\ldots\).

E3 · Reading Lab 2, Part D

Lab 2, Part D, prints that the net present value at the break-even margin is exactly zero in 200 of 200 generated cases, and that 156 of the 200 have a break-even margin below ten percent. Say what each printed count establishes, and what it does not.

Solution

The count of 200 establishes that the identity of Proposition 3.2 holds in every generated case, which is an exact check of the algebra on those inputs. The count of 156 is a fact about the seed and the ranges the generator used. It does not show how often a real seller faces a break-even margin below ten percent, because the generator's ranges were chosen for the lab and are not estimated from any market. Neither count is evidence about firms.

Research

R1 · An extension that is never recovered

Suppose the extra receivable is never recovered, and the margin is earned for \(n\) years only. Find the break-even margin \(m_{\mathrm{nr}}\) and show that it exceeds \(m^\ast\) for every \(n\) and every \(r > 0\). Evaluate it for the extension of Problem M2.

Solution

With no recovery, \[\mathrm{NPV}(m) = -\Delta I + m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n)\], so \[m_{\mathrm{nr}} = \Delta I/(\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n))\]. Using \(A(r,n) = (1-(1+r)^{-n})/r\) gives \[m_{\mathrm{nr}} = m^\ast/(1-(1+r)^{-n})\]. Since \(0 < (1+r)^{-n} < 1\), the denominator lies in \((0,1)\), so \(m_{\mathrm{nr}} > m^\ast\). For Problem M2, \(A(0.08,4) = 3.3121268\ldots\), so \[m_{\mathrm{nr}} = 591{,}780.82/(600{,}000 \cdot 3.312126) = 0.297785\ldots\], which is 29.78 percent, against 7.89 percent with recovery. The ratio of the margin without recovery to \(m^\ast\) is \(1/(1-1.08^{-4}) = 3.77\).

R2 · A concentrated set of customers

Extra sales come from three large customers, not spread across all customers. Describe which inputs of Assumption 3.0 fail, and what to measure before the break-even margin is used.

Solution

Assumption 3.0 item (2) assumes the extra revenue arrives as a stable flow each year. Three customers can leave, delay or renegotiate, so the flow is uncertain and its timing is lumpy. Item (6) assumes bad debts are unchanged, and a large customer's default is a large change. Before the margin is used, the analyst should measure each customer's history of payment against terms, the share of the extra revenue each customer supplies, and the margin on each customer's sales. The model does not give these inputs, and its result depends on them.

R3 · What data would show a change in a buyer's DPO

A supplier wants to know whether its large customer lengthened its payable period after joining a supply-chain finance program. State the three quantities needed and the test that uses them, without assuming the answer.

Solution

The three quantities are the customer's year-end payables, its purchases in the year, and the due dates on its open invoices before and after joining the program. From the first two, the customer's DPO follows from Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1, before and after. The due dates show whether the contract term changed, which Remark 4.3 treats as a contract matter, separate from the program. The test compares DPO across the two periods on the same basis and checks whether the due-date change accounts for it. A change in DPO can come from the payables balance, from purchases, or from a change in the due dates. Holding purchases fixed separates the balance effect, and the payment dates on paid invoices, compared with the due dates, separate timing from the contract term. The analyst needs both before naming a cause.
