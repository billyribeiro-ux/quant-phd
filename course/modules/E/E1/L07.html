Lesson 6 gives three outcomes for one market, and they differ in volume and in fee. The question a regulator asks is what the difference costs. This lesson measures consumers' gain as an area under the demand curve, measures the exchanges' profit from the same volume and fee, and measures the shortfall from the competitive outcome, which Section 1 defines as the loss of surplus. In this model the loss is an exact square, so the three structures are compared without an approximation.

1Surplus and the competitive benchmark

In this lesson total surplus means the sum of consumer surplus and exchange profit. It is not a social welfare function, and the lesson does not state a welfare theorem. The market terms are those of Lesson 6: trade demand \(Q(f) = \max(0, a - bf)\), the inverse demand \(P(Q) = (a - Q)/b\), the constant marginal cost \(c > 0\), and \(d = a - bc > 0\), under the hypotheses of Lesson 6, Definition 1.1.

Definition 1.1 (consumer and producer surplus)

Let \(0 \le Q \le a\) units trade, at one fee \(p = P(Q)\) cents per unit. The consumer and producer surplus has two parts. Consumer surplus is \[ CS(Q) = \int_0^Q P(x)\,dx - pQ, \] the area under the inverse demand curve up to \(Q\), minus what the traders pay. Producer surplus is the exchange's profit, \(PS(Q) = (p - c)\,Q\), since there is no fixed cost. Total surplus is \(W(Q) = CS(Q) + PS(Q)\).

The fee \(p = P(Q)\) is the price at which the volume \(Q\) clears, so every trader pays the same fee. The definition applies to the three structures of Lesson 6, whose volumes \(d/2\), \(2d/3\) and \(d\) all lie in \([0, a]\), because \(0 < d \le a\).

Theorem 1.2 (surplus under linear demand)

Assume the hypotheses of Lesson 6, Definition 1.1, and let \(0 \le Q \le a\). Then \[ CS(Q) = \frac{Q^2}{2b}, \qquad PS(Q) = \frac{(d - Q)\,Q}{b}, \qquad W(Q) = \frac{2dQ - Q^2}{2b} = \int_0^Q \bigl(P(x) - c\bigr)\,dx. \]

Proof

The antiderivative of \(P(x) = (a - x)/b\) is \((ax - x^2/2)/b\), so \(\int_0^Q P(x)\,dx = (aQ - Q^2/2)/b\). Then \(CS(Q) = (aQ - Q^2/2)/b - (a - Q)Q/b = (aQ - Q^2/2 - aQ + Q^2)/b = Q^2/(2b)\). By Lemma 1.3 of Lesson 6, \(PS(Q) = (P(Q) - c)Q = (d - Q)Q/b\). Adding, \(W(Q) = Q^2/(2b) + (dQ - Q^2)/b = (2dQ - Q^2)/(2b)\). Finally \(\int_0^Q (P(x) - c)\,dx = (aQ - Q^2/2)/b - cQ = (aQ - Q^2/2 - bcQ)/b = (dQ - Q^2/2)/b\), which is \((2dQ - Q^2)/(2b)\).

The consumer surplus is the triangle with base \(Q\) and height \(P(0) - P(Q) = Q/b\), whose area is \(Q^2/(2b)\). Theorem 1.2 says the same thing algebraically, and it is the basis of the exact checks in Lab 1.

Definition 1.3 (the competitive benchmark and deadweight loss)

The competitive benchmark is the outcome in which the fee equals marginal cost, \(P(Q) = c\). This lesson takes that condition as the definition of the benchmark, and it does not derive it from a supply curve. It has volume \(d\). The deadweight loss at volume \(Q\) is \[ DWL(Q) = W(d) - W(Q), \] the shortfall of total surplus below its competitive level. Surplus that a trade would have created and that no one receives is what the loss measures.

Lemma 1.4 (the competitive volume maximises total surplus)

Assume the hypotheses of Lesson 6, Definition 1.1. On \(0 \le Q \le a\), \(W\) is largest at \(Q = d\), where \(W(d) = d^2/(2b)\).

Proof

Since \(d^2 - (Q - d)^2 = 2dQ - Q^2\), Theorem 1.2 gives \(W(Q) = (d^2 - (Q - d)^2)/(2b)\). This is largest only where \((Q - d)^2 = 0\), that is at \(Q = d\), and \(d \in [0, a]\). At \(Q = d\), \(W(d) = d^2/(2b)\).

Theorem 1.5 (the deadweight loss under linear demand)

Assume the hypotheses of Lesson 6, Definition 1.1, and let \(0 \le Q \le a\). Then \(DWL(Q) = (d - Q)^2/(2b)\). The loss is zero only at \(Q = d\), and it is positive for every other volume in \([0, a]\).

Proof

By Theorem 1.2 and Lemma 1.4, \(DWL(Q) = d^2/(2b) - (2dQ - Q^2)/(2b) = (d^2 - 2dQ + Q^2)/(2b) = (d - Q)^2/(2b)\). This is zero only when \(Q = d\), and positive otherwise.

2The three structures compared

Corollary 2.1 (the surplus of each structure)

Assume the hypotheses of Lesson 6, Definition 1.1. Using the volumes and fees of Lesson 6, Proposition 1.4 and Theorem 2.5 and Theorem 3.3 there, the three structures have the following surplus values.

Monopoly, volume \(d/2\): \(CS = d^2/(8b)\), \(PS = d^2/(4b)\), \(W = 3d^2/(8b)\), \(DWL = d^2/(8b)\).
Cournot, volume \(2d/3\): \(CS = 2d^2/(9b)\), \(PS = 2d^2/(9b)\), \(W = 4d^2/(9b)\), \(DWL = d^2/(18b)\).
Bertrand, volume \(d\): \(CS = d^2/(2b)\), \(PS = 0\), \(W = d^2/(2b)\), \(DWL = 0\).

Proof

Substitute each volume into Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.5. Monopoly: \(CS = (d/2)^2/(2b) = d^2/(8b)\), \(PS = (d/2)(d/2)/b = d^2/(4b)\), \(W = 3d^2/(8b)\), and \(DWL = (d/2)^2/(2b) = d^2/(8b)\). Cournot: \(CS = (2d/3)^2/(2b) = 2d^2/(9b)\), \(PS = (d/3)(2d/3)/b = 2d^2/(9b)\), \(W = 4d^2/(9b)\), and \(DWL = (d/3)^2/(2b) = d^2/(18b)\). Bertrand: \(CS = d^2/(2b)\), \(PS = (d - d)d/b = 0\), \(W = d^2/(2b)\), and \(DWL = 0\). Each row satisfies \(CS + PS = W\), and each \(DWL = d^2/(2b) - W\).

Example 2.2 (the reference case in cents)

With \((a, b, c) = (120, 1, 20)\), \(d = 100\) and \(d^2/(2b) = 5000\) cents. The monopoly has consumer surplus 1250 cents, producer surplus 2500 cents, total surplus 3750 cents, and deadweight loss 1250 cents. Cournot has consumer surplus \(20000/9\) cents, producer surplus \(20000/9\) cents, total surplus \(40000/9\) cents, and deadweight loss \(5000/9\) cents. The exact value \(5000/9\) cents is about 555.56 cents, and rounding it half up gives 556 cents. Bertrand has consumer surplus 5000 cents, producer surplus zero, total surplus 5000 cents, and deadweight loss zero. The integer-cent values are the exact values rounded half up, and every calculation uses the exact values.

Proposition 2.3 (ordering of the losses and of the shares)

Assume the hypotheses of Lesson 6, Definition 1.1. Then \(0 < DWL_C < DWL_M\), where \(DWL_M = d^2/(8b)\) and \(DWL_C = d^2/(18b)\), and \(DWL_B = 0\). The consumer surplus, producer surplus and total surplus satisfy \(CS_M < CS_C < CS_B\), \(PS_M > PS_C > PS_B = 0\) and \(W_M < W_C < W_B\). The monopoly loss is one quarter of the competitive total surplus \(d^2/(2b)\), and the Cournot loss is one ninth of it.

Proof

Using Corollary 2.1, \(DWL_M - DWL_C = d^2/(8b) - d^2/(18b) = d^2(9 - 4)/(72b) = 5d^2/(72b) > 0\). The consumer surplus values are \(1/8\), \(2/9\) and \(1/2\) times \(d^2/b\), in increasing order, since \(2/9 - 1/8 = 7/72 > 0\) and \(1/2 - 2/9 > 0\). The producer surplus values are \(1/4\), \(2/9\) and \(0\) times \(d^2/b\), since \(1/4 - 2/9 = 1/36 > 0\). The total surplus values are \(3/8\), \(4/9\) and \(1/2\) times \(d^2/b\), with \(4/9 - 3/8 = 5/72 > 0\) and \(1/2 - 4/9 > 0\). The shares are \(DWL_M/W_B = (d^2/(8b))/(d^2/(2b)) = 1/4\) and \(DWL_C/W_B = (d^2/(18b))/(d^2/(2b)) = 1/9\). Each step is an arithmetic comparison of positive fractions.

Proposition 2.3 has an accounting check. Consumer surplus rises by \(7/72\) and producer surplus falls by \(2/72\) when the monopoly is replaced by Cournot competition, both in units of \(d^2/b\). Total surplus rises by \(5/72\), which is the difference of the two.

Lab 1 · Surplus integrals in exact arithmetic and the three losses

Stated before running: for three cases, (1) the midpoint rule with 1, 2, 10 and 97 steps gives the exact integrals of \(P\) and of \(P - c\) over \([0, Q]\), for \(Q = d/2\), \(2d/3\) and \(d\), because both integrands are linear; (2) for the reference case, the consumer surplus, producer surplus, total surplus and deadweight loss of the three structures are the exact values of Example 2.2, and their integer-cent values are the exact values rounded half up; (3) in each of the three cases, the deadweight loss equals \((d - Q)^2/(2b)\), the Bertrand loss is zero, the monopoly loss exceeds the Cournot loss, which is positive, and the monopoly profit exceeds the Cournot profit of the two exchanges together. The lab uses exact Fractions. Each integer-cent value is the exact value rounded half up, once.

"""E1 L07 lab 1: surplus integrals in exact arithmetic, and the deadweight loss of each market structure.

Inputs are illustrative parameters, not data. Trade demand is Q(f) = a - b f units at a fee of f cents per unit, so the
inverse demand is P(Q) = (a - Q) / b cents per unit. Each exchange has a constant marginal cost of c cents per unit and
no fixed cost. The competitive benchmark sets the fee at c, which gives the volume d = a - b c. Three cases are run: the
reference case (a, b, c) = (120, 1, 20) and two further cases. No random draws are used, and no file is read.

Part A: the midpoint rule with n steps is applied to the integral of P over [0, Q] and to the integral of P - c over
[0, Q]. Both integrands are linear, so the midpoint rule is exact for every n. The lab checks equality with the
antiderivatives in exact Fractions for n = 1, 2, 10 and 97, at Q = d/2, 2d/3 and d, and checks that the total surplus
equals (2 d Q - Q^2) / (2 b) at the same volumes.
Part B: for the reference case, each structure's volume, fee, consumer surplus, producer surplus (the exchange profit,
since there is no fixed cost), total surplus and deadweight loss against the competitive total surplus are printed. The
Cournot figures are for the two exchanges together. Each money value is exact, followed by its integer-cent value, which
is the exact value rounded half up.
Part C: for each case, the lab checks that the deadweight loss equals (d - Q)^2 / (2 b) for every structure, that the
Bertrand loss is zero, that the monopoly loss exceeds the Cournot loss, which is positive, and that the monopoly profit
exceeds the Cournot profit of the two exchanges together.

Every comparison is made on exact values. The list of cases is finite, so the lab checks these inputs only. The closed
forms for every input are proved on the page.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from collections.abc import Callable
from fractions import Fraction
from functools import partial

CASES = ((120, 1, 20), (90, 3, 10), (200, 4, 5))  # (a, b, c): demand intercept, demand slope, marginal cost
MIDPOINT_COUNTS = (1, 2, 10, 97)


def inverse_demand(q: Fraction, a: int, b: int) -> Fraction:
    """The fee in cents per unit at which q units trade: P(q) = (a - q) / b."""
    return (a - q) / b


def excess_price(q: Fraction, a: int, b: int, c: int) -> Fraction:
    """The margin P(q) - c in cents per unit at which the q-th unit adds to total surplus."""
    return inverse_demand(q, a, b) - c


def midpoint_integral(f: Callable[[Fraction], Fraction], lower: Fraction, upper: Fraction, n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Midpoint Riemann sum of f over [lower, upper] with n equal steps, in exact arithmetic."""
    step = (upper - lower) / n
    total = Fraction(0)
    for i in range(n):
        total += f(lower + (i + Fraction(1, 2)) * step) * step
    return total


def cents_half_up(x: Fraction) -> int:
    """The exact non-negative value x in whole cents, rounded half up to the nearest cent."""
    if x < 0:
        raise ValueError("cents_half_up is for non-negative values")
    return math.floor(x + Fraction(1, 2))


def total_surplus(q: Fraction, a: int, b: int, c: int) -> Fraction:
    """Total surplus W(q) = integral from 0 to q of (P(x) - c) dx, by the midpoint rule with n = 10."""
    return midpoint_integral(lambda x: excess_price(x, a, b, c), Fraction(0), q, 10)


def consumer_surplus(q: Fraction, fee: Fraction, a: int, b: int) -> Fraction:
    """Consumer surplus: the integral from 0 to q of P(x) dx, minus fee times q, by the midpoint rule with n = 10."""
    return midpoint_integral(lambda x: inverse_demand(x, a, b), Fraction(0), q, 10) - fee * q


def structures(a: int, b: int, c: int) -> dict[str, tuple[Fraction, Fraction]]:
    """Each structure's (volume in units, fee in cents): monopoly, Cournot with two exchanges, and Bertrand."""
    d = Fraction(a - b * c)
    return {
        "monopoly": (d / 2, Fraction(a + b * c, 2 * b)),
        "cournot": (2 * d / 3, Fraction(a + 2 * b * c, 3 * b)),
        "bertrand": (d, Fraction(c)),
    }


def part_a() -> None:
    print("Part A: midpoint sums of linear integrands equal the antiderivatives")
    for a, b, c in CASES:
        d = Fraction(a - b * c)
        for q in (d / 2, 2 * d / 3, d):
            exact_surplus = (2 * d * q - q * q) / (2 * b)
            exact_margin = (a * q - q * q / 2) / b - c * q
            exact_price = (a * q - q * q / 2) / b
            for n in MIDPOINT_COUNTS:
                margin_sum = midpoint_integral(partial(excess_price, a=a, b=b, c=c), Fraction(0), q, n)
                price_sum = midpoint_integral(partial(inverse_demand, a=a, b=b), Fraction(0), q, n)
                if margin_sum != exact_margin or price_sum != exact_price:
                    raise AssertionError(f"midpoint sum differs from the antiderivative at a={a}, b={b}, Q={q}, n={n}")
            if total_surplus(q, a, b, c) != exact_surplus:
                raise AssertionError(f"total surplus differs from (2 d Q - Q^2)/(2 b) at a={a}, b={b}, Q={q}")
        counts = ", ".join(str(n) for n in MIDPOINT_COUNTS)
        print(
            f"  (a, b, c) = ({a}, {b}, {c}), d = {d}: integrals of P and P - c equal the antiderivatives "
            f"for n = {counts} and Q = d/2, 2d/3, d"
        )


def part_b() -> None:
    a, b, c = CASES[0]
    d = Fraction(a - b * c)
    w_star = total_surplus(d, a, b, c)
    print(f"Part B: reference case (a, b, c) = ({a}, {b}, {c}), d = {d}, competitive total surplus {w_star}")
    for name, (q, fee) in structures(a, b, c).items():
        cs = consumer_surplus(q, fee, a, b)
        ps = (fee - c) * q
        w = total_surplus(q, a, b, c)
        dwl = w_star - w
        if cs + ps != w:
            raise AssertionError(f"{name}: consumer plus producer surplus differs from total surplus")
        print(
            f"  {name}: volume {q}, fee {fee}, consumer surplus {cs} ({cents_half_up(cs)} cents), "
            f"producer surplus {ps} ({cents_half_up(ps)} cents), total {w} ({cents_half_up(w)} cents), "
            f"deadweight loss {dwl} ({cents_half_up(dwl)} cents)"
        )


def part_c() -> None:
    print("Part C: deadweight loss, ordering and profit on every case")
    for a, b, c in CASES:
        d = Fraction(a - b * c)
        w_star = total_surplus(d, a, b, c)
        losses: dict[str, Fraction] = {}
        profits: dict[str, Fraction] = {}
        for name, (q, fee) in structures(a, b, c).items():
            dwl = w_star - total_surplus(q, a, b, c)
            if dwl != (d - q) ** 2 / (2 * b):
                raise AssertionError(f"{name} deadweight loss differs from (d - Q)^2 / (2 b) at a={a}, b={b}, c={c}")
            losses[name] = dwl
            profits[name] = (fee - c) * q
        if losses["bertrand"] != 0:
            raise AssertionError("Bertrand deadweight loss is not zero")
        if not losses["monopoly"] > losses["cournot"] > 0:
            raise AssertionError("the ordering monopoly loss > Cournot loss > 0 fails")
        if not profits["monopoly"] > profits["cournot"]:
            raise AssertionError("monopoly profit does not exceed the Cournot profit of the two exchanges")
        share_m = losses["monopoly"] / w_star
        share_c = losses["cournot"] / w_star
        print(
            f"  (a, b, c) = ({a}, {b}, {c}), d = {d}: loss monopoly {losses['monopoly']}, Cournot {losses['cournot']}, "
            f"Bertrand {losses['bertrand']}; loss as a share of the competitive total surplus: monopoly {share_m}, "
            f"Cournot {share_c}; profit monopoly {profits['monopoly']}, Cournot two exchanges together "
            f"{profits['cournot']}"
        )


def main() -> None:
    part_a()
    part_b()
    part_c()

3Where the canon breaks

  • The benchmark is total surplus. Lemma 1.4 shows that the competitive volume maximises total surplus in this model. It says nothing about other measures of welfare, and this lesson does not compute them. Consumer surplus \(Q^2/(2b)\) rises with volume up to \(Q = a\), where the fee is zero, so consumer surplus is largest at the most trade, \(Q = a\).
  • Transfers are not losses. The exchange's profit is part of total surplus, and a transfer from consumers to the exchange raises one part and lowers the other by the same amount. The deadweight loss counts the surplus that no one receives. Measured against the competitive benchmark, the monopoly's profit of 2500 cents is not the loss; the loss is 1250 cents.
  • Linear demand. The exact squares in Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.5 rely on linear inverse demand and on constant marginal cost. This lesson does not derive the loss for any other demand curve or cost function.
  • One fee for every trader. Each exchange charges one fee to all traders, so price discrimination is excluded. Charging different fees to different traders is outside this model, and this lesson does not analyse that case.

4Misconceptions

  1. “The deadweight loss is the profit that the monopoly keeps.”

    The profit is a transfer from consumers, and Theorem 1.2 counts it in total surplus. The loss is \(W(d) - W(Q)\), which is 1250 cents for the reference monopoly, while the monopoly's profit is 2500 cents. The two numbers are different quantities.

  2. “With two exchanges there is no deadweight loss.”

    Cournot competition has a deadweight loss of \(d^2/(18b) > 0\) (Corollary 2.1). Of the three structures, only the Bertrand outcome has a loss of zero, because its volume is \(d\), and the loss is zero only at \(Q = d\) (Lemma 1.4 and Theorem 1.5).

  3. “The competitive outcome maximises consumer surplus.”

    The competitive outcome maximises total surplus. Consumer surplus \(Q^2/(2b)\) is larger at \(Q = a\), where the fee is zero, than at \(Q = d\), because \(a > d\). Its maximiser is not the competitive volume.

Mechanics

M1 · Surplus for new numbers

For \((a, b, c) = (90, 3, 10)\), find the monopoly volume, the consumer surplus, the producer surplus, the total surplus and the deadweight loss.

Solution

\(d = 60\), so the monopoly volume is \(Q = d/2 = 30\), with fee \((a + bc)/(2b) = 120/6 = 20\) cents. Consumer surplus is \(Q^2/(2b) = 900/6 = 150\). Producer surplus is \((d - Q)Q/b = 30 \cdot 30/3 = 300\). Total surplus is \(450\). The competitive total surplus is \(d^2/(2b) = 3600/6 = 600\), so the loss is \(600 - 450 = 150\). This agrees with \(d^2/(8b) = 3600/24 = 150\).

M2 · The Cournot loss

For \((a, b, c) = (90, 3, 10)\), find the Cournot deadweight loss in cents, exact and rounded half up.

Solution

The Cournot volume is \(2d/3 = 40\). The loss is \((d - Q)^2/(2b) = 20^2/6 = 400/6 = 200/3\) cents, which is about 66.67 cents. Rounded half up to a whole cent, it is 67 cents. The check \(d^2/(18b) = 3600/54 = 200/3\) gives the same exact value.

M3 · A midpoint sum with two steps

For the reference case, compute the midpoint sum with two steps of \(\int_0^{50} (120 - x)\,dx\), and check it against the antiderivative.

Solution

The step is \(h = 25\), and the midpoints are \(12.5\) and \(37.5\). The integrand is \(120 - 12.5 = 107.5\) and \(120 - 37.5 = 82.5\). The sum is \((107.5 + 82.5)\cdot 25 = 4750\). The antiderivative gives \(120 \cdot 50 - 50^2/2 = 6000 - 1250 = 4750\). The two agree, as they must for a linear integrand.

P1 · The share of competitive surplus that is lost

Show that for \(0 \le Q \le d\), \(DWL(Q)/W(d) = (1 - Q/d)^2\). Use it to recover the shares in Proposition 2.3.

Solution

By Theorem 1.5 and Lemma 1.4, \(DWL(Q)/W(d) = \frac{(d - Q)^2/(2b)}{d^2/(2b)} = \left(\frac{d - Q}{d}\right)^2 = (1 - Q/d)^2\). The monopoly volume has \(Q/d = 1/2\), so the share is \((1/2)^2 = 1/4\). The Cournot volume has \(Q/d = 2/3\), so the share is \((1/3)^2 = 1/9\).

P2 · The consumer surplus as a triangle

Using only the area of a triangle, show that consumer surplus at volume \(Q\) is \(Q^2/(2b)\) under the definitions of this lesson.

Solution

The region between the inverse demand curve and the fee \(p = P(Q)\), for quantities from 0 to \(Q\), is a right triangle. Its horizontal side is \(Q\), and its vertical side is \(P(0) - P(Q) = a/b - (a - Q)/b = Q/b\). Its area is \(\tfrac12 \cdot Q \cdot Q/b = Q^2/(2b)\), which is \(CS(Q)\) by Definition 1.1.

P3 · The ratio of the two losses

For any \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) with \(a > bc > 0\) and \(b > 0\), find the ratio \(DWL_M / DWL_C\).

Solution

By Corollary 2.1, \(DWL_M = d^2/(8b)\) and \(DWL_C = d^2/(18b)\). The ratio is \(\frac{d^2/(8b)}{d^2/(18b)} = \frac{18}{8} = \frac{9}{4}\), which does not depend on \(a\), \(b\) or \(c\). The monopoly loss is \(9/4\) times the Cournot loss in every case.

Empirical

E1 · Why the Cournot loss prints as 556

Lab 1 prints the Cournot deadweight loss as 556 cents, and the exact value is \(5000/9\). Explain why the printed value is not 555.

Solution

\(5000/9\) cents is about 555.56 cents, with the repeating digit 5 after the decimal point. Rounding half up to a whole cent takes the exact value to the nearest cent, with ties going up. Since the exact value is above 555.5, the nearest cent is 556. The lab rounds the exact fraction once, so the value is not assembled from rounded parts. The difference of the rounded totals, \(5000 - 4444 = 556\), agrees here, but the rule is applied to the exact value.

E2 · The loss shares are the same in every case

Lab 1 prints the same loss shares, 1/4 and 1/9, for all three cases, although \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) differ. Explain why.

Solution

By P1 the share is \((1 - Q/d)^2\). The monopoly volume is always \(Q = d/2\) and the Cournot volume is always \(2d/3\), whatever \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) are, so the ratio \(Q/d\) is \(1/2\) or \(2/3\) in every case. Only the scale \(d^2/b\) changes, and it cancels in the share.

E3 · Why the midpoint rule is exact

Lab 1 checks the midpoint rule with one step as well as with 97 steps. Explain why one step gives the exact integral of a linear function.

Solution

For a linear function \(f\), the value at the midpoint of an interval equals the average of the values at its two ends. With one step on \([0, Q]\), the rule gives \(Q \cdot f(Q/2)\), and \(f(Q/2) = (f(0) + f(Q))/2\). The integral of a linear function over an interval is the length times the average of the end values, so the one-step rule is exact. The same argument applies on each of \(n\) equal pieces, which is why every step count in Lab 1 is exact.

Research

R1 · The volume at half the monopoly loss

Find the volume \(Q\) at which the deadweight loss is half the monopoly loss, and check that it lies between \(d/2\) and \(2d/3\).

Solution

Half the monopoly loss is \(d^2/(16b)\). Setting \((d - Q)^2/(2b) = d^2/(16b)\) gives \((d - Q)^2 = d^2/8\), so \(d - Q = d/(2\sqrt2)\) and \(Q = d(1 - \sqrt2/4)\). Numerically \(\sqrt2/4 \approx 0.35355\), so \(Q \approx 0.64645\, d\). This lies between \(d/2\) and \(2d/3\), since \(2/3 \approx 0.66667\). The Cournot loss is \(4/9\) of the monopoly loss, which is less than one half, so the Cournot volume lies past this point.

R2 · A fee cap at marginal cost

A regulator caps the fee at \(c\). Find the volume and the surplus under the cap, and say what the exchange earns and what the analysis does not cover.

Solution

For a fee \(f \le c\), the exchange's profit is \((f - c)\max(0, a - bf) \le 0\), with equality at \(f = c\). So the exchange sets \(f = c\) and trades \(d\) units, which is the competitive volume. The deadweight loss is zero, consumer surplus is \(d^2/(2b)\), the largest of the three structures, and the exchange earns zero. If the exchange refuses to trade at zero profit, the cap removes the trade, a case this analysis does not cover.

R3 · A regulator who weights consumers

A regulator ranks outcomes by \(W_\lambda = \lambda\,CS + PS\), where \(\lambda \ge 0\) weights consumer surplus. For which \(\lambda\) does Cournot rank above the monopoly?

Solution

In units of \(d^2/b\), \(W_\lambda\) equals \(\lambda/8 + 1/4\) for the monopoly and \(2\lambda/9 + 2/9\) for Cournot. Their difference is \(\lambda(2/9 - 1/8) + (2/9 - 1/4) = 7\lambda/72 - 1/36 = (7\lambda - 2)/72\). Cournot ranks strictly above the monopoly when \(\lambda > 2/7\), ties at \(\lambda = 2/7\), and ranks below for \(\lambda < 2/7\). At \(\lambda = 1\), which is total surplus, Cournot ranks above, as Proposition 2.3 states.
