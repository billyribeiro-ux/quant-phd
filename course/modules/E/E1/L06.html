Oligopoly: Cournot and Bertrand

  • Uses: monopoly pricing, profit maximization, competitive equilibrium, proof by induction
  • Introduces: Cournot competition, Bertrand competition
  • Ttheorem

  • Bpublished, not settled

A market with two exchanges (venues that each charge a fee per unit traded) is neither a monopoly nor a competitive market, and its outcome depends on what the exchanges choose. If they choose trade volumes, the outcome lies between the monopoly outcome and the competitive outcome, which is the outcome in which the fee equals marginal cost. If they choose fees, it is the competitive outcome. This lesson derives both outcomes for one demand curve and one cost, proves them, and checks them on exact grids. Lesson 7 turns the outcomes into surplus and deadweight loss.

1The model and the monopoly benchmark

This lesson covers one monopoly, two exchanges that compete on trade volume, and two exchanges that compete on fee. It does not cover entry, product differentiation, or more than two exchanges, except in research problem R1. Every exchange in the lesson faces the same demand and has the same cost.

Definition 1.1 (the market)

Let \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) be real numbers with \(b > 0\), \(c > 0\) and \(a > bc\). Write \(d = a - bc\), so \(d > 0\). A unit is one trade. At a fee of \(f\) cents per unit, the trade demand is \(Q(f) = \max(0, a - bf)\) units. The fee at which a total volume \(Q\) trades is the inverse demand \(f(Q) = (a - Q)/b\) cents per unit, for every \(Q \ge 0\). This is negative when \(Q > a\). The proofs below do not depend on the sign of the fee, and they apply the formula at every \(Q \ge 0\).

Definition 1.2 (exchange profit)

An exchange that trades \(q\) units at fee \(f\) earns profit \(\pi = (f - c)\,q\) in cents. Each unit traded costs \(c\) cents to the exchange (its marginal cost), and there is no fixed cost. An exchange chooses its trade volume to make this profit as large as possible, as in profit maximization in Lesson 2.

Lemma 1.3 (the margin identity)

Assume the hypotheses of Definition 1.1. For every total volume \(Q \ge 0\), \(f(Q) - c = (d - Q)/b\). So an exchange that trades \(q\) units while the total volume is \(Q\) earns \(q\,(d - Q)/b\) cents.

Proof

\(f(Q) - c = (a - Q)/b - c = (a - Q - bc)/b = (d - Q)/b\), using \(d = a - bc\). Multiplying by \(q\) gives the profit, by Definition 1.2.

Lemma 1.3 is the identity the rest of the lesson uses. The margin over cost falls by \(1/b\) cent for each extra unit traded, and it is zero at the competitive volume \(d = a - bc\), where the fee equals marginal cost \(c\).

Proposition 1.4 (the monopoly benchmark)

Assume the hypotheses of Definition 1.1. A single exchange that chooses its total volume \(Q \ge 0\) earns \(\pi(Q) = Q\,(d - Q)/b\). Its unique maximiser is \(Q_m = d/2\), at fee \(f(d/2) = (a + bc)/(2b)\), and its maximum profit is \(d^2/(4b)\). This is the monopoly outcome of Lesson 5 (monopoly pricing).

Proof

By Lemma 1.3, \(\pi(Q) = Q(d - Q)/b\). Expanding the square gives \(d^2/4 - (Q - d/2)^2 = dQ - Q^2\), so \(Q(d - Q) = d^2/4 - (Q - d/2)^2\). This is largest over \(Q \ge 0\) only at \(Q = d/2\), which is nonnegative, and it equals \(d^2/4\) there. At \(Q = d/2\), \(f = (a - d/2)/b = (a + bc)/(2b)\), and \(\pi = (d/2)(d/2)/b = d^2/(4b)\).

Example 1.5 (the reference case)

Take \((a, b, c) = (120, 1, 20)\), so \(d = 100\). The monopoly trades \(50\) units at a fee of \(70\) cents, and earns \(2500\) cents. Every number in this example is exact, and the cent values are integers, so no rounding is needed.

2Cournot competition: volumes

Definition 2.1 (Cournot competition, two exchanges)

Cournot competition is the following simultaneous choice. Each of two exchanges chooses a trade volume \(q_i \ge 0\), without seeing the other's choice. The total volume is \(Q = q_1 + q_2\), the fee is \(f(Q)\), and exchange \(i\) earns \(\pi_i = q_i\,(d - q_1 - q_2)/b\) by Lemma 1.3.

Definition 2.2 (Cournot equilibrium)

A pair \((q_1^*, q_2^*)\) of volumes is a Cournot equilibrium when each exchange's volume maximises its own profit, given the other exchange's volume. In words: no exchange can raise its profit by changing its own volume while the other keeps its volume.

Definition 2.3 (reaction function)

For a rival volume \(x \ge 0\), the reaction function \(R(x)\) is the volume \(q \ge 0\) that maximises \(q\,(d - q - x)/b\), the profit of an exchange that faces the rival volume \(x\).

Lemma 2.4 (the reaction function)

Assume the hypotheses of Definition 1.1. For every \(x \ge 0\), the maximiser is unique and equals \(R(x) = \max\bigl(0,\ (d - x)/2\bigr)\).

Proof

Let \(D = d - x\) and \(g(q) = q\,(D - q)/b\) for \(q \ge 0\). If \(D \le 0\), then for every \(q > 0\) we have \(D - q < 0\), so \(g(q) < 0 = g(0)\), and the unique maximiser is \(q = 0\). If \(D > 0\), then \(q(D - q) = D^2/4 - (q - D/2)^2\), which is largest only at \(q = D/2\), a nonnegative number. So \(R(x) = D/2\) when \(D > 0\), and \(R(x) = 0\) otherwise, which is \(\max(0, D/2)\) in both cases.

Theorem 2.5 (the Cournot equilibrium)

Assume the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, and the Cournot game of Definition 2.1. There is exactly one Cournot equilibrium, \((q^*, q^*)\) with \(q^* = d/3\). At it, the total volume is \(2d/3\), the fee is \((a + 2bc)/(3b)\), and each exchange earns \(d^2/(9b)\).

Proof

Let \((q_1, q_2)\) be a Cournot equilibrium. By Definition 2.2 and Lemma 2.4, \(q_1 = R(q_2)\) and \(q_2 = R(q_1)\). Suppose \(q_1 = 0\). Then \(q_2 = R(0) = d/2\), by Lemma 2.4 with \(d > 0\). But then \(q_1 = R(d/2) = d/4 > 0\), a contradiction. So \(q_1 > 0\), and by the same argument with the roles exchanged, \(q_2 > 0\). Since \(R(q_2) = q_1 > 0\), Lemma 2.4 gives \(q_1 = (d - q_2)/2\), and likewise \(q_2 = (d - q_1)/2\). Subtracting the second equation from the first gives \(q_1 - q_2 = (q_1 - q_2)/2\), so \(q_1 = q_2\). Then \(q_1 = (d - q_1)/2\) gives \(q_1 = d/3\). This is the only solution.

Conversely, \(R(d/3) = (d - d/3)/2 = d/3\), so \((d/3, d/3)\) is an equilibrium. The total volume is \(2d/3\), so the fee is \(f(2d/3) = (a - 2d/3)/b = (a + 2bc)/(3b)\). By Lemma 1.3 the margin is \((d - 2d/3)/b = d/(3b)\), so each exchange earns \((d/3)\cdot d/(3b) = d^2/(9b)\).

Remark 2.6 (the slope of the reaction function)

The reaction function \(R\) has slope \(-1/2\) wherever \(R(x) > 0\). If the rival adds one unit, an exchange's best response falls by half a unit. Because that slope has magnitude \(1/2 < 1\), the iteration in Proposition 2.8 converges.

Compared with the monopoly, the two exchanges together trade more: \(2d/3\) against \(d/2\). Each trades \(d/3\), which is less than the monopoly volume, and the fee falls from \((a + bc)/(2b)\) to \((a + 2bc)/(3b)\). Proposition 4.1 gives the full ordering.

Example 2.7 (the reference case, Cournot)

With \((a, b, c) = (120, 1, 20)\), the Cournot equilibrium has each exchange trading \(100/3\) units. The total volume is \(200/3\), the fee is \(160/3\) cents, and each exchange earns \(10000/9\) cents, which is about \(1111.11\) cents. Rounding that exact value half up gives \(1111\) cents. Later steps use the exact value.

Proposition 2.8 (the best-response iteration from zero)

Assume the hypotheses of Definition 1.1. Let \(x_0 = 0\) and \(x_{t+1} = R(x_t)\) for each integer \(t \ge 0\). This describes both exchanges moving at once to the other's previous volume, which is the same volume for both, since both start at zero and use the same reaction function. Then for every integer \(t \ge 0\), \[ x_t = \frac{d}{3}\left(1 - \left(-\tfrac12\right)^t\right), \qquad \left|x_t - \frac{d}{3}\right| = \frac{d}{3}\left(\tfrac12\right)^t. \]

Proof

Write \(r = -1/2\) and let \(P(t)\) be the statement \(x_t = (d/3)(1 - r^t)\). The base case \(P(0)\) holds, because \(x_0 = 0 = (d/3)(1 - 1)\). For the inductive step, assume \(P(t)\). Since \(|r| = 1/2\), we have \(r^t \in [-1/2, 1]\) for every integer \(t \ge 0\), so \(x_t \in [0, d/2]\). Then \(d - x_t \ge d/2 > 0\), and Lemma 2.4 gives \(x_{t+1} = (d - x_t)/2\). Substituting \(P(t)\), \[ x_{t+1} = \frac{d}{2}\left(1 - \frac{1}{3}\bigl(1 - r^t\bigr)\right) = \frac{d}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3} + \frac{r^t}{3}\right) = \frac{d}{3}\left(1 + \frac{r^t}{2}\right). \] Since \(r^{t+1} = -r^t/2\), the factor \(1 + r^t/2\) equals \(1 - r^{t+1}\), which is \(P(t+1)\). By induction, \(P(t)\) holds for every integer \(t \ge 0\). The error is \(x_t - d/3 = -(d/3)r^t\), whose absolute value is \((d/3)(1/2)^t\).

Proposition 2.8 says the error halves at every step, and the limit is the fixed point \(d/3\) of Theorem 2.5. The proof uses only the reaction function, so the convergence is a consequence of Lemma 2.4 and not an assumption.

3Bertrand competition: fees

Definition 3.1 (Bertrand competition, two exchanges)

Bertrand competition is the following simultaneous choice. Each of two exchanges sets a fee \(f_i \ge 0\). The market fee is the lower of the two fees, \(f = \min(f_1, f_2)\), and the total trade volume is \(\max(0, a - bf)\). If \(f_i < f_j\), exchange \(i\) trades the whole volume, and exchange \(j\) trades nothing. If \(f_1 = f_2 = f\), the same total volume is split with shares \(s_1, s_2 \ge 0\), \(s_1 + s_2 = 1\), chosen by a tie rule. Theorem 3.3 holds for every tie rule. Exchange \(i\) earns \(\pi_i = v_i\,(f_i - c)\), where \(v_i\) is its trade volume.

Lemma 3.2 (the undercut profit)

Assume the hypotheses of Definition 1.1. Write \(h(g) = (g - c)(a - bg)\), the profit of an exchange that takes the whole demand at fee \(g\). Then \(h(g) > 0\) for every \(g\) with \(c < g < a/b\), and \(a/b > c\).

Proof

Since \(a > bc\) and \(b > 0\), \(a/b > c\). For \(c < g < a/b\), both \(g - c\) and \(a - bg\) are positive, so their product is positive.

Theorem 3.3 (the Bertrand equilibrium)

Assume the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, and the Bertrand game of Definition 3.1 with any tie rule. The unique Bertrand equilibrium is the fee pair \((f_1, f_2) = (c, c)\), at which neither exchange can raise its profit by changing its own fee while the other keeps its fee. At it, the total volume is \(d\), each exchange's profit is zero, and the split of \(d\) between the two exchanges depends on the tie rule.

Proof

Step 1: \((c, c)\) is an equilibrium. Both profits are zero. If an exchange moves to a fee \(f < c\), it has the lower fee and trades \(\max(0, a - bf) \ge 0\) units at a negative margin, so its profit is at most zero. If it moves to \(f > c\), the rival's fee \(c\) is lower, so it trades nothing and earns zero. A move to \(f = c\) also earns zero. No move is strictly better.

Step 2: no equilibrium has a fee below \(c\). Suppose an equilibrium has lowest fee \(m < c\). Then \(a - bm > 0\), since \(m < c < a/b\). The total volume at \(m\) is positive, so some exchange \(i\) with fee \(m\) has a share \(v_i > 0\). Its profit is \(v_i(m - c) < 0\). If it moves to the fee \(c\), its margin is zero, so its profit is zero whatever its volume. That is strictly better, a contradiction.

Step 3: no equilibrium has its lowest fee above \(c\). Suppose the lowest fee is \(m > c\), held by exchange \(i\). Choose any \(g\) with \(c < g < \min(m, a/b)\). This interval is nonempty, by Lemma 3.2.

Case A: the other exchange \(j\) has fee above \(m\). Then \(j\) trades nothing and earns zero. If \(j\) moves to \(g < m\), it has the lowest fee and trades \(a - bg > 0\) units, so its profit is \(h(g) > 0\) by Lemma 3.2. This is strictly better, a contradiction.

Case B: both fees equal \(m\). The shares sum to one, so some exchange \(i\) has \(s_i < 1\), and its profit is \(s_i H\), where \(H = h(m)\) if \(m < a/b\) and \(H = 0\) if \(m \ge a/b\). If \(m \ge a/b\), exchange \(i\) moves to any \(g\) in \((c, a/b)\), takes the whole demand, and earns \(h(g) > 0 = s_i H\). If \(m < a/b\), then \(H = h(m) > 0\) and \(s_i H < H\). The function \(h\) is continuous, so some \(g\) in \((c, m)\) has \(h(g) > s_i H\). Moving to that \(g\) is strictly better, a contradiction.

Step 4: the other fee is \(c\). By Steps 2 and 3 the lowest fee is \(c\). Let \(k\) be the other exchange, with fee \(f_k \ge c\). If \(f_k > c\), the exchange at \(c\) takes the whole demand \(d\) and earns zero. If it moves to any \(g\) with \(c < g < \min(f_k, a/b)\), its fee is still the lower one, so it takes the whole demand and earns \(h(g) > 0\). So \(f_k\) must equal \(c\). Step 1 shows that \((c, c)\) is an equilibrium, which completes the proof.

The Bertrand outcome is the price-taking benchmark for this demand and cost: the fee equals marginal cost \(c\), and the volume is \(a - bc = d\). It is not an application of the competitive equilibrium of Lesson 3, which is derived for a cost with a quadratic term, \(k > 0\), that this lesson does not have.

Remark 3.4 (why undercutting pays above marginal cost)

Undercutting pays when the cheaper fee still earns a margin. Lemma 3.2 says that a fee strictly between \(c\) and \(a/b\) earns a positive profit when it takes the whole demand. The proof of Step 3 turns this into a profitable move for any rival fee above \(c\). Sharing the market at a rival fee \(r\), with an even split of the volume (the tie rule Lab 1 uses), gives this exchange half the volume \(a - br\) at margin \(r - c\), so it earns \((r - c)(a - br)/2 = h(r)/2\). An undercut that takes the whole demand just below \(r\) earns \(h(r)\) in the limit, which is twice the sharing profit. Lab 1, Part C, shows the size of the gain at four rival fees for each of three cases.

Lab 1 · The reaction function, its iteration, and the undercut on exact grids

Stated before running: for three cases with \(a > bc\), (1) the grid maximiser of an exchange's Cournot profit, over outputs in steps of \(1/12\) unit, equals the reaction function of Lemma 2.4 for each rival output tested; (2) the symmetric iteration from zero output matches the closed form of Proposition 2.8 at each of 30 steps, exactly; (3) with the rival's fee at marginal cost, the largest profit on a grid of fees in steps of \(1/10\) cent is zero, and the lab counts the grid fees that attain it and prints whether all of them lie at or above \(c\); (4) for four rival fees above \(c\), undercutting by \(1/1000\) cent earns more than sharing. The lab does not prove these for all inputs. The proofs in Sections 2 and 3 do that.

"""E1 L06 lab 1: Cournot best responses, their iteration, and the Bertrand fee, on exact grids.

Inputs are illustrative parameters, not data. Trade demand is Q(f) = a - b f units at a fee of f cents per unit, and
each exchange has a constant marginal cost of c cents per unit. Write d = a - b c, which is the volume at which the fee
equals marginal cost. Three cases are run: the reference case (a, b, c) = (120, 1, 20) and two further cases with the
same structure. No random draws are used, and no file is read.

Part A: the Cournot best response max(0, (d - q_j) / 2) is compared with the output that maximises an exchange's profit
q_i (d - q_i - q_j) / b over the grid of outputs i / 12 units, for each rival output q_j in a fixed list that does not
exceed d. Profit is in cents. The lab asserts that the closed form lies on the grid for each rival output tested, and
that the grid maximiser equals it; the printed counts are the rival outputs that passed both assertions.
Part B: simultaneous best responses are iterated from zero output for 30 steps, in exact Fractions. The lab checks that
the symmetric iterate equals (d / 3) (1 - (-1/2)^t) at every step t, and prints the first step at which the iterate is
within 10^-6 units of d / 3.
Part C: with the rival's fee at marginal cost, the lab scans fees in steps of 1/10 cent from 0 to a / b and counts the
fees that earn the largest profit. For four rival fees above marginal cost, it compares the profit from undercutting by
1/1000 cent with the profit from sharing the market at the rival's fee.

Every comparison is made on exact values. The grids are finite, so the lab checks these inputs only. The proofs that
cover all inputs are on the page.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from decimal import Decimal, localcontext
from fractions import Fraction

CASES = ((120, 1, 20), (90, 3, 10), (200, 4, 5))  # (a, b, c): demand intercept, demand slope, marginal cost
RIVAL_OUTPUT_FRACTIONS = (Fraction(0), Fraction(10), Fraction(25), Fraction(100, 3), Fraction(50), Fraction(100))
OUTPUT_STEP = Fraction(1, 12)
FEE_STEP = Fraction(1, 10)
UNDERCUT = Fraction(1, 1000)
ITERATIONS = 30
TOLERANCE = Fraction(1, 10**6)


def margin_gap(a: int, b: int, c: int) -> Fraction:
    """d = a - b c in units: the volume at which the fee a/b - Q/b equals the marginal cost c."""
    return Fraction(a - b * c)


def cournot_profit(q_own: Fraction, q_rival: Fraction, b: int, d: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Profit in cents of an exchange trading q_own units against a rival trading q_rival units: q_own (d - Q) / b."""
    return q_own * (d - q_own - q_rival) / b


def best_response(q_rival: Fraction, d: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The closed-form Cournot best response max(0, (d - q_rival) / 2) in units."""
    return max(Fraction(0), (d - q_rival) / 2)


def grid_best_response(q_rival: Fraction, b: int, d: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The output on the grid i / 12, for i from 0 to 12 d, with the largest profit against q_rival."""
    best_q, best_pi = Fraction(0), None
    for i in range(int(d / OUTPUT_STEP) + 1):
        q = i * OUTPUT_STEP
        pi = cournot_profit(q, q_rival, b, d)
        if best_pi is None or pi > best_pi:
            best_q, best_pi = q, pi
    return best_q


def bertrand_profit(f_own: Fraction, f_rival: Fraction, a: int, b: int, c: int) -> Fraction:
    """Profit in cents at fee f_own. The lower fee takes the demand a - b f units; a tie splits it evenly."""
    demand = max(Fraction(0), a - b * f_own)
    if f_own < f_rival:
        volume = demand
    elif f_own == f_rival:
        volume = demand / 2
    else:
        volume = Fraction(0)
    return volume * (f_own - c)


def decimal_text(x: Fraction) -> str:
    """The value of x as a decimal at 60 significant digits. It is checked to be exact, so no value is rounded."""
    with localcontext() as ctx:
        ctx.prec = 60
        text = format(Decimal(x.numerator) / Decimal(x.denominator), "f")
    if Fraction(text) != x:
        raise AssertionError(f"{x} is not exact at 60 significant digits")
    return text


def part_a() -> None:
    print("Part A: Cournot best response against the grid maximiser of profit")
    for a, b, c in CASES:
        d = margin_gap(a, b, c)
        checked = 0
        for q_rival in RIVAL_OUTPUT_FRACTIONS:
            if q_rival > d:
                continue
            closed = best_response(q_rival, d)
            on_grid = (closed / OUTPUT_STEP).denominator == 1
            grid = grid_best_response(q_rival, b, d)
            if not on_grid or grid != closed:
                raise AssertionError(f"best response mismatch at a={a}, b={b}, c={c}, rival {q_rival}")
            checked += 1
        print(f"  (a, b, c) = ({a}, {b}, {c}), d = {d}: {checked} rival outputs, grid equals closed form at each")


def part_b() -> None:
    print("Part B: simultaneous best responses from zero output")
    for a, b, c in CASES:
        d = margin_gap(a, b, c)
        q = Fraction(0)
        first_within = None
        for t in range(ITERATIONS + 1):
            if q != d / 3 * (1 - Fraction(-1, 2) ** t):
                raise AssertionError(f"iterate differs from the closed form at step {t}")
            if first_within is None and abs(q - d / 3) < TOLERANCE:
                first_within = t
            q = best_response(q, d)
        print(
            f"  (a, b, c) = ({a}, {b}, {c}): {ITERATIONS} steps match (d/3)(1 - (-1/2)^t) exactly; "
            f"first step within 10^-6 of d/3 is {first_within}"
        )
    d = margin_gap(120, 1, 20)
    q_last = Fraction(0)
    for _ in range(ITERATIONS):
        q_last = best_response(q_last, d)
    print(f"reference case after {ITERATIONS} steps: gap to d/3 = {q_last - d / 3}")


def part_c() -> None:
    print("Part C: Bertrand fees on a grid of 1/10 cent, and undercutting above marginal cost")
    for a, b, c in CASES:
        top = Fraction(a, b)
        fees = [k * FEE_STEP for k in range(int(top / FEE_STEP) + 1)]
        profits = [bertrand_profit(f, Fraction(c), a, b, c) for f in fees]
        best = max(profits)
        winners = [f for f, pi in zip(fees, profits, strict=True) if pi == best]
        below = [pi for f, pi in zip(fees, profits, strict=True) if f < c]
        all_at_or_above = all(f >= c for f in winners)
        print(
            f"  (a, b, c) = ({a}, {b}, {c}): rival at c; best own profit {decimal_text(best)} cents, attained at "
            f"{len(winners)} of {len(fees)} grid fees, all at or above c: {all_at_or_above}; "
            f"largest profit below c: {decimal_text(max(below))} cents"
        )
        for share in (Fraction(1, 20), Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(3, 4)):
            rival = c + (top - c) * share
            undercut = rival - UNDERCUT
            undercut_profit = bertrand_profit(undercut, rival, a, b, c)
            shared_profit = bertrand_profit(rival, rival, a, b, c)
            print(
                f"    rival fee {decimal_text(rival)}: undercut by 1/1000 earns {decimal_text(undercut_profit)} cents, "
                f"sharing earns {decimal_text(shared_profit)} cents, undercut is larger: "
                f"{undercut_profit > shared_profit}"
            )


def main() -> None:
    part_a()
    part_b()
    part_c()

4Comparing the outcomes

Proposition 4.1 (the ordering of the three structures)

Assume the hypotheses of Definition 1.1. Let \(Q_m\), \(Q_C\) and \(Q_B\) be the total volumes under monopoly, Cournot and Bertrand competition, and let \(f_m\), \(f_C\) and \(f_B\) be the fees. Let \(\Pi_m\) and \(\Pi_C\) be the industry profits, where \(\Pi_C\) is the profit of the two Cournot exchanges together, and \(\Pi_B\) the Bertrand industry profit. Then \[ Q_m = \frac{d}{2} < Q_C = \frac{2d}{3} < Q_B = d, \qquad f_m = \frac{a + bc}{2b} > f_C = \frac{a + 2bc}{3b} > f_B = c, \qquad \Pi_m = \frac{d^2}{4b} > \Pi_C = \frac{2d^2}{9b} > \Pi_B = 0. \] The differences are \(Q_C - Q_m = d/6\), \(Q_B - Q_C = d/3\), \(f_m - f_C = d/(6b)\), \(f_C - f_B = d/(3b)\), \(\Pi_m - \Pi_C = d^2/(36b)\), and \(\Pi_C - \Pi_B = 2d^2/(9b)\).

Proof

The volumes are \(d/2\) (Proposition 1.4), \(2d/3\) (Theorem 2.5) and \(d\) (Theorem 3.3), and \(d > 0\) gives the strict inequalities, with \(2d/3 - d/2 = d/6\) and \(d - 2d/3 = d/3\). The fees are \((a + bc)/(2b)\), \((a + 2bc)/(3b)\) and \(c\). Their differences are \(\frac{3(a + bc) - 2(a + 2bc)}{6b} = \frac{a - bc}{6b} = \frac{d}{6b}\) and \(\frac{a + 2bc - 3bc}{3b} = \frac{d}{3b}\), both positive. The profits are \(d^2/(4b)\), \(2d^2/(9b)\) (two exchanges, each \(d^2/(9b)\)) and zero (Theorem 3.3). Their difference is \(\frac{d^2}{4b} - \frac{2d^2}{9b} = \frac{9d^2 - 8d^2}{36b} = \frac{d^2}{36b}\).

5Where the canon breaks

  • Volume or fee as the choice. Cournot treats the trade volume as the strategic choice, and Bertrand treats the fee. Proposition 4.1 shows that the two give different outcomes for the same market. The lesson does not decide which model fits a real exchange. That question needs evidence this lesson does not contain.
  • What the Bertrand result uses. The proof of Theorem 3.3 relies on the model's assumptions: the two exchanges trade identical units, the lower fee takes the whole demand, no exchange faces a capacity limit, and the choice is one simultaneous decision. The proof does not cover a capacity limit, and the lesson does not analyse one.
  • Negative fees in Cournot. The inverse demand \(f(Q) = (a - Q)/b\) is negative when \(Q > a\). The Cournot proofs do not use the sign of the fee. The lesson does not model a fee floor.
  • Two exchanges only. Every result in Sections 2 to 4 concerns two exchanges, and the monopoly. Research problem R1 gives the Cournot volume for \(n\) exchanges, and this lesson does not treat Bertrand competition with more than two exchanges.

6Misconceptions

  1. “Two exchanges compete their way to the competitive outcome.”

    That holds for Bertrand competition (Theorem 3.3), and it fails for Cournot competition. Under Cournot competition the total volume is \(2d/3\), which is less than \(d\), and the fee is \((a + 2bc)/(3b)\), which is above \(c\). Each exchange earns \(d^2/(9b) > 0\) (Theorem 2.5).

  2. “A Cournot exchange trades the monopoly volume.”

    The monopoly volume \(d/2\) is the reaction only to a rival that trades nothing, since \(R(0) = d/2\). For a rival volume \(x\) at most \(d\), the reaction is \((d - x)/2\), which falls by half a unit for each unit the rival adds, and it is 0 for \(x \ge d\) (Lemma 2.4).

  3. “A tiny undercut is not worth making, because it lowers the fee by a tiny amount.”

    The undercut moves the whole market to the lower fee, not only a small part of it. The profit from taking the whole demand at \(g\) is \(h(g)\) (Lemma 3.2), and as \(g\) approaches a rival fee \(r\) from below, \(h(g)\) approaches \(h(r)\), which is twice the profit from sharing at \(r\) with an even split (Remark 3.4). Lab 1, Part C, shows this at four rival fees for each case.

7Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · A reaction function

Take \((a, b, c) = (90, 3, 10)\), so \(d = 60\). Find the Cournot reaction when the rival trades \(x = 24\) units, and when it trades \(x = 70\) units.

Solution

Lemma 2.4 gives \(R(x) = \max(0, (d - x)/2)\). For \(x = 24\), \(R = (60 - 24)/2 = 18\) units. For \(x = 70\), \(d - x = -10 < 0\), so \(R = 0\): the exchange trades nothing against a rival trading 70 units.

M2 · The Cournot equilibrium for new numbers

For \((a, b, c) = (90, 3, 10)\), find the equilibrium volume of each exchange, the total volume, the fee, and the profit of each exchange.

Solution

\(q^* = d/3 = 20\) units each, total \(40\) units. The fee is \((a + 2bc)/(3b) = (90 + 60)/9 = 50/3\) cents, which is about 16.67 cents. The margin is \(f - c = 50/3 - 10 = 20/3\), and each exchange earns \(20 \cdot 20/3 = 400/3\) cents. Check with Lemma 1.3: \(q(d - Q)/b = 20 \cdot (60 - 40)/3 = 400/3\). Both agree.

M3 · The Bertrand outcome for new numbers

For \((a, b, c) = (90, 3, 10)\), give the Bertrand equilibrium fee, the total trade volume, and the profit of each exchange.

Solution

By Theorem 3.3 the fee is \(c = 10\) cents. The total volume is \(d = a - bc = 90 - 30 = 60\) units, which is the demand at the fee \(c\), since \(a - bc = 90 - 3 \cdot 10 = 60\). Each exchange earns zero, because its margin is \(c - c = 0\). The split of the 60 units depends on the tie rule.

Proof

P1 · Monopoly profit against Cournot industry profit

Show that for every \(d > 0\) and \(b > 0\), the monopoly profit \(d^2/(4b)\) exceeds the Cournot industry profit \(2d^2/(9b)\).

Solution

\(\frac{d^2}{4b} - \frac{2d^2}{9b} = \frac{d^2}{b}\left(\frac14 - \frac29\right) = \frac{d^2}{b}\cdot\frac{9 - 8}{36} = \frac{d^2}{36b}\). Since \(d > 0\) and \(b > 0\), this is positive. This is the difference stated in Proposition 4.1.

P2 · A fee pair above marginal cost is not an equilibrium

Let \(0 < \delta < (a - bc)/b\). Show that the fee pair \((c, c + \delta)\) is not a Bertrand equilibrium.

Solution

The exchange at \(c\) has the lower fee and trades the whole demand, but its margin is zero. Let \(g = c + \delta/2\). Then \(c < g < c + \delta < a/b\), because \(c + \delta < a/b\) is equivalent to \(\delta < (a - bc)/b\). If the exchange at \(c\) moves to \(g\), it still has the lower fee, since \(g < c + \delta\). It then trades \(a - bg > 0\) units at margin \(g - c > 0\), so its profit is \(h(g) > 0\), by Lemma 3.2. Its profit before was zero, so the move is strictly better, and the pair is not an equilibrium.

P3 · The Cournot total volume lies between the benchmarks

For \(d > 0\), show that \(d/2 < 2d/3 < d\), and find the differences.

Solution

\(2d/3 - d/2 = (4d - 3d)/6 = d/6 > 0\), and \(d - 2d/3 = d/3 > 0\). So \(d/2 < 2d/3 < d\). The differences are \(d/6\) and \(d/3\), the volume differences in Proposition 4.1.

Empirical

E1 · Predict the convergence step

Before running Lab 1, predict the first step \(t\) at which the iterate of Proposition 2.8 is within \(10^{-6}\) of \(d/3\) in the reference case. Then compare with the lab output.

Solution

The error after \(t\) steps is \((d/3)(1/2)^t = (100/3)\cdot 2^{-t}\). This is below \(10^{-6}\) when \(2^t > (100/3) \cdot 10^6 \approx 3.33 \times 10^7\). Since \(2^{24} \approx 1.68 \times 10^7\) is too small and \(2^{25} \approx 3.36 \times 10^7\) is large enough, \(t = 25\). Lab 1 prints 25 for the reference case.

E2 · Count the fees that earn zero

Lab 1 reports that 1001 of the 1201 grid fees earn zero profit against a rival at \(c = 20\). Explain the count from the grid.

Solution

The grid runs in steps of \(1/10\) cent from 0 to \(a/b = 120\), which gives 1201 fees. A fee \(f\) earns zero when \(f \ge 20\), because at \(f = 20\) the margin is zero and above \(20\) the exchange trades nothing. The fees from 20 to 120 in steps of \(1/10\) number \((120 - 20) \cdot 10 + 1 = 1001\). Below 20 the profit is negative.

E3 · Undercut against sharing at a rival fee of 45

In the reference case, a rival charges 45 cents. Compute the profit from sharing at 45 and the profit from undercutting to just below 45. Compare with Lab 1.

Solution

Sharing at 45 gives the rival and this exchange half of the demand each: the volume is \(a - 45 = 75\) units in total, so the exchange trades 37.5 units at a margin of \(25\) cents, earning \(937.5\) cents. Undercutting to just below 45 takes all 75 units at a margin of about 25 cents, earning about \(1875\) cents. The ratio is about two, which matches Remark 3.4 and the lab output of \(1874.95\) against \(937.5\) at this rival fee.

Research

R1 · Cournot with n exchanges

Let \(n \ge 2\) exchanges choose volumes simultaneously as in Definition 2.1, with the profit \(q_i(d - Q)/b\) and total volume \(Q\). Find the symmetric Cournot volume per exchange and the total volume, and show that the total approaches \(d\) as \(n\) grows.

Solution

Let \(Q_{-i}\) be the volume of the other \(n - 1\) exchanges. Exchange \(i\) earns \(q_i(d - q_i - Q_{-i})/b\), a quadratic in \(q_i\) with the same form as in Lemma 2.4 with \(x = Q_{-i}\). Its best response is \(\max(0, (d - Q_{-i})/2)\), by the same completing-the-square argument. In a symmetric equilibrium each exchange trades \(q\), so \(Q_{-i} = (n - 1)q\), and \(q = (d - (n-1)q)/2\) when this is positive. Then \(2q = d - (n-1)q\), so \((n+1)q = d\) and \(q = d/(n+1)\). The total volume is \(nd/(n+1)\). For \(n = 2\) this is \(2d/3\), as in Theorem 2.5. Since \(nd/(n+1) = d - d/(n+1)\), the total approaches \(d\) as \(n\) grows.

R2 · Where the tie rule enters the proof

Identify each step of the proof of Theorem 3.3 that depends on the tie rule, and say whether the conclusion changes for a rule that gives one exchange the whole tied volume.

Solution

The tie rule enters Step 2 and Case B of Step 3. Step 2 uses only that some exchange at the lowest fee has a positive share, which holds because the shares sum to one. Case B uses that some share is below one, which also holds for every rule with \(s_1 + s_2 = 1\). Under a rule that gives exchange \(i\) the whole tied volume, exchange \(j\) has share zero and profit zero at a tied fee \(m > c\). Exchange \(j\) moves to any \(g\) in \((c, \min(m, a/b))\) and takes the whole demand, so it earns \(h(g) > 0\), and the case is still excluded. The total volume and the profit at \((c, c)\) do not depend on the rule, because the margin there is zero.

R3 · Commitment and the two models

Argue which of the two games, Cournot or Bertrand, makes the more natural assumption about commitment when an exchange sets a per-unit fee that it can change in minutes. Your argument must rest on the models' assumptions and not on data.

Solution

In Definition 2.1 the choice is a volume, and the fee adjusts to clear it. That treats the volume as the variable that is hard to change, which suits capacity or a committed trading book. In Definition 3.1 the choice is the fee itself, which the exchange can change at once, so the fee is the strategic variable. A fee that can be changed at will matches the Bertrand assumption. The lesson draws no conclusion about a real exchange, because it contains no data on how fast any exchange changes its fee.
