Lesson 1 defined the best bundle for one consumer. Here two consumers hold fixed totals of two goods, and the question is whether any reshuffle of those totals can make one of them better off without making the other worse off. Section 1 defines the holdings for which no such reshuffle exists (Definition 1.3). For the product utilities used in this lesson, the holdings of this kind in which each consumer keeps some of each good are exactly the points of one straight line, strictly between its ends (0, 0) and (X, Y). The lesson proves this in both directions. Section 3 shows how far a finite check of allocations reaches, and where it stops.

1Setup: two consumers and fixed totals

Two consumers, A and B, consume two goods, x and y. The economy has totals \(X > 0\) of good x and \(Y > 0\) of good y, and every unit is held by one of the two consumers. An allocation is the bundle that A holds, \((x, y)\). Then B holds \((X - x, Y - y)\).

Definition 1.1 (feasible allocation)

An allocation \((x, y)\) is a feasible allocation when \(0 \le x \le X\) and \(0 \le y \le Y\). It is interior when \(0 < x < X\) and \(0 < y < Y\). The feasible allocations form the rectangle \([0, X] \times [0, Y]\), which this lesson calls the box of totals. A feasible allocation that is not interior lies on the boundary of the box.

The utilities are an assumption of this lesson, not a result. Consumer A's utility is \(u_A = xy\), and consumer B's is \(u_B = (X - x)(Y - y)\). Both are products of the amounts held. Section 4 states what the results do not cover.

Definition 1.2 (Pareto improvement)

A feasible allocation \(z\) is a Pareto improvement on a feasible allocation \(w\) when each consumer's utility at \(z\) is at least its utility at \(w\), and at least one consumer's utility at \(z\) is strictly greater than at \(w\).

Definition 1.3 (Pareto efficiency)

A feasible allocation is Pareto efficient when no feasible allocation is a Pareto improvement on it.

Definition 1.2 requires a strict gain for at least one consumer. An allocation is not a Pareto improvement on itself, and a reshuffle that leaves both utilities unchanged is not one either.

2Efficient allocations are the diagonal

The marginal rate of substitution of good x for good y is defined for one consumer in Lesson 1, Section 2, as \((\partial u/\partial x)/(\partial u/\partial y)\). The partial derivatives of \(u_A = xy\) are \(\partial u_A/\partial x = y\) and \(\partial u_A/\partial y = x\), so this ratio for A is \(r_A = y/x\). For B, with \(u_B = (X - x)(Y - y)\), both partial derivatives carry a minus sign, which cancels in the ratio, so \(r_B = (Y - y)/(X - x)\).

An allocation is on the diagonal when \(yX = xY\). The diagonal is the segment of the box from \((0, 0)\) to \((X, Y)\).

Lemma 2.1 (equal rates are the diagonal)

Assume \(X > 0\) and \(Y > 0\). For an interior allocation, \(r_A = r_B\) if and only if \(yX = xY\).

Proof

Both denominators are positive, so \(r_A = r_B\) is equivalent to \(y(X - x) = x(Y - y)\). Expanding gives \(yX - xy = xY - xy\), and cancelling \(-xy\) on both sides gives \(yX = xY\). Each step is an equivalence, so the two conditions are the same.

Proposition 2.2 (off the diagonal, an interior allocation is improved)

Assume \(X > 0\), \(Y > 0\), \(u_A = xy\) and \(u_B = (X - x)(Y - y)\). If an interior allocation satisfies \(yX \ne xY\), then there is a feasible allocation that strictly raises both utilities. Hence it is not Pareto efficient.

Proof

By Lemma 2.1, \(r_A \ne r_B\). Suppose first that \(r_B < r_A\). Choose \(\lambda\) with \(r_B < \lambda < r_A\), and consider the trade in which A receives \(h > 0\) units of x from B and gives \(\lambda h\) units of y to B. A's new utility is \((x + h)(y - \lambda h)\), so its change is \[ (x + h)(y - \lambda h) - xy = h\,(y - \lambda x - \lambda h). \] Since \(\lambda < r_A\), we have \(y - \lambda x > 0\), so this change is positive whenever \(0 < h < (y - \lambda x)/\lambda\). B holds \(X - x - h\) of good x and \(Y - y + \lambda h\) of good y. Writing \(a = X - x\) and \(b = Y - y\), its change is \[ (a - h)(b + \lambda h) - ab = h\,(\lambda a - b - \lambda h). \] Since \(\lambda > r_B\), we have \(\lambda a - b > 0\), so this change is positive whenever \(0 < h < (\lambda a - b)/\lambda\). For every \(h\) that is positive, smaller than both bounds, and small enough that A's and B's amounts stay non-negative, both utilities rise strictly. The case \(r_A < r_B\) is the same argument with the names A and B exchanged.

Proposition 2.3 (the diagonal is efficient, including on the boundary)

Assume \(X > 0\), \(Y > 0\), \(u_A = xy\) and \(u_B = (X - x)(Y - y)\). Every feasible allocation with \(yX = xY\) is Pareto efficient, whether or not it is interior. For every feasible allocation, \(\sqrt{u_A} + \sqrt{u_B} \le \sqrt{XY}\), with equality exactly on the diagonal.

Proof

For a feasible allocation put \(p = x(Y - y) \ge 0\) and \(q = y(X - x) \ge 0\). Then \(u_A u_B = xy(X - x)(Y - y) = pq\). Expanding \(u_A + u_B = xy + (X - x)(Y - y)\) gives \(u_A + u_B = XY - xY - yX + 2xy\). Also \(p + q = xY + yX - 2xy = XY - (XY - xY - yX + 2xy) = XY - (u_A + u_B)\), so \[ p + q = XY - u_A - u_B. \] The inequality of the arithmetic and geometric means gives \(2\sqrt{pq} \le p + q\), so \(2\sqrt{u_A u_B} \le XY - u_A - u_B\). Therefore \[ (\sqrt{u_A} + \sqrt{u_B})^2 = u_A + u_B + 2\sqrt{u_A u_B} \le XY, \] which is the bound. Equality in the bound holds exactly when \(p = q\). Since \(p - q = xY - yX\), this is exactly the diagonal \(yX = xY\). Now let \(w\) be a feasible allocation on the diagonal, so the bound holds with equality at \(w\). Suppose a feasible allocation \(z\) is a Pareto improvement on \(w\). Then \(\sqrt{u_A(z)} \ge \sqrt{u_A(w)}\) and \(\sqrt{u_B(z)} \ge \sqrt{u_B(w)}\), with one inequality strict, because the square root is strictly increasing. Adding gives \(\sqrt{u_A(z)} + \sqrt{u_B(z)} > \sqrt{XY}\), which contradicts the bound at \(z\). So \(w\) is efficient.

Theorem 2.4 (efficient interior allocations)

Assume \(X > 0\), \(Y > 0\), \(u_A = xy\) and \(u_B = (X - x)(Y - y)\). An interior allocation is Pareto efficient if and only if \(yX = xY\). Equivalently, the efficient interior allocations are the points of the line \(y = (Y/X)\,x\) with \(0 < x < X\), which is the diagonal of the box of totals from the origin \((0, 0)\) to the corner \((X, Y)\).

Proof

If \(yX \ne xY\), Proposition 2.2 gives a Pareto improvement, so the allocation is not efficient. If \(yX = xY\), Proposition 2.3 gives efficiency. The two cases cover every interior allocation.

Example 2.5 (efficiency does not rank allocations)

Take \(X = 8\) and \(Y = 4\). The diagonal is \(y = x/2\). The allocations \((6, 3)\) and \((2, 1)\) both lie on it, so both are efficient by Theorem 2.4. At \((6, 3)\) the utilities are \(u_A = 18\) and \(u_B = 2\). At \((2, 1)\) they are \(u_A = 2\) and \(u_B = 18\). Neither is a Pareto improvement on the other, because each gives one consumer more and the other less.

Remark 2.6 (scope of Theorem 2.4)

Theorem 2.4 characterizes interior allocations only. Proposition 2.3 covers boundary allocations on the diagonal, such as \((0, 0)\) and \((X, Y)\). A boundary allocation off the diagonal is not covered by the theorem. Exercise R3 gives one that is not efficient.

3Checking the claims on a grid

A computer can test efficiency over a finite set of allocations. The test on a grid asks whether any grid allocation is a Pareto improvement. A grid allocation that survives the test is grid-efficient. Proposition 2.3 gives one direction for free: a grid allocation that improved a diagonal grid point would be a feasible allocation of the economy that improved it, which Proposition 2.3 forbids. For off-diagonal points, an improving allocation may lie between grid points, and then the grid misses it. Lab 1 runs both checks on the grid of totals \(X = 8\) and \(Y = 4\), with the 9 by 5 integer allocations of A. For every off-diagonal interior grid-efficient allocation it also prints an exact improvement, and in each case at least one printed amount is a non-integer, so no improvement shown is on the grid.

Lab 1 · Pareto efficiency on a grid, with exact improvements

ILLUSTRATIVE INPUT, NOT DATA: totals \(X = 8\) and \(Y = 4\), product utilities, and the 45 integer allocations of A. Stated before running: the three interior diagonal allocations \((2, 1)\), \((4, 2)\) and \((6, 3)\) are grid-efficient, by Proposition 2.3; the bound of Proposition 2.3 holds with equality at exactly the diagonal grid points, including \((0, 0)\) and \((8, 4)\); every interior grid-efficient allocation off the diagonal has an exact improvement, by Proposition 2.2, and the run prints each improvement, with its amounts. The run also counts the grid-efficient allocations, and the proofs do not predict that count.

"""E1 lab L08: Pareto efficiency in a two-consumer exchange economy, with exact arithmetic.

Standard library only (fractions). No randomness and no network. The totals are X_TOTAL = 8 units of good x and
Y_TOTAL = 4 units of good y. Consumer A holds (x, y) and consumer B holds the rest, (X_TOTAL - x, Y_TOTAL - y). Both
consumers have product utilities, u = (units of x) * (units of y).

Part A checks the integer grid of the 9 * 5 = 45 feasible allocations of A: an allocation is grid-efficient when no
other grid allocation weakly raises both utilities and strictly raises one. Part B lists the interior diagonal
allocations, which satisfy y * X_TOTAL = x * Y_TOTAL. Part C takes each interior grid-efficient allocation that is
off the diagonal and exhibits an exact rational allocation that strictly raises both utilities, with a non-integer
amount. Part D shows that two diagonal allocations with unequal utilities are both grid-efficient. Part E checks, in
integers, the bound sqrt(uA) + sqrt(uB) <= sqrt(X_TOTAL * Y_TOTAL) at every grid point, and finds the points
where it holds with equality. The checks are finite and exact; they test these instances only, and the
proof for every economy is on the page.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

X_TOTAL = 8
Y_TOTAL = 4

Allocation = tuple[Fraction, Fraction]


def utility_a(x: Fraction, y: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Consumer A's utility x * y for the units of good x and good y that A holds."""
    return x * y


def utility_b(x: Fraction, y: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Consumer B's utility of the rest of the totals, (X_TOTAL - x) * (Y_TOTAL - y)."""
    return (X_TOTAL - x) * (Y_TOTAL - y)


def grid() -> list[tuple[int, int]]:
    """The 9 * 5 feasible integer allocations of A, with 0 <= x <= X_TOTAL and 0 <= y <= Y_TOTAL."""
    return [(x, y) for x in range(X_TOTAL + 1) for y in range(Y_TOTAL + 1)]


def weakly_raises_both_strictly_one(z: Allocation, w: Allocation) -> bool:
    """True when z Pareto-dominates w: no consumer is worse off at z, and at least one is strictly better off."""
    ua_z, ub_z = utility_a(*z), utility_b(*z)
    ua_w, ub_w = utility_a(*w), utility_b(*w)
    return ua_z >= ua_w and ub_z >= ub_w and (ua_z > ua_w or ub_z > ub_w)


def grid_efficient(w: Allocation) -> bool:
    """True when no grid allocation Pareto-dominates w."""
    return not any(weakly_raises_both_strictly_one((Fraction(x), Fraction(y)), w) for x, y in grid())


def on_diagonal(x: int, y: int) -> bool:
    """The diagonal y / x = Y_TOTAL / X_TOTAL, written without division so that x = 0 is allowed."""
    return y * X_TOTAL == x * Y_TOTAL


def interior(x: int, y: int) -> bool:
    """Both consumers hold a positive amount of both goods."""
    return 0 < x < X_TOTAL and 0 < y < Y_TOTAL


def improvement(x: int, y: int) -> Allocation:
    """An exact rational allocation for A that strictly raises both utilities over the interior allocation (x, y).

    The ratio of A's marginal rates is y / x; B's is (Y - y) / (X - x). When they differ, a trade at a rate lambda
    strictly between them, of size h, raises both utilities. The largest admissible h is found from the two
    inequalities, and h is taken as half of it, so both utilities are strictly higher.
    """
    xa, ya = Fraction(x), Fraction(y)
    xb, yb = X_TOTAL - xa, Y_TOTAL - ya
    rate_a, rate_b = ya / xa, yb / xb
    lam = (rate_a + rate_b) / 2
    if rate_b < rate_a:
        # A receives h units of good x from B and gives lam * h units of good y to B.
        h = min((ya - lam * xa) / lam, (lam * xb - yb) / lam) / 2
        new_a = (xa + h, ya - lam * h)
    else:
        # A gives h units of good x to B and receives lam * h units of good y from B.
        h = min((lam * xa - ya) / lam, (yb - lam * xb) / lam) / 2
        new_a = (xa - h, ya + lam * h)
    if not weakly_raises_both_strictly_one(new_a, (xa, ya)):
        raise AssertionError(f"no improvement found over ({x}, {y})")
    if utility_a(*new_a) <= utility_a(xa, ya) or utility_b(*new_a) <= utility_b(xa, ya):
        raise AssertionError(f"the improvement over ({x}, {y}) is not strict for both consumers")
    return new_a


def part_a() -> list[tuple[int, int]]:
    efficient = [(x, y) for x, y in grid() if grid_efficient((Fraction(x), Fraction(y)))]
    print(f"part A: feasible grid allocations = {len(grid())}; grid-efficient allocations = {len(efficient)}")
    interior_efficient = [(x, y) for x, y in efficient if interior(x, y)]
    boundary_efficient = [(x, y) for x, y in efficient if not interior(x, y)]
    print(f"part A: grid-efficient interior allocations (0 < x < {X_TOTAL}, 0 < y < {Y_TOTAL}) = {interior_efficient}")
    print(f"part A: grid-efficient boundary allocations = {boundary_efficient}")
    return interior_efficient


def part_b(interior_efficient: list[tuple[int, int]]) -> None:
    diagonal = [(x, y) for x, y in grid() if interior(x, y) and on_diagonal(x, y)]
    if not all(grid_efficient((Fraction(x), Fraction(y))) for x, y in diagonal):
        raise AssertionError("a diagonal interior allocation is not grid-efficient")
    print(f"part B: interior diagonal allocations = {diagonal}; all grid-efficient: True")
    on_line = [p for p in interior_efficient if on_diagonal(*p)]
    print(f"part B: interior grid-efficient allocations on the diagonal = {on_line}")


def part_c(interior_efficient: list[tuple[int, int]]) -> None:
    off = [(x, y) for x, y in interior_efficient if not on_diagonal(x, y)]
    print(f"part C: interior grid-efficient allocations off the diagonal = {len(off)}")
    for x, y in off:
        new_a = improvement(x, y)
        new_b = (X_TOTAL - new_a[0], Y_TOTAL - new_a[1])
        print(
            f"part C: from ({x}, {y}) (utilities {utility_a(Fraction(x), Fraction(y))} and "
            f"{utility_b(Fraction(x), Fraction(y))}), A and B can move to A = ({new_a[0]}, {new_a[1]}) and "
            f"B = ({new_b[0]}, {new_b[1]}), with utilities {utility_a(*new_a)} and {utility_b(*new_a)}"
        )


def part_d() -> None:
    for held_a, held_b in (((6, 3), (2, 1)), ((2, 1), (6, 3))):
        point = (Fraction(held_a[0]), Fraction(held_a[1]))
        if not grid_efficient(point):
            raise AssertionError(f"the diagonal allocation {held_a} is not grid-efficient")
        print(
            f"part D: A holds {held_a}, B holds {held_b}: utilities {utility_a(*point)} and "
            f"{utility_b(*point)}; grid-efficient: True"
        )


def part_e() -> None:
    equality: list[tuple[int, int]] = []
    for x, y in grid():
        ua, ub = utility_a(Fraction(x), Fraction(y)), utility_b(Fraction(x), Fraction(y))
        slack = X_TOTAL * Y_TOTAL - ua - ub
        if slack < 0 or 4 * ua * ub > slack * slack:
            raise AssertionError(f"the bound fails at ({x}, {y})")
        if 4 * ua * ub == slack * slack:
            equality.append((x, y))
    diagonal = [(x, y) for x, y in grid() if on_diagonal(x, y)]
    print(f"part E: the bound sqrt(uA) + sqrt(uB) <= sqrt({X_TOTAL * Y_TOTAL}) holds at all {len(grid())} grid points")
    print(f"part E: equality at {len(equality)} grid points = {equality}")
    print(f"part E: the equality points are exactly the diagonal allocations: {sorted(equality) == sorted(diagonal)}")


def main() -> None:
    interior_efficient = part_a()
    part_b(interior_efficient)
    part_c(interior_efficient)
    part_d()
    part_e()


main()

Of the 13 interior grid-efficient allocations, 10 are off the diagonal. Part E checks the bound of Proposition 2.3 at all 45 grid points in integers, and its equality points are exactly the diagonal points. The run tests one pair of totals. It shows nothing about grids with other totals, and it does not describe what a finer grid would show.

4Where the canon breaks

  • Efficiency is not equity. Example 2.5 gives two efficient allocations, with utilities \((18, 2)\) and \((2, 18)\). Pareto efficiency ranks neither above the other, so it cannot say which distribution is fairer. A welfare judgment needs a rule beyond the Pareto criterion, and this lesson does not supply one.
  • The Pareto order is partial. Definition 1.2 ranks one allocation above another only when no consumer is worse off under it and at least one is strictly better off. Example 2.5 gives two efficient allocations in which each consumer is better off under one of them, so neither is ranked above the other.
  • The proofs use product utilities. Lemma 2.1 and Proposition 2.3 use the product form, and Proposition 2.2 uses the marginal rates that the product form gives. For other utility functions, the diagonal argument does not apply, and this lesson does not establish which allocations are efficient for them. Exercise R2 gives the first-order necessary condition under stated differentiability assumptions, and nothing here establishes sufficiency for general utilities.
  • Grid results are instance results. Lab 1 uses one pair of totals and one grid. Its 10 off-diagonal grid-efficient allocations show that a grid can pass allocations that are not efficient. They do not give a count for any other grid.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A Pareto-efficient allocation is the fair one.”

    Efficiency says that no reshuffle helps one consumer without hurting the other. Example 2.5 gives two efficient allocations, with utilities \((18, 2)\) and \((2, 18)\), that divide the same totals in different ways. Each is efficient by Proposition 2.3, and neither is fairer by the definition.

  2. “Any reshuffle that leaves nobody worse off is a Pareto improvement.”

    Definition 1.2 also requires a strict gain for at least one consumer. A reshuffle that changes nothing leaves nobody worse off and improves nobody, so it is not a Pareto improvement. Part A applies Definition 1.2: a grid allocation counts as an improvement only when no consumer is worse off and one is strictly better off. Part C's improvements raise both utilities strictly.

  3. “An allocation that no grid allocation improves is efficient.”

    A grid test examines grid points. Lab 1, Part A finds 13 interior allocations that no grid allocation improves, and 10 of them lie off the diagonal. Part C gives an exact improvement for each of the 10, for example from the allocation \((3, 1)\), where utilities are 3 and 15, to holdings \(A = (18/7, 6/5)\) and \(B = (38/7, 14/5)\), where utilities are \(108/35\) and \(76/5\). Both utilities are strictly higher, so \((3, 1)\) is not efficient.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · The efficient allocation with a given holding

Take \(X = 10\) and \(Y = 5\), with product utilities. Find the amount \(y\) of good y that makes the interior allocation with \(x = 4\) efficient, and give both utilities there.

Solution

By Theorem 2.4, the allocation is efficient exactly when \(yX = xY\), so \(10y = 4 \times 5 = 20\) and \(y = 2\). A holds \((4, 2)\), with \(u_A = 8\). B holds \((6, 3)\), with \(u_B = 18\).

M2 · An explicit improvement

For \(X = 10\) and \(Y = 5\), show that the allocation \((4, 1)\) is not efficient, and give an exact trade that strictly raises both utilities.

Solution

Here \(yX = 10\) and \(xY = 20\), so the allocation is off the diagonal and Proposition 2.2 applies. The ratios are \(r_A = 1/4\) and \(r_B = 4/6 = 2/3\), so \(r_A < r_B\), and A should give good x and receive good y. Take \(\lambda = 11/24\), which lies between \(1/4\) and \(2/3\). A gives \(h\) units of x and receives \(\lambda h\) units of y. Its change is \(h(\lambda x - y - \lambda h) = h(11/6 - 1 - 11h/24)\), so the bound is \(h < (\lambda x - y)/\lambda = (5/6)/(11/24) = 20/11\). B's change is \(h(b - \lambda a - \lambda h)\) with \(a = 6\) and \(b = 4\), positive when \(h < (b - \lambda a)/\lambda = (5/4)/(11/24) = 30/11\). Take \(h = 10/11\). A gives \(10/11\) of x and receives \(5/12\) of y, so A holds \((34/11, 17/12)\) with \(u_A = 289/66 \approx 4.379 > 4\). B holds \((76/11, 43/12)\) with \(u_B = 817/33 \approx 24.758 > 24\).

M3 · The square-root sum at a diagonal point

For \(X = 8\) and \(Y = 4\), compute \(\sqrt{u_A} + \sqrt{u_B}\) at the allocation \((6, 3)\), and compare it with \(\sqrt{XY}\).

Solution

A holds \((6, 3)\), so \(u_A = 18\). B holds \((2, 1)\), so \(u_B = 2\). Then \(\sqrt{18} + \sqrt{2} = 3\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{2} = 4\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{32} = \sqrt{XY}\). The sum equals the bound, as Proposition 2.3 says for a point on the diagonal.

P1 · The equal-rate condition

Prove Lemma 2.1 without using the word diagonal: show that for an interior allocation, \(y/x = (Y - y)/(X - x)\) holds if and only if \(y/x = Y/X\).

Solution

Multiply both sides of \(y/x = (Y - y)/(X - x)\) by \(x(X - x) > 0\). This gives \(y(X - x) = x(Y - y)\), which expands to \(yX = xY\). Dividing by \(xX > 0\) gives \(y/x = Y/X\). Each step is reversible, so the two equations are equivalent, and the claim holds.

P2 · The admissible trade size

For an interior allocation with \(r_B < \lambda < r_A\), show that A's utility rises strictly for a trade of \(h > 0\) units of x for \(\lambda h\) units of y if and only if \(h < (y - \lambda x)/\lambda\).

Solution

A's new utility minus its old is \((x + h)(y - \lambda h) - xy = xy - \lambda hx + hy - \lambda h^2 - xy = h(y - \lambda x - \lambda h)\). Since \(h > 0\), the sign is the sign of \(y - \lambda x - \lambda h\). This is positive exactly when \(\lambda h < y - \lambda x\), that is, \(h < (y - \lambda x)/\lambda\). The condition \(\lambda < r_A\) gives \(y - \lambda x > 0\), so the bound is positive.

P3 · The identity behind the bound

For every allocation in the box, prove that \(XY - u_A - u_B = x(Y - y) + y(X - x)\) and that \(u_A u_B = [x(Y - y)][y(X - x)]\).

Solution

Expanding \(u_A + u_B = xy + (X - x)(Y - y) = xy + XY - Xy - xY + xy = XY - Xy - xY + 2xy\). So \(XY - u_A - u_B = Xy + xY - 2xy\). The right side is \(x(Y - y) + y(X - x) = xY - xy + yX - xy\), which is the same expression. For the product, \(u_A u_B = xy(X - x)(Y - y) = [x(Y - y)][y(X - x)]\), by regrouping the four factors.

Empirical

E1 · Who gains more from the trade at (3, 1)

Lab 1, Part C gives an improvement from the allocation \((3, 1)\) with \(X = 8\) and \(Y = 4\). Verify that it strictly raises both utilities, and say which consumer gains more in utility.

Solution

A's new holding is \((18/7, 6/5)\), so \(u_A = (18/7)(6/5) = 108/35\), and \(108/35 - 3 = 3/35 \approx 0.086\). B's new holding is \((38/7, 14/5)\), so \(u_B = (38/7)(14/5) = 532/35 = 76/5\), and \(76/5 - 15 = 1/5 = 0.2\). Both gains are strictly positive. In absolute utility B gains more, 0.2 against 0.086. As a share of its starting utility, A gains 2.9% (3/35 divided by 3 = 1/35, about 2.86%) and B gains 1.3% (1/5 divided by 15 = 1/75, about 1.33%).

E2 · The share of efficient points on the grid

Part A of Lab 1 prints 13 interior grid-efficient allocations. How many of the 13 lie on the diagonal, and what share of the interior grid points is grid-efficient?

Solution

Lab 1, Part B lists three interior diagonal allocations, \((2, 1)\), \((4, 2)\) and \((6, 3)\), and all three are grid-efficient. So 3 of the 13 lie on the diagonal and 10 do not, as Part C shows. The 21 interior grid points are \(7 \times 3\), since \(0 < x < 8\) gives 7 values and \(0 < y < 4\) gives 3. The share is \(13/21 \approx 0.619\), or 61.9 per cent of the interior grid points, using the count of interior grid points as the base.

E3 · The points where the bound is an equality

Lab 1, Part E reports five grid points at which the bound of Proposition 2.3 holds with equality. Name them, and explain why \((0, 0)\) and \((8, 4)\) belong to the list.

Solution

The points are \((0, 0)\), \((2, 1)\), \((4, 2)\), \((6, 3)\) and \((8, 4)\). Equality holds exactly when \(p = q\), with \(p = x(Y - y)\) and \(q = y(X - x)\). At \((0, 0)\), both \(p\) and \(q\) are zero. At \((8, 4)\), \(p = 8 \times 0 = 0\) and \(q = 4 \times 0 = 0\). Both boundary points satisfy \(yX = xY\), so they lie on the diagonal, and equality holds there even though the allocation is on the boundary.

Research

R1 · The diagonal in square-root coordinates

On the diagonal, let \(s = \sqrt{u_A}\) and \(t = \sqrt{u_B}\). Show that \(s + t\) is constant, and describe the image of the diagonal in the \((s, t)\) plane.

Solution

On the diagonal \(y = (Y/X)x\), so \(u_A = x \cdot (Y/X)x\) and \(s = x\sqrt{Y/X}\). Also \(u_B = (X - x)(Y - (Y/X)x) = (X - x)(Y/X)(X - x)\), so \(t = (X - x)\sqrt{Y/X}\). Therefore \(s + t = X\sqrt{Y/X} = \sqrt{XY}\), a constant. As \(x\) runs over \([0, X]\), \(s\) runs over \([0, \sqrt{XY}]\), so the image is the segment of the line \(s + t = \sqrt{XY}\) between \((0, \sqrt{XY})\) and \((\sqrt{XY}, 0)\).

R2 · A first-order necessary condition for general utilities

Let \(u_A\) and \(u_B\) be differentiable at an interior allocation. Each consumer's partial derivatives are taken with respect to its own holdings of x and y, and all four are positive there. Let \(\mathrm{MRS}_i = (\partial u_i/\partial x)/(\partial u_i/\partial y)\) for each consumer. Show that a Pareto-efficient interior allocation has \(\mathrm{MRS}_A = \mathrm{MRS}_B\).

Solution

Consider the trade in which A receives \(h > 0\) units of x and gives \(\lambda h\) units of y. Differentiability gives \(u_A(x + h, y - \lambda h) - u_A(x, y) = h(\partial_x u_A - \lambda \partial_y u_A) + o(h)\). This is positive for small \(h\) when \(\lambda < \mathrm{MRS}_A\), since dividing by \(\partial_y u_A > 0\) shows that the first-order term is positive exactly when \(\lambda < \mathrm{MRS}_A\). For B, which gives \(h\) units of x and receives \(\lambda h\) units of y, the change is \(h(\lambda \partial_y u_B - \partial_x u_B) + o(h)\), positive for small \(h\) when \(\lambda > \mathrm{MRS}_B\). If \(\mathrm{MRS}_B < \mathrm{MRS}_A\), choose \(\lambda\) strictly between them. Both changes are then positive for small \(h > 0\), and the trade stays feasible because the allocation is interior. So the allocation is not efficient. Hence an efficient interior allocation has \(\mathrm{MRS}_A \le \mathrm{MRS}_B\). Exchanging the names A and B gives \(\mathrm{MRS}_B \le \mathrm{MRS}_A\), so the two rates are equal.

R3 · A boundary allocation off the diagonal

For \(X = 8\) and \(Y = 4\), show that the allocation in which A holds \((0, 2)\) is not Pareto efficient, and give an allocation that improves it.

Solution

A holds no good x, so \(u_A = 0\). B holds \((8, 2)\), so \(u_B = 16\). Let A receive \(8/3\) units of x from B and give B 1 unit of y. A then holds \((8/3, 1)\), with \(u_A = 8/3 > 0\). B holds \((16/3, 3)\), with \(u_B = (16/3)(3) = 16\), which is equal to its old utility. Neither consumer is worse off, and A is strictly better off, so this is a Pareto improvement. The point \((0, 2)\) is off the diagonal, since \(yX = 16\) and \(xY = 0\), and it lies on the boundary, where Theorem 2.4 does not apply.
