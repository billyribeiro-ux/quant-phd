Externalities and Public Goods

  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study

A market prices what its buyers and sellers pay for, and ignores what the rest of the economy bears. In the model below, a damage per unit to third parties, defined in Section 1, makes the untaxed quantity too large when it is positive, and the loss is a triangle of lost surplus. A good that no one can be kept from consuming, and that one person's use does not reduce, has a different aggregation rule: what each consumer would pay for a common quantity is added at that quantity. This lesson proves both results, derives the tax that corrects the first, and checks the arithmetic on one exact example.

1Externalities and a corrective tax

Consider a market for one good. Inverse demand is \(P(q) = a - bq\) in cents per unit, for \(0 \le q \le a/b\), with \(a > 0\) and \(b > 0\). Each unit costs the producer a constant \(c\) cents, with \(0 \le c < a\). Production also causes a constant damage of \(e\) cents per unit to third parties, with \(e \ge 0\). These are assumptions of this lesson. Money is in cents. One currency unit (CU) is 100 cents, and each CU figure on this page or in the lab output is the cent amount divided by 100.

Definition 1.1 (externality)

An externality is an effect of one agent's activity on another agent's utility or profit that the market does not price. A negative externality is one that lowers the other agent's welfare. When \(e > 0\), the damage \(e\) is a negative externality.

Definition 1.2 (marginal social cost and efficient quantity)

The marginal social cost of a unit is \(c + e\), its private cost plus the marginal damage. The efficient quantity \(q_s\) is the quantity at which the demand price equals the marginal social cost, \(a - bq_s = c + e\). The private quantity \(q_p\) is the quantity at which the demand price equals the private cost, \(a - bq_p = c\), which is the untaxed outcome of this model, where each unit is supplied at its private cost \(c\). This lesson takes that rule as the model's definition and does not derive it from a supply curve.

Solving the two equations gives \(q_p = (a - c)/b\) and \(q_s = (a - c - e)/b\). The efficient quantity is defined here by the marginal condition; Proposition 1.4 shows that it maximizes net surplus W(q), which is defined in the display below as the area under demand less the social cost of production.

Proposition 1.3 (the untaxed market overproduces when \(e > 0\))

Assume \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\), \(0 \le c < a\), \(e \ge 0\), and \(c + e < a\), so that \(q_s > 0\). Then \(q_p - q_s = e/b\). In particular \(q_p > q_s\) whenever \(e > 0\).

Proof

Subtract the two solutions: \(q_p - q_s = (a - c)/b - (a - c - e)/b = e/b\). If \(e > 0\) and \(b > 0\), this is positive, so \(q_p > q_s\).

Net surplus is the area under demand, less the social cost of production: \[ W(q) = \int_0^q (a - bs)\,ds - (c + e)\,q = aq - \tfrac{b}{2}q^2 - (c + e)\,q. \] The integral is the consumers' total willingness to pay for \(q\) units, and the second term is the producers' cost and the damage. The tax revenue is a transfer, and it does not enter \(W\).

Proposition 1.4 (the efficient quantity maximizes net surplus)

Assume \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\), \(c + e < a\). For every real \(q\), \[ W(q) = W(q_s) - \tfrac{b}{2}\,(q - q_s)^2. \] Hence \(q_s\) is the unique maximizer of \(W\), and the net-surplus loss of a quantity \(q\) is \(\tfrac{b}{2}(q - q_s)^2\).

Proof

Let \(k = c + e\). Since \(a - k = bq_s\), the net surplus is \(W(q) = (a - k)q - \tfrac{b}{2}q^2 = bq_s q - \tfrac{b}{2}q^2\). Then \(W(q_s) = bq_s^2 - \tfrac{b}{2}q_s^2 = \tfrac{b}{2}q_s^2\). Expanding the right side, \[ W(q_s) - \tfrac{b}{2}(q - q_s)^2 = \tfrac{b}{2}q_s^2 - \tfrac{b}{2}q^2 + bq_s q - \tfrac{b}{2}q_s^2 = bq_s q - \tfrac{b}{2}q^2, \] which is \(W(q)\) for every real \(q\). Since \(b > 0\), the subtracted term is smallest at \(q = q_s\), where it is zero and every other \(q\) gives a positive value. So \(W\) is largest at \(q_s\), and \(q_s\) is the unique maximizer.

Corollary 1.5 (deadweight loss of the untaxed market)

Under the assumptions of Proposition 1.4, the deadweight loss of the untaxed market is \(W(q_s) - W(q_p) = e^2/(2b)\).

Proof

By Proposition 1.4 with \(q = q_p\), the loss is \(\tfrac{b}{2}(q_p - q_s)^2\), and Proposition 1.3 gives \(q_p - q_s = e/b\). So the loss is \(\tfrac{b}{2}(e/b)^2 = e^2/(2b)\). The same number is the area of the triangle between the demand price and the marginal social cost over \([q_s, q_p]\): the gap, marginal social cost \(c + e\) minus the demand price, is zero at \(q_s\) and equals \(e\) at \(q_p\), and the interval has length \(e/b\).

Proposition 1.6 (the corrective tax)

Assume \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\), \(e \ge 0\) and \(c + e < a\). Let a per-unit tax \(t \ge 0\) raise the private cost to \(c + t\), so the market quantity becomes \(q(t) = (a - c - t)/b\). Then \(q(t) = q_s\) if and only if \(t = e\). The tax \(t = e\), called the Pigouvian tax, therefore makes the market quantity the maximizer of \(W\). Its revenue is \(e\,q_s\), which is a transfer and is not part of \(W\).

Proof

\(q(t) = q_s\) means \((a - c - t)/b = (a - c - e)/b\), which holds exactly when \(t = e\), since \(b > 0\). With \(t = e\), the market quantity is \(q_s\), which maximizes \(W\) by Proposition 1.4.

2Public goods and the vertical sum

Definition 2.1 (public good)

A public good is a good that is non-rival, so one consumer's use does not reduce the amount left for another, and non-excludable, so no consumer can be kept from consuming it. Every consumer then consumes the same quantity \(g\). A consumer's willingness to pay at quantity \(g\) is the price per unit at which that consumer would take exactly \(g\) units.

For a private good, each consumer chooses its own quantity at a given price, so the market quantity at that price is the sum of individual quantities, a horizontal sum. For a public good, all consumers share one quantity, so the sum is taken at each quantity: the vertical sum of the willingness-to-pay curves.

Proposition 2.2 (the efficient quantity of a public good)

Let there be two consumers with willingness to pay \(w_1(g)\) and \(w_2(g)\) per unit, each continuous, non-negative and non-increasing on \([0, \infty)\). Let the cost be \(c_g > 0\) per unit of the good. Let \(A(g) = w_1(g) + w_2(g)\) be the vertical sum, and let \(S(g) = \int_0^g A(s)\,ds - c_g\,g\) be total surplus. If \(g^* \ge 0\) satisfies \(A(g^*) = c_g\), then \(g^*\) maximizes \(S\) on \([0, \infty)\).

Proof

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, \(S\) is differentiable with \(S'(g) = A(g) - c_g\). Since \(A\) is non-increasing, \(A(g) \ge A(g^*) = c_g\) for \(g \le g^*\), so \(S' \ge 0\) on \([0, g^*]\), and \(A(g) \le c_g\) for \(g \ge g^*\), so \(S' \le 0\) on \([g^*, \infty)\). Hence \(S\) rises up to \(g^*\) and falls after it, so \(g^*\) is a maximizer.

The two consumers' willingness to pay enters through the sum \(A\) at the same quantity. Proposition 2.2 says nothing about how the cost is shared between the consumers. A rule for sharing it, and whether each consumer then accepts the quantity, are separate questions that this lesson does not answer.

Lab 1 · A Pigouvian tax and a vertical sum, in exact arithmetic

ILLUSTRATIVE INPUT, NOT DATA: inverse demand \(1000 - 10q\) cents per unit, private cost 200 cents per unit, damage 120 cents per unit; a public good with willingness to pay \(1000 - 100g\) and \(\max(0, 600 - 200g)\) cents per unit, and cost 400 cents per unit. Stated before running: the untaxed quantity is 80 units and the efficient quantity is 68 units; the only integer tax from 0 to 200 cents that gives 68 units is 120 cents; the deadweight loss of the untaxed market is 720 cents, by the wedge and by the surplus difference; the integer quantity that maximises total surplus is 68 units in Part A; in Part B the vertical sum equals the cost at 6 units, which maximises total surplus over the integers from 0 to 10, and the horizontal sum at 400 cents would give 7 units, with total surplus at 7 units below that at 6.

"""E1 lab L09: a Pigouvian tax on a negative externality, and the vertical sum for a public good, in exact arithmetic.

Standard library only (fractions). No randomness and no network. Money is in cents and quantities are in units, so
every price is in cents per unit. Part A is a market with linear inverse demand P(q) = 1000 - 10 q, a constant
private marginal cost of 200 cents per unit, and a constant marginal external damage of 120 cents per unit. Part A
finds the private quantity, the efficient quantity, the tax that makes them equal, and the deadweight loss without
the tax, by two exact methods. It then checks every integer tax from 0 to 200 cents, and the integer quantity from 0
to 100 that maximises total surplus. Part B is a public good with
two consumers and a constant marginal cost of 400 cents per unit. Its willingness to pay is summed vertically, at
each quantity, and the total surplus is checked on the integer quantities 0 to 10. Both parts are exact
computations on the stated inputs, not simulations and not market data.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

CHOKE_CENTS = 1000  # the inverse demand P(q) = CHOKE_CENTS - SLOPE * q is in cents per unit
SLOPE = 10  # cents per unit, per unit of quantity
PRIVATE_COST = 200  # cents per unit: the private marginal cost, constant
DAMAGE = 120  # cents per unit: the marginal external damage, constant
PUBLIC_COST = 400  # cents per unit: the marginal cost of the public good, constant


def demand_price(q: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Inverse demand in cents per unit at quantity q (units), for q up to the choke quantity."""
    return CHOKE_CENTS - SLOPE * q


def private_quantity(tax: int) -> Fraction:
    """The quantity at which demand price equals private marginal cost plus the tax, in units."""
    return Fraction(CHOKE_CENTS - PRIVATE_COST - tax, SLOPE)


def welfare_loss_wedge(q: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The gap in cents per unit between the marginal social cost and the demand price at quantity q."""
    return (PRIVATE_COST + DAMAGE) - demand_price(q)


def social_surplus(q: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Total surplus in cents: the area under inverse demand from 0 to q, less the social cost (cost plus damage).

    Inverse demand is linear, so the trapezoid rule over [0, q] is exact.
    """
    area = (demand_price(Fraction(0)) + demand_price(q)) / 2 * q
    return area - (PRIVATE_COST + DAMAGE) * q


def part_a() -> None:
    private = private_quantity(0)
    efficient = private_quantity(DAMAGE)
    print(f"part A: private equilibrium quantity without a tax = {private} units")
    print(f"part A: efficient quantity, where demand price equals marginal social cost = {efficient} units")
    matching = [t for t in range(0, 201) if private_quantity(t) == efficient]
    print(f"part A: integer taxes from 0 to 200 cents that give the efficient quantity = {matching}")
    tax = matching[0]
    revenue = tax * efficient
    print(f"part A: revenue at the Pigouvian tax = {revenue} cents = {float(revenue / 100):.2f} CU")
    by_wedge = (welfare_loss_wedge(efficient) + welfare_loss_wedge(private)) / 2 * (private - efficient)
    by_surplus = social_surplus(efficient) - social_surplus(private)
    print(
        f"part A: deadweight loss of the untaxed market, by the wedge over [{efficient}, {private}] = {by_wedge} cents"
    )
    print(f"part A: deadweight loss of the untaxed market, by the surplus difference = {by_surplus} cents")
    grid = [Fraction(q) for q in range(0, 101)]
    best = max(grid, key=social_surplus)
    print(f"part A: integer quantity from 0 to 100 that maximises total surplus = {best} units")


def willingness_1(g: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Consumer 1's willingness to pay in cents per unit at quantity g of the public good."""
    return 1000 - 100 * g


def willingness_2(g: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Consumer 2's willingness to pay in cents per unit at quantity g; zero once the line reaches zero."""
    return max(Fraction(0), 600 - 200 * g)


def aggregate_willingness(g: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The vertical sum: both consumers' willingness to pay at the same quantity g, added in cents per unit."""
    return willingness_1(g) + willingness_2(g)


def public_surplus(g: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Total surplus in cents: the area under the vertical sum from 0 to g, less the public cost of g units.

    The vertical sum is linear on [0, 3] and on [3, 10] (consumer 2's line reaches zero at g = 3), so the trapezoid
    rule is exact when the interval is split at 3.
    """
    kink = Fraction(3)
    pieces = [(Fraction(0), min(g, kink))]
    if g > kink:
        pieces.append((kink, g))
    area = Fraction(0)
    for a, b in pieces:
        area += (aggregate_willingness(a) + aggregate_willingness(b)) / 2 * (b - a)
    return area - PUBLIC_COST * g


def part_b() -> None:
    print(f"part B: vertical sum at quantity 3 = {aggregate_willingness(Fraction(3))} cents per unit")
    print(f"part B: consumer 2 at quantity 6 = {willingness_2(Fraction(6))}; consumer 1 = {willingness_1(Fraction(6))}")
    efficient = [Fraction(g) for g in range(0, 11) if aggregate_willingness(Fraction(g)) == PUBLIC_COST]
    shown = [int(g) for g in efficient]
    print(f"part B: integer quantity where the vertical sum equals the cost of 400 cents per unit = {shown}")
    grid = [Fraction(g) for g in range(0, 11)]
    best = max(grid, key=public_surplus)
    print(f"part B: integer quantity from 0 to 10 that maximises total surplus = {best} units")
    print(f"part B: total surplus at 6 units = {public_surplus(Fraction(6))} cents")
    horizontal = Fraction(1000 - PUBLIC_COST, 100) + max(Fraction(0), Fraction(600 - PUBLIC_COST, 200))
    print(f"part B: quantity from adding the two demands at the same price of 400 cents = {horizontal} units")
    print(f"part B: total surplus at 7 units = {public_surplus(Fraction(7))} cents")


def main() -> None:
    part_a()
    part_b()

Part A reproduces Proposition 1.3: the untaxed quantity 80 exceeds the efficient quantity 68 by \(e/b = 12\) units. 3Where the canon breaks

  • The tax needs the damage. Proposition 1.6 gives the Pigouvian tax as \(e\), the marginal damage. A tax set at any other value leaves the quantity away from \(q_s\). The result also assumes that \(e\) is constant. Exercise R1 gives the Pigouvian tax for a damage that grows with output, and Exercise R2 shows that a tax set at the marginal damage at the untaxed quantity misses the efficient quantity.
  • The surplus is not Pareto efficiency. Lesson 8 defines Pareto efficiency for allocations of goods. The criterion in this lesson is total surplus net of damage, and it ignores who receives the tax revenue. A Pareto comparison of the taxed and untaxed markets would have to say who receives the revenue, which this lesson does not do.
  • Vertical summation assumes one shared quantity. Proposition 2.2 applies to goods that every consumer consumes in the same amount. For a rival good, the market quantity at a price is a horizontal sum, and the vertical rule does not apply.
  • The example is linear. Part A and Part B use linear demand and linear willingness to pay with one kink. The lab does not show the same answers for curved demand, and it does not treat a cost that depends on the number of consumers.

4Misconceptions

  1. “With a negative externality, the market produces too little.”

    The sign runs the other way. Proposition 1.3 gives \(q_p - q_s = e/b > 0\), so the untaxed market produces too much. Lab 1, Part A gives 80 units untaxed against 68 efficient. Each of the 12 units numbered 69 to 80 has a demand price below the marginal social cost of 320 cents, so the untaxed market produces them at a loss to society.

  2. “A public good's demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands, as for a private good.”

    Each consumer of a public good consumes the same quantity, so the sum is taken at each quantity. Lab 1, Part B shows the difference: at a price of 400 cents, the horizontal sum gives 7 units, but total surplus at 7 units is 2,650 cents, below the 2,700 cents at the efficient 6 units.

  3. “Any tax on the polluting good moves the market to the efficient quantity.”

    Only the tax equal to \(e\) does (Proposition 1.6). By the formula of Section 1, a tax of 100 cents leaves the market at \((800 - 100)/10 = 70\) units, not 68. The lab prints the list of taxes from 0 to 200 cents that give 68 units, and the list is [120]. The net-surplus loss of the 100-cent tax is \(\tfrac{b}{2}(70 - 68)^2 = 20\) cents, which is positive.

Mechanics

M1 · The untaxed and efficient quantities

Inverse demand is \(600 - 5q\) cents per unit, the private cost is 100 cents per unit, and the marginal damage is 50 cents per unit. Find \(q_p\), \(q_s\), the Pigouvian tax, and the deadweight loss of the untaxed market in cents.

Solution

Here \(a = 600\), \(b = 5\), \(c = 100\) and \(e = 50\). The untaxed quantity solves \(600 - 5q = 100\), so \(q_p = 100\) units. The efficient quantity solves \(600 - 5q = 150\), so \(q_s = 90\) units. The difference is \(10 = e/b\), as Proposition 1.3 says. The Pigouvian tax is \(e = 50\) cents per unit (Proposition 1.6). The deadweight loss is \(e^2/(2b) = 2500/10 = 250\) cents, which is 2.50 CU. The triangle check agrees: the base is \(e/b = 10\) units and the height is 50 cents, so the area is \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 50 = 250\).

M2 · A public good with two straight lines

Consumer 1's willingness to pay is \(800 - 100g\) and consumer 2's is \(400 - 100g\) cents per unit, both positive on \([0, 3.5]\). The cost is 500 cents per unit. Find the efficient quantity and the total surplus there.

Solution

The vertical sum is \(A(g) = 1200 - 200g\). Setting \(A(g^*) = 500\) gives \(g^* = 3.5\) units. Consumer 2's line is \(400 - 350 = 50 > 0\) at that quantity, so both lines are active and the sum is correct. Total surplus is \(\int_0^{3.5}(1200 - 200s)\,ds - 500 \times 3.5 = [1200s - 100s^2]_0^{3.5} - 1750 = 4200 - 1225 - 1750 = 1225\) cents, which is 12.25 CU.

M3 · Horizontal against vertical

For the two lines of M2, find the quantity that the horizontal sum gives at a price of 500 cents per unit, and compare it with the efficient quantity of M2.

Solution

Each consumer's quantity at price 500 solves its line. Consumer 1: \(800 - 100q_1 = 500\) gives \(q_1 = 3\). Consumer 2: \(400 - 100q_2 = 500\) gives \(q_2 = -1\), which is not possible, so \(q_2 = 0\). The horizontal sum is \(3\) units. The efficient quantity of M2 is 3.5 units, so the horizontal rule understates it by half a unit. The vertical rule does not sum quantities at a price; it sums willingness to pay at a quantity.

P1 · The overproduction gap

Starting from the two marginal conditions, prove that \(q_p - q_s = e/b\), and show that the gap does not depend on \(a\) or \(c\).

Solution

The untaxed condition is \(a - bq_p = c\) and the efficient condition is \(a - bq_s = c + e\). Subtracting the first from the second eliminates \(a\) and \(c\): \(b(q_p - q_s) = e\), so \(q_p - q_s = e/b\). The gap depends only on the damage and the slope. If \(e > 0\), it is positive.

P2 · The completed square

Show that for every real \(q\), \(W(q) = W(q_s) - \tfrac{b}{2}(q - q_s)^2\), where \(W(q) = aq - \tfrac{b}{2}q^2 - (c + e)q\), and deduce the deadweight loss of Corollary 1.5.

Solution

Write \(W(q) = aq - \tfrac{b}{2}q^2 - (c + e)q\). Since \(a - (c + e) = bq_s\), this is \(W(q) = bq_s q - \tfrac{b}{2}q^2\). Then \(W(q_s) = bq_s^2 - \tfrac{b}{2}q_s^2 = \tfrac{b}{2}q_s^2\). Now \(W(q_s) - \tfrac{b}{2}(q - q_s)^2 = \tfrac{b}{2}q_s^2 - \tfrac{b}{2}(q^2 - 2qq_s + q_s^2) = bq_s q - \tfrac{b}{2}q^2\), which is \(W(q)\). Taking \(q = q_p\) gives \(W(q_s) - W(q_p) = \tfrac{b}{2}(e/b)^2 = e^2/(2b)\).

P3 · The sign argument for a public good

Under the hypotheses of Proposition 2.2, suppose \(g^*\) satisfies \(A(g^*) = c_g\), and let \(g > g^*\). Show that \(S(g) \le S(g^*)\) without assuming that \(A\) is differentiable.

Solution

\(S(g) - S(g^*) = \int_{g^*}^{g} A(s)\,ds - c_g(g - g^*)\). For \(s \ge g^*\), monotonicity gives \(A(s) \le A(g^*) = c_g\), so the integrand \(A(s) - c_g\) is at most zero. Integrating a non-positive function over \([g^*, g]\) gives a non-positive value. Hence \(S(g) \le S(g^*)\). The case \(g < g^*\) is symmetric: \(S(g^*) - S(g) = \int_g^{g^*}(A(s) - c_g)\,ds \ge 0\), because \(A(s) \ge c_g\) on that interval.

Empirical

E1 · The loss under a tax of 100 cents

In the market of Lab 1, Part A, a tax of 100 cents leaves the quantity at 70 units by the formula of Section 1, although the lab's list of taxes that give 68 units is [120]. Find the net-surplus loss of this tax against the efficient 68 units, and check it by the wedge.

Solution

Here \(q(100) = (1000 - 200 - 100)/10 = 70\). By Proposition 1.4, the loss against the efficient quantity is \(\tfrac{b}{2}(70 - 68)^2 = 5 \times 4 = 20\) cents. By the wedge, the gap between the marginal social cost of 320 cents and the demand price is zero at 68 units and \(320 - 300 = 20\) cents at 70 units. The area under a straight gap that rises from 0 to 20 cents over 2 units is \(\tfrac{1}{2}(0 + 20) \times 2 = 20\) cents, which matches the proposition.

E2 · The share of maximum surplus at 7 units

In Lab 1, Part B, total surplus is 2,700 cents at 6 units and 2,650 cents at 7 units. What share of the maximum does 7 units achieve, and what is the base of that share?

Solution

The base is the maximum total surplus, 2,700 cents, at the efficient 6 units. The share is \(2650/2700 = 0.9815\), or 98.1 per cent of the maximum. The base is stated because a share of a different quantity gives a different percentage: at 4 units the surplus is 2,500 cents, and \(2500/2700 = 0.9259\), or 92.6 per cent.

E3 · Which consumer sets the efficient quantity

In the public good of Lab 1, Part B, which consumer's willingness to pay determines the efficient quantity, and why does the other consumer not affect it?

Solution

The efficient quantity is 6 units. At 6 units, consumer 2's line \(600 - 200 \times 6 = -600\) is below zero, so consumer 2's willingness to pay is zero, as Lab 1 prints. Consumer 1's line \(1000 - 600 = 400\) equals the cost of 400 cents. So consumer 1 alone sets the quantity. Consumer 2 affects the sum only on \([0, 3]\), where its line is positive, and that part of the surplus does not determine the maximizer.

Research

R1 · A tax on a damage that grows with output

Let the marginal damage at output \(q\) be \(k q\) cents per unit, with \(k > 0\), so the marginal social cost is \(c + kq\). Find the efficient quantity and the Pigouvian tax that equals the marginal damage at the efficient quantity.

Solution

The efficient quantity solves \(a - bq = c + kq\), so \(q_s = (a - c)/(b + k)\). The marginal damage at that quantity is \(kq_s\). A constant tax \(t\) moves the untaxed market to \(q(t) = (a - c - t)/b\), and \(q(t) = q_s\) requires \(t = a - c - bq_s\). Substituting \(q_s\) gives \(t = (a - c)\bigl(1 - b/(b + k)\bigr) = (a - c)k/(b + k) = kq_s\). So the tax equals the damage at the efficient quantity, \(kq_s\).

R2 · A tax set at the untaxed quantity misses

With the marginal damage \(kq\) of R1 and \(k > 0\), suppose the regulator sets the tax equal to the marginal damage at the untaxed quantity, \(t = kq_p\). Show that the resulting quantity is not \(q_s\), and find it for \(a = 1000\), \(b = 10\), \(c = 200\), \(k = 5\).

Solution

The untaxed quantity is \(q_p = (a - c)/b\). With the tax \(t = kq_p\), the market quantity is \(q = (a - c - kq_p)/b = q_p(1 - k/b)\). The efficient quantity is \(q_s = (a - c)/(b + k) = q_p\, b/(b + k)\). These are equal only if \((b - k)/b = b/(b + k)\), that is \((b - k)(b + k) = b^2\), that is \(k^2 = 0\). So for \(k > 0\) the quantities differ. For the numbers given, \(q_p = 80\), the tax is \(t = 400\), and the quantity is \((800 - 400)/10 = 40\) units. The efficient quantity is \(800/15 \approx 53.3\) units, so the regulator's rule leaves the market 13.3 units short of the efficient quantity.

R3 · The public good's maximizer is unique

For the public good with \(w_1(g) = 1000 - 100g\), \(w_2(g) = \max(0, 600 - 200g)\) and cost 400 cents per unit, show that \(g = 6\) is the unique maximizer of \(S\) on \([0, 10]\), where both willingness-to-pay functions are non-negative.

Solution

The vertical sum is \(A(g) = 1600 - 300g\) on \([0, 3]\) and \(A(g) = 1000 - 100g\) on \([3, 10]\), where it is strictly decreasing and equals 400 at \(g = 6\). For \(g < 6\), \(A(g) > 400\), so \(S' > 0\). For \(g > 6\), \(A(g) < 400\), so \(S' < 0\). The derivative changes sign only once, at 6, and it is strictly positive before and strictly negative after, so \(S\) increases up to 6 and decreases after, and 6 is the unique maximizer on \([0, 10]\).
