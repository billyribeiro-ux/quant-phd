Competitive Equilibrium

  • Uses: producer profit maximization (Lesson 2), for the supply of each firm (the output rule of Proposition 1.3 there)

A price is the point at which the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer. Questions about what moves that point start from this definition. This lesson sets out the simplest market in which the point can be computed exactly: linear demand, and a market supply made of identical price-taking firms whose supply is the output of Lesson 2. It proves that the equilibrium exists, is unique, and has a closed form, and it shows when the market trades at all. Lab 1 checks the equilibrium and the sign of excess demand on a grid of prices.

1Existence and uniqueness of the equilibrium

Setting (linear demand, identical price-taking firms)

Demand at price \(p \ge 0\) is \(D(p) = a - bp\), with \(a > 0\) and \(b > 0\). There are \(n\) identical firms, each with the cost of Lesson 2, \(C(q) = F + cq + kq^2\) with \(k > 0\). By Proposition 2.2 of Lesson 2, each firm supplies \(q(p) = \max(0, (p - c)/(2k))\). Market supply is therefore

\[ S(p) = n\,q(p) = s\,\max(0,\ p - c), \qquad s = \frac{n}{2k} > 0. \]

Assume also \(a > bc\). Every statement in Section 1 assumes this setting unless it says otherwise.

Definition 1.1 (competitive equilibrium)

A competitive equilibrium is a price \(p^* \ge 0\) at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, \(D(p^*) = S(p^*)\). The common value \(Q^* = D(p^*)\) is the equilibrium quantity. Each firm takes \(p^*\) as given, so its output is the one of Lesson 2 at that price.

Definition 1.2 (excess demand)

The excess demand at price \(p\) is \(Z(p) = D(p) - S(p)\). A negative value is an excess supply.

Proposition 1.3 (existence, uniqueness and the closed form)

Under the Setting, there is exactly one competitive equilibrium price \(p^*\), and

\[ p^* = \frac{a + sc}{b + s}, \qquad Q^* = a - bp^* = s\,(p^* - c) > 0. \]

Moreover \(p^* > c\), and \(p^* - c = (a - bc)/(b + s)\).

Proof

Step 1: \(Z\) is continuous and strictly decreasing on \([0, \infty)\). For \(0 \le p \le c\), \(S(p) = 0\), so \(Z(p) = a - bp\), which is strictly decreasing. For \(p \ge c\), \(Z(p) = a - bp - s(p - c)\), with slope \(-(b + s) < 0\). At \(p = c\) both formulas give \(a - bc\), so \(Z\) is continuous, and it is strictly decreasing on the whole half-line.

Step 2: a zero exists above \(c\). Since \(S(c) = 0\), \(Z(c) = a - bc > 0\) by the assumption \(a > bc\). On \([c, \infty)\), \(Z\) is linear with slope \(-(b + s)\), so it tends to \(-\infty\). By the intermediate value theorem there is a zero \(\hat p\) in \((c, \infty)\).

Step 3: the zero is unique and has the closed form. Strict monotonicity from Step 1 allows at most one zero, so \(\hat p = p^*\) is the unique equilibrium. On \(p \ge c\), the equation \(a - bp - s(p - c) = 0\) gives \((b + s)p = a + sc\), so \(p^* = (a + sc)/(b + s)\). Then \(p^* - c = (a + sc - (b + s)c)/(b + s) = (a - bc)/(b + s) > 0\). The quantity is \(Q^* = a - bp^* = s(p^* - c)\), which is positive because \(p^* > c\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.4 (the sign pattern of excess demand)

Because \(Z\) is strictly decreasing and \(Z(p^*) = 0\), \(Z(p) > 0\) for \(p < p^*\) (excess demand) and \(Z(p) < 0\) for \(p > p^*\) (excess supply). A price-adjustment rule that raises the price when excess demand is positive and lowers it when excess demand is negative therefore points toward \(p^*\) from either side. The lesson does not analyze the speed of such a rule.

Example 1.5 (demand 100 − 2p, ten firms)

Take \(a = 100\), \(b = 2\), \(n = 10\), \(c = 2\) and \(k = 1\). Then \(s = 10/2 = 5\), \(a > bc\) because \(100 > 4\), and Proposition 1.3 gives \(p^* = (100 + 10)/7 = 110/7\) and \(Q^* = 480/7\). Check: \(D(110/7) = 100 - 220/7 = 480/7\), and \(S(110/7) = 5 \cdot (110/7 - 2) = 5 \cdot 96/7 = 480/7\). The price is not an integer, and it is kept as the exact fraction \(110/7\) rather than rounded.

2Firms, market supply and the fixed cost

Proposition 2.1 (the fixed cost does not affect the equilibrium)

Under the Setting, the equilibrium price and quantity are the same for every fixed cost \(F \ge 0\) of each firm.

Proof

The output \(q(p)\) of each firm, from Proposition 2.2 of Lesson 2, is \(\max(0, (p - c)/(2k))\) and does not contain \(F\). So \(S\), \(Z\), \(p^*\) and \(Q^*\) in Proposition 1.3 do not contain \(F\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.2 (one firm is not the market)

Market supply is \(n\) times one firm's supply. At \(p^*\) of Example 1.5, one firm supplies \((p^* - c)/(2k) = 48/7\), and the ten firms together supply \(480/7 = 10 \cdot 48/7\). Setting one firm's supply equal to demand would give the wrong equilibrium, because it uses one tenth of the market quantity.

Lab 1 · The linear equilibrium in exact arithmetic, and the firm check

Predicted before running. The lab computes the equilibrium of Example 1.5 as exact fractions and expects \(p^* = 110/7\) and \(Q^* = 480/7\), with demand equal to supply at \(p^*\). On the 141 grid prices \(i/7\) for \(i = 0\) to \(140\), excess demand should be positive at the 110 prices below \(p^*\), zero at \(p^*\) (grid point 110), and negative at the 30 prices above it. At \(p = 20\) the excess should be \(-30\). One firm's supply at \(p^*\) should be \(48/7\), a tenth of the market quantity, and the firm's grid maximiser should be \(48/7\) whether \(F = 0\) or \(F = 100\).

"""E1 lab L03: the linear competitive equilibrium in exact arithmetic, and the firm-level check behind the supply.

Standard library only (fractions). No randomness and no network. Demand is D(p) = a - b p with a = 100 and
b = 2. There are n = 10 identical price-taking firms, each with the cost of Lesson 2 at c = 2 and k = 1, so each
firm supplies (p - c) / (2 k) for p >= c and nothing below c. The market supply is S(p) = 5 (p - 2) for p >= 2 and
0 below 2. Prices are exact Fractions in the price unit of the example. The lab uses the equilibrium formula
p* = (a + s c) / (b + s) with s = n / (2 k), and it checks four things. First, D(p*) = S(p*), so the market clears.
Second, on the grid p = i / 7 for i = 0 to 140 (prices 0 to 20), the excess demand Z(p) = D(p) - S(p) is positive
below p*, zero at p* and negative above it. Third, it prints demand, market supply and excess demand at p = 20, the
end of the grid, and at p* the ratio of market supply to one firm's supply, which is n = 10. Fourth, at p* a firm's
grid maximiser, on output steps of 1 / 14 from 0 to 10, is 48 / 7 for fixed cost F = 0 and for F = 100. A grid is
finite: it tests these inputs only, and the proofs for every linear case are on the page.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

A = Fraction(100)  # demand intercept: D(p) = A - B p
B = Fraction(2)  # demand slope
N_FIRMS = 10
UNIT_COST = Fraction(2)  # c
CAPACITY_SLOPE = Fraction(1)  # k, in the firm's cost C(q) = F + c q + k q^2
S_SLOPE = Fraction(N_FIRMS) / (2 * CAPACITY_SLOPE)  # s = n / (2 k): market supply is s (p - c) for p >= c


def demand(p: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Quantity demanded D(p) = a - b p at price p."""
    return A - B * p


def firm_supply(p: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """One firm's supply (p - c) / (2 k) at price p, zero below the unit cost c."""
    return max(Fraction(0), (p - UNIT_COST) / (2 * CAPACITY_SLOPE))


def supply(p: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Market supply S(p) = n times one firm's supply at price p."""
    return N_FIRMS * firm_supply(p)


def excess_demand(p: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Excess demand Z(p) = D(p) - S(p)."""
    return demand(p) - supply(p)


def equilibrium_price() -> Fraction:
    """p* = (a + s c) / (b + s), the price at which the linear market supply meets demand."""
    return (A + S_SLOPE * UNIT_COST) / (B + S_SLOPE)


def profit(q: Fraction, p: Fraction, fixed: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """One firm's profit p q - F - c q - k q^2 at output q, price p and fixed cost F."""
    return p * q - fixed - UNIT_COST * q - CAPACITY_SLOPE * q * q


def firm_grid_maximiser(p: Fraction, fixed: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The output on the grid j / 14 for j = 0 to 140 with the largest profit at price p."""
    best_q, best_pi = Fraction(0), None
    for j in range(141):
        q = Fraction(j, 14)
        pi = profit(q, p, fixed)
        if best_pi is None or pi > best_pi:
            best_q, best_pi = q, pi
    return best_q


def main() -> None:
    p_star = equilibrium_price()
    q_star = demand(p_star)
    print(f"demand D(p) = {A} - {B} p; market supply S(p) = {S_SLOPE} (p - {UNIT_COST}) for p >= {UNIT_COST}")
    print(f"equilibrium price p* = {p_star}; equilibrium quantity Q* = {q_star}")
    print(f"D(p*) = {demand(p_star)}, S(p*) = {supply(p_star)}, market clears: {demand(p_star) == supply(p_star)}")
    print(f"a > b c ({A} > {B * UNIT_COST}): {A > B * UNIT_COST}, so p* > c: {p_star > UNIT_COST}")
    grid = [Fraction(i, 7) for i in range(141)]
    below = sum(excess_demand(p) > 0 for p in grid if p < p_star)
    at = sum(excess_demand(p) == 0 for p in grid)
    above = sum(excess_demand(p) < 0 for p in grid if p > p_star)
    print(
        f"grid p = i / 7, i = 0 to 140: excess demand positive below p* at {below} points, "
        f"zero at {at} point, negative above p* at {above} points"
    )
    price_end = Fraction(20)
    print(
        f"at p = {price_end}: D = {demand(price_end)}, S = {supply(price_end)}, "
        f"excess demand {excess_demand(price_end)}"
    )
    one_firm = firm_supply(p_star)
    print(f"one firm's supply at p*: {one_firm}; market supply S(p*) / one firm's supply: {supply(p_star) / one_firm}")
    q_free = firm_grid_maximiser(p_star, Fraction(0))
    q_fixed = firm_grid_maximiser(p_star, Fraction(100))
    print(
        f"firm grid maximiser at p*: F=0 gives q={q_free}, F=100 gives q={q_fixed}; "
        f"both equal 48/7: {q_free == Fraction(48, 7) and q_fixed == Fraction(48, 7)}"
    )


main()

The equilibrium of Example 1.5 is exact, demand equals supply at it, and the excess demand changes sign once, from positive to negative, across the 141 grid prices. That is a finite check of the sign pattern of Remark 1.4 on these prices and this market, not a proof for all prices; Proposition 1.3 gives the proof. The firm check shows that the fixed cost leaves the output unchanged, as Proposition 2.1 states, and it shows that the market quantity is ten firms' output, not one firm's.

3Where the canon breaks

The equilibrium above rests on the Setting, and four limits apply to it.

  • The number of firms is fixed. The model holds \(n\) fixed, so it describes a short-run market. Whether firms enter or leave until profit is zero is not modelled here, and the profit at \(p^*\) is not zero in general: with \(F = 0\) it is \((p^* - c)^2/(4k) > 0\) for Example 1.5.
  • Uniqueness comes from the assumptions. Proposition 1.3 gets a single equilibrium from the strict decrease of \(Z\), which holds because demand is strictly decreasing and supply is nondecreasing. Without monotonicity, a zero of excess demand need not be unique. This lesson does not construct such a market.
  • The sign pattern is not a stability theorem. Remark 1.4 gives the direction of a price rule, not its convergence. The lesson does not study how fast prices move or whether a particular rule converges.
  • Price taking is assumed. Each firm takes the price as given, as in Lesson 2. A market in which firms set prices through their own output is not this market.

4Misconceptions

  1. “At a price above the equilibrium, the market is short of goods.”

    Above \(p^*\) the market has excess supply. In Example 1.5 at \(p = 20\), demand is \(D(20) = 60\), supply is \(S(20) = 5 \cdot 18 = 90\), and \(Z(20) = -30\). Lab 1 prints this value. A shortage happens below \(p^*\), where \(Z > 0\) (Remark 1.4).

  2. “Setting one firm's supply equal to demand gives the market equilibrium.”

    At \(p^* = 110/7\), one firm supplies \(48/7\), while demand is \(480/7\). The market quantity is \(n\) times one firm's supply (Remark 2.2), so the market equation uses \(S(p) = n\,q(p)\).

  3. “A higher fixed cost for each firm raises the equilibrium price.”

    The fixed cost does not enter the output of any firm, by Lesson 2, Proposition 1.5, so it does not enter the market supply or the equilibrium (Proposition 2.1). Lab 1 prints the same firm output, \(48/7\), for \(F = 0\) and \(F = 100\).

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · An equilibrium with other numbers

Demand is \(D(p) = 50 - p\), and market supply is \(S(p) = 4(p - 1)\) for \(p \ge 1\). Find \(p^*\) and \(Q^*\).

Solution

Here \(a = 50\), \(b = 1\), \(s = 4\) and \(c = 1\), and \(a > bc\). Proposition 1.3 gives \(p^* = (50 + 4)/(1 + 4) = 54/5\) and \(Q^* = 50 - 54/5 = 196/5\). Check: \(4(54/5 - 1) = 4 \cdot 49/5 = 196/5\), which equals \(Q^*\).

M2 · Excess demand at a given price

For the market of M1, find \(Z(12)\) and say whether the market is in excess demand or excess supply at \(p = 12\).

Solution

\(D(12) = 38\) and \(S(12) = 4 \cdot 11 = 44\). So \(Z(12) = 38 - 44 = -6\). The value is negative, so the market has excess supply at \(p = 12\), which is above \(p^* = 54/5\), as Remark 1.4 requires.

M3 · How many firms give the slope

Each firm has \(k = 1/2\). How many identical firms are needed for market supply \(S(p) = 4(p - c)\)?

Solution

The market slope is \(s = n/(2k)\). With \(k = 1/2\), \(2k = 1\), so \(s = n\). Setting \(s = 4\) gives \(n = 4\) firms.

Proof

P1 · The sign pattern

Prove that for \(0 \le p_1 < p_2\), \(Z(p_1) > Z(p_2)\). Use only Step 1 of the proof of Proposition 1.3, and say which step uses \(b > 0\) and which uses \(s > 0\).

Solution

On \([0, c]\) and on \([c, \infty)\), \(Z\) is linear with negative slope, and it is continuous at \(c\). A continuous function that is decreasing on each of two adjacent intervals is decreasing on their union, so \(Z(p_1) > Z(p_2)\). The slope on \([0, c]\) is \(-b\), which is negative because \(b > 0\). The slope on \([c, \infty)\) is \(-(b + s)\), negative because \(b > 0\) and \(s > 0\).

P2 · No trade when \(a \le bc\)

Under the Setting except \(a > bc\), show that the equilibrium quantity is zero.

Solution

If \(a \le bc\), then \(Z(c) = a - bc \le 0\). On \([0, c]\), \(Z(p) = a - bp\) has its zero at \(p = a/b \le c\), where supply is \(0\). At that price \(D = 0\) and \(S = 0\), so \(p = a/b\) is the equilibrium and \(Q^* = 0\). By Step 1 of Proposition 1.3 this is the only zero, so no trade takes place.

P3 · Market supply is nondecreasing in price

Prove that \(S(p) = s\max(0, p - c)\) is nondecreasing in \(p\), and that \(S(p) = 0\) for \(p \le c\).

Solution

For \(p \le c\), \(\max(0, p - c) = 0\), so \(S(p) = 0\). For \(p > c\), \(S(p) = s(p - c)\), which is positive and increasing because \(s > 0\). At \(p = c\) both pieces give 0, so the function is continuous and nondecreasing on the whole line.

Empirical

E1 · The printed equilibrium (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the equilibrium price and quantity of Example 1.5. Read the printed values and check them against Proposition 1.3.

Solution

The lab prints \(p^* = 110/7\) and \(Q^* = 480/7\). Proposition 1.3 gives \(p^* = (100 + 5 \cdot 2)/(2 + 5) = 110/7\), and \(Q^* = 100 - 2 \cdot 110/7 = 480/7\). The printed values are the exact fractions of the formula.

E2 · Excess supply at \(p = 20\) (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the excess demand at \(p = 20\) as \(-30\). Recompute it from the demand and supply of Example 1.5.

Solution

\(D(20) = 100 - 40 = 60\) and \(S(20) = 5 \cdot (20 - 2) = 90\). So \(Z(20) = 60 - 90 = -30\). The lab prints the same value, which is an excess supply, as Misconception 1 says.

E3 · Counting the grid signs (Lab 1)

Lab 1 reports that excess demand is positive at 110 of the 141 grid prices, zero at one, and negative at 30. Explain these counts from the grid \(i/7\), \(i = 0\) to \(140\), and from \(p^* = 110/7\).

Solution

The grid price \(i/7\) equals \(p^* = 110/7\) at \(i = 110\). Prices with \(i = 0\) to \(109\) lie below \(p^*\), which is 110 prices, and they have positive excess demand by Remark 1.4. Prices with \(i = 111\) to \(140\) lie above \(p^*\), which is 30 prices, and they have negative excess demand. The total is \(110 + 1 + 30 = 141\).

Research

R1 · Signs of the closed-form derivatives

Using the closed form of Proposition 1.3, find the signs of \(\partial p^*/\partial a\) and \(\partial p^*/\partial b\) under the Setting.

Solution

From \(p^* = (a + sc)/(b + s)\), \(\partial p^*/\partial a = 1/(b + s) > 0\), and \(\partial p^*/\partial b = -(a + sc)/(b + s)^2 < 0\), since \(a + sc > 0\). A higher demand intercept raises the price, and a steeper demand (larger \(b\)) lowers it. Lesson 4 treats how an equilibrium moves when a parameter moves.

R2 · Supply through the origin

Set \(c = 0\) in the Setting. Find \(p^*\) and \(Q^*\), and state the condition that makes the equilibrium quantity positive.

Solution

With \(c = 0\), Proposition 1.3 gives \(p^* = a/(b + s)\) and \(Q^* = sa/(b + s)\). The quantity is positive exactly when \(a > 0\), which the Setting assumes, and the condition \(a > bc\) becomes \(a > 0\).

R3 · Existence and uniqueness for general curves

Let \(D\) be continuous and strictly decreasing, let \(S\) be continuous and nondecreasing on \([0, \infty)\), with \(D(0) > S(0)\) and \(D(\bar p) < S(\bar p)\) for some \(\bar p\). Prove that there is exactly one \(p^*\) with \(D(p^*) = S(p^*)\).

Solution

Let \(Z = D - S\). It is continuous, and \(Z(0) > 0 > Z(\bar p)\). By the intermediate value theorem there is a zero \(p^*\) in \((0, \bar p)\). Since \(D\) is strictly decreasing and \(S\) is nondecreasing, \(Z\) is strictly decreasing, so it has at most one zero. Strict decrease of \(D\) together with monotonicity of \(S\) gives uniqueness, and continuity of both curves gives the zero. The linear case of Proposition 1.3 is a special case.
