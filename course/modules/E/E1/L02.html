A firm chooses how much to produce, and the choice depends on the price it receives and on what production costs. This lesson studies one firm. Lesson 3 uses its supply function (Definition 2.1) to construct a market supply. It takes a firm that takes its price as given (Definition 1.1) and whose cost has a fixed part and a part that rises with output. It derives the profit-maximizing output, shows that at a positive output the price equals the rate at which total cost rises with output (Definition 1.2), and shows that the fixed cost leaves the output unchanged. Lab 1 checks each case on a grid of outputs.

1Profit maximization for a price-taking firm

Setting (one firm, price taken as given)

A firm sells output \(q \ge 0\) at a price \(p > 0\). Its total cost is

\[ C(q) = F + cq + kq^2, \qquad F \ge 0,\ c \ge 0,\ k > 0. \]

Its profit is \(\pi(q) = pq - C(q)\). The firm treats \(p\) as given, so \(p\) does not change with \(q\). Each proposition and remark in Sections 1 and 2 assumes this setting unless it says otherwise.

Definition 1.1 (price taking)

A price-taking firm is one that treats the market price as fixed when it chooses its output. Its own output does not move the price.

Definition 1.2 (producer profit maximization)

Profit maximization is the problem of choosing an output \(q \ge 0\) at which \(\pi(q)\) is largest. The marginal cost is \(\mathrm{MC}(q) = C'(q) = c + 2kq\), the rate at which total cost rises with output, by the rule for the derivative of a polynomial (derivative, M2).

Proposition 1.3 (the profit-maximizing output)

Under the Setting, the profit-maximizing output is unique and equals

\[ q^* = \max\Bigl(0,\ \frac{p - c}{2k}\Bigr). \]

If \(p > c\), then \(q^* = (p - c)/(2k) > 0\) and \(\mathrm{MC}(q^*) = p\). If \(p \le c\), then \(q^* = 0\).

Proof

Let \(q_0 = (p - c)/(2k)\) and \(\pi_0 = (p - c)^2/(4k) - F\). Expanding the right side below shows that for every real \(q\),

\[ \pi(q) = \pi_0 - k\,(q - q_0)^2. \]

To check it, note that \(-k(q - q_0)^2 = -kq^2 + (p - c)q - (p - c)^2/(4k)\), so \(\pi_0 - k(q - q_0)^2 = -kq^2 + (p - c)q - F = pq - C(q)\). For \(q \ge 0\), the function \(-k(q - q_0)^2\) is largest at the point of \([0, \infty)\) nearest to \(q_0\), which is \(\max(0, q_0)\), and that point is unique. So \(q^* = \max(0, q_0)\). If \(p > c\), then \(q_0 > 0\) and \(\mathrm{MC}(q_0) = c + 2k \cdot (p - c)/(2k) = p\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.4 (price 10, unit cost 2, fixed cost 12)

With \(p = 10\), \(c = 2\), \(k = 1\) and \(F = 12\), Proposition 1.3 gives \(q^* = 4\). The cost is \(C(4) = 12 + 8 + 16 = 36\), and the profit is \(40 - 36 = 4\). The marginal cost at \(q = 4\) is \(2 + 2 \cdot 4 = 10\), which equals the price. Lab 1 computes the same output from a grid.

Proposition 1.5 (the fixed cost does not change the output)

Under the Setting, two fixed costs \(F\) and \(F'\) with the same \(p\), \(c\) and \(k\) give the same profit-maximizing output. Their profit functions differ by the constant \(F' - F\): the profit under \(F'\) equals the profit under \(F\) minus \(F' - F\) at every output.

Proof

The output \(q^*\) in Proposition 1.3 does not contain \(F\). The fixed cost enters \(\pi_0\) only, and \(\pi_0\) is a constant that does not depend on \(q\). Let \(\pi\) and \(\pi'\) be the profit functions with \(F\) and \(F'\). Then \(\pi'(q) = \pi(q) - (F' - F)\) for every \(q\). Subtracting a constant does not move the maximizer, so the output is the same, and the maximum profit under \(F'\) equals the maximum under \(F\) minus \(F' - F\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.6 (producing beats shutting down)

Shutting down means producing \(q = 0\), which gives profit \(-F\). For \(p \ge c\), the profit at \(q^*\) is \(\pi(q^*) = (p - c)^2/(4k) - F\), so the difference from shutting down is \(\pi(q^*) - \pi(0) = (p - c)^2/(4k) \ge 0\). For \(p < c\), \(q^* = 0\) and the difference is 0. The firm therefore produces, even when \(\pi(q^*) < 0\), provided \(p > c\). Example 1.4 has positive profit. With \(F = 100\) in its place, the profit at \(q^* = 4\) is \(-84\), which is still better than \(-100\) at \(q = 0\).

Remark 1.7 (zero quadratic cost: no optimum when \(p > c\))

Suppose \(k = 0\), so \(\pi(q) = (p - c)q - F\). If \(p > c\), \(\pi\) grows without bound as \(q\) grows, so no profit-maximizing output exists. If \(p = c\), \(\pi\) is constant at \(-F\), and every output is optimal. If \(p < c\), \(\pi\) falls as \(q\) grows, and \(q = 0\) is the optimum. In the first two cases the first-order condition \(\pi'(q) = 0\), which here reads \(p = \mathrm{MC}(q)\), either has no solution or holds at every output, so it does not single out an output, which is why Proposition 1.3 needs \(k > 0\).

2Supply of a price-taking firm

Definition 2.1 (supply function)

The supply function of the firm gives, for each price \(p\), the profit-maximizing output \(q(p)\) from Proposition 1.3.

Proposition 2.2 (supply is nondecreasing and equals marginal cost above \(c\))

Under the Setting, \(q(p) = \max(0, (p - c)/(2k))\). The function \(q\) is nondecreasing in \(p\). For \(p \ge c\), \(q(p)\) is the unique output at which \(\mathrm{MC}(q) = p\).

Proof

The formula is Proposition 1.3 with \(p\) in place of the price. The map \(p \mapsto (p - c)/(2k)\) increases with \(p\), and it is \(0\) at \(p = c\). So \(\max(0, (p - c)/(2k))\) does not decrease as \(p\) rises. For \(p \ge c\), \(\mathrm{MC}(q) = c + 2kq = p\) has the unique solution \(q = (p - c)/(2k)\), which is the output of the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Profit-maximizing output on a grid of outputs

Predicted before running. The lab evaluates profit at 73 outputs \(j/12\) for \(j = 0\) to \(72\), which runs from 0 to 6. In cases 1 to 3 the grid maximiser should be the closed form of Proposition 1.3: output 4, 4 and 4/3, with profits 4, 3 and 16/3. Price should equal marginal cost at each closed-form output, and producing should beat shutting down in each case. Case 4 is case 1 with \(F = 100\), so its output should equal case 1's output. Then the profit at output \(\sqrt{12}\) should be below the profit at output 4. Misconception 1 shows that \(\sqrt{12}\) is the output at which average cost \(C(q)/q\) is lowest in case 1.

"""E1 lab L02: the profit-maximising output of a price-taking firm, on a grid, against the closed form.

Standard library only (fractions, math). No randomness and no network. The cost of output q is
C(q) = F + c q + k q^2, with fixed cost F >= 0, unit cost c >= 0 and k > 0, in the currency unit of the
example. For price p the profit is pi(q) = p q - C(q). The lab evaluates pi exactly on the grid
q = j / 12 for j = 0 to 72 (outputs 0 to 6) and reports the grid maximiser. It compares that maximiser with the
closed form q* = (p - c) / (2 k), the output at which price equals marginal cost MC(q) = c + 2 k q. Every case
has q* on the grid. The lab also computes the profit at q = 0 (shutdown, profit -F), and it prints whether cases
1 and 4 share p, c and k, with the grid output of each. Last, for case 1 it computes the profit at sqrt(F/k),
the output that minimises average cost C(q) / q, in floating point to four decimals, and compares it with the
profit at q*. The check is finite: it tests these cases only, and the proof for every input in the Setting
(p > 0, F >= 0, c >= 0, k > 0) is on the page.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction

STEPS_PER_UNIT = 12  # grid spacing 1 / 12 of a unit of output
MAX_OUTPUT_STEP = 72  # grid points j = 0 to 72, that is outputs 0 to 6


def cost(q: Fraction, fixed: Fraction, c: Fraction, k: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Total cost C(q) = F + c q + k q^2 of output q >= 0."""
    return fixed + c * q + k * q * q


def profit(q: Fraction, p: Fraction, fixed: Fraction, c: Fraction, k: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Profit pi(q) = p q - C(q) of a firm that takes the price p as given."""
    return p * q - cost(q, fixed, c, k)


def closed_form(p: Fraction, c: Fraction, k: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The profit-maximising output q* = (p - c) / (2 k) when p >= c and k > 0."""
    return (p - c) / (2 * k)


def marginal_cost(q: Fraction, c: Fraction, k: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Marginal cost MC(q) = dC/dq = c + 2 k q."""
    return c + 2 * k * q


def grid_optimum(p: Fraction, fixed: Fraction, c: Fraction, k: Fraction) -> tuple[int, Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Index, output and profit of the grid point with the largest profit."""
    best_j, best_q, best_pi = 0, Fraction(0), None
    for j in range(MAX_OUTPUT_STEP + 1):
        q = Fraction(j, STEPS_PER_UNIT)
        pi = profit(q, p, fixed, c, k)
        if best_pi is None or pi > best_pi:
            best_j, best_q, best_pi = j, q, pi
    assert best_pi is not None
    return best_j, best_q, best_pi


CASES = (
    # (p, c, k, F): the reference case
    (Fraction(10), Fraction(2), Fraction(1), Fraction(12)),
    (Fraction(7), Fraction(3), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(5)),
    (Fraction(9), Fraction(1), Fraction(3), Fraction(0)),
    (Fraction(10), Fraction(2), Fraction(1), Fraction(100)),
)


def main() -> None:
    print(f"grid: outputs j / {STEPS_PER_UNIT} for j = 0 to {MAX_OUTPUT_STEP}, so outputs 0 to 6")
    for n, (p, c, k, fixed) in enumerate(CASES, start=1):
        qs = closed_form(p, c, k)
        j, q_grid, pi_grid = grid_optimum(p, fixed, c, k)
        pi_star = profit(qs, p, fixed, c, k)
        print(f"case {n}: p={p}, c={c}, k={k}, F={fixed}: closed form q*={qs}, profit {pi_star}")
        print(f"  grid maximiser: j={j}, q={q_grid}, profit {pi_grid}; equals closed form: {q_grid == qs}")
        print(f"  price equals marginal cost at q*: {marginal_cost(qs, c, k) == p}")
        print(
            f"  shutdown profit -F = {profit(Fraction(0), p, fixed, c, k)}; producing q* is better: "
            f"{pi_star > profit(Fraction(0), p, fixed, c, k)}"
        )
    base_case, changed_case = CASES[0], CASES[3]
    base = grid_optimum(base_case[0], base_case[3], base_case[1], base_case[2])[1]
    fixed_cost_changed = grid_optimum(changed_case[0], changed_case[3], changed_case[1], changed_case[2])[1]
    same_inputs = base_case[:3] == changed_case[:3]
    print(
        f"cases 1 and 4 share p, c and k: {same_inputs}; fixed costs {base_case[3]} and {changed_case[3]}; "
        f"grid output {base} and {fixed_cost_changed}, equal: {base == fixed_cost_changed}"
    )
    p, c, k, fixed = CASES[0]
    q_avg = math.sqrt(float(fixed / k))
    pi_avg = float(p) * q_avg - (float(fixed) + float(c) * q_avg + float(k) * q_avg * q_avg)
    print(
        f"case 1 at the minimum-average-cost output sqrt(F/k) = {q_avg:.4f}: profit {pi_avg:.4f}, "
        f"below the profit {float(profit(closed_form(p, c, k), p, fixed, c, k)):.4f} at q*"
    )

3Where the canon breaks

The results above rest on the Setting, and four limits apply to them.

  • Price taking is assumed. A firm whose own output moves the price faces a different first-order condition, and Lesson 5 treats pricing by a single seller, in which the firm sets the price through its output.
  • The output rule needs \(k > 0\). Remark 1.7 shows that with constant marginal cost and a price above unit cost no profit-maximizing output exists.
  • The fixed-cost result holds in a static model. Proposition 1.5 compares two fixed costs, \(F\) and \(F'\), with the same price and cost parameters. The model has no time dimension, so it does not model whether a firm with a fixed cost stays in business over a longer horizon.
  • The cost function is quadratic by assumption. A cost function with a different marginal cost can give a different supply curve, but a change in the fixed cost alone does not (Proposition 1.5). The closed form of Proposition 1.3 is specific to the quadratic cost of the Setting.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A firm maximizes profit by producing where its average cost is lowest.”

    Average cost \(\mathrm{AC}(q) = C(q)/q = F/q + c + kq\) has derivative \(\mathrm{AC}'(q) = k - F/q^2\) for \(q > 0\). This is negative for \(q < \sqrt{F/k}\) and positive for \(q > \sqrt{F/k}\), so for \(F > 0\) average cost is lowest on \(q > 0\) at \(q = \sqrt{F/k}\), which is \(\sqrt{12}\) in case 1. With \(F = 0\), average cost \(c + kq\) rises for every \(q > 0\). The profit at \(\sqrt{12}\) is \(10\sqrt{12} - (12 + 2\sqrt{12} + 12) = 8\sqrt{12} - 24 = 16\sqrt{3} - 24\), about 3.71, while the profit at \(q^* = 4\) is 4. Lab 1 prints both values. The optimum is where price equals marginal cost, not where average cost is lowest.

  2. “A higher fixed cost lowers the profit-maximizing output.”

    Proposition 1.5 shows that the fixed cost changes only the profit level: replacing \(F\) by \(F'\) changes the profit at every output by the constant \(-(F' - F)\). Case 4 of Lab 1 has \(F = 100\) and the same output 4 as case 1, which has \(F = 12\).

  3. “If profit is negative, the firm should shut down.”

    Shutting down gives profit \(-F\), not zero, because the fixed cost is paid either way. At \(F = 100\) the firm earns \(-84\) at \(q^* = 4\) and \(-100\) at \(q = 0\), so producing is better (Remark 1.6). In this model shutting down never earns more than producing, since \(\pi(q^*) \ge -F\) at every price, with equality when \(p \le c\) and \(q^* = 0\). A shutdown rule needs a cost structure in which shutting down saves the fixed cost, as in Problem R3.

Mechanics

M1 · Output and profit with no fixed cost

A price-taking firm has \(C(q) = 4q + 2q^2\) and faces price \(p = 12\). Find its profit-maximizing output, its profit, and check that price equals marginal cost there.

Solution

Here \(F = 0\), \(c = 4\) and \(k = 2\). By Proposition 1.3, \(q^* = (12 - 4)/(2 \cdot 2) = 2\). The profit is \(12 \cdot 2 - (4 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 4) = 24 - 16 = 8\). Also \(\mathrm{MC}(2) = 4 + 4 \cdot 2 = 12\), which equals the price. The closed form \((p - c)^2/(4k) - F = 64/8 = 8\) agrees with the profit.

M2 · Producing with a loss

A firm has \(C(q) = 50 + 3q + q^2\) and faces price \(p = 9\). Find its profit-maximizing output and its profit, and say whether it produces or shuts down.

Solution

Here \(F = 50\), \(c = 3\), \(k = 1\). By Proposition 1.3, \(q^* = (9 - 3)/2 = 3\). The profit is \(27 - (50 + 9 + 9) = 27 - 68 = -41\). Shutting down gives \(-50\). Since \(-41 > -50\), the firm produces at a loss, as Remark 1.6 states.

M3 · Supply at a given price

For \(c = 3\) and \(k = 1/2\), find the supply \(q(7)\) and the supply at price 2.

Solution

By Proposition 2.2, \(q(7) = (7 - 3)/(2 \cdot 1/2) = 4\). At price 2, which is below \(c = 3\), the formula gives \(\max(0, (2 - 3)/1) = 0\), so the firm supplies nothing. Both values follow from the formula, and the second is the zero region of the supply function.

Proof

P1 · Fixed cost and the optimum

Prove Proposition 1.5 directly from the definition of profit, without using the closed form of Proposition 1.3.

Solution

Let \(\pi_F(q) = pq - F - cq - kq^2\) and \(\pi_{F'}(q) = pq - F' - cq - kq^2\). Then \(\pi_{F'}(q) = \pi_F(q) + (F - F')\) for every \(q\). If \(q^\dagger\) maximizes \(\pi_F\) over \(q \ge 0\), then \(\pi_{F'}(q^\dagger) \ge \pi_{F'}(q)\) for every \(q \ge 0\), because the two functions differ by the same constant. So \(q^\dagger\) also maximizes \(\pi_{F'}\). The two maximizers coincide, and the maximum profit under \(F'\) equals the maximum under \(F\) minus \(F' - F\).

P2 · No optimum with constant marginal cost

Prove that if \(k = 0\) and \(p > c\), no output maximizes profit, using only the definition of \(\pi\).

Solution

With \(k = 0\), \(\pi(q) = (p - c)q - F\). For any output \(q\), the output \(q + 1\) gives \(\pi(q + 1) = \pi(q) + (p - c) > \pi(q)\), because \(p - c > 0\). So no output is at least as profitable as every other, and no maximiser exists. This is the argument of Remark 1.7.

P3 · Supply is nondecreasing in price

Prove that the supply function \(q(p)\) of Proposition 2.2 is nondecreasing in \(p\) for all \(p > 0\), with the two pieces joined at \(p = c\).

Solution

For \(p \le c\), \(q(p) = 0\). For \(p > c\), \(q(p) = (p - c)/(2k)\), which is positive and increasing in \(p\). The two pieces agree at \(p = c\), both giving 0, and every value of the second piece is at least 0. So for \(p_1 < p_2\), \(q(p_1) \le q(p_2)\) in every case: both zero, both on the increasing piece, or \(p_1 \le c < p_2\) with \(q(p_1) = 0 < q(p_2)\).

Empirical

E1 · Case 3 of Lab 1

Lab 1 prints the output and profit for case 3, with \(p = 9\), \(c = 1\), \(k = 3\) and \(F = 0\). Read the printed values and check them against Proposition 1.3.

Solution

Proposition 1.3 gives \(q^* = (9 - 1)/(2 \cdot 3) = 8/6 = 4/3\), and the profit is \(9 \cdot 4/3 - (1 \cdot 4/3 + 3 \cdot 16/9) = 12 - 4/3 - 16/3 = 16/3\). These are the exact values the lab prints as fractions. The grid point is \(j = 16\), since \(4/3 = 16/12\).

E2 · The average-cost comparison (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints a profit of 3.7128 at output \(\sqrt{12}\) in case 1, and 4.0000 at \(q^* = 4\). Confirm the first value from the formula \(16\sqrt{3} - 24\), and state the difference.

Solution

\(\sqrt{3} \approx 1.7320508\), so \(16\sqrt{3} \approx 27.712813\), and \(16\sqrt{3} - 24 \approx 3.712813\), which is 3.7128 to four decimals. The difference from the optimal profit 4 is \(4 - (16\sqrt{3} - 24) = 28 - 16\sqrt{3} \approx 0.2872\). The lab computes the profit at \(\sqrt{12}\) in floating point, and the formula confirms the printed value.

E3 · Equal outputs in cases 1 and 4 (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the grid output for case 1 and case 4. Explain why they are equal, and say what the printed profits show about the fixed cost.

Solution

Cases 1 and 4 have the same \(p = 10\), \(c = 2\) and \(k = 1\), and differ only in \(F\) (12 and 100). By Proposition 1.5 the output is the same, which the lab shows: both are output 4. The printed profits are 4 and \(-84\), which differ by \(88 = 100 - 12\), the change in the fixed cost.

Research

R1 · The profit function of the price

For \(p \ge c\), let \(\pi^*(p) = \pi(q(p))\) be the maximum profit at price \(p\). Show that \(d\pi^*/dp = q(p)\), and that the slope \(d\pi^*/dp\) increases with \(p\).

Solution

By Proposition 1.3, \(\pi^*(p) = (p - c)^2/(4k) - F\) for \(p \ge c\). Its derivative is \(d\pi^*/dp = (p - c)/(2k) = q(p)\), which is the envelope result: the change in the optimal profit equals the output at the optimum, because the output's own effect on profit vanishes at the optimum. The second derivative is \(1/(2k) > 0\), so the slope \(d\pi^*/dp\) is increasing on \(p \ge c\).

R2 · The envelope identity without the closed form

Assuming the implicit-function theorem (Assumption 1 in the solution), and using only the first-order condition \(p = \mathrm{MC}(q^*)\) and the chain rule, show that \(d\pi^*/dp = q^*\) for \(p > c\).

Solution

Assumption 1 (implicit-function theorem, an M2 result that this page states and does not prove): if \(G(q, p) = 0\) at \((q_0, p_0)\), \(G\) is continuously differentiable, and \(\partial G/\partial q \ne 0\) there, then near \((q_0, p_0)\) the equation defines \(q\) as a differentiable function of \(p\).

Let \(G(q, p) = p - \mathrm{MC}(q) = p - c - 2kq\), so the first-order condition is \(G(q^*(p), p) = 0\), and \(\partial G/\partial q = -2k \ne 0\). The second derivative of \(\pi\) in \(q\) is \(-2k < 0\), so the first-order condition identifies the maximiser. Since \(G\) is linear in \(q\), it has one solution for each \(p\), and Assumption 1 gives a differentiable branch of solutions near each \(p > c\) that coincides with \(q^*(p)\). So \(q^*\) is differentiable for \(p > c\).

Write \(\pi^*(p) = p\,q^*(p) - C(q^*(p))\). Differentiate by the chain rule: \(d\pi^*/dp = q^* + (p - \mathrm{MC}(q^*))\,dq^*/dp\). The first-order condition makes the second term zero, so \(d\pi^*/dp = q^*\).

R3 · A shutdown price with a fixed cost paid only when producing

Keep the cost \(C(q) = F + cq + kq^2\) for \(q > 0\), but suppose the fixed cost is paid only if the firm produces, so that shutting down gives profit 0. For \(p > c\), find the lowest price at which producing is at least as profitable as shutting down.

Solution

Producing at the optimum gives profit \((p - c)^2/(4k) - F\), and shutting down gives 0. The firm produces when \((p - c)^2/(4k) \ge F\). For \(p > c\) this is \(p - c \ge 2\sqrt{kF}\), so the lowest such price is \(c + 2\sqrt{kF}\). In case 1 this is \(2 + 2\sqrt{12}\), about 8.93, below the price 10 of Example 1.4, so the firm produces there.

  4. At a profit-maximizing output with \(p > c\), which holds?

    Options

    Proposition 1.3: \(\mathrm{MC}(q^*) = p\). Average cost is not the test.

  5. How does a higher fixed cost change the profit-maximizing output?

    Options

    Proposition 1.5: the fixed cost shifts profit by a constant and leaves the maximizer unchanged.

  6. For \(C(q) = 12 + 2q + q^2\), what is the marginal cost at \(q = 4\)?

    \(\mathrm{MC}(q) = c + 2kq = 2 + 2 \cdot 4 = 10\), which equals the price 10.

  7. If \(k = 0\) and \(p > c\), what happens to profit as output grows?

    Options

    Remark 1.7: with \(k = 0\), profit is \((p - c)q - F\), which is unbounded above when \(p > c\).

  8. For \(c = 3\) and \(k = 1/2\), what is the supply at price \(p = 7\)?

    Proposition 2.2: \(q(7) = (7 - 3)/(2 \cdot 1/2) = 4\).

  9. A price-taking firm faces a price below its unit cost \(c\). What output maximizes its profit?

    Options

    Proposition 1.3: \(q^* = \max(0, (p - c)/(2k))\), which is 0 when \(p \le c\).

  10. For \(p = 7\), \(c = 3\), \(k = 1/2\) and \(F = 5\), what is the profit at the optimal output?

    At \(q = 4\), revenue is 28 and cost is \(5 + 12 + 8 = 25\), so the profit is 3. Lab 1 prints it as case 2.

  11. Why can a firm with negative profit still prefer to produce?

    Options

    Remark 1.6: the shutdown profit is \(-F\). With \(F = 100\) the profit at \(q^* = 4\) is \(-84\), which is higher.
