A consumer with a fixed income chooses how much of each good to buy. The bundle the consumer chooses at given prices and income is the object this lesson studies. This lesson takes a utility function that is the product of the two quantities, so the steps can be computed exactly. It derives the optimal bundle, the condition that the ratio of the two partial derivatives of utility equals the price ratio at that bundle, and the limits of that condition. Section 2 (Definition 2.1) names that ratio the marginal rate of substitution. Lab 1 checks the closed forms on a grid of affordable bundles.

1The consumer problem and its solution

Setting (two goods, one budget)

Two goods have quantities \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\) and prices \(p_x > 0\) and \(p_y > 0\). The consumer has income \(m > 0\). The utility of the bundle \((x, y)\) is \(u(x, y) = xy\). The affordable set is

\[ B = \{(x, y) : x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0,\ p_x x + p_y y \le m\}. \]

Its upper boundary, the set of bundles with \(p_x x + p_y y = m\), is the budget line. Each proposition and remark in Sections 1 and 2 assumes this setting unless it says otherwise.

Definition 1.1 (utility maximization)

Utility maximization is the problem of choosing a bundle in \(B\) at which \(u\) takes its largest value on \(B\). A bundle that solves the problem is an optimal bundle, and the largest value of \(u\) on \(B\) is the maximum utility \(\bar{u}\).

Assumption 1 (the extreme value theorem)

Stated here and not proved on this page: a continuous function on a nonempty, closed and bounded subset of the plane attains a largest value there. Proposition 1.2 uses it. Proposition 1.4 does not, because it exhibits the optimal bundle and bounds the utility of every bundle in \(B\) directly.

Proposition 1.2 (an optimum exists)

Assuming Assumption 1, and under the Setting, \(u\) attains a largest value on \(B\).

Proof

The set \(B\) is nonempty, since \((0, 0) \in B\). It is closed, because it is defined by non-strict inequalities between continuous functions. It is bounded, because every point of \(B\) has \(0 \le x \le m/p_x\) and \(0 \le y \le m/p_y\). The function \(u\) is continuous. By Assumption 1, a continuous function on a nonempty closed bounded set attains its maximum there. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.3 (the budget binds)

Under the Setting, every optimal bundle \((x, y)\) has \(x > 0\), \(y > 0\), and \(p_x x + p_y y = m\).

Proof

The bundle \((m/(2p_x),\ m/(2p_y))\) lies in \(B\), and its utility is \(m^2/(4p_xp_y) > 0\). So the maximum utility is positive. Any bundle with \(x = 0\) or \(y = 0\) has utility \(0\), so an optimal bundle has \(xy > 0\), which gives \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\). Suppose the optimal bundle has slack \(s = m - p_x x - p_y y > 0\). Put \(t = s/p_x > 0\) and \((x', y) = (x + t,\ y)\). Then \(p_x x' + p_y y = (m - s) + p_x t = m - s + s = m\), so \((x', y) \in B\). Also \(u(x', y) = (x + t)y > xy\), because \(y > 0\). This contradicts optimality, so \(s = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.4 (the optimal bundle)

Under the Setting, the optimal bundle is unique and equals

\[ (\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = \Bigl(\frac{m}{2p_x},\ \frac{m}{2p_y}\Bigr), \qquad \bar{u} = \frac{m^2}{4p_x p_y}. \]

Proof

Let \(x_0 = m/(2p_x)\) and \(y_0 = m/(2p_y)\). For a real number \(x\), put \(f(x) = x\,(m - p_x x)/p_y = (mx - p_x x^2)/p_y\), which is the utility \(x\,y\) at abscissa \(x\) on the budget line, where \(y = (m - p_x x)/p_y\). Completing the square gives the identity, which holds for every real \(x\):

\[ f(x) = \frac{m^2}{4p_xp_y} - \frac{p_x}{p_y}\,(x - x_0)^2. \]

To check it, expand the right side. The coefficient of \(x\) is \((p_x/p_y)\cdot 2x_0 = m/p_y\), and the constant term is \(m^2/(4p_xp_y) - (p_x/p_y)x_0^2 = 0\). Since \(p_x/p_y > 0\), the identity gives \(f(x) \le m^2/(4p_xp_y) = f(x_0)\) for every real \(x\), with equality only at \(x = x_0\).

Now take any bundle \((x, y) \in B\). Put \(s = m - p_x x - p_y y \ge 0\) and \(x' = x + s/p_x \ge x\). Then \(p_x x' + p_y y = m\), so \((x', y)\) lies on the budget line and \(y = (m - p_x x')/p_y\). Therefore \(f(x') = x'y\). Because \(x' \ge x\) and \(y \ge 0\),

\[ u(x, y) = xy \le x'y = f(x') \le f(x_0) = \frac{m^2}{4p_xp_y}. \]

The bundle \((x_0, y_0)\) lies in \(B\), since \(p_x x_0 + p_y y_0 = m/2 + m/2 = m\), and its utility is \(x_0 y_0 = m^2/(4p_xp_y)\). So it is an optimal bundle over \(B\), and the maximum utility is \(\bar{u} = m^2/(4p_xp_y)\). This argument does not use Assumption 1.

For uniqueness, let \((x, y) \in B\) be optimal, so \(u(x, y) = \bar{u}\). Then every inequality in the chain is an equality. So \(f(x') = f(x_0)\), which by the identity forces \(x' = x_0\). The budget then gives \(y = (m - p_x x_0)/p_y = y_0\). Now \(xy = \bar{u} = x_0y_0\) with \(y = y_0 > 0\), so \(x = x_0\). Hence the optimal bundle is unique and equals \((x_0, y_0) = (m/(2p_x), m/(2p_y))\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.5 (the same point by differentiation)

The derivative of \(f\) is \(f'(x) = (m - 2p_x x)/p_y\), and it vanishes at \(x_0\). The second derivative is \(f''(x) = -2p_x/p_y < 0\), so \(f'\) is strictly decreasing on the line, and its zero is the maximum of \(f\). The completing-square identity reaches the same conclusion without derivatives, and it also shows the size of the loss away from the optimum.

Example 1.6 (prices 2 and 3, income 120)

With \(p_x = 2\), \(p_y = 3\) and \(m = 120\), Proposition 1.4 gives \(\bar{x} = 30\), \(\bar{y} = 20\) and \(\bar{u} = 600\). The budget is exhausted, since \(2 \cdot 30 + 3 \cdot 20 = 120\). Lab 1 computes the same bundle from the grid and prints it for this case.

Remark 1.7 (the utility ordering ranks bundles)

The level 600 does not rank bundles on its own: the ordering that \(u\) gives over bundles does. If \(g\) is strictly increasing on the values that \(u\) takes on \(B\), the bundles that maximize \(g(u)\) on \(B\) are the bundles that maximize \(u\), because \(g\) orders those values in the same way. With \(g = \ln\), which is defined only for \(u > 0\), the comparison covers the bundles with \(u > 0\). The maximum of \(u\) is positive (Proposition 1.4), so the optimal bundle is the same, and a different utility representation of the same ranking gives the same choice.

2Marginal rate of substitution and tangency

Definition 2.1 (marginal rate of substitution)

The marginal rate of substitution of good \(x\) for good \(y\) at a bundle is \(\mathrm{MRS} = (\partial u/\partial x)/(\partial u/\partial y)\), when \(\partial u/\partial y \ne 0\). It is the amount of \(y\) that offsets one small unit of \(x\) and leaves utility unchanged.

Proposition 2.2 (the MRS of \(xy\))

Under the Setting, \(\partial u/\partial x = y\) and \(\partial u/\partial y = x\). So for \(x > 0\), \(\mathrm{MRS} = y/x\).

Proof

Hold \(y\) fixed and differentiate \(xy\) with respect to \(x\), which gives \(y\). Hold \(x\) fixed and differentiate with respect to \(y\), which gives \(x\). Dividing \(y\) by \(x\) gives the MRS. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.3 (tangency at the optimum)

Under the Setting, the optimal bundle satisfies \(\mathrm{MRS} = p_x/p_y\).

Proof

By Proposition 1.4, \(\bar{x} = m/(2p_x) > 0\) and \(\bar{y} = m/(2p_y) > 0\), so Proposition 2.2 applies. Then \(\mathrm{MRS} = \bar{y}/\bar{x} = \dfrac{m/(2p_y)}{m/(2p_x)} = \dfrac{p_x}{p_y}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.4 (the condition fails away from the optimum)

Take the case of Example 1.6 and the bundle with \(x = 20\). The budget gives \(y = (120 - 40)/3 = 80/3\). There \(\mathrm{MRS} = (80/3)/20 = 4/3\), while \(p_x/p_y = 2/3\). For a small extra unit of \(x\), the consumer would give up \(4/3\) units of \(y\) and keep utility unchanged. One whole extra unit of \(x\) at constant utility offsets \(80/63\) (about \(1.27\)) units of \(y\), because \((21, 1600/63)\) has the same utility \(1600/3\) as \((20, 80/3)\). The budget trade is different. One more unit of \(x\) costs \(p_x = 2\) of money, which is the proceeds of selling \(2/3\) of a unit of \(y\) at \(p_y = 3\), and it raises utility by \(38/3\) (Problem E2). Because the MRS exceeds the price ratio, a small extra unit of \(x\) is worth more to the consumer than its price in \(y\), so buying more \(x\) along the budget line raises utility. The derivative of \(f\) at \(x = 20\) is \((120 - 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 20)/3 = 40/3 > 0\), by Remark 1.5. Moving along the line raises utility until the MRS falls to \(2/3\), which Proposition 1.4 places at \(x = 30\).

Lab 1 · The consumer optimum on a grid of affordable bundles

Predicted before running. The lab takes 60 equal steps along the budget line in four cases, so there are 61 grid points and the midpoint is point 30. In every case the grid maximiser should be point 30, equal to the closed form of Proposition 1.4, with the budget exhausted and the MRS equal to the price ratio. For the off-optimum bundle \((20, 80/3)\) of case 1 the MRS should be \(4/3\) and the price ratio \(2/3\), so the equality should print False. All numbers are exact fractions.

"""E1 lab L01: the consumer optimum for u(x, y) = x * y on the budget line, against the closed form.

Standard library only (fractions). No randomness and no network. Every number is an exact Fraction and nothing
is rounded. The budget is p_x * x + p_y * y = m with p_x, p_y and m all positive. For each case the lab takes
STEPS = 60 equal steps along the budget line, from x = 0 to x = m / p_x, so grid point 30 is the midpoint of the
line. It reports the grid point with the largest utility, the closed form x_bar = m / (2 p_x), y_bar = m / (2 p_y) and
the marginal rate of substitution y / x at the closed-form bundle, which is the ratio of the marginal utilities
u_x = y and u_y = x. The check is finite: it tests these cases only. The proof that the closed form is the optimum
for every price and income in the Setting (p_x, p_y, m > 0) is on the page.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

STEPS = 60  # equal steps along the budget line; grid point STEPS // 2 is the midpoint x = m / (2 p_x)


def utility(x: Fraction, y: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Utility u(x, y) = x * y for non-negative quantities x and y."""
    return x * y


def budget_y(px: Fraction, py: Fraction, m: Fraction, x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The quantity of good y that exhausts income m when x units of good x are bought."""
    return (m - px * x) / py


def closed_form(px: Fraction, py: Fraction, m: Fraction) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """The optimal bundle x_bar = m / (2 p_x) and y_bar = m / (2 p_y) for u = x * y."""
    return m / (2 * px), m / (2 * py)


def mrs(x: Fraction, y: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Marginal rate of substitution of good x for good y: (du/dx) / (du/dy) = y / x, for x > 0."""
    return y / x


def grid_optimum(px: Fraction, py: Fraction, m: Fraction) -> tuple[int, Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Index, x and utility of the grid point on the budget line with the largest utility."""
    best_i, best_x, best_u = 0, Fraction(0), Fraction(-1)
    for i in range(STEPS + 1):
        x = (m / px) * i / STEPS
        u = utility(x, budget_y(px, py, m, x))
        if u > best_u:
            best_i, best_x, best_u = i, x, u
    return best_i, best_x, best_u


CASES = (
    (Fraction(2), Fraction(3), Fraction(120)),
    (Fraction(1), Fraction(1), Fraction(50)),
    (Fraction(5), Fraction(2), Fraction(70)),
    (Fraction(3), Fraction(4), Fraction(60)),
)


def main() -> None:
    print(f"grid: {STEPS + 1} points on the budget line, x from 0 to m / p_x; the midpoint is point {STEPS // 2}")
    for px, py, m in CASES:
        xs, ys = closed_form(px, py, m)
        us = utility(xs, ys)
        i, x_grid, u_grid = grid_optimum(px, py, m)
        y_grid = budget_y(px, py, m, x_grid)
        agrees = i == STEPS // 2 and x_grid == xs and y_grid == ys
        print(f"case p_x={px}, p_y={py}, m={m}: closed form x_bar={xs}, y_bar={ys}, u_bar={us}")
        print(f"  grid maximiser: point {i}, x={x_grid}, y={y_grid}, u={u_grid}; equals closed form: {agrees}")
        print(f"  budget exhausted at x_bar, y_bar: {px * xs + py * ys == m}")
        print(
            f"  MRS y/x at x_bar, y_bar: {mrs(xs, ys)}; price ratio p_x/p_y: {px / py}; equal: {mrs(xs, ys) == px / py}"
        )
    px, py, m = CASES[0]
    x_off = Fraction(20)
    y_off = budget_y(px, py, m, x_off)
    print(
        f"off the optimum, case p_x={px}, p_y={py}, m={m}, at x={x_off}: y={y_off}, "
        f"MRS={mrs(x_off, y_off)}, price ratio {px / py}, equal: {mrs(x_off, y_off) == px / py}"
    )

The four cases agree with the closed forms: the grid maximiser is the midpoint of the budget line, and at that bundle the budget is exhausted and the MRS equals the price ratio. This is a finite check on four price and income triples, with exact arithmetic and no tolerance. It does not show optimality for other prices or incomes, which Proposition 1.4 proves for every case in the Setting. The off-optimum line confirms Remark 2.4: at \((20, 80/3)\) the MRS is \(4/3\), not \(2/3\), so the tangency condition does not hold there.

3Where the canon breaks

The results of Sections 1 and 2 hold under the Setting, and four limits apply to them.

  • The proof uses the form of the utility function. Proposition 1.4 depends on \(u = xy\) and on the completing-square identity. The lesson does not prove that tangency characterizes the optimum for other utilities. Problem R1 gives a function on a budget line whose tangency point is a minimum, so tangency alone does not identify an optimum.
  • Positive prices and income are assumed. The Setting requires positive prices and income. With a price of zero, or a utility that is zero on an axis, the argument of Proposition 1.3 does not apply, and this lesson does not analyze those cases.
  • Utility levels are not welfare levels. By Remark 1.7, the ordering that \(u\) gives over bundles determines the choice, not the level \(\bar{u}\), and a strictly increasing relabelling of \(u\) leaves the optimal bundle unchanged. A statement that one consumer is twice as well off as another is not supported by a utility level alone.
  • Prices and income are held fixed in each application. Proposition 1.4 gives the optimal bundle at any given prices and income, and problem M3 works one change in income. The lesson does not state the response of the optimal bundle to a change in price as a comparative-static result. Lesson 4 treats how an equilibrium price moves when a demand parameter moves.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Maximizing utility means buying as much of both goods as possible.”

    The budget binds. Proposition 1.3 shows that an optimal bundle exhausts income, and Proposition 1.4 gives the bundle \((30, 20)\) for Example 1.6, where \(2 \cdot 30 + 3 \cdot 20 = 120\). A bundle with more of both goods is not affordable.

  2. “The marginal rate of substitution is always the ratio of the quantities of the two goods.”

    Definition 2.1 gives the MRS as a ratio of marginal utilities. For \(u = xy\) that ratio is \(y/x\) by Proposition 2.2, which is why the mistake is easy to make here. For \(u(x, y) = x + y\), both partial derivatives equal \(1\), so the MRS is \(1\) at every bundle, while \(y/x\) changes from bundle to bundle.

  3. “At any bundle on the budget line, the MRS equals the price ratio.”

    Proposition 2.3 gives the equality at the optimum. At \((20, 80/3)\) on the line of Example 1.6, the MRS is \(4/3\) and the price ratio is \(2/3\), as Lab 1 prints. The consumer gains by moving along the line toward the optimum (Remark 2.4).

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · An optimal bundle with other prices

For \(u = xy\), with \(p_x = 4\), \(p_y = 5\) and \(m = 90\), find the optimal bundle, the maximum utility, and the budget check.

Solution

By Proposition 1.4, \(\bar{x} = 90/(2 \cdot 4) = 45/4\) and \(\bar{y} = 90/(2 \cdot 5) = 9\). The maximum utility is \(\bar{u} = 90^2/(4 \cdot 4 \cdot 5) = 8100/80 = 405/4\), which is also \((45/4) \cdot 9\). The budget check is \(4 \cdot (45/4) + 5 \cdot 9 = 45 + 45 = 90\), so the budget is exhausted as Proposition 1.3 requires.

M2 · The MRS at a given bundle

For \(u = xy\), find the marginal rate of substitution at \((x, y) = (6, 8)\), and say whether the consumer would trade \(y\) for \(x\) at the price ratio \(p_x/p_y = 1\).

Solution

By Proposition 2.2, \(\mathrm{MRS} = y/x = 8/6 = 4/3\). The MRS exceeds the price ratio \(1\), so for a small extra unit of \(x\) the consumer would give up \(4/3\) units of \(y\) to keep utility unchanged. The market requires only \(1\) unit of \(y\) per unit of \(x\) at the price ratio, so the consumer would trade \(y\) for \(x\) at the market rate.

M3 · Doubling income

For \(u = xy\) and fixed prices, what happens to the optimal quantity of \(x\) and to the maximum utility when income doubles?

Solution

By Proposition 1.4, \(\bar{x} = m/(2p_x)\), which doubles when \(m\) doubles. The maximum utility is \(\bar{u} = m^2/(4p_xp_y)\), which is multiplied by \(2^2 = 4\). So the optimal quantity doubles and the maximum utility quadruples.

Proof

P1 · Scaling prices and income together

Prove that multiplying \(p_x\), \(p_y\) and \(m\) by the same \(t > 0\) leaves the optimal bundle unchanged.

Solution

The budget set with prices \(tp_x\), \(tp_y\) and income \(tm\) is \(\{tp_x x + tp_y y \le tm\}\), which is the same set as \(\{p_x x + p_y y \le m\}\) after dividing by \(t > 0\). The utility is unchanged, so the two problems have the same optimum. Directly, \(\bar{x} = tm/(2tp_x) = m/(2p_x)\), and likewise \(\bar{y}\).

P2 · The maximum utility increases with income

Prove that \(\bar{u}\) is a strictly increasing function of \(m\) for \(m > 0\), with the prices fixed.

Solution

By Proposition 1.4, \(\bar{u} = m^2/(4p_xp_y)\). For \(0 < m_1 < m_2\), \(m_1^2 < m_2^2\), and dividing by the positive constant \(4p_xp_y\) gives \(\bar{u}(m_1) < \bar{u}(m_2)\). So \(\bar{u}\) is strictly increasing in \(m\).

P3 · The MRS at the optimum from the partial derivatives

Prove Proposition 2.3 using only Proposition 2.2 and the values of \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\), and say which step uses the positivity of \(p_x\) and \(p_y\).

Solution

Proposition 2.2 gives \(\mathrm{MRS} = y/x\) for \(x > 0\). At the optimum, \(\bar{y}/\bar{x} = (m/(2p_y))/(m/(2p_x))\). The factors \(m/2\) cancel, leaving \(p_x/p_y\). The step that divides by \(p_x\) and \(p_y\) needs them positive, which the Setting assumes. The step that divides by \(\bar{x}\) needs \(\bar{x} > 0\), which Proposition 1.3 gives.

Empirical

E1 · The printed maximum utility (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the optimum for \(p_x = 1\), \(p_y = 1\) and \(m = 50\). Read the printed maximum utility and check it against the formula of Proposition 1.4.

Solution

The lab prints x_bar=25, y_bar=25 and u_bar=625 (the closed-form line for case 2). The formula gives \(\bar{u} = 50^2/(4 \cdot 1 \cdot 1) = 2500/4 = 625\), which agrees. The grid point that Lab 1 selects for this case is point 30, the midpoint of the 60-step budget line, as the closed form requires.

E2 · A small trade off the optimum (Lab 1 and Remark 2.4)

Starting from the bundle \((20, 80/3)\) of case 1, which Lab 1 prints, buy one more unit of \(x\) and sell \(2/3\) of a unit of \(y\). Compute the exact change in utility and say whether it is positive.

Solution

The new bundle is \((21, 80/3 - 2/3) = (21, 26)\). Its utility is \(21 \cdot 26 = 546\), and the old utility is \(20 \cdot 80/3 = 1600/3\). The change is \(546 - 1600/3 = (1638 - 1600)/3 = 38/3\), which is positive. The first-order estimate of Remark 2.4 gives \(40/3\). The exact change differs from it by \(-2/3\), which is the second-order term \(-2\,dx^2/3\) at \(dx = 1\) in the expansion \((20 + dx)(80/3 - 2\,dx/3) = 1600/3 + 40\,dx/3 - 2\,dx^2/3\).

E3 · The grid spacing (Lab 1)

Lab 1 uses 60 steps along the budget line for case 1. Compute the spacing in units of \(x\), and say why the midpoint is grid point 30.

Solution

The line runs from \(x = 0\) to \(x = m/p_x = 120/2 = 60\), so 60 steps of length \(60/60 = 1\) unit of \(x\) cover it. The midpoint \(x = 30\) is reached after \(30\) steps, which is grid point 30, and it is the closed-form \(\bar{x}\) of Example 1.6.

Research

R1 · A tangency point that is a minimum

Let \(g(x, y) = (x - 1)^2 + y\), which is not the utility of the Setting. Along the budget line \(p_x x + p_y y = m\), show that the stationary point of \(g\) is a local minimum, and say what this shows about tangency.

Solution

On the line, \(h(x) = (x - 1)^2 + (m - p_x x)/p_y\). Then \(h'(x) = 2(x - 1) - p_x/p_y\), which vanishes at \(x_1 = 1 + p_x/(2p_y)\). The second derivative is \(h''(x) = 2 > 0\), so \(x_1\) is a strict local minimum of \(h\). The first-order condition \(h'(x_1) = 0\) therefore holds at a minimum here, so it alone does not identify a maximum for a general function. Proposition 1.4 avoids this because \(f''(x) < 0\) on the line (Remark 1.5), so its stationary point is a maximum.

R2 · The Cobb-Douglas share

Let \(u = x^a y^{1-a}\) with \(0 < a < 1\), and prices and income as in the Setting. Assuming Assumption 1 (so that an optimal bundle exists, since \(u\) is continuous on the nonempty, closed and bounded set \(B\)) and Assumption 2 (stated in the solution), show that the optimal bundle spends the share \(a\) of income on \(x\).

Solution

Assumption 2 (the interior first-order condition, stated here and not proved on this page): if a function of one variable is differentiable at an interior point of an open interval where it attains a maximum, its derivative there is zero. Proposition 2.3 is proved only for \(u = xy\), so this problem takes tangency from Assumption 2 instead.

Step 1. A bundle with \(x = 0\) or \(y = 0\) has utility 0, while \((am/p_x, (1-a)m/p_y)\) lies in \(B\) and has positive utility. So the optimal bundle has \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\). If it had slack \(s = m - p_x x - p_y y > 0\), the bundle \((x + s/p_x, y)\) would lie in \(B\) and have larger utility, because \(u\) increases in \(x\) when \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\). So the budget binds.

Step 2. On the budget line put \(y(x) = (m - p_x x)/p_y\) and \(h(x) = u(x, y(x))\) for \(0 < x < m/p_x\). The optimal \(\bar{x}\) lies in this open interval, because \(\bar{y} > 0\). For \(x\) in the interval, \((x, y(x))\) lies in \(B\), so \(h(x) \le u(\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = h(\bar{x})\); thus \(h\) attains a maximum at \(\bar{x}\) on the interval, and Assumption 2 applies there. Then \(h\) is differentiable there, and the chain rule gives \(h'(x) = \partial u/\partial x - (p_x/p_y)\,\partial u/\partial y\). Assumption 2 gives \(h'(\bar{x}) = 0\), so at the optimum \(\partial u/\partial x = (p_x/p_y)\,\partial u/\partial y\).

Step 3. Here \(\partial u/\partial x = a x^{a-1} y^{1-a}\) and \(\partial u/\partial y = (1-a) x^a y^{-a}\), so \(\mathrm{MRS} = a y/((1-a) x)\), and Step 2 gives \(a y/((1-a) x) = p_x/p_y\). Clearing denominators gives \(a\,p_y\,y = (1-a)\,p_x\,x\). Add \(a\,p_x\,x\) to both sides: \(p_x x = a(p_x x + p_y y)\). The budget binds (Step 1), so \(p_x x = a m\). The share of income spent on \(x\) is \(a\). Steps 2 and 3 use \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\), which Step 1 gives.

R3 · Why the number 600 is not a welfare measure

Show that replacing \(u\) by \(\ln u\) leaves the optimal bundle of Example 1.6 unchanged, and explain what this shows about the level \(600\).

Solution

The natural logarithm is strictly increasing on positive numbers, so for bundles with \(xy > 0\), \(\ln u(x, y) > \ln u(x', y')\) exactly when \(u(x, y) > u(x', y')\). The maximiser of \(\ln u\) over the bundles with \(xy > 0\) is therefore the maximiser of \(u\), which is \((30, 20)\), and it lies in that set because its utility is positive. The number changes under \(\ln\), from 600 to \(\ln 600\), while the choice does not, so the choice does not depend on the level \(600\).

  1. What does utility maximization choose?

    Options

    Definition 1.1: the maximum is taken over the affordable set \(B\), not over all bundles.

  2. For \(u = xy\) with \(p_x = 2\), \(p_y = 3\) and \(m = 120\), what is the optimal quantity \(\bar{x}\)?

    Proposition 1.4 gives \(\bar{x} = m/(2p_x) = 120/4 = 30\). Lab 1 prints this case.

  3. For the same case, what is the optimal quantity \(\bar{y}\)?

    Proposition 1.4 gives \(\bar{y} = m/(2p_y) = 120/6 = 20\), and \(2 \cdot 30 + 3 \cdot 20 = 120\).

  4. At the optimum for \(u = xy\), what is true of the budget?

    Options

    Proposition 1.3: a positive slack would allow more of \(x\), which raises \(u\) because \(y > 0\).

  5. For \(u = xy\), what is the marginal rate of substitution at \((x, y) = (6, 8)\)? Give it as a decimal.

    Proposition 2.2 gives \(\mathrm{MRS} = y/x = 8/6 = 4/3\), which is 1.3333 to four decimals.

  6. For \(u(x, y) = x + y\), what is the marginal rate of substitution at every bundle?

    Options

    Both partial derivatives of \(x + y\) equal 1, so the ratio in Definition 2.1 is 1.

  7. For \(u = xy\) with \(p_x = p_y = 1\) and \(m = 50\), what is the maximum utility \(\bar{u}\)?

    Proposition 1.4: \(\bar{u} = m^2/(4p_xp_y) = 2500/4 = 625\). Lab 1 prints this case.

  8. What is the partial derivative of \(xy\) with respect to \(y\)?

    Options

    Holding \(x\) fixed, \(xy\) is \(x\) times \(y\), so its slope in \(y\) is \(x\) (Proposition 2.2).

  9. For \(u = xy\) and fixed prices, what happens to the optimal quantity \(\bar{x}\) when income doubles?

    Options

    Proposition 1.4: \(\bar{x} = m/(2p_x)\) is proportional to \(m\).

  10. In case 1 (\(p_x = 2\), \(p_y = 3\), \(m = 120\)), what is the MRS at the off-optimum bundle \((20, 80/3)\)? Give it as a decimal.

  11. In case 1, what is the MRS at the optimum \((30, 20)\)? Give it as a decimal.

    \(y/x = 20/30 = 2/3\), which equals the price ratio \(p_x/p_y = 2/3\) (Proposition 2.3).
