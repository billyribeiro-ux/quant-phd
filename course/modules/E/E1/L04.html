Demand Responsiveness and Comparative Statics

A demand curve answers two questions that one price cannot: how far quantity moves when price moves, and where the market settles when a condition changes. The first is measured by the price elasticity, defined in §1, a unit-free ratio of percent changes. The second is measured by comparative statics, defined in §2, the derivative of the equilibrium price with respect to a parameter. For linear demand the elasticity is \(-bp/(a-bp)\), and its size exceeds 1 exactly above the midpoint \(a/(2b)\). Lab 1 differentiates the equilibrium price of one market by the implicit-function theorem and checks that derivative against finite differences. Lesson L05 (its Definition 5.1, monopoly pricing) uses the elasticity of Definition 1.1.

1Price elasticity: quantity against price, in units that cancel

Quantities are counted in units. Prices are in cents per unit, so a price of 10 is 10 cents for one unit. Every derivative in §1 is a derivative of a function of price, as in M2. In §2 the derivatives \(dp^{*}/da\) and \(dQ^{*}/da\), and in §3 the derivatives of \(p(a)\), are taken with respect to the demand intercept \(a\), while \(D_p\), \(F_p\) and \(S'(p)\) are taken with respect to price. Where \(Q\) denotes a demand function, \(Q'(p)\) is its derivative.

Definition 1.1 (price elasticity of demand and of supply)

Let \(Q(p) > 0\) be a differentiable demand function. The price elasticity of demand at price \(p\) is \[ \varepsilon(p) = \frac{Q'(p)\,p}{Q(p)}. \] For a differentiable supply function \(S(p) > 0\), the price elasticity of supply is \(\eta(p) = S'(p)\,p / S(p)\). Both are dimensionless: the units of quantity divide out, and so do the cents. A demand curve that slopes down has \(\varepsilon(p) < 0\). This lesson keeps that sign, and calls \(|\varepsilon(p)|\) the magnitude.

Remark 1.2 (the elasticity as a log-derivative)

Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, \(\varepsilon(p) = d(\ln Q)/d(\ln p)\). The derivative of the logarithm is \(d(\ln x)/dx = 1/x\), so by the chain rule \[ \frac{d\ln Q}{d\ln p} = \frac{d\ln Q/dp}{d\ln p/dp} = \frac{Q'(p)/Q(p)}{1/p} = \frac{Q'(p)\,p}{Q(p)} = \varepsilon(p). \] The same identity gives a first-order reading. A change \(\Delta p\) moves quantity by \(Q'(p)\,\Delta p\) to first order, so the percent change in quantity is \(\Delta Q / Q \approx \varepsilon(p)\,(\Delta p / p)\). A 1% rise in price therefore moves quantity by about \(\varepsilon(p)\) percent. The word “about” is exact only in the limit of small changes. Problem E2 computes a finite change in the intercept and compares it with the derivative-based value.

Example 1.3 (linear demand)

Let \(Q(p) = a - bp\) with \(a > 0\) and \(b > 0\). Then \(Q'(p) = -b\), so \(\varepsilon(p) = -bp/(a - bp)\) at every \(p\) with \(0 \le p < a/b\), where \(Q(p) > 0\). At \(p = 10\) and \(Q = 120 - 2p\), this is \(\varepsilon(10) = -20/100 = -0.2\). At \(p = 40\) the same demand curve gives \(\varepsilon(40) = -80/40 = -2\), although its slope is \(-2\) at both prices.

Proposition 1.4 (linear demand is elastic above the midpoint)

Assume \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\), and \(Q(p) = a - bp\). Let \(0 < p < a/b\). Then \(|\varepsilon(p)| > 1\) if \(p > a/(2b)\), \(|\varepsilon(p)| = 1\) if \(p = a/(2b)\), and \(|\varepsilon(p)| < 1\) if \(p < a/(2b)\).

Proof

Since \(0 < p < a/b\), both \(bp > 0\) and \(a - bp = Q(p) > 0\). By Example 1.3, \(|\varepsilon(p)| = bp/(a - bp)\). Multiplying by the positive number \(a - bp\), the inequality \(bp/(a - bp) > 1\) holds if and only if \(bp > a - bp\), which holds if and only if \(2bp > a\), which holds if and only if \(p > a/(2b)\). The cases \(=\) and \(<\) follow by the same three equivalences with \(=\) or \(<\) in place of \(>\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.5 (a power-law supply has constant elasticity)

If \(S(p) = d\,p^{k}\) with \(d > 0\) and \(k > 0\), then \(\eta(p) = k\) at every \(p > 0\). Proof: \(S'(p) = kd\,p^{k-1}\) by the power rule, so \(S'(p)\,p/S(p) = kd\,p^{k}/(d\,p^{k}) = k\). The supply curve of Lab 1 is \(S(p) = p^{2}\), which is this case with \(d = 1\) and \(k = 2\), so its elasticity is 2 at every price.

2Comparative statics: how an equilibrium moves when a parameter moves

Definition 2.1 (comparative statics)

Let a demand intercept \(a\) enter the market through a demand function \(D(p, a)\), quantity demanded at price \(p\), and let \(S(p)\) be quantity supplied at price \(p\). The equilibrium of L03 is a price \(p^{*}\) at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so it solves \(F(p, a) = D(p, a) - S(p) = 0\). Comparative statics is the study of how \(p^{*}\) and the equilibrium quantity \(Q^{*} = S(p^{*})\) change when \(a\) changes, with every other parameter held fixed. Its basic object is the derivative \(dp^{*}/da\). It compares two equilibria. It does not describe how the market moves from one to the other (§4).

Remark 2.2 (the formula for the derivative)

Suppose \(F\) is continuously differentiable near \((p^{*}, a)\), with \(F = 0\) at that point and \(F_p \ne 0\) there. The implicit-function theorem (M2) gives a differentiable local solution \(p^{*}(a)\) of \(F(p^{*}(a), a) = 0\). Differentiating that identity with the chain rule, \(F_p\,dp^{*}/da + F_a = 0\), so \[ \frac{dp^{*}}{da} = -\frac{F_a}{F_p}, \] where \(F_a\) and \(F_p\) are partial derivatives evaluated at \((p^{*}, a)\). The theorem supplies the existence of \(p^{*}(a)\). The formula above is the chain-rule step.

Proposition 2.3 (a rightward demand shift raises the price and the quantity)

Assume that \(D(p, a)\) and \(S(p)\) are continuously differentiable, that \(D_a > 0\) at the equilibrium (a higher intercept raises quantity demanded at the equilibrium price), that \(D_p < 0\), and that \(S'(p) > 0\). Let \(p^{*}(a)\) be the local solution of \(D(p^{*}, a) = S(p^{*})\) from Remark 2.2. Then \[ \frac{dp^{*}}{da} = \frac{D_a}{S'(p^{*}) - D_p(p^{*}, a)} > 0, \qquad \frac{dQ^{*}}{da} = S'(p^{*})\,\frac{dp^{*}}{da} > 0. \]

Proof

Let \(F(p, a) = D(p, a) - S(p)\). Then \(F_a = D_a\) and \(F_p = D_p - S'(p)\). Because \(D_p < 0\) and \(S' > 0\), \(F_p < 0\), so \(F_p \ne 0\) and Remark 2.2 applies. The formula gives \(dp^{*}/da = -F_a/F_p = D_a/(S'(p^{*}) - D_p)\). The numerator is positive because \(D_a > 0\). The denominator is positive because \(S' > 0\) and \(-D_p > 0\). So \(dp^{*}/da > 0\). The equilibrium quantity is \(Q^{*}(a) = S(p^{*}(a))\), so the chain rule gives \(dQ^{*}/da = S'(p^{*})\,dp^{*}/da\). This is a product of two positive numbers. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.4 (the market of Lab 1)

Let \(D(p, a) = a - 2p\) and \(S(p) = p^{2}\). The equilibrium equation \(a - 2p = p^{2}\) rearranges to \(p^{2} + 2p + 1 = a + 1\), that is \((p + 1)^{2} = 1 + a\). Its positive root is \(p^{*}(a) = \sqrt{1 + a} - 1\), which is positive for \(a > 0\). At \(a = 120\), \(p^{*} = \sqrt{121} - 1 = 10\) cents per unit and \(Q^{*} = 10^{2} = 100\) units. Differentiating the closed form, \(dp^{*}/da = 1/(2\sqrt{1 + a}) = 1/22\) at \(a = 120\). Proposition 2.3 gives the same value: \(D_a/(S' - D_p) = 1/(20 + 2) = 1/22\), using \(S'(10) = 20\) and \(D_p = -2\). Also \(dQ^{*}/da = S'(10)\,dp^{*}/da = 20/22 = 10/11\), which is the same as \(\partial D/\partial a + D_p\,dp^{*}/da = 1 - 2/22 = 10/11\). The hypotheses of Proposition 2.3 hold at this equilibrium.

Lab 1 · Demand-intercept shift: the implicit derivative against finite differences

Stated before running. The inputs are illustrative, not data: demand \(a - 2p\) in units, supply \(p^{2}\) in units, prices in cents per unit, with \(a = 120\). The closed form predicts an equilibrium price of 10 cents per unit, a quantity of 100 units, and \(dp^{*}/da = 1/22 = 0.0454545455\). It also predicts that the forward quotient \((p(a+h)-p(a))/h\) has an error that falls by a factor of about 10 for each tenfold fall in the step \(h\), and that the central quotient \((p(a+h)-p(a-h))/(2h)\) has an error that falls by a factor of about 100. The code uses no random draws and reads no file.

"""E1 L04 lab 1: a demand-intercept shift in a market with linear demand and quadratic supply.

Inputs are illustrative parameters, not data. Demand is Q_d(p) = a - b p units, with a = 120 units, b = 2 units per
cent. Supply is Q_s(p) = d p^2 units, with d = 1 unit per cent squared. The price p is in cents per unit. No random
draws are used, and no file is read.

The equilibrium price p(a) is the positive root of a - b p = d p^2, namely p(a) = (-b + S) / (2 d) with
S = sqrt(b^2 + 4 a d). Part A prints the equilibrium at a = 120 and checks that demand equals supply there.
Part B prints the implicit-function derivative dp/da = -F_a / F_p, for F(p, a) = a - b p - d p^2, beside the
derivative of the closed form, and the elasticity of the equilibrium price with respect to a.
Part C prints forward and central finite-difference quotients of p(a) at a = 120 for step sizes 1, 0.1, 0.01 and
0.001, with the error of each against dp/da.
Part D prints the price elasticities of demand and supply at the equilibrium.

Every value is a Decimal computed at 50 significant digits. Errors are printed in scientific notation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from decimal import Decimal, getcontext

getcontext().prec = 50

A_INTERCEPT = Decimal(120)
B_SLOPE = Decimal(2)
D_SUPPLY = Decimal(1)
STEPS = (Decimal(1), Decimal("0.1"), Decimal("0.01"), Decimal("0.001"))


def equilibrium_price(a: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """The positive root of a - b p - d p^2 = 0, in cents per unit, for demand intercept a."""
    return (-B_SLOPE + (B_SLOPE * B_SLOPE + 4 * a * D_SUPPLY).sqrt()) / (2 * D_SUPPLY)


def demand(price: Decimal, a: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """Quantity demanded in units at a price in cents per unit: a - b p."""
    return a - B_SLOPE * price


def supply(price: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """Quantity supplied in units at a price in cents per unit: d p^2."""
    return D_SUPPLY * price * price


def implicit_derivative(price: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """dp/da = -F_a / F_p at the equilibrium, with F_a = 1 and F_p = -(b + 2 d p) for F = a - b p - d p^2."""
    return 1 / (B_SLOPE + 2 * D_SUPPLY * price)


def closed_form_derivative(a: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """d/da of p(a) = (-b + S) / (2 d). Since dS/da = 2 d / S, the derivative is 1 / S with S = sqrt(b^2 + 4 a d)."""
    return 1 / (B_SLOPE * B_SLOPE + 4 * a * D_SUPPLY).sqrt()


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A to D. Each line is a fixed label or a number, so the output is reproducible."""
    print("Part A: equilibrium at demand intercept a = 120")
    p0 = equilibrium_price(A_INTERCEPT)
    q0 = demand(p0, A_INTERCEPT)
    print(f"equilibrium_price_cents {p0:.10f}")
    print(f"equilibrium_quantity {q0:.10f}")
    print(f"supply_at_equilibrium {supply(p0):.10f}")
    print(f"demand_equals_supply {abs(q0 - supply(p0)) < Decimal('1e-30')}")

    print("Part B: implicit-function derivative dp/da beside the derivative of the closed form")
    dp_implicit = implicit_derivative(p0)
    dp_closed = closed_form_derivative(A_INTERCEPT)
    print(f"dp_da_implicit {dp_implicit:.10f}")
    print(f"dp_da_closed_form {dp_closed:.10f}")
    print(f"implicit_equals_closed_form {abs(dp_implicit - dp_closed) < Decimal('1e-30')}")
    print(f"price_elasticity_of_equilibrium_in_a {A_INTERCEPT / p0 * dp_implicit:.10f}")

    print("Part C: finite-difference quotients of p(a) at a = 120 against dp/da")
    for h in STEPS:
        upper = equilibrium_price(A_INTERCEPT + h)
        lower = equilibrium_price(A_INTERCEPT - h)
        forward = (upper - p0) / h
        central = (upper - lower) / (2 * h)
        print(
            f"step {h} forward {forward:.10f} forward_error {abs(forward - dp_implicit):.6e} "
            f"central {central:.10f} central_error {abs(central - dp_implicit):.6e}"
        )

    print("Part D: price elasticities at the equilibrium")
    demand_elasticity = -B_SLOPE * p0 / demand(p0, A_INTERCEPT)
    supply_elasticity = (2 * D_SUPPLY * p0) * p0 / supply(p0)
    print(f"demand_elasticity {demand_elasticity:.10f}")
    print(f"supply_elasticity {supply_elasticity:.10f}")

Remark 2.5 (a supply curve that slopes down reverses the sign)

Proposition 2.3 needs \(S' > 0\). Without it the sign of \(dp^{*}/da\) is no longer fixed. Problem R1 gives a market in which a rightward demand shift lowers the price.

3Finite differences: the order of the error in the check

Proposition 3.1 (orders of the two difference quotients)

Let \(p(a) = \sqrt{1 + a} - 1\) for \(a > -1\). Let \(h > 0\) be small enough that \(a \pm h > -1\). Then \[ \frac{p(a + h) - p(a)}{h} - p'(a) = \frac{h}{2}\,p''(a) + O(h^{2}), \qquad \frac{p(a + h) - p(a - h)}{2h} - p'(a) = \frac{h^{2}}{6}\,p'''(a) + O(h^{4}). \] At \(a = 120\), \(p''(120) = -1/5324\) and \(p'''(120) = 3/1288408\). So the forward error is \(-h/10648\) to leading order, and the central error is \(h^{2}/2576816\) to leading order.

Proof

The function \(p\) is smooth on \((-1, \infty)\). Taylor’s theorem with remainder (M2) gives \(p(a \pm h) = p(a) \pm h\,p'(a) + \tfrac{h^{2}}{2}p''(a) \pm \tfrac{h^{3}}{6}p'''(a) + O(h^{4})\). The plus-sign expansion gives \(p(a + h) - p(a) = h\,p'(a) + \tfrac{h^{2}}{2}p''(a) + O(h^{3})\), and dividing by \(h\) gives the forward expansion. In the second quotient, the even terms cancel and \(p(a + h) - p(a - h) = 2h\,p'(a) + \tfrac{h^{3}}{3}p'''(a) + O(h^{5})\). Dividing by \(2h\) gives the central expansion. For the values at \(a = 120\), \(p'(a) = 1/(2\sqrt{1+a})\), so \(p''(a) = -1/(4(1+a)^{3/2})\) and \(p'''(a) = 3/(8(1+a)^{5/2})\). Here \((1 + a)^{3/2} = 121^{3/2} = 1331\) and \((1 + a)^{5/2} = 161051\). So \(p''(120) = -1/5324\), and \(p'''(120) = 3/(8 \cdot 161051) = 3/1288408\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.2 (the leading terms against Lab 1)

At \(h = 0.001\), the leading term of the forward error is \(0.001/10648 = 9.3914350\times 10^{-8}\), and the printed forward error is \(9.391396\times 10^{-8}\). The ratio of the unrounded error to the leading term is 0.99999587 (from unrounded values). At the same step, the leading term of the central error is \(10^{-6}/2576816 = 3.8807583\times 10^{-13}\), and the unrounded central error agrees with it to a relative 3 in \(10^{11}\). The leading term is a good description of the error at small \(h\) because the next term in each expansion is of higher order in \(h\). At \(h = 1\) the leading term is less accurate: the central error is 1.0000299 times it, and the forward error is 0.99589 times it, both from unrounded values.

Remark 3.3 (forward against central at one step)

At \(h = 0.01\) the unrounded forward error is \(9.39104696\times 10^{-7}\) and the unrounded central error is \(3.88075828\times 10^{-11}\). The ratio of the unrounded forward error to the unrounded central error is 24,199 (from unrounded values; 24198.99998). Proposition 3.1 explains why: the forward error is of order \(h\) and the central error is of order \(h^{2}\). At this step the central quotient is far more accurate than the forward quotient. The central quotient is the better check on a derivative, and the forward quotient is its one-sided version.

4Where the canon breaks

  • The derivative is local. \(dp^{*}/da\) describes a small change in \(a\) from the point where it is taken. Problem E2 gives the exact change for a 1% rise, 0.5441088%, which differs from the derivative-based 0.5454545% in the third significant figure. A large change needs the full function \(p^{*}(a)\), which the derivative does not give.
  • Comparative statics does not describe adjustment. Definition 2.1 compares two equilibria. It does not say how the price moves between them, or whether a market that is out of equilibrium moves toward the new one. The lab computes no path.
  • One equilibrium is assumed. Remark 2.2 uses a local solution. An equation \(F(p, a) = 0\) can have several solutions, and the derivative at one of them does not describe the others. In Example 2.4 the positive root is unique because \((p+1)^{2} = 1 + a\) has one root with \(p + 1 > 0\).
  • Ceteris paribus is a choice. Every result in §2 holds the supply curve fixed. A change in both demand and supply has a different effect on the price, and needs the two derivatives taken together.
  • The parameters are illustrative. The intercept 120, the slope 2 and the supply coefficient 1 are chosen for exact arithmetic. They are not estimates of any market.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A slope of \(-2\) means the elasticity is \(-2\) everywhere on the demand curve.”

    The slope is \(Q'(p)\) and the elasticity is \(Q'(p)\,p/Q(p)\) (Definition 1.1). They differ wherever \(p/Q(p)\) changes along the curve, as it does here. For \(Q = 120 - 2p\) the slope is \(-2\) at every price, but Example 1.3 gives \(\varepsilon(10) = -0.2\) and \(\varepsilon(40) = -2\).

  2. “A rightward shift of demand raises the price and lowers the quantity sold.”

    With an upward-sloping supply curve, a rightward shift raises both. Proposition 2.3 gives \(dQ^{*}/da = S'(p^{*})\,dp^{*}/da > 0\). In Example 2.4, \(dQ^{*}/da = 10/11\) at \(a = 120\). Without an upward-sloping supply curve the sign of the price response can reverse (Problem R1), and that is the case Proposition 2.3 excludes.

  3. “The forward and central quotients are equally accurate, so either one is an adequate check on a derivative.”

    The forward error is of order \(h\) and the central error of order \(h^{2}\) (Proposition 3.1). Part C of Lab 1 prints a forward error of 9.391047e-7 at \(h = 0.01\) and a central error of 3.880758e-11 at the same step. Their unrounded ratio is 24,199 (Remark 3.3).

Mechanics

M1 · The elasticity of steeper linear demand

Demand is \(Q(p) = 100 - 4p\), with prices in cents per unit. Compute \(\varepsilon(10)\), and say whether demand is elastic or inelastic at \(p = 10\).

Solution

Here \(Q'(p) = -4\) and \(Q(10) = 100 - 40 = 60\). So \(\varepsilon(10) = Q'(10)\cdot 10/Q(10) = -40/60 = -2/3\), which is about \(-0.6667\). Its magnitude is \(2/3 < 1\), so demand is inelastic at this price. This agrees with Proposition 1.4: \(a/(2b) = 100/8 = 12.5\), and \(10 < 12.5\).

M2 · The elasticity of a cubic supply curve

Supply is \(S(p) = 5p^{3}\), with \(p > 0\). Compute \(\eta(p)\) for every \(p > 0\).

Solution

Remark 1.5 with \(d = 5\) and \(k = 3\) gives \(\eta(p) = 3\) at every \(p > 0\). Directly: \(S'(p) = 15p^{2}\), so \(S'(p)\,p/S(p) = 15p^{3}/(5p^{3}) = 3\). The value does not depend on \(p\), and it does not depend on the coefficient 5.

M3 · Exponential demand

Demand is \(D(p) = 50\,e^{-0.1p}\). Compute \(\varepsilon(p)\) for every \(p\), and its value at \(p = 20\).

Solution

The derivative is \(D'(p) = 50(-0.1)e^{-0.1p} = -5e^{-0.1p}\). So \(\varepsilon(p) = pD'(p)/D(p) = p(-5e^{-0.1p})/(50e^{-0.1p}) = -0.1p\). The exponential cancels, so the elasticity is linear in \(p\). At \(p = 20\), \(\varepsilon(20) = -2\), and demand is elastic there.

P1 · The unit-elastic point, by substitution

For \(Q(p) = a - bp\) with \(a, b > 0\), show that \(\varepsilon(a/(2b)) = -1\).

Solution

At \(p = a/(2b)\), \(bp = a/2\) and \(Q(p) = a - a/2 = a/2\). Then \(\varepsilon(p) = -bp/Q(p) = -(a/2)/(a/2) = -1\). This is the equality case of Proposition 1.4.

P2 · Revenue and the elasticity

Let \(Q > 0\) be differentiable, with elasticity \(\varepsilon(p)\), and let \(R(p) = pQ(p)\). Show that \(R'(p) = Q(p)\bigl(1 + \varepsilon(p)\bigr)\).

Solution

By the product rule, \(R'(p) = Q(p) + pQ'(p)\). Factor out \(Q(p)\): \(R'(p) = Q(p)\bigl(1 + pQ'(p)/Q(p)\bigr) = Q(p)\bigl(1 + \varepsilon(p)\bigr)\), by Definition 1.1. So revenue rises with price exactly when \(\varepsilon(p) > -1\). For linear demand this is the condition \(p < a/(2b)\), by Proposition 1.4.

P3 · The closed form of the market of Lab 1

For \(D(p, a) = a - 2p\) and \(S(p) = p^{2}\), show that the positive equilibrium price is \(p^{*}(a) = \sqrt{1 + a} - 1\), and derive \(dp^{*}/da\) from this closed form.

Solution

Equilibrium requires \(a - 2p = p^{2}\), which is \(p^{2} + 2p + 1 = 1 + a\), that is \((p + 1)^{2} = 1 + a\). Taking the square root of a positive quantity, \(p + 1 = \sqrt{1 + a}\) (the other root \(p + 1 = -\sqrt{1 + a}\) gives a negative price). So \(p^{*} = \sqrt{1 + a} - 1\), which is positive for \(a > 0\). Differentiating, \(dp^{*}/da = 1/(2\sqrt{1 + a})\). At \(a = 120\), this is \(1/22\).

Empirical

E1 · The ratio of central errors in Lab 1

Read Part C of Lab 1. Find the ratio of the central error at \(h = 0.1\) to the central error at \(h = 0.01\). Then say what Proposition 3.1 predicts for this ratio.

Solution

Part C prints 3.880759e-9 at \(h = 0.1\) and 3.880758e-11 at \(h = 0.01\). From the printed values the ratio is about 100.00. From the unrounded values it is 100.00003. Proposition 3.1 predicts a ratio of \(100\), because the central error is of order \(h^{2}\) and a tenfold fall in \(h\) multiplies \(h^{2}\) by one hundredth. The unrounded ratio differs from 100 by 0.00003 because of the higher-order terms.

E2 · The percent response from Part B

Part B of Lab 1 prints 0.5454545455 as the elasticity of the equilibrium price with respect to \(a\). Explain what this number says about a 1% rise in \(a\), and what it does not say.

Solution

The number is \((a/p^{*})(dp^{*}/da) = (120/10)(1/22) = 6/11 = 0.5454545\) (unrounded). It says that, for a small change in \(a\), the percent change in \(p^{*}\) is about 0.545 times the percent change in \(a\). It does not say that a 1% rise gives exactly 0.5454545%. Computing the exact change for \(a\) from 120 to 121.2, the price moves from 10 to 10.0544109, a rise of 0.5441088%. The gap between the two figures is the higher-order part that the derivative does not capture.

E3 · Which side responds more to price

Part D of Lab 1 prints the demand elasticity and the supply elasticity at the equilibrium. Which side responds more, in percent terms, to a 1% change in price, and by how much?

Solution

Part D prints \(-0.2\) for demand and 2 for supply. The supply magnitude, 2, is ten times the demand magnitude, 0.2, at this equilibrium. Supply responds more: a 1% price rise moves quantity supplied up by about 2% and quantity demanded down by about 0.2%, so the supply magnitude is ten times the demand magnitude. This is a statement about the two elasticities at \(p = 10\). It does not say how a particular shock divides between price and quantity, which needs the comparative statics of Proposition 2.3 for that shock.

Research

R1 · A rightward demand shift that lowers the price

Let \(D(p, a) = a - p\) and \(S(p) = c - 3p\), with \(c\) fixed. Find \(dp^{*}/da\) and show which hypothesis of Proposition 2.3 fails.

Solution

Equilibrium gives \(a - p = c - 3p\), so \(2p = c - a\) and \(p^{*}(a) = (c - a)/2\). Then \(dp^{*}/da = -1/2 < 0\). Check with the formula: \(D_a = 1 > 0\), \(D_p = -1\), \(S' = -3\), and \(D_a/(S' - D_p) = 1/(-3 + 1) = -1/2\). The hypothesis that fails is \(S'(p) > 0\). With \(S' = -3\), the denominator is negative, and the sign of \(dp^{*}/da\) reverses.

R2 · Proposition 2.3 for a nonlinear demand curve

Let \(D(p, a) = a/p\) for \(p > 0\) and \(S(p) = p\). Find \(p^{*}(a)\) and \(dp^{*}/da\), and verify the formula of Proposition 2.3 at the equilibrium.

Solution

Equilibrium gives \(a/p = p\), so \(p^{*} = \sqrt{a}\) (the positive root). Then \(dp^{*}/da = 1/(2\sqrt{a})\). The hypotheses hold: \(D_a = 1/p > 0\), \(D_p = -a/p^{2} < 0\), and \(S' = 1 > 0\). At \(p = \sqrt{a}\), \(D_p = -1\), and \(D_a/(S' - D_p) = (1/\sqrt{a})/(1 + 1) = 1/(2\sqrt{a})\), which agrees.

R3 · The price elasticity in closed form

For the market of Example 2.4, show that \((a/p^{*})(dp^{*}/da) = a/(2p^{*}(1 + p^{*}))\), and evaluate it at \(a = 120\).

Solution

By Problem P3, \(dp^{*}/da = 1/(2\sqrt{1+a})\), and \(\sqrt{1 + a} = 1 + p^{*}\). So \(dp^{*}/da = 1/(2(1 + p^{*}))\), and \((a/p^{*})(dp^{*}/da) = a/(2p^{*}(1 + p^{*}))\). At \(a = 120\), \(p^{*} = 10\), so the value is \(120/(2 \cdot 10 \cdot 11) = 120/220 = 6/11 = 0.5454545\ldots\).

    Options

    \(dp^{*}/da\) is the derivative of the equilibrium price with respect to a parameter (Definition 2.1). The elasticity is a different object (Definition 1.1).
