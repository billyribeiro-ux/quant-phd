Sudden Stops & Twin Crises

  • Uses: balance of payments (E8), first- and second-generation currency-crisis models (L03), policy rate (E7).
  • Introduces: sudden stop, banking crisis.
  • Ttheorem

  • Bpublished, not settled

  • Dlore

When cross-border financial flows abruptly halt, an emerging economy must eliminate its current account deficit immediately. Under balance-sheet currency mismatches, the required real depreciation does not stimulate exports smoothly; instead, it collapses the relative price of domestic non-traded assets, wipes out commercial bank equity, and triggers systemic banking insolvency. These simultaneous currency and banking collapses form twin crises that generate catastrophic output losses.

1The Calvo (1998) Sudden Stop Mechanism

Definition 1.1 (sudden stop)

A sudden stop is an abrupt, unexpected cessation or sharp reversal of gross private capital inflows to an economy (\(\Delta \mathrm{FA} \ll 0\)), accompanied by a collapse in foreign exchange liquidity and a spike in sovereign credit spreads.

Theorem 1.2 (relative price collapse of non-traded goods)

In the Calvo (1998) two-sector open economy with traded goods \(T\) and non-traded goods \(N\), representative consumer preferences \(u(c_T, c_N) = \gamma \ln c_T + (1 - \gamma) \ln c_N\), and exogenous endowments \((y_T, y_N)\), the equilibrium relative price of non-traded goods \(p_N\) satisfies:

\[ p_N = \left(\frac{1 - \gamma}{\gamma}\right) \frac{y_T + \mathrm{FA}}{y_N} \]

where \(\mathrm{FA}\) is net foreign capital inflow. When a sudden stop forces \(\mathrm{FA}\) from positive inflows \(\mathrm{FA}_0 > 0\) to capital outflows \(\mathrm{FA}_1 < 0\), the relative price \(p_N\) collapses by:

\[ \frac{p_N(1)}{p_N(0)} = \frac{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_1}{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_0} < 1 \]

generating a sharp real exchange rate depreciation (\(1 / p_N\) spikes).

Proof

The representative consumer maximizes \(\gamma \ln c_T + (1 - \gamma) \ln c_N\) subject to the budget constraint \(c_T + p_N c_N = y_T + p_N y_N + \mathrm{FA}\). The first-order condition equating the marginal rate of substitution to the relative price ratio is:

\[ \frac{\partial u / \partial c_N}{\partial u / \partial c_T} = \frac{(1 - \gamma)/c_N}{\gamma / c_T} = \frac{1 - \gamma}{\gamma} \frac{c_T}{c_N} = p_N \]

Because non-traded goods cannot be exported or imported, the domestic non-traded goods market must clear in domestic absorption: \(c_N = y_N\). By the balance of payments identity, the current account deficit equals net capital inflows: \(\mathrm{CA} = -\mathrm{FA}\). Since \(\mathrm{CA} = y_T - c_T\), traded goods consumption must equal endowment plus capital inflows: \(c_T = y_T + \mathrm{FA}\). Substituting \(c_T\) and \(c_N\) into the first-order condition yields directly:

\[ p_N = \left(\frac{1 - \gamma}{\gamma}\right) \frac{y_T + \mathrm{FA}}{y_N} \]

When capital inflows drop from \(\mathrm{FA}_0\) to \(\mathrm{FA}_1\), dividing the new price by the old price yields \(\frac{p_N(1)}{p_N(0)} = \frac{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_1}{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_0}\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Balance-Sheet Currency Mismatch and Banking Insolvency

Definition 2.1 (banking crisis and original sin)

A banking crisis occurs when severe balance-sheet distress, depositor runs, or asset defaults lead to widespread bank insolvencies, exhaustion of bank capital, and systemic intervention by public authorities. When domestic commercial banks suffer from liability dollarization ("original sin")—borrowing in foreign currency (traded goods) while lending in domestic currency to the non-traded real estate and service sectors—a sudden stop translates directly into bank insolvency.

Theorem 2.2 (banking sector insolvency boundary)

Let domestic banks hold non-traded loan assets \(L_N\) (denominated in non-traded units) and foreign-currency debt \(D^*\) (denominated in traded units). Total bank capital in traded units is \(K = p_N L_N - D^*\). The banking sector becomes insolvent (\(K < 0\)) if and only if net capital inflows fall below a critical threshold \(\mathrm{FA}^*\):

\[ \mathrm{FA} < \mathrm{FA}^* = y_N \left(\frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\right) \frac{D^*}{L_N} - y_T \]
Proof

Bank equity is positive if and only if \(p_N L_N - D^* > 0 \iff p_N > D^* / L_N\). Substituting the equilibrium price of non-traded goods from Theorem 1.2:

\[ \left(\frac{1 - \gamma}{\gamma}\right) \frac{y_T + \mathrm{FA}}{y_N} > \frac{D^*}{L_N} \]

Multiplying both sides by \(\left(\frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\right) y_N\) yields \(y_T + \mathrm{FA} > y_N \left(\frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\right) \frac{D^*}{L_N}\). Subtracting \(y_T\) gives the threshold condition:

\[ \mathrm{FA} > \mathrm{FA}^* \equiv y_N \left(\frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\right) \frac{D^*}{L_N} - y_T \]

Whenever capital inflows fall below \(\mathrm{FA}^*\), bank capital \(K\) turns negative, triggering systemic insolvency. \(\blacksquare\)

3Twin Crises: The Reinhart–Rogoff Evidence

Proposition 3.1 (twin crisis sequencing and output losses)

Reinhart and Rogoff (2009), synthesizing eight centuries of financial data and replicating Kaminsky and Reinhart (1999), document that banking crises and currency crises systematically coincide as twin crises. The typical sequencing exhibits three distinct phases:

  1. Financial liberalization and foreign capital inflows fuel an unsustainable domestic credit boom and asset price appreciation.
  2. A banking crisis begins as credit defaults emerge; the central bank injects liquidity and acts as lender of last resort.
  3. Liquidity creation and reserve depletion undermine the currency peg, precipitating a full currency crash that feeds back into banking insolvencies, producing cumulative real GDP declines averaging 9.3% and public debt surges averaging 86% over three years.

Lab 1 · Sudden Stops, Real Depreciation, and Banking Insolvency

Under a Calvo two-sector economy, a reversal of capital inflows from +20 to -20 causes a 33.3% collapse in the relative price of non-traded goods. Under currency mismatch, bank capital turns negative (-14.0), triggering systemic banking insolvency.

"""Lab 4: Sudden stops, real exchange rate collapse, and banking insolvency (Calvo 1998).

SIMULATED two-sector open economy. Standard library only.
Verifies the relative price adjustment of non-traded goods following a sudden stop,
the balance-sheet currency mismatch, and the threshold for banking crisis insolvency.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def relative_nontraded_price(
    traded_output: float,
    capital_inflow: float,
    nontraded_output: float,
    traded_share: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute equilibrium relative price of non-traded goods p_N under Cobb-Douglas utility.

    Utility: u(c_T, c_N) = gamma * ln(c_T) + (1 - gamma) * ln(c_N).
    Equilibrium: p_N = [(1 - gamma) / gamma] * [ (y_T + FA) / y_N ]
    where c_T = y_T + FA and c_N = y_N.
    """
    if traded_share <= 0.0 or traded_share >= 1.0:
        msg = f"Traded expenditure share gamma must be in (0, 1), got {traded_share}"
        raise ValueError(msg)
    c_traded = traded_output + capital_inflow
    if c_traded <= 0.0:
        msg = f"Traded goods consumption must be positive: y_T + FA = {c_traded}"
        raise ValueError(msg)
    return ((1.0 - traded_share) / traded_share) * (c_traded / nontraded_output)


def bank_capital(
    price_nontraded: float,
    nontraded_loans: float,
    foreign_debt: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute banking sector equity capital.

    Assets are backed by non-traded sector revenues (valued at p_N),
    liabilities are denominated in foreign currency (traded goods):
    Capital = p_N * L_N - D*
    """
    return price_nontraded * nontraded_loans - foreign_debt


def main() -> None:
    # Model parameters
    y_t = 100.0  # Traded goods endowment
    y_n = 100.0  # Non-traded goods endowment
    gamma = 0.50  # Equal preference weights (gamma = 0.5 => (1 - gamma)/gamma = 1.0)

    # Banking balance sheet parameters
    l_n = 120.0  # Non-traded loans
    d_star = 110.0  # Foreign-currency liabilities (denominated in traded units)

    # 1. Pre-crisis baseline: Net capital inflow of +20 (FA = +20)
    fa_pre = 20.0
    p_n_pre = relative_nontraded_price(y_t, fa_pre, y_n, gamma)
    k_pre = bank_capital(p_n_pre, l_n, d_star)
    cap_ratio_pre = k_pre / (p_n_pre * l_n)

    # 2. Sudden Stop episode: Inflows reverse to net outflow of -20 (FA = -20)
    fa_post = -20.0
    p_n_post = relative_nontraded_price(y_t, fa_post, y_n, gamma)
    k_post = bank_capital(p_n_post, l_n, d_star)
    cap_ratio_post = k_post / (p_n_post * l_n)

    # 3. Calculate threshold capital flow causing bank insolvency (K = 0)
    # p_N * L_N - D* = 0 => p_N = D* / L_N
    # p_N = (y_T + FA*) / y_N => FA* = y_N * (D* / L_N) - y_T
    p_n_crit = d_star / l_n
    fa_crit = y_n * p_n_crit - y_t

    price_decline_pct = (p_n_post - p_n_pre) / p_n_pre * 100.0

    print("=== Calvo (1998) Sudden Stop & Banking Insolvency ===")
    print(f"Pre-Crisis Capital Inflow FA:     +{fa_pre:.2f}")
    print(f"Pre-Crisis Relative Price p_N:    {p_n_pre:.4f}")
    print(f"Pre-Crisis Bank Capital:          {k_pre:.4f}")
    print(f"Pre-Crisis Capital Ratio:         {cap_ratio_pre * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print("\n=== Post-Sudden Stop Capital Reversal ===")
    print(f"Post-Crisis Capital Flow FA:      {fa_post:.2f}")
    print(f"Post-Crisis Relative Price p_N:   {p_n_post:.4f}")
    print(f"Relative Price Decline:           {price_decline_pct:.2f}%")
    print(f"Post-Crisis Bank Capital:         {k_post:.4f}")
    print(f"Post-Crisis Capital Ratio:        {cap_ratio_post * 100.0:.2f}%")
    print("\n=== Insolvency Boundary Analysis ===")
    print(f"Critical Relative Price for K=0:  {p_n_crit:.4f}")
    print(f"Critical Capital Inflow FA*:      {fa_crit:.2f}")

    assert p_n_pre == 1.20, "Pre-crisis price must be 1.0 * (120 / 100) = 1.20"
    assert p_n_post == 0.80, "Post-crisis price must be 1.0 * (80 / 100) = 0.80"
    assert k_pre > 0.0, "Pre-crisis banking sector must be solvent"
    assert k_post < 0.0, "Post-crisis banking sector must be insolvent (banking crisis)"
    assert round(fa_crit, 2) == -8.33, "Critical FA* must be 100 * (110 / 120) - 100 = -8.33"


main()

Pre-Crisis Bank Capital:          34.0000
Pre-Crisis Capital Ratio:         23.61%

The model demonstrates the mechanical link between external capital reversals and domestic financial collapse: when capital flows fall below the critical threshold of -8.33, bank equity is completely wiped out by asset revaluation.

4Where the Canon Breaks

Modern macro-financial policy in emerging markets has evolved to insulate balance sheets from classical sudden stops:

  • Local-Currency Sovereign Bond Markets: Over the past two decades, emerging market sovereigns have substantially mitigated "original sin" by issuing domestic-currency local-law debt (e.g., Brazilian NTN-F, Mexican Bonos). While this protects the sovereign balance sheet from mechanical currency revaluation, foreign investors holding local bonds still dump assets during global risk-off shocks, transmitting stress through yields.
  • FX Reserve Warchests: Emerging market central banks accumulated trillions in foreign exchange reserves following the 1997 crisis. While these reserves provide a liquidity cushion, sterilized intervention carries high quasi-fiscal carrying costs.
  • Macroprudential Foreign-Exchange Regulations: Regulators now impose strict limits on commercial bank net open foreign exchange positions and foreign-currency lending to unhedged non-traded borrowers.

Misconceptions

  1. A sudden stop affects only sovereigns that run large public fiscal deficits.

    False. Sudden stops (such as East Asia in 1997 or Spain/Ireland in 2008) frequently occur in countries running fiscal surpluses and low public debt, where capital inflows finance private sector investment and corporate borrowing booms.

  2. Currency depreciation during a crisis immediately boosts economic output by improving export competitiveness.

    False. In economies with widespread foreign-currency liabilities, the contractionary balance-sheet effect (soaring debt service and corporate bankruptcies) massively overwhelms the traditional trade-competitiveness expenditure-switching channel in the short run.

  3. Banking crises and currency crises are independent macroeconomic events caused by different shocks.

    False. Reinhart and Rogoff (2009) prove that banking and currency crises are deeply intertwined twin crises. A banking bailout forces monetary expansion that destroys the currency; conversely, currency devaluation bankrupts dollar-indebted banks.

Problem Set

Mechanics

M1. In a Calvo economy, traded endowment is \(y_T = 150\), non-traded endowment is \(y_N = 100\), and expenditure share is \(\gamma = 0.60\). If foreign capital inflow drops from \(\mathrm{FA}_0 = +50\) to \(\mathrm{FA}_1 = -30\), calculate the pre-crisis price \(p_N(0)\), the post-crisis price \(p_N(1)\), and the percentage decline in \(p_N\).

Solution

The preference coefficient is \(\frac{1 - \gamma}{\gamma} = \frac{0.40}{0.60} = \frac{2}{3}\). Pre-crisis: \(p_N(0) = \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{150 + 50}{100} = \frac{2}{3} \times 2.0 = 1.3333\). Post-crisis: \(p_N(1) = \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{150 - 30}{100} = \frac{2}{3} \times 1.2 = 0.8000\). The percentage decline is \(\frac{0.8000 - 1.3333}{1.3333} = \frac{-0.5333}{1.3333} = -40.0\%\). The relative price collapses by 40%.

M2. Using the parameters of problem M1, domestic banks hold non-traded loans of \(L_N = 120\) and foreign liabilities of \(D^* = 110\). Compute bank capital \(K\) pre-crisis and post-crisis, and find the critical capital flow \(\mathrm{FA}^*\) that causes bank insolvency.

Solution

Pre-crisis bank capital: \(K_0 = p_N(0) L_N - D^* = 1.3333 \times 120 - 110 = 160.0 - 110.0 = +50.0\) (solvent). Post-crisis: \(K_1 = p_N(1) L_N - D^* = 0.8000 \times 120 - 110 = 96.0 - 110.0 = -14.0\) (insolvent). Critical price is \(p_N^* = D^* / L_N = 110 / 120 = 0.9167\). Critical capital flow is \(\mathrm{FA}^* = y_N \left(\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right) \frac{D^*}{L_N} - y_T = 100 \times 1.5 \times 0.9167 - 150 = 137.5 - 150 = -12.5\).

M3. According to Reinhart and Rogoff (2009), real public debt increases by an average of 86% in the three years following a systemic banking crisis. If a country enters a banking crisis with a public debt ratio of 45% of GDP, what is its projected debt ratio after three years under this historical benchmark?

Solution

An 86% expansion represents a growth factor of \(1 + 0.86 = 1.86\). The projected public debt ratio after three years is \(b_3 = 1.86 \times 45\% = 83.7\%\) of GDP. This surge stems primarily from fiscal revenue collapse and bank bailout recapitalizations rather than direct bailout spending alone.

Proof

P1. Prove that the magnitude of the real exchange rate depreciation following a sudden stop of size \(\Delta \mathrm{FA} = \mathrm{FA}_0 - \mathrm{FA}_1 > 0\) is strictly decreasing in the size of the initial traded endowment \(y_T\).

Solution

The price ratio is \(\rho(y_T) = \frac{p_N(1)}{p_N(0)} = \frac{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_1}{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_0} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{FA}_0 - \mathrm{FA}_1}{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_0} = 1 - \frac{\Delta \mathrm{FA}}{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_0}\). The percentage price decline is \(1 - \rho(y_T) = \frac{\Delta \mathrm{FA}}{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_0}\). Differentiating with respect to \(y_T\): \(\frac{d}{d y_T} \left( \frac{\Delta \mathrm{FA}}{y_T + \mathrm{FA}_0} \right) = -\frac{\Delta \mathrm{FA}}{(y_T + \mathrm{FA}_0)^2} < 0\). Thus, economies with a larger traded goods sector experience a smaller percentage price collapse in non-traded goods for a given dollar sudden stop.

P2. Prove that an economy with higher foreign liability dollarization \(D^*\) exhibits a strictly higher critical threshold \(\mathrm{FA}^*\), making it more fragile to sudden stops.

Solution

The insolvency threshold is \(\mathrm{FA}^* = y_N \left(\frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\right) \frac{D^*}{L_N} - y_T\). Differentiating with respect to \(D^*\): \(\frac{\partial \mathrm{FA}^*}{\partial D^*} = \frac{y_N}{L_N} \left(\frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\right) > 0\). Because \(\mathrm{FA}^*\) is strictly increasing in \(D^*\), a sovereign with higher dollar debt requires larger ongoing capital inflows simply to prevent bank insolvency; even a mild slowdown in inflows (\(\mathrm{FA} > 0\)) can trigger insolvency if \(D^*\) is sufficiently high.

P3. Prove that if non-traded production exhibits sticky wages, a sudden stop induces involuntary unemployment in the non-traded goods sector.

Solution

Let non-traded production be \(y_N = F(L_N) = L_N^\alpha\) with \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\). Profit-maximizing firms hire labor until marginal revenue product equals nominal wage: \(p_N \alpha L_N^{\alpha - 1} = W\). If nominal wages \(W = \bar{W}\) are sticky downwards, then \(L_N = \left( \frac{\alpha p_N}{\bar{W}} \right)^{\frac{1}{1 - \alpha}}\). By Theorem 1.2, a sudden stop collapses \(p_N\). Because \(\frac{\partial L_N}{\partial p_N} = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \frac{L_N}{p_N} > 0\), a drop in \(p_N\) forces firms to cut labor demand, generating involuntary unemployment in the non-traded sector.

Empirical

E1. In the 1997 Korean financial crisis, foreign short-term bank debt exceeded 100 billion USD with foreign reserves under 10 billion USD. When Japanese and European banks refused to roll over short-term interbank lines, the won depreciated from 900 to 1800 KRW/USD. Explain how this sudden stop transformed solvent chaebols into bankrupt entities.

Solution

Korean industrial conglomerates (chaebols) had high debt-to-equity ratios (often exceeding 400%), heavily financed by short-term dollar borrowing through domestic merchant banks. When the won halved in value (100% depreciation against USD), chaebol debt service doubled in domestic currency terms. Because chaebol revenues were largely domestic won, cash flows were insufficient to cover dollar interest, causing widespread bankruptcies that collapsed the merchant banking system.

E2. In Iceland's 2008 banking crisis, the three major banks (Kaupthing, Landsbanki, Glitnir) accumulated foreign assets exceeding 10 times Iceland's annual GDP. Why was the Central Bank of Iceland powerless to act as a lender of last resort?

Solution

A central bank can print unlimited quantities of domestic fiat currency (Icelandic krona). However, bank liabilities were denominated in euros, British pounds, and Dutch retail deposits (Icesave). The Central Bank of Iceland had foreign exchange reserves of less than 4 billion USD against over 100 billion USD in foreign bank liabilities. Because it could not print foreign currency and lacked central bank swap lines, it could not function as an international lender of last resort, resulting in total banking collapse.

E3. During the 1998 Russian financial crisis, the Russian government defaulted on domestic ruble-denominated debt (GKOs) and devalued the ruble. Why did defaulting on domestic currency debt represent an unprecedented institutional break?

Solution

Governments can technically always print domestic currency to avoid nominal default on local-currency obligations. Russia defaulted on GKOs because printing rubles to redeem 40 billion USD equivalent in short-term bills would have triggered hyperinflation. Choosing open default over hyperinflation protected price stability at the expense of sovereign credit reputation, wiping out the domestic commercial banking sector.

Research

R1. How do central bank macroprudential policies (such as caps on loan-to-value ratios and reserve requirements on foreign-currency borrowing) reduce the probability of a Calvo sudden stop?

Solution

Macroprudential reserve requirements on foreign-currency bank liabilities increase the effective cost of dollar funding, forcing banks to internalize the systemic fire-sale externality of sudden stops (Korinek 2018). By limiting gross external leverage \(D^*\) and restricting unhedged borrowing, macroprudential rules lower the critical insolvency threshold \(\mathrm{FA}^*\), ensuring the financial system remains solvent even during severe capital outflow shocks.

R2. In the sovereign-bank "doom loop" (Acharya et al. 2014, Farhi & Tirole 2018), explain the two-way feedback mechanism between sovereign credit risk and domestic bank solvency.

Solution

The feedback operates through two self-reinforcing channels: (1) Bank-to-Sovereign: When private banks experience distress, the government guarantees deposits or injects recapitalization funds, converting private debt into sovereign debt and worsening sovereign solvency. (2) Sovereign-to-Bank: Domestic banks hold large portfolios of domestic sovereign bonds. A sovereign rating downgrade reduces bond prices, depleting bank regulatory capital, restricting credit creation, contracting GDP, and triggering a systemic banking crisis.

R3. Evaluate the empirical finding in Reinhart and Rogoff (2009) that public debt surges by an average of 86% following a banking crisis: why are revenue collapses far larger than bailout costs?

Solution

Public perceptions often attribute post-crisis debt surges to direct bank bailout cash transfers. However, empirical decomposition reveals that direct recapitalization transfers account for less than a quarter of the debt surge. The overwhelming driver is the severe, persistent contraction in real GDP growth and tax revenues (corporate taxes, VAT, personal income taxes), combined with automatic stabilizer spending, which generates multi-year primary fiscal deficits that compound debt.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What defines a "sudden stop" in international macroeconomics (Calvo 1998)?

    Options

    Calvo (1998) defines a sudden stop as an acute reversal of capital inflows forcing immediate external adjustment.

  2. Why does the relative price of non-traded goods \(p_N\) collapse during a sudden stop?

    Options

    Under market clearing \(c_N = y_N\), the drop in traded consumption \(c_T = y_T + \mathrm{FA}\) drives down the relative price \(p_N\).

  3. If \(\gamma = 0.50\), \(y_T = 100\), \(y_N = 100\), and \(\mathrm{FA} = +20\), what is the equilibrium relative price of non-traded goods \(p_N\)?

    \(p_N = \frac{1 - 0.5}{0.5} \times \frac{100 + 20}{100} = 1.0 \times 1.20 = 1.20\).

  4. What is "original sin" on bank and corporate balance sheets?

    Options

    Original sin refers to foreign-currency liabilities that cannot be hedged, leading to balance-sheet distress upon depreciation.

  5. If \(y_T = 100\), \(y_N = 100\), \(\gamma = 0.50\), \(L_N = 120\), and \(D^* = 110\), what is the critical capital flow \(\mathrm{FA}^*\) below which bank capital turns negative?

    \(\mathrm{FA}^* = 100 \times 1.0 \times \frac{110}{120} - 100 = 91.67 - 100 = -8.33\).

  6. According to Reinhart and Rogoff (2009), what typically happens to public debt in the three years following a systemic banking crisis?

    Options

    Historical evidence documents an average 86% surge in real public debt post-banking crisis.

  7. Why is an emerging market central bank limited in its ability to rescue domestic banks during a sudden stop?

    Options

    Lender-of-last-resort capability in foreign currency is strictly constrained by usable foreign reserves.

  8. What is the "sovereign-bank doom loop"?

    Options

    The doom loop describes reciprocal balance-sheet contagion between banks and sovereign treasuries.
