Public debt sustainability hinges on the differential between the real interest rate government pays on its liabilities and the real growth rate of output. When real growth exceeds the borrowing rate, public debt can roll over indefinitely without fiscal cost or tax increases. However, if macroeconomic conditions reverse, a sign flip in this differential transforms a benign debt path into an explosive sovereign debt crisis.

1The Government Budget Constraint and Exact Debt Dynamics

Definition 1.1 (nominal government budget constraint)

Let \(B_t\) denote total nominal government debt outstanding at the end of period \(t\), \(i_t\) the nominal effective interest rate paid on debt issued at \(t-1\), \(G_t\) nominal government primary expenditure (excluding interest payments), \(T_t\) nominal government tax revenues, and \(\Delta M_t\) nominal central bank seigniorage creation. The flow budget constraint of the sovereign is:

\[ B_t = (1 + i_t) B_{t-1} + (G_t - T_t) - \Delta M_t \]

In economies without direct monetary financing (\(\Delta M_t = 0\)), the primary deficit is \(D_t = G_t - T_t\), and the primary surplus is \(S_t = T_t - G_t = -D_t\).

Theorem 1.2 (exact discrete-time public debt dynamics)

Let \(Y_t\) denote real gross domestic product, \(P_t\) the GDP price deflator, \(b_t = B_t / (P_t Y_t)\) the debt-to-GDP ratio, \(s_t = S_t / (P_t Y_t)\) the primary fiscal surplus as a share of GDP, \(\pi_t = (P_t - P_{t-1}) / P_{t-1}\) the inflation rate, \(g_t = (Y_t - Y_{t-1}) / Y_{t-1}\) the real GDP growth rate, and \(r_t = (1 + i_t) / (1 + \pi_t) - 1\) the ex-post real interest rate. The exact recurrence relation governing the public debt-to-GDP ratio is:

\[ b_t = \left(\frac{1 + r_t}{1 + g_t}\right) b_{t-1} - s_t \]
Proof

Begin with the nominal flow identity without seigniorage: \(B_t = (1 + i_t) B_{t-1} - S_t\). Divide both sides by nominal GDP \(P_t Y_t\):

\[ \frac{B_t}{P_t Y_t} = (1 + i_t) \frac{B_{t-1}}{P_t Y_t} - \frac{S_t}{P_t Y_t} \]

Expand the denominator of the lagged debt term using nominal GDP growth \(P_t Y_t = P_{t-1} (1 + \pi_t) Y_{t-1} (1 + g_t)\):

\[ b_t = (1 + i_t) \frac{B_{t-1}}{P_{t-1} Y_{t-1} (1 + \pi_t)(1 + g_t)} - s_t = \frac{1 + i_t}{(1 + \pi_t)(1 + g_t)} b_{t-1} - s_t \]

Substituting the definition of the real interest rate \(1 + r_t = (1 + i_t)/(1 + \pi_t)\) yields:

\[ b_t = \left(\frac{1 + r_t}{1 + g_t}\right) b_{t-1} - s_t \]

This completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.3 (truncation error in linear approximations)

Writers frequently employ the first-order Taylor expansion \(\frac{1+r_t}{1+g_t} \approx 1 + r_t - g_t\), writing \(\Delta b_t \approx (r_t - g_t) b_{t-1} - s_t\). The truncation error is:

\[ \frac{1 + r_t}{1 + g_t} - (1 + r_t - g_t) = \frac{g_t (g_t - r_t)}{1 + g_t} \]

For high debt ratios (\(b \ge 1.0\)) and multi-period horizons, this error compounds significantly, making exact discrete-time modeling mandatory for quantitative debt sustainability analysis.

2The \(r - g\) Differential and Blanchard's Thesis

Proposition 2.1 (steady-state debt ratio and stability)

Assume constant rates \(r_t = r\), \(g_t = g\), and constant primary balance \(s_t = s\). The debt accumulation equation admits a unique stationary steady state \(\bar{b}\) if and only if \(\frac{1+r}{1+g} \ne 1\), given by:

\[ \bar{b} = \frac{-s}{\frac{r - g}{1 + g}} \]

The stability properties depend strictly on the sign of \(r - g\):

  • If \(r < g\) (\(\frac{1+r}{1+g} < 1\)): the steady state \(\bar{b}\) is globally asymptotically stable. A government running a persistent primary deficit (\(s < 0\)) converges to a strictly positive bounded debt ratio \(\bar{b} > 0\).
  • If \(r > g\) (\(\frac{1+r}{1+g} > 1\)): the steady state is unstable. Any initial debt \(b_0 > \bar{b}\) triggers an explosive divergence towards infinity unless primary surpluses are increased.
Proof

At a stationary steady state \(\bar{b}\), the recurrence satisfies \(\bar{b} = \left(\frac{1+r}{1+g}\right) \bar{b} - s\). Rearranging gives \(\left[ 1 - \frac{1+r}{1+g} \right] \bar{b} = -s\). Factoring the bracketed expression yields \(\frac{g - r}{1 + g} \bar{b} = -s\), which gives \(\bar{b} = \frac{-s}{(r - g)/(1 + g)}\). Let \(e_t = b_t - \bar{b}\) be the deviation from steady state. Then \(e_t = \left(\frac{1+r}{1+g}\right) e_{t-1} = \left(\frac{1+r}{1+g}\right)^t e_0\). If \(r < g\), \(\left|\frac{1+r}{1+g}\right| < 1\), so \(\lim_{t \to \infty} e_t = 0\), establishing global asymptotic stability. If \(r > g\), \(\frac{1+r}{1+g} > 1\), so deviations grow without bound, establishing instability. \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 2.2 (Blanchard 2019 low-rate fiscal irrelevance)

Blanchard (2019) demonstrates that across advanced economies—and specifically the United States from 1950 to 2018—the average real effective interest rate on public debt has been systematically lower than the real GDP growth rate (\(r < g\) on average). Under these empirical conditions, issuing public debt to finance a one-off deficit does not require a future increase in taxes to keep debt sustainable; the debt ratio naturally erodes to zero over time through economic growth.

Example 2.3 (debt roll-over arithmetic)

Suppose real growth is \(g = 3.0\%\) and the real borrowing rate is \(r = 1.0\%\), so \(r - g = -2.0\%\). The sovereign begins with \(b_0 = 0.80\) (80% of GDP) and runs a permanent primary deficit of \(1.5\%\) of GDP (\(s = -0.015\)). What is the steady-state debt ratio?

\[ \bar{b} = \frac{-(-0.015)}{\frac{0.01 - 0.03}{1 + 0.03}} = \frac{0.015}{-0.02 / 1.03} = \frac{0.015 \times 1.03}{-0.02} = \text{invalid sign convention if s is deficit} \]

Notice that \(\frac{1+r}{1+g} = \frac{1.01}{1.03} \approx 0.98058\). The difference equation is \(b_t = 0.98058 b_{t-1} + 0.015\). At steady state, \(\bar{b} (1 - 0.98058) = 0.015 \implies \bar{b} = 0.015 / 0.019417 = 0.7725\) (77.25% of GDP). Even though the government runs a permanent primary deficit of 1.5% each year, debt converges from 80% down to 77.25%.

3Safe Assets and Dynamic Inefficiency

Proposition 3.1 (welfare effects of debt under dynamic inefficiency)

When the marginal product of capital or sovereign borrowing rate is below the rate of economic growth (\(r < g\)), the economy exhibits dynamic inefficiency or a high convenience yield on safe public debt. In an overlapping generations (OLG) framework, issuing public debt crowds out excess capital accumulation, transferring resources across generations and increasing Pareto welfare without imposing fiscal burdens.

Lab 1 · Public Debt Dynamics: Exact vs Linear Simulation

Under \(r < g\), exact discrete-time public debt dynamics converge to a bounded steady state despite primary deficits. An adverse shock flipping \(r - g\) to positive territory triggers explosive debt divergence, demonstrating the non-linear rollover risks of high public debt.

"""Lab 1: Public-debt dynamics, r minus g, and interest-rate-growth differentials.

SIMULATED 20-year debt trajectories. Standard library only.
Verifies exact discrete-time debt-to-GDP accumulation versus the linear approximation,
evaluating debt stability under r < g and explosive dynamics under r > g.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def debt_step_exact(b_prev: float, r: float, g: float, s: float) -> float:
    """Compute exact discrete-time debt-to-GDP ratio for next period.

    Identity:
    b_t = [(1 + r) / (1 + g)] * b_{t-1} - s
    where:
    b is debt-to-GDP ratio,
    r is real interest rate,
    g is real GDP growth rate,
    s is primary balance as share of GDP (positive for surplus, negative for deficit).
    """
    if g <= -1.0:
        msg = f"Real GDP growth rate must be strictly greater than -1.0, got {g}"
        raise ValueError(msg)
    return ((1.0 + r) / (1.0 + g)) * b_prev - s


def debt_step_approx(b_prev: float, r: float, g: float, s: float) -> float:
    """Compute first-order linear approximation of debt-to-GDP ratio.

    Approximation:
    b_t = (1 + r - g) * b_{t-1} - s
    """
    return (1.0 + r - g) * b_prev - s


def steady_state_debt(r: float, g: float, s: float) -> float:
    """Compute stationary debt ratio b* when (1 + r) / (1 + g) < 1.

    At steady state:
    b* = -s / [ (r - g) / (1 + g) ]
    """
    growth_factor = (1.0 + r) / (1.0 + g)
    if growth_factor >= 1.0:
        msg = f"No stable steady state exists when (1 + r) / (1 + g) >= 1.0: factor={growth_factor:.4f}"
        raise ValueError(msg)
    return -s / (1.0 - growth_factor)


def main() -> None:
    # Baseline parameters (Blanchard 2019 regime: r < g)
    b0 = 0.80  # 80% initial debt-to-GDP
    r_low = 0.01  # 1.0% real rate
    g_normal = 0.03  # 3.0% real growth
    s_deficit = -0.015  # 1.5% primary deficit

    # 1. Verify steady state under r < g
    b_star = steady_state_debt(r_low, g_normal, s_deficit)

    # 2. Simulate 20 years under regime transition at t = 10
    # Years 1-10: r < g (r = 1.0%, g = 3.0%, s = -1.5%)
    # Years 11-20: r > g (r = 0.045, g = 0.015, s = -1.5% - sign flip shock)
    exact_path: list[float] = [b0]
    approx_path: list[float] = [b0]

    b_curr_exact = b0
    b_curr_approx = b0

    for t in range(1, 21):
        if t <= 10:
            r = r_low
            g = g_normal
        else:
            r = 0.045
            g = 0.015
        s = s_deficit

        b_curr_exact = debt_step_exact(b_curr_exact, r, g, s)
        b_curr_approx = debt_step_approx(b_curr_approx, r, g, s)

        exact_path.append(b_curr_exact)
        approx_path.append(b_curr_approx)

    print("=== Public Debt Dynamics: Exact vs Linear Approximation ===")
    print(f"Initial Debt Ratio b0:           {b0:.4f}")
    print(f"Regime 1 (r < g): r={r_low:.4f}, g={g_normal:.4f}, r-g={r_low - g_normal:.4f}, s={s_deficit:.4f}")
    print(f"Steady-State Debt Ratio b*:      {b_star:.4f}")
    print(f"Year 5 Debt Ratio (Exact):       {exact_path[5]:.4f}")
    print(f"Year 5 Debt Ratio (Approx):      {approx_path[5]:.4f}")
    print(f"Year 10 Debt Ratio (Exact):      {exact_path[10]:.4f}")
    print(f"Year 10 Debt Ratio (Approx):     {approx_path[10]:.4f}")
    print("\n=== Regime 2 Shock (t=11..20: r=0.045, g=0.015, r-g=+0.030) ===")
    print(f"Year 15 Debt Ratio (Exact):      {exact_path[15]:.4f}")
    print(f"Year 15 Debt Ratio (Approx):     {approx_path[15]:.4f}")
    print(f"Year 20 Debt Ratio (Exact):      {exact_path[20]:.4f}")
    print(f"Year 20 Debt Ratio (Approx):     {approx_path[20]:.4f}")
    print(f"Approximation Error at Year 20:  {abs(exact_path[20] - approx_path[20]):.4f}")

    assert 0.77 <= b_star <= 0.78, "Steady state debt ratio must be approximately 77.25%"
    assert exact_path[10] < b0, "Debt ratio must decline under r < g despite running primary deficit"
    assert exact_path[20] > 1.0, "Debt ratio must explode above 100% after r > g reversal"

4Where the Canon Breaks

The seductive conclusion that "\(r < g\) implies public debt is free" breaks down decisively in the presence of rollover risk, endogenous interest rates, and tail risk shocks:

  • Endogenous Interest Rate Spreads: The borrowing rate \(r\) is not exogenous to the debt level. As the debt ratio rises, sovereign credit risk premia expand non-linearly (\(r(b) = r^* + \sigma(b)\)), rapidly flipping the sign of \(r - g\) from negative to positive.
  • Tail Risk and Asymmetry: While \(r < g\) holds in the sample median for peaceful advanced economies, crises are characterized by sharp growth collapses (\(g \ll 0\)) accompanied by sovereign risk spread spikes (\(r \gg 0\)). During sovereign crises, \((r - g)\) can suddenly widen to \(+10\%\) or higher, causing sudden debt explosion.
  • Fiscal Fatigue: Governments cannot credibly promise arbitrarily high primary surpluses if macroeconomic conditions worsen. Empirical evidence demonstrates that fiscal adjustment capacity hits an institutional ceiling at 2% to 4% of GDP.

Misconceptions

  1. A country running a primary deficit will always experience an expanding debt-to-GDP ratio.

    False. If the real GDP growth rate exceeds the real interest rate on debt (\(g > r\)), the denominator of the debt-to-GDP ratio expands faster than debt accumulates through interest charges. As shown in Proposition 2.1, a government can sustain a permanent primary deficit while stabilizing its debt ratio at a finite bounded steady state.

  2. Because \(r < g\) has held historically in the United States, public debt expansion carries zero macroeconomic risk.

    False. Blanchard (2019) explicitly emphasizes that debt remains risky because \(r - g\) can flip sign during financial distress. If inflation shocks or market liquidity dry-ups cause \(r\) to rise above \(g\), a high debt ratio magnifies the required fiscal consolidation into severe austerity.

  3. The linear approximation \(\Delta b_t \approx (r_t - g_t) b_{t-1} - s_t\) is interchangeable with the exact discrete-time formula.

    False. The exact formula uses the quotient \((1 + r) / (1 + g)\). The linear approximation omits higher-order cross-terms \(g(g - r) / (1 + g)\). Over a 10-to-20-year horizon with volatile growth, this omission introduces cumulative forecasting errors of several percentage points of GDP.

Problem Set

Mechanics

M1. A sovereign has an initial debt-to-GDP ratio of \(b_0 = 1.00\) (100% of GDP). Over the next three years, the real effective borrowing rate is \(r = 4.0\%\), real GDP growth is \(g = 2.0\%\), and the government runs a primary surplus of \(1.0\%\) of GDP (\(s = 0.01\)) each year. Compute the exact debt-to-GDP ratio \(b_3\) at the end of year 3.

Solution

The exact accumulation recurrence is \(b_t = \frac{1+r}{1+g} b_{t-1} - s\). The growth factor is \(\frac{1.04}{1.02} = 1.0196078\). Step 1: \(b_1 = 1.0196078 \times 1.00 - 0.01 = 1.0096078\). Step 2: \(b_2 = 1.0196078 \times 1.0096078 - 0.01 = 1.0294038 - 0.01 = 1.0194038\). Step 3: \(b_3 = 1.0196078 \times 1.0194038 - 0.01 = 1.0393922 - 0.01 = 1.0293922 \approx 1.0294\) (102.94% of GDP). Despite running a 1% primary surplus, debt ratio expands because \((r - g)/(1+g) \times b_0 = (0.02/1.02) \times 1.0 = 1.96\% > 1.0\%\).

M2. A government has \(b_0 = 0.60\), \(r = 2.0\%\), and \(g = 3.5\%\). If the government runs a permanent primary deficit of \(s = -0.012\) (1.2% deficit), determine whether the debt ratio is stable, and calculate the stationary steady-state debt ratio \(\bar{b}\).

Solution

Because \(r = 0.02 < g = 0.035\), the growth factor is \(\frac{1.02}{1.035} \approx 0.985507 < 1\). The difference equation is globally stable. The steady state is \(\bar{b} = \frac{-s}{1 - (1+r)/(1+g)} = \frac{0.012}{1 - 0.985507} = \frac{0.012}{0.014493} = 0.82799 \approx 0.8280\) (82.80% of GDP). The debt ratio converges smoothly from 60% up to 82.8%.

M3. Calculate the primary surplus \(s^*\) required to stabilize public debt at \(b^* = 0.85\) when \(r = 4.5\%\) and \(g = 1.5\%\). If the actual primary balance is \(s = -0.005\), what is the primary fiscal gap?

Solution

The debt-stabilizing primary balance satisfies \(s^* = \frac{r - g}{1 + g} b^* = \frac{0.045 - 0.015}{1 + 0.015} \times 0.85 = \frac{0.030}{1.015} \times 0.85 = 0.029557 \times 0.85 = 0.025123\) (2.51% of GDP). The primary gap is \(\mathrm{gap} = s^* - s = 0.025123 - (-0.005) = 0.030123 \approx 3.01\%\) of GDP. The government must implement 3.01% of GDP in net fiscal tightening.

Proof

P1. Prove that if \(\frac{1+r}{1+g} > 1\) and the government maintains a constant primary deficit \(d = -s > 0\), the debt-to-GDP ratio \(b_t\) grows exponentially toward infinity from any initial debt level \(b_0 > 0\).

Solution

Let \(\lambda = \frac{1+r}{1+g} > 1\). The first-order linear recurrence is \(b_t = \lambda b_{t-1} + d\). Solving by backward induction: \(b_1 = \lambda b_0 + d\), \(b_2 = \lambda^2 b_0 + \lambda d + d\), and in general \(b_t = \lambda^t b_0 + d \sum_{k=0}^{t-1} \lambda^k\). Using the finite geometric sum formula for \(\lambda \ne 1\): \(b_t = \lambda^t b_0 + d \frac{\lambda^t - 1}{\lambda - 1} = \lambda^t \left( b_0 + \frac{d}{\lambda - 1} \right) - \frac{d}{\lambda - 1}\). Because \(\lambda > 1\), \(b_0 > 0\), and \(d > 0\), the coefficient \(\left( b_0 + \frac{d}{\lambda - 1} \right) > 0\). As \(t \to \infty\), \(\lambda^t \to \infty\), which implies \(\lim_{t \to \infty} b_t = +\infty\). Thus, debt explodes exponentially.

P2. Show that the cumulative truncation error between the exact discrete-time path \(b_t\) and the linear approximation path \(\tilde{b}_t\) over horizon \(t\) satisfies \(b_t - \tilde{b}_t = b_0 \left[ \left(\frac{1+r}{1+g}\right)^t - (1 + r - g)^t \right]\) under zero primary balances (\(s = 0\)).

Solution

Under zero primary balance (\(s_t = 0\)), the exact path satisfies \(b_t = \left(\frac{1+r}{1+g}\right) b_{t-1} = \left(\frac{1+r}{1+g}\right)^t b_0\). The linear approximation path satisfies \(\tilde{b}_t = (1 + r - g) \tilde{b}_{t-1} = (1 + r - g)^t b_0\). Taking the difference directly: \(b_t - \tilde{b}_t = b_0 \left[ \left(\frac{1+r}{1+g}\right)^t - (1 + r - g)^t \right]\). Because \(\frac{1+r}{1+g} = (1 + r)(1 - g + g^2 - \dots) = 1 + r - g - rg + g^2 + \dots > 1 + r - g\) whenever \(g^2 > rg \iff g > r\) for positive rates, the exact compounding term strictly exceeds the linear approximation, causing the approximation to underestimate debt accumulation.

P3. Prove that under the No-Ponzi-Game (NPG) condition with \(r > g\), the current debt-to-GDP ratio \(b_0\) must equal the present discounted value of all future primary surpluses as a share of GDP.

Solution

Iterating the recurrence \(b_t = \lambda b_{t-1} - s_t\) forward: \(b_1 = \lambda b_0 - s_1 \implies b_0 = \lambda^{-1} s_1 + \lambda^{-1} b_1\). Continuing for \(T\) periods: \(b_0 = \sum_{t=1}^T \lambda^{-t} s_t + \lambda^{-T} b_T\), where discount factor is \(\lambda^{-1} = \frac{1+g}{1+r}\). The No-Ponzi-Game condition requires that the present value of terminal debt vanishes: \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \lambda^{-T} b_T = 0\). Taking the limit as \(T \to \infty\) yields \(b_0 = \sum_{t=1}^\infty \left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^t s_t\). Thus, initial debt must be fully backed by the present value of future primary surpluses.

Empirical

E1. In Blanchard's (2019) sample of US historical data from 1950 to 2018, the average 10-year Treasury nominal yield was 5.6%, average CPI inflation was 3.5%, and average nominal GDP growth was 6.3%. Calculate the historical average \(r - g\) differential in percentage points.

Solution

Real interest rate is \(r = \frac{1 + 0.056}{1 + 0.035} - 1 = \frac{1.056}{1.035} - 1 = 1.02029 - 1 = 2.03\%\). Real GDP growth is \(g = \frac{1 + 0.063}{1 + 0.035} - 1 = \frac{1.063}{1.035} - 1 = 1.02705 - 1 = 2.71\%\). The differential is \(r - g = 2.03\% - 2.71\% = -0.68\%\) (approximately -68 basis points). In nominal terms: \(i - g_{\mathrm{nominal}} = 5.6\% - 6.3\% = -0.70\%\). Both measures confirm that \(r < g\) held on average over the post-WWII US history.

E2. A developing sovereign has 70% of its public debt denominated in US dollars and 30% in local currency. If the local currency depreciates by 25% against the US dollar in period \(t\), calculate the direct valuation impact on the sovereign's debt-to-GDP ratio assuming real output and domestic prices are initially unchanged.

Solution

Let total debt be \(b_0 = b_{\mathrm{dom}} + b_{\mathrm{fx}} = 0.30 b_0 + 0.70 b_0\). A 25% currency depreciation means the domestic price of foreign currency increases by 25% (\(S_t / S_{t-1} = 1.25\)). The foreign currency component of debt revalues to \(b_{\mathrm{fx}}' = 1.25 \times 0.70 b_0 = 0.875 b_0\). New total debt ratio is \(b' = 0.30 b_0 + 0.875 b_0 = 1.175 b_0\). If initial debt was \(b_0 = 0.80\) (80% of GDP), new debt ratio is \(1.175 \times 0.80 = 0.940\) (94% of GDP)—a 14 percentage point increase from currency revaluation alone.

E3. If a sovereign's credit rating is downgraded from A to BB, its 5-year sovereign credit default swap (CDS) spread rises by 300 basis points. In an economy where public debt is 90% of GDP with an average maturity of 4.5 years, what is the annualized rollover interest cost increase once all maturing debt is refinanced?

Solution

A spread widening of 300 basis points equals \(\Delta r = 0.0300\). When the entire debt stock is rolled over at the new yield, additional interest expenditure as a share of GDP is \(\Delta \text{Interest} = \Delta r \times b = 0.0300 \times 0.90 = 0.0270 = 2.70\%\) of GDP. Each year, roughly \(1 / 4.5 \approx 22.2\%\) of debt rolls over, adding \(0.222 \times 2.70\% \approx 0.60\%\) of GDP in annual interest burden, reaching the full 2.70% of GDP within 4.5 years.

Research

R1. Evaluate the hypothesis that safe-asset convenience yields (Krishnamurthy & Vissing-Jorgensen 2012) account for why advanced economies enjoy \(r < g\) while emerging market economies systematically face \(r > g\).

Solution

Advanced reserve currency issuers (such as the United States and Germany) issue safe assets that provide non-pecuniary convenience yields \(\gamma_t\), reducing the required cash return: \(r_t = r^* - \gamma_t\). Because global financial intermediaries face regulatory constraints (Basel III SLR, LCR) and demand collateral, \(\gamma_t\) averages 50 to 100 basis points. In contrast, emerging market sovereigns do not issue global safe assets; their sovereign bonds carry credit risk, illiquidity, and currency depreciation premia (\(\sigma_t > 0\)), leading to borrowing rates \(r_{\mathrm{EM}} = r^* + \sigma_t > g_{\mathrm{EM}}\). Consequently, EM sovereigns cannot rely on Blanchard's low-rate debt rollover.

R2. In a DSGE model with fiscal fatigue (Bohn 1998, Ghosh et al. 2013), the primary balance response function is non-linear: \(s(b) = \alpha + \beta_1 b - \beta_2 b^2\). Explain how this non-linearity generates a fiscal limit beyond which debt becomes uncontrollable.

Solution

At moderate debt levels, governments increase primary surpluses as debt grows (\(\beta_1 > 0\)). However, due to political resistance, maximum taxable capacity, and social spending rigidities, marginal fiscal consolidation diminishes (\(-\beta_2 b^2\)). The maximum achievable primary surplus is \(s_{\max} = \alpha + \frac{\beta_1^2}{4 \beta_2}\). The fiscal limit occurs at debt level \(\bar{b}_{\mathrm{crit}}\) where the required debt-stabilizing surplus \(\frac{r - g}{1+g} b\) exceeds \(s_{\max}\). Beyond this point, no politically feasible primary balance can prevent debt from exploding, triggering inevitable default or restructuring.

R3. How does inflation interact with public debt under unexpected monetary shocks versus indexed debt?

Solution

For nominal fixed-rate unindexed debt, unexpected inflation \(\pi_t > \pi_t^e\) reduces the real value of outstanding debt by \(\frac{1}{1 + \pi_t}\), acting as a capital levy on bondholders. However, if debt is indexed to inflation (e.g., TIPS) or foreign currency, unexpected inflation fails to reduce the real debt burden. Furthermore, if higher inflation forces the central bank to raise policy rates aggressively, new debt issues face sharply higher nominal and real interest rates, increasing refinancing costs and shortening the average maturity profile.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the exact recurrence relation for the public debt-to-GDP ratio \(b_t\)?

    Options

    Exact discrete-time derivation yields \(b_t = \frac{1 + r_t}{1 + g_t} b_{t-1} - s_t\).

  2. When real growth rate exceeds real interest rate (\(r < g\)), what happens to the debt-to-GDP ratio under a zero primary balance (\(s = 0\))?

    Options

    Because \(\frac{1+r}{1+g} < 1\), with \(s=0\), \(b_t = \left(\frac{1+r}{1+g}\right)^t b_0 \to 0\).

  3. If \(b_0 = 0.85\), \(r = 4.0\%\), \(g = 2.0\%\), and primary deficit is \(1.0\%\) of GDP (\(s = -0.01\)), what is the exact debt-to-GDP ratio \(b_1\)?

    \(b_1 = \frac{1.04}{1.02} \times 0.85 - (-0.01) = 1.019608 \times 0.85 + 0.01 = 0.866667 + 0.01 = 0.8767\).

  4. According to Blanchard (2019), what empirical regularity characterizes US public debt and economic growth over 1950–2018?

    Options

    Blanchard (2019) documents that the nominal interest rate on debt averaged below nominal GDP growth (\(r < g\)).

  5. Why does the linear approximation \(\Delta b \approx (r - g) b - s\) introduce material bias over multi-year horizons?

    Options

    The first-order expansion discards denominator interaction terms, causing compounding truncation error.

  6. What is the primary balance in percent of GDP (\(s^*\)) required to stabilize debt at \(b^* = 0.80\) when \(r = 4.0\%\) and \(g = 1.0\%\)?

    \(s^* = \frac{0.04 - 0.01}{1.01} \times 0.80 = \frac{0.03}{1.01} \times 0.80 = 0.02376 = 2.38\%\) of GDP.

  7. What is "sign-flip risk" in public debt dynamics?

    Options

    Sign-flip risk occurs when borrowing rates rise above growth rates (\(r > g\)), requiring large fiscal surpluses to prevent explosion.

  8. How does safe-asset convenience yield impact the sovereign's borrowing cost (Krishnamurthy & Vissing-Jorgensen 2012)?

    Options

    Convenience yields reduce sovereign debt yields because investors accept lower cash returns for liquidity and pledgeability.
