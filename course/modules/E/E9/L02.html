Unlike corporate borrowers who face legal liquidation under bankruptcy courts, sovereign states possess sovereign immunity. Foreign creditors cannot seize in-country physical infrastructure or tax revenues. Consequently, sovereign debt contracts are sustained not by collateral enforcement, but by the borrower's forward-looking willingness to pay, trading off debt service against the output penalties and market exclusion triggered by repudiation.

1Sovereign Immunity and Willingness to Pay

Definition 1.1 (sovereign immunity and strategic repudiation)

A sovereign default model formalizes international borrowing when legal enforcement is absent due to sovereign immunity. A sovereign defaults strategically when the continuation value of honoring debt service is strictly lower than the continuation value of default:

\[ V^D(y_t) > V^R(b_t, y_t) \]

where \(V^R\) is the sovereign value function under full debt repayment, \(V^D\) is the value function under repudiation, \(b_t\) is debt due, and \(y_t\) is current stochastic endowment output.

Proposition 1.2 (limits of legal attachment)

Foreign courts under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) and international law cannot attach or liquidate commercial assets situated within the sovereign's territory. Legal recovery is confined to external trade payments and offshore sovereign assets, making the threat of legal liquidation an ineffective contract enforcement device.

2The Eaton–Gersovitz (1981) Framework

Theorem 2.1 (Eaton–Gersovitz value functions and default cutoff)

In the Eaton and Gersovitz (1981) endowment economy with stochastic output \(y_t \sim F(y)\), an output penalty \(\delta \in (0, 1)\) upon default, and temporary exclusion from international capital markets, the sovereign default decision follows a cutoff rule. If the sovereign repays debt \(b_t\):

\[ V^R(b_t, y_t) = u\left(y_t - b_t + q(b_{t+1}, y_t) b_{t+1}\right) + \beta \E_t\left[V(b_{t+1}, y_{t+1})\right] \]

If the sovereign defaults:

\[ V^D(y_t) = u\left(y_t (1 - \delta)\right) + \beta \E_t\left[\theta V(0, y_{t+1}) + (1 - \theta) V^D(y_{t+1})\right] \]

where \(\theta \in [0, 1]\) is the exogenous probability of market re-entry. For any debt level \(b_{t+1} = b'\), default occurs in period \(t+1\) if and only if realization \(y_{t+1}\) falls below an endogenous threshold \(y^*(b')\):

\[ y_{t+1} < y^*(b') \implies \text{Default} \]
Proof

Consider the static period-2 trade-off without future borrowing. The sovereign compares consumption under repayment \(c^R = y - b\) with consumption under default \(c^D = y(1 - \delta) = y - \delta y\). For any strictly increasing concave utility function \(u(c)\), the sovereign defaults if and only if:

\[ u(y(1 - \delta)) > u(y - b) \iff y(1 - \delta) > y - b \iff \delta y < b \iff y < \frac{b}{\delta} \]

Define the default cutoff as \(y^*(b) = b / \delta\). For all output realizations \(y < y^*(b)\), default yields strictly higher current consumption because the proportional sanction \(\delta y\) is smaller than the fixed debt obligation \(b\). In the dynamic infinite-horizon case with concave utility, marginal utility of consumption \(u'(y - b)\) is highest in low-output states, reinforcing the property that default occurs in recessions where \(y_t < y^*(b')\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Endogenous Bond Pricing and the Debt Laffer Curve

Definition 3.1 (endogenous bond pricing schedule)

Let foreign lenders be competitive and risk-neutral with opportunity cost equal to the world risk-free rate \(r^*\). The equilibrium bond price \(q(b')\) for one-period sovereign discount bonds paying 1 unit in period \(t+1\) upon repayment satisfies:

\[ q(b') = \frac{1 - \Prob\left(\text{default} \mid b'\right)}{1 + r^*} = \frac{1 - F(y^*(b'))}{1 + r^*} \]

where \(F\) is the cumulative distribution function of output.

Theorem 3.2 (the sovereign Debt Laffer curve)

Total borrowing proceeds raised by the sovereign, \(\mathrm{Rev}(b') = q(b') b'\), are non-monotonic in debt issuance \(b'\). There exists a maximum revenue-generating debt capacity \(b^*_{\max}\) beyond which issuing additional nominal debt strictly reduces borrowing proceeds:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathrm{Rev}(b')}{\partial b'} = q(b') + b' \frac{\partial q(b')}{\partial b'} = 0 \quad \text{at } b^*_{\max} \]
Proof

Differentiating \(\mathrm{Rev}(b') = \frac{1 - F(y^*(b'))}{1 + r^*} b'\) with respect to \(b'\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathrm{Rev}(b')}{\partial b'} = \frac{1 - F(y^*(b'))}{1 + r^*} - \frac{f(y^*(b')) \frac{\partial y^*(b')}{\partial b'}}{1 + r^*} b' \]

For \(b' = 0\), \(y^*(0) = 0\), so \(\frac{\partial \mathrm{Rev}}{\partial b'}\Big|_{b'=0} = \frac{1}{1 + r^*} > 0\). As \(b'\) increases, \(y^*(b') = b' / \delta\) increases, so \(F(y^*(b')) \to 1\), and \(q(b') \to 0\). For sufficiently high \(b'\), \(y^*(b') \ge y_{\max}\), implying \(\Prob(\text{default}) = 1\) and \(\mathrm{Rev}(b') = 0\). By Rolle's theorem on the continuous function \(\mathrm{Rev}(b')\) on \([0, \bar{b}]\), there exists an interior point \(b^*_{\max} \in (0, \bar{b})\) where \(\frac{\partial \mathrm{Rev}}{\partial b'} = 0\), maximizing total debt issuance proceeds. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Sovereign Default Schedule and Debt Laffer Curve

Under the Eaton–Gersovitz model with uniform output distribution and a 20% default sanction penalty, the endogenous bond pricing schedule drops non-linearly with debt issuance, producing a peak in the Debt Laffer curve beyond which borrowing capacity contracts.

"""Lab 2: Sovereign default and willingness to pay (Eaton & Gersovitz 1981).

SIMULATED discrete-state endowment economy. Standard library only.
Computes default thresholds, endogenous bond price schedule q(b),
and the sovereign Debt Laffer curve with borrowing capacity limits.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def default_threshold(debt_issued: float, sanction_penalty_fraction: float) -> float:
    """Compute output threshold y* below which default is optimal.

    Under linear default penalty y_def = y * (1 - delta):
    Repayment utility u(y - b) vs Default utility u(y * (1 - delta)).
    For any strictly increasing utility function u:
    Default iff y * (1 - delta) > y - b <=> delta * y < b <=> y < b / delta.
    """
    if sanction_penalty_fraction <= 0.0 or sanction_penalty_fraction > 1.0:
        msg = f"Sanction penalty fraction must be in (0, 1], got {sanction_penalty_fraction}"
        raise ValueError(msg)
    return debt_issued / sanction_penalty_fraction


def default_probability(
    debt_issued: float,
    states: list[float],
    probabilities: list[float],
    sanction_penalty_fraction: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute probability of sovereign default given debt issuance b'."""
    y_star = default_threshold(debt_issued, sanction_penalty_fraction)
    prob_default = sum(p for y, p in zip(states, probabilities, strict=True) if y < y_star)
    return prob_default


def bond_price(
    debt_issued: float,
    states: list[float],
    probabilities: list[float],
    sanction_penalty_fraction: float,
    risk_free_rate: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute competitive bond price schedule q(b') under risk neutrality.

    q(b') = (1 - p(b')) / (1 + r*)
    """
    p_def = default_probability(debt_issued, states, probabilities, sanction_penalty_fraction)
    return (1.0 - p_def) / (1.0 + risk_free_rate)


def main() -> None:
    # Endowment distribution: 10 discrete output states from 70 to 130
    states = [70.0, 76.67, 83.33, 90.0, 96.67, 103.33, 110.0, 116.67, 123.33, 130.0]
    n_states = len(states)
    probabilities = [1.0 / n_states] * n_states  # uniform discrete distribution

    delta = 0.20  # 20% output penalty upon default
    r_star = 0.04  # 4% risk-free world interest rate

    debt_levels = [0.0, 10.0, 15.0, 18.0, 20.0, 22.0, 24.0, 26.0, 28.0]

    prices: list[float] = []
    revenues: list[float] = []
    probs: list[float] = []

    for b in debt_levels:
        p_def = default_probability(b, states, probabilities, delta)
        q = bond_price(b, states, probabilities, delta, r_star)
        rev = q * b
        probs.append(p_def)
        prices.append(q)
        revenues.append(rev)

    # Find peak of Debt Laffer curve
    max_rev = max(revenues)
    max_idx = revenues.index(max_rev)
    b_star_laffer = debt_levels[max_idx]

    print("=== Sovereign Default Schedule & Debt Laffer Curve ===")
    print(f"World Risk-Free Rate r*:        {r_star:.4f}")
    print(f"Sanction Penalty Fraction delta: {delta:.4f}")
    print(f"Endowment Support Range:         [{states[0]:.2f}, {states[-1]:.2f}]")
    print("\nDebt Issued | Default Prob | Bond Price q(b) | Total Revenue q*b")
    for b, p, q, rev in zip(debt_levels, probs, prices, revenues, strict=True):
        print(f"  {b:6.2f}    |    {p:6.4f}    |     {q:6.4f}    |    {rev:6.4f}")

    print("\n=== Debt Laffer Peak Analysis ===")
    print(f"Maximum Borrowing Revenue:       {max_rev:.4f}")
    print(f"Revenue-Maximizing Debt Level:   {b_star_laffer:.2f}")
    print(f"Default Risk at Laffer Peak:     {probs[max_idx]:.4f}")
    print(f"Bond Price at Laffer Peak:       {prices[max_idx]:.4f}")

    # Verify Laffer curve properties: non-monotonic revenue
    assert revenues[-1] < max_rev, "Revenue beyond peak must be lower than peak (Debt Laffer property)"
    assert prices[0] > prices[-1], "Bond price must decrease as default risk rises"


main()

4Where the Canon Breaks

The standard Eaton–Gersovitz framework abstracts from key institutional realities that dominate real-world debt restructurings:

  • Bulow–Rogoff Impossibility: Bulow and Rogoff (1989) proved that if sovereigns can invest their trade surpluses in risk-free cash accounts abroad that cannot be seized, the threat of exclusion from international borrowing alone cannot support positive sovereign debt. Pure reputation for repayment is insufficient without direct trade sanctions or domestic banking costs.
  • Holdout Creditors and Pari Passu: Modern sovereign debt restructurings face collective action frictions. Distressed debt hedge funds ("vulture funds") buy defaulted bonds at deep discounts and litigate in New York and London courts under pari passu clauses to block payments to restructured creditors (as in the Argentina NML Capital litigation).
  • Domestic Debt and Financial Repression: Governments frequently default selectively—inflating away domestic-currency debt or restructuring foreign-law debt while continuing to service local banks to prevent financial collapse.

Misconceptions

  1. A sovereign defaults when its cash revenues drop below scheduled debt repayments (mechanical inability to pay).

    False. Sovereigns possess extensive powers of taxation, expenditure reduction, and state asset disposal. Sovereign default is an endogenous economic decision: governments default when the domestic economic and political cost of continued fiscal extraction exceeds the cost of sanctions and exclusion (willingness to pay).

  2. A credit-constrained sovereign can always borrow additional funds by offering a sufficiently high contractual interest rate.

    False. As proven in Theorem 3.2 (Debt Laffer curve), beyond the peak debt level \(b^*_{\max}\), increasing contractual repayment promises increases default probability faster than the promised interest rate, reducing total expected lender recovery. Lenders impose absolute credit rationing.

  3. A sovereign debtor can unambiguously enrich itself by secretly repurchasing its defaulted debt in the secondary market at a 70% discount.

    False. The Bulow–Rogoff (1988) debt buyback paradox shows that using scarce sovereign cash reserves to buy back secondary market debt transfers resources primarily to remaining non-tendering creditors, whose expected recovery rate rises, generating negligible net relief for the debtor.

Problem Set

Mechanics

M1. In an Eaton–Gersovitz economy, output \(y\) in period 2 is uniformly distributed on \([80, 140]\). The sovereign faces a proportional default sanction of \(\delta = 0.25\) (25% output loss upon repudiation). What is the default threshold \(y^*\) when the sovereign issues debt of \(b' = 25\), and what is the resulting default probability?

Solution

The default threshold is \(y^*(b') = b' / \delta = 25 / 0.25 = 100.0\). For \(y \sim U(80, 140)\), the support width is \(140 - 80 = 60\). The default probability is \(P(y < 100) = \frac{100 - 80}{140 - 80} = \frac{20}{60} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.3333\) (33.33%).

M2. Using the parameters of problem M1, if the risk-free rate is \(r^* = 5.0\%\), compute the equilibrium bond price \(q(25)\) and the total borrowing revenue raised by the sovereign.

Solution

The bond price is \(q(b') = \frac{1 - P(\text{default})}{1 + r^*} = \frac{1 - 1/3}{1 + 0.05} = \frac{2/3}{1.05} = \frac{0.666667}{1.05} = 0.63492\). Total borrowing revenue is \(\mathrm{Rev} = q(b') \times b' = 0.63492 \times 25 = 15.873\). The sovereign receives 15.873 units of goods today in exchange for a promise to repay 25 units tomorrow.

M3. For \(y \sim U(a, b)\) with \(a = 60, b = 120\), sanction penalty \(\delta = 0.20\), and \(r^* = 0.0\), find the debt issuance level \(b^*_{\max}\) that maximizes borrowing proceeds.

Solution

Default threshold is \(y^*(b) = b / 0.20 = 5b\). Default probability is \(P(\text{default}) = \frac{5b - 60}{120 - 60} = \frac{5b - 60}{60} = \frac{b}{12} - 1\). The bond price is \(q(b) = 1 - P(\text{default}) = 1 - (\frac{b}{12} - 1) = 2 - \frac{b}{12}\). Revenue is \(\mathrm{Rev}(b) = b (2 - b/12) = 2b - b^2/12\). First-order condition: \(\frac{d\mathrm{Rev}}{db} = 2 - b/6 = 0 \implies b^*_{\max} = 12.0\). Maximum revenue is \(2(12) - 144/12 = 24 - 12 = 12.0\).

Proof

P1. Prove that if the default penalty is a fixed lump-sum amount \(\phi > 0\) (so \(y^{\mathrm{def}} = y - \phi\)) rather than a proportional sanction \(\delta y\), the sovereign defaults in high-endowment states rather than low-endowment states under concave utility.

Solution

Under a fixed penalty \(\phi\), default utility is \(u(y - \phi)\) and repayment utility is \(u(y - b)\). If \(b > \phi\), then \(y - \phi > y - b\) for all \(y\), so default is preferred for every output realization regardless of \(y\). If \(b < \phi\), repayment gives strictly higher consumption \(y - b > y - \phi\) for all \(y\), so repayment is always preferred. In contrast, under dynamic models with credit market exclusion, the option value of borrowing to smooth consumption is least valuable when output is very high; hence, if penalty is purely fixed, default incentives peak in high-income states where the sovereign is self-sufficient, violating empirical facts where defaults occur during severe recessions.

P2. Prove that the equilibrium sovereign credit spread \(s(b') = \frac{1}{q(b')} - (1 + r^*)\) is strictly increasing and convex in debt issuance \(b'\) under a uniform output distribution.

Solution

With \(y \sim U(a, b)\), \(F(y^*(b')) = \frac{b'/\delta - a}{b - a} = \alpha b' - \kappa\). The bond price is \(q(b') = \frac{1 - \alpha b' + \kappa}{1 + r^*}\). The gross spread is \(1 + s(b') = \frac{1}{q(b')} = \frac{1 + r^*}{1 + \kappa - \alpha b'}\). Taking the first derivative: \(\frac{ds}{db'} = \frac{\alpha (1 + r^*)}{(1 + \kappa - \alpha b')^2} > 0\). Taking the second derivative: \(\frac{d^2 s}{d(b')^2} = \frac{2 \alpha^2 (1 + r^*)}{(1 + \kappa - \alpha b')^3} > 0\) for all \(q(b') > 0\). Thus, credit spreads widen at an accelerating rate as debt increases.

P3. Prove that an increase in the trade sanction penalty \(\delta\) unambiguously increases the sovereign's borrowing capacity \(b^*_{\max}\) and lowers credit spreads.

Solution

The default threshold is \(y^*(b'; \delta) = b' / \delta\). For any given \(b'\), \(\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial \delta} = -b' / \delta^2 < 0\). A higher sanction \(\delta\) reduces the cutoff output below which default is optimal. Default probability is \(P(\text{default}) = F(y^*(b'; \delta))\); by chain rule, \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial \delta} = f(y^*) \frac{\partial y^*}{\partial \delta} < 0\). Consequently, bond price \(q(b'; \delta) = \frac{1 - P}{1 + r^*}\) strictly increases with \(\delta\), and credit spread \(s(b')\) strictly decreases. Because \(\mathrm{Rev}(b'; \delta) = q(b'; \delta) b'\) shifts upward with positive derivative with respect to \(\delta\), the maximum borrowing ceiling \(b^*_{\max}\) expands.

Empirical

E1. In sovereign debt restructurings between 1970 and 2010 (Cruces & Trebesch 2013), haircuts averaged 37%, ranging from under 10% to over 80%. If a sovereign with debt of 80 billion USD defaults and restructures with a 45% present-value haircut, calculate the debt relief in billions and the new debt burden.

Solution

A present-value haircut of \(H = 45\%\) on debt of \(D = 80\) billion yields debt relief of \(\Delta D = H \times D = 0.45 \times 80 = 36.0\) billion USD. The remaining restructured debt burden in present value terms is \(D_{\mathrm{new}} = (1 - H) D = (1 - 0.45) \times 80 = 0.55 \times 80 = 44.0\) billion USD.

E2. A sovereign's 5-year bonds trade at 60 cents on the dollar (\(q = 0.60\)) while the risk-free benchmark yield is 4.0%. Assuming zero recovery upon default for simplicity, calculate the risk-neutral annual default probability implied by this bond quote.

Solution

Under zero recovery, \(q = \frac{1 - p}{1 + r^*} \implies 0.60 = \frac{1 - p}{1.04} \implies 1 - p = 0.60 \times 1.04 = 0.6240 \implies p = 1 - 0.6240 = 0.3760 = 37.6\%\) cumulative 1-year default probability. In terms of yield: \(1 + y = 1 / q = 1 / 0.60 = 1.6667 \implies y = 66.67\%\), generating a spread of \(66.67\% - 4.00\% = 62.67\%\) (6267 bp).

E3. In the 2001 Argentine debt default, Argentina owed 100 billion USD in foreign-currency bonds. Following default, real GDP contracted by 10.9% in 2002, and the Argentine peso depreciated by over 70%. Relate this output collapse to the sanction penalty \(\delta\) in Eaton–Gersovitz.

Solution

In Eaton–Gersovitz, default is deterred by the output penalty \(\delta y\). Argentina's 10.9% real output drop in 2002 reflects the real economic costs of default: trade credit freezes, banking runs, breakdown of the domestic payment system, and exclusion from international financing. This empirical collapse suggests that \(\delta \approx 0.10\) to \(0.15\) in severe sovereign default episodes.

Research

R1. State the Bulow–Rogoff (1989) critique of sovereign lending reputation, and explain how the introduction of domestic banking sector holdings of sovereign debt resolves the paradox.

Solution

Bulow and Rogoff (1989) show that if a sovereign can hold cash deposits in foreign banks yielding the world risk-free rate, it could default, invest its unspent debt service in these offshore accounts, and achieve superior consumption smoothing through self-insurance, destroying any reputational equilibrium. The modern resolution (Gennaioli, Martin & Rossi 2014) notes that sovereign default inflicts severe collateral damage on the domestic banking system, which holds large portfolios of sovereign debt. Default causes bank insolvencies, a credit crunch, and catastrophic domestic output contraction, providing an internal enforcement mechanism that does not rely on foreign reputational exclusion.

R2. Analyze the mechanism of Collective Action Clauses (CACs) in sovereign bond indentures. How do single-limb aggregation clauses eliminate holdout creditor arbitrage?

Solution

Under traditional unanimous consent clauses, a single holdout creditor holding a blocking position (e.g. 26% of a single bond issue) can reject a restructuring and sue for 100% face value. Collective Action Clauses allow a qualified majority (e.g., 75%) to bind all bondholders. However, dual-limb CACs still allowed holdouts to target small individual series. Modern "single-limb" aggregation clauses (ICMA 2014 standards) poll all bondholders across all outstanding series collectively; if a supermajority (e.g., 75% aggregate) votes for the restructuring, the terms automatically bind every single bond series, neutralizing holdout litigation strategies.

R3. Explain the Debt Laffer curve paradox in secondary market debt buybacks (Bulow & Rogoff 1988): why does purchasing discounted debt in the open market fail to provide proportionate debt relief?

Solution

Suppose a sovereign has 100 billion face debt trading at 20 cents on the dollar, indicating expected repayment of 20 billion. If the sovereign spends 10 billion in cash to repurchase 50 billion face debt, the remaining 50 billion debt is now backed by the same expected economic capacity. The secondary market price of remaining debt jumps from 20 cents to 40 cents. Creditors receive the full 10 billion cash, while the sovereign's remaining market value of debt is unchanged at \(50 \times 0.40 = 20\) billion. The sovereign spent 10 billion of reserves to reduce expected debt service by 0!

    Because proportional penalty \(\delta y\) is small when \(y\) is low, the sovereign prefers default in bad economic states.
