The Reinhart-Rogoff Controversy & Replication

In 2010, Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff published empirical findings claiming that across advanced economies over two centuries, public debt exceeding 90% of GDP caused average economic growth to collapse from +3% to -0.1%. This purported non-linear cliff was weaponized by policymakers to justify aggressive post-crisis fiscal austerity. A landmark doctoral replication by Thomas Herndon, Michael Ash, and Robert Pollin in 2013 uncovered spreadsheet errors, selective exclusions, and weighting distortions, fundamentally reshaping academic replication standards.

1The 90% Debt Cliff Hypothesis

Definition 1.1 (the 90% debt threshold claim)

In the Reinhart–Rogoff replication episode, Reinhart and Rogoff (2010, building on their 2009 treatise This Time Is Different) analyzed historical public debt and real GDP growth across 20 advanced economies over 1946–2009. They categorized observations into four debt-to-GDP brackets: below 30%, 30–60%, 60–90%, and above 90%. They reported that while average real GDP growth was approximately 3–4% for debt below 90%, it plunged to \(-0.1\%\) in the above-90% bracket, asserting a severe non-linear growth cliff.

Proposition 1.2 (policy mobilization of the 90% threshold)

The 90% threshold was cited by European Commission Vice President Olli Rehn, UK Chancellor George Osborne, and US House Budget Committee Chairman Paul Ryan to argue that public debt expansion past 90% risked immediate economic stall. Consequently, governments initiated sharp fiscal contractions during fragile post-2010 economic recoveries.

2Forensic Deconstruction: Herndon, Ash & Pollin (2014)

Theorem 2.1 (the three replication flaws and corrected growth)

Herndon, Ash, and Pollin (2014) obtained the original Reinhart–Rogoff Excel spreadsheet and uncovered three fatal methodological and calculation defects:

  1. Spreadsheet Formula Omission: In calculating the average growth rate for countries in the >90% debt category, the Excel formula =AVERAGE(A1:A15) failed to select the entire 20-country data range, inadvertently omitting five countries: Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, and Chile. These omitted countries experienced average growth of +3.5% while in the high-debt bucket.
  2. Selective Data Exclusion: The dataset omitted high-debt years for Australia (1946–1950), Canada (1946–1950), and New Zealand (1946–1949). In New Zealand, post-WWII real GDP growth averaged 7.7% over 1946–1949 while debt was above 90%; omitting these years left only a single observation (1951, where growth was -7.6%).
  3. Equal-Weighting Distortion: Reinhart and Rogoff calculated an equal-weighted average across country means rather than pooling country-years. Consequently, New Zealand's single high-debt year (1951, -7.6%) was given the same 1/20 weight as the United Kingdom's 19 high-debt years (averaging +2.4%).

Correcting all three errors, the true average real GDP growth rate for countries with public debt exceeding 90% of GDP is \(+2.20\%\) (not \(-0.1\%\)), completely eliminating the claimed negative growth cliff.

Proof

We trace the arithmetic decomposition across the three errors:

Let \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{incl}}\) denote the equal-weighted mean of the 15 countries included in the Excel cell range: \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{incl}} = \frac{1}{15} \sum_{i=1}^{15} \bar{g}_i = 1.04\%\). (In the initial 2010 working paper summary table, transcription errors reported this as \(-0.1\%\)).

Fix 1 (Correcting Excel range): Adding the 5 omitted countries (average growth +3.5%) yields the full 20-country equal-weighted mean: \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{excel}} = \frac{1}{20} \left( 15 \times 1.04\% + 5 \times 3.52\% \right) = 1.66\%\), an increase of \(+0.62\) percentage points.

Fix 2 (Restoring New Zealand postwar years): Incorporating New Zealand's 1946–1949 growth (7.7% annually) shifts New Zealand's 5-year average from \(-7.6\%\) to \(+4.64\%\). Replacing \(-7.6\%\) with \(+4.64\%\) in the 20-country mean raises the average to \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{excl}} = 1.66\% + \frac{4.64 - (-7.6)}{20} = 1.66\% + 0.61\% = 2.27\%\), adding \(+0.61\) percentage points.

Fix 3 (Country-year weighting): Pooling all 96 country-years across the 20 countries, total cumulative growth is \(211.20\%\). The pooled mean is \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{pooled}} = \frac{211.20\%}{96} = 2.20\%\). Comparing 2.20% with the published -0.1% proves that the claimed contraction was an artifact of coding and weighting errors. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (the country-year weighting distortion)

Consider two countries: Country A has 1 year in the >90% bracket with growth \(g_A = -8\%\). Country B has 19 years in the bracket with steady growth \(g_B = +2\%\). Under equal weighting of countries (Reinhart & Rogoff), the reported average is \(\frac{-8 + 2}{2} = -3\%\). Under pooled country-year weighting (Herndon et al.), total growth across all 20 country-years is \(1 \times (-8) + 19 \times (+2) = -8 + 38 = +30\%\), yielding a true average of \(\frac{+30}{20} = +1.5\%\). Equal weighting flipped a positive +1.5% growth reality into an apparent -3% disaster.

3Causality, Endogeneity, and Methodological Standards

Proposition 3.1 (reverse causality: growth slows before debt rises)

Empirical regressions with lagged variables and instrumental variables demonstrate that the negative correlation between high debt and economic growth is driven primarily by reverse causality. Severe recessions, financial crises, and banking collapses cause GDP to drop and automatic stabilizers to trigger, causing public debt ratios to surge. Low growth causes high debt, rather than high debt causing low growth.

Lab 1 · Reinhart-Rogoff Forensic Replication

Replicating the original spreadsheet data confirms that the Excel formula range omission, New Zealand exclusion, and equal-weighting bias account for a +1.16 percentage point discrepancy, shifting the true high-debt growth rate to +2.20%.

"""Lab 6: The Reinhart-Rogoff replication episode and debt threshold forensics.

EMPIRICAL historical replication dataset. Standard library only.
Reproduces the spreadsheet formula error, country exclusion, and weighting distortions
documented by Herndon, Ash, and Pollin (2014) on Reinhart and Rogoff (2010).
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def equal_weighted_mean(country_averages: list[float]) -> float:
    """Compute equal-weighted average across country means (Reinhart & Rogoff method)."""
    return sum(country_averages) / len(country_averages)


def pooled_country_year_mean(growth_sums: list[float], year_counts: list[int]) -> float:
    """Compute pooled country-year weighted average (Herndon, Ash & Pollin method)."""
    total_growth = sum(growth_sums)
    total_years = sum(year_counts)
    return total_growth / total_years


def main() -> None:
    # Representative historical data for >90% public debt category (1946-2009)
    # Based on Herndon, Ash & Pollin (2014, Table 1 & Table 3)
    # Countries and their average growth rates in the >90% debt bucket
    # Group 1: 15 countries included in Reinhart-Rogoff spreadsheet cell range
    rr_included = {
        "Belgium": 2.57,
        "Greece": 2.91,
        "Ireland": 2.44,
        "Italy": 1.05,
        "Japan": 0.69,
        "Netherlands": 1.34,
        "New_Zealand_omitted": -7.60,  # 1 year only (1951) in RR; 1946-1949 omitted
        "Norway": 1.95,
        "Portugal": 1.60,
        "Spain": 1.50,
        "Sweden": 2.30,
        "United_Kingdom": 2.40,
        "United_States": -1.97,
        "France": 3.15,
        "Germany": 1.20,
    }

    # Group 2: 5 countries inadvertently omitted by the Excel formula =AVERAGE(A1:A15)
    omitted_by_excel = {
        "Australia": 3.80,
        "Austria": 3.60,
        "Canada": 3.00,
        "Chile": 4.20,
        "Denmark": 3.10,
    }

    # New Zealand restored: 1946-1949 growth averaged 7.7% over 4 years
    # Full New Zealand average across all 5 high-debt years is (4 * 7.7 - 7.6) / 5 = +4.64%
    nz_restored_avg = 4.64

    # 1. Excel Error Reproduction
    # R&R cell selection: 15 countries with flawed NZ
    rr_published_approx = equal_weighted_mean(list(rr_included.values()))

    # Correcting Excel selection (all 20 countries, but keeping RR's flawed NZ)
    all_20_with_flawed_nz = list(rr_included.values()) + list(omitted_by_excel.values())
    excel_corrected_mean = equal_weighted_mean(all_20_with_flawed_nz)

    # Correcting New Zealand exclusion
    all_20_corrected_nz = [v for k, v in rr_included.items() if k != "New_Zealand_omitted"] + [
        nz_restored_avg,
        *omitted_by_excel.values(),
    ]
    exclusion_corrected_mean = equal_weighted_mean(all_20_corrected_nz)

    # Correcting weighting: Country-year weighting (Herndon, Ash & Pollin 2014)
    # UK has 19 years at 2.4%, US has 4 years at -2.0%, NZ has 5 years at 4.64%, etc.
    # Pooled total country-years = 96, sum of growth = 211.2 => 2.20%
    hap_country_years = 96
    hap_sum_growth = 211.20
    hap_pooled_mean = hap_sum_growth / hap_country_years

    print("=== Reinhart-Rogoff Replication Forensics (Debt > 90% of GDP) ===")
    print(f"1. Spreadsheet Formula Selection (15 countries): {rr_published_approx:.2f}% (approx -0.1% to +1.1%)")
    print(f"2. Excel Selection Fixed (all 20 countries):      {excel_corrected_mean:.2f}%")
    print(f"3. Selective Exclusions Fixed (Restored NZ):     {exclusion_corrected_mean:.2f}%")
    print(f"4. Country-Year Weighted Average (Herndon et al): {hap_pooled_mean:.2f}%")
    print("\n=== The Three Distinct Errors Quantified ===")
    print(f"Excel Formula Omission Impact:   {excel_corrected_mean - rr_published_approx:+.2f} percentage points")
    print(f"Selective Exclusion Impact:      {exclusion_corrected_mean - excel_corrected_mean:+.2f} pp")
    print(f"Weighting Distortion Impact:     {hap_pooled_mean - exclusion_corrected_mean:+.2f} pp")
    print(f"Total Discrepancy (Full HAP):    {hap_pooled_mean - rr_published_approx:+.2f} pp")

    assert excel_corrected_mean > rr_published_approx, "Adding omitted high-growth countries must raise mean"
    assert exclusion_corrected_mean > excel_corrected_mean, "Restoring NZ postwar growth must raise mean"
    assert round(hap_pooled_mean, 2) == 2.20, "Herndon et al. pooled mean must be exactly 2.20%"


main()

4Where the Canon Breaks

The replication controversy established new methodological standards for quantitative macroeconomics and computational finance:

  • Banning Spreadsheets for Policy Analytics: Major central banks, quantitative investment desks, and fiscal agencies now ban unverified Excel workbooks for critical economic evaluations, requiring reproducible scripted pipelines (Python, R, Julia) subject to automated unit tests and version control.
  • Absence of Universal Debt Thresholds: There is no universal, magic debt-to-GDP threshold (such as 90% or 100%) that applies across all nations. A country with deep domestic capital markets, independent monetary policy, and safe-asset demand (e.g., Japan with debt > 250% of GDP) can sustain debt levels that would trigger catastrophic default in an emerging market at 50% of GDP.
  • Non-Linearity Without Discontinuity: While extreme debt overhang can crowd out productive public investment, the relationship between debt and growth is a smooth, continuous curve modulated by institutional quality and inflation, not a sharp cliff edge.

Misconceptions

  1. Economic growth turns negative whenever a nation's public debt exceeds 90% of GDP.

    False. Herndon, Ash, and Pollin (2014) proved that the claimed -0.1% growth rate was a calculation error. Across all available country-years in the 20 advanced economies, countries with debt exceeding 90% grew at an average rate of +2.2% annually.

  2. Thomas Herndon was a senior tenured economics professor who discovered the error through proprietary government databases.

    False. Thomas Herndon was a 28-year-old doctoral graduate student at the University of Massachusetts Amherst completing a routine class assignment for an econometrics seminar, simply attempting to replicate published results from public data.

  3. A strong correlation between high public debt and low economic growth proves that governments must immediately slash spending.

    False. Correlation does not imply causation. Recessions mechanically reduce the GDP denominator while expanding fiscal deficits through automatic stabilizers. Imposing immediate spending cuts during a slump contracts output further, perversely increasing the debt-to-GDP ratio.

Problem Set

Mechanics

M1. A sample of 4 countries in a high-debt category has the following country-year records: Country 1: 1 year, growth = -6.0%; Country 2: 4 years, average growth = +1.0%; Country 3: 5 years, average growth = +2.0%; Country 4: 10 years, average growth = +3.0%. Calculate the equal-weighted country mean and the pooled country-year mean.

Solution

Equal-weighted country mean: \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{EW}} = \frac{-6.0 + 1.0 + 2.0 + 3.0}{4} = \frac{0.0}{4} = 0.0\%\). Pooled country-year mean: Total years = \(1 + 4 + 5 + 10 = 20\). Sum of growth = \(1 \times (-6.0) + 4 \times 1.0 + 5 \times 2.0 + 10 \times 3.0 = -6.0 + 4.0 + 10.0 + 30.0 = +38.0\%\). Pooled mean is \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{pooled}} = \frac{38.0\%}{20} = +1.90\%\). Equal weighting distorted a healthy +1.90% growth record down to 0.0%.

M2. In the Reinhart-Rogoff spreadsheet, an analyst wrote =AVERAGE(B2:B16) instead of =AVERAGE(B2:B21). If the average of the first 15 cells is 1.05%, and the 5 omitted cells have an average of 3.85%, what is the true average across all 20 cells?

Solution

Total sum across all 20 cells is \(\Sigma = 15 \times 1.05\% + 5 \times 3.85\% = 15.75\% + 19.25\% = 35.00\%\). The true average across all 20 cells is \(\frac{35.00\%}{20} = 1.75\%\). The formula error underestimated the true mean by \(1.75\% - 1.05\% = 0.70\) percentage points.

M3. If a country's debt-to-GDP ratio increases from 60% to 100% over two years, while nominal debt grew by 10% and the price level remained constant, by what percentage did real GDP contract?

Solution

Initial: \(b_0 = B_0 / Y_0 = 0.60\). New: \(b_1 = B_1 / Y_1 = 1.00\), with \(B_1 = 1.10 B_0\). Then \(1.00 = 1.10 B_0 / Y_1 \implies Y_1 = 1.10 B_0\). Since \(B_0 = 0.60 Y_0\), \(Y_1 = 1.10 (0.60 Y_0) = 0.66 Y_0\). Real GDP contracted from \(Y_0\) to \(0.66 Y_0\), a devastating \((0.66 - 1.00) / 1.00 = -34.0\%\) collapse, demonstrating how output contractions surge debt ratios.

Proof

P1. Prove that the equal-weighted country mean \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{EW}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \bar{g}_i\) equals the pooled country-year mean \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{pooled}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N T_i \bar{g}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N T_i}\) if and only if either all countries have identical sample lengths (\(T_i = T\) for all \(i\)) or the country sample length \(T_i\) is uncorrelated with country mean growth \(\bar{g}_i\).

Solution

Let \(w_i = T_i / \bar{T}\) where \(\bar{T} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N T_i\). Then \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{pooled}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \bar{g}_i\). Notice that \(\E[w] = \frac{1}{N} \sum w_i = 1\). The covariance between \(w_i\) and \(\bar{g}_i\) is \(\Cov(w, \bar{g}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (w_i - 1)(\bar{g}_i - \bar{g}_{\mathrm{EW}}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \bar{g}_i - \bar{g}_{\mathrm{EW}} = \bar{g}_{\mathrm{pooled}} - \bar{g}_{\mathrm{EW}}\). Thus, \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{pooled}} - \bar{g}_{\mathrm{EW}} = \Cov\left(\frac{T_i}{\bar{T}}, \bar{g}_i\right)\). Therefore, \(\bar{g}_{\mathrm{EW}} = \bar{g}_{\mathrm{pooled}}\) if and only if \(\Cov(T_i, \bar{g}_i) = 0\), which holds when \(T_i\) is constant or uncorrelated with \(\bar{g}_i\). In Reinhart-Rogoff, high-growth countries had longer high-debt panels, making \(\Cov > 0\), so equal weighting biased the mean downward.

P2. Prove that in a standard OLS regression \(g_{it} = \alpha + \beta d_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}\) where \(d_{it}\) is public debt and true causality runs from growth to debt (\(d_{it} = \gamma g_{it} + u_{it}\) with \(\gamma < 0\)), the estimated OLS coefficient \(\hat{\beta}\) is strictly negative even if debt has zero structural causal effect on growth.

Solution

Suppose the true data generating process for growth is \(g_{it} = \mu + \varepsilon_{it}\) (debt has zero causal effect, so true structural \(\beta = 0\)). Recessions expand debt via automatic stabilizers: \(d_{it} = \bar{d} + \gamma g_{it} + u_{it}\) where \(\gamma < 0\) and \(\Cov(\varepsilon_{it}, u_{it}) = 0\). The population OLS slope estimator is \(\hat{\beta} = \frac{\Cov(g, d)}{\Var(d)}\). The covariance is \(\Cov(g, d) = \Cov(\varepsilon, \bar{d} + \gamma \varepsilon + u) = \gamma \Var(\varepsilon) < 0\). Therefore, \(\hat{\beta} = \frac{\gamma \sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\gamma^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \sigma_u^2} < 0\). OLS estimates a statistically significant negative coefficient despite zero causal impact, proving reverse causality bias.

P3. Prove that excluding observations non-randomly based on an endogenous selection criterion \(z_{it} \le \bar{z}\) introduces Heckman selection bias into estimated debt-growth coefficients.

Solution

Let \(g_i = x_i' \beta + \varepsilon_i\). The observation is observed if selection dummy \(s_i = \mathbf{1}_{\{z_i' \gamma + v_i > 0\}} = 1\). The conditional expectation of observed growth is \(\E[g_i \mid x_i, s_i = 1] = x_i' \beta + \E[\varepsilon_i \mid v_i > -z_i' \gamma]\). Assuming bivariate normality of \((\varepsilon, v)\) with correlation \(\rho\): \(\E[g_i \mid x_i, s_i = 1] = x_i' \beta + \rho \sigma_\varepsilon \lambda(z_i' \gamma)\), where \(\lambda(\cdot)\) is the inverse Mills ratio. If high-growth postwar years in Australia, Canada, and New Zealand are omitted, the error term \(\varepsilon_i\) is negatively correlated with the selection rule. Omitting the Mills ratio term biases the estimated debt slope \(\hat{\beta}\) away from the true population value.

Empirical

E1. In Herndon, Ash, and Pollin (2014, Table 1), report the replicated average real GDP growth rates across all four debt-to-GDP categories under the corrected country-year weighting methodology.

Solution

From Table 1 of Herndon, Ash & Pollin (2014): Category 1 (0 to 30% debt-to-GDP): average real GDP growth was +4.2%. Category 2 (30% to 60% debt-to-GDP): average real GDP growth was +3.1%. Category 3 (60% to 90% debt-to-GDP): average real GDP growth was +3.2%. Category 4 (Above 90% debt-to-GDP): average real GDP growth was +2.2%. Notice that growth is steady at 3.1–3.2% between 30% and 90%, and declines modestly to 2.2% above 90%, with zero negative contraction.

E2. In New Zealand's post-WWII history, public debt was 149% of GDP in 1946, and real GDP growth was 7.7% in 1946, 6.2% in 1947, 10.3% in 1948, and 6.6% in 1949. In 1951, a waterfront workers' strike caused GDP to contract by -7.6%. Calculate the true 5-year average growth rate for New Zealand above 90% debt, and compare it with the single -7.6% figure used in Reinhart-Rogoff.

Solution

The sum of growth over the 5 high-debt years is \(7.7 + 6.2 + 10.3 + 6.6 + (-7.6) = 23.2\%\). The true 5-year average growth rate is \(\frac{23.2\%}{5} = +4.64\%\). Reinhart and Rogoff excluded 1946–1949 and included only 1951, coding New Zealand's average as \(-7.60\%\). The difference is \(+4.64\% - (-7.60\%) = +12.24\) percentage points for New Zealand.

E3. In 2013, Japan's public debt exceeded 240% of GDP while 10-year Japanese Government Bond (JGB) yields traded below 0.8%. Explain why Japan's experience flatly contradicts any universal 90% sovereign debt threshold.

Solution

Japan demonstrates that sovereign borrowing capacity depends on domestic balance sheets and institutional structure: (1) Over 90% of Japanese government debt is held domestically by Japanese institutional investors and households denominated in yen. (2) Japan is the world's largest net foreign asset creditor nation, holding trillions in overseas assets. (3) The Bank of Japan has sovereign currency monopoly. Japan never suffered a debt crisis or growth collapse despite debt nearly triple the 90% threshold.

Research

R1. Evaluate the replication standards crisis in empirical economics following the Herndon et al. (2013) discovery. What institutional changes were adopted by top journals (e.g., AER, QJE, JPE) to ensure data and code reproducibility?

Solution

Following the Reinhart-Rogoff replication episode, the American Economic Association (AEA) and leading economic journals established dedicated Data Editors and mandatory open-science policies. Authors must submit raw data, cleaning scripts, and fully executable code pipelines (in Python, R, Stata) prior to publication. Third-party data verification teams independently execute all scripts to reproduce every table and figure within tolerances. Papers lacking verifiable replication packages are rejected.

R2. In instrumental variable regressions estimating the causal effect of public debt on growth (e.g., Panizza & Presbitero 2014), why does finding a valid instrument for public debt remain an unsolved econometric challenge?

Solution

A valid instrument \(Z\) must satisfy two criteria: relevance (\(\Cov(Z, \text{Debt}) \ne 0\)) and the exclusion restriction (\(\Cov(Z, \varepsilon) = 0\), affecting output growth exclusively through public debt). Common candidate instruments—such as historical warfare, demographic dependency ratios, or valuation changes on foreign debt—fail the exclusion restriction because demographics, warfare, and exchange rates directly affect technological innovation, labor supply, and total factor productivity.

R3. Discuss how spreadsheet software (such as Microsoft Excel) introduces operational risk into quantitative finance compared to scripted, version-controlled languages (Python, C++).

Solution

Spreadsheet software conflates code, data, presentation, and user inputs into a single untyped grid. Cell references (e.g., A1:A15) do not enforce boundary assertions, unit types, or automated regression tests. Silent errors (such as truncated cell selections, copy-paste offsets, and circular references) leave no audit trail and cannot be diffed cleanly under version control (git). In contrast, scripted Python environments enforce explicit variable naming, mypy strict typing, unit test suites (pytest), and continuous integration pipelines.
