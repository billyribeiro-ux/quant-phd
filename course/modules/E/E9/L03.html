Currency Crises: Fundamentals vs. Self-Fulfilling Runs

  • Uses: uncovered interest parity (E8), policy rate (E7), discount factor (M1).
  • Introduces: first- and second-generation currency-crisis models.
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Fixed exchange rate regimes collapse either because underlying fiscal and monetary policies are fundamentally incompatible with the peg, or because market expectations coordinate on a self-fulfilling speculative attack. Distinguishing between first-generation fundamentals-driven reserve depletion and second-generation escape-clause multiple equilibria is essential for currency crisis forecasting, central bank defense strategies, and international macro risk management.

1First-Generation Models: Inconsistent Fundamentals

Definition 1.1 (the Krugman 1979 balance-of-payments model)

In first- and second-generation currency-crisis models, the first-generation framework (Krugman 1979, Flood & Garber 1984) formalizes the collapse of a fixed exchange rate peg \(\bar{s}\) under chronic fiscal deficits monetized through domestic credit expansion. The central bank balance sheet satisfies:

\[ M_t = D_t + R_t \]

where \(M_t\) is nominal money supply, \(D_t\) is domestic credit growing at constant rate \(\mu > 0\) (\(\dot{D}_t = \mu\)), and \(R_t\) is the stock of foreign exchange reserves. Money demand follows the Cagan specification:

\[ m_t - p_t = -\alpha i_t \]

where \(m_t = \ln M_t\), \(p_t = \ln P_t\), and \(\alpha > 0\) is the semi-elasticity of money demand. Under purchasing power parity and uncovered interest parity with foreign price level and foreign interest rate normalized to zero (\(P^* = 1, i^* = 0\)):

\[ p_t = s_t, \quad i_t = \dot{s}_t \]

where \(s_t\) is the log nominal exchange rate (domestic currency per foreign currency).

Theorem 1.2 (shadow exchange rate and speculative attack timing)

In the Krugman (1979) model, the shadow exchange rate \(\tilde{s}_t\)—the floating rate that would prevail if central bank foreign reserves were zero—is given by:

\[ \tilde{s}_t = d_t + \alpha \mu \]

A speculative attack occurs at the exact date \(T\) when the shadow exchange rate equals the fixed peg:

\[ \tilde{s}_T = \bar{s} \implies T = \frac{\bar{s} - d_0 - \alpha \mu}{\mu} \]

At time \(T\), speculators execute an instantaneous run, exhausting all remaining central bank reserves \(R(T) = \alpha \mu > 0\). The nominal exchange rate transitions continuously from the peg to the float without an arbitrage jump: \(s(T^+) = s(T^-) = \bar{s}\).

Proof

Under a post-collapse float with zero reserves, money supply is purely domestic credit: \(m_t = d_t = d_0 + \mu t\). Money market equilibrium requires \(d_t - s_t = -\alpha \dot{s}_t \implies s_t = d_t + \alpha \dot{s}_t\). Looking for a stationary floating path where the exchange rate depreciates at the rate of monetary expansion, \(\dot{s}_t = \mu\). Substituting into the money demand equation yields the shadow exchange rate: \(\tilde{s}_t = d_t + \alpha \mu\).

Now consider the timing of the speculative attack. Suppose speculators wait until reserves are depleted naturally to zero at date \(t_1 > T\). At date \(t_1\), \(\tilde{s}_{t_1} > \tilde{s}_T = \bar{s}\). The exchange rate would jump discretely upward from \(\bar{s}\) to \(\tilde{s}_{t_1}\). An anticipated discrete jump in the exchange rate represents an infinite rate of return, violating no-arbitrage. Speculators would attempt to buy reserves at date \(t_1 - \varepsilon\). Conversely, if speculators attack at \(t_0 < T\), \(\tilde{s}_{t_0} < \bar{s}\); the post-attack floating currency would appreciate, inflicting capital losses on speculators who bought foreign currency at \(\bar{s}\). Therefore, no attack can occur before \(T\). The unique non-arbitrage equilibrium timing is \(\tilde{s}_T = \bar{s}\). At \(T\), money demand drops from \(\bar{m} = \bar{s}\) (since \(\dot{s} = 0\) under the peg) to \(m(T^+) = \bar{s} - \alpha \mu\). The sudden drop in money demand \(\Delta M = \alpha \mu\) is absorbed by an instantaneous purchase of central bank foreign reserves, setting \(R(T^+) = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Second-Generation Models: Escape Clauses and Multiple Equilibria

Definition 2.1 (the Obstfeld 1996 escape-clause model)

In the second-generation model (Obstfeld 1996), currency crises do not require chronic fiscal monetization. Instead, a benevolent government minimizes a quadratic loss function balancing the benefits of exchange rate stability against the domestic economic costs of defense (such as unemployment, mortgage distress, or banking sector fragility):

\[ \mathcal{L} = (y - y^*)^2 + C \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{\text{devalue}\}} \]

where \(y = \bar{y} - \theta - \beta \pi^e\) is output, \(\theta\) is a fundamental adverse economic shock, \(\pi^e\) is the private sector's expected probability of devaluation, and \(C > 0\) is a fixed political and institutional cost of abandoning the peg.

Theorem 2.2 (Obstfeld 1996 multiple equilibria zones)

Under the escape-clause framework, the state space of fundamentals \(\theta\) partitions into three distinct zones:

  1. Sound Zone (\(\theta < \theta_L\)): Fundamentals are sufficiently strong that the government defends the peg even if all market participants attack (\(\pi^e = 1\)). The unique equilibrium is no crisis.
  2. Vulnerable Intermediate Zone (\(\theta_L \le \theta \le \theta_H\)): Multiple equilibria exist! If speculators expect defense (\(\pi^e = 0\)), defending is optimal; if speculators attack (\(\pi^e = 1\)), the domestic cost of raising interest rates to defend the peg exceeds \(C\), making devaluation optimal. The crisis is self-fulfilling.
  3. Hopeless Zone (\(\theta > \theta_H\)): Fundamentals are so severely compromised that the government abandons the peg even if no one attacks (\(\pi^e = 0\)). The unique equilibrium is devaluation.
Proof

Let the government loss from defending the peg be \(\mathcal{L}^D(\theta, \pi^e) = (\theta + \beta \pi^e)^2\), and the loss from devaluing be \(\mathcal{L}^V = C\). The government devalues if and only if \(\mathcal{L}^D > \mathcal{L}^V \iff \theta + \beta \pi^e > \sqrt{C}\).

Case 1: Optimistic expectations (\(\pi^e = 0\)). Defense cost is \(\theta^2\). The government devalues iff \(\theta > \sqrt{C}\). Define \(\theta_H = \sqrt{C}\).

Case 2: Pessimistic expectations (\(\pi^e = 1\)). Defense cost is \((\theta + \beta)^2\). The government defends iff \(\theta + \beta \le \sqrt{C} \iff \theta \le \sqrt{C} - \beta\). Define \(\theta_L = \max(0, \sqrt{C} - \beta)\).

For any \(\theta \in [\theta_L, \theta_H]\), if \(\pi^e = 0\), \(\theta \le \theta_H \implies \text{Defend}\); if \(\pi^e = 1\), \(\theta \ge \theta_L \implies \text{Devalue}\). Expectations are strictly self-fulfilling, establishing multiple equilibria. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Krugman Attack Timing and Obstfeld Multiple Equilibria

Krugman's first-generation model predicts an exact attack time T where the shadow rate reaches the peg before reserves run out. Obstfeld's second-generation model identifies intermediate fundamental zones where market runs are self-fulfilling.

"""Lab 3: Currency crises: First-generation timing and second-generation escape clauses.

SIMULATED crisis models. Standard library only.
Verifies Krugman (1979) speculative attack timing and reserve run,
and Obstfeld (1996) second-generation multiple equilibria zones.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def krugman_attack_time(
    log_peg: float,
    initial_log_credit: float,
    credit_growth_rate: float,
    interest_semi_elasticity: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute exact time T of speculative attack under Krugman (1979).

    Shadow exchange rate: s_tilde(t) = d_0 + mu * t + alpha * mu
    Attack occurs when s_tilde(T) = s_bar:
    T = (s_bar - d_0 - alpha * mu) / mu
    """
    if credit_growth_rate <= 0.0:
        msg = f"Credit growth rate must be positive: {credit_growth_rate}"
        raise ValueError(msg)
    t_attack = (log_peg - initial_log_credit - interest_semi_elasticity * credit_growth_rate) / credit_growth_rate
    if t_attack < 0.0:
        msg = f"Initial credit already exceeds speculative threshold: T={t_attack:.4f}"
        raise ValueError(msg)
    return t_attack


def shadow_exchange_rate(
    t: float,
    initial_log_credit: float,
    credit_growth_rate: float,
    interest_semi_elasticity: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute shadow exchange rate at time t."""
    return initial_log_credit + credit_growth_rate * t + interest_semi_elasticity * credit_growth_rate


def obstfeld_defense_cost(
    fundamental_shock: float,
    attack_fraction: float,
    sensitivity: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute government economic loss from defending the peg under Obstfeld (1996).

    Cost = (theta + sensitivity * pi_e)^2
    where theta is the fundamental unemployment shock and pi_e is attack fraction.
    """
    return (fundamental_shock + sensitivity * attack_fraction) ** 2


def obstfeld_zones(
    realign_fixed_cost: float,
    sensitivity: float,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Compute lower (theta_L) and upper (theta_H) fundamental thresholds for multiple equilibria.

    Devaluation occurs if Cost_defense > realign_fixed_cost.
    When no one attacks (pi_e = 0): Cost = theta^2. Devalues if theta > sqrt(C) => theta_H = sqrt(C).
    When everyone attacks (pi_e = 1): Cost = (theta + sensitivity)^2.
    Defends if (theta + sensitivity) < sqrt(C) => theta_L = sqrt(C) - sensitivity.
    Zone [theta_L, theta_H] exhibits self-fulfilling runs.
    """
    c_sqrt = realign_fixed_cost**0.5
    theta_h = c_sqrt
    theta_l = max(0.0, c_sqrt - sensitivity)
    return theta_l, theta_h


def main() -> None:
    # 1. Krugman (1979) parameters
    s_bar = 2.0  # Log peg
    d0 = 1.0  # Initial log domestic credit
    mu = 0.10  # Credit growth rate per year (10%)
    alpha = 2.0  # Money demand semi-elasticity

    t_collapse = krugman_attack_time(s_bar, d0, mu, alpha)
    s_shadow_0 = shadow_exchange_rate(0.0, d0, mu, alpha)
    s_shadow_t = shadow_exchange_rate(t_collapse, d0, mu, alpha)

    # 2. Obstfeld (1996) parameters
    fixed_cost_c = 16.0  # Cost of devaluation C (sqrt(C) = 4.0)
    beta_attack = 1.5  # Speculative feedback parameter

    theta_l, theta_h = obstfeld_zones(fixed_cost_c, beta_attack)

    # Evaluate 3 representative fundamentals
    theta_sound = 2.0  # Sound: theta < theta_L
    theta_vulnerable = 3.0  # Vulnerable: theta_L <= theta <= theta_H
    theta_hopeless = 4.5  # Hopeless: theta > theta_H

    cost_sound_no_attack = obstfeld_defense_cost(theta_sound, 0.0, beta_attack)
    cost_sound_attack = obstfeld_defense_cost(theta_sound, 1.0, beta_attack)

    cost_vuln_no_attack = obstfeld_defense_cost(theta_vulnerable, 0.0, beta_attack)
    cost_vuln_attack = obstfeld_defense_cost(theta_vulnerable, 1.0, beta_attack)

    cost_hope_no_attack = obstfeld_defense_cost(theta_hopeless, 0.0, beta_attack)

    print("=== Krugman (1979) First-Generation Attack Timing ===")
    print(f"Log Exchange Rate Peg s_bar:     {s_bar:.4f}")
    print(f"Initial Log Credit d0:           {d0:.4f}")
    print(f"Credit Growth Rate mu:           {mu:.4f}")
    print(f"Money Semi-Elasticity alpha:     {alpha:.4f}")
    print(f"Shadow Exchange Rate at t=0:     {s_shadow_0:.4f}")
    print(f"Exact Speculative Attack Time T: {t_collapse:.4f} years")
    print(f"Shadow Exchange Rate at Attack:  {s_shadow_t:.4f}")

    print("\n=== Obstfeld (1996) Second-Generation Multiple Equilibria ===")
    print(f"Fixed Cost of Devaluation C:     {fixed_cost_c:.4f}")
    print(f"Attack Sensitivity Parameter:    {beta_attack:.4f}")
    print(f"Sound Threshold theta_L:         {theta_l:.4f}")
    print(f"Hopeless Threshold theta_H:      {theta_h:.4f}")
    print(f"Vulnerable Intermediate Window:  [{theta_l:.4f}, {theta_h:.4f}]")
    print(f"Sound State (theta=2.0) Defense Cost (No Attack):    {cost_sound_no_attack:.4f} (Defends)")
    print(f"Sound State (theta=2.0) Defense Cost (Attack):       {cost_sound_attack:.4f} (Defends)")
    print(f"Vulnerable State (theta=3.0) Defense Cost (No Attack): {cost_vuln_no_attack:.4f} (Defends)")
    print(f"Vulnerable State (theta=3.0) Defense Cost (Attack):    {cost_vuln_attack:.4f} (Devalues)")
    print(f"Hopeless State (theta=4.5) Defense Cost (No Attack):   {cost_hope_no_attack:.4f} (Devalues)")

    assert abs(s_shadow_t - s_bar) < 1e-9, "Shadow exchange rate must equal peg at attack time T"
    assert t_collapse == 8.0, "Krugman attack time should be exactly (2.0 - 1.0 - 0.2) / 0.1 = 8.0 years"
    assert theta_l == 2.5 and theta_h == 4.0, "Obstfeld thresholds must match sqrt(16) - 1.5 = 2.5 and 4.0"
    assert cost_sound_attack < fixed_cost_c, "In sound state, defense cost is below C even under attack"
    assert cost_hope_no_attack > fixed_cost_c, "In hopeless state, defense cost exceeds C even without attack"
    assert cost_vuln_no_attack < fixed_cost_c < cost_vuln_attack, (
        "In vulnerable state, cost must be below C without attack and above C with attack"
    )

3Where the Canon Breaks

Both first- and second-generation models present stylised abstractions that fail to capture modern financial infrastructure:

  • Offshore Derivative Positioning: Speculative attacks in the 21st century rarely involve physical domestic bank cash withdrawals. Speculators accumulate massive short domestic-currency positions via non-deliverable forwards (NDFs), cross-currency swaps, and options in offshore centres (London, Singapore, New York) beyond the jurisdiction of the domestic central bank.
  • Bilateral Central Bank Swap Lines: In the 1990s, central bank foreign exchange reserves were strictly finite. Today, standing liquidity swap lines between major central banks (e.g., Federal Reserve CMLS facilities) provide elastic reserve backstops, neutralizing classical Krugman reserve-drain attacks for partner central banks.
  • Corporate Balance-Sheet Mismatches: Second-generation models focus on government unemployment costs. In emerging markets, the primary cost of currency devaluation is corporate balance-sheet insolvencies from dollar-denominated debt (Third-Generation models).

Misconceptions

  1. Speculative attacks happen only after a central bank has completely exhausted its foreign exchange reserves.

    False. Krugman (1979) proved that an attack occurs well before reserves reach zero, at the exact moment when the shadow exchange rate equals the peg. The remaining reserves (\(R(T) = \alpha \mu\)) are liquidated in a sudden, discrete run.

  2. A currency crisis indicates that the government's macroeconomic fundamentals must have worsened immediately prior to the collapse.

    False. Obstfeld (1996) demonstrated that in the intermediate vulnerable zone, fundamentals can be completely constant while a shift in private sector market sentiment coordinates self-fulfilling runs that force devaluation.

  3. Raising central bank policy interest rates can always successfully defend an exchange rate peg.

    False. While higher interest rates support the currency via UIP, they inflict devastating damage on domestic banks, floating-rate mortgage holders, and public debt servicing. When the government's tolerance for domestic economic pain is reached, the peg collapses despite high interest rates.

Problem Set

Mechanics

M1. In a Krugman (1979) economy, the exchange rate peg is \(\bar{s} = 3.0\), initial log domestic credit is \(d_0 = 1.5\), credit grows at \(\mu = 0.08\) per year, and money demand semi-elasticity is \(\alpha = 2.5\). Calculate the exact time \(T\) of the speculative collapse and the remaining reserves \(R(T)\) if nominal money is \(M = 100\) billion.

Solution

The shadow rate is \(\tilde{s}_t = d_0 + \mu t + \alpha \mu\). Attack occurs when \(\tilde{s}_T = \bar{s} \implies T = \frac{\bar{s} - d_0 - \alpha \mu}{\mu} = \frac{3.0 - 1.5 - 2.5 \times 0.08}{0.08} = \frac{1.5 - 0.20}{0.08} = \frac{1.30}{0.08} = 16.25\) years. At attack time \(T\), the drop in log money demand is \(\alpha \mu = 2.5 \times 0.08 = 0.20\) log units, representing the sudden reserve run that exhausts remaining reserves.

M2. In an Obstfeld (1996) model, the government's fixed cost of realigning the peg is \(C = 25.0\) (so \(\sqrt{C} = 5.0\)), and the private sector attack sensitivity parameter is \(\beta = 2.0\). Determine the lower threshold \(\theta_L\) and upper threshold \(\theta_H\) that define the self-fulfilling crisis zone.

Solution

The threshold for devaluation when \(\pi^e = 0\) is \(\theta_H = \sqrt{C} = \sqrt{25} = 5.0\). The threshold for devaluation when \(\pi^e = 1\) is \(\theta_L = \sqrt{C} - \beta = 5.0 - 2.0 = 3.0\). The vulnerable intermediate zone is \([\theta_L, \theta_H] = [3.0, 5.0]\). For any shock in this range, multiple equilibria exist.

M3. If a central bank raises its policy interest rate from 4% to 14% to defend against a speculative attack, and total outstanding domestic commercial bank loans are 150% of GDP with 60% floating rate, calculate the annualized additional borrowing burden on the private non-financial sector.

Solution

The policy rate increase is \(\Delta i = 14\% - 4\% = 10\% = 0.10\). Floating-rate debt is \(0.60 \times 1.50 = 0.90\) (90% of GDP). The annualized increase in private interest expense is \(\Delta \text{Expense} = \Delta i \times \text{Floating Debt} = 0.10 \times 0.90 = 0.090 = 9.0\%\) of GDP. This crushing 9% of GDP interest surcharge illustrates why the domestic cost of interest rate defense is prohibitive.

Proof

P1. Prove that in the Krugman (1979) model, an increase in central bank initial reserves \(R_0\) delays the crisis time \(T\) without preventing the eventual collapse of the peg.

Solution

Because total money supply is \(M_t = D_t + R_t\), let \(M_0 = D_0 + R_0\). Under fixed peg \(\bar{s}\), nominal money demand is constant: \(\bar{M} = \exp(\bar{s})\). Therefore, \(D_0 = \bar{M} - R_0\). Substituting into the attack time formula: \(T = \frac{\bar{s} - \ln(\bar{M} - R_0) - \alpha \mu}{\mu}\). Taking the partial derivative with respect to \(R_0\): \(\frac{\partial T}{\partial R_0} = -\frac{1}{\mu} \frac{-1}{\bar{M} - R_0} = \frac{1}{\mu (\bar{M} - R_0)} > 0\). Thus, higher initial reserves delay \(T\) proportionally to \(1 / \mu\). However, since \(\mu > 0\) is constant, \(T\) remains finite for any finite \(R_0\). The peg inevitably collapses unless \(\mu\) is eliminated.

P2. Prove that if the private sector uses a sunspot variable \(S \in \{0, 1\}\) to coordinate speculative attacks in the Obstfeld vulnerable zone \([\theta_L, \theta_H]\), the sunspot equilibrium is fully rational and Nash.

Solution

Let speculators play the strategy: attack if and only if \(S = 1\). Then \(\pi^e = \Prob(S = 1)\). If \(S = 1\), all speculators attack (\(\pi^e = 1\)). For \(\theta \ge \theta_L\), the government loss from defending satisfies \(\mathcal{L}^D = (\theta + \beta)^2 \ge (\theta_L + \beta)^2 = C = \mathcal{L}^V\). The government's unique best response is to devalue. Hence, speculators who attacked earn positive profits. If \(S = 0\), no speculators attack (\(\pi^e = 0\)). For \(\theta \le \theta_H\), \(\mathcal{L}^D = \theta^2 \le \theta_H^2 = C\). The government's unique best response is to defend. Hence, any rogue speculator who attacked would suffer transaction losses. Therefore, the sunspot-contingent strategy is a Nash equilibrium.

P3. Prove that under Uncovered Interest Parity, a central bank attempting to maintain an exchange rate peg while facing market expectations of devaluation \(\pi^e > 0\) of magnitude \(\Delta s\) over horizon \(\tau\) must raise domestic nominal rates to \(i = i^* + \frac{\pi^e \Delta s}{\tau}\).

Solution

Uncovered Interest Parity states \(i - i^* = \frac{\E[\Delta s]}{\tau}\). Under private sector expectations, with probability \(\pi^e\) the peg devalues by \(\Delta s\), and with probability \(1 - \pi^e\) the peg holds (\(\Delta s = 0\)). Expected depreciation is \(\E[\Delta s] = \pi^e \Delta s + (1 - \pi^e) \times 0 = \pi^e \Delta s\). Substituting into UIP: \(i - i^* = \frac{\pi^e \Delta s}{\tau} \implies i = i^* + \frac{\pi^e \Delta s}{\tau}\). If \(\Delta s = 20\%\), \(\tau = 1/12\) (one month), and \(\pi^e = 0.50\), the required domestic interest rate is \(i = i^* + \frac{0.10}{1/12} = i^* + 120\%\) annualized, demonstrating the impossibility of prolonged interest rate defense.

Empirical

E1. In the UK Black Wednesday crisis of September 16, 1992, the Bank of England raised its base rate from 10% to 12%, and then announced an increase to 15% on the same day, while spending billions in foreign reserves defending the pound within the ERM band. Relate this event to the Obstfeld second-generation model.

Solution

The UK in 1992 was in the Obstfeld vulnerable zone. The UK economy was suffering from high unemployment (10%) and a depressed housing market dominated by floating-rate mortgages. Speculators (including George Soros's Quantum Fund) recognized that the British government could not politically sustain 15% interest rates without causing widespread home foreclosures and deep recession. Speculative selling forced the UK government to evaluate \(\mathcal{L}^D > C\), abandoning the ERM peg that evening.

E2. In the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis, the Bank of Thailand defended the baht peg (25 THB/USD) by committing 23 billion USD in forward contracts against its reported 30 billion USD in gross reserves. Why did headline gross reserve figures fail to signal the impending collapse?

Solution

Central banks report gross foreign reserves on balance sheet. However, by selling forward foreign currency to defend the exchange rate, the Bank of Thailand accumulated massive off-balance-sheet forward liabilities. Net usable reserves were \(R_{\mathrm{net}} = R_{\mathrm{gross}} - R_{\mathrm{forward}} = 30 - 23 = 7\) billion USD. Once market participants deduced that net reserves were nearly exhausted, a final speculative run drained remaining liquidity, forcing the baht to float on July 2, 1997.

E3. In the Mexican Peso Crisis (Tequila Crisis) of December 1994, the Mexican government converted maturing domestic-currency Cetes into dollar-indexed short-term Tesobonos. How did this liability substitution shift Mexico from a first-generation to a second-generation crisis trap?

Solution

By issuing Tesobonos, the government eliminated currency risk for investors in the short term, but concentrated debt rollover risk. By December 1994, Mexico had 29 billion USD in Tesobonos maturing within 12 months against only 6 billion USD in foreign reserves. Investors realized that if other investors refused to roll over Tesobonos, the government would default, creating a classic self-fulfilling rollover run that precipitated the December 20, 1994 devaluation.

Research

R1. Compare the policy effectiveness of capital controls versus bilateral central bank foreign-exchange swap lines in arresting second-generation currency runs.

Solution

Capital controls (e.g., Malaysia 1998) restrict domestic currency convertibility and short-selling, temporarily cutting the link between speculative attacks and domestic interest rates. However, they introduce severe economic distortions, reduce foreign direct investment, and create black market currency discounts. In contrast, bilateral central bank liquidity swap lines (e.g., Federal Reserve swap lines) provide elastic, credible foreign currency liquidity without capital account restrictions, expanding effective reserves and eliminating the vulnerable zone by anchoring market expectations.

R2. In global games models of currency crises (Morris & Shin 1998), imperfect private information with idiosyncratic noise \(\theta_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i\) eliminates multiple equilibria. Explain the economic mechanism that yields a unique threshold.

Solution

In Obstfeld (1996), common knowledge of fundamentals creates multiple equilibria because speculators coordinate costlessly on sunspots. Morris and Shin (1998) introduce private signals: each speculator receives \(\theta_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i\). When signals are slightly noisy, speculators face strategic uncertainty regarding what other speculators believe. By iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies, there exists a unique cut-off threshold \(\theta^*\) such that a speculator attacks if and only if their signal falls below \(\theta^*\). Imperfect information restores determinism to crisis timing.

R3. How do non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) traded in offshore financial centers enable foreign investors to conduct speculative attacks without holding domestic bank balances?

Solution

An NDF is a cash-settled forward contract where the settlement currency is US dollars, calculated as the difference between the contracted NDF rate and the realized onshore spot fix. Because no domestic currency ever changes hands, offshore speculators do not require onshore bank accounts, domestic credit lines, or central bank clearing access. Speculative pressure in offshore NDFs drives onshore forward points down through bank arbitrage, forcing central banks to intervene or capitulate.

    The shadow rate is the market exchange rate that clears the money market after reserves are fully exhausted.

  3. If \(\bar{s} = 2.0\), \(d_0 = 1.0\), \(\mu = 0.10\), and \(\alpha = 2.0\), at what time \(T\) in years does the speculative attack occur?

    \(T = \frac{2.0 - 1.0 - 2.0 \times 0.10}{0.10} = \frac{1.0 - 0.20}{0.10} = \frac{0.80}{0.10} = 8.0\) years.

  4. Why does the exchange rate NOT jump discretely at the moment of the speculative attack in the Krugman model?

    Options

    No-arbitrage smooth pasting ensures \(\tilde{s}_T = \bar{s}\), so the exchange rate transition is continuous.

  5. In Obstfeld's (1996) second-generation crisis model, what characterizes the "vulnerable intermediate zone"?

    Options

    The vulnerable zone features multiple equilibria where speculative attacks are self-fulfilling.

  6. If the fixed cost of realigning the peg is \(C = 16.0\) (\(\sqrt{C} = 4.0\)) and attack sensitivity is \(\beta = 1.5\), what is the lower threshold \(\theta_L\)?

    \(\theta_L = \sqrt{C} - \beta = 4.0 - 1.5 = 2.5\).

  7. Why is an interest rate defense of a currency peg limited in duration?

    Options

    The domestic economic costs of ultra-high interest rates (recession, bank defaults, fiscal burden) force the government to abandon defense.

  8. How does the Morris & Shin (1998) global games framework resolve the indeterminacy of second-generation models?

    Options

    Private information noise removes common knowledge, generating a unique cut-off threshold via iterated dominance.
