The Mundell–Fleming model is the foundational workhorse for open-economy macroeconomic policy analysis. By integrating the IS-LM framework with cross-border goods trade and international capital mobility, it reveals how exchange rate regimes fundamentally alter the effectiveness of fiscal and monetary policy. Under flexible exchange rates, monetary policy is maximally potent while fiscal policy is completely neutralized by currency appreciation. Under fixed exchange rates, the roles reverse: fiscal policy achieves its maximum Keynesian multiplier, while monetary policy becomes entirely powerless.

1The Open-Economy IS-LM-BoP Structure

Definition 1.1 (Mundell–Fleming model)

The Mundell–Fleming model extends the closed-economy IS-LM model to a small open economy with goods trade and cross-border capital mobility. In linear form, the economy is described by three relations:

  • Goods Market Equilibrium (\(\mathrm{IS}\)): \[ Y = c_1(Y - T) + I_0 - b i + G + NX(Y, e) \] where \(c_1 \in (0,1)\) is the marginal propensity to consume, \(b > 0\) is investment interest sensitivity, \(G\) is government spending, and net exports are \(NX = x_0 + x_e e - (m_0 + m_1 Y - m_e e)\), with \(m_1 \in (0,1)\) the marginal propensity to import and \(\eta = x_e + m_e > 0\) the exchange rate sensitivity satisfying the Marshall–Lerner condition. Here \(e\) is the nominal exchange rate quoted such that an increase in \(e\) represents currency depreciation.
  • Money Market Equilibrium (\(\mathrm{LM}\)): \[ \frac{M}{P} = k Y - h i \] where \(k > 0\) is money demand output sensitivity, \(h > 0\) is interest rate sensitivity, and \(P\) is the fixed domestic price level in the short run.
  • Balance of Payments Parity (\(\mathrm{BoP}\)): \[ i = i^\ast \] under perfect international capital mobility with static exchange rate expectations, domestic interest rates must equal the exogenous world rate \(i^\ast\).
Assumption 1.2 (small open economy and static expectations)

Assume (A1) the domestic economy is small relative to world financial markets, taking the foreign interest rate \(i^\ast\) as an exogenous constant; (A2) goods prices are rigid in the short run (\(P = \bar{P}\)); and (A3) exchange rate expectations are static (\(\E[\Delta e] = 0\)), ensuring that perfect capital mobility enforces \(i = i^\ast\).

2Equilibrium under Floating Exchange Rates

Theorem 2.1 (policy multipliers under floating exchange rates)

Under Assumption 1.2 with a freely floating exchange rate and perfect capital mobility:

  1. Fiscal policy is completely ineffective: \[ \frac{dY}{dG} = 0 \] A fiscal expansion (\(\Delta G > 0\)) leaves output unchanged, inducing a currency appreciation (\(\Delta e < 0\)) that crowds out net exports 1-for-1.
  2. Monetary policy achieves maximum output expansion: \[ \frac{dY}{dM} = \frac{1}{k P} > 0 \] A monetary expansion (\(\Delta M > 0\)) stimulates output by inducing capital outflows, causing currency depreciation (\(\Delta e > 0\)) that boosts net exports.
Proof

Because capital mobility enforces \(i = i^\ast\), the LM equation reduces to \(\frac{M}{P} = k Y - h i^\ast\). Notice that output \(Y\) is uniquely determined by money supply and the world interest rate:

\[ Y = \frac{M/P + h i^\ast}{k} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(G\) yields \(\frac{dY}{dG} = 0\), proving that fiscal spending cannot alter output. From the IS equation, \(Y [1 - c_1 + m_1] = A_0 - b i^\ast + G + \eta e\). Substituting the fixed output \(Y\) and differentiating with respect to \(G\) gives \(0 = 1 + \eta \frac{de}{dG} \implies \frac{de}{dG} = -\frac{1}{\eta} < 0\). The currency appreciates by exactly enough to reduce net exports by \(\Delta NX = \eta \Delta e = -1 \cdot \Delta G = -\Delta G\), achieving 100% exchange rate crowding out.

Differentiating \(Y\) with respect to \(M\) gives \(\frac{dY}{dM} = \frac{1}{k P}\). To clear the goods market at higher output, the exchange rate must depreciate: \(\frac{de}{dM} = \frac{1 - c_1 + m_1}{\eta k P} > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Equilibrium under Fixed Exchange Rates

Theorem 3.1 (policy multipliers under fixed exchange rates)

Under Assumption 1.2 with a fixed exchange rate (\(e = \bar{e}\)) and perfect capital mobility:

  1. Fiscal policy achieves its maximum Keynesian multiplier: \[ \frac{dY}{dG} = \frac{1}{1 - c_1 + m_1} > 0 \] A fiscal expansion induces capital inflows, forcing the central bank to intervene by purchasing foreign reserves, expanding the domestic money supply endogenously.
  2. Monetary policy is completely ineffective: \[ \frac{dY}{dM} = 0 \] Any attempt to expand the money supply causes capital flight, forcing the central bank to sell reserves until the money supply contracts back to its initial level.
Proof

Under a fixed exchange rate, \(e = \bar{e}\) is pegged, and the central bank must maintain \(i = i^\ast\). Substituting \(\bar{e}\) and \(i^\ast\) into the IS equation gives:

\[ Y = \frac{A_0 - b i^\ast + G + \eta \bar{e}}{1 - c_1 + m_1} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(G\) yields \(\frac{dY}{dG} = \frac{1}{1 - c_1 + m_1}\). To maintain \(i = i^\ast\) at this higher output, the endogenous money supply must expand to satisfy the LM equation: \(\Delta M = P k \Delta Y = \frac{P k}{1 - c_1 + m_1} \Delta G\).

Conversely, if the central bank attempts an autonomous monetary expansion \(\Delta M > 0\), interest rates momentarily dip below \(i^\ast\). Unbounded capital flight immediately forces the central bank to sell foreign currency reserves and buy domestic currency to defend \(\bar{e}\), contracting the money supply until \(i = i^\ast\) and \(M\) returns to its original equilibrium. Thus \(\frac{dY}{dM} = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Mundell–Fleming Multiplier Verification in Exact Rational Arithmetic

Solves the linear Mundell–Fleming open economy system under both fixed and floating regimes in exact Fraction arithmetic, demonstrating that dY/dG = 0 under floating and dY/dM = 0 under fixed exchange rates.

"""Lab 5: The Mundell-Fleming Linear Model Multiplier Verification.

Exact Fraction arithmetic. Standard library only.
Solves the IS-LM-BoP open economy system under perfect capital mobility (i = i*).
Compares fiscal and monetary policy effectiveness under Fixed vs. Floating exchange rates.
"""

from fractions import Fraction


class MundellFlemingModel:
    def __init__(
        self,
        c1: Fraction,  # Marginal propensity to consume
        m1: Fraction,  # Marginal propensity to import
        eta: Fraction,  # Marshall-Lerner sensitivity of net exports to exchange rate
        k: Fraction,  # Money demand sensitivity to output
        h: Fraction,  # Money demand sensitivity to interest rate
        p: Fraction,  # Price level (fixed in short run)
    ) -> None:
        self.c1 = c1
        self.m1 = m1
        self.eta = eta
        self.k = k
        self.h = h
        self.p = p

    def solve_floating(
        self,
        m_supply: Fraction,
        g_spending: Fraction,
        i_world: Fraction,
        a0: Fraction,
    ) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
        """Under Floating FX: Money market pins Y; Goods market pins exchange rate e.

        Y = (M/P + h * i*) / k
        e = [ Y * (1 - c1 + m1) - (A0 - b*i* + G) ] / eta
        """
        # LM determines Y
        real_balances = m_supply / self.p
        y = (real_balances + self.h * i_world) / self.k

        # IS determines equilibrium exchange rate e
        autonomous_exp = a0 + g_spending
        numerator = y * (Fraction(1, 1) - self.c1 + self.m1) - autonomous_exp
        e = numerator / self.eta
        return y, e

    def solve_fixed(
        self,
        e_target: Fraction,
        g_spending: Fraction,
        i_world: Fraction,
        a0: Fraction,
    ) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
        """Under Fixed FX: Goods market pins Y; Money supply M adjusts endogenously to clear LM.

        Y = (A0 - b*i* + G + eta * e_target) / (1 - c1 + m1)
        M = P * (k * Y - h * i*)
        """
        multiplier = Fraction(1, 1) / (Fraction(1, 1) - self.c1 + self.m1)
        autonomous_exp = a0 + g_spending + self.eta * e_target
        y = multiplier * autonomous_exp

        # Endogenous money supply required to maintain i = i*
        m_endogenous = self.p * (self.k * y - self.h * i_world)
        return y, m_endogenous

    def multipliers(self) -> dict[str, Fraction]:
        """Compute analytical multipliers dY/dG and dY/dM under both regimes."""
        # Floating
        dy_dg_float = Fraction(0, 1)
        dy_dm_float = Fraction(1, 1) / (self.k * self.p)

        # Fixed
        dy_dg_fixed = Fraction(1, 1) / (Fraction(1, 1) - self.c1 + self.m1)
        dy_dm_fixed = Fraction(0, 1)

        return {
            "dy_dg_floating": dy_dg_float,
            "dy_dm_floating": dy_dm_float,
            "dy_dg_fixed": dy_dg_fixed,
            "dy_dm_fixed": dy_dm_fixed,
        }


def main() -> None:
    # Model parameters: c1 = 0.6, m1 = 0.2, eta = 500, k = 0.5, h = 1000, P = 1.0
    model = MundellFlemingModel(
        c1=Fraction(6, 10),
        m1=Fraction(2, 10),
        eta=Fraction(500, 1),
        k=Fraction(1, 2),
        h=Fraction(1000, 1),
        p=Fraction(1, 1),
    )

    i_world = Fraction(3, 100)  # 3.0%
    a0 = Fraction(200, 1)  # Autonomous expenditure baseline
    g_base = Fraction(100, 1)  # Baseline government spending
    m_base = Fraction(400, 1)  # Baseline money supply

    # Baseline floating
    y_float_base, e_float_base = model.solve_floating(m_base, g_base, i_world, a0)

    # Fiscal expansion under floating (Delta G = +50)
    g_expanded = g_base + Fraction(50, 1)
    y_float_fiscal, e_float_fiscal = model.solve_floating(m_base, g_expanded, i_world, a0)

    # Monetary expansion under floating (Delta M = +50)
    m_expanded = m_base + Fraction(50, 1)
    y_float_monetary, e_float_monetary = model.solve_floating(m_expanded, g_base, i_world, a0)

    # Fixed regime targeting e = e_float_base
    y_fixed_base, m_fixed_base = model.solve_fixed(e_float_base, g_base, i_world, a0)
    y_fixed_fiscal, m_fixed_fiscal = model.solve_fixed(e_float_base, g_expanded, i_world, a0)

    mults = model.multipliers()

    print("=== Mundell–Fleming Analytical Multipliers ===")
    print(f"Floating Regime: dY/dG = {mults['dy_dg_floating']} (Fiscal policy ineffective)")
    print(f"Floating Regime: dY/dM = {float(mults['dy_dm_floating']):.2f} (Monetary policy maximum effectiveness)")
    print(f"Fixed Regime:    dY/dG = {float(mults['dy_dg_fixed']):.2f} (Fiscal policy maximum effectiveness)")
    print(f"Fixed Regime:    dY/dM = {mults['dy_dm_fixed']} (Monetary policy ineffective)\n")

    print("=== Policy Shock Responses (Exact) ===")
    print(f"Base Output (Y): {float(y_float_base):.2f}, Base Exchange Rate (e): {float(e_float_base):.4f}")
    delta_y_fiscal = float(y_float_fiscal - y_float_base)
    delta_e_fiscal = float(e_float_fiscal - e_float_base)
    print(f"Floating Fiscal (+50 G): Delta Y = {delta_y_fiscal:.2f}, FX change = {delta_e_fiscal:+.4f}")
    delta_y_mon = float(y_float_monetary - y_float_base)
    delta_e_mon = float(e_float_monetary - e_float_base)
    print(f"Floating Monetary (+50 M): Delta Y = {delta_y_mon:.2f}, FX change = {delta_e_mon:+.4f}")
    delta_y_fixed = float(y_fixed_fiscal - y_fixed_base)
    delta_m_fixed = float(m_fixed_fiscal - m_fixed_base)
    print(f"Fixed Fiscal (+50 G): Delta Y = {delta_y_fixed:.2f}, Money expansion = {delta_m_fixed:.2f}")

    assert mults["dy_dg_floating"] == Fraction(0, 1), "Floating fiscal multiplier must be 0"
    assert mults["dy_dm_fixed"] == Fraction(0, 1), "Fixed monetary multiplier must be 0"
    assert y_float_fiscal == y_float_base, "Fiscal policy under floating must not change output"
    assert y_fixed_fiscal > y_fixed_base, "Fiscal policy under fixed must expand output"


main()

The lab confirms the classic Mundell–Fleming policy dichotomy: under floating rates, fiscal policy produces zero output change due to complete exchange rate crowding out, while monetary policy delivers full expansion. Under fixed rates, fiscal policy achieves a multiplier of 1.67 while monetary policy produces zero output change.

Where the Canon Breaks

The standard Mundell–Fleming model is static and abstracts from intertemporal optimization and price dynamics:

  • Absence of Price Dynamics: Prices are assumed strictly fixed (\(P = \bar{P}\)). When prices adjust over time, output returns to potential, and monetary expansions generate inflation rather than permanent output gains.
  • Imperfect Capital Mobility: In economies with capital controls or sovereign risk spreads, the BoP curve is upward-sloping rather than horizontal, giving fiscal policy some traction under floating rates and restoring partial monetary autonomy under pegs.

Misconceptions

  1. Fiscal stimulus always expands output regardless of the exchange rate regime.

    Under flexible exchange rates with open capital, fiscal expansion fails to expand output. The upward pressure on domestic interest rates attracts massive capital inflows, appreciating the exchange rate until net exports fall by the exact amount of the fiscal injection.

  2. A country with a fixed exchange rate can use open-market operations to lower interest rates and stimulate investment.

    Any attempt to lower policy rates below the foreign rate triggers capital flight. Defending the peg requires selling foreign currency reserves, which drains domestic liquidity and reverses the initial monetary expansion completely.

  3. Monetary policy stimulates output under floating exchange rates primarily by reducing the domestic interest rate.

    With perfect capital mobility, the domestic interest rate cannot stay below the world rate in equilibrium (\(i = i^\ast\)). The true transmission channel is exchange rate depreciation: capital outflows depress the currency, which stimulates export competitiveness and net exports.

Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

In a floating economy, \(k = 0.40\), \(P = 1.0\), and \(h = 500\). The world interest rate is \(i^\ast = 0.03\). If the central bank expands the nominal money supply by \(\Delta M = 80\), what is the resulting change in equilibrium output \(\Delta Y\)?

Solution

Under floating rates, output is determined by the LM relation: \(\Delta Y = \frac{\Delta M}{k P} = \frac{80}{0.40 \times 1.0} = 200\).

In a fixed exchange rate economy, the marginal propensity to consume is \(c_1 = 0.75\) and the marginal propensity to import is \(m_1 = 0.15\). Compute the fiscal multiplier \(\frac{dY}{dG}\).

Solution

The fixed exchange rate fiscal multiplier is \(\frac{dY}{dG} = \frac{1}{1 - c_1 + m_1} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.75 + 0.15} = \frac{1}{0.40} = 2.50\).

In a floating economy, \(G\) increases by 60. Sensitivity of net exports to the exchange rate is \(\eta = 300\). By how much must the nominal exchange rate \(e\) change to restore goods market equilibrium?

Solution

Under floating rates, output does not change (\(\Delta Y = 0\)), so net exports must change by \(\Delta NX = -\Delta G = -60\). Because \(\Delta NX = \eta \Delta e\), we have \(\Delta e = -60 / 300 = -0.20\) (a currency appreciation of 0.20 units).

Proof Tier

Prove that the fiscal multiplier under fixed exchange rates is strictly smaller than the closed-economy Keynesian multiplier \(1/(1 - c_1)\) when imports depend positively on income (\(m_1 > 0\)).

Solution

In a closed economy, the multiplier is \(K_{\mathrm{closed}} = \frac{1}{1 - c_1}\). In the open fixed economy, it is \(K_{\mathrm{open}} = \frac{1}{1 - c_1 + m_1}\). Since \(m_1 > 0\), the denominator is strictly larger: \(1 - c_1 + m_1 > 1 - c_1 > 0\). Taking reciprocals yields \(K_{\mathrm{open}} < K_{\mathrm{closed}}\). Import leakage drains a fraction \(m_1\) of each dollar of induced spending abroad, dampening domestic multiplier expansion.

Prove that under floating rates, an increase in foreign interest rates \(i^\ast\) causes domestic output to expand.

Solution

Differentiating the floating equilibrium output \(Y = \frac{M/P + h i^\ast}{k}\) with respect to \(i^\ast\) yields \(\frac{dY}{di^\ast} = \frac{h}{k} > 0\). An increase in foreign rates induces capital outflows, depreciating the domestic currency. The resulting surge in export competitiveness stimulates net exports and expands aggregate output.

Show that if capital mobility is completely absent (vertical BoP curve), fiscal policy is effective under floating exchange rates.

Solution

Without capital mobility, the balance of payments depends strictly on net exports: \(\mathrm{BoP} = NX(Y, e) = 0\). A fiscal expansion raises income \(Y\), which increases imports and causes a trade deficit. To restore balance of payments equilibrium, the exchange rate must depreciate (\(\Delta e > 0\)) rather than appreciating. The depreciation further boosts net exports, reinforcing rather than crowding out the fiscal expansion.

Empirical Tier

Evaluate the Reagan-era policy mix of the early 1980s (large fiscal deficits combined with tight monetary policy) in the United States through the lens of the floating Mundell–Fleming model.

Solution

The US operated under floating exchange rates. Large fiscal deficits (\(\Delta G > 0\)) and Volcker monetary tightening (\(\Delta M < 0\)) pushed domestic interest rates higher, attracting massive foreign capital inflows. As predicted by the model, the US dollar appreciated by over 50% between 1980 and 1985, severely depressing US manufacturing exports and creating a massive trade deficit.

Why do Eurozone member states (such as Germany or Italy) have high fiscal multipliers compared to floating economies like the UK?

Solution

Eurozone nations share a common currency, operating under a fixed bilateral exchange rate regime with open capital. National fiscal expansions cannot induce national currency appreciation. The European Central Bank maintains the union-wide interest rate, allowing domestic fiscal stimulus to work with its full Keynesian multiplier without exchange rate crowding out.

Explain why the "beggar-thy-neighbour" critique is levied against monetary easing under floating exchange rates.

Solution

In the floating Mundell–Fleming model, monetary expansion expands domestic output primarily by depreciating the exchange rate, which diverts international expenditure toward domestic goods and away from foreign goods (\(\Delta NX > 0\)). From the perspective of foreign trading partners, their currencies appreciate, their net exports contract, and their domestic output falls.

Research Tier

How does introducing rational forward-looking exchange rate expectations alter the Mundell–Fleming policy multipliers?

Solution

When expectations are forward-looking, exchange rates can jump discontinuously in anticipation of future policy changes (Dornbusch overshooting). A permanent fiscal expansion causes the exchange rate to jump immediately to its new appreciated level. If market participants anticipate future policy reversals, the initial currency appreciation is amplified, accelerating the crowding out of net exports.

Formulate the Mundell–Fleming model with sovereign risk premia, where domestic interest rates satisfy \(i = i^\ast + \theta(D/Y)\) with debt-to-GDP ratio \(D/Y\).

Solution

A fiscal expansion funded by borrowing increases \(D/Y\), driving up the sovereign risk spread \(\theta\). Domestic interest rates rise above \(i^\ast\). In the goods market, higher interest rates depress private investment \(I(i)\). If the risk premium effect is large, the contraction in private investment can exceed the initial fiscal stimulus, causing output to contract even under a fixed exchange rate (contractionary fiscal expansion).

Evaluate how the transition to New Keynesian open-economy macroeconomics modifies the Mundell–Fleming results.

Solution

New Keynesian models replace static IS-LM equations with forward-looking Euler equations and price-setting under Calvo contracts. Monetary policy still works through exchange rate depreciation, but terms-of-trade passthrough depends crucially on whether export prices are preset in producer currency (PCP) or dominant currency (DCP). Under dollar invoicing (DCP), the expenditure-switching channel of depreciation is severely muted.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. Under a floating exchange rate with perfect capital mobility, what is the value of the fiscal multiplier \(dY/dG\)?

    Options

    Fiscal expansion induces capital inflows, appreciating the currency until net exports decline 1-for-1 with spending.

  2. Under a fixed exchange rate with perfect capital mobility, what is the value of the monetary multiplier \(dY/dM\)?

    Options

    Attempted monetary expansion induces capital flight, forcing the central bank to sell foreign reserves and contract money supply back to initial equilibrium.

  3. If \(c_1 = 0.70\) and \(m_1 = 0.20\), what is the fiscal multiplier \(dY/dG\) under a fixed exchange rate?

    \(dY/dG = 1 / (1 - 0.70 + 0.20) = 1 / 0.50 = 2.0\).

  4. What is the primary transmission mechanism of monetary policy under floating exchange rates in the Mundell–Fleming model?

    Options

    Because interest rates cannot permanently diverge from world rates under perfect capital mobility, monetary expansion works by depreciating the currency and expanding net exports.

  5. In a floating economy with \(k = 0.5\), by how much does output \(Y\) change if government purchases increase by 100?

    Under floating rates with perfect capital mobility, the fiscal multiplier is exactly 0.0.

  6. Why is the open-economy fixed exchange rate multiplier smaller than the closed-economy Keynesian multiplier?

    Options

    A fraction \(m_1\) of each dollar of induced spending is spent on imported foreign goods, dampening the domestic multiplier round.

  7. Under floating rates with \(k = 0.40\) and \(P = 1.0\), what output expansion \(\Delta Y\) is generated by an injection of \(\Delta M = 100\)?

    \(\Delta Y = \Delta M / (k P) = 100 / 0.40 = 250\).

  8. In the Mundell–Fleming model, what condition guarantees that currency depreciation improves net exports?

    Options

    The Marshall–Lerner condition ensures that the price elasticities of export and import demand are sufficiently high for depreciation to improve the trade balance.
