The Dominant-Currency Paradigm

Traditional international trade models assume that prices are set either in the exporter's currency (Producer Currency Pricing) or the importer's currency (Local Currency Pricing). In reality, the vast majority of cross-border trade is invoiced in a single vehicle currency: the US dollar. Under the Dominant-Currency Paradigm established by Gopinath et al. (2020), this dollar dominance overturns textbook predictions. When a non-US currency depreciates, its export volumes to third countries fail to expand because its dollar-invoiced prices are sticky, while domestic import prices spike 100%. Consequently, a strong US dollar acts as a synchronized contractionary tax on global trade.

1Trade Invoicing Currency Regimes

Definition 1.1 (dominant-currency paradigm)

The dominant-currency paradigm (DCP) is the international trade and pricing framework in which cross-border goods prices are preset and sticky in a dominant vehicle currency—overwhelmingly the US dollar—regardless of the geographic origin or destination of the trade flow. DCP is contrasted with two traditional benchmarks:

  • Producer Currency Pricing (\(\mathrm{PCP}\)): Exporters set and maintain sticky prices in their own domestic currency. A bilateral currency depreciation lowers the price faced by foreign buyers in foreign currency, expanding export volumes.
  • Local Currency Pricing (\(\mathrm{LCP}\)): Exporters set sticky prices in the currency of the destination market. A bilateral currency depreciation leaves consumer prices unchanged abroad, altering exporter profit markups without shifting export volumes.
  • Dominant Currency Paradigm (\(\mathrm{DCP}\)): All firms set sticky prices in US dollars (\(\$\)), such that both export and import prices are rigid in dollars.
Assumption 1.2 (preset dollar invoicing)

Consider three countries: Home (\(H\)), Foreign (\(F\)), and the United States (\(\$\)). Assume (A1) all bilateral trade between \(H\) and \(F\) is invoiced and preset in US dollars; (A2) prices in the invoice currency are rigid in the short run; and (A3) demand for exports and imports has a constant price elasticity \(\varepsilon > 0\).

2Passthrough and Volume Asymmetry under DCP

Theorem 2.1 (DCP passthrough and volume response theorem)

Under Assumption 1.2, suppose the Home currency depreciates by fraction \(\delta > 0\) against the US dollar and against the Foreign currency (\(\Delta s_{H/\$} = \Delta s_{H/F} = \delta\)):

  1. Zero Export Volume Stimulus: Home export prices in US dollars remain sticky (\(\Delta p_{H,X}^{\$} = 0\)). Because the Foreign currency did not change relative to the dollar (\(\Delta s_{F/\$} = 0\)), the price of Home exports in Foreign currency is unchanged: \[ \Delta p_{H,X}^F = 0 \implies \Delta \ln Q_{H,X} = 0 \] Home export volume to third countries does not expand.
  2. Full Import Price Passthrough: Home import prices are preset in dollars. The price of imports in Home currency rises 1-for-1 with the dollar exchange rate: \[ \Delta p_{H,M}^H = \delta \implies \Delta \ln Q_{H,M} = -\varepsilon \delta \] Home import volume contracts sharply.
Proof

By Assumption 1.2, Home exports to Foreign are invoiced in USD with price \(P_{H,X}^{\$}\). The price faced by Foreign consumers in Foreign currency is \(P_{H,X}^F = P_{H,X}^{\$} / S_{F/\$}\), where \(S_{F/\$}\) is the price of USD in Foreign currency. In log notation, \(p_{H,X}^F = p_{H,X}^{\$} - s_{F/\$}\). Since the Foreign currency did not move relative to the dollar (\(\Delta s_{F/\$} = 0\)) and dollar prices are sticky (\(\Delta p_{H,X}^{\$} = 0\)), we obtain \(\Delta p_{H,X}^F = 0\). With constant elasticity \(\varepsilon\), export demand satisfies \(\Delta \ln Q_{H,X} = -\varepsilon \Delta p_{H,X}^F = 0\). This proves statement 1.

For imports, Foreign exports to Home are invoiced in USD at price \(P_{F,X}^{\$}\). The price paid by Home buyers in Home currency is \(P_{H,M}^H = P_{F,X}^{\$} \cdot S_{H/\$}\). In log terms, \(p_{H,M}^H = p_{F,X}^{\$} + s_{H/\$}\). Since \(p_{F,X}^{\$}\) is sticky in dollars (\(\Delta p_{F,X}^{\$} = 0\)), we have \(\Delta p_{H,M}^H = \Delta s_{H/\$} = \delta\). The import volume response is \(\Delta \ln Q_{H,M} = -\varepsilon \Delta p_{H,M}^H = -\varepsilon \delta\), proving statement 2. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.2 (the contractionary dollar channel)

Theorem 2.1 establishes a critical macroeconomic implication: when the US dollar appreciates against all other currencies, non-US economies face higher import prices in domestic currency, suppressing their real import volumes. Crucially, non-US exporters do NOT experience offsetting export volume gains. As a result, global trade volumes contract whenever the US dollar strengthens globally (Gopinath et al. 2020).

Lab 1 · Dominant-Currency Paradigm Trade Response Verification

Calculates export and import price passthrough and volume responses under Producer Currency Pricing, Local Currency Pricing, and the Dominant-Currency Paradigm following a 10% domestic currency depreciation against the USD.

"""Lab 7: Dominant-Currency Paradigm (DCP) Trade Passthrough and Volume Elasticity.

Exact calculation. Standard library only.
Compares Producer Currency Pricing (PCP), Local Currency Pricing (LCP), and DCP (Gopinath et al. 2020).
Shows that under DCP, a non-US currency depreciation fails to stimulate export volumes to third countries
while driving 100% passthrough into domestic import prices.
"""


def compute_trade_responses(
    deprec_vs_usd: float,  # Depreciation of Home currency vs USD (e.g. +0.10 = 10%)
    deprec_vs_third: float,  # Depreciation of Home currency vs Third Country (e.g. +0.10)
    price_elasticity: float = 1.0,  # Trade volume price elasticity
) -> dict[str, dict[str, float]]:
    """Compute passthrough to export prices in buyer currency, import prices in home currency, and volume responses."""
    # 1. Producer Currency Pricing (PCP): prices preset in exporter's currency
    # Home exports to Third Country: price in Third Country currency falls by deprec_vs_third
    pcp_export_price_buyer = -deprec_vs_third
    pcp_export_volume = -price_elasticity * pcp_export_price_buyer  # Increases
    # Home imports from Third Country: price in Home currency rises by deprec_vs_third
    pcp_import_price_home = deprec_vs_third
    pcp_import_volume = -price_elasticity * pcp_import_price_home  # Decreases

    # 2. Local Currency Pricing (LCP): prices preset in buyer's currency
    # Home exports to Third Country: price in Third Country currency is fixed (0)
    lcp_export_price_buyer = 0.0
    lcp_export_volume = 0.0
    # Home imports from Third Country: price in Home currency is fixed (0)
    lcp_import_price_home = 0.0
    lcp_import_volume = 0.0

    # 3. Dominant Currency Paradigm (DCP): all trade invoiced in USD
    # Home exports to Third Country: priced in USD.
    # Third Country did not depreciate against USD, so buyer price is unchanged.
    dcp_export_price_buyer = 0.0
    dcp_export_volume = 0.0
    # Home imports from Third Country: invoiced in USD. Price in Home currency rises by deprec_vs_usd.
    dcp_import_price_home = deprec_vs_usd
    dcp_import_volume = -price_elasticity * dcp_import_price_home

    return {
        "PCP": {
            "export_price_buyer": pcp_export_price_buyer,
            "export_volume": pcp_export_volume,
            "import_price_home": pcp_import_price_home,
            "import_volume": pcp_import_volume,
            "net_trade_stimulus": pcp_export_volume - pcp_import_volume,
        },
        "LCP": {
            "export_price_buyer": lcp_export_price_buyer,
            "export_volume": lcp_export_volume,
            "import_price_home": lcp_import_price_home,
            "import_volume": lcp_import_volume,
            "net_trade_stimulus": lcp_export_volume - lcp_import_volume,
        },
        "DCP": {
            "export_price_buyer": dcp_export_price_buyer,
            "export_volume": dcp_export_volume,
            "import_price_home": dcp_import_price_home,
            "import_volume": dcp_import_volume,
            "net_trade_stimulus": dcp_export_volume - dcp_import_volume,
        },
    }


def main() -> None:
    deprec = 0.10  # 10% Home currency depreciation
    results = compute_trade_responses(deprec_vs_usd=deprec, deprec_vs_third=deprec, price_elasticity=1.0)

    print("=== Dominant-Currency Paradigm vs Traditional Pricing ===")
    print(f"Shock: Home currency depreciates by {deprec * 100:.1f}% against USD and third countries.\n")

    hdr = f"{'Regime':<8} | {'Export Price':<14} | {'Export Volume':<14} | {'Import Price':<14} | {'Import Volume':<14}"
    print(hdr)
    print("-" * 72)
    for regime in ("PCP", "LCP", "DCP"):
        r = results[regime]
        ep = r["export_price_buyer"] * 100
        ev = r["export_volume"] * 100
        ip = r["import_price_home"] * 100
        iv = r["import_volume"] * 100
        print(f"{regime:<8} | {ep:+13.1f}% | {ev:+13.1f}% | {ip:+13.1f}% | {iv:+13.1f}%")

    print("\nKey Takeaway (Gopinath et al. 2020):")
    print("Under DCP (dollar invoicing), bilateral depreciation provides NO export volume boost")
    dcp_ev = results["DCP"]["export_volume"] * 100
    print(f"(Export volume change = {dcp_ev:.1f}%), while import prices surge by 100% of the shock.")

    assert results["PCP"]["export_volume"] > 0, "PCP should boost export volume"
    assert results["DCP"]["export_volume"] == 0.0, "DCP exports to third countries should not expand"
    assert results["DCP"]["import_price_home"] == deprec, "DCP import prices must rise by full depreciation"

Currency depreciation fails to provide competitive export stimulus under dollar trade invoicing.

Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook open-economy models (such as Mundell–Fleming) rely heavily on the expenditure-switching channel of currency depreciation. Under DCP:

  • Neutralization of Expenditure Switching: The classic argument that flexible exchange rates insulate economies from shocks via export expansion fails for goods trade invoiced in dollars.
  • Stagflationary Currency Depreciation: For a small open economy with high dollar invoicing, depreciation acts as a stagflationary negative supply shock: it spikes imported inflation without generating an export-led GDP expansion.

Misconceptions

  1. A country that exports goods manufactured entirely with domestic labour will always invoice in its own currency.

    Empirical evidence across Latin America, Asia, and emerging markets shows that over 80% of exports are invoiced in US dollars even when neither trading partner is the United States, reflecting network complementarities in international trade invoicing.

  2. Currency depreciation provides an immediate competitive advantage that expands bilateral trade balances.

    Under DCP, export quantities do not expand in the short run because foreign buyers see unchanged dollar prices. Simultaneously, import values in domestic currency rise immediately, causing the trade balance to worsen before improving (the classic J-curve aggravated by dollar pricing).

  3. The US dollar's dominance in international trade is driven purely by bilateral trade with the United States.

    The share of global trade invoiced in US dollars exceeds 40% (and reaches 80% outside Europe), more than four times the United States' actual share of global goods imports, establishing the dollar as the preeminent global vehicle currency.

Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

Under DCP with full dollar invoicing, South Korea exports machinery to Brazil priced at 10,000 USD. The Korean won depreciates by 12% against the USD, while the Brazilian real is unchanged against the USD. What is the price change of the machinery in Brazilian real, and what is the export volume response assuming a price elasticity of 1.5?

Solution

Under DCP, the machinery is invoiced in USD at 10,000 USD. Since the Brazilian real did not move against the USD, the price in Brazilian real is unchanged (0% change). With zero price change in the buyer's currency, the export volume change is \(\Delta \ln Q = -1.5 \times 0 = 0.0\%\).

Under PCP, a Japanese exporter prices a component at 150,000 JPY. The JPY depreciates by 10% against the US dollar. If the initial exchange rate was 150 JPY/USD, what is the new price in USD, and what is the percentage change in export price for US buyers?

Solution

The initial price in USD was \(150{,}000 / 150 = 1{,}000\) USD. After a 10% JPY depreciation, the new exchange rate is \(150 \times 1.10 = 165\) JPY/USD. The new price in USD is \(150{,}000 / 165 = 909.09\) USD, representing a \(9.09\%\) price drop (\(\approx -10\%\) in log terms) for US buyers.

An economy has import price elasticity \(\varepsilon_m = 0.8\). 90% of imports are invoiced in US dollars. If the domestic currency depreciates by 15% against the USD, what is the percentage contraction in import volume?

Solution

Import price in domestic currency rises by \(0.90 \times 15\% = 13.5\%\). With elasticity \(\varepsilon_m = 0.8\), import volume contracts by \(\Delta \ln Q_m = -0.8 \times 13.5\% = -10.8\%\).

Proof Tier

Prove that under 100% DCP, a global appreciation of the US dollar against all other currencies strictly contracts total world trade volume.

Solution

Let all non-US currencies depreciate by \(\delta > 0\) against the USD. By Theorem 2.1, for every non-US country \(i\), export prices in foreign destinations are invariant (\(\Delta p_{i,X}^j = 0\)), so export volumes do not expand (\(\Delta \ln Q_{i,X} = 0\)). However, import prices in every non-US country rise by \(\delta\), contracting import volumes by \(-\varepsilon \delta < 0\). Summing across all bilateral trade pairs, total world trade volume change is strictly negative: \(\Delta \ln Q_{\mathrm{world}} = -\sum_i w_i \varepsilon \delta < 0\).

Prove that terms of trade (export price over import price in domestic currency) deteriorate under DCP following a domestic currency depreciation.

Solution

Let export price in USD be \(P_X^{\$}\) and import price in USD be \(P_M^{\$}\). In domestic currency, \(P_X^H = P_X^{\$} S\) and \(P_M^H = P_M^{\$} S\). Under 100% DCP with identical invoicing currency, \(\mathrm{ToT} = P_X^H / P_M^H = P_X^{\$} / P_M^{\$}\), which is unchanged in pure homogeneous trade. However, when domestic goods have sticky domestic wages while imports are dollar-invoiced, domestic export prices in local currency rise sluggishly while import prices jump immediately by \(\Delta s\), causing the domestic terms of trade to deteriorate sharply: \(\Delta \mathrm{ToT} < 0\).

Show that strategic complementarities in price-setting induce a unique Nash equilibrium where all exporters choose the dominant currency for trade invoicing.

Solution

Let firm \(j\) set price to minimize distance from competitors' prices. If a fraction \(\alpha\) of competitors invoice in USD, firm \(j\)'s optimal invoicing choice weights USD by \(\alpha\). When market share of USD exceeds a critical coordination threshold, the best response of every firm is to invoice in USD to avoid bilateral terms-of-trade volatility against competitors, establishing 100% dollar invoicing as an asymptotically stable Nash equilibrium.

Empirical Tier

Describe the empirical findings of Gopinath et al. (2020) regarding US bilateral trade passthrough compared to non-US bilateral trade.

Solution

Gopinath et al. (2020) demonstrate that for the United States, import price passthrough in USD is nearly zero (foreign exporters absorb exchange rate swings to keep dollar prices constant), while for non-US countries, import price passthrough from the dollar exchange rate is close to 100% within one quarter. This empirical asymmetry confirms the global dominance of dollar pricing.

Why does the Dominant Currency Paradigm make floating exchange rates less effective as shock absorbers for emerging market economies?

Solution

In traditional models, currency depreciation stimulates export demand to buffer domestic recessions. Under DCP, depreciation produces zero export volume expansion while instantly driving up domestic imported inflation and the cost of imported intermediate inputs, causing stagflationary pressure rather than economic stabilization.

How does the prevalence of global value chains (GVCs) reinforce the dominant-currency invoicing of intermediate inputs?

Solution

Firms participating in complex multi-country supply chains purchase intermediate components invoiced in US dollars. To hedge their operating margins against currency mismatch, these firms invoice their final output in US dollars as well, creating an input-output propagation mechanism that locks in dollar invoicing across the entire supply chain.

Research Tier

How does the Dominant Currency Paradigm alter optimal monetary policy rules in a small open economy?

Solution

In classical PCP models, the central bank should maintain flexible exchange rates and target domestic producer inflation. Under DCP, exchange rate volatility produces severe import price volatility without export volume benefits. Optimal monetary policy under DCP assigns a positive weight to stabilizing the nominal exchange rate against the US dollar, justifying managed floating or foreign exchange intervention.

Evaluate the international role of the euro versus the US dollar in trade invoicing.

Solution

The euro serves as a powerful regional dominant currency within the European Union and European neighbourhood trade. However, on a truly global scale (trade involving Asia, Latin America, Africa, and commodity markets), the US dollar remains the dominant global vehicle currency, accounting for the vast majority of non-European international trade invoicing.

What structural economic changes could potentially challenge the US dollar's dominance as the primary vehicle currency in international trade?

Solution

Challenging dollar dominance requires: (1) substantial expansion of non-US bilateral trade settlements (e.g. petro-yuan contracts); (2) deep, liquid financial markets in alternative invoicing currencies free of capital controls; and (3) a massive coordinated shift among global commodity exporters that breaks the strategic network complementarity of dollar pricing.

  1. Under the Dominant Currency Paradigm, what currency dominates international trade invoicing?

    Options

    Gopinath et al. (2020) show that the US dollar serves as the preeminent vehicle currency for international trade pricing.

  2. What happens to a non-US country's export volumes to third countries when its currency depreciates under DCP?

    Options

    Because prices are sticky in dollars, third-country buyers face unchanged prices in their own currencies, so export demand does not change.

  3. Under 100% dollar invoicing, what is the short-run percentage passthrough of a dollar exchange rate depreciation into domestic import prices?

    Because import prices are sticky in dollars, local currency import prices rise 1-for-1 with the dollar exchange rate (100% passthrough).

  4. What effect does an appreciation of the US dollar against all other currencies have on global trade volume under DCP?

    Options

    Higher dollar import prices suppress imports globally without triggering offsetting export volume surges, contracting world trade.

  5. Under DCP, by how many percent does export volume to third countries increase following a 15% bilateral currency depreciation?

    Export volume change is exactly 0.0% under preset dollar invoicing.

  6. How does Producer Currency Pricing (PCP) differ fundamentally from DCP?

    Options

    Under PCP, depreciation lowers the foreign currency price of exports, stimulating foreign demand.

  7. Why do firms in emerging economies actively choose to invoice trade in US dollars?

    Options

    Strategic complementarities and hedging dollar-invoiced intermediate inputs encourage coordination on dollar pricing.

  8. If import price elasticity is 0.8 and the domestic currency depreciates by 10% against the dollar under 100% DCP, what is the percentage drop in import volume?

    \(\Delta \ln Q_m = 0.8 \times 10\% = 8.0\%\) contraction.
