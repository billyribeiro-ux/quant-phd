Covered Interest Parity and the Cross-Currency Basis

Covered Interest Parity (CIP) is among the most celebrated theoretical no-arbitrage relationships in international financial economics. It asserts that the forward exchange rate premium must exactly offset the money-market interest rate differential. Prior to the 2007–2008 global financial crisis, CIP held with remarkable precision in liquid currencies. Since then, CIP has failed persistently, giving rise to a non-zero cross-currency basis. A negative basis means borrowing dollars synthetically through foreign currency swaps costs substantially more than cash dollar borrowing, reflecting post-crisis bank balance-sheet constraints and severe global dollar funding strains.

1Theoretical Derivation of Covered Interest Parity

Definition 1.1 (covered interest parity)

Covered interest parity (CIP) is the theoretical condition under which the interest rate earned on a domestic deposit equals the hedged return earned by converting funds into foreign currency, investing at the foreign money-market rate, and selling the foreign currency forward at rate \(F_t\):

\[ 1 + i_t \tau_{\mathrm{usd}} = \frac{S_t}{F_t} (1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}}) \]

where \(S_t\) is the spot exchange rate (USD per unit foreign currency), \(F_t\) is the forward rate for maturity \(d\) days, \(i_t\) is the annual cash dollar interest rate, \(i_t^\ast\) is the annual foreign interest rate, and \(\tau_{\mathrm{usd}}, \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}}\) are the respective day-count accrual fractions (typically \(\tau = d / 360\)).

Assumption 1.2 (frictionless no-arbitrage)

Assume (A1) zero bid-ask transaction costs; (A2) absence of default risk across domestic and foreign deposit counterparties; (A3) perfectly liquid, unconstrained borrowing and lending at posted money-market rates; and (A4) zero capital requirements or regulatory levies on intermediary balance-sheet expansion.

Theorem 1.3 (CIP theoretical forward rate)

Under Assumption 1.2, no-arbitrage requires the forward exchange rate \(F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}\) to satisfy:

\[ F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}} = S_t \frac{1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}}}{1 + i_t \tau_{\mathrm{usd}}} \]
Proof

Consider two alternative strategies for a financial institution seeking riskless dollar returns over period \([t, t + \tau]\):

  • Strategy A (Cash Dollar Deposit): Invest 1 USD in a cash domestic deposit to receive \(1 + i_t \tau_{\mathrm{usd}}\) at maturity.
  • Strategy B (Synthetic Dollar Deposit): Convert 1 USD into \(1/S_t\) units of foreign currency at spot rate \(S_t\). Invest in a riskless foreign deposit to yield \((1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}})/S_t\) foreign units at maturity. Simultaneously enter a forward contract at time \(t\) to sell all foreign proceeds at forward exchange rate \(F_t\), locking in \(\frac{F_t}{S_t}(1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}})\) USD.

Both strategies are completely riskless and deliver cash at identical maturity dates. Under Assumption 1.2, if \(1 + i_t \tau_{\mathrm{usd}} < \frac{F_t}{S_t}(1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}})\), an arbitrageur could borrow in cash dollars and invest in synthetic dollars, generating unbounded riskless profit. If the reverse inequality holds, the arbitrageur borrows synthetically and deposits cash dollars. To preclude arbitrage, both payoffs must be identical: \(1 + i_t \tau_{\mathrm{usd}} = \frac{F_t}{S_t}(1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}})\). Solving for \(F_t\) establishes the theoretical CIP forward rate \(F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

2The Cross-Currency Basis and Synthetic Dollar Borrowing

Definition 2.1 (cross-currency basis and implied rate)

When market quotes deviate from theoretical CIP, the implied dollar interest rate \(i_{\mathrm{implied}}\) earned by lending dollars synthetically via an FX swap is:

\[ i_{\mathrm{implied}} = \frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{usd}}} \left[ \frac{S_t}{F_t} (1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}}) - 1 \right] \]

The cross-currency basis \(b_t\) (expressed in basis points) is the spread between the cash dollar interest rate and the implied dollar rate:

\[ b_t = (i_t - i_{\mathrm{implied}}) \times 10{,}000 \]

Equivalently, the basis is the spread added to the cash dollar rate such that synthetic dollar borrowing clears:

\[ 1 + \left(i_t - \frac{b_t}{10{,}000}\right) \tau_{\mathrm{usd}} = \frac{S_t}{F_t}(1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}}) \]
Proposition 2.2 (basis sign interpretation)

The sign of the cross-currency basis indicates the relative price of direct versus synthetic dollar funding:

  • Zero Basis (\(b_t = 0\)): Theoretical CIP holds exactly. Forward price equals \(F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}\).
  • Negative Basis (\(b_t < 0\)): The implied rate exceeds the cash rate (\(i_{\mathrm{implied}} > i_t\)). Synthetic dollar borrowing costs more than cash borrowing. Forward dollars trade at a premium (\(F_t < F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}\)). Non-US institutions face a premium to access US dollar liquidity.
  • Positive Basis (\(b_t > 0\)): Cash dollar borrowing costs more than synthetic borrowing (\(i_{\mathrm{implied}} < i_t\)), implying an abundance of offshore dollar liquidity relative to domestic rates.
Proof

From Definition 2.1, \(b_t = (i_t - i_{\mathrm{implied}}) \times 10{,}000\). If \(i_{\mathrm{implied}} > i_t\), then \(b_t < 0\). An institution borrowing dollars synthetically must swap foreign currency into dollars today at spot \(S_t\) and repay at forward \(F_t\), paying effective interest \(i_{\mathrm{implied}} = i_t - b_t/10{,}000 > i_t\). Thus, synthetic borrowing incurs a penalty spread of \(-b_t > 0\) basis points over cash borrowing. If \(F_t = F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}\), Theorem 1.3 shows \(i_{\mathrm{implied}} = i_t\), so \(b_t = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Empirical CIP Deviations Post-2008

Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018) document that while CIP deviations were negligible prior to 2008, significant negative cross-currency bases emerged across major currencies (including EUR, JPY, CHF, and GBP) after the global financial crisis and persisted for years. Why do global arbitrageurs fail to eliminate this apparent 20 to 60 basis point riskless spread? The breakdown stems directly from post-crisis regulatory capital constraints:

  • Balance-Sheet Costs: Arbitrage requires a bank to simultaneously borrow cash dollars, enter an FX swap, and deposit foreign currency. Even though market risk is fully hedged, both sides of the trade expand the bank's gross balance sheet.
  • Basel III Leverage Ratio: Under the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR), banks face minimum capital requirements against gross unweighted assets. Intermediary balance-sheet space is costly, requiring a persistent spread to justify arbitrage capital allocation.

Lab 1 · Covered Interest Parity and Cross-Currency Basis Computation

Calculates the theoretical CIP forward rate in exact rational arithmetic, confirms zero basis for frictionless parity, and computes the cross-currency basis in basis points for simulated EUR/USD and JPY/USD quotes showing post-2008 negative bases.

"""Lab 3: Covered Interest Parity (CIP) and the Cross-Currency Basis.

SIMULATED numbers. Standard library only.
Computes the CIP forward rate and the cross-currency basis in basis points.
Sign convention: basis = (usd_rate - implied_usd_rate) * 10,000.
Negative basis indicates dollar funding shortage (synthetic borrowing costs > cash borrowing).
"""

from fractions import Fraction


def compute_cip_forward(
    spot: Fraction,
    usd_rate: Fraction,
    foreign_rate: Fraction,
    tau_usd: Fraction,
    tau_foreign: Fraction,
) -> Fraction:
    """Compute frictionless CIP theoretical forward exchange rate (USD per unit foreign)."""
    num = spot * (Fraction(1, 1) + foreign_rate * tau_foreign)
    den = Fraction(1, 1) + usd_rate * tau_usd
    return num / den


def compute_cross_currency_basis_bp(
    spot: float,
    forward: float,
    usd_rate: float,
    foreign_rate: float,
    tau_usd: float,
    tau_foreign: float,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Compute implied USD rate and cross-currency basis in basis points.

    Convention:
    implied_usd_rate = (1 / tau_usd) * [ (spot / forward) * (1 + foreign_rate * tau_foreign) - 1 ]
    basis_bp = (usd_rate - implied_usd_rate) * 10,000
    """
    if spot <= 0 or forward <= 0 or tau_usd <= 0 or tau_foreign <= 0:
        msg = "Quotes and accrual fractions must be strictly positive"
        raise ValueError(msg)

    foreign_accrual = 1.0 + foreign_rate * tau_foreign
    implied_usd = (1.0 / tau_usd) * ((spot / forward) * foreign_accrual - 1.0)
    basis_bp = (usd_rate - implied_usd) * 10000.0
    return implied_usd, basis_bp


def main() -> None:
    # 3-month tenor (90 days on actual/360)
    tau_usd = Fraction(90, 360)
    tau_eur = Fraction(90, 360)
    tau_jpy = Fraction(90, 360)

    # Base rates: USD 5.0%, EUR 3.0%, JPY -0.1%
    i_usd_frac = Fraction(5, 100)
    i_eur_frac = Fraction(3, 100)
    i_jpy_frac = Fraction(-1, 1000)

    # 1. CIP theoretical parity check (EUR/USD, spot = 1.10)
    s_eur = Fraction(11, 10)
    f_cip_eur = compute_cip_forward(s_eur, i_usd_frac, i_eur_frac, tau_usd, tau_eur)

    implied_cip, basis_cip = compute_cross_currency_basis_bp(
        float(s_eur), float(f_cip_eur), float(i_usd_frac), float(i_eur_frac), float(tau_usd), float(tau_eur)
    )

    # 2. Observed EUR/USD with negative basis (dollar shortage post-2008)
    # Observed forward is lower than CIP forward (synthetic USD borrowing rate is higher than cash rate)
    f_obs_eur = 1.0938
    implied_eur, basis_eur = compute_cross_currency_basis_bp(
        float(s_eur), f_obs_eur, float(i_usd_frac), float(i_eur_frac), float(tau_usd), float(tau_eur)
    )

    # 3. Observed JPY/USD with pronounced negative basis (Japanese institutional dollar demand)
    # Spot quote: 150.0 JPY per USD => Direct quote = 1/150 USD per JPY
    s_jpy = 1.0 / 150.0
    # CIP forward would be (1/150) * (1 - 0.001*0.25)/(1 + 0.05*0.25) ~= 0.0065827
    # Observed forward: 0.0065748 (dollar forward is expensive)
    f_obs_jpy = 0.0065748
    implied_jpy, basis_jpy = compute_cross_currency_basis_bp(
        s_jpy, f_obs_jpy, float(i_usd_frac), float(i_jpy_frac), float(tau_usd), float(tau_jpy)
    )

    print("=== Covered Interest Parity and Basis (SIMULATED) ===")
    print(f"Theoretical CIP Forward (EUR/USD): {float(f_cip_eur):.6f}")
    print(f"Frictionless CIP Basis:            {basis_cip:+.2f} bp (Implied USD: {implied_cip * 100:.3f}%)")
    print(f"\nEUR/USD Observed Forward:          {f_obs_eur:.6f}")
    print(f"EUR/USD Implied USD Rate:          {implied_eur * 100:.3f}% (Cash: {float(i_usd_frac) * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"EUR/USD Cross-Currency Basis:      {basis_eur:+.1f} bp")
    print(f"\nJPY/USD Observed Forward:          {f_obs_jpy:.8f} (Spot: {s_jpy:.8f})")
    print(f"JPY/USD Implied USD Rate:          {implied_jpy * 100:.3f}% (Cash: {float(i_usd_frac) * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"JPY/USD Cross-Currency Basis:      {basis_jpy:+.1f} bp")

    assert abs(basis_cip) < 1e-9, f"CIP forward must yield zero basis, got {basis_cip}"
    assert basis_eur < 0, f"Observed EUR basis must be negative, got {basis_eur}"
    assert basis_jpy < 0, f"Observed JPY basis must be negative, got {basis_jpy}"

Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook open-economy macroeconomics treats CIP as a structural identity that holds with certainty. In actual financial markets:

  • Persistent Regulatory Wedges: Post-crisis regulation transformed CIP from a frictionless parity identity into a balance-sheet shadow price. The cross-currency basis is non-zero in almost all tenors and currencies.
  • Funding Segmentation across Currencies: The basis differs materially across currency pairs depending on local institutional hedging demand. Japanese pension funds and life insurers seeking dollar assets drive JPY basis deeply negative, whereas Australian dollar bases can be positive.

Misconceptions

  1. A persistent non-zero cross-currency basis represents pure free money that any trader can extract.

    Exploiting the CIP deviation requires borrowing unsecured cash in one currency and lending in another. Global banks face regulatory leverage capital charges, credit valuation adjustments (CVA), and internal funding hurdle rates that exceed the 20 to 50 bp spread, rendering the net arbitrage profit negative after regulatory capital costs.

  2. The cross-currency basis is driven primarily by sovereign default risk on government debt.

    Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018) demonstrate that CIP deviations persist even when evaluated using sovereign debt yields of identical credit rating, and when measured against swap benchmarks. The basis reflects intermediary balance-sheet costs and dollar funding imbalances rather than differential default risk.

  3. A negative basis means the US dollar is expected to depreciate in the future.

    The cross-currency basis measures covered deviations (fully hedged against exchange rate movements with forward contracts). It contains zero information regarding expected currency appreciation or depreciation, which is the domain of uncovered interest parity.

Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

Spot rate is \(S = 1.1200\) USD per EUR. The 6-month USD rate is 5.0% and the EUR rate is 3.5% (both on Actual/360 with \(d = 180\) days). Calculate the theoretical CIP forward rate \(F^{\mathrm{CIP}}\).

Solution

Accrual fraction is \(\tau = 180 / 360 = 0.50\). Foreign accrual: \(1 + 0.035 \times 0.50 = 1.0175\). Domestic accrual: \(1 + 0.050 \times 0.50 = 1.0250\). The CIP forward is \(F^{\mathrm{CIP}} = 1.1200 \times \frac{1.0175}{1.0250} = 1.1200 \times 0.9926829 = 1.111805\) USD per EUR.

Given \(S = 1.1000\), \(F = 1.0920\), \(i_{\mathrm{usd}} = 5.20\%\), \(i_{\mathrm{eur}} = 3.60\%\), and \(\tau = 0.25\) (3-month). Compute the implied dollar rate and the cross-currency basis in basis points.

Solution

Foreign gross accrual is \(1 + 0.036 \times 0.25 = 1.0090\). The ratio \(S/F = 1.1000 / 1.0920 = 1.007326\). Gross implied factor: \(1.007326 \times 1.0090 = 1.016392\). Implied rate: \((1.016392 - 1.0) / 0.25 = 0.065568\) (6.557%). Basis: \(b = (0.0520 - 0.065568) \times 10{,}000 = -135.7\) bp.

If the observed cross-currency basis on 3-month JPY/USD is \(-60.0\) bp, and the cash USD rate is 4.80%, what is the effective annual dollar borrowing rate for a Japanese bank borrowing dollars synthetically via an FX swap?

Solution

Because \(b = (i_{\mathrm{usd}} - i_{\mathrm{implied}}) \times 10{,}000\), we have \(i_{\mathrm{implied}} = i_{\mathrm{usd}} - b/10{,}000 = 0.0480 - (-0.0060) = 0.0540\) (5.40%). The Japanese bank pays 5.40% effective interest, an extra 60 basis points above the cash dollar market rate.

Proof Tier

Prove that in the continuous-time limit, the log theoretical forward premium equals the exact instantaneous interest rate differential.

Solution

With continuous compounding over horizon \(T\), the no-arbitrage condition is \(e^{i T} = \frac{S}{F} e^{i^\ast T} \iff F = S e^{(i - i^\ast) T}\). Taking natural logarithms gives \(\ln F - \ln S = (i - i^\ast) T\). Dividing by \(T\) and letting \(T \to 0\) yields \(\frac{f - s}{T} = i - i^\ast\), proving that the instantaneous forward premium is identically equal to the instantaneous interest differential.

Prove that a positive bid-ask spread in spot and forward markets creates an arbitrage-free band within which CIP deviations cannot be profitably exploited.

Solution

Let spot quotes be \(S^b < S^a\) and forward quotes be \(F^b < F^a\). An arbitrageur borrowing cash dollars must borrow at ask rate \(i_{\mathrm{usd}}^a\), convert at spot ask \(S^a\), invest at foreign bid rate \(i_{\mathrm{foreign}}^b\), and sell forward at bid \(F^b\). Profit requires \(\frac{F^b}{S^a}(1 + i_{\mathrm{foreign}}^b \tau) - (1 + i_{\mathrm{usd}}^a \tau) > 0\). The reverse direction requires \((1 + i_{\mathrm{usd}}^b \tau) - \frac{F^a}{S^b}(1 + i_{\mathrm{foreign}}^a \tau) > 0\). These two inequality thresholds form a non-degenerate positive band around zero within which transactions costs eliminate any net arbitrage profit.

Show that the cross-currency basis can be expressed directly as the difference in forward rates between observed forward \(F_t\) and the CIP forward \(F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}\).

Solution

By definition, \(1 + i_{\mathrm{implied}} \tau_{\mathrm{usd}} = \frac{S_t}{F_t}(1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}})\) and \(1 + i_t \tau_{\mathrm{usd}} = \frac{S_t}{F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}}(1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}})\). Subtracting the first from the second gives \((i_t - i_{\mathrm{implied}}) \tau_{\mathrm{usd}} = S_t(1 + i_t^\ast \tau_{\mathrm{foreign}}) \left( \frac{1}{F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}} - \frac{1}{F_t} \right) = (1 + i_t \tau_{\mathrm{usd}}) \left( 1 - \frac{F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}}{F_t} \right) \approx \frac{F_t - F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}}{F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}}\). Multiplying by \(10{,}000 / \tau_{\mathrm{usd}}\) shows that the basis in basis points is proportional to the percentage mispricing of the forward contract.

Empirical Tier

Why did the cross-currency basis for major currencies fail to revert to zero following the conclusion of the 2008 global financial crisis?

Solution

As documented by Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018), post-crisis banking regulations—chiefly the Basel III leverage ratio (SLR), the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR), and Volcker rule limits on proprietary trading—permanently raised the shadow cost of intermediary balance-sheet space. Banks can no longer expand their balance sheets costlessly to arbitrage away small rate discrepancies.

How does heavy institutional hedging demand by non-US institutional investors (such as European pension funds holding US Treasuries) affect the cross-currency basis?

Solution

When European institutions buy US assets, they hedge their dollar exposure by selling dollars forward and buying euros forward. This creates massive structural one-way order flow selling forward dollars, pushing \(F_t\) down below \(F_t^{\mathrm{CIP}}\). Intermediaries must take the other side, and their limited risk-bearing capacity causes the cross-currency basis to widen negatively.

Why does the cross-currency basis typically exhibit pronounced spikes at calendar quarter-ends?

Solution

Global systemically important banks (G-SIBs), particularly in Europe and Japan, report their balance sheets for regulatory capital and leverage ratios based on quarter-end snapshots (March 31, June 30, September 30, December 31). To window-dress and reduce capital charges, dealers aggressively shrink repo and FX swap provision right at the quarter end, causing the basis to drop dramatically.

Research Tier

Formulate the balance-sheet optimization problem of a global dealer bank subject to a leverage ratio constraint, and derive the shadow price that generates a non-zero CIP basis.

Solution

Consider a dealer maximizing expected arbitrage profit \(\pi = b \cdot x\) subject to a leverage constraint \(\frac{\mathrm{Equity}}{\mathrm{Assets}_0 + 2x} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}}\), where \(x\) is the size of the CIP arbitrage trade requiring both cash borrowing and synthetic lending (expanding assets by \(2x\)). The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal{L} = b x - \mu (2x + \mathrm{Assets}_0 - \mathrm{Equity}/\lambda_{\mathrm{SLR}})\). First-order condition with respect to \(x\) yields \(b = 2\mu\), where \(\mu\) is the shadow value of balance-sheet capacity. When the constraint binds (\(\mu > 0\)), the equilibrium cross-currency basis must equal \(2\mu > 0\) to cover the marginal regulatory capital penalty.

Evaluate the efficacy of central bank reciprocal currency swap lines (such as the Federal Reserve liquidity swap lines) in dampening negative basis spikes.

Solution

The Federal Reserve provides swap lines allowing foreign central banks (ECB, BOJ, SNB, BOE) to borrow USD against domestic currency collateral at fixed rates. Foreign central banks then auction dollars to domestic banks. This circumvents the impaired private intermediary balance-sheet bottleneck, capping how negative the basis can drop and restoring offshore dollar funding stability during global crises.

How does the choice of interest rate benchmark (LIBOR, SOFR, OIS, or government bill rates) alter the measured cross-currency basis?

Solution

Because different benchmarks reflect different degrees of credit risk, term premia, and regulatory liquidity weights, the measured basis varies across benchmarks. Du et al. (2018) show that using OIS rates isolates pure funding spreads free of bank credit risk, whereas using interbank deposit rates incorporates bank credit premia. However, non-zero bases persist across all benchmark choices post-2008.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What instrument is combined with a spot currency transaction to construct a covered, riskless foreign investment?

    Options

    Covered Interest Parity relies on a forward contract to lock in the foreign exchange proceeds risklessly at inception.

  2. What does a negative cross-currency basis (\(b < 0\)) imply about the cost of borrowing US dollars synthetically via an FX swap?

    Options

    A negative basis means \(i_{\mathrm{implied}} > i_{\mathrm{usd}}\), so borrowing dollars via an FX swap costs more than cash money-market borrowing.

  3. In basis points, what is the theoretical cross-currency basis under perfect, frictionless covered interest parity?

    Under theoretical frictionless CIP, the basis is identically 0.0 bp.

  4. According to Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018), what primary regulatory factor prevents arbitrageurs from eliminating the post-2008 cross-currency basis?

    Options

    Basel III leverage ratios impose equity capital requirements on unweighted gross assets, making gross balance-sheet expansion costly for arbitrageurs.

  5. If the cash dollar rate is 5.20% and the implied dollar rate from an FX swap is 5.50%, what is the cross-currency basis in basis points?

    \(b = (0.0520 - 0.0550) \times 10{,}000 = -0.0030 \times 10{,}000 = -30\) bp.

  6. When an investor lends dollars in an FX swap, what spot and forward transactions are executed?

    Options

    Lending dollars via swap means providing dollars today (selling USD spot for foreign currency) and contracting to receive dollars back at maturity (buying USD forward).

  7. Why does heavy institutional hedging demand by European pension funds buying US assets exert downward pressure on the EUR/USD basis?

    Options

    Selling forward dollars pushes the forward dollar rate down, widening the gap between market forwards and CIP theoretical forwards.

  8. If \(S = 1.10\) USD per EUR, \(i_{\mathrm{foreign}} = 2.0\%\), \(i_{\mathrm{usd}} = 2.936\%\), and \(\tau = 1.0\), what is the theoretical CIP forward rate \(F^{\mathrm{CIP}}\)?

    \(F = 1.10 \times (1 + 0.02) / (1 + 0.02936) = 1.10 \times 1.02 / 1.02936 = 1.122 / 1.02936 \approx 1.090\) USD per EUR.
