Mechanism design and the revelation principle

  • Uses: Bayesian–Nash equilibrium, expectation, signaling, screening
  • Introduces: mechanism design, the revelation principle
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study

A seller who cannot see a buyer's value still sets the rules of the sale. Mechanism design is the study of those rules. This lesson defines the two constraints that a direct mechanism must meet to be feasible for the designer, incentive compatibility and individual rationality. It proves the revelation principle for one agent with finite sets, which says that a direct mechanism, in which the agent reports its type, can reproduce every equilibrium outcome of a more complicated mechanism, in the one-agent setting of §2. It then solves a two-type revenue problem. Lesson L08 applies the same logic to an auction.

1Types, mechanisms and the two constraints

Money is in integer cents. A buyer has a private type \(\theta\), which is its value for one unit of a good, in cents. The seller knows only the set of types and the prior probability of each. The seller chooses rules, and the buyer chooses a report. Let \(\Theta = \{L, H\}\) be the set of two types, with values \(v_L = 6000\) and \(v_H = 10000\) cents, and let \(\Delta = v_H - v_L = 4000\) cents. The prior probabilities are \(p_L = 3/10\) and \(p_H = 7/10\). A reported type gives a probability of trade \(q\) and a payment \(\tau\) in cents. The word type is used in this lesson for the private value, and it has no other meaning here.

Assumption 1 (types and prior)

The set \(\Theta\) of types is finite, and each type \(\theta\) has a known prior probability \(p_\theta > 0\), with \(\sum_{\theta} p_\theta = 1\). The seller knows \(\Theta\) and the prior, and does not observe the type.

Assumption 2 (risk neutrality and quasi-linear utility)

A buyer of type \(\theta\) who gets probability \(q\) of trade and pays \(\tau\) cents has utility \(u_\theta(q, \tau) = v_\theta q - \tau\), in cents. The buyer is risk neutral: it cares about the expected value of the outcome, and the utility is linear in \(q\). The outside option, which is declining to take part, has utility 0.

Definition 1.1 (mechanism design)

Mechanism design is the choice, by a designer, of the rules of a game: the messages each agent may send and the outcome that each message profile produces. The designer's aim is stated in advance, and the rules are chosen so that the agents' own choices of messages serve that aim. In this lesson the designer is the seller, the agent is the buyer, and the messages are reports.

Definition 1.2 (direct mechanism, incentive compatibility, individual rationality)

A direct mechanism is a map from each reported type \(r \in \Theta\) to an outcome \((q_r, \tau_r)\). The list of these outcomes, one for each report, is also called a menu. It is incentive compatible (IC) when, for every type \(\theta\) and every report \(r\), \(u_\theta(q_\theta, \tau_\theta) \ge u_\theta(q_r, \tau_r)\). That is, truthful reporting is a best report for every type. It is individually rational (IR) when \(u_\theta(q_\theta, \tau_\theta) \ge 0\) for every type, so every type does at least as well as the outside option. Both conditions are written out for each type and each report in Lab 1.

Remark 1.3 (what IC does not require)

IC compares the truthful outcome of each type with the outcome of every report, both evaluated at that type's value. IR compares the truthful outcome with 0. Neither condition requires a particular price or a particular probability. A mechanism can satisfy IC and fail IR, and Lab 1 Part A shows one such menu.

2The revelation principle, for one agent with finite sets

A mechanism in general asks the agent to send messages from a set that the designer chooses, and the outcome depends on the message. This section states the setting in which the principle is proved, and then proves it. The setting is deliberately narrow. Nothing here is claimed for several agents.

Assumption 3 (one agent, finite sets)

There is one agent, with a finite type set \(\Theta\) and a finite message set \(M\). The outcome set \(X\) is finite, and a mechanism is an outcome rule \(x : M \to X\). The agent's utility from outcome \(y \in X\) is \(u_\theta(y)\) when its type is \(\theta\). A strategy is a map \(s : \Theta \to M\). A strategy is an equilibrium when, for every type \(\theta\) and every message \(m \in M\), \(u_\theta(x(s(\theta))) \ge u_\theta(x(m))\).

For one agent, the condition in the last line is the Bayesian–Nash condition of L02 with the other players removed. The other players are absent, so the expected utility of a type depends only on that type's own message and outcome. A type's expected payoff is therefore its own utility, and the prior enters only the designer's expected revenue, not the agent's best reply.

Definition 2.1 (the revelation principle, the statement proved here)

The revelation principle, in the setting of Assumption 3, is the statement of Proposition 2.2: every equilibrium outcome of a mechanism is the outcome of a direct mechanism under which truthful reporting is an equilibrium. The lesson gives this result no historical attribution. Its literature is outside the registry (see §9).

Proposition 2.2 (a direct mechanism reproduces every equilibrium outcome)

Under Assumption 3, let \(x : M \to X\) be a mechanism and let \(s\) be an equilibrium. Define the direct mechanism \(\hat{x}(\theta) = x(s(\theta))\) for each type. Then truthful reporting is an equilibrium of \(\hat{x}\): for every type \(\theta\) and every report \(\theta'\), \(u_\theta(\hat{x}(\theta)) \ge u_\theta(\hat{x}(\theta'))\). Each type receives the same outcome under \(\hat{x}\) as under \(x\) and \(s\).

Proof

Fix a type \(\theta\) and a report \(\theta'\). By definition \(\hat{x}(\theta') = x(s(\theta'))\). The message \(s(\theta')\) is one message in \(M\), so the equilibrium condition of Assumption 3, applied to the type \(\theta\) and the message \(m = s(\theta')\), gives \(u_\theta(x(s(\theta))) \ge u_\theta(x(s(\theta')))\). The left side is \(u_\theta(\hat{x}(\theta))\), because \(\hat{x}(\theta) = x(s(\theta))\). The right side is \(u_\theta(\hat{x}(\theta'))\). So \(u_\theta(\hat{x}(\theta)) \ge u_\theta(\hat{x}(\theta'))\), which is the truthful-report condition. The outcome of type \(\theta\) under \(\hat{x}\) is \(x(s(\theta))\), which is its outcome under \(x\) and \(s\). Since the outcome of every type is the same, the distribution of outcomes, and so every expected payment and every expected trade, is the same under both mechanisms.

Remark 2.3 (what the proof does not use)

The proof uses only the equilibrium condition for the one agent and the definition of \(\hat{x}\). It does not use any structure of \(M\), of \(X\), or of the utilities. Where a type has more than one equilibrium message, the proof uses the message \(s(\theta)\) that the chosen equilibrium specifies. Different equilibria can give different direct mechanisms. Part C of Lab 1 shows two of them, which give different revenues.

Remark 2.4 (the special cases of L04 and L05)

Screening (screening, L05) is a case of the menu of Definition 1.2: the uninformed party offers a menu, and in L05 the menu has one item for each type, so it is a direct mechanism with the type as the report. Signaling (signaling, L04) is different. There the informed worker moves first with an observable action, and the firm sets the wage after it sees that action. This lesson does not treat the game of L04 as a case of Assumption 3.

3The two-type revenue problem

The designer now chooses a direct mechanism \((q_L, \tau_L, q_H, \tau_H)\) to maximize expected revenue, subject to IC and IR. The result below is about this problem, and it uses Assumptions 1 and 2.

Assumption 4 (the designer's problem)

The designer maximizes expected revenue \(R = p_L \tau_L + p_H \tau_H\) over direct mechanisms with \(q_\theta \in [0, 1]\) for each type, subject to IC and IR for each type (Definition 1.2). Revenue is in cents.

Lemma 3.1 (the incentive and rationality constraints reduce to an interval)

Under Assumptions 1, 2 and 4, with \(\Delta = v_H - v_L > 0\), let \(U_\theta = v_\theta q_\theta - \tau_\theta\) be the utility of type \(\theta\) from its truthful report. A direct mechanism with \(q_\theta \in [0, 1]\) is IC and IR if and only if \(U_L \ge 0\), \(q_L \le q_H\), and \(U_L + \Delta q_L \le U_H \le U_L + \Delta q_H\).

Proof

Write the two IC conditions that compare reports across types. Type H does not gain by reporting L: \(U_H \ge v_H q_L - \tau_L = U_L + (v_H - v_L) q_L = U_L + \Delta q_L\). Type L does not gain by reporting H: \(U_L \ge v_L q_H - \tau_H\). Since \(v_L q_H - \tau_H = (v_H q_H - \tau_H) - \Delta q_H = U_H - \Delta q_H\), this is \(U_H \le U_L + \Delta q_H\). The IR conditions are \(U_L \ge 0\) and \(U_H \ge 0\). The second follows from the first, because \(U_H \ge U_L + \Delta q_L \ge 0\). The two bounds on \(U_H\) can both hold only if \(U_L + \Delta q_L \le U_L + \Delta q_H\), that is \(\Delta q_L \le \Delta q_H\). Since \(\Delta > 0\), this is \(q_L \le q_H\). Conversely, if the stated conditions hold, every inequality above holds, so the mechanism is IC and IR.

Proposition 3.2 (the revenue-maximizing direct mechanism for two types)

Under Assumptions 1, 2 and 4, with \(\Delta = v_H - v_L > 0\), let \(c = p_L v_L - p_H \Delta\). The expected revenue is maximized by a direct mechanism with \(q_H = 1\), with \(U_L = 0\), and with \(q_L = 0\) when \(c < 0\), or with \(q_L = 1\) when \(c > 0\), and either choice when \(c = 0\). When \(c < 0\) the maximum is \(p_H v_H\). When \(c > 0\) it is \(v_L\). When \(c = 0\) both choices give \(p_H v_H = v_L\).

Proof

Let \(R\) be the expected revenue in terms of \(U_L\), \(U_H\), \(q_L\) and \(q_H\). Using \(\tau_\theta = v_\theta q_\theta - U_\theta\) and \(p_L + p_H = 1\), \[ R = p_L(v_L q_L - U_L) + p_H(v_H q_H - U_H). \] By Lemma 3.1, \(U_H \ge U_L + \Delta q_L\). Revenue falls as \(U_H\) rises, so for given \(U_L\) and allocations, the best choice is \(U_H = U_L + \Delta q_L\). Substituting, \[ R = p_H v_H q_H + (p_L v_L - p_H \Delta) q_L - U_L = p_H v_H q_H + c\, q_L - U_L. \] Revenue falls as \(U_L\) rises, and \(U_L \ge 0\) by Lemma 3.1, so the best choice is \(U_L = 0\). Then \(U_H = \Delta q_L\), and this choice satisfies the interval of Lemma 3.1 because \(q_L \le q_H\). So the designer's problem is to maximize \(f(q_L, q_H) = p_H v_H q_H + c\,q_L\) over \(0 \le q_L \le q_H \le 1\). For fixed \(q_L\), \(f\) is increasing in \(q_H\) because \(p_H v_H > 0\), so \(q_H = 1\) is optimal, and this choice is feasible for every \(q_L \le 1\). With \(q_H = 1\), \(f\) is linear in \(q_L\) on \([0, 1]\), so its maximum is at an endpoint: \(q_L = 0\) gives \(p_H v_H\), and \(q_L = 1\) gives \(p_H v_H + c = p_H v_H + p_L v_L - p_H \Delta = v_L\), using \(p_H \Delta = p_H v_H - p_H v_L\) and \(p_H + p_L = 1\). If \(c < 0\), the endpoint \(q_L = 0\) is the larger, so the maximum is \(p_H v_H\). If \(c > 0\), the endpoint \(q_L = 1\) is the larger, so the maximum is \(v_L\). If \(c = 0\), the two endpoints are equal, and \(p_H v_H = p_H \Delta + p_H v_L = p_L v_L + p_H v_L = v_L\), so both \(q_L = 0\) and \(q_L = 1\) attain the maximum. The proof uses \(q_L \le q_H\) only to allow the choice \(q_H = 1\), which is feasible for every \(q_L\).

Example 3.3 (the menus of Lab 1)

Take \(v_L = 6000\), \(v_H = 10000\), \(p_H = 7/10\). Then \(c = (3/10)(6000) - (7/10)(4000) = 1800 - 2800 = -1000\), which is negative. Proposition 3.2 gives the optimal menu \(q_L = 0\), \(q_H = 1\), \(\tau_H = 10000\), with \(\tau_L = 0\), and revenue \(0.7 \times 10000 = 7000\) cents. Selling to both types at the low value gives \(6000\) cents, a menu that gives both types the same trade and price. This is called pooling. Selling only to type H, with type L excluded, is called exclusion. Part A of Lab 1 prints both revenues, and it prints the menu that sells to both types at the high value, which passes IC and fails IR. Part B prints the grid optimum, which is the same menu, at 7000 cents.

Lab 1 · Direct menus, every misreport, and the revelation construction

The inputs are illustrative: values of 6000 and 10000 cents, and a prior of 7/10 on the high type. Before running, the prediction is this. Part A prints the utility of every type under every report, for four menus. Proposition 2.2 needs no printed check, but Part C prints the direct menu induced by a best-response strategy, and it should pass IC. Part B prints the number of grid points that pass the enumerated checks, and by Proposition 3.2 the optimum should be \(q_L = 0\), \(q_H = 1\), with revenue 7000 cents. The lab uses no random draws, so the output is fixed.

"""E2 L07 lab 1: a two-type direct mechanism, every misreport enumerated, and the revelation construction.

Inputs are illustrative, not data. A buyer has one of two values in integer cents: 6000 (type L) or 10000
(type H), with prior probability 3/10 on L and 7/10 on H. A mechanism gives each reported type a probability of
trade q (a number from 0 to 1) and a payment t (in cents). A buyer of value v who reports r gets the utility
v * q(r) - t(r), in cents. No random draws are used.

Part A: two feasible direct mechanisms, one menu that fails individual rationality, and one menu that fails
incentive compatibility. Every report of every type is enumerated. Each line
prints a utility, and the last lines print whether the mechanism is incentive compatible and individually
rational, and its expected revenue in whole cents (half up).
Part B: a grid search over the allocation pairs (q_L, q_H) with 0 <= q_L <= q_H <= 1 in steps of 1/4. Each pair
gets the payments that leave type L with zero utility and leave type H indifferent to reporting L. The search
keeps the pair with the largest expected revenue among grid points that pass the enumerated checks.
Part C: a menu of three messages, a strategy that maps each type to a message, and the direct mechanism that
the strategy induces. A type with a tie between two messages picks the one listed first (low-price is listed
before decline). The induced direct mechanism is checked by the same enumeration as Part A.

Money is integer cents. Probabilities and expected values are exact Fractions; only the printed cents are rounded.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction

VALUES = {"L": 6000, "H": 10000}
PRIOR = {"L": Fraction(3, 10), "H": Fraction(7, 10)}
TYPES = ("L", "H")


def utility(value: int, trade: Fraction, payment: int) -> Fraction:
    """Utility in cents of a buyer with the given value who gets probability trade and pays payment cents."""
    return value * trade - payment


def half_up_cents(amount: Fraction) -> int:
    """The amount in whole cents, a tie going up (amount is nonnegative here)."""
    return math.floor(amount + Fraction(1, 2))


def check(mechanism: dict[str, tuple[Fraction, int]]) -> tuple[bool, bool, Fraction]:
    """Enumerate every type and every report. Return (incentive compatible, individually rational, revenue).

    mechanism maps each reported type to (probability of trade, payment in cents). Revenue is the prior-weighted
    payment in cents, as an exact Fraction.
    """
    ic = True
    ir = True
    for theta in TYPES:
        truthful = utility(VALUES[theta], *mechanism[theta])
        if truthful < 0:
            ir = False
        for report in TYPES:
            if utility(VALUES[theta], *mechanism[report]) > truthful:
                ic = False
    revenue = sum((PRIOR[theta] * mechanism[theta][1] for theta in TYPES), Fraction(0))
    return ic, ir, revenue


def show(name: str, mechanism: dict[str, tuple[Fraction, int]]) -> None:
    """Print one direct mechanism with every type-report utility and its checks."""
    print(f"mechanism {name}")
    for theta in TYPES:
        for report in TYPES:
            u = utility(VALUES[theta], *mechanism[report])
            print(f"  type {theta} reports {report} utility {u}")
    ic, ir, revenue = check(mechanism)
    print(f"  incentive_compatible {ic} individually_rational {ir} revenue_cents {half_up_cents(revenue)}")


def part_a() -> None:
    """Part A: two feasible menus, one that fails individual rationality, and one that fails incentive compatibility."""
    print("Part A: every report of every type, for four direct mechanisms")
    show("exclude_type_L", {"L": (Fraction(0), 0), "H": (Fraction(1), 10000)})
    show("pool_at_low_value", {"L": (Fraction(1), 6000), "H": (Fraction(1), 6000)})
    show("pool_at_high_value", {"L": (Fraction(1), 10000), "H": (Fraction(1), 10000)})
    show("high_price_for_H_only_trade", {"L": (Fraction(1), 6000), "H": (Fraction(0), 0)})


def part_b() -> None:
    """Part B: the grid over (q_L, q_H) with q_L <= q_H, payments set by the two binding constraints."""
    print("Part B: grid search over allocation pairs, payments leave L with zero utility")
    steps = [Fraction(k, 4) for k in range(5)]
    best: tuple[Fraction, Fraction, Fraction] | None = None
    passing = 0
    for q_low in steps:
        for q_high in steps:
            if q_low > q_high:
                continue
            t_low = int(VALUES["L"] * q_low)
            t_high = int(VALUES["H"] * q_high - (VALUES["H"] - VALUES["L"]) * q_low)
            mechanism = {"L": (q_low, t_low), "H": (q_high, t_high)}
            ic, ir, revenue = check(mechanism)
            if ic and ir:
                passing += 1
                if best is None or revenue > best[2]:
                    best = (q_low, q_high, revenue)
    print(f"grid_points_passing {passing}")
    assert best is not None
    print(f"best_q_low {best[0]} best_q_high {best[1]} best_revenue_cents {half_up_cents(best[2])}")
    print(f"exact_best_revenue {best[2]}")


def part_c() -> None:
    """Part C: a menu with three messages, a best-response strategy, and the induced direct mechanism."""
    print("Part C: the revelation construction on a menu of three messages")
    menu = {
        "low-price": (Fraction(1), 6000),
        "high-price": (Fraction(1), 10000),
        "decline": (Fraction(0), 0),
    }
    order = list(menu)
    strategy: dict[str, str] = {}
    for theta in TYPES:
        best_message = order[0]
        best_utility = utility(VALUES[theta], *menu[best_message])
        for message in order[1:]:
            u = utility(VALUES[theta], *menu[message])
            if u > best_utility:
                best_message, best_utility = message, u
        strategy[theta] = best_message
        print(f"type {theta} chooses {best_message} utility {best_utility}")
    induced = {theta: menu[strategy[theta]] for theta in TYPES}
    ic, ir, revenue = check(induced)
    print(f"induced_direct_mechanism L {induced['L'][0]} {induced['L'][1]} H {induced['H'][0]} {induced['H'][1]}")
    print(f"induced_incentive_compatible {ic} induced_individually_rational {ir}")
    print(f"induced_revenue_cents {half_up_cents(revenue)}")
    alternative = dict(induced)
    alternative["L"] = menu["decline"]
    alt_ic, alt_ir, alt_revenue = check(alternative)
    print(f"tie_break_declines_for_L incentive_compatible {alt_ic} individually_rational {alt_ir}")
    print(f"tie_break_declines_for_L_revenue_cents {half_up_cents(alt_revenue)}")


def main() -> None:
    """Run Parts A, B and C in order."""
    part_a()
    part_b()
    part_c()


main()

The third menu passes IC and fails IR, because type L gets utility -4000 cents, so it is not feasible even though it earns the most (10000 cents). 4Where the canon breaks

  • More than one agent. Proposition 2.2 is proved for one agent with finite sets (Assumption 3). The statement for several agents, where each agent's best reply depends on the others' strategies, is not proved on this page, and no claim about it is made here.
  • Equilibrium choice. The construction in Proposition 2.2 takes as input an equilibrium \(s\), and the proof works for any equilibrium. Lab 1 Part C shows that two equilibria of one menu induce direct menus with different revenues, 6000 and 4200 cents. The revelation principle gives equal outcomes for the chosen equilibrium, and it does not say which equilibrium a buyer will choose.
  • IC without IR. A menu can satisfy every IC condition and still fail IR. Lemma 3.1 keeps both constraints, and Lab 1 Part A prints one such menu.
  • Two types. Proposition 3.2 and Lemma 3.1 are stated for two types. The lesson does not state the analogous constraints or a general menu for more types.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The seller should always sell to every type, because a low-value buyer is still a sale.”

    Proposition 3.2 gives \(q_L = 0\) when \(c < 0\). In Example 3.3, selling only to type H earns \(7000\) cents, and selling to both types at \(6000\) cents earns less (Lab 1, Parts A and B). A sale to type L at a low price lowers the price that type H would pay, because type H can report L (Lemma 3.1).

  2. “Any menu that makes every type prefer truthful reporting is an acceptable design.”

    IC is one condition. The menu that sells to both types at 10000 cents passes IC in Lab 1 Part A, and it fails IR because type L gets \(-4000\) cents. A type that is worse off than its outside option does not take part, so the menu is not feasible.

  3. “The revelation principle says every equilibrium of a mechanism earns the same revenue as its direct version.”

    The direct version reproduces the outcome of the equilibrium used to build it (Proposition 2.2). Two equilibria of the same menu can give different outcomes. Lab 1 Part C prints the induced revenue of 6000 cents for one tie-break and 4200 cents for the other.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · The utility of type H from reporting L

With \(v_H = 10000\) cents, a menu gives report L the outcome \(q_L = 1\) and \(\tau_L = 6000\) cents. What utility does a type H buyer get from reporting L? Does it gain by reporting L, if its own truthful report gives it 4000 cents?

Solution

Type H reporting L gets \(v_H q_L - \tau_L = 10000 \times 1 - 6000 = 4000\) cents. In the menu that sells to both types at 6000 cents (Lab 1, Part A), type H's truthful report also gives \(10000 - 6000 = 4000\) cents. The two utilities are equal, so type H is indifferent and does not gain by reporting L.

M2 · Exclude or pool at p_H = 0.5

Keep \(v_L = 6000\) and \(v_H = 10000\) cents, and set \(p_H = 0.5\). Compute \(c\), say which menu Proposition 3.2 gives, and state its revenue in cents.

Solution

Here \(p_L = 0.5\) and \(\Delta = 4000\). Then \(c = 0.5 \times 6000 - 0.5 \times 4000 = 3000 - 2000 = 1000\), which is positive. Proposition 3.2 gives \(q_L = 1\) and \(q_H = 1\), with \(U_L = 0\). The payments are \(\tau_L = 6000\) and \(\tau_H = 10000 - 4000 = 6000\). Revenue is \(v_L = 6000\) cents. The exclusion menu would earn \(0.5 \times 10000 = 5000\) cents, which is less.

M3 · The revenue of a mixed menu

Take \(q_L = 1/2\) and \(q_H = 1\) with \(p_H = 7/10\), \(v_L = 6000\) and \(v_H = 10000\) cents. Use Lemma 3.1 to set the payments with \(U_L = 0\), and compute the expected revenue in cents.

Solution

With \(U_L = 0\), \(\tau_L = v_L q_L = 3000\). Also \(U_H = \Delta q_L = 4000 \times 1/2 = 2000\), so \(\tau_H = v_H q_H - U_H = 10000 - 2000 = 8000\). Check IC: type H gets \(2000\) truthfully and \(10000 \times 1/2 - 3000 = 2000\) by reporting L, equal. Expected revenue is \(0.3 \times 3000 + 0.7 \times 8000 = 900 + 5600 = 6500\) cents. This agrees with the formula \(p_H v_H q_H + c q_L = 7000 + (-1000)(1/2) = 6500\).

Proof

P1 · Why \(q_L \le q_H\) is necessary

With \(v_H > v_L\), and using only the two IC conditions, show that every IC direct mechanism has \(q_L \le q_H\).

Solution

Add the two IC inequalities. Type H's IC gives \(v_H q_H - \tau_H \ge v_H q_L - \tau_L\), and type L's IC gives \(v_L q_L - \tau_L \ge v_L q_H - \tau_H\). Adding them and cancelling the payments gives \(v_H q_H + v_L q_L \ge v_H q_L + v_L q_H\), so \((v_H - v_L)(q_H - q_L) \ge 0\). Since \(v_H > v_L\), \(q_H \ge q_L\). No IR condition was used.

P2 · The truthful report in the direct mechanism

In Proposition 2.2, let \(s\) be an equilibrium and \(\hat{x}(\theta) = x(s(\theta))\). Show that the outcome of type \(\theta\) under \(\hat{x}\) is the outcome it would get by playing \(s(\theta)\) in the original mechanism, and that a type that reports \(\theta'\) gets the outcome of the message \(s(\theta')\).

Solution

By definition \(\hat{x}(\theta) = x(s(\theta))\), which is the outcome of the message \(s(\theta)\) in \(x\). When type \(\theta\) reports \(\theta'\) in \(\hat{x}\), the outcome is \(\hat{x}(\theta') = x(s(\theta'))\), which is the outcome of the message \(s(\theta')\) in \(x\). This is the step used in the proof, where the equilibrium condition compares these two messages for type \(\theta\).

P3 · The high type's utility when the low type is excluded

In the optimal menu of Proposition 3.2 with \(c < 0\), find \(U_H\), and explain in one sentence what it means for the high type.

Solution

In that menu \(q_L = 0\), so \(U_H = U_L + \Delta q_L = 0 + 0 = 0\). An information rent is the surplus that the designer must leave a type to keep it from copying a lower type's report. The high type gets no surplus: it pays its full value \(v_H = 10000\) cents for the good, so there is no information rent in this menu, and the excluded low type leaves no such surplus to protect.

Empirical

E1 · How many grid points pass

Read Part B of Lab 1. How many grid points pass both checks, and how many grid points with \(q_L \le q_H\) are there in all?

Solution

Part B prints grid_points_passing 15. The grid uses the five values \(0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1\) for each of \(q_L\) and \(q_H\), and the pairs with \(q_L \le q_H\) are 15 in number. So all 15 pass. This matches Lemma 3.1, because the payments built in Part B satisfy the interval of the lemma whenever \(q_L \le q_H\).

E2 · Which menus pass which check

From Part A of Lab 1, list the IC and IR results for the four menus, and say which menu is revenue-maximizing among the two that pass both.

Solution

Exclude type L: IC True, IR True, revenue 7000 cents. Pool at low value: IC True, IR True, revenue 6000 cents. Pool at high value: IC True, IR False, revenue 10000 cents. High price for H only trade: IC False, IR True, revenue 1800 cents. Among the menus that pass both, exclusion gives the larger revenue, 7000 cents against 6000 cents. The menu that fails IR earns more, but it is not feasible.

E3 · The gap from the induced menu to the optimum

Solution

Part C prints induced_revenue_cents 6000 for the low-price tie-break. Part B prints exact_best_revenue 7000. The gap is \(7000 - 6000 = 1000\) cents. The induced menu sells to both types, and the optimum does not sell to type L.

Research

R1 · The decline tie-break

Suppose type L chooses decline in Part C, and type H chooses low-price. Show that the induced direct menu is IC and IR, and compute its revenue in cents. Explain why the proof of Proposition 2.2 does not decide between the two tie-breaks.

Solution

The induced menu gives type L \((q_L, \tau_L) = (0, 0)\) and type H \((q_H, \tau_H) = (1, 6000)\). Type L's utility is 0 truthfully and \(6000 \times 1 - 6000 = 0\) by reporting H, so IC holds for type L, and its utility is 0, so IR holds. Type H's utility is 4000 truthfully and \(10000 \times 0 - 0 = 0\) by reporting L, so IC holds for type H, and 4000 is at least 0, so IR holds. Revenue is \(0.7 \times 6000 = 4200\) cents, which Lab 1 Part C prints. The proof of Proposition 2.2 works for any equilibrium \(s\), so both tie-breaks are equilibria that the proof covers. It does not say which one a type chooses.

R2 · Where \(\Delta > 0\) is used

Identify the step in Lemma 3.1 where \(\Delta > 0\) is used, and say what the interval of the lemma becomes when \(\Delta = 0\).

Solution

The step is the claim that \(\Delta q_L \le \Delta q_H\) implies \(q_L \le q_H\). Dividing by \(\Delta\) needs \(\Delta > 0\). When \(\Delta = 0\), the two IC conditions give \(U_H = U_L\) and place no restriction on \(q_L\) relative to \(q_H\). The condition \(q_L \le q_H\) is then not implied, so the lemma as stated, which assumes \(\Delta > 0\), does not apply to identical types, and this lesson does not treat that case.

R3 · The threshold for exclusion

With \(v_L = 6000\) and \(v_H = 10000\) cents, find the value of \(p_H\) at which \(c = 0\), and state which menu is optimal above and below it.

Solution

Write \(p_L = 1 - p_H\). Then \(c = (1 - p_H) \times 6000 - p_H \times 4000 = 6000 - 10000\,p_H\). The sign of \(c\) is the sign of \(6000 - 10000\,p_H\), so \(c = 0\) at \(p_H = 6000/10000 = 3/5\). Above \(3/5\), \(c < 0\) and exclusion is optimal, with revenue \(10000\,p_H\). Below \(3/5\), \(c > 0\) and selling to both types at 6000 cents is optimal. At \(p_H = 3/5\) both menus earn 6000 cents. Lab 1 uses \(p_H = 7/10\), which is above the threshold.
