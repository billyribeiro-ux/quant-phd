Treasury auction design

A debt issuer sells identical units to bidders in a sealed-bid auction, under one of two payment rules. You will write the engine that computes who wins and what each winner pays, prove the single-unit bid and revenue results that the design question rests on, and recommend a format with the numbers that support the recommendation.

Lab 1 · Worked example: one auction under both formats

Stated before running. The bids are 5200, 4800, 6100 and 3900 cents. The two highest bids win, so bidders 0 and 2 win. The uniform price is the highest losing bid, 4800 cents, so the uniform revenue is \(2 \times 4800 = 9600\) cents. The discriminatory winners pay 5200 and 6100 cents, so the discriminatory revenue is 11300 cents. The gap is 1700 cents, which is 17.7 % of the uniform revenue (1700 divided by 9600).

"""Rung 1 worked example: payments and revenue of a two-unit, four-bidder auction, in integer cents.

SIMULATED: the four bids are typed in for this example. They are illustrative, not generated and not data.
"""


def main() -> None:
    bids = [5200, 4800, 6100, 3900]  # cents; bidder i bids bids[i], for i = 0, 1, 2, 3
    units = 2  # the auction sells two identical units, so 1 <= units < n with n = 4
    n = len(bids)
    # Step 1. Rank the bidders from the highest bid to the lowest. A tie goes to the lower index.
    ranked = sorted(range(n), key=lambda i: (-bids[i], i))
    print("ranking (bidder indices, highest bid first):", ranked)
    # Step 2. The winners are the first `units` bidders in the ranking. Each winner receives one unit.
    winners = sorted(ranked[:units])
    print("winners (bidder indices):", winners)
    # Step 3. Uniform price: every winner pays the (units + 1)-th highest bid, the highest losing bid.
    # Counting from 0, that bid is ranked[units].
    uniform_price = bids[ranked[units]]
    uniform = [uniform_price if i in winners else 0 for i in range(n)]
    # Step 4. Discriminatory: each winner pays its own bid. A loser pays nothing in either format.
    discriminatory = [bids[i] if i in winners else 0 for i in range(n)]
    # Step 5. Revenue is the sum of what the bidders pay, in each format.
    print("uniform price (cents):", uniform_price)
    print("uniform payments (cents):", uniform)
    print("uniform revenue (cents):", sum(uniform))
    print("discriminatory payments (cents):", discriminatory)
    print("discriminatory revenue (cents):", sum(discriminatory))
    gap = sum(discriminatory) - sum(uniform)
    print("discriminatory minus uniform (cents):", gap)
    print(f"gap as a share of the uniform revenue: {100 * gap / sum(uniform):.1f} %")

Rung 3 · Specification

Context
A debt issuer sells identical units, units of them, to n bidders in a sealed-bid auction, and each bidder wants one unit. The payment rule can change what the winners pay, the revenue, the shading of the bids below value and the allocative efficiency of the result. The design question is which format to recommend, and how far the answer depends on the model. This page makes no claim about the rules of any real auction, and it uses no real data.
Inputs
No data file. The autograder's instance comes from grader/instance.py, seeded from your workspace ID. It holds the bidder count n (2 to 5), the unit count units with 1 <= units < n, and the bids and values in integer cents. Every generated instance is SIMULATED, and none of it is a real bid or a real price. The reference case of Lab 3 is typed in and illustrative.
Required outputs
In a repository of your own: auction_sim.py at its root, with the five functions of the interface below, and beside it three files. run_report.py is Lab 3 with its engine calls replaced by calls to auction_sim, and it prints the same lines. memo.md is the design memo described in the brief, with its appendix of proofs. self_assessment.md is the summary that the form in the self-assessment section produces. Start from the starter file, whose signatures are complete. 
Interface (signatures only, not runnable):
allocate(bids: list[int], units: int) -> list[bool]
payments(format: str, bids: list[int], units: int) -> list[int]
revenue(format: str, bids: list[int], units: int) -> int
efficiency(values: list[int], bids: list[int], units: int) -> tuple[int, int]
symmetric_bid_single_unit(format: str, n: int, value: int) -> int
Constraints
Standard library only. No network, no file read, and no printing or warning in the module. Money is integer cents: each money output is an int. The symmetric bid is computed from an exact rational number and rounded once, to the nearest cent, with a half cent going up (the rounding rule of the conventions). Four conditions raise ValueError, and the convention names each of them. Any other invalid input may raise any exception or return a value, and the suite does not require a type for it.
Grading mode
Both the autograder and the rubric apply. The code is autograded by grader/test_project.py, which runs in your browser and on your machine with warnings as errors. It checks the bid and the payment rules against an oracle written separately from the reference solution, on the seeded instance and on further seeds, together with the tie rule, the exception table and the integer outputs. Its integrity rests on the seeded instances, not on secrecy. The memo is graded by the six-criterion rubric in the rubric, each criterion with four observable levels. You score it yourself with the form in the self-assessment section, and you calibrate on two annotated exemplars. The memo meets the bar at level 3 or above on each criterion.

Conventions

  • Bids and values. A bid and a value are non-negative integers in cents. Bidder i is indexed from 0 to n - 1. A bid is the amount the bidder names; a value is what the unit is worth to that bidder.
  • Winners. allocate awards the units highest bids. A tie goes to the lower bidder index. The ranking is the list of indices from the highest bid to the lowest, with ties broken by the lower index.
  • Uniform price. Each winner pays the (units + 1)-th highest bid, which is the highest losing bid. With 0-based indexing it is ranked[units]. A loser pays 0.
  • Discriminatory price. Each winner pays its own bid. A loser pays 0.
  • Revenue is the sum of the payments, in cents. Efficiency is the pair (value of the winners, value of the units highest values), both in cents. The share of efficiency is the first entry divided by the second.
  • The symmetric bid, one unit. symmetric_bid_single_unit("discriminatory", n, value) is \((n-1)\,v/n\), the first-price symmetric bid of the derivations, rounded half up to the cent: \(\lfloor (n-1)v/n + 1/2 \rfloor\) in integer arithmetic. symmetric_bid_single_unit("uniform", n, value) is the value itself, because with one unit the uniform price is the second-highest bid, and Proposition A2 shows that the value is a weakly dominant bid. The function requires \(n \ge 2\).
  • Exception table. Four conditions raise ValueError. A format that is not "uniform" or "discriminatory" raises it, and the message names both allowed names. Units below 1, or at least n, raise it with the message units must satisfy 1 <= units < n. A negative bid or value raises it, and the message names that condition. In symmetric_bid_single_unit, n < 2 raises it with the message n must be at least 2.

The model and the results it rests on

The engine computes outcomes for given bids. The single-unit bid and the revenue results need a model of values and strategies. The model is stated here so that each result has its assumptions in front of it.

  1. Assumption 1 (risk neutrality). A bidder who wins one unit at price \(p\) has payoff \(v - p\), and a bidder who loses has payoff 0.
  2. Assumption 2 (independent private values). The values \(V_1, \dots, V_n\) are independent and each is uniform on \([0,1]\). In cents the values are uniform on \([0, 10000]\), so the value cap is 10000 cents; the proofs below use the unit interval, and a result scales with the cap: a bid in cents is 10000 times the bid on \([0,1]\).
  3. Assumption 3 (symmetry). All bidders use the same strategy, a function \(\beta\) that maps a value to a bid.
  4. Assumption 4 (one unit). There are \(n \ge 2\) bidders and one unit, so \(k = 1\), and each bidder wants one unit.
  5. Assumption 5 (no ties). Under continuous values, two bids are equal with probability 0, so the winner is almost surely the unique highest bidder. Assumption 5 is used in the first-price argument of Proposition A1 and in the revenue identities of Proposition A3.

A bidder's strategy is a best response when no other bid gives a higher expected payoff. The symmetric equilibrium of this section is the profile in which every bidder plays \(\beta\) and \(\beta\) is a best response to itself. It is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium, the concept of Lesson 2.

Proposition A1 · First-price symmetric bid Under Assumptions 1 to 5 (one unit, so \(k = 1\)), with \(n \ge 2\), the function \(\beta(v) = \frac{n-1}{n}\,v\) is a symmetric equilibrium of the first-price auction: \(\beta\) is a best response to the others playing \(\beta\). Its value is \(\beta(v) = cv\) with \(c = (n-1)/n\), and the shading \(v - \beta(v) = v/n\) is strictly positive for every \(v > 0\).

Proof. Let a bidder with value \(v\) bid \(b\), and let the other \(n-1\) bidders bid \(\beta(V_j) = cV_j\). Since \(\beta\) is strictly increasing, the bid \(b \le c\) wins exactly when \(cV_j < b\) for every rival \(j\), that is, when \(V_j < b/c\) for every rival. By Assumption 2 the probability of this is \(\min(b/c, 1)^{n-1}\), and by Assumption 5 ties do not change it. So the expected payoff of the bid is \(\pi(b) = (v-b)\,(b/c)^{n-1}\) for \(0 \le b \le c\). For \(b > c\) the bid wins with probability 1 and \(\pi(b) = v - b < v - c = \pi(c)\), so no bid above \(c\) does better than \(c\), and \(c\) does no better than the maximiser found below. On \((0, c)\) the derivative is \[ \pi'(b) = \frac{b^{n-2}}{c^{\,n-1}}\bigl[(n-1)(v-b) - b\bigr] = \frac{b^{n-2}}{c^{\,n-1}}\bigl[(n-1)v - nb\bigr]. \] For \(n = 2\) the factor \(b^{n-2}\) equals 1. The bracket is positive for \(b < (n-1)v/n\) and negative for \(b > (n-1)v/n\). So \(\pi\) rises, then falls, and its unique maximum on \([0, c]\) is at \(b^* = cv\). Since \(v \le 1\) gives \(cv \le c\), the maximum lies in the range. Hence \(\beta(v) = cv\) is a best response to \(\beta\). For \(v = 0\) every bid pays at most 0 and \(\beta(0) = 0\). The shading is \(v - cv = v/n\), which is positive for \(v > 0\) and finite \(n\). \(\blacksquare\)

The derivative identity is the first-order condition: setting \(\pi'(b) = 0\) gives \((n-1)(v-b) = b\), so \(b = (n-1)v/n\). The sign argument is what shows that this stationary point is the maximum and not a minimum.

Proposition A2 · Second-price bidding with one unit Under Assumptions 1, 2 and 4, with \(n \ge 2\), in the uniform-price auction with one unit a bid of the value \(v\) is weakly dominant: for every bid \(b\) and every rival profile, bidding \(v\) gives a payoff at least as high as bidding \(b\). Hence the symmetric bid for symmetric_bid_single_unit("uniform", n, value) is \(v\).

Proof. Let \(M\) be the highest bid of the rivals. If the bidder wins, the uniform price is the highest losing bid, which is \(M\), so the payoff is \(v - M\). If the bidder loses, the payoff is 0. The bidder wins with bid \(b\) when \(b > M\), and also when \(b = M\) and the bidder has the lower index. Compare truthful \(b = v\) with any \(b\). If \(M < v\), truthful bidding wins with payoff \(v - M > 0\), and no bid does better, because every winning bid pays \(M\). If \(M > v\), truthful bidding loses with payoff 0, while a bid \(b > M\) wins with payoff \(v - M < 0\), which is worse. If \(M = v\), the truthful payoff is 0, and any winning bid also has payoff \(v - M = 0\), so no bid does better. In every case truthful bidding is at least as good. A weakly dominant bid is a best response to every profile, so the profile in which all bidders bid their values is an equilibrium. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition A3 · Expected revenue with one unit Under Assumptions 1 to 5 (one unit, so \(k = 1\)), with \(n \ge 2\), the expected revenue of the first-price auction and of the second-price (uniform) auction, both with the symmetric bid of Propositions A1 and A2, are equal: \(\E[R_{\text{first}}] = \E[R_{\text{second}}] = (n-1)/(n+1)\), as a share of the value cap.

Proof. Let \(V_{(1)} \ge V_{(2)}\) be the two highest of the \(n\) values. For \(x \in [0,1]\), \(V_{(1)} > x\) holds when at least one value exceeds \(x\), so \(P(V_{(1)} > x) = 1 - x^n\). The integral is \(\E[V_{(1)}] = \int_0^1 (1 - x^n)\,dx = 1 - \frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{n}{n+1}\). Next, \(V_{(2)} > x\) holds when at least two values exceed \(x\), so \(P(V_{(2)} > x) = 1 - x^n - n(1-x)x^{n-1}\), and \(\int_0^1 n(1-x)x^{n-1}\,dx = 1 - \frac{n}{n+1} = \frac{1}{n+1}\). Therefore \(\E[V_{(2)}] = 1 - \frac{1}{n+1} - \frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{n-1}{n+1}\). In the second-price auction the winner is the highest bidder, and it pays the highest losing bid, which is \(V_{(2)}\) because each bidder bids its value. So \(\E[R_{\text{second}}] = \E[V_{(2)}] = (n-1)/(n+1)\). In the first-price auction the winner is the highest bidder, which holds the highest value because \(\beta\) is increasing, and it pays \(\beta(V_{(1)}) = \frac{n-1}{n} V_{(1)}\). So \(\E[R_{\text{first}}] = \frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{n+1} = \frac{n-1}{n+1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

This is the revenue-equivalence result for this model: the two formats have the same expected revenue. The proof above is self-contained, and the page cites no source for it.

Lab 3 · The rung 3 check: the reference case and the single-unit checks

The reference case has five bidders and three units, with bids of 7400, 6200, 8100, 6200 and 5300 cents, and values of 6000, 7800, 9000, 6900 and 5000 cents. The tie at 6200 is between bidders 1 and 3, and the lower index wins it. Stated before running: the winners are bidders 0, 1 and 2; the uniform payments are 6200 cents each, a revenue of 18600 cents; the discriminatory revenue is 21700 cents; the gap is 3100 cents, which is 16.7 % of the uniform revenue (3100 divided by 18600); and the winners' value is 22800 cents against 23700 cents for the three highest values, a share of 96.2 %. For a single unit at value 7300 cents, the closed-form first-price bid, \((n-1)\cdot 7300/n\) rounded half up, equals the integer bid that maximises the expected payoff over the grid of cent bids, for \(n = 2\) to 5. Last, for one unit, the exact expected revenues of the two formats are equal for each \(n\) from 2 to 5.

"""Rung 3 check: the reference engine on a reference case, and the exact single-unit checks.

Standard library only. Model: n bidders with independent private values, each uniform on the cent grid [0, 10000],
risk neutral, each wanting one unit. The bids and values of the reference case are typed in for this check. They are
illustrative, not generated and not data.

Copy this file into run_report.py beside auction_sim.py, then replace the calls to these functions with calls
to auction_sim.
"""

from fractions import Fraction
from math import comb, factorial

CAP = 10000  # the value cap, in cents: a value is uniform on [0, CAP]


def ranking(bids: list[int]) -> list[int]:
    """Bidder indices from the highest bid to the lowest; a tie goes to the lower index."""
    return sorted(range(len(bids)), key=lambda i: (-bids[i], i))


def allocate(bids: list[int], units: int) -> list[bool]:
    """True for each of the `units` highest bidders."""
    winners = set(ranking(bids)[:units])
    return [i in winners for i in range(len(bids))]


def payments(format_name: str, bids: list[int], units: int) -> list[int]:
    """Cents paid by each bidder: a winner pays the uniform price or its own bid; a loser pays 0."""
    won = allocate(bids, units)
    clearing = bids[ranking(bids)[units]]
    if format_name == "uniform":
        return [clearing if w else 0 for w in won]
    return [bid if w else 0 for bid, w in zip(bids, won, strict=True)]


def revenue(format_name: str, bids: list[int], units: int) -> int:
    """The sum of the payments, in cents."""
    return sum(payments(format_name, bids, units))


def efficiency(values: list[int], bids: list[int], units: int) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """The value of the winners, and the value of the `units` highest values, in cents."""
    won = allocate(bids, units)
    achieved = sum(v for v, w in zip(values, won, strict=True) if w)
    best = sum(sorted(values, reverse=True)[:units])
    return achieved, best


def symmetric_bid_single_unit(format_name: str, n: int, value: int) -> int:
    """The symmetric equilibrium bid with one unit, in cents: (n-1)/n of the value, or the value under uniform."""
    if format_name == "uniform":
        return value
    return (2 * (n - 1) * value + n) // (2 * n)  # (n-1)*value/n rounded half up, in integers


def beta_integral(a: int, b: int) -> Fraction:
    """The exact integral of x**a * (1-x)**b over [0, 1], which is a! b! / (a+b+1)!."""
    return Fraction(factorial(a) * factorial(b), factorial(a + b + 1))


def expected_order_statistic(n: int, j: int) -> Fraction:
    """E[V_(j)] for the j-th highest of n independent uniform[0,1] values, as the integral of P(V_(j) > x) over [0, 1].

    V_(j) exceeds x when at least j values exceed x, and m values exceed x with probability C(n,m) (1-x)^m x^(n-m).
    """
    return sum((comb(n, m) * beta_integral(n - m, m) for m in range(j, n + 1)), Fraction(0))


def first_price_payoff(n: int, value: int, bid: int) -> Fraction:
    """Expected payoff of a bid against n-1 rivals who bid (n-1)/n of their values, with values uniform on [0, CAP].

    A rival's bid is below `bid` when its value is below bid*n/(n-1), so the bid wins with that
    probability to the power n-1.
    """
    cutoff = min(Fraction(bid * n, n - 1), Fraction(CAP))
    return (value - bid) * (cutoff / CAP) ** (n - 1)


def main() -> None:
    bids = [7400, 6200, 8100, 6200, 5300]  # cents; the tie at 6200 is between bidders 1 and 3
    values = [6000, 7800, 9000, 6900, 5000]  # cents, the values behind the bids
    units = 3
    won = allocate(bids, units)
    print("ranking (bidder indices):", ranking(bids))
    print("winners (bidder indices):", [i for i, w in enumerate(won) if w])
    uniform_revenue = revenue("uniform", bids, units)
    print("uniform payments (cents):", payments("uniform", bids, units), "| revenue:", uniform_revenue)
    print(
        "discriminatory payments (cents):",
        payments("discriminatory", bids, units),
        "| revenue:",
        revenue("discriminatory", bids, units),
    )
    gap = revenue("discriminatory", bids, units) - uniform_revenue
    print(
        f"discriminatory minus uniform (cents): {gap} | share of the uniform revenue: "
        f"{100 * gap / uniform_revenue:.1f} %"
    )
    achieved, best = efficiency(values, bids, units)
    print("value of the winners (cents):", achieved, "| value of the 3 highest values (cents):", best)
    print(f"allocative efficiency, winners' value as a share of the best: {100 * achieved / best:.1f} %")
    print("single unit, first price, value 7300 cents: closed form, grid best bid, agree")
    for n in range(2, 6):
        closed = symmetric_bid_single_unit("discriminatory", n, 7300)
        grid = max(range(CAP + 1), key=lambda b: first_price_payoff(n, 7300, b))
        print(f"n = {n}: bid {closed} | grid best bid {grid} | agree: {closed == grid}")
    print("one unit, exact expected revenue as a share of the value cap (values uniform on [0, 1]):")
    for n in range(2, 6):
        first = Fraction(n - 1, n) * expected_order_statistic(n, 1)
        second = expected_order_statistic(n, 2)
        print(f"n = {n}: first price {first} | second price {second} | equal: {first == second}")

The reference bids are typed in, so the printed revenues and efficiency are the outcome of these bids and not expectations. The grid check is at one value, 7300 cents, and it finds the closed-form bid for each \(n\) from 2 to 5; it does not test other values, and it does not show that the bid is the equilibrium of a game with other strategies. The expected revenues are exact fractions of the value cap, and they agree between the formats for each \(n\) from 2 to 5. Those are the one-unit cases that Proposition A3 covers for every \(n \ge 2\). The lab does not test the module; the suite does.

The brief

Write for a reader who knows the model above and has worked Lessons 8 and 9, not for a referee of this case. Use these six sections, in this order and under these headings, followed by the appendix.

  1. Summary: the case, the two revenues and the efficiency share, and the recommendation, in at most 80 words.
  2. Model and rules: the formats, the bidder count and the unit count, the value distribution, the payment rules, the tie rule, and Assumptions 1 to 5 with their scope.
  3. Single-unit bids: the first-order condition, the bid \((n-1)v/n\) for the stated \(n\), the check of that bid against the grid, and the second-price bid of the uniform format.
  4. Revenue, shading and efficiency: the reference case under both formats in cents, the gap with its base, the shading, the efficiency share with its basis, and the expected revenues from Proposition A3.
  5. Common value and the winner's curse: a signal model in which the value is common and the bidders' signals are private, and the expected overpayment of a winner from a seeded simulation of your own, labelled SIMULATED, with its seed and the number of auctions. The term is defined in Lesson 8.
  6. Recommendation and limits: the format you recommend or the statement that neither is recommended, the number that drives the choice, and the limits of the model.

Appendix A · Proofs. Three results, each stated with its assumptions and proved in your own words: A1, the first-price symmetric bid of Proposition A1; A2, the weak dominance of the value in the uniform-price auction with one unit; A3, the equal expected revenues of Proposition A3. A proof may use an earlier result of the appendix.

Length. The body (the six sections) runs 500 to 1000 words. The appendix has no limit. A word is a run of characters between spaces.

Numbers. Every number the memo quotes is printed by run_report.py, with its unit: cents, a share, or an exact fraction. A cent amount is an integer, and you say so when you quote one. A share is quoted to one decimal place with its base stated.

Scoring. Each criterion scores the highest level whose each element the memo shows. Each level contains the one below it: level 4 is level 3 plus the elements its cell names; level 2 is any attempt that misses an element of level 3 or makes an error its cell names; level 1 is the absence its cell names. The memo meets the bar at level 3 or above on each criterion.

Exemplar A

Official scoring: C1 4 · C2 4 · C3 3 · C4 3 · C5 3 · C6 2. The model, the bid and the one-unit revenue results are proved with their assumptions. Every number is printed by Lab 3 with its unit. Summary

The reference case has five bidders and three units. The uniform price is 6200 cents, so the uniform revenue is 18600 cents. The discriminatory revenue is 21700 cents, which is 3100 cents or 16.7 % above the uniform revenue. On these fixed bids the discriminatory revenue is the higher of the two. The expected revenue for three units is not computed here, so the memo recommends neither format on revenue alone.

  • Meets C3 level 3: the gap is stated with its sign and its base, and only figures that Lab 3 prints are quoted.
  • Misses C3 level 4: the expected revenue of the three-unit reference case is not stated. Proposition A3 covers one unit (Assumption 4), and the memo gives expected revenue only for one unit.
Model and rules

Five bidders and three units. Under the uniform price each winner pays the highest losing bid, \(ranked[3]\), and under the discriminatory price each winner pays its own bid. A tie goes to the lower index. Values are uniform on \([0,10000]\) cents. Assumptions 1 to 5 are the model of the project page: risk neutrality, independence, symmetry, one unit in the proofs, and no ties under continuous values.

  • Meets C1 level 4: gives both payment rules with the clearing rank written out, and the tie rule.
Single-unit bids

Let \(c = (n-1)/n\). With the rivals bidding \(cV_j\), the bid \(b\) wins with probability \((b/c)^{n-1}\), and the payoff \((v-b)(b/c)^{n-1}\) has the derivative \(\frac{b^{n-2}}{c^{n-1}}[(n-1)v - nb]\). It is positive below \(cv\) and negative above it, so the bid is \((n-1)v/n\). At value 7300 cents, Lab 3 prints 3650, 4867, 5475 and 5840 cents for \(n = 2\) to 5, and the grid of integer cent bids gives the same four bids. Each bid is the closed form rounded half up to the cent. The grid check is at one value, so it does not show the bid for other values; Proposition A1 does.

  • Meets C2 level 4: derives the bid and checks it against the grid, and states what the grid check does not show.
Revenue, shading and efficiency

The reference bids give winners' payments of 6200, 6200 and 6200 cents under the uniform price, a revenue of 18600 cents. Under the discriminatory price the payments are 7400, 6200 and 8100 cents, a revenue of 21700 cents. The gap of 3100 cents is 16.7 % of the uniform revenue. The winners' value is 22800 cents, against 23700 cents for the three highest values, so the efficiency share is 96.2 %. The exact expected revenues for one unit, as shares of the value cap, are 1/2 for \(n = 3\) and 2/3 for \(n = 5\) in both formats.

  • Meets C3 level 3: reports both revenues in cents, the gap with its base, and quotes only figures that Lab 3 prints.
  • Meets C5 level 3: computes the efficiency share in cents with its basis.
Common value and the winner's curse

The memo takes a signal model in which the value is common to the bidders, and each bidder observes a private signal of it. A high bid is a sign that the bidder's signal is high, so the winner's estimate of the value is too high on average, and the winner pays more than the value it will realise. The memo does not give the size of the overpayment.

  • Meets C4 level 3: names the signal model and states how a high bid changes the estimate of the value.
  • Misses C4 level 4: gives no overpayment from a seeded simulation, with its seed and number of auctions, so the sign is asserted and not computed.
Recommendation and limits

The memo recommends neither format on revenue alone. The reference case has three units, and Proposition A3 covers one unit only (Assumption 4), so the expected revenue gap for three units is not computed here. That gap is the number that drives the choice, and this memo does not claim that it is zero. The 3100-cent gap of the reference run is a difference between two sets of payments on fixed bids, not an expected difference between formats. On these fixed bids, the efficiency share is 96.2 % under both formats, because allocate depends on the bids and not on the payment rule. If the bidders' values were asymmetric, Proposition A3 would not apply, and the expected gap would have to be computed for that model; that change is a stated hypothesis, not a computed effect. The limits are risk neutrality, the independence of values, symmetry, the one-unit proofs, and the absence of real data. The memo computes none of the effects of the first three.

  • Meets C5 level 3: recommends neither format, computes the reference efficiency share with its base, and names the number that drives the choice, the expected revenue gap for three units, which the memo does not compute.
  • Misses C6 level 3: risk neutrality, the independence of values, and the absence of real data are named, but the memo does not say what would change without them. It says only that it computes none of the effects of the first three.
Appendix A · Proofs

A1. Assume Assumptions 1 to 5 with \(n \ge 2\). The payoff \(\pi(b) = (v-b)(b/c)^{n-1}\) on \([0,c]\) has derivative \(\frac{b^{n-2}}{c^{n-1}}[(n-1)v - nb]\), which is positive below \(cv\) and negative above it. So the unique maximum on \([0,c]\) is \(cv\), and bids above \(c\) pay less than \(c\). Hence \(\beta(v) = cv\) is a best response to \(\beta\).

A3. The integrals \(\int_0^1 (1-x^n)\,dx = n/(n+1)\) and \(\int_0^1 [1 - x^n - n(1-x)x^{n-1}]\,dx = (n-1)/(n+1)\) give the two expected order statistics, and the first-price revenue is \(\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{n+1}\).

  • Meets C1 level 4: states the assumptions that A1 and A3 use.
  • Meets C2 level 4: derives the bid from the sign of the derivative, step by step.
