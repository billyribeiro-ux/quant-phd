Moral hazard

  • Bpublished, not settled

A principal pays an agent for an outcome it can see, while the agent chooses an effort it cannot see. Pay tied to output gives the agent a reason to work, but it also makes the agent bear risk, pays for luck, and can leave the principal paying more than the effort is worth. This lesson defines moral hazard, derives the best contract when the agent is risk neutral and protected by limited liability, and checks the optimum on an exact grid in cents.

1An unobserved effort and a payment on output

Money is in integer cents, with 1 CU = 100 cents. The output is \(X\) cents, and it takes one of two values: \(0\) on failure and \(\bar{X} = 1{,}000{,}000\) cents on success. The agent chooses an effort \(a \in \{H, L\}\) that the principal cannot observe. High effort raises the probability of success to \(3/4\), and low effort gives success probability \(1/4\). High effort costs the agent \(c_H = 100{,}000\) cents, and low effort costs \(c_L = 0\).

Definition 1.1 (moral hazard)

Moral hazard is a contracting problem in which the principal pays for an outcome that depends on an agent's action, and the action is not observable to the principal. The contract can depend on the outcome but not directly on the action. The lesson uses "moral hazard" only in this sense.

Let \(p_a\) be the probability of success under action \(a\). The expected output is \(E_H = \tfrac{3}{4} \cdot 1{,}000{,}000 = 750{,}000\) cents under high effort and \(E_L = \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 1{,}000{,}000 = 250{,}000\) cents under low effort. The gap is \(\Delta_E = E_H - E_L = 500{,}000\) cents. The first-best profit is the principal's profit when effort is observable and the agent is paid exactly its cost \(c_H\) for high effort, so it is \(E_H - c_H = 650{,}000\) cents.

Assumption 1 (risk-neutral agent)

The agent's utility from a payment \(w\) in cents and an action \(a\) is \(w - c_a\). The agent compares expected payments net of cost, so it is indifferent to risk.

Assumption 2 (outside option)

If the agent refuses the contract, its utility is 0 and the principal's profit is 0.

Assumption 3 (limited liability)

The payment is nonnegative on both outcomes: \(w(0) \ge 0\) and \(w(\bar{X}) \ge 0\), in cents.

Assumption 4 (tie-breaking)

When the agent is indifferent between two actions, it takes high effort.

Lemma 1.2 (the contract is affine in output)

Let a contract pay \(w(0)\) on failure and \(w(\bar{X})\) on success. Put \(\alpha = w(0)\) and \(\beta = (w(\bar{X}) - w(0)) / \bar{X}\). Then the payment is \(\alpha + \beta x\) at output \(x \in \{0, \bar{X}\}\). The expected payment under action \(a\) is \(\alpha + \beta E_a\).

Proof

At \(x = 0\), \(\alpha + \beta \cdot 0 = w(0)\). At \(x = \bar{X}\), \(\alpha + \beta \bar{X} = w(0) + w(\bar{X}) - w(0) = w(\bar{X})\). The expected payment is \(\Prob(\text{success} \mid a) \cdot (\alpha + \beta \bar{X}) + \Prob(\text{failure} \mid a) \cdot \alpha = \alpha + \beta E_a\), since \(E_a = \Prob(\text{success} \mid a) \bar{X}\). \(\square\)

Lemma 1.2 says that a linear contract \(\alpha + \beta x\) is the general contract in this two-outcome model. The share \(\beta\) of output is the slope, and \(\alpha\) is the fixed amount.

Proposition 1.3 (the optimal contract under limited liability)

Under Assumptions 1 to 4, the principal's expected profit \(E_{\text{profit}} = E_a - \text{expected payment}\) is largest at the contract \(\alpha = 0\), \(\beta = 1/5\). At this contract the agent takes high effort, its expected utility is 50,000 cents, and the principal's expected profit is 600,000 cents. No other contract attains this profit.

Proof

Incentive constraint. Under \((\alpha, \beta)\) the agent's utility is \(\alpha + \beta E_H - c_H\) from high effort and \(\alpha + \beta E_L\) from low effort. Given that the agent accepts, high effort is chosen (Assumption 4) exactly when \(\alpha + \beta E_H - c_H \ge \alpha + \beta E_L\), that is, when \(\beta \Delta_E \ge c_H\), that is, when \(\beta \ge c_H / \Delta_E = 1/5\). The constant \(\alpha\) cancels.

Bound for high effort. Suppose the agent takes high effort. Then \(\beta \ge 1/5\) by the incentive constraint, and \(\alpha \ge 0\) by Assumption 3. The principal's profit is \(E_H - (\alpha + \beta E_H) \le E_H(1 - \beta) \le E_H \cdot 4/5 = 600{,}000\). Equality needs \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\beta = 1/5\).

Attainment. At \(\alpha = 0\), \(\beta = 1/5\), the incentive constraint holds with equality (a tie, so high effort is taken by Assumption 4). The agent's utility is \(\tfrac{1}{5} \cdot 750{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 50{,}000\) cents, which is at least 0, so participation holds. The payments \(w(0) = 0\) and \(w(\bar{X}) = 200{,}000\) cents are nonnegative. The principal's profit is \(750{,}000 - 150{,}000 = 600{,}000\) cents.

Low effort. Suppose the agent takes low effort. Its expected payment \(\alpha + \beta E_L\) is a weighted average of \(w(0)\) and \(w(\bar{X})\) with weights \(3/4\) and \(1/4\), so it is nonnegative by Assumption 3. The principal's profit is at most \(E_L = 250{,}000\) cents, which is less than 600,000 cents.

Refusal. If the agent refuses, the principal's profit is 0 by Assumption 2, which is less than 600,000 cents. \(\square\)

Lab 1 · The linear contract on an exact grid of shares

The inputs are illustrative, not data: \(\bar{X} = 1{,}000{,}000\) cents, success probabilities \(3/4\) and \(1/4\), and a high-effort cost of 100,000 cents. Part A prints five shares from the grid of 101 shares 0.00 to 1.00, with \(\alpha = 0\), the agent's utility under each action in cents, the action the agent takes, and the principal's profit in cents. Part B searches all 101 grid shares for the best one. Part C sets \(\alpha\) so that participation binds, allowing negative payments, and shows the profit. Proposition 1.3 predicts that the best share is 0.20, with a profit of 600,000 cents. Remark 1.4 predicts that Part C reaches the first-best profit of 650,000 cents.

"""E2 L06 lab 1: a two-action effort problem with a linear contract under limited liability.

Inputs are illustrative, not data. The output is X = 1,000,000 cents (10,000 CU) on success and 0 on failure.
Success has probability 3/4 under high effort and 1/4 under low effort. High effort costs 100,000 cents (1,000 CU)
and low effort costs 0. The agent is risk neutral with quasi-linear utility, and its outside option is 0.
The contract pays the agent a fixed amount alpha and a share beta of the output on success, so the payment is
alpha on failure and alpha + beta * X on success.

Part A prints five illustrative shares from the grid of 101 shares 0.00 to 1.00 in steps of 0.01. Each row uses
alpha = 0 (limited liability, Assumption 3 on the page) and gives the agent's utility under each action, the action
the agent takes (high effort on a tie), and the principal's expected profit in cents.
Part B searches all 101 grid shares for the best one, and prints its profit beside the first-best profit.
Part C: risk neutrality without limited liability. For three shares, alpha is set to the value that makes the
participation constraint bind, which can be negative, and the profit is printed. It equals the first-best profit.

Probabilities and expectations are exact Fractions. Money is computed exactly and printed in integer cents.
No random draws are used, so every run prints the same lines.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction

OUTPUT = 1_000_000  # cents on success
P_HIGH_EFFORT = Fraction(3, 4)  # probability of success under high effort
P_LOW_EFFORT = Fraction(1, 4)  # probability of success under low effort
COST_HIGH = 100_000  # cents, cost of high effort to the agent
COST_LOW = 0  # cents, cost of low effort to the agent

E_HIGH = P_HIGH_EFFORT * OUTPUT  # expected output under high effort, in cents
E_LOW = P_LOW_EFFORT * OUTPUT  # expected output under low effort, in cents
FIRST_BEST = E_HIGH - COST_HIGH  # principal's profit when effort is observable and the agent is paid its cost


def cents(x: Fraction) -> int:
    """Round an exact amount half up to whole cents."""
    return math.floor(x + Fraction(1, 2))


def agent_choice(beta: Fraction) -> tuple[str, Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Return the action taken, and the utility under high effort and under low effort, at alpha = 0."""
    u_high = beta * E_HIGH - COST_HIGH
    u_low = beta * E_LOW - COST_LOW
    if u_high >= u_low and u_high >= 0:
        return "high", u_high, u_low
    if u_low >= 0:
        return "low", u_high, u_low
    return "refuses", u_high, u_low


def principal_profit(beta: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Principal's expected profit in cents at alpha = 0 and share beta, given the agent's choice."""
    action, _, _ = agent_choice(beta)
    if action == "high":
        return E_HIGH - beta * E_HIGH
    if action == "low":
        return E_LOW - beta * E_LOW
    return Fraction(0)


def main() -> None:
    print("Part A: contracts with alpha = 0, share beta from 0.00 to 1.00 (utilities and profit in cents)")
    print("beta | agent utility, high | agent utility, low | agent takes | principal profit")
    grid = [Fraction(k, 100) for k in range(0, 101)]
    for beta in grid:
        if beta not in (Fraction(0), Fraction(1, 10), Fraction(1, 5), Fraction(3, 10), Fraction(1)):
            continue
        action, u_high, u_low = agent_choice(beta)
        print(
            f"{float(beta):.2f} | {cents(u_high):19d} | {cents(u_low):18d} | {action:11s} | "
            f"{cents(principal_profit(beta)):16d}"
        )

    best = max(grid, key=lambda b: (principal_profit(b), -b))
    best_action, _, _ = agent_choice(best)
    print("Part B: the best grid share")
    print(f"best share on the grid: {float(best):.2f}, agent takes {best_action} effort")
    print(f"principal profit at the best share, in cents: {cents(principal_profit(best))}")
    print(f"first-best profit, in cents: {cents(FIRST_BEST)}")
    print(f"agency loss, first best minus best share, in cents: {cents(FIRST_BEST - principal_profit(best))}")
    print(f"low-effort profit at share 0.00, in cents: {cents(principal_profit(Fraction(0)))}")

    print("Part C: risk neutral agent, alpha may be negative (no limited liability)")
    for beta in (Fraction(1, 5), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1)):
        alpha = COST_HIGH - beta * E_HIGH
        profit = E_HIGH - (alpha + beta * E_HIGH)
        print(f"beta = {float(beta):.2f}: alpha = {cents(alpha)} cents, principal profit = {cents(profit)} cents")
    for beta in (Fraction(1, 5), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1)):
        alpha = COST_HIGH - beta * E_HIGH
        profit = E_HIGH - (alpha + beta * E_HIGH)
        print(f"beta = {float(beta):.2f}: profit equals first-best profit: {profit == FIRST_BEST}")

Part A shows that at share 0.20 the agent's utility is 50,000 cents under both actions, so the tie goes to high effort, and that at share 0.30 the principal's profit is 525,000 cents, below the 600,000 cents at share 0.20. Part B confirms that 0.20 is the best of the 101 grid shares, and Part C shows that without limited liability the principal reaches 650,000 cents at each of the three shares it prints. The lab checks grid shares and the closed form of Remark 1.4 at those shares, and Proposition 1.3 covers every contract that satisfies Assumptions 1 to 4. The lab does not model risk aversion.

Remark 1.4 (no limited liability removes the loss)

Without Assumption 3, set \(\alpha = c_H - \beta E_H\) for any \(\beta \ge 1/5\). The participation constraint then binds, the expected payment is \(c_H\), and the principal's profit is \(E_H - c_H = 650{,}000\) cents, which is the first-best profit. Part C of Lab 1 checks this for \(\beta = 0.20, 0.50, 1.00\). The payments can be negative, so the agent pays a fixed amount on failure, which Assumption 3 rules out.

2The agency cost and the limits of the model

Remark 2.1 (the agency cost)

The first-best profit is 650,000 cents and the limited-liability optimum is 600,000 cents, so the agency cost is 50,000 cents. At the optimum the agent's expected utility is also 50,000 cents. This is an identity. For any contract under which the agent takes high effort, the principal's profit plus the agent's utility is \((E_H - \alpha - \beta E_H) + (\alpha + \beta E_H - c_H) = E_H - c_H\). Here \(600{,}000 + 50{,}000 = 650{,}000\) cents, which is \(E_H - c_H\).

4Where the canon breaks

The model has a risk-neutral agent (Assumption 1), two outcomes, two actions, and a contract that depends on output only. Holmström (1979), "Moral Hazard and Observability", Bell Journal of Economics 10(1), is cited for the topic of moral hazard by its title, year and venue only; no result or page of that paper is used. The risk-averse case is not derived here. With more than two outcomes, the affine form of Lemma 1.2 no longer covers every contract (Problem L06-r1).

5Misconceptions

  1. “A larger share of output always raises the principal's profit, because it raises effort.”

    Above the threshold share 0.20 the agent's effort does not change, so the extra payment is pure cost. At share 0.30 the principal's profit is 525,000 cents, below the 600,000 cents at share 0.20 (Lab 1, Part A). The profit formula is \(E_H (1 - \beta)\) above the threshold.

  2. “Moral hazard always costs the principal the full effort cost.”

    Without limited liability the principal pays exactly the effort cost and loses nothing (Remark 1.4). With limited liability the loss is 50,000 cents in the base case (Remark 2.1). In this model the loss therefore comes from Assumption 3, not from hidden effort alone.

  3. “Moral hazard means the agent is dishonest.”

    The agent takes the action that maximizes its own utility under the contract, given what the principal can see. The problem is that the action is not observable, and the agent is not assumed to lie about it. Proposition 1.3 treats the agent as a utility maximizer with no dishonesty.

6Problem set

Mechanics

L06 · Utilities at share 0.25

In the base case with \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\beta = 1/4\), compute the agent's utility under high effort and under low effort, which action it takes, and the principal's profit in cents.

Solution

Under high effort the utility is \(\tfrac14 \cdot 750{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 87{,}500\) cents. Under low effort it is \(\tfrac14 \cdot 250{,}000 = 62{,}500\) cents. The agent takes high effort, since \(87{,}500 > 62{,}500\) and the utility is positive. The principal's profit is \(750{,}000 \cdot (1 - 1/4) = 562{,}500\) cents.

L06 · Participation at share 2/15

In the base case with \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\beta = 2/15\), find the agent's utility under high effort, and say whether the incentive constraint holds.

Solution

The utility under high effort is \(\tfrac{2}{15} \cdot 750{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 100{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 0\) cents, so participation holds with equality. Under low effort the utility is \(\tfrac{2}{15} \cdot 250{,}000 \approx 33{,}333\) cents, which is more than 0. The incentive constraint needs \(\beta \ge 1/5\), and \(2/15 < 1/5\), so the agent takes low effort at this share.

L06 · A fixed amount at the optimal share

At share \(\beta = 1/5\), let \(\alpha = 10{,}000\) cents. Compute the principal's profit and check the incentive constraint.

Solution

The expected payment is \(\alpha + \beta E_H = 10{,}000 + 150{,}000 = 160{,}000\) cents, so the profit is \(750{,}000 - 160{,}000 = 590{,}000\) cents. The incentive constraint does not depend on \(\alpha\), so it holds with equality as before. The profit is 10,000 cents below the optimum of 600,000 cents, which is consistent with Proposition 1.3.

Proof

L06 · The incentive constraint is a threshold in the share

Show that under Assumptions 1 and 4, the agent prefers high effort to low effort if and only if \(\beta \ge 1/5\), for any \(\alpha\). Then find a contract with \(\beta \ge 1/5\) that the agent refuses, and say which constraint fails.

Solution

The agent prefers high effort when \(\alpha + \beta E_H - c_H \ge \alpha + \beta E_L\). The constant \(\alpha\) cancels, leaving \(\beta (E_H - E_L) \ge c_H\), that is, \(\beta \cdot 500{,}000 \ge 100{,}000\). Dividing by 500,000 gives \(\beta \ge 1/5\), whatever \(\alpha\) is. Assumption 4 settles the tie at \(\beta = 1/5\). This is a preference condition only. Acceptance is a separate constraint: with \(\alpha = -1{,}000{,}000\) and \(\beta = 1/2\), the condition holds, but the utility under high effort is \(-1{,}000{,}000 + 375{,}000 - 100{,}000 = -725{,}000\) cents and under low effort \(-1{,}000{,}000 + 125{,}000 = -875{,}000\) cents, both below the outside option 0, so the agent refuses.

L06 · The profit bound

Show that for any contract with \(\alpha \ge 0\) under which the agent takes high effort, the principal's profit is at most 600,000 cents, with equality only at \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\beta = 1/5\).

Solution

The profit is \(E_H - \alpha - \beta E_H\). Since \(\alpha \ge 0\), it is at most \(E_H(1 - \beta)\). Since \(\beta \ge 1/5\), this is at most \(750{,}000 \cdot 4/5 = 600{,}000\). Equality needs \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\beta = 1/5\), since the two inequalities used are equalities only there.

L06 · Low effort cannot beat the optimum

Show that a contract that implements low effort gives the principal at most 250,000 cents.

Solution

The expected payment under low effort is \(\alpha + \beta E_L = \tfrac{3}{4} w(0) + \tfrac{1}{4} w(\bar{X})\), which is nonnegative by Assumption 3. The principal's profit is therefore at most \(E_L = 250{,}000\) cents, which is below 600,000 cents.

Empirical

L06 · A higher effort cost

Set the high-effort cost to 150,000 cents and keep the other base-case inputs. Find the optimal share, the principal's profit and the agency cost, in cents, with the first best computed from the same cost. Then change the cost in the twin and run it. No data are used.

Solution

The incentive constraint now needs \(\beta \ge 150{,}000 / 500{,}000 = 3/10\). The profit is \(750{,}000 \cdot 7/10 = 525{,}000\) cents. The first best is \(750{,}000 - 150{,}000 = 600{,}000\) cents, so the agency cost is 75,000 cents. The variant should print a best share of 0.30 and a profit of 525,000 cents.

L06 · Success probabilities 0.8 and 0.2

Set the success probabilities to 0.8 under high effort and 0.2 under low effort, with the base-case output and cost. Find the optimal share, and the principal's profit as an exact value and rounded half up to cents.

Solution

Now \(E_H = 800{,}000\), \(E_L = 200{,}000\), and \(\Delta_E = 600{,}000\). The incentive constraint needs \(\beta \ge 100{,}000 / 600{,}000 = 1/6\). The profit is \(800{,}000 \cdot (5/6) = 666{,}666\tfrac{2}{3}\) cents, which rounds half up to 666,667 cents. The first best is 700,000 cents.

L06 · Two surpluses

In the base case, compute the total surplus under high effort, the principal's profit at the optimum, and the agent's expected utility at the optimum, and check that the surplus equals the sum of the other two.

Solution

The total surplus is \(E_H - c_H = 650{,}000\) cents. The principal's profit at the optimum is 600,000 cents, and the agent's expected utility is \(\tfrac15 \cdot 750{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 50{,}000\) cents. The sum \(600{,}000 + 50{,}000 = 650{,}000\) matches the surplus.

Research

L06 · Why two outcomes make the linear form general

Explain why Lemma 1.2 depends on there being exactly two outcomes, and state what a third outcome adds to the problem.

Solution

With two outcomes a payment schedule has two free values, \(w(0)\) and \(w(\bar{X})\), and any two values define an affine function of output. With three outcomes a schedule has three values, and an affine function has only two parameters, so a general schedule is not affine. The lesson does not solve the three-outcome problem.

L06 · Risk aversion

State what changes in Proposition 1.3 if the agent is risk averse. Name the assumption that would have to change, and say which claim the lesson does not make.

Solution

Assumption 1 would change from risk neutral to a concave utility of the payment. The expected utility would then depend on the spread of payments, not only on their mean, so the incentive constraint would no longer reduce to \(\beta \ge 1/5\). The lesson does not derive the risk-averse optimum, and it makes no claim about its form.

L06 · What observability would change

State the question that the title of Holmström (1979) poses, in the terms of this lesson, and say what the lesson can and cannot answer about it.

Solution

The title names observability. In this lesson's terms the question is how the contract changes when the principal can observe the agent's action. The lesson cites the paper by title only, so it states none of the paper's answers. Its first-best profit (Section 1) is the profit when effort is observable, and the lesson uses it only as a benchmark in this model.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. In the base case under limited liability, what is the optimal share \(\beta\)?

    Options

    Proposition 1.3: the incentive constraint needs \(\beta \ge 100{,}000 / 500{,}000 = 1/5\), and profit falls above it.

  2. In cents, what is the principal's expected profit at the optimal contract?

    \(750{,}000 - 150{,}000 = 600{,}000\) cents (Lab 1, Part B).

  3. At share 0.20 with \(\alpha = 0\), which action does the agent take?

    Options

    Lab 1, Part A: both utilities are 50,000 cents at share 0.20.

  4. In cents, what is the agent's expected utility at share 0.20 with \(\alpha = 0\)?

    \(0.2 \cdot 750{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 50{,}000\) cents.

  5. What is the first-best profit in the base case, in cents?

    Options

    The first best pays the cost \(c_H = 100{,}000\) cents, so the profit is \(750{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 650{,}000\) cents.

  6. If the high-effort cost is 150,000 cents and the other base-case inputs hold, what is the optimal share?

    \(150{,}000 / 500{,}000 = 0.3\), as in Problem L06-e1.

  7. Why does the principal pay a share of output, when the agent is risk neutral?

    Options

    A risk-neutral agent does not need risk sharing. The share is there for the incentive constraint (Proposition 1.3).

  8. With \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\beta > 1/5\), how does the principal's profit change as \(\beta\) rises?

    Options

    Effort is already high above the threshold, so the extra payment is pure cost (Lab 1, Part A, share 0.30).
