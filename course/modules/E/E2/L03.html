Adverse Selection

  • Uses: Bayesian–Nash equilibrium (L02), expectation and conditional expectation (M4), economic surplus (E1)

  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled

When sellers know the quality of what they sell and buyers do not, the price a buyer will pay reflects the average quality of the goods offered at that price, and the goods offered depend on the price. High-quality sellers can withdraw, and gains from trade are lost. This loss is adverse selection. The lesson defines a market-consistent price, proves which prices are consistent in a two-quality market, and computes the gains that are lost. Lab 3 checks the same prices in integer cents. Signaling, defined in L04, and screening, defined in L05, are the next two lessons.

1A two-quality market

Definition 1.1 (the two-quality market)

Each seller holds one unit of a good with quality \(t \in \{H, L\}\), where \(H\) is high and \(L\) is low. The population share of high-quality sellers is \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\), and the share of low-quality sellers is \(1 - \alpha\). A buyer values a unit of quality \(t\) at \(w_t\) cents, and a seller of quality \(t\) has cost \(c_t\) cents. The values are \(w_H = 12000\), \(w_L = 6000\), \(c_H = 10000\) and \(c_L = 4000\). Gains from trade are \(g_t = w_t - c_t\), which are \(g_H = 2000\) and \(g_L = 2000\) cents.

The ordering \(c_H > w_L\) holds in this market: a high-quality seller costs more than a low-quality unit is worth to a buyer. The gains are equal across types for this choice of parameters, so the losses computed below come from withheld trades, not from different gain sizes.

Definition 1.2 (offered mix and market-consistent price)

Let a price \(p\) be a whole number of cents. The offered mix at \(p\) is the set of sellers whose cost is at most \(p\). Write \(m_t(p)\) for the population share of quality \(t\) in the offered mix, so \(m_H(p) = \alpha\) if \(c_H \le p\) and 0 otherwise, and \(m_L(p) = 1 - \alpha\) if \(c_L \le p\) and 0 otherwise. Let \(M(p) = m_H(p) + m_L(p)\). A price \(p\) is a market-consistent price when \(M(p) > 0\) and

\[p = \frac{m_H(p)\,w_H + m_L(p)\,w_L}{M(p)}.\]

The right-hand side is the expectation of the value of a unit drawn from the offered mix, using the population shares as weights. The buyer pays that expectation, and the offered mix is the set of sellers who accept it.

Definition 1.2 is a stated pricing rule for this model. It says that the buyer pays the expected value of the units offered at the price, and the offered units are those whose cost the price covers. Assumptions 1 to 3 below give the setting in which the rule is applied.

Proposition 2.1 (the market-consistent prices)

Assumption 1. Buyers are risk neutral, and a buyer's value of a unit of quality \(t\) is \(w_t\).

Assumption 2. Each seller knows its own quality. Buyers do not observe quality, and the price is the same for every unit offered.

Assumption 3. Each seller holds one unit and accepts a price when the price is at least its cost. The price satisfies Definition 1.2.

Under Assumptions 1 to 3, with \(\alpha\) the share of high-quality sellers, the set of market-consistent prices is

\[\{6000 : \alpha < 1\} \cup \{6000 + 6000\alpha : \alpha \ge 2/3\}.\]

In particular, when \(\alpha < 2/3\) the price 6000 is the only market-consistent price, and when \(\alpha = 1\) the price 12000 is the only one.

Proof

Consider the three ranges of \(p\) into which the costs divide the price axis. The ranges are \(p < 4000\), \(4000 \le p < 10000\) and \(p \ge 10000\), since \(c_L = 4000\) and \(c_H = 10000\).

Range 1, \(p < 4000\). No cost is at most \(p\), so \(M(p) = 0\). By Definition 1.2 no price in this range is market-consistent.

Range 2, \(4000 \le p < 10000\). Only low-quality sellers are offered, so \(M(p) = 1 - \alpha\), and \(m_H(p) = 0\). The rule requires \(1 - \alpha > 0\) and \(p = w_L = 6000\). The price 6000 lies in this range, so 6000 is market-consistent exactly when \(\alpha < 1\).

Range 3, \(p \ge 10000\). Both qualities are offered, so \(M(p) = \alpha + (1 - \alpha) = 1\), and the expected value of the offered mix is \(\alpha \cdot 12000 + (1 - \alpha) \cdot 6000 = 6000 + 6000\alpha\). The rule requires \(p = 6000 + 6000\alpha\). That value is at least 10000 exactly when \(\alpha \ge 2/3\), since \(6000 + 6000\alpha \ge 10000\) is equivalent to \(\alpha \ge 4000/6000\). So the price \(6000 + 6000\alpha\) is market-consistent exactly when \(\alpha \ge 2/3\).

The three ranges cover every whole-number price, and the conditions above are the only ones that Definition 1.2 imposes. This gives the set in the statement. For \(\alpha < 2/3\) the second set is empty, so 6000 is the only price, and for \(\alpha = 1\) the first set is empty while the second gives \(6000 + 6000 = 12000\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (half the sellers are high quality)

Let \(\alpha = 1/2\). The average value of all sellers is \((12000 + 6000)/2 = 9000\) cents. A buyer who paid 9000 would face a seller of quality H with cost 10000 cents, who would withdraw. By Proposition 2.1, the set of market-consistent prices is \(\{6000\}\), because \(\alpha < 2/3\). At 6000 only the low-quality sellers trade, and the average value of the offered units is 6000, which is the price. The average over all sellers is not the price, because the price is set by the offered mix.

Proposition 2.3 (trade and gains lost)

Assume the setting of Proposition 2.1. At the price 6000, which is market-consistent when \(\alpha < 1\), the share of sellers who trade is \(1 - \alpha\), and the gains lost per seller are \(2000\alpha\) cents. At the price \(6000 + 6000\alpha\), which is market-consistent when \(\alpha \ge 2/3\), every seller trades, and no gain is lost. Under full information, in which each seller with a positive gain trades, every seller's gain is 2000 cents, so the surplus per seller is 2000 cents for every \(\alpha\).

Proof

At the price 6000 the offered mix is the low-quality sellers, which have share \(1 - \alpha\) of the population, so the traded share is \(1 - \alpha\). The high-quality sellers, with share \(\alpha\), do not trade. Each of them has a gain of 2000 cents, so the gains lost per seller are \(2000\alpha\). At the price \(6000 + 6000\alpha \ge 10000\) both types are offered, so the traded share is 1 and no gain is lost. The full-information gain per seller is \(\alpha \cdot 2000 + (1 - \alpha) \cdot 2000 = 2000\), which does not depend on \(\alpha\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.4 (the threshold share)

The share at which the second branch of Proposition 2.1 first appears is found from \(6000 + 6000\alpha = 10000\), which gives \(\alpha = 4000/6000 = 2/3\). In general the threshold is \((c_H - w_L)/(w_H - w_L)\). The lab prints this value as the share at which a price at cost H first clears.

"""E2 lab L03: adverse selection in a two-quality market, with exact prices in integer cents.

Standard library only: fractions.Fraction for every share and price comparison. No randomness and no network.
Sellers hold one unit each. A seller is high quality (H) with population share alpha, and low quality (L)
with share 1 - alpha. The buyer's value of a unit is W[H] = 12000 cents or W[L] = 6000 cents, and the seller's
cost is C[H] = 10000 cents or C[L] = 4000 cents. The buyer cannot observe quality, so it pays the expected
value of the units that are offered at the price p. A price p is market-consistent when p equals that
expected value, taken over the sellers whose cost is at most p. Prices are integer cents from 0 to 15000,
and every one is tested, so each printed price list is exact for the cent grid.

The full-information benchmark gives each seller's gain from trade as W - C for its own type, with no
withholding. The lab prints the gains, the share of sellers who trade at each market-consistent price, and
the gains lost to the withheld sellers, in cents per seller, for alpha = k/10 with k from 0 to 10.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

VALUE = {"H": 12000, "L": 6000}
COST = {"H": 10000, "L": 4000}
MAX_PRICE = 15000


def offered_mass(alpha: Fraction, price: int) -> dict[str, Fraction]:
    """Population share of sellers whose cost is at most price, by quality."""
    share = {"H": alpha, "L": 1 - alpha}
    return {t: share[t] for t in ("H", "L") if COST[t] <= price}


def consistent_prices(alpha: Fraction) -> list[tuple[int, Fraction]]:
    """Each market-consistent price with the share of sellers who trade at it."""
    found: list[tuple[int, Fraction]] = []
    for price in range(MAX_PRICE + 1):
        mass = offered_mass(alpha, price)
        total = sum(mass.values(), Fraction(0))
        if total == 0:
            continue
        expected_value = sum((mass[t] * VALUE[t] for t in mass), Fraction(0)) / total
        if expected_value == price:
            found.append((price, total))
    return found


def main() -> None:
    gains = {t: VALUE[t] - COST[t] for t in ("H", "L")}
    print(f"cents: value H {VALUE['H']}, value L {VALUE['L']}, cost H {COST['H']}, cost L {COST['L']}")
    print(f"gain from trade H {gains['H']}, gain from trade L {gains['L']}")
    threshold = Fraction(COST["H"] - VALUE["L"], VALUE["H"] - VALUE["L"])
    print(f"share of high quality at which a price at cost H first clears: {threshold}")
    print(f"price grid 0 to {MAX_PRICE} cents, {MAX_PRICE + 1} prices tested per share")
    for k in range(11):
        alpha = Fraction(k, 10)
        found = consistent_prices(alpha)
        full = alpha * gains["H"] + (1 - alpha) * gains["L"]
        prices = [p for p, _ in found]
        traded = [str(share) for _, share in found]
        losses = [int(full - full * share) for _, share in found]
        print(
            f"alpha = {alpha}: market-consistent prices {prices}, traded share {traded}, "
            f"gains lost per seller in cents {losses}, full-information gains per seller {int(full)}"
        )
    naive = Fraction(1, 2) * VALUE["H"] + Fraction(1, 2) * VALUE["L"]
    print(f"average value at alpha = 1/2: {int(naive)}; high-quality cost {COST['H']} is above it: {COST['H'] > naive}")

3Where the canon breaks

The result above is a model result. Four features of the model carry the conclusion, and each one can change it.

  • The pricing rule is an assumption. Definition 1.2 states how the price is set. Other rules, such as a price set by bargaining or by a contract that conditions on quality, give other predictions, and this lesson does not derive the rule from a larger game.
  • Several prices can be consistent. For \(\alpha \ge 2/3\), Proposition 2.1 gives two market-consistent prices. The definition does not choose between them, and the lesson states no selection rule.
  • Two qualities only. With a continuum of qualities, the offered mix at a price is a truncated distribution, and the case analysis of Proposition 2.1 does not apply as written. Lab 3 does not cover that case.
  • Buyers are risk neutral. Assumption 1 sets the buyer's value at the expected value of quality. Buyers with a different attitude to risk would have a different price rule, which this lesson does not compute.

Akerlof's paper is the registry source for the adverse-selection idea, under the key akerlof1970market, read through its registry title and year only. The two-quality model here is a teaching model. No result of that paper is attributed on this page.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Adverse selection only lowers the price of the bad goods.”

    The main loss is withheld trade. In Proposition 2.3 at the price 6000, the high-quality sellers do not trade, and their gains of 2000 cents each are lost. Lab 3 prints a traded share of \(1 - \alpha\) at 6000, so the share of all sellers who leave the market is \(\alpha\), and every one of them is high quality.

  2. “The price is the average quality of all sellers.”

    Definition 1.2 averages over the offered mix only. In Example 2.2, the average over all sellers is 9000, but at 9000 the high-quality sellers withdraw, so the offered mix is low quality and the price is 6000. The average over all sellers is not a market-consistent price for \(\alpha = 1/2\).

  3. “If one price attracts every seller, the market is efficient.”

    At \(\alpha = 0.7\), Lab 3 prints two market-consistent prices, 6000 with traded share 3/10 and 10200 with traded share 1. The full-trade price exists, but the withdrawal price is also consistent. The existence of an efficient price does not rule out the inefficient one, and the definition does not say which one a market reaches.

5Problem set

Attempt each problem before opening its solution. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 3.

Mechanics

M1 · The threshold share

Using the values and costs of Definition 1.1, find the share \(\alpha\) at which the price \(6000 + 6000\alpha\) equals the high-quality cost.

Solution

Solve \(6000 + 6000\alpha = 10000\). This gives \(6000\alpha = 4000\), so \(\alpha = 2/3\). This agrees with Remark 2.4, since \((c_H - w_L)/(w_H - w_L) = 4000/6000 = 2/3\).

M2 · The consistent prices at a share of 0.8

Find every market-consistent price when \(\alpha = 0.8\).

Solution

Since \(\alpha < 1\), the price 6000 is consistent. Since \(\alpha \ge 2/3\), the price \(6000 + 6000 \times 0.8 = 10800\) is also consistent, and 10800 is at least 10000 as required. So the consistent prices are 6000 and 10800, which agrees with the Lab 3 output for \(\alpha = 4/5\).

M3 · Gains lost at a low share

Let \(\alpha = 1/4\). At the price 6000, what share of sellers trades, and what gains are lost per seller in cents?

Solution

The traded share is \(1 - \alpha = 3/4\), since only the low-quality sellers, which are 3/4 of the population, are offered at 6000. The gains lost per seller are \(2000\alpha = 2000/4 = 500\) cents. This is the high-quality share times the gain of 2000 cents for each high-quality seller.

Proof

P1 · No offers below the low cost

Show that no whole-number price below 4000 is market-consistent.

Solution

For \(p < 4000\), neither cost is at most \(p\), because \(c_L = 4000\) and \(c_H = 10000\). So \(m_H(p) = m_L(p) = 0\), and \(M(p) = 0\). Definition 1.2 requires \(M(p) > 0\), so no price below 4000 is market-consistent.

P2 · The expected value of the full mix

For \(p \ge 10000\), show that the expected value of the offered mix is \(6000 + 6000\alpha\).

Solution

At \(p \ge 10000\) both costs are covered, so \(m_H = \alpha\) and \(m_L = 1 - \alpha\), and \(M = 1\). The expected value is \(\alpha \cdot 12000 + (1 - \alpha) \cdot 6000 = 6000 + 6000\alpha\), because \(12000\alpha - 6000\alpha = 6000\alpha\) and the constant term is 6000.

P3 · The price 6000 when \(\alpha = 1\)

Show that 6000 is not market-consistent when \(\alpha = 1\), and identify the consistent price.

Solution

When \(\alpha = 1\) the low-quality share is \(1 - \alpha = 0\), so at the price 6000 the offered mix has \(M(6000) = m_L(6000) = 0\). Definition 1.2 requires \(M(p) > 0\), so 6000 fails. At \(p \ge 10000\) only the high-quality sellers are offered, and the expected value is 12000. The price 12000 satisfies the rule, and \(6000 + 6000 \cdot 1 = 12000\) agrees with the formula of Proposition 2.1.

Empirical

E1 · Two prices at a share of 0.7

Lab 3 prints, for \(\alpha = 0.7\), the consistent prices 6000 and 10200, with traded shares 3/10 and 1. Explain each pair.

Solution

The price 6000 has traded share \(1 - 0.7 = 0.3 = 3/10\), since only low-quality sellers are offered. The price 10200 is \(6000 + 6000 \times 0.7\), and at that price both qualities are offered, so the traded share is 1. Both prices satisfy Definition 1.2, which the lab checks for every whole-number price.

E2 · Gains lost at a share of 0.9

Lab 3 prints gains lost per seller of 1800 cents at the price 6000 when \(\alpha = 0.9\). Check this value.

Solution

The gains lost are \(2000\alpha = 2000 \times 0.9 = 1800\) cents per seller, which matches the printed value. The basis is the population of all sellers, and the high-quality share of that population is 0.9.

E3 · The threshold line

Lab 3 prints the threshold share as 2/3. Show that the printed value follows from the parameters in the lab's constants.

Solution

The lab's constants are \(c_H = 10000\), \(w_L = 6000\) and \(w_H = 12000\). The threshold is \((c_H - w_L)/(w_H - w_L) = 4000/6000 = 2/3\), which matches Remark 2.4 and Problem M1.

Research

R1 · The rule as an assumption

Definition 1.2 is a stated rule. Name one feature of a market that a derivation of the rule from a larger game would have to model, and say what would change if that feature were modeled differently.

Solution

A derivation would need to model how buyers form beliefs about the quality of a unit at a price that no seller offers. Definition 1.2 uses the offered mix at each price, and says nothing about beliefs at prices where the offered mix is empty. If buyers held a different belief at such a price, the market-consistent set could change, and the lesson's result would depend on that choice. The lesson does not model this belief.

R2 · A ratio with a stated base

Using Proposition 2.3, compute the ratio of gains lost to full-information surplus, for each \(\alpha\), and state the base of the ratio.

Solution

The base is the full-information surplus per seller, which is 2000 cents for every \(\alpha\). The gains lost per seller at the price 6000 are \(2000\alpha\). The ratio is therefore \(2000\alpha / 2000 = \alpha\), which equals the high-quality share. The ratio is per seller of the population, not per traded unit.

R3 · A continuum of qualities

Explain what changes in the case analysis of Proposition 2.1 when quality is a continuous variable with a cost \(c(q)\) and a value \(w(q)\) for each quality \(q\).

Solution

The offered mix at a price \(p\) is the distribution of quality restricted to the qualities with \(c(q) \le p\). The expected value of the offered mix is then an integral over that restricted distribution, and the consistent prices are the fixed points of a function of \(p\), not two cases. The two-range analysis of Proposition 2.1 does not extend directly, and the lesson does not analyze that case.
