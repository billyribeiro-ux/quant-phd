Common-value auctions, the winner's curse and bid shading

  • Uses: mechanism design, Bayesian–Nash equilibrium, expectation
  • Introduces: common-value auction, winner's curse, bid shading
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

When every bidder estimates the same unknown value with its own error, the bidder with the highest estimate is the one with the largest error. This lesson defines a common-value auction and proves that a winner who bids its own estimate loses money on average, which is the winner's curse. It computes the expected size of that loss exactly, and it derives the shading that removes it. The seeded simulation in Lab 1 is SIMULATED. It checks the exact figures, and it makes no claim about real bidders.

1Common values, signals and the highest signal

Money is in integer cents. An auction sells one unit. There are \(n\) bidders, with \(n \ge 2\), and the number \(n\) is fixed and known. The unit has one value \(V\), in cents, which is the same for every bidder. No bidder observes \(V\). Each bidder \(i\) observes a signal \(S_i\), which is an estimate of \(V\) with an error. The lesson uses the letter \(n\) for the count of bidders, and \(\varepsilon_i\) for the error in bidder \(i\)'s signal.

Assumption 1 (common value and signal errors)

The common value \(V\) is a whole number of cents, and it has any prior distribution. Each signal is \(S_i = V + \varepsilon_i\), where the errors \(\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n\) are independent of each other and of \(V\). Each error is uniform on the whole numbers \(-a, \dots, a\) for a fixed whole number \(a \ge 1\), with \(a\) in cents. Each error has mean 0, because the range is symmetric about 0.

Assumption 2 (the format and the payoff)

The auction is a first-price auction for one unit. The bidder with the highest bid wins, a tie goes to the lower bidder index, and the winner pays its own bid. A bidder's profit when it wins is \(V\) minus its price, in cents, and it is 0 when it loses. Bidders are risk neutral, so each one maximizes expected profit.

Definition 1.1 (common-value auction)

A common-value auction is an auction in which every bidder's payoff depends on the same unknown value \(V\), and each bidder has only a signal about it. This is the case of Assumption 1. A private-value auction, in which each bidder knows its own value, is a different model and is not analyzed here.

Definition 1.2 (the winner's curse)

The winner's curse is the fact that, in a common-value auction in which each bidder bids its own signal, the bidder who wins has a negative expected profit. The winner is the bidder whose signal is the largest. Its error is the largest of the errors, \(M\), and \(\E[M] > 0\) (Proposition 2.1), so the winning signal is too high on average.

Definition 1.3 (bid shading)

Bid shading is a bid below the bidder's own signal. A shading rule has the form \(b = S - c\), where \(c\) is a whole number of cents that does not depend on the bidder's signal. The rule is a fixed offset, and this lesson does not claim that it is an equilibrium of the auction (see §4).

2The winner loses money on average when it bids its signal

Let \(M = \max(\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n)\) be the largest error. The largest error is the one the winner has. Under the naive rule in which each bidder bids its signal, \(b_i = S_i\), the largest signal wins. Because every signal has the same \(V\), the largest signal is \(V + M\), and the winner is the bidder with the largest error.

Proposition 2.1 (the naive winner's expected profit is \(-\E[M]\))

Under Assumptions 1 and 2, let each bidder bid its signal. The winner's profit in cents is \(-M\), in every realization. Its expected profit is \(-\E[M]\). For \(n \ge 2\) and \(a \ge 1\), \(\E[M] > 0\), so the expected profit of the winner is strictly negative.

Proof

Each bid is \(b_i = V + \varepsilon_i\). The winner is the bidder with the largest bid, and a tie goes to the lower index. Every bid is \(V + \varepsilon_i\), so the largest bid is \(V + M\), and the winner's bid is \(V + M\) whichever bidder holds it. The winner pays \(V + M\), so its profit is \(V - (V + M) = -M\). This holds in every realization, including ties. Taking expectations gives \(-\E[M]\).

For the sign, \(M \ge \varepsilon_1\) in every realization, so \(\E[M] \ge \E[\varepsilon_1] = 0\). Also \(M - \varepsilon_1 \ge (\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1)^{+}\), where \((x)^{+} = \max(x, 0)\), so \(\E[M] \ge \E[\varepsilon_1] + \E[(\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1)^{+}] = \E[(\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1)^{+}]\). The event \(\varepsilon_2 = a\) and \(\varepsilon_1 = -a\) has probability \((2a+1)^{-2} > 0\), and on it \(\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1 = 2a > 0\). So \(\E[(\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1)^{+}] > 0\), and \(\E[M] > 0\).

Proposition 2.2 (the expected largest error, in closed form)

Under Assumption 1, with \(F(x) = (x + a + 1)/(2a + 1)\) for whole \(x\) with \(-a \le x \le a\), \[ \E[M] = -a + \sum_{x=-a}^{a-1} \bigl(1 - F(x)^{n}\bigr). \]

Proof

Each error is at most \(a\) and at least \(-a\), so \(M\) takes whole values in \(-a, \dots, a\). Since each error is at most \(x\) with probability \(F(x)\), and the errors are independent, \(\Prob(M \le x) = F(x)^{n}\) for those \(x\). For a whole number \(m\) with \(-a \le m \le a\), the number of whole \(x\) with \(-a \le x \le a - 1\) and \(x < m\) is \(m + a\). So \(M + a = \sum_{x=-a}^{a-1} \indic\{M > x\}\) in every realization. Taking expectations, \(\E[M] + a = \sum_{x=-a}^{a-1} \Prob(M > x) = \sum_{x=-a}^{a-1} (1 - F(x)^{n})\).

Remark 2.3 (the continuous analogue)

If each error were uniform on the whole interval \([-a, a]\), then \(M = -a + 2a\,U\), where \(U\) is the largest of \(n\) independent uniform draws on \([0, 1]\). The largest draw has \(\Prob(U > u) = 1 - u^{n}\), so \(\E[U] = \int_0^1 (1 - u^{n})\,du = n/(n+1)\), and \(\E[M] = -a + 2an/(n+1) = a(n-1)/(n+1)\). For \(n = 5\) and \(a = 2000\) cents this is \(4000/3 = 1333.33\) cents. The discrete model of Assumption 1 gives \(\E[M] = 85418692002000/64048012001 \approx 1333.66656253\) cents. This value is Proposition 2.2 evaluated exactly with fractions (grade T, basis: Proposition 2.2; Lab 1 Part B uses it). The two values differ by \(1333.66656253 - 1333.3333333 = 0.3332292\) cents, which rounds to 0.3332 cents; the rounded figures 1333.67 and 1333.33 differ by 0.34. This lesson does not analyze that difference beyond the two formulas.

Example 2.4 (two bidders, a half-width of one cent)

Take \(n = 2\) and \(a = 1\). Each error is \(-1\), 0 or 1 with probability 1/3 each. Then \(\Prob(M \le -1) = (1/3)^{2} = 1/9\) and \(\Prob(M \le 0) = (2/3)^{2} = 4/9\). So \(\Prob(M = -1) = 1/9\), \(\Prob(M = 0) = 3/9\) and \(\Prob(M = 1) = 5/9\), and \(\E[M] = -1/9 + 5/9 = 4/9\) cents. Part A of Lab 1 checks this by listing all nine pairs of errors.

3Shading by the expected largest error removes the loss

Proposition 3.1 (the expected profit under a fixed shading)

Under Assumptions 1 and 2, let each bidder bid \(S_i - c\) for a fixed whole number \(c\) of cents. The winner's expected profit is \(c - \E[M]\). It is nonnegative exactly when \(c \ge \E[M]\).

Proof

Subtracting the same \(c\) from every signal does not change the order of the bids, so the winner is still the bidder with the largest signal, and its bid is \(V + M - c\). Its profit is \(V - (V + M - c) = c - M\). Taking expectations gives \(c - \E[M]\). This is nonnegative exactly when \(c \ge \E[M]\).

Remark 3.2 (the shading in whole cents)

The shading of Proposition 3.1 must be a whole number of cents, because a bid is a money amount. With \(\E[M] = 1333.66656253\) cents for \(n = 5\) and \(a = 2000\), the smallest whole-cent shading that makes the expected profit nonnegative is 1334. Lab 1 Part C uses 1334 cents, the rounded value, with a tie going up. Rounding down to 1333 would leave an expected profit of \(1333 - 1333.66656253 = -0.66656253\) cents per auction, which rounds to \(-0.67\) cents.

Lab 1 · Winner's profit in a seeded common-value simulation (SIMULATED)

SIMULATED. The values, signals and errors in this lab are generated by a seeded pseudo-random generator with seed 20261028. No auction data is used. The model is that of Assumption 1 with \(n = 5\) bidders, a half-width of 2000 cents, and a common value uniform on the whole numbers 5000 to 15000 cents. Before running, the prediction is this. """E2 L08 lab 1 (SIMULATED): the winner's curse in a common-value auction, and a shading rule that offsets it.

SIMULATED. Every value and signal below is generated by a seeded pseudo-random generator. No auction data is used.

Model, in integer cents. The common value V is uniform on the whole numbers 5000 to 15000. Bidder i observes
the signal S_i = V + e_i, where the noises e_i are independent and uniform on the whole numbers -2000 to 2000.
There are n = 5 bidders and one unit. The highest bidder wins and pays its own bid, and ties go to the lower
bidder index. Each winner's profit in cents is V minus the price it pays.

Part A checks the exact formula for the expected largest noise M, the largest of the n noises e_1 through e_n,
against a full enumeration
of the nine noise pairs for n = 2 and a half-width of 1 cent.
Part B bids the signal itself (the naive rule) in 20000 simulated auctions with seed 20261028, and prints the
total profit of the winner, next to the exact expected total.
Part C bids the signal less a shading amount c, equal to the rounded exact expected largest noise, in the same
simulated auctions, and prints the total profit of the winner, next to the exact expected total.

Totals are whole cents. The exact expected totals are rounded to the nearest whole cent, a tie going up. Each
simulated total is printed with its standard error, the estimated sampling error of a total over the auctions.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
import math
import random
from fractions import Fraction

HALF_WIDTH = 2000
BIDDERS = 5
AUCTIONS = 20000
SEED = 20261028
V_LOW = 5000
V_HIGH = 15000


def expected_max_noise(n: int, a: int) -> Fraction:
    """Exact E[M] for M the largest of n independent noises, each uniform on the whole numbers -a to a.

    P(M <= x) = ((x + a + 1) / (2a + 1))^n for whole x in -a..a, and M + a = sum over x in -a..a-1 of 1{M > x}.
    """
    total = Fraction(-a)
    for x in range(-a, a):
        cdf = Fraction(x + a + 1, 2 * a + 1) ** n
        total += 1 - cdf
    return total


def enumerated_expected_max_noise(n: int, a: int) -> Fraction:
    """Exact E[M] by listing every tuple of noises; feasible only for small n and a."""
    values = range(-a, a + 1)
    tuples = list(itertools.product(values, repeat=n))
    return Fraction(sum(max(t) for t in tuples), len(tuples))


def half_up_cents(amount: Fraction) -> int:
    """The amount in whole cents, a tie going up."""
    return math.floor(amount + Fraction(1, 2))


def run_auctions(shade: int, rng: random.Random) -> list[int]:
    """Profit in cents of the winner in each of AUCTIONS auctions, when each bidder bids its signal less shade."""
    profits: list[int] = []
    for _ in range(AUCTIONS):
        value = rng.randint(V_LOW, V_HIGH)
        signals = [value + rng.randint(-HALF_WIDTH, HALF_WIDTH) for _ in range(BIDDERS)]
        bids = [s - shade for s in signals]
        winner = max(range(BIDDERS), key=lambda i: (bids[i], -i))
        profits.append(value - bids[winner])
    return profits


def standard_error_of_total(profits: list[int]) -> int:
    """Standard error of the simulated total profit, in whole cents, a tie going up.

    The sample variance of the profits is (N * sum of squares - (sum)^2) / (N * (N - 1)), so the standard error
    of the total over N auctions is the square root of N times that variance, which is the square root of
    (N * sum of squares - (sum)^2) / (N - 1).
    """
    n = len(profits)
    numerator = n * sum(p * p for p in profits) - sum(profits) ** 2
    return math.floor(math.sqrt(numerator / (n - 1)) + 0.5)


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A, B and C in order. The output is fixed by the seed."""
    print("Part A: expected largest noise, formula against enumeration")
    formula = expected_max_noise(2, 1)
    enumerated = enumerated_expected_max_noise(2, 1)
    print(f"pairs_enumerated {len(list(itertools.product(range(-1, 2), repeat=2)))}")
    print(f"formula_n2_a1 {formula}")
    print(f"enumeration_n2_a1 {enumerated}")
    print(f"formula_equals_enumeration {formula == enumerated}")

    expected = expected_max_noise(BIDDERS, HALF_WIDTH)
    shade = half_up_cents(expected)
    print(f"Part B: naive bids equal the signal, n = {BIDDERS}, half-width {HALF_WIDTH} cents, {AUCTIONS} auctions")
    naive = run_auctions(0, random.Random(SEED))
    print(f"naive_winner_profit_total_cents {sum(naive)}")
    print(f"naive_standard_error_cents {standard_error_of_total(naive)}")
    print(f"exact_naive_winner_profit_total_cents {half_up_cents(-AUCTIONS * expected)}")

    print(f"Part C: shaded bids, shade {shade} cents, the rounded exact expected largest noise")
    shaded = run_auctions(shade, random.Random(SEED))
    print(f"shade_cents {shade}")
    print(f"shaded_winner_profit_total_cents {sum(shaded)}")
    print(f"shaded_standard_error_cents {standard_error_of_total(shaded)}")
    print(f"exact_shaded_winner_profit_total_cents {half_up_cents(AUCTIONS * (shade - expected))}")

4Where the canon breaks

  • The shading is not an equilibrium. Proposition 3.1 averages the winner's profit over all signal realizations. It does not condition on a bidder's own signal, and it does not condition on winning with that signal. A Bayesian–Nash equilibrium (L02) needs each bidder's best bid for each of its own signals, given the other bidders' bids. This lesson does not compute such an equilibrium.
  • Mechanism design. The auction of Assumption 2 is a mechanism in the sense of Definition 1.1 of L07, in which the messages are bids. The format is given by Assumption 2, so the lesson does not choose the rules, and it does not use the revelation principle of L07.
  • The model is narrow. The errors are independent and uniform with mean zero (Assumption 1), there is one unit, the format is first-price, and the bidders are risk neutral (Assumption 2). Proposition 2.1 needs the errors to be independent and identically distributed, and it needs \(a \ge 1\) for the strict inequality. The results of §2 and §3 are derived under these assumptions, and this lesson does not derive them for other models.
  • Real bidders. The lesson proves a result about the model. Whether bidders in real auctions overpay in this way is an empirical question. The registry does not hold a source for the empirical claim, so the lesson makes no such claim (see §9).
  • Private values. The winner's curse of Definition 1.2 needs a common value. When each bidder knows its own value, a winner's profit is not defined by the same identity, and this lesson does not analyze that case.

5Misconceptions

  1. “If every signal is an unbiased estimate of the value, bidding the signal is a fair bid.”

    Each signal is unbiased for \(V\), since \(\E[S_i \mid V] = V\). But the winner is the bidder with the largest error, and its expected profit under naive bidding is \(-\E[M] < 0\) (Proposition 2.1). Part B of Lab 1 prints a negative total for the winners. Unbiased signals do not make the bid of the winner unbiased.

  2. “Winning an auction is good news about the value.”

    Under naive bidding, winning means that one's signal was the largest, so the winner's own signal is too high on average (\(\E[M] > 0\), Proposition 2.1), and that error is the cause of the loss. The price the winner pays equals \(V + M\), which is above \(V\) by the largest error. The result is a loss of \(M\) cents, on average \(\E[M] > 0\).

  3. “Any positive shading removes the winner's curse.”

    Proposition 3.1 gives an expected profit of \(c - \E[M]\). A shading below \(\E[M]\) leaves the expected profit negative. For \(n = 5\) and \(a = 2000\) cents, a shading of 1333 cents leaves \(-0.67\) cents per auction (Remark 3.2). The size of the shading matters, not only its sign.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · The winner's expected profit for two bidders and a half-width of one cent

Take \(n = 2\) and \(a = 1\) (errors are \(-1\), 0 or 1 cent with probability 1/3 each). Find the expected profit of the winner under naive bidding, in cents.

Solution

By Proposition 2.1 the expected profit is \(-\E[M]\). Example 2.4 gives \(\E[M] = 4/9\), so the expected profit is \(-4/9\) cents, which is about \(-0.44\) cents. Part A of Lab 1 confirms \(\E[M] = 4/9\) by listing the nine pairs of errors.

M2 · The expected largest error for three bidders and a half-width of one cent

Take \(n = 3\) and \(a = 1\). Find \(\E[M]\) from the closed form of Proposition 2.2.

Solution

Here \(F(-1) = 1/3\) and \(F(0) = 2/3\). Proposition 2.2 gives \(\E[M] = -1 + (1 - F(-1)^{3}) + (1 - F(0)^{3}) = -1 + (1 - 1/27) + (1 - 8/27) = -1 + 26/27 + 19/27 = -1 + 45/27 = 2/3\). As a check, \(\Prob(M \le -1) = 1/27\) and \(\Prob(M \le 0) = 8/27\), so \(\Prob(M = -1) = 1/27\), \(\Prob(M = 0) = 7/27\), \(\Prob(M = 1) = 19/27\), and \(\E[M] = -1/27 + 19/27 = 18/27 = 2/3\).

M3 · A one-cent shading for two bidders

With \(n = 2\) and \(a = 1\), a bidder shades every bid by 1 cent. Find the winner's expected profit, and say whether the shading is large enough.

Solution

By Proposition 3.1 the expected profit is \(c - \E[M] = 1 - 4/9 = 5/9\) cents. It is positive, and by Proposition 3.1 it is nonnegative exactly when \(c \ge \E[M] = 4/9\). A shading of 1 cent is therefore large enough in this model, and the smallest whole-cent shading that is large enough is also 1 cent, because \(0 < 4/9 \le 1\).

Proof

P1 · The winner's profit with ties

Show that the identity \(\text{profit} = -M\) of Proposition 2.1 holds when two bidders have the same largest signal.

Solution

If two bidders share the largest signal \(V + M\), the rule gives the win to the lower index, and the price is that common bid, \(V + M\). The winner's profit is \(V - (V + M) = -M\), the same as with no tie. The identity uses only the value of the largest bid, so it holds with or without ties.

P2 · The distribution of the largest error

Under Assumption 1, show that \(\Prob(M \le x) = F(x)^{n}\) for whole \(x\) with \(-a \le x \le a\).

Solution

The event \(M \le x\) is the event that every error is at most \(x\), that is \(\varepsilon_1 \le x, \dots, \varepsilon_n \le x\). The errors are independent, so the probability is the product \(\prod_i \Prob(\varepsilon_i \le x)\). Each factor is \(\Prob(\varepsilon_i \le x) = F(x)\), because the error is uniform on \(-a, \dots, a\) and \(F(x) = (x + a + 1)/(2a + 1)\) counts the whole numbers from \(-a\) to \(x\). So \(\Prob(M \le x) = F(x)^{n}\).

P3 · Strict positivity of the expected largest error

Using only Assumption 1, show that \(\E[M] > 0\) when \(n \ge 2\) and \(a \ge 1\), and explain why the argument needs \(a \ge 1\).

Solution

The proof of Proposition 2.1 gives \(M \ge \varepsilon_1\), so \(\E[M] \ge \E[\varepsilon_1] = 0\). The inequality is strict because \(M - \varepsilon_1 \ge (\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1)^{+}\), and on the event \(\varepsilon_2 = a\), \(\varepsilon_1 = -a\), which has probability \((2a+1)^{-2}\), the right side equals \(2a\). This event needs \(a \ge 1\), because when \(a = 0\) every error is 0, and then \(M = 0\) and \(\E[M] = 0\). So \(\E[M] > 0\) requires \(a \ge 1\).

Empirical

E1 · The simulated winner's profit against its expected value

Read Part B of Lab 1. The simulated total is \(-26620718\) cents and the exact total is \(-26673331\) cents. Find the difference in standard errors, and state the standard error per auction.

Solution

The difference is \(-26620718 - (-26673331) = 52613\) cents. The lab prints the standard error of the simulated total as 79810 cents, so the difference is \(52613 / 79810 = 0.66\) standard errors. The standard error of the simulated average per auction is \(79810 / 20000 = 3.99\) cents. The exact total is rounded to the cent, a tie going up, from \(-26673331.25\) cents. Using the unrounded value changes the difference by 0.25 cents, which does not change the 0.66 figure to two decimal places.

E2 · The shading shift in the simulated totals

Read Parts B and C of Lab 1. Show that the simulated shaded total minus the simulated naive total is exactly \(20000 \times 1334\) cents.

Solution

The shaded total is 59282 cents and the naive total is \(-26620718\) cents. Their difference is \(59282 + 26620718 = 26680000\) cents. Also \(20000 \times 1334 = 26680000\). The two parts use the same seed and the same sequence of draws, and the shading only lowers each bid by 1334 cents, so the winner's profit rises by 1334 cents in every auction.

E3 · The exact expected profit at each shading

Using the exact expected largest error of Part B (\(\E[M] = 1333.66656253\) cents, unrounded; Part B's total is \(-20000 \times \E[M]\) rounded to cents), find the exact expected profit per auction at a shading of 1334 cents and at 1333 cents.

Solution

At 1334 cents the expected profit per auction is \(1334 - 1333.66656253 = 0.33343747\) cents, so over 20000 auctions the exact total is \(6668.749\) cents, which the lab prints rounded as 6669 cents. At 1333 cents it is \(1333 - 1333.66656253 = -0.66656253\) cents, which is negative. So the shading must reach the expected largest error, and 1334 is the smallest whole-cent shading that makes the expected profit nonnegative.

Research

R1 · The continuous and discrete expected largest error

Using Remark 2.3, compute the continuous \(\E[M] = a(n-1)/(n+1)\) for \(n = 2\) and \(a = 1\), and compare it with the discrete value of Example 2.4.

Solution

The continuous value is \(a(n-1)/(n+1) = 1 \times 1 / 3 = 1/3\), which is 0.3333. The discrete value of Example 2.4 is \(4/9 = 0.4444\). The two models differ by \(4/9 - 1/3 = 1/9\), which is 0.1111 cents. The lesson does not explain this difference beyond the two formulas. The discrete value is the one used in Lab 1.

R2 · What an equilibrium check would need

Explain why the calculation in Proposition 3.1 does not show that bidding \(S - \E[M]\) is a Bayesian–Nash equilibrium, and name the quantity an equilibrium check must compute for a bidder with signal \(s\).

Solution

Proposition 3.1 averages the winner's profit over all realizations of the signals. A bidder choosing a bid makes that choice with its own signal \(s\) known. An equilibrium check therefore computes the expected profit of a bid conditional on the bidder's own signal \(s\) and on winning with that bid, given the other bidders' bids. That conditional expectation is not the unconditional average of Proposition 3.1, so the calculation does not establish an equilibrium.

R3 · The set of shadings with nonnegative expected profit

In the model of Proposition 3.1, find the set of whole-number shadings \(c \ge 0\) for which the winner's expected profit is nonnegative, when \(n = 5\) and \(a = 2000\).

Solution

By Proposition 3.1 the expected profit is \(c - \E[M]\), which is nonnegative exactly when \(c \ge \E[M] = 1333.6666\) cents. Among whole-number shadings, the set is \(\{1334, 1335, 1336, \dots\}\), so the smallest member is 1334. Larger shadings give a larger expected profit, which is a larger margin for the winner, but a larger shading also means a lower bid. This lesson does not model how the number of wins changes with the shading.

8Retrieval quiz

Answer from memory, then check. Finishing the quiz adds this lesson's cards to your review deck.

  1. In a common-value auction with naive bidding, which bidder wins?

    Options

    Under naive bidding the bids are the signals, so the largest signal wins (§2). Its error is the largest of the errors, \(M\).

  2. Two bidders, a half-width of one cent. What is the winner's expected profit under naive bidding, in cents, to four decimal places?

    \(-\E[M] = -4/9 = -0.4444\) cents (Example 2.4, Problem M1).

  3. Under Assumption 1, what is the sign of \(\E[M]\) for \(n \ge 2\) and \(a \ge 1\)?

    Options

    Proposition 2.1: \(M\) is at least \(\varepsilon_1\) in every realization, and the strict inequality follows from \(\varepsilon_2 = a\), \(\varepsilon_1 = -a\).

  4. With \(n = 5\) and \(a = 2000\) cents, what is the smallest whole-cent shading that makes the winner's expected profit nonnegative?

    \(\E[M] = 1333.6666\) cents, so the smallest whole number at or above it is 1334 (Remark 3.2, Problem R3).

  5. Which statement about the continuous analogue for \(n = 5\) and \(a = 2000\) is correct?

    Options

    Remark 2.3: \(a(n-1)/(n+1) = 2000 \times 4 / 6 = 4000/3\).

  6. In Part B of Lab 1, the simulated winner's total profit is negative. What does that show about the model?

    Options

    The lab is SIMULATED. It prints the exact expected totals beside the simulated totals, and §4 says the lesson makes no claim about real bidders.

  7. With three bidders and a half-width of one cent (\(n = 3\), \(a = 1\)), what is \(\E[M]\) in cents, to four decimal places?

    \(\E[M] = 2/3 = 0.6667\) cents (Problem M2).

  8. Does a shading of 1333 cents remove the winner's curse for \(n = 5\) and \(a = 2000\)?

    Options

    Proposition 3.1 and Problem E3: \(1333 - 1333.6666 = -0.6666\) cents.

  9. Under naive bidding the winner pays \(V + M\). If the common value is \(V = 9000\) cents and the largest error is \(M = 250\) cents, what is the winner's profit in cents?

    Profit is \(V - (V + M) = -M = -250\) cents, by Proposition 2.1.
