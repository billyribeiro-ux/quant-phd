The Natural Rate, FTPL, and Post-Pandemic Inflation

Monetary policy cannot be evaluated in a vacuum: setting interest rates requires a neutral benchmark—the natural rate of interest \(r^*\)—while the ultimate anchor of the price level depends on fiscal backing. In the wake of the 2020 pandemic, massive unbacked fiscal transfers and severe supply chain dislocations collided, triggering the most severe global inflation surge in four decades and reshaping central bank operating doctrine.

1. The Natural Rate of Interest (\(r^*\))

The natural rate of interest (r-star), denoted \(r^*\), is the real short-term interest rate consistent with output equaling its potential level and inflation remaining stable at the central bank's target. Tracing back to Knut Wicksell (1898), \(r^*\) serves as the neutral anchor for monetary policy: when the real policy rate \(r_t = i_t - \E_t[\pi_{t+1}]\) exceeds \(r_t^*\), monetary policy is contractionary; when \(r_t < r_t^*\), policy is expansionary.

Because \(r^*\) is an unobservable latent variable, it must be inferred using structural time-series models. Holston, Laubach, & Williams (2017) (HLW) specify a multi-country state-space model estimated via the Kalman filter:

Definition: The Holston-Laubach-Williams (2017) Model

The structural economy consists of an empirical dynamic IS equation and a backward-looking Phillips curve:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{y}_t &= a_1 \tilde{y}_{t-1} + a_2 \tilde{y}_{t-2} - \frac{a_r}{2} \sum_{j=1}^2 (r_{t-j} - r_{t-j}^*) + \varepsilon_{\tilde{y}, t} \\ \pi_t &= b_\pi \pi_{t-1} + (1 - b_\pi) \pi_{t-2:4} + b_y \tilde{y}_{t-1} + \varepsilon_{\pi, t} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\tilde{y}_t = 100 \cdot (y_t - y_t^*)\) is the output gap between log real GDP \(y_t\) and latent potential output \(y_t^*\). The natural rate of interest is decomposed into trend productivity growth \(g_t\) and a persistent non-growth factor \(z_t\):

\[ r_t^* = g_t + z_t \]

The state variables follow random walks:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t^* &= y_{t-1}^* + g_{t-1} + \varepsilon_{y^*, t} \\ g_t &= g_{t-1} + \varepsilon_{g, t} \\ z_t &= z_{t-1} + \varepsilon_{z, t} \end{aligned} \]

Holston et al. (2017) document a synchronized secular decline in \(r^*\) across advanced economies (United States, Euro Area, United Kingdom, Canada) from approximately \(3.5\%\) in the 1970s and 1980s to below \(0.5\%\) to \(1.0\%\) following the 2008 global financial crisis. This downward trend is driven by slower trend productivity growth (\(g\)), demographic aging (increasing desired savings), and an elevated global demand for safe, liquid sovereign assets (\(z < 0\)). A low \(r^*\) severely compresses the policy rate headroom, increasing the frequency and duration of zero lower bound episodes.

2. The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level (FTPL)

Standard New Keynesian models assume monetary dominance (active monetary policy satisfies the Taylor principle, while fiscal policy passively adjusts lump-sum taxes to satisfy intertemporal solvency). However, what happens when fiscal policy is active and refuses to raise taxes to back public debt?

The fiscal theory of the price level (FTPL), synthesized comprehensively by Cochrane (2023), establishes that the aggregate price level is determined by the intertemporal government budget constraint, where nominal government debt functions like equity in the government.

Definition: The Government Debt Valuation Equation

Under FTPL, the real value of outstanding nominal government debt \(B_{t-1}\) must equal the expected present discounted value of future real primary fiscal surpluses \(\{s_{t+j}\}_{j=0}^\infty\):

\[ \frac{B_{t-1}}{P_t} = \E_t \sum_{j=0}^\infty \mathcal{M}_{t, t+j} s_{t+j} \]

where:

  • \(B_{t-1}\) is total nominal government liabilities (currency, bank reserves, and Treasury securities) held by the public entering period \(t\).
  • \(P_t\) is the price level.
  • \(s_{t+j} = T_{t+j} - G_{t+j}\) is the real primary fiscal surplus (tax revenues minus non-interest government spending).
  • \(\mathcal{M}_{t, t+j}\) is the real stochastic discount factor.

Under FTPL, this equation is not an accounting constraint that forces future taxes to adjust; rather, it is an equilibrium market-clearing valuation equation for government debt. If the government issues nominal debt to fund transfers without a credible commitment to run future surpluses to redeem it (an "unbacked fiscal expansion"), the expected present value of surpluses does not change. Therefore, the price level \(P_t\) must rise until the real value of outstanding debt matches the present value of real surpluses:

\[ P_t = \frac{B_{t-1}}{\E_t \sum_{j=0}^\infty \mathcal{M}_{t, t+j} s_{t+j}} \]

As Cochrane (2023) demonstrates, unbacked fiscal transfers generate inflation even if the central bank holds interest rates constant or operates at the effective lower bound.

3. The 2021–2023 Post-Pandemic Inflation Surge

The 2021–23 inflation episode was the abrupt, worldwide acceleration of headline and core inflation following the COVID-19 pandemic, during which US headline CPI inflation peaked at \(9.1\%\) in June 2022—the highest rate since 1981. This episode challenged central banks' forward guidance and ignited intense academic debates over supply-side bottlenecks, labor tightness, and fiscal stimulus.

Bernanke & Blanchard (2025) provide a structural econometric decomposition of the pandemic inflation surge across the United States, Euro Area, and United Kingdom, isolating three successive phases:

  1. Phase 1: Commodity and Sectoral Shortage Shocks (2021–early 2022): The initial inflation acceleration was overwhelmingly driven by large positive shocks to energy and agricultural commodity prices, combined with unprecedented supply-chain bottlenecks and goods shortages (e.g. automotive semiconductors). Because consumers shifted demand from in-person services to physical goods while supply networks were snarled, goods prices surged without offsetting service price declines.
  2. Phase 2: Labor Market Tightness and Wage Catch-up (2022–2023): As commodity prices stabilized, inflation persistence shifted to the labor market. Unprecedented labor tightness—measured by the ratio of job vacancies to unemployed workers (\(v/u\)), which reached a historic peak of \(1.9\) in early 2022—generated wage pressures as workers sought to recover lost real purchasing power.
  3. Phase 3: Rapid Disinflation without Massive Unemployment (2023–2024): Inflation subsequently fell rapidly from \(9.1\%\) toward \(3.0\%\) without an accompanying surge in unemployment. Bernanke & Blanchard (2025) show that because the initial surge was primarily driven by relative price shocks rather than entrenched long-run inflation expectations, the unwinding of supply bottlenecks and lower commodity prices allowed disinflation without the severe recessions that characterized the 1970s and 1980s.

from __future__ import annotations


def calculate_hlw_natural_rate(
    trend_growth_g: float,
    time_preference_z: float,
) -> float:
    """Calculate natural real rate r* = g + z per Holston, Laubach, and Williams (2017)."""
    return trend_growth_g + time_preference_z


def bernanke_blanchard_inflation_decomposition(
    initial_inflation: float,
    energy_price_growth: float,
    shortage_index: float,
    labor_tightness_vu: float,  # vacancies / unemployed ratio
    beta_energy: float = 0.08,
    beta_shortage: float = 0.15,
    beta_labor: float = 0.65,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Decompose headline inflation surge into energy, supply bottlenecks, and labor market tightness."""
    contrib_energy = beta_energy * energy_price_growth
    contrib_shortage = beta_shortage * shortage_index
    contrib_labor = beta_labor * max(0.0, labor_tightness_vu - 1.0)  # tightness above balanced ratio 1.0

    total_predicted = initial_inflation + contrib_energy + contrib_shortage + contrib_labor

    return {
        "baseline_inflation": initial_inflation,
        "energy_contribution": contrib_energy,
        "shortage_contribution": contrib_shortage,
        "labor_tightness_contribution": contrib_labor,
        "total_predicted_inflation": total_predicted,
    }

    # 1970s vs 2010s comparison
    r_star_1975 = calculate_hlw_natural_rate(trend_growth_g=2.80, time_preference_z=0.70)
    r_star_2019 = calculate_hlw_natural_rate(trend_growth_g=1.10, time_preference_z=-0.60)

    print("Holston, Laubach & Williams (2017) Natural Rate (r*) Trends:")

    print(f"  Secular decline in r*: {r_star_2019 - r_star_1975:.2f} percentage points")

    # Part 2: Bernanke & Blanchard (2025) 2021-2023 Inflation Surge Decomposition
    # Calibration for peak US inflation in 2022 (CPI ~ 8.5%)

    print(f"  Energy commodity shock:     +{decomp_2022['energy_contribution']:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Supply chain shortages:     +{decomp_2022['shortage_contribution']:.2f}%")

Where the Textbook Canon Breaks Down

  • Massive Real-Time Imprecision of \(r^*\): Standard monetary textbooks present \(r^*\) as a crisp, well-measured constant in the Taylor rule. In reality, Kalman filter estimates of \(r^*\) have enormous statistical confidence bands: the 95% confidence interval for \(r^*\) in the Holston-Laubach-Williams model typically spans \(\pm 1.5\) to \(\pm 2.0\) percentage points. Real-time revisions in \(r^*\) frequently exceed the size of typical monetary policy moves (25 to 50 bps).
  • The "Transitory" Miscalculation: In 2021, major central banks (the Federal Reserve, ECB, Bank of England) universally diagnosed the inflation surge as "transitory," expecting bottleneck effects to dissolve within months without requiring policy tightening. They failed to anticipate that massive fiscal stimulus would sustain aggregate demand long enough for goods shortages to pass through into nominal wage demands and services inflation.
  • Monetary vs. Fiscal Inflation Attribution: The New Keynesian consensus attributed post-pandemic inflation almost exclusively to monetary accommodation and supply disruptions. The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level (Cochrane 2023) argues that the $5 trillion in unbacked US pandemic fiscal transfers directly debased the purchasing power of debt, demonstrating that monetary policy alone cannot anchor prices without fiscal discipline.

Common Misconceptions

  • \(r^*\) is an interest rate set by the central bank at FOMC meetings.

    The central bank sets the nominal policy rate \(i_t\). The natural rate \(r^*\) is determined by structural real fundamentals—productivity growth, demographics, savings preferences, and global capital flows. The central bank adjusts its policy rate relative to \(r^*\) to stabilize inflation and output.

  • Under FTPL, inflation occurs because the central bank monetizes the debt by printing currency.

    Under FTPL, inflation occurs even if the central bank conducts zero money creation. When the public holds more government bonds than can be repaid by future primary surpluses, bondholders attempt to spend their wealth on real goods and services, driving up the price level until real debt equals expected surpluses.

  • The 2021–2023 inflation surge was solely caused by an overheated US labor market.

    Econometric decompositions (Bernanke & Blanchard 2025) show that energy spikes and supply shortages accounted for the vast majority of headline inflation during 2021 and early 2022. Labor market tightness (\(v/u\)) became the dominant driver only later in 2022 and 2023.

Practice Problems

Problem 1.1: Holston-Laubach-Williams Natural Rate

In a Kalman filter estimation of the HLW model, the estimated trend productivity growth is \(\hat{g} = 1.25\%\) and the estimated persistent non-growth factor is \(\hat{z} = -0.45\%\). If expected inflation over the coming year is \(\pi^e = 2.0\%\), what nominal policy rate \(i_t\) represents a neutral monetary policy stance?

Show solution

Under the HLW model, the natural real rate of interest is: \[ r^* = g + z = 1.25\% + (-0.45\%) = 0.80\% \] A neutral monetary policy stance sets the real policy rate equal to \(r^*\): \[ r_t = i_t - \pi^e = r^* \implies i_t = r^* + \pi^e = 0.80\% + 2.00\% = 2.80\% \] A nominal policy rate of 2.80% represents a perfectly neutral stance. Any rate above 2.80% is contractionary.

Problem 1.2: FTPL Debt Valuation and Price Level Jump

Outstanding nominal government debt is \(B = \$25\text{ trillion}\). Initially, the price level is \(P_0 = 1.0\) and the expected present value of future primary surpluses is \(PV(s) = \$25\text{ trillion}\). The government enacts a permanent, unbacked fiscal transfer of \(\Delta B = \$5\text{ trillion}\) with no change in expected future primary surpluses. Calculate the new equilibrium price level \(P_1\) and the cumulative inflation rate.

Show solution

Under FTPL, the valuation equation must hold: \[ \frac{B_1}{P_1} = PV(s) \] With \(B_1 = 25 + 5 = \$30\text{ trillion}\) and \(PV(s) = \$25\text{ trillion}\): \[ P_1 = \frac{B_1}{PV(s)} = \frac{30}{25} = 1.20 \] The cumulative inflation rate is: \[ \pi = \frac{P_1 - P_0}{P_0} = \frac{1.20 - 1.00}{1.00} = +20.0\% \] The price level jumps by exactly 20% to debase the real value of debt back to the unchanged present value of surpluses.

Problem 1.3: Bernanke-Blanchard Inflation Calculation

Using the calibrated Bernanke & Blanchard model: baseline inflation is \(2.0\%\), energy price growth is \(+40\%\) (coefficient \(0.08\)), shortage index is \(15.0\) (coefficient \(0.15\)), and vacancy-to-unemployment ratio is \(v/u = 1.80\) with balanced baseline \(1.0\) (coefficient \(0.65\)). Compute the total predicted inflation.

Show solution

Calculate the individual components: - Energy contribution: \(0.08 \times 40 = 3.20\%\) - Shortage contribution: \(0.15 \times 15.0 = 2.25\%\) - Labor market tightness: \(0.65 \times (1.80 - 1.0) = 0.65 \times 0.80 = 0.52\%\) Total predicted inflation: \[ \pi = 2.00\% + 3.20\% + 2.25\% + 0.52\% = 7.97\% \] Energy and shortages together account for \(5.45\%\) of the \(5.97\%\) excess inflation above the \(2\%\) target.

Problem 2.1: Price Level Determination under Passive Monetary Policy

Prove that under a pegged nominal interest rate \(i_t = \bar{i}\) (passive monetary policy) and active fiscal policy (exogenous primary surpluses \(s_t\)), the Fisher equation and the government debt valuation equation uniquely determine the initial price level \(P_0\).

Show solution

Under a pegged nominal rate \(\bar{i}\), the Fisher relation with real rate \(r\) implies expected inflation \(\E_t[\pi_{t+1}] = \bar{i} - r\). In a standard New Keynesian model, an interest rate peg results in price-level indeterminacy because any inflation path satisfying the Fisher equation is an equilibrium. However, the FTPL intertemporal debt condition requires: \[ \frac{B_{-1}}{P_0} = \sum_{j=0}^\infty \left(\frac{1}{1+r}\right)^j s_j \] Since \(B_{-1}\) is predetermined and \(\{s_j\}\) is exogenously determined by the fiscal authority, the right-hand side is a unique strictly positive real number \(V_0 > 0\). Thus: \[ P_0 = \frac{B_{-1}}{V_0} \] This uniquely pins down the initial price level, resolving the nominal indeterminacy of an interest rate peg.

Problem 2.2: Median Unbiased Estimation of Variance Ratios

In the HLW model, standard maximum likelihood estimation of the state variance \(\sigma_g^2\) suffers from the "pile-up problem" where \(\hat{\sigma}_g = 0\) with high probability. Explain why Stock & Watson's (1998) median unbiased estimator resolves this and derive the state-space Kalman update equation for potential output.

Show solution

When the signal-to-noise ratio \(\lambda_g = \sigma_g / \sigma_{\tilde{y}}\) is small, the likelihood function has zero derivative at the boundary \(\lambda_g = 0\), causing ML to pile up at zero even when true trend growth is time-varying. The Stock-Watson median-unbiased estimator inverts the median of a supremum Wald test statistic to obtain consistent variance ratio estimates. Given \(\lambda_g\), the Kalman filter state update for potential output is: \[ y_{t|t}^* = y_{t|t-1}^* + K_t \left( y_t - y_{t|t-1}^* \right) \] where \(K_t = P_{t|t-1} / (P_{t|t-1} + \sigma_{\tilde{y}}^2)\) is the Kalman gain, weighting the prior forecast against incoming GDP observations.

Problem 2.3: Labor Tightness and Beveridge Curve Shifts

During the pandemic recovery, the US Beveridge curve (vacancies vs. unemployment) shifted outward dramatically. Show how an outward shift in the matching function \(m(u, v) = \mu u^\alpha v^{1-\alpha}\) (a decline in matching efficiency \(\mu\)) drives up the equilibrium \(v/u\) ratio for a given level of unemployment, amplifying wage pressures.

Show solution

In a Diamond-Mortensen-Pissarides search model, job finding rate is \(f(\theta) = m(u, v)/u = \mu \theta^{1-\alpha}\), where \(\theta = v/u\) is labor market tightness. To maintain a target level of employment and hires \(H\), the required tightness is: \[ \theta = \left(\frac{H}{\mu u}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \] When pandemic labor mismatch and sectoral reallocation reduce matching efficiency \(\mu \downarrow\), achieving the same hiring rate requires a dramatic increase in vacancies: \[ \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial \mu} = -\frac{1}{1-\alpha} \mu^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{1-\alpha}} \left(\frac{H}{u}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} < 0 \] Thus, lower matching efficiency drove \(v/u\) to historical highs of 1.9, generating acute wage pressure despite moderate unemployment rates.

Problem 3.1: Secular Stagnation vs. Post-2022 Natural Rate Rebound

Prior to 2020, the consensus view was that \(r^*\) had entered a permanent era of "secular stagnation" near 0%. Post-2022, long-term real forward rates (e.g. 5-year/5-year forward real yields) rebounded to 1.5%–2.0%. Evaluate whether this rebound reflects a structural shift in \(g\) or \(z\), or term premia distortions.

Show solution

Under \(r^* = g + z\), a rebound to ~1.8% could reflect: 1. Higher trend productivity growth \(g\): massive capital investment in artificial intelligence, robotics, and energy transition. 2. Structural fiscal deficits: persistent peacetime primary deficits of 5%–7% of GDP increase sovereign debt supply, absorbing global savings and raising \(z\). 3. Higher term premia: inflation volatility and fiscal sustainability risks widen term premia on long-term bonds, elevating forward yields without necessarily shifting the true spot \(r^*\). Empirical HLW updates through 2024 indicate only a modest increase in \(r^*\) (to ~1.0%), suggesting higher forward yields largely reflect term premia and fiscal supply pressures.

Problem 3.2: Accounting for the $5 Trillion US Pandemic Stimulus

Between March 2020 and March 2021, the US federal government enacted roughly $5 trillion in cumulative fiscal relief (CARES Act, Consolidated Appropriations Act, American Rescue Plan). If nominal GDP was $22 trillion and initial debt held by the public was $20 trillion, apply Cochrane's FTPL framework to calculate the implied cumulative price level adjustment if none of the $5 trillion is paid back by future surpluses.

Show solution

Under FTPL, the real debt valuation condition requires \(\frac{B_0 + \Delta B}{P_1} = PV(s)\). Assuming initial debt was fully backed (\(PV(s) = B_0 = \$20\text{ trillion}\)) and \(\Delta B = \$5\text{ trillion}\) is completely unbacked: \[ P_1 = \frac{20 + 5}{20} = 1.25 \] This implies a 25% cumulative increase in the price level over the medium term. Between 2019:Q4 and 2024:Q1, the US CPI actually rose by approximately 21.5%, closely matching the FTPL prediction of unbacked debt debasement.

Problem 3.3: Why Was the 2023–24 Disinflation Relatively Painless?

Historically, disinflations engineered by the Federal Reserve (e.g., Volcker in 1981–82) required steep recessions with unemployment rising above 10%. Using the Bernanke & Blanchard (2025) framework, explain why US inflation fell from 9.1% to near 3% while unemployment remained below 4%.

Show solution

Bernanke & Blanchard show that unlike 1979–81: 1. Long-term inflation expectations remained firmly anchored around 2.0%–2.5%, preventing wage-price spirals from becoming self-fulfilling. 2. The initial inflation spike was driven by commodity prices and supply chain bottlenecks, not trend wage growth. As global supply chains normalized and energy prices reversed, headline inflation fell mechanically. 3. Labor market rebalancing occurred along the downward-sloping segment of the Beveridge curve: job vacancies declined from 12 million to 8.5 million without substantial layoffs, cooling \(v/u\) without generating mass unemployment.

Problem 4.1: Shadow Rate Estimation at the Effective Lower Bound

When nominal rates are constrained by the ZLB, the effective stance of policy cannot be measured by \(i_t\). Analyze the Wu-Xia (2016) shadow rate framework and examine how incorporating the shadow rate alters Kalman filter estimates of \(r^*\) during 2008–2015.

Show solution

The Wu-Xia shadow rate \(s_t = \min(i_t, \underline{i}) + \text{option value}\) captures the stimulative effect of quantitative easing and forward guidance, dipping to as low as \(-3.0\%\) during 2013–2014. If an econometrician inputs the bounded nominal rate \(i_t \approx 0.10\%\) into the dynamic IS equation during the ZLB, the model interprets the slow output recovery as evidence that \(r^*\) must have collapsed to extreme negative territory. Using the shadow rate accounts for unconventional easing, yielding a more stable, less negative \(r^*\) estimate.

Problem 4.2: FTPL in a Multi-Country Currency Union

Formulate the Fiscal Theory of the Price Level for a currency union (the Euro Area) with a single central bank (ECB) and \(N\) sovereign fiscal authorities with heterogeneous debt levels \(B_i\) and primary surpluses \(s_i\). What prevents fiscal profligacy in one member state from debasing the common currency?

Show solution

In a currency union, the aggregate valuation condition is: \[ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N B_{i, t-1}}{P_t} = \E_t \sum_{j=0}^\infty \mathcal{M}_{t, t+j} \left( \sum_{i=1}^N s_{i, t+j} \right) \] If country \(k\) runs unbacked deficits, but other member states or the union-wide treaty (Maastricht / Stability and Growth Pact) enforces a no-bailout rule, country \(k\)'s debt becomes subject to sovereign default risk rather than currency debasement: \[ \frac{(1 - d_k) B_{k, t-1}}{P_t} = \E_t \sum_{j=0}^\infty \mathcal{M}_{t, t+j} s_{k, t+j} \] Sovereign default haircuts \(d_k\) absorb the fiscal shortfall, protecting the union-wide price level \(P_t\) from debasement.

Problem 4.3: Artificial Intelligence and the Future Trajectory of \(r^*\)

Construct a theoretical growth model where generative AI increases the annual total factor productivity (TFP) growth rate by 1.0 percentage point. Under standard Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans preferences with intertemporal elasticity of substitution \(\sigma = 0.5\), calculate the permanent shift in \(r^*\) and discuss the central bank balance-sheet implications.

Show solution

In the steady state of a Ramsey model, the natural rate is given by the Euler equation: \[ r^* = \rho + \frac{1}{\sigma} g \] where \(\rho\) is the subjective rate of time preference. If TFP growth increases the steady-state output growth rate \(g\) by \(\Delta g = 1.0\% = 0.01\) and \(\sigma = 0.5\): \[ \Delta r^* = \frac{1}{\sigma} \Delta g = \frac{1}{0.5} \times 1.0\% = +2.0\% \] A 200 bps increase in \(r^*\) expands nominal policy rate headroom from ~3% to ~5%, virtually eliminating the risk of falling into the zero lower bound. However, it also permanently increases sovereign debt financing costs, requiring higher primary surpluses to avoid FTPL inflationary pressures.

    Latent state estimation of \(r^*\) is plagued by statistical imprecision; 95% confidence bands often exceed 300 basis points, making real-time policy benchmarking treacherous.

  8. Why did inflation fall rapidly in 2023 without triggering a major surge in unemployment?

    Options

    Because inflation was driven by relative commodity/supply shocks rather than unanchored expectations, the unwinding of supply chains and lower oil prices drove rapid disinflation.
