Operating Frameworks: Corridor vs. Floor Systems

Modern central banks implement monetary policy through operating frameworks that translate policy targets into market rates. Contrasting scarce-reserve corridor systems with abundant-reserve floor systems illuminates how central banks manage liquidity, why non-bank segmentation creates the Bech-Klee arbitrage spread below IORB, and how overnight reverse repurchase facilities enforce an effective floor.

1. The Corridor Framework with Scarce Reserves

Before the 2008 global financial crisis, major central banks (including the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, and the Reserve Bank of Australia) implemented monetary policy using floor vs. corridor operating frameworks. In a corridor framework, the central bank maintains structural reserve scarcity and establishes two standing facilities that bound the overnight interbank rate within an administered corridor:

  1. Standing Lending Facility (Ceiling): The central bank stands ready to lend reserves overnight against eligible collateral at rate \(i_{\text{lend}}\) (e.g. the discount window / primary credit rate). No bank will pay a higher rate in the interbank market: \(r_t \le i_{\text{lend}}\).
  2. Standing Deposit Facility (Floor): Depository institutions can place surplus reserves overnight at rate \(i_{\text{dep}}\). No bank will lend in the interbank market at a lower rate: \(r_t \ge i_{\text{dep}}\).

The width of the corridor is \(i_{\text{lend}} - i_{\text{dep}}\) (typically 50 to 200 basis points). The target policy rate sits at the center. Because aggregate reserves are scarce, the central bank conducts daily open market operations (fine-tuning repos or reverse repos) to shift the vertical reserve supply schedule so that it intersects the downward-sloping reserve demand curve exactly at the target rate.

2. The Floor Framework with Abundant Reserves

As documented by Bernanke (2020), large-scale asset purchase programs (Quantitative Easing) flooded commercial banks with trillions of dollars in excess reserves, pushing aggregate reserves far past the satiation point \(R^*\). In this abundant reserves environment, the marginal value of liquidity falls to zero.

In a pure floor framework, the central bank does not adjust reserve quantities daily. Instead, it adjusts the administered interest rate paid on reserve balances: \[ i_{\text{IORB}} = r_{\text{target}} \] Because commercial banks earn \(i_{\text{IORB}}\) risk-free on every additional dollar of reserves, they have no incentive to lend in the overnight interbank market at any lower rate. The policy rate is controlled directly through the remuneration rate, completely decoupling interest rate policy from central bank balance sheet expansion.

3. Market Segmentation and the Bech-Klee Arbitrage Model

In the United States, the federal funds rate traded persistently 5 to 25 basis points below the IORB rate throughout the post-2008 period. This apparent failure of the floor was explained by Bech and Klee (2011) through financial market institutional segmentation.

Theorem 4.1 (Bech-Klee Arbitrage Equilibrium). In the US interbank market:

  1. Government-Sponsored Enterprises (GSEs, such as the Federal Home Loan Banks, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac) maintain master reserve accounts at the Federal Reserve but are statutory non-banks ineligible to earn IORB.
  2. Commercial depository institutions are eligible to earn IORB on reserve balances, but incur balance-sheet costs \(c > 0\) (such as FDIC regulatory assessment fees and Basel III Supplementary Leverage Ratio capital charges) for holding reserves.
  3. Eligible GSEs lend surplus cash to commercial banks at the effective federal funds rate \(r_{\text{eff}}\). Nash bargaining over the net surplus \(i_{\text{IORB}} - c - i_{\text{outside}}\) with GSE bargaining weight \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\) produces the equilibrium market rate: \[ r_{\text{eff}} = i_{\text{outside}} + \lambda (i_{\text{IORB}} - c - i_{\text{outside}}) < i_{\text{IORB}} \] where \(i_{\text{outside}}\) is the GSE's outside investment return (the Overnight Reverse Repo rate \(i_{\text{ON RRP}}\)).

To establish a firm lower bound beneath non-bank cash suppliers, the Federal Reserve introduced the Overnight Reverse Repurchase (ON RRP) facility in 2013. The ON RRP facility allows eligible non-bank financial institutions (GSEs and money market mutual funds) to invest cash directly with the Fed at an administered sub-floor rate \(i_{\text{ON RRP}}\). This sets \(i_{\text{outside}} = i_{\text{ON RRP}}\), bounding the discount below IORB and establishing the modern 'two-rate floor' operating framework.

"""Lab E7-L04-1: Operating Frameworks: Corridor vs Floor Systems and Bech-Klee Arbitrage.

Models the equilibrium interbank overnight rate under:
1. Symmetric corridor framework (standing lending facility ceiling, deposit facility floor, scarce reserves)
2. Floor framework (abundant reserves, IORB, and non-bank GSE cash suppliers per Bech-Klee 2011)

Calculates the equilibrium trading volume and the Bech-Klee arbitrage spread:
r_eff = (1 - lambda) * r_IOR + lambda * r_bargain
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_corridor_equilibrium(
    reserve_supply: float,
    reserve_target: float,
    rate_lending: float,
    rate_deposit: float,
    target_rate: float,
) -> float:
    """Interbank clearing rate in a symmetric corridor system."""
    # When supply equals target, clearing rate hits the target exactly
    # Deviations move linearly toward standing facilities
    corridor_width = rate_lending - rate_deposit
    deviation = (reserve_supply - reserve_target) / reserve_target
    rate = target_rate - 0.5 * corridor_width * max(-1.0, min(1.0, deviation))
    return rate


def solve_bech_klee_floor_arbitrage(
    rate_iorb: float,  # Rate paid to commercial banks on reserves
    rate_on_rrp: float,  # Sub-floor offered to non-bank financial institutions (e.g. GSEs, MMFs)
    bank_balance_sheet_cost: float,  # Leverage ratio / FDIC assessment fee per dollar of reserves
    bargaining_power_nonbank: float,  # Nash bargaining weight lambda of cash lenders
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate the equilibrium federal funds rate below IORB in a floor system with non-banks.

    Per Bech and Klee (2011): Government-Sponsored Enterprises (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHLBs)
    hold deposits at the Fed but are ineligible to earn IORB. Commercial banks borrow from GSEs
    at rate r_eff and deposit at the Fed earning r_iorb, incurring balance-sheet cost c.
    GSEs receive outside option rate_on_rrp.
    Surplus = rate_iorb - bank_balance_sheet_cost - rate_on_rrp.
    """
    surplus = rate_iorb - bank_balance_sheet_cost - rate_on_rrp
    rate_eff = rate_on_rrp if surplus < 0 else rate_on_rrp + bargaining_power_nonbank * surplus

    arbitrage_margin_bank = rate_iorb - rate_eff - bank_balance_sheet_cost
    spread_below_iorb = rate_iorb - rate_eff

    return {
        "rate_eff": rate_eff,
        "spread_below_iorb_bps": spread_below_iorb * 100.0,
        "bank_arbitrage_margin_bps": arbitrage_margin_bank * 100.0,
        "net_surplus_bps": surplus * 100.0,
    }


def main() -> None:
    # 1. Corridor system benchmark
    r_lend = 5.25
    r_dep = 4.25
    r_target = 4.75
    r_corridor_scarce = solve_corridor_equilibrium(95.0, 100.0, r_lend, r_dep, r_target)

    # 2. Floor system with Bech-Klee non-bank segmentation
    r_iorb = 5.40
    r_on_rrp = 5.30
    c_cost = 0.05  # 5 bps regulatory balance sheet cost
    lambda_gse = 0.40  # GSE bargaining power

    bk = solve_bech_klee_floor_arbitrage(r_iorb, r_on_rrp, c_cost, lambda_gse)

    print("Operating Frameworks Comparison:")
    print("Corridor Framework (Scarce Reserves):")
    print(f"  Ceiling (Lending facility): {r_lend:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Floor (Deposit facility):   {r_dep:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Market rate (5% scarcity):  {r_corridor_scarce:.4f}%")

    print("\nFloor Framework with Bech-Klee (2011) Market Segmentation:")
    print(f"  IORB rate (Commercial bank floor): {r_iorb:.2f}%")
    print(f"  ON RRP rate (Non-bank sub-floor):  {r_on_rrp:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Effective interbank rate:          {bk['rate_eff']:.4f}%")
    print(f"  Discount below IORB:               {bk['spread_below_iorb_bps']:.1f} bps")
    print(f"  Commercial bank net margin:        {bk['bank_arbitrage_margin_bps']:.1f} bps")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Operating framework models frequently make institutional assumptions that oversimplify money market plumbing:

  • Homogeneous market access: Textbook models assume all financial institutions access standing facilities on equal terms. In reality, modern money markets are heavily segmented between banks and shadow-banking non-banks.
  • Zero regulatory balance sheet costs: Perfect arbitrage requires costless balance-sheet expansion. Post-crisis regulations (SLR, G-SIB surcharges, FDIC fees) impose non-trivial balance sheet costs that widen arbitrage spreads.
  • Frictionless collateral velocity: Secured money markets (repo) require pristine collateral (Treasuries). Haircut spikes or collateral scarcity can break rate transmission even when aggregate bank reserves are abundant.

Misconceptions

  1. Trading below the IORB floor represents an illegal violation of Federal Reserve statutory rules.

    Trading below IORB is a natural market outcome of non-bank financial institutions (such as FHLBs) lacking statutory eligibility to earn interest on reserve balances.

  2. In a corridor system, the central bank directly sets every single overnight market interest rate by decree.

    The central bank sets only the administered standing facility rates; the market rate clears competitively based on daily reserve supply and demand fluctuations.

  3. Returning from a floor framework to a corridor framework is a simple administrative decree.

    Re-establishing a scarce-reserve corridor requires draining trillions of dollars of reserves through active balance sheet runoff or massive term repo operations without disrupting repo stability.

Problem Set

Problem 4.1 (Corridor Target Rate). A central bank operates a symmetric corridor with lending facility rate \(i_{\text{lend}} = 4.50\%\) and deposit facility rate \(i_{\text{dep}} = 3.50\%\). If the reserve target is $100B but actual reserves are $95B, calculate the clearing rate if the sensitivity is 0.5 times the corridor width.

Solution

The midpoint target is \(i^* = (4.50 + 3.50)/2 = 4.00\%\). Corridor width is \(W = 1.00\%\). With a 5% reserve shortage: \[ r = i^* - 0.5 \times W \times \left( \frac{95 - 100}{100} \right) = 4.00 - 0.5 \times 1.00 \times (-0.05) = 4.00 + 0.025 = 4.025\% \] The market rate rises by 2.5 basis points above target.

Problem 4.2 (Bech-Klee Rate Calculation). If IORB is \(5.25\%\), ON RRP is \(5.05\%\), bank balance sheet cost is \(c = 0.04\%\), and GSE bargaining power is \(\lambda = 0.35\), calculate the effective federal funds rate \(r_{\text{eff}}\).

Solution

The net surplus is \(S = i_{\text{IORB}} - c - i_{\text{ON RRP}} = 5.25 - 0.04 - 5.05 = 0.16\% = 16\text{ bps}\). Under Nash bargaining: \[ r_{\text{eff}} = i_{\text{ON RRP}} + \lambda S = 5.05 + 0.35 \times 0.16 = 5.05 + 0.056 = 5.106\% \] The effective rate clears at 5.106%, trading 14.4 bps below IORB.

Problem 4.3 (Commercial Bank Arbitrage Margin). For the parameters in Problem 4.2, calculate the net profit margin captured by commercial banks borrowing from GSEs and depositing at the Fed.

Solution

The bank's net margin is: \[ \text{Margin} = i_{\text{IORB}} - r_{\text{eff}} - c = 5.25 - 5.106 - 0.04 = 0.104\% = 10.4\text{ bps} \] Commercial banks earn 10.4 basis points of net profit per dollar borrowed.

Problem 4.4 (Bounds on Bech-Klee Rate). Prove that the effective rate in the Bech-Klee framework is strictly bounded: \(i_{\text{ON RRP}} \le r_{\text{eff}} \le i_{\text{IORB}} - c\) for any \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\).

Solution

From \(r_{\text{eff}} = i_{\text{ON RRP}} + \lambda (i_{\text{IORB}} - c - i_{\text{ON RRP}})\): When \(\lambda = 0\) (banks hold all bargaining power), \(r_{\text{eff}} = i_{\text{ON RRP}}\). When \(\lambda = 1\) (GSEs hold all bargaining power), \(r_{\text{eff}} = i_{\text{ON RRP}} + (i_{\text{IORB}} - c - i_{\text{ON RRP}}) = i_{\text{IORB}} - c\). Because \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\) and \(i_{\text{IORB}} - c > i_{\text{ON RRP}}\), \(r_{\text{eff}}\) lies strictly between the outside option floor and the net bank return ceiling.

Problem 4.5 (Elimination of Arbitrage under Equal Access). Prove that if GSEs are granted legal eligibility to earn IORB, the Bech-Klee arbitrage spread collapses to zero.

Solution

If GSEs can earn IORB directly, their outside option is \(i_{\text{outside}} = i_{\text{IORB}}\). No GSE would lend in the interbank market for any rate \(r < i_{\text{IORB}}\). Commercial banks cannot pay \(r > i_{\text{IORB}} - c\). Since \(i_{\text{IORB}} > i_{\text{IORB}} - c\), interbank borrowing by banks from GSEs ceases entirely, and the floor binds perfectly at \(i_{\text{IORB}}\).

Problem 4.6 (Corridor Asymmetry). Show that an asymmetric corridor where \(i_{\text{lend}} - i^* > i^* - i_{\text{dep}}\) induces an upward bias in interbank rate volatility under symmetric reserve shocks.

Solution

If the ceiling is further from target than the floor (\(\Delta_{\text{ceil}} > \Delta_{\text{floor}}\)), a reserve deficit of size \(\delta\) pushes the rate upward by up to \(\Delta_{\text{ceil}}\), while an equivalent reserve surplus pushes the rate downward by only \(\Delta_{\text{floor}}\). Under symmetric reserve shocks, the rate distribution is positively skewed with higher upward volatility.

Problem 4.7 (FHLB Dominance in Fed Funds). Federal Reserve data indicates that Federal Home Loan Banks (FHLBs) account for over 80% of overnight lending in the fed funds market. Why do domestic commercial banks rarely lend to other commercial banks in the fed funds market today?

Solution

Domestic commercial banks earn IORB directly at the Fed without taking credit risk. Lending to another commercial bank requires taking private credit and liquidity risk; therefore, no commercial bank will lend uncollateralized below IORB. Only institutions lacking IORB eligibility (primarily FHLBs) lend in the fed funds market.

Problem 4.8 (SLR Exemption Impact). In April 2020, the Fed temporarily exempted Treasuries and central bank reserves from the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) calculation for holding companies. How did this regulatory relief affect the Bech-Klee balance sheet cost \(c\)?

Solution

Exempting reserves eliminated the regulatory capital charge associated with expanding balance sheets to absorb deposits, driving \(c \to 0\). This expanded bank arbitrage capacity, narrowing the spread between fed funds and IORB.

Problem 4.9 (ON RRP Volume Surge in 2021-2022). In late 2021, daily ON RRP usage surged past $1.5 trillion. What economic conditions caused money market funds to park unprecedented cash in ON RRP rather than Treasury bills or commercial paper?

Solution

Massive Treasury bill supply contractions (due to the debt ceiling) and abundant banking reserves depressed short-term market yields below the Fed's administered ON RRP rate (which was adjusted upward to 5 bps). MMFs allocated surplus cash to the higher-yielding, zero-risk ON RRP facility.

Problem 4.10 (Standing Repo Facility Mechanics). In 2021, the Federal Reserve established the Standing Repo Facility (SRF). How does the SRF restore a ceiling on overnight repo rates in an abundant reserve regime?

Solution

The SRF allows primary dealers and eligible banks to borrow cash overnight against Treasuries, agency debt, and agency MBS at an administered rate set equal to the top of the target range. This acts as an automated ceiling, preventing rate spikes like the September 2019 episode.

Problem 4.11 (Tiered Remuneration in Europe and Japan). During periods of negative policy rates, both the ECB and the Bank of Japan implemented tiered deposit remuneration. Explain why tiering was necessary.

Solution

Under negative interest rates, taxing 100% of excess reserves would severely impair commercial bank net interest margins and lending capacity. Tiering exempts a baseline volume of reserves from negative rates, preserving the marginal incentive to lend while safeguarding bank capital.

Problem 4.12 (Optimal Corridor Width). Analyze the trade-off in setting the corridor width \(W = i_{\text{lend}} - i_{\text{dep}}\) in a corridor system.

Solution

A narrow corridor reduces interbank interest rate volatility and protects banks from liquidity squeezes, but disincentivizes private market interbank monitoring and trading. A wide corridor fosters private market development but exposes banks to high liquidity risk.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What bounds the policy rate from above in a corridor operating framework?

    Options

    No commercial bank will pay more in the market than the rate at which it can borrow directly from the central bank.

  2. Why did the federal funds rate trade below IORB in the post-2008 floor framework?

    Options

    GSEs lack IORB eligibility and lend cash below IORB to banks who capture the arbitrage spread.

  3. If IORB is 5.25%, ON RRP is 5.05%, bank balance sheet cost is 0.04%, and GSE bargaining power is 0.35, what is r_eff (in %)?

    \(r_{\text{eff}} = 5.05 + 0.35(5.25 - 0.04 - 5.05) = 5.05 + 0.056 = 5.106\%\).

  4. What facility was introduced by the Federal Reserve to establish a firm sub-floor for non-banks?

    Options

    The ON RRP facility provides an administered floor for money market funds and GSEs.

  5. How does an abundant-reserve floor framework affect open market operations?

    Options

    In a floor system, rates are controlled by administered interest on reserves rather than daily quantity adjustments.

  6. If IORB is 5.25%, r_eff is 5.106%, and bank cost is 0.04%, what is the bank net margin in basis points?

    \((5.25 - 5.106 - 0.04) \times 100 = 10.4\text{ bps}\).

  7. What happens to the policy rate in a scarce corridor system if the central bank fails to offset a large tax drain?

    Options

    In a scarce regime, an unsterilized reserve drain pushes the market up the steep demand curve.

  8. Why do bank balance sheet costs (c) reduce the rate paid by commercial banks to GSEs?

    Options

    Regulatory capital costs shrink the total arbitrage surplus, pulling the market rate further below IORB.
