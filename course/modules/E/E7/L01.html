Rules, Discretion, and Time Inconsistency

Monetary policy cannot be treated as a purely static engineering problem. When forward-looking private agents form rational expectations, a benevolent central bank operating under period-by-period discretion faces a destructive time inconsistency: the incentive to engineer surprise inflation to boost employment generates an inefficient inflation bias without achieving any systematic output gains.

1. The Time Inconsistency Framework

The time inconsistency of optimal policy was discovered by Kydland & Prescott (1977). A policy plan chosen at date \(0\) that is optimal from the perspective of date \(0\) ceases to be optimal at a future date \(t > 0\) once private economic expectations have already been locked into nominal contracts, tempting the policymaker to renege.

Definition 1.1 (Time Inconsistency). Let \(\pi_t\) denote the central bank's policy choice (inflation) and \(\pi_t^e = \E_{t-1}[\pi_t]\) denote the rational expectation formed by private agents prior to date \(t\). A policy plan \(\pi_t^*(\pi_t^e)\) is dynamically consistent if and only if: \[ \pi_t^*(\pi_t^e) = \arg\min_{\pi_t} \mathcal{L}(\pi_t, \pi_t^e) \] evaluated taking \(\pi_t^e\) as predetermined. If the policymaker would choose a different inflation rate \(\pi_t \neq \pi_t^*\) after \(\pi_t^e\) is set, the optimal commitment rule is time inconsistent.

Following Kydland & Prescott (1977), consider an economy where real aggregate output \(y_t\) is determined by an expectations-augmented Phillips curve:

\[ y_t = y_n + \alpha (\pi_t - \pi_t^e) + \varepsilon_t \]

where \(y_n\) is the natural rate of output, \(\alpha > 0\) is the Phillips curve slope, and \(\varepsilon_t\) is a mean-zero supply shock with variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Private wage-setters form expectations \(\pi_t^e\) prior to the realization of \(\varepsilon_t\) and the central bank's choice of \(\pi_t\).

2. Social Loss and the Central Bank Objective

Society suffers quadratic losses from deviations of inflation from zero and from deviations of output from an ambitious target \(y^* = k y_n\), where \(k > 1\):

\[ \mathcal{L}(y_t, \pi_t) = \frac{1}{2} (y_t - k y_n)^2 + \frac{\gamma}{2} \pi_t^2 \]

where \(\gamma > 0\) governs the relative social weight on price stability versus output stabilization. The parameter \(k > 1\) reflects distortionary labor taxation, union wage markups, or monopolistic competition, which keep the natural rate of unemployment above the socially optimal level.

Theorem 1.1 (Commitment vs Discretion). Under rational expectations:

  1. Under binding commitment (Ramsey rule), the central bank chooses a policy rule before expectations form. The equilibrium inflation and output are: \[ \pi_t^{\text{commit}} = -\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} \varepsilon_t, \quad y_t^{\text{commit}} = y_n + \frac{\gamma}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} \varepsilon_t \] yielding an expected inflation of \(\E[\pi_t^{\text{commit}}] = 0\).
  2. Under pure discretion (Nash equilibrium), the central bank chooses \(\pi_t\) taking \(\pi_t^e\) as fixed. The equilibrium inflation is: \[ \pi_t^{\text{disc}} = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}(k - 1) y_n - \frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} \varepsilon_t \] yielding an expected inflation of \(\E[\pi_t^{\text{disc}}] = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}(k - 1) y_n > 0\).

Proof. Consider first the discretionary regime. Taking \(\pi_t^e\) as given, the central bank minimizes expected loss: \[ \min_{\pi_t} \left\{ \frac{1}{2} [y_n + \alpha(\pi_t - \pi_t^e) + \varepsilon_t - k y_n]^2 + \frac{\gamma}{2} \pi_t^2 \right\} \] The first-order condition with respect to \(\pi_t\) requires: \[ \alpha [y_n + \alpha(\pi_t - \pi_t^e) + \varepsilon_t - k y_n] + \gamma \pi_t = 0 \] Solving for \(\pi_t\): \[ \pi_t = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} \left[ (k - 1) y_n + \alpha \pi_t^e - \varepsilon_t \right] \] Under rational expectations, private agents anticipate this reaction function: \[ \pi_t^e = \E[\pi_t] = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} \left[ (k - 1) y_n + \alpha \pi_t^e \right] \] Multiplying both sides by \((\alpha^2 + \gamma)\) and collecting \(\pi_t^e\): \[ \gamma \pi_t^e = \alpha (k - 1) y_n \implies \pi_t^e = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}(k - 1) y_n \] Substituting \(\pi_t^e\) back into the first-order condition yields the discretionary policy: \[ \pi_t^{\text{disc}} = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}(k - 1) y_n - \frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} \varepsilon_t \] For commitment, the central bank announces a linear rule \(\pi(\varepsilon_t) = a + b \varepsilon_t\). Because \(\pi^e = a\), the expected loss is: \[ \E[\mathcal{L}] = \frac{1}{2} (y_n - k y_n)^2 + \frac{\gamma}{2} a^2 + \frac{1}{2} \E\left[ (\alpha b \varepsilon_t + \varepsilon_t)^2 + \gamma b^2 \varepsilon_t^2 \right] \] Minimizing with respect to \(a\) sets \(a = 0\), establishing \(\E[\pi_t^{\text{commit}}] = 0\). Minimizing with respect to \(b\) yields \(b = -\alpha / (\alpha^2 + \gamma)\). This completes the proof.

3. Policy Rules as a Commitment Device

To overcome time inconsistency, macroeconomists advocate transparent policy rules. In a classic contribution, Taylor (1993) demonstrated that a simple interest rate rule could approximate the optimal commitment path while providing a public benchmark against discretionary political pressure:

\[ i_t = r^* + \pi_t + 0.5(\pi_t - \pi^*) + 0.5(y_t - y_n) \]

where \(r^*\) is the equilibrium real interest rate and \(\pi^*\) is the target inflation rate. As Taylor (1993) showed using historical Federal Reserve data from 1987 to 1992, systematic rules anchor private expectations, eliminating the discretionary inflation bias while retaining flexible stabilization.

Lab E7-L01-1: Numerical Simulation of Discretionary Inflation Bias

Hypothesis: Discretionary optimization results in an inflation bias strictly proportional to \((k - 1) y_n\), generating deadweight welfare loss with zero mean output expansion.

"""Lab E7-L01-1: Time Inconsistency and the Discretionary Inflation Bias.

Analytically solves the Kydland-Prescott (1977) and Barro-Gordon (1983) model under:
1. Pure Discretion (Nash equilibrium where central bank takes expectations as given)
2. Binding Commitment (Ramsey social planner rule)
3. Delegation to a Rogoff (1985) conservative central banker

Verifies that discretion produces positive inflation bias with zero employment gain.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_time_inconsistency_model(
    alpha: float,  # Phillips curve slope: y = y_n + alpha * (pi - pi_e)
    gamma: float,  # Weight on inflation squared in loss: L = (y - k*y_n)^2 + gamma * pi^2
    k: float,  # Output target distortion ratio (k > 1 implies target above natural rate)
    y_n: float,  # Natural rate of output
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate inflation, output, and social loss under commitment vs discretion."""
    # Commitment: The central bank commits to a policy rule before expectations form.
    # Min E[L] subject to pi_e = pi => y = y_n
    # L = (y_n - k*y_n)^2 + gamma * pi^2 => dL/dpi = 2 * gamma * pi = 0 => pi_commit = 0
    pi_commit = 0.0
    y_commit = y_n
    loss_commit = (y_commit - k * y_n) ** 2 + gamma * (pi_commit**2)

    # Discretion: Central bank chooses pi taking pi_e as given.
    # L = (y_n + alpha*(pi - pi_e) - k*y_n)^2 + gamma * pi^2
    # dL/dpi = 2 * alpha * (y_n + alpha*(pi - pi_e) - k*y_n) + 2 * gamma * pi = 0
    # Rational expectations require pi_e = pi in equilibrium:
    # 2 * alpha * (y_n - k*y_n) + 2 * gamma * pi = 0
    # gamma * pi = alpha * (k - 1) * y_n
    # pi_discretion = (alpha / gamma) * (k - 1) * y_n
    pi_discretion = (alpha / gamma) * (k - 1.0) * y_n
    y_discretion = y_n  # Since pi_e = pi, output remains at natural rate!
    loss_discretion = (y_discretion - k * y_n) ** 2 + gamma * (pi_discretion**2)

    inflation_bias = pi_discretion - pi_commit
    welfare_cost = loss_discretion - loss_commit

    return {
        "pi_commit": pi_commit,
        "y_commit": y_commit,
        "loss_commit": loss_commit,
        "pi_discretion": pi_discretion,
        "y_discretion": y_discretion,
        "loss_discretion": loss_discretion,
        "inflation_bias": inflation_bias,
        "welfare_cost": welfare_cost,
    }


def main() -> None:
    alpha = 1.0  # Normalized Phillips curve slope
    gamma = 2.0  # Inflation weight in loss
    k = 1.05  # Target output 5% above natural rate
    y_n = 100.0  # Natural output level

    res = solve_time_inconsistency_model(alpha, gamma, k, y_n)

    print("Kydland-Prescott (1977) Time Inconsistency Model:")
    print(f"  Structural parameters: alpha={alpha:.2f}, gamma={gamma:.2f}, k={k:.2f}, y_n={y_n:.1f}")
    print("\nRamsey Commitment Equilibrium:")
    print(f"  Inflation pi*: {res['pi_commit']:.4f}")
    print(f"  Output y*:     {res['y_commit']:.2f}")
    print(f"  Social loss:   {res['loss_commit']:.4f}")
    print("\nDiscretionary Nash Equilibrium:")
    print(f"  Inflation pi_d: {res['pi_discretion']:.4f}")
    print(f"  Output y_d:     {res['y_discretion']:.2f}")
    print(f"  Social loss:    {res['loss_discretion']:.4f}")
    print("\nDeadweight Discretion Penalty:")
    print(f"  Inflation bias: {res['inflation_bias']:.4f}")
    print(f"  Welfare cost:   {res['welfare_cost']:.4f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical Kydland-Prescott static time inconsistency model rests on simplifying assumptions that break down in practical central banking:

  • Exogenous target wedge: The model assumes the central bank targets output strictly above potential (\(k > 1\)). If central banks understand potential output and target \(k = 1\), the static inflation bias vanishes, yet stabilization bias persists.
  • Single-period horizon: In reality, central banks interact repeatedly with financial markets. Repeated interactions introduce reputational capital that penalizes cheating, altering the incentive structure.
  • Known model parameters: In practice, the natural rate \(y_n\) is unobservable and estimated with massive real-time uncertainty, leading to policy mistakes that mimic discretionary bias (Orphanides 2002).

Misconceptions

  1. Central banks generate inflation bias because they are malevolent or corrupt.

    The Kydland-Prescott model assumes a completely benevolent central bank maximizing social welfare. The inflation bias is a systemic failure of discretion under rational expectations, not a failure of benevolent intent.

  2. Discretionary monetary policy succeeds in boosting average output above the natural rate.

    Under rational expectations, private agents anticipate the central bank's incentive to inflate and raise their wage and price demands accordingly. In equilibrium, surprise inflation is zero on average, and output remains stuck at the natural rate \(y_n\).

  3. A policy rule requires tying the central bank's hands to a fixed constant money supply growth rate.

    Optimal commitment rules are state-contingent: they respond systematically to exogenous shocks (such as \(\varepsilon_t\)) while pre-committing to zero average inflation bias.

Problem Set

Problem 1.1 (Calculating Inflation Bias). Given Phillips curve slope \(\alpha = 0.8\), inflation aversion \(\gamma = 1.6\), natural output \(y_n = 100\), and output target distortion \(k = 1.04\), calculate the analytical discretionary inflation bias.

Solution

Applying the Kydland-Prescott formula: \[ \pi_d = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma} (k - 1) y_n = \frac{0.8}{1.6} \times (1.04 - 1.0) \times 100 = 0.5 \times 4.0 = 2.0\% \] The discretionary policy yields an equilibrium inflation bias of 2.0% with zero gain in expected output.

Problem 1.2 (Welfare Loss Evaluation). For the parameters in Problem 1.1, calculate the social loss under commitment versus discretion in the absence of supply shocks (\(\varepsilon_t = 0\)).

Solution

Under commitment, \(\pi = 0\) and \(y = y_n = 100\): \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{commit}} = \frac{1}{2}(100 - 104)^2 + \frac{1.6}{2}(0)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(16) = 8.0 \] Under discretion, \(\pi = 2.0\) and \(y = y_n = 100\): \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{disc}} = \frac{1}{2}(100 - 104)^2 + \frac{1.6}{2}(2.0)^2 = 8.0 + 0.8 \times 4.0 = 8.0 + 3.2 = 11.2 \] The discretionary deadweight loss penalty is \(11.2 - 8.0 = 3.2\).

Problem 1.3 (Shock Stabilization Under Commitment). If a supply shock \(\varepsilon_t = -2.0\) hits the economy with \(\alpha = 1.0\) and \(\gamma = 3.0\), calculate the optimal commitment inflation response.

Solution

The optimal commitment response is: \[ \pi_t = -\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} \varepsilon_t = -\frac{1.0}{1.0 + 3.0} (-2.0) = \frac{2.0}{4.0} = 0.5\% \] The central bank accommodates half a percentage point of inflation to cushion the negative supply disturbance.

Problem 1.4 (Monotonicity of Inflation Bias). Prove that the discretionary inflation bias \(\pi_d\) is strictly decreasing in the central bank's inflation aversion \(\gamma\) and strictly increasing in the target distortion \(k\).

Solution

Differentiating \(\pi_d = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma} (k - 1) y_n\) with respect to \(\gamma\): \[ \frac{\partial \pi_d}{\partial \gamma} = -\frac{\alpha}{\gamma^2} (k - 1) y_n < 0 \] since \(\alpha > 0, k > 1, y_n > 0\). Differentiating with respect to \(k\): \[ \frac{\partial \pi_d}{\partial k} = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma} y_n > 0 \] Thus, greater inflation conservatism reduces the bias, whereas more ambitious output targets exacerbate it.

Problem 1.5 (Derivation of Stabilization Coefficient). Prove that the shock response coefficient \(b = -\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2 + \gamma}\) is identical under commitment and discretion.

Solution

In both regimes, the marginal tradeoff between inflation variance and output variance around the deterministic mean is governed by the same static first-order condition \(\alpha (y_t - y^*) + \gamma \pi_t = 0\). Because \(\varepsilon_t\) is mean-zero and unforecastable, \(\E_{t-1}[\varepsilon_t] = 0\). The expectational wedge \(\pi^e\) affects only the constant intercept, leaving the slope response with respect to unexpected supply shocks identical across regimes.

Problem 1.6 (Lucas Critique Equivalence). Explain why evaluating discretionary policy using an estimated Phillips curve with fixed expectations violates the Lucas Critique.

Solution

The Lucas Critique establishes that decision rules of private agents depend on the perceived policy regime. If an econometrician assumes \(\pi^e\) is invariant to policy, the central bank appears able to exploit the Phillips curve to achieve output \(y^* > y_n\). Once the policymaker acts, private agents adjust \(\pi^e\), shifting the Phillips curve upward and destroying the simulated output expansion.

Problem 1.7 (Estimating the Great Inflation Bias). In the 1970s, US inflation averaged 7.5% while natural unemployment was estimated around 5.5%. If the structural Phillips curve slope was \(\alpha = 0.5\) and the output gap averaged 0.0%, what implied target distortion \(k - 1\) explains this inflation bias under \(\gamma = 1.0\)?

Solution

Equating \(\pi_d = 0.075 = \frac{0.5}{1.0} (k - 1) \implies k - 1 = \frac{0.075}{0.5} = 0.15\). The policy authority behaved as if potential output was 15% higher than actual natural capacity, consistent with historical accounts of 1970s Federal Reserve mismeasurement of productivity slowdowns.

Problem 1.8 (Taylor 1993 Rule Evaluation). In 1990:Q1, US inflation was 4.4% and the output gap was -0.2%. Using the classic Taylor (1993) rule with \(r^* = 2\%\) and \(\pi^* = 2\%\), calculate the prescribed federal funds rate.

Solution

Applying the Taylor (1993) rule: \[ i = 2.0 + 4.4 + 0.5(4.4 - 2.0) + 0.5(-0.2) = 6.4 + 0.5(2.4) - 0.1 = 6.4 + 1.2 - 0.1 = 7.5\% \] The Taylor rule prescribed a policy rate of 7.50%.

Problem 1.9 (Sacrifice Ratio). If reducing expected inflation \(\pi^e\) by 1% temporarily lowers output by 2% for one year, calculate the implied empirical sacrifice ratio.

Solution

The sacrifice ratio is defined as the cumulative percentage output loss divided by the permanent reduction in inflation: \[ \text{Sacrifice Ratio} = \frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta \pi} = \frac{2\%}{1\%} = 2.0 \] A sacrifice ratio of 2.0 indicates that disinflation entails temporary real economic costs during expectational adjustment.

Problem 1.10 (Endogenous Natural Rate). Suppose natural output exhibits hysteresis: \(y_{n, t} = y_{n, t-1} + \theta (y_{t-1} - y_{n, t-1})\). How does hysteresis alter the discretionary inflation bias?

Solution

When current output permanently raises future potential output, the central bank's perceived long-run benefit from surprise expansion increases. This raises the effective marginal benefit of surprise inflation at date \(t\), compounding the static inflation bias into an even larger dynamic inflation bias.

Problem 1.11 (Walsh Inflation Contracts). Show that a performance contract penalizing the central bank governor by \(T(\pi) = -R \cdot \pi\) eliminates the discretionary inflation bias if \(R = \alpha (k - 1) y_n\).

Solution

With penalty \(R \cdot \pi\) added to the central bank's loss function, the first-order condition becomes \(\alpha(y - k y_n) + \gamma \pi + R = 0\). Imposing rational expectations \(y = y_n\): \(-\alpha(k - 1)y_n + \gamma \pi + R = 0 \implies \pi = \frac{\alpha(k - 1)y_n - R}{\gamma}\). Setting \(R = \alpha (k - 1) y_n\) drives equilibrium inflation to exactly zero.

Problem 1.12 (Inflation Target Conservatism). If society cannot change \(\gamma\), show that setting an official inflation target \(\pi^* < 0\) can eliminate the net inflation bias.

Solution

If loss is \(\frac{1}{2}(y - k y_n)^2 + \frac{\gamma}{2}(\pi - \pi^*)^2\), discretionary inflation is \(\pi_d = \pi^* + \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}(k - 1)y_n\). Setting \(\pi^* = -\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}(k - 1)y_n\) achieves \(\pi_d = 0\) in equilibrium, though such negative targets risk zero lower bound complications.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the core source of time inconsistency in the Kydland-Prescott (1977) model?

    Options

    Time inconsistency arises because the optimal policy choice changes once private expectations are fixed in nominal contracts.

  2. Under discretionary monetary policy, what is the average output level in rational expectations equilibrium?

    Options

    Rational expectations eliminate systematic surprise, leaving output at the natural rate \(y_n\).

  3. Calculate the inflation bias \(\pi_d\) when \(\alpha = 1.0, \gamma = 2.0, k = 1.05\), and \(y_n = 100\).

    \(\pi_d = (1.0/2.0) \times (1.05 - 1.0) \times 100 = 2.50\%\).

  4. How does an increase in the central bank's inflation aversion \(\gamma\) affect the discretionary inflation bias?

    Options

    Higher \(\gamma\) penalizes inflation more heavily, lowering the marginal incentive to create surprise inflation.

  5. What does the Lucas Critique imply about macroeconomic policymaking?

    Options

    Private expectations and decision rules adapt endogenously to the prevailing policy regime.

  6. Under the Taylor (1993) rule with \(r^*=2, \pi^*=2\), what is prescribed rate \(i\) if \(\pi=4.4\) and output gap is \(-0.2\)?

    \(i = 2 + 4.4 + 0.5(4.4 - 2) + 0.5(-0.2) = 7.5\%\).

  7. How does the optimal commitment rule respond to supply shocks compared to discretion?

    Options

    Both regimes share the same shock sensitivity \(b = -\alpha / (\alpha^2 + \gamma)\); discretion merely adds an unnecessary average inflation bias.

  8. What institutional reform can eliminate inflation bias without legislative rules?

    Options

    Rogoff (1985) showed that delegating policy to a conservative central banker lowers equilibrium inflation bias.
