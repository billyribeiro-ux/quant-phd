Barro-Gordon Credibility, Inflation Bias, and Reputation

Central banks are not single-shot players: they interact with financial markets and wage negotiators over an infinite horizon. The Barro-Gordon repeated game framework reveals how reputational capital and trigger strategies can overcome time inconsistency without statutory rules, formalizing the exact discount factor threshold required to sustain credible price stability.

1. The Infinitely Repeated Policy Game

In their classic extension of Kydland-Prescott, Barro & Gordon (1983) modeled monetary policy as an infinitely repeated game between the central bank and private wage-setters. Time is discrete, \(t = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), and the central bank discounts future social losses with subjective factor \(\beta \in (0, 1)\).

In each period \(t\), the central bank incurs social loss:

\[ \mathcal{L}_t = \frac{1}{2} (y_t - k y_n)^2 + \frac{\gamma}{2} \pi_t^2 \]

subject to the expectations-augmented Phillips curve \(y_t = y_n + \alpha (\pi_t - \pi_t^e)\). If the central bank can credibly commit to zero inflation, \(\pi_t = 0\) and \(\pi_t^e = 0\), yielding per-period commitment loss:

\[ \mathcal{L}^C = \frac{1}{2} (1 - k)^2 y_n^2 \]

Conversely, if private agents expect discretionary inflation \(\pi_t^e = \pi_d = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}(k - 1)y_n\), the Nash discretionary equilibrium yields per-period loss:

\[ \mathcal{L}^D = \frac{1}{2} (1 - k)^2 y_n^2 + \frac{\gamma}{2} \left[ \frac{\alpha}{\gamma}(k - 1)y_n \right]^2 = \mathcal{L}^C + \frac{\alpha^2}{2\gamma} (k - 1)^2 y_n^2 \]

2. The Temptation-Punishment Tradeoff

Suppose the central bank has announced a zero-inflation commitment policy and private agents believe the promise, setting \(\pi_t^e = 0\). The central bank faces a short-run temptation to cheat by engineering surprise inflation:

\[ \min_{\pi_t} \left\{ \frac{1}{2} [y_n + \alpha \pi_t - k y_n]^2 + \frac{\gamma}{2} \pi_t^2 \right\} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\pi_t\) yields the optimal cheating inflation rate:

\[ \pi^{\text{cheat}} = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} (k - 1) y_n \]

Substituting \(\pi^{\text{cheat}}\) back into the loss function gives the cheating loss \(\mathcal{L}^{\text{cheat}} < \mathcal{L}^C\). The central bank captures an immediate one-period temptation gain:

\[ \text{Temptation} = \mathcal{L}^C - \mathcal{L}^{\text{cheat}} = \frac{\alpha^2}{2(\alpha^2 + \gamma)} (k - 1)^2 y_n^2 > 0 \]

Theorem 2.1 (Grim Trigger Sustainability). Under the Grim Trigger strategy (where private agents expect \(\pi^e = 0\) as long as the central bank has never inflated, but permanently revert to discretionary expectations \(\pi^e = \pi_d\) forever if the central bank ever reneges), zero inflation is an incentive-compatible subgame perfect equilibrium if and only if: \[ \beta \ge \beta^* = \frac{\text{Temptation}}{\text{Temptation} + \text{Punishment}} = \frac{\gamma}{\alpha^2 + 2\gamma} \] where \(\text{Punishment} = \mathcal{L}^D - \mathcal{L}^C = \frac{\alpha^2}{2\gamma} (k - 1)^2 y_n^2\).

Proof. Cheating yields an immediate gain \(\text{Temptation}\) at date \(t\), but triggers permanent discretionary reversion from date \(t+1\) onward, inflicting penalty \(\text{Punishment}\) in every future period. The discounted expected cost of cheating is: \[ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \beta^j \text{Punishment} = \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta} \text{Punishment} \] The central bank honors its commitment if and only if the discounted punishment exceeds the one-period temptation: \[ \text{Temptation} \le \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta} \text{Punishment} \iff \beta (\text{Temptation} + \text{Punishment}) \ge \text{Temptation} \] Solving for \(\beta\) establishes the critical discount factor: \[ \beta^* = \frac{\text{Temptation}}{\text{Temptation} + \text{Punishment}} \] Substituting \(\text{Temptation} = \frac{\alpha^2}{2(\alpha^2 + \gamma)} \Omega\) and \(\text{Punishment} = \frac{\alpha^2}{2\gamma} \Omega\), where \(\Omega = (k - 1)^2 y_n^2\): \[ \beta^* = \frac{\frac{1}{\alpha^2 + \gamma}}{\frac{1}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} + \frac{1}{\gamma}} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma + (\alpha^2 + \gamma)} = \frac{\gamma}{\alpha^2 + 2\gamma} \] This completes the proof.

3. Institutional Delegation: The Rogoff Conservative Central Banker

When the central bank's discount factor \(\beta < \beta^*\), pure reputation cannot sustain zero inflation. In an influential resolution, Rogoff (1985) demonstrated that society can delegate monetary policy to an independent central banker whose personal weight on inflation \(\gamma_{\text{CB}}\) strictly exceeds society's weight \(\gamma\).

Under delegation, the conservative central banker chooses inflation according to their personal objective, resulting in equilibrium inflation bias:

\[ \pi_d^{\text{Rogoff}} = \frac{\alpha}{\gamma_{\text{CB}}} (k - 1) y_n < \frac{\alpha}{\gamma} (k - 1) y_n \]

As Taylor (1993) noted, institutional independence and inflation-targeting mandates provide institutional substitutes for the Folk Theorem's reputational requirements.

Lab E7-L02-1: Reputational Equilibrium and Critical Discount Factor

Hypothesis: The critical discount factor \(\beta^* = \gamma / (\alpha^2 + 2\gamma)\) strictly guarantees incentive compatibility against surprise inflation temptation.

"""Lab E7-L02-1: Reputational Equilibrium in the Barro-Gordon (1983) Repeated Game.

Evaluates Grim Trigger strategies in an infinitely repeated Barro-Gordon game:
1. Honest commitment loss per period
2. One-period temptation gain from surprise cheating
3. Subsequent punishment loss in perpetual discretionary Nash
4. Analytical calculation of the critical discount factor beta* required for sustainable commitment
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_barro_gordon_reputation(
    alpha: float,
    gamma: float,
    k: float,
    y_n: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate the temptation, enforcement, and critical discount factor."""
    # Commitment: pi = 0, pi_e = 0
    # L_C = (y_n - k*y_n)^2 + gamma * 0^2 = (1 - k)^2 * y_n^2
    target_gap = (1.0 - k) * y_n
    loss_c = target_gap**2

    # Discretionary Nash: pi_e = pi_d = (alpha / gamma) * (k - 1) * y_n
    pi_d = (alpha / gamma) * (k - 1.0) * y_n
    loss_d = target_gap**2 + gamma * (pi_d**2)

    # Cheating (Temptation): People expect pi_e = 0 (commitment), but CB cheats!
    # CB chooses pi_cheat to min (y_n + alpha*(pi - 0) - k*y_n)^2 + gamma * pi^2
    # dL/dpi = 2 * alpha * (y_n + alpha*pi - k*y_n) + 2 * gamma * pi = 0
    # (alpha^2 + gamma) * pi = alpha * (k - 1) * y_n
    pi_cheat = (alpha * (k - 1.0) * y_n) / (alpha**2 + gamma)
    y_cheat = y_n + alpha * pi_cheat
    loss_cheat = (y_cheat - k * y_n) ** 2 + gamma * (pi_cheat**2)

    # Temptation gain in date t:
    temptation = loss_c - loss_cheat

    # Punishment loss per period in dates t+1, t+2, ...:
    punishment = loss_d - loss_c

    # Sustainable commitment condition:
    # Temptation <= sum_{j=1}^inf beta^j * Punishment = (beta / (1 - beta)) * Punishment
    # beta / (1 - beta) >= Temptation / Punishment
    # beta* = Temptation / (Temptation + Punishment)
    critical_beta = temptation / (temptation + punishment)

    return {
        "loss_c": loss_c,
        "loss_cheat": loss_cheat,
        "loss_d": loss_d,
        "pi_cheat": pi_cheat,
        "pi_d": pi_d,
        "temptation": temptation,
        "punishment": punishment,
        "critical_beta": critical_beta,
    }


def main() -> None:
    alpha = 1.0
    gamma = 2.0
    k = 1.10
    y_n = 100.0

    res = solve_barro_gordon_reputation(alpha, gamma, k, y_n)

    print("Barro-Gordon (1983) Reputational Mechanism:")
    print(f"  Baseline loss under zero-inflation commitment: {res['loss_c']:.2f}")
    print(f"  Cheating temptation loss (surprise inflation):  {res['loss_cheat']:.2f}")
    print(f"  Perpetual discretionary Nash loss:              {res['loss_d']:.2f}")
    print("\nIncentive Compatibility:")
    print(f"  One-period temptation gain: {res['temptation']:.4f}")
    print(f"  Per-period punishment loss: {res['punishment']:.4f}")
    print(f"  Critical discount factor beta*: {res['critical_beta']:.4f}")
    print(f"  Commitment sustainable for beta > {res['critical_beta']:.4f}: True")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The Barro-Gordon repeated game formulation exhibits significant limitations in realistic monetary policy contexts:

  • Grim trigger fragility: Grim trigger strategies assume infinite punishment upon the slightest observed deviation. In noisy environments with unobserved supply shocks, a single large shock can trigger permanent breakdown into high inflation.
  • Multiplicity of Folk Theorem equilibria: The repeated game admits an infinite continuum of sustainable inflation equilibria, providing no unique prediction for which equilibrium private agents coordinate upon.
  • Finite political horizons: Central bank governors and elected politicians face finite terms of office. Near the end of a term, backward induction threatens to unravel reputational cooperation.

Misconceptions

  1. A central bank can establish credibility simply by publicly announcing an ambitious zero inflation target.

    Cheap talk announcements lack credibility unless backed by incentive-compatible punishment threats or institutional mechanisms (such as central bank independence and legal mandates).

  2. Appointing an infinitely inflation-averse central banker (\(\gamma_{\text{CB}} \to \infty\)) is always socially optimal.

    While an infinitely conservative central banker eliminates the inflation bias entirely, they also refuse to stabilize real output during adverse supply shocks, generating extreme output volatility.

  3. Reputational equilibria require central bank governors to personally care about social welfare across future generations.

    Institutional continuity, institutional prestige, and career concerns align the governor's effective discount factor with the long-term institutional horizon of the central bank.

Problem Set

Problem 2.1 (Critical Discount Factor Calculation). If \(\alpha = 1.2\) and \(\gamma = 1.8\), calculate the analytical critical discount factor \(\beta^*\) required to sustain commitment under Grim Trigger strategies.

Solution

Applying Theorem 2.1: \[ \beta^* = \frac{\gamma}{\alpha^2 + 2\gamma} = \frac{1.8}{(1.2)^2 + 2(1.8)} = \frac{1.8}{1.44 + 3.60} = \frac{1.8}{5.04} \approx 0.3571 \] Commitment is sustainable for any subjective discount factor \(\beta \ge 0.3571\).

Problem 2.2 (Cheating Inflation Rate). For \(\alpha = 0.8, \gamma = 1.2, k = 1.05\), and \(y_n = 100\), calculate the optimal cheating inflation rate \(\pi^{\text{cheat}}\) when private agents expect \(\pi^e = 0\).

Solution

The optimal cheating inflation rate is: \[ \pi^{\text{cheat}} = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2 + \gamma} (k - 1) y_n = \frac{0.8}{(0.8)^2 + 1.2} \times 5.0 = \frac{0.8}{0.64 + 1.2} \times 5.0 = \frac{4.0}{1.84} \approx 2.1739\% \] The central bank inflates by 2.17% to engineer a temporary output boom.

Problem 2.3 (Rogoff Bias Reduction). If society has \(\gamma = 1.0\) and delegates policy to a central banker with \(\gamma_{\text{CB}} = 4.0\), by what percentage is the discretionary inflation bias reduced?

Solution

The inflation bias is inversely proportional to \(\gamma\): \(\pi_d \propto 1/\gamma\). Under delegation: \[ \frac{\pi_d^{\text{Rogoff}}}{\pi_d^{\text{society}}} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{\text{CB}}} = \frac{1.0}{4.0} = 0.25 \] The inflation bias is reduced by \(1 - 0.25 = 75\%\).

Problem 2.4 (Comparative Statics of \(\beta^*\)). Prove that \(\beta^* = \frac{\gamma}{\alpha^2 + 2\gamma}\) is strictly decreasing in the Phillips curve slope \(\alpha\) and strictly increasing in inflation aversion \(\gamma\).

Solution

Differentiating with respect to \(\alpha\): \[ \frac{\partial \beta^*}{\partial \alpha} = -\frac{2\alpha \gamma}{(\alpha^2 + 2\gamma)^2} < 0 \] A steeper Phillips curve increases the punishment penalty more than the temptation gain, making commitment easier to sustain. Differentiating with respect to \(\gamma\): \[ \frac{\partial \beta^*}{\partial \gamma} = \frac{(\alpha^2 + 2\gamma) - 2\gamma}{(\alpha^2 + 2\gamma)^2} = \frac{\alpha^2}{(\alpha^2 + 2\gamma)^2} > 0 \] Higher \(\gamma\) lowers the discretionary punishment penalty \(\mathcal{L}^D - \mathcal{L}^C = \frac{\alpha^2}{2\gamma}\Omega\), requiring a more patient central bank to deter cheating.

Problem 2.5 (Finite Punishment Horizon). Suppose private agents punish cheating by reverting to discretion for \(T\) periods instead of permanently. Prove that the required discount factor \(\beta\) must strictly exceed \(\beta^*\).

Solution

The discounted punishment over \(T\) periods is \(P_T = \sum_{j=1}^T \beta^j \text{Punishment} = \frac{\beta(1 - \beta^T)}{1 - \beta} \text{Punishment}\). Since \(\beta \in (0, 1)\), \(1 - \beta^T < 1\), so \(P_T < P_\infty\). To satisfy \(\text{Temptation} \le P_T\), \(\beta\) must be strictly larger than the infinite-horizon threshold \(\beta^*\).

Problem 2.6 (Equilibrium Selection via Renegotiation Proofness). Discuss why Grim Trigger strategies may fail renegotiation-proofness criteria once punishment is triggered.

Solution

Once cheating occurs and the economy enters the punishment phase, both the central bank and private agents suffer the high discretionary loss \(\mathcal{L}^D\). Both parties have an ex-post incentive to mutually forgive past cheating and reset expectations to zero inflation, undermining the ex-ante credibility of the punishment threat.

Problem 2.7 (Central Bank Independence and Inflation). Cross-country empirical studies (Alesina & Summers 1993) find a strong negative correlation between legal central bank independence indices and average inflation rates. How does the Barro-Gordon model interpret this finding?

Solution

Central bank independence insulates monetary authorities from electoral political pressures to boost short-run employment (\(k > 1\)), effectively lowering the target distortion to \(k \approx 1\) and lengthening the effective discount factor \(\beta\), eliminating the discretionary inflation bias.

Problem 2.8 (Volcker Disinflation Credibility). In 1979, Chairman Paul Volcker announced a dramatic shift to money growth targeting. Why did inflation expectations take nearly three years (until 1982) to fully adjust downward?

Solution

Because private agents faced imperfect information regarding whether the Fed had permanently transitioned to a conservative type or was merely engaging in temporary rhetoric, learning took time under Bayesian updating, causing expectations \(\pi^e\) to lag actual policy tightening.

Problem 2.9 (Evaluating Inflation Targets). In an inflation-targeting regime with target \(\pi^* = 2\%\), market 5-year breakeven inflation averages 2.05% with standard deviation 0.15%. What does this imply about the credibility of the commitment?

Solution

A tight distribution of breakeven rates centered within 5 bps of the official target indicates near-perfect credibility: private markets expect the central bank to offset deviations rather than exploit short-term Phillips curve tradeoffs.

Problem 2.10 (Optimal Degree of Conservatism). Formulate the social planner's problem for choosing the optimal Rogoff conservatism \(\gamma_{\text{CB}}\) when the economy is subject to stochastic supply shocks \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 > 0\).

Solution

The planner minimizes expected social loss: \[ \min_{\gamma_{\text{CB}}} \left\{ \frac{\gamma}{2} \left[ \frac{\alpha}{\gamma_{\text{CB}}}(k - 1)y_n \right]^2 + \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{\gamma^2 + \alpha^2 \gamma_{\text{CB}}^2}{(\alpha^2 + \gamma_{\text{CB}})^2} \right] \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \right\} \] Trading off lower inflation bias against worse shock stabilization yields an interior optimal \(\gamma_{\text{CB}}^* \in (\gamma, \infty)\).

Problem 2.11 (Career Concerns and Transparency). How does transparency in publishing voting records affect the reputational incentive of individual central bank governors?

Solution

Publishing individual voting records increases personal accountability and reputational stakes in the post-central-bank labor market, strengthening individual incentives to adhere to conservative price stability mandates.

Problem 2.12 (Hysteresis and Discretion). If recessions cause human capital decay (hysteresis), explain why discretionary monetary policy becomes even more inflationary.

Solution

With hysteresis, avoiding a downturn prevents permanent capacity losses, giving the central bank a larger future benefit from current expansion. This raises the perceived marginal gain from surprise inflation, amplifying the discretionary bias.
