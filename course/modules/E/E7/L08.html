Narrative Monetary Shock Identification

High-frequency asset price surprises capture unexpected policy shifts over short market windows, but they cannot isolate historical episodes before modern futures markets existed, nor do they fully purge central bank private forecasts. Narrative identification overcomes this by scouring internal central bank records to measure intended policy decisions and purging them of staff macroeconomic projections, providing an exogenous policy shock series spanning decades.

1. The Narrative Identification Methodology

Narrative shock identification is an empirical identification strategy that isolates exogenous policy innovations by combining historical primary-source documentation (such as FOMC minutes, transcripts, and Records of Policy Actions) with central bank staff macroeconomic forecasts. Pioneered in modern macroeconomics by Romer & Romer (2004), this approach addresses the two fundamental identification challenges of monetary economics:

  1. Anticipation and endogeneity: Central banks raise the policy rate when output and inflation are projected to accelerate. An unadjusted regression of output growth on policy rate changes produces an attenuated or positive coefficient (a spurious "price puzzle" or "output puzzle").
  2. Information asymmetries: Central bankers possess dedicated research staffs producing internal forecasts (the Greenbook/Tealbook) that are more comprehensive than private-sector forecasts at the time of the meeting.

Unlike pure market-based high-frequency surprises (Lesson 07), the narrative method directly identifies what policy makers intended to do and why they intended to do it, purging the intended change of all systematic responses to staff economic forecasts.

2. The Romer & Romer (2004) Two-Step Derivation

The derivation of the Romer-Romer narrative monetary shock proceeds in two distinct steps:

Step 1: Extraction of Intended Target Rate Changes. Prior to 1994, the FOMC did not announce quantitative targets for the federal funds rate following meetings. Instead, the FOMC issued instructions regarding the degree of reserve pressure. Romer & Romer (2004) examined the Record of Policy Actions and meeting minutes for each FOMC meeting \(m\) from 1969 to 1996 to determine the precise target change intended by the Committee, denoted \(\Delta i_m^* = i_m^* - i_{m-1}^*\). This series captures the FOMC's intentional policy stance, discarding changes in the effective funds rate caused by operational reserve demand fluctuations or tax settlement dates.

Step 2: Greenbook Forecast Orthogonalization. To eliminate the systematic component of monetary policy driven by expected economic conditions, the intended change \(\Delta i_m^*\) is regressed on internal Federal Reserve staff forecasts compiled in the Greenbook (prepared by Board staff 3 to 5 days before each FOMC meeting): \[ \Delta i_m^* = \alpha + \beta i_{m-1}^* + \sum_{j=-1}^2 \gamma_j \tilde{\pi}_{m, t+j}^{GB} + \sum_{j=-1}^2 \delta_j \tilde{y}_{m, t+j}^{GB} + \sum_{j=-1}^2 \theta_j (\tilde{y}_{m, t+j}^{GB} - \tilde{y}_{m-1, t+j}^{GB}) + \varepsilon_m^{RR} \] where \(i_{m-1}^*\) is the beginning-of-period funds target, \(\tilde{\pi}_{m, t+j}^{GB}\) is the staff inflation forecast at horizon \(t+j\), \(\tilde{y}_{m, t+j}^{GB}\) is the real GDP forecast, and \(\varepsilon_m^{RR}\) is the narrative monetary policy shock.

Theorem 8.1 (Orthogonality of the Purged Narrative Residual). Under the hypothesis that the central bank's reaction function depends on staff information \(\mathcal{I}_m^{GB}\) and an exogenous policy preference shock \(\varepsilon_m^{RR}\): \[ \Delta i_m^* = f(\mathcal{I}_m^{GB}) + \varepsilon_m^{RR}, \quad \text{with } \E[\varepsilon_m^{RR} \mid \mathcal{I}_m^{GB}] = 0 \] The OLS residual \(\hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR}\) satisfies \(\Cov(\hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR}, \mathcal{I}_m^{GB}) = 0\) by construction. As a consequence, \(\hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR}\) is uncorrelated with expected future inflation and output growth known to the central bank at the time of the policy decision.

Proof. Let \(\mathbf{y} = \Delta \mathbf{i}^*\) and \(\mathbf{X}\) be the \(N \times K\) matrix of Greenbook forecasts including a constant. The OLS normal equations require \(\mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0}\). Defining \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR} \equiv \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\), it follows immediately that \(\mathbf{X}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR} = \mathbf{0}\). Since \(\mathbf{X}\) includes the intercept, \(\sum_{m=1}^N \hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR} = 0\), establishing that \(\widehat{\Cov}(\mathbf{x}_k, \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR}) = 0\) for every forecast variable \(k\). This completes the proof.

3. Monthly Aggregation and Empirical Transmission

Because FOMC meetings occur at irregular intervals (roughly 8 meetings per year), the meeting-level shock \(\varepsilon_m^{RR}\) is aggregated into a monthly time series \(S_t^{RR}\):

\[ S_t^{RR} = \sum_{m \in \text{month } t} \varepsilon_m^{RR} \]

If no meeting occurred in month \(t\), \(S_t^{RR} = 0\). If multiple meetings occurred, their shocks are summed.

Romer & Romer (2004) estimate the response of real industrial production and consumer prices to a 100 basis point contractionary narrative shock. Key findings include:

  • Resolution of the Price Puzzle: In unadjusted VAR models, interest rate hikes frequently appear to precede rising prices (the "price puzzle") because the Fed raises rates in anticipation of future inflation. In contrast, the narrative shock generates a monotonic decline in the price level after an initial lag of 18–24 months.
  • Peak Output Contraction: Industrial production contracts substantially, reaching a trough contraction of approximately \(-4.0\%\) to \(-4.5\%\) between 24 and 36 months after the shock.
  • Persistent Transmission: The real macroeconomic consequences of monetary policy are larger and considerably more persistent than estimated by Cholesky-identified vector autoregressions.

Lab E7-L08-1: Estimating Romer-Romer Narrative Monetary Shocks

Hypothesis: Regressing intended federal funds rate target changes on Greenbook staff inflation and output forecasts isolates exogenous monetary policy innovations orthogonal to internal central bank projections.

"""Lab E7-L08-1: Narrative Monetary Shock Identification (Romer & Romer 2004).

Implements the Romer & Romer (2004) two-step narrative shock derivation:
1. Extract intended policy target changes Delta i_m from historical FOMC minutes.
2. Regress intended changes on internal Federal Reserve Greenbook forecasts of inflation and real activity:
   Delta i_m = alpha + sum_i beta_i * pi^GB_{m, i} + sum_j gamma_j * y^GB_{m, j} + e_m
3. The residual e_m is orthogonal to central bank private information and economic projections.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def romer_romer_orthogonalization(
    intended_changes: list[float],
    forecast_inflation_t: list[float],
    forecast_inflation_t1: list[float],
    forecast_output_t: list[float],
    forecast_output_t1: list[float],
) -> tuple[list[float], float, float]:
    """Regress intended target changes on Greenbook forecasts using pure OLS."""
    n = len(intended_changes)
    # Design matrix: [1.0, pi_0, pi_1, y_0, y_1]
    x_mat: list[list[float]] = []
    y: list[float] = intended_changes[:]

    for i in range(n):
        x_mat.append(
            [1.0, forecast_inflation_t[i], forecast_inflation_t1[i], forecast_output_t[i], forecast_output_t1[i]]
        )

    k = 5
    # OLS: beta = inv(X'X) * X'y
    xtx = [[sum(x_mat[t][i] * x_mat[t][j] for t in range(n)) for j in range(k)] for i in range(k)]
    xty = [sum(x_mat[t][i] * y[t] for t in range(n)) for i in range(k)]

    # Invert XtX (Gauss-Jordan)
    aug = [[xtx[i][j] for j in range(k)] + [1.0 if i == j else 0.0 for j in range(k)] for i in range(k)]
    for col in range(k):
        pivot = col
        max_val = abs(aug[col][col])
        for row in range(col + 1, k):
            if abs(aug[row][col]) > max_val:
                max_val = abs(aug[row][col])
                pivot = row
        aug[col], aug[pivot] = aug[pivot], aug[col]
        pivot_val = aug[col][col]
        for j in range(2 * k):
            aug[col][j] /= pivot_val
        for row in range(k):
            if row != col:
                factor = aug[row][col]
                for j in range(2 * k):
                    aug[row][j] -= factor * aug[col][j]

    inv_xtx = [[aug[i][k + j] for j in range(k)] for i in range(k)]
    beta = [sum(inv_xtx[i][j] * xty[j] for j in range(k)) for i in range(k)]

    residuals = [y[t] - sum(x_mat[t][j] * beta[j] for j in range(k)) for t in range(n)]

    # Compute R^2
    mean_y = sum(y) / n
    ss_tot = sum((yi - mean_y) ** 2 for yi in y)
    ss_res = sum(ri**2 for ri in residuals)
    r2 = 1.0 - (ss_res / ss_tot) if ss_tot > 1e-12 else 0.0

    return residuals, r2, beta[1]


def main() -> None:
    # Representative sample of 10 FOMC meetings with Greenbook forecasts
    # Intended federal funds rate changes (in bps)
    delta_i = [25.0, 0.0, 50.0, -25.0, 0.0, 25.0, 25.0, 0.0, -50.0, 25.0]

    # Greenbook forecast revisions (inflation and real output growth, in %)
    pi_0 = [2.4, 2.1, 3.2, 1.8, 2.0, 2.7, 2.9, 2.2, 1.5, 2.8]
    pi_1 = [2.5, 2.2, 3.5, 1.9, 2.1, 2.8, 3.0, 2.3, 1.4, 2.9]
    y_0 = [2.8, 2.0, 3.5, 1.0, 2.2, 3.0, 3.2, 2.1, 0.5, 3.1]
    y_1 = [2.9, 2.1, 3.6, 1.2, 2.3, 3.1, 3.3, 2.2, 0.8, 3.2]

    shocks, r2, beta_inf = romer_romer_orthogonalization(delta_i, pi_0, pi_1, y_0, y_1)

        )

Where the Canon Breaks

While narrative identification provides historical depth, it faces key institutional and econometric limitations:

  • The 5-Year Information Embargo: The Federal Reserve Board imposes a statutory 5-year public embargo on Greenbook/Tealbook staff forecasts and unredacted meeting transcripts. As a result, narrative shocks cannot be constructed in real time by financial market participants or policy analysts.
  • Subjective Intent Interpretation: Converting qualitative FOMC policy directives prior to 1994 into quantitative target changes requires qualitative reading of archival minutes. While subsequent research using machine learning text classification has generally validated Romer & Romer's classifications, small coding differences in borderline meetings alter estimated shock magnitudes.
  • Omitted Forecast Variables: If the FOMC adjusts the policy rate in response to financial stability indicators, stock market valuations, or credit conditions omitted from staff GDP and inflation forecasts, the regression residuals remain contaminated by endogenous policy reactions.

Misconceptions

  1. The Romer-Romer shock is simply the actual change in the effective federal funds rate.

    The effective funds rate fluctuates daily due to reserve demand noise, Treasury tax balance swings, and seasonal liquidity pressures. The narrative shock extracts the intended target change from official minutes and purges all systematic responses to staff inflation and growth forecasts.

  2. Because the regression includes staff forecasts, the Romer-Romer shocks capture the Federal Reserve's information advantage over the public.

    The regression subtracts the staff forecasts from the intended policy change. Therefore, any policy action taken because central bank economists anticipated higher inflation or higher growth is purged. The residual captures policy shifts unexplained by economic forecasts.

  3. Narrative shocks and high-frequency futures surprises are interchangeable instruments.

    They have a correlation of only about 0.3 to 0.4. High-frequency surprises capture unexpected shocks within 30-minute windows (which may still convey central bank information to the market), whereas narrative shocks purge internal staff forecasts over a multi-decade horizon back to 1969.

Problem Set

Problem 8.1 (Greenbook Forecast Orthogonalization). An FOMC meeting concludes with an intended policy hike of \(\Delta i_m^* = +50\text{ bps}\). The staff forecast for next-quarter inflation is \(\tilde{\pi}_{m, t+1} = 3.5\%\) and output growth is \(\tilde{y}_{m, t+1} = 3.0\%\). The estimated reaction coefficients from the historical sample are \(\hat{\alpha} = -10\text{ bps}\), \(\hat{\gamma}_\pi = 12.0\text{ bps/percentage point}\), and \(\hat{\delta}_y = 5.0\text{ bps/percentage point}\). Calculate the systematic policy adjustment and the resulting narrative shock \(\hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR}\).

Solution

The systematic response predicted by staff forecasts is: \[ \widehat{\Delta i_m^*} = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\gamma}_\pi \tilde{\pi}_{m, t+1} + \hat{\delta}_y \tilde{y}_{m, t+1} = -10 + (12.0)(3.5) + (5.0)(3.0) = -10 + 42.0 + 15.0 = +47.0\text{ bps} \] The narrative monetary shock is the residual: \[ \hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR} = \Delta i_m^* - \widehat{\Delta i_m^*} = 50.0 - 47.0 = +3.0\text{ bps} \] Thus, out of the 50 bps rate hike, 47 bps represented a systematic response to economic forecasts, leaving a modest 3 bps exogenous contractionary shock.

Problem 8.2 (Monthly Shock Aggregation). In October 1979, the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker conducted an unscheduled intermeeting policy action on October 6 with an identified narrative shock of \(+150\text{ bps}\), followed by a scheduled meeting on October 23 with an identified narrative shock of \(+75\text{ bps}\). Compute the monthly narrative shock \(S_t^{RR}\) for October 1979.

Solution

Following Romer & Romer (2004), meeting-level narrative shocks occurring within the same calendar month are summed to form the monthly shock: \[ S_{\text{Oct 1979}}^{RR} = \varepsilon_{\text{Oct 6}}^{RR} + \varepsilon_{\text{Oct 23}}^{RR} = 150 + 75 = +225\text{ bps} = +2.25\% \] This massive positive shock reflects the Volcker monetary regime shift aimed at breaking entrenched inflation expectations.

Problem 8.3 (Systematic vs. Unsystematic Variance). In a regression of intended rate changes on Greenbook forecasts over 200 FOMC meetings, the total sum of squares of \(\Delta i_m^*\) is \(SST = 8,000\), and the residual sum of squares is \(SSR = 5,600\). What fraction of intended rate changes is systematic, and what is the standard deviation of the narrative shock series?

Solution

The \(R^2\) of the regression measures the fraction explained by systematic forecast variables: \[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSR}{SST} = 1 - \frac{5600}{8000} = 1 - 0.70 = 0.30 \quad (30\%) \] The variance of the narrative shock residuals is \(s_e^2 = \frac{SSR}{N - K} \approx \frac{5600}{200} = 28.0\text{ (bps)}^2\), yielding a standard deviation of: \[ s_e = \sqrt{28.0} \approx 5.29\text{ bps} \] Thus, 30% of intended rate movements reflect systematic responses to Greenbook projections, while 70% of variance is treated as residual exogenous policy variation.

Problem 8.4 (Proof of Orthogonality to Forecast Space). Prove that by properties of ordinary least squares projection, the estimated Romer-Romer shock series \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{X}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR} = \mathbf{0}\), and explain why this mathematically prevents Greenbook forecasts from directly predicting the narrative shock series.

Solution

Let \(\mathbf{y} = \Delta \mathbf{i}^*\) and \(\mathbf{X}\) be the \(N \times K\) matrix of Greenbook forecasts and a constant. The OLS normal equations require: \[ \mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0} \] Substituting the definition of the residual vector \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR} \equiv \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\): \[ \mathbf{X}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR} = \mathbf{0} \] For any column \(\mathbf{x}_k\) of \(\mathbf{X}\) (such as forecasted inflation or GDP growth), \(\mathbf{x}_k^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR} = \sum_{m=1}^N x_{m, k} \hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR} = 0\). Since the first column of \(\mathbf{X}\) is a vector of ones, \(\sum_{m=1}^N \hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR} = 0\), which implies: \[ \widehat{\Cov}(\mathbf{x}_k, \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR}) = \frac{1}{N} \mathbf{x}_k^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{RR} - \bar{x}_k \bar{\varepsilon} = 0 - 0 = 0 \] Therefore, any linear forecast in the column space of \(\mathbf{X}\) has zero sample correlation with the narrative shock, establishing sample orthogonality.

Problem 8.5 (Omitted Information and Endogeneity Contamination). Suppose the true policy rule is \(\Delta i_m^* = \mathbf{X}_m \boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}_m \boldsymbol{\delta} + u_m\), where \(\mathbf{Z}_m\) represents credit market stress observed by the FOMC but omitted from the Greenbook econometric regression. Derive the expectation of the narrative shock \(\hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR}\) conditional on \(\mathbf{Z}\), and state the condition under which \(\hat{\varepsilon}_m^{RR}\) is validly exogenous.

Solution

The estimated regression is \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\). Substituting the true data-generating process: \[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}} = \mathbf{M}_X \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{M}_X (\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\delta} + \mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{M}_X \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\delta} + \mathbf{M}_X \mathbf{u} \] where \(\mathbf{M}_X = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\) is the orthogonal projection matrix. Taking expectations conditional on \(\mathbf{X}\) and \(\mathbf{Z}\): \[ \E[\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}} \mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Z}] = \mathbf{M}_X \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\delta} \] For \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\) to be purely exogenous with respect to macroeconomic credit conditions (i.e. \(\E[\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}} \mid \mathbf{Z}] = \mathbf{0}\)), one of two conditions must hold: 1. \(\boldsymbol{\delta} = \mathbf{0}\) (credit market stress does not systematically influence the policy target), or 2. \(\mathbf{M}_X \mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{0}\), meaning \(\mathbf{Z}\) lies entirely in the span of \(\mathbf{X}\) (staff forecasts already perfectly span credit conditions).

Problem 8.6 (Attenuation Bias from Coding Measurement Error). Assume the true intended policy change is \(i_m^*\), but the historical narrative researcher observes \(\tilde{i}_m^* = i_m^* + \eta_m\), where \(\eta_m \sim \text{iid}(0, \sigma_\eta^2)\) is independent measurement noise from ambiguous textual interpretation. Show the effect of this measurement error on the estimated variance of the narrative shock and the subsequent impulse response coefficient in a bivariate regression.

Solution

Regressing \(\tilde{i}_m^*\) on \(\mathbf{X}_m\) yields residuals \(\tilde{\varepsilon}_m = \hat{\varepsilon}_m + \mathbf{M}_X \boldsymbol{\eta}\). The variance of the estimated shock is: \[ \Var(\tilde{\varepsilon}_m) = \Var(\hat{\varepsilon}_m) + \sigma_\eta^2 > \Var(\hat{\varepsilon}_m) \] When these noisy shocks \(\tilde{\varepsilon}\) are subsequently used as the regressor in a second-stage local projection or regression \(y_{t+h} = \theta_h \tilde{\varepsilon}_t + v_t\), classical errors-in-variables applies: \[ \text{plim } \hat{\theta}_h = \theta_h \cdot \frac{\Var(\hat{\varepsilon})}{\Var(\hat{\varepsilon}) + \sigma_\eta^2} < \theta_h \] Thus, textual coding errors attenuate the estimated macroeconomic impulse response toward zero.

Problem 8.7 (Resolution of the Price Puzzle). Explain why a standard VAR with actual federal funds rate innovations typically exhibits a "price puzzle" (prices rising following an interest rate hike), whereas the Romer-Romer narrative shock series exhibits no such anomaly.

Solution

In standard VARs, the monetary policy innovation is identified assuming policy makers only observe current or lagged macroeconomic variables (e.g. via Cholesky ordering). However, central banks are forward-looking: they raise interest rates precisely when internal staff forecasts anticipate surging future inflation. Because standard VARs omit these forward-looking staff forecasts, rate hikes systematically coincide with impending inflation, creating the illusion that rate hikes cause inflation.

The Romer-Romer procedure explicitly conditions on Greenbook forecasts up to two quarters ahead, removing this forward-looking systematic response. Once the anticipation effect is eliminated, the price puzzle completely disappears, and prices fall monotonically.

Problem 8.8 (Instrument Relevance and Weak Instrument Diagnostics). In a Proxy SVAR setting (Gertler & Karadi 2015), narrative shocks are used as an external instrument \(Z_t\) for the reduced-form policy rate residual \(u_t^p\). If the first-stage regression \(u_t^p = \gamma Z_t + v_t\) yields an \(F\)-statistic of \(F = 8.5\), evaluate whether the instrument is strong, and discuss the consequences for macroeconomic inference.

Solution

Under the conventional Staiger-Stock rule of thumb, an external instrument is weak if the first-stage \(F\)-statistic is below 10 (or below Montiel Olea-Pflueger critical values of ~23 for 10% maximum bias). An \(F\)-statistic of 8.5 indicates weak instrument problems: two-stage least squares estimators suffer from substantial finite-sample bias toward OLS, and standard Wald test confidence intervals have severe coverage distortions. In such cases, Anderson-Rubin confidence sets must be reported instead of asymptotic Wald bands.

Problem 8.9 (Pre-1979 vs Post-1982 Policy Regimes). Analyze how the coefficients \(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\delta}\) in the Greenbook regression change when splitting the sample before and after October 1979 (the onset of the Volcker disinflation). Why is parameter stability in the first-stage regression critical for narrative identification?

Solution

Prior to 1979, the Federal Reserve accommodated inflation shocks, exhibiting an inflation response coefficient \(\gamma_\pi < 1\) (violating the Taylor principle). Under Paul Volcker (1979–1987), the Fed aggressively escalated real interest rates (\(\gamma_\pi > 1.5\)). If a single pooled regression is estimated across both regimes, the fitted values underpredict Volcker's systematic anti-inflation response and overpredict the 1970s response, artificially misclassifying systematic regime adjustments as exogenous shocks. Subsample estimation or regime-dependent coefficients are required to maintain shock validity.

Problem 8.10 (Machine Learning and NLP for Narrative Coding). Recent literature uses Large Language Models and transformer-based sentence classifiers to read historical FOMC transcripts and predict intended target changes. Formulate a rigorous loss function and cross-validation framework to test whether algorithmic narrative coding replicates Romer & Romer's manual readings without human researcher bias.

Solution

Formulate a multi-task transformer architecture that processes transcript paragraphs \(d_{m, p}\) for meeting \(m\). The model optimizes a joint loss: \[ \mathcal{L} = \sum_{m} \left( \Delta i_m^* - \sum_p w_p f_\theta(d_{m, p}) \right)^2 + \lambda \|\theta\|_2^2 \] subject to attention weights \(w_p = \text{softmax}(g_\phi(d_{m, p}))\). Cross-validation must be blocked by historical Chair tenures (e.g., train on Burns/Miller/Greenspan, test on Volcker) to guard against memorization of period-specific colloquialisms. Benchmark the algorithmic series against the human Romer-Romer series via correlation and verify that both generate identical impulse response dynamics.

Problem 8.11 (Combining Narrative and High-Frequency Identification). Propose an econometric estimator that integrates high-frequency futures surprises (Lesson 07) and narrative Greenbook-purged shocks (Lesson 08) into a unified proxy SVAR framework to exploit both market precision and historical time span.

Solution

Construct a multi-instrument GMM or Bayesian proxy SVAR where the vector of external instruments is \(\mathbf{Z}_t = [Z_t^{HF}, Z_t^{RR}]^\top\). For periods with overlapping data (1989–2018), both instruments are utilized to overidentify the policy column of the structural impact matrix \(\mathbf{B}\). For historical periods (1969–1988), only \(Z_t^{RR}\) is available, handled via unbalanced data Kalman filtering. Overidentifying restrictions tests (\(J\)-test) can then formally test whether high-frequency and narrative shocks identify the identical structural transmission channel.

Problem 8.12 (International Narrative Identification). How does the institutional structure of the European Central Bank (ECB) or Bank of England affect the feasibility of the Romer-Romer narrative strategy compared to the Federal Reserve?

Solution

For the Bank of England, the Monetary Policy Committee publishes voting records, detailed minutes, and the Inflation Report forecast with immediate or short lags, making intent extraction straightforward. In contrast, the ECB historically operates without published individual member voting records and does not publish unredacted verbatim transcripts, relying instead on consensus press conferences. Furthermore, the ECB must set a unified policy for 20 sovereign economies, meaning staff forecasts represent euro-area aggregates while national fragmentation shocks may drive policy, complicating the staff forecast regression.
