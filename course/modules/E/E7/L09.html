Jordà Local Projections for Policy Dynamics

Estimating dynamic causal effects with classical vector autoregressions requires iterating single-period transition matrices forward over time. Any specification error—such as truncated lag lengths or missing state variables—compounds exponentially across projection horizons. Jordà local projections solve this vulnerability by estimating separate, direct predictive regressions for each horizon, insulating medium- and long-term policy multipliers from misspecification bias.

1. The Iteration Bottleneck in Vector Autoregressions

In standard macroeconomic time-series analysis (vector autoregressions), dynamic causal transmission is computed by estimating a one-step-ahead linear model:

\[ \mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{A}_1 \mathbf{y}_{t-1} + \dots + \mathbf{A}_p \mathbf{y}_{t-p} + \mathbf{u}_t \]

The impulse response function (IRF) at horizon \(h\) is obtained by iterating the transition matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) forward: \(\text{IRF}(h) = \mathbf{A}^h \mathbf{B} \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\). While asymptotically efficient if the data-generating process (DGP) is strictly a VAR(\(p\)), iterated impulse responses suffer from two severe vulnerabilities:

  1. Compounding Misspecification: If the true lag length exceeds \(p\), or if underlying structural dynamics contain moving-average (MA) components or nonlinearities, computing \(\mathbf{A}^h\) powers up the one-step approximation error exponentially.
  2. Curse of Dimensionality: Adding nonlinearities, state dependence (e.g. recession vs. boom), or asymmetry requires complex generalized impulse response functions (GIRFs) requiring high-dimensional numerical integration.

2. The Jordà (2005) Estimator

To eliminate iterative compounding, local projections estimate dynamic responses by projecting the variable of interest at horizon \(t+h\) directly onto the identified shock and controls via independent horizon-specific regressions.

Definition 9.1 (Univariate and Multivariate Local Projection). For each horizon \(h \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, H\}\), estimate the linear regression: \[ y_{t+h} - y_{t-1} = \alpha^{(h)} + \beta^{(h)} \text{shock}_t + \sum_{j=1}^p \boldsymbol{\gamma}_j^{(h)\top} \mathbf{x}_{t-j} + u_{t+h}^{(h)} \] where \(y_{t+h} - y_{t-1}\) is the cumulative change in the target macroeconomic variable from the period prior to the shock, \(\text{shock}_t\) is the identified monetary policy innovation (such as a high-frequency surprise or narrative shock), \(\mathbf{x}_{t-j}\) is a vector of predetermined macroeconomic control variables, and \(\beta^{(h)} = \frac{\partial y_{t+h}}{\partial \text{shock}_t}\) is the local projection impulse response at horizon \(h\).

Because each horizon \(h\) is estimated independently by ordinary least squares, errors at horizon \(h=1\) do not contaminate parameter estimates at horizon \(h=12\) or \(h=24\).

3. Serial Correlation and Newey-West HAC Inference

While the local projection estimator is computationally straightforward, the structure of its error terms requires careful statistical treatment.

Theorem 9.1 (Moving Average Error Structure of Multi-Step Horizons). Even if the fundamental structural macroeconomic shocks \(e_t\) are serially uncorrelated white noise, the multi-step forecast error \(u_{t+h}^{(h)}\) is inherently autocorrelated as an \(\text{MA}(h)\) moving average process.

Proof. Consider the forecast error at horizon \(h\): \[ u_{t+h}^{(h)} = y_{t+h} - \E_{t}[y_{t+h}] = \sum_{k=1}^h \psi_k e_{t+k} \] Now consider the forecast error for an observation originating in adjacent period \(t+1\) at horizon \(h-1\): \[ u_{t+1+(h-1)}^{(h-1)} = u_{t+h}^{(h-1)} = \sum_{j=1}^{h-1} \psi_j e_{t+1+j} = \sum_{k=2}^h \psi_{k-1} e_{t+k} \] Because both \(u_{t+h}^{(h)}\) and \(u_{t+h-1}^{(h)}\) share structural innovations \(\{e_{t+2}, \dots, e_{t+h}\}\), their covariance is strictly non-zero: \(\E[u_{t+h}^{(h)} u_{t+h-s}^{(h)}] \neq 0\) for all \(s \le h\). For all \(s > h\), \(\E[u_{t+h}^{(h)} u_{t+h-s}^{(h)}] = 0\). Thus, the error process is an \(\text{MA}(h)\) process by mathematical identity. This completes the proof.

Consequently, standard OLS standard errors are severely biased downwards. Hypothesis testing on local projection impulse responses requires Heteroskedasticity and Autocorrelation Consistent (HAC) covariance matrices, such as the Newey-West estimator with lag truncation parameter \(L \ge h + 1\):

\[ \widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}} = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_0 + \sum_{l=1}^L \left(1 - \frac{l}{L+1}\right) (\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_l + \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_l^\top) \]

Lab E7-L09-1: Estimating Impulse Responses via Jordà Local Projections

Hypothesis: Local projections estimate unbiased impulse responses to identified monetary shocks without compounding misspecification bias, outperforming misspecified iterated VARs.

"""Lab E7-L09-1: Jordà (2005) Local Projections for Policy Impulse Responses.

Estimates impulse response functions to an identified monetary shock using:
1. Jordà (2005) Local Projections (direct single-step multi-horizon regressions)
2. Classical Iterated VAR(1) extrapolation

Demonstrates that local projections do not compound misspecification bias at long horizons.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def estimate_lp_irf(y: list[float], shock: list[float], max_horizon: int) -> list[float]:
    """Calculate univariate local projection IRFs without compounding parametric restrictions."""
    n = len(y)
    irf: list[float] = []

    for h in range(max_horizon + 1):
        # Regression: y_{t+h} - y_{t-1} = alpha + beta^(h) * shock_t + e_{t+h}
        n_obs = n - h - 1
        y_target = [y[t + h] - y[t - 1] for t in range(1, n_obs + 1)]
        s_reg = [shock[t] for t in range(1, n_obs + 1)]

        mean_s = sum(s_reg) / len(s_reg)
        mean_y = sum(y_target) / len(y_target)

        cov_sy = sum((s_reg[i] - mean_s) * (y_target[i] - mean_y) for i in range(len(s_reg)))
        var_s = sum((s_reg[i] - mean_s) ** 2 for i in range(len(s_reg)))

        beta_h = cov_sy / var_s if var_s > 1e-12 else 0.0
        irf.append(beta_h)

    return irf


def estimate_var_iterated_irf(y: list[float], shock: list[float], max_horizon: int) -> list[float]:
    """Calculate iterated VAR(1) impulse response function: y_t = rho * y_{t-1} + gamma * shock_t."""
    n = len(y)
    # Estimate VAR(1) parameters
    y_dep = [y[t] for t in range(1, n)]
    y_lag = [y[t - 1] for t in range(1, n)]
    s_curr = [shock[t] for t in range(1, n)]

    # Simple bivariate OLS for y_t on y_{t-1} and shock_t
    # Invert 2x2 matrix
    s11 = sum(y_lag[i] ** 2 for i in range(len(y_dep)))
    s12 = sum(y_lag[i] * s_curr[i] for i in range(len(y_dep)))
    s22 = sum(s_curr[i] ** 2 for i in range(len(y_dep)))

    det = s11 * s22 - s12 * s12
    sy1 = sum(y_lag[i] * y_dep[i] for i in range(len(y_dep)))
    sy2 = sum(s_curr[i] * y_dep[i] for i in range(len(y_dep)))

    rho = (s22 * sy1 - s12 * sy2) / det
    gamma = (s11 * sy2 - s12 * sy1) / det

    # Iterated impulse responses: IRF(h) = rho^h * gamma
    irf = [gamma * (rho**h) for h in range(max_horizon + 1)]
    return irf


def main() -> None:
    # Synthetic simulation where true DGP has a nonlinear hump at horizon h=2
    # shock innovates at t=0
    import random

    rng = random.Random("E7-L09-simulated")
    n_samples = 250
    shock = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n_samples)]
    y = [0.0] * n_samples
    # True DGP: y_t = 0.6 * y_{t-1} - 0.4 * shock_{t-1} - 0.7 * shock_{t-2} + e_t
    for t in range(2, n_samples):
        y[t] = 0.60 * y[t - 1] - 0.35 * shock[t - 1] - 0.65 * shock[t - 2] + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.4)

    h_max = 6
    lp_irf = estimate_lp_irf(y, shock, h_max)
    var_irf = estimate_var_iterated_irf(y, shock, h_max)

    print("Impulse Response Function Estimation (Jordà 2005 vs Iterated VAR):")
    print("  Horizon h | Jordà Local Projection | Iterated VAR(1) | Difference (Bias)")
    for h in range(h_max + 1):
        diff = lp_irf[h] - var_irf[h]
        print(f"     h={h}   |         {lp_irf[h]:+6.4f}         |     {var_irf[h]:+6.4f}    |      {diff:+6.4f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

While local projections provide robustness against dynamic misspecification, they face notable trade-offs:

  • Efficiency Loss at Long Horizons: While local projections are robust to lag misspecification, they are statistically less efficient than a correctly specified VAR. By discarding the cross-equation dynamic restrictions of a structural VAR, local projection point estimates exhibit substantially wider confidence intervals at horizons \(h \ge 18\).
  • Sample Truncation at the Boundary: For a maximum projection horizon \(H\), the effective estimation sample size for the \(H\)-step ahead regression is reduced from \(T\) to \(T - H\). In small macroeconomic samples (e.g. quarterly post-2008 data with \(T \approx 60\)), projecting to \(H=20\) quarters discards one-third of the available degrees of freedom.
  • Jagged Impulse Curves: Because each \(\beta^{(h)}\) is estimated independently without smoothing priors, raw local projection impulse response plots frequently display jagged oscillations from quarter to quarter, in contrast to the smooth decay of parametric VARs. Modern practice often incorporates spline basis functions or Gaussian process smoothing.

Misconceptions

  1. Local projection residuals are i.i.d. because the identified monetary shock is exogenous.

    Even when the monetary shock is purely exogenous, multi-step projection residuals \(u_{t+h}^{(h)}\) contain subsequent macroeconomic shocks that occur between \(t+1\) and \(t+h\). They form an \(\text{MA}(h)\) process by mathematical identity, mandating HAC standard errors.

  2. Local projections always produce superior impulse responses compared to VARs.

    If the true DGP is well approximated by a low-order VAR, the iterated VAR estimator is the minimum-variance unbiased estimator. Local projections exhibit higher variance, trading efficiency for robustness against misspecification.

  3. Local projections require estimating a full simultaneous equation system.

    Local projections can be estimated equation-by-equation via single-equation OLS. If only the response of unemployment to monetary policy is needed, no other endogenous equations need to be specified or solved.

Problem Set

Problem 9.1 (Local Projection Coefficient Calculation). You regress the cumulative 12-month change in log industrial production \(y_{t+12} - y_{t-1}\) on an identified monetary shock \(s_t\) over 180 months. The sample covariance between \(s_t\) and \(y_{t+12} - y_{t-1}\) is \(-0.0075\), and the sample variance of \(s_t\) is \(0.0050\). Calculate the local projection impulse response point estimate \(\hat{\beta}^{(12)}\).

Solution

The bivariate OLS local projection coefficient is: \[ \hat{\beta}^{(12)} = \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(s_t, y_{t+12} - y_{t-1})}{\widehat{\Var}(s_t)} = \frac{-0.0075}{0.0050} = -1.50 \] This indicates that a 1-unit (100 basis point) contractionary monetary shock reduces industrial production by 1.50% after 12 months.

Problem 9.2 (Newey-West Truncation Lag Selection). You are estimating local projections at horizons \(h = 0, 1, 2, \dots, 24\) months. According to theoretical properties of multi-step forecast errors, what is the minimum truncation lag \(L\) for the Newey-West kernel at horizon \(h=18\)?

Solution

Because the multi-step forecast error is an \(\text{MA}(h)\) moving average process, the autocovariance of residuals is nonzero for lags up to \(s = h\). To ensure all nonzero autocovariances receive positive kernel weight, the Newey-West lag truncation parameter must satisfy: \[ L \ge h + 1 = 18 + 1 = 19 \] Practitioners commonly set \(L = h + 1\) or use data-dependent bandwidth selectors like Andrews (1991).

Problem 9.3 (Degrees of Freedom Degradation). A macroeconomic dataset contains quarterly observations from 2000:Q1 to 2023:Q4 (\(T = 96\) quarters). You include 4 lags of 3 control variables (\(K = 1 + 1 + 12 = 14\) parameters). Calculate the remaining degrees of freedom at horizon \(h=0\) and horizon \(h=16\).

Solution

With 4 lags, 4 quarters are lost to initial conditioning, leaving 92 usable baseline periods. At horizon \(h=0\): usable observations \(N_0 = 92\), degrees of freedom \(df_0 = 92 - 14 = 78\). At horizon \(h=16\): 16 future periods are lost at the sample boundary, leaving usable observations \(N_{16} = 92 - 16 = 76\), degrees of freedom \(df_{16} = 76 - 14 = 62\). Degrees of freedom decline by 20.5% at the 4-year horizon.

Problem 9.4 (Population Equivalence under a True VAR(1)). Prove that if the true data-generating process is a stationary VAR(1): \(y_t = \rho y_{t-1} + \gamma s_t + e_t\) with \(|\rho| < 1\), the population local projection coefficient \(\beta^{(h)}\) in the regression \(y_{t+h} = \beta^{(h)} s_t + \dots\) exactly equals the iterated VAR impulse response \(\gamma \rho^h\).

Solution

By repeated forward substitution of the VAR(1): \[ y_{t+h} = \rho^h y_t + \sum_{j=1}^h \rho^{h-j} (\gamma s_{t+j} + e_{t+j}) = \rho^h (\rho y_{t-1} + \gamma s_t + e_t) + \sum_{j=1}^h \rho^{h-j} (\gamma s_{t+j} + e_{t+j}) \] Grouping terms: \[ y_{t+h} = \rho^{h+1} y_{t-1} + (\gamma \rho^h) s_t + \left[ \rho^h e_t + \sum_{j=1}^h \rho^{h-j} (\gamma s_{t+j} + e_{t+j}) \right] \] Since \(s_t\) is exogenous and independent of future shocks and conditioning on \(y_{t-1}\), the population projection of \(y_{t+h}\) on \(s_t\) conditional on \(y_{t-1}\) yields: \[ \beta^{(h)} = \frac{\partial \E[y_{t+h} \mid y_{t-1}, s_t]}{\partial s_t} = \gamma \rho^h \] Thus, under a correctly specified VAR, local projections and iterated VARs converge to the exact same population parameter.

Problem 9.5 (Misspecification Robustness Proof). Suppose the true DGP is a VAR(2): \(y_t = \rho_1 y_{t-1} + \rho_2 y_{t-2} + \gamma s_t + e_t\), but the researcher mistakenly estimates an iterated VAR(1). Prove that the iterated VAR(1) impulse response at horizon \(h=2\) is biased, while the local projection estimator at horizon \(h=2\) remains unbiased.

Solution

In the misspecified VAR(1), the estimated coefficient is \(\hat{\rho}_1 = \rho_1 + \rho_2 \frac{\Cov(y_{t-1}, y_{t-2})}{\Var(y_{t-1})} \neq \rho_1\). The iterated VAR response at \(h=2\) is: \[ \text{IRF}^{\text{VAR}}(2) = \hat{\gamma} \hat{\rho}_1^2 \] However, from the true VAR(2), the actual response at \(h=2\) is: \[ y_{t+2} = (\rho_1^2 + \rho_2) \gamma s_t + \dots \implies \text{True IRF}(2) = (\rho_1^2 + \rho_2) \gamma \] Since \(\hat{\rho}_1^2 \gamma \neq (\rho_1^2 + \rho_2) \gamma\) in general, the iterated VAR estimator is biased. In contrast, the local projection regresses \(y_{t+2}\) directly on \(s_t\). Because \(s_t\) is orthogonal to lagged \(y\) and future errors, \(\E[\hat{\beta}^{(2)}] = \frac{\partial \E[y_{t+2}]}{\partial s_t} = (\rho_1^2 + \rho_2)\gamma\), which is completely unbiased.

Problem 9.6 (Consistency of State-Dependent Local Projections). Show that when the state indicator \(S_t\) is predetermined at time \(t\) (e.g. determined by indicators up to \(t-1\)), the OLS interaction estimator in the state-dependent local projection consistently estimates \(\beta_1^{(h)}\) and \(\beta_0^{(h)}\) without requiring structural transition matrices.

Solution

Let \(\mathbf{z}_t = [S_t s_t, (1-S_t)s_t]^\top\). If \(S_t \in \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) and \(s_t\) is an exogenous shock such that \(\E[s_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\), then: \[ \E[u_{t+h}^{(h)} \mid \mathbf{z}_t, \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0 \] Because the regressor matrix has full column rank asymptotically as long as both states occur with positive probability, standard OLS consistency holds: \[ \text{plim } \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\beta}_1^{(h)} \\ \hat{\beta}_0^{(h)} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \beta_1^{(h)} \\ \beta_0^{(h)} \end{bmatrix} \] Unlike nonlinear threshold VARs, no modeling of the transition law between states is required.

Problem 9.7 (Local Projections with Instrumental Variables). Explain how Local Projections Instrumental Variables (LP-IV, Gertler & Karadi 2015) identifies the impulse response of output to a policy rate change \(\Delta i_t\) using a high-frequency surprise \(z_t\) as an external instrument. Write out the two-stage regression equations.

Solution

In LP-IV, the policy rate change \(\Delta i_t\) is endogenous, but an external instrument \(z_t\) satisfies relevance \(\Cov(z_t, \Delta i_t) \neq 0\) and exogeneity \(\Cov(z_t, u_{t+h}) = 0\). Stage 1 (instrumented policy rate): \[ \Delta i_t = \pi_0 + \pi_1 z_t + \boldsymbol{\pi}_x^\top \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + v_t \] Stage 2 (local projection for each horizon \(h\)): \[ y_{t+h} - y_{t-1} = \alpha^{(h)} + \beta^{(h)} \widehat{\Delta i}_t + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(h)\top} \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + u_{t+h}^{(h)} \] The 2SLS estimate \(\hat{\beta}^{(h)}\) measures the causal response of \(y_{t+h}\) to a 1-unit exogenous increase in the policy rate.

Problem 9.8 (Smooth Local Projections). Empirical local projection IRF plots frequently look erratic. Explain the Barnichon and Matthes (2018) smooth local projections approach using B-splines or polynomial regularizers to reduce sample variance.

Solution

Barnichon and Matthes approximate the sequence of coefficients \(\{\beta^{(h)}\}_{h=0}^H\) as a linear combination of a small number of smooth basis functions (such as cubic B-splines): \[ \beta^{(h)} = \sum_{k=1}^K a_k B_k(h) \] where \(K \ll H\). Instead of estimating \(H+1\) unconstrained parameters, the system estimates \(K\) basis coefficients \(a_k\) jointly across all horizons. This imposes smoothness across adjacent horizons, shrinking high-frequency sample variance and bridging the gap between flexible local projections and smooth VAR dynamics.

Problem 9.9 (Monetary Policy Transmission at the ZLB). Formulate an empirical test using local projections to evaluate whether contractionary monetary policy shocks have larger output effects than expansionary shocks when policy rates are near the zero lower bound.

Solution

Define two dummy variables: \(D_t^+ = \mathbb{I}(\text{shock}_t > 0)\) (tightening) and \(D_t^- = \mathbb{I}(\text{shock}_t < 0)\) (easing), interacted with a ZLB regime indicator \(ZLB_t\): \[ y_{t+h} - y_{t-1} = \beta_{ZLB,+}^{(h)} (ZLB_t \cdot D_t^+ s_t) + \beta_{ZLB,-}^{(h)} (ZLB_t \cdot D_t^- s_t) + \dots \] Test the null hypothesis of symmetry at the ZLB: \(H_0: |\beta_{ZLB,+}^{(h)}| = |\beta_{ZLB,-}^{(h)}|\) via a Wald test with Newey-West standard errors. If \(|\beta_{ZLB,+}^{(h)}| > |\beta_{ZLB,-}^{(h)}|\), tightening remains potent at the ZLB while easing is blunted by the lower bound.

Problem 9.10 (High-Dimensional Local Projections). When the number of macro controls \(P\) is large relative to sample size \(T\), standard OLS local projections suffer from overfitting. Design a penalized regularized local projection and establish whether standard penalization maintains \(\sqrt{T}\)-consistency for \(\beta^{(h)}\).

Solution

A naive LASSO penalizing all coefficients including \(\beta^{(h)}\) induces regularization shrinkage bias, violating \(\sqrt{T}\)-consistency. To preserve unbiased inference, employ the Double/Debiased Machine Learning (Chernozhukov et al.) framework: 1. Partial out controls \(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\) from target \(y_{t+h}\) using cross-fitted LASSO: \(\tilde{y}_{t+h} = y_{t+h} - \hat{g}(x)\). 2. Partial out controls from the shock: \(\tilde{s}_t = s_t - \hat{m}(x)\). 3. Regress \(\tilde{y}_{t+h}\) on \(\tilde{s}_t\) without penalty. This Neyman-orthogonal score guarantees that first-stage machine learning approximation errors decay at rate \(o_p(T^{-1/2})\), restoring asymptotic normality and valid confidence intervals.

Problem 9.11 (Panel Local Projections with Country Fixed Effects). In international central banking studies, researchers estimate panel local projections across \(N\) countries. Analyze the Nickell (1981) bias when country fixed effects and lagged dependent variables are included in multi-step local projections.

Solution

In panel regressions with fixed effects \(\alpha_i\) and lagged dependent variables \(y_{i, t-1}\), the within-transformation introduces a correlation between the demeaned regressor and the demeaned error of order \(\mathcal{O}(1/T)\). In multi-step local projections where the error is \(\text{MA}(h)\), this Nickell bias extends across \(h+1\) periods. If \(T\) is large (\(T \ge 80\) quarters), Nickell bias is negligible; for short panels (\(T < 30\)), GMM estimators (Arellano-Bond) or analytical bias corrections must be applied to avoid distorting long-horizon impulse responses.

Problem 9.12 (Plagborg-Møller & Wolf Equivalence Result). Plagborg-Møller & Wolf (2021) demonstrate that local projections and vector autoregressions estimate the exact same population impulse responses if lag lengths are allowed to grow with sample size. Critically appraise this theorem in empirical finite samples.

Solution

The Plagborg-Møller and Wolf theorem proves non-parametric population equivalence: as lag length \(p \to \infty\) with \(p/T \to 0\), the population linear projection of \(y_{t+h}\) onto the shock history spanned by a VAR(\(\infty\)) is identical to the population local projection. However, in finite macroeconomic samples (\(T \sim 200\)), researchers cannot set \(p \to \infty\). If a researcher truncates the VAR at \(p=2\) while true dynamics require \(p=8\), the iterated VAR produces substantial bias, whereas local projections remain robust. Conversely, if true dynamics are VAR(1), local projections pay a finite-sample efficiency penalty.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the fundamental difference between an iterated VAR impulse response and a Jordà local projection?

    Options

    Jordà local projections estimate horizon-specific regressions directly, avoiding the exponential compounding of misspecified transition matrices.

  2. Why are the residuals \(u_{t+h}^{(h)}\) in a multi-step local projection serially correlated?

    Options

    By mathematical identity, an \(h\)-step ahead forecast error incorporates structural innovations occurring from \(t+1\) through \(t+h\), creating an \(\text{MA}(h)\) error structure.

  3. What is the minimum Newey-West truncation lag \(L = h + 1\) for a local projection at horizon \(h=18\)?

    \(L = 18 + 1 = 19\).

  4. What covariance matrix estimator is required when conducting inference on local projection impulse responses?

    Options

    Due to the moving average error structure of multi-step projections, HAC standard errors with bandwidth \(L \ge h+1\) are mandatory.

  5. What is the primary econometric trade-off of using local projections instead of a correctly specified VAR?

    Options

    By not imposing cross-equation restrictions, local projections are robust to misspecification but sacrifice sample efficiency, leading to wider confidence intervals at long horizons.

  6. If the covariance between \(s_t\) and \(y_{t+12} - y_{t-1}\) is -0.0075 and the variance of \(s_t\) is 0.0050, what is \(\hat{\beta}^{(12)}\)?

    \(\hat{\beta}^{(12)} = -0.0075 / 0.0050 = -1.50\).

  7. How does a researcher test for state-dependent monetary policy transmission in a local projection?

    Options

    State dependence is evaluated by interacting the shock with state dummies (e.g. recession vs. boom) and performing a Wald test on coefficient differences.

  8. According to Plagborg-Møller & Wolf (2021), when do local projections and VARs yield identical population impulse responses?

    Options

    Non-parametrically, when lag lengths tend to infinity, the population linear projections of both methods converge to the identical structural impulse responses.
