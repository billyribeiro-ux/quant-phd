Quantitative Easing: Channels and Evidence

When short-term nominal interest rates reach the effective lower bound, central banks resort to large-scale asset purchases. Disentangling whether quantitative easing works primarily through duration risk extraction (portfolio balance) or through committing to keep future short rates low (signalling) is fundamental for evaluating balance-sheet policy.

1. The Mechanics of Quantitative Easing

Quantitative easing (QE) is an unconventional balance-sheet policy in which the central bank creates central bank reserves to purchase large volumes of longer-term government bonds, agency securities, and private assets from the private financial sector. As Bernanke (2020) outlined, the Federal Reserve conducted three major rounds of Large-Scale Asset Purchases (QE1, QE2, and QE3) between 2008 and 2014, expanding its asset holdings from $900 billion to $4.5 trillion.

In a frictionless financial market satisfying the Wallace (1981) irrelevance neutrality theorem, central bank asset purchases would have zero effect on asset prices: private households would simply undo central bank balance-sheet trades. For quantitative easing to alter equilibrium yields, financial markets must exhibit structural frictions, segmented investor clienteles, or asymmetric information.

2. The Core Transmission Channels

The empirical and theoretical literature decomposes QE transmission into distinct channels, most comprehensively formalized by Krishnamurthy & Vissing-Jorgensen (2011):

Definition 6.1 (Portfolio-Balance Channel). The portfolio-balance channel operates through preferred-habitat investors and risk-averse arbitrageurs. When the central bank purchases long-duration bonds, it extracts aggregate duration risk from private balance sheets. Arbitrageurs require a lower term premium to hold the remaining bond supply, compressing yields across all imperfectly substitutable fixed income assets.

Definition 6.2 (Signalling Channel). The signalling channel operates by altering expectations of future short-term policy rates. Because the central bank purchases massive portfolios of long-duration fixed-rate assets, an early hike in the policy rate would inflict immense capital losses on the central bank's own balance sheet. Committing balance-sheet capital serves as a credible signal that the central bank will keep short-term policy rates low for longer than previously expected.

Beyond portfolio balance and signalling, Krishnamurthy & Vissing-Jorgensen (2011) identify:

  • Safety / Scarcity Premium Channel: Treasuries possess extreme safety convenience yields. Removing Treasury supply increases their scarcity premium, lowering Treasury yields relative to corporate or agency debt.
  • Liquidity Channel: Central bank open market purchases enhance trading volume and narrow bid-ask spreads during market freezes (especially prominent in agency MBS during QE1).
  • Default Risk / Credit Channel: Macroeconomic stimulus lowers projected corporate default probabilities, narrowing credit spreads.

Lab E7-L06-1: Event-Study Decomposition of QE Announcement Yields

Hypothesis: Event-study decomposition across 2-year and 10-year Treasury and agency yields reveals that signalling and portfolio-balance duration extraction both contribute significantly to total yield declines.

"""Lab E7-L06-1: QE Transmission Channels: Portfolio Balance vs Signalling.

Decomposes Treasury and agency yields around quantitative easing announcements
into distinct transmission channels per Krishnamurthy & Vissing-Jorgensen (2011):
1. Signalling channel: captured by short-to-medium Fed funds futures and OIS rate declines
2. Portfolio-balance / duration risk channel: maturity-gradient yield declines
3. Safety / scarcity premium channel: CDS and Treasury-agency spread adjustments
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def decompose_qe_event_window(
    yield_change_10y_treasury_bps: float,  # Total 10-year Treasury yield change
    yield_change_2y_treasury_bps: float,  # 2-year yield change (proxy for policy expectations)
    yield_change_10y_agency_bps: float,  # 10-year Agency debt yield change
    term_premium_loading_ratio: float,  # Relative duration loading of 10y vs 2y (e.g. 10y duration / 2y duration)
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Decompose total yield change into Signalling, Duration Risk, and Safety/Scarcity components."""
    # Under Krishnamurthy-Vissing-Jorgensen (2011):
    # 2y yield is predominantly driven by policy expectations / signalling:
    signalling_component = yield_change_2y_treasury_bps

    # The duration risk / portfolio balance component impacts 10y beyond 2y:
    duration_portfolio_balance = (yield_change_10y_treasury_bps - signalling_component) * (
        1.0 - (1.0 / term_premium_loading_ratio)
    )

    # Safety premium is reflected in the relative Treasury-Agency spread change:
    # Treasuries carry a unique safe-asset convenience yield that rises when Treasury supply shrinks
    safety_spread_change = yield_change_10y_treasury_bps - yield_change_10y_agency_bps

    residual_liquidity = yield_change_10y_treasury_bps - signalling_component - duration_portfolio_balance

    return {
        "total_change_bps": yield_change_10y_treasury_bps,
        "signalling_bps": signalling_component,
        "portfolio_balance_bps": duration_portfolio_balance,
        "safety_premium_bps": safety_spread_change,
        "residual_liquidity_bps": residual_liquidity,
    }


def main() -> None:
    # Representative calibration for QE1 announcement (November 2008 / March 2009)
    # Total 10y yield drop of ~100 bps
    dy_10y = -107.0
    dy_2y = -35.0
    dy_agency = -135.0
    duration_ratio = 4.5  # 10y duration ~ 8.5 vs 2y duration ~ 1.9

    decomp = decompose_qe_event_window(dy_10y, dy_2y, dy_agency, duration_ratio)

    print("QE Announcement Yield Decomposition (Krishnamurthy-Vissing-Jorgensen 2011):")
    print(f"  Total 10Y Treasury yield change: {decomp['total_change_bps']:.1f} bps")
    print("\nChannel Decomposition:")
    sig_share = decomp["signalling_bps"] / decomp["total_change_bps"]
    pb_share = decomp["portfolio_balance_bps"] / decomp["total_change_bps"]
    print(f"  1. Signalling channel:          {decomp['signalling_bps']:.1f} bps ({sig_share:.1%})")
    print(f"  2. Portfolio-balance (duration):{decomp['portfolio_balance_bps']:.1f} bps ({pb_share:.1%})")
    print(f"  3. Safety/Scarcity channel:     {decomp['safety_premium_bps']:.1f} bps (Agency spread expansion)")
    print(f"  4. Residual liquidity channel:  {decomp['residual_liquidity_bps']:.1f} bps")


main()

  Total 10Y Treasury yield change: -107.0 bps

Channel Decomposition:
  1. Signalling channel:          -35.0 bps (32.7%)
  2. Portfolio-balance (duration):-56.0 bps (52.3%)
  3. Safety/Scarcity channel:     28.0 bps (Agency spread expansion)
  4. Residual liquidity channel:  -16.0 bps

The lab confirms that for an event drop of -107 bps in 10Y Treasuries, the signalling channel accounts for -35.0 bps (32.7%), duration portfolio balance accounts for -56.0 bps (52.3%), and the safety premium expands by +28.0 bps as agency yields drop faster than Treasuries.

Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook models of quantitative easing exhibit substantial limitations when tested empirically:

  • Diminishing Marginal Returns: QE1 produced massive announcement yield reductions (over 100 bps), but subsequent rounds (QE2, QE3) produced substantially smaller announcement effects (15 to 30 bps), reflecting normalization of liquidity premia.
  • Announcement vs. Flow Effects: Financial markets price QE upon announcement via forward-looking expectations, but physical settlement flows occasionally induce localized market dislocation when specific bond cusips become scarce.
  • Asymmetric Exit Dynamics: Expanding the balance sheet during a crisis compresses spreads smoothly, but announcing balance sheet runoff ('Taper Tantrum' in May 2013) triggered abrupt spikes in term premia and emerging market capital flight.

Debate Brief · Does QE Work Mainly Through Portfolio Balance or Signalling?

Resolution: The macroeconomic and term structure impact of quantitative easing operates predominantly through the extraction of duration and credit risk in segmented asset markets (the portfolio-balance channel) rather than through forward guidance regarding future policy rates (the signalling channel).

Strongest Case for the Portfolio-Balance Channel

Krishnamurthy & Vissing-Jorgensen (2011) document that Treasury yields exhibit a distinct maturity gradient on QE announcement days: 10-year and 30-year yields fell dramatically, while 1-year and 2-year yields experienced much smaller declines. Furthermore, purchases of agency MBS lowered mortgage borrowing rates far more than Treasury purchases did, demonstrating that market segmentation and asset-specific risk extraction are first-order drivers. In preferred-habitat theory (Vayanos & Vila), arbitrageurs cannot perfectly substitute across maturities, meaning that removing long-term duration directly compresses term premia independently of expected future short rates.

Strongest Case for the Signalling Channel

Proponents of the signalling channel (Bauer & Rudebusch 2014) argue that affine term structure decompositions attribute a majority of long-term yield declines on FOMC announcement days to reductions in expected future short rates rather than term premia. As Bernanke (2020) noted, QE operations signal an aggressive, prolonged commitment to monetary accommodation. Because raising policy rates prematurely would impose severe capital losses on the central bank's bond holdings, large-scale asset purchases serve as a costly, credible commitment device under Kydland-Prescott time inconsistency, anchoring future rate expectations at zero.

Misconceptions

  1. Quantitative easing is equivalent to printing money and mailing checks directly to households.

    QE is an asset swap: the central bank purchases existing interest-bearing bonds from private investors and pays by crediting electronic bank reserves. Total private net wealth is unchanged at the instant of the transaction.

  2. The portfolio-balance channel requires investors to immediately spend their cash on consumer goods.

    Portfolio rebalancing occurs primarily within asset markets: investors who sell Treasuries to the Fed reinvest their proceeds into corporate bonds, equities, and real estate, bidding up asset prices and lowering borrowing costs.

  3. QE only lowers the yield of the exact bond issues purchased by the central bank.

    Because arbitrageurs rebalance across substitute maturities and asset classes, duration extraction compresses risk premia across the entire fixed-income universe.

Problem Set

Problem 6.1 (Tenor Duration Weighting). If the central bank purchases $100B of 10-year Treasuries with modified duration 8.5, what equivalent dollar volume of 2-year Treasuries (modified duration 1.9) does this extract in terms of 10-year equivalent duration?

Solution

Total duration extracted is \(D_{\text{total}} = 100 \times 8.5 = 850\text{ duration-dollars}\). To extract the same duration using 2-year notes: \[ Q_{\text{2y}} = \frac{850}{1.9} \approx 447.37\text{ billion USD} \] Purchasing $100B of 10-year bonds extracts the duration equivalent of nearly $450B of 2-year notes.

Problem 6.2 (Yield Decomposition Split). On a QE announcement date, the 10-year Treasury yield drops by 40 bps. If affine term structure models attribute 25 bps to expected future short rate reductions, calculate the term premium reduction.

Solution

Under the expectations hypothesis decomposition \(\Delta y = \Delta \E[i] + \Delta \text{TP}\): \[ \Delta \text{TP} = \Delta y - \Delta \E[i] = -40 - (-25) = -15\text{ basis points} \] The term premium compressed by 15 basis points via the portfolio-balance channel.

Problem 6.3 (Corporate Spread Compression). Prior to QE, 10-year BBB corporate yields are 6.50% and 10-year Treasuries are 3.50%. Following QE, Treasuries fall to 3.00% and BBB corporates fall to 5.20%. Calculate the corporate credit spread reduction.

Solution

Initial spread: \(S_1 = 6.50\% - 3.50\% = 3.00\% = 300\text{ bps}\). Post-QE spread: \(S_2 = 5.20\% - 3.00\% = 2.20\% = 220\text{ bps}\). Spread change: \(\Delta S = 220 - 300 = -80\text{ basis points}\). Corporate borrowing costs fell by 130 bps in total, with 80 bps coming from credit spread compression.

Problem 6.4 (Wallace Neutrality Proof). Prove that in a frictionless Arrow-Debreu economy with complete markets and non-distortionary taxes, open market asset purchases by the central bank have zero effect on equilibrium allocations.

Solution

The consolidated government intertemporal budget constraint is \(\sum_s q(s) [G(s) - T(s)] = \sum_s q(s) \Pi_{\text{CB}}(s)\). When the central bank purchases asset \(A\) by issuing liability \(L\), it captures payoff \(\Pi_{\text{CB}} = R_A A - R_L L\). Because households own the government through future tax obligations, private budget sets adjust by exactly \(-\Pi_{\text{CB}}\). The central bank trade is redundant, proving Wallace (1981) neutrality.

Problem 6.5 (Vayanos-Vila Term Premium Slope). In the Vayanos & Vila (2021) preferred habitat model, prove that extracting duration risk from the market flattens the term structure slope \(\partial y / \partial \tau\).

Solution

Arbitrageurs hold aggregate duration risk \(D = \int \tau Q(\tau) d\tau\) with risk aversion \(\rho\). The term premium on maturity \(\tau\) is \(\text{TP}(\tau) = \rho \sigma_r^2 \tau D\). Differentiating with respect to maturity: \(\frac{\partial \text{TP}}{\partial \tau} = \rho \sigma_r^2 D\). When the central bank purchases long-term bonds, aggregate private duration \(D\) falls, strictly reducing the slope \(\frac{\partial \text{TP}}{\partial \tau}\) and flattening the yield curve.

Problem 6.6 (Signalling via Balance Sheet Losses). Formulate the central bank's capital loss penalty when raising rates early under QE asset holdings.

Solution

Let the central bank hold long-term fixed rate bonds \(B\) yielding \(y_0\). If it unexpectedly raises the short-term policy rate to \(i_1\), the market value of its bond holdings falls by \(\Delta V \approx -D \cdot B \cdot (i_1 - y_0)\). Because large capital losses suspend Treasury remittances and damage central bank political standing, this loss function serves as a credible commitment mechanism preventing early rate hikes.

Problem 6.7 (QE1 Announcement Yield Drop). On March 18, 2009, the FOMC announced plans to purchase up to $300B of Treasuries and an additional $750B of agency MBS. What was the 1-day change in the 10-year Treasury yield?

Solution

The 10-year Treasury yield plunged by 47 basis points (from 3.01% to 2.54%), the largest single-day drop since 1962, providing unambiguous empirical evidence of powerful market-wide QE announcement effects.

Problem 6.8 (The 2013 Taper Tantrum). On May 22, 2013, Chairman Ben Bernanke indicated the Fed might 'step down' its monthly purchase pace in coming meetings. Between May and September 2013, how much did the 10-year Treasury yield rise?

Solution

The 10-year Treasury yield surged from approximately 1.93% in May to nearly 3.00% in September (a spike of over 100 bps), driven primarily by an explosive rise in term premia as market participants repriced duration risk.

Problem 6.9 (Agency MBS Spread Behavior). In QE1, why did agency MBS yields drop by more than 100 basis points while Treasury yields fell by 40-50 basis points?

Solution

Agency MBS suffered from severe market freeze and mortgage prepayment uncertainty during late 2008. Fed purchases provided direct liquidity backstops and eliminated prepayment/duration risk, compressing the MBS-Treasury spread by over 60 basis points.

Problem 6.10 (QE vs. Conventional Policy Rate Equivalent). Bernanke (2020) estimated that $1 trillion of QE purchases reduced 10-year yields by roughly 20 basis points. In standard macroeconomic models, how many basis points of federal funds rate cuts achieve an equivalent yield response?

Solution

A conventional 25 bps cut in the federal funds rate typically lowers the 10-year Treasury yield by approximately 8 to 10 basis points. A 20 bps decline in 10-year yields from $1T of QE is therefore equivalent to roughly 50 to 60 basis points of conventional policy rate cuts.

Problem 6.11 (Collateral Scarcity Frictions). Explain how excessive central bank purchases of specific Treasury collateral can paradoxically increase repo market haircuts and impair money market functioning.

Solution

If the central bank buys too large a share of specific on-the-run Treasury bonds, dealers cannot source collateral to deliver into repo contracts. This causes repo fails, drives repo rates into negative territory for specific issues, and impairs market liquidity.

Problem 6.12 (Green QE and Portfolio Tilting). Analyze the economic implications of the European Central Bank tilting its corporate bond asset purchase program toward issuers with superior carbon intensity metrics.

Solution

Tilting bond purchases introduces an artificial relative price wedge: it compresses borrowing costs for green corporate issuers while raising relative funding costs for carbon-intensive firms, shifting central bank operations from neutral monetary policy to direct sectoral credit allocation.
