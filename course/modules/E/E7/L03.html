Central Bank Balance Sheets and Reserve Demand

Monetary policy implementation ultimately operates through the central bank's balance sheet. Understanding the accounting identities connecting asset purchases to commercial bank reserves, alongside the nonlinear reserve demand curve, is essential for modeling how central banks control overnight interest rates across scarce and abundant liquidity regimes.

1. Central Bank Balance Sheet Accounting

A central bank's balance sheet reflects its statutory functions: issuer of currency, banker to the commercial banking system, fiscal agent to the national Treasury, and provider of emergency lender-of-last-resort liquidity. As Bernanke (2020) emphasized, the composition and total scale of the balance sheet underwent a structural transformation following the 2008 financial crisis.

The consolidated central bank balance sheet satisfies the fundamental accounting identity:

\[ \text{Assets}_t \equiv \text{Liabilities}_t + \text{Capital}_t \]

On the asset side:

  • Government Securities: Domestic sovereign debt (Treasury bills, notes, and bonds).
  • Private & Agency Assets: Agency mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and corporate/commercial paper purchased during quantitative easing.
  • Discount Window Loans & Liquidity Facilities: Repurchase agreements (repos) and direct emergency lending to depository institutions.
  • Foreign Exchange Reserves: Sovereign foreign currency reserves and gold certificates.

On the liability side:

  • Currency in Circulation (\(C_t\)): Physical banknotes held by the non-bank public and vault cash.
  • Reserve Balances (\(R_t\)): Electronic deposit accounts held by commercial banks at the central bank.
  • Treasury General Account (\(\text{TGA}_t\)): The operating cash account of the sovereign government.
  • Overnight Reverse Repurchase Facility (\(\text{ON RRP}_t\)): Collateralized deposits from eligible non-bank financial institutions (money market funds and government-sponsored enterprises).

2. Autonomous Factors and Reserve Creation

Commercial bank reserve balances \(R_t\) represent the residual clearing liability of the central bank. Isolating reserves yields:

\[ R_t = \text{Assets}_t - \left( C_t + \text{TGA}_t + \text{ON RRP}_t + \text{Capital}_t \right) \]

The components inside parentheses are termed autonomous liquidity factors because their day-to-day fluctuations are driven by external economic activity and Treasury tax collections rather than monetary policy operations. For example, when corporations pay federal taxes, funds transfer from commercial bank deposits into the TGA, draining bank reserves dollar-for-dollar.

When the central bank purchases an asset (e.g. a Treasury bond) from a primary dealer, it pays by crediting the reserve account of the dealer's clearing bank. This expands both sides of the balance sheet simultaneously: central bank assets rise, and total reserve balances \(R_t\) expand.

3. The Nonlinear Reserve Demand Curve

In classical monetary theory (Poole 1968), commercial banks hold reserve balances for two reasons: satisfying statutory reserve requirements and managing intraday payment uncertainty to avoid overdraft penalties.

Definition 3.1 (Reserve Demand Curve). Let \(r_t\) denote the overnight interbank rate, \(i_{\text{IOR}}\) denote the interest paid on reserve balances, and \(i_{\text{DW}}\) denote the penalty discount window rate. The aggregate reserve demand function is: \[ r_t - i_{\text{IOR}} = \Phi\left( \frac{R_t - R^*}{\sigma_R} \right) \] where \(R^*\) represents the satiation point where banks' liquidity needs are fully satisfied, \(\sigma_R\) is payment shock volatility, and \(\Phi(\cdot)\) is a monotonically decreasing function bounded above by \(i_{\text{DW}} - i_{\text{IOR}}\) and bounded below by zero.

As Bernanke (2020) demonstrated, this generates two distinct operational regimes:

  1. Scarce Reserves Regime (\(R_t \ll R^*\)): The demand curve is steep. Small shifts in autonomous factors generate sharp fluctuations in the interbank rate, requiring daily open market operations to align reserves with demand.
  2. Abundant Reserves Regime (\(R_t \gg R^*\)): Reserves are abundant, satisfying all precautionary demand. The demand curve is completely flat, and the interbank rate is pegged to the administered floor \(i_{\text{IOR}}\).

Lab E7-L03-1: Central Bank Balance Sheet Expansion and Reserve Demand

Hypothesis: Massive asset purchases expand bank reserves into the flat region of the demand curve, compressing the interbank spread over the deposit facility floor to near zero.

"""Lab E7-L03-1: Central Bank Balance Sheet Mechanics and Reserve Demand.

Simulates central bank balance sheet expansion under asset purchases:
1. Assets: Treasury holdings, agency MBS, discount loans.
2. Liabilities: Currency in circulation, reserve balances, Treasury General Account (TGA), ON RRP.
3. Estimates the reserve demand elasticity function:
   r_t - i_IOR = f(Reserves)
   verifying the transition from a downward-sloping scarcity regime to a flat satiation plateau.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def simulate_balance_sheet_expansion(
    initial_assets: float,
    qe_purchase: float,
    currency_demand: float,
    tga_balance: float,
    on_rrp_balance: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Calculate central bank liabilities and total reserves following asset purchases."""
    total_assets = initial_assets + qe_purchase

    # Autonomous factors outside central bank direct daily control
    autonomous_liabilities = currency_demand + tga_balance + on_rrp_balance

    # Reserve balances adjust as the balancing liability item:
    # Assets = Liabilities + Capital => Reserves = Assets - Autonomous_Liabilities (assuming 0 net capital)
    reserves = total_assets - autonomous_liabilities
    if reserves < 0:
        raise ValueError("Autonomous liabilities exceed total assets")

    return {
        "total_assets": total_assets,
        "reserves": reserves,
        "autonomous_liabilities": autonomous_liabilities,
        "reserve_ratio": reserves / total_assets,
    }


def reserve_demand_curve(reserves: float, satiation_threshold: float, r_ceiling: float, r_floor: float) -> float:
    """Nonlinear reserve demand function (Poole 1968 / Afonso et al. 2020).

    When reserves < satiation_threshold, the market rate sits strictly above the floor
    and slopes downward. Once reserves exceed satiation_threshold, the market rate hits the floor.
    """
    if reserves <= 0:
        return r_ceiling

    # Logistic / smooth transition between ceiling and floor
    # As reserves -> 0, rate -> r_ceiling; as reserves >> threshold, rate -> r_floor
    spread = (r_ceiling - r_floor) / (1.0 + math.exp(4.0 * (reserves - satiation_threshold) / satiation_threshold))
    rate = r_floor + spread
    return rate


def main() -> None:
    # Units in billions USD
    initial_assets = 900.0  # Pre-crisis baseline
    qe_purchase = 3000.0  # Large-scale asset purchase program
    currency = 1200.0
    tga = 400.0
    on_rrp = 300.0

    bs = simulate_balance_sheet_expansion(initial_assets, qe_purchase, currency, tga, on_rrp)

    satiation = 1500.0  # Satiation point where demand becomes completely flat
    r_ceiling = 4.50  # Discount window rate (bps / %)
    r_floor = 3.50  # Interest on reserve balances (IORB)

    rate_pre = reserve_demand_curve(bs["total_assets"] - qe_purchase - currency - tga, satiation, r_ceiling, r_floor)
    rate_post = reserve_demand_curve(bs["reserves"], satiation, r_ceiling, r_floor)

    print("Central Bank Balance Sheet and Reserve Demand Dynamics:")
    print(f"  Pre-QE assets:   ${initial_assets:.1f}B")
    print(f"  QE expansion:    +${qe_purchase:.1f}B")
    print(f"  Post-QE assets:  ${bs['total_assets']:.1f}B")
    print(f"  Bank reserves:   ${bs['reserves']:.1f}B ({bs['reserve_ratio']:.1%} of assets)")
    print("\nInterbank Market Clearing Rate:")
    spread_pre = (rate_pre - r_floor) * 100
    spread_post = (rate_post - r_floor) * 100
    print(f"  Scarce reserves regime rate:  {rate_pre:.4f}% (spread over floor: +{spread_pre:.1f} bps)")
    print(f"  Abundant reserves regime rate: {rate_post:.4f}% (spread over floor: +{spread_post:.1f} bps)")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Traditional textbook descriptions of central bank balance sheets and money creation exhibit major real-world failures:

  • The Money Multiplier Myth: Classical textbooks teach that central banks control the money supply via a fixed reserve multiplier \(M = R / \text{rr}\). In reality, banks create deposits endogenously when making loans; reserves do not constrain credit in an abundant reserve regime.
  • Disregard of the TGA: Standard models assume central bank liabilities consist entirely of currency and bank reserves. In practice, Treasury cash balance swings in the TGA (often hundreds of billions of dollars) exert massive autonomous shocks on liquidity.
  • Sub-floor Leakages: The Poole model assumes all interbank participants can earn interest on reserves. In the US, non-bank cash lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, money market funds) lack IORB access, allowing the market rate to trade below the floor.

Misconceptions

  1. Central bank reserve creation causes runaway hyperinflation by automatically expanding commercial bank lending.

    Reserves created through asset purchases cannot leave the central bank's electronic ledger; they can only circulate between banks. Commercial banks lend based on borrower creditworthiness and capital adequacy, not reserve holdings.

  2. The central bank can expand its assets without altering its liabilities.

    Every asset purchase is settled by crediting reserve accounts or creating currency. Central bank balance sheet expansion is fundamentally constrained by double-entry accounting.

  3. Paying interest on reserves is a corporate subsidy to commercial banks.

    Interest on reserve balances is an essential monetary policy instrument. Without paying interest on reserves, central banks could not raise interest rates while maintaining abundant liquidity.

Problem Set

Problem 3.1 (Balance Sheet Residual Calculation). A central bank holds $7,500B in assets and $50B in capital. Currency in circulation is $2,300B, the TGA holds $750B, and the ON RRP facility holds $1,800B. Calculate the level of commercial bank reserve balances.

Solution

Applying the balance sheet identity: \[ \begin{aligned} R_t &= \text{Assets} - \text{Capital} - (C_t + \text{TGA}_t + \text{ON RRP}_t) \\ &= 7500 - 50 - (2300 + 750 + 1800) = 7450 - 4850 = 2600\text{ billion USD} \end{aligned} \] Commercial bank reserve balances equal $2,600 billion.

Problem 3.2 (TGA Tax Flow Impact). During corporate tax week, taxpayers transfer $250B from commercial bank checking accounts to the IRS account at the Fed (the TGA). Assuming no other central bank operations, what happens to commercial bank reserves?

Solution

The transaction debits commercial bank reserve accounts at the Fed and credits the Treasury General Account. Central bank total assets remain unchanged, but liabilities shift: \(\Delta \text{TGA} = +250\text{B}\) and \(\Delta R = -250\text{B}\). Commercial bank reserves drain by exactly $250 billion.

Problem 3.3 (Reserve Demand Spread). If the discount window rate is \(i_{\text{DW}} = 5.50\%\), IORB is \(i_{\text{IOR}} = 4.50\%\), and reserves are scarce such that the market rate clears at 5.15%, calculate the spread of the interbank rate over the IORB floor.

Solution

The spread over the floor is: \[ r - i_{\text{IOR}} = 5.15\% - 4.50\% = 0.65\% = 65\text{ basis points} \] The market rate trades 65 bps above the floor, well within the 100 bps corridor.

Problem 3.4 (Floor Satiation Slope Zero). Let the reserve demand curve be given by \(r(R) = i_{\text{IOR}} + (i_{\text{DW}} - i_{\text{IOR}}) e^{-\kappa (R - R^*)}\) for \(R \ge R^*\). Prove that as \(R \to \infty\), the elasticity of the interbank rate with respect to reserves approaches zero.

Solution

The derivative is \(r'(R) = -\kappa (i_{\text{DW}} - i_{\text{IOR}}) e^{-\kappa (R - R^*)}\). As \(R \to \infty\), \(e^{-\kappa (R - R^*)} \to 0\), so \(r'(R) \to 0\). The interest rate elasticity \(\epsilon_R = \frac{dr}{dR} \frac{R}{r} \to 0\). In abundant reserves, open market sales or purchases have zero marginal effect on the policy rate.

Problem 3.5 (Invariance of Abundant Regime to TGA Volatility). Prove that in the flat region of the reserve demand curve, autonomous fluctuations in the TGA do not alter the market clearing interest rate.

Solution

In the abundant regime, \(r(R) = i_{\text{IOR}}\) for all \(R \ge R^*\). If a TGA surge drains reserves from \(R_1\) to \(R_2\), as long as \(R_2 \ge R^*\), \(r(R_2) = i_{\text{IOR}} = r(R_1)\). The central bank does not need to conduct offsetting open market operations to maintain its target rate.

Problem 3.6 (Equivalence of Reverse Repos and Reserves). Show that an overnight reverse repo (ON RRP) drain of bank reserves leaves total central bank interest expenses unchanged if \(i_{\text{IOR}} = i_{\text{ON RRP}}\).

Solution

Total interest expense is \(E = i_{\text{IOR}} R + i_{\text{ON RRP}} \text{ONRRP}\). If a money market fund shifts funds from bank deposits to ON RRP, commercial bank reserves fall by \(\Delta\), and ON RRP rises by \(\Delta\). If \(i_{\text{IOR}} = i_{\text{ON RRP}} = i\), \(\Delta E = -i \Delta + i \Delta = 0\).

Problem 3.7 (September 2019 Repo Spike). On September 16-17, 2019, corporate tax dates and Treasury debt settlements drained bank reserves to approximately $1,400B, causing overnight repo rates to spike from 2.2% to nearly 10.0%. What does this empirical event reveal about \(R^*\)?

Solution

The spike revealed that the lowest comfortable level of reserves (LCLoR) was substantially higher than the Fed's prior estimate (~$1,000B). When reserves dropped below ~$1,450B, the financial system crossed from the flat abundant regime into the steep scarce regime, generating acute rate spikes.

Problem 3.8 (Estimating Precautionary Liquidity Demand). Following Basel III, large banks must maintain a Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) of High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) exceeding 100% of 30-day net cash outflows. If large US banks face $2,500B in potential outflows, what is the regulatory minimum reserve demand if Treasuries cover $1,500B?

Solution

To satisfy \(\text{HQLA} \ge \$2500\text{B}\) with Treasuries providing $1,500B, commercial banks must hold at least: \[ R_{\min} = 2500 - 1500 = 1000\text{ billion USD} \] Regulatory liquidity requirements permanently shifted the reserve demand curve to the right.

Problem 3.9 (Quantitative Tightening Runoff Rate). If the Federal Reserve caps monthly Treasury runoff at $60B and MBS runoff at $35B, what is the maximum annual contraction in central bank assets?

Solution

Total monthly runoff cap is \(60 + 35 = 95\text{ billion USD}\). The maximum annual contraction is: \[ \Delta \text{Assets}_{\text{annual}} = 95 \times 12 = 1140\text{ billion USD} = \$1.14\text{ trillion} \] Assets and liabilities contract by up to $1.14T per year.

Problem 3.10 (Central Bank Digital Currencies and Bank Disintermediation). Analyze how a retail Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) alters the liability structure of commercial banks and the central bank.

Solution

If households shift deposits from commercial banks into retail CBDC, commercial banks lose low-cost customer funding and their reserve balances at the central bank fall. The central bank's liabilities shift from commercial bank reserves to direct CBDC liabilities, potentially forcing the central bank to intermediate credit directly.

Problem 3.11 (Central Bank Solvency and Remittances). When a central bank increases policy rates under abundant reserves, its interest expense on reserves can exceed the fixed yield on its long-term asset holdings. Explain the accounting mechanics of a 'deferred asset'.

Solution

When operating expenses exceed earnings, the central bank suspends remittances to the national Treasury and records a negative liability called a 'deferred asset'. The central bank cannot be forced into bankruptcy because its liabilities are legal tender denominated in its own unit of account.

Problem 3.12 (Optimal Balance Sheet Size). Formulate the social trade-off between operating in a scarce corridor system versus an abundant floor system.

Solution

A scarce corridor minimizes the central bank's footprint in financial markets, reducing political scrutiny and fiscal risk. An abundant floor guarantees complete liquidity safety, eliminates interbank friction, and decouples monetary policy interest rate choices from balance sheet size.

    Options

    Tax payments transfer cash from bank reserve balances into the Treasury's account at the Fed.

  4. What characterizes the reserve demand curve in the abundant reserves regime?

    Once banks' liquidity needs are satiated, reserves trade at the interest rate paid on reserve balances.
