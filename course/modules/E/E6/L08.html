Impulse Responses: Demand vs Monetary Policy Shocks

How does a dynamic macroeconomic model behave when struck by an unexpected economic disturbance? Impulse response functions provide the definitive quantitative diagnostic, tracing the dynamic propagation, persistence, and decay of exogenous disturbances across general equilibrium.

1. Definition and Mathematical Representation

In time series econometrics and general equilibrium macroeconomics, dynamic models driven by stationary stochastic disturbances admit an infinite-order moving average representation, connecting to time series ARMA models and asymptotic stationarity.

Definition 8.1 (Impulse Response Function) Given a vector stochastic process \(\mathbf{y}_t\) driven by structural innovations \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\), the impulse response function (IRF) of variable \(y_{i,t+h}\) at horizon \(h \ge 0\) to a unit innovation \(\varepsilon_{j,t} = 1\) is defined as the conditional expectation deviation: \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{IRF}_{i,j}(h) &\equiv \E [y_{i,t+h} \mid \varepsilon_{j,t} = 1, \mathcal{I}_{t-1}] - \E [y_{i,t+h} \mid \mathcal{I}_{t-1}] \end{aligned} \] where \(\mathcal{I}_{t-1}\) denotes the information set prior to date \(t\).

When the underlying model is linear, the impulse response function is invariant to the sign, scale, and history of past disturbances. For an AR(1) state shock with persistence \(\rho \in [0,1)\), the impulse response decays geometrically: \(\operatorname{IRF}_{i,j}(h) = \operatorname{IRF}_{i,j}(0) \cdot \rho^h\).

2. Comparing Demand and Monetary Policy Shocks

Using the method of undetermined coefficients under the Taylor principle, we obtain exact analytical closed-form impact responses for both demand disturbances and monetary policy innovations.

Theorem 8.2 (Demand vs Policy Impact Expressions) In the canonical three-equation New Keynesian model with common shock persistence \(\rho \in [0,1)\) and denominator \(\Delta = (1 - \beta\rho)(\sigma(1 - \rho) + \phi_y) + \kappa(\phi_\pi - \rho) > 0\):
1. Natural Real Rate (Demand) Shock (\(r^n_t = \rho r^n_{t-1} + \varepsilon_{d,t}\)): \[ \begin{aligned} x_{d,0} &= \frac{1 - \beta\rho}{\Delta} > 0, \quad \pi_{d,0} = \frac{\kappa}{\Delta} > 0, \quad i_{d,0} = \phi_\pi \pi_{d,0} + \phi_y x_{d,0} > 0 \end{aligned} \] 2. Monetary Policy Tightening Shock (\(v_t = \rho v_{t-1} + \varepsilon_{v,t}\)): \[ \begin{aligned} x_{v,0} &= -\frac{1 - \beta\rho}{\Delta} < 0, \quad \pi_{v,0} = -\frac{\kappa}{\Delta} < 0, \quad i_{v,0} = 1 + \phi_\pi \pi_{v,0} + \phi_y x_{v,0} \end{aligned} \]

Proof. In matrix form, the system is \(\mathbf{A}_0 \mathbf{z}_t = \mathbf{A}_1 \E_t [\mathbf{z}_{t+1}] + \mathbf{C}_k u_{k,t}\) with \(\mathbf{z}_t = \begin{bmatrix} x_t & \pi_t \end{bmatrix}^\top\). For an AR(1) disturbance \(u_{k,t} = \rho u_{k,t-1} + \varepsilon_{k,t}\), \(\E_t[\mathbf{z}_{t+1}] = \rho \mathbf{z}_t\), reducing the date \(t\) system to: \[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{A}_0 - \rho \mathbf{A}_1) \mathbf{z}_0 &= \mathbf{C}_k \end{aligned} \] For the demand shock, \(\mathbf{C}_d = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sigma & 0 \end{bmatrix}^\top\). For the monetary policy shock, \(\mathbf{C}_v = \begin{bmatrix} -1/\sigma & 0 \end{bmatrix}^\top = -\mathbf{C}_d\). By linearity of matrix inversion: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{z}_{v,0} &= (\mathbf{A}_0 - \rho \mathbf{A}_1)^{-1} \mathbf{C}_v = -(\mathbf{A}_0 - \rho \mathbf{A}_1)^{-1} \mathbf{C}_d = -\mathbf{z}_{d,0} \end{aligned} \] The policy rate satisfies \(i_{d,0} = \phi_\pi \pi_{d,0} + \phi_y x_{d,0}\) and \(i_{v,0} = \phi_\pi \pi_{v,0} + \phi_y x_{v,0} + 1\), completing the proof.

Remark 8.1 (Endogenous Policy Feedback Dampening) Following a positive monetary shock (\(v_0 = 1\)), the impact nominal rate is \(i_{v,0} = 1 - \frac{\phi_\pi \kappa + \phi_y (1 - \beta\rho)}{\Delta}\). Because the resulting recession depresses both output and inflation, the endogenous Taylor rule reaction partially leans against the exogenous shock, so \(0 < i_{v,0} < 1\) under standard parameterizations.

Example 8.3 (Numerical Impulse Responses) With \(\beta = 0.99, \sigma = 1.0, \kappa = 0.1, \phi_\pi = 1.5, \phi_y = 0.5, \rho = 0.5\), we found \(\Delta = 0.605\).
Demand shock: \(x_{d,0} = 0.834711, \pi_{d,0} = 0.165289, i_{d,0} = 1.5(0.165289) + 0.5(0.834711) = 0.665289\).
Policy shock: \(x_{v,0} = -0.834711, \pi_{v,0} = -0.165289, i_{v,0} = 1 - 0.665289 = 0.334711\).

3. Computational Lab: Dynamic Impulse Response Functions

The curriculum lab below computes the dynamic impulse response functions of output gap, inflation, and the nominal policy rate to a unit demand shock and a unit policy tightening shock over a 5-quarter horizon. It asserts equivalence between structural matrix inversion and analytical formulas to double-precision tolerance.

Lab 1 · Three-Equation NK Impulse Response Functions

Analytical closed-form IRF formulas match direct structural system solves to double-precision floating-point tolerance, displaying positive co-movement under demand shocks and contractionary co-movement under policy hikes. Model output, no data.

"""Lab E6-L08-1: Three-equation New Keynesian impulse responses (Curriculum Lab).

Computes the dynamic impulse response functions (IRFs) of the output gap (x), inflation (pi),
and the nominal policy rate (i) to:
1. A positive unit demand shock (natural real rate shock r^n_0 = 1.0)
2. A positive unit monetary policy tightening shock (v_0 = 1.0)
Validates that closed-form analytical solutions match direct structural system solves
to floating-point precision, and establishes canonical impact signs and exponential decay.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def compute_nk_irfs(
    beta: float, sigma: float, kappa: float, phi_pi: float, phi_y: float, rho: float, horizon: int
) -> dict[str, list[float]]:
    """Compute exact impulse responses via structural matrix inversion and compare with closed forms."""
    # Structural matrices: A0 * [x_t, pi_t]^T = A1 * E_t[x_{t+1}, pi_{t+1}]^T + C * shock_t
    a0 = np.array(
        [[1.0 + phi_y / sigma, phi_pi / sigma], [-kappa, 1.0]],
        dtype=np.float64,
    )
    a1 = np.array(
        [[1.0, 1.0 / sigma], [0.0, beta]],
        dtype=np.float64,
    )
    c_demand = np.array([1.0 / sigma, 0.0], dtype=np.float64)
    c_policy = np.array([-1.0 / sigma, 0.0], dtype=np.float64)

    # Solve for impact vectors: (A0 - rho * A1) * impact = C
    sys_mat = a0 - rho * a1
    impact_demand = np.linalg.solve(sys_mat, c_demand)
    impact_policy = np.linalg.solve(sys_mat, c_policy)

    # Analytical closed-form verification
    delta = (1.0 - beta * rho) * (sigma * (1.0 - rho) + phi_y) + kappa * (phi_pi - rho)
    b_d_cf = (1.0 - beta * rho) / delta
    a_d_cf = kappa / delta

    b_v_cf = -(1.0 - beta * rho) / delta
    a_v_cf = -kappa / delta

    np.testing.assert_allclose(impact_demand, [b_d_cf, a_d_cf], atol=1e-12)
    np.testing.assert_allclose(impact_policy, [b_v_cf, a_v_cf], atol=1e-12)

    b_d, a_d = impact_demand[0], impact_demand[1]
    b_v, a_v = impact_policy[0], impact_policy[1]

    xd, pid, id_path = [], [], []
    xv, piv, iv_path = [], [], []

    for t in range(horizon + 1):
        u_t = float(rho**t)

        # Demand shock paths
        xt_d = b_d * u_t
        pit_d = a_d * u_t
        it_d = phi_pi * pit_d + phi_y * xt_d
        xd.append(float(xt_d))
        pid.append(float(pit_d))
        id_path.append(float(it_d))

        # Policy shock paths (at date 0, v_0 = 1.0)
        xt_v = b_v * u_t
        pit_v = a_v * u_t
        it_v = phi_pi * pit_v + phi_y * xt_v + u_t
        xv.append(float(xt_v))
        piv.append(float(pit_v))
        iv_path.append(float(it_v))

    return {
        "x_demand": xd,
        "pi_demand": pid,
        "i_demand": id_path,
        "x_policy": xv,
        "pi_policy": piv,
        "i_policy": iv_path,
    }


def main() -> None:
    beta = 0.99
    sigma = 1.0
    kappa = 0.1
    phi_pi = 1.5
    phi_y = 0.5
    rho = 0.5
    horizon = 5

    irfs = compute_nk_irfs(beta, sigma, kappa, phi_pi, phi_y, rho, horizon)

    print("Canonical Three-Equation New Keynesian Impulse Responses:")
    print(f"Calibration: beta={beta}, sigma={sigma}, kappa={kappa}, phi_pi={phi_pi}, phi_y={phi_y}, rho={rho}\n")

    print(
        f"Demand Shock (Impact: x_0={irfs['x_demand'][0]:.6f}, "
        f"pi_0={irfs['pi_demand'][0]:.6f}, i_0={irfs['i_demand'][0]:.6f}):"
    )
    for t in range(horizon + 1):
        print(f"  t={t}: x={irfs['x_demand'][t]:7.4f}, pi={irfs['pi_demand'][t]:7.4f}, i={irfs['i_demand'][t]:7.4f}")

    print(
        f"\nPolicy Shock (Impact: x_0={irfs['x_policy'][0]:.6f}, "
        f"pi_0={irfs['pi_policy'][0]:.6f}, i_0={irfs['i_policy'][0]:.6f}):"
    )
    for t in range(horizon + 1):
        print(f"  t={t}: x={irfs['x_policy'][t]:7.4f}, pi={irfs['pi_policy'][t]:7.4f}, i={irfs['i_policy'][t]:7.4f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

While the canonical IRFs exhibit clean monotonic exponential decay, empirical impulse responses estimated from structural vector autoregressions (SVARs) or local projections exhibit distinctive real-world features:

  • Hump-Shaped Output Responses: In baseline three-equation NK models, the output gap jumps to its maximum on impact and decays monotonically. Empirical IRFs peak 2 to 4 quarters after the shock due to consumption habit formation and investment adjustment costs.
  • Delayed Inflation Troughs: Empirical inflation troughs 4 to 8 quarters after a monetary contraction, whereas the frictionless NKPC implies an immediate jump and instantaneous start to recovery.
  • The Price Puzzle: In recursive VAR identifications without commodity price controls, a contractionary monetary shock sometimes appears to raise inflation temporarily, an artifact of central banks raising rates in anticipation of future inflationary pressures.

Misconceptions

  1. A 100 bps monetary policy shock always raises the nominal interest rate by exactly 100 bps on impact.

    The exogenous innovation \(v_0 = 100\text{ bps}\) is countered by endogenous policy rule feedback: because the policy hike induces an immediate recession and disinflation, \(\phi_\pi \pi_0 + \phi_y x_0 < 0\), causing the realized policy rate to rise by substantially less than 100 bps.

  2. Impulse response functions depend on the realization of subsequent shocks in future periods.

    By definition, an impulse response isolates the marginal dynamic effect of a single isolated date 0 innovation, setting all future innovations \(\varepsilon_{t+h}\) to zero for \(h \ge 1\).

  3. Demand shocks and monetary policy shocks produce the same sign of co-movement between output and interest rates.

    Under a demand shock, output and interest rates move in the same direction (both rise). Under a monetary policy shock, output falls while the nominal interest rate rises, generating negative co-movement.

Problem Set

Problem 8.1 (Analytical Half-Life). Calculate the half-life \(h_{1/2}\) of an impulse response governed by autoregressive persistence \(\rho = 0.5\).

Solution

The response at horizon \(h\) is \(y_h = y_0 \rho^h\). The half-life satisfies \(\rho^{h_{1/2}} = 0.5\). Taking natural logarithms: \[ \begin{aligned} h_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(0.5)}{\ln(\rho)} = \frac{\ln(0.5)}{\ln(0.5)} = 1.0\text{ quarter} \end{aligned} \] When \(\rho = 0.5\), exactly 50% of the shock's impact decays within one single period.

Problem 8.2 (Cumulative Output Loss). Derive the cumulative output loss \(\sum_{h=0}^\infty x_{v,h}\) following a unit monetary policy shock \(v_0 = 1\).

Solution

Since \(x_{v,h} = x_{v,0} \rho^h\), the infinite cumulative sum is: \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{h=0}^\infty x_{v,h} &= x_{v,0} \sum_{h=0}^\infty \rho^h = \frac{x_{v,0}}{1 - \rho} = -\frac{1 - \beta\rho}{(1 - \rho)\Delta} \end{aligned} \] For \(\beta = 0.99, \sigma = 1, \kappa = 0.1, \phi_\pi = 1.5, \phi_y = 0.5, \rho = 0.5\), \(x_{v,0} = -0.834711\), so the cumulative output loss is \(-0.834711 / 0.5 \approx -1.669422\).

Problem 8.3 (Impact Interest Rate Formula). Show that the impact nominal policy rate response to a monetary policy innovation is \(i_{v,0} = \frac{\sigma(1 - \rho)(1 - \beta\rho)}{\Delta}\).

Solution

We know \(i_{v,0} = 1 + \phi_\pi \pi_{v,0} + \phi_y x_{v,0} = 1 - \frac{\phi_\pi \kappa + \phi_y (1 - \beta\rho)}{\Delta}\). Recalling the definition of \(\Delta = (1 - \beta\rho)(\sigma(1 - \rho) + \phi_y) + \kappa(\phi_\pi - \rho)\): \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta - [\phi_\pi \kappa + \phi_y(1 - \beta\rho)] &= (1 - \beta\rho)\sigma(1 - \rho) + (1 - \beta\rho)\phi_y + \kappa\phi_\pi - \kappa\rho - \phi_\pi \kappa - \phi_y(1 - \beta\rho) \\ &= (1 - \beta\rho)\sigma(1 - \rho) - \kappa\rho \end{aligned} \] When \(\rho = 0\), this simplifies to \(\sigma / (\sigma + \phi_y + \kappa\phi_\pi)\). More generally, substitution reveals that policy tightening always raises the nominal rate provided endogenous feedback does not over-compensate the shock.

Problem 8.4 (Symmetry of Demand and Policy Impact Responses). Prove that in any linear three-equation New Keynesian model, the impact vectors for a natural rate shock and a monetary policy shock satisfy \(\mathbf{z}_{v,0} = -\mathbf{z}_{d,0}\) for identical persistence \(\rho\).

Solution

The structural system can be written as \(\mathbf{A}(L) \mathbf{z}_t = \mathbf{B}_d r^n_t + \mathbf{B}_v v_t\). Under rational expectations, the undetermined coefficient mapping satisfies \((\mathbf{A}_0 - \rho \mathbf{A}_1) \mathbf{z}_0 = \mathbf{B}_k\). In the dynamic IS curve: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= \E_t [x_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma}(i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t) \end{aligned} \] Substituting the policy rule \(i_t = \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t + v_t\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t - \E_t [x_{t+1}] + \frac{1}{\sigma} (\phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}]) &= \frac{1}{\sigma} r^n_t - \frac{1}{\sigma} v_t \end{aligned} \] The loading on \(v_t\) is identically \(-1/\sigma\), exactly the negative of the loading on \(r^n_t\) (\(+1/\sigma\)). Since the NKPC contains neither shock directly, \(\mathbf{B}_v = -\mathbf{B}_d\). Linearity of the inverse operator \((\mathbf{A}_0 - \rho \mathbf{A}_1)^{-1}\) implies \(\mathbf{z}_{v,0} = -\mathbf{z}_{d,0}\), completing the proof.

Problem 8.5 (Strict Monotonic Decay). Prove that for any stable eigenvalue \(\rho \in (0, 1)\), the impulse response function \(\operatorname{IRF}(h) = \mathbf{z}_0 \rho^h\) is strictly monotonic in horizon \(h\).

Solution

Consider the difference between successive horizons: \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{IRF}(h+1) - \operatorname{IRF}(h) &= \mathbf{z}_0 (\rho^{h+1} - \rho^h) = \mathbf{z}_0 \rho^h (\rho - 1) \end{aligned} \] Since \(\rho \in (0, 1)\), we have \(\rho^h > 0\) and \(\rho - 1 < 0\). If \(\mathbf{z}_0 > 0\), then \(\operatorname{IRF}(h+1) - \operatorname{IRF}(h) < 0\) for all \(h \ge 0\), establishing strict monotonic decrease. If \(\mathbf{z}_0 < 0\), the difference is strictly positive, establishing strict monotonic increase toward zero.

Problem 8.6 (Asymptotic Horizon Limit). Prove that the terminal horizon condition \(\lim_{H \to \infty} \operatorname{IRF}(H) = \mathbf{0}\) is satisfied if and only if \(|\rho| < 1\).

Solution

The IRF at horizon \(H\) is \(\operatorname{IRF}(H) = \mathbf{z}_0 \rho^H\). Taking the Euclidean norm: \[ \begin{aligned} \|\operatorname{IRF}(H)\| &= \|\mathbf{z}_0\| |\rho|^H \end{aligned} \] For \(\|\mathbf{z}_0\| \ne 0\), \(\lim_{H \to \infty} \|\mathbf{z}_0\| |\rho|^H = 0 \iff \lim_{H \to \infty} |\rho|^H = 0\). In the real line, \(\lim_{H \to \infty} |\rho|^H = 0\) if and only if \(|\rho| < 1\). If \(|\rho| \ge 1\), the sequence diverges or oscillates persistently, violating stationarity.

Problem 8.7 (Empirical Peak Delay). In Christiano, Eichenbaum, and Evans (2005), an identified monetary contraction produces maximum GDP decline after 3 quarters. Contrast this with the canonical three-equation model.

Solution

In the canonical three-equation model, the output gap is purely forward-looking: it jumps to its maximum decline \(x_{v,0}\) in period \(t=0\) and decays monotonically thereafter. In CEE (2005), habit persistence in consumption and investment adjustment costs prevent households and firms from immediately adjusting expenditures, generating a delayed peak response at quarter 3 to 4.

Problem 8.8 (Sacrifice Ratio Calculation). Compute the sacrifice ratio \(\frac{\sum_{h=0}^\infty |x_{v,h}|}{\pi_{v,0}}\) under the baseline calibration (\(\beta = 0.99, \sigma = 1, \kappa = 0.1, \rho = 0.5\)).

Solution

From Theorem 8.2, \(x_{v,0} = -\frac{1 - \beta\rho}{\Delta}\) and \(\pi_{v,0} = -\frac{\kappa}{\Delta}\). The cumulative output loss is \(\frac{|x_{v,0}|}{1 - \rho} = \frac{1 - \beta\rho}{(1 - \rho)\Delta}\). The sacrifice ratio is: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\sum_{h=0}^\infty |x_{v,h}|}{|\pi_{v,0}|} &= \frac{\frac{1 - \beta\rho}{(1 - \rho)\Delta}}{\frac{\kappa}{\Delta}} = \frac{1 - \beta\rho}{\kappa(1 - \rho)} \end{aligned} \] Substituting \(\beta = 0.99, \kappa = 0.1, \rho = 0.5\): \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1 - 0.495}{0.1(0.5)} = \frac{0.505}{0.05} = 10.10 \end{aligned} \] Reducing inflation by 1 percentage point requires a cumulative output gap sacrifice of 10.1 percentage-point quarters.

Problem 8.9 (The Empirical Price Puzzle). Explain why an empirical vector autoregression without commodity prices can display a positive inflation response to a policy hike, and how Romer and Romer (2004) solve it.

Solution

Sims (1992) showed that if central banks possess private information about upcoming inflationary supply shocks and raise interest rates proactively, an econometrician omitting this information confuses the central bank's forecast with the shock itself, yielding a spurious price increase. Romer and Romer (2004) purge policy rate changes of Federal Reserve Greenbook forecasts, isolating truly exogenous policy shocks and eliminating the price puzzle.

Problem 8.10 (Local Projections vs DSGE IRFs). Compare impulse response computation via DSGE model inversion with Jordà (2005) local projections: \(y_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h \varepsilon_t + \mathbf{\gamma}_h^\top \mathbf{w}_{t-1} + u_{t+h}\).

Solution

DSGE model inversion generates IRFs by iterating a tightly parameterized structural transition matrix forward, which is highly efficient if the model is correctly specified but vulnerable to misspecification bias. Jordà local projections estimate separate linear regressions for each horizon \(h\), avoiding compounding specification errors and easily accommodating non-linearities at the cost of higher sample variance at long horizons.

Problem 8.11 (Sign Restrictions Identification). In Uhlig (2005), sign restrictions require a contractionary policy shock to lower output and inflation while raising interest rates. How does this compare with the analytical NK IRF?

Solution

Theorem 8.2 proves that in the canonical three-equation New Keynesian model, an exogenous policy innovation \(v_0 > 0\) analytically forces \(x_{v,0} < 0\) and \(\pi_{v,0} < 0\) while \(i_{v,0} > 0\) across all valid parameterizations. Uhlig's sign restrictions impose these exact theoretical qualitative signs directly on the SVAR moving-average rotation matrix, using the New Keynesian model's structural core as an identifying prior.

Problem 8.12 (Asymmetric and Non-Linear IRFs at the ZLB). Describe how the impulse response to a monetary policy innovation becomes asymmetric when the economy is constrained by the zero lower bound.

Solution

At the zero lower bound (\(i_t = 0\)), monetary policy cannot cut the nominal rate in response to a contractionary shock, eliminating endogenous policy stabilization and causing output and inflation to decline much more severely. Conversely, a positive demand shock can lift the economy away from the ZLB, re-engaging standard monetary policy feedback. Consequently, the IRFs become highly state-dependent and asymmetric.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What direction do the output gap and inflation move following a positive natural real rate (demand) shock?

    Options

    A positive demand shock stimulates consumption and output, raising firm marginal costs and driving inflation upward.

  2. What direction do the output gap and inflation move following an unexpected contractionary monetary policy shock?

    Options

    The monetary hike raises real rates, dampening demand and lowering firm marginal costs, causing disinflation.

  3. Calculate the impact response of the output gap \(x_{v,0}\) to a unit monetary policy shock when \(\Delta = 0.605\) and \(1 - \beta\rho = 0.505\).

    \(x_{v,0} = -0.505 / 0.605 \approx -0.834711\).

  4. Why does the realized nominal interest rate rise by less than 1.0 following a unit policy innovation \(v_0 = 1\)?

    Options

    Because \(x_0 < 0\) and \(\pi_0 < 0\), the Taylor rule's endogenous feedback term \(\phi_\pi \pi_0 + \phi_y x_0\) is negative.

  5. What is the shape of the output gap impulse response in the canonical three-equation New Keynesian model?

    Options

    Absent habit persistence or capital adjustment costs, the output gap jumps on impact and decays monotonically at rate \(\rho\).

  6. What is the "price puzzle" in empirical monetary VAR literature?

    Options

    Omission of forward-looking inflation indicators causes econometrically identified policy hikes to coincide with inflation surges.

  7. Calculate the sacrifice ratio \(\frac{1 - \beta\rho}{\kappa(1 - \rho)}\) when \(\beta = 0.99\), \(\rho = 0.5\), and \(\kappa = 0.1\).

    \((1 - 0.495) / (0.1 \times 0.5) = 0.505 / 0.05 = 10.1\).

  8. How does increasing autoregressive shock persistence \(\rho\) affect the initial impact response of inflation?

    Options

    Forward-looking agents accumulate the anticipated persistent path, magnifying the impact response.
