The Real Business Cycle Model

Modern dynamic macroeconomic models ground aggregate fluctuations in the microeconomic decisions of households and firms interacting in general equilibrium. The real business cycle framework establishes the foundational benchmark where aggregate fluctuations arise as Pareto-optimal equilibrium responses to exogenous technology shocks without nominal rigidities or market frictions.

1. The Economic Environment

The standard one-sector real business cycle model formalizes macroeconomic fluctuations as the equilibrium outcome of optimizing agents in frictionless, competitive markets, as originated in the seminal work of Kydland & Prescott (1982). Time is discrete, indexed by \(t = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), and extends over an infinite horizon.

The economy is populated by a continuum of identical, infinitely lived households normalized to measure one. The representative household maximizes expected discounted lifetime utility over consumption \(C_t\) and labor supply \(L_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{\{C_t, L_t, K_{t+1}\}} \quad & \E_0 \left[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t U(C_t, 1 - L_t) \right] \end{aligned} \]

where \(\beta \in (0, 1)\) is the subjective discount factor and \(\E_t\) denotes the mathematical expectation conditioned on the filtration available at date \(t\). Throughout this unit, period utility is separable and takes the constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) form:

\[ \begin{aligned} U(C_t, L_t) &= \frac{C_t^{1-\sigma} - 1}{1 - \sigma} - \chi \frac{L_t^{1+\eta}}{1 + \eta} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\sigma > 0\) represents the coefficient of relative risk aversion (with the logarithmic limit \(\ln C_t\) as \(\sigma \to 1\)), \(\eta \ge 0\) is the inverse Frisch elasticity of labor supply, and \(\chi > 0\) governs the relative disutility of labor.

In each period \(t\), the household faces the flow budget constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} C_t + I_t &\le w_t L_t + r_t K_t \end{aligned} \]

where \(w_t\) is the real wage per unit of labor, \(r_t\) is the real rental price of physical capital, and \(I_t\) denotes gross investment. Capital accumulates according to the linear law of motion:

\[ \begin{aligned} K_{t+1} &= (1 - \delta) K_t + I_t \end{aligned} \]

where \(\delta \in (0, 1]\) is the physical depreciation rate of capital per period. Substituting investment yields the integrated flow budget constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} C_t + K_{t+1} - (1 - \delta) K_t &\le w_t L_t + r_t K_t \end{aligned} \]

2. Production and Firm Optimization

A continuum of competitive firms hires labor and rents capital to produce a homogeneous final output according to an aggregate Cobb-Douglas production function with constant returns to scale:

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_t &= A_t K_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\) is the capital share and \(A_t > 0\) is exogenous total factor productivity, extending the classic Solow growth model technology. In the deterministic steady state, \(A_t = A = 1\).

Competitive profit maximization requires that factor prices equal their marginal products in every period:

\[ \begin{aligned} r_t &= \alpha A_t \left( \frac{K_t}{L_t} \right)^{\alpha - 1} \\ w_t &= (1 - \alpha) A_t \left( \frac{K_t}{L_t} \right)^\alpha \end{aligned} \]

3. General Equilibrium and the Planner Problem

A competitive equilibrium consists of price sequences \(\{r_t, w_t\}_{t=0}^\infty\) and allocation sequences \(\{C_t, L_t, K_{t+1}, Y_t, I_t\}_{t=0}^\infty\) such that households optimize, firms maximize profits, and all markets clear: labor demand equals supply, capital rental clears, and the aggregate resource constraint holds:

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_t &= C_t + I_t = C_t + K_{t+1} - (1 - \delta)K_t \end{aligned} \]

Because there are no externalities, distortionary taxes, or market incompleteness, the First Fundamental Theorem of Welfare Economics applies. The competitive allocation can be solved equivalently via the central planner's Pareto problem using the Lagrange multiplier method or the recursive Bellman equation:

\[ \begin{aligned} V(K_t, A_t) &= \max_{K_{t+1}, L_t} \left\{ U(A_t K_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha} + (1-\delta)K_t - K_{t+1}, L_t) \right. \\ & \quad \left. + \, \beta \E_t [ V(K_{t+1}, A_{t+1}) ] \right\} \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 1.1 (Household First-Order Conditions) Under assumptions:
1. Discount factor \(\beta \in (0, 1)\),
2. Risk aversion \(\sigma > 0\) and Frisch elasticity \(\eta \ge 0\),
3. Interior consumption \(C_t > 0\) and capital \(K_{t+1} > 0\),
the optimal household allocation satisfies the intertemporal Euler equation: \[ \begin{aligned} C_t^{-\sigma} &= \beta \E_t \left[ C_{t+1}^{-\sigma} (1 + r_{t+1} - \delta) \right] \end{aligned} \] and the intratemporal labor-leisure condition: \[ \begin{aligned} \chi L_t^\eta &= C_t^{-\sigma} w_t \end{aligned} \] together with the transversality condition \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \beta^T \E_0 [ C_T^{-\sigma} K_{T+1} ] = 0\).

Proof. Define the Lagrangian for the representative household problem: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} &= \sum_{t=0}^\infty \beta^t \E_0 \left[ \frac{C_t^{1-\sigma} - 1}{1 - \sigma} - \chi \frac{L_t^{1+\eta}}{1 + \eta} \right. \\ & \quad \left. + \, \lambda_t \left( w_t L_t + (1 + r_t - \delta) K_t - C_t - K_{t+1} \right) \right] \end{aligned} \] The first-order necessary condition with respect to \(C_t\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial C_t} &= \beta^t \left( C_t^{-\sigma} - \lambda_t \right) = 0 \implies \lambda_t = C_t^{-\sigma} \end{aligned} \] The first-order condition with respect to \(K_{t+1}\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial K_{t+1}} &= -\beta^t \lambda_t + \beta^{t+1} \E_t [ \lambda_{t+1} (1 + r_{t+1} - \delta) ] = 0 \end{aligned} \] Substituting \(\lambda_t = C_t^{-\sigma}\) directly gives the consumption Euler equation. The condition with respect to \(L_t\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial L_t} &= \beta^t \left( -\chi L_t^\eta + \lambda_t w_t \right) = 0 \implies \chi L_t^\eta = C_t^{-\sigma} w_t \end{aligned} \] completing the proof.

4. The Deterministic Steady State

In a deterministic steady state, all aggregate variables are constant over time: \(C_t = c^*\), \(K_t = k^*\), \(L_t = L^*\), and \(r_t = r^*\). With stationary technology normalized to \(A = 1\), the steady state can be characterized in closed form.

Proposition 1.2 (Analytical RBC Steady State) Assuming \(\beta \in (0, 1)\), \(\delta \in (0, 1]\), \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\), and normalized labor supply \(L^* = 1\), the unique steady-state allocation satisfies: \[ \begin{aligned} r^* &= \frac{1}{\beta} - 1 + \delta \\ k^* &= \left( \frac{\alpha}{1/\beta - 1 + \delta} \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \\ y^* &= (k^*)^\alpha \\ i^* &= \delta k^* \\ c^* &= y^* - i^* = (k^*)^\alpha - \delta k^* \end{aligned} \] and both the consumption Euler equation and the aggregate resource constraint hold with zero residual.

Proof. In steady state, \(C_t = C_{t+1} = c^*\). The Euler equation reduces to: \[ \begin{aligned} (c^*)^{-\sigma} &= \beta (c^*)^{-\sigma} (1 + r^* - \delta) \implies 1 = \beta (1 + r^* - \delta) \end{aligned} \] Dividing by \(\beta\) yields \(r^* = 1/\beta - 1 + \delta\). Equating this to the firm's marginal product of capital \(r^* = \alpha (k^*)^{\alpha - 1}\) gives: \[ \begin{aligned} \alpha (k^*)^{\alpha - 1} &= \frac{1}{\beta} - 1 + \delta \implies k^* = \left( \frac{\alpha}{1/\beta - 1 + \delta} \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \end{aligned} \] Steady-state investment satisfies \(k^* = (1 - \delta)k^* + i^* \implies i^* = \delta k^*\). Output is \(y^* = (k^*)^\alpha\), and consumption follows from the resource constraint \(c^* = y^* - i^*\).

Lab 1 · Verifying the RBC Steady State

The analytical steady-state formulas satisfy the consumption Euler equation and resource constraint with exactly zero residual in rational arithmetic and to double precision floating-point tolerance. Model output, no data.

"""Lab E6-L01-1: The RBC steady state in closed form.

Solves the deterministic steady state of the standard one-sector Real Business Cycle model
and verifies that both the consumption Euler equation and the aggregate resource constraint
hold to zero residual in exact rational arithmetic (Fraction) and in double precision floats.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction


def solve_rbc_steady_state_fraction(
    alpha: Fraction, beta: Fraction, delta: Fraction
) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction, Fraction, Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Solve the steady state in exact arithmetic for alpha=1/2."""
    # Euler equation in steady state: beta * (1 + r* - delta) = 1 => r* = 1/beta - 1 + delta
    r_star = Fraction(1, beta) - Fraction(1) + delta
    # Marginal product of capital: r* = alpha * (k*)^(alpha - 1)
    # For alpha = 1/2: r* = (1/2) * (k*)^(-1/2) => (k*)^(1/2) = alpha / r_star
    ratio = alpha / r_star
    k_star = ratio * ratio
    y_star = ratio  # y* = (k*)^(1/2) = ratio
    i_star = delta * k_star
    c_star = y_star - i_star
    return r_star, k_star, y_star, c_star, i_star


def solve_rbc_steady_state_float(alpha: float, beta: float, delta: float) -> tuple[float, float, float, float, float]:
    """Solve the steady state in double precision for standard quarterly calibration."""
    r_star = 1.0 / beta - 1.0 + delta
    k_star = (alpha / r_star) ** (1.0 / (1.0 - alpha))
    y_star = k_star**alpha
    i_star = delta * k_star
    c_star = y_star - i_star
    return r_star, k_star, y_star, c_star, i_star


def main() -> None:
    # ---------------------------------------------------------
    # Part 1: Exact rational arithmetic (alpha = 1/2, beta = 4/5, delta = 1/10)
    # ---------------------------------------------------------
    alpha_q = Fraction(1, 2)
    beta_q = Fraction(4, 5)
    delta_q = Fraction(1, 10)

    r_q, k_q, y_q, c_q, i_q = solve_rbc_steady_state_fraction(alpha_q, beta_q, delta_q)

    # Euler equation check: beta * (1 + alpha * (k*)^(-1/2) - delta) - 1 == 0
    mpk_q = alpha_q / Fraction(math.isqrt(k_q.numerator), math.isqrt(k_q.denominator))
    euler_residual_q = beta_q * (Fraction(1) + mpk_q - delta_q) - Fraction(1)
    resource_residual_q = y_q - c_q - i_q

    print(f"Exact rational steady state (alpha={alpha_q}, beta={beta_q}, delta={delta_q}):")
    print(f"  Rental rate r*: {r_q}")
    print(f"  Capital stock k*: {k_q}")
    print(f"  Output y*: {y_q}")
    print(f"  Consumption c*: {c_q}")
    print(f"  Investment i*: {i_q}")
    print(f"  Euler equation residual: {euler_residual_q}")
    print(f"  Resource constraint residual: {resource_residual_q}")

    # ---------------------------------------------------------
    # Part 2: Standard float calibration (alpha = 1/3, beta = 0.99, delta = 0.025)
    # ---------------------------------------------------------
    alpha_f = 1.0 / 3.0
    beta_f = 0.99
    delta_f = 0.025

    r_f, k_f, y_f, c_f, i_f = solve_rbc_steady_state_float(alpha_f, beta_f, delta_f)

    mpk_f = alpha_f * (k_f ** (alpha_f - 1.0))
    euler_residual_f = beta_f * (1.0 + mpk_f - delta_f) - 1.0
    resource_residual_f = y_f - c_f - i_f

    print("\nStandard quarterly calibration (alpha=1/3, beta=0.99, delta=0.025):")
    print(f"  Rental rate r*: {r_f:.6f}")
    print(f"  Capital stock k*: {k_f:.6f}")
    print(f"  Output y*: {y_f:.6f}")
    print(f"  Consumption c*: {c_f:.6f}")
    print(f"  Investment i*: {i_f:.6f}")
    print(f"  Euler equation residual: {euler_residual_f:.2e}")
    print(f"  Resource constraint residual: {resource_residual_f:.2e}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The standard RBC model relies on competitive, frictionless markets and instantaneous price adjustment. It exhibits three critical canonical limitations:

  • Absence of nominal rigidities: Money is neutral; nominal money supply changes do not affect real variables. Real business cycle theory cannot analyze monetary policy transmission or central bank interest rate rules.
  • Labor market clearing: Fluctuations in employment arise solely from voluntary labor-leisure substitution along the household labor supply schedule. The model cannot account for involuntary unemployment or persistent wage stickiness.
  • Sole reliance on technology shocks: Empirically, accounting for observed business cycle volatility through total factor productivity shocks requires large, frequent technological regressions, which lack direct microeconomic validation.

Misconceptions

  1. Business cycle fluctuations in the RBC model represent market failures that require fiscal or monetary stabilization.

    Under the first welfare theorem, the competitive RBC equilibrium is Pareto optimal. Aggregate output fluctuations are the efficient equilibrium response of an optimizing economy to technological shocks; government intervention to smooth output would reduce welfare.

  2. The steady-state capital stock in the RBC model depends on household risk aversion \(\sigma\).

    In deterministic steady state, consumption growth is zero (\(C_{t+1}/C_t = 1\)), so the curvature term \((C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\sigma}\) drops out of the Euler equation entirely. The capital-labor ratio is determined strictly by \(\beta\), \(\delta\), and production parameter \(\alpha\).

  3. Higher capital depreciation \(\delta\) increases the steady-state capital stock because more investment is needed.

    Higher \(\delta\) increases the required gross rental rate of capital \(r^* = 1/\beta - 1 + \delta\). Since the marginal product of capital is strictly decreasing in \(k\), a higher required rental rate unambiguously reduces the equilibrium capital-labor ratio \(k^*\).

Problem Set

Problem 1.1 (Steady-State Rental Rate). Compute the quarterly steady-state real rental rate of capital \(r^*\) when \(\beta = 0.99\) and depreciation is \(\delta = 0.025\) per quarter.

Solution

From the steady-state Euler equation: \[ \begin{aligned} r^* &= \frac{1}{\beta} - 1 + \delta = \frac{1}{0.99} - 1 + 0.025 \approx 0.010101 + 0.025 = 0.035101 \end{aligned} \] The real rental rate is approximately 3.51% per quarter.

Problem 1.2 (Capital-Output Ratio). Show that the steady-state capital-output ratio \(k^*/y^*\) is given by \(\alpha / r^*\), and evaluate it for \(\alpha = 0.33\) and \(r^* = 0.035101\).

Solution

Since \(y^* = (k^*)^\alpha\), dividing \(k^*\) by \(y^*\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{k^*}{y^*} &= \frac{k^*}{(k^*)^\alpha} = (k^*)^{1-\alpha} \end{aligned} \] From the firm's first-order condition, \(r^* = \alpha (k^*)^{\alpha - 1} \implies (k^*)^{1-\alpha} = \alpha / r^*\). Thus: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{k^*}{y^*} &= \frac{0.33}{0.035101} \approx 9.4014 \end{aligned} \] The capital stock is approximately 9.4 times quarterly output.

Problem 1.3 (Steady-State Consumption Share). Find the steady-state consumption share \(c^*/y^*\) as a function of \(\alpha, \delta\), and \(r^*\).

Solution

From the resource constraint: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{c^*}{y^*} &= 1 - \frac{i^*}{y^*} = 1 - \delta \frac{k^*}{y^*} = 1 - \delta \frac{\alpha}{r^*} \end{aligned} \] Using \(\alpha = 0.33, \delta = 0.025\), and \(r^* = 0.035101\): \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{c^*}{y^*} &= 1 - 0.025 \times 9.4014 \approx 1 - 0.2350 = 0.7650 \end{aligned} \] Consumption accounts for approximately 76.5% of steady-state output.

Problem 1.4 (Monotonicity of Capital Stock). Prove that the steady-state capital stock \(k^*\) is strictly increasing in the discount factor \(\beta\) and strictly decreasing in the depreciation rate \(\delta\).

Solution

Recall the closed form \(k^*(\beta, \delta) = \left( \frac{\alpha}{1/\beta - 1 + \delta} \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\). Let \(g(\beta, \delta) = 1/\beta - 1 + \delta\). Since \(\beta \in (0, 1)\), \(\partial g / \partial \beta = -1/\beta^2 < 0\). Because \(1/(1-\alpha) > 0\), by the chain rule: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial k^*}{\partial \beta} &= \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \left( \frac{\alpha}{g} \right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}} \left( -\frac{\alpha}{g^2} \right) \left( -\frac{1}{\beta^2} \right) > 0 \end{aligned} \] Similarly, \(\partial g / \partial \delta = 1 > 0\), so \(\partial k^* / \partial \delta < 0\). A more patient household accumulates a larger steady-state capital stock, whereas faster physical depreciation reduces capital accumulation.

Problem 1.5 (Euler Equation Log Approximation). Prove that taking a first-order Taylor expansion of the Euler equation around steady state produces a relation that is independent of the steady-state consumption level \(c^*\).

Solution

Define \(f(C_t, C_{t+1}) = \ln C_t - \ln C_{t+1}\). The exact Euler equation is \(C_t^{-\sigma} = \beta (1 + R_{t+1}) C_{t+1}^{-\sigma}\). Taking natural logarithms: \[ \begin{aligned} -\sigma \ln C_t &= \ln \beta + \ln(1 + R_{t+1}) - \sigma \ln C_{t+1} \end{aligned} \] Rearranging: \[ \begin{aligned} \ln C_t - \ln C_{t+1} &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} \left[ \ln(1 + R_{t+1}) + \ln \beta \right] \end{aligned} \] Subtracting steady-state \(\ln c^*\) from both sides: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{c}_t - \hat{c}_{t+1} &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} \hat{r}_{t+1} \end{aligned} \] Because logarithmic differentiation divides by the base level, \(c^*\) cancels out entirely, establishing independence.

Problem 1.6 (Golden Rule Capital Stock). Prove that the consumption-maximizing steady-state capital stock (the Golden Rule) satisfies \(\alpha (k_{\text{gold}})^{\alpha - 1} = \delta\), and show that the decentralized steady state \(k^* < k_{\text{gold}}\) for any \(\beta < 1\).

Solution

In steady state, \(c = k^\alpha - \delta k\). Maximizing with respect to \(k\): \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial c}{\partial k} &= \alpha k^{\alpha - 1} - \delta = 0 \implies \alpha (k_{\text{gold}})^{\alpha - 1} = \delta \end{aligned} \] In the decentralized equilibrium, \(r^* = \alpha (k^*)^{\alpha - 1} = 1/\beta - 1 + \delta\). Since \(\beta < 1\), \(1/\beta - 1 > 0\), which implies \(r^* > \delta\). Because marginal productivity \(\alpha k^{\alpha - 1}\) is strictly decreasing in \(k\), \(r^* > \delta\) implies \(k^* < k_{\text{gold}}\). Households discount the future, resulting in underaccumulation relative to the Golden Rule.

Problem 1.7 (Calibrating the Capital Share). In national accounts, employee compensation is approximately 64% of GDP while operating surplus and depreciation account for 36%. If national accounts map directly to the Cobb-Douglas aggregate, what is \(\alpha\)?

Solution

With Cobb-Douglas technology and competitive labor markets, total labor compensation is \(w^* L^* = (1 - \alpha) y^*\). Equating this to the empirical labor share: \[ \begin{aligned} 1 - \alpha &= 0.64 \implies \alpha = 0.36 \end{aligned} \] The calibrated capital elasticity \(\alpha\) is 0.36, which is standard in quantitative macroeconomic calibrations.

Problem 1.8 (Annualizing Real Rates). If a model calibrated to quarterly data produces a steady-state rental rate of \(r^* = 0.035101\) and depreciation is \(\delta = 0.025\) per quarter, calculate the implied annualized net real interest rate.

Solution

The net quarterly real interest rate is \(r_{\text{net}} = r^* - \delta = 0.035101 - 0.025 = 0.010101\) (approximately 1.01% per quarter). Using compounding: \[ \begin{aligned} r_{\text{annual}} &= (1 + r_{\text{net}})^4 - 1 = (1.010101)^4 - 1 \approx 0.0410 \end{aligned} \] The annualized net real rate is 4.10% per year.

Problem 1.9 (Solow Residual Variance). Suppose measured Solow residuals exhibit a standard deviation of 0.007 per quarter. If output gap volatility in data is 0.015, what fraction of aggregate output variance can technology shocks directly account for if the model multiplier is 1.5?

Solution

The standard deviation of model output predicted by technology is \(\sigma_y^{\text{model}} = 1.5 \times 0.007 = 0.0105\). The ratio of variances is: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{(\sigma_y^{\text{model}})^2}{\sigma_y^2} &= \frac{(0.0105)^2}{(0.015)^2} = \frac{0.00011025}{0.000225} = 0.49 \end{aligned} \] Technology shocks alone account for approximately 49% of observed cyclical output variance, illustrating the classic RBC finding that real shocks explain roughly half of business cycle fluctuations.

Problem 1.10 (Endogenous Labor Supply Elasticity). Derive how the steady-state labor supply \(L^*\) responds to an increase in steady-state productivity \(A\) when utility is separable with CRRA parameter \(\sigma = 1\) (log utility in consumption).

Solution

With log utility (\(\sigma = 1\)), the intratemporal labor-leisure condition is \(\chi (L^*)^\eta = w^* / c^*\). In steady state, both the wage \(w^* = (1 - \alpha) A (k^* / L^*)^\alpha\) and consumption \(c^* = [A (k^* / L^*)^\alpha - \delta (k^* / L^*)] L^*\) scale linearly with \(A\). Therefore, the ratio \(w^* / c^*\) is strictly independent of \(A\). The income and substitution effects of higher permanent productivity on labor supply exactly cancel out, ensuring that hours worked remain stationary over long-run growth trends.

Problem 1.11 (Welfare Losses of Smoothing). Explain why imposing an artificial regulatory cap on investment volatility in an RBC economy strictly lowers household welfare.

Solution

In the frictionless RBC economy, equilibrium fluctuations represent Pareto-optimal adjustments to productivity disturbances. When technology is temporarily high, the marginal product of capital is elevated, making it optimal to invest heavily to smooth consumption into the future. Restricting investment prevents households and firms from intertemporally substituting resources efficiently, introducing a binding distortion that reduces expected lifetime utility.

Problem 1.12 (Equivalence to Social Planner). Formalize the equivalence between the decentralized competitive equilibrium allocation and the dynamic programming solution of the central planner problem.

Solution

The planner's Bellman equation is \(V(K) = \max_{K'} \{ U(F(K, 1) + (1-\delta)K - K') + \beta V(K') \}\). The first-order condition is \(U_C(C) = \beta V'(K')\), and the Benveniste-Scheinkman condition gives \(V'(K) = U_C(C) [F_K(K, 1) + 1 - \delta]\). Advancing one period: \(U_C(C_t) = \beta \E_t [ U_C(C_{t+1}) (F_K(K_{t+1}, 1) + 1 - \delta) ]\). Since competitive profit maximization sets \(r_{t+1} = F_K(K_{t+1}, 1)\), the planner's Euler equation is identical to the household's Euler equation in competitive equilibrium, confirming the First Welfare Theorem.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What determines the steady-state rental price of capital \(r^*\) in the deterministic RBC model?

    Options

    In steady state, \(r^* = 1/\beta - 1 + \delta\) from the consumption Euler equation; risk aversion drops out when consumption growth is zero.

  2. Why is the competitive equilibrium in the baseline RBC model Pareto optimal?

    Options

    The first fundamental welfare theorem holds because there are no externalities, distortions, or incomplete markets.

  3. Calculate the steady-state rental rate \(r^*\) when \(\beta = 0.99\) and \(\delta = 0.025\) (enter as a decimal).

    \(r^* = 1/0.99 - 1 + 0.025 = 0.010101 + 0.025 = 0.035101\).

  4. How does an increase in capital depreciation \(\delta\) affect the steady-state capital stock \(k^*\)?

    Options

    A higher \(\delta\) raises the required gross rental rate \(r^* = 1/\beta - 1 + \delta\), which decreases the steady-state capital stock since marginal productivity is diminishing.

  5. Which parameter governs the intertemporal elasticity of substitution in household consumption?

    Options

    Under CRRA utility \(U(C) = (C^{1-\sigma} - 1)/(1 - \sigma)\), the intertemporal elasticity of substitution is \(1/\sigma\).

  6. What happens to steady-state hours worked when \(\sigma = 1\) (log utility) and total factor productivity \(A\) increases permanently?

    Options

    With log utility in consumption, the positive substitution effect of higher wages is exactly offset by the negative income effect.

  7. In exact rational arithmetic, if \(\alpha = 1/2\), \(\beta = 4/5\), and \(\delta = 1/10\), what is the denominator of the steady-state rental rate \(r^* = 7/20\)?

    \(r^* = 5/4 - 1 + 1/10 = 7/20\); its denominator is 20.

  8. Which friction is explicitly absent from the baseline real business cycle model?

    Options

    The RBC model has fully flexible prices and wages; nominal stickiness is introduced in the New Keynesian framework.
