The Three-Equation New Keynesian Model

The workhorse framework of modern monetary economics synthesizes intertemporal consumption optimization, staggered price adjustment, and systematic interest rate feedback. It shows how monetary policy controls inflation and the output gap by steering real interest rates.

1. System Architecture and Microfoundations

The Three-equation New Keynesian model provides the foundational general equilibrium framework for contemporary central bank policy analysis, synthesized famously by Clarida, Galí, and Gertler [clarida1999science] and expounded in Galí [gali2015monetary] and Woodford [woodford2004interest].

The economy consists of three interacting sectors:

  1. Representative Households: Maximize expected discounted utility over consumption and leisure, trading riskless nominal bonds. Their optimal intertemporal behavior yields the dynamic IS curve.
  2. Monopolistically Competitive Firms: Produce differentiated goods and face Calvo price-setting frictions with resetting probability \(1-\theta\). Aggregation across firms yields the forward-looking New Keynesian Phillips curve.
  3. Monetary Authority: Sets the nominal policy rate systematically according to an endogenous Taylor rule, reacting to inflation and the output gap.

Definition 5.1 (The Canonical Three-Equation System) The log-linearized three-equation New Keynesian model is defined by: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= \E_t [x_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \left( i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t \right) \\ \pi_t &= \beta \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t + e_t \\ i_t &= \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t + v_t \end{aligned} \] where \(x_t \equiv \hat{y}_t - y^n_t\) is the output gap, \(\pi_t\) is the net inflation rate, \(i_t\) is the nominal policy rate, \(r^n_t\) is the exogenous natural rate of interest, \(e_t\) is an exogenous cost-push markup shock, and \(v_t\) is an exogenous monetary policy innovation.

2. Transmission Mechanism and Money Neutrality

In classical flexible-price models such as the RBC model, monetary policy cannot affect real output or employment. In the three-equation New Keynesian model, nominal price rigidity breaks classical neutrality.

Theorem 5.2 (Real Rate Transmission Channel) Because the aggregate price level \(P_t\) is predetermined by staggered contracts, a change in the nominal interest rate \(i_t\) induces a contemporaneous movement in the short-term ex-ante real interest rate: \[ \begin{aligned} r_t &= i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] \end{aligned} \] When \(r_t\) departs from the natural real interest rate \(r^n_t\), households adjust their consumption trajectory along the dynamic IS curve, shifting the output gap \(x_t\), which subsequently drives real marginal costs and firm price adjustments via the NKPC.

Proof. Under Calvo pricing, a fraction \(\theta \in (0,1)\) of firms cannot change prices in period \(t\). The aggregate price index \(P_t = [\theta P_{t-1}^{1-\epsilon} + (1-\theta) (P_t^*)^{1-\epsilon}]^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}\) cannot jump immediately to neutralize changes in \(i_t\). Substituting the Fisher relation \(r_t = i_t - \E_t[\pi_{t+1}]\) into the dynamic IS curve yields: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= \E_t [x_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} (r_t - r^n_t) \end{aligned} \] Thus, \(\partial x_t / \partial r_t = -1/\sigma < 0\). Variations in \(x_t\) feed directly into firm real marginal costs \(\widehat{mc}_t = (\sigma + \varphi) x_t\), which alters optimal reset prices \(p_t^*\) and induces inflation \(\pi_t = \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t\).

Remark 5.1 (The Cashless Economy Simplification) As shown by Woodford [woodford2004interest], under additively separable utility in money balances or in the cashless limit, the quantity of money plays no allocative role. The central bank sets the target policy rate \(i_t\) directly, and the money supply endogenously accommodates money demand.

3. Semi-Reduced Form and Residual Formulation

Substituting the policy rule into the dynamic IS equation eliminates \(i_t\), reducing the three equations to a coupled two-variable forward-looking system in \((x_t, \pi_t)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= \E_t [x_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \left( \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t + v_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t \right) \end{aligned} \] Rearranging terms into an implicit residual system for computational validation: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{R}_t^{\mathrm{IS}} &\equiv x_t - \E_t [x_{t+1}] + \frac{1}{\sigma} \left( i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t \right) = 0 \\ \mathcal{R}_t^{\mathrm{NKPC}} &\equiv \pi_t - \beta \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - \kappa x_t - e_t = 0 \\ \mathcal{R}_t^{\mathrm{TR}} &\equiv i_t - (\phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t + v_t) = 0 \end{aligned} \]

Example 5.3 (Equilibrium Under AR(1) Demand Shock) Suppose the natural real rate follows an AR(1) process \(r^n_t = \rho r^n_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) with \(e_t = v_t = 0\). Using undetermined coefficients \(x_t = b r^n_t\) and \(\pi_t = a r^n_t\), the exact solution coefficients satisfy: \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta &= (1 - \beta\rho)(\sigma(1 - \rho) + \phi_y) + \kappa(\phi_\pi - \rho) \\ b &= \frac{1 - \beta\rho}{\Delta}, \quad a = \frac{\kappa}{\Delta} \end{aligned} \] When \(\beta = 0.99\), \(\sigma = 1.0\), \(\kappa = 0.1\), \(\phi_\pi = 1.5\), \(\phi_y = 0.5\), and \(\rho = 0.5\): \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta &= (1 - 0.99 \times 0.5)(1.0 \times 0.5 + 0.5) + 0.1 \times (1.5 - 0.5) \\ &= (0.505)(1.0) + 0.1 \times 1.0 = 0.605 \\ b &= \frac{0.505}{0.605} = \frac{101}{121} \approx 0.834711 \\ a &= \frac{0.1}{0.605} = \frac{20}{121} \approx 0.165289 \end{aligned} \] The policy rate responds as \(i_t = \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t = (1.5 \times 0.165289 + 0.5 \times 0.834711) r^n_t \approx 0.665289 r^n_t\).

4. Computational Lab: Dynamic Residual Verification

The lab below calculates the undetermined coefficients for the three-equation New Keynesian model under an AR(1) natural real rate disturbance, simulates the system forward along the deterministic shock decay path, and verifies that the structural residuals for the dynamic IS curve, the NKPC, and the Taylor rule evaluate to zero within floating-point roundoff precision.

Lab 1 · Three-Equation NK Dynamic Residual Verification

The derived undetermined coefficients satisfy the dynamic IS curve, the New Keynesian Phillips curve, and the contemporaneous Taylor rule with zero residual across the entire transition path under double precision floating-point arithmetic. Model output, no data.

"""Lab E6-L05-1: Three-equation New Keynesian model: residual verification along a shock path.

Computes the equilibrium trajectory of the output gap, inflation, and policy rate
under an AR(1) natural real rate disturbance. Verifies that the dynamic IS curve,
the New Keynesian Phillips curve, and the contemporaneous Taylor rule hold with
zero residual at every date.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_undetermined_coefficients(
    beta: float, sigma: float, kappa: float, phi_pi: float, phi_y: float, rho: float
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Analytical undetermined coefficients for AR(1) natural rate shock."""
    delta = (1.0 - beta * rho) * (sigma * (1.0 - rho) + phi_y) + kappa * (phi_pi - rho)
    b = (1.0 - beta * rho) / delta
    a = kappa / delta
    return b, a


def main() -> None:
    beta = 0.99
    sigma = 1.0
    kappa = 0.1
    phi_pi = 1.5
    phi_y = 0.5
    rho = 0.5
    r_n_0 = 1.0

    b, a = solve_undetermined_coefficients(beta, sigma, kappa, phi_pi, phi_y, rho)
    print("Three-equation NK model calibration:")
    print(f"  beta={beta}, sigma={sigma}, kappa={kappa}, phi_pi={phi_pi}, phi_y={phi_y}, rho={rho}")
    print(f"  Coefficients: b (output gap) = {b:.6f}, a (inflation) = {a:.6f}")
    print("\nTrajectory and equation residuals along shock path:")

    max_res = 0.0
    for t in range(6):
        rn_t = r_n_0 * (rho**t)
        rn_next = r_n_0 * (rho ** (t + 1))

        xt = b * rn_t
        pit = a * rn_t
        it = phi_pi * pit + phi_y * xt

        xt_next = b * rn_next
        pit_next = a * rn_next

        # Equation residuals
        res_is = xt - xt_next + (1.0 / sigma) * (it - pit_next - rn_t)
        res_nk = pit - beta * pit_next - kappa * xt
        res_tr = it - (phi_pi * pit + phi_y * xt)

        current_max = max(abs(res_is), abs(res_nk), abs(res_tr))
        if current_max > max_res:
            max_res = current_max

        print(
            f"  t={t}: r^n={rn_t:6.4f}, x={xt:6.4f}, pi={pit:6.4f}, i={it:6.4f} | "
            f"residuals: IS={res_is:9.2e}, NKPC={res_nk:9.2e}, TR={res_tr:9.2e}"
        )

    print(f"\nMaximum absolute residual across all dates: {max_res:.2e}")

Where the Canon Breaks

The canonical three-equation New Keynesian model provides essential intuition for modern monetary macroeconomics, but abstracts from several critical real-world phenomena:

  • The Zero Lower Bound (ZLB): The linear policy rule assumes nominal interest rates can fall without bound. In reality, the effective lower bound on nominal rates induces severe non-linearities, deflationary spirals, and amplifies demand shocks.
  • Financial Market Frictions and Credit Spreads: The model links aggregate consumption directly to the central bank's policy rate, ignoring commercial banking spreads, default risk, and borrower leverage.
  • Inflation Persistence: Pure forward-looking Calvo pricing produces zero intrinsic inflation persistence, requiring exogenous auto-correlated markup shocks to match observed inflation persistence.

Misconceptions

  1. The central bank directly sets the real interest rate in the economy.

    The central bank sets only the nominal policy rate \(i_t\). The real interest rate \(r_t = i_t - \E_t[\pi_{t+1}]\) is determined endogenously by the public's rational inflation expectations. In the long run under flexible prices, the real interest rate is pinned down by structural parameters and technology.

  2. An increase in the policy rate always causes an immediate expansion in output if money demand grows.

    In the New Keynesian cashless economy, money demand is passive. Raising the policy rate increases the real rate gap, depressing household consumption and driving the output gap into negative territory.

  3. The three-equation New Keynesian model predicts that money growth is the primary driver of inflation.

    Inflation is determined by forward-looking firm pricing over anticipated future real marginal costs and output gaps, governed by the central bank's interest rate rule, rather than by an exogenous monetary aggregate.

Problem Set

Problem 5.1 (Matrix Form of the System). Express the three-equation New Keynesian model in the canonical matrix form \(\mathbf{A}_0 \mathbf{z}_t = \mathbf{A}_1 \E_t [\mathbf{z}_{t+1}] + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}_t\), where \(\mathbf{z}_t = \begin{bmatrix} x_t & \pi_t & i_t \end{bmatrix}^\top\) and \(\mathbf{u}_t = \begin{bmatrix} r^n_t & e_t & v_t \end{bmatrix}^\top\).

Solution

Arranging each equation with date \(t\) endogenous variables on the left and expectations on the right: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t + \frac{1}{\sigma} i_t &= \E_t [x_{t+1}] + \frac{1}{\sigma} \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] + \frac{1}{\sigma} r^n_t \\ \pi_t - \kappa x_t &= \beta \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] + e_t \\ i_t - \phi_\pi \pi_t - \phi_y x_t &= v_t \end{aligned} \] In matrix form: \[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \frac{1}{\sigma} \\ -\kappa & 1 & 0 \\ -\phi_y & -\phi_\pi & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_t \\ \pi_t \\ i_t \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac{1}{\sigma} & 0 \\ 0 & \beta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \E_t [x_{t+1}] \\ \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] \\ \E_t [i_{t+1}] \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sigma} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} r^n_t \\ e_t \\ v_t \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \] This represents the standard structural system prior to dynamic inversion.

Problem 5.2 (Exogenous Policy Innovation). Consider an exogenous, purely temporary monetary policy innovation \(v_t\) with \(\E_t[v_{t+1}] = 0\), assuming \(r^n_t = e_t = 0\). Find the impact on the output gap \(x_t\) and inflation \(\pi_t\).

Solution

Since the shock is purely temporary and there are no state variables, \(\E_t [x_{t+1}] = 0\) and \(\E_t [\pi_{t+1}] = 0\). The system reduces to: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} i_t \\ \pi_t &= \kappa x_t \\ i_t &= \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t + v_t = (\phi_\pi \kappa + \phi_y) x_t + v_t \end{aligned} \] Substituting \(i_t\) into the dynamic IS curve: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} [(\phi_\pi \kappa + \phi_y) x_t + v_t] \implies x_t \left( 1 + \frac{\phi_y + \kappa\phi_\pi}{\sigma} \right) = -\frac{1}{\sigma} v_t \end{aligned} \] Solving for \(x_t\) and \(\pi_t\): \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma + \phi_y + \kappa\phi_\pi} v_t \\ \pi_t &= -\frac{\kappa}{\sigma + \phi_y + \kappa\phi_\pi} v_t \end{aligned} \] Both output and inflation contract unambiguously following a positive policy rate surprise.

Problem 5.3 (Determinant Positivity). Show that the denominator \(\Delta = (1 - \beta\rho)(\sigma(1 - \rho) + \phi_y) + \kappa(\phi_\pi - \rho)\) is strictly positive for any standard parameterization with \(\beta \in (0,1)\), \(\rho \in [0,1)\), \(\sigma > 0\), \(\phi_y \ge 0\), \(\kappa > 0\), and \(\phi_\pi > 1\).

Solution

Since \(\beta \in (0,1)\) and \(\rho \in [0,1)\), we have \(1 - \beta\rho > 0\). Furthermore, \(\sigma(1 - \rho) > 0\) and \(\phi_y \ge 0\), so the first term \((1 - \beta\rho)(\sigma(1 - \rho) + \phi_y) > 0\). Since \(\phi_\pi > 1 > \rho\), we have \(\phi_\pi - \rho > 0\). Because \(\kappa > 0\), the second term \(\kappa(\phi_\pi - \rho) > 0\). The sum of two strictly positive real numbers is strictly positive: \(\Delta > 0\).

Problem 5.4 (Cost-Push Shock Transmission). Let \(e_t\) be an AR(1) cost-push markup shock with persistence \(\rho_e \in (0,1)\), with \(r^n_t = v_t = 0\). Show that the output gap and inflation move in opposite directions.

Solution

Using undetermined coefficients \(x_t = b_e e_t\) and \(\pi_t = a_e e_t\), the NKPC yields: \[ \begin{aligned} a_e e_t = \beta \rho_e a_e e_t + \kappa b_e e_t + e_t \implies (1 - \beta\rho_e) a_e - \kappa b_e = 1 \end{aligned} \] From the dynamic IS curve with policy rule: \[ \begin{aligned} b_e (1 - \rho_e) + \frac{1}{\sigma} (\phi_y b_e + \phi_\pi a_e - \rho_e a_e) = 0 \implies b_e [\sigma(1 - \rho_e) + \phi_y] = -(\phi_\pi - \rho_e) a_e \end{aligned} \] Since \(\sigma(1 - \rho_e) + \phi_y > 0\) and \(\phi_\pi > \rho_e\), \(b_e\) and \(a_e\) must have opposite signs. Specifically, \(a_e > 0\) (stagflationary price increase) and \(b_e < 0\) (recessionary contraction in output gap).

Problem 5.5 (Policy Response to Natural Rate Variations). Under what condition on the policy parameters \((\phi_\pi, \phi_y)\) can the central bank achieve complete stabilization (\(x_t = 0\) and \(\pi_t = 0\)) in response to natural rate fluctuations?

Solution

If \(x_t = 0\) and \(\pi_t = 0\) for all \(t\), the NKPC \(\pi_t = \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t\) is identically satisfied with \(e_t = 0\). The dynamic IS curve requires: \[ \begin{aligned} 0 = 0 - \frac{1}{\sigma} (i_t - 0 - r^n_t) \implies i_t = r^n_t \end{aligned} \] To achieve complete stabilization, the nominal interest rate must track the natural rate of interest one-for-one: \(i_t = r^n_t\). If the policy rule is modified to \(i_t = r^n_t + \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t\), the unique bounded equilibrium features \(x_t \equiv 0\) and \(\pi_t \equiv 0\).

Problem 5.6 (Comparative Statics with Respect to Price Rigidity). In the AR(1) natural rate solution, differentiate \(a = \kappa / \Delta\) with respect to \(\kappa\) and interpret the limit as prices become fully flexible (\(\kappa \to \infty\)).

Solution

Recall \(\Delta = C + \kappa(\phi_\pi - \rho)\) where \(C = (1 - \beta\rho)(\sigma(1 - \rho) + \phi_y) > 0\). Then: \[ \begin{aligned} a(\kappa) = \frac{\kappa}{C + \kappa(\phi_\pi - \rho)} = \frac{1}{\frac{C}{\kappa} + (\phi_\pi - \rho)} \end{aligned} \] Taking the derivative with respect to \(\kappa\): \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial a}{\partial \kappa} = \frac{C}{[C + \kappa(\phi_\pi - \rho)]^2} > 0 \end{aligned} \] As \(\kappa \to \infty\) (flexible prices), \(b(\kappa) = \frac{1-\beta\rho}{C + \kappa(\phi_\pi - \rho)} \to 0\), so output gap fluctuations vanish (\(x_t \to 0\)), and actual output tracks natural output perfectly.

Problem 5.7 (Calibrated Shock Response). Using \(\beta = 0.99\), \(\sigma = 1.0\), \(\kappa = 0.1\), \(\phi_\pi = 1.5\), \(\phi_y = 0.5\), and \(\rho = 0.5\), compute the initial nominal policy rate increase following a 100 bps natural rate shock.

Solution

From Example 5.3, the undetermined coefficients are \(b = 0.834711\) and \(a = 0.165289\). The Taylor rule response is: \[ \begin{aligned} i_0 &= \phi_\pi \pi_0 + \phi_y x_0 = [1.5 \times (0.165289 \times 0.01)] + [0.5 \times (0.834711 \times 0.01)] \\ &= 1.5 \times 0.00165289 + 0.5 \times 0.00834711 \\ &= 0.00247934 + 0.00417356 = 0.00665290 \end{aligned} \] The nominal rate rises by approximately 66.5 basis points. Because this is less than the 100 bps increase in \(r^n_t\), the real rate gap \(\hat{r}_t = i_t - \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t\) remains negative, sustaining a positive output gap.

Problem 5.8 (Impact of Monetary Policy Inertia). Suppose the central bank uses an inertial rule \(i_t = \rho_i i_{t-1} + (1 - \rho_i)(\phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t) + v_t\) with \(\rho_i = 0.8\). Explain how this alters the endogenous state space.

Solution

With \(\rho_i > 0\), the lagged policy rate \(i_{t-1}\) enters the date \(t\) system as an endogenous state variable. The state vector expands from the purely exogenous shock \(r^n_t\) to include \(i_{t-1}\). The equilibrium policy functions must take the form \(x_t = c_x i_{t-1} + b_x r^n_t\) and \(\pi_t = c_\pi i_{t-1} + a_\pi r^n_t\), creating endogenous propagation and historical persistence even after exogenous shocks subside.

Problem 5.9 (Volcker Disinflation Simulation). How does the three-equation New Keynesian model account for the output contraction observed during the 1979–1982 Volcker disinflation?

Solution

Under the three-equation NK model, a credible aggressive increase in the policy coefficient \(\phi_\pi\) raises real interest rates above the natural rate. Through the dynamic IS curve, the higher expected real interest path depresses aggregate demand, generating a negative output gap \(x_t < 0\). This negative output gap dampens wage demands and marginal costs, causing forward-looking firms to lower prices and driving inflation downward along the NKPC.

Problem 5.10 (The Divine Coincidence). In the three-equation model without cost-push shocks (\(e_t = 0\)), show that stabilizing inflation completely (\(\pi_t = 0\)) is equivalent to eliminating the output gap (\(x_t = 0\)).

Solution

The NKPC is \(\pi_t = \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t\). If \(\pi_t = 0\) for all \(t\), then \(\E_t[\pi_{t+1}] = 0\). Substituting these into the NKPC gives \(0 = \beta(0) + \kappa x_t = \kappa x_t \implies x_t = 0\) since \(\kappa > 0\). Blanchard and Galí term this the "divine coincidence": absent supply shocks, there is no trade-off between price stability and output gap stabilization.

Problem 5.11 (Optimal Discretionary Policy). Formulate the central bank's period loss function \(\mathcal{L}_t = \pi_t^2 + \lambda x_t^2\) and derive the discretionary optimality condition between inflation and the output gap under Calvo pricing.

Solution

Under discretion, the monetary authority minimizes \(\frac{1}{2}(\pi_t^2 + \lambda x_t^2)\) subject to the NKPC \(\pi_t = \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t + e_t\), taking future expectations \(\E_t[\pi_{t+1}]\) as given. The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}(\pi_t^2 + \lambda x_t^2) + \mu_t (\pi_t - \kappa x_t - \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] - e_t)\). First-order conditions are \(\pi_t + \mu_t = 0\) and \(\lambda x_t - \kappa \mu_t = 0\). Eliminating the multiplier \(\mu_t\) yields the targeting rule \(x_t = -\frac{\kappa}{\lambda} \pi_t\).

Problem 5.12 (Cost-Push Shocks and the Policy Trade-off). Contrast the policy response to an exogenous markup shock \(e_t > 0\) under discretion versus strict inflation targeting.

Solution

With \(e_t > 0\), the NKPC requires \(\pi_t - \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] = \kappa x_t + e_t\). Under strict inflation targeting (\(\pi_t = 0\)), the central bank must force \(x_t = -e_t / \kappa\), causing a severe recession. Under discretion, the central bank balances the trade-off along \(x_t = -(\kappa / \lambda) \pi_t\), absorbing part of the shock as positive inflation and part as a milder economic contraction.

    Interest rate smoothing adds \(i_{t-1}\) as a state variable, causing policy rates and real activity to exhibit gradual adjustment.
