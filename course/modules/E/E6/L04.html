The New Keynesian Phillips Curve

Aggregate price adjustment in modern macroeconomics is forward-looking. The New Keynesian Phillips curve microfounds inflation dynamics through staggered price setting by monopolistically competitive firms, establishing that current inflation is the discounted present value of current and expected future economic slack.

1. Staggered Price Setting and Market Structure

In the New Keynesian framework, price stickiness is introduced via Calvo staggered price setting. A continuum of monopolistically competitive firms on the unit interval \(i \in [0, 1]\) produces differentiated varieties of a consumption good. Output is aggregated into a final good via a Dixit-Stiglitz aggregator with constant elasticity of substitution \(\epsilon > 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_t &= \left( \int_0^1 Y_t(i)^{\frac{\epsilon - 1}{\epsilon}} \, di \right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon - 1}} \end{aligned} \]

The demand for variety \(i\) is \(Y_t(i) = (P_t(i) / P_t)^{-\epsilon} Y_t\), where \(P_t = (\int_0^1 P_t(i)^{1-\epsilon} \, di)^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}}\) is the aggregate price level.

In each period, a firm faces an exogenous probability \(1 - \theta\) of being able to reset its price, where \(\theta \in (0, 1)\) is the Calvo stickiness parameter. A firm that cannot reset keeps its previous price \(P_t(i) = P_{t-1}(i)\). The average duration of a price contract is \(1 / (1 - \theta)\) periods. A resetting firm chooses an optimal price \(P_t^*\) to maximize the expected present discounted value of future profits over the random duration for which the price remains active:

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{P_t^*} \quad & \sum_{k=0}^\infty (\beta \theta)^k \E_t \left[ Q_{t,t+k} \left( P_t^* Y_{t+k|t} - \Psi(Y_{t+k|t}) \right) \right] \end{aligned} \]

where \(Q_{t,t+k} = \beta^k (C_{t+k} / C_t)^{-\sigma}\) is the household stochastic discount factor, \(Y_{t+k|t} = (P_t^* / P_{t+k})^{-\epsilon} Y_{t+k}\), and \(\Psi(\cdot)\) is the total cost function.

2. Deriving the New Keynesian Phillips Curve

Solving the resetting firm's first-order condition and combining it with the aggregate price level dynamic yields the structural inflation equation.

Theorem 4.1 (The New Keynesian Phillips Curve) Under assumptions:
1. Calvo staggered price setting with reset probability \(1 - \theta\), \(\theta \in (0, 1)\),
2. Monopolistic competition with elasticity of substitution \(\epsilon > 1\),
3. Linear production technology \(Y_t(i) = A_t L_t(i)\) with real marginal cost log deviation \(\widehat{mc}_t = (\sigma + \eta) x_t\),
4. Subjective discount factor \(\beta \in (0, 1)\),
the New Keynesian Phillips curve (NKPC) is given by: \[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= \beta \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t \end{aligned} \] where the composite slope parameter \(\kappa\) is strictly positive and defined by: \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa &\equiv \frac{(1 - \theta)(1 - \beta \theta)}{\theta} (\sigma + \eta) \end{aligned} \] and \(x_t\) is the output gap established in the dynamic IS curve.

Proof. The first-order condition for the optimal reset price \(P_t^*\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^\infty (\beta \theta)^k \E_t \left[ Q_{t,t+k} Y_{t+k|t} \left( P_t^* - \frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon - 1} MC_{t+k|t} \right) \right] &= 0 \end{aligned} \] Log-linearizing this condition around zero-inflation steady state: \[ \begin{aligned} p_t^* - p_{t-1} &= (1 - \beta \theta) \sum_{k=0}^\infty (\beta \theta)^k \E_t \left[ \widehat{mc}_{t+k} + (p_{t+k} - p_{t-1}) \right] \end{aligned} \] which can be expressed recursively as: \[ \begin{aligned} p_t^* - p_{t-1} &= \beta \theta \E_t [p_{t+1}^* - p_t] + \pi_t + (1 - \beta \theta) \widehat{mc}_t \end{aligned} \] The aggregate price index satisfies \(P_t^{1-\epsilon} = \theta P_{t-1}^{1-\epsilon} + (1 - \theta) (P_t^*)^{1-\epsilon}\). Log-linearizing around steady state yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= (1 - \theta)(p_t^* - p_{t-1}) \implies p_t^* - p_{t-1} = \frac{\pi_t}{1 - \theta} \end{aligned} \] Substituting this into the recursive reset equation: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\pi_t}{1 - \theta} &= \beta \theta \frac{\E_t [\pi_{t+1}]}{1 - \theta} + \pi_t + (1 - \beta \theta) \widehat{mc}_t \end{aligned} \] Multiplying by \(1 - \theta\) and rearranging: \[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= \beta \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] + \frac{(1 - \theta)(1 - \beta \theta)}{\theta} \widehat{mc}_t \end{aligned} \] Under competitive labor markets, real marginal cost is \(\widehat{mc}_t = \hat{w}_t - \hat{a}_t = (\sigma \hat{y}_t + \eta \hat{l}_t) - \hat{a}_t = (\sigma + \eta) x_t\). Substituting \(\widehat{mc}_t = (\sigma + \eta) x_t\) produces \(\pi_t = \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t\), concluding the proof.

This forward-looking Phillips curve contrasts fundamentally with the backward-looking Phillips curve and accelerationist natural rate of unemployment models of Lesson E5, as articulated in Galí (2015) and Clarida, Galí & Gertler (1999).

3. Forward Solution of the NKPC

Iterating the New Keynesian Phillips curve forward in time reveals that inflation is purely forward-looking: it equals the discounted present value of current and expected future output gaps.

Proposition 4.2 (Discounted Present Value of Output Gaps) Imposing the terminal condition \(\lim_{H \to \infty} \beta^H \E_t [\pi_{t+H}] = 0\), the NKPC solves forward as: \[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= \kappa \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \beta^k \E_t [x_{t+k}] \end{aligned} \] Current inflation is driven entirely by the anticipated trajectory of future economic activity.

If the output gap follows an AR(1) process \(x_{t+k} = \rho^k x_t\) with persistence \(|\rho| < 1\), the infinite geometric series sums to the closed form:

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t^* &= \frac{\kappa}{1 - \beta \rho} x_t \end{aligned} \]

Truncating the forward sum at horizon \(H\) incurs a truncation error of \(\frac{\kappa (\beta \rho)^H}{1 - \beta \rho} x_t\), which decays exponentially with compound rate \(\beta \rho\).

Lab 1 · Forward Solution and Geometric Decay of the NKPC

Truncating the forward sum of the NKPC at horizons H in {4, 8, 12, 16, 20} matches the theoretical truncation error formula with zero machine discrepancy and decays geometrically as (beta * rho)^H. Model output, no data.

"""Lab E6-L04-1: Forward solution of the New Keynesian Phillips Curve.

Iterates the NKPC forward for an AR(1) output gap process.
Demonstrates that the forward-looking inflation rate converges to its closed form
pi_t = (kappa / (1 - beta * rho)) * x_t, with truncation error decaying at geometric rate (beta * rho)^H.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_nkpc_truncated(beta: float, kappa: float, rho: float, x_0: float, horizon: int) -> float:
    """Compute the finite-horizon truncated forward sum for inflation."""
    return float(kappa * sum(((beta * rho) ** k) * x_0 for k in range(horizon)))


def solve_nkpc_exact(beta: float, kappa: float, rho: float, x_0: float) -> float:
    """Compute the exact infinite-horizon forward inflation: kappa * x_0 / (1 - beta * rho)."""
    return float(kappa * x_0 / (1.0 - beta * rho))


def main() -> None:
    beta = 0.99  # Quarterly discount factor
    kappa = 0.1275  # Composite Phillips curve slope
    rho = 0.6  # Output gap persistence
    x_0 = 0.05  # 5% initial output gap deviation

    pi_exact = solve_nkpc_exact(beta, kappa, rho, x_0)

    print(f"NKPC forward solution parameters (beta={beta}, kappa={kappa}, rho={rho}, x_0={x_0}):")
    print(f"  Exact infinite-horizon inflation pi*: {pi_exact:.6f}")
    print("\nForward horizon truncation analysis:")

    horizons = [4, 8, 12, 16, 20]
    for h in horizons:
        pi_trunc = solve_nkpc_truncated(beta, kappa, rho, x_0, h)
        num_err = abs(pi_exact - pi_trunc)
        th_err = abs(kappa * (((beta * rho) ** h) * x_0) / (1.0 - beta * rho))
        diff = abs(num_err - th_err)

        print(
            f"  H = {h:2d}: pi_trunc = {pi_trunc:9.6f}, "
            f"error = {num_err:10.6e}, "
            f"theoretical = {th_err:10.6e}, "
            f"discrepancy = {diff:.2e}"
        )

Where the Canon Breaks

The standard Calvo New Keynesian Phillips curve makes strong stylized assumptions that face empirical challenges:

  • Absence of inflation inertia: The baseline NKPC contains no lagged inflation term \(\pi_{t-1}\). In historical data, inflation exhibits substantial persistence; disinflations are rarely costless and typically require prolonged periods of negative output gaps.
  • Time-dependent vs. state-dependent pricing: The constant Calvo reset probability \(1 - \theta\) assumes firms adjust prices based on a random Poisson timer regardless of how misaligned their prices are. In reality, large inflationary shocks induce firms to pay menu costs and adjust prices endogenously (state-dependent pricing).
  • Price dispersion distortions: Staggered price setting creates artificial price dispersion among identical varieties, misallocating demand across firms and generating welfare losses that scale with the square of the inflation rate.

Misconceptions

  1. A credible disinflation in the NKPC requires high unemployment, just like the traditional Phillips curve.

    Because the NKPC is forward-looking (\(\pi_t = \kappa \sum_{k=0}^\infty \beta^k \E_t[x_{t+k}]\)), a fully credible announcement that future output gaps will be zero allows inflation to drop immediately without any output loss (the Ball-Sargent costless disinflation hypothesis).

  2. The slope \(\kappa\) of the Phillips curve is an invariant empirical constant.

    The slope \(\kappa = \frac{(1 - \theta)(1 - \beta \theta)}{\theta}(\sigma + \eta)\) is a structural composite parameter. If firms adjust prices more frequently (lower \(\theta\)), the Phillips curve becomes dramatically steeper.

  3. The NKPC implies that high current inflation is caused by past money supply growth.

    In the New Keynesian model, inflation is determined by expected future real marginal costs and output gaps, not backward-looking monetary aggregates.

Problem Set

Problem 4.1 (Computing the Composite Slope). Calculate the NKPC slope \(\kappa\) when the Calvo parameter is \(\theta = 0.75\) (prices stick for 4 quarters on average), \(\beta = 0.99\), risk aversion is \(\sigma = 1.0\), and inverse Frisch elasticity is \(\eta = 1.0\).

Solution

Substitute the parameters into the definition of \(\kappa\): \[ \begin{aligned} 1 - \theta &= 1 - 0.75 = 0.25 \\ 1 - \beta \theta &= 1 - 0.99 \times 0.75 = 1 - 0.7425 = 0.2575 \\ \sigma + \eta &= 1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0 \end{aligned} \] Then: \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa &= \frac{0.25 \times 0.2575}{0.75} \times 2.0 = \frac{0.064375}{0.75} \times 2.0 = 0.085833 \times 2.0 = 0.171667 \end{aligned} \] The composite slope is approximately 0.1717.

Problem 4.2 (Inflation Response to Output Gap Boom). For \(\beta = 0.99, \kappa = 0.15\), and persistence \(\rho = 0.7\), compute the inflation rate \(\pi_t\) associated with a 2% output gap (\(x_t = 0.02\)).

Solution

Using the closed-form forward solution: \[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= \frac{\kappa}{1 - \beta \rho} x_t = \frac{0.15}{1 - 0.99 \times 0.7} \times 0.02 = \frac{0.15}{1 - 0.693} \times 0.02 = \frac{0.15}{0.307} \times 0.02 \\ &\approx 0.488599 \times 0.02 \approx 0.009772 \end{aligned} \] Inflation rises by approximately 0.98% (98 basis points) in response to the persistent output boom.

Problem 4.3 (Price Duration Comparative Statics). What happens to the composite slope \(\kappa\) if price stickiness decreases such that the average price duration falls from 4 quarters (\(\theta = 0.75\)) to 2 quarters (\(\theta = 0.50\))? Assume \(\beta = 0.99\) and \(\sigma + \eta = 2.0\).

Solution

For \(\theta = 0.50\): \[ \begin{aligned} 1 - \theta &= 0.50 \\ 1 - \beta \theta &= 1 - 0.99 \times 0.50 = 0.505 \\ \kappa &= \frac{0.50 \times 0.505}{0.50} \times 2.0 = 0.505 \times 2.0 = 1.010 \end{aligned} \] Comparing with \(\kappa \approx 0.1717\) at \(\theta = 0.75\), the slope increases nearly six-fold (from 0.172 to 1.010), demonstrating that prices become dramatically more responsive to economic slack as stickiness falls.

Problem 4.4 (Hybrid NKPC with Rule-of-Thumb Price Setters). Suppose a fraction \(\omega \in [0, 1)\) of firms use a backward-looking rule of thumb to set prices (\(p_t^b = p_{t-1}^* + \pi_{t-1}\)). Prove that the resulting Phillips curve contains lagged inflation \(\pi_{t-1}\).

Solution

The aggregate reset price becomes a weighted average of backward-looking and forward-looking prices: \(\bar{p}_t^* = (1 - \omega) p_t^f + \omega p_t^b\). Aggregating over the price distribution and log-linearizing yields the hybrid New Keynesian Phillips curve: \[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= \gamma_b \pi_{t-1} + \gamma_f \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa_{\text{hybrid}} x_t \end{aligned} \] where \(\gamma_b = \omega / \phi\), \(\gamma_f = \beta \theta / \phi\), and \(\phi \equiv \theta + \omega [1 - \theta (1 - \beta)]\). This proves that introducing rule-of-thumb price indexation generates intrinsic backward-looking inertia, reconciling the model with empirical persistence.

Problem 4.5 (Costless Disinflation under Credible Announcement). Prove that if the central bank credibly announces a permanent policy rule that maintains \(x_{t+k} = 0\) for all \(k \ge 0\), the NKPC predicts that inflation drops to zero immediately without transitional unemployment.

Solution

From Proposition 4.2: \[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= \kappa \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \beta^k \E_t [x_{t+k}] \end{aligned} \] Under full credibility, agents expect \(\E_t[x_{t+k}] = 0\) for all \(k \ge 0\). The infinite sum evaluates to \(\pi_t = \kappa \times 0 = 0\) at date \(t\). Resetting firms anticipate zero future marginal cost pressure and immediately set prices consistent with zero inflation, eliminating the sacrifice ratio.

Problem 4.6 (Decreasing Returns and Strategic Complementarity). Suppose production exhibits decreasing returns to labor \(Y_t(i) = A_t L_t(i)^{1-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\). Prove that decreasing returns lowers the slope \(\kappa\).

Solution

With decreasing returns, firm marginal cost depends on firm-specific output: \(MC_t(i) \propto Y_t(i)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\). When a resetting firm raises its relative price, its demand falls, lowering its labor requirement and reducing its marginal cost. This strategic complementarity makes resetting firms reluctant to change prices aggressively. The composite slope becomes: \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa &= \frac{(1 - \theta)(1 - \beta \theta)}{\theta} \Theta (\sigma + \eta) \end{aligned} \] where \(\Theta = \frac{1 - \alpha}{1 - \alpha + \alpha \epsilon} < 1\). Because \(\Theta < 1\), decreasing returns flattens the Phillips curve, requiring smaller price adjustments for a given output gap.

Problem 4.7 (Microeconomic Price Frequency Data). Bils & Klenow (2004) and Nakamura & Steinsson (2008) analyze micro CPI price quotes and find that the median duration of consumer prices in the US is approximately 4 to 7 months. What range of quarterly Calvo parameters \(\theta\) does this imply?

Solution

In quarterly terms, 4 months corresponds to \(4/3 \approx 1.33\) quarters, and 7 months corresponds to \(7/3 \approx 2.33\) quarters. Since the expected duration is \(D = 1 / (1 - \theta)\): \[ \begin{aligned} 1 - \theta_1 &= \frac{1}{1.33} \approx 0.75 \implies \theta_1 \approx 0.25 \\ 1 - \theta_2 &= \frac{1}{2.33} \approx 0.43 \implies \theta_2 \approx 0.57 \end{aligned} \] The microeconomic frequency of price changes implies a quarterly Calvo parameter \(\theta\) between 0.25 and 0.57, which is substantially lower than the \(\theta \approx 0.75\) often needed to match aggregate macro persistence.

Problem 4.8 (The Flattening of the Phillips Curve). Many empirical studies observe that the slope of the Phillips curve has flattened substantially since the 1980s (falling from \(\kappa \approx 0.20\) to \(\kappa \approx 0.03\)). Compute the implied change in the output loss needed to reduce inflation by 1 percentage point for an i.i.d. disinflation shock.

Solution

For an unexpected one-period disinflation, \(\Delta \pi = \kappa \Delta x \implies \Delta x = \Delta \pi / \kappa\). To reduce inflation by 1% (0.01): \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta x_{\text{1980s}} &= \frac{-0.01}{0.20} = -0.05 \quad (-5.0\%) \\ \Delta x_{\text{recent}} &= \frac{-0.01}{0.03} \approx -0.3333 \quad (-33.3\%) \end{aligned} \] A flatter Phillips curve implies that an enormous output gap contraction is required to disinflate if expectations remain unchanged, explaining why central bank credibility and anchoring of long-term expectations are critical.

Problem 4.9 (Price Indexation in US Data). In DSGE estimations (e.g., Smets & Wouters 2007), the price indexation parameter \(\iota_p\) is estimated around 0.22. What does this indicate about the relative importance of forward-looking vs backward-looking pricing behavior?

Solution

An indexation parameter of \(\iota_p = 0.22\) indicates that forward-looking price optimization accounts for roughly 78% of pricing dynamics, while automatic backward-looking indexation to past inflation accounts for only 22%. This demonstrates that while backward indexation improves empirical fit, forward-looking expectations dominate the structural transmission mechanism.

Problem 4.10 (State-Dependent Pricing and the Selection Effect). Contrast Calvo time-dependent pricing with Golosov & Lucas (2007) menu-cost state-dependent pricing regarding the speed of inflation adjustment.

Solution

Under Calvo pricing, firms are selected at random to adjust prices, regardless of how far their price is from the optimal target. Under state-dependent menu-cost pricing, only firms whose prices are severely misaligned pay the menu cost to adjust (the selection effect). Following an aggregate monetary shock, the firms that adjust are precisely those with the largest desired price changes, generating rapid aggregate price adjustment and much smaller, less persistent real output effects than predicted by Calvo models.

Problem 4.11 (Welfare Costs of Inflation and Price Dispersion). Show why the welfare loss function derived from a second-order approximation of household utility contains a term proportional to inflation variance \(\Var(\pi_t)\).

Solution

Under Calvo pricing, non-zero inflation causes identical varieties to sell at different prices because some firms adjust while others do not. Consumers substitute toward cheaper varieties, distorting consumption baskets away from optimal symmetric proportions. Woodson (2003) proves that the cross-sectional variance of relative prices \(\int_0^1 (p_t(i) - P_t)^2 di\) follows the law of motion \(\Delta_t = \theta \Delta_{t-1} + \frac{\theta}{1 - \theta} \pi_t^2\). Second-order welfare loss is thus proportional to \(\lambda_\pi \pi_t^2 + \lambda_x x_t^2\), establishing that stabilizing inflation directly minimizes productive inefficiency from price dispersion.

Problem 4.12 (Cost-Push Shocks and the Breakdown of Divine Coincidence). In the baseline NKPC, stabilizing inflation (\(\pi_t = 0\)) automatically stabilizes the output gap (\(x_t = 0\)), known as the divine coincidence. Explain how introducing an exogenous wage markup shock \(u_t\) breaks this coincidence.

Solution

Adding a cost-push shock yields \(\pi_t = \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t + u_t\). If the central bank targets \(\pi_t = 0\), it must set \(x_t = -u_t / \kappa \ne 0\), forcing a contraction in real economic activity. Conversely, if it targets \(x_t = 0\), inflation rises to \(\pi_t = u_t / (1 - \beta \rho_u) \ne 0\). The divine coincidence breaks down because the policy authority faces an unavoidable trade-off between inflation and output gap stability.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What does the Calvo parameter \(\theta \in (0, 1)\) measure?

    Options

    \(\theta\) is the fraction of firms that keep their price unchanged; \(1 - \theta\) is the probability of resetting.

  2. How does current inflation behave if expected future output gaps are all zero and current output is at potential?

    Options

    By the forward solution \(\pi_t = \kappa \sum_{k=0}^\infty \beta^k \E_t[x_{t+k}]\), if all output gaps are zero, inflation is zero.

  3. Calculate the composite slope \(\kappa\) when \(\theta = 0.75\), \(\beta = 0.99\), and \(\sigma + \eta = 2.0\).

    \(\kappa = \frac{0.25 \times (1 - 0.7425)}{0.75} \times 2.0 = \frac{0.064375}{0.75} \times 2.0 \approx 0.1717\).

  4. What is the "divine coincidence" in the baseline New Keynesian Phillips curve?

    Options

    Without supply shocks, \(\pi_t = \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t\); setting \(\pi_t = 0\) implies \(x_t = 0\), so monetary policy faces no trade-off.

  5. How does lower price stickiness (smaller \(\theta\)) affect the slope \(\kappa\) of the Phillips curve?

    Options

    When prices adjust more frequently, prices respond more aggressively to real marginal costs, steepening the slope.

  6. What economic distortion arises directly from staggered price setting in the presence of non-zero inflation?

    Options

    When some firms adjust and others do not, identical goods sell at different prices, distorting consumption and production efficiency.

  7. If the Calvo parameter is \(\theta = 0.75\), what is the average duration of a price in quarters (\(1 / (1 - \theta)\))?

    \(1 / (1 - 0.75) = 1 / 0.25 = 4.0\) quarters.

  8. Why can disinflation theoretically be costless in the forward-looking New Keynesian Phillips curve?

    Options

    Because inflation depends on expected future output gaps, credibly anchoring expectations of potential output eliminates inflation instantly.
