Heterogeneous Agents: The HANK Model

Representative-agent macroeconomics assumes all households smooth consumption across time. In reality, a large fraction of households live hand-to-mouth with high marginal propensities to consume, shifting the primary channel of monetary transmission from direct intertemporal substitution to indirect general equilibrium income multipliers.

1. The Breakdown of Representative-Agent Macroeconomics

In the canonical Representative Agent New Keynesian (RANK) framework, aggregate consumption is dictated by a single household's consumption Euler equation. Ricardian equivalence holds, and the transmission of monetary policy operates almost exclusively through the direct substitution channel: changes in the ex-ante real interest rate induce the representative household to shift consumption across time along the dynamic IS curve, contrasting with the capital-accumulation dynamics of the RBC model.

However, microeconomic empirical evidence from consumer expenditure surveys reveals profound household heterogeneity. A substantial portion of households hold virtually zero liquid financial assets, leaving them unable to smooth consumption against income shocks.

Definition 9.1 (The HANK Model) A Heterogeneous Agent New Keynesian model (HANK) embeds uninsurable idiosyncratic labor income risk and borrowing constraints (as in Bewley-Huggett-Aiyagari models) into a general equilibrium New Keynesian framework with sticky prices and endogenous monetary policy. Household decisions are governed by non-linear policy functions over individual liquid and illiquid wealth distributions.

As pioneered by Kaplan, Moll, and Violante [kaplan2018monetary], household heterogeneity fundamentally decomposes households into two broad categories:
1. Poor Hand-to-Mouth: Consumers with zero net worth who consume their entire disposable labor income in every period.
2. Wealthy Hand-to-Mouth: Consumers with substantial illiquid assets (housing, retirement funds) but near-zero liquid balances, exhibiting high marginal propensities to consume (MPCs) out of transitory income innovations.

2. Direct vs. Indirect Transmission Channels

In RANK, when the central bank cuts the policy rate, the increase in consumption is driven by the direct intertemporal substitution effect: \(r_t \downarrow \implies C_t \uparrow\). In HANK, Kaplan, Moll, and Violante show that this direct channel accounts for less than a third of the total consumption response.

Theorem 9.2 (Direct vs Indirect Decomposition) The aggregate consumption response to an unexpected interest rate cut \(\mathrm{d}i_0 < 0\) decomposes into: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}C_0}{\mathrm{d}i_0} &= \underbrace{\int \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial r} \mathrm{d}\Phi_i}_{\text{Direct Channel}} + \underbrace{\int \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial y_i} \frac{\mathrm{d}y_i}{\mathrm{d}Y} \frac{\mathrm{d}Y}{\mathrm{d}i_0} \mathrm{d}\Phi_i}_{\text{Indirect General Equilibrium Channel}} \end{aligned} \] where \(\Phi_i\) denotes the equilibrium joint distribution of wealth and idiosyncratic productivity. Because constrained households have marginal propensities to consume approaching unity, the indirect general equilibrium channel dominates the direct substitution channel.

Proof. Each household \(i\) chooses consumption \(c_i(a_i, y_i, r)\) based on liquid assets \(a_i\), idiosyncratic labor income \(y_i\), and the real rate \(r\). Aggregating across the population: \[ \begin{aligned} C(r, Y) &= \int c_i(a_i, y_i(Y), r) \mathrm{d}\Phi(a_i, y_i) \end{aligned} \] Differentiating with respect to the policy innovation \(\mathrm{d}i_0\), applying the chain rule, and taking the conditional expectation over the distribution yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}i_0} &= \int \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial r} \frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}i_0} \mathrm{d}\Phi + \int \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial y_i} \frac{\mathrm{d}y_i}{\mathrm{d}Y} \frac{\mathrm{d}Y}{\mathrm{d}i_0} \mathrm{d}\Phi \end{aligned} \] For unconstrained households, \(\partial c_i / \partial y_i \approx r \ll 1\), so direct substitution dominates. For hand-to-mouth households, \(\partial c_i / \partial r \approx 0\) and \(\partial c_i / \partial y_i \approx 1.0\). When hand-to-mouth households constitute 30% of the population and receive labor income, the second term exceeds the first, establishing that indirect income effects dominate.

Remark 9.1 (Failure of Ricardian Equivalence) In HANK models, government fiscal policy and transfer timing directly alter aggregate demand. Transitory tax cuts funded by future government debt stimulate immediate spending because hand-to-mouth households consume their tax rebates immediately rather than saving them to pay future taxes, completely breaking Ricardian equivalence.

3. A Tractable Two-Agent (TANK) IS Curve

To capture the core intuition of HANK analytically, macroeconomists examine a Two-Agent New Keynesian (TANK) model. A fraction \(\mu \in [0, 1)\) of households are hand-to-mouth (HtM) and consume their current labor income \(C_t^H = Y_t^H\). The remaining fraction \(1-\mu\) are unconstrained Ricardian (R) households who satisfy the standard Euler equation.

Theorem 9.3 (Aggregate IS Sensitivity Amplification) Let individual labor income have elasticity \(\lambda > 1\) with respect to aggregate output (\(\hat{y}_t^H = \lambda \hat{y}_t\)). The log-linearized aggregate dynamic IS curve is: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_t &= \E_t [\hat{y}_{t+1}] - \mathcal{S}(\mu) \hat{r}_t \end{aligned} \] where the aggregate real interest rate sensitivity is: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{S}(\mu) &= \frac{1}{\sigma} \frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \mu\lambda} \end{aligned} \] provided \(\mu\lambda < 1\). The amplification factor relative to the representative-agent baseline \(\mathcal{S}(0) = 1/\sigma\) is \(\frac{1-\mu}{1-\mu\lambda} > 1\).

Proof. Aggregate output equals aggregate consumption: \(\hat{y}_t = \mu \hat{c}_t^H + (1 - \mu) \hat{c}_t^R\). Since HtM consumers consume their income, \(\hat{c}_t^H = \lambda \hat{y}_t\). Substituting gives: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_t &= \mu \lambda \hat{y}_t + (1 - \mu) \hat{c}_t^R \implies \hat{c}_t^R = \frac{1 - \mu\lambda}{1 - \mu} \hat{y}_t \end{aligned} \] The Ricardian household satisfies the Euler equation \(\hat{c}_t^R = \E_t [\hat{c}_{t+1}^R] - \frac{1}{\sigma}\hat{r}_t\). Substituting \(\hat{c}_t^R\) into this Euler equation: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1 - \mu\lambda}{1 - \mu} \hat{y}_t &= \frac{1 - \mu\lambda}{1 - \mu} \E_t [\hat{y}_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma}\hat{r}_t \end{aligned} \] Multiplying both sides by \(\frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \mu\lambda}\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_t &= \E_t [\hat{y}_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \left( \frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \mu\lambda} \right) \hat{r}_t \end{aligned} \] Since \(\lambda > 1\), \(1 - \mu\lambda < 1 - \mu\), which implies \(\frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \mu\lambda} > 1\), completing the proof.

Example 9.4 (Amplification Calculation) Suppose \(\sigma = 1.0\), \(\lambda = 1.25\), and the share of hand-to-mouth households is \(\mu = 0.30\) (consistent with US Survey of Consumer Finances estimates). \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{S}(0.30) &= \frac{1}{1.0} \frac{1 - 0.30}{1 - 0.30 \times 1.25} = \frac{0.70}{1 - 0.375} = \frac{0.70}{0.625} = 1.1200 \end{aligned} \] The aggregate interest rate sensitivity is amplified by 12.0% above the representative-agent baseline. If \(\mu = 0.40\), \(\mathcal{S}(0.40) = \frac{0.60}{1 - 0.50} = 1.2000\), generating a 20.0% amplification.

4. Computational Lab: HANK Interest Rate Sensitivity Check

The lab below computes the aggregate interest rate sensitivity and amplification factor \(\mathcal{S}(\mu)/\mathcal{S}(0)\) across varying shares of hand-to-mouth households \(\mu \in [0.0, 0.4]\), demonstrating how liquidity constraints steepen aggregate demand responses.

Lab 1 · Hand-to-Mouth Share and IS Sensitivity

The analytical amplification factor (1 - mu) / (1 - mu * lambda) strictly exceeds 1 for procyclical labor elasticity lambda > 1, increasing monotonically with the hand-to-mouth share mu. Model output, no data.

"""Lab E6-L09-1: Heterogeneous agents: Hand-to-mouth share and aggregate interest rate sensitivity.

Evaluates the closed-form aggregate IS interest rate sensitivity in a tractable
heterogeneous-agent (HANK / TANK) economy with hand-to-mouth share mu and procyclical
labor income elasticity lambda > 1.
Computes the analytical amplification factor S(mu) / S(0) = (1 - mu) / (1 - mu * lambda)
over a parameter grid, showing how household heterogeneity amplifies the transmission of
real interest rate innovations.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def hank_interest_sensitivity(sigma: float, mu: float, lambda_val: float) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Compute aggregate real interest rate sensitivity and amplification factor.

    Parameters
    ----------
    sigma : float
        Intertemporal elasticity / CRRA risk aversion of unconstrained households.
    mu : float
        Share of hand-to-mouth households in the population (0 <= mu < 1/lambda).
    lambda_val : float
        Cyclicality of labor income relative to aggregate output (lambda > 1).

    Returns
    -------
    tuple[float, float]
        (aggregate_sensitivity, amplification_factor)
    """
    if mu * lambda_val >= 1.0:
        raise ValueError("Indeterminate/explosive Keynesian cross multiplier: mu * lambda >= 1")

    base_sensitivity = 1.0 / sigma
    amplification = (1.0 - mu) / (1.0 - mu * lambda_val)
    aggregate_sensitivity = base_sensitivity * amplification
    return aggregate_sensitivity, amplification


def main() -> None:
    sigma = 1.0  # Unit risk aversion
    lambda_val = 1.25  # Labor income elasticity under sticky-price countercyclical markups

    print("Heterogeneous-Agent Aggregate Sensitivity Check:")
    print(f"Parameters: sigma={sigma}, labor income elasticity lambda={lambda_val}")
    print("Representative-agent (RANK) baseline sensitivity S(0) = 1.0000\n")

    mu_grid = [0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4]
    for mu in mu_grid:
        sens, amp = hank_interest_sensitivity(sigma, mu, lambda_val)
        print(f"  Hand-to-mouth share mu = {mu:.1f}: aggregate sensitivity S = {sens:6.4f}, amplification = {amp:6.4f}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

HANK models represent a major frontier in modern general equilibrium macroeconomics, but present substantial theoretical and computational challenges:

  • Computational Curse of Dimensionality: Solving full non-linear HANK models requires tracking an infinite-dimensional time-varying wealth and income distribution \(\Phi_t(a, y)\), requiring advanced dimensional-reduction algorithms (e.g., Reiter method, sequence space Jacobian).
  • Sensitivity to Micro-Targeting of Shocks: Because households have heterogeneous MPCs, macroeconomic responses depend sensitively on exactly which households receive income or transfers (e.g., whether profits accrue to wealthy Ricardians or workers).
  • Asset Market Incompleteness Assumptions: HANK results rely heavily on the assumption that borrowing constraints are strictly exogenous and cannot be circumvented through informal private credit or fintech lending platforms.

Misconceptions

  1. Hand-to-mouth households consist exclusively of low-income, unemployed, or impoverished individuals.

    Kaplan, Violante, and Weidner (2014) demonstrate that roughly two-thirds of all hand-to-mouth households are "wealthy hand-to-mouth": they earn middle-to-high incomes and own illiquid housing or retirement wealth, but hold near-zero liquid buffer stock balances.

  2. Monetary policy affects hand-to-mouth households primarily by lowering the borrowing rate on their credit card debt.

    The primary transmission channel to hand-to-mouth households is indirect: lower interest rates expand overall business investment and consumption, creating jobs and raising total hours worked, which directly increases their wage earnings.

  3. HANK models predict that the central bank's policy rate has no direct effect on aggregate demand.

    The policy rate continues to directly affect the unconstrained Ricardian households who smooth consumption; however, their initial consumption change triggers an indirect labor-income multiplier that amplifies aggregate spending.

Problem Set

Problem 9.1 (Keynesian Cross Multiplier). Given the sensitivity formula \(\mathcal{S}(\mu) = \frac{1}{\sigma} \frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \mu\lambda}\), find the critical hand-to-mouth share \(\mu^*\) at which the multiplier becomes explosive (\(\mu\lambda = 1\)) when \(\lambda = 1.5\).

Solution

The singularity occurs when the denominator equals zero: \[ \begin{aligned} 1 - \mu^* \lambda = 0 \implies \mu^* = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{1.5} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.6667 \end{aligned} \] If the hand-to-mouth share reaches or exceeds 66.7%, the static Keynesian multiplier feedback between income and consumption becomes unbounded.

Problem 9.2 (Countercyclical Labor Elasticity). If markups are constant rather than countercyclical so that labor income is strictly proportional to output (\(\lambda = 1.0\)), calculate the amplification factor \(\mathcal{S}(\mu) / \mathcal{S}(0)\).

Solution

Substitute \(\lambda = 1.0\) into the amplification formula: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathcal{S}(\mu)}{\mathcal{S}(0)} &= \frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \mu(1.0)} = \frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \mu} = 1.0 \end{aligned} \] When labor income moves one-for-one with output, the reduction in Ricardian consumption weight \((1-\mu)\) exactly cancels the hand-to-mouth multiplier, leaving aggregate sensitivity unchanged from the RANK baseline.

Problem 9.3 (Aggregate MPC Calculation). In an economy where 30% of households are hand-to-mouth with an MPC of \(0.95\) and 70% are Ricardian with an MPC of \(0.05\), compute the population average marginal propensity to consume.

Solution

The population average MPC is the weighted sum: \[ \begin{aligned} \overline{\mathrm{MPC}} &= \mu \mathrm{MPC}_H + (1 - \mu) \mathrm{MPC}_R \\ &= 0.30 \times 0.95 + 0.70 \times 0.05 \\ &= 0.285 + 0.035 = 0.320 \end{aligned} \] The average MPC is 0.32, more than six times higher than the representative-agent baseline (\(0.05\)).

Problem 9.4 (Monotonicity of Amplification). Prove that for any \(\lambda > 1\) and \(\mu \in [0, 1/\lambda)\), the amplification factor \(\mathcal{A}(\mu) = \frac{1 - \mu}{1 - \mu\lambda}\) is strictly increasing in \(\mu\).

Solution

Differentiate \(\mathcal{A}(\mu)\) with respect to \(\mu\) using the quotient rule: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}}{\mathrm{d}\mu} &= \frac{(-1)(1 - \mu\lambda) - (1 - \mu)(-\lambda)}{(1 - \mu\lambda)^2} \\ &= \frac{-1 + \mu\lambda + \lambda - \mu\lambda}{(1 - \mu\lambda)^2} = \frac{\lambda - 1}{(1 - \mu\lambda)^2} \end{aligned} \] Since \(\lambda > 1\), the numerator is strictly positive: \(\lambda - 1 > 0\). The denominator is a squared real number, hence strictly positive for all \(\mu \ne 1/\lambda\). Therefore \(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}}{\mathrm{d}\mu} > 0\), proving strict monotonicity.

Problem 9.5 (Breakdown of Ricardian Equivalence). Formally prove that a deficit-financed lump-sum tax rebate \(\Delta T_t < 0\) alters aggregate consumption in TANK, whereas \(\Delta C_t = 0\) in RANK.

Solution

In RANK, the intertemporal budget constraint of the representative household satisfies \(\sum_{t=0}^\infty R^{-t} C_t = \sum_{t=0}^\infty R^{-t} (Y_t - T_t)\). Because government debt satisfies \(\sum_{t=0}^\infty R^{-t} T_t = \sum_{t=0}^\infty R^{-t} G_t\), the present value of tax liabilities is invariant to the timing of taxes: \(\Delta C_t = 0\). In TANK, hand-to-mouth households consume \(C_t^H = Y_t^H - T_t^H\). A tax rebate targeted at HtM households yields \(\Delta C_t^H = -\Delta T_t^H > 0\). Since Ricardians do not offset this immediate spending, aggregate consumption rises by \(\mu(-\Delta T_t^H) > 0\), proving Ricardian equivalence fails.

Problem 9.6 (Indirect Income Channel Dominance). In the two-asset HANK model of Kaplan, Moll, and Violante (2018), prove that as the transaction cost of withdrawing funds from the illiquid asset approaches infinity, the direct substitution channel of wealthy hand-to-mouth households vanishes.

Solution

Let the household asset adjustment cost be \(\chi(d)\) where \(d\) is deposit/withdrawal from illiquid assets. As \(\chi \to \infty\), the optimal deposit/withdrawal rate \(d_i^* \to 0\). The household's liquid asset budget constraint reduces to \(\dot{a} = y_i + r a_i - c_i\). For a household at the borrowing limit \(a_i = 0\) with \(\dot{a} = 0\), consumption is constrained by \(c_i = y_i\). Taking the partial derivative with respect to the interest rate: \(\partial c_i / \partial r = a_i = 0\). Hence the direct substitution channel vanishes, leaving only indirect labor income effects \(\partial c_i / \partial y_i = 1\).

Problem 9.7 (Empirical Hand-to-Mouth Shares). According to Kaplan, Violante, and Weidner (2014), estimate the proportion of US households categorized as "wealthy hand-to-mouth" versus "poor hand-to-mouth".

Solution

Analyzing the Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), Kaplan, Violante, and Weidner find that approximately 31% of US households are hand-to-mouth. Of these, approximately 10% are "poor hand-to-mouth" (possessing neither liquid nor illiquid wealth), while approximately 21% are "wealthy hand-to-mouth" (holding positive net housing or pension wealth exceeding one month of income, but liquid wealth below two weeks of income).

Problem 9.8 (Fiscal Stimulus Checks MPC). During the 2020 CARES Act economic impact payments, empirical studies (e.g., Parker et al., 2022) found 3-month spending MPCs of approximately 0.40. Contrast this with the prediction of a RANK model.

Solution

Under a standard annual calibration of RANK with quarterly discount factor \(\beta = 0.99\) and interest rate \(r = 0.01\), the permanent income hypothesis predicts a quarterly MPC out of transitory windfall transfers of approximately \(r / (1 + r) \approx 0.01\) (1%). The observed MPC of 0.40 is forty times larger than the RANK prediction, strongly supporting the presence of substantial hand-to-mouth households as modeled in HANK.

Problem 9.9 (Inequality and the Slope of the NKPC). How does household heterogeneity in labor supply affect the empirical slope \(\kappa\) of the New Keynesian Phillips curve in HANK models?

Solution

In HANK models, low-wealth households have higher marginal utility of consumption, leading to higher labor supply elasticity under income effects. When aggregate demand rises, constrained households increase labor hours without demanding large real wage increases, dampening the rise in real marginal costs. Consequently, HANK models predict a flatter Phillips curve (\(\kappa_{\mathrm{HANK}} < \kappa_{\mathrm{RANK}}\)), helping explain the missing inflation observed during pre-pandemic economic expansions.

Problem 9.10 (Sequence-Space Jacobian Method). Outline the Sequence Space Jacobian (SSJ) methodology of Auclert, Bardóczy, Rognlie, and Straub (2021) for solving high-dimensional HANK models.

Solution

Instead of linearizing around the cross-sectional distribution \(\Phi\) state-by-state, the SSJ method computes the total Jacobian matrices of aggregate demand with respect to the sequence of future real interest rates \(\mathbf{J}_{C,r} = \partial \mathbf{C} / \partial \mathbf{r}\) and labor incomes \(\mathbf{J}_{C,Y} = \partial \mathbf{C} / \partial \mathbf{Y}\) in sequence space. The general equilibrium market clearing condition \(\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{Y})\) is then inverted as a single linear matrix system: \(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Y} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{J}_{C,Y})^{-1} \mathbf{J}_{C,r} \mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\), providing enormous computational acceleration.

Problem 9.11 (Redistribution Channel of Monetary Policy). In Auclert (2019), monetary policy redistributes wealth through the Fisher channel, earnings heterogeneity channel, and unhedged interest rate exposure. Explain each component.

Solution

Auclert decomposes the monetary transmission into: (i) Earnings heterogeneity: monetary expansion disproportionately boosts hours and wages for low-income workers with high MPCs; (ii) Fisher channel: unexpected inflation reduces the real value of nominal debt, transferring wealth from low-MPC creditors to high-MPC debtors; (iii) Unhedged interest rate exposure: lower real rates hurt net savers with short-term assets and benefit net borrowers with maturing liabilities. All three channels redistribute income to high-MPC agents, magnifying aggregate consumption.

Problem 9.12 (Forward Guidance Puzzle Resolution in HANK). Explain why HANK models attenuate the "forward guidance puzzle" relative to RANK models.

Solution

In RANK, the forward-looking Euler equation has no discounting on future interest rates, causing interest rate commitments at horizon \(H = 20\) to have an implausibly large impact on date 0 output. In HANK, hand-to-mouth households cannot respond to future interest rates directly, and unconstrained households face idiosyncratic uninsurable income risk and mortality/borrowing bounds that induce effective compound discounting on future income. Consequently, the general equilibrium Keynesian multiplier decays rapidly with the forward horizon, substantially resolving the forward guidance puzzle.
