Equilibrium Determinacy and the Taylor Principle

An improperly designed interest rate rule can plunge a macroeconomic system into self-fulfilling sunspot fluctuations. The Taylor principle establishes the exact mathematical condition under which monetary policy guarantees a unique, stable rational expectations equilibrium.

1. Blanchard-Kahn Conditions and Root Counting

In expectational general equilibrium models, not all valid solutions to the structural difference equations represent desirable or fundamental macroeconomic outcomes. If the system is indeterminate, arbitrary self-fulfilling expectations (sunspots) can trigger real economic fluctuations. In contrast, explosive paths violate household transversality conditions.

Equilibrium determinacy refers to the existence of a unique, non-explosive rational expectations path along which endogenous variables are driven exclusively by fundamental structural disturbances.

Definition 7.1 (Blanchard-Kahn Determinacy) Consider a linear rational expectations system written in companion form: \[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}_{t+1} \\ \E_t [\mathbf{y}_{t+1}] \end{bmatrix} &= \mathbf{M} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}_t \\ \mathbf{y}_t \end{bmatrix} + \mathbf{C} \mathbf{s}_t \end{aligned} \] where \(\mathbf{x}_t\) is an \(n_x\)-dimensional vector of predetermined (state) variables with initial conditions \(\mathbf{x}_0\), and \(\mathbf{y}_t\) is an \(n_y\)-dimensional vector of non-predetermined (jump) variables. By the Blanchard-Kahn (1980) theorem, the system possesses a unique stationary rational expectations equilibrium if and only if the number of eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{M}\) strictly outside the unit circle (\(|\lambda_i| > 1\)) equals exactly \(n_y\).

In the canonical three-equation New Keynesian model without capital or habit formation, there are no predetermined state variables (\(n_x = 0\)), and both the output gap and inflation are forward-looking jump variables (\(n_y = 2\)). Therefore, determinacy requires that both eigenvalues of the companion matrix lie strictly outside the unit circle.

2. The Forward Companion Matrix

Substituting the policy rule \(i_t = \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t\) into the dynamic IS curve and solving for expectations \(\E_t[x_{t+1}]\) and \(\E_t[\pi_{t+1}]\) yields the dynamic system:

Theorem 7.2 (Companion Matrix Representation) The expectational dynamics of the three-equation New Keynesian system can be expressed as: \[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} \E_t [x_{t+1}] \\ \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] \end{bmatrix} &= \mathbf{M} \begin{bmatrix} x_t \\ \pi_t \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \] where the companion matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) is given by: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{M} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 + \frac{\phi_y}{\sigma} + \frac{\kappa}{\beta\sigma} & \frac{\phi_\pi - 1/\beta}{\sigma} \\ -\frac{\kappa}{\beta} & \frac{1}{\beta} \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

Proof. Begin with the NKPC: \[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= \beta \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t \implies \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] = \frac{1}{\beta} \pi_t - \frac{\kappa}{\beta} x_t \end{aligned} \] Next, rearrange the dynamic IS curve: \[ \begin{aligned} \E_t [x_{t+1}] &= x_t + \frac{1}{\sigma} (i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}]) \end{aligned} \] Substitute \(i_t = \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t\) and the expression for \(\E_t [\pi_{t+1}]\): \[ \begin{aligned} \E_t [x_{t+1}] &= x_t + \frac{1}{\sigma} \left[ \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t - \left( \frac{1}{\beta} \pi_t - \frac{\kappa}{\beta} x_t \right) \right] \\ &= \left( 1 + \frac{\phi_y}{\sigma} + \frac{\kappa}{\beta\sigma} \right) x_t + \left( \frac{\phi_\pi - 1/\beta}{\sigma} \right) \pi_t \end{aligned} \] Arranging the two equations in vector form yields matrix \(\mathbf{M}\).

3. The Generalized Taylor Principle

Analyzing the characteristic polynomial of \(\mathbf{M}\) via the Schur-Cohn or Jury criterion yields the analytical determinacy boundary established by Bullard and Mitra (2002) and Woodford [woodford2004interest].

Definition 7.3 (The Generalized Taylor Principle) In the canonical three-equation New Keynesian model, the monetary authority ensures equilibrium determinacy if and only if the policy rule coefficients satisfy the Taylor principle: \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1 - \beta)\phi_y &> 0 \end{aligned} \] When \(\phi_y = 0\), this condition simplifies to the celebrated requirement \(\phi_\pi > 1\): the central bank must respond to an increase in inflation by raising the nominal interest rate more than one-for-one, thereby raising the real interest rate.

Remark 7.1 (Intuition of Indeterminacy) When \(\phi_\pi < 1\) (a passive policy rule), an exogenous increase in expected inflation induces the central bank to raise nominal rates by less than the inflation expectations increase. Consequently, the expected real interest rate \(r_t = i_t - \E_t[\pi_{t+1}]\) falls. The lower real rate stimulates aggregate consumption through the dynamic IS curve, overheating the economy and driving actual inflation upward via the NKPC, completely validating the initial arbitrary expectations.

Example 7.4 (Determinacy Boundary Calculation) With \(\beta = 0.99\) and \(\kappa = 0.1\): \[ \begin{aligned} \phi_\pi &> 1 - \frac{1 - \beta}{\kappa} \phi_y = 1 - \frac{0.01}{0.1} \phi_y = 1 - 0.1 \phi_y \end{aligned} \] If \(\phi_y = 0.5\), the critical threshold is \(\phi_\pi^* = 1 - 0.05 = 0.95\). If \(\phi_\pi = 0.8\), \(\kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1-\beta)\phi_y = 0.1(-0.2) + 0.01(0.5) = -0.02 + 0.005 = -0.015 < 0\), and the system is indeterminate.

4. Computational Lab: Determinacy Grid and Eigenvalues

The lab below constructs the companion matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) across a grid of policy parameters \((\phi_\pi, \phi_y)\), computes its eigenvalues numerically, tests whether both roots lie outside the unit circle, and validates perfect agreement with the analytical generalized Taylor principle.

Lab 1 · Companion Matrix Eigenvalues and Determinacy

The numerical eigenvalues of companion matrix M lie strictly outside the unit circle if and only if the analytical generalized Taylor principle condition holds. Model output, no data.

"""Lab E6-L07-1: Determinacy, root counting, and the Taylor principle.

Constructs the dynamic companion matrix for the three-equation New Keynesian system
across a parameter grid for (phi_pi, phi_y). Computes system eigenvalues and tests whether
both roots lie outside the unit circle in the complex plane, matching the analytical
generalized Taylor principle condition: kappa * (phi_pi - 1) + (1 - beta) * phi_y > 0.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def analyze_determinacy(
    beta: float, sigma: float, kappa: float, phi_pi: float, phi_y: float
) -> tuple[bool, bool, float, float]:
    """Compute eigenvalues and check determinacy conditions."""
    # System companion matrix M for [E_t[x_{t+1}], E_t[pi_{t+1}]]^T = M * [x_t, pi_t]^T
    m_mat = np.array(
        [
            [1.0 + phi_y / sigma + kappa / (beta * sigma), (phi_pi - 1.0 / beta) / sigma],
            [-kappa / beta, 1.0 / beta],
        ],
        dtype=np.float64,
    )
    eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvals(m_mat)
    moduli = np.abs(eigenvalues)

    # Determinacy requires all non-predetermined roots outside the unit circle (|lambda| > 1)
    roots_outside = bool(np.all(moduli > 1.0 + 1e-10))

    # Analytical generalized Taylor principle
    det_metric = kappa * (phi_pi - 1.0) + (1.0 - beta) * phi_y
    analytical_cond = bool(det_metric > 1e-10)

    return roots_outside, analytical_cond, float(moduli[0]), float(moduli[1])


def main() -> None:
    beta = 0.99
    sigma = 1.0
    kappa = 0.1

    print("Determinacy and Taylor Principle Analysis (beta=0.99, sigma=1.0, kappa=0.1):")
    print("  Critical boundary: phi_pi = 1.0 - (1 - beta) * phi_y / kappa = 1.0 - 0.1 * phi_y\n")

    phi_y_grid = [0.0, 0.5, 1.0]
    for phi_y in phi_y_grid:
        crit_phi_pi = 1.0 - (1.0 - beta) * phi_y / kappa
        test_points = [
            (crit_phi_pi - 0.2, "Indeterminate"),
            (crit_phi_pi, "Boundary (Unit Root)"),
            (crit_phi_pi + 0.2, "Determinate"),
        ]

        print(f"--- phi_y = {phi_y:.1f} (Critical phi_pi = {crit_phi_pi:.4f}) ---")
        for phi_pi, regime in test_points:
            roots_out, cond_holds, mod1, mod2 = analyze_determinacy(beta, sigma, kappa, phi_pi, phi_y)
            agreement = roots_out == cond_holds
            print(
                f"  phi_pi = {phi_pi:6.4f} [{regime:21s}] | "
                f"|lambda_1| = {mod1:6.4f}, |lambda_2| = {mod2:6.4f} | "
                f"Determinate: {roots_out!s:5s} | Agreement: {agreement!s}"
            )

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical Taylor principle provides a clean prescription for determinacy, but breaks down under key institutional and empirical realities:

  • The Zero Lower Bound and Liquidity Traps: When the nominal interest rate hits the zero lower bound, the central bank cannot raise rates in response to deflationary pressures (\(\phi_\pi = 0\) locally), precipitating indeterminacy and potential liquidity-trap deflationary spirals.
  • Forecast-Targeting and Lagged Information: If the central bank responds to long-horizon future forecasts (\(i_t = \phi_\pi \E_t[\pi_{t+k}]\)) or uses noisy real-time data, hyper-aggressive feedback (\(\phi_\pi \gg 2.5\)) can paradoxically introduce cyclical indeterminacy and explosive flip-flop dynamics.
  • Fiscal Dominance and Non-Ricardian Regimes: In the Fiscal Theory of the Price Level (Leeper 1991), if fiscal policy is active (debt is non-Ricardian), monetary determinacy requires a passive monetary rule (\(\phi_\pi < 1\)), completely reversing the Taylor principle.

Misconceptions

  1. An indeterminate economy is unstable and immediately explodes towards infinity.

    Indeterminacy means that too many roots are stable (inside the unit circle). This allows multiple bounded, stationary paths to exist, enabling non-fundamental sunspot fluctuations rather than explosive divergence.

  2. The Taylor principle requires \(\phi_\pi > 1\) under all circumstances.

    When the policy rule includes positive output gap feedback (\(\phi_y > 0\)), the condition is generalized to \(\kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1-\beta)\phi_y > 0\). An aggressive output response can sustain determinacy even when \(\phi_\pi\) is slightly below 1.

  3. Determinacy means that the economy is shielded from all macroeconomic shocks.

    Determinacy ensures that the equilibrium is unique and governed exclusively by fundamental structural shocks; it does not eliminate economic volatility caused by technology, preference, or policy disturbances.

Problem Set

Problem 7.1 (Characteristic Polynomial). Derive the characteristic polynomial \(P(\lambda) = \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{M})\) of the companion matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) in terms of \(\beta, \sigma, \kappa, \phi_\pi, \phi_y\).

Solution

The companion matrix is: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{M} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 + \frac{\phi_y}{\sigma} + \frac{\kappa}{\beta\sigma} & \frac{\phi_\pi - 1/\beta}{\sigma} \\ -\frac{\kappa}{\beta} & \frac{1}{\beta} \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \] The trace is \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M}) = 1 + \frac{\phi_y}{\sigma} + \frac{\kappa}{\beta\sigma} + \frac{1}{\beta}\). The determinant is: \[ \begin{aligned} \det(\mathbf{M}) &= \frac{1}{\beta} \left(1 + \frac{\phi_y}{\sigma} + \frac{\kappa}{\beta\sigma}\right) - \left(-\frac{\kappa}{\beta}\right)\left(\frac{\phi_\pi - 1/\beta}{\sigma}\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{\beta} + \frac{\phi_y}{\beta\sigma} + \frac{\kappa}{\beta^2\sigma} + \frac{\kappa\phi_\pi}{\beta\sigma} - \frac{\kappa}{\beta^2\sigma} = \frac{1}{\beta} + \frac{\phi_y + \kappa\phi_\pi}{\beta\sigma} \end{aligned} \] The characteristic polynomial is \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M})\lambda + \det(\mathbf{M})\).

Problem 7.2 (Evaluating P(1)). Evaluate the characteristic polynomial at \(\lambda = 1\) and show that \(P(1) > 0\) is equivalent to the generalized Taylor principle.

Solution

Substitute \(\lambda = 1\) into \(P(\lambda)\): \[ \begin{aligned} P(1) &= 1 - \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M}) + \det(\mathbf{M}) \\ &= 1 - \left(1 + \frac{\phi_y}{\sigma} + \frac{\kappa}{\beta\sigma} + \frac{1}{\beta}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{\beta} + \frac{\phi_y + \kappa\phi_\pi}{\beta\sigma}\right) \\ &= -\frac{\phi_y}{\sigma} - \frac{\kappa}{\beta\sigma} + \frac{\phi_y}{\beta\sigma} + \frac{\kappa\phi_\pi}{\beta\sigma} \\ &= \frac{1}{\beta\sigma} \left[ \phi_y(1 - \beta) + \kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) \right] \end{aligned} \] Since \(\beta > 0\) and \(\sigma > 0\), \(P(1) > 0\) if and only if \(\kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1 - \beta)\phi_y > 0\).

Problem 7.3 (Trace and Unit Circle). Prove that for any non-negative policy parameters (\(\phi_\pi \ge 0, \phi_y \ge 0\)), \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M}) > 2\).

Solution

The trace is: \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M}) &= 1 + \frac{1}{\beta} + \frac{\phi_y}{\sigma} + \frac{\kappa}{\beta\sigma} \end{aligned} \] Since \(\beta \in (0,1)\), \(1/\beta > 1\), so \(1 + 1/\beta > 2\). Furthermore, \(\sigma > 0\), \(\kappa > 0\), and \(\phi_y \ge 0\), meaning \(\frac{\phi_y}{\sigma} + \frac{\kappa}{\beta\sigma} > 0\). Thus \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M}) > 2\) strictly holds.

Problem 7.4 (Blanchard-Kahn Root Counting Proof). Prove that when \(P(1) > 0\), \(\det(\mathbf{M}) > 1\), and \(P(-1) > 0\), both eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{M}\) lie strictly outside the unit circle.

Solution

For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, the conditions for both roots to lie outside the unit circle correspond to the inverse roots \(\mu_i = 1/\lambda_i\) lying strictly inside the unit circle. The characteristic polynomial for \(\mu\) is \(Q(\mu) = \mu^2 - \frac{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M})}{\det(\mathbf{M})}\mu + \frac{1}{\det(\mathbf{M})}\). By the Jury / Cohn-Schur stability criterion, both roots of \(Q(\mu)\) lie strictly inside the open unit disk if and only if: (i) \(|Q(0)| = 1 / |\det(\mathbf{M})| < 1 \iff \det(\mathbf{M}) > 1\); (ii) \(Q(1) = P(1) / \det(\mathbf{M}) > 0 \iff P(1) > 0\); (iii) \(Q(-1) = P(-1) / \det(\mathbf{M}) > 0 \iff P(-1) > 0\). These conditions ensure both \(\mu_i \in (-1, 1)\), which proves that both original eigenvalues satisfy \(|\lambda_i| > 1\).

Problem 7.5 (Indeterminacy and Sunspot Construction). Prove that if \(\kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1-\beta)\phi_y < 0\), there exists a bounded stationary rational expectations equilibrium driven by an arbitrary zero-mean martingale difference sunspot process \(\xi_t\).

Solution

When the condition fails, \(P(1) < 0\). Since \(\lim_{\lambda \to \infty} P(\lambda) = +\infty\), the intermediate value theorem implies that \(\mathbf{M}\) has one real eigenvalue \(\lambda_1 \in (0, 1)\) inside the unit circle and one eigenvalue \(\lambda_2 > 1\). Let \(\mathbf{v}_1\) be the left eigenvector associated with \(\lambda_1\): \(\mathbf{v}_1 \mathbf{M} = \lambda_1 \mathbf{v}_1\). The projection \(z_t \equiv \mathbf{v}_1 \mathbf{z}_t\) satisfies \(\E_t[z_{t+1}] = \lambda_1 z_t\). Setting \(z_{t+1} = \lambda_1 z_t + \xi_{t+1}\) with \(\E_t[\xi_{t+1}] = 0\) satisfies rational expectations for any bounded sunspot process \(\xi_t\) because \(|\lambda_1| < 1\) prevents geometric explosion, proving indeterminacy.

Problem 7.6 (Equivalence to Real Interest Rate Response). Prove that the Taylor principle condition is mathematically equivalent to requiring that in response to any persistent inflation increase, the long-run endogenous real interest rate rises.

Solution

Consider a permanent increase in inflation \(\Delta \pi\). From the NKPC in steady state, the corresponding output gap change is \(\Delta x = \frac{1 - \beta}{\kappa} \Delta \pi\). Substituting into the Taylor rule gives the long-run nominal rate response: \[ \begin{aligned} \Delta i &= \phi_\pi \Delta \pi + \phi_y \Delta x = \left( \phi_\pi + \phi_y \frac{1 - \beta}{\kappa} \right) \Delta \pi \end{aligned} \] The real interest rate response is \(\Delta r = \Delta i - \Delta \pi = \left[ (\phi_\pi - 1) + \frac{1 - \beta}{\kappa}\phi_y \right] \Delta \pi\). Multiplying by \(\kappa > 0\) reveals that \(\Delta r / \Delta \pi > 0\) if and only if \(\kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1 - \beta)\phi_y > 0\), establishing equivalence.

Problem 7.7 (The Great Inflation Empirical Estimate). Clarida, Galí, and Gertler (2000) estimate \(\phi_\pi = 0.83\) and \(\phi_y = 0.27\) for the pre-Volcker era (1960–1979). Using \(\beta = 0.99\) and \(\kappa = 0.05\), evaluate whether the US economy was in the indeterminate regime.

Solution

Evaluate the generalized Taylor metric: \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1 - \beta)\phi_y &= 0.05(0.83 - 1.0) + (1 - 0.99)(0.27) \\ &= 0.05(-0.17) + 0.01(0.27) \\ &= -0.0085 + 0.0027 = -0.0058 < 0 \end{aligned} \] The metric is strictly negative, confirming that pre-Volcker monetary policy violated the Taylor principle, allowing self-fulfilling sunspot fluctuations that contributed to the high and volatile inflation of the 1970s.

Problem 7.8 (Post-Volcker Regime Shift). In the post-1982 period, empirical estimates find \(\phi_\pi = 2.15\) and \(\phi_y = 0.93\). Calculate the value of \(P(1)\) for \(\sigma = 1, \beta = 0.99, \kappa = 0.05\).

Solution

From Problem 7.2, \(P(1) = \frac{1}{\beta\sigma} [\kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1 - \beta)\phi_y]\). Substituting: \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1 - \beta)\phi_y &= 0.05(2.15 - 1.0) + 0.01(0.93) \\ &= 0.05(1.15) + 0.0093 = 0.0575 + 0.0093 = 0.0668 \\ P(1) &= \frac{0.0668}{0.99 \times 1.0} \approx 0.067475 > 0 \end{aligned} \] The policy aggressively satisfied the Taylor principle, anchoring expectations and guaranteeing a unique, determinate macroeconomic equilibrium.

Problem 7.9 (Interest Rate Smoothing and Determinacy). If the central bank uses an inertial policy rule \(i_t = \rho_i i_{t-1} + (1 - \rho_i)(\phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t)\), explain how the determinacy boundary in long-run response coefficients \((\phi_\pi, \phi_y)\) is affected by \(\rho_i\).

Solution

In the steady-state long-run relation, \(i = \rho_i i + (1 - \rho_i)(\phi_\pi \pi + \phi_y x) \implies i = \phi_\pi \pi + \phi_y x\). As demonstrated by Woodford (2003), the determinacy condition in terms of the long-run feedback coefficients \((\phi_\pi, \phi_y)\) remains identical to the static rule: \(\kappa(\phi_\pi - 1) + (1 - \beta)\phi_y > 0\) for any \(\rho_i \in [0, 1)\). Inertia alters short-run dynamics but does not relax the fundamental long-run real rate requirement.

Problem 7.10 (Forward-Looking Policy Rules). Suppose the central bank targets expected future inflation: \(i_t = \phi_\pi \E_t [\pi_{t+1}]\). Show that an upper bound on \(\phi_\pi\) emerges to prevent cyclical indeterminacy.

Solution

Substituting \(i_t = \phi_\pi \E_t [\pi_{t+1}]\) into the dynamic IS curve gives \(\E_t [x_{t+1}] = x_t + \frac{\phi_\pi - 1}{\sigma} \E_t [\pi_{t+1}]\). Combining with NKPC, the determinant of the forward companion matrix is \(\det(\mathbf{M}) = \frac{\sigma}{\beta\sigma + \kappa(\phi_\pi - 1)}\). If \(\phi_\pi\) is excessively large, \(\det(\mathbf{M}) < 1\), causing an eigenvalue to enter the unit circle via \(-1\). Thus, forward-looking rules require both a lower bound (\(\phi_\pi > 1\)) and an upper bound to avoid indeterminacy.

Problem 7.11 (Fiscal Theory of the Price Level Reversal). In Leeper's (1991) taxonomy of monetary-fiscal interactions, why does a Regime M (active monetary, passive fiscal) require \(\phi_\pi > 1\), while Regime F (passive monetary, active fiscal) requires \(\phi_\pi < 1\)?

Solution

The joint system contains one predetermined state variable (real government debt \(b_{t-1}\)) and two jump variables (\(x_t, \pi_t\)), requiring exactly two unstable eigenvalues and one stable eigenvalue. In Regime M, passive fiscal policy stabilizes debt (\(|\lambda_{debt}| < 1\)), requiring active monetary policy (\(\phi_\pi > 1\)) to supply two unstable roots. In Regime F, active fiscal policy makes debt explosive on its own, so monetary policy must be passive (\(\phi_\pi < 1\)) to provide a stable root that allows the price level to jump to satisfy the intertemporal government budget constraint.

Problem 7.12 (Cost Channel of Monetary Policy). Suppose firms must borrow at the nominal rate \(i_t\) to pay wage bills in advance, altering the NKPC to \(\pi_t = \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t + \psi i_t\) with \(\psi > 0\). Explain how this alters the determinacy threshold.

Solution

Under the working-capital cost channel, a policy rate hike directly increases firm marginal costs, pushing current inflation up. The real interest rate channel pushes aggregate demand down, creating opposing forces on inflation. Ravenstein and Schabert (2008) show that if \(\psi\) is sufficiently large, the cost channel can overturn determinacy, requiring a significantly higher \(\phi_\pi\) or rendering the standard Taylor principle insufficient to eliminate sunspots.
