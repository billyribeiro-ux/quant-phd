Estimating DSGE Models: State Space, Likelihood, and Identification

Calibrated parameters allow qualitative exploration, but empirical quantitative policy requires estimating structural primitives from macroeconomic data. State-space methods and the Kalman filter prediction error decomposition bridge theoretical DSGE models and empirical likelihood evaluation.

1. Structural State-Space Representation

Once a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium model is solved using undetermined coefficients or Jordan/QZ spectral decomposition, the policy functions and exogenous shock processes form a linear state-space model.

Definition 10.1 (DSGE State-Space System) For a parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^p\), the solved DSGE system is expressed as: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{s}_t &= \mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \mathbf{s}_{t-1} + \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t, \quad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})) \\ \mathbf{y}_t &= \mathbf{Z}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \mathbf{s}_t + \mathbf{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + \mathbf{v}_t, \quad \mathbf{v}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta})) \end{aligned} \] where \(\mathbf{s}_t \in \mathbb{R}^m\) is the unobserved state vector (capital stock, natural interest rates, technological productivity), \(\mathbf{y}_t \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the vector of observable macroeconomic time series (real GDP growth, inflation, policy rates), \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\) is the vector of fundamental structural innovations, and \(\mathbf{v}_t\) is measurement error.

DSGE model estimation refers to the econometric process of estimating structural preference, technology, and policy parameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) by maximizing the sample likelihood or calculating the posterior distribution conditional on observed time series data.

2. Kalman Filter Prediction Error Decomposition

Because the state vector \(\mathbf{s}_t\) is latent, evaluating the sample likelihood requires integrating out unobserved states. When shocks and measurement errors follow a multivariate normal distribution, the sequential Kalman filter computes the exact likelihood recursively.

Theorem 10.2 (Prediction Error Likelihood Decomposition) Given observations \(\mathbf{y}_{1:T} = \{\mathbf{y}_1, \dots, \mathbf{y}_T\}\), the exact Gaussian log-likelihood evaluates to: \[ \begin{aligned} \ln \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:T}) &= -\frac{Tn}{2} \ln(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{t=1}^T \left( \ln|\mathbf{F}_t(\boldsymbol{\theta})| + \boldsymbol{\nu}_t(\boldsymbol{\theta})^\top \mathbf{F}_t(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{-1} \boldsymbol{\nu}_t(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \right) \end{aligned} \] where \(\boldsymbol{\nu}_t \equiv \mathbf{y}_t - \E[\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}] = \mathbf{y}_t - \mathbf{Z} \mathbf{s}_{t|t-1} - \mathbf{d}\) is the one-step-ahead forecast innovation, and \(\mathbf{F}_t \equiv \operatorname{Var}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}) = \mathbf{Z} \mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} \mathbf{Z}^\top + \mathbf{H}\) is its conditional covariance matrix.

Proof. By the chain rule of conditional probability: \[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{y}_{1:T} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \prod_{t=1}^T p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \end{aligned} \] Under linear Gaussian assumptions, \(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t|t-1}, \mathbf{F}_t)\). Taking logarithms of the multivariate normal density: \[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) &= -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\ln|\mathbf{F}_t| - \frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{y}_t - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t|t-1})^\top \mathbf{F}_t^{-1} (\mathbf{y}_t - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t|t-1}) \end{aligned} \] Summing over \(t = 1, \dots, T\) yields the prediction error decomposition.

Remark 10.1 (Equivalence to Joint Multivariate Normal Density) For a stationary state-space model, the observation vector \(\mathbf{Y} = [\mathbf{y}_1^\top, \dots, \mathbf{y}_T^\top]^\top\) has a block Toeplitz covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_Y\). The recursive Kalman log-likelihood is mathematically identical to the direct Cholesky log-density \(-\frac{1}{2}[Tn\ln(2\pi) + \ln|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_Y| + \mathbf{Y}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_Y^{-1} \mathbf{Y}]\), but requires \(\mathcal{O}(T m^3)\) operations rather than \(\mathcal{O}(T^3 n^3)\).

3. Estimation: Maximum Likelihood and Bayesian MCMC

Parameters can be estimated via classical maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or modern Bayesian inference.

Definition 10.3 (Bayesian DSGE Posterior Kernel) Combining the likelihood with prior density \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\), the posterior distribution satisfies: \[ \begin{aligned} p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:T}) &\propto \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:T}) \, p(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \end{aligned} \] Priors regularize weakly identified structural parameters: Beta distributions for bounded parameters \(\beta, \rho \in [0, 1)\), Gamma distributions for positive parameters \(\sigma, \kappa > 0\), and Inverse-Gamma distributions for shock innovation standard deviations \(\sigma_\varepsilon > 0\).

Example 10.4 (Scalar State-Space Likelihood Evaluation) Consider a scalar AR(1) state \(s_t = \rho s_{t-1} + w_t\) with observation \(y_t = s_t + v_t\), where \(\sigma_w = 0.5, \sigma_v = 0.3, \rho = 0.7\).
The stationary initial state variance is \(P_{1|0} = \frac{\sigma_w^2}{1 - \rho^2} = \frac{0.25}{1 - 0.49} = \frac{0.25}{0.51} \approx 0.490196\).
The initial forecast variance is \(F_1 = P_{1|0} + \sigma_v^2 = 0.490196 + 0.09 = 0.580196\).
For \(y_1 = 0.42\), the innovation is \(\nu_1 = 0.42\), giving period 1 log-likelihood contribution: \[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(y_1) &= -0.5 \left[ \ln(2\pi) + \ln(0.580196) + \frac{0.42^2}{0.580196} \right] \\ &= -0.5 [1.837877 - 0.544389 + 0.304035] = -0.5 [1.597523] \approx -0.798762 \end{aligned} \]

4. Computational Lab: Exact Kalman Filter Likelihood

The lab below simulates a stationary scalar state-space process (SIMULATED data, seed 42) and computes the exact log-likelihood using the sequential Kalman prediction-error decomposition. It asserts exact numerical agreement against the full \(T \times T\) joint multivariate normal Toeplitz density to floating-point precision.

Lab 1 · Kalman Filter Log-Likelihood vs Toeplitz Density

The sequential Kalman filter prediction-error log-likelihood matches the direct full joint multivariate Gaussian log-density with Toeplitz covariance within double-precision floating-point roundoff. Simulated data, seed 42.

"""Lab E6-L10-1: Exact Gaussian likelihood via the Kalman filter vs direct multivariate density.

Simulates a stationary scalar AR(1) state-space observation sequence (SIMULATED data, seed 42).
Computes the exact log-likelihood using the sequential Kalman prediction-error decomposition.
Compares the result against the full joint multivariate normal log-density with Toeplitz
covariance, verifying exact numerical agreement to floating-point precision.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def kalman_filter_loglik(y: np.ndarray, rho: float, sigma_w: float, sigma_v: float) -> float:
    """Compute exact log-likelihood via Kalman recursion."""
    # Stationary prior variance: P_{1|0} = sigma_w^2 / (1 - rho^2)
    p_prior = (sigma_w**2) / (1.0 - rho**2)
    s_prior = 0.0
    loglik = 0.0

    for yt in y:
        nu = yt - s_prior
        f_val = p_prior + sigma_v**2
        loglik += -0.5 * (np.log(2.0 * np.pi) + np.log(f_val) + (nu**2) / f_val)

        # Update step
        k_gain = p_prior / f_val
        s_post = s_prior + k_gain * nu
        p_post = (1.0 - k_gain) * p_prior

        # Predict step for next period
        s_prior = rho * s_post
        p_prior = (rho**2) * p_post + sigma_w**2

    return float(loglik)


def direct_gaussian_loglik(y: np.ndarray, rho: float, sigma_w: float, sigma_v: float) -> float:
    """Compute exact log-likelihood via full T x T Toeplitz covariance matrix."""
    t_len = len(y)
    cov_y = np.zeros((t_len, t_len), dtype=np.float64)
    gamma_0 = (sigma_w**2) / (1.0 - rho**2)

    for i in range(t_len):
        for j in range(t_len):
            k = abs(i - j)
            cov_y[i, j] = gamma_0 * (rho**k) + (sigma_v**2 if k == 0 else 0.0)

    chol = np.linalg.cholesky(cov_y)
    log_det = 2.0 * np.sum(np.log(np.diag(chol)))
    sol_y = np.linalg.solve(chol, y)
    quad_form = np.sum(sol_y**2)
    loglik = -0.5 * (t_len * np.log(2.0 * np.pi) + log_det + quad_form)
    return float(loglik)


def main() -> None:
    # Seeded SIMULATED data generation (not market data)
    seed = 42
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    t_len = 40
    rho = 0.7
    sigma_w = 0.5
    sigma_v = 0.3

    s = np.zeros(t_len)
    w = rng.normal(0, sigma_w, t_len)
    v = rng.normal(0, sigma_v, t_len)
    s_prev = rng.normal(0, sigma_w / np.sqrt(1.0 - rho**2))
    for t in range(t_len):
        s[t] = rho * s_prev + w[t]
        s_prev = s[t]
    y = s + v

    loglik_kalman = kalman_filter_loglik(y, rho, sigma_w, sigma_v)
    loglik_direct = direct_gaussian_loglik(y, rho, sigma_w, sigma_v)
    discrepancy = abs(loglik_kalman - loglik_direct)

    print(f"DSGE State-Space Likelihood Evaluation (SIMULATED path, seed={seed}, T={t_len}):")
    print(f"Parameters: rho={rho}, sigma_w={sigma_w}, sigma_v={sigma_v}")
    print(f"  Kalman filter log-likelihood:       {loglik_kalman:14.8f}")
    print(f"  Direct multivariate log-likelihood: {loglik_direct:14.8f}")
    print(f"  Absolute discrepancy:               {discrepancy:14.2e}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

While state-space DSGE estimation provides a rigorous structural econometric framework, several fundamental challenges arise in empirical applications:

  • The Stochastic Singularity Problem: If the econometrician uses more observable time series \(N\) than the number of structural economic shocks \(k\) (\(N > k\)), the model-implied covariance matrix of observables has rank \(k < N\), causing the likelihood to collapse to zero unless ad-hoc measurement errors are appended.
  • Weak Identification and Likelihood Flatness: In New Keynesian models, distinct structural parameters frequently enter in product form (e.g., \(\kappa = \frac{(1-\theta)(1-\beta\theta)}{\theta} \Theta\)). Consequently, the likelihood surface possesses flat ridges, making individual parameters (such as the Calvo price duration \(\theta\)) weakly identified.
  • Priors Dominating Likelihoods: When parameters are weakly identified, the posterior distribution reflects the researcher's subjective prior distribution rather than information contained in historical macroeconomic data.

Misconceptions

  1. The Kalman filter can only be applied to models where all variables are observed directly without measurement noise.

    The Kalman filter is explicitly designed for partially observed state-space systems: it optimally infers latent state variables (e.g., the natural real rate \(r^n_t\) or potential output) from noisy and incomplete observable data.

  2. Bayesian DSGE estimation eliminates the problem of parameter identification.

    Bayesian methods produce a well-behaved posterior even when parameters are unidentified because the prior acts as an informative penalty; however, this creates a false sense of precision when the data contain no identifying information.

  3. Maximizing the Kalman filter likelihood guarantees that the estimated DSGE model will forecast better than an unrestricted VAR.

    Because DSGE models impose tight cross-equation theoretical restrictions, their out-of-sample forecast accuracy is frequently inferior to flexible unrestricted or Bayesian VARs (BVARs), as documented by Smets and Wouters (2007).

Problem Set

Problem 10.1 (Stationary Prior Variance). Given a scalar state process \(s_t = \rho s_{t-1} + w_t\) with \(w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_w^2)\) and \(|\rho| < 1\), derive the unconditional steady-state variance \(P_{1|0}\).

Solution

In steady state, the unconditional variance satisfies \(\operatorname{Var}(s_t) = \operatorname{Var}(\rho s_{t-1} + w_t) = \rho^2 \operatorname{Var}(s_{t-1}) + \sigma_w^2\). Stationarity implies \(\operatorname{Var}(s_t) = \operatorname{Var}(s_{t-1}) = P_{1|0}\). Solving: \[ \begin{aligned} P_{1|0} = \rho^2 P_{1|0} + \sigma_w^2 \implies P_{1|0}(1 - \rho^2) = \sigma_w^2 \implies P_{1|0} = \frac{\sigma_w^2}{1 - \rho^2} \end{aligned} \] This initial variance initializes the stationary Kalman recursion.

Problem 10.2 (Steady-State Kalman Gain). In the scalar state-space model \(s_t = \rho s_{t-1} + w_t\) and \(y_t = s_t + v_t\), write the algebraic Riccati equation for the steady-state prior variance \(P_\infty\) and steady-state Kalman gain \(K_\infty\).

Solution

The updating steps are \(F = P + \sigma_v^2\), \(K = P / F\), and \(P_{\mathrm{post}} = (1 - K)P = \frac{P \sigma_v^2}{P + \sigma_v^2}\). The prediction step gives \(P = \rho^2 P_{\mathrm{post}} + \sigma_w^2\). In steady state: \[ \begin{aligned} P_\infty &= \frac{\rho^2 P_\infty \sigma_v^2}{P_\infty + \sigma_v^2} + \sigma_w^2 \end{aligned} \] Multiplying by \(P_\infty + \sigma_v^2\) yields the quadratic algebraic Riccati equation: \[ \begin{aligned} P_\infty^2 + [(1 - \rho^2)\sigma_v^2 - \sigma_w^2] P_\infty - \sigma_w^2 \sigma_v^2 &= 0 \end{aligned} \] The steady-state gain is then \(K_\infty = \frac{P_\infty}{P_\infty + \sigma_v^2}\).

Problem 10.3 (Computational Complexity Comparison). Compare the computational floating-point operation count (FLOPs) of the Kalman filter versus direct multivariate normal density inversion for a sample of \(T = 200\) observations with state dimension \(m = 5\) and observable dimension \(n = 3\).

Solution

For the Kalman filter, each step requires matrix inversions and multiplications of dimension at most \(m \times m\) and \(n \times n\), yielding approximately \(\mathcal{O}(T(m^3 + n^3))\) operations. With \(T = 200, m = 5, n = 3\): \(200 \times (125 + 27) \approx 30,400\text{ FLOPs}\). In contrast, direct density evaluation constructs a \(Tn \times Tn = 600 \times 600\) covariance matrix and performs a Cholesky factorization requiring \(\frac{1}{3}(Tn)^3 = \frac{1}{3}(600)^3 = 72,000,000\text{ FLOPs}\). The Kalman filter achieves a speedup of over \(2,300\times\).

Problem 10.4 (Prediction Error Orthogonality). Prove that the Kalman filter innovations \(\boldsymbol{\nu}_t = \mathbf{y}_t - \E[\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}]\) form a martingale difference sequence, so that \(\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_t, \boldsymbol{\nu}_s) = \mathbf{0}\) for all \(t \ne s\).

Solution

Let \(s < t\). By the law of iterated expectations: \[ \begin{aligned} \E [\boldsymbol{\nu}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:s}] &= \E [ \E [\boldsymbol{\nu}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}] \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:s} ] \end{aligned} \] By definition of the innovation, \(\E[\boldsymbol{\nu}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}] = \E[\mathbf{y}_t - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t|t-1} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}] = \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t|t-1} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t|t-1} = \mathbf{0}\). Thus \(\E[\boldsymbol{\nu}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:s}] = \mathbf{0}\). Since \(\boldsymbol{\nu}_s\) is a measurable function of \(\mathbf{y}_{1:s}\), the cross-covariance is: \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_t, \boldsymbol{\nu}_s) &= \E [ \boldsymbol{\nu}_t \boldsymbol{\nu}_s^\top ] = \E [ \E [ \boldsymbol{\nu}_t \boldsymbol{\nu}_s^\top \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:s} ] ] = \E [ \E[\boldsymbol{\nu}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:s}] \boldsymbol{\nu}_s^\top ] = \mathbf{0} \end{aligned} \] The innovations are mutually orthogonal, validating the exact log-likelihood sum.

Problem 10.5 (Stochastic Singularity Proof). Prove that if the number of observable variables \(n\) strictly exceeds the rank \(k\) of the shock innovation covariance matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) in a system without measurement error (\(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{0}\)), the one-step forecast covariance matrix \(\mathbf{F}_t\) is singular.

Solution

With \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{0}\), \(\mathbf{F}_t = \mathbf{Z} \mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} \mathbf{Z}^\top\). The prior state covariance satisfies \(\mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} = \mathbf{T} \mathbf{P}_{t-1|t-1} \mathbf{T}^\top + \mathbf{R} \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R}^\top\). By Sylvester's rank inequality, \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}^\top) \le \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Q}) = k\). Since all economic dynamics are driven by these \(k\) independent shocks, the stationary state distribution has rank at most \(k\). Therefore: \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{F}_t) &= \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Z} \mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} \mathbf{Z}^\top) \le \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{P}_{t|t-1}) \le k \end{aligned} \] Since \(k < n\), the \(n \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{F}_t\) has rank at most \(k < n\), so \(\det(\mathbf{F}_t) = 0\). The Gaussian density requires \(\mathbf{F}_t^{-1}\), which does not exist, proving stochastic singularity.

Problem 10.6 (Information Matrix Equivalence). In the neighborhood of the true parameter \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\), prove that the negative expected Hessian of the log-likelihood equals the Fisher information matrix \(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) = \E [ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ln \mathcal{L} \, (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ln \mathcal{L})^\top ]\).

Solution

Differentiating the identity \(\int p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \mathrm{d}\mathbf{y} = 1\) twice with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\): \[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \int p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \mathrm{d}\mathbf{y} &= \int \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ln p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \, p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \mathrm{d}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0} \end{aligned} \] Differentiating a second time: \[ \begin{aligned} \int \left[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^2 \ln p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \, p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ln p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}))^\top \right] \mathrm{d}\mathbf{y} &= \mathbf{0} \end{aligned} \] Substituting \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} p = p \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ln p\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \E [ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^2 \ln \mathcal{L} ] + \E [ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ln \mathcal{L} (\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ln \mathcal{L})^\top ] &= \mathbf{0} \implies -\E [ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^2 \ln \mathcal{L} ] = \mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) \end{aligned} \] which establishes the information equality.

Problem 10.7 (Smets-Wouters Model Observable Choice). In the influential Smets and Wouters (2007) medium-scale DSGE model, exactly 7 observable time series are matched to 7 structural economic shocks. Explain why matching the number of shocks to observables is necessary.

Solution

To avoid stochastic singularity without adding arbitrary measurement errors, the number of structural shocks \(k\) must be at least as large as the number of observables \(n\). Smets and Wouters selected exactly \(n = 7\) macroeconomic time series (real GDP growth, consumption growth, investment growth, real wage growth, inflation, hours worked, and federal funds rate) and introduced exactly \(k = 7\) structural orthogonal shocks (total factor productivity, investment-specific technology, risk premium, government spending, price markup, wage markup, and monetary policy shocks), ensuring non-singular likelihood evaluation.

Problem 10.8 (Calvo Duration Identification Ridge). Explain why the Calvo price stickiness parameter \(\theta\) and the strategic complementarity parameter \(\Theta\) are jointly weakly identified from aggregate inflation and output gap data.

Solution

In the New Keynesian Phillips curve \(\pi_t = \beta \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t\), the observable macro data identify only the composite slope parameter \(\kappa\). The theoretical mapping is \(\kappa = \frac{(1 - \theta)(1 - \beta\theta)}{\theta} \Theta\). An economy with high price stickiness (\(\theta = 0.8\)) and high strategic complementarity (\(\Theta = 0.1\)) yields the exact same slope \(\kappa\) as an economy with moderate price stickiness (\(\theta = 0.5\)) and low strategic complementarity (\(\Theta = 0.5\)). Consequently, \(\theta\) and \(\Theta\) lie along a flat likelihood ridge and cannot be disentangled without microeconomic price-change frequency data.

Problem 10.9 (Metropolis-Hastings Acceptance Rate Tuning). In Bayesian DSGE estimation using the random walk Metropolis-Hastings (RWMH) algorithm, what is the target acceptance rate for proposal draws and why?

Solution

Gelman, Roberts, and Gilks (1996) prove that for high-dimensional multivariate Gaussian target distributions, the optimal acceptance rate that balances exploration of parameter space against proposal rejection is approximately 23.4% (typically calibrated between 20% and 35%). Researchers scale the covariance matrix of the proposal distribution \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{prop}} = c^2 \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{MLE}}\) by tuning the scalar \(c\) until the empirical acceptance rate falls within this optimal range.

Problem 10.10 (Particle Filter for Non-Linear DSGE Models). When a DSGE model is solved to second-order perturbation or features occasionally binding ZLB constraints, explain why the standard Kalman filter fails and how the particle filter evaluates the likelihood.

Solution

Non-linear transition equations \(\mathbf{s}_t = g(\mathbf{s}_{t-1}, \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t)\) generate non-Gaussian conditional state distributions, violating the linear Kalman recursion. The particle filter (Sequential Monte Carlo) represents the latent filtering density \(p(\mathbf{s}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t})\) using a cloud of \(N_p\) weighted particles \(\{\mathbf{s}_t^{(i)}, w_t^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_p}\). At each period, particles are propagated through the non-linear state equations, weighted by observation likelihood \(w_t^{(i)} \propto p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t^{(i)})\), and resampled to prevent particle degeneracy, computing an unbiased estimate of the likelihood.

Problem 10.11 (Iskrev Identification Rank Criterion). Outline the Iskrev (2010) rank condition for local identification of DSGE models from first and second moments.

Solution

Iskrev shows that if the model is locally identified from second moments, the Jacobian matrix \(\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{\partial \operatorname{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_Y(0), \dots, \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_Y(q))}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top}\) of observable autocovariances with respect to structural parameters must have full column rank equal to the dimension of \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\). If \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{J}) < p\), the model is unidentifiable, and the null space of \(\mathbf{J}\) identifies the exact directions of parameter collinearity.

Problem 10.12 (Invertibility and Non-Fundamental Shocks). Explain the Fernández-Villaverde, Rubio-Ramírez, Sargent, and Watson (2007) condition for state-space invertibility when estimating structural shocks from VARs.

Solution

A DSGE model has an invertible VAR representation if the structural shocks \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\) can be recovered from current and past observable history \(\mathbf{y}^t\). In the state-space system, this requires that the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mathbf{T} - \mathbf{K}_\infty \mathbf{Z}\) lie strictly inside the unit circle, where \(\mathbf{K}_\infty\) is the steady-state Kalman gain. If any eigenvalue exceeds unity, the model is non-invertible, and shocks recovered from an empirical VAR do not coincide with the true structural innovations.
