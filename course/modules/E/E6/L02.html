Log-Linearization

Nonlinear dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models cannot be solved in closed form away from steady state. First-order log-linearization around the deterministic steady state transforms complex nonlinear forward-looking differential and difference equations into tractable linear rational-expectations systems, preserving local dynamics with quadratic error decay.

1. Principles of Log-Deviation Approximations

In the study of dynamic macroeconomic models, such as the real business cycle model, the equilibrium conditions comprise nonlinear stochastic difference equations. Log-linearization provides a systematic method for approximating these equations by replacing each variable with its percentage (or log) deviation from its deterministic steady state.

Let \(X_t\) be a strictly positive macroeconomic variable with deterministic steady-state value \(X^* > 0\). The log-deviation \(\hat{x}_t\) is defined via the natural logarithm using exponential and logarithm functions:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{x}_t &\equiv \ln \left( \frac{X_t}{X^*} \right) = \ln X_t - \ln X^* \end{aligned} \]

Equivalently, \(X_t = X^* e^{\hat{x}_t}\). Expanding \(e^{\hat{x}_t}\) in a first-order Taylor series around \(\hat{x}_t = 0\) by Taylor's theorem yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} X_t &= X^* \left( 1 + \hat{x}_t + \mathcal{O}(\hat{x}_t^2) \right) \approx X^* (1 + \hat{x}_t) \end{aligned} \]

which confirms that \(\hat{x}_t \approx (X_t - X^*) / X^*\) is approximately the relative fractional deviation of \(X_t\) from steady state.

2. Algebraic Rules for Log-Linear Systems

Applying the first-order derivative to products, powers, and sums yields straightforward algebraic rules:

Multiplication and Division: If \(Z_t = X_t^\alpha Y_t^\gamma\), then taking logs gives \(\ln Z_t = \alpha \ln X_t + \gamma \ln Y_t\). Evaluating at steady state \(\ln Z^* = \alpha \ln X^* + \gamma \ln Y^*\) and subtracting yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{z}_t &= \alpha \hat{x}_t + \gamma \hat{y}_t \end{aligned} \]

Linear Sums: If \(Z_t = X_t + Y_t\), write \(Z^* e^{\hat{z}_t} = X^* e^{\hat{x}_t} + Y^* e^{\hat{y}_t}\). Approximating \(e^u \approx 1 + u\) gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} Z^* (1 + \hat{z}_t) &\approx X^* (1 + \hat{x}_t) + Y^* (1 + \hat{y}_t) \end{aligned} \]

Since \(Z^* = X^* + Y^*\), subtracting steady states and dividing by \(Z^*\) yields the share-weighted sum:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{z}_t &\approx s_x \hat{x}_t + s_y \hat{y}_t \end{aligned} \]

where \(s_x = X^* / Z^*\) and \(s_y = Y^* / Z^*\) with \(s_x + s_y = 1\).

3. Log-Linearizing the Intertemporal Euler Equation

Consider the stochastic consumption Euler equation derived in Lesson 1, where expectations are conditioned on date \(t\) information via \(\E_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} C_t^{-\sigma} &= \beta \E_t \left[ C_{t+1}^{-\sigma} (1 + R_{t+1}) \right] \end{aligned} \]

where \(1 + R_{t+1} \equiv 1 + r_{t+1} - \delta\) is the gross real return on capital. In steady state, \(\beta (1 + R^*) = 1\).

Theorem 2.1 (Log-Linear Consumption Euler Equation) Under assumptions:
1. Stationary steady state with \(\beta (1 + R^*) = 1\),
2. Bounded log deviations \(\|\hat{c}_t\|, \|\hat{c}_{t+1}\|, \|\hat{r}_{t+1}\| < \epsilon\),
3. Definition of real interest rate log deviation \(\hat{r}_{t+1} \equiv \ln(1 + R_{t+1}) - \ln(1 + R^*)\),
the first-order log-linear Euler equation is: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{c}_t &= \E_t [\hat{c}_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \hat{r}_{t+1} \end{aligned} \] with an approximation error of order \(\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)\).

Proof. Write the exact nonlinear Euler equation in terms of log deviations from steady state: \[ \begin{aligned} (c^*)^{-\sigma} e^{-\sigma \hat{c}_t} &= \beta \E_t \left[ (c^*)^{-\sigma} e^{-\sigma \hat{c}_{t+1}} (1 + R^*) e^{\hat{r}_{t+1}} \right] \end{aligned} \] Canceling \((c^*)^{-\sigma}\) and using \(\beta (1 + R^*) = 1\): \[ \begin{aligned} e^{-\sigma \hat{c}_t} &= \E_t \left[ e^{\hat{r}_{t+1} - \sigma \hat{c}_{t+1}} \right] \end{aligned} \] Expanding both sides via first-order Taylor series around zero: \[ \begin{aligned} 1 - \sigma \hat{c}_t + \mathcal{O}(\hat{c}_t^2) &= \E_t \left[ 1 + \hat{r}_{t+1} - \sigma \hat{c}_{t+1} + \mathcal{O}(\|\hat{r}_{t+1} - \sigma \hat{c}_{t+1}\|^2) \right] \end{aligned} \] Subtracting 1 and taking conditional expectations: \[ \begin{aligned} -\sigma \hat{c}_t &= \hat{r}_{t+1} - \sigma \E_t [\hat{c}_{t+1}] + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2) \end{aligned} \] Dividing by \(-\sigma\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{c}_t &= \E_t [\hat{c}_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \hat{r}_{t+1} + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2) \end{aligned} \] which completes the derivation.

Foundational treatments of log-linear rational-expectations systems are established in Woodford and Clarida, Galí & Gertler (1999).

4. Approximation Accuracy and Taylor Remainder Bounds

By Taylor's theorem, for any twice continuously differentiable function \(f(x)\), the remainder after a first-order expansion around \(x^*\) is given by \(R_1(x) = \frac{1}{2} f''(\xi) (x - x^*)^2\) for some \(\xi\) between \(x\) and \(x^*\). Therefore, when the deviation size \(\epsilon\) is halved, the approximation error shrinks by a factor of approximately \(2^2 = 4\).

Lab 1 · Verifying Quadratic Error Decay in Log-Linearization

As the perturbation size is halved iteratively (from 0.10 to 0.05, 0.025, and 0.0125), the approximation error of the log-linearized resource constraint and return equation decays by a ratio of approximately 4.00, confirming quadratic Taylor remainder convergence. Model output, no data.

"""Lab E6-L02-1: Log-linearization and Taylor approximation error.

Compares the exact nonlinear aggregate resource constraint and the exact nonlinear Euler equation
with their first-order log-linearizations across geometric refinements of deviation size.
Verifies that the approximation error decays quadratically (ratio ~4 when step size halves),
confirming Taylor's remainder theorem.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def evaluate_resource_error(c_hat: float, s_c: float, s_i: float) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Compute the exact vs log-linearized output gap for a given consumption perturbation."""
    # Choose i_hat such that linear output gap is zero to isolate residual
    i_hat = -c_hat * (s_c / s_i)
    linear_y_hat = s_c * c_hat + s_i * i_hat  # exactly 0.0

    # Exact nonlinear expenditure ratio: Y / Y* = s_c * exp(c_hat) + s_i * exp(i_hat)
    expenditure_ratio = s_c * math.exp(c_hat) + s_i * math.exp(i_hat)
    exact_y_hat = math.log(expenditure_ratio)

    error = abs(exact_y_hat - linear_y_hat)
    return linear_y_hat, exact_y_hat, error


def evaluate_euler_log_error(r_deviation: float) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Compute the error in the log-linear real return approximation ln(1 + r) ~= r."""
    linear_r = r_deviation
    exact_log_r = math.log(1.0 + r_deviation)
    error = abs(exact_log_r - linear_r)
    return linear_r, exact_log_r, error


def main() -> None:
    s_c = 0.75
    s_i = 0.25
    steps = [0.10, 0.05, 0.025, 0.0125]

    print("Part 1: Log-linear aggregate resource constraint (s_c=0.75, s_i=0.25):")
    res_errors: list[float] = []
    for eps in steps:
        _, exact_val, err = evaluate_resource_error(eps, s_c, s_i)
        res_errors.append(err)
        print(f"  c_hat = {eps:7.4f}: exact y_hat = {exact_val:10.6e}, error = {err:10.6e}")

    print("\nError reduction ratios for resource constraint (target 4.00):")
    for i in range(len(res_errors) - 1):
        ratio = res_errors[i] / res_errors[i + 1]
        print(f"  ratio {steps[i]:.4f} -> {steps[i + 1]:.4f}: {ratio:.2f}")

    print("\nPart 2: Return log-approximation ln(1 + r) ~= r:")
    return_errors: list[float] = []
    for eps in steps:
        _, exact_val, err = evaluate_euler_log_error(eps)
        return_errors.append(err)
        print(f"  r = {eps:7.4f}: exact ln(1+r) = {exact_val:10.6e}, error = {err:10.6e}")

    print("\nError reduction ratios for return log-approximation (target 4.00):")
    for i in range(len(return_errors) - 1):
        ratio = return_errors[i] / return_errors[i + 1]
        print(f"  ratio {steps[i]:.4f} -> {steps[i + 1]:.4f}: {ratio:.2f}")

Where the Canon Breaks

Log-linearization is a strictly local approximation method around a specific steady state. Its validity breaks down in several key regimes:

  • Large shocks and disaster risks: For substantial perturbations (such as financial crises or sharp pandemics where output falls by 10% or more), second-order and higher curvature terms generate significant welfare and allocation errors.
  • Zero Lower Bound (ZLB) and asymmetries: The non-negativity constraint on nominal interest rates creates a sharp kink in policy reaction functions that cannot be captured by linear approximations without piecewise boundary methods.
  • Precautionary savings and risk premia: First-order approximations enforce certainty equivalence (\(\E_t[f(X_{t+1})] \approx f(\E_t[X_{t+1}])\)), eliminating the effects of second moments on policy functions. Asset pricing risk premia and precautionary wealth accumulation require at least second-order perturbation methods.

Misconceptions

  1. Log-linearization provides exact solutions for any linear combinations of variables.

    Log-linearization is only exact for multiplicative functions of powers. Linear sums \(Z_t = X_t + Y_t\) are not linear in logarithms and incur a nonzero second-order approximation error proportional to \(\frac{1}{2} s_x s_y (\hat{x}_t - \hat{y}_t)^2\).

  2. First-order log-linearized models can predict changes in risk premia across business cycles.

    First-order perturbation methods exhibit certainty equivalence: covariance and variance terms drop out of the policy functions, so agents behave as if future shocks had zero variance. Asset pricing premia require second-order or higher expansions.

  3. The choice of steady state does not affect the coefficients of the log-linearized model.

    The coefficients in share-weighted linearizations depend directly on the steady-state ratios \(s_c = c^*/y^*\) and \(s_i = i^*/y^*\), which vary with deep structural parameters like \(\beta, \alpha\), and \(\delta\).

Problem Set

Problem 2.1 (Log-Linear Cobb-Douglas). Log-linearize the Cobb-Douglas production function \(Y_t = A_t K_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}\) around steady state \((A^*, K^*, L^*)\).

Solution

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides: \[ \begin{aligned} \ln Y_t &= \ln A_t + \alpha \ln K_t + (1 - \alpha) \ln L_t \end{aligned} \] In steady state, \(\ln Y^* = \ln A^* + \alpha \ln K^* + (1 - \alpha) \ln L^*\). Subtracting the steady-state equation from the dynamic equation: \[ \begin{aligned} (\ln Y_t - \ln Y^*) &= (\ln A_t - \ln A^*) + \alpha (\ln K_t - \ln K^*) + (1 - \alpha) (\ln L_t - \ln L^*) \end{aligned} \] Using the log-deviation notation \(\hat{x}_t \equiv \ln(X_t / X^*)\): \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_t &= \hat{a}_t + \alpha \hat{k}_t + (1 - \alpha) \hat{l}_t \end{aligned} \] This expression is exact in logs without approximation error because the Cobb-Douglas function is log-additive.

Problem 2.2 (Log-Linear Capital Accumulation). Log-linearize the capital law of motion \(K_{t+1} = (1 - \delta) K_t + I_t\) around steady state.

Solution

In steady state, \(I^* = \delta K^*\), so \(K^* = (1 - \delta) K^* + I^*\). Express variables in terms of log deviations: \[ \begin{aligned} K^* e^{\hat{k}_{t+1}} &= (1 - \delta) K^* e^{\hat{k}_t} + I^* e^{\hat{i}_t} \end{aligned} \] Expanding \(e^u \approx 1 + u\): \[ \begin{aligned} K^* (1 + \hat{k}_{t+1}) &\approx (1 - \delta) K^* (1 + \hat{k}_t) + I^* (1 + \hat{i}_t) \end{aligned} \] Subtracting the steady-state relation \(K^* = (1 - \delta)K^* + I^*\) and dividing by \(K^*\): \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{k}_{t+1} &= (1 - \delta) \hat{k}_t + \frac{I^*}{K^*} \hat{i}_t = (1 - \delta) \hat{k}_t + \delta \hat{i}_t \end{aligned} \] Capital next period is a weighted average of current capital and investment with weights \(1 - \delta\) and \(\delta\).

Problem 2.3 (Log-Linear Resource Constraint). If steady-state consumption is 75% of output and investment is 25%, write the log-linearized aggregate resource constraint \(Y_t = C_t + I_t\).

Solution

Using the linear sum rule \(\hat{z}_t = s_x \hat{x}_t + s_y \hat{y}_t\): \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_t &= s_c \hat{c}_t + s_i \hat{i}_t = 0.75 \hat{c}_t + 0.25 \hat{i}_t \end{aligned} \] A 1% increase in consumption contributes 0.75% to output growth, while a 1% increase in investment contributes 0.25%.

Problem 2.4 (Second-Order Remainder for Linear Sum). Prove that the second-order Taylor remainder for the log-linearization of \(Z = X + Y\) around \((X^*, Y^*)\) is \(R_2 = \frac{1}{2} s_x s_y (\hat{x} - \hat{y})^2\).

Solution

Define \(g(\hat{x}, \hat{y}) = \ln(s_x e^{\hat{x}} + s_y e^{\hat{y}})\), where \(s_x + s_y = 1\). Note that \(g(0, 0) = \ln(1) = 0\). The first partial derivatives are: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial g}{\partial \hat{x}} &= \frac{s_x e^{\hat{x}}}{s_x e^{\hat{x}} + s_y e^{\hat{y}}} \implies \frac{\partial g}{\partial \hat{x}}(0, 0) = s_x \\ \frac{\partial g}{\partial \hat{y}} &= \frac{s_y e^{\hat{y}}}{s_x e^{\hat{x}} + s_y e^{\hat{y}}} \implies \frac{\partial g}{\partial \hat{y}}(0, 0) = s_y \end{aligned} \] The second partial derivatives evaluated at \((0, 0)\) are: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2 g}{\partial \hat{x}^2}(0, 0) &= s_x (1 - s_x) = s_x s_y \\ \frac{\partial^2 g}{\partial \hat{y}^2}(0, 0) &= s_y (1 - s_y) = s_x s_y \\ \frac{\partial^2 g}{\partial \hat{x} \partial \hat{y}}(0, 0) &= -s_x s_y \end{aligned} \] The quadratic form in the second-order Taylor remainder is: \[ \begin{aligned} R_2 &= \frac{1}{2} \left[ s_x s_y \hat{x}^2 - 2 s_x s_y \hat{x} \hat{y} + s_x s_y \hat{y}^2 \right] = \frac{1}{2} s_x s_y (\hat{x} - \hat{y})^2 \end{aligned} \] This confirms that the remainder is strictly non-negative and vanishes only when \(\hat{x} = \hat{y}\).

Problem 2.5 (Log-Linear Intratemporal FOC). Prove that log-linearizing the intratemporal labor-leisure condition \(\chi L_t^\eta = C_t^{-\sigma} w_t\) yields \(\eta \hat{l}_t = \hat{w}_t - \sigma \hat{c}_t\).

Solution

Take the natural logarithm of both sides: \[ \begin{aligned} \ln \chi + \eta \ln L_t &= -\sigma \ln C_t + \ln w_t \end{aligned} \] In steady state, \(\ln \chi + \eta \ln L^* = -\sigma \ln c^* + \ln w^*\). Subtracting the steady-state equation: \[ \begin{aligned} \eta (\ln L_t - \ln L^*) &= -\sigma (\ln C_t - \ln c^*) + (\ln w_t - \ln w^*) \end{aligned} \] Substituting the log deviations gives \(\eta \hat{l}_t = \hat{w}_t - \sigma \hat{c}_t\). Rearranging for labor supply yields \(\hat{l}_t = \frac{1}{\eta} (\hat{w}_t - \sigma \hat{c}_t)\), confirming that labor supply responds positively to real wages with Frisch elasticity \(1/\eta\) and negatively to consumption through wealth effects.

Problem 2.6 (Jensen's Inequality Gap in Euler Equation). Show why log-linearization ignores the Jensen's inequality term \(\frac{1}{2} \Var_t(\hat{c}_{t+1})\) in expected utility.

Solution

Suppose \(\hat{c}_{t+1}\) is conditionally normally distributed: \(\hat{c}_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\E_t[\hat{c}_{t+1}], \sigma_c^2)\). Then \(e^{-\sigma \hat{c}_{t+1}}\) is log-normal. By the properties of the log-normal distribution: \[ \begin{aligned} \E_t \left[ e^{-\sigma \hat{c}_{t+1}} \right] &= \exp \left( -\sigma \E_t[\hat{c}_{t+1}] + \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \sigma_c^2 \right) \end{aligned} \] Taking logarithms: \[ \begin{aligned} \ln \E_t \left[ e^{-\sigma \hat{c}_{t+1}} \right] &= -\sigma \E_t[\hat{c}_{t+1}] + \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \sigma_c^2 \end{aligned} \] First-order log-linearization sets \(\ln \E_t[e^{-\sigma \hat{c}_{t+1}}] \approx -\sigma \E_t[\hat{c}_{t+1}]\), explicitly dropping the precautionary variance term \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2} \sigma_c^2\), which is of second order in volatility.

Problem 2.7 (Evaluating Approximation Error Magnitude). If consumption drops by 5% (\(\hat{c} = -0.05\)) and investment drops by 15% (\(\hat{i} = -0.15\)), with shares \(s_c = 0.75, s_i = 0.25\), calculate the exact percentage error between the linear approximation and the exact nonlinear output drop.

Solution

The linear approximation is \(\hat{y}_{\text{lin}} = 0.75(-0.05) + 0.25(-0.15) = -0.0375 - 0.0375 = -0.075\) (-7.50%). The exact nonlinear ratio is: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{Y}{Y^*} &= 0.75 e^{-0.05} + 0.25 e^{-0.15} \approx 0.75(0.951229) + 0.25(0.860708) \\ &= 0.713422 + 0.215177 = 0.928599 \end{aligned} \] The exact log deviation is \(\hat{y}_{\text{exact}} = \ln(0.928599) \approx -0.074078\) (-7.41%). The absolute approximation error is: \[ \begin{aligned} |\hat{y}_{\text{exact}} - \hat{y}_{\text{lin}}| &= |-0.074078 - (-0.075000)| = 0.000922 \end{aligned} \] The error is approximately 0.09 percentage points (less than one-tenth of one percent), verifying high accuracy for standard macroeconomic fluctuations.

Problem 2.8 (Return Deviation Conversion). A quarterly nominal policy rate moves from 4.0% annualized to 5.0% annualized. If steady-state gross return is \(1 + R^* = 1.01\) per quarter, compute the quarterly return log deviation \(\hat{r}\).

Solution

A 5.0% annualized rate corresponds to a quarterly net rate of \(R \approx 0.05 / 4 = 0.0125\). The steady-state quarterly rate is \(R^* = 0.04 / 4 = 0.0100\). The log return deviation is: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{r} &= \ln \left( \frac{1 + R}{1 + R^*} \right) = \ln \left( \frac{1.0125}{1.0100} \right) = \ln(1.002475) \approx 0.002472 \end{aligned} \] The quarterly deviation is approximately 0.247% (24.7 basis points).

Problem 2.9 (Log Approximation Error vs Simple Percentage). Compare \(\ln(1 + x)\) and \(x\) for an asset return shock of \(x = 0.20\) (20%). What is the absolute discrepancy?

Solution

For \(x = 0.20\): \[ \begin{aligned} \ln(1 + 0.20) &= \ln(1.20) \approx 0.182322 \end{aligned} \] The discrepancy between the simple percentage change and the exact log deviation is \(|0.20 - 0.182322| = 0.017678\) (approximately 1.77 percentage points). For large percentage movements, simple linear deviations deviate significantly from log differences.

Problem 2.10 (Perturbation Order and State-Dependent Volatility). Explain why a first-order perturbation of a DSGE model cannot generate time-varying impulse response functions conditional on the level of economic uncertainty.

Solution

In a first-order approximation, the policy function for states \(S_t\) is linear: \(X_t = \mathbf{P} S_t + \mathbf{Q} \varepsilon_t\). The matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) determines the impulse response \(\partial X_{t+k} / \partial \varepsilon_t = \mathbf{P}^k \mathbf{Q}\), which is constant and completely independent of the volatility of \(\varepsilon_t\) or the sign and magnitude of the state \(S_t\). To capture state-dependent shock transmission, at least second-order terms \(\frac{1}{2} S_t^\top \mathbf{H} S_t\) or stochastic volatility interactions are required.

Problem 2.11 (Accuracy under Non-Stationary Trends). In models with permanent technological growth (\(A_{t+1} / A_t = \gamma_A > 1\)), explain the necessary transformation required before log-linearization can be applied.

Solution

Taylor expansions require stationary reference points. With permanent growth, \(Y_t, C_t, K_t\) grow without bound, so no fixed steady state exists in levels. The system must first be detrended: variables are scaled by the technological trend (e.g., \(c_t \equiv C_t / A_t^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\)). Log-linearization is then performed on the stationary detrended variables \(\hat{c}_t = \ln(c_t / c^*)\) around the balanced growth path steady state.

Problem 2.12 (Pruning in Higher-Order Perturbations). Why do higher-order (second and third-order) approximations sometimes introduce explosive spurious sample paths, and how does the Kim-Kim-Schaumburg-Sims (2008) pruning algorithm resolve this?

Solution

Second-order perturbation systems contain quadratic terms of the state \(S_t^2\). If a shock pushes \(S_t\) far from steady state, the quadratic term acts as a positive feedback mechanism that can overwhelm the stable linear roots, driving the simulated path to infinity. Pruning decomposes the state into first-order and second-order components (\(S_t = S_t^{(1)} + S_t^{(2)}\)), evaluating the quadratic terms strictly on the stable first-order state \(S_t^{(1)}\), which prevents spurious non-linear explosive paths.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the log-linearization of the product relation \(Z_t = X_t^\alpha Y_t^\beta\)?

    Options

    Taking logarithms converts powers into coefficients and multiplication into addition, which is exact in logs.

  2. How does the first-order approximation error change when the deviation size \(\epsilon\) is cut in half?

    Options

    By Taylor's theorem, the leading omitted remainder is quadratic \(\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)\), so halving \(\epsilon\) reduces error by \(2^2 = 4\).

  3. What property of first-order perturbation approximations eliminates precautionary savings?

    Options

    In first-order perturbations, conditional variance terms drop out of decision rules, enforcing certainty equivalence.

  4. What theoretical factor does the approximation error decrease by when the perturbation size is halved?

    Because the remainder is \(\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)\), \((1/2)^2 = 1/4\), meaning error shrinks by a factor of 4.

  5. In the log-linearized Euler equation \(\hat{c}_t = \E_t[\hat{c}_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma}\hat{r}_{t+1}\), what does \(1/\sigma\) represent?

    Options

    \(1/\sigma\) measures the responsiveness of consumption growth to the real interest rate.

  6. For a linear sum \(Z_t = X_t + Y_t\), how are the coefficients of \(\hat{x}_t\) and \(\hat{y}_t\) determined in \(\hat{z}_t\)?

    Options

    Linear sums are weighted by their respective steady-state shares \(s_x\) and \(s_y\), which sum to one.

  7. Why does log-linearization fail when evaluating the Zero Lower Bound (ZLB)?

    Options

    Linearization assumes smooth differentiability everywhere and cannot capture the piecewise non-differentiable kink at \(i_t \ge 0\).

  8. If \(Y_t = C_t + I_t\) and in steady state \(C^* = 75\) and \(I^* = 25\), what is the weight \(s_i\) on \(\hat{i}_t\)?

    \(s_i = I^* / Y^* = 25 / 100 = 0.25\).
