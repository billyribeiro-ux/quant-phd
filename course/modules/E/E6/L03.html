The Dynamic IS Curve

In modern general equilibrium macroeconomics, aggregate demand is forward-looking. The dynamic IS curve replaces the traditional static IS schedule by demonstrating that the current output gap is governed by expected future output and the cumulative sum of anticipated real interest rate deviations from the natural rate.

1. Microfoundations of Aggregate Demand

In the canonical cashless New Keynesian economy, investment and government purchases are abstracted away, so all output is consumed: \(Y_t = C_t\). Under this goods market clearing condition, the log-linearized consumption Euler equation derived in Lesson 2 directly describes the evolution of aggregate output:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_t &= \E_t [\hat{y}_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} (i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^*) \end{aligned} \]

where \(i_t\) is the nominal policy rate set by the monetary authority, \(\pi_{t+1} \equiv \ln(P_{t+1} / P_t)\) is the net inflation rate, and \(\E_t\) denotes rational expectations conditioned on date \(t\) information. The ex-ante real interest rate is given by the Fisher relationship \(r_t = i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}]\), and \(r^* = 1/\beta - 1\) is the steady-state real interest rate.

To analyze stabilization policy, macroeconomists define the natural level of output \(y^n_t\) as the equilibrium output that would obtain under perfectly flexible prices. The output gap \(x_t\) is defined as the deviation of actual output from its natural level:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_t &\equiv \hat{y}_t - y^n_t \end{aligned} \]

The natural rate of interest \(r^n_t\) is the real interest rate consistent with output remaining at its natural level in every period: \(y^n_t = \E_t [y^n_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma}(r^n_t - r^*)\).

2. Deriving the Dynamic IS Curve

Subtracting the natural-rate equilibrium condition from the actual aggregate demand equation yields the structural equation governing output gap dynamics.

Theorem 3.1 (The Dynamic IS Curve) Under assumptions:
1. Goods market clearing \(Y_t = C_t\),
2. Log-linear consumption Euler equation with CRRA risk aversion \(\sigma > 0\),
3. Definition of the output gap \(x_t \equiv \hat{y}_t - y^n_t\) and natural real rate \(r^n_t\),
the dynamic IS curve is given by: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= \E_t [x_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \left( i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t \right) \end{aligned} \] where \(r^n_t\) acts as an exogenous aggregate demand disturbance.

Proof. Begin with the log-linear Euler equation: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_t &= \E_t [\hat{y}_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} (i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^*) \end{aligned} \] By definition of the natural rate of interest, under flexible prices \(x_t = 0\) for all \(t\), which requires: \[ \begin{aligned} y^n_t &= \E_t [y^n_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} (r^n_t - r^*) \end{aligned} \] Subtracting the second equation from the first yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_t - y^n_t &= \E_t [\hat{y}_{t+1} - y^n_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \left[ (i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^*) - (r^n_t - r^*) \right] \end{aligned} \] Substituting \(x_t = \hat{y}_t - y^n_t\) and \(x_{t+1} = \hat{y}_{t+1} - y^n_{t+1}\): \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= \E_t [x_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \left( i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t \right) \end{aligned} \] which establishes the proposition.

This formulation is established in Galí (2015) and Woodford.

3. The Forward-Looking Solution

The presence of the expectation term \(\E_t[x_{t+1}]\) makes the dynamic IS schedule fundamentally different from the static IS curve of undergraduate macroeconomics. By repeated forward substitution, the current output gap is shown to depend on the entire expected future path of monetary policy.

Proposition 3.2 (Forward Sum of Real Interest Rate Gaps) Imposing the terminal transversality condition \(\lim_{H \to \infty} \E_t [x_{t+H}] = 0\), the dynamic IS curve can be solved forward as: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \E_t \left[ i_{t+j} - \pi_{t+j+1} - r^n_{t+j} \right] \end{aligned} \] The current output gap is proportional to the negative cumulative sum of all future expected real interest rate deviations from the natural rate.

Proof. Advance the dynamic IS curve forward by one period: \[ \begin{aligned} x_{t+1} &= \E_{t+1} [x_{t+2}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \hat{r}_{t+1} \end{aligned} \] where \(\hat{r}_t \equiv i_t - \E_t [\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t\). Taking expectations at date \(t\) and using the law of iterated expectations: \[ \begin{aligned} \E_t [x_{t+1}] &= \E_t [x_{t+2}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \E_t [\hat{r}_{t+1}] \end{aligned} \] Substituting this into the date \(t\) equation yields: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} \hat{r}_t - \frac{1}{\sigma} \E_t [\hat{r}_{t+1}] + \E_t [x_{t+2}] \end{aligned} \] Iterating \(H\) periods forward: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} \sum_{j=0}^{H-1} \E_t [\hat{r}_{t+j}] + \E_t [x_{t+H}] \end{aligned} \] Taking the limit as \(H \to \infty\) with \(\lim_{H \to \infty} \E_t [x_{t+H}] = 0\) completes the proof.

4. Truncation Error under AR(1) Dynamics

When the ex-ante real interest rate gap follows an AR(1) process \(\E_t [\hat{r}_{t+j}] = \rho^j \hat{r}_t\) with persistence \(|\rho| < 1\), the infinite forward sum converges to the closed form:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_t^* &= -\frac{1}{\sigma (1 - \rho)} \hat{r}_t \end{aligned} \]

A finite forward sum truncated at horizon \(H\) incurs a truncation error equal to \(-\frac{\rho^H}{\sigma(1 - \rho)}\hat{r}_t\), which decays exponentially with the forecast horizon.

Lab 1 · Forward Recursion and Truncation Error of the Dynamic IS Curve

Truncating the forward sum of the dynamic IS curve at horizons H in {4, 8, 16, 32} produces numerical truncation errors that match the theoretical formula to machine precision and decay geometrically as rho^H. Model output, no data.

"""Lab E6-L03-1: Forward recursion and truncation error of the dynamic IS curve.

Solves the dynamic IS equation forward under an AR(1) real interest rate gap process.
Compares finite forward truncations at horizons H in {4, 8, 16, 32} against the exact
infinite-horizon closed form, demonstrating that truncation error decays geometrically as rho^H.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def solve_dynamic_is_truncated(sigma: float, rho: float, r_gap_0: float, horizon: int) -> float:
    """Compute the finite-horizon truncated forward sum for the output gap."""
    return float(-(1.0 / sigma) * sum((rho**j) * r_gap_0 for j in range(horizon)))


def solve_dynamic_is_exact(sigma: float, rho: float, r_gap_0: float) -> float:
    """Compute the exact infinite-horizon closed-form output gap: -r_gap_0 / (sigma * (1 - rho))."""
    return float(-r_gap_0 / (sigma * (1.0 - rho)))


def main() -> None:
    sigma = 1.0  # Log utility / unit intertemporal elasticity of substitution
    rho = 0.7  # Real interest rate gap persistence
    r_gap_0 = 0.02  # Initial 200 basis point ex-ante real interest rate tightening

    x_exact = solve_dynamic_is_exact(sigma, rho, r_gap_0)

    print(f"Dynamic IS forward solution parameters (sigma={sigma}, rho={rho}, r_gap={r_gap_0}):")
    print(f"  Exact infinite-horizon output gap x*: {x_exact:.6f}")
    print("\nForward horizon truncation analysis:")

    horizons = [4, 8, 16, 32]
    for h in horizons:
        x_trunc = solve_dynamic_is_truncated(sigma, rho, r_gap_0, h)
        num_error = abs(x_exact - x_trunc)
        th_error = abs((rho**h) * r_gap_0 / (sigma * (1.0 - rho)))
        diff = abs(num_error - th_error)

        print(
            f"  H = {h:2d}: x_trunc = {x_trunc:9.6f}, "
            f"error = {num_error:10.6e}, "
            f"theoretical = {th_error:10.6e}, "
            f"discrepancy = {diff:.2e}"
        )

Where the Canon Breaks

The canonical forward-looking dynamic IS curve is an elegant baseline, but exhibits key empirical and theoretical breakdowns:

  • The forward guidance puzzle: Because all future real rate gaps enter with equal weight \(1/\sigma\), an announcement of an interest rate change far in the future (say, in 20 quarters) has an enormous, implausibly large impact on current output, which is not observed empirically.
  • Absence of habit persistence: Under standard time-separable CRRA utility, output responds immediately and jumps sharply to interest rate shocks. In reality, output exhibits a hump-shaped, delayed response due to consumption habit persistence and capital adjustment costs.
  • Omission of credit and balance-sheet frictions: The representative-agent assumption implies frictionless borrowing and lending at the riskless policy rate, ignoring mortgage credit spreads, collateral constraints, and default risk.

Misconceptions

  1. The dynamic IS curve implies that only the current policy rate determines aggregate output.

    Because the equation solves forward, the current output gap is determined by the entire expected future trajectory of real interest rates. A central bank that changes expectations of future policy can shift output today without changing the current policy rate.

  2. The natural rate of interest \(r^n_t\) is a constant parameter fixed by technology.

    The natural rate of interest varies dynamically over time in response to technology shocks, fiscal policy shifts, and household preference disturbances.

  3. A positive output gap \(x_t > 0\) always requires an expansionary nominal interest rate cut.

    If the natural rate of interest \(r^n_t\) increases sharply (e.g., due to an investment boom), maintaining the nominal rate unchanged is expansionary because \(r_t < r^n_t\), creating a positive output gap without any rate cut.

Problem Set

Problem 3.1 (Calculating the Output Gap Impact). If \(\sigma = 1.0\), the persistence of real rate shocks is \(\rho = 0.5\), and the central bank raises the real rate gap \(\hat{r}_t = i_t - \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t\) by 100 basis points (0.01), compute the immediate impact on the output gap \(x_t\).

Solution

Using the closed-form forward solution: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma (1 - \rho)} \hat{r}_t = -\frac{1}{1.0 \times (1 - 0.5)} \times 0.01 = -\frac{0.01}{0.5} = -0.02 \end{aligned} \] The output gap contracts by 2.0% (200 basis points).

Problem 3.2 (Multiplier of Persistence). How does the sensitivity of the output gap to a real interest rate shock change if persistence \(\rho\) rises from 0.5 to 0.8?

Solution

The response multiplier is \(M(\rho) = 1 / (\sigma(1 - \rho))\). For \(\sigma = 1\): \[ \begin{aligned} M(0.5) &= \frac{1}{1 - 0.5} = 2.0 \\ M(0.8) &= \frac{1}{1 - 0.8} = 5.0 \end{aligned} \] The impact multiplier increases from 2.0 to 5.0 (a 2.5-fold increase), because households anticipate that the interest rate tightening will remain in place much longer, compounding the cumulative intertemporal substitution effect.

Problem 3.3 (Natural Rate Shock). Suppose the natural real rate increases temporarily by \(r^n_t = 0.015\) (150 bps) with persistence \(\rho = 0.6\). If the central bank holds the nominal rate and inflation constant, what is the output gap response?

Solution

Holding \(i_t - \E_t[\pi_{t+1}]\) constant implies the real interest rate gap is \(\hat{r}_t = -r^n_t = -0.015\). For \(\sigma = 1\): \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma (1 - \rho)} (-r^n_t) = \frac{1}{1.0 \times (1 - 0.6)} \times 0.015 = \frac{0.015}{0.4} = 0.0375 \end{aligned} \] The output gap expands by 3.75% because monetary policy failed to track the higher natural rate.

Problem 3.4 (Forward Guidance Equivalence). Prove that an announced 100 bps one-period interest rate cut scheduled for date \(t+k\) has the exact same impact on current output gap \(x_t\) as an immediate 100 bps cut at date \(t\), under frictionless dynamic IS dynamics.

Solution

From Proposition 3.2: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \E_t [\hat{r}_{t+j}] \end{aligned} \] A one-period cut of magnitude \(\Delta r\) at date \(t\) contributes \(-\frac{1}{\sigma} \Delta r\) to the sum. An announced one-period cut of the same magnitude at date \(t+k\) also contributes \(-\frac{1}{\sigma} \Delta r\) to the infinite sum, because there is no discounting in the forward sum of the dynamic IS curve. Thus, \(\partial x_t / \partial \hat{r}_{t+k} = -1/\sigma\) for all \(k \ge 0\). This proves the forward guidance equivalence and illustrates the theoretical origin of the forward guidance puzzle.

Problem 3.5 (Discounted Dynamic IS Curve). Suppose a fraction of households are finite-horizon or myopic, yielding the discounted dynamic IS curve \(x_t = \gamma \E_t[x_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma} \hat{r}_t\) with \(\gamma \in (0, 1)\). Prove that the impact of a date \(t+k\) rate cut decays geometrically with horizon \(k\).

Solution

Iterating forward: \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} \hat{r}_t + \gamma \E_t [x_{t+1}] = -\frac{1}{\sigma} \sum_{j=0}^{H-1} \gamma^j \E_t [\hat{r}_{t+j}] + \gamma^H \E_t [x_{t+H}] \end{aligned} \] As \(H \to \infty\), \(x_t = -\frac{1}{\sigma} \sum_{j=0}^\infty \gamma^j \E_t [\hat{r}_{t+j}]\). The marginal impact of an expected rate shock at horizon \(k\) is: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial x_t}{\partial \E_t [\hat{r}_{t+k}]} &= -\frac{1}{\sigma} \gamma^k \end{aligned} \] Since \(\gamma < 1\), \(\lim_{k \to \infty} \gamma^k = 0\), resolving the forward guidance puzzle by damping the influence of distant future policy announcements.

Problem 3.6 (Habit Persistence and Output Sluggishness). Let utility exhibit external habit persistence \(U(C_t, C_{t-1}) = \frac{(C_t - h C_{t-1})^{1-\sigma} - 1}{1 - \sigma}\). Prove that the log-linearized Euler equation contains lagged consumption \(\hat{c}_{t-1}\).

Solution

Marginal utility of consumption is \(\lambda_t = (C_t - h C_{t-1})^{-\sigma}\). In steady state, \(\lambda^* = (1 - h)^{-\sigma} (c^*)^{-\sigma}\). Log-linearizing \(\lambda_t\) around steady state: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\lambda}_t &= -\frac{\sigma}{1 - h} (\hat{c}_t - h \hat{c}_{t-1}) \end{aligned} \] The Euler equation requires \(\hat{\lambda}_t = \E_t [\hat{\lambda}_{t+1}] + \hat{r}_{t+1}\). Substituting \(\hat{\lambda}_t\): \[ \begin{aligned} -\frac{\sigma}{1 - h} (\hat{c}_t - h \hat{c}_{t-1}) &= -\frac{\sigma}{1 - h} (\E_t [\hat{c}_{t+1}] - h \hat{c}_t) + \hat{r}_{t+1} \end{aligned} \] Rearranging for \(\hat{c}_t\): \[ \begin{aligned} (1 + h) \hat{c}_t &= h \hat{c}_{t-1} + \E_t [\hat{c}_{t+1}] - \frac{1 - h}{\sigma} \hat{r}_{t+1} \end{aligned} \] Lagged consumption enters with coefficient \(h / (1 + h) > 0\), generating endogenous persistence and sluggish adjustment.

Problem 3.7 (Estimating the Natural Rate of Interest). If Laubach-Williams Kalman filter estimates suggest that the US natural real rate \(r^n_t\) declined from 2.5% in the 1990s to 0.5% in the 2010s, calculate the change in the steady-state nominal policy rate for a 2% inflation target.

Solution

In steady state, the Fisher equation gives \(i^* = r^* + \pi^*\). In the 1990s: \(i^* = 2.5\% + 2.0\% = 4.5\%\). In the 2010s: \(i^* = 0.5\% + 2.0\% = 2.5\%\). The steady-state neutral nominal policy rate declined by 200 basis points (from 4.5% to 2.5%), significantly reducing the central bank's policy buffer above the zero lower bound.

Problem 3.8 (Truncation Horizon for Central Bank Projections). A central bank macroeconomic model projects policy rates over a 3-year horizon (12 quarters). For persistence \(\rho = 0.7\), what percentage of the total infinite-horizon cumulative interest rate response is captured within the 12-quarter projection window?

Solution

The finite sum is \(S_{12} = \sum_{j=0}^{11} \rho^j = \frac{1 - \rho^{12}}{1 - \rho}\), while the infinite sum is \(S_\infty = \frac{1}{1 - \rho}\). The fraction captured is: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{S_{12}}{S_\infty} &= 1 - \rho^{12} = 1 - (0.7)^{12} \approx 1 - 0.01384 = 0.98616 \end{aligned} \] Approximately 98.62% of the cumulative intertemporal demand response is captured within the 12-quarter horizon.

Problem 3.9 (Empirical Intertemporal Elasticity of Substitution). Macroeconomic calibration studies frequently assume \(\sigma = 1\), whereas microeconomic estimates of the elasticity of substitution \(1/\sigma\) based on asset market participation often find values closer to 0.2 (\(\sigma = 5\)). If \(\sigma = 5\) and \(\rho = 0.6\), calculate the revised output gap response to a 100 bps rate shock.

Solution

With \(\sigma = 5\): \[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= -\frac{1}{\sigma (1 - \rho)} \hat{r}_t = -\frac{1}{5.0 \times (1 - 0.6)} \times 0.01 = -\frac{0.01}{2.0} = -0.005 \end{aligned} \] The output response is -0.50% (50 basis points), one-fifth of the response predicted under log utility (\(\sigma = 1\)).

Problem 3.10 (Open Economy IS Adjustments). In an open economy with international risk sharing, explain how the dynamic IS curve is modified by foreign output \(y_t^*\) and the real exchange rate.

Solution

In an open economy, domestic output equals domestic consumption plus net exports: \(Y_t = C_t + NX_t\). Under complete international financial markets, the consumption ratio across countries is linked to the real exchange rate \(Q_t\) via the risk-sharing condition \(C_t = C_t^* Q_t^{1/\sigma}\). The open-economy dynamic IS curve becomes \(x_t = \E_t[x_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma_\alpha}(i_t - \E_t[\pi_{t+1}] - r^n_t) + \alpha \Delta \E_t[y_{t+1}^*]\), where \(\sigma_\alpha\) reflects trade openness and real exchange rate expenditure switching.

Problem 3.11 (Heterogeneous Agents and IS Slope). Explain why heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian (HANK) models predict that the slope of the dynamic IS curve with respect to the current policy rate is steeper than in the representative-agent baseline.

Solution

In RANK models, all households smooth consumption across time via the intertemporal Euler equation. In HANK models, a substantial fraction of households are hand-to-mouth (HtM) or liquidity-constrained with marginal propensities to consume (MPCs) near 1. An interest rate cut increases aggregate demand, raising labor income; HtM households immediately spend this additional income, generating a potent indirect Keynesian multiplier that steepens the aggregate IS response.

Problem 3.12 (Fiscal Policy in the Dynamic IS Curve). Extend the dynamic IS curve to include government purchases \(G_t\) funded by lump-sum taxation, and derive the balanced-budget fiscal multiplier when the real rate is held constant.

Solution

With government spending, market clearing is \(Y_t = C_t + G_t\). In log deviations around steady state: \(\hat{y}_t = (1 - s_g) \hat{c}_t + \hat{g}_t\), where \(s_g = G^* / Y^*\) and \(\hat{g}_t \equiv (G_t - G^*) / Y^*\). Substituting the Euler equation \(\hat{c}_t = \E_t[\hat{c}_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma}\hat{r}_t\): \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_t &= \E_t [\hat{y}_{t+1}] - \frac{1 - s_g}{\sigma} \hat{r}_t + (\hat{g}_t - \E_t [\hat{g}_{t+1}]) \end{aligned} \] Holding the real rate gap constant (\(\hat{r}_t = 0\)), if government spending is purely temporary (\(\E_t[\hat{g}_{t+1}] = 0\)), the contemporaneous fiscal multiplier is \(\partial \hat{y}_t / \partial \hat{g}_t = 1.0\).

Retrieval Quiz

  1. How does the dynamic IS curve differ from the traditional Keynesian static IS curve?

    Options

    The dynamic IS curve is derived from the household's intertemporal Euler equation, making current demand depend on expected future output and the anticipated interest rate path.

  2. What is the economic definition of the natural rate of interest \(r^n_t\)?

    Options

    The natural rate is the equilibrium real interest rate that would prevail in the absence of nominal rigidities, keeping output at potential.

  3. Calculate the output gap \(x_t\) when \(\sigma = 1\), \(\rho = 0.5\), and the real rate gap is \(\hat{r}_t = 0.01\).

    \(x_t = -0.01 / (1.0 \times (1 - 0.5)) = -0.02\).

  4. What does the "forward guidance puzzle" refer to in the context of the dynamic IS curve?

    Options

    Because the forward sum has no discounting on future interest rates, an announcement about rates far in the future shifts current output with undiminished strength.

  5. If the real interest rate equals the natural rate of interest (\(r_t = r^n_t\)) at all dates, what path does the output gap follow?

    Options

    When the real rate gap is zero at all dates, the forward sum is identically zero, so output exactly equals potential.

  6. How does external habit persistence modify the dynamic IS curve?

    Options

    Habit persistence links current marginal utility to past consumption, adding lagged output to the dynamic IS relation.

  7. For \(\sigma = 1\) and persistence \(\rho = 0.8\), what is the response multiplier \(1 / (\sigma(1 - \rho))\)?

    \(1 / (1.0 \times (1 - 0.8)) = 1 / 0.2 = 5.0\).

  8. What is the terminal condition used to solve the dynamic IS curve forward to an infinite sum?

    Options

    The no-bubble / transversality condition ensures that the expected output gap returns to steady state at distant horizons.
