Growth Accounting and the Solow Residual

  • Uses: national accounts and GDP (Lesson 1), and exponential and logarithm functions (M1)
  • Introduces: growth accounting; total factor productivity
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Growth accounting splits the growth of output into a part explained by growth in capital and labor and a part that is left over. This lesson states the Cobb-Douglas production function, derives the exact log-growth decomposition of its output, and defines the residual that the decomposition leaves. It shows that the residual changes with the chosen capital share whenever average capital and labour growth differ, so the residual cannot be read off the measured series alone. The curriculum lab runs growth accounting on a generated economy labeled SIMULATED, because no redistributable national-accounts series is embedded in this program yet.

1The Cobb-Douglas production function and log growth

Definition 1.1 (Cobb-Douglas production)

A production function maps the inputs of an economy to its output. The Cobb-Douglas form uses a positive level \(A\) of productivity, a positive capital stock \(K\), a positive labor input \(L\) and a parameter \(\alpha\) with \(0 < \alpha < 1\). Output is

\[ Y = A\, K^{\alpha}\, L^{1 - \alpha}. \]

The parameter \(\alpha\) is the exponent on capital. This lesson treats \(\alpha\) as an input. It is not derived here from factor prices, and no claim is made about its value in any economy.

Definition 1.2 (log growth and simple growth)

For a positive series \(x_t\), the log growth in period \(t\) is \(\Delta \ln x_t = \ln x_t - \ln x_{t-1}\), and the simple growth is \(x_t / x_{t-1} - 1\). The growth figures in this lesson state which of the two they are. Log growth is the convention for the decomposition in Theorem 1.4.

Assumption 1.3 (a Cobb-Douglas economy over a horizon)

Periods run from 0 to a horizon \(H\). In every period \(t\), output is \(Y_t = A_t K_t^{\alpha} L_t^{1 - \alpha}\) with \(A_t\), \(K_t\) and \(L_t\) positive and the same \(\alpha\) with \(0 < \alpha < 1\) throughout.

Theorem 1.4 (the exact log-growth decomposition)

Under Assumption 1.3, for every \(t\) from 1 to \(H\),

\[ \Delta \ln Y_t = \Delta \ln A_t + \alpha\, \Delta \ln K_t + (1 - \alpha)\, \Delta \ln L_t. \]

Proof

Take logarithms of Definition 1.1. The logarithm of a product of positive numbers is the sum of their logarithms, and \(\ln(x^{c}) = c \ln x\) for a positive \(x\) and a real \(c\). So \(\ln Y_t = \ln A_t + \alpha \ln K_t + (1 - \alpha) \ln L_t\) for each \(t\). Subtract the same identity for \(t - 1\), which uses the same \(\alpha\) by Assumption 1.3. The difference of each side is the log growth of that variable, which gives the displayed equation. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.5 (constant returns to scale)

Assume (A1) \(A > 0\), \(K > 0\), \(L > 0\), \(0 < \alpha < 1\) and \(\lambda > 0\). Then the Cobb-Douglas output satisfies \(A (\lambda K)^{\alpha} (\lambda L)^{1 - \alpha} = \lambda\, A K^{\alpha} L^{1 - \alpha}\). Doubling both inputs therefore doubles output.

Proof

For positive \(\lambda\), \((\lambda K)^{\alpha} = \lambda^{\alpha} K^{\alpha}\) and \((\lambda L)^{1 - \alpha} = \lambda^{1 - \alpha} L^{1 - \alpha}\). The product of the two powers of \(\lambda\) is \(\lambda^{\alpha + 1 - \alpha} = \lambda\). Multiplying by \(A\) gives the displayed identity. With \(\lambda = 2\) the output doubles. \(\blacksquare\)

2Growth accounting and the residual

Definition 2.1 (growth accounting)

Growth accounting takes measured series \(Y_t\), \(K_t\) and \(L_t\) and a chosen capital share \(\alpha\) in \((0, 1)\). It computes, for each period, the capital contribution \(\alpha\, \Delta \ln K_t\), the labor contribution \((1 - \alpha)\, \Delta \ln L_t\), and the residual

\[ \mathrm{Res}_t(\alpha) = \Delta \ln Y_t - \alpha\, \Delta \ln K_t - (1 - \alpha)\, \Delta \ln L_t. \]

The residual is the part of log output growth that the chosen inputs and the chosen \(\alpha\) do not account for.

Definition 2.2 (the Solow residual)

The residual of Definition 2.1 is called the Solow residual. The name records how the quantity is computed, and this lesson uses it with no attribution to any paper. Total factor productivity, TFP, is the level \(A\) of Definition 1.1, and its log growth is the quantity that the residual is meant to measure. The next theorem says that the two are equal when \(\alpha\) is the economy's own share.

Theorem 2.3 (the residual is TFP growth, given the technology)

Under Assumption 1.3, the residual of Definition 2.1 computed with the \(\alpha\) of the economy satisfies \(\mathrm{Res}_t(\alpha) = \Delta \ln A_t\) for every \(t\) from 1 to \(H\).

Proof

Rearrange Theorem 1.4: \(\Delta \ln A_t = \Delta \ln Y_t - \alpha \Delta \ln K_t - (1 - \alpha) \Delta \ln L_t\), which is the definition of \(\mathrm{Res}_t(\alpha)\) with the same \(\alpha\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 2.3 needs the \(\alpha\) of the economy in general. It also holds for every \(\alpha\) when capital and labour grow at the same rate in every period, because then the residual does not depend on \(\alpha\) (Theorem 1.4 and Problem P2). In a measured economy the true \(\alpha\) and the true \(A_t\) are not observed, so the residual must be computed with a chosen \(\alpha\). Proposition 2.4 shows what that choice does.

Proposition 2.4 (the residual depends on the chosen capital share)

Let \(\alpha\) and \(\alpha'\) both lie in \((0, 1)\). For every period \(t\),

\[ \mathrm{Res}_t(\alpha) - \mathrm{Res}_t(\alpha') = -(\alpha - \alpha')\, \bigl(\Delta \ln K_t - \Delta \ln L_t\bigr). \]

Averaging over \(t\), the average residual changes by \(-(\alpha - \alpha')\) times the average of \(\Delta \ln K_t - \Delta \ln L_t\). Given \(\alpha \ne \alpha'\), the two average residuals coincide if and only if the average growth of capital equals the average growth of labor.

Proof

Expand Definition 2.1: \(\mathrm{Res}_t(\alpha) = \Delta \ln Y_t - \Delta \ln L_t - \alpha (\Delta \ln K_t - \Delta \ln L_t)\), because \((1 - \alpha) \Delta \ln L_t = \Delta \ln L_t - \alpha \Delta \ln L_t\). Subtracting the same expression with \(\alpha'\) cancels the first two terms and leaves \(-(\alpha - \alpha')(\Delta \ln K_t - \Delta \ln L_t)\). Averaging the identity over \(t\) is linear, so it passes to the averages. \(\blacksquare\)

The consequence is that the decomposition is exact for every \(\alpha\), and the residual is a different number for different values of \(\alpha\) whenever the average growth of capital and of labour differ (Proposition 2.4). The residual is therefore a function of the accounting choice, and Lab 1 shows the size of that dependence on one economy.

Lab 1 · Growth accounting on a seeded SIMULATED economy

Predicted before running. The economy is SIMULATED: levels of \(A\), \(K\) and \(L\) are generated from a seeded random generator over 40 periods with capital share \(\alpha = 0.3\), and output is computed from the levels. Theorem 2.3 predicts that the residual equals the generated growth of \(A\) in every period, so the largest gap between them is zero to floating-point precision, and the check line reports True. The three shares of average output growth add to 1 by the definition of the residual. """E5 L02 lab 1: growth accounting on a seeded SIMULATED Cobb-Douglas economy.

SIMULATED. The economy is generated from random.Random(2026) with uniform draws only, so every run prints the same
lines. Its levels are illustrative and are not national-accounts data. No redistributable national-accounts series is
embedded in this program yet, so the lesson's curriculum lab runs on this generated economy and says so on the page.

The economy has output Y_t = A_t * K_t**ALPHA * L_t**(1 - ALPHA) for periods t = 0 to HORIZON, with capital share
ALPHA = 0.3. Output is computed from the levels. The growth of Y is then measured from the Y levels, and the three-term
growth-accounting identity is checked against the generated growth of A.

Logs are natural logs. Growth rates are log growth, ln x_t - ln x_(t-1), unless a line says simple. Each printed number
is shown to six decimal places; the comparisons use the unrounded floats.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random

ALPHA = 0.3
HORIZON = 40
SEED = 2026


def simulate_economy(seed: int, horizon: int) -> tuple[list[float], list[float], list[float], list[float]]:
    """Generate levels of A, K, L and Y for periods 0 to horizon.

    A, K and L grow at seeded uniform log rates: A between 0.005 and 0.02, K between 0.02 and 0.05, and L between 0 and
    0.015 per period. Output is Y = A * K**ALPHA * L**(1 - ALPHA), computed from the levels. Returns (A, K, L, Y).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    a_levels, k_levels, l_levels = [1.0], [100.0], [50.0]
    for _ in range(horizon):
        a_levels.append(a_levels[-1] * math.exp(rng.uniform(0.005, 0.02)))
        k_levels.append(k_levels[-1] * math.exp(rng.uniform(0.02, 0.05)))
        l_levels.append(l_levels[-1] * math.exp(rng.uniform(0.0, 0.015)))
    y_levels = [
        a_t * k_t**ALPHA * l_t ** (1 - ALPHA) for a_t, k_t, l_t in zip(a_levels, k_levels, l_levels, strict=True)
    ]
    return a_levels, k_levels, l_levels, y_levels


def log_growth(levels: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    """Log growth ln x_t - ln x_(t-1) for t = 1 to len(levels) - 1, one value per period."""
    return [math.log(levels[t]) - math.log(levels[t - 1]) for t in range(1, len(levels))]


def simple_growth(levels: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    """Simple growth x_t / x_(t-1) - 1 for t = 1 to len(levels) - 1, one value per period."""
    return [levels[t] / levels[t - 1] - 1.0 for t in range(1, len(levels))]


def residual_growth(dln_y: list[float], dln_k: list[float], dln_l: list[float], alpha: float) -> list[float]:
    """Solow residual per period: dln Y - alpha * dln K - (1 - alpha) * dln L, for capital share alpha in (0, 1)."""
    return [y - alpha * k - (1.0 - alpha) * lab for y, k, lab in zip(dln_y, dln_k, dln_l, strict=True)]


def mean(values: list[float]) -> float:
    """Arithmetic mean of a non-empty list."""
    return sum(values) / len(values)


def main() -> None:
    """Print the identity check, the average decomposition, the sensitivity to alpha and the growth conventions."""
    a_levels, k_levels, l_levels, y_levels = simulate_economy(SEED, HORIZON)
    dln_y = log_growth(y_levels)
    dln_k = log_growth(k_levels)
    dln_l = log_growth(l_levels)
    dln_a = log_growth(a_levels)
    residual = residual_growth(dln_y, dln_k, dln_l, ALPHA)

    print(f"SIMULATED economy: seed {SEED}, horizon {HORIZON} periods, capital share alpha {ALPHA}")
    identity_gap = max(abs(r - a) for r, a in zip(residual, dln_a, strict=True))
    print(f"largest gap between the residual and the generated growth of A: {identity_gap:.6f}")
    print(f"residual equals the generated growth of A to 1e-12 in every period: {identity_gap < 1e-12}")

    mean_y = mean(dln_y)
    mean_k = mean(dln_k)
    mean_l = mean(dln_l)
    capital_term = ALPHA * mean_k
    labour_term = (1.0 - ALPHA) * mean_l
    mean_residual = mean(residual)
    print(f"average log growth of Y {mean_y:.6f}")
    print(f"average capital term alpha * dln K {capital_term:.6f}")
    print(f"average labour term (1 - alpha) * dln L {labour_term:.6f}")
    print(f"average residual {mean_residual:.6f}")
    share_capital = capital_term / mean_y
    share_labour = labour_term / mean_y
    share_residual = mean_residual / mean_y
    print(
        f"shares of average log growth of Y: capital {share_capital:.6f}, labour {share_labour:.6f}, "
        f"residual {share_residual:.6f}"
    )
    print(f"the three shares sum to 1: {abs(share_capital + share_labour + share_residual - 1.0) < 1e-12}")

    for alpha in (0.2, 0.4):
        alt = mean(residual_growth(dln_y, dln_k, dln_l, alpha))
        print(f"average residual with alpha {alpha}: {alt:.6f}")
    print(
        f"the residual depends on the chosen alpha: "
        f"{mean(residual_growth(dln_y, dln_k, dln_l, 0.2)) != mean(residual_growth(dln_y, dln_k, dln_l, 0.4))}"
    )

    simple_y = simple_growth(y_levels)
    never_above = all(math.log(1.0 + s) <= s for s in simple_y)
    print(f"average simple growth of Y {mean(simple_y):.6f}; average log growth of Y {mean_y:.6f}")
    print(f"log growth is at most simple growth in every period: {never_above}")

3Where the canon breaks

The decomposition is exact, and the interpretation of its residual depends on four limits that the model imposes.

  • The residual is what the inputs do not explain. Definition 2.1 puts into the residual everything that is not in \(K\), \(L\) and \(\alpha\). That includes measurement error in \(Y\), \(K\) and \(L\), changes in how intensively the inputs are used, and any input the model leaves out. Theorem 2.3 identifies the residual with \(\Delta \ln A\) inside the model, where \(A\) is the only source of residual growth by construction.
  • The capital share is held constant. Assumption 1.3 fixes \(\alpha\) in every period. A changing share, or a share that differs across sectors, is outside the model, and the lesson does not analyze it.
  • Capital and labor are single aggregates. The model has one capital stock and one labor input. Differences in skills, vintages or types of capital are not represented, and Lab 1 has no such differences.
  • No empirical claim is made about any economy. The lesson gives no size for the residual in any country or period. Any empirical statement about it needs measurement sources that this lesson does not cite, and no source for one is embedded in this program yet.

4Misconceptions

  1. “The Solow residual measures technology.”

    Definition 2.1 defines the residual from measured output and inputs and a chosen \(\alpha\). Proposition 2.4 shows that changing \(\alpha\) changes it whenever the average growth of capital and of labour differ. In Lab 1 the average residual is \(0.016254\) at \(\alpha = 0.2\) and \(0.010665\) at \(\alpha = 0.4\) for the same economy. Theorem 2.3 identifies the residual with TFP growth only inside the model, where \(A\) is known.

  2. “The decomposition is exact for any \(\alpha\), so the choice of \(\alpha\) does not matter.”

    The decomposition is an identity for every \(\alpha\), as Theorem 1.4 shows. But the residual is a different number for different \(\alpha\) whenever the average growth of capital and of labour differ (Proposition 2.4). Proposition 2.4 gives the difference exactly. The two average residuals that Lab 1 prints, \(0.016254\) at \(\alpha = 0.2\) and \(0.010665\) at \(\alpha = 0.4\), differ by \(0.005589\). An identity can hold exactly for every \(\alpha\) and still give a different residual for each \(\alpha\).

  3. “Simple growth rates add across inputs with the capital share as the weight.”

    Take a 10 percent simple growth in \(K\), flat \(L\), flat \(A\) and \(\alpha = 0.3\). The weighted simple sum gives \(0.3 \times 0.10 = 0.030000\) for output growth. The model gives \(Y_1 / Y_0 = 1.1^{0.3} = 1.029006\), so simple output growth is \(0.029006\), not \(0.030000\). The log form is exact: \(0.3 \ln 1.1 = 0.028593\) is the log growth of output, as Theorem 1.4 requires. The additive rule belongs to log growth.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · Output from the production function

Let \(A = 2\), \(K = 16\), \(L = 9\) and \(\alpha = 1/2\). Compute \(Y\) from Definition 1.1.

Solution

\(K^{1/2} = 4\) and \(L^{1/2} = 3\), so \(Y = 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 24\). The answer is 24, and the exponent \(1 - \alpha = 1/2\) applies to \(L\).

M2 · Log growth when capital doubles

Let \(A\) and \(L\) be constant, \(\alpha = 0.3\), and let \(K\) double from one period to the next. Give the log growth of \(Y\), to six decimal places.

Solution

By Theorem 1.4 with \(\Delta \ln A = \Delta \ln L = 0\), the log growth of output is \(\alpha \Delta \ln K = 0.3 \ln 2\). Since \(\ln 2 = 0.693147\), the value is \(0.207944\) to six decimal places.

M3 · The residual for one period

In one period \(\Delta \ln Y = 0.04\), \(\Delta \ln K = 0.05\) and \(\Delta \ln L = 0.02\), with \(\alpha = 0.25\). Compute the residual of Definition 2.1.

Solution

The residual is \(0.04 - 0.25 \cdot 0.05 - 0.75 \cdot 0.02 = 0.04 - 0.0125 - 0.015 = 0.0125\). The labor term is \((1 - \alpha)\Delta \ln L = 0.75 \cdot 0.02 = 0.015\), and the capital term is \(0.0125\).

Proof

P1 · Homogeneity of degree one

Prove Proposition 1.5 directly from Definition 1.1, using only the rules of exponents for positive numbers.

Solution

\((\lambda K)^{\alpha}(\lambda L)^{1-\alpha} = \lambda^{\alpha} K^{\alpha} \lambda^{1-\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}\), by the rule \((xy)^c = x^c y^c\) for positive \(x\) and \(y\). Then \(\lambda^{\alpha} \lambda^{1-\alpha} = \lambda^{1}\) by the rule \(x^a x^b = x^{a+b}\), so the expression equals \(\lambda K^{\alpha} L^{1-\alpha}\). Multiplying by \(A\) completes the argument.

P2 · Equal input growth makes the residual output growth less the common rate

Suppose \(\Delta \ln K_t = \Delta \ln L_t = g\) in every period. Show that \(\mathrm{Res}_t(\alpha) = \Delta \ln Y_t - g\) for every \(\alpha\).

Solution

Substitute into Definition 2.1: \(\mathrm{Res}_t(\alpha) = \Delta \ln Y_t - \alpha g - (1 - \alpha) g = \Delta \ln Y_t - g\), because \(\alpha g + (1 - \alpha) g = g\). The residual no longer depends on \(\alpha\) in this case, which is the special case of Proposition 2.4 with \(\Delta \ln K_t - \Delta \ln L_t = 0\).

P3 · The log growth of output when technology is constant

Show that if \(A_t\) is constant, then \(\Delta \ln Y_t = \alpha \Delta \ln K_t + (1 - \alpha) \Delta \ln L_t\) in every period.

Solution

Set \(\Delta \ln A_t = 0\) in Theorem 1.4. The equation becomes \(\Delta \ln Y_t = \alpha \Delta \ln K_t + (1 - \alpha) \Delta \ln L_t\), which is the case of pure input growth. Under this condition the residual of Definition 2.1 is zero for the true \(\alpha\), by Theorem 2.3.

Empirical

E1 · The three shares sum to one (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the shares of average log output growth as 0.362162 for capital, 0.180872 for labor and 0.456966 for the residual. Add them and explain why the sum is 1.

Solution

\(0.362162 + 0.180872 + 0.456966 = 1.000000\). The sum is 1 by construction: the capital and labor terms and the residual add to average output growth, so their shares add to 1. The check is an identity in the accounting and does not test the model.

E2 · The gap between the two average residuals (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints average residuals of 0.016254 at \(\alpha = 0.2\) and 0.010665 at \(\alpha = 0.4\). Using only the printed average capital term \(0.010667\) and average labor term \(0.005327\), verify that their difference equals \(0.2\) times the average of \(\Delta \ln K_t - \Delta \ln L_t\).

Solution

The average capital term is \(\alpha \cdot \overline{\Delta \ln K} = 0.3 \cdot \overline{\Delta \ln K} = 0.010667\), so \(\overline{\Delta \ln K} = 0.035557\). The average labor term is \(0.7 \cdot \overline{\Delta \ln L} = 0.005327\), so \(\overline{\Delta \ln L} = 0.007610\). Their difference is \(0.027947\), and \(0.2 \times 0.027947 = 0.005589\). The printed difference is \(0.016254 - 0.010665 = 0.005589\), which agrees, as Proposition 2.4 requires.

E3 · The average capital term at another share (Lab 1)

Using only the printed average capital term, compute the average capital term that would arise at \(\alpha = 0.4\).

Solution

The printed average capital term at \(\alpha = 0.3\) is 0.010667, so the average of \(\Delta \ln K_t\) is \(0.010667 / 0.3 = 0.035557\). The unrounded average of \(\Delta \ln K_t\) is 0.0355562, recomputed from the same seeded draws; the lab does not print it. At \(\alpha = 0.4\) the average capital term is \(0.4 \times 0.0355562 = 0.014222\), to six decimal places. Multiplying the rounded intermediate 0.035557 instead gives 0.014223, which is a rounding artifact. The lab does not print this number.

Research

R1 · Changing the capital share

A student replaces \(\alpha = 0.3\) with \(\alpha = 0.5\) in the lab. State what changes in each period's residual, what does not change, and what the lesson can and cannot say about which share is correct.

Solution

By Proposition 2.4 the residual in each period changes by \(-(0.5 - 0.3)(\Delta \ln K_t - \Delta \ln L_t) = -0.2(\Delta \ln K_t - \Delta \ln L_t)\). The identity of Theorem 1.4 still holds, because it holds for every \(\alpha\), so the output growth is unchanged. The attribution of growth between inputs and the residual changes. The lesson cannot say which share is correct, because \(\alpha\) is an input here and is not derived from factor prices or measured in any economy.

R2 · What the generated test does and does not show

The lab recovers the generated technology growth from the generated inputs and output. Explain what this test establishes, and why it does not show that the same method recovers technology in a measured economy.

Solution

Because the generator fixes \(A_t\) and computes \(Y_t\) from it, the residual is compared with a known quantity, and the agreement follows from Theorem 2.3 and the arithmetic of the lab. The test establishes that the accounting is computed correctly when the model holds exactly. In a measured economy, \(\alpha\) is not known, \(A_t\) is not observed, the inputs are measured with error, and the model may be wrong, so none of these conditions is tested by the lab.

R3 · A negative residual

Give a period in which the residual of Definition 2.1 is negative, with \(\alpha = 0.3\), and say what the negative value records.

Solution

Take \(\Delta \ln Y = 0.01\), \(\Delta \ln K = 0.05\) and \(\Delta \ln L = 0\). The residual is \(0.01 - 0.3 \cdot 0.05 - 0.7 \cdot 0 = 0.01 - 0.015 = -0.005\). A negative residual records output growth below the growth the chosen inputs would produce at the chosen \(\alpha\). Under Theorem 2.3, with the economy's own \(\alpha\), it would mean that TFP fell, but in a measured economy it may instead reflect any of the sources listed in §3.

Answer from memory, then check. Finishing the quiz adds this lesson's cards to your review deck.

  1. In Theorem 1.4, which quantity multiplies \(\Delta \ln L_t\)?

    Options

    The exponent on \(L\) in Definition 1.1 is \(1 - \alpha\), and the log of \(L^{1-\alpha}\) is \((1 - \alpha) \ln L\).

  2. With \(A = 2\), \(K = 16\), \(L = 9\) and \(\alpha = 1/2\), what is \(Y\)?

    \(Y = 2 \cdot 16^{1/2} \cdot 9^{1/2} = 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 24\) (Problem M1).

  3. Which statement about the Solow residual is correct?

    Options

    Proposition 2.4 gives the change in the residual when \(\alpha\) changes, and Definition 2.1 defines the residual from measured inputs and a chosen \(\alpha\).

  4. In a Cobb-Douglas economy with \(0 < \alpha < 1\), what happens to output when both \(K\) and \(L\) double?

    Options

    Proposition 1.5 with \(\lambda = 2\): the exponents \(\alpha\) and \(1 - \alpha\) sum to 1.

  5. With \(A\) and \(L\) constant, \(\alpha = 0.3\), and \(K\) doubling, what is the log growth of \(Y\) to six decimal places?

    \(0.3 \ln 2 = 0.207944\) (Problem M2).

  6. Which quantity does Definition 2.1 subtract from log output growth?

    Options

    Definition 2.1 subtracts both weighted input terms. The third option describes Theorem 2.3's identity, not the definition.

  7. In one period \(\Delta \ln Y = 0.04\), \(\Delta \ln K = 0.05\), \(\Delta \ln L = 0.02\) and \(\alpha = 0.25\). What is the residual?

    \(0.04 - 0.25 \cdot 0.05 - 0.75 \cdot 0.02 = 0.0125\) (Problem M3).

  8. Why is a simple-growth weighted sum not exact for output growth?

    Options

    Misconception 3: \(1.1^{0.3} - 1 = 0.029006\), while \(0.3 \times 0.10 = 0.030000\); the log form \(0.3 \ln 1.1 = 0.028593\) is exact.
