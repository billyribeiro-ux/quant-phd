National Accounts, Price Indices and Measured Inflation

Every macroeconomic claim in this unit is a claim about a measured total: output, the price level, or the rate at which the price level changes. Those totals are fixed by accounting definitions and by index formulas, and the formulas can give different answers for the same economy. This lesson states the identities that the accounts satisfy by definition, defines the Laspeyres and Paasche price indices, and fixes the conventions for measured inflation. The lab computes each identity and each index exactly, on SIMULATED numbers, and shows that the gap between the two indices has no fixed sign.

1The national accounts and the expenditure identity

Definition 1.1 (gross domestic product)

The market value of a good is its price times its quantity in the period. A good is final when it is sold to its last user in the period and is not used up as an input to another good in that period. Gross domestic product, GDP, is the market value of all final goods and services produced within the economy in the period. The national accounts are the system of such definitions, applied so that each transaction in the period is counted once.

Definition 1.2 (expenditure components)

Let \(C\) be household consumption, \(I\) gross investment (including the change in inventories), \(G\) government purchases of goods and services, \(X\) exports and \(M\) imports, all in one period and in one currency. Net exports are \(NX = X - M\). Write \(Y\) for GDP measured on the expenditure side.

Assumption 1.3 (exhaustive classification)

Every final expenditure on domestic output in the period belongs to exactly one of \(C\), \(I\), \(G\) or \(X\), and every import is subtracted once, in \(M\). The accounts are taken to satisfy this by definition. Nothing in this lesson models a gap that could open when two data sources measure the same flow differently.

Theorem 1.4 (the expenditure identity)

Under Assumption 1.3, \(Y = C + I + G + NX\).

Proof

By Definition 1.1, GDP is final expenditure on domestic output. Final spending on goods and services is \(C + I + G\), which includes spending on imported goods. Exports \(X\) are purchases by foreigners of domestic output, and they are not in the first three components. By Assumption 1.3, each import is subtracted exactly once, through \(M\), which removes the imported goods from the first three components. So \(Y = C + I + G + X - M = C + I + G + NX\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 1.4 is a property of the definitions. It says that the accounting convention is internally consistent. It says nothing about the size of any component in any economy, and no number in this lesson is a claim about a real economy.

Definition 1.5 (income side and the balancing entry)

On the income side, GDP is the sum of compensation of employees \(W\), taxes less subsidies on production \(\mathrm{TLS}\), consumption of fixed capital \(\mathrm{Dep}\), and an operating surplus \(\mathrm{OS}\). The operating surplus is the balancing entry: it is defined as the expenditure-side GDP minus the other three terms. So the income identity \(Y = W + \mathrm{TLS} + \mathrm{Dep} + \mathrm{OS}\) holds by construction, and it is a check on the arithmetic of a table, not an economic result.

Definition 1.6 (nominal and real GDP)

Let \(p_{i,t}\) be the price and \(q_{i,t}\) the quantity of good \(i\) in period \(t\). Nominal GDP in period \(t\) is \(\sum_i p_{i,t} q_{i,t}\), at the prices of that period. Real GDP in period \(t\) at base period \(0\) is \(\sum_i p_{i,0} q_{i,t}\), which holds prices at their base-period values. A deflator is a price index \(D_t > 0\) with \(D_0 = 1\), and real GDP can be defined as nominal GDP divided by the deflator. This lesson uses that deflator form in Proposition 2.6, and it does not use the base-price form.

Proposition 1.7 (closed-economy saving equals investment)

Assume (A1) the economy is closed, so \(X = M = 0\) and \(NX = 0\); (A2) taxes net of transfers are \(\mathrm{Tax}\), and private saving is defined as \(S_p = Y - \mathrm{Tax} - C\) and public saving as \(S_g = \mathrm{Tax} - G\). Then \(S_p + S_g = I\).

Proof

Under A1, Theorem 1.4 gives \(Y = C + I + G\). Adding the two saving definitions gives \(S_p + S_g = (Y - \mathrm{Tax} - C) + (\mathrm{Tax} - G) = Y - C - G\). Substituting \(Y - C - G = I\) from Theorem 1.4 gives \(S_p + S_g = I\). The tax \(\mathrm{Tax}\) cancels, so the result does not depend on its size. \(\blacksquare\)

2Price indices and measured inflation

Definition 2.1 (Laspeyres and Paasche indices)

Take a basket of \(n\) goods with base-period prices \(p^0\) and quantities \(q^0\), and period-1 prices \(p^1\) and quantities \(q^1\). The Laspeyres index of period 1 on base period 0 is

\[ P^{L} = \frac{\sum_i p^1_i\, q^0_i}{\sum_i p^0_i\, q^0_i}, \]

which fixes the quantities at their base-period values. The Paasche index is

\[ P^{P} = \frac{\sum_i p^1_i\, q^1_i}{\sum_i p^0_i\, q^1_i}, \]

which uses the period-1 quantities. Both are taken relative to the base period 0, so each is a ratio of two values of the same basket.

Definition 2.2 (measured inflation and its conventions)

Let \(P_t\) be a price index with \(P_t > 0\). Measured inflation between periods \(t-1\) and \(t\) is the rate of change of the index. Its simple form is \(\pi_t = P_t / P_{t-1} - 1\), a fraction per period. Its log form is \(\ell_t = \ln(P_t / P_{t-1})\). Every inflation figure on this page states which form it uses and the period it covers.

Definition 2.3 (chain-linked index)

A chain-linked index multiplies the link ratios \(P_t / P_{t-1}\) across successive periods, so that the index at horizon \(H\) relative to period 0 is \(\prod_{t=1}^{H} P_t / P_{t-1}\). When all the link ratios come from one sequence \(P_0, \dots, P_H\), the product telescopes to \(P_H / P_0\).

Proposition 2.4 (neither index is always larger)

Assume (A1) both indices are computed on the same two-good basket as in Definition 2.1, with positive prices and quantities. Then the sign of \(P^{L} - P^{P}\) is not fixed: there exist baskets satisfying A1 with \(P^{L} > P^{P}\) and baskets satisfying A1 with \(P^{L} < P^{P}\).

Proof

Basket A has \(p^0 = (2, 5)\), \(q^0 = (10, 4)\), \(p^1 = (3, 5)\) and \(q^1 = (8, 6)\). The numerators are \(\sum p^1 q^0 = 30 + 20 = 50\) and \(\sum p^1 q^1 = 24 + 30 = 54\). The denominators are \(\sum p^0 q^0 = 20 + 20 = 40\) and \(\sum p^0 q^1 = 16 + 30 = 46\). So \(P^{L} = 50/40 = 5/4\) and \(P^{P} = 54/46 = 27/23\). Since \(5/4 - 27/23 = (115 - 108)/92 = 7/92 > 0\), basket A has \(P^{L} > P^{P}\).

Basket B has \(p^0 = (1, 1)\), \(q^0 = (1, 9)\), \(p^1 = (2, 1)\) and \(q^1 = (9, 1)\). Then \(\sum p^1 q^0 = 2 + 9 = 11\), \(\sum p^0 q^0 = 10\), \(\sum p^1 q^1 = 18 + 1 = 19\) and \(\sum p^0 q^1 = 9 + 1 = 10\). So \(P^{L} = 11/10\) and \(P^{P} = 19/10\), and \(11/10 - 19/10 = -4/5 < 0\). Both baskets satisfy A1, so neither index is always larger. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.5 (log growth lies below simple growth)

Assume (A1) \(x > -1\) is a real number, with \(\ln\) the natural logarithm. Then \(\ln(1 + x) \le x\), with equality only at \(x = 0\). Consequently the log inflation \(\ell_t\) is never larger than the simple inflation \(\pi_t\) for the same index.

Proof

Let \(f(x) = x - \ln(1 + x)\) on \((-1, \infty)\). Then \(f(0) = 0\) and, by the derivative of the logarithm, \(f'(x) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + x} = \frac{x}{1 + x}\). For \(-1 < x < 0\) the derivative is negative, so \(f\) is decreasing there, and for \(x > 0\) it is positive, so \(f\) is increasing there. Hence \(f\) attains its minimum at \(x = 0\), where \(f(0) = 0\), so \(f(x) \ge 0\) on \((-1, \infty)\), with equality only at 0. Taking \(1 + x = P_t / P_{t-1}\) gives \(\ell_t \le \pi_t\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.6 (the deflator identity)

Assume (A1) real GDP is defined as nominal GDP divided by a deflator \(D_t\) with \(D_0 = 1\) and \(D_t > 0\); let \(g_n\) be the growth of nominal GDP, \(g_r\) the growth of real GDP and \(\pi\) the simple inflation of the deflator, all between periods 0 and 1. Then \((1 + g_n) = (1 + g_r)(1 + \pi)\) exactly.

Proof

Let \(N_0\) and \(N_1\) be nominal GDP and let real GDP be \(R_t = N_t / D_t\). Then \(1 + g_n = N_1 / N_0\). Also \(1 + g_r = R_1 / R_0 = (N_1 / D_1) / (N_0 / D_0)\), and \(1 + \pi = D_1 / D_0\). Multiplying the last two gives \((1 + g_r)(1 + \pi) = (N_1 / D_1)(D_0 / N_0)(D_1 / D_0) = N_1 / N_0 = 1 + g_n\). \(\blacksquare\)

"""E5 L01 lab 1: the accounting identities, two price indices, the deflator and the log convention.

Standard library only (fractions, math). No randomness and no network, so every run prints the same lines.
The accounts table and the basket prices are SIMULATED: they are illustrative integers typed for this lab in
currency units (CU). They are not taken from any statistical agency, and no claim about any real economy rests on them.

Part A: the expenditure-side and income-side identities for one accounts table, and the closed-economy saving identity.
Part B: two price indices on two-good baskets, Laspeyres and Paasche, as exact fractions, with the gap between them.
Part C: simple and log inflation from one index, the deflator identity for real and nominal growth, and the
chain product of link ratios.

Every index value is a Fraction. Floats appear only in the logarithm lines, which print six decimal places.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction


def expenditure_gdp(c: int, i: int, g: int, exports: int, imports: int) -> int:
    """Expenditure-side GDP in CU: Y = C + I + G + (X - M), with net exports X - M."""
    return c + i + g + (exports - imports)


def income_gdp(wages: int, operating_surplus: int, taxes_less_subsidies: int, depreciation: int) -> int:
    """Income-side GDP in CU: compensation of employees, operating surplus, taxes less subsidies and depreciation."""
    return wages + operating_surplus + taxes_less_subsidies + depreciation


def laspeyres(p0: tuple[int, ...], q0: tuple[int, ...], p1: tuple[int, ...]) -> Fraction:
    """Laspeyres price index of period 1 on base period 0: sum of p1 * q0 over sum of p0 * q0.

    The quantities are fixed at the base period, so the weights do not change when prices do.
    """
    numerator = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(p1, q0, strict=True))
    denominator = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(p0, q0, strict=True))
    return Fraction(numerator, denominator)


def paasche(p0: tuple[int, ...], p1: tuple[int, ...], q1: tuple[int, ...]) -> Fraction:
    """Paasche price index of period 1 on base period 0: sum of p1 * q1 over sum of p0 * q1.

    The quantities are those of period 1, so the weights move with the quantities bought.
    """
    numerator = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(p1, q1, strict=True))
    denominator = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(p0, q1, strict=True))
    return Fraction(numerator, denominator)


def simple_rate(index_now: Fraction, index_before: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Simple growth rate of an index: index_now / index_before - 1, an exact fraction."""
    return index_now / index_before - 1


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A, B and C in a fixed order."""
    # Part A: one accounts table, all entries SIMULATED integers in CU.
    consumption, investment, government = 620, 180, 210
    exports, imports = 95, 110
    wages, taxes_less_subsidies, depreciation = 640, 40, 15
    gdp_expenditure = expenditure_gdp(consumption, investment, government, exports, imports)
    # The operating surplus is the balancing entry on the income side, so the two sides agree by construction.
    surplus_residual = gdp_expenditure - (wages + taxes_less_subsidies + depreciation)
    gdp_income = income_gdp(wages, surplus_residual, taxes_less_subsidies, depreciation)
    print(f"expenditure GDP {gdp_expenditure} CU; income GDP {gdp_income} CU")
    print(f"expenditure and income sides agree: {gdp_expenditure == gdp_income}")
    print(f"operating surplus as the balancing entry {surplus_residual} CU")

    # Closed economy with the same C, I and G: no exports or imports, and a tax T.
    tax = 230
    closed_y = consumption + investment + government
    private_saving = closed_y - tax - consumption
    public_saving = tax - government
    total_saving = private_saving + public_saving
    print(f"closed economy Y {closed_y} CU; private saving {private_saving}; public saving {public_saving}")
    print(f"total saving {total_saving} equals investment {investment}: {total_saving == investment}")

    # Part B: two baskets of two goods, prices p0 and p1 and quantities q0 and q1, in CU per unit.
    p0_a, q0_a = (2, 5), (10, 4)
    p1_a, q1_a = (3, 5), (8, 6)
    lasp_a = laspeyres(p0_a, q0_a, p1_a)
    pasc_a = paasche(p0_a, p1_a, q1_a)
    gap_a = lasp_a - pasc_a
    print(f"basket A: Laspeyres {lasp_a} ({float(lasp_a):.6f}); Paasche {pasc_a} ({float(pasc_a):.6f})")
    print(f"basket A: Laspeyres minus Paasche {gap_a} ({float(gap_a):.6f}); Laspeyres is larger: {gap_a > 0}")

    p0_b, q0_b = (1, 1), (1, 9)
    p1_b, q1_b = (2, 1), (9, 1)
    lasp_b = laspeyres(p0_b, q0_b, p1_b)
    pasc_b = paasche(p0_b, p1_b, q1_b)
    gap_b = lasp_b - pasc_b
    print(f"basket B: Laspeyres {lasp_b} ({float(lasp_b):.6f}); Paasche {pasc_b} ({float(pasc_b):.6f})")
    print(f"basket B: Laspeyres minus Paasche {gap_b} ({float(gap_b):.6f}); Laspeyres is larger: {gap_b > 0}")

    # Part C: inflation conventions and the deflator identity, on basket A.
    simple_lasp = simple_rate(lasp_a, Fraction(1))
    simple_pasc = simple_rate(pasc_a, Fraction(1))
    print(f"simple inflation, Laspeyres basis: {float(simple_lasp):.6f}")
    print(f"simple inflation, Paasche basis: {float(simple_pasc):.6f}")
    print(f"log inflation, Laspeyres basis: {math.log(float(lasp_a)):.6f}")
    print(f"log inflation, Paasche basis: {math.log(float(pasc_a)):.6f}")

    # Deflator identity: real GDP = nominal GDP / deflator, so (1 + g_nominal) = (1 + g_real)(1 + pi) exactly.
    nominal_0, nominal_1 = Fraction(995), Fraction(1100)
    deflator_0, deflator_1 = Fraction(1), pasc_a
    real_0, real_1 = nominal_0 / deflator_0, nominal_1 / deflator_1
    g_nominal = nominal_1 / nominal_0 - 1
    g_real = real_1 / real_0 - 1
    pi = deflator_1 / deflator_0 - 1
    identity = (1 + g_nominal) == (1 + g_real) * (1 + pi)
    print(f"nominal growth {float(g_nominal):.6f}; real growth {float(g_real):.6f}; deflator inflation {float(pi):.6f}")
    print(f"(1 + nominal) equals (1 + real)(1 + inflation) exactly: {identity}")

    # Simple and log growth: ln(1 + x) <= x for x >= 0, so x - ln(1 + x) is non-negative on the grid.
    grid = (Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(5, 100), Fraction(25, 100))
    gaps = [float(x) - math.log(1 + float(x)) for x in grid]
    print("x - ln(1 + x) at x = 0.01, 0.05, 0.25: " + ", ".join(f"{g:.6f}" for g in gaps))
    print(f"all gaps non-negative: {all(g >= 0 for g in gaps)}")

    # Chain: the product of link ratios over three periods equals the direct ratio of the index.
    levels = (Fraction(1), Fraction(5, 4), Fraction(27, 23), Fraction(3, 2))
    links = [levels[k] / levels[k - 1] for k in range(1, len(levels))]
    product = Fraction(1)
    for link in links:
        product *= link
    print(
        f"chain product of link ratios {product} equals direct ratio {levels[-1] / levels[0]}: "
        f"{product == levels[-1] / levels[0]}"
    )

3Where the canon breaks

The accounting and index definitions in this lesson are exact, and they have four limits that matter for every later lesson.

  • Non-market activity is outside GDP by definition. Definition 1.1 values output at market prices. A good that is produced and not sold has no market price in the definition, so the definition does not count it. The lesson does not say how large that omitted activity is.
  • The indices hold a basket fixed. Both formulas use one set of goods. A new good, or a change in a good's quality, is not in either formula unless the basket is redefined. Proposition 2.4 shows that the two standard weightings can disagree in either direction, and neither index is shown here to be the right one.
  • Real GDP depends on the deflator. Proposition 2.6 holds for any positive deflator with \(D_0 = 1\). The growth of real output that a reader sees therefore depends on the deflator chosen, and the lesson takes no position on the choice.
  • The two-good baskets are illustrations. Lab 1 uses two goods and two periods. A proof about two goods does not extend to many goods without a separate argument, and the lesson does not make one.

4Misconceptions

  1. “GDP adds up every sale in the economy.”

    Definition 1.1 counts final goods only. If a farmer sells flour for 3 CU to a baker, and the baker sells bread made from it for 10 CU to a household, the household's purchase is final and the flour sale is not. GDP in this case is 10 CU, not 13 CU, because the flour is used up in making the bread. Adding every sale would count the flour twice.

  2. “The Laspeyres index always overstates inflation.”

    Proposition 2.4 gives two baskets with opposite signs. For basket A, \(P^{L} = 5/4\) exceeds \(P^{P} = 27/23\). For basket B, \(P^{L} = 11/10\) is below \(P^{P} = 19/10\). Lab 1 prints both signs, so the word "always" is refuted by an explicit computation. Which index is closer to a true cost of living is a separate question that this lesson does not settle.

  3. “Log inflation and simple inflation are interchangeable at any rate.”

    Proposition 2.5 gives \(\ell_t \le \pi_t\). At a 25 percent simple inflation rate, the log rate is \(\ln 1.25 = 0.223144\), which is 0.026856 lower. The gap is small at low rates and grows with the rate, as Lab 1 shows with \(x = 0.01, 0.05, 0.25\). The percent figure in any statement must state its form.

  4. “When nominal GDP grows, output has grown.”

    Proposition 2.6 gives \((1 + g_n) = (1 + g_r)(1 + \pi)\). In the lab's SIMULATED example, nominal GDP rises by \(105/995 \approx 0.105528\), while the deflator rises by \(4/23 \approx 0.173913\). Real growth is then \(-0.058254\), which is negative. Nominal growth alone does not show output growth.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · Expenditure GDP from components

Let \(C = 400\), \(I = 120\), \(G = 150\), \(X = 60\) and \(M = 75\), all in CU. Compute \(Y\) by Theorem 1.4.

Solution

Net exports are \(NX = 60 - 75 = -15\). Then \(Y = 400 + 120 + 150 - 15 = 655\) CU. The answer is 655 CU, and the negative net exports reduce the total.

M2 · A Laspeyres index for a new basket

Base prices are \(p^0 = (1, 4)\) and base quantities \(q^0 = (6, 2)\). Period-1 prices are \(p^1 = (2, 3)\). Compute the Laspeyres index \(P^{L}\) as an exact fraction.

Solution

The numerator is \(\sum p^1 q^0 = 2 \cdot 6 + 3 \cdot 2 = 18\). The denominator is \(\sum p^0 q^0 = 1 \cdot 6 + 4 \cdot 2 = 14\). So \(P^{L} = 18/14 = 9/7\), which is approximately 1.285714.

M3 · Simple and log inflation at ten percent

An index rises from 1 to 1.1. Give the simple inflation and the log inflation, each to six decimal places, and state which is larger.

Solution

The simple inflation is \(1.1 / 1 - 1 = 0.100000\). The log inflation is \(\ln 1.1 = 0.095310\) to six decimal places. The simple figure is larger, as Proposition 2.5 requires for any \(x > -1\), and the difference is \(0.004690\).

Proof

P1 · Equal relative prices make the indices equal to the price ratio

Suppose \(p^1 = \lambda p^0\) for a single positive number \(\lambda\), so every price rises by the same factor. Show that \(P^{L} = P^{P} = \lambda\), with the quantities of both periods as in Definition 2.1.

Solution

The Laspeyres numerator is \(\sum \lambda p^0_i q^0_i = \lambda \sum p^0_i q^0_i\), which equals \(\lambda\) times the denominator \(\sum p^0_i q^0_i\). So \(P^{L} = \lambda\), provided the denominator is not zero. For the Paasche index, \(\sum p^1_i q^1_i = \lambda \sum p^0_i q^1_i\), which is \(\lambda\) times the Paasche denominator, so \(P^{P} = \lambda\) as well. Both indices therefore record the common price change.

P2 · Equal quantities make the indices equal

Show that if \(q^1 = q^0\), then \(P^{L} = P^{P}\), and state the common value.

Solution

With \(q^1 = q^0\), the Paasche numerator is \(\sum p^1 q^1 = \sum p^1 q^0\), which is the Laspeyres numerator. The Paasche denominator is \(\sum p^0 q^1 = \sum p^0 q^0\), which is the Laspeyres denominator. The two indices are the same fraction, \(\sum p^1 q^0 / \sum p^0 q^0\), and that is their common value.

P3 · The chain product takes logarithms additively

For positive indices \(P_0, P_1, P_2\), show that \(\ln(P_2 / P_0) = \ln(P_1 / P_0) + \ln(P_2 / P_1)\), and state what this means for the log inflation of two periods.

Solution

The sum \(\ln(P_1 / P_0) + \ln(P_2 / P_1)\) equals \(\ln\big((P_1 / P_0)(P_2 / P_1)\big)\) by the rule \(\ln a + \ln b = \ln (ab)\) for positive \(a\) and \(b\). The product telescopes to \(P_2 / P_0\), so the sum is \(\ln(P_2 / P_0)\). The log inflation over two periods is therefore the sum of the two one-period log inflation rates, which is why the log form is the convention for multi-period growth.

Empirical

E1 · The gap for basket A (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the Laspeyres minus Paasche gap for basket A as \(7/92\). Confirm this by subtraction, and state its sign.

Solution

\(5/4 = 115/92\) and \(27/23 = 108/92\). Their difference is \(7/92\), which is about 0.076087 and positive. The lab prints the same fraction and the same sign.

E2 · The reversed gap for basket B (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the gap for basket B as \(-4/5\). Confirm it from the two index values the lab prints.

Solution

The Laspeyres index is \(11/10\) and the Paasche index is \(19/10\), so the gap is \(11/10 - 19/10 = -8/10 = -4/5\). The value \(-0.800000\) matches the printed decimal, and the negative sign is the reversal that Proposition 2.4 uses.

E3 · Real and nominal growth from Lab 1's deflator example

In Lab 1, nominal GDP moves from 995 to 1100 CU, and the deflator moves from 1 to \(27/23\). Compute nominal growth, real growth and the deflator inflation as exact fractions, and check Proposition 2.6.

Solution

Nominal growth is \(1100/995 - 1 = 105/995\), which is about 0.105528. Real GDP in period 1 is \(1100 / (27/23) = 25300/27\), and real GDP in period 0 is 995, so real growth is \((25300/27)/995 - 1 = 25300/26865 - 1 = -1565/26865\), about \(-0.058254\). The deflator inflation is \(27/23 - 1 = 4/23\), about 0.173913. Then \((1 + g_r)(1 + \pi) = (25300/26865)(27/23) = 1100/995\), which is \(1 + g_n\), as Proposition 2.6 states.

Research

R1 · Substitution toward cheaper goods

An analyst argues that a Laspeyres index overstates inflation because buyers move toward goods whose prices rose least. State the mechanism in Definition 2.1 that would produce such a bias, and say which period's quantities a test of the argument would need, given that the Paasche index uses \(q^1\).

Solution

The Laspeyres formula keeps the base quantities \(q^0\), so it cannot register a shift in purchases toward goods whose relative prices fell, and the measured price change is then larger than the cost of the period-1 basket. The Paasche formula uses \(q^1\) and so does record that shift. A test would need period-1 quantities, to compare the two indices on the same goods. Basket B in Proposition 2.4 is a case where purchases shift toward the good whose price rose most, so the Laspeyres index there is the smaller of the two. The analyst's argument therefore depends on the direction of the substitution in the data, and this lesson gives no data to decide it.

R2 · A quality change recorded as a price change

A good's price rises from 2 CU to 3 CU, and over the same period its quality doubles with no price adjustment. Compute the change in price per unit of quality, and explain why a price index that records the 2 to 3 change overstates the price of an unchanged amount of quality.

Solution

Price per unit of quality was \(2/1 = 2\) CU and is now \(3/2 = 1.5\) CU, a change of \(1.5/2 - 1 = -0.25\), so it fell by 25 percent. The index records a rise of \(3/2 - 1 = 0.5\), or 50 percent. The index measures the price of the good, not the price of quality, so it overstates the price of an unchanged amount of quality by the quality change. Definition 2.1 has no term for quality, which is the limit listed in §3.

R3 · Two baskets that agree and two that do not

Give one basket for which \(P^{L} \ne P^{P}\) and one for which \(P^{L} = P^{P}\), and state which result of this lesson each example uses.

Solution

Basket A of Proposition 2.4 has \(P^{L} = 5/4\) and \(P^{P} = 27/23\), which differ. A basket with \(q^1 = q^0\), for example basket A with \(q^1\) replaced by \(q^0 = (10, 4)\), has \(P^{L} = P^{P}\), by Problem P2. The first example uses the proof of Proposition 2.4, and the second uses the equal-quantity case.
