Fiscal Policy and the Multiplier

Government spending raises output by more than its own amount in a simple closed model, and the reason is a chain of induced spending. This lesson fixes the model that produces the chain: a constant marginal propensity to consume between 0 and 1, fixed prices, and a fixed interest rate. Under those assumptions the multiplier is \(1/(1-c)\). The lesson derives it two ways, from the sum of the stages of spending and from the equilibrium equation, and shows that the two agree. Lab 1 checks both exactly. The result describes the model, not any measured economy.

1The Keynesian cross and the stages of spending

Definition 1.1 (the closed Keynesian-cross economy)

Output \(Y\), consumption \(C\), investment \(I\), government spending \(G\) and lump-sum taxes \(\mathrm{Tax}\) are amounts in cents per period. A lump-sum tax is a fixed amount that does not depend on output or income. Consumption is \(C = a + c(Y - \mathrm{Tax})\), where \(a\) is autonomous consumption and \(c\) is the marginal propensity to consume: the increase in consumption per cent of an increase in disposable income \(Y - \mathrm{Tax}\). Equilibrium output satisfies \(Y = C + I + G\).

Assumptions (for Propositions 2.2 to 2.5)
  1. A1. \(0 < c < 1\) is a constant.
  2. A2. \(a\), \(I\), \(G\) and \(\mathrm{Tax}\) are constants. The price level and the policy rate of Lesson 7 are fixed, so neither enters the equation.
  3. A3. The economy is in equilibrium, \(Y = C + I + G\), at each level of \(G\) and \(\mathrm{Tax}\) that the page considers.
Definition 1.2 (the fiscal multiplier)

The fiscal multiplier of government spending is the ratio of the change in equilibrium output to the change in government spending that causes it, in cents per cent, so it is a number with no unit. A lump-sum tax multiplier is defined the same way with \(\mathrm{Tax}\) in place of \(G\). This page uses the multiplier for the comparative-statics question of how equilibrium output moves when \(G\) or \(\mathrm{Tax}\) moves.

Definition 1.3 (a stage of spending)

Let \(\Delta G\) be a change in government spending. Stage 0 is the change \(\Delta G\) itself, which is income for the recipients. Each recipient spends the fraction \(c\) of new income, so stage \(k\) adds \(c^{k}\Delta G\) to output. The running total after \(N\) stages is \(S_N = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} c^{k}\Delta G\). A stage is a device for summing a series. This lesson assigns no time to a stage.

Proposition 2.1 (the finite sum of stages)

Under A1, for every whole number \(N \ge 1\), \(S_N = \Delta G\,(1 - c^{N})/(1 - c)\).

Proof

Multiply \(S_N\) by \(1 - c\). Then \((1 - c)S_N = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(c^{k} - c^{k+1})\Delta G\). The sum telescopes: each term \(-c^{k+1}\Delta G\) cancels the term \(c^{k+1}\Delta G\) of the next index, and only \(c^{0}\Delta G\) and \(-c^{N}\Delta G\) remain. So \((1-c)S_N = (1 - c^{N})\Delta G\). Since A1 gives \(1 - c > 0\), dividing by \(1 - c\) gives the formula.

Lab 1 · The stages of spending against the equilibrium multiplier

The inputs are illustrative, not estimates. The marginal propensity to consume is \(c = 3/5\), autonomous consumption is 20,000 cents, investment is 15,000 cents, government spending is 30,000 cents, and the change in spending is \(\Delta G = 10{,}000\) cents. Part A prints the first five stages and the running total, and checks the closed form of Proposition 2.1 for 30 stages. Part B prints the gap between the running total and \(\Delta G/(1-c)\) for five stage counts. Part C solves the equilibrium before and after the change and prints the change in output. Part D repeats the stage sum for a lump-sum tax increase of 10,000 cents and prints the balanced-budget change. Propositions 2.1 to 2.4 predict the printed values: a multiplier of 5/2, an output change of 25,000 cents, and a tax change of \(-15{,}000\) cents. The code uses exact fractions and no random draws.

"""E5 L08 lab 1: the Keynesian-cross multiplier, checked against a finite sum of stages of spending.

Standard library only (fractions). No randomness and no network, so every run prints the same lines.

Every input is an illustrative value chosen for the arithmetic, not an estimate: a marginal propensity to consume
c = 3/5, autonomous consumption a = 20,000 cents, investment I = 15,000 cents, government spending G = 30,000 cents,
and a change in spending of 10,000 cents (100 CU). The model is closed and holds prices and the interest rate fixed.
Amounts are in whole cents; a stage can carry a fraction of a cent, and such amounts are printed as exact fractions.

Part A: the stages of spending. Stage k adds c^k times the change in spending. The running total after N stages equals
the closed form dG (1 - c^N) / (1 - c), checked exactly for N = 1 to 30.
Part B: the gap between the running total and the multiplier total dG / (1 - c), which equals dG c^N / (1 - c).
Part C: the equilibrium Y = (a + I + G) / (1 - c) solved exactly at two levels of G, and the change in output per
cent of spending, which is 1 / (1 - c).
Part D: the lump-sum tax case, where the change in output is -c dTax / (1 - c), and the balanced budget, whose
multiplier is 1.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

C = Fraction(3, 5)
AUTONOMOUS = 20_000
INVESTMENT = 15_000
GOVERNMENT = 30_000
DELTA_G = 10_000
DELTA_T = 10_000
MAX_STAGES = 30
REPORT_STAGES = (1, 5, 10, 20, 30)


def running_total(change: Fraction, marginal: Fraction, stages: int) -> Fraction:
    """Sum of the first `stages` stages of a change in spending: stage k (from 0) adds change * marginal**k."""
    return sum((change * marginal**k for k in range(stages)), Fraction(0))


def closed_form(change: Fraction, marginal: Fraction, stages: int) -> Fraction:
    """The finite geometric sum change * (1 - marginal**stages) / (1 - marginal), for marginal != 1."""
    return change * (1 - marginal**stages) / (1 - marginal)


def equilibrium(autonomous: int, investment: int, government: int, marginal: Fraction, tax: int) -> Fraction:
    """Output in cents where Y = a + c (Y - T) + I + G, solved exactly: (a - cT + I + G) / (1 - c)."""
    return (autonomous - marginal * tax + investment + government) / (1 - marginal)


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A to D in a fixed order. Each line is an exact fraction, an integer or a fixed string."""
    print(
        f"inputs: c {C} a {AUTONOMOUS} I {INVESTMENT} G {GOVERNMENT} dG {DELTA_G} dTax {DELTA_T} "
        f"multiplier {1 / (1 - C)}"
    )

    print("Part A: stages of spending in cents, and the running total")
    first_stages = [DELTA_G * C**k for k in range(5)]
    print("stage_amounts " + " ".join(str(x) for x in first_stages))
    totals = [running_total(Fraction(DELTA_G), C, n) for n in range(1, 6)]
    print("running_totals " + " ".join(str(x) for x in totals))
    matches = all(
        running_total(Fraction(DELTA_G), C, n) == closed_form(Fraction(DELTA_G), C, n) for n in range(1, MAX_STAGES + 1)
    )
    print(f"closed_form_matches_stages_N_1_to_{MAX_STAGES} {matches}")

    print("Part B: gap between the running total and the multiplier total, dG / (1 - c)")
    total = Fraction(DELTA_G) / (1 - C)
    print(f"multiplier_total_cents {total}")
    for n in REPORT_STAGES:
        gap = total - running_total(Fraction(DELTA_G), C, n)
        exact = gap == Fraction(DELTA_G) * C**n / (1 - C)
        print(f"stages {n} gap_cents {float(gap):.6f} equals_dG_c_N_over_1_minus_c {exact}")

    print("Part C: equilibrium output at two levels of government spending")
    y0 = equilibrium(AUTONOMOUS, INVESTMENT, GOVERNMENT, C, 0)
    y1 = equilibrium(AUTONOMOUS, INVESTMENT, GOVERNMENT + DELTA_G, C, 0)
    print(f"Y_before {y0} Y_after {y1} change {y1 - y0}")
    fixed_point = y1 == AUTONOMOUS + INVESTMENT + GOVERNMENT + DELTA_G + C * y1
    print(f"after_satisfies_Y_equals_a_plus_I_plus_G_plus_cY {fixed_point}")
    per_cent = equilibrium(AUTONOMOUS, INVESTMENT, GOVERNMENT + 1, C, 0) - y0
    print(f"change_per_cent_of_spending {per_cent} equals_one_over_one_minus_c {per_cent == 1 / (1 - C)}")
    print(f"change_equals_multiplier_times_dG {y1 - y0 == DELTA_G / (1 - C)}")

    print("Part D: lump-sum tax and the balanced budget")
    # A lump-sum tax increase of dTax lowers disposable income by dTax, so stage 0 of the tax case is -c dTax:
    # the first fall in consumption spending.
    tax_first = -C * DELTA_T
    tax_total = tax_first / (1 - C)
    tax_stages = [tax_first * C**k for k in range(3)]
    print("tax_stage_amounts " + " ".join(str(x) for x in tax_stages))
    tax_matches = all(running_total(tax_first, C, n) == closed_form(tax_first, C, n) for n in range(1, MAX_STAGES + 1))
    print(f"tax_closed_form_matches_stages_N_1_to_{MAX_STAGES} {tax_matches}")
    tax_change = equilibrium(AUTONOMOUS, INVESTMENT, GOVERNMENT, C, DELTA_T) - y0
    print(f"tax_change {tax_change} equals_minus_c_dTax_over_1_minus_c {tax_change == tax_total}")
    balanced = (y1 - y0) + tax_change
    print(f"balanced_budget_change {balanced} equals_spending_change {balanced == DELTA_G}")


main()

Proposition 2.2 (the equilibrium multiplier)

Under A1 to A3, equilibrium output is \(Y^{\ast} = (a - c\mathrm{Tax} + I + G)/(1 - c)\). Hence a change \(\Delta G\) in government spending, with \(\mathrm{Tax}\) fixed, changes equilibrium output by \(\Delta G/(1-c)\), and the fiscal multiplier is \(1/(1-c)\).

Proof

Substitute \(C = a + c(Y - \mathrm{Tax})\) into \(Y = C + I + G\). This gives \(Y = a + cY - c\mathrm{Tax} + I + G\), so \((1 - c)Y = a - c\mathrm{Tax} + I + G\). A1 gives \(1 - c > 0\), so \(Y^{\ast}\) is the unique solution. Raising \(G\) by \(\Delta G\) and holding \(\mathrm{Tax}\) fixed changes the numerator by \(\Delta G\) and leaves the denominator unchanged. The change is \(\Delta G/(1-c)\), and the ratio of the change in output to \(\Delta G\) is \(1/(1-c)\).

Proposition 2.3 (the stages converge to the multiplier)

Under A1, for \(\Delta G\) and every \(N\) as in Proposition 2.1, \(S_N = \dfrac{\Delta G}{1-c} - \dfrac{\Delta G\,c^{N}}{1-c}\). Also \(c^{N} \le 1/(1 + N(1/c - 1))\) for every \(N \ge 1\), so \(c^{N} \to 0\) and \(S_N \to \Delta G/(1-c)\).

Proof

The identity is Proposition 2.1 with \(1/(1-c)\) split out. For the bound, put \(b = 1/c > 1\) and \(d = b - 1 > 0\). Bernoulli's inequality \(b^{N} \ge 1 + Nd\) holds for \(N = 1\). If it holds for \(N\), then \(b^{N+1} = b \cdot b^{N} \ge b(1 + Nd) = 1 + (N+1)d + Nd^{2} \ge 1 + (N+1)d\), using \(b > 0\). So it holds for every \(N \ge 1\). Hence \(c^{N} = 1/b^{N} \le 1/(1 + Nd)\), which tends to 0 as \(N\) grows. The second term of the identity therefore tends to 0, and \(S_N\) tends to \(\Delta G/(1-c)\), the equilibrium change of Proposition 2.2.

Proposition 2.4 (the tax multiplier and the balanced budget)

Under A1 to A3, a change \(\Delta\mathrm{Tax}\) in lump-sum taxes with \(G\) fixed changes equilibrium output by \(-c\,\Delta\mathrm{Tax}/(1-c)\). If \(\Delta G = \Delta\mathrm{Tax}\), the change in equilibrium output is \(\Delta G\), so the balanced-budget multiplier is 1. The tax multiplier is \(-c\) times the spending multiplier.

Proof

By Proposition 2.2 with \(G\) fixed, the change in \(Y^{\ast}\) from a change in \(\mathrm{Tax}\) is \(-c\,\Delta\mathrm{Tax}/(1-c)\), because \(\mathrm{Tax}\) enters the numerator as \(-c\mathrm{Tax}\). If \(\Delta G = \Delta\mathrm{Tax}\), the two changes add to \(\Delta G/(1-c) - c\,\Delta G/(1-c) = \Delta G(1-c)/(1-c) = \Delta G\). The ratio of the tax multiplier to the spending multiplier is \(\dfrac{-c/(1-c)}{1/(1-c)} = -c\).

Remark 2.5 (what the multiplier does not include)

Under A2 the policy rate of Lesson 7 is held fixed, and investment is a constant. The multiplier of Proposition 2.2 is therefore the goods-market value \(1/(1-c)\). Lesson 4 calls the IS–LM ratio of output to government spending the spending multiplier. There the interest rate is determined in the money market, and Lesson 4 states that this multiplier is smaller than \(1/(1-c)\) when investment responds to the interest rate and money demand responds to output. This page does not recompute the IS–LM case. It also does not compute the multiplier when a rule such as the one in Lesson 7 responds to output.

2Where the canon breaks

  • Fixed prices. A2 holds the price level fixed. The AD–AS model of Lesson 5 lets the price level respond, and this lesson does not compute that case.
  • A fixed interest rate. A2 holds the policy rate fixed. If the rate responds to the economy, the multiplier of Proposition 2.2 no longer describes the outcome, and this lesson does not compute the replacement. Remark 2.5 says what is left out.
  • Lump-sum taxes. Definition 1.1 makes taxes a fixed amount. A tax that is a share of income changes the multiplier, as Problem R2 shows for one case.
  • No leakage outside the economy. The page is closed, so no part of a stage leaves the economy. An import or saving leakage changes the stage sum, and this page does not compute it.

3Misconceptions

  1. “A multiplier of 5/2 means the first stage adds 2.5 times the spending.”

    Stage 0 adds \(\Delta G\) itself, which is 10,000 cents in Part A of Lab 1. The multiplier of 5/2 is the limit of the running total. Part A prints running totals of 10,000 and 16,000 cents after one and two stages, and Part B prints the gap to 25,000 cents, which shrinks towards zero as the stages add up.

  2. “A tax cut and a spending rise of the same size move output by the same amount.”

    A tax change multiplies by \(-c/(1-c)\), not by \(1/(1-c)\) (Proposition 2.4). With the lab's inputs, a tax increase of 10,000 cents lowers output by 15,000 cents (Part D), and a spending rise of 10,000 cents raises it by 25,000 cents (Part C). A tax cut of the same size raises output by 15,000 cents, which is less than the spending rise.

  3. “The multiplier 1/(1 - c) is an estimate of what fiscal policy does in data.”

    It is a property of the model under A1 to A3 (Proposition 2.2). This page gives no estimate of any multiplier in any economy. Whether real economies match the model is an empirical question, and this unit's registry holds no source for it. The page therefore states no empirical claim about it.

4Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · The multiplier at a marginal propensity of one half

With \(c = 1/2\) and a spending change of 40 CU, find the multiplier, the equilibrium output change, and the running total after three stages.

Solution

The multiplier is \(1/(1 - 1/2) = 2\), so the equilibrium output change is \(2 \times 40 = 80\) CU. The stages are 40, then \(40 \times 1/2 = 20\), then \(20 \times 1/2 = 10\). Their sum is 70 CU. The closed form of Proposition 2.1 gives \(40(1 - 1/8)/(1/2) = 40 \times (7/8) \times 2 = 70\) CU, which agrees.

M2 · A lump-sum tax increase

With \(c = 3/4\), find the change in equilibrium output from a lump-sum tax increase of 100 CU, with spending fixed.

Solution

Proposition 2.4 gives \(-c\,\Delta\mathrm{Tax}/(1-c) = -(3/4)(100)/(1/4) = -300\) CU. The output change is minus 300 CU.

M3 · A balanced budget

With \(c = 4/5\), a rise in government spending of 50 CU is financed by a lump-sum tax rise of 50 CU. Find the change in equilibrium output.

Solution

By Proposition 2.4 the balanced-budget multiplier is 1, so the change in output is 50 CU. Checking the two parts: the spending change alone gives \(50/(1/5) = 250\) CU, and the tax change alone gives \(-(4/5)(50)/(1/5) = -200\) CU. Their sum is 50 CU.

Proof

P1 · The stage sum for three stages

Show directly that \(1 + c + c^{2} = (1 - c^{3})/(1 - c)\) for \(c \ne 1\), and state the consequence for \(S_3\).

Solution

Multiply the left side by \(1 - c\): \((1 - c)(1 + c + c^{2}) = 1 + c + c^{2} - c - c^{2} - c^{3} = 1 - c^{3}\). Dividing by \(1 - c \ne 0\) gives the identity. With \(N = 3\), Proposition 2.1 gives \(S_3 = \Delta G(1 - c^{3})/(1-c) = \Delta G(1 + c + c^{2})\), the sum of the first three stages.

P2 · The running total increases with the stages

Under A1 and with \(\Delta G > 0\), show that \(S_{N+1} > S_N\) for every \(N \ge 1\).

Solution

By definition \(S_{N+1} - S_N = c^{N}\Delta G\). Since \(0 < c\) by A1, \(c^{N} > 0\), and \(\Delta G > 0\) gives a strictly positive difference. So \(S_{N+1} > S_N\).

P3 · The equilibrium equation has one solution

Show that under A1 the equation \(Y = a + c(Y - \mathrm{Tax}) + I + G\) has exactly one solution in \(Y\), and state where A1 is used.

Solution

The equation is linear in \(Y\) and can be written \((1 - c)Y = a - c\mathrm{Tax} + I + G\). The coefficient \(1 - c\) is positive by A1, so the equation has exactly one solution, \(Y^{\ast} = (a - c\mathrm{Tax} + I + G)/(1 - c)\). A1 is used to divide by \(1 - c\), and the condition \(0 < c < 1\) of A1 makes \(c^{N} \to 0\) in Proposition 2.3.

Empirical

E1 · The share of the multiplier total after twenty stages

Read Part B of Lab 1. What share of the multiplier total of 25,000 cents does the running total reach after 20 stages, to six decimal places?

Solution

Part B prints a gap of 0.914040 cents after 20 stages, so the running total is \(25{,}000 - 0.914040 = 24{,}999.085960\) cents. The share is \(1 - c^{20}\), because \(S_{20}/(\Delta G/(1-c)) = 1 - c^{20}\). The printed gap, divided by 25,000, gives \(c^{20} = 0.00003656\) to eight decimal places, so the share is \(1 - 0.0000365616 = 0.999963\) to six decimal places.

E2 · The tax multiplier as a share of the spending multiplier

Read Parts C and D of Lab 1. Divide the tax change printed in Part D by the spending change printed in Part C. What number do you get, and what does Proposition 2.4 predict?

Solution

Part D prints \(-15{,}000\) cents and Part C prints 25,000 cents. The ratio is \(-15000/25000 = -3/5\), which is \(-c\) with \(c = 3/5\). Proposition 2.4 predicts that the tax multiplier is \(-c\) times the spending multiplier, so the two printed changes agree with it.

E3 · The fifth stage against the total

Read Part A of Lab 1. The running total after five stages is printed as 23,056 cents. What share of the 25,000-cent total is that, and what is the exact value?

Solution

The share is \(23056/25000 = 0.92224\) exactly, which is 92.224 percent of the multiplier total. Part B of the lab prints the gap after five stages as 1,944 cents, and \(25{,}000 - 1{,}944 = 23{,}056\), which agrees.

Research

R1 · What fails at \(c = 1\)

Suppose \(c = 1\) is allowed. Find \(S_N\), and explain why Proposition 2.2 no longer gives a unique equilibrium.

Solution

At \(c = 1\) each stage adds \(\Delta G\), so \(S_N = N\Delta G\), which grows without bound as \(N\) grows. Proposition 2.1 divides by \(1 - c = 0\) and does not apply. The equilibrium equation becomes \(0 \cdot Y = a - \mathrm{Tax} + I + G\). If the right side is nonzero there is no solution, and if it is zero every \(Y\) solves the equation. Neither case gives the unique equilibrium of Proposition 2.2, and A1 excludes both.

R2 · A proportional tax

Let taxes be a share \(\tau\) of output, so that \(C = a + c(1 - \tau)Y\) with \(0 \le \tau < 1\) and \(c(1-\tau) < 1\). Find the spending multiplier, and compare it with \(1/(1-c)\) for \(c = 3/5\) and \(\tau = 1/2\).

Solution

Substituting into \(Y = C + I + G\) gives \((1 - c(1-\tau))Y = a + I + G\), so the multiplier is \(1/(1 - c(1-\tau))\). With \(c = 3/5\) and \(\tau = 1/2\), \(c(1-\tau) = 3/10\), and the multiplier is \(1/(7/10) = 10/7\). The lump-sum multiplier of Proposition 2.2 is \(5/2\). The proportional tax gives a smaller multiplier, because \(1 - c(1-\tau) > 1 - c\) whenever \(\tau > 0\).

R3 · How many stages reach ninety-nine percent

With \(c = 3/5\), find the smallest \(N\) for which the running total reaches at least 99 percent of the multiplier total.

Solution

By Proposition 2.1 the running total is a share \(1 - c^{N}\) of the multiplier total. It reaches 99 percent when \(c^{N} \le 0.01\). Here \(0.6^{9} = 0.010077696\), which is larger than 0.01, so nine stages are not enough. \(0.6^{10} = 0.0060466176\), which is at most 0.01. The answer is \(N = 10\) stages.

  9. With \(c = 3/5\), what is \(S_1\) divided by the multiplier total \(\Delta G/(1-c)\)?

    \(S_1 = \Delta G\) and the multiplier total is \(\Delta G/(1-c)\), so the ratio is \(1 - c = 2/5 = 0.4\) (Proposition 2.1 with \(N = 1\)).
