The Phillips Curve and the Natural Rate

  • Uses: the aggregate demand–aggregate supply model, its short-run supply curve in §1
  • Introduces: the Phillips curve, the natural rate of unemployment
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A Phillips curve links inflation to unemployment, and its position depends on the inflation that people expect. This lesson takes the short-run supply curve of Lesson 5 and an output–unemployment relation, and derives the curve from them. The derivation gives an exact slope that depends on the price level, and it gives the natural rate as the unemployment rate at which output is potential. Under adaptive expectations, inflation changes from one period to the next exactly when unemployment differs from the natural rate. Lab 1 checks the derivation in exact fractions and on a seeded simulated path. No empirical slope is estimated here.

1The variables, the relations and the three assumptions

Unemployment rates are decimals, so 5 percent is 0.05. The unemployment rate \(u_t\) is the share of the labour force without work in period \(t\), and the labour force is the number of people working or seeking work. Let \(P_t\) be the price level in period \(t\), an index as in Lesson 5. The inflation rate for period \(t\) is the simple rate of change of the price level over the period, \[ \pi_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}. \] With periods of one year, \(\pi_t\) is an annual rate. It is a decimal, so 0.03 is 3 percent. The expected inflation \(\pi^{e}_t\) is the inflation rate that is expected for period \(t\), formed at the end of period \(t - 1\). The expected price level for period \(t\) is \(P^{e}_t = P_{t-1}(1 + \pi^{e}_t)\).

Assumption A1 (the short-run supply curve in each period)

Output in period \(t\) is \(Y_t = \bar Y + \nu \,(P_t - P^{e}_t)\), with \(\nu > 0\), as in Assumption A2 of Lesson 5. The price level in period \(t - 1\) is positive, \(P_{t-1} > 0\).

Assumption A2 (the output–unemployment relation)

Output in period \(t\) and unemployment in period \(t\) satisfy \(Y_t - \bar Y = -\kappa\,(u_t - u^{n})\), with \(\kappa > 0\). The number \(u^{n}\) is a parameter of the model, the natural rate of unemployment defined in Definition 1.2. The slope \(\kappa\) is measured in index points per unit of the unemployment rate. The relation is a specification: this lesson does not derive it from labour-market behaviour.

Assumption A3 (adaptive expectations)

Expected inflation equals last period's inflation, \(\pi^{e}_t = \pi_{t-1}\). This is the expectation rule used in §3 and in Lab 1. Section 4 states what the rule removes.

Definition 1.1 (the Phillips curve in this lesson)

The Phillips curve of this lesson is the relation between inflation, expected inflation and unemployment that A1 and A2 imply, \(\pi_t = \pi^{e}_t - \beta_t\,(u_t - u^{n})\), with the slope \(\beta_t = \kappa/(\nu \,P_{t-1})\) defined in Proposition 2.1. The curve has one slope \(\beta_t\) for each period.

Definition 1.2 (the natural rate of unemployment)

The natural rate of unemployment \(u^{n}\) is the unemployment rate at which output equals potential output. Under A2 it is the unique unemployment rate with \(Y = \bar Y\), because \(\kappa > 0\) makes the relation strictly decreasing in \(u\).

2The Phillips curve follows from the supply curve

Proposition 2.1 (the Phillips curve)

Assume A1 and A2, with \(\nu > 0\), \(\kappa > 0\) and \(P_{t-1} > 0\). Then \[ \pi_t = \pi^{e}_t - \beta_t\,(u_t - u^{n}), \qquad \beta_t = \frac{\kappa}{\nu \,P_{t-1}} > 0. \] Holding \(P_{t-1}\) fixed, the inflation rate falls by \(\beta_t\) for each one-unit rise in \(u_t\).

Proof

Assumptions: A1 and A2, as above. A1 gives \(P_t - P^{e}_t = (Y_t - \bar Y)/\nu \), by dividing \(Y_t - \bar Y = \nu (P_t - P^{e}_t)\) by \(\nu > 0\). A2 gives \(Y_t - \bar Y = -\kappa(u_t - u^{n})\). Substituting, \(P_t = P^{e}_t - (\kappa/\nu )(u_t - u^{n})\). Subtract \(P_{t-1}\) from both sides and divide by \(P_{t-1} > 0\): \[ \pi_t = \frac{P^{e}_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} - \frac{\kappa}{\nu \,P_{t-1}}\,(u_t - u^{n}). \] The first term is \(\bigl(P_{t-1}(1 + \pi^{e}_t) - P_{t-1}\bigr)/P_{t-1} = \pi^{e}_t\). Hence \(\pi_t = \pi^{e}_t - \beta_t(u_t - u^{n})\). The slope is positive because \(\kappa > 0\), \(\nu > 0\) and \(P_{t-1} > 0\).

Remark 2.2 (the slope depends on the price level)

The slope \(\beta_t\) is the output slope \(\kappa\) divided by \(\nu \,P_{t-1}\), so it depends on last period's price level. A given gap in unemployment moves inflation by more when \(P_{t-1}\) is smaller. Lab 1 uses \(\kappa = 200\) and \(\nu = 200\), so \(\beta_t = 1/P_{t-1}\). At \(P_{t-1} = 1\) the slope is 1, and at \(P_{t-1} = 6/5\) it is \(5/6\). The slope is therefore not a fixed number across periods unless the price level is fixed.

Lab 1 · The Phillips curve from the supply curve, and the natural rate on a seeded path

The lab is SIMULATED. Its unemployment path is generated from a seeded pseudo-random draw, and it is a model path with no data behind it. The inputs are \(\nu = 200\), \(\kappa = 200\), \(u^{n} = 0.05\), \(P_0 = 6/5\) and \(\pi_0 = 0.03\). Part A predicts that the inflation rate from the price levels equals the Phillips form, 13/600, or 0.021667, at \(u = 0.06\). Part B predicts that the identity \(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} - \beta_t(u_t - u^{n})\) holds in each of 12 periods, with the inflation change equal to \(-\beta_t\) times the gap. It also predicts that inflation does not change in a period only when the gap is zero. Part C predicts that the one-period change is zero at \(u^{n}\), and that it is positive below and negative above. The code uses exact fractions and a fixed seed, so the output is fixed.

"""E5 L06 lab 1: the expectations-augmented Phillips curve, derived from the AS curve, on a seeded SIMULATED path.

SIMULATED: the unemployment path is generated from a seeded pseudo-random draw (random.Random(seed)). It is a
model path with no data behind it. The unemployment rate, inflation and the price level are model quantities.

Model (Lesson 6, Assumptions A1 to A3): short-run AS is Y = Ybar + nu (P - Pe) with nu = 200 (Lesson 5); the linear
output-unemployment relation Y - Ybar = -kappa (u - un) with kappa = 200 and natural rate un = 5 percent; expected
inflation is last period's inflation, pe_t = pi_(t-1) (adaptive expectations); inflation is the simple per-period rate
pi_t = (P_t - P_(t-1)) / P_(t-1). Unemployment rates are decimals, so 5 percent is 0.05.

Part A: one period. The price level and inflation from the AS and output-unemployment relations, against
the Phillips form pi_t = pe_t - beta_t (u_t - un) with beta_t = kappa / (nu P_(t-1)).
Part B: a seeded path of 12 periods. Each period checks the identity pi_t = pi_(t-1) - beta_t (u_t - un) exactly.
Part C: the one-period inflation change on a grid of unemployment rates at P_(t-1) = 6/5, where the change is
zero at the natural rate.

Every value is an exact fraction. A printed decimal is the exact fraction rounded half to even at six decimal
places.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from fractions import Fraction

YBAR = Fraction(1000)
NU = Fraction(200)
KAPPA = Fraction(200)
UN = Fraction(5, 100)
SEED = 20261028
PERIODS = 12
P_START = Fraction(6, 5)
PI_START = Fraction(3, 100)


def fmt(x: Fraction) -> str:
    """The exact value rounded half to even at six decimal places, with a minus sign where needed."""
    scaled = round(x * 10**6)
    sign = "-" if scaled < 0 else ""
    whole, frac = divmod(abs(scaled), 10**6)
    return f"{sign}{whole}.{frac:06d}"


def beta(price_prev: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The Phillips slope kappa / (nu P_(t-1)) for the previous price level, a positive number."""
    return KAPPA / (NU * price_prev)


def next_price(pe: Fraction, u: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The price level from AS and the output-unemployment relation.

    P = pe + (Y - Ybar) / nu, where Y - Ybar = -kappa (u - un).
    """
    return pe - KAPPA * (u - UN) / NU


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A, B and C. Every line is computed from the exact fractions and the seeded draws."""
    print("Part A: one period from the AS and output-unemployment relations, against the Phillips form")
    u_a = Fraction(6, 100)
    pe_a = P_START * (1 + PI_START)
    p_a = next_price(pe_a, u_a)
    pi_levels = (p_a - P_START) / P_START
    pi_form = PI_START - beta(P_START) * (u_a - UN)
    print(f"expected_price {fmt(pe_a)} price {fmt(p_a)}")
    print(f"inflation_from_levels {fmt(pi_levels)} inflation_from_phillips {fmt(pi_form)}")
    print(f"beta {fmt(beta(P_START))} identity_holds {pi_levels == pi_form}")

    print("Part B: seeded SIMULATED path of 12 periods, adaptive expectations")
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    price, pi = P_START, PI_START
    zero_gap = 0
    no_change = 0
    all_hold = True
    no_change_only_at_zero_gap = True
    for t in range(1, PERIODS + 1):
        draw = rng.randint(-100, 100)
        gap = Fraction(draw, 10000)
        u = UN + gap
        pe = price * (1 + pi)
        new_price = next_price(pe, u)
        new_pi = (new_price - price) / price
        form = pi - beta(price) * (u - UN)
        hold = new_pi == form
        all_hold = all_hold and hold
        zero_gap += gap == 0
        no_change += new_pi == pi
        no_change_only_at_zero_gap = no_change_only_at_zero_gap and (new_pi != pi or gap == 0)
        print(
            f"t {t} unemployment_gap_bp {draw} unemployment {fmt(u * 100)} percent "
            f"inflation {fmt(new_pi)} change {fmt(new_pi - pi)} identity {hold}"
        )
        price, pi = new_price, new_pi
    print(f"identity_every_period {all_hold}")
    print(f"zero_gap_periods {zero_gap} no_change_periods {no_change}")
    print(f"no_change_only_at_natural_rate {no_change_only_at_zero_gap}")

    print("Part C: one-period inflation change at P(t-1) = 6/5, by unemployment rate")
    slope = beta(P_START)
    print(f"slope_at_price_6_5 {fmt(slope)}")
    signs_ok = True
    for step in range(-4, 5):
        u = UN + Fraction(step, 200)
        change = -slope * (u - UN)
        if step < 0:
            signs_ok = signs_ok and change > 0
        elif step > 0:
            signs_ok = signs_ok and change < 0
        else:
            signs_ok = signs_ok and change == 0
        print(f"unemployment {fmt(u * 100)} percent change {fmt(change)}")
    print(f"change_positive_below_un_zero_at_un_negative_above {signs_ok}")

3The natural rate and the path of inflation

Proposition 3.1 (inflation equals expected inflation at the natural rate)

Assume A1 and A2, with \(\nu > 0\), \(\kappa > 0\) and \(P_{t-1} > 0\). Then \(\pi_t = \pi^{e}_t\) if and only if \(u_t = u^{n}\), if and only if \(Y_t = \bar Y\).

Proof

Assumptions: A1 and A2 with the stated signs. By Proposition 2.1, \(\pi_t - \pi^{e}_t = -\beta_t(u_t - u^{n})\), and \(\beta_t > 0\). The product of a positive number and \(u_t - u^{n}\) is zero if and only if \(u_t = u^{n}\). So \(\pi_t = \pi^{e}_t\) if and only if \(u_t = u^{n}\). For the last equivalence, A2 gives \(Y_t - \bar Y = -\kappa(u_t - u^{n})\), which is zero if and only if \(u_t = u^{n}\), because \(\kappa > 0\).

Corollary 3.2 (the change of inflation under adaptive expectations)

Assume A1, A2 and A3. Then \(\pi_t - \pi_{t-1} = -\beta_t\,(u_t - u^{n})\) for every period \(t\), and \(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1}\) if and only if \(u_t = u^{n}\).

Proof

Assumptions: A1, A2 and A3, with the stated signs. Under A3, \(\pi^{e}_t = \pi_{t-1}\), so Proposition 2.1 reads \(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} - \beta_t(u_t - u^{n})\). Subtracting \(\pi_{t-1}\) from both sides gives the change. The change is zero exactly when \((u_t - u^{n}) = 0\), because \(\beta_t > 0\).

Remark 3.3 (what the natural rate is, and is not, in this model)

In this model \(u^{n}\) is the unemployment rate at which output is potential, by Definition 1.2. It is a parameter of A2 and the model does not say how it is measured. Proposition 3.1 says that the natural rate is the only unemployment rate at which the curve gives inflation equal to expected inflation. A rate below \(u^{n}\) is not a floor, as §5 explains.

4Where the canon breaks

  • Adaptive expectations are a specification. A3 sets expected inflation to last period's inflation. Under this rule Corollary 3.2 gives the one-period change in inflation, and this lesson describes no other dynamics. An expectation rule that uses other information gives a different relation, and this lesson does not analyse one.
  • There is no supply shock. Proposition 2.1 has no term for shocks to supply. With a constant shock \(v\) added to the curve and A3 kept, the unemployment rate that keeps inflation constant moves from \(u^{n}\) to \(u^{n} + v/\beta\). This extension is Problem L06-R2, outside the model of this lesson.
  • The slope is not a constant parameter. Remark 2.2 shows that \(\beta_t\) depends on \(P_{t-1}\). A specification that treats \(\beta\) as a fixed number is an additional assumption, and this lesson does not make it.
  • The natural rate is a parameter, and no estimate is given. Definition 1.2 defines \(u^{n}\) through the model, and no measured value of it appears on this page. Any empirical claim about the slope or the natural rate would need counter-evidence that this lesson does not cite.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Unemployment below the natural rate can be kept forever with inflation stable.”

    Under A3 the change in inflation is \(-\beta_t(u_t - u^{n})\), by Corollary 3.2. A gap of \(u_t - u^{n} = -0.01\) at \(\beta_t = 5/6\) changes inflation by \(+0.008333\) in one period (exactly \(1/120\), shown to six decimal places), so inflation is not stable. Lab 1 Part C prints the change at 4 percent, which is 0.008333, and Part B prints a non-zero change in each of its 12 periods.

  2. “The natural rate is the lowest unemployment rate the economy can reach.”

    In the model, \(u^{n}\) is the point where output is potential (Definition 1.2), and nothing in A1 or A2 makes it a lower bound. Lab 1 Part C prints a change at 3 percent, below the natural rate, so the model's curve is defined there and gives an inflation rise of 0.016667 (exactly \(1/60\), shown to six decimal places).

  3. “The Phillips curve is a fixed menu, so a policy can choose any pair of inflation and unemployment.”

    Proposition 2.1 gives the curve at a given expected inflation \(\pi^{e}_t\). The curve's position depends on \(\pi^{e}_t\), so a change in expected inflation moves it. Under A3 the expected inflation of period \(t\) is fixed by period \(t - 1\), so one period's curve offers no free choice of inflation at a given unemployment rate.

6Problem set

Mechanics

L06-M1 · The slope and the inflation rate with \(\kappa = 100\)

Let \(\kappa = 100\), \(\nu = 200\) and \(P_{t-1} = 1\). Find \(\beta_t\). Then find \(\pi_t\) when \(\pi^{e}_t = 0.04\) and \(u_t - u^{n} = 0.02\).

Solution

Proposition 2.1 gives \(\beta_t = 100/(200 \times 1) = 0.5\). Then \(\pi_t = 0.04 - 0.5 \times 0.02 = 0.03\), which is 3 percent. The unemployment gap is positive, so inflation is below its expected rate.

L06-M2 · A negative gap at a higher price level

Let \(\kappa = 200\), \(\nu = 200\) and \(P_{t-1} = 1.25\). Find \(\beta_t\), and find \(\pi_t\) when \(\pi^{e}_t = 0.04\) and \(u_t - u^{n} = -0.01\).

Solution

Proposition 2.1 gives \(\beta_t = 200/(200 \times 1.25) = 0.8\). Then \(\pi_t = 0.04 - 0.8 \times (-0.01) = 0.048\), which is 4.8 percent. The gap is negative, so inflation is above its expected rate by 0.008.

L06-M3 · Inflation with and without a gap under adaptive expectations

Let \(\pi_{t-1} = 0.02\), \(\beta_t = 0.5\), and compare \(\pi_t\) at \(u_t = u^{n}\) and at \(u_t = u^{n} + 0.03\), using A3.

Solution

Under A3, \(\pi^{e}_t = \pi_{t-1} = 0.02\). At \(u_t = u^{n}\), \(\pi_t = 0.02 - 0.5 \times 0 = 0.02\), so inflation does not change. At \(u_t = u^{n} + 0.03\), \(\pi_t = 0.02 - 0.5 \times 0.03 = 0.005\), so inflation falls by 0.015 in one period.

Proof

L06-P1 · The slope of the curve in unemployment

Under A1 and A2, with \(P_{t-1}\), \(\pi^{e}_t\) and \(\nu \) held fixed, show that \(\partial\pi_t/\partial u_t = -\beta_t\), and state its sign.

Solution

Proposition 2.1 writes \(\pi_t = \pi^{e}_t - \beta_t(u_t - u^{n})\). With \(\pi^{e}_t\), \(\beta_t\) and \(u^{n}\) fixed, the expression is linear in \(u_t\) with coefficient \(-\beta_t\). So the derivative is \(-\beta_t\). Since \(\beta_t = \kappa/(\nu P_{t-1}) > 0\), the derivative is negative: a higher unemployment rate lowers inflation, for given expectations.

L06-P2 · Inflation equals expected inflation exactly when output is potential

Under A1 and A2, show that \(\pi_t = \pi^{e}_t\) if and only if \(Y_t = \bar Y\).

Solution

Both inflation rates use the same denominator, since \(\pi_t = (P_t - P_{t-1})/P_{t-1}\) and \(\pi^{e}_t = (P^{e}_t - P_{t-1})/P_{t-1}\). Hence \(\pi_t = \pi^{e}_t\) if and only if \(P_t = P^{e}_t\). By A1, \(P_t = P^{e}_t\) if and only if \(Y_t - \bar Y = \nu (P_t - P^{e}_t) = 0\), which is \(Y_t = \bar Y\). The last step uses \(\nu > 0\), so that the supply relation has no other solution.

L06-P3 · The natural rate is unique

Suppose that an unemployment rate \(\tilde u\) satisfies \(Y = \bar Y\) under A2. Show that \(\tilde u = u^{n}\).

Solution

Under A2, \(Y - \bar Y = -\kappa(\tilde u - u^{n})\). The condition \(Y = \bar Y\) makes this zero, so \(\kappa(\tilde u - u^{n}) = 0\). Since \(\kappa > 0\), \(\tilde u = u^{n}\). So every unemployment rate at which output is potential equals \(u^{n}\).

Empirical

L06-E1 · Checking the 4 percent row of Part C

Lab 1 Part C prints a change of 0.008333 at 4 percent. Confirm the value from Proposition 2.1 at \(\beta = 5/6\).

Solution

The change is \(-\beta(u - u^{n}) = -(5/6)(0.04 - 0.05) = (5/6)(0.01) = 1/120 = 0.008333\) to six decimal places. The printed value agrees, and the sign is positive because 4 percent is below the natural rate.

L06-E2 · The signs in the seeded path

Using only the twelve printed lines of Part B, count the periods with a positive gap, and state whether the inflation change has the opposite sign to the gap in each of them.

Solution

The printed gaps are positive in periods 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 11 and 12, which is 9 periods. Their printed changes are all negative. The gaps are negative in periods 6, 9 and 10, which is 3 periods, and their changes are all positive. In all twelve printed periods the change has the opposite sign to the gap, as Corollary 3.2 requires.

L06-E3 · Period 1 of the path, from the Phillips slope

Period 1 of Part B has a gap of 58 basis points and a printed change of \(-0.004833\). The previous price level is \(P_0 = 6/5\). Confirm the printed change from \(\beta_1 = \kappa/(\nu P_0)\), and explain why it differs from the 5.5 percent row of Part C.

Solution

The slope is \(\beta_1 = 200/(200 \times 6/5) = 5/6\). The gap is \(58/10000 = 0.0058\), so the change is \(-(5/6)(0.0058) = -0.0048333\), which prints as \(-0.004833\). The 5.5 percent row of Part C has a gap of 0.005, so its change is \(-(5/6)(0.005) = -0.004167\). The two rows differ because their gaps differ (0.0058 and 0.005), not because their slopes differ.

Research

L06-R1 · The slope at two price levels

With \(\kappa = \nu = 200\), compare the slope \(\beta\) at \(P_{t-1} = 1\) and at \(P_{t-1} = 6/5\), and state the ratio of the two slopes.

Solution

At \(P_{t-1} = 1\), \(\beta = 200/(200 \times 1) = 1\). At \(P_{t-1} = 6/5\), \(\beta = 5/6\). The ratio of the first slope to the second is \(1/(5/6) = 6/5\). This equals the ratio of the second price level to the first, \(1.2/1\), because \(\beta\) is proportional to \(1/P_{t-1}\).

L06-R2 · An extension with a supply shock (outside the model of this lesson)

Suppose the curve is \(\pi_t = \pi^{e}_t - \beta(u_t - u^{n}) + v\) with a constant \(v = 0.01\), and A3 holds. Find the unemployment rate at which inflation does not change, with \(\beta = 5/6\). This extension is not in the model of §2 and is worked here as an extension.

Solution

Under A3, inflation does not change when \(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1}\), which with \(\pi^{e}_t = \pi_{t-1}\) requires \(-\beta(u_t - u^{n}) + v = 0\). So \(u_t = u^{n} + v/\beta = u^{n} + 0.01/(5/6) = 0.05 + 0.012 = 0.062\), which is 6.2 percent. The natural rate no longer marks the point of constant inflation when \(v \ne 0\).

L06-R3 · What an estimate of the slope would need

Name the data and the modelling choices that an empirical estimate of \(\beta\) would need, given the model of this lesson. Give no numerical value.

Solution

An estimate needs a measured inflation series and a measured unemployment series on the same periods. It needs a measure of expected inflation, which this lesson does not provide, since A3 is a specification and not a measurement. It needs a way to separate supply shocks from the gap, because Proposition 2.1 has no shock term. It also needs a choice for the price level in \(\beta_t = \kappa/(\nu P_{t-1})\), since the slope depends on it. The lesson gives no estimate, and any claim about the size of \(\beta\) would need counter-evidence cited in the registry, which this lesson does not yet have.

  1. In this lesson's model, what is the natural rate of unemployment?

    Options

    Definition 1.2 defines the natural rate as the unemployment rate with \(Y = \bar Y\). It is not a floor (§5).

  2. With \(\kappa = 200\) and \(\nu = 200\), what is the Phillips slope \(\beta\) when \(P_{t-1} = 1\)?

    \(\beta = \kappa/(\nu P_{t-1}) = 200/(200 \times 1) = 1\) (Remark 2.2).

  3. When unemployment is above the natural rate, what is the sign of the one-period inflation change under A3?

    Options

  4. With \(\beta = 5/6\), what is the one-period inflation change when unemployment is 4 percent and the natural rate is 5 percent? Give the decimal to six places.

    \(-\beta(u - u^{n}) = -(5/6)(0.04 - 0.05) = 0.008333\) (Part C of Lab 1).

  5. Under A3, what is expected inflation for period \(t\)?
