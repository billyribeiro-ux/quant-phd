The Solow growth model

  • Uses: growth accounting, mathematical induction, partial derivatives, the implicit-function theorem
  • Introduces: Solow growth model
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore

L02 split output growth into input growth and a residual. The Solow model asks what capital per worker does when saving is a fixed share of output. This lesson proves that the recursion has exactly one positive long-run level of capital per worker, gives it in closed form, and proves convergence to it from every positive start. It derives how that level responds to saving and depreciation, by the implicit-function theorem. Lab 1 checks the closed form, one path, the sign lemma on a grid and the derivatives; it does not check Remark 2.5 (ii) or the uniqueness in Proposition 2.2. Section 5 names what the one-sector model without technological progress removes.

1The capital recursion

A period is the model's unit of time, and no calendar length is assigned to it. Let \(K_t\) be the capital stock at the start of period \(t\), and let \(L_t\) be labour, the input of L02. Output is \(Y_t\). The model uses \(Y_t = K_t^{\alpha} L_t^{1-\alpha}\) with the technology level fixed at 1, so that output is the Cobb–Douglas function of capital and labour that L02 introduces. Let \(s\) be the share of output saved, \(\delta\) the share of the capital stock that depreciates in a period, and \(n\) the growth rate of labour, so that \(L_{t+1} = (1+n)L_t\).

Definition 1.1 (the Solow growth model)

Capital accumulates by \(K_{t+1} = (1-\delta)K_t + sY_t\). The Solow growth model is this accumulation with labour growing at rate \(n\), written per worker. Let \(k_t = K_t/L_t\) be capital per worker. The model is the recursion \[ k_{t+1} = g(k_t), \qquad g(k) = \frac{s\,k^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)\,k}{1+n}, \] where \(f(k) = k^{\alpha}\) is output per worker. The recursion is the object of this lesson. Its economic reading is a closed economy with a constant saving rate, and this lesson does not derive that rate from any choice.

Assumptions (stated for every result on this page)
  • A1. \(0 < \alpha < 1\), and the technology level is 1 in every period.
  • A2. \(0 < s < 1\), and the saving rate is the same in every period.
  • A3. \(0 < \delta < 1\) and \(n + \delta > 0\). Since \(\delta < 1\), \(n > -\delta > -1\), so \(1 + n > 0\).
  • A4. \(k_0 > 0\).
Derivation 1.2 (from the stock to the per-worker recursion)

Divide \(K_{t+1} = (1-\delta)K_t + sK_t^{\alpha}L_t^{1-\alpha}\) by \(L_{t+1} = (1+n)L_t\). The left side becomes \(K_{t+1}/L_{t+1} = k_{t+1}\). On the right, \(K_t/L_{t+1} = k_t/(1+n)\) and \(K_t^{\alpha}L_t^{1-\alpha}/L_{t+1} = (K_t/L_t)^{\alpha}/(1+n) = k_t^{\alpha}/(1+n)\), because \(K_t^{\alpha}L_t^{1-\alpha}/L_t = (K_t/L_t)^{\alpha}\). Hence \(k_{t+1} = \bigl(s k_t^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)k_t\bigr)/(1+n) = g(k_t)\). This step uses only the production function, the labour growth rule and the definitions, so it is grade T.

Remark 1.3 (capital stays positive)

Under A1 to A3, \(g(k) > 0\) for every \(k > 0\). The term \(s k^{\alpha}\) is positive, the term \((1-\delta)k\) is positive because \(\delta < 1\), and \(1+n > 0\) by A3. So A4 keeps every \(k_t\) in \((0, \infty)\), where the power \(k^{\alpha}\) is defined, and the recursion can be iterated indefinitely.

2The steady state and its closed form

A steady state is a fixed point of the recursion. The term is used in this lesson only with the meaning of Definition 2.1.

Definition 2.1 (steady state)

A steady state of the recursion is a number \(k^{\ast} > 0\) with \(g(k^{\ast}) = k^{\ast}\).

Proposition 2.2 (the positive steady state)

Under A1 to A3, the recursion has exactly one steady state. It is \[ k^{\ast} = \left(\frac{s}{n+\delta}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}. \]

Proof

Let \(k > 0\). Then \(g(k) = k\) if and only if \((1+n)k = s k^{\alpha} + (1-\delta)k\). Since \((1+n) - (1-\delta) = n + \delta\), this holds if and only if \((n+\delta)k = s k^{\alpha}\). Divide by \(k > 0\) to get \(n + \delta = s k^{\alpha-1}\), and multiply by \(k^{1-\alpha} > 0\) to get \((n+\delta)k^{1-\alpha} = s\). By A3, \(n + \delta > 0\), so the condition is \(k^{1-\alpha} = s/(n+\delta)\). The right side is positive by A2 and A3. The map \(x \mapsto x^{1-\alpha}\) is a strictly increasing bijection of \((0, \infty)\) onto itself, because \(1-\alpha > 0\) by A1, and its inverse is \(x \mapsto x^{1/(1-\alpha)}\). So \(k = (s/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}\) is the only positive solution. Every step is an equivalence, so no other positive \(k\) satisfies \(g(k) = k\).

Remark 2.3 (zero is also a fixed point)

By A1, \(0^{\alpha} = 0\), so \(g(0) = 0\), and zero is a fixed point of \(g\). Proposition 2.2 concerns positive fixed points only. A4 excludes zero, because the recursion keeps capital at zero once capital is zero. This is the reason A4 is stated.

Example 2.4 (illustrative parameters)

Take \(\alpha = 0.3\), \(s = 0.2\), \(\delta = 0.05\) and \(n = 0.01\). Then \(n + \delta = 0.06\), \(s/(n+\delta) = 10/3\), and the exponent is \(1/(1-0.3) = 10/7\), so \(k^{\ast} = (10/3)^{10/7}\). Lab 1 Part A prints \(k^{\ast} = 5.5843115042\) and output per worker at the steady state \((k^{\ast})^{0.3} = 1.6752934513\), both to ten decimal places. The parameters are illustrative and are not estimates for any economy.

Remark 2.5 (what the steady state implies)

(i) If \(k_t = k^{\ast}\), then \(k_{t+1} = g(k^{\ast}) = k^{\ast}\), so capital per worker stays at \(k^{\ast}\), and output per worker \(f(k^{\ast}) = (k^{\ast})^{\alpha}\) is constant. (ii) Output is \(Y_t = K_t^{\alpha}L_t^{1-\alpha} = L_t\,(K_t/L_t)^{\alpha} = L_t\,k_t^{\alpha}\). At the steady state, \(Y_{t+1}/Y_t = (L_{t+1}/L_t)(k_{t+1}/k_t)^{\alpha} = 1+n\). So aggregate output grows at rate \(n\) per period while output per worker is constant. Both claims are derived from the definitions and Proposition 2.2 (grade T).

3Convergence from every positive starting value

Proposition 3.1 (monotone convergence)

Under A1 to A4, the sequence \((k_t)\) converges to \(k^{\ast}\), and its direction is set by the starting value. If \(k_0 < k^{\ast}\), then \(k_0 < k_1 < k_2 < \cdots < k^{\ast}\). If \(k_0 > k^{\ast}\), then \(k^{\ast} < \cdots < k_2 < k_1 < k_0\). If \(k_0 = k^{\ast}\), then \(k_t = k^{\ast}\) for every \(t\).

Proof

Step 1, the sign lemma. For \(k > 0\), let \(D(k) = g(k) - k = \bigl(s k^{\alpha} - (n+\delta)k\bigr)/(1+n)\). Since \(1+n > 0\), the sign of \(D(k)\) is the sign of \(s k^{\alpha} - (n+\delta)k\). Dividing by \(k > 0\), this is positive if and only if \(s k^{\alpha-1} > n + \delta\), and multiplying by \(k^{1-\alpha} > 0\), if and only if \(s > (n+\delta)k^{1-\alpha}\), that is, \(k^{1-\alpha} < s/(n+\delta)\). The map \(x \mapsto x^{1-\alpha}\) is strictly increasing on \((0,\infty)\), so this holds if and only if \(k < k^{\ast}\). The same argument with the inequalities reversed gives \(D(k) < 0\) if and only if \(k > k^{\ast}\). So \(D(k) > 0\) for \(0 < k < k^{\ast}\), and \(D(k) < 0\) for \(k > k^{\ast}\).

Step 2, monotonicity of \(g\). Let \(0 < a < b\). Since \(\alpha > 0\), \(a^{\alpha} < b^{\alpha}\). Since \(s > 0\) and \(1-\delta > 0\), \(g(a) < g(b)\). So \(g\) is strictly increasing on \((0,\infty)\). It is also continuous there, because it is built from the powers and linear terms.

Step 3, the case \(0 < k_0 < k^{\ast}\). We show by induction on \(t\) that \(0 < k_t < k^{\ast}\) and \(k_{t+1} > k_t\). The base case \(t = 0\) is the assumption. Suppose \(0 < k_t < k^{\ast}\). By Step 1, \(D(k_t) > 0\), so \(k_{t+1} > k_t > 0\). By Step 2 and \(g(k^{\ast}) = k^{\ast}\), \(k_{t+1} = g(k_t) < g(k^{\ast}) = k^{\ast}\). This completes the induction. The sequence is increasing and bounded above by \(k^{\ast}\), so by the monotone convergence theorem for real sequences it converges to some \(\bar k\) with \(k_0 \le \bar k \le k^{\ast}\), and \(\bar k > 0\). Since \(g\) is continuous at \(\bar k\), \(\bar k = \lim k_{t+1} = \lim g(k_t) = g(\bar k)\). So \(\bar k\) is a positive fixed point, and \(\bar k = k^{\ast}\) by Proposition 2.2.

Step 4, the case \(k_0 > k^{\ast}\). The same induction, with the inequalities reversed, uses \(D(k_t) < 0\) and Step 2 to show \(k^{\ast} < k_{t+1} < k_t\). The sequence is decreasing and bounded below by \(k^{\ast}\), and its limit is a positive fixed point, so it is \(k^{\ast}\).

Step 5, the case \(k_0 = k^{\ast}\). Then \(g(k^{\ast}) = k^{\ast}\), so the sequence is constant. Together the cases cover every \(k_0 > 0\), which is A4.

Remark 3.2 (what the proof does not give)

Proposition 3.1 gives monotone convergence and no rate. Lab 1 Part B prints the gap to \(k^{\ast}\) at selected periods and the first period at which the gap is below \(10^{-12}\). That is one parameter set, so it illustrates the proposition and does not establish a rate of convergence.

4Comparative statics by the implicit-function theorem

The steady state depends on the parameters \(s\), \(\delta\) and \(n\). Its derivatives follow from the implicit-function theorem, which the curriculum assigns to M2 (implicit-function theorem). This page uses the statement given below, and does not reprove it.

Fact 4.1 (implicit-function theorem, statement used here, not proved on this page)

Let \(H\) be continuously differentiable near \((x_0, \theta_0)\), with \(H(x_0, \theta_0) = 0\) and \(\partial H/\partial x \neq 0\) at \((x_0, \theta_0)\). Then near \(\theta_0\) there is a continuously differentiable function \(x(\theta)\) with \(x(\theta_0) = x_0\) and \(H(x(\theta), \theta) = 0\), and \(x'(\theta_0) = -\bigl(\partial H/\partial \theta\bigr)/\bigl(\partial H/\partial x\bigr)\) at \((x_0, \theta_0)\).

Proposition 4.2 (derivatives of the steady state)

Under A1 to A3 with \(0 < s < 1\), \(0 < \delta < 1\) and \(n + \delta > 0\), the steady state \(k^{\ast}(s, \delta, n)\) of Proposition 2.2 is differentiable in \(s\), \(\delta\) and \(n\), and \[ \frac{\partial k^{\ast}}{\partial s} = \frac{k^{\ast}}{(1-\alpha)\,s}, \qquad \frac{\partial k^{\ast}}{\partial \delta} = \frac{\partial k^{\ast}}{\partial n} = -\frac{k^{\ast}}{(1-\alpha)(n+\delta)}. \]

Proof

Fix \(n\). Let \(H(k, s, \delta) = s k^{\alpha} - (n+\delta)k\) for \(k > 0\). The proof of Proposition 2.2 shows that, for \(k > 0\), \(H(k, s, \delta) = 0\) if and only if \(k = k^{\ast}(s, \delta, n)\), with the parameters in place of the stated ones. The partial derivatives are \[ H_k = s\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - (n+\delta), \qquad H_s = k^{\alpha}, \qquad H_\delta = H_n = -k. \] At the steady state, \(n + \delta = s(k^{\ast})^{\alpha-1}\), so \(H_k = s\alpha (k^{\ast})^{\alpha-1} - s(k^{\ast})^{\alpha-1} = -(1-\alpha)(n+\delta)\). This is nonzero, because \(1-\alpha > 0\) and \(n + \delta > 0\). Fact 4.1 gives a continuously differentiable \(\kappa(s)\) near \(s_0\) with \(H(\kappa(s), s, \delta) = 0\) and \(\kappa(s_0) = k^{\ast}\). Shrinking the neighbourhood keeps \(\kappa(s) > 0\), so by the equivalence above \(\kappa(s) = k^{\ast}(s, \delta, n)\). Hence \(k^{\ast}\) is differentiable in \(s\), and \[ \frac{\partial k^{\ast}}{\partial s} = -\frac{H_s}{H_k} = \frac{(k^{\ast})^{\alpha}}{(1-\alpha)(n+\delta)} = \frac{k^{\ast}}{(1-\alpha)s}, \] where the last step uses \(n + \delta = s(k^{\ast})^{\alpha-1}\), so that \((k^{\ast})^{\alpha}/(n+\delta) = k^{\ast}/s\). The same argument with \(H_\delta = H_n = -k^{\ast}\) gives \[ \frac{\partial k^{\ast}}{\partial \delta} = \frac{\partial k^{\ast}}{\partial n} = -\frac{H_\delta}{H_k} = -\frac{k^{\ast}}{(1-\alpha)(n+\delta)}. \] The two derivatives in the statement are therefore equal, because \(H\) depends on \(n\) and \(\delta\) only through \(n + \delta\).

Remark 4.3 (the elasticity with respect to saving)

From Proposition 4.2, \((s/k^{\ast})\,\partial k^{\ast}/\partial s = 1/(1-\alpha)\). This is the elasticity of the steady state with respect to the saving rate, and it does not depend on \(s\), \(\delta\) or \(n\). At \(\alpha = 0.3\) it is \(10/7\). Lab 1 Part D prints \(1.4285714286\) for this elasticity, and it also prints the two derivatives at the parameters of Example 2.4.

Lab 1 · The recursion, the steady state and its derivatives

The inputs are illustrative parameters, not data: \(\alpha = 0.3\), \(s = 0.2\), \(\delta = 0.05\), \(n = 0.01\), and \(k_0 = 1\). Before running, the lab is expected to show five things. Part A gives the closed-form steady state and a residual of zero. Part B shows a path that stays ordered on the side of \(k^{\ast}\) on which it starts and reaches a gap below \(10^{-12}\) at some period (Proposition 3.1). Part C finds no grid point where the sign of \(g(k) - k\) differs from the side of \(k^{\ast}\) on which the point lies (the sign lemma). Part D shows that the implicit-function derivatives in \(s\) and \(\delta\) agree with central differences (Proposition 4.2). Part E shows that the derivative in \(n\) equals the derivative in \(\delta\). The code uses no random draws, so its output is fixed.

"""E5 L03 lab 1: the Solow capital recursion, its steady state, and the comparative statics of the steady state.

Inputs are illustrative parameters, not data: output per worker y = k^alpha with alpha = 0.3, a saving rate
s = 0.2, a depreciation rate delta = 0.05 per period, a population growth rate n = 0.01 per period, and an
initial capital stock per worker k_0 = 1. Every quantity is a Decimal computed at 60 significant digits, so each
run prints the same lines. No random draws are used.

Part A: the closed-form steady state k* = (s/(n + delta))^(1/(1 - alpha)), and the residual of the recursion at k*.
Part B: the recursion k_{t+1} = (s k_t^alpha + (1 - delta) k_t)/(1 + n) iterated for 1000 periods, its gap to k*,
and the first period at which the gap is below 1e-12.
Part C: the sign of g(k) - k on a grid of 50 capital values, against the side of k* on which each value lies.
Part D: the derivatives dk*/ds and dk*/ddelta from the implicit-function theorem, against central differences
with step 1e-10.
Part E: the derivative of k* with respect to the population growth rate n, against its derivative with respect to
delta.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from decimal import Decimal, getcontext

getcontext().prec = 60

ALPHA = Decimal("0.3")
SAVING = Decimal("0.2")
DEPRECIATION = Decimal("0.05")
GROWTH = Decimal("0.01")
K0 = Decimal(1)
PERIODS = 1000
STEP = Decimal("1e-10")
TOLERANCE = Decimal("1e-12")
REPORT_PERIODS = (0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000)


def power(base: Decimal, exponent: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """The positive number base^exponent, for base > 0."""
    return base**exponent


def steady_state(alpha: Decimal, saving: Decimal, depreciation: Decimal, growth: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """The positive steady state k* = (s/(n + delta))^(1/(1 - alpha)) of the recursion, in capital per worker."""
    return power(saving / (growth + depreciation), 1 / (1 - alpha))


def step(k: Decimal, alpha: Decimal, saving: Decimal, depreciation: Decimal, growth: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """One period of the recursion, g(k) = (s k^alpha + (1 - delta) k)/(1 + n), for capital per worker k > 0."""
    return (saving * power(k, alpha) + (1 - depreciation) * k) / (1 + growth)


def part_a(kstar: Decimal) -> None:
    """Print the closed-form steady state and the residual of the recursion at it."""
    print("Part A: closed-form steady state")
    print(f"steady_state_capital {kstar:.10f}")
    print(f"output_per_worker_at_steady_state {power(kstar, ALPHA):.10f}")
    residual = step(kstar, ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION, GROWTH) - kstar
    print(f"residual_at_steady_state {residual:.3e}")


def part_b(kstar: Decimal) -> None:
    """Iterate the recursion from k_0, print its gap to k*, and test the ordering of successive iterates."""
    print("Part B: recursion from k_0 against the steady state")
    k = K0
    ordered = True
    first_within = -1
    if abs(k - kstar) < TOLERANCE:
        first_within = 0
    print(f"period 0 capital {k:.10f} gap {kstar - k:.10f}")
    for t in range(1, PERIODS + 1):
        nxt = step(k, ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION, GROWTH)
        if k < kstar:
            ordered = ordered and k < nxt < kstar
        elif k > kstar:
            ordered = ordered and kstar < nxt < k
        k = nxt
        if first_within < 0 and abs(k - kstar) < TOLERANCE:
            first_within = t
        if t in REPORT_PERIODS:
            print(f"period {t} capital {k:.10f} gap {kstar - k:.10f}")
    print(f"iterates_move_toward_steady_state {ordered}")
    print(f"first_period_gap_below_tolerance {first_within}")


def part_c(kstar: Decimal) -> None:
    """Check the sign of g(k) - k on a grid of 50 capital values against the side of k* each value lies on."""
    print("Part C: sign of g(k) - k on a grid of 50 capital values")
    grid = [Decimal(i) / 5 for i in range(1, 51)]
    below = 0
    mismatches = 0
    for k in grid:
        nxt = step(k, ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION, GROWTH)
        if k < kstar:
            below += 1
        if (nxt > k) != (k < kstar):
            mismatches += 1
    print(f"grid_points_below_steady_state {below}")
    print(f"grid_points_above_steady_state {len(grid) - below}")
    print(f"sign_mismatches {mismatches}")


def part_d(kstar: Decimal) -> None:
    """Compare the implicit-function derivatives of k* with central differences in s and in delta."""
    print("Part D: comparative statics of k* against central differences")
    dk_ds = kstar / ((1 - ALPHA) * SAVING)
    dk_ddelta = -kstar / ((1 - ALPHA) * (GROWTH + DEPRECIATION))
    fd_ds = (
        steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING + STEP, DEPRECIATION, GROWTH)
        - steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING - STEP, DEPRECIATION, GROWTH)
    ) / (2 * STEP)
    fd_ddelta = (
        steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION + STEP, GROWTH)
        - steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION - STEP, GROWTH)
    ) / (2 * STEP)
    rel_s = abs(fd_ds - dk_ds) / abs(dk_ds)
    rel_delta = abs(fd_ddelta - dk_ddelta) / abs(dk_ddelta)
    print(f"ift_dk_ds {dk_ds:.10f}")
    print(f"finite_difference_dk_ds {fd_ds:.10f}")
    print(f"relative_error_dk_ds {rel_s:.3e}")
    print(f"ift_dk_ddelta {dk_ddelta:.10f}")
    print(f"finite_difference_dk_ddelta {fd_ddelta:.10f}")
    print(f"relative_error_dk_ddelta {rel_delta:.3e}")
    print(f"within_tolerance {rel_s < TOLERANCE and rel_delta < TOLERANCE}")
    elasticity = SAVING / kstar * dk_ds
    print(f"elasticity_of_k_with_respect_to_s {elasticity:.10f}")


def part_e() -> None:
    """Compare the derivative of k* with respect to n with its derivative with respect to delta."""
    print("Part E: population growth and depreciation act through n + delta")
    base = steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION, GROWTH)
    dn = (
        steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION, GROWTH + STEP)
        - steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION, GROWTH - STEP)
    ) / (2 * STEP)
    ddelta = (
        steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION + STEP, GROWTH)
        - steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION - STEP, GROWTH)
    ) / (2 * STEP)
    print(f"finite_difference_dk_dn {dn:.10f}")
    print(f"finite_difference_dk_ddelta_again {ddelta:.10f}")
    print(f"difference_between_them {abs(dn - ddelta):.3e}")
    print(f"equal_within_tolerance {abs(dn - ddelta) < TOLERANCE * abs(base)}")


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A to E. Each line is a fixed string, a Decimal at a fixed precision, or a boolean."""
    kstar = steady_state(ALPHA, SAVING, DEPRECIATION, GROWTH)
    part_a(kstar)
    part_b(kstar)
    part_c(kstar)
    part_d(kstar)
    part_e()

5Where the canon breaks

  • One sector and no technological progress. The technology level is fixed at 1 (A1). Output per worker therefore cannot grow in the steady state (Remark 2.5 (i)). Growth in output per worker over time needs a technology term that grows, and this model does not have one. The residual that L02 measured is not modelled here.
  • A saving rate taken as given. The recursion takes \(s\) as a constant. Nothing in this lesson shows how households or firms would choose it, so the comparative statics of Proposition 4.2 are statements about the recursion, not about a choice.
  • One production function. Propositions 2.2 and 4.2 use the power form \(f(k) = k^{\alpha}\) with a constant \(\alpha\). The closed form of \(k^{\ast}\) and the elasticity \(1/(1-\alpha)\) depend on that form. A different production function needs its own steady state and derivative.
  • Closed economy without government. The recursion has no government spending and no trade. Fiscal policy enters in L08, and the IS-LM model of L04 is a separate static model with fixed prices.
  • Constant rates. Population growth \(n\) and depreciation \(\delta\) are constants. The steady state depends on them only through \(n + \delta\) (Proposition 4.2), but a path with time-varying rates is outside this lesson.

6Misconceptions

  1. “A higher saving rate raises the long-run growth rate of output per worker.”

    A higher \(s\) raises the level of the steady state, because \(\partial k^{\ast}/\partial s = k^{\ast}/((1-\alpha)s) > 0\) (Proposition 4.2). Lab 1 Part D prints \(39.8879393156\) for this derivative. Output per worker at the steady state is constant, so its long-run growth rate is zero for every saving rate in A2 (Remark 2.5 (i)). The saving rate changes the growth of output per worker during the transition, while capital per worker is still moving toward the steady state (Proposition 3.1), and not at the steady state.

  2. “At the steady state, output stops growing.”

    Output per worker stops changing, but aggregate output does not. Remark 2.5 (ii) gives \(Y_{t+1}/Y_t = 1+n\) at the steady state. Lab 1 does not print aggregate output, so this result rests on the derivation in Remark 2.5 alone.

  3. “Population growth and depreciation lower capital per worker through different channels.”

    In this model the two enter only through the sum \(n + \delta\), because \(H\) depends on \(n\) and \(\delta\) only through that sum (Proposition 4.2). Their derivatives of \(k^{\ast}\) are equal. Lab 1 Part E prints \(-132.9597977186\) for the derivative in \(n\) and the same value for the derivative in \(\delta\).

7Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · The steady state with \(\alpha = 1/2\), \(s = 1/4\), \(\delta = 1/10\) and \(n = 0\)

Find \(k^{\ast}\) and output per worker \(y^{\ast} = (k^{\ast})^{\alpha}\). Then find consumption per worker \(c^{\ast} = (1-s)y^{\ast}\), where consumption is the output not saved.

Solution

Here \(n + \delta = 1/10\), so \(s/(n+\delta) = (1/4)/(1/10) = 5/2\). The exponent is \(1/(1-1/2) = 2\), so \(k^{\ast} = (5/2)^{2} = 25/4 = 6.25\). Then \(y^{\ast} = \sqrt{25/4} = 5/2 = 2.5\), and \(c^{\ast} = (3/4)(5/2) = 15/8 = 1.875\). These are exact fractions, so no rounding is involved.

M2 · Two steps of the recursion

With the parameters of M1 and \(k_0 = 1\), compute \(k_1\) exactly and \(k_2\) to ten decimal places.

Solution

The recursion is \(k_{t+1} = (s k_t^{1/2} + (1-\delta)k_t)/(1+n)\) with \(s = 1/4\), \(\delta = 1/10\) and \(n = 0\). From \(k_0 = 1\), \(k_1 = (1/4)(1) + (9/10)(1) = 23/20 = 1.15\). Then \(k_2 = (1/4)\sqrt{1.15} + (9/10)(23/20) = (1/4)\sqrt{1.15} + 1.035\). Since \(\sqrt{1.15} = 1.0723805295\) to ten places, \(k_2 = 0.2680951324 + 1.035 = 1.3030951324\) to ten places. Capital per worker is still below \(k^{\ast} = 6.25\), as Proposition 3.1 predicts for a starting value below the steady state.

M3 · The two derivatives at the parameters of M1

At the parameters of M1, find \(\partial k^{\ast}/\partial s\) and \(\partial k^{\ast}/\partial \delta\) exactly.

Solution

Proposition 4.2 gives \(\partial k^{\ast}/\partial s = k^{\ast}/((1-\alpha)s) = (25/4)/((1/2)(1/4)) = (25/4)/(1/8) = 50\). It gives \(\partial k^{\ast}/\partial \delta = -k^{\ast}/((1-\alpha)(n+\delta)) = -(25/4)/((1/2)(1/10)) = -(25/4)/(1/20) = -125\). To first order, a unit increase in \(s\) raises \(k^{\ast}\) by 50 and a unit increase in \(\delta\) lowers it by 125.

Proof

P1 · The monotonicity of \(g\)

Under A1 to A3, show that \(g\) is strictly increasing on \((0, \infty)\). Name the assumption each inequality uses.

Solution

Let \(0 < a < b\). Since \(\alpha > 0\) (A1), \(x \mapsto x^{\alpha}\) is strictly increasing, so \(a^{\alpha} < b^{\alpha}\). Since \(s > 0\) (A2), \(s a^{\alpha} < s b^{\alpha}\). Since \(1-\delta > 0\) (A3), \((1-\delta)a < (1-\delta)b\). Adding the two strict inequalities and dividing by \(1+n > 0\) (A3) gives \(g(a) < g(b)\). This is Step 2 of Proposition 3.1.

P2 · The sign lemma

Under A1 to A3, show that \(g(k) > k\) if and only if \(0 < k < k^{\ast}\), for every \(k > 0\).

Solution

Let \(k > 0\). Then \(g(k) > k\) if and only if \(s k^{\alpha} > (n+\delta)k\), because \(1+n > 0\) and \(g(k) - k = (s k^{\alpha} - (n+\delta)k)/(1+n)\). Dividing by \(k > 0\), this is \(s k^{\alpha-1} > n+\delta\). Multiplying by \(k^{1-\alpha} > 0\), it is \(s > (n+\delta)k^{1-\alpha}\), that is, \(k^{1-\alpha} < s/(n+\delta) = (k^{\ast})^{1-\alpha}\). Since \(x \mapsto x^{1-\alpha}\) is strictly increasing, this is \(k < k^{\ast}\). The first claim of the statement follows, and the same argument with the inequality reversed gives the second.

P3 · Zero is the only other fixed point

Under A1 to A3, show that \(g\) has exactly two fixed points in \([0, \infty)\), namely \(0\) and \(k^{\ast}\).

Solution

By A1, \(g(0) = (s \cdot 0 + 0)/(1+n) = 0\), so \(0\) is a fixed point. A fixed point \(k > 0\) is a steady state, and Proposition 2.2 says there is only one, \(k^{\ast}\). So the fixed points in \([0, \infty)\) are exactly \(0\) and \(k^{\ast}\). The point \(0\) is not a positive steady state, which is why A4 requires \(k_0 > 0\).

Empirical

E1 · The ratio of two printed gaps

Read Part B of Lab 1. What is the ratio of the gap at period 100 to the gap at period 50? Give the value to three decimal places and state the basis.

Solution

Part B prints the gap at period 50 as 0.6623874908 and at period 100 as 0.0805861272. The ratio is \(0.0805861272/0.6623874908 = 0.1217\) to four decimal places, or 0.122 to three. The basis is the two printed gaps, which Part B gives to ten decimal places; the ratio is not computed from the unprinted gaps. Proposition 3.1 says the gap falls, so the ratio is less than one, and Remark 3.2 says it is one parameter set.

E2 · A one-point rise in the saving rate

Part D prints the derivative of \(k^{\ast}\) with respect to \(s\) as 39.8879393156. Using this derivative, by how much does \(k^{\ast}\) change when \(s\) rises by 0.01, and what is that change as a percentage of \(k^{\ast}\)?

Solution

The linear change is \(0.01 \times 39.8879393156 = 0.3988793932\). Part A prints \(k^{\ast} = 5.5843115042\), so the change is \(0.3988793932/5.5843115042 = 0.0714\), or 7.14% of \(k^{\ast}\). The base is the steady-state capital \(k^{\ast}\) at \(s = 0.2\), and the figure is a first-order approximation. Equivalently, it is the elasticity from Remark 4.3 times the proportional change in \(s\): \(1.4285714286 \times (0.01/0.2) = 0.0714\).

E3 · A depreciation change from Part D

Part D prints the derivative of \(k^{\ast}\) with respect to \(\delta\) as \(-132.9597977186\). Using this derivative, what is the linear change in \(k^{\ast}\) when \(\delta\) rises from 0.05 to 0.051? Is the sign what Proposition 4.2 predicts?

Solution

The linear change is \(0.001 \times (-132.9597977186) = -0.1329597977\). The sign is negative, which Proposition 4.2 predicts because \(\partial k^{\ast}/\partial\delta < 0\). The change is first order: the derivative is evaluated at \(\delta = 0.05\), and the exact change from the closed form would differ from the linear one by a second-order term that this problem does not compute.

Research

R1 · Which assumption the convergence proof needs for \(\delta\)

Suppose \(\delta = 1\), which is outside A3. Show that Proposition 3.1 still holds, and identify the step of its proof that needs \(1 - \delta \ge 0\) rather than \(1 - \delta > 0\).

Solution

With \(\delta = 1\), \(n + \delta = n + 1 > 0\) still holds as long as \(n > -1\), and the recursion is \(g(k) = s k^{\alpha}/(1+n)\). Proposition 2.2 holds with \(n + \delta = n + 1\). The sign lemma in Step 1 uses only \(n+\delta > 0\) and \(1+n > 0\), and both hold here, so it holds. Step 2 needs a nonnegative coefficient on \(k\) and a positive coefficient on \(k^{\alpha}\): here the coefficient on \(k\) is zero and that on \(k^{\alpha}\) is \(s/(1+n) > 0\), so \(g\) is still strictly increasing. Steps 3 to 5 use only Steps 1 and 2 and the monotone convergence theorem. So the proposition holds at \(\delta = 1\), and the step that needs \(1-\delta \ge 0\) is Step 2. The assumption \(\delta < 1\) in A3 is therefore stronger than the proof requires.

R2 · The elasticity as a function of \(\alpha\)

Compute the elasticity \((s/k^{\ast})\,\partial k^{\ast}/\partial s\) for \(\alpha = 1/2\) and for \(\alpha = 3/10\). Say how the elasticity depends on \(\alpha\), and why this matters for the claim that saving raises the steady state.

Solution

By Remark 4.3 the elasticity is \(1/(1-\alpha)\). For \(\alpha = 1/2\) it is \(2\). For \(\alpha = 3/10\) it is \(10/7 = 1.4285714286\), which Lab 1 Part D prints. The elasticity is increasing in \(\alpha\), because \(1-\alpha\) falls as \(\alpha\) rises. A larger capital share makes the steady state more responsive to the saving rate. The claim that saving raises the steady state holds for every \(\alpha\) in A1, because the elasticity is positive, but its size depends on \(\alpha\).

R3 · The zero capital trap

Show that if \(k_0 = 0\), then \(k_t = 0\) for every \(t\), and state which assumption of Proposition 3.1 excludes this.

Solution

By Remark 2.3, \(g(0) = 0\). Starting from \(k_0 = 0\), the recursion gives \(k_1 = g(0) = 0\), and by induction \(k_{t+1} = g(k_t) = g(0) = 0\) for every \(t\). So the sequence stays at zero and never converges to \(k^{\ast}\). The excluding assumption is A4, \(k_0 > 0\). Step 3 of the proof of Proposition 3.1 needs \(k_t > 0\) at every step, so that \(D(k_t)\) and the sign lemma apply.
