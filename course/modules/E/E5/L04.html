A business cycle (Definition 1.1 below) is a movement of output about its trend. This lesson defines the term without measuring it. It then sets up the IS–LM model (Definition 2.1 below), two linear relations in output and the interest rate with prices fixed, and solves it exactly for its unique equilibrium (Proposition 3.2 below). The change in equilibrium output for a rise in government spending is strictly smaller than the goods-market benchmark \(1/(1-c)\) (Remark 4.4 below) when \(b > 0\) and \(d > 0\). Lab 1 computes the spending and monetary multipliers (Definition 4.1 below) from finite shifts in exact fractions, and prints the closed forms beside them in Parts B and C. L05 adds prices to this model.

1The business cycle

Output \(Y\) is the aggregate output that L01 measures. Trend output is a reference path for output. The lesson takes the trend as given and does not estimate it.

Definition 1.1 (business cycle and output gap)

The business cycle is the fluctuation of output about its trend. The output gap at a date is output minus trend output, in the units of output, and it is positive when output is above trend. Both are definitions. Neither says how large the fluctuations are, how often they occur, or what causes them.

Remark 1.2 (what the word cycle does not claim here)

The lesson uses the word cycle as Definition 1.1 gives it. It makes no claim that fluctuations recur at a fixed interval, because that would be a statement about data. Measuring a cycle needs statistics such as filters, standard deviations and correlations, which this lesson does not use. The static model of §2 to §4 does not generate a path at all, so a fall in output in that model is a comparison of two equilibria.

2The IS and LM relations

The model has two markets and one interest rate. The goods market is summarised by the IS relation, and the money market by the LM relation. Both are written with linear behaviour, and the parameters are constants.

Definition 2.1 (the IS–LM model)

Let \(Y\) be output and \(i\) the interest rate. The marginal propensity to consume \(c\) is the share of an extra unit of disposable income that is spent on consumption. Disposable income is \(Y - \mathrm{Tax}\), where \(\mathrm{Tax}\) is a lump-sum tax. Autonomous spending is the part of spending that does not depend on output or the interest rate. The real money supply is \(\bar m = M/P\), the nominal money stock divided by the price level. The IS–LM model is the system of two relations: \[ \text{IS:}\quad Y = C + I + G,\qquad C = c_0 + c\,(Y - \mathrm{Tax}),\qquad I = b_0 - b\,i, \] \[ \text{LM:}\quad \bar m = dY - h\,i, \] where \(G\) is government spending, \(c_0\) and \(b_0\) are autonomous consumption and investment, \(b\) and \(d\) are the sensitivities of investment to the interest rate and of money demand to output, and \(h\) is the sensitivity of money demand to the interest rate. The LM relation says that real money demand \(dY - h i\) equals the real money supply.

Assumptions (used by the results of §§2 to 4)
  • A1. The economy is closed. \(G\), \(\mathrm{Tax}\), \(c_0\), \(b_0\), \(M\) and \(P\) are constants.
  • A2. \(0 < c < 1\), \(b \ge 0\), \(d \ge 0\) and \(h > 0\).
  • A3. Prices are fixed at \(P\), and the inflation rate expected for the period is zero. So the nominal interest rate \(i\) is the only interest rate in the model. L05 relaxes this assumption by making the price level a variable.
  • A4. The relations are linear in \(Y\) and \(i\) as written, with no lags, and with no expectation term other than the zero expected inflation of A3.
Derivation 2.2 (the two relations in standard form)

Substitute the consumption and investment functions into the goods-market identity: \(Y = c_0 + c(Y - \mathrm{Tax}) + b_0 - b\,i + G\). Collect the terms in \(Y\): \(Y - cY = c_0 - c\,\mathrm{Tax} + b_0 + G - b\,i\). With \[ A = c_0 - c\,\mathrm{Tax} + b_0 + G, \] this is \((1-c)Y + b\,i = A\). The LM relation is \(dY - h\,i = \bar m\). Together they form a linear system in \((Y, i)\) with right-hand side \((A, \bar m)\). The constant \(A\) is autonomous demand, and its value is set by \(c_0\), \(\mathrm{Tax}\), \(b_0\) and \(G\). This step uses only the definitions in Definition 2.1 and is grade T.

3The equilibrium

An equilibrium of the model is a pair \((Y, i)\) at which both relations hold, so that the goods market clears and money demand equals money supply. In this lesson the word has that sense. The model specifies no agents who take prices as given, and the competitive market equilibrium of E1 is not the object studied here.

Definition 3.1 (equilibrium, and the constant D)

An equilibrium of the IS–LM model is a pair \((Y^{\ast}, i^{\ast})\) that satisfies both relations. Let \[ D = h(1-c) + bd. \] Under A2, \(D \ge h(1-c) > 0\), since \(h > 0\) and \(1 - c > 0\) and \(bd \ge 0\).

Proposition 3.2 (unique equilibrium in closed form)

Under A1 to A4, the IS–LM model has exactly one equilibrium, and \[ Y^{\ast} = \frac{hA + b\bar m}{D}, \qquad i^{\ast} = \frac{dA - (1-c)\bar m}{D}. \]

Proof

Let \((Y, i)\) be any solution of \((1-c)Y + b\,i = A\) and \(dY - h\,i = \bar m\). The coefficient matrix of this linear system has determinant \((1-c)(-h) - bd = -D\). By Definition 3.1, \(D \neq 0\), so the system has exactly one solution, and it is given by Cramer's rule as the formulas in the statement. For a direct check, substitute the two formulas. First, \((1-c)Y^{\ast} + b\,i^{\ast} = \bigl[(1-c)(hA + b\bar m) + b(dA - (1-c)\bar m)\bigr]/D = \bigl[(h(1-c) + bd)A\bigr]/D = A\). Second, \(dY^{\ast} - h\,i^{\ast} = \bigl[d(hA + b\bar m) - h(dA - (1-c)\bar m)\bigr]/D = \bigl[(bd + h(1-c))\bar m\bigr]/D = \bar m\). Both relations hold at \((Y^{\ast}, i^{\ast})\), so it is an equilibrium, and it is the only one.

4The two multipliers

Definition 4.1 (spending and monetary multipliers)

The spending multiplier is \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial G\), the change in equilibrium output per unit change in government spending. The monetary multiplier is \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial \bar m\), the change in equilibrium output per unit change in the real money supply. Both are ratios of an output change to a change in a policy variable, measured in units of output per unit of the variable.

Proposition 4.2 (the comparative statics of the equilibrium)

Under A1 to A4, \[ \frac{\partial Y^{\ast}}{\partial G} = \frac{h}{D}, \qquad \frac{\partial Y^{\ast}}{\partial \bar m} = \frac{b}{D}, \qquad \frac{\partial Y^{\ast}}{\partial \mathrm{Tax}} = -\frac{ch}{D}, \] \[ \frac{\partial i^{\ast}}{\partial G} = \frac{d}{D}, \qquad \frac{\partial i^{\ast}}{\partial \bar m} = -\frac{1-c}{D}. \] Each derivative is the rate of change of one equilibrium value with one parameter, the other parameters held fixed. These are comparative statics in the sense of E1.

Proof

By Proposition 3.2, \(Y^{\ast} = (hA + b\bar m)/D\) and \(i^{\ast} = (dA - (1-c)\bar m)/D\). The constant \(D\) depends on none of \(G\), \(\mathrm{Tax}\) or \(\bar m\). From Derivation 2.2, \(\partial A/\partial G = 1\) and \(\partial A/\partial \mathrm{Tax} = -c\). Differentiate: \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial G = h/D\), \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial \mathrm{Tax} = -ch/D\), \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial \bar m = b/D\), \(\partial i^{\ast}/\partial G = d/D\), and \(\partial i^{\ast}/\partial \bar m = -(1-c)/D\).

Proposition 4.3 (the interest-rate effect on the spending multiplier)

Under A1 to A4, the spending multiplier satisfies \[ \frac{\partial Y^{\ast}}{\partial G} = \frac{h}{D} \le \frac{1}{1-c}, \] with equality if and only if \(bd = 0\).

Proof

Both \(h/D\) and \(1/(1-c)\) are positive. So \(h/D \le 1/(1-c)\) holds if and only if \(h(1-c) \le D\), by multiplying both sides by \(D(1-c) > 0\). Since \(D = h(1-c) + bd\), this holds if and only if \(0 \le bd\). The condition holds under A2, and it is an equality if and only if \(bd = 0\).

Remark 4.4 (the goods-market benchmark)

If \(b = 0\), investment does not depend on \(i\), and the IS relation does not involve \(i\). Then \(D = h(1-c)\), and Proposition 4.2 gives \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial G = 1/(1-c)\) and \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial \bar m = 0\). The value \(1/(1-c)\) is therefore the spending multiplier when the interest rate has no effect on investment. Proposition 4.3 says that any positive \(b\) with \(d > 0\) makes the spending multiplier strictly smaller. Lab 1 Part B prints the benchmark value, and Part D prints the spending multiplier at \(b = 0\), which equals it.

Remark 4.5 (the interest rate rises with spending)

Proposition 4.2 gives \(\partial i^{\ast}/\partial G = d/D \ge 0\) under A2. So an increase in government spending raises the equilibrium interest rate, or leaves it unchanged when \(d = 0\). Investment is \(I = b_0 - b\,i\), so the rise in the interest rate lowers investment by \(b\,\partial i^{\ast}/\partial G = bd/D\) per unit of \(G\). This reduction is zero if and only if \(bd = 0\), since \(D > 0\).

Lab 1 · The IS–LM equilibrium and its multipliers

The inputs are illustrative parameters, not data: \(c = 3/4\), \(c_0 = 1\), \(b_0 = 1\), \(b = 2\), \(d = 1/2\), \(h = 1\), \(\mathrm{Tax} = 0\), \(G = 1\), and \(\bar m = 2\). The lab uses exact fractions, so it predicts exact values. Part A should give \(Y^{\ast} = 28/5\) and \(i^{\ast} = 4/5\), with both residuals equal to zero. Part B should give a spending multiplier of \(h/D = 4/5\) for both the step from \(G = 1\) to \(11/10\) and the step to \(2\), and a benchmark of \(1/(1-c) = 4\). Part C should give a monetary multiplier of \(b/D = 8/5\) for the step from \(\bar m = 2\) to \(5/2\), and an interest change of \(-1/5\) per unit of money. Part D should give a spending multiplier of 4 when \(b = 0\). The code uses no random draws, and its output is fixed.

"""E5 L04 lab 1: the IS-LM equilibrium solved exactly, and its fiscal and monetary multipliers.

Inputs are illustrative parameters, not data: marginal propensity to consume c = 3/4, autonomous consumption 1,
autonomous investment 1, investment sensitivity to the interest rate b = 2, money-demand sensitivity to output
d = 1/2, money-demand sensitivity to the interest rate h = 1, lump-sum tax 0, government spending G = 1, and real
money supply mbar = 2. Every quantity is a Fraction, so each step is exact and the printed fractions are the
solution itself. No random draws are used.

Part A: the IS-LM equilibrium (Y*, i*) from the closed form, and the residuals of the IS and LM equations at it.
Part B: the fiscal multiplier, the change in Y* for an increase in G from 1 to 11/10 and from 1 to 2, against
1/(1 - c).
Part C: the monetary multiplier, the change in Y* and in i* for an increase in real money from 2 to 5/2.
Part D: the fiscal multiplier with b = 0, where investment does not respond to the interest rate.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

C = Fraction(3, 4)
AUTONOMOUS_CONSUMPTION = Fraction(1)
AUTONOMOUS_INVESTMENT = Fraction(1)
B = Fraction(2)
D_OUTPUT = Fraction(1, 2)
H = Fraction(1)
TAX = Fraction(0)
G_BASE = Fraction(1)
MBAR_BASE = Fraction(2)


def autonomous_demand(g: Fraction, tax: Fraction = TAX) -> Fraction:
    """The constant A = c0 - c*Tax + b0 + G in the IS relation (1 - c) Y + b i = A."""
    return AUTONOMOUS_CONSUMPTION - C * tax + AUTONOMOUS_INVESTMENT + g


def denominator(c: Fraction, b: Fraction, d: Fraction, h: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The determinant magnitude D = h (1 - c) + b d of the IS-LM system.

    D is positive under A2: 0 < c < 1, h > 0, b >= 0, d >= 0.
    """
    return h * (1 - c) + b * d


def equilibrium(g: Fraction, mbar: Fraction, b: Fraction = B) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """The IS-LM solution (Y*, i*) = ((h A + b mbar)/D, (d A - (1 - c) mbar)/D) for the given G, mbar and b."""
    a = autonomous_demand(g)
    d_value = denominator(C, b, D_OUTPUT, H)
    y = (H * a + b * mbar) / d_value
    i = (D_OUTPUT * a - (1 - C) * mbar) / d_value
    return y, i


def is_residual(y: Fraction, i: Fraction, g: Fraction, b: Fraction = B) -> Fraction:
    """Y minus the goods-market demand c0 + c (Y - Tax) + b0 - b i + G. It is zero at an IS point."""
    demand = AUTONOMOUS_CONSUMPTION + C * (y - TAX) + AUTONOMOUS_INVESTMENT - b * i + g
    return y - demand


def lm_residual(y: Fraction, i: Fraction, mbar: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Real money demand d Y - h i minus the real money supply mbar. It is zero at an LM point."""
    return D_OUTPUT * y - H * i - mbar


def part_a() -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Print the closed-form equilibrium and the two exact residuals."""
    y, i = equilibrium(G_BASE, MBAR_BASE)
    print("Part A: IS-LM equilibrium")
    print(f"autonomous_demand {autonomous_demand(G_BASE)}")
    print(f"denominator_D {denominator(C, B, D_OUTPUT, H)}")
    print(f"Y_star {y} {float(y):.10f}")
    print(f"i_star {i} {float(i):.10f}")
    print(f"is_residual {is_residual(y, i, G_BASE)}")
    print(f"lm_residual {lm_residual(y, i, MBAR_BASE)}")
    return y, i


def part_b(y0: Fraction, i0: Fraction) -> None:
    """Print the fiscal multiplier from two exact shifts in G, and the closed form h/D."""
    print("Part B: fiscal multiplier")
    crossing = 1 / (1 - C)
    print(f"goods_market_benchmark {crossing}")
    for g_new in (Fraction(11, 10), Fraction(2)):
        y1, i1 = equilibrium(g_new, MBAR_BASE)
        multiplier = (y1 - y0) / (g_new - G_BASE)
        print(f"shift_G_to {g_new} delta_Y {y1 - y0} multiplier {multiplier} ({float(multiplier):.10f})")
        print(f"shift_G_to {g_new} delta_i {i1 - i0} ({float(i1 - i0):.10f})")
        print(f"is_residual_after_shift {is_residual(y1, i1, g_new)}")
    closed_form = H / denominator(C, B, D_OUTPUT, H)
    print(f"closed_form_h_over_D {closed_form}")
    print(f"multiplier_below_cross {closed_form < crossing}")


def part_c(y0: Fraction, i0: Fraction) -> None:
    """Print the monetary multiplier from an exact shift in real money, and its closed form b/D."""
    print("Part C: monetary multiplier")
    m_new = Fraction(5, 2)
    y1, i1 = equilibrium(G_BASE, m_new)
    print(f"shift_mbar_to {m_new} delta_Y {y1 - y0} multiplier {(y1 - y0) / (m_new - MBAR_BASE)}")
    print(f"shift_mbar_to {m_new} delta_i {i1 - i0} per_unit {(i1 - i0) / (m_new - MBAR_BASE)}")
    print(f"closed_form_b_over_D {B / denominator(C, B, D_OUTPUT, H)}")
    print(f"closed_form_minus_one_minus_c_over_D {-(1 - C) / denominator(C, B, D_OUTPUT, H)}")
    print(f"is_residual_after_shift {is_residual(y1, i1, G_BASE)} lm_residual_after_shift {lm_residual(y1, i1, m_new)}")


def part_d() -> None:
    """Print the fiscal multiplier with investment insensitive to the interest rate (b = 0)."""
    print("Part D: fiscal multiplier with b = 0")
    y0, _ = equilibrium(G_BASE, MBAR_BASE, Fraction(0))
    y1, _ = equilibrium(Fraction(2), MBAR_BASE, Fraction(0))
    print(f"multiplier_b_zero {(y1 - y0) / (Fraction(2) - G_BASE)}")
    print(f"equals_one_over_one_minus_c {(y1 - y0) / (Fraction(2) - G_BASE) == 1 / (1 - C)}")


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A to D. Values are exact Fractions; decimals print to ten places, checks print True or False,
    and the exact residuals print 0."""
    y0, i0 = part_a()
    part_b(y0, i0)
    part_c(y0, i0)
    part_d()

5Where the canon breaks

  • Fixed prices. A3 holds prices fixed and sets the inflation rate expected for the period to zero. Output and the interest rate are then the only moving variables. In a model with prices, output and the price level are determined together, which is what L05 adds.
  • A static model. Proposition 3.2 gives one equilibrium at a time. The model contains no path from one equilibrium to another and no mechanism that makes output return to trend. The business cycle of Definition 1.1 is therefore not explained by this model. The model compares two equilibria that differ in one parameter.
  • A money supply fixed by assumption. The LM relation takes \(\bar m\) as given (A1). A central bank that sets the interest rate by a rule, and not the money stock, needs a different relation, which L07 treats as a rule that sets the nominal policy rate from inflation and the output gap.
  • A closed economy. There are no exports, imports or exchange rates. Open-economy models are outside E5.
  • Linear behaviour. The solution in Proposition 3.2 uses the linear forms of Definition 2.1. A nonlinear IS or LM relation needs its own analysis, and this lesson does not provide one.

6Misconceptions

  1. “In IS–LM, the spending multiplier is \(1/(1-c)\).”

    This is the value when investment does not respond to the interest rate (Remark 4.4). With \(bd > 0\), Proposition 4.3 gives a strictly smaller spending multiplier, \(h/D\). Lab 1 Part B prints 0.8000000000 at the parameters of Part A, while the benchmark \(1/(1-c)\) prints 4 (Part B). Part D prints a multiplier of 4 at \(b = 0\).

  2. “An increase in government spending leaves output unchanged, because the higher interest rate offsets it.”

    The interest rate offsets part of the increase, not all of it. Proposition 4.2 gives \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial G = h/D > 0\) under A2, so output rises. Lab 1 Part B shows a rise in output of \(4/5\) for each unit of spending (step to \(G = 2\)), together with a rise in the interest rate of \(2/5\).

  3. “The IS–LM equilibrium explains why business cycles recur.”

    Proposition 3.2 gives one equilibrium for each set of parameters, and the model has no dynamics (§5). A single equilibrium does not recur, and a static model cannot generate a path. Whether cycles recur is a question about data, which this lesson does not address (Remark 1.2).

Mechanics

M1 · The equilibrium with \(c = 1/2\), \(b = 2\), \(d = 1\), \(h = 2\), \(A = 4\) and \(\bar m = 3\)

Find \(D\), \(Y^{\ast}\) and \(i^{\ast}\), and check both relations at the solution.

Solution

Here \(D = h(1-c) + bd = 2(1/2) + 2(1) = 3\). Then \(Y^{\ast} = (hA + b\bar m)/D = (8 + 6)/3 = 14/3\), and \(i^{\ast} = (dA - (1-c)\bar m)/D = (4 - 3/2)/3 = 5/6\). Check the IS relation: \((1-c)Y^{\ast} + b\,i^{\ast} = (1/2)(14/3) + 2(5/6) = 7/3 + 5/3 = 4 = A\). Check the LM relation: \(dY^{\ast} - h\,i^{\ast} = 14/3 - 2(5/6) = 14/3 - 5/3 = 3 = \bar m\). Both hold exactly.

M2 · The spending multiplier at the parameters of M1

At the parameters of M1, find the spending multiplier \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial G\), and compare it with the benchmark \(1/(1-c)\).

Solution

By Proposition 4.2, \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial G = h/D = 2/3\). The benchmark is \(1/(1-c) = 1/(1/2) = 2\). The spending multiplier is one third of the benchmark, which is smaller as Proposition 4.3 requires when \(bd = 2 > 0\). The ratio of the two is \((2/3)/2 = 1/3\), and the basis is these exact values.

M3 · The monetary multipliers at the parameters of M1

At the parameters of M1, find \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial \bar m\) and \(\partial i^{\ast}/\partial \bar m\) exactly.

Solution

By Proposition 4.2, \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial \bar m = b/D = 2/3\), and \(\partial i^{\ast}/\partial \bar m = -(1-c)/D = -(1/2)/3 = -1/6\). An increase of one unit in the real money supply raises output by \(2/3\) and lowers the interest rate by \(1/6\), exactly, because the system is linear.

Proof

P1 · The IS relation in standard form

Starting from \(Y = C + I + G\) with the consumption and investment functions of Definition 2.1, derive \((1-c)Y + b\,i = A\), and state which assumption makes \(c\) the marginal propensity to consume.

Solution

Substituting, \(Y = c_0 + c(Y - \mathrm{Tax}) + b_0 - b\,i + G\). Expanding \(c(Y - \mathrm{Tax}) = cY - c\,\mathrm{Tax}\) and moving \(cY\) to the left gives \((1-c)Y = c_0 - c\,\mathrm{Tax} + b_0 + G - b\,i\). Adding \(b\,i\) to both sides gives \((1-c)Y + b\,i = A\). The number \(c\) is the marginal propensity to consume by Definition 2.1, and A2 requires \(0 < c < 1\), so \(1-c > 0\) and the coefficient on \(Y\) is positive.

P2 · The positivity of \(D\)

Under A2, show that \(D > 0\) even when \(b = 0\) or \(d = 0\), and state which parameter restriction is needed for the spending multiplier to be strictly smaller than \(1/(1-c)\).

Solution

Since \(bd \ge 0\) under A2 and \(h(1-c) > 0\), \(D = h(1-c) + bd \ge h(1-c) > 0\). This holds for \(b = 0\) and for \(d = 0\), because \(h(1-c) > 0\) alone is enough. For the strict inequality in Proposition 4.3, Proposition 4.3's proof shows that \(h/D < 1/(1-c)\) if and only if \(bd > 0\). So the restriction needed is \(b > 0\) and \(d > 0\) together.

P3 · The slopes of the two relations

In the plane with \(Y\) on the horizontal axis and \(i\) on the vertical axis, find the slope of the IS relation and of the LM relation, and state their signs under A2 with \(b > 0\) and \(d > 0\).

Solution

The IS relation is \(i = (A - (1-c)Y)/b\), so its slope is \(-(1-c)/b\). Since \(0 < c < 1\) and \(b > 0\), the slope is negative. The LM relation is \(i = (dY - \bar m)/h\), so its slope is \(d/h\). Since \(d > 0\) and \(h > 0\), the slope is positive. The IS relation slopes down and the LM relation slopes up.

Empirical

E1 · The interest change per unit of output change under a spending shift

Read Part B of Lab 1 for the step from \(G = 1\) to \(11/10\). What is the ratio of the change in the interest rate to the change in output, and what closed form does it equal?

Solution

Part B prints a change in the interest rate of \(1/25\), or 0.0400000000, and a change in output of \(2/25\). The lab prints the fraction only, and \(2/25\) is 0.08 as a decimal. The ratio is \((1/25)/(2/25) = 1/2\). The closed form is \((d/D)/(h/D) = d/h = (1/2)/1 = 1/2\). The closed form holds for any nonzero change in \(G\): both equilibria satisfy the LM relation with the same \(\bar m\), so \(d\,\Delta Y = h\,\Delta i\), and \(\Delta Y = (h/D)\,\Delta G \ne 0\) by Proposition 4.2. The basis is the printed values, the LM relation, and Proposition 4.2.

E2 · The monetary interest change per unit of money

Read Part C of Lab 1. What is the change in the interest rate per unit increase in the real money supply, and how does it compare with the closed form?

Solution

Part C prints the change in the interest rate as \(-1/10\) for a change in \(\bar m\) of \(1/2\), so the change per unit is \(-1/5\), and Part C prints this as per_unit -1/5. The closed form is \(-(1-c)/D = -(1/4)/(5/4) = -1/5\). They agree exactly, so the monetary result is confirmed at these parameters.

E3 · How much of the benchmark multiplier survives

Read Parts B and D of Lab 1. What share of the benchmark spending multiplier of Part D is the spending multiplier of Part B, at the same parameters except \(b\)?

Solution

Part B prints a spending multiplier of 0.8000000000, at \(b = 2\). Part D prints 4 at \(b = 0\), which is the benchmark \(1/(1-c)\). The share is \(0.8/4 = 1/5\), or 20% of the benchmark. The base is the Part D value. The closed form for the share is \(h(1-c)/D = (1/4)/(5/4) = 1/5\), which gives the same value. The two parameter sets differ only in \(b\), and \(b\) enters the multiplier \(h/D\) only through \(D = h(1-c) + bd\), so the reduction comes from the \(bd\) term.

Research

R1 · The fiscal multiplier with money demand independent of output

Suppose \(d = 0\). Show that the spending multiplier equals \(1/(1-c)\) and that the equilibrium interest rate does not respond to \(G\). Say what this case means for the LM relation.

Solution

With \(d = 0\), \(D = h(1-c)\), so \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial G = h/D = h/(h(1-c)) = 1/(1-c)\). The interest-rate response is \(\partial i^{\ast}/\partial G = d/D = 0\). With \(d = 0\), the LM relation is \(i = -\bar m/h\), which does not depend on \(Y\). It is a horizontal line in the plane of Y and i, so the interest rate is fixed by the money supply and output moves without any interest-rate feedback. The spending multiplier is then the goods-market benchmark of Remark 4.4.

R2 · The monetary multiplier when investment is insensitive to the interest rate

Show that with \(b = 0\) the monetary multiplier is zero, and explain in one sentence why money has no effect on output in that case.

Solution

With \(b = 0\), \(\partial Y^{\ast}/\partial \bar m = b/D = 0\). Investment does not depend on \(i\), and the IS relation \((1-c)Y = A\) does not involve \(i\). It fixes output at \(A/(1-c)\) regardless of \(\bar m\). The money market then determines the interest rate alone, so money changes the interest rate and leaves output unchanged.

R3 · The sign of the interest response to spending

Under A2, show that \(\partial i^{\ast}/\partial G\) is nonnegative, and state when it is zero.

Solution

Proposition 4.2 gives \(\partial i^{\ast}/\partial G = d/D\). Under A2, \(d \ge 0\) and \(D > 0\) (P2), so the derivative is nonnegative. It is zero exactly when \(d = 0\), which is the case of R1. When \(d > 0\) it is strictly positive, and the interest rate rises with spending.
