Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply

  • Uses: the IS–LM model, and comparative statics from E1
  • Introduces: the aggregate demand–aggregate supply model
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled

A demand shock (a change in the intercept \(a\) of Assumption A1; Proposition 2.2) moves the price level (defined in §1) and output together, and the split between the two depends on the slopes of the demand and short-run supply curves. The aggregate demand–aggregate supply model (Definition 1.1 below) puts both curves on one diagram with one price level and output. This lesson states the model in linear form, solves its short-run equilibrium in closed form, traces a demand shock to its new price and output, and derives the long-run equilibrium, where expected prices equal actual prices. Lab 1 checks each formula exactly. Lesson 6 takes the short-run supply curve used here and turns it into a Phillips curve.

1The model and its three assumptions

Output is measured in index points and the price level \(P\) is an index. The model is static: it describes one period, with no dynamics and no money stock. Two levels are taken as given. The potential output \(\bar Y\) is the output the economy produces when its resources are used at their normal rate. The expected price level \(P^{e}\) is the price level that firms and households expect for the period. A price level of 1 means the index equals its base value.

Assumption A1 (aggregate demand is linear in the price level)

Aggregate demand \(Y^{d}\) is given by \(Y^{d} = a - \eta P\), with \(a > 0\) and \(\eta > 0\). The intercept \(a\) collects the components of spending that do not depend on the price level, measured in index points. The slope \(-\eta \) is negative.

Assumption A2 (short-run aggregate supply)

Output supplied in the short run is \(Y^{\mathrm{s}} = \bar Y + \nu \,(P - P^{e})\), with \(\nu > 0\). The expected price level \(P^{e}\) is taken as given in the short run. A higher actual price level than the expected one raises output above potential.

Assumption A3 (long-run expectations)

In the long run the expected price level equals the actual price level, \(P^{e} = P\). A3 is used in Definition 1.1, which defines the long-run equilibrium, and in Proposition 3.1.

Definition 1.1 (the aggregate demand–aggregate supply model)

The aggregate demand–aggregate supply model is the pair of relations in A1 and A2, with the expected price level \(P^{e}\) given. A short-run equilibrium is a pair \((P, Y)\) at which \(Y = Y^{d} = Y^{\mathrm{s}}\). A long-run equilibrium is a short-run equilibrium that also satisfies A3.

Remark 1.2 (why the linear form is an assumption)

Lesson 4 gives the IS–LM output as an affine function of the real money supply \(\bar m = M/P\), where \(M\) is the nominal money stock (Lesson 4, Definition 2.1). Substituting \(M/P\) makes the demand curve depend on \(P\) through \(M/P\), so it is an affine function of \(1/P\). That function is not linear in \(P\) unless its slope in \(1/P\) is zero, and the slope is zero when investment does not respond to the interest rate (Lesson 4, Remark 4.4). This lesson does not derive its demand curve from the IS–LM specification of Lesson 4. It takes the linear form A1 as the model, and §4 states what that choice removes.

2The short-run equilibrium and a demand shock

Proposition 2.1 (the short-run equilibrium)

Assume A1 and A2. Then the model has exactly one short-run equilibrium, with \[ P^{*} = \frac{a - \bar Y + \nu \,P^{e}}{\eta + \nu }, \qquad Y^{*} = a - \eta P^{*}. \] At this equilibrium \(Y^{*} - \bar Y = \nu \,(P^{*} - P^{e})\).

Proof

Assumptions: A1 with \(a, \eta > 0\), and A2 with \(\nu > 0\) and \(P^{e}\) given. A pair \((P, Y)\) is an equilibrium when both \(Y = a - \eta P\) and \(Y = \bar Y + \nu (P - P^{e})\) hold. Subtract the second equation from the first to get \(0 = a - \eta P - \bar Y - \nu (P - P^{e})\). Collect the terms in \(P\): \((\eta + \nu )P = a - \bar Y + \nu P^{e}\). Since \(\eta + \nu > 0\), this has exactly one solution, \(P^{*}\). Substituting \(P^{*}\) into A1 gives \(Y^{*} = a - \eta P^{*}\), and A2 at \(P^{*}\) gives the same \(Y^{*}\) because the two equations were equal at \(P^{*}\). The identity \(Y^{*} - \bar Y = \nu (P^{*} - P^{e})\) is A2 evaluated at \(P^{*}\).

Proposition 2.2 (a demand shock in the short run)

Assume A1 and A2. Let the intercept move from \(a\) to \(a + \Delta a\), holding \(\eta \), \(\nu \), \(\bar Y\) and \(P^{e}\) fixed. Then \[ \Delta P = \frac{\Delta a}{\eta + \nu }, \qquad \Delta Y = \frac{\nu }{\eta + \nu }\,\Delta a. \] The ratio \(\Delta Y / \Delta a = \nu /(\eta + \nu )\) lies strictly between 0 and 1. Output rises with a positive shift and the price level rises too. The ratio \(\Delta Y / \Delta a\) is the output share of the shock, the change in output per unit of the change in \(a\).

Proof

Proposition 2.1 gives \(P^{*}(a) = (a - \bar Y + \nu P^{e})/(\eta + \nu )\). The shift replaces \(a\) by \(a + \Delta a\), so \(\Delta P = P^{*}(a + \Delta a) - P^{*}(a) = \Delta a/(\eta + \nu )\). Next, \(\Delta Y = \Delta a - \eta \,\Delta P\), because \(Y^{*} = a - \eta P^{*}\). Substituting \(\Delta P\) gives \(\Delta Y = \Delta a - \eta \,\Delta a/(\eta + \nu ) = \Delta a\,\bigl(1 - \eta /(\eta + \nu )\bigr) = \nu \,\Delta a/(\eta + \nu )\). For the bounds, \(\nu > 0\) and \(\eta > 0\) give \(0 < \nu /(\eta + \nu )\), and \(\nu /(\eta + \nu ) < 1\) because \(1 - \nu /(\eta + \nu ) = \eta /(\eta + \nu ) > 0\). Since \(\Delta P\) has the sign of \(\Delta a\), a positive shift raises the price level.

Remark 2.3 (the share rises as the short-run supply curve flattens)

The output share \(\nu /(\eta + \nu )\) rises with \(\nu \). A flat short-run supply curve (large \(\nu \)) passes more of the shock into output, and a steep one (small \(\nu \)) passes more into the price level. The limit of \(\nu /(\eta + \nu )\) as \(\nu \) grows without bound is 1, because \(\nu /(\eta + \nu ) = 1/(1 + \eta /\nu )\) and \(\eta /\nu \) tends to 0. Lab 1 Part D prints the share for five values of \(\nu \).

Lab 1 · The linear AD–AS equilibrium and a demand shift

The inputs are illustrative model units, not data: \(a = 1160\), \(\eta = 100\), \(\bar Y = 1000\), \(\nu = 200\) and \(P^{e} = 1\). Part A predicts the short-run equilibrium \(P^{*} = 1.2\) and \(Y^{*} = 1040\), with both equations holding exactly. Part B shifts \(a\) by 30 and predicts \(\Delta P = 0.1\) and \(\Delta Y = 20\), from Proposition 2.2. Part C predicts a long-run price of 1.6 before the shift and 1.9 after it, with output at potential, from Proposition 3.1. Part D predicts an output share of \(\nu /(\eta + \nu )\) and a price change of \(30/(\eta + \nu )\) for five values of \(\nu \). The code uses exact fractions and no random draws, so the output is fixed.

"""E5 L05 lab 1: the linear AD-AS equilibrium, a demand shift traced exactly, and the long-run price level.

Inputs are illustrative model units, not data. Output is in index points, the price level is an index with
expected price level 1 at the base date, and every value is an exact fraction. Nothing is random.

The model (Assumptions A1 to A3 of Lesson 5): AD is Y = a - eta P with a, eta > 0; short-run AS is
Y = Ybar + nu (P - Pe) with nu > 0; potential output Ybar = 1000 index points; expected price level Pe = 1.

Part A: the short-run equilibrium price and output at a = 1160, and the residual of both equations.
Part B: a demand shift of +30 in a, the new equilibrium, and the exact changes beside their formulas: the price
change 30/(eta+nu) and the output change 30 nu/(eta+nu). It also prints the output share nu/(eta+nu) and the
price change per unit of a, 1/(eta+nu).
Part C: the long-run equilibrium, where expected prices equal the actual price, before and after the shift.
Part D: the output share nu/(eta+nu) and the price change for five slopes nu of the short-run AS curve.

Rounding rule: a printed decimal is the exact fraction rounded half to even at six decimal places.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

YBAR = Fraction(1000)
ETA = Fraction(100)
NU = Fraction(200)
PE = Fraction(1)
A_BASE = Fraction(1160)
SHOCK = Fraction(30)


def fmt(x: Fraction) -> str:
    """The exact value rounded half to even at six decimal places, with a minus sign where needed."""
    scaled = round(x * 10**6)
    sign = "-" if scaled < 0 else ""
    whole, frac = divmod(abs(scaled), 10**6)
    return f"{sign}{whole}.{frac:06d}"


def short_run(a: Fraction, eta: Fraction, nu: Fraction, ybar: Fraction, pe: Fraction) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """The price and output where AD (Y = a - eta P) meets short-run AS (Y = ybar + nu (P - pe)), with eta + nu > 0."""
    price = (a - ybar + nu * pe) / (eta + nu)
    return price, a - eta * price


def long_run(a: Fraction, eta: Fraction, ybar: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The price where expected prices equal the actual price and output is potential: Ybar = a - eta P."""
    return (a - ybar) / eta


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A to D. Every line is computed from the exact fractions above."""
    print("Part A: short-run equilibrium at a = 1160")
    p0, y0 = short_run(A_BASE, ETA, NU, YBAR, PE)
    print(f"price {fmt(p0)} output {fmt(y0)}")
    print(f"ad_residual {fmt(y0 - (A_BASE - ETA * p0))} as_residual {fmt(y0 - (YBAR + NU * (p0 - PE)))}")
    print(f"output_above_potential {y0 > YBAR}")

    print("Part B: demand shift of +30 in a")
    p1, y1 = short_run(A_BASE + SHOCK, ETA, NU, YBAR, PE)
    print(f"price {fmt(p1)} output {fmt(y1)}")
    print(f"delta_price {fmt(p1 - p0)} delta_output {fmt(y1 - y0)}")
    print(f"formula_delta_price {fmt(SHOCK / (ETA + NU))} formula_delta_output {fmt(SHOCK * NU / (ETA + NU))}")
    print(f"exact_match {p1 - p0 == SHOCK / (ETA + NU) and y1 - y0 == SHOCK * NU / (ETA + NU)}")
    print(f"output_share {fmt(NU / (ETA + NU))} price_share {fmt(1 / (ETA + NU))}")

    print("Part C: long-run equilibrium with expected price equal to the actual price")
    lr0 = long_run(A_BASE, ETA, YBAR)
    lr1 = long_run(A_BASE + SHOCK, ETA, YBAR)
    ly0 = A_BASE - ETA * lr0
    ly1 = A_BASE + SHOCK - ETA * lr1
    print(f"long_run_price_before {fmt(lr0)} long_run_price_after {fmt(lr1)}")
    print(f"long_run_price_change {fmt(lr1 - lr0)} long_run_output_before {fmt(ly0)} long_run_output_after {fmt(ly1)}")
    print(f"long_run_output_is_potential {ly0 == YBAR and ly1 == YBAR}")
    print(f"long_run_is_short_run_fixed_point {short_run(A_BASE + SHOCK, ETA, NU, YBAR, lr1)[0] == lr1}")

    print("Part D: output share and price change for five slopes of short-run AS")
    for slope in (Fraction(50), Fraction(100), Fraction(200), Fraction(400), Fraction(800)):
        share = slope / (ETA + slope)
        price_change = SHOCK / (ETA + slope)
        pa, ya = short_run(A_BASE, ETA, slope, YBAR, PE)
        pb, yb = short_run(A_BASE + SHOCK, ETA, slope, YBAR, PE)
        print(
            f"nu {slope} output_share {fmt(share)} price_change {fmt(pb - pa)} "
            f"output_change {fmt(yb - ya)} consistent {yb - ya == share * SHOCK and pb - pa == price_change}"
        )

3The long-run equilibrium

Proposition 3.1 (the long-run equilibrium)

Assume A1, A2 and A3. Then the long-run equilibrium has \[ P_{\mathrm{LR}} = \frac{a - \bar Y}{\eta }, \qquad Y_{\mathrm{LR}} = \bar Y. \] Under a demand shift \(\Delta a\), the long-run price changes by \(\Delta a / \eta \) and long-run output does not change.

Proof

Assumptions: A1, A2 and A3, with \(a, \eta > 0\), \(\nu > 0\) and \(P^{e} = P\). Under A3, A2 becomes \(Y = \bar Y + \nu (P - P) = \bar Y\), so long-run output is potential. A1 then gives \(\bar Y = a - \eta P\), so \(P_{\mathrm{LR}} = (a - \bar Y)/\eta \). This price is a fixed point of Proposition 2.1: with \(P^{e} = (a - \bar Y)/\eta \), the formula gives \(P^{*} = (a - \bar Y)(1 + \nu /\eta )/(\eta + \nu ) = (a - \bar Y)\,(\eta + \nu )/(\eta \,(\eta + \nu )) = (a - \bar Y)/\eta \). The shift in \(a\) changes \(P_{\mathrm{LR}}\) by \(\Delta a / \eta \), and it leaves \(Y_{\mathrm{LR}} = \bar Y\) unchanged.

Remark 3.2 (what A3 does)

In the short run the demand shock in Proposition 2.2 raises output by \(\nu \,\Delta a/(\eta + \nu )\). In the long run, under A3, the same shock raises the price level by \(\Delta a/\eta \) and leaves output at \(\bar Y\). With the Lab 1 values, the shock of 30 raises the long-run price by 0.3, and Part C prints this change. The model does not say how the economy moves from the short-run point to the long-run point, because it has no dynamics.

4Where the canon breaks

  • The demand curve is a specification. Assumption A1 makes aggregate demand linear in \(P\). Remark 1.2 shows that the IS–LM demand curve written with \(M/P\) is not linear in \(P\) unless investment does not respond to the interest rate (Lesson 4, Remark 4.4). Under the linear form, the proof of Proposition 2.1 needs no more than the linear algebra shown there. With a non-linear demand curve, the equilibrium equation is no longer linear, and the closed forms of Proposition 2.1 do not apply.
  • The expected price level is fixed in the short run. Proposition 2.2 holds \(P^{e}\) fixed. Proposition 2.2 does not cover a case in which expectations move with the shock. If they did, the short-run changes would be \(\Delta P = (\Delta a + \nu \Delta P^{e})/(\eta + \nu )\) and \(\Delta Y = \nu (\Delta a - \eta \Delta P^{e})/(\eta + \nu )\), which differ from Proposition 2.2 whenever \(\Delta P^{e} \neq 0\); this lesson does not compute that case further.
  • The long run assumes full adjustment. Proposition 3.1 uses A3, which sets \(P^{e} = P\) and makes output potential. If expectations do not adjust, the model does not give a long-run result. If the expected price stays at 1 after the shift of Lab 1 Part B, the short-run point has output 1060, which is 60 index points above potential.
  • Potential output is a parameter. \(\bar Y\) does not move in the model. A change in potential output, through a supply shock or a change in the resources available, is outside this specification.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The aggregate demand curve slopes upward, because higher prices raise income.”

    Assumption A1 gives \(Y^{d} = a - \eta P\) with \(\eta > 0\), so the curve slopes downward. The lesson's specification does not contain a channel from the price level to income that would reverse the slope. With \(a = 1160\) and \(\eta = 100\), demand is \(1160 - 100 \times 1 = 1060\) at \(P = 1\) and \(1160 - 100 \times 1.6 = 1000\) at \(P = 1.6\), so a rise of 0.6 in the price level lowers demand by 60.

  2. “A demand shift changes only the price level, because prices absorb it.”

    Proposition 2.2 gives \(\Delta Y = \nu \,\Delta a/(\eta + \nu )\), which is positive for every positive shift under A1 and A2. Part B of Lab 1 prints an output change of 20.000000 for a shift of 30, so two thirds of the shift reaches output at \(\nu = 200\).

  3. “In the long run a demand shift still changes output, because the economy stays on the short-run curve.”

    Under A3, the long-run output is \(\bar Y\) for every value of \(a\), by Proposition 3.1. Part C of Lab 1 prints long-run output of 1000 before and after the shift, and the long-run effect on the price level is the change of 0.3, with no change in output.

Mechanics

L05-M1 · Short-run equilibrium with a higher expected price

Let \(a = 1100\), \(\eta = 100\), \(\bar Y = 1000\), \(\nu = 100\) and \(P^{e} = 1.1\). Find the short-run equilibrium price and output, and check the short-run supply equation.

Solution

Proposition 2.1 gives \(P^{*} = (1100 - 1000 + 100 \times 1.1)/(100 + 100) = (100 + 110)/200 = 1.05\). Then \(Y^{*} = 1100 - 100 \times 1.05 = 995\). The supply equation gives \(1000 + 100 \times (1.05 - 1.1) = 1000 - 5 = 995\), which agrees.

L05-M2 · The output share of a shock with a flatter supply curve

Let \(\eta = 50\) and \(\nu = 150\). A demand shock raises \(a\) by 40. Find the output share, the change in output and the change in the price level.

Solution

The output share is \(\nu /(\eta + \nu ) = 150/200 = 3/4\). Proposition 2.2 gives \(\Delta Y = (3/4) \times 40 = 30\) and \(\Delta P = 40/200 = 0.2\). The output change is 30 index points and the price change is 0.2 index points.

L05-M3 · The long-run price before and after a shift

With \(a = 1100\), \(\eta = 100\) and \(\bar Y = 1000\), find the long-run price. Then find it when \(a\) rises to 1140, and state the long-run output in both cases.

Solution

Proposition 3.1 gives \(P_{\mathrm{LR}} = (1100 - 1000)/100 = 1\) before the shift and \((1140 - 1000)/100 = 1.4\) after it. The long-run output is \(\bar Y = 1000\) in both cases. Check at the new price: \(1140 - 100 \times 1.4 = 1000\).

Proof

L05-P1 · Uniqueness of the short-run equilibrium

Under A1 and A2, show that two short-run equilibria \((P_1, Y_1)\) and \((P_2, Y_2)\) must be equal.

Solution

Both pairs satisfy the supply equation, so \(Y_1 - \bar Y = \nu (P_1 - P^{e})\) and \(Y_2 - \bar Y = \nu (P_2 - P^{e})\). Both also satisfy the demand equation, so \(Y_1 = a - \eta P_1\) and \(Y_2 = a - \eta P_2\). Subtracting the two demand equations gives \(Y_2 - Y_1 = -\eta (P_2 - P_1)\), and subtracting the two supply equations gives \(Y_2 - Y_1 = \nu (P_2 - P_1)\). Equating the two differences gives \((\eta + \nu )(P_2 - P_1) = 0\). Since \(\eta + \nu > 0\), \(P_2 = P_1\), and then \(Y_2 = Y_1\) from the demand equation.

L05-P2 · The output share lies below 1 only because \(\eta > 0\)

Show that \(1 - \nu /(\eta + \nu ) = \eta /(\eta + \nu )\), and state the sign of this quantity under A1 and A2.

Solution

Write \(1 = (\eta + \nu )/(\eta + \nu )\), so \(1 - \nu /(\eta + \nu ) = (\eta + \nu - \nu )/(\eta + \nu ) = \eta /(\eta + \nu )\). Under A1, \(\eta > 0\), and under A2, \(\nu > 0\), so \(\eta + \nu > 0\). The quantity \(\eta /(\eta + \nu )\) is therefore strictly positive. Without \(\eta > 0\), the output share could equal 1, and Proposition 2.2 would state no strict bound.

L05-P3 · The long-run price is a fixed point

Show that if \(P^{e} = (a - \bar Y)/\eta \), then the short-run price of Proposition 2.1 equals \(P^{e}\).

Solution

Substitute \(P^{e} = (a - \bar Y)/\eta \) into \(P^{*} = (a - \bar Y + \nu P^{e})/(\eta + \nu )\). The numerator is \((a - \bar Y)(1 + \nu /\eta ) = (a - \bar Y)(\eta + \nu )/\eta \). Dividing by \(\eta + \nu \) gives \(P^{*} = (a - \bar Y)/\eta = P^{e}\).

Empirical

L05-E1 · Checking one row of Part D

Lab 1 Part D prints, for \(\nu = 400\), an output change of 24.000000 and a price change of 0.060000 for a shift of 30. Confirm both values from Proposition 2.2 and state the output share.

Solution

The output share is \(400/(100 + 400) = 4/5\), so \(\Delta Y = (4/5) \times 30 = 24\). The price change is \(30/(100 + 400) = 30/500 = 0.06\). Both agree with the printed row, and the printed line reads consistent True.

L05-E2 · How the output change grows with the slope

Using only the five output changes printed in Part D (for \(\nu = 50, 100, 200, 400, 800\)), compute the increase in output between consecutive slopes.

Solution

The printed output changes are 10, 15, 20, 24 and 26.666667. The increases are 15 − 10 = 5, 20 − 15 = 5, 24 − 20 = 4, and 26.666667 − 24 = 2.666667. Each increase is no larger than the one before, which matches \(\nu /(\eta + \nu )\) rising at a decreasing rate in \(\nu \). The exact increase from \(\nu = 400\) to \(\nu = 800\) is \(30 \times (8/9 - 4/5) = 30 \times 4/45 = 8/3 = 2.666667\).

L05-E3 · Reading the long-run price from Part C

Part C prints long-run prices of 1.6 and 1.9. Use Proposition 3.1 to find which shift in \(a\) produced the printed change, and state the basis of the rounding in the printed values.

Solution

The change is \(1.9 - 1.6 = 0.3\), and Proposition 3.1 gives a change of \(\Delta a/\eta = \Delta a/100\). So \(\Delta a = 30\), the shift used in Parts B and D. The printed values are the exact fractions 8/5 and 19/10, rounded half to even at six decimal places. Both are exact at that precision, so the rounding changes nothing.

Research

L05-R1 · Uniqueness with a non-linear demand curve

Replace A1 by a demand curve \(Y^{d} = F(P)\), where \(F\) is continuous and strictly decreasing on \((0, \infty)\), with \(F(P) \to \infty\) as \(P \to 0^{+}\). Keep A2 with \(\nu > 0\). Show that a short-run equilibrium exists and is unique.

Solution

Define \(H(P) = F(P) - \bar Y - \nu (P - P^{e})\) on \((0, \infty)\). An equilibrium is a zero of \(H\). The function \(F\) is strictly decreasing and \(-\nu (P - P^{e})\) is strictly decreasing because \(\nu > 0\), so \(H\) is continuous and strictly decreasing. As \(P \to 0^{+}\), \(H(P) \to \infty\) because \(F(P) \to \infty\) and the other terms stay bounded. As \(P \to \infty\), \(H(P) \to -\infty\) because \(-\nu P \to -\infty\) and \(F\) is decreasing, so \(F(P) \le F(1)\) for \(P \ge 1\). The intermediate value theorem gives a zero, and strict monotonicity gives only one zero.

L05-R2 · A dynamic question the model cannot answer

Suppose that after the shift of Lab 1 Part B, expectations for the next period were set equal to the new price 1.3. Use Proposition 2.1 to find the next short-run equilibrium, and state whether the lesson's model determines the sequence of points beyond the first.

Solution

With \(a = 1190\), \(\bar Y = 1000\), \(\nu = 200\), \(\eta = 100\) and \(P^{e} = 1.3\), Proposition 2.1 gives \(P^{*} = (190 + 200 \times 1.3)/300 = 450/300 = 1.5\), and \(Y^{*} = 1190 - 150 = 1040\). The model gives this one new point. It does not define how \(P^{e}\) is set in later periods, so it does not determine a sequence beyond the first. A complete sequence needs a dynamic model, which this lesson does not contain.

L05-R3 · Which assumption the long-run result needs

Name the assumption that Proposition 3.1 needs in addition to A1 and A2, state what the model gives without it, and compute the output gap that Part B's post-shift point would show if expectations stayed at 1.

Solution

Proposition 3.1 needs A3, the full adjustment of expectations, \(P^{e} = P\). Without A3, the model gives only the short-run equilibrium of Proposition 2.1 for the given \(P^{e}\), and it does not give a long-run point. If \(P^{e}\) stayed at 1 after the shift of 30, Part B's post-shift point has \(Y = 1060\), and the output gap is \(1060 - 1000 = 60\) index points. This is the short-run equilibrium with the old expected price, computed with Proposition 2.1.
