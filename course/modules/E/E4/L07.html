Price informativeness

  • Uses: random variable, expectation, variance, correlation, least-squares projection, noisy rational-expectations equilibrium
  • Introduces: price informativeness
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or finite check

A price is informative when a trader who sees it knows more about the payoff. That needs a number, and the number must respond to the things that change informativeness. This lesson uses the squared correlation between the payoff and the price. It shows that the number is the \(R^2\) of the best linear forecast of the payoff from the price (Lemma 7.4), that its closed form follows from the equilibrium of Lesson L06, and that, with the other parameters fixed, it falls as the noise supply grows and rises as more investors are informed (Proposition 7.7). Lesson L08 makes the informed fraction a choice, using the same measure.

1A measure of informativeness

Definition 7.1 (covariance, correlation and noise volume)

For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) with \(\Var(Y) > 0\) and \(\Var(X) > 0\), the correlation is \(\Corr(X, Y) = \Cov(X, Y)/\sqrt{\Var(X)\Var(Y)}\). In the model of Lesson L06, the noise volume is the standard deviation \(\sigma_u\) of the noise-trader supply \(u\), so the noise volume is measured in units of the asset.

Definition 7.2 (price informativeness)

The price informativeness is the population squared correlation between the payoff and the price, \[ \rho^2 = \Corr(v, p)^2 = \frac{\Cov(v, p)^2}{\Var(v)\, \Var(p)} . \] It is a number in \([0, 1]\) by Lemma 7.3, and it does not depend on the units of \(p\), by Remark 7.5.

Lemma 7.3 (bounds on correlation)

For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) with \(\Var(X) > 0\) and \(\Var(Y) > 0\), \(\Cov(X, Y)^2 \leq \Var(X)\Var(Y)\). Hence \(0 \leq \Corr(X, Y)^2 \leq 1\).

Proof

For every real \(t\), the variance \(q(t) = \Var(X + tY) = \Var(X) + 2t\,\Cov(X, Y) + t^2 \Var(Y)\) is nonnegative. This is a quadratic in \(t\) with leading coefficient \(\Var(Y) > 0\). A quadratic that is nonnegative for every real \(t\) has a discriminant that is not positive. The discriminant is \(4\Cov(X, Y)^2 - 4 \Var(X)\Var(Y)\), so \(\Cov(X, Y)^2 \leq \Var(X)\Var(Y)\). Dividing by \(\Var(X)\Var(Y) > 0\) gives the bounds.

Lemma 7.4 (squared correlation is the \(R^2\) of the best linear forecast)

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) have zero means, with \(\Var(X) > 0\) and \(\Var(Y) > 0\). The linear forecast \(bY\) with the smallest mean squared error \(\E(X - bY)^2\) has \(b = \Cov(X, Y)/\Var(Y)\). Its residual variance is \(\Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\), so the proportion of the variance of \(X\) that the forecast explains is \(1 - \Var(X - bY)/\Var(X) = \Corr(X, Y)^2\).

Proof

Since the means are zero, \(\E(X - bY)^2 = \Var(X) - 2b\,\Cov(X, Y) + b^2 \Var(Y)\). This quadratic in \(b\) has leading coefficient \(\Var(Y) > 0\), so it has a unique minimizer, at which the derivative \(-2\Cov(X, Y) + 2b\Var(Y)\) is zero. That gives \(b = \Cov(X, Y)/\Var(Y)\). At this \(b\), the value is \(\Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\). Dividing by \(\Var(X)\) and subtracting from 1 gives \(\Cov(X, Y)^2/(\Var(X)\Var(Y)) = \Corr(X, Y)^2\).

Remark 7.5 (scaling)

For any constant \(\alpha \neq 0\), \(\Corr(X, \alpha Y)^2 = \Corr(X, Y)^2\). The covariance is multiplied by \(\alpha\), and \(\Var(\alpha Y)\) is multiplied by \(\alpha^2\). The factors \(\alpha^2\) cancel in the ratio. So the informativeness of \(p\) and of \(p/\theta_s\) is the same, for any \(\theta_s \neq 0\).

2Informativeness in the model

Proposition 7.6 (closed form)

Assume A1 to A4 and G of Lesson L06, with \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\) and the equilibrium price \(p = \theta_s^\ast s - \theta_u^\ast u\) of Proposition 6.7 of that lesson. Then \[ \rho^2 = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2} = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \dfrac{a^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^4}{\lambda^2}\,\sigma_u^2} . \] Equivalently, \(\rho^2 = \tau_\eta/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\).

Proof

Let \(Y = p/\theta_s^\ast\). Since \(\theta_s^\ast = \bar\tau/(\tau_v + \bar\tau) > 0\), the division is valid, and Step 2 of the proof of Proposition 6.7 gives \(Y = v + \eta\), where \(\eta = \varepsilon - \kappa u\) is independent of \(v\) and has variance \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2\). By Remark 7.5, \(\Corr(v, p)^2 = \Corr(v, Y)^2\). Since \(\Cov(v, Y) = \Cov(v, v) + \Cov(v, \eta) = \sigma_v^2\) and \(\Var(Y) = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2\), the squared correlation is \(\sigma_v^4/(\sigma_v^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)) = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)\). Substituting \(\kappa^2 = a^2\sigma_\varepsilon^4/\lambda^2\) gives the explicit form. The equivalent form follows because \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\) and \(\sigma_v^2 = 1/\tau_v\).

Proposition 7.7 (the signs of the responses)

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition 7.6, with \(\sigma_v > 0\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon > 0\), \(a > 0\), \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) and \(\sigma_u > 0\). Hold the other parameters fixed. Then \(\rho^2\) is strictly decreasing in \(\sigma_u\), strictly decreasing in \(a\), strictly decreasing in \(\sigma_\varepsilon\), and strictly increasing in \(\lambda\).

Proof

Write \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/D\) with \(D = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\,\sigma_u^2\) and \(\chi = a^2\sigma_\varepsilon^4/\lambda^2\). Since \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\), \(\rho^2\) is strictly decreasing in \(D\) and strictly increasing in \(1/D\). Each claim follows from the sign of the change in \(D\), because \(D > 0\) throughout. Increasing \(\sigma_u\) raises \(\chi\sigma_u^2\) if \(\chi > 0\), which is true here, so \(D\) rises. Increasing \(a\) raises \(\chi\), since \(\chi\) is proportional to \(a^2\) with \(a > 0\), so \(D\) rises. Increasing \(\lambda\) lowers \(\chi\), since \(\chi\) is proportional to \(\lambda^{-2}\), so \(D\) falls. Increasing \(\sigma_\varepsilon > 0\) raises \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) and \(\sigma_\varepsilon^4\), and both enter \(D\) with nonnegative coefficients, so \(D\) rises. Each conclusion is strict, because the changed term is strictly monotone in its argument.

Remark 7.8 (the full-revelation benchmark)

Let \(\rho_0^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\), which is \(\Corr(v, s)^2\) because \(\Cov(v, s) = \sigma_v^2\) and \(\Var(s) = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Proposition 7.6 gives \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\sigma_u^2)\) with \(\chi > 0\). So \(\rho^2 < \rho_0^2\) for every \(\sigma_u > 0\), and \(\rho^2 \to \rho_0^2\) as \(\sigma_u \to 0\). The benchmark is the informativeness of the signal itself. It is not reached when the supply is noisy. This lesson does not treat \(\sigma_u = 0\) as a case of Proposition 6.7, which assumes \(\sigma_u > 0\).

Remark 7.9 (price variance is a different object)

The variance of the price is \(\Var(p) = \theta_s^{\ast 2}(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2) + \theta_u^{\ast 2}\sigma_u^2\), which includes the supply term. A larger supply variance adds to \(\Var(p)\), while Proposition 7.7 says that the same change lowers \(\rho^2\). Lab 1, Part B, prints both quantities across a sweep of the noise volume. The two move in opposite directions there.

Lab 1 · Informativeness from exact moments, sweeps and a seeded check

The lab uses the base instance of Lesson L06, with \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and noise volume \(\sigma_u = \tfrac12\). Parts A to E are exact, computed with Fraction values. Before running it, the hypothesis is this. The squared correlation from the moments equals the closed form \(\rho^2 = 1/3\) (Proposition 7.6). Over the noise volumes \(\sigma_u \in \{1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4\}\), \(\rho^2\) falls strictly, while \(\Var(p)\) rises. Over \(\lambda \in \{1/4, 1/2, 3/4\}\), \(\rho^2\) rises strictly, and over \(a \in \{1/2, 1, 2\}\) it falls strictly (Proposition 7.7). Every noise-sweep value lies below the benchmark \(1/2\) (Remark 7.8). Part F is SIMULATED: one seeded draw of 20 000 observations, with seed 7, and the sample squared correlation should lie within 0.02 of \(1/3\), a tolerance fixed before the run.

"""E4 L07 lab 1: price informativeness in the linear noisy rational-expectations model, from exact population formulas.

Inputs are illustrative numbers, plus one seeded simulation that is labelled SIMULATED. The model is the one of
lesson L06: a payoff v with prior variance SIG_V2, a signal s = v + eps with noise variance SIG_E2, a noise-trader
supply u with standard deviation sd (the noise volume), a mass lam of CARA investors with coefficient a who observe s,
and a price p = theta_s * s - theta_u * u that clears the market.

Part A: the squared correlation of payoff and price, Corr(v, p)^2, computed from the exact moments, and compared with
the closed form sig_v2 / (sig_v2 + sig_eta2), where sig_eta2 = sig_e2 + kappa^2 sd^2 and kappa = a sig_e2 / lam.
Part B: the sweep over the noise volume sd, with the price variance printed next to the informativeness.
Part C: the sweep over the informed fraction lam at the base noise volume.
Part D: the sweep over the CARA coefficient a at the base noise volume.
Part E: the benchmark of no supply noise, as a limit, and the largest value on the sweep.
Part F: SIMULATED. A seeded sample of N_DRAWS draws of (v, eps, u), and the sample squared correlation of v and p.

Units: the payoff v is in units of the asset (its liquidation value). Corr(v, p)^2 is dimensionless and lies
in [0, 1]. Every population value is an exact Fraction. The sample uses floating point and prints six decimals.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
from fractions import Fraction
from typing import NamedTuple

SIG_V2 = Fraction(1)
SIG_E2 = Fraction(1)
A_BASE = Fraction(1)
LAM_BASE = Fraction(1, 2)
SD_BASE = Fraction(1, 2)
SD_GRID = (Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1), Fraction(2), Fraction(4))
LAM_GRID = (Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(3, 4))
A_GRID = (Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1), Fraction(2))
SD_TINY = Fraction(1, 100)
SEED = 7
N_DRAWS = 20000
TOLERANCE = 0.02


class Coefficients(NamedTuple):
    """The linear price p = theta_s * s - theta_u * u, with the variance of the uninformed signal sig_eta2."""

    sig_eta2: Fraction
    theta_s: Fraction
    theta_u: Fraction


def check_inputs(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, a: Fraction, lam: Fraction, sd: Fraction) -> None:
    """Raise ValueError unless the variances are positive, a is positive, lam lies in (0, 1) and sd >= 0."""
    if sig_v2 <= 0 or sig_e2 <= 0:
        raise ValueError("payoff and signal variances must be positive")
    if a <= 0:
        raise ValueError("CARA coefficient must be positive")
    if not 0 < lam < 1:
        raise ValueError("informed fraction must lie strictly between 0 and 1")
    if sd < 0:
        raise ValueError("noise volume must be nonnegative")


def coefficients(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, a: Fraction, lam: Fraction, sd: Fraction) -> Coefficients:
    """The closed-form price coefficients of lesson L06, valid for sd > 0 and, at sd = 0, as the limit they define."""
    check_inputs(sig_v2, sig_e2, a, lam, sd)
    tau_v = 1 / sig_v2
    tau_e = 1 / sig_e2
    kappa = a / (lam * tau_e)
    sig_eta2 = sig_e2 + kappa * kappa * sd * sd
    tau_eta = 1 / sig_eta2
    tau_bar = lam * tau_e + (1 - lam) * tau_eta
    theta_s = tau_bar / (tau_v + tau_bar)
    return Coefficients(sig_eta2, theta_s, kappa * theta_s)


def rho2_from_moments(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, coef: Coefficients, sd: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Corr(v, p)^2 from the moments of (v, p): Cov(v, p)^2 / (Var(v) Var(p))."""
    cov_vp = coef.theta_s * sig_v2
    var_p = coef.theta_s * coef.theta_s * (sig_v2 + sig_e2) + coef.theta_u * coef.theta_u * sd * sd
    return cov_vp * cov_vp / (sig_v2 * var_p)


def rho2_closed(sig_v2: Fraction, coef: Coefficients) -> Fraction:
    """Corr(v, p)^2 = sig_v2 / (sig_v2 + sig_eta2): the squared correlation of v with Y = p / theta_s."""
    return sig_v2 / (sig_v2 + coef.sig_eta2)


def var_price(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, coef: Coefficients, sd: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Var(p) = theta_s^2 (sig_v2 + sig_e2) + theta_u^2 sd^2."""
    return coef.theta_s * coef.theta_s * (sig_v2 + sig_e2) + coef.theta_u * coef.theta_u * sd * sd


def part_a() -> Coefficients:
    """The base instance: the moment formula and the closed form agree exactly."""
    print("Part A: informativeness at the base instance")
    coef = coefficients(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, A_BASE, LAM_BASE, SD_BASE)
    moments = rho2_from_moments(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, coef, SD_BASE)
    closed = rho2_closed(SIG_V2, coef)
    print(f"base sig_v2 {SIG_V2} sig_e2 {SIG_E2} a {A_BASE} lam {LAM_BASE} noise_volume_sd {SD_BASE}")
    print(f"sig_eta2 {coef.sig_eta2} theta_s {coef.theta_s} theta_u {coef.theta_u}")
    print(f"rho2_from_moments {moments} rho2_closed_form {closed} equal {moments == closed}")
    return coef


def part_b() -> list[Fraction]:
    """The sweep over the noise volume, with the price variance beside the informativeness."""
    print("Part B: sweep over the noise volume sd, other parameters at the base instance")
    values: list[Fraction] = []
    for sd in SD_GRID:
        coef = coefficients(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, A_BASE, LAM_BASE, sd)
        rho2 = rho2_from_moments(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, coef, sd)
        values.append(rho2)
        print(f"sd {sd} sig_eta2 {coef.sig_eta2} var_p {var_price(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, coef, sd)} rho2 {rho2}")
    decreasing = all(values[i] > values[i + 1] for i in range(len(values) - 1))
    print(f"rho2_strictly_decreasing_in_sd {decreasing}")
    return values


def part_c() -> list[Fraction]:
    """The sweep over the informed fraction at the base noise volume."""
    print("Part C: sweep over the informed fraction lam, other parameters at the base instance")
    values: list[Fraction] = []
    for lam in LAM_GRID:
        coef = coefficients(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, A_BASE, lam, SD_BASE)
        rho2 = rho2_from_moments(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, coef, SD_BASE)
        values.append(rho2)
        print(f"lam {lam} sig_eta2 {coef.sig_eta2} rho2 {rho2}")
    increasing = all(values[i] < values[i + 1] for i in range(len(values) - 1))
    print(f"rho2_strictly_increasing_in_lam {increasing}")
    return values


def part_d() -> list[Fraction]:
    """The sweep over the CARA coefficient at the base noise volume."""
    print("Part D: sweep over the CARA coefficient a, other parameters at the base instance")
    values: list[Fraction] = []
    for a in A_GRID:
        coef = coefficients(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, a, LAM_BASE, SD_BASE)
        rho2 = rho2_from_moments(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, coef, SD_BASE)
        values.append(rho2)
        print(f"a {a} sig_eta2 {coef.sig_eta2} rho2 {rho2}")
    decreasing = all(values[i] > values[i + 1] for i in range(len(values) - 1))
    print(f"rho2_strictly_decreasing_in_a {decreasing}")
    return values


def part_e(sweep_values: list[Fraction]) -> None:
    """The benchmark of no supply noise, as a limit, and a tiny noise volume near it."""
    print("Part E: benchmark without supply noise, as a limit")
    benchmark = SIG_V2 / (SIG_V2 + SIG_E2)
    print(f"benchmark_corr_v_s_squared {benchmark}")
    coef = coefficients(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, A_BASE, LAM_BASE, SD_TINY)
    tiny = rho2_closed(SIG_V2, coef)
    print(f"sd {SD_TINY} rho2 {tiny} below_benchmark {tiny < benchmark}")
    below = all(value < benchmark for value in sweep_values)
    print(f"largest_sweep_value {max(sweep_values)} all_sweep_values_below_benchmark {below}")


def part_f(coef: Coefficients) -> None:
    """SIMULATED: the squared sample correlation of v and p against the population value."""
    print("Part F: SIMULATED sample check")
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    sd_v = math.sqrt(float(SIG_V2))
    sd_e = math.sqrt(float(SIG_E2))
    sd_u = float(SD_BASE)
    theta_s = float(coef.theta_s)
    theta_u = float(coef.theta_u)
    v_draws: list[float] = []
    p_draws: list[float] = []
    for _ in range(N_DRAWS):
        v = rng.gauss(0.0, sd_v)
        eps = rng.gauss(0.0, sd_e)
        u = rng.gauss(0.0, sd_u)
        v_draws.append(v)
        p_draws.append(theta_s * (v + eps) - theta_u * u)
    n = len(v_draws)
    mean_v = sum(v_draws) / n
    mean_p = sum(p_draws) / n
    cov = sum((v - mean_v) * (p - mean_p) for v, p in zip(v_draws, p_draws, strict=True))
    var_v = sum((v - mean_v) ** 2 for v in v_draws)
    var_p = sum((p - mean_p) ** 2 for p in p_draws)
    sample_rho2 = cov * cov / (var_v * var_p)
    population = float(rho2_closed(SIG_V2, coef))
    print(f"seed {SEED} draws {N_DRAWS} tolerance {TOLERANCE}")
    print(
        f"sample_rho2 {sample_rho2:.6f} population {population:.6f} within {abs(sample_rho2 - population) <= TOLERANCE}"
    )


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A to F. Parts A to E are exact; Part F is one seeded simulation."""
    coef = part_a()
    sweep = part_b()
    part_c()
    part_d()
    part_e(sweep)
    part_f(coef)


main()

3Where the canon breaks

  • Linear measure. \(\rho^2\) measures linear association. A price can carry information through a nonlinear function of the payoff while \(\rho^2 = 0\). Witness: let \(v \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) and \(p = v^2\). Then \(\Var(p) = \E[v^4] - 1 = 2 > 0\), and \(\Cov(v, p) = \E[v^3] - \E[v]\,\E[v^2] = 0\) because \(\E[v] = \E[v^3] = 0\) by symmetry, so \(\rho^2 = 0\). Yet \(p\) determines \(|v|\). Lemma 7.4 gives the \(R^2\) of the best linear forecast only.
  • Model dependence. Proposition 7.6 uses A1 to A4 and G of Lesson L06. Under non-normal primitives, the closed form of Proposition 7.6 is not established by this proof, and the formula is not claimed.
  • Population object. \(\rho^2\) is a population quantity. A sample correlation from finite data differs from it by sampling error, which Lab 1, Part F, shows for one seed.
  • Fixed informed fraction. Proposition 7.7 holds \(\lambda\) fixed. When the informed fraction is itself chosen, \(\lambda\) is no longer a fixed parameter, and Lesson L08 treats that case.
  • Units of noise. The noise volume is the standard deviation \(\sigma_u\) in units of the asset (Definition 7.1). The sweeps use that scale. A strictly increasing reparametrisation of the noise volume, such as \(\sigma_u^2\), changes the numbers but not the decreasing sign of Proposition 7.7 in \(\sigma_u\).

4Misconceptions

  1. “A price with more variance is a more informative price.”

    In Lab 1, Part B, the variance of the price rises from \(729/1444\) to \(35937/4802\) across the noise sweep, while \(\rho^2\) falls from \(4/9\) to \(1/66\). Price variance contains the supply term \(\theta_u^2\sigma_u^2\), which carries no payoff information (Remark 7.9).

  2. “Informativeness is the precision of the noise in the price.”

    The precision of the price's noise in the uninformed inference is \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\). At the base instance \(\tau_\eta = 1/2\) (Lesson L06, Lab 1, Part A), but \(\rho^2 = 1/3\). The correct formula is \(\rho^2 = \tau_\eta/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\), which also depends on the precision of the prior, \(\tau_v = 1\).

  3. “Noise makes the price uninformative, so any positive noise volume gives zero informativeness.”

    Proposition 7.6 gives \(\rho^2 > 0\) for every finite \(\sigma_u\). At the largest noise volume on the sweep, \(\sigma_u = 4\), \(\rho^2 = 1/66\) is small but positive (Lab 1, Part B).

  4. “More informed investors make the price less informative, because their demands add noise.”

    The mechanism runs the other way. The price's signal is \(Y = s - \kappa u\) with \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\), so a larger informed fraction lowers \(\kappa\) and the noise that the supply adds to \(Y\). Proposition 7.7 gives a strictly increasing \(\rho^2\) in \(\lambda\), and Lab 1, Part C, shows \(1/6\), \(1/3\) and \(9/22\) for \(\lambda = 1/4, 1/2, 3/4\).

5Problem set

Attempt each problem before opening its solution.

Mechanics

M1 · Informativeness at a higher noise volume

Take \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(\sigma_u = 1\). Compute \(\sigma_\eta^2\) and \(\rho^2\) from Proposition 7.6.

Solution

Here \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda = 2\), so \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + 4 \cdot 1 = 5\). Then \(\rho^2 = 1/(1 + 5) = 1/6\). This matches the \(\sigma_u = 1\) row of Lab 1, Part B.

M2 · A higher informed fraction

Take \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\sigma_u = \tfrac12\) and \(\lambda = \tfrac34\). Compute \(\rho^2\).

Solution

\(\kappa = 1/(3/4) = 4/3\), so \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + (16/9)(1/4) = 13/9\). Then \(\rho^2 = 1/(1 + 13/9) = 9/22\). This matches the \(\lambda = 3/4\) row of Lab 1, Part C, and it is larger than the value \(1/3\) at \(\lambda = 1/2\).

M3 · Two payoff variances

Take \(\sigma_v^2 = 2\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(\sigma_u = 1\). Compute \(\rho^2\), and state whether a larger payoff variance raises or lowers \(\rho^2\) at these values.

Solution

\(\kappa = 2\) and \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + 4 = 5\), so \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) = 2/7\). Holding the other values fixed, \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 5\) does not depend on \(\sigma_v^2\), so \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + 5)\). Its derivative in \(\sigma_v^2\) is \(5/(\sigma_v^2 + 5)^2 > 0\), so a larger payoff variance raises \(\rho^2\) here.

Proof

P1 · The discriminant bound

Prove Lemma 7.3 without quoting the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. State where you use \(\Var(Y) > 0\), and say what happens if \(\Var(Y) = 0\).

Solution

Expand \(q(t) = \Var(X + tY) = \Var(X) + 2t\Cov(X, Y) + t^2\Var(Y)\), which is nonnegative for every \(t\), because it is a variance. The leading coefficient \(\Var(Y)\) is positive, which makes \(q\) a genuine quadratic that is nonnegative everywhere, so its discriminant must be nonpositive. That gives \(\Cov(X, Y)^2 \leq \Var(X)\Var(Y)\). If \(\Var(Y) = 0\), then \(q\) is linear in \(t\), and nonnegativity forces \(\Cov(X, Y) = 0\), so the inequality still holds, but the proof above does not cover that case.

P2 · The explained proportion is the squared correlation

Under the zero-mean hypotheses of Lemma 7.4, show that the residual variance of the best linear forecast equals \(\Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\), using only the expansion of \(\E(X - bY)^2\).

Solution

With zero means, \(\E(X - bY)^2 = \E X^2 - 2b\E(XY) + b^2\E Y^2 = \Var(X) - 2b\Cov(X, Y) + b^2\Var(Y)\). Substituting \(b = \Cov(X, Y)/\Var(Y)\) gives \(\Var(X) - 2\Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y) + \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y) = \Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\). Every step uses only the zero means and the expansion.

P3 · Scaling leaves the squared correlation unchanged

Prove Remark 7.5 for a constant \(\alpha \neq 0\), and state which property of the covariance and the variance it uses.

Solution

\(\Cov(X, \alpha Y) = \alpha\Cov(X, Y)\) and \(\Var(\alpha Y) = \alpha^2 \Var(Y)\), by bilinearity of covariance and the rule \(\Var(\alpha Y) = \alpha^2\Var(Y)\). Then \(\Corr(X, \alpha Y)^2 = \alpha^2\Cov(X, Y)^2/(\Var(X)\alpha^2\Var(Y))\). The factor \(\alpha^2\) cancels, which needs \(\alpha \neq 0\) so that \(\Var(\alpha Y) > 0\).

Empirical

E1 · Reading the seeded check

Lab 1, Part F, prints a sample squared correlation of 0.330625, against the population value \(1/3\) and a tolerance of 0.02. Compute the difference in units of the tolerance, and state whether the check passes.

Solution

The difference is \(1/3 - 0.330625 = 0.002708\). In units of the tolerance it is \(0.002708/0.02 = 0.135\). The check passes, because the absolute difference is below the tolerance. The result is one simulated draw, labelled SIMULATED, so it does not measure the sampling distribution of the estimator.

E2 · Recovering a noise volume from a reported \(R^2\)

A hypothetical regression of the payoff on the price reports \(R^2 = 0.25\). The numbers are invented, not data. Assume the parameters \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\) and \(\lambda = \tfrac12\). What noise volume \(\sigma_u\) makes the population \(\rho^2\) equal to 0.25?

Solution

Treat the reported \(R^2\) as the population \(\rho^2\). This is an assumption, and Problem E3 explains why a sample \(R^2\) can differ from \(\rho^2\). Setting \(1/(1 + \sigma_\eta^2) = 1/4\) gives \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 3\). With \(\kappa = 2\), \(1 + 4\sigma_u^2 = 3\), so \(\sigma_u^2 = 1/2\) and \(\sigma_u = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.7071\). This inversion assumes the model is correct, which is what the regression cannot test by itself.

E3 · Why a sample \(R^2\) is not the population measure

Give two reasons why a regression \(R^2\) from a finite sample of prices can differ from \(\rho^2\), and say which of them Lab 1, Part F, can and cannot detect.

Solution

First, a finite sample has sampling error, so the sample \(R^2\) fluctuates around \(\rho^2\). Second, the regression may be misspecified, for example if the true forecast is nonlinear, so the linear \(R^2\) differs from the best forecast's explained share. Lab 1, Part F, detects only the first, and only for data generated by the model. It cannot detect misspecification, because the sample comes from the model itself.

Research

R1 · Informativeness as the CARA coefficient grows

Hold the other parameters fixed and let \(a \to \infty\). Show that \(\rho^2 \to 0\), and identify the step of Proposition 7.7 that carries the limit.

Solution

Since \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\) grows without bound, \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2 \to \infty\) when \(\sigma_u > 0\). Then \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) \to 0\). The step that carries the limit is the increase of \(\chi\) with \(a\), which makes \(D\) grow without bound.

R2 · Half of the benchmark

Find the noise volume \(\sigma_u\), in closed form, at which \(\rho^2\) equals half of the full-revelation benchmark \(\rho_0^2\) of Remark 7.8. Then evaluate it at \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\) and \(\lambda = \tfrac12\).

Solution

Setting \(\sigma_v^2/D = \tfrac12\sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\) gives \(D = 2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\). Since \(D = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\sigma_u^2\), this gives \(\chi\sigma_u^2 = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2\), so \(\sigma_u = \sqrt{(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)/\chi}\) with \(\chi = a^2\sigma_\varepsilon^4/\lambda^2\). At the stated values \(\chi = 4\) and \(\sigma_u = \sqrt{2/4} = 1/\sqrt{2}\).

R3 · The derivative in the noise volume

Compute \(\partial\rho^2/\partial(\sigma_u^2)\) from Proposition 7.6, and state its sign for every admissible parameter set. Show that it is not a constant multiple of \(\rho^2\).

Solution

With \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/D\) and \(D = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\sigma_u^2\), the derivative is \(-\sigma_v^2 \chi/D^2\). Its sign is negative, because \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\), \(\chi > 0\) and \(D > 0\). The ratio of the derivative to \(\rho^2\) is \(-\chi/D\), which depends on \(D\), and \(D\) changes with \(\sigma_u\). So the elasticity is not constant in \(\sigma_u\).

    Options
