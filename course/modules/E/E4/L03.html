The first welfare theorem

  • Uses: Arrow–Debreu equilibrium, Arrow–Debreu security, gradient, Pareto efficiency
  • Introduces: first welfare theorem
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or finite check
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

An equilibrium is a claim about prices, and it is also a claim about outcomes. The efficiency result (Proposition 3.4) says that, in the economy of Lesson L02, no feasible reallocation (Definition 3.1) can make some agent better off without making anyone worse off, at a competitive equilibrium. It states its assumptions and proves both parts. Part (a) is that statement, in the terms of Definition 3.2. Lemma 2.4 makes every equilibrium bundle interior, with every entry strictly positive. Part (b) says that, at an equilibrium, the rate at which an agent trades one state's good for another's at the same utility (Definition 3.3) equals the price ratio for both agents. A grid search is a check, not a proof.

1Feasibility, Pareto improvements and marginal rates

The economy is the one of Lesson L02: \(S\) states, probabilities \(\pi_s\), two agents with endowments \(e^1, e^2\), aggregate \(\bar e = e^1 + e^2\), and utilities \(U_i\). The assumptions used in this lesson are A1, A3 and A4. A1 is stated in Lesson L01, and A3 and A4 in Lesson L02; they keep their numbers here. The symbol \(e^i\) with an agent superscript is an endowment, as in Lesson L02. The security \(e_s\) of Lesson L01 (Definition 1.3) is used only in the proof of part (b) of Proposition 3.4, where it is named.

Definition 3.1 (feasible allocation)

A feasible allocation is a pair \((y^1, y^2)\) of bundles \(y^i \in \R^{S}_{+}\) with \(y^1 + y^2 = \bar e\). The condition \(y^1 + y^2 = \bar e\) says that the whole aggregate is distributed, with no good destroyed. Research problem R1 treats the case with free disposal.

Definition 3.2 (Pareto improvement)

Let \((x^1, x^2)\) be a feasible allocation. A feasible allocation \((y^1, y^2)\) is a Pareto improvement of it when \(U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^i)\) for both agents, with strict inequality for at least one. An allocation with no Pareto improvement is Pareto efficient, the notion of E1 applied to this economy.

Definition 3.3 (marginal rate of substitution between states)

Let \(u_i\) be differentiable as in A4, and let \(x\) be a strictly positive bundle (\(x_r > 0\) for every state \(r\)). A bundle with a zero entry has \(U_i = -\infty\) under A4, so the partial derivatives below are defined only at strictly positive bundles. The derivative \(u_i'\) is positive on \((0, \infty)\), as shown in the proof of Proposition 3.4 (b), so the ratio is well defined. The marginal rate of substitution of agent \(i\) between states \(s\) and \(t\) at \(x\) is \[ \mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x) = \frac{\partial U_i / \partial x_s}{\partial U_i / \partial x_t} = \frac{\pi_s\, u_i'(x_s)}{\pi_t\, u_i'(x_t)} . \] It is the number of units of the good in state \(t\) that agent \(i\) would give up for one more unit in state \(s\), at the same utility.

2The first welfare theorem

The statement below is the first welfare theorem for the economy of Definition 2.3. Its name is conventional. The result is a proposition that this lesson proves.

Proposition 3.4 (first welfare theorem)

Assume A1, A3 and A4, and let \((q^*, (x^{1*}, x^{2*}))\) be an Arrow–Debreu equilibrium in the sense of Definition 2.3, with prices \(q^* \in (0, \infty)^{S}\). Then: (a) no feasible allocation is a Pareto improvement of \((x^{1*}, x^{2*})\); and (b) for every agent \(i\) and every pair of states \(s\) and \(t\), \[ \mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x^{i*}) = \frac{q^*_s}{q^*_t}. \] In particular, the two agents have the same marginal rate of substitution between any two states.

Proof of (a)

Write \(w_i = q^* \cdot e^i\), so that \(w_1 + w_2 = q^* \cdot \bar e\). Here \(w_i > 0\), because \(q^*\) is strictly positive and \(e^i\) is strictly positive by A3. Lemma 2.4 therefore gives that every entry of \(x^{i*}\) is strictly positive, so \(U_i(x^{i*})\) is finite, and \(q^* \cdot x^{i*} = w_i\) by Proposition 2.5. Suppose \((y^1, y^2)\) is a Pareto improvement.
Step 1. If \(U_i(y^i) > U_i(x^{i*})\), then \(q^* \cdot y^i > w_i\). Otherwise \(y^i\) lies in the budget set \(B_i(q^*)\) and has a strictly higher utility than the demand \(x^{i*}\), which is impossible.
Step 2. If \(U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^{i*})\), then \(q^* \cdot y^i \ge w_i\). Suppose instead \(q^* \cdot y^i < w_i\). Since \(U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^{i*}) > -\infty\), the bundle \(y^i\) has positive entries. Here \(\iota = (1, \ldots, 1)\) is the vector of ones. Choose \(\delta > 0\) with \(q^* \cdot (y^i + \delta \iota) \le w_i\). Then \(y^i + \delta \iota \in B_i(q^*)\), and \(U_i(y^i + \delta \iota) > U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^{i*})\) by A4 and A1, which contradicts the demand property of \(x^{i*}\).
Step 3. Since the allocation is a Pareto improvement, some agent \(k\) satisfies \(U_k(y^k) > U_k(x^{k*})\), so \(q^* \cdot y^k > w_k\) by Step 1. The other agent \(j\) satisfies \(q^* \cdot y^j \ge w_j\) by Step 2. Adding, \[ q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) > w_1 + w_2 = q^* \cdot \bar e . \] But \(y^1 + y^2 = \bar e\) by feasibility, so the left side equals \(q^* \cdot \bar e\), which is a contradiction.

Proof of (b)

Fix an agent \(i\), two states \(s\) and \(t\), and write \(x = x^{i*}\), which is strictly positive by Lemma 2.4 (its wealth is positive, as in the proof of (a)). Define the bundle change \(d = e_s / q^*_s - e_t / q^*_t\), where \(e_s\) is the Arrow–Debreu security of Definition 1.3. Then \(q^* \cdot d = 1 - 1 = 0\), so \(x + \varepsilon d\) costs the same as \(x\) for every \(\varepsilon\). For \(|\varepsilon|\) small enough, \(x + \varepsilon d\) is strictly positive, so it lies in the budget set. Since \(u_i\) is differentiable, \(\varepsilon \mapsto U_i(x + \varepsilon d)\) has derivative at 0 equal to \[ G = \frac{\pi_s u_i'(x_s)}{q^*_s} - \frac{\pi_t u_i'(x_t)}{q^*_t} . \] If \(G > 0\), then \(U_i(x + \varepsilon d) > U_i(x)\) for small \(\varepsilon > 0\), and the bundle is affordable, which contradicts the demand property. If \(G < 0\), the same holds for small \(\varepsilon < 0\). So \(G = 0\). The derivative \(u_i'(x_t)\) is positive. Indeed, \(u_i'\) is nonnegative because \(u_i\) is strictly increasing, and nonincreasing because \(u_i\) is concave. If \(u_i'(x_t) = 0\), then \(0 \le u_i'(y) \le 0\) for every \(y > x_t\), so \(u_i\) is constant on \((x_t, \infty)\), which contradicts strict monotonicity. Hence \(\pi_t u_i'(x_t) / q^*_t > 0\), and dividing \(G = 0\) by it gives \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x) = q^*_s / q^*_t\), which is (b). The two agents share the price ratio, so their marginal rates agree.

Remark 3.5 (what each part uses)

Part (a) uses A4's strict monotonicity, A1 and A3 through Lemma 2.4, and the positivity of the prices. It uses no derivative. Part (b) uses the differentiability in A4 and the strict monotonicity and concavity of A4, which show that \(u_i'\) is positive (proof of (b)). Its strictly positive equilibrium bundle is the one that Lemma 2.4 supplies for part (a), so part (b) adds no positivity hypothesis. A boundary allocation with a zero entry is outside (b), and the lesson does not assert the MRS condition there.

Example 3.6 (the equilibrium of Lesson L02)

Take the economy of Example 2.11, with \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)\), prices \(q^* = (\tfrac18, \tfrac1{32}, \tfrac1{16})\) and bundles \(x^{1*} = (\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) and \(x^{2*} = (\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\). With \(u_i = \ln\), Definition 3.3 gives \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x) = (\pi_s / x_s) / (\pi_t / x_t)\). States are numbered 1, 2 and 3, as in Example 2.11. For agent 1, \(\mathrm{MRS}_{12} = 4\), \(\mathrm{MRS}_{13} = 2\) and \(\mathrm{MRS}_{23} = \tfrac12\). For agent 2 the same three numbers hold, and they equal \(q^*_1 / q^*_2 = 4\), \(q^*_1 / q^*_3 = 2\) and \(q^*_2 / q^*_3 = \tfrac12\). Lab 3 Part A checks these equalities in exact arithmetic.

Lab 3 · Marginal rates at the equilibrium, and a finite search for Pareto improvements

The lab uses no data. It takes the economy of Example 2.11 and computes its equilibrium in exact Fractions. Before running it, the hypothesis is this. Part A prints the marginal rates \(4\), \(2\) and \(\tfrac12\) for both agents, which equal the price ratios, so each line reads True (Proposition 3.4 (b)). Part B searches a grid of 125 feasible allocations, with each agent's share of each state on the grid \(\{0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, 1\}\). At the equilibrium the search should find no Pareto improvement on the grid, which is consistent with Proposition 3.4 (a) but is only a finite check. At the endowment the search should find at least one improvement on the grid. Problem R2 gives an explicit improvement by hand, and the printed count is the evidence for the grid search. Comparisons use floating-point utilities with a tolerance of \(10^{-12}\). No random draws are used.

"""E4 L03 lab 1: marginal rates of substitution at an Arrow-Debreu equilibrium, and a finite Pareto search.

Inputs are illustrative numbers, not data. This is the economy of Lesson L02: three states with probabilities PROBS,
an aggregate endowment equal to the sum of ENDOWMENTS, and two agents. Agent i's expected utility of a bundle x is the
sum over states of PROBS[s] times ln(x[s]). The equilibrium prices and demands are those of Lesson L02, which are
computed here in exact Fractions.

Part A: the marginal rate of substitution between states, (PROBS[s] / x[s]) / (PROBS[t] / x[t]), for each agent at
the equilibrium, checked exactly against the price ratio q[s] / q[t]. States are numbered 1 to 3 in the printed output,
as in the lesson, and indexed 0 to 2 in the code, so printed "states 1 2" is the code pair (0, 1).
Part B: a finite search. Agent 1 receives the fraction f[s] of the aggregate in state s, with f[s] in the grid
GRID, and agent 2 receives the rest. The search counts the grid allocations that make one agent strictly better off
and no agent worse off than the equilibrium, and the same count against the endowment allocation.

The utilities in Part B are floating-point. A comparison counts as worse when the new utility is below the reference by
more than TOL, and as better when it is above the reference by more than TOL, with TOL = 1e-12.

No random draws are used, so every run prints the same lines.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product

Payoff = tuple[Fraction, ...]

PROBS: Payoff = (Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 4))
ENDOWMENTS: tuple[Payoff, ...] = (
    (Fraction(3), Fraction(4), Fraction(1)),
    (Fraction(1), Fraction(4), Fraction(3)),
)
GRID: tuple[Fraction, ...] = (Fraction(0), Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(3, 4), Fraction(1))
TOL = 1e-12


def dot(a: Payoff, b: Payoff) -> Fraction:
    """The inner product of two vectors with one entry per state, as an exact Fraction."""
    return sum((x * y for x, y in zip(a, b, strict=True)), Fraction(0))


def aggregate(endowments: tuple[Payoff, ...]) -> Payoff:
    """The aggregate endowment in each state."""
    n_states = len(endowments[0])
    return tuple(sum((e[s] for e in endowments), Fraction(0)) for s in range(n_states))


def equilibrium(probabilities: Payoff, endowments: tuple[Payoff, ...]) -> tuple[Payoff, Payoff, Payoff]:
    """Prices q[s] = PROBS[s] / aggregate[s], and demands x[s] = PROBS[s] * w / q[s] with wealth w = q . endowment."""
    total = aggregate(endowments)
    prices = tuple(p / t for p, t in zip(probabilities, total, strict=True))
    demands = []
    for endowment in endowments:
        wealth = dot(prices, endowment)
        demands.append(tuple(p * wealth / q for p, q in zip(probabilities, prices, strict=True)))
    return prices, demands[0], demands[1]


def utility(probabilities: Payoff, bundle: Payoff) -> float:
    """The expected log utility, the sum of PROBS[s] * ln(bundle[s]) as a float; a zero entry gives minus infinity."""
    total = 0.0
    for p, x in zip(probabilities, bundle, strict=True):
        if x == 0:
            return -math.inf
        total += float(p) * math.log(float(x))
    return total


def improves(candidate: tuple[float, float], reference: tuple[float, float]) -> bool:
    """True when no agent is worse off by more than TOL and at least one agent is better off by more than TOL."""
    no_one_worse = all(c >= r - TOL for c, r in zip(candidate, reference, strict=True))
    someone_better = any(c > r + TOL for c, r in zip(candidate, reference, strict=True))
    return no_one_worse and someone_better


def grid_allocations(total: Payoff) -> list[tuple[Payoff, Payoff]]:
    """Every allocation with agent 1 receiving GRID[k] of the aggregate in each state and agent 2 the rest."""
    found: list[tuple[Payoff, Payoff]] = []
    for fractions in product(GRID, repeat=len(total)):
        first = tuple(f * t for f, t in zip(fractions, total, strict=True))
        second = tuple((1 - f) * t for f, t in zip(fractions, total, strict=True))
        found.append((first, second))
    return found


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A and B. Exact Fractions in Part A and integer counts in Part B make the output reproducible."""
    total = aggregate(ENDOWMENTS)
    prices, x1, x2 = equilibrium(PROBS, ENDOWMENTS)
    print("Part A: marginal rates of substitution at the equilibrium")
    print(f"equilibrium_prices {tuple(str(q) for q in prices)}")
    for left, right in ((0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 2)):
        price_ratio = prices[left] / prices[right]
        mrs = []
        for bundle in (x1, x2):
            mrs.append((PROBS[left] / bundle[left]) / (PROBS[right] / bundle[right]))
        same = mrs[0] == mrs[1] == price_ratio
        print(
            f"states {left + 1} {right + 1} mrs_agent_1 {mrs[0]} mrs_agent_2 {mrs[1]} "
            f"price_ratio {price_ratio} all_equal {same}"
        )
    feasible = all(x1[s] + x2[s] == total[s] for s in range(len(total)))
    print(f"equilibrium_feasible {feasible}")

    print("Part B: finite search over the grid of allocations")
    reference_eq = (utility(PROBS, x1), utility(PROBS, x2))
    reference_endowment = (utility(PROBS, ENDOWMENTS[0]), utility(PROBS, ENDOWMENTS[1]))
    candidates = grid_allocations(total)
    print(f"grid_allocations {len(candidates)} grid_points_per_state {len(GRID)}")
    improving_eq = [(a, b) for a, b in candidates if improves((utility(PROBS, a), utility(PROBS, b)), reference_eq)]
    print(f"pareto_improvements_on_grid_at_equilibrium {len(improving_eq)}")
    improving_endow = [
        (a, b) for a, b in candidates if improves((utility(PROBS, a), utility(PROBS, b)), reference_endowment)
    ]
    print(f"pareto_improvements_on_grid_at_endowment {len(improving_endow)}")
    if improving_endow:
        first_a, first_b = improving_endow[0]
        print(
            f"first_improvement_at_endowment agent_1 {tuple(str(z) for z in first_a)} "
            f"agent_2 {tuple(str(z) for z in first_b)}"
        )


main()

Part A prints, for each pair of states, the marginal rate of each agent, the price ratio and a flag. Each flag reads True, so the equality of Proposition 3.4 (b) holds exactly for these bundles. Part B counts the grid allocations. It finds 0 Pareto improvements of the equilibrium among 125 grid allocations. It finds 2 Pareto improvements of the endowment, and it prints the first one, in which agent 1 has \((2, 4, 3)\) and agent 2 has \((2, 4, 1)\). The two endowment improvements are a finite witness that the endowment is not efficient.

3Where the canon breaks

  • Efficiency is not fairness. Definition 3.2 counts an allocation as better only when no agent is worse off. It does not rank an allocation that helps one agent and hurts another. Misconception 1 gives the consequence.
  • The equilibrium is taken as given. Proposition 3.4 assumes an equilibrium. Existence is Assumption A5 of Lesson L02, which this lesson does not prove.
  • Complete Arrow–Debreu markets. The theorem is about the economy of Definition 2.3, in which a good is traded in every state. It is not a statement about markets in which some claims cannot be traded. Lesson L04 studies that case.
  • Complete preferences. Proposition 3.4 compares bundles only through the utilities \(U_i\) of A4, which take each agent's ranking of bundles to be complete and represented by \(U_i\). An incomplete ranking, or one that no function of the form in A4 represents, is outside the theorem.
  • No externalities and no frictions. Each \(U_i\) depends only on agent \(i\)'s own bundle, and the budget set has no transaction costs. An externality, in the sense of E1, is outside the theorem.
  • Boundary allocations are outside (b). Part (b) needs differentiability in A4, and its bundles are strictly positive by Lemma 2.4, as in part (a). It says nothing about a boundary allocation with zero consumption in some state.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A competitive equilibrium is the best allocation for everyone.”

    Definition 3.2 says that a Pareto improvement needs no agent to be worse off. So Proposition 3.4 (a) rules out a Pareto improvement, which leaves every agent at least as well off and one agent strictly better off. It says nothing about an allocation that makes one agent better off and another worse off. Lab 3 Part B counts the grid allocations that satisfy Definition 3.2.

  2. “Equal marginal rates of substitution mean equal consumption.”

    Proposition 3.4 (b) says the rates are equal, and it says nothing about the bundles. In Example 3.6 both agents have the marginal rate 4 between states 1 and 2, but their bundles differ, \((\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) and \((\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\), as Lab 2 Part C prints. Equal rates at different bundles are consistent, because the rate depends on the bundle.

  3. “Efficiency is a property of allocations, so the theorem holds in any market.”

    The theorem is about the economy of Definition 2.3, with a price for each state's good and with the assumptions of Lesson L02. The proof uses the budget sets that those prices define (Step 1 and Step 2 of Proposition 3.4 (a)). Without that market structure the proof has no budget set to work with, so the theorem does not apply.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · A marginal rate between two states

Let \(\pi = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23)\) and \(x = (1, 4)\). Compute \(\mathrm{MRS}_{12}(x)\) for an agent with logarithmic utility.

Solution

The marginal rate is \(\mathrm{MRS}_{12} = (\pi_1 / x_1) / (\pi_2 / x_2) = (\tfrac13 / 1) / (\tfrac23 / 4) = \tfrac13 / \tfrac16 = 2\). So the agent would give up 2 units of the good in state 2 for one more unit in state 1, at the same utility. If \(q_1 / q_2 = 2\) are the equilibrium prices and this bundle is the agent's equilibrium bundle, Proposition 3.4 (b) says that the rate equals the price ratio, as here.

M2 · A demand at prices with ratio 2

Let \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(q = (2, 1)\) and wealth \(w = 6\). Find the logarithmic demand, and check that its marginal rate equals the price ratio.

Solution

By Proposition 2.8, \(x = (\tfrac12 \cdot 6 / 2, \tfrac12 \cdot 6 / 1) = (\tfrac32, 3)\). The spending is \(2 \cdot \tfrac32 + 3 = 6 = w\). The marginal rate is \((\tfrac12 / \tfrac32) / (\tfrac12 / 3) = \tfrac13 / \tfrac16 = 2\), which equals \(q_1 / q_2 = 2\). The proof of Proposition 3.4 (b) derives this equality for a strictly positive demand at prices \(q\). It uses the demand property, the budget, and the differentiability of A4, together with \(u_i' > 0\) from strict monotonicity and concavity. Proposition 3.4 itself is stated for equilibrium bundles, and this problem is one agent's demand at given prices.

M3 · Feasibility of a split

Let \(\bar e = (2, 2)\). Check whether \(y^1 = (1, 1)\) and \(y^2 = (1, 1)\) form a feasible allocation in the sense of Definition 3.1.

Solution

Both bundles are nonnegative, and \(y^1 + y^2 = (2, 2) = \bar e\). Definition 3.1 requires exactly this, so the split is feasible. It gives each agent half of the aggregate in each state, which is an equal split that is not necessarily an equilibrium allocation.

Proof

P1 · The case in which every agent gains

Under the assumptions of Proposition 3.4, show directly that no feasible allocation makes every agent strictly better off than the equilibrium.

Solution

Let \((y^1, y^2)\) be feasible with \(U_i(y^i) > U_i(x^{i*})\) for both agents. By Step 1 of the proof of Proposition 3.4 (a), \(q^* \cdot y^i > w_i\) for each \(i\). Adding gives \(q^* \cdot \bar e = q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) > w_1 + w_2 = q^* \cdot \bar e\), which is impossible. So no such allocation is feasible.

P2 · The marginal rates of the two-agent example

In the economy of Proposition 2.9, show that \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x^{i*}) = q^*_s / q^*_t\) for both agents and all states \(s\), \(t\), using the formulas of Proposition 2.9 and the derivative of the logarithm, \(u_i'(x) = 1/x\).

Solution

Here \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\), and \(u_i'(x) = 1/x\). So \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st} = (\pi_s / (\theta_i \bar e_s)) / (\pi_t / (\theta_i \bar e_t)) = (\pi_s \bar e_t) / (\pi_t \bar e_s)\). The share \(\theta_i\) cancels. Also \(q^*_s / q^*_t = (\pi_s / \bar e_s) / (\pi_t / \bar e_t) = (\pi_s \bar e_t) / (\pi_t \bar e_s)\). The two expressions are equal for both agents, which is (b) for this economy.

P3 · Which hypotheses each part uses

List the hypotheses that part (a) of Proposition 3.4 uses, and the hypotheses that part (b) uses in addition. Say which part would still hold if utilities were not differentiable.

Solution

Part (a) uses A1 and A3 through Lemma 2.4 (which gives positive optimal bundles), the strict monotonicity in A4, the positivity of \(q^*\), and feasibility. Part (b) uses in addition the differentiability in A4 and \(u_i' > 0\), which follows from the strict monotonicity and the concavity in A4. Part (b) needs no strict positivity beyond that of part (a), because Lemma 2.4 already gives the strict positivity of \(x^{i*}\) in the proof of part (a). Part (a) would still hold without differentiability, because its proof compares utilities and budgets and takes no derivative. Part (b) would not be established by this argument.

Empirical

E1 · Reading the marginal rates from Lab 3

In Part A of Lab 3, what do the printed values for states 1 and 2 equal, and what does the flag check?

Solution

The line reads "states 1 2 mrs_agent_1 4 mrs_agent_2 4 price_ratio 4 all_equal True". Both agents have marginal rate 4, and the price ratio \(q^*_1 / q^*_2 = (1/8) / (1/32) = 4\) is also 4. The flag checks that the two rates and the price ratio are all equal, which is Proposition 3.4 (b) for this pair of states.

E2 · What the endowment improvements show

Part B of Lab 3 prints 2 Pareto improvements of the endowment and gives the first one. What does this count show about the endowment, and what is the witness?

Solution

The count of 2 shows that the grid search finds allocations that make both agents at least as well off and one strictly better off, relative to the endowment. The conclusion that the endowment is not Pareto efficient rests on the hand witness of Problem R2, not on the count. The first witness gives agent 1 the bundle \((2, 4, 3)\) and agent 2 the bundle \((2, 4, 1)\), which is feasible because the two bundles add to \((4, 8, 4)\). Relative to the endowment, each agent's expected utility rises by \(\tfrac14 \ln\tfrac43 > 0\) at this witness, which is checked by hand (grade T). The count of 2 is a finite grid enumeration (grade C), and it does not show the full set of improvements.

E3 · The zero count at the equilibrium

Part B of Lab 3 prints 0 Pareto improvements of the equilibrium among the 125 grid allocations. Why does this count not prove efficiency, and which result does prove it?

Solution

The equilibrium allocation is not on the grid, and the grid has only 125 feasible points out of the continuum of feasible allocations. A zero count is therefore a finite enumeration, grade C with that basis. The efficiency claim is proved by Proposition 3.4 (a), which covers every feasible allocation.

Research

R1 · Free disposal

Replace the feasibility condition of Definition 3.1 by \(y^1 + y^2 \le \bar e\). Show that Proposition 3.4 (a) still holds, and identify the step that uses the strict positivity of every price \(q^*_s\).

Solution

Steps 1 and 2 of the proof give \(q^* \cdot y^i \ge w_i\) for each agent, with a strict inequality for the improved agent. Adding, \(q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) > q^* \cdot \bar e\). Now \(\bar e - (y^1 + y^2) \ge 0\), and \(q^* \cdot (\bar e - (y^1 + y^2)) \ge 0\) because every price is nonnegative. This gives \(q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) \le q^* \cdot \bar e\), which contradicts the strict inequality. So the last step needs only \(q^*_s \ge 0\). The strict positivity of the prices enters earlier, through Lemma 2.4, which makes each equilibrium utility finite.

R2 · A proof that the endowment is not efficient

In the economy of Example 2.11, show that the endowment \((e^1, e^2)\) is not Pareto efficient, by exhibiting a feasible allocation that is a Pareto improvement for small \(\varepsilon > 0\).

Solution

Let agent 1 give \(\varepsilon\) units of state 1 and receive \(4\varepsilon\) units of state 2, and agent 2 do the reverse. The new bundles are \(y^1 = (3 - \varepsilon, 4 + 4\varepsilon, 1)\) and \(y^2 = (1 + \varepsilon, 4 - 4\varepsilon, 3)\), which add to \(\bar e\) and are strictly positive for small \(\varepsilon\). The derivative of \(U_1(y^1)\) at \(\varepsilon = 0\) is \(\tfrac12 \cdot (-\tfrac13) + \tfrac14 \cdot 1 = \tfrac{1}{12} > 0\). The derivative of \(U_2(y^2)\) is \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot (-1) = \tfrac14 > 0\). So both utilities rise for small \(\varepsilon > 0\), and the endowment is not efficient. This is consistent with the marginal rates of the endowment between states 1 and 2, which are \(\tfrac83\) for agent 1 and 8 for agent 2 and differ. Agent 1 would give up \(\tfrac83\) units of state 2 for one more unit of state 1, so it would accept \(\tfrac38\) units of state 2 for each unit of state 1 that it gives up. The trade gives it 4 units of state 2 per unit, so agent 1 gains. Agent 2 would give up 8 units of state 2 for one more unit of state 1, and the trade costs it only 4 units of state 2 per unit, so agent 2 gains.

R3 · Which hypothesis part (b) needs

Explain why the proof of part (b) of Proposition 3.4 does not extend to a boundary allocation in which some agent consumes zero in some state, under A4.

Solution

The argument in the proof of (b) moves the bundle in both directions, with \(\varepsilon > 0\) and \(\varepsilon < 0\). At a bundle with a zero entry, a move that lowers that entry leaves the nonnegative orthant. Under the convention of A4 the utility there is \(-\infty\), so the derivative at \(\varepsilon = 0\) does not exist for that move. Lemma 2.4 ensures that equilibrium bundles are strictly positive when wealth is positive, so the argument applies to them.
