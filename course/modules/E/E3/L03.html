Constant relative risk aversion

  • Uses: expected utility (Lesson 1), Arrow–Pratt risk aversion and certainty equivalent (Lesson 2), random variable and expectation (M4), derivative (M2), and exponential and logarithm functions (M1)
  • Introduces: constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) utility
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A utility of wealth that is a power function has one parameter, the exponent. Under constant relative risk aversion (defined in §1) that exponent fixes the relative measure of risk aversion at every wealth level, and the certainty equivalent of a lottery scales with its stakes. This lesson defines the family, proves that constant relative risk aversion pins the family down up to scale and an additive constant, and derives the certainty equivalent in closed form. Lab 1 computes it for one fixed lottery in whole cents and checks the limit at exponent one and the scaling identity.

1The power family and its logarithmic limit

Assumptions A1 and A2 (wealth and a limit)
  • A1 (positive wealth). Wealth is a real number \(w > 0\), measured in cents. Every power and logarithm below is applied to positive numbers only.
  • A2 (slope of the exponential at zero). \(\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^h - 1}{h} = 1\). Module M1, Lesson 3 defines \(e\) as \(e^1\) and does not derive this limit, so the lesson takes it as given.
Assumptions F1 and F2 (calculus facts assumed without proof)
  • F1 (positive or zero derivative on an interval). A differentiable function with zero derivative on an interval is constant there, and a differentiable function with positive derivative on an open interval is strictly increasing there. Both are consequences of the mean value theorem.
  • F2 (elementary derivatives). For \(w > 0\) and real \(a\), \(\tfrac{d}{dw} w^{a} = a\,w^{a-1}\), and \(\tfrac{d}{dw} \ln w = 1/w\).
Definition 1.1 (a real power of a positive number)

For \(w > 0\) and a real number \(a\), the power is \(w^{a} = e^{a \ln w}\), with \(\ln\) the natural logarithm of Module M1, Lesson 3. The definition uses only the exponential and the logarithm of Lesson M1.

Proposition 1.2 (powers of powers)

For \(w > 0\) and real \(a\), \(b\): \((w^{a})^{b} = w^{ab}\). For positive \(x\) and \(y\) and real \(c\): \((xy)^{c} = x^{c} y^{c}\).

Proof

By Module M1, Lesson 3, Proposition 1.4, \(\ln(e^{y}) = y\) for every real \(y\). Applying this with \(y = a \ln w\) gives \(\ln(w^{a}) = a \ln w\). Then \((w^{a})^{b} = e^{b \ln(w^{a})} = e^{ab \ln w} = w^{ab}\). For the second claim, Module M1, Lesson 3, Proposition 1.6(b), gives \(\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y\), so \((xy)^{c} = e^{c(\ln x + \ln y)} = e^{c \ln x} e^{c \ln y} = x^{c} y^{c}\), where the middle equality is the addition rule A2 of Module M1, Lesson 3. \(\blacksquare\)

Definition 1.3 (CRRA utility, normalized)

For a real number \(\gamma\) and \(w > 0\), the CRRA utility with coefficient \(\gamma\) is

\[ u_{\gamma}(w) = \frac{w^{1-\gamma} - 1}{1 - \gamma} \quad (\gamma \ne 1), \qquad u_{1}(w) = \ln w. \]

The subtracted \(1\) makes \(u_{\gamma}(1) = 0\) for every \(\gamma\). The normalized form differs from \(w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)\) by the constant \(-1/(1-\gamma)\), which depends on \(\gamma\) and not on \(w\). That constant changes no certainty equivalent (Proposition 2.7 of this lesson) and no Arrow–Pratt measure (Lesson L02, Proposition 2.2, with \(b = 1\)), and Proposition 1.4 uses it for the limit at \(\gamma = 1\).

Proposition 1.4 (continuity at gamma equal to one)

Assuming A2, for every \(w > 0\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma \to 1} u_{\gamma}(w) = \ln w\).

Proof

Fix \(w > 0\), write \(L = \ln w\), and put \(s = 1 - \gamma\), so that \(s \to 0\) as \(\gamma \to 1\) and \(s \ne 0\) for \(\gamma \ne 1\). By Definition 1.1, \(w^{s} = e^{sL}\). If \(L = 0\), then \(w = 1\) and \(u_{\gamma}(1) = 0 = \ln 1\) for every \(\gamma\), so the limit holds. If \(L \ne 0\), write \(h = sL\), which tends to \(0\) as \(s\) does. Then

\[ u_{\gamma}(w) = \frac{e^{sL} - 1}{s} = L \cdot \frac{e^{h} - 1}{h}. \]

By A2, \((e^{h} - 1)/h \to 1\), so \(u_{\gamma}(w) \to L = \ln w\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.5 (three familiar members)

At \(\gamma = 0\), \(u_{0}(w) = (w - 1)/1 = w - 1\), which is affine in wealth with \(a = -1\) and \(b = 1\). By L01 Proposition 3.3 it ranks lotteries as the risk-neutral utility \(w\) does (L01 Definition 3.2). At \(\gamma = 1\), \(u_{1}(w) = \ln w\), which is the limit of Proposition 1.4. At \(\gamma = 2\), \(u_{2}(w) = (w^{-1} - 1)/(-1) = 1 - 1/w\), which is concave on \(w > 0\), as Proposition 1.7 shows.

Definition 1.6 (Arrow–Pratt measures, recalled)

For a twice differentiable utility \(u\) on \((0, \infty)\) with \(u'(w) > 0\), the absolute Arrow–Pratt measure is \(r_{A}(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w)\), and the relative measure is \(r_{R}(w) = w\,r_{A}(w)\). Lesson L02 defines both and names them.

Proposition 1.7 (the CRRA measures)

Assuming F2, for every real \(\gamma\) and every \(w > 0\), \(u_{\gamma}'(w) = w^{-\gamma}\) and \(u_{\gamma}''(w) = -\gamma\,w^{-\gamma - 1}\). Hence \(r_{A}(w) = \gamma / w\) and \(r_{R}(w) = \gamma\) for every \(w > 0\). So the relative measure is constant at \(\gamma\), and the absolute measure falls as wealth rises when \(\gamma > 0\).

Proof

Let \(\gamma \ne 1\) and \(s = 1 - \gamma\). By F2, the power rule \(\frac{d}{dw} w^{a} = a\,w^{a-1}\), valid for \(w > 0\) and real \(a\), gives \(u_{\gamma}'(w) = s\,w^{s-1}/s = w^{-\gamma}\), since \(s - 1 = -\gamma\). Differentiating again, \(u_{\gamma}''(w) = -\gamma\,w^{-\gamma - 1}\). For \(\gamma = 1\), \(u_{1}'(w) = 1/w = w^{-1}\) and \(u_{1}''(w) = -w^{-2} = -1 \cdot w^{-1 - 1}\), which are the same formulas at \(\gamma = 1\). Then \(r_{A}(w) = -u''/u' = \gamma\,w^{-\gamma - 1}/w^{-\gamma} = \gamma/w\), and \(r_{R}(w) = w \cdot \gamma/w = \gamma\). When \(\gamma > 0\), \(r_{A}\) is a decreasing function of \(w\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Characterization and the certainty equivalent

Theorem 2.1 (constant relative risk aversion pins the family down)

Assuming F1 and F2, assume (A3) \(u\) is twice differentiable on \((0, \infty)\) with \(u'(w) > 0\) for every \(w > 0\), and (A4) \(r_{R}(w) = \gamma\) for every \(w > 0\), for one real \(\gamma\). Then there are a constant \(K > 0\) and a constant \(C\) such that \(u(w) = K u_{\gamma}(w) + C\) for every \(w > 0\).

Proof

By A3, \(\ln u'\) is defined and differentiable on \((0, \infty)\), with derivative \(u''/u'\). By Definition 1.6 and A4, \(u''(w)/u'(w) = -r_{A}(w) = -\gamma/w\). Let \(g(w) = \ln u'(w) + \gamma \ln w\). Then \(g'(w) = -\gamma/w + \gamma/w = 0\) on \((0, \infty)\), using F2 for \(\frac{d}{dw}\ln w = 1/w\). By F1, a function with zero derivative on an interval is constant, so \(g(w) = c\) for a constant \(c\). Put \(K = e^{c} > 0\). Then \(u'(w) = e^{c} e^{-\gamma \ln w} = K w^{-\gamma}\). Let \(v = K u_{\gamma}\). By Proposition 1.7, \(v'(w) = K w^{-\gamma} = u'(w)\). So \((u - v)' = 0\) on \((0, \infty)\), and \(u - v\) equals a constant \(C\). Hence \(u = K u_{\gamma} + C\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.2 (what the theorem does not say)

Theorem 2.1 says that every utility satisfying A3 and A4 is an affine image \(K u_{\gamma} + C\) with \(K > 0\). It does not say that any investor has constant relative risk aversion. Whether a real decision maker has it is an empirical question, and this lesson cites no source that answers it.

Definition 2.3 (certainty equivalent of a finite lottery)

A lottery is a finite lottery as in L01 Definition 1.1: a random variable \(X\) with finite support, with outcomes \(x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}\), in cents on this page, and probabilities \(p_{1}, \dots, p_{n}\), where \(p_{i} \ge 0\) and \(\sum_{i} p_{i} = 1\). The certainty equivalent of \(X\) under a utility \(u\) is the amount \(c\) with \(u(c) = \E\, u(X)\), as in Lesson L02. Here every outcome is positive, so each \(x_{i}\) lies in the domain of Assumption A1.

Proposition 2.4 (closed form for CRRA)

Let \(X\) be a lottery with positive outcomes and \(\gamma \ne 1\). Put \(s = 1 - \gamma\) and \(M = \sum_{i} p_{i} x_{i}^{s}\). Then \(M > 0\), and the certainty equivalent under \(u_{\gamma}\) is \(\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma}(X) = M^{1/s}\). For \(\gamma = 1\), \(\mathrm{CE}_{1}(X) = \exp\big(\sum_{i} p_{i} \ln x_{i}\big)\).

Proof

Since \(x_{i} > 0\), each \(x_{i}^{s} > 0\), and the probabilities sum to 1, so \(M > 0\). Expected utility is \(\E\, u_{\gamma}(X) = \sum_{i} p_{i} (x_{i}^{s} - 1)/s = (M - 1)/s\), using \(\sum_{i} p_{i} = 1\). The equation \(u_{\gamma}(c) = (M - 1)/s\) reads \((c^{s} - 1)/s = (M - 1)/s\). Multiplying by \(s \ne 0\) gives \(c^{s} = M\). By Proposition 1.2, \(c = (c^{s})^{1/s} = M^{1/s}\), and this \(c\) is positive, so it lies in the domain. For \(\gamma = 1\), \(\E \ln X = \sum_{i} p_{i} \ln x_{i} = m\), and \(\ln c = m\) holds exactly when \(c = e^{m}\), by Module M1, Lesson 3, Proposition 1.4 and the uniqueness in its A3. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.5 (two exact certainty equivalents)

Let \(X\) take 1000 cents with probability \(1/2\) and 4000 cents with probability \(1/2\). At \(\gamma = 2\), \(s = -1\), \(M = \tfrac12 \cdot 1000^{-1} + \tfrac12 \cdot 4000^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{1600}\), so \(\mathrm{CE}_{2}(X) = 1600\) cents. At \(\gamma = 1\), \(\mathrm{CE}_{1}(X) = \exp(\tfrac12 \ln 1000 + \tfrac12 \ln 4000) = \sqrt{4\,000\,000} = 2000\) cents. Both values are computed by hand from Proposition 2.4, with no rounding.

Proposition 2.6 (scaling the stakes scales the certainty equivalent)

Let \(X\) be a lottery with positive outcomes and \(k > 0\). For every real \(\gamma\), \(\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma}(kX) = k\,\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma}(X)\), where \(kX\) has outcomes \(k x_{i}\) with the same probabilities.

Proof

Let \(\gamma \ne 1\) and \(s = 1 - \gamma\). By Proposition 1.2, \((k x_{i})^{s} = k^{s} x_{i}^{s}\), so \(M(kX) = k^{s} M(X)\). By Proposition 2.4 and Proposition 1.2 again, \(\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma}(kX) = (k^{s} M(X))^{1/s} = (k^{s})^{1/s} M(X)^{1/s} = k\,\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma}(X)\). For \(\gamma = 1\), \(\sum_{i} p_{i} \ln(k x_{i}) = \ln k + \sum_{i} p_{i} \ln x_{i}\), using \(\sum p_{i} = 1\) and Module M1, Lesson 3, Proposition 1.6(b). Then \(\mathrm{CE}_{1}(kX) = \exp(\ln k + m) = k\,e^{m} = k\,\mathrm{CE}_{1}(X)\), by the addition rule of Lesson M1. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.7 (affine transformations leave certainty equivalents unchanged)

Let \(a > 0\) and \(b\) be real, let \(\tilde u = a u + b\), and let \(X\) be a lottery whose certainty equivalent under \(u\) exists and is unique. Then the certainty equivalent of \(X\) under \(\tilde u\) exists, is unique, and equals its certainty equivalent under \(u\).

Proof

Under \(\tilde u\), the equation for the certainty equivalent \(c\) is \(a u(c) + b = \E\,\tilde u(X) = a\,\E u(X) + b\). Subtracting \(b\) and dividing by \(a > 0\) gives \(u(c) = \E u(X)\), which is the equation under \(u\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.8 (rounding a money amount once)

A certainty equivalent is an amount of money, and this unit reports it in whole cents. The rule used in Lab 1 is: compute the value once as a floating-point number, then round it to the nearest whole cent with halves rounded up, which is \(\lfloor v + 1/2 \rfloor\) for a value \(v \ge 0\). The rule does not round an intermediate value. If \(v\) is the unrounded value and \(R(v)\) is its rounded value, then \(|R(v) - v| \le 1/2\). For a whole number \(k \ge 1\), the rounded value of \(kv\) differs from \(k R(v)\) by at most \((k+1)/2\) cents, since \(|R(kv) - kv| \le 1/2\) and \(|kR(v) - kv| \le k/2\). Lab 1 checks this bound on its grid.

Lab 1 · The CRRA certainty equivalent in integer cents

Predicted from Propositions 1.4 to 2.6 before running, on one fixed lottery: prizes of 5000, 10000 and 20000 cents with probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. The expected prize is 11250 cents exactly. At \(\gamma = 0\) the certainty equivalent should equal 11250 cents, because \(u_{0}\) is linear. At \(\gamma = 1\) it should equal the geometric mean, which is 10000 cents exactly. At \(\gamma = 2\) the exact value is \(1/\sum_i p_i/x_i = 80000/9\), about 8888.89 cents, which rounds to 8889. The certainty equivalent should fall as \(\gamma\) rises over the grid 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4. The limit at \(\gamma = 1\) and the scaling identity should hold to the tolerances printed by the lab. No data is read, and no random number is drawn.

"""E3 lab L03: the CRRA certainty equivalent in integer cents, checked on one fixed lottery.

The lottery is fixed in the code: three final-wealth outcomes in cents, with exact probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4.
No random numbers are drawn, and no data is read, so nothing here is SIMULATED. Standard library only (itertools,
math and fractions), no network. Exact values use fractions.Fraction. The power forms are floating-point, so their
checks use stated tolerances. A certainty equivalent is a money amount: it is computed once as a float and rounded to
whole cents by floor(value + 1/2), which rounds halves up. Both the unrounded float and the rounded cents are printed.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
import math
from fractions import Fraction

PRIZES_CENTS = (5000, 10000, 20000)
PROBS = (Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1, 4))
GAMMAS = (0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0)
TOL = 1e-9


def crra_utility(w: float, gamma: float) -> float:
    """CRRA utility of positive wealth w in cents: (w**(1 - gamma) - 1) / (1 - gamma) if gamma != 1, else ln(w).

    The subtracted 1 is a constant. It changes no certainty equivalent and no Pratt measure, and it makes the
    utility continuous in gamma at gamma = 1. Raises ValueError when w is not positive, because the power form is
    defined on positive wealth only.
    """
    if w <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"CRRA utility needs positive wealth, got {w}")
    if gamma == 1.0:
        return math.log(w)
    return float((w ** (1.0 - gamma) - 1.0) / (1.0 - gamma))


def certainty_equivalent(prizes: tuple[int, ...], probs: tuple[Fraction, ...], gamma: float) -> float:
    """Unrounded certainty equivalent in cents: the amount c with u(c) equal to the expected CRRA utility.

    For gamma != 1 it equals (sum of p * x**(1 - gamma)) ** (1 / (1 - gamma)); for gamma = 1 it equals
    exp(sum of p * ln x). All prizes must be positive, so every power is defined.
    """
    p = [float(q) for q in probs]
    if gamma == 1.0:
        return math.exp(float(sum(pi * math.log(x) for pi, x in zip(p, prizes, strict=True))))
    s = 1.0 - gamma
    return float(sum(pi * x**s for pi, x in zip(p, prizes, strict=True)) ** (1.0 / s))


def to_cents(value: float) -> int:
    """Round a non-negative money amount, in cents, to the nearest whole cent, with halves rounded up."""
    if value < 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"money amounts here are non-negative, got {value}")
    return math.floor(value + 0.5)


def close(x: float, y: float) -> bool:
    """True when |x - y| <= TOL * max(1, |y|): an absolute tolerance near 1, a relative one above 1."""
    return abs(x - y) <= TOL * max(1.0, abs(y))


def main() -> None:
    mean = sum(q * x for q, x in zip(PROBS, PRIZES_CENTS, strict=True))
    print(f"prizes in cents: {list(PRIZES_CENTS)}; probabilities: {[str(q) for q in PROBS]}")
    print(f"expected prize in cents, exact: {mean}")

    ces: dict[float, float] = {}
    for g in GAMMAS:
        ce = certainty_equivalent(PRIZES_CENTS, PROBS, g)
        ces[g] = ce
        print(f"gamma {g:g}: certainty equivalent {ce:.4f} cents, rounded to {to_cents(ce)} cents")

    harmonic = 1 / sum(q / x for q, x in zip(PROBS, PRIZES_CENTS, strict=True))
    print(f"gamma 2 exact value 1 / sum of p/x: {harmonic} cents")
    print(f"gamma 2 float agrees with the exact value: {close(ces[2.0], float(harmonic))}")
    print(f"gamma 0 equals the expected prize: {close(ces[0.0], float(mean))}")
    print(f"gamma 1 equals the exact geometric mean 10000 cents: {close(ces[1.0], 10000.0)}")

    falling = all(ces[a] >= ces[b] for a, b in itertools.pairwise(GAMMAS))
    below = all(ces[g] <= float(mean) + TOL * float(mean) for g in GAMMAS)
    print(f"certainty equivalent falls as gamma rises on this grid: {falling}")
    print(f"every certainty equivalent is at most the expected prize on this grid: {below}")

    gap = max(abs(certainty_equivalent(PRIZES_CENTS, PROBS, 1.0 + s * 1e-6) - ces[1.0]) for s in (-1.0, 1.0))
    print(f"certainty equivalent at gamma 1 +/- 1e-6 is within 0.01 cents of gamma 1: {gap <= 0.01}")
    slopes = []
    for h in (1e-3, 1e-6):
        up = certainty_equivalent(PRIZES_CENTS, PROBS, 1.0 + h)
        down = certainty_equivalent(PRIZES_CENTS, PROBS, 1.0 - h)
        slopes.append((up - down) / (2.0 * h))
    agree = abs(slopes[0] - slopes[1]) <= 1e-3 * abs(slopes[1])
    print(f"central slope in gamma at gamma 1 agrees for h = 1e-3 and h = 1e-6 to 0.1 percent: {agree}")

    # The error of (w**s - 1) / s against ln(w), with s = 1 - gamma, is about s * (ln w)**2 / 2 (a first-order
    # term), so the check allows 1e-6 * (ln w)**2 at s = 1e-6.
    limit_ok = True
    for w in (0.5, 2.0, 10000.0):
        err = abs(crra_utility(w, 1.0 - 1e-6) - math.log(w))
        limit_ok = limit_ok and err <= 1e-6 * math.log(w) ** 2
    print(f"CRRA utility at gamma 1 - 1e-6 matches ln(w) within 1e-6 (ln w)^2 for w in 0.5, 2 and 10000: {limit_ok}")

    worst_rel = 0.0
    cents_ok = True
    for k in (2, 3, 10):
        scaled = tuple(k * x for x in PRIZES_CENTS)
        for g in GAMMAS:
            scaled_ce = certainty_equivalent(scaled, PROBS, g)
            worst_rel = max(worst_rel, abs(scaled_ce / (k * ces[g]) - 1.0))
            if abs(to_cents(scaled_ce) - k * to_cents(ces[g])) > (k + 1) / 2:
                cents_ok = False
    print(f"homogeneity CE(kx) = k CE(x) for k in 2, 3, 10 and every gamma, unrounded: {worst_rel <= 1e-12}")
    print(f"cents bound |round(CE(kx)) - k round(CE(x))| <= (k + 1) / 2 holds on this grid: {cents_ok}")

3Where the canon breaks

The family is a model of one parameter, and four limits apply to what this lesson establishes.

  • The domain is positive wealth. A1 restricts every utility here to \(w > 0\). The power and the logarithm are not defined for zero or negative wealth, so the results of this lesson do not cover a model with wealth at or below zero.
  • Constant relative risk aversion is one property. Theorem 2.1 characterizes the family by that property, under A3 and A4, and the calculus facts F1 and F2. A utility with a varying relative risk aversion is outside the family, and the lesson does not argue that real preferences are of this form. Remark 2.2 states what Theorem 2.1 does not say.
  • The numbers in Lab 1 are one lottery. The falling order of the certainty equivalent in \(\gamma\) holds on the lab's grid and for the fixed lottery only, and this page does not prove it for all exponents (Problem R3). The difference-quotient check at \(\gamma = 1\) is numerical and is not a proof of differentiability there. Proposition 2.6 proves the homogeneity identity that the lab checks on its grid.
  • Cent values are rounded floats. For a general exponent the certainty equivalent need not be a whole number of cents, and the cent value is a rounded floating-point result. A value that lies very close to a half cent may round differently from exact arithmetic, and the lab's tolerances do not remove that case.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Constant relative risk aversion means the utility of wealth is constant.”

    The utility \(u_{\gamma}(w)\) changes with \(w\). What is constant is the relative measure \(r_{R}(w) = \gamma\) (Proposition 1.7). The absolute measure \(r_{A}(w) = \gamma/w\) falls as wealth rises when \(\gamma > 0\). This lesson makes no claim about the size of a premium at two wealth levels, and L02 Problem R1 shows what the limit in L02 Proposition 3.1 does and does not give.

  2. “The logarithm is a separate case that sits outside the family.”

    The logarithm is the member at \(\gamma = 1\), and Proposition 1.4 shows that the family converges to it as \(\gamma \to 1\). The limit is not a rounding trick: the normalized form has the logarithm as its limit, and Lab 1 checks the limit of the certainty equivalent at \(\gamma = 1 \pm 10^{-6}\) against its value at \(\gamma = 1\).

  3. “Only the logarithm is scale invariant, so for other exponents doubling the stakes does not double the certainty equivalent.”

    Proposition 2.6 gives \(\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma}(kX) = k\,\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma}(X)\) for every real \(\gamma\), by the power identity \((kx)^{s} = k^{s} x^{s}\). The lab checks the identity for \(k = 2, 3, 10\) at every exponent on its grid. A non-integer exponent does not change the identity, but it can give a certainty equivalent that is not a whole number of cents (the lab prints 10931.7733 cents at \(\gamma = 0.25\)), and Remark 2.8 states the rounding rule for that case.

Mechanics

M1 · Both Arrow–Pratt measures at a point

Let \(u(w) = u_{1/2}(w) = (w^{1/2} - 1)/(1/2)\) for \(w > 0\). Find \(r_{A}(4)\) and \(r_{R}(4)\).

Solution

Here \(\gamma = 1/2\). By Proposition 1.7, \(r_{R}(w) = \gamma = 1/2\) for every \(w > 0\), so \(r_{R}(4) = 1/2\). Also \(r_{A}(w) = \gamma/w\), so \(r_{A}(4) = (1/2)/4 = 1/8\). A direct check: \(u'(w) = w^{-1/2}\) and \(u''(w) = -\tfrac12 w^{-3/2}\), so at \(w = 4\), \(-u''/u' = \tfrac12 \cdot 4^{-3/2} / 4^{-1/2} = \tfrac12 \cdot 4^{-1} = 1/8\).

M2 · A closed-form certainty equivalent

Let \(X\) take 1000 cents with probability \(1/4\) and 9000 cents with probability \(3/4\). Find \(\mathrm{CE}_{2}(X)\) exactly, in cents.

Solution

Here \(\gamma = 2\), so \(s = -1\) and \(M = \tfrac14 \cdot 1000^{-1} + \tfrac34 \cdot 9000^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{4000} + \tfrac{1}{12000} = \tfrac{4}{12000} = \tfrac{1}{3000}\). By Proposition 2.4, \(\mathrm{CE}_{2}(X) = M^{1/s} = M^{-1} = 3000\) cents exactly. The expected prize is \(7000\) cents, so the certainty equivalent lies below it, as L02 Proposition 1.3(a) gives for a concave utility.

M3 · Scaling the same lottery

Use the lottery of M2. Compute \(\mathrm{CE}_{2}(3X)\) directly from the closed form, and check it against Proposition 2.6.

Solution

The outcomes of \(3X\) are 3000 and 27000 cents with the same probabilities. Then \(M(3X) = \tfrac14 \cdot 3000^{-1} + \tfrac34 \cdot 27000^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{12000} + \tfrac{1}{36000} = \tfrac{4}{36000} = \tfrac{1}{9000}\), so \(\mathrm{CE}_{2}(3X) = 9000\) cents. Proposition 2.6 gives \(3 \times 3000 = 9000\), which agrees.

Proof

P1 · Certainty equivalents do not depend on affine scale

Prove Proposition 2.7 for a lottery whose outcomes are all positive, and say which of A1 and A2 the proof uses.

Solution

Let \(\tilde u = a u + b\) with \(a > 0\). A certainty equivalent \(c\) under \(\tilde u\) solves \(\tilde u(c) = \E \tilde u(X)\), that is \(a u(c) + b = a\,\E u(X) + b\). Subtracting \(b\) and dividing by \(a\) gives \(u(c) = \E u(X)\), which is the equation under \(u\), so the two certainty equivalents coincide. The proof uses the linearity of expectation and the positivity of \(a\). It uses neither A1 nor A2 by name: its one hypothesis is that the certainty equivalent under \(u\) exists and is unique, as the statement of Proposition 2.7 requires.

P2 · Concavity of the CRRA family for positive exponents

Show that \(u_{\gamma}\) is strictly increasing and concave on \((0, \infty)\) when \(\gamma > 0\), using Proposition 1.7.

Solution

By Proposition 1.7, \(u_{\gamma}'(w) = w^{-\gamma} > 0\) for every \(w > 0\), so \(u_{\gamma}\) is strictly increasing by F1. Also \(u_{\gamma}''(w) = -\gamma\,w^{-\gamma - 1}\), which is negative when \(\gamma > 0\) and \(w > 0\). A twice differentiable function with a negative second derivative is concave, which is the condition \(u'' \le 0\) of Lesson L02.

P3 · The risk-neutral member

Show that \(\mathrm{CE}_{0}(X) = \E X\) for every lottery with positive outcomes, using Proposition 2.4.

Solution

At \(\gamma = 0\), \(s = 1\), and Proposition 2.4 gives \(M = \sum_{i} p_{i} x_{i}\) and \(\mathrm{CE}_{0}(X) = M^{1/1} = M\). The sum \(\sum_{i} p_{i} x_{i}\) is the expected value \(\E X\), so \(\mathrm{CE}_{0}(X) = \E X\). This matches Example 1.5, where \(u_{0}(w) = w - 1\) is affine with slope 1: the equation \(c - 1 = \E X - 1\) gives \(c = \E X\) directly.

Empirical

E1 · Rounding the printed certainty equivalent at gamma 0.5 (formula input)

Lab 1 prints the certainty equivalent at \(\gamma = 0.5\) as 10615.8009 cents and the rounded value as 10616 cents. Explain which value the rounding rule of Remark 2.8 uses, and confirm the rounded cent value.

Solution

The rule rounds the unrounded floating-point value once. The four-decimal print 10615.8009 is a display of that value, and its fractional part 0.8009 is above one half, so the nearest whole cent with halves rounded up is 10616. The lab's second number is that rounding, and it is computed from the unrounded float, not from the printed four decimals. Both numbers agree with the rule.

E2 · Checking the exact gamma 2 value (formula input)

Lab 1 prints the exact value \(1/\sum_i p_i/x_i\) as 80000/9 cents and the float as 8888.8889. Show that these agree to four decimals, using Proposition 2.4.

Solution

At \(\gamma = 2\), Proposition 2.4 gives \(\mathrm{CE}_{2} = M^{-1}\) with \(M = \sum_i p_i/x_i = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{1}{5000} + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{1}{10000} + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{1}{20000} = \tfrac{1}{20000} + \tfrac{1}{20000} + \tfrac{1}{80000} = \tfrac{9}{80000}\). So \(\mathrm{CE}_{2} = 80000/9 = 8888.888\ldots\), and to four decimals this is 8888.8889, which matches the printed float. The proof of the agreement is the exact fraction; the printed float is a check on it.

E3 · The cents bound for a factor of ten (formula input)

Lab 1 checks the cents bound of Remark 2.8 for \(k = 10\). What is the bound in cents, and why does the bound allow the printed check to pass even when the rounded values differ?

Solution

The bound is \((k + 1)/2 = 11/2 = 5.5\) cents. It has two terms. The first rounding of \(10 \cdot \mathrm{CE}\) contributes at most \(1/2\) cent, since \(|R(v) - v| \le 1/2\) for the unrounded value \(v\). Multiplying the rounding error of \(\mathrm{CE}\), which is at most \(1/2\) cent, by ten contributes at most 5 cents. The total is at most 5.5 cents, which is the bound of Remark 2.8. The check therefore passes whenever the difference is at most 5.5 cents, and it does not require the two cent values to be equal.

Research

R1 · The certainty equivalent as the smallest outcome shrinks

Fix a lottery with two outcomes, \(x_{1} > 0\) with probability \(p_{1}\), where \(0 < p_{1} < 1\), and \(x_{2} > 0\) with probability \(1 - p_{1}\), and let \(x_{1} \to 0\). Show that \(\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma}\) tends to 0 when \(\gamma > 1\) and does not tend to 0 when \(0 < \gamma < 1\).

Solution

Let \(s = 1 - \gamma\). If \(\gamma > 1\), then \(s < 0\), and \(x_{1}^{s} \to \infty\) as \(x_{1} \to 0\). So \(M = p_{1} x_{1}^{s} + (1 - p_{1}) x_{2}^{s} \to \infty\), and \(\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma} = M^{1/s} = M^{-1/|s|} \to 0\). If \(0 < \gamma < 1\), then \(s > 0\), and \(x_{1}^{s} \to 0\), so \(M \to (1 - p_{1}) x_{2}^{s} > 0\), and \(\mathrm{CE}_{\gamma} \to ((1 - p_{1}) x_{2}^{s})^{1/s} = (1 - p_{1})^{1/s} x_{2} > 0\). So the certainty equivalent stays positive in that case.

R2 · Why a uniqueness theorem is not a description of behaviour

Theorem 2.1 says that a utility with constant relative risk aversion is an affine image of \(u_{\gamma}\). Explain why this theorem alone does not show that a particular person has that utility.

Solution

The theorem is an implication: if a utility satisfies A3 and A4, then it is \(K u_{\gamma} + C\). It says nothing about which utility a person holds. To use it for a person, one must first show that the person's preferences have a utility function with constant relative risk aversion, which is a claim about data and is outside this lesson. Remark 2.2 states the same point.

R3 · What a grid check cannot establish

Lab 1 finds that the certainty equivalent falls over the six exponents on its grid. Explain why this finite check is not a proof that it falls for every exponent, and state what would be needed to prove it.

Solution

The check tests six values of \(\gamma\) for one lottery, so it can refute the claim for those values but cannot establish it for the uncountable set of all real exponents and all lotteries. A proof would need an argument that the map \(\gamma \mapsto \mathrm{CE}_{\gamma}(X)\) is nonincreasing for every lottery with positive outcomes and every exponent. This lesson gives no such argument. The statement is therefore a grid result, labelled C in the key-results table.
