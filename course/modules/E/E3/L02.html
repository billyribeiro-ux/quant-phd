Risk aversion and the certainty equivalent

  • Uses: expected utility, expectation, variance and standard deviation, derivative
  • Introduces: certainty equivalent, Arrow–Pratt absolute and relative risk aversion
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A decision maker who prefers a sure amount to a gamble with the same expected value is risk averse. The certainty equivalent states that preference in money: it is the sure amount with the same expected utility as the gamble. The risk premium is the expected value minus that amount. These two objects measure risk aversion for the rest of the unit. This lesson proves that a concave utility gives a certainty equivalent no larger than the expected value. It defines the Arrow–Pratt measures of absolute and relative risk aversion, and it derives the second-order approximation of the premium for small gambles. Lab 1 checks the approximation on one gamble and shows that its gap shrinks as the gamble shrinks.

1Concavity and the certainty equivalent

Assumption A1 (finite support)

The gamble \(X\) is a finite lottery with outcomes \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) and probabilities \(p_1, \ldots, p_n\), as in Definition 1.1 of L01.

Assumption A2 (a concave utility on an interval)

\(u\) is twice differentiable on an open interval \(I\), with \(u'(y) > 0\) and \(u''(y) \le 0\) for every \(y \in I\). The first condition makes \(u\) strictly increasing. The second makes it concave. This lesson defines concavity by the sign of \(u''\) and uses no other definition of concavity or convexity.

Assumption A3 (outcomes in the domain)

Every outcome \(x_i\) of \(X\) lies in \(I\).

Assumption A4 (strict concavity)

\(u(y) < 0\) for every \(y \in I\). Assumption A4 is used in Proposition 1.3(b) and in Problems P2, P3 and R3.

Proposition 1.1 (the certainty equivalent exists and is unique)

Under A1 to A3, \(\E u(X)\) lies in the image \(u(I)\), and there is exactly one \(c \in I\) with \(u(c) = \E u(X)\).

Proof

The function \(u\) is differentiable on \(I\), so it is continuous, and the image \(u(I)\) of the interval \(I\) is an interval by the intermediate value theorem, a fact of calculus used without proof here. Let \(a = \min_i u(x_i)\) and \(b = \max_i u(x_i)\). Both belong to \(u(I)\) by A3. The expected utility \(\E u(X) = \sum_i p_i u(x_i)\) is a weighted average of the \(u(x_i)\) with weights summing to one, so \(a \le \E u(X) \le b\). The interval \(u(I)\) contains \(a\) and \(b\), so it contains \(\E u(X)\). Hence some \(c \in I\) satisfies \(u(c) = \E u(X)\). Since \(u\) is strictly increasing on \(I\) by A2, \(u\) is injective there, so \(c\) is unique.

Definition 1.2 (certainty equivalent and risk premium)

Under A1 to A3, the certainty equivalent of \(X\) under \(u\) is the unique \(c\) of Proposition 1.1, written \(\mathrm{CE}(X; u)\), so that \(u(\mathrm{CE}) = \E u(X)\). It is the sure amount with the same expected utility as the gamble. The certainty-equivalent risk premium is \(\E X - \mathrm{CE}(X; u)\). The phrase risk premium has other uses in finance, such as the equity risk premium, which is a different quantity. On this page, the risk premium is the certainty-equivalent risk premium, and nothing else.

Proposition 1.3 (a concave utility gives a certainty equivalent no larger than the expected value)

Under A1 to A3: (a) \(\mathrm{CE}(X; u) \le \E X\). (b) If, in addition, \(u''(y) < 0\) for every \(y \in I\) (Assumption A4), and some outcome with positive probability differs from \(\E X\), then \(\mathrm{CE}(X; u) < \E X\).

Proof

Let \(m = \E X\). Since \(\min_i x_i \le m \le \max_i x_i\) and \(I\) is an interval that contains every \(x_i\) by A3, \(m \in I\). Step 1 (the tangent line lies above \(u\)). Let \(y \in I\). Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder, a fact of calculus used without proof, gives a point \(\xi\) between \(m\) and \(y\), which lies in \(I\), with \[ u(y) = u(m) + u'(m)(y - m) + \tfrac12 u''(\xi)(y - m)^{2}. \] Since \(u''(\xi) \le 0\) by A2, \(u(y) \le u(m) + u'(m)(y - m)\). Step 2 (average over the lottery). Apply Step 1 to \(y = x_i\) and multiply by \(p_i \ge 0\). Summing over \(i\) and using \(\sum_i p_i = 1\) and \(\sum_i p_i (x_i - m) = m - m = 0\), \[ \E u(X) \le u(m) + u'(m) \sum_i p_i (x_i - m) = u(m). \] Step 3 (compare the certainty equivalent with \(m\)). Let \(c = \mathrm{CE}(X; u)\). Then \(u(c) = \E u(X) \le u(m)\). Both \(c\) and \(m\) lie in \(I\), and \(u\) is strictly increasing on \(I\), so \(c \le m\). This proves (a). For (b), under A4 the remainder term in Step 1 is strictly negative for \(y \ne m\), so the inequality of Step 1 is strict for each \(x_i \ne m\). Some \(x_i \ne m\) has \(p_i > 0\), so Step 2 is strict and \(\E u(X) < u(m)\). Then \(u(c) < u(m)\), and \(c < m\).

Remark 1.4 (the hypotheses used)

The proof uses A2 only through \(u''(\xi) \le 0\) in Step 1 and through \(u'\) being positive in the statement that \(u\) is strictly increasing. It uses A3 to place the outcomes and their mean in \(I\). For a utility with \(u'' \ge 0\), the same argument reverses the inequalities. This lesson does not state that case as a result.

Example 1.5 (the square root of wealth)

Let \(u(w) = \sqrt{w}\) on \(I = (0, \infty)\), and let the gamble be the wealth lottery \(W\) that pays 40, 100 and 160 CU with probabilities \(1/4\), \(1/2\) and \(1/4\). Its expected value is \(\E W = 100\) CU. Its expected utility is \(\E \sqrt{W} = \tfrac14\sqrt{40} + \tfrac12 \cdot 10 + \tfrac14\sqrt{160}\), which is about 9.743416. The certainty equivalent is the square of that number, \(\mathrm{CE} = 94.934165\) CU, and the premium is \(100 - 94.934165 = 5.065835\) CU. Part A of Lab 1 prints the same premium, 507 whole cents after the half-up rule, and it prints the certainty equivalent as 9493 cents. Proposition 1.3(a) holds: \(94.93 < 100\).

2The Arrow–Pratt measures

Definition 2.1 (absolute and relative risk aversion)

Let \(u\) be twice differentiable at \(w\), with \(u'(w) \ne 0\). The Arrow–Pratt absolute risk aversion at \(w\) is \(r_A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w)\), in units of 1/CU. The relative risk aversion at \(w\) is \(r_R(w) = w\, r_A(w)\), which is dimensionless. The two measures are the Arrow–Pratt pair named in the curriculum for this concept, and both are defined as formulas here.

Proposition 2.2 (invariance under positive affine transformations)

Let \(v = a + b u\) with \(b > 0\). Then \(r_A^{v}(w) = r_A^{u}(w)\) at every \(w\) where \(u'(w) \ne 0\).

Proof

The derivatives of \(v\) are \(v' = b\,u'\) and \(v'' = b\,u''\). So \(r_A^{v}(w) = -b\,u''(w)/(b\,u'(w)) = -u''(w)/u'(w)\), because \(b \ne 0\) cancels.

Example 2.3 (the square root and the linear utility)

For \(u(w) = \sqrt{w}\) on \((0, \infty)\), \(u'(w) = \tfrac12 w^{-1/2}\) and \(u''(w) = -\tfrac14 w^{-3/2}\). Therefore \(r_A(w) = \tfrac{1}{2w}\) and \(r_R(w) = \tfrac12\) at every \(w > 0\). At \(w = 100\) CU, \(r_A = 0.005\) per CU and \(r_R = 0.5\), as Part A of Lab 1 prints. The relative measure is constant here, which is the constant-relative-risk-aversion case that L03 defines as the CRRA utility. For the linear utility \(u(w) = w\), \(u'' = 0\), so \(r_A = 0\) at every \(w\).

3The second-order approximation of the risk premium

Fix a wealth level \(w\) and a finite gamble \(X\) with \(\E X = 0\) (A1 holds). Let \(s^{2} = \Var(X) = \sum_i p_i x_i^{2}\). For \(\varepsilon > 0\), the wealth lottery is \(W_\varepsilon = w + \varepsilon X\), with \(\E W_\varepsilon = w\). Its risk premium is \(\pi(\varepsilon) = w - \mathrm{CE}(W_\varepsilon; u)\), by Definition 1.2 with \(\E W_\varepsilon = w\).

Assumption A5 (local smoothness at the wealth level)

\(u\) is twice differentiable on an open interval \(I\) that contains \(w\), with \(u'(y) > 0\) for every \(y \in I\). Because \(I\) is open and \(X\) has finitely many outcomes, every \(\varepsilon > 0\) small enough puts all of \(w + \varepsilon x_i\) in \(I\), and only such \(\varepsilon\) are used below.

Proposition 3.1 (the second-order limit of the premium)

Under A1 and A5, \(\pi(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon^{2} \to \tfrac12\, r_A(w)\, s^{2}\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0^{+}\).

Proof

Twice differentiability of \(u\) at \(w\) gives Taylor's expansion with the Peano remainder, a fact of calculus used without proof: for \(h\) near 0, \[ u(w + h) = u(w) + u'(w)\, h + \tfrac12 u''(w)\, h^{2} + R(h), \qquad R(h)/h^{2} \to 0 \text{ as } h \to 0. \] Step 1 (expected utility of the wealth lottery). Apply the expansion to \(h = \varepsilon x_i\) and average with weights \(p_i\). Since \(\sum_i p_i x_i = 0\) and \(\sum_i p_i x_i^{2} = s^{2}\), \[ \E u(W_\varepsilon) = u(w) + \tfrac12 \varepsilon^{2} u''(w)\, s^{2} + \varepsilon^{2} \rho(\varepsilon), \qquad \rho(\varepsilon) = \varepsilon^{-2} \sum_i p_i R(\varepsilon x_i). \] Each \(R(\varepsilon x_i)/\varepsilon^{2}\) tends to 0 because \(x_i\) is fixed, and the sum is finite, so \(\rho(\varepsilon) \to 0\). Step 2 (the premium tends to zero). Let \(c_\varepsilon = \mathrm{CE}(W_\varepsilon; u) = w - \pi(\varepsilon)\). Then \(u(c_\varepsilon) = \E u(W_\varepsilon) \to u(w)\). The inverse of a continuous, strictly increasing function on an interval is continuous, so \(c_\varepsilon \to w\) and \(\pi(\varepsilon) \to 0\). Step 3 (expand the certainty equivalent). Apply the expansion with \(h = -\pi(\varepsilon)\), writing \(u(w - \pi) = u(w) - \pi\, u'(w) + \pi\, \eta(\pi)\) with \(\eta(\pi) \to 0\) as \(\pi \to 0\). Equating with Step 1, since \(u(c_\varepsilon) = \E u(W_\varepsilon)\), \[ -\pi(\varepsilon)\, u'(w) + \pi(\varepsilon)\, \eta(\pi(\varepsilon)) = \tfrac12 \varepsilon^{2} u''(w)\, s^{2} + \varepsilon^{2} \rho(\varepsilon). \] Divide by \(\varepsilon^{2}\) and set \(q(\varepsilon) = \pi(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon^{2}\): \[ q(\varepsilon)\bigl[\eta(\pi(\varepsilon)) - u'(w)\bigr] = \tfrac12 u''(w)\, s^{2} + \rho(\varepsilon). \] As \(\varepsilon \to 0^{+}\), the bracket tends to \(-u'(w)\), which is non-zero by A5, and the right side tends to \(\tfrac12 u''(w)\, s^{2}\). Hence \(q(\varepsilon) \to \tfrac12 u''(w)\, s^{2} / (-u'(w)) = \tfrac12 r_A(w)\, s^{2}\).

Remark 3.2 (the approximation and its error)

Proposition 3.1 says that \(\pi(\varepsilon) = \tfrac12 r_A(w)\, \Var(\varepsilon X) + o(\varepsilon^{2})\), where \(\Var(\varepsilon X) = \varepsilon^{2} s^{2}\). The term \(\tfrac12 r_A(w)\, \Var(\varepsilon X)\) is the approximation, and the error is the difference, which is \(o(\varepsilon^{2})\). This is an asymptotic statement. It says that the error divided by \(\varepsilon^{2}\) tends to 0 as \(\varepsilon\) falls. It does not give a size for the error at a fixed \(\varepsilon\), and Lab 1 reports the error at \(\varepsilon = 1\) as a fact about that gamble only.

Lab 1 · The certainty equivalent, the premium and the second-order approximation

The lab uses no data. It applies the formulas of this lesson to one gamble, the wealth lottery \(W = 100 + \varepsilon X\), where \(X\) pays \(-60\), 0 and 60 CU with probabilities \(1/4\), \(1/2\) and \(1/4\). The mean of \(X\) is 0 and its variance is 1800 CU squared. Before running it, the hypothesis is this. Under \(u(w) = \sqrt{w}\), the certainty equivalent at \(\varepsilon = 1\) is below 100 CU (Proposition 1.3), and the exact premium exceeds the approximation \(\tfrac12 r_A(100)\,\mathrm{Var}(X) = 4.5\) CU by a positive gap. As \(\varepsilon\) falls, the premium divided by \(\varepsilon^{2}\) approaches 4.5, so the gap shrinks at each printed scale (Proposition 3.1). Under \(u(w) = w\) the premium is zero at every scale. Floating-point arithmetic is used at full double precision. Money values are whole cents, computed from the unrounded CU value and rounded once, with a tie going up.

"""E3 L02 lab 1: certainty equivalents, risk premia and the second-order approximation on one gamble.

Inputs are illustrative, not data. The initial wealth is w = 100 CU. The gamble is X, with outcomes -60, 0 and 60 CU
and probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4 (exact Fractions). Its mean is 0 and its variance is 1800 CU squared. The wealth
lottery at scale eps is W = w + eps X, so its mean is w for every eps.

Two Bernoulli utilities are compared on this one gamble: u(w) = sqrt(w), which is strictly concave, and u(w) = w,
which is linear. The certainty equivalent is CE = u^{-1}(E u(W)); for sqrt, u^{-1}(y) = y^2 for y >= 0, and for the
linear utility u^{-1}(y) = y. The risk premium is w - CE. The Arrow-Pratt measures are r_A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w) and
r_R(w) = w r_A(w). The second-order approximation of the premium is 0.5 r_A(w) Var(eps X) = 0.5 r_A(w) eps^2 Var(X).

Floating-point arithmetic is used, at full double precision. Money outputs are whole cents: a CU amount is multiplied
by 100 and rounded once, with a tie going up (half up), by the function half_up_cents. Other values are unrounded CU
quantities, printed with six decimal places and labelled as unrounded.

Part A: at eps = 1, the certainty equivalent and the premium in cents, and the exact premium beside the approximation.
Part B: the ratio of the exact premium to eps^2 at five scales, beside the limit 0.5 r_A(w) Var(X).
Part C: for the linear utility, the premium is zero at every scale.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
import math
from collections.abc import Callable
from dataclasses import dataclass
from decimal import ROUND_HALF_UP, Decimal
from fractions import Fraction

WEALTH = 100
GAMBLE: list[tuple[int, Fraction]] = [(-60, Fraction(1, 4)), (0, Fraction(1, 2)), (60, Fraction(1, 4))]


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Utility:
    """A Bernoulli utility with its first and second derivatives and the inverse of u on its range."""

    name: str
    value: Callable[[float], float]
    slope: Callable[[float], float]
    curvature: Callable[[float], float]
    inverse: Callable[[float], float]

    def arrow_pratt(self, w: float) -> float:
        """The absolute risk aversion r_A(w) = -u''(w) / u'(w), in 1/CU."""
        return -self.curvature(w) / self.slope(w) + 0.0

    def relative(self, w: float) -> float:
        """The relative risk aversion r_R(w) = w r_A(w), which is dimensionless."""
        return w * self.arrow_pratt(w)


def sqrt_value(w: float) -> float:
    """u(w) = sqrt(w), for w > 0."""
    return math.sqrt(w)


def sqrt_slope(w: float) -> float:
    """u'(w) = 1/(2 sqrt(w)), for w > 0."""
    return 0.5 / math.sqrt(w)


def sqrt_curvature(w: float) -> float:
    """u''(w) = -1/(4 w^(3/2)), for w > 0."""
    return -0.25 / (w * math.sqrt(w))


def sqrt_inverse(y: float) -> float:
    """The inverse of sqrt on y >= 0: u^{-1}(y) = y^2."""
    return y * y


def linear_value(w: float) -> float:
    """u(w) = w."""
    return w


def linear_slope(w: float) -> float:
    """u'(w) = 1."""
    return 1.0


def linear_curvature(w: float) -> float:
    """u''(w) = 0."""
    return 0.0


def linear_inverse(y: float) -> float:
    """The inverse of the identity: u^{-1}(y) = y."""
    return y


SQRT = Utility("sqrt", sqrt_value, sqrt_slope, sqrt_curvature, sqrt_inverse)
LINEAR = Utility("linear", linear_value, linear_slope, linear_curvature, linear_inverse)


def half_up_cents(amount_cu: float) -> int:
    """The CU amount in whole cents: 100 times the amount, rounded once, with a tie going up."""
    return int(Decimal(amount_cu * 100).quantize(Decimal(1), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP))


def mean_and_variance() -> tuple[float, float]:
    """The exact mean and variance of the gamble X, computed from its Fractions and returned as floats."""
    mean = sum((p * x for x, p in GAMBLE), Fraction(0))
    variance = sum((p * x * x for x, p in GAMBLE), Fraction(0)) - mean * mean
    return float(mean), float(variance)


def certainty_equivalent(u: Utility, eps: float) -> float:
    """The wealth CE = u^{-1}(E u(W)) of the lottery W = WEALTH + eps X, unrounded, in CU."""
    expected = 0.0
    for x, p in GAMBLE:
        expected += float(p) * u.value(WEALTH + eps * x)
    return u.inverse(expected)


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A, B and C. Every value comes from the formulas above, so the output is reproducible."""
    mean, variance = mean_and_variance()
    print(f"gamble mean_cu {mean:.6f} variance_cu2 {variance:.6f} wealth_cu {WEALTH}")

    print("Part A: eps = 1; CE and premium in whole cents; unrounded CU for the approximation")
    for u in (SQRT, LINEAR):
        ce = certainty_equivalent(u, 1.0)
        premium = WEALTH - ce
        r_a = u.arrow_pratt(WEALTH)
        approx = 0.5 * r_a * variance
        print(
            f"utility {u.name} ce_cents {half_up_cents(ce)} premium_cents {half_up_cents(premium)} "
            f"r_A {r_a:.6f} r_R {u.relative(WEALTH):.6f} exact_premium_cu {premium:.6f} "
            f"pratt_premium_cu {approx:.6f} error_cu {premium - approx:.6f} "
            f"ce_below_mean {ce < WEALTH}"
        )

    print("Part B: premium over eps^2 for the square-root utility, beside the limit 0.5 r_A(w) Var(X)")
    limit = 0.5 * SQRT.arrow_pratt(WEALTH) * variance
    gaps: list[float] = []
    for eps in (1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.1, 0.01):
        premium = WEALTH - certainty_equivalent(SQRT, eps)
        ratio = premium / (eps * eps)
        gaps.append(abs(ratio - limit))
        print(f"eps {eps} premium_over_eps_squared {ratio:.6f} limit {limit:.6f} gap {abs(ratio - limit):.6f}")
    shrinking = all(later < earlier for earlier, later in itertools.pairwise(gaps))
    print(f"gaps_shrink_as_eps_falls {shrinking}")

    print("Part C: the linear utility has zero premium at every scale")
    zero = True
    for eps in (1.0, 0.5, 0.1):
        premium = WEALTH - certainty_equivalent(LINEAR, eps)
        zero = zero and half_up_cents(premium) == 0
        print(f"eps {eps} linear_premium_cents {half_up_cents(premium)} linear_r_A {LINEAR.arrow_pratt(WEALTH):.6f}")
    print(f"linear_premium_zero_at_all_scales {zero}")


main()

Part A prints, for \(\varepsilon = 1\), the certainty equivalent and the premium in whole cents, the Arrow–Pratt measures \(r_A\) and \(r_R\) at 100 CU, the exact premium, the second-order approximation, and their difference, all in CU except the cent values. 4Where the canon breaks

  • The approximation is local and asymptotic. Proposition 3.1 is a limit at a fixed wealth level. For the gamble of Lab 1 at \(\varepsilon = 1\), the approximation is off by 0.565835 CU, which is 11.17 percent of the exact premium of 5.065835 CU (0.565835 divided by 5.065835). The approximation is not a bound, and no size of error is claimed for a gamble that is not small.
  • Concavity is a property of the chosen function. The results here are conditional on A2. A concave \(u\) is a model of a decision maker, and the lesson does not claim that people hold such a utility. Whether choices under risk satisfy the expected-utility model is an empirical question. Lessons L06 and L07 take up models that depart from it, and no evidence for or against the model is cited here.
  • Absolute risk aversion is defined where the derivative is non-zero. Definition 2.1 needs \(u'(w) \ne 0\), which A2 and A5 guarantee on their intervals. Outside those intervals the measures are not defined by this lesson.
  • The premium is a wealth-level object. Every premium here is computed at a stated wealth \(w\). A premium at one wealth does not determine the premium at another, and the lesson does not compare wealth levels except in Problem R1.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The risk premium is half the variance of the gamble.”

    The premium is \(\tfrac12 r_A(w)\, \Var(X)\) only in the limit of Proposition 3.1, and \(r_A\) is not 1. For the gamble of Lab 1 at \(w = 100\) CU, \(\tfrac12 \Var(X) = 900\) CU, while \(\tfrac12 r_A(100)\, \Var(X) = 4.5\) CU and the exact premium is 5.065835 CU. Part A of Lab 1 prints both numbers. The half-variance rule gives 900 CU, which is about 178 times the exact premium of 5.065835 CU.

  2. “Absolute and relative risk aversion measure the same thing, so either one fixes the premium.”

    The two measures differ by the factor \(w\). For the square root at \(w = 100\) CU, \(r_A = 0.005\) per CU and \(r_R = 0.5\), which Part A of Lab 1 prints. Their units differ, since \(r_A\) is per CU and \(r_R\) is dimensionless (Definition 2.1). Each fixes a different feature of \(u\), and the premium in Proposition 3.1 uses \(r_A\).

  3. “A linear utility gives a zero premium only for a degenerate gamble.”

    For \(u(w) = w\), \(u(\mathrm{CE}) = \E u(X) = \E X\), so \(\mathrm{CE} = \E X\) for every finite lottery, and the premium is zero whatever the gamble. The linear utility is affine, which is the case of L01 Proposition 3.1 with \(a = 0\) and \(b = 1\). Part C of Lab 1 prints zero premiums for the linear utility at three scales of a non-degenerate gamble.

6Problem set

Attempt each problem before opening its solution.

Mechanics

M1 · The certainty equivalent of a two-point wealth lottery

A wealth lottery \(W\) pays 4 CU or 16 CU with probability \(1/2\) each. Under \(u(w) = \sqrt{w}\), compute \(\mathrm{CE}(W; u)\) and the risk premium.

Solution

The expected utility is \(\E \sqrt{W} = \tfrac12 \cdot 2 + \tfrac12 \cdot 4 = 3\). By Proposition 1.1, the certainty equivalent solves \(\sqrt{c} = 3\), so \(\mathrm{CE} = 9\) CU. The expected value is \(\E W = 10\) CU, so the risk premium is \(10 - 9 = 1\) CU. The premium is positive, as Proposition 1.3(b) predicts for a non-degenerate gamble under a strictly concave utility.

M2 · The Arrow–Pratt measures of the logarithm

For \(u(w) = \ln w\) on \((0, \infty)\), compute \(r_A(w)\) and \(r_R(w)\).

Solution

Here \(u'(w) = 1/w\) and \(u''(w) = -1/w^{2}\), both by the derivative of the logarithm, a fact of calculus. So \(r_A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w) = (1/w^{2})/(1/w) = 1/w\), in units of 1/CU. Then \(r_R(w) = w \cdot 1/w = 1\) at every \(w > 0\). The logarithm therefore has constant relative risk aversion equal to 1, which is the case \(\gamma = 1\) of the CRRA family that L03 defines.

M3 · The absolute measure of an exponential utility

For \(a > 0\), let \(u(w) = -e^{-aw}\). Compute \(r_A(w)\) and \(r_R(w)\).

Solution

The derivatives are \(u'(w) = a e^{-aw}\) and \(u''(w) = -a^{2} e^{-aw}\). Then \(r_A(w) = a^{2} e^{-aw}/(a e^{-aw}) = a\), which is constant. The relative measure is \(r_R(w) = a w\), which grows with wealth. The utility is strictly increasing because \(u' > 0\), so it satisfies A2 on \(\R\).

Proof

P1 · Scaling wealth and the relative measure

Show that if \(u\) has \(r_A(w) = a\) at every \(w\) (a constant), then \(r_R(w) = a w\), and that \(r_A\) does not change when \(u\) is replaced by \(v = 5 + 2u\).

Solution

Definition 2.1 gives \(r_R(w) = w\, r_A(w) = w a\). For \(v = 5 + 2u\), Proposition 2.2 with \(b = 2 > 0\) gives \(r_A^{v}(w) = r_A^{u}(w) = a\) at every \(w\). The constant 5 has no effect on either derivative, and the factor 2 cancels in the ratio.

P2 · The linear utility gives the expected value

Let \(u(x) = x\) on \(I = (0, \infty)\) and let \(X\) be any finite lottery with outcomes in \(I\). Show that \(\mathrm{CE}(X; u) = \E X\).

Solution

Here \(\E u(X) = \E X\) by Definition 1.3 of L01 with the identity utility. The certainty equivalent solves \(u(c) = c = \E X\), so \(c = \E X\). The utility is strictly increasing with \(u' = 1 > 0\) and \(u'' = 0\), so A2 holds with \(\le\), and A4 fails, so the strict inequality of Proposition 1.3(b) does not apply. The equality here is consistent with Proposition 1.3(a).

P3 · Degenerate gambles

Under A1 to A3, show that if \(X\) takes one value \(x\) with probability 1, then \(\mathrm{CE}(X; u) = x = \E X\), so the bound in Proposition 1.3 is attained.

Solution

With one outcome, \(\E u(X) = u(x)\), so \(u(c) = u(x)\). Since \(u\) is strictly increasing on \(I\), the unique solution in \(I\) is \(c = x\). The expected value is also \(x\), so the two are equal. Proposition 1.3(b) excludes this case because every outcome equals \(\E X\), which is the case that the strict inequality excludes.

E1 · The Part B gap at scale 0.1

E2 · The approximation error as a share of the premium

Using the exact premium and the approximation from Part A of Lab 1 at \(\varepsilon = 1\), compute the error as a percentage of the exact premium.

Solution

The error is \(5.065835 - 4.500000 = 0.565835\) CU. Its basis is the exact premium, so the share is \(0.565835 / 5.065835 = 0.1117\), which is 11.17 percent of the exact premium. Taken as a share of the approximation, it would be \(0.565835 / 4.5 = 12.57\) percent. The basis matters, and this answer uses the exact premium.

E3 · Checking the cent values in Part A

Part A of Lab 1 prints a certainty equivalent of 9493 cents and a premium of 507 cents for the square root. Check both from the unrounded CU values.

Solution

The unrounded certainty equivalent is 94.934165 CU, which is 9493.4165 cents, and the rounding rule keeps 9493. The unrounded premium is 5.065835 CU, which is 506.5835 cents, and it rounds half up to 507. Both values are whole cents, and each comes from one rounding of the unrounded value.

Research

R1 · Absolute risk aversion falling with wealth

Suppose \(r_A\) is decreasing in \(w\) on \(I\), and let \(s^{2} > 0\) be fixed. Show that the limit of \(\pi(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon^{2}\) in Proposition 3.1 is decreasing in \(w\), and state precisely what this does not show about the premium at a fixed \(\varepsilon\).

Solution

By Proposition 3.1, the limit at wealth \(w\) is \(\tfrac12 r_A(w)\, s^{2}\). Since \(s^{2} > 0\) is the same at every \(w\), the limit is a positive multiple of \(r_A(w)\), so it inherits the decrease. The statement is about the limit as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) only. It does not show that \(\pi(\varepsilon)\) decreases in \(w\) at a fixed \(\varepsilon\), because the error term in Remark 3.2 may depend on \(w\).

R2 · Why Lab 1 cannot prove the limit

Part B of Lab 1 checks five scales. Explain why these five values cannot establish Proposition 3.1 and what they do establish.

Solution

A limit as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) is a statement about every sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\), and five values are finitely many points of that set. No finite list can show that the gap is below every positive number for all small scales. What the five values establish is the printed sequence of gaps, which shrinks at each step, and the printed gaps are a numerical check of the proposition at those scales. The proof is in §3.

R3 · A strictly positive premium at every wealth level

Suppose A2 holds with \(u'' < 0\) on \(I\) (A4), and let \(X\) be a non-degenerate finite gamble with \(\E X = 0\). Show that the premium \(w - \mathrm{CE}(w + X; u)\) is strictly positive at every wealth level \(w\) for which \(w + X\) has outcomes in \(I\).

Solution

The wealth lottery \(w + X\) has expected value \(w\), so its risk premium is \(\E(w + X) - \mathrm{CE} = w - \mathrm{CE}\). Proposition 1.3(b) applies to the lottery \(w + X\), since its outcomes lie in \(I\) by assumption, and the lottery is non-degenerate because \(X\) is. It gives \(\mathrm{CE} < \E(w + X) = w\). The premium is therefore strictly positive at each such \(w\).
