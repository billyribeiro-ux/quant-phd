A decision under risk compares gambles, and a gamble is described by its outcomes and their probabilities. This lesson defines finite lotteries and expected utility, the score that averages a utility function over the outcomes with their probabilities. It proves that the score is linear in the probabilities, and it shows that a linear utility gives the expected value. Lesson L02 defines risk aversion through the shape of the utility function, and Lesson L04 compares the scores that many utility functions give to two gambles. This lesson does not prove that a preference ordering has the expected-utility form. Section 7 lists what is proved, and Remark 2.4 says what linearity does not give.

1Finite lotteries and expected utility

Money is measured in currency units (CU). One CU is 100 cents. This lesson's lab takes its amounts in whole cents. The L02 lab works in CU and prints its money values in whole cents, and the text uses CU for readability.

Definition 1.1 (finite lottery)

A finite lottery is a list of pairs \((x_1, p_1), \ldots, (x_n, p_n)\) in which the outcomes \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) are distinct real numbers, the probabilities \(p_i\) are non-negative, and \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i = 1\). Equivalently, a finite lottery is a random variable \(X\) with finitely many values, where \(\Prob(X = x_i) = p_i\). Each outcome is a final amount, in CU, that the decision maker receives.

Definition 1.2 (expected value of a lottery)

The expected value of a finite lottery is \(\E X = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i x_i\). This is the expectation of the random variable \(X\) of Definition 1.1.

Definition 1.3 (Bernoulli utility and expected utility)

A Bernoulli utility function is a real-valued function \(u\) defined on the outcomes. The expected utility of the finite lottery \(X\) under \(u\) is \(\E u(X) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\, u(x_i)\). The expected utility is a number, and it depends on \(u\) as well as on the lottery.

Assumption A1 (finite support)

The lottery \(X\) has finitely many outcomes, and \(u\) is real-valued on the set of those outcomes. Every result in this lesson that uses a sum over outcomes uses A1, and no result here allows a lottery with infinitely many outcomes.

Example 1.4 (one lottery under two utilities)

Let \(A\) be the lottery that pays 0 CU with probability \(1/4\), 100 CU with probability \(1/2\) and 400 CU with probability \(1/4\). Its expected value is \(\E X = 0 \cdot \tfrac14 + 100 \cdot \tfrac12 + 400 \cdot \tfrac14 = 150\) CU. Under the linear utility \(u(x) = x\) (in CU), its expected utility is \(\E u(X) = 150\) CU. Under \(u(x) = x^{2}\) (in CU squared), its expected utility is \(\E u(X) = 0 \cdot \tfrac14 + 10000 \cdot \tfrac12 + 160000 \cdot \tfrac14 = 45000\) CU squared. Lab 1 computes this lottery with outcomes in cents, under the utilities \(x\) and \(x^{2}/10000\), and prints 15000 cents for the linear utility, which is the 150 CU above, and 45000 for the quadratic one. Since \(x\) in cents is \(100\) times \(x\) in CU, the second utility is \(x^{2}\) in CU, and Part A of Lab 1 prints 45000 for it.

2Mixtures and linearity in the probabilities

Definition 2.1 (mixture)

Let \(L\) and \(M\) be finite lotteries, and let \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\). Write \(p_L(x)\) for the probability that \(L\) pays \(x\), and \(p_M(x)\) for the probability that \(M\) pays \(x\), with the convention that \(p_L(x) = 0\) when \(x\) is not an outcome of \(L\). The mixture \(\alpha L + (1 - \alpha) M\) is the lottery on the union of the outcomes of \(L\) and \(M\) that pays \(x\) with probability \(\alpha\, p_L(x) + (1 - \alpha)\, p_M(x)\).

Proposition 2.2 (a mixture is a lottery)

Under A1, for every \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\), the mixture \(\alpha L + (1 - \alpha) M\) is a finite lottery.

Proof

The union of the outcomes is finite, because each lottery has finitely many outcomes (A1). Each weight \(\alpha p_L(x) + (1 - \alpha) p_M(x)\) is non-negative, because \(\alpha\), \(1 - \alpha\), \(p_L(x)\) and \(p_M(x)\) are all non-negative. The weights sum to one, since \[ \sum_{x} \bigl[\alpha\, p_L(x) + (1 - \alpha)\, p_M(x)\bigr] = \alpha \sum_{x} p_L(x) + (1 - \alpha) \sum_{x} p_M(x) = \alpha \cdot 1 + (1 - \alpha) \cdot 1 = 1. \] The sums run over the union of the outcomes, and \(p_L(x) = 0\) off the outcomes of \(L\), so each sum equals the total probability of \(L\) or of \(M\).

Proposition 2.3 (expected utility is linear in the probabilities)

Let A1 hold, and let \(u\) be real-valued on the outcomes of \(L\) and \(M\). For every \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\), \[ \E u\bigl(\alpha L + (1 - \alpha) M\bigr) = \alpha\, \E u(L) + (1 - \alpha)\, \E u(M). \]

Proof

Let \(S\) be the union of the outcomes of \(L\) and \(M\), which is finite by A1. By Definition 2.1 and the convention for \(p_L\) and \(p_M\), \[ \E u\bigl(\alpha L + (1 - \alpha) M\bigr) = \sum_{x \in S} \bigl[\alpha\, p_L(x) + (1 - \alpha)\, p_M(x)\bigr]\, u(x). \] A finite sum splits into two sums, and each product is distributed: \[ \sum_{x \in S} \bigl[\alpha\, p_L(x) + (1 - \alpha)\, p_M(x)\bigr]\, u(x) = \alpha \sum_{x \in S} p_L(x) u(x) + (1 - \alpha) \sum_{x \in S} p_M(x) u(x). \] Since \(p_L(x) = 0\) for \(x\) outside the outcomes of \(L\), the first sum equals \(\E u(L)\). The same argument gives \(\E u(M)\) for the second sum.

Remark 2.4 (what linearity does and does not say)

Proposition 2.3 is an identity about the formula in Definition 1.3. It does not say that a decision maker's preferences over lotteries have this form. The question of which preference orderings over lotteries are represented by an expected utility is an axiomatic question, and this lesson neither answers it nor cites a source for its answer. Here the phrase names the formula only.

3Expected value, risk neutrality and affine transformations

Proposition 3.1 (an affine utility gives the expected value)

Let A1 hold, and let \(u(x) = a + b x\) for real constants \(a\) and \(b\); such a utility is called affine. Then \(\E u(X) = a + b\, \E X\). In particular, when \(a = 0\) and \(b = 1\), the expected utility of a lottery is its expected value.

Proof

By Definition 1.3, \(\E u(X) = \sum_i p_i (a + b x_i) = a \sum_i p_i + b \sum_i p_i x_i\). The first sum is 1 by Definition 1.1, and the second is \(\E X\) by Definition 1.2. Hence \(\E u(X) = a + b\, \E X\).

Definition 3.2 (risk neutrality)

A utility \(u(x) = bx\) with \(b > 0\) is called risk neutral. By Proposition 3.1 with \(a = 0\), the expected utility under a risk-neutral utility is \(b\, \E X\). Two lotteries therefore have the same expected utility under a risk-neutral utility exactly when they have the same expected value.

Proposition 3.3 (a positive affine transformation keeps the ranking)

Let A1 hold, and let \(v = a + b u\) with real constants \(a\) and \(b > 0\). For every finite lottery \(L\), \(\E v(L) = a + b\, \E u(L)\). Consequently, for any two finite lotteries \(L\) and \(M\), \(\E v(L) \ge \E v(M)\) if and only if \(\E u(L) \ge \E u(M)\).

Proof

By Definition 1.3, \(\E v(L) = \sum_i p_i (a + b\, u(x_i)) = a \sum_i p_i + b \sum_i p_i\, u(x_i) = a + b\, \E u(L)\), using \(\sum_i p_i = 1\). Subtracting the two expressions gives \(\E v(L) - \E v(M) = b\bigl(\E u(L) - \E u(M)\bigr)\). Since \(b > 0\), the two differences have the same sign, which is the equivalence stated.

Example 3.4 (two lotteries with the same expected value)

Let \(C\) pay 0, 150 and 300 CU with probability \(1/3\) each. Its expected value is 150 CU, the same as that of \(A\) in Example 1.4. Under the linear utility the two lotteries have equal expected utility, 150 CU in both cases, so the ranking is a tie. Under \(u(x) = x^{2}\) they do not tie: \(\E u(C) = (0 + 22500 + 90000)/3 = 37500\) CU squared, below the 45000 CU squared of \(A\). Proposition 3.3 says that a positive affine transformation of either utility keeps its ranking of these lotteries, so the tie under the linear utility and the strict ranking under the quadratic are properties of the two utilities. Part C of Lab 1 checks these orderings.

Lab 1 · Expected values and expected utilities of three lotteries

The lab uses no data. It is a set of exact computations on three lotteries A, B and C, with outcomes in cents and exact fraction probabilities. Before running it, the hypothesis is this. Under the linear utility, each lottery's expected utility equals its expected value (Proposition 3.1). Under the quadratic utility, the expected utilities of A, B and C are 45000, 21250 and 37500 squared CU, which are not the squares of the expected values. The linearity identity of Proposition 2.3 holds exactly for three mixing weights under both utilities. Each of the three pairs of A, B and C keeps its ordering under the transformation \(3 + 2u\) (Proposition 3.3). A lottery with a negative probability, and a lottery whose probabilities do not sum to 1, are each rejected with a ValueError. The code uses no random draws, so every run prints the same lines.

"""E3 L01 lab 1: expected value and expected utility of finite lotteries, computed exactly.

Inputs are illustrative, not data. An outcome is a final amount in whole cents, and every probability is an exact
Fraction. A lottery is a list of (outcome in cents, probability) pairs; its probabilities are non-negative and sum to
exactly 1. Two Bernoulli utilities are used: u(x) = x (linear in cents) and u(x) = x^2 / 10000 (quadratic). Both return
exact Fractions, so no rounding occurs and every printed value is exact.

Part A: the expected value and the expected utility of three lotteries under both utilities.
Part B: linearity in the probabilities, checked exactly for three mixing weights under both utilities.
Part C: a positive affine transformation v = 3 + 2u leaves the ordering of each pair of A, B and C unchanged; checked
exactly for each of the three pairs under both utilities.
Part D: a lottery with negative probabilities, and one whose probabilities do not sum to 1, are
rejected with ValueError.

No random draws are used, so every run prints the same lines.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections.abc import Callable
from fractions import Fraction

Lottery = list[tuple[int, Fraction]]
Utility = Callable[[int], Fraction]

LOTTERIES: dict[str, Lottery] = {
    "A": [(0, Fraction(1, 4)), (10000, Fraction(1, 2)), (40000, Fraction(1, 4))],
    "B": [(5000, Fraction(1, 2)), (20000, Fraction(1, 2))],
    "C": [(0, Fraction(1, 3)), (15000, Fraction(1, 3)), (30000, Fraction(1, 3))],
}


def check_lottery(lottery: Lottery) -> None:
    """Raise ValueError unless every probability is non-negative and the probabilities sum to exactly 1."""
    if any(p < 0 for _, p in lottery):
        raise ValueError("probabilities must be non-negative")
    if sum((p for _, p in lottery), Fraction(0)) != 1:
        raise ValueError("probabilities must sum to 1")


def expected_value(lottery: Lottery) -> Fraction:
    """The expected value E X = sum of p_i * x_i, in cents, as an exact Fraction."""
    check_lottery(lottery)
    return sum((p * x for x, p in lottery), Fraction(0))


def expected_utility(lottery: Lottery, u: Utility) -> Fraction:
    """The expected utility sum of p_i * u(x_i) for a Bernoulli utility u, as an exact Fraction."""
    check_lottery(lottery)
    return sum((p * u(x) for x, p in lottery), Fraction(0))


def mixture(alpha: Fraction, first: Lottery, second: Lottery) -> Lottery:
    """The lottery that pays the outcomes of first with probability alpha and of second with probability 1 - alpha.

    The probability of an outcome is alpha times its probability in first plus (1 - alpha) times its probability in
    second, with a probability of 0 where an outcome does not appear. The result is sorted by outcome.
    """
    weights: dict[int, Fraction] = {}
    for x, p in first:
        weights[x] = weights.get(x, Fraction(0)) + alpha * p
    for x, p in second:
        weights[x] = weights.get(x, Fraction(0)) + (1 - alpha) * p
    return sorted(weights.items())


def linear(x: int) -> Fraction:
    """u(x) = x, in cents: a linear utility, which is risk neutral."""
    return Fraction(x)


def quadratic(x: int) -> Fraction:
    """u(x) = x^2 / 10000: a convex utility, in cents squared over 10000."""
    return Fraction(x * x, 10000)


def affine_of(u: Utility) -> Utility:
    """The utility v(x) = 3 + 2 u(x), a positive affine transformation of u."""

    def v(x: int) -> Fraction:
        return Fraction(3) + 2 * u(x)

    return v


def order(a: Fraction, b: Fraction) -> str:
    """The ordering of two numbers as a word: greater, less or equal."""
    if a > b:
        return "greater"
    if a < b:
        return "less"
    return "equal"


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A to D. Every value is an exact Fraction, so the output is reproducible."""
    print("Part A: expected value and expected utility, exact")
    for name, lottery in LOTTERIES.items():
        print(
            f"lottery {name} expected_value_cents {expected_value(lottery)} "
            f"eu_linear {expected_utility(lottery, linear)} eu_quadratic {expected_utility(lottery, quadratic)}"
        )

    print("Part B: linearity in the probabilities for the mixture alpha*A + (1 - alpha)*C")
    linearity_holds = True
    for alpha in (Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 3), Fraction(1, 2)):
        mixed = mixture(alpha, LOTTERIES["A"], LOTTERIES["C"])
        for label, u in (("linear", linear), ("quadratic", quadratic)):
            lhs = expected_utility(mixed, u)
            rhs = alpha * expected_utility(LOTTERIES["A"], u) + (1 - alpha) * expected_utility(LOTTERIES["C"], u)
            linearity_holds = linearity_holds and lhs == rhs
            print(f"alpha {alpha} utility {label} mixture_eu {lhs} linear_combination {rhs} equal {lhs == rhs}")
    print(f"all_linearity_checks {linearity_holds}")

    print("Part C: pairwise order under u and under v = 3 + 2u")
    orders_preserved = True
    for left, right in (("A", "B"), ("A", "C"), ("B", "C")):
        for label, u in (("linear", linear), ("quadratic", quadratic)):
            under_u = order(expected_utility(LOTTERIES[left], u), expected_utility(LOTTERIES[right], u))
            under_v = order(
                expected_utility(LOTTERIES[left], affine_of(u)),
                expected_utility(LOTTERIES[right], affine_of(u)),
            )
            orders_preserved = orders_preserved and under_u == under_v
            print(f"pair {left} {right} utility {label} order_u {under_u} order_v {under_v} same {under_u == under_v}")
    print(f"all_affine_orders_preserved {orders_preserved}")

    print("Part D: invalid lotteries are rejected")
    negative = [(0, Fraction(-1, 2)), (100, Fraction(3, 2))]
    short = [(0, Fraction(1, 2)), (100, Fraction(1, 3))]
    for label, bad in (("negative", negative), ("short", short)):
        try:
            expected_utility(bad, linear)
        except ValueError as exc:
            print(f"rejected {label} message {exc}")


main()

Part A prints the expected value in cents and the expected utilities under both utilities. For A, B and C the linear expected utilities (15000, 12500 and 15000) equal the expected values, as Proposition 3.1 predicts with \(a = 0\) and \(b = 1\). 4Where the canon breaks

  • Finitely many outcomes. Every sum in this lesson is finite (A1). This lesson defines neither an integral nor a convergence argument, so none of its results is stated for a lottery with a continuum of outcomes.
  • The representation is a definition here. Definition 1.3 defines expected utility as a formula. The lesson proves properties of the formula. It does not prove that any preference ordering over lotteries can be written in this form, and it cites no source for that result (Remark 2.4).
  • The outcomes are final amounts. The utility function is defined on the outcomes as the lesson writes them. Whether the outcomes are final wealth or changes in wealth is a modelling choice that this lesson leaves open. Lesson L02 takes final wealth as the argument.
  • Known probabilities. Every probability in this lesson is given and exact. A set of probabilities that is not known is treated in Lesson L08 under ambiguity aversion, and the present lesson does not treat it.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The expected utility of a lottery is the utility of its expected outcome.”

    This holds when \(u\) is affine (Proposition 3.1). For lottery \(A\) of Example 1.4 under \(u(x) = x^{2}\), the expected outcome is 150 CU, so \(u(\E X) = 150^{2} = 22500\) CU squared. The expected utility is 45000 CU squared, which is twice that value. Part A of Lab 1 prints 45000 for the quadratic utility of \(A\).

  2. “A risk-neutral decision maker ignores the probabilities.”

    Under a risk-neutral utility the expected utility is \(b\, \E X\) (Definition 3.2), and \(\E X\) is the probability-weighted average of the outcomes. The probabilities enter the calculation. Part A of Lab 1 prints a linear expected utility equal to the expected value for each of A, B and C (15000, 12500 and 15000 cents), and each expected value is computed from that lottery's probabilities.

  3. “A utility number means something by itself, so adding 3 to every utility, or doubling it, can change which lottery is preferred.”

    Proposition 3.3 shows that a positive affine transformation of \(u\) preserves every comparison of expected utilities. Part C of Lab 1 prints the ordering of each of the three pairs under both utilities and under \(3 + 2u\), and the six comparisons agree in every case. Among the nonzero scale factors, the sign decides between keeping and reversing the ordering: Proposition 3.3 keeps it for \(b > 0\), and Problem P2 reverses it for \(b < 0\). At \(b = 0\) every pair ties, so the ordering is lost.

6Problem set

Attempt each problem before opening its solution.

Mechanics

M1 · Expected value and expected utility under a square

A lottery pays 20 CU with probability \(1/5\), 50 CU with probability \(3/10\) and 80 CU with probability \(1/2\). Compute \(\E X\) and \(\E u(X)\) for \(u(x) = x^{2}\), and compare \(\E u(X)\) with \(u(\E X)\).

Solution

The probabilities are \(1/5 + 3/10 + 1/2 = 1\), so the lottery is valid under Definition 1.1. The expected value is \(\E X = 20 \cdot \tfrac15 + 50 \cdot \tfrac{3}{10} + 80 \cdot \tfrac12 = 4 + 15 + 40 = 59\) CU. The expected utility is \(\E u(X) = 400 \cdot \tfrac15 + 2500 \cdot \tfrac{3}{10} + 6400 \cdot \tfrac12 = 80 + 750 + 3200 = 4030\) CU squared. The utility of the expected outcome is \(u(59) = 3481\) CU squared, so \(\E u(X) - u(\E X) = 549\) CU squared, which is positive in this example. This lesson proves no general comparison of that kind.

M2 · A mixture and the linearity identity

Let \(L\) pay 0 CU or 100 CU with probability \(1/2\) each, and let \(M\) pay 100 CU with probability \(1/4\) and 200 CU with probability \(3/4\). Under \(u(x) = x\), compute the mixture \(\tfrac13 L + \tfrac23 M\) and check Proposition 2.3 for it.

Solution

The mixture's weight on 0 CU is \(\tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac16\). Its weight on 100 CU is \(\tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac16 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac13\). Its weight on 200 CU is \(\tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac34 = \tfrac12\). The weights sum to \(\tfrac16 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac12 = 1\). Its expected utility is \(\tfrac13 \cdot 100 + \tfrac12 \cdot 200 = \tfrac{400}{3}\) CU, or 133.333 CU to three decimal places. The right side of Proposition 2.3 is \(\tfrac13 \cdot \E u(L) + \tfrac23 \cdot \E u(M)\). Here \(\E u(L) = 50\) CU and \(\E u(M) = 25 + 150 = 175\) CU, so the right side is \(\tfrac{50}{3} + \tfrac{350}{3} = \tfrac{400}{3}\) CU. The two sides agree.

M3 · A ranking under an affine transformation

Let \(X\) pay 10 or 30 CU with probability \(1/2\) each, and let \(Y\) pay 0 CU with probability \(1/4\) and 40 CU with probability \(3/4\). Under \(u(x) = x\), compare the two lotteries. Then compare them under \(v(x) = 5 + 3x\).

Solution

Under \(u\), \(\E u(X) = 20\) CU and \(\E u(Y) = 30\) CU, so \(Y\) ranks higher. Under \(v\), Proposition 3.1 gives \(\E v(X) = 5 + 3 \cdot 20 = 65\) and \(\E v(Y) = 5 + 3 \cdot 30 = 95\), so \(Y\) still ranks higher. A direct check agrees: \(v(10) = 35\) and \(v(30) = 95\), so \(\E v(X) = (35 + 95)/2 = 65\). The ranking is preserved, as Proposition 3.3 states with \(a = 5\) and \(b = 3 > 0\).

Proof

P1 · The mixture of two point masses

Let \(L\) pay \(x\) with probability 1 and \(M\) pay \(y\) with probability 1, where \(x \ne y\). Show that \(\alpha L + (1 - \alpha) M\) pays \(x\) with probability \(\alpha\) and \(y\) with probability \(1 - \alpha\), and that its expected utility is \(\alpha\, u(x) + (1 - \alpha)\, u(y)\).

Solution

By Definition 2.1, the weight on \(x\) is \(\alpha \cdot 1 + (1 - \alpha) \cdot 0 = \alpha\), because \(M\) pays \(x\) with probability 0 (the outcome \(x\) is not an outcome of \(M\)). Symmetrically, the weight on \(y\) is \(1 - \alpha\). Since \(x \ne y\), these are the only two outcomes with positive weight. The expected utility is \(\alpha\, u(x) + (1 - \alpha)\, u(y)\) by Definition 1.3. This is the case of Proposition 2.3 in which both lotteries are degenerate.

P2 · A negative scale reverses the ranking

Let \(v = a + b u\) with \(b < 0\). Show that for finite lotteries \(L\) and \(M\), \(\E v(L) \ge \E v(M)\) holds if and only if \(\E u(M) \ge \E u(L)\).

Solution

The computation in the proof of Proposition 3.3 still gives \(\E v(L) - \E v(M) = b\bigl(\E u(L) - \E u(M)\bigr)\). With \(b < 0\), this difference is non-negative exactly when \(\E u(L) - \E u(M)\) is non-positive. So \(\E v(L) \ge \E v(M)\) if and only if \(\E u(M) \ge \E u(L)\), which reverses the ranking. So the sign condition \(b > 0\) in Proposition 3.3 cannot be replaced by \(b < 0\) without reversing the ranking.

P3 · Why the sums are finite under A1

Explain which step of the proof of Proposition 2.3 would fail if a lottery could have countably many outcomes, and why the finiteness in A1 removes the difficulty.

Solution

The proof splits the sum \(\sum_{x \in S} [\alpha p_L(x) + (1 - \alpha) p_M(x)] u(x)\) into two sums. For a finite sum this split is always valid. For countably many outcomes, the split requires each of the two series to converge, and the expected utility is defined only when the series \(\sum_i p_i u(x_i)\) converges, which need not hold for an unbounded \(u\). A1 makes the sums finite, so no convergence question arises, and the split is an identity for every finite lottery.

Empirical

E1 · Reading the quadratic values from Lab 1

From Part A of Lab 1, what are the quadratic expected utilities of lotteries A and C, and how large is the difference? State the basis for the expected values of both lotteries.

Solution

Part A prints 45000 for A and 37500 for C under the quadratic utility. The difference is \(45000 - 37500 = 7500\) CU squared. The expected value of both lotteries is 15000 cents, which is 150 CU, as Part A also prints. The two lotteries share an expected value and differ in expected utility, which Example 3.4 explains.

E2 · Checking one linearity line from Lab 1

Part B of Lab 1 prints a line for \(\alpha = 1/3\) under the quadratic utility. Read the two numbers on that line and say whether they are equal, and what they check.

Solution

The line reads "mixture_eu 40000 linear_combination 40000 equal True". The mixture's expected utility and the weighted sum \(\tfrac13 \cdot 45000 + \tfrac23 \cdot 37500 = 15000 + 25000 = 40000\) are equal, so the check of Proposition 2.3 passes for this weight. The line checks one weight and one utility, and the other five lines check the remaining cases.

E3 · Which validation message is printed for which error

Part D of Lab 1 rejects two lotteries. Which message is printed for the lottery with a negative probability, and which for the lottery whose probabilities sum to 5/6?

Solution

The lottery with a negative probability, \(-1/2\) and \(3/2\), prints "probabilities must be non-negative". Its probabilities sum to 1, so the sum check does not fail, and the first check does. The second lottery, with probabilities \(1/2\) and \(1/3\), sums to \(5/6\), which is \(1/6\) less than 1, and it prints "probabilities must sum to 1". The order of the two checks in check_lottery decides which message a lottery receives when both checks fail.

Research

R1 · The best lottery on a fixed set of outcomes

Fix a finite set of outcomes \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) and a Bernoulli utility \(u\). Among all finite lotteries on this set, which lottery maximizes expected utility, and what is the maximum?

Solution

Let \(m = \max_i u(x_i)\). For every lottery, \(\E u(X) = \sum_i p_i u(x_i) \le \sum_i p_i\, m = m\), because each \(u(x_i) \le m\) and \(p_i \ge 0\). Equality holds exactly when \(p_i = 0\) for every outcome with \(u(x_i) < m\), so the maximizers put all their probability on outcomes that attain \(m\). The lottery that pays, for certain, an outcome whose utility equals \(m\) attains the maximum \(m\). The argument uses Definition 1.3, the fact from Definition 1.1 that the probabilities sum to 1, and the finiteness in A1.

R2 · Linearity is not a representation

Does Proposition 2.3 show that a preference ordering over finite lotteries has the expected-utility form? Justify your answer from the statement of Proposition 2.3.

Solution

No. Proposition 2.3 is an identity about the formula in Definition 1.3, under A1 and a real-valued \(u\). It contains no assumption on any ordering over lotteries, so it proves no representation result. This lesson states no assumption on an ordering over lotteries and claims no representation result (Remark 2.4).

R3 · Equal expected values under a convex utility

Let \(u(x) = x^{2}\) on the nonnegative outcomes. Find two finite lotteries with the same expected value whose expected utilities differ, and show that the lottery with the larger spread has the larger expected utility.

Solution

Take \(X\) with outcomes 100 and 200 CU, each with probability \(1/2\), and \(Y\) with outcome 150 CU with probability 1. Both have expected value 150 CU. Then \(\E u(X) = (10000 + 40000)/2 = 25000\) CU squared, and \(\E u(Y) = 22500\) CU squared. The lottery with the larger spread, \(X\), has the larger expected utility. The example shows one pair of lotteries and does not establish a general pattern. It uses only the formula of Definition 1.3.

  1. What is the expected value of the lottery that pays 0 CU with probability \(1/4\), 100 CU with probability \(1/2\) and 400 CU with probability \(1/4\)?

    Options

    \(0 \cdot \tfrac14 + 100 \cdot \tfrac12 + 400 \cdot \tfrac14 = 150\) CU (Example 1.4).

  2. A lottery pays 20 with probability \(1/5\), 50 with probability \(3/10\) and 80 with probability \(1/2\). What is its expected utility under \(u(x) = x^{2}\), in CU squared?

  3. For \(u(x) = 3 + 2x\), what is \(\E u(X)\) in terms of the expected value \(\E X\)?

    Options

    Proposition 3.1 with \(a = 3\) and \(b = 2\).

  4. Which condition on \(u\) is sufficient for \(\E u(X) = u(\E X)\) to hold for every finite lottery \(X\)?

    Options

    Proposition 3.1 gives \(\E u(X) = a + b\,\E X = u(\E X)\) when \(u\) is affine. A convex \(u\) such as \(x^{2}\) fails the equality for lottery A (Misconception 1).

  5. Two lotteries have the same expected value. Under a risk-neutral utility \(u(x) = bx\) with \(b > 0\), what is true of their expected utilities?

    Options

    Definition 3.2 gives \(\E u(X) = b\,\E X\), which depends only on the expected value.

  6. If \(v = 5 + 3u\) and \(u\) ranks lottery \(L\) above lottery \(M\), what does \(v\) do to that ranking?

    Options

    Proposition 3.3 with \(b = 3 > 0\). The reversal needs a negative factor (Problem P2).

  7. Lottery A is the one in Example 1.4, which pays 0 CU with probability \(1/4\). Lottery D pays 0 CU with probability \(1/2\). What is the probability of 0 CU in the mixture \(\tfrac13 A + \tfrac23 D\)?

    Options

    \(\tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac14 + \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac1{12} + \tfrac13 = \tfrac5{12}\) (Definition 2.1).

  8. Under \(u(x) = x\), let \(L\) pay 100 CU for certain and \(M\) pay 0 CU for certain. What is the expected utility of \(0.3\,L + 0.7\,M\), in CU?

    The mixture pays 100 CU with probability 0.3 and 0 CU otherwise, so its expected utility is \(0.3 \cdot 100 = 30\) CU (Proposition 2.3).

  9. Which statement about Proposition 2.3 is correct?

    Options

    Remark 2.4. Proposition 2.3 needs only A1 and a real-valued \(u\), and it makes no claim about preference orderings.

  10. Under \(u(x) = x\), what is the expected utility of the lottery that pays 0 CU with probability \(3/4\) and 200 CU with probability \(1/4\), in CU?

    \(0 \cdot \tfrac34 + 200 \cdot \tfrac14 = 50\) CU, which equals the expected value (Proposition 3.1).
