Double-Entry Bookkeeping and Accrual Accounting

The statements a firm publishes are drawn from recorded entries, and each entry has equal total debits and total credits, so an entry that posts an amount to one side only shows up as an imbalance in that entry. This lesson sets out the rules for recording entries, proves the property that keeps the record consistent, and separates two timings that the rest of the course depends on: when a firm earns or incurs an amount, and when cash moves. Both identities proved here are the arithmetic that later lessons use to link the statements.

1Debits, credits and the ledger

A transaction changes at least two accounts. Accounting records each change as one debit or one credit, in integer cents, and groups the changes into a journal entry (Definition 1.1, below). A debit is the left side of an account and a credit is the right side. Which side increases an account depends on its type. The table fixes the convention used throughout AC1 and in the lab.

Definition 1.1 (journal entry)

A journal entry is a finite set of lines, each naming one account and carrying either a debit or a credit of a non-negative integer number of cents. The entry is balanced when the sum of its debits equals the sum of its credits. Amounts are integers because a fractional cent cannot be posted; the lab rejects any other type.

Definition 1.2 (ledger and T-account)

A ledger is the sequence of accepted entries. The T-account for one account is the list of its debits on the left and its credits on the right, with the balance on the account's normal side. The trial balance is the table of those balances for all accounts together.

2Double-entry bookkeeping

Double-entry bookkeeping is the rule that each transaction is recorded on two sides, so that the books stay in balance. The rule is enforced at posting, not at the end of a period. An entry that is not balanced is refused, and the ledger is left unchanged. Lab 1 shows both the accepted entries and the refused ones.

Proposition 2.1 (a ledger of balanced entries is balanced)

Let a ledger contain only balanced entries. Then its total debits equal its total credits, and so does the trial balance.

Proof

Let the entries be \(E_1, \dots, E_n\), and let \(D_i\) and \(C_i\) be the total debits and the total credits of \(E_i\). Each entry is balanced, so \(D_i = C_i\) for every \(i\). The total debits of the ledger are \(\sum_i D_i\), and the total credits are \(\sum_i C_i\). These sums are equal term by term, so they are equal. Every line of every entry is counted once in its own total, and no line belongs to two entries, so the trial balance, which adds the same lines account by account, has the same totals. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.1 is a statement about the bookkeeping, not about the firm. A ledger with equal totals can still contain a wrong entry that is balanced: a sale posted to the wrong customer account, or to an expense account, keeps the totals equal. The rule catches arithmetic errors in one entry and cannot catch a well-formed entry that describes the wrong event. The lab shows the first kind of error refused.

Remark 2.2 (what the check does not test)

Posting checks that each entry balances and that its accounts exist. It does not check that the event happened, that its amount is right, or that its account is the right one. Those are questions about the source documents, and the ledger cannot answer them.

# Account -> (type, normal side). The normal side is the side that increases the balance.
CHART: dict[str, tuple[str, str]] = {
    "cash": ("asset", "debit"),
    "accounts_receivable": ("asset", "debit"),
    "accounts_payable": ("liability", "credit"),
    "common_stock": ("equity", "credit"),
    "sales": ("revenue", "credit"),
    "operating_expense": ("expense", "debit"),
}


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Line:
    """One side of one account. Debit and credit are non-negative integer cents; one of them is zero."""

    account: str
    debit: int = 0
    credit: int = 0


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Entry:
    """A journal entry: the day of the quarter, a memo, and its lines."""

    day: int
    memo: str
    lines: tuple[Line, ...]


def check_entry(entry: Entry) -> None:
    """Raise ValueError if the entry uses an unknown account, a bad amount, or does not balance."""
    debits = 0
    credits = 0
    for line in entry.lines:
        if line.account not in CHART:
            raise ValueError(f"{entry.memo!r}: unknown account {line.account!r}")
        for amount in (line.debit, line.credit):
            if isinstance(amount, bool) or not isinstance(amount, int) or amount < 0:
                raise ValueError(f"{entry.memo!r}: amounts must be non-negative integer cents, got {amount!r}")
        if line.debit and line.credit:
            raise ValueError(f"{entry.memo!r}: one line carries both a debit and a credit on {line.account!r}")
        debits += line.debit
        credits += line.credit
    if debits != credits:
        raise ValueError(f"{entry.memo!r}: debits {debits} do not equal credits {credits}")


class Ledger:
    """Accepts only entries that pass check_entry. A rejected entry leaves the ledger unchanged."""

    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self.entries: list[Entry] = []

    def post(self, entry: Entry) -> None:
        check_entry(entry)
        self.entries.append(entry)

    def debit_total(self, account: str) -> int:
        return sum(line.debit for e in self.entries for line in e.lines if line.account == account)

    def credit_total(self, account: str) -> int:
        return sum(line.credit for e in self.entries for line in e.lines if line.account == account)

    def balance(self, account: str) -> int:
        """Balance on the account's normal side, in integer cents, never negative in this lab."""
        debit, credit = self.debit_total(account), self.credit_total(account)
        return debit - credit if CHART[account][1] == "debit" else credit - debit


def money(cents: int) -> str:
    """Format integer cents as dollars and cents, for example 1234567 -> 12,345.67."""
    sign = "-" if cents < 0 else ""
    dollars, rest = divmod(abs(cents), 100)
    return f"{sign}{dollars:,}.{rest:02d}"


def generate_quarter(rng: random.Random, ledger: Ledger) -> dict[str, int]:

    Returns the amounts posted, so that the printed comparisons use the same integers as the entries.
    """
    ledger.post(Entry(1, "owner invests cash", (Line("cash", debit=8_000_000), Line("common_stock", credit=8_000_000))))
    amounts: dict[str, int] = {}
    amounts["credit_sale"] = rng.randrange(18_000_000, 24_000_000)
    ledger.post(
        Entry(
            5,
            "sale on credit",
            (Line("accounts_receivable", debit=amounts["credit_sale"]), Line("sales", credit=amounts["credit_sale"])),
        )
    )
    amounts["cash_sale"] = rng.randrange(2_000_000, 4_000_000)
    ledger.post(
        Entry(9, "cash sale", (Line("cash", debit=amounts["cash_sale"]), Line("sales", credit=amounts["cash_sale"])))
    )
    amounts["expense_on_account"] = rng.randrange(3_000_000, 4_000_000)
    ledger.post(
        Entry(
            14,
            "operating expense incurred on account",
            (
                Line("operating_expense", debit=amounts["expense_on_account"]),
                Line("accounts_payable", credit=amounts["expense_on_account"]),
            ),
        )
    )
    amounts["expense_paid"] = rng.randrange(1_000_000, 2_000_000)
    ledger.post(
        Entry(
            20,
            "operating expense paid in cash",
            (Line("operating_expense", debit=amounts["expense_paid"]), Line("cash", credit=amounts["expense_paid"])),
        )

    ledger.post(
        Entry(

    )
    return amounts

3Accrual accounting

Cash accounting records an amount when cash moves. Accrual accounting records revenue when it is earned and expense when it is incurred, whether or not cash has moved. The two timings differ by the changes in the balances of the receivable and payable accounts. The next identities show exactly how much, and they are the reason the accrual timing can be checked against the cash timing.

Definition 3.1 (accrual accounting)

Accrual accounting records a sale as revenue in the period in which it is made, and records a cost as an expense in the period in which it is incurred. A receivable or a payable holds the difference until cash moves. In this lab a credit sale and a cash sale are both recognized as revenue on the date of the sale.

Proposition 3.2 (revenue less cash receipts is the change in receivables)

Hypotheses. (i) The only entries that debit accounts receivable are credit sales, and the only entries that credit it are collections, so there are no write-offs, returns or allowances. (ii) Revenue is credit sales plus cash sales. (iii) The only cash received from customers is cash sales and collections. (iv) The only entries that credit accounts payable are operating expense incurred on account, and the only entries that debit it are payments of payables. (v) Operating expense is either incurred on account or paid in cash, with no other non-cash expense such as depreciation. Under (i) to (v), let \(R\) be the revenue earned in a period, \(K\) the cash received from customers in the period, and \(\Delta A\) the change in accounts receivable over the period. Then \(R - K = \Delta A\). Likewise, if \(X\) is the operating expense recognized, \(P\) the cash paid for operating costs, and \(\Delta L\) the change in accounts payable, then \(X - P = \Delta L\).

Proof

Split revenue into credit sales \(S_c\) and cash sales \(S_k\), so \(R = S_c + S_k\). Cash received from customers is the cash sales plus the collections \(Q\) of receivables, so \(K = S_k + Q\). A receivable rises by the credit sales and falls by the collections, so \(\Delta A = S_c - Q\). Subtracting gives \(R - K = S_c - Q = \Delta A\). For expense, split it into the amount incurred on account \(X_a\) and the amount paid in cash \(X_k\), so \(X = X_a + X_k\). Cash paid is \(P = X_k + V\), where \(V\) is the payment of payables. A payable rises by \(X_a\) and falls by \(V\), so \(\Delta L = X_a - V\). Subtracting gives \(X - P = X_a - V = \Delta L\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.2 holds for any opening balances, since it uses only the changes. Lab 1 starts from zero receivables and zero payables, so there the change equals the closing balance, and the lab prints both to test that.

Example 3.3 (the same quarter on two bases)

In Lab 1 the firm sells on credit for 192,834.97 and for cash for 21,046.51, and collects 154,267.97 of the credit sales. Accrual revenue is therefore 213,881.48 and cash from customers is 175,314.48. The difference, 38,567.00, is the closing receivable, as Proposition 3.2 requires. A reader who uses cash receipts alone would report 175,314.48 for the quarter and miss the 38,567.00 that the firm has earned and not yet collected.

Remark 3.4 (an estimate enters through the amount, not the rule)

The rule that revenue is recognized when earned does not say what amount is earned when the amount depends on a later event, such as a return or a non-payment. The lab's amounts come from its seed, from fixed fractions of a balance (four fifths of receivables, one half of payables), or from one fixed opening investment of 80,000.00, so no estimate enters the lab. Where a real amount depends on a judgement, the identity still holds, but the amount it holds for is the firm's estimate and not an observed cash flow.

4Two timings, one ledger

The same ledger yields both timings. A statement on the accrual basis reads the revenue and expense accounts. A statement on the cash basis reads the cash lines alone. The difference between the two is not an error in either. It is the change in the receivable and payable accounts, which Proposition 3.2 gives exactly. Later lessons use the same identity to tie the income statement to the balance sheet and to the cash-flow statement.

Remark 4.1 (the trial balance is a check, not a statement)

A trial balance with equal totals shows that the entries are consistent with double entry. It does not show that the balances are correct. Lab 1 prints the trial balance, and its equal totals are Proposition 2.1 at work on the quarter's entries.

5Where the canon breaks

The double-entry rule is a rule about arithmetic, and it is complete for that purpose. Three points mark where a reader who takes it as a full account of the firm goes wrong.

  • Balance is necessary, not sufficient. Proposition 2.1 guarantees that the totals agree. It does not guarantee that each entry describes an event that happened, or that its amount is right. Remark 2.2 states what the check tests.
  • The accrual timing depends on a recognition rule. Proposition 3.2 holds for whatever amounts the rule recognizes. The lab fixes those amounts, so it shows the mechanics of the timing and not the judgements that set the amounts.
  • Cash receipts are not a measure of performance in a period. Cash can arrive in a period in which nothing is earned, and revenue can be earned in a period with no cash. The identity in Proposition 3.2 is the link between the two, and it does not say which is the better measure.

6Misconceptions

  1. “A debit is good and a credit is bad.”

    A debit and a credit are sides, not values. A debit increases an asset or an expense and decreases a liability, equity or revenue account. Lab 1's T-accounts show cash with debits of 255,314.48 and credits of 30,594.17, a balance of 224,720.31 on the debit side, and sales with a credit balance of 213,881.48. Both are normal balances.

  2. “If the trial balance balances, the books are right.”

    Proposition 2.1 establishes that the entries are internally consistent. Remark 2.2 gives the case that passes the check and is still wrong: a balanced entry posted to the wrong account. The equal totals in Lab 1 say nothing about whether each entry describes an event that happened.

  3. “Cash is the honest figure and accrual is an adjustment to it.”

    Both are timings of the same ledger. Proposition 3.2 gives the exact difference between them, which is the change in receivables and payables. Neither timing is the adjusted version of the other. In Lab 1 the expense recognized is 49,831.40 and the cash paid is 30,594.17, and the gap of 19,237.23 is the closing payable.

7Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked on Lab 1 use the SIMULATED quarter that the lab generates.

Mechanics

M1 · Post a sale on credit

A firm sells goods on credit for 1,250.00. Write the journal entry, state which account increases with each line, and check that the entry balances.

Solution

Accounts receivable is an asset, and it increases with a debit. Sales is a revenue account, and it increases with a credit. The entry is Dr accounts receivable 125,000 cents, Cr sales 125,000 cents, where 1,250.00 is 125,000 cents. The debits total 125,000 and the credits total 125,000, so the entry balances.

M2 · The normal balance of an account

An account of type liability has a debit of 300 and a credit of 1,000 posted to it. What is its balance on its normal side?

Solution

A liability has a credit normal side. Its balance is credits minus debits, \(1{,}000 - 300 = 700\). The balance is a credit of 700, which is the amount the firm owes.

M3 · Refuse or accept

An entry debits cash 500 and credits sales 450 and credits accounts receivable 50. Is it accepted under Definition 1.1, and why?

Solution

The debits total 500. The credits total \(450 + 50 = 500\). The entry is balanced and every amount is a non-negative integer, so it is accepted. A single entry may have more than two lines, and this one has three.

Proof

P1 · A reversal keeps the balance

Show that if every line of a balanced entry has its debit and credit exchanged, the resulting entry is also balanced.

Solution

Let the entry have debits totalling \(D\) and credits totalling \(C\), with \(D = C\). After the exchange the old debits become credits and the old credits become debits. The new debits total \(C\) and the new credits total \(D\). Since \(D = C\), the new debits equal the new credits, so the reversed entry is balanced.

P2 · Proposition 3.2 with opening balances

Let the opening receivable be \(A_0\) and the closing receivable \(A_1\). Show that revenue less cash received equals \(A_1 - A_0\), not \(A_1\).

Solution

The proof of Proposition 3.2 gives \(R - K = S_c - Q\). The change in receivables is \(\Delta A = A_1 - A_0 = S_c - Q\), because the receivable rises by the credit sales and falls by the collections, and the opening balance is carried forward. Therefore \(R - K = A_1 - A_0\). When \(A_0 = 0\), as in Lab 1, this reduces to \(R - K = A_1\).

P3 · Expense on the accrual basis with opening payables

Show that \(X - P = \Delta L\) also holds when the opening payable \(L_0\) is non-zero, where \(\Delta L = L_1 - L_0\).

Solution

The payable rises by the amounts incurred on account \(X_a\) and falls by the payments \(V\), so \(L_1 = L_0 + X_a - V\). Hence \(X_a - V = L_1 - L_0 = \Delta L\). The proof of Proposition 3.2 gives \(X - P = X_a - V\), and combining the two equalities gives \(X - P = \Delta L\) for any opening balance.

Empirical

E1 · The receivable share of revenue (SIMULATED)

From Lab 1's output, what share of the quarter's revenue is still uncollected at the quarter end? Give the answer to one decimal place as a percentage, and state the basis.

Solution

The lab prints revenue earned of 213,881.48 and an accounts receivable balance of 38,567.00. The share is \(38{,}567.00 / 213{,}881.48 = 0.18032\) to five decimal places, which is 18.0% to one decimal place. The basis is the two printed figures, divided exactly and then shown to one decimal place. The result describes the SIMULATED firm.

E2 · The expense gap (SIMULATED)

Lab 1 prints the expense recognized and the cash paid for operating costs. Compute the gap and check it against the closing payable the lab prints.

Solution

Expense recognized is 49,831.40 and cash paid is 30,594.17. The gap is \(49{,}831.40 - 30{,}594.17 = 19{,}237.23\). The lab prints the accounts payable balance at quarter end as 19,237.23, so the gap equals the payable, as Proposition 3.2 requires for the second identity.

E3 · Reading a trial balance (SIMULATED)

Using only the T-account table in Lab 1, add the debit balances and the credit balances of the six accounts that have a balance, and show that the totals are equal.

Solution

The debit-side balances are cash 224,720.31, accounts receivable 38,567.00 and operating expense 49,831.40, which total 313,118.71. The credit-side balances are accounts payable 19,237.23, common stock 80,000.00 and sales 213,881.48, which total 313,118.71. The two totals agree, which is Proposition 2.1 applied to the quarter's entries.

R1 · A test on a real firm's filing

You have one real firm's quarterly filing, which reports revenue, cash from customers and receivables. Specify a test of Proposition 3.2 on that filing. State what the test can establish and what it cannot.

Solution

Take the reported revenue \(R\), the reported cash from customers \(K\), and the opening and closing receivables \(A_0\) and \(A_1\). Compute \(R - K - (A_1 - A_0)\). A value of zero is consistent with the identity. A non-zero value shows that the reported lines do not satisfy the identity as stated. A reclassification, a write-off, an acquisition or a non-cash item is one candidate cause, and the filing's notes must be read to find it. The test can establish consistency between the reported lines. It cannot establish that each reported number is correct, and it needs the filing's own definitions of revenue and cash from customers.

R2 · A recognition policy that moves revenue early

Suppose a firm recognizes a credit sale at the order date, although the goods ship a quarter later. Describe the effect on Proposition 3.2 and on the receivable, and say which identity would show the problem.

Solution

Recognizing at the order date raises revenue in the order quarter, and the receivable rises by the same amount, so the identity \(R - K = \Delta A\) still holds. The identity cannot detect the policy, because it holds for any recognition timing that keeps the entry balanced. What reveals the early recognition is the comparison of the receivable with the shipping records, or with a later quarter in which the goods ship and the revenue is reversed. The lesson here is that an accounting identity constrains consistency and does not constrain timing.

R3 · What the cash timing hides

A firm's cash from customers exceeds its revenue in a quarter. What does Proposition 3.2 say about the change in receivables that quarter, and what should a reader check before drawing a conclusion about sales?

Solution

Since \(R - K = \Delta A\) and \(K > R\), we have \(\Delta A < 0\): the receivable fell. The firm collected more than it billed in the period, which is consistent with collections of earlier sales. The excess can be collections of earlier sales, so the reader should check the receivables from the prior quarter before reading it as new business. Reading the cash figure as sales would report more than the revenue earned in the quarter.

9Retrieval quiz

Answer from memory, then check. Finishing the quiz adds this lesson's cards to your review deck.

  1. A sale on credit is posted as a debit to accounts receivable and a credit to sales. Which statement is correct?

    Options

    Receivables are an asset and increase with a debit; sales increase with a credit. The two amounts are equal, so the entry balances (Definition 1.1).

  2. Which account increases with a credit?

    Options

    A liability increases with a credit. Cash and expense increase with a debit (Section 1 table).

  3. A balanced entry has debits of 500 cents on cash and credits of 300 cents on sales and 200 cents on receivables. What is the difference between total debits and total credits, in cents?

    Debits are 500 and credits are 300 + 200 = 500, so the difference is 0 (Definition 1.1).

  4. Under Proposition 2.1, what does a trial balance with equal totals establish?

    Options

    Balance is a check of arithmetic within each entry. Remark 2.2 states what it does not test.

  5. A firm has revenue of 1,000 and cash from customers of 800 in a period. Which change in receivables does Proposition 3.2 require?

    Options

    \(R - K = \Delta A\), so \(\Delta A = 1{,}000 - 800 = 200\), a rise.

  6. In Lab 1, the receivable at quarter end divided by revenue earned is a fraction. What is it, to two decimal places?

    38,567.00 divided by 213,881.48 is 0.18 to two decimal places (exercise E1).

  7. Why does the lab refuse a fractional amount such as 1.5 cents?

    Options

    Definition 1.1 requires non-negative integer cents. A fraction is not a posting, and the lab refuses it instead of changing it.

  8. Lab 1's expense recognized is 49,831.40 and cash paid is 30,594.17. What is the gap, in USD?

    \(49{,}831.40 - 30{,}594.17 = 19{,}237.23\), which equals the closing payable (exercise E2).

  9. When the lab posts the same sale in a second ledger that is later found to hold the sale on the wrong customer, which check fails?

    Options

    The lab tests balance, integer amounts and known account names. A balanced entry on the wrong customer passes all three (Remark 2.2).

  10. How many of the seven entries in Lab 1 have a line on the cash account?

    Five: the owner's investment, the cash sale, the operating expense paid in cash, the collection of receivables and the payment of payables. The sale on credit and the expense incurred on account have no cash line.
