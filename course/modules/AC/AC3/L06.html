Why do corporations voluntarily disclose proprietary data when regulations do not require it? Microeconomic disclosure theory explains how market forces compel firms to reveal private information. Understanding when disclosure unravelling succeeds and when it fails allows quantitative investors to price corporate silence and identify hidden adverse selection.

1. The Voluntary Disclosure Dilemma

Mandatory financial accounting rules dictate minimum disclosure standards. However, modern corporations routinely publish vast amounts of discretionary information, including monthly key performance indicators, ESG metrics, pipeline trials, and management earnings forecasts. The fundamental economic question is what governs the boundary between public transparency and corporate silence.

Definition 6.1: Voluntary Disclosure

Voluntary disclosure is the discretionary release of verifiable or unverifiable financial, operational, or strategic information by a corporate entity beyond statutory and regulatory mandates.

In game-theoretic terms, voluntary disclosure is an asymmetric information signaling game where an informed insider (the manager) selects a disclosure policy \(D \in \{\text{reveal } s, \, \text{withhold}\}\) to influence the market valuation \(P\) set by rational outside investors.

2. Unravelling, Proprietary Costs, and Information Uncertainty

The baseline benchmark in economic disclosure theory is the unravelling result (Milgrom 1982; Grossman 1981). When disclosure is costless, information is strictly verifiable (no "cheap talk"), and outside investors know with certainty that the manager is informed, the unique rational expectations equilibrium features full disclosure.

The intuition for unravelling is recursive. The firm with the highest possible realization discloses to separate itself from all lower types. Rational investors know that any remaining silent firm must possess value below this peak. The highest firm in the remaining pool then discloses, cascading downwards until even the second-worst firm discloses, leaving only the worst-possible realization silent.

In practice, complete unravelling is rarely observed. Corporate finance theory identifies two primary friction mechanisms that break the unravelling chain:

  • Proprietary costs: Disclosing information (such as product profit margins or customer concentration) reveals valuable intelligence to commercial competitors, suppliers, or labor unions, imposing an external friction cost \(c > 0\).
  • Information uncertainty (Dye 1985; Jung & Kwon 1988): Investors do not know whether the manager received a private information signal (arrival probability \(p < 1\)). When a firm stays silent, investors cannot differentiate between an informed manager concealing bad news and an uninformed manager who genuinely knows nothing.

Theorem 6.1: Dye-Jung-Kwon Disclosure Equilibrium

Let firm fundamental value \(v\) be distributed according to uniform prior \(v \sim U[0, 1]\). Suppose the manager receives a verifiable signal of \(v\) with probability \(p \in (0, 1)\) and remains uninformed with probability \(1 - p\). Disclosing incurs proprietary cost \(c \ge 0\). Rational investors price the firm as its conditional expected value given the manager's action.

  1. The unique equilibrium strategy of an informed manager is a cutoff rule: disclose if \(v \ge v^*\) and remain silent if \(v < v^*\).
  2. The equilibrium disclosure cutoff \(v^* \in (0, 1)\) is the unique positive root in \([0, 1]\) of the quadratic equation:
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2} p (v^*)^2 + (1 - p - p c) v^* - \left(\frac{1}{2} + c\right)(1 - p) = 0 \end{aligned} \]
  1. The equilibrium market price under silence is:
\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{silence}) = v^* - c = \E\left[v \mid \text{silence}\right] = \frac{\frac{1}{2}(1 - p) + \frac{1}{2} p (v^*)^2}{(1 - p) + p v^*} \end{aligned} \]
  1. Comparative statics: As information certainty approaches 1 (\(p \to 1\)) with zero proprietary cost (\(c = 0\)), the cutoff collapses to zero (\(v^* \to 0\)), restoring Milgrom-Grossman full unravelling. As proprietary costs \(c\) increase, \(v^*\) increases monotonically (\(\frac{\partial v^*}{\partial c} > 0\)), suppressing disclosure.

Proof of Theorem 6.1

An informed manager observing signal \(v\) who discloses receives net firm valuation \(v - c\). If the manager stays silent, the firm receives silence price \(P(\text{silence})\). The manager strictly prefers disclosure if \(v - c > P(\text{silence})\), implying the cutoff condition \(v^* - c = P(\text{silence})\).

Rational investors compute the conditional expectation of \(v\) under silence. Silence occurs under two mutually exclusive events:

  1. The manager is uninformed (probability \(1 - p\)), in which case \(v \sim U[0, 1]\) with expected value \(\frac{1}{2}\).
  2. The manager is informed (probability \(p\)) but observed \(v < v^*\), in which case \(v \sim U[0, v^*]\) with expected value \(\frac{1}{2} v^*\).

By Bayes' rule, the conditional expected value under silence is:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{silence}) = \frac{(1 - p) \cdot \frac{1}{2} + p v^* \cdot \frac{1}{2} v^*}{(1 - p) + p v^*} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}(1 - p) + \frac{1}{2} p (v^*)^2}{(1 - p) + p v^*} \end{aligned} \]

Equating \(v^* - c = P(\text{silence})\) and multiplying both sides by the denominator \((1 - p) + p v^*\) yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} (v^* - c) \left[ (1 - p) + p v^* \right] = \frac{1}{2}(1 - p) + \frac{1}{2} p (v^*)^2 \end{aligned} \]

Expanding the left-hand side:

\[ \begin{aligned} p (v^*)^2 + (1 - p - p c) v^* - c (1 - p) = \frac{1}{2}(1 - p) + \frac{1}{2} p (v^*)^2 \end{aligned} \]

Subtracting the right-hand side produces:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2} p (v^*)^2 + (1 - p - p c) v^* - \left(\frac{1}{2} + c\right)(1 - p) = 0 \end{aligned} \]

Since the quadratic coefficient \(\frac{1}{2} p > 0\) and the constant term \(-\left(\frac{1}{2} + c\right)(1 - p) < 0\) for \(p \in (0, 1)\), Descartes' rule of signs guarantees a unique positive real root \(v^* \in (0, 1)\), completing the proof.

Example 6.1: Calculating the Disclosure Cutoff

Suppose \(v \sim U[0, 1]\), information probability \(p = 0.70\), and proprietary disclosure cost \(c = 0.05\). The quadratic coefficients are:

\[ \begin{aligned} a &= 0.5 \times 0.70 = 0.35 \\ b &= (1 - 0.70) - 0.70 \times 0.05 = 0.30 - 0.035 = 0.265 \\ c_{\text{quad}} &= -(0.50 + 0.05) \times (1 - 0.70) = -0.55 \times 0.30 = -0.165 \end{aligned} \]

Solving via the quadratic formula:

\[ \begin{aligned} v^* &= \frac{-0.265 + \sqrt{0.265^2 - 4(0.35)(-0.165)}}{2 \times 0.35} \\ &= \frac{-0.265 + \sqrt{0.070225 + 0.2310}}{0.70} = \frac{-0.265 + \sqrt{0.301225}}{0.70} \approx \frac{-0.265 + 0.54884}{0.70} \approx 0.4055 \end{aligned} \]

The market price under silence is \(P(\text{silence}) = v^* - c = 0.4055 - 0.05 = 0.3555\). An informed manager will disclose if and only if fundamental value exceeds \(0.4055\).

Lab 1: Dye-Jung-Kwon Voluntary Disclosure Equilibrium

Hypothesis: The analytical quadratic formula root for the Dye-Jung-Kwon disclosure cutoff matches the conditional expectation fixed point to within machine float precision, cutoffs decrease toward zero as information probability approaches 1 (unravelling), and cutoffs increase monotonically with proprietary cost.

"""AC3 lab L06: voluntary disclosure equilibrium and unraveling under information uncertainty.

SIMULATED: the voluntary disclosure game of Dye (1985) and Jung & Kwon (1988) with uniform
prior on firm value v ~ U[0, 1], information arrival probability p, and proprietary cost c.
Solves the equilibrium disclosure cutoff v* and silence pricing P_silent analytically and
verifies with fixed-point iteration. Standard library math and float only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

SEED = 20261110


def solve_dye_cutoff(p: float, c: float) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Solve equilibrium disclosure cutoff v* and silence price P_silent for v ~ U[0, 1].

    Cutoff satisfies 0.5 * p * (v*)^2 + (1 - p - p * c) * v* - (0.5 + c) * (1 - p) = 0.
    Returns (v_star, p_silent).
    """
    coeff_a = 0.5 * p
    coeff_b = (1.0 - p) - p * c
    coeff_c = -(0.5 + c) * (1.0 - p)

    if coeff_a == 0.0:
        # p = 0: no information ever arrives
        v_star = 1.0
        p_silent = 0.5
        return v_star, p_silent

    discriminant = coeff_b * coeff_b - 4.0 * coeff_a * coeff_c
    if discriminant < 0:
        raise ValueError(f"Negative discriminant: {discriminant}")

    v_star = (-coeff_b + math.sqrt(discriminant)) / (2.0 * coeff_a)
    # Clip to valid domain [0, 1]
    v_star = max(0.0, min(1.0, v_star))
    p_silent = v_star - c
    return v_star, p_silent


def verify_fixed_point(v_star: float, p: float, c: float) -> float:
    """Evaluate difference between v* - c and conditional expectation E[v | silence]."""
    num = 0.5 * (1.0 - p) + 0.5 * p * (v_star**2)
    denom = (1.0 - p) + p * v_star
    e_silent = num / denom if denom > 0 else 0.5
    return abs((v_star - c) - e_silent)


def main() -> None:
    print(f"seed {SEED} dye_jung_kwon_disclosure_equilibrium")
    print(f"{'p (prob)':8s} | {'c (cost)':8s} | {'v* (cutoff)':11s} | {'P_silent':10s} | {'Fixed_pt_err':12s}")
    print("-" * 60)

    # 1. Varying information probability p with zero proprietary cost c = 0
    c_zero = 0.0
    probs = [0.10, 0.30, 0.50, 0.70, 0.90, 0.99]
    cutoffs_p: list[float] = []

    for p in probs:
        v_star, p_sil = solve_dye_cutoff(p, c_zero)
        err = verify_fixed_point(v_star, p, c_zero)
        cutoffs_p.append(v_star)
        print(f"{p:8.2f} | {c_zero:8.2f} | {v_star:11.4f} | {p_sil:10.4f} | {err:12.2e}")

    # Check unravelling property: as p -> 1, cutoff v* -> 0 (Milgrom-Grossman full unraveling)
    print("-" * 60)
    is_decreasing = all(cutoffs_p[i] > cutoffs_p[i + 1] for i in range(len(cutoffs_p) - 1))
    print(f"cutoff_decreases_with_information_prob: {is_decreasing}")
    print(f"substantial_unraveling_at_p_099: {cutoffs_p[-1] < 0.10}")

    # 2. Varying proprietary cost c with fixed information probability p = 0.70
    p_fixed = 0.70
    costs = [0.00, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30]
    print("\nimpact_of_proprietary_cost (p = 0.70):")
    print(f"{'p (prob)':8s} | {'c (cost)':8s} | {'v* (cutoff)':11s} | {'P_silent':10s} | {'Fixed_pt_err':12s}")
    print("-" * 60)

    cutoffs_c: list[float] = []
    for c in costs:
        v_star, p_sil = solve_dye_cutoff(p_fixed, c)
        err = verify_fixed_point(v_star, p_fixed, c)
        cutoffs_c.append(v_star)
        print(f"{p_fixed:8.2f} | {c:8.2f} | {v_star:11.4f} | {p_sil:10.4f} | {err:12.2e}")

    print("-" * 60)
    is_increasing = all(cutoffs_c[i] < cutoffs_c[i + 1] for i in range(len(cutoffs_c) - 1))
    print(f"cutoff_increases_with_proprietary_cost: {is_increasing}")


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks

While the Dye-Jung-Kwon framework formalizes the core friction of information uncertainty, several real-world complexities expand beyond the single-signal model:

  • Multi-dimensional and selective disclosure: Real firms possess dozens of distinct performance metrics. Managers strategically disclose favorable operating segments while concealing underperforming divisions behind aggregated financial reporting line items.
  • Cheap talk versus audit verification: Disclosures regarding future operational milestones (such as AI capabilities or clinical trials) often lack third-party audit verification, exposing markets to strategic cheap-talk equilibria and greenwashing.
  • Adverse selection and lemon markets: When disclosure quality cannot be authenticated, the classical market-for-lemons dynamic (Akerlof 1970) takes over, causing high-quality firms to face prohibitive cost-of-capital penalties unless they deploy costly signals (Spence 1973).

5. Misconceptions

  • Market participants always interpret a firm's silence as an indication of the worst possible outcome.

    When outside investors recognize that management might genuinely be uninformed (arrival probability \(p < 1\)), silence is priced at the blended conditional expectation \(P(\text{silence}) = \E[v \mid \text{silence}] > 0\), rather than collapsing to the minimum support \(v=0\).

  • Voluntary disclosure is always value-maximizing for existing shareholders.

    Disclosing proprietary information regarding customer contracts, product margins, or intellectual property alerts commercial rivals and suppliers. When proprietary costs exceed capital market liquidity gains, shareholder value is maximized by remaining silent.

  • Any voluntary disclosure issued by management is legally guaranteed to be truthful.

    Qualitative statements and subjective forward-looking declarations are protected by PSLRA safe-harbor provisions and are often non-verifiable cheap talk. Only audited historical financial statements carry statutory auditor liability.

6. Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

Problem M.1. In the Dye-Jung-Kwon model with \(v \sim U[0, 1]\), information probability \(p = 0.50\), and zero proprietary cost \(c = 0\), calculate the quadratic formula coefficients and find the exact equilibrium cutoff \(v^*\).

Worked Solution

From Theorem 6.1: \(a = 0.5 p = 0.25\), \(b = (1 - p) = 0.50\), \(c_{\text{quad}} = -0.5(1 - p) = -0.25\). The equation is \(0.25 (v^*)^2 + 0.50 v^* - 0.25 = 0 \iff (v^*)^2 + 2 v^* - 1 = 0\). The positive root is \(v^* = \frac{-2 + \sqrt{4 + 4}}{2} = \sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.4142\).

Problem M.2. Following Problem M.1, compute the market price under silence \(P(\text{silence})\) and confirm that \(P(\text{silence}) = v^* - c\).

Worked Solution

With \(c = 0\), \(P(\text{silence}) = v^* - 0 = \sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.4142\). Verifying via the conditional expectation: numerator is \(0.5(1 - 0.5) + 0.5(0.5)(\sqrt{2}-1)^2 = 0.25 + 0.25(3 - 2\sqrt{2}) = 0.25 + 0.75 - 0.5\sqrt{2} = 1 - 0.5\sqrt{2}\). Denominator is \((1 - 0.5) + 0.5(\sqrt{2}-1) = 0.5\sqrt{2}\). Ratio: \(\frac{1 - 0.5\sqrt{2}}{0.5\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{0.5\sqrt{2}} - 1 = \sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.4142\), matching exactly.

Problem M.3. A company with information probability \(p = 0.80\) faces proprietary cost \(c = 0.15\). The equilibrium cutoff is \(v^* = 0.55\). If the manager observes signal \(v = 0.50\), what is the manager's optimal disclosure decision, and what price does the firm receive?

Worked Solution

Since \(v = 0.50 < v^* = 0.55\), the manager's optimal decision is to remain silent. If the manager disclosed, the net price would be \(v - c = 0.50 - 0.15 = 0.35\). By remaining silent, the firm receives \(P(\text{silence}) = v^* - c = 0.55 - 0.15 = 0.40 > 0.35\).

Proof Tier

Problem P.1. Prove that as information probability \(p \to 1\) with zero proprietary cost \(c = 0\), the Dye-Jung-Kwon equilibrium cutoff converges to zero (\(\lim_{p \to 1} v^* = 0\)), proving that Milgrom-Grossman unravelling is the limiting case of information uncertainty.

Worked Solution

From Theorem 6.1 with \(c=0\), the quadratic equation is \(\frac{1}{2} p (v^*)^2 + (1 - p) v^* - \frac{1}{2}(1 - p) = 0\). As \(p \to 1\), the term \((1 - p) \to 0\). The equation becomes \(\frac{1}{2}(1)(v^*)^2 + 0 \cdot v^* - 0 = 0 \implies \frac{1}{2}(v^*)^2 = 0 \implies v^* = 0\). Under full information certainty, even the slightest positive value above zero separates from lower values, cascading until all non-zero types disclose.

Problem P.2. Prove that \(\frac{\partial v^*}{\partial c} > 0\): the equilibrium disclosure cutoff is strictly increasing in the proprietary disclosure cost \(c\).

Worked Solution

Define the implicit function \(F(v^*, c) = \frac{1}{2} p (v^*)^2 + (1 - p - p c) v^* - \left(\frac{1}{2} + c\right)(1 - p) = 0\). By the implicit function theorem, \(\frac{\partial v^*}{\partial c} = -\frac{\partial F / \partial c}{\partial F / \partial v^*}\). Compute partial derivatives: \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial c} = -p v^* - (1 - p) = -[(1 - p) + p v^*] < 0\) for \(p \in (0, 1)\) and \(v^* \in [0, 1]\). For the denominator: \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial v^*} = p v^* + (1 - p - p c)\). At the equilibrium root, this derivative corresponds to the slope of the convex quadratic crossing zero from below, which is strictly positive (\(> 0\)). Thus \(\frac{\partial v^*}{\partial c} = -\frac{-[(1 - p) + p v^*]}{\partial F / \partial v^*} > 0\).

Problem P.3. Show that if investors possess rational expectations, the unconditional expected market value of the firm across all states equals the unconditional prior expectation of fundamental value \(\E[P] = \E[v] = 0.50\).

Worked Solution

Under rational pricing, market prices are conditional expectations: \(P(D) = \E[v \mid D]\), where \(D\) is the observed disclosure action (disclosure of \(v\) or silence). By the Law of Iterated Expectations, the unconditional expected price across all realized events is \(\E[P] = \E[\E[v \mid D]] = \E[v]\). For \(v \sim U[0, 1]\), \(\E[v] = \frac{0 + 1}{2} = 0.50\). Rational market pricing is an unbiased fair game.

Empirical Tier

Problem E.1. In a cross-sectional study of 300 manufacturing firms, researchers measure voluntary product-level margin disclosure. Highly competitive industries (low barriers to entry, high proprietary costs) have a disclosure rate of 18%, while monopolistic industries have a disclosure rate of 62%. Explain this divergence using Theorem 6.1.

Worked Solution

In highly competitive industries, disclosing detailed product margins reveals commercial trade secrets to agile competitors, corresponding to high proprietary cost \(c\). By Theorem 6.1, higher \(c\) increases the disclosure threshold \(v^*\), shrinking the disclosure interval \([v^*, 1]\) and resulting in a low 18% disclosure rate. In monopolistic industries where entry barriers protect market share, \(c \approx 0\), lowering \(v^*\) and resulting in a high 62% disclosure rate.

Problem E.2. A quantitative equity fund implements a strategy that shorts silent firms in industries with high information probability \(p\) and low proprietary cost \(c\). What is the theoretical justification for expecting silent firms in such industries to underperform?

Worked Solution

When information arrival probability \(p\) is high and proprietary cost \(c\) is near zero, Theorem 6.1 dictates that the disclosure cutoff \(v^*\) is close to zero. Consequently, almost all informed firms disclose. The tiny residual pool of silent firms consists overwhelmingly of firms that received catastrophic negative news (\(v < v^*\)). As subsequent mandatory reports reveal this hidden bad news, these silent firms experience severe downward price corrections.

Problem E.3. How does the implementation of mandatory segment reporting under SFAS 131 affect voluntary disclosure behavior in diversified conglomerates?

Worked Solution

SFAS 131 mandated disclosure of operating segment profitability aligned with internal management reporting. By legally compelling disclosure of formerly concealed segment data, the regulation lowered the residual proprietary cost friction of voluntary disclosures. Firms whose profitable segments were newly exposed expanded voluntary text and guidance to contextualize margins, whereas unprofitable segments faced immediate capital market discipline.

Research Tier

Problem R.1. How do ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) voluntary disclosures differ structurally from voluntary earnings guidance with respect to audit verification and cheap-talk dynamics?

Worked Solution

Voluntary earnings guidance is disciplined by subsequent mandatory audited 10-Q/10-K filings, which provide an objective, legally binding ground-truth realization. In contrast, many voluntary ESG disclosures lack standardized third-party audit standards and standardized accounting taxonomies, enabling corporate managers to engage in strategic cheap talk ("greenwashing")—selectively emphasizing qualitative sustainability narratives while concealing carbon emissions or labor liabilities without facing legal consequences.

Problem R.2. Discuss how strategic disclosure timing across earnings season (clustering disclosures on Friday afternoons vs Monday mornings) interacts with limited investor attention.

Worked Solution

When corporate managers are forced to disclose adverse voluntary news, they frequently release announcements on Friday afternoons after market close or concurrent with major macroeconomic data releases (e.g. FOMC statements). Under limited investor attention, market participants allocate cognitive processing capacity to broad market movements, dampening the immediate announcement abnormal return and delaying full price discovery over subsequent trading days.

Problem R.3. How can machine learning models applied to mandatory Form 8-K filings distinguish between genuinely unexpected voluntary events and routine compliance disclosures?

Worked Solution

Form 8-K filings span diverse item classifications (e.g. Item 2.02 for results of operations, Item 7.01 for Reg FD disclosures, Item 8.01 for other voluntary events). Machine learning classifiers combine Item classification tags, filing timestamps relative to market hours, textual cosine distance against historical boilerplates, and word embeddings of forward-looking verbs to isolate high-conviction discretionary disclosures from routine administrative filings.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. Under what conditions does the Milgrom-Grossman (1982) unravelling result predict complete voluntary disclosure?

    Options

    Unravelling requires that information is verifiable, disclosure is costless, and investors know the manager possesses the signal.

  2. In the Dye-Jung-Kwon model with \(v \sim U[0, 1]\), \(p = 0.50\), and \(c = 0\), what is the equilibrium cutoff \(v^* = \sqrt{2} - 1\)? (e.g. 0.41)

    \(v^* = \sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.4142\).

  3. Why does information uncertainty (\(p < 1\)) prevent complete disclosure unravelling?

    Options

    If the manager might be uninformed, silence is not an unambiguous signal of the worst outcome, providing cover for informed managers with bad news to remain silent.

  4. How does an increase in proprietary disclosure cost \(c\) affect the equilibrium disclosure cutoff \(v^*\)?

    Options

    Higher proprietary cost imposes a competitive penalty on disclosure, raising the bar \(v^*\) required for an informed manager to justify revealing news.

  5. What foundational concept from Akerlof (1970) describes market breakdown under unmitigated information asymmetry?

    Options

    Akerlof (1970) showed that without credible disclosure or warranties, adverse selection causes bad types to drive good types out of the market.

  6. Under a uniform prior \(v \sim U[0, 1]\), what is the unconditional expected market value \(\E[P]\) across all disclosure outcomes? (e.g. 0.50)

    By the Law of Iterated Expectations, \(\E[P] = \E[\E[v \mid D]] = \E[v] = 0.50\).

  7. Why are voluntary ESG disclosures often vulnerable to cheap talk compared to management earnings guidance?

    Options

    Without standardized accounting rules and audited realization benchmarks, non-financial disclosures can degenerate into unverifiable cheap talk.

  8. What timing tactic do managers use when compelled to release adverse voluntary news?
