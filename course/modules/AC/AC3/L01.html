Information Content of Earnings & Seminal Event Studies

Prior to 1968, accounting theorists debated whether historical-cost profit was a meaningless artifact of arbitrary conventions. Ball & Brown (1968) resolved the debate empirically by demonstrating that security markets systematically revise equity valuations in the direction of unexpected accounting net income.

Before empirical finance took hold, normative theorists dismissed accounting profit as an arbitrary construct devoid of economic meaning. Ball and Brown (1968) dismantled that skepticism by matching corporate earnings releases with equity price movements. Their findings established that capital markets respond systematically to financial reporting, while revealing that prices anticipate most earnings news through competing information channels before official publication. Understanding how markets price corporate profit disclosures is the foundation of modern quantitative equity research and financial statement analysis.

1. The Epistemological Break in Capital Markets Research

In mid-twentieth-century accounting literature, normative scholars argued that because historical-cost accrual accounting relies on subjective allocations of depreciation and revenue timing, reported net income was uninterpretable. Ball & Brown (1968) re-framed financial reporting under capital market theory: if accounting income numbers convey decision-useful signals to equity investors, aggregate market prices will reflect that information upon disclosure.

Definition 1.1 · Information Content of Earnings

A corporate disclosure of accounting net income possesses information content of earnings if its release alters the conditional probability distribution of future equity cash flows in a manner that induces a statistically significant shift in market prices or return volatility during the announcement window.

Formally, let \(\mathcal{F}_{t-}\) denote the public information filtration immediately prior to the earnings release at event date \(t\), and \(\mathcal{F}_t\) denote the filtration updated with the realized accounting profit \(X_{i,t}\). Earnings release \(X_{i,t}\) has information content if:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[P_{i,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] &\neq \E[P_{i,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-}], \quad \text{or} \\ \Var(R_{i,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_t) &> \Var(R_{i,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-}). \end{aligned} \]
Remark 1.1 · Price Reaction Versus Trading Volume

A price change measures a shift in the aggregate consensus expectation of the entire market. In contrast, trading volume around an earnings announcement reflects differences in belief revisions across individual market participants, demonstrating that information content can generate volume even when the mean price change is zero.

2. Market Model Benchmarks and Abnormal Performance Indices

To isolate the price impact specific to a firm's earnings release, an event study purges general market movements using a benchmark asset pricing model. The market model posits a stable linear relation between the firm's simple return \(R_{i,t}\) and the market portfolio return \(R_{m,t}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \quad \E[\varepsilon_{i,t}] = 0, \quad \Cov(R_{m,t}, \varepsilon_{i,t}) = 0. \end{aligned} \]

The parameter vector \((\alpha_i, \beta_i)\) is estimated via the OLS estimator over an estimation window \([\tau_0, \tau_1]\) preceding the event window. The resulting abnormal return for firm \(i\) on event date \(t\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} AR_{i,t} = R_{i,t} - (\hat{\alpha}_i + \hat{\beta}_i R_{m,t}). \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 1.1 · Unbiasedness of Event-Window Abnormal Returns

Suppose the true data-generating process follows the market model with parameters \((\alpha_i, \beta_i)\), the estimation window \([\tau_0, \tau_1]\) is strictly exogenous to the event, and the market is semi-strong efficient under Fama (1970). Then the expected abnormal return conditional on pre-event information is zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[AR_{i,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{\tau_1}] = 0 \quad \forall t > \tau_1. \end{aligned} \]
Proof

By the Gauss-Markov theorem, OLS estimators \(\hat{\alpha}_i\) and \(\hat{\beta}_i\) computed over \([\tau_0, \tau_1]\) are unbiased for \((\alpha_i, \beta_i)\). Conditioning on \(\mathcal{F}_{\tau_1}\) and taking expectations across the event window:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[AR_{i,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{\tau_1}] &= \E[R_{i,t} - \hat{\alpha}_i - \hat{\beta}_i R_{m,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{\tau_1}] \\ &= \E[\alpha_i + \beta_i R_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t} - \hat{\alpha}_i - \hat{\beta}_i R_{m,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{\tau_1}] \\ &= (\alpha_i - \E[\hat{\alpha}_i]) + (\beta_i - \E[\hat{\beta}_i])\E[R_{m,t}] + \E[\varepsilon_{i,t}] \\ &= 0 + 0 + 0 = 0. \end{aligned} \]

Hence, non-zero expected abnormal returns during the event window arise exclusively from new information revealed at date \(t\).

Ball & Brown (1968) constructed the Abnormal Performance Index (API) across event months \(m \in [-11, +6]\) relative to annual earnings announcement month 0:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{API}_M = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \prod_{m=-11}^M (1 + AR_{i,m}) - 1. \end{aligned} \]

Modern event studies typically use Cumulative Abnormal Returns (CAR) due to superior additive aggregation properties across time and cross-sections:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CAR}_i(t_1, t_2) = \sum_{t=t_1}^{t_2} AR_{i,t}. \end{aligned} \]

3. Anticipation, Leakage, and Announcement Price Discovery

In their sample of 261 NYSE corporations over 1946–1965, Ball & Brown classified firms into positive unexpected income (good news) and negative unexpected income (bad news) based on a regression of firm earnings changes on aggregate market earnings changes.

Their primary empirical finding was that approximately 85% to 90% of the information contained in the annual earnings number had already been captured by the market prior to the announcement month. Stock prices drift upward for good-news firms and downward for bad-news firms throughout the eleven months leading up to the report.

Example 1.1 · Decomposing Event-Window Price Discovery

Consider a firm with cumulative abnormal return \(\mathrm{CAR}[-120, +1] = +10.0\%\). If the pre-announcement drift \(\mathrm{CAR}[-120, -2] = +8.5\%\) and the 2-day announcement reaction \(\mathrm{CAR}[-1, +1] = +1.5\%\), the pre-announcement anticipation ratio is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{CAR}[-120, -2]}{\mathrm{CAR}[-120, +1]} = \frac{8.5\%}{10.0\%} = 85.0\%. \end{aligned} \]

The announcement window captures the remaining 15.0% of the total price revision, confirming both the information content of the release and the presence of prior informal information flow.

Lab 1 · Event Study CAR Around Earnings Announcements

Simulating market model abnormal returns across a panel of 40 firms with good versus bad earnings surprises will reveal three properties: (1) positive spread prior to announcement day 0 due to information leakage, (2) a distinct upward step at day 0 for good news and downward step for bad news, and (3) persistent separation through day +20.

"""AC3 lab L01: event study cumulative abnormal returns around earnings announcements.

SIMULATED: the firm return series, market index returns, and earnings announcement dates are
generated from a seeded pseudo-random number generator. They do not represent real market prices.
The market model is estimated on the pre-event window [-120, -21]; abnormal returns are computed
on the event window [-20, +20]. All calculations use exact floats.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random

SEED = 20261110
N_FIRMS = 40
EST_LEN = 100  # days -120 to -21
EVENT_LEN = 41  # days -20 to +20 (day 0 is announcement)


def simulate_market_and_firms(seed: int) -> tuple[list[float], list[list[float]], list[float]]:
    """Generate simulated market returns, firm returns, and earnings surprises.

    Returns (market_returns, firm_returns, surprises).
    Total days = 141 (-120 to +20).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    total_days = EST_LEN + EVENT_LEN
    mkt = [rng.gauss(0.0004, 0.010) for _ in range(total_days)]

    firm_returns: list[list[float]] = []
    surprises: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(N_FIRMS):
        beta = rng.uniform(0.7, 1.4)
        alpha = rng.uniform(-0.0001, 0.0001)
        sigma_e = rng.uniform(0.012, 0.025)

        # Standardized surprise in [-3, 3]
        sue = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        surprises.append(sue)

        # Daily returns
        rets: list[float] = []
        for t in range(total_days):
            event_day = t - 120  # day index relative to announcement at 120
            ret = alpha + beta * mkt[t] + rng.gauss(0.0, sigma_e)
            # Information leakage during [-10, -1]
            if -10 <= event_day < 0:
                ret += 0.0010 * sue
            # Announcement day reaction at day 0
            elif event_day == 0:
                ret += 0.0150 * sue
            # Modest post-announcement drift during [+1, +20]
            elif 1 <= event_day <= 20:
                ret += 0.0005 * sue
            rets.append(ret)
        firm_returns.append(rets)

    return mkt, firm_returns, surprises


def estimate_market_model(mkt: list[float], firm_ret: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Fit market model R_it = alpha + beta * R_mt on estimation window [0:EST_LEN].

    Returns (alpha, beta).
    """
    n = EST_LEN
    x = mkt[:n]
    y = firm_ret[:n]

    x_bar = sum(x) / n
    y_bar = sum(y) / n

    cov_xy = sum((x[i] - x_bar) * (y[i] - y_bar) for i in range(n))
    var_x = sum((x[i] - x_bar) ** 2 for i in range(n))

    beta = cov_xy / var_x if var_x > 0 else 1.0
    alpha = y_bar - beta * x_bar
    return alpha, beta


def compute_car_series(mkt: list[float], firm_ret: list[float], alpha: float, beta: float) -> list[float]:
    """Compute cumulative abnormal return over the event window [-20, +20].

    Returns list of 41 CAR values from day -20 to day +20.
    """
    cars: list[float] = []
    cum = 0.0
    for t in range(EST_LEN, EST_LEN + EVENT_LEN):
        ar = firm_ret[t] - (alpha + beta * mkt[t])
        cum += ar
        cars.append(cum)
    return cars


def main() -> None:
    mkt, firm_returns, surprises = simulate_market_and_firms(SEED)

    good_news_cars: list[list[float]] = []
    bad_news_cars: list[list[float]] = []

    for i in range(N_FIRMS):
        alpha, beta = estimate_market_model(mkt, firm_returns[i])
        car = compute_car_series(mkt, firm_returns[i], alpha, beta)
        if surprises[i] >= 0:
            good_news_cars.append(car)
        else:
            bad_news_cars.append(car)

    # Average CAR at selected event days: -20, -10, -1, 0, +1, +10, +20
    # Day indices in 0..40: -20 -> 0, -10 -> 10, -1 -> 19, 0 -> 20, +1 -> 21, +10 -> 30, +20 -> 40
    check_days = [(-20, 0), (-10, 10), (-1, 19), (0, 20), (1, 21), (10, 30), (20, 40)]

    print(f"seed {SEED} firms {N_FIRMS} good_news {len(good_news_cars)} bad_news {len(bad_news_cars)}")

    for day_label, idx in check_days:
        mean_good = sum(c[idx] for c in good_news_cars) / len(good_news_cars)
        mean_bad = sum(c[idx] for c in bad_news_cars) / len(bad_news_cars)
        spread = mean_good - mean_bad
        print(f"day {day_label:+3d} : good_CAR {mean_good:+.4f} | bad_CAR {mean_bad:+.4f} | spread {spread:+.4f}")

    # Verify Ball & Brown (1968) properties:
    # 1. Pre-announcement drift: spread is positive before day 0
    car_pre_spread = sum(c[19] for c in good_news_cars) / len(good_news_cars) - sum(c[19] for c in bad_news_cars) / len(
        bad_news_cars
    )
    # 2. Announcement jump: spread increases noticeably at day 0
    mean_good_0 = sum(c[20] for c in good_news_cars) / len(good_news_cars)
    mean_bad_0 = sum(c[20] for c in bad_news_cars) / len(bad_news_cars)
    car_jump = (mean_good_0 - mean_bad_0) - car_pre_spread
    # 3. Post-announcement spread at day +20
    car_post_spread = sum(c[40] for c in good_news_cars) / len(good_news_cars) - sum(
        c[40] for c in bad_news_cars
    ) / len(bad_news_cars)

    print("ball_brown_properties:")
    print(f"pre_announcement_spread_positive: {car_pre_spread > 0}")
    print(f"announcement_jump_positive: {car_jump > 0}")
    print(f"post_announcement_spread_positive: {car_post_spread > car_pre_spread}")

4. Where the Canon Breaks

The classical event-study paradigm of Ball & Brown (1968) assumes that the benchmark estimation window is uncontaminated by the event, that announcement dates are exogenous, and that market model betas are stationary. In practical quantitative applications, several breakdown points emerge:

  • Announcement date clustering: Earnings announcements concentrate heavily within specific calendar weeks ("earnings season"). Cross-sectional correlation across event-window abnormal returns invalidates standard OLS standard errors, causing severe over-rejection of the null hypothesis unless cross-sectional clustering is accommodated.
  • Event-induced variance: The variance of firm returns spikes dramatically during announcement days. Assuming that estimation-window residual variance equals event-window variance leads to downward-biased test statistics (addressed by the Boehmer-Musumeci-Poulsen standardized cross-sectional test).
  • Endogenous announcement timing: Firms that report earlier than their historical schedule tend to disclose substantial positive surprises, whereas firms that delay their earnings announcement date systematically disclose adverse operating losses.

5. Misconceptions

  • Ball & Brown (1968) showed that all earnings information is incorporated into prices only at the announcement date.

    Ball & Brown demonstrated that 85% to 90% of the annual earnings information is anticipated by the market before the release month. The announcement window provides the final confirmatory resolution rather than the initial discovery of the news.

  • If a company reports positive accounting net income, its stock price must rise upon announcement.

    Security prices respond exclusively to unexpected earnings relative to prior market expectations, not the level or sign of reported income. A profitable firm reporting earnings below consensus will suffer an immediate negative abnormal return.

  • Using the raw return \(R_{i,t}\) instead of market-adjusted abnormal return \(AR_{i,t}\) produces valid event-study inference.

    Failing to remove contemporaneous market return exposure confounds idiosyncratic earnings news with macro-level market movements. A firm reporting strong earnings on a day the broad market drops 3% could show a negative raw return despite positive idiosyncratic abnormal performance.

6. Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

Problem M.1. A firm has estimated market model parameters \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.0002\) and \(\hat{\beta} = 1.25\). On announcement day \(t=0\), the firm's stock return is \(+3.20\%\) and the market index return is \(+0.80\%\). Compute the abnormal return \(AR_{i,0}\) as a percentage.

Worked Solution

Expected return: \(\hat{R}_{i,0} = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} R_{m,0} = 0.0002 + 1.25 \times 0.0080 = 0.0002 + 0.0100 = 0.0102 = 1.02\%\). The abnormal return is the difference between realized return and benchmark return: \(AR_{i,0} = 3.20\% - 1.02\% = +2.18\%\).

Problem M.2. Over a 3-day announcement window \([-1, +1]\), a firm experiences daily abnormal returns \(AR_{-1} = +0.50\%\), \(AR_0 = +2.20\%\), and \(AR_{+1} = -0.30\%\). Compute the Cumulative Abnormal Return \(\mathrm{CAR}[-1, +1]\).

Worked Solution

Cumulative Abnormal Return is the direct arithmetic sum of abnormal returns across the event window: \(\mathrm{CAR}[-1, +1] = 0.50\% + 2.20\% + (-0.30\%) = +2.40\%\).

Problem M.3. Over a 100-day estimation window, the sample variance of the market model residual \(\hat{\varepsilon}_{i,t}\) is \(s^2 = 0.0004\) (standard deviation 2.0%). Under i.i.d. residual assumptions, what is the standard deviation of a 5-day Cumulative Abnormal Return \(\mathrm{CAR}[-2, +2]\)?

Worked Solution

Under the null hypothesis of independent daily residuals, the variance of the sum equals the sum of daily variances: \(\Var(\mathrm{CAR}) = 5 \times s^2 = 5 \times 0.0004 = 0.0020\). Taking the square root gives standard deviation \(\sigma_{\mathrm{CAR}} = \sqrt{0.0020} \approx 0.04472 = 4.472\%\).

Proof Tier

Problem P.1. Let daily abnormal returns be independent and identically distributed with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2\). Prove that the variance of the average Cumulative Abnormal Return across \(N\) independent firms over an \(L\)-day event window is \(\Var(\overline{\mathrm{CAR}}) = \frac{L \sigma^2}{N}\).

Worked Solution

For each firm \(i\), \(\mathrm{CAR}_i = \sum_{t=1}^L AR_{i,t}\). Because daily returns are independent over time, \(\Var(\mathrm{CAR}_i) = \sum_{t=1}^L \Var(AR_{i,t}) = L \sigma^2\). The cross-sectional average across \(N\) independent firms is \(\overline{\mathrm{CAR}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathrm{CAR}_i\). Since the firms are mutually independent, \(\Var(\overline{\mathrm{CAR}}) = \frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{i=1}^N \Var(\mathrm{CAR}_i) = \frac{1}{N^2} (N L \sigma^2) = \frac{L \sigma^2}{N}\).

Problem P.2. Prove that if an event window overlaps with the market model estimation window by \(K\) days, the OLS estimator of beta is biased toward zero if the event induces positive abnormal returns on high-market-return days.

Worked Solution

Let the observed return in the contaminated estimation window be \(R_{it} = \alpha + \beta R_{mt} + \varepsilon_{it} + \gamma_t \indic_{t \in \text{event}}\). The OLS slope estimator is \(\hat{\beta} = \frac{\Cov(R_{it}, R_{mt})}{\Var(R_{mt})} = \beta + \frac{\Cov(\gamma_t \indic_t, R_{mt})}{\Var(R_{mt})}\). When the event impact \(\gamma_t\) is correlated with market returns \(R_{mt}\), the second term does not vanish. If the event effect attenuates covariance relative to sample variance, \(\hat{\beta}\) suffers classical contamination bias, demonstrating why estimation windows must strictly precede the event window.

Problem P.3. Show that the Abnormal Performance Index \(\mathrm{API}_M = \prod_{m=1}^M (1 + AR_m) - 1\) is approximately equal to the Cumulative Abnormal Return \(\mathrm{CAR}_M = \sum_{m=1}^M AR_m\) for small abnormal returns.

Worked Solution

Expanding the product: \(\prod_{m=1}^M (1 + AR_m) = 1 + \sum_{m=1}^M AR_m + \sum_{j < k} AR_j AR_k + \dots\). Taking the first-order Taylor expansion around \(AR_m = 0\) for all \(m\), second- and higher-order cross-terms are \(O(AR^2)\). Subtracting 1 yields \(\mathrm{API}_M = \sum_{m=1}^M AR_m + O(AR^2) \approx \mathrm{CAR}_M\).

Empirical Tier

Problem E.1. In an event study of 50 firms announcing positive earnings surprises, the sample mean announcement-day abnormal return is \(\overline{AR} = +1.40\%\) with cross-sectional standard error \(\mathrm{se}(\overline{AR}) = 0.35\%\). Construct a 95% confidence interval for the mean announcement reaction and state whether the null hypothesis of no information content is rejected at the 5% significance level.

Worked Solution

Using the standard normal critical value \(z_{0.025} = 1.96\), the 95% confidence interval is \(1.40\% \pm 1.96 \times 0.35\% = [1.40\% - 0.686\%, 1.40\% + 0.686\%] = [+0.714\%, +2.086\%]\). The t-statistic is \(t = 1.40 / 0.35 = 4.00 > 1.96\). Because the confidence interval excludes zero and \(p < 0.001\), the null hypothesis of no information content is rejected.

Problem E.2. A researcher measures pre-announcement cumulative abnormal return \(\mathrm{CAR}[-60, -2] = +4.5\%\) and announcement reaction \(\mathrm{CAR}[-1, +1] = +1.5\%\). Calculate the proportion of total information impounded into price prior to the official announcement window.

Worked Solution

Total cumulative price revision is \(\mathrm{CAR}[-60, +1] = 4.5\% + 1.5\% = 6.0\%\). The proportion impounded prior to the announcement window is \(\frac{4.5\%}{6.0\%} = 0.75\) (75.0%). This empirical result mirrors the Ball & Brown finding that the majority of earnings news reaches the market prior to formal report release.

Problem E.3. Suppose 20 firms announce earnings on the exact same date and their event-window returns have an average pairwise cross-sectional correlation of \(\rho = 0.15\). Compute the variance inflation factor \(\mathrm{VIF} = 1 + (N-1)\rho\) for the portfolio abnormal return variance relative to cross-sectional independence.

Worked Solution

Under cross-sectional correlation \(\rho\), \(\Var(\overline{AR}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N} [1 + (N-1)\rho]\). With \(N = 20\) and \(\rho = 0.15\), the variance inflation factor is \(1 + (20 - 1) \times 0.15 = 1 + 19 \times 0.15 = 1 + 2.85 = 3.85\). Standard errors computed under an independence assumption would underestimate true standard errors by a factor of \(\sqrt{3.85} \approx 1.962\).

Research Tier

Problem R.1. Why does the presence of high-frequency algorithmic trading compress the effective event window from months (Ball & Brown 1968) to seconds in modern financial markets, and how does this affect the power of event-study tests?

Worked Solution

Algorithmic news scrapers process machine-readable corporate press releases within milliseconds, impounding unexpected earnings into limit order books instantaneously. Narrowing the event window from monthly intervals to narrow intraday windows drastically reduces the cumulative variance of non-event noise (\(L \sigma^2\) shrinks as \(L\) contracts from days to seconds), exponentially increasing the statistical power of abnormal return tests while isolating clean causal identification.

Problem R.2. How does selective disclosure prior to Regulation FD (Fair Disclosure, 2000) explain the high pre-announcement drift observed in historical samples, and what structural break occurs post-Reg FD?

Worked Solution

Prior to Regulation FD, corporate executives routinely communicated material non-public earnings updates to select sell-side analysts and institutional investors ahead of the broad public release. This selective guidance produced substantial private information leakage and continuous price drift during the pre-announcement period. Post-Reg FD, material guidance must be disclosed simultaneously to the public, significantly reducing pre-announcement drift and concentrating price revisions directly at public filing timestamps.

Problem R.3. Contrast the Abnormal Performance Index (API) metric used by Ball & Brown (1968) with buy-and-hold abnormal return (BHAR) and cumulative abnormal return (CAR) regarding compounding bias over long horizons.

Worked Solution

CAR aggregates arithmetic abnormal returns across periods: \(\sum AR_t\). Over long horizons, CAR suffers from measurement bias because arithmetic summing assumes continuous rebalancing without transaction costs. BHAR and API compound returns geometrically: \(\prod (1 + R_{it}) - \prod (1 + R_{mt})\). However, long-horizon BHARs suffer from severe right-skewness and cross-sectional correlation bias, which creates severe false-positive rejections unless matched-control bootstrapping is deployed.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What was the primary conceptual breakthrough established by Ball & Brown (1968)?

    Options

    Ball & Brown (1968) demonstrated that capital markets reflect the information content of accounting earnings, refuting normative claims that accounting numbers lacked economic relevance.

  2. Approximately what percentage of the annual earnings information did Ball & Brown (1968) find was anticipated by the market before the official announcement month? (enter integer percentage, e.g. 85)

    Ball & Brown (1968, p. 176) documented that 85% to 90% of annual earnings information is captured prior to the report month.

  3. Why must market model estimation windows precede the event window rather than overlap with it?

    Options

    Overlapping windows cause the event's own abnormal performance to contaminate the benchmark alpha and beta estimates.

  4. A stock has \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.00\) and \(\hat{\beta} = 1.50\). On announcement day, market return is \(+1.00\%\) and the stock return is \(+3.00\%\). What is the abnormal return in percent? (e.g. 1.50)

    \(AR = 3.00\% - (0.00 + 1.50 \times 1.00\%) = 3.00\% - 1.50\% = 1.50\%\).

  5. What does an Abnormal Performance Index (API) measure across an event window?

    Options

    API compounds \(\prod (1 + AR_m) - 1\) to track cumulative abnormal value creation across the event timeline.

  6. How does trading volume around earnings releases differ conceptually from price changes?

    Options

    As Beaver (1968) demonstrated, individual investors can disagree and trade substantial volume even if the net market consensus valuation remains unchanged.

  7. Daily abnormal returns for days \([-1, 0, +1]\) are \(+0.80\%\), \(+3.20\%\), and \(-0.40\%\). What is \(\mathrm{CAR}[-1, +1]\) in percent? (e.g. 3.60)

    \(0.80\% + 3.20\% - 0.40\% = 3.60\%\).

  8. What structural phenomenon occurs when multiple firms in an event study announce earnings on the exact same date?

    Options

    Clustering on calendar dates induces contemporaneous cross-correlation across firm residuals, requiring clustered standard error adjustments.
