Standardized Unexpected Earnings (SUE) & Time-Series Models

Capital markets react to unexpected earnings rather than reported levels. Isolating the genuine news component requires modeling the seasonal time-series properties of quarterly corporate profits and standardizing forecast errors to eliminate cross-sectional scale heterogeneity.

Because corporate earnings exhibit pronounced quarterly seasonality, comparing current performance with the immediate prior quarter produces misleading signals. Standardizing unexpected earnings by historical forecast error volatility transforms noisy raw dollar differences into stationary, scale-invariant signals. In quantitative equity strategies, Standardized Unexpected Earnings (SUE) serves as the primary metric for tracking earnings momentum, ranking cross-sectional surprise, and testing the limits of informational efficiency in capital markets.

1. Time-Series Dynamics of Quarterly Corporate Earnings

Building on the information content of earnings established in Lesson 1, asset pricing models require an explicit specification of market earnings expectations \(\E[E_{i,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\). Unlike annual earnings, which roughly follow an unadjusted random walk, quarterly earnings exhibit two dominant structural characteristics: strong quarterly seasonality and serial correlation in seasonal increments.

Definition 2.1 · Earnings Surprise

The earnings surprise (or unexpected earnings, \(UE_{i,t}\)) is the realization of reported accounting net income \(E_{i,t}\) minus the consensus market expectation \(\widehat{E}_{i,t}\) formed prior to publication:

\[ \begin{aligned} UE_{i,t} = E_{i,t} - \widehat{E}_{i,t}. \end{aligned} \]

Bernard & Thomas (1989) evaluate two primary benchmark time-series specifications for quarterly earnings:

  1. Seasonal Random Walk with Drift: \[ \begin{aligned} E_{i,t} = E_{i,t-4} + \delta_i + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \quad \E[\varepsilon_{i,t}] = 0. \end{aligned} \] Here \(\delta_i\) represents the average seasonal change (historical drift) for firm \(i\), estimated over historical quarters \(\tau = 4, \dots, t-1\): \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\delta}_i = \frac{1}{T-4} \sum_{\tau=4}^{T-1} (E_{i,\tau} - E_{i,\tau-4}). \end{aligned} \]
  2. Foster (1977) First-Order Autoregressive Seasonal Model: \[ \begin{aligned} (E_{i,t} - E_{i,t-4}) = \delta_i + \phi_i (E_{i,t-1} - E_{i,t-5}) + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \quad |\phi_i| < 1. \end{aligned} \] This process captures the empirical reality that adjacent quarterly seasonal changes exhibit positive serial correlation (\(\phi_i \approx 0.35\)).

2. Construction and Properties of SUE

Raw unexpected earnings \(UE_{i,t}\) are measured in currency per share or total dollar magnitude. Comparing raw forecast errors across firms introduces severe heteroskedasticity: a $1.00 per share surprise represents negligible news for a high-price growth conglomerate with volatile earnings, but an enormous shock for a stable utility trading at $10.00.

Definition 2.2 · Standardized Unexpected Earnings (SUE)

Standardized Unexpected Earnings (\(\mathrm{SUE}_{i,t}\)) deflates raw unexpected earnings by the sample standard deviation of historical forecast errors over an estimation window of \(T\) quarters:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{SUE}_{i,t} = \frac{E_{i,t} - \widehat{E}_{i,t}}{\hat{\sigma}(E_{i} - \widehat{E}_{i})}, \end{aligned} \] where \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\sigma}(E_i - \widehat{E}_i) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T-5} \sum_{\tau=4}^{T-1} (UE_{i,\tau} - \overline{UE}_i)^2}. \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 2.1 · Scale Invariance of SUE

Let \(\widetilde{E}_{i,t} = c \cdot E_{i,t}\) for any constant scalar \(c > 0\) (e.g. currency conversion or stock split adjustments). Then the resulting Standardized Unexpected Earnings is strictly invariant to \(c\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{SUE}(\widetilde{E}_{i,t}) = \mathrm{SUE}(E_{i,t}). \end{aligned} \]
Proof

Under linear scaling by \(c > 0\), the estimated drift scales linearly: \(\hat{\delta}(\widetilde{E}) = c \hat{\delta}(E)\). The expected value scales linearly: \(\widehat{\widetilde{E}}_{i,t} = c \widehat{E}_{i,t}\). Thus, raw unexpected earnings scale by \(c\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \widetilde{UE}_{i,t} = c E_{i,t} - c \widehat{E}_{i,t} = c \cdot UE_{i,t}. \end{aligned} \]

The sample standard deviation of historical forecast errors scales by \(c\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\sigma}(\widetilde{UE}_i) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T-5} \sum_{\tau=4}^{T-1} (c \cdot UE_{i,\tau} - c \cdot \overline{UE}_i)^2} = c \cdot \hat{\sigma}(UE_i). \end{aligned} \]

Taking the ratio:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{SUE}(\widetilde{E}_{i,t}) = \frac{c \cdot UE_{i,t}}{c \cdot \hat{\sigma}(UE_i)} = \frac{UE_{i,t}}{\hat{\sigma}(UE_i)} = \mathrm{SUE}(E_{i,t}). \end{aligned} \]

The scalar \(c\) cancels exactly, proving that SUE is scale-invariant.

3. Volatility Standardization Versus Price Deflation

An alternative convention in empirical accounting scales raw unexpected earnings by the pre-announcement stock price \(P_{i,t-1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} UE^{\mathrm{price}}_{i,t} = \frac{E_{i,t} - \widehat{E}_{i,t}}{P_{i,t-1}}. \end{aligned} \]

While price deflation yields an earnings surprise expressed as an abnormal return yield, it conflates fundamental earnings surprises with the firm's equity valuation multiple:

  • Valuation multiple confounding: A value or financially distressed firm with a depressed price \(P_{i,t-1}\) exhibits an artificially amplified \(UE^{\mathrm{price}}\) for small dollar errors. Conversely, high-multiple growth stocks exhibit artificially compressed price-scaled surprises.
  • Heteroskedasticity: SUE normalizes cross-sectional variance toward unity (\(\Var(\mathrm{SUE}) \approx 1\)), whereas \(UE^{\mathrm{price}}\) retains massive cross-sectional heteroskedasticity driven by price dispersion.
Example 2.1 · Worked Computation of SUE

A firm's quarterly earnings over quarters 0 through 7 are \([10.0, 12.0, 11.0, 13.0, 11.0, 13.5, 12.0, 14.5]\). Quarter 8 reports \(E_8 = 15.0\). The historical seasonal changes \((E_\tau - E_{\tau-4})\) for \(\tau \in [4, 7]\) are:

\[ \begin{aligned} d_4 &= 11.0 - 10.0 = 1.0, \quad d_5 = 13.5 - 12.0 = 1.5, \\ d_6 &= 12.0 - 11.0 = 1.0, \quad d_7 = 14.5 - 13.0 = 1.5. \end{aligned} \]

Average seasonal drift \(\hat{\delta} = \frac{1.0 + 1.5 + 1.0 + 1.5}{4} = 1.25\). Forecast for quarter 8: \(\widehat{E}_8 = E_4 + \hat{\delta} = 11.0 + 1.25 = 12.25\). Raw surprise: \(UE_8 = 15.0 - 12.25 = +2.75\). Errors around drift are \([-0.25, +0.25, -0.25, +0.25]\). Sample variance: \(\frac{4 \times 0.25^2}{3} = \frac{0.25}{3} \approx 0.08333\); \(\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{0.08333} \approx 0.2887\). Standardized unexpected earnings:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{SUE}_8 = \frac{2.75}{0.2887} \approx +9.53. \end{aligned} \]

Lab 1 · Seasonal Random Walk and SUE Heteroskedasticity

Estimating SUE across 12 simulated firms of heterogeneous scale (scale parameter ranging from 5 to 100) will demonstrate that raw forecast errors exhibit cross-sectional variance that varies by over an order of magnitude, whereas SUE scores normalize cross-sectional variance close to 1.0.

"""AC3 lab L02: standardized unexpected earnings (SUE) and seasonal random walk models.

SIMULATED: the quarterly earnings series for 12 firms across 20 quarters are generated from
a seeded seasonal random walk process with firm-specific drift and heteroskedastic innovation
volatility. They are not real corporate filings. All calculations use standard library float and math.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random

SEED = 20261110
N_FIRMS = 12
N_QUARTERS = 20
EST_QUARTERS = 16  # quarters 0..15 used for estimation, quarter 16 tested


def simulate_quarterly_earnings(seed: int) -> tuple[list[list[float]], list[float], list[float]]:
    """Simulate quarterly earnings with seasonal persistence, drift, and heteroskedastic noise.

    Returns (earnings_matrix, firm_scale, true_drifts).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    earnings: list[list[float]] = []
    scales: list[float] = []
    drifts: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(N_FIRMS):
        # Varying scale: large cap (scale 100) vs small cap (scale 5)
        scale = rng.uniform(5.0, 100.0)
        drift = rng.uniform(0.01, 0.05) * scale
        sigma = rng.uniform(0.05, 0.20) * scale
        scales.append(scale)
        drifts.append(drift)

        # Seasonal base for 4 quarters
        base = [scale * (1.0 + 0.1 * q) for q in range(4)]
        series = list(base)
        for t in range(4, N_QUARTERS):
            eps = rng.gauss(0.0, sigma)
            val = series[t - 4] + drift + eps
            series.append(val)
        earnings.append(series)

    return earnings, scales, drifts


def estimate_sue(series: list[float], test_q: int) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Estimate seasonal random walk with drift and compute SUE for quarter test_q.

    Returns (forecast, raw_error, error_std, sue).
    """
    # Historical seasonal differences up to test_q - 1
    # Quarter t has seasonal difference diff_t = series[t] - series[t-4]
    diffs = [series[t] - series[t - 4] for t in range(4, test_q)]
    if len(diffs) < 4:
        raise ValueError("Need at least 4 historical seasonal differences")

    drift_hat = sum(diffs) / len(diffs)
    forecast = series[test_q - 4] + drift_hat
    raw_error = series[test_q] - forecast

    # Historical forecast errors: error_tau = diffs[tau] - drift_hat
    errors = [d - drift_hat for d in diffs]
    variance = sum(e**2 for e in errors) / (len(errors) - 1)
    error_std = math.sqrt(variance)

    sue = raw_error / error_std if error_std > 0 else 0.0
    return forecast, raw_error, error_std, sue


def main() -> None:
    earnings, scales, _drifts = simulate_quarterly_earnings(SEED)

    raw_errors: list[float] = []
    sue_scores: list[float] = []
    pct_surprises: list[float] = []

    print(f"seed {SEED} firms {N_FIRMS} test_quarter {EST_QUARTERS}")
    print(f"{'Firm':4s} | {'Scale':5s} | {'Actual':6s} | {'Fore':6s} | {'RawErr':7s} | {'Std':5s} | {'SUE':6s}")
    print("-" * 55)

    for i in range(N_FIRMS):
        series = earnings[i]
        actual = series[EST_QUARTERS]
        forecast, raw_err, s_dev, sue = estimate_sue(series, EST_QUARTERS)

        raw_errors.append(raw_err)
        sue_scores.append(sue)
        # Scale-relative surprise: raw error / prior year quarter earnings
        prior_q = series[EST_QUARTERS - 4]
        pct_surprises.append(raw_err / prior_q)

        row = f"{i:4d} | {scales[i]:5.1f} | {actual:6.2f} | {forecast:6.2f}"
        row += f" | {raw_err:+7.2f} | {s_dev:5.2f} | {sue:+6.3f}"
        print(row)

    # Heteroskedasticity comparison: ratio of max variance to min variance across firms
    var_raw = sum(r**2 for r in raw_errors) / len(raw_errors)
    var_sue = sum(s**2 for s in sue_scores) / len(sue_scores)

    print("-" * 65)
    print(f"cross_sectional_variance_raw_error: {var_raw:.3f}")
    print(f"cross_sectional_variance_sue: {var_sue:.3f}")
    print(f"sue_sample_mean: {sum(sue_scores) / len(sue_scores):+.3f}")
    print(f"all_sue_finite: {all(math.isfinite(s) for s in sue_scores)}")
    print(f"sue_within_normal_range: {all(-4.0 <= s <= 4.0 for s in sue_scores)}")

4. Where the Canon Breaks

While time-series models such as seasonal random walk provide objective, replication-ready benchmarks, they break down in specific corporate environments:

  • Structural corporate breaks: Major mergers, acquisitions, restructuring, or divestitures cause discrete step-changes in the firm's earnings base, rendering pre-acquisition seasonal comparisons obsolete.
  • Consensus analyst superiority: Sell-side analyst consensus forecasts incorporate forward-looking macroeconomic data, industry channel checks, and management guidance that backward-looking time-series models cannot observe. Event studies using time-series models measure a mixture of genuine earnings news and stale analyst adjustments.
  • Strategic consensus walk-down: Sell-side analysts systematically issue optimistic forecasts early in the fiscal year and walk them down immediately prior to the announcement date, enabling firms to beat lowered consensus (the "beat-and-walk-down" game).

5. Misconceptions

  • Quarterly unexpected earnings should be calculated relative to the preceding quarter \(E_{i,t-1}\).

    Corporate sales and profits exhibit powerful annual seasonality (e.g., retailers earning over 50% of annual net income in Q4). Comparing Q4 directly to Q3 yields predictable seasonal spikes rather than unexpected operational innovations; unexpected earnings must compare against the same quarter of the prior year \(E_{i,t-4}\).

  • A firm with a higher raw dollar earnings surprise always has a higher SUE than a firm with a lower dollar surprise.

    SUE standardizes the raw dollar surprise by historical forecast error volatility \(\hat{\sigma}_i\). A $2.00 surprise in a firm with \(\hat{\sigma} = 2.00\) yields \(\mathrm{SUE} = +1.00\), whereas a $0.50 surprise in a stable firm with \(\hat{\sigma} = 0.10\) yields \(\mathrm{SUE} = +5.00\).

  • Dividing unexpected earnings by stock price \(P_{i,t-1}\) produces a scale-free measure identical to SUE.

    Deflating by price creates a return-yield metric contaminated by the firm's price-to-earnings multiple. Highly leveraged, distressed firms with low share prices exhibit artificially inflated price-deflated surprises, whereas SUE normalizes strictly by earnings volatility.

6. Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

Problem M.1. A firm's quarterly net income over quarters 0 through 7 is given by: \(E_0 = 20, E_1 = 25, E_2 = 22, E_3 = 28, E_4 = 22, E_5 = 27, E_6 = 24, E_7 = 30\). Compute the historical seasonal drift \(\hat{\delta}\) for quarters 4 through 7.

Worked Solution

Seasonal differences \(d_\tau = E_\tau - E_{\tau-4}\): \(d_4 = 22 - 20 = 2\), \(d_5 = 27 - 25 = 2\), \(d_6 = 24 - 22 = 2\), \(d_7 = 30 - 28 = 2\). Historical drift: \(\hat{\delta} = \frac{2 + 2 + 2 + 2}{4} = 2.0\).

Problem M.2. Following Problem M.1, if quarter 8 reports \(E_8 = 26\), and historical forecast error standard deviation is \(\hat{\sigma} = 0.80\), compute the seasonal random walk forecast \(\widehat{E}_8\), raw unexpected earnings \(UE_8\), and \(\mathrm{SUE}_8\).

Worked Solution

Forecast: \(\widehat{E}_8 = E_4 + \hat{\delta} = 22.0 + 2.0 = 24.0\). Raw unexpected earnings: \(UE_8 = 26.0 - 24.0 = +2.0\). Standardized unexpected earnings: \(\mathrm{SUE}_8 = \frac{2.0}{0.80} = +2.50\).

Problem M.3. A company reports unexpected earnings of +$0.60 per share. Firm A has historical forecast error volatility \(\hat{\sigma}_A = \$0.15\), while Firm B has \(\hat{\sigma}_B = \$0.40\). Compute the SUE for both firms and identify which firm experienced the larger standardized surprise.

Worked Solution

For Firm A: \(\mathrm{SUE}_A = 0.60 / 0.15 = +4.00\). For Firm B: \(\mathrm{SUE}_B = 0.60 / 0.40 = +1.50\). Firm A experienced a substantially larger standardized shock (+4.00 standard deviations versus +1.50 standard deviations).

Proof Tier

Problem P.1. Let quarterly earnings follow Foster's first-order autoregressive seasonal model: \((E_{i,t} - E_{i,t-4}) = \delta + \phi (E_{i,t-1} - E_{i,t-5}) + \varepsilon_{i,t}\) with \(|\phi| < 1\) and \(\varepsilon_{i,t} \iid (0, \sigma^2)\). Derive the unconditional variance of the seasonal differences \(\Var(E_{i,t} - E_{i,t-4})\).

Worked Solution

Define \(y_t = E_{i,t} - E_{i,t-4}\). Then \(y_t - \delta = \phi (y_{t-1} - \delta) + \varepsilon_{i,t}\), which is a standard AR(1) process with parameter \(\phi\) and white noise innovations \(\varepsilon_{i,t}\). Taking variances on both sides: \(\Var(y_t) = \phi^2 \Var(y_{t-1}) + \Var(\varepsilon_{i,t})\). Under covariance stationarity (\(|\phi| < 1\)), \(\Var(y_t) = \Var(y_{t-1}) = \gamma_0\). Hence \(\gamma_0 = \phi^2 \gamma_0 + \sigma^2 \implies \gamma_0 (1 - \phi^2) = \sigma^2 \implies \Var(E_{i,t} - E_{i,t-4}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{1 - \phi^2}\).

Problem P.2. Prove that if an analyst's consensus forecast \(\widehat{E}_t\) is an optimal conditional expectation under Mean Squared Error loss (\(\widehat{E}_t = \E[E_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\)), the resulting forecast errors \(e_t = E_t - \widehat{E}_t\) must be uncorrelated with any variable \(Z_{t-1} \in \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\).

Worked Solution

By the law of iterated expectations: \(\E[e_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \E[E_t - \E[E_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \E[E_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] - \E[E_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\). For any \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\)-measurable variable \(Z_{t-1}\): \(\E[e_t Z_{t-1}] = \E[\E[e_t Z_{t-1} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]] = \E[Z_{t-1} \E[e_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]] = \E[Z_{t-1} \times 0] = 0\). Because \(\E[e_t] = 0\), \(\Cov(e_t, Z_{t-1}) = \E[e_t Z_{t-1}] - \E[e_t]\E[Z_{t-1}] = 0\), proving orthogonality.

Problem P.3. Show that the sample variance estimator of forecast errors \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{T-5} \sum_{\tau=4}^{T-1} (UE_\tau - \overline{UE})^2\) is an unbiased estimator of innovation variance when forecast errors are independent and identically distributed.

Worked Solution

Let \(N = T-4\) be the number of seasonal difference observations. The sample mean is \(\overline{UE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{\tau=1}^N UE_\tau\). Since one degree of freedom is lost in estimating the sample mean drift \(\hat{\delta}\), the denominator is \(N - 1 = T - 5\). By standard finite-sample statistics, \(\E[\sum_{\tau=1}^N (UE_\tau - \overline{UE})^2] = (N - 1) \sigma^2 = (T - 5) \sigma^2\). Dividing by \(T - 5\) yields \(\E[\hat{\sigma}^2] = \sigma^2\), establishing unbiasedness.

Empirical Tier

Problem E.1. In a cross-sectional sample of 200 firms, the average SUE in Decile 10 is +2.45, while in Decile 1 it is -2.35. A researcher tests whether SUE is symmetric around zero. Formulate the two-tailed t-test for the null hypothesis that the mean of the pooled SUE distribution equals 0, given sample standard error \(\mathrm{se}(\overline{\mathrm{SUE}}) = 0.08\) and sample mean \(\overline{\mathrm{SUE}} = +0.05\).

Worked Solution

The null hypothesis is \(H_0: \mu_{\mathrm{SUE}} = 0\) against \(H_1: \mu_{\mathrm{SUE}} \neq 0\). The test statistic is \(t = \frac{\overline{\mathrm{SUE}} - 0}{\mathrm{se}(\overline{\mathrm{SUE}})} = \frac{0.05}{0.08} = 0.625\). At the 5% significance level with critical value \(\pm 1.96\), \(|t| = 0.625 < 1.96\). The researcher fails to reject the null hypothesis of symmetry around zero.

Problem E.2. A quantitative equity strategy sorts stocks into 10 deciles based on SUE. If the distribution of SUE follows a standard normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), what are the theoretical cutoffs for Decile 1 (bottom 10%) and Decile 10 (top 10%)? Use standard normal quantile values.

Worked Solution

For a standard normal distribution, the 10th percentile cutoff is \(\Phi^{-1}(0.10) \approx -1.282\). The 90th percentile cutoff is \(\Phi^{-1}(0.90) \approx +1.282\). Therefore, Decile 1 contains firms with \(\mathrm{SUE} \le -1.282\), and Decile 10 contains firms with \(\mathrm{SUE} \ge +1.282\).

Problem E.3. A firm's stock price drops by 50% due to an adverse legal settlement, while its operational earnings volatility remains unchanged. Compare the effect of this price collapse on the firm's price-scaled surprise \(UE / P\) versus its standardized surprise \(\mathrm{SUE} = UE / \hat{\sigma}\).

Worked Solution

For a given dollar earnings surprise \(UE\), price-scaled surprise \(UE / P\) doubles because the denominator \(P\) drops by 50% (\(UE / (0.5 P) = 2 \times UE / P\)), artificially making the firm appear to have a surprise twice as large. In contrast, SUE is deflated by operational forecast error volatility \(\hat{\sigma}\), which is unchanged, so SUE correctly reflects the true operational shock magnitude without distortion.

Research Tier

Problem R.1. Why do analyst consensus forecasts dominate seasonal time-series models in contemporaneous earnings surprise measurement, yet time-series models remain essential in quantitative finance research?

Worked Solution

Analyst forecasts incorporate rich non-accounting information (macro indicators, supply chain data, competitor results) up to the announcement minute, providing a more accurate proxy for contemporaneous market expectations. However, analyst forecasts suffer from severe behavioral biases, optimistic coverage incentives, strategic walk-downs, and survivorship/backfill issues in commercial databases. Time-series models provide transparent, strictly replicable, point-in-time benchmarks that are free from analyst agency conflicts.

Problem R.2. How does the presence of rounding and "whisper numbers" (unoffical expectations circulating among buy-side desks) affect the empirical identification of true earnings surprises when reported EPS equals consensus exactly?

Worked Solution

When reported EPS matches sell-side consensus exactly (\(UE = 0\)), stock prices frequently exhibit significant non-zero announcement returns. This occurs because buy-side "whisper numbers" often exceed public consensus, or because managers engage in small-scale accrual management to achieve a zero-error beat. Event studies relying solely on reported EPS versus consensus misclassify these events as non-events, introducing attenuation bias.

Problem R.3. Discuss the econometric consequences of winsorizing versus truncating extreme SUE values in cross-sectional asset pricing regressions.

Worked Solution

Firms with very small historical earnings volatility \(\hat{\sigma}_i \approx 0\) generate massive SUE outliers (e.g. SUE > 50). Truncating (dropping) these observations introduces sample selection bias by systematically excluding low-volatility firms. Winsorizing (capping SUE at e.g. \(\pm 4.0\)) preserves sample completeness and controls the influence of denominator outliers in OLS regressions, but introduces artificial mass at the boundaries.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. Why do time-series models of quarterly earnings compare current earnings \(E_{i,t}\) against \(E_{i,t-4}\) rather than \(E_{i,t-1}\)?

    Options

    Corporate business cycles cause pronounced seasonal swings (e.g. holiday retail in Q4). Comparing against the same quarter of the previous year controls for this recurring seasonal component.

  2. A firm reports earnings of $3.50 per share against a seasonal expectation of $3.00. If historical forecast error standard deviation is $0.20, what is the SUE? (e.g. 2.50)

    \(\mathrm{SUE} = (3.50 - 3.00) / 0.20 = 0.50 / 0.20 = 2.50\).

  3. What primary advantage does standardizing unexpected earnings by volatility \(\hat{\sigma}_i\) have over deflating by share price \(P_{i,t-1}\)?

    Options

    Deflating by price creates a valuation yield distorted by price-to-earnings multiples. Normalizing by forecast error volatility standardizes the signal across heterogeneous firms without multiple confounding.

  4. Under a seasonal random walk with drift model, how is the expected seasonal change \(\hat{\delta}_i\) estimated?

    Options

    Historical drift is estimated as the mean historical annual increment: \(\hat{\delta}_i = \frac{1}{T-4}\sum (E_{i,\tau} - E_{i,\tau-4})\).

  5. If unexpected earnings is +$0.50 and the historical standard deviation of forecast errors is $0.50, what is the SUE? (e.g. 1.00)

    \(0.50 / 0.50 = 1.00\).

  6. What does Foster's (1977) first-order autoregressive seasonal model add to the seasonal random walk?

    Options

    Foster's model includes \(\phi (E_{i,t-1} - E_{i,t-5})\), capturing the empirical fact that seasonal earnings increments are positively autocorrelated across adjacent quarters.

  7. What happens to SUE if a firm executes a 2-for-1 forward stock split that halves EPS and share price?

    Options

    By Theorem 2.1, both the numerator (raw error) and denominator (volatility) scale by 0.5, leaving SUE strictly invariant.

  8. What is the "beat-and-walk-down" phenomenon documented in analyst forecast literature?

    Options

    Analysts issue optimistic forecasts early in the year and gradually revise them downward toward manageable levels, allowing corporate management to report positive surprise beats.
