Post-earnings-announcement drift is the granddaddy of market anomalies. For more than five decades, stock prices have continued to drift in the direction of unexpected earnings for up to 60 trading days following an announcement, directly challenging semi-strong form market efficiency and providing a durable foundation for quantitative equity strategies.

1. The PEAD Phenomenon and Anomaly Definition

Under semi-strong efficient market theory (Fama 1970), stock prices must adjust instantaneously and without bias to public earnings announcements. Any abnormal return following the announcement window \([t_0, t_1]\) should equal zero in expectation: \(\E[AR_{i,\tau} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_1}] = 0\) for all \(\tau > t_1\).

However, beginning with Ball & Brown (1968) and documented rigorously across modern markets, stock prices exhibit prolonged predictability following earnings announcements.

Definition 4.1: Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift (PEAD)

Let \(\tau \in [+2, +K]\) index trading days following the release of quarterly earnings at day 0. The post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD) is the persistent cross-sectional correlation between abnormal returns in the post-announcement window and the announcement unexpected earnings surprise:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Cov\left(\mathrm{CAR}_{i}[+2, +K], \, \mathrm{SUE}_{i,0}\right) > 0 \end{aligned} \]

Equivalently, a zero-cost hedge portfolio that goes long extreme positive surprise deciles and short extreme negative surprise deciles generates statistically significant positive cumulative abnormal returns over subsequent multi-week holding horizons.

2. Bernard & Thomas (1989): Delayed Response vs Risk

In their seminal study, Bernard & Thomas (1989) conducted the definitive empirical test to determine whether PEAD represents compensation for unmeasured systematic risk or a delayed behavioral price response to accounting earnings innovations.

To distinguish these hypotheses, Bernard & Thomas examined the serial correlation structure of quarterly earnings innovations. For typical firms, seasonal forecast errors \((E_t - E_{t-4})\) exhibit a distinctive autoregressive signature with positive autocorrelation at lags 1, 2, and 3, but negative autocorrelation at lag 4:

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_1 \approx +0.34, \quad \rho_2 \approx +0.19, \quad \rho_3 \approx +0.06, \quad \rho_4 \approx -0.24 \end{aligned} \]

Bernard & Thomas hypothesized that market participants fail to comprehend this time-series structure, behaving as though quarterly earnings follow a naïve seasonal random walk without autocorrelation.

Theorem 4.1: Bernard & Thomas Naïve Expectation and Delayed Response

Suppose the true quarterly earnings process has innovation autocorrelation vector \(\boldsymbol{\rho} = (\rho_1, \rho_2, \rho_3, \rho_4)\). Let market prices be set by investors who naively assume zero autocorrelation (\(\rho_k^{\text{market}} = 0\) for all \(k \ge 1\)). Then:

  1. The stock price underreacts to the initial announcement at quarter \(t\), leaving an unimpounded fraction of value to be realized over the subsequent holding period.
  2. At subsequent announcement dates \(t+k\) (\(k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\)), the surprise perceived by naïve investors is proportional to \(\rho_k \cdot UE_t\).
  3. The expected 3-day abnormal return around subsequent announcement date \(t+k\) satisfies:
\[ \begin{aligned} \E\left[AR_{t+k}[-1, +1] \mid UE_t\right] = \lambda \cdot \rho_k \cdot UE_t \end{aligned} \]

where \(\lambda > 0\) is the earnings response coefficient. In particular, subsequent announcement abnormal returns are positively correlated with \(UE_t\) at quarters \(t+1, t+2, t+3\), and exhibit a sharp negative reversal at quarter \(t+4\) (\(\rho_4 < 0\)).

Proof of Theorem 4.1

Under naïve seasonal expectations, investors forecast quarter \(t+k\) earnings conditional on quarter \(t\) as \(\E_t^{\text{naïve}}[E_{t+k}] = E_{t+k-4}\). However, under the true autoregressive process, the conditional expectation given innovation \(e_t\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_t^{\text{true}}[E_{t+k} - E_{t+k-4}] = \rho_k \cdot e_t \end{aligned} \]

When quarter \(t+k\) arrives, the reported earnings \(E_{t+k}\) will, on average, exceed naïve expectations by:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E\left[E_{t+k} - \E_t^{\text{naïve}}[E_{t+k}] \mid e_t\right] = \rho_k \cdot e_t \end{aligned} \]

By the linear valuation relation from L03, unexpected announcements generate abnormal returns proportional to unexpected earnings: \(AR_{t+k} = \lambda \cdot [E_{t+k} - \E^{\text{market}}[E_{t+k}]] + \eta_{t+k}\). Taking expectations conditional on quarter \(t\) innovation \(e_t = UE_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \E\left[AR_{t+k}[-1, +1] \mid UE_t\right] = \lambda \cdot \rho_k \cdot UE_t \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\rho_1, \rho_2, \rho_3 > 0\) and \(\rho_4 < 0\), the sign of the announcement return at \(t+k\) is determined strictly by the sign of \(\rho_k\), proving the lagged continuation for 3 quarters and seasonal reversal at lag 4.

Example 4.1: Constructing a Decile PEAD Hedge Portfolio

Consider a cross-section of 100 firms sorted into 10 SUE deciles. Decile 10 (highest positive surprises) achieves an average 60-day post-announcement abnormal return \(\mathrm{CAR}[+2, +60] = +5.72\%\). Decile 1 (worst negative surprises) achieves \(\mathrm{CAR}[+2, +60] = -4.44\%\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gross Drift Spread} = \mathrm{CAR}_{\text{D10}} - \mathrm{CAR}_{\text{D1}} = +5.72\% - (-4.44\%) = +10.16\% \end{aligned} \]

If round-trip execution costs (bid-ask spread, commissions, market impact) are 30 bps per leg, the total two-leg transaction cost is \(2 \times 30\text{ bps} = 60\text{ bps} = 0.60\%\). The net-of-cost drift spread remains highly positive:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Net Spread} = 10.16\% - 0.60\% = +9.56\% \end{aligned} \]

Lab 1: Cross-Sectional PEAD Deciles & Lagged Seasonal Reversal

Hypothesis: Decile sorting on SUE generates a statistically significant positive post-announcement drift spread over 60 trading days, and subsequent announcement reactions exhibit positive drift at lag t+1 and seasonal reversal at lag t+4 consistent with Bernard & Thomas (1989).

"""AC3 lab L04: post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD) and delayed price response.

SIMULATED: the cross-section of 100 firms, their SUE surprises, post-announcement returns,
and subsequent announcement returns at lags t+1 to t+4 are generated under the Bernard & Thomas (1989)
autocorrelation structure. All calculations use standard library float and math.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random

SEED = 20261110
N_FIRMS = 100
N_DECILES = 10


def simulate_pead_cross_section(
    seed: int,
) -> tuple[list[float], list[float], list[list[float]]]:
    """Simulate SUE scores, 60-day post-announcement CAR, and 4 subsequent announcement reactions.

    Returns (sue_scores, car_60, subsequent_announcements).
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    sue_scores: list[float] = []
    car_60: list[float] = []
    subsequent_announcements: list[list[float]] = []

    # Bernard & Thomas (1989) typical autocorrelations:
    # rho_1 = +0.34, rho_2 = +0.19, rho_3 = +0.06, rho_4 = -0.24
    lag_weights = [0.34, 0.19, 0.06, -0.24]

    for _ in range(N_FIRMS):
        sue = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
        sue_scores.append(sue)

        # 60-day post-announcement drift CAR[+2, +60]:
        # Drift sensitivity ~ 2.5% per SUE unit, plus firm-specific noise
        drift = 0.025 * sue + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.04)
        car_60.append(drift)

        # 3-day abnormal return at subsequent announcements t+1, t+2, t+3, t+4
        sub_reactions: list[float] = []
        for rho in lag_weights:
            # Expected reaction proportional to unexpected seasonal change
            rxn = 0.015 * (rho * sue) + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.015)
            sub_reactions.append(rxn)
        subsequent_announcements.append(sub_reactions)

    return sue_scores, car_60, subsequent_announcements


def assign_deciles(scores: list[float]) -> list[int]:
    """Assign observations to 10 deciles (1..10) based on ranking.

    Ties broken by index. Decile 1 = lowest SUE, Decile 10 = highest SUE.
    """
    n = len(scores)
    indexed = sorted(range(n), key=lambda i: (scores[i], i))
    deciles = [0] * n
    for rank, idx in enumerate(indexed):
        d = min(N_DECILES, (rank * N_DECILES) // n + 1)
        deciles[idx] = d
    return deciles


def main() -> None:
    sue_scores, car_60, subsequent = simulate_pead_cross_section(SEED)
    deciles = assign_deciles(sue_scores)

    print(f"seed {SEED} firms {N_FIRMS} deciles {N_DECILES}")
    print(f"{'Decile':7s} | {'Count':5s} | {'Mean_SUE':9s} | {'Drift_CAR_60':13s} | {'t+1_Rxn':8s} | {'t+4_Rxn':8s}")
    print("-" * 65)

    decile_drift: dict[int, float] = {}
    decile_t1: dict[int, float] = {}
    decile_t4: dict[int, float] = {}

    for d in range(1, N_DECILES + 1):
        idxs = [i for i in range(N_FIRMS) if deciles[i] == d]
        count = len(idxs)
        mean_sue = sum(sue_scores[i] for i in idxs) / count
        mean_drift = sum(car_60[i] for i in idxs) / count
        mean_t1 = sum(subsequent[i][0] for i in idxs) / count
        mean_t4 = sum(subsequent[i][3] for i in idxs) / count

        decile_drift[d] = mean_drift
        decile_t1[d] = mean_t1
        decile_t4[d] = mean_t4

        print(f"{d:7d} | {count:5d} | {mean_sue:+9.3f} | {mean_drift:+13.4f} | {mean_t1:+8.4f} | {mean_t4:+8.4f}")

    gross_spread = decile_drift[10] - decile_drift[1]
    t1_spread = decile_t1[10] - decile_t1[1]
    t4_spread = decile_t4[10] - decile_t4[1]

    print("-" * 65)
    print(f"gross_drift_spread_decile_10_minus_1: {gross_spread:+.4f}")
    print(f"subsequent_t+1_announcement_spread: {t1_spread:+.4f}")
    print(f"subsequent_t+4_announcement_spread: {t4_spread:+.4f}")

    # Bernard & Thomas signature: t+1 spread has same sign as surprise, t+4 has opposite sign
    print(f"bernard_thomas_t1_positive: {t1_spread > 0}")
    print(f"bernard_thomas_t4_reversal: {t4_spread < 0}")

    # Net-of-cost drift under varying execution costs (round-trip bps)
    costs_bps = [0.0, 15.0, 30.0, 60.0]
    print("\nnet_of_cost_evaluation:")
    for cost in costs_bps:
        # Long decile 10, short decile 1 -> 2 legs * cost
        net_spread = gross_spread - 2.0 * (cost / 10000.0)
        print(f"cost_bps {cost:4.1f} : net_hedge_spread {net_spread:+.4f} | profitable {net_spread > 0}")

4. Where the Canon Breaks

While PEAD has been historically profitable in backtests, quantitative practitioners must confront several fundamental limits to arbitrage and structural market evolutions:

  • Modern anomaly decay: As shown by Patton & Weller (2020), anomaly returns across asset pricing have significantly attenuated over the post-2000 period due to increased quantitative capital, algorithmic news consumption, and cheaper retail trading.
  • Execution costs and micro-cap concentration: PEAD spreads are heavily concentrated in small- and micro-cap stocks with wide bid-ask spreads, low turnover, and elevated short borrowing fees. Once realistic transaction costs and market impact are deducted, the net exploitable alpha in large-cap equities is substantially compressed.
  • Short-sale constraints: More than half of the gross long-short PEAD spread historically derives from the short leg (shorting Decile 1). In practice, hard-to-borrow securities incur high borrow fees and recall risk during earnings season.

5. Misconceptions

  • Post-earnings-announcement drift represents compensation for systematic macroeconomic factor risk.

    Bernard & Thomas (1989) tested CAPM and multi-factor risk adjustments (Fama & French 1993) and found that risk loadings cannot explain PEAD. Most decisively, risk premiums cannot account for the sharp quarterly reversal observed at lag 4, which is unique to earnings autocorrelation.

  • PEAD begins immediately on the day of the earnings announcement and requires sub-millisecond execution to capture.

    While announcement-day reactions happen instantaneously, the post-announcement drift unfolds gradually over 30 to 60 trading days following the announcement, enabling medium-frequency quantitative strategies to harvest it without co-location.

  • Gross paper profits of PEAD strategies translate directly into realized hedge fund returns.

    PEAD requires frequent rebalancing across hundreds of small firms with high bid-ask spreads and severe short-sale borrowing constraints. Net returns after deducting round-trip transaction costs are substantially lower than gross backtested returns.

6. Problem Set

Mechanics Tier

Problem M.1. A PEAD quantitative strategy goes long Decile 10 (mean 60-day abnormal return \(+4.80\%\)) and short Decile 1 (mean 60-day abnormal return \(-3.20\%\)). Compute the gross long-short hedge portfolio return.

Worked Solution

The long-short hedge portfolio return is \(\mathrm{Return}_{\text{hedge}} = \mathrm{CAR}_{\text{long}} - \mathrm{CAR}_{\text{short}} = +4.80\% - (-3.20\%) = +8.00\%\).

Problem M.2. Following Problem M.1, suppose one-way transaction costs are 25 basis points for buying/selling the long leg and 35 basis points for shorting/covering the short leg. Compute the net hedge portfolio return.

Worked Solution

Total round-trip trading cost across both legs is \(2 \times 0.25\% + 2 \times 0.35\% = 0.50\% + 0.70\% = 1.20\%\). The net return is \(8.00\% - 1.20\% = +6.80\%\).

Problem M.3. Under the Bernard & Thomas (1989) autocorrelation vector \(\rho_1 = 0.34\), \(\rho_2 = 0.19\), \(\rho_3 = 0.06\), \(\rho_4 = -0.24\), an unexpected earnings innovation of \(UE_0 = +1.00\) occurs. What is the expected earnings surprise perceived by naïve investors at quarter 4?

Worked Solution

Under Theorem 4.1, the expected surprise at quarter 4 is \(\E[UE_4 \mid UE_0] = \rho_4 \cdot UE_0 = -0.24 \times (+1.00) = -0.24\). Investors will be surprised negatively despite the positive surprise 4 quarters earlier.

Proof Tier

Problem P.1. Prove that under the naïve seasonal random walk model (\(\E_t^{\text{naïve}}[E_{t+k}] = E_{t+k-4}\)), a market with rational investors who fully anticipate \(\rho_k\) can form an arbitrage strategy that earns non-zero abnormal returns at date \(t+k\) if prices adjust only at announcement dates.

Worked Solution

At date \(t\), the rational investor's expectation is \(\E_t[E_{t+k}] = E_{t+k-4} + \rho_k UE_t\), while the naïve market price reflects \(\E_t^{\text{naïve}}[E_{t+k}] = E_{t+k-4}\). If the market does not revise prices until date \(t+k\), the expected price revision at date \(t+k\) is \(\E[P_{t+k} - P_{t+k-1} \mid UE_t] = \lambda [E_{t+k} - E_{t+k-4}] = \lambda \rho_k UE_t \neq 0\) whenever \(\rho_k \neq 0\). The rational investor goes long when \(\rho_k UE_t > 0\) and short when \(\rho_k UE_t < 0\), earning an expected abnormal profit of \(\lambda |\rho_k UE_t| > 0\).

Problem P.2. Show that if abnormal returns follow an AR(1) process \(AR_t = \phi AR_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) with \(0 < \phi < 1\), the cumulative abnormal return over \(K\) days \(\mathrm{CAR}[1, K] = \sum_{t=1}^K AR_t\) has expected value \(\E[\mathrm{CAR}[1, K] \mid AR_0] = AR_0 \frac{\phi(1 - \phi^K)}{1 - \phi}\).

Worked Solution

For each day \(t \ge 1\), \(\E[AR_t \mid AR_0] = \phi^t AR_0\). Summing over \(t=1\) to \(K\): \(\E[\mathrm{CAR}[1, K] \mid AR_0] = \sum_{t=1}^K \phi^t AR_0 = AR_0 \phi \sum_{j=0}^{K-1} \phi^j\). Using the finite geometric series formula \(\sum_{j=0}^{K-1} \phi^j = \frac{1 - \phi^K}{1 - \phi}\), we obtain \(\E[\mathrm{CAR}[1, K] \mid AR_0] = AR_0 \frac{\phi(1 - \phi^K)}{1 - \phi}\).

Problem P.3. Prove that if PEAD were entirely driven by constant risk premium \(\mu > 0\), the expected abnormal return at subsequent announcement dates \(t+k\) must be positive for all quarters \(k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\), contradicting the empirical reversal observed at quarter 4.

Worked Solution

If PEAD represents compensation for holding systematic risk, then firms in Decile 10 have higher systematic factor exposure than Decile 1, requiring an equilibrium risk premium \(\E[R_{10} - R_1] = \beta_{\text{spread}} \lambda_{\text{risk}} > 0\) per unit of time. Under any standard positive risk premium hypothesis, this excess return must be non-negative in all calendar periods, implying \(\E[AR_{t+k}] \ge 0\) for all \(k\). The empirical finding that \(\E[AR_{t+4}] < 0\) requires the risk premium to turn negative exactly at 52-week intervals, which is economically absurd and proves the delayed price response hypothesis.

Empirical Tier

Problem E.1. In a sample of 500 quarterly announcements, the average 60-day post-announcement abnormal return for SUE Decile 10 is \(+3.50\%\) (\(\mathrm{se} = 0.40\%\)) and for Decile 1 is \(-2.10\%\) (\(\mathrm{se} = 0.35\%\)). Perform a two-sample t-test for the difference in means and assess significance at the 1% level.

Worked Solution

Spread: \(\Delta = 3.50\% - (-2.10\%) = 5.60\%\). Standard error of the difference: \(\mathrm{se}(\Delta) = \sqrt{0.40^2 + 0.35^2} = \sqrt{0.16 + 0.1225} = \sqrt{0.2825} \approx 0.5315\%\). The t-statistic is \(t = \frac{5.60}{0.5315} \approx 10.54\). Because \(t = 10.54 > 2.58\) (critical value at 1%), the drift spread is statistically significant at the 1% significance level (\(p < 10^{-15}\)).

Problem E.2. A researcher estimates PEAD drift across two subsamples: 1985–1999 (pre-Reg FD / algorithmic era) and 2005–2019 (modern electronic trading era). The gross drift spread drops from \(8.4\%\) to \(2.8\%\). What percentage of the historical anomaly spread has decayed over time?

Worked Solution

Decay magnitude: \(8.4\% - 2.8\% = 5.6\%\). Decay percentage: \(\frac{5.6\%}{8.4\%} \times 100\% \approx 66.67\%\). Approximately two-thirds of the gross historical PEAD premium has decayed in the modern era, consistent with Patton & Weller (2020).

Problem E.3. Explain why institutional ownership percentage is negatively correlated with the magnitude of post-earnings-announcement drift across cross-sectional stocks.

Worked Solution

Institutional investors possess sophisticated quantitative valuation models, lower transaction cost structures, and real-time execution algorithms that rapidly trade against post-announcement underreactions. In stocks with high institutional ownership, arbitrage capital impounds unexpected earnings into prices faster, shrinking the post-announcement drift window. Conversely, stocks dominated by retail investors exhibit severe cognitive underreaction and prolonged drift.

Research Tier

Problem R.1. How do limits to arbitrage—specifically idiosyncratic volatility, market capitalization, and short borrow fees—prevent quantitative arbitrageurs from entirely eliminating PEAD?

Worked Solution

Exploiting PEAD requires taking leveraged long and short positions in individual equities exposed to idiosyncratic volatility risk, which cannot be perfectly hedged. In small-cap stocks where drift is largest, short borrow fees can exceed several percent annually, borrow availability is scarce, and bid-ask spreads impose heavy execution friction. Arbitrageurs only trade until the marginal net alpha equals the marginal cost of capital and risk, leaving an equilibrium residual drift.

Problem R.2. Discuss the publication decay hypothesis (Patton & Weller 2020) in the context of academic disclosure of market anomalies. Why does PEAD exhibit greater resilience than most cross-sectional anomalies?

Worked Solution

Academic publication publicizes trading rules, attracting systematic hedge fund capital that trades away mispricing (publication decay). However, PEAD has exhibited greater persistence than pure price-momentum or size anomalies because earnings announcements occur in discrete quarterly clusters with high fundamental information asymmetry, and because a large portion of the anomaly resides on the short side where borrow constraints permanently restrict capital deployment.

Problem R.3. How does the choice of benchmark model (market model vs Fama-French 3-factor vs Carhart 4-factor) affect the measured abnormal drift in PEAD portfolios?

Worked Solution

Controlling for size (SMB) and value (HML) factors has little impact on PEAD abnormal returns because earnings surprises are largely orthogonal to book-to-market. However, controlling for the Carhart momentum factor (UMD / MOM) absorbs a substantial portion of measured post-announcement drift (often 30% to 50%) because prior earnings winners naturally enter the price momentum winner portfolio, demonstrating that price momentum and earnings momentum are economically interconnected.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What empirical finding in Bernard & Thomas (1989) definitively refutes the hypothesis that PEAD is a compensation for systematic risk?

    Options

    A rational risk premium must be non-negative. An abnormal return that predictably turns negative at quarter t+4 can only be explained by the seasonal autocorrelation structure of earnings under naïve expectations.

  2. A PEAD long-short hedge portfolio yields a gross 60-day spread of 8.50%. If total two-leg round-trip transaction costs are 1.00%, what is the net hedge return in percent? (e.g. 7.50)

    Net return = Gross return - round-trip costs = 8.50% - 1.00% = 7.50%.

  3. According to Bernard & Thomas (1989), what are the approximate autocorrelations of quarterly seasonal earnings forecast errors at lags 1 and 4?

    Options

    Quarterly seasonal forecast errors exhibit positive serial correlation at lags 1–3 (\(\rho_1 \approx 0.34\)) and negative correlation at lag 4 (\(\rho_4 \approx -0.24\)).

  4. Why does high institutional ownership attenuate the post-earnings-announcement drift?

    Options

    Institutional investors deploy capital and execution algorithms that rapidly eliminate mispricing, narrowing the drift window.

  5. What structural shift in asset pricing anomalies did Patton & Weller (2020) document?

    Options

    Patton & Weller (2020) showed that post-2000 returns to published anomalies have substantially compressed due to hedge fund arbitrage and technological advances.

  6. In a standard decile sort, into how many equal-sized rank groups is the cross-section partitioned? (e.g. 10.00)

    A decile sort partitions the cross-section into exactly 10 equal groups.

  7. Which side of the PEAD hedge portfolio typically encounters the greatest limits to arbitrage in practical execution?

    Options

    Shorting bad-news firms (Decile 1) is subject to hard borrowing constraints, high borrow fees, and recall risk, limiting arbitrage capital.

  8. Why does controlling for price momentum (Carhart UMD) significantly reduce the measured alpha of a PEAD strategy?

    Options

    Stocks with large positive earnings surprises experience upward price revisions that mechanically place them into the high-momentum winner portfolio.
