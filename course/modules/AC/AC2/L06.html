Earnings Management and Discretionary Accruals

  • Uses: operating accruals, earnings and cash-flow quality, the accruals anomaly, accrual accounting, revenue recognition, depreciation and amortization, outlier detection
  • Introduces: earnings management, discretionary accruals (modified Jones model)
  • Ttheorem

  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore

Managers have discretion over estimates such as receivables, depreciation and reserves, and over the timing of revenue. Accrual-based tests try to find the part of accruals that discretion explains, after removing the part that normal growth explains. The modified Jones model is the standard version of that split, and it is also easy to misuse. This lesson sets out the model as the A15 contract fits it, proves what its no-intercept regression does to the residuals, and shows in a SIMULATED cross-section with a planted manipulation how well the residuals recover it. The limits are the lesson's main result, and they are stated with the numbers.

1What earnings management is

Definition 1.1 (earnings management)

Earnings management is the use of managerial discretion over accounting estimates, classifications and the timing of recognition to change the reported earnings of a period. Discretion within the accounting rules is the usual case. Outright misstatement is a separate and more serious category that accrual tests also target but do not establish.

The A15 contract attributes the modified form this lesson uses to Dechow et al. (1995). The paper compares accrual-based models for detecting earnings management. This build has not read the paper's tables, so no detection rate from it is quoted. The results that follow come from the lab, and the lab is SIMULATED.

Definition 1.2 (nondiscretionary and discretionary accruals)

Scaled total accruals are split into two parts: \(TA_i/A_{i,t-1} = NDA_i + DA_i\), where \(A_{i,t-1}\) is lagged total assets, \(NDA_i\) is the part that normal growth and the asset mix explain, and \(DA_i\) is the remainder. The remainder is the discretionary accrual. It is the quantity an accrual test reads as evidence of management.

2The modified Jones model

Definition 2.1 (discretionary accruals, modified Jones model)

Discretionary accruals (modified Jones model) are the residuals of the regression \[ \frac{TA_i}{A_{i,t-1}} = a_1\frac{1}{A_{i,t-1}} + a_2\frac{\Delta REV_i - \Delta REC_i}{A_{i,t-1}} + a_3\frac{PPE_i}{A_{i,t-1}} + \eta_i, \] estimated by ordinary least squares without an intercept. Here \(TA_i\) is total accruals, \(\Delta REV_i\) and \(\Delta REC_i\) are the changes in revenue and in receivables, and \(PPE_i\) is net PP&E, all in CU. The fitted values \(\widehat{NDA}_i\) are the nondiscretionary accruals, and \(\widehat{DA}_i = TA_i/A_{i,t-1} - \widehat{NDA}_i\) are the discretionary accruals.

The model is Jones's regression with one change. Jones's regression uses the change in revenue. The modified version subtracts the change in receivables from it, so that revenue recognised as receivables is treated as the part of revenue growth that is most open to discretion. Dechow et al. (1995) motivate the adjustment on this ground. The A15 contract fits the same three regressors, so the lesson uses the contract's definition exactly. This build has not confirmed whether the published regression includes an intercept, and Section 4 shows why that choice matters.

3What the no-intercept regression does to its residuals

Proposition 3.1 (normal equations)

Let \(X\) be the \(n \times 3\) design with columns \(1/A\), \((\Delta REV - \Delta REC)/A\) and \(PPE/A\), let \(y = TA/A\), and let \(b\) be the least-squares coefficient vector with \(e = y - Xb\). Then \(X^\top e = 0\). The residuals sum to zero if and only if the column of ones lies in the column space of \(X\).

Proof

The least-squares solution satisfies the normal equations \(X^\top(y - Xb) = 0\), which is \(X^\top e = 0\). The sum of the residuals is \(\mathbf{1}^\top e\). It is zero for every \(y\) if and only if \(\mathbf{1}\) is orthogonal to the residual space, which holds exactly when \(\mathbf{1}\) is in the column space of \(X\). Without an intercept, \(\mathbf{1}\) is not a column of \(X\), so the sum is not forced to zero.

Remark 3.2 (a two-firm counterexample)

Take two firms with \(A = 1\) and \(A = 2\), and a single regressor \(1/A\), so that \(X = (1, 0.5)^\top\). Let \(y = (1, 1)^\top\). Then \(b = (x^\top y)/(x^\top x) = 1.5/1.25 = 1.2\), the fitted values are \((1.2, 0.6)\), and the residuals are \((-0.2, 0.4)\). They sum to \(0.2\), not zero. The discretionary accruals of this sample do not average to zero.

Proposition 3.3 (the estimator carries the planted term)

Suppose the dependent variable is \(y = Xb^\ast + d + u\), where \(d\) is a vector of discretionary terms and \(u\) is a zero-mean error independent of \(X\). Then the least-squares estimator satisfies \(\hat b - b^\ast = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top d + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top u\). Its mean error is \((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top d\), which is the least-squares projection of \(d\) onto the regressors.

Proof

Substitute \(y = Xb^\ast + d + u\) into \(\hat b = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\). This gives \(\hat b = b^\ast + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top d + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top u\). Taking the conditional mean given \(X\), the last term vanishes because \(u\) has zero mean and is independent of \(X\). The mean error is the first term, which is the projection of \(d\) onto the column space of \(X\).

Proposition 3.3 is the reason the estimation sample matters. If the discretionary term \(d\) is not orthogonal to the regressors, the estimate of \(b\) absorbs part of it, and the residuals then understate it. The size of the projection depends on how the planted term co-moves with the regressors. The regressor \(1/A\) is largest for the smallest firms, so a planted term concentrated among small firms projects onto it most strongly. Lesson L06 does not claim that every real sample has this problem. It identifies the condition under which the problem arises.

The units of the coefficients follow from Definition 2.1. Because \(TA/A\) is a ratio and \(1/A\) carries units of \(1/\text{CU}\), the coefficient \(a_1\) is measured in CU. A coefficient such as 0.05 is 0.05 CU, and its size depends on the currency unit of the filer. The other two coefficients are unitless, since \((\Delta REV - \Delta REC)/A\) and \(PPE/A\) are ratios of CU to CU. Under a common scaling by \(k\), \(a_1\) is multiplied by \(k\), while \(a_2\), \(a_3\) and the residuals are unchanged. This is the wording of the A15 contract's Scale row.

Lab 1 · Recovering a planted manipulation with the modified Jones regression

The lab generates 2,000 SIMULATED firm-years. Ten per cent are manipulators, and each manipulator has a planted discretionary accrual of 0.05 times lagged assets. Non-manipulators have a discretionary accrual with standard deviation 0.01. Stated before running: the regression estimated on the non-manipulators (an oracle, not available in practice) should recover the planted \(a_2\) and \(a_3\) to within about two classical standard errors. The coefficient \(a_1\) on \(1/A\) is weakly identified, so its standard error should be large relative to its planted value. Estimated on all firms, the coefficients should shift, by Proposition 3.3. Flagging the top 10% of discretionary accruals should separate manipulators well when the planted shift is 0.05, and poorly when it is 0.01.

"""AC2 L06 lab 1: the modified Jones model on a SIMULATED cross-section with a planted manipulation.

SIMULATED: every firm-year is generated here from the seeded model below. No filing is read.
The manipulation is planted by the author: a known share of firms receives a positive discretionary
accrual of SHIFT times lagged assets. The lab asks whether the regression recovers it, and reports
the limits of that recovery. A flagged firm in this lab is a firm the generator made manipulate.
In real data, a large discretionary accrual is evidence of a candidate, not of intent.

Model, per firm i (all money in currency units, CU; ratios are per unit of lagged assets):
    lag_i     lagged total assets, lognormal around 1000 CU
    drev_i    change in revenue, drec_i change in receivables
    ppe_i     net PP&E
    total_i   total accruals = C1 / lag + C2 * (drev - drec) / lag + C3 * ppe / lag + DA_i
    DA_i      = SHIFT * manipulator_i + NOISE_SD * v_i,  v_i iid N(0, 1)

The estimator is the A15 contract's modified_jones: regress total/lag on
[1/lag, (drev - drec)/lag, ppe/lag] WITHOUT an intercept, by least squares (numpy lstsq, rcond=None).
Its fitted values are the nondiscretionary accruals and its residuals the discretionary accruals.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

F64 = NDArray[np.float64]

N_FIRMS = 2000
MANIP_SHARE = 0.10  # share of firm-years that manipulate, planted
C_TRUE = (0.05, 0.10, -0.08)  # planted coefficients of the nondiscretionary part, on 1/lag, (drev-drec)/lag, ppe/lag
NOISE_SD = 0.01  # normal discretionary variation, ratio to lagged assets
SEED = 606


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Sample:
    """A cross-section of firm-years: money in CU, manipulator flags are the planted truth."""

    total: F64
    lag: F64
    drev: F64
    drec: F64
    ppe: F64
    manipulator: NDArray[np.bool_]


def make_sample(seed: int, shift: float, n: int = N_FIRMS) -> Sample:
    """Generate n firm-years; shift is the planted discretionary accrual of a manipulator, in ratio units."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    lag = np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(1000.0), 0.6, n))
    drev = lag * rng.normal(0.10, 0.20, n)
    drec = drev * rng.uniform(0.10, 0.30, n) + lag * rng.normal(0.0, 0.02, n)
    ppe = lag * rng.uniform(0.20, 0.60, n)
    manipulator = np.zeros(n, dtype=np.bool_)
    manipulator[rng.permutation(n)[: round(MANIP_SHARE * n)]] = True
    noise = rng.standard_normal(n)
    da = shift * manipulator + NOISE_SD * noise
    nondiscretionary = C_TRUE[0] / lag + C_TRUE[1] * (drev - drec) / lag + C_TRUE[2] * ppe / lag
    total = lag * (nondiscretionary + da)
    return Sample(total=total, lag=lag, drev=drev, drec=drec, ppe=ppe, manipulator=manipulator)


def design(s: Sample) -> tuple[F64, F64]:
    """Regressors [1/lag, (drev - drec)/lag, ppe/lag] and the dependent variable total/lag, one row per firm-year."""
    x = np.column_stack([1.0 / s.lag, (s.drev - s.drec) / s.lag, s.ppe / s.lag])
    return x, s.total / s.lag


def modified_jones(s: Sample, mask: NDArray[np.bool_] | None = None) -> tuple[tuple[float, float, float], F64, F64]:
    """A15 contract's modified_jones on the rows selected by mask (all rows if None).

    Returns the coefficients (a1, a2, a3), the fitted nondiscretionary accruals and the discretionary accruals
    (total/lag minus fitted) for every row of the sample, all in ratio units of lagged assets.
    """
    keep = np.ones(s.lag.size, dtype=np.bool_) if mask is None else mask
    x, y = design(s)
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(x[keep], y[keep], rcond=None)
    fitted = x @ coef
    return (float(coef[0]), float(coef[1]), float(coef[2])), fitted, y - fitted


def classical_se(s: Sample, mask: NDArray[np.bool_]) -> F64:
    """Classical OLS standard errors of (a1, a2, a3) on the rows in mask, with iid errors assumed."""
    x, y = design(s)
    xk, yk = x[mask], y[mask]
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(xk, yk, rcond=None)
    resid = yk - xk @ coef
    s2 = float(resid @ resid) / (yk.size - xk.shape[1])
    return np.sqrt(np.diag(s2 * np.linalg.inv(xk.T @ xk)))


def report(label: str, s: Sample) -> None:
    """Estimate on all firm-years (the practical case) and on the non-manipulators (an oracle, not available)."""
    (a1, a2, a3), _, da = modified_jones(s)
    print(f"{label} coefficients_all_firms {a1:.4f} {a2:.4f} {a3:.4f}")
    (o1, o2, o3), _, da_oracle = modified_jones(s, ~s.manipulator)
    print(f"{label} coefficients_non_manipulators {o1:.4f} {o2:.4f} {o3:.4f}")
    se = classical_se(s, ~s.manipulator)
    print(f"{label} se_non_manipulators {se[0]:.4f} {se[1]:.4f} {se[2]:.4f}")
    print(f"{label} planted_coefficients {C_TRUE[0]:.4f} {C_TRUE[1]:.4f} {C_TRUE[2]:.4f}")

    m = s.manipulator
    print(f"{label} mean_da_manipulators {float(da[m].mean()):.4f} mean_da_others {float(da[~m].mean()):.4f}")

    # Flag the 10% of firm-years with the largest discretionary accruals, the same share as the planted one.
    cut = float(np.quantile(da, 1.0 - MANIP_SHARE))
    flagged = da >= cut
    tpr = float(np.mean(flagged[m]))
    fpr = float(np.mean(flagged[~m]))
    print(f"{label} all_firms_top10pct true_positive_rate {tpr:.4f} false_positive_rate {fpr:.4f}")

    cut_o = float(np.quantile(da_oracle, 1.0 - MANIP_SHARE))
    flagged_o = da_oracle >= cut_o
    print(
        f"{label} oracle_top10pct true_positive_rate {float(np.mean(flagged_o[m])):.4f} "
        f"false_positive_rate {float(np.mean(flagged_o[~m])):.4f}"
    )
    print(f"{label} flags_differing_all_vs_oracle {int(np.sum(flagged != flagged_o))}")


SCALE_SEEDS = (606, 607, 608, 609, 610)


def se_scaling() -> None:
    """Mean classical standard error of a1 on the non-manipulators, over SCALE_SEEDS, at two sample sizes."""
    for n in (2000, 64000):
        ses: list[float] = []
        for seed in SCALE_SEEDS:
            sample = make_sample(seed, 0.05, n)
            ses.append(classical_se(sample, ~sample.manipulator)[0])
        print(f"a1_se_mean firms {n} seeds {len(SCALE_SEEDS)} {float(np.mean(ses)):.4f}")


def main() -> None:
    print(f"seed {SEED} firms {N_FIRMS} manipulator_share {MANIP_SHARE}")
    report("strong_shift_0.05", make_sample(SEED, 0.05))
    report("weak_shift_0.01", make_sample(SEED, 0.01))
    se_scaling()

The difference is 0.2085, which is 0.74 of the classical standard error 0.2824 for \(a_1\). That is the contamination of Proposition 3.3 in the sample. 4Estimation choices that the lab exposes

Three choices in the fit decide what the residuals mean, and each is visible in Lab 1. The first is the intercept. Remark 3.2 shows that without one the discretionary accruals need not average to zero, so a positive mean among manipulators can appear as a shift in the coefficients rather than in the residuals. The second is the estimation sample. Proposition 3.3 shows that including manipulators in the estimation carries the planted term into \(\hat b\). The third is the scale of the regressors. The coefficient on \(1/A\) is measured in CU and its regressor is small, so its standard error is large. In Lab 1 the classical standard error of \(a_1\) is 0.2824, which is 5.6 times the planted value of 0.05 (\(0.2824/0.05 = 5.6\)). The estimate of \(a_1\) is therefore too imprecise to recover the planted value, whatever the intercept or the sample.

5Where the canon breaks

The modified Jones model is a model of normal accruals. The residual is a measurement of discretion only if the model is correct about normal accruals, and the lesson's lab removes that doubt by construction. Real data remove it, and the following limits apply.

  • The residual is not a test of intent. A large discretionary accrual can come from a growing firm whose normal model is misspecified, from an estimate that was honest but wrong, or from deliberate choices. The residual measures a component, and the component is not identified as manipulation. Dechow et al. (1995) evaluate the accrual models' ability to detect earnings management; this build has not confirmed their design or results, so none is quoted.
  • The receivables adjustment assumes the manipulation runs through revenue. The adjustment subtracts receivables growth from revenue growth, which is a sensible channel only if the manipulation is in revenue. In Lab 1 the planted manipulation enters the discretionary term directly, so the adjustment does nothing to it. A manipulation in another channel, such as reserves or capitalised costs, would not be captured by the adjustment at all.
  • Accrual tests see only accruals. The model of Definition 2.1 has no term for management that works through real activity, such as the timing of discretionary spending or of production. An accrual test is therefore blind to such management by construction. This build holds no registry source that measures that practice, so the lesson makes no claim about its size, and the source request is listed under REFERENCE_REQUESTS in the unit report.
  • Grouping changes the model. Published applications of this model are commonly estimated within groups defined by industry and year, so that industry-wide accrual patterns are absorbed; this build has not confirmed that design from the paper. The contract estimates one cross-section, and Lab 1 has one cross-section, so the lab cannot show the effect of grouping.

6Misconceptions

  1. “A large discretionary accrual proves earnings management.”

    The residual is a component of accruals that the model does not explain (Definition 2.1). It is a candidate, and its size is evidence of unexplained accrual, not of intent. In Lab 1 the top-10% flag identifies 197 manipulators in the strong scenario, which is a clean result only because the generator guarantees that the manipulation enters the residual. In the weak scenario the same rule flags 129 non-manipulators as well as 71 manipulators.

  2. “When manipulation is rare, estimating on all firms is harmless.”

    Proposition 3.3 gives the estimation error as the projection of the planted term onto the regressors. Manipulation at 10% share is not rare enough to make that projection small. In Lab 1 the coefficient on \(1/A\) moves from 0.1806 to 0.3891 when manipulators are included, a shift of 0.2085 that is 0.74 of its classical standard error. The rarity of the manipulation does not bound the projection, which depends on the regressors.

  3. “A residual test that flags the top decile identifies the manipulators.”

    Flagging the top 10% gives a precision equal to the true-positive share among flagged firms. In the weak scenario that precision is 71 of 200, or 0.355 (E1), so most flagged firms are not manipulators. The rule identifies the manipulators only when the planted shift is large relative to the noise, which Lab 1 shows in the strong scenario (E2).

Mechanics

M1 · Discretionary accrual by hand

Coefficients are \(a_1 = 0.05\), \(a_2 = 0.10\) and \(a_3 = -0.08\), all with the units of Definition 2.1. A firm has \(A = 1000\) CU, \(\Delta REV = 120\), \(\Delta REC = 30\), \(PPE = 400\), and total accruals \(TA = -30\) CU. Compute \(\widehat{NDA}\), \(\widehat{DA}\), and say what the sign means.

Solution

The nondiscretionary ratio is \(0.05/1000 + 0.10 \times (120 - 30)/1000 - 0.08 \times 400/1000 = 0.00005 + 0.009 - 0.032 = -0.02295\). The scaled total accrual is \(-30/1000 = -0.03\). So \(\widehat{DA} = -0.03 - (-0.02295) = -0.00705\). The discretionary accrual is negative: the firm's accruals are more negative than the model's normal level for a firm of this size and mix. A negative residual is not evidence of income-decreasing manipulation on its own, for the reasons of Section 6.

M2 · Detection under the planted shift

Suppose a manipulator's discretionary accrual is \(N(0.05, 0.01^2)\) and a non-manipulator's is \(N(0, 0.01^2)\). A rule flags a firm if its accrual exceeds a threshold \(c\) that gives a 5% false-positive rate. Find \(c\) and the detection rate.

Solution

A 5% one-sided false-positive rate needs \(c = 0.01 \times 1.6449 = 0.01645\). The detection rate is \(P(N(0.05, 0.01^2) \gt c) = \Phi((0.05 - 0.01645)/0.01) = \Phi(3.355) \approx 0.9996\). The planted shift is five standard deviations of the noise, so under these assumed distributions the rule almost never misses. The lab's strong-scenario rate of 0.985 is lower than this figure because the lab applies a top-decile rule to estimated residuals, not a 5% threshold on known distributions. The gap is a reminder that the distributions are not known in practice.

M3 · The contamination in standard-error units

From Lab 1, the all-firm and non-manipulator estimates of \(a_1\) are 0.3891 and 0.1806, and the classical standard error of the non-manipulator estimate is 0.2824. Express the contamination in standard-error units and say whether it is large.

Solution

The difference is \(0.3891 - 0.1806 = 0.2085\). In standard-error units it is \(0.2085/0.2824 = 0.738\). The contamination is about three quarters of one standard error. It is not large by the usual test standard, but it is comparable to the estimate's own sampling error, and it moves the estimate in the direction that Proposition 3.3 predicts. Whether it matters depends on the question. In Lab 1's strong scenario the top-decile flags are the same under both estimates. The lab prints flags_differing_all_vs_oracle 12 for the weak scenario, so 12 of the 200 flags change between the two estimates, while the printed true-positive and false-positive rates are the same under both.

Proof

P1 · Residuals need not sum to zero without an intercept

Using Proposition 3.1, prove that the residuals sum to zero when the design has a column of ones, and show that the two-firm example of Remark 3.2 has no column of ones.

Solution

If \(\mathbf{1}\) is a column of \(X\), then \(\mathbf{1}^\top e = 0\) by the normal equations \(X^\top e = 0\), so the residuals sum to zero. In Remark 3.2 the only column is \((1, 0.5)^\top\), which is the values of \(1/A\) for the two firms, and it is not constant, so \(\mathbf{1}\) is not in the column space. The residuals \((-0.2, 0.4)\) sum to 0.2, as the proposition requires. The example is the smallest case in which the condition fails.

P2 · Discretionary accruals are scale invariant

Show that replacing each money item of every firm by \(k\) times its value, for a constant \(k \gt 0\), leaves \(\widehat{DA}_i\) unchanged, though \(a_1\) is multiplied by \(k\).

Solution

Under scaling, \(TA/A\) is unchanged, \((\Delta REV - \Delta REC)/A\) is unchanged, and \(PPE/A\) is unchanged, because each is a ratio of money items. The regressor \(1/A\) becomes \(1/(kA) = (1/k)(1/A)\). A least-squares fit with a regressor rescaled by \(1/k\) has its coefficient rescaled by \(k\), so \(\hat a_1\) becomes \(k\hat a_1\), and the fitted values \(X\hat b\) are unchanged. Hence \(\widehat{NDA}_i\) and \(\widehat{DA}_i = TA_i/A_i - \widehat{NDA}_i\) are unchanged. The coefficients \(a_2\) and \(a_3\) are also invariant, because their regressors are ratios of money items and \(y\) is unchanged. Only \(a_1\) scales with \(k\), because it multiplies \(1/A\) and carries CU, as Section 3 states. This is the wording of the A15 contract's Scale row: residuals, \(a_2\) and \(a_3\) are invariant, and \(a_1\) scales.

P3 · The projection is the bias

Prove Proposition 3.3 without assuming \(u\) is independent of \(X\), by assuming only \(\E[u \mid X] = 0\). Then say what the proposition implies when \(d\) is orthogonal to every column of \(X\).

Solution

Taking conditional expectations of \(\hat b - b^\ast = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top d + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top u\) given \(X\) requires only \(\E[u \mid X] = 0\), since \(X\) is conditioned on and \((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top d\) is a function of \(X\) and \(d\). The result is \(\E[\hat b \mid X] - b^\ast = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top d\), which holds if \(d\) is a function of \(X\) and the error has conditional mean zero. If \(d\) is orthogonal to every column of \(X\), then \(X^\top d = 0\), the bias is zero, and the discretionary term is absorbed only in the residuals. That is the case in which the planted term does not contaminate the coefficients, and it is not the case of Lab 1.

Empirical

E1 · Precision of the flags in the weak scenario

In the weak scenario of Lab 1, the top-decile rule flags 200 firm-years of 2,000, and 71 of the flagged firms are manipulators. What is the precision of the flag, and how does it compare with the 10% base rate of manipulators?

Solution

Precision is \(71/200 = 0.355\). The base rate of manipulators is 10%. The flags therefore raise the share of manipulators among flagged firms from 10% to 35.5%, a factor of 3.55. The rule is better than chance, but most flagged firms are not manipulators. The false-positive rate is 0.0717, which means 129 of the 1,800 non-manipulators are flagged, and \(129/200 = 0.645\) of the flags are false.

E2 · Precision in the strong scenario

In the strong scenario of Lab 1, 197 of the 200 flagged firm-years are manipulators. Compute the precision, the false-positive count, and say which of these two numbers depends most on the planted shift.

Solution

Precision is \(197/200 = 0.985\). The false-positive rate is 0.0017, which is \(0.0017 \times 1800 \approx 3\) non-manipulators flagged, matching the 3 flags that are not manipulators. Both numbers depend on the planted shift in this generator, and neither is a property of real filers.

E3 · How far the planted \(a_2\) is

In the strong scenario, the non-manipulator estimate of \(a_2\) is 0.1031 against a planted 0.10, and its classical standard error is 0.0015. Express the difference in standard-error units. Is one seed enough to judge the regression's recovery?

Solution

The difference is \(0.1031 - 0.10 = 0.0031\), which is \(0.0031/0.0015 = 2.07\) standard errors. Under correct specification and iid errors, a deviation of about two standard errors occurs in roughly 4% of draws, so this is an unusual but not impossible draw. A single seed cannot settle whether the estimator recovers \(a_2\) without bias. The judgement needs many seeds, which is R1's design and not a result of this lesson.

Research

R1 · How much data the coefficient on 1/A needs

In Lab 1 the classical standard error of \(a_1\) is about 0.28 at 2,000 firm-years. Using the same generator with more firm-years, find the sample size at which the mean standard error over five seeds falls to about the planted value 0.05.

Solution

The twin's se_scaling function, whose output the lab prints, computes the classical standard error of \(a_1\) on the non-manipulators over the five seeds 606 to 610. Its printed means are 0.2781 at 2,000 firm-years and 0.0489 at 64,000 firm-years, each a mean of five replications. The square-root rule from the 2,000 result gives \(0.2781\sqrt{2000/64000} = 0.0492\), close to the printed 0.0489. Setting that rule equal to 0.05 gives \(n = 2000 \times (0.2781/0.05)^2 \approx 61{,}900\), so the printed 64,000 result is of the order the rule predicts. The answer is a property of this generator, whose 1/A regressor has the scale it has. A filer sample with a different size distribution needs a different number. The point of the exercise is that the coefficient on \(1/A\) is data-hungry, and a result that depends on it needs a sample far larger than a single industry-year usually holds.

R2 · A real version of the test

Specify the design of a real modified Jones test: the grouping, the estimation sample, the treatment of the intercept, the outlier rule, and the way the discretionary accrual would be linked to a later outcome. Name the input most likely to make the flags meaningless.

Solution

Group firm-years by industry and year, so that the normal accrual model is estimated within a set of firms that face similar accounting and economic conditions. Estimate on all firms in the group, but report the estimates with and without a robust outlier rule, because Proposition 3.3 says that a few firms with large planted or real terms can move the coefficients. Settle the intercept choice before estimation, and report the residual sums. Link the residual to an outcome such as a later restatement or an enforcement action, with the outcome defined before the sort. The input most likely to make the flags meaningless is the normal-accrual model itself: if the model misses a real driver of accruals in a group, the residuals measure the miss. The lab cannot test this, because its normal model is exactly right by construction.

R3 · What a SIMULATED result can and cannot show

State what Lab 1 shows about the modified Jones regression, and what a real study would need in addition before its flags could support a claim about a specific firm.

Solution

Lab 1 shows that, when the normal model is correct and the manipulation enters the discretionary term, the regression recovers the planted structure to a degree that depends on the planted shift and the sample size, and that estimating on all firms moves the coefficients by a projection of the planted term. It does not show that a real manipulation enters the discretionary term, that the normal model is correct for a real industry, or that a flagged firm manipulated. A claim about a specific firm needs the outcome, the normal model's fit to that firm's industry, the alternative explanations for the residual, and an external check of the accounting, none of which the lab supplies.
