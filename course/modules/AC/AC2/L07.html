The Piotroski F-score

  • Uses: financial ratio analysis, operating accruals, accrual accounting, the cash-flow statement, the balance sheet
  • Introduces: the Piotroski F-score (defined in §1)
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A ratio can move for reasons unrelated to the firm getting better. Piotroski (2000) answers that problem with a simple device: nine yes-or-no tests of profitability, leverage and liquidity, and operating efficiency, added together. The score can be computed from any two annual reports. Its claims are narrow. The paper applies it to high book-to-market firms over 1976 to 1996, and several of its signals are proxies for what the author wanted to measure. This lesson states each signal as the A15 contract implements it, shows where the implementation departs from the paper, and reports each published number with its locator.

1The nine signals

Fix a firm and a fiscal year-end \(t\). Let \(\mathrm{NI}_t\) be net income, \(\mathrm{CFO}_t\) cash flow from operations, \(\mathrm{TA}_t\) total assets, \(\mathrm{LTD}_t\) long-term debt, \(\mathrm{CA}_t\) and \(\mathrm{CL}_t\) current assets and liabilities, \(\mathrm{Rev}_t\) revenue, \(\mathrm{COGS}_t\) cost of revenue, and \(\mathrm{Sh}_t\) shares outstanding. The contract's ratios are

\[ \mathrm{ROA}_t = \frac{\mathrm{NI}_t}{\mathrm{TA}_{t-1}},\quad \mathrm{CFO}^{\ast}_t = \frac{\mathrm{CFO}_t}{\mathrm{TA}_{t-1}},\quad \mathrm{LEV}_t = \frac{\mathrm{LTD}_t}{\bar{\mathrm{TA}}_t},\quad \mathrm{CR}_t = \frac{\mathrm{CA}_t}{\mathrm{CL}_t},\quad \mathrm{GM}_t = \frac{\mathrm{Rev}_t-\mathrm{COGS}_t}{\mathrm{Rev}_t},\quad \mathrm{ATO}_t = \frac{\mathrm{Rev}_t}{\mathrm{TA}_{t-1}}, \]

where \(\bar{\mathrm{TA}}_t = (\mathrm{TA}_t + \mathrm{TA}_{t-1})/2\). Beginning-of-year assets scale the flow measures, net income, cash flow and revenue, as the paper does for ROA, CFO and asset turnover (Piotroski 2000, pp. 7 and 9). Average assets scale the debt measure, as the paper does for leverage (p. 8).

Definition 1.1 (Piotroski F-score)

Let \(s_1,\dots,s_9 \in \{0,1\}\) be the indicators of the table below, where \(s_i = 1\) when the stated condition holds and \(0\) otherwise. The Piotroski F-score is \(F = \sum_{i=1}^{9} s_i\), an integer from 0 to 9.

#SignalCondition for 1Group
\(s_1\)Return on assets\(\mathrm{ROA}_t > 0\)Profitability
\(s_2\)Operating cash flow\(\mathrm{CFO}^{\ast}_t > 0\)Profitability
\(s_3\)Change in ROA\(\mathrm{ROA}_t > \mathrm{ROA}_{t-1}\)Profitability
\(s_4\)Accruals\(\mathrm{CFO}^{\ast}_t > \mathrm{ROA}_t\), that is \(\mathrm{CFO}_t > \mathrm{NI}_t\)Profitability
\(s_5\)Change in leverage\(\mathrm{LEV}_t < \mathrm{LEV}_{t-1}\)Leverage, liquidity, source of funds
\(s_6\)Change in current ratio\(\mathrm{CR}_t > \mathrm{CR}_{t-1}\)Leverage, liquidity, source of funds
\(s_7\)No equity issuance (proxy)\(\mathrm{Sh}_t \le \mathrm{Sh}_{t-1}\)Leverage, liquidity, source of funds
\(s_8\)Change in gross margin\(\mathrm{GM}_t > \mathrm{GM}_{t-1}\)Operating efficiency
\(s_9\)Change in asset turnover\(\mathrm{ATO}_t > \mathrm{ATO}_{t-1}\)Operating efficiency

The conditions are the A15 contract's piotroski_f_score definition, in the paper's order. The three groups are the paper's own (Piotroski 2000, p. 7; the source-of-funds heading is on p. 8).

2Where the implementation departs from the paper

The contract encodes the paper's conditions. Three departures are deliberate proxies, which the contract records under "Departures from the published definitions" in atlas/A15.contract.md. They are the contract's choices, made because the engine works from standardized items only, and they are documentation, not errors. Each is checked against the paper's text.

  1. Net income (the contract's choice). The paper defines ROA and CFO on net income before extraordinary items (Piotroski 2000, p. 7). The contract reads the NetIncomeLoss tag. This lesson does not check whether the two coincide for any filer. A filer with material extraordinary items would need its own mapping.
  2. Equity issuance (the contract's proxy). The paper's indicator \(\mathrm{EQOFFER}\) is 1 when the firm did not issue common equity in the year before portfolio formation (p. 9). The contract's \(s_7\) tests whether shares outstanding did not rise. The two can disagree in both directions. A firm that issues shares and retires the same number in buybacks has a flat count, so \(s_7 = 1\), though it did issue equity. A share count that rises without a cash issuance, for example through a stock-funded acquisition, gives \(s_7 = 0\). The paper's text does not address that case. The score built on \(s_7\) is a proxy score.
  3. Debt (the contract's choice). The paper measures leverage as total long-term debt over average total assets (p. 8). The contract uses the LongTermDebtNoncurrent tag. A filer that reports current maturities of debt separately gets a different debt figure from the paper's wording.

The signals are equally weighted. The paper says that chosen signals are not claimed to be optimal, and that "factor analysis may more aptly extract an optimal combination of signals, but such an approach has costs in terms of implementability" (p. 7, n. 2). The paper does not claim its nine equally weighted signals are the optimal set.

3The sample, the conditioning and what the paper reports

Piotroski's sample is high book-to-market firms. Each year from 1976 to 1996, he ranks firms on book-to-market and keeps the highest quintile with enough financial data to compute the signals (p. 11). The paper reads the signals as conditional on distress. The paper describes the average high book-to-market firm as financially distressed (p. 6). For such firms a rise in leverage is a bad signal, because the negative implications of increased leverage seem more plausible than a benefit from reduced agency costs (p. 7). The paper states the conditioning directly: the stated ex ante implication of each signal is conditioned on the fact that these firms are financially distressed at some level.

The headline numbers are these. Selecting financially strong high book-to-market firms raises the mean return of a high book-to-market investor by at least 7.5% a year. A strategy that buys expected winners and shorts expected losers returns 23% a year over 1976 to 1996 (Abstract, p. 1; repeated in the introduction, p. 3). The paper reports a mean-return gain of at least 7.5% a year from selecting financially strong high book-to-market firms, and a 23% annual return to a long-short strategy, both for 1976 to 1996 in the U.S. This is one sample, one period and one market, so the grade is C. Confirmation status of these figures: read on 2026-10-27 in the extracted text of the Ivey-hosted copy of Piotroski (2000), Abstract on p. 1 and the introduction on p. 3. The JSTOR record of the same article could not be read (bot check), so the Ivey-hosted text is the source that was read.

The score is then used to form portfolios. Firms with a score of 8 or 9 form the high F-score group, and firms with 0 or 1 form the low group (p. 12). The return tests use a bootstrap of pseudo-portfolios with the same sizes as the real groups, which the paper gives as 1,448 high and 396 low observations (p. 12). The paper reports 1,448 observations in the high F-score group and 396 in the low group, for the bootstrap of its return tests. Confirmation status: read on 2026-10-27 in the extracted text of the Ivey-hosted copy of the Journal of Accounting Research 38 supplement (pdftotext), with the printed page numbers matched to the page headers. The lesson does not reproduce these counts.

The accrual signal rests on prior evidence the paper attributes to Sloan. Earnings driven by positive accruals, where profit exceeds operating cash flow, are a bad signal about future profitability and returns, as the paper attributes to Sloan (1996). The lesson takes this attribution from the paper, read on 2026-10-27, and does not restate Sloan's own result, which has not been read. The accruals anomaly is taught in L05.

The lab builds the nine signals on generated firm-years. Every firm is SIMULATED. The lab checks two properties the contract requires: the score is an integer from 0 to 9, and every signal is unchanged when all money items are multiplied by 1,000.

Lab 1 · The nine signals on SIMULATED firm-years

Stated before running: every score is an integer from 0 to 9, and every signal is unchanged when money is scaled by 1,000, because each signal compares ratios or share counts. The lab does not predict the score distribution. It reports the mean and the counts so that the generator's output can be read, not interpreted as evidence about firms. All firms are SIMULATED.

"""Lab AC2-L07-1: the nine Piotroski signals on generated firm-years.

Every firm here is SIMULATED: the statements are drawn from a seeded generator,
no filer is represented, and no figure is an estimate of any real company.
The signal definitions are the contract's (atlas/A15.contract.md, piotroski_f_score),
which follow Piotroski (2000), pp. 8-10 (printed pages) of the Journal of
Accounting Research 38 supplement. Money is in SIMULATED currency units (SCU).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass

SEED = 20260527
N_FIRMS = 400


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class YearStatement:
    """One fiscal year of a SIMULATED firm. Money in SCU; shares in millions."""

    revenue: float
    cogs: float
    net_income: float
    cfo: float
    total_assets: float
    current_assets: float
    current_liabilities: float
    long_term_debt: float
    shares: float


def gross_margin(s: YearStatement) -> float:
    """Gross margin (revenue - cogs) / revenue, dimensionless."""
    if s.revenue <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError("revenue must be positive")
    return (s.revenue - s.cogs) / s.revenue


def current_ratio(s: YearStatement) -> float:
    """Current assets / current liabilities, dimensionless."""
    if s.current_liabilities <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError("current liabilities must be positive")
    return s.current_assets / s.current_liabilities


def piotroski_signals(y2: YearStatement, y1: YearStatement, y0: YearStatement) -> tuple[int, ...]:
    """Return the nine binary signals for year t = y0, given t-1 = y1 and t-2 = y2.

    Scaling follows the contract: ROA and CFO use beginning-of-year assets
    (y1.total_assets for t); leverage uses average total assets; asset turnover
    uses beginning-of-year assets. Each signal is 1 when its condition holds.
    """
    if min(y2.total_assets, y1.total_assets, y0.total_assets) <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError("total assets must be positive")
    roa0 = y0.net_income / y1.total_assets
    roa1 = y1.net_income / y2.total_assets
    cfo0 = y0.cfo / y1.total_assets
    lev0 = y0.long_term_debt / ((y0.total_assets + y1.total_assets) / 2.0)
    lev1 = y1.long_term_debt / ((y1.total_assets + y2.total_assets) / 2.0)
    ato0 = y0.revenue / y1.total_assets
    ato1 = y1.revenue / y2.total_assets
    return (
        int(roa0 > 0.0),
        int(cfo0 > 0.0),
        int(roa0 > roa1),
        int(cfo0 > roa0),
        int(lev0 < lev1),
        int(current_ratio(y0) > current_ratio(y1)),
        int(y0.shares <= y1.shares),
        int(gross_margin(y0) > gross_margin(y1)),
        int(ato0 > ato1),
    )


def generate_firm(rng: random.Random) -> tuple[YearStatement, YearStatement, YearStatement]:
    """Generate years t-2, t-1, t for one SIMULATED firm (all values positive, SCU)."""
    assets = rng.uniform(800.0, 1200.0)
    margin = rng.uniform(0.03, 0.12)
    gm = rng.uniform(0.25, 0.55)
    turn = rng.uniform(0.8, 1.6)
    ltd_share = rng.uniform(0.10, 0.45)
    shares = rng.uniform(40.0, 60.0)
    years: list[YearStatement] = []
    for _ in range(3):
        growth = rng.uniform(0.92, 1.10)
        assets = assets * growth
        revenue = assets * turn * rng.uniform(0.9, 1.1)
        cogs = revenue * (1.0 - gm * rng.uniform(0.9, 1.1))
        net_income = revenue * margin * rng.uniform(0.6, 1.4)
        accrual = rng.uniform(-0.04, 0.04) * assets
        cfo = net_income - accrual
        current_assets = assets * rng.uniform(0.25, 0.45)
        current_liabilities = assets * rng.uniform(0.15, 0.35)
        long_term_debt = assets * ltd_share * rng.uniform(0.9, 1.1)
        shares = shares * rng.choice((1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.02))
        years.append(
            YearStatement(
                revenue=revenue,
                cogs=cogs,
                net_income=net_income,
                cfo=cfo,
                total_assets=assets,
                current_assets=current_assets,
                current_liabilities=current_liabilities,
                long_term_debt=long_term_debt,
                shares=shares,
            )
        )
        margin = max(0.005, margin + rng.uniform(-0.01, 0.01))
        gm = min(0.9, max(0.05, gm + rng.uniform(-0.02, 0.02)))
        turn = max(0.3, turn + rng.uniform(-0.1, 0.1))
    return years[0], years[1], years[2]


def scale_money(s: YearStatement, k: float) -> YearStatement:
    """Multiply every money item by k > 0; shares are unchanged."""
    return YearStatement(
        revenue=s.revenue * k,
        cogs=s.cogs * k,
        net_income=s.net_income * k,
        cfo=s.cfo * k,
        total_assets=s.total_assets * k,
        current_assets=s.current_assets * k,
        current_liabilities=s.current_liabilities * k,
        long_term_debt=s.long_term_debt * k,
        shares=s.shares,
    )


def main() -> None:
    """Generate the firms, print six worked firm-years, the score distribution and the scale check."""
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    rows: list[tuple[int, tuple[int, ...]]] = []
    for firm_id in range(N_FIRMS):
        y2, y1, y0 = generate_firm(rng)
        rows.append((firm_id, piotroski_signals(y2, y1, y0)))

    print(f"SIMULATED firm-years (seed {SEED}): signals s1..s9 and F-score")
    for firm_id, sig in rows[:6]:
        bits = "".join(str(b) for b in sig)
        print(f"SIM-{firm_id:03d}  signals={bits}  score={sum(sig)}")

    counts = [0] * 10
    for _, sig in rows:
        counts[sum(sig)] += 1
    print(f"score distribution over {N_FIRMS} SIMULATED firm-years:")
    for score, n in enumerate(counts):
        print(f"  score {score}: {n}")
    mean_score = sum(sum(sig) for _, sig in rows) / N_FIRMS
    print(f"mean score: {mean_score:.4f}")

    # Scale invariance (contract, Conventions): multiplying money by k > 0 leaves every signal unchanged.
    rng2 = random.Random(SEED)
    unchanged = 0
    for _ in range(N_FIRMS):
        y2, y1, y0 = generate_firm(rng2)
        k = 1000.0
        if piotroski_signals(y2, y1, y0) == piotroski_signals(
            scale_money(y2, k), scale_money(y1, k), scale_money(y0, k)
        ):
            unchanged += 1
    print(f"signals unchanged under money x1000: {unchanged}/{N_FIRMS}")

5Where the canon breaks

  • The sample sets the sign. Every signal's good and bad reading is conditioned on distress (p. 7). A firm outside the high book-to-market population has no such conditioning, and the paper's sign convention is not evidence there. No claim about other populations follows from the paper.
  • The cutoffs are a choice. Profitability signals are sign tests at zero. Equity uses a non-increase in shares. The efficiency and liquidity signals test the sign of a change. The paper notes an alternative: a deterioration in liquidity is a negative signal only if the current ratio is near one, and a cutoff of 1.5 gives stronger return results than the liquidity metric and aggregate score used in the paper. The paper reports this in a footnote, not as its main result.
  • Equal weights. Each signal counts once. The paper says a statistical combination may do better (p. 7, n. 2), so the score is a summary and not an optimal one.
  • Proxies. \(s_7\) is a share-count proxy for equity issuance (§2), and the net-income and debt tags differ from the paper's wording. A replication must state which proxy it used.
  • Out-of-sample evidence is missing from the registry. Tests of the F-score in other samples and periods exist in the literature, but the registry holds no verified entry for them. This lesson reports the paper's result only, and it does not say the result holds elsewhere. The missing source is listed in plans/AC2-builder-requests.md.

6Misconceptions

  1. “A high ROA guarantees a high F-score.”

    Signal 1 tests only that ROA is positive, and signal 2 only that CFO is positive. Their contribution is one point each, whatever the level of profitability. Signals 3 to 9 test changes from the prior year, so a firm with a high ROA that is falling can score low. Take a firm with ROA 0.15 (down from 0.20), CFO above zero but below net income, rising leverage, falling liquidity, a rising share count, falling margin and falling turnover. Its signals are \(s_1 = 1\), \(s_2 = 1\), and \(s_3 = s_4 = \dots = s_9 = 0\), so \(F = 2\). The score sorts changes within a population, conditional on distress (p. 7); it is not a level measure of profitability.

  2. “Multiplying every money item by a constant changes the score, so the score depends on the currency or on whether figures are in thousands.”

    Each signal compares ratios that share a scale. Multiplying every money item by a positive constant \(k\) leaves each ratio unchanged, so each signal is unchanged. Lab 1 shows the invariance for every SIMULATED firm. Shares are counts and are not multiplied, but \(s_7\) compares counts and is unaffected.

  3. “The F-score is a rule, so any firm with a score of 8 or 9 beats the market.”

    The paper's return result is a comparison inside the high book-to-market portfolio over 1976 to 1996, tested by the bootstrap of p. 12. It reports a mean-return gain of at least 7.5% a year for that sample. That is one period in one market, graded C. The paper makes no claim for firms outside the sample, and the lesson does not extend it.

7Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · The accrual test on the worked firm

For the worked firm in §4, compute \((\mathrm{NI}_t - \mathrm{CFO}_t)/\mathrm{TA}_{t-1}\) and state \(s_4\).

Solution

\((117 - 96)/1100 = 21/1100 = 0.0191\). Since \(\mathrm{CFO}_t = 96 < 117 = \mathrm{NI}_t\), \(\mathrm{CFO}^{\ast}_t < \mathrm{ROA}_t\), so \(s_4 = 0\).

M2 · Change one number

Keep the worked firm unchanged except \(\mathrm{NI}_t = -20\) (a loss). Recompute \(s_1,\dots,s_9\) and \(F\).

Solution

\(\mathrm{ROA}_t = -20/1100 = -0.0182\). Then \(s_1 = 0\), since it is not positive. \(s_2 = 1\) is unchanged, since \(\mathrm{CFO}_t = 96 > 0\). \(s_3 = 0\), since \(-0.0182 < 0.0660\). \(s_4 = 1\), since \(\mathrm{CFO}^{\ast}_t = 0.0873 > -0.0182\). The debt, liquidity, shares, margin and turnover signals do not use net income, so \(s_5 = \dots = s_9 = 1\). The vector is \((0,1,0,1,1,1,1,1,1)\) and \(F = 7\).

M3 · Scale

Multiply every money item of the worked firm by 1,000. Show that \(\mathrm{ROA}_t\) and the score are unchanged.

Solution

Every money item, including \(\mathrm{TA}_{t-1}\), is multiplied by 1000, so \(\mathrm{ROA}_t = 117000/1100000 = 117/1100 = 0.1064\). The same holds for every ratio in §1, because numerator and denominator carry the same factor. Shares (50) are counts and are not multiplied, but \(s_7\) compares counts and stays 1. The score stays 8.

Proof

P1 · The accrual indicator

Prove that \(s_4 = 1\) if and only if \(\mathrm{CFO}_t > \mathrm{NI}_t\). Relate this to the paper's ACCRUAL indicator.

Solution

Let \(D = \mathrm{TA}_{t-1} > 0\). Then \(\mathrm{CFO}^{\ast}_t > \mathrm{ROA}_t\) is \(\mathrm{CFO}_t/D > \mathrm{NI}_t/D\). Multiplying by \(D > 0\) preserves the inequality, so it is equivalent to \(\mathrm{CFO}_t > \mathrm{NI}_t\). Hence \(s_4 = 1\) if and only if \(\mathrm{CFO}_t > \mathrm{NI}_t\). The paper defines ACCRUAL as net income less cash flow, scaled by beginning assets, and sets its indicator to one when CFO exceeds ROA (p. 8). That is the same condition, because ACCRUAL \(< 0\) is equivalent to \(\mathrm{CFO} > \mathrm{NI}\).

P2 · The score is an integer in 0 to 9, and scale-invariant

Prove that \(F\) is an integer in \(\{0,\dots,9\}\), and that \(F\) is unchanged when every money item is multiplied by \(k > 0\). Shares are not money.

Solution

Each \(s_i\) is an indicator, so \(s_i \in \{0,1\}\) and \(F = \sum s_i\) is an integer with \(0 \le F \le 9\). For scale: \(\mathrm{ROA}_t\), \(\mathrm{CFO}^{\ast}_t\), \(\mathrm{ATO}_t\), \(\mathrm{GM}_t\), \(\mathrm{LEV}_t\) and \(\mathrm{CR}_t\) are each a ratio of money to money, so under \(\mathrm{money} \mapsto k\cdot\mathrm{money}\) each becomes \(k x/(k y) = x/y\). Each comparison in \(s_1\) to \(s_6\), \(s_8\) and \(s_9\) compares two such ratios, so its truth value is unchanged. \(s_7\) compares share counts, which are unchanged. Hence each \(s_i\), and so \(F\), is unchanged.

P3 · Average versus beginning assets

Show by example that \(s_5\) can be 0 even when long-term debt fell. Use \(\mathrm{TA}_{t-2} = 1100\), \(\mathrm{TA}_{t-1} = 1000\), \(\mathrm{TA}_t = 900\), \(\mathrm{LTD}_{t-1} = 310\) and \(\mathrm{LTD}_t = 300\).

Solution

Debt fell: \(300 < 310\). But \(\bar{\mathrm{TA}}_t = (900+1000)/2 = 950\) and \(\bar{\mathrm{TA}}_{t-1} = (1000+1100)/2 = 1050\). So \(\mathrm{LEV}_t = 300/950 = 0.3158\) and \(\mathrm{LEV}_{t-1} = 310/1050 = 0.2952\). Since \(0.3158 > 0.2952\), \(\mathrm{LEV}_t > \mathrm{LEV}_{t-1}\) and \(s_5 = 0\). The signal depends on assets as well as debt, because the ratio is scaled by average assets. The inputs are illustrative, not data.

Empirical

E1 · Read Lab 1's distribution

In Lab 1's output (400 SIMULATED firm-years), how many firm-years score 8 or 9, and what fraction is that? State what the fraction does and does not tell you about the paper's high F-score group.

Solution

The output lists 24 firm-years at score 8 and 7 at score 9. Together that is 31 of 400, a fraction of \(31/400 = 0.0775\). This describes the generator's settings. It is not the share of real high book-to-market firms that score 8 or 9, and it cannot be compared with the paper's group, which the paper defines by score within its own sample (p. 12).

E2 · The smallest change that lifts the worked firm to 9

On the worked firm, find the smallest integer value of \(\mathrm{CFO}_t\) that makes \(s_4 = 1\), and state the resulting score.

Solution

\(s_4 = 1\) requires \(\mathrm{CFO}_t > \mathrm{NI}_t = 117\) (P1), so the smallest integer is 118. Check: \(118/1100 = 0.1073 > 0.1064\). Every other signal is unchanged, so \(F = 9\).

E3 · Sensitivity to the debt tag

Suppose the worked firm's filer reports \(\mathrm{LTD}_t\) as 300 instead of 264. Does \(s_5\) change? Compute \(\mathrm{LEV}_t\) and decide.

Solution

\(\mathrm{LEV}_t = 300/1150 = 0.2609\), still below \(\mathrm{LEV}_{t-1} = 0.3143\), so \(s_5 = 1\) and the score stays 8. This is one case. It shows that \(s_5\) survives this tag change here, and it makes no claim for firms whose leverage is near the prior year's.

Research

R1 · Design a replication with the paper's sample rules

Specify a replication of the paper's sample selection and return windows in a different market and period. List the sample rules, the timing of portfolio formation, and the signal definitions you would take from the A15 contract. State which departures from the paper you would disclose.

Solution

(1) Sample: each fiscal year, rank firms on book-to-market, using the prior year's distribution for the cutoffs, and keep the top quintile (p. 11). (2) Returns: one- and two-year buy-and-hold returns from the beginning of the fifth month after fiscal year-end (p. 11). (3) Signals: the contract's definitions (§1), with the net-income and debt tags. (4) Disclosures: the \(s_7\) proxy and the net-income and debt tags (§2), and every firm dropped for missing items, so the sample is the one the signals can compute. (5) Test: the bootstrap of p. 12 for the high-minus-low difference. The replication changes market and period only, and it reports each departure.

R2 · Does the score add to book-to-market?

The paper's sample is already selected on book-to-market. State a test of whether the F-score adds information beyond book-to-market, and say what outcome would count against the score.

Solution

Within each book-to-market quintile, not only the highest, sort firms by F-score and compare the mean one-year-ahead return of the high group (8 or 9) with the low group (0 or 1), using the bootstrap of p. 12 within each quintile. The score adds information if the high-minus-low difference is positive in the highest quintile and is not explained by the quintile. It counts against the score if the difference is zero there, or if it is explained by size, share turnover or analyst coverage, which the paper examines (p. 1). This is a design. The lesson reports no result for it.

R3 · Test the cutoff alternative in advance

The paper's footnote says a current-ratio cutoff of 1.5 gives stronger return results (p. 9, n. 5). State a pre-registered test of that alternative: the hypothesis, the sample, and the decision rule.

Solution

Hypothesis: replacing \(s_6\) with \(\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{CR}_t > 1.5\}\) raises the high-minus-low return difference relative to the paper's \(s_6\), on a holdout period not used to choose 1.5. Sample: the replication sample of R1, split at a date fixed before any return is examined. Decision rule: accept the alternative only if its high-minus-low difference is larger on the holdout and its bootstrap interval excludes the paper's version. The footnote motivates the test; it is not the test.
