The Accruals Anomaly

  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled

  • Dlore

If operating accruals are the part of earnings that cash has not yet confirmed, the market may price that part too generously. The accruals anomaly is the claim that firms with high accruals earn lower subsequent returns than firms with low accruals. It tests whether accounting information is priced correctly, and it is also a test that is easy to run badly. This lesson sets out the sorted-portfolio test, the arithmetic that makes its spread predictable, and the replication evidence that limits what the spread can show. Lab 1 is a SIMULATED study of the mechanics. It does not reproduce the empirical result.

1The claim and the test

Definition 1.1 (the accruals anomaly)

The accruals anomaly is the regularity that firms with high operating accruals earn lower subsequent stock returns than firms with low operating accruals. The accruals are scaled as in L04, Definition 1.3, which puts the sort variable on one scale across firms.

Sloan (1996) investigates whether stock prices reflect information about future earnings contained in the accrual and cash-flow components of current earnings. The lesson states no finding of that paper: no sample period, return level or spread is quoted from it.

Definition 1.2 (the hedge spread)

Each month \(t\), sort the \(N\) firms on the scaled accrual \(z_{i,t}\) into quintiles of equal size. Let \(\bar r_{L,t}\) and \(\bar r_{H,t}\) be the equal-weighted average next-month returns of the lowest- and highest-accrual quintiles. The monthly spread is \(S_t = \bar r_{L,t} - \bar r_{H,t}\), a zero-investment portfolio that is long the low-accrual firms and short the high-accrual firms. Over \(T\) months, the test statistic is \[ t = \frac{\bar S}{s_S / \sqrt{T}}, \qquad \bar S = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} S_t, \qquad s_S^2 = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(S_t - \bar S)^2. \]

The spread is a return difference between two groups of firms. It is not the return an investor earns from a strategy that trades every firm at the stated weights and costs. Section 4 returns to that distinction.

2What the spread equals, and the quintile arithmetic

Proposition 2.1 (the expected spread under a linear accrual effect)

Suppose that in month \(t\) the next-month return of firm \(i\) is \(r_{i,t} = a + \beta z_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}\), where \(\E[\varepsilon_{i,t} \mid z_{\cdot,t}] = 0\) and \(a\), \(\beta\) are constants. Let \(\bar z_{L,t}\) and \(\bar z_{H,t}\) be the average accrual score in the lowest and highest quintiles of the month-\(t\) sort. Then \(\E[S_t \mid z_{\cdot,t}] = \beta(\bar z_{L,t} - \bar z_{H,t})\). With \(\beta \lt 0\), the expected spread is positive.

Proof

The average return of a quintile is the average of \(a + \beta z_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t}\) over its members. The constant \(a\) cancels in the difference. The average of \(\beta z_{i,t}\) over the low quintile is \(\beta \bar z_{L,t}\), and over the high quintile \(\beta \bar z_{H,t}\). The average of \(\varepsilon_{i,t}\) over either quintile has conditional mean zero, because the sort depends only on \(z\). Taking the conditional expectation of the difference gives the result.

Remark 2.2 (a null effect gives a zero expected spread)

If \(\beta = 0\), Proposition 2.1 gives \(\E[S_t \mid z] = 0\). Under iid months and finite variance, the central limit theorem makes the distribution of \(t\) approximately standard normal for large \(T\). This is the reference the lab's false-positive rate is compared with. The approximation is not exact at \(T = 120\), and the lab measures the error rather than assuming it away.

Proposition 2.3 (the quintile gap for standard normal scores)

Let \(Z\) be standard normal, \(q = \Phi^{-1}(0.2)\), and \(\varphi\) its density. The mean of \(Z\) over its lowest quintile is \(-\varphi(q)/0.2\), and the mean over its highest quintile is \(+\varphi(q)/0.2\). The gap \(\bar z_L - \bar z_H\) is therefore \(-2\varphi(q)/0.2\), which is approximately \(-2.800\).

Proof

The lowest quintile of \(Z\) is the event \(Z \le q\), which has probability \(\Phi(q) = 0.2\). Its mean is \(\E[Z \mid Z \le q] = \frac{1}{0.2}\int_{-\infty}^{q} z\,\varphi(z)\,dz\). Since \(\varphi'(z) = -z\varphi(z)\), the integral equals \([-\varphi(z)]_{-\infty}^{q} = -\varphi(q)\), and the mean is \(-\varphi(q)/0.2\). The symmetry \(\varphi(-z) = \varphi(z)\) gives the highest quintile. Numerically, \(q \approx -0.8416\) and \(\varphi(q) \approx 0.2800\), so the gap is \(\approx -2.800\).

Proposition 2.3 says how far apart the quintile scores are in standard deviations. A sort on a standard normal score puts about 1.4 standard deviations of accrual below the mean in the low quintile and about 1.4 above it in the high quintile. The planted spread in Lab 1 is therefore \(\beta\) times about \(-2.800\), which is a known number before the simulation runs.

Lab 1 · The sorted-portfolio test on a SIMULATED universe

The lab simulates 200 universes, each with 500 firms and 120 months, under two settings. Under the null (\(\beta = 0\)), the spread should average zero and the test's rejection rate at \(|t| \gt 1.96\) should be near the nominal 5%. Under the planted effect \(\beta = -0.0008\), the recovered mean spread should match the planted spread. Proposition 2.1 gives that planted spread as \(\beta\) times the mean quintile gap, about \(0.0008 \times 2.8 = 0.0022\) per month. At 120 months the test should have modest power, because the standard error of the mean spread is about 0.0010. The results are a check of the mechanics on a universe whose effect the author chose. They are not an estimate of the accruals effect in real stocks.

"""AC2 L05 lab 1: an accrual-sorted portfolio study on a SIMULATED universe. It checks the mechanics only.

SIMULATED: every return in this lab is generated from the model below. No market data is read and no
published return is reproduced. The planted effect BETA is chosen by the author, so the spread this
lab recovers is the spread the generator put in. The lab shows how the sort, the spread and the
t-statistic behave when the effect is known. It says nothing about the size of the accruals effect
in real stock returns, and it does not reproduce Sloan (1996).

Model, per month t and firm i (units: monthly simple return, so 0.01 is one percent):
    z[t, i]   standardized accrual signal, the sort variable (known exactly here)
    r[t, i]   = BASE + BETA * z[t, i] + SIGMA * eps[t, i],  eps iid N(0, 1)
Each month the firms are sorted on z into quintiles of equal size. The strategy is long the lowest-accrual
quintile and short the highest, equal-weighted. The monthly spread is r_low - r_high.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

F64 = NDArray[np.float64]

N_FIRMS = 500
N_MONTHS = 120  # ten years of monthly spreads
SIGMA = 0.08  # idiosyncratic monthly volatility of each simulated stock
BASE = 0.005  # simulated mean monthly return of the universe
BETA = -0.0008  # the planted effect: a one-SD higher accrual lowers the monthly return by 8 basis points
N_SEEDS = 200
SWEEP_SEEDS = 20  # seeds per firm count in the sweep of realised gaps
QUINTILE = N_FIRMS // 5
CRITICAL = 1.96  # normal approximation to the 5% two-sided critical value for |t|


def simulate(seed: int, beta: float, n_firms: int = N_FIRMS) -> tuple[F64, F64]:
    """Monthly spreads (long low-accrual, short high-accrual) and the realised z-gap for one seeded universe.

    Each month sorts n_firms firms into quintiles of n_firms // 5 firms, so the z-gap is the realised gap of that sort.
    """
    quintile = n_firms // 5
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    z = rng.standard_normal((N_MONTHS, n_firms))
    eps = rng.standard_normal((N_MONTHS, n_firms))
    r = BASE + beta * z + SIGMA * eps
    order = np.argsort(z, axis=1, kind="stable")
    low = order[:, :quintile]
    high = order[:, -quintile:]
    r_low = np.take_along_axis(r, low, axis=1).mean(axis=1)
    r_high = np.take_along_axis(r, high, axis=1).mean(axis=1)
    z_low = np.take_along_axis(z, low, axis=1).mean(axis=1)
    z_high = np.take_along_axis(z, high, axis=1).mean(axis=1)
    return r_low - r_high, z_low - z_high


def t_stat(x: F64) -> float:
    """Mean over its standard error, with n - 1 degrees of freedom in the standard deviation."""
    return float(x.mean() / (x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(x.size)))


def main() -> None:
    null_t: list[float] = []
    effect_t: list[float] = []
    effect_means: list[float] = []
    gaps: list[float] = []
    for seed in range(N_SEEDS):
        spread0, _ = simulate(seed, 0.0)
        spread1, gap = simulate(seed, BETA)
        null_t.append(t_stat(spread0))
        effect_t.append(t_stat(spread1))
        effect_means.append(float(spread1.mean()))
        gaps.append(float(gap.mean()))

    null = np.array(null_t)
    effect = np.array(effect_t)
    planted_exact = BETA * float(np.mean(gaps))
    planted = planted_exact  # expected monthly spread: BETA times the mean z-gap of the quintiles

    print(f"seeds {N_SEEDS} months {N_MONTHS} firms {N_FIRMS} sigma {SIGMA} beta {BETA}")
    print(f"mean_z_gap_simulated {float(np.mean(gaps)):.4f}")
    print(f"planted_monthly_spread {planted:.5f}")
    sd_theory = SIGMA * np.sqrt(2.0 / QUINTILE)  # sd of a two-leg spread: each leg averages QUINTILE firms
    print(f"spread_sd_theory {sd_theory:.5f}")
    print(f"expected_t_at_planted {planted / (sd_theory / np.sqrt(N_MONTHS)):.3f}")
    print(f"recovered_mean_monthly_spread {float(np.mean(effect_means)):.5f}")
    print(f"null_mean_t {float(null.mean()):.3f} null_sd_t {float(null.std(ddof=1)):.3f}")
    print(f"null_false_positive_rate {float(np.mean(np.abs(null) > CRITICAL)):.3f}")
    print(f"effect_rejection_rate_power {float(np.mean(np.abs(effect) > CRITICAL)):.3f}")
    print(f"effect_median_t {float(np.median(effect)):.3f}")
    print(f"seed0_spread_mean {float(effect_means[0]):.5f} seed0_t {effect_t[0]:.3f}")
    # The realised gap of the sort depends on how many firms are sorted each month: a sweep over firm counts.
    for n_firms in (50, 100, 500, 5000):
        gap_n = [simulate(seed, 0.0, n_firms)[1].mean() for seed in range(SWEEP_SEEDS)]
        print(f"mean_z_gap_firms_{n_firms}_seeds_{SWEEP_SEEDS} {float(np.mean(gap_n)):.4f}")
    print(f"seed0_distance_in_se {(effect_means[0] - planted_exact) / (sd_theory / np.sqrt(N_MONTHS)):.3f}")

SIMULATED throughout. The recovered mean spread, 0.00219, is close to the planted 0.00223, so the sort and the spread recover the effect that was put in. The null false-positive rate is 0.060 across 200 seeds. That is 0.6 standard errors above the nominal 5% (the standard error of a rate from 200 draws is about 0.015), so it is consistent with a correct 5% test. The median t-statistic under the planted effect, 2.155, is close to the expected t of 2.164 that the lab prints (expected_t_at_planted), which is the planted spread divided by its theoretical standard error at 120 months. Power at this effect is 0.590, so about 41% of samples this size would miss the effect at the 5% level. The single seed-0 run shows the noise: its t-statistic is 0.676. The lab says nothing about the size of the accruals effect in real returns, and it does not reproduce Sloan (1996).

3Why accruals might predict returns

The question Sloan (1996) asks, in the form given in Section 1, is a price question: whether the accrual and cash-flow components of current earnings carry information about future earnings that prices reflect. Lesson L04, Proposition 3.1, gives the algebra of accruals under its assumptions A1 to A4: accruals equal the growth in net operating assets less capital expenditure. The sorted test is one way to ask the price question.

A sorted spread can be consistent with mispricing, with compensation for a risk that the sort loads on, or with measurement error in accruals. This lesson does not choose among them. Lab 1 cannot choose either, because the effect it recovers is planted by the author.

4Where the canon breaks

Two registry papers are relevant to whether the spread survives scrutiny. This lesson names each paper by its title and states no finding from either.

  • Publication and decay. McLean and Pontiff, "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?", are in the registry under mclean2016academic. Their results are not stated here. Whether the question they ask applies to accrual-sorted spreads is not settled by this lesson.
  • Replication under a common data set. Hou, Xue and Zhang, "Replicating Anomalies", are in the registry under hou2018replicating. Their results are not stated here. Whether a spread that is significant under one set of choices survives another set is not settled by this lesson.
  • Implementation. The spread is gross, equal-weighted and rebalanced monthly. It ignores trading costs, short-sale constraints and the concentration of the short leg in small or hard-to-borrow stocks. Each of these changes what an investor could earn, and the lab does not model them. A spread that is large in a sorted test can be small or negative after costs, and that is a separate calculation.
  • The sort variable is measured with error in real data. The lab sorts on the true accrual score. If the sort variable carries measurement error, the quintile averages in Proposition 2.1 are computed on a noisier score. The generator has no measurement-error term, so Lab 1 cannot show how large that effect is, and this lesson does not quantify it.

Honest pending. A real version of this test needs filer accruals from Lesson L04 and monthly stock returns for the same firms. The filer data are the SEC company-facts files, and their reuse statement is not yet confirmed. A return file would also need a licence. The Kenneth French Data Library is recorded as not cleared for redistribution in BLUEPRINT §5.3, so this lesson does not use it. Every number in Lab 1 comes from a simulated universe. The open source requests are listed in plans/AC2-open-items.md.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Lab 1 reproduces Sloan's result.”

    The lab plants the effect. Its spread equals \(\beta\) times the quintile gap by Proposition 2.1, which is the input the author chose. What the lab shows is that the sort, the spread and the test recover a known effect and keep their nominal error rate under the null. It is a check of the machinery, and it gives no evidence about the size of the effect in real returns.

  2. “A significant t-statistic in one sample is evidence for the effect.”

    Seed 0 of Lab 1 has a mean spread of 0.00075 and a t-statistic of 0.676, although the planted spread is 0.00223. The standard error of the 120-month mean spread is about 0.0010, so the seed-0 mean lies about 1.43 standard errors below the planted value (unrounded, lab output seed0_distance_in_se −1.433). Power is 0.590 at this effect and sample length, so a single sample of this size misses the effect more than 40% of the time.

  3. “The spread is the return an investor would earn from trading on accruals.”

    The spread is a gross, equal-weighted difference between two groups, with no costs and no shorting constraints (Section 4). Definition 1.2 defines a test statistic, and nothing in it is a forecast of an implementable portfolio. An implementable return needs a separate construction, with costs, and the lab does not build one.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · Quintile sizes

The lab sorts 500 firms each month into quintiles of equal size. How many firms are in each quintile, and how many firms are in the middle three quintiles together?

Solution

Each quintile holds \(500/5 = 100\) firms. The middle three quintiles hold \(3 \times 100 = 300\) firms. The lab's code uses \(N/5\) for each extreme quintile, so the two extreme groups together contain 200 firms and the spread compares 100 firms in each.

M2 · A monthly spread and its sum

A month has \(\bar r_{L} = 0.0120\) and \(\bar r_{H} = 0.0040\). Compute the monthly spread. Then compute the sum of 12 such spreads, and say why that sum is not the compound return of the spread.

Solution

\(S = 0.0120 - 0.0040 = 0.0080\). The sum over 12 months with the same spread is \(12 \times 0.0080 = 0.096\), a cumulative simple difference of 9.6 percentage points. Compounding \((1.0080)^{12} - 1 \approx 0.1003\) treats the spread as a return on capital that is reinvested. The spread is the return on a zero-investment portfolio, with no capital at risk, so compounding it does not give a return on capital. The sum is the correct cumulative object for the test.

M3 · The t-statistic by hand

Over \(T = 120\) months the mean spread is 0.0020 and the sample standard deviation of the spreads is 0.0100. Compute \(t\) and decide at the 5% level, using the normal approximation of Remark 2.2.

Solution

The standard error is \(0.0100/\sqrt{120} = 0.0100/10.954 = 0.000913\). Then \(t = 0.0020/0.000913 = 2.191\). Since \(|t| \gt 1.96\), the null of a zero mean spread is rejected at the 5% level under the normal approximation. The normal approximation is an approximation at this \(T\), and a t-distribution with 119 degrees of freedom gives a slightly larger critical value.

Proof

P1 · Quintiles are invariant to monotone rescaling

Show that replacing each accrual score \(z_{i,t}\) by \(g(z_{i,t})\), for any strictly increasing \(g\), leaves the quintile membership of every firm unchanged. Explain why that matters for the choice of a scaling in L04.

Solution

A strictly increasing \(g\) preserves order: \(z_i \lt z_j\) if and only if \(g(z_i) \lt g(z_j)\). The ranks of the firms are therefore the same, and quintiles defined by ranks, with equal counts, are the same sets. The groups, and therefore the spread, are unchanged. Only the coefficient \(\beta\) of Proposition 2.1 changes, because the score it multiplies has been redefined. The consequence for L04 is that the choice between scalings by total assets and by other size measures matters only if it changes the ranks. A monotone transformation that preserves the ranks leaves the sort unchanged.

P2 · The truncated-normal mean

Prove that \(\int_{-\infty}^{q} z\,\varphi(z)\,dz = -\varphi(q)\) for any real \(q\), and use it to recover the quintile gap of Proposition 2.3 with \(q = \Phi^{-1}(0.2)\).

Solution

The derivative of \(\varphi(z) = (2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-z^2/2}\) is \(\varphi'(z) = -z\varphi(z)\). Hence \(\int_{-\infty}^{q} z\varphi(z)\,dz = -\int_{-\infty}^{q}\varphi'(z)\,dz = -[\varphi(q) - \varphi(-\infty)] = -\varphi(q)\), because \(\varphi(-\infty) = 0\). With \(q = \Phi^{-1}(0.2)\), \(\Phi(q) = 0.2\), so the conditional mean of the lowest quintile is \(-\varphi(q)/0.2\). Numerically \(q \approx -0.8416\) and \(\varphi(q) \approx 0.2800\), giving \(-1.400\). The highest quintile is \(+1.400\) by symmetry, and the gap is \(-2.800\).

P3 · The null spread has zero mean

Under \(\beta = 0\) and independent noise, show that \(\E[S_t] = 0\) and that the unconditional mean of \(\bar S\) is zero. State which assumption about the noise is used.

Solution

With \(\beta = 0\), \(r_{i,t} = a + \varepsilon_{i,t}\), so each quintile average is \(a\) plus the average of noise over its members. The constant \(a\) cancels in \(S_t\). The conditional mean of the noise average over a quintile is zero, because the noise has zero conditional mean given the sort. Taking expectations over the sort as well, \(\E[S_t] = 0\), and \(\E[\bar S] = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \E[S_t] = 0\). The assumption used is that \(\E[\varepsilon \mid z] = 0\), which Proposition 2.1 also needs. Serial independence of the months is not needed for the mean, but it is needed for the normal approximation of Remark 2.2.

Empirical

E1 · Is a 6.0% rejection rate consistent with 5%?

In Lab 1, the null false-positive rate is 0.060 across 200 seeds. Decide whether this is consistent with a correct 5% test, using the binomial standard error.

Solution

The standard error of a rate estimated from 200 independent draws at true rate 0.05 is \(\sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95/200} = \sqrt{0.0002375} = 0.01541\). The observed rate exceeds 0.05 by 0.010, which is \(0.010/0.01541 = 0.65\) standard errors. That is well within sampling error, so the lab's 6.0% is consistent with a correct 5% test. The 200 seeds give 200 separately generated universes, so the binomial reference applies.

E2 · The expected t at the planted effect

Using Proposition 2.1 and the monthly spread standard deviation implied by \(\sigma = 0.08\) and 100 firms per leg, compute the expected \(t\) at \(\beta = -0.0008\) and \(T = 120\). Compare with the median of 2.155 in Lab 1.

Solution

Each leg averages 100 firms, so the variance of a leg average is \(\sigma^2/100\), and the spread of the two legs has variance \(2\sigma^2/100\). Its standard deviation is \(\sigma\sqrt{2/100} = 0.08 \times 0.1414 = 0.01131\), which the lab prints as spread_sd_theory. The planted mean spread is \(\beta\) times the mean simulated z-gap the lab prints, \(-0.0008 \times (-2.7937) = 0.0022350\), printed as planted_monthly_spread 0.00223. The z-gap is \(-2.7937\) (200 seeds) rather than the analytic \(-2.7996\) of Proposition 2.3. The lab prints a sweep of the realised gap over firm counts, with 20 seeds each: \(-2.7437\) at 50 firms, \(-2.7728\) at 100, \(-2.7912\) at 500 and \(-2.7991\) at 5,000 (mean_z_gap_firms_50_seeds_20 to mean_z_gap_firms_5000_seeds_20). Their distances from the analytic value, computed as \(-2.7996\) minus each printed value, are 0.0559, 0.0268, 0.0084 and 0.0005. These four printed values are consistent with a finite-sort effect that shrinks as the number of firms sorted each month grows. Four counts with 20 seeds each do not establish the rate of that decay. The 200-seed and 20-seed means at 500 firms differ by 0.0025, which is seed-to-seed variation. The expected \(t\) is \(0.0022350/(0.01131/\sqrt{120}) = 2.164\), printed as expected_t_at_planted 2.164. The lab's median of 2.155 is close to that value.

E3 · Why one seed misleads

Seed 0 in Lab 1 gives a mean spread of 0.00075 and a t-statistic of 0.676. Using the planted spread of 0.00223, locate the seed-0 result in standard-error units and say what the result shows about a single sample.

Solution

The standard error of the 120-month mean spread is \(0.011314/\sqrt{120} = 0.0010328\), using the unrounded theoretical standard deviation. The lab prints the distance computed from unrounded values, with the seed-0 mean and the planted spread at full precision: seed0_distance_in_se \(-1.433\), so the seed-0 draw lies 1.43 standard errors below the planted value. Rounded inputs, 0.00075 and 0.00223, give \(-1.44\), which is an artefact of rounding. It shows that a single sample of this length can land on the wrong side of the effect, and that a t-statistic of 0.676 is not evidence against the effect. The lab's power of 0.590 summarises the same point across 200 seeds.

Research

R1 · Sample length for 80% power

Using the normal approximation, how many months \(T\) are needed for 80% power at the planted effect, with a two-sided 5% test? Use the same monthly standard deviation as E2.

Solution

Under the normal approximation the test rejects with power \(0.8\) when the mean of the statistic, \(\mu_t = (\text{planted mean}) \times \sqrt{T}/s\), exceeds \(1.96 + 0.8416 = 2.8016\), where \(s = 0.01131\) is the monthly standard deviation and \(0.8416 = \Phi^{-1}(0.8)\). Solving \(0.0022350\sqrt{T}/0.01131 = 2.8016\), with the planted spread \(0.0022350\) of E2, gives \(\sqrt{T} = 14.18\) and \(T \approx 201\) months, about 17 years. The answer is an approximation under the normal reference and an iid assumption, and it treats the planted effect as known. A real study does not know the effect, which is why the power calculation is a design input and not a result.

R2 · A real version of the test

Specify the inputs of a real accrual-sorted test: the sample of firms, the accrual measure and its scaling, the return horizon and its alignment with the filing date, the weights, and the test. Name the single input most likely to bias the spread, and say how the lab's design would miss it.

Solution

Inputs: a set of firm-years with the accrual of L04 for each, a monthly return for the same firms, and the filing date of each accrual so that the sort uses only information available at the sort date. The return should start after the filing, not at the fiscal year end. The weights are equal within quintiles, and the test is the statistic of Definition 1.2. The input most likely to bias the spread is the alignment of the sort with the information date: if the accrual is sorted on before the filing, the spread uses information the market did not have. The lab does not exhibit this problem, because it has no filing dates and sorts on the true score at the sort date. Its design therefore cannot show the bias that a misaligned real sort would produce.

R3 · What a sorted spread cannot separate

List the three explanations of a non-zero sorted spread that Section 3 names, and state one observable that would help separate mispricing from risk compensation. Say what the observable cannot separate.

Solution

The explanations are mispricing through investor inattention to accrual persistence, compensation for a risk that the sort loads on, and measurement error that makes the sort noisy in a way correlated with returns. An observable that helps is a return-on-risk pattern that survives after adjusting for known risk factors: if the spread shrinks to zero under a risk model, risk is the likelier explanation; if it persists with no change in risk loadings, mispricing gains support. That observable cannot separate mispricing from a risk factor that the model omits, and it cannot distinguish a real effect from measurement error in the accrual. Each of those needs a design of its own, and this lesson does not supply one.
