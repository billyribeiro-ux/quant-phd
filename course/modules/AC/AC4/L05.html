Master and flexible budgets

  • Uses: fixed, variable and mixed costs, contribution margin
  • Introduces: the master budget, the flexible budget

A budget is a plan with numbers in it, and a plan made for one volume cannot be read directly against results at another. This lesson sets out two budgets, defined in Section 1: the plan at the budgeted volume, and the same cost function evaluated at the volume that actually happened. Set side by side, they split the gap between plan and result into the part that comes from the units sold and the part that does not. Lab 1 runs both budgets on a SIMULATED two-product case.

1Two budgets and one sign convention

Definition 1.1 (the master budget)

The master budget is the plan for a period at the budgeted volume. It states the budgeted units of each product, the budgeted selling price and standard unit cost of each, and the budgeted fixed costs. A standard unit cost is the cost per unit that the budget assumes. The master budget combines them into budgeted revenue, costs and operating profit, which is revenue less total cost (the operating income of Lesson L01).

Definition 1.2 (the flexible budget)

The flexible budget for a period is the budget at the actual volume. It uses the budgeted selling prices and standard unit costs from the master budget, applied to the actual units of each product, and it leaves fixed costs at their budgeted amount. A flexible budget is defined for a volume, so it can be computed for any volume that the cost function of Section 2 covers.

Definition 1.3 (sign convention)

Every variance in this lesson is stated as its effect on operating profit. A variance is actual minus budget for each line that adds to profit, and the cost lines enter with the sign that makes a higher cost reduce profit. A positive variance is favourable, meaning profit is higher than the comparison budget. A negative variance is unfavourable. This convention applies in Lesson L06 without change.

2The cost function and what flexing changes

Each cost line is written as a cost function of the units \(Q\) produced and sold, \(C(Q) = F + vQ\), where \(F\) is the fixed part and \(v\) is the variable cost per unit (Lesson L01). In this case the material is variable, so its standard cost per unit is the standard kilograms per unit times the material price. The fixed overhead is \(F\). For product \(i\), write \(p_i\) for the budgeted selling price and \(c_i\) for the standard variable cost per unit, and \(\text{CM}_i = p_i - c_i\) for the contribution margin per unit of Lesson L01.

Proposition 2.1 (flexing at budgeted units returns the master budget)

Under the cost function of Section 2, let \(q_{b,i}\) be the budgeted units of product \(i\), and let \(\Pi_b(\mathbf q)\) denote the budget operating profit computed from the price, standard cost and fixed-cost rules at units \(\mathbf q = (q_1, \dots, q_n)\). Then \(\Pi_b(\mathbf q_b) = \Pi_m\), where \(\Pi_m\) is the master-budget operating profit.

Proof

The master budget is the budget at the budgeted units by Definition 1.1, and the flexible budget is the same function evaluated at the units in the argument by Definition 1.2. Evaluating one function at one argument gives one value, so \(\Pi_b(\mathbf q_b) = \Pi_m\). Written out, \(\Pi_b(\mathbf q) = \sum_i \text{CM}_i\, q_i - F\), and at \(\mathbf q_b\) this is \(\sum_i \text{CM}_i\, q_{b,i} - F = \Pi_m\).

Proposition 2.2 (the static variance splits into a volume-and-mix part and a flexible part)

Let \(\Pi_a\) be actual operating profit, \(\Pi_m\) the master-budget operating profit and \(\Pi_f\) the flexible-budget operating profit at the actual units. Define the static variance as \(\Pi_a - \Pi_m\) and the flexible variance as \(\Pi_a - \Pi_f\). Then \(\Pi_a - \Pi_m = (\Pi_f - \Pi_m) + (\Pi_a - \Pi_f)\) exactly, with no assumption on the cost function. Under the cost function of Section 2, the first term, \(\Pi_f - \Pi_m\), equals \(\sum_i \text{CM}_i (q_{a,i} - q_{b,i})\), the change in operating profit from the units sold, including their mix. The second term is the flexible variance at the actual units.

Proof

Add and subtract \(\Pi_f\) on the right-hand side: \((\Pi_f - \Pi_m) + (\Pi_a - \Pi_f) = \Pi_a - \Pi_m\). The identity needs no assumption about the cost function. With the cost function above, \(\Pi_f - \Pi_m = \sum_i \text{CM}_i (q_{a,i} - q_{b,i})\), because the fixed costs are the same in both budgets. That is a sum over products, so a shift in mix is inside the first term. Lesson L06 separates the two.

Remark 2.3 (what the flexible budget holds fixed)

The flexible budget changes the units and nothing else in the cost function. Prices and standard unit costs stay at budget, and fixed costs stay at budget. A flexible budget therefore cannot show an efficiency or price effect. Those effects are measured when actual prices or actual kilograms replace the budget values, which is the step from the flexible budget to actual results. Both effects are defined in Lesson L06.

3Running both budgets on a SIMULATED case

SIMULATED. The case has two products, A and B, and one material. Every input is an invented value. The budget has 600 units of A and 400 units of B, budget selling prices of 2,500 and 3,400 cents, standard material of 2 kg and 3 kg per unit at 500 cents per kg, and fixed overhead of 500,000 cents. The actual period sold 650 units of A and 500 of B, at 2,400 and 3,450 cents, and used 2,950 kg of material, with fixed overhead of 510,000 cents. The material price equals the standard price in this case, so no price variance on material arises. All money is in integer cents, and every step of the lab is whole-number arithmetic, so every total is exact and no rounding is applied.

Lab 1 · The master budget, the flexed budget at actual volume, and the static variance

Before running: the master budget gives 1,160,000 cents of operating profit. The actual result is 1,300,000 cents, so the static variance is favourable by 140,000. Because 1,150 units are sold against 1,000 budgeted, the flexed budget at actual units should show more profit than the master budget, and the flexible variance at actual units should be unfavourable. Of the 265,000 cents of volume-and-mix part, the 150 extra units account for 249,000 cents at the budget contribution per unit of 1,660 cents (1,660,000 cents on 1,000 budgeted units, as the lab prints), and the shift in mix toward B accounts for the other 16,000 cents. Lab 1 prints that 265,000 as one figure. Lesson L06, Lab 1, computes the two parts from the same case and prints them separately.

"""AC4 L05 lab 1: the master budget, then the flexible budget at actual volume. SIMULATED company.

SIMULATED: every number in this lab is an invented input chosen by the author. No company's accounts are read and no
published figure is reproduced. The lab checks the arithmetic of two budgets and one comparison. It says nothing about
how any real firm budgets.

All money is integer cents (1 dollar = 100 cents). Quantities are whole units or whole kilograms. Nothing is a float,
so every total below is exact.

Case, for two products A and B and one material:
    budget (master) units        A 600, B 400
    budget selling price         A 2,500 cents, B 3,400 cents per unit
    standard material per unit   A 2 kg, B 3 kg
    standard material price      500 cents per kg (the actual price equals it in this case)
    fixed overhead               500,000 cents per period, whatever the output
    actual units sold            A 650, B 500
    actual selling price         A 2,400 cents, B 3,450 cents per unit
    actual material used         1,350 kg of A's material and 1,600 kg of B's (2,950 kg in total)
    actual fixed overhead        510,000 cents

Cost behaviour used here: material is variable, so its cost is the standard kilograms per unit times the material
price, times the units. Fixed overhead does not change with units. The flexible budget applies the same cost
function to the actual units and leaves fixed overhead unchanged.

Sign convention: a variance is actual minus budget for a profit line, so a positive value is favourable.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import NamedTuple

MATERIAL_PRICE = 500  # cents per kg, standard and actual
FIXED_BUDGET = 500_000  # cents per period
FIXED_ACTUAL = 510_000  # cents per period


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Product:
    """One product: budget inputs, then actual results. Money in cents, quantities in units or kilograms."""

    name: str
    price_budget: int  # cents per unit
    std_kg: int  # standard kilograms of material per unit
    units_budget: int
    price_actual: int  # cents per unit
    units_actual: int
    kg_actual: int  # kilograms of material used in the period


PRODUCTS: tuple[Product, ...] = (
    Product("A", 2_500, 2, 600, 2_400, 650, 1_350),
    Product("B", 3_400, 3, 400, 3_450, 500, 1_600),
)


class Totals(NamedTuple):
    """Revenue, material, contribution and operating profit for one set of units, in cents."""

    revenue: int
    material: int
    contribution: int
    fixed: int
    operating: int


def budget_at(products: tuple[Product, ...], units: dict[str, int]) -> Totals:
    """Budget figures at the given units, at budget prices and standard material cost.

    Fixed overhead is FIXED_BUDGET whatever the units, because it does not flex.
    """
    revenue = sum(p.price_budget * units[p.name] for p in products)
    material = sum(p.std_kg * MATERIAL_PRICE * units[p.name] for p in products)
    contribution = revenue - material
    return Totals(revenue, material, contribution, FIXED_BUDGET, contribution - FIXED_BUDGET)


def actual_totals(products: tuple[Product, ...]) -> Totals:
    """Actual results: actual prices, actual kilograms at the material price, actual fixed overhead."""
    revenue = sum(p.price_actual * p.units_actual for p in products)
    material = MATERIAL_PRICE * sum(p.kg_actual for p in products)
    contribution = revenue - material
    return Totals(revenue, material, contribution, FIXED_ACTUAL, contribution - FIXED_ACTUAL)


def main() -> None:
    master_units = {p.name: p.units_budget for p in PRODUCTS}
    actual_units = {p.name: p.units_actual for p in PRODUCTS}

    master = budget_at(PRODUCTS, master_units)
    flexed = budget_at(PRODUCTS, actual_units)
    actual = actual_totals(PRODUCTS)

    # Identity: the static variance splits into a volume-and-mix part and a flexible variance, with no residual.
    # Flexing at the budgeted units returning the master budget holds by construction (one function, one argument)
    # and is not checked here.
    static = actual.operating - master.operating
    volume_and_mix = flexed.operating - master.operating
    flexible = actual.operating - flexed.operating
    assert static == volume_and_mix + flexible

    print(f"master_units {sum(master_units.values())}")
    for p in PRODUCTS:
        rev, mat = p.price_budget * p.units_budget, p.std_kg * MATERIAL_PRICE * p.units_budget
        print(f"master_{p.name}_revenue {rev} material {mat}")
    print(f"master_contribution {master.contribution}")
    print(f"master_operating_profit {master.operating}")
    print(f"actual_units {sum(actual_units.values())}")
    for p in PRODUCTS:
        rev, mat = p.price_budget * p.units_actual, p.std_kg * MATERIAL_PRICE * p.units_actual
        print(f"flexed_{p.name}_revenue {rev} material {mat}")
    print(f"flexed_revenue {flexed.revenue} material {flexed.material} fixed {flexed.fixed}")
    print(f"flexed_contribution {flexed.contribution}")
    print(f"flexed_operating_profit {flexed.operating}")
    print(f"actual_revenue {actual.revenue} material {actual.material}")
    print(f"actual_contribution {actual.contribution}")
    print(f"actual_operating_profit {actual.operating} fixed {actual.fixed}")
    print(f"static_variance {static}")
    print(f"volume_and_mix_variance {volume_and_mix}")
    print(f"flexible_variance {flexible}")


main()

Flexing the same cost function to the 1,150 units actually sold gives 1,425,000 cents, with fixed overhead still at 500,000. The static variance is +140,000, but 265,000 of the difference from plan is the volume-and-mix part (the flexed budget minus the master budget), and the flexible variance at actual units is −125,000. 4What the flexible budget does not show

Three limits are set here so that the lab's output is not read past what it can support.

  • The relevant range. The flexible budget is linear in units. It applies over the relevant range of the cost behaviour in Lesson L01, the volumes for which the cost function holds. The lab evaluates the budget only at 1,000 budgeted units and at 1,150 actual units, and it makes no claim about any other volume. If capacity must be added above some volume, fixed overhead may rise there, and a flexed budget that keeps it at 500,000 cents then understates the cost at that volume.
  • The split of mixed costs. The fixed and variable parts of a cost are estimates. An error in the variable cost per unit carries into the flexed budget in proportion to volume; an error in the fixed amount carries into it unchanged at every volume. The lab takes the split as given and gives no estimate of its error.
  • The standard is an assumption. The standard kilograms and the standard price are the budget's assumptions. If the standard is wrong, the efficiency effect in Lesson L06 measures the wrong thing. A flexible budget cannot correct a bad standard. It removes the volume-and-mix effect from the comparison (Proposition 2.2) and leaves the other effects in the flexible variance.

5Where the canon breaks

The two budgets are arithmetic on stated inputs. The lesson states no finding about how firms use budgets in practice, and it cites no source for one. Section 4 sets out three limits, and a fourth applies here. A flexed budget removes the volume-and-mix effect from the comparison. It does not make a comparison fair on plan quality: a plan that set an unattainable price or an unreachable standard yields a flexible variance that reports the miss faithfully and attributes no cause to it. Lesson L06 gives the labels for the parts of that miss, and it cannot say which one was the plan's own error.

6Misconceptions

  1. “Comparing actual results with the master budget measures the manager's performance.”

    The static variance in Lab 1 is +140,000, and it contains +265,000 from the volume and mix of units sold (Proposition 2.2). The actual result is below the flexed budget by 125,000 cents. Read against the master budget alone, the result shows +140,000 as performance. That figure includes the 265,000 from units sold, and the −125,000 that the flexible budget isolates is hidden inside it.

  2. “Flexing a budget scales the fixed costs with the volume.”

    Definition 1.2 leaves fixed costs at their budgeted amount. Lab 1 prints a flexed fixed overhead of 500,000 cents at 1,150 units, the same as the master budget at 1,000 units. The printed master contribution of 1,660,000 less the printed master operating profit of 1,160,000 gives that 500,000. Fixed costs flex only if the cost function says they do, and in this case it says they do not.

  3. “The flexible budget is built from actual costs, so it shows what the costs should have been.”

    The flexible budget uses budgeted prices and standard unit costs (Definition 1.2). If it used the actual kilograms, the efficiency effect would vanish from the comparison. Lab 1's flexible variance of −125,000 contains that effect. Its efficiency part is −75,000 cents (150 kg above the standard allowance at 500 cents per kg, Lesson L06), and the remaining −50,000 is the selling-price variance of −40,000 (Lesson L06) and the fixed-overhead variance of −10,000.

7Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · Master budget for product A

Product A has a budgeted 600 units, a budgeted price of 2,500 cents, and a standard material use of 2 kg per unit at 500 cents per kg. Compute its budgeted revenue, standard material cost and contribution, in cents.

Solution

Revenue is \(600 \times 2{,}500 = 1{,}500{,}000\) cents. Standard material is \(600 \times 2 \times 500 = 600{,}000\) cents. The contribution is \(1{,}500{,}000 - 600{,}000 = 900{,}000\) cents, which is \(600 \times \text{CM}_A\) with \(\text{CM}_A = 2{,}500 - 1{,}000 = 1{,}500\) cents per unit. The lab prints 1,500,000 and 600,000 for these lines.

M2 · Flex product B to actual units

Flex product B to its actual 500 units, at budgeted prices and standard material (3 kg per unit at 500 cents per kg). Compute the flexed revenue, material and contribution.

Solution

Revenue is \(500 \times 3{,}400 = 1{,}700{,}000\) cents. Material is \(500 \times 3 \times 500 = 750{,}000\) cents. The contribution is \(1{,}700{,}000 - 750{,}000 = 950{,}000\) cents, which is \(500 \times 1{,}900\), since \(\text{CM}_B = 3{,}400 - 1{,}500 = 1{,}900\) cents per unit. The lab prints 1,700,000 and 750,000.

M3 · Fixed overhead at two volumes

The flexed contribution at actual units is 1,925,000 cents. Fixed overhead is 500,000 cents in the budget. Compute the flexed operating profit, and state the fixed overhead in the flexed budget at 1,150 units and at 1,000 units.

Solution

Operating profit is \(1{,}925{,}000 - 500{,}000 = 1{,}425{,}000\) cents, which the lab prints. Fixed overhead in the flexed budget is 500,000 cents at 1,150 units, and it is 500,000 cents at 1,000 units. It does not move with the units, because Definition 1.2 keeps it at the budgeted amount.

Proof

P1 · Flexing at budgeted units returns the master budget

Prove Proposition 2.1 by writing the budget operating profit as a function of units and evaluating it at the budgeted units.

Solution

With the cost function of this case, the budget operating profit at units \(\mathbf q\) is \(\Pi_b(\mathbf q) = \sum_i (p_i - c_i) q_i - F\). The master budget is \(\Pi_m = \Pi_b(\mathbf q_b)\), because Definition 1.1 states the budgeted units and the budgeted prices and costs. So the flexed budget at the budgeted units is \(\Pi_b(\mathbf q_b) = \Pi_m\). The argument uses only that the same function is evaluated twice, and nothing about the values of the inputs.

P2 · The static variance splits exactly

Show that \(\Pi_a - \Pi_m = (\Pi_f - \Pi_m) + (\Pi_a - \Pi_f)\), and identify which of the two terms in the decomposition contains the change in mix.

Solution

Expand the right-hand side: \(\Pi_f - \Pi_m + \Pi_a - \Pi_f = \Pi_a - \Pi_m\), since \(\Pi_f\) cancels. The first term is \(\sum_i \text{CM}_i(q_{a,i} - q_{b,i})\) by Proposition 2.2, which depends on each product's units separately, so with two products, a change in mix that leaves the total units unchanged gives a nonzero first term whenever the two contribution margins differ, as they do here (1,500 and 1,900 cents per unit). The change in mix is therefore inside \(\Pi_f - \Pi_m\), and Lesson L06 separates it. The second term, \(\Pi_a - \Pi_f\), contains price and efficiency and the change in fixed overhead.

P3 · Linearity of a single-product flexed budget

For a single product with price \(p\), standard variable cost \(c\) and fixed cost \(F\), show that the flexed operating profit at \(Q\) units is linear in \(Q\), and state its slope and intercept.

Solution

The flexed operating profit is \(\Pi(Q) = pQ - cQ - F = (p - c)Q - F\). It is linear in \(Q\), with slope \(p - c = \text{CM}\) per unit and intercept \(-F\). The slope is the contribution margin, and the intercept is minus the fixed cost, which is the operating loss at zero units. Linearity is an assumption of the cost function and holds only in the relevant range (Section 4).

Empirical

E1 · Share of the static variance from volume and mix

Using the lab's printed values, what fraction of the static variance of +140,000 cents is the volume-and-mix part? Explain why the fraction is larger than one.

Solution

The volume-and-mix part is 265,000 cents and the static variance is 140,000 cents, so the fraction is \(265{,}000 / 140{,}000 \approx 1.893\), or 189.3% to one decimal place. The fraction exceeds one because the flexible variance of −125,000 cents offsets part of it: \(265{,}000 - 125{,}000 = 140{,}000\). Both parts are in the same units, and the static variance is their sum (Proposition 2.2).

E2 · Recompute the flexible variance from the printed profits

The lab prints an actual operating profit of 1,300,000 cents and a flexed operating profit of 1,425,000 cents. Compute the flexible variance and compare it with the printed value.

Solution

The flexible variance is \(1{,}300{,}000 - 1{,}425{,}000 = -125{,}000\) cents, which matches the lab's flexible_variance line. The two inputs come from the lab's printed totals, which are themselves computed from the case inputs, so the check confirms the arithmetic and not the inputs.

E3 · Actual profit against the plan

Express the actual operating profit of Lab 1 as a percentage of the master-budget operating profit, to one decimal place.

Solution

The ratio is \(1{,}300{,}000 / 1{,}160{,}000 \approx 1.12069\), so the actual profit is 112.1% of the plan, with the plan as the base. The excess is 140,000 cents, which is 12.1% of the plan (140,000 / 1,160,000 \(\approx\) 0.12069). That excess is the sum of the volume-and-mix effect of 265,000 cents and the flexible variance of −125,000 cents. The ratio hides that composition, and a single ratio cannot show either part.

Research

R1 · A volume outside the relevant range

The lab's case would sell 1,600 units in a period where a second shift adds fixed overhead, split in the budget mix (960 units of A and 640 of B, from 0.6 and 0.4 of 1,600). Say what the flexible budget can and cannot tell a manager about that period.

Solution

The flexible budget at 1,600 units, with fixed overhead held at 500,000 cents, is a number the function produces, but the function is not valid there if a second shift adds fixed cost. The budget would then understate the cost at that volume. What the budget can tell the manager is the effect of the units at the budgeted prices and standard costs, provided the second shift's fixed cost is included as a separate fixed amount. The flexed comparison cannot separate the shift's cost from the performance of the units, because the shift's cost is not in the cost function the lab uses.

R2 · Flex to 1,300 units at the budget mix

Flex the case to 1,300 total units at the budgeted mix shares of 0.6 for A and 0.4 for B. Compute the flexed revenue, material and operating profit in cents, using the budgeted prices and standard material.

Solution

At the budget mix, A has \(0.6 \times 1{,}300 = 780\) units and B has \(0.4 \times 1{,}300 = 520\). Revenue is \(780 \times 2{,}500 + 520 \times 3{,}400 = 1{,}950{,}000 + 1{,}768{,}000 = 3{,}718{,}000\) cents. Material is \(780 \times 1{,}000 + 520 \times 1{,}500 = 780{,}000 + 780{,}000 = 1{,}560{,}000\) cents. The operating profit is \(3{,}718{,}000 - 1{,}560{,}000 - 500{,}000 = 1{,}658{,}000\) cents. The calculation uses no lab output, so it is an exercise in the same cost function.

R3 · Where an overspend on fixed overhead appears

The lab's fixed overhead is 10,000 cents over budget. Say in which comparison that overspend appears, and state the assumption that places it there.

Solution

The overspend is the difference between actual fixed overhead of 510,000 cents and the flexed amount of 500,000 cents. It appears in the flexible variance, the comparison of actual results with the flexed budget, and not in the volume-and-mix part, because that part depends only on units and the cost function. The assumption that places it there is that fixed overhead does not flex with units, which holds inside the relevant range. If a part of the overhead moves with volume, the flexed budget must include that part, and the 10,000 cents would be measured against a different flexed amount.
