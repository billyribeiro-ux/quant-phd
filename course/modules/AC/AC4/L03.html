Relevant Costs for Decisions

  • Uses: fixed, variable and mixed costs, contribution margin, depreciation and amortization
  • Introduces: relevant cost (a future cost that differs between the alternatives)
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or simulation
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A manager choosing between two actions needs only the costs that the choice changes. Costs that are the same under both actions cancel, and costs that were already spent cannot be recovered by either action. Including either kind of cost changes the answer, as the lab below shows. This lesson defines the relevant cost, proves the rule that follows from it, and applies the rule to make-or-buy, keep-or-drop and a special order on a SIMULATED plant. The lab prints the full-cost answer next to the relevant-cost answer, so the two can be compared on the same figures.

1Relevant costs and the decision rule

Definition 1.1 (relevant cost)

A relevant cost is a future cash cost that differs between the alternatives being compared. A cost that is future and the same under every alternative is not relevant. A cost that has already been incurred is not relevant either, whatever its size, because no alternative can change it.

Three words in the definition do the work. Future excludes what has been spent. Cash excludes depreciation and allocations, which are book entries that accompany a cash flow or no cash flow at all. Differs excludes every cost that both alternatives share. The rest of this lesson uses the definition as a filter: each cost passes or fails it. A relevant cost that stops when an alternative is chosen is called avoidable in this lesson, and the avoidable cost of an alternative is the part of its relevant cost that it removes.

Proposition 1.2 (the ranking uses only relevant costs)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be two alternatives with total future costs \(T_A\) and \(T_B\). Split each total into a part common to both, \(K\), and a part that differs, \(R_A\) and \(R_B\), so that \(T_A = K + R_A\) and \(T_B = K + R_B\). Then \(T_A - T_B = R_A - R_B\), and the alternative with the lower total is the alternative with the lower relevant cost. The ranking does not depend on \(K\).

Proof

By construction \(T_A - T_B = (K + R_A) - (K + R_B) = R_A - R_B\). The sign of \(T_A - T_B\) is therefore the sign of \(R_A - R_B\), and so the lower-cost alternative is the same under both measures. The constant \(K\) appears in both totals and cancels, so no value of \(K\) can change the ranking. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.3 (what the proposition does not say)

Proposition 1.2 ranks the alternatives. It does not give the profit of either one. A full-cost statement reports every cost, including \(K\), and it is the right statement for the profit report. It is the wrong input to a choice between two alternatives, because \(K\) is the same under both and so tells the manager nothing about which to choose.

2Make or buy, on a SIMULATED plant

A plant makes a component K for its own assembly line. The figures below are SIMULATED. Money is in currency units (CU), with two decimals. The plant uses 20,000 units a year. Making one unit costs 4.00 in material, 3.00 in labour and 1.50 in variable overhead. A supervisor and dedicated tooling cost 12,000.00 a year and would be avoided if the part were bought. Plant overhead of 2.00 per unit is spread over the units; that overhead exists whether or not component K is made. The supplier's price is 10.50 per unit. The existing equipment could be sold for scrap for 3,000.00 if the part is bought, and its book value of 20,000.00 is a sunk cost.

Example 2.1 (make or buy, relevant-cost method)

The avoidable cost of making is the variable cost plus the dedicated fixed cost: \(20{,}000 \times (4.00 + 3.00 + 1.50) + 12{,}000 = 182{,}000\). The net cost of buying is the purchase price less the scrap cash: \(20{,}000 \times 10.50 - 3{,}000 = 207{,}000\). Making is cheaper by \(207{,}000 - 182{,}000 = 25{,}000\). The plant should make the part. Lab 1 prints the same figures.

Proposition 2.2 (make-minus-buy identity)

Let \(Q\) be annual units, \(m\), \(l\) and \(v\) the material, labour and variable overhead per unit, \(F\) the dedicated fixed cost that the purchase avoids, \(p\) the purchase price per unit and \(S\) the scrap cash received on buying. Then the cost of making minus the net cost of buying is \[ \Delta = Q\,(m + l + v - p) + F + S. \] Making is cheaper when \(\Delta < 0\), and the book value of the equipment does not appear in \(\Delta\).

Proof

The avoidable cost of making is \(Q(m+l+v) + F\). The net cost of buying is \(Qp - S\). Subtracting the second from the first gives \(Q(m+l+v) + F - Qp + S = Q(m+l+v-p) + F + S\). The book value \(B\) of the equipment is incurred whichever alternative is chosen, so it enters neither expression, and \(\Delta\) contains no term in \(B\). The plant overhead of 2.00 per unit is also the same under both alternatives, so it enters neither expression either. \(\blacksquare\)

Substituting the SIMULATED inputs gives \(20{,}000\,(8.50 - 10.50) + 12{,}000 + 3{,}000 = -25{,}000\), the figure in Example 2.1. Per unit, the avoidable make cost is 9.10 (8.50 of variable cost and 0.60 of dedicated fixed cost) and the net buy cost is 10.35 (the price less 0.15 of scrap per unit). The difference of 1.25 per unit over 20,000 units is the 25,000 CU.

Lab 1 · Make or buy, sunk cost and keep-or-drop on a SIMULATED plant

Before running: the relevant-cost method should choose make, with make cheaper by 25,000.00 CU a year. The full-cost method, which spreads the dedicated fixed cost and adds the 2.00 allocated overhead, should choose buy, because it compares 11.10 with 10.50. Changing the equipment's book value should leave the difference unchanged, and changing the allocated overhead should also leave it unchanged. A price of 9.50 should keep the decision at make. Product line B should be kept, because dropping it lowers firm profit. The special order should be accepted with the machine-hour rule, and the per-hour shortcut should reject it.

"""AC4 lab L03 (lab 1): make-or-buy, sunk cost and keep-or-drop on a SIMULATED case.

SIMULATED: the firm, its products and every figure below are invented inputs of a hypothetical plant. Nothing here
is a company's data, and no network or file is read. Money is integer cents (100 cents = 1 currency unit, CU);
volumes are units or machine hours. Standard library only.

Rule the lab tests: a cost is relevant to a decision when it is a future cash flow that differs between the
alternatives. A sunk cost is the same under both alternatives, and so is any allocated common cost that stays
after one alternative is chosen. The lab prints the decision under the relevant-cost method and under the full-cost
method, changes the sunk and allocated inputs, and checks that the relevant-cost difference does not move.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass, replace


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class MakeOrBuy:
    """Annual make-or-buy inputs for component K, all money in cents."""

    units: int
    material_per_unit: int
    labour_per_unit: int
    variable_overhead_per_unit: int
    dedicated_fixed: int  # supervisor and dedicated tooling; avoided if the part is bought
    allocated_overhead_per_unit: int  # plant overhead spread over units; stays after the decision
    buy_price_per_unit: int
    scrap_value: int  # cash received for the existing equipment if the part is bought
    equipment_book_value: int  # sunk: depreciated cost already incurred


def fmt(cents: int) -> str:
    """Render integer cents as CU with two decimals, without rounding."""
    sign = "-" if cents < 0 else ""
    magnitude = abs(cents)
    return f"{sign}{magnitude // 100:,}.{magnitude % 100:02d}"


def avoidable_make_cost(c: MakeOrBuy) -> int:
    """Future cash cost of making in-house that stops if the part is bought, in cents per year."""
    per_unit = c.material_per_unit + c.labour_per_unit + c.variable_overhead_per_unit
    return c.units * per_unit + c.dedicated_fixed


def net_buy_cost(c: MakeOrBuy) -> int:
    """Future cash cost of buying: purchase price less the scrap cash received, in cents per year."""
    return c.units * c.buy_price_per_unit - c.scrap_value


def relevant_difference(c: MakeOrBuy) -> int:
    """Make cost minus buy cost. A positive value means buying is cheaper by that many cents per year."""
    return avoidable_make_cost(c) - net_buy_cost(c)


def full_cost_per_unit(c: MakeOrBuy) -> int:
    """Full unit cost of making: variable cost, allocated overhead and the dedicated fixed cost spread per unit.
    The division is exact here, which the lab checks."""
    if c.dedicated_fixed % c.units != 0:
        raise ValueError("dedicated fixed cost does not divide evenly by units; choose inputs that do")
    return (
        c.material_per_unit
        + c.labour_per_unit
        + c.variable_overhead_per_unit
        + c.allocated_overhead_per_unit
        + c.dedicated_fixed // c.units
    )


def full_cost_decision(c: MakeOrBuy) -> str:
    """Compare full unit cost of making with the buy price per unit."""
    return "buy" if c.buy_price_per_unit < full_cost_per_unit(c) else "make"


def relevant_decision(c: MakeOrBuy) -> str:
    """Choose the alternative with the lower relevant (differential) cash cost."""
    return "buy" if net_buy_cost(c) < avoidable_make_cost(c) else "make"


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class ProductLine:
    """Annual figures for product line B, all money in cents."""

    revenue: int
    variable_cost: int
    avoidable_fixed: int  # product-specific staff and advertising; stops if the line is dropped
    common_fixed: int  # plant-level cost allocated to B; stays if B is dropped


def operating_income(p: ProductLine) -> int:
    """Reported operating income of the line, including its share of common fixed cost."""
    return p.revenue - p.variable_cost - p.avoidable_fixed - p.common_fixed


def profit_if_kept(p: ProductLine) -> int:
    """Total profit of the firm from the line, with the common fixed cost counted once."""
    return operating_income(p)


def profit_if_dropped(p: ProductLine) -> int:
    """Total profit with the line dropped: revenue and avoidable costs go, the common fixed cost stays."""
    return -p.common_fixed


def main() -> None:
    base = MakeOrBuy(
        units=20_000,
        material_per_unit=400,
        labour_per_unit=300,
        variable_overhead_per_unit=150,
        dedicated_fixed=1_200_000,
        allocated_overhead_per_unit=200,
        buy_price_per_unit=1_050,
        scrap_value=300_000,
        equipment_book_value=2_000_000,
    )

    print("Part K, make or buy: 20,000 units a year (SIMULATED)")
    avoidable_per_unit = avoidable_make_cost(base) // base.units
    print(f"  avoidable make cost per unit (variable + dedicated fixed / units): {fmt(avoidable_per_unit)}")
    full_per_unit = fmt(full_cost_per_unit(base))
    print(f"  full make cost per unit (adds allocated overhead 2.00): {full_per_unit}")
    print(f"  buy price per unit: {fmt(base.buy_price_per_unit)}")
    print(f"  annual avoidable make cost: {fmt(avoidable_make_cost(base))}")
    print(f"  annual net buy cost (price less scrap {fmt(base.scrap_value)}): {fmt(net_buy_cost(base))}")
    print(f"  relevant difference (make minus buy): {fmt(relevant_difference(base))}")
    print(f"  relevant-cost decision: {relevant_decision(base)}")
    print(f"  full-cost decision: {full_cost_decision(base)}")

    sunk_changed = replace(base, equipment_book_value=5_000_000)
    print("Sunk cost check: book value of the equipment changed")
    print(f"  book value {fmt(base.equipment_book_value)} gives difference {fmt(relevant_difference(base))}")
    changed_diff = fmt(relevant_difference(sunk_changed))
    print(f"  book value {fmt(sunk_changed.equipment_book_value)} gives difference {changed_diff}")
    print(f"  decision unchanged: {relevant_decision(sunk_changed) == relevant_decision(base)}")

    overhead_changed = replace(base, allocated_overhead_per_unit=400)
    print("Allocated overhead check: allocated overhead per unit raised from 200 to 400")
    print(f"  full cost per unit: {fmt(full_cost_per_unit(overhead_changed))}")
    print(f"  relevant difference: {fmt(relevant_difference(overhead_changed))}")
    print(f"  relevant decision unchanged: {relevant_decision(overhead_changed) == relevant_decision(base)}")

    price_cut = replace(base, buy_price_per_unit=950)
    print("Price check: supplier offers 9.50 per unit")
    print(f"  relevant difference: {fmt(relevant_difference(price_cut))}")
    print(f"  relevant decision: {relevant_decision(price_cut)}")
    breakeven_price = (avoidable_make_cost(base) + base.scrap_value) // base.units
    print(f"  buy price at which buying equals making, scrap netted: {fmt(breakeven_price)}")

    line = ProductLine(
        revenue=1_500_000,
        variable_cost=900_000,
        avoidable_fixed=400_000,
        common_fixed=300_000,
    )
    print("Product line B, keep or drop: annual figures (SIMULATED)")
    print(f"  contribution margin: {fmt(line.revenue - line.variable_cost)}")
    print(f"  reported operating income after common fixed cost: {fmt(operating_income(line))}")
    print(f"  firm profit if kept: {fmt(profit_if_kept(line))}")
    print(f"  firm profit if dropped: {fmt(profit_if_dropped(line))}")
    print(f"  change in firm profit from dropping: {fmt(profit_if_dropped(line) - profit_if_kept(line))}")
    print(f"  keep-or-drop decision: {'keep' if profit_if_kept(line) > profit_if_dropped(line) else 'drop'}")

    spare_hours = 40_000 - 36_000
    order_units = 3_000
    order_price = 1_200
    order_variable = 700
    order_hours_per_unit = 2
    order_hours = order_units * order_hours_per_unit
    order_contribution = order_units * (order_price - order_variable)
    excess_hours = max(0, order_hours - spare_hours)
    displaced_contribution_per_unit = 1_000
    displaced_hours_per_unit = 2
    displaced_contribution_per_hour = displaced_contribution_per_unit // displaced_hours_per_unit
    lost_contribution = excess_hours * displaced_contribution_per_hour
    net_order = order_contribution - lost_contribution
    print("Special order under a machine-hour constraint (SIMULATED)")
    print(f"  machine hours needed: {order_hours}, spare hours: {spare_hours}, excess hours: {excess_hours}")
    print(f"  order contribution: {fmt(order_contribution)}")
    per_hour = fmt(displaced_contribution_per_hour)
    print(f"  contribution forgone on product D ({per_hour} per hour, excess hours): {fmt(lost_contribution)}")
    print(f"  net effect of accepting: {fmt(net_order)}")
    print(f"  decision: {'accept' if net_order > 0 else 'reject'}")
    naive_order_per_hour = (order_price - order_variable) // order_hours_per_unit
    print(f"  naive per-hour comparison: order earns {fmt(naive_order_per_hour)}, D earns {per_hour} per hour")
    print(f"  naive decision: {'accept' if naive_order_per_hour > displaced_contribution_per_hour else 'reject'}")

3Keep or drop a product line

Example 3.1 (product line B, SIMULATED)

Product line B has revenue of 15,000.00, variable cost of 9,000.00, product-specific fixed cost of 4,000.00 that would stop if the line were dropped, and 3,000.00 of plant-level fixed cost allocated to it that would stay. Its contribution is \(15{,}000 - 9{,}000 = 6{,}000\). Its reported operating income is \(6{,}000 - 4{,}000 - 3{,}000 = -1{,}000\), which looks like a loss. Dropping the line removes revenue, variable cost and the product-specific fixed cost, and the 3,000.00 stays. Firm profit is \(-1{,}000\) if the line is kept and \(-3{,}000\) if it is dropped. Dropping it lowers profit by 2,000.00. The decision is to keep.

Remark 3.2 (the rule for keep-or-drop)

Dropping a line changes firm profit by minus its contribution less its avoidable fixed cost. Here that is \(-(6{,}000 - 4{,}000) = -2{,}000\). The allocated plant-level cost is the same under both alternatives, so it has no place in the test. A line should be dropped only when its contribution is less than its avoidable fixed cost.

4A special order under a machine-hour limit

A special order for 3,000 units of product P at 12.00 per unit has variable cost of 7.00 per unit and needs 2 machine hours per unit. The plant has 40,000 machine hours a year and uses 36,000 for its regular products, so 4,000 hours are spare. Product D, the regular product that would give up hours, earns a contribution of 10.00 per unit and needs 2 machine hours per unit, which is 5.00 per machine hour.

Proposition 4.1 (accept rule with a machine-hour limit)

Let the order need \(h\) machine hours, let \(H\) be the spare hours, and let \(c\) be the contribution per hour of the displaced product. The order contributes \(C\) in total. Accepting changes profit by \(C - c\,\max(0,\, h - H)\). The order should be accepted when this quantity is positive.

Proof

The first \(H\) hours of the order use spare capacity, which earns nothing otherwise and has no opportunity cost. Any hours beyond \(H\) must come from the displaced product, which gives up \(c\) per hour. The lost contribution is therefore \(c\max(0, h-H)\), and the net gain is \(C\) minus that amount. \(\blacksquare\)

With the SIMULATED figures, \(h = 6{,}000\), \(H = 4{,}000\) and \(C = 3{,}000 \times 5.00 = 15{,}000\). The excess is 2,000 hours, and at 5.00 per hour the lost contribution is 10,000. The net gain is 5,000, so the order is accepted. Lab 1 prints both numbers.

Remark 4.2 (the per-hour shortcut is wrong when hours are spare)

A shorter test compares the order's contribution per machine hour, \(5.00 / 2\), which is 2.50, with the displaced product's 5.00 per hour, and rejects the order. That comparison charges the order's hours at the displaced product's rate even for the 4,000 spare hours, which carry no opportunity cost. Lab 1 prints this result beside the correct one, so the difference is visible.

5Where the canon breaks

The relevant-cost method is exact for the inputs it is given. Four assumptions decide whether those inputs are the right ones. Each is stated here, because the lab's numbers hold only under them.

  • The overhead that stays must really stay. Proposition 2.2 assumes the 2.00 plant overhead per unit is unchanged by the decision. If buying lets the plant cut supervision or utilities, those costs change, so they are relevant and enter \(\Delta\). The test is whether a cost changes, not whether it is allocated. The lab does not model any such cut.
  • The avoidable fixed cost must be avoidable within the horizon. The 12,000.00 supervisor cost is treated as avoided in the year. If the supervisor's contract runs for three years, the cost is not avoidable in the first year, and a one-year model overstates the saving. The model is one year and has no discounting.
  • Scrap is the cash that buying causes, and it is not the whole story when the equipment has another use. If the equipment could earn a contribution on another product, that use is an opportunity cost of whichever alternative gives it up, and the scrap value no longer measures what buying gives up. Remark 3.2 and Proposition 4.1 use the opportunity-cost idea in plain terms: the value of the hours or the equipment given up. The lab does not value any alternative use.
  • Quality, delivery and supplier risk are not in the model. A cheaper supplier can deliver late or in a lower grade. Those effects are real, and they are outside the differential-cost arithmetic. The lab reports the cash result and no more.

6Misconceptions

  1. “A cost allocated to a product is relevant to a decision about that product.”

    An allocation is relevant only if the decision changes the total amount of the cost. In Lab 1 the 2.00 per unit allocated overhead is the same under both alternatives, so Proposition 1.2 removes it. Raising the allocation to 4.00 changes the full cost to 13.10 and leaves the difference at 25,000.00 CU.

  2. “The cost already spent on the equipment should be recovered by the decision.”

    The book value of 20,000.00 is sunk. Lab 1 changes it to 50,000.00 and the difference stays at 25,000.00 CU, because Proposition 2.2 has no term in the book value. Only the scrap cash, which the choice does change, enters the difference.

  3. “A product line with negative reported operating income should be dropped.”

    Example 3.1 gives the line a reported operating income of \(-1{,}000\). Dropping it leaves its plant-level fixed cost of 3,000.00 in place and lowers firm profit to \(-3{,}000\). The line covers its avoidable fixed cost and leaves 2,000.00 towards the common cost that stays, so dropping it is the wrong choice.

  4. “With a machine-hour limit, an order is rejected when its contribution per hour is below the displaced product's.”

    Proposition 4.1 charges opportunity cost only on hours beyond the spare capacity. In Lab 1 the naive comparison of 2.50 with 5.00 per hour rejects the order, while the correct test accepts it with a net gain of 5,000.00 CU.

7Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · Make or buy with dedicated fixed cost

A plant makes 1,000 units a year at a variable cost of 5.00 per unit. Dedicated fixed cost of 2,000.00 a year would be avoided if the part were bought. The supplier charges 6.50 per unit and there is no scrap. Which alternative is cheaper, and by how much?

Solution

The avoidable cost of making is \(1{,}000 \times 5.00 + 2{,}000 = 7{,}000\). The cost of buying is \(1{,}000 \times 6.50 = 6{,}500\). Buying is cheaper by \(7{,}000 - 6{,}500 = 500\). Proposition 2.2 with \(S = 0\) gives \(\Delta = 1{,}000(5.00 - 6.50) + 2{,}000 = 500\). A positive \(\Delta\) means making costs 500 more, so buying is cheaper by 500.

M2 · Scrap and book value

Old equipment has a book value of 80,000.00 and would fetch 15,000.00 in scrap if the part were bought. Which of the two numbers enters the make-or-buy decision?

Solution

The book value of 80,000.00 is sunk, because it is incurred whichever alternative is chosen. The scrap of 15,000.00 is a future cash flow that buying causes, so it is relevant and enters the net cost of buying as a reduction. The book value enters neither alternative, and so it cannot change the choice.

M3 · Keep or drop

Product line C has revenue of 50,000.00, variable cost of 30,000.00, avoidable fixed cost of 25,000.00 and allocated common cost of 10,000.00 that stays if C is dropped. Keep or drop?

Solution

The contribution is \(50{,}000 - 30{,}000 = 20{,}000\). The avoidable fixed cost is 25,000.00, which is more than the contribution. Keeping C gives profit of \(20{,}000 - 25{,}000 - 10{,}000 = -15{,}000\), and dropping it gives \(-10{,}000\). Dropping C improves profit by 5,000.00, so drop it. The allocated common cost of 10,000.00 is the same under both alternatives and does not affect the answer.

Proof

P1 · Ranking invariance

Show that if a constant \(K\) is added to the total cost of both alternatives, the ranking of the alternatives does not change, and that the ranking is the same as the ranking by \(R_A\) and \(R_B\).

Solution

Let \(T_A = K + R_A\) and \(T_B = K + R_B\). Then \(T_A < T_B\) if and only if \(K + R_A < K + R_B\), which holds if and only if \(R_A < R_B\), by subtracting \(K\) from both sides. The ranking by totals therefore equals the ranking by relevant costs for every value of \(K\), so adding a common constant cannot change the ranking.

P2 · Sunk cost has zero effect

In Proposition 2.2 let the book value be \(B\), and suppose \(B\) enters both the cost of making and the cost of buying with the same coefficient \(\beta\). Show that \(\Delta\) does not depend on \(\beta\).

Solution

The cost of making becomes \(Q(m+l+v) + F + \beta B\), and the net cost of buying becomes \(Qp - S + \beta B\). Subtracting, the terms in \(\beta B\) cancel, giving \(\Delta = Q(m+l+v-p) + F + S\), which contains neither \(\beta\) nor \(B\). A cost that enters both alternatives with the same coefficient is a common cost, so it drops out of every difference.

P3 · The accept threshold

Using Proposition 4.1, find the contribution per hour \(c^\ast\) of the displaced product at which accepting the order is exactly neutral, when \(C = 15{,}000\), \(h = 6{,}000\) and \(H = 4{,}000\).

Solution

Neutrality requires \(C - c^\ast\max(0, h-H) = 0\). With \(h - H = 2{,}000\), this gives \(c^\ast = 15{,}000 / 2{,}000 = 7.50\) CU per machine hour. The displaced product must earn more than 7.50 per hour for the order to be rejected, and at the lab's 5.00 per hour the order is accepted.

Empirical

E1 · Break-even buy price on the SIMULATED plant

Using the Lab 1 inputs, find the buy price per unit at which the plant is indifferent between making and buying. Check the answer by recomputing \(\Delta\).

Solution

Indifference means \(\Delta = 0\). With \(Q = 20{,}000\), \(m+l+v = 8.50\), \(F = 12{,}000\) and \(S = 3{,}000\), solving \(20{,}000(8.50 - p) + 12{,}000 + 3{,}000 = 0\) gives \(p = 8.50 + 15{,}000/20{,}000 = 9.25\). Recomputing: \(20{,}000(8.50 - 9.25) + 15{,}000 = -15{,}000 + 15{,}000 = 0\). Lab 1 prints the same break-even price, 9.25.

E2 · Spare capacity doubled

On the Lab 1 special order, suppose the plant had 6,000 spare hours instead of 4,000. Find the net effect of accepting, and say whether the decision changes.

Solution

The excess hours are \(6{,}000 - 6{,}000 = 0\), so no product D hours are displaced and the lost contribution is 0. The net effect is the full order contribution of 15,000.00 CU. The decision is still accept, and it is accepted by a larger margin, because the order no longer displaces anything.

E3 · Common cost in the keep-or-drop test

On the Lab 1 product line B, raise the plant-level fixed cost allocated to B from 3,000.00 to 5,000.00 and report whether the keep-or-drop decision changes.

Solution

Keeping gives profit \(6{,}000 - 4{,}000 - 5{,}000 = -3{,}000\), and dropping gives \(-5{,}000\). The decision is still keep, because the 5,000.00 stays under both alternatives. The change moves reported operating income to \(-3{,}000\) and leaves the decision unchanged. Lab 1 prints the 3,000.00 version; the 5,000.00 version follows from the same formula.

Research

R1 · When does the scrap cash matter

Suppose the equipment's scrap value would be received in a later year if the part were bought, and nothing would be received if the part were made. Explain how the make-or-buy comparison changes, and state the information needed to make it.

Solution

The scrap is then a future flow at a later date, not in the same year as the purchase cost. The one-year identity of Proposition 2.2 no longer holds as written, because the cash flows fall at different times. To compare them, the analyst needs the timing of each flow and a discount rate, and then compares present values. The lab has neither a horizon nor a rate, so the answer for this case is not computed here.

R2 · Opportunity cost of the equipment

Suppose the equipment could instead produce another product with a contribution of 40,000.00 a year. Use the Lab 1 figures, and find which alternative is cheaper once that use is counted.

Solution

Making uses the equipment, so it forgoes the 40,000.00 contribution of the other product. Its relevant cost is \(182{,}000 + 40{,}000 = 222{,}000\). Buying leaves the equipment free for the other product, so its cost is \(210{,}000 - 40{,}000 = 170{,}000\), with no scrap received. Buying is cheaper by 52,000. The scrap value stopped being the right measure of what buying gives up, because the alternative use is larger. Making stays the cheaper choice only if the other use earns less than 25,000.00 a year.

R3 · A bottleneck that moves

Explain why the special-order test of Proposition 4.1 can give the wrong answer when two constraints bind at once, such as machine hours and a labour-hour limit.

Solution

Proposition 4.1 uses one constraint and one displaced product. With two binding constraints, the displaced product may differ by constraint, and the order may need hours from two resources. The opportunity cost of each resource is then a shadow price from a linear program, and one per-hour rate does not describe both. The one-constraint test is exact only when a single resource is short and the displaced product is the same for every hour lost.
