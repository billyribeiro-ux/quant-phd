Activity-Based Costing

  • Uses: fixed, variable and mixed costs, contribution margin, relevant cost
  • Introduces: activity-based costing (a two-stage allocation of overhead through activity cost pools and their drivers)
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or simulation
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A product's cost depends on how overhead is spread across products, and a common spread can make a small, complex product look profitable while a large, simple product carries its cost. This lesson shows how a single volume driver produces that result on a SIMULATED plant, proves that a two-stage allocation spends the whole overhead pool, and measures the cross-subsidy between the two products. It also shows where the method is weak: its answer is only as good as its driver counts.

1Pools, drivers and the two-stage allocation

Overhead is the cost of resources shared by several products. A cost pool is a group of overhead costs that share one cause, and a cost driver is the measured quantity that drives the pool's cost, such as the number of setups or the number of inspections. A volume-based method puts all overhead into one pool and spreads it by a volume measure, such as direct labour hours. An activity-based method keeps several pools, each with its own driver.

Definition 1.1 (activity-based costing)

Activity-based costing is a two-stage allocation. Stage one assigns each activity cost pool to the products in proportion to each product's count of that pool's driver. Stage two divides the amount assigned to a product by the product's units to give overhead per unit.

Proposition 1.2 (the two-stage allocation spends the whole pool)

Let pools indexed by \(k\) from 1 to \(K\) have costs \(C_k\), let \(N_{kp}\) be product \(p\)'s driver count for pool \(k\), and let \(N_k = \sum_p N_{kp} > 0\). Stage one assigns \(A_p = \sum_k C_k\, N_{kp}/N_k\) to product \(p\). Then \(\sum_p A_p = \sum_k C_k\), the total overhead.

Proof

Exchange the order of summation: \(\sum_p A_p = \sum_p \sum_k C_k N_{kp}/N_k = \sum_k (C_k/N_k) \sum_p N_{kp}\). By definition \(\sum_p N_{kp} = N_k\), so each inner term is \(C_k\) and the sum is \(\sum_k C_k\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.3 (a rate must divide exactly)

The rate for pool \(k\) is \(C_k/N_k\). Lab 1 requires this division to be exact in cents and raises an error otherwise, so that no rounding can break the identity of Proposition 1.2. Rounding an overhead rate in a real system is a practical convention; the proposition is about the exact arithmetic.

2A SIMULATED plant with two products

Product X is a high-volume product: 10,000 units a year, direct cost 12.00 per unit and price 20.00 per unit. It uses 8,000 direct labour hours. Product Y is a low-volume product with a complex setup: 2,000 units a year, direct cost 15.00 per unit and price 30.00 per unit, using 2,000 direct labour hours. Total overhead is 100,000.00 CU a year. All figures are SIMULATED. Money is in currency units (CU), with two decimals.

Example 2.1 (volume-based costing)

Spread the 100,000.00 over the 10,000 direct labour hours, a rate of 10.00 per hour. Product X receives \(8{,}000 \times 10.00 = 80{,}000\), which is 8.00 per unit. Product Y receives \(2{,}000 \times 10.00 = 20{,}000\), which is 10.00 per unit. Product Y's full unit cost is \(15.00 + 10.00 = 25.00\) against a price of 30.00, so it appears to earn 5.00 per unit.

On this plant the overhead comes from setups, inspections and machine hours, and their counts are not proportional to labour hours (Proposition 3.2). Y uses 60 setups and 300 inspections for its 2,000 units, while X uses 20 setups and 100 inspections for its 10,000 units. The next example puts each pool's cost on the products that cause it.

Example 2.2 (two-stage activity-based costing)

The plant has three pools. Setups cost 40,000.00 and are driven by setup count, with X at 20 setups and Y at 60, so the rate is \(40{,}000 / 80 = 500.00\) per setup. Inspections cost 20,000.00 and are driven by inspection count, with X at 100 and Y at 300, so the rate is \(20{,}000 / 400 = 50.00\) per inspection. Machine hours cost 40,000.00 and are driven by machine hours, with X at 6,000 and Y at 2,000, so the rate is \(40{,}000 / 8{,}000 = 5.00\) per hour. Stage one gives X \(10{,}000 + 5{,}000 + 30{,}000 = 45{,}000\) and Y \(30{,}000 + 15{,}000 + 10{,}000 = 55{,}000\). Stage two gives X 4.50 per unit and Y 27.50 per unit. The total is 100,000.00, as Proposition 1.2 requires.

Under activity-based costing Y's full unit cost is \(15.00 + 27.50 = 42.50\) against a price of 30.00. The same product that looked profitable under volume-based costing loses 12.50 per unit.

3The cross-subsidy and when the two methods agree

Proposition 3.1 (the methods reallocate, they do not change the total)

Let \(V_p\) and \(A_p\) be the overhead of product \(p\) under volume-based and activity-based costing. Then \(\sum_p (A_p - V_p) = 0\). The gains and losses across the products cancel.

Proof

Both methods allocate the whole pool, so \(\sum_p V_p = \sum_p A_p = \sum_k C_k\), by Proposition 1.2 for the activity method and by the same exchange of summation for the single pool. Subtracting, \(\sum_p (A_p - V_p) = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

On the Lab 1 figures, \(A_X - V_X = 45{,}000 - 80{,}000 = -35{,}000\) and \(A_Y - V_Y = 55{,}000 - 20{,}000 = +35{,}000\). Volume-based costing charges X 35,000.00 more than its activity cost, and it charges Y 35,000.00 less. That transfer is the cross-subsidy: under volume-based costing, the high-volume product pays part of the cost that the low-volume product causes.

Proposition 3.2 (when the methods agree)

If, for every pool \(k\), the driver counts are proportional to the volume driver, \(N_{kp} = s_k\,h_p\) for constants \(s_k > 0\) and direct labour hours \(h_p\), then \(A_p = V_p\) for every product.

Proof

With \(N_{kp} = s_k h_p\), we have \(N_k = s_k H\) where \(H = \sum_p h_p\). Then \(A_p = \sum_k C_k\, s_k h_p/(s_k H) = (h_p/H)\sum_k C_k\). This is the volume-based allocation, since \(V_p = (h_p/H)\sum_k C_k\). The Lab 1 counts are not proportional, which is why the hypothesis fails there. Setups per labour hour are \(20/8{,}000 = 0.0025\) for X and \(60/2{,}000 = 0.03\) for Y, and the two methods give different allocations on those counts. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.2 is a statement about when the answer does not change. It says the volume driver is a special case of the activity method, the one in which every pool moves with labour hours. Whether a real plant is in that special case is a question about its data, and the lesson's SIMULATED case is not in it.

Lab 1 · Volume-based and activity-based unit costs on two SIMULATED products

Before running: both methods should allocate exactly 100,000.00 CU. Volume-based costing should give X 8.00 and Y 10.00 of overhead per unit, and activity-based costing should give X 4.50 and Y 27.50. The activity-based overhead of X should be 35,000.00 below its volume-based overhead, and Y's should be 35,000.00 above. Y's annual margin should be positive under volume-based costing and negative under activity-based costing. Cutting Y's setups from 60 to 20 should lower Y's activity-based unit overhead to 22.50.

"""AC4 lab L04 (lab 1): volume-based and activity-based unit costs for two SIMULATED products.

SIMULATED: the two products, the overhead pools and every driver count are invented inputs of a hypothetical plant.
Nothing here is a company's data, and no network or file is read. Money is integer cents (100 cents = 1 currency
unit, CU). Every allocation divides exactly; a rate that does not divide evenly raises ValueError, so no
rounding can hide a reconciliation error. Standard library only.

Two methods are compared on the same overhead pool of 100,000.00 CU. Volume-based costing spreads the pool by one
volume driver (direct labour hours). Activity-based costing (two stages) first assigns each activity pool to products by
its own driver, then divides by product volume. The lab checks that each method allocates the whole pool, and prints
the cross-subsidy between the two products.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Product:
    """Annual volume and direct figures for one product. Money in cents."""

    name: str
    units: int
    direct_cost_per_unit: int
    price_per_unit: int
    labour_hours: int  # direct labour hours in total for the year (volume driver)


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class ActivityPool:
    """One activity cost pool with its cost driver counts per product, in cents."""

    name: str
    cost: int
    driver_counts: tuple[int, ...]  # one count per product, same order as the products


def fmt(cents: int) -> str:
    """Render integer cents as CU with two decimals, without rounding."""
    sign = "-" if cents < 0 else ""
    magnitude = abs(cents)
    return f"{sign}{magnitude // 100:,}.{magnitude % 100:02d}"


def exact_rate(cost: int, driver_total: int, label: str) -> int:
    """Cost per driver unit in cents. Raises ValueError unless the division is exact."""
    if driver_total <= 0:
        raise ValueError(f"{label}: the driver total must be positive")
    if cost % driver_total != 0:
        raise ValueError(f"{label}: {cost} does not divide evenly by {driver_total} driver units")
    return cost // driver_total


def volume_based(products: tuple[Product, ...], pool: int) -> dict[str, int]:
    """Overhead per product in cents: the pool spread by direct labour hours."""
    total_hours = sum(p.labour_hours for p in products)
    rate = exact_rate(pool, total_hours, "labour-hour rate")
    return {p.name: rate * p.labour_hours for p in products}


def activity_based(products: tuple[Product, ...], pools: tuple[ActivityPool, ...]) -> dict[str, int]:
    """Overhead per product in cents: stage one assigns each pool by its driver counts, stage two sums per product."""
    totals = {p.name: 0 for p in products}
    for pool in pools:
        if len(pool.driver_counts) != len(products):
            raise ValueError(f"{pool.name}: one driver count per product is required")
        rate = exact_rate(pool.cost, sum(pool.driver_counts), pool.name)
        for product, count in zip(products, pool.driver_counts, strict=True):
            totals[product.name] += rate * count
    return totals


def unit_cost(product: Product, overhead: int) -> int:
    """Full unit cost in cents: direct cost plus overhead per unit, with exact division."""
    if overhead % product.units != 0:
        raise ValueError(f"{product.name}: overhead does not divide evenly by units")
    return product.direct_cost_per_unit + overhead // product.units


def unit_margin(product: Product, overhead: int) -> int:
    """Price less full unit cost, in cents per unit."""
    return product.price_per_unit - unit_cost(product, overhead)


def main() -> None:
    pool_total = 10_000_000
    x = Product("X", units=10_000, direct_cost_per_unit=1_200, price_per_unit=2_000, labour_hours=8_000)
    y = Product("Y", units=2_000, direct_cost_per_unit=1_500, price_per_unit=3_000, labour_hours=2_000)
    products = (x, y)
    pools = (
        ActivityPool("setups", 4_000_000, (20, 60)),
        ActivityPool("inspections", 2_000_000, (100, 300)),
        ActivityPool("machine hours", 4_000_000, (6_000, 2_000)),
    )

    print("Overhead pool, total: " + fmt(pool_total) + " (SIMULATED)")
    print("Product inputs (SIMULATED)")
    for p in products:
        print(
            f"  {p.name}: units {p.units:,}, direct cost {fmt(p.direct_cost_per_unit)}, "
            f"price {fmt(p.price_per_unit)}, labour hours {p.labour_hours:,}"
        )

    vol = volume_based(products, pool_total)
    print("Volume-based costing (direct labour hours)")
    print(f"  labour-hour rate: {fmt(pool_total // sum(p.labour_hours for p in products))} per hour")
    for p in products:
        print(f"  {p.name}: overhead {fmt(vol[p.name])}, per unit {fmt(vol[p.name] // p.units)}")
    print(f"  allocated total: {fmt(sum(vol.values()))}, equals pool: {sum(vol.values()) == pool_total}")

    act = activity_based(products, pools)
    print("Activity-based costing (two stages)")
    for pool in pools:
        rate = exact_rate(pool.cost, sum(pool.driver_counts), pool.name)
        print(f"  {pool.name}: pool {fmt(pool.cost)}, rate {fmt(rate)} per driver unit")
        for p, count in zip(products, pool.driver_counts, strict=True):
            print(f"    {p.name}: {count:,} drivers, {fmt(rate * count)}")
    for p in products:
        print(f"  {p.name}: overhead {fmt(act[p.name])}, per unit {fmt(act[p.name] // p.units)}")
    print(f"  allocated total: {fmt(sum(act.values()))}, equals pool: {sum(act.values()) == pool_total}")

    print("Cross-subsidy and unit margins")
    for p in products:
        shift = act[p.name] - vol[p.name]
        print(f"  {p.name}: activity-based minus volume-based overhead {fmt(shift)}")
        print(f"    unit margin, volume-based: {fmt(unit_margin(p, vol[p.name]))}")
        print(f"    unit margin, activity-based: {fmt(unit_margin(p, act[p.name]))}")
    profit_vol = {p.name: unit_margin(p, vol[p.name]) * p.units for p in products}
    profit_act = {p.name: unit_margin(p, act[p.name]) * p.units for p in products}
    print(f"  annual margin, volume-based: X {fmt(profit_vol['X'])}, Y {fmt(profit_vol['Y'])}")
    print(f"  annual margin, activity-based: X {fmt(profit_act['X'])}, Y {fmt(profit_act['Y'])}")
    total_margin = sum(profit_vol.values())
    print(f"  total margin, both methods: {fmt(total_margin)} and {fmt(sum(profit_act.values()))}")
    print(f"  totals agree: {total_margin == sum(profit_act.values())}")
    y_break_vol = y.direct_cost_per_unit + vol["Y"] // y.units
    y_break_act = y.direct_cost_per_unit + act["Y"] // y.units
    print(
        f"  price at which Y breaks even per unit: volume-based {fmt(y_break_vol)}, activity-based {fmt(y_break_act)}"
    )

    sens_pools = (
        ActivityPool("setups", 4_000_000, (20, 20)),
        pools[1],
        pools[2],
    )
    sens = activity_based(products, sens_pools)
    print("Sensitivity: Y setups cut from 60 to 20 (same pools)")
    for p in products:
        print(f"  {p.name}: overhead {fmt(sens[p.name])}, per unit {fmt(sens[p.name] // p.units)}")
    print(f"  allocated total: {fmt(sum(sens.values()))}, equals pool: {sum(sens.values()) == pool_total}")

4Which costs a decision can avoid

An activity-based unit cost is an allocation. Its full amount is not what disappears if a product is dropped. Lesson 3 gives the test: only the costs that stop when the product stops are relevant. Example 4.1 applies that test to Y, under a stated assumption about which pools stop.

Example 4.1 (dropping Y, with an avoidability assumption)

Assume that dropping Y removes its 60 setups and its 300 inspections, so setup and inspection costs fall by \(30{,}000 + 15{,}000 = 45{,}000\), but that the machines run for X either way, so the machine cost of 10,000 for Y stays. Y's contribution is \(2{,}000 \times 15.00 = 30{,}000\). Keeping Y gives a result of \(30{,}000 - 45{,}000 - 10{,}000 = -25{,}000\), which is the activity-based margin of Lab 1. Dropping Y gives \(-10{,}000\). Dropping Y improves firm profit by 15,000.00, not by the 25,000.00 that the activity-based margin would suggest if all 55,000.00 disappeared.

Remark 4.2 (the assumption is the decision)

The 15,000.00 depends on which pools stop, and the activity-based figures do not say which. The lab has no information about the machines' cost behaviour. The point of Example 4.1 is that the decision needs an avoidability statement for each pool, which the allocation cannot supply.

5Where the canon breaks

The method's output is an exact function of its inputs, and the inputs are the weak point. Four limits follow from the structure of the calculation, and the lab does not test any of them against a real plant.

  • The driver counts are measured, and they can be wrong. Lab 1 shows that cutting Y's setups from 60 to 20 moves Y's activity-based overhead from 55,000.00 to 45,000.00 and its unit overhead from 27.50 to 22.50. An error in the count changes the answer, as Problem R2 computes. The counts come from records the method does not check.
  • One driver per pool assumes the pool is homogeneous. A pool that mixes two activities with different drivers allocates one of them wrongly. The two-stage calculation cannot detect this, because it sees one driver per pool.
  • Measuring the drivers has a cost. Counting setups and inspections takes records and time. The lab does not charge that cost against the gain in accuracy, so it does not show that the method pays for itself.
  • An allocated cost is not an avoidable cost. Example 4.1 shows that the activity-based margin overstates what dropping a product saves, when part of each pool is fixed. The relevant-cost test of Lesson 3 is the right test for a decision, and the activity-based unit cost is the right input for a pricing or cost-to-serve discussion only with that caveat.

6Misconceptions

  1. “Volume-based allocation is accurate because labour hours are a real measure of the work.”

    Labour hours measure the work only when overhead rises with work. Lab 1's Y uses 2,000 labour hours and 60 setups, while X uses 8,000 labour hours and 20 setups. The volume rate charges Y 10.00 of overhead per unit, against 27.50 under the activity method, because Y's setups and inspections are many relative to its labour hours.

  2. “If total overhead is the same under both methods, product costs cannot differ much.”

    Proposition 3.1 says the totals agree and the splits need not. Lab 1 moves 35,000.00 of overhead between X and Y, which changes Y's unit cost by 17.50 (from 10.00 to 27.50 per unit of overhead).

  3. “The full activity-based cost of a product is the saving from dropping it.”

    Example 4.1 shows that the saving is the avoidable part only. Of Y's 55,000.00 of allocated overhead, 45,000.00 is assumed to stop, and the 10,000.00 of machine cost stays. The saving is 15,000.00 under that assumption, not 55,000.00.

7Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · Setup rate

A setup pool costs 60,000.00 CU. Product A has 50 setups and product B has 100. What is the rate per setup, and what does each product receive?

Solution

The total count is \(50 + 100 = 150\), so the rate is \(60{,}000 / 150 = 400.00\) CU per setup. Product A receives \(50 \times 400 = 20{,}000\) and product B receives \(100 \times 400 = 40{,}000\). The two amounts sum to 60,000, as Proposition 1.2 requires.

M2 · Volume-based rate per unit

An overhead pool of 30,000.00 CU is spread by machine hours. Product A uses 3,000 hours and produces 1,000 units. Product B uses 1,000 hours and produces 500 units. What is the overhead per unit of each product?

Solution

The rate is \(30{,}000 / 4{,}000 = 7.50\) CU per hour. Product A receives \(3{,}000 \times 7.50 = 22{,}500\), which is 22.50 per unit. Product B receives \(1{,}000 \times 7.50 = 7{,}500\), which is 15.00 per unit. The total is 30,000.00.

M3 · Full unit cost

A product has direct cost of 8.00 per unit and receives 14,000.00 of activity-based overhead over 700 units. What is its full unit cost?

Solution

Overhead per unit is \(14{,}000 / 700 = 20.00\) CU. The full unit cost is \(8.00 + 20.00 = 28.00\) CU. The overhead is spread over units only after the activity stage, so the 20.00 is the product's share of its own pools.

Proof

P1 · Conservation for a single pool

Show that Proposition 1.2 with \(K = 1\) gives the volume-based allocation of the same pool, whenever the single driver is the volume driver.

Solution

With one pool, \(A_p = C_1 N_{1p}/N_1\). If the single driver is the volume driver, \(N_{1p} = h_p\), so \(A_p = C_1 h_p / \sum_q h_q\). This is the volume-based allocation of Example 2.1, which spreads the pool by labour hours. Both formulas sum to \(C_1\).

P2 · The cross-subsidy has zero sum

Suppose there are two products. Show that the activity-based gain of one product equals the activity-based loss of the other when both methods allocate the same pool.

Solution

Proposition 3.1 gives \(\sum_p (A_p - V_p) = 0\). With two products, \((A_1 - V_1) + (A_2 - V_2) = 0\), so \(A_1 - V_1 = -(A_2 - V_2)\). The gain of one equals the loss of the other. Lab 1 shows \(-35{,}000\) for X and \(+35{,}000\) for Y.

P3 · Proportional counts

Suppose setups for X and Y were 2 per 1,000 labour hours for both products. Show that the activity method then gives the same overhead as the volume method for this pool.

Solution

Setups are then \(N_{1p} = 0.002\,h_p\), a proportional count with \(s_1 = 0.002\). Proposition 3.2 gives \(A_p = (h_p/H)\,C_1\), which is the volume allocation. Check: X has 8,000 hours and therefore 16 setups, and Y has 2,000 hours and therefore 4 setups. Their shares are 16/20 and 4/20, which equal 8,000/10,000 and 2,000/10,000.

Empirical

E1 · Moving cost between pools

Using the Lab 1 inputs, suppose the inspection pool of 20,000.00 is moved into the machine pool, so that inspections cost nothing and the machine pool is 60,000.00. The setup pool stays at 40,000.00. Find the overhead per unit of each product.

Solution

The machine rate is \(60{,}000 / 8{,}000 = 7.50\) per hour. X receives setups 10,000 and machines \(6{,}000 \times 7.50 = 45{,}000\), a total of 55,000, which is 5.50 per unit. Y receives setups 30,000 and machines \(2{,}000 \times 7.50 = 15{,}000\), a total of 45,000, which is 22.50 per unit. The total is 100,000.00, so the pool is still fully allocated.

E2 · Halving Y's inspections

Using the Lab 1 inputs, suppose Y had 150 inspections instead of 300, with the same 20,000.00 pool. Find the inspection rate and the overhead per unit of each product.

Solution

Total inspections are \(100 + 150 = 250\), so the rate is \(20{,}000 / 250 = 80.00\) per inspection. X receives \(100 \times 80 = 8{,}000\) and Y receives \(150 \times 80 = 12{,}000\). The inspection share of Y falls, but the rate rises because the count fell, so X's share rises. X's full overhead is \(10{,}000 + 8{,}000 + 30{,}000 = 48{,}000\), which is 4.80 per unit. Y's is \(30{,}000 + 12{,}000 + 10{,}000 = 52{,}000\), which is 26.00 per unit. The total is 100,000.00.

E3 · What dropping Y saves

Using Example 4.1, suppose the 10,000.00 of machine cost for Y did become avoidable on dropping Y. Find the change in firm profit from dropping Y, and say which assumption the answer depends on.

Solution

If all 55,000.00 of Y's allocated overhead stopped, keeping Y gives \(30{,}000 - 55{,}000 = -25{,}000\) and dropping it gives 0, so the change is +25,000.00. The answer depends on the avoidability of the machine cost, which the lab does not model. Under the assumption of Example 4.1, the machine cost stays and the change is +15,000.00.

Research

R1 · Cost to serve or cost to avoid

Explain why the activity-based unit cost of Y is a measure of cost to serve, and why it is not a measure of what a decision avoids. Design a test that would tell the two apart for a real plant.

Solution

The activity cost assigns every pool cost to Y in proportion to its driver use, including cost that exists whether Y is made or not. That is a cost to serve. What a decision avoids is the part of each pool that falls when Y's drivers fall. A test would record, for each pool, the cost before and after a reduction in Y's driver count, and count only the change. Plant data showing that a pool's cost does not move with its driver would show that the cost is fixed in the short run.

R2 · Error in a driver count

Suppose Y's setup count is measured with an error of plus or minus 10 setups. How much can Y's activity-based unit overhead change, and which information would reduce the error?

Solution

On the Lab 1 figures, Y's setup overhead is \(60 \times 500 = 30{,}000\). If the true count is 50 or 70, with X's count fixed at 20, the rate is \(40{,}000/70 = 571.43\) or \(40{,}000/90 = 444.44\) per setup. Y's setup overhead is then \(50 \times 571.43 = 28{,}571.43\) or \(70 \times 444.44 = 31{,}111.11\), against the 30,000.00 allocated at the recorded count of 60. That is a change of about \(-1{,}429\) or \(+1{,}111\), or about \(-0.71\) or \(+0.56\) per unit. The error is reduced by counting setups from the machine log or the work orders, and by reconciling the count with the labour records before the rate is set.

R3 · Mixed pools

Describe a pool that mixes setup activity with machine maintenance, and explain what a second driver would require. State the test that would show whether splitting the pool changes the answer.

Solution

Setups and maintenance may be driven by different counts, so one pool allocates maintenance by setup counts, or the reverse. Splitting the pool requires a count for each activity and a cost for each, which the plant's records may not give separately. The test compares the allocation under the mixed pool with the allocation under the split pools, product by product. If any product's overhead changes by more than the decision would tolerate, the split matters for that decision.
