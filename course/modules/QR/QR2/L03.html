Transaction Costs & Non-Linear Market Impact

A quantitative strategy that is profitable on paper frequently collapses when deployed with real capital because transaction friction was modeled naively. While exchange fees and bid-ask spreads scale linearly with trading volume, market impact scales non-linearly with order participation. As assets under management increase, the square-root law of market impact accelerates total execution drag superlinearly, establishing a hard capacity ceiling beyond which every additional dollar of capital destroys risk-adjusted returns.

1. Mathematical Architecture of Backtest Cost Models

In production quantitative research, transaction costs represent the divergence between theoretical portfolio returns and realized brokerage equity. Following Perold's implementation shortfall accounting [perold1988implementation], execution drag decomposes into explicit brokerage fees, bid-ask spread crossing, and price impact.

Backtest Transaction-Cost Model

A backtest transaction-cost model is a mapping \(\Phi: (q, P, V, \sigma) \to \R_{\ge 0}^3\) that assigns to an order of \(q \in \R\) shares at prevailing price \(P > 0\), market volume \(V > 0\), and volatility \(\sigma > 0\), a three-component friction tuple \((\text{Fee}, \text{Spread}, \text{Impact})\):

\[ \text{Cost}(q, P, V, \sigma) = \text{Fee}(q, P) + \text{Spread}(q, P) + \text{Impact}(q, P, V, \sigma) \]
  1. Brokerage Commission and Exchange Fees: \[ \text{Fee}(q, P) = c_{\text{bps}} \times 10^{-4} \cdot |q| \cdot P + c_{\text{fixed}} \]
  2. Bid-Ask Spread Half-Cost: \[ \text{Spread}(q, P) = \frac{1}{2} s_{\text{bps}} \times 10^{-4} \cdot |q| \cdot P \]
  3. Nonlinear Market Impact Cost: \[ \text{Impact}(q, P, V, \sigma) = |q| \cdot |\Delta P_{\text{impact}}| \]

2. The Square-Root Law of Market Impact

Extensive empirical microstructure research demonstrates that price impact does not scale linearly with order size. When an institutional trader executes an order of size \(Q\) over a trading interval with total volume \(V\), the expected price concession follows an anomalous universal square-root scaling [almgren2001optimal], [toth2011anomalous]:

Theorem 3.1 · Empirical Square-Root Price Impact [toth2011anomalous]

Let an order of \(q\) shares be executed in an asset with daily volume \(V\), daily price volatility \(\sigma\), and reference price \(P\). The realized price dislocation \(\Delta P_{\text{impact}}\) satisfies:

\[ \Delta P_{\text{impact}} = \operatorname{sign}(q) \cdot Y \cdot \sigma \cdot P \cdot \sqrt{\frac{|q|}{V}} \]

where \(Y \approx 0.5\) is a dimensionless universal scaling coefficient. Consequently, the total dollar friction absorbed by the order scales superlinearly with quantity as:

\[ \text{DollarImpact}(q) = |q| \cdot |\Delta P_{\text{impact}}| = Y \cdot \sigma \cdot P \cdot \frac{|q|^{1.5}}{\sqrt{V}} \]

Proof

Multiplying the per-share price dislocation \(|\Delta P_{\text{impact}}| = Y \sigma P (|q|/V)^{0.5}\) by the total number of executed shares \(|q|\) yields \(|q| \times Y \sigma P |q|^{0.5} V^{-0.5} = Y \sigma P |q|^{1.5} V^{-0.5}\). Because \(1.5 > 1\), the marginal cost of trading an additional share \(\frac{\partial}{\partial |q|} \text{DollarImpact} = 1.5 Y \sigma P \sqrt{|q| / V}\) is strictly increasing in order size \(|q|\), proving superlinear scaling.

Theorem 3.2 · Strategy Capacity Exhaustion

Let a strategy manage capital \(A\) with daily portfolio turnover ratio \(\theta \in (0, 1]\), gross daily expected return \(\mu_{\text{gross}} > 0\), and return volatility \(\sigma_{\text{gross}}\). Under the square-root impact law, the net annualized Sharpe ratio \(\mathrm{SR}_{\text{net}}(A)\) is a strictly concave function of assets under management \(A\), reaching an optimal capacity ceiling \(A^*\) beyond which net risk-adjusted returns collapse.

Proof

Daily traded notional is \(\theta A\), corresponding to share quantity \(q = \theta A / P\). Linear costs scale as \(c_{\text{lin}} \theta A\). Impact cost scales as \(c_{\text{imp}} P (\theta A / P)^{1.5} V^{-0.5} = \kappa A^{1.5}\). The net expected return is \(\mu_{\text{net}}(A) = \mu_{\text{gross}} - c_{\text{lin}} \theta - \kappa A^{0.5}\). Assuming portfolio return variance is dominated by market noise \(\sigma_{\text{gross}}^2\), the net Sharpe ratio is:

\[ \mathrm{SR}_{\text{net}}(A) = \frac{\mu_{\text{gross}} - c_{\text{lin}} \theta - \kappa \sqrt{A}}{\sigma_{\text{gross}}} \sqrt{252} \]

Taking the first and second derivatives with respect to \(A\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathrm{SR}_{\text{net}}}{\partial A} = -\frac{\kappa \sqrt{252}}{2 \sigma_{\text{gross}} \sqrt{A}} < 0, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \mathrm{SR}_{\text{net}}}{\partial A^2} = \frac{\kappa \sqrt{252}}{4 \sigma_{\text{gross}} A^{1.5}} > 0 \]

Because the second derivative of total dollar net profit \(\Pi(A) = A \cdot \mu_{\text{net}}(A) = A(\mu_{\text{gross}} - c_{\text{lin}}\theta) - \kappa A^{1.5}\) is strictly negative (\(\Pi''(A) = -0.75 \kappa A^{-0.5} < 0\)), total dollar profit is strictly concave and achieves a unique maximum at \(A^* = \left(\frac{2(\mu_{\text{gross}} - c_{\text{lin}}\theta)}{3\kappa}\right)^2\).

3. Empirical Lab · Market Impact and Capacity Scaling

We now simulate how strategy Sharpe ratio and dollar execution drag evolve across five capital tiers under the square-root impact model.

Lab 1 · Market Impact and Nonlinear Capacity Scaling

Under the empirical square-root law of market impact, execution drag scales superlinearly with capital, compressing net strategy Sharpe ratio from profitable (>1.5) to marginal (<1.0) as portfolio capital scales from $100k to $50M.

"""Lab QR2-L03-1: Market Impact and Nonlinear Capacity Scaling.

Demonstrates the degradation of net Sharpe ratio and strategy capacity limits
under the square-root law of market impact.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def simulate_strategy_capacity(
    capital: float,
    daily_volume: float = 1_000_000.0,
    daily_vol: float = 0.02,
    y_coeff: float = 0.5,
    commission_bps: float = 5.0,
    half_spread_bps: float = 2.0,
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    """Computes gross Sharpe, fees bps, impact bps, and net Sharpe for given capital."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(202)
    n_days = 250
    p0 = 100.0

    # Strategy with positive gross edge (annual gross Sharpe ~ 1.5)
    gross_returns = rng.normal(0.0006, 0.01, size=n_days)
    gross_sharpe = float(np.mean(gross_returns) / np.std(gross_returns, ddof=1) * math.sqrt(252))

    # Average daily turnover: 20% of portfolio traded daily
    turnover_ratio = 0.20
    trade_notional = capital * turnover_ratio
    trade_shares = trade_notional / p0

    # Fixed + Spread costs (bps)
    linear_bps = commission_bps + half_spread_bps

    # Square-root impact: Delta P = Y * vol * P * sqrt(Q / V)
    participation = trade_shares / daily_volume
    impact_pct = y_coeff * daily_vol * math.sqrt(participation)
    impact_bps = impact_pct * 1e4

    total_cost_bps = linear_bps + impact_bps
    daily_cost_ret = (total_cost_bps * 1e-4) * turnover_ratio
    net_returns = gross_returns - daily_cost_ret

    net_sharpe = float(np.mean(net_returns) / np.std(net_returns, ddof=1) * math.sqrt(252))
    return gross_sharpe, linear_bps, impact_bps, net_sharpe


def main() -> None:
    capitals = [100_000.0, 1_000_000.0, 5_000_000.0, 20_000_000.0, 50_000_000.0]

    print("Strategy Capacity & Market Impact Scaling:")
    print("----------------------------------------------------------------------")
    print(f"{'Capital ($)':<15}{'Gross SR':<12}{'Fixed (bps)':<14}{'Impact (bps)':<14}{'Net SR':<10}{'Capacity State'}")
    print("----------------------------------------------------------------------")

    for cap in capitals:
        g_sr, f_bps, i_bps, n_sr = simulate_strategy_capacity(cap)
        state = "Profitable" if n_sr > 1.0 else ("Marginal" if n_sr > 0.0 else "Capacity Violated")
        print(f"${cap:<14,.0f}{g_sr:<12.2f}{f_bps:<14.1f}{i_bps:<14.2f}{n_sr:<10.2f}{state}")

    print("----------------------------------------------------------------------")
    print("Invariant Verified: Nonlinear impact scales as sqrt(Q), causing superlinear cost drag.")


main()

The experiment confirms Theorem 3.2: linear fees remain fixed at 7.0 bps across all capital tiers, but market impact expands from 1.41 bps at $100,000 to 31.62 bps at $50,000,000. Consequently, the net annualized Sharpe ratio deteriorates from 1.61 down to 0.69, crossing the strategy capacity frontier. Flat-fee backtests hide this deterioration.

4. Where the Canon Breaks

The academic literature on factor investing frequently assumes a uniform trading cost of 5 basis points across all stocks [novymarx2015taxonomy], [patton2020you]. This simplification breaks down severely in practice:

  • Cross-sectional heterogeneity: Bid-ask spreads and market depth vary by orders of magnitude between mega-cap S&P 500 stocks (spreads < 1 bp) and Russell 2000 micro-caps (spreads > 50 bps). Applying a uniform 5 bps fee over-costs liquid leaders while under-costing illiquid small-caps.
  • Permanent vs. temporary impact dichotomy: The square-root law models instantaneous price displacement, but execution algorithms (such as Almgren-Chriss optimal execution schedules) must distinguish temporary impact (transient book replenishment) from permanent impact (adverse selection from informed order flow).
  • Short borrow availability and fee spikes: Hard-to-borrow equities incur annualized negative borrow rates ranging from 2% to over 50%, alongside locate recall risk. Assuming costless symmetric short positions produces spurious long-short anomaly profitability.

5. Misconceptions

  1. A constant basis-point fee model (e.g. 5 bps per trade) accurately represents execution friction for institutional capital.

    Constant basis-point fee models only account for linear brokerage and exchange tariffs. They completely ignore market impact, which scales as \(\sqrt{Q / V}\), leading to gross over-estimation of strategy capacity for large funds.

  2. Passive limit orders eliminate transaction costs because they do not cross the bid-ask spread.

    Resting limit orders do not pay the spread, but they suffer from adverse selection: limit orders are filled with high probability when the market moves against the trader, and left unfilled when the market moves favorably (Perold's opportunity cost).

  3. Market impact costs dissipate completely once an order finishes executing.

    While temporary impact decays as liquidity replenishes, permanent impact reflects information revealed to market participants through order flow, permanently shifting the equilibrium mid-price.

6. Practice Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An institutional fund purchases 10,000 shares of an asset trading at \$100. Average daily volume is 1,000,000 shares, daily volatility is 0.02, and \(Y = 0.5\). Calculate the expected price impact in dollars and in basis points.

Worked solution

Participation is \(q / V = 10{,}000 / 1{,}000{,}000 = 0.01\). The square root of participation is \(\sqrt{0.01} = 0.10\). Price impact is \(\Delta P = Y \cdot \sigma \cdot P \cdot \sqrt{q/V} = 0.5 \times 0.02 \times \$100 \times 0.10 = \$0.10\). In basis points, this represents \((\$0.10 / \$100) \times 10^4 = 10.0\) basis points.

For the order in Problem 1, what is the total dollar impact cost absorbed across the entire 10,000-share fill?

Worked solution

Total dollar impact cost is \(q \cdot \Delta P = 10{,}000 \times \$0.10 = \$1{,}000.00\). This matches the direct formula \(Y \sigma P q^{1.5} / \sqrt{V} = 0.5 \times 0.02 \times 100 \times 10{,}000^{1.5} / 1{,}000 = 1.0 \times 1{,}000{,}000 / 1{,}000 = \$1{,}000.00\).

If the trade size in Problem 1 increases by a factor of 9 to 90,000 shares, by what factor does total dollar impact cost increase?

Worked solution

Under the square-root impact law, total dollar impact cost scales as \(q^{1.5}\). Increasing trade quantity by a factor of 9 increases dollar impact cost by \(9^{1.5} = (\sqrt{9})^3 = 3^3 = 27\). The total dollar impact cost increases by a factor of 27 from \$1,000 to \$27,000.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that under the square-root law of market impact, the average execution cost per share \(\Delta P_{\text{impact}}\) is a concave function of order quantity \(q\).

Worked solution

Let \(f(q) = \Delta P_{\text{impact}} = c \sqrt{q}\) for \(q > 0\) where \(c = Y \sigma P / \sqrt{V} > 0\). The first derivative is \(f'(q) = \frac{c}{2 \sqrt{q}} > 0\). The second derivative is \(f''(q) = -\frac{c}{4 q^{1.5}} < 0\). Because the second derivative is strictly negative for all \(q > 0\), the per-share price impact is strictly concave in order size.

Prove that Perold's implementation shortfall identity accounts for 100% of the difference between paper return and realized portfolio return.

Worked solution

Let decision price be \(P_d\), arrival price be \(P_a\), executed shares be \(q_e\) at average price \(\bar{P}_e\), unexecuted shares be \(q_u = q - q_e\), final price be \(P_n\), and explicit fees be \(F\). Paper portfolio gain is \(q(P_n - P_d)\). Realized portfolio gain is \(q_e(P_n - \bar{P}_e) - F\). The difference is \(q(P_n - P_d) - [q_e(P_n - \bar{P}_e) - F] = q(P_a - P_d) + q_e(\bar{P}_e - P_a) + q_u(P_n - P_d) + F\), exactly decomposing into delay, impact, opportunity, and cash fee costs.

Prove that if market impact scaled linearly with volume participation (\(\Delta P \propto q/V\)), round-trip arbitrage across split orders would be impossible, whereas sub-linear impact requires trade scheduling.

Worked solution

Under linear impact \(\Delta P = \gamma q\), total cost for executing \(q\) shares in \(m\) equal chunks is \(m \cdot [\gamma (q/m)^2] = \gamma q^2 / m\). As \(m \to \infty\), execution cost would approach zero if price recovered instantaneously. Under square-root impact, instantaneous execution creates infinite instantaneous impact density, requiring calculus of variations (Almgren-Chriss framework) to balance execution risk against impact friction.

Tier 3 · Empirical

In Lab 1, why does market impact grow from 1.41 bps at \$100,000 capital to 31.62 bps at \$50,000,000 capital?

Worked solution

In Lab 1, daily volume is 1,000,000 shares and price is \$100. At \$100,000 capital with 20% daily turnover, traded shares are 200, representing participation of \(200 / 1{,}000{,}000 = 0.0002\). Impact is \(0.5 \times 0.02 \times \sqrt{0.0002} \times 10^4 \approx 1.41\) bps. At \$50,000,000 capital, traded shares are 100,000, participation is 0.10, and impact is \(0.5 \times 0.02 \times \sqrt{0.10} \times 10^4 \approx 31.62\) bps, scaling by \(\sqrt{500} \approx 22.36\).

How does the square-root impact model alter the optimal rebalancing frequency of a momentum strategy compared to a flat 5 bps assumption?

Worked solution

Under flat fees, high turnover frequencies (e.g. daily rebalancing) appear viable because costs scale linearly with turnover. Under square-root impact, large single-day rebalances incur punitive impact penalties. The strategy shifts to slower rebalancing intervals (e.g. weekly or monthly) or implements smooth execution filters (such as exponential moving average target tracking) to minimize peak participation rates.

Suppose a factor model selects small-cap stocks with average daily volume \$2M and large-cap stocks with volume \$200M. For a \$1M position rebalance, compare their impact costs.

Worked solution

For the large-cap stock, participation is \(\$1\text{M} / \$200\text{M} = 0.005\), and \(\sqrt{0.005} \approx 0.0707\). For the small-cap stock, participation is \(\$1\text{M} / \$2\text{M} = 0.50\), and \(\sqrt{0.50} \approx 0.7071\). The small-cap stock suffers exactly 10 times higher price impact (\(\sqrt{100} = 10\)), turning a theoretically attractive small-cap premium into an unprofitable live trade.

Tier 4 · Research

Discuss how Novy-Marx and Velikov (2015) categorize financial anomalies by turnover and transaction costs.

Worked solution

Novy-Marx and Velikov (2015) classify capital market anomalies into low, medium, and high-turnover tiers. They prove that most high-turnover anomalies (such as short-term idiosyncratic volatility and liquidity provision) fail to survive actual effective transaction costs. Only low-turnover strategies (such as value and profitability) generate statistically significant net alpha after accounting for execution friction.

Explain the Almgren-Chriss framework for balancing execution risk variance against market impact cost in institutional order execution.

Worked solution

Almgren and Chriss (2001) formulate optimal execution as an optimal control problem: trading rapidly incurs high temporary and permanent market impact, whereas trading slowly over a long time horizon incurs portfolio variance risk from adverse market price drift. The trader minimizes a mean-variance objective \(E[x] + \lambda V[x]\), yielding an optimal hyperbolic execution trajectory (TWAP/VWAP hybrid).

How do dark pools and crossing networks alter empirical market impact compared to continuous lit order books?

Worked solution

Dark pools allow mid-point crossing without displaying visible quotes, reducing pre-trade information leakage and eliminating the half-spread cost. However, dark execution introduces non-execution risk (opportunity cost) and adverse selection: fills in dark venues occur disproportionately when informed institutional order flow is absent, while toxic flow sweeps both lit and dark liquidity simultaneously.

8. Retrieval Quiz

  1. Under the empirical square-root law of market impact, how does price dislocation \(\Delta P\) scale with order quantity \(Q\)?

    Options

    Theorem 3.1 establishes that price impact scales with the square root of participation: \(\Delta P = Y \cdot \sigma \cdot P \sqrt{Q / V}\).

  2. How does total dollar market impact cost scale with order quantity \(Q\)?

    Options

    Multiplying executed shares \(Q\) by per-share price impact \(\Delta P \propto Q^{0.5}\) yields total dollar friction scaling as \(Q \times Q^{0.5} = Q^{1.5}\).

  3. If order quantity \(Q\) is quadrupled (\(4\times\)) while volume and volatility remain fixed, by what factor does total dollar impact cost increase?

    \(4^{1.5} = (\sqrt{4})^3 = 2^3 = 8\).

  4. In Lab 1, what was the market impact cost in basis points for the \$50,000,000 capital tier?

    The lab printed Impact (bps): 31.62 for \$50,000,000 capital.

  5. What are the four components of Perold's implementation shortfall accounting?

    Options

    Perold (1988) decomposes execution shortfall into delay cost, market impact, opportunity cost of unfilled shares, and explicit fees.

  6. Why does a flat 5 basis-point fee assumption fail for institutional capacity analysis?

    Options

    Flat fees imply infinite scalability. In reality, large order sizes move the market against the fund via the square-root law, exhausting capacity.

  7. What value of the dimensionless coefficient \(Y\) is standard in empirical equity market impact literature?

    Options

    Extensive studies (Toth et al. 2011; Almgren et al. 2005) identify \(Y \approx 0.5\) across international equity venues.
