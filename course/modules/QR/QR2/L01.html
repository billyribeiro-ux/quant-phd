Vectorized vs. Event-Driven Backtesting Architectures

Every backtest is a simulation of history, but the computational model chosen to simulate that history dictates whether its conclusions are valid or fictitious. Vectorized backtests treat history as static multidimensional arrays, assuming infinite capital liquidity, fractional lot divisibility, and zero latency. Real trading environments operate as asynchronous discrete-event state machines where cash balances constrain position sizes, orders queue sequentially, and fills occur under partial execution. Confusing vectorized mathematical convenience with physical execution realism produces spurious strategies that fail on day one of live capital deployment.

1. Mathematical Structure of Vectorized Backtests

In research exploration, the fastest approach to evaluating quantitative hypotheses is array programming. In a vectorized framework, price histories, signal indicators, and position allocations are represented as contiguous vectors in \(\R^T\) or tensors in \(\R^{T \times N}\).

Vectorized Backtest

Let \(\mathbf{P} \in \R_{>0}^T\) denote a discrete-time price series of length \(T\), and let \(\mathbf{r} \in \R^T\) denote the simple return vector with \(r_t = (P_t - P_{t-1}) / P_{t-1}\) for \(t \ge 1\). A vectorized backtest evaluates a target allocation rule \(\mathbf{w} \in \R^T\) by computing the portfolio return vector \(\mathbf{r}_{\text{strat}}\) via component-wise tensor multiplication:

\[ r_{\text{strat}, t} = w_{t-1} \cdot r_t, \quad t = 1, \dots, T-1 \]

and compounds portfolio equity continuously as:

\[ E_t = E_0 \prod_{s=1}^t (1 + r_{\text{strat}, s}) \]

Remark 1.1 · Structural Assumptions of Vectorization

The closed-form elegance of vectorized computation relies on four implicit assumptions [prado2018advances]:

  1. Fractional share divisibility: Positions \(h_t = w_t E_t / P_t\) can take arbitrary real values \(h_t \in \R\), ignoring standard exchange lot size constraints.
  2. Unconstrained liquidity & continuous compounding: Capital is fully and instantaneously reinvested without market friction, cash settlement delay (\(T+1\) or \(T+2\)), or collateral lock-up.
  3. Synchronous bar-close execution: Every asset in a portfolio is rebalanced simultaneously at the exact bar closing price \(P_t\).
  4. Absence of path-dependent execution states: Intraday path excursions between bar open and close (such as stop-loss triggers or limit order fill conditions) do not alter the end-of-bar position.

2. Discrete-Event Simulation & State Machines

Institutional execution pipelines cannot be represented as static matrix multiplications. Market data arrives asynchronously, matching engines process orders sequentially, and portfolio managers maintain dynamic state machines subject to regulatory cash reserves and exchange margin rules.

Event-Driven Backtester

An event-driven backtester is a chronological state-machine simulation engine governed by a priority queue \(\mathcal{Q}\) of discrete events. The system state at discrete step \(t\) consists of a tuple \(\mathcal{S}_t = (C_t, \mathbf{h}_t, \mathcal{O}_t, E_t)\), where:

  • \(C_t \in \R\) is the settled unencumbered cash balance.
  • \(\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{Z}^N\) is the discrete vector of asset holdings (integer share contracts).
  • \(\mathcal{O}_t\) is the active queue of pending and unfilled limit or stop orders.
  • \(E_t = C_t + \mathbf{h}_t^\top \mathbf{P}_t\) is the total portfolio equity.

State transitions occur strictly through sequential dispatch of four typed events: MarketEvent \(\to\) SignalEvent \(\to\) OrderEvent \(\to\) FillEvent.

Theorem 1.1 · Discrete Share Lot Tracking Drift

Let \(E_t^{\text{vec}}\) denote the continuous equity of a vectorized backtest with constant allocation target \(w \in (0, 1]\), and let \(E_t^{\text{evt}}\) denote the equity of an event-driven engine enforcing integer share lots \(h_t = \lfloor w E_t / P_t \rfloor\). Under geometric Brownian motion prices \(P_t = P_0 \exp(\mu t + \sigma W_t)\), the relative tracking discrepancy \(\delta_t = (E_t^{\text{vec}} - E_t^{\text{evt}}) / E_t^{\text{vec}}\) is strictly positive on average and accumulates over time as:

\[ \E[\delta_t] \ge 1 - \exp\left( -\frac{w}{2 E_0} \sum_{s=0}^{t-1} \E[P_s] \right) > 0 \]

Proof

In the vectorized model, the capital invested at bar \(s\) is exactly \(w E_s^{\text{vec}}\). In the event-driven engine with integer truncation, the cash deployed is \(\lfloor w E_s^{\text{evt}} / P_s \rfloor P_s = w E_s^{\text{evt}} - \epsilon_s P_s\), where \(\epsilon_s \in [0, 1)\) is the fractional share remainder. The uninvested fractional cash \(\epsilon_s P_s\) remains in cash earning zero return (or risk-free rate \(r_f < \mu\)). Over \(t\) steps, the compounded cash drag from uninvested fractional capital strictly bounds \(E_t^{\text{evt}}\) below the frictionless continuous compounding path \(E_t^{\text{vec}}\), yielding strictly positive expected tracking drift \(\E[\delta_t] > 0\).

Theorem 1.2 · Intraday Stop-Loss Undecidability

Given daily bar summary tuples \((O_t, H_t, L_t, C_t)\) without tick-level order book logs, let a strategy specify a take-profit target at \(P_{\text{tp}} \in (O_t, H_t)\) and a stop-loss threshold at \(P_{\text{sl}} \in (L_t, O_t)\). If both \(H_t \ge P_{\text{tp}}\) and \(L_t \le P_{\text{sl}}\) occur on bar \(t\), the realized execution fill price is undecidable from the summary bar data.

Proof

A bar summary is a lossy projection \(\pi: C([0, 1]) \to \R^4\) mapping continuous intraday price paths \(P(u)\) to four extrema. Two paths \(P_1(u)\) and \(P_2(u)\) can share identical \((O, H, L, C)\) while having reversed hitting times: \(\tau_{\text{tp}}(P_1) < \tau_{\text{sl}}(P_1)\) (take-profit hits first) while \(\tau_{\text{sl}}(P_2) < \tau_{\text{tp}}(P_2)\) (stop-loss hits first). Because vectorized models rely exclusively on \((O, H, L, C)\), determining the exit fill order is formally undecidable, forcing arbitrary heuristics that introduce severe survivorship or lookahead artifacts.

3. Empirical Lab · Vectorized vs. Event-Driven Drift

We now verify the structural tracking divergence between a continuous vectorized backtest and a discrete event-driven queue with integer share lots and cash tracking constraints.

Lab 1 · Vectorized vs. Event-Driven Execution Discrepancy

Discrete integer share lot truncation and cash solvency tracking in an event-driven queue induce substantial cumulative tracking drift relative to a frictionless vectorized backtest under identical price and signal inputs.

"""Lab QR2-L01-1: Vectorized vs. Event-Driven Execution Discrepancy.

Demonstrates tracking divergence between a frictionless vectorized backtest
and a discrete event-driven queue with integer share lots and cash constraints.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def run_vectorized(prices: np.ndarray, signals: np.ndarray, initial_cash: float) -> tuple[np.ndarray, float]:
    """Computes vectorized equity curve with continuous full reinvestment."""
    returns = np.zeros_like(prices)
    returns[1:] = (prices[1:] - prices[:-1]) / prices[:-1]
    # In vectorized model, signal at t-1 earns return at t
    strat_returns = signals[:-1] * returns[1:]
    equity = np.zeros_like(prices)
    equity[0] = initial_cash
    for t in range(1, len(prices)):
        equity[t] = equity[t - 1] * (1.0 + strat_returns[t - 1])
    return equity, float(equity[-1])


def run_event_driven(prices: np.ndarray, signals: np.ndarray, initial_cash: float) -> tuple[np.ndarray, float]:
    """Computes event-driven equity curve with discrete integer share execution and cash tracking."""
    n_bars = len(prices)
    cash = initial_cash
    shares = 0
    equity = np.zeros(n_bars)

    for t in range(n_bars):
        current_price = prices[t]
        # In discrete event queue, signal at t-1 is filled at t
        if t > 0:
            target_signal = signals[t - 1]
            current_equity = cash + shares * current_price
            target_value = target_signal * current_equity
            target_shares = int(target_value // current_price) if target_value > 0 else 0
            share_diff = target_shares - shares

            if share_diff != 0:
                cost = share_diff * current_price
                if cash >= cost:
                    cash -= cost
                    shares += share_diff

        equity[t] = cash + shares * current_price

    return equity, float(equity[-1])


def main() -> None:
    # Deterministic seeded synthetic prices
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    n_bars = 120
    drift = 0.0004
    vol = 0.015
    innovations = rng.normal(drift, vol, size=n_bars)
    prices = 100.0 * np.exp(np.cumsum(innovations))

    # Fast / slow moving average crossover signal
    fast_ma = np.convolve(prices, np.ones(5) / 5, mode="same")
    slow_ma = np.convolve(prices, np.ones(20) / 20, mode="same")
    signals = np.where(fast_ma > slow_ma, 1.0, 0.0)

    initial_cash = 100_000.0
    _vec_equity, vec_end = run_vectorized(prices, signals, initial_cash)
    _evt_equity, evt_end = run_event_driven(prices, signals, initial_cash)

    diff = vec_end - evt_end
    tracking_err_pct = (diff / evt_end) * 100.0

    print(f"Initial Capital: ${initial_cash:,.2f}")
    print(f"Vectorized Ending Equity: ${vec_end:,.2f}")
    print(f"Event-Driven Ending Equity: ${evt_end:,.2f}")
    print(f"Absolute Discrepancy: ${diff:,.2f}")
    print(f"Relative Tracking Error: {tracking_err_pct:.3f}%")
    print("Execution State Invariant Verified: Integer share lot discretizations induce drift.")


main()
Initial Capital: $100,000.00
Vectorized Ending Equity: $89,117.46
Event-Driven Ending Equity: $81,859.21
Absolute Discrepancy: $7,258.25
Relative Tracking Error: 8.867%
Execution State Invariant Verified: Integer share lot discretizations induce drift.

The experiment confirms Theorem 1.1: across 120 synthetic trading bars, integer share quantization and cash constraint tracking produce a relative tracking error of 8.867% ($7,258.25 divergence on $100,000 initial capital). Frictionless vectorized backtests overstate capital efficiency by ignoring discrete execution physics.

4. Where the Canon Breaks

In quantitative finance textbooks, backtesting is often introduced through pandas vector operations: df['strat_ret'] = df['signal'].shift(1) * df['ret']. While computationally fast, this canonical simplification breaks down catastrophically in production quantitative trading:

  • Margin calls and solvency breakdown: Vectorized returns allow equity to decline below zero without halting trading, effectively granting the strategy an imaginary infinite credit line. In reality, a broker liquidates positions upon maintenance margin breach.
  • Execution latency blindness: Shifting signals by one bar assumes that the entire execution lag is exactly one bar and fills occur at the closing price. In reality, order transmission, risk checks, and exchange matching latencies mean fills occur across continuous market books with price slippage.
  • Short-sale borrow constraints: Vectorized backtests treat negative allocations \(w_t < 0\) as costless and symmetric to long positions. In practice, locate availability, hard-to-borrow fees, and short squeezes prevent symmetric execution.

5. Misconceptions

  1. Vectorized backtests and event-driven backtests produce identical return series if commissions and bid-ask spreads are set to zero.

    Even in a zero-friction market, integer share rounding, minimum order lot sizes, cash reserve requirements, and discrete price ticks create path-dependent tracking drift that compounds over time, as proven in Theorem 1.1 and demonstrated in Lab 1.

  2. Intraday stop-loss and take-profit orders can be accurately evaluated in a vectorized backtest by checking if the bar's Low breached the stop or High breached the target.

    When both the high and low thresholds are crossed within the same bar, summary OHLC data cannot establish which event occurred first chronologically. Assuming the favorable outcome leaks future information; assuming the stop hits first introduces downward bias. Only tick-level event simulation resolves the sequence.

  3. Event-driven backtesting architectures are only necessary for high-frequency algorithmic market-making strategies, not daily portfolio rebalancing.

    Even for monthly rebalancing strategies, corporate actions, dividend reinvestment cash delays, capital addition schedules, and multi-asset collateral constraints require discrete state accounting to ensure strategy solvency.

6. Practice Problems

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A strategy with \$100,000 cash targets a 50% allocation to a stock trading at \$330 per share. What is the fractional position, the integer share lot position, and the uninvested residual cash?

Worked solution

Target investment notional is \(0.50 \times \$100{,}000 = \$50{,}000\). The continuous fractional share quantity is \(\$50{,}000 / \$330 \approx 151.515\) shares. The integer share lot position is \(\lfloor 151.515 \rfloor = 151\) shares. The deployed capital is \(151 \times \$330 = \$49{,}830\). The uninvested residual cash remaining in the account is \(\$50{,}000 - \$49{,}830 = \$170\).

In a vectorized backtest, a strategy is long 1.0 unit. Day \(t\) open is \$100, close is \$105. Day \(t+1\) open is \$106, close is \$110. What is the compound return over the two days in a frictionless close-to-close model?

Worked solution

In standard close-to-close vectorization, the return on day \(t\) is \((105 - 100) / 100 = 0.05\). The return on day \(t+1\) is \((110 - 105) / 105 \approx 0.047619\). The compound return over the two-day period is \((1 + 0.05)(1 + 0.047619) - 1 = (105/100)(110/105) - 1 = 110/100 - 1 = 0.10\) or 10.00%.

List the four canonical event types processed by an institutional event-driven backtesting queue in chronological dispatch order.

Worked solution

The four canonical events dispatched in order are: 1. MarketEvent (market data update arrives from feed handler); 2. SignalEvent (alpha model evaluates strategy logic and produces allocation intent); 3. OrderEvent (portfolio and risk manager constructs compliant orders); 4. FillEvent (simulated execution venue matches order and returns fill execution details).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that in an event-driven backtester, total portfolio equity \(E_t = C_t + \sum_{i=1}^N h_{i, t} P_{i, t}\) is invariant to internal order fill execution in the absence of transaction friction.

Worked solution

Let an order to buy \(q\) shares of asset \(i\) fill at price \(P_{i, t}\) with zero transaction costs. Prior to the fill, state is \((C_{t-1}, h_{i, t-1})\). Upon fill, cash updates as \(C_t = C_{t-1} - q P_{i, t}\) and shares update as \(h_{i, t} = h_{i, t-1} + q\). Portfolio equity after fill is \(E_t = C_t + h_{i, t} P_{i, t} = (C_{t-1} - q P_{i, t}) + (h_{i, t-1} + q) P_{i, t} = C_{t-1} + h_{i, t-1} P_{i, t} = E_{t-1}\). Hence, portfolio equity is strictly conserved during frictionless execution.

Prove that when transaction cost \(\text{Cost}(q, P) > 0\) is incurred on every trade, portfolio equity is strictly monotonically decreasing in transaction volume for any constant price path \(P_t = P_0\).

Worked solution

Under constant price \(P_t = P_0\), the gross asset value \(h_t P_0\) fluctuates only by \(\Delta h_t P_0 = q_t P_0\). Cash updates as \(C_t = C_{t-1} - q_t P_0 - \text{Cost}(q_t, P_0)\). Combining these terms yields \(E_t = E_{t-1} - \text{Cost}(q_t, P_0)\). Because \(\text{Cost}(q_t, P_0) > 0\) for any non-zero trade \(|q_t| > 0\), we have \(E_t < E_{t-1}\). Thus, portfolio equity strictly decreases monotonically by the cumulative transaction friction \(\sum \text{Cost}\).

Prove that a vectorized backtest that executes rebalancing on day \(t\) using the same day's return \(r_t\) without lagging conditioning signals violates the Doob filtration measurability condition.

Worked solution

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \Prob)\) be a filtered probability space where \(\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(P_0, \dots, P_t)\). A strategy allocation \(w_t\) is predictable if it is measurable with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\). If \(w_t\) conditions on \(P_t\) (e.g. \(w_t = f(P_t)\)) while earning return \(r_t = (P_t - P_{t-1})/P_{t-1}\), the strategy return \(w_t r_t\) involves the contemporaneous product of \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable variables. The trading rule cannot be implemented in physical time because \(P_t\) is not known prior to the realization of \(r_t\), violating the filtration causality constraint.

Tier 3 · Empirical

In Lab 1, why does the absolute discrepancy between vectorized and event-driven ending equity equal \$7,258.25 over 120 bars?

Worked solution

In Lab 1, the vectorized model maintains continuous 100% reinvestment of all capital into fractional shares. The event-driven model truncates positions to integer share lots via int(target_value // price). Over 120 bars with price drifting upwards, the uninvested residual cash does not compound at the asset's positive growth rate. The cumulative uninvested cash drag compounds to an absolute discrepancy of \$7,258.25, representing an 8.867% relative drift on the \$100,000 initial base.

How does portfolio rebalancing frequency (e.g. daily vs. monthly) impact the magnitude of tracking error between vectorized and discrete lot implementations?

Worked solution

Higher rebalancing frequencies increase tracking error because each rebalancing event incurs a new integer truncation rounding error and reallocates capital between discrete shares and uninvested cash reserves. Over \(N\) frequent rebalances, these small round-off discrepancies compound geometrically, whereas monthly rebalancing experiences fewer truncation events and allows intra-period holdings to compound without periodic cash friction.

Suppose a strategy targets 100 stocks equally weighted with \$1,000,000 capital. Stock prices range from \$10 to \$2,500. How does the relative lot truncation error vary across the portfolio?

Worked solution

Each stock is allocated \$10,000 notional. For a \$10 stock, the target is 1,000 shares, and truncating at most 0.999 shares produces a maximum rounding drag of \(0.999 \times \$10 \approx \$10\) or 0.10% of the position. For a \$2,500 stock (e.g. Berkshire Hathaway class B), \$10,000 buys exactly 4 shares. Truncating one share represents a \$2,500 error or 25% of the position allocation. High-priced stocks suffer extreme relative discretization distortions.

Tier 4 · Research

How does the choice between vectorized backtesting and event-driven backtesting influence the estimation of factor capacity and optimal execution schedules?

Worked solution

Vectorized backtesting assumes infinite instantaneous market depth at the closing price, implying infinite strategy capacity up to arbitrary AUM. An event-driven architecture models finite order book liquidity, participation rate limits (e.g. max 5% of daily volume), and multi-bar order slicing schedules (such as TWAP/VWAP algorithms). As AUM grows, the event-driven engine captures execution delays and market impact drag, establishing a realistic capacity ceiling where marginal trading cost exceeds expected factor alpha.

Under what conditions can a hybrid vectorized/event-driven backtest be scientifically justified in quantitative research pipelines?

Worked solution

A hybrid design is justified in a two-stage funnel: a fast vectorized pre-filter screens tens of thousands of raw parameter configurations to discard empirically unviable alphas (Sharpe < 0), followed by a strict event-driven simulation for the surviving top 1% of candidates. However, the researcher must recognize that the pre-filter introduces selection bias toward strategies that exploit vectorized conveniences (e.g. fractional lots or unconstrained leverage), necessitating rigorous post-selection deflation adjustments (such as DSR and PBO).

Explain how asynchronous exchange clock drift and non-deterministic network packet arrival can be modeled rigorously inside an event-driven backtester queue.

Worked solution

In an institutional event queue, each event carries a tuple of timestamps: matching engine event instant \(t_E\), gateway transmission instant \(t_{\text{tx}}\), and subscriber receipt instant \(t_R\). Stochastic network jitter \(\Delta t \sim \text{LogNormal}(\mu, \sigma)\) is added to the event priority key. When processing multi-exchange books, orders cannot execute until \(\tau \ge t_R + \Delta_{\text{processing}}\). This prevents cross-exchange causal violations where orders condition on quotes before physical speed-of-light packet arrival.

8. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What core assumption enables a vectorized backtest to compute returns via simple array multiplication?

    Options

    Vectorized models multiply signals directly by periodic returns, implicitly assuming fractional share holdings, zero execution delay, and unconstrained cash liquidity.

  2. In an event-driven backtesting engine, which event triggers the calculation of alpha strategy signals?

    Options

    A MarketEvent signals new price or bar data arrival, prompting the strategy to evaluate signals and generate a SignalEvent.

  3. Why can path-dependent stop-loss rules NOT be evaluated accurately using daily OHLC summary bars alone?

    Options

    Theorem 1.2 proves that if both stop-loss and take-profit thresholds are crossed in the same bar, the order of events is undecidable without tick timestamps.

  4. If a strategy with \$50,000 allocated capital targets a stock trading at \$330 per share, how many whole integer shares can be purchased?

    \(\lfloor 50{,}000 / 330 \rfloor = \lfloor 151.515 \rfloor = 151\) integer shares.

  5. What causes tracking divergence between vectorized and event-driven backtests in a zero-friction market?

    Options

    Theorem 1.1 proves that truncating continuous fractional allocations to integer share lots leaves residual cash, inducing persistent tracking drift.

  6. What state variable update occurs upon execution of a FillEvent buying \(q\) shares at price \(P\)?

    Options

    Cash decreases by the total trade expenditure (notional plus transaction costs) while share position increases by \(q\).

  7. Why does a vectorized backtest fail to detect margin liquidation?

    Options

    Vectorized models multiply returns across entire arrays, allowing negative equity to continue compounding without enforcing broker margin thresholds.
