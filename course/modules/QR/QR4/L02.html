Bag-of-Words, Sparsity & Sublinear TF-IDF Weighting

Raw word count vectors distort quantitative signals because document lengths vary widely across corporate filings, and common non-informative words dominate raw inner products. The vector space model resolves this by weighting each term by its local within-document prominence and penalizing its global across-corpus prevalence. Sublinear term-frequency inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) weighting transforms sparse document-term counts into continuous geometric vectors whose cosine similarities capture genuine economic alignment. Mastering TF-IDF weighting and its mathematical properties is essential for building robust disclosure similarity signals and industry peer group factors.

1. The Bag-of-Words Assumption and Vector Space Model

In text as data, the most widely utilized baseline representation is the bag-of-words model [gentzkow2019text].

Bag-of-words representation

A bag-of-words representation models a document \(d_i\) as an unordered multiset of vocabulary tokens, discarding grammatical syntax, word order, and phrase structure, while retaining only word multiplicity:

\[ \mathbf{c}_i = [c_{i,1}, c_{i,2}, \dots, c_{i,V}]^\top \in \N_0^V \]

Under the statistical assumption of conditional word exchangeability, the joint probability of the token sequence \((w_{(1)}, w_{(2)}, \dots, w_{(L)})\) given document topic parameters \(\mathbf{\theta}_i\) factors as a multinomial distribution:

\[ \Prob(w_{(1)}, \dots, w_{(L)} \mid \mathbf{\theta}_i) = \frac{L!}{\prod_{j=1}^V c_{i,j}!} \prod_{j=1}^V \theta_{i,j}^{c_{i,j}} \]

While the exchangeability assumption is an approximation (ignoring grammar and negation), it provides a tractable linear vector space where documents are represented as points in \(\R^V\).

2. Sublinear TF-IDF Weighting and Cosine Geometry

Raw occurrence counts \(c_{i,j}\) suffer from two severe econometrics flaws: length bias (longer 10-K filings have higher counts without greater information content) and frequency bias (words common across all firms receive huge weights). The Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) transformation resolves both flaws [hastie2009elements].

Definition 2.1 · Sublinear Smoothed TF-IDF

Let corpus \(\mathcal{C}\) contain \(D\) documents, and let \(\text{df}_j = \sum_{i=1}^D \indic\{c_{i,j} > 0\}\) denote the document frequency of vocabulary term \(w_j\). The sublinear smoothed TF-IDF weight \(x_{i,j}\) of term \(w_j\) in document \(d_i\) is:

\[ x_{i,j} = \operatorname{tf}_{\text{sub}}(c_{i,j}) \cdot \operatorname{idf}_{\text{smooth}}(\text{df}_j, D) \]

where:

  • \(\operatorname{tf}_{\text{sub}}(c_{i,j}) = 1 + \ln(c_{i,j})\) for \(c_{i,j} \ge 1\), and \(0\) otherwise.
  • \(\operatorname{idf}_{\text{smooth}}(\text{df}_j, D) = \ln\left(\frac{1 + D}{1 + \text{df}_j}\right) + 1\).
Theorem 2.1 · Cosine Similarity Bounds and Invariance to Scale

Let \(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2 \in \R_{\ge 0}^V\) be non-negative TF-IDF document vectors. The cosine similarity:

\[ \cos(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) = \frac{\mathbf{x}_1^\top \mathbf{x}_2}{\|\mathbf{x}_1\|_2 \|\mathbf{x}_2\|_2} \]

satisfies \(\cos(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) \in [0, 1]\). Furthermore, \(\cos(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2)\) is strictly invariant to positive document length rescalings: \(\cos(\alpha \mathbf{x}_1, \beta \mathbf{x}_2) = \cos(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2)\) for all \(\alpha, \beta > 0\).

Proof

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, \(|\mathbf{x}_1^\top \mathbf{x}_2| \le \|\mathbf{x}_1\|_2 \|\mathbf{x}_2\|_2\), so \(\cos(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) \le 1\). Because all entries of \(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2\) are non-negative (\(x_{i,j} \ge 0\)), their inner product satisfies \(\mathbf{x}_1^\top \mathbf{x}_2 = \sum_{j=1}^V x_{1,j} x_{2,j} \ge 0\), establishing \(\cos(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) \ge 0\). For positive scalars \(\alpha, \beta > 0\), the numerator scales by \(\alpha \beta\) while the denominator scales by \(\|\alpha \mathbf{x}_1\|_2 \|\beta \mathbf{x}_2\|_2 = \alpha \beta \|\mathbf{x}_1\|_2 \|\mathbf{x}_2\|_2\), canceling exactly and proving scale invariance.

Example 2.1 · Industry Peer Identification via 10-K Cosine Similarity

Suppose an equities desk forms industry peer clusters using 10-K Business Descriptions (Item 1). Two cloud software firms share high TF-IDF weights on specialized terms like 'saas', 'multi-tenant', and 'subscription', yielding a cosine similarity of 0.72. An oil exploration firm shares zero specialized terms with the software firms, yielding a cosine similarity of 0.00. Normalizing by document length ensures that a 40-page software 10-K aligns perfectly with a 15-page competitor without length penalty.

3. Inline Lab: Sublinear TF-IDF and Sector Similarity

The following laboratory computes sublinear TF-IDF vectors across financial earnings excerpts, calculates the pairwise cosine similarity matrix, and measures n-gram vocabulary expansion.

Lab 1 · Sublinear TF-IDF Weighting and Cosine Similarity

Verifies that within-sector corporate disclosures exhibit strictly higher cosine similarity than cross-sector disclosures, and analyzes n-gram vocabulary expansion.

"""Lab QR4-L02-1: Sublinear TF-IDF Weighting and Cosine Similarity in Financial Disclosures.

Demonstrates the bag-of-words vector space model, computes sublinear TF-IDF weights,
evaluates pairwise cosine similarity across earnings releases, and analyzes the
exponential sparsity explosion when expanding from unigrams to bigrams.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import re

import numpy as np


def extract_ngrams(tokens: list[str], n: int) -> list[str]:
    """Extract n-grams of degree n from token list."""
    if len(tokens) < n:
        return []
    return ["_".join(tokens[i : i + n]) for i in range(len(tokens) - n + 1)]


def tokenize_text(text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Tokenize text into lowercase alphabetic words."""
    return re.findall(r"\b[a-z]{2,}\b", text.lower())


def compute_sublinear_tfidf(
    corpus: list[str],
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, list[str]]:
    """Compute sublinear TF-IDF matrix: w_{i,j} = (1 + log(tf_{i,j})) * log((1 + D) / (1 + df_j)) + 1."""
    d = len(corpus)
    tokenized_docs = [tokenize_text(doc) for doc in corpus]

    # Build unique vocabulary
    vocab_set: set[str] = set()
    for doc in tokenized_docs:
        vocab_set.update(doc)
    vocab = sorted(vocab_set)
    v = len(vocab)
    term_to_idx = {term: i for i, term in enumerate(vocab)}

    # Document frequency
    df = np.zeros(v, dtype=np.float64)
    for doc in tokenized_docs:
        unique_terms = set(doc)
        for t in unique_terms:
            df[term_to_idx[t]] += 1.0

    # Smooth inverse document frequency: idf_j = ln((1 + D) / (1 + df_j)) + 1
    idf = np.log((1.0 + d) / (1.0 + df)) + 1.0

    # Matrix of TF-IDF
    tfidf = np.zeros((d, v), dtype=np.float64)
    for doc_idx, doc in enumerate(tokenized_docs):
        term_counts: dict[str, int] = {}
        for t in doc:
            term_counts[t] = term_counts.get(t, 0) + 1
        for t, count in term_counts.items():
            col = term_to_idx[t]
            # Sublinear TF scaling
            tf_weight = 1.0 + math.log(count)
            tfidf[doc_idx, col] = tf_weight * idf[col]

    # L2 normalize each document vector
    norms = np.linalg.norm(tfidf, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    norms[norms == 0.0] = 1.0
    tfidf_norm = tfidf / norms

    return tfidf_norm, vocab


def cosine_similarity_matrix(vectors: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Compute pairwise cosine similarity matrix for L2-normalized rows."""
    return np.asarray(vectors @ vectors.T, dtype=np.float64)


def main() -> None:
    # 4 corporate disclosure items: 2 tech sector, 2 energy sector
    corpus = [
        "Cloud software subscription annualized recurring revenue expanded thirty percent year over year.",
        "Enterprise cloud platform adoption accelerated software recurring billings across commercial customers.",
        "Crude oil production volumes increased following upstream drilling operations in Permian basin.",
        "Refining margins contracted due to higher crude feedstock prices and energy maintenance shutdowns.",
    ]

    tfidf_norm, vocab = compute_sublinear_tfidf(corpus)
    sim_matrix = cosine_similarity_matrix(tfidf_norm)

    print(f"Corpus size: {len(corpus)} documents")
    print(f"Vocabulary size: {len(vocab)} unique terms")
    print("\nPairwise Cosine Similarity Matrix (L2 normalized TF-IDF):")
    for i in range(len(corpus)):
        row_str = "  ".join(f"{sim_matrix[i, j]:.4f}" for j in range(len(corpus)))
        print(f"  Doc {i}: [{row_str}]")

    tech_cross_sim = sim_matrix[0, 1]
    energy_cross_sim = sim_matrix[2, 3]
    tech_energy_sim = sim_matrix[0, 2]

    print(f"\nWithin-sector similarity (Tech Doc 0 vs Doc 1): {tech_cross_sim:.4f}")
    print(f"Within-sector similarity (Energy Doc 2 vs Doc 3): {energy_cross_sim:.4f}")
    print(f"Cross-sector similarity (Tech Doc 0 vs Energy Doc 2): {tech_energy_sim:.4f}")

    # n-gram sparsity expansion analysis
    doc_tokens = [tokenize_text(d) for d in corpus]
    unigrams = set(t for doc in doc_tokens for t in doc)
    bigrams = set(bg for doc in doc_tokens for bg in extract_ngrams(doc, 2))

    print(f"\nUnique unigrams: {len(unigrams)}")
    print(f"Unique bigrams: {len(bigrams)}")
    print(f"Bigram-to-unigram vocabulary expansion ratio: {len(bigrams) / len(unigrams):.2f}")
    print("Verification Invariant: Within-sector semantic similarity strictly exceeds cross-sector similarity.")

4. Where the Canon Breaks

While the bag-of-words TF-IDF model is standard in computer science information retrieval, it exhibits critical pathologies when applied to financial alpha generation:

  • Negation Blindness: Under bag-of-words exchangeability, "profit increased, no loss reported" and "loss increased, no profit reported" produce identical token counts \(\mathbf{c}_1 = \mathbf{c}_2\) and identical cosine similarity 1.0, despite representing diametrically opposite financial outcomes.
  • Orthogonality of Synonyms: The vector space model treats every vocabulary word as an orthogonal basis vector (\(\mathbf{e}_i^\top \mathbf{e}_j = 0\) for \(i \ne j\)). Two companies describing identical underlying business models using different vocabulary (e.g., 'automotive manufacturer' versus 'car producer') receive zero cosine similarity unless augmented by word embeddings or topic models.

5. Misconceptions

  1. Using raw linear term frequencies in TF-IDF is superior because it preserves exact word counts.

    Raw linear term frequency allows repetitive terms in long filings to dominate the entire document vector. A word appearing 20 times does not convey 20 times the economic information of a single mention. Sublinear scaling \(1 + \ln(\text{tf})\) prevents long-document distortion.

  2. Expanding the bag-of-words to tri-grams and 4-grams always improves financial predictive accuracy.

    Higher-order n-grams cause the feature dimension \(V\) to explode combinatorially. In low-SNR financial panels, this massive dimensionality inflation creates extreme sparsity, causing estimators to severely overfit sample noise.

  3. Cosine similarity is sensitive to differences in filing length.

    By Theorem 2.1, cosine similarity normalizes by vector \(L_2\) norms, making it strictly invariant to scalar multiples of document vectors. It evaluates the angle between vectors, not their magnitudes.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Let term \(w_1\) have count \(c_{1} = 9\) in document \(d_1\). In a corpus of \(D = 500\) filings, \(w_1\) appears in 24 documents. Calculate its sublinear TF-IDF weight using smoothed IDF.

Worked solution

Sublinear term frequency is \(\operatorname{tf}_{\text{sub}}(9) = 1 + \ln(9) = 1 + 2.1972 = 3.1972\). Smoothed IDF is \(\operatorname{idf} = \ln\left(\frac{1 + 500}{1 + 24}\right) + 1 = \ln\left(\frac{501}{25}\right) + 1 = \ln(20.04) + 1 = 2.9977 + 1 = 3.9977\). The TF-IDF weight is \(x_{1} = 3.1972 \times 3.9977 \approx 12.7816\).

Let two documents have normalized TF-IDF vectors \(\mathbf{x}_1 = [0.6, 0.8, 0.0]^\top\) and \(\mathbf{x}_2 = [0.0, 0.8, 0.6]^\top\). Calculate their inner product and cosine similarity.

Worked solution

Because \(\|\mathbf{x}_1\|_2 = \sqrt{0.6^2 + 0.8^2} = 1.0\) and \(\|\mathbf{x}_2\|_2 = \sqrt{0.8^2 + 0.6^2} = 1.0\), the vectors are already unit normalized. The inner product is \(\mathbf{x}_1^\top \mathbf{x}_2 = 0.6(0) + 0.8(0.8) + 0.0(0.6) = 0.64\). Cosine similarity is \(\frac{0.64}{1.0 \times 1.0} = 0.64\).

In a corpus of \(D = 2,000\) filings, term \(w_{\text{common}}\) appears in 1,999 documents. Calculate its smoothed IDF weight and contrast it with a term appearing in only 1 document.

Worked solution

For \(w_{\text{common}}\), \(\operatorname{idf} = \ln\left(\frac{2001}{2000}\right) + 1 = \ln(1.0005) + 1 \approx 0.0005 + 1 = 1.0005\). For a rare term appearing once, \(\operatorname{idf} = \ln\left(\frac{2001}{2}\right) + 1 = \ln(1000.5) + 1 = 6.9083 + 1 = 7.9083\). The rare term receives a 7.9-fold higher penalty multiplier, heavily discounting ubiquitous words.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for non-negative vectors \(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2 \in \R_{\ge 0}^V\), Euclidean distance between their \(L_2\)-normalized vectors is monotonically decreasing in their cosine similarity.

Worked solution

Let \(\mathbf{u}_i = \frac{\mathbf{x}_i}{\|\mathbf{x}_i\|_2}\) be unit vectors. The squared Euclidean distance is \(\|\mathbf{u}_1 - \mathbf{u}_2\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{u}_1\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{u}_2\|_2^2 - 2 \mathbf{u}_1^\top \mathbf{u}_2 = 1 + 1 - 2 \cos(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) = 2(1 - \cos(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2))\). Taking the square root gives \(\|\mathbf{u}_1 - \mathbf{u}_2\|_2 = \sqrt{2(1 - \cos(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2))}\). Since the derivative with respect to cosine similarity is \(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1 - \cos)}}\), distance strictly decreases as cosine similarity increases.

Prove that under the bag-of-words multiset representation, reversing the sequential order of all words in a document leaves its document-term vector \(\mathbf{c}\) unchanged.

Worked solution

Let document \(d\) be the sequence \((w_{(1)}, w_{(2)}, \dots, w_{(L)})\). Let \(d_{\text{rev}}\) be the reversed sequence \((w_{(L)}, w_{(L-1)}, \dots, w_{(1)})\). For any vocabulary term \(w_j\), the count is \(c_j = \sum_{k=1}^L \indic\{w_{(k)} = w_j\}\). Because addition over finite index sets is commutative, \(\sum_{k=1}^L \indic\{w_{(L-k+1)} = w_j\} = \sum_{k=1}^L \indic\{w_{(k)} = w_j\} = c_j\). The count vector is identical, formally demonstrating negation and sequence blindness.

Prove that sublinear term-frequency scaling is strictly concave on \([1, \infty)\) and derive its marginal information elasticity.

Worked solution

Let \(f(c) = 1 + \ln(c)\) for \(c \ge 1\). First derivative: \(f'(c) = \frac{1}{c} > 0\). Second derivative: \(f''(c) = -\frac{1}{c^2} < 0\) for all \(c \ge 1\). Since the second derivative is strictly negative, \(f(c)\) is strictly concave. The elasticity of information weight with respect to count is \(\mathcal{E} = \frac{c}{f(c)} f'(c) = \frac{c}{1 + \ln(c)} \frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{1 + \ln(c)}\), which declines monotonically from 1.0 at \(c=1\) toward 0 as \(c \to \infty\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

In quantitative asset management, how does pairwise 10-K cosine similarity outperform traditional SIC industry classifications in explaining return co-movement?

Worked solution

Hoberg & Phillips (2016) show that fixed government SIC industry codes are backward-looking and fail to capture emerging multi-segment business models. Pairwise 10-K product description cosine similarity generates dynamic, continuous peer networks that explain 30% more cross-sectional return co-movement and hedge industry shocks more effectively.

What empirical Sharpe ratio degradation occurs when training a sentiment factor on raw term counts versus sublinear TF-IDF weights?

Worked solution

Raw term counts allow long corporate filings to generate extreme outlier factor scores. When traded in an equity market-neutral portfolio, these extreme position sizes create massive idiosyncratic turnover and crash during corporate restructuring announcements, degrading realized Sharpe ratios by over 50% compared to sublinearly weighted factors.

How does vocabulary truncation (e.g. keeping only the top 5,000 TF-IDF terms) affect out-of-sample portfolio turnover?

Worked solution

Truncating vocabulary stabilizes the feature space across time. Retaining unpruned tail vocabulary introduces rare words that appear in one quarter and vanish the next, causing portfolio factor weights to fluctuate wildly and inflating transaction turnover drag.

Tier 4 · Research

Formulate the optimal trade-off between n-gram order \(n\) and estimator variance in financial return prediction models.

Worked solution

Increasing n-gram order from \(n=1\) to \(n=2\) captures local negation phrases (e.g. 'not profitable'), reducing model misspecification bias \(\Delta B^2\). However, the vocabulary space expands from \(V\) to \(\mathcal{O}(V^2)\), inflating parameter variance by \(\frac{V^2}{D} \sigma_\epsilon^2\). In low-SNR asset pricing regimes where \(\sigma_\epsilon^2\) is large, variance inflation overwhelms bias reduction, making \(n > 2\) counterproductive without aggressive LASSO sparsity.

Explain how corporate strategic disclosure obfuscation (e.g., inflating filing length to conceal deteriorating fundamentals) alters the empirical properties of TF-IDF vectors.

Worked solution

Distressed firms strategically obfuscate bad news by dramatically lengthening their 10-K filings with legal boilerplate. This dilutes the term frequency \(\text{tf}_{j}\) of core operational metrics and increases document \(L_2\) norms, artificially suppressing cosine similarity with healthy peers and signaling negative forward earnings quality.

Why does calculating IDF across an expanding historical window introduce look-back drift into quantitative text factors?

Worked solution

As the document count \(D_t\) and term frequencies \(\text{df}_{j,t}\) grow over calendar time, the IDF weight of emerging terms decays non-linearly. A point-in-time backtester must freeze the IDF weighting matrix as of the historical portfolio decision date rather than computing IDF on the entire pooled sample.

8. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What core assumption underlies the bag-of-words text representation?

    Options

    By Definition 2.1, the bag-of-words model assumes words are exchangeable given document parameters, ignoring sequence order.

  2. How does sublinear term-frequency scaling \(\operatorname{tf}_{\text{sub}}(c) = 1 + \ln(c)\) alter the impact of term repetition?

    Options

    Logarithmic scaling reflects the economic reality that 20 mentions of a word convey diminishing marginal information compared to the initial occurrence.

  3. What does inverse document frequency (IDF) measure?

    Options

    IDF penalizes words that appear across almost all documents, elevating specialized domain words that differentiate firms.

  4. Why is cosine similarity invariant to positive rescaling of document vectors (\(\cos(\alpha \mathbf{x}, \beta \mathbf{y}) = \cos(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\))?

    Options

    By Theorem 2.1, \(\frac{(\alpha \mathbf{x})^\top (\beta \mathbf{y})}{\|\alpha \mathbf{x}\| \|\beta \mathbf{y}\|} = \frac{\alpha \beta (\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y})}{\alpha \beta \|\mathbf{x}\| \|\mathbf{y}\|} = \cos(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\).

  5. What is a major limitation of the bag-of-words model when analyzing financial disclosures?

    Options

    Because word order is discarded, negation qualifiers are decoupled from the terms they modify, leading to negation blindness.

  6. Why does expanding from unigrams to bigrams cause parameter estimation variance to explode in low-SNR regimes?

    Options

    Combinatorial explosion in vocabulary size without an increase in sample size drastically increases model capacity, causing severe overfitting to noise.

  7. How does Hoberg & Phillips (2016) use 10-K text cosine similarity in corporate finance?

    Options

    Pairwise cosine similarity on product descriptions defines peer firms dynamically based on commercial overlap rather than arbitrary administrative codes.

  8. What happens to the cosine similarity between two documents if they share no common vocabulary terms?

    Options

    When vectors share no non-zero indices, their dot product is zero, resulting in a cosine similarity of zero.
