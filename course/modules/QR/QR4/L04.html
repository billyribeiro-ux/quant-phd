Distributed Representations & Financial Word Embeddings

Discrete bag-of-words models treat every vocabulary word as an orthogonal basis vector, completely ignoring synonymous relationships and semantic context. In financial disclosures, this assumption causes severe misspecification: 'treasury' and 'gilt' are treated as mutually orthogonal assets. Distributed representations project words into a dense, continuous latent vector space where geometric proximity reflects economic meaning. Word embeddings allow quants to perform vector arithmetic on corporate concepts, resolve financial synonyms without manual lexicons, and track macroeconomic semantic regime drift across economic cycles.

1. The Distributional Hypothesis and Vector Semantics

In classical bag-of-words representations, words are one-hot indicator vectors \(\mathbf{e}_i \in \{0, 1\}^V\), enforcing \(\mathbf{e}_i^\top \mathbf{e}_j = 0\) for all \(i \ne j\). Modern textual econometrics replaces this discrete orthogonality with the distributional hypothesis (Firth, 1957): words that appear in similar textual contexts share similar semantic meanings [gentzkow2019text].

Word embedding

A word embedding is a continuous mapping \(\mathbf{v}: \mathcal{V} \to \R^d\) that assigns each discrete vocabulary term \(w_j\) to a low-dimensional dense real vector \(\mathbf{v}_j \in \R^d\) (where \(d \ll V\), typically \(d \in [50, 300]\)), such that inner products and cosine distances in \(\R^d\) preserve statistical co-occurrence and semantic relationships across the corpus.

Under the Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) architecture, given a sequence of words \((w_1, w_2, \dots, w_T)\), the model maximizes the log-probability of observing context words \(c\) within a sliding window of radius \(m\) around center target word \(w_t\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}} = \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{-m \le j \le m, j \ne 0} \left( \ln \sigma(\mathbf{u}_{w_{t+j}}^\top \mathbf{v}_{w_t}) + \sum_{k=1}^K \E_{w_n \sim P_n}[\ln \sigma(-\mathbf{u}_{w_n}^\top \mathbf{v}_{w_t})] \right) \]

where \(\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\) is the sigmoid function, \(\mathbf{v}_w\) is the center embedding, \(\mathbf{u}_c\) is the context embedding, and \(P_n(w) \propto f(w)^{3/4}\) is the unigram noise distribution.

Theorem 4.1 · Equivalence to Shifted Pointwise Mutual Information Factorization

Let \(\mathbf{M} \in \R^{V \times V}\) be the shifted Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) matrix of corpus co-occurrences, with entries:

\[ M_{i,j} = \max\left( \ln\left(\frac{P(w_i, c_j)}{P(w_i) P(c_j)}\right) - \ln(K), \; 0 \right) \]

where \(K\) is the number of negative samples. In the asymptotic limit of infinite corpus length and embedding dimension \(d\), the matrix product of center and context embedding matrices \(\mathbf{V} \mathbf{U}^\top\) provides the optimal rank-\(d\) low-rank factorization of the shifted PMI matrix under Frobenius norm loss:

\[ \mathbf{V}^*, \mathbf{U}^* = \argmin_{\mathbf{V}, \mathbf{U} \in \R^{V \times d}} \|\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{V} \mathbf{U}^\top\|_F^2 \]

Proof Sketch

Consider the objective for a single pair \((w_i, c_j)\): \(\ell(w_i, c_j) = N_{i,j} \ln \sigma(\mathbf{v}_i^\top \mathbf{u}_j) + K \frac{N_i N_j}{N} \ln \sigma(-\mathbf{v}_i^\top \mathbf{u}_j)\), where \(N_{i,j}\) is the empirical co-occurrence count. Differentiating with respect to the scalar product \(z_{i,j} = \mathbf{v}_i^\top \mathbf{u}_j\) and setting to zero yields \(N_{i,j} (1 - \sigma(z_{i,j})) - K \frac{N_i N_j}{N} \sigma(z_{i,j}) = 0\). Using \(\frac{\sigma(z)}{1 - \sigma(z)} = e^z\), we obtain \(e^{z_{i,j}} = \frac{N_{i,j} N}{K N_i N_j} = \frac{P(w_i, c_j)}{K P(w_i) P(c_j)}\). Taking the natural logarithm gives \(z_{i,j}^* = \ln\left(\frac{P(w_i, c_j)}{P(w_i) P(c_j)}\right) - \ln(K) = \text{PMI}(w_i, c_j) - \ln(K)\). The global objective is thus minimized when \(\mathbf{V} \mathbf{U}^\top\) approximates the shifted PMI matrix, which by the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem corresponds to truncated SVD factorization.

Example 4.1 · Resolving Financial Synonyms via Embeddings

In a standard bag-of-words matrix, 'shares' and 'equities' are orthogonal (\(\mathbf{e}_{\text{shares}}^\top \mathbf{e}_{\text{equities}} = 0\)). When trained on 10-K filings, both terms co-occur with 'dividend', 'dilution', 'common stock', and 'voting rights'. In a 100-dimensional Word2Vec embedding space, their unit vectors \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{shares}}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{equities}}\) achieve a cosine similarity of 0.94, automatically bridging vocabulary discrepancies without hand-crafted synonym dictionaries.

2. Financial Vector Arithmetic and Analogy Geometry

A remarkable emergent property of linear word embedding spaces is their linear compositionality. Semantic and economic relationships correspond to consistent translational vector offsets in \(\R^d\) [gentzkow2019text].

Definition 4.1 · Semantic Vector Analogy Arithmetic

An analogy query "Word \(A\) is to Word \(B\) as Word \(C\) is to Word \(X\)" is solved in embedding space \(\R^d\) by vector arithmetic using 3CosAdd:

\[ X^* = \argmax_{w \in \mathcal{V} \setminus \{A, B, C\}} \cos(\mathbf{v}_w, \; \mathbf{v}_B - \mathbf{v}_A + \mathbf{v}_C) \]

In financial domain spaces, this geometric relationship solves structural corporate analogies:

\[ \mathbf{v}(\text{equity}) - \mathbf{v}(\text{dividend}) + \mathbf{v}(\text{coupon}) \approx \mathbf{v}(\text{debt}) \] \[ \mathbf{v}(\text{CEO}) - \mathbf{v}(\text{firm}) + \mathbf{v}(\text{nation}) \approx \mathbf{v}(\text{president}) \]

Lab 1 · Word Embedding Geometry & Vector Analogies

Verifies that synonymous asset classes achieve cosine similarity exceeding 0.95 in dense embedding space, and solves structural financial analogies via vector arithmetic.

"""Lab QR4-L04-1: Distributed Semantic Representations, Cosine Similarity, and Analogy Arithmetic.

Demonstrates distributed word embeddings in continuous vector spaces, evaluates cosine similarity
geometry across asset-class taxonomies, and computes vector arithmetic analogies
(e.g., v(equity) - v(dividend) + v(coupon) ≈ v(debt)).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def cosine_similarity(v1: np.ndarray, v2: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """Compute cosine similarity between two 1D vectors."""
    n1 = float(np.linalg.norm(v1))
    n2 = float(np.linalg.norm(v2))
    if n1 == 0.0 or n2 == 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return float(np.dot(v1, v2) / (n1 * n2))


def find_nearest(
    target: np.ndarray,
    embeddings: dict[str, np.ndarray],
    exclude: set[str] | None = None,
) -> tuple[str, float]:
    """Find the word in embeddings with highest cosine similarity to target, excluding specified keys."""
    if exclude is None:
        exclude = set()
    best_word = ""
    best_sim = -1.0
    for word, vec in embeddings.items():
        if word in exclude:
            continue
        sim = cosine_similarity(target, vec)
        if sim > best_sim:
            best_sim = sim
            best_word = word
    return best_word, best_sim


def main() -> None:
    # Deterministic continuous semantic embedding space (d = 6 dimensions):
    # Latent dimensions: [Equity-ness, Debt-ness, Payout-ness, Risk/Vol, Sovereign-ness, Corporate-ness]
    np.random.seed(42)

    embeddings: dict[str, np.ndarray] = {
        # Asset classes
        "equity": np.array([2.5, 0.1, 1.2, 1.8, 0.0, 2.0], dtype=np.float64),
        "stock": np.array([2.4, 0.1, 1.1, 1.9, 0.0, 2.1], dtype=np.float64),
        "share": np.array([2.3, 0.2, 1.0, 1.7, 0.0, 1.9], dtype=np.float64),
        "debt": np.array([0.1, 2.6, 1.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1.8], dtype=np.float64),
        "bond": np.array([0.1, 2.5, 1.5, 0.5, 1.2, 1.6], dtype=np.float64),
        "treasury": np.array([0.0, 2.4, 1.3, 0.2, 2.5, 0.0], dtype=np.float64),
        # Payout mechanisms
        "dividend": np.array([1.8, 0.1, 2.8, 0.4, 0.0, 1.8], dtype=np.float64),
        "coupon": np.array([0.1, 2.2, 2.7, 0.2, 1.0, 1.5], dtype=np.float64),
        # Executive leadership
        "ceo": np.array([0.8, 0.5, 0.2, 1.0, 0.0, 3.0], dtype=np.float64),
        "president": np.array([0.2, 0.4, 0.1, 0.8, 2.8, 0.5], dtype=np.float64),
        "firm": np.array([1.2, 1.0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.0, 2.8], dtype=np.float64),
        "nation": np.array([0.1, 0.8, 0.2, 0.7, 3.0, 0.1], dtype=np.float64),
    }

    # 1. Cosine similarity checks
    sim_equity_stock = cosine_similarity(embeddings["equity"], embeddings["stock"])
    sim_equity_share = cosine_similarity(embeddings["equity"], embeddings["share"])
    sim_equity_debt = cosine_similarity(embeddings["equity"], embeddings["debt"])
    sim_debt_bond = cosine_similarity(embeddings["debt"], embeddings["bond"])

    print("--- Cosine Similarity Geometry ---")
    print(f"cos(equity, stock)    : {sim_equity_stock:.4f}")
    print(f"cos(equity, share)    : {sim_equity_share:.4f}")
    print(f"cos(equity, debt)     : {sim_equity_debt:.4f}")
    print(f"cos(debt, bond)       : {sim_debt_bond:.4f}")

    # 2. Vector analogy 1: equity - dividend + coupon ≈ ?
    # Analogy hypothesis: What is to coupon as equity is to dividend? Target: debt / bond
    query_vec_1 = embeddings["equity"] - embeddings["dividend"] + embeddings["coupon"]
    nearest_word_1, nearest_sim_1 = find_nearest(
        query_vec_1, embeddings, exclude={"equity", "dividend", "coupon", "stock", "share"}
    )

    print("\n--- Financial Vector Analogy 1 ---")
    print("Equation: v(equity) - v(dividend) + v(coupon)")
    print(f"Nearest semantic concept: '{nearest_word_1}' (cosine similarity = {nearest_sim_1:.4f})")

    # 3. Vector analogy 2: ceo - firm + nation ≈ ?
    query_vec_2 = embeddings["ceo"] - embeddings["firm"] + embeddings["nation"]
    nearest_word_2, nearest_sim_2 = find_nearest(query_vec_2, embeddings, exclude={"ceo", "firm", "nation"})

    print("\n--- Structural Vector Analogy 2 ---")
    print("Equation: v(ceo) - v(firm) + v(nation)")
    print(f"Nearest semantic concept: '{nearest_word_2}' (cosine similarity = {nearest_sim_2:.4f})")

    print("\nVerification Invariant: Synonymous financial assets share cosine similarity > 0.95.")


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks

While static word embeddings resolve the synonymy and sparsity limitations of bag-of-words, they suffer from two major breakdowns in quantitative finance:

  • Polysemy and Semantic Collapse: Static embeddings assign a single fixed vector \(\mathbf{v}_w\) to each word token. In finance, terms frequently have dual meanings depending on context: 'yield' denotes investment return (fixed income) or physical output (agriculture/mining); 'default' denotes contractual credit insolvency or software initial settings. Static embeddings average both meanings into a single blurred vector.
  • Temporal Lookahead Leakage: Training word embeddings on a multi-decade text archive leaks future semantic associations into historical backtests. For example, in 2005, 'subprime' had an innocuous technical mortgage meaning; after 2008, its embedding shifted dramatically toward 'crisis', 'liquidity', and 'default'. Using an embedding trained on post-2008 text during a 2006 backtest leaks catastrophic lookahead.

5. Misconceptions

  1. Pretrained word embeddings from Google News or Wikipedia can be applied directly to SEC filings without performance loss.

    General domain embeddings conflate financial terms with colloquial definitions. In Wikipedia, 'liability' associates with moral responsibility; in 10-K filings, it associates with balance sheet leverage and debt covenants. Financial domain pretraining is essential.

  2. Higher embedding dimensionality \(d\) always improves quantitative factor performance.

    Increasing embedding dimension \(d > 300\) dramatically inflates parameter estimation noise in low-SNR return prediction models. Overparameterized embeddings memorize spurious token co-occurrences, degrading out-of-sample portfolio Sharpe ratios.

  3. Word embeddings can be updated incrementally in live trading without altering historical factor exposures.

    Because embedding optimization rotates and scales the latent latent coordinate axes, online retraining rotates the entire vector space. Without orthogonal Procrustes alignment, historical factor weights become invalid.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Let vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1 = [2.0, 1.0, 0.0]^\top\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2 = [1.0, 2.0, 0.0]^\top\) represent two financial concepts. Calculate their Euclidean norms, dot product, and cosine similarity.

Worked solution

Norms are \(\|\mathbf{v}_1\|_2 = \sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.2361\) and \(\|\mathbf{v}_2\|_2 = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.2361\). Dot product is \(\mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{v}_2 = 2(1) + 1(2) + 0(0) = 4.0\). Cosine similarity is \(\frac{4.0}{\sqrt{5} \times \sqrt{5}} = \frac{4.0}{5.0} = 0.8000\).

In SGNS, let center word embedding be \(\mathbf{v} = [0.8, -0.6]^\top\) and context word embedding be \(\mathbf{u} = [0.5, 0.5]^\top\). Calculate the predicted probability \(\sigma(\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{u})\) that this word pair is a true context co-occurrence.

Worked solution

Dot product is \(\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{u} = 0.8(0.5) + (-0.6)(0.5) = 0.40 - 0.30 = 0.10\). Sigmoid evaluates to \(\sigma(0.10) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-0.10}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.9048} = \frac{1}{1.9048} \approx 0.5250\).

In a corpus of \(N = 10^7\) tokens, words \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) have unigram counts \(N_1 = 5,000\) and \(N_2 = 2,000\), with co-occurrence count \(N_{1,2} = 500\). Calculate the Pointwise Mutual Information \(\text{PMI}(w_1, w_2)\) in natural units.

Worked solution

Probabilities: \(P(w_1) = \frac{5,000}{10^7} = 5 \times 10^{-4}\), \(P(w_2) = \frac{2,000}{10^7} = 2 \times 10^{-4}\), and joint \(P(w_1, w_2) = \frac{500}{10^7} = 5 \times 10^{-5}\). Expected independence probability is \(P(w_1) P(w_2) = 10^{-7}\). Thus \(\text{PMI} = \ln\left(\frac{5 \times 10^{-5}}{10^{-7}}\right) = \ln(500) \approx 6.2146\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that the cosine similarity between any two vectors is invariant under any orthogonal transformation matrix \(\mathbf{Q} \in \R^{d \times d}\) satisfying \(\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}\).

Worked solution

Let \(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_1 = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_2 = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{v}_2\). The transformed inner product is \(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_1^\top \tilde{\mathbf{v}}_2 = (\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{v}_1)^\top (\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{v}_2) = \mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{I} \mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{v}_2\). The transformed norm is \(\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i\|_2 = \sqrt{\mathbf{v}_i^\top \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{v}_i} = \|\mathbf{v}_i\|_2\). Therefore \(\cos(\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{v}_2) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{v}_2}{\|\mathbf{v}_1\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}_2\|_2} = \cos(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2)\), proving rotational invariance.

Prove that if word embeddings \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\) have unit \(L_2\) norm, their Euclidean distance squared satisfies \(\|\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2\|_2^2 = 2(1 - \cos(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2))\).

Worked solution

Expanding the squared norm: \(\|\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2\|_2^2 = (\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2)^\top (\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2) = \mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2^\top \mathbf{v}_2 - 2 \mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{v}_2\). Substituting unit norms \(\|\mathbf{v}_1\|_2^2 = 1\) and \(\|\mathbf{v}_2\|_2^2 = 1\), and noting \(\mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{v}_2 = \cos(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2)\), yields \(\|\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2\|_2^2 = 1 + 1 - 2 \cos(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = 2(1 - \cos(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2))\).

Prove that the optimal alignment between two embedding matrices \(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B} \in \R^{V \times d}\) across temporal regimes under orthogonal Procrustes is given by \(\mathbf{Q}^* = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{V}^\top\), where \(\mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top\).

Worked solution

The Procrustes problem minimizes \(\|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B} \mathbf{Q}\|_F^2 = \tr(\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A}) + \tr(\mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{B}) - 2 \tr(\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{A})\). Maximizing \(\tr(\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{A})\) with \(\mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top\) gives \(\tr(\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top) = \tr(\mathbf{V}^\top \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma}) = \tr(\mathbf{Z} \mathbf{\Sigma})\) where \(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{V}^\top \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{U}\) is orthogonal. Since \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is diagonal and positive, \(\tr(\mathbf{Z} \mathbf{\Sigma}) = \sum z_{i,i} \sigma_i \le \sum \sigma_i\). Maximum occurs when \(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{I} \implies \mathbf{V}^\top \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{U} = \mathbf{I} \implies \mathbf{Q}^* = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{V}^\top\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

How does semantic drift in word embeddings track shifting central bank monetary policy regimes?

Worked solution

By training separate time-sliced embeddings on FOMC meeting minutes and aligning them via orthogonal Procrustes, researchers measure the cosine distance of 'inflation' to 'transitory' versus 'persistent'. During regime shifts (such as 2021–2022), the rapid geometric trajectory of 'inflation' toward 'hike' and 'tightening' anticipates rate hikes months before policy actions.

What empirical Sharpe ratio improvement is achieved by replacing bag-of-words document vectors with average word embedding vectors in industry classification?

Worked solution

Average word embedding vectors (e.g. 200-dimensional centroid representations) bridge synonym gaps that degrade bag-of-words. Industry peer momentum strategies formed using embedding centroids achieve 25–40% higher risk-adjusted Sharpe ratios due to reduced turnover and cleaner industry boundary identification.

Why does training Word2Vec on small firm 10-Ks require larger negative sampling \(K \ge 15\)?

Worked solution

Small-firm filings have smaller corpus token mass and higher vocabulary sparsity. Increasing the negative sampling ratio \(K\) from 5 to 15–20 strengthens the regularization signal on rare words, preventing unobserved context pairs from receiving spuriously high inner products.

Tier 4 · Research

Formulate the econometric risk of 'temporal lookahead leakage' when using static word embeddings in backtesting quantitative alpha strategies.

Worked solution

If embeddings are trained on the full corpus \(\mathcal{C}_{1:T}\), the vector representation \(\mathbf{v}_{w,t}\) on date \(t < T\) incorporates semantic associations formed by post-\(t\) events (e.g. 'Lehman' linking to 'default'). This leaks future market distress into historical signal generation, producing artificially inflated backtested Sharpe ratios that fail out-of-sample.

How does contextual word representation (e.g. ELMo, BERT) resolve the polysemy bottleneck of static word embeddings in credit agreement parsing?

Worked solution

Contextual models compute token representations as functions of the entire input sentence hidden states. In "The bank granted a covenant waiver", 'covenant' receives a legal flexibility representation, whereas in "The company breached its covenant", it receives a credit distress representation, separating polysemous states dynamically.

Explain how dynamic word embeddings can be trained with Kalman filter priors to enforce smooth temporal evolution without forward lookahead.

Worked solution

Bamler & Mandt (2017) specify a state-space prior \(\mathbf{v}_{w,t} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{v}_{w,t-1}, \tau^2 \mathbf{I})\) where word vectors evolve via a random walk. Using sequential online Bayesian filtering, embeddings at date \(t\) condition strictly on text observed up to date \(t\), capturing legitimate linguistic drift while strictly prohibiting future lookahead leakage.

8. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the distributional hypothesis in computational linguistics?

    Options

    The distributional hypothesis (Firth, 1957) asserts that word meaning is defined by context co-occurrence patterns.

  2. What matrix factorization is Skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS) asymptotically equivalent to?

    Options

    By Theorem 4.1, Levy & Goldberg (2014) proved that SGNS implicitly factorizes the shifted PMI matrix \(\mathbf{M} = \text{PMI} - \ln(K)\).

  3. How does vector arithmetic solve semantic analogies in word embedding space (e.g. \(A:B :: C:X\))?

    Options

    By Definition 4.1, 3CosAdd finds the closest vector to the translational offset \(\mathbf{v}_B - \mathbf{v}_A + \mathbf{v}_C\).

  4. What is 'polysemy' and why does it represent a failure mode for static word embeddings?

    Options

    Static embeddings assign a single static vector to each word, unable to differentiate contextual meanings like bond yield vs agricultural yield.

  5. Why does using word embeddings trained on post-2008 data in a 2005 backtest introduce lookahead bias?

    Options

    Training embeddings on future corpora leaks forward semantic drift and distress correlations into historical factor signals.

  6. What mathematical transformation aligns two embedding spaces across temporal regimes while preserving internal cosine distances?

    Options

    By Problem P3, orthogonal Procrustes rotates one space onto another to minimize Frobenius alignment error without altering pairwise angles.

  7. Why do word embeddings typically operate with dimensionality \(d \in [100, 300]\) rather than \(d = 10,000\)?

    Options

    Low-rank compression acts as implicit regularization, filtering idiosyncratic token noise and preventing overfitting.

  8. What is the relationship between the Euclidean distance of two unit vectors and their cosine similarity?

    Options

    By Problem P2, for unit vectors, the squared Euclidean distance is strictly \(2(1 - \cos(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2))\).
