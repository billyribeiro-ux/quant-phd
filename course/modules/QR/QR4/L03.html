Financial Domain Lexicons & Loughran-McDonald Sentiment

Early quantitative textual studies imported general-purpose psychological dictionaries, such as the Harvard IV-4 lexicon, to measure corporate sentiment. Loughran & McDonald (2011) demonstrated that this practice introduces massive measurement error: almost three-quarters of the negative words identified by Harvard in 10-K filings are routine accounting terminology (such as 'tax', 'cost', 'liability', and 'depreciation') that convey no operational distress. Domain-specific financial sentiment lexicons recalibrate word classifications to financial context, isolating genuine operational risk. Calibrating financial dictionaries is fundamental to extracting clean sentiment alpha from regulatory disclosures.

1. The Loughran-McDonald Critique of General Dictionaries

In textual sentiment analysis, a dictionary maps individual words to semantic sentiment categories (positive, negative, uncertainty, litigious). Prior to 2011, empirical researchers routinely applied general-purpose sociological dictionaries, predominantly the Harvard IV-4 / General Inquirer (GI) dictionary [loughran2011liability].

Finance-specific sentiment dictionary

A finance-specific sentiment dictionary is a curated lexical mapping \(\mathcal{L}: \mathcal{V} \to \mathcal{S}\) designed specifically for financial and accounting disclosures, where each word is categorized based on its institutional economic meaning rather than its colloquial psychological usage. The seminal Loughran-McDonald (LM) dictionary partitions financial vocabulary into distinct semantic categories:

\[ \mathcal{S} = \{\text{Negative}, \text{Positive}, \text{Uncertainty}, \text{Litigious}, \text{Constraining}, \text{Modal}\} \]

where standard business accounting terms are explicitly purged from the negative list.

Theorem 3.1 · The Harvard Misclassification Wedge

Let \(\mathcal{W}_{\text{GI}}\) denote the negative word list of the Harvard IV-4 dictionary, and let \(\mathcal{W}_{\text{LM}}\) denote the Loughran-McDonald negative financial word list. In a comprehensive sample of SEC 10-K filings from 1994 to 2008, the proportion of Harvard negative word occurrences that are non-negative in financial context exceeds 70%:

\[ \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{W}_{\text{GI}} \setminus \mathcal{W}_{\text{LM}}} \text{Count}(t)}{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{W}_{\text{GI}}} \text{Count}(t)} \approx 73.8\% \]

The top six misclassified words ('tax', 'cost', 'capital', 'board', 'liability', 'foreign') account for over 50% of all Harvard negative word counts in SEC filings.

Proof Sketch (Empirical Identification)

Loughran & McDonald (2011) examine 50,115 Form 10-K filings spanning 1994 to 2008. Across all filings, the Harvard IV-4 negative dictionary identifies 19,890,323 word tokens as negative. Loughran and McDonald manually audit the most frequent tokens. Words such as 'tax' (appearing 2,752,900 times), 'cost' (2,058,187 times), 'expense' (911,213 times), and 'liability' (539,949 times) represent standard balance sheet line items. Summing token frequencies of terms in \(\mathcal{W}_{\text{GI}}\) absent from \(\mathcal{W}_{\text{LM}}\) yields 14,682,536 occurrences, or exactly 73.82% of all Harvard negative hits.

Example 3.1 · When Is a Liability Not a Liability?

Consider the 10-K sentence: "Total current liabilities increased by $50 million due to deferred tax obligations and operational cost of goods sold." The Harvard dictionary flags 'liabilities', 'tax', and 'cost' as three negative words, scoring the sentence as severely negative. In corporate accounting, current liabilities and taxes are standard operating balances. The Loughran-McDonald dictionary classifies none of these terms as negative, correctly scoring the sentence as neutral accounting description.

2. Tone Metrics and Filing Return Response

Given the calibrated Loughran-McDonald word lists, a document's net sentiment tone is quantified by scaling the count of negative and positive words [loughran2011liability].

Definition 3.1 · Loughran-McDonald Document Tone

For a document \(d_i\) containing token sequence \(\mathcal{T}_i\) with total word count \(L_i = |\mathcal{T}_i|\), let \(N_{\text{neg}, i} = \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_i} \indic\{t \in \mathcal{W}_{\text{LM, neg}}\}\) and \(N_{\text{pos}, i} = \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_i} \indic\{t \in \mathcal{W}_{\text{LM, pos}}\}\). The net negative tone is defined as:

\[ \text{Tone}_{\text{net}, i} = \frac{N_{\text{pos}, i} - N_{\text{neg}, i}}{L_i} \]

and the sentiment polarity is:

\[ \text{Polarity}_i = \frac{N_{\text{pos}, i} - N_{\text{neg}, i}}{N_{\text{pos}, i} + N_{\text{neg}, i} + \epsilon} \]

Loughran & McDonald (2011) demonstrate that while Harvard negative tone has no statistically significant relationship with subsequent filing announcement abnormal returns (\(t = -0.66\)), Loughran-McDonald negative tone produces a highly significant negative drift (\(t = -3.85\)), establishing that domain calibration restores economic signal.

3. Inline Lab: Financial Lexicons vs General Dictionaries

The following laboratory demonstrates the distortion created by general-purpose dictionaries on routine accounting prose versus genuine financial distress disclosures.

Lab 1 · Loughran-McDonald vs General Inquirer Sentiment

Verifies that routine balance sheet descriptions are misclassified as negative by the Harvard General Inquirer dictionary, while the Loughran-McDonald lexicon correctly classifies them as neutral or positive.

"""Lab QR4-L03-1: Financial Domain Lexicons vs General Dictionaries (Loughran-McDonald 2011).

Demonstrates why general-purpose sentiment dictionaries (such as Harvard IV-4) fail in finance
by misclassifying standard business accounting terms as negative, whereas the domain-specific
Loughran & McDonald (2011) financial lexicon isolates genuine operational distress.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import re


def tokenize(text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Tokenize text into lowercase alphabetic words."""
    return re.findall(r"\b[a-z]{2,}\b", text.lower())


def score_sentiment(
    tokens: list[str],
    pos_words: set[str],
    neg_words: set[str],
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Score sentiment tone: net = (pos - neg) / total_tokens."""
    total = len(tokens)
    if total == 0:
        return {"pos_count": 0.0, "neg_count": 0.0, "net_tone": 0.0, "polarity": 0.0}

    pos_count = sum(1 for t in tokens if t in pos_words)
    neg_count = sum(1 for t in tokens if t in neg_words)
    net_tone = (pos_count - neg_count) / float(total)
    polarity = (pos_count - neg_count) / float(pos_count + neg_count) if (pos_count + neg_count) > 0 else 0.0

    return {
        "pos_count": float(pos_count),
        "neg_count": float(neg_count),
        "net_tone": float(net_tone),
        "polarity": float(polarity),
    }


def main() -> None:
    # Loughran-McDonald (2011) financial domain word sets (representative subsets)
    lm_positive = {"gain", "profitable", "exceeded", "strong", "improved", "expansion", "growth"}
    lm_negative = {"loss", "default", "restructuring", "impairment", "litigation", "adverse", "recession", "breach"}

    # Harvard-IV-4 / General Inquirer general dictionary (contains ordinary accounting nouns as negative)
    # Harvard IV-4 notoriously classifies 'tax', 'cost', 'liability', 'foreign', 'depreciation' as negative.
    gi_positive = {"gain", "profitable", "exceeded", "strong", "improved", "expansion", "growth", "share"}
    gi_negative = {
        "loss",
        "default",
        "restructuring",
        "impairment",
        "litigation",
        "adverse",
        "recession",
        "breach",
        "liability",
        "cost",
        "tax",
        "depreciation",
        "expense",
        "board",
    }

    # Two 10-K disclosures:
    # Doc A: Normal healthy balance sheet description with routine accounting terminology
    doc_a = (
        "Total liabilities increased due to deferred tax obligations and routine cost of goods sold. "
        "Depreciation expense remained steady as the board approved the annual capital allocation. "
        "Operating growth and profitable expansion exceeded our previous multi-year guidance."
    )

    # Doc B: Genuinely distressed filing describing default, breach, and litigation
    doc_b = (
        "The company incurred severe operating loss and remains in breach of debt covenants. "
        "Adverse macroeconomic headwinds prompted restructuring and asset impairment charges. "
        "Ongoing litigation poses material risk of accelerated default."
    )

    tokens_a = tokenize(doc_a)
    tokens_b = tokenize(doc_b)

    lm_a = score_sentiment(tokens_a, lm_positive, lm_negative)
    gi_a = score_sentiment(tokens_a, gi_positive, gi_negative)

    lm_b = score_sentiment(tokens_b, lm_positive, lm_negative)
    gi_b = score_sentiment(tokens_b, gi_positive, gi_negative)

    print("--- Document A: Healthy 10-K Balance Sheet Description ---")
    print(f"Total tokens: {len(tokens_a)}")
    print(
        f"Loughran-McDonald (2011): Pos={lm_a['pos_count']:.0f}, Neg={lm_a['neg_count']:.0f} | "
        f"Tone={lm_a['net_tone']:+.4f} | Polarity={lm_a['polarity']:+.2f}"
    )
    print(
        f"General Inquirer (Harvard): Pos={gi_a['pos_count']:.0f}, Neg={gi_a['neg_count']:.0f} | "
        f"Tone={gi_a['net_tone']:+.4f} | Polarity={gi_a['polarity']:+.2f}"
    )

    print("\n--- Document B: Material Distress Disclosure ---")
    print(f"Total tokens: {len(tokens_b)}")
    print(
        f"Loughran-McDonald (2011): Pos={lm_b['pos_count']:.0f}, Neg={lm_b['neg_count']:.0f} | "
        f"Tone={lm_b['net_tone']:+.4f} | Polarity={lm_b['polarity']:+.2f}"
    )
    print(
        f"General Inquirer (Harvard): Pos={gi_b['pos_count']:.0f}, Neg={gi_b['neg_count']:.0f} | "
        f"Tone={gi_b['net_tone']:+.4f} | Polarity={gi_b['polarity']:+.2f}"
    )

    # Accounting term false-negative analysis
    accounting_words = ["liability", "tax", "cost", "depreciation", "expense", "board"]
    misclassified = [w for w in accounting_words if w in gi_negative and w not in lm_negative]
    print(f"\nRoutine accounting words misclassified as negative by General Inquirer: {misclassified}")
    print(
        "Verification Invariant: LM classifies healthy disclosure as net positive; "
        "Harvard misclassifies it as net negative."
    )

4. Where the Canon Breaks

While the Loughran-McDonald lexicon is a marked improvement over general dictionaries, dictionary-based sentiment methods encounter three fundamental breakdowns in institutional trading:

  • Context and Syntactic Modifiers: Simple word counting cannot resolve conditional clauses (e.g. "We do not anticipate default unless revenues contract severely") or sarcastic/rhetorical constructions.
  • Strategic Management Camouflage: Because corporate executive teams and investor relations consultants are aware of the Loughran-McDonald word lists, corporate disclosures are actively scrubbed of explicit LM negative terms before filing, substituting passive or circumlocutory phrasing that bypasses word-list detection.

5. Misconceptions

  1. A larger dictionary with more negative words produces a more sensitive sentiment factor.

    Expanding word lists into general vocabulary admits high-frequency neutral terms, diluting true negative signal with massive measurement noise as proven by Loughran & McDonald (2011).

  2. Positive word counts in 10-K filings provide equal predictive power to negative word counts.

    Loughran & McDonald (2011) show that positive words in 10-Ks are dominated by promotional boilerplate and offer virtually zero incremental return predictability. Negative words carry almost all of the predictive alpha.

  3. Dictionary sentiment signals can be executed at the market close of the filing date.

    Most SEC filings are accepted after 16:00 ET. Executing trades on day \(t\) based on day \(t\) filing sentiment creates severe trade-on-close lookahead bias.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A 10-K filing contains \(L = 10,000\) total words. The Loughran-McDonald lexicon identifies 120 negative words and 40 positive words. Calculate the net negative tone and the sentiment polarity.

Worked solution

Net tone is \(\text{Tone} = \frac{N_{\text{pos}} - N_{\text{neg}}}{L} = \frac{40 - 120}{10,000} = \frac{-80}{10,000} = -0.0080\) or -0.80%. Polarity is \(\text{Polarity} = \frac{40 - 120}{40 + 120} = \frac{-80}{160} = -0.5000\).

In a corporate disclosure, the Harvard IV-4 dictionary identifies 50 negative words, of which 37 are routine accounting words ('cost', 'tax', 'liability', 'depreciation'). If the LM dictionary identifies only the remaining 13 words as negative, calculate the percentage misclassification rate of Harvard IV-4 on this filing.

Worked solution

The number of false negative words identified by Harvard is 37 out of 50. The misclassification rate is \(\frac{37}{50} = 0.74\) or 74.0%, exactly matching the empirical aggregate wedge identified by Loughran & McDonald (2011).

Suppose an unregularized regression of 3-day filing announcement returns on Harvard negative tone yields a slope of \(\beta_{\text{GI}} = -0.02\) with standard error \(\operatorname{se} = 0.03\), while regression on LM negative tone yields \(\beta_{\text{LM}} = -0.15\) with \(\operatorname{se} = 0.039\). Calculate the \(t\)-statistics and assess statistical significance at the 5% level.

Worked solution

For Harvard tone, \(t_{\text{GI}} = \frac{-0.02}{0.03} = -0.67\), which is statistically insignificant (\(|t| < 1.96\)). For LM tone, \(t_{\text{LM}} = \frac{-0.15}{0.039} \approx -3.85\), which is highly significant (\(|t| > 3.0\)). This confirms that removing accounting misclassifications restores predictive power.

Tier 2 · Proof

Let true economic sentiment be \(S^*\) and observed dictionary tone be \(S = S^* + \eta\), where measurement error \(\eta \sim (0, \sigma_\eta^2)\) is classical and independent of \(S^*\). Prove that in a predictive return regression \(r = \beta S + \epsilon\), the estimated OLS coefficient \(\hat{\beta}\) suffers attenuation bias proportional to the noise-to-signal variance ratio.

Worked solution

Under classical errors-in-variables, the probability limit of the OLS estimator is \(\operatorname{plim} \hat{\beta} = \frac{\Cov(r, S)}{\Var(S)} = \frac{\Cov(\beta S^* + \epsilon, S^* + \eta)}{\Var(S^* + \eta)} = \beta \frac{\Var(S^*)}{\Var(S^*) + \sigma_\eta^2} = \beta \left( \frac{1}{1 + \sigma_\eta^2 / \sigma_{S^*}^2} \right)\). Because \(\sigma_\eta^2 > 0\), the attenuation factor \(\frac{1}{1 + \sigma_\eta^2 / \sigma_{S^*}^2} < 1\), shrinking the estimated return response toward zero.

Prove that if the Harvard dictionary adds 73.8% uninformative noise tokens \(\eta\) to true negative counts \(N^*\), the attenuation bias in a return regression exceeds 70%.

Worked solution

From Problem P1, the attenuation multiplier is \(\lambda = \frac{\Var(N^*)}{\Var(N^*) + \Var(\eta)}\). If uninformative accounting words \(\eta\) account for 73.8% of the total measured variance, then \(\frac{\Var(\eta)}{\Var(N^*) + \Var(\eta)} \approx 0.738\). Thus \(\lambda = 1 - 0.738 = 0.262\), proving that the estimated slope coefficient is attenuated by over 73%, explaining why Harvard tone fails to achieve statistical significance.

Prove that the polarity metric \(\text{Polarity} = \frac{N_{\text{pos}} - N_{\text{neg}}}{N_{\text{pos}} + N_{\text{neg}}}\) is strictly bounded in \([-1, +1]\) for all non-negative counts with \(N_{\text{pos}} + N_{\text{neg}} > 0\).

Worked solution

Let \(a = N_{\text{pos}} \ge 0\) and \(b = N_{\text{neg}} \ge 0\) with \(a + b > 0\). Since \(a \ge 0\) and \(b \ge 0\), \(- (a + b) \le a - b \le a + b\). Dividing through by the strictly positive denominator \(a + b\) preserves inequalities: \(-1 \le \frac{a - b}{a + b} \le 1\). The lower bound \(-1\) is achieved when \(a = 0, b > 0\) (pure negative), and the upper bound \(+1\) is achieved when \(a > 0, b = 0\) (pure positive).

Tier 3 · Empirical

Why did Loughran & McDonald (2011) discover that positive word counts in SEC 10-K filings have no statistically significant relationship with stock returns?

Worked solution

Loughran & McDonald (2011) found that corporate managers universally employ promotional and optimistic language in annual reports regardless of financial performance. Because positive language is ubiquitous boilerplate, it lacks cross-sectional variance and provides zero incremental alpha.

What post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD) occurs following filings with extreme Loughran-McDonald negative tone?

Worked solution

Firms in the highest quintile of 10-K negative tone experience an immediate negative price response of -0.5% to -1.5% around the filing date, followed by persistent negative drift over the subsequent 60 trading days, indicating that market participants underreact to qualitative operational risks.

How does the Loughran-McDonald 'uncertainty' word category behave around macroeconomic crises compared to the 'negative' category?

Worked solution

While the negative category spikes during firm-specific earnings distress and restructurings, the uncertainty category ('fluctuate', 'approximate', 'contingency') correlates strongly with implied volatility (VIX), capital expenditure deferrals, and macroeconomic policy uncertainty shocks.

Tier 4 · Research

How do corporate investor relations departments strategically game static dictionary word lists across multi-year reporting cycles?

Worked solution

Because static dictionaries like Loughran-McDonald are publicly documented, corporate IR teams run automated pre-filing compliance checks to flag and replace designated negative words with neutral circumlocutions (e.g. replacing 'loss' with 'headwind to margin expansion'). This creates Goodhart's law decay, diminishing dictionary signal strength over time.

Contrast the econometric performance of human-curated financial dictionaries versus machine-learned supervised sentiment classifiers.

Worked solution

Human-curated dictionaries provide auditable, transparent, zero-lookahead factor definitions that never overfit sample noise. Supervised machine-learned classifiers capture subtle phrase interactions and context but risk memorizing in-sample return noise and suffering lookahead contamination across temporal regime shifts.

Why must a quantitative research note state the exact timestamp convention (acceptance datetime vs calendar date) when testing 10-K sentiment signals?

Worked solution

Over 60% of SEC EDGAR filings are processed after 16:00 ET. If an empirical test matches a filing by its calendar date to that day's closing return, the test incorporates trade-on-close lookahead. Disclosing acceptance datetime proves that the signal was formed prior to trade execution.
