Text as Data, Document-Term Representation & Tokenization

Modern quantitative finance treats qualitative corporate disclosures, central bank minutes, and earnings announcements as high-dimensional numerical data. Unstructured prose cannot be directly processed by statistical regressors or portfolio optimization engines. Instead, raw text must be systematically tokenized, filtered, and projected into a structured document-term matrix. Because financial vocabularies exhibit extreme sparsity and power-law frequency decay, naive representations explode parameter variance. Formalizing text as data establishes the mathematical foundation for converting corporate narratives into rigorous quantitative signals.

1. The High-Dimensional Text as Data Framework

In empirical asset pricing, market participants process qualitative narrative streams alongside numerical accounting variables. Gentzkow, Kelly & Taddy (2019) formulate the textual econometrics framework where unstructured text is transformed into a high-dimensional vector space amenable to statistical estimation [gentzkow2019text].

Text as data

Text as data is the econometric paradigm that represents an unstructured textual corpus \(\mathcal{C} = \{d_1, d_2, \dots, d_D\}\) of \(D\) documents as a sequence of mathematical mapping operations:

\[ \mathcal{C} \xrightarrow{\quad \text{Tokenize} \quad} \mathcal{T} \xrightarrow{\quad \text{Vocabulary Mapping} \quad} \mathbf{C} \in \N_0^{D \times V} \]

where \(\mathcal{V} = \{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_V\}\) is a discrete vocabulary of size \(V\), and \(\mathbf{C}\) is the document-term matrix (DTM) whose entry \(C_{i,j}\) records the count of vocabulary token \(w_j\) in document \(d_i\).

In corporate finance, each document \(d_i\) corresponds to a distinct regulatory disclosure (such as SEC Form 10-K Item 7 Management's Discussion and Analysis) or an earnings call conference transcript. The fundamental statistical challenge is that vocabulary dimension \(V\) routinely exceeds the cross-sectional sample size \(D\) (\(V \gg D\)), necessitating severe dimensionality reduction.

Theorem 1.1 · Sparsity and Zipfian Support Bounds

Let corpus \(\mathcal{C}\) consist of \(D\) documents with average document length \(L = \frac{1}{D} \sum_{i=1}^D \sum_{j=1}^V C_{i,j}\). If word frequencies follow Zipf's law with rank-frequency power exponent \(\alpha \ge 1\), such that the frequency of the \(r\)-th most frequent word satisfies \(f(r) \propto r^{-\alpha}\), then the proportion of non-zero entries in the document-term matrix \(\mathbf{C}\) satisfies:

\[ \text{Density}(\mathbf{C}) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^D \sum_{j=1}^V \indic\{C_{i,j} > 0\}}{D \cdot V} \le \frac{L}{V} \]

and the sparsity \(\text{Sparsity}(\mathbf{C}) = 1 - \text{Density}(\mathbf{C}) \ge 1 - \frac{L}{V}\). As vocabulary size \(V\) expands with corpus volume while individual filing length \(L\) remains bounded, \(\lim_{V \to \infty} \text{Sparsity}(\mathbf{C}) = 1\).

Proof

Each non-zero entry in row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{C}\) requires at least one token occurrence. Therefore, the number of distinct terms in document \(i\) cannot exceed its total token count: \(\sum_{j=1}^V \indic\{C_{i,j} > 0\} \le L_i = \sum_{j=1}^V C_{i,j}\). Summing over all \(D\) documents yields \(\sum_{i=1}^D \sum_{j=1}^V \indic\{C_{i,j} > 0\} \le \sum_{i=1}^D L_i = D \cdot L\). Dividing both sides by the total matrix size \(D \cdot V\) gives \(\text{Density}(\mathbf{C}) \le \frac{D \cdot L}{D \cdot V} = \frac{L}{V}\). Consequently, \(\text{Sparsity}(\mathbf{C}) = 1 - \text{Density}(\mathbf{C}) \ge 1 - \frac{L}{V}\).

Example 1.1 · SEC 10-K Filing Matrix Dimension

Consider an annual panel of Russell 3000 index firms (\(D = 3,000\) annual 10-K filings). An unfiltered vocabulary of alphanumeric tokens spans \(V = 80,000\) unique terms. If the average MD&A section contains \(L = 12,000\) tokens, the upper bound on matrix density is \(\frac{12,000}{80,000} = 0.15\). The resulting document-term matrix is at least 85% sparse. If rare words with corpus frequency below 5 are retained, the vocabulary exceeds \(V = 250,000\), driving sparsity above 95%.

2. Tokenization and Linguistic Normalization

Before constructing the document-term matrix, raw textual characters must be converted into standardized discrete linguistic units. In financial text processing, tokenization involves three sequential stages:

  • Boundary Segmentation: Dividing character sequences into tokens using whitespace and punctuation delimiters while preserving financial compounds (e.g., 'debt-to-equity', 'year-over-year').
  • Case Normalization and Filtering: Lowercasing characters and pruning punctuation, numerical digits, and non-ASCII artifacts.
  • Stopword Filtering: Removing ubiquitous functional grammatical words (e.g., 'the', 'and', 'to', 'of') that carry syntactic structure but negligible cross-sectional alpha.

Remark 1.1 · Preserving Numerical and Accounting Signs

Standard NLP pipelines in general linguistics aggressively strip all numbers. In quantitative financial disclosures, stripping numerical values removes essential quantitative signals (e.g., 'earnings grew 25%' versus 'earnings grew 0.2%'). While high-dimensional factor models often abstract numbers to focus on qualitative narrative sentiment, multi-modal architectures must treat numerical scales as distinct quantitative covariates.

3. Inline Lab: DTM Construction and Sparsity Verification

The following laboratory demonstrates the construction of a document-term matrix from corporate 10-K disclosure snippets, evaluates vocabulary mapping, and verifies the Zipfian sparsity bound.

Lab 1 · Document-Term Matrix Construction & Sparsity

Verifies that raw term-document matrices on corporate disclosures exhibit sparsity exceeding 70%, and that term frequency follows power-law decay.

"""Lab QR4-L01-1: Document-Term Matrix Construction, Vocabulary Mapping, and Sparsity.

Demonstrates high-dimensional textual representation via document-term matrices (DTM),
calculates token frequency distributions, and verifies Zipf's law rank-frequency decay
across simulated corporate 10-K disclosure snippets.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import re
from collections import Counter

import numpy as np


def tokenize(text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Tokenize text into lowercase alphabetic terms."""
    return re.findall(r"\b[a-z]{2,}\b", text.lower())


def build_vocabulary(corpus: list[str], min_df: int = 1) -> dict[str, int]:
    """Build vocabulary mapping term to column index for terms with df >= min_df."""
    doc_tokens = [set(tokenize(doc)) for doc in corpus]
    doc_counts: Counter[str] = Counter()
    for tokens in doc_tokens:
        doc_counts.update(tokens)

    vocab = {term: idx for idx, (term, count) in enumerate(sorted(doc_counts.items())) if count >= min_df}
    return vocab


def build_dtm(corpus: list[str], vocab: dict[str, int]) -> np.ndarray:
    """Build document-term count matrix of shape (D, V)."""
    d = len(corpus)
    v = len(vocab)
    dtm = np.zeros((d, v), dtype=np.int64)

    for doc_idx, doc in enumerate(corpus):
        tokens = tokenize(doc)
        for token in tokens:
            if token in vocab:
                dtm[doc_idx, vocab[token]] += 1
    return dtm


def compute_sparsity(matrix: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """Compute matrix sparsity as proportion of zero entries."""
    total_elements = matrix.size
    zero_elements = int(np.sum(matrix == 0))
    return float(zero_elements / total_elements)


def main() -> None:
    # 5 simulated 10-K Item 7 (MD&A) disclosure excerpts
    corpus = [
        "Operating revenue increased primarily due to higher cloud infrastructure subscription volume.",
        "Operating expenses grew as research and development headcount expanded significantly across divisions.",
        "Liquidity and capital resources remain sufficient to fund debt service and capital expenditure obligations.",
        "Revenue growth moderated in commercial segments due to supply chain disruption and inflation pressures.",
        "Operating cash flows declined reflecting working capital requirements and higher inventory levels.",
    ]

    vocab = build_vocabulary(corpus, min_df=1)
    dtm = build_dtm(corpus, vocab)
    sparsity = compute_sparsity(dtm)

    print(f"Corpus documents: {len(corpus)}")
    print(f"Vocabulary size: {len(vocab)} unique terms")
    print(f"DTM shape: {dtm.shape}")
    print(f"DTM sparsity: {sparsity:.4f}")

    # Term total occurrences across corpus
    total_counts = np.sum(dtm, axis=0)
    sorted_indices = np.argsort(-total_counts)
    top_terms = [(list(vocab.keys())[idx], int(total_counts[idx])) for idx in sorted_indices[:5]]

    print("Top 5 most frequent terms in disclosures:")
    for term, count in top_terms:
        print(f"  {term:15s}: {count}")

    # Document lengths
    doc_lengths = np.sum(dtm, axis=1)
    print(f"Mean document length: {np.mean(doc_lengths):.1f} tokens")
    print(f"Min/Max document length: {np.min(doc_lengths)} / {np.max(doc_lengths)} tokens")
    print("Verification Invariant: Sparsity strictly exceeds 0.70 in raw term-document matrices.")


main()

The experiment confirms that even across concise 12-token corporate disclosure snippets, vocabulary expansion creates an empirical sparsity of 75.60%, conforming to Theorem 1.1.

4. Where the Canon Breaks

The classical statistical learning literature assumes that features are low-dimensional, dense, and conditionally Gaussian or elliptical. In financial textual analysis, this paradigm breaks down completely:

  • High-Dimensional Collinearity: Financial disclosures exhibit extreme vocabulary collinearity. Synonymous terms ('operating income', 'operating profit', 'EBIT') load on identical economic underlying cash flows, producing singular empirical covariance matrices.
  • Non-Stationary Vocabulary Drift: Regulatory standards, emerging technologies, and accounting terminology evolve across time. A vocabulary calibrated on 2005 filings fails to capture terms such as 'cloud infrastructure', 'generative AI', or 'pandemic supply disruption', causing out-of-sample feature attrition.

5. Misconceptions

  1. Textual data analysis requires retaining every word in a document to preserve economic meaning.

    Retaining idiosyncratic rare words inflates the vocabulary dimension \(V\), exponentially increasing estimator variance in low-SNR regimes without adding predictive power. Aggressive vocabulary pruning and minimum document frequency thresholds are essential.

  2. Financial text can be modeled with standard off-the-shelf NLP stopword lists.

    Standard general-purpose stopword lists frequently filter words that carry decisive financial meaning, such as 'interest', 'capital', or 'more', distorting accounting context.

  3. Document-term matrices preserve the sequential syntactic arguments of corporate disclosures.

    A raw document-term matrix collapses sequential syntax into unconditional term counts, discarding word order and negation scoping unless extended to n-grams or sequence models.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Let a corpus contain \(D = 500\) 10-K filings with an average length of \(L = 8,000\) words. If the vocabulary contains \(V = 40,000\) unique terms, calculate the theoretical lower bound on matrix sparsity.

Worked solution

By Theorem 1.1, the matrix density satisfies \(\text{Density}(\mathbf{C}) \le \frac{L}{V} = \frac{8,000}{40,000} = 0.20\). Therefore, the theoretical lower bound on matrix sparsity is \(1 - \frac{L}{V} = 1 - 0.20 = 0.80\) or 80.0%.

Suppose a filing contains the term 'profit' 16 times and 'litigation' 4 times. Calculate the ratio of their raw counts versus their sublinearly scaled weights \(w(t) = 1 + \ln(c(t))\).

Worked solution

The raw count ratio is \(\frac{16}{4} = 4.0\). Under sublinear scaling, \(w(\text{profit}) = 1 + \ln(16) = 1 + 2.7726 = 3.7726\), and \(w(\text{litigation}) = 1 + \ln(4) = 1 + 1.3863 = 2.3863\). The sublinear ratio is \(\frac{3.7726}{2.3863} \approx 1.581\), dampening the extreme count advantage by 60.5%.

In a corpus of \(D = 1,000\) documents, word \(w_j\) appears in 10 documents. Calculate its document frequency \(\text{df}_j\) and smooth inverse document frequency \(\text{idf}_j = \ln\left(\frac{1 + D}{1 + \text{df}_j}\right) + 1\).

Worked solution

The document frequency is \(\text{df}_j = 10\). Smooth IDF evaluates to \(\ln\left(\frac{1 + 1000}{1 + 10}\right) + 1 = \ln\left(\frac{1001}{11}\right) + 1 = \ln(91.0) + 1 = 4.5109 + 1 = 5.5109\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that under Zipf's law with rank exponent \(\alpha = 1\) across a vocabulary of size \(V\), the total corpus word count scales logarithmically with vocabulary size.

Worked solution

Under Zipf's law with \(\alpha = 1\), the frequency of the \(r\)-th word is \(f(r) = \frac{C_0}{r}\) for normalizing constant \(C_0\). Total corpus token count is \(N_{\text{total}} = \sum_{r=1}^V f(r) = C_0 \sum_{r=1}^V \frac{1}{r}\). The harmonic sum satisfies \(\sum_{r=1}^V \frac{1}{r} = \ln(V) + \emgamma + \mathcal{O}(1/V)\), where \(\emgamma \approx 0.5772\) is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Thus \(N_{\text{total}} \sim C_0 \ln(V)\), scaling logarithmically with vocabulary dimension.

Prove that if two documents \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) have non-overlapping vocabularies, their cosine similarity in the document-term vector space is identically zero.

Worked solution

Let \(\mathbf{c}_1, \mathbf{c}_2 \in \R^V\) be the document-term count vectors. Cosine similarity is \(\cos(\mathbf{c}_1, \mathbf{c}_2) = \frac{\mathbf{c}_1^\top \mathbf{c}_2}{\|\mathbf{c}_1\|_2 \|\mathbf{c}_2\|_2}\). If \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) share no vocabulary terms, for every index \(j \in \{1, \dots, V\}\), either \(c_{1,j} = 0\) or \(c_{2,j} = 0\), implying \(c_{1,j} c_{2,j} = 0\). Thus \(\mathbf{c}_1^\top \mathbf{c}_2 = \sum_{j=1}^V c_{1,j} c_{2,j} = 0\), so \(\cos(\mathbf{c}_1, \mathbf{c}_2) = 0\).

Prove that adding a constant scalar \(k > 0\) to all document-term counts before computing cosine similarity produces strictly positive spurious similarity between orthogonal documents.

Worked solution

Let \(\mathbf{c}_1, \mathbf{c}_2\) be non-negative orthogonal vectors (\(\mathbf{c}_1^\top \mathbf{c}_2 = 0\)). Let \(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_i = \mathbf{c}_i + k \mathbf{1}\). The inner product becomes \(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_1^\top \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_2 = (\mathbf{c}_1 + k \mathbf{1})^\top (\mathbf{c}_2 + k \mathbf{1}) = \mathbf{c}_1^\top \mathbf{c}_2 + k \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{c}_1 + k \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{c}_2 + k^2 V = 0 + k \|\mathbf{c}_1\|_1 + k \|\mathbf{c}_2\|_1 + k^2 V > 0\). Because norms are strictly positive, \(\cos(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_1, \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_2) > 0\), proving spurious similarity.

Tier 3 · Empirical

In Lab 1, why does pruning terms with document frequency \(\text{df} < 2\) dramatically compress vocabulary dimension while retaining most corpus token mass?

Worked solution

In accordance with Zipf's law and Heaps' law, a massive proportion of unique vocabulary words are hapax legomena (words appearing exactly once in the entire corpus). Filtering hapax legomena eliminates 40–60% of vocabulary columns \(V\) while discarding less than 5% of total corpus token mass, vastly reducing matrix sparsity and estimator variance.

How does Gentzkow, Kelly & Taddy (2019) recommend handling high-dimensional text vectors in predictive regressions?

Worked solution

Gentzkow, Kelly & Taddy (2019) demonstrate that because text features \(V \gg D\) suffer extreme collinearity, researchers must apply penalized regression (LASSO/Ridge) or sufficient dimension reduction (topic modeling, Latent Dirichlet Allocation, or word embeddings) before predicting economic outcomes.

What empirical failure occurs when training a sentiment dictionary on contemporaneous market returns without a temporal validation embargo?

Worked solution

When dictionary terms are selected based on their in-sample correlation with contemporaneous returns, words associated with idiosyncratic noise spikes receive extreme weights. Out-of-sample, these spuriously weighted terms fail to generalize, producing negative out-of-sample \(R^2\).

Tier 4 · Research

Contrast the econometric trade-offs between bag-of-words document-term representations versus distributed sequence representations for corporate 10-K filings.

Worked solution

Bag-of-words matrices are transparent, linear, computationally lightweight, and directly interpretable via factor loadings, but they discard syntactic word order, negation, and long-range semantic dependencies. Distributed sequence models capture syntactic context and semantic nuance but introduce massive parameter overhead, non-convex optimization, and acute risk of temporal lookahead contamination.

How does vocabulary truncation (setting a maximum vocabulary cutoff \(V_{\max}\)) introduce omitted-variable bias into textual asset pricing factors?

Worked solution

Truncating rare vocabulary terms discards specialized technical or emerging corporate risks (e.g., 'subprime' in 2006, 'coronavirus' in 2019). If those excluded terms are correlated with included vocabulary and forward asset distress, the estimated factor coefficients suffer omitted-variable bias.

Why does SEC disclosure boilerplate text create artificial common factor structure in corporate text matrices?

Worked solution

Corporate legal departments copy standardized regulatory language verbatim across firms. This boilerplate text inflates common document-term co-occurrence, introducing a massive uninformative market-wide factor that must be residualized to isolate firm-specific operational alpha.

8. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What does entry \(C_{i,j}\) represent in a document-term matrix \(\mathbf{C} \in \N_0^{D \times V}\)?

    Options

    By Definition 1.1, \(C_{i,j}\) records the count of vocabulary term \(w_j\) in document \(d_i\).

  2. Why does the document-term matrix of financial disclosures exhibit extreme sparsity (typically > 80%)?

    Options

    By Theorem 1.1, matrix density is bounded by \(L/V\). Since \(V\) is large and individual documents utilize a tiny fraction of the global lexicon, most matrix cells are zero.

  3. What empirical regularity is described by Zipf's law in textual corpora?

    Options

    Zipf's law states that the \(r\)-th most frequent word has frequency proportional to \(r^{-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha \approx 1\).

  4. What is a primary danger of stripping all numerical digits during financial text tokenization?

    Options

    Numbers convey vital economic scale and performance metrics in financial disclosures that qualitative words alone cannot capture.

  5. What are 'hapax legomena' in corpus linguistics?

    Options

    Hapax legomena are words occurring only once. They inflate feature dimensionality and are typically pruned by minimum document frequency thresholds.

  6. Why do Gentzkow, Kelly & Taddy (2019) emphasize dimensionality reduction for textual data in economics?

    Options

    When \(V \gg D\), standard OLS regressions are underdetermined, requiring regularization or projection onto low-dimensional latent topic spaces.

  7. What effect does sublinear term-frequency weighting \(w(t) = 1 + \ln(c(t))\) have on high-frequency terms?

    Options

    Logarithmic scaling prevents long documents with dozens of repetitive mentions from dominating the sentiment score.

  8. Why does boilerplate legal text across corporate 10-K filings distort document similarity metrics?

    Options

    Boilerplate legal disclosures share identical standardized paragraphs, inducing spurious common factor correlations unless residualized.
