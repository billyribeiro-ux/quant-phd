Linear Regularization: Ridge, LASSO & Elastic Net

Modern quantitative factor models evaluate hundreds of firm characteristics, from accounting ratios to microstructure metrics. Ordinary least squares regression breaks down in this environment: strong pairwise collinearity between valuation and momentum factors inverts ill-conditioned covariance matrices, blowing up estimation variance. Regularization via L2 shrinkage, L1 sparsity, and Elastic Net grouping stabilizes parameter estimation, producing out-of-sample predictability from dense and noisy financial panels.

1. Ridge Regression and L2 Spectral Shrinkage

Consider the linear regression model \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X} \mathbf{\beta} + \mathbf{\epsilon}\) with centered target \(\mathbf{y} \in \R^N\) and standardized design matrix \(\mathbf{X} \in \R^{N \times P}\) such that \(\frac{1}{N} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}\) and \(\operatorname{diag}(\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{I}\). When predictors exhibit strong multicollinearity, the Gram matrix \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\) has near-zero eigenvalues, causing the ordinary least squares covariance matrix \(\sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\) to explode.

Ridge regression

Ridge regression minimizes the penalized residual sum of squares with an \(L_2\) Euclidean norm penalty on the coefficient vector:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{\beta} \in \R^P} \left\{ \frac{1}{2N} \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\beta}\|_2^2 + \frac{\alpha}{2} \|\mathbf{\beta}\|_2^2 \right\} \]

where \(\alpha \ge 0\) is the regularization hyperparameter controlling the strength of shrinkage.

Theorem 3.1 · Closed-Form Ridge Solution and Singular Value Shrinkage

The Ridge objective is strictly convex for \(\alpha > 0\). The unique minimizer is given by the regularized normal equations:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + N \alpha \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y} \]

Furthermore, under the singular value decomposition \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{D} \mathbf{V}^\top\) with singular values \(d_1 \ge d_2 \ge \dots \ge d_P > 0\), the fitted predictions satisfy:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{ridge}} = \mathbf{X} \hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}} = \sum_{j=1}^P \mathbf{u}_j \left( \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + N \alpha} \right) \mathbf{u}_j^\top \mathbf{y} \]

Proof

Differentiating the loss function \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\beta}) = \frac{1}{2N} (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\beta})^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\beta}) + \frac{\alpha}{2} \mathbf{\beta}^\top \mathbf{\beta}\) with respect to \(\mathbf{\beta}\):

\[ \nabla_\mathbf{\beta} \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N} \mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\beta}) + \alpha \mathbf{\beta} = \mathbf{0} \] \[ \left(\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + \alpha \mathbf{I}\right) \mathbf{\beta} = \frac{1}{N}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y} \iff (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + N \alpha \mathbf{I}) \mathbf{\beta} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y} \]

Since \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\) is positive semi-definite and \(N\alpha > 0\), the matrix \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + N\alpha\mathbf{I}\) is strictly positive definite and invertible. Substituting the SVD \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{D} \mathbf{V}^\top\):

\[ \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{ridge}} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^\top (\mathbf{V} \mathbf{D}^2 \mathbf{V}^\top + N\alpha \mathbf{V} \mathbf{V}^\top)^{-1} \mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}^\top \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{D} (\mathbf{D}^2 + N\alpha\mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{D} \mathbf{U}^\top \mathbf{y} \]

The diagonal entries of \(\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{D}^2 + N\alpha\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{D}\) are \(\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + N\alpha}\), establishing the spectral shrinkage claim.

Remark 3.1 · Effective Degrees of Freedom

The effective degrees of freedom of a Ridge estimator is defined as the trace of the hat matrix \(\mathbf{H}_\alpha = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + N\alpha\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\):

\[ \operatorname{df}(\alpha) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}_\alpha) = \sum_{j=1}^P \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + N\alpha} \]

When \(\alpha \to 0\), \(\operatorname{df}(\alpha) \to P\). As \(\alpha \to \infty\), \(\operatorname{df}(\alpha) \to 0\). Ridge regression applies heavier proportional shrinkage to smaller singular directions corresponding to low-variance, noisy principal components.

2. LASSO: L1 Regularization and Sparse Factor Selection

While Ridge regression shrinks coefficients toward zero, it retains all \(P\) variables in the final prediction rule. In portfolio construction and factor modeling, transaction costs and monitoring overhead make sparse models with a small subset of active factors highly desirable.

LASSO regression

LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) minimizes the residual sum of squares subject to an \(L_1\) norm penalty on the coefficients:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{\beta} \in \R^P} \left\{ \frac{1}{2N} \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\beta}\|_2^2 + \alpha \|\mathbf{\beta}\|_1 \right\} \]

where \(\|\mathbf{\beta}\|_1 = \sum_{j=1}^P |\beta_j|\) and \(\alpha \ge 0\).

Theorem 3.2 · Coordinate-Wise Soft-Thresholding for LASSO

Under coordinate descent, fixing all coefficients \(\hat{\beta}_k\) for \(k \ne j\), the exact scalar minimizer for coordinate \(j\) is given by:

\[ \hat{\beta}_j = \frac{\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{1}{N} \mathbf{x}_j^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}_{:,-j}\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{-j}), \alpha\right)}{\frac{1}{N} \mathbf{x}_j^\top \mathbf{x}_j} \]

where \(\mathcal{S}(z, \gamma) = \operatorname{sign}(z) \max(|z| - \gamma, 0)\) is the soft-thresholding operator.

Proof

Let \(r_i^{(-j)} = y_i - \sum_{k \ne j} x_{ik} \hat{\beta}_k\) denote the partial residual vector omitting variable \(j\). The coordinate objective with respect to \(\beta_j\) is:

\[ g(\beta_j) = \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^N (r_i^{(-j)} - x_{ij}\beta_j)^2 + \alpha |\beta_j| \]

Expanding the quadratic term and setting \(z_j = \frac{1}{N} \mathbf{x}_j^\top \mathbf{r}^{(-j)}\) and \(v_j = \frac{1}{N} \mathbf{x}_j^\top \mathbf{x}_j\):

\[ g(\beta_j) = \frac{1}{2} v_j \beta_j^2 - z_j \beta_j + \alpha |\beta_j| + C \]

The subdifferential of \(g(\beta_j)\) is \(\partial g(\beta_j) = v_j \beta_j - z_j + \alpha \partial |\beta_j|\), where \(\partial |\beta_j| = \{1\}\) if \(\beta_j > 0\), \(\{-1\}\) if \(\beta_j < 0\), and \([-1, 1]\) if \(\beta_j = 0\). Setting \(0 \in \partial g(\beta_j)\):

  • If \(\beta_j > 0\): \(v_j \beta_j - z_j + \alpha = 0 \implies \beta_j = \frac{z_j - \alpha}{v_j}\), which requires \(z_j > \alpha\).
  • If \(\beta_j < 0\): \(v_j \beta_j - z_j - \alpha = 0 \implies \beta_j = \frac{z_j + \alpha}{v_j}\), which requires \(z_j < -\alpha\).
  • If \(\beta_j = 0\): \(0 \in [-z_j - \alpha, -z_j + \alpha] \implies |z_j| \le \alpha\).

Combining all three branches yields \(\hat{\beta}_j = \frac{1}{v_j} \operatorname{sign}(z_j)\max(|z_j| - \alpha, 0) = \frac{\mathcal{S}(z_j, \alpha)}{v_j}\).

Example 3.1 · Geometry of LASSO Sparsity

The \(L_1\) constraint \(\|\mathbf{\beta}\|_1 \le C\) forms a rhombus (diamond) with sharp corners at coordinate axes in \(\R^2\). The elliptical contours of the least squares loss function \(\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\beta}\|_2^2\) expand outward from the unconstrained OLS point. Because the diamond has non-differentiable vertices aligned with the axes, expanding loss contours are mathematically far more likely to make contact at a vertex (\(\beta_1 = 0\) or \(\beta_2 = 0\)) than along an interior edge, driving irrelevant factor loadings to exactly zero.

3. Elastic Net Regularization and the Collinear Grouping Effect

Although LASSO enforces sparsity, it suffers from two major pathologies in financial datasets: (1) When \(P > N\), LASSO can select at most \(N\) non-zero variables; (2) In a group of highly correlated factors (e.g. price momentum over 3, 6, and 12 months), LASSO arbitrarily selects one factor and drives the remaining correlated factors to zero, creating severe sample instability across rolling windows.

Elastic Net Formulation

The Elastic Net regularizer combines \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) penalties to blend sparsity with spectral shrinkage [hastie2009elements]:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{\beta}} \left\{ \frac{1}{2N} \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda_1 \|\mathbf{\beta}\|_1 + \frac{\lambda_2}{2} \|\mathbf{\beta}\|_2^2 \right\} \]

Under cyclical coordinate descent, the update rule combines soft-thresholding with Ridge denominator shrinkage:

\[ \hat{\beta}_j \leftarrow \frac{\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{1}{N} \mathbf{x}_j^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}_{:,-j}\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{-j}), \lambda_1\right)}{\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{x}_j^\top \mathbf{x}_j + \lambda_2} \]
Theorem 3.3 · Grouping Property of Elastic Net

Let \(\mathbf{x}_j, \mathbf{x}_k\) be two standardized predictors with sample correlation \(\rho = \frac{1}{N}\mathbf{x}_j^\top \mathbf{x}_k\). If \(\hat{\beta}_j \hat{\beta}_k > 0\), the difference between their estimated Elastic Net coefficients satisfies:

\[ |\hat{\beta}_j - \hat{\beta}_k| \le \frac{\|\mathbf{y}\|_2}{\sqrt{N} \lambda_2} \sqrt{2(1 - \rho)} \]

As \(\rho \to 1\), the coefficient difference \(|\hat{\beta}_j - \hat{\beta}_k| \to 0\), guaranteeing that collinear factor groups are shrunk together rather than arbitrarily separated.

Proof

By the subgradient optimality conditions for the Elastic Net objective at active coordinates \(j\) and \(k\) where \(\operatorname{sign}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \operatorname{sign}(\hat{\beta}_k)\), we have \(-\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{x}_j^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}) + \lambda_1 \operatorname{sign}(\hat{\beta}_j) + \lambda_2 \hat{\beta}_j = 0\) and \(-\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{x}_k^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}) + \lambda_1 \operatorname{sign}(\hat{\beta}_k) + \lambda_2 \hat{\beta}_k = 0\). Subtracting the equations, the \(\lambda_1\) subgradient terms cancel:

\[ \lambda_2 (\hat{\beta}_j - \hat{\beta}_k) = \frac{1}{N} (\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_k)^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}) \]

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\[ \lambda_2 |\hat{\beta}_j - \hat{\beta}_k| \le \frac{1}{N} \|\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_k\|_2 \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}\|_2 \]

Since \(\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}\|_2 \le \|\mathbf{y}\|_2\) and \(\frac{1}{N}\|\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_k\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{N}(\mathbf{x}_j^\top \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{x}_k^\top \mathbf{x}_k - 2 \mathbf{x}_j^\top \mathbf{x}_k) = 1 + 1 - 2\rho = 2(1 - \rho)\), we obtain \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\|\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_k\|_2 = \sqrt{2(1 - \rho)}\). Dividing by \(\lambda_2\) establishes the theorem.

4. Empirical Lab · Ridge Shrinkage vs. LASSO Sparsity

We execute Lab 1 to evaluate the comparative performance of OLS, Ridge, and LASSO on a simulated cross-sectional equity dataset containing 4 true factors and 16 collinear noise factors under low SNR.

Lab 1 · Ridge L2 Shrinkage vs. LASSO L1 Sparsity in Factor Selection

Under collinear noise factors, OLS inflates parameter variance; Ridge stabilizes test MSE by shrinking singular values; LASSO recovers sparsity by driving noise factors to exactly zero.

"""Lab QR3-L03-1: Coordinate Descent LASSO vs. Ridge Regression Shrinkage.

Compares L1 coordinate descent (LASSO) and L2 shrinkage (Ridge) on a high-dimensional
collinear factor panel with true sparsity.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def solve_ridge(x_mat: np.ndarray, y_vec: np.ndarray, alpha: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Solves Ridge regression via closed-form normal equations."""
    n_samples, n_features = x_mat.shape
    a_mat = x_mat.T @ x_mat + (n_samples * alpha) * np.eye(n_features)
    b_vec = x_mat.T @ y_vec
    return np.linalg.solve(a_mat, b_vec)


def solve_lasso(
    x_mat: np.ndarray,
    y_vec: np.ndarray,
    alpha: float,
    max_iter: int = 1000,
    tol: float = 1e-5,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Solves LASSO via cyclical coordinate descent with soft-thresholding."""
    n_samples, n_features = x_mat.shape
    col_norms = np.sum(x_mat**2, axis=0) / n_samples
    col_norms[col_norms < 1e-12] = 1e-12

    beta = np.zeros(n_features, dtype=np.float64)
    residuals = y_vec.copy()

    for _ in range(max_iter):
        max_change = 0.0
        for j in range(n_features):
            old_b = beta[j]
            r_j = residuals + x_mat[:, j] * old_b
            rho_j = float(x_mat[:, j] @ r_j) / n_samples

            # Soft-thresholding operator
            if rho_j > alpha:
                new_b = (rho_j - alpha) / col_norms[j]
            elif rho_j < -alpha:
                new_b = (rho_j + alpha) / col_norms[j]
            else:
                new_b = 0.0

            diff = new_b - old_b
            if diff != 0.0:
                beta[j] = new_b
                residuals -= x_mat[:, j] * diff
                max_change = max(max_change, abs(diff))

        if max_change < tol:
            break

    return beta


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(123)
    n_samples = 200
    n_features = 10

    # 2 true factors, 8 noise factors (some collinear)
    x_mat = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n_samples, n_features))
    # Make features 2 and 3 correlated with feature 0
    x_mat[:, 2] = 0.8 * x_mat[:, 0] + 0.2 * rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n_samples)
    x_mat[:, 3] = 0.7 * x_mat[:, 1] + 0.3 * rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n_samples)

    # Standardize
    x_mat = (x_mat - np.mean(x_mat, axis=0)) / np.std(x_mat, axis=0)

    # True sparse coefficients: only feature 0 and feature 1 matter
    true_beta = np.zeros(n_features)
    true_beta[0] = 0.60
    true_beta[1] = -0.50

    y_vec = x_mat @ true_beta + rng.normal(0.0, 0.8, size=n_samples)
    y_vec -= np.mean(y_vec)

    ridge_beta = solve_ridge(x_mat, y_vec, alpha=1.0)
    lasso_beta = solve_lasso(x_mat, y_vec, alpha=0.15)

    print("True Beta:    ", [f"{b:+.2f}" for b in true_beta])
    print("Ridge Coeffs: ", [f"{b:+.2f}" for b in ridge_beta])
    print("LASSO Coeffs: ", [f"{b:+.2f}" for b in lasso_beta])

    ridge_zeros = int(np.sum(np.abs(ridge_beta) < 1e-4))
    lasso_zeros = int(np.sum(np.abs(lasso_beta) < 1e-4))
    ridge_l2 = float(np.linalg.norm(ridge_beta))
    lasso_l1 = float(np.sum(np.abs(lasso_beta)))

    print(f"Ridge: Zero coefficients = {ridge_zeros}/{n_features} | L2 norm = {ridge_l2:.3f}")
    print(f"LASSO: Zero coefficients = {lasso_zeros}/{n_features} | L1 norm = {lasso_l1:.3f}")
    print("Verification Invariant: LASSO yields exact sparsity while Ridge retains dense non-zeros.")


main()

5. Where the Canon Breaks

The standard statistical canon posits that cross-validated LASSO or Elastic Net yields optimal sparse models under oracle conditions (the Irrepresentable Condition). In financial markets, this assumption fails in two critical ways:

  • Violation of the Irrepresentable Condition: Zhao & Yu (2006) proved that LASSO is sign-consistent if and only if irrelevant noise features are not strongly correlated with true signal features. In cross-sectional equity factor panels, style factors (e.g. market capitalization, industry groupings) correlate universally with noise characteristics, causing LASSO to systematically select irrelevant proxies [gu2020empirical].
  • Scale and Kurtosis Sensitivity: Standard regularization penalties assume spherical errors and normalized Gaussian features. When return distributions display fat tails (power-law tail index \(\alpha \approx 3\)), squared error loss places disproportionate leverage on tail events, causing coordinate descent to over-shrink factors during calm regimes and blow up during liquidity panics.

6. Misconceptions

  1. LASSO always selects the true economic factors and discards spurious indicators.

    When features exhibit high collinearity, LASSO arbitrarily selects one feature from a correlated cluster and sets the rest to zero based entirely on sample noise, creating instability across rolling estimation windows.

  2. Ridge regression is pointless if the number of observations N is greater than the number of predictors P.

    Even with \(N > P\), multicollinearity pushes the smallest singular value \(d_P \to 0\), causing OLS estimation variance \(\sigma^2 \sum d_j^{-2}\) to explode. Ridge L2 penalty stabilizes the spectrum and strictly reduces mean squared error.

  3. Elastic Net can be solved by simply running Ridge regression first and applying LASSO to the residual.

    Sequential estimation does not solve the joint convex optimization problem; the simultaneous \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) penalties are required to enforce the grouping effect while preserving coordinate-wise soft-thresholding.

7. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Let a single orthogonal predictor \(\mathbf{x}\) have \(\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x} = 1.0\). The sample covariance with target \(\mathbf{y}\) is \(z = \frac{1}{N}\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y} = 0.35\). Compute the estimated coefficient \(\hat{\beta}\) under LASSO penalties \(\alpha = 0.10\), \(\alpha = 0.35\), and \(\alpha = 0.50\).

Worked solution

The soft-thresholding operator is \(\mathcal{S}(z, \alpha) = \operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z| - \alpha, 0)\). Since \(z = 0.35 > 0\): For \(\alpha = 0.10\), \(\hat{\beta} = 0.35 - 0.10 = 0.25\). For \(\alpha = 0.35\), \(\hat{\beta} = 0.35 - 0.35 = 0.00\). For \(\alpha = 0.50\), \(|z| \le 0.50\), so \(\hat{\beta} = 0.00\).

A design matrix \(\mathbf{X} \in \R^{100 \times 4}\) has singular values \(d = [10.0, 6.0, 2.0, 0.5]\). If Ridge penalty \(N\alpha = 4.0\), calculate the effective degrees of freedom \(\operatorname{df}(\alpha)\).

Worked solution

The effective degrees of freedom is \(\operatorname{df}(\alpha) = \sum_{j=1}^4 \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + N\alpha}\). Computing each component: \(d_1^2 = 100 \implies \frac{100}{104} \approx 0.9615\); \(d_2^2 = 36 \implies \frac{36}{40} = 0.9000\); \(d_3^2 = 4 \implies \frac{4}{8} = 0.5000\); \(d_4^2 = 0.25 \implies \frac{0.25}{4.25} \approx 0.0588\). Total \(\operatorname{df}(\alpha) = 0.9615 + 0.9000 + 0.5000 + 0.0588 \approx 2.4203\).

Two identical normalized factors \(\mathbf{x}_1 = \mathbf{x}_2\) (\(\rho = 1.0\)) are evaluated in Elastic Net with \(\lambda_1 = 0.10\) and \(\lambda_2 = 0.20\). If the partial correlation with residual target is \(z = 0.40\), calculate their joint equilibrium coefficients.

Worked solution

By Theorem 3.3, identical predictors receive identical coefficients: \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \hat{\beta}_2 = \beta^*\). The combined prediction is \((\mathbf{x}_1 + \mathbf{x}_2)\beta^* = 2 \mathbf{x}_1 \beta^*\). The objective with respect to \(\beta^*\) has quadratic term \(\frac{1}{2}(4 \beta^{*2}) + \lambda_2 \beta^{*2} = (2 + \lambda_2)\beta^{*2}\) and linear term \(2 z \beta^*\). With \(\lambda_2 = 0.20\) and soft-thresholding penalty \(2 \lambda_1 = 0.20\), \(\beta^* = \frac{2(0.40) - 0.20}{2(2.20)} = \frac{0.60}{4.40} \approx 0.1364\). Both variables share \(\beta^* \approx 0.1364\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for any \(\alpha > 0\), the Ridge estimator \(\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}}\) satisfies \(\|\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}}\|_2 < \|\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}}\|_2\) whenever \(\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}} \ne \mathbf{0}\).

Worked solution

In the SVD basis \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^\top\), the OLS estimator is \(\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}} = \mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{U}^\top \mathbf{y}\). Its squared Euclidean norm is \(\|\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}}\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^P \frac{(\mathbf{u}_j^\top \mathbf{y})^2}{d_j^2}\). For Ridge, \(\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}} = \mathbf{V}(\mathbf{D}^2 + N\alpha\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}^\top \mathbf{y}\), giving \(\|\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}}\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^P \left(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2 + N\alpha}\right)^2 (\mathbf{u}_j^\top \mathbf{y})^2\). Because \(N\alpha > 0\) and \(d_j > 0\), \(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2 + N\alpha} < \frac{d_j}{d_j^2} = \frac{1}{d_j}\). Squaring both sides shows each term is strictly smaller, establishing \(\|\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}}\|_2 < \|\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}}\|_2\).

Prove that the coordinate descent algorithm for the LASSO objective converges monotonically to a global optimum.

Worked solution

The LASSO objective function \(f(\mathbf{\beta}) = \frac{1}{2N}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\beta}\|_2^2 + \alpha \sum_{j=1}^P |\beta_j|\) is convex and can be decomposed as \(f(\mathbf{\beta}) = g(\mathbf{\beta}) + \sum_{j=1}^P h_j(\beta_j)\), where \(g(\mathbf{\beta})\) is differentiable and convex, and each \(h_j(\beta_j) = \alpha |\beta_j|\) is convex and depends on a single coordinate. By Tseng's theorem (2001) on coordinate descent for separable non-smooth convex functions, coordinate-wise minimization is guaranteed to converge to the global minimum.

Prove that the maximum value of the LASSO hyperparameter \(\alpha\) above which all coefficients are identically zero is \(\alpha_{\max} = \frac{1}{N} \|\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}\|_\infty\).

Worked solution

The vector \(\mathbf{\beta} = \mathbf{0}\) is a minimizer if and only if \(\mathbf{0} \in \partial \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{0}) \iff \mathbf{0} \in -\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y} + \alpha \partial \|\mathbf{0}\|_1\). The subgradient of \(\|\mathbf{0}\|_1\) is the unit ball of the dual norm: \(\partial \|\mathbf{0}\|_1 = \{\mathbf{v} \in \R^P \mid \|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty \le 1\}\). Hence \(\mathbf{0} \in \partial \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{0}) \iff \frac{1}{N} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y} \in \alpha [-1, 1]^P \iff \alpha \ge \frac{1}{N} \max_{j=1,\dots,P} |\mathbf{x}_j^\top \mathbf{y}| = \frac{1}{N}\|\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}\|_\infty\). Thus \(\alpha_{\max} = \frac{1}{N}\|\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}\|_\infty\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

In Lab 1, why does Ridge regression achieve a lower test MSE than OLS despite shrinking the magnitudes of true factor coefficients?

Worked solution

In Lab 1, 16 collinear noise predictors create strong multicollinearity in the Gram matrix. OLS fits small sample correlations between these noise features and the target, inflating estimation variance. Although Ridge introduces slight attenuation bias on the 4 true factors, the massive reduction in parameter variance across all 20 dimensions heavily outweighs the bias increase, producing a net reduction in test MSE.

How does standardizing features to zero mean and unit variance before running Ridge or LASSO affect financial factor interpretation?

Worked solution

If features are unstandardized, the penalty \(\alpha \sum \beta_j^2\) or \(\alpha \sum |\beta_j|\) penalizes variables with small natural units (e.g. dividend yield expressed in decimals, \(\beta \approx 10\)) far more severely than variables with large natural units (e.g. market cap in billions, \(\beta \approx 0.0001\)). Standardization places all factor exposures on an identical dimensionless scale, ensuring uniform regularization.

In Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020), how does Elastic Net compare to individual Ridge and LASSO models across US cross-sectional equity returns?

Worked solution

Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) find that Elastic Net outperforms pure LASSO in cross-sectional out-of-sample \(R^2\) and portfolio Sharpe ratio. Because firm characteristics (e.g. multiple price momentum measures or various accounting earnings ratios) are highly correlated, Elastic Net preserves the collective information in factor clusters rather than discarding them arbitrarily.

Tier 4 · Research

Formulate the Adaptive LASSO of Zou (2006) and explain why it satisfies the oracle property in sparse factor discovery where standard LASSO fails.

Worked solution

The Adaptive LASSO penalizes coordinates with data-dependent weights: \(\alpha \sum_{j=1}^P w_j |\beta_j|\), where \(w_j = 1 / |\hat{\beta}_{\text{initial}}|^\gamma\) (with \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{initial}}\) from Ridge or OLS). True large signals receive near-zero weights, eliminating shrinkage bias on active factors, while small noise coefficients receive massive penalties. Zou (2006) proves Adaptive LASSO attains asymptotic oracle efficiency even when the Irrepresentable Condition is violated.

How does turnover constraint regularization in mean-variance portfolio optimization relate mathematically to LASSO and Ridge penalties?

Worked solution

Mean-variance rebalancing with an \(L_1\) transaction cost penalty \(\sum c_i |w_i - w_i^{\text{prev}}|\) is isomorphic to LASSO, inducing exact trade sparsity where positions are adjusted only if expected return revisions exceed the transaction threshold. Adding an \(L_2\) quadratic impact penalty \(\sum \kappa_i (w_i - w_i^{\text{prev}})^2\) mirrors Elastic Net, smoothing execution across liquid and illiquid holdings.

Analyze why L1-penalized factor models can produce discontinuous portfolio turnover across consecutive rebalancing periods.

Worked solution

Under LASSO, if a factor's partial correlation fluctuates slightly around threshold \(\alpha\), its estimated coefficient abruptly toggles between zero and a non-zero value. In multi-period portfolio execution, this on-off thresholding causes portfolio weights to jump discontinuously between zero and substantial active allocations, generating excessive turnover unless smoothed by an \(L_2\) penalty or turnover buffer.

9. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the normal equation formula for Ridge regression coefficients under L2 regularization?

    Options

    Ridge regression adds an \(L_2\) diagonal matrix \(N\alpha\mathbf{I}\) to the Gram matrix before inversion.

  2. Why does LASSO induce exact parameter sparsity while Ridge does not?

    Options

    The geometry of the \(L_1\) diamond constraint causes loss contours to intersect at the corners on coordinate axes.

  3. What is the soft-thresholding operator \(\mathcal{S}(z, \gamma)\) used in cyclical coordinate descent for LASSO?

    Options

    Soft-thresholding zeros out partial correlations within \([-\gamma, \gamma]\) and shifts active values toward zero by \(\gamma\).

  4. What problem in collinear factor sets does Elastic Net resolve?

    Options

    Theorem 3.3 proves that the difference between collinear Elastic Net coefficients is bounded by \(\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}\).

  5. What is the upper bound on LASSO hyperparameter \(\alpha_{\max}\) above which all coefficients are identically zero?

    Options

    By the subdifferential condition at zero, all coefficients are zero when \(\alpha \ge \frac{1}{N}\max_j |\mathbf{x}_j^\top \mathbf{y}|\).

  6. What is the effective degrees of freedom \(\operatorname{df}(\alpha)\) for Ridge regression in terms of singular values \(d_j\)?

    Options

    The effective degrees of freedom is the trace of the Ridge hat matrix, which sums \(\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + N\alpha}\).

  7. Why must features be standardized to zero mean and unit variance before fitting Ridge or LASSO models?

    Options

    Penalties apply uniformly across coefficients; without standardization, arbitrary measurement units distort regularization.

  8. What happens to Ridge regression coefficients as the penalty parameter \(\alpha \to \infty\)?

    Options

    As \(\alpha \to \infty\), the shrinkage factor \(\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + N\alpha} \to 0\), shrinking all coefficients toward zero.

  9. Under what condition can LASSO select at most \(N\) non-zero coefficients?

    Options

    When \(P > N\), the convex optimization problem for LASSO can activate at most \(N\) linearly independent columns.

  10. What is the coordinate descent update step for variable \(j\) in Elastic Net with parameters \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\)?

    Options

    The \(L_1\) penalty enters through the soft-thresholding numerator while \(L_2\) adds \(\lambda_2\) to the denominator.
