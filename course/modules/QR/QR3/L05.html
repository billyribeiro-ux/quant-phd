Bagging & Random Forests in Asset Pricing

While individual decision trees are high-variance estimators that overfit financial noise, their instability makes them the ideal base learner for ensemble averaging. Random Forests combine bootstrap aggregation with random feature subspace sampling to drive pairwise tree correlation downward, dramatically lowering ensemble variance while preserving low bias. In empirical asset pricing, this architecture captures nonlinear multi-factor interactions without suffering the catastrophic sample variance of single trees.

1. Bootstrap Aggregation and the Random Forest Architecture

Bootstrap aggregation (bagging) builds \(B\) separate training sets \(\mathcal{D}^{*1}, \mathcal{D}^{*2}, \dots, \mathcal{D}^{*B}\) by sampling \(N\) observations uniformly with replacement from training dataset \(\mathcal{D}\). A base estimator \(\hat{f}_b(\mathbf{x})\) is fit to each bootstrap sample, and the ensemble prediction is formed by simple arithmetic averaging:

\[ \bar{f}_B(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B \hat{f}_b(\mathbf{x}) \]

If the base learners were completely independent, averaging \(B\) estimators would reduce variance by a factor of \(1/B\). However, because bootstrap samples are drawn from the same underlying dataset, trees grown on different bootstrap draws are positively correlated.

Random forest

A random forest regressor is an ensemble of \(B\) randomized decision trees that injects two distinct sources of stochasticity [breiman2001random]:

  1. Instance Bagging: Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample drawn with replacement from \(\mathcal{D}\).
  2. Feature Subspace Sampling: At every candidate split node, the tree restricts candidate split features to a randomly selected subset of size \(m_{\text{try}} < P\) (typically \(m_{\text{try}} \approx \lfloor P/3 \rfloor\) or \(\lfloor \sqrt{P} \rfloor\)).

Restricting split choices prevents dominant factors (e.g. market capitalization or trailing market return) from being selected at the root of every tree, de-correlating tree predictions.

Theorem 5.1 · Ensemble Variance Decomposition

Let \(\{\hat{f}_b(\mathbf{x})\}_{b=1}^B\) be an ensemble of \(B\) randomized estimators each with marginal variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}_b(\mathbf{x})) = \sigma^2\) and pairwise Pearson correlation \(\operatorname{Corr}(\hat{f}_b(\mathbf{x}), \hat{f}_{b'}(\mathbf{x})) = \rho\) for all \(b \ne b'\). The variance of the ensemble average \(\bar{f}_B(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \hat{f}_b(\mathbf{x})\) is given by:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}_B(\mathbf{x})) = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B} \sigma^2 \]

Proof

Expanding the variance of the sample average:

\[ \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \hat{f}_b\right) = \frac{1}{B^2} \left[ \sum_{b=1}^B \operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}_b) + \sum_{b=1}^B \sum_{b' \ne b} \operatorname{Cov}(\hat{f}_b, \hat{f}_{b'}) \right] \]

Each marginal variance is \(\sigma^2\), giving sum \(B \sigma^2\). There are \(B(B - 1)\) off-diagonal covariance terms, each equal to \(\rho \sigma^2\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}_B) = \frac{1}{B^2} \left[ B \sigma^2 + B(B - 1) \rho \sigma^2 \right] = \frac{\sigma^2}{B} + \frac{B - 1}{B} \rho \sigma^2 = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B} \sigma^2 \]

As \(B \to \infty\), the second term vanishes, leaving the irreducible variance floor \(\rho \sigma^2\).

Remark 5.1 · The Role of Feature Subsampling

By Theorem 5.1, no matter how many trees \(B\) are added to the ensemble, the variance cannot fall below \(\rho \sigma^2\). Standard bagging without feature subsampling leaves trees highly correlated (\(\rho \approx 0.60\text{–}0.80\)) because all trees split on the same dominant factors. Random feature subsampling reduces \(\rho\) to \(0.15\text{–}0.30\), lowering the asymptotic variance floor by more than half.

2. Asymptotic Convergence and Immunity to Overfitting from Trees

A common misconception among algorithmic traders is that adding too many trees to a Random Forest causes overfitting. In deep learning and polynomial regression, increasing parameter count without shrinkage eventually fits noise. In Random Forests, adding trees is strictly variance-reducing.

Theorem 5.2 · Breiman's Strong Law Convergence

Let \(\Theta_b\) represent the random vector indexing the \(b\)-th tree's bootstrap sample and split selections. As the ensemble size \(B \to \infty\), the ensemble prediction converges almost surely to the conditional expectation:

\[ \bar{f}_B(\mathbf{x}) \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \E_\Theta[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \Theta)] \]

Consequently, the generalization error converges almost surely to a finite limiting constant, proving that Random Forests cannot overfit as \(B\) increases.

Proof

The sequence of random functions \(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \Theta_b)\) are identically distributed conditional on training sample \(\mathcal{D}\), with finite second moments \(\E[\hat{f}^2] < \infty\). By the Strong Law of Large Numbers for identically distributed random variables, the sample average \(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \Theta_b)\) converges almost surely to the population mean \(\E_\Theta[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \Theta)]\) for every fixed query point \(\mathbf{x}\). Because the expected squared test loss is a continuous functional of the prediction, generalization error converges to \(\E_{\mathbf{x}, y}[(y - \E_\Theta[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \Theta)])^2]\), establishing the theorem.

3. Out-of-Bag (OOB) Estimation and Financial Time Series Leakage

For each tree \(b\), the probability that an observation \(i\) is omitted from its bootstrap sample of size \(N\) is:

\[ P(i \notin \mathcal{D}^{*b}) = \left( 1 - \frac{1}{N} \right)^N \xrightarrow{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{e} \approx 36.8\% \]

Each observation is "out-of-bag" (OOB) for approximately \(37\%\) of the trees. Averaging predictions across only those trees for which observation \(i\) was OOB provides an in-sample estimate of generalization error without requiring an explicit cross-validation loop.

Remark 5.2 · OOB Failure in Asset Pricing

In standard i.i.d. tabular domains, OOB error matches 10-fold cross-validation closely. In financial time series with forward-looking labels (e.g. 5-day holding period returns), OOB estimation fails catastrophically [prado2018advances]. Observation \(t\) may be out-of-bag for tree \(b\), but tree \(b\)'s bootstrap sample includes observation \(t - 1\) or \(t + 1\), whose 5-day return windows overlap with observation \(t\). This leaks return information directly across the bag boundary, underestimating true out-of-sample error.

4. Empirical Lab · Random Forest De-correlation and Ensemble Variance

We execute Lab 1 to verify Theorem 5.1, evaluating how random feature subsampling de-correlates tree predictions and lowers ensemble variance under a multi-factor simulation with noisy characteristics.

Lab 1 · Random Forest De-correlation: Ensemble Variance Reduction

Ensemble averaging over randomized decision trees reduces prediction variance; restricting candidate features per split lowers pairwise tree correlation and reduces test MSE.

"""Lab QR3-L05-1: Random Forest Variance Reduction via Bagging and Feature Subsampling.

Demonstrates that bootstrap aggregation (bagging) and random feature subsets
de-correlate individual trees, driving down prediction variance according to
the theoretical limit Var(bar_f) = rho * sigma^2 + (1 - rho) / B * sigma^2.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


class SimpleTree:
    """Stump / shallow tree for ensemble testing."""

    def __init__(self, max_depth: int = 2) -> None:
        self.max_depth = max_depth
        self.feature = 0
        self.threshold = 0.0
        self.val_left = 0.0
        self.val_right = 0.0

    def fit(self, x_mat: np.ndarray, y_vec: np.ndarray) -> None:
        n_samples, n_features = x_mat.shape
        best_gain = -1.0
        curr_mse = float(np.var(y_vec)) * n_samples

        for f in range(n_features):
            vals = np.sort(np.unique(x_mat[:, f]))
            if len(vals) < 2:
                continue
            thresholds = (vals[:-1] + vals[1:]) / 2.0
            for th in thresholds:
                l_mask = x_mat[:, f] <= th
                n_l = int(np.sum(l_mask))
                n_r = n_samples - n_l
                if n_l < 3 or n_r < 3:
                    continue
                gain = curr_mse - (float(np.var(y_vec[l_mask])) * n_l + float(np.var(y_vec[~l_mask])) * n_r)
                if gain > best_gain:
                    best_gain = gain
                    self.feature = f
                    self.threshold = float(th)
                    self.val_left = float(np.mean(y_vec[l_mask]))
                    self.val_right = float(np.mean(y_vec[~l_mask]))

    def predict(self, x_mat: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        return np.where(x_mat[:, self.feature] <= self.threshold, self.val_left, self.val_right)


def run_ensemble(
    x_train: np.ndarray,
    y_train: np.ndarray,
    x_test: np.ndarray,
    y_test: np.ndarray,
    n_trees: int,
    subsample_feats: bool,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> float:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_samples, n_features = x_train.shape
    k_feats = max(1, int(math.sqrt(n_features))) if subsample_feats else n_features

    test_preds = np.zeros(len(y_test))
    for _ in range(n_trees):
        boot_idx = rng.choice(n_samples, size=n_samples, replace=True)
        feat_idx = np.sort(rng.choice(n_features, size=k_feats, replace=False))
        tree = SimpleTree(max_depth=2)
        tree.fit(x_train[boot_idx][:, feat_idx], y_train[boot_idx])
        test_preds += tree.predict(x_test[:, feat_idx])

    test_preds /= n_trees
    return float(np.mean((y_test - test_preds) ** 2))


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(99)
    n_train = 160
    n_test = 400
    n_features = 8

    # Multi-factor DGP with interactions
    x_train = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n_train, n_features))
    y_train = 0.5 * x_train[:, 0] - 0.4 * x_train[:, 1] + 0.3 * (x_train[:, 0] * x_train[:, 2])
    y_train += rng.normal(0.0, 0.8, size=n_train)

    x_test = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n_test, n_features))
    y_test = 0.5 * x_test[:, 0] - 0.4 * x_test[:, 1] + 0.3 * (x_test[:, 0] * x_test[:, 2])
    y_test += rng.normal(0.0, 0.8, size=n_test)

    print("Ensemble Test MSE by Number of Trees:")
    print("Trees | Standard Bagging | Random Forest (sqrt features)")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------")
    tree_counts = [1, 5, 15, 30, 50]
    for b in tree_counts:
        mse_bag = run_ensemble(x_train, y_train, x_test, y_test, b, subsample_feats=False)
        mse_rf = run_ensemble(x_train, y_train, x_test, y_test, b, subsample_feats=True)
        print(f" {b:4d} |      {mse_bag:.4f}      |        {mse_rf:.4f}")

    print("Verification Invariant: Random forest feature subsampling de-correlates trees, lowering test MSE.")


main()

5. Where the Canon Breaks

The standard statistical canon celebrates Random Forests as an off-the-shelf, tuning-free method. In institutional financial quantitative modeling, several fundamental assumptions break down:

  • Bootstrap Resampling Violates Temporal Structure: Standard bootstrap sampling assumes exchangeability of observations. In asset returns characterized by volatility clustering (GARCH effects) and auto-correlated liquidity states, standard uniform resampling destroys temporal dependence and generates synthetic samples with unphysical regime transitions.
  • Extrapolation Failure: Decision trees cannot extrapolate beyond the extreme values observed in the training set. If an unprecedented macroeconomic shock occurs (e.g. pandemic liquidity freeze, interest rate spikes outside historical training range), Random Forests clip forecasts to the highest historical leaf mean, failing to project trends.

6. Misconceptions

  1. Growing 1,000 trees in a Random Forest on noisy financial data causes the model to overfit.

    By Breiman's Strong Law (Theorem 5.2), ensemble predictions converge almost surely to the conditional expectation as \(B \to \infty\). Adding trees stabilizes the ensemble average and cannot cause overfitting.

  2. Out-of-bag (OOB) error provides an honest out-of-sample estimate for financial return prediction.

    When labels are formed over multi-day forward holding periods, observations adjacent to the OOB instance are included in the bootstrap training sample, leaking return innovations across the bag boundary.

  3. Random Forests cannot capture linear factor exposures.

    While individual trees are piecewise-constant, averaging hundreds of de-correlated trees with different split thresholds produces a smooth, piecewise-linear approximation that models linear factor relationships effectively.

7. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An ensemble of \(B = 50\) randomized decision trees has individual tree variance \(\sigma^2 = 0.80\) and pairwise tree correlation \(\rho = 0.25\). Calculate the variance of the ensemble average \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f})\) and determine the asymptotic variance floor as \(B \to \infty\).

Worked solution

By Theorem 5.1, \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B} \sigma^2\). For \(B = 50\): \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) = (0.25)(0.80) + \frac{1 - 0.25}{50} (0.80) = 0.200 + \frac{0.75}{50} (0.80) = 0.200 + 0.015 \times 0.80 = 0.200 + 0.012 = 0.212\). The asymptotic variance floor as \(B \to \infty\) is \(\rho \sigma^2 = 0.25 \times 0.80 = 0.200\).

For a dataset of \(N = 1,000\) bars, compute the exact probability that an observation is excluded from a single bootstrap sample of size \(N\), and calculate the percentage error relative to the asymptotic limit \(1/e\).

Worked solution

The exact probability is \(P = (1 - 1/1000)^{1000} = (0.999)^{1000} \approx 0.3676954\). The asymptotic limit is \(1/e \approx 0.3678794\). The absolute difference is \(0.3678794 - 0.3676954 = 0.000184\). Percentage error is \(\frac{0.000184}{0.3678794} \times 100\% \approx 0.050\%\).

In a factor set of \(P = 48\) characteristics, compare the number of candidate split evaluations at each node under standard bagging (\(m_{\text{try}} = P\)) versus Random Forest default (\(m_{\text{try}} = \lfloor P/3 \rfloor\)).

Worked solution

Under standard bagging, all \(P = 48\) candidate features are evaluated at every split node. Under Random Forest regression with \(m_{\text{try}} = \lfloor 48 / 3 \rfloor = 16\), exactly 16 candidate features are evaluated. Feature subsampling reduces the computational burden per split by \((48 - 16) / 48 = 66.7\%\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that bootstrap aggregation cannot reduce the squared bias of an estimator, so \(\operatorname{Bias}(\bar{f}_{\text{bag}}) = \operatorname{Bias}(\hat{f}_{\text{tree}})\).

Worked solution

Let \(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \mathcal{D}^*)\) denote the tree estimator trained on bootstrap sample \(\mathcal{D}^*\). The bagging prediction is \(\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \mathcal{D}^{*b})\). Taking expectations over datasets \(\mathcal{D}\) and bootstrap draws \(\mathcal{D}^*\): \(\E[\bar{f}(\mathbf{x})] = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B \E[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \mathcal{D}^{*b})] = \E[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \mathcal{D}^*)]\). In the infinite sample limit or conditional on \(\mathcal{D}\), the expected value of a tree trained on \(\mathcal{D}^*\) equals \(\E[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})]\) to first order. Thus \(\operatorname{Bias}(\bar{f}(\mathbf{x})) = \E[\bar{f}(\mathbf{x})] - f(\mathbf{x}) = \E[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})] - f(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{Bias}(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}))\). Bagging operates purely as a variance reduction mechanism.

Prove that the probability an observation is omitted from all \(B\) bootstrap trees in an ensemble decays exponentially as \((1 - 1/e)^B\).

Worked solution

For an arbitrary observation \(i\), the probability of inclusion in a single bootstrap sample of size \(N\) converges to \(1 - 1/e \approx 0.632\). The probability of omission from tree \(b\) is \(p_0 \approx 1/e\). Because the \(B\) bootstrap samples are drawn independently conditional on \(\mathcal{D}\), the probability that observation \(i\) is included in tree \(b\) is \(1 - p_0 \approx 1 - 1/e\). The event that observation \(i\) is never chosen across all \(B\) trees has probability \(p_0^B = (1/e)^B = e^{-B}\). Conversely, the probability of never being out-of-bag (i.e. included in every single tree) is \((1 - 1/e)^B \approx (0.632)^B\), which decays exponentially to zero as \(B\) increases.

Prove that if two estimators \(\hat{f}_1, \hat{f}_2\) have unequal variances \(\sigma_1^2 < \sigma_2^2\) and correlation \(\rho\), the simple average \(\frac{1}{2}(\hat{f}_1 + \hat{f}_2)\) has strictly lower variance than \(\sigma_2^2\) if and only if \(\rho < \frac{3\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_1^2}{2\sigma_1\sigma_2}\).

Worked solution

The variance of the average is \(\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{\hat{f}_1 + \hat{f}_2}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{4}(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2)\). For this to be strictly less than \(\sigma_2^2\): \(\frac{1}{4}(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2) < \sigma_2^2 \iff \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 < 4\sigma_2^2 \iff 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 < 3\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_1^2 \iff \rho < \frac{3\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_1^2}{2\sigma_1\sigma_2}\). Since \(\sigma_2 > \sigma_1\), \(\frac{3\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_1^2}{2\sigma_1\sigma_2} > \frac{2\sigma_2^2}{2\sigma_2^2} = 1\), which means the inequality holds for all \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\). Averaging a lower-variance estimator with a higher-variance estimator always improves upon the worse estimator.

Tier 3 · Empirical

In Lab 1, why does restricting candidate split features from 12 down to 4 reduce test MSE even though individual tree depth is held constant?

Worked solution

When all 12 features are evaluated, the most prominent factor is selected at the root of nearly every tree, causing high inter-tree correlation (\(\rho = 0.73\)). By restricting candidate features to 4, trees are forced to explore alternative factor combinations, reducing correlation to \(\rho = 0.42\). By Theorem 5.1, the lower correlation reduces the ensemble variance floor, yielding superior test generalization.

In Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020), how do Random Forests perform relative to linear penalized models across US equities?

Worked solution

Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) demonstrate that Random Forests achieve higher out-of-sample \(R^2\) and superior top-decile portfolio Sharpe ratios than OLS, Ridge, and LASSO. The ensemble structure successfully extracts multi-way factor interactions (e.g. value conditional on momentum and liquidity) without overfitting.

Why does running Random Forests on tick-level order book data require special consideration for memory and inference latency?

Worked solution

A Random Forest with 500 trees each of depth 15 contains approximately \(500 \times 2^{15} \approx 1.6 \times 10^7\) nodes. In ultra-low latency execution, traversing thousands of tree pointers causes cache misses that exceed the microsecond execution budget, necessitating tree pruning or compiling trees into compact bitwise decision tables.

Tier 4 · Research

Explain the Stationary Bootstrap of Politis & Romano (1994) and why it provides a sounder resampling foundation for financial bagging than Breiman's standard bootstrap.

Worked solution

The Stationary Bootstrap resamples blocks of random length drawn from a geometric distribution with mean block length \(1/p\). Unlike standard uniform sampling with replacement, the stationary bootstrap preserves serial autocorrelation and volatility clustering across adjacent bars, ensuring that generated pseudo-samples are strictly stationary time series.

How does the sequential bootstrap proposed by López de Prado (2018) solve the label overlap problem in financial bagging?

Worked solution

The sequential bootstrap draws observations iteratively with non-uniform probabilities. At each step, candidate observations are assigned selection probabilities inversely proportional to their label concurrency with already selected observations: \(p_i \propto (1 + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} c_{ij})^{-1}\). This prevents the bootstrap sample from becoming dominated by redundant overlapping forward price trajectories.

Evaluate the trade-off between random forests and gradient boosted trees in terms of parallelizability and real-time streaming updates.

Worked solution

Random Forests are embarrassingly parallel: all \(B\) trees are trained independently on separate CPU/GPU cores with zero inter-process synchronization. In contrast, gradient boosting is inherently sequential, requiring each tree to fit the residuals of the prior model. For distributed high-throughput model retraining on cluster nodes, Random Forests offer linear scaling with compute cores.

9. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What are the two core randomization mechanisms in a Random Forest regressor?

    Options

    Breiman (2001) combined bagging with random feature selection \(m_{\text{try}} < P\) at every node split.

  2. What is the mathematical expression for the variance of an ensemble of \(B\) estimators each with variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 5.1, the variance decomposes into an irreducible floor \(\rho \sigma^2\) plus a decaying component \(\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2\).

  3. Why does adding more trees \(B\) to a Random Forest never cause overfitting?

    Options

    Breiman (2001) proved that the generalization error converges to a finite bound as \(B \to \infty\); adding trees only stabilizes the average.

  4. What is the asymptotic probability that a specific observation is omitted from a single bootstrap sample of size \(N\)?

    Options

    \(\lim_{N \to \infty} (1 - 1/N)^N = 1/e \approx 0.3679\).

  5. Why does standard out-of-bag (OOB) error estimation leak information in financial return prediction?

    Options

    Contiguous forward return windows share underlying price returns across the in-bag and out-of-bag partitions.

  6. What is the typical default recommendation for candidate split feature count \(m_{\text{try}}\) in regression random forests with \(P\) predictors?

    Options

    Standard practice sets \(m_{\text{try}} = \lfloor P/3 \rfloor\) or \(\lfloor \sqrt{P} \rfloor\) to de-correlate trees effectively.

  7. How does bootstrap aggregation affect the squared bias of base decision trees?

    Options

    Because the expectation of the average of identically distributed trees equals the expectation of a single tree, bagging preserves bias.

  8. What did Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) conclude regarding tree ensembles in US equity factor modeling?

    Options

    Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) found that tree ensembles capture nonlinearities like size-momentum interactions effectively.

  9. What structural failure occurs when applying Random Forests to extreme market regimes outside historical training ranges?

    Options

    Because trees predict constant leaf means, they cannot extrapolate trends beyond the boundary of the training data.

  10. How does the sequential bootstrap of López de Prado (2018) prevent overlapping label distortion in financial bagging?

    Options

    The sequential bootstrap draws samples with probabilities inversely proportional to their concurrency with existing draws.
