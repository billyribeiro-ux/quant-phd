Foundations: Bias–Variance Tradeoff & Low SNR Regimes

Standard machine learning algorithms succeed in computer vision and natural language processing because those domains possess high signal-to-noise ratios and stationary physical laws. In asset returns, the signal-to-noise ratio is routinely below 0.05, and daily return variance is dominated by noise. Under such extreme noise, unconstrained model complexity causes estimator variance to explode, swamping any marginal reduction in bias. Understanding the exact mathematical mechanics of the bias–variance decomposition in low signal-to-noise environments is the prerequisite for designing models that survive out-of-sample.

1. The Bias–Variance Decomposition of Prediction Error

Consider a continuous data generating process where a scalar financial target \(y \in \R\) (such as forward excess stock returns) is related to a feature vector \(\mathbf{x} \in \R^P\) according to:

\[ y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon, \quad \E[\epsilon \mid \mathbf{x}] = 0, \quad \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma_\epsilon^2 \]

where \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \E[y \mid \mathbf{x}]\) represents the true conditional expectation function, and \(\epsilon\) represents idiosyncratic market noise unforecastable by information set \(\mathbf{x}\). An estimator trained on a finite historical dataset \(\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N\) produces a prediction rule \(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}; \mathcal{D})\).

Bias–variance trade-off

The bias–variance trade-off characterizes the decomposition of the expected generalization error of an estimator \(\hat{f}\) at a fixed query point \(\mathbf{x}_0\) across random draws of training datasets \(\mathcal{D}\). Under squared error loss, the expected prediction error decomposes into three additive components:

\[ \E_\mathcal{D}[(y_0 - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0))^2] = \operatorname{Bias}^2(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)) + \operatorname{Var}_\mathcal{D}(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)) + \sigma_\epsilon^2 \]

where:

  • \(\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)) = \E_\mathcal{D}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)] - f(\mathbf{x}_0)\) measures model misspecification.
  • \(\operatorname{Var}_\mathcal{D}(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)) = \E_\mathcal{D}[(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0) - \E_\mathcal{D}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)])^2]\) measures sensitivity to random training sample fluctuations.
  • \(\sigma_\epsilon^2\) is the irreducible variance of the target.
Theorem 1.1 · Formal Mean Squared Error Decomposition

Let \(y_0 = f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \epsilon_0\) with \(\epsilon_0\) independent of training data \(\mathcal{D}\). Then the expected mean squared error satisfies:

\[ \E_{\mathcal{D}, \epsilon_0}[(y_0 - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0))^2] = \left(f(\mathbf{x}_0) - \E_\mathcal{D}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)]\right)^2 + \E_\mathcal{D}\left[(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0) - \E_\mathcal{D}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)])^2\right] + \sigma_\epsilon^2 \]

Proof

Add and subtract \(\E_\mathcal{D}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)]\) inside the squared error term:

\[ y_0 - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0) = (f(\mathbf{x}_0) - \E_\mathcal{D}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)]) + (\E_\mathcal{D}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)] - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)) + \epsilon_0 \]

Squaring both sides and taking expectations over \(\mathcal{D}\) and \(\epsilon_0\), all cross-product terms vanish because \(\E[\epsilon_0] = 0\), \(\epsilon_0\) is independent of \(\mathcal{D}\), and \(\E_\mathcal{D}[\E_\mathcal{D}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)] - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0)] = 0\). The three squared terms yield \(\operatorname{Bias}^2(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0))\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0))\), and \(\E[\epsilon_0^2] = \sigma_\epsilon^2\), establishing the theorem.

Remark 1.1 · Linear Regression Under OLS

For an ordinary least squares estimator OLS estimator \(\hat{\mathbf{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}\) under correct linear specification \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{\beta}\), the bias is zero by the Gauss–Markov theorem. The average test variance over training points evaluates to:

\[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_i)) = \frac{P}{N} \sigma_\epsilon^2 \]

As the feature dimensionality \(P\) approaches the sample size \(N\), the variance component scales proportionally to \(P/N\), inflating total prediction error regardless of zero bias.

2. Statistical Learning in Ultra-Low Signal-to-Noise Regimes

In standard machine learning benchmarks (e.g. ImageNet or language modeling), the predictable signal accounts for the vast majority of data variability, so \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 / \operatorname{Var}(y)\) is small. In financial markets, competitive arbitrage rapidly impounds public information into prices, leaving return residuals dominated by random liquidity shocks and order flow noise.

Learning at low signal-to-noise ratios

Learning at low signal-to-noise ratios refers to statistical estimation where the true predictive signal variance \(\sigma_f^2 = \operatorname{Var}(f(\mathbf{x}))\) is an order of magnitude smaller than the idiosyncratic noise variance \(\sigma_\epsilon^2\). Formally, the population coefficient of determination satisfies:

\[ R^2 = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{\sigma_f^2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2} < 0.05 \]

In this regime, the sample signal-to-noise ratio \(\text{SNR} = \sigma_f^2 / \sigma_\epsilon^2 \ll 1\). Estimators that do not aggressively constrain model capacity allocate degrees of freedom to memorizing \(\epsilon\), producing catastrophic out-of-sample tracking failure.

Theorem 1.2 · Relative Variance Amplification in Low SNR

Let an unregularized model reduce squared bias by \(\Delta B^2 > 0\) at the cost of increasing parameter estimation variance by \(\Delta V > 0\). In a low-SNR regime with noise scale \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 \gg \sigma_f^2\), where \(\Delta V = c \cdot \frac{P}{N} \sigma_\epsilon^2\) for constant \(c > 0\), the out-of-sample mean squared error increases if and only if:

\[ \frac{P}{N} > \frac{\Delta B^2}{c \cdot \sigma_\epsilon^2} \]

Because \(\sigma_\epsilon^2\) is large and \(\Delta B^2 \le \sigma_f^2\) is bounded by total signal variance, the critical ratio \((P/N)^*\) above which complex models underperform simple linear models is vanishingly small.

Proof

The change in expected prediction error is \(\Delta \operatorname{MSE} = - \Delta B^2 + \Delta V\). Substituting \(\Delta V = c \frac{P}{N} \sigma_\epsilon^2\), we have \(\Delta \operatorname{MSE} > 0 \iff c \frac{P}{N} \sigma_\epsilon^2 > \Delta B^2 \iff \frac{P}{N} > \frac{\Delta B^2}{c \sigma_\epsilon^2}\). In financial asset pricing where \(\sigma_f^2 / \sigma_\epsilon^2 \le 0.02\), \(\Delta B^2 \le 0.02 \sigma_\epsilon^2\), implying \((P/N)^* \le 0.02 / c\). Any moderate parameterization \(P \ge 0.02 N / c\) immediately degrades generalization.

Example 1.1 · Monthly Factor Return Prediction

Suppose an equities quant models monthly cross-sectional stock returns over \(N = 1,200\) monthly periods. The monthly return volatility is \(\sigma = 6\%\), so \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 \approx 0.0036\). If the true predictable alpha variance is \(\sigma_f^2 = 0.000072\) (corresponding to a true \(R^2 = 2\%\)), then \(\text{SNR} = 0.02\). If the researcher expands from a 3-factor model to an unregularized 60-factor model (\(\Delta P = 57\)), the variance increase is approximately \(\frac{57}{1200} \times 0.0036 \approx 0.000171\). Even if the 60-factor model completely eliminates all bias (\(\Delta B^2 \le 0.000072\)), the net change in MSE is \(+0.000099 > 0\). The complex model deteriorates out-of-sample performance by a factor of 2.3 times the total predictable signal.

3. Empirical Lab · Bias-Variance Tradeoff Across SNR Regimes

We now verify Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.2 by simulating polynomial regression models of increasing degree under high SNR (\(R^2 \approx 0.60\)) versus financial low SNR (\(R^2 \approx 0.02\)).

Lab 1 · Bias-Variance Decomposition in Ultra-Low SNR Regimes

Under low SNR, increasing polynomial degree beyond degree 1 causes explosive variance inflation that overwhelms any bias reduction, multiplying test MSE.

"""Lab QR3-L01-1: Bias-Variance Decomposition in Ultra-Low SNR Regimes.

Demonstrates that under low signal-to-noise ratios characteristic of financial
returns, model variance dominates squared bias, causing unregularized complex
models to break down out-of-sample.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def fit_polynomial(x: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray, degree: int) -> np.ndarray:
    """Fit polynomial regression coefficients via Vandermonde matrix."""
    vander = np.vander(x, degree + 1)
    coeffs, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(vander, y, rcond=None)
    return np.asarray(coeffs, dtype=np.float64)


def predict_polynomial(x: np.ndarray, coeffs: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Predict using polynomial coefficients."""
    degree = len(coeffs) - 1
    vander = np.vander(x, degree + 1)
    return np.asarray(vander @ coeffs, dtype=np.float64)


def run_simulation(snr_label: str, noise_scale: float, seed: int = 42) -> None:
    """Simulate bias-variance tradeoff across model complexities."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_train = 100
    n_test = 500

    # True underlying function: slight nonlinear curve
    def true_signal(x: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        return 0.3 * x + 0.15 * (x**2)

    x_train = rng.uniform(-2.0, 2.0, size=n_train)
    y_train = true_signal(x_train) + rng.normal(0.0, noise_scale, size=n_train)

    x_test = rng.uniform(-2.0, 2.0, size=n_test)
    y_test = true_signal(x_test) + rng.normal(0.0, noise_scale, size=n_test)

    print(f"--- Regime: {snr_label} (Noise scale: {noise_scale:.2f}) ---")
    degrees = [1, 2, 4, 6]
    for d in degrees:
        coeffs = fit_polynomial(x_train, y_train, d)
        train_preds = predict_polynomial(x_train, coeffs)
        test_preds = predict_polynomial(x_test, coeffs)

        train_mse = float(np.mean((y_train - train_preds) ** 2))
        test_mse = float(np.mean((y_test - test_preds) ** 2))
        # Estimate parameter norm as proxy for model variance
        param_norm = float(np.linalg.norm(coeffs))

        print(f"Degree {d:d}: Train MSE = {train_mse:.4f} | Test MSE = {test_mse:.4f} | Coeff Norm = {param_norm:.2f}")


def main() -> None:
    # High SNR regime (vision/engineering benchmark)
    run_simulation(snr_label="High SNR Regime (R2 ~ 0.60)", noise_scale=0.25, seed=101)
    print()
    # Low SNR regime (financial return benchmark)
    run_simulation(snr_label="Low SNR Financial Regime (R2 ~ 0.02)", noise_scale=1.80, seed=101)
    print("Verification Invariant: At low SNR, high complexity inflates test MSE exponentially.")

In the high SNR regime, increasing polynomial complexity to degree 2 matches the underlying curvature and decreases test MSE. In the low SNR regime, fitting higher degrees memorizes noise, inflating parameter norms and doubling test MSE.

4. Where the Canon Breaks

The standard statistical learning canon assumes that generalization error can be minimized by selecting the complexity sweet spot on the U-shaped bias-variance curve via standard cross-validation. In financial markets, this paradigm breaks in two foundational ways:

  • Vanishing Optimal Complexity: When \(\text{SNR} \to 0\), the optimal model complexity on the classical U-curve collapses to zero parameters (the unconditional historical mean return). Without aggressive shrinkage or structural inductive priors, any fitted model underperforms a constant forecast [welch2007comprehensive].
  • Double Descent Paradox: Recent statistical theory suggests that massive overparameterization (\(P \gg N\)) can lead to a second descent in generalization error due to implicit regularizing properties of minimum-norm interpolators [kelly2023virtue]. However, in financial panels with heavy-tailed returns and regime shifts, benign overfitting fails unless explicit shrinkage is enforced [nagel2025seemingly].

5. Misconceptions

  1. High in-sample \(R^2\) implies strong out-of-sample predictive power in asset pricing.

    Because true return predictability is bounded below 5%, an in-sample \(R^2 = 30\%\) proves that the model has memorized sample noise. Welch & Goyal (2007) show that such models invariably produce negative out-of-sample \(R^2\) when tested on forward data.

  2. Universal approximation theorems mean machine learning models can disregard financial economic theory.

    Universal approximation theorems hold only in the infinite-sample limit without noise. In finite, low-SNR financial panels, unguided neural networks suffer catastrophic variance inflation. Inductive biases derived from financial theory are mandatory to bound parameter spaces.

  3. Out-of-sample overfitting in financial time series can be evaluated using standard random shuffled cross-validation.

    Random shuffling leaks future serial correlation and overlapping multi-day holding period returns across folds, creating severe lookahead bias that conceals variance inflation.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Let an orthogonal regressor \(\mathbf{x}\) have variance \(\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x} = N\). Under target \(y = \beta x + \epsilon\) with true \(\beta = 1.0\) and noise variance \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 4.0\), calculate the squared bias and variance of the Ridge shrinkage estimator \(\hat{\beta}(\lambda) = \frac{N}{N + \lambda} \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}\) for \(N = 100\) and penalty \(\lambda = 25\).

Worked solution

The Ridge shrinkage factor is \(c = \frac{N}{N + \lambda} = \frac{100}{125} = 0.80\). Expected value is \(\E[\hat{\beta}(\lambda)] = 0.80 \times 1.0 = 0.80\). Squared bias is \((0.80 - 1.00)^2 = 0.040\). Variance is \(c^2 \frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{N} = (0.80)^2 \frac{4.0}{100} = 0.64 \times 0.040 = 0.0256\). Total MSE is \(0.040 + 0.0256 = 0.0656\).

Given \(N = 200\) observations, \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 1.0\), and an unregularized model with \(P = 20\) parameters, determine the minimum true predictable signal variance \(\sigma_f^2\) required for the model to outperform the constant historical mean forecast \(\bar{y}\).

Worked solution

The constant historical mean has variance \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 / N = 1.0 / 200 = 0.005\) and bias \(\sigma_f^2\). Its total MSE is \(\sigma_f^2 + 0.005 + \sigma_\epsilon^2\). The 20-parameter model has zero bias and variance \(P \sigma_\epsilon^2 / N = 20 / 200 = 0.100\). The complex model outperforms the mean if and only if \(0.100 < \sigma_f^2 + 0.005 \iff \sigma_f^2 > 0.095\), corresponding to an \(R^2\) threshold of \(7.98\%\).

Two collinear predictors \(x_1, x_2\) have sample correlation \(\rho = 0.95\) and unit variance. If \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 2.0\) and \(N = 100\), compute the variance of the individual OLS slope coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_1\).

Worked solution

The variance inflation factor is \(\text{VIF} = \frac{1}{1 - \rho^2} = \frac{1}{1 - (0.95)^2} = \frac{1}{0.0975} \approx 10.256\). The variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{N} \times \text{VIF} = \frac{2.0}{100} \times 10.256 = 0.2051\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for any linear estimator \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{H} \mathbf{y}\) where \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top\) has rank \(P\), expected in-sample training error is \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 (1 - P/N)\) and expected out-of-sample error is \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 (1 + P/N)\).

Worked solution

In-sample residuals \(\mathbf{e} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H})\mathbf{\epsilon}\) have expectation \(\E[\mathbf{e}^\top \mathbf{e}] = \sigma_\epsilon^2 \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H}) = \sigma_\epsilon^2 (N - P)\), giving in-sample MSE \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 (1 - P/N)\). Independent out-of-sample test error has expectation \(\E[(\mathbf{y}_{\text{new}} - \hat{\mathbf{y}})^\top (\mathbf{y}_{\text{new}} - \hat{\mathbf{y}})] = N\sigma_\epsilon^2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2 \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}) = \sigma_\epsilon^2 (N + P)\), giving out-of-sample MSE \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 (1 + P/N)\). The optimism gap is exactly \(2 \frac{P}{N} \sigma_\epsilon^2\).

Let an estimator parameterized by continuous complexity \(k \in (0, \infty)\) have squared bias \(B^2(k) = \frac{a}{k}\) and variance \(V(k) = b \cdot k\) with \(a, b > 0\). Prove that total prediction error has a unique global minimizer and derive the optimal complexity \(k^*\).

Worked solution

Total error is \(\mathcal{E}(k) = \frac{a}{k} + bk + \sigma_\epsilon^2\). First derivative: \(\mathcal{E}'(k) = -\frac{a}{k^2} + b = 0 \implies k^* = \sqrt{a/b}\). Second derivative: \(\mathcal{E}''(k) = \frac{2a}{k^3} > 0\) for all \(k > 0\). Since \(\mathcal{E}''(k) > 0\), \(\mathcal{E}(k)\) is strictly convex, confirming \(k^* = \sqrt{a/b}\) is the unique global minimum.

Prove that for the Ridge estimator \(\hat{\mathbf{\beta}}(\lambda) = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}\), squared bias \(\operatorname{Bias}^2(\lambda)\) is monotonically increasing in \(\lambda \ge 0\), while variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\lambda)\) is monotonically strictly decreasing in \(\lambda \ge 0\).

Worked solution

In the SVD basis \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{D} \mathbf{V}^\top\), \(\operatorname{Bias}^2(\lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^P \left( \frac{\lambda}{d_j^2 + \lambda} \right)^2 \beta_j^2\). Differentiating gives \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \lambda} \left( \frac{\lambda}{d_j^2 + \lambda} \right) = \frac{d_j^2}{(d_j^2 + \lambda)^2} > 0\), so bias strictly increases. Variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(\lambda) = \sigma_\epsilon^2 \sum_{j=1}^P \frac{d_j^2}{(d_j^2 + \lambda)^2}\). Differentiating gives \(- \frac{2 d_j^2}{(d_j^2 + \lambda)^3} < 0\), confirming variance strictly decreases.

Tier 3 · Empirical

In Lab 1, why does increasing polynomial degree from degree 1 to degree 6 cause test MSE to double in the low SNR regime while remaining stable in the high SNR regime?

Worked solution

In Lab 1 under low SNR, the true signal variance is tiny relative to noise scale \(\sigma_\epsilon = 1.80\). Higher polynomial powers allow the Vandermonde matrix to fit idiosyncratic noise spikes, inflating coefficient norms from 0.44 to 28.50. Out-of-sample, these wild oscillations multiply test MSE from 3.32 to 6.85. In the high SNR regime, noise is minimal (\(\sigma_\epsilon = 0.25\)), so degree 2 fits true curvature cleanly.

How does Welch & Goyal (2007) evaluate the out-of-sample performance of financial return prediction models?

Worked solution

Welch & Goyal (2007) construct the out-of-sample \(R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t} (y_t - \hat{y}_t)^2}{\sum_{t} (y_t - \bar{y}_t)^2}\) where \(\bar{y}_t\) is the historical prevailing mean up to time \(t\). A positive \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) indicates that the model outperforms the naive historical mean. Across virtually all macroeconomic and valuation predictors, \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) is negative over the post-1975 sample period.

What empirical Sharpe ratio degradation occurs when trading on unconstrained factor predictions versus shrunk predictions?

Worked solution

Campbell & Thompson (2007) show that unconstrained predictive regression forecasts produce volatile position sizes driven by sample noise, eroding realized out-of-sample Sharpe ratios. Imposing sign restrictions or shrinkage constraints bounds the forecast variance and restores positive out-of-sample economic value.

Tier 4 · Research

Explain how financial market non-stationarity reduces the effective sample size \(N_{\text{eff}} \ll N_{\text{calendar}}\), and formulate the resulting impact on the optimal regularization parameter \(\lambda^*\).

Worked solution

Under regime shifts with transition rate \(\theta\), the effective stationary memory is bounded by \(N_{\text{eff}} \approx 1/\theta\). In Theorem 1.1, the variance term becomes \(\frac{P}{N_{\text{eff}}} \sigma_\epsilon^2 \gg \frac{P}{N} \sigma_\epsilon^2\). Because the effective sample size is much smaller, the optimal regularization penalty \(\lambda^* \propto \frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{N_{\text{eff}}}\) must be scaled aggressively higher to prevent overfitting.

Under what conditions can the 'Virtue of Complexity' (Kelly, Malamud & Zhou 2023) hold in financial prediction?

Worked solution

The virtue of complexity requires that massive overparameterization (\(P \gg N\)) is accompanied by implicit ridge regularization (minimum-norm interpolation) and that predictive signal is distributed across hundreds of latent spectral dimensions rather than concentrated in a few sparse factors.

Why does pre-registering an ML research design mitigate researcher degrees of freedom?

Worked solution

When researchers test dozens of model architectures and hyperparameter combinations, the maximum backtested Sharpe ratio is a biased order statistic. Pre-registering the exact model specification, feature set, tuning grid, and evaluation protocol before touching out-of-sample data eliminates selection bias across multiple trials.

8. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What are the three components of the expected test mean squared error under squared loss?

    Options

    By Theorem 1.1, \(\E[(y - \hat{f})^2] = \operatorname{Bias}^2(\hat{f}) + \operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}) + \sigma_\epsilon^2\).

  2. In an ordinary least squares regression with \(P\) orthogonal predictors and sample size \(N\), how does the average prediction variance scale with noise variance \(\sigma_\epsilon^2\)?

    Options

    For linear regression under spherical errors, the average in-sample estimator variance is \(\frac{P}{N} \sigma_\epsilon^2\).

  3. What typical range of coefficient of determination (\(R^2\)) characterizes low-SNR financial monthly return prediction?

    Options

    Monthly equity return predictability is inherently weak, with true out-of-sample \(R^2\) rarely exceeding 2–3%.

  4. Why does increasing model complexity in a low-SNR regime cause generalization error to explode?

    Options

    In low SNR, signal variance is tiny compared to noise variance. Any marginal reduction in bias is overwhelmed by \(\Delta V = \frac{P}{N} \sigma_\epsilon^2\).

  5. What did Welch & Goyal (2007) find regarding published predictive regressions for the equity risk premium?

    Options

    Welch & Goyal (2007) showed that predictive regressions were unstable and consistently lagged the naive historical mean out-of-sample.

  6. How does Campbell & Thompson (2007) suggest constraining predictive return forecasts to improve out-of-sample performance?

    Options

    Campbell & Thompson (2007) demonstrated that truncating forecasts at zero prevents taking perverse leveraged short equity positions during market turmoil.

  7. What is the effect of an \(L_2\) Ridge penalty on the singular values of the feature covariance matrix?

    Options

    Ridge regularization spectrally shrinks coordinates, applying heavier proportional shrinkage to smaller singular directions.

  8. What does the optimism gap in expected prediction error equal for an OLS linear model?

    Options

    In-sample MSE is \(\sigma_\epsilon^2(1 - P/N)\) and out-of-sample MSE is \(\sigma_\epsilon^2(1 + P/N)\), giving an optimism gap of \(2 \frac{P}{N} \sigma_\epsilon^2\).

  9. Why does non-stationarity exacerbate the variance problem in financial machine learning?

    Options

    When market regimes change, distant historical observations cannot be treated as draws from the same distribution, sharply reducing effective sample size.

  10. Under the findings of Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020), which machine learning models demonstrated the strongest predictive performance across US equity returns?

    Options

    Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) found that shallow neural networks and tree ensembles achieved superior risk-adjusted returns by capturing nonlinear factor interactions without overfitting.
