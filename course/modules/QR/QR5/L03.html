Policy Gradients, Continuous Action Spaces & Actor-Critic

Value-based methods like Q-learning fail when trading actions are continuous—such as selecting portfolio target weights \(\mathbf{w} \in \Delta^{N-1}\) or continuous execution rates \(v_t \in [0, v_{\max}]\). Discretizing continuous action spaces leads to an exponential curse of dimensionality. Policy gradient methods parameterize trading policies directly as differentiable probability distributions, optimizing expected risk-adjusted utility via gradient ascent without requiring an explicit argmax over infinite action candidates.

1. The Policy Gradient Theorem and REINFORCE

Let \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) denote a parameterized stochastic policy with parameter vector \(\theta \in \R^d\). The objective is to maximize expected cumulative discounted return:

\[ J(\theta) = \E_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta} [R(\tau)] = \int P(\tau; \theta) R(\tau) \mathrm{d}\tau \]

where trajectory \(\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots, s_T)\) has probability \(P(\tau; \theta) = \mu(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)\).

Policy-gradient methods

Policy-gradient methods are reinforcement learning algorithms that optimize a parameterized policy \(\pi_\theta\) by ascending the analytical gradient of expected return with respect to policy parameters: \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \nabla_\theta J(\theta)\). By the Policy Gradient Theorem, the gradient evaluates as:

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \E_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^T \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) G_t \right] \]

where \(G_t = \sum_{k=t}^T \gamma^{k-t} R_{k+1}\) is the cumulative future return from step \(t\), and \(\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\) is the likelihood ratio or score function.

Theorem 3.1 · The Policy Gradient Theorem

For any differentiable policy \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) and any Markov decision process, the gradient \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\) does not depend on the derivatives of the state distribution or transition kernel \(P(s' \mid s, a)\):

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_{s \in S} d^\pi(s) \sum_{a \in A} \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) Q^\pi(s, a) = \E_{s \sim d^\pi, a \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s) Q^\pi(s, a) \right] \]

where \(d^\pi(s) = \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t \Prob(S_t = s \mid s_0, \pi)\) is the discounted state visitation distribution.

Proof

Compute the gradient of trajectory probability \(P(\tau; \theta)\):

\[ \nabla_\theta P(\tau; \theta) = P(\tau; \theta) \frac{\nabla_\theta P(\tau; \theta)}{P(\tau; \theta)} = P(\tau; \theta) \nabla_\theta \ln P(\tau; \theta) \]

Expanding the log trajectory probability:

\[ \ln P(\tau; \theta) = \ln \mu(s_0) + \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \ln \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) + \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \ln P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t) \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\theta\), the terms \(\mu(s_0)\) and \(P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)\) vanish because market dynamics do not depend directly on policy parameters \(\theta\):

\[ \nabla_\theta \ln P(\tau; \theta) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \]

Substituting back into \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \int \nabla_\theta P(\tau; \theta) R(\tau) \mathrm{d}\tau\) gives the expectation over \(\tau\). Causality further dictates that past rewards cannot depend on future actions, so \(\sum_{k=0}^T R_{k+1}\) reduces to the future return \(G_t = \sum_{k=t}^T \gamma^{k-t} R_{k+1}\), establishing the theorem.

2. Continuous Action Parameterizations in Portfolio Control

In quantitative portfolio management, the action represents allocation weights or trade speed. Two primary parameterizations are employed:

Gaussian Continuous Policy

For an unconstrained action \(a_t \in \R\), the policy is parameterized by a conditional Gaussian density:

\[ \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(a - \mu_\theta(s))^2}{2 \sigma^2} \right) \]

where the score function evaluates analytically to:

\[ \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \frac{a - \mu_\theta(s)}{\sigma^2} \nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s) \]

Remark 3.1 · Squashed Gaussian Policies for Bounded Positions

To enforce leverage or inventory boundaries \(a \in [-a_{\max}, a_{\max}]\), the policy samples latent Gaussian noise \(u \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(s), \sigma^2)\) and applies a squashing bijection \(a = a_{\max} \tanh(u)\). By change of variables, the score function includes the Jacobian determinant \(\ln |1 - \tanh^2(u)|\) to maintain exact probability density evaluation.

3. Variance Reduction via Baselines and Actor-Critic

The raw REINFORCE gradient estimator suffers from severe sample variance. Subtracting an arbitrary state-dependent baseline \(b(s)\) eliminates variance without introducing bias.

Theorem 3.2 · Baseline Unbiasedness

For any baseline function \(b: S \to \R\) that does not depend on action \(a\):

\[ \E_{a \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s) b(s) \right] = 0 \]

Proof

Expand the expectation as an integral over the action space:

\[ \int_A \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s) b(s) \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a = b(s) \int_A \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a = b(s) \nabla_\theta \int_A \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a \]

Since \(\int_A \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a = 1\) for all \(\theta\), its gradient with respect to \(\theta\) is identically zero: \(\nabla_\theta (1) = 0\), establishing the theorem.

Setting \(b(s) = V^\phi(s)\) gives the advantage function \(A^\pi(s, a) = Q^\pi(s, a) - V^\pi(s)\), which forms the core of Actor-Critic architectures: the actor updates \(\theta\) in the direction of \(\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s) A(s, a)\), while the critic updates \(\phi\) to minimize value prediction error \((R_{t+1} + \gamma V^\phi(S_{t+1}) - V^\phi(S_t))^2\).

4. Numerical Verification · Policy Gradient & Baseline Variance Reduction

We now verify the policy gradient theorem numerically, comparing empirical score function estimates against finite differences and measuring variance reduction from baseline subtraction.

Lab 1 · Continuous Policy Gradient Verification & Variance Reduction

Verifies that the analytical score function policy gradient matches numerical finite differences to high precision, and that state-value baseline subtraction reduces gradient variance without biasing the estimate.

"""Lab QR5-L03-1: Continuous Policy Gradient Verification & Variance Reduction.

Demonstrates:
1. Continuous action Gaussian policy parameterization for portfolio allocation.
2. Derivation and implementation of the REINFORCE score function estimator.
3. Variance reduction via state-value baseline subtraction.
4. Numerical validation of empirical policy gradient against finite difference approximation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def evaluate_trajectory_return(theta: float, noise: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Simulate portfolio rebalancing with Gaussian policy a ~ Normal(theta, sigma^2).

    Reward R(a) = mu_asset * a - 0.5 * lambda_cost * a^2.
    """
    sigma_a = 0.5
    action = theta + sigma_a * noise
    # True market return: quadratic utility
    reward = float(0.12 * action - 0.05 * (action**2))
    # Score function d ln pi(a | theta) / d theta = (a - theta) / sigma_a^2
    score = float((action - theta) / (sigma_a**2))
    return reward, score


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    n_samples = 40000
    theta_0 = 1.0

    shocks = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n_samples)

    # 1. Analytical Expected Return J(theta) = E[0.12*(theta + sigma*z)
    #    - 0.05*(theta^2 + 2*theta*sigma*z + sigma^2*z^2)]
    # J(theta) = 0.12*theta - 0.05*theta^2 - 0.05*sigma^2
    # dJ/dtheta = 0.12 - 0.10*theta
    expected_grad_analytical = 0.12 - 0.10 * theta_0

    # 2. REINFORCE Empirical Policy Gradient
    scores = np.zeros(n_samples)
    rewards = np.zeros(n_samples)

    for i in range(n_samples):
        r, sc = evaluate_trajectory_return(theta_0, shocks[i])
        rewards[i] = r
        scores[i] = sc

    # Raw REINFORCE gradient
    raw_grad_samples = scores * rewards
    var_raw = float(np.var(raw_grad_samples))

    # Baseline-subtracted REINFORCE
    baseline = float(np.mean(rewards))
    baseline_grad_samples = scores * (rewards - baseline)
    grad_baseline = float(np.mean(baseline_grad_samples))
    var_baseline = float(np.var(baseline_grad_samples))

    # 3. Finite Difference Gradient
    delta = 1e-4
    rewards_plus = np.zeros(n_samples)
    rewards_minus = np.zeros(n_samples)
    for i in range(n_samples):
        r_p, _ = evaluate_trajectory_return(theta_0 + delta, shocks[i])
        r_m, _ = evaluate_trajectory_return(theta_0 - delta, shocks[i])
        rewards_plus[i] = r_p
        rewards_minus[i] = r_m

    grad_fd = float(np.mean((rewards_plus - rewards_minus) / (2.0 * delta)))

    variance_reduction = (var_raw - var_baseline) / var_raw * 100.0

    print(f"Analytical gradient dJ/dtheta: {expected_grad_analytical:.6f}")
    print(f"Finite difference gradient: {grad_fd:.6f}")
    print(f"REINFORCE empirical gradient: {grad_baseline:.6f}")
    print(f"Raw gradient variance: {var_raw:.6f}")
    print(f"Baseline gradient variance: {var_baseline:.6f}")
    print(f"Variance reduction from baseline: {variance_reduction:.2f}%")


main()
Analytical gradient dJ/dtheta: 0.020000
Finite difference gradient: 0.019779
REINFORCE empirical gradient: 0.019729
Raw gradient variance: 0.008796
Baseline gradient variance: 0.007075
Variance reduction from baseline: 19.57%

The analytical score function gradient matches finite differences within 0.0002, and subtracting the baseline reduces gradient variance by 19.57% without altering the expected gradient value.

Where the Canon Breaks

Standard policy gradient methods (REINFORCE, PPO, SAC) assume rewards follow sub-Gaussian distributions with finite higher moments. In financial asset markets:

  • Heavy-tailed jumps: Extreme market returns (kurtosis \(\gg 3\)) produce outlier score products \(\nabla_\theta \ln \pi \cdot G_t\), causing gradient explosions and destabilizing policy updates.
  • Variance-induced exploration failure: In continuous spaces, increasing policy variance \(\sigma\) to explore causes catastrophic drawdown during market stress, conflicting with capital preservation.

Misconceptions

  1. Subtracting \(V(s)\) from the return introduces an approximation bias into the policy gradient.

    By Theorem 3.2, \(\E[\nabla_\theta \ln \pi(a \mid s) b(s)] = 0\) identically for any function independent of \(a\). The expectation is strictly zero, guaranteeing zero bias.

  2. Clipping Gaussian actions at execution time preserves policy gradient convergence.

    Post-hoc clipping alters the action distribution without informing the score function \(\nabla_\theta \ln \pi(a \mid s)\), producing inconsistent gradient steps. Positions must be bounded via smooth transformations (e.g., \(\tanh\)) or Beta distributions.

  3. Standard policy gradient algorithms automatically maximize the portfolio Sharpe ratio.

    The standard objective is expected cumulative return \(J(\theta) = \E[\sum R_t]\). The Sharpe ratio \(\frac{\E[R]}{\sqrt{\Var(R)}}\) is non-additive and non-separable across time steps, requiring differential Sharpe ratio formulation.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Problem 3.1 · Gaussian Policy Score Function Calculation

A continuous execution policy parameterizes trade rate as a Gaussian \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(s), \sigma^2)\) where \(\mu_\theta(s) = \theta^\top \mathbf{x} = 2.5\) and fixed dispersion \(\sigma = 0.5\). If the executed action is \(a = 3.2\) and return is \(G = 12.0\), calculate the scalar score factor \(\frac{a - \mu}{\sigma^2}\) and the raw policy gradient update factor.

Worked solution

The scalar score factor is:

\[ \text{Score} = \frac{a - \mu}{\sigma^2} = \frac{3.2 - 2.5}{0.5^2} = \frac{0.7}{0.25} = 2.80 \]

The policy gradient update factor is \(\text{Score} \times G = 2.80 \times 12.0 = 33.6\). The parameter update moves in the direction \(33.6 \mathbf{x}\).

Problem 3.2 · Baseline Variance Reduction Ratio

Across 1,000 simulated trading episodes, a raw policy gradient sample has variance \(\sigma_{\text{raw}}^2 = 0.0450\). Subtracting a linear value baseline \(V^\phi(s)\) reduces the sample variance to \(\sigma_{\text{base}}^2 = 0.0180\). Calculate the percentage variance reduction and the required sample size reduction to achieve identical estimation error.

Worked solution

Percentage variance reduction:

\[ \text{Reduction} = \frac{0.0450 - 0.0180}{0.0450} \times 100\% = \frac{0.0270}{0.0450} \times 100\% = 60.0\% \]

Since estimation variance scales as \(\sigma^2 / N\), the required sample size to achieve identical precision scales proportionally: \(N_{\text{base}} = \frac{0.0180}{0.0450} N_{\text{raw}} = 0.40 N_{\text{raw}}\), representing a 60% reduction in required training samples.

Problem 3.3 · GAE Advantage Computation

Given 1-step TD errors \(\delta_0 = 1.2\), \(\delta_1 = -0.5\), \(\delta_2 = 2.0\), with discount factor \(\gamma = 0.95\) and GAE parameter \(\lambda = 0.90\), calculate the generalized advantage estimate \(\hat{A}_0^{\text{GAE}}\).

Worked solution

The GAE formula is \(\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}} = \sum_{l=0}^\infty (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}\). Here \(\gamma \lambda = 0.95 \times 0.90 = 0.855\):

\[ \hat{A}_0^{\text{GAE}} = \delta_0 + (0.855) \delta_1 + (0.855)^2 \delta_2 = 1.2 + 0.855 \times (-0.5) + (0.731025) \times 2.0 = 1.2 - 0.4275 + 1.46205 = 2.23455 \]

Tier 2 · Proof

Problem 3.4 · Invariance to Constant Baseline

Prove that for any constant \(C \in \R\), \(\E_{a \sim \pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \cdot C] = 0\).

Worked solution

By linearity of the integral:

\[ \int_A \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \cdot C \cdot \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a = C \int_A \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a = C \nabla_\theta \int_A \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a \]

Since \(\int_A \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a = 1\) identically, its derivative is zero: \(C \nabla_\theta (1) = 0\).

Problem 3.5 · Score Function Expectation Property

Prove that the score function \(\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) has expectation zero: \(\E_{a \sim \pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)] = 0\).

Worked solution

Follows immediately from Problem 3.4 with \(C = 1\):

\[ \E_{a \sim \pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)] = \int_A \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a = \nabla_\theta \int_A \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a = \nabla_\theta (1) = 0 \]

Problem 3.6 · Optimal Variance-Minimizing Baseline

Derive the state-dependent baseline \(b^*(s)\) that minimizes the variance of the policy gradient estimator \(\Var(\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s) [Q(s, a) - b(s)])\).

Worked solution

The variance of the component-wise estimator is \(\E[\|\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\|^2 (Q(s, a) - b(s))^2]\). Differentiating with respect to \(b(s)\) and setting to zero:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial b(s)} \E[\|\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\|^2 (Q(s, a)^2 - 2 b(s) Q(s, a) + b(s)^2)] = 0 \] \[ \implies -2 \E[\|\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\|^2 Q(s, a)] + 2 b(s) \E[\|\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\|^2] = 0 \] \[ b^*(s) = \frac{\E_{a \sim \pi_\theta}[\|\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\|^2 Q(s, a)]}{\E_{a \sim \pi_\theta}[\|\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\|^2]} \]

This is a weighted expectation of \(Q(s, a)\) weighted by the squared score norm.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Problem 3.7 · Empirical Policy Gradient Verification vs. Finite Differences

For a parameter \(\theta = 0.50\) and objective \(J(\theta)\), evaluate policy gradient estimate \(\hat{g}\) across \(N = 50,000\) sample paths. Compare with centered finite difference \(\frac{J(\theta + \epsilon) - J(\theta - \epsilon)}{2 \epsilon}\) for \(\epsilon = 10^{-4}\). What is the empirical relative discrepancy?

Worked solution

By the central limit theorem, the empirical policy gradient estimator has standard error \(\sigma / \sqrt{N}\). With \(N = 50,000\) and \(\sigma \approx 0.1\), \(\operatorname{SE} \approx 0.00045\). Centered finite differences have truncation error \(O(\epsilon^2) = 10^{-8}\). The empirical relative error between the two estimators is typically under 1%, verifying code implementation integrity.

Problem 3.8 · PPO Clipping Frequency in High Volatility

In Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), the surrogate objective clips the probability ratio \(r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}\) to \([1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon]\). Under market volatility doubling, how does the clipping activation frequency behave if learning rate is held constant?

Worked solution

Under doubled return volatility, advantage estimates \(\hat{A}_t\) scale proportionally to volatility, multiplying gradient step sizes. Consequently, parameter updates between epochs induce larger ratio shifts \(r_t(\theta)\), causing clipping activation to rise from typical levels of 15–20% to over 60%, heavily dampening policy updates and preserving numerical stability.

Problem 3.9 · Maximum Entropy Regularization in Soft Actor-Critic

Soft Actor-Critic adds entropy bonus \(\alpha \mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t))\) to the reward. For a Gaussian policy with standard deviation \(\sigma\), entropy is \(\frac{1}{2} \ln(2\pi e \sigma^2)\). Calculate the marginal gradient of the entropy term with respect to policy dispersion \(\sigma\).

Worked solution

Differentiating with respect to \(\sigma\):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma} \left[ \frac{1}{2} \ln(2\pi e \sigma^2) \right] = \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma} \left[ \frac{1}{2} \ln(2\pi e) + \ln \sigma \right] = \frac{1}{\sigma} \]

The entropy gradient pushes dispersion outward with force proportional to \(1/\sigma\), preventing the policy from collapsing to a deterministic Dirac delta prematurely.

Tier 4 · Research

Problem 3.10 · Direct Sharpe Ratio Optimization via Policy Gradients

Formulate the analytical policy gradient for the Sharpe ratio \(\operatorname{SR} = \frac{\E[R_t]}{\sqrt{\E[R_t^2] - (\E[R_t])^2}}\) directly using the chain rule and the Policy Gradient Theorem.

Worked solution

Let \(A = \E[R_t]\) and \(B = \E[R_t^2]\), so \(\operatorname{SR} = \frac{A}{\sqrt{B - A^2}}\). By the chain rule:

\[ \nabla_\theta \operatorname{SR} = \frac{\nabla_\theta A \sqrt{B - A^2} - A \frac{\nabla_\theta B - 2 A \nabla_\theta A}{2 \sqrt{B - A^2}}}{B - A^2} = \frac{(B - A^2) \nabla_\theta A - \frac{1}{2} A \nabla_\theta B + A^2 \nabla_\theta A}{(B - A^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{B \nabla_\theta A - \frac{1}{2} A \nabla_\theta B}{(B - A^2)^{3/2}} \]

Substituting the policy gradient expressions \(\nabla_\theta A = \E[\nabla_\theta \ln \pi R_t]\) and \(\nabla_\theta B = \E[\nabla_\theta \ln \pi R_t^2]\) gives the direct Sharpe ratio policy gradient.

Problem 3.11 · Off-Policy Correction in Continuous Action Spaces

Why does importance sampling \(w(a) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\beta(a \mid s)}\) suffer from unbounded variance in multi-dimensional continuous action spaces? How does Deterministic Policy Gradient (DPG) sidestep importance sampling ratios entirely?

Worked solution

In high dimensions, the likelihood ratio of continuous Gaussian densities contains the exponential term \(\exp(-\frac{1}{2} (a-\mu_\pi)^\top \Sigma_\pi^{-1} (a-\mu_\pi) + \frac{1}{2} (a-\mu_\beta)^\top \Sigma_\beta^{-1} (a-\mu_\beta))\), whose variance diverges exponentially in action dimension \(D\). Silver et al. (2014) show that DPG parameterizes a deterministic policy \(a = \mu_\theta(s)\). Its gradient is \(\nabla_\theta J = \E[\nabla_a Q(s, a) \nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)]\), completely eliminating action importance sampling weights.

Problem 3.12 · Heavy-Tailed Gradient Clipping in Execution

Design an adaptive gradient clipping threshold \(\tau_k\) based on rolling empirical quantiles of score norms \(\|\nabla_\theta \ln \pi \cdot G_t\|\). How does adaptive clipping preserve gradient direction while preventing ruinous parameter jumps during market flash crashes?

Worked solution

Maintain an exponential moving median and interquartile range (IQR) of gradient norms: \(\tau_k = \operatorname{Median}_k + 3 \times \operatorname{IQR}_k\). For any sample gradient \(g_i\), scale by \(\min(1, \tau_k / \|g_i\|)\). Because the vector is scaled by a non-negative scalar, its directional cosine \(\frac{g_i^\top \nabla J}{\|g_i\| \|\nabla J\|}\) is exactly preserved, while individual outlier gradient norms are capped, preventing network weight corruption during liquidity vacuums.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the score function \(\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) for a Gaussian policy with mean \(\mu_\theta(s)\) and variance \(\sigma^2\)?

    Options

    Taking the natural logarithm of the Gaussian density and differentiating with respect to \(\theta\) yields \(\frac{a - \mu_\theta(s)}{\sigma^2} \nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)\).

  2. Why does subtracting a baseline \(b(s)\) not alter the expected policy gradient?

    Options

    Since \(\int \pi(a \mid s) \mathrm{d}a = 1\), differentiating both sides yields \(\int \nabla \pi \mathrm{d}a = 0\), so \(\E[\nabla \ln \pi \cdot b(s)] = 0\).

  3. In continuous action spaces, why is a squashed Gaussian policy \(a = \tanh(u)\) preferred over hard clipping?

    Options

    Hard clipping creates zero derivatives at boundaries, breaking backpropagation. The \(\tanh\) squashing transformation is smooth and invertible.

  4. What does the advantage function \(A^\pi(s, a) = Q^\pi(s, a) - V^\pi(s)\) quantify?

    Options

    The advantage measures the relative superiority of action \(a\) over the baseline average value \(V^\pi(s)\).

  5. What is the primary role of the GAE parameter \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\)?

    Options

    Generalized Advantage Estimation trades off bias and variance through the exponential decay parameter \(\lambda\).

  6. Why does PPO clip the probability ratio \(r_t(\theta)\) to \([1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]\)?

    Options

    Clipping the surrogate objective penalizes updates that move the new policy too far from the old policy, ensuring stable monotonic policy improvement.

  7. Why can policy gradient training fail catastrophically during market jump events?

    Options

    Heavy-tailed returns violate Gaussian assumptions and cause massive gradient variance unless gradients are clipped or regularized.

  8. What does the Policy Gradient Theorem demonstrate about the transition kernel \(P(s' \mid s, a)\)?

    Options

    A profound insight of Sutton et al. (1999) is that the gradient of expected return depends only on policy derivatives, not on transition derivatives.
