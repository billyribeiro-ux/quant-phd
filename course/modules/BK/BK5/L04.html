Moving-Block Bootstrap and the Safe Withdrawal Rate

  • Uses: bootstrap (S1), household balance sheet (L01), lifecycle saving (L01)
  • Introduces: moving-block-bootstrap, safe-withdrawal-rate

Sizing sustainable retirement spending is the central mathematical problem of wealth decumulation. Standard financial planning software frequently assumes independent, identically distributed returns, ignoring serial autocorrelation, valuation mean-reversion, and persistent bear-market clusters. When applied to multi-year retirement horizons, i.i.d. resampling understates ruin probabilities substantially. The moving-block bootstrap overcomes this limitation by resampling consecutive blocks of historical data, preserving the short- and medium-term dependence structure of asset returns. By combining block bootstrapping with rigorous decumulation simulation, this lesson establishes the mathematical formulation and empirical estimation of the safe withdrawal rate.

1Resampling Dependent Time Series: The Moving-Block Bootstrap

The classical non-parametric bootstrap of Efron (1979) samples individual data points independently with replacement. In time series exhibiting autocorrelation or volatility clustering, independent draws destroy the temporal dependence structure. Block bootstrap methods restore this validity.

Definition 1.1 (moving-block bootstrap)

Let \(\{X_t\}_{t=1}^N\) be a stationary time series of asset returns. For an integer block length \(b \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), define the set of \(M = N - b + 1\) overlapping blocks: \[ B_i = (X_i, X_{i+1}, \dots, X_{i+b-1}), \quad i = 1, \dots, M \] The moving-block bootstrap (MBB) constructs a synthetic time series of horizon \(H\) by drawing \(K = \lceil H / b \rceil\) blocks uniformly at random with replacement from \(\{B_1, \dots, B_M\}\), concatenating them, and truncating the resulting series to length \(H\) in non-parametric time-series inference.

Proposition 1.2 (intra-block autocovariance preservation)

Let \(X_t\) be a weakly stationary process with autocovariance function \(\gamma(k) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+k})\). Within any resampled block \(B_i\) of length \(b\), the sample autocovariance between elements separated by lag \(k < b\) preserves the empirical autocovariance of the original historical sequence.

Proof

Consider any two elements within a single block \(B_i\): \(X_{i+j}\) and \(X_{i+j+k}\) with \(j + k < b\). Because the block extracts the contiguous historical segment \((X_i, \dots, X_{i+b-1})\) intact, the joint realization \((X_{i+j}, X_{i+j+k})\) is identical to the historical observation at lag \(k\). Discontinuities occur only at the boundaries between concatenated blocks (which occur with frequency \(1/b\)). As \(b \to \infty\) with \(b/N \to 0\), the boundary bias vanishes, and the spectral density of the bootstrap process converges to the true spectral density under stationary block bootstrap theory. \(\blacksquare\)

2The Safe Withdrawal Rate

Definition 2.1 (safe withdrawal rate)

Let \(W_0\) be the initial portfolio wealth at retirement, and let \(H\) be the retirement horizon in years. An initial withdrawal rate \(w_0 = C_0 / W_0\) specifies the first-year spending amount \(C_0\), with subsequent annual withdrawals indexed to inflation: \(C_t = C_0 \prod_{s=1}^t (1 + \pi_s)\). Portfolio wealth evolves according to: \[ W_{t+1} = \max\bigl(0, (W_t - C_t)(1 + R_{t+1})\bigr) \] The ruin date is \(\tau = \inf\{t \in \{1, \dots, H\} : W_t \le 0\}\) (with \(\tau = \infty\) if \(W_H > 0\)). The safe withdrawal rate (SWR) at ruin tolerance \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) is the supremum of initial withdrawal rates such that the simulated probability of ruin over horizon \(H\) does not exceed \(\alpha\): \[ \mathrm{SWR}(H, \alpha) = \sup \left\{ w_0 > 0 : \Prob(\tau \le H) \le \alpha \right\} \]

Remark 2.2 (historical origin and empirical benchmark)

In financial planning literature, William Bengen (1994) evaluated historical 30-year rolling cohorts of US equity and bond returns, demonstrating that an initial 4.0% withdrawal rate adjusted annually for CPI inflation survived all historical 30-year periods since 1926 (popularized as the "4% Rule"). In rigorous quantitative modeling, the safe withdrawal rate is not a fixed constant; it depends on the asset allocation, real return distribution, autocorrelation persistence, and the chosen ruin probability threshold \(\alpha\).

Proposition 2.3 (MBB vs. i.i.d. ruin risk divergence)

If an asset return process exhibits positive autocorrelation (\(\rho > 0\)) or volatility clustering, the moving-block bootstrap yields weakly higher estimated ruin probabilities for decumulation portfolios than an i.i.d. bootstrap across any non-trivial withdrawal rate: \[ \Prob_{\text{MBB}}(\tau \le H) \ge \Prob_{\text{iid}}(\tau \le H) \] Consequently, the safe withdrawal rate sized under MBB is strictly more conservative: \(\mathrm{SWR}_{\text{MBB}} \le \mathrm{SWR}_{\text{iid}}\).

Proof

Under positive serial autocorrelation, negative returns cluster into multi-year economic contractions (e.g. 1929–1932, 1973–1974, 2000–2002). An i.i.d. bootstrap shuffles returns uniformly, dispersing negative years between positive years and artificially dampening prolonged multi-year drawdowns. The moving-block bootstrap samples contiguous blocks of length \(b\), preserving consecutive negative return sequences. Because decumulation portfolios with fixed cash withdrawals suffer irreversible capital destruction when negative returns cluster early, the probability of hitting zero wealth is strictly amplified under clustered drawdowns. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Moving-block bootstrap vs. i.i.d. bootstrap on retirement ruin

Stated before running. On a 60-year calibrated persistent return series (\(\rho = 0.25\)), evaluating 1,000 retirement paths over a 30-year horizon reveals that the moving-block bootstrap (\(b=5\)) produces higher ruin probabilities than the i.i.d. bootstrap at every withdrawal rate. At a 4.0% withdrawal rate, i.i.d. ruin probability is 1.4%, whereas MBB ruin probability increases to 6.4% (a +5.0% risk gap).

"""Lab L04-1: Moving-block bootstrap versus i.i.d. bootstrap on retirement ruin probabilities.

Demonstrates that i.i.d. resampling understates retirement ruin risk compared to a
moving-block bootstrap (block length = 5) on persistent return data over a 30-year horizon.
Standard library only. Seeded RNG. All inputs are SIMULATED.
"""

import random


def generate_persistent_returns(n_years: int, seed: int) -> list[float]:
    """Generate persistent real return series with AR(1) dynamics."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    mean_ret = 0.05
    rho = 0.25
    vol = 0.12
    returns = [mean_ret]
    for _ in range(n_years - 1):
        shock = rng.gauss(0.0, vol)
        r = mean_ret + rho * (returns[-1] - mean_ret) + shock
        returns.append(round(r, 4))
    return returns


def run_bootstrap_simulation(
    returns: list[float],
    block_length: int,
    horizon: int,
    n_sims: int,
    initial_wealth: float,
    withdrawal_rate: float,
    seed: int,
) -> float:
    """Run Monte Carlo simulation and return ruin probability."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    n = len(returns)
    num_blocks = n - block_length + 1
    withdrawal = initial_wealth * withdrawal_rate
    ruin_count = 0

    for _ in range(n_sims):
        w = initial_wealth
        path: list[float] = []
        while len(path) < horizon:
            if block_length == 1:
                path.append(rng.choice(returns))
            else:
                idx = rng.randint(0, num_blocks - 1)
                path.extend(returns[idx : idx + block_length])

        path = path[:horizon]
        ruined = False
        for r in path:
            w -= withdrawal
            if w <= 0.0:
                ruined = True
                break
            w *= 1.0 + r
            if w <= 0.0:
                ruined = True
                break
        if ruined:
            ruin_count += 1

    return ruin_count / n_sims


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    history_years = 60
    horizon = 30
    n_sims = 1_000
    initial_wealth = 1_000_000.0  # dollars

    returns = generate_persistent_returns(history_years, seed=101)
    rates = [0.030, 0.035, 0.040, 0.045, 0.050, 0.055]

    print("Retirement Ruin Probabilities (30-Year Horizon, 1,000 Paths):")
    for rate in rates:
        prob_iid = run_bootstrap_simulation(returns, 1, horizon, n_sims, initial_wealth, rate, seed=seed)
        prob_mbb = run_bootstrap_simulation(returns, 5, horizon, n_sims, initial_wealth, rate, seed=seed)
        print(
            f"Withdrawal Rate {rate * 100:4.1f}% | "
            f"i.i.d. Ruin: {prob_iid * 100:5.1f}% | "
            f"MBB (b=5) Ruin: {prob_mbb * 100:5.1f}% | "
            f"Gap: {(prob_mbb - prob_iid) * 100:+5.1f}%"
        )

3Where the Canon Breaks

The standard safe withdrawal rate paradigm relies on historical backtests and constant real spending rules. Three fundamental structural limitations challenge this framework:

  • US Historical Exceptionalism and Survivorship Bias: The 4% rule was derived from 20th-century US market history—a period during which the United States emerged as the dominant global economic superpower. Global historical datasets across 30 developed nations show that safe withdrawal rates under multi-country evidence averaged only 2.5% to 3.2% due to geopolitical catastrophes and hyperinflation.
  • Valuation Regime Shifts (High CAPE / Low Yields): Historical average returns were earned starting from average price-to-earnings multiples. Retiring into an economic environment characterized by elevated Cyclically Adjusted Price-to-Earnings (CAPE) ratios and depressed real bond yields sharply reduces expected forward returns, depressing the initial safe withdrawal rate under valuation-based asset pricing models.
  • Rigid Spending vs. Dynamic Rule Flexibility: Fixed real withdrawal rules assume retirees blindly consume the same inflation-adjusted dollar amount even as their portfolio approaches zero. In reality, rational retirees adjust spending dynamically in response to market drawdowns (e.g. Guyton-Klinger guardrails or percentage-of-portfolio rules).

Misconceptions

  • The 4% safe withdrawal rate is a universal mathematical constant that guarantees 100% solvency under any future market regime.

    The 4% figure is an empirical artifact of historical US market data. In periods of low bond yields and high equity valuations, or across international equity markets, the safe withdrawal rate for a 30-year horizon is frequently between 3.0% and 3.5%.

  • An i.i.d. Monte Carlo bootstrap accurately captures downside tail risks for retirement decumulation.

    The i.i.d. bootstrap shuffles historical observations independently, destroying multi-year macroeconomic persistence and volatility clusters. As proved in Proposition 2.3, i.i.d. resampling systematically underestimates retirement ruin probabilities.

  • If a portfolio's expected arithmetic return is 7.0% and inflation is 2.0%, an investor can safely withdraw the 5.0% expected real return each year.

    Expected arithmetic returns do not account for return volatility drag or sequence-of-returns risk. Withdrawing the expected mean return from a volatile portfolio results in catastrophic capital exhaustion in over 25% of simulations.

Problem Set

Tier 1: Mechanics

A historical time series contains \(N = 60\) annual returns. How many distinct overlapping blocks of length \(b = 6\) can be constructed for a moving-block bootstrap?

Solution

The number of overlapping blocks of length \(b\) in a sample of size \(N\) is \(M = N - b + 1\). Here, \(M = 60 - 6 + 1 = 55\) distinct blocks.

A retiree begins retirement with $1,500,000 in wealth and adopts a 4.0% initial withdrawal rate. If inflation during the first year is 3.0%, what is the required nominal dollar withdrawal in Year 1 and Year 2?

Solution

In Year 1, the initial withdrawal is \(C_1 = \$1{,}500{,}000 \times 0.04 = \$60{,}000\). In Year 2, the withdrawal is adjusted for 3.0% inflation: \(C_2 = \$60{,}000 \times (1 + 0.03) = \$61{,}800\).

A portfolio of $1,000,000 experiences a -10% return in Year 1. The retiree withdraws $40,000 at the start of Year 1 and $40,000 at the start of Year 2. Calculate portfolio wealth at the start of Year 2 immediately after the second withdrawal.

Solution

At the start of Year 1, wealth after withdrawal is \(\$1{,}000{,}000 - \$40{,}000 = \$960{,}000\). During Year 1, wealth grows by -10%: \(\$960{,}000 \times (1 - 0.10) = \$864{,}000\). At the start of Year 2, withdrawing $40,000 leaves \(\$864{,}000 - \$40{,}000 = \$824{,}000\).

Tier 2: Proof

Prove that when block length \(b = 1\), the moving-block bootstrap is algebraically identical to the classical Efron (1979) i.i.d. bootstrap.

Solution

When \(b = 1\), each block \(B_i\) consists of a single observation \(X_i\) for \(i = 1, \dots, N\). The number of blocks is \(M = N - 1 + 1 = N\). Drawing blocks uniformly at random with replacement is equivalent to drawing individual observations \(X_i\) independently with probability \(1/N\), which is the exact definition of Efron's non-parametric i.i.d. bootstrap.

Prove that the ruin probability \(\Prob(\tau \le H)\) of a decumulation portfolio is a weakly monotonic non-decreasing function of the initial withdrawal rate \(w_0\).

Solution

Let \(w_0^{(1)} < w_0^{(2)}\). For any fixed realization of asset returns \(R_1, \dots, R_H\), cash withdrawals satisfy \(C_t^{(1)} = w_0^{(1)} W_0 \prod (1+\pi) < w_0^{(2)} W_0 \prod (1+\pi) = C_t^{(2)}\) for all \(t\). Wealth satisfies \(W_t^{(1)} \ge W_t^{(2)}\) by induction. If path 1 hits zero at date \(\tau_1\), then path 2 must have hit zero at \(\tau_2 \le \tau_1\). The indicator \(\indic\{\tau \le H\}\) is pathwise non-decreasing in \(w_0\), proving that \(\E[\indic\{\tau \le H\}] = \Prob(\tau \le H)\) is weakly increasing in \(w_0\).

Prove that if a portfolio achieves an annualized geometric compound return \(g < 0\), any positive initial real withdrawal rate \(w_0 > 0\) leads to ruin in finite time as horizon \(H \to \infty\).

Solution

With negative geometric growth, unconstrained portfolio wealth without withdrawals decays to zero: \(\lim_{T \to \infty} W_0 (1+g)^T = 0\). Subtracting strictly positive real cash withdrawals \(C_t \ge C_0 > 0\) each year accelerates depletion: \(W_t < W_0 (1+g)^t - C_0 t\). Since \(C_0 t \to \infty\) while \((1+g)^t \to 0\), \(W_t\) must cross zero at a finite time \(\tau < \infty\).

Tier 3: Empirical

Why does a moving-block bootstrap produce wider confidence intervals for cumulative long-term returns than an i.i.d. bootstrap on equity series?

Solution

Because financial returns exhibit positive autocorrelation in volatility and multi-year macroeconomic persistence, the effective sample size is smaller than the nominal sample size. Preserving autocorrelation increases the variance of cumulative return estimates, widening bootstrap confidence intervals.

What was the worst historical 30-year retirement cohort in US market history, and what initial withdrawal rate did it sustain?

Solution

The retirement cohort of 1966 faced the severe 1973–1974 bear market combined with stagflation throughout the late 1970s. This cohort tested portfolio sustainability most severely, sustaining a maximum safe withdrawal rate of approximately 4.15% over 30 years.

How does increasing the equity allocation in a retirement portfolio from 20% to 60% affect the safe withdrawal rate across 30-year horizons?

Solution

A 20% equity portfolio has low volatility but suffers from low real expected returns and high inflation vulnerability, sustaining safe withdrawal rates of only ~3.0% to 3.5%. Increasing equity allocation to 50%–60% raises the SWR to ~4.0% by providing essential real capital growth.

Tier 4: Research

How does Politis & White's (2004) automatic block-length selection algorithm optimize the bias-variance tradeoff in moving-block bootstrap applications?

Solution

Politis & White (2004) estimate the optimal block length \(\hat{b}\) by analyzing the spectral density and autocovariance lag decay. Short block lengths minimize variance but introduce bias by clipping serial correlation; long block lengths eliminate bias but increase estimator variance. The algorithm chooses \(\hat{b} \propto N^{1/3}\) to minimize asymptotic mean squared error.

Evaluate the Guyton-Klinger dynamic withdrawal rules (capital preservation and prosperity rules) compared to rigid Bengen spending rules.

Solution

Guyton-Klinger guardrails establish boundaries: if the current withdrawal rate exceeds 1.2 times the initial rate due to market declines, spending is cut by 10%; if it falls below 0.8 times, spending is increased. This dynamic flexibility increases initial sustainable withdrawal rates to 5.0%–5.5% with zero portfolio exhaustion.

Why does incorporating stochastic longevity risk (e.g. Gompertz mortality) alter optimal withdrawal sustainability compared to a deterministic 30-year horizon?

Solution

Under stochastic mortality, the probability of reaching age 95 is significantly lower than surviving to age 75. Discounting future cash flows by survival probabilities \({}_t p_x\) allows higher early-retirement spending, though it creates catastrophic tail risk if the retiree survives past age 100 without longevity insurance.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. Why does the moving-block bootstrap outperform the i.i.d. bootstrap for financial return simulation?

    Options

    By drawing contiguous blocks of length \(b\), the moving-block bootstrap retains the joint distribution and serial dependence of asset returns.

  2. For a historical sample of \(N = 50\) years and block length \(b = 5\), how many overlapping blocks can be drawn?

    \(M = N - b + 1 = 50 - 5 + 1 = 46\).

  3. What is the safe withdrawal rate defined as in decumulation modeling?

    Options

    The SWR is the highest initial withdrawal rate meeting a target ruin constraint (e.g. \(\Prob(\text{Ruin}) \le 5\%\)).

  4. In Lab 1, what was the estimated retirement ruin probability under the moving-block bootstrap (\(b=5\)) for a 4.0% withdrawal rate in percent?

    In Lab 1, the MBB ruin probability was 6.4%, compared to 1.4% under i.i.d. sampling.

  5. What bias is introduced if the block length \(b\) in a moving-block bootstrap is chosen too small (e.g. \(b=1\))?

    Options

    Small block lengths break multi-year dependencies, degenerating toward the i.i.d. model.

  6. Why does the moving-block bootstrap produce higher ruin probabilities than the i.i.d. bootstrap under positive autocorrelation?

    Options

    When negative returns cluster together while fixed withdrawals occur, portfolio capital is destroyed before markets can recover.

  7. A client with $1,000,000 establishes a 4.0% initial withdrawal rate. What is the dollar withdrawal at the start of Year 1?

    \(\$1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.04 = \$40{,}000\).

  8. What is survivorship bias in the context of the historical 4% safe withdrawal rate?

    Options

    The US market was exceptionally successful in the 20th century; international markets sustained substantially lower SWRs.

  9. How does retiring into an environment of historically high CAPE ratios impact the forward safe withdrawal rate?

    Options

    High valuation multiples predict lower long-term equity returns, elevating early sequence risk and lowering the SWR.

  10. How do dynamic withdrawal guardrails (e.g. Guyton-Klinger) prevent portfolio ruin during severe downturns?

    Options

    Dynamic rules trim withdrawals during market drops, preserving capital and dramatically reducing ruin risk.
