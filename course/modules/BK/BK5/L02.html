Retirement Accounts, Taxes, and Asset Location

Taxes impose a massive frictional drag on long-term compound wealth accumulation. Over a 30-year investing horizon, annual taxation of interest, dividends, and realized capital gains can consume more than 25% of terminal purchasing power. Understanding the mathematical equivalence between Traditional and Roth accounts under flat tax rates allows households to exploit intertemporal tax rate differentials. Furthermore, because different asset classes face asymmetric tax treatment—ordinary income tax on bond interest versus preferential rates and deferral on equity capital gains—the deliberate placement of assets across taxable and tax-sheltered accounts yields substantial risk-free after-tax alpha.

1Tax-Advantaged Accounts: Taxable, TDA, and Roth

Modern households execute lifecycle saving across three distinct institutional tax environments, each governed by specific contribution, growth, and withdrawal rules.

Definition 1.1 (tax-advantaged account)

A tax-advantaged account is an investment vehicle established by statute that confers preferential tax treatment to incentivize retirement savings. It falls into two primary structural categories:

  • Tax-Deferred Account (TDA): Contributions are deductible from current pre-tax income. Investments compound tax-deferred without annual taxation of dividends, interest, or capital gains. Distributions during retirement are taxed as ordinary income at the marginal tax rate prevailing at withdrawal (e.g. Traditional 401(k), Traditional IRA).
  • Tax-Exempt Account (Roth): Contributions are made with post-tax dollars with no upfront deduction. Investment growth compounds tax-free, and qualified distributions in retirement are entirely exempt from federal and state income taxes (e.g. Roth 401(k), Roth IRA).
In contrast, a standard taxable account receives post-tax contributions, incurs annual taxes on interest, realized dividends, and realized capital gains, and pays capital gains tax upon asset liquidation under statutory tax rules.

Proposition 1.2 (Traditional vs. Roth analytical equivalence)

Let \(Y\) denote pre-tax earnings allocated to retirement, let \(R\) denote the gross annual rate of return, and let \(T\) denote the investment horizon. If the tax rate at contribution \(\tau_0\) equals the tax rate at withdrawal \(\tau_T = \tau_0 = \tau\), then terminal after-tax wealth in a Traditional TDA exactly equals terminal after-tax wealth in a Roth account: \[ W_{\text{TDA}} = W_{\text{Roth}} \]

Proof

In a Roth account, income \(Y\) is taxed immediately at rate \(\tau\), investing \(Y(1-\tau)\). After \(T\) periods of compound growth at gross return \(R\), terminal wealth is withdrawn tax-free: \[ W_{\text{Roth}} = Y(1-\tau)(1+R)^T \] In a Traditional TDA, the entire pre-tax amount \(Y\) is invested directly. After \(T\) periods, the balance grows to \(Y(1+R)^T\). Upon withdrawal at date \(T\), the entire sum is taxed at rate \(\tau\), leaving: \[ W_{\text{TDA}} = Y(1+R)^T(1-\tau) \] By the commutativity of real multiplication, \(Y(1-\tau)(1+R)^T = Y(1+R)^T(1-\tau)\). Hence \(W_{\text{TDA}} = W_{\text{Roth}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.3 (intertemporal tax rate arbitrage)

When the contribution tax rate \(\tau_0\) differs from the withdrawal tax rate \(\tau_T\), terminal wealth diverges: \[ \frac{W_{\text{TDA}}}{W_{\text{Roth}}} = \frac{1 - \tau_T}{1 - \tau_0} \] If a worker expects lower marginal tax rates in retirement (\(\tau_T < \tau_0\)), the Traditional TDA dominates by \((1-\tau_T)/(1-\tau_0) > 1\). Conversely, if tax rates are expected to rise (\(\tau_T > \tau_0\)), the Roth vehicle strictly dominates. This ratio is independent of both the investment horizon \(T\) and the rate of return \(R\).

2The Asset Location Problem

While asset allocation determines the aggregate proportions of equities and fixed income on the household balance sheet, asset location optimizes where those assets are held across account types.

Definition 2.1 (asset location)

Asset location is the optimal placement of specific asset classes across taxable, tax-deferred, and tax-exempt accounts to maximize total after-tax terminal wealth subject to fixed overall portfolio weights and account capacity constraints in multi-account household wealth management.

Proposition 2.2 (asset location tax efficiency hierarchy)

Suppose an investor holds a portfolio of bonds generating ordinary income at rate \(r_b\) and equities generating total return \(r_e\) composed of dividend yield \(d\) and capital appreciation \(g\) (\(r_e = d + g\)). Tax rates satisfy \(\tau_{\text{ord}} > \tau_d \ge \tau_{cg} > 0\). If account capacities across Taxable, TDA, and Roth are given, total after-tax wealth is maximized by following the priority hierarchy:

  1. Bonds to TDA: Bonds are located in Traditional TDA first up to capacity, completely sheltering high-tax ordinary income from annual taxation.
  2. Equities to Roth: High expected return equities are located in Roth up to capacity, maximizing the dollar value of the permanent tax exemption.
  3. Residual Equities to Taxable: Equities occupy taxable accounts preferentially over bonds, exploiting preferential qualified dividend tax rates and the tax deferral of unrealized capital gains.

Proof

In a taxable account, a bond yields net return \(r_b(1 - \tau_{\text{ord}})\), suffering annual tax drag \(r_b \tau_{\text{ord}}\). An equity asset with dividend yield \(d\) and unrealized capital gain \(g\) suffers annual dividend tax drag \(d \tau_d\), while capital gains tax is deferred until liquidation at effective rate \(\tau_{cg,\text{eff}} < \tau_{cg}\). Because bond interest faces \(\tau_{\text{ord}}\) annually without deferral, locating $1 of bonds in a taxable account generates a larger percentage tax drag on cash flow than locating equities in taxable whenever \(r_b \tau_{\text{ord}} > d \tau_d\), which holds under standard market parameters (\(r_b \approx 4.5\%, \tau_{\text{ord}} = 25\%\) drag = 1.125%; \(d \approx 2.0\%, \tau_d = 15\%\) drag = 0.30%). Furthermore, placing equities with return \(r_e > r_b\) in a Roth account shields a higher expected return from all future taxation than placing lower-yielding bonds in Roth. Hence, the stated priority minimizes aggregate discounted tax liabilities. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Account tax equivalence and asset location alpha

Stated before running. A $10,000 pre-tax contribution invested at 8.0% for 30 years yields exactly identical terminal wealth of $76,476 in Roth and TDA under equal 24% tax rates. If retirement taxes fall from 32% to 22%, TDA outperforms Roth by $10,062.66. Across a $500,000 portfolio split equally between equities and bonds, optimal asset location (bonds in TDA, equities in taxable) delivers over $260,000 in incremental after-tax wealth compared to suboptimal location over 30 years.

"""Lab L02-1: Tax-advantaged accounts and asset location tax-drag analysis.

Compares terminal wealth across Taxable, Traditional TDA, and Roth accounts under flat,
rising, and falling tax rates, and quantifies the asset location premium over 30 years.
Standard library only. All inputs are SIMULATED.
"""


def terminal_wealth_single_deposit(
    pre_tax_deposit: float,
    gross_annual_return: float,
    years: int,
    tax_rate_initial: float,
    tax_rate_terminal: float,
    account_type: str,
    annual_tax_drag: float = 0.0,
) -> float:
    """Compute terminal after-tax wealth for a single lump-sum pre-tax contribution."""
    if account_type == "roth":
        # Post-tax contribution, tax-free growth and withdrawal
        after_tax_initial = pre_tax_deposit * (1.0 - tax_rate_initial)
        return after_tax_initial * ((1.0 + gross_annual_return) ** years)
    elif account_type == "tda":
        # Pre-tax contribution, tax-deferred growth, ordinary income tax on full withdrawal
        pre_tax_growth = pre_tax_deposit * ((1.0 + gross_annual_return) ** years)
        return pre_tax_growth * (1.0 - tax_rate_terminal)
    elif account_type == "taxable":
        # Post-tax contribution, annual tax drag on returns
        after_tax_initial = pre_tax_deposit * (1.0 - tax_rate_initial)
        net_return = gross_annual_return - annual_tax_drag
        return after_tax_initial * ((1.0 + net_return) ** years)
    else:
        raise ValueError(f"Unknown account_type: {account_type}")


def main() -> None:
    deposit = 10_000.0  # pre-tax dollars
    horizon = 30  # years
    equity_gross_return = 0.08  # 8.0% annual return
    bond_gross_return = 0.045  # 4.5% annual return

    # Part A: Flat tax rate (24% working, 24% retirement)
    tw_roth_flat = terminal_wealth_single_deposit(deposit, equity_gross_return, horizon, 0.24, 0.24, "roth")
    tw_tda_flat = terminal_wealth_single_deposit(deposit, equity_gross_return, horizon, 0.24, 0.24, "tda")
    print(
        f"Flat tax (24% -> 24%): Roth {round(tw_roth_flat):6d} | "
        f"TDA {round(tw_tda_flat):6d} | diff {tw_tda_flat - tw_roth_flat:6.2f}"
    )

    # Part B: Falling tax rate (32% working -> 22% retirement)
    tw_roth_fall = terminal_wealth_single_deposit(deposit, equity_gross_return, horizon, 0.32, 0.22, "roth")
    tw_tda_fall = terminal_wealth_single_deposit(deposit, equity_gross_return, horizon, 0.32, 0.22, "tda")
    print(
        f"Falling tax (32% -> 22%): Roth {round(tw_roth_fall):6d} | "
        f"TDA {round(tw_tda_fall):6d} | TDA advantage {tw_tda_fall - tw_roth_fall:6.2f}"
    )

    # Part C: Rising tax rate (22% working -> 32% retirement)
    tw_roth_rise = terminal_wealth_single_deposit(deposit, equity_gross_return, horizon, 0.22, 0.22, "roth")
    tw_tda_rise = terminal_wealth_single_deposit(deposit, equity_gross_return, horizon, 0.22, 0.32, "tda")
    print(
        f"Rising tax (22% -> 32%): Roth {round(tw_roth_rise):6d} | "
        f"TDA {round(tw_tda_rise):6d} | Roth advantage {tw_roth_rise - tw_tda_rise:6.2f}"
    )

    # Part D: Asset location evaluation on a $500,000 portfolio (50% Equities, 50% Bonds)
    # Account capacities: $250,000 Taxable (after-tax money), $250,000 TDA (pre-tax money)
    # Tax rates: Ordinary income 25%, Qualified dividend / Long-term cap gain 15%
    # Bonds: 4.5% interest -> taxed at 25% ordinary income in Taxable (tax drag = 4.5% * 0.25 = 1.125%)
    # Equities: 8.0% return (2% dividend taxed at 15% = 0.30% drag, 6% capital gain deferred with 15% final tax)
    # Optimal location: Bonds in TDA ($250k), Equities in Taxable ($250k)
    # Suboptimal location: Equities in TDA ($250k), Bonds in Taxable ($250k)
    portfolio_size = 250_000.0  # per account
    tau_ord = 0.25
    tau_cg = 0.15

    # Optimal:
    # 1. TDA holds Bonds: grows at gross 4.5%, taxed at 25% upon withdrawal after 30 years
    tda_bonds_growth = portfolio_size * ((1.0 + bond_gross_return) ** horizon) * (1.0 - tau_ord)
    # 2. Taxable holds Equities: 2% div taxed at 15% annual drag = 0.30%. Net growth = 8.0% - 0.30% = 7.70%
    # At end, capital gains tax paid on unrealized appreciation:
    equity_taxable_terminal_pre_cg = portfolio_size * ((1.0 + 0.077) ** horizon)
    gain = equity_taxable_terminal_pre_cg - portfolio_size
    taxable_equities_net = equity_taxable_terminal_pre_cg - (gain * tau_cg * 0.6)  # effective deferred cg rate
    optimal_total = tda_bonds_growth + taxable_equities_net

    # Suboptimal:
    # 1. TDA holds Equities: grows at 8.0%, taxed at 25% ordinary income upon withdrawal
    tda_equities_growth = portfolio_size * ((1.0 + equity_gross_return) ** horizon) * (1.0 - tau_ord)
    # 2. Taxable holds Bonds: 4.5% interest taxed annually at 25% ordinary income: net return = 4.5% * 0.75 = 3.375%
    taxable_bonds_net = portfolio_size * ((1.0 + (bond_gross_return * (1.0 - tau_ord))) ** horizon)
    suboptimal_total = tda_equities_growth + taxable_bonds_net

    location_alpha = optimal_total - suboptimal_total
    print(
        f"Asset location over 30y: Optimal {round(optimal_total):8d} | "
        f"Suboptimal {round(suboptimal_total):8d} | Location Alpha {round(location_alpha):7d}"
    )

3Where the Canon Breaks

The clean algebraic models of account equivalence and asset location rest on simplified tax codes. Real-world wealth planning encounters critical non-linearities:

  • Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs): Traditional TDAs require mandatory distributions starting at age 73–75 under IRS rules. Large TDA balances can push retirees into higher tax brackets, triggering increased Medicare Part B/D premiums (IRMAA) and taxation of Social Security benefits.
  • Legislative Tax Risk: The assumption that future statutory tax rates \(\tau_T\) are knowable is false. Tax brackets and capital gains rates are subject to political and legislative revisions over multi-decade lifespans.
  • Step-Up in Cost Basis at Death: In taxable accounts, Section 1014 of the US Internal Revenue Code resets the cost basis of appreciated assets to fair market value upon the owner's death, eliminating all deferred capital gains tax liability for heirs. This creates a powerful estate planning incentive to hold highest-appreciation equities in taxable accounts until death.

Misconceptions

  • A Roth account is always strictly superior to a Traditional TDA because withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free.

    Proposition 1.2 proves that if tax rates are equal, after-tax terminal wealth is identical. If a worker's tax rate is higher during peak earnings than during retirement, a Traditional TDA provides strictly higher net wealth due to upfront tax deduction savings.

  • Asset location is irrelevant as long as the aggregate portfolio matches the target equity/bond asset allocation.

    Holding identical aggregate asset allocations but placing bonds in taxable accounts and equities in TDA subjects bond interest to immediate ordinary income tax and converts equity capital gains into ordinary income upon withdrawal, destroying hundreds of thousands of dollars in after-tax wealth.

  • Holding tax-exempt municipal bonds inside an IRA provides double tax protection.

    Municipal bonds offer lower yields precisely because their interest is exempt from federal income tax in taxable accounts. Holding them in an IRA sacrifices yield while subjecting future withdrawals to ordinary income tax, completely wasting the tax exemption.

Problem Set

Tier 1: Mechanics

An investor has $20,000 of pre-tax income to invest for 25 years at an 8% gross return. Their working tax rate is 30% and their retirement tax rate is 20%. Calculate terminal wealth in a Traditional TDA versus a Roth account.

Solution

In a TDA, $20,000 grows at 8% for 25 years: \(20{,}000 \times (1.08)^{25} \approx 20{,}000 \times 6.8485 = \$136{,}970\). Taxed at 20% on withdrawal: \(136{,}970 \times (1 - 0.20) = \$109{,}576\). In a Roth, $20,000 is taxed at 30% initially, investing $14,000: \(14{,}000 \times (1.08)^{25} = \$95{,}879\). The TDA provides an advantage of $13,697.

A taxable bond yields 5.0% annual interest. An investor faces a 32% ordinary income tax rate. What is the after-tax annual return of the bond in a taxable account?

Solution

The after-tax yield is \(r_{\text{net}} = r_b \times (1 - \tau_{\text{ord}}) = 0.05 \times (1 - 0.32) = 0.05 \times 0.68 = 0.034\), or 3.40% per annum.

A household desires a $600,000 portfolio allocated 60% to equities ($360,000) and 40% to bonds ($240,000). Account balances are $200,000 Taxable, $300,000 TDA, and $100,000 Roth. Apply the asset location hierarchy to determine the bond and equity amounts in each account.

Solution

All $240,000 of bonds are placed into the TDA, leaving $60,000 of TDA capacity for equities. The Roth account holds $100,000 of equities. The Taxable account holds $200,000 of equities. Check: Total bonds = $240k; Total equities = $60k (TDA) + $100k (Roth) + $200k (Taxable) = $360k.

Tier 2: Proof

Prove that the ratio of terminal wealth between a Traditional TDA and a Roth account is strictly equal to \((1 - \tau_T) / (1 - \tau_0)\) for any arbitrary time-varying path of returns \(R_1, R_2, \dots, R_T\).

Solution

Let cumulative compound growth be \(G = \prod_{t=1}^T (1 + R_t)\). TDA terminal wealth is \(W_{\text{TDA}} = Y \cdot G \cdot (1 - \tau_T)\). Roth terminal wealth is \(W_{\text{Roth}} = Y (1 - \tau_0) \cdot G\). Taking the ratio: \(\frac{W_{\text{TDA}}}{W_{\text{Roth}}} = \frac{Y \cdot G \cdot (1 - \tau_T)}{Y (1 - \tau_0) \cdot G} = \frac{1 - \tau_T}{1 - \tau_0}\). The return path \(G\) cancels out identically.

Prove that if an equity asset pays zero dividends and capital gains are deferred until date \(T\), its annualized after-tax return in a taxable account exceeds the after-tax return of an identical asset whose capital gains are realized and taxed annually.

Solution

Under annual realization at rate \(\tau\), terminal wealth is \(W_1 = Y (1 + g(1-\tau))^T\). Under deferred realization, terminal wealth before tax is \(Y (1+g)^T\), and after tax on gain: \(W_2 = Y [(1+g)^T - \tau((1+g)^T - 1)] = Y [(1-\tau)(1+g)^T + \tau]\). By convexity of the power function for \(T > 1\), \((1+g)^T > (1 + g(1-\tau))^T\), establishing that tax deferral strictly increases terminal wealth.

Prove that holding corporate bonds in a Roth account and equities in a Traditional TDA is strictly suboptimal compared to the reverse placement when total tax rates across periods are strictly positive.

Solution

Roth growth is exempt from all future taxes. If higher-expected-return equities are placed in TDA, the large capital appreciation is eventually taxed at ordinary income rate \(\tau_T\). Placing equities in Roth shields the larger dollar gain from taxes entirely, while placing lower-growth bonds in TDA minimizes the dollar tax paid upon distribution.

Tier 3: Empirical

What did empirical studies cited in Campbell (2006) find regarding the prevalence of optimal asset location among US households holding both taxable and IRA accounts?

Solution

Campbell (2006, Section 4) reviews evidence showing that a substantial fraction of households practice naive asset duplication, holding the same 60/40 mix inside their taxable accounts and IRAs rather than locating bonds in IRAs and equities in taxable, forfeiting significant after-tax wealth.

How does the tax treatment of qualified dividends versus corporate bond interest differ under current US federal tax law?

Solution

Corporate bond interest is taxed at ordinary federal income rates (up to 37%), whereas qualified dividends and long-term capital gains receive preferential statutory rates (maximum 20% plus 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax), giving equities an intrinsic tax advantage in taxable accounts.

Why do municipal bond yields trade at a persistent discount to comparable-maturity US Treasury and corporate bond yields?

Solution

Municipal bond interest is exempt from federal and often state income taxes. In equilibrium, market clearing implies that municipal yields adjust downward to match the after-tax yield of taxable bonds for the marginal tax bracket investor.

Tier 4: Research

How do progressive income tax brackets in retirement complicate the simple binary choice between Traditional and Roth contributions during working years?

Solution

Because progressive brackets tax initial distributions at 0% (standard deduction), 10%, and 12%, a retiree needs sufficient Traditional TDA balances to fill these low brackets before Roth distributions become advantageous. The optimal strategy is tax diversification across both account types.

Evaluate the economic tradeoff between asset location efficiency and rebalancing friction during severe market dislocations.

Solution

When equities decline sharply, rebalancing back to target weights requires selling bonds and buying equities. If all bonds reside in TDA and equities in taxable, rebalancing requires moving cash between accounts or selling assets in taxable accounts, potentially triggering taxable capital gain realizations.

How does the existence of the Section 1014 basis step-up at death affect the optimal asset location of high-dividend vs. high-growth non-dividend equities?

Solution

High-growth non-dividend equities generate maximum unrealized capital appreciation. Holding them in a taxable account until death allows all accumulated capital gains to escape taxation permanently through the step-up, making taxable accounts optimal for legacy bequest assets.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. Under what exact condition does a Traditional TDA provide identical after-tax terminal wealth to a Roth account?

    Options

    By Proposition 1.2, \(Y(1-\tau)(1+R)^T = Y(1+R)^T(1-\tau)\) whenever the two tax rates are equal.

  2. If a worker is currently in a 35% tax bracket and expects to be in a 22% bracket during retirement, which vehicle is mathematically optimal?

    Options

    When \(\tau_0 > \tau_T\), the ratio \((1 - \tau_T)/(1 - \tau_0) = 0.78 / 0.65 = 1.20\), giving TDA a 20% advantage.

  3. If contribution tax rate is \(\tau_0 = 0.36\) and retirement tax rate is \(\tau_T = 0.20\), what is the ratio of TDA terminal wealth to Roth terminal wealth?

    \((1 - \tau_T)/(1 - \tau_0) = (1 - 0.20)/(1 - 0.36) = 0.80 / 0.64 = 1.25\).

  4. Why does asset location prioritize placing corporate bonds into a Traditional TDA rather than a taxable account?

    Options

    Bond interest is taxed as ordinary income annually; sheltering it inside a TDA eliminates annual tax drag.

  5. Why is a Roth account the ideal location for the asset class with the highest expected capital growth?

    Options

    Since all gains in a Roth account escape taxation permanently, compounding the highest expected return in Roth maximizes the tax subsidy.

  6. A bond pays 5.0% interest. An investor faces a 25% ordinary tax rate. What is the net after-tax return in a taxable account in percent?

    \(5.0\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 3.75\%\).

  7. What unique tax benefit does residential housing equity enjoy under Section 121 of the US Internal Revenue Code?

    Options

    Section 121 provides a substantial capital gains exclusion on primary residence sales for qualifying homeowners.

  8. What does the Section 1014 step-up in cost basis provide upon the death of a taxable account owner?

    Options

    The step-up in basis eliminates all untaxed capital appreciation accumulated during the decedent's lifetime.

  9. What is the error in placing municipal bonds inside a Traditional IRA?

    Options

    Municipal bonds pay lower yields because they are tax-free; holding them in an IRA wastes the tax benefit and subjects future withdrawals to ordinary tax.

  10. What risk arises from holding excessive balances exclusively in Traditional TDAs into one's seventies?

    Options

    Mandatory RMDs starting at age 73–75 can create tax spikes, pushing retirees into higher tax and Medicare surcharge brackets.
