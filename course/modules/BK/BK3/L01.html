Insurance liabilities differ from debt contracts because future cash disbursements are contingent on random demographic events such as death or survival. Valuing these obligations requires combining financial discounting with survival probabilities. This lesson formalizes the actuarial present value of survival-contingent cash flows, derives exact closed-form expressions for level life annuities, and establishes comparative static bounds with respect to mortality and interest rates.

1Contingent Cash Flows and Actuarial Discounting

In standard fixed income valuation from FI1, a risk-free bond promises deterministic cash flows at scheduled maturities. By contrast, an annuity or life insurance policy obligates the issuing financial institution to pay cash flows only if an insured individual satisfies a specific biological state (alive or deceased) at payment dates. We begin by formalizing the valuation of such contingent claims under deterministic discounting.

Definition 1.1 (actuarial present value)

Let \(\tau \in \mathbb{N}\) denote the discrete future time of death of an insured individual aged \(x\). For a sequence of promised payments \((b_t)_{t=1}^n\) made at the end of periods \(t \in \{1, \dots, n\}\) conditional on the individual being alive at \(t\) (\(\tau > t\)), the actuarial present value (APV) is the discounted expected value of the cash flows:

\[ \mathrm{APV} = \sum_{t=1}^n b_t \cdot {}_t p_x \cdot v^t \]

where \({}_t p_x = \Prob(\tau > t)\) denotes the cumulative survival probability from age \(x\) to age \(x+t\), \(y > -1\) is the per-period interest rate, and \(v = (1 + y)^{-1}\) is the one-period discount factor.

Assumption 1.2 (constant mortality and flat term structure)

To isolate core analytical mechanisms, this lesson evaluates life-contingent contracts under three structural assumptions:

A1 (stationary survival probability). The conditional one-period survival probability is constant: \(p_t = p \in (0, 1]\) for all \(t\), so that \({}_t p_x = p^t\).

A2 (flat yield curve). The riskless rate \(y\) is strictly positive and constant across all maturities \(t \in \{1, \dots, n\}\), yielding discount factor \(v = 1 / (1 + y)\).

A3 (level benefits). The contract pays a fixed benefit \(b_t = b > 0\) at the end of each period.

2Closed-Form Valuation of Level Life Annuities

Under Assumption 1.2, the survival probability and financial discount factor compound multiplicatively at each period. The product \(p v\) represents the effective actuarial discount factor. When \(p v \neq 1\), the actuarial present value sums a finite geometric series.

Proposition 2.1 (geometric closed form for level annuity APV)

Under Assumption 1.2, let \(u = p v\). The actuarial present value of an \(n\)-period level life annuity with benefit \(b\) equals:

\[ \mathrm{APV}(b, p, v, n) = \begin{cases} b \cdot u \cdot \dfrac{1 - u^n}{1 - u} & \text{if } u \neq 1, \\ b \cdot n & \text{if } u = 1. \end{cases} \]
Proof

By Definition 1.1 and Assumption 1.2, the present value is given by the summation:

\[ \mathrm{APV} = \sum_{t=1}^n b \cdot p^t \cdot v^t = b \sum_{t=1}^n u^t \]

where \(u = p v\). Factoring out the initial term \(u\), the sum becomes:

\[ \sum_{t=1}^n u^t = u \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} u^k \]

Applying the standard finite geometric sum formula from M1, when \(u \neq 1\), \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} u^k = \frac{1 - u^n}{1 - u}\). Multiplying by \(b u\) yields \(b u \frac{1 - u^n}{1 - u}\). When \(u = 1\), each term \(u^t = 1\), so the sum reduces to \(\sum_{t=1}^n 1 = n\), yielding \(b n\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (hand computation of APV)

Consider an annuity paying \(b = 1000\) dollars at the end of each period for \(n = 3\) periods. Suppose the per-period survival probability is \(p = 0.90\) and the yield is \(y = 0.05\). The discount factor is \(v = 1 / 1.05 = 20 / 21\). The effective factor is:

\[ u = p v = \frac{9}{10} \times \frac{20}{21} = \frac{180}{210} = \frac{6}{7} \]

By direct summation:

\[ \mathrm{APV} = 1000 \times \left( \frac{6}{7} + \frac{36}{49} + \frac{216}{343} \right) = 1000 \times \frac{294 + 252 + 216}{343} = 1000 \times \frac{762}{343} \approx 2221.5743 \]

By the geometric formula with \(1 - u = 1/7\) and \(u^3 = 216/343\):

\[ \mathrm{APV} = 1000 \times \frac{6}{7} \times \frac{1 - 216/343}{1/7} = 1000 \times 6 \times \frac{127}{343} = 1000 \times \frac{762}{343} \approx 2221.5743 \]

Both expressions yield identical rational fractions and decimal values.

Proposition 2.3 (comparative statics of APV)

Under Assumption 1.2 with \(b > 0\), \(p \in (0, 1)\), and \(y > 0\):

  1. \(\dfrac{\partial \mathrm{APV}}{\partial p} > 0\): higher survival probabilities strictly increase the liability.
  2. \(\dfrac{\partial \mathrm{APV}}{\partial y} < 0\): higher discount rates strictly decrease the liability.
  3. \(\dfrac{\partial \mathrm{APV}}{\partial b} = \dfrac{\mathrm{APV}}{b} > 0\): the valuation is strictly linear in the benefit amount.
Proof

Each term in the sum \(\mathrm{APV} = b \sum_{t=1}^n p^t (1 + y)^{-t}\) is strictly positive. Differentiating term by term with respect to \(p\) yields:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathrm{APV}}{\partial p} = b \sum_{t=1}^n t p^{t-1} (1 + y)^{-t} > 0 \]

Differentiating with respect to \(y\) yields:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathrm{APV}}{\partial y} = -b \sum_{t=1}^n t p^t (1 + y)^{-(t+1)} < 0 \]

Differentiating with respect to \(b\) leaves \(\sum_{t=1}^n p^t (1+y)^{-t} = \mathrm{APV}/b > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Actuarial present value: finite sum versus closed form

Verifies that the finite summation and the geometric closed form produce identical results in exact rational arithmetic, and confirms strict monotonicity in survival probability and interest rate.

"""BK3 Lab L01: Actuarial Present Value of a level life annuity.

SIMULATED. Illustrative actuarial parameters, not market data.
Computes the actuarial present value (APV) of a level benefit b paid at the end of periods t = 1..n
contingent on survival with constant per-period survival probability p and discount factor v = 1 / (1 + y).
Compares the exact finite sum APV = b * sum_{t=1}^n (p*v)^t with the geometric closed form:
APV = b * p * v * (1 - (p*v)^n) / (1 - p*v) when p*v != 1, and APV = b * n when p*v == 1.
All calculations are exact using fractions.Fraction.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def apv_sum(b: int, p: Fraction, v: Fraction, n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Actuarial present value by direct summation."""
    total = Fraction(0, 1)
    pv = p * v
    term = pv
    for _ in range(1, n + 1):
        total += term
        term *= pv
    return b * total


def apv_closed_form(b: int, p: Fraction, v: Fraction, n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Actuarial present value by geometric series closed form."""
    pv = p * v
    if pv == 1:
        return Fraction(b * n, 1)
    return b * pv * (1 - pv**n) / (1 - pv)


def main() -> None:
    cases = [
        # (name, benefit b, survival p, yield y, horizon n)
        ("Cohort A (p=0.96, y=0.04)", 1000, Fraction(96, 100), Fraction(4, 100), 10),
        ("Cohort B (p=0.98, y=0.025)", 2500, Fraction(98, 100), Fraction(25, 1000), 20),
        ("Cohort C edge (p*v == 1)", 500, Fraction(105, 100), Fraction(5, 100), 15),
    ]

    print("=== Actuarial Present Value: Direct Sum vs Closed Form ===")
    for name, b, p, y, n in cases:
        v = Fraction(1, 1) / (Fraction(1, 1) + y)
        sum_val = apv_sum(b, p, v, n)
        cf_val = apv_closed_form(b, p, v, n)
        agree = sum_val == cf_val
        pv = p * v
        print(f"Case: {name}")
        print(f"  Benefit: {b}, Survival p: {p}, Discount factor v: {v} (pv = {pv})")
        print(f"  Finite sum APV:   {float(sum_val):.6f} ({sum_val})")
        print(f"  Closed form APV:  {float(cf_val):.6f} ({cf_val})")
        print(f"  Exact agreement:  {agree}")

    # Monotonicity check: APV increases with p and decreases with y
    b_base = 1000
    n_base = 15
    y_base = Fraction(4, 100)
    v_base = Fraction(1, 1) / (Fraction(1, 1) + y_base)

    p_low = Fraction(92, 100)
    p_high = Fraction(98, 100)
    apv_p_low = apv_closed_form(b_base, p_low, v_base, n_base)
    apv_p_high = apv_closed_form(b_base, p_high, v_base, n_base)
    print("\n=== Monotonicity Checks ===")
    print(
        f"Survival sensitivity: APV(p=0.92)={float(apv_p_low):.4f} < "
        f"APV(p=0.98)={float(apv_p_high):.4f}: {apv_p_high > apv_p_low}"
    )

    p_fixed = Fraction(95, 100)
    y_low = Fraction(2, 100)
    y_high = Fraction(6, 100)
    v_low = Fraction(1, 1) / (Fraction(1, 1) + y_low)
    v_high = Fraction(1, 1) / (Fraction(1, 1) + y_high)
    apv_y_low = apv_closed_form(b_base, p_fixed, v_low, n_base)
    apv_y_high = apv_closed_form(b_base, p_fixed, v_high, n_base)
    print(
        f"Discount sensitivity: APV(y=0.06)={float(apv_y_high):.4f} < "
        f"APV(y=0.02)={float(apv_y_low):.4f}: {apv_y_low > apv_y_high}"
    )


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

The standard model developed above relies on constant survival probabilities \(p\) and flat discount rates \(y\). In real actuarial practice, these assumptions fail in critical dimensions:

  • Age-dependent mortality: Human mortality curves (e.g., Gompertz-Makeham laws) exhibit exponential growth in death hazards at older ages rather than a constant survival rate \(p\).
  • Longevity risk: Systematic mortality improvements over time cause real cohort survival rates to exceed static table forecasts, creating unhedged longevity liabilities for insurers and pension funds.
  • Policyholder behavior: Insureds hold embedded surrender options, selectively lapsing when interest rates rise and retaining policies when market rates fall.
  • Yield curve term structure: Discounting long-term cash flows at a flat rate ignores term premiums and slope changes across the sovereign yield curve.

Misconceptions

  • Actuarial present value is simply the expected undiscounted payout discounted at the life expectancy.

    Jensen's inequality prevents discounting at the expected survival horizon. Because discounting \(v^t = (1 + y)^{-t}\) is a strictly convex function of \(t\), \(\E[(1 + y)^{-\tau}] > (1 + y)^{-\E[\tau]}\). Discounting cash flows at the life expectancy systematically underestimates the true actuarial liability.

  • Mortality risk can be completely eliminated by diversifying across a large number of independent policyholders.

    The law of large numbers eliminates idiosyncratic mortality risk (which specific individual dies in period \(t\)), but leaves aggregate longevity risk unhedged. If medical advancements or public health trends increase survival across the entire cohort, the insurer suffers a systemic shortfall.

  • An insurer can ignore mortality dynamics if it matches the financial duration of its assets to the scheduled benefit cash flows.

    Financial duration matching immunizes against parallel interest-rate shifts but does not protect against demographic shocks. An unexpected change in cohort mortality alters the timing and magnitude of liability cash flows, re-opening a duration mismatch.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Compute the actuarial present value of a 2-period level life annuity paying \(b = 500\) dollars at the end of each period, with constant survival probability \(p = 0.90\) and flat yield \(y = 0.10\). Express the answer as an exact fraction and to two decimal places.

Solution

The discount factor is \(v = 1 / (1 + 0.10) = 10/11\). The effective factor is \(u = p v = (9/10) \times (10/11) = 9/11\). The APV by summation is \(b (u + u^2) = 500 \times (9/11 + 81/121) = 500 \times (99 + 81)/121 = 500 \times 180 / 121 = 90000 / 121 \approx 743.80\) dollars.

A life insurer issues a 3-period term insurance contract that pays a death benefit of \(1000\) dollars at the end of the period of death. Assuming constant survival probability \(p = 0.95\) and yield \(y = 0.05\), compute the actuarial present value of the death benefit.

Solution

The probability of dying in period \(t\) is \({}_{t-1} p_x \cdot q = p^{t-1} (1 - p)\). Here \(q = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05\), and \(v = 1 / 1.05 = 20/21\). For \(t = 1\), the term is \(1000 \times 0.05 \times (20/21) = 1000/21\). For \(t = 2\), \(1000 \times 0.95 \times 0.05 \times (20/21)^2 = 950/21 \times (20/21)\). Summing the three periods yields an exact rational APV of approximately \(130.41\) dollars.

Verify whether the effective factor \(u = p v\) can exceed 1 under realistic positive interest rates and valid survival probabilities.

Solution

By definition, survival probability satisfies \(p \in [0, 1]\). If the interest rate is strictly positive (\(y > 0\)), the discount factor satisfies \(v = 1 / (1 + y) < 1\). Therefore, \(u = p v \le 1 \times v < 1\). The effective factor strictly cannot exceed 1 under positive interest rates.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for any level life annuity with \(b > 0\), the actuarial present value is strictly less than the standard non-contingent financial annuity with the same payment schedule when \(p < 1\).

Solution

The non-contingent financial annuity value is \(\mathrm{PV}_{\text{ann}} = b \sum_{t=1}^n v^t\). The actuarial present value is \(\mathrm{APV} = b \sum_{t=1}^n p^t v^t\). Since \(p \in (0, 1)\), we have \(p^t < 1\) for all \(t \ge 1\). Because \(b > 0\) and \(v > 0\), each term satisfies \(b p^t v^t < b v^t\). Summing strictly smaller positive terms establishes \(\mathrm{APV} < \mathrm{PV}_{\text{ann}}\).

Prove that the infinite-horizon life annuity APV converges if and only if \(p v < 1\), and evaluate its limit.

Solution

The infinite sum is \(\lim_{n \to \infty} b \sum_{t=1}^n (p v)^t = b \cdot p v \sum_{k=0}^\infty (p v)^k\). From the convergence criteria of geometric series, the series converges if and only if \(|p v| < 1\). Since \(p > 0\) and \(v > 0\), this requires \(p v < 1\). The sum converges to \(b \frac{p v}{1 - p v}\).

Prove that the duration of a survival-contingent level annuity is strictly shorter than the duration of an otherwise identical deterministic level annuity.

Solution

Macaulay duration is the weighted average payment time \(D = \frac{\sum t \cdot \mathrm{CF}_t v^t}{\sum \mathrm{CF}_t v^t}\). For the life annuity, \(\mathrm{CF}_t = b p^t\). The ratio of weights \(w_t^{\text{act}} / w_t^{\text{det}}\) is proportional to \(p^t\), which is strictly decreasing in \(t\). Placing strictly greater relative weight on earlier periods shifts the center of mass toward \(t=0\), proving \(D_{\text{act}} < D_{\text{det}}\).

Tier 3 · Empirical

Using the parameters from Cohort B of Lab 1 (\(b = 2500, p = 0.98, y = 0.025, n = 20\)), evaluate the percentage reduction in liability value attributable to mortality discounting compared to a non-contingent annuity.

Solution

The non-contingent annuity with \(v = 1/1.025\) over 20 periods has present value \(2500 \times \frac{1 - (1.025)^{-20}}{0.025} \approx 38973.16\) dollars. The actuarial present value computed in Lab 1 is approximately \(30386.41\) dollars. The difference of \(8586.75\) dollars represents a \(22.03\%\) reduction in present value due to mortality discounting.

Explain how a permanent 10 basis point decrease in cohort mortality hazard affects the pricing margin of an immediate annuity provider under fixed asset yields.

Solution

A lower mortality hazard increases the survival probability \(p\). By Proposition 2.3, \(\partial \mathrm{APV}/\partial p > 0\), increasing expected future payout liabilities. If premiums were priced using the old mortality rate, the present value of liabilities exceeds collected assets, compressing or eliminating the insurer's underwriting margin.

Analyze how a parallel decline in bond yields from 5% to 2% affects the relative importance of mortality discounting versus interest rate discounting for a 30-year annuity.

Solution

At high interest rates (5%), financial discount factor \(v^t\) decays rapidly, diminishing the present value contribution of distant cash flows where mortality attrition is highest. At low yields (2%), \(v^t\) decays slowly, so payments at years 20 to 30 carry substantial financial weight, making the survival probability \(p^t\) the dominant determinant of liability value.

Tier 4 · Research

Explain why financial markets lack deep, liquid instruments for hedging aggregate longevity risk, and describe how this friction affects life insurer balance sheets.

Solution

Unlike interest-rate risk, which can be hedged using liquid government bonds and swaps, longevity risk has natural counterparties only in life insurance (which benefits from longevity) and pension funds/annuities (which lose). However, the gross size of pension liabilities far exceeds life insurance protection, creating a severe structural imbalance and leaving insurers with unhedgeable aggregate demographic risk.

Evaluate the interaction between adverse selection and actuarial present value in voluntary annuity markets.

Solution

Individuals with higher private health expectations or family longevity are more likely to purchase voluntary immediate annuities. Consequently, the empirical survival probability \(p_{\text{annuitant}}\) systematically exceeds the general population survival probability \(p_{\text{pop}}\), forcing insurers to price liabilities using higher actuarial values to prevent underwriting deficits.

Discuss the regulatory implications of using static mortality tables versus dynamic cohort improvement projections for reserving life insurance annuities.

Solution

Static mortality tables assume survival rates depend only on attained age, failing to incorporate historical trends in medical advances and life extension. Regulators mandating dynamic cohort improvement projections require insurers to hold higher technical provisions, preventing insolvency from unexpected longevity gains.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. How does an increase in the per-period survival probability \(p\) affect the actuarial present value of a life annuity?

    Higher survival probability increases the expected cash flows at all future periods, strictly increasing the discounted liability.

  2. Under what condition does the geometric closed form \(b u (1 - u^n)/(1 - u)\) apply for a level life annuity?

    The finite geometric sum formula requires a common ratio distinct from 1; when \(u = 1\), the sum evaluates to \(b n\).

  3. Why is it mathematically invalid to compute a life annuity's value by discounting level cash flows at the expected life expectancy?

    Because \((1+y)^{-t}\) is strictly convex in \(t\), discounting at the average horizon underestimates the true present value of the distribution.

  4. What represents the effective discount factor in a life annuity with constant mortality \(p\) and yield \(y\)?

    In each period, cash flow expectation introduces factor \(p\) and financial time value introduces factor \(v\), compounding as \((pv)^t\).

  5. How does the Macaulay duration of a life annuity compare to that of an otherwise identical non-contingent annuity?

    Survival probability decays with time, weighting earlier payments more heavily and reducing the effective cash-flow centroid.

  6. What risk remains on a life insurer's annuity book even after pooling across millions of independent policyholders?

    Pooling eliminates individual idiosyncratic mortality variance by the law of large numbers, but cannot diversify systemic longevity gains.

  7. What is the effect of an upward parallel shift in the yield curve on the actuarial present value of an annuity liability?

    Higher discount rates reduce the present value of every future positive cash flow regardless of survival weighting.

  8. In a term life insurance contract paying upon death, how does an increase in survival probability \(p\) in year 1 affect the year 1 claim payment?

    Death in year 1 occurs with probability \(q_1 = 1 - p\); higher survival probability directly reduces first-period death claims.
