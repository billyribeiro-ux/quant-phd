Defined-benefit pension plans hold legal obligations to pay lifetime retirement annuities to millions of workers. The reported solvency of a pension fund is exquisitely sensitive to the interest rate chosen to discount these multi-decade obligations. This lesson formalizes the pension funding ratio, analyzes the economic and accounting debate over the liability discount rate, proves the strict monotonicity of funding status in the discount rate, and evaluates the empirical evidence on public pension solvency.

1Pension Obligations and the Funding Ratio

A defined-benefit (DB) pension plan promises retirees a formulaic income stream, typically proportional to years of service and final average salary. Evaluating whether the plan holds sufficient accumulated assets to meet these obligations requires comparing asset market value against the discounted value of future cash flows.

Definition 1.1 (pension funding ratio and liability discount rate)

Let a defined-benefit pension scheme possess accumulated investment assets of market value \(A > 0\) and projected future benefit cash flows \((c_t)_{t=1}^n\), where \(c_t \ge 0\) denotes expected benefit payments in period \(t\), with \(\sum_{t=1}^n c_t > 0\).

The liability discount rate is the interest rate or term structure of interest rates \((y_t)_{t=1}^n\) selected to compute the actuarial present value of promised obligations:

\[ L((y_t)) = \sum_{t=1}^n c_t (1 + y_t)^{-t} \]

The pension funding ratio (\(\mathrm{FR}\)) is the ratio of asset market value to the actuarial liability:

\[ \mathrm{FR} = \frac{A}{L((y_t))} = \frac{A}{\sum_{t=1}^n c_t (1 + y_t)^{-t}} \]

A plan is described as fully funded when \(\mathrm{FR} \ge 1\), and underfunded when \(\mathrm{FR} < 1\).

Assumption 1.2 (discount-rate choices in pension finance)

In global pension accounting and financial economics, three primary discount-rate conventions are contested:

Choice 1 (risk-free government rate). Cash flows are discounted at sovereign bond yields or zero-coupon spot rates obtained via curve bootstrapping from default-free government debt. Under financial economic principles, because accrued pension benefits possess legal seniority and low default risk, they must be discounted at a risk-free rate regardless of the assets held.

Choice 2 (high-quality corporate bond curve). Cash flows are discounted using yield curves derived from investment-grade corporate bonds (e.g., AA corporate debt, as prescribed under US GAAP ASC 715 and IFRS IAS 19).

Choice 3 (assumed return on plan assets). Cash flows are discounted at the expected rate of return on the pension plan's risky investment portfolio (e.g., 7.0% to 8.0%, commonly permitted in US public pension accounting under GASB standards).

2Monotonicity and the Accounting Wedge

The selection of the liability discount rate is not merely a technical assumption; it is the dominant numerical driver of reported pension liabilities. We formalize the mathematical relationship between the discount rate and the funding ratio.

Proposition 2.1 (monotonicity of pension funding ratio)

Under Definition 1.1, for any constant discount rate \(y > -1\) with \(A > 0\) and positive cash flows \(c_t \ge 0\) (not all zero):

  1. The liability function \(L(y) = \sum_{t=1}^n c_t (1 + y)^{-t}\) is strictly decreasing and strictly convex in \(y\): \(\dfrac{d L}{d y} < 0\) and \(\dfrac{d^2 L}{d y^2} > 0\).
  2. The funding ratio \(\mathrm{FR}(y) = \dfrac{A}{L(y)}\) is strictly increasing in \(y\): \(\dfrac{d \mathrm{FR}}{d y} > 0\).
Proof

Computing the first derivative of \(L(y)\) with respect to \(y\):

\[ \frac{d L}{d y} = \sum_{t=1}^n c_t \frac{d}{d y}(1 + y)^{-t} = -\sum_{t=1}^n t c_t (1 + y)^{-(t+1)} \]

Because \(c_t \ge 0\) with at least one \(c_t > 0\), and \(1 + y > 0\), each non-zero term is strictly negative, establishing \(\frac{d L}{d y} < 0\). Differentiating once more:

\[ \frac{d^2 L}{d y^2} = \sum_{t=1}^n t(t+1) c_t (1 + y)^{-(t+2)} > 0 \]

establishing strict convexity. Now differentiate the funding ratio \(\mathrm{FR}(y) = A \cdot [L(y)]^{-1}\):

\[ \frac{d \mathrm{FR}}{d y} = -A [L(y)]^{-2} \frac{d L}{d y} = -\frac{A}{L(y)^2} \frac{d L}{d y} \]

Since \(A > 0\) and \(L(y) > 0\), the prefactor \(-\frac{A}{L(y)^2}\) is strictly negative. Because \(\frac{d L}{d y} < 0\), the product is strictly positive: \(\frac{d \mathrm{FR}}{d y} > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (funding ratio under competing rates)

Consider a plan with \(A = 10\) million dollars in assets and a single liability payment of \(c_{20} = 40\) million dollars due in 20 years.

  • Under a risk-free yield of \(y_1 = 3.0\%\): \(L_1 = 40 \times (1.03)^{-20} = 40 \times 0.55368 = 22.147\) million dollars. The funding ratio is \(\mathrm{FR}_1 = 10 / 22.147 = 45.15\%\) (severe underfunding).
  • Under an expected asset return rate of \(y_3 = 7.0\%\): \(L_3 = 40 \times (1.07)^{-20} = 40 \times 0.25842 = 10.337\) million dollars. The funding ratio is \(\mathrm{FR}_3 = 10 / 10.337 = 96.74\%\) (nearly fully funded).

The identical fund with identical assets and promised benefits appears either critically underfunded (45.15%) or practically solvent (96.74%) purely based on the discount-rate choice.

Remark 2.3 (empirical evidence on public pension liabilities)

Novy-Marx and Rauh (2011) examine the funding status of US state public pension systems. They demonstrate that discounting promises at expected asset returns (typically 8%) understates aggregate liabilities by trillions of dollars relative to risk-free municipal or Treasury discounting that properly reflects the credit characteristics of accrued obligations. The choice of discount rate is intensely contested in public finance: while financial economists advocate riskless discounting, governmental accounting standards allow expected asset returns, citing the long-term investment horizon of public sponsors.

Lab 1 · Pension funding ratio under discount-rate choices

Verifies that the funding ratio of a defined-benefit pension scheme is strictly increasing in the discount rate across three standard market conventions: a flat risk-free rate (3%), an upward-sloping bootstrapped spot curve (2% to 4.5%), and an expected asset return rate (7%).

"""BK3 Lab L04: Pension funding ratio under discount-rate choices.

SIMULATED. Illustrative pension cash flows and discount rates, not market data.
Computes the defined-benefit pension funding ratio FR = A / L across three discount-rate choices:
  Choice 1: Flat risk-free government bond yield (y_1 = 3.00% per annum).
  Choice 2: Upward-sloping zero-coupon spot curve y_t rising linearly from 2.00% at year 1 to 4.50% at year 20.
  Choice 3: Expected return on plan assets / actuarial discount rate (y_3 = 7.00% per annum).
Evaluates the actuarial liability L = sum_{t=1}^n c_t * (1 + y_t)^(-t) for a 20-year illustrative
benefit payout schedule and verifies the strict monotonic relationship between discount rate and funding ratio.
All calculations are exact using fractions.Fraction and rounded half-up for display.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction


def round_half_up_dec(val: Fraction, places: int) -> float:
    factor = 10**places
    floored: int = int(math.floor(val * factor + Fraction(1, 2)))  # noqa: RUF046
    return float(floored) / float(factor)


def main() -> None:
    # 20 years of illustrative retirement benefit obligations (in dollars)
    cash_flows = [
        Fraction(500_000, 1),
        Fraction(550_000, 1),
        Fraction(600_000, 1),
        Fraction(650_000, 1),
        Fraction(700_000, 1),
        Fraction(750_000, 1),
        Fraction(800_000, 1),
        Fraction(850_000, 1),
        Fraction(900_000, 1),
        Fraction(950_000, 1),
        Fraction(1_000_000, 1),
        Fraction(950_000, 1),
        Fraction(900_000, 1),
        Fraction(850_000, 1),
        Fraction(800_000, 1),
        Fraction(750_000, 1),
        Fraction(700_000, 1),
        Fraction(650_000, 1),
        Fraction(600_000, 1),
        Fraction(550_000, 1),
    ]
    n = len(cash_flows)
    total_undiscounted = sum(cash_flows)

    # Plan assets (fixed at $10,000,000)
    assets = Fraction(10_000_000, 1)

    # Choice 1: Flat risk-free rate 3.0%
    y_flat_rf = Fraction(3, 100)
    liab_flat_rf = sum(cf / ((Fraction(1, 1) + y_flat_rf) ** t) for t, cf in enumerate(cash_flows, start=1))
    fr_flat_rf = assets / liab_flat_rf

    # Choice 2: Spot curve: y_t = 0.02 + 0.025 * (t - 1) / 19  (from 2.0% to 4.5%)
    spot_rates = [Fraction(2, 100) + Fraction(25, 1000) * Fraction(t - 1, 19) for t in range(1, n + 1)]
    liab_spot = sum(
        cf / ((Fraction(1, 1) + y_t) ** t)
        for t, (cf, y_t) in enumerate(zip(cash_flows, spot_rates, strict=False), start=1)
    )
    fr_spot = assets / liab_spot

    # Choice 3: Actuarial expected return on assets 7.0%
    y_actuarial = Fraction(7, 100)
    liab_actuarial = sum(cf / ((Fraction(1, 1) + y_actuarial) ** t) for t, cf in enumerate(cash_flows, start=1))
    fr_actuarial = assets / liab_actuarial

    print("=== Pension Funding Ratio Under Alternative Discount-Rate Choices ===")
    print(f"Total Undiscounted Promised Cash Flows (20 years): ${float(total_undiscounted):,.2f}")
    print(f"Fixed Pension Plan Assets:                         ${float(assets):,.2f}\n")

    print(f"{'Discount Rate Choice':<35} | {'Liability ($)':<16} | {'Funding Ratio':<14} | {'Surplus / (Deficit)'}")
    print("-" * 88)

    choices = [
        ("Choice 1: Flat Risk-Free (3.00%)", liab_flat_rf, fr_flat_rf),
        ("Choice 2: Spot Curve (2.00% - 4.50%)", liab_spot, fr_spot),
        ("Choice 3: Assumed Asset Return (7.00%)", liab_actuarial, fr_actuarial),
    ]

    for label, liab, fr in choices:
        surplus = assets - liab
        fr_rounded = round_half_up_dec(fr, 4)
        print(f"{label:<35} | ${float(liab):14,.2f} | {fr_rounded:12.4f} ({float(fr):.2%}) | ${float(surplus):+13,.2f}")

    print("\n=== Mathematical Monotonicity Checks ===")
    monotone_l = liab_flat_rf > liab_spot > liab_actuarial
    print(f"Liability decreases with discount rate: L(3.0%) > L(Spot) > L(7.0%): {monotone_l}")
    monotone_fr = fr_flat_rf < fr_spot < fr_actuarial
    print(f"Funding ratio strictly increases with discount rate: FR(3.0%) < FR(Spot) < FR(7.0%): {monotone_fr}")
    deficit_to_surplus = (assets < liab_flat_rf) and (assets > liab_actuarial)
    print(f"Plan appears underfunded under risk-free rate but overfunded under expected return: {deficit_to_surplus}")


main()

The lab evaluates a 20-year schedule of pension obligations totaling $15.2M in undiscounted cash flows against $10.0M in assets. Under the flat 3.00% risk-free rate, liabilities equal $11.45M, yielding an underfunded ratio of 0.8732 (87.32%). Under the upward-sloping spot curve (2.0% to 4.5%), liabilities decrease to $10.97M, raising the funding ratio to 0.9113 (91.13%). Under the 7.00% expected asset return rate, liabilities collapse to $8.07M, generating an apparent surplus with a funding ratio of 1.2393 (123.93%). The monotonicity condition L(3%) > L(Spot) > L(7%) and FR(3%) < FR(Spot) < FR(7%) holds strictly.

Where the Canon Breaks

While the mathematical model proves strict monotonicity for deterministic cash flows, actual pension dynamics introduce complex non-linearities:

  • Cost-of-living adjustments (COLA): In many public plans, benefits are indexed to inflation. When inflation surges, cash flows \(c_t\) expand dynamically, potentially outpacing the liability reductions from rising nominal yields.
  • Incentive for risk-seeking: Because accounting rules allow discounting at expected asset returns, underfunded pension sponsors face a moral hazard incentive to reallocate portfolios toward high-beta equities, private equity, and hedge funds to artificially justify higher discount rates.
  • Sponsor credit risk: In private corporate pensions, benefit security is limited by sponsor bankruptcy risk and pension guaranty caps (e.g., PBGC), whereas public pension promises frequently enjoy state constitutional protections.
  • Mortality improvement updates: Upward revisions to longevity tables systematically expand cash flows \(c_t\) across later retirement years, offsetting gains from rising discount rates.

Misconceptions

  • Discounting pension liabilities at the expected return on risky assets is justified because the plan holds a diversified long-term investment portfolio.

    In financial economics, the discount rate applied to a liability must reflect the risk characteristics of the liability itself, not the expected return of the assets chosen to fund it. Holding risky equities cannot reduce the true economic value of a legally guaranteed debt obligation.

  • A pension fund with a funding ratio of 100% under expected return accounting is economically solvent.

    A plan that is 100% funded at an 8% discount rate is severely underfunded in economic terms. If the risk-free rate is 3%, the market-consistent value of liabilities is substantially larger, meaning that without high equity returns the plan will exhaust assets and require sponsor bailouts.

  • Changing the liability discount rate generates real economic wealth for pension beneficiaries.

    Altering the discount rate is an accounting revaluation that changes only the present value measurement. It does not alter the contractual cash payouts promised to retirees or create additional investable cash assets.

Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A pension plan has assets of 50 million dollars. Its future obligations consist of a single payment of 100 million dollars due in 15 years. Compute the pension funding ratio under discount rates of 4% and 8% per annum.

Solution

At 4%, the liability is \(L_4 = 100 / (1.04)^{15} = 100 / 1.80094 = 55.526\) million dollars, giving a funding ratio of \(\mathrm{FR}_4 = 50 / 55.526 \approx 90.05\%\). At 8%, the liability is \(L_8 = 100 / (1.08)^{15} = 100 / 3.17217 = 31.524\) million dollars, giving a funding ratio of \(\mathrm{FR}_8 = 50 / 31.524 \approx 158.61\%\).

Compute the Macaulay duration of the single-payment pension liability in Problem M1 at a 4% discount rate.

Solution

For a single bullet payment due at year 15, all cash flow weight is concentrated at \(t = 15\). Thus, the Macaulay duration is exactly 15.0 years, independent of the discount rate.

A pension plan has 200 million in assets and 250 million in liabilities. If an actuarial assumption update increases the discount rate such that liabilities decrease by 12%, compute the new funding ratio.

Solution

The initial funding ratio is \(200 / 250 = 80.0\%\). The new liability is \(L_{\text{new}} = 250 \times (1 - 0.12) = 220\) million dollars. The new funding ratio is \(\mathrm{FR}_{\text{new}} = 200 / 220 = 10 / 11 \approx 90.91\%\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that the percentage sensitivity of the funding ratio to the discount rate equals the modified duration of the liabilities: \(\frac{1}{\mathrm{FR}} \frac{d \mathrm{FR}}{d y} = D_{\text{mod}, L}\).

Solution

By definition, \(\mathrm{FR}(y) = A / L(y)\). Taking natural logarithms: \(\ln \mathrm{FR}(y) = \ln A - \ln L(y)\). Differentiating with respect to \(y\): \(\frac{1}{\mathrm{FR}} \frac{d \mathrm{FR}}{d y} = 0 - \frac{1}{L} \frac{d L}{d y} = -\frac{1}{L} \frac{d L}{d y}\). By definition of modified duration, \(D_{\text{mod}, L} = -\frac{1}{L} \frac{d L}{d y}\). Therefore, \(\frac{1}{\mathrm{FR}} \frac{d \mathrm{FR}}{d y} = D_{\text{mod}, L}\).

Prove that for any two cash flow schedules with the same undiscounted sum, the schedule with cash flows weighted further in the future exhibits strictly greater funding ratio sensitivity to discount-rate changes.

Solution

From Problem P1, \(\frac{d \ln \mathrm{FR}}{d y} = D_{\text{mod}, L}\). Modified duration is proportional to Macaulay duration \(D_{\text{Mac}} = \frac{\sum t c_t v^t}{\sum c_t v^t}\). Shifting cash flows to later dates increases \(t\), which strictly increases Macaulay duration and modified duration, thereby proving that the percentage change in funding ratio is strictly larger.

Prove that if an asset portfolio is invested in cash earning zero yield (\(D_A = 0\)), any interest rate increase strictly improves the net pension surplus \(A - L(y)\).

Solution

Surplus is \(S(y) = A - L(y)\). Differentiating with respect to \(y\): \(\frac{d S}{d y} = \frac{d A}{d y} - \frac{d L}{d y}\). Since cash has zero duration, \(\frac{d A}{d y} = 0\). By Proposition 2.1, \(\frac{d L}{d y} < 0\). Therefore, \(\frac{d S}{d y} = 0 - \frac{d L}{d y} = -\frac{d L}{d y} > 0\), establishing strict improvement.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Citing Novy-Marx and Rauh (2011), describe the magnitude of the funding gap identified across US state pension plans when moving from actuarial discount rates to taxable municipal or Treasury rates.

Solution

Novy-Marx and Rauh (2011) show that while US state pensions reported aggregate unfunded liabilities of approximately 1 trillion dollars under 8% actuarial rates, discounting liabilities at risk-free Treasury rates increased the shortfall to over 3 trillion dollars, revealing that conventional actuarial methods obscured more than 2 trillion dollars in economic underfunding.

Explain how corporate defined-benefit sponsors responded to the Pension Protection Act of 2006, which required discounting corporate pension liabilities using high-quality corporate bond yields rather than long-term Treasury yields.

Solution

The Act forced corporate sponsors to mark liabilities against a corporate spot curve, introducing significant volatility into corporate earnings and balance sheets. This regulatory shift accelerated corporate plan freezes and spurred adoption of liability-driven investment strategies to hedge rate risk.

Using the results from Lab 1, evaluate why a public pension sponsor might resist adopting market-consistent spot curve discounting.

Solution

In Lab 1, moving from the assumed 7% asset return to the market spot curve expands liabilities from $8.07M to $10.97M, turning an apparent surplus (123.93% funding) into an underfunded deficit (91.13%). Adopting market rates would legally force government sponsors to increase tax-funded contributions or cut public services.

Tier 4 · Research

Contrast the financial economics perspective on pension discounting (Arrow-Debreu pricing) with the actuarial going-concern perspective.

Solution

Financial economics dictates that the price of any liability equals the cost of a replicating portfolio with identical cash-flow timing and credit risk, implying risk-free discounting for guaranteed promises. Actuaries counter that public pensions are perpetual going concerns with intergenerational risk-sharing capacity, arguing that discounting at expected long-term portfolio returns reflects the average funding cost over centuries.

Analyze how accounting rules linking the liability discount rate to expected asset returns create perverse asset-allocation incentives for pension trustees.

Solution

When discount rates depend on expected returns, trustees can reduce reported liabilities by shifting assets into riskier, higher-expected-return asset classes (e.g. private equity). This creates a moral hazard: trustees increase portfolio tail risk and beta exposure specifically to generate the accounting appearance of solvency.

Evaluate the macroeconomic consequences of persistent pension underfunding on municipal and sovereign borrowing costs.

Solution

Unfunded pension obligations represent senior off-balance-sheet debt. Credit rating agencies incorporate unfunded pension liabilities into sovereign and municipal debt ratings. Severe pension deficits trigger municipal rating downgrades, raising borrowing costs for public infrastructure and constraining fiscal flexibility.

Retrieval Quiz

  1. How is the defined-benefit pension funding ratio defined?

    The funding ratio measures the proportion of accumulated assets available to cover the present value of accrued benefit obligations.

  2. What mathematical property describes the relationship between the pension funding ratio and the liability discount rate?

    Proposition 2.1 proves that higher discount rates reduce the present value of future benefit cash flows, strictly increasing the funding ratio.

  3. According to financial economics principles, what should determine the discount rate for accrued pension benefits?

    In financial economics, the discount rate applied to any financial claim reflects its own risk profile, not the return on funding assets.

  4. What did Novy-Marx and Rauh (2011) discover regarding US state pension liabilities?

    Novy-Marx and Rauh (2011) demonstrated that public pensions masked enormous economic shortfalls by discounting at 8% assumed asset returns.

  5. What moral hazard arises when accounting rules permit pension liabilities to be discounted at expected asset returns?

    Linking discount rates to expected returns rewards sponsors for shifting into high-beta, high-volatility assets to suppress reported liabilities.

  6. How does the percentage sensitivity of the funding ratio to the discount rate relate to liability duration?

    Taking the logarithmic derivative shows that the percentage change in the funding ratio per unit change in yield equals liability modified duration.

  7. If a plan's benefit cash flows are concentrated 30 years into the future rather than 10 years, how does its funding ratio respond to yield changes?

    Longer-duration cash flows experience larger percentage present-value swings under interest-rate changes, amplifying funding ratio volatility.

  8. Under US GAAP (ASC 715) and IFRS (IAS 19), what discount rate must corporate defined-benefit plans use?

    Corporate accounting standards mandate high-quality corporate bond yields matching the maturity of pension obligations.
