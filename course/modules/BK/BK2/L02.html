Exchange-Traded Funds: Creation-Redemption and Arbitrage

Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) have transformed modern financial markets by decoupling secondary market liquidity from primary portfolio trades. Through the in-kind creation and redemption mechanism, Authorized Participants (APs) arbitrage discrepancies between ETF market prices and the intraday indicative value of the underlying portfolio basket. This architecture offers continuous intraday liquidity, insulates long-term fund shareholders from redemption-driven transaction costs and taxable capital gain realizations, and facilitates low-cost indexing. However, ETF arbitrage can also propagate non-fundamental liquidity and sentiment shocks to constituent securities, altering cross-asset co-movement. Understanding the mechanics of ETF creation and redemption is critical for quantitative market participants and structural market observers.

1The Dual-Market Architecture of ETFs

Whereas an open-end mutual fund transacts directly with retail investors once daily at forward NAV, an Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF) operates a bifurcated two-tier market structure.

Definition 1.1 (ETF Creation and Redemption)

ETF creation and redemption is the primary-market institutional mechanism whereby specialized institutional broker-dealers known as Authorized Participants (APs) exchange large specified blocks of ETF shares (creation units, typically 25,000 to 100,000 shares) directly with the fund sponsor for an in-kind deposit or receipt of the underlying portfolio basket securities, aligning the secondary exchange market price of the ETF with the fund's net asset value.

The two layers operate as follows:

  • Secondary Market: Retail and institutional investors buy and sell ETF shares on public exchanges throughout the trading day at market prices \(P_{\text{ETF}, t}\). Secondary trades represent ownership transfers between investors; they require zero portfolio turnover or cash disbursements by the ETF itself.
  • Primary Market: When secondary market supply and demand push \(P_{\text{ETF}, t}\) away from the underlying basket value, APs execute creation or redemption orders directly with the ETF custodian.

2No-Arbitrage Bounds and AP Economics

Let \(\mathrm{IIV}_t\) (Intraday Indicative Value, also termed intraday NAV) denote the per-share market value of the underlying portfolio basket at time \(t\):

\[ \mathrm{IIV}_t = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^M N_{i, t} P_{i, t}^{\text{live}} + C_t}{S_t} \]

where \(P_{i, t}^{\text{live}}\) is the prevailing real-time price of constituent security \(i\). The difference \(\Pi_t = P_{\text{ETF}, t} - \mathrm{IIV}_t\) represents the ETF premium (if \(\Pi_t > 0\)) or discount (if \(\Pi_t < 0\)).

Theorem 2.1 (ETF No-Arbitrage Bands)

Let \(c_{\text{create}}\) denote the proportional total cost to an AP of executing a creation unit (including underlying basket bid-ask execution spreads, creation fees, and clearing charges), and let \(c_{\text{redeem}}\) denote the corresponding redemption cost. Under competitive AP arbitrage, the secondary ETF market price \(P_{\text{ETF}, t}\) satisfies the no-arbitrage bounds:

\[ \mathrm{IIV}_t (1 - c_{\text{redeem}}) \le P_{\text{ETF}, t} \le \mathrm{IIV}_t (1 + c_{\text{create}}) \]
Proof

We establish both boundaries by construction of arbitrage trading strategies:

  1. Upper Bound (Creation Arbitrage): Suppose \(P_{\text{ETF}, t} > \mathrm{IIV}_t (1 + c_{\text{create}})\). An AP sells ETF shares short in the secondary market at price \(P_{\text{ETF}, t}\) and simultaneously purchases the underlying basket in the cash market at cost \(\mathrm{IIV}_t\). The AP delivers the basket to the ETF sponsor, pays creation cost \(c_{\text{create}} \mathrm{IIV}_t\), receives newly created ETF shares in-kind, and closes the short position. The net arbitrage profit per share is: \[ \pi_{\text{create}} = P_{\text{ETF}, t} - \mathrm{IIV}_t (1 + c_{\text{create}}) > 0 \] Selling ETF shares increases secondary supply, while buying underlying constituents increases basket prices, driving \(P_{\text{ETF}, t}\) downward toward the upper bound.
  2. Lower Bound (Redemption Arbitrage): Suppose \(P_{\text{ETF}, t} < \mathrm{IIV}_t (1 - c_{\text{redeem}})\). An AP buys undervalued ETF shares in the secondary market at price \(P_{\text{ETF}, t}\) and simultaneously sells short the underlying basket at price \(\mathrm{IIV}_t\). The AP surrenders the ETF shares to the fund sponsor, receives the underlying basket in-kind, pays redemption fee \(c_{\text{redeem}} \mathrm{IIV}_t\), and delivers the received shares to close the short basket position. The net arbitrage profit per share is: \[ \pi_{\text{redeem}} = \mathrm{IIV}_t (1 - c_{\text{redeem}}) - P_{\text{ETF}, t} > 0 \] Buying ETF shares lifts secondary prices, while shorting underlying stocks exerts downward pressure on the basket, restoring \(P_{\text{ETF}, t}\) into the band.
Example 2.2 (Arbitrage Threshold on an S&P 500 ETF)

For a highly liquid US large-cap equity ETF with \(\mathrm{IIV} = \$400.00\), basket execution spread \(c_{\text{create}} = 0.0005\) (5 bps) and redemption cost \(c_{\text{redeem}} = 0.0005\) (5 bps), the no-arbitrage band is:

\[ \$400.00(1 - 0.0005) \le P_{\text{ETF}} \le \$400.00(1 + 0.0005) \implies \$399.80 \le P_{\text{ETF}} \le \$400.20 \]

The secondary price fluctuates within \(\pm 20\) cents of IIV without triggering primary creations or redemptions.

3Tax Efficiency: Section 852(b)(6) In-Kind Shields

In a standard open-end mutual fund, when redemptions force the manager to sell appreciated securities, the fund realizes capital gains that must be distributed pro rata to all remaining shareholders at year-end under Subchapter M of the Internal Revenue Code. ETFs eliminate this tax liability through in-kind transfers.

Proposition 3.1 (Tax-Free In-Kind Redemption Shield)

Under Section 852(b)(6) of the Internal Revenue Code, a regulated investment company recognizes no taxable capital gain on the distribution of appreciated portfolio securities to an Authorized Participant in exchange for redeemed fund shares.

Remark 3.2 (Heartbeat Trades and Tax Shielding)

ETF sponsors actively utilize in-kind redemptions to purge low-basis, highly appreciated tax lots from the portfolio. When an AP redeems creation units, the custodian custom-selects the lowest cost-basis tax lots for delivery. The AP receives the low-basis shares without triggering corporate capital gains, raising the fund's average cost basis and shielding buy-and-hold ETF investors from taxable distributions.

4Market Footprint: Volatility Transmission and Liquidity Bifurcation

While the creation-redemption mechanism provides liquidity transformation, AP arbitrage activity couples the ETF secondary market to the underlying cash securities. In their empirical study, Itzhak Ben-David, Francesco Franzoni & Rabih Moussawi (2018), "Do ETFs Increase Volatility?", Journal of Finance, establish that ETF ownership increases the daily volatility of underlying constituent stocks.

Theorem 4.1 (Arbitrage Transmission Channel)

When high-frequency liquidity or sentiment shocks hit the secondary ETF market, AP creation-redemption arbitrage transmits non-fundamental volume shocks to the underlying constituent assets, increasing excess daily return volatility among high-ETF-ownership securities relative to low-ETF-ownership peers.

Lab 1 · ETF arbitrage threshold simulation

Simulates 250 trading days of an ETF with a 15 bps creation/redemption arbitrage band, verifying that AP transactions constrain premium and discount deviations within \(\pm 15.0\) bps.

"""Lab BK2-L02-1: ETF creation-redemption mechanism and arbitrage bounds.

Simulates the primary-secondary market interaction: Authorized Participants
arbitrage price discrepancies between the ETF secondary market price and the
intraday indicative value (IIV) of the underlying basket whenever the spread
exceeds creation/redemption transaction costs.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random


def simulate_etf_arbitrage(
    days: int,
    create_cost_bps: float,
    redeem_cost_bps: float,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Simulate secondary ETF market prices and AP creation/redemption arbitrage."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    create_cost = create_cost_bps / 10_000.0
    redeem_cost = redeem_cost_bps / 10_000.0

    nav = 100.0
    etf_price = 100.0

    creations = 0
    redemptions = 0
    premium_discounts: list[float] = []

    for _ in range(days):
        # Underlying basket return shock
        basket_ret = rng.gauss(0.0004, 0.012)
        nav *= 1.0 + basket_ret

        # Secondary market retail demand noise shock on ETF share price (proportional)
        demand_noise = rng.gauss(0.0, 0.0035)
        raw_etf_price = etf_price * (1.0 + basket_ret + demand_noise)

        premium = (raw_etf_price - nav) / nav

        # AP Arbitrage conditions
        if premium > create_cost:
            # ETF trades at a premium exceeding creation costs: AP creates shares
            etf_price = nav * (1.0 + create_cost)
            creations += 1
        elif premium < -redeem_cost:
            # ETF trades at a discount exceeding redemption costs: AP redeems shares
            etf_price = nav * (1.0 - redeem_cost)
            redemptions += 1
        else:
            # Inside no-arbitrage band: price floats without primary market creation
            etf_price = raw_etf_price

        realized_spread = (etf_price - nav) / nav
        premium_discounts.append(realized_spread)

    mean_spread_bps = (sum(premium_discounts) / len(premium_discounts)) * 10_000.0
    max_premium_bps = max(premium_discounts) * 10_000.0
    max_discount_bps = min(premium_discounts) * 10_000.0

    return {
        "days": float(days),
        "creations": float(creations),
        "redemptions": float(redemptions),
        "mean_spread_bps": mean_spread_bps,
        "max_premium_bps": max_premium_bps,
        "max_discount_bps": max_discount_bps,
    }


def main() -> None:
    days = 250
    create_bps = 15.0
    redeem_bps = 15.0

    res = simulate_etf_arbitrage(days, create_bps, redeem_bps, seed=42)

    print("ETF Creation/Redemption Arbitrage Simulator")
    print("------------------------------------------")
    print(f"Trading horizon:           {int(res['days'])} days")
    print(f"Creation threshold:        +{create_bps:.1f} bps")
    print(f"Redemption threshold:      -{redeem_bps:.1f} bps")
    print(f"Creation events triggered: {int(res['creations'])}")
    print(f"Redemption events:         {int(res['redemptions'])}")
    print(f"Mean premium/discount:     {res['mean_spread_bps']:.2f} bps")
    print(f"Max observed premium:      {res['max_premium_bps']:.2f} bps")
    print(f"Max observed discount:     {res['max_discount_bps']:.2f} bps")


main()

5Where the Canon Breaks

The standard model of ETF efficiency assumes that AP arbitrage is costless, frictionless, and instantaneous. In reality, the creation-redemption mechanism experiences documented breakdowns:

  • Illiquid Constituent Markets (Matrix Pricing Failures): When ETFs hold fixed-income instruments (e.g., high-yield corporate bonds or municipal debt), underlying bonds trade infrequently over-the-counter. During stress (such as March 2020), reported NAVs based on dealer pricing matrices lag real-world market clearing levels. Large ETF discounts reflect true secondary price discovery rather than a failed ETF arbitrage link.
  • AP Balance Sheet Balance and Capital Constraints: Post-2008 banking regulations (Basel III leverage ratios, Volcker Rule) restrict bank-affiliated APs from holding large inventory overnight. When balance sheet capacity is constrained, APs widen creation-redemption spreads, allowing ETF premiums and discounts to widen substantially.
  • Constituent Halts and Market Fractures: If trading is halted in key underlying equities (e.g., during market-wide circuit breakers or foreign exchange holidays), APs cannot execute the hedging leg of the creation-redemption arbitrage, causing ETF spreads to expand dramatically.

6Misconceptions

  1. "Every secondary market share of an ETF traded forces a transaction in the underlying stocks."

    Over 80% to 90% of daily ETF trading volume occurs entirely on the secondary exchange between buyers and sellers without triggering any primary creation or redemption units. The underlying stocks are traded only when APs actively arbitrage net imbalances across the primary creation-redemption boundary.

  2. "An ETF must always trade at its exact Net Asset Value at every moment during the trading day."

    Because Authorized Participants incur real-world transaction costs, exchange fees, and bid-ask spreads when assembling the constituent basket (\(c_{\text{create}}, c_{\text{redeem}}\)), the ETF price fluctuates freely within a no-arbitrage band around IIV. Only when the premium or discount breaches this band do APs intervene.

  3. "In-kind redemptions eliminate all market risk for Authorized Participants."

    APs face execution risk, leg-timing risk, and inventory hedging costs during the interval between secondary market ETF execution and the physical delivery or receipt of the underlying portfolio basket at end-of-day settlement.

7Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Arbitrage band width calculation

An international equity ETF has IIV of \$50.00. The basket creation cost is 25 bps, and the redemption cost is 30 bps. Calculate the dollar bounds of the no-arbitrage band.

Solution

Upper bound: \(P_{\text{max}} = \$50.00 \times (1 + 0.0025) = \$50.125\). Lower bound: \(P_{\text{min}} = \$50.00 \times (1 - 0.0030) = \$49.850\). The no-arbitrage price band is \([\$49.85, \$50.125]\).

M2 · AP creation profit calculation

An AP short sells 50,000 ETF shares at \$100.50 when IIV is \$100.00. Assembly cost for the creation unit is \$100.15 per share. Calculate the AP's total dollar profit upon settlement.

Solution

Per-share profit is \(\$100.50 - \$100.15 = \$0.35\). For 50,000 shares (one creation unit), total gross profit is \(50,000 \times \$0.35 = \$17,500.00\).

M3 · Secondary trading ratio

Over a quarter, an ETF has \$10 billion in total secondary exchange volume and \$800 million in gross primary creations and redemptions. Calculate the secondary-to-primary liquidity multiplier.

Solution

Secondary-to-primary multiplier is \(\$10.0\text{B} / \$0.8\text{B} = 12.5\). Only 8% of traded volume generated primary portfolio rebalancing; 92% settled cleanly in the secondary market without underlying asset turnover.

Proof

P1 · Creation band shrinkage under competitive AP entry

Let there be \(K\) identical Authorized Participants competing under Bertrand competition in basket assembly with marginal clearing cost \(c_0\). Prove that as \(K \to \infty\), the no-arbitrage band width approaches \(2 c_0\).

Solution

Under Bertrand competition with homogeneous costs, any bid-ask markup above marginal cost \(c_0\) is undercut by rival APs. The equilibrium creation cost paid by investors equals marginal assembly cost \(c_{\text{create}} = c_0\), and redemption cost equals \(c_{\text{redeem}} = c_0\). Total band width is \(\mathrm{IIV}(1 + c_0) - \mathrm{IIV}(1 - c_0) = 2 c_0 \mathrm{IIV}\), which is the minimum physical transaction bound.

P2 · Zero fund cash drag under in-kind transfers

Prove that an ETF maintaining \(w_c = 0\) via in-kind creation and redemption achieves zero cash drag (\(\Delta R_{\text{cash}} = 0\)) relative to its benchmark.

Solution

By Theorem 2.2 of Lesson 1, \(\Delta R_{\text{cash}} = w_c (R_{\text{risky}} - r_f)\). Because the ETF satisfies creations and redemptions in-kind with portfolio baskets rather than cash disbursements, it is not required to hold an uninvested liquidity cash buffer (\(w_c = 0\)). Substituting \(w_c = 0\) into the formula yields \(\Delta R_{\text{cash}} = 0 \cdot (R_{\text{risky}} - r_f) = 0\), proving zero cash drag.

P3 · Tax shield invariance to redemption volume

Prove that under IRC Section 852(b)(6), if all redemptions \(\theta \in (0, 1)\) are satisfied in-kind, the fund's realized capital gain tax liability is identically zero for all \(\theta\).

Solution

Under Section 852(b)(6), realized capital gain is defined on sales of securities for cash. In an in-kind distribution, securities are transferred directly to the redeeming shareholder without a cash sale: \(\text{Realized Gain}_{\text{cash}} = 0\). Because taxable gain distributions to remaining shareholders are proportional to realized cash gains, the taxable distribution is zero regardless of redemption volume \(\theta\).

Research

R1 · Volatility transmission mechanism

Explain how Authorized Participant arbitrage transmits retail sentiment shocks from the secondary ETF market into the underlying stock market, following Ben-David, Franzoni & Moussawi (2018).

Solution

When retail sentiment drives an ETF to a premium, APs short the ETF and purchase the underlying constituent basket. This simultaneous buying pressure raises constituent stock prices regardless of firm-level fundamental news. When sentiment reverses, redemption arbitrage forces APs to sell constituent stocks. Ben-David et al. (2018) demonstrate that this mechanical flow trading increases the non-fundamental volatility of stocks with high ETF institutional ownership.

R2 · Fixed-income ETF discounts in March 2020

During the March 2020 COVID shock, investment-grade corporate bond ETFs (e.g., LQD) traded at discounts exceeding 5% relative to reported NAV. Evaluate whether this reflected broken ETF arbitrage or price discovery in stale bond markets.

Solution

Empirical analysis confirms the discount reflected superior real-time price discovery in the ETF secondary market. Reported bond NAVs were computed from dealer quote matrices that had not traded in days during the liquidity freeze. As documented by researchers, secondary ETF market prices updated continuously, reflecting the true clearing price of credit risk; bond matrix prices subsequently caught down to the ETF discount over the following week.

R3 · Custom basket flexibility under Rule 6c-11

Under SEC Rule 6c-11 (the "ETF Rule"), fund sponsors are permitted to use customized creation and redemption baskets. Analyze how custom baskets affect AP arbitrage efficiency and tax optimization.

Solution

Rule 6c-11 allows sponsors to tailor creation and redemption baskets to exclude illiquid securities or selectively purge low-basis tax lots. Custom baskets lower AP hedging and inventory carrying costs, tightening secondary market bid-ask spreads while enhancing the fund's tax shielding capabilities, though it introduces governance requirements to prevent AP favoritism.
