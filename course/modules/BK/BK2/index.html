Asset Management Industry

The asset management industry mobilizes tens of trillions of dollars of global wealth through pooled investment vehicles. This module analyzes the institutional micro-structure and equilibrium economics of delegated money management. You will evaluate the open-end mutual fund and its liquidity-transformation vulnerabilities, trace the arbitrage mechanics of exchange-traded fund (ETF) creation and redemption, deconstruct asymmetric hedge fund compensation contracts with high-water marks, examine the multi-decade compounding of fee drag, analyze empirical flow–performance convexity, and derive the rational equilibrium of active management formulated by Berk and Green (2004). The unit concludes with an independent project building a dynamic fund-economics model.

  • Prerequisites: FC3 Market Efficiency: Evidence Primer, which provides the arithmetic of active management and foundational empirical asset pricing principles.
  • Project: Fund-economics model, modeling AUM, fees, flows, and capacity under Berk–Green dynamics. Graded by an autograder and a rubric.
  • Data: Numerical labs and project instances use exact analytical formulas and calibrated SIMULATED parameters. No external licensed downloads required.

35 h lessons · 15 h project (30% of the 50 module hours)

Objectives

By the end of BK2 you can:

  1. Formulate forward Net Asset Value (NAV) pricing under Rule 22c-1, quantify daily redemption liquidity demands, and calculate annual cash drag and dilution costs in open-end mutual funds.
  2. Derive the no-arbitrage bounds governing ETF secondary market pricing, trace the in-kind creation and redemption mechanism executed by Authorized Participants, and evaluate tax efficiency and cross-asset volatility transmission.
  3. Model asymmetric hedge fund compensation structures ("2 and 20"), high-water mark updating, and hurdle rates, and prove the option-like risk-shifting incentives faced by lagging managers.
  4. Compute multi-decade compounded fee drag across retail and institutional fee tiers, proving that modest annual basis points consume a substantial fraction of terminal wealth accumulation.
  5. Evaluate the empirical convexity of the flow–performance relationship, prove the Berk–Green zero-net-alpha equilibrium theorem, and demonstrate how skilled managers capture gross economic surplus through equilibrium fee extraction.
  6. Implement an object-oriented fund lifecycle simulator in Python, validating Bayesian skill updating, endogenous capacity sizing, and flow dynamics across multiple market environments.

Placement diagnostic

Eight questions: six on the prerequisite foundation (FC3, AP2, S1, M1), and two skill checks on what rungs 1 and 2 of the project ladder teach. The mastery threshold is 80 percent (at least 7 of 8 correct). To skip rungs 1 and 2, you must achieve 80 percent mastery and answer both skill checks correctly.

  1. Under Sharpe's arithmetic of active management (FC3), what is the aggregate relationship between active and passive investors before and after fees?

    Options

    Sharpe (1991): Active and passive investors in aggregate hold the market portfolio. Before costs, both earn the market return. Because active management incurs higher fees and transaction expenses, active dollars must net less than passive dollars.

  2. A portfolio has beta \(\beta = 1.20\), market risk premium \(\mathbb{E}[R_m - R_f] = 5.0\%\), and realized excess return \(R_p - R_f = 7.0\%\). What is Jensen's alpha in basis points?

    \(\alpha = 7.0\% - (1.20 \times 5.0\%) = 7.0\% - 6.0\% = 1.0\% = 100.0\text{ bps}\).

  3. In Gaussian Bayesian updating (S1), if prior belief is \(a \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) and an independent signal \(s \sim \mathcal{N}(a, \sigma_\epsilon^2)\) is observed, how is the posterior precision defined?

    Options

    Gaussian conjugate updating adds precisions linearly: posterior precision equals prior precision plus observation noise precision.

  4. An investment of \$10,000 grows at 8.0% annually for 10 years without fees, reaching \$21,589.25. If an annual fee of 1.0% reduces net return to 7.0%, terminal wealth is \$19,671.51. How much wealth is accumulated (in thousands of dollars)?

    \$10,000 \times 1.07^{10} = \$19,671.51 = 19.67\text{ thousand dollars}\).

  5. Why does the Joint Hypothesis Problem (FC3) complicate empirical tests of managerial skill?

    Options

    Fama (1970, 1991): Alpha cannot be evaluated without specifying expected return benchmark; an apparent nonzero alpha may reflect genuine skill or factor model misspecification.

  6. If asset returns are independent and identically distributed, how does the variance of cumulative multi-period returns scale with investment horizon \(T\)?

    Options

    Under i.i.d. returns, variances of independent random variables add linearly over time.

  7. Skill check (rung 1). A fund manager has expected gross skill \(\mu = 3.5\%\), annual management fee \(f = 1.5\%\), and scale cost parameter \(\lambda = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}\) per million of AUM. Under the Berk–Green condition \(\mu - \lambda q^* - f = 0\), what is equilibrium capacity AUM in millions of dollars?

    \(q^* = \frac{\mu - f}{\lambda} = \frac{0.035 - 0.015}{2.0 \times 10^{-5}} = \frac{0.020}{0.00002} = 1000.0\text{ million}\).

  8. Skill check (rung 2). Prior skill belief is \(\mu_0 = 2.0\%\) with precision \(\tau_0 = 2500\) (\(\sigma_0^2 = 0.0004\)). An informative return signal \(s = 5.0\%\) is observed with noise precision \(\tau_\epsilon = 1250\) (\(\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 0.0008\)). What is the updated posterior mean skill \(\mu_1\)?

    \(\mu_1 = \frac{\tau_0 \mu_0 + \tau_\epsilon s}{\tau_0 + \tau_\epsilon} = \frac{2500(0.02) + 1250(0.05)}{3750} = \frac{50 + 62.5}{3750} = \frac{112.5}{3750} = 0.030\) (3.0%).

The project ladder

The module culminates in the Fund-Economics Model project. Rungs 1 and 2 scaffold the mathematical calculations tested in the placement diagnostic. Mastering them unlocks the full independent modeling deliverable.

  1. Rung 1 · Worked example: Step-by-step walkthrough of single-period Berk-Green capacity determination, Bayesian skill updating, end-of-period asset evolution, and fee revenue calculation.
  2. Rung 2 · Completion problem: The same analytical framework with missing mathematical operations, verified immediately by an inline interactive test.
  3. Rung 3 · Independent project: Complete multi-period dynamic fund lifecycle simulator (fund_model.py) tested across 50 random seeds by pytest, accompanied by an executive economic memo.
  4. Rung 4 · Stretch extension: Multi-fund competitive industry simulation with investor search cost heterogeneity, endogenous fee competition, and capacity constraints across multiple asset classes.

Data status

No external licensed data is required for BK2. All inline labs and autograder test suites compute directly from exact closed-form equations and seeded SIMULATED parameters. Where empirical calibrations are utilized, they are explicitly labelled as SIMULATED.

Core reading

These seminal publications provide the empirical and theoretical foundations of the module: Berk and Green (2004) establish the rational equilibrium theory of active management, Sirri and Tufano (1998) establish the empirical convexity of mutual fund flows, and Chevalier and Ellison (1997) identify agency-driven risk-taking incentives in response to flow convexity. In addition, the empirical market effects of ETF indexing are examined following the findings of Itzhak Ben-David, Francesco Franzoni & Rabih Moussawi (2018), "Do ETFs Increase Volatility?", The Journal of Finance.
