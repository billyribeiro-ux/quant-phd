The Deposit Franchise and Deposit Beta

  • Uses: net interest income sensitivity from L05, financial intermediary from FC1
  • Introduces: deposit franchise, deposit beta
  • Ttheorem (deductive mathematical result)
  • Aempirically replicated finding
  • Bpublished empirical finding, not fully settled

  • Dpractitioner lore

Retail deposits represent the core funding foundation of commercial banking. Because banks operate geographic branch networks, provide payment and checking infrastructure, and benefit from depositor inertia, retail deposit interest rates adjust sluggishly to central bank policy rate hikes. This incomplete pass-through defines the deposit beta. The economic value generated by paying below-market funding rates is the deposit franchise. In stable markets, the deposit franchise acts as a natural economic hedge against rising interest rates. However, as recent banking crises demonstrate, when rate hikes are large or uninsured depositors run, deposit betas surge, destroying franchise stability. This lesson formalizes deposit beta mechanics and evaluates the empirical evidence.

1Deposit Beta and the Deposits Channel

When the central bank adjusts policy rates, commercial banks do not transmit the rate change one-for-one to retail customer deposit accounts.

Definition 1.1 (deposit beta)

The deposit beta (\(\beta\)) is the sensitivity or pass-through elasticity of changes in deposit interest rates (\(\Delta r_D\)) to changes in wholesale benchmark market interest rates (\(\Delta r\)):

\[ \beta = \frac{\Delta r_D}{\Delta r} \in [0, 1] \]

In quantitative banking models, \(\beta\) is parameterized as a decimal or in basis points (\(\beta_{\text{bp}} \in [0, 10000]\)). A beta of 0 represents completely sticky zero-pass-through deposits; a beta of 1 represents perfect 100% pass-through.

Definition 1.2 (deposit franchise)

The deposit franchise is the institutional capacity of a commercial bank to attract and retain non-maturity retail deposits at below-market interest rates, generating a sustainable funding cost advantage over wholesale money markets .

Proposition 1.3 (linearity of NII sensitivity in deposit beta)

Let a bank hold floating earning assets \(A\) that reprice fully with a parallel market shock \(\Delta r\), wholesale liabilities \(L_{\text{wh}}\) that reprice fully, and non-maturity deposits \(D\) that reprice with deposit beta \(\beta\). Over a 12-month horizon, the change in Net Interest Income is strictly linear in \(\beta\):

\[ \Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta) = \left[(A - L_{\text{wh}}) - \beta D\right] \Delta r \]

Furthermore, the net benefit generated by the deposit franchise relative to full pass-through (\(\beta = 1\)) is:

\[ \mathrm{Benefit}_{\text{franchise}} = (1 - \beta) \cdot D \cdot \Delta r \]
Proof

Under a parallel shift \(\Delta r\), gross interest income increases by \(\Delta \mathrm{II} = A \cdot \Delta r\). Wholesale borrowing expense increases by \(\Delta \mathrm{IE}_{\text{wh}} = L_{\text{wh}} \cdot \Delta r\). Deposit interest expense increases by \(\Delta \mathrm{IE}_D = D \cdot \Delta r_D = D \cdot (\beta \Delta r) = \beta D \Delta r\). Net interest income change is \(\Delta \mathrm{NII} = \Delta \mathrm{II} - \Delta \mathrm{IE}_{\text{wh}} - \Delta \mathrm{IE}_D = A \Delta r - L_{\text{wh}} \Delta r - \beta D \Delta r = [(A - L_{\text{wh}}) - \beta D] \Delta r\). Differentiating with respect to \(\beta\) yields \(\frac{\partial (\Delta \mathrm{NII})}{\partial \beta} = -D \Delta r\), which is constant, establishing exact linearity. If deposits repriced fully (\(\beta = 1\)), \(\Delta \mathrm{NII}(1) = (A - L_{\text{wh}} - D) \Delta r\). Subtracting this baseline from \(\Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta)\) gives \([(A - L_{\text{wh}}) - \beta D - (A - L_{\text{wh}} - D)] \Delta r = (1 - \beta) D \Delta r\). \(\blacksquare\)

2The Deposits Channel and Market Power

Drechsler, Savov & Schnabl (2017) demonstrate empirically that banks exercise market power over retail deposits. In concentrated local banking markets where a few institutions dominate branch networks, banks set deposit rates substantially below Fed funds rates, resulting in low empirical betas (\(\beta \approx 0.20\) to \(0.40\)).

Example 1.4 (evaluating NII across deposit betas)

A bank holds \$1,000,000 in floating assets funded by \$800,000 in core deposits. Under a +200 bp (+0.02) rate hike: If \(\beta = 0.00\), \(\Delta \mathrm{NII} = \$1\text{M} \times 0.02 - 0 = +\$20,000\). If \(\beta = 0.50\), \(\Delta \mathrm{NII} = \$20,000 - \$800\text{k} \times 0.50 \times 0.02 = \$20,000 - \$8,000 = +\$12,000\). If \(\beta = 1.00\), \(\Delta \mathrm{NII} = \$20,000 - \$800\text{k} \times 1.00 \times 0.02 = \$20,000 - \$16,000 = +\$4,000\). At \(\beta = 0.50\), the NII change of \$12,000 is the exact midpoint between \$20,000 and \$4,000, and the franchise benefit is \((1 - 0.50) \times \$800\text{k} \times 0.02 = \$8,000\).

Lab 6 · Deposit Beta Linearity and Franchise Hedge

Verifying that on a SIMULATED balance sheet of 100,000,000 cents in floating assets and 80,000,000 cents in deposits, NII change is strictly linear in deposit beta across [0.00, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00], and beta=0.50 equals the exact midpoint of beta=0.00 and beta=1.00.

"""Lab BK1-L06-1: Deposit franchise and deposit beta pass-through.

Deposit beta measures the pass-through of market interest rate changes to deposit rates:
    beta = Delta r_D / Delta r,  where beta in [0, 1]

Drechsler, Savov & Schnabl (2017, 2021) show that market power enables banks to pay
sticky below-market deposit rates (beta < 1), generating a deposit franchise that acts
as a hedge against rising rates. However, as Jiang, Matvos, Piskorski & Seru (2024) show,
run risk and deposit disintermediation can rapidly erode this hedge.

This lab simulates:
1. Floating assets of 100,000,000 cents ($1,000,000) repricing fully with market rates.
2. Core non-maturity deposits of 80,000,000 cents ($800,000) with deposit beta.
3. Market rate shock Delta r = +200 bp.
4. Net Interest Income (NII) change across betas [0.00, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00].
5. Exact linearity check: Delta NII at beta=0.50 is the midpoint between beta=0.00 and beta=1.00.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

ASSETS_CENTS = 100_000_000  # $1,000,000 floating-rate assets
DEPOSITS_CENTS = 80_000_000  # $800,000 core deposits
SHOCK_BP = 200  # +200 bp rate rise


def compute_nii_change(assets_cents: int, deposits_cents: int, shock_bp: int, beta_bp: int) -> int:
    """Calculate annual NII change in cents for given deposit beta in bp."""
    shock_frac = Fraction(shock_bp, 10_000)
    beta_frac = Fraction(beta_bp, 10_000)

    asset_income_change = Fraction(assets_cents, 1) * shock_frac
    dep_expense_change = Fraction(deposits_cents, 1) * (beta_frac * shock_frac)

    delta_nii = asset_income_change - dep_expense_change
    # Round half away from zero
    if delta_nii >= 0:
        return (delta_nii.numerator * 2 + delta_nii.denominator) // (delta_nii.denominator * 2)
    else:
        return -((-delta_nii.numerator * 2 + delta_nii.denominator) // (delta_nii.denominator * 2))


def main() -> None:
    print("A. Deposit Beta and 1-Year NII Sensitivity (+200 bp shock, SIMULATED, in cents)")
    betas_bp = [0, 2500, 5000, 7500, 10_000]

    results: dict[int, int] = {}
    for b_bp in betas_bp:
        delta_nii = compute_nii_change(ASSETS_CENTS, DEPOSITS_CENTS, SHOCK_BP, b_bp)
        results[b_bp] = delta_nii
        beta_float = b_bp / 10_000
        # Deposit franchise margin gain: (1 - beta) * shock * deposits
        franchise_gain = Fraction(DEPOSITS_CENTS, 1) * Fraction(10_000 - b_bp, 10_000) * Fraction(SHOCK_BP, 10_000)
        print(
            f"Beta = {beta_float:4.2f} ({b_bp:5d} bp) | "
            f"Delta NII: {delta_nii:+9d} cents | Franchise Benefit: {int(franchise_gain):8d} cents"
        )

    print()
    print("B. Linearity and Invariant Checks")
    nii_0 = results[0]
    nii_50 = results[5000]
    nii_100 = results[10_000]

    print(f"NII change at beta 0.50 equals midpoint of beta 0 and 1: {nii_50 == (nii_0 + nii_100) // 2}")
    print(f"Monotonicity: Higher deposit beta strictly reduces NII gain: {nii_0 > nii_50 > nii_100}")
    print(f"Franchise hedge positive for beta < 1: {results[5000] > results[10_000]}")


main()

The lab validates Proposition 1.3. For a +200 bp rate shock, NII change drops linearly from +2,000,000 cents at \(\beta = 0.00\) to +400,000 cents at \(\beta = 1.00\). 3Franchise Fragility and Uninsured Run Risk

While the deposit franchise provides a powerful interest rate hedge under moderate rate adjustments, empirical research by Jiang, Matvos, Piskorski & Seru (2024) reveals that the franchise is highly fragile during rapid tightening cycles. When market rates rise by 400 to 500 bp, the spread between zero-yielding deposits and Treasury bills widens dramatically, encouraging depositors to seek money market alternatives ("cash sorting"). If uninsured deposits represent a large fraction of liabilities and the bank holds unhedged mark-to-market EVE losses, depositor coordination breaks down into panic runs, causing empirical deposit betas to jump to 1.0 or forcing emergency borrowing at penalty discount window rates.

Where the Canon Breaks

The classical deposit beta model relies on simplifying assumptions that fail during stress periods:

  • Beta acceleration and non-linearity: Empirical deposit betas are not constant across rate levels. During initial rate hikes (0–100 bp), deposit betas are low (\(\beta \approx 0.10\)). As rate hikes continue beyond 300 bp, customer awareness increases, triggering non-linear beta acceleration (\(\beta \to 0.70+\)).
  • Deposit disintermediation: The model assumes deposit volume \(D\) is invariant to the deposit rate. When banks pay 0.5% while Treasury bills yield 5.0%, depositors withdraw balances into money market mutual funds, shrinking total core deposits.
  • Disparities between insured and uninsured deposits: Retail deposits under the FDIC \$250,000 insurance cap exhibit sticky behavior and low betas. Uninsured institutional and corporate deposits exhibit high betas and run rapidly during stress.
  • Asymmetry in rate cuts versus hikes: Banks exhibit downward sticky pricing: when policy rates drop, banks cut deposit rates quickly; when policy rates rise, banks raise deposit rates sluggishly.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Deposit beta is a fixed institutional parameter determined by bank management.”

    While bank management sets posted interest rates, empirical deposit beta is determined by market competition, depositor sophistication, and alternative yields. If fintech platforms or rival institutions offer high-yield savings accounts, a bank that refuses to raise rates suffers massive deposit outflows, forcing beta upward.

  2. “The deposit franchise guarantees that commercial banks never need to hedge duration risk.”

    Drechsler et al. (2021) demonstrate that the deposit franchise acts as a hedge against rising rates. However, as Jiang et al. (2024) prove, this hedge holds only as long as depositors remain at the bank. If mark-to-market duration losses exceed capital and uninsured depositors run, the deposit franchise vanishes instantaneously, exposing the unhedged asset duration.

  3. “Deposit betas are identical across all deposit categories.”

    Deposit betas vary radically by product type. Non-interest checking accounts have \(\beta \approx 0.05\); consumer savings accounts have \(\beta \approx 0.25\); wholesale commercial accounts and certificates of deposit (CDs) have \(\beta \approx 0.80\) to \(1.00\).

5Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Deposit rate calculation from beta

A bank's base deposit rate is 0.50% (50 bp). Its empirical deposit beta is \(\beta = 0.35\). If the Federal Reserve increases policy rates by +200 bp (+2.00%), what is the new deposit rate in percent?

Solution

The deposit rate change is \(\Delta r_D = \beta \cdot \Delta r = 0.35 \times 2.00\% = 0.70\%\) (70 bp). The new deposit rate is \(r_D = 0.50\% + 0.70\% = 1.20\%\) (120 bp).

M2 · Franchise benefit dollar calculation

A bank holds \$500,000,000 in retail core deposits with a deposit beta of 0.40. Under a +150 bp (+0.015) market interest rate increase, calculate the annual dollar benefit generated by the deposit franchise relative to 100% pass-through.

Solution

By Proposition 1.3, \(\mathrm{Benefit}_{\text{franchise}} = (1 - \beta) \cdot D \cdot \Delta r = (1 - 0.40) \times \$500,000,000 \times 0.0150 = 0.60 \times \$500\text{M} \times 0.0150 = \$4,500,000\).

M3 · Break-even deposit beta

A bank holds \$300M in floating assets and \$200M in core deposits. Under a rate hike, find the critical deposit beta \(\beta^*\) that makes Net Interest Income sensitivity identically zero (\(\Delta \mathrm{NII} = 0\)).

Solution

From Proposition 1.3 with \(L_{\text{wh}} = 0\), \(\Delta \mathrm{NII} = (A - \beta D) \Delta r = 0 \iff A = \beta D \iff \beta^* = \frac{A}{D} = \frac{300}{200} = 1.50\). Because \(\beta \le 1.0\), the bank's floating assets exceed its deposits, meaning \(\Delta \mathrm{NII} > 0\) for all feasible betas \(\beta \in [0, 1]\).

Proof

P1 · Monotonicity of NII sensitivity in deposit beta

Prove that for any positive rate shock \(\Delta r > 0\) and positive core deposit volume \(D > 0\), 12-month Net Interest Income sensitivity \(\Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta)\) is strictly decreasing in deposit beta \(\beta\).

Solution

From Proposition 1.3, \(\Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta) = [(A - L_{\text{wh}}) - \beta D] \Delta r\). Differentiating with respect to \(\beta\): \(\frac{\partial (\Delta \mathrm{NII})}{\partial \beta} = -D \Delta r\). Since by hypothesis \(D > 0\) and \(\Delta r > 0\), the product \(D \Delta r > 0\), which implies \(\frac{\partial (\Delta \mathrm{NII})}{\partial \beta} < 0\). This proves that NII sensitivity is strictly decreasing across \(\beta \in [0, 1]\).

P2 · Linear midpoint theorem for deposit beta

Prove that for any \(\beta_1, \beta_2 \in [0, 1]\), the Net Interest Income change at the midpoint \(\bar{\beta} = \frac{\beta_1 + \beta_2}{2}\) equals the arithmetic average of the NII changes: \(\Delta \mathrm{NII}(\bar{\beta}) = \frac{1}{2}[\Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta_1) + \Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta_2)]\).

Solution

From Proposition 1.3, \(\Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta)\) is an affine function of \(\beta\): \(\Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta) = C_0 - \beta C_1\), where \(C_0 = (A - L_{\text{wh}})\Delta r\) and \(C_1 = D \Delta r\). Evaluating at \(\bar{\beta}\): \(\Delta \mathrm{NII}(\bar{\beta}) = C_0 - \frac{\beta_1 + \beta_2}{2} C_1 = \frac{1}{2}(C_0 - \beta_1 C_1) + \frac{1}{2}(C_0 - \beta_2 C_1) = \frac{1}{2}[\Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta_1) + \Delta \mathrm{NII}(\beta_2)]\). This confirms exact linearity without higher-order curvature.

P3 · Present value of deposit franchise asset

Assume constant deposit balances \(D\), constant market rate \(r\), constant beta \(\beta\), and continuous discount rate \(r\). Prove that the capitalized present value of the deposit franchise over an infinite horizon is \(V_{\text{franchise}} = (1 - \beta) D\).

Solution

The continuous cash flow generated by the deposit franchise per period is \((r - r_D) D = (r - \beta r) D = (1 - \beta) r D\). The capitalized present value discounted at market rate \(r\) over an infinite horizon is \(V = \int_0^\infty (1 - \beta) r D e^{-rt}\,dt = (1 - \beta) r D \int_0^\infty e^{-rt}\,dt = (1 - \beta) r D \left[\frac{1}{r}\right] = (1 - \beta) D\). The franchise value equals the non-pass-through share of total deposits.

Research

R1 · Drechsler et al. (2017) deposits channel mechanism

Explain the macroeconomic transmission mechanism identified by Drechsler, Savov & Schnabl (2017) through which bank deposit market power contracts bank lending when the Federal Reserve raises policy rates.

Solution

When the Fed raises policy rates, banks with local market power widen deposit spreads by keeping deposit rates low. Depositors respond to this widened spread by withdrawing funds to invest in non-bank liquid assets (Treasury bills). This deposit outflow drains bank funding reserves, forcing banks to contract commercial and mortgage lending, demonstrating how deposit market power transmits monetary policy into real credit contraction.

R2 · SVB and uninsured depositor concentration

How did Silicon Valley Bank's extreme concentration of uninsured commercial deposits negate the deposit franchise hedge described in Drechsler et al. (2021)?

Solution

Drechsler et al.'s deposit franchise model relies on retail depositor fragmentation, geographical branch lock-in, and FDIC deposit insurance guarantees. Over 90% of SVB's deposits were corporate accounts well above the $250,000 FDIC limit, concentrated among venture capital startups communicating via social media. When SVB announced unhedged securities losses, uninsured depositors faced zero switching frictions and withdrew over \$42 billion in a single day, collapsing the franchise hedge.

R3 · Digital banking and beta acceleration

How has the rise of mobile banking, fintech cash-management apps, and online Treasury brokers altered modern empirical deposit betas compared to the 1990s and 2000s?

Solution

In past cycles, transferring deposits required visiting physical branches, creating high friction and low betas. Today, digital banking eliminates physical friction, allowing instant electronic transfers to high-yield online banks or money market funds. This financial technology acceleration has compressed depositor inertia, driving empirical deposit betas higher and accelerating the speed of deposit disintermediation during rate hikes.
