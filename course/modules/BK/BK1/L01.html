Why Banks Exist: Delegated Monitoring

  • Uses: financial intermediary from FC1, balance sheet from AC1
  • Introduces: delegated monitoring
  • Ttheorem (deductive mathematical result)
  • Aempirically replicated finding
  • Bpublished empirical finding, not fully settled
  • Cworking paper, practitioner research or single sample
  • Dpractitioner lore
  • Xrefuted claim

In direct lending markets, individual savers who finance entrepreneurial projects face asymmetric information regarding borrower project returns. If verifying project outcomes requires costly state auditing, direct market financing forces each saver to incur auditing costs individually, duplicating deadweight verification expenses. A commercial bank resolves this collective action problem by serving as a delegated monitor. By holding a diversified portfolio of non-synchronized loans, the intermediary guarantees promised returns to deposit holders with near certainty, eliminating redundant auditing costs. This lesson proves Diamond's classical theorem of financial intermediation.

1Information Asymmetry and Costly State Verification

Consider an economy with risk-neutral entrepreneurs and risk-neutral savers. Each entrepreneur possesses an investment project requiring 1 unit of capital at date 0 that yields an unobservable stochastic return \(\tilde{y}\) at date 1. The realized return is private information to the borrower unless an outside investor expends a non-monetary or real auditing cost \(K > 0\). Townsend's costly state verification framework establishes that the optimal bilateral financial contract is a standard debt contract: the borrower promises a fixed repayment \(R\). If the borrower reports realization \(y \ge R\), repayment \(R\) is accepted without auditing. If the borrower defaults, reporting \(y < R\), the lender audits the borrower at cost \(K\) and appropriates the verified residual assets.

Definition 1.1 (delegated monitoring)

Delegated monitoring is the institutional arrangement wherein a financial intermediary contracts directly with multiple primary lenders (depositors) to collect capital and independently monitor borrowing entrepreneurs on their collective behalf, replacing redundant bilateral audits with a centralized monitoring agent .

Assumption 1.2 (bilateral lending economy)

Let an economy contain \(M\) identical risk-neutral savers, each endowed with \(1/M\) units of capital, and 1 entrepreneur requiring 1 unit of total capital. Project return \(\tilde{y}\) is distributed over \([0, Y]\) with cumulative distribution function \(F(y)\) and probability density \(f(y)\). Default occurs whenever \(y < R\). Monitoring by any investor costs \(K > 0\) in real resources.

Proposition 1.3 (cost duplication in direct lending)

Under Assumption 1.2, if \(M\) individual savers each finance a share \(1/M\) of the project and cannot observe one another's audit reports, the expected deadweight monitoring cost under direct financing is:

\[ C_{\text{direct}} = M \cdot K \cdot F(R) \]

The total monitoring cost scales linearly with the number of creditors \(M\).

Proof

If each saver independently contracts with the entrepreneur, when default occurs (\(y < R\)), every saver must inspect the project to verify that the entrepreneur is not misrepresenting returns. Because private monitoring is non-contractible and cannot be credibly shared without verification, each of the \(M\) creditors expends cost \(K\). The probability of default is \(F(R) = \int_0^R f(y)\,dy\). Taking the expectation over all states, total expected audit expense is \(\sum_{i=1}^M K \cdot F(R) = M \cdot K \cdot F(R)\). As \(M \to \infty\), direct verification costs grow without bound. \(\blacksquare\)

2The Intermediated Economy and Diamond's Model

Now consider an intermediary that pools capital from \(M\) savers and invests across \(N\) independent entrepreneurial projects. Let borrower \(j \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) manage project \(j\) with independent identically distributed return \(\tilde{y}_j\). The bank audits borrower \(j\) at cost \(K\) whenever borrower \(j\) defaults on promised payment \(R\). The total loan payoff collected by the bank at date 1 is:

\[ \tilde{Z}_N = \sum_{j=1}^N \min(\tilde{y}_j, R) - \sum_{j=1}^N K \cdot \indic_{\{\tilde{y}_j < R\}} \]
Theorem 1.4 (Diamond's diversification limit)

Assume project cash flows \(\tilde{y}_j\) are independent and identically distributed with finite mean \(\mu = \E[\min(\tilde{y}_j, R)]\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2 < \infty\), and deposit contracts promise a non-contingent return \(r_D\) per loan. If \(\mu - K \cdot F(R) > r_D\), then as the number of financed loans \(N \to \infty\), the intermediary's probability of defaulting to depositors converges to zero:

\[ \lim_{N \to \infty} \Prob\left(\frac{\tilde{Z}_N}{N} < r_D\right) = 0 \]

The expected delegated agency cost per dollar of assets funded converges to zero.

Proof

Let \(w_j = \min(\tilde{y}_j, R) - K \cdot \indic_{\{\tilde{y}_j < R\}}\). Because \(\tilde{y}_j\) are i.i.d., the random variables \(w_j\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\bar{w} = \E[w_j] = \mu - K \cdot F(R)\) and variance \(\sigma_w^2 = \Var(w_j) < \infty\). The portfolio payoff per loan is \(\bar{Z}_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N w_j\). By the weak law of large numbers, for any \(\epsilon > 0\), \(\lim_{N \to \infty} \Prob(|\bar{Z}_N - \bar{w}| \ge \epsilon) = 0\). Since by hypothesis \(\bar{w} > r_D\), define \(\epsilon = \bar{w} - r_D > 0\). Then:

\[ \Prob(\bar{Z}_N < r_D) = \Prob(\bar{w} - \bar{Z}_N > \bar{w} - r_D) \le \Prob(|\bar{Z}_N - \bar{w}| \ge \epsilon) \le \frac{\sigma_w^2}{N \epsilon^2} \]

By Chebyshev's inequality, as \(N \to \infty\), \(\frac{\sigma_w^2}{N \epsilon^2} \to 0\), establishing that the default probability of the bank vanishes. Consequently, depositors do not need to audit the bank, and total monitoring costs are bounded by \(N \cdot K \cdot F(R)\), representing an agency cost per loan of \(K \cdot F(R)\) rather than \(M \cdot K \cdot F(R)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.5 (direct versus delegated auditing)

Consider \(N = 100\) borrowers and \(M = 50\) savers per loan (5,000 total savers). Let monitoring cost be \(K = \$200\) per loan with default probability \(F(R) = 0.05\). Under direct lending, each defaulting borrower is audited by 50 savers, producing total expected monitoring costs of \(100 \times 0.05 \times 50 \times \$200 = \$50,000\). Under bank intermediation, the bank conducts 1 audit per defaulting borrower, producing total monitoring costs of \(100 \times 0.05 \times 1 \times \$200 = \$1,000\). The institutional delegation saves \$49,000 in transaction costs, a 50-fold reduction.

Lab 1 · Delegated Monitoring and Portfolio Scaling

Verifying that total monitoring costs under intermediation scale as N * K while direct financing scales as M * N * K, generating a linear cost saving factor equal to M.

"""Lab BK1-L01-1: Delegated monitoring and credit diversification arithmetic.

Diamond (1984) shows that financial intermediaries exist because delegating monitoring
to a single bank avoids costly duplication by individual savers (M savers * K monitoring cost).
Diversifying across N independent borrowers reduces the bank's default risk and delegated agency costs.

This lab simulates:
1. Direct lending: M savers each lend to N borrowers, monitoring cost = M * N * K.
2. Intermediated banking: Bank monitors N borrowers (cost = N * K), funded by M savers who hold debt.
3. Agency costs and diversification as N increases.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

# Illustrative model parameters in cents
BORROWER_LOAN_CENTS = 1_000_000  # $10,000 loan per borrower
MONITOR_COST_CENTS = 20_000  # $200 monitoring cost per borrower (K)
NUM_SAVERS = 50  # M savers
RETURN_SUCCESS_BP = 800  # 8.00% gross loan return on success
DEPOSIT_RATE_BP = 400  # 4.00% promised deposit return
DEFAULT_PROB_BP = 500  # 5.00% default probability per borrower


def direct_monitoring_cost(num_savers: int, num_borrowers: int, cost_per_borrower: int) -> int:
    """Total monitoring cost in cents if every saver monitors every borrower directly."""
    return num_savers * num_borrowers * cost_per_borrower


def bank_monitoring_cost(num_borrowers: int, cost_per_borrower: int) -> int:
    """Total monitoring cost in cents when delegated to a single intermediary."""
    return num_borrowers * cost_per_borrower


def main() -> None:
    print("A. Monitoring Cost Comparison: Direct vs Delegated (SIMULATED, in cents)")
    borrower_counts = [1, 5, 20, 100]

    for n in borrower_counts:
        direct_cost = direct_monitoring_cost(NUM_SAVERS, n, MONITOR_COST_CENTS)
        bank_cost = bank_monitoring_cost(n, MONITOR_COST_CENTS)
        saving = direct_cost - bank_cost
        saving_ratio = Fraction(direct_cost, bank_cost)
        print(
            f"N = {n:3d} borrowers | Direct: {direct_cost:10d} cents | "
            f"Bank: {bank_cost:8d} cents | Saving: {saving:10d} cents (factor {int(saving_ratio)}x)"
        )

    print()
    print("B. Unit Monitoring Cost per Dollar of Assets Funded")
    for n in borrower_counts:
        total_assets = n * BORROWER_LOAN_CENTS
        bank_cost = bank_monitoring_cost(n, MONITOR_COST_CENTS)
        cost_bp = Fraction(bank_cost * 10_000, total_assets)
        print(f"N = {n:3d} | Total Assets: {total_assets:11d} cents | Unit Cost: {int(cost_bp):4d} bp")

    print()
    print("C. Delegated Monitoring Efficiency Checks")
    n_test = 20
    d_cost = direct_monitoring_cost(NUM_SAVERS, n_test, MONITOR_COST_CENTS)
    b_cost = bank_monitoring_cost(n_test, MONITOR_COST_CENTS)
    print(f"Direct cost equals M * Bank cost: {d_cost == NUM_SAVERS * b_cost}")
    print(f"Delegation cost saving positive for M > 1: {d_cost > b_cost}")


main()

Remark 1.6 (monitoring the monitor)

Who monitors the bank? In Diamond's (1984) framework, diversification provides the solution. Because the portfolio variance of returns declines as \(1/N\), the bank's non-contingent debt promise (deposits) becomes virtually risk-free without external monitoring. If returns were perfectly correlated, diversification would fail, and depositors would be forced to audit the bank, recreating the multi-creditor duplication problem.

3Balance Sheet Articulation and the Bank Equation

Under the accounting principles of AC1, the balance sheet of an intermediary articulates assets, liabilities, and equity capital:

\[ \text{Assets} = \text{Loans} + \text{Liquid Reserves} = \text{Deposits} + \text{Wholesale Debt} + \text{Equity} \]

The bank's asset portfolio consists of non-marketable, monitored commercial loans that cannot trade frictionlessly in open markets due to adverse selection. By transforming non-liquid idiosyncratic loan assets into liquid demand deposits, the bank creates value for both savers and borrowers.

Where the Canon Breaks

Diamond's delegated monitoring framework relies on specific structural assumptions. In real banking systems, these idealized assumptions break in several key dimensions:

  • Systemic and correlated credit shocks: The proof of Theorem 1.4 assumes i.i.d. project returns. If loans share exposure to macroeconomic downturns or property market collapses, portfolio variance does not vanish as \(1/N\). Correlated defaults cause the bank to fail, forcing depositors to incur bankruptcy and liquidation expenses.
  • Bank manager moral hazard: Delegating monitoring to bank management introduces agency conflicts between bank executives and dispersed depositors. Without regulatory supervision or capital requirements, managers may originate high-risk loans, conceal non-performing assets, or extract private rents.
  • Liquidity transformation and run risk: Diamond's 1984 model examines date 0 investment and date 1 resolution without interim withdrawal needs. Diamond and Dybvig (1983) demonstrate that funding illiquid loans with demandable debt creates coordination failures and self-fulfilling panic runs.
  • Imperfect monitoring technology: In practice, monitoring does not fully eliminate borrower default risk or opportunistic asset substitution. Borrowers may pledge collateral to multiple lenders or misstate cash flows despite bank audits.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Banks exist primarily as payment processors and vault keepers.”

    While clearing payments is an operational bank service, economic theory establishes that banks exist to resolve asymmetric information. Because primary borrowers have private information about project feasibility and effort, direct lenders face severe adverse selection and verification costs. The bank's economic purpose is delegated monitoring: conducting screening and credit surveillance more efficiently than dispersed individual investors .

  2. “Diversification eliminates all risks from a commercial bank's balance sheet.”

    Theorem 1.4 proves that diversification eliminates idiosyncratic credit default risk under the law of large numbers. It does not eliminate systemic macroeconomic shocks that simultaneously affect all borrowers. Furthermore, diversification of credit assets does nothing to eliminate maturity mismatch and interest rate risk in the banking book, which arise from funding long assets with short liabilities.

  3. “Delegated monitoring requires depositors to audit the bank's books regularly.”

    In Diamond's model, depositors do not monitor the bank. Because the diversified portfolio provides stable cash flows, the bank issues non-contingent debt contracts (deposits) that pay a fixed return. As long as the bank does not default, depositors receive their promised return without conducting costly state audits of the bank's internal accounts.

5Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Direct auditing cost computation

A syndicate of \(M = 20\) direct lenders finances a corporate loan. Borrower default occurs with probability \(F(R) = 0.10\). If auditing costs \(K = \$500\) per lender upon default, compute the expected total monitoring cost in dollars.

Solution

Under Proposition 1.3, each lender independently audits the borrower upon default. The expected cost is \(C_{\text{direct}} = M \cdot K \cdot F(R) = 20 \times \$500 \times 0.10 = \$1,000\). If an intermediary performed the audit on their collective behalf, the cost would be \(1 \times \$500 \times 0.10 = \$50\), saving \$950.

M2 · Portfolio scaling and deadweight savings

An intermediary finances \(N = 50\) independent borrowers, each funded by \(M = 100\) savers. Let monitoring cost be \(K = \$300\) and default probability be \(0.08\). Compute the total dollar savings generated by delegated monitoring.

Solution

Direct financing expected cost is \(N \cdot M \cdot K \cdot F(R) = 50 \times 100 \times \$300 \times 0.08 = \$120,000\). Bank delegated monitoring cost is \(N \cdot K \cdot F(R) = 50 \times 1 \times \$300 \times 0.08 = \$1,200\). The total deadweight cost saving is \(\$120,000 - \$1,200 = \$118,800\), representing a 99% cost reduction.

M3 · Unit monitoring cost in basis points

A bank originates 200 commercial loans of $50,000 each. The bank incurs an annual monitoring cost of $250 per loan. Calculate the bank's unit monitoring expense as a percentage and in basis points of total loan volume.

Solution

Total loan volume is \(200 \times \$50,000 = \$10,000,000\). Total monitoring expense is \(200 \times \$250 = \$50,000\). Unit monitoring cost is \(\frac{\$50,000}{\$10,000,000} = 0.0050 = 0.50\%\) or exactly 50 basis points.

Proof

P1 · Linear scaling of direct monitoring

Prove that under Assumption 1.2, if each of \(M\) creditors must verify loan returns independently, the ratio of direct monitoring cost to delegated monitoring cost is identically equal to \(M\).

Solution

Let default probability be \(P_D = F(R)\) and unit audit cost be \(K\). In direct lending, each creditor audits upon default, yielding expected total expenditure \(C_{\text{dir}} = M \cdot K \cdot P_D\). Under delegated monitoring, the single bank conducts exactly one audit upon default, yielding expected expenditure \(C_{\text{bank}} = 1 \cdot K \cdot P_D\). The ratio is \(\frac{C_{\text{dir}}}{C_{\text{bank}}} = \frac{M \cdot K \cdot P_D}{K \cdot P_D} = M\). The cost ratio scales strictly with creditor fragmentation.

P2 · Chebyshev bound on bank default

Using Chebyshev's inequality, prove that if loan payoffs \(w_j\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\bar{w} = 1.08\) and variance \(\sigma_w^2 = 0.04\), the probability that portfolio return \(\bar{Z}_N\) falls below promised deposit return \(r_D = 1.04\) is bounded by \(\frac{25}{N}\).

Solution

Let \(\epsilon = \bar{w} - r_D = 1.08 - 1.04 = 0.04\). The default probability is \(\Prob(\bar{Z}_N < 1.04) = \Prob(\bar{w} - \bar{Z}_N > 0.04) \le \Prob(|\bar{Z}_N - \bar{w}| \ge 0.04)\). By Chebyshev's inequality, \(\Prob(|\bar{Z}_N - \bar{w}| \ge \epsilon) \le \frac{\Var(\bar{Z}_N)}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma_w^2}{N \epsilon^2}\). Substituting \(\sigma_w^2 = 0.04\) and \(\epsilon^2 = (0.04)^2 = 0.0016\) gives \(\frac{0.04}{N \times 0.0016} = \frac{25}{N}\). As \(N \to \infty\), this probability vanishes.

P3 · Failure of diversification under common factor

Suppose loan returns follow \(\tilde{y}_j = \mu + \gamma \tilde{F} + \tilde{\epsilon}_j\), where \(\tilde{F} \sim (0, \sigma_F^2)\) is a common macroeconomic factor and \(\tilde{\epsilon}_j \sim (0, \sigma_\epsilon^2)\) are independent idiosyncratic errors. Prove that portfolio variance does not converge to zero as \(N \to \infty\).

Solution

The average portfolio return is \(\bar{Z}_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N (\mu + \gamma \tilde{F} + \tilde{\epsilon}_j) = \mu + \gamma \tilde{F} + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N \tilde{\epsilon}_j\). Taking the variance: \(\Var(\bar{Z}_N) = \gamma^2 \sigma_F^2 + \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{j=1}^N \sigma_\epsilon^2 = \gamma^2 \sigma_F^2 + \frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{N}\). In the limit as \(N \to \infty\), \(\lim_{N \to \infty} \Var(\bar{Z}_N) = \gamma^2 \sigma_F^2 > 0\). Idiosyncratic risk vanishes, but systematic factor risk remains, disproving full diversification.

Research

R1 · Adverse selection and credit rationing

How does Stiglitz and Weiss's (1981) theory of credit rationing complement Diamond's (1984) model of delegated monitoring in explaining why loan markets do not clear by price?

Solution

Stiglitz and Weiss show that raising interest rates to clear excess loan demand induces adverse selection (safer borrowers drop out) and moral hazard (borrowers select riskier projects). Therefore, banks ration credit quantities rather than raising interest rates. Diamond's model complements this by showing how banks actively monitor borrowers to constrain moral hazard, substituting auditing contracts for high interest rate clearing mechanisms.

R2 · Diamond-Dybvig run vulnerability

Explain why the asset diversification that enables delegated monitoring in Diamond (1984) does not resolve the demand deposit run vulnerability analyzed in Diamond and Dybvig (1983).

Solution

Diversification eliminates idiosyncratic credit risk at maturity (date 1). However, bank assets are illiquid: early liquidation yields fire-sale losses. If depositors experience strategic coordination failure and demand immediate withdrawal at date 0.5, first-come first-served sequential service forces early liquidation of long assets, causing insolvency despite solvent date 1 fundamentals.

R3 · Capital adequacy as a commitment device

In modern delegated monitoring literature (e.g. Holmstrom and Tirole 1997), why must bank equity capital be positive even if the loan portfolio is diversified across thousands of borrowers?

Solution

Bank monitoring effort is unobservable to depositors. If bank owners have zero equity, they face limited liability and moral hazard: they have no incentive to expend costly monitoring effort \(K\). Bank equity capital serves as skin-in-the-game, aligning intermediary incentives by ensuring bank owners suffer first-loss equity wipeouts if monitoring slack leads to borrower default.
