Bank capital functions as an equity buffer absorbing credit losses and market asset write-downs before depositors and senior creditors suffer impairment. In response to the 2007–2008 global financial crisis, international regulators overhauled prudential standards under the Basel III framework, introducing strict Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) minima, capital conservation buffers, and a non-risk-based leverage backstop. Quantitative asset-liability managers must understand both the algebraic mechanics of risk-weighted assets and the critical structural divergence between regulatory book capital and market economic value of equity.

1The Basel III Regulatory Capital Architecture

A bank's solvency depends on whether the economic value of its assets exceeds its contractual liabilities. Under international prudential regulation established by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), capital adequacy is measured relative to credit, market, and operational risk exposures .

Definition 1.1 (Basel III capital requirements)

The Basel III capital requirements constitute an international regulatory framework mandating that commercial banks maintain minimum proportions of loss-absorbing equity capital relative to their risk-weighted assets (RWA) and total unweighted on- and off-balance-sheet exposures .

The Basel III framework establishes a strict hierarchy of capital quality based on loss-absorption capacity:

  1. Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1): The highest quality of capital, consisting of ordinary common shares, stock surplus, retained earnings, and accumulated other comprehensive income (subject to regulatory adjustments). CET1 absorbs losses on a going-concern basis immediately.
  2. Additional Tier 1 (AT1): Subordinated instruments with no maturity date (perpetual) and full dividend discretion that contain contractual loss-absorption mechanisms (such as principal write-down or mandatory conversion to common equity upon breaching a trigger CET1 ratio).
  3. Tier 1 Capital: The sum of CET1 and AT1 (\(\text{Tier 1} = \text{CET1} + \text{AT1}\)).
  4. Tier 2 Capital: Gone-concern loss-absorbing capital, comprising subordinated term debt (minimum original maturity of five years) and qualifying general loan-loss reserves (up to 1.25% of credit RWA under the standardized approach).
  5. Total Capital: The sum of Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital (\(\text{Total Capital} = \text{Tier 1} + \text{Tier 2}\)).
Definition 1.2 (risk-weighted assets)

Let a bank hold \(K\) asset exposures, where asset \(k\) has nominal accounting value \(A_k\) and regulatory risk weight \(w_k \ge 0\). The total credit Risk-Weighted Assets (\(\text{RWA}\)) are defined by the linear combination:

\[ \text{RWA} = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k A_k \]

The regulatory capital ratios are defined by:

\[ \text{CET1 Ratio} = \frac{\text{CET1}}{\text{RWA}}, \quad \text{Tier 1 Ratio} = \frac{\text{Tier 1}}{\text{RWA}}, \quad \text{Total Capital Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Capital}}{\text{RWA}} \]

Under the Basel III Standardized Approach for credit risk, regulatory risk weights reflect counterparty type and external credit ratings:

Asset Category Standardized Risk Weight (\(w_k\)) Regulatory Rationale
Central Bank Cash & Reserves 0% Default risk-free domestic sovereign claim
High-Grade Domestic Sovereign Bonds (AAA to AA-) 0% Negligible nominal sovereign credit default probability
Residential Mortgages (standard loan-to-value) 35% – 50% Secured by residential collateral with historical loss buffer
Commercial Loans (unrated corporate borrowers) 100% Standard corporate credit risk profile
High-Risk Commercial Real Estate / Equity Holdings 150% – 250% High asset volatility and elevated default severity

2Regulatory Minimums, Buffers, and the Leverage Ratio

Basel III introduced macroprudential and microprudential buffers on top of absolute baseline minimums to restrict discretionary bonus and dividend distributions when capital approaches critical floors .

Capital Metric Minimum Requirement Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) Total Minimum with CCB
CET1 Ratio 4.50% (450 bp) 2.50% (250 bp) 7.00% (700 bp)
Tier 1 Capital Ratio 6.00% (600 bp) 2.50% (250 bp) 8.50% (850 bp)
Total Capital Ratio 8.00% (800 bp) 2.50% (250 bp) 10.50% (1050 bp)

If a bank's CET1 ratio dips below 7.00% into the 2.50% Capital Conservation Buffer, it does not breach legal solvency but faces progressively severe statutory caps on discretionary dividend payouts, share buybacks, and executive discretionary compensation.

Definition 2.1 (Basel III leverage ratio)

To guard against the gaming of internal risk weights and model risk, Basel III introduced a non-risk-based backstop known as the Leverage Ratio:

\[ \text{Leverage Ratio} = \frac{\text{Tier 1 Capital}}{\text{Total Exposure Measure}} \ge 3.0\% \]

where the Total Exposure Measure comprises all on-balance-sheet assets plus off-balance-sheet commitments, repo gross exposures, and derivative counterparty credit exposures without any risk-weight discounting .

3Credit Loss Absorption and Solvency Breaches

When credit defaults occur, loans are written down. The loss reduces asset book value dollar-for-dollar and directly reduces CET1 equity capital dollar-for-dollar. However, because the written-off asset had a non-zero risk weight, RWA also decreases. This simultaneous reduction creates a non-linear feedback on the capital ratio.

Proposition 3.1 (credit loss threshold to target capital ratio)

Let a bank possess initial common equity \(\text{CET1}_0\) and initial risk-weighted assets \(\text{RWA}_0\). Suppose an asset category with regulatory risk weight \(w \in (0, \infty)\) incurs a credit loss write-down of magnitude \(L\). Then the bank's CET1 ratio reaches target threshold \(t \in (0, 1)\) if and only if the cumulative loss satisfies :

\[ L^* = \frac{\text{CET1}_0 - t \cdot \text{RWA}_0}{1 - t \cdot w} \]
Proof

Writing down an asset by amount \(L\) reduces total accounting equity by \(L\):

\[ \text{CET1}_{\text{post}} = \text{CET1}_0 - L \]

Simultaneously, the asset balance of exposure with risk weight \(w\) is reduced by \(L\), reducing total risk-weighted assets by \(w \cdot L\):

\[ \text{RWA}_{\text{post}} = \text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot L \]

Equating the post-loss capital ratio to target threshold \(t\):

\[ \frac{\text{CET1}_0 - L}{\text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot L} = t \]

Multiplying both sides by the post-loss denominator \((\text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot L)\):

\[ \text{CET1}_0 - L = t (\text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot L) = t \cdot \text{RWA}_0 - t \cdot w \cdot L \]

Collecting terms in \(L\) on the right-hand side and constants on the left-hand side:

\[ \text{CET1}_0 - t \cdot \text{RWA}_0 = L - t \cdot w \cdot L = L (1 - t \cdot w) \]

Since \(t \cdot w < 1\) under all standard prudential regimes (for instance, \(t = 0.045\) and \(w = 1.0\) yields \(1 - t \cdot w = 0.955 > 0\)), we divide by \((1 - t \cdot w)\):

\[ L^* = \frac{\text{CET1}_0 - t \cdot \text{RWA}_0}{1 - t \cdot w} \]

This completes the deductive proof. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.1 (numerical evaluation on simulated banking book)

Consider the simulated bank balance sheet evaluated in Lab BK1-L07-1:

  • Cash & Reserves: 20,000,000 cents ($200,000) at \(w = 0\%\) \(\implies \text{RWA} = 0\).
  • Sovereign Debt: 30,000,000 cents ($300,000) at \(w = 0\%\) \(\implies \text{RWA} = 0\).
  • Residential Mortgages: 60,000,000 cents ($600,000) at \(w = 50\%\) \(\implies \text{RWA} = 30,000,000\).
  • Commercial Loans: 90,000,000 cents ($900,000) at \(w = 100\%\) \(\implies \text{RWA} = 90,000,000\).

Total Assets = 200,000,000 cents ($2,000,000). Total \(\text{RWA}_0 = 120,000,000\) cents ($1,200,000) (RWA density = 60%).

Capital structure: \(\text{CET1}_0 = 14,000,000\) cents ($140,000), Tier 2 = 6,000,000 cents ($60,000), Total Capital = 20,000,000 cents ($200,000).

Initial ratios:

\[ \text{CET1 Ratio} = \frac{14,000,000}{120,000,000} = 11.6667\% = 1167 \text{ bp} \] \[ \text{Total Capital Ratio} = \frac{20,000,000}{120,000,000} = 16.6667\% = 1667 \text{ bp} \]

To reach the statutory minimum CET1 ratio of \(t = 4.50\% = 0.045\) (450 bp) via commercial loan write-offs (\(w = 1.0\)):

\[ L^* = \frac{14,000,000 - 0.045 \times 120,000,000}{1 - 0.045 \times 1.0} = \frac{14,000,000 - 5,400,000}{0.955} = \frac{8,600,000}{0.955} \approx 9,005,235.6 \implies 9,005,236 \text{ cents} \]

Verifying post-loss values:

\[ \text{CET1}_{\text{post}} = 14,000,000 - 9,005,236 = 4,994,764 \text{ cents} \] \[ \text{RWA}_{\text{post}} = 120,000,000 - 9,005,236 = 110,994,764 \text{ cents} \] \[ \frac{\text{CET1}_{\text{post}}}{\text{RWA}_{\text{post}}} = \frac{4,994,764}{110,994,764} = 0.045000003 \implies 450 \text{ bp} \]
Remark 3.1 (regulatory capital vs economic value of equity)

Regulatory capital is an accounting book value construct. Under historical cost and Held-to-Maturity (HTM) accounting rules, fixed-income securities are not marked to market on regulatory balance sheets. Consequently, a bank can exhibit a robust CET1 ratio exceeding 12% while simultaneously suffering severe economic insolvency (\(\text{EVE} < 0\)) due to duration mismatch under large interest-rate shocks .

"""Lab BK1-L07-1: Basel III capital requirements and loss absorption.

Basel III capital regulation requires banks to maintain capital ratios against
Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA):
    Capital Ratio = Capital / RWA

The framework establishes hierarchical capital tiers:
- Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1): pure loss-absorbing equity (common stock, retained earnings).
- Tier 1 Capital: CET1 + Additional Tier 1 (AT1 instruments).
- Total Capital: Tier 1 + Tier 2 (subordinated debt, qualifying loan loss reserves).

This lab evaluates:
1. A banking book with illustrative risk-weighted assets (SIMULATED, in cents):
   - Cash / Reserves: 0% risk weight
   - High-grade sovereign debt: 0% risk weight
   - Residential mortgages: 50% risk weight
   - Commercial loans: 100% risk weight
2. Pre-loss CET1 and Total Capital ratios.
3. Post-loss ratios after a credit write-down L on commercial loans (100% risk weight).
4. The exact loss threshold L* that reduces CET1 to a target ratio (e.g. 4.50% = 450 bp).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
from fractions import Fraction


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class AssetExposure:
    name: str
    cents: int
    weight_bp: int


# Illustrative balance sheet in cents
ASSETS: list[AssetExposure] = [
    AssetExposure("Cash & Reserves", 20_000_000, 0),
    AssetExposure("Sovereign Debt", 30_000_000, 0),
    AssetExposure("Residential Mortgages", 60_000_000, 5000),  # 50%
    AssetExposure("Commercial Loans", 90_000_000, 10_000),  # 100%
]

CET1_CENTS = 14_000_000  # $140,000 Common Equity Tier 1
TIER2_CENTS = 6_000_000  # $60,000 Tier 2 subordinated debt
TOTAL_CAPITAL_CENTS = CET1_CENTS + TIER2_CENTS  # 20,000,000 cents


def compute_rwa(asset_list: list[AssetExposure]) -> int:
    """Compute total RWA in cents."""
    total_rwa = Fraction(0, 1)
    for a in asset_list:
        w_frac = Fraction(a.weight_bp, 10_000)
        total_rwa += Fraction(a.cents, 1) * w_frac
    return int(total_rwa)


def capital_ratios(cet1: int, total_cap: int, rwa: int) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """Return (cet1_ratio_bp, total_cap_ratio_bp) rounded to nearest basis point."""
    cet1_bp = (cet1 * 10_000 * 2 + rwa) // (rwa * 2)
    total_bp = (total_cap * 10_000 * 2 + rwa) // (rwa * 2)
    return cet1_bp, total_bp


def solve_loss_for_target_cet1(cet1_0: int, rwa_0: int, target_bp: int, loan_weight_bp: int = 10_000) -> int:
    """Solve for credit loss L on commercial loans such that:

    (CET1_0 - L) / (RWA_0 - w * L) = target_bp / 10000
    => (CET1_0 - L) = t * (RWA_0 - w * L)
    => CET1_0 - t * RWA_0 = L * (1 - t * w)
    => L = (CET1_0 - t * RWA_0) / (1 - t * w)
    """
    t = Fraction(target_bp, 10_000)
    w = Fraction(loan_weight_bp, 10_000)
    num = Fraction(cet1_0, 1) - t * Fraction(rwa_0, 1)
    denom = Fraction(1, 1) - t * w
    loss_frac = num / denom
    # Round half away from zero
    return (loss_frac.numerator * 2 + loss_frac.denominator) // (loss_frac.denominator * 2)


def main() -> None:
    print("A. Base Balance Sheet and Capital Ratios (SIMULATED, in cents)")
    base_rwa = compute_rwa(ASSETS)
    total_assets = sum(a.cents for a in ASSETS)
    cet1_bp, total_bp = capital_ratios(CET1_CENTS, TOTAL_CAPITAL_CENTS, base_rwa)

    print(f"Total Assets:            {total_assets:11d} cents")
    print(f"Risk-Weighted Assets:    {base_rwa:11d} cents (RWA density: {base_rwa * 100 // total_assets}%)")
    print(f"CET1 Capital:            {CET1_CENTS:11d} cents | CET1 Ratio:  {cet1_bp:5d} bp ({cet1_bp / 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"Total Capital:           {TOTAL_CAPITAL_CENTS:11d} cents | Total Ratio: {total_bp:5d} bp")

    print()
    print("B. Solvency Stress: Credit Loss to Reach Target CET1 Ratio (4.50% / 450 bp)")
    target_bp = 450  # 4.50%
    loss_cents = solve_loss_for_target_cet1(CET1_CENTS, base_rwa, target_bp, loan_weight_bp=10_000)
    print(f"Credit Loss required to reach {target_bp} bp CET1: {loss_cents:9d} cents")

    # Verify post-loss position
    post_cet1 = CET1_CENTS - loss_cents
    post_rwa = base_rwa - loss_cents  # 100% risk weight written off
    post_cet1_bp, _ = capital_ratios(post_cet1, TOTAL_CAPITAL_CENTS - loss_cents, post_rwa)
    print(f"Post-loss CET1 Capital:  {post_cet1:11d} cents")
    print(f"Post-loss RWA:           {post_rwa:11d} cents")
    print(f"Post-loss CET1 Ratio:    {post_cet1_bp:5d} bp (matches target {target_bp} bp)")

    print()
    print("C. Mathematical Invariant Checks")
    print(f"Post-loss ratio matches target exactly: {post_cet1_bp == target_bp}")
    print(f"Loss is less than total CET1 (bank maintains solvency): {0 < loss_cents < CET1_CENTS}")


main()

Where the Canon Breaks

Standard textbook banking theory assumes that higher risk weights consistently align with higher portfolio risk. In practice, regulatory capital regimes create powerful regulatory arbitrage incentives:

  • The Zero Sovereign Risk Weight Illusion: Under Basel rules, national regulators can apply a 0% risk weight to domestic sovereign debt regardless of duration or sovereign creditworthiness. Prior to 2011, European peripheral banks accumulated massive Greek and Italian sovereign bonds at 0% RWA, generating high book returns with zero regulatory capital charges until sovereign spreads blew out.
  • The Interest Rate Risk Blind Spot: Basel III credit risk weights measure only default probability and recovery rates; they assign 0% risk weight to 30-year US Treasury bonds and agency mortgage-backed securities. When central banks raised policy rates by 500 basis points in 2022–2023, banks holding long-duration fixed-rate securities suffered devastating mark-to-market losses that completely wiped out their tangible common equity, even though their Basel CET1 ratios appeared pristine .

4Misconceptions

  1. “Bank capital is a cash reserve held in a vault.”

    Capital is not an asset; it is an equity claim on the liabilities and equity side (right-hand side) of the balance sheet. Cash and central bank reserves are assets on the left-hand side. A bank can possess $100 million in regulatory CET1 capital while holding zero liquid cash, having invested all funds in illiquid corporate loans.

  2. “A bank meeting its 4.5% CET1 ratio is legally and operationally safe.”

    A CET1 ratio between 4.50% and 7.00% enters the Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB). While the bank is not closed by regulators, it is legally prohibited from paying dividends, repurchasing shares, or paying discretionary executive bonuses. Furthermore, wholesale market counterparties typically demand CET1 ratios well above 10% before extending unsecured lines of credit.

  3. “A bank with a high Tier 1 capital ratio cannot fail from an interest rate shock.”

    As demonstrated by the 2023 US banking failures (Silicon Valley Bank, First Republic), regulatory Tier 1 capital can remain well above 12% on an accounting basis while the bank's mark-to-market economic value of equity has turned deeply negative . When uninsured depositors demand their funds, the bank is forced to liquidate assets at market prices, converting unrealized accounting losses into realized capital destruction.

Mechanics

M1 · RWA and Capital Ratio Calculation

A bank possesses $500 million in total assets consisting of: $50 million cash (0% risk weight), $150 million government securities (0% risk weight), $100 million residential mortgages (50% risk weight), and $200 million corporate loans (100% risk weight). Its balance sheet includes $35 million of CET1 capital and $10 million of Tier 2 subordinated debt. Compute the bank's total RWA, its CET1 ratio (in bp), and its Total Capital ratio (in bp).

Solution

We calculate total Risk-Weighted Assets by summing the weighted asset exposures:

\[ \text{RWA} = 50 \times 0.0 + 150 \times 0.0 + 100 \times 0.50 + 200 \times 1.00 = 0 + 0 + 50 + 200 = \$250\text{ million} \]

The bank's CET1 ratio is:

\[ \text{CET1 Ratio} = \frac{\$35\text{M}}{\$250\text{M}} = 0.1400 = 14.00\% = 1400\text{ bp} \]

The bank's Total Capital is \(\text{Tier 1} + \text{Tier 2} = \$35\text{M} + \$10\text{M} = \$45\text{M}\). The Total Capital ratio is:

\[ \text{Total Capital Ratio} = \frac{\$45\text{M}}{\$250\text{M}} = 0.1800 = 18.00\% = 1800\text{ bp} \]

Both ratios substantially exceed regulatory minimums and conservation buffers.

M2 · Leverage Ratio Evaluation

Using the bank from Problem M1, assume the bank has $50 million in off-balance-sheet commitments with a 100% credit conversion factor, yielding a Total Exposure Measure of $550 million. Compute the bank's Basel III Leverage Ratio. Does the bank satisfy the 3.0% Basel III requirement?

Solution

Tier 1 Capital equals CET1 capital of $35 million (assuming AT1 is zero). The Total Exposure Measure is the sum of on-balance-sheet assets ($500M) and off-balance-sheet commitments ($50M):

\[ \text{Total Exposure Measure} = 500 + 50 = \$550\text{ million} \]

The Leverage Ratio is computed as:

\[ \text{Leverage Ratio} = \frac{\text{Tier 1}}{\text{Total Exposure}} = \frac{\$35\text{M}}{\$550\text{M}} \approx 0.06364 = 6.36\% = 636\text{ bp} \]

Because 6.36% strictly exceeds the statutory 3.00% minimum, the bank comfortably satisfies the Basel III leverage requirement.

M3 · Write-down on 50% Risk-Weighted Assets

Suppose a bank has initial \(\text{CET1}_0 = \$50\text{M}\) and initial \(\text{RWA}_0 = \$400\text{M}\). Credit losses occur exclusively on residential mortgages carrying a risk weight of \(w = 50\% = 0.50\). Calculate the exact credit loss write-down \(L^*\) that reduces the bank's CET1 ratio to exactly the 7.00% CCB threshold (\(t = 0.07\)).

Solution

Using Proposition 3.1 with \(w = 0.50\) and \(t = 0.07\):

\[ L^* = \frac{\text{CET1}_0 - t \cdot \text{RWA}_0}{1 - t \cdot w} = \frac{50 - 0.07 \times 400}{1 - 0.07 \times 0.50} = \frac{50 - 28}{1 - 0.035} = \frac{22}{0.965} \approx \$22.7979\text{ million} \]

Let us verify: post-loss CET1 is \(50 - 22.7979 = \$27.2021\)M. Post-loss RWA is \(400 - 0.50 \times 22.7979 = 400 - 11.3990 = \$388.6010\)M. The ratio is \(27.2021 / 388.6010 = 0.07000 = 7.00\%\), confirming exact agreement.

Proof

P1 · Sensitivity of Capital Ratio to Loan Risk Weights

Prove analytically that for a fixed credit loss \(L > 0\), the post-loss capital ratio \(\text{CET1}_{\text{post}} / \text{RWA}_{\text{post}}\) is strictly increasing in the risk weight \(w\) of the written-off asset.

Solution

The post-loss capital ratio as a function of \(w\) is given by:

\[ \rho(w) = \frac{\text{CET1}_0 - L}{\text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot L} \]

Assuming the bank remains solvent (\(\text{CET1}_0 - L > 0\)) and positive RWA remains (\(\text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot L > 0\)), we take the first derivative with respect to \(w\):

\[ \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial w} = \frac{0 - (\text{CET1}_0 - L)(-L)}{(\text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot L)^2} = \frac{L (\text{CET1}_0 - L)}{(\text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot L)^2} \]

Since \(L > 0\), \(\text{CET1}_0 - L > 0\), and the squared denominator is strictly positive, \(\partial \rho / \partial w > 0\). Writing off high risk-weight assets removes more RWA from the denominator, cushioning the decline in the regulatory capital ratio compared to writing off low risk-weight assets.

P2 · Lower Bound on Credit Loss for Full Insolvency

Prove that under any regulatory risk weight \(w \ge 0\), a credit loss of magnitude \(L = \text{CET1}_0\) results in a CET1 ratio of exactly 0%, and that for any positive target ratio \(t > 0\), the required loss satisfies \(L^* < \text{CET1}_0\).

Solution

From the definition of post-loss CET1, \(\text{CET1}_{\text{post}} = \text{CET1}_0 - L\). When \(L = \text{CET1}_0\), the numerator equals zero: \(\text{CET1}_{\text{post}} = 0\). As long as post-loss RWA is positive (\(\text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot \text{CET1}_0 > 0\)), the ratio is identically 0%.

For any target ratio \(t > 0\), consider the loss threshold formula:

\[ L^* = \frac{\text{CET1}_0 - t \cdot \text{RWA}_0}{1 - t \cdot w} \]

Subtracting \(\text{CET1}_0\) from \(L^*\):

\[ L^* - \text{CET1}_0 = \frac{\text{CET1}_0 - t \cdot \text{RWA}_0 - \text{CET1}_0 (1 - t \cdot w)}{1 - t \cdot w} = \frac{-t \cdot \text{RWA}_0 + t \cdot w \cdot \text{CET1}_0}{1 - t \cdot w} = \frac{-t (\text{RWA}_0 - w \cdot \text{CET1}_0)}{1 - t \cdot w} \]

Because initial RWA exceeds the risk-weighted equity (\(\text{RWA}_0 > w \cdot \text{CET1}_0\)) and \(1 - t \cdot w > 0\), the term is strictly negative, proving \(L^* < \text{CET1}_0\).

P3 · Convexity of Required Loss in Target Ratio

Show that the loss function \(L^*(t) = \frac{\text{CET1}_0 - t \cdot \text{RWA}_0}{1 - t \cdot w}\) is strictly convex in the target ratio \(t\) on the domain \(t \in [0, 1/w)\).

Solution

Let \(y(t) = \frac{a - b t}{1 - w t}\) where \(a = \text{CET1}_0 > 0\) and \(b = \text{RWA}_0 > 0\). We compute the first derivative with respect to \(t\):

\[ y'(t) = \frac{-b(1 - w t) - (-w)(a - b t)}{(1 - w t)^2} = \frac{-b + b w t + a w - b w t}{(1 - w t)^2} = \frac{a w - b}{(1 - w t)^2} \]

Since typical bank leverage implies \(b = \text{RWA}_0 > a w = w \cdot \text{CET1}_0\), the numerator \(a w - b < 0\), so \(y'(t) < 0\). Differentiating a second time:

\[ y''(t) = (a w - b) \frac{\partial}{\partial t} (1 - w t)^{-2} = (a w - b) \cdot (-2)(-w) (1 - w t)^{-3} = \frac{2 w (b - a w)}{(1 - w t)^3} \]

Since \(b - a w > 0\), \(w > 0\), and \(1 - w t > 0\) for \(t < 1/w\), the second derivative \(y''(t) > 0\). Hence, \(L^*(t)\) is strictly convex in \(t\).

Empirical

E1 · 2023 HTM Bond Accounting Divergence

In early 2023, a regional bank reported regulatory CET1 capital of $12 billion and total RWA of $100 billion (CET1 ratio = 12.0%). However, the bank held $90 billion in Held-to-Maturity (HTM) Treasuries and MBS with an average duration of 7 years, having experienced an unhedged 300 bp rate increase. Compute the mark-to-market loss on the securities portfolio and evaluate the true tangible economic equity.

Solution

Using the duration approximation for bond price changes:

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \times \Delta y = -7 \times (+0.03) = -0.21 = -21\% \]

The mark-to-market loss on the $90 billion portfolio is:

\[ \text{Loss}_{\text{MTM}} \approx 90 \times (-0.21) = -\$18.9\text{ billion} \]

Adjusting the reported book CET1 equity ($12 billion) for the unrecognized economic loss:

\[ \text{Tangible Economic Equity} = 12 - 18.9 = -\$6.9\text{ billion} \]

While the bank's regulatory CET1 ratio was superficially strong at 12.0%, the bank was in reality economically insolvent by nearly $7 billion, matching the empirical mechanisms documented by Jiang et al. (2024).

E2 · Capital Conservation Buffer Restrictions

Under Basel III rules, if a bank's CET1 ratio falls into the CCB quartile [5.125%, 5.750%), its statutory maximum dividend payout ratio is capped at 20% of current-year earnings. If the bank generates $400 million in net income, what is the maximum dollar dividend it can distribute, and what must happen to the remaining $320 million?

Solution

The statutory cap of 20% limits dividend distributions to:

\[ \text{Max Dividend} = 0.20 \times \$400\text{M} = \$80\text{ million} \]

The remaining 80% ($320 million) must be retained and added directly to retained earnings within CET1 capital, forcing the bank to rebuild its capital conservation buffer organically over time.

E3 · RWA Compression via Securitization

A bank holds $1 billion in residential mortgages with a 50% risk weight ($500M RWA). It originates a senior synthetic credit tranching: an external insurer sells credit default protection on the first 10% loss tranche, allowing the remaining 90% senior tranche to receive a 20% risk weight under securitization rules. Compute the reduction in total RWA.

Solution

The initial RWA is $1,000\text{M} \times 0.50 = \$500\text{ million}$. After synthetic tranching, the $900M senior tranche carries a 20% weight ($900\text{M} \times 0.20 = \$180\text{M}$), and the retained first-loss or mezzanine portion carries a higher weight. Assuming the senior tranche is $900M at 20% ($180M) and the mezzanine is $100M at 100% ($100M), total new RWA is $280 million. This achieves an RWA compression of $\$500\text{M} - \$280\text{M} = \$220\text{ million}$, dramatically raising regulatory capital ratios without altering underlying asset cash flows.

Research

R1 · Interaction of Basel III RWA and Deposit Beta

Explain how a bank with a low deposit beta (\(\beta = 0.20\)) can offset regulatory capital compression during an economic downturn compared to a bank reliant on rate-sensitive wholesale funding (\(\beta = 0.90\)).

Solution

A low deposit beta allows the bank to maintain high Net Interest Margins (NIM) as market rates rise, generating strong recurring operating income. This recurring net income flows directly into retained earnings, which augment CET1 capital on a quarterly basis. Conversely, a bank with \(\beta = 0.90\) experiences compressed NIM, zero organic capital generation, and has no internal cash flow to absorb credit loan write-downs, forcing it to issue expensive dilutive equity or contract lending.

R2 · Systemic Effects of Simultaneous De-risking

When multiple large banks experience capital declines toward the CCB boundary, they face incentives to cut RWA by shrinking commercial lending and buying 0% risk-weight sovereign bonds. Analyze the macroprudential systemic consequence of this procyclical behavior.

Solution

When banks simultaneously contract commercial lending to shed 100% risk-weighted assets, they induce an economy-wide credit crunch. Viable corporate borrowers are denied credit or debt rollovers, driving corporate defaults and increasing loan write-downs across all banks. This feedback loop creates macroprudential procyclicality: individual bank efforts to optimize regulatory capital ratios exacerbate aggregate economic downturns, the primary motivation for the Basel III countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB).

R3 · The Basel Endgame and Internal Ratings-Based (IRB) Models

Under the Basel III final reforms (often termed the "Basel Endgame"), regulators introduced an "output floor" mandating that banks using internal ratings-based (IRB) models cannot compute aggregate RWA lower than 72.5% of the standardized approach. Evaluate why regulators imposed this floor despite banks arguing that their statistical probability-of-default (PD) models are more granular.

Solution

Empirical evidence revealed that international banks using internal ratings-based models systematically underestimated default probabilities and loss-given-default parameters to optimize and deflate RWA, producing CET1 ratios that were not comparable across jurisdictions. The 72.5% output floor constrains model optimization and regulatory arbitrage, restoring a robust standardized baseline that ensures adequate loss-absorbing capital across global financial institutions.
