Maturity Transformation and Refinancing Risk

  • Uses: delegated monitoring from L01, balance sheet from AC1

Beyond delegated monitoring, the defining economic function of commercial banks is maturity transformation: converting short-term, liquid liabilities into long-term, illiquid assets. Households desire liquid on-demand savings, while productive enterprises require multi-year fixed capital. By bridging this temporal divergence, banks create economic liquidity but assume structural refinancing risk. When short-term interest rates increase, rolling liabilities reprice immediately at higher costs, while fixed-rate asset coupons remain locked, compressing net interest spreads and generating severe refinancing deficits. This lesson formalizes the multi-period cash flow arithmetic of maturity mismatch.

1The Mechanics of Maturity Transformation

In standard financial contracting, savers prefer liquid instruments with short maturities (\(\tau_L \approx 0\) or \(\tau_L \le 1\) year) to accommodate unpredictable liquidity needs. In contrast, corporate borrowers, homeowners, and infrastructure developers require long-term funding (\(\tau_A \ge 5\) to \(30\) years) matching the gestation period of physical assets.

Definition 1.1 (maturity transformation)

Maturity transformation is the process whereby a financial institution issues liabilities with short effective maturities \(\tau_L\) to fund assets with substantially longer maturities \(\tau_A > \tau_L\), generating a net maturity mismatch \(\tau_A - \tau_L > 0\) on its balance sheet.

Definition 1.2 (refinancing gap)

At any future date \(t\), the refinancing gap is the difference between the principal of assets maturing or repricing at date \(t\) and the principal of liabilities maturing or repricing at date \(t\):

\[ \mathrm{Gap}_{\text{ref}}(t) = A_t^{\text{reprice}} - L_t^{\text{reprice}} \]

When \(\mathrm{Gap}_{\text{ref}}(t) < 0\), the institution is liability-sensitive at date \(t\): it must refinance an excess of maturing liabilities using newly issued debt at prevailing market rates.

Proposition 1.3 (multi-period refinancing shortfall)

Let a bank hold an asset of principal \(A\) yielding fixed annual coupon \(r_A\) maturing at year \(T\). Let this asset be funded entirely by 1-year rolling liabilities of principal \(L = A\). If 1-year deposit rates follow sequence \(\{r_{D,t}\}_{t=1}^T\), the net interest cash flow at year \(t\) is:

\[ \mathrm{NII}_t = A \cdot (r_A - r_{D,t}) \]

A cumulative cash shortfall occurs over the asset's life whenever the cumulative liability interest payments exceed cumulative asset interest income:

\[ \sum_{t=1}^T \mathrm{NII}_t = A \cdot \sum_{t=1}^T (r_A - r_{D,t}) < 0 \iff \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{D,t} > r_A \]
Proof

Because the asset has a fixed coupon \(r_A\) and principal \(A\), annual interest earned at each year \(t \in \{1, \dots, T\}\) is identically \(I_t = A \cdot r_A\). Because liabilities roll over annually at rate \(r_{D,t}\), annual interest expense is \(E_t = L \cdot r_{D,t} = A \cdot r_{D,t}\). Net interest income in period \(t\) is \(\mathrm{NII}_t = I_t - E_t = A(r_A - r_{D,t})\). Summing across all \(T\) periods yields \(\sum_{t=1}^T \mathrm{NII}_t = A \sum_{t=1}^T (r_A - r_{D,t}) = A [T \cdot r_A - \sum_{t=1}^T r_{D,t}]\). Dividing by \(A \cdot T\) establishes that the cumulative sum is negative if and only if the arithmetic mean of deposit rates \(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T r_{D,t}\) strictly exceeds asset yield \(r_A\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Margin Inversion and Rollover Risk

When monetary policy tightens, the short end of the yield curve shifts upward. If the deposit rate resets higher than the asset yield (\(r_{D,t} > r_A\)), the net interest spread \(s_t = r_A - r_{D,t}\) inverts, turning negative. Under negative net interest margin, the bank incurs operational losses that directly deplete retained earnings and equity capital.

Example 1.4 (3-year fixed loan funded by rolling deposits)

A bank originates a 3-year commercial loan of $100,000 at a fixed yield of 6.00% (600 bp). The loan is funded by 1-year deposits rolling over at rates \(r_{D,1} = 3.00\%\), \(r_{D,2} = 5.00\%\), and \(r_{D,3} = 7.50\%\). In Year 1, interest income is \$6,000, interest expense is \$3,000, and NII is +\$3,000. In Year 2, interest expense rises to \$5,000, reducing NII to +\$1,000. In Year 3, interest expense reaches \$7,500, producing a negative NII of -\$1,500. Over the 3-year cycle, cumulative net interest income is \(3,000 + 1,000 - 1,500 = \$2,500\), demonstrating margin compression and Year 3 inversion.

Lab 2 · Refinancing Gap and Cash Flow Shortfall

Simulating a 3-year fixed asset funded by 1-year rolling liabilities demonstrates that rising rollover rates cause net interest income to compress and flip negative in year 3, verifying Proposition 1.3.

"""Lab BK1-L02-1: Maturity transformation and refinancing risk.

Maturity transformation occurs when a financial institution funds long-term, fixed-rate
assets using short-term, rolling liabilities. This generates a structural refinancing gap:
the asset yield is locked, while funding liabilities reprice at prevailing market rates.

This lab simulates:
1. A 3-year fixed-rate asset of 10,000,000 cents ($100,000) earning 6.00% per year.
2. Funded by rolling 1-year deposits, resetting each year at rates:
   Year 1: 3.00% (300 bp)
   Year 2: 5.00% (500 bp)
   Year 3: 7.50% (750 bp)
3. The refinancing gap, annual net interest income, and margin compression.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

PRINCIPAL_CENTS = 10_000_000  # $100,000 principal
ASSET_RATE_BP = 600  # 6.00% annual coupon / yield
DEPOSIT_RATES_BP = [300, 500, 750]  # Rolling 1-year deposit rates in bp


def annual_cash_flows(principal_cents: int, asset_rate_bp: int, dep_rates_bp: list[int]) -> list[dict[str, int]]:
    """Compute interest income, interest expense, and net interest income in cents per year."""
    flows: list[dict[str, int]] = []
    for year_idx, dep_rate_bp in enumerate(dep_rates_bp, start=1):
        income = Fraction(principal_cents * asset_rate_bp, 10_000)
        expense = Fraction(principal_cents * dep_rate_bp, 10_000)
        net_income = income - expense
        flows.append(
            {
                "year": year_idx,
                "asset_rate_bp": asset_rate_bp,
                "dep_rate_bp": dep_rate_bp,
                "income_cents": int(income),
                "expense_cents": int(expense),
                "nii_cents": int(net_income),
            }
        )
    return flows


def main() -> None:
    print("A. Maturity Mismatch: 3-Year Fixed Asset vs Rolling 1-Year Liabilities (SIMULATED, in cents)")
    flows = annual_cash_flows(PRINCIPAL_CENTS, ASSET_RATE_BP, DEPOSIT_RATES_BP)

    cumulative_nii = 0
    for row in flows:
        cumulative_nii += row["nii_cents"]
        spread_bp = row["asset_rate_bp"] - row["dep_rate_bp"]
        print(
            f"Year {row['year']} | Asset: {row['asset_rate_bp']} bp, Dep: {row['dep_rate_bp']} bp | "
            f"Spread: {spread_bp:+4d} bp | Income: {row['income_cents']:8d} | "
            f"Expense: {row['expense_cents']:8d} | NII: {row['nii_cents']:+8d} cents"
        )

    print()
    print("B. Cumulative Net Interest Income across 3 Years")
    print(f"Total Cumulative NII: {cumulative_nii:+8d} cents")

    print()
    print("C. Refinancing Gap and Invariant Checks")
    # Asset repricing occurs only at year 3; liability repricing occurs at years 1, 2, 3
    # At t=1 and t=2, rate-sensitive liabilities = 10,000,000, rate-sensitive assets = 0
    refinancing_gap_t1 = 0 - PRINCIPAL_CENTS
    refinancing_gap_t2 = 0 - PRINCIPAL_CENTS
    refinancing_gap_t3 = PRINCIPAL_CENTS - PRINCIPAL_CENTS  # Both mature
    print(f"Refinancing Gap Year 1: {refinancing_gap_t1} cents (negative indicates liability sensitive)")
    print(f"Refinancing Gap Year 2: {refinancing_gap_t2} cents")
    print(f"Refinancing Gap Year 3: {refinancing_gap_t3} cents")
    print(f"Margin inversion occurs in Year 3 (deposit rate > asset rate): {flows[2]['nii_cents'] < 0}")


main()

Remark 1.5 (refinancing risk versus credit risk)

In this analysis, the borrower never defaults: all promised interest and principal cash flows on the asset are paid on time and in full. Maturity transformation creates solvency risk purely through market rate reset mismatches, completely independent of credit default risk.

3Rollover Failure and Liquidity Runs

A severe manifestation of refinancing risk occurs when creditors refuse to roll over maturing short-term liabilities at any price. If depositors or wholesale commercial paper investors demand immediate cash repayment, the bank cannot immediately call its illiquid multi-year loans. In the absence of central bank lender-of-last-resort facilities or liquid asset reserves, the bank is forced to liquidate assets at fire-sale discounts, turning a liquidity deficit into instantaneous balance sheet insolvency.

Where the Canon Breaks

The simplified maturity mismatch model presented here operates under idealizations that diverge from institutional practice:

  • No liability run-off: The model assumes short-term creditors always roll over principal, merely demanding higher interest rates. In acute banking panics, depositors withdraw funds completely rather than demanding higher yields.
  • Absence of loan prepayments: Real fixed-rate loans (especially U.S. residential mortgages) contain embedded prepayment options. When market rates fall, borrowers refinance away, truncating high-yielding assets; when rates rise, borrowers extend loan lives, exacerbating maturity mismatches.
  • Deterministic interest rate paths: In reality, future refinancing rates are stochastic. ALM desks model interest rate term structures using stochastic short-rate or Hull-White models rather than fixed rate projections.
  • Unhedged balance sheets: Modern commercial banks utilize interest rate swaps (swapping fixed loan receipts for floating cash flows) to neutralize structural maturity transformation gaps.

4Misconceptions

  1. “If all borrowers repay their loans in full, a bank cannot fail.”

    Borrower credit repayment guarantees only asset cash flows. If a bank funds 10-year 4% mortgages with 1-year liabilities that reprice to 7%, the bank suffers a 300 bp annual cash drain. Compounded across years, negative net interest margin drains equity capital until regulatory insolvency thresholds are breached, even with zero credit defaults.

  2. “Maturity transformation is profitable only when the yield curve is upward-sloping.”

    While an upward-sloping term structure widens initial term spreads, banks also earn profit from credit spreads (lending to commercial borrowers at yields above risk-free benchmarks) and deposit market power (paying retail deposit rates below wholesale money market rates).

  3. “A negative refinancing gap means the bank has more assets than liabilities.”

    A negative refinancing gap at date \(t\) means that the bank has more liabilities repricing at date \(t\) than assets repricing at date \(t\). Total accounting assets and liabilities still balance via equity; the gap reflects the temporal distribution of interest rate resets.

5Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Single-period spread and NII

A bank holds $5,000,000 in 5-year fixed loans yielding 7.00%. It funds them with 6-month certificates of deposit currently paying 4.20%. Calculate the bank's annualized net interest spread in basis points and annual net interest income in dollars.

Solution

Net interest spread is \(r_A - r_L = 7.00\% - 4.20\% = 2.80\%\) or 280 basis points. Annual net interest income is \(A \cdot (r_A - r_L) = \$5,000,000 \times 0.0280 = \$140,000\).

M2 · Multi-year margin compression

A bank originates a 2-year loan of $1,000,000 at 5.50%. Year 1 deposit rate is 3.00%; Year 2 deposit rate resets to 6.50%. Compute NII in Year 1, Year 2, and cumulative 2-year NII in dollars.

Solution

Year 1 NII is \(\$1,000,000 \times (0.055 - 0.030) = \$25,000\). Year 2 NII is \(\$1,000,000 \times (0.055 - 0.065) = -\$10,000\). Cumulative 2-year NII is \(\$25,000 + (-\$10,000) = \$15,000\).

M3 · Break-even refinancing rate

A bank holds an 8.00% fixed loan of $200,000 for 4 years. In Years 1, 2, and 3, deposit rates were 4.00%, 6.00%, and 9.00%. What is the maximum deposit rate in Year 4 that allows the bank to avoid a cumulative cash shortfall?

Solution

By Proposition 1.3, cumulative NII is non-negative if \(\frac{1}{4}(r_{D,1} + r_{D,2} + r_{D,3} + r_{D,4}) \le r_A = 8.00\%\). Total allowable deposit interest is \(4 \times 8.00\% = 32.00\%\). Sum of first 3 years is \(4.00\% + 6.00\% + 9.00\% = 19.00\%\). Thus \(r_{D,4} \le 32.00\% - 19.00\% = 13.00\%\). The break-even rate is 13.00%.

Proof

P1 · Inversion of net interest spread

Prove that for any bank where liabilities reprice more frequently than assets (\(\tau_L < \tau_A\)), an upward parallel rate shift \(\Delta r > r_A - r_L\) strictly inverts the net interest spread.

Solution

Let initial asset yield be \(r_A\) and liability cost be \(r_L\) with initial spread \(s_0 = r_A - r_L > 0\). If liabilities reprice immediately to \(r_L + \Delta r\) while asset yield remains locked at \(r_A\), the new spread is \(s_1 = r_A - (r_L + \Delta r) = (r_A - r_L) - \Delta r = s_0 - \Delta r\). If \(\Delta r > s_0\), then \(s_1 < 0\), proving strict margin inversion.

P2 · Refinancing gap zero sum over full horizon

Prove that if an asset of maturity \(T_A\) is funded by liabilities rolling at intervals \(\tau_L\), the sum of all refinancing gaps across the full life of the institution \(\sum_{t=1}^{T_A} \mathrm{Gap}_{\text{ref}}(t)\) equals zero when terminal principal is repaid.

Solution

At each interim rollover date \(t_k\), liability principal \(L\) matures without asset principal redemption, contributing \(-L\) to the gap. At terminal date \(T_A\), asset principal \(A\) matures while final liability principal \(L\) matures. Since \(A = L\), the sum of all principal repayments across assets is \(+A\) and across liabilities is \(-A\). Hence \(\sum_t \mathrm{Gap}_{\text{ref}}(t) = A - A = 0\). The timing of gaps differs, but the undiscounted principal sum is identically zero.

P3 · Present value of refinancing shortfalls

Let discount factors be \(P(0, t)\). Prove that if the forward rate curve lies strictly above asset coupon \(r_A\) for all \(t\), the present value of net interest income \(\sum_{t=1}^T P(0, t) \mathrm{NII}_t\) is strictly negative.

Solution

Under no-arbitrage, the expected refinancing cost at date \(t\) is forward rate \(f(t-1, t)\). By hypothesis, \(f(t-1, t) > r_A\) for all \(t \in \{1, \dots, T\}\). Therefore, \(\mathrm{NII}_t = A(r_A - f(t-1, t)) < 0\) for every \(t\). Because discount factors satisfy \(P(0, t) > 0\), every term in the sum \(P(0, t) \mathrm{NII}_t\) is strictly negative, ensuring the total present value is strictly negative.

Research

R1 · S&L crisis and maturity mismatch

Explain how the U.S. Savings and Loan crisis of the 1980s illustrates the systemic failure of maturity transformation under unhedged interest rate shocks.

Solution

Savings and Loan associations held portfolios of 30-year fixed-rate residential mortgages yielding 6% to 8%, funded by short-term passbook savings deposits. When the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker raised short-term policy rates above 15% to combat inflation, S&L deposit funding costs escalated while mortgage yields remained locked, producing massive negative net interest margins that bankrupted hundreds of thrifts.

R2 · Prepayment optionality and negative convexity

Why does borrower mortgage prepayment behavior create negative convexity, worsening the maturity transformation mismatch when interest rates decline?

Solution

When interest rates fall, borrowers exercise their embedded call options to refinance mortgages at lower rates, shortening asset duration and returning cash when reinvestment yields are depressed. Conversely, when rates rise, prepayments cease, extending mortgage duration. This negative convexity means asset duration lengthens precisely when the bank wants short duration, amplifying maturity mismatch.

R3 · Interest rate swap immunization

How can a bank use a pay-fixed, receive-floating interest rate swap to immunize a 10-year fixed loan funded by 3-month commercial paper against refinancing risk?

Solution

The bank enters a swap paying fixed coupon \(s_{\text{fix}}\) and receiving floating rate \(r_{\text{float}}\). On net, the bank receives fixed loan yield \(r_A\), pays fixed swap rate \(s_{\text{fix}}\), receives floating swap cash flow \(r_{\text{float}}\), and pays floating deposit cost \(r_{\text{float}}\). The floating cash flows cancel, locking in a net interest spread of \(r_A - s_{\text{fix}}\) regardless of future short-term rate movements.
