Modern bank balance sheet risk management operates across two complementary horizons: short-term earnings and long-term economic solvency. While Net Interest Income (NII) sensitivity measures accounting cash flows over a 12-month budget cycle, Economic Value of Equity (EVE) measures the present value of all expected asset cash flows minus all expected liability cash flows. Because banks fund long-duration assets with short-duration liabilities, upward shifts in interest rates cause asset market values to drop much faster than liability values, severely depleting economic equity. This lesson derives the mathematics of EVE duration gaps and models empirical yield curve twists using Federal Reserve market data.

1Economic Value of Equity: Definition and Discounting

Under the economic capital perspective, a bank's net worth is the difference between the present value of its assets and the present value of its liabilities.

Definition 1.1 (economic value of equity)

The economic value of equity (\(\mathrm{EVE}\)) of a financial institution is the difference between the present value of its total assets and the present value of its total liabilities:

\[ \mathrm{EVE} = \mathrm{PV}(\text{Assets}) - \mathrm{PV}(\text{Liabilities}) \]

Under the standardized monthly compounding convention, the discount factor for a contractual cash flow maturing at whole month \(m \ge 1\) at annual rate \(r(m)\) is:

\[ \mathrm{DF}(m) = \left(1 + \frac{r(m)}{12}\right)^{-m} \]

Non-rate-sensitive positions (cash, premises) carry \(m = \text{None}\) and are valued at par (face value). In this baseline model, core non-maturity deposits are carried at face value.

Definition 1.2 (net interest income sensitivity)

Net interest income sensitivity (\(\Delta \mathrm{NII}\)) is the change in projected Net Interest Income over a defined 12-month horizon resulting from a specified interest rate shock \(\Delta r\):

\[ \Delta \mathrm{NII} = \mathrm{NII}(\text{shocked}) - \mathrm{NII}(\text{base}) \]

Positions repricing at month \(m \le 12\) are weighted by the remaining horizon fraction \(\frac{12 - m}{12}\).

2Duration Gap and First-Order Taylor Approximations

Using duration principles from FI1 and Taylor theorem from M2, the first-order change in EVE under a parallel yield curve shock \(\Delta r\) is governed by the duration mismatch between assets and liabilities.

Theorem 2.1 (EVE duration gap sensitivity)

Let total asset present value be \(A = \mathrm{PV}(\text{Assets})\) with modified duration \(D_A\), and total liability present value be \(L = \mathrm{PV}(\text{Liabilities})\) with modified duration \(D_L\). For a small parallel shift \(\Delta r\), the change in EVE satisfies:

\[ \Delta \mathrm{EVE} = -(D_A A - D_L L) \Delta r + R_1(\Delta r) \]

where the remainder term satisfies \(|R_1(\Delta r)| \le \frac{1}{2} (C_A A + C_L L) (\Delta r)^2\) using asset and liability convexities \(C_A\) and \(C_L\). Dividing by base equity \(E = A - L\):

\[ \frac{\Delta \mathrm{EVE}}{E} \approx -\left[D_A - D_L \left(\frac{L}{A}\right)\right] \left(\frac{A}{E}\right) \Delta r = -D_{\text{gap}} \cdot \Delta r \]

where \(D_{\text{gap}} = [D_A - D_L (L/A)] (A/E)\) is the leverage-adjusted duration gap.

Proof

By definition, \(\mathrm{EVE}(r) = A(r) - L(r)\). Since cash flows are fixed, \(A(r)\) and \(L(r)\) are twice continuously differentiable in discount rate \(r\). Expanding \(\mathrm{EVE}(r_0 + \Delta r)\) in a first-order Taylor series around \(r_0\):

\[ \mathrm{EVE}(r_0 + \Delta r) - \mathrm{EVE}(r_0) = \left[\frac{dA}{dr} - \frac{dL}{dr}\right] \Delta r + R_1(\Delta r) \]

From the definition of modified duration, \(\frac{dA}{dr} = -D_A A\) and \(\frac{dL}{dr} = -D_L L\). Substituting these derivatives yields \(\Delta \mathrm{EVE} = -[D_A A - D_L L] \Delta r + R_1(\Delta r)\). By Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder, \(R_1(\Delta r) = \frac{1}{2} \mathrm{EVE}''(\xi) (\Delta r)^2 = \frac{1}{2} [C_A(\xi) A(\xi) - C_L(\xi) L(\xi)] (\Delta r)^2\), bounding the second-order truncation error. Dividing through by \(E = A - L\) gives the normalized duration gap formula. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.2 (leverage amplification of duration risk)

Commercial banks operate with high leverage, where asset-to-equity ratios \(\frac{A}{E}\) typically range from 10 to 20. Even if the net duration mismatch \(D_A - D_L (L/A)\) is only 1.5 years, a leverage multiplier of \(\frac{A}{E} = 12\) expands the effective equity duration gap to \(D_{\text{gap}} = 1.5 \times 12 = 18\) years. Consequently, a +200 bp (+0.02) rate rise destroys approximately \(18 \times 0.02 = 36\%\) of economic equity capital.

3Yield Curve Twists and Empirical Shocks

Actual monetary policy tightening cycles rarely produce parallel yield curve shifts. Instead, policy rate increases trigger yield curve flattening or inversion twists: short-term interest rates surge while long-term rates rise more modestly.

Definition 2.3 (yield curve twist shift)

A twist shift \(\mathrm{Twist}(s, l)\) specifies short-rate shift \(s\) in basis points for tenors \(m \le 3\) months, long-rate shift \(l\) in basis points for tenors \(m \ge 120\) months (10 years), and linear interpolation in tenor \(m\) between 3 and 120 months:

\[ \Delta r(m) = \begin{cases} s & m \le 3 \\ s + (l - s) \cdot \left(\frac{m - 3}{117}\right) & 3 < m < 120 \\ l & m \ge 120 \end{cases} \]

When \(s = l\), the twist shift collapses identically to a parallel shift of magnitude \(s\).

"""Lab BK1-L05-1: Economic value of equity under parallel and twist shocks.

Economic Value of Equity (EVE) measures the long-term, economic solvency of a bank:
    EVE = PV(Assets) - PV(Liabilities) - Deposits(face value)

Under interest rate shocks, duration mismatch between long-term assets and short-term
funding causes EVE to fluctuate:
    Delta EVE ≈ - (D_assets * PV(Assets) - D_liab * PV(Liabilities)) * Delta r

This lab evaluates:
1. SIMULATED bank balance sheet with long-term assets (duration ~6 years) funded
   by short liabilities & core deposits (duration ~1 year).
2. Base EVE under monthly compounding at base rate r_0 = 200 bp.
3. EVE change under parallel rate shock (+100 bp).
4. EVE change under twist rate shock using observed 2022 US Treasury tightening
   from data/ust_daily.csv (3m T-bill +421 bp, 10y Treasury +225 bp).
5. Identity check: TwistShift(s, s) == ParallelShift(s).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import csv
from dataclasses import dataclass
from fractions import Fraction
from pathlib import Path


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Position:
    cents: int
    months: int | None
    side: str


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class ParallelShift:
    bp: int


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class TwistShift:
    short_bp: int
    long_bp: int


def round_cents(f: Fraction) -> int:
    if f >= 0:
        return (f.numerator * 2 + f.denominator) // (f.denominator * 2)
    else:
        return -((-f.numerator * 2 + f.denominator) // (f.denominator * 2))


def shift_at_month(m: int, shift: ParallelShift | TwistShift | None) -> Fraction:
    if shift is None:
        return Fraction(0, 1)
    if isinstance(shift, ParallelShift):
        return Fraction(shift.bp, 1)
    if isinstance(shift, TwistShift):
        if m <= 3:
            return Fraction(shift.short_bp, 1)
        if m >= 120:
            return Fraction(shift.long_bp, 1)
        return Fraction(shift.short_bp, 1) + Fraction((shift.long_bp - shift.short_bp) * (m - 3), 117)
    raise TypeError(f"Unknown shift type: {type(shift)}")


def compute_eve(
    positions: list[Position],
    deposits_cents: int,
    base_rate_bp: int,
    shift: ParallelShift | TwistShift | None = None,
) -> int:
    eve_frac = Fraction(0, 1)
    for p in positions:
        if p.months is None:
            pv = Fraction(p.cents, 1)
        else:
            s_bp = shift_at_month(p.months, shift)
            effective_bp = Fraction(base_rate_bp, 1) + s_bp
            denom = Fraction(120_000, 1) + effective_bp
            df = (Fraction(120_000, 1) / denom) ** p.months
            pv = Fraction(p.cents, 1) * df

        if p.side == "asset":
            eve_frac += pv
        else:
            eve_frac -= pv

    eve_frac -= Fraction(deposits_cents, 1)
    return round_cents(eve_frac)


def load_observed_2022_twist(csv_path: Path) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """Read 3m T-bill and 10y Treasury yield change in 2022 from ust_daily.csv."""
    with csv_path.open(encoding="utf-8") as f:
        reader = csv.DictReader(f)
        records = {r["date"]: r for r in reader if r["date"] in ("2022-01-03", "2022-12-30")}

    r_start = records["2022-01-03"]
    r_end = records["2022-12-30"]

    # Multiply percent by 100 to get basis points
    # Round half-away-from-zero on float readings
    t3m_start = round(float(r_start["tbill_3m"]) * 100)
    t3m_end = round(float(r_end["tbill_3m"]) * 100)
    c10y_start = round(float(r_start["cmt_10y"]) * 100)
    c10y_end = round(float(r_end["cmt_10y"]) * 100)

    short_shift_bp = t3m_end - t3m_start
    long_shift_bp = c10y_end - c10y_start
    return short_shift_bp, long_shift_bp


def main() -> None:
    # 1. Load real rate twist from ust_daily.csv
    csv_file = Path("data") / "ust_daily.csv"
    short_bp, long_bp = load_observed_2022_twist(csv_file)

    print("A. Historical 2022 Yield Curve Move (ust_daily.csv, H.15 Fed Release)")
    print(f"Observed 3-Month T-Bill yield change:  {short_bp:+4d} bp")
    print(f"Observed 10-Year Treasury yield change: {long_bp:+4d} bp")
    print("Yield curve underwent severe bear flattening / inversion (short rate rose faster).")

    # 2. SIMULATED Bank Book
    # Assets: $100M total ($10M cash, $20M 1y loans, $70M 10y mortgages)
    # Liabilities: $85M ($60M core deposits at face, $25M 2y term notes)
    # Equity: $15M ($15,000,000 cents face net worth)
    positions: list[Position] = [
        Position(10_000_000, None, "asset"),  # Cash & reserves (face)
        Position(20_000_000, 12, "asset"),  # 1-year loan
        Position(70_000_000, 120, "asset"),  # 10-year mortgage
        Position(25_000_000, 24, "liability"),  # 2-year term funding
    ]
    deposits_cents = 60_000_000  # $600,000 core deposits
    base_rate_bp = 200  # 2.00% base rate

    print()
    print("B. Economic Value of Equity under Rate Shocks (SIMULATED Book, in cents)")
    base_eve = compute_eve(positions, deposits_cents, base_rate_bp, None)
    print(f"Base EVE (0 bp shift):            {base_eve:10d} cents")

    # Parallel shock +100 bp
    eve_par100 = compute_eve(positions, deposits_cents, base_rate_bp, ParallelShift(100))
    delta_par100 = eve_par100 - base_eve
    print(f"Parallel +100 bp EVE:             {eve_par100:10d} cents (Delta: {delta_par100:+10d} cents)")

    # Parallel shock +200 bp
    eve_par200 = compute_eve(positions, deposits_cents, base_rate_bp, ParallelShift(200))
    delta_par200 = eve_par200 - base_eve
    print(f"Parallel +200 bp EVE:             {eve_par200:10d} cents (Delta: {delta_par200:+10d} cents)")

    # Observed 2022 historical twist shock
    hist_twist = TwistShift(short_bp=short_bp, long_bp=long_bp)
    eve_hist = compute_eve(positions, deposits_cents, base_rate_bp, hist_twist)
    delta_hist = eve_hist - base_eve
    print(f"Observed 2022 Twist EVE:          {eve_hist:10d} cents (Delta: {delta_hist:+10d} cents)")

    print()
    print("C. Mathematical Invariants and Properties")
    # Identity: TwistShift(s, s) must equal ParallelShift(s)
    twist_equal = compute_eve(positions, deposits_cents, base_rate_bp, TwistShift(100, 100))
    print(f"TwistShift(100, 100) equals ParallelShift(100): {twist_equal == eve_par100}")

    # Monotonicity: Asset duration exceeds liability duration -> loss under rate rise
    print(f"EVE loss under +100 bp parallel shock: {delta_par100 < 0}")
    print(f"EVE loss increases monotonically with shock size: {delta_par200 < delta_par100}")

Where the Canon Breaks

The standard EVE duration model makes several critical assumptions that diverge from real-world banking conditions:

  • Face value valuation of core deposits: The baseline EVE model carries non-maturity deposits at par. In reality, sticky below-market retail deposits generate a substantial deposit franchise value that behaves as a natural interest rate hedge, partially insulating economic equity (Drechsler et al. 2021).
  • Run risk and deposit fragility: As Jiang, Matvos, Piskorski & Seru (2024) document, if depositors recognize large unhedged mark-to-market EVE losses on bank securities portfolios, uninsured depositors coordinate and run, rapidly destroying the deposit franchise and forcing fire-sale insolvency.
  • Convexity truncation: The first-order duration approximation ignores positive asset convexity, causing it to overstate EVE losses for very large upward rate shocks.
  • Prepayment optionality: Residential mortgages exhibit negative convexity due to borrower refinancing options, meaning asset duration lengthens precisely as rates rise, amplifying EVE losses.

4Misconceptions

  1. “If a bank intends to hold bonds to maturity (HTM), mark-to-market EVE losses do not matter.”

    Under accounting rules, Held-to-Maturity (HTM) securities are not marked to market in reported net income or regulatory capital. However, economic insolvency is real regardless of accounting labels. If funding costs rise or uninsured depositors withdraw funds, the bank must finance underwater assets at high current market rates or liquidate them, turning unrealized economic losses into realized cash insolvency, as occurred in the 2023 collapse of Silicon Valley Bank .

  2. “A bank with positive accounting book equity cannot have negative economic equity.”

    Book equity records historical cost minus depreciation under accrual rules. When interest rates rise by 300 to 500 basis points, the fair market value of long-term fixed loans and bonds drops by 15% to 30%. Because banks operate with 8% to 10% equity-to-asset ratios, a 15% drop in asset value completely wipes out tangible economic equity, creating negative EVE while book equity remains positive on paper.

  3. “NII sensitivity and EVE sensitivity always move in the same direction.”

    A bank can be asset-sensitive in 1-year NII (\(\Delta \mathrm{NII} > 0\)) due to floating loans repricing in months 1–3, while simultaneously having a huge positive duration gap that generates massive economic equity losses (\(\Delta \mathrm{EVE} < 0\)) due to holding 30-year fixed mortgages. ALCO must manage both metrics simultaneously.

5Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Base EVE computation

A bank holds \$100,000,000 in assets with present value \$95,000,000 and \$85,000,000 in liabilities with present value \$82,000,000. It also holds \$5,000,000 in core deposits carried at face value. Calculate the Economic Value of Equity in dollars.

Solution

By Definition 1.1, \(\mathrm{EVE} = \mathrm{PV}(\text{Assets}) - \mathrm{PV}(\text{Liabilities}) - \text{Deposits} = \$95,000,000 - \$82,000,000 - \$5,000,000 = \$8,000,000\).

M2 · First-order duration EVE loss

A bank has assets of \$200,000,000 with duration \(D_A = 6.0\) years, funded by liabilities of \$180,000,000 with duration \(D_L = 1.5\) years. Using the first-order duration formula, estimate the change in EVE in dollars for a +100 bp (+0.01) parallel rate shift.

Solution

By Theorem 2.1, \(\Delta \mathrm{EVE} \approx -(D_A A - D_L L) \Delta r = -[6.0 \times \$200\text{M} - 1.5 \times \$180\text{M}] \times 0.01 = -[\$1,200\text{M} - \$270\text{M}] \times 0.01 = -\$930\text{M} \times 0.01 = -\$9,300,000\).

M3 · Monthly compounding discount factor

Calculate the discount factor \(\mathrm{DF}(m)\) for a cash flow maturing at month \(m = 24\) (2 years) under an annual nominal discount rate of 6.00% under monthly compounding. Round to four decimal places.

Solution

Monthly rate is \(r/12 = 0.06 / 12 = 0.005\). The discount factor is \(\mathrm{DF}(24) = (1 + 0.005)^{-24} = (1.005)^{-24} = 0.887185\ldots \approx 0.8872\).

Proof

P1 · Monotonicity of EVE losses under positive duration gap

Prove that if \(D_A A > D_L L\) and discount factors are strictly decreasing in rate \(r\), then \(\Delta \mathrm{EVE} < 0\) for all \(\Delta r > 0\), and \(\Delta \mathrm{EVE}\) is strictly decreasing in \(\Delta r\).

Solution

Let \(\mathrm{EVE}(r) = \sum_i A_i (1 + r/12)^{-m_i} - \sum_j L_j (1 + r/12)^{-m_j}\). Differentiating: \(\frac{d\mathrm{EVE}}{dr} = -\frac{1}{12} [\sum_i m_i A_i (1 + r/12)^{-(m_i+1)} - \sum_j m_j L_j (1 + r/12)^{-(m_j+1)}] = -(D_A A - D_L L)\). If \(D_A A - D_L L > 0\), then \(\frac{d\mathrm{EVE}}{dr} < 0\) for all \(r\). By the mean value theorem, \(\Delta \mathrm{EVE} = \mathrm{EVE}(r + \Delta r) - \mathrm{EVE}(r) = \frac{d\mathrm{EVE}}{dr}(\xi) \Delta r < 0\) for any \(\Delta r > 0\). Furthermore, \(\frac{d}{d(\Delta r)} \Delta \mathrm{EVE} = \frac{d\mathrm{EVE}}{dr}(r + \Delta r) < 0\), proving strict loss monotonicity.

P2 · Equivalence of equal-shift twist and parallel shock

Prove that for any twist specification \(\mathrm{Twist}(s, l)\), setting \(s = l\) yields a rate shift function that is identically constant for all tenors \(m \ge 0\), reproducing the parallel shift \(\mathrm{Parallel}(s)\).

Solution

From Definition 2.3: For \(m \le 3\), \(\Delta r(m) = s\). For \(m \ge 120\), \(\Delta r(m) = l = s\). For \(3 < m < 120\), \(\Delta r(m) = s + (l - s) \frac{m - 3}{117}\). When \(s = l\), the interpolation term \((l - s) = 0\), so \(\Delta r(m) = s + 0 = s\). Therefore, \(\Delta r(m) = s\) for all \(m \ge 0\), which is the exact definition of a parallel shift of size \(s\).

P3 · Taylor remainder bound on EVE

Prove that if asset and liability cash flows are positive bullet payments, EVE is strictly convex in discount rate \(r\) whenever \(C_A A > C_L L\), ensuring that first-order duration approximations strictly overestimate EVE losses for upward rate shocks.

Solution

The second derivative of EVE is \(\mathrm{EVE}''(r) = A''(r) - L''(r) = C_A A - C_L L\). If \(C_A A > C_L L\), then \(\mathrm{EVE}''(r) > 0\), meaning EVE is strictly convex. By Taylor's theorem, \(\mathrm{EVE}(r_0 + \Delta r) = \mathrm{EVE}(r_0) + \mathrm{EVE}'(r_0)\Delta r + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{EVE}''(\xi)(\Delta r)^2\). Since the remainder \(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{EVE}''(\xi)(\Delta r)^2 > 0\), the actual shocked EVE is strictly greater than the first-order linear approximation: \(\mathrm{EVE}_{\text{actual}} > \mathrm{EVE}_{\text{linear}}\). Thus, the linear duration estimate predicts a larger decline than actually occurs, strictly overestimating losses.

Research

R1 · Jiang et al. (2024) and the 2023 banking panic

How do Jiang, Matvos, Piskorski & Seru (2024) quantify aggregate mark-to-market EVE losses across the U.S. banking system following the 2022 Federal Reserve tightening cycle?

Solution

Jiang et al. (2024) analyze call report balance sheets across 4,800 U.S. banks. They find that the 2022 rate hikes generated over \$2.2 trillion in aggregate mark-to-market losses on bank asset portfolios (loans and held-to-maturity securities). For 10% of banks, unhedged mark-to-market losses exceeded their entire tangible common equity capital, creating systemic run vulnerability among uninsured depositors.

R2 · Deposit franchise as an EVE asset

According to Drechsler, Savov & Schnabl (2021), why does accounting EVE understate a bank's true economic franchise value during rising interest rate environments?

Solution

Standard EVE models treat deposits as par liabilities with zero duration. Drechsler et al. (2021) show that market power enables banks to pay sticky below-market deposit rates. The present value of this future interest savings constitutes a "deposit franchise asset" with substantial positive duration. When interest rates rise, the franchise asset value expands, acting as a natural economic hedge that partially immunizes total equity value.

R3 · Uninsured depositor runs and coordination failure

Explain why uninsured depositors face a prisoner's dilemma coordination failure when a bank's mark-to-market EVE approaches zero.

Solution

Because bank deposits operate under first-come, first-served sequential service, depositors who withdraw first receive 100 cents on the dollar, while those who wait absorb fire-sale liquidation losses. When uninsured depositors recognize that unhedged asset EVE losses threaten bank solvency, withdrawing immediately becomes the dominant individual strategy, converting solvency deterioration into an uncontrollable deposit run.
