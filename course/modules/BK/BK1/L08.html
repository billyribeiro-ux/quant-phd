Liquidity risk is the risk that a solvent bank cannot honor immediate cash outflow demands without incurring catastrophic liquidation losses. During the 2007–2008 financial crisis, banks holding nominally adequate regulatory capital suffered runs when short-term wholesale funding markets froze. In response, the Basel Committee created the first binding international quantitative liquidity standards: the 30-day Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and the one-year Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR). Quantitative asset-liability managers must calibrate both metrics, balancing high-quality liquid asset yields against acute run-off stress, structural maturity transformation, and modern digital run velocity.

1The Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)

While capital adequacy protects against credit and accounting insolvency, liquidity regulation protects against cash insolvency. The Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) ensures that a bank maintains an unencumbered buffer of High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) capable of being converted into cash to survive an acute 30-day stress scenario specified by regulators .

Definition 1.1 (liquidity coverage ratio)

The liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) is the ratio of a bank's stock of unencumbered High-Quality Liquid Assets (\(\mathrm{HQLA}\)) to its total net cash outflows over a 30-day standardized stress horizon:

\[ \mathrm{LCR} = \frac{\mathrm{HQLA}}{\text{Total Net Cash Outflows over 30 Days}} \ge 100\% \]

where the statutory requirement demands \(\mathrm{LCR} \ge 100\%\) (10,000 basis points) at all times .

The stock of HQLA is categorized into asset tiers based on liquidity, market depth, and central bank eligibility:

HQLA Category Eligible Instruments Regulatory Haircut Portfolio Cap
Level 1 Assets Central bank reserves, 0% risk-weight sovereign debt 0% No cap (minimum 60% of total HQLA)
Level 2A Assets 20% risk-weight sovereign debt, qualifying investment-grade non-financial corporate bonds (AA- or higher) 15% Level 2A + Level 2B capped at 40% of total HQLA
Level 2B Assets Qualifying investment-grade corporate debt (BBB- to A+), qualifying major equity indices, residential MBS 50% Level 2B capped at 15% of total HQLA

Total Net Cash Outflows over the 30-day horizon are calculated as gross stressed contractual and contingent outflows minus capped contractual inflows:

\[ \text{Total Net Cash Outflows} = \sum (\text{Outflow Exposure}_i \times \text{Run-off Rate}_i) - \min\left(\sum (\text{Inflow Exposure}_j \times \text{Inflow Rate}_j), \; 0.75 \times \text{Gross Outflows}\right) \]

The regulatory 75% cap on inflows ensures that banks cannot rely exclusively on anticipated maturing receivables to fund acute deposit withdrawals.

Liability / Commitment Category Standardized Basel III 30-Day Run-off Rate
Fully Insured Stable Retail Deposits (transactional / relationship) 5% (or 3% in select jurisdictions)
Less Stable / Uninsured Retail Deposits 10%
Operational Wholesale Deposits (clearing, custody, cash management) 25%
Non-Operational Wholesale Funding from Non-Financial Corporates 40%
Non-Operational Wholesale Funding from Financial Institutions 100%
Undrawn Committed Credit / Liquidity Facilities to Non-Financials 10% – 30%

2The Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)

While the LCR addresses acute 30-day survival, the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) addresses structural maturity transformation by requiring banks to maintain a durable medium-to-long-term funding profile over a one-year horizon .

Definition 2.1 (net stable funding ratio)

The net stable funding ratio (NSFR) is the ratio of a bank's Available Stable Funding (\(\mathrm{ASF}\)) to its Required Stable Funding (\(\mathrm{RSF}\)) over an ongoing one-year horizon:

\[ \mathrm{NSFR} = \frac{\mathrm{Available\ Stable\ Funding\ (ASF)}}{\mathrm{Required\ Stable\ Funding\ (RSF)}} \ge 100\% \]

where the statutory requirement enforces \(\mathrm{NSFR} \ge 100\%\) (10,000 basis points) .

Available Stable Funding (ASF): The capital and liabilities expected to remain reliable over a one-year time horizon. Each liability category is multiplied by an ASF factor reflecting funding stickiness:

  • 100% ASF: Regulatory capital (Tier 1 and Tier 2) and other liabilities with effective residual maturity of one year or more.
  • 90% – 95% ASF: "Stable" insured retail and small business demand deposits.
  • 50% ASF: Unsecured wholesale funding and non-operational deposits from non-financial corporate customers with residual maturity under one year.
  • 0% ASF: Short-term wholesale funding from financial institutions with residual maturity under six months.

Required Stable Funding (RSF): The assets and off-balance-sheet exposures requiring stable funding based on their liquidity and liquidation horizon. Each asset is multiplied by an RSF factor:

  • 0% RSF: Central bank cash, reserves, and Level 1 sovereign debt (which can be immediately monetized in repo markets).
  • 50% RSF: Operational loans to non-financial corporates and retail customers with residual maturity under one year.
  • 65% RSF: Residential mortgages with residual maturity of one year or more.
  • 85% RSF: Long-term unrated commercial loans with residual maturity of one year or more.
  • 100% RSF: Non-performing loans, physical fixed assets, and equity investments.

3Dual Liquidity Invariants and Numerical Mechanics

Proposition 3.1 (dual liquidity feasibility)

Let a bank operate a balance sheet with high-quality liquid assets \(\mathrm{HQLA}\), 30-day net outflows \(\mathrm{Out}_{30}\), available stable funding \(\mathrm{ASF}\), and required stable funding \(\mathrm{RSF}\). The bank simultaneously satisfies regulatory LCR and NSFR standards if and only if :

\[ \mathrm{HQLA} \ge \mathrm{Out}_{30} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{ASF} \ge \mathrm{RSF} \]

Furthermore, under an incremental retail deposit run of volume \(\Delta D\) with LCR run-off parameter \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) and NSFR stability factor \(\gamma \in [0, 1]\), the post-shock liquidity ratios satisfy:

\[ \mathrm{LCR}_{\text{post}} = \frac{\mathrm{HQLA} - \Delta D}{\mathrm{Out}_{30} + \alpha \Delta D - \Delta D} \quad \text{(if funded from HQLA)} \] \[ \mathrm{NSFR}_{\text{post}} = \frac{\mathrm{ASF} - \gamma \Delta D}{\mathrm{RSF}} \]
Proof

The conditions \(\mathrm{LCR} \ge 100\%\) and \(\mathrm{NSFR} \ge 100\%\) directly translate to \(\mathrm{HQLA} / \mathrm{Out}_{30} \ge 1 \iff \mathrm{HQLA} \ge \mathrm{Out}_{30}\) and \(\mathrm{ASF} / \mathrm{RSF} \ge 1 \iff \mathrm{ASF} \ge \mathrm{RSF}\), since all denominators are strictly positive for operating commercial banks.

When retail deposits contract by \(\Delta D\), if the bank pays depositors out of its central bank cash reserves (Level 1 HQLA), HQLA is reduced dollar-for-dollar by \(\Delta D\). Simultaneously, available stable funding decreases by \(\gamma \Delta D\), where \(\gamma\) is the deposit's ASF factor (e.g. 0.90 for stable retail deposits), leaving RSF unchanged as long as illiquid loan assets are not liquidated. This establishes the post-shock ratio formulas. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.1 (the 2023 social media run velocity and LCR calibration)

The Basel III LCR models run-off behavior as a 30-day cumulative process, assuming retail outflows do not exceed 5%–10% and uninsured corporate deposits run at 40%. In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank lost $42 billion in deposits in approximately 10 hours (an annualized run rate exceeding 80% per day) via digital mobile banking and social-media-driven depositor coordination . This empirical event proved that 30-day LCR metrics can severely underestimate intra-day digital run velocity.

"""Lab BK1-L08-1: Liquidity regulation — Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR).

The Basel III framework introduces two complementary liquidity standards:
1. Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR): Short-term 30-day survival under acute stress:
       LCR = HQLA / Total Net Cash Outflows over 30 Days >= 100%
   where HQLA comprises Level 1 (0% haircut) and Level 2A (15% haircut) assets,
   and net outflows equal gross outflows minus inflows (capped at 75% of outflows).

2. Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR): Structural 1-year maturity transformation constraint:
       NSFR = Available Stable Funding (ASF) / Required Stable Funding (RSF) >= 100%

This lab evaluates:
1. Base HQLA, 30-day net outflows, and LCR on a SIMULATED balance sheet (in cents).
2. Base Available Stable Funding, Required Stable Funding, and NSFR.
3. Impact on LCR when retail deposit stress escalates (outflow rate rises from 5% to 25%).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

# --- LCR Inputs in cents ---
# Liquid Assets
CASH_RESERVES_CENTS = 25_000_000  # Level 1 (0% haircut) -> 100% value
SOVEREIGN_BONDS_CENTS = 35_000_000  # Level 1 (0% haircut) -> 100% value
CORPORATE_BONDS_CENTS = 20_000_000  # Level 2A (15% haircut) -> 85% value

# 30-Day Outflow Liabilities
RETAIL_DEPOSITS_CENTS = 120_000_000  # Base run-off: 5%
WHOLESALE_DEPOSITS_CENTS = 60_000_000  # Non-operational: 40% run-off
UNDRAWN_COMMITTED_CENTS = 40_000_000  # Committed facilities: 10% draw

# 30-Day Contractual Inflows
CONTRACTUAL_INFLOWS_CENTS = 15_000_000  # Cap applies at 75% of outflows

# --- NSFR Inputs in cents ---
CAPITAL_AND_LONG_DEBT = 40_000_000  # 100% ASF factor
STABLE_RETAIL_DEPOSITS = 120_000_000  # 90% ASF factor
SHORT_WHOLESALE_FUNDING = 40_000_000  # 0% ASF factor

LEVEL1_LIQUID_ASSETS = 60_000_000  # 0% RSF factor
MORTGAGES_LONG = 70_000_000  # 65% RSF factor
COMMERCIAL_LOANS_LONG = 70_000_000  # 85% RSF factor


def compute_hqla() -> int:
    """Total High-Quality Liquid Assets in cents."""
    lvl1 = CASH_RESERVES_CENTS + SOVEREIGN_BONDS_CENTS
    lvl2a = Fraction(CORPORATE_BONDS_CENTS * 85, 100)
    return int(lvl1 + lvl2a)


def compute_net_outflows(retail_run_pct: int) -> int:
    """Total 30-day net outflows in cents given retail deposit run-off percent."""
    out_retail = Fraction(RETAIL_DEPOSITS_CENTS * retail_run_pct, 100)
    out_wholesale = Fraction(WHOLESALE_DEPOSITS_CENTS * 40, 100)
    out_commit = Fraction(UNDRAWN_COMMITTED_CENTS * 10, 100)
    gross_outflows = out_retail + out_wholesale + out_commit

    # Inflow cap: max 75% of gross outflows
    cap = gross_outflows * Fraction(75, 100)
    capped_inflows = min(Fraction(CONTRACTUAL_INFLOWS_CENTS, 1), cap)
    net_outflows = gross_outflows - capped_inflows
    return int(net_outflows)


def compute_nsfr() -> tuple[int, int, int]:
    """Compute (ASF_cents, RSF_cents, nsfr_ratio_bp)."""
    asf = (
        Fraction(CAPITAL_AND_LONG_DEBT, 1) * 1
        + Fraction(STABLE_RETAIL_DEPOSITS, 1) * Fraction(90, 100)
        + Fraction(SHORT_WHOLESALE_FUNDING, 1) * 0
    )
    rsf = (
        Fraction(LEVEL1_LIQUID_ASSETS, 1) * 0
        + Fraction(MORTGAGES_LONG, 1) * Fraction(65, 100)
        + Fraction(COMMERCIAL_LOANS_LONG, 1) * Fraction(85, 100)
    )
    ratio_bp = (asf * 10_000) / rsf
    return int(asf), int(rsf), int(ratio_bp)


def main() -> None:
    print("A. Liquidity Coverage Ratio (30-Day Stress, SIMULATED, in cents)")
    hqla = compute_hqla()
    net_outflows_base = compute_net_outflows(retail_run_pct=5)
    lcr_base_bp = int(Fraction(hqla, net_outflows_base) * 10_000)

    print(f"Total HQLA (after haircuts):      {hqla:11d} cents")
    print(f"Base 30-Day Net Outflows (5% run): {net_outflows_base:11d} cents")
    print(f"Base LCR:                         {lcr_base_bp:5d} bp ({lcr_base_bp / 100:.2f}%) [Min = 10000 bp / 100%]")

    print()
    print("B. Severe Retail Deposit Stress Scenario (Run-off rises to 25%)")
    net_outflows_stress = compute_net_outflows(retail_run_pct=25)
    lcr_stress_bp = int(Fraction(hqla, net_outflows_stress) * 10_000)
    print(f"Stressed 30-Day Net Outflows:     {net_outflows_stress:11d} cents")
    print(f"Stressed LCR:                     {lcr_stress_bp:5d} bp ({lcr_stress_bp / 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"LCR deficit below 100% regulatory standard: {lcr_stress_bp < 10_000}")

    print()
    print("C. Net Stable Funding Ratio (1-Year Horizon, SIMULATED, in cents)")
    asf, rsf, nsfr_bp = compute_nsfr()
    print(f"Available Stable Funding (ASF):   {asf:11d} cents")
    print(f"Required Stable Funding (RSF):    {rsf:11d} cents")
    print(f"NSFR:                             {nsfr_bp:5d} bp ({nsfr_bp / 100:.2f}%) [Min = 10000 bp / 100%]")

    print()
    print("D. Invariant Checks")
    print(f"Base LCR satisfies regulatory standard: {lcr_base_bp >= 10_000}")
    print(f"Base NSFR satisfies regulatory standard: {nsfr_bp >= 10_000}")
    print(f"Severe deposit run strictly reduces LCR: {lcr_stress_bp < lcr_base_bp}")

Where the Canon Breaks

The Basel III liquidity framework is built upon institutional assumptions that broke down during the 2023 banking failures:

  • The Intra-Day Velocity Break: The LCR assumes liquidity stress develops over 30 calendar days. With modern instant-payment rails (FedNow, RTP) and smartphone banking apps, billions of dollars can flee a bank within seconds, transforming a 30-day liquidity challenge into an immediate intra-day settlement failure .
  • HQLA Monetization and Fire-Sale Contagion: Long-duration sovereign bonds are counted at 100% value (0% haircut) in Level 1 HQLA. However, if market interest rates have risen sharply, selling these bonds to meet depositor withdrawals forces the immediate accounting realization of massive mark-to-market losses, instantly destroying CET1 regulatory capital and triggering regulatory closure .
  • Siloed Liquidity and Capital Regulation: LCR treats liquidity independently from capital adequacy. In practice, liquidity runs are triggered by market perceptions of unhedged mark-to-market capital insolvency. A bank with 200% LCR can still experience a fatal run if uninsured depositors learn that its true economic value of equity is negative.

4Misconceptions

  1. “LCR and NSFR measure identical risks over different time horizons.”

    LCR measures acute short-term cash flow survival under severe systemic and idiosyncratic stress over 30 days. NSFR measures the structural equilibrium of the balance sheet, ensuring that long-term illiquid assets are funded by durable, non-volatile funding sources over a one-year horizon. A bank can have an excellent NSFR (long-term structural match) yet fail an LCR test if it lacks immediate unencumbered cash to survive a sudden 30-day liquidity squeeze.

  2. “All Level 1 HQLA assets can be liquidated with zero financial cost during a liquidity crisis.”

    Level 1 HQLA (such as US Treasuries) possesses zero credit default risk and receives a 0% regulatory haircut under LCR. However, Level 1 assets carry substantial duration and interest rate risk. If rates have risen, liquidating $10 billion of 10-year Treasuries incurs large capital losses, wiping out equity even though the LCR metric was superficially satisfied.

  3. “A bank with an LCR of 150% is immune to bank runs.”

    The LCR standard tests against an assumed 5% to 10% run on retail deposits and a 40% run on non-operational wholesale funds. In 2023, regional banks experienced runs exceeding 50% of total deposits within 48 hours. When empirical run rates dwarf regulatory parameters, an LCR of 150% is depleted in days.

5Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · High-Quality Liquid Asset Haircut Calculation

A commercial bank holds $40 million in central bank reserves, $60 million in domestic sovereign bonds, $30 million in qualifying Level 2A corporate bonds (15% haircut), and $20 million in qualifying Level 2B equities (50% haircut). Calculate the bank's total eligible HQLA, verifying that the 40% cap on Level 2 assets is satisfied.

Solution

Level 1 assets (0% haircut) are:

\[ \text{Level 1} = 40 + 60 = \$100\text{ million} \]

Level 2A post-haircut value is \(30 \times (1 - 0.15) = 30 \times 0.85 = \$25.5\text{ million}\). Level 2B post-haircut value is \(20 \times (1 - 0.50) = \$10.0\text{ million}\). Total tentative Level 2 assets \(= 25.5 + 10.0 = \$35.5\text{ million}\).

Under Basel III rules, Level 2 assets cannot exceed 40% of total HQLA. With Level 1 at $100M, the maximum allowed Level 2 is \(\frac{40}{60} \times 100 = \$66.67\text{M}\). Since $35.5M is less than $66.67M, no cap adjustment is binding. Total eligible HQLA is:

\[ \mathrm{HQLA} = 100 + 35.5 = \$135.5\text{ million} \]
M2 · LCR Inflow Cap Application

A bank calculates gross 30-day cash outflows of $80 million and gross contractual cash inflows of $70 million. Calculate the capped inflow amount allowed under Basel III and determine the total net cash outflows.

Solution

Basel III caps contractual cash inflows at 75% of total gross cash outflows:

\[ \text{Inflow Cap} = 0.75 \times \$80\text{M} = \$60\text{ million} \]

Because gross inflows of $70 million exceed the $60 million cap, the allowable inflow is capped at $60 million. Total net cash outflows are:

\[ \text{Net Cash Outflows} = \text{Gross Outflows} - \text{Capped Inflows} = 80 - 60 = \$20\text{ million} \]
M3 · NSFR Calculation with Changing Liabilities

A bank possesses $200 million in Required Stable Funding (RSF). Its initial Available Stable Funding (ASF) is $220 million (NSFR = 110%). The bank experiences a rollover failure: $30 million of 6-month wholesale debt (carrying an initial 50% ASF factor) matures and cannot be replaced, being paid off using central bank cash reserves (which carry 0% RSF). Compute the new NSFR.

Solution

When the $30 million wholesale debt matures, ASF decreases by its weighted factor:

\[ \Delta \mathrm{ASF} = -30 \times 0.50 = -\$15\text{ million} \implies \mathrm{ASF}_{\text{new}} = 220 - 15 = \$205\text{ million} \]

Paying off the debt reduces cash reserves by $30 million. Because cash reserves carry a 0% RSF factor, Required Stable Funding is unaffected: \(\mathrm{RSF}_{\text{new}} = \$200\text{ million}\). The new NSFR is:

\[ \mathrm{NSFR}_{\text{new}} = \frac{205}{200} = 1.025 = 102.5\% = 10,250\text{ bp} \]

The bank remains compliant above the 100% threshold.

Proof

P1 · Monotonicity of LCR in Run-off Parameters

Let net outflows be given by \(\mathrm{Out}(\alpha) = \sum \alpha_i L_i - I_{\text{cap}}\). Prove that \(\mathrm{LCR}\) is strictly decreasing in any individual run-off parameter \(\alpha_k\) for which the inflow cap is non-binding.

Solution

When the inflow cap is non-binding, contractual inflows \(I < 0.75 \sum \alpha_i L_i\), so \(\mathrm{Out} = \sum \alpha_i L_i - I\). The LCR is:

\[ \mathrm{LCR}(\alpha_k) = \frac{\mathrm{HQLA}}{\sum_{i \ne k} \alpha_i L_i + \alpha_k L_k - I} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\alpha_k\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathrm{LCR}}{\partial \alpha_k} = \frac{-\mathrm{HQLA} \cdot L_k}{\left(\sum \alpha_i L_i - I\right)^2} \]

Since \(\mathrm{HQLA} > 0\), \(L_k > 0\), and the denominator is squared, the derivative is strictly negative: \(\partial \mathrm{LCR} / \partial \alpha_k < 0\). Higher run-off rates monotonically erode the liquidity coverage ratio.

P2 · Invariance of NSFR to Cash-Funded Level 1 Purchases

Prove that if a bank uses central bank cash reserves to purchase Level 1 sovereign bonds, its Net Stable Funding Ratio remains identically unchanged.

Solution

Both central bank reserves and Level 1 sovereign bonds carry a regulatory Required Stable Funding factor of \(\mathrm{RSF}_{\text{factor}} = 0\%\). Let cash of amount \(X\) be transferred into sovereign bonds. The change in Required Stable Funding is:

\[ \Delta \mathrm{RSF} = -X \times 0.0 + X \times 0.0 = 0 \]

Because the transaction involves only asset side reallocations and no change in liabilities or equity, \(\Delta \mathrm{ASF} = 0\). Thus, \(\mathrm{NSFR}_{\text{post}} = \mathrm{ASF} / \mathrm{RSF} = \mathrm{NSFR}_{\text{pre}}\), proving invariance.

P3 · Bounds on LCR under Maximum Inflow Relief

Prove that for any bank with gross outflows \(G > 0\) and arbitrary inflows \(I \ge 0\), the net outflows satisfy \(\text{Net Outflows} \ge 0.25 \, G\), and hence \(\mathrm{LCR} \le 4.0 \times \frac{\mathrm{HQLA}}{G}\).

Solution

By the Basel III inflow cap rule, allowable inflows are \(\min(I, 0.75 G) \le 0.75 G\). Therefore, net outflows satisfy:

\[ \text{Net Outflows} = G - \min(I, 0.75 G) \ge G - 0.75 G = 0.25 G \]

Substituting this lower bound into the LCR definition:

\[ \mathrm{LCR} = \frac{\mathrm{HQLA}}{\text{Net Outflows}} \le \frac{\mathrm{HQLA}}{0.25 G} = 4.0 \times \frac{\mathrm{HQLA}}{G} \]

This establishes that contractual inflows can never boost LCR by more than a factor of four relative to gross outflows.

Empirical

E1 · The 2023 Regional Banking Run Speed

During the March 2023 banking panic, Silicon Valley Bank suffered $42 billion of deposit withdrawals in a single business day against $173 billion in total deposits. Calculate the single-day empirical run-off rate and compare it to the Basel III 30-day LCR parameter for uninsured deposits (40%).

Solution

The single-day empirical withdrawal percentage was:

\[ \text{Single-Day Run Rate} = \frac{\$42\text{B}}{\$173\text{B}} \approx 0.2428 = 24.28\% \]

Under Basel III LCR, regulators assume a 40% cumulative run-off over an entire 30-day stress window (approximately 1.33% per day linearly). SVB experienced more than half of that 30-day stress volume in less than 10 hours, demonstrating that digital banking platforms and social media coordination amplify withdrawal velocity far beyond traditional Basel stress calibration, as analyzed by Jiang et al. (2024).

E2 · HQLA Liquidation and Realized Capital Losses

A bank facing an LCR deficit of $10 billion chooses to liquidate $10 billion of 10-year US Treasury bonds held at amortized cost in its HTM portfolio. Due to an unhedged 250 bp increase in benchmark yields, the bonds trade at an average market price of $0.85 per dollar of par. Calculate the realized loss on sale and evaluate its impact on CET1 capital.

Solution

Selling the bonds generates cash proceeds of \(10 \times 0.85 = \$8.5\text{ billion}\), realizing an immediate accounting loss of:

\[ \text{Realized Loss} = \$10\text{B} - \$8.5\text{B} = \$1.5\text{ billion} \]

This $1.5 billion loss flows directly into the income statement, immediately reducing retained earnings and CET1 equity capital dollar-for-dollar. In monetizing HQLA to satisfy cash demands, the bank converts an unrealized duration loss into immediate regulatory capital impairment.

E3 · Operational vs Non-Operational Wholesale Deposit Arbitrage

A large corporate client maintains $100 million at a commercial bank. If classified as a non-operational wholesale deposit, it carries a 40% LCR run-off factor. If classified as an operational deposit tied to payroll clearing, it carries a 25% run-off factor. Calculate the reduction in required HQLA if the bank successfully structures the account as operational.

Solution

Under non-operational treatment, the 30-day outflow is \(100 \times 0.40 = \$40\text{ million}\). Under operational treatment, the 30-day outflow is \(100 \times 0.25 = \$25\text{ million}\). Structuring the relationship to qualify as operational reduces net cash outflows by $\$40\text{M} - \$25\text{M} = \$15\text{ million}\), allowing the bank to release $15 million of low-yielding HQLA into higher-yielding commercial lending without reducing its reported LCR.

R1 · Deposit Beta and Structural Funding Stability

How does a bank's empirical deposit beta (\(\beta\)) influence its ability to maintain NSFR compliance during an aggressive monetary tightening cycle?

Solution

When market rates rise, a bank with a low deposit beta (\(\beta = 0.20\)) pays sticky below-market rates. While this widens net interest margin, it creates strong incentives for depositors to move funds to higher-yielding money market funds ("cash sorting"). If retail deposits flee, the bank loses 90% ASF funding and must replace it with short-term wholesale borrowing carrying a 0% ASF factor. Consequently, maintaining a low deposit beta during rapid rate hikes can paradoxically degrade NSFR stability through deposit disintermediation.

R2 · Systemic Hoarding of Level 1 HQLA in Crises

Explain why the introduction of the 100% LCR requirement can cause systemic liquidity freezes in interbank lending markets during financial panics.

Solution

Because bank regulators penalize any breach of the 100% LCR floor with heightened supervision and reputational stigma, bank treasurers treat the 100% minimum as a hard barrier rather than a usable buffer. During a crisis, banks hoard central bank reserves and Level 1 sovereign debt to preserve their reported LCR buffers, refusing to lend surplus cash to peer institutions in unsecured interbank markets, thereby accelerating systemic liquidity contagion.

R3 · Central Bank Discount Window Pre-Positioning

Under proposed regulatory liquidity reforms post-2023, regulators recommend requiring banks to pre-position pledged collateral at the Federal Reserve Discount Window. Evaluate how discount window borrowing capacity interacts with LCR metrics.

Solution

Currently, Basel III LCR rules do not count undrawn central bank discount window capacity as HQLA. Pre-positioning commercial loans at the discount window allows banks to convert illiquid 85% RSF assets into immediate central bank reserves within minutes. Allowing certified discount window collateral to count toward emergency liquidity backstops would bridge the gap between 30-day LCR models and modern intra-day digital run velocity.
