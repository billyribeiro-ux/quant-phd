Net Interest Margin (NIM) is the primary operational benchmark of commercial banking profitability under accrual accounting. It quantifies the net yield generated by a bank's interest-earning assets over its interest-bearing liabilities. Because assets and liabilities reprice at asymmetric speeds during monetary tightening or easing cycles, NIM fluctuates continuously with market interest rates. Deconstructing NIM into asset yields, funding costs, and leverage exposes whether a bank's earnings will expand or compress when central banks alter policy rates. This lesson formalizes the algebraic mechanics of NIM sensitivity under rate resets.

1Definition and Decomposition of Net Interest Margin

Under the accounting principles of AC1, bank revenues are dominated by interest income accrued on commercial loans, mortgages, and investment securities, while expenses are dominated by interest accrued on customer deposits, repo borrowings, and long-term debt.

Definition 1.1 (net interest income and net interest margin)

Let Net Interest Income (\(\mathrm{NII}\)) over accounting period \([0, t]\) be the difference between total gross interest income (\(\mathrm{II}\)) and total gross interest expense (\(\mathrm{IE}\)):

\[ \mathrm{NII} = \mathrm{II} - \mathrm{IE} \]

The net interest margin (\(\mathrm{NIM}\)) is the ratio of annualized Net Interest Income to Average Earning Assets (\(\mathrm{AEA}\)):

\[ \mathrm{NIM} = \frac{\mathrm{NII}}{\mathrm{AEA}} = \frac{\mathrm{II} - \mathrm{IE}}{\mathrm{AEA}} \]

Earning assets exclude non-interest-bearing cash in vault, non-earning central bank reserves, and fixed physical assets.

Proposition 1.2 (NIM yield-spread decomposition)

Let \(y_A = \frac{\mathrm{II}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\) be the effective gross yield on average earning assets, and let \(y_L = \frac{\mathrm{IE}}{\mathrm{IBL}}\) be the effective cost of interest-bearing liabilities (\(\mathrm{IBL}\)). Then NIM decomposes algebraically into:

\[ \mathrm{NIM} = y_A - y_L \cdot \left(\frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\right) \]

Furthermore, defining the net interest spread as \(s = y_A - y_L\), NIM can be expressed as:

\[ \mathrm{NIM} = s + y_L \cdot \left(1 - \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\right) \]
Proof

From Definition 1.1, \(\mathrm{NIM} = \frac{\mathrm{II} - \mathrm{IE}}{\mathrm{AEA}} = \frac{\mathrm{II}}{\mathrm{AEA}} - \frac{\mathrm{IE}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\). By definition, \(\frac{\mathrm{II}}{\mathrm{AEA}} = y_A\). Multiplying and dividing the second term by \(\mathrm{IBL}\) yields \(\frac{\mathrm{IE}}{\mathrm{IBL}} \cdot \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}} = y_L \cdot \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\). Subtracting this establishes the first formula. For the second, add and subtract \(y_L\): \(\mathrm{NIM} = y_A - y_L + y_L - y_L \cdot \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}} = (y_A - y_L) + y_L \left(1 - \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\right) = s + y_L \left(1 - \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\right)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.3 (the free-funding endowment effect)

Because every solvent bank has positive equity and non-interest-bearing liabilities (e.g. demand deposits), \(\mathrm{AEA} > \mathrm{IBL}\), so the funding ratio \(\frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}} < 1\). The term \(y_L \cdot (1 - \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}) > 0\) is the endowment effect: the additional margin earned by funding earning assets with free equity and zero-cost deposits rather than interest-bearing liabilities.

2Asymmetric Repricing and NIM Sensitivity

When interest rates change by \(\Delta r\), earning assets and liabilities adjust according to their respective repricing fractions \(\alpha_A \in [0, 1]\) and \(\alpha_L \in [0, 1]\). Assets that are fixed-rate over the horizon have \(\alpha = 0\); assets that reprice immediately have \(\alpha = 1\).

Proposition 1.4 (NIM rate sensitivity)

If market interest rates experience a parallel shock \(\Delta r\), effective asset yield changes by \(\Delta y_A = \alpha_A \Delta r\) and liability cost changes by \(\Delta y_L = \alpha_L \Delta r\). The resulting change in Net Interest Margin is:

\[ \Delta \mathrm{NIM} = \left[\alpha_A - \alpha_L \cdot \left(\frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\right)\right] \Delta r \]

The bank is asset-sensitive if \(\alpha_A > \alpha_L \cdot \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\), in which case rising rates expand NIM. The bank is liability-sensitive if \(\alpha_A < \alpha_L \cdot \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\), in which case rising rates compress NIM.

Proof

Differentiating Proposition 1.2 with respect to the rate shift: \(\Delta \mathrm{NIM} = \Delta y_A - \Delta y_L \cdot \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}} = (\alpha_A \Delta r) - (\alpha_L \Delta r) \cdot \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}} = \left[\alpha_A - \alpha_L \cdot \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\right] \Delta r\). If the bracketed term is positive, \(\Delta \mathrm{NIM}\) has the same sign as \(\Delta r\). If negative, \(\Delta \mathrm{NIM}\) has the opposite sign. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.5 (evaluating NIM under rate shock)

A bank has \(\mathrm{AEA} = \$1,000,000\) earning \(5.50\%\) and \(\mathrm{IBL} = \$800,000\) paying \(2.50\%\). The ratio \(\frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}} = 0.80\). Base NIM is \(5.50\% - 2.50\% \times 0.80 = 5.50\% - 2.00\% = 3.50\%\) (350 bp). If \(\alpha_A = 0.40\) and \(\alpha_L = 0.70\), under a +100 bp (+0.01) shock, \(\Delta \mathrm{NIM} = [0.40 - 0.70 \times 0.80] \times (+0.01) = [0.40 - 0.56] \times 0.01 = -0.16\% = -16\) bp. New NIM is \(3.50\% - 0.16\% = 3.34\%\) (334 bp).

Lab 3 · NIM Dynamics under Asymmetric Repricing

Verifying that for a liability-sensitive bank with 40% asset repricing and 70% liability repricing on an 80% funding ratio, a +100 bp rate rise compresses NIM by exactly 16 bp, from 350 bp to 334 bp.

"""Lab BK1-L03-1: Net interest margin (NIM) and interest rate resets.

Net interest margin (NIM) measures the net yield generated by a bank's earning assets:
    NIM = (Interest Income - Interest Expense) / Average Earning Assets = NII / AEA

When interest rates change, NIM responds according to the relative repricing speeds
of assets versus liabilities. If liabilities reprice faster than assets (liability-sensitive),
rising interest rates squeeze the net interest margin.

This lab simulates:
1. Base balance sheet (SIMULATED, in cents):
   - Average Earning Assets (AEA): 100,000,000 cents ($1,000,000) earning 5.50% (550 bp)
   - Interest-Bearing Liabilities (IBL): 80,000,000 cents ($800,000) paying 2.50% (250 bp)
   - Equity: 20,000,000 cents ($200,000)
2. Asymmetric repricing speeds:
   - Asset repricing share: 40% (60% fixed)
   - Liability repricing share: 70% (30% fixed)
3. Rate shock scenarios: +100 bp, +200 bp, -100 bp.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

AEA_CENTS = 100_000_000  # $1,000,000 average earning assets
IBL_CENTS = 80_000_000  # $800,000 interest-bearing liabilities
BASE_ASSET_YIELD_BP = 550  # 5.50%
BASE_LIAB_COST_BP = 250  # 2.50%

ASSET_REPRICING_SHARE = Fraction(40, 100)  # 40%
LIAB_REPRICING_SHARE = Fraction(70, 100)  # 70%


def compute_nim(shock_bp: int) -> dict[str, int]:
    """Calculate annual interest income, expense, NII, and NIM in basis points."""
    # New effective rates in bp as Fractions
    effective_asset_bp = Fraction(BASE_ASSET_YIELD_BP, 1) + ASSET_REPRICING_SHARE * Fraction(shock_bp, 1)
    effective_liab_bp = Fraction(BASE_LIAB_COST_BP, 1) + LIAB_REPRICING_SHARE * Fraction(shock_bp, 1)

    income = Fraction(AEA_CENTS, 1) * (effective_asset_bp / Fraction(10_000, 1))
    expense = Fraction(IBL_CENTS, 1) * (effective_liab_bp / Fraction(10_000, 1))
    nii = income - expense
    nim_bp = (nii / Fraction(AEA_CENTS, 1)) * Fraction(10_000, 1)

    return {
        "shock_bp": shock_bp,
        "income_cents": int(income),
        "expense_cents": int(expense),
        "nii_cents": int(nii),
        "nim_bp": int(nim_bp),
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("A. Net Interest Margin under Asymmetric Repricing (SIMULATED, in cents)")
    base = compute_nim(0)
    print(
        f"Base (0 bp)  | Income: {base['income_cents']:8d} | Expense: {base['expense_cents']:8d} | "
        f"NII: {base['nii_cents']:8d} | NIM: {base['nim_bp']:4d} bp"
    )

    shocks = [100, 200, -100]
    for s in shocks:
        res = compute_nim(s)
        delta_nii = res["nii_cents"] - base["nii_cents"]
        delta_nim = res["nim_bp"] - base["nim_bp"]
        print(
            f"Shock {s:+4d} bp | Income: {res['income_cents']:8d} | Expense: {res['expense_cents']:8d} | "
            f"NII: {res['nii_cents']:8d} ({delta_nii:+7d}) | NIM: {res['nim_bp']:4d} bp ({delta_nim:+3d} bp)"
        )

    print()
    print("B. NIM Sensitivity Invariant Checks")
    # Verify exact NIM formula: NIM = y_A - y_L * (IBL / AEA)
    # At base: 550 - 250 * (80/100) = 550 - 200 = 350 bp
    expected_base_nim = BASE_ASSET_YIELD_BP - int(BASE_LIAB_COST_BP * Fraction(IBL_CENTS, AEA_CENTS))
    print(f"Base NIM equals theoretical formula (350 bp): {base['nim_bp'] == expected_base_nim}")

    # For liability-sensitive bank (IBL repricing share > AEA repricing share),
    # positive rate shock compresses NIM:
    res_pos = compute_nim(100)
    print(f"Positive rate shock compresses NIM for liability-sensitive book: {res_pos['nim_bp'] < base['nim_bp']}")


main()

The lab confirms the analytical formula. Under a +100 bp rate shock, interest income rises by 400,000 cents while interest expense rises by 560,000 cents, reducing annual NII from 3,500,000 to 3,340,000 cents (-160,000 cents) and lowering NIM by 16 bp. Where the Canon Breaks

The standard NIM formula is an accrual accounting measure with important analytical limitations:

  • Ignores economic capital mark-to-market: NIM measures accounting income over a 12-month period. A bank can report a positive, stable NIM while simultaneously suffering catastrophic economic value of equity (EVE) losses as long-term fixed assets lose market value.
  • Deposit migration and product switching: The model assumes a fixed funding ratio \(\frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\). In reality, when rates rise, depositors migrate balances from zero-interest checking accounts into high-yield certificates of deposit, driving \(\mathrm{IBL}\) and \(y_L\) up faster than modeled.
  • Credit loss interaction: Earning assets are not risk-free. When monetary policy tightens, floating-rate borrowers face higher debt service burdens, increasing loan delinquency rates and charge-offs that erode net interest revenue.
  • Static asset volumes: Banks do not maintain static balance sheets. Loan origination volume typically contracts when interest rates rise, shrinking the average earning asset denominator.

3Misconceptions

  1. “Higher interest rates are always beneficial for commercial bank profitability.”

    Popular commentary frequently asserts that banks prosper when interest rates rise. As Proposition 1.4 proves, whether a rate increase benefits a bank depends entirely on its asset-liability repricing structure. If liabilities reprice faster than assets (liability-sensitive), rising interest rates compress NIM and reduce net interest income.

  2. “Net Interest Margin is identical to Net Interest Spread.”

    Net interest spread is simply \(y_A - y_L\). Net interest margin is \(\frac{\mathrm{II} - \mathrm{IE}}{\mathrm{AEA}} = y_A - y_L \cdot (\frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}})\). Because earning assets exceed interest-bearing liabilities (\(\mathrm{AEA} > \mathrm{IBL}\)), NIM is strictly greater than spread whenever liability cost \(y_L > 0\) due to the endowment effect (Proposition 1.2).

  3. “A high NIM proves that a bank has sound interest rate risk management.”

    A bank can achieve an artificially elevated NIM by originating high-risk subprime loans or extending asset maturities to 30 years to capture term premium. However, such a strategy dramatically heightens credit risk and duration risk, leaving the bank vulnerable to sudden insolvency during yield curve shocks.

4Problem Set

Mechanics

M1 · Base NIM computation

A bank reports \$40,000,000 in gross interest income and \$18,000,000 in gross interest expense over a fiscal year. Its Average Earning Assets are \$500,000,000. Calculate Net Interest Income in dollars and Net Interest Margin in percent and basis points.

Solution

Net Interest Income is \(\mathrm{NII} = \mathrm{II} - \mathrm{IE} = \$40,000,000 - \$18,000,000 = \$22,000,000\). Net Interest Margin is \(\mathrm{NIM} = \frac{\$22,000,000}{\$500,000,000} = 0.0440 = 4.40\%\) or exactly 440 basis points.

M2 · Yield spread vs NIM

A bank has \(\mathrm{AEA} = \$200,000,000\) earning 6.00% and \(\mathrm{IBL} = \$150,000,000\) costing 3.00%. Calculate the net interest spread \(s\) and the Net Interest Margin in basis points.

Solution

Spread is \(s = y_A - y_L = 6.00\% - 3.00\% = 3.00\%\) (300 bp). NIM is \(y_A - y_L \cdot (\frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}) = 6.00\% - 3.00\% \times \frac{150}{200} = 6.00\% - 2.25\% = 3.75\%\) (375 bp). NIM exceeds spread by 75 bp due to the endowment effect of free equity/deposits.

M3 · Rate shock impact on NIM

Using Example 1.5 parameters (\(\mathrm{AEA} = \$1\text{M}\), \(\mathrm{IBL} = \$800\text{k}\), \(\alpha_A = 0.60\), \(\alpha_L = 0.50\)), calculate \(\Delta \mathrm{NIM}\) in basis points for a +100 bp rate rise. State whether the bank is asset-sensitive or liability-sensitive.

Solution

By Proposition 1.4, \(\Delta \mathrm{NIM} = [\alpha_A - \alpha_L \times (\frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}})] \times \Delta r = [0.60 - 0.50 \times 0.80] \times 0.01 = [0.60 - 0.40] \times 0.01 = +0.0020 = +20\) bp. Because \(\Delta \mathrm{NIM} > 0\), the bank is asset-sensitive.

Proof

P1 · Strict positivity of the endowment effect

Prove that for any bank with strictly positive equity capital and non-negative deposit liabilities, \(\mathrm{NIM} > s\) whenever liability borrowing cost \(y_L > 0\).

Solution

By balance sheet articulation, \(\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}\). Since \(\text{Equity} > 0\), \(\text{Total Assets} > \text{Total Liabilities} \ge \mathrm{IBL}\). Hence \(\mathrm{AEA} > \mathrm{IBL}\), which implies \(\frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}} < 1\), so \(1 - \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}} > 0\). From Proposition 1.2, \(\mathrm{NIM} = s + y_L (1 - \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}})\). If \(y_L > 0\), the product \(y_L (1 - \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}) > 0\), strictly proving \(\mathrm{NIM} > s\).

P2 · Break-even asset repricing speed

Given liability repricing fraction \(\alpha_L\) and funding ratio \(\rho = \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}}\), prove that the critical asset repricing fraction \(\alpha_A^*\) that immunizes NIM against parallel rate shifts (\(\Delta \mathrm{NIM} = 0\)) is \(\alpha_A^* = \alpha_L \cdot \rho\).

Solution

From Proposition 1.4, \(\Delta \mathrm{NIM} = (\alpha_A - \alpha_L \cdot \rho) \Delta r\). Setting \(\Delta \mathrm{NIM} = 0\) for arbitrary non-zero \(\Delta r\) requires \(\alpha_A - \alpha_L \cdot \rho = 0\), which yields directly \(\alpha_A^* = \alpha_L \cdot \rho\). If \(\alpha_A = \alpha_A^*\), the dollar increase in asset interest income identically offsets the dollar increase in liability interest expense.

P3 · Bounds on NIM rate sensitivity

Assuming \(\alpha_A, \alpha_L \in [0, 1]\) and funding ratio \(\rho = \frac{\mathrm{IBL}}{\mathrm{AEA}} \in (0, 1)\), prove that the rate sensitivity coefficient \(\beta_{\mathrm{NIM}} = \frac{\Delta \mathrm{NIM}}{\Delta r}\) is strictly bounded: \(-\rho \le \beta_{\mathrm{NIM}} \le 1\).

Solution

\(\beta_{\mathrm{NIM}} = \alpha_A - \alpha_L \cdot \rho\). To minimize \(\beta_{\mathrm{NIM}}\), set \(\alpha_A = 0\) (fully fixed assets) and \(\alpha_L = 1\) (fully floating liabilities), yielding minimum \(\beta_{\min} = 0 - 1 \cdot \rho = -\rho\). To maximize \(\beta_{\mathrm{NIM}}\), set \(\alpha_A = 1\) (fully floating assets) and \(\alpha_L = 0\) (fully fixed liabilities), yielding maximum \(\beta_{\max} = 1 - 0 \cdot \rho = 1\). Thus \(-\rho \le \beta_{\mathrm{NIM}} \le 1\).

E2 · +200 bp shock compression

According to Lab 3 Part A, what is the NII change in cents and NIM change in basis points under a +200 bp parallel shock?

Under a +200 bp shock, NII falls to 3,180,000 cents, representing a \(\Delta \mathrm{NII}\) of -320,000 cents (-$3,200). NIM falls to 318 bp, representing a compression of exactly -32 basis points.

E3 · Rate cut asymmetry

Using Lab 3 Part A, what happens to NIM when interest rates fall by -100 bp? Explain the economic reason for the direction of change.

Solution

NIM expands by +16 bp to 366 bp, and NII increases by +160,000 cents ($1,600). Because the bank is liability-sensitive (\(\alpha_L \cdot \rho = 0.56 > 0.40 = \alpha_A\)), funding costs fall faster than asset yields, widening profit margins.

Research

R1 · Deposit beta and the deposits channel

How does Drechsler, Savov & Schnabl's (2017) empirical findings on the "deposits channel" explain why retail bank funding costs do not rise 1-for-1 with Federal Reserve policy rate hikes?

Solution

Drechsler, Savov & Schnabl (2017) document that retail banking markets are imperfectly competitive. Banks exercise local market power by paying sticky deposit rates that lag policy rate hikes, resulting in deposit betas substantially below 1.0 (typically 0.20 to 0.40). This allows banks to capture a widening deposit franchise margin during tightening cycles, offsetting asset duration losses.

R2 · NIM compression in European negative rates

During the ECB's negative interest rate policy (NIRP) period (2014–2022), European bank NIMs experienced severe structural compression. Explain the economic mechanism using Proposition 1.2.

Solution

Under NIRP, asset yields \(y_A\) followed sovereign yields below zero, while retail deposit rates were bounded at a zero lower bound (\(y_L \approx 0\)) due to the threat of physical cash hoarding. With \(y_A\) falling but \(y_L\) unable to fall symmetrically, the net interest spread \(s = y_A - y_L\) compressed drastically, destroying bank net interest margins.

R3 · Accounting NIM vs Economic Value of Equity

Critique why relying exclusively on 1-year NIM projections misled management and supervisors prior to the failure of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023.

Solution

NIM is an accrual metric reflecting interest cash flows over a short accounting horizon (12 months), ignoring capital value changes. SVB invested heavily in 10-year fixed-rate Treasuries and MBS. As rates rose, 1-year NIM appeared manageable initially, but the market value of fixed-rate assets plunged by over $15 billion, completely wiping out tangible common equity and triggering a run by uninsured depositors.
