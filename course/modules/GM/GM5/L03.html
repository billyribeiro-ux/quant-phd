Currency Pegs, Speculative Attacks & Asymmetric Defenses

Fixed exchange rate regimes promise macroeconomic stability, exchange rate predictability, and imported monetary credibility. Yet currency pegs frequently terminate in violent speculative attacks, discrete devaluation gaps, and systemic financial turmoil. Classical first-generation models formalize reserve depletion when fiscal deficits monetize debt, while second-generation models demonstrate how market expectations create self-fulfilling run equilibria. Crucially, defending against appreciation differs fundamentally from defending against depreciation. Analyzing these asymmetric mechanisms across landmark episodes is indispensable for global macro traders.

1. First-Generation vs. Second-Generation Speculative Attack Models

A currency peg is a central bank commitment to buy and sell foreign exchange at a fixed nominal parity \(\bar{s}\). In international monetary economics, crises of fixed parities are categorized into fundamental versus self-fulfilling frameworks.

Definition 3.1 (currency-peg abandonment) Currency-peg abandonment is the formal termination or non-discretionary capitulation of a central bank's fixed exchange rate commitment, resulting in an unconstrained float, a discrete devaluation, or a band re-alignment when defense costs exceed institutional capacity.

In Krugman's (1979) first-generation model, a government runs a persistent fiscal deficit financed by continuous domestic credit expansion \(\mu = \dot{D} > 0\). Money market equilibrium requires: \[ M_t = D_t + R_t = P_t L(Y, i_t) \] where \(D_t\) is domestic credit, \(R_t\) is foreign reserves, \(P_t\) is domestic price level, and \(i_t\) is domestic nominal interest rate. Under purchasing power parity (\(P_t = S_t P^*\)) and uncovered interest parity, maintaining the peg \(\bar{s}\) requires the central bank to sell foreign reserves at rate \(\dot{R} = -\mu\) to absorb excess domestic liquidity. Rather than reserves drifting smoothly to zero, rational speculators anticipate the post-collapse floating exchange rate—the shadow exchange rate \(\tilde{s}_t\): \[ \tilde{s}_t = \frac{D_t + \eta \mu}{\alpha} \] where \(\eta\) is the interest semi-elasticity of money demand. Because no-arbitrage prohibits an anticipated discrete jump in the exchange rate at the attack moment, speculators launch a coordinated run at the exact instant \(T^*\) when \(\tilde{s}_{T^*} = \bar{s}\), exhausting the remaining reserve stock \(R(T^*)\) in a single speculative surge.

In Obstfeld's (1996) second-generation model, crises occur even when foreign reserves are ample. The central bank optimizes an objective loss function trading off exchange rate stability against domestic economic distress (such as unemployment or mortgage debt distress caused by high interest rates). If speculators do not attack, the government maintains the peg with modest interest rates. If speculators attack, the government must raise interest rates to punitive levels (e.g. 15% or 500%) to defend the peg. The domestic economic pain of high interest rates alters the government's cost-benefit calculation, leading to self-fulfilling peg abandonment.

2. The Asymmetric Peg Dilemma: Ceilings vs. Floors

Defending an overvalued peg (resisting currency depreciation) is structurally asymmetric to defending an undervalued peg (resisting currency appreciation):

Theorem 3.1 (Asymmetry of Foreign Exchange Intervention Capacity) Let \(R_t\) denote a central bank's liquid foreign exchange reserves, and let \(M_t\) denote domestic currency liabilities.
1. Ceiling Defense (Resisting Depreciation): Intervention capacity is strictly bounded by available liquid foreign reserves: \[ \int_0^T \text{Intervention}_t \, dt \le R_0 + \text{SwapLines} \] When reserves reach exhaustion, peg abandonment is mathematically compulsory.
2. Floor Defense (Resisting Appreciation): The central bank sells domestic currency to purchase foreign assets. Because a central bank has infinite legal capacity to issue domestic liabilities, intervention capacity is theoretically unbounded: \[ \int_0^T \text{Intervention}_t \, dt < \infty \] However, abandonment occurs endogenously when balance-sheet valuation risk and domestic liquidity expansion exceed political tolerance.

Proof Sketch A central bank cannot print foreign legal tender (USD or EUR); it can only sell foreign currency from existing reserve stocks or official reciprocal central bank swap lines. Thus, depreciation defense is bounded by \(R_0 < \infty\). Conversely, to resist appreciation, the central bank credits commercial bank reserve accounts in domestic currency, which it creates at zero marginal cost. However, foreign asset accumulation expands the central bank's balance sheet relative to GDP. A subsequent appreciation of the domestic currency inflicts mark-to-market losses on foreign reserves, threatening central bank equity capital. This establishes the structural asymmetry.

The 2015 Swiss Franc (CHF) De-Peg Episode

In September 2011, the Swiss National Bank (SNB) introduced a minimum exchange rate of 1.20 CHF per EUR to counter severe deflationary appreciation pressures during the Eurozone sovereign debt crisis. For over three years, the SNB defended the 1.20 floor by purchasing hundreds of billions of euros. By late 2014, as the European Central Bank prepared to launch sovereign Quantitative Easing (QE), capital inflows into Switzerland surged. Defending the floor required the SNB's balance sheet to expand beyond 80% of Swiss GDP (SNB 2015). Recognizing that continuing the intervention would create uncontrollable balance-sheet expansion and catastrophic valuation losses upon eventual exit, the SNB abruptly discontinued the minimum exchange rate on January 15, 2015. Within minutes, the EUR/CHF exchange rate plunged by over 20% to parity (and temporarily below 0.85), wiping out major retail FX brokers and causing immense dealer losses.

3. Primary Case Studies: 1992 ERM & 1997 Asia

1992 European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) Black Wednesday

In October 1990, the United Kingdom joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism, committing to maintain the British Pound within a \(\pm 6\%\) band around 2.95 Deutsche Marks per Pound. Following German reunification, the Deutsche Bundesbank raised interest rates aggressively to combat domestic inflation. The Bank of England was caught in an impossible dilemma: domestic recession and high unemployment required interest rate cuts, while defending the ERM parity required matching German rate hikes. Anticipating that the UK government could not politically sustain prolonged high rates, speculative capital (notably George Soros's Quantum Fund) launched massive short positions. On September 16, 1992 ("Black Wednesday"), despite raising minimum lending rates from 10% to 12% and announcing a further hike to 15%, and burning £3.3 billion in foreign reserves in hours, the UK government suspended ERM membership and allowed sterling to float, immediately devaluing by 15%.

1997 Asian Financial Crisis

Prior to 1997, East Asian economies (Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia) maintained quasi-fixed exchange rate pegs against the US dollar. Rapid economic growth attracted massive short-term foreign currency capital inflows ("hot money"). As analyzed by Calvo (1998), domestic banks and corporations accumulated severe currency and maturity mismatches: borrowing short-term USD to fund long-term domestic real estate and industrial projects. When the US dollar appreciated and export growth slowed, speculative attacks targeted the Thai Baht. The Bank of Thailand depleted its foreign reserves through forward market commitments. On July 2, 1997, Thailand was forced to abandon the peg. The baht devalued by over 50%, triggering a "double drain" (a simultaneous domestic bank run and sovereign currency run) across Southeast Asia (Reinhart & Rogoff 2009).

4. Inline Lab: Simulating Krugman Speculative Attack Dynamics

The lab below simulates the Krugman-Obstfeld speculative attack model, tracking domestic credit expansion, shadow exchange rate divergence, and the exact day of reserve exhaustion.

Lab 1 · Currency Peg Attack & Reserve Depletion

Simulating persistent domestic credit growth against a fixed peg demonstrates that foreign reserves bleed at an accelerating exponential rate as the shadow exchange rate approaches the peg, triggering sudden de-peg and a discrete devaluation jump.

"""Lab GM5-L03-1: Currency Peg Abandonment & Speculative Attack Dynamics.

Simulates the Krugman (1979) and Obstfeld (1996) speculative attack framework,
modeling reserve depletion, shadow exchange rates, and post-abandonment jumps.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from typing import Any


def simulate_speculative_attack(
    peg_rate: float,
    initial_reserves: float,
    domestic_credit_growth: float,
    money_demand_semi_elasticity: float,
    speculative_capital_flow_rate: float,
    n_days: int = 60,
) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """Simulate central bank reserve depletion and shadow exchange rate dynamics.

    Parameters
    ----------
    peg_rate : float
        Fixed exchange rate parity (e.g. 1.20 CHF per EUR or GBP parity).
    initial_reserves : float
        Foreign exchange reserves in billions USD.
    domestic_credit_growth : float
        Daily domestic credit expansion rate (driving fundamental divergence).
    money_demand_semi_elasticity : float
        Interest rate semi-elasticity of money demand (eta).
    speculative_capital_flow_rate : float
        Sensitivity of speculative outflows to shadow exchange rate premium.
    n_days : int
        Simulation horizon in days.

    Returns
    -------
    dict[str, float | int | list[float]]
        Trajectory of reserves, shadow rates, attack day, and de-peg devaluation jump.
    """
    reserves = [initial_reserves]
    shadow_rates = [peg_rate * 0.95]
    realized_rates = [peg_rate]
    attack_day = -1
    peg_intact = True

    current_r = initial_reserves
    domestic_credit = 100.0

    for day in range(1, n_days + 1):
        domestic_credit += domestic_credit_growth

        # Shadow floating rate s_tilde_t = m_t + eta * mu (Krugman 1979)
        # where shadow rate rises as domestic credit expands
        shadow_s = peg_rate * (1.0 + (domestic_credit - 100.0) / 200.0)
        shadow_rates.append(shadow_s)

        if peg_intact:
            # Baseline reserve drain from trade/macro imbalance
            baseline_drain = domestic_credit_growth * 0.40

            # Speculative attack intensity escalates when shadow rate approaches or exceeds peg
            premium = max(0.0, (shadow_s - peg_rate) / peg_rate)
            speculative_drain = speculative_capital_flow_rate * (math.exp(premium * 25.0) - 1.0)
            total_drain = baseline_drain + speculative_drain

            current_r = max(0.0, current_r - total_drain)
            reserves.append(current_r)

            # Central bank abandons peg when reserves breach minimum threshold or speculative flow overwhelms
            if current_r <= 5.0 or total_drain > 15.0:
                peg_intact = False
                attack_day = day
                realized_rates.append(shadow_s)
            else:
                realized_rates.append(peg_rate)
        else:
            # Post-abandonment floating exchange rate tracks shadow rate
            reserves.append(current_r)
            realized_rates.append(shadow_s)

    devaluation_jump_pct = ((realized_rates[attack_day] - peg_rate) / peg_rate) * 100.0 if attack_day > 0 else 0.0

    return {
        "attack_day": attack_day,
        "terminal_reserves": reserves[-1],
        "terminal_exchange_rate": realized_rates[-1],
        "devaluation_jump_pct": devaluation_jump_pct,
        "reserves_trajectory": reserves,
        "realized_rates": realized_rates,
    }


def main() -> None:
    peg = 1.20  # e.g., CHF minimum exchange rate (1.20 per EUR)
    r0 = 100.0  # 100 billion foreign reserves

    results = simulate_speculative_attack(
        peg_rate=peg,
        initial_reserves=r0,
        domestic_credit_growth=0.80,
        money_demand_semi_elasticity=2.5,
        speculative_capital_flow_rate=0.50,
        n_days=45,
    )

    print("Peg Exchange Rate:", round(peg, 4))
    print("Initial Reserves ($B):", round(r0, 1))
    print("Speculative Attack Day:", results["attack_day"])
    print("Terminal Reserves ($B):", round(float(results["terminal_reserves"]), 2))
    print("Terminal Exchange Rate:", round(float(results["terminal_exchange_rate"]), 4))
    print("Devaluation Jump (%):", round(float(results["devaluation_jump_pct"]), 2))


main()
Peg Exchange Rate: 1.2
Initial Reserves ($B): 100.0
Speculative Attack Day: 31
Terminal Reserves ($B): 0.0
Terminal Exchange Rate: 1.416
Devaluation Jump (%): 12.4

The numerical simulation confirms that on Day 31, speculative capital outflows overwhelm central bank reserves ($0.0B terminal), forcing peg abandonment and generating an instantaneous 12.4% devaluation jump.

5. Where the Canon Breaks: The Impossible Trinity

The standard macroeconomic paradigm formalizes the Mundell-Fleming Impossible Trinity (or Trilemma): a nation cannot simultaneously maintain all three of the following:

  • A fixed exchange rate parity.
  • Free international capital mobility.
  • An independent sovereign monetary policy.

Where the classical canon breaks is assuming that capital controls or macroprudential FX restrictions reliably preserve monetary independence. In modern financial markets with sophisticated cross-currency swaps, non-deliverable forwards (NDFs), and multinational supply chains, speculative capital circumvents physical capital controls through trade misinvoicing and synthetic derivative overlays.

6. Misconceptions

  1. A central bank with positive foreign exchange reserves cannot be forced to abandon its currency peg.

    Second-generation models demonstrate that governments abandon pegs long before reserves reach zero because the interest rate defense inflicts severe collateral damage on the domestic banking system, housing market, and employment.

  2. Defending against currency appreciation can continue indefinitely because the central bank prints domestic money.

    As the Swiss National Bank demonstrated in 2015, resisting appreciation forces massive expansion of central bank balance sheets, exposing the institution to catastrophic equity losses and uncontrollable domestic monetary expansion.

  3. Speculative attacks are caused purely by evil speculators destroying otherwise viable economies.

    Speculators can only profit if the underlying economic fundamentals or policy objectives are inconsistent with the fixed parity. Speculative capital acts as a catalyst that accelerates the resolution of an existing fundamental disequilibrium.

7. Problem Set

In a fixed exchange rate regime with peg \(\bar{s} = 1.50\) USD per GBP, the domestic price level is \(P = 150\) and foreign price level is \(P^* = 100\). Calculate the real exchange rate \(q = \frac{S \cdot P^*}{P}\) and determine whether the currency is overvalued or undervalued.

Solution

The real exchange rate is \(q = \frac{1.50 \times 100}{150} = \frac{150}{150} = 1.00\). If domestic inflation rises such that \(P = 180\) while \(P^* = 100\) and \(S\) is pegged at 1.50, then \(q = \frac{150}{180} = 0.833\), meaning the domestic currency is substantially overvalued in real terms, eroding export competitiveness.

A central bank holds $20 billion in foreign reserves. Due to capital flight, reserves drain at $800 million per day. Assuming no interest rate response or intervention acceleration, how many days can the peg survive?

Solution

Under linear depletion, the survival horizon is \(T = \frac{R_0}{\dot{R}} = \frac{20,000}{800} = 25\text{ days}\). In practice, speculative attacks accelerate non-linearly as reserves dwindle, collapsing the peg much earlier than 25 days.

If the Swiss franc minimum exchange rate was 1.20 CHF per EUR, and EUR/CHF gapped to 0.96 immediately following the SNB de-peg announcement, calculate the percentage appreciation of the Swiss franc.

Solution

The value of one Swiss franc rose from \(\frac{1}{1.20} = 0.8333\text{ EUR}\) to \(\frac{1}{0.96} = 1.0417\text{ EUR}\). The percentage appreciation of CHF against EUR is \(\frac{1.0417 - 0.8333}{0.8333} \times 100\% = 25.0\%\) instantaneous appreciation.

Prove that in Krugman's (1979) model, the speculative attack must occur at the exact point where the shadow exchange rate equals the fixed parity (\(\tilde{s}_{T^*} = \bar{s}\)).

Solution

Suppose the attack occurred when \(\tilde{s} < \bar{s}\). Speculators acquiring foreign reserves at \(\bar{s}\) would immediately suffer a capital loss upon floating at \(\tilde{s}\), so no rational speculator would attack. If the attack occurred when \(\tilde{s} > \bar{s}\), speculators would earn an instantaneous arbitrage profit \(\tilde{s} - \bar{s} > 0\). Competition among speculators to capture this gain shifts the attack time earlier until \(\tilde{s}_{T^*} = \bar{s}\) by backward induction.

Show that in Obstfeld's (1996) model with multiple equilibria, an increase in the cost of high domestic interest rates expands the range of fundamentals where a self-fulfilling crisis can occur.

Solution

Let government loss be \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{peg}} = \theta (i - i^*)^2\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{float}} = C\) (the political cost of devaluation). If market expectations set \(i = i^* + \Delta s^e\), the defense cost is \(\theta (\Delta s^e)^2\). Increasing the domestic sensitivity parameter \(\theta\) makes \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{peg}} > \mathcal{L}_{\text{float}}\) for smaller values of \(\Delta s^e\), expanding the set of fundamental states where speculation forces abandonment.

Prove that a central bank defending against currency appreciation experiences a mechanical equity loss when it abandons the peg and allows the currency to appreciate.

Solution

To defend against appreciation, the central bank accumulates foreign assets \(A^*\) denominated in foreign currency (EUR) while issuing domestic currency liabilities \(L\) in domestic currency (CHF). Net equity in domestic currency is \(E_{\text{CB}} = S_t A^* - L\). When the peg is abandoned and the domestic currency appreciates, the spot rate \(S_t\) (CHF per EUR) drops, causing an immediate reduction in \(S_t A^*\) and driving \(E_{\text{CB}}\) lower or negative.

According to official Swiss National Bank reports, what was the ratio of SNB foreign exchange reserves to Swiss annual GDP by December 2014?

Solution

As documented in SNB (2015), cumulative foreign currency purchases had expanded the SNB's balance sheet to over 500 billion CHF, exceeding 80% of Switzerland's gross domestic product and making continued defense mathematically unsustainable.

In Calvo (1998), what is the definition and primary consequence of a "sudden stop" in emerging market capital flows?

Solution

A sudden stop is an abrupt, unexpected cessation of foreign capital inflows paired with large outflows. It forces an immediate compression in the current account deficit, requiring sharp domestic consumption collapse and precipitous currency devaluation.

What level did Bank of England minimum lending rates reach on the morning of September 16, 1992 prior to floating the pound?

Solution

The Chancellor of the Exchequer authorized a rate hike from 10% to 12% at 11:00 AM, and announced an intended further hike to 15% later that afternoon, but market selling continued unabated, forcing suspension from the ERM by 7:30 PM.

Design a systematic trading strategy using options to express an asymmetric view on currency peg abandonment.

Solution

Purchase out-of-the-money put options on the pegged currency (or calls on the foreign currency) funded by selling deep out-of-the-money calls on the wrong side of the peg band. Because peg defense limits upside moves, the short leg carries bounded risk while the long leg captures explosive devaluation gap optionality.

How does the existence of central bank foreign exchange swap lines (such as the Fed swap lines) modify the first-generation exhaustion threshold?

Solution

Bilateral swap lines augment liquid reserves by \(+\text{SwapCapacity}\), shifting the exhaustion boundary outward. However, swap lines create repayment obligations in foreign currency, meaning they bridge temporary liquidity shocks but cannot resolve fundamental solvency or fiscal deficits.

Evaluate the economic consequences of currency board arrangements (such as Hong Kong's HKD peg) vs conventional pegs during global dollar liquidity shortages.

Solution

A currency board maintains 100% foreign reserve backing for the monetary base, eliminating discretionary credit growth. When capital outflows occur, the monetary base shrinks automatically, driving interbank interest rates up and restoring equilibrium, albeit at the cost of extreme domestic real economic volatility.

9. Retrieval Quiz

  1. In Krugman's (1979) first-generation crisis model, why does a speculative attack occur before foreign reserves reach zero naturally?

    Options

    Rational expectations and no-arbitrage prevent discrete exchange rate jumps, forcing the run to occur precisely when the shadow floating rate reaches the fixed parity.

  2. What distinguishes Obstfeld's (1996) second-generation crisis model from first-generation models?

    Options

    Second-generation models incorporate government escape clauses where defensive rate hikes create unemployment, making abandonment self-fulfilling.

  3. Why did the Swiss National Bank abandon its 1.20 CHF per EUR minimum exchange rate on January 15, 2015?

    Options

    The SNB's balance sheet swelled past 80% of GDP, creating immense valuation risk from holding depreciating euros.

  4. What was the primary vulnerability of East Asian economies prior to the 1997 financial crisis?

    Options

    Asian corporations borrowed short-term unhedged USD, leaving them insolvent when currencies devalued.

  5. What is the "Mundell-Fleming Impossible Trinity"?

    Options

    The Trilemma establishes that policy can achieve at most two of the three macroeconomic goals.

  6. What happened on UK "Black Wednesday" (September 16, 1992)?

    Options

    Speculative short sterling positions forced the UK government to suspend ERM membership and float the pound.

  7. Why is defending against currency depreciation structurally more difficult than defending against appreciation?

    Options

    A central bank cannot print foreign legal tender; its depreciation intervention capacity is strictly bounded by foreign reserves.

  8. In Calvo's (1998) analysis, what triggers a "sudden stop"?

    Options

    Sudden stops occur when foreign capital inflows suddenly cease, requiring sharp compression in the current account.
