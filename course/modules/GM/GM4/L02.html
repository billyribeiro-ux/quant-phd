Relative-Value Trades & Curve Structures

Macroeconomic relative-value trading strips out uncompensated market-wide directional beta to isolate pure cross-sectional or term structure anomalies. In fixed income, the first principal component of yield changes—parallel level shifts—accounts for over seventy-five percent of total yield curve variance. Without rigorous duration weighting, an intended curve slope trade degenerates into an unhedged directional bet on central bank policy. Mastering DV01-neutral hedge ratios, butterfly curvature trades, and sovereign spread mechanics allows quantitative macro managers to harvest pure term premia while immunizing capital against broad market turbulence.

1. Principles of Relative-Value Trading

In contrast to directional investing, which wagers on the future level of an asset class, relative-value strategies exploit structural mispricings, policy divergences, or risk premium differentials between economically linked assets while hedging common factor exposures.

Definition 2.1 (relative-value trade) A relative-value trade is a multi-leg trade expression consisting of long and short positions across two or more related instruments, weighted such that the portfolio's net factor loading with respect to dominant systematic risk factors (e.g., market level, equity index returns, or parallel yield shifts) is zero:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \beta_{i, \text{systematic}} = 0 \]

The trade monetizes a localized divergence in expected return, term premium, or relative liquidity while eliminating systematic market directionality.

Relative-value trading rests on the empirical decomposition of asset returns into systematic factor risk and residual spread dynamics. In sovereign yield curves, Litterman and Scheinkman established that three principal components explain over ninety-eight percent of curve variations: Level (parallel shift), Slope (steepening/flattening), and Curvature (butterfly bending) (Adrian, Crump, and Moench 2013; Gürkaynak, Sack, and Wright 2007).

2. Curve Structures: Steepeners, Flatteners, and Butterflies

Yield curve trades allow macro investors to express targeted views on monetary policy cycles, economic re-acceleration, or inflation premia without taking outright interest rate duration risk.

Definition 2.2 (Curve Spread Types) For two tenors \(T_1 < T_2\) with yields \(y_1\) and \(y_2\), the curve slope is \(S_{1,2} = y_2 - y_1\):

  • Bull Steepener: Long front-end (\(T_1\)), short back-end (\(T_2\)) with yields falling across the curve but front-end yields declining faster (\(\Delta y_1 < \Delta y_2 < 0\)). Typical of early central bank monetary easing during recessions.
  • Bear Steepener: Short back-end (\(T_2\)) with long front-end (\(T_1\)) as long-term yields rise faster due to expanding fiscal deficits or term premium spikes (\(\Delta y_2 > \Delta y_1 > 0\)).
  • Bull Flattener: Long back-end (\(T_2\)), short front-end (\(T_1\)) as long yields fall faster, typically driven by flight-to-safety or expectations of future disinflation.
  • Bear Flattener: Short front-end (\(T_1\)), long back-end (\(T_2\)) as central banks aggressively hike policy rates, flattening or inverting the curve (\(\Delta y_1 > \Delta y_2 > 0\)).

A butterfly trade combines three tenors \(T_{\text{short}} < T_{\text{belly}} < T_{\text{long}}\) (e.g., 2Y–5Y–10Y or 5Y–10Y–30Y). The investor takes opposing positions in the "wings" (\(T_{\text{short}}, T_{\text{long}}\)) against the "body" (\(T_{\text{belly}}\)) to capture localized curvature mispricings while immunizing against both parallel shifts and linear slope tilts.

3. Duration Weighting and DV01 Neutrality

Establishing a true relative-value spread requires sizing legs by their dollar price sensitivity to interest rates rather than by nominal par value.

Definition 2.3 (DV01 and Duration) The Dollar Value of an 01 (DV01) is the dollar change in the value of a bond position resulting from a one basis point (\(0.0001 = 10^{-4}\)) parallel increase in yield:

\[ \text{DV01} = -\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \times 0.0001 = P \cdot D_{\text{mod}} \times 0.0001 \]

where \(P\) is the dirty bond price and \(D_{\text{mod}} = \frac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1 + y/m}\) is the modified duration.

Theorem 2.1 (Duration-Neutral Hedge Ratio Invariance) Consider a two-legged spread between leg 1 and leg 2 with respective DV01 sensitivities \(\text{DV01}_1 > 0\) and \(\text{DV01}_2 > 0\) per unit of notional. To ensure that the portfolio P&L has zero first-order sensitivity to a parallel yield curve shock \(\Delta y_1 = \Delta y_2 = \Delta y_{\text{parallel}}\), the ratio of notional positions \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) must satisfy:

\[ \beta_{\text{DV01}} = -\frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{\text{DV01}_1}{\text{DV01}_2} \]

Consequently, the duration-neutral portfolio P&L under arbitrary yield shifts \((\Delta y_1, \Delta y_2)\) isolates the pure non-parallel curve movement:

\[ \Delta \Pi_{\text{spread}} = N_1 \cdot \text{DV01}_1 \cdot (\Delta y_2 - \Delta y_1) \times 10^4 \]

Proof of Theorem 2.1 The first-order dollar P&L of a two-bond portfolio is given by the Taylor expansion:

\[ \Delta \Pi = -\left( N_1 \cdot \text{DV01}_1 \cdot \frac{\Delta y_1}{10^{-4}} + N_2 \cdot \text{DV01}_2 \cdot \frac{\Delta y_2}{10^{-4}} \right) \]

Under a parallel shift \(\Delta y_1 = \Delta y_2 = \Delta y\), the change in portfolio value simplifies to:

\[ \Delta \Pi_{\text{parallel}} = -\frac{\Delta y}{10^{-4}} \left( N_1 \cdot \text{DV01}_1 + N_2 \cdot \text{DV01}_2 \right) \]

Setting \(\Delta \Pi_{\text{parallel}} = 0\) for all non-zero \(\Delta y\) requires the bracketed term to vanish identically:

\[ N_1 \cdot \text{DV01}_1 + N_2 \cdot \text{DV01}_2 = 0 \implies N_2 = -N_1 \frac{\text{DV01}_1}{\text{DV01}_2} \]

Substituting \(N_2 = -N_1 \frac{\text{DV01}_1}{\text{DV01}_2}\) into the general P&L equation under heterogeneous yield shifts yields:

\[ \Delta \Pi = -10^4 \left( N_1 \cdot \text{DV01}_1 \Delta y_1 - N_1 \cdot \text{DV01}_1 \Delta y_2 \right) = N_1 \cdot \text{DV01}_1 \cdot (\Delta y_2 - \Delta y_1) \times 10^4 \]

which is proportional strictly to the change in yield curve slope \(\Delta S = \Delta y_2 - \Delta y_1\), with zero exposure to the parallel level component. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · US Treasury 2s10s Duration-Neutral Steepener Backtest

Evaluates historical US Treasury data from ust_daily.csv (retrieved 2026-10-17) to confirm that DV01-neutral curve weighting isolates slope changes and eliminates correlation with parallel level shocks.

"""Lab GM4-L02-1: Relative-Value Curve Trade: Duration-Neutral Yield Curve Steepener.

Backtests a duration-neutral 3M/10Y Treasury curve steepener using historical US Treasury data

from __future__ import annotations

import csv
from pathlib import Path

import numpy as np


def load_treasury_data(filepath: Path) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, list[str]]:
    dates: list[str] = []
    r_3m: list[float] = []
    r_10y: list[float] = []
    with filepath.open("r", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        reader = csv.DictReader(f)
        for row in reader:
            try:
                val_3m = float(row["tbill_3m"])
                val_10y = float(row["cmt_10y"])
                dates.append(row["date"])
                r_3m.append(val_3m)
                r_10y.append(val_10y)
            except ValueError, KeyError:
                continue
    return np.array(r_3m, dtype=np.float64), np.array(r_10y, dtype=np.float64), dates


def main() -> None:
    data_file = Path("data") / "ust_daily.csv"
    if not data_file.exists():
        data_file = Path("course/assets/data/market/ust_daily.csv")

    rates_3m, rates_10y, dates = load_treasury_data(data_file)
    n_days = len(rates_3m)

    # Yield curve slope: 10Y minus 3M in basis points
    slope_bps = (rates_10y - rates_3m) * 100.0

    # Daily changes in basis points
    delta_3m = np.diff(rates_3m) * 100.0
    delta_10y = np.diff(rates_10y) * 100.0

    # Duration assumptions: 3M duration = 0.25, 10Y duration = 7.85
    dur_3m = 0.25
    dur_10y = 7.85

    # DV01 per $1M notional: dur * 1e-4 * 1,000,000 = dur * 100 ($ per bp)
    dv01_3m = dur_3m * 100.0  # $25 / bp per $1M
    dv01_10y = dur_10y * 100.0  # $785 / bp per $1M

    # Duration-neutral hedge ratio: notional_3m per notional_10y = dv01_10y / dv01_3m
    hedge_ratio = dv01_10y / dv01_3m  # 31.4x notional

    # Steepener portfolio: Long 3M (pays off when short rate drops), Short 10Y (pays off when long rate rises)
    # Daily P&L ($ per $1M 10Y notional) = -notional_10y * dv01_10y * delta_10y + notional_3m * dv01_3m * (-delta_3m)
    # where negative yield change means positive bond return
    # Daily dollar return:
    pnl_steepener = -(dv01_10y * delta_10y) + (hedge_ratio * dv01_3m * (-delta_3m))

    # Compare correlation with parallel shift factor (level = 0.5 * (r_3m + r_10y))
    parallel_shift = 0.5 * (delta_3m + delta_10y)
    slope_change = delta_10y - delta_3m

    corr_level = float(np.corrcoef(pnl_steepener, parallel_shift)[0, 1])
    corr_slope = float(np.corrcoef(pnl_steepener, slope_change)[0, 1])

    mean_pnl = float(np.mean(pnl_steepener))
    std_pnl = float(np.std(pnl_steepener, ddof=1))
    annualized_sr = (mean_pnl / std_pnl) * np.sqrt(252) if std_pnl > 0 else 0.0

    print(f"Historical Sample Dates: {dates[0]} to {dates[-1]}")
    print(f"Total Observations: {n_days}")
    print(f"Initial 10Y-3M Slope: {slope_bps[0]:.1f} bps")
    print(f"Ending 10Y-3M Slope: {slope_bps[-1]:.1f} bps")
    print(f"DV01 Hedge Ratio (3M per 10Y notional): {hedge_ratio:.2f}")
    print(f"Correlation with Level Shift: {corr_level:.4f}")
    print(f"Correlation with Slope Change: {corr_slope:.4f}")
    print(f"Daily P&L Mean ($): {mean_pnl:.2f}")
    print(f"Daily P&L Std ($): {std_pnl:.2f}")
    print(f"Annualized Sharpe Ratio: {annualized_sr:.3f}")

4. Where the Canon Breaks

Classical fixed-income relative-value analysis assumes that static DV01 weighting provides complete immunization against interest rate level risk. In practice, this assumption breaks down under large interest rate shocks due to convexity mismatch and time-varying beta. Because a 10-year Treasury note has substantially higher positive convexity than a 2-year note, a large parallel yield decline causes the 10Y leg's duration to expand faster than the 2Y leg's duration, creating an unintended short duration bias. Furthermore, during monetary regime transitions, empirical yield changes across tenors do not follow parallel shifts; short rates exhibit higher volatility than long rates (\(\sigma(\Delta y_{\text{short}}) > \sigma(\Delta y_{\text{long}})\)), causing simple DV01-neutral portfolios to experience significant directional beta leakages (Ilmanen 2011; Pedersen 2015).

5. Misconceptions

  • Misconception: Equal-notional spreads (e.g., $10M long 2Y and $10M short 10Y) are market-neutral.

    Why it is wrong: A 10-year Treasury note has roughly four times the modified duration of a 2-year note. An equal-notional spread is overwhelmingly short duration (negative DV01), causing the portfolio to suffer massive losses whenever broad interest rates decline, regardless of slope movements.

  • Misconception: A steepener trade always profits when the central bank cuts interest rates.

    Why it is wrong: If market expectations had already priced in a 75 bps emergency rate cut and the central bank delivers only 25 bps, the yield curve violently bear-flattens as front-end yields re-price higher, inflicting severe losses on steepener positions despite the nominal policy cut.

  • Misconception: Yield curve butterfly trades carry zero directional or curve risk.

    Why it is wrong: While a 50/50 DV01 butterfly (e.g., 2Y/5Y/10Y) is immunized against parallel shifts and linear slope tilts, it remains exposed to curve twist, asymmetric volatility, and structural curvature shifts driven by Treasury issuance supply pressures.

Mechanics

Problem 2.1 · DV01 Hedge Ratio Calculation

A portfolio manager holds $25,000,000 notional of a 5-year Treasury note with a modified duration of 4.35 years and a market price of 99.50. The manager hedges this position using a 30-year Treasury bond with a modified duration of 18.20 years and a market price of 96.00. Calculate the exact DV01 per million of both bonds, the duration-neutral hedge ratio, and the required short notional in the 30-year bond.

Worked Solution

1. Compute DV01 per $1M notional: \(\text{DV01} = \text{Price} \times D_{\text{mod}} \times 0.0001\).

For 5Y note: \(\text{DV01}_{5Y} = \$995,000 \times 4.35 \times 10^{-4} = \$432.825\) per $1M.

For 30Y bond: \(\text{DV01}_{30Y} = \$960,000 \times 18.20 \times 10^{-4} = \$1,747.200\) per $1M.

2. Hedge ratio: \(\beta_{\text{DV01}} = \frac{432.825}{1747.200} \approx 0.247725\) (units of 30Y per unit of 5Y).

3. Required 30Y short notional: \(N_{30Y} = -25 \times 0.247725 \times \$1,000,000 = -\$6,193,125\).

Problem 2.2 · Butterfly Spread Weighting

Construct a 2s5s10s Treasury butterfly trade using 2Y, 5Y, and 10Y notes with respective DV01s of $40.00, $90.00, and $160.00 per $1M notional. Sizing the 5Y belly at -$10,000,000 short, determine the long notionals in the 2Y and 10Y wings under a 50/50 DV01 weighting scheme to ensure net zero DV01.

Worked Solution

Total belly DV01 to be hedged: \(10 \times \$90.00 = \$900.00\).

Under a 50/50 DV01 allocation, each wing absorbs half of the belly's DV01: \(\text{DV01}_{\text{wing}} = \$450.00\).

1. 2Y notional: \(N_{2Y} = \frac{\$450.00}{\$40.00} \times \$1,000,000 = \$11,250,000\).

2. 10Y notional: \(N_{10Y} = \frac{\$450.00}{\$160.00} \times \$1,000,000 = \$2,812,500\).

Net DV01 check: \(11.25 \times 40 + 2.8125 \times 160 - 10 \times 90 = 450 + 450 - 900 = 0.00\).

Problem 2.3 · P&L of a Curve Flattener

An investor holds a duration-neutral 2s10s flattener: short $20M of 2Y note (\(\text{DV01} = \$42.00/\$1\text{M}\)) and long $10M of 10Y note (\(\text{DV01} = \$84.00/\$1\text{M}\)). Over a monthly holding period, 2Y yields rise by 35 bps while 10Y yields rise by 15 bps (a 20 bps bear flattening). Calculate the total dollar P&L of the trade.

Worked Solution

Total 2Y DV01: \(20 \times \$42.00 = \$840.00/\text{bp}\). Position is short, so yield rise yields profit:

\(\text{P\&L}_{2Y} = +840 \times 35 = +\$29,400\).

Total 10Y DV01: \(10 \times \$84.00 = \$840.00/\text{bp}\). Position is long, so yield rise yields loss:

\(\text{P\&L}_{10Y} = -840 \times 15 = -\$12,600\).

Total spread P&L: \(\Delta \Pi = \$29,400 - \$12,600 = +\$16,800\).

Equivalently: \(\text{DV01} \times (\Delta y_{2Y} - \Delta y_{10Y}) = 840 \times (35 - 15) = 840 \times 20 = \$16,800\).

Proof

Problem 2.4 · Regression-Based Hedge Ratio Optimality

Let \(\Delta y_1\) and \(\Delta y_2\) be random yield changes with covariance matrix \(\Sigma_y\). Prove that the hedge ratio \(\beta^* = \frac{\Cov(\Delta y_1, \Delta y_2)}{\Var(\Delta y_2)}\) minimizes the variance of the hedged spread P&L, and derive the conditions under which the regression hedge ratio equals the duration-neutral ratio.

Worked Solution

The dollar return of the spread is \(\Delta \Pi = -N_1 \text{DV01}_1 \Delta y_1 - N_2 \text{DV01}_2 \Delta y_2\). Normalizing by \(N_1 \text{DV01}_1 = 1\) and setting \(h = \frac{N_2 \text{DV01}_2}{N_1 \text{DV01}_1}\), the variance is \(\Var(\Delta \Pi) = \Var(\Delta y_1 + h \Delta y_2) = \sigma_1^2 + 2 h \sigma_{12} + h^2 \sigma_2^2\).

Differentiating with respect to \(h\) and setting to zero: \(\frac{d \Var}{d h} = 2 \sigma_{12} + 2 h \sigma_2^2 = 0 \implies h^* = -\frac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_2^2} = -\beta_{\text{reg}}\).

Thus, \(N_2 = -N_1 \frac{\text{DV01}_1}{\text{DV01}_2} \beta_{\text{reg}}\).

The regression hedge ratio equals the duration-neutral ratio if and only if \(\beta_{\text{reg}} = 1.0\), which occurs when the two yields move in perfect lockstep with equal variances (\(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2\) and \(\rho_{12} = 1.0\)), representing an exact parallel shift. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 2.5 · Second-Order Convexity Bias in DV01 Spreads

Prove that for a duration-neutral spread with zero net DV01, the second-order P&L under a large parallel yield shock \(\Delta y\) is strictly positive if the long leg has higher dollar convexity than the short leg.

Worked Solution

The second-order Taylor expansion of portfolio P&L is:

\[ \Delta \Pi = -(\text{DV01}_{\text{net}}) \frac{\Delta y}{10^{-4}} + \frac{1}{2} (\text{Dollar Convexity}_{\text{net}}) (\Delta y)^2 \]

Since the spread is duration-neutral, \(\text{DV01}_{\text{net}} = N_1 \text{DV01}_1 + N_2 \text{DV01}_2 = 0\), eliminating the first-order term.

The dollar convexity of the portfolio is \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{net}} = N_1 P_1 C_1 + N_2 P_2 C_2\), where \(C_i = \frac{1}{P_i} \frac{\partial^2 P_i}{\partial y_i^2}\). If \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{net}} > 0\), then for any non-zero shock \(\Delta y \ne 0\), \((\Delta y)^2 > 0\), which guarantees \(\Delta \Pi = \frac{1}{2} \mathcal{C}_{\text{net}} (\Delta y)^2 > 0\), proving the assertion. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 2.6 · Orthogonality of Principal Component Yield Factors

Prove that if yield curve variations are decomposed via PCA on the yield covariance matrix \(\Sigma_Y = V \Lambda V^\top\), a portfolio whose weights are proportional to an eigenvector \(v_k\) has zero covariance with all other principal component portfolios.

Worked Solution

Let \(\Delta y \in \R^M\) have covariance \(\Sigma_Y\). Since \(\Sigma_Y\) is symmetric real, its spectral decomposition is \(\Sigma_Y = V \Lambda V^\top\), where \(V = [v_1, \dots, v_M]\) is orthogonal (\(V^\top V = I\)) and \(\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_M)\).

Define the \(k\)-th principal component factor portfolio return as \(z_k = v_k^\top \Delta y\). The covariance between portfolio \(j\) and portfolio \(k\) is:

\[ \Cov(z_j, z_k) = \E[(v_j^\top \Delta y)(\Delta y^\top v_k)] = v_j^\top \E[\Delta y \Delta y^\top] v_k = v_j^\top \Sigma_Y v_k \]

Substituting \(\Sigma_Y v_k = \lambda_k v_k\): \(\Cov(z_j, z_k) = v_j^\top (\lambda_k v_k) = \lambda_k (v_j^\top v_k)\).

By orthogonality of the eigenvectors, \(v_j^\top v_k = 0\) for all \(j \ne k\). Therefore, \(\Cov(z_j, z_k) = 0\), proving that the PC factor expressions are mutually uncorrelated. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem 2.7 · Historical Yield Curve Level Variance Share

Using the historical UST daily dataset (2000–2026), suppose the sample covariance matrix between 3M and 10Y yields has eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 = 0.0384\) and \(\lambda_2 = 0.0066\). Calculate the proportion of total yield variance explained by the first principal component (level factor) and interpret its significance for duration hedging.

Worked Solution

Total variance is the trace of the covariance matrix: \(\tr(\Sigma) = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 0.0384 + 0.0066 = 0.0450\).

Variance share of PC1: \(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2} = \frac{0.0384}{0.0450} = 0.8533\) (85.33%).

Because parallel level shifts explain over 85% of total curve variance, an unhedged relative-value expression will have its P&L dominated by directional market shifts rather than relative spread movements.

Problem 2.8 · Sovereign Credit Spread Breakdown (BTP vs Bund)

During the 2011 European sovereign debt crisis, the 10-year Italian BTP yield rose by 250 bps while the 10-year German Bund yield fell by 90 bps due to flight-to-safety. For an investor long $10M BTP hedged with a duration-matched Bund short (\(\text{DV01} = \$850/\text{bp}\)), compute the net P&L and explain why classical duration neutrality failed.

Worked Solution

1. BTP long P&L: \(-850 \times (+250) = -\$212,500\).

2. Bund short P&L: \(+850 \times (-90) = -\$76,500\).

3. Total spread P&L: \(-\$212,500 - \$76,500 = -\$289,000\).

Classical duration neutrality assumes domestic yield shifts are correlated. In sovereign stress, the core-periphery correlation breaks down from positive to negative, causing both the long position and the short hedge to lose money simultaneously.

Problem 2.9 · Roll-Down Contribution in Bull Steepeners

A duration-neutral 2s10s steepener holds long 2Y notes and short 10Y notes. Over a 1-year holding period, the 2Y note rolls down 8 bps (duration 1.95), generating a +15.6 bps roll gain. The 10Y note rolls down 18 bps (duration 8.10), generating a +145.8 bps roll gain. Because the 10Y note is held short, compute the net roll-down drag of the steepener.

Worked Solution

Long 2Y roll gain: \(+1.95 \times 8 = +15.6\) bps.

Short 10Y roll loss (since shorting a rolling bond loses the roll-down gain): \(-8.10 \times 18 = -145.8\) bps.

On a duration-neutral basis (\(N_{10Y} / N_{2Y} \approx 0.24\)):

Net roll contribution = \(+15.6 - 0.24 \times 145.8 = 15.6 - 35.0 = -19.4\) bps.

Steepener trades along an upward-sloping curve incur negative roll-down drag, meaning the curve must steepen by at least 19.4 bps per year just to break even.

Research

Problem 2.10 · Yield Curve Inversion as a Macro Regime Driver

Based on Estrella and Mishkin (1998) and Adrian, Crump, and Moench (2013), synthesize how yield curve inversion influences banking system lending profitability and causes endogenous credit tightening.

Worked Solution

Commercial banks operate under maturity transformation, borrowing at short-term policy/deposit rates and lending at long-term fixed rates. An inverted yield curve (\(y_{\text{short}} > y_{\text{long}}\)) compresses net interest margins (NIM) to zero or negative territory. As bank profitability collapses, institutions aggressively tighten credit underwriting standards and restrict commercial lending, directly depressing capital investment and employment, which precipitates the macroeconomic recession predicted by the term spread signal.

Problem 2.11 · Quantitative Easing and Term Premium Extraction

Analyze the portfolio balance channel of Quantitative Easing (QE): how does central bank purchase of long-dated duration bonds compress term premia, and what are the implications for relative-value fly trades?

Worked Solution

By extracting large quantities of duration risk from the private market, central bank asset purchases reduce the aggregate duration risk premium demanded by risk-averse investors (the portfolio balance channel). This causes long-dated yields to trade below their affine term structure fair value, flattening the curve and inducing negative curvature in the 10Y–30Y sector. Relative-value butterfly traders exploit this by selling rich belly tenors targeted by QE while buying un-purchased wings.

Problem 2.12 · Cross-Asset Transmission of Sovereign Spread Widening

Explain how a widening sovereign yield spread (e.g., French OAT vs German Bund) transmits into corporate credit spreads and domestic equity volatility multiples.

Worked Solution

Sovereign bonds define the domestic risk-free benchmark floor. When sovereign credit risk escalates, rating agencies enforce the "sovereign ceiling" rule, downgrading domestic corporations whose ratings cannot exceed the sovereign. Domestic commercial banks holding sovereign debt suffer mark-to-market capital losses, triggering credit contraction. Furthermore, higher sovereign yields raise the corporate cost of capital and increase the equity discount rate, driving multiple compression and elevating implied equity volatility.

8. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the primary objective of duration weighting in a yield curve spread?

    Options

    Duration weighting balances dollar DV01 across long and short positions so that broad parallel moves produce offsetting gains and losses.

  2. What yield curve movement characterizes a "bull steepener"?

    Options

    A bull steepener occurs when falling interest rates (bullish for bond prices) steepen the curve because the central bank rapidly cuts short-term policy rates.

  3. If Leg 1 has DV01 = $40 and Leg 2 has DV01 = $80, what is the duration-neutral hedge ratio \(\beta_{\text{DV01}} = N_2 / N_1\)?

    Options

    \(\beta_{\text{DV01}} = \text{DV01}_1 / \text{DV01}_2 = 40 / 80 = 0.50\).

  4. Why do yield curve steepeners typically incur negative roll-down drag along an upward-sloping curve?

    Options

    Because long bonds have higher duration and steeper slope roll-down, shorting them forfeits roll gains that exceed the front-end long leg's roll income.

  5. In a 2s5s10s butterfly spread, what positions constitute the "wings" and "body"?

    Options

    The outside tenors (2Y and 10Y) form the wings, while the middle tenor (5Y belly) forms the body.

  6. According to empirical PCA studies, approximately what fraction of yield curve variance is driven by the first principal component (Level)?

    Options

    Level shifts dominate the term structure, accounting for 75% to 90% of total variance across global sovereign markets.

  7. Why does a duration-neutral spread develop a directional duration bias under large yield changes?

    Options

    Convexity is the derivative of duration with respect to yield. Higher-duration bonds have higher convexity, unbalancing static hedge ratios as yields shift.

  8. In sovereign relative-value trading (e.g., BTP vs Bund), what risk causes duration hedges to fail during financial crises?

    Options

    Flight-to-quality dynamics push core yields down while peripheral sovereign spreads widen, causing both long and short legs to incur losses simultaneously.
