Sizing & Cross-Asset Allocation under Scenarios

Even the most insightful macroeconomic trade expression results in financial ruin if sized improperly. Directional bets, curve relative-value trades, and option structures possess radically distinct risk profiles, volatility footprints, and tail exposures. Classical mean-variance optimization produces notoriously unstable allocations that over-concentrate in low-volatility assets with poorly estimated expected returns. By applying the continuous Kelly criterion, fractional sizing rules, volatility targeting, and risk budgeting under stressed scenario correlations, quantitative portfolio managers insulate their portfolios against parameter estimation uncertainty and catastrophic drawdowns.

1. Sizing Principles: The Continuous Kelly Criterion and Log Utility

In quantitative portfolio management, position sizing determines how much equity capital to risk on a given macro thesis. While directional forecasting seeks positive expected excess return (\(\mu > 0\)), position sizing balances compounding speed against the risk of catastrophic drawdown (Kelly 1956; MacLean, Thorp, and Ziemba 2011).

Definition 5.1 (The Continuous Kelly Criterion) Let an asset have expected annual excess return \(\mu\) and return volatility \(\sigma\). Let \(W_t\) denote portfolio wealth evolving under continuous geometric Brownian motion with position weight \(w\). The expected compound growth rate of log wealth \(g(w) = \frac{1}{t} \E[\ln(W_t / W_0)]\) is:

\[ g(w) = w \mu - \frac{1}{2} w^2 \sigma^2 \]

The unconstrained optimal growth weight \(w^*\) that maximizes \(g(w)\) is given by:

\[ w^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} = \frac{\text{Sharpe}}{\sigma} \]

For an \(N\)-asset portfolio with expected excess return vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \R^N\) and covariance matrix \(\mathbf{\Sigma} \in \R^{N \times N}\), the Kelly vector is:

\[ \mathbf{w}^* = \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} \]

While Full Kelly (\(w^*\)) asymptotically maximizes median terminal wealth over long horizons, it has severe practical shortcomings. In continuous time, a Full Kelly investor has a 50% probability of suffering a 50% drawdown before doubling wealth, and a 33% probability of experiencing a 67% drawdown (MacLean et al. 2011; Pedersen 2015).

2. Parameter Estimation Uncertainty and Fractional Kelly

In live trading, the true parameters \(\mu\) and \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) are never known with certainty; they must be estimated from historical data. Parameter estimation uncertainty creates an acute asymmetry between under-sizing and over-sizing (MacLean et al. 2011; Pedersen 2015).

Theorem 5.1 (Asymmetric Growth Penalty under Sizing Error) Let \(w^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}\) be the true optimal Kelly weight with maximum growth rate \(g^* = \frac{\mu^2}{2 \sigma^2}\). If an investor implements weight \(w = (1 + \epsilon) w^*\) where \(\epsilon \in (-1, \infty)\) represents relative sizing error, the realized growth rate is:

\[ g(w) = g^* (1 - \epsilon^2) \]

Proof: Substitute \(w = (1 + \epsilon) \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}\) into the growth equation \(g(w) = w \mu - \frac{1}{2} w^2 \sigma^2\):

\[ g(w) = (1 + \epsilon) \frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2} - \frac{1}{2} (1 + \epsilon)^2 \frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2} \left[ (1 + \epsilon) - \frac{1}{2}(1 + 2\epsilon + \epsilon^2) \right] \]

\[ g(w) = \frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2} \left[ 1 + \epsilon - \frac{1}{2} - \epsilon - \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^2 \right] = \frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2} \left[ \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^2 \right] = \frac{\mu^2}{2 \sigma^2} (1 - \epsilon^2) = g^* (1 - \epsilon^2) \]

This completes the proof. Notice that if \(\epsilon \ge 1\) (over-sizing by 100% or more, such that \(w \ge 2 w^*\)), the realized compound growth rate becomes non-positive (\(g(w) \le 0\)), leading to almost-sure capital ruin even though the asset has a strictly positive expected return \(\mu > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Because sample estimators \(\hat{\mu}\) regularly overestimate expected returns by 50% to 100%, institutional macro funds universally employ Fractional Kelly sizing:

\[ w_{\text{frac}} = f \cdot w^*, \quad f \in [0.25, 0.50] \]

At Half-Kelly (\(f = 0.50\)), the portfolio achieves \(75\%\) of maximum theoretical growth while reducing return variance by \(75\%\) and dramatically shortening recovery time from drawdowns.

3. Volatility Targeting and Cross-Asset Risk Budgeting

Macro strategies span assets with vast differences in annualized volatility: sovereign bonds (~4% to 8%), foreign exchange (~7% to 12%), equity indices (~15% to 22%), and commodities (~20% to 40%). Sizing positions by nominal dollars would cause commodities and equities to completely dominate portfolio risk (Asness, Frazzini, and Pedersen 2012; Pedersen 2015).

Definition 5.2 (Volatility Targeting and Risk Parity) To equalize risk contributions, each position is sized inversely proportional to its conditional volatility \(\sigma_i\):

\[ w_i \propto \frac{1}{\sigma_i} \implies w_i = \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{N \sigma_i} \]

Under Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) or Risk Parity across \(N\) assets with covariance \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\), portfolio weights \(\mathbf{w}\) satisfy:

\[ \text{RC}_i = w_i \frac{(\mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{w})_i}{\sigma_p} = \frac{\sigma_p}{N}, \quad \forall i = 1, \dots, N \]

Furthermore, during macroeconomic crises, historical correlations break down. Safe-haven bonds and equities, which exhibit negative correlation in tranquil regimes, can experience positive co-movement during sudden inflation scares or liquidity freezes (as in 2022). To guard against correlation shocks, macro managers employ stress covariance matrices constructed by shrinking sample covariances toward crisis factor scenarios (Pedersen 2015; Markowitz 1952).

Lab 1 · Multi-Period Sizing under Estimation Uncertainty

Hypothesis: In a 5-year Monte Carlo simulation with leverage caps, Full Kelly sizing produces extreme median drawdowns exceeding 60%, whereas Fractional Half-Kelly and Quarter-Kelly achieve comparable median terminal wealth with significantly reduced drawdowns.

"""Lab GM4-L05-1: Sizing and Allocation under Estimation Uncertainty.

Simulates multi-period wealth growth and drawdown dynamics for Full Kelly,
Half Kelly, and Quarter Kelly sizing subject to a 4x gross leverage ceiling,
illustrating why fractional Kelly dominates under parameter uncertainty.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_wealth_paths(
    true_mu: float,
    true_vol: float,
    fraction: float,
    max_leverage: float,
    n_days: int,
    n_paths: int,
    rng: np.random.Generator,
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Simulate geometric terminal wealth and maximum drawdown across paths."""
    dt = 1.0 / 252.0

    # True optimal continuous Kelly fraction: mu / vol^2
    unconstrained_k = true_mu / (true_vol**2)
    nominal_w = fraction * unconstrained_k
    w = max(-max_leverage, min(max_leverage, nominal_w))

    # Daily returns: w * (mu * dt + vol * sqrt(dt) * Z)
    drift = w * true_mu * dt
    diffusion = w * true_vol * np.sqrt(dt)

    shocks = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=(n_paths, n_days))
    daily_returns = drift + diffusion * shocks

    # Compound wealth trajectories: log wealth
    log_wealth = np.zeros((n_paths, n_days + 1), dtype=np.float64)
    np.cumsum(np.log(np.maximum(1e-6, 1.0 + daily_returns)), axis=1, out=log_wealth[:, 1:])

    wealth = np.exp(log_wealth)

    # Maximum Drawdown calculation per path
    running_max = np.maximum.accumulate(wealth, axis=1)
    drawdowns = (running_max - wealth) / running_max
    max_dd_per_path = np.max(drawdowns, axis=1)

    terminal_wealth = wealth[:, -1]
    median_terminal = float(np.median(terminal_wealth))
    mean_terminal = float(np.mean(terminal_wealth))
    median_max_dd = float(np.median(max_dd_per_path))

    return median_terminal, mean_terminal, median_max_dd


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    true_annual_excess = 0.08  # 8% annual excess return
    true_annual_vol = 0.16  # 16% annual volatility
    leverage_cap = 4.0
    n_days = 252 * 5  # 5-year investment horizon
    n_paths = 5_000

    full_med, full_mean, full_dd = simulate_wealth_paths(
        true_annual_excess, true_annual_vol, 1.0, leverage_cap, n_days, n_paths, rng
    )
    half_med, half_mean, half_dd = simulate_wealth_paths(
        true_annual_excess, true_annual_vol, 0.50, leverage_cap, n_days, n_paths, rng
    )
    quarter_med, quarter_mean, quarter_dd = simulate_wealth_paths(
        true_annual_excess, true_annual_vol, 0.25, leverage_cap, n_days, n_paths, rng
    )

    print("=== 5-Year Macro Portfolio Sizing Simulation (5,000 Paths) ===")
    print(f"{'Sizing Strategy':<18}{'Median Wealth':<16}{'Mean Wealth':<16}{'Median Max DD':<14}")
    print("-" * 64)
    print(f"Full Kelly (1.0x)  ${full_med:<15.2f}${full_mean:<15.2f}{full_dd * 100:<6.1f}%")
    print(f"Half Kelly (0.5x)  ${half_med:<15.2f}${half_mean:<15.2f}{half_dd * 100:<6.1f}%")
    print(f"Quarter Kelly (.25)${quarter_med:<15.2f}${quarter_mean:<15.2f}{quarter_dd * 100:<6.1f}%")


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook portfolio theory assumes that returns are stationary, normally distributed, and that asset correlation structures remain constant across bull and bear regimes. In macroeconomic reality, these assumptions fail decisively (Pedersen 2015; Markowitz 1952).

First, unconstrained mean-variance optimization acts as an "error maximizer" (Michaud 1989; Black and Litterman 1992). The mathematical inversion of sample covariance matrices (\(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\)) places enormous positive weights on assets with slight upward sample return errors and large short weights on assets with slight negative errors, producing wildly erratic and fragile allocations.

Second, the Kelly criterion assumes continuous rebalancing with zero transaction costs. In practice, dynamically rebalancing large macro positions in foreign exchange or government bond futures incurs market impact drag and crossing fees that swallow compounding benefits.

Third, risk parity models that rely on inverse-volatility weighting become severely leveraged when bond yields are near historical zero. As documented by Asness, Frazzini, and Pedersen (2012), levering low-volatility fixed income to match equity risk exposes the fund to massive capital losses when central banks initiate rapid, unexpected tightening cycles.

5. Misconceptions

  • Misconception: Full Kelly sizing guarantees the highest return with minimal risk of loss over finite horizons.

    Why it is wrong: Full Kelly is optimal only asymptotically as time \(T \to \infty\). Over finite 1-year to 5-year horizons, Full Kelly generates severe volatility and has a 50% probability of a 50% drawdown. If expected returns are even slightly overestimated, Full Kelly causes almost-sure capital ruin.

  • Misconception: An equal-dollar portfolio ($1M equity, $1M bonds, $1M crude oil) is balanced and well-diversified.

    Why it is wrong: Crude oil has ~35% annualized volatility while 10-year Treasury notes have ~6% volatility. In an equal-dollar portfolio, crude oil and equity volatility will generate more than 90% of total portfolio variance, rendering the bond allocation functionally irrelevant.

  • Misconception: Historical covariance matrices estimated over the past three years accurately reflect scenario risk.

    Why it is wrong: Quiet bull markets feature low realized correlations and calm credit spreads. In macroeconomic crisis regimes, correlations between risk assets converge toward +1.0 while market liquidity evaporates, invalidating tranquil covariance estimates.

6. Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 5.1 · Unconstrained Kelly Weight Calculation

A macro quantitative strategy forecasts an annualized excess return of 6.0% (\(\mu = 0.06\)) for an equity index relative-value trade with annualized return volatility of 15.0% (\(\sigma = 0.15\)). Calculate the unconstrained Full Kelly weight \(w^*\) and the Half Kelly weight \(w_{\text{half}}\).

Worked Solution

1. Full Kelly weight: \(w^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} = \frac{0.06}{(0.15)^2} = \frac{0.06}{0.0225} \approx 2.667\) (266.7% leverage).

2. Half Kelly weight: \(w_{\text{half}} = 0.50 \times w^* = 0.50 \times 2.667 \approx 1.333\) (133.3% leverage).

Problem 5.2 · Volatility-Targeted Weight Sizing

A macro fund targets an annual portfolio volatility of 10.0% (\(\sigma_{\text{target}} = 0.10\)). It allocates capital across two uncorrelated assets: 10-Year Treasury futures (\(\sigma_1 = 6.0\%\)) and Emerging Market FX futures (\(\sigma_2 = 12.0\%\)). Using inverse-volatility weighting, calculate the portfolio weights \(w_1\) and \(w_2\).

Worked Solution

1. Relative inverse-volatility scores: \(s_1 = \frac{1}{0.06} \approx 16.667\), \(s_2 = \frac{1}{0.12} \approx 8.333\). Sum: \(S = 25.000\).

2. Normalized relative weights: \(\tilde{w}_1 = \frac{16.667}{25.000} = 0.6667\), \(\tilde{w}_2 = \frac{8.333}{25.000} = 0.3333\).

3. Standalone volatility of relative portfolio (uncorrelated): \(\sigma_{\text{rel}} = \sqrt{(0.6667 \times 0.06)^2 + (0.3333 \times 0.12)^2} = \sqrt{(0.040)^2 + (0.040)^2} = \sqrt{0.0016 + 0.0016} = \sqrt{0.0032} \approx 0.05657\) (5.66%).

4. Leverage scalar to reach target: \(k = \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\sigma_{\text{rel}}} = \frac{0.10}{0.05657} \approx 1.768\).

5. Final weights: \(w_1 = 1.768 \times 0.6667 \approx 1.179\) (117.9%), \(w_2 = 1.768 \times 0.3333 \approx 0.589\) (58.9%). Gross leverage = \(1.179 + 0.589 = 1.768\).

Problem 5.3 · Growth Penalty from Overestimating Mean

An analyst believes an asset has an expected return of \(\hat{\mu} = 10.0\%\) with \(\sigma = 20.0\%\), sizing position at Full Kelly based on this estimate. In reality, the true expected return is \(\mu = 5.0\%\) with the same volatility \(\sigma = 20.0\%\). Determine the realized compound growth rate \(g(w)\) and compare it to the true maximum growth rate \(g^*\).

Worked Solution

1. Implemented weight: \(w = \frac{\hat{\mu}}{\sigma^2} = \frac{0.10}{0.04} = 2.50\).

2. True optimal weight: \(w^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} = \frac{0.05}{0.04} = 1.25\).

3. Sizing error: \(\epsilon = \frac{w - w^*}{w^*} = \frac{2.50 - 1.25}{1.25} = 1.00\) (100% over-sizing).

4. True maximum growth: \(g^* = \frac{\mu^2}{2 \sigma^2} = \frac{0.0025}{2 \times 0.04} = 0.03125\) (3.125% per year).

5. Realized growth: \(g(w) = g^* (1 - \epsilon^2) = 0.03125 \times (1 - 1.0^2) = 0.000\%\).

Over-sizing by a factor of 2 completely destroys the compound growth rate, reducing expected long-term capital appreciation to zero.

Proof

Problem 5.4 · Log Utility Optimality of Kelly Betting

Prove that maximizing the expected log utility \(\E[\ln(W_T)]\) of terminal wealth is strictly equivalent to maximizing the asymptotic compound growth rate of capital \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \ln(W_T)\) almost surely.

Worked Solution

Let terminal wealth be \(W_T = W_0 \prod_{t=1}^T (1 + R_t)\), where \(R_t\) are independent and identically distributed returns with portfolio weight \(w\).

Taking the natural logarithm: \(\frac{1}{T} \ln(W_T / W_0) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \ln(1 + R_t)\).

By Kolmogorov's Strong Law of Large Numbers, since \(\E[|\ln(1 + R_t)|] < \infty\), the sample average converges almost surely to the expected value: \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \ln(W_T / W_0) = \E[\ln(1 + R_t)]\) almost surely.

Therefore, choosing \(w\) to maximize \(\E[\ln(1 + R_t)]\) uniquely maximizes the almost-sure asymptotic growth rate of wealth. Any other weight vector \(w' \neq w^*\) yields a strictly lower almost-sure compound growth rate, proving that Kelly betting dominates all alternative stationary sizing rules asymptotically. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 5.5 · Drawdown Probability of Kelly Betting

Prove that for a continuous-time geometric Brownian motion with Full Kelly weight \(w^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}\), the probability that wealth ever drops to a fraction \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\) of its peak value before reaching infinity is exactly \(\Prob(\text{Drawdown} \ge 1 - \alpha) = 1 - \alpha\).

Worked Solution

Under Full Kelly sizing, log wealth \(X_t = \ln(W_t)\) follows a Brownian motion with drift \(g^* = \frac{\mu^2}{2 \sigma^2}\) and diffusion volatility \(\sigma_w = w^* \sigma = \frac{\mu}{\sigma}\).

Let \(M_t = \max_{0 \le s \le t} X_s\) be the running maximum. The drawdown process is \(D_t = M_t - X_t \ge 0\). We seek the probability that \(D_t\) ever reaches \(b = -\ln(\alpha) > 0\).

By standard results on the scale function and excursion theory of drifted Brownian motion (Karatzas and Shreve 1991), the drawdown process is regenerative with exponential distribution: \(\Prob(\sup_{t \ge 0} D_t \ge b) = e^{-\gamma b}\), where \(\gamma = \frac{2 \text{drift}}{\text{variance}} = \frac{2 (\mu^2 / (2 \sigma^2))}{(\mu / \sigma)^2} = \frac{\mu^2 / \sigma^2}{\mu^2 / \sigma^2} = 1\).

Substituting \(\gamma = 1\) and \(b = -\ln(\alpha)\): \(\Prob(\sup_{t \ge 0} D_t \ge -\ln \alpha) = e^{-(-\ln \alpha)} = \alpha\).

Thus, the probability of suffering at least a \((1 - \alpha)\) drawdown is \(\alpha\). For \(\alpha = 0.50\) (a 50% drawdown), the probability is exactly \(50\%\). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 5.6 · Invariance of Kelly Sizing to Independent Noise Assets

Prove that adding an uncorrelated asset with zero expected excess return (\(\mu_{N+1} = 0\), \(\sigma_{N+1} > 0\), \(\Cov(R_i, R_{N+1}) = 0\)) leaves the optimal Kelly weights of the existing \(N\) assets completely unchanged.

Worked Solution

Let the augmented excess return vector be \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{aug}} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\mu} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and the augmented covariance matrix be block-diagonal: \(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{aug}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma} & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0}^T & \sigma_{N+1}^2 \end{bmatrix}\).

The inverse of a block-diagonal matrix is block-diagonal: \(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{aug}}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0}^T & \sigma_{N+1}^{-2} \end{bmatrix}\).

Computing the augmented Kelly vector: \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{aug}}^* = \mathbf{\Sigma}_{\text{aug}}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{aug}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} \\ \sigma_{N+1}^{-2} \times 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{w}^* \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\).

The weights assigned to the original \(N\) assets are identically \(\mathbf{w}^*\), and the weight assigned to the zero-alpha asset is zero, proving that unconstrained Kelly sizing is invariant to the presence of independent noise instruments. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem 5.7 · Empirical Volatility Spike in March 2020

In February 2020, 10-year Treasury note daily volatility stood at 4.2% annualized. By mid-March 2020, realized daily volatility surged to 16.8% annualized. For a volatility-targeted macro fund maintaining a constant 8.0% annualized risk target, calculate the required deleveraging ratio.

Worked Solution

1. Initial position weight: \(w_0 = \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\sigma_0} = \frac{0.08}{0.042} \approx 1.905\) (190.5% exposure).

2. Crisis position weight: \(w_1 = \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\sigma_1} = \frac{0.08}{0.168} \approx 0.476\) (47.6% exposure).

3. Position reduction ratio: \(\frac{w_1}{w_0} = \frac{0.476}{1.905} = \frac{0.042}{0.168} = 0.250\) (75.0% reduction in notional).

To preserve constant portfolio risk, the volatility-targeting algorithm forced a 75% reduction in Treasury exposure, contributing to market-wide dealer balance sheet liquidations during the March 2020 liquidity shock.

Problem 5.8 · Ledoit-Wolf Shrinkage under Crisis Correlation Spikes

During normal regimes, equity and sovereign bond returns exhibit a correlation of -0.35. During the 2022 stagflation regime, their realized correlation rose to +0.48. If a shrinkage estimator blends the sample covariance matrix with an equicorrelation target (\(\bar{\rho} = 0.20\)) with shrinkage intensity \(\delta = 0.30\), compute the shrunk correlation coefficient.

Worked Solution

1. Sample correlation in 2022: \(\rho_{\text{sample}} = +0.48\).

2. Target correlation: \(\rho_{\text{target}} = +0.20\).

3. Shrunk correlation: \(\rho_{\text{shrunk}} = (1 - \delta) \rho_{\text{sample}} + \delta \rho_{\text{target}} = (1 - 0.30) \times 0.48 + 0.30 \times 0.20 = 0.70 \times 0.48 + 0.06 = 0.336 + 0.06 = 0.396\).

Shrinkage pulls the extreme positive correlation toward the long-term structural prior, dampening portfolio turnover while accounting for the regime change.

Problem 5.9 · Leverage Aversion in Risk Parity Portfolios

Asness, Frazzini, and Pedersen (2012) document that leverage-constrained investors overpay for high-beta assets (like equities) and under-allocate to low-beta assets (like bonds), causing unleveraged bonds to offer superior risk-adjusted Sharpe ratios. Explain how a macro fund exploits this via leverage.

Worked Solution

Because many institutional investors (pension funds, retail mutual funds) are legally prohibited or structurally reluctant to use leverage, they concentrate capital in high-volatility, high-beta assets to achieve target return hurdles. This excess demand compresses the Sharpe ratio of high-beta assets.

A quantitative macro hedge fund with access to low-cost repo financing borrows cash to scale up low-beta sovereign bonds to equity-like volatility. By holding a levered, risk-balanced portfolio of high-Sharpe bonds and equities, the fund captures a higher overall portfolio Sharpe ratio than an unleveraged equity-heavy benchmark.

Research

Problem 5.10 · Dynamic Kelly Allocation with Regime-Switching Returns

Formulate the continuous-time optimal Kelly growth problem when expected excess returns \(\mu(S_t)\) and volatility \(\sigma(S_t)\) follow a two-state continuous-time Markov chain \(S_t \in \{1, 2\}\) representing "Expansion" and "Crisis" regimes.

Worked Solution

Let \(S_t \in \{1, 2\}\) transition with generator matrix \(\mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix} -q_{12} & q_{12} \\ q_{21} & -q_{21} \end{bmatrix}\). Wealth follows \(d W_t = W_t [w_t \mu(S_t) dt + w_t \sigma(S_t) d B_t]\).

The investor maximizes expected long-term logarithmic growth \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \E[\ln W_T]\). By dynamic programming, let \(V(w, s) = \ln W + v(s)\). The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman system is:

\[ \max_w \left\{ w \mu(s) - \frac{1}{2} w^2 \sigma(s)^2 + \sum_{s' \neq s} q_{s s'} [v(s') - v(s)] \right\} = \lambda \]

Because the Markov state transitions are independent of portfolio weight \(w\) in this unconstrained setting, the static first-order condition holds state-by-state: \(w^*(s) = \frac{\mu(s)}{\sigma(s)^2}\). In practice, because state transition uncertainty induces learning (Bayesian filter), optimal sizing shades toward the fractional Kelly weight of the high-risk state.

Problem 5.11 · Black-Litterman Integration with Macroeconomic Factor Views

Explain how the Black-Litterman (1992) framework prevents extreme corner allocations in macro cross-asset portfolios by blending reverse-engineered CAPM equilibrium market weights with subjective trader views.

Worked Solution

Standard Markowitz optimization inverts sample means, producing erratic 100% long / 100% short weights due to estimation error. Black and Litterman (1992) resolve this by setting the neutral benchmark prior as the implied equilibrium returns \(\boldsymbol{\Pi} = \lambda \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}_{\text{mkt}}\) backed out from market capitalization weights.

Macro views are expressed as linear projections \(\mathbf{P} \boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{Q} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) with diagonal view uncertainty matrix \(\mathbf{\Omega}\). The posterior expected return is a precision-weighted combination: \(\E[\boldsymbol{\mu}] = [(\tau \mathbf{\Sigma})^{-1} + \mathbf{P}^T \mathbf{\Omega}^{-1} \mathbf{P}]^{-1} [(\tau \mathbf{\Sigma})^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Pi} + \mathbf{P}^T \mathbf{\Omega}^{-1} \mathbf{Q}]\). Where the trader has zero view, the allocation defaults smoothly to market equilibrium without arbitrary corner solutions.

Problem 5.12 · Maximum Drawdown Constraints in Capital Growth Theory

Synthesize Grossman and Zhou's (1993) model of optimal portfolio choice under a hard maximum drawdown constraint (\(W_t \ge \alpha M_t\), \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\)). How does optimal leverage vary with distance to the drawdown floor?

Worked Solution

Grossman and Zhou (1993) establish that when an investor faces a strict drawdown floor \(W_t \ge \alpha M_t\) (where \(M_t = \max_{0 \le s \le t} W_s\)), the effective risk cushion is \(C_t = W_t - \alpha M_t\). As wealth approaches the floor (\(C_t \to 0\)), risk tolerance must approach zero to avoid default.

The optimal risky exposure is proportional to the cushion rather than total wealth: \(\text{Position}_t = k \cdot (W_t - \alpha M_t) \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}\), where \(k > 0\) is a constant derived from the non-linear value function. At all-time highs (\(W_t = M_t\)), cushion is maximized and the manager deploys peak leverage; after a drawdown, leverage automatically contracts, eliminating catastrophic ruin risk at the cost of slower post-drawdown recovery.

7. Key Results and Formulas

Concept / Formula Mathematical Expression Description
Continuous Kelly Criterion \(w^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} = \frac{\text{Sharpe}}{\sigma}\) Position weight that maximizes the asymptotic compound growth rate of wealth.
Growth Penalty under Error \(g(w) = g^* (1 - \epsilon^2)\) Asymmetric reduction in compound growth rate from relative sizing error \(\epsilon\).
Fractional Kelly Weight \(w_{\text{frac}} = f \cdot w^*, \quad f \in [0.25, 0.50]\) Pragmatic sizing rule providing 75–88% of peak growth with drastically lower drawdowns.
Inverse-Volatility Sizing \(w_i = \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{N \sigma_i}\) Allocates risk equally across heterogeneous assets by scaling notional by inverse volatility.
Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) \(\text{RC}_i = w_i \frac{(\mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{w})_i}{\sigma_p} = \frac{\sigma_p}{N}\) Ensures every trade leg contributes an identical share to total portfolio volatility.

8. Spaced Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the continuous-time optimal Kelly weight \(w^*\) for an asset with expected excess return \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\)?

    Options

    Maximizing the expected log-growth rate \(w \mu - \frac{1}{2} w^2 \sigma^2\) yields the first-order condition \(\mu - w \sigma^2 = 0 \implies w^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}\).

  2. What occurs if a portfolio manager sizes positions at double the true Kelly weight (\(w = 2 w^*\), \(\epsilon = 1.0\))?

    Options

    Under Theorem 5.1, \(g(w) = g^* (1 - \epsilon^2)\). When \(\epsilon = 1.0\), \(g(w) = 0\), turning a positive-alpha asset into zero capital growth due to excessive variance drag.

  3. Why is Half Kelly (\(f = 0.50\)) universally preferred over Full Kelly in quantitative macro practice?

    Options

    Half Kelly captures \(1 - (0.5)^2 = 75\%\) of maximum growth while cutting portfolio variance by a factor of 4, providing immense safety against estimation errors.

  4. In an equal-risk-contribution (risk parity) portfolio, how are asset weights scaled relative to asset volatility?

    Options

    To balance risk contributions, lower-volatility assets receive larger dollar weights and higher-volatility assets receive smaller weights.

  5. What is the "error maximizer" critique of classical Markowitz mean-variance optimization (Michaud 1989)?

    Options

    Matrix inversion acts as an amplifier of estimation errors, allocating heaviest to the most overestimated assets.

  6. What is the probability that a Full Kelly investor experiences a 50% peak-to-trough drawdown over an infinite horizon?

    Options

    By scale function analysis of Brownian motion with Kelly drift, \(\Prob(\text{Drawdown} \ge 1 - \alpha) = \alpha\), so \(\Prob(\text{Drawdown} \ge 50\%) = 50\%\).

  7. Why do macro managers apply Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage to sample covariance matrices during regime transitions?

    Options

    Shrinkage estimators condition ill-conditioned covariance matrices, reducing extreme out-of-sample portfolio turnover and instability.

  8. Under Black-Litterman optimization, what is the default portfolio prior when the manager holds no active views?

    Options

    The Black-Litterman model anchors on the market capitalization equilibrium portfolio via reverse optimization, diverging only when active views have high confidence.
