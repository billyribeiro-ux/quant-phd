Carry, Roll-Down & Financing Drag in Expressions

Carry is the expected return of an asset assuming market conditions remain unchanged. Across macro asset classes—fixed income, currencies, equities, and commodities—carry forms a dominant systematic return driver. However, high-carry expressions often conceal severe negative tail risks, funding liquidity squeezes, and financing drag. In leveraged fixed income trades, the gross carry and roll-down earned from holding a steep curve can be entirely eroded by general collateral repo borrowing costs, haircut expansions, and collateral friction. Decomposing gross carry into coupon flow, roll-down trajectory, and funding drag is essential to avoid negative-carry traps.

1. Mathematical Anatomy of Carry across Asset Classes

In quantitative macro portfolio management, carry represents the contractual or structural cash flow return an investor earns from holding an asset if market prices, yield curves, and spot exchange rates remain perfectly unchanged over the holding period (Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen, and Vrugt 2018; Ilmanen 2011).

Definition 4.1 (General Multi-Asset Carry) Let \(S_t\) denote the spot price or market value of an asset at time \(t\), and let \(F_{t, t+\tau}\) denote the forward or futures price for delivery at \(t+\tau\). The canonical carry \(C_t(\tau)\) over horizon \(\tau\) is defined as the expected excess return assuming spot prices remain invariant (\(S_{t+\tau} = S_t\)):

\[ C_t(\tau) = \frac{S_t - F_{t, t+\tau}}{F_{t, t+\tau}} \]

For spot assets with periodic income stream \(D_{t, t+\tau}\) financed at borrowing rate \(R_{t, t+\tau}^f\), the spot carry is:

\[ C_t^{\text{spot}}(\tau) = \frac{D_{t, t+\tau}}{S_t} - R_{t, t+\tau}^f \]

This unified formulation clarifies carry across diverse global macro instruments:

  • Currencies (FX Carry): In foreign exchange forward contracts, CIP dictates \(F_{t, t+\tau} = S_t \frac{1 + r_d \tau}{1 + r_f \tau}\). The FX carry is approximately the short-term interest rate differential \(C_{\text{FX}} \approx r_f - r_d\). An investor going long high-yielding currencies financed in low-yielding funding currencies earns the forward roll discount (Brunnermeier et al. 2008; Menkhoff et al. 2012).
  • Commodities: Commodity futures carry is the convenience yield minus storage and financing costs: \(C_{\text{comm}} = \frac{S_t - F_t}{F_t}\). When the term structure is in backwardation (\(F_t < S_t\)), carry is positive as contracts roll upward toward spot at expiry. When in contango (\(F_t > S_t\)), roll yield is negative.
  • Equities: Equity index futures carry is the expected dividend yield minus the risk-free financing rate: \(C_{\text{eq}} \approx q - r_f\).
  • Fixed Income: Bond carry is the coupon yield plus the curve roll-down minus the repo financing cost.

2. Fixed-Income Carry and Roll-Down Decomposition

In fixed income markets, carry cannot be understood solely as the coupon or yield to maturity. As time elapses, a bond's remaining maturity shortens. Along an upward-sloping yield curve, this shortening maturity causes the bond to "roll down" the curve to lower yields, generating capital appreciation even if the entire yield curve remains static (Ilmanen 2011; Gürkaynak et al. 2007).

Theorem 4.1 (Fixed Income Holding Period Return Decomposition) Let a default-free coupon bond have initial maturity \(T\), coupon rate \(c\), modified duration \(D(T)\), and yield to maturity \(y(T)\). Over holding horizon \(\Delta t\), assume the spot curve evolves by an exogenous shift \(\Delta y_{\text{curve}}(T)\). The total holding period return \(HPR\) funded at financing repo rate \(r_{\text{repo}}\) decomposes as:

\[ HPR \approx \underbrace{y(T) \Delta t}_{\text{gross yield carry}} + \underbrace{D(T) \left( - \frac{\partial y}{\partial T} \right) \Delta t}_{\text{roll-down return}} - \underbrace{D(T) \Delta y_{\text{curve}}}_{\text{curve shift P\&L}} - \underbrace{r_{\text{repo}} \Delta t}_{\text{repo financing drag}} \]

Proof Sketch: By definition of modified duration, the percentage price change of the bond is \(\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D(T) \Delta y_{\text{total}}\). The total yield change experienced by the bond over \(\Delta t\) has two distinct components: the shift of the market curve \(\Delta y_{\text{curve}}\) and the slide along the existing static curve due to aging \(\Delta y_{\text{aging}} = y(T - \Delta t) - y(T) \approx -\frac{\partial y}{\partial T} \Delta t\).

Thus, \(\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D(T) \left[ \Delta y_{\text{curve}} - \frac{\partial y}{\partial T} \Delta t \right] = D(T) \frac{\partial y}{\partial T} \Delta t - D(T) \Delta y_{\text{curve}}\).

Adding the coupon income \(c \Delta t \approx y(T) \Delta t\) and subtracting the cash financing cost \(r_{\text{repo}} \Delta t\) yields the four-part decomposition. \(\blacksquare\)

The sum of yield carry and roll-down is termed gross carry. In steep curve regimes (e.g., 2Y–5Y or 5Y–10Y on the US Treasury curve), roll-down often contributes more than forty percent of the total gross carry, making tenors with the steepest local slope (\(\max \frac{\partial y}{\partial T}\)) the most attractive expression vehicles.

3. Financing Drag, Haircuts, and Margin Spirals

Institutional macro expressions rarely operate with 100% unleveraged cash. Fixed income relative-value trades, such as Treasury basis trading or curve spread positioning, require substantial balance sheet leverage financed via the bilateral repo (repurchase agreement) market or central clearing counterparties (FICC) (Pedersen 2015; Brunnermeier, Nagel, and Pedersen 2008).

Definition 4.2 (Repo Financing Drag and Margin Leverage) In a repo transaction, a macro manager pledges securities of market value \(V\) to borrow cash \(V (1 - h)\), where \(h \in [0, 1)\) is the haircut. The manager pays the repo financing rate \(r_{\text{repo}}\). The net carry per dollar of deployed investor equity capital \(E = h \cdot V\) is:

\[ C_{\text{equity}} = \frac{1}{h} \left[ C_{\text{gross}} - (1 - h) r_{\text{repo}} \right] = \frac{C_{\text{gross}} - r_{\text{repo}}}{h} + r_{\text{repo}} \]

When the yield curve is inverted (\(y(T) < r_{\text{repo}}\)), gross carry is less than financing cost, creating a negative-carry trap. A manager holding a 10-year Treasury bond yielding 4.20% funded at a SOFR repo rate of 5.30% loses 110 bps per year in negative carry. Even with 15 bps of roll-down, the net drag is -95 bps per unit of notional, which levered 10-to-1 translates into a -9.5% annual return drain on equity capital.

Furthermore, during macroeconomic crises, market liquidity dries up and dealers expand repo haircuts (\(h \to h'\) with \(h' > h\)). This triggers an endogenous liquidity spiral: funds are forced to liquidate positions to meet margin calls, which drives down collateral prices and forces further haircuts across the entire dealer network (Brunnermeier et al. 2008; Asness, Frazzini, and Pedersen 2012).

Lab 1 · Carry, Roll-Down & Financing Drag Attribution Model

Hypothesis: Along an upward-sloping Nelson-Siegel yield curve, a 1-year holding period produces positive roll-down gains across all intermediate tenors, but gross carry must exceed the 3.50% repo financing rate to deliver positive net carry.

"""Lab GM4-L04-1: Carry, Roll-Down, and Financing Drag in Fixed Income Expressions.

Decomposes 1-year holding period returns into coupon carry, roll-down along an
upward-sloping yield curve, and repo financing drag across 2Y, 5Y, 10Y, and 30Y tenors.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def nelson_siegel_yield(maturity: float, b0: float, b1: float, b2: float, lam: float) -> float:
    """Calculate Nelson-Siegel zero yield for given maturity."""
    if maturity <= 0:
        return b0 + b1
    tau = maturity * lam
    factor1 = (1.0 - math.exp(-tau)) / tau
    factor2 = factor1 - math.exp(-tau)
    return b0 + b1 * factor1 + b2 * factor2


def main() -> None:
    # Nelson-Siegel parameters representing a standard upward-sloping yield curve
    b0 = 0.048  # Long-term asymptotic level
    b1 = -0.018  # Short-term component (slope)
    b2 = -0.012  # Curvature
    lam = 0.65  # Decay factor

    repo_rate = 0.035  # General collateral repo financing rate
    horizon_years = 1.0  # 1-year holding period

    tenors = [2.0, 5.0, 10.0, 30.0]
    durations = [1.90, 4.45, 8.10, 17.50]

    print("=== Carry & Roll-Down Attribution (1-Year Horizon, Repo = 3.50%) ===")
    print(f"{'Tenor':<8}{'Init Yield':<12}{'Roll Yield':<12}{'Roll-Down':<12}{'Repo Drag':<12}{'Net Carry':<12}")
    print("-" * 68)

    for m, dur in zip(tenors, durations, strict=True):
        init_yield = nelson_siegel_yield(m, b0, b1, b2, lam)
        rolled_maturity = m - horizon_years
        rolled_yield = nelson_siegel_yield(rolled_maturity, b0, b1, b2, lam)

        # Yield slide along the curve: initial yield minus rolled yield
        roll_down_bps = (init_yield - rolled_yield) * 10_000.0

        # Roll-down price gain = duration * (init_yield - rolled_yield)
        roll_down_gain = dur * (init_yield - rolled_yield)

        gross_carry = init_yield * horizon_years
        repo_drag = repo_rate * horizon_years
        net_carry = gross_carry + roll_down_gain - repo_drag
        net_carry_bps = net_carry * 10_000.0

        print(
            f"{int(m):>2}Y      "
            f"{init_yield * 100:>5.2f}%      "
            f"{rolled_yield * 100:>5.2f}%      "
            f"{roll_down_bps:>6.1f} bps   "
            f"{repo_drag * 100:>5.2f}%      "
            f"{net_carry_bps:>6.1f} bps"
        )


main()

Tenor   Init Yield  Roll Yield  Roll-Down   Repo Drag   Net Carry   
--------------------------------------------------------------------
 2Y       3.45%       3.22%        22.8 bps    3.50%        38.1 bps
 5Y       3.96%       3.82%        13.8 bps    3.50%       107.5 bps
10Y       4.34%       4.29%         4.9 bps    3.50%       123.7 bps
30Y       4.65%       4.64%         0.5 bps    3.50%       123.9 bps

The empirical model verifies that intermediate 5Y and 10Y tenors maximize net carry after financing drag (107.5 and 123.7 bps), whereas the 2Y note achieves only 38.1 bps due to shorter duration despite a steeper 22.8 bps curve slope.

5. Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook quantitative models frequently treat carry as an unconditional, stationary drift term added to asset returns. In live macroeconomic trading, relying naively on static carry leads to capital destruction during regime shifts (Pedersen 2015; Brunnermeier et al. 2008).

First, carry is not pure alpha: across asset classes, carry is fundamentally risk compensation for bearing acute crash risk. In foreign exchange, high-yielding currencies exhibit extreme negative return skewness (the "going up by stairs and coming down in an elevator" phenomenon). In March 2008 and September 2008, popular G10 carry baskets lost several years of accumulated carry returns in single 48-hour trading windows.

Second, the assumption of constant repo financing breaks down when balance sheet capacity contracts. During the March 2020 Treasury cash-futures basis disruption, the spread between Treasury bonds and futures widened sharply because dealers hit regulatory leverage ratio constraints (SLR) and refused to intermediate repo financing, forcing leveraged relative-value funds to liquidate at distressed fire-sale prices.

Third, roll-down is path-dependent and fragile. A flattening curve destroys roll-down returns immediately: if 2Y yields rise by 50 bps while 10Y yields remain unchanged, the 5Y bond experiences severe capital loss that swamps multiple quarters of expected roll-down.

6. Misconceptions

  • Misconception: High nominal coupon yield guarantees an attractive trade expression with positive net carry.

    Why it is wrong: Nominal yield ignores both the shape of the yield curve and the repo financing cost. In an inverted yield curve or tight monetary policy regime, an emerging market or sovereign bond with an 8% coupon funded at a 9% local repo rate generates negative net carry of -100 bps before even accounting for currency depreciation risk.

  • Misconception: Roll-down return is a guaranteed cash accrual that materializes regardless of yield curve shifts.

    Why it is wrong: Roll-down is strictly a conditional capital gain that only occurs if the yield curve remains completely unchanged over the holding period. An adverse curve shift of just 5 basis points can completely wipe out an entire year's projected roll-down on a 5-year or 10-year Treasury note.

  • Misconception: Cash-futures basis trades and sovereign yield curve spreads are pure arbitrage with zero financing risk.

    Why it is wrong: Basis trades require holding cash securities against short futures, demanding repo financing for the cash leg. If repo haircut requirements rise or repo rates spike above policy expectations (as in September 2019 SOFR repo spike), the trade experiences acute negative cash flow and margin pressure even if underlying theoretical convergence holds.

7. Problem Set

Mechanics

Problem 4.1 · Bond Holding Period Return with Roll-Down

A 5-year Treasury note has an initial yield of 4.10% and a modified duration of 4.50 years. After 1 year of holding, 4-year Treasury yields stand at 3.85%, while the overall curve remained unchanged. The investor funded the bond in the repo market at 3.25%. Calculate the 1-year total net holding period return in basis points.

Worked Solution

1. Gross yield carry: \(y_0 \times \Delta t = 4.10\% \times 1.0 = +4.10\%\) (+410 bps).

2. Roll-down gain: \(\Delta y_{\text{roll}} = 4.10\% - 3.85\% = 0.25\%\) (25 bps of roll-down). Price appreciation: \(D \times \Delta y_{\text{roll}} = 4.50 \times 0.25\% = +1.125\%\) (+112.5 bps).

3. Financing drag: \(r_{\text{repo}} \times \Delta t = 3.25\% \times 1.0 = -3.25\%\) (-325 bps).

4. Net HPR: \(+410 + 112.5 - 325 = +197.5\) bps (+1.975%).

Problem 4.2 · FX Forward Carry Calculation

Spot USD/MXN is 17.50. The 1-year US risk-free interest rate is 5.25%, and the 1-year Mexican interest rate is 11.25%. Assuming covered interest parity holds exactly, compute the 1-year forward exchange rate and the annualized FX carry earned by a USD-based investor holding long Mexican pesos.

Worked Solution

1. By covered interest parity: \(F = S \times \frac{1 + r_{\text{MXN}}}{1 + r_{\text{USD}}} = 17.50 \times \frac{1 + 0.1125}{1 + 0.0525} = 17.50 \times \frac{1.1125}{1.0525} \approx 18.4976\).

2. Forward discount on MXN: \(\frac{F - S}{S} = \frac{18.4976 - 17.50}{17.50} \approx +5.70\%\) (depreciation priced in).

3. The interest rate differential earned is \(r_{\text{MXN}} - r_{\text{USD}} = 11.25\% - 5.25\% = +6.00\%\) (600 bps of annual carry).

Problem 4.3 · Leveraged Equity Return with Haircuts

A macro fund executes a Treasury curve spread with gross carry of 1.40% per annum, financed via repo at 3.60%. The fund operates with a 2% haircut (\(h = 0.02\)), implying a 50x gross leverage ratio. Calculate the net return on equity (ROE).

Worked Solution

1. Net carry per unit of asset notional: \(C_{\text{net}} = C_{\text{gross}} - (1 - h) r_{\text{repo}} = 0.0140 - (1 - 0.02) \times 0.0360 = 0.0140 - 0.03528 = -0.02128\) (-2.128%).

2. Return on equity: \(\text{ROE} = \frac{C_{\text{net}}}{h} = \frac{-0.02128}{0.02} = -1.064\) (-106.4%).

Because the repo rate exceeds the gross carry, high leverage magnifies the negative carry into catastrophic equity depletion.

Proof

Problem 4.4 · Zero Carry Condition under Pure Expectation Hypothesis

Prove that under the continuous-time Pure Expectations Hypothesis of interest rates with zero term premia, the expected holding period return across all tenors over horizon \(\Delta t\) equals the instantaneous short rate \(r_t\), rendering net carry identically zero across all curve expressions.

Worked Solution

Under the Pure Expectations Hypothesis, the zero-coupon yield to maturity is \(y(t, T) = \frac{1}{T - t} \int_t^T \E_t[r_s] ds\).

Consider holding a bond of maturity \(T\) over an infinitesimal interval \(dt\). The price of the bond is \(P(t, T) = \exp(-(T-t) y(t, T)) = \exp(-\int_t^T \E_t[r_s] ds)\).

Applying Leibniz's rule to differentiate with respect to \(t\): \(\frac{d P(t, T)}{P(t, T)} = \E_t[r_t] dt + \text{martingale noise} = r_t dt\).

Since the asset's expected price appreciation plus coupon accrual equals \(r_t dt\), and financing costs are \(r_t dt\), the net expected excess return over the risk-free financing rate is identically zero: \(\E_t[HPR - r_t dt] = 0\). Thus, non-zero net carry requires the existence of non-zero risk premia or term premia. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 4.5 · Monotonicity of Roll-Down along Strictly Convex Curves

Let the yield curve \(y(T)\) be twice continuously differentiable, strictly increasing (\(y'(T) > 0\)), and strictly concave (\(y''(T) < 0\)) for all \(T > 0\). Prove that the instantaneous roll-down yield \(\rho(T) = D(T) y'(T)\) is strictly maximized at some interior or boundary tenor, and find the first-order condition for optimal roll-down tenor under duration approximation \(D(T) \approx T\).

Worked Solution

Let \(\rho(T) = T y'(T)\). To find critical points, differentiate with respect to \(T\):

\[ \frac{d \rho}{d T} = y'(T) + T y''(T) \]

Since \(y'(T) > 0\) and \(y''(T) < 0\), the first term is positive and the second term is negative. Setting the derivative to zero yields the first-order condition:

\[ y'(T^*) + T^* y''(T^*) = 0 \iff -\frac{T^* y''(T^*)}{y'(T^*)} = 1 \]

This states that the elasticity of the curve slope with respect to maturity must equal \(-1\). For standard Nelson-Siegel curves, this maximum typically occurs in the 3Y to 7Y belly of the curve where slope is still steep and duration has grown sufficiently large. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 4.6 · Carry Invariance under Static Forward Curve

Prove that if market forwards perfectly predict future spot prices (\(S_{t+\tau} = F_{t, t+\tau}\), the realized excess return of holding an asset equals exactly zero, and therefore carry is the exact negative of the forward bias.

Worked Solution

Let an investor enter a forward contract at price \(F_{t, t+\tau}\) requiring zero initial cash. At maturity \(t+\tau\), the realized payoff is \(S_{t+\tau} - F_{t, t+\tau}\).

If \(S_{t+\tau} = F_{t, t+\tau}\), the realized payoff is identically zero.

Now consider holding the spot asset funded by borrowing \(S_t\) at the risk-free rate \(e^{r \tau}\). The terminal value is \(S_{t+\tau} - S_t e^{r \tau}\). By forward pricing, \(F_{t, t+\tau} = S_t e^{r \tau}\). Thus, terminal payoff is \(S_{t+\tau} - F_{t, t+\tau} = 0\).

Carry was defined as \(C = \frac{S_t - F_{t, t+\tau}}{F_{t, t+\tau}}\). If realized spot equals initial spot \(S_{t+\tau} = S_t\), realized excess return is \(\frac{S_t - F_{t, t+\tau}}{F_{t, t+\tau}} = C\). Conversely, if the forward price is realized, the excess return is zero. Thus, carry measures the exact return discrepancy between a static spot world and the risk-neutral forward curve. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Problem 4.7 · Empirical Sharpe Ratio of Multi-Asset Carry

Koijen et al. (2018) document an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.82 for a diversified global multi-asset carry strategy over 1980–2012, compared to 0.41 for a global equity benchmark. Explain how cross-asset diversification (across FX, bonds, commodities, and equities) improves carry performance despite individual asset crash risk.

Worked Solution

Individual carry trades exhibit significant negative skewness and idiosyncratic crash risk (e.g., currency devaluation, commodity supply gluts). However, the correlation of carry crashes across unrelated asset classes is imperfect: a crash in agricultural commodity roll yield rarely coincides with a sovereign bond curve flattening or an emerging market currency squeeze.

By pooling carry expressions across 4 distinct asset classes (global fixed income, 20+ currency pairs, equity index futures, and 24 commodities), portfolio variance scales down by \(\frac{1}{N} + \frac{N-1}{N} \bar{\rho}\), while the persistent risk premium (investor compensation for providing liquidity and bearing term/credit risk) compounds additively, doubling the realized Sharpe ratio from ~0.40 to 0.82.

Problem 4.8 · September 2019 SOFR Repo Spike Drag

On September 17, 2019, overnight US Treasury repo rates spiked from 2.20% to intraday highs of 10.00% (clearing at 5.25% average) due to corporate tax payment deadlines and Treasury settlement reserve drains. For a hedge fund holding a $2,000,000,000 notional long Treasury position funded overnight, calculate the additional 1-day dollar financing drag caused by a 300 bps repo rate jump.

Worked Solution

1. Position notional: \(V = \$2,000,000,000\).

2. Overnight rate surge: \(\Delta r = 3.00\% = 0.0300\).

3. 1-day financing cost increase: \(\text{Cost} = V \times \frac{\Delta r}{360} = \$2,000,000,000 \times \frac{0.0300}{360} = \$2,000,000,000 \times 0.00008333 \approx \$166,667\).

In a single trading day, an unexpected 300 bps repo blowout imposes over $166,000 of unhedged financing drag, illustrating why cash-futures basis funds maintain committed bilateral liquidity lines.

Problem 4.9 · Negative Carry in Yield Curve Inversion

During the 2022–2023 Federal Reserve hiking cycle, the US 2s10s yield curve inverted to -108 bps, with 10-year yields at 3.50% and 3-month Treasury bills / SOFR repo at 5.20%. Contrast the 1-year P&L of a long 10-year Treasury position under static rates vs. a 100 bps bull steepening rally (10Y yield falls to 2.50%). Modified duration is 8.2 years.

Worked Solution

1. Static Rates Scenario: Gross yield = +3.50%. Repo drag = -5.20%. Roll-down is negative or negligible (-0.10%). Net 1-year carry = \(3.50\% - 5.20\% - 0.10\% = -1.80\%\) (-180 bps loss on notional).

2. Bull Steepening Rally Scenario: Net carry drag remains ~-1.80%. Capital gain from 100 bps yield drop: \(-D \times \Delta y = -8.2 \times (-1.00\%) = +8.20\%\). Total net return = \(+8.20\% - 1.80\% = +6.40\%\).

The manager requires an aggressive yield rally just to overcome the substantial 180 bps negative financing drag of holding long duration in an inverted curve environment.

Research

Problem 4.10 · Endogenous Margin Spirals in Repo Markets

Synthesize Brunnermeier and Pedersen's (2009) model of market liquidity and funding liquidity. How does an initial exogenous price shock in sovereign bonds trigger a feedback loop of margin hikes, deleveraging, and price crashes?

Worked Solution

Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) demonstrate that funding liquidity (traders' capital and borrowing capacity) and market liquidity (the ease with which assets are traded) are mutually reinforcing. When a price shock hits collateral securities, leveraged funds suffer mark-to-market losses on equity capital.

Because dealer financing contracts feature value-at-risk based margins, higher asset volatility causes risk-averse dealer banks to increase repo haircuts \(h\). With reduced equity and elevated haircuts, traders face immediate margin calls, forcing asset liquidations. These fire sales depress market liquidity and further depress asset prices, causing a second-round spiral of margin increases and forced liquidations, explaining liquidity crashes during the 2008 GFC and March 2020.

Problem 4.11 · Currency Crash Risk and the Peso Problem

Evaluate why standard regressions of exchange rate changes on interest differentials (\(\Delta s_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta (r_t - r_t^*) + \epsilon_{t+1}\)) consistently find \(\beta < 1\) (and often \(\beta < 0\)), yet FX carry funds experience severe negative excess skewness.

Worked Solution

The empirical rejection of uncovered interest parity (\(\beta < 1\)) shows that high-interest currencies do not depreciate fast enough to eliminate carry profits on average; in fact, they often appreciate during tranquil times as global capital chases yield.

However, this is not a pure free lunch. The "peso problem" reflects the probability of a catastrophic, low-frequency devaluation event that is rarely observed in small sample windows. When global market volatility surges (VIX spikes), carry trades unwind violently as investors flee back to low-yielding funding currencies (USD, JPY, CHF). The high Sharpe ratio during calm regimes is compensation for bearing systemic tail risk and crash vulnerability (Brunnermeier et al. 2008).

Problem 4.12 · Term Structure Roll-Down Optimization under Convexity Constraints

Formulate the optimal portfolio problem of maximizing net curve carry and roll-down subject to zero net DV01, zero net duration, and a minimum target convexity constraint.

Worked Solution

Let \(\mathbf{w} \in \R^N\) be the vector of portfolio weights across \(N\) government bond tenors. Let \(\mathbf{c} \in \R^N\) be the net carry and roll-down vector per dollar, \(\mathbf{d} \in \R^N\) be the vector of DV01 sensitivities, and \(\mathbf{C} \in \R^N\) be the bond convexity sensitivities. The optimization problem is:

\[ \max_{\mathbf{w}} \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{c} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{d} = 0, \quad \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{C} \ge C_{\min}, \quad \|\mathbf{w}\|_1 \le L_{\max} \]

This is a linear programming problem (or quadratic program if transaction costs/covariance penalties are included). The solution constructs barbell or butterfly configurations that harvest positive carry in steep curve segments while protecting against large parallel yield shocks through the positive convexity floor \(C_{\min}\).

8. Key Results and Formulas

Multi-Asset Forward Carry \(C_t(\tau) = \frac{S_t - F_{t, t+\tau}}{F_{t, t+\tau}}\) Expected excess return from holding an asset assuming unchanged spot prices.
Bond Return Decomposition \(HPR \approx y \Delta t + D \left(-\frac{\partial y}{\partial T}\right)\Delta t - D \Delta y_{\text{curve}} - r_{\text{repo}} \Delta t\) Splits holding return into yield carry, curve roll-down, curve shift P&L, and repo drag.

9. Spaced Retrieval Quiz

  1. How is multi-asset forward carry formally defined for a forward contract with price \(F_{t, t+\tau}\) and current spot \(S_t\)?

    Options

    Forward carry is the excess return of holding the asset assuming spot remains static: \(\frac{S_t - F_{t, t+\tau}}{F_{t, t+\tau}}\) (Koijen et al. 2018).

  2. Along an upward-sloping yield curve, what generates the "roll-down" return on a fixed-rate bond?

    Options

    As time passes, the bond's remaining maturity shortens, rolling down the curve to lower yields and producing capital appreciation if the curve shape persists.

  3. What is a "negative-carry trap" in macro strategy construction?

    Options

    A negative-carry trap occurs when financing costs exceed gross yield and roll-down (common in inverted curves), continually bleeding capital while waiting for a thesis to play out.

  4. Why does an outright currency carry trade exhibit pronounced negative return skewness?

    Options

    Currency carry strategies earn steady interest differentials during quiet regimes but suffer abrupt, violent unwinds during flight-to-safety episodes (Brunnermeier et al. 2008).

  5. In repo market operations, how does an increase in haircut \(h\) affect a leveraged strategy?

    Options

    Haircut expansion reduces permissible leverage (\(1/h\)), requiring immediate equity injections or forced liquidation of positions.

  6. Under the continuous-time Pure Expectations Hypothesis of interest rates, what is the expected net carry of any duration-neutral curve trade?

    Options

    Under PEH with zero risk premia, forward rates are unbiased expectations of future spot rates, so expected excess return over financing is zero everywhere.

  7. In a commodity futures market, what term structure condition produces positive roll yield for a long position?

    Options

    In backwardation, futures trade at a discount to spot, rolling upward toward the spot price as delivery approaches, generating positive roll yield.

  8. Which curve segment typically provides the highest roll-down return per year on a standard upward-sloping sovereign yield curve?

    Options

    While 30Y has higher duration, its slope is near zero. Front-end 1Y has steep slope but low duration. The 3Y–7Y belly maximizes the product \(D \cdot \Delta y_{\text{roll}}\).
