Options vs. Delta-One: Convexity & Asymmetry

Expressing a macroeconomic thesis via linear delta-one instruments locks the investor into symmetric upside and downside exposure. When economic uncertainty is elevated or tail outcomes predominate, linear expressions expose portfolios to catastrophic path dependency and liquidation risk. Options transform linear underlying movements into positively convex, path-independent terminal payoffs with structurally defined maximum loss. However, options extract an unavoidable structural toll: continuous theta decay and the volatility variance risk premium. Quantitative macro managers must rigorously evaluate whether the convexity of expression justifies its negative carry.

1. Payoff Geometry: Linear vs. Non-Linear Expressions

Every macro instrument belongs to one of two fundamental payoff families: linear delta-one contracts or non-linear contingent claims.

Definition 3.1 (options vs. delta-one expression) The strategic choice between an options vs. delta-one expression is the architectural decision in a trade expression to monetize a macroeconomic view using either:

  1. Delta-One Instruments: Contracts whose value changes linearly with the underlying asset price (\(\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = \text{const}\), \(\Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = 0\)), such as spot assets, futures, forwards, and swaps. Delta-one expressions provide 100% price pass-through, zero theta decay, but strictly symmetric downside risk.
  2. Options Expressions: Non-linear contingent claims whose terminal value depends convexly on the underlying state (\(\Gamma > 0\)), such as calls, puts, straddles, and swaptions. Options provide asymmetric upside participation, bounded maximum loss, and path independence, at the cost of continuous theta decay and the variance risk premium.

In delta-one expressions, path dependency is acute: an adverse intraday excursion can trigger a margin call or breach a stop-out tripwire even if the macro view eventually proves correct at horizon \(T\). In contrast, long European options grant complete path immunity prior to expiration.

2. Convexity of Expression and Gamma Dynamics

Convexity is the defining mathematical property of non-linear trade expressions, governing how sensitivity to the macro view expands as the trade moves in the investor's favor and contracts when it moves against them.

Definition 3.2 (convexity of a trade expression) The convexity of a trade expression is the positive second derivative of the expression's portfolio value \(V(S)\) with respect to the underlying macroeconomic asset or state variable \(S\):

\[ \Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} > 0 \]

Expressed in dollar terms per percentage move, dollar convexity is \(\mathcal{C} = \frac{1}{2} S^2 \Gamma\). A positively convex expression increases net delta exposure during favorable asset moves and decreases net delta exposure during adverse moves.

Theorem 3.1 (Convexity Gains under Mean-Preserving Spreads) Let \(V(S)\) be a strictly convex trade expression payoff (\(V''(S) > 0\) for all \(S\)). Let \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) be two macroeconomic state distributions with identical expected value \(\E[S_1] = \E[S_2] = S_0\), where \(S_2\) is a mean-preserving spread of \(S_1\) (i.e., \(S_2\) has higher uncertainty/volatility in the sense of second-order stochastic dominance). Then the expected payoff of the convex expression is strictly higher under greater uncertainty:

\[ \E[V(S_2)] > \E[V(S_1)] \ge V(S_0) \]

Proof of Theorem 3.1 By Jensen's Inequality, for any strictly convex function \(V\) and non-degenerate random variable \(S\), \(\E[V(S)] > V(\E[S])\). By Rothschild and Stiglitz (1970), a random variable \(S_2\) is a mean-preserving spread of \(S_1\) if and only if for every convex function \(\phi\), \(\E[\phi(S_2)] \ge \E[\phi(S_1)]\). Since \(V''(S) > 0\) strictly everywhere, the inequality is strict for all distributions with non-identical variance: \(\E[V(S_2)] > \E[V(S_1)] \ge V(S_0)\). \(\blacksquare\)

3. Risk–Reward Asymmetry and Skewness Engineering

Institutional investors demand asymmetric payoff profiles: structures where potential gains substantially exceed potential losses under reasonable scenario intervals.

Definition 3.3 (risk–reward asymmetry) The risk–reward asymmetry of a trade expression is the ratio \(\mathcal{A}\) of expected gains to expected losses under a specified scenario probability measure \(\Prob^M\):

\[ \mathcal{A} = \frac{\E^M[\max(0, \Delta \Pi)]}{\E^M[\max(0, -\Delta \Pi)]} \]

where \(\Delta \Pi\) is the dollar P&L of the position over horizon \(T\). An expression exhibits positive risk-reward asymmetry when \(\mathcal{A} > 1.0\). For bounded-downside option structures where maximum loss is limited to the option premium \(P_{\text{opt}}\), the downside is strictly bounded: \(\max(0, -\Delta \Pi) \le P_{\text{opt}}\), whereas upside is unbounded.

Macro managers engineer asymmetry by combining long options with out-of-the-money financing legs (collars, ratio spreads, and risk reversals), structuring expressions that match the anticipated skewness of macroeconomic shocks (Pedersen 2015).

4. The Cost of Convexity: Theta Decay and Volatility Drag

Convexity is never free. In arbitrage-free markets, the Black-Scholes partial differential equation establishes a strict conservation law between time decay (\(\Theta\)) and spatial convexity (\(\Gamma\)):

\[ \Theta + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma + r S \Delta - r V = 0 \]

For a delta-neutral portfolio (\(\Delta = 0\)) with zero interest rate, this simplifies to \(\Theta = -\frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma\). Positive gamma (\(\Gamma > 0\)) mathematically forces negative theta (\(\Theta < 0\)). The investor pays for convexity every calendar day through option premium bleed.

Lab 1 · Convexity and Payoff Asymmetry Comparator

Simulates delta-one, outright call, call spread, and straddle payoffs under fat-tailed macroeconomic shocks (Student-t with df=4) to quantify empirical asymmetry ratios and maximum loss limits.

"""Lab GM4-L03-1: Convexity of Expression and Payoff Asymmetry: Options vs Delta-One.

Simulates terminal payoffs of delta-one, outright call, call spread, and straddle
under heavy-tailed macroeconomic shocks (Student-t distribution), demonstrating
convexity benefits and empirical risk-reward asymmetry ratios.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def bsm_call_price(s: float, k: float, v: float, t: float, r: float) -> float:
    sqt = v * math.sqrt(t)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * v * v) * t) / sqt
    d2 = d1 - sqt
    c1 = 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(d1 / math.sqrt(2.0)))
    c2 = 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(d2 / math.sqrt(2.0)))
    return s * c1 - k * math.exp(-r * t) * c2


def bsm_put_price(s: float, k: float, v: float, t: float, r: float) -> float:
    c = bsm_call_price(s, k, v, t, r)
    return c + k * math.exp(-r * t) - s


def compute_asymmetry(pnl: np.ndarray) -> float:
    upside = np.maximum(0.0, pnl)
    downside = np.maximum(0.0, -pnl)
    mu_up = float(np.mean(upside))
    mu_down = float(np.mean(downside))
    return mu_up / mu_down if mu_down > 1e-12 else float("inf")


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    s0 = 100.0
    vol = 0.22
    t = 0.5  # 6-month expiry
    r = 0.04
    n_sims = 20_000

    # Heavy-tailed macro shock distribution: Student-t with df=4, scaled to match annualized vol
    df = 4
    scale = vol * math.sqrt(t) * math.sqrt((df - 2) / df)
    log_shocks = rng.standard_t(df, size=n_sims) * scale
    terminal_spots = s0 * np.exp(log_shocks)

    # Instrument 1: Delta-One long position (scaled to 1.0 delta)
    pnl_delta_one = terminal_spots - s0

    # Instrument 2: Long ATM Call (K=100)
    c_atm = bsm_call_price(s0, 100.0, vol, t, r)
    payoff_call = np.maximum(0.0, terminal_spots - 100.0)
    pnl_call = payoff_call - c_atm

    # Instrument 3: Call Spread (Long 100 Call, Short 110 Call)
    c_otm = bsm_call_price(s0, 110.0, vol, t, r)
    spread_cost = c_atm - c_otm
    payoff_spread = np.maximum(0.0, terminal_spots - 100.0) - np.maximum(0.0, terminal_spots - 110.0)
    pnl_spread = payoff_spread - spread_cost

    # Instrument 4: Long Straddle (Long 100 Call + Long 100 Put)
    p_atm = bsm_put_price(s0, 100.0, vol, t, r)
    straddle_cost = c_atm + p_atm
    payoff_straddle = np.maximum(0.0, terminal_spots - 100.0) + np.maximum(0.0, 100.0 - terminal_spots)
    pnl_straddle = payoff_straddle - straddle_cost

    # Asymmetry and skewness metrics
    def stats(pnl: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
        mean_val = float(np.mean(pnl))
        std_val = float(np.std(pnl))
        skew_val = float(np.mean(((pnl - mean_val) / std_val) ** 3))
        asym = compute_asymmetry(pnl)
        return mean_val, std_val, skew_val, asym

    m_d1, s_d1, sk_d1, as_d1 = stats(pnl_delta_one)
    m_c, s_c, sk_c, as_c = stats(pnl_call)
    m_cs, s_cs, sk_cs, as_cs = stats(pnl_spread)
    m_st, s_st, sk_st, as_st = stats(pnl_straddle)

    print("--- 1. Delta-One Underlying ---")
    print(f"Mean P&L: {m_d1:.2f} | Std: {s_d1:.2f} | Skewness: {sk_d1:.2f} | Asymmetry Ratio: {as_d1:.3f}")
    print(f"Max Loss: {float(np.min(pnl_delta_one)):.2f} | Max Gain: {float(np.max(pnl_delta_one)):.2f}")

    print("\n--- 2. Long ATM Call Option ---")
    print(f"Mean P&L: {m_c:.2f} | Std: {s_c:.2f} | Skewness: {sk_c:.2f} | Asymmetry Ratio: {as_c:.3f}")
    print(f"Max Loss: {float(np.min(pnl_call)):.2f} | Max Gain: {float(np.max(pnl_call)):.2f}")

    print("\n--- 3. Bull Call Spread (100/110) ---")
    print(f"Mean P&L: {m_cs:.2f} | Std: {s_cs:.2f} | Skewness: {sk_cs:.2f} | Asymmetry Ratio: {as_cs:.3f}")
    print(f"Max Loss: {float(np.min(pnl_spread)):.2f} | Max Gain: {float(np.max(pnl_spread)):.2f}")

    print("\n--- 4. Long ATM Straddle ---")
    print(f"Mean P&L: {m_st:.2f} | Std: {s_st:.2f} | Skewness: {sk_st:.2f} | Asymmetry Ratio: {as_st:.3f}")
    print(f"Max Loss: {float(np.min(pnl_straddle)):.2f} | Max Gain: {float(np.max(pnl_straddle)):.2f}")


main()

5. Where the Canon Breaks

Textbook derivatives theory assumes that option implied volatility surfaces are stationary and that volatility risk can be delta-hedged continuously without friction. In quantitative macro practice, implied volatility is itself a stochastic macroeconomic state variable that correlates strongly with market direction (equity leverage effect, FX skewness). In tail crises, implied volatility explodes while underlying asset liquidity vanishes. As documented by Pedersen (2015) and Brunnermeier, Nagel, and Pedersen (2008), delta-hedging a long option portfolio under discontinuous price jumps and volatility spikes introduces massive gamma slippage, while short options positions suffer catastrophic margin calls during volatility regime shifts.

6. Misconceptions

  • Misconception: Buying out-of-the-money options is always a low-risk trade because risk is limited to the premium paid.

    Why it is wrong: While the maximum loss per contract is bounded, out-of-the-money options have a high probability of expiring completely worthless. Over repeated trials, continuous theta decay and the volatility variance risk premium create a severe negative-drift drag that rapidly destroys fund equity if market timing is imperfect (Ilmanen 2011).

  • Misconception: Delta-one futures positions are always cheaper to hold than options.

    Why it is wrong: Delta-one futures incur financing costs, dividend drag, and cash roll-over friction. More importantly, maintaining a delta-one position through adverse volatility spikes requires continuous cash variation margin, which can force liquidation during liquidity panics even if terminal spot prices eventually recover.

  • Misconception: An investor with a directional view should always buy outright calls or puts.

    Why it is wrong: Outright options require paying the full implied volatility premium. If market volatility is already elevated, buying an outright call exposes the trader to severe "volatility crush" post-announcement. Expressing the view via a vertical spread (e.g., bull call spread) or risk reversal sells rich out-of-the-money volatility to finance the directional exposure.

Problem 3.1 · Black-Scholes Greeks Calculation

An equity index sits at spot \(S = 100\). A 6-month European call option (\(T = 0.5\)) has strike \(K = 100\), annualized implied volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\), and risk-free interest rate \(r = 0.04\). Compute the exact analytical values of \(d_1\), \(d_2\), Delta (\(\Delta\)), and Gamma (\(\Gamma\)).

Worked Solution

1. \(\sigma \sqrt{T} = 0.20 \times \sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.20 \times 0.707107 = 0.141421\).

2. \(d_1 = \frac{\ln(100/100) + (0.04 + 0.5 \times 0.04) \times 0.5}{0.141421} = \frac{0 + 0.06 \times 0.5}{0.141421} = \frac{0.03}{0.141421} \approx 0.212132\).

3. \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T} = 0.212132 - 0.141421 = 0.070711\).

4. \(\Delta = N(d_1) = N(0.2121) \approx 0.5840\).

5. \(\phi(d_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-0.5 \times (0.2121)^2} \approx 0.398942 \times 0.977759 \approx 0.390069\).

6. \(\Gamma = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{T}} = \frac{0.390069}{100 \times 0.141421} = \frac{0.390069}{14.1421} \approx 0.027582\).

Problem 3.2 · Theta-Gamma Trade-off

Using the option from Problem 3.1, verify the Black-Scholes zero-interest-rate relationship \(\Theta \approx -\frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma\). Calculate the daily theta decay in dollar terms per 100 option contracts (where 1 contract covers 100 shares).

Worked Solution

1. Theoretical annual theta: \(\Theta = -\frac{1}{2} \times (0.20)^2 \times (100)^2 \times 0.027582 = -0.5 \times 0.04 \times 10,000 \times 0.027582 = -200 \times 0.027582 = -5.5164\) per share per year.

2. Daily theta (252 trading days): \(\Theta_{\text{daily}} = \frac{-5.5164}{252} \approx -\$0.02189\) per share.

3. For 100 contracts (10,000 shares): \(\text{Daily Dollar Decay} = 10,000 \times (-\$0.02189) = -\$218.90\) per day.

Problem 3.3 · Asymmetry Ratio of a Call Spread

A macro trader purchases a 100/110 call spread for a net premium of $3.50. Under an adversarial scenario distribution, the probability of finishing below 100 is 50% (losing $3.50), the probability of finishing between 100 and 110 has an average payout of $4.00 (net gain $0.50) with 25% probability, and the probability of finishing above 110 is 25% (net gain $6.50). Compute the expected upside, expected downside, and the risk-reward asymmetry ratio \(\mathcal{A}\).

Worked Solution

1. Expected downside: \(\mu_- = 0.50 \times \$3.50 = \$1.75\).

2. Expected upside: \(\mu_+ = 0.25 \times \$0.50 + 0.25 \times \$6.50 = \$0.125 + \$1.625 = \$1.75\).

3. Asymmetry ratio: \(\mathcal{A} = \frac{\mu_+}{\mu_-} = \frac{\$1.75}{\$1.75} = 1.000\).

The spread has neutral asymmetry (\(\mathcal{A} = 1.0\)); to improve asymmetry, the trader would need higher upside probability or a cheaper entry premium.

Proof

Problem 3.4 · Mathematical Proof of Positive Gamma for Vanilla Options

Prove that for any standard European call or put option under the Black-Scholes model with \(S > 0\), \(\sigma > 0\), and \(T > 0\), the gamma \(\Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\) is strictly positive everywhere.

Worked Solution

The Black-Scholes call price is \(C(S) = S N(d_1) - K e^{-r T} N(d_2)\), where \(d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}}\) and \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T}\).

Differentiating with respect to \(S\): \(\Delta = \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} = N(d_1) + S \phi(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} - K e^{-r T} \phi(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial S}\).

Note that \(\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} = \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial S} = \frac{1}{S \sigma \sqrt{T}}\). The identity \(S \phi(d_1) = K e^{-r T} \phi(d_2)\) holds identically: \(\ln(S \phi(d_1)) - \ln(K e^{-r T} \phi(d_2)) = \ln(S/K) + r T - \frac{1}{2}(d_1^2 - d_2^2) = \ln(S/K) + r T - \frac{1}{2}(d_1 - d_2)(d_1 + d_2) = \ln(S/K) + r T - \frac{1}{2}(\sigma \sqrt{T})(\frac{2 \ln(S/K) + 2 r T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}}) = 0\).

Thus, the secondary terms cancel: \(\Delta = N(d_1)\).

Differentiating again: \(\Gamma = \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial S} = \phi(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial S} = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S \sigma \sqrt{T}}\).

Since the Gaussian density \(\phi(d_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-d_1^2/2} > 0\) strictly for all real \(d_1\), and \(S, \sigma, \sqrt{T} > 0\), it follows that \(\Gamma > 0\) strictly for all \(S > 0\). For puts, put-call parity \(P = C - S + K e^{-r T}\) implies \(\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial S^2} = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} = \Gamma > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 3.5 · Asymptotic Payoff Invariance of Straddles

Prove that a delta-neutral ATM straddle has asymptotic terminal payoff slope of \(+1.0\) as \(S_T \to \infty\) and \(-1.0\) as \(S_T \to 0\), and that its asymmetry ratio converges to \(1.0\) under any symmetric shock distribution around the forward price.

Worked Solution

A straddle consists of long 1 call and long 1 put with identical strike \(K = F = S e^{r T}\). The terminal payoff is \(V(S_T) = \max(0, S_T - K) + \max(0, K - S_T) = |S_T - K|\).

For \(S_T > K\), \(V(S_T) = S_T - K\), so \(\frac{d V}{d S_T} = +1.0\). As \(S_T \to \infty\), the payoff is linear with slope \(+1.0\).

For \(S_T < K\), \(V(S_T) = K - S_T\), so \(\frac{d V}{d S_T} = -1.0\). As \(S_T \to 0\), the payoff is linear with slope \(-1.0\).

Let \(\Delta S = S_T - K\). Then net P&L is \(\Pi(\Delta S) = |\Delta S| - V_0\). If the probability density \(f(\Delta S)\) is symmetric around 0 (\(f(x) = f(-x)\)), then \(\E[\max(0, \Pi)] = \int_{|\Delta S| > V_0} (|\Delta S| - V_0) f(\Delta S) d\Delta S = 2 \int_{V_0}^\infty (x - V_0) f(x) dx\).

Downside loss occurs when \(|\Delta S| < V_0\): \(\E[\max(0, -\Pi)] = \int_{-V_0}^{V_0} (V_0 - |x|) f(x) dx = 2 \int_0^{V_0} (V_0 - x) f(x) dx\).

Because the upside integral scales with the tail mass while the downside is bounded by \(V_0\), under symmetric distributions the ratio of positive to negative deviations is balanced, yielding \(\mathcal{A} = 1.0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 3.6 · Monotonicity of Call Prices with Respect to Volatility

Prove that European call option prices are strictly monotonically increasing in volatility (\(\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} > 0\)) for all \(S, K, T > 0\).

Worked Solution

Using the Black-Scholes formula, differentiate \(C = S N(d_1) - K e^{-r T} N(d_2)\) with respect to \(\sigma\):

\[ \mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} = S \phi(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} - K e^{-r T} \phi(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial \sigma} \]

Recall the identity \(S \phi(d_1) = K e^{-r T} \phi(d_2)\). Factoring gives:

\[ \mathcal{V} = S \phi(d_1) \left( \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} - \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial \sigma} \right) \]

Since \(d_1 - d_2 = \sigma \sqrt{T}\), we have \(\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} - \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma} (\sigma \sqrt{T}) = \sqrt{T}\).

Therefore, Vega is: \(\mathcal{V} = S \phi(d_1) \sqrt{T}\).

Since \(S > 0\), \(\sqrt{T} > 0\), and the standard normal density satisfies \(\phi(d_1) > 0\) everywhere, \(\mathcal{V} > 0\) strictly, proving that option value increases strictly with volatility. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem 3.7 · Empirical Variance Risk Premium Drag

Over a 10-year period, 1-month S&P 500 implied volatility averages 17.5%, while realized volatility averages 14.2%. On an option portfolio rolling $5,000,000 in monthly straddle premium, compute the annualized variance risk premium drag paid to market makers.

Worked Solution

The variance risk premium spread is \(\Delta \sigma = 17.5\% - 14.2\% = 3.3\%\) (330 bps of volatility).

Under ATM straddle approximations, straddle price scales approximately as \(V \approx 0.8 \sigma S \sqrt{T}\). The excess premium paid due to the variance risk premium is roughly proportional to \(\frac{17.5 - 14.2}{17.5} = \frac{3.3}{17.5} \approx 18.86\%\) of the premium.

Annual premium expenditure: \(12 \times \$5,000,000 = \$60,000,000\).

Annualized drag: \(\$60,000,000 \times 0.1886 \approx \$11,314,286\), illustrating the steep cost of permanent volatility insurance.

Problem 3.8 · Post-FOMC Volatility Crush

Prior to a scheduled FOMC meeting, 1-week S&P 500 ATM implied volatility trades at 28.0% due to event uncertainty. Following the announcement, the market rallies 0.80% while 1-week implied volatility collapses to 15.0%. For an outright long call option (\(\Delta = 0.50\), \(\mathcal{V} = \$0.15\) per vol point per share), calculate the net P&L per share.

Worked Solution

1. Delta gain: \(\Delta P_{\text{delta}} = \Delta \times \Delta S = 0.50 \times (+0.80) = +\$0.40\).

2. Vega loss (volatility crush): \(\Delta P_{\text{vega}} = \mathcal{V} \times \Delta \sigma = 0.15 \times (15.0 - 28.0) = 0.15 \times (-13.0) = -\$1.95\).

3. Net option P&L: \(\Delta P_{\text{net}} = +\$0.40 - \$1.95 = -\$1.55\) per share.

Despite correctly anticipating the market rally, the outright long call loses money due to volatility crush, demonstrating why spread structures are necessary for event trading.

Problem 3.9 · Risk Reversal Asymmetry in Foreign Exchange

In currency trading (e.g., USD/JPY), the 25-delta risk reversal quotes the volatility spread \(\sigma_{\text{call}} - \sigma_{\text{put}}\). When USD/JPY trades with a -2.5% risk reversal (puts trading at a 2.5 vol premium over calls), what does this reveal about market-implied crash asymmetry?

Worked Solution

A negative risk reversal means OTM yen calls (USD/JPY puts) trade at higher implied volatility than OTM yen puts. By the Breeden-Litzenberger relation, this implies that the market assigns substantial probability mass to sudden, violent downward crashes in USD/JPY (rapid yen appreciation during global carry unwinds), reflecting pronounced negative skewness in currency carry returns (Brunnermeier et al. 2008).

Research

Problem 3.10 · Dynamic Delta Hedging under Jump Discontinuities

Synthesize Merton's (1976) jump-diffusion framework: why does the Black-Scholes continuous-time replication argument fail in the presence of discrete macroeconomic jump shocks?

Worked Solution

Black-Scholes delta hedging relies on market completeness and local martingale representation under continuous Brownian filtration \(d W_t\). Under a compound Poisson jump process \(d q_t\) with random jump amplitude \(Y\), the underlying asset price exhibits discrete discontinuities: \(S_{t} - S_{t-} = S_{t-} (Y - 1)\). Because a jump can transition the asset across an infinite continuum of values in zero elapsed time, a single delta hedge cannot span the two independent sources of risk (Brownian diffusion and Poisson jump magnitude). The market becomes fundamentally incomplete, and the delta-hedger incurs unavoidable jump slippage.

Problem 3.11 · Convexity Engineering in Macro Sovereign Debt

Explain how high-coupon, low-duration bonds versus low-coupon, high-duration bonds generate divergent convexity profiles, and how quantitative macro funds structure "convexity barbell" trades.

Worked Solution

Bond convexity scales quadratically with maturity (\(\approx T^2\)) and inversely with coupon rate. Zero-coupon long bonds have the highest positive convexity per dollar of duration. A convexity barbell goes long high-convexity long-duration paper (e.g., 30Y STRIPS) and short low-convexity short-duration paper (e.g., 5Y notes) weighted to net zero DV01. The portfolio captures positive net convexity, profiting from large yield moves in either direction while financing the negative theta decay via coupon carry.

Problem 3.12 · Volatility Smirk and Central Bank Forward Guidance

Analyze how the adoption of central bank forward guidance post-2008 flattened short-term interest rate swaption volatility smiles while steepening out-of-the-money tail skewness.

Worked Solution

By credibly committing to keep policy rates pinned near zero for an extended horizon, forward guidance compressed short-rate realized volatility, flattening the ATM swaption smile at short maturities. However, forward guidance amplified tail risk regarding eventual exit regimes: the probability distribution became bimodal, with one mode centered at perpetual stagnation and the other at rapid inflation catch-up. This bimodal risk-neutral density manifested as elevated swaption wing skewness (smirk).

9. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What distinguishes a delta-one expression from an options expression?

    Options

    Delta-one instruments provide linear passthrough of underlying price moves (\(\Gamma = 0\)), while options deliver non-linear convex payoffs (\(\Gamma > 0\)).

  2. What does Theorem 3.1 (Convexity Gains under Mean-Preserving Spreads) prove?

    Options

    By Jensen's inequality and second-order stochastic dominance, higher dispersion in the underlying state expands the expected value of convex functions.

  3. How is risk-reward asymmetry \(\mathcal{A}\) mathematically defined?

    Options

    Risk-reward asymmetry measures the dollar magnitude of expected favorable outcomes relative to expected adverse outcomes.

  4. Under the Black-Scholes PDE with zero interest rate, what is the exact relationship between Theta (\(\Theta\)) and Gamma (\(\Gamma\))?

    Options

    The Black-Scholes PDE establishes that time decay exactly offsets the expected gains from local gamma convexity in arbitrage-free markets.

  5. Why does an outright long call option often lose money after a major macroeconomic announcement despite the underlying asset rallying?

    Options

    Event risk resolution drives a sharp collapse in implied volatility ("volatility crush"), inflicting severe mark-to-market vega losses on long option holders.

  6. What structural advantage do options provide over delta-one expressions regarding path dependency?

    Options

    Because maximum loss is bounded by the premium paid, long options cannot be liquidated by variation margin calls during adverse interim price swings.

  7. How does a vertical bull call spread (e.g., long 100 call, short 110 call) mitigate negative carry relative to an outright call?

    Options

    Selling the out-of-the-money call harvests theta from the short leg, reducing net premium expenditure and lowering the breakeven hurdle.

  8. What does a negative 25-delta risk reversal in foreign exchange indicate?

    Options

    Risk reversals quote the vol differential between calls and puts; higher put vol indicates market willingness to pay a premium for downside crash protection.
