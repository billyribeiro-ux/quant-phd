High-Frequency Identification & FOMC Event Studies

Central banks adjust policy rates systematically in response to macro conditions, confounding causal effects with endogenous responses. Estimating how monetary shocks affect asset prices requires isolating unexpected policy changes. High-frequency identification around Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) announcements solves this by measuring futures price jumps in narrow 30-minute windows. Because Fed funds futures settle on monthly average rates, raw price changes must be scaled to extract target surprises. Understanding these contract mechanics and equity sensitivities is essential for quantitative macro trading.

1. Fed Funds Futures Mechanics & Contract Settlement

30-Day Federal Funds Futures (contract code ZQ traded on the Chicago Board of Trade) settle on the arithmetic average of the daily effective federal funds rate (EFFR) over the calendar month of contract expiration. Let \(M\) denote the total number of calendar days in month \(m\), and let \(r_\tau\) denote the effective federal funds rate on calendar day \(\tau \in \{1, \dots, M\}\). The terminal settlement price of the futures contract expiring in month \(m\) is defined as:

\[ P_{\text{settle}} = 100 - \bar{r}_m = 100 - \frac{1}{M} \sum_{\tau=1}^M r_\tau \]

Prior to expiration, the quoted price of the futures contract reflects market expectations of this monthly average: \(f_t = 100 - \E_t[\bar{r}_m]\). Because weekend and holiday dates inherit the EFFR established on the preceding business day, each calendar day carries equal weight in the monthly average.

Definition 1.1 (FOMC event study) An FOMC event study is a high-frequency empirical identification framework that measures asset price revisions in a narrow time window \([t - \Delta t_{\text{pre}}, t + \Delta t_{\text{post}}]\) (typically 30 minutes: 10 minutes prior to 20 minutes post-announcement) surrounding scheduled Federal Reserve policy announcements. Under the identifying assumption that no confounding macroeconomic information arrives during the window, the observed jump in asset prices represents the causal impact of the unanticipated component of the monetary policy decision.

In standard daily event studies, background macroeconomic noise, unexpected earnings releases, and endogenous central bank feedback introduce substantial measurement error. Narrow intraday event windows isolate the exogenous monetary shock by drastically raising the signal-to-noise ratio of policy shocks relative to confounding news.

2. The Kuttner Futures Scaling Formula

Suppose an FOMC rate decision occurs on day \(d\) of a calendar month containing \(M\) days. Let \(i_{\text{pre}}\) denote the target rate prevailing prior to day \(d\), and suppose the Federal Reserve announces an unanticipated discrete target rate change \(\Delta i_t^u\). For all remaining days \(\tau \in \{d, \dots, M\}\), the effective rate shifts by \(\Delta i_t^u\).

Theorem 1.1 (Kuttner 2001 Futures Scaling) Let \(f_{t, \text{pre}}\) and \(f_{t, \text{post}}\) denote the implied futures rate (\(100 - P_t\)) immediately before and immediately after an FOMC announcement occurring on calendar day \(d\) of a month with \(M\) days. Under the assumption that the target change is expected to persist through the end of the month, the discrete target surprise \(\Delta i_t^u\) is given by: \[ \Delta i_t^u = \frac{M}{M - d} (f_{t, \text{post}} - f_{t, \text{pre}}) \] where \(\frac{M}{M - d} \ge 1.0\) represents the Kuttner futures scaling factor.

Proof The implied futures rate prior to the announcement incorporates expectations of rates through day \(d-1\) and pre-announcement expectations for days \(d\) to \(M\): \[ f_{t, \text{pre}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{\tau=1}^{d-1} r_\tau + \frac{M - d}{M} \E_{\text{pre}}[r_\tau \mid \tau \ge d] \] Immediately following the announcement on day \(d\), the expectations for days \(\tau \ge d\) revise to \(\E_{\text{post}}[r_\tau] = \E_{\text{pre}}[r_\tau] + \Delta i_t^u\). Realized rates for days \(\tau < d\) are fixed historical facts and cannot revise. Therefore, the post-announcement implied futures rate is: \[ f_{t, \text{post}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{\tau=1}^{d-1} r_\tau + \frac{M - d}{M} \left(\E_{\text{pre}}[r_\tau \mid \tau \ge d] + \Delta i_t^u\right) \] Subtracting \(f_{t, \text{pre}}\) from \(f_{t, \text{post}}\) yields: \[ f_{t, \text{post}} - f_{t, \text{pre}} = \frac{M - d}{M} \Delta i_t^u \] Multiplying both sides by \(\frac{M}{M - d}\) establishes the scaling formula. This completes the derivation.

Remark 1.1 (Month-End Boundary Adjustment) When an announcement occurs on the final day (\(d = M\)) or penultimate day (\(d = M - 1\)) of the month, the denominator \(M - d\) approaches or equals zero, causing the scaling factor to diverge and amplifying microstructure noise. In empirical practice per Kuttner (2001), researchers switch to using the next-month futures contract (\(m+1\)) price jump: \[ \Delta i_t^u = f_{t, \text{post}}^{(m+1)} - f_{t, \text{pre}}^{(m+1)} \] Because the new policy target applies to the entire subsequent month, the next-month contract jump requires no scaling factor (\(\frac{M_{m+1}}{M_{m+1}} = 1.0\)).

3. Equity Response to Monetary Policy Surprises

Armed with exogenous monetary policy target surprises \(\Delta i_t^u\), we estimate the contemporaneous response of equity asset prices. In their landmark empirical analysis, Bernanke & Kuttner (2005) estimated the OLS regression of broad equity returns on monetary surprises across FOMC announcement days:

\[ R_t = \alpha + \beta \Delta i_t^u + \varepsilon_t \]

where \(R_t\) is the CRSP value-weighted or S&P 500 equity return, and \(\Delta i_t^u\) is measured in basis points. Bernanke & Kuttner obtain an estimated slope coefficient of \(\hat{\beta} \approx -4.7\) (or approximately \(-0.047\) bps equity return per 1 bps surprise rate hike). Thus, an unanticipated 25 bps rate hike induces an immediate \(-1.2\%\) drop in broad equities.

Example 1.1 (Target Surprise on Day 15) Consider an FOMC rate decision on October 15 (\(M = 31, d = 15\)). Spot-month Fed funds futures price jumps from 95.80 to 95.72 immediately following the 2:00 PM statement release. Implied futures rate jumps from \(4.20\%\) to \(4.28\%\) (\(\Delta f = +8\) bps). The discrete target surprise is: \[ \Delta i_t^u = \frac{31}{31 - 15} \times 8.0 \text{ bps} = \frac{31}{16} \times 8.0 = 1.9375 \times 8.0 = 15.5 \text{ bps} \] Applying the Bernanke-Kuttner sensitivity \(\beta = -4.7\), expected equity return is: \[ \E[R_t] = -4.7 \times \frac{15.5}{100} = -0.73\% = -73 \text{ bps} \]

Lab 1 · High-Frequency Surprise Identification & Equity Regression

Computing Kuttner surprises from futures jumps and regressing equity returns verifies that surprise rate increases generate a negative equity sensitivity beta (\(\beta < 0\)).

"""Lab GM3-L01-1: High-Frequency Monetary Surprise Identification.

Implements the Kuttner (2001) Fed funds futures scaling formula and estimates
the Bernanke & Kuttner (2005) equity sensitivity regression.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def compute_kuttner_surprises(
    pre_rates: np.ndarray,
    post_rates: np.ndarray,
    days_in_month: np.ndarray,
    announcement_days: np.ndarray,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Compute discrete target rate surprises in basis points using Kuttner (2001) formula."""
    delta_f = post_rates - pre_rates
    n = len(pre_rates)
    surprises = np.zeros(n, dtype=np.float64)
    for i in range(n):
        m = float(days_in_month[i])
        d = float(announcement_days[i])
        scale = 1.0 if d >= m - 1 else m / (m - d)
        surprises[i] = scale * delta_f[i] * 100.0
    return surprises


def regress_ols(x: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Perform simple OLS regression y = alpha + beta * x + epsilon."""
    n = len(x)
    mean_x = float(np.mean(x))
    mean_y = float(np.mean(y))
    ss_xx = float(np.sum((x - mean_x) ** 2))
    ss_xy = float(np.sum((x - mean_x) * (y - mean_y)))
    beta = ss_xy / ss_xx
    alpha = mean_y - beta * mean_x
    residuals = y - (alpha + beta * x)
    ss_res = float(np.sum(residuals**2))
    ss_tot = float(np.sum((y - mean_y) ** 2))
    r2 = 1.0 - (ss_res / ss_tot)
    se_beta = math.sqrt((ss_res / (n - 2)) / ss_xx)
    t_stat = beta / se_beta
    return {
        "alpha": alpha,
        "beta": beta,
        "r2": r2,
        "t_stat": t_stat,
        "se_beta": se_beta,
    }


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    n_meetings = 100

    # Calendar metadata: M in {28, 30, 31}, d in [2, M-2]
    days_in_month = rng.choice([28, 30, 31], size=n_meetings, p=[0.08, 0.35, 0.57])
    announcement_days = np.array([int(rng.integers(2, int(m) - 2)) for m in days_in_month], dtype=int)

    # True latent discrete target surprise in basis points (mean 0, std ~ 7 bps)
    true_surprises_bps = rng.normal(0.0, 7.0, size=n_meetings)

    # Derive pre and post futures rates
    pre_rates = 3.5 + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0.0, 0.05, size=n_meetings))
    scale_factors = days_in_month / (days_in_month - announcement_days)
    futures_jumps_bps = true_surprises_bps / scale_factors
    post_rates = pre_rates + (futures_jumps_bps / 100.0)

    # Calculate Kuttner surprises
    estimated_surprises = compute_kuttner_surprises(pre_rates, post_rates, days_in_month, announcement_days)

    # Equity return reaction per Bernanke & Kuttner (2005): beta ~ -4.7 (% return per 100 bps surprise)
    # in basis points: return_bps = -0.047 * surprise_bps + noise
    equity_returns_bps = -0.0468 * estimated_surprises + rng.normal(0.0, 12.0, size=n_meetings)

    reg = regress_ols(estimated_surprises, equity_returns_bps)

    print("Kuttner Surprises Identified:", len(estimated_surprises))
    print("Mean Surprise (bps):", round(float(np.mean(estimated_surprises)), 4))
    print("Std Surprise (bps):", round(float(np.std(estimated_surprises, ddof=1)), 4))
    print("OLS Alpha (bps):", round(reg["alpha"], 4))
    print("OLS Beta (equity sensitivity):", round(reg["beta"], 4))
    print("OLS t-statistic:", round(reg["t_stat"], 4))
    print("OLS R-squared:", round(reg["r2"], 4))


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks: Zero Lower Bound & Forward Guidance

The classical Kuttner scaling formula assumes that monetary policy decisions are unidimensional discrete changes in the current overnight policy rate target. Between December 2008 and December 2015, and again from March 2020 to March 2022, the Federal Reserve pinned the target federal funds rate at the Zero Lower Bound (ZLB, \(0.00\%\)–\(0.25\%\)). During ZLB regimes, current target surprises \(\Delta i_t^u\) were mechanically zero on virtually every FOMC meeting.

However, financial markets exhibited massive asset price volatility around FOMC statements during these periods. This breakdown occurs because monetary policy operates along multiple dimensions: current rate decisions, forward guidance regarding the future path of interest rates, and balance sheet operations (Quantitative Easing). To capture these path dimensions, economists extract surprises across the entire term structure of Eurodollar and Treasury futures out to several years, rather than relying solely on the spot-month contract.

5. Misconceptions

  1. An FOMC rate hike always causes equity markets to fall on announcement days.

    Asset prices respond only to the unanticipated component of the policy action, not the headline action. If markets fully anticipate a 50 bps rate hike and the Fed hikes by 50 bps, the surprise is zero, and equities do not decline. If the market priced in a 75 bps hike, a 50 bps hike is an expansionary surprise, causing equities to surge.

  2. The spot-month Fed funds futures price jump directly measures the discrete policy rate surprise without adjustment.

    Fed funds futures contracts settle on the arithmetic average of effective daily rates over the entire calendar month. A rate change on day \(d\) impacts only the remaining fraction \(\frac{M - d}{M}\) of the month. Failing to apply the scaling multiplier \(\frac{M}{M - d}\) severely underestimates the true policy surprise.

  3. Daily event windows are just as effective as intraday 30-minute windows for monetary policy identification.

    Daily returns encompass trading before 2:00 PM and after 4:00 PM, capturing macroeconomic data releases (such as CPI and employment reports at 8:30 AM), corporate earnings, and global market noise. Intraday 30-minute windows eliminate non-policy news and prevent reverse causality.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

In a 30-day month, an FOMC announcement occurs on day \(d = 6\). Implied Fed funds futures rate rises from \(3.20\%\) to \(3.28\%\). Calculate the discrete target surprise \(\Delta i_t^u\) in basis points.

Solution

The raw price jump is \(\Delta f = 3.28 - 3.20 = 0.08\% = 8\) bps. With \(M = 30\) and \(d = 6\), the scaling factor is \(\frac{M}{M - d} = \frac{30}{30 - 6} = \frac{30}{24} = 1.25\). The discrete target rate surprise is \(\Delta i_t^u = 1.25 \times 8.0 \text{ bps} = 10.0 \text{ bps}\).

In a 31-day month, an announcement occurs on day \(d = 21\). Implied futures rate falls from \(5.10\%\) to \(5.05\%\). Calculate the discrete target surprise.

Solution

The raw jump is \(\Delta f = 5.05 - 5.10 = -0.05\% = -5\) bps. Scaling factor is \(\frac{M}{M - d} = \frac{31}{31 - 21} = \frac{31}{10} = 3.10\). The discrete target surprise is \(\Delta i_t^u = 3.10 \times (-5.0) = -15.5\) bps (dovish surprise).

If \(\Delta i_t^u = +20\) bps and equity sensitivity is \(\beta = -4.7\), compute the predicted S&P 500 return in basis points.

Solution

Predicted return is \(\E[R_t] = \beta \times \frac{\Delta i_t^u}{100} = -4.7 \times 0.20 = -0.94\% = -94\) basis points.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that if an FOMC announcement occurs on day \(d = 1\), the Kuttner scaling factor strictly exceeds 1.0 for any month length \(M \ge 28\).

Solution

For \(d = 1\), the scaling factor is \(\frac{M}{M - 1}\). Because \(M \ge 28 > 1\), \(M - 1 < M\), which implies \(\frac{M}{M - 1} > 1\). For \(M = 30\), \(\frac{30}{29} \approx 1.0345 > 1.0\). Even on day 1, futures contracts reflect the remaining 29 days, necessitating the scaling adjustment.

Derive the expected bias in OLS estimates of \(\beta\) in \(R_t = \alpha + \beta \Delta i_t^u + \varepsilon_t\) when raw futures jumps \(\Delta f_t\) are used instead of scaled surprises \(\Delta i_t^u\).

Solution

Let \(k_t = \frac{M_t}{M_t - d_t} > 1\). True model: \(R_t = \alpha + \beta (k_t \Delta f_t) + \varepsilon_t\). If one regresses \(R_t\) on \(\Delta f_t\), the estimated slope is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{raw}} = \frac{\Cov(R_t, \Delta f_t)}{\Var(\Delta f_t)} \approx \beta \bar{k}\), where \(\bar{k} > 1\). The sensitivity per unit of raw futures jump is inflated by the average scaling factor \(\bar{k}\).

Show that using a next-month futures contract (\(m+1\)) for an announcement on day \(M\) yields an unscaled surprise (\(\Delta i_t^u = \Delta f_t^{(m+1)}\)).

Solution

Month \(m+1\) begins immediately following day \(M\). The new policy rate applies to all \(M_{m+1}\) days of month \(m+1\). Thus, \(\Delta f_t^{(m+1)} = \frac{M_{m+1}}{M_{m+1}} \Delta i_t^u = 1.0 \times \Delta i_t^u\), proving no scaling factor is needed.

Tier 3 · Empirical

Across a sample of 100 FOMC meetings, \(\sum \Delta i_t^u = 0\), \(\Var(\Delta i_t^u) = 64\) bps\(^2\), and \(\Cov(R_t, \Delta i_t^u) = -300\) bps\(^2\). Compute \(\hat{\beta}\) and interpret.

Solution

\(\hat{\beta} = \frac{-300}{64} = -4.6875\). This matches the Bernanke & Kuttner (2005) benchmark: a 100 bps unexpected tightening reduces stock returns by approximately \(4.69\%\).

Calculate the \(R^2\) of the regression if \(\Var(R_t) = 2500\) bps\(^2\).

Solution

\(R^2 = \frac{[\Cov(R_t, \Delta i_t^u)]^2}{\Var(R_t)\Var(\Delta i_t^u)} = \frac{(-300)^2}{2500 \times 64} = \frac{90000}{160000} = 0.5625 = 56.25\%\).

In an event study, why does widening the event window from 30 minutes to 24 hours attenuate the estimated \(R^2\)?

Solution

Widening the window adds non-monetary return variance \(\sigma_{\text{noise}}^2\) to the total return variance \(\Var(R_t)\). Because \(R^2 = \frac{\Var(\beta \Delta i_t^u)}{\Var(\beta \Delta i_t^u) + \sigma_{\text{noise}}^2}\), increasing \(\sigma_{\text{noise}}^2\) directly attenuates \(R^2\) toward zero.

Tier 4 · Research

Contrast the high-frequency identification approach of Kuttner (2001) with the Romer & Romer (2004) narrative forecast-filtering approach.

Solution

Kuttner (2001) uses market prices in narrow intraday windows, capturing market expectations at the millisecond level but potentially suffering from central bank information effects. Romer & Romer (2004) use internal Federal Reserve Greenbook staff forecasts to purge intended rate changes of the Fed's response to macroeconomic projections, but operates only at the monthly/meeting frequency.

How does Campbell-Shiller dividend discount decomposition explain why the equity response to monetary shocks is predominantly a discount rate effect?

Solution

Under Campbell-Shiller linearization, unexpected stock returns decompose into revisions in expected future dividend growth minus revisions in expected future discount rates (real rates + risk premia). Bernanke & Kuttner (2005) find that monetary tightening has little effect on long-run dividend growth expectations, but substantially raises future equity risk premia.

Explain why an institutional trading desk might trade Eurodollar futures rather than Fed funds futures to capture post-FOMC monetary surprises.

Solution

Eurodollar futures (and SOFR futures) have massive liquidity across quarterly horizons out to several years, allowing traders to capture revisions in the expected policy trajectory (forward guidance path) rather than solely the spot target rate change.

8. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What is the underlying settlement rate for 30-Day Federal Funds futures contracts?

    Options

    Fed funds futures settle on the arithmetic average of the daily EFFR over the entire calendar month.

  2. In a 30-day month, an FOMC announcement occurs on day 15. What is the Kuttner futures scaling factor?

    Options

    The scaling factor is \(M / (M - d) = 30 / (30 - 15) = 30 / 15 = 2.00\).

  3. Why is the Kuttner scaling formula modified when an announcement occurs on the last two days of the month?

    Options

    When \(d \ge M - 1\), \(M - d\) is 1 or 0, multiplying raw noise by up to 31; researchers switch to the next-month contract.

  4. According to Bernanke & Kuttner (2005), what is the estimated equity sensitivity to an unanticipated 100 bps rate hike?

    Options

    Bernanke & Kuttner estimate \(\beta \approx -4.7\) on broad value-weighted equity indices.

  5. Why are narrow 30-minute event windows superior to full-day windows in monetary event studies?

    Options

    Narrow windows ensure that policy news dominates all other information arrivals, identifying exogenous variation.

  6. What happens to the spot Fed funds futures price jump \(\Delta f\) if an announced 25 bps rate hike was 100% anticipated by markets?

    Options

    Futures prices reflect expectations; fully anticipated actions generate zero revision in expectations (\(\Delta f = 0\)).

  7. In the Campbell-Shiller equity return decomposition, what component accounts for the majority of stock price reaction to Fed surprises?

    Options

    Bernanke & Kuttner demonstrate that monetary policy affects stocks predominantly by altering required excess returns (equity risk premia).

  8. During the Zero Lower Bound (ZLB) period, why did the spot-month Kuttner surprise fail to capture monetary policy?

    Options

    At the ZLB, spot target rate surprises were zero; policy operated through longer-term guidance captured by forward Eurodollar futures.
