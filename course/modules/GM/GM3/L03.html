The Fed Information Effect & Central Bank Communication

Standard macroeconomic theory predicts that an unexpected monetary policy tightening depresses economic activity, lowers inflation, and depresses asset prices. Yet empirical event studies frequently discover that professional growth forecasts rise and equities occasionally rally following hawkish announcements. Nakamura & Steinsson (2018) resolved this paradox by formalizing the Fed information effect: central bank announcements simultaneously reveal private economic assessments alongside policy decisions. Decomposing market surprises into pure policy shocks versus Delphic growth signals is crucial for correctly pricing yield curves, equities, and inflation breakevens.

1. The Co-movement Puzzle in Monetary Shocks

In standard New Keynesian macroeconomic models, an exogenous policy rate increase raises the real interest rate, dampens interest-sensitive consumption and investment, and causes output and inflation to decline along the Phillips curve. If financial markets viewed central bank rate changes purely as exogenous policy disturbances, a hawkish surprise would lead private forecasters to downgrade future Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth.

However, when Nakamura & Steinsson (2018) regressed revisions in Blue Chip consensus GDP forecasts on FOMC interest rate surprises, they found a positive and statistically significant coefficient: an unanticipated 25 bps rate hike led private forecasters to raise their one-year-ahead real GDP forecasts by approximately \(+0.15\) percentage points. This positive co-movement directly contradicts the pure monetary policy transmission mechanism.

2. The Policy News Shock & Information Decomposition

To capture the multi-dimensional nature of central bank announcements across both target rates and forward guidance, Nakamura & Steinsson construct a composite high-frequency measure spanning the interest rate term structure.

Definition 3.1 (Fed information effect) The Fed information effect is the causal phenomenon whereby FOMC policy actions and accompanying communications reveal the Federal Reserve's private assessment or superior information regarding current and future economic fundamentals. An announcement conveys two distinct components:

  1. Pure monetary policy shock (\(\varepsilon_t^m\)): An exogenous shift in the central bank's policy preferences or reaction function intercept, raising discount rates.
  2. Information shock (\(\varepsilon_t^i\)): A Delphic signal revealing that the economy is fundamentally stronger (hawkish information) or weaker (dovish information) than private markets previously estimated.

When the Federal Reserve surprises markets with a hawkish action or statement, rational market participants update: "The Fed would only hike rates aggressively if its staff economists observe stronger underlying demand and employment growth than public data currently indicates." This upward revision in cash flow growth counteracts the negative discount rate channel.

Theorem 3.1 (Nakamura & Steinsson Policy News Shock) Let \(z_t = [\Delta f_t^{(1)}, \Delta f_t^{(2)}, \Delta ed_t^{(2)}, \Delta ed_t^{(3)}, \Delta ed_t^{(4)}]' \in \R^5\) denote the vector of 30-minute interest rate futures price jumps surrounding an FOMC announcement, spanning spot Fed funds out to 1-year Eurodollar futures. The policy news shock \(S_t\) is defined as the first principal component of \(z_t\): \[ S_t = w_1' (z_t - \bar{z}) \] where \(w_1\) is the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix \(\Sigma_z\). The first principal component explains over \(80\%\) of the total variance across the five futures surprises.

Remark 3.1 (Term Premium vs. Expected Real Rate) Nakamura & Steinsson demonstrate that a 100 bps policy news shock increases 10-year Treasury yields by approximately 40 bps, driven predominantly by a revision in expected real interest rates rather than nominal term premia. This implies that FOMC forward guidance has powerful, persistent effects on real economic discount rates out to horizons of several years.

3. Disentangling Pure Policy from Information Shocks

How can a quantitative portfolio manager or empirical macroeconomist separate \(\varepsilon_t^m\) from \(\varepsilon_t^i\) in real time? Two leading methodologies have emerged in the econometric literature:

  1. High-Frequency Sign Restrictions: Jarociński & Karadi (2020) exploit the contemporaneous co-movement between interest rate surprises and equity returns:
    • Pure Monetary Policy Shock (\(\varepsilon^m > 0\)): Policy news shock is positive (\(\Delta i > 0\)) and stock returns are negative (\(\Delta P < 0\)). The higher discount rate depresses equity valuations.
    • Fed Information Shock (\(\varepsilon^i > 0\)): Policy news shock is positive (\(\Delta i > 0\)) and stock returns are positive (\(\Delta P > 0\)). The upward revision in expected future cash flows and corporate earnings outweighs the higher discount rate.
  2. Internal Forecast Narrative Purification: Romer & Romer (2004) regress policy changes on Federal Reserve Greenbook internal staff forecasts of inflation, output growth, and unemployment: \[ \Delta i_t = \alpha + \sum_{k=-1}^2 \gamma_k \hat{y}_{t, k}^{\text{GB}} + \sum_{k=-1}^2 \phi_k \hat{\pi}_{t, k}^{\text{GB}} + \varepsilon_t^{\text{narrative}} \] The regression residual \(\varepsilon_t^{\text{narrative}}\) is purged of the central bank's endogenous response to private economic assessments, isolating the true exogenous policy innovation.

Lab 1 · Policy News Shock PCA & Sign-Restriction Identification

Principal Component Analysis on multi-horizon futures surprises extracts a single dominant policy news shock (explaining > 90% of variance), and high-frequency sign restrictions classify announcement windows into pure monetary vs Delphic information shocks.

"""Lab GM3-L03-1: The Fed Information Effect and Policy News Shocks.

Implements the Nakamura & Steinsson (2018) high-frequency policy news shock
via principal component analysis across the interest rate term structure,
and applies high-frequency sign restrictions to identify information shocks.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)
    n_events = 120

    # 5 interest rate futures surprises in 30-min FOMC window:
    # ff1 (current month), ff2 (next month), ed2 (2Q ahead), ed3 (3Q ahead), ed4 (4Q ahead)
    # Underlying latent factors: factor 1 (policy path / level shock) + idiosyncratic noise
    latent_shock = rng.normal(0.0, 5.0, size=n_events)

    # Loadings across horizons: near contracts have smaller forward guidance loadings; further contracts larger
    loadings = np.array([0.7, 0.85, 1.0, 0.95, 0.90])
    noise_cov = 0.8 * np.eye(5)

    surprises = np.outer(latent_shock, loadings) + rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(5), noise_cov, size=n_events)

    # Principal Component Analysis (eigen-decomposition of sample covariance)
    surprises_centered = surprises - np.mean(surprises, axis=0)
    cov_matrix = np.cov(surprises_centered, rowvar=False)
    eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(cov_matrix)

    # Sort descending
    idx_sorted = np.argsort(eigvals)[::-1]
    eigvals_sorted = eigvals[idx_sorted]
    eigvecs_sorted = eigvecs[:, idx_sorted]

    var_explained_pc1 = float(eigvals_sorted[0] / np.sum(eigvals_sorted))

    # First principal component (policy news shock series)
    pc1_weights = eigvecs_sorted[:, 0]
    # Ensure positive correlation with short rate
    if pc1_weights[0] < 0:
        pc1_weights = -pc1_weights

    policy_news_shock = surprises_centered @ pc1_weights

    # High-frequency equity reaction in the same 30-min window
    # Pure monetary shocks generate negative equity reaction (discount rate channel)
    # Information shocks generate positive equity reaction (cash flow growth signal)
    # Assume 30% of announcements are dominated by information effects
    is_information = rng.binomial(1, 0.30, size=n_events)
    equity_responses_bps = np.zeros(n_events)
    for i in range(n_events):
        if is_information[i] == 1:
            # Positive co-movement: Fed hike signals economic strength
            equity_responses_bps[i] = 1.2 * policy_news_shock[i] + rng.normal(0.0, 8.0)
        else:
            # Negative co-movement: Pure policy tightening
            equity_responses_bps[i] = -2.5 * policy_news_shock[i] + rng.normal(0.0, 8.0)

    # Sign restriction classification:
    # Pure contractionary: shock > 0 and equity < 0
    # Pure expansionary: shock < 0 and equity > 0
    # Hawkish information: shock > 0 and equity > 0
    # Dovish information: shock < 0 and equity < 0
    pure_tightening = int(np.sum((policy_news_shock > 0.5) & (equity_responses_bps < -0.5)))
    pure_easing = int(np.sum((policy_news_shock < -0.5) & (equity_responses_bps > 0.5)))
    hawkish_info = int(np.sum((policy_news_shock > 0.5) & (equity_responses_bps > 0.5)))
    dovish_info = int(np.sum((policy_news_shock < -0.5) & (equity_responses_bps < -0.5)))

    corr_shock_equity = float(np.corrcoef(policy_news_shock, equity_responses_bps)[0, 1])

    print("FOMC Policy Windows Analyzed:", n_events)
    print("PC1 Variance Explained:", round(var_explained_pc1, 4))
    print("Policy News Shock Std Dev (bps):", round(float(np.std(policy_news_shock, ddof=1)), 4))
    print("Overall Shock-Equity Correlation:", round(corr_shock_equity, 4))
    print("Pure Monetary Tightening Events:", pure_tightening)
    print("Pure Monetary Easing Events:", pure_easing)
    print("Hawkish Information Effect Events:", hawkish_info)
    print("Dovish Information Effect Events:", dovish_info)


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks: Transparent Reaction Functions & Market Backlash

The classical theory of the Fed information effect posits an asymmetric information structure: the central bank possesses superior private knowledge about macroeconomic fundamentals relative to private financial institutions. In modern financial markets with ubiquitous nowcasting models, alternative data (credit card transactions, satellite imagery, real-time mobility), and hundreds of PhD economists employed on Wall Street, does the Federal Reserve truly possess superior private information?

Recent empirical literature suggests a structural shift: the information effect is not driven by superior Fed data, but by market uncertainty regarding the central bank's reaction function. When the Fed hikes unexpectedly, markets deduce that the Fed's inflation tolerance is lower or its assessment of neutral \(r^*\) is higher than previously modeled. When central banks attempt to signal economic optimism through aggressive tightening, market participants frequently misinterpret the signal as a policy error, triggering severe volatility spikes and credit spread widening rather than equity rallies.

5. Misconceptions

  1. A policy rate surprise contains only information about the central bank's policy preferences.

    Every central bank announcement is a joint revelation: it communicates the chosen policy action alongside the central bank's macroeconomic outlook. Without decomposing these two orthogonal drivers, empirical estimates conflate policy non-neutrality with endogenous macroeconomic forecasting.

  2. A single spot Fed funds futures surprise captures the entirety of an FOMC policy announcement.

    Spot-month futures capture only the target change for the immediate calendar month. The vast majority of macroeconomic impact and asset price variance stems from revisions in forward guidance path factors out to one and two years, captured by Eurodollar and SOFR futures.

  3. If stocks rally following an FOMC rate hike, markets are behaving irrationally or efficiently violating no-arbitrage.

    Under the Fed information effect, a rate hike signals that the central bank's outlook for corporate earnings, industrial production, and employment is substantially stronger than consensus. If the upward revision in expected cash flows exceeds the discount rate increase, rational equity prices must rise.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Consider 5 futures surprise changes: \(\Delta z = [4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 5.5, 5.0]\) bps. If the normalized first eigenvector is \(w_1 = [0.35, 0.45, 0.55, 0.45, 0.40]'\), calculate the policy news shock \(S_t\).

Solution

\(S_t = w_1' \Delta z = 0.35(4.0) + 0.45(5.0) + 0.55(6.0) + 0.45(5.5) + 0.40(5.0) = 1.40 + 2.25 + 3.30 + 2.475 + 2.00 = 11.425\) bps.

In a 30-minute FOMC window, the 2-year Treasury yield rises by \(+12\) bps and the S&P 500 index gains \(+0.85\%\). Classify this event under Jarociński & Karadi (2020) sign restrictions.

Solution

Interest rate change is positive (\(\Delta i > 0\)) and equity return is positive (\(\Delta P > 0\)). This positive co-movement classifies the announcement as a hawkish Fed information shock: markets interpret the move as a signal of superior economic strength.

If the eigenvalues of a \(5 \times 5\) covariance matrix of futures surprises are \(\lambda = [45.2, 3.1, 1.2, 0.4, 0.1]\), calculate the proportion of variance explained by the first principal component.

Solution

Total variance: \(\sum \lambda_i = 45.2 + 3.1 + 1.2 + 0.4 + 0.1 = 50.0\). Proportion explained by PC1: \(\frac{45.2}{50.0} = 0.904 = 90.4\%\).

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that if monetary surprises \(\Delta i_t\) contain classical measurement error \(\eta_t\) orthogonal to true shocks \(\varepsilon_t^m\), OLS regressions of output on \(\Delta i_t\) suffer from attenuation bias toward zero.

Solution

Let \(\Delta i_t = \varepsilon_t^m + \eta_t\), with \(\Cov(\varepsilon^m, \eta) = 0\). The true model is \(y_t = \beta \varepsilon_t^m + u_t\). In OLS on \(\Delta i_t\), \(\hat{\beta} = \frac{\Cov(y, \Delta i)}{\Var(\Delta i)} = \frac{\Cov(\beta \varepsilon^m, \varepsilon^m + \eta)}{\Var(\varepsilon^m) + \Var(\eta)} = \beta \frac{\Var(\varepsilon^m)}{\Var(\varepsilon^m) + \Var(\eta)}\). Because \(\frac{\Var(\varepsilon^m)}{\Var(\varepsilon^m) + \Var(\eta)} < 1\), \(|\hat{\beta}| < |\beta|\), proving attenuation bias.

Show that if the policy news shock \(S_t\) is a linear combination of a pure shock \(\varepsilon_t^m\) and an information shock \(\varepsilon_t^i\), equity return sensitivity \(\beta_{\text{stock}}\) is a weighted average of discount rate and cash flow sensitivities.

Solution

Let \(S_t = \varepsilon_t^m + \varepsilon_t^i\), with \(\Cov(\varepsilon^m, \varepsilon^i) = 0\). Let \(R_t = -\kappa_m \varepsilon_t^m + \kappa_i \varepsilon_t^i + \nu_t\), where \(\kappa_m, \kappa_i > 0\). Then \(\Cov(R_t, S_t) = -\kappa_m \Var(\varepsilon^m) + \kappa_i \Var(\varepsilon^i)\). Thus \(\beta_{\text{OLS}} = \frac{\Cov(R, S)}{\Var(S)} = -\kappa_m \frac{\Var(\varepsilon^m)}{\Var(S)} + \kappa_i \frac{\Var(\varepsilon^i)}{\Var(S)}\). If \(\kappa_i \Var(\varepsilon^i) > \kappa_m \Var(\varepsilon^m)\), the estimated regression slope is positive.

Prove that PCA weights \(w_1\) maximize the sample variance of the projected shock \(S_t = w_1' z_t\) subject to the normalization constraint \(w_1' w_1 = 1\).

Solution

The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal{L}(w_1, \lambda) = w_1' \Sigma w_1 - \lambda (w_1' w_1 - 1)\). First-order condition: \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_1} = 2 \Sigma w_1 - 2 \lambda w_1 = 0 \implies \Sigma w_1 = \lambda w_1\). Pre-multiplying by \(w_1'\) gives \(w_1' \Sigma w_1 = \lambda\). To maximize variance, \(\lambda\) must be the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\max}\), and \(w_1\) the corresponding eigenvector.

Tier 3 · Empirical

In a sample of 150 FOMC announcements, 45 exhibit \(\Delta i_t > 0\) and \(\Delta P_t > 0\). Estimate the proportion of announcements exhibiting the Fed information effect, and compute its 95% confidence interval.

Solution

\(\hat{p} = \frac{45}{150} = 0.30 = 30.0\%\). Standard error: \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.30 \times 0.70}{150}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.21}{150}} = \sqrt{0.0014} \approx 0.0374\). 95% confidence interval: \(0.30 \pm 1.96(0.0374) = [0.227, 0.373]\) or \(22.7\%\) to \(37.3\%\).

Why does controlling for Greenbook forecast revisions eliminate the positive GDP response to monetary policy surprises in vector autoregressions?

Solution

Controlling for Greenbook forecasts removes the central bank's endogenous policy response to anticipated growth. Once the private information component \(\varepsilon^i\) is conditioned out, the purified residual shock captures only exogenous policy changes \(\varepsilon^m\), restoring the textbook negative output impulse response.

Following an FOMC meeting, 10-year nominal Treasury yields rise by 15 bps, while 10-year TIPS yields (real rate) rise by 18 bps. What is the implied change in 10-year inflation breakevens?

Solution

Nominal yield \(y = r_{\text{real}} + \pi_{\text{breakeven}}\). Therefore, \(\Delta \pi_{\text{breakeven}} = \Delta y - \Delta r_{\text{real}} = 15 - 18 = -3\) basis points. The announcement caused expected inflation / inflation risk premia to drop by 3 bps.

Tier 4 · Research

How does modern central bank forward guidance differ from traditional discretion in creating information shocks?

Solution

Under discretion, central banks adjust current rates with minimal guidance, concentrating information in the spot decision. Under explicit forward guidance, central banks project rate paths and policy thresholds (e.g. Evans Rule), communicating future state-contingent reaction functions that shift the entire term structure and generate Delphic forecasting signals.

Evaluate the risks of trading high-frequency straddles if an FOMC announcement is dominated by an information effect rather than a pure policy shock.

Solution

If an announcement is a pure policy shock, equities and bonds move inversely (negative correlation), and asset prices adjust rapidly to the discount rate shock. If an information shock dominates, the direction of equity move depends on the growth vs discount rate tug-of-war, increasing cross-asset correlation uncertainty and risk of directional whipsaws.

Why do Nakamura & Steinsson (2018) argue that monetary non-neutrality is larger than implied by standard high-frequency regressions?

Solution

Standard regressions conflate contractionary policy with expansionary information. Because the information effect biases the output response upward (toward positive numbers), failing to control for it masks the true potency of monetary policy. Purging the information effect reveals that monetary policy has substantially larger contractionary effects on output.
