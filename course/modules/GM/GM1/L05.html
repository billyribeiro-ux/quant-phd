Asset prices do not react to the level of economic activity; they react to how economic reality diverges from market expectations. A quarterly GDP growth rate of 3.0% sparks an equity rally if the market priced 1.5%, but triggers a steep sell-off if the consensus anticipated 4.5%. Economic surprise indices aggregate these discrete, heterogeneous expectation errors into a continuous, stationary state variable tracking real-time macroeconomic sentiment. By weighting announcements by their historical market impact and decaying old prints exponentially, surprise indices quantify momentum, identify cyclical turning points, and drive systematic regime-based cross-asset rebalancing.

1. Defining Standardized Macroeconomic Surprises

Every week, financial media and market data providers publish consensus forecasts compiled from surveys of institutional economists. When the actual data prints, market participants measure the discrepancy between reality and expectations.

Definition 5.1 · Standardized Announcement Surprise

Let \(X_{i,t}\) denote the actual published value of macroeconomic indicator \(i\) for period \(t\), and let \(\hat{X}_{i,t}^C\) denote the median pre-release survey consensus forecast. The raw forecast surprise is:

\[E_{i,t} = X_{i,t} - \hat{X}_{i,t}^C\]

Because macroeconomic series are measured in disparate units—thousands of jobs, percentage points of inflation, index points—raw surprises cannot be summed directly. The standardized announcement surprise is defined as:

\[S_{i,t} = \frac{X_{i,t} - \hat{X}_{i,t}^C}{\hat{\sigma}_i}\]

where \(\hat{\sigma}_i = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M-1} \sum_{m=1}^M (E_{i,m} - \bar{E}_i)^2}\) is the rolling sample standard deviation of historical raw surprises for series \(i\) over a trailing lookback window (typically 12 to 24 months).

Standardizing by rolling volatility transforms heterogeneous releases into dimensionless z-scores with approximate unit variance (\(\Var(S_{i,t}) \approx 1\)), allowing systematic cross-sectional aggregation.

2. Aggregation and Exponential Decay Dynamics

Definition 5.2 · Economic Surprise Index

An economic surprise index is an exponentially smoothed, weighted composite index that tracks whether high-frequency macroeconomic data releases are systematically exceeding or falling short of market consensus expectations over a rolling calendar window.

On calendar day \(t\), let \(\mathcal{A}_t\) denote the set of economic indicators published on that day. The composite daily surprise shock is:

\[S_t = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{A}_t} w_i S_{i,t}\]

where \(w_i \ge 0\) are normalized indicator importance weights (\(\sum_i w_i = 1\)). The economic surprise index evolves according to discrete exponential smoothing:

\[\text{ESI}_t = \lambda \text{ESI}_{t-1} + S_t\]

where \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) is the daily autoregressive decay factor. For a target half-life of \(\tau\) trading days (e.g. \(\tau = 30\) days), the decay parameter is:

\[\lambda = \exp\left(-\frac{\ln 2}{\tau}\right)\]

3. Cyclical Mean Reversion and Asset Market Responses

A fundamental econometric characteristic of economic surprise indices is their cyclical mean reversion:

Theorem 5.1 · Covariance Stationarity and Mean Reversion of Surprise Indices

Assume that forecasters form rational expectations such that announcement surprises \(S_t\) are mean-zero and serially uncorrelated white noise: \(\E[S_t] = 0\) and \(\E[S_t S_{t-k}] = 0\) for all \(k \neq 0\), with unconditional variance \(\sigma_S^2 = \Var(S_t)\). Then for any decay parameter \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\):

  1. The economic surprise index \(\text{ESI}_t = \lambda \text{ESI}_{t-1} + S_t\) is an AR(1) process that is strictly covariance-stationary.
  2. The unconditional mean is zero: \(\E[\text{ESI}_t] = 0\).
  3. The unconditional variance is: \[\Var(\text{ESI}_t) = \frac{\sigma_S^2}{1 - \lambda^2}\]
  4. The process exhibits geometric mean reversion toward zero with half-life \(\tau = -\frac{\ln 2}{\ln \lambda}\).

Proof. Expanding \(\text{ESI}_t\) by backward substitution:

\[\text{ESI}_t = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k S_{t-k}\]

Since \(|\lambda| < 1\) and \(\E[S_{t-k}^2] = \sigma_S^2 < \infty\), the sum converges in mean square and almost surely. Taking expectations:

\[\E[\text{ESI}_t] = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \lambda^k \E[S_{t-k}] = 0\]

Because \(S_{t-k}\) are mutually uncorrelated, the variance of the infinite sum equals the sum of the variances:

\[\Var(\text{ESI}_t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty (\lambda^k)^2 \Var(S_{t-k}) = \sigma_S^2 \sum_{k=0}^\infty (\lambda^2)^k = \frac{\sigma_S^2}{1 - \lambda^2}\]

The \(k\)-step autocovariance is \(\gamma(k) = \Cov(\text{ESI}_t, \text{ESI}_{t-k}) = \lambda^k \Var(\text{ESI}_t)\). The autocorrelation \(\rho(k) = \lambda^k\) decays exponentially. Solving \(\lambda^\tau = \frac{1}{2}\) yields \(\tau \ln \lambda = -\ln 2 \implies \tau = -\frac{\ln 2}{\ln \lambda}\). \(\blacksquare\)

In financial markets, the economic surprise index drives asset prices across multiple asset classes (Gertler & Karadi 2015):

  • Equities: Positive surprises signal robust corporate revenues and earnings growth, expanding valuation multiples and lifting equity returns during expansionary regimes.
  • Fixed Income: Positive surprises raise market expectations of central bank policy rate hikes, driving benchmark Treasury yields higher and causing duration losses in bond portfolios.
  • Currencies: Positive growth surprises trigger capital inflows seeking higher real rates, appreciating the domestic currency.

Lab 5 · Economic Surprise Index & Cross-Asset Sensitivity

Constructing an exponentially smoothed economic surprise index from standardized release surprises generates a stationary, mean-reverting process with an empirical half-life matching the decay parameter, driving statistically significant equity and bond return sensitivities.

"""Lab GM1-L05-1: Economic Surprise Index Construction & Asset Market Sensitivity.

Constructs an exponentially weighted macroeconomic surprise index (Scotti 2016) from
standardized release surprises. Demonstrates empirical stationarity, cyclical mean-reversion,
and measures asset market return sensitivity across surprise regimes.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

import numpy as np


def build_macro_surprise_index(
    surprises: np.ndarray,
    importance_weights: np.ndarray,
    half_life_days: float = 30.0,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Calculate exponentially smoothed economic surprise index."""
    t_len, _ = surprises.shape
    norm_w = importance_weights / float(np.sum(importance_weights))
    decay = math.exp(-math.log(2.0) / half_life_days)

    esi = np.zeros(t_len, dtype=np.float64)
    running_level = 0.0

    for t in range(t_len):
        period_surp = float(np.dot(surprises[t], norm_w))
        running_level = running_level * decay + period_surp
        esi[t] = running_level

    # Normalize to mean zero and unit variance
    mean_val = float(np.mean(esi))
    std_val = float(np.std(esi, ddof=1))
    return (esi - mean_val) / std_val


def main() -> None:
    print("GM1 Lab L05-1: Economic Surprise Index & Cross-Asset Sensitivity [SIMULATED]")
    print("==========================================================================")

    rng = np.random.default_rng(303)
    t_days = 252  # 1 trading year of daily releases
    n_indicators = 5  # Payrolls, Core CPI, Retail Sales, ISM Mfg, Housing Starts
    importance_weights = np.array([0.35, 0.25, 0.18, 0.14, 0.08], dtype=np.float64)

    # Simulate surprises: zero-mean, serially uncorrelated announcement innovations
    # S_{i, t} ~ N(0, 1)
    standardized_surprises = rng.standard_normal((t_days, n_indicators))

    # Construct Economic Surprise Index (30-day half-life)
    esi = build_macro_surprise_index(standardized_surprises, importance_weights, half_life_days=30.0)

    # Autocorrelation of the surprise index: AR(1) estimate
    esi_curr = esi[1:]
    esi_prev = esi[:-1]
    phi_est, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(esi_prev[:, None], esi_curr, rcond=None)
    phi = float(phi_est[0])
    empirical_half_life = -math.log(2.0) / math.log(phi) if 0.0 < phi < 1.0 else 0.0

    # Cross-asset sensitivity: Equities (S&P 500) and 10Y Treasury Bond yield changes
    # Equities respond positively to growth surprises; Treasury yields rise on positive growth/inflation surprises
    daily_surprise_shock = standardized_surprises @ (importance_weights / np.sum(importance_weights))

    equity_returns = 0.004 * daily_surprise_shock + rng.normal(0, 0.008, size=t_days)
    bond_yield_changes_bps = 2.5 * daily_surprise_shock + rng.normal(0, 3.0, size=t_days)

    # OLS sensitivity betas
    beta_eq, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(daily_surprise_shock[:, None], equity_returns, rcond=None)
    beta_bond, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(daily_surprise_shock[:, None], bond_yield_changes_bps, rcond=None)

    print(f"Sample length:                        {t_days} daily trading periods")
    print(f"Economic indicators in index:         {n_indicators}")
    print("--------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    print("Surprise Index Statistical Properties:")
    print(f"  Sample Mean:                        {float(np.mean(esi)):7.4f}")
    print(f"  Sample Standard Deviation:          {float(np.std(esi, ddof=1)):7.4f}")
    print(f"  First-order Autocorrelation (AR1):  {phi:7.4f}")
    print(f"  Implied Mean-Reversion Half-Life:   {empirical_half_life:5.1f} days")
    print("--------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    print("Cross-Asset Market Reaction to +1 Standard Deviation Macro Surprise Shock:")
    print(f"  Equity Index Return Impact:         {float(beta_eq[0]) * 10000:+6.1f} bps")
    print(f"  10Y Treasury Yield Impact:          {float(beta_bond[0]):+6.2f} bps")
    print("--------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    # Confirm stationarity and mean reversion
    assert abs(np.mean(esi)) < 1e-10, "Normalized surprise index must have zero mean"
    assert abs(np.std(esi, ddof=1) - 1.0) < 1e-10, "Normalized surprise index must have unit standard deviation"
    assert 0.85 < phi < 0.99, "Exponential smoothing with 30-day half-life yields high daily persistence"
    print("Surprise index normalization and stationarity verified: True")

Where the Canon Breaks

Standard Wall Street commentary treats economic surprise indices as pure gauges of macroeconomic health. In quantitative reality, surprise indices reflect the interaction of economic health and analyst psychology:

  • Consensus Lag and Herd Behavior: Sell-side economists systematically lag turning points. Following a recession, analysts set forecasts excessively low; subsequent mediocre actuals look like massive "positive surprises," pushing the surprise index to euphoric highs even as the real economy remains depressed.
  • Strategic Forecast Revisions: When an indicator prints strong, forecasters immediately revise future estimates upward. The bar is raised; subsequent prints must be even stronger to beat expectations. Consequently, extreme positive surprise readings reliably predict future negative surprises—a behavioral boom-bust feedback loop.
  • Non-Stationary Volatility Scaling: Using a static historical standard deviation \(\hat{\sigma}_i\) misweights surprises during structural volatility breaks (e.g. 2008 or 2020), where volatility surges by 500% and distorts index scaling.

Common Pitfalls & Misconceptions

  • An economic surprise index above zero indicates that the real economy is expanding rapidly.

    An index above zero indicates only that data is beating analyst expectations, not that growth is positive. In a severe recession, if GDP contracts by -1.0% against a consensus expectation of -3.0%, the surprise is strongly positive (+2.0%) despite deep economic contraction.

  • Raw surprise differences (\(X - \hat{X}\)) should be summed directly across all economic series.

    Raw surprises have non-comparable dimensions. Summing an employment surprise (+100,000 jobs) with an inflation surprise (+0.2% CPI) would allow payrolls to dominate the index by a factor of 500,000. Surprises must be standardized by their historical standard deviations.

  • Extreme readings of the economic surprise index are trend-following buy signals for risk assets.

    Because surprise indices are stationary and mean-reverting (Theorem 5.1), extreme high readings signal peak expectations and imminent negative surprises. Empirical quantitative models use extreme ESI readings as contrarian reversal signals rather than trend continuation indicators.

Practice Problems

Mechanics Tier

Problem M1. Core CPI inflation prints at \(+0.40\%\) month-over-month against a consensus expectation of \(+0.25\%\). The trailing 24-month standard deviation of Core CPI surprises is \(\hat{\sigma} = 0.06\%\). Compute the standardized surprise \(S_{\text{CPI}}\).

Solution

The raw announcement forecast error is:

\[E_{\text{CPI}} = X_{\text{CPI}} - \hat{X}_{\text{CPI}}^C = 0.40\% - 0.25\% = +0.15\%\]

Dividing by the historical standard deviation \(\hat{\sigma} = 0.06\%\):

\[S_{\text{CPI}} = \frac{E_{\text{CPI}}}{\hat{\sigma}} = \frac{+0.15\%}{0.06\%} = +2.50\]

The inflation print represents a \(+2.50\) standard deviation upside shock, a significant inflationary surprise.

Problem M2. A macro trading desk uses an economic surprise index with an exponential half-life of \(\tau = 45\) trading days. Calculate the exact daily decay parameter \(\lambda\). If today's index level is \(\text{ESI}_{t-1} = 1.20\) and today's composite surprise shock is \(S_t = -0.30\), compute today's updated index level \(\text{ESI}_t\).

Solution

The daily decay factor is:

\[\lambda = \exp\left(-\frac{\ln 2}{45}\right) = \exp(-0.015403) \approx 0.98471\]

The updated index level is:

\[\text{ESI}_t = \lambda \text{ESI}_{t-1} + S_t = 0.98471 \times 1.20 + (-0.30) = 1.18165 - 0.30 = 0.88165 \approx 0.882\]

The index declines from \(1.20\) down to \(0.882\) due to overnight decay and the negative daily release surprise.

Problem M3. An empirical regression of daily 10-year Treasury yield changes (\(\Delta y_{10}\) in basis points) on economic surprise shocks yields \(\Delta y_{10, t} = 0.15 + 2.40 \cdot S_t + \epsilon_t\). If the US Employment Situation prints a combined surprise shock of \(S_t = +1.80\), what is the expected change in 10-year Treasury yields?

Solution

Applying the estimated regression equation:

\[\E[\Delta y_{10, t} \mid S_t = 1.80] = 0.15 + 2.40 \times 1.80 = 0.15 + 4.32 = +4.47 \text{ bps}\]

The 10-year Treasury yield is expected to increase by \(4.47\) basis points immediately following the announcement.

Proof Tier

Problem P1. Prove Theorem 5.1 part 3: show that for the autoregressive surprise index \(\text{ESI}_t = \lambda \text{ESI}_{t-1} + S_t\) with white noise shocks \(S_t \sim (0, \sigma_S^2)\) and \(|\lambda| < 1\), the unconditional variance is \(\Var(\text{ESI}_t) = \frac{\sigma_S^2}{1 - \lambda^2}\).

Solution

Taking the variance of both sides of \(\text{ESI}_t = \lambda \text{ESI}_{t-1} + S_t\):

\[\Var(\text{ESI}_t) = \Var(\lambda \text{ESI}_{t-1} + S_t) = \lambda^2 \Var(\text{ESI}_{t-1}) + \Var(S_t) + 2\lambda \Cov(\text{ESI}_{t-1}, S_t)\]

Since \(\text{ESI}_{t-1} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda^{k-1} S_{t-k}\) depends only on past innovations \(\{S_{t-1}, S_{t-2}, \dots\}\), and \(S_t\) is serially uncorrelated white noise, \(\Cov(\text{ESI}_{t-1}, S_t) = 0\).

Under covariance stationarity, \(\Var(\text{ESI}_t) = \Var(\text{ESI}_{t-1}) = \gamma_0\). Substituting:

\[\gamma_0 = \lambda^2 \gamma_0 + \sigma_S^2\] \[\gamma_0 (1 - \lambda^2) = \sigma_S^2 \implies \gamma_0 = \frac{\sigma_S^2}{1 - \lambda^2}\]

Because \(|\lambda| < 1\), \(1 - \lambda^2 > 0\), ensuring a strictly positive finite unconditional variance. \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P2. Derive the relationship between the continuous-time Ornstein-Uhlenbeck mean-reverting process \(d X_t = -\theta X_t dt + \sigma dW_t\) and the discrete-time economic surprise index equation \(\text{ESI}_t = \lambda \text{ESI}_{t-1} + S_t\).

Solution

The exact discrete-time solution to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic differential equation over time step \(\Delta t\) is:

\[X_t = e^{-\theta \Delta t} X_{t-1} + \int_{t-\Delta t}^t e^{-\theta(t - s)} \sigma dW_s\]

Let \(\lambda = e^{-\theta \Delta t}\) and \(S_t = \int_{t-\Delta t}^t e^{-\theta(t - s)} \sigma dW_s\). The shock \(S_t\) is normally distributed with mean zero and variance:

\[\Var(S_t) = \sigma^2 \int_0^{\Delta t} e^{-2\theta u} du = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta} (1 - e^{-2\theta \Delta t}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta} (1 - \lambda^2)\]

This matches the discrete AR(1) form \(\text{ESI}_t = \lambda \text{ESI}_{t-1} + S_t\). The continuous mean-reversion speed is \(\theta = -\frac{\ln \lambda}{\Delta t}\), and the half-life is \(\tau = \frac{\ln 2}{\theta}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P3. Prove that if consensus forecasts exhibit optimistic herd bias (\(\E[X_{i,t} - \hat{X}_{i,t}^C] = \mu_E < 0\)), the uncentered surprise index drifts toward a strictly negative steady-state mean, and show how z-score normalization restores unbiasedness.

Solution

If \(\E[S_t] = \mu_S < 0\), taking the expectation of the recursive equation \(\text{ESI}_t = \lambda \text{ESI}_{t-1} + S_t\) at steady state gives:

\[\mu_{\text{ESI}} = \lambda \mu_{\text{ESI}} + \mu_S \implies \mu_{\text{ESI}} = \frac{\mu_S}{1 - \lambda} < 0\]

The index suffers from persistent negative bias. Standardizing the index by subtracting the running sample mean \(\bar{\mu}_{\text{ESI}} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \text{ESI}_t\) and dividing by sample standard deviation \(\hat{\sigma}_{\text{ESI}}\):

\[\widetilde{\text{ESI}}_t = \frac{\text{ESI}_t - \bar{\mu}_{\text{ESI}}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\text{ESI}}}\]

By linearity of the expectation operator, \(\E[\widetilde{\text{ESI}}_t] = \frac{\E[\text{ESI}_t] - \mu_{\text{ESI}}}{\sigma_{\text{ESI}}} = 0\), restoring exact zero-mean symmetry around economic neutral. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical Tier

Problem E1. Analyze the Citigroup Economic Surprise Index (CESI) behavior following major business cycle peaks (e.g. 2007 and 2000), and evaluate why the index consistently plunges below -80.

Solution

At business cycle turning points, consensus forecasts are anchored to the preceding expansionary regime. Economists project continued trend growth (e.g. +2.5% GDP). When the downturn arrives, actual data deteriorates faster than analysts can adjust their models, producing consecutive negative surprises across employment, production, and consumer spending. Because the 30-day exponential smoother accumulates consecutive negative shocks, the CESI plunges to extreme troughs (-80 to -100). The index rebounds only after forecasters capitulate and aggressively slash expectations, lowering the hurdle for future positive surprises.

Problem E2. Design a systematic tactical asset allocation strategy that trades cyclical versus defensive equity sectors based on the 30-day Economic Surprise Index.

Solution

Construct a Long/Short equity sector portfolio:

  • High Surprise Regime (\(\text{ESI} > +1.0\)): Overweight cyclical sectors (Industrials, Consumer Discretionary, Financials) that benefit from unexpected macro momentum, and underweight defensive sectors (Utilities, Consumer Staples).
  • Low Surprise Regime (\(\text{ESI} < -1.0\)): Rotate into defensive sectors and cash, reducing beta to broad equities.
  • Extreme Reversal Signal (\(\text{ESI} > +2.5\) or \(\text{ESI} < -2.5\)): Implement contrarian fading: take profit on cyclicals when ESI exceeds +2.5 as consensus expectations peak, preparing for negative mean reversion.

Problem E3. In fixed income markets, positive inflation surprises trigger immediate sell-offs in short-dated Treasuries (the 2-year yield spikes). Explain why the sensitivity of the 2-year yield to surprise shocks is higher than that of the 30-year yield.

Solution

The 2-year Treasury yield is dominated by market expectations of central bank policy rate actions over the immediate monetary policy cycle (the Fed funds path over the next 12 to 24 months). When inflation surprises to the upside, markets immediately price in imminent policy rate hikes (Gertler & Karadi 2015). In contrast, the 30-year yield reflects long-run terminal growth, inflation anchor credibility, and term premiums. Rate hikes intended to combat inflation dampen long-term growth prospects, flattening the 2s30s yield curve and moderating the 30-year yield's sensitivity.

Research Tier

Problem R1. Compare a fixed-weight economic surprise index against an endogenous weight index where indicator weights \(w_i(t)\) are determined by high-frequency asset price sensitivities (Scotti 2016).

Solution

Fixed-weight indices (like Bloomberg or Citigroup) use heuristic rules (e.g. GDP and payrolls receive high arbitrary weights). Scotti (2016) proposes endogenous market-implied weights: estimate high-frequency regressions \(\Delta y_{\text{asset}, \tau} = \alpha + \beta_i S_{i, \tau} + \epsilon_\tau\) in a 15-minute event window around release \(\tau\). Setting \(w_i \propto |\beta_i|\) weights indicators by their actual market pricing relevance. This dynamic weighting accounts for time-varying macroeconomic themes (e.g. during supply-shock regimes, inflation prints dominate market pricing, while during credit-crunch regimes, liquidity and labor indicators dominate).

Problem R2. Formulate a multi-country macro surprise arbitrage strategy that trades currency pairs (FX G10) based on cross-country economic surprise differentials.

Solution

For each currency pair \(j/k\) (e.g. EUR/USD), construct the surprise spread \(\Delta \text{ESI}_{j/k, t} = \text{ESI}_{j, t} - \text{ESI}_{k, t}\). Under the uncovered interest parity and monetary policy reaction framework, the currency with the stronger relative macro momentum experiences relative monetary tightening and capital inflows. Take long positions in currencies where \(\Delta \text{ESI} > 0\) and short positions where \(\Delta \text{ESI} < 0\). Control for risk by sizing positions inversely to 30-day realized FX volatility and overlaying carry filters.

Problem R3. Investigate the empirical interaction between the Economic Surprise Index and the Dynamic Factor Nowcast (L03). Can a hybrid signal combining factor level and surprise momentum generate superior risk-adjusted alpha?

Solution

Dynamic factor nowcasts (\(\hat{y}_t^{\text{DFM}}\)) capture the estimated level of underlying latent activity, while surprise indices (\(\text{ESI}_t\)) capture the derivative (momentum relative to consensus). Construct a four-quadrant macro regime matrix:

  1. Quadrant 1: High Level, Positive Surprise (Strong Boom): Maximize risk exposure in high-beta cyclicals.
  2. Quadrant 2: High Level, Negative Surprise (Late-Cycle Deceleration): Take profit on cyclicals; shift toward quality growth.
  3. Quadrant 3: Low Level, Negative Surprise (Contraction / Crisis): Maximize defensive assets and long duration bonds.
  4. Quadrant 4: Low Level, Positive Surprise (Early-Cycle Recovery): Buy deeply discounted credit and cyclical equities.

Empirical backtests confirm that this two-dimensional state classification substantially reduces drawdowns compared to single-indicator timing strategies.

Retrieval Practice

  1. Why must macroeconomic release surprises \(X - \hat{X}^C\) be standardized by their historical standard deviation before aggregation?

    Options

    Without dividing by historical volatility, high-magnitude numbers (like 100k payrolls) would completely swamp percentage-based indicators (like 0.2% CPI).

  2. What does an economic surprise index value of +1.5 indicate about the underlying economy?

    Options

    The surprise index measures performance relative to consensus expectations, not absolute economic output levels.

  3. Under rational expectations, why is the economic surprise index an AR(1) process that is strictly stationary?

    Options

    An exponentially smoothed sum of white noise innovations with decay \(|\lambda| < 1\) is an AR(1) process with bounded variance and zero unconditional mean.

  4. How do 10-year Treasury bond yields typically respond to a significant positive macroeconomic surprise shock?

    Options

    Stronger-than-expected macro data prompts markets to price in monetary policy tightening, pushing bond yields higher.

  5. Retail sales print at \(+1.00\%\) against a consensus forecast of \(+0.50\%\). The trailing standard deviation of retail sales surprises is \(0.20\%\). What is the standardized surprise z-score?

    Raw surprise is \(1.00\% - 0.50\% = 0.50\%\). Standardized surprise is \(0.50 / 0.20 = 2.5\).

  6. An economic surprise index has daily autoregressive decay parameter \(\lambda = 0.97716\). What is the implied half-life of economic surprises in trading days?

    Half-life is \(\tau = -\ln(2) / \ln(\lambda) = -0.69315 / (-0.023105) \approx 30.0\) trading days.

  7. Why do sell-side consensus forecast revisions create cyclical boom-bust dynamics in the economic surprise index?

    Options

    Endogenous raising and lowering of the consensus hurdle causes surprise indices to cycle between positive and negative extremes.

  8. In tactical asset allocation, why do quantitative desks treat extreme high readings of the Economic Surprise Index as contrarian warning signals?

    Options

    Stationarity and mean-reversion dictate that unsustainably high expectations will eventually be disappointed, making peak ESI a contrarian signal.
