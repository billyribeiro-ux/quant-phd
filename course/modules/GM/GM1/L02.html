Macroeconomic policy and asset prices are shaped by hundreds of asynchronous time series tracking industrial production, labor markets, prices, and consumer confidence. Standard vector autoregressions collapse under parameter proliferation when analyzing large systems (the curse of dimensionality). Dynamic factor models solve this dimensionality dilemma by projecting high-dimensional macroeconomic panels onto a small number of latent pervasive factors that capture economy-wide business cycles. Grounded in state space, dynamic factor models provide the foundational econometric architecture for real-time monitoring, macroeconomic regime detection, and systematic cross-asset forecasting.

1. Large Panels and the Static Factor Representation

Let \(X_t = [x_{1,t}, x_{2,t}, \dots, x_{N,t}]'\) be an \(N\)-dimensional vector of stationary, standardized macroeconomic time series observed at period \(t = 1, \dots, T\). In modern macroeconomics, \(N\) is large (often 50 to 200 indicators), exceeding the degrees of freedom required to estimate unrestricted vector autoregressions.

Definition 2.1 · Dynamic Factor Model

A dynamic factor model decomposes an \(N\)-dimensional panel of stationary macroeconomic indicators \(X_t\) into a low-dimensional vector of \(r\) common latent factors \(F_t = [f_{1,t}, \dots, f_{r,t}]'\) (where \(r \ll N\)) and an \(N\)-dimensional vector of idiosyncratic disturbances \(e_t\):

\[X_t = \Lambda F_t + e_t\]

where \(\Lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times r}\) is the matrix of factor loadings. The common factors follow an autonomous linear vector autoregressive dynamic process:

\[F_t = A_1 F_{t-1} + A_2 F_{t-2} + \dots + A_p F_{t-p} + u_t, \quad u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q)\]

where the common shocks \(u_t\) and idiosyncratic errors \(e_t\) are mutually orthogonal at all leads and lags: \(\E[u_t e_s'] = 0\) for all \(t, s\).

As shown by Stock & Watson (2002), a dynamic model with lagged factor loadings \(\sum_{j=0}^k \Gamma_j f_{t-j}\) can always be written in the static form of Definition 2.1 by stacking current and lagged dynamic factors into the state vector \(F_t = [f_t', f_{t-1}', \dots, f_{t-k}']'\). This static representation allows static Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to extract consistent estimates of the factor space.

2. Principal Component Estimation and Consistency

Given a balanced panel \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times N}\), the factor loadings \(\Lambda\) and common factors \(F\) are estimated by solving the least-squares optimization problem:

\[\min_{\Lambda, F} \frac{1}{NT} \sum_{t=1}^T (X_t - \Lambda F_t)'(X_t - \Lambda F_t) = \min_{\Lambda, F} \frac{1}{NT} \tr\left((X - F \Lambda')'(X - F \Lambda')\right)\]

subject to the normalization constraint \(\frac{1}{T} F' F = I_r\), or alternatively \(\frac{1}{N} \Lambda' \Lambda = I_r\).

Theorem 2.1 · Principal Component Factor Extraction

Let \(\Sigma_{XX} = \frac{1}{T} X' X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}\) be the sample covariance matrix of standardized panel \(X\). The least-squares estimator of the factor loadings \(\hat{\Lambda}\) is given by the \(r\) eigenvectors of \(\Sigma_{XX}\) corresponding to the \(r\) largest eigenvalues, normalized such that \(\hat{\Lambda}' \hat{\Lambda} = I_r\). The estimated static factors are given by:

\[\hat{F} = X \hat{\Lambda}\]

Under the conditions that \(\frac{1}{N} \Lambda' \Lambda \to D_\Lambda\) (positive definite) and mild cross-sectional correlation in idiosyncratic errors \(e_t\), as \(N \to \infty\) and \(T \to \infty\), \(\hat{F}_t\) consistently recovers the true latent factor space up to an invertible rotation matrix \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}\):

\[\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \|\hat{F}_t - H F_t\|^2 \xrightarrow{\;p\;} 0\]

Proof Sketch. Concentrating out \(\Lambda\) from the objective: for any fixed \(F\), OLS yields \(\hat{\Lambda}' = (F' F)^{-1} F' X\). Imposing the normalization \(\frac{1}{T} F' F = I_r\), \(\hat{\Lambda} = \frac{1}{T} X' F\). Substituting \(\hat{\Lambda}\) back into the trace objective reduces the problem to maximizing \(\tr(F' X X' F)\). By Rayleigh's quotient theorem, the columns of \(\hat{F} / \sqrt{T}\) must be the eigenvectors of the \(T \times T\) matrix \(\frac{1}{NT} X X'\) corresponding to the \(r\) largest eigenvalues. Dually, the loadings \(\hat{\Lambda}\) are the eigenvectors of \(\frac{1}{T} X' X\). Consistency as \(N, T \to \infty\) follows from the asymptotic dominance of the pervasive eigenvalues of order \(O(N)\) over the bounded idiosyncratic eigenvalues of order \(O(1)\), as proved in Stock & Watson (2002). \(\blacksquare\)

3. State-Space Representation and VAR Transition

Embedding the dynamic factor model into a formal state-space model enables exact filtering and prediction under temporal lags:

Definition 2.2 · State-Space Formulation of Dynamic Factors

The state-space representation of a dynamic factor model consists of two equations:

  1. Measurement Equation: Links observed macro indicators \(X_t\) to latent factors \(F_t\): \[X_t = \Lambda F_t + e_t, \quad e_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R)\] where \(R = \diag(\sigma_{e,1}^2, \dots, \sigma_{e,N}^2)\) is diagonal in the strict factor model.
  2. Transition Equation: Governs the temporal evolution of the latent factors: \[F_t = A F_{t-1} + u_t, \quad u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q)\] where \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}\) satisfies the stationarity condition: all eigenvalues of \(A\) lie strictly within the unit circle (\(|\lambda_i(A)| < 1\)).

In practice, \(A\) and \(Q\) are estimated by fitting a VAR(1) via OLS on the extracted factor series \(\hat{F}_t\):

\[\hat{A} = \left(\sum_{t=2}^T \hat{F}_t \hat{F}_{t-1}'\right) \left(\sum_{t=2}^T \hat{F}_{t-1} \hat{F}_{t-1}'\right)^{-1}\] \[\hat{Q} = \frac{1}{T-1} \sum_{t=2}^T (\hat{F}_t - \hat{A} \hat{F}_{t-1})(\hat{F}_t - \hat{A} \hat{F}_{t-1})'\]

Lab 2 · Stock-Watson Dynamic Factor Extraction in State Space

Estimating principal component factors on a 30-variable simulated macroeconomic panel will recover orthogonal latent factors that explain the majority of cross-sectional variance and yield a stable VAR(1) transition matrix.

"""Lab GM1-L02-1: Stock-Watson Dynamic Factor Extraction in State Space.

Estimates a static dynamic factor model via principal components on a 30-variable
macroeconomic panel (Stock & Watson 2002), fits a VAR(1) transition equation on the
latent factors, and verifies factor orthogonality and transition matrix stability.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def extract_factors_and_fit_var(panel: np.ndarray, n_factors: int = 2) -> dict[str, np.ndarray | float | bool]:
    """Extract static principal component factors and fit VAR(1) state space transition."""
    t_obs, _ = panel.shape

    # 1. Standardize columns
    means = np.mean(panel, axis=0)
    stds = np.std(panel, axis=0, ddof=1)
    x_std = (panel - means) / stds

    # 2. Covariance matrix and spectral decomposition
    cov = (x_std.T @ x_std) / float(t_obs)
    eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(cov)

    # Sort descending
    sort_idx = np.argsort(eigvals)[::-1]
    eigvals = eigvals[sort_idx]
    eigvecs = eigvecs[:, sort_idx]

    total_var = float(np.sum(eigvals))
    explained_ratio = eigvals[:n_factors] / total_var

    # 3. Factor loadings and static factors
    loadings = eigvecs[:, :n_factors]
    factors = x_std @ loadings  # Shape (T, r)

    # Factor sample covariance
    factor_cov = (factors.T @ factors) / float(t_obs)

    # 4. VAR(1) transition: F_t = A F_{t-1} + u_t
    f_curr = factors[1:]
    f_prev = factors[:-1]
    trans_a, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(f_prev, f_curr, rcond=None)
    mat_a = trans_a.T  # Shape (r, r)

    # Transition stability: spectral radius
    eigenvalues_a = np.linalg.eigvals(mat_a)
    spectral_radius = float(np.max(np.abs(eigenvalues_a)))
    is_stable = bool(spectral_radius < 1.0)

    # Residual covariance Q
    resids = f_curr - f_prev @ trans_a
    q_cov = (resids.T @ resids) / float(len(resids) - 1)

    return {
        "eigenvalues": eigvals[:5],
        "explained_ratio": explained_ratio,
        "factor_cov": factor_cov,
        "mat_a": mat_a,
        "spectral_radius": spectral_radius,
        "is_stable": is_stable,
        "q_cov": q_cov,
    }


def main() -> None:
    print("GM1 Lab L02-1: Dynamic Factor Model Estimation in State Space [SIMULATED]")
    print("=========================================================================")

    rng = np.random.default_rng(101)
    t_obs = 240  # 20 years of monthly data
    n_series = 30  # 30 macro indicators (production, labor, prices, credit, orders)
    n_factors = 2

    # Simulate true 2-factor latent macro dynamics:
    # Factor 1: Real activity (persistent cycle)
    # Factor 2: Nominal / price-rates pressure
    true_factors = np.zeros((t_obs, n_factors), dtype=np.float64)
    true_a = np.array([[0.75, 0.04], [-0.08, 0.65]], dtype=np.float64)
    q_true = np.array([[0.30, 0.02], [0.02, 0.25]], dtype=np.float64)
    chol_q = np.linalg.cholesky(q_true)

    for t in range(1, t_obs):
        true_factors[t] = true_a @ true_factors[t - 1] + chol_q @ rng.standard_normal(n_factors)

    # Factor loadings: series 0-14 load heavily on factor 1 (real), series 15-29 on factor 2 (nominal)
    true_loadings = np.zeros((n_series, n_factors), dtype=np.float64)
    true_loadings[:15, 0] = rng.uniform(0.5, 0.9, size=15)
    true_loadings[:15, 1] = rng.uniform(-0.3, 0.3, size=15)
    true_loadings[15:, 0] = rng.uniform(-0.2, 0.4, size=15)
    true_loadings[15:, 1] = rng.uniform(0.6, 0.95, size=15)

    # Idiosyncratic noise
    idiosyncratic_noise = rng.normal(0.0, 0.45, size=(t_obs, n_series))
    raw_panel = true_factors @ true_loadings.T + idiosyncratic_noise

    res = extract_factors_and_fit_var(raw_panel, n_factors=n_factors)

    print(f"Panel dimensions: T = {t_obs} observations, N = {n_series} variables")
    print(f"Number of extracted static factors: {n_factors}")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------------")
    print("Top 5 Eigenvalues of Standardized Panel Covariance:")
    eigs = res["eigenvalues"]
    assert isinstance(eigs, np.ndarray)
    for i, val in enumerate(eigs):
        print(f"  Eigenvalue {i + 1}: {float(val):6.4f}")

    ratios = res["explained_ratio"]
    assert isinstance(ratios, np.ndarray)
    print(f"Variance explained by Factor 1: {float(ratios[0]) * 100:5.2f}%")
    print(f"Variance explained by Factor 2: {float(ratios[1]) * 100:5.2f}%")
    print(f"Cumulative Variance (r=2):     {float(np.sum(ratios)) * 100:5.2f}%")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    fcov = res["factor_cov"]
    assert isinstance(fcov, np.ndarray)
    fcov_clean = np.where(np.abs(fcov) < 1e-12, 0.0, fcov)
    print("Factor Sample Covariance Matrix (Orthogonality check F'F / T):")
    print(f"  [[{fcov_clean[0, 0]:6.4f}, {fcov_clean[0, 1]:6.4f}],")
    print(f"   [{fcov_clean[1, 0]:6.4f}, {fcov_clean[1, 1]:6.4f}]]")
    assert abs(fcov[0, 1]) < 1e-12, "Sample factors must be strictly orthogonal"
    print("Factor orthogonality confirmed: True")
    print("-------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    mat_a = res["mat_a"]
    assert isinstance(mat_a, np.ndarray)
    print("Estimated VAR(1) State Transition Matrix A:")
    print(f"  Row 1: [{mat_a[0, 0]:6.4f}, {mat_a[0, 1]:6.4f}]")
    print(f"  Row 2: [{mat_a[1, 0]:6.4f}, {mat_a[1, 1]:6.4f}]")
    print(f"Spectral radius |lambda_max|:   {float(res['spectral_radius']):.4f}")
    print(f"State space system is stable:   {res['is_stable']}")

Where the Canon Breaks

Standard dynamic factor model theory rests on idealizations that break under financial and macroeconomic stress:

  • Structural Break Instability in Loadings: Standard PCA assumes the loading matrix \(\Lambda\) is constant across the entire sample. During sudden regime shifts (such as the 2008 Financial Crisis or 2020 pandemic lockdowns), the sensitivity of indicators to common shocks shifts violently. Constant-loading models misattribute structural parameter shifts to massive idiosyncratic error spikes.
  • Pervasiveness Failures and Sectoral Clusters: If an econometrician includes 40 housing market variables and only 5 industrial production series, the top principal component will capture local housing market variation rather than the aggregate macro business cycle. Factors must be extracted from balanced categories rather than raw available vendor dumps.
  • Sign and Scale Indeterminacy: Because \(\Lambda F_t = (\Lambda H^{-1})(H F_t)\) for any orthonormal matrix \(H\), the sign of an extracted factor is arbitrary. An unmonitored factor can silently flip sign between estimation windows, inverting systematic long/short trading signals unless re-anchored to an economic reference variable (such as GDP or industrial production).

Misconceptions

  • Adding more macroeconomic variables always improves dynamic factor model estimation.

    Adding redundant, noisy, or uninformative series increases the noise-to-signal ratio and contaminates factor loadings. Variables must satisfy the pervasiveness condition: their variance must be dominated by common market-wide shocks rather than idiosyncratic noise.

  • Principal component factors directly identify economic primitives such as monetary policy or technology shocks.

    Statistical PCA factors are purely mathematical orthogonal bases that span the common space. They do not possess structural economic identities without explicit identification rotations (e.g. sign restrictions, Cholesky decompositions, or external instrumental variables).

  • Dynamic factor models require idiosyncratic errors to be strictly independent across series.

    Approximate dynamic factor models (Stock & Watson 2002) allow mild cross-sectional and temporal correlation among idiosyncratic errors, provided that the largest eigenvalue of the idiosyncratic covariance matrix remains bounded as \(N \to \infty\).

Practice Problems

Mechanics Tier

Problem M1. In a standardized panel of \(N = 50\) macroeconomic indicators, the top three eigenvalues of the sample covariance matrix \(\Sigma_{XX}\) are \(d_1 = 16.5\), \(d_2 = 8.5\), and \(d_3 = 4.0\). Calculate the percentage of total variance explained by the first two factors, and determine the cumulative variance explained by all three factors.

Solution

Because each indicator is standardized to unit variance, the total variance of the \(N = 50\) panel equals the trace of the correlation matrix:

\[\tr(\Sigma_{XX}) = \sum_{i=1}^{50} d_i = N = 50.0\]

The variance explained by Factor 1 and Factor 2 is:

\[\text{Share}(F_1) = \frac{16.5}{50.0} = 33.0\%, \quad \text{Share}(F_2) = \frac{8.5}{50.0} = 17.0\%\] \[\text{Cumulative Share}(F_1 + F_2) = \frac{16.5 + 8.5}{50.0} = \frac{25.0}{50.0} = 50.0\%\]

Adding Factor 3:

\[\text{Cumulative Share}(F_1 + F_2 + F_3) = \frac{25.0 + 4.0}{50.0} = \frac{29.0}{50.0} = 58.0\%\]

Problem M2. A dynamic factor model in state space has two latent factors \(F_t = [f_{1,t}, f_{2,t}]'\) with VAR(1) transition matrix:

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & 0.1 \\ -0.2 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\]

Compute the eigenvalues of \(A\) and verify whether the latent state dynamics are asymptotically stationary.

Solution

The eigenvalues solve the characteristic polynomial \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\):

\[(0.6 - \lambda)(0.5 - \lambda) - (0.1)(-0.2) = 0\] \[\lambda^2 - 1.1\lambda + 0.30 + 0.02 = \lambda^2 - 1.1\lambda + 0.32 = 0\]

Applying the quadratic formula:

\[\lambda = \frac{1.1 \pm \sqrt{1.21 - 4(0.32)}}{2} = \frac{1.1 \pm \sqrt{1.21 - 1.28}}{2} = \frac{1.1 \pm \sqrt{-0.07}}{2} = 0.55 \pm i \frac{\sqrt{0.07}}{2}\]

The modulus of the complex conjugate pair is:

\[|\lambda| = \sqrt{0.55^2 + \frac{0.07}{4}} = \sqrt{0.3025 + 0.0175} = \sqrt{0.32} \approx 0.5657\]

Because \(|\lambda| = 0.5657 < 1.0\), the state-space system is strictly asymptotically stationary.

Problem M3. For an indicator \(x_{i,t}\) standardized to mean 0 and variance 1, the estimated factor loadings are \(\lambda_{i,1} = 0.70\) and \(\lambda_{i,2} = -0.30\). If the factors are orthogonal with unit variance (\(\Var(f_1) = \Var(f_2) = 1, \Cov(f_1, f_2) = 0\)), compute the common variance share (communalities) and the idiosyncratic variance \(\sigma_{e,i}^2\).

Solution

Under the factor model \(x_{i,t} = \lambda_{i,1} f_{1,t} + \lambda_{i,2} f_{2,t} + e_{i,t}\), total variance decomposes as:

\[\Var(x_{i,t}) = \lambda_{i,1}^2 \Var(f_1) + \lambda_{i,2}^2 \Var(f_2) + \Var(e_{i,t})\] \[1.0 = (0.70)^2 \cdot 1.0 + (-0.30)^2 \cdot 1.0 + \sigma_{e,i}^2 = 0.49 + 0.09 + \sigma_{e,i}^2 = 0.58 + \sigma_{e,i}^2\]

The common variance share (communality) is \(0.58\) (58%), and the idiosyncratic variance is:

\[\sigma_{e,i}^2 = 1.0 - 0.58 = 0.42 \quad (42\%)\]

Proof Tier

Problem P1. Prove that the sample static factors \(\hat{F} = X \hat{\Lambda}\) extracted from the spectral decomposition of \(\Sigma_{XX} = \frac{1}{T} X' X\) with normalization \(\hat{\Lambda}' \hat{\Lambda} = I_r\) are mutually orthogonal with sample covariance matrix equal to \(\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r)\).

Solution

By definition, the columns of \(\hat{\Lambda}\) are the orthonormal eigenvectors of \(\Sigma_{XX}\):

\[\Sigma_{XX} \hat{\Lambda} = \hat{\Lambda} D, \quad \text{where } D = \diag(d_1, \dots, d_r) \text{ and } \hat{\Lambda}' \hat{\Lambda} = I_r\]

The sample covariance matrix of the extracted factors \(\hat{F} = X \hat{\Lambda}\) is:

\[\frac{1}{T} \hat{F}' \hat{F} = \frac{1}{T} (X \hat{\Lambda})' (X \hat{\Lambda}) = \hat{\Lambda}' \left(\frac{1}{T} X' X\right) \hat{\Lambda} = \hat{\Lambda}' \Sigma_{XX} \hat{\Lambda}\]

Substitute \(\Sigma_{XX} \hat{\Lambda} = \hat{\Lambda} D\):

\[\frac{1}{T} \hat{F}' \hat{F} = \hat{\Lambda}' (\hat{\Lambda} D) = (\hat{\Lambda}' \hat{\Lambda}) D = I_r D = D = \diag(d_1, \dots, d_r)\]

Since \(D\) is diagonal, the sample factors have zero sample covariance: \(\frac{1}{T} \hat{F}_i' \hat{F}_j = 0\) for all \(i \neq j\). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P2. Prove that for any invertible \(r \times r\) matrix \(H\), the factor model \(X_t = \Lambda F_t + e_t\) is observationally equivalent to \(X_t = \Lambda^* F_t^* + e_t\) where \(\Lambda^* = \Lambda H^{-1}\) and \(F_t^* = H F_t\), and show that PCA identifies factors only up to an orthonormal rotation.

Solution

Substitute \(\Lambda^* = \Lambda H^{-1}\) and \(F_t^* = H F_t\) into the common component:

\[\Lambda^* F_t^* = (\Lambda H^{-1})(H F_t) = \Lambda (H^{-1} H) F_t = \Lambda I_r F_t = \Lambda F_t\]

The observed data vector \(X_t = \Lambda^* F_t^* + e_t = \Lambda F_t + e_t\) is algebraically identical for all \(t\). If both representations satisfy the normalization constraint \(\frac{1}{T} F' F = I_r\) and \(\frac{1}{T} (F^*)' F^* = I_r\):

\[I_r = \frac{1}{T} (H F)' (H F) = H \left(\frac{1}{T} F' F\right) H' = H I_r H' = H H'\]

The condition \(H H' = I_r\) implies that \(H\) must be an orthogonal matrix (\(H^{-1} = H'\)). Thus, unconstrained principal component analysis identifies the factor space only up to an arbitrary orthogonal rotation (rotation indeterminacy). \(\blacksquare\)

Problem P3. Let \(X_t = \lambda f_t + e_t\) be a single-factor model (\(r = 1\)) where \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^N\) with \(\|\lambda\|^2 = N \bar{\lambda}^2\). Let \(\Cov(e_t) = \sigma_e^2 I_N\). Prove that as \(N \to \infty\), the largest eigenvalue of \(\Sigma = \E[X_t X_t']\) grows at rate \(O(N)\) while all other eigenvalues are bounded at \(\sigma_e^2\).

Solution

The population covariance matrix is \(\Sigma = \lambda \lambda' \Var(f_t) + \sigma_e^2 I_N\). Normalizing \(\Var(f_t) = 1\), \(\Sigma = \lambda \lambda' + \sigma_e^2 I_N\). Consider multiplying \(\Sigma\) by the vector \(\lambda\):

\[\Sigma \lambda = (\lambda \lambda' + \sigma_e^2 I_N)\lambda = \lambda (\lambda' \lambda) + \sigma_e^2 \lambda = (\|\lambda\|^2 + \sigma_e^2)\lambda = (N \bar{\lambda}^2 + \sigma_e^2)\lambda\]

This shows that \(\lambda\) is an eigenvector of \(\Sigma\) with eigenvalue \(d_1 = N \bar{\lambda}^2 + \sigma_e^2 = O(N)\). Now let \(v \in \mathbb{R}^N\) be any non-zero vector orthogonal to \(\lambda\) (\(\lambda' v = 0\)):

\[\Sigma v = (\lambda \lambda' + \sigma_e^2 I_N)v = \lambda (\lambda' v) + \sigma_e^2 v = 0 + \sigma_e^2 v = \sigma_e^2 v\]

Thus, all other \(N - 1\) eigenvalues are identically equal to \(\sigma_e^2 = O(1)\). As \(N \to \infty\), the ratio of the common eigenvalue to the idiosyncratic eigenvalues diverges: \(d_1 / d_2 \to \infty\), proving that the pervasive common factor dominates the spectral decomposition. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical Tier

Problem E1. An econometrician estimates a dynamic factor model on a panel of 100 macroeconomic series over 300 months. The Bai & Ng (2002) \(IC_{p2}\) information criterion is defined as:

\[IC_{p2}(k) = \ln(V(k)) + k \left(\frac{N + T}{NT}\right) \ln(\min(N, T))\]

where \(V(k) = \frac{1}{NT} \sum_{t=1}^T \|X_t - \hat{\Lambda}_k \hat{F}_{k,t}\|^2\). Given values \(V(1) = 0.72\), \(V(2) = 0.54\), \(V(3) = 0.51\), and \(V(4) = 0.49\), compute \(IC_{p2}(k)\) for \(k = 1, 2, 3, 4\) and determine the optimal number of factors.

Solution

Given \(N = 100\), \(T = 300\):

\[\min(N, T) = 100, \quad \ln(\min(N, T)) = \ln(100) \approx 4.60517\] \[P(N, T) = \frac{N + T}{NT} \ln(\min(N, T)) = \frac{400}{30000} \cdot 4.60517 = \frac{4}{300} \cdot 4.60517 \approx 0.013333 \times 4.60517 \approx 0.06140\]

Now evaluate \(IC_{p2}(k) = \ln(V(k)) + k \times 0.06140\):

  • \(k = 1\): \(\ln(0.72) + 1(0.06140) = -0.3285 + 0.0614 = -0.2671\)
  • \(k = 2\): \(\ln(0.54) + 2(0.06140) = -0.6162 + 0.1228 = -0.4934\)
  • \(k = 3\): \(\ln(0.51) + 3(0.06140) = -0.6733 + 0.1842 = -0.4891\)
  • \(k = 4\): \(\ln(0.49) + 4(0.06140) = -0.7133 + 0.2456 = -0.4677\)

The criterion is minimized at \(k = 2\) (\(-0.4934 < -0.4891\)). Thus, the optimal number of static factors selected by the Bai-Ng criterion is \(r^* = 2\).

Problem E2. An algorithmic trader uses the first principal component of an 80-variable macroeconomic panel as an equity market exposure filter. Over a rolling 5-year window, the eigenvector of the first factor is recomputed monthly. In month 36, the correlation between the new factor \(\hat{f}_{t}^{(36)}\) and the previous month's factor \(\hat{f}_{t}^{(35)}\) is \(-0.998\). Explain what happened and describe the mandatory safeguard required in automated deployment.

Solution

The algorithm encountered the classic sign indeterminacy of eigendecomposition: numerical solvers can arbitrarily return either \(\hat{v}\) or \(-\hat{v}\) as the leading eigenvector because both satisfy \(\Sigma v = \lambda v\). A sign flip changes the factor correlation to \(-1.0\), causing a long-exposure rule to instantly switch to a short-exposure rule without any underlying economic change.

The mandatory automated safeguard is sign-alignment: at each rolling estimation step, compute the inner product \(\langle \hat{\Lambda}_{\text{new}, 1}, \hat{\Lambda}_{\text{old}, 1} \rangle\) (or correlation with a designated anchor series such as industrial production). If the inner product is negative, multiply both the loadings and factors by \(-1.0\) before generating signals.

Problem E3. A quantitative macro fund compares out-of-sample forecast accuracy for 10-year Treasury yield changes using: (Model A) an unregularized 40-variable VAR(1), versus (Model B) a 2-factor dynamic factor model. Over an 80-quarter evaluation sample, the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of Model A is \(82.4\) bps and Model B is \(48.6\) bps. Compute the percentage reduction in RMSE achieved by dimensionality reduction.

Solution

The percentage reduction in forecast RMSE is:

\[\Delta \text{RMSE} = \frac{48.6 - 82.4}{82.4} = \frac{-33.8}{82.4} \approx -41.02\%\]

Dimensionality reduction via the dynamic factor model reduces forecast error by 41.02%, demonstrating how eliminating estimation noise from the \(40 \times 40 = 1600\) parameters of the unrestricted VAR drastically improves out-of-sample prediction.

Problem R1. Formulate the EM (Expectation-Maximization) algorithm for maximum likelihood estimation of dynamic factor models when data are subject to arbitrary missingness patterns, and compare its convergence properties to two-step PCA.

Solution

In the presence of missing data, PCA cannot be applied directly without imputation. The EM algorithm iterates between two steps:

  1. E-step (Expectation): Given current parameter estimates \(\theta^{(k)} = \{\Lambda, A, Q, R\}\), compute the smoothed state estimates \(\hat{F}_{t|T} = \E[F_t \mid X_{1:T}, \theta^{(k)}]\) and cross-covariances \(P_{t, t-1|T}\) using the Kalman smoother modified for missing observations.
  2. M-step (Maximization): Maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood with respect to \(\theta\): \[\hat{\Lambda}^{(k+1)} = \left(\sum_{t=1}^T X_t^* \hat{F}_{t|T}'\right) \left(\sum_{t=1}^T \hat{F}_{t|T} \hat{F}_{t|T}' + P_{t|T}\right)^{-1}\] where missing entries in \(X_t\) are replaced by their model expectations \(\Lambda \hat{F}_{t|T}\). Update \(A, Q, R\) via corresponding sample moments.

While EM yields the exact maximum likelihood estimator under Gaussianity, it converges slowly on large panels (\(N > 100\)). The two-step procedure of Doz, Giannone & Reichlin (2011) initializes parameters via PCA on balanced subsets and runs a single EM iteration, achieving asymptotic equivalence to MLE at orders-of-magnitude lower computational cost.

Problem R2. Evaluate the econometric consequences of estimating factor loadings on non-stationary series that have been differenced versus estimating in levels with cointegrated factors.

Solution

If macroeconomic series are I(1) non-stationary:

  1. First Differencing: Differencing ensures stationarity (\(I(0)\)), satisfying the core assumptions of Stock-Watson consistency. However, overdifferencing removes common stochastic trends (cointegration) that contain powerful long-horizon predictive information for interest rate spreads and productivity.
  2. Levels with Cointegration: Estimating factors directly on levels preserves cointegrating relationships (e.g. common unit root stochastic trends). However, standard PCA estimators on non-stationary data exhibit non-standard asymptotic distributions and can produce spurious factor convergence unless non-stationary factor model adjustments (Bai 2004) are implemented.

Problem R3. Design a structural dynamic factor model that disentangles real activity shocks from nominal supply shocks using zero-sign restrictions on factor loadings.

Solution

Let \(X_t = \Lambda F_t + e_t\) with \(r = 2\) where \(F_t = [f_{\text{demand}, t}, f_{\text{supply}, t}]'\). Impose sign restrictions on the loading matrix \(\Lambda\):

  • Positive Aggregate Demand Shock (\(f_{\text{demand}}\)): Output variables (industrial production, payrolls) have positive loadings (\(\lambda_{y, 1} > 0\)) AND price variables (CPI, PPI) have positive loadings (\(\lambda_{\pi, 1} > 0\)).
  • Positive Aggregate Supply Shock (\(f_{\text{supply}}\)): Output variables have positive loadings (\(\lambda_{y, 2} > 0\)) BUT price variables have negative loadings (\(\lambda_{\pi, 2} < 0\)).

Starting from an arbitrary statistical PCA decomposition \(\hat{F}_t\), draw random orthonormal rotation matrices \(Q \in \mathbb{O}(2)\) via QR decomposition of standard normal matrices. Retain rotations where \(\hat{\Lambda}^* = \hat{\Lambda} Q'\) satisfies the joint sign restrictions across all classified output and inflation series, producing economically identified structural factors.

Retrieval Practice

  1. What mathematical technique does the static representation of Stock & Watson (2002) use to extract consistent common factors from large macroeconomic panels?

    Options

    Stock & Watson (2002) prove that static principal components consistently recover the common factor space as \(N, T \to \infty\).

  2. Why does an unrestricted Vector Autoregression (VAR) fail when applied directly to a panel of 100 macroeconomic indicators?

    Options

    Unrestricted VAR systems suffer quadratic parameter explosion in \(N\), causing massive in-sample overfitting and catastrophic out-of-sample forecast failure.

  3. What is the asymptotic behavior of the largest \(r\) eigenvalues of the panel covariance matrix \(\Sigma_{XX}\) as cross-sectional dimension \(N \to \infty\) in a dynamic factor model?

    Options

    Pervasive common factors load on a non-vanishing fraction of series, causing their sample eigenvalues to expand linearly with \(N\), dominating idiosyncratic variance.

  4. What structural condition must the factor VAR(1) transition matrix \(A\) satisfy to guarantee that the state-space model is stationary?

    Options

    A discrete-time linear state-space transition equation is asymptotically covariance-stationary if and only if the spectral radius of the transition matrix is strictly less than unity.

  5. Why are PCA factor loadings and latent factor paths identified only up to an arbitrary invertible rotation matrix \(H\)?

    Options

    Linear factor models exhibit inherent rotation indeterminacy: any invertible linear combination of the factors can be offset by the inverse transformation on the loadings.

  6. In a panel of 40 standardized series, the top two eigenvalues of the covariance matrix are \(d_1 = 15.0\) and \(d_2 = 9.0\). What percentage of total variance is captured by these two factors?

    Total variance is \(N = 40.0\). Sum of eigenvalues is \(15.0 + 9.0 = 24.0\). The percentage is \((24.0 / 40.0) \times 100 = 60.0\%\).

  7. An indicator has factor loading \(\lambda_1 = 0.60\) on a normalized factor with unit variance. If the indicator has unit variance and zero loading on all other factors, what is its idiosyncratic variance \(\sigma_e^2\)?

    Common variance is \(\lambda_1^2 = 0.60^2 = 0.36\). The idiosyncratic variance is \(1.0 - 0.36 = 0.64\).

  8. What risk arises when using rolling-window PCA to estimate factors in live trading strategies?

    Options

    Eigenvectors are identified only up to sign (\(\pm 1\)). Rolling estimations without sign alignment can flip signs from one month to the next, reversing portfolio positions.
