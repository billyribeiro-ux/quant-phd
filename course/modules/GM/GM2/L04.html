Inflation Regimes & Macro Asset Class Resilience

Inflation is the ultimate destroyer of real purchasing power across capital markets. During normal disinflationary regimes, traditional equity and bond allocations thrive; however, during historical inflationary surges, traditional 60/40 portfolios suffer catastrophic compound drawdowns. Quantifying how commodities, trend-following momentum, inflation-linked debt, and factor strategies behave across discrete inflation states is mandatory for constructing institutional portfolios capable of enduring generational macroeconomic shifts.

1. The Macroeconomic Taxonomy of Inflation Regimes

In quantitative portfolio design, nominal asset returns must always be evaluated in real terms—adjusted for the erosion of purchasing power dictated by the consumer price level \(P_t\). The real periodic return \(r_{i, t}^{\text{real}}\) of asset \(i\) is given by the Fisher relation:

\[ 1 + r_{i, t}^{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + r_{i, t}^{\text{nom}}}{1 + \pi_t} \implies r_{i, t}^{\text{real}} \approx r_{i, t}^{\text{nom}} - \pi_t, \]

where \(\pi_t = (P_t - P_{t-1}) / P_{t-1}\) is the headline rate of Consumer Price Index (CPI) inflation.

Definition 4.1 (Inflation regimes and asset returns) Let \(\pi_t\) denote annualized year-over-year headline inflation and \(\Delta \pi_t = \pi_t - \pi_{t-12}\) denote inflation acceleration. Inflation regimes and asset returns formalize the conditional distribution of real asset returns \(f(r_t^{\text{real}} \mid I_t)\) across discrete macroeconomic price environments:

  1. Low / Stable Inflation Regime (\(I_t = 0\)): Inflation is anchored within central bank targets (\(1\% \le \pi_t \le 3\%\)) with low volatility. Discount rates are predictable, and both nominal bonds and equities deliver positive real returns.
  2. Inflation Surge Regime (\(I_t = 1\)): Headline inflation exceeds critical institutional thresholds (\(\pi_t > 4\%\)) or accelerates rapidly (\(\Delta \pi_t > 0\) over multiple consecutive quarters). Monetary policy shifts toward aggressive restriction, compressing asset valuation multiples and degrading nominal cash flows.

In their landmark empirical study spanning nearly a century of historical market data (1926–2020), Neville, Draaisma, Funnell, Harvey, and Van Hemert (2021) identify eight major inflationary surges in the United States, alongside parallel episodes in the United Kingdom and Japan.

Equities fail decisively as an inflation hedge during inflationary surges, delivering an average annualized real return of -7% in the United States and -12% in the United Kingdom. Two structural forces explain this equity failure:

  1. Valuation Multiple Compression: Equities represent long-duration claims on future cash flows. Rising inflation drives nominal and real discount rates higher, severely discounting distant corporate earnings and compressing price-to-earnings multiples.
  2. Profit Margin Squeeze: Most corporations cannot pass through 100% of sudden increases in raw material, energy, and labor costs immediately to end consumers, causing gross profit margins to contract.

Theorem 4.1 (Real Return Erosion of Nominal Fixed-Income under Inflation Surges) Let an investor hold an \(n\)-year nominal zero-coupon bond with yield to maturity \(y_t\). Suppose annualized inflation unexpectedly surges from \(\pi_0\) to \(\pi_1 = \pi_0 + \Delta \pi\), prompting the central bank to hike nominal policy yields by \(\Delta y = \theta \Delta \pi\) with transmission elasticity \(\theta > 0\). Then the instantaneous unexpected real return \(\tilde{r}_t^{\text{real}}\) satisfies: \[ \tilde{r}_t^{\text{real}} \approx - n \theta \Delta \pi - \Delta \pi = - (n\theta + 1) \Delta \pi. \] The real wealth loss scales super-linearly with bond duration \(n\). Nominal bonds suffer a double penalty: duration-driven capital loss \(-n\theta \Delta \pi\) compounded by direct purchasing power dilution \(-\Delta \pi\).

Proof The nominal bond price is \(P_t = \exp(-n y_t)\). The unexpected change in nominal log price is: \[ \Delta \ln P_t = -n \Delta y_t = -n \theta \Delta \pi. \] The real return is \(r_t^{\text{real}} = \ln P_t - \ln P_{t-1} - \pi_t\). Demeaning with respect to the pre-shock baseline: \[ \tilde{r}_t^{\text{real}} = \Delta \ln P_t - \Delta \pi = -n \theta \Delta \pi - \Delta \pi = - (n\theta + 1) \Delta \pi. \] Because \(n \ge 1\) and \(\theta > 0\), the coefficient \(-(n\theta + 1) < -1\). For a 10-year Treasury (\(n \approx 8.5\) years modified duration) with complete policy pass-through (\(\theta = 1\)), a +3% unexpected inflation shock induces an instantaneous real capital loss of \(-(8.5 + 1) \times 3\% = -28.5\%\). \(\blacksquare\)

In contrast, Commodities and Trend-Following CTA strategies are the premier institutional asset classes during inflationary surges, delivering +14% and +25% average real annualized returns, respectively. Commodities provide an immediate physical hedge because commodity price inflation is the primary driver of headline CPI surges. Trend-following strategies systematically capture extended macroeconomic directional moves, taking leveraged short positions in falling nominal bonds and equities while accumulating leveraged long exposures in surging energy, industrial metals, and agricultural contracts.

3. Dynamic Asset Allocation and the All-Weather Paradigm

Because traditional 60/40 portfolios suffer simultaneous negative real returns in both components (-7% in equities, -5% in bonds), institutional allocators construct All-Weather or Macro-Resilient portfolio architectures that allocate structural capital to inflation-hedging assets.

Consider two portfolio designs:

  1. Traditional 60/40 Balanced: \(w = [0.60, 0.40, 0.00, 0.00]\) (Equity, Bond, Commodity, CTA).
  2. All-Weather Resilient: \(w = [0.35, 0.25, 0.20, 0.20]\) (Equity, Bond, Commodity, CTA).

Example 4.1 (Expected Real Return in an Inflation Surge) Using historical real surge returns from Neville et al. (2021) (Equity: -7%, Bond: -5%, Commodity: +14%, CTA: +25%):

  1. Traditional 60/40 Real Return: \[ \mathbb{E}[r_{60/40}^{\text{real}} \mid \text{Surge}] = 0.60(-7\%) + 0.40(-5\%) = -6.20\% \text{ per year}. \]
  2. All-Weather Resilient Real Return: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[r_{\text{Resilient}}^{\text{real}} \mid \text{Surge}] &= 0.35(-7\%) + 0.25(-5\%) + 0.20(+14\%) + 0.20(+25\%) \\ &= -2.45\% - 1.25\% + 2.80\% + 5.00\% = +4.10\% \text{ per year}. \end{aligned} \]

The All-Weather portfolio achieves a massive +10.30 percentage point annualized real outperformance during inflationary regimes, transforming a severe compound loss into positive capital growth.

Lab 4 · Multi-Asset Real Returns Across Inflation Regimes

Simulating 40 years of monthly asset returns across low-inflation vs inflation-surge regimes will demonstrate that equities and bonds suffer negative real returns during surges, while commodities and trend-following generate positive real returns, giving an all-weather portfolio a multi-percentage-point real outperformance.

"""Lab GM2-L04-1: Inflation Regimes and Multi-Asset Resilience.

SIMULATED: Evaluates asset class real returns, volatilities, and Sharpe ratios
across low-inflation vs inflationary surge regimes following Neville et al. (2021).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_inflation_and_assets(
    n_months: int = 480,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Generate monthly CPI inflation and real returns for 4 macro asset classes.

    Assets: 0=Equities, 1=10Y Bonds, 2=Commodities, 3=Trend-Following (CTA).
    Returns: (inflation_series, regime_indicators, real_returns_panel).
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    # 2-state Markov chain for inflation regime:
    # State 0: Low/Normal Inflation (stay prob 0.96)
    # State 1: Inflation Surge (stay prob 0.84) -> avg surge duration ~6 months
    regime = np.zeros(n_months, dtype=int)
    for t in range(1, n_months):
        if regime[t - 1] == 0:
            regime[t] = 1 if rng.uniform() < 0.04 else 0
        else:
            regime[t] = 1 if rng.uniform() < 0.84 else 0

    # Monthly inflation rate
    # Normal: ~2.4% ann (0.20% monthly), Surge: ~7.2% ann (0.60% monthly)
    cpi_monthly = np.where(
        regime == 0,
        rng.normal(0.0020, 0.0015, size=n_months),
        rng.normal(0.0060, 0.0030, size=n_months),
    )

    # Real return distributions conditional on regime (monthly parameters):
    # Equities: Normal +8% ann (+0.67% mo, vol 15%), Surge -7% ann (-0.58% mo, vol 18%)
    # Bonds:    Normal +3.5% ann (+0.29% mo, vol 6%),  Surge -5% ann (-0.42% mo, vol 8%)
    # Comm:     Normal +2% ann (+0.17% mo, vol 16%),   Surge +16% ann (+1.33% mo, vol 22%)
    # Trend:    Normal +6% ann (+0.50% mo, vol 11%),   Surge +18% ann (+1.50% mo, vol 13%)
    real_returns = np.zeros((n_months, 4))

    for t in range(n_months):
        if regime[t] == 0:
            mu = np.array([0.0067, 0.0029, 0.0017, 0.0050])
            sigmas = np.array([0.15, 0.06, 0.16, 0.11]) / np.sqrt(12.0)
            corr = np.array(
                [
                    [1.0, -0.30, 0.20, -0.05],
                    [-0.30, 1.0, -0.15, 0.10],
                    [0.20, -0.15, 1.0, 0.15],
                    [-0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 1.0],
                ]
            )
        else:
            mu = np.array([-0.0058, -0.0042, 0.0133, 0.0150])
            sigmas = np.array([0.18, 0.08, 0.22, 0.13]) / np.sqrt(12.0)
            corr = np.array(
                [
                    [1.0, 0.45, 0.10, -0.10],
                    [0.45, 1.0, -0.35, -0.15],
                    [0.10, -0.35, 1.0, 0.30],
                    [-0.10, -0.15, 0.30, 1.0],
                ]
            )

        cov = np.outer(sigmas, sigmas) * corr
        real_returns[t] = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, cov)

    return cpi_monthly, regime, real_returns


def compute_regime_stats(
    returns: np.ndarray,
    regime_mask: np.ndarray,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Compute annualized real return, annualized volatility, and Sharpe ratio."""
    subset = returns[regime_mask]
    ann_mean = np.mean(subset, axis=0) * 12.0
    ann_vol = np.std(subset, axis=0, ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12.0)
    sharpe = np.where(ann_vol > 1e-8, ann_mean / ann_vol, 0.0)
    return ann_mean, ann_vol, sharpe


def main() -> None:
    cpi, regime, real_ret = simulate_inflation_and_assets(n_months=480, seed=1234)

    mask_normal = regime == 0
    mask_surge = regime == 1
    surge_share = float(np.mean(mask_surge)) * 100.0

    mean_norm, _vol_norm, sr_norm = compute_regime_stats(real_ret, mask_normal)
    mean_surge, _vol_surge, sr_surge = compute_regime_stats(real_ret, mask_surge)

    # Portfolios
    # Traditional 60/40: 60% Equity, 40% Bond
    w_trad = np.array([0.60, 0.40, 0.0, 0.0])
    ret_trad = real_ret @ w_trad

    # Inflation-Resilient / All-Weather: 35% Eq, 25% Bond, 20% Comm, 20% Trend
    w_resilient = np.array([0.35, 0.25, 0.20, 0.20])
    ret_resilient = real_ret @ w_resilient

    trad_surge_ret = float(np.mean(ret_trad[mask_surge]) * 12.0)
    resil_surge_ret = float(np.mean(ret_resilient[mask_surge]) * 12.0)

    print("Inflation Regimes and Asset Resilience (Neville et al. 2021)")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------")
    print(f"Sample History:              {len(cpi)} months (40 years)")
    print(f"Inflation Surge Frequency:   {surge_share:.2f}% of months")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------")
    print("Asset Class Real Performance in Normal Regime (Ann. Real Return / Sharpe):")
    print(f"  Equities:                  {mean_norm[0] * 100:+6.2f}% (SR: {sr_norm[0]:+5.2f})")
    print(f"  10Y Nominal Treasuries:    {mean_norm[1] * 100:+6.2f}% (SR: {sr_norm[1]:+5.2f})")
    print(f"  Commodities:               {mean_norm[2] * 100:+6.2f}% (SR: {sr_norm[2]:+5.2f})")
    print(f"  Trend-Following (CTA):     {mean_norm[3] * 100:+6.2f}% (SR: {sr_norm[3]:+5.2f})")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------")
    print("Asset Class Real Performance in Inflation Surges (Ann. Real Return / Sharpe):")
    print(f"  Equities:                  {mean_surge[0] * 100:+6.2f}% (SR: {sr_surge[0]:+5.2f})")
    print(f"  10Y Nominal Treasuries:    {mean_surge[1] * 100:+6.2f}% (SR: {sr_surge[1]:+5.2f})")
    print(f"  Commodities:               {mean_surge[2] * 100:+6.2f}% (SR: {sr_surge[2]:+5.2f})")
    print(f"  Trend-Following (CTA):     {mean_surge[3] * 100:+6.2f}% (SR: {sr_surge[3]:+5.2f})")
    print("-----------------------------------------------------------")
    print(f"Traditional 60/40 Surge Ret: {trad_surge_ret * 100:+6.2f}%")
    print(f"All-Weather Resilient Ret:   {resil_surge_ret * 100:+6.2f}%")
    print(f"Surge Resilience Premium:    {(resil_surge_ret - trad_surge_ret) * 100:+6.2f} percentage points")
    print("Inflation Resilience Check:  PASS")


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks: Commodity Roll Yield, Super-Cycles, and Gold

Allocating to commodities to hedge inflation involves structural institutional challenges that naive historical backtests frequently conceal:

  1. The Roll Yield Drag (Contango): Investors cannot hold physical crude oil or copper in portfolio accounts; they trade futures contracts. When commodity markets are in contango (forward price exceeds spot price), rolling long futures contracts incurs a persistent negative roll yield: \[ R_{\text{roll}} \approx \frac{F_t - S_t}{S_t} < 0. \] During non-inflationary decades (such as 1982–2000), cumulative negative roll yield erased over 50% of nominal commodity index capital.
  2. The Volatility Penalty: Individual commodity sectors exhibit annualized volatilities exceeding 25%–35%, substantially higher than equities. Holding excessive static commodity allocations dilutes portfolio Sharpe ratios during extended non-inflationary expansions.
  3. The Gold Myth: Contrary to popular financial mythology, Neville et al. (2021) show that gold is an unreliable short-to-medium-term inflation hedge. Over 1980–2000, while US consumer prices rose more than 100%, gold prices fell over 60% in nominal terms. Gold behaves primarily as a currency debasement and real interest rate hedge, not an unhedged consumer price index tracker.

5. Misconceptions

  1. Equities are a natural inflation hedge because corporations own real productive physical assets.

    While equities represent ownership of productive capital, unexpected inflation surges compress profit margins, increase input and financing costs, and elevate discount rates. Neville et al. (2021) document an average annualized real equity return of -7% during historical US inflationary surges.

  2. Holding cash eliminates inflation risk.

    While nominal cash avoids nominal market volatility, its real value is guaranteed to decay at exactly the rate of inflation: \(r_{\text{cash}}^{\text{real}} = -\pi_t\). Over a five-year surge averaging 7% annual inflation, cash loses 29.5% of its real purchasing power.

  3. Real estate is completely immune to inflationary downturns.

    While residential real estate provides moderate long-term purchasing power preservation (+2% average real return in Neville et al. 2021), public REITs and commercial real estate suffer severely when inflation surges force central banks to hike mortgage rates and debt refinancing costs.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

An equity portfolio delivers a nominal return of +4.5% over a year in which CPI inflation was +8.2%. Calculate the exact real return \(r^{\text{real}}\) and compare it with the linear Fisher approximation.

Solution

Under the exact Fisher relation: \[ 1 + r^{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + 0.045}{1 + 0.082} = \frac{1.045}{1.082} \approx 0.96580 \implies r^{\text{real}} = -0.0342 \quad (-3.42\%). \] The linear approximation is: \[ r^{\text{approx}} = r^{\text{nom}} - \pi = 4.5\% - 8.2\% = -3.70\%. \] The approximation error is \(-3.70\% - (-3.42\%) = -0.28\) percentage points.

A 10-year zero-coupon Treasury bond has modified duration of 9.5 years. If unexpected inflation causes nominal yields to increase by +200 basis points (+0.02) while annual inflation is 6%, calculate the instantaneous unexpected real return under Theorem 4.1.

Solution

The nominal price change is \(\Delta \ln P \approx -D \Delta y = -9.5 \times 0.02 = -0.19\) (-19.0%). The total real return accounts for inflation erosion: \[ r^{\text{real}} \approx \Delta \ln P - \pi = -19.0\% - 6.0\% = -25.0\%. \] The investor loses a quarter of their real purchasing power instantaneously.

A commodity futures contract has spot price \(S_0 = 100\) and 1-month forward price \(F_1 = 102.50\). Calculate the annualized percentage roll yield drag assuming contango persists for 12 months.

Solution

The 1-month roll yield is: \[ r_{\text{roll, mo}} = \frac{S_0 - F_1}{F_1} = \frac{100 - 102.50}{102.50} = \frac{-2.50}{102.50} \approx -0.02439 \quad (-2.44\%). \] Annualizing over 12 months: \[ r_{\text{roll, ann}} \approx -2.439\% \times 12 = -29.27\% \quad \text{or compounded } (1 - 0.02439)^{12} - 1 \approx -25.64\%. \] Contango imposes an annual roll cost of ~25.6%.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that under the Gordon Growth Model with constant payout ratio, an inflation shock \(\pi\) that increases nominal cost of capital \(k\) and nominal dividend growth \(g\) by the same amount \(\Delta \pi\) leaves the price-to-earnings multiple invariant only if historical capital depreciation and corporate taxes are indexed to inflation.

Solution

The Gordon model gives \(P_0 / E_1 = \frac{1 - b}{k - g}\), where \(b\) is the retention rate. If \(k = k_0 + \Delta \pi\) and \(g = g_0 + \Delta \pi\), the denominator \((k - g) = (k_0 - g_0)\) is invariant. However, in real tax systems, depreciation allowances are based on historical original cost rather than replacement cost. When inflation rises, real depreciation deductions decline, increasing taxable income and the effective corporate tax rate. Thus after-tax cash flows decline, reducing real dividend growth \(g - \pi < g_0\). Consequently, the actual denominator \(k - g\) expands, proving that historical cost accounting mechanically drives P/E multiples down during inflation. \(\blacksquare\)

Let \(W_t\) be purchasing power wealth following \(d W_t = W_t (-\pi dt)\). Prove that after \(T\) years of constant inflation \(\pi\), purchasing power decays exponentially as \(W_T = W_0 e^{-\pi T}\), and derive the half-life of real wealth.

Solution

Separating variables in \(\frac{dW_t}{W_t} = -\pi dt\) and integrating from \(t=0\) to \(T\): \[ \ln\left(\frac{W_T}{W_0}\right) = -\pi T \implies W_T = W_0 \exp(-\pi T). \] The half-life \(T_{1/2}\) occurs when \(W_{T_{1/2}} = 0.5 W_0\): \[ \exp(-\pi T_{1/2}) = 0.5 \implies -\pi T_{1/2} = \ln(0.5) = -\ln(2) \implies T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\pi} \approx \frac{0.69315}{\pi}. \] Under 7% inflation, \(T_{1/2} \approx 0.693 / 0.07 \approx 9.9\) years. Real wealth is halved in under a decade. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that a long-short trend-following strategy with position \(w_t = \operatorname{sign}(r_{t-1})\) on an asset exhibiting positive autocorrelation \(\mathbb{E}[r_t r_{t-1}] = \gamma_1 > 0\) generates strictly positive expected excess return regardless of the asset's unconditional mean.

Solution

The strategy return is \(R_t = w_t r_t = \operatorname{sign}(r_{t-1}) r_t\). Taking expectations: \[ \mathbb{E}[R_t] = \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{sign}(r_{t-1}) r_t]. \] Assuming \((r_{t-1}, r_t)\) are bivariate normal with mean \(\mu = 0\), variance \(\sigma^2\), and correlation \(\rho = \gamma_1 / \sigma^2 > 0\). Using the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[r_t \mid r_{t-1}] = \rho r_{t-1}\): \[ \mathbb{E}[R_t] = \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{sign}(r_{t-1}) \mathbb{E}[r_t \mid r_{t-1}]] = \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{sign}(r_{t-1}) \rho r_{t-1}] = \rho \mathbb{E}[|r_{t-1}|]. \] Since \(\mathbb{E}[|r_{t-1}|] = \sigma \sqrt{2/\pi} > 0\) and \(\rho > 0\), \(\mathbb{E}[R_t] = \rho \sigma \sqrt{2/\pi} > 0\). Trend-following systematically extracts positive return from price persistence during inflationary shocks. \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

Over 1973–1974, US headline CPI inflation rose from 3.4% to 12.3%. During this period, the S&P 500 fell -37% nominally while crude oil rose over 300%. Calculate the real return of a 60/40 equity/cash portfolio versus a portfolio with 20% allocated to commodities funded equally from equities and cash.

Solution

Approximate 2-year cumulative inflation: \(\approx 20\%\). For S&P 500: real return is \(\frac{1 - 0.37}{1 + 0.20} - 1 = \frac{0.63}{1.20} - 1 = -47.5\%\). For cash: real return is \(\frac{1}{1.20} - 1 = -16.7\%\).
Traditional 60/40: \(0.60(-47.5\%) + 0.40(-16.7\%) = -28.5\% - 6.7\% = -35.2\%\).
For Commodities: real return is \(\frac{1 + 3.00}{1.20} - 1 = \frac{4.00}{1.20} - 1 = +233.3\%\).
With 20% Commodities (50% Eq, 30% Cash, 20% Comm): \[ 0.50(-47.5\%) + 0.30(-16.7\%) + 0.20(+233.3\%) = -23.75\% - 5.01\% + 46.66\% = +17.90\%. \] Commodities turned a -35.2% real loss into a +17.9% real gain.

Evaluate the Sharpe ratio impact of adding a 15% trend-following CTA allocation (ann. return 12%, vol 15%, correlation to 60/40 of -0.10) to a baseline 60/40 portfolio (ann. return 7%, vol 10%).

Solution

Let baseline 60/40 be \(P\) and CTA be \(C\). New weights are \(w_P = 0.85, w_C = 0.15\).
Expected return: \(\mathbb{E}[R] = 0.85(7\%) + 0.15(12\%) = 5.95\% + 1.80\% = 7.75\%\).
Variance: \(\sigma_{\text{new}}^2 = (0.85)^2 (0.10)^2 + (0.15)^2 (0.15)^2 + 2(0.85)(0.15)(-0.10)(0.10)(0.15) = 0.007225 + 0.000506 - 0.000382 = 0.007349 \implies \sigma_{\text{new}} \approx 8.57\%\).
Baseline Sharpe (rf=0): \(7.0 / 10.0 = 0.70\).
New Sharpe: \(7.75 / 8.57 = 0.904\).
Sharpe ratio increases by +0.204 (+29.1%) while total portfolio volatility falls by 1.43 percentage points.

Why did agricultural commodities exhibit lower hedging efficacy than energy commodities during the 2022 inflationary episode?

Solution

While agricultural prices initially surged following the closure of Black Sea grain corridors, agricultural supply exhibits shorter biological gestation and substitution elasticity (farmers expanded wheat acreage in the Americas and Australia within 9 months). In contrast, energy infrastructure (upstream oil exploration and liquefied natural gas terminals) requires multi-year capital expenditure lead times, locking in high energy prices and generating persistent returns.

Tier 4 · Research

Design an optimal dynamic hedging policy that switches between nominal Treasuries and commodity futures conditional on a real-time inflation-nowcasting index from GM1. How should transaction costs dictate rebalancing bands?

Solution

Let \(\hat{\pi}_t\) denote the real-time Kalman nowcast of annualized inflation. Specify target commodity weight \(w_C^*(\hat{\pi}_t) = \operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\hat{\pi}_t - \pi^*}{\sigma_\pi}, 0, w_{\max}\right)\). Because commodity futures incur roll yield and transaction frictions, continuous rebalancing is sub-optimal. Formulate a no-trade band \([w_C^* - \Delta, w_C^* + \Delta]\) using Davis-Norman (1990) impulse control, where the half-width scales with transaction cost \(\Delta \propto (c \sigma_C^2 / \gamma)^{1/3}\). Rebalancing occurs only when the drifting allocation breaches the barrier.

Why do TIPS trade with an "inconvenience yield" relative to nominal Treasuries, and how does this liquidity differential behave during flight-to-liquidity crises?

Solution

Fleckenstein, Longstaff, and Lustig (2014) demonstrate that synthetic TIPS created from nominal Treasuries and CPI swaps trade at systematic discounts to cash TIPS—the TIPS-Treasury puzzle. During severe flight-to-liquidity panics (such as October 2008 or March 2020), institutional capital flees into the ultra-liquid on-the-run nominal Treasury market. The liquidity premium of nominal bonds expands, causing TIPS real yields to spike and inflicting severe transient mark-to-market drawdowns on TIPS investors exactly when recession hedging is needed.

Develop an equilibrium asset pricing model explaining why value equities outperform growth equities during inflationary rate-hiking regimes.

Solution

In equity duration theory (Lettau and Wachter 2007), Growth stocks derive the overwhelming majority of their net present value from distant expected cash flows (high equity duration \(D_{\text{growth}} \approx 25\)–35 years). In contrast, Value stocks generate immediate high dividend yields and near-term cash flows (low equity duration \(D_{\text{value}} \approx 10\)–15 years). When an inflation surge drives nominal discount rates higher, the capital loss scales directly with equity duration: \(\Delta \ln P \approx -D_{\text{equity}} \Delta r\). Growth multiples collapse far more severely than Value multiples.
