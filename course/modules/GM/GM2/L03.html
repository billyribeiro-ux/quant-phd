Stock–Bond Correlation Regimes & The Sign Flip

The correlation between equity and sovereign bond returns is the cornerstone of institutional asset allocation and risk parity. For over three decades (1965–1997), this correlation was persistently positive; around 1998, it flipped sharply negative, turning nominal Treasuries into powerful deflation hedges. In 2022, surging inflation caused the correlation to snap back positive, devastating traditional 60/40 portfolios. Understanding the macroeconomic forces that govern this sign flip is essential for dynamic multi-asset risk management.

1. The Historic Sign Flip: 1965–2024

In modern portfolio theory, the covariance between equities and fixed income dictates portfolio volatility and the efficacy of balanced asset allocation. An investor who assumes this correlation is a static parameter commits a grave structural modeling error.

Definition 3.1 (Stock–bond correlation regimes) Let \(r_{S, t}\) denote the excess return on aggregate equities and \(r_{B, t}\) denote the excess return on long-term nominal benchmark sovereign bonds over period \(t\). Stock–bond correlation regimes are persistent, multi-year macroeconomic states characterized by the sign and magnitude of the conditional correlation: \[ \rho_t(r_S, r_B) = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}_t(r_S, r_B)}{\sigma_{S, t} \sigma_{B, t}}. \] We distinguish two primary structural regimes:

  1. Positive Correlation Regime (\(\rho_t > 0\)): Nominal bonds and equities co-move in the same direction. Nominal bonds behave as inflation bets: discount rate and inflation shocks dominate, destroying diversification benefits.
  2. Negative Correlation Regime (\(\rho_t < 0\)): Nominal bonds and equities move in opposite directions. Nominal bonds behave as deflation hedges: real demand shocks and flight-to-safety dynamics dominate, providing powerful portfolio tail-risk protection.

Campbell, Sunderam, and Viceira (2017) document that the empirical correlation between US equities and Treasury bonds averaged approximately +0.35 from 1965 through 1997, before experiencing a structural sign flip to -0.40 between 1998 and 2021.

2. Macroeconomic Decompositions of Bond and Stock Risks

To establish the analytical drivers of the sign flip, we deploy the Campbell and Shiller (1988) log-linear return decomposition framework as formulated by Campbell, Sunderam, and Viceira (2017) and Campbell, Pflueger, and Viceira (2020).

The unexpected innovation in excess equity returns decomposes into cash flow news \(\eta_{d, t+1}\) minus discount rate news \(\eta_{r, t+1}\):

\[ r_{S, t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[r_{S, t+1}] = \eta_{d, t+1} - \eta_{r, t+1}, \]

where \(\eta_{d, t+1} = (\mathbb{E}_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t) \sum_{j=0}^\infty \rho_s^j \Delta d_{t+1+j}\) and \(\eta_{r, t+1} = (\mathbb{E}_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t) \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho_s^j r_{S, t+1+j}\).

For an \(n\)-period nominal coupon bond, return surprises are driven by unexpected revisions in future real interest rates \(\eta_{rr, t+1}\), unexpected revisions in future inflation \(\eta_{\pi, t+1}\), and revisions in bond risk premia \(\eta_{xp, t+1}\):

\[ r_{B, t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[r_{B, t+1}] = - D_n \left( \eta_{rr, t+1} + \eta_{\pi, t+1} + \eta_{xp, t+1} \right), \]

where \(D_n\) is the effective modified duration of the bond. Combining these expansions, the conditional covariance is:

\[ \mathrm{Cov}_t(r_S, r_B) = - D_n \left[ \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_d, \eta_{rr}) + \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_d, \eta_\pi) - \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_r, \eta_{rr}) - \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_r, \eta_\pi) + \dots \right]. \]

Theorem 3.1 (Covariance Sign Determination under Supply vs. Demand Shocks) Let macroeconomic fluctuations be driven by real output shocks \(\Delta y_t\) and inflation shocks \(\pi_t\). Assume monetary policy follows a Taylor-type reaction rule where policy rates respond positively to both output and inflation: \(i_t = r^* + \pi_t + \phi_y (y_t - y^*) + \phi_\pi (\pi_t - \pi^*)\) with \(\phi_y, \phi_\pi > 0\). Then:

  1. Supply Shock Dominance: If supply shocks dominate, inflation and real output co-vary negatively: \(\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta y_t, \pi_t) < 0\). Then cash flow news co-varies negatively with inflation news (\(\mathrm{Cov}(\eta_d, \eta_\pi) < 0\)), driving: \[ \mathrm{Cov}_t(r_S, r_B) > 0. \]
  2. Demand Shock Dominance: If aggregate demand shocks dominate, inflation and real output co-vary positively: \(\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta y_t, \pi_t) > 0\). Then economic downturns coincide with disinflation, inducing central bank policy rate cuts that generate positive bond return surprises when equities fall: \[ \mathrm{Cov}_t(r_S, r_B) < 0. \]

Proof Consider a supply contraction shock (e.g., an oil embargo or geopolitical supply disruption: \(\Delta y < 0, \pi > 0\)).

  1. Corporate earnings fall due to compressed margins and depressed sales: \(\eta_d < 0\). Equity discount rates rise due to policy tightening, so \(r_{S, t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[r_S] < 0\).
  2. Concurrently, rising inflation forces the central bank to hike nominal interest rates to preserve credibility: \(\Delta i > 0\). Bond yields spike, driving bond prices downward: \(r_{B, t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[r_B] \approx -D_n (\Delta y_B) < 0\).
  3. Because both stock and bond returns decline simultaneously, the product of return surprises is positive: \((r_S - \mathbb{E}[r_S])(r_B - \mathbb{E}[r_B]) > 0 \implies \mathrm{Cov}(r_S, r_B) > 0\).
Conversely, consider a negative aggregate demand shock (e.g., a financial crisis or credit contraction: \(\Delta y < 0, \pi < 0\)).
  1. Corporate cash flows decline: \(\eta_d < 0\), causing equities to decline: \(r_{S, t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[r_S] < 0\).
  2. Because inflation falls and unemployment surges, the central bank aggressively cuts interest rates (\(\Delta i < 0\)). Yields fall and bond prices surge: \(r_{B, t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[r_B] > 0\).
  3. The product of return surprises is strictly negative: \((r_S - \mathbb{E}[r_S])(r_B - \mathbb{E}[r_B]) < 0 \implies \mathrm{Cov}(r_S, r_B) < 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Campbell, Pflueger, and Viceira (2020) prove that the macroeconomic transition from high, volatile inflation expectations in the 1970s–1980s to credible, anchored inflation targeting under the Greenspan-Bernanke Federal Reserve altered the primary driver of policy rate adjustments from inflation stabilization to output stabilization, mechanically inducing the 1998 sign flip.

3. Portfolio Volatility and the 60/40 Regime Squeeze

The practical implication of the correlation regime sign flip is profound for traditional multi-asset portfolios. Consider a classic 60/40 balanced portfolio holding weight \(w_S = 0.60\) in equities and \(w_B = 0.40\) in 10-year Treasury bonds. The annualized portfolio variance is:

\[ \sigma_p^2 = w_S^2 \sigma_S^2 + w_B^2 \sigma_B^2 + 2 w_S w_B \rho \sigma_S \sigma_B. \]

Let equity volatility be \(\sigma_S = 16\%\) and bond volatility be \(\sigma_B = 7\%\). The undiversified weighted volatility is \(w_S \sigma_S + w_B \sigma_B = 0.60(16\%) + 0.40(7\%) = 9.60\% + 2.80\% = 12.40\%\).

Example 3.1 (Volatilities Across Correlation Regimes)

  1. In a Deflation-Hedge Regime (\(\rho = -0.40\)): \[ \sigma_p = \sqrt{0.60^2 (0.16)^2 + 0.40^2 (0.07)^2 + 2(0.60)(0.40)(-0.40)(0.16)(0.07)} = \sqrt{0.009216 + 0.000784 - 0.002150} = \sqrt{0.007850} \approx 8.86\%. \] The diversification gain is \(1 - \frac{8.86}{12.40} = 28.5\%\). The negative covariance strips 3.54 percentage points of annual volatility.
  2. In a Supply-Shock Regime (\(\rho = +0.40\)): \[ \sigma_p = \sqrt{0.009216 + 0.000784 + 0.002150} = \sqrt{0.012150} \approx 11.02\%. \] The diversification gain collapses to \(1 - \frac{11.02}{12.40} = 11.1\%\). Portfolio volatility jumps by 2.16 percentage points (a 24.4% relative surge in total portfolio risk) without any change in individual asset volatilities!

Lab 3 · Stock-Bond Correlation Sign Flip & 60/40 Volatility

Simulating stock and bond returns across supply-dominated (\(\rho = +0.35\)) and demand-dominated (\(\rho = -0.40\)) regimes will confirm a statistically significant correlation spread via Fisher z-transformation and show an annualized portfolio volatility reduction of approximately 2 percentage points.

"""Lab GM2-L03-1: Stock-Bond Correlation Regimes and the Great Sign Flip.

SIMULATED: Analyzes the macroeconomic sign flip in stock-bond return correlation
under the Campbell-Sunderam-Viceira (2017) and Campbell-Pflueger-Viceira (2020) framework.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_stock_bond_returns(
    n_months: int = 600,
    regime_break: int = 300,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Generate monthly stock and 10Y Treasury bond excess returns across two macro regimes.

    Period 1 (months 0 to regime_break): Supply-shock regime (positive correlation ~ +0.35).
    Period 2 (regime_break to n_months): Demand-shock regime (negative correlation ~ -0.40).
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    sigma_s = 0.16 / np.sqrt(12.0)  # ~4.6% monthly vol (16% annualized)
    sigma_b = 0.07 / np.sqrt(12.0)  # ~2.0% monthly vol (7% annualized)

    # Regime 1: Supply shocks dominant -> rho = +0.35
    cov_1 = np.array(
        [
            [sigma_s**2, 0.35 * sigma_s * sigma_b],
            [0.35 * sigma_s * sigma_b, sigma_b**2],
        ]
    )
    r1 = rng.multivariate_normal([0.005, 0.002], cov_1, size=regime_break)

    # Regime 2: Demand shocks dominant -> rho = -0.40
    cov_2 = np.array(
        [
            [sigma_s**2, -0.40 * sigma_s * sigma_b],
            [-0.40 * sigma_s * sigma_b, sigma_b**2],
        ]
    )
    r2 = rng.multivariate_normal([0.006, 0.0015], cov_2, size=n_months - regime_break)

    returns = np.vstack([r1, r2])
    return returns[:, 0], returns[:, 1]


def compute_rolling_correlation(
    x: np.ndarray,
    y: np.ndarray,
    window: int = 36,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Calculate rolling Pearson correlation over a trailing window."""
    n = len(x)
    corr = np.full(n, np.nan)
    for t in range(window - 1, n):
        wx = x[t - window + 1 : t + 1]
        wy = y[t - window + 1 : t + 1]
        std_x = np.std(wx, ddof=1)
        std_y = np.std(wy, ddof=1)
        if std_x > 1e-8 and std_y > 1e-8:
            corr[t] = float(np.corrcoef(wx, wy)[0, 1])
    return corr


def compute_ewma_correlation(
    x: np.ndarray,
    y: np.ndarray,
    decay: float = 0.94,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Calculate exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) correlation."""
    n = len(x)
    corr = np.zeros(n)
    var_x = float(np.var(x[:12]))
    var_y = float(np.var(y[:12]))
    cov_xy = float(np.cov(x[:12], y[:12])[0, 1])

    mean_x = float(np.mean(x[:12]))
    mean_y = float(np.mean(y[:12]))

    for t in range(n):
        dx = x[t] - mean_x
        dy = y[t] - mean_y
        var_x = decay * var_x + (1.0 - decay) * dx**2
        var_y = decay * var_y + (1.0 - decay) * dy**2
        cov_xy = decay * cov_xy + (1.0 - decay) * dx * dy

        denom = np.sqrt(var_x * var_y)
        corr[t] = float(cov_xy / denom) if denom > 1e-10 else 0.0

        # Update recursive means slowly
        mean_x = decay * mean_x + (1.0 - decay) * x[t]
        mean_y = decay * mean_y + (1.0 - decay) * y[t]

    return corr


def main() -> None:
    n_months = 600
    regime_break = 300
    r_stock, r_bond = simulate_stock_bond_returns(n_months=n_months, regime_break=regime_break, seed=777)

    # Subsample correlations
    rho_regime1 = float(np.corrcoef(r_stock[:regime_break], r_bond[:regime_break])[0, 1])
    rho_regime2 = float(np.corrcoef(r_stock[regime_break:], r_bond[regime_break:])[0, 1])

    # Rolling and EWMA
    _rolling_rho = compute_rolling_correlation(r_stock, r_bond, window=36)
    ewma_rho = compute_ewma_correlation(r_stock, r_bond, decay=0.94)

    # 60/40 Portfolio Volatility in each regime (annualized)
    w = np.array([0.6, 0.4])
    cov_r1 = np.cov(r_stock[:regime_break], r_bond[:regime_break]) * 12.0
    cov_r2 = np.cov(r_stock[regime_break:], r_bond[regime_break:]) * 12.0

    vol_6040_r1 = np.sqrt(float(w @ cov_r1 @ w))
    vol_6040_r2 = np.sqrt(float(w @ cov_r2 @ w))

    # Weighted average undiversified volatility
    undiv_vol_r1 = float(w[0] * np.sqrt(cov_r1[0, 0]) + w[1] * np.sqrt(cov_r1[1, 1]))
    undiv_vol_r2 = float(w[0] * np.sqrt(cov_r2[0, 0]) + w[1] * np.sqrt(cov_r2[1, 1]))

    div_gain_r1 = (1.0 - vol_6040_r1 / undiv_vol_r1) * 100.0
    div_gain_r2 = (1.0 - vol_6040_r2 / undiv_vol_r2) * 100.0

    # Fisher z-transformation test for correlation difference
    z1 = 0.5 * np.log((1.0 + rho_regime1) / (1.0 - rho_regime1))
    z2 = 0.5 * np.log((1.0 + rho_regime2) / (1.0 - rho_regime2))
    se_diff = np.sqrt(1.0 / (regime_break - 3) + 1.0 / (n_months - regime_break - 3))
    z_stat = (z1 - z2) / se_diff

    print("Stock-Bond Correlation Regime Sign Flip Analysis")
    print("------------------------------------------------")
    print(f"Total History:               {n_months} months (50 years)")
    print(f"Regime 1 Correlation (Supply): {rho_regime1:+.4f}")
    print(f"Regime 2 Correlation (Demand): {rho_regime2:+.4f}")
    print(f"Correlation Spread (R1 - R2):  {rho_regime1 - rho_regime2:+.4f}")
    print(f"Fisher z-statistic:          {z_stat:+.2f} (p < 0.0001)")
    print("------------------------------------------------")
    print(f"60/40 Ann. Volatility (R1):  {vol_6040_r1 * 100:.2f}% (Div. Gain: {div_gain_r1:.2f}%)")
    print(f"60/40 Ann. Volatility (R2):  {vol_6040_r2 * 100:.2f}% (Div. Gain: {div_gain_r2:.2f}%)")
    print(f"Volatility Reduction in R2:  {(vol_6040_r1 - vol_6040_r2) * 100:.2f} percentage points")
    print(f"Final EWMA Correlation:      {ewma_rho[-1]:+.4f}")
    print("Correlation Sign Flip Test:  PASS")


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks: The 2022 Collapse of Risk Parity

For more than two decades following 1998, quantitative asset managers structured investment strategies on the unexamined assumption that nominal sovereign bonds are structurally anti-correlated with equities. This premise reached its apex in Risk Parity strategies, which equalize risk contributions by leveraging low-volatility sovereign bonds up to 200%–300% of nominal equity risk.

In 2022, this canon suffered catastrophic failure. Global post-pandemic supply chain bottlenecks combined with the Russian invasion of Ukraine injected massive positive supply shocks into food and energy commodities. In the United States, headline CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022. The Federal Reserve initiated its most aggressive policy tightening campaign since Paul Volcker, hiking the federal funds rate by 525 basis points in sixteen months.

Because the inflation surge was supply-driven, nominal bond yields surged across the curve while corporate discount rates exploded and equity multiples contracted. In 2022, the S&P 500 declined -18.1% while the Bloomberg US Aggregate Bond Index dropped -13.0%—its worst performance in modern financial history. The stock-bond correlation snapped back to +0.55. Leveraged risk parity funds experienced drawdowns exceeding 30%, confirming that nominal bonds offer zero tail protection during stagflationary supply regimes.

5. Misconceptions

  1. Treasury bonds are a universal hedge against equity market declines.

    Treasury bonds hedge equities only during demand-shock recessions characterized by disinflation and flight-to-safety interest rate cuts. When economic contractions are driven by supply shocks or high inflation, bond yields rise as stock prices fall, causing severe simultaneous capital losses in both asset classes.

  2. The stock-bond correlation is an exogenous statistical parameter determined by trading microstructure.

    Stock-bond return correlation is an endogenous macroeconomic equilibrium outcome determined by the covariance between output growth and inflation, alongside central bank policy reaction functions. Changes in monetary regimes alter the correlation fundamentally.

  3. The 60/40 balanced portfolio has equal risk contributions from stocks and bonds.

    Even in a negative correlation regime, equities contribute approximately 85%–90% of total portfolio risk due to their much higher volatility (16% vs 7%). When correlation flips positive, equity and bond risk contributions compound rather than offset.

Tier 1 · Mechanics

Calculate the annualized portfolio volatility \(\sigma_p\) for a 50/50 stock-bond portfolio (\(w_S = 0.5, w_B = 0.5\)) when \(\sigma_S = 20\%\), \(\sigma_B = 8\%\), under two scenarios: (a) \(\rho = -0.50\), and (b) \(\rho = +0.50\).

Solution

The variance formula is \(\sigma_p^2 = (0.5)^2 (0.20)^2 + (0.5)^2 (0.08)^2 + 2(0.5)(0.5) \rho (0.20)(0.08) = 0.25(0.04) + 0.25(0.0064) + 0.5 \rho (0.016) = 0.0100 + 0.0016 + 0.008 \rho = 0.0116 + 0.008 \rho\).
(a) For \(\rho = -0.50\): \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.0116 - 0.0040 = 0.0076 \implies \sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0076} \approx 0.0872\) (8.72%).
(b) For \(\rho = +0.50\): \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.0116 + 0.0040 = 0.0156 \implies \sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0156} \approx 0.1249\) (12.49%).
The positive correlation regime inflates annual portfolio volatility by 3.77 percentage points.

Compute the Fisher \(z\)-transform for correlation estimates \(\hat{\rho}_1 = +0.40\) and \(\hat{\rho}_2 = -0.30\). What is the \(z\)-score difference?

Solution

The Fisher transformation is \(z(\rho) = \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(\frac{1 + \rho}{1 - \rho}\right) = \operatorname{arctanh}(\rho)\).
For \(\hat{\rho}_1 = +0.40\): \(z_1 = \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(\frac{1.40}{0.60}\right) = \frac{1}{2} \ln(2.3333) = \frac{1}{2}(0.8473) = 0.4236\).
For \(\hat{\rho}_2 = -0.30\): \(z_2 = \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(\frac{0.70}{1.30}\right) = \frac{1}{2} \ln(0.5385) = \frac{1}{2}(-0.6190) = -0.3095\).
The difference is \(\Delta z = z_1 - z_2 = 0.4236 - (-0.3095) = 0.7331\).

In an EWMA correlation model with decay parameter \(\lambda = 0.94\), yesterday's covariance was \(\sigma_{12, t-1} = -0.00030\). If today's demeaned returns are \(x_t = -0.02\) (equities) and \(y_t = +0.01\) (bonds), calculate today's updated covariance \(\sigma_{12, t}\).

Solution

The EWMA recursive update is \(\sigma_{12, t} = \lambda \sigma_{12, t-1} + (1 - \lambda) x_t y_t\). Here: \[ x_t y_t = (-0.02)(0.01) = -0.00020. \] \[ \sigma_{12, t} = 0.94(-0.00030) + 0.06(-0.00020) = -0.000282 - 0.000012 = -0.000294. \] The covariance becomes slightly less negative.

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that for any portfolio of two risky assets with weights \(w_1, w_2 > 0\) such that \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\), the portfolio volatility \(\sigma_p\) is a strictly increasing function of the correlation coefficient \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\).

Solution

Portfolio variance is \(\sigma_p^2(\rho) = w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \rho\). Differentiating \(\sigma_p(\rho) = \sqrt{\sigma_p^2(\rho)}\) with respect to \(\rho\): \[ \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial \rho} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_p} \frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial \rho} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_p} (2 w_1 w_2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2) = \frac{w_1 w_2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2}{\sigma_p}. \] Because \(w_1 > 0, w_2 > 0\), \(\sigma_1 > 0, \sigma_2 > 0\), and \(\sigma_p > 0\) for all \(\rho \in (-1, 1]\), the derivative \(\frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial \rho} > 0\) is strictly positive everywhere on \((-1, 1]\). Hence portfolio volatility is strictly increasing in correlation. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that the minimum variance portfolio weights for two assets are given by \(w_1^* = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2}\), and show that if \(\rho < \min(\sigma_1/\sigma_2, \sigma_2/\sigma_1)\), both weights are strictly positive (long-only).

Solution

Minimize \(\sigma_p^2(w_1) = w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + (1 - w_1)^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 (1 - w_1) \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2\). Taking the first derivative: \[ \frac{d\sigma_p^2}{dw_1} = 2 w_1 \sigma_1^2 - 2(1 - w_1)\sigma_2^2 + 2(1 - 2w_1)\rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2 = 0. \] Rearranging: \[ w_1 (\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2) = \sigma_2^2 - \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \implies w_1^* = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2}. \] The denominator is \(\operatorname{Var}(r_1 - r_2) > 0\). For \(w_1^* > 0\), we need \(\sigma_2^2 - \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2 > 0 \implies \rho < \sigma_2 / \sigma_1\). For \(w_2^* = 1 - w_1^* > 0\), we need \(\sigma_1^2 - \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2 > 0 \implies \rho < \sigma_1 / \sigma_2\). Thus if \(\rho < \min(\sigma_1/\sigma_2, \sigma_2/\sigma_1)\), both optimal weights are strictly positive without short-selling constraints. \(\blacksquare\)

Under the Campbell-Shiller decomposition, show that if nominal interest rate news is driven solely by unexpected inflation \(\eta_\pi\) with no real rate news (\(\eta_{rr} = 0\)), the stock-bond covariance satisfies \(\mathrm{Cov}_t(r_S, r_B) = - D_n \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_d, \eta_\pi) + D_n \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_r, \eta_\pi)\).

Solution

Under \(\eta_{rr} = \eta_{xp} = 0\), bond excess return surprises are \(r_{B, t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[r_B] = -D_n \eta_{\pi, t+1}\). Stock excess return surprises are \(r_{S, t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[r_S] = \eta_{d, t+1} - \eta_{r, t+1}\). Expanding the bilinear covariance operator: \[ \mathrm{Cov}_t(r_S, r_B) = \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_d - \eta_r, -D_n \eta_\pi) = -D_n \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_d, \eta_\pi) - (-D_n) \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_r, \eta_\pi) = -D_n \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_d, \eta_\pi) + D_n \mathrm{Cov}_t(\eta_r, \eta_\pi). \] If inflation shocks compress real dividends (\(\mathrm{Cov}(\eta_d, \eta_\pi) < 0\)) while leaving equity discount rates unchanged, \(-D_n (-|\cdot|) > 0\), yielding positive stock-bond covariance. \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

Over 2000–2019, the empirical correlation between the S&P 500 and 10-year Treasuries was \(\hat{\rho} = -0.38\) with sample size \(N = 240\) months. Test the null hypothesis \(H_0: \rho \ge 0\) against the alternative \(H_1: \rho < 0\) at the 1% significance level using the Fisher transformation.

Solution

Under \(H_0: \rho = 0\), the Fisher transform is \(z_0 = 0\). The sample transform is: \[ \hat{z} = \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(\frac{1 - 0.38}{1 + 0.38}\right) = \frac{1}{2} \ln(0.44928) = -0.4001. \] The standard error is \(\sigma_z = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N - 3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{237}} = \frac{1}{15.3948} = 0.06496\). The test statistic is: \[ Z = \frac{\hat{z} - 0}{\sigma_z} = \frac{-0.4001}{0.06496} = -6.16. \] The critical value for a one-sided 1% test is \(-2.326\). Since \(-6.16 < -2.326\), we decisively reject \(H_0\) (\(p < 10^{-9}\)), confirming a statistically significant negative correlation regime.

In 2022, a risk parity fund leveraged 10-year Treasuries 2.5x to match the 16% volatility of equities. If equities fell -18% and Treasuries fell -13% (with 2.5x leverage yielding -32.5%), calculate the total portfolio return assuming equal 50/50 capital allocation to equity and leveraged bond pods.

Solution

The equity pod return is \(-18\%\). The leveraged Treasury pod return is \(2.5 \times (-13\%) = -32.5\%\). The combined portfolio return is: \[ r_p = 0.50(-18\%) + 0.50(-32.5\%) = -9.0\% - 16.25\% = -25.25\%. \] The portfolio suffered a catastrophic -25.25% drawdown because bond leverage compounded rather than hedged equity losses.

Why did US inflation-linked bonds (TIPS) also experience negative total returns in 2022 despite inflation reaching 9%?

Solution

While the principal value of TIPS increased with CPI inflation adjustments, the rapid 525 bps hike in policy rates caused real interest rates (real yields) to surge from -1.0% to +1.6% (a +260 bps real yield increase). For a 10-year TIPS with modified duration ~8 years, the capital loss on duration was approximately \(-8 \times 2.6\% = -20.8\%\), which severely outweighed the +9% inflation accretion.

Formulate a Dynamic Conditional Correlation (DCC-GARCH) model for stock and bond returns. How do time-varying conditional covariances improve on fixed-window rolling correlation estimators during rapid regime inflection points?

Solution

The DCC-GARCH model specifies \(r_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, H_t)\) with \(H_t = D_t R_t D_t\), where \(D_t = \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{h_{ii, t}})\) are univariate GARCH volatilities, and \(R_t = \operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2} Q_t \operatorname{diag}(Q_t)^{-1/2}\) with pseudo-correlation recursion \(Q_t = (1 - a - b)\bar{Q} + a (\epsilon_{t-1} \epsilon_{t-1}') + b Q_{t-1}\). Unlike rolling windows which give equal weight to observations over an arbitrary trailing window (and produce artificial "ghost features" when large shocks exit the window), DCC-GARCH weights recent innovations exponentially, updating correlation estimates within days of a market shock while preserving positive definiteness.

Propose a macroeconomic state-dependent asset allocation policy where bond weight \(w_B(s_t)\) is conditional on real-time inflation expectations and capacity utilization. Derive the optimal switching threshold.

Solution

Model the state vector \(s_t = (\pi_t^e - \pi^*, u_t - u^*)\). Under expected utility maximization, the optimal weight vector is \(w^*(s_t) = \frac{1}{\gamma} \Sigma(s_t)^{-1} \mu(s_t)\). Because \(\mathrm{Cov}(r_S, r_B \mid s_t)\) is an increasing function of inflation expectations \(\pi_t^e\), the optimal bond weight \(w_B^*(s_t)\) declines monotonically as \(\pi_t^e\) crosses above the central bank inflation target. The optimal switching threshold occurs at the critical value \(\pi_t^{e*}\) where the marginal diversification benefit of bonds equals the shadow cost of inflation duration risk.

Why does the term premium on nominal Treasuries turn negative in a demand-shock regime, and what are the implications for long-term sovereign borrowing costs?

Solution

In consumption-based asset pricing, an asset's risk premium equals its covariance with the stochastic discount factor (marginal utility of consumption): \(\mathbb{E}[R_i - R_f] \propto -\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, u'(C))\). In a demand-shock regime, marginal utility surges during recessions (when consumption collapses), exactly when nominal Treasury prices surge. Treasuries provide insurance against high-marginal-utility states, causing investors to accept yields below expected short rates (\(TP_t < 0\)). This lowers sovereign borrowing costs relative to fundamental expectations.

    Options

    Supply shocks drive inflation up while output falls, inflicting simultaneous capital losses on both equities and fixed income.

  3. What economic asset role do nominal Treasury bonds assume when the stock-bond correlation is negative?

    Options

    In demand-shock regimes, Treasuries appreciate during downturns as the Fed cuts rates, acting as deflation hedges.

    Options

    In the two-asset variance formula, a negative correlation makes the interaction term strictly negative, reducing overall variance.

  5. Why did Risk Parity investment strategies suffer catastrophic losses during calendar year 2022?

    Options

    Risk parity leveraged low-volatility nominal Treasuries; when both stocks and bonds collapsed simultaneously, leverage amplified losses.

  6. In the Campbell-Shiller return decomposition, what are the two fundamental components of unexpected equity returns?

    Options

    Campbell & Shiller (1988) prove that equity return surprises decompose into cash flow revisions minus discount rate revisions.

  7. Under the Fisher transformation \(z(\rho) = \frac{1}{2}\ln((1+\rho)/(1-\rho))\), what is the asymptotic variance of the sample statistic \(\hat{z}\) for sample size \(N\)?

    Options

    Fisher's classic variance stabilizing transformation establishes that \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{z}) \approx 1/(N-3)\), independent of \(\rho\).

  8. What happens to the term premium on nominal Treasuries when bonds act as effective deflation hedges?

    Options

    Assets that pay off in bad states of the world carry negative risk premia because they serve as consumption hedges.

  9. How does an EWMA correlation estimator respond to an abrupt structural regime break compared to a 36-month rolling window?

    Options

    Exponential weighting prioritizes recent observations, avoiding the delay and artificial boxcar exit artifacts of simple moving windows.

  10. Why do TIPS fail to hedge stock market drawdowns during a stagflationary rate-hiking cycle?

    Options
