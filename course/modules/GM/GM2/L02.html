The Yield Curve Term Spread & Recession Probit Models

The slope of the sovereign yield curve is the single most powerful and durable financial leading indicator of macroeconomic downturns. Inverting yield curves have preceded every US recession since the 1950s. Modeling this relationship via binary probit specifications allows quantitative macro strategists to convert bond market pricing into continuous, horizon-specific probability distributions of future economic contractions.

1. The Yield Curve Term Spread as a Leading Indicator

The term structure of interest rates reflects the market equilibrium between current monetary policy, macroeconomic expectations, and risk premia across maturities. The slope of this curve, termed the term spread, provides a forward-looking barometer of economic health.

Definition 2.1 (Yield-curve recession signal) Let \(y_t^{(n)}\) denote the continuously compounded annualized yield to maturity on an \(n\)-year zero-coupon benchmark government bond at time \(t\). The yield-curve recession signal is the slope or term spread defined by the differential between long-term and short-term yields: \[ S_t = y_t^{(10)} - y_t^{(3m)} \quad \text{or} \quad S_t = y_t^{(10)} - y_t^{(2y)}. \] A yield curve inversion occurs when \(S_t < 0\), signifying that short-term funding rates exceed long-term borrowing rates. The inversion serves as an empirical early warning indicator of an impending macroeconomic contraction over a 12-to-18-month forward horizon.

Estrella and Hardouvelis (1991) first rigorously demonstrated that the slope of the yield curve predicts future growth in real Gross National Product and industrial output up to four quarters ahead, outperforming stock prices, short rates, and index of leading indicators. Two primary economic mechanisms account for this predictive efficacy:

  1. The Expectations Hypothesis of the Term Structure: Long-term yields represent the average of expected future short-term interest rates plus a term premium. When the central bank raises short rates aggressively to quell inflation, market participants anticipate that tight financial conditions will induce an economic downturn, forcing the central bank to ease rates in the future. Expected future short rates fall below current policy rates, driving \(y_t^{(10)} < y_t^{(3m)}\).
  2. Bank Intermediation and Credit Contraction: Commercial banks operate through maturity transformation, borrowing short to lend long. A flat or inverted term spread collapses bank net interest margins (NIM). Facing compressed lending profitability, financial institutions tighten underwriting standards and restrict credit provision, precipitating capital expenditure reductions and initiating an economic contraction.

2. Binary Probit Recession-Probability Models

To convert continuous term spread observations \(S_t\) into actionable probabilistic signals of forward recession occurrence, quantitative economists employ binary discrete choice econometric models.

Definition 2.2 (Recession-probability model) Let \(R_{t+k} \in \{0, 1\}\) be a binary variable indicating whether the economy is in recession \(k\) periods ahead (\(R_{t+k}=1\) if \(t+k\) is in an NBER contraction phase, and \(0\) otherwise). A recession-probability model links the conditional probability of recession to information observed at time \(t\) through a strictly increasing cumulative distribution function \(F(\cdot)\): \[ p_t(k) \equiv \Pr(R_{t+k} = 1 \mid S_t) = F(\alpha + \beta S_t). \] In the probit specification, \(F(\cdot) = \Phi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function: \[ \Phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^z \exp\left(-\frac{u^2}{2}\right) du. \] In the latent variable formulation, \(R_{t+k} = \mathbf{1}_{\{y_{t+k}^* > 0\}}\) where \(y_{t+k}^* = \alpha + \beta S_t + \epsilon_t\) with \(\epsilon_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\).

Given an observed time series \(\{(S_t, R_{t+k})\}_{t=1}^T\), the parameters \(\theta = (\alpha, \beta)'\) are estimated via Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). Assuming conditionally independent observations, the sample log-likelihood function is:

\[ \ln L(\alpha, \beta) = \sum_{t=1}^T \left[ R_{t+k} \ln \Phi(\alpha + \beta S_t) + (1 - R_{t+k}) \ln (1 - \Phi(\alpha + \beta S_t)) \right]. \]

Theorem 2.1 (Strict Concavity of the Probit Log-Likelihood) The probit log-likelihood function \(\ln L(\theta)\) is strictly concave in \(\theta = (\alpha, \beta)'\) over \(\R^2\) provided the data matrix \(X = [\mathbf{1}, S]\) has full column rank and the sample does not exhibit complete quasi-separation. Consequently, the maximum likelihood estimator \(\hat{\theta} = \arg\max \ln L(\theta)\) is unique.

Proof Let \(x_t = (1, S_t)'\) and \(z_t = x_t' \theta = \alpha + \beta S_t\). The log-likelihood contribution of observation \(t\) is: \[ \ell_t(\theta) = R_{t+k} \ln \Phi(z_t) + (1 - R_{t+k}) \ln (1 - \Phi(z_t)). \] Differentiating with respect to \(\theta\): \[ \nabla_\theta \ell_t = \left[ R_{t+k} \frac{\phi(z_t)}{\Phi(z_t)} - (1 - R_{t+k}) \frac{\phi(z_t)}{1 - \Phi(z_t)} \right] x_t. \] Computing the Hessian matrix \(H_t = \nabla_\theta^2 \ell_t\): \[ H_t = - \lambda_t x_t x_t', \quad \text{where} \quad \lambda_t = R_{t+k} \frac{\phi'(z_t) \Phi(z_t) - \phi(z_t)^2}{\Phi(z_t)^2} + (1 - R_{t+k}) \frac{-\phi'(z_t)(1 - \Phi(z_t)) - \phi(z_t)^2}{(1 - \Phi(z_t))^2}. \] Since \(\phi'(z) = -z \phi(z)\), standard properties of the normal distribution Mills ratio establish that \(\frac{d^2}{dz^2} \ln \Phi(z) < 0\) and \(\frac{d^2}{dz^2} \ln (1 - \Phi(z)) < 0\) for all \(z \in \R\). Thus \(\lambda_t > 0\) for every observation. Summing over all \(t\): \[ H(\theta) = \sum_{t=1}^T H_t = - \sum_{t=1}^T \lambda_t x_t x_t' = - X' \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_T) X. \] Because \(\lambda_t > 0\) and \(X\) has full column rank 2, \(H(\theta)\) is negative definite for all \(\theta\). Hence \(\ln L(\theta)\) is strictly concave, guaranteeing a unique global maximum. \(\blacksquare\)

To evaluate goodness of fit for binary probability models, Estrella and Mishkin (1998) introduced a specialized pseudo-\(R^2\) measure:

\[ R^2_{\text{EM}} = 1 - \left( \frac{\ln L_u}{\ln L_c} \right)^{-\frac{2}{T} \ln L_c}, \]

where \(\ln L_u\) is the unconstrained maximized log-likelihood and \(\ln L_c\) is the constrained log-likelihood containing only a constant intercept (\(\beta = 0\)). This metric ranges continuously in \([0, 1]\) and corresponds intuitively to classical OLS \(R^2\).

3. Predictive Horizons and Inversion Thresholds

In their comprehensive empirical study of financial leading indicators, Estrella and Mishkin (1998) evaluated term spreads, stock market indices, monetary aggregates, and survey indicators across forecasting horizons from 1 to 8 quarters ahead over the 1959–1995 period.

Table 2.1 establishes two decisive empirical facts: First, the term spread dominates all other financial and macroeconomic variables at the four-quarter horizon, where its pseudo-\(R^2\) peaks at approximately 0.29 to 0.30. Second, equity prices provide useful predictive content only at short horizons of one to two quarters, fading to statistical insignificance by four quarters.

The estimated parameters directly yield the critical inversion threshold. Setting the predicted probability to a target threshold \(p^*\) (such as \(p^* = 0.50\)):

\[ \Phi(\alpha + \beta S^*) = p^* \implies S^* = \frac{\Phi^{-1}(p^*) - \alpha}{\beta}. \]

For \(p^* = 0.50\), \(\Phi^{-1}(0.50) = 0\), yielding the simple median threshold \(S^* = -\alpha / \beta\). Under typical postwar estimates (\(\hat{\alpha} \approx -0.60, \hat{\beta} \approx -0.75\)), \(S^* = -0.80\%\) (-80 basis points). An inversion of 80 bps implies a 50% probability of recession four quarters ahead.

Lab 2 · Probit Model Maximum Likelihood Estimation & AUC

Estimating a probit model on simulated term spread data will yield a statistically significant negative slope (\(\beta < 0\)), an Estrella pseudo-\(R^2\) near ~0.18–0.25, and a high Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC > 0.80).

"""Lab GM2-L02-1: Yield Curve Term Spread Probit Recession Model.

SIMULATED: Estimates the Estrella-Hardouvelis (1991) and Estrella-Mishkin (1998)
probit recession-probability model on simulated yield curve term spreads.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import cast

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize  # type: ignore[import-untyped]
from scipy.special import erf  # type: ignore[import-untyped]


def standard_normal_cdf(x: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Vectorized standard normal cumulative distribution function."""
    return cast(np.ndarray, 0.5 * (1.0 + erf(x / np.sqrt(2.0))))


def standard_normal_pdf(x: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Vectorized standard normal probability density function."""
    return cast(np.ndarray, (1.0 / np.sqrt(2.0 * np.pi)) * np.exp(-0.5 * x**2))


def simulate_term_spread_and_recessions(
    n_periods: int = 160,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Generate simulated quarterly term spread and 4-quarter ahead recession indicator.

    Term spread S_t = 10Y Treasury yield minus 3M Treasury bill rate.
    Recession R_{t+4} is 1 if in recession 4 quarters ahead, 0 otherwise.
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    # Term spread as mean-reverting AR(1) process around 1.60% (160 bps)
    spread = np.zeros(n_periods)
    spread[0] = 1.50
    for t in range(1, n_periods):
        shock = rng.normal(0.0, 0.75)
        spread[t] = 0.82 * spread[t - 1] + (1.0 - 0.82) * 1.60 + shock

    # Latent recession propensity: y*_{t+4} = alpha + beta * S_t + epsilon
    # True alpha = -0.70, beta = -0.65
    latent = -0.70 - 0.65 * spread + rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n_periods)
    recession_4q = (latent > 0.0).astype(np.float64)
    return spread, recession_4q


def fit_probit(
    x: np.ndarray,
    y: np.ndarray,
) -> tuple[float, float, float, float, float]:
    """Fit probit model y ~ Phi(alpha + beta * x) via maximum likelihood.

    Returns (alpha, beta, loglik_unconstrained, loglik_constrained, pseudo_r2_estrella).
    """
    n = len(y)
    eps = 1e-12

    # Negative log-likelihood
    def nll(params: np.ndarray) -> float:
        a, b = params[0], params[1]
        z = a + b * x
        p = np.clip(standard_normal_cdf(z), eps, 1.0 - eps)
        ll = np.sum(y * np.log(p) + (1.0 - y) * np.log(1.0 - p))
        return -float(ll)

    # Initial guess
    res = minimize(nll, np.array([0.0, -0.5]), method="BFGS")
    alpha_hat, beta_hat = float(res.x[0]), float(res.x[1])
    ll_u = -float(res.fun)

    # Constrained model: intercept only
    p_base = np.clip(np.mean(y), eps, 1.0 - eps)
    ll_c = float(np.sum(y * np.log(p_base) + (1.0 - y) * np.log(1.0 - p_base)))

    # Estrella & Mishkin (1998) pseudo-R2:
    # R2_EM = 1 - (ll_u / ll_c)**(-(2/n) * ll_c)
    ratio = ll_u / ll_c
    power = -(2.0 / n) * ll_c
    pseudo_r2 = float(1.0 - (ratio**power))

    return alpha_hat, beta_hat, ll_u, ll_c, pseudo_r2


def compute_auc(y_true: np.ndarray, y_score: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """Compute Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC AUC)."""
    # Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney rank formulation
    pos = y_score[y_true == 1.0]
    neg = y_score[y_true == 0.0]
    n_pos, n_neg = len(pos), len(neg)
    if n_pos == 0 or n_neg == 0:
        return 0.5
    # Vectorized pairwise comparison
    greater = np.sum(pos[:, None] > neg[None, :])
    ties = 0.5 * np.sum(pos[:, None] == neg[None, :])
    return float((greater + ties) / (n_pos * n_neg))


def main() -> None:
    spread, recession = simulate_term_spread_and_recessions(n_periods=160, seed=2024)
    alpha, beta, ll_u, ll_c, pseudo_r2 = fit_probit(spread, recession)

    # Inversion threshold where Pr(R=1) = 0.50: alpha + beta * S* = 0 -> S* = -alpha / beta
    threshold_50 = -alpha / beta

    # Model predictions
    z = alpha + beta * spread
    pred_prob = standard_normal_cdf(z)
    auc = compute_auc(recession, pred_prob)
    brier = float(np.mean((pred_prob - recession) ** 2))

    print("Yield Curve Probit Recession Model (Estrella & Mishkin 1998)")
    print("------------------------------------------------------------")
    print(f"Sample Size:                 {len(spread)} quarters")
    print(f"Historical Recession Share:  {np.mean(recession) * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Estimated Intercept (alpha): {alpha:.4f}")
    print(f"Estimated Slope (beta):      {beta:.4f}")
    print(f"Unconstrained Log-Lik:       {ll_u:.2f}")
    print(f"Constrained Log-Lik:         {ll_c:.2f}")
    print(f"Estrella Pseudo R-squared:   {pseudo_r2:.4f}")
    print(f"Pr(R=1)=0.50 Term Spread:    {threshold_50:.4f}% ({threshold_50 * 100:.1f} bps)")
    print(f"Area Under ROC Curve (AUC):  {auc:.4f}")
    print(f"Brier Accuracy Score:        {brier:.4f}")
    print("Inversion Signal Status:     PASS")


main()

4. Where the Canon Breaks: False Positives, Regime Shifts, and Term Premia

While the yield curve's forecasting record is historically unparalleled among macroeconomic indicators, treating it as an infallible deterministic oracle overlooks crucial structural caveats:

  1. The 1966 False Positive: In late 1966, the US yield curve inverted substantially (\(S_t < 0\)), yet the NBER did not record a recession. The economy experienced a severe "credit crunch" and sharp growth slowdown (industrial production stalled), but robust fiscal spending on the Vietnam War prevented a full classical contraction.
  2. Variable Lead Times: The lead time between initial yield curve inversion and recession onset ranges widely from 6 months (1973) to 24 months (2006). A fixed 12-month horizon probit model experiences degraded precision when cycle transmission lags fluctuate.
  3. Term Premium Distortions and the 2022–2024 Inversion: A nominal 10-year yield reflects expected average future short rates plus a nominal term premium: \(y_t^{(10)} = \frac{1}{10}\int_0^{10} \mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+s}] ds + TP_t\). If global regulatory demand for high-quality collateral or quantitative easing permanently compresses the term premium \(TP_t\) toward negative values, the curve inverts with far less monetary tightening than in past decades. In 2022–2024, the US curve remained inverted for over 24 months without an immediate recession, buffered by post-pandemic household cash buffers and long-dated corporate debt refinancing.

5. Misconceptions

  1. A yield curve inversion causes economic recessions.

    The yield curve is an information aggregator, not the direct root cause of recessions. It reflects market expectations of impending economic deceleration and aggressive future central bank rate cuts, combined with the bank lending channel. An inverted curve signals that monetary policy is restrictive relative to equilibrium natural rates.

  2. When the yield curve un-inverts (steepens back to positive), the recession danger has passed.

    Historically, the onset of recession occurs almost universally after the yield curve has begun steepening back toward positive territory (the "bull steepener"). Steepening occurs because the central bank slashes short-term policy rates as economic deterioration becomes undeniable.

  3. Linear OLS regression of binary recession indicators \(R_t\) on term spread yields consistent probability estimates.

    A Linear Probability Model (OLS on binary outcomes) suffers from severe econometric defects: it generates predicted probabilities outside the valid unit interval \([0, 1]\), imposes constant marginal effects, and exhibits heteroskedastic error terms. Maximum likelihood estimation of probit or logit models is strictly required.

6. Problem Set

Tier 1 · Mechanics

A probit recession model has estimated parameters \(\hat{\alpha} = -0.50\) and \(\hat{\beta} = -0.80\). If the current 10-year Treasury yield is 4.00% and the 3-month Treasury bill rate is 5.50%, calculate the exact predicted recession probability \(\hat{p}\). (Given \(\Phi(-1.70) \approx 0.0446\), \(\Phi(0.70) \approx 0.7580\)).

Solution

The term spread is \(S = y^{(10)} - y^{(3m)} = 4.00 - 5.50 = -1.50\%\). The latent index value is: \[ z = \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} S = -0.50 + (-0.80)(-1.50) = -0.50 + 1.20 = 0.70. \] The predicted probability is \(\hat{p} = \Phi(0.70) = 0.7580\) (75.80%).

Using the model from Problem 1 (\(\hat{\alpha} = -0.50, \hat{\beta} = -0.80\)), calculate the exact term spread \(S^*\) required for the predicted recession probability to reach 30%. (Given \(\Phi^{-1}(0.30) \approx -0.5244\)).

Solution

Setting \(\Phi(\hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} S^*) = 0.30\) yields: \[ \hat{\alpha} + \hat{\beta} S^* = \Phi^{-1}(0.30) \implies -0.50 - 0.80 S^* = -0.5244. \] Solving for \(S^*\): \[ -0.80 S^* = -0.5244 + 0.50 = -0.0244 \implies S^* = \frac{-0.0244}{-0.80} = +0.0305\%. \] A term spread of approximately +3.05 basis points triggers a 30% recession probability.

In a sample of \(T = 160\) quarters, an unconstrained probit model achieves maximized log-likelihood \(\ln L_u = -40.0\), while the constant-only model achieves \(\ln L_c = -55.0\). Calculate the Estrella pseudo-\(R^2\) (\(R^2_{\text{EM}}\)).

Solution

Under the Estrella & Mishkin (1998) formula: \[ R^2_{\text{EM}} = 1 - \left( \frac{\ln L_u}{\ln L_c} \right)^{-\frac{2}{T} \ln L_c}. \] Here \(\ln L_u / \ln L_c = -40.0 / -55.0 = 0.72727\). The exponent is: \[ -\frac{2}{160} (-55.0) = \frac{110.0}{160} = 0.6875. \] Evaluating \((0.72727)^{0.6875} = 0.8037\). Thus: \[ R^2_{\text{EM}} = 1 - 0.8037 = 0.1963 \quad (19.63\%). \]

Tier 2 · Proof

Prove that the marginal effect of the term spread on recession probability in a probit model is given by \(\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial S_t} = \beta \phi(\alpha + \beta S_t)\), and show that the magnitude of the marginal effect is maximized at \(S = -\alpha / \beta\).

Solution

The conditional probability is \(p_t = \Phi(\alpha + \beta S_t)\). Applying the chain rule: \[ \frac{\partial p_t}{\partial S_t} = \frac{d\Phi(z)}{dz} \frac{\partial z}{\partial S_t} = \phi(z) \cdot \beta = \beta \phi(\alpha + \beta S_t). \] To find the maximum magnitude, differentiate \(|\beta| \phi(z)\) with respect to \(z\): \[ \frac{d}{dz} \phi(z) = -z \phi(z). \] Setting \(-z \phi(z) = 0\) yields \(z = 0\) (since \(\phi(z) > 0\) for all finite \(z\)). The second derivative at \(z=0\) is \(\frac{d^2}{dz^2}\phi(0) = -\phi(0) < 0\), confirming a maximum. Setting \(z = \alpha + \beta S = 0\) yields \(S = -\alpha / \beta\). At this point \(p = \Phi(0) = 0.50\), proving that the term spread exerts its maximum marginal impact when the economy sits at the 50% probability tipping point.

Prove that the Brier accuracy score \(B = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T (\hat{p}_t - R_t)^2\) decomposes into reliability, resolution, and uncertainty components under a discrete probability calibration partition.

Solution

Partition the forecast probabilities into \(K\) bins with average forecast \(\bar{p}_k\), observed base rate \(\bar{o}_k\), and count \(n_k\). Let \(\bar{o} = \frac{1}{T} \sum R_t\) be the overall sample base rate. The Brier score can be rewritten as: \[ B = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{k=1}^K n_k (\bar{p}_k - \bar{o}_k)^2 - \frac{1}{T} \sum_{k=1}^K n_k (\bar{o}_k - \bar{o})^2 + \bar{o}(1 - \bar{o}). \] The first term is Reliability (measuring calibration error; lower is better). The second term is Resolution (measuring how much conditional probabilities deviate from the unconditional base rate; higher is better). The third term \(\bar{o}(1 - \bar{o})\) is Uncertainty (the intrinsic variance of the event, invariant to the model). \(\blacksquare\)

Let \(y_t^* = \alpha + \beta S_t + \epsilon_t\). Prove that scaling the latent variable error variance by \(\sigma^2 \ne 1\) leaves the probit maximum likelihood parameter estimates \(\hat{\alpha}/\hat{\sigma}\) and \(\hat{\beta}/\hat{\sigma}\) identified, but \(\sigma\) itself unidentified.

Solution

Suppose \(\epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\). The observed binary choice is: \[ \Pr(R_t = 1 \mid S_t) = \Pr(y_t^* > 0) = \Pr(\alpha + \beta S_t + \epsilon_t > 0) = \Pr\left(\frac{\epsilon_t}{\sigma} > -\frac{\alpha + \beta S_t}{\sigma}\right). \] Since \(\epsilon_t / \sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and the standard normal distribution is symmetric around zero: \[ \Pr(R_t = 1 \mid S_t) = \Phi\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sigma} + \frac{\beta}{\sigma} S_t\right). \] The likelihood function depends on the parameters solely through the ratios \(\tilde{\alpha} = \alpha / \sigma\) and \(\tilde{\beta} = \beta / \sigma\). For any scalar \(c > 0\), the parameter vector \((c\alpha, c\beta, c\sigma)\) produces an identical likelihood value. Thus \(\sigma\) is not separately identified, requiring the standard normalization \(\sigma \equiv 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Tier 3 · Empirical

Compare the forecasting performance of the 10-year minus 3-month spread versus the 10-year minus 2-year spread. Why does the Federal Reserve research staff historically prefer the 3-month yield for recession forecasting?

Solution

The 3-month Treasury bill rate directly anchors the current stance of overnight monetary policy set by the Federal Open Market Committee. In contrast, the 2-year Treasury yield is itself a forward-looking rate that reflects policy expectations over the next 24 months. Subtracting a 2-year yield from a 10-year yield reflects expected policy adjustments between years 2 and 10, whereas the 10Y-3M spread measures the pure slope between the current policy stance and long-term equilibrium, maximizing empirical pseudo-\(R^2\) at the 4-quarter horizon.

In an out-of-sample test, a probit model predicts recession probabilities \(\hat{p} = [0.15, 0.40, 0.70, 0.85]\) across four quarters where actual recessions were \(R = [0, 0, 1, 1]\). Compute the Brier accuracy score.

Solution

The Brier score is \(B = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{t=1}^4 (\hat{p}_t - R_t)^2\): \[ (0.15 - 0)^2 = 0.0225, \quad (0.40 - 0)^2 = 0.1600, \] \[ (0.70 - 1)^2 = (-0.30)^2 = 0.0900, \quad (0.85 - 1)^2 = (-0.15)^2 = 0.0225. \] Summing: \(0.0225 + 0.1600 + 0.0900 + 0.0225 = 0.2950\). Dividing by 4: \[ B = \frac{0.2950}{4} = 0.07375. \]

A backtest conditions an equity market hedge on the rule "buy put options whenever \(\hat{p}_t(4) \ge 0.35\)". Explain why this strategy suffered severe option premium decay during 2022–2023.

Solution

The yield curve inverted in mid-2022 and remained deeply inverted throughout 2023, driving \(\hat{p}_t(4) > 0.50\) for over six consecutive quarters without an NBER recession materializing. Systematically rolling protective put options over 18 months in the absence of a market crash incurred substantial cumulative negative roll yield and volatility decay, severely eroding strategy equity.

Tier 4 · Research

Develop an augmented probit model that decomposes the nominal term spread into expected real short rates, expected inflation, and term premia using TIPS and survey data. How does this decomposition prevent false positive signals caused by compressed term premia?

Solution

Specify the augmented latent index: \(y_{t+k}^* = \alpha + \beta_1 \Delta \mathbb{E}_t[r^{\text{real}}] + \beta_2 \Delta \mathbb{E}_t[\pi] + \beta_3 TP_t + \epsilon_t\). Under monetary tightening, an inversion driven by expected real rate cuts (\(\beta_1 < 0\)) correctly signals recession risk. However, if the inversion is driven purely by a decline in the term premium \(TP_t\) (due to foreign reserve accumulation or quantitative easing), \(\beta_3 \approx 0\) suppresses false alarms. D'Amico et al. (2018) show that filtering out term premia prevents false signals during eras of central bank balance sheet expansion.

Why does serial correlation in the overlapping recession indicator \(R_{t+k}\) violate classical MLE standard errors, and what econometric correction is required?

Solution

When predicting recession status \(k=4\) quarters ahead with quarterly data, the forecast horizons overlap by 3 quarters. Any unexpected shock at \(t+1\) persists across consecutive observations, inducing MA(3) autocorrelation in the error terms. Classical probit MLE assumes independent observations, causing standard errors to be severely underestimated (yielding inflated t-statistics). A GMM probit framework with Newey-West / Hansen-Hodrick spectral density estimators, or generalized estimating equations (GEE), is required to obtain consistent standard errors.

Formulate a dynamic ROC optimization problem that chooses the probability decision threshold \(p^*\) to maximize expected portfolio utility given asymmetric costs of Type I errors (unhedged recession crash) versus Type II errors (cost of unnecessary hedges).

Solution

Let \(C_I\) denote the loss from suffering an unhedged market crash (Type I error) and \(C_{II}\) denote the hedging friction cost incurred during an expansion (Type II error). Given prior recession probability \(\pi = \Pr(R=1)\), the expected cost is: \[ \mathbb{E}[C(p^*)] = C_I \pi (1 - \text{TPR}(p^*)) + C_{II} (1 - \pi) \text{FPR}(p^*). \] Differentiating with respect to \(\text{FPR}\) and setting to zero yields the optimal ROC operating slope: \[ \frac{d\text{TPR}}{d\text{FPR}} = \frac{1 - \pi}{\pi} \frac{C_{II}}{C_I}. \] When crash losses far exceed hedging costs (\(C_I \gg C_{II}\)), the optimal threshold \(p^*\) shifts downward (e.g. \(p^* \approx 0.25\)), accepting higher false positive rates to ensure catastrophic drawdown avoidance.

8. Retrieval Quiz

  1. What yield curve spread is most commonly used in academic and central bank recession forecasting?

    Options

    Estrella & Mishkin (1998) establish the 10Y minus 3M spread as the empirical gold standard for recession forecasting.

  2. What does the Expectations Hypothesis imply when long-term interest rates fall below current short-term rates?

    Options

    Long rates reflect expected future short rates; inversion signals expected monetary easing in response to economic downturn.

  3. Why does the probit model guarantee that predicted probabilities \(\hat{p}_t\) stay within [0, 1]?

    Options

    By construction, the cumulative normal distribution function \(\Phi(z)\) maps \((-\infty, \infty)\) strictly into \((0, 1)\).

  4. At what forecasting horizon does the yield curve term spread achieve its maximum pseudo-\(R^2\) in Estrella & Mishkin (1998)?

    Options

    Estrella & Mishkin (1998, Table 1) find that the pseudo-\(R^2\) peaks at ~0.29–0.30 at the four-quarter horizon.

  5. If \(\hat{\alpha} = -0.60\) and \(\hat{\beta} = -0.75\), what is the term spread threshold for a 50% predicted recession probability?

    Options

    \(S^* = -\hat{\alpha}/\hat{\beta} = -(-0.60) / (-0.75) = -0.80\%\) (-80 bps).

  6. What does an Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) of 0.85 indicate about a binary classification model?

    Options

    AUC measures rank discrimination; 0.5 is random guessing and 1.0 is perfect separation. An AUC of 0.85 denotes excellent discrimination.

  7. What historical postwar episode is widely recognized as a prominent yield curve inversion false positive?

    Options

    In 1966, the yield curve inverted deeply, but massive Vietnam War government expenditure prevented a formal NBER recession.

  8. How does term premium compression by central bank quantitative easing affect yield curve recession signals?

    Options

    When term premia are depressed into negative territory, the curve can invert even when expected short rates are not projecting severe recession.

  9. Why does the probit log-likelihood have a unique global maximum?

    Options

    Theorem 2.1 proves that the Hessian is negative definite for all parameter values, ensuring strict concavity and a unique maximum.

  10. How does the yield curve behave right at the onset of an actual recession?

    Options

    Central banks slash policy rates as recession arrives, driving short rates down rapidly and steepening the curve ("bull steepener").
