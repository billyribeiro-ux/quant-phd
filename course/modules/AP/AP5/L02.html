Campbell-Shiller Log-Linearization

  • Uses: excess volatility, log returns, geometric series, present value
  • Introduces: Campbell–Shiller decomposition
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore

Non-linear present-value equations in price levels suffer from unit roots and non-stationarity. In a landmark 1988 paper, John Campbell and Robert Shiller solved this problem by taking a first-order Taylor approximation of log returns around the long-run mean dividend-price ratio. The resulting log-linear framework transforms a non-linear accounting definition into an exact linear dynamic identity. By solving this relation forward, the log dividend-price ratio is expressed as a discounted sum of expected future returns and expected future dividend growth. This decomposition serves as the workhorse framework for all modern empirical asset pricing.

1The log return identity and first-order Taylor expansion

Let \(P_t\) be the ex-dividend stock price and \(D_t\) the dividend. Lowercase letters denote natural logarithms: \(p_t = \ln(P_t)\) and \(d_t = \ln(D_t)\). The gross simple return is \(R_{t+1} = (P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}) / P_t\), so the continuously compounded log return \(r_{t+1} = \ln(R_{t+1})\) satisfies

\[ r_{t+1} = \ln(P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}) - \ln(P_t) = p_{t+1} - p_t + \ln\left(1 + \frac{D_{t+1}}{P_{t+1}}\right). \]

Define the log dividend-price ratio as \(\delta_t = d_t - p_t\). Substituting \(\delta_{t+1}\) into the return definition yields

\[ r_{t+1} = p_{t+1} - p_t + \ln\big(1 + \exp(\delta_{t+1})\big). \]
Theorem 1.1 (Campbell-Shiller log-linear return approximation)

A first-order Taylor expansion of \(r_{t+1}\) around the sample mean log dividend-price ratio \(\bar{\delta} = \E[\delta_t]\) yields

\[ r_{t+1} \approx k - \rho\,\delta_{t+1} + \delta_t + \Delta d_{t+1}, \]

where \(\Delta d_{t+1} = d_{t+1} - d_t\) is log dividend growth, and the linearization constants are

\[ \rho = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(\bar{\delta})} \in (0, 1), \qquad k = -\ln(\rho) - (1 - \rho)\ln(1/\rho - 1). \]
Proof

Consider the non-linear function \(f(\delta) = \ln\big(1 + \exp(\delta)\big)\). The first derivative is

\[ f'(\delta) = \frac{\exp(\delta)}{1 + \exp(\delta)} = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + \exp(\delta)} = 1 - \rho(\delta). \]

Evaluating at the expansion point \(\bar{\delta}\), let \(\rho = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(\bar{\delta})}\). Then \(1 - \rho = \frac{\exp(\bar{\delta})}{1 + \exp(\bar{\delta})}\), so \(\exp(\bar{\delta}) = \frac{1 - \rho}{\rho} = \frac{1}{\rho} - 1\). Thus,

\[ f(\bar{\delta}) = \ln\left(1 + \frac{1 - \rho}{\rho}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right) = -\ln(\rho). \]

The first-order Taylor expansion around \(\bar{\delta}\) is

\[ f(\delta) \approx f(\bar{\delta}) + f'(\bar{\delta})(\delta - \bar{\delta}) = -\ln(\rho) + (1 - \rho)(\delta - \bar{\delta}). \]

Rearranging the constant term,

\[ -\ln(\rho) - (1 - \rho)\bar{\delta} = -\ln(\rho) - (1 - \rho)\ln\left(\frac{1}{\rho} - 1\right) = k. \]

Now express \(p_{t+1} - p_t\) in terms of \(\delta\) and \(\Delta d\):

\[ p_{t+1} - p_t = (d_{t+1} - \delta_{t+1}) - (d_t - \delta_t) = \Delta d_{t+1} - \delta_{t+1} + \delta_t. \]

Adding \(f(\delta_{t+1}) \approx k + (1 - \rho)\delta_{t+1}\) to \(p_{t+1} - p_t\) gives

\[ r_{t+1} \approx \Delta d_{t+1} - \delta_{t+1} + \delta_t + k + (1 - \rho)\delta_{t+1} = k - \rho\,\delta_{t+1} + \delta_t + \Delta d_{t+1}. \]

2Solving forward: the dynamic present-value identity

Rearranging the one-period approximation expresses the current dividend-price ratio \(\delta_t\) as a function of next period's dividend yield, return, and dividend growth:

\[ \delta_t \approx -k + r_{t+1} - \Delta d_{t+1} + \rho\,\delta_{t+1}. \]

Iterating this equation forward for \(H\) periods by substituting \(\delta_{t+1}, \delta_{t+2}, \dots, \delta_{t+H-1}\) produces

\[ \delta_t \approx -\sum_{j=1}^H \rho^{j-1} k + \sum_{j=1}^H \rho^{j-1} (r_{t+j} - \Delta d_{t+j}) + \rho^H \delta_{t+H}. \]

Summing the finite geometric series \(-\sum_{j=1}^H \rho^{j-1} k = -\frac{k(1 - \rho^H)}{1 - \rho}\), we obtain the finite-horizon forward identity.

Definition 2.1 (Campbell-Shiller decomposition)

Imposing the transversality condition \(\lim_{H \to \infty} \rho^H \delta_{t+H} = 0\) and taking conditional expectations at date \(t\), the Campbell–Shiller decomposition expresses the log dividend-price ratio as

\[ \delta_t = -\frac{k}{1 - \rho} + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[r_{t+j}] - \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\Delta d_{t+j}]. \]

In terms of demeaned variables \(\tilde{\delta}_t = \delta_t - \bar{\delta}\), \(\tilde{r}_{t+j} = r_{t+j} - \bar{r}\), and \(\Delta \tilde{d}_{t+j} = \Delta d_{t+j} - \bar{g}\), the constant vanishes:

\[ \tilde{\delta}_t = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\tilde{r}_{t+j}] - \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\Delta \tilde{d}_{t+j}]. \]

3Economic intuition of the identity

The Campbell-Shiller decomposition is a mathematical tautology (up to a small Taylor approximation error of order \(10^{-4}\)). It imposes no behavioral assumptions and no asset pricing model. It states that if the dividend-price ratio is unusually high today, at least one of three things must happen in the future:

  1. High future returns: Stock returns \(\{r_{t+j}\}\) must be high on average (discount-rate effect).
  2. Low future dividend growth: Dividend growth \(\{\Delta d_{t+j}\}\) must be low on average (cash-flow effect).
  3. Permanent bubble: The dividend-price ratio must explode to infinity (\(\lim_{H\to\infty} \rho^H \delta_{t+H} \ne 0\)).
Remark 3.1 (why linearization succeeds where level bounds failed)

Unlike price levels \(P_t\) and dividends \(D_t\), which possess unit roots, log returns \(r_t\), dividend growth rates \(\Delta d_t\), and the log dividend-price ratio \(\delta_t\) are stationary \(\mathcal{I}(0)\) time series in empirical US data. The Campbell-Shiller framework avoids spurious regression problems caused by integrated levels.

Lab 1 · Campbell-Shiller Log-Linearization and Forward Identity

On a simulated 120-period path of stock prices and dividends, the first-order Campbell-Shiller approximation achieves a correlation with exact log returns greater than 0.999, with a maximum absolute approximation error under 0.005. Furthermore, solving the identity forward over a 50-period horizon matches the initial log dividend-price ratio to within numerical tolerance.

"""Lab AP5-L02-1: Campbell-Shiller Log-Linear Present-Value Identity.

SIMULATED: verifies the Campbell-Shiller log-linear return approximation and
the forward-solved present value identity on a simulated asset price path.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(123)
    n_periods = 120  # 120 periods (e.g. 10 years of monthly or 120 quarters)

    # Simulate dividend growth and dividend-price ratio
    # delta_t = d_t - p_t follows persistent AR(1) around mean -3.2 (approx 4.0% annual dividend yield)
    delta_mean = -3.20
    phi_delta = 0.92
    sigma_delta = 0.08
    delta = np.zeros(n_periods)
    delta[0] = delta_mean
    for t in range(1, n_periods):
        delta[t] = delta_mean + phi_delta * (delta[t - 1] - delta_mean) + sigma_delta * rng.standard_normal()

    # Dividend growth: Delta d_{t+1} has mean 0.01 and vol 0.03
    delta_d = 0.01 + 0.03 * rng.standard_normal(n_periods)

    # Reconstruct log dividends and log prices
    d = np.cumsum(delta_d)
    p = d - delta

    # Exact gross return and log return
    # R_{t+1} = (P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}) / P_t
    r_exact = np.zeros(n_periods - 1)
    for t in range(n_periods - 1):
        p_curr = np.exp(p[t])
        p_next = np.exp(p[t + 1])
        d_next = np.exp(d[t + 1])
        r_exact[t] = np.log((p_next + d_next) / p_curr)

    # Campbell-Shiller linearization constants
    d_minus_p_bar = float(np.mean(delta[:-1]))
    rho = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(d_minus_p_bar))
    k = -np.log(rho) - (1.0 - rho) * np.log(1.0 / rho - 1.0)

    # Linearized return: r_{t+1}^approx = k - rho*delta_{t+1} + delta_t + Delta d_{t+1}
    r_approx = np.zeros(n_periods - 1)
    for t in range(n_periods - 1):
        r_approx[t] = k - rho * delta[t + 1] + delta[t] + delta_d[t + 1]

    # Calculate approximation error
    errors = r_exact - r_approx
    max_abs_err = float(np.max(np.abs(errors)))
    mean_abs_err = float(np.mean(np.abs(errors)))
    corr = float(np.corrcoef(r_exact, r_approx)[0, 1])

    # Check multi-period forward identity for horizon = 50
    # delta_t = -k/(1-rho) + sum_{j=1}^H rho^{j-1}(r_j - Delta d_j) + rho^H delta_H
    horizon = 50
    lhs = delta[0]
    discount_factors = rho ** np.arange(horizon)
    sum_r = float(np.sum(discount_factors * r_approx[:horizon]))
    sum_d = float(np.sum(discount_factors * delta_d[1 : horizon + 1]))
    terminal = (rho**horizon) * delta[horizon]
    const_term = -k * (1.0 - rho**horizon) / (1.0 - rho)
    rhs = const_term + sum_r - sum_d + terminal
    identity_gap = abs(lhs - rhs)

    print("Campbell-Shiller (1988) Log-Linearization Verification")
    print(f"Sample length T:             {n_periods} periods")
    print(f"Mean log dividend-price:     {d_minus_p_bar:.4f}")
    print(f"Linearization parameter rho: {rho:.4f}")
    print(f"Linearization constant k:    {k:.4f}")
    print(f"Max abs approximation error: {max_abs_err:.6f}")
    print(f"Mean abs approx error:       {mean_abs_err:.6f}")
    print(f"Correlation (exact, approx): {corr:.6f}")
    print(f"Forward identity (H={horizon}):     LHS={lhs:.5f}, RHS={rhs:.5f}")
    print(f"Identity gap:                {identity_gap:.8e}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

Despite its universal adoption, the log-linearization relies on conditions that fail in certain financial environments:

  • Disappearing dividends and share repurchases: As documented by Fama and French (2001), many modern corporations return cash via share buybacks rather than cash dividends. When a company pays zero dividends, \(\delta_t = \ln(0) = -\infty\), breaking the logarithmic transformation. Modern applications must substitute total payout yields (dividends plus net repurchases) or book-to-market ratios.
  • Extreme market crashes: The Taylor expansion is centered around the historical mean \(\bar{\delta}\). During severe market crashes (such as October 1987 or March 2020), dividend yields deviate by multiple standard deviations from \(\bar{\delta}\), increasing higher-order Taylor truncation errors.
  • Structural parameter shifts: The discount factor parameter \(\rho = 1 / (1 + \exp(\bar{\delta}))\) depends on the sample mean dividend yield. If long-term dividend payout policies shift permanently (e.g., the secular decline in US dividend yields post-1990), a constant \(\rho\) induces parameter instability.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “The Campbell-Shiller decomposition is an economic theory that assumes investor rationality.”

    The Campbell-Shiller relation is a mathematical approximation of the definition of return. It holds identically regardless of whether market participants are rational expected-utility maximizers, noise traders, or behavioral investors.

  2. “A high dividend yield today guarantees that future dividends will increase.”

    In the Campbell-Shiller decomposition, future dividend growth enters with a negative sign: \(\delta_t \approx \sum \rho^j r_{t+j} - \sum \rho^j \Delta d_{t+j}\). A high dividend yield indicates either higher future returns or lower future dividend growth.

  3. “The discount parameter \(\rho\) is the subjective time-preference discount factor \(\beta\) of a representative agent.”

    \(\rho\) is purely an algebraic expansion parameter derived from the long-run average dividend-price ratio: \(\rho = 1 / (1 + \bar{D}/\bar{P})\). For typical annual dividend yields of 4%, \(\rho \approx 1 / (1 + 0.04) \approx 0.96\).

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate Campbell-Shiller parameters rho and k

Suppose the historical average dividend yield is \(\bar{D}/\bar{P} = 0.04\), so \(\bar{\delta} = \ln(0.04) = -3.21888\). Compute the exact numerical values of the linearization parameters \(\rho\) and \(k\).

Solution

The parameter \(\rho\) is \(\rho = 1 / (1 + 0.04) \approx 0.9615\).

The linearization constant \(k\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} k &= -\ln(\rho) - (1 - \rho)\ln(1/\rho - 1) \\ &= -\ln(0.9615) - (0.0385)\ln(0.04) \\ &\approx 0.0392 + 0.1238 = 0.1630 \end{aligned} \]
M2 · One-period log return approximation

At date \(t\), \(\delta_t = -3.20\). At date \(t+1\), \(\delta_{t+1} = -3.10\) and dividend growth is \(\Delta d_{t+1} = 0.02\). Using \(\rho = 0.96\) and \(k = 0.16\), compute the approximate log return \(r_{t+1}\).

Solution

Using the log-linear formula \(r_{t+1} \approx k - \rho\,\delta_{t+1} + \delta_t + \Delta d_{t+1}\): \(r_{t+1} \approx 0.16 - 0.96(-3.10) + (-3.20) + 0.02 = 0.16 + 2.976 - 3.20 + 0.02 = -0.044\). The approximate log return is \(-4.4\%\).

M3 · Geometric discount weight accumulation

With \(\rho = 0.96\), what proportion of the infinite-horizon present value weight \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} = \frac{1}{1 - \rho} = 25\) is accumulated within the first 10 years?

Solution

The 10-year partial sum is \(\sum_{j=1}^{10} \rho^{j-1} = \frac{1 - \rho^{10}}{1 - \rho}\). With \(\rho = 0.96\), \(\rho^{10} \approx 0.664833\), so \(\frac{1 - 0.664833}{0.04} = \frac{0.335167}{0.04} \approx 8.3792\). The proportion of total weight is \(1 - \rho^{10} = 1 - 0.664833 = 0.335167\) (approximately \(33.52\%\)).

Proof

P1 · Forward iteration of the difference equation

Prove by mathematical induction that \(\delta_t = -\sum_{j=1}^H \rho^{j-1} k + \sum_{j=1}^H \rho^{j-1} (r_{t+j} - \Delta d_{t+j}) + \rho^H \delta_{t+H}\).

Solution

Base case (\(H=1\)): From Theorem 1.1, \(r_{t+1} = k - \rho\delta_{t+1} + \delta_t + \Delta d_{t+1}\), which rearranges directly to \(\delta_t = -k + (r_{t+1} - \Delta d_{t+1}) + \rho\delta_{t+1}\). This matches the formula for \(H=1\). Induction step: Assume the formula holds for \(H\). For \(\delta_{t+H}\), substitute the one-period relation \(\delta_{t+H} = -k + (r_{t+H+1} - \Delta d_{t+H+1}) + \rho\delta_{t+H+1}\). Multiplying by \(\rho^H\) gives \(\rho^H \delta_{t+H} = -\rho^H k + \rho^H(r_{t+H+1} - \Delta d_{t+H+1}) + \rho^{H+1}\delta_{t+H+1}\). Adding this to the sum from \(j=1\) to \(H\) produces the sum from \(j=1\) to \(H+1\), completing the induction.

P2 · Gordon growth as a special case

Show that when returns are constant (\(r_{t+j} = r\)) and dividend growth is constant (\(\Delta d_{t+j} = g\)), the Campbell-Shiller identity simplifies to the continuous Gordon growth formula \(\delta = r - g - k/(1-\rho)\).

Solution

Substitute constant values \(\E_t[r_{t+j}] = r\) and \(\E_t[\Delta d_{t+j}] = g\) into the infinite identity: \(\delta = -\frac{k}{1 - \rho} + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} (r - g) = -\frac{k}{1 - \rho} + \frac{r - g}{1 - \rho}\). When evaluated at the steady state \(\bar{\delta}\), the constant satisfies \(\bar{\delta}(1 - \rho) = -k + (\bar{r} - \bar{g})\), exactly matching the long-run Gordon relation.

P3 · Taylor remainder bound

Let \(f(\delta) = \ln(1 + e^\delta)\). Prove that the second derivative is bounded by \(0 < f''(\delta) \le 1/4\) for all \(\delta \in \R\), and deduce that the Taylor approximation error is bounded by \(\frac{1}{8}(\delta - \bar{\delta})^2\).

Solution

The first derivative is \(f'(\delta) = \frac{e^\delta}{1 + e^\delta}\). Differentiating again, \(f''(\delta) = \frac{e^\delta(1 + e^\delta) - e^{2\delta}}{(1 + e^\delta)^2} = \frac{e^\delta}{(1 + e^\delta)^2} = p(1 - p)\), where \(p = \frac{e^\delta}{1 + e^\delta} \in (0, 1)\). The quadratic \(p(1 - p)\) attains its unique global maximum at \(p = 1/2\), where its value is \(1/4\). By Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder, \(|f(\delta) - [f(\bar{\delta}) + f'(\bar{\delta})(\delta - \bar{\delta})]| = \frac{1}{2} f''(\xi) (\delta - \bar{\delta})^2 \le \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{4} (\delta - \bar{\delta})^2 = \frac{1}{8}(\delta - \bar{\delta})^2\).

Empirical

E1 · Empirical magnitude of linearization error

Using annual US S&P 500 data, the standard deviation of \(\delta_t - \bar{\delta}\) is approximately 0.35. Compute the upper bound on the average Taylor linearization error using Problem P3.

Solution

From Problem P3, the remainder is bounded by \(\frac{1}{8}(\delta - \bar{\delta})^2\). Evaluating at one standard deviation (0.35), the error is at most \(\frac{1}{8}(0.35)^2 = \frac{1}{8}(0.1225) \approx 0.0153\) (about 1.5% in log return). In practice, because \(\delta_t\) clusters tightly near \(\bar{\delta}\), the mean absolute error is under 15 basis points (0.0015), as confirmed in Lab 1.

E2 · Transversality condition in real markets

Explain why the assumption \(\lim_{H \to \infty} \rho^H \delta_{t+H} = 0\) is economically plausible for stock market indices.

Solution

Because \(\rho \approx 0.96 < 1\), \(\rho^H\) decays exponentially to zero as \(H \to \infty\). For \(\rho^H \delta_{t+H}\) to remain non-zero, the log dividend-price ratio \(\delta_{t+H}\) would have to explode at an exponential rate \((1/\rho)^H \approx (1.04)^H\). That would mean the dividend yield either approaches infinity (prices go to zero) or approaches zero (prices are infinitely higher than dividends), both of which are ruled out by market arbitrage and economic feasibility.

E3 · Impact of share repurchases on dividend yield

Since the mid-1980s, US corporate repurchases have grown from 10% of total cash payouts to over 50%. How has this shift affected empirical estimates of \(\bar{\delta}\) and \(\rho\)?

Solution

The substitution from dividends to share buybacks caused the average cash dividend yield to decline from historical levels of ~4% to ~2%. Consequently, \(\bar{\delta} = \ln(\bar{D}/\bar{P})\) dropped from \(-3.22\) to \(-3.91\). Correspondingly, \(\rho = 1 / (1 + \bar{D}/\bar{P})\) increased from \(0.96\) to \(0.98\), giving greater weight to distant future expectations.

Research

R1 · Variance decomposition of the dividend-price ratio

From \(\tilde{\delta}_t = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\tilde{r}_{t+j}] - \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\Delta \tilde{d}_{t+j}]\), show how multiplying by \(\tilde{\delta}_t\) and taking expectations yields an exact decomposition of \(\Var(\delta_t)\).

Solution

Multiply both sides by \(\tilde{\delta}_t\) and take unconditional expectations: \(\Var(\tilde{\delta}_t) = \Cov\Big(\tilde{\delta}_t, \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\tilde{r}_{t+j}]\Big) - \Cov\Big(\tilde{\delta}_t, \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\Delta \tilde{d}_{t+j}]\Big)\). Dividing both sides by \(\Var(\tilde{\delta}_t)\) yields \(1 = \beta_r - \beta_d\), where \(\beta_r\) is the sum of regression coefficients of future returns on the dividend yield, and \(\beta_d\) is the sum of regression coefficients of future dividend growth on the dividend yield.

R2 · Price-earnings ratio adaptation

How does Campbell and Shiller (1988b) adapt the log-linear framework to price-earnings ratios, and what accounting issues arise?

Solution

Campbell and Shiller adapt the framework by defining the log earnings-price ratio \(e_t - p_t\). Because earnings fluctuate violently over the business cycle and can become negative during recessions, \(\ln(E_t)\) is undefined. They resolve this by defining cyclically adjusted earnings as a 10-year moving average of real earnings (the CAPE ratio), which smooths out temporary cyclical volatility and guarantees strictly positive earnings.

R3 · Return predictability as a consequence of stationarity

Explain Cochrane's (2008) argument that if the dividend-price ratio is stationary, returns or dividend growth must be forecastable.

Solution

From the identity \(\Var(\delta) = \Cov(\delta, \text{future } r) - \Cov(\delta, \text{future } \Delta d)\), if \(\delta_t\) is stationary with strictly positive variance \(\Var(\delta) > 0\), the covariance on the right-hand side cannot be zero. Therefore, it is mathematically impossible for both returns and dividend growth to be unforecastable. If dividend growth is unforecastable (as empirical evidence indicates), returns must be predictable.
