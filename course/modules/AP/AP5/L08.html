Cross-Asset Discount Rate Dynamics

  • Uses: discount-rate variation, cash-flow versus discount-rate news

  • Areplicated

For decades, asset pricing studied equities, Treasury bonds, foreign exchange, and corporate credit as separate silos. In his 2011 presidential address, John Cochrane showed that a single economic mechanism governs all of them: prices are not random walks, and time-varying risk premia are ubiquitous across markets. In every asset class, valuation ratios and yield spreads forecast returns rather than cash flows, and expected returns across equities, government bonds, currencies, and credit co-move strongly over the business cycle. This final lesson synthesizes the present-value framework into a unified macro-finance theory of discount rates.

1The cross-asset predictability phenomenon

In classical theory under risk neutrality and constant discount rates, forward prices and spreads equal unbiased expectations of future spot rates and cash flows. In empirical reality, every major asset market exhibits return predictability driven by valuation ratios and spreads:

In all major asset classes, price ratios and yield spreads forecast excess returns rather than cash flows or spot rate changes. The regression of cash-flow changes on price ratios yields coefficients near zero, while the regression of excess returns yields coefficients near one.

2The unified common discount-rate factor

Let \(r_{i, t+1}^e\) denote the excess return on asset class \(i \in \{\text{equity}, \text{Treasury}, \text{currency}, \text{credit}\}\). Extracting the expected excess return \(\mu_{i, t} = \E_t[r_{i, t+1}^e]\) from each predictive regression reveals a striking empirical fact: the expected returns across all asset classes share a powerful common principal component.

Definition 2.1 (common discount-rate factor)

The common discount-rate factor \(f_t^{\text{DR}}\) is the first principal component of expected excess returns across asset classes:

\[ \mu_{i, t} = \alpha_i + \gamma_i\,f_t^{\text{DR}} + \xi_{i, t}, \qquad \Cov(f_t^{\text{DR}}, \xi_{i, t}) = 0. \]

The common factor \(f_t^{\text{DR}}\) explains 60% to 75% of the total variation in expected returns across markets. It is intensely countercyclical: it spikes during recessions (such as 1974, 1982, 2001, 2008, and 2020) and reaches troughs during late-stage economic expansions.

3Macroeconomic and institutional foundations

Why do discount rates vary across all asset classes in synchrony? Modern asset pricing offers three complementary theoretical explanations:

  1. Time-varying risk aversion (Habit Formation): In the Campbell and Cochrane (1999) habit model, representative consumers have utility \(U(C_t - X_t)\), where \(X_t\) is a slow-moving consumption habit. In recessions, consumption falls toward the habit level, causing local curvature of the utility function (effective risk aversion) to explode. Investors demand high risk premia across all assets.
  2. Long-run macroeconomic risks: In the Bansal and Yaron (2004) model, consumers fear persistent shifts in expected growth and economic uncertainty (time-varying economic volatility \(\sigma_t\)). When macro volatility rises, risk premia across equities, bonds, and credit surge simultaneously.
  3. Intermediary asset pricing: In He and Krishnamurthy (2013) and Brunnermeier and Sannikov (2014), marginal asset prices are set by financial intermediaries (broker-dealers and banks). In financial crises, intermediary balance sheets contract, capital constraints bind, and required risk premia spike across all leveraged asset classes.

Lab 1 · Cross-Asset Return Predictability and Discount-Rate Synthesis

Simulating a countercyclical macroeconomic risk premium driver that loads on both the equity dividend-price ratio and the Treasury term spread will produce statistically significant predictive regressions in both markets. The fitted expected returns will exhibit strong positive cross-asset correlation (> 0.70), confirming the existence of a common discount-rate factor.

"""Lab AP5-L08-1: Cross-Asset Return Predictability and Discount-Rate Synthesis.

SIMULATED: evaluates Cochrane's (2011) discount rate synthesis across equities and
Treasuries, estimating predictive regressions and extracting the common discount-rate factor.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2011)
    n_obs = 200  # 200 quarters (50 years)

    # Macroeconomic business cycle state s_t: countercyclical risk premium driver
    phi_s = 0.90
    sigma_s = 0.25
    s = np.zeros(n_obs + 1)
    for t in range(n_obs):
        s[t + 1] = phi_s * s[t] + sigma_s * rng.standard_normal()

    # Asset 1: Equity market
    # Dividend-price ratio is loaded heavily on macro state s_t
    dp = -3.20 + 0.35 * s[:-1] + 0.08 * rng.standard_normal(n_obs)
    # Equity excess return: positive loading on s_t + idiosyncratic risk
    rx_eq = 0.018 + 0.08 * s[:-1] + 0.16 * rng.standard_normal(n_obs)

    # Asset 2: Treasury bond market
    # Term spread is also countercyclical (widens in recessions)
    term_spread = 0.015 + 0.012 * s[:-1] + 0.004 * rng.standard_normal(n_obs)
    # 5-year Treasury excess return: positive loading on term spread and s_t
    rx_bond = 0.005 + 0.025 * s[:-1] + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal(n_obs)

    # OLS 1: Equity predictive regression: rx_eq = a_eq + b_eq * dp + e
    dp_m = float(np.mean(dp))
    rx_eq_m = float(np.mean(rx_eq))
    sxx_dp = float(np.sum((dp - dp_m) ** 2))
    sxy_eq = float(np.sum((dp - dp_m) * (rx_eq - rx_eq_m)))
    syy_eq = float(np.sum((rx_eq - rx_eq_m) ** 2))
    b_eq = sxy_eq / sxx_dp
    r2_eq = (sxy_eq**2) / (sxx_dp * syy_eq)
    se_eq = float(np.sqrt(max(1e-12, (syy_eq - b_eq * sxy_eq) / ((n_obs - 2) * sxx_dp))))
    t_eq = b_eq / se_eq

    # OLS 2: Bond predictive regression: rx_bond = a_bond + b_bond * term_spread + e
    ts_m = float(np.mean(term_spread))
    rx_bd_m = float(np.mean(rx_bond))
    sxx_ts = float(np.sum((term_spread - ts_m) ** 2))
    sxy_bd = float(np.sum((term_spread - ts_m) * (rx_bond - rx_bd_m)))
    syy_bd = float(np.sum((rx_bond - rx_bd_m) ** 2))
    b_bond = sxy_bd / sxx_ts
    r2_bond = (sxy_bd**2) / (sxx_ts * syy_bd)
    se_bond = float(np.sqrt(max(1e-12, (syy_bd - b_bond * sxy_bd) / ((n_obs - 2) * sxx_ts))))
    t_bond = b_bond / se_bond

    # Extract fitted expected excess returns (time-varying risk premia)
    exp_rx_eq = rx_eq_m + b_eq * (dp - dp_m)
    exp_rx_bd = rx_bd_m + b_bond * (term_spread - ts_m)
    corr_risk_premia = float(np.corrcoef(exp_rx_eq, exp_rx_bd)[0, 1])

    print("Cochrane (2011) Cross-Asset Discount Rate Synthesis")
    print(f"Sample length T:             {n_obs} quarters ({n_obs / 4:.0f} years)")
    print(f"Equity predictive slope:     {b_eq:.4f}")
    print(f"Equity OLS t-stat (R^2):     {t_eq:.2f} ({r2_eq * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"Bond predictive slope:       {b_bond:.4f}")
    print(f"Bond OLS t-stat (R^2):       {t_bond:.2f} ({r2_bond * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"Risk premia correlation:     {corr_risk_premia:.4f}")
    print("Cross-asset implication:     Common countercyclical discount-rate factor")

4Where the canon breaks

While the unified discount-rate factor captures broad business-cycle swings, cross-asset linkages break down under specific market frictions:

  • Flight-to-safety decoupling: In extreme liquidity panics (e.g., September 2008 or March 2020), equity risk premia and credit spreads explode, while Treasury term premia plunge as investors panic into short-term sovereign paper. This creates temporary negative correlation between equity and Treasury risk premia.
  • Capital segmentation and limits to arbitrage: Specialized institutional mandates prevent capital from flowing frictionlessly across desks. A mortgage-backed security dislocation (2008) or a Treasury basis dislocation (March 2020) can persist for weeks because equity hedge funds cannot readily deploy capital into fixed-income arbitrage.
  • Inflation regime shifts: The correlation between stock and bond returns flipped from negative (post-2000) to sharply positive during the 2022 inflation surge. When inflation shocks dominate real growth shocks, bonds cease to act as hedges, altering cross-asset discount rate covariance.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Equities and bonds are priced by fundamentally different economic principles.”

    Both equities and bonds are priced by the same stochastic discount factor \(M_{t+1}\). The Campbell-Shiller decomposition applies equally to stocks (where cash flows are dividends) and bonds (where cash flows are coupons and principal), with discount rates driving variation in both.

  2. “The expectations hypothesis of the term structure holds even if equity returns are predictable.”

    The expectations hypothesis assumes constant bond risk premia. Fama-Bliss (1987) and Cochrane-Piazzesi (2005) refuted the expectations hypothesis, proving that bond term premia vary significantly over time and load on the same business-cycle discount rate factor as equities.

  3. “A high risk premium means an asset is a good investment that is guaranteed to outperform.”

    A high risk premium means that expected return is elevated because the asset is extraordinarily risky: it pays off poorly in recessions when investors' marginal utility of wealth is highest. High expected return is compensation for systematic macroeconomic distress.

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate bond excess return from forward rates

A 2-year zero-coupon Treasury bond trades at price \(p_t^{(2)} = -0.08\) (log price), and a 1-year bond trades at \(p_t^{(1)} = -0.035\). At date \(t+1\), the remaining 1-year bond trades at \(p_{t+1}^{(1)} = -0.040\). Compute the 1-year holding period excess return over the risk-free rate.

Solution

The 1-year risk-free rate is \(r_{f, t} = -p_t^{(1)} = 0.035\) (3.5%). The 1-year holding period return on the 2-year bond is \(r_{t+1}^{(2)} = p_{t+1}^{(1)} - p_t^{(2)} = -0.040 - (-0.080) = +0.040\) (4.0%). The excess return is \(rx_{t+1}^{(2)} = r_{t+1}^{(2)} - r_{f, t} = 0.040 - 0.035 = 0.005\) (50 basis points).

M2 · Fama currency carry trade excess return

The US dollar 1-year interest rate is \(i_{\text{US}} = 0.05\), and the Japanese yen interest rate is \(i_{\text{JP}} = 0.01\). Over the year, the yen depreciates against the dollar by \(2\%\) (\(\Delta s = -0.02\)). Compute the dollar return of the carry trade (borrow in yen, invest in dollars).

Solution

The excess return on the foreign exchange carry trade is \(rx^{\text{FX}} = (i_{\text{US}} - i_{\text{JP}}) + \Delta s\). Here, \((i_{\text{US}} - i_{\text{JP}}) = 0.05 - 0.01 = 0.04\) (400 bps interest differential). The yen depreciated, so holding dollar assets earned an additional currency gain (or paying back yen cost 2% less). The total return is \(0.04 - (-0.02) = 0.04 + 0.02 = 0.06\) (6.0%). Under UIP, the currency should have appreciated by 4%, producing zero excess return.

M3 · Common discount-rate factor extraction

Expected excess returns on equities and Treasuries satisfy \(\mu_{\text{eq}, t} = 0.04 + 1.20\,f_t\) and \(\mu_{\text{bd}, t} = 0.01 + 0.30\,f_t\). If the common factor variance is \(\Var(f_t) = 1.0\), and idiosyncratic noise variances are \(\Var(\xi_{\text{eq}}) = 0.005\) and \(\Var(\xi_{\text{bd}}) = 0.001\), compute \(\Cov(\mu_{\text{eq}}, \mu_{\text{bd}})\).

Solution

Because idiosyncratic noises are uncorrelated with \(f_t\) and with each other, \(\Cov(\mu_{\text{eq}, t}, \mu_{\text{bd}, t}) = \Cov(1.20\,f_t, 0.30\,f_t) = 1.20 \times 0.30 \times \Var(f_t) = 0.36 \times 1.0 = 0.36\).

Proof

P1 · Forward rate decomposition under term premia

Let \(f_t^{(n)} = p_t^{(n-1)} - p_t^{(n)}\) be the forward rate. Prove that the forward rate decomposes into expected spot rate plus expected excess bond return: \(f_t^{(n)} = \E_t[y_{t+n-1}^{(1)}] + \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \E_t[rx_{t+j}^{(n-j+1)}]\).

Solution

By definition of holding-period return, \(r_{t+1}^{(n)} = p_{t+1}^{(n-1)} - p_t^{(n)} = y_t^{(1)} + rx_{t+1}^{(n)}\). Rearranging gives \(p_t^{(n)} = p_{t+1}^{(n-1)} - y_t^{(1)} - rx_{t+1}^{(n)}\). Subtracting \(p_t^{(n-1)}\), the forward rate is \(f_t^{(n)} = p_t^{(n-1)} - p_t^{(n)} = y_t^{(1)} + rx_{t+1}^{(n)} - (p_{t+1}^{(n-1)} - p_t^{(n-1)})\). Taking conditional expectations and iterating forward \(n-1\) periods, the terminal price is \(p_{t+n}^{(0)} = 0\), leaving \(f_t^{(n)} = \E_t[y_{t+n-1}^{(1)}] + \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \E_t[rx_{t+j}^{(n-j+1)}]\). If term premia are zero, the forward rate equals the expected spot rate (the expectations hypothesis).

P2 · The Fama (1984) regression decomposition

In the currency regression \(\Delta s_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta (i_t - i_t^*) + \epsilon_{t+1}\), prove that \(\beta < 0\) if and only if \(\Cov(\E_t[rx_{t+1}^{\text{FX}}], i_t - i_t^*) > \Var(i_t - i_t^*)\).

Solution

Write the currency excess return as \(rx_{t+1}^{\text{FX}} = (i_t - i_t^*) - \Delta s_{t+1}\). Rearranging, \(\Delta s_{t+1} = (i_t - i_t^*) - rx_{t+1}^{\text{FX}}\). The OLS slope of \(\Delta s_{t+1}\) on \((i_t - i_t^*)\) is \(\beta = \frac{\Cov(\Delta s_{t+1}, i_t - i_t^*)}{\Var(i_t - i_t^*)} = \frac{\Cov(i_t - i_t^* - rx_{t+1}^{\text{FX}}, i_t - i_t^*)}{\Var(i_t - i_t^*)} = 1 - \frac{\Cov(rx_{t+1}^{\text{FX}}, i_t - i_t^*)}{\Var(i_t - i_t^*)}\). Therefore, \(\beta < 0 \iff 1 - \frac{\Cov(\E_t[rx_{t+1}^{\text{FX}}], i_t - i_t^*)}{\Var(i_t - i_t^*)} < 0 \iff \Cov(\E_t[rx_{t+1}^{\text{FX}}], i_t - i_t^*) > \Var(i_t - i_t^*)\). An empirical slope of \(\beta \approx -0.8\) requires currency risk premia to be more volatile than interest rate differentials.

P3 · Consumption CAPM habit discount factor

In the Campbell-Cochrane (1999) habit model, local relative risk aversion is \(\gamma_t = \gamma / S_t\), where \(S_t = (C_t - X_t)/C_t\) is the surplus consumption ratio. Prove that when consumption falls toward habit (\(S_t \to 0\)), the maximum Sharpe ratio \(\sigma(M_t)/\E[M_t]\) approaches infinity.

Solution

The stochastic discount factor is \(M_{t+1} = \beta (C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\gamma} (S_{t+1}/S_t)^{-\gamma}\). Under lognormal consumption with surplus ratio sensitivity \(\lambda(S_t)\), the log SDF innovation is \(\ln(M_{t+1}) - \E_t[\ln(M_{t+1})] = -\gamma [1 + \lambda(S_t)] v_{t+1}\), where \(v_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_c^2)\). Campbell and Cochrane set \(\lambda(S_t) = \frac{1}{\bar{S}}\sqrt{1 - 2(s_t - \bar{s})} - 1\). As \(S_t \to 0\), \(\lambda(S_t) \to \infty\), so \(\Var_t(\ln M_{t+1}) = \gamma^2 [1 + \lambda(S_t)]^2 \sigma_c^2 \to \infty\). By the Hansen-Jagannathan bound, the maximum Sharpe ratio equals \(\frac{\sigma_t(M)}{\E_t[M]} = \sqrt{\exp(\Var_t(\ln M)) - 1} \to \infty\).

Empirical

E1 · The Cochrane-Piazzesi (2005) tent-shaped factor

Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005) regressed 1-year Treasury bond excess returns across maturities (2 to 5 years) on five forward rates. What functional shape did the estimated coefficients exhibit, and what \(R^2\) was achieved?

Solution

Cochrane and Piazzesi discovered that the regression coefficients on forward rates followed a remarkable "tent shape": negative on the 1-year rate, positive on 3-year and 4-year forward rates, and negative on the 5-year rate. This single linear combination of forward rates predicted excess bond returns across all maturities with an \(R^2\) exceeding 35% to 44%, demonstrating that a single macro risk factor drives bond risk premia across the entire yield curve.

E2 · The Fama (1984) forward premium puzzle

In foreign exchange regressions of currency depreciation on interest rate differentials, what is the empirical estimate of \(\beta\), and how does it refute uncovered interest parity (UIP)?

Solution

Under UIP, \(\beta\) should equal \(+1.0\) (high interest rate currencies should depreciate to offset the yield advantage). In empirical tests across major currencies, \(\beta\) is consistently negative, averaging \(-0.8\) to \(-1.2\). High-yield currencies appreciate on average, generating the famous "carry trade" anomaly, proving that currency returns are dominated by time-varying risk premia.

E3 · March 2020 dash for cash and cross-asset breakdown

During the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in mid-March 2020, what unusual co-movement occurred between equity prices and 10-year Treasury bond yields?

Solution

Normally, when equities crash, Treasury bond prices surge (yields fall) due to flight to safety. In mid-March 2020, equities plummeted while long-term Treasury yields simultaneously spiked (bond prices collapsed). Highly leveraged hedge funds facing margin calls on Treasury basis trades were forced to liquidate off-the-run Treasuries for cash, temporarily decoupling fixed-income markets until the Federal Reserve intervened with massive quantitative easing.

Research

R1 · Intermediary asset pricing: He and Krishnamurthy (2013)

How does intermediary asset pricing explain why risk premia across diverse assets (mortgages, credit, equity, currencies) spike together in crises?

Solution

He and Krishnamurthy (2013) model financial intermediaries as the marginal investors in sophisticated asset markets. During normal times, intermediary capital is abundant, risk constraints do not bind, and risk premia are low. When large adverse shocks deplete intermediary equity capital, regulatory constraints bind, forcing balance sheet shrinkage across all trading desks. The shadow price of intermediary capital explodes, causing required risk premia across all leveraged asset classes to surge simultaneously.

R2 · Global risk factors: Miranda-Agrippino and Rey (2020)

Miranda-Agrippino and Rey (2020) documented a "Global Financial Cycle" driven by US monetary policy. How does this explain international cross-asset predictability?

Solution

They showed that a single global factor explains a large portion of returns in risky assets (equities, corporate bonds, commodity futures) worldwide. This global factor is driven by US monetary policy, which influences the leverage and risk tolerance of global global banks and broker-dealers. When the Fed eases, global risk aversion drops, credit spreads narrow, and asset prices rise worldwide; when the Fed tightens, discount rates surge across all international markets.

R3 · Unified multi-asset tactical allocation strategy

How does a quantitative macro fund operationalize Cochrane's discount-rate synthesis into an institutional multi-asset strategy?

Solution

The strategy estimates a unified state-space model extracting the common discount-rate factor \(f_t\) from equity dividend yields, Treasury yield curve slope/curvature, corporate credit spreads, and currency carry differentials. When \(f_t\) is elevated (recession troughs, high market distress), the fund systematically overweights equities, high-yield credit, and long-term bonds to capture high risk premia; when \(f_t\) is depressed (market euphoria, low spreads), it trims risk exposures and rotates into cash.
