Out-of-Sample Predictability & The Welch-Goyal Critique

  • Uses: dividend-price predictive regressions, the OLS estimator, spurious regressions
  • Introduces: out-of-sample R-squared
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

In a devastating 2008 empirical study, Ivo Welch and Amit Goyal evaluated every popular predictor in the academic literature—including dividend yield, earnings yield, term spreads, and default spreads—in recursive out-of-sample forecasts of US equity returns. They discovered that almost every variable produced negative out-of-sample \(R^2\), failing to beat a naive historical prevailing average. However, Campbell and Thompson (2008) showed that imposing elementary economic restrictions (the predictive slope must be positive and expected equity premia cannot be negative) eliminates estimation noise and restores out-of-sample profitability. This debate defines quantitative tactical asset allocation.

1The out-of-sample \(R^2\) metric

Consider an investor at date \(t\) forecasting the next-period stock return \(r_{t+1}\). To prevent lookahead bias, all parameters must be estimated recursively using only data observed up to date \(t\).

Definition 1.1 (out-of-sample R-squared)

Let \(\hat{r}_{t+1}\) be the recursive predictive regression forecast generated by an econometric model, and let \(\bar{r}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^t r_s\) be the historical prevailing mean benchmark forecast. For an evaluation period spanning \(t = t_0\) to \(T\), the out-of-sample \(R^2\) is

\[ R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t=t_0}^{T-1} (r_{t+1} - \hat{r}_{t+1})^2}{\sum_{t=t_0}^{T-1} (r_{t+1} - \bar{r}_{t+1})^2} = 1 - \frac{\text{MSE}_{\text{model}}}{\text{MSE}_{\text{benchmark}}}. \]

The interpretation of \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) differs fundamentally from in-sample \(R^2\):

  • If \(R^2_{\text{OOS}} > 0\), the predictive model delivers lower mean squared forecast error than the historical mean benchmark, providing evidence of genuine predictability.
  • If \(R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0\), the model generates worse forecasts than a simple constant historical average. Notice that \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) can be arbitrarily negative if the predictive model overfits.

2The Welch and Goyal (2008) comprehensive critique

Welch and Goyal (2008) systematically tested 14 popular economic predictors of the equity risk premium over the period 1926–2005. Their test suite included dividend-price ratio, dividend yield, earnings-price ratio, dividend payout ratio, book-to-market ratio, net equity expansion, Treasury bill rate, long-term yield, term spread, default spread, inflation, and investment-to-capital ratio.

Proposition 2.1 (Welch and Goyal empirical findings)

Across the entire post-war sample (1947–2005) and multiple sub-periods, unconstrained recursive OLS regressions with popular macroeconomic predictors produced negative out-of-sample \(R^2\) against the historical prevailing mean benchmark. Most predictors showed good in-sample performance only due to isolated historical episodes (such as the 1973–1974 oil shock), failing entirely out-of-sample.

Welch and Goyal concluded that an investor who used published predictive regression models would have suffered lower returns and higher risk than an investor who simply assumed expected returns equal historical average returns.

3Economic restrictions: Campbell and Thompson (2008)

Why do predictive models fail out-of-sample even when in-sample evidence is compelling? Because expected returns have low signal-to-noise ratios (true monthly \(R^2 \approx 0.5\%\)), unconstrained OLS estimates are overwhelmed by sampling error. In small samples, OLS occasionally estimates negative slopes or negative expected returns.

Theorem 3.1 (Campbell & Thompson 2008 economic restrictions)

Imposing two economically motivated sign restrictions on recursive forecasts significantly improves out-of-sample predictability:

  1. Sign of slope: \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{CT}} = \max(0, \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}})\) (a higher dividend yield cannot imply lower expected returns).
  2. Sign of forecast: \(\hat{r}_{t+1}^{\text{CT}} = \max(0, \hat{r}_{t+1})\) (the expected equity risk premium cannot be negative).

These restrictions truncate estimation noise, flipping negative out-of-sample \(R^2\) into positive \(R^2_{\text{OOS}} \in [0.5\%, 2.0\%]\) and producing statistically and economically significant utility gains.

Remark 3.2 (economic value of small R-squared)

In high-noise financial returns, a seemingly tiny out-of-sample \(R^2\) translates into large portfolio gains. Campbell and Thompson (2008) proved that for a mean-variance investor with risk aversion \(\gamma\), the proportional increase in expected utility from timing the market is approximately

\[ \Delta U \approx \frac{R^2_{\text{OOS}} + S^2}{2\gamma(1 - R^2_{\text{OOS}})}, \]

where \(S\) is the historical unconditional Sharpe ratio. An \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) of just 1% per month yields an annualized utility gain of over 200 to 400 basis points.

Lab 1 · Out-of-Sample Return Predictability and Economic Restrictions

On a simulated 20-year monthly dataset with mild return predictability (\(R^2 \approx 1.5\%\)), unconstrained recursive OLS will overfit estimation noise, producing negative or near-zero out-of-sample \(R^2\). Imposing Campbell-Thompson economic sign restrictions (\(\hat{\beta} \ge 0\) and \(\hat{r} \ge 0\)) eliminates noise, yields strictly positive \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\), and generates annualized utility gains exceeding 1% per year.

"""Lab AP5-L06-1: Out-of-Sample Return Predictability and Economic Restrictions.

SIMULATED: replicates the Welch-Goyal (2008) vs Campbell-Thompson (2008) out-of-sample
predictability horse race with recursive expanding estimation and sign restrictions.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(999)
    n_obs = 240  # 240 months (20 years)
    n_init = 60  # initial burn-in window of 5 years (60 months)

    # Predictor x_t: persistent valuation ratio (AR(1) phi = 0.96)
    # Return r_{t+1} = alpha + beta * x_t + u_{t+1}
    # Mild predictability with true R^2 approx 1.5%
    phi = 0.96
    sigma_v = 0.05
    sigma_u = 0.16
    cov_uv = -0.70 * sigma_u * sigma_v
    cov_matrix = np.array([[sigma_u**2, cov_uv], [cov_uv, sigma_v**2]])

    noise = rng.multivariate_normal(mean=[0.0, 0.0], cov=cov_matrix, size=n_obs + 1)
    u = noise[:, 0]
    v = noise[:, 1]

    x = np.zeros(n_obs + 1)
    for t in range(n_obs):
        x[t + 1] = phi * x[t] + v[t + 1]

    # True return: beta = 0.06
    beta_true = 0.06
    r = 0.005 + beta_true * x[:-1] + u[1:]
    x_pred = x[:-1]

    # Recursive out-of-sample forecasting loop
    actuals = []
    bench_forecasts = []
    ols_forecasts = []
    ct_forecasts = []

    for t in range(n_init, n_obs):
        # Data available at time t: returns r[0:t] and predictors x_pred[0:t]
        y_hist = r[:t]
        x_hist = x_pred[:t]

        # 1. Historical prevailing mean benchmark
        r_bar = float(np.mean(y_hist))
        bench_forecasts.append(r_bar)

        # 2. Unconstrained OLS: r_{s+1} = a + b * x_s
        x_m = float(np.mean(x_hist))
        y_m = float(np.mean(y_hist))
        sxx = float(np.sum((x_hist - x_m) ** 2))
        sxy = float(np.sum((x_hist - x_m) * (y_hist - y_m)))
        b_hat = sxy / sxx if sxx > 1e-12 else 0.0
        a_hat = y_m - b_hat * x_m

        # Predict period t+1 return given x_pred[t]
        r_pred_ols = a_hat + b_hat * x_pred[t]
        ols_forecasts.append(r_pred_ols)

        # 3. Campbell & Thompson (2008) economically constrained forecast:
        # Constraint 1: b_hat >= 0 (positive risk premium slope)
        b_ct = max(0.0, b_hat)
        a_ct = y_m - b_ct * x_m
        r_pred_ct_raw = a_ct + b_ct * x_pred[t]
        # Constraint 2: predicted excess return >= 0
        r_pred_ct = max(0.0, r_pred_ct_raw)
        ct_forecasts.append(r_pred_ct)

        actuals.append(r[t])

    y_true = np.array(actuals)
    f_bench = np.array(bench_forecasts)
    f_ols = np.array(ols_forecasts)
    f_ct = np.array(ct_forecasts)

    # Compute Mean Squared Errors
    mse_bench = float(np.mean((y_true - f_bench) ** 2))
    mse_ols = float(np.mean((y_true - f_ols) ** 2))
    mse_ct = float(np.mean((y_true - f_ct) ** 2))

    # Out-of-sample R^2 = 1 - MSE / MSE_bench
    r2_oos_ols = 1.0 - (mse_ols / mse_bench)
    r2_oos_ct = 1.0 - (mse_ct / mse_bench)

    # Annualized mean-variance utility gain (gamma = 3)
    gamma = 3.0
    sharpe_hist = float(np.mean(y_true) / np.std(y_true, ddof=1))
    delta_u_ct = (r2_oos_ct + sharpe_hist**2) / (2.0 * gamma * (1.0 - r2_oos_ct)) * 12.0

    print("Welch & Goyal (2008) vs Campbell & Thompson (2008) OOS Test")
    print(f"Total evaluation periods:    {len(y_true)} months")
    print(f"Historical mean MSE:         {mse_bench:.6f}")
    print(f"Unconstrained OLS MSE:       {mse_ols:.6f}")
    print(f"Constrained CT MSE:          {mse_ct:.6f}")
    print(f"Unconstrained OOS R^2:       {r2_oos_ols * 100:.3f}%")
    print(f"Constrained OOS R^2:         {r2_oos_ct * 100:.3f}%")
    print(f"Annualized utility gain:     {delta_u_ct * 100:.2f}% per year")


main()

Historical mean MSE:         0.026719
Unconstrained OLS MSE:       0.026741
Constrained CT MSE:          0.025993

The numerical simulation reproduces the Welch-Goyal vs. Campbell-Thompson horse race. Naive recursive OLS suffers from estimation noise, dragging down out-of-sample forecast accuracy. Applying the Campbell-Thompson non-negativity constraints prevents the model from taking irrational short equity positions during market dips, transforming negative OOS performance into positive \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) and generating meaningful mean-variance utility gains.

4Where the canon breaks

Out-of-sample predictability evaluation faces critical methodological boundaries:

  • Structural parameter instability: Real-world macroeconomic relationships undergo regime shifts (e.g., changes in Federal Reserve monetary policy, inflation regimes, financial globalization). A model trained over 1950–1980 can fail over 1980–2010 due to genuine structural breaks.
  • Lookahead bias in predictor discovery: Even if forecasts are generated recursively, the choice of which 14 variables to evaluate was made with hindsight after seeing full-sample literature. As documented by Sullivan, Timmermann, and White (1999), data snooping across thousands of candidate indicators inflates perceived predictability.
  • Transaction costs and short-sale limits: Rebalancing equity allocations monthly based on valuation ratios incurs turnover, bid-ask spread costs, and tax liabilities that can eliminate small theoretical utility gains in live trading.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “An out-of-sample \(R^2\) of 1% is statistically zero and practically useless for an asset manager.”

    Because stock returns have immense noise, monthly \(R^2\) can never exceed 2% to 3% in efficient markets. A monthly \(R^2\) of 1% compounds into a 10% annual advantage and generates 200–400 bps in annual alpha, which is massive for institutional portfolios.

  2. “If a predictive model fails out-of-sample, return predictability was entirely a statistical illusion.”

    In-sample tests have much higher statistical power than recursive out-of-sample tests (Inoue and Kilian 2004). Out-of-sample failure often reflects small-sample parameter estimation error rather than an absence of underlying population predictability.

  3. “The benchmark in \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) is the zero-forecast (\(\hat{r} = 0\)).”

    The benchmark is the historical prevailing average return \(\bar{r}_t = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^t r_s\). Beating zero is trivial because equities earn a positive equity premium; the challenge is beating the unconditional historical mean.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate out-of-sample R-squared

Over a 100-month evaluation period, the mean squared error of a predictive regression model is \(\text{MSE}_{\text{model}} = 0.002450\), while the mean squared error of the historical prevailing mean benchmark is \(\text{MSE}_{\text{bench}} = 0.002500\). Compute \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\).

Solution

Using Definition 1.1, \(R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 1 - \frac{\text{MSE}_{\text{model}}}{\text{MSE}_{\text{bench}}} = 1 - \frac{0.002450}{0.002500} = 1 - 0.9800 = +0.0200\) (or \(+2.0\%\)). Because the model MSE is strictly lower than the benchmark, \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) is positive.

M2 · Negative out-of-sample R-squared calculation

Suppose an overfitted predictive model achieves \(\text{MSE}_{\text{model}} = 0.002625\) against benchmark \(\text{MSE}_{\text{bench}} = 0.002500\). Compute \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) and interpret.

Solution

\(R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 1 - \frac{0.002625}{0.002500} = 1 - 1.0500 = -0.0500\) (\(-5.0\%\)). A negative \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) means that the predictive model generates 5% greater squared forecast error than a naive historical average, confirming that the model harms forecasting performance.

M3 · Campbell-Thompson utility gain calculation

An investor with risk aversion \(\gamma = 3\) achieves monthly \(R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.01\) on an asset with historical monthly Sharpe ratio \(S = 0.15\). Compute the annualized utility gain \(\Delta U \times 12\).

Solution

The monthly utility gain is \(\Delta U \approx \frac{R^2_{\text{OOS}} + S^2}{2\gamma(1 - R^2_{\text{OOS}})} = \frac{0.01 + (0.15)^2}{2 \times 3 \times (1 - 0.01)} = \frac{0.01 + 0.0225}{6 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.0325}{5.94} \approx 0.005471\). Annualizing by multiplying by 12 yields \(\Delta U_{\text{annual}} \approx 0.005471 \times 12 \approx 0.065657\) (approximately \(6.57\%\) per year).

Proof

P1 · Decomposition of OOS forecast error

Let \(r_{t+1} = \beta x_t + \epsilon_{t+1}\). Show that the expected forecast error \(\E[(r_{t+1} - \hat{\beta}x_t)^2]\) decomposes into irreducible risk \(\sigma_\epsilon^2\) plus parameter estimation error \(\Var(\hat{\beta})\E[x_t^2]\).

Solution

Write \(r_{t+1} - \hat{\beta}x_t = (\beta x_t + \epsilon_{t+1}) - \hat{\beta}x_t = \epsilon_{t+1} - (\hat{\beta} - \beta)x_t\). Squaring both sides, \((r_{t+1} - \hat{\beta}x_t)^2 = \epsilon_{t+1}^2 - 2\epsilon_{t+1}(\hat{\beta} - \beta)x_t + (\hat{\beta} - \beta)^2 x_t^2\). Because \(\epsilon_{t+1}\) is independent of data up to date \(t\), \(\E[\epsilon_{t+1}(\hat{\beta} - \beta)x_t] = 0\). Taking expectations yields \(\E[(r_{t+1} - \hat{\beta}x_t)^2] = \E[\epsilon_{t+1}^2] + \E[(\hat{\beta} - \beta)^2 x_t^2] = \sigma_\epsilon^2 + \Var(\hat{\beta})\E[x_t^2]\). If the sampling variance \(\Var(\hat{\beta})\E[x_t^2]\) exceeds the true reduction in variance \(\beta^2 \Var(x)\), the predictive model performs worse than the sample mean.

P2 · Non-negativity constraint optimality

Prove that if the true parameter satisfies \(\beta \ge 0\), the truncated estimator \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{CT}} = \max(0, \hat{\beta})\) weakly dominates \(\hat{\beta}\) in mean squared estimation error: \(\E[(\hat{\beta}_{\text{CT}} - \beta)^2] \le \E[(\hat{\beta} - \beta)^2]\).

Solution

Compare the squared errors pointwise for any realization of \(\hat{\beta}\): If \(\hat{\beta} \ge 0\), \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{CT}} = \hat{\beta}\), so \((\hat{\beta}_{\text{CT}} - \beta)^2 = (\hat{\beta} - \beta)^2\). If \(\hat{\beta} < 0\), because \(\beta \ge 0\), we have \(\hat{\beta} < 0 \le \beta\). The distance from 0 to \(\beta\) is strictly less than the distance from \(\hat{\beta}\) to \(\beta\): \((\hat{\beta}_{\text{CT}} - \beta)^2 = (0 - \beta)^2 = \beta^2 < (\hat{\beta} - \beta)^2 = \hat{\beta}^2 - 2\hat{\beta}\beta + \beta^2\). Since the error is weakly lower for every realization, taking expectations guarantees \(\E[(\hat{\beta}_{\text{CT}} - \beta)^2] \le \E[(\hat{\beta} - \beta)^2]\).

P3 · Clark and West (2007) nested model adjustment

Explain why standard Diebold-Mariano tests are biased against rejecting the benchmark when the benchmark model is nested within the predictive model.

Solution

Under the null hypothesis that \(\beta = 0\), the predictive model must estimate an extra parameter \(\hat{\beta}\). By Problem P1, estimating an irrelevant parameter introduces sample noise that inflates \(\text{MSE}_{\text{model}}\) by approximately \(\Var(\hat{\beta})\E[x_t^2] > 0\). Therefore, under the true null, the population expected difference \(\E[\text{MSE}_{\text{bench}} - \text{MSE}_{\text{model}}] < 0\). Clark and West (2007) showed that testing without correcting for this parameter estimation noise severely under-rejects the predictive model, requiring an adjusted test statistic that adds back the estimated sample noise.

Empirical

E1 · The 1970s oil crisis episode

Welch and Goyal (2008) observed that the predictive power of the dividend-price ratio was concentrated around the 1973–1975 oil crisis. Explain why this specific historical period had such outsized influence.

Solution

During the 1973–1974 recession, stock prices collapsed by over 45% while inflation spiked, pushing the dividend-price ratio to historic highs. In 1975–1976, stocks rebounded with massive positive returns. In a regression, extreme leverage points at the boundary of the sample exert massive influence on OLS slope estimates. Welch and Goyal showed that if the 1973–1975 period is excluded, the in-sample predictive \(t\)-statistic for the dividend yield drops below significance thresholds.

E2 · Expanding vs. rolling estimation windows

In recursive out-of-sample testing, what are the trade-offs between an expanding estimation window versus a rolling 10-year estimation window?

Solution

An expanding window uses all available historical data, reducing parameter estimation variance at rate \(1/t\). However, it adapts very slowly to structural breaks. A rolling window (e.g. 10 years) adapts rapidly to regime changes and shifting valuation benchmarks, but suffers from high sampling variance because \(T\) is permanently small, often producing volatile, noisy out-of-sample forecasts.

E3 · Out-of-sample performance of the CAPE ratio

How does Shiller's 10-year Cyclically Adjusted Price-Earnings (CAPE) ratio perform out-of-sample compared to the raw dividend-price ratio?

Solution

The CAPE ratio outperforms the raw dividend-price ratio in out-of-sample tests over modern post-1990 data. Because corporate managers increasingly substituted share repurchases for dividends, the cash dividend yield suffered a secular downward drift that deteriorated its out-of-sample forecasting power. By contrast, 10-year smoothed earnings capture total corporate profitability independent of payout channel, maintaining positive out-of-sample predictive power at 5- to 10-year horizons.

Research

R1 · Inoue and Kilian (2004) in-sample vs out-of-sample power

Inoue and Kilian (2004) proved that out-of-sample tests of predictability are asymptotically less powerful than in-sample tests. Summarize their mathematical argument.

Solution

Inoue and Kilian showed that in-sample OLS tests utilize all sample data efficiently under standard Gauss-Markov conditions. Out-of-sample tests discard a substantial fraction of the sample during the initial burn-in window and introduce recursive parameter estimation noise into each forecast. Under local alternatives, in-sample \(F\)-tests achieve the optimal asymptotic power envelope, whereas recursive out-of-sample tests suffer from severe power loss, explaining why true economic predictability can be rejected out-of-sample.

R2 · Machine learning and shrinkage in return prediction

How does the modern literature (e.g., Gu, Kelly, and Xiu 2020) apply machine learning to overcome the Welch-Goyal critique across hundreds of predictors?

Solution

Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) demonstrated that while individual univariate OLS regressions fail due to estimation noise (the Welch-Goyal problem), combining dozens of macro predictors and firm characteristics using regularized shrinkage (Elastic Net, Random Forests, and Neural Networks) drastically reduces variance. Non-linear interactions and shrinkage prevent overfitting, achieving out-of-sample \(R^2\) of 1.5% to 2.5% per month and doubling portfolio Sharpe ratios.

R3 · Forecast combination and Bayesian model averaging

Rapach, Strauss, and Zhou (2010) proposed combining individual forecasts rather than running a multivariate regression. Why does forecast combination succeed out-of-sample?

Solution

Running a single multivariate regression with 14 correlated predictors estimates a \(15 \times 15\) covariance matrix, which blows up estimation variance in small samples. Rapach et al. estimated 14 separate univariate models and averaged their forecasts (simple equal weighting or Bayesian model averaging). Averaging diversifies away model-specific idiosyncratic estimation error and hedge instability across business cycle phases, consistently beating the historical mean out-of-sample.
