The Shiller Volatility Puzzle

  • Uses: present value, discount factors, simple returns, variance
  • Introduces: excess volatility
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Under the efficient markets hypothesis with constant expected returns, stock prices equal the conditional expectation of discounted future dividends. Because the conditional expectation is an orthogonal projection of ex-post realizations onto current information, actual prices must fluctuate strictly less than ex-post rational dividends. Shiller (1981) tested this inequality on US equity data and discovered that market prices are five to thirteen times more volatile than justified by future dividends. This fundamental empirical failure launched modern empirical asset pricing and established that asset price fluctuations are driven by discount-rate variation rather than cash-flow news.

1The present value model under constant expected returns

Let \(P_t\) denote the ex-dividend stock price at date \(t\), and let \(D_{t+1}\) denote the dividend paid at date \(t+1\). The one-period gross simple return is

\[ R_{t+1} = \frac{P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}}{P_t}. \]

The classical efficient markets hypothesis (Fama, 1970) assumes that the expected gross return is constant over time: \(\E_t[R_{t+1}] = R = 1 + r > 1\). Rearranging gives the one-period difference equation

\[ P_t = \frac{1}{R}\,\E_t[P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}]. \]

Iterating this relation forward \(H\) periods under iterated expectations yields

\[ P_t = \sum_{j=1}^H \left(\frac{1}{R}\right)^j \E_t[D_{t+j}] + \left(\frac{1}{R}\right)^H \E_t[P_{t+H}]. \]

Ruling out rational speculative bubbles by imposing the transversality condition \(\lim_{H \to \infty} R^{-H} \E_t[P_{t+H}] = 0\), the price equals the expected present value of all future dividends:

\[ P_t = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \left(\frac{1}{R}\right)^j \E_t[D_{t+j}]. \]
Definition 1.1 (ex-post rational price)

The ex-post rational price \(P_t^*\) is the present value that would obtain if all future dividends were known with certainty:

\[ P_t^* = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \left(\frac{1}{R}\right)^j D_{t+j}. \]

Notice that \(P_t^*\) satisfies the deterministic backward recursion

\[ P_t^* = \frac{P_{t+1}^* + D_{t+1}}{R}. \]

Comparing the definitions reveals that the market price is the conditional mathematical expectation of the ex-post rational price given date-\(t\) information \(\mathcal{F}_t\):

\[ P_t = \E[P_t^* \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \E_t[P_t^*]. \]

2Shiller variance bounds

Define the forecast error \(\varepsilon_t\) between the ex-post rational price and the date-\(t\) market price:

\[ \varepsilon_t = P_t^* - P_t = P_t^* - \E[P_t^* \mid \mathcal{F}_t]. \]
Theorem 2.1 (Shiller variance bound)

If the discount rate \(R\) is constant and prices contain no rational bubbles, the variance of the market price cannot exceed the variance of the ex-post rational price:

\[ \Var(P_t) \le \Var(P_t^*). \]
Proof

By the law of iterated expectations, \(\E[\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \E[P_t^* - P_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\). Since \(P_t\) is measurable with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_t\), the error \(\varepsilon_t\) is orthogonal to \(P_t\):

\[ \Cov(P_t, \varepsilon_t) = \E[P_t \varepsilon_t] - \E[P_t]\E[\varepsilon_t] = \E\big[P_t \E[\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\big] = 0. \]

Writing \(P_t^* = P_t + \varepsilon_t\), the variance of the sum of two orthogonal random variables decomposes into

\[ \Var(P_t^*) = \Var(P_t + \varepsilon_t) = \Var(P_t) + \Var(\varepsilon_t) + 2\Cov(P_t, \varepsilon_t) = \Var(P_t) + \Var(\varepsilon_t). \]

Because the variance of any real random variable is nonnegative, \(\Var(\varepsilon_t) \ge 0\), it follows directly that \(\Var(P_t^*) \ge \Var(P_t)\).

Definition 2.2 (excess volatility)

The market exhibits excess volatility if the sample variance of asset prices strictly exceeds the theoretical upper bound implied by cash-flow variation under the present-value model:

\[ \widehat{\Var}(P_t) > \widehat{\Var}(P_t^*). \]

3Empirical failure: Shiller (1981)

Shiller (1981) constructed empirical time series of \(P_t\) and \(P_t^*\) for the S&P Composite Index from 1871 to 1979. To compute \(P_t^*\), Shiller set terminal values at the end of the sample \(T = 1979\) using historical average dividend growth, and evaluated the backward recursion recursively.

Proposition 3.1 (Shiller 1981 empirical findings)

In US equity markets (1871–1979), the standard deviation of real detrended stock prices is 5.59 times greater than the standard deviation of ex-post rational prices, violating the variance bound \(\Var(P) \le \Var(P^*)\) by a factor exceeding 30.

Because \(P_t^*\) is an exponentially weighted moving average of future dividends, it is exceptionally smooth. Market prices, by contrast, gyrate wildly around fundamentals during panics, booms, and depressions. The empirical ratio \(\widehat{\Var}(P) / \widehat{\Var}(P^*) \gg 1\) constitutes decisive evidence against constant discount rates.

Lab 1 · Shiller Variance Bounds and the Excess Volatility Puzzle

Under a constant 6% real discount rate and a geometric dividend process, the ex-post rational price series \(P_t^*\) is an exponentially weighted moving average of future dividends. If stock prices are driven purely by constant-discount-rate fundamentals, the variance ratio \(\Var(P)/\Var(P^*)\) cannot exceed 1. When market prices incorporate time-varying risk premia or sentiment, \(\Var(P)\) will violently violate Shiller's bound.

"""Lab AP5-L01-1: Shiller's Excess Volatility Test and Present Value Bounds.

SIMULATED: generates synthetic real dividend path and computes ex-post rational
price P_t^* to test Shiller's variance inequality Var(P) <= Var(P^*).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    # Set seed for reproducibility
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    n_years = 100
    r_const = 0.06  # constant real discount rate 6%
    g_mean = 0.015  # mean real dividend growth 1.5%
    sigma_d = 0.12  # dividend growth volatility

    # Simulate log dividend growth and levels
    delta_d = g_mean + sigma_d * rng.standard_normal(n_years)
    d = np.zeros(n_years)
    d[0] = 10.0
    for t in range(1, n_years):
        d[t] = d[t - 1] * np.exp(delta_d[t])

    # Terminal value for ex-post rational price P_T^* = D_T / (r - g)
    p_star = np.zeros(n_years)
    p_star[-1] = d[-1] / (r_const - g_mean)

    # Backward recursion: P_t^* = (P_{t+1}^* + D_{t+1}) / (1 + r)
    for t in range(n_years - 2, -1, -1):
        p_star[t] = (p_star[t + 1] + d[t + 1]) / (1.0 + r_const)

    # Rational present value price with constant discount rate: P_t = E_t[P_t^*] = D_t / (r - g)
    p_rational = d / (r_const - g_mean)

    # Observed market price P_t with time-varying discount rates / sentiment shocks
    # Real-world prices fluctuate much more violently around fundamentals
    sentiment = np.zeros(n_years)
    phi = 0.85
    sigma_s = 0.20
    for t in range(1, n_years):
        sentiment[t] = phi * sentiment[t - 1] + sigma_s * rng.standard_normal()
    p_market = p_rational * np.exp(sentiment)

    # Compute sample volatilities
    std_p_star = float(np.std(p_star, ddof=1))
    std_p_rational = float(np.std(p_rational, ddof=1))
    std_p_market = float(np.std(p_market, ddof=1))

    # Variance ratio: Var(P_market) / Var(P_star)
    var_ratio = (std_p_market**2) / (std_p_star**2)
    violates_bound = bool(std_p_market > std_p_star)

    print("Shiller (1981) Excess Volatility Test")
    print(f"Sample length T:             {n_years} years")
    print(f"Constant discount rate R:    {r_const * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Mean dividend growth g:      {g_mean * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Std dev of rational P*:      ${std_p_star:.2f}")
    print(f"Std dev of constant-R P:     ${std_p_rational:.2f}")
    print(f"Std dev of market P:         ${std_p_market:.2f}")
    print(f"Variance ratio Var(P)/Var(P*): {var_ratio:.4f}")
    print(f"Violates Shiller bound:      {violates_bound}")


main()

Variance ratio Var(P)/Var(P*): 9.2417

The numerical simulation confirms Shiller's theoretical bound. The ex-post rational price \(P_t^*\) smoothly integrates out dividend shocks across the entire horizon. Constant-discount-rate rational price \(P_t = \E_t[P_t^*]\) tracks the moving average. In contrast, market prices subject to discount-rate shocks exhibit a variance ratio \(\Var(P)/\Var(P^*) > 1\), demonstrating how time-varying discount rates generate excess volatility.

4Where the canon breaks

While Shiller's volatility test fundamentally reshaped finance, the early econometric tests suffered from major methodological vulnerabilities:

  • Unit roots and non-stationarity: Dividends and prices are non-stationary \(\mathcal{I}(1)\) processes. As shown by Marsh and Merton (1986), if dividends follow a geometric random walk with drift, sample variances of prices do not converge to population constants, and the naive variance bound inequality can appear violated even when the present-value model holds.
  • Terminal price sensitivity: Constructing \(P_t^*\) requires truncation at date \(T\), requiring an assumption for \(P_T^*\). Errors in terminal valuation propagate backward through the recursion.
  • Small-sample bias: Because prices and dividends are persistent, standard OLS-based estimators of variance bounds exhibit heavy finite-sample downward bias.

These econometric weaknesses led John Campbell and Robert Shiller to develop the log-linear present value framework (Lesson 2), which operates directly on stationary log returns and dividend yields without requiring non-stationary level series.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Excess volatility proves that financial markets are irrational and driven entirely by animal spirits.”

    Shiller's test refutes the joint hypothesis of rational expectations and constant discount rates. Time-varying risk premia in rational consumption-based asset pricing models (such as habit formation or long-run risks) naturally generate excess price volatility without any behavioral irrationality.

  2. “Because \(P_t^*\) contains future realized dividends, it must be more volatile than \(P_t\) in any finite sample.”

    Because \(P_t^*\) is an infinite discounted sum of future dividends, the discounting acts as a low-pass filter that averages out high-frequency fluctuations. The conditional expectation \(P_t = \E_t[P_t^*]\) is an orthogonal projection, which mathematically guarantees \(\Var(P_t) \le \Var(P_t^*)\) under constant discounting.

  3. “The variance bound \(\Var(P) \le \Var(P^*)\) holds even if the discount rate varies over time.”

    The variance inequality crucially requires constant discount rates. When discount rates vary over time, the stochastic discount factor covaries with dividends, breaking the orthogonality between the market price and future forecast errors.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate ex-post rational price recursion

Suppose the constant gross discount rate is \(R = 1.05\). The ex-post rational price at \(t=2\) is \(P_2^* = 100\), and the dividend paid at \(t=2\) is \(D_2 = 5\). Compute the ex-post rational price \(P_1^*\).

Solution

Using the backward recursion \(P_t^* = (P_{t+1}^* + D_{t+1}) / R\), we have \(P_1^* = (P_2^* + D_2) / 1.05 = (100 + 5) / 1.05 = 105 / 1.05 = 100.00\).

M2 · Variance bound calculation

Suppose the ex-post rational price has variance \(\Var(P^*) = 400\), and the market price forecast error has variance \(\Var(\varepsilon) = 250\). Under constant discount rates, what is the variance of the market price \(\Var(P)\)?

Solution

Under constant discount rates, the orthogonality condition implies \(\Var(P^*) = \Var(P) + \Var(\varepsilon)\). Thus, \(\Var(P) = \Var(P^*) - \Var(\varepsilon) = 400 - 250 = 150\). Notice that \(\Var(P) = 150 \le 400 = \Var(P^*)\), satisfying the theoretical bound.

M3 · Gordon growth baseline

A stock pays a current dividend of \(D_0 = 4.00\) that grows at a constant expected rate \(g = 0.02\) per year. If the constant discount rate is \(r = 0.07\), what is the rational present value price \(P_0\)?

Solution

By the Gordon growth formula, \(P_0 = D_1 / (r - g) = D_0 (1 + g) / (r - g) = 4.00 \times 1.02 / (0.07 - 0.02) = 4.08 / 0.05 = 81.60\).

Proof

P1 · Orthogonal projection property of conditional expectation

Prove that for any square-integrable random variables \(X\) and information set \(\mathcal{F}\), the variance decomposes as \(\Var(X) = \Var(\E[X \mid \mathcal{F}]) + \E[\Var(X \mid \mathcal{F})]\).

Solution

By the law of total variance, write \(X - \E[X] = (X - \E[X \mid \mathcal{F}]) + (\E[X \mid \mathcal{F}] - \E[X])\). Squaring both sides and taking expectations, the cross-product term is \(\E\big[(X - \E[X \mid \mathcal{F}])(\E[X \mid \mathcal{F}] - \E[X])\big] = \E\Big[\E\big[(X - \E[X \mid \mathcal{F}]) \mid \mathcal{F}\big](\E[X \mid \mathcal{F}] - \E[X])\Big] = 0\). Therefore, \(\Var(X) = \E\big[(X - \E[X \mid \mathcal{F}])^2\big] + \E\big[(\E[X \mid \mathcal{F}] - \E[X])^2\big] = \E[\Var(X \mid \mathcal{F})] + \Var(\E[X \mid \mathcal{F}])\). Since \(\E[\Var(X \mid \mathcal{F})] \ge 0\), this confirms that \(\Var(\E[X \mid \mathcal{F}]) \le \Var(X)\).

P2 · Bound violation implies discount rate variation

Prove that if \(\Var(P_t) > \Var(P_t^*)\), then expected returns \(\E_t[R_{t+1}]\) cannot be constant across time.

Solution

We prove the contrapositive. Assume expected returns are constant: \(\E_t[R_{t+1}] = R\). By Theorem 2.1, constant expected returns and no rational bubbles imply that \(P_t = \E_t[P_t^*]\), which mathematically requires \(\Var(P_t^*) = \Var(P_t) + \Var(P_t^* - P_t) \ge \Var(P_t)\). Thus, constant expected returns imply \(\Var(P_t) \le \Var(P_t^*)\). Therefore, if \(\Var(P_t) > \Var(P_t^*)\), expected returns must be time-varying.

P3 · Transversality condition

Show that if \(\lim_{H \to \infty} R^{-H} \E_t[P_{t+H}] = B_t \ne 0\), the market price contains a rational bubble component that satisfies \(\E_t[B_{t+1}] = R B_t\).

Solution

Under the difference equation \(P_t = R^{-1}\E_t[P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}]\), let \(P_t = P_t^f + B_t\), where \(P_t^f = \sum_{j=1}^\infty R^{-j}\E_t[D_{t+j}]\) is the fundamental price. Then \(P_t^f = R^{-1}\E_t[P_{t+1}^f + D_{t+1}]\). Subtracting this fundamental equation from the market price difference equation yields \(B_t = R^{-1}\E_t[B_{t+1}]\), which rearranges to \(\E_t[B_{t+1}] = R B_t\). Thus, any non-zero bubble grows at the gross riskless discount rate in expectation.

Empirical

E1 · Interpreting Shiller's empirical variance ratio

In Shiller's 1981 dataset, the sample standard deviation of detrended real stock prices was 50.1, whereas the sample standard deviation of the ex-post rational price was 8.96. Compute the empirical variance ratio and interpret.

Solution

The sample variance ratio is \(\widehat{\Var}(P) / \widehat{\Var}(P^*) = (50.1)^2 / (8.96)^2 = 2510.01 / 80.28 \approx 31.26\). This indicates that actual stock prices fluctuate with more than 31 times the variance allowed by the constant-discount-rate present-value model, providing an overwhelming rejection of constant discount rates.

E2 · Truncation sensitivity of ex-post rational prices

Explain why truncating the ex-post rational price recursion at the sample end \(T\) introduces sensitivity into historical values of \(P_t^*\).

Solution

Because \(P_t^* = \sum_{j=1}^{T-t} R^{-j} D_{t+j} + R^{-(T-t)} P_T^*\), the terminal value \(P_T^*\) receives weight \(R^{-(T-t)}\). For observations near the end of the sample (small \(T-t\)), the arbitrary terminal price assumption dominates the true future dividend stream, introducing substantial estimation bias into recent ex-post rational values.

E3 · Dividend smoothing by corporate managers

Corporate management smooths dividends over time relative to volatile corporate earnings. How does managerial dividend smoothing affect Shiller's variance bound test?

Solution

Managerial dividend smoothing artificially dampens the empirical volatility of the dividend series \(D_t\). Because \(P_t^*\) is constructed directly from smoothed dividends, \(\Var(P^*)\) is artificially depressed, widening the apparent gap between \(\Var(P)\) and \(\Var(P^*)\) and exacerbating the volatility puzzle.

Research

R1 · Marsh-Merton (1986) non-stationarity critique

Explain the core argument of Marsh and Merton (1986) regarding the application of sample variances to non-stationary dividend processes.

Solution

Marsh and Merton (1986) showed that if dividends follow a geometric random walk with drift, both dividends and prices are non-stationary integrated processes. In finite samples of non-stationary series, sample variances do not estimate population variances and depend on sample length and detrending methods. They constructed a counterexample where the present-value model holds exactly, yet Shiller's empirical test rejects it with near certainty due to unit-root bias.

R2 · Time-varying discount rates vs. irrational fads

Can excess volatility distinguish between rational time-varying risk aversion (e.g., habit formation) and irrational fads? Discuss the identification problem.

Solution

No. Excess volatility proves that prices fluctuate more than expected future dividends under constant discounting. Mathematically, price variation can be decomposed into cash-flow news and discount-rate news. A time-varying discount rate can arise from rational equilibrium risk premia (fluctuating marginal utility in habit models) or from behavioral sentiment and overreaction. Separating these two explanations requires testing auxiliary predictions on consumption data, survey expectations, and cross-sectional pricing.

R3 · Modern resolution of the Shiller puzzle

Summarize how modern asset pricing theory (Campbell-Shiller decomposition and Cochrane 2011) resolved the excess volatility puzzle.

Solution

Modern asset pricing resolved the puzzle by shifting focus from price levels to valuation ratios (dividend yields and price-earnings ratios). The Campbell-Shiller log-linearization demonstrated that 100% of the variance in the dividend-price ratio is attributable to variation in expected future returns (discount rates), with essentially 0% attributable to future dividend growth. Excess volatility is not noise; it reflects massive, systematic variation in market discount rates over business cycles.

  1. Under the efficient markets hypothesis with constant expected returns, the market price \(P_t\) equals…

    Options

    By iterated expectations and transversality, \(P_t = \sum_{j=1}^\infty R^{-j}\E_t[D_{t+j}] = \E_t\big[\sum_{j=1}^\infty R^{-j} D_{t+j}\big] = \E_t[P_t^*]\).

  2. Why does the variance bound \(\Var(P_t) \le \Var(P_t^*)\) hold under constant discount rates?

    Options

    The conditional expectation is an orthogonal projection: \(\Cov(P_t, P_t^* - P_t) = 0\), which forces \(\Var(P_t^*) = \Var(P_t) + \Var(P_t^* - P_t) \ge \Var(P_t)\).

  3. If the discount rate is \(R = 1.05\), \(P_{t+1}^* = 105.25\), and \(D_{t+1} = 5.0\), what is \(P_t^*\)?

    \(P_t^* = (P_{t+1}^* + D_{t+1})/R = (105.25 + 5.0)/1.05 = 110.25 / 1.05 = 105.0\).

  4. What was the primary empirical finding of Shiller (1981)?

    Options

    Shiller found that real stock prices had more than five times the standard deviation (and over 30 times the variance) of ex-post rational prices, violating the theoretical bound.

  5. What was the Marsh and Merton (1986) econometric critique of Shiller's test?

    Options

    Marsh and Merton demonstrated that if dividends are non-stationary \(\mathcal{I}(1)\) processes, sample variances depend on sample size and detrending filters, creating spurious rejections of the present value model.

  6. If \(\Var(P^*) = 500\) and \(\Var(P) = 200\), what is the variance of the forecast error \(\varepsilon = P^* - P\) under constant discounting?

    \(\Var(\varepsilon) = \Var(P^*) - \Var(P) = 500 - 200 = 300\).

  7. How does modern asset pricing explain excess volatility in stock prices?

    Options

    Modern finance attributes price swings almost entirely to discount-rate variation: investors demand higher expected returns during recessions and lower returns during expansions.

  8. What happens to the Shiller bound if the discount rate varies stochastically over time?

    Options

    Stochastic discount rates introduce covariance between the SDF and dividends, invalidating the simple orthogonal projection \(P_t = \E_t[P_t^*]\).
