The CAPE Ratio and Long-Horizon Swings

Annual corporate earnings fluctuate violently with the business cycle, collapsing during recessions and surging in booms. To overcome this cyclical distortion, Campbell and Shiller (1988, 1998) introduced the Cyclically Adjusted Price-to-Earnings (CAPE) ratio, dividing real stock prices by a 10-year moving average of real corporate earnings. Over a century and a half of US market history, CAPE has exhibited powerful mean reversion, forecasting subsequent 10-year annualized real stock returns with \(R^2\) exceeding 35%. Understanding CAPE is essential for quantifying long-run equity risk premia and identifying historic market bubbles.

1Construction of the Shiller CAPE ratio

Single-year trailing earnings \(E_t\) are highly noisy and can become zero or negative during severe economic contractions (e.g., Q4 2008). Taking the natural logarithm of negative earnings is impossible. Benjamin Graham and David Dodd (1934) originally recommended averaging earnings over seven to ten years to gauge fundamental corporate earning power.

Definition 1.1 (cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio)

Let \(P_t\) be the real (inflation-adjusted) price of an equity index at month \(t\), and let \(E_{t-s}\) be real quarterly earnings per share. The cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio, also denoted P/E 10 or the Shiller P/E, is defined as

\[ \text{CAPE}_t = \frac{P_t}{\frac{1}{10}\sum_{j=0}^9 E_{t-j}^{\text{real}}} = \frac{P_t}{\bar{E}_{10, t}}. \]

Smoothing earnings over 10 years effectively filters out the temporary swings of the business cycle. In the Campbell-Shiller present-value identity, log CAPE, \(p_t - \bar{e}_{10,t}\), serves as a robust proxy for valuation that avoids the distortion of corporate payout channel choices.

2Ten-year predictive regressions and mean reversion

Let \(r_{t \to t+10} = \frac{1}{10}\ln(P_{t+10}/P_t) + \bar{y}_{\text{div}}\) denote the annualized 10-year real compound return. The long-horizon predictive regression is

\[ r_{t \to t+10} = \alpha + \beta\,\ln(\text{CAPE}_t) + u_{t+10}. \]
Proposition 2.1 (long-horizon predictive power of CAPE)

In US equity data from 1881 to the present, the predictive regression of 10-year future real stock returns on \(\ln(\text{CAPE}_t)\) yields a statistically significant negative slope \(\hat{\beta} \approx -0.05\) to \(-0.06\) with an \(R^2\) between 30% and 45%.

This negative slope reflects powerful mean reversion: when CAPE is high (stocks are expensive relative to 10-year earnings), subsequent 10-year real returns are low. When CAPE is low (stocks are depressed), subsequent 10-year returns are elevated. The historical distribution of CAPE features notable benchmarks:

3Inference with overlapping returns: Hansen-Hodrick

When measuring 10-year returns sampled annually or monthly, consecutive observations share multiple years of identical returns. The forecast errors \(u_{t+10}\) and \(u_{t+10+j}\) follow a moving average MA(\(H-1\)) process with non-zero autocorrelation up to lag \(H-1\).

Theorem 3.1 (Hansen-Hodrick 1980 standard error estimator)

Under the null of no extra-model autocorrelation, the Hansen-Hodrick estimator for the asymptotic covariance of \(\hat{\beta}\) with overlap horizon \(H\) is

\[ \widehat{\Var}_{\text{HH}}(\hat{\beta}) = \frac{\hat{\Omega}_{\text{HH}}}{(S_{xx})^2}, \qquad \hat{\Omega}_{\text{HH}} = \sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t^2 u_t^2 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{H-1} \sum_{t=j+1}^T (\tilde{x}_t u_t)(\tilde{x}_{t-j} u_{t-j}). \]

Unlike the Newey-West estimator, which applies Bartlett linear decay weights \((1 - j/(L+1))\) to guarantee positive semi-definiteness, Hansen-Hodrick applies uniform weights of 1.0 to the first \(H-1\) sample autocovariances, exactly matching the theoretical MA(\(H-1\)) error structure of multi-period forecasts.

Lab 1 · Shiller CAPE and 10-Year Return Predictability

Constructing a 10-year moving average of simulated real earnings smooths cyclical noise. Regressing annualized 10-year real stock returns on \(\ln(\text{CAPE})\) will produce a statistically significant negative slope (\(t_{\text{HH}} < -2.5\)) and an \(R^2\) exceeding 30%, confirming the empirical power of valuation ratios at decadal horizons.

"""Lab AP5-L07-1: Cyclically Adjusted Price-Earnings (CAPE) and 10-Year Return Predictability.

SIMULATED: generates real earnings and stock price series to construct Shiller's CAPE ratio
and estimate 10-year predictive regressions with Hansen-Hodrick overlapping error adjustments.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def hansen_hodrick_se(x: np.ndarray, e: np.ndarray, overlap: int) -> float:
    """Compute Hansen-Hodrick (1980) standard error for overlapping returns."""
    x_mean = np.mean(x)
    x_dev = x - x_mean
    sxx = float(np.sum(x_dev**2))

    u = x_dev * e
    omega = float(np.sum(u**2))
    for j in range(1, overlap):
        gamma = float(np.sum(u[j:] * u[:-j]))
        omega += 2.0 * gamma

    var_beta = omega / (sxx**2)
    return float(np.sqrt(max(1e-12, var_beta)))


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(1881)
    n_total = 100  # 100 years of annual data
    horizon = 10  # 10-year horizon

    # Simulate real earnings with business cycle volatility and steady trend
    g_e = 0.02  # 2% real trend earnings growth
    sigma_e = 0.14  # annual earnings volatility
    delta_e = g_e + sigma_e * rng.standard_normal(n_total)
    earnings = np.zeros(n_total)
    earnings[0] = 5.0
    for t in range(1, n_total):
        earnings[t] = earnings[t - 1] * np.exp(delta_e[t])

    # 10-year moving average earnings E10_t
    e10 = np.zeros(n_total)
    for t in range(9, n_total):
        e10[t] = np.mean(earnings[t - 9 : t + 1])

    # Valuation multiple (log CAPE): mean approx ln(18) = 2.89, persistent AR(1)
    phi_cape = 0.88
    cape_mean_log = np.log(18.0)
    sigma_cape = 0.22
    log_cape = np.zeros(n_total)
    log_cape[9] = cape_mean_log
    for t in range(10, n_total):
        log_cape[t] = cape_mean_log + phi_cape * (log_cape[t - 1] - cape_mean_log) + sigma_cape * rng.standard_normal()

    # Reconstruct stock prices: P_t = E10_t * exp(log_cape_t)
    price = np.zeros(n_total)
    for t in range(9, n_total):
        price[t] = e10[t] * np.exp(log_cape[t])

    # Compute 10-year annualized return: r_{t->t+10} = (ln(P_{t+10}) - ln(P_t)) / 10 + avg_dividend_yield
    valid_t = n_total - horizon - 9
    ret_10y = np.zeros(valid_t)
    x_cape = np.zeros(valid_t)

    div_yield = 0.035  # 3.5% constant average dividend yield component
    for i, t in enumerate(range(9, n_total - horizon)):
        ret_10y[i] = (np.log(price[t + horizon]) - np.log(price[t])) / horizon + div_yield
        x_cape[i] = log_cape[t]

    # OLS Regression: ret_10y = alpha + beta * x_cape + e
    x_mean = float(np.mean(x_cape))
    y_mean = float(np.mean(ret_10y))
    sxx = float(np.sum((x_cape - x_mean) ** 2))
    sxy = float(np.sum((x_cape - x_mean) * (ret_10y - y_mean)))
    syy = float(np.sum((ret_10y - y_mean) ** 2))

    beta_ols = sxy / sxx
    alpha_ols = y_mean - beta_ols * x_mean
    r2 = (sxy**2) / (sxx * syy)

    resids = ret_10y - (alpha_ols + beta_ols * x_cape)
    se_hh = hansen_hodrick_se(x_cape, resids, overlap=horizon)
    t_hh = beta_ols / se_hh

    # Summary statistics of CAPE
    cape_levels = np.exp(x_cape)
    mean_cape = float(np.mean(cape_levels))
    min_cape = float(np.min(cape_levels))
    max_cape = float(np.max(cape_levels))

    print("Shiller CAPE 10-Year Return Predictive Regression")
    print(f"Sample length:               {valid_t} overlapping 10-year windows")
    print(f"Mean CAPE level:             {mean_cape:.2f}")
    print(f"Min / Max CAPE level:        {min_cape:.2f} / {max_cape:.2f}")
    print(f"Predictive slope (annual):   {beta_ols:.4f}")
    print(f"Hansen-Hodrick SE (t-stat):  {se_hh:.4f} ({t_hh:.2f})")
    print(f"Regression R^2:              {r2 * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"10-year expected return gap: {abs(beta_ols * (np.log(30) - np.log(12))) * 100:.2f}% per year")


main()

The lab demonstrates the empirical strength of the Shiller CAPE predictive regression. While 1-year returns are dominated by noise, 10-year annualized returns are strongly forecastable by the log CAPE ratio (\(R^2 > 35\%\)). 4Where the canon breaks

Despite its widespread adoption by institutional investors, relying on historical CAPE benchmarks presents serious practical challenges:

  • Structural shifts in accounting standards: Changes in US GAAP (such as FASB rule changes regarding goodwill amortization, mark-to-market accounting, and intangible assets) cause reported earnings to be systematically lower and more volatile post-1990 than in pre-1980 data. As shown by Siegel (2016), using NIPA (National Income and Product Accounts) economic profits reduces apparent modern overvaluation.
  • Permanent secular trend in valuation multiples: Over the past 30 years (1995–2025), CAPE has averaged approximately 27, well above its 1881–1990 average of 15. Investors who treated 15 as an immutable mean reversion anchor remained permanently under-invested in equities during one of history's greatest bull markets.
  • Low frequency of independent observations: A 140-year sample provides only 14 non-overlapping 10-year periods. Statistical degrees of freedom are limited, raising the risk of small-sample macroeconomic regime confounding.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “A high CAPE ratio means that a stock market crash is imminent within the next 12 months.”

    CAPE has essentially zero predictive power for 1-year returns. During the Dot-Com bubble, CAPE crossed its historic peak of 33 in 1997, yet the market continued to surge for three more years, peaking at 44.19 in late 1999. CAPE forecasts 10-year average returns, not short-term market timing.

  2. “CAPE can be calculated using 10 years of nominal stock prices and nominal earnings.”

    CAPE strictly requires inflation-adjusted (real) values. Because inflation compounds over a decade, comparing current nominal prices with nominal earnings from nine years prior would compare deflated historical dollars with modern inflated dollars, producing severe distortion.

  3. “When CAPE is 35, the expected 10-year stock return must be negative.”

    High CAPE implies lower-than-average expected returns (e.g., 2% to 4% real instead of the historical 6.8% real), but rarely guarantees a negative nominal or real return, because trend corporate earnings growth and dividend payouts continue to accrue over the decade.

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate 10-year real smoothed earnings and CAPE

A stock index trades at real price \(P_t = 3600\). Real earnings over the past 10 years are: 100, 110, 105, 120, 115, 130, 125, 140, 135, 150. Calculate the 10-year smoothed earnings \(\bar{E}_{10,t}\) and the current CAPE ratio.

Solution

The sum of real earnings is \(100 + 110 + 105 + 120 + 115 + 130 + 125 + 140 + 135 + 150 = 1230\). The 10-year average is \(\bar{E}_{10,t} = 1230 / 10 = 123.0\). The CAPE ratio is \(\text{CAPE}_t = P_t / \bar{E}_{10,t} = 3600 / 123.0 \approx 29.268\).

M2 · Expected 10-year return forecast from regression

Suppose the estimated predictive regression is \(r_{t \to t+10} = 0.22 - 0.055\,\ln(\text{CAPE}_t)\). If current CAPE is 30, compute the expected annualized 10-year real return.

Solution

\(\ln(\text{CAPE}) = \ln(30) \approx 3.4012\). The expected return is \(r_{t \to t+10} = 0.22 - 0.055 \times 3.4012 = 0.22 - 0.187066 = 0.032934\) (approximately \(3.29\%\) per year in real terms).

M3 · Valuation gap between market trough and market peak

Using the regression in M2, compute the difference in expected annualized 10-year return between an undervalued market with CAPE = 12 and an overvalued market with CAPE = 36.

Solution

The difference is \(\Delta r = -0.055 \times [\ln(36) - \ln(12)] = -0.055 \times \ln(36 / 12) = -0.055 \times \ln(3) = -0.055 \times 1.098612 \approx -0.060424\). The undervalued market offers approximately \(6.04\%\) higher annualized return per year for the next ten years.

Proof

P1 · Moving average error structure of overlapping forecasts

Let \(r_{t \to t+H} = \sum_{j=1}^H r_{t+j}\). If 1-period innovations \(\epsilon_{t+j}\) are i.i.d. with variance \(\sigma^2\), prove that the forecast errors \(u_t = \sum_{j=1}^H \epsilon_{t+j}\) have zero autocorrelation at lags \(k \ge H\), and find \(\Cov(u_t, u_{t-k})\) for \(0 \le k < H\).

Solution

For \(0 \le k < H\), write \(u_t = \sum_{j=1}^H \epsilon_{t+j}\) and \(u_{t-k} = \sum_{m=1}^H \epsilon_{t-k+m}\). The terms overlap for indices between \(t+1\) and \(t-k+H\), containing exactly \(H - k\) common i.i.d. innovations. Since \(\E[\epsilon_s \epsilon_l] = \sigma^2\) for \(s = l\) and \(0\) otherwise, \(\Cov(u_t, u_{t-k}) = \sum_{s=t+1}^{t+H-k} \Var(\epsilon_s) = (H - k)\sigma^2\). For \(k \ge H\), the two sums share no common innovations, so \(\Cov(u_t, u_{t-k}) = 0\). This proves that the overlapping error follows an exact MA(\(H-1\)) process.

P2 · Consistency of Hansen-Hodrick estimator under MA(H-1)

Prove that under the null hypothesis of correct conditional expectation, \(\E[u_{t+H} \mid x_t] = 0\), the Hansen-Hodrick estimator \(\hat{\Omega}_{\text{HH}}\) converges in probability to the true asymptotic variance matrix of the score.

Solution

The score vector is \(s_t = \tilde{x}_t u_{t+H}\). Its autocovariances are \(\Gamma_j = \E[s_t s_{t-j}^\top]\). From Problem P1, for \(j \ge H\), \(\E[u_{t+H} u_{t+H-j} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-j}] = 0\), so \(\Gamma_j = 0\). Therefore, the spectral density at frequency zero is \(\Omega = \Gamma_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{H-1} (\Gamma_j + \Gamma_j^\top)\). By the ergodic theorem for stationary processes with finite fourth moments, the sample autocovariances \(\hat{\Gamma}_j = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=j+1}^T s_t s_{t-j}^\top \xrightarrow{p} \Gamma_j\). Because the sum is finite (truncated at fixed \(H-1\)), \(\hat{\Omega}_{\text{HH}} = \hat{\Gamma}_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{H-1} (\hat{\Gamma}_j + \hat{\Gamma}_j^\top) \xrightarrow{p} \Omega\).

P3 · Why Hansen-Hodrick can fail to be positive semi-definite

Explain why the Hansen-Hodrick matrix \(\hat{\Omega}_{\text{HH}}\) can produce negative variance estimates in small samples, whereas Newey-West is guaranteed positive semi-definite.

Solution

The Hansen-Hodrick estimator uses a rectangular spectral window with weights \(w_j = 1\) for \(j < H\) and \(0\) for \(j \ge H\). The Fourier transform of a rectangular window is the Dirichlet kernel, which takes negative values across certain frequencies. In finite samples with strong sample autocorrelations, this can make the estimated spectrum at frequency zero negative. Newey-West uses the triangular Bartlett kernel \(w_j = 1 - j/(L+1)\), whose Fourier transform is the Fejér kernel, which is strictly non-negative everywhere, mathematically guaranteeing that the estimated covariance matrix is positive semi-definite.

Empirical

E1 · The Dot-Com bubble peak in 1999–2000

In December 1999, the S&P 500 CAPE ratio reached an all-time record of 44.19. What was the subsequent 10-year annualized real return of the S&P 500 from 2000 to 2010?

Solution

From December 1999 to December 2009 (the "Lost Decade"), the S&P 500 experienced two major bear markets (the 2000–2002 tech crash and the 2008 global financial crisis). The annualized compound real return over this 10-year period was approximately \(-3.4\%\) per year, exactly in line with the dismal returns predicted by the extreme valuation of CAPE = 44.

E2 · Siegel's (2016) NIPA earnings critique

Jeremy Siegel (2016) argued that modern accounting rules artificially depress reported earnings during recessions. What modification did he propose to the denominator of CAPE?

Solution

Siegel proposed replacing GAAP reported earnings with S&P operating earnings or NIPA (National Income and Product Accounts) economic corporate profits compiled by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). Under GAAP rules, companies must take massive asset write-downs during recessions, which depresses reported earnings without reflecting cash generation. Using NIPA profits eliminates non-cash write-downs, lowering the adjusted CAPE and providing a more stable valuation benchmark.

E3 · International cross-sectional CAPE investing

In quantitative global asset allocation, how is CAPE used in cross-sectional equity country selection, and what are its empirical results?

Solution

Country allocation strategies sort 30–40 global national equity markets by their CAPE ratios, going long the quintile of lowest-CAPE countries and short (or underweight) the highest-CAPE countries. Empirical studies (e.g., Faber 2012) show that value-sorted country portfolios earn a significant return spread of 4% to 6% per year over market cap weights, proving that mean reversion in valuation multiples operates globally across national indices.

Research

R1 · Excess CAPE Yield (ECY) and interest rate conditioning

Shiller introduced the Excess CAPE Yield (ECY), defined as \(\text{ECY}_t = \frac{1}{\text{CAPE}_t} - r_{10, t}^{\text{real}}\). Explain why this metric conditions on real bond yields.

Solution

The raw earnings yield \(1/\text{CAPE}\) measures the real return on equity if valuations remain constant. However, equities compete with risk-free sovereign bonds. In a world of secularly low real interest rates (e.g., post-2008), a CAPE of 30 implies an earnings yield of 3.33%. If real 10-year Treasury yields are 0.5%, the equity risk premium over bonds (\(\text{ECY} = 3.33\% - 0.5\% = 2.83\%\)) remains healthy, explaining why high CAPE multiples can persist in low-rate regimes without creating an immediate crash.

R2 · Total return CAPE (TR-CAPE) and share buybacks

How does the total return CAPE (TR-CAPE) constructed by Asness et al. adjust for the shift from cash dividends to share repurchases?

Solution

When firms repurchase shares instead of paying dividends, cash is distributed without reducing share count in price-weighted indices in the same manner. Total return CAPE reinvests all cash distributions (dividends and net repurchases) into the price series and scales historical earnings per share by the accumulation factor of total return, preventing buyback-induced earnings per share growth from artificially distorting the 10-year average.

R3 · Machine learning vs. decadal CAPE

Can complex non-linear machine learning models improve upon the 10-year forecasting performance of simple log CAPE?

Solution

At 10-year horizons, the effective sample size is very small (10 to 14 independent business cycles). Highly parameterized machine learning models (e.g., deep neural networks) easily overfit historical noise and regime idiosyncrasies. Simple parsimonious linear models based on CAPE or valuation multiples have consistently matched or outperformed complex ML architectures out-of-sample at decadal horizons, demonstrating the virtue of economic parsimony.
