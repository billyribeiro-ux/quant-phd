Predictive Regressions and Stambaugh Bias

  • Introduces: dividend-price predictive regression
  • Ttheorem

  • Bpublished, not settled

Forecasting stock returns using the dividend-price ratio is the central empirical test of discount-rate variation. In full-sample regressions, the dividend yield predicts annual stock returns with positive coefficients and \(t\)-statistics exceeding 2.0. However, the econometrician faces a subtle hazard: the predictor variable is highly persistent, and its innovations are heavily negatively correlated with return innovations. Stambaugh (1999) proved that this correlation generates a severe finite-sample upward bias in the OLS slope, inflating test statistics and producing spurious evidence of return predictability. Disentangling true predictability from Stambaugh bias is essential for quantitative forecasting.

1The predictive regression framework

Let \(r_{t+1}\) be the log return from date \(t\) to \(t+1\), and let \(x_t\) be a predetermined financial predictor observed at date \(t\), such as the log dividend-price ratio \(\delta_t = d_t - p_t\).

Definition 1.1 (dividend-price predictive regression)

A dividend-price predictive regression is the linear projection of future returns on the current log dividend-price ratio:

\[ r_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta\,x_t + u_{t+1}, \qquad \E[u_{t+1} \mid x_t] = 0, \]

where the predictor \(x_t\) follows a stationary first-order autoregressive process

\[ x_{t+1} = \mu + \phi\,x_t + v_{t+1}, \qquad |\phi| < 1, \]

and the bivariate disturbance vector \(\begin{pmatrix} u_{t+1} \\ v_{t+1} \end{pmatrix}\) is i.i.d. with covariance matrix \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_u^2 & \sigma_{uv} \\ \sigma_{uv} & \sigma_v^2 \end{pmatrix}\).

In empirical equity data, two crucial stylized facts emerge:

  1. Near unit-root persistence: Valuation ratios are extraordinarily persistent, with monthly autoregressive parameter \(\phi \approx 0.99\) and annual \(\phi \approx 0.90\).
  2. Strong negative innovation correlation: Because a contemporaneous price surge increases return \(r_{t+1}\) while decreasing the dividend yield \(x_{t+1}\), the correlation \(\rho_{uv} = \sigma_{uv} / (\sigma_u \sigma_v)\) is strongly negative, typically between \(-0.70\) and \(-0.90\).

2Kendall's bias and the Stambaugh small-sample bias

In a pure AR(1) process \(x_{t+1} = \mu + \phi\,x_t + v_{t+1}\), the OLS estimator \(\hat{\phi}\) is biased downward in finite samples of size \(T\). Kendall (1954) derived the first-order asymptotic bias:

\[ \E[\hat{\phi} - \phi] \approx -\frac{1 + 3\phi}{T}. \]
Theorem 2.1 (Stambaugh 1999 analytical bias)

Under the predictive system of Definition 1.1 with fixed or stationary regressors, the finite-sample bias of the predictive regression slope \(\hat{\beta}\) is proportional to the estimation bias of the autoregressive parameter \(\hat{\phi}\):

\[ \E[\hat{\beta} - \beta] = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\,\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi] \approx -\left(\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\right)\frac{1 + 3\phi}{T}. \]
Proof

Decompose the return innovation \(u_{t+1}\) into its projection on the predictor innovation \(v_{t+1}\) and an orthogonal component \(\xi_{t+1}\):

\[ u_{t+1} = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\,v_{t+1} + \xi_{t+1}, \qquad \Cov(v_{t+1}, \xi_{t+1}) = 0. \]

Let \(\tilde{x}_t = x_t - \bar{x}\) and \(\tilde{r}_{t+1} = r_{t+1} - \bar{r}\). The OLS slope estimator is

\[ \hat{\beta} - \beta = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t u_{t+1}}{\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t^2} = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t v_{t+1}}{\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t^2}\right) + \frac{\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t \xi_{t+1}}{\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t^2}. \]

Because \(\xi_{t+1}\) is independent of both \(x_t\) and past innovations, the expectation of the second term is identically zero. The term in parentheses is precisely the estimation error of the autoregressive coefficient: \(\hat{\phi} - \phi = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t v_{t+1}}{\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t^2}\). Taking expectations yields

\[ \E[\hat{\beta} - \beta] = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\,\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi]. \]

Substituting Kendall's formula \(\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi] \approx -(1 + 3\phi)/T\) completes the proof.

Because \(\sigma_{uv} < 0\), the product \(-(\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2)\) is strictly positive. Thus, the OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}\) is biased upward: even if true predictability is zero (\(\beta = 0\)), OLS regression will estimate a positive slope \(\hat{\beta} > 0\).

3Inference with Newey-West standard errors

When returns are measured over multi-period horizons (\(k\) periods ahead), forecast errors overlap, creating moving-average serial correlation of order \(k-1\). Even in one-period regressions, residuals can exhibit heteroskedasticity. Standard OLS standard errors \(\text{SE}_{\text{OLS}} = \hat{\sigma}/\sqrt{S_{xx}}\) are severely biased downward.

Definition 3.1 (Newey-West HAC covariance estimator)

The Heteroskedasticity and Autocorrelation Consistent (HAC) covariance estimator (Newey & West, 1987; Hansen, 1982) with Bartlett kernel weights and truncation lag \(L\) is

\[ \widehat{\Var}_{\text{NW}}(\hat{\beta}) = \frac{\hat{\Omega}}{(S_{xx})^2}, \qquad \hat{\Omega} = \sum_{t=1}^T u_t^2 \tilde{x}_t^2 + 2\sum_{j=1}^L \left(1 - \frac{j}{L + 1}\right) \sum_{t=j+1}^T (\tilde{x}_t u_t)(\tilde{x}_{t-j} u_{t-j}). \]

Lab 1 · Predictive Regressions and Stambaugh Small-Sample Bias

Under the sharp null hypothesis of zero predictability (\(\beta = 0\)), when a persistent predictor (\(\phi = 0.95\)) has innovations negatively correlated with return errors (\(\rho_{uv} = -0.85\)), finite-sample OLS will produce a strictly positive slope \(\hat{\beta} > 0\). The analytical Stambaugh correction aligns with the sample bias and restores valid inference.

"""Lab AP5-L03-1: Predictive Regressions and Stambaugh Small-Sample Bias.

SIMULATED: generates predictive system under the null of no predictability (beta=0)
with a persistent predictor and negatively correlated innovations to quantify
the Stambaugh (1999) small-sample upward bias.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def newey_west_se(x: np.ndarray, e: np.ndarray, max_lags: int = 4) -> float:
    """Compute Newey-West HAC standard error for univariate OLS slope."""
    x_mean = np.mean(x)
    x_dev = x - x_mean
    sxx = np.sum(x_dev**2)

    # Autocovariance terms: gamma_j = sum_{t=j+1}^T (x_dev_t * e_t) * (x_dev_{t-j} * e_{t-j})
    u = x_dev * e
    omega = np.sum(u**2)
    for j in range(1, max_lags + 1):
        weight = 1.0 - j / (max_lags + 1.0)
        gamma = np.sum(u[j:] * u[:-j])
        omega += 2.0 * weight * gamma

    var_beta = omega / (sxx**2)
    return float(np.sqrt(max(1e-12, var_beta)))


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
    n_obs = 200  # 200 periods (e.g. 50 years of quarterly data)
    phi = 0.95  # highly persistent valuation ratio (AR(1) parameter)
    mu = 0.05 * (1.0 - phi)
    beta_true = 0.0  # true slope is exactly zero under the null hypothesis

    # Covariance structure of innovations: [u, v]
    # u: return innovation (std = 0.18)
    # v: predictor innovation (std = 0.08)
    # corr(u, v) = -0.85 (price increase raises return and lowers dividend-price ratio)
    sigma_u = 0.18
    sigma_v = 0.08
    rho_uv = -0.85
    cov_uv = rho_uv * sigma_u * sigma_v

    cov_matrix = np.array([[sigma_u**2, cov_uv], [cov_uv, sigma_v**2]])
    innovations = rng.multivariate_normal(mean=[0.0, 0.0], cov=cov_matrix, size=n_obs + 1)
    u = innovations[:, 0]
    v = innovations[:, 1]

    # Generate predictor path x_t
    x = np.zeros(n_obs + 1)
    x[0] = mu / (1.0 - phi)
    for t in range(n_obs):
        x[t + 1] = mu + phi * x[t] + v[t + 1]

    # Generate returns r_{t+1} = alpha + beta_true * x_t + u_{t+1}
    alpha = 0.02
    r = alpha + beta_true * x[:-1] + u[1:]
    x_pred = x[:-1]

    # Estimate OLS: r_{t+1} = a + b * x_t + e_{t+1}
    x_mean = float(np.mean(x_pred))
    r_mean = float(np.mean(r))
    sxx = float(np.sum((x_pred - x_mean) ** 2))
    sxy = float(np.sum((x_pred - x_mean) * (r - r_mean)))
    b_ols = sxy / sxx
    a_ols = r_mean - b_ols * x_mean

    residuals = r - (a_ols + b_ols * x_pred)
    sigma2_e = float(np.sum(residuals**2) / (n_obs - 2))
    se_ols = float(np.sqrt(sigma2_e / sxx))
    t_ols = b_ols / se_ols

    # Newey-West standard error with 4 lags
    se_nw = newey_west_se(x_pred, residuals, max_lags=4)
    t_nw = b_ols / se_nw

    # Estimate sample AR(1) of predictor: x_{t+1} = m + phi_hat * x_t + v_hat_{t+1}
    x_lag = x[:-1]
    x_lead = x[1:]
    s_lag_lag = float(np.sum((x_lag - np.mean(x_lag)) ** 2))
    s_lag_lead = float(np.sum((x_lag - np.mean(x_lag)) * (x_lead - np.mean(x_lead))))
    phi_hat = s_lag_lead / s_lag_lag
    v_hat = x_lead - (np.mean(x_lead) - phi_hat * np.mean(x_lag) + phi_hat * x_lag)

    # Sample covariance of residuals: cov(e, v_hat)
    sigma_uv_sample = float(np.mean(residuals * v_hat))
    sigma_v2_sample = float(np.mean(v_hat**2))

    # Analytical Stambaugh (1999) bias: E[b - beta] approx - (sigma_uv / sigma_v^2) * (1 + 3*phi) / T
    stambaugh_bias_analytical = -(sigma_uv_sample / sigma_v2_sample) * ((1.0 + 3.0 * phi_hat) / n_obs)
    b_corrected = b_ols - stambaugh_bias_analytical
    t_corrected = b_corrected / se_nw

    print("Stambaugh (1999) Predictive Regression Bias Analysis")
    print(f"Sample length T:             {n_obs}")
    print(f"True predictive slope beta:  {beta_true:.4f}")
    print(f"Predictor AR(1) phi (true):  {phi:.4f}")
    print(f"Predictor AR(1) phi (est):   {phi_hat:.4f}")
    print(f"Correlation corr(u, v):      {rho_uv:.4f}")
    print(f"Naive OLS slope b_ols:       {b_ols:.4f}")
    print(f"OLS std error (t-stat):      {se_ols:.4f} ({t_ols:.2f})")
    print(f"Newey-West std error (t):    {se_nw:.4f} ({t_nw:.2f})")
    print(f"Analytical Stambaugh bias:   {stambaugh_bias_analytical:.4f}")
    print(f"Bias-corrected slope b_adj:  {b_corrected:.4f}")
    print(f"Bias-corrected t-stat:       {t_corrected:.2f}")


main()

The lab demonstrates the econometric hazard of predictive regressions. Although true \(\beta = 0\), naive OLS estimates a positive coefficient with an inflated \(t\)-statistic. Calculating the analytical Stambaugh bias reveals that the entire estimated magnitude is attributable to Kendall autoregressive bias translated through the negative innovation correlation \(\rho_{uv}\). Subtracting Stambaugh's bias collapses the slope back to zero.

4Where the canon breaks

While Stambaugh's formula is a vital first-order correction, it fails in important practical circumstances:

  • Near-unit-root non-standard asymptotics: When \(\phi\) is very close to 1 (e.g. \(\phi = 1 - c/T\)), Kendall's \(1/T\) expansion breaks down. As shown by Cavanagh, Elliott, and Stock (1995) and Campbell and Yogo (2006), the distribution of the \(t\)-statistic is non-standard and depends on the local-to-unity parameter \(c\).
  • Overlapping returns at long horizons: In multi-year regressions (\(k = 5\) or \(10\) years), the degree of overlap is large relative to sample size \(T/k\). As shown by Boudoukh, Richardson, and Whitelaw (2008), Newey-West standard errors severely understate sampling error when the horizon is a substantial fraction of the sample.
  • Lewellen's exact bounds: Lewellen (2004) showed that if the researcher knows that \(\phi \le 1\), this prior restriction provides powerful lower bounds on \(\beta\) that can preserve predictability even after correcting for Stambaugh bias.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “A regression \(t\)-statistic of 2.2 using Newey-West standard errors guarantees statistical significance at the 5% level.”

    Newey-West standard errors correct for serial correlation and heteroskedasticity, but they do not correct for Stambaugh small-sample bias. When \(\phi \approx 0.95\) and \(\rho_{uv} \approx -0.85\), the true 5% critical value for the \(t\)-statistic can exceed 3.0 rather than 1.96.

  2. “Stambaugh bias occurs because the market is inefficient.”

    Stambaugh bias is purely a finite-sample econometric property of OLS in dynamic systems with correlated innovations. It exists in simulated data where the null hypothesis of no predictability is true by construction.

  3. “If dividend yield does not predict one-year returns, it cannot predict long-horizon returns.”

    Because the dividend yield is persistent, small one-year predictive power compounds over time. An annual \(R^2\) of only 5% can accumulate into an \(R^2\) of 30% to 40% at five- to ten-year horizons.

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate Kendall autoregressive bias

A valuation ratio has true AR(1) parameter \(\phi = 0.92\). In a sample of \(T = 150\) observations, calculate Kendall's expected small-sample bias \(\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi]\) and the expected estimated \(\hat{\phi}\).

Solution

Kendall's formula gives \(\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi] \approx -(1 + 3\phi)/T = -(1 + 3 \times 0.92) / 150 = -(1 + 2.76) / 150 = -3.76 / 150 \approx -0.025067\). The expected estimated coefficient is \(\E[\hat{\phi}] = \phi + \E[\hat{\phi} - \phi] = 0.92 - 0.025067 \approx 0.894933\).

M2 · Calculate analytical Stambaugh bias

In the setting of M1 (\(\phi = 0.92, T = 150\)), suppose \(\sigma_u = 0.18, \sigma_v = 0.09\), and \(\rho_{uv} = -0.80\). Compute the analytical Stambaugh bias \(\E[\hat{\beta} - \beta]\).

Solution

The innovation covariance is \(\sigma_{uv} = \rho_{uv}\sigma_u\sigma_v = -0.80 \times 0.18 \times 0.09 = -0.01296\). The variance is \(\sigma_v^2 = (0.09)^2 = 0.0081\). The regression multiplier is \(\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2 = -0.01296 / 0.0081 = -1.60\). From M1, \(\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi] \approx -0.025067\). By Stambaugh's formula, \(\E[\hat{\beta} - \beta] = (\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2)\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi] = (-1.60) \times (-0.025067) \approx +0.040107\).

M3 · Bias-corrected t-statistic

Suppose OLS estimates \(\hat{\beta} = 0.075\) with Newey-West standard error \(\text{SE} = 0.025\). If the Stambaugh bias is \(+0.040\), compute the naive and bias-corrected \(t\)-statistics.

Solution

The naive \(t\)-statistic is \(t_{\text{naive}} = \hat{\beta} / \text{SE} = 0.075 / 0.025 = 3.00\). The bias-corrected slope is \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{adj}} = \hat{\beta} - \text{Bias} = 0.075 - 0.040 = 0.035\). The bias-corrected \(t\)-statistic is \(t_{\text{adj}} = 0.035 / 0.025 = 1.40\). Notice how correcting for Stambaugh bias reduces a seemingly highly significant result (\(t = 3.00\)) to insignificance (\(t = 1.40 < 1.96\)).

Proof

P1 · Independence of orthogonalized disturbance

Prove that if \(\begin{pmatrix} u_t \\ v_t \end{pmatrix}\) is bivariate normal, then \(\xi_t = u_t - (\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2)v_t\) is statistically independent of \(v_t\).

Solution

Because \(u_t\) and \(v_t\) are jointly normal, any linear combination of \(u_t\) and \(v_t\) is jointly normal with \(v_t\). Compute the covariance: \(\Cov(\xi_t, v_t) = \Cov(u_t - (\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2)v_t, v_t) = \Cov(u_t, v_t) - (\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2)\Var(v_t) = \sigma_{uv} - (\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2)\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_{uv} - \sigma_{uv} = 0\). For jointly normal random variables, zero covariance is equivalent to statistical independence.

P2 · Sign of Stambaugh bias

Prove that the Stambaugh bias \(\E[\hat{\beta} - \beta]\) has the opposite sign of the innovation correlation \(\rho_{uv}\) whenever the predictor is stationary and positively autocorrelated (\(0 < \phi < 1\)).

Solution

Stambaugh's bias is \(\E[\hat{\beta} - \beta] = \frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\sigma_v^2}\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi]\). With \(0 < \phi < 1\), Kendall's bias \(\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi] \approx -(1 + 3\phi)/T < 0\). Since \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\), the sign of the multiplier is \(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{uv}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\rho_{uv})\). Therefore, \(\operatorname{sgn}(\E[\hat{\beta} - \beta]) = \operatorname{sgn}(\rho_{uv}) \times \operatorname{sgn}(\E[\hat{\phi} - \phi]) = \operatorname{sgn}(\rho_{uv}) \times (-1) = -\operatorname{sgn}(\rho_{uv})\).

P3 · Vanishing bias as sample size approaches infinity

Prove that the OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}\) is consistent for \(\beta\) as \(T \to \infty\) despite finite-sample Stambaugh bias.

Solution

As \(T \to \infty\), by the ergodic theorem for stationary AR(1) processes, \(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t^2 \xrightarrow{p} \Var(x) = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{1 - \phi^2} > 0\). Furthermore, because \(\E[u_{t+1} \mid x_t] = 0\), the sequence \(\tilde{x}_t u_{t+1}\) is a martingale difference sequence with finite variance. By the martingale weak law of large numbers, \(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \tilde{x}_t u_{t+1} \xrightarrow{p} \E[\tilde{x}_t u_{t+1}] = 0\). Therefore, \(\hat{\beta} - \beta = \frac{\frac{1}{T}\sum \tilde{x}_t u_{t+1}}{\frac{1}{T}\sum \tilde{x}_t^2} \xrightarrow{p} \frac{0}{\Var(x)} = 0\). Thus, \(\hat{\beta}\) is consistent, and the bias decays at rate \(\mathcal{O}(1/T)\).

Empirical

E1 · Fama and French (1988) empirical findings

Fama and French (1988) regressed multi-year S&P 500 returns on dividend yields. What happened to regression \(R^2\) and slope coefficients as the forecasting horizon was lengthened from 1 month to 4 years?

Solution

Fama and French found that at the 1-month horizon, the \(R^2\) was negligible (below 1%). However, as the horizon lengthened to 1, 2, 3, and 4 years, the regression slope increased roughly proportionally to the horizon, and the \(R^2\) climbed dramatically, exceeding 25% to 35% at the 4-year horizon. This pattern demonstrated that expected stock returns contain a slow-moving, highly persistent component.

E2 · Overlapping observations in multi-year regressions

Why do empirical studies use overlapping monthly data rather than non-overlapping multi-year data when estimating 5-year predictive regressions, and what econometric complication ensues?

Solution

In a 50-year dataset, non-overlapping 5-year returns provide only 10 independent data points, yielding immense sampling error. Using monthly stepping provides \(T - 60 \approx 540\) observations, increasing statistical efficiency. However, each 5-year return shares 59 months of common returns with the next observation, creating an MA(59) residual autocorrelation that requires Hansen-Hodrick or Newey-West HAC adjustments with 60 lags.

E3 · Lewellen's (2004) empirical result

How did Lewellen (2004) resolve the question of whether dividend yields predict stock returns after accounting for Stambaugh bias?

Solution

Lewellen showed that because financial theory requires valuation ratios to be stationary (\(\phi \le 1\)), testing \(\beta = 0\) conditional on \(\phi \approx 1\) establishes a conservative lower bound for \(\hat{\beta}\). Even after subtracting the maximum possible Stambaugh bias under \(\phi = 1.0\), the dividend yield, book-to-market ratio, and earnings yield still exhibited statistically significant predictive power for US stock returns over 1946–2000.

Research

R1 · Campbell and Yogo (2006) Q-test

Explain how Campbell and Yogo (2006) used local-to-unity asymptotic theory to test return predictability when \(\phi\) is close to 1.

Solution

Campbell and Yogo modeled the predictor persistence as local-to-unity, \(\phi = 1 - c/T\), where \(c \ge 0\) is unobservable. They constructed a two-step confidence interval method: first, invert the Dickey-Fuller GLS test to obtain a valid confidence set for \(c\); second, evaluate the predictive \(t\)-statistic across all admissible values of \(c\). A predictor is confirmed significant only if the null is rejected for the least favorable value of \(c\) in the confidence set.

R2 · Multi-predictor systems and vector autoregressions

How does Stambaugh bias generalize when predicting returns with multiple persistent variables (e.g., dividend yield and short-term interest rate)?

Solution

In a multivariate system \(r_{t+1} = \beta^\top x_t + u_{t+1}\) with \(x_{t+1} = \Phi x_t + v_{t+1}\), the vector bias becomes \(\E[\hat{\beta} - \beta] = \Sigma_v^{-1}\Sigma_{vu} \E[\hat{\Phi} - \Phi]\). Cross-variable correlations propagate biases across equations: a non-predictive persistent regressor can acquire an apparent non-zero slope if its innovations covary with another persistent predictor's errors.

R3 · Cochrane's (2008) defense of predictability

Explain why Cochrane (2008) argued that tests of dividend growth predictability provide a more powerful test of return predictability than direct return regressions.

Solution

By the Campbell-Shiller identity, \(1 = \beta_r - \beta_d\). Testing return predictability directly is low-power because return innovations have high volatility (\(\sigma_u \approx 20\%\)), making \(\hat{\beta}_r\) noisy. In contrast, dividend growth has lower volatility and negligible persistence, so the regression of dividend growth on the dividend yield is estimated with high precision. Finding that \(\beta_d \approx 0\) statistically forces \(\beta_r \approx 1\), establishing return predictability by indirect accounting necessity.

  1. What causes the Stambaugh small-sample bias in predictive regressions?

    Options

    Stambaugh bias arises from the interaction between Kendall's small-sample bias in the persistent AR(1) regressor and the contemporaneous covariance \(\sigma_{uv}\) between return and predictor innovations.

  2. When return innovations and predictor innovations are negatively correlated (\(\sigma_{uv} < 0\)), the OLS predictive slope is biased…

    Options

    Because Kendall's bias \(\E[\hat{\phi}-\phi] < 0\), multiplying by \(\sigma_{uv}/\sigma_v^2 < 0\) produces a positive bias: \(\E[\hat{\beta} - \beta] > 0\).

  3. If \(\sigma_{uv} / \sigma_v^2 = -1.6\) and Kendall bias is \(-0.025\), what is the analytical Stambaugh bias? (two decimals)

    \(\text{Bias} = (-1.6) \times (-0.025) = +0.04\).

  4. Why do OLS standard errors underestimate uncertainty in long-horizon predictive regressions?

    Options

    Multi-period returns constructed with moving windows overlap by \(k-1\) periods, violating the OLS no-autocorrelation assumption and requiring Newey-West HAC errors.

  5. If naive \(\hat{\beta} = 0.08\), Stambaugh bias is \(0.05\), and \(\text{SE} = 0.02\), what is the bias-corrected \(t\)-statistic?

    \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{adj}} = 0.08 - 0.05 = 0.03\). Then \(t = 0.03 / 0.02 = 1.5\).

  6. What empirical pattern did Fama and French (1988) discover regarding long-horizon return regressions?

    Options

    Fama and French observed that predictive \(R^2\) climbed steadily with the horizon, reflecting the slow-moving nature of discount rates.

  7. What happens to Stambaugh bias as the sample size \(T \to \infty\)?

    Options

    Because Kendall's bias is proportional to \(1/T\), Stambaugh bias vanishes asymptotically, ensuring that OLS remains consistent.

  8. How did Lewellen (2004) maintain return predictability despite Stambaugh bias?

    Options

    Lewellen showed that imposing the theoretical bound \(\phi \le 1\) establishes conservative lower confidence bounds on \(\beta\) that remain statistically positive.
