Long-Horizon Variance Decomposition

  • Introduces: discount-rate variation

Why do price-dividend ratios vary over time? The Campbell-Shiller identity establishes that every basis point of variation in the dividend-price ratio must be accounted for by either predictable future returns or predictable future dividend growth: \(1 = b_r - b_d\). In his seminal work, Cochrane (2008, 2011) showed that the long-run regression coefficient on dividend growth is statistically indistinguishable from zero (\(b_d \approx 0\)). By mathematical necessity, the return coefficient must equal one (\(b_r \approx 1\)). This fact establishes that 100% of aggregate stock market valuation ratio variance is driven by discount-rate variation rather than cash-flow expectations.

1The long-horizon identity: \(1 = b_r - b_d\)

Recall the demeaned infinite-horizon Campbell-Shiller decomposition from Lesson 2:

\[ \tilde{\delta}_t = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\tilde{r}_{t+j}] - \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\Delta \tilde{d}_{t+j}], \]

where \(\tilde{\delta}_t = \delta_t - \bar{\delta}\) is the demeaned log dividend-price ratio, \(\tilde{r}_{t+j}\) is the demeaned log return, and \(\Delta \tilde{d}_{t+j}\) is demeaned log dividend growth.

Definition 1.1 (discount-rate variation)

Discount-rate variation is the phenomenon whereby temporal fluctuations in asset prices and valuation ratios are driven by shifts in expected future returns (required risk premia and interest rates) rather than revisions in expected future cash flows.

Theorem 1.1 (Cochrane long-horizon variance decomposition identity)

Define the long-horizon discounted return regression coefficient \(b_r\) and dividend growth regression coefficient \(b_d\) by

\[ b_r = \frac{\Cov\left(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} r_{t+j}, \delta_t\right)}{\Var(\delta_t)}, \qquad b_d = \frac{\Cov\left(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \Delta d_{t+j}, \delta_t\right)}{\Var(\delta_t)}. \]

Then the coefficients satisfy the exact identity

\[ 1 = b_r - b_d. \]
Proof

Multiply the Campbell-Shiller dynamic identity by \(\tilde{\delta}_t\) and take unconditional expectations:

\[ \E[\tilde{\delta}_t^2] = \E\left[\tilde{\delta}_t \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\tilde{r}_{t+j}]\right] - \E\left[\tilde{\delta}_t \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \E_t[\Delta \tilde{d}_{t+j}]\right]. \]

By the law of iterated expectations, \(\E[\tilde{\delta}_t \E_t[\tilde{r}_{t+j}]] = \E[\tilde{\delta}_t \tilde{r}_{t+j}] = \Cov(\tilde{r}_{t+j}, \delta_t)\), and similarly for dividend growth. Thus,

\[ \Var(\delta_t) = \Cov\left(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} r_{t+j}, \delta_t\right) - \Cov\left(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \Delta d_{t+j}, \delta_t\right). \]

Dividing both sides by \(\Var(\delta_t)\) yields

\[ 1 = \frac{\Cov\left(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} r_{t+j}, \delta_t\right)}{\Var(\delta_t)} - \frac{\Cov\left(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \Delta d_{t+j}, \delta_t\right)}{\Var(\delta_t)} = b_r - b_d. \]

2The dog that did not bark

Cochrane (2008, 2011) evaluated the empirical values of \(b_r\) and \(b_d\) using US equity market data from 1926 to 2010. The results overturned classical Gordon-growth intuition:

Proposition 2.1 (Cochrane 2011 empirical decomposition)

In US equity data, the empirical regression coefficients are

\[ b_r \approx 1.05 \quad (\text{SE} \approx 0.18), \qquad b_d \approx 0.05 \quad (\text{SE} \approx 0.15). \]

The dividend growth coefficient \(b_d\) is statistically indistinguishable from zero, while \(b_r\) is statistically indistinguishable from 1.00.

Cochrane termed this finding "The Dog That Did Not Bark" (borrowing from Sherlock Holmes): in a world where prices reflect discounted cash flows, common sense suggests that a low price-dividend ratio signals depressed future dividends. In reality, future dividends do not move at all in response to the dividend-price ratio. Because \(b_d \approx 0\), the identity \(1 = b_r - b_d\) forces \(b_r = 1\). All of the variation in price-dividend ratios is driven by expected returns.

3Lyapunov solution of the VAR variance decomposition

In a companion VAR(1) with state vector \(z_t = [r_t - \bar{r}, \delta_t - \bar{\delta}]^\top\) and transition matrix \(\Phi\), the population unconditional covariance matrix \(\Gamma_0 = \E[z_t z_t^\top]\) solves the discrete Lyapunov equation:

\[ \Gamma_0 = \Phi\,\Gamma_0\,\Phi^\top + \Sigma, \qquad \operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_0) = (I - \Phi \otimes \Phi)^{-1} \operatorname{vec}(\Sigma). \]

The infinite-horizon return forecasting operator is \((I - \rho\Phi)^{-1}\Phi\). Let \(e_r = (1, 0)^\top\) and \(e_{\text{dp}} = (0, 1)^\top\). Then

\[ b_r = \frac{e_r^\top (I - \rho\Phi)^{-1}\Phi\,\Gamma_0\,e_{\text{dp}}}{e_{\text{dp}}^\top \Gamma_0\,e_{\text{dp}}}. \]

Similarly, using the dividend growth identity \(\Delta d_{t+1} \approx r_{t+1} + \rho\,\delta_{t+1} - \delta_t\), the long-run dividend growth operator is \((e_r^\top \Phi + \rho\,e_{\text{dp}}^\top \Phi - e_{\text{dp}}^\top)(I - \rho\Phi)^{-1}\), yielding

\[ b_d = \frac{(e_r^\top \Phi + \rho\,e_{\text{dp}}^\top \Phi - e_{\text{dp}}^\top)(I - \rho\Phi)^{-1}\Gamma_0\,e_{\text{dp}}}{e_{\text{dp}}^\top \Gamma_0\,e_{\text{dp}}}. \]

Lab 1 · Dividend-Yield Variance Decomposition and The Dog That Did Not Bark

Under Cochrane's (2008, 2011) empirical VAR parameterization of US equities, the long-run dividend growth coefficient \(b_d\) evaluates near zero, while the long-run return coefficient \(b_r\) accounts for essentially 100% of valuation variance. The theoretical identity \(b_r - b_d = 1.0000\) holds exactly via the population Lyapunov equation.

"""Lab AP5-L05-1: Dividend-Yield Variance Decomposition and The Dog That Did Not Bark.

SIMULATED: evaluates Cochrane's (2008, 2011) variance decomposition of the dividend-price
ratio into expected return variation (b_r) and expected dividend growth variation (b_d)
to test whether 100% of price fluctuations come from discount rates.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(777)
    n_obs = 300
    rho = 0.96

    # 2-variable system: z_t = [r_t - E[r], dp_t - E[dp]]^T
    # Cochrane (2008, 2011) empirical calibration:
    # r_{t+1}  = 0.097 * dp_t + eps_r_{t+1}
    # dp_{t+1} = 0.940 * dp_t + eps_dp_{t+1}
    phi = np.array([[0.00, 0.097], [0.00, 0.940]])
    sigma_r = 0.19
    sigma_dp = 0.15
    cov_r_dp = -0.70 * sigma_r * sigma_dp
    cov_eps = np.array([[sigma_r**2, cov_r_dp], [cov_r_dp, sigma_dp**2]])

    # Simulate VAR
    eps = rng.multivariate_normal(mean=[0.0, 0.0], cov=cov_eps, size=n_obs + 1)
    z = np.zeros((n_obs + 1, 2))
    for t in range(n_obs):
        z[t + 1] = phi @ z[t] + eps[t + 1]

    # Reconstruct implied dividend growth: Delta d_{t+1} = r_{t+1} + rho * dp_{t+1} - dp_t
    r_series = z[1:, 0]
    dp_series = z[:-1, 1]
    dp_lead = z[1:, 1]
    delta_d = r_series + rho * dp_lead - dp_series

    # 1. Direct 1-period predictive regressions
    var_dp = float(np.var(dp_series, ddof=1))
    cov_r = float(np.cov(r_series, dp_series, ddof=1)[0, 1])
    beta_r_1 = cov_r / var_dp

    cov_d = float(np.cov(delta_d, dp_series, ddof=1)[0, 1])
    beta_d_1 = cov_d / var_dp

    cov_dp_lead = float(np.cov(dp_lead, dp_series, ddof=1)[0, 1])
    beta_dp_1 = cov_dp_lead / var_dp
    one_period_sum = beta_r_1 - beta_d_1 + rho * beta_dp_1

    # 2. Population VAR variance decomposition (exact infinite horizon)
    eye_2 = np.eye(2)
    long_run_r_op = np.linalg.inv(eye_2 - rho * phi) @ phi

    # Dividend operator: E_t[Delta d_{t+1}] = (e_r^T Phi + rho e_dp^T Phi - e_dp^T) z_t
    e_r = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
    e_dp = np.array([0.0, 1.0])
    long_run_d_op = (e_r @ phi + rho * e_dp @ phi - e_dp) @ np.linalg.inv(eye_2 - rho * phi)

    # Solve discrete Lyapunov equation for population covariance Gamma_0
    eye_4 = np.eye(4)
    gamma_pop = (np.linalg.inv(eye_4 - np.kron(phi, phi)) @ cov_eps.flatten()).reshape((2, 2))

    b_r_pop = float((e_r @ long_run_r_op @ gamma_pop @ e_dp) / (e_dp @ gamma_pop @ e_dp))
    b_d_pop = float((long_run_d_op @ gamma_pop @ e_dp) / (e_dp @ gamma_pop @ e_dp))
    decomp_sum = b_r_pop - b_d_pop

    print("Cochrane (2008, 2011) Dividend-Yield Variance Decomposition")
    print(f"Sample length T:             {n_obs} years")
    print(f"Discount factor rho:         {rho:.4f}")
    print(f"1-period return slope:       {beta_r_1:.4f}")
    print(f"1-period dividend slope:     {beta_d_1:.4f}")
    print(f"1-period persistence slope:  {beta_dp_1:.4f}")
    print(f"1-period identity sum:       {one_period_sum:.4f}")
    print(f"Long-run return share b_r:   {b_r_pop * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Long-run dividend share b_d: {b_d_pop * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Identity b_r - b_d:          {decomp_sum * 100:.2f}%")
    print("The dog that did not bark:   b_d approx 0 (dividend growth unforecastable)")


main()

The numerical results verify Cochrane's theorem. In the 1-period regression, dividend yield persistence \(\phi_{\text{dp}} = 0.94\) and return slope \(\beta_r \approx 0.10\) combine via the Lyapunov operator to generate a long-run return share \(b_r \approx 100\%\), while the long-run dividend growth share \(b_d\) is virtually zero. The identity \(b_r - b_d = 1.0000\) holds to machine precision.

4Where the canon breaks

While Cochrane's \(b_r \approx 1, b_d \approx 0\) result is established for aggregate US equities over 1926–2010, the canon exhibits important boundaries:

  • Cross-country evidence: In international markets with concentrated corporate ownership and high bank financing (e.g., the United Kingdom, Germany, Japan), dividend growth exhibits significant predictability (\(b_d > 0.30\)), and returns are less forecastable than in the US.
  • Pre-1926 US equity data: Over the 1871–1925 period studied by Shiller, dividend growth was substantially more volatile and exhibited modest predictability, reflecting different corporate payout policies prior to modern earnings management.
  • Repurchase substitution: In the post-1990 era, firms adjust share buybacks countercyclically to absorb cash-flow shocks, leaving cash dividends completely flat. As shown by Binsbergen and Koijen (2010), total payout yields restore the full predictability of returns.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Stock price movements are 50% cash flow and 50% discount rate.”

    This common intuition is refuted by data. Long-run variance decomposition proves that discount rates account for nearly 100% of price-dividend ratio fluctuations in the US stock market.

  2. “If 1-period return regressions have low \(R^2\) (e.g. 5%), discount rates cannot explain the dividend-price ratio.”

    Because the dividend-price ratio is highly persistent (\(\phi \approx 0.94\)), a 5% to 10% annual return slope compounds geometrically over decades: \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \phi^{j-1} \beta \approx \beta / (1 - \rho\phi) \approx 0.10 / (1 - 0.96 \times 0.94) \approx 1.03\).

  3. “The identity \(b_r - b_d = 1\) requires that the CAPM holds.”

    The identity \(b_r - b_d = 1\) is an accounting necessity derived from the definition of return. It holds identically under any asset pricing model, or under complete market irrationality.

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate b_r from 1-period coefficients

Suppose the dividend yield AR(1) persistence is \(\phi = 0.93\), the 1-period return predictive slope is \(\beta_r = 0.08\), and the discount parameter is \(\rho = 0.96\). Under the assumption that the dividend yield is the only state variable, compute the implied long-run return share \(b_r\).

Solution

In a univariate AR(1) system, the long-run operator is \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1}\E_t[r_{t+j}] = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \beta_r \phi^{j-1} \delta_t = \frac{\beta_r}{1 - \rho\phi} \delta_t\). Thus, \(b_r = \frac{\beta_r}{1 - \rho\phi} = \frac{0.08}{1 - 0.96 \times 0.93} = \frac{0.08}{1 - 0.8928} = \frac{0.08}{0.1072} \approx 0.746269\) (74.63%).

M2 · Implied dividend growth share

From the result of M1 where \(b_r = 0.7463\), compute the implied long-run dividend growth coefficient \(b_d\) using Cochrane's identity.

Solution

By Theorem 1.1, \(1 = b_r - b_d \implies b_d = b_r - 1\). Therefore, \(b_d = 0.7463 - 1.0000 = -0.2537\) (-25.37%). A negative \(b_d\) implies that a high dividend yield predicts lower future dividend growth, reinforcing rather than dampening price volatility.

M3 · 1-period identity verification

At date \(t\), \(\beta_r^{(1)} = 0.09\), \(\beta_d^{(1)} = 0.02\), and the dividend yield persistence is \(\beta_{\text{dp}}^{(1)} = 0.94\). If \(\rho = 0.96\), compute \(\beta_r^{(1)} - \beta_d^{(1)} + \rho\,\beta_{\text{dp}}^{(1)}\).

Solution

From the 1-period log-linear relation \(\delta_t \approx r_{t+1} - \Delta d_{t+1} + \rho\,\delta_{t+1}\), taking regressions on \(\delta_t\) requires \(1 = \beta_r^{(1)} - \beta_d^{(1)} + \rho\,\beta_{\text{dp}}^{(1)}\). Compute: \(0.09 - 0.02 + 0.96 \times 0.94 = 0.07 + 0.9024 = 0.9724\). Up to small Taylor linearization discrepancy, the sum is approximately 1.00.

Proof

P1 · General multi-period variance decomposition

Prove that for any finite horizon \(H\), the sample regression slopes satisfy \(1 = b_r^{(H)} - b_d^{(H)} + \rho^H b_{\text{dp}}^{(H)}\).

Solution

Recall the \(H\)-period forward identity: \(\delta_t = -\frac{k(1-\rho^H)}{1-\rho} + \sum_{j=1}^H \rho^{j-1} r_{t+j} - \sum_{j=1}^H \rho^{j-1} \Delta d_{t+j} + \rho^H \delta_{t+H}\). Taking covariances with \(\delta_t\) on both sides: \(\Var(\delta_t) = \Cov\left(\sum_{j=1}^H \rho^{j-1} r_{t+j}, \delta_t\right) - \Cov\left(\sum_{j=1}^H \rho^{j-1} \Delta d_{t+j}, \delta_t\right) + \rho^H \Cov(\delta_{t+H}, \delta_t)\). Dividing by \(\Var(\delta_t)\) yields \(1 = b_r^{(H)} - b_d^{(H)} + \rho^H b_{\text{dp}}^{(H)}\). As \(H \to \infty\), because \(\delta_t\) is stationary and \(\rho < 1\), \(\rho^H b_{\text{dp}}^{(H)} \to 0\), leaving \(1 = b_r - b_d\).

P2 · Lyapanov solution uniqueness

Prove that if all eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) of \(\Phi\) satisfy \(|\lambda_i| < 1\), the Lyapunov equation \(\Gamma_0 = \Phi\Gamma_0\Phi^\top + \Sigma\) has a unique symmetric solution.

Solution

Applying the vectorization operator, \(\operatorname{vec}(\Phi\Gamma_0\Phi^\top) = (\Phi \otimes \Phi)\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_0)\). Thus, \(\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_0) = (\Phi \otimes \Phi)\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_0) + \operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\), which rearranges to \([I - (\Phi \otimes \Phi)]\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_0) = \operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\). The eigenvalues of \(\Phi \otimes \Phi\) are all pairwise products \(\lambda_i \lambda_j\). Since \(|\lambda_i| < 1\) for all \(i\), \(|\lambda_i \lambda_j| < 1\). Thus, no eigenvalue of \(\Phi \otimes \Phi\) equals 1, guaranteeing that \(I - (\Phi \otimes \Phi)\) is invertible. Hence, \(\operatorname{vec}(\Gamma_0) = [I - (\Phi \otimes \Phi)]^{-1} \operatorname{vec}(\Sigma)\) exists and is unique. Symmetry follows from \(\Sigma = \Sigma^\top\).

P3 · Gordon growth variance counterexample

Show that if the Gordon growth model with constant discount rate \(r\) held, then \(b_r = 0\) and \(b_d = -1\).

Solution

If the discount rate is constant, \(\E_t[r_{t+j}] = r\) for all \(j\). Consequently, \(\Cov(r_{t+j}, \delta_t) = 0\), so \(b_r = 0\). Then by the identity \(1 = b_r - b_d\), we must have \(1 = 0 - b_d \implies b_d = -1\). Under the constant discount rate hypothesis, 100% of dividend yield variation must come from predictable dividend growth with a coefficient of \(-1.0\). The empirical finding \(b_d \approx 0\) decisively refutes this Gordon model prediction.

Empirical

E1 · Long-horizon compounding of predictability

In annual data, the 1-year regression slope of returns on dividend yield is \(\beta = 0.10\) with \(R^2 = 0.06\). At the 5-year horizon, the slope is \(\beta_5 \approx 0.40\) with \(R^2 = 0.28\). Explain how this aligns with \(b_r \approx 1\).

Solution

Because the dividend yield is persistent (\(\phi \approx 0.90\)), the expected return next year remains elevated if the dividend yield is elevated today. The cumulative 5-year return is \(R_{t, t+5} = \sum_{j=1}^5 r_{t+j}\). Its regression slope on \(\delta_t\) is approximately \(\sum_{j=1}^5 \beta \phi^{j-1} = \beta \frac{1 - \phi^5}{1 - \phi} = 0.10 \times \frac{1 - (0.90)^5}{0.10} = 1 - 0.59049 = 0.4095\). As the horizon grows, the cumulative coefficient approaches \(b_r \approx 1.0\), explaining why long-horizon regressions exhibit high explanatory power.

E2 · International variance decomposition comparison

In the United Kingdom, Campbell (2003) reported \(b_r \approx 0.65\) and \(b_d \approx -0.35\). How does this compare with the US finding?

Solution

In the UK, dividend growth is partially predictable (\(b_d = -0.35\)), meaning that high dividend yields correctly forecast lower future dividend payouts. Consequently, discount-rate variation accounts for approximately 65% of UK price-dividend variance, compared to essentially 100% in the United States. Cash-flow news plays a larger role in economies with higher dividend volatility.

E3 · Earnings yield vs. dividend yield variance decomposition

When the variance decomposition is applied to the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (CAPE) rather than the dividend yield, does the finding \(b_r \approx 1\) hold?

Solution

Yes. Campbell and Shiller (1998) and Cochrane (2011) showed that when using cyclically adjusted earnings (10-year smoothed real earnings), long-run earnings growth is unforecastable by CAPE, and expected return variation accounts for approximately 100% of CAPE fluctuations. The discount-rate dominance result is robust across alternative valuation multiples.

Research

R1 · Cochrane's Presidential Address (2011) paradigm shift

In his 2011 AFA Presidential Address, Cochrane declared: "Discount rates vary over time… this is the central fact of modern asset pricing." Explain how this shifted the research agenda.

Solution

Throughout the 1970s and 1980s, empirical finance was organized around testing whether markets were efficient under the assumption of constant expected returns (random walks). Cochrane argued that return predictability is not an anomaly to be rejected, but the foundational economic phenomenon: discount rates vary systematically over the business cycle. The modern research agenda shifted from asking whether returns are predictable to understanding why risk premia vary, focusing on macroeconomic models of habit formation, long-run risks, and intermediary asset pricing.

R2 · Binsbergen and Koijen's (2010) latent state-space model

Binsbergen and Koijen (2010) estimated expected returns and dividend growth using a latent variable Kalman filter rather than standard VARs. What did they find regarding \(b_r\) and \(b_d\)?

Solution

Binsbergen and Koijen (2010) found that modeling expected returns and dividend growth as unobservable latent processes significantly improved forecasting accuracy. They found that dividend growth does contain a small predictable component, but expected returns are even more persistent and volatile than indicated by OLS regressions, reinforcing that discount-rate variation explains the vast majority of stock market valuation swings.

R3 · Return predictability across asset classes

How does Cochrane's (2011) discount-rate synthesis link stock return predictability to bond return predictability and currency carry trades?

Solution

Cochrane demonstrated that identical discount-rate dynamics govern all major asset classes: (1) In equities, dividend yields predict returns, not dividend growth (\(b_r \approx 1\)); (2) In Treasury bonds, yield spreads predict bond excess returns, not future interest rate changes (Fama-Bliss and Cochrane-Piazzesi); (3) In foreign exchange, interest rate differentials predict currency returns, violating uncovered interest parity (Fama puzzle); (4) In credit, credit spreads predict excess corporate bond returns. Across all markets, valuation ratios forecast risk premia, not cash flows.
