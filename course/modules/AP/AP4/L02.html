External Habit Formation: The Campbell–Cochrane Model

  • Uses: consumption CAPM, CRRA utility, stochastic discount factor, equity premium puzzle, risk-free rate puzzle
  • Introduces: External habit model
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Standard consumption asset pricing fails because risk aversion is time-invariant. Campbell & Cochrane (1999) solved this by introducing an external habit level of consumption. When consumption approaches habit during recessions, households become intensely risk-averse, demanding large equity risk premia. Crucially, their non-linear sensitivity function offsets intertemporal substitution against precautionary savings, keeping the equilibrium risk-free rate constant.

1Preferences & Surplus Consumption Ratio

Consider an infinite-horizon economy populated by identical households with external habit preferences (“catching up with the Joneses”). An individual consumer derives utility from the difference between consumption \(C_t\) and an external habit level \(X_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} U(\{C_t\}_{t=0}^\infty) &= \E_0 \left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty \delta^t \frac{(C_t - X_t)^{1-\gamma} - 1}{1 - \gamma} \right], \quad \delta \in (0, 1), \quad \gamma > 0 \end{aligned} \]

where \(X_t\) is determined by aggregate historical consumption and treated as exogenous by each individual agent.

Definition 2.1 (Surplus Consumption Ratio & External Habit Model)

In the external habit model of Campbell & Cochrane (1999), define the surplus consumption ratio \(S_t\) and its logarithm \(s_t\) by

\[ \begin{aligned} S_t &\equiv \frac{C_t - X_t}{C_t} \in (0, 1], \quad s_t \equiv \ln S_t \end{aligned} \]

Marginal utility with respect to individual consumption is \(u'(C_t) = (C_t - X_t)^{-\gamma} = (S_t C_t)^{-\gamma}\). Local relative risk aversion \(\gamma_t\) is time-varying and countercyclical:

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma_t &\equiv -\frac{C_t u''(C_t)}{u'(C_t)} = \frac{\gamma}{S_t} \end{aligned} \]

When consumption falls toward habit in economic downturns, \(S_t \to 0\), causing effective risk aversion \(\gamma_t \to \infty\). In expansions, \(S_t\) expands toward 1, and risk aversion moderates toward \(\gamma\).

2Endowment Dynamics & The Sensitivity Function

Log consumption growth follows an i.i.d. Gaussian random walk:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta c_{t+1} &= g + v_{t+1}, \quad v_{t+1} \sim \text{i.i.d.} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \end{aligned} \]

The log surplus consumption ratio evolves according to a heteroskedastic AR(1) process:

\[ \begin{aligned} s_{t+1} &= (1 - \phi) \bar{s} + \phi s_t + \lambda(s_t) v_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\phi \in (0, 1)\) is the persistence parameter, \(\bar{s} = \ln \bar{S}\) is the steady-state log surplus ratio, and \(\lambda(s_t)\) is a non-linear sensitivity function governing habit adjustment to macroeconomic innovations.

Theorem 2.1 (Constant Risk-Free Rate Sensitivity Function)

To ensure that the equilibrium real risk-free rate \(r_f\) is strictly constant across all dates and states, the sensitivity function \(\lambda(s)\) and steady-state surplus ratio \(\bar{S}\) must satisfy

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{S} &= \sigma \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{1 - \phi}} \\ \lambda(s) &= \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\bar{S}} \sqrt{1 - 2(s - \bar{s})} - 1, & s \le s_{\max} \\ 0, & s > s_{\max} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]

where \(s_{\max} \equiv \bar{s} + \frac{1}{2}(1 - \bar{S}^2)\). Under this specification, the log real risk-free rate is given by

\[ \begin{aligned} r_f &= -\ln \delta + \gamma g - \frac{1}{2} \gamma (1 - \phi) \end{aligned} \]
Proof

The stochastic discount factor is \(M_{t+1} = \delta \left(\frac{S_{t+1} C_{t+1}}{S_t C_t}\right)^{-\gamma} = \delta \exp(-\gamma [\Delta c_{t+1} + \Delta s_{t+1}])\). Substituting the laws of motion for \(\Delta c_{t+1}\) and \(s_{t+1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln M_{t+1} &= \ln \delta - \gamma g - \gamma (1 - \phi)(\bar{s} - s_t) - \gamma [1 + \lambda(s_t)] v_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

The conditional expectation of the log SDF under lognormality yields the gross risk-free rate \(r_{f, t} = -\ln \E_t[M_{t+1}] = -\E_t[\ln M_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{2}\Var_t(\ln M_{t+1})\):

\[ \begin{aligned} r_{f, t} &= -\ln \delta + \gamma g + \gamma (1 - \phi)(\bar{s} - s_t) - \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma^2 [1 + \lambda(s_t)]^2 \end{aligned} \]

For \(r_{f, t}\) to be independent of the state \(s_t\), the linear term \(\gamma(1-\phi)(\bar{s} - s_t)\) (intertemporal substitution / mean reversion) must be identically cancelled by the quadratic precautionary savings term \(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma^2 [1 + \lambda(s_t)]^2\) for all \(s_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma (1 - \phi)(\bar{s} - s_t) - \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma^2 [1 + \lambda(s_t)]^2 &= \text{constant} \equiv -\frac{1}{2} \gamma (1 - \phi) \end{aligned} \]

Solving for \(1 + \lambda(s_t)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} [1 + \lambda(s_t)]^2 &= \frac{1 - \phi}{\gamma \sigma^2} [1 - 2(s_t - \bar{s})] = \frac{1}{\bar{S}^2} [1 - 2(s_t - \bar{s})] \\ 1 + \lambda(s_t) &= \frac{1}{\bar{S}} \sqrt{1 - 2(s_t - \bar{s})} \end{aligned} \]

Subtracting 1 yields the Campbell-Cochrane sensitivity function. When \(s_t = \bar{s}\), \(\lambda(\bar{s}) = \frac{1}{\bar{S}} - 1\). Imposing \(\lambda(s) \ge 0\) prevents the habit from exceeding consumption and identifies \(s_{\max}\).

3Equilibrium Asset Pricing & Predictability

Because the real risk-free rate is constant, all variation in equity returns is driven by shocks to the price-dividend ratio. Let \(z(s_t) \equiv \ln(P_t/D_t)\). The Euler equation for the equity claim (where dividends equal consumption, \(D_t = C_t\), or levered dividends) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \exp(z(s_t)) &= \E_t \left[ \delta \exp(-\gamma[g + v_{t+1} + \Delta s_{t+1}]) (\exp(z(s_{t+1})) + 1) \exp(g + v_{t+1}) \right] \end{aligned} \]

In equilibrium, \(z(s)\) is a monotonically increasing function of surplus consumption \(s\). In recessions (low \(s\)), expected returns are high, depressing price-dividend ratios. In expansions (high \(s\)), risk aversion falls, driving equity valuations upward.

Lab 2 · Campbell-Cochrane Habit Simulation

Simulating the Campbell-Cochrane model under published annual parameters will verify that the real risk-free rate is analytically constant, while effective risk aversion gamma_t varies countercyclically over a wide range.

"""Lab AP4-L02-1: Campbell-Cochrane (1999) surplus consumption ratio and constant risk-free rate.

Simulates the external habit formation model, computes the steady-state surplus ratio,
evaluates the sensitivity function lambda(s), and verifies that the real risk-free rate is constant.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np

# Published annual calibration parameters (Campbell & Cochrane 1999)
g = 0.0189  # Mean consumption growth
sigma = 0.0150  # Consumption growth standard deviation
phi = 0.87  # Persistence of surplus consumption ratio
gamma = 2.0  # Curvature parameter
delta = 0.89  # Subjective discount factor

# Steady state surplus consumption ratio S_bar and max log surplus s_max
s_bar_val = sigma * np.sqrt(gamma / (1.0 - phi))
s_bar = float(np.log(s_bar_val))
s_max = float(s_bar + 0.5 * (1.0 - s_bar_val**2))

# Analytical log risk-free rate
rf_log = -np.log(delta) + gamma * g - 0.5 * gamma * (1.0 - phi)
rf_net = float(np.exp(rf_log) - 1.0)

print(f"Steady-state surplus ratio S_bar: {s_bar_val:.4f} (ln S_bar = {s_bar:.4f})")
print(f"Maximum log surplus ratio s_max: {s_max:.4f}")
print(f"Analytical real risk-free rate r_f: {rf_net * 100:.2f}%")

# Simulation over 500 years
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
t_len = 500
s = np.zeros(t_len)
v = rng.normal(0.0, sigma, t_len)
s[0] = s_bar

for t in range(t_len - 1):
    lam = (1.0 / s_bar_val) * np.sqrt(max(0.0, 1.0 - 2.0 * (s[t] - s_bar))) - 1.0 if s[t] <= s_max else 0.0
    s[t + 1] = (1.0 - phi) * s_bar + phi * s[t] + lam * v[t + 1]

# Effective risk aversion: gamma / S_t
s_levels = np.exp(s)
effective_gamma = gamma / s_levels

print(f"Simulated Surplus Ratio: Mean = {np.mean(s_levels):.4f}, Std = {np.std(s_levels):.4f}")
print(
    f"Effective Relative Risk Aversion gamma_t: Min = {np.min(effective_gamma):.1f}, "
    f"Mean = {np.mean(effective_gamma):.1f}, Max = {np.max(effective_gamma):.1f}"
)
Steady-state surplus ratio S_bar: 0.0588 (ln S_bar = -2.8330)
Maximum log surplus ratio s_max: -2.3348
Analytical real risk-free rate r_f: 2.46%
Simulated Surplus Ratio: Mean = 0.0641, Std = 0.0213
Effective Relative Risk Aversion gamma_t: Min = 18.0, Mean = 37.8, Max = 285.9

The numerical simulation confirms that the steady-state surplus consumption ratio is S_bar = 0.0588 (5.88%), yielding an analytically constant risk-free rate of 2.46%. Local relative risk aversion gamma_t fluctuates countercyclically from a low of 18.0 in expansions to over 285 in extreme downturns, generating large and volatile risk premia.

Where the canon breaks

While Campbell & Cochrane (1999) successfully resolves the equity premium, risk-free rate, and excess volatility puzzles, the model faces three significant empirical and theoretical critiques:

  • Unobservable Habit Process: The habit level \(X_t\) is not directly observable in consumption micro-data. Household surveys reveal little evidence of such extreme, slow-moving external habit persistence (Campbell 2006).
  • Extreme Local Risk Aversion: To generate the equity premium with smooth aggregate consumption, effective risk aversion \(\gamma / S_t\) must exceed 100 in typical recessions and approach 300 in severe contractions, conflicting with experimental and labor-supply evidence.
  • Zero Risk-Free Volatility: The assumption that \(\lambda(s)\) exactly zeroes out risk-free rate volatility is an engineering trick. In actual data, real interest rates fluctuate and term premia vary over the business cycle.

Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “In habit models, consumers have internal habits where they actively internalize how today's consumption increases tomorrow's habit.”

    In Campbell & Cochrane (1999), the habit is strictly external (“catching up with the Joneses”). Each consumer treats \(X_t\) as an exogenous aggregate benchmark, meaning there are no strategic intertemporal hedging terms in the Euler equation.

  2. “Because risk aversion is high in recessions, the risk-free rate must be high in recessions.”

    The entire point of the non-linear sensitivity function \(\lambda(s)\) is that intertemporal substitution (which pushes rates up in bad times) is exactly balanced by precautionary savings demand (which pushes rates down), keeping \(r_f\) perfectly constant.

  3. “The surplus consumption ratio \(S_t\) can become negative if consumption falls low enough.”

    The sensitivity function \(\lambda(s) \to \infty\) as \(s \to -\infty\), guaranteeing that \(\ln S_t\) has reflective boundaries such that \(C_t > X_t\) holds strictly at all dates.

Problem 2.1 · Steady-State Surplus Ratio Calculation

Calculate the steady-state surplus ratio \(\bar{S}\) and its logarithm \(\bar{s}\) for \(\sigma = 0.018\), \(\gamma = 2.5\), and \(\phi = 0.85\).

Solution

Using the steady-state formula: \(\bar{S} = \sigma \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{1 - \phi}} = 0.018 \sqrt{\frac{2.5}{1 - 0.85}} = 0.018 \sqrt{\frac{2.5}{0.15}} = 0.018 \sqrt{16.6667} = 0.018 \times 4.0825 \approx 0.0735\). Then \(\bar{s} = \ln(0.0735) \approx -2.6105\).

Problem 2.2 · Sensitivity Function Evaluation

For \(\bar{S} = 0.06\) and \(\bar{s} = -2.8134\), evaluate \(\lambda(s)\) when \(s = \bar{s}\) and when \(s = \bar{s} - 0.10\).

Solution

At \(s = \bar{s}\): \(\lambda(\bar{s}) = \frac{1}{\bar{S}}\sqrt{1 - 0} - 1 = \frac{1}{0.06} - 1 \approx 16.6667 - 1 = 15.6667\).

At \(s = \bar{s} - 0.10\): \(1 - 2(s - \bar{s}) = 1 - 2(-0.10) = 1.20\). Thus, \(\lambda(s) = \frac{1}{0.06}\sqrt{1.20} - 1 = \frac{1.0954}{0.06} - 1 \approx 18.2574 - 1 = 17.2574\).

Problem 2.3 · Effective Risk Aversion

In the Campbell-Cochrane economy with \(\gamma = 2.0\), what is the effective relative risk aversion \(\gamma_t\) when the surplus consumption ratio is at steady state (\(S_t = 0.0588\)) versus a recession where \(S_t = 0.0150\)?

Solution

At steady state: \(\gamma_t = \gamma / \bar{S} = 2.0 / 0.0588 \approx 34.0\).

In the recession: \(\gamma_t = \gamma / S_t = 2.0 / 0.0150 \approx 133.3\). Local risk aversion quadruples as consumption approaches habit.

Problem 2.4 · Monotonicity of Risk Aversion in Surplus Ratio

Prove that under Campbell-Cochrane external habit preferences, local Arrow-Pratt relative risk aversion is strictly decreasing in the surplus consumption ratio \(S_t\).

Solution

Given \(u(C, X) = \frac{(C - X)^{1-\gamma} - 1}{1-\gamma}\), marginal utility is \(u'(C) = (C - X)^{-\gamma}\) and \(u''(C) = -\gamma (C - X)^{-\gamma-1}\). The Arrow-Pratt coefficient of relative risk aversion is \(\gamma_t(C) = -C \frac{u''(C)}{u'(C)} = -C \frac{-\gamma (C - X)^{-\gamma-1}}{(C - X)^{-\gamma}} = \gamma \frac{C}{C - X} = \frac{\gamma}{S_t}\). Taking the first derivative with respect to \(S_t\): \(\frac{d\gamma_t}{d S_t} = -\frac{\gamma}{S_t^2} < 0\) for all \(\gamma > 0, S_t > 0\). Thus, relative risk aversion is strictly decreasing in \(S_t\).

Problem 2.5 · Maximum Log Surplus Ratio Derivation

Derive the upper bound \(s_{\max} = \bar{s} + \frac{1}{2}(1 - \bar{S}^2)\) from the condition that habit must remain non-decreasing with respect to consumption innovations at steady state.

Solution

In the sensitivity function \(\lambda(s) = \frac{1}{\bar{S}}\sqrt{1 - 2(s - \bar{s})} - 1\), the term inside the square root must be non-negative: \(1 - 2(s - \bar{s}) \ge 0 \implies s - \bar{s} \le 1/2\). Furthermore, Campbell and Cochrane require \(\lambda(s) \ge 0\). Setting \(\lambda(s) = 0\) requires \(\frac{1}{\bar{S}}\sqrt{1 - 2(s - \bar{s})} = 1 \implies \sqrt{1 - 2(s - \bar{s})} = \bar{S} \implies 1 - 2(s - \bar{s}) = \bar{S}^2 \implies 2(s - \bar{s}) = 1 - \bar{S}^2 \implies s_{\max} = \bar{s} + \frac{1}{2}(1 - \bar{S}^2)\).

Problem 2.6 · Independence of Risk-Free Rate from State Variables

Show that differentiating the conditional Euler equation for the risk-free bond with respect to \(s_t\) yields zero under the sensitivity function \(\lambda(s_t)\).

Solution

The conditional risk-free rate is \(r_{f, t} = -\ln \delta + \gamma g + \gamma(1-\phi)(\bar{s} - s_t) - \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma^2 [1 + \lambda(s_t)]^2\). Substitute \([1 + \lambda(s_t)]^2 = \frac{1}{\bar{S}^2}[1 - 2(s_t - \bar{s})] = \frac{1 - \phi}{\gamma \sigma^2}[1 - 2(s_t - \bar{s})]\). Then \(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma^2 [1 + \lambda(s_t)]^2 = -\frac{1}{2}\gamma(1-\phi)[1 - 2(s_t - \bar{s})] = -\frac{1}{2}\gamma(1-\phi) + \gamma(1-\phi)(s_t - \bar{s})\). Adding the linear term \(\gamma(1-\phi)(\bar{s} - s_t)\): \(\gamma(1-\phi)(\bar{s} - s_t) + \gamma(1-\phi)(s_t - \bar{s}) - \frac{1}{2}\gamma(1-\phi) = -\frac{1}{2}\gamma(1-\phi)\). The terms depending on \(s_t\) sum identically to zero, so \(\frac{\partial r_{f, t}}{\partial s_t} = 0\).

Empirical

Problem 2.7 · Calibrated Equity Sharpe Ratio

In Campbell & Cochrane (1999 Table 2), the annualized equity premium is \(6.64\%\) and equity volatility is \(15.2\%\). Compute the model-implied Sharpe ratio and compare it to the US historical benchmark.

Solution

The model-implied Sharpe ratio is \(\text{SR} = 0.0664 / 0.152 \approx 0.4368\). This closely matches the historical post-war US Sharpe ratio of \(\approx 0.40\) to \(0.45\), successfully clearing the Hansen-Jagannathan bound.

Problem 2.8 · Price-Dividend Ratio Volatility

In the Campbell-Cochrane calibration, the standard deviation of the log price-dividend ratio is \(\sigma(p-d) \approx 0.18\) in monthly simulation (\(\approx 0.28\) annually), compared to the empirical US figure of \(0.40\). What accounts for the model generating substantial valuation volatility despite smooth consumption growth?

Solution

Valuation volatility is generated endogenously: when a negative consumption shock occurs, \(s_t\) drops via \(\lambda(s_t) v_{t+1}\). Because \(\lambda(s_t) \approx 16\), a \(1.5\%\) consumption shock creates an immediate \(\approx 25\%\) collapse in surplus consumption. Households become intensely risk-averse, raising expected future discount rates and causing stock prices to plunge far more than current consumption.

Problem 2.9 · Return Predictability Regressions

Simulating the Campbell-Cochrane model over long samples yields a 5-year return predictability regression \(R^2\) of approximately 35% when regressing cumulative returns on the dividend-price ratio. Why does the model replicate this empirical fact?

Solution

Because the surplus consumption ratio \(s_t\) is highly persistent (\(\phi = 0.87\) annual), a low price-dividend ratio indicates that \(s_t\) is far below \(\bar{s}\). Over the subsequent 5 years, \(s_t\) slowly mean-reverts back to \(\bar{s}\), during which risk aversion falls and asset prices experience high excess returns, generating strong long-horizon predictability.

Research

Problem 2.10 · Microeconomic Foundations of Habit

Critique the macroeconomic realism of the Campbell-Cochrane surplus consumption specification. What microeconomic mechanisms could give rise to an external habit benchmark that adjusts slowly over a multi-year horizon?

Solution

Potential microeconomic foundations include: (1) social status competition and conspicuous consumption (“keeping up with the Joneses”), where utility depends on relative social standing; (2) physiological or psychological habituation to luxury goods and living standards; and (3) institutional consumption commitments (e.g. fixed mortgage obligations, private school tuitions, health insurance) that households cannot adjust downwards in the short run without severe adjustment costs.

Problem 2.11 · Cross-Sectional Asset Pricing Implications

How does the Campbell-Cochrane habit framework apply to the cross-section of value versus growth stocks? Does habit formation naturally explain the value premium?

Solution

Standard habit models struggle to explain the value premium without additional cash-flow assumptions. If all stocks have the same cash-flow exposure to consumption, growth stocks (whose cash flows are concentrated in the far future) have higher duration and thus load more heavily on discount-rate risk, predicting a growth premium rather than a value premium. To explain the value premium, value firm cash flows must be explicitly modeled as more cyclical in severe recessions (distress risk).

Problem 2.12 · Term Structure of Interest Rates

By construction, Campbell and Cochrane force the real risk-free rate to be perfectly constant. If an author extends the model to allow for time-varying interest rates, what trade-off arises between matching term premia and preserving the resolution of the equity premium puzzle?

Solution

Allowing the interest rate to vary requires modifying \(\lambda(s)\) so that intertemporal substitution and precautionary savings no longer cancel. If intertemporal substitution dominates, the risk-free rate becomes countercyclical (high in recessions), exacerbating the Weil puzzle. If precautionary savings dominates, \(r_f\) becomes procyclical, but bond returns become positively correlated with equity returns, distorting the equity premium and bond risk premia (Wachter 2006).

Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Campbell & Cochrane (1999) model, how is the habit level \(X_t\) treated by the representative household?

    Options

    Habits are external (“catching up with the Joneses”), meaning households do not internalize how their individual consumption decisions affect future habit levels.

  2. What is local relative risk aversion \(\gamma_t\) equal to in the Campbell-Cochrane model?

    Options

    From \(-C u''(C)/u'(C) = \gamma C / (C - X) = \gamma / S_t\). As consumption approaches habit (\(S_t \to 0\)), risk aversion approaches infinity.

  3. Compute the steady-state surplus consumption ratio \(\bar{S}\) for \(\sigma = 0.0150\), \(\gamma = 2.0\), and \(\phi = 0.87\) to four decimal places.

    \(\bar{S} = 0.0150 \times \sqrt{2.0 / (1 - 0.87)} = 0.0150 \times \sqrt{15.3846} = 0.0150 \times 3.9223 \approx 0.0588\).

  4. What economic mechanism ensures that the real risk-free rate is constant in the Campbell-Cochrane model?

    Options

    Campbell and Cochrane engineer \(\lambda(s)\) so that as \(s_t\) falls, higher precautionary savings demand exactly offsets the desire to borrow against higher expected future consumption.

  5. In recessions when the surplus consumption ratio \(S_t\) is low, how does the price-dividend ratio behave?

    Options

    When \(S_t\) is low, households demand high risk premia, depressing equity valuations. As the economy recovers and \(S_t\) expands, valuations rise.

  6. If \(\bar{S} = 0.06\), compute the value of the sensitivity function \(\lambda(\bar{s})\) at steady state (\(s = \bar{s}\)) to two decimal places.

    \(\lambda(\bar{s}) = (1 / 0.06) \times \sqrt{1 - 0} - 1 = 16.6667 - 1 \approx 15.67\).

  7. What is a primary theoretical critique of the Campbell-Cochrane model?

    Options

    Microeconomic empirical studies find little support for such extreme risk aversion swings or slow-moving external habit persistence at the household level.

  8. Why does the Campbell-Cochrane model generate strong return predictability by the dividend-price ratio?

    Options

    High dividend yields reflect depressed surplus ratios. As \(s_t\) mean-reverts toward \(\bar{s}\), expected excess returns remain elevated, generating long-horizon return predictability.
