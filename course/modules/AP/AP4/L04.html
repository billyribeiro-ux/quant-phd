Rare Disasters & Tail Risk: Rietz, Barro, and Wachter

Standard consumption models assume macroeconomic fluctuations follow thin-tailed Gaussian distributions. Rietz (1988) and Barro (2006) showed that incorporating rare, catastrophic economic contractions—such as the Great Depression, World Wars, and financial collapses—reconciles observed equity premia with low risk aversion. Massive marginal utility during disasters generates high risk premia and intense precautionary demand for risk-free assets, while Wachter (2013) proved that time-varying disaster probability generates excess market volatility.

1The Rietz Hypothesis & International Historical Disasters

In response to Mehra & Prescott (1985), Rietz (1988) proposed that equity risk premia compensate investors for rare, devastating economic events that may not even occur in a sample window. While originally dismissed as improbable, Barro (2006) constructed a comprehensive century-long panel of 35 countries across the 20th century, documenting that macroeconomic disasters are regular, global occurrences.

Definition 4.1 (Rare-Disasters Model)

In the rare-disasters model, aggregate consumption \(C_t\) and corporate dividends \(D_t\) evolve in discrete time with compound Poisson jump contractions:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln(C_{t+1}/C_t) &= g + \sigma \epsilon_{t+1} \\ &\quad + \ln(1 - b_{t+1}) J_{t+1} \\ \ln(D_{t+1}/D_t) &= g_d + \sigma_d \epsilon_{d, t+1} \\ &\quad + \ln(1 - b_{d, t+1}) J_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\epsilon_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) is normal Gaussian diffusion, \(J_{t+1} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)\) indicates the occurrence of a rare disaster with annual probability \(p \approx 0.017\) (1.7% per year), and \(b_{t+1} \in (0, 1)\) is the disaster contraction fraction with mean \(\bar{b} \approx 0.29\) (a 29% drop in per capita consumption).

Because marginal utility with CRRA preferences is \(u'(C) = C^{-\gamma}\), a 29% contraction in consumption multiplies marginal utility during a disaster by \((1 - 0.29)^{-\gamma} \approx 0.71^{-4} \approx 3.93\) when \(\gamma = 4\). For a 50% collapse, marginal utility multiplies by \(0.50^{-4} = 16.0\). Consequently, disaster states dominate risk expectations even when the disaster probability \(p\) is under 2% per year.

2Analytical Asset Pricing: Precautionary Rates & Calm-Sample Premia

Consider a representative agent with time discount factor \(\delta\) and relative risk aversion \(\gamma\). The stochastic discount factor is \(M_{t+1} = \delta (C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\gamma}\).

Theorem 4.1 (Disaster-Adjusted Equilibrium Interest Rate)

The equilibrium gross risk-free rate \(R_f = 1/\E_t[M_{t+1}]\) satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} R_f &= \frac{\exp(\gamma g - \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma^2)}{\delta \left[ (1 - p) + p \E \left[ (1 - b)^{-\gamma} \right] \right]} \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\E[(1 - b)^{-\gamma}] > 1\), the presence of rare disasters sharply lowers the equilibrium risk-free rate via a massive precautionary savings motive, resolving the risk-free rate puzzle.

Proof sketch

Evaluate the expected pricing kernel \(\E_t[M_{t+1}]\). Because diffusion \(\epsilon_{t+1}\) is independent of disaster occurrence \(J_{t+1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_t[M_{t+1}] &= \delta \exp(-\gamma g + \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma^2) \\ &\quad \times \left[ (1 - p) + p \E \left[ (1 - b)^{-\gamma} \right] \right] \end{aligned} \]

Inverting \(\E_t[M_{t+1}]\) yields \(R_f\). Because \((1-b) \in (0, 1)\) and \(\gamma > 0\), \((1-b)^{-\gamma} > 1\), increasing expected pricing kernel \(\E_t[M_{t+1}]\) and strictly depressing the real risk-free rate \(R_f\).

3The Peso Problem & Observed Calm-Sample Equity Premia

A central insight of disaster asset pricing is the sharp divergence between the ex-ante population equity premium and the ex-post observed sample equity premium.

Definition 4.2 (The Peso Problem in Asset Pricing)

A peso problem occurs when asset prices reflect the possibility of rare catastrophic events that happen not to occur within an econometrician's finite historical sample. In calm (non-disaster) periods, realized stock returns appear abnormally high relative to realized smooth consumption growth, leading econometricians to erroneously estimate extreme risk aversion.

Let \(P_t / D_t\) be the price-dividend ratio of equity. The equity claim satisfies \(\E_t \left[ M_{t+1} \frac{P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}}{D_{t+1}} \frac{D_{t+1}}{D_t} \right] = \frac{P_t}{D_t}\). In steady state, \(\frac{P}{D} = \frac{\alpha_d}{1 - \alpha_d}\), where

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_d &= \delta \exp\left( (1 - \gamma) g + \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - \gamma)^2 \sigma^2 \right) \\ &\quad \times \left[ (1 - p) + p \E \left[ (1 - b)^{-\gamma} (1 - b_d) \right] \right] \end{aligned} \]

In a calm sample where no disaster occurs (\(J_{t+1} = 0\)), the realized return on equity is

\[ \begin{aligned} R_{m, t+1}^{\text{calm}} &= \frac{P_{t+1} + D_{t+1}}{P_t} \\ &= \frac{P/D + 1}{P/D} \exp(g_d + \sigma_d \epsilon_{d, t+1}) \end{aligned} \]

The observed sample equity premium in calm periods is \(\E[R_m^{\text{calm}}] - R_f\), which easily reaches 4% to 6% per annum even when true relative risk aversion is only \(\gamma = 3\) to \(4\).

4Time-Varying Disaster Risk: The Wachter (2013) Extension

While Barro's constant-probability framework explains unconditional means (\(R_f\) and \(\E[R_m - R_f]\)), it cannot explain excess volatility or return predictability because price-dividend ratios remain strictly constant. Wachter (2013) resolved this by modeling the disaster probability \(\lambda_t\) as a continuous-time mean-reverting process:

\[ \begin{aligned} d\lambda_t &= \kappa (\bar{\lambda} - \lambda_t) dt + \sigma_\lambda \sqrt{\lambda_t} dW_t^\lambda \end{aligned} \]

Combined with Epstein-Zin recursive preferences, when disaster probability \(\lambda_t\) spikes, investors fear catastrophic contractions, depressing stock prices and raising dividend yields. As disaster risk recedes, prices recover, generating time-varying volatility and robust return predictability matching empirical financial markets.

Lab 1 · Barro (2006) Rare Disaster Asset Pricing

Calibrating Barro's rare-disaster model with an annual disaster probability of 1.7% and average 29% consumption contraction will reduce the equilibrium risk-free rate below 9% via precautionary savings and produce an observed calm-sample equity premium exceeding 3% with relative risk aversion gamma = 4.

"""Lab AP4-L04-1: Barro (2006) rare disaster pricing and resolution of asset pricing puzzles.

Evaluates the equity premium, precautionary risk-free rate, and price-dividend ratio
under compound Poisson macroeconomic disasters with international calibration parameters.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np

# International historical disaster calibration (Barro 2006 Table IV)
g = 0.025  # Normal per-capita GDP/consumption growth rate
sigma = 0.020  # Normal consumption growth standard deviation
p_disaster = 0.017  # Disaster probability per year (~1.7% per year)
b_mean = 0.29  # Average contraction in consumption during disaster (29%)
b_d = 0.435  # Average contraction in equity/dividends during disaster (43.5%)
delta = 0.970  # Subjective discount factor
gamma = 4.0  # Relative risk aversion

# 1. Expected pricing kernel for risk-free bond: E[M]
# Under log-normal normal growth + Bernoulli disaster jump:
# E[M] = delta * exp(-gamma * g + 0.5 * gamma^2 * sigma^2) * [(1 - p) + p * (1 - b)^(-gamma)]
base_disc = delta * np.exp(-gamma * g + 0.5 * gamma**2 * sigma**2)
precautionary_term = (1.0 - p_disaster) + p_disaster * ((1.0 - b_mean) ** (-gamma))
em = base_disc * precautionary_term
rf_gross = 1.0 / em
rf_net = float(rf_gross - 1.0)

# 2. Risk-free rate without disasters (standard Lucas CCAPM):
rf_no_disaster = float(1.0 / base_disc - 1.0)

# 3. Pricing kernel expectation for equity: E[M * (D'/D)]
base_equity = delta * np.exp((1.0 - gamma) * g + 0.5 * (1.0 - gamma) ** 2 * sigma**2)
equity_jump_term = (1.0 - p_disaster) + p_disaster * ((1.0 - b_mean) ** (-gamma)) * (1.0 - b_d)
alpha_d = base_equity * equity_jump_term
pd_ratio = float(alpha_d / (1.0 - alpha_d))

# 4. Expected equity return in calm (non-disaster) sample:
expected_calm_return = float(((pd_ratio + 1.0) / pd_ratio) * np.exp(g + 0.5 * (0.05**2)) - 1.0)
calm_equity_premium = expected_calm_return - rf_net

print(f"Risk Aversion gamma: {gamma:.1f}")
print(f"Annual Disaster Probability: {p_disaster * 100:.2f}%")
print(f"Disaster Contraction Size: Consumption = {b_mean * 100:.1f}%, Equity = {b_d * 100:.1f}%")
print(f"Risk-free rate without disasters: {rf_no_disaster * 100:.2f}%")
print(f"Precautionary savings factor: {precautionary_term:.4f}")
print(f"Equilibrium Risk-free rate with disasters r_f: {rf_net * 100:.2f}%")
print(f"Equilibrium Price-Dividend ratio P/D: {pd_ratio:.2f}")
print(f"Expected Equity Return (calm sample): {expected_calm_return * 100:.2f}%")
print(f"Observed Equity Premium (calm sample): {calm_equity_premium * 100:.2f}%")
Risk Aversion gamma: 4.0
Annual Disaster Probability: 1.70%
Disaster Contraction Size: Consumption = 29.0%, Equity = 43.5%
Risk-free rate without disasters: 13.57%
Precautionary savings factor: 1.0499
Equilibrium Risk-free rate with disasters r_f: 8.17%
Equilibrium Price-Dividend ratio P/D: 11.54
Expected Equity Return (calm sample): 11.55%
Observed Equity Premium (calm sample): 3.38%

The numerical simulation confirms that rare disaster risk reduces the risk-free rate from 13.57% down to 8.17% via a 5% precautionary savings factor. The calm-sample observed equity premium is 3.38%, explaining how modest risk aversion (gamma = 4) resolves the equity premium and risk-free rate puzzles.

Where the canon breaks

While the rare-disasters framework provides an intuitive, non-linear resolution of macroeconomic asset pricing puzzles, it faces three critical challenges:

  • Empirical Non-Falsifiability: Because disasters occur roughly once every 60 years in any given nation, standard statistical tests cannot reject or confirm the hypothesis in single-country post-war data. Any market anomaly can be rationalized by positing an unobserved tail disaster.
  • Government Bond Default Risk: Barro (2006) assumes government bonds are strictly default-free during disasters. Historically, countries experiencing 30%+ GDP contractions frequently defaulted on sovereign debt or hyperinflated currency, reducing the flight-to-safety precautionary savings effect.
  • Deep Out-of-the-Money Option Prices: If disaster probability were high enough to generate the entire equity premium, deep out-of-the-money put options would trade at implied volatilities significantly higher than historically observed in index option markets.

Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “The rare-disasters model assumes investors are irrational or suffer from panic delusions about nonexistent crises.”

    Investors are fully rational expected-utility maximizers. The 20th-century historical record confirms that catastrophic disasters (World Wars, Russian Revolution, Great Depression) occurred regularly across global economies, justifying rational ex-ante risk pricing.

  2. “Disaster risk increases the equilibrium risk-free rate because investors demand higher yields during uncertain times.”

    Disaster risk dramatically decreases the equilibrium risk-free rate. Because default-free bonds pay off in disaster states where marginal utility is astronomical, households exhibit enormous precautionary demand to hold safe bonds, driving their yields downward.

  3. “The ex-ante population equity premium is identical to the average excess return observed in historical US data.”

    Because the United States experienced fewer devastating wartime destructions than the global average during the 20th century, the US historical sample is a 'calm' realization. Observed US equity premia are therefore higher than the true ex-ante population expectation.

Mechanics

Problem 4.1 · Marginal Utility Disaster Multiplier

If relative risk aversion \(\gamma = 3.5\), calculate the ratio of marginal utility in a disaster state with contraction \(b = 0.35\) to marginal utility in the pre-disaster state.

Solution

The marginal utility ratio is \((1 - b)^{-\gamma}\). For \(b = 0.35\), \((0.65)^{-3.5} \approx 4.49\). Marginal utility is nearly 4.5 times higher in the disaster state.

Problem 4.2 · Precautionary Savings Factor

Compute the precautionary savings factor \((1 - p) + p(1 - b)^{-\gamma}\) for disaster probability \(p = 0.02\), contraction \(b = 0.40\), and risk aversion \(\gamma = 3.0\).

Solution

\((1 - b)^{-\gamma} = (0.60)^{-3} \approx 4.6296\). Precautionary factor is \(0.98 + 0.02 \times 4.6296 = 1.0726\).

Problem 4.3 · Price-Dividend Ratio Under Disaster Risk

If \(\alpha_d = 0.94\), compute the equilibrium price-dividend ratio \(P/D = \frac{\alpha_d}{1 - \alpha_d}\) and the corresponding dividend yield \(D/P\).

Solution

\(P/D = 0.94 / 0.06 \approx 15.67\). The dividend yield is \(D/P = 1 / 15.6667 \approx 0.0638\) (6.38%).

Proof

Problem 4.4 · Monotonicity of Risk-Free Rate with Respect to Disaster Probability

Prove that \(\frac{\partial R_f}{\partial p} < 0\), showing that an increase in disaster probability strictly reduces the equilibrium risk-free rate.

Solution

From Theorem 4.1, \(R_f = K / [1 + p(\E[(1 - b)^{-\gamma}] - 1)]\), where \(K > 0\) is independent of \(p\). Because \(b \in (0, 1)\) and \(\gamma > 0\), \((1 - b)^{-\gamma} > 1\), so \(\E[(1 - b)^{-\gamma}] - 1 > 0\). Differentiating with respect to \(p\): \(\frac{\partial R_f}{\partial p} = \frac{-K (\E[(1 - b)^{-\gamma}] - 1)}{[1 + p(\E[(1 - b)^{-\gamma}] - 1)]^2} < 0\). Thus, higher disaster probability increases precautionary demand and lowers the risk-free rate.

Problem 4.5 · Derivation of Calm-Sample Return Wedge

Show that the expected equity return conditioned on no disaster occurring strictly exceeds the unconditional ex-ante expected equity return.

Solution

Unconditional gross return is a probability-weighted expectation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[R_{m, t+1}] &= (1 - p) \E[R_m \mid J=0] + p \E[R_m \mid J=1] \ &= \E[R_m \mid J=0] - p (\E[R_m \mid J=0] - \E[R_m \mid J=1]) \end{aligned} \]

During disasters, \(\E[R_m \mid J=1] < \E[R_m \mid J=0]\), which implies \(\E[R_{m, t+1}] < \E[R_m \mid J=0]\). Conditioning on no disaster removes the tail, producing a calm-sample return wedge equal to \(p (\E[R_m \mid J=0] - \E[R_m \mid J=1]) > 0\).

Problem 4.6 · Convexity of Precautionary Demand in Contraction Size

Prove that the precautionary savings term \(\E[(1 - b)^{-\gamma}]\) is strictly convex in the contraction magnitude \(b\) for any \(\gamma > 0\).

Solution

Let \(f(b) = (1 - b)^{-\gamma}\). Taking the first derivative with respect to \(b\): \(f'(b) = \gamma (1 - b)^{-\gamma - 1} > 0\). Taking the second derivative: \(f''(b) = \gamma (\gamma + 1) (1 - b)^{-\gamma - 2} > 0\) for all \(b \in (0, 1)\) and \(\gamma > 0\). By Jensen's inequality, an increase in the dispersion or uncertainty of disaster severity \(\text{Var}(b)\) holding mean \(\E[b]\) constant strictly increases \(\E[(1 - b)^{-\gamma}]\), further driving down the risk-free rate.

Empirical

Problem 4.7 · Barro's International 20th-Century Dataset

Describe Barro's (2006) identification criteria for macroeconomic disasters. What peak-to-trough GDP decline constitutes a disaster, and how many disaster events were identified across 35 countries?

Solution

Barro defined a macroeconomic disaster as a cumulative peak-to-trough contraction in real per capita GDP of at least 15%. Across 35 countries over the 20th century (1900–2000), Barro identified 60 major disaster episodes, corresponding to an empirical disaster arrival rate of approximately 1.7% per year per country, with an average cumulative contraction of 29%.

Problem 4.8 · Out-of-the-Money Index Put Options

How do deep out-of-the-money S&P 500 index put options provide an empirical test of the rare-disasters model? What do empirical implied volatility smirks indicate about disaster probability?

Solution

Deep out-of-the-money put options act as catastrophe insurance. If the rare disaster hypothesis is correct, option-implied disaster probabilities extracted from the volatility smirk should match macroeconomic tail probabilities. Du (2011) and Backus et al. (2011) find that while option smirks confirm priced jump risk, option prices imply disaster frequencies that fluctuate substantially over time, supporting Wachter's time-varying disaster model rather than a constant-intensity disaster model.

Problem 4.9 · Sovereign Bond Haircuts in Wartime

Evaluate the empirical realism of assuming government bonds are completely default-free during major macroeconomic disasters. How do sovereign haircuts alter the risk-free rate prediction?

Solution

In Barro & Ursúa (2012), sovereign debt data reveals that over 30% of major macroeconomic crises involved partial sovereign default, restructuring, or hyperinflationary debasement. When government bonds experience default haircuts during disasters, bonds lose their status as pure hedges. This weakens the precautionary savings demand for government bonds, predicting higher equilibrium bond yields and reducing the model's ability to simultaneously match low bond yields and high equity premia.

Research

Problem 4.10 · Microeconomic Risk-Sharing vs Macroeconomic Disasters

How does idiosyncratic consumption risk interact with aggregate rare disasters? Does disaster risk concentrate in specific sub-populations (e.g. unemployed, leveraged businesses)?

Solution

During severe macroeconomic contractions, unemployment surges and household income dispersions widen dramatically. Schmidt (2014) and Guvenen et al. (2014) show that aggregate disasters are accompanied by severe left-skewed idiosyncratic earning shocks. When incomplete markets prevent risk-sharing, the disaster burden is concentrated on liquidity-constrained households, magnifying effective pricing kernel volatility beyond representative-agent benchmarks.

Problem 4.11 · Time-Varying Disaster Probability vs Habit Formation

Compare the mechanisms generating return predictability in Wachter (2013) versus Campbell & Cochrane (1999). What macroeconomic data can discriminate between time-varying tail risk and time-varying risk aversion?

Solution

In Campbell-Cochrane, return predictability is driven by time-varying risk aversion generated by slow-moving surplus consumption ratios. In Wachter, risk aversion is constant, but the perceived objective hazard rate of disaster \(\lambda_t\) varies over time. Discriminating between them requires forward-looking option prices (which directly reflect tail risk intensities) and credit default swap spreads: Wachter predicts option implied volatility and disaster intensity track price-dividend ratios, whereas habit models predict smooth shifts in willingness to take Gaussian risk.

Problem 4.12 · The Role of Production and Capital Accumulation

In pure exchange models (Barro 2006), disasters destroy the consumption endowment exogenously. What happens when rare disasters are introduced into DSGE production economies with endogenous capital accumulation (Gourio 2012)?

Solution

In a production economy with capital (Gourio 2012), disaster shocks destroy physical capital directly or lower total factor productivity. An increase in disaster risk causes firms to slash investment, leading to an immediate recession in output and employment even before a disaster occurs. Furthermore, endogenous capital accumulation gives households an alternative saving vehicle, dampening the precautionary yield drop on bonds unless safe assets are explicitly differentiated from risky physical capital.
