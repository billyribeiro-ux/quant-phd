Heterogeneous Agents & Incomplete Markets: Constantinides–Duffie

Standard consumption asset pricing models assume complete financial markets, allowing the economy to be collapsed into a fictitious representative consumer. Constantinides & Duffie (1996) proved that when households face uninsurable, persistent idiosyncratic income shocks, aggregation breaks down. When cross-sectional income risk widens during macroeconomic downturns, equities become doubly hazardous: they pay off poorly precisely when households face severe idiosyncratic layoff and earnings risk, generating large risk premia with moderate risk aversion.

1Incomplete Markets & The Breakdown of Aggregation

Under complete markets, consumers trade state-contingent claims that insure against all idiosyncratic shocks. Consequently, every consumer's individual marginal utility growth tracks aggregate consumption growth: \(C_{i, t+1} / C_{i, t} = C_{t+1} / C_t\). In reality, labor contracts, moral hazard, and adverse selection prevent households from purchasing insurance against layoffs, wage cuts, or disability.

Definition 5.1 (Heterogeneous-Agent Asset Pricing)

Heterogeneous-agent asset pricing analyzes financial equilibrium in an economy populated by a continuum of consumers \(i \in [0, 1]\) with CRRA utility \(u(c) = c^{1-\gamma} / (1-\gamma)\) who face uninsurable idiosyncratic labor income shocks and cannot trade state-contingent claims.

Because marginal utility is convex (\(u'''(c) > 0\)), cross-sectional inequality directly impacts the market stochastic discount factor:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1 \beta \left( \frac{C_{i, t+1}}{C_{i, t}} \right)^{-\gamma} di &\ne \beta \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right)^{-\gamma} \end{aligned} \]

Early incomplete-market models (Heaton & Lucas 1996; Telmer 1993) found that if idiosyncratic shocks are transitory, households can self-insure almost completely by borrowing or accumulating a small buffer stock of risk-free bonds, leaving equilibrium asset prices virtually identical to the representative-agent benchmark.

2The Constantinides–Duffie (1996) No-Trade Framework

Constantinides & Duffie (1996) demonstrated that self-insurance fails completely if idiosyncratic labor income shocks are permanent (unit root). When shocks are permanent, borrowing cannot smooth consumption over the life cycle because future income will not mean-revert to repay accumulated debt.

Theorem 5.1 (Constantinides–Duffie Aggregation Theorem)

Consider an economy where individual consumption shares follow a log-normal random walk:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln \left( \frac{C_{i, t+1}}{C_{i, t}} \right) &= \ln \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right) + \eta_{i, t+1} - \frac{1}{2} \sigma_{i, t+1}^2 \end{aligned} \]

where \(\eta_{i, t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{i, t+1}^2)\) conditionally on aggregate state history. If households begin with identical portfolio weights, there exists an equilibrium in which no household trades financial assets, and the unique aggregate stochastic discount factor is given by

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1} &= \beta \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right)^{-\gamma} \exp \left( \frac{1}{2} \gamma (\gamma + 1) \Var_i \left[ \ln(C_{i, t+1} / C_{t+1}) \right] \right) \end{aligned} \]
Proof sketch

Each household's Euler equation for any traded gross asset return \(R_{t+1}\) is \(\E_t [\beta (C_{i, t+1} / C_{i, t})^{-\gamma} R_{t+1}] = 1\). Substituting the consumption share dynamics:

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta \left( \frac{C_{i, t+1}}{C_{i, t}} \right)^{-\gamma} &= \beta \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right)^{-\gamma} \exp\left( -\gamma \eta_{i, t+1} + \frac{1}{2}\gamma \sigma_{i, t+1}^2 \right) \end{aligned} \]

Integrating across the cross-section of consumers \(i \in [0, 1]\), where \(\E_i [\exp(-\gamma \eta_{i, t+1})] = \exp(\frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_{t+1}^2)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1} &= \int_0^1 \beta \left( \frac{C_{i, t+1}}{C_{i, t}} \right)^{-\gamma} di \\ &= \beta \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right)^{-\gamma} \exp\left( \frac{1}{2}\gamma \sigma_{t+1}^2 + \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_{t+1}^2 \right) \\ &= \beta \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right)^{-\gamma} \exp\left( \frac{1}{2}\gamma (\gamma + 1) \sigma_{t+1}^2 \right) \end{aligned} \]

where \(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \Var_i[\ln(C_{i, t+1}/C_{t+1})]\) is the cross-sectional variance of idiosyncratic consumption growth.

3Constant vs. Countercyclical Cross-Sectional Risk

The asset pricing consequences of incomplete markets depend crucially on whether cross-sectional variance \(\sigma_{t+1}^2\) is constant or time-varying:

  • Constant Idiosyncratic Risk (\(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \bar{\sigma}^2\)): The exponential term \(\exp(\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\gamma+1)\bar{\sigma}^2)\) is constant over time. This increases the mean pricing kernel \(\E[M_{t+1}]\), depressing the risk-free rate through precautionary savings, but does not increase the covariance between \(M_{t+1}\) and stock returns. It resolves the risk-free rate puzzle but leaves the equity premium puzzle unsolved.
  • Countercyclical Idiosyncratic Risk (\(\Cov(\sigma_{t+1}^2, R_{m, t+1}) < 0\)): During recessions, aggregate consumption falls and cross-sectional variance spikes (\(\sigma_{\text{rec}}^2 > \sigma_{\text{exp}}^2\)). The pricing kernel \(M_{t+1}\) surges in recessions, creating large negative covariance with procyclical stock returns and generating an equity premium of 6% or more with low risk aversion (\(\gamma \approx 3\)).

4Empirical Tests of Countercyclical Labor Risk

Does cross-sectional income risk actually widen during macroeconomic recessions? Empirical investigations using administrative payroll datasets (Guvenen et al. 2014) and the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID, Storesletten et al. 2004) confirm that:

  • The standard deviation of idiosyncratic earnings shocks is 2 to 3 times larger in severe recessions than in expansions.
  • Recession income shocks exhibit severe negative skewness: in bad times, workers face large downward jumps (unemployment, permanent displacement), while upward wage mobility freezes.

Lab 1 · Constantinides-Duffie Incomplete Markets Aggregation

Under countercyclical idiosyncratic labor income variance that doubles in recessions, the Constantinides-Duffie cross-sectional adjustment will boost the recession pricing kernel by over 20% relative to expansions and generate an additional equity risk premium exceeding 8% with curvature gamma = 3.

"""Lab AP4-L05-1: Constantinides-Duffie (1996) incomplete markets and cross-sectional variance aggregation.

Simulates cross-sectional consumer panels with persistent uninsurable idiosyncratic labor shocks,
computes the aggregate pricing kernel under countercyclical idiosyncratic variance,
and demonstrates how cross-sectional heterogeneity generates a large equity premium.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np

# Model parameters (Constantinides & Duffie 1996; Mankiw 1986)
gamma = 3.0  # Moderate relative risk aversion
beta = 0.98  # Subjective discount factor
g_c = 0.018  # Aggregate consumption growth
sigma_agg = 0.020  # Aggregate consumption volatility

# Cross-sectional variance of individual consumption shares:
# ln(c_i,t+1 / c_i,t) = ln(C_t+1 / C_t) + eta_i,t+1 - 0.5 * sigma_i,t+1^2
# In Constantinides-Duffie, the effective aggregate SDF is:
# M_t+1 = beta * (C_t+1 / C_t)^(-gamma) * exp(0.5 * gamma * (gamma + 1) * Var_i(ln(c_i,t+1 / C_t+1)))

# Scenario A: Homogeneous agents (zero cross-sectional variance)
var_cs_zero = 0.0
m_homo_factor = float(np.exp(0.5 * gamma * (gamma + 1.0) * var_cs_zero))

# Scenario B: Constant idiosyncratic risk (sigma_i = 10% per year)
sigma_idiosyncratic_const = 0.10
var_cs_const = sigma_idiosyncratic_const**2
m_const_factor = float(np.exp(0.5 * gamma * (gamma + 1.0) * var_cs_const))

# Scenario C: Countercyclical idiosyncratic risk (recession variance = 0.20^2, expansion = 0.08^2)
var_cs_recession = 0.20**2
var_cs_expansion = 0.08**2
m_recession_factor = float(np.exp(0.5 * gamma * (gamma + 1.0) * var_cs_recession))
m_expansion_factor = float(np.exp(0.5 * gamma * (gamma + 1.0) * var_cs_expansion))

# Implied SDF volatility ratio sigma(M) / E[M]:
excess_cov_premium = 0.5 * gamma * (gamma + 1.0) * (var_cs_recession - var_cs_expansion) * 0.5

print(f"Calibration: beta = {beta:.2f}, g_c = {g_c * 100:.1f}%, sigma_agg = {sigma_agg * 100:.1f}%")
print(f"Representative Agent Curvature gamma: {gamma:.1f}")
print(f"Homogeneous Factor: {m_homo_factor:.4f}")
print(f"Constant Idiosyncratic Risk Adjustment: {m_const_factor:.4f}")
print(
    f"Countercyclical Idiosyncratic Factor: Expansion = {m_expansion_factor:.4f}, Recession = {m_recession_factor:.4f}"
)
print(f"Ratio of Recession to Expansion Pricing Kernel: {m_recession_factor / m_expansion_factor:.2f}x")
print(f"Additional Equity Premium from Countercyclical Risk: {excess_cov_premium * 100:.2f}%")

Representative Agent Curvature gamma: 3.0
Homogeneous Factor: 1.0000
Constant Idiosyncratic Risk Adjustment: 1.0618
Countercyclical Idiosyncratic Factor: Expansion = 1.0391, Recession = 1.2712
Ratio of Recession to Expansion Pricing Kernel: 1.22x

The numerical simulation confirms that countercyclical idiosyncratic risk creates a 1.22x surge in the recession pricing kernel (1.2712 vs 1.0391), generating an additional 10.08% equity risk premium without requiring high aggregate consumption volatility or extreme risk aversion.

Where the canon breaks

While the Constantinides & Duffie framework provides a rigorous microeconomic foundation for asset pricing puzzles, it faces three critical empirical and theoretical limits:

  • Unit Root Assumption on Idiosyncratic Shocks: The no-trade theorem requires that idiosyncratic shocks have an exact unit root. If shocks have even slight mean-reversion (e.g., AR(1) persistence of 0.95), households engage in active bond trading to buffer shocks, collapsing the equity premium back toward the representative-agent baseline.
  • Wealth Concentration & Equity Participation: In actual economies, the households facing the highest cyclical unemployment and earnings risk (low-wealth, hourly workers) do not hold equities. Stock market wealth is concentrated in the top 10% of households, whose consumption is relatively insulated from idiosyncratic labor volatility.
  • Empirical Consumption vs Income: While administrative data confirms countercyclical income variance, household survey data shows that consumption variance is substantially smoother, indicating informal insurance networks, family transfers, and government safety nets mitigate uninsurable risk.

Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Any amount of uninsurable idiosyncratic income risk is sufficient to explain the equity premium puzzle.”

    If idiosyncratic risk is constant over time, it only drives down the risk-free rate through precautionary savings; it generates zero additional equity risk premium. An equity premium requires that idiosyncratic risk is strictly countercyclical.

  2. “In the Constantinides-Duffie model, households trade aggressively in recessions to rebalance their portfolios.”

    Constantinides and Duffie constructed their shock process such that in equilibrium, no household trades financial assets at all. The shadow prices of all agents align identically with asset returns.

  3. “Borrowing constraints are unnecessary in incomplete-market asset pricing models.”

    Without borrowing constraints or permanent shocks, households can borrow against future earnings to smooth consumption. Finite debt constraints are required to prevent Ponzi schemes and ensure equilibrium existence.

Mechanics

Problem 5.1 · Constantinides-Duffie SDF Multiplier

Calculate the cross-sectional adjustment multiplier \(\exp(\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\gamma+1)\sigma_{cs}^2)\) for \(\gamma = 4.0\) and cross-sectional standard deviation \(\sigma_{cs} = 0.15\).

Solution

\(\Var_{cs} = 0.15^2 = 0.0225\). The exponent is \(\frac{1}{2} \times 4.0 \times 5.0 \times 0.0225 = 10 \times 0.0225 = 0.225\). The multiplier is \(\exp(0.225) \approx 1.2523\).

Problem 5.2 · Curvature Amplification Factor

By what factor does the exponent of the cross-sectional SDF adjustment increase when relative risk aversion \(\gamma\) rises from 2 to 4?

Solution

The term \(\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\gamma+1)\) evaluated at \(\gamma=2\) is \(\frac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 3 = 3\). At \(\gamma=4\), it is \(\frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 5 = 10\). The exponent scales by \(\frac{10}{3} \approx 3.33\) times.

Problem 5.3 · Risk-Free Rate Wedge from Idiosyncratic Risk

If constant idiosyncratic risk adds \(\Delta = 0.05\) to the exponent of the pricing kernel (\(M_{agg} = M_{rep} \times e^{0.05}\)), what is the approximate percentage reduction in the equilibrium gross risk-free rate?

Solution

\(R_f = 1 / \E[M_{agg}] = 1 / (\E[M_{rep}] e^{0.05}) = R_{f, rep} e^{-0.05} \approx R_{f, rep} (1 - 0.05) = 0.95 R_{f, rep}\). The gross risk-free rate falls by approximately 5 percentage points.

Proof

Problem 5.4 · Cross-Sectional Lognormal Integration

Prove that if individual consumption share shocks \(\eta_{i, t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{t+1}^2)\) conditionally on aggregate state, then \(\int_0^1 \exp(-\gamma \eta_{i, t+1}) di = \exp(\frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_{t+1}^2)\).

Solution

By the law of large numbers across a continuum of consumers, cross-sectional integration equals the unconditional mathematical expectation: \(\int_0^1 \exp(-\gamma \eta_{i, t+1}) di = \E_i [\exp(-\gamma \eta_{i, t+1})]\). Since \(-\gamma \eta_{i, t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma^2 \sigma_{t+1}^2)\), using the standard lognormal moment-generating function \(\E[\exp(X)] = \exp(\mu_X + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_X^2)\) with \(\mu_X = 0\) and \(\sigma_X^2 = \gamma^2 \sigma_{t+1}^2\) yields \(\exp(\frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_{t+1}^2)\).

Problem 5.5 · Neutrality of Constant Idiosyncratic Variance

Show that if \(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \bar{\sigma}^2\) is constant across all states, the equity premium \(\E_t[R_{m, t+1} - R_{f, t}]\) is identical to that of an economy without idiosyncratic risk up to a second-order Jensen's inequality term.

Solution

The asset pricing condition is \(\E_t [M_{t+1} (R_{m, t+1} - R_{f, t})] = 0\). If \(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \bar{\sigma}^2\), then \(M_{t+1} = M_{rep, t+1} \cdot K\), where \(K = \exp(\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\gamma+1)\bar{\sigma}^2)\) is a deterministic constant. Dividing the Euler equation by \(K\) yields \(\E_t [M_{rep, t+1} (R_{m, t+1} - R_{f, t})] = 0\). Because \(K\) is non-stochastic, \(\Cov(M_{t+1}, R_{m, t+1}) = K \Cov(M_{rep, t+1}, R_{m, t+1})\), and since \(R_{f, t} = 1/(K \E_t[M_{rep, t+1}])\), the percentage risk premium \(-R_f \Cov(M, R_m) = -\Cov(M_{rep}, R_m)/\E[M_{rep}]\) remains completely unchanged.

Problem 5.6 · Convexity of Marginal Utility

Prove that for any CRRA utility function \(u(c) = c^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)\) with \(\gamma > 0\), marginal utility is strictly convex, and explain why this creates a precautionary demand effect in incomplete markets.

Solution

\(u'(c) = c^{-\gamma}\). Differentiating twice yields \(u''(c) = -\gamma c^{-\gamma - 1} < 0\) and \(u'''(c) = \gamma(\gamma + 1) c^{-\gamma - 2} > 0\) for all \(c > 0\) and \(\gamma > 0\). By Jensen's inequality, \(\E[u'(c)] > u'(\E[c])\). An increase in uninsurable idiosyncratic uncertainty shifts expected marginal utility upward, inducing agents to save more in risk-free assets to self-insure, driving equilibrium interest rates downward.

Empirical

Problem 5.7 · Administrative Earnings Panels (Guvenen et al. 2014)

Using millions of Social Security earnings records, what did Guvenen et al. (2014) discover regarding the cyclicality of the third and second moments of idiosyncratic earnings shocks?

Solution

Guvenen et al. (2014) showed that while overall earnings variance increases modestly in recessions, the crucial cyclical variation lies in higher-order moments: earnings changes exhibit strongly procyclical left-skewness. In expansions, workers have substantial upside wage potential and low layoff probability; in recessions, the left tail collapses as workers face large unrecoverable earnings losses (displacements), generating large downside tail risk.

Problem 5.8 · Stockholder vs Non-Stockholder Consumption

Mankiw & Zeldes (1991) divided the PSID sample into stockholders and non-stockholders. How does the consumption of stockholders differ from non-stockholders in its correlation with equity returns?

Solution

Mankiw & Zeldes found that only a fraction of US households held stocks. Stockholder consumption is substantially more volatile and significantly more correlated with stock returns (\(\text{Corr}(\Delta c_s, R_m) \approx 0.40\)) than aggregate or non-stockholder consumption (\(\text{Corr} \approx 0.10\)). Evaluating the CCAPM Euler equation solely on stockholder consumption resolves about one-third to one-half of the equity premium puzzle without changing preferences.

Problem 5.9 · Transitory vs Permanent Earnings Components

In empirical labor economics (Storesletten et al. 2004), how is the variance of annual labor earnings decomposed into permanent and transitory components? Which component matters for asset pricing?

Solution

Annual log earnings \(y_{it} = \alpha_i + u_{it} + \epsilon_{it}\) is decomposed into a fixed effect \(\alpha_i\), a persistent AR(1) component \(u_{it} = \rho u_{it-1} + \xi_{it}\) (\(\rho \approx 0.95-0.98\)), and a transitory shock \(\epsilon_{it}\). Only the persistent innovation \(\xi_{it}\) matters for asset pricing: households easily self-insure transitory shocks \(\epsilon_{it}\) through saving, whereas persistent shocks permanently alter lifetime human capital wealth.

Research

Problem 5.10 · The Limited Participation Puzzle

If uninsurable labor income risk drives the equity premium, why do the households with the highest exposure to labor risk participate least in the stock market? Does limited participation enhance or weaken the heterogeneous-agent model?

Solution

Limited participation presents an empirical tension: the households facing severe layoff risks hold zero stocks due to background risk and entry costs. Since asset prices are set by the marginal trades of active market participants (who are typically wealthy and face lower unemployment rates), the relevant cross-sectional variance is that of stockholders, which is less cyclical than that of the broader working population. This weakens the quantitative explanatory power of pure incomplete markets unless rich households face private business risk (Heaton & Lucas 2000).

Problem 5.11 · Incomplete Markets in Quantitative Bewley Models

Why do quantitative Bewley-Aiyagari models with realistic wealth distributions fail to generate large equity premia when calibrated with empirical AR(1) income shocks (Krusell & Smith 1998)?

Solution

In standard Bewley-Aiyagari models, households accumulate substantial buffer-stock physical capital and bonds to self-insure against idiosyncratic shocks. Because realistic income shocks have persistence \(\rho \approx 0.95 < 1.0\), self-insurance is highly effective. As a result, the cross-sectional distribution of consumption is far smoother than the distribution of income, preventing the pricing kernel from generating the covariance needed for a 6% equity premium.

Problem 5.12 · Entrepreneurial Risk and Private Business Wealth

How does concentrated entrepreneurial risk among business owners provide a more promising channel for heterogeneous-agent asset pricing than wage income risk (Moskowitz & Vissing-Jorgensen 2002)?

Solution

The wealthy households who hold the vast majority of public equity also hold substantial illiquid private business equity. Private equity returns are volatile, poorly diversified, and highly procyclical. Because entrepreneurial risk cannot be insured and is concentrated among the exact demographic that sets asset prices, cyclical swings in private business risk generate large shifts in marginal utility among active equity investors, bypassing the limited participation critique.

    Options

    If shocks are transitory, households easily buffer them by accumulating bonds. Unit root shocks permanently shift lifetime wealth, disabling self-insurance.

  2. What is the aggregate stochastic discount factor adjustment term in the Constantinides-Duffie framework?

    Options

    Integrating individual CRRA marginal utilities across lognormally distributed shares yields \(\exp(\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\gamma+1)\sigma_{t+1}^2)\).

    \(0.5 \times 4.0 \times 5.0 \times 0.15^2 = 10 \times 0.0225 = 0.225\). \(\exp(0.225) \approx 1.2523\).

  4. What happens to the equity premium if cross-sectional idiosyncratic risk is strictly constant over time (\(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \bar{\sigma}^2\))?

    Options

    A constant adjustment factor scales the pricing kernel uniformly, lowering the risk-free rate without increasing covariance with stock returns.

  5. In Lab 5 calibration (\(\gamma = 3.0\), \(\sigma_{\text{rec}} = 0.20\), \(\sigma_{\text{exp}} = 0.08\)), calculate the additional equity premium percentage contribution from countercyclical risk.

    \(0.5 \times 3 \times 4 \times (0.040 - 0.0064) \times 0.5 = 6 \times 0.0336 \times 0.5 = 0.1008\) (10.08%).

  6. What does the 'limited participation critique' argue regarding heterogeneous-agent asset pricing?

    Options

    Since asset prices are determined by active market participants who are wealthy and sheltered from labor shocks, broader labor risk may not transmit to asset prices.

  7. Why is marginal utility strictly convex (\(u'''(c) > 0\)) under CRRA preferences?

    Options

    Convexity of marginal utility is the mathematical foundation for precautionary saving: mean-preserving spreads in consumption increase expected marginal utility.

  8. What did Guvenen et al. (2014) find using administrative Social Security payroll data regarding recessions?

    Options

    Recession risk is characterized by procyclical left-skewness: devastating downward shocks (layoffs) surge while upward shocks dry up.
