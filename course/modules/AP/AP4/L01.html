The Benchmark Failure & Demands on a Resolution

  • Uses: consumption CAPM, equity premium puzzle, risk-free rate puzzle, Hansen-Jagannathan bound, Lucas tree model, CRRA utility, stochastic discount factor

The representative-agent consumption CAPM with power utility forms the neoclassical foundation of dynamic macro-finance. Yet, tested against historical data, it collapses along three dimensions: it fails to explain the magnitude of the equity premium, produces counterfactually volatile or astronomical risk-free rates, and generates constant valuation ratios that cannot reconcile market volatility. Understanding why the benchmark fails establishes the non-negotiable constraints any structural resolution must satisfy.

1The Canonical Power-Utility Benchmark

Consider the standard pure-exchange Lucas tree economy developed in AP3. A representative household maximizes lifetime expected utility under time-separable constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) preferences:

\[ \begin{aligned} U(\{C_t\}_{t=0}^\infty) &= \E_0 \left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty \beta^t \frac{C_t^{1-\gamma} - 1}{1 - \gamma} \right], \quad \beta \in (0, 1), \quad \gamma > 0 \end{aligned} \]

The equilibrium stochastic discount factor between dates \(t\) and \(t+1\) is given by the household marginal rate of substitution:

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1} &= \beta \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right)^{-\gamma} = \beta \exp(-\gamma \Delta c_{t+1}) \end{aligned} \]

where \(c_t \equiv \ln C_t\). For any gross asset return \(R_{i, t+1}\), the fundamental Euler equation requires \(\E_t[M_{t+1} R_{i, t+1}] = 1\).

Theorem 1.1 (Lognormal CCAPM Risk and Return Relations)

Assume aggregate consumption growth \(\Delta c_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_c, \sigma_c^2)\) and log gross return \(r_{i, t+1} \equiv \ln R_{i, t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)\) are jointly bivariate normal. Then the log risk-free rate \(r_{f, t+1} \equiv \ln R_{f, t+1}\) and the log excess return \(\E_t[r_{i, t+1} - r_{f, t+1}] + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2\) satisfy

\[ \begin{aligned} r_{f, t+1} &= -\ln \beta + \gamma \mu_c - \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_c^2 \\ \E_t[r_{i, t+1} - r_{f, t+1}] + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2 &= \gamma \Cov_t(\Delta c_{t+1}, r_{i, t+1}) \end{aligned} \]
Proof

From the Euler equation for the risk-free bond, \(\exp(-r_{f, t+1}) = \E_t[M_{t+1}] = \E_t[\beta \exp(-\gamma \Delta c_{t+1})]\). Applying the lognormal moment-generating function \(\E[\exp(X)] = \exp(\E[X] + \frac{1}{2}\Var(X))\) yields

\[ \begin{aligned} -r_{f, t+1} &= \ln \beta - \gamma \mu_c + \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_c^2 \implies r_{f, t+1} = -\ln \beta + \gamma \mu_c - \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_c^2 \end{aligned} \]

Similarly, for any asset return \(R_{i, t+1}\), the condition \(\E_t[\exp(m_{t+1} + r_{i, t+1})] = 1\) requires \(\E_t[m_{t+1} + r_{i, t+1}] + \frac{1}{2}\Var_t(m_{t+1} + r_{i, t+1}) = 0\). Expanding the variance term \(\Var_t(m + r_i) = \Var_t(m) + \Var_t(r_i) + 2\Cov_t(m, r_i)\) and substituting \(m_{t+1} = \ln \beta - \gamma \Delta c_{t+1}\) yields the exact premium relation.

2The Anatomy of Benchmark Failure

The neoclassical benchmark breaks down across three fundamental empirical dimensions:

Definition 1.1 (The Triad of Neoclassical Failures)
  1. The Equity Premium Puzzle (Mehra & Prescott 1985): In long-run US data (1889–1978), the real excess return on equity is approximately \(6.18\%\) per year, with \(\sigma_i \approx 16.54\%\), while per capita consumption growth exhibits \(\mu_c \approx 1.83\%\) and \(\sigma_c \approx 3.57\%\). Because \(\Cov(\Delta c, r_i) \le \sigma_c \sigma_i \approx 0.0357 \times 0.1654 \approx 0.0059\), matching a \(6\%\) premium requires: \[ \gamma \ge \frac{0.0618}{0.0059} \approx 10.5 \] When account is taken of the empirical correlation \(\text{Corr}(\Delta c, r_i) \approx 0.20\) to \(0.30\), the required risk aversion climbs to \(\gamma > 35\), violating microeconomic bounds (\(\gamma \le 10\)).
  2. The Risk-Free Rate Puzzle (Weil 1989): In CRRA utility, the elasticity of intertemporal substitution is linked to risk aversion: \(\psi = 1/\gamma\). Setting \(\gamma = 35\) implies \(\psi \approx 0.0286\). Since \(\mu_c \approx 0.0183\), the term \(\gamma \mu_c = 35 \times 0.0183 \approx 0.64\) (\(64\%\) per year). Unless the subjective discount factor \(\beta\) is implausibly greater than 1 (negative subjective time preference), the model predicts an astronomical real interest rate, contradicting the historical US average of \(0.80\%\).
  3. The Excess Volatility Puzzle (Shiller 1981): In the Lucas tree with i.i.d. consumption growth, the price-dividend ratio is constant. In actual data, the price-dividend ratio fluctuates widely (\(\sigma(\ln(P/D)) \approx 0.40\)), driving predictable countercyclical expected excess returns that the time-invariant power-utility model cannot generate.

3The Hansen-Jagannathan Volatility Diagnostic

The Hansen-Jagannathan bound provides a model-free benchmark. For any asset or portfolio with excess return \(R^e\), the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality dictates:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} &\ge \frac{|\E[R^e]|}{\sigma(R^e)} = \text{Sharpe Ratio} \end{aligned} \]

For historical US equities, the annualized Sharpe ratio is \(\approx 0.32\) to \(0.37\). For lognormal consumption growth, the CRRA discount factor has normalized volatility:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} &= \sqrt{\exp(\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2) - 1} \approx \gamma \sigma_c \end{aligned} \]

With annual consumption volatility \(\sigma_c \approx 1.5\%\) in post-war quarterly data, setting \(\gamma = 2\) yields \(\sigma(M)/\E[M] \approx 0.03\), undercutting the lower bound by an order of magnitude.

Lab 1 · Hansen-Jagannathan Bound Diagnostics

Testing the CRRA stochastic discount factor against the historical Hansen-Jagannathan lower bound will demonstrate that standard risk aversion values (gamma <= 5) fail to enter the feasible volatility region.

"""Lab AP4-L01-1: Hansen-Jagannathan bound diagnostics for standard CRRA CCAPM.

Evaluates whether time-separable CRRA power utility can satisfy the nonparametric
Hansen-Jagannathan volatility bound using historical US equity and Treasury return moments.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np

# Historical US annual moments (1889-1978, Mehra & Prescott 1985; Campbell 2003)
mean_rf = 0.0080
mean_re = 0.0618
vol_re = 0.1654
mean_gc = 0.0183
vol_gc = 0.0357

# Empirical Sharpe ratio: lower bound on sigma(M) / E[M]
sharpe = mean_re / vol_re
e_m = 1.0 / (1.0 + mean_rf)
hj_lower_bound = sharpe * e_m

print(f"Historical Consumption Growth: {mean_gc * 100:.2f}% (vol = {vol_gc * 100:.2f}%)")
print(f"Historical Equity Premium: {mean_re * 100:.2f}%")
print(f"Historical Excess Volatility: {vol_re * 100:.2f}%")
print(f"Historical Sharpe Ratio: {sharpe:.4f}")
print(f"Hansen-Jagannathan Minimum Volatility sigma(M) >= {hj_lower_bound:.4f}")
print("--- CRRA Model Comparison ---")

gammas = [1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0, 20.0, 30.0]
for g in gammas:
    # For lognormal consumption growth: M = beta * exp(-gamma * Delta c)
    # Var(ln M) = gamma^2 * vol_gc^2 => sigma(M)/E[M] approx gamma * vol_gc
    model_vol_ratio = np.sqrt(np.exp(g**2 * vol_gc**2) - 1.0)
    satisfied = model_vol_ratio >= sharpe
    print(f"gamma = {g:4.1f}: sigma(M)/E[M] = {model_vol_ratio:.4f} | HJ Bound Satisfied: {satisfied}")

The numerical simulation confirms that power utility requires gamma > 10 to clear the Hansen-Jagannathan lower bound of 0.3707. At plausible microeconomic levels of risk aversion (gamma = 1 to 5), the standard CCAPM explains less than half of the required pricing kernel variance.

4The Taxonomy of Structural Resolutions

To reconcile theory with data, modern macroeconomic asset pricing abandons one or more assumptions of the canonical benchmark. The literature partitions into four major structural mechanisms:

  • Dynamic Risk Preferences (Habit Formation): Campbell & Cochrane (1999) introduce a slow-moving external habit \(X_t\). The surplus consumption ratio \(S_t = (C_t - X_t)/C_t\) generates countercyclical risk aversion \(\gamma_t = \gamma/S_t\), creating large and volatile risk premia while keeping interest rates constant.
  • Non-Expected Utility & Cash Flow Dynamics (Long-Run Risks): Bansal & Yaron (2004) deploy Epstein-Zin recursive preferences to separate \(\gamma\) from \(\psi\), combining them with a small, persistent predictable component in consumption growth and stochastic volatility.
  • Non-Gaussian Endowments (Rare Disasters): Rietz (1988) and Barro (2006) demonstrate that low-probability catastrophic contractions generate substantial equity premia and high precautionary savings demand for bonds.
  • Market Incompleteness & Heterogeneity: Constantinides & Duffie (1996) show that uninsurable, persistent idiosyncratic labor income shocks with countercyclical cross-sectional variance can price equity returns without high aggregate risk.

Where the canon breaks

The classical CCAPM fails because it assumes that aggregate per-capita consumption growth is identical to the consumption growth of stock-market participants, that consumption risk is conditionally Gaussian and independent over time, and that preferences are time-separable. Decades of microeconomic survey data demonstrate that stock ownership is heavily concentrated, idiosyncratic labor risk is uninsurable, and financial market volatility is driven by discount-rate shocks rather than cash-flow innovations (Cochrane 2011).

Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “The equity premium puzzle exists simply because risk aversion is unobservable, so setting \(\gamma = 40\) resolves the issue.”

    Setting \(\gamma = 40\) in standard CRRA utility creates the Weil risk-free-rate puzzle: because \(\psi = 1/\gamma = 0.025\), the implied risk-free rate explodes to over \(50\%\) per annum unless one assumes an implausible subjective discount factor \(\beta > 1\).

  2. “If stocks are volatile because dividends are volatile, the standard Lucas tree model naturally explains stock return volatility.”

    In standard exchange economies, the price-dividend ratio is constant under i.i.d. growth. As Shiller (1981) proved, realized stock prices fluctuate dramatically more than the present value of future dividends discounted at a constant rate.

  3. “The Hansen-Jagannathan bound only applies to consumption-based models.”

    The Hansen-Jagannathan bound is completely model-free. It applies to every valid stochastic discount factor, whether constructed from macro variables, factor portfolios, or option pricing kernels.

Mechanics

Problem 1.1 · Risk-Free Rate Calculation under High Risk Aversion

Consider an economy with annual consumption growth mean \(\mu_c = 0.02\), volatility \(\sigma_c = 0.02\), and subjective discount factor \(\beta = 0.99\). Compute the log risk-free rate \(r_f\) under CRRA utility for \(\gamma = 2\) and \(\gamma = 30\).

Solution

The log risk-free rate equation is \(r_f = -\ln \beta + \gamma \mu_c - \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_c^2\). Here \(-\ln(0.99) \approx 0.01005\).

  • For \(\gamma = 2\): \(r_f = 0.01005 + 2(0.02) - 0.5(4)(0.0004) = 0.01005 + 0.04 - 0.0008 = 0.04925\) (\(4.93\%\)).
  • For \(\gamma = 30\): \(r_f = 0.01005 + 30(0.02) - 0.5(900)(0.0004) = 0.01005 + 0.60 - 0.18 = 0.43005\) (\(43.01\%\)).

Under \(\gamma = 30\), the real risk-free rate exceeds \(43\%\), illustrating the Weil puzzle.

Problem 1.2 · Hansen-Jagannathan Volatility Ratio

An equity index has an annual expected excess return of \(7.0\%\) and an annual return volatility of \(18.0\%\). The gross risk-free rate is \(R_f = 1.02\). Compute the minimum volatility \(\sigma(M)\) of any valid stochastic discount factor.

Solution

The Sharpe ratio is \(\text{SR} = 0.07 / 0.18 \approx 0.3889\). The expected discount factor is \(\E[M] = 1/R_f = 1/1.02 \approx 0.9804\). The Hansen-Jagannathan lower bound requires \(\sigma(M) \ge \text{SR} \times \E[M] = 0.3889 \times 0.9804 \approx 0.3813\).

Problem 1.3 · Required Correlation for Equity Premium

Suppose \(\gamma = 5\), \(\sigma_c = 0.02\), and equity volatility is \(\sigma_i = 0.20\). What correlation \(\text{Corr}(\Delta c, r_i)\) between consumption growth and equity returns would be required to generate an equity premium of \(6.0\%\)?

Solution

From \(\E[r_i - r_f] + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2 = \gamma \text{Corr}(\Delta c, r_i) \sigma_c \sigma_i\):

\[ 0.06 + 0.5(0.04) = 0.08 = 5 \times \text{Corr} \times 0.02 \times 0.20 = 0.02 \times \text{Corr} \implies \text{Corr} = \frac{0.08}{0.02} = 4.0 \]

Because correlation cannot exceed 1.0, a risk aversion of \(\gamma = 5\) cannot explain the equity premium under these parameters.

Problem 1.4 · Derivation of the Hansen-Jagannathan Bound

Prove that for any zero-cost portfolio with excess return \(R^e\), the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies \(\frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} \ge \frac{|\E[R^e]|}{\sigma(R^e)}\).

Solution

From the Euler equation \(\E[M R^e] = 0\), we decompose expectation into covariance: \(\Cov(M, R^e) + \E[M]\E[R^e] = 0 \implies \E[M]\E[R^e] = -\Cov(M, R^e)\). Taking absolute values and applying Cauchy-Schwarz: \(|\E[M]\E[R^e]| = |\Cov(M, R^e)| \le \sigma(M)\sigma(R^e)\). Dividing both sides by \(\E[M]\sigma(R^e)\) yields \(\frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} \ge \frac{|\E[R^e]|}{\sigma(R^e)}\).

Problem 1.5 · Invariance of Price-Dividend Ratio under Log Utility

Prove that in a discrete-time Lucas tree economy with log utility (\(u(c) = \ln c\)) and arbitrary Markovian consumption growth, the equilibrium price-dividend ratio \(P_t / D_t\) is constant and equals \(\frac{\beta}{1 - \beta}\).

Solution

The Euler equation for the tree (where \(C_t = D_t\)) is \(P_t u'(C_t) = \beta \E_t[u'(C_{t+1})(P_{t+1} + D_{t+1})]\). For \(u(c) = \ln c\), \(u'(c) = 1/c\), so \(P_t / C_t = \beta \E_t[\frac{1}{C_{t+1}}(P_{t+1} + C_{t+1})] = \beta \E_t[\frac{P_{t+1}}{C_{t+1}} + 1]\). Let \(v_t \equiv P_t / C_t\). The functional equation becomes \(v_t = \beta (\E_t[v_{t+1}] + 1)\). By backward induction and the transversality condition \(\lim_{T \to \infty} \beta^T \E_t[v_{t+T}] = 0\), the unique bounded solution is the constant \(v = \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta}\).

Problem 1.6 · Precautionary Savings Effect on the Risk-Free Rate

Prove that the term \(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2\) in the log risk-free rate formula represents precautionary savings demand, and show that its derivative with respect to \(\sigma_c^2\) is negative.

Solution

By Jensen's inequality, since marginal utility \(u'(c) = c^{-\gamma}\) is strictly convex (\(u'''(c) = \gamma(\gamma+1)c^{-\gamma-2} > 0\)), an increase in consumption risk \(\sigma_c^2\) increases expected future marginal utility \(\E_t[u'(c_{t+1})]\). This increases the household's willingness to save, raising bond prices and lowering the equilibrium risk-free rate. Mathematically, \(\frac{\partial r_f}{\partial \sigma_c^2} = -\frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 < 0\).

Empirical

Problem 1.7 · Evaluating Historical US Equity Premium

Using Mehra & Prescott's (1985) sample moments (\(\E[R_m] = 1.0698\), \(R_f = 1.0080\), \(\E[\Delta c] = 0.0183\), \(\sigma_c = 0.0357\), \(\text{Corr}(\Delta c, r_m) = 0.40\), \(\sigma(r_m) = 0.1654\)), estimate the implied coefficient of relative risk aversion \(\gamma\).

Solution

The sample covariance is \(\Cov(\Delta c, r_m) = 0.40 \times 0.0357 \times 0.1654 \approx 0.00236\). The excess return is \(\E[R_m - R_f] \approx 0.0618\). Under the linearized Euler equation, \(\gamma = \frac{\E[R_m - R_f]}{\Cov(\Delta c, r_m)} = \frac{0.0618}{0.00236} \approx 26.2\). This confirms that \(\gamma\) must exceed 25 to match the sample.

Problem 1.8 · Cross-Country Equity Premia

Campbell (2003) finds that across 11 developed equity markets, annualized Sharpe ratios range from 0.20 to 0.45, while consumption growth volatilities are consistently below 3.0%. What does this cross-country evidence imply for the universality of the equity premium puzzle?

Solution

Because consumption is smooth across all OECD economies while equity Sharpe ratios are universally high, the equity premium puzzle is not a US-specific data anomaly or post-war survivorship fluke. In every international market, the Hansen-Jagannathan bound requires \(\sigma(M)/\E[M] \ge 0.25\), forcing standard CRRA models to demand \(\gamma > 15\) across all countries.

Problem 1.9 · Predictability of Excess Returns by P/D Ratio

In historical US data, univariate regressions of multi-year excess returns on the dividend-price ratio yield positive coefficients with \(R^2\) exceeding 25% at 5-year horizons. Explain why this empirical fact refutes the standard CRRA Lucas tree model.

Solution

In the standard Lucas tree model with i.i.d. growth, the price-dividend ratio is constant, so its predictive regression slope is undefined or zero. In persistent Markov extensions with CRRA utility, price-dividend variation reflects expected dividend growth rather than expected return variation. The empirical finding that dividend-price ratios forecast future returns rather than future dividend growth directly contradicts standard CRRA pricing.

Research

Problem 1.10 · The Role of Stock Market Participation

Mankiw & Zeldes (1991) show that only a minority of US households held stocks directly during the 20th century, and that stockholders' consumption growth was significantly more volatile than non-stockholders'. Evaluate whether limited market participation fully resolves the equity premium puzzle.

Solution

While stockholders' consumption volatility is higher (\(\sigma_{c, \text{stockholder}} \approx 3.5\%\) to \(4.5\%\) compared to \(1.5\%\) for aggregate consumption), and their correlation with stock returns is stronger, calibrating the Euler equation to stockholder consumption reduces the required risk aversion from \(\gamma \approx 35\) to \(\gamma \approx 15\). While this mitigates the puzzle, \(\gamma \approx 15\) still exceeds traditional microeconomic bounds and fails to explain excess volatility and return predictability.

Problem 1.11 · Discount-Rate vs. Cash-Flow Volatility

Campbell & Shiller (1988) show via the log-linear present-value decomposition that \(\Var(p_t - d_t) = \Cov(p_t - d_t, \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \Delta d_{t+j}) - \Cov(p_t - d_t, \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} r_{t+j})\). Explain why empirical variance decompositions conclude that discount-rate variation dominates cash-flow variation.

Solution

Empirical regressions show that the dividend-price ratio has virtually zero covariance with future dividend growth (\(\Cov(p-d, \Delta d) \approx 0\)), but strongly negative covariance with future returns (\(-\Cov(p-d, r) > 0\)). Consequently, discount-rate variation accounts for nearly 100% of the variance of the price-dividend ratio. A successful asset pricing model must therefore generate volatile, countercyclical discount rates rather than relying on cash-flow shocks.

Problem 1.12 · Criteria for a Robust Resolution

Synthesize the non-negotiable quantitative criteria that any candidate structural asset pricing model must simultaneously satisfy to be considered a viable resolution to neoclassical asset pricing puzzles.

Solution

A viable structural resolution must simultaneously: (1) generate an annualized equity premium of \(5\%\) to \(7\%\); (2) maintain an annualized real risk-free rate of \(1\%\) to \(2\%\) with low volatility (\(\sigma(r_f) \le 3\%\)); (3) produce substantial stock return volatility (\(15\%\) to \(20\%\)) through endogenous price-dividend variation; (4) match the empirical persistence and predictability of price-dividend ratios; and (5) accomplish this using plausible microeconomic preference parameters without counterfactual consumption growth predictability.

Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Mehra-Prescott (1985) equity premium puzzle, what is the primary economic reason that power utility fails?

    Options

    Because aggregate consumption volatility is only ~1.5% to 3.5% and correlation with equity returns is moderate, covariance is minuscule, demanding implausibly large risk aversion.

  2. What preference restriction links risk aversion and intertemporal substitution under standard CRRA utility?

    Options

    In power utility, the single curvature parameter \(\gamma\) simultaneously governs static risk aversion and intertemporal substitution (\(\psi = 1/\gamma\)).

  3. For an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.3736 and a risk-free rate of 0.80% (\(\E[M] = 1/1.008 \approx 0.9921\)), compute the Hansen-Jagannathan minimum SDF volatility \(\sigma(M)\) to four decimal places.

    \(\sigma(M) \ge 0.3736 \times (1 / 1.008) = 0.3736 \times 0.99206 \approx 0.3707\).

  4. What does the Weil (1989) risk-free rate puzzle demonstrate?

    Options

    When \(\psi = 1/\gamma\) is tiny, consumers demand very high reward to defer consumption during positive growth, driving equilibrium interest rates skyward.

  5. Why is the price-dividend ratio constant in the benchmark Lucas tree model under log utility?

    Options

    With log utility (\(\gamma = 1, \psi = 1\)), income and substitution effects offset perfectly, making valuation independent of state variables.

  6. According to Cochrane's (2011) discount-rate decomposition, what accounts for nearly 100% of price-dividend ratio variance?

    Options

    Empirically, dividend-price ratios forecast future returns rather than dividend growth, proving that discount-rate variation dominates.

  7. Which model resolves the puzzles by making relative risk aversion countercyclical through a slow-moving habit?

    Options

    Campbell and Cochrane introduce the surplus consumption ratio, generating countercyclical risk aversion while maintaining a constant risk-free rate.

  8. Which model resolves the puzzles by separating risk aversion from EIS and introducing a small, persistent predictable growth component?

    Options

    Bansal and Yaron combine Epstein-Zin preferences (\(\psi > 1\)) with long-run persistent growth shocks and stochastic volatility.
