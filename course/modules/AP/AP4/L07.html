Synthesis & Debate: Is the Equity Premium a Risk Premium?

  • Uses: consumption CAPM, external habit model, long-run risk model, rare-disasters model, heterogeneous-agent asset pricing, moment matching model evaluation

  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled

Four decades of research have produced three canonical macroeconomic paradigms to resolve the equity premium puzzle: habit formation, long-run risks, and rare disasters. While each paradigm mathematically generates high equity premia and low risk-free rates, they disagree fundamentally on the underlying economic source of risk. Evaluating these frameworks against one another reveals severe structural trade-offs and culminates in the ultimate question of modern finance: is the equity premium truly compensation for macroeconomic risk, or an artifact of behavioral biases and institutional frictions?

1The Canonical Horse Race: Comparing the Three Paradigms

Modern asset pricing features three distinct rational macroeconomic explanations for why equity returns exceed Treasury bill yields by 6% annually:

Definition 7.1 (Macro-Finance Pricing Kernel Taxonomy)

Every dynamic macroeconomic asset pricing resolution to the equity premium puzzle specifies a distinct state space \(\{S_t\}\) and stochastic discount factor representation \(M_{t+1}(S_t, S_{t+1})\):

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1}^{CC} &= \delta \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right)^{-\gamma} \left( \frac{S_{t+1}}{S_t} \right)^{-\gamma} \\ M_{t+1}^{BY} &= \beta^\theta \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right)^{-\frac{\theta}{\psi}} \left( R_{a, t+1} \right)^{\theta - 1} \\ M_{t+1}^{BR} &= \delta \left( \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t} \right)^{-\gamma} (1 - b_{t+1})^{-\gamma J_{t+1}} \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 7.1 (SDF Volatility Bound and Sharpe Ratio Frontier)

Under any arbitrage-free asset pricing model, the maximum Sharpe ratio achievable across any portfolio of traded assets is strictly bounded by the coefficient of variation of the stochastic discount factor:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{R^e} \frac{\E[R^e]}{\sigma(R^e)} &\le \frac{\sigma(M_{t+1})}{\E[M_{t+1}]} \end{aligned} \]

Consequently, any model attempting to resolve the equity premium puzzle must generate an annual SDF volatility \(\sigma(M) / \E[M] \ge 0.32\).

Remark 7.1 (The Trade-Off Between Return Predictability and Bond Stability)

Because the real risk-free rate is \(R_{f, t} = 1 / \E_t[M_{t+1}]\), any mechanism that induces large variation in \(\E_t[M_{t+1}]\) to generate return predictability risks generating counterfactually volatile real interest rates unless an offset (such as \(\lambda(s)\) in Campbell-Cochrane or \(\psi > 1\) in Bansal-Yaron) is introduced.

2Cross-Model Scorecard Analysis

When evaluated across the 12 canonical Beeler-Campbell moments under identical historical sample lengths, no single model achieves uniform dominance:

  • Campbell & Cochrane (1999) excels at matching equity return volatility (13.8% vs 19.4% target) and generates strong negative return predictability (\(\beta_{dp} = -0.24\) vs \(-0.15\) target). However, it sets risk-free rate volatility to zero by construction (\(\sigma(R_f) = 0.00\%\)), understating empirical bond yield volatility (5.40%).
  • Bansal & Yaron (2004) achieves the closest match to the historical annual Sharpe ratio (0.301 vs 0.320 target) and matches mean log price-dividend ratios. However, without extreme parameter tuning, its annual return predictability slope is near zero (\(\beta_{dp} \approx 0.00\)), and it faces the Beeler & Campbell (2012) critique on consumption predictability.
  • Barro (2006) explains how calm-sample excess returns of 3% to 6% emerge with low risk aversion (\(\gamma = 4\)), but because disaster probability is held constant, its log price-dividend ratio has zero volatility (\(\sigma(p-d) = 0.00\)) and zero return predictability (\(\beta_{dp} = 0.00\)).

Lab 1 · Multi-Model Horse Race

Simulating Campbell-Cochrane, Bansal-Yaron, and Barro-Rietz under identical 500-year calibration seeds will demonstrate that Campbell-Cochrane produces the highest return predictability, Bansal-Yaron matches the Sharpe ratio most closely, and Barro-Rietz understates valuation volatility due to constant disaster intensity.

"""Lab AP4-L07-1: Multi-model canonical horse-race synthesis and comparison.

Simulates Campbell-Cochrane, Bansal-Yaron, and Barro-Rietz on a standardized seed
and compares their performance on the equity premium, risk-free rate, and valuation volatility.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def compute_twelve_moments(data: dict[str, np.ndarray]) -> dict[str, float]:
    dc = np.asarray(data["consumption_growth"], dtype=np.float64)
    re = np.asarray(data["excess_return"], dtype=np.float64)
    rf = np.asarray(data["risk_free_rate"], dtype=np.float64)
    pd_log = np.asarray(data["log_pd"], dtype=np.float64)

    def autocorr_lag1(series: np.ndarray) -> float:
        if len(series) < 2:
            return 0.0
        centered = series - np.mean(series)
        var = np.mean(centered**2)
        if var <= 1e-12:
            return 0.0
        return float(np.mean(centered[1:] * centered[:-1]) / var)

    if len(re) > 1 and len(pd_log) > 1:
        x_pred = pd_log[:-1] - np.mean(pd_log[:-1])
        y_pred = re[1:]
        var_x = np.mean(x_pred**2)
        pred_slope = float(np.mean(x_pred * y_pred) / var_x) if var_x > 1e-12 else 0.0
    else:
        pred_slope = 0.0

    mean_re = float(np.mean(re))
    vol_re = float(np.std(re, ddof=1)) if len(re) > 1 else 0.0
    sharpe = float(mean_re / vol_re) if vol_re > 1e-8 else 0.0

    return {
        "mean_consumption_growth": float(np.mean(dc)),
        "vol_consumption_growth": float(np.std(dc, ddof=1)) if len(dc) > 1 else 0.0,
        "ac1_consumption_growth": autocorr_lag1(dc),
        "mean_equity_premium": mean_re,
        "vol_equity_return": vol_re,
        "sharpe_ratio": sharpe,
        "mean_risk_free_rate": float(np.mean(rf)),
        "vol_risk_free_rate": float(np.std(rf, ddof=1)) if len(rf) > 1 else 0.0,
        "mean_log_pd": float(np.mean(pd_log)),
        "vol_log_pd": float(np.std(pd_log, ddof=1)) if len(pd_log) > 1 else 0.0,
        "ac1_log_pd": autocorr_lag1(pd_log),
        "predictability_slope_1y": pred_slope,
    }


def simulate_cc(t_len: int, seed: int) -> dict[str, np.ndarray]:
    g, sigma, phi, gamma, delta = 0.0189, 0.0150, 0.87, 2.0, 0.89
    b_s, pd_mean = 0.48, 3.38
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    s_bar_val = sigma * np.sqrt(gamma / (1.0 - phi))
    s_bar = float(np.log(s_bar_val))
    s_max = float(s_bar + 0.5 * (1.0 - s_bar_val**2))

    s = np.zeros(t_len, dtype=np.float64)
    dc = np.zeros(t_len, dtype=np.float64)
    s[0] = s_bar
    v = rng.normal(0.0, sigma, t_len)

    for t in range(t_len - 1):
        lam = (1.0 / s_bar_val) * np.sqrt(max(0.0, 1.0 - 2.0 * (s[t] - s_bar))) - 1.0 if s[t] <= s_max else 0.0
        s[t + 1] = (1.0 - phi) * s_bar + phi * s[t] + lam * v[t + 1]

    dc = g + v
    rf_const = -np.log(delta) + gamma * g - 0.5 * gamma * (1.0 - phi)
    rf = np.full(t_len, rf_const, dtype=np.float64)
    pd_log = pd_mean + b_s * (s - s_bar)

    re = np.zeros(t_len, dtype=np.float64)
    for t in range(t_len - 1):
        p_ratio = np.exp(pd_log[t + 1] - pd_log[t])
        re[t] = p_ratio * np.exp(dc[t + 1]) - np.exp(rf[t])
    re[-1] = re[-2]

    return {"consumption_growth": dc, "excess_return": re, "risk_free_rate": rf, "log_pd": pd_log}


def simulate_by(t_len: int, seed: int) -> dict[str, np.ndarray]:
    mu, rho, phi_e, sigma_bar, nu, sigma_w = 0.0180, 0.979, 0.044, 0.015, 0.987, 0.0000023
    mu_d, phi_d, phi_u, delta, gamma, psi, kappa1 = 0.0180, 3.0, 4.5, 0.99, 10.0, 1.5, 0.96
    a2_m, pd_mean = -15.0, 3.38
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    x = np.zeros(t_len, dtype=np.float64)
    sigma2 = np.full(t_len, sigma_bar**2, dtype=np.float64)
    eta = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, t_len)
    e = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, t_len)
    w = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, t_len)
    u = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, t_len)

    for t in range(t_len - 1):
        st = float(np.sqrt(max(1e-8, sigma2[t])))
        x[t + 1] = rho * x[t] + phi_e * st * e[t + 1]
        sigma2[t + 1] = max(1e-8, sigma_bar**2 + nu * (sigma2[t] - sigma_bar**2) + sigma_w * w[t + 1])

    st_arr = np.sqrt(np.maximum(1e-8, sigma2))
    dc = mu + x + st_arr * eta
    dd = mu_d + phi_d * x + phi_u * st_arr * u

    a1_m = (phi_d - 1.0 / psi) / (1.0 - kappa1 * rho)
    pd_log = pd_mean + a1_m * x + a2_m * (sigma2 - sigma_bar**2)

    rf0 = -np.log(delta) + mu / psi
    rf = rf0 + (1.0 / psi) * x - 0.5 * (gamma - 1.0 / psi) * (sigma2 - sigma_bar**2)

    re = np.zeros(t_len, dtype=np.float64)
    for t in range(t_len - 1):
        p_ratio = np.exp(pd_log[t + 1] - pd_log[t])
        ret = p_ratio * np.exp(dd[t + 1]) - np.exp(rf[t])
        re[t] = ret
    re[-1] = re[-2]

    return {"consumption_growth": dc, "excess_return": re, "risk_free_rate": rf, "log_pd": pd_log}


def simulate_br(t_len: int, seed: int) -> dict[str, np.ndarray]:
    g, sigma, sigma_d, p_disaster, b_mean, b_div_mult, delta, gamma = (
        0.025,
        0.020,
        0.050,
        0.017,
        0.29,
        1.5,
        0.97,
        4.0,
    )
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    eps_c = rng.normal(0.0, sigma, t_len)
    eps_d = rng.normal(0.0, sigma_d, t_len)
    jump = rng.binomial(1, p_disaster, t_len)

    b_div = min(0.99, b_mean * b_div_mult)
    dc = g + eps_c + np.log(1.0 - b_mean) * jump
    dd = g + eps_d + np.log(1.0 - b_div) * jump

    base_disc = delta * np.exp(-gamma * g + 0.5 * (gamma**2) * (sigma**2))
    prec_term = (1.0 - p_disaster) + p_disaster * ((1.0 - b_mean) ** (-gamma))
    em = base_disc * prec_term
    rf_val = float(-np.log(em))
    rf = np.full(t_len, rf_val, dtype=np.float64)

    base_eq = delta * np.exp((1.0 - gamma) * g + 0.5 * ((1.0 - gamma) ** 2) * (sigma**2))
    eq_jump = (1.0 - p_disaster) + p_disaster * ((1.0 - b_mean) ** (-gamma)) * (1.0 - b_div)
    alpha_d = float(base_eq * eq_jump)
    alpha_d = max(0.01, min(0.99, alpha_d))
    pd_const = float(alpha_d / (1.0 - alpha_d))
    pd_log = np.full(t_len, float(np.log(pd_const)), dtype=np.float64)

    re = np.zeros(t_len, dtype=np.float64)
    for t in range(t_len - 1):
        div_growth = np.exp(dd[t + 1])
        ret = ((pd_const + 1.0) / pd_const) * div_growth - np.exp(rf[t])
        re[t] = ret
    re[-1] = re[-2]

    return {"consumption_growth": dc, "excess_return": re, "risk_free_rate": rf, "log_pd": pd_log}

Debate Brief: Is the Equity Premium a Risk Premium?

1. Resolution

Resolved: The historical excess return of equities over Treasury bills represents rational equilibrium compensation for non-diversifiable macroeconomic risk, rather than persistent behavioral mispricing or institutional market frictions.

2. Strongest Case for the Rational Risk Paradigm

The rational risk paradigm, defended by Cochrane (2011), Campbell & Cochrane (1999), and Bansal & Yaron (2004), rests on robust theoretical and empirical pillars:

  • Endogenous Countercyclical Risk Premia: Equity Sharpe ratios and expected returns surge during severe economic downturns (e.g. 1930s Great Depression, 2008 Great Recession). Rational models demonstrate that when households face habit depletion or high economic uncertainty, their marginal utility of wealth spikes, demanding high compensation to hold procyclical assets.
  • Cross-Market Pricing Consistency: The same stochastic discount factor that explains equity returns successfully prices corporate bond credit spreads, Treasury yield curves, and foreign exchange risk premia. An arbitrary behavioral mispricing would not manifest with consistent pricing kernels across multi-trillion-dollar institutional debt and currency markets.
  • Survival Across Centuries and Geographies: The equity premium is not an isolated artifact of post-war US data. Barro (2006) documented substantial equity premia across 35 countries over a century of data. Behavioral cognitive errors would be learned away or arbitraged away by sophisticated institutional capital over 100+ years.

3. Strongest Case for the Behavioral / Frictions / Non-Risk Paradigm

Proponents of behavioral finance and market frictions contend that macro-risk models are mathematical epicycles designed to defend an empirically bankrupt dogma:

  • Failure of Macro Covariance: The empirical correlation between quarterly consumption growth and stock returns is virtually zero (\(\approx 0.10\)). For macro risk to explain a 6% equity premium, households would have to possess implausibly extreme risk aversion (\(\gamma > 50\) in CRRA, or \(\gamma > 100\) in recessions under habits).
  • Myopic Loss Aversion & Mental Accounting: Benartzi & Thaler (1995) and Barberis, Huang, & Santos (2001) demonstrate that if investors evaluate their portfolios annually with loss aversion (Kahneman-Tversky prospect theory), an equity premium of 6% arises naturally with moderate risk aversion, without relying on aggregate consumption.
  • Institutional Mandates & Intermediary Frictions: Asset prices are set by delegated institutional intermediaries (hedge funds, banks, pension funds) subject to regulatory capital constraints, value-at-risk limits, and short-term performance evaluations (He & Krishnamurthy 2013). Fire sales and leverage constraints generate risk premia that reflect balance-sheet capacity rather than household marginal utility.

4. State of the Literature & Empirical Discriminators

The academic consensus has shifted from testing whether the representative-agent consumption model is strictly true (it is universally rejected) to identifying which combinations of structural mechanisms dominate across different market regimes:

  • Survey Expectations vs Rational Expectations: Greenwood & Shleifer (2014) showed that investor subjective return expectations are procyclical (investors expect high returns at market peaks), whereas rational risk models require expectations to be countercyclical. Reconciling subjective survey data with objective return predictability is the central battleground of contemporary asset pricing.
  • Cross-Asset Tail Hedging: Option implied volatility surfaces and deep out-of-the-money put spreads provide direct forward-looking measures of disaster fear, allowing researchers to disentangle rational tail risk from investor sentiment.

Where the canon breaks

The debate between rational risk and behavioral mispricing highlights the fundamental boundaries of quantitative asset pricing:

  • The Joint-Hypothesis Problem: As Eugene Fama (1970) demonstrated, any test of market efficiency is simultaneously a test of the specific asset pricing model employed. A rejection of the model cannot unambiguously distinguish between investor irrationality and model misspecification.
  • Epistemological Fragility of Calibrated Models: With modern structural models featuring 10+ degrees of freedom, any asset pricing puzzle can be matched by introducing an unobservable state variable (habits, latent growth, disaster hazards, or intermediary wealth shares). Without external microeconomic identification, such models risk becoming sophisticated curve-fitting exercises.
  • Unstable Structural Parameters: Calibrated parameters (such as the EIS \(\psi\), surplus ratio persistence \(\phi\), or disaster probability \(p\)) shift across historical eras, failing out-of-sample stability tests during unprecedented macroeconomic regime shifts.

Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Because consumption-based asset pricing models fail to fit the data without extreme assumptions, the equity premium must be purely behavioral.”

    Rejecting simple representative-agent models does not prove behavioral mispricing. Rational incomplete-market models, intermediary asset pricing, and rare-disaster frameworks offer fully rational micro-foundations that bypass the representative-agent consumption critique.

  2. “All three canonical models generate return predictability through the exact same mechanism.”

    Their predictability channels are fundamentally distinct: Campbell-Cochrane generates predictability via slow-moving surplus consumption ratios (time-varying risk aversion); Bansal-Yaron generates predictability via time-varying macroeconomic volatility; Wachter generates predictability via time-varying disaster probability.

  3. “In a multi-model horse race, the model with the lowest average error on return moments is scientifically superior.”

    A model that matches return moments by severely distorting macroeconomic endowments (e.g. generating excessive consumption growth predictability or explosive dividend leverage) is structurally misspecified, regardless of its return-fit score.

Mechanics

Problem 7.1 · Scorecard Weighted Distance Computation

Suppose a candidate model achieves relative errors of \(e_1 = 0.05\) on equity premium and \(e_2 = 0.30\) on risk-free rate volatility. If the weights are \(w_1 = 3.0\) and \(w_2 = 1.0\), compute the weighted score \(J = w_1 e_1^2 + w_2 e_2^2\).

Solution

The weighted quadratic error score is calculated as \(J = 3.0 \times (0.05)^2 + 1.0 \times (0.30)^2 = 3.0 \times 0.0025 + 0.0900 = 0.0075 + 0.0900 = 0.0975\). This penalty metric heavily penalizes the large 30% relative error on risk-free rate volatility.

Problem 7.2 · Sharpe Ratio Comparison

Model A produces an annual equity premium of \(5.0\%\) with return volatility \(16.0\%\). Model B produces an equity premium of \(6.0\%\) with return volatility \(22.0\%\). Which model produces a Sharpe ratio closer to the historical US target of \(0.320\)?

Solution

For Model A, the annualized Sharpe ratio is \(\text{SR}_A = 0.050 / 0.160 = 0.3125\). For Model B, \(\text{SR}_B = 0.060 / 0.220 \approx 0.2727\). Model A has an absolute error of \(|0.3125 - 0.3200| = 0.0075\), whereas Model B has an error of \(|0.2727 - 0.3200| = 0.0473\). Model A matches the empirical target much more closely.

Problem 7.3 · Mean Absolute Percentage Error Calculation

Given three moments with relative errors of \(0.04\), \(0.12\), and \(0.20\), calculate the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) to two decimal places.

Solution

The Mean Absolute Percentage Error is the unweighted arithmetic mean of the relative errors: \(\text{MAPE} = \frac{0.04 + 0.12 + 0.20}{3} = \frac{0.36}{3} = 0.1200\), or 12.00% across the three evaluated moments.

Problem 7.4 · Fama's Joint-Hypothesis Equivalence

Prove that any empirical statistical rejection of the orthogonality condition \(\E_t[M_{t+1}(\theta) R_{t+1}^e] = 0\) cannot distinguish between informational inefficiency and model misspecification of the pricing kernel \(M\).

Solution

Let \(\mathcal{F}_t\) denote the public information filtration. Market efficiency requires that \(\E[M_{t+1}^* R_{t+1}^e | \mathcal{F}_t] = 0\) under the true unobserved economic pricing kernel \(M^*\). When an econometrician tests a candidate model \(M(\theta)\), the observed test statistic evaluates \(\hat{g}_T = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T M_{t+1}(\theta) R_{t+1}^e z_t\). If \(\hat{g}_T \ne 0\), the decomposition \(M(\theta) R^e = M^* R^e + [M(\theta) - M^*] R^e\) reveals that rejection occurs if either \(\E[M^* R^e z_t] \ne 0\) (market inefficiency / mispricing) or \(M(\theta) \ne M^*\) (structural misspecification of the model). Because the true \(M^*\) is latent, one can never reject market efficiency independently of the assumed model.

Problem 7.5 · Invariance of Sharpe Ratio to Linear Transformations

Prove that the maximum achievable Sharpe ratio in an economy is strictly bounded by the volatility of the stochastic discount factor, \(\frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]}\), and explain why this imposes a universal lower bound on pricing kernel volatility across all three canonical models.

Solution

From the Euler equation for any excess return \(R^e\), \(\E[M R^e] = 0 \implies \E[M] \E[R^e] + \Cov(M, R^e) = 0\). Rearranging gives \(\frac{\E[R^e]}{\sigma(R^e)} = -\frac{\Cov(M, R^e)}{\E[M] \sigma(R^e)} = -\rho_{M, R^e} \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]}\). Because the correlation coefficient satisfies \(|\rho_{M, R^e}| \le 1\), the maximum Sharpe ratio is \(\text{SR}_{\max} \le \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]}\). Since the historical US equity Sharpe ratio is \(\approx 0.32\) to \(0.40\), every candidate model (CC, BY, or Barro-Rietz) mathematically must generate an SDF volatility of at least 32% to 40% annually to be admissible.

Problem 7.6 · Campbell-Cochrane vs Bansal-Yaron Correlation Structure

Show that while Campbell-Cochrane generates a near-perfect correlation between price-dividend ratios and surplus consumption, Bansal-Yaron generates a dual dependency on expected growth and volatility states.

Solution

In Campbell-Cochrane, the only state variable is log surplus consumption \(s_t\), so the log price-dividend ratio is a deterministic monotonic function \(z(s_t)\), implying \(\text{Corr}(p_t - d_t, s_t) \approx 1.0\). In Bansal-Yaron, the price-dividend ratio is a linear combination of two independent state variables: \(z_{m, t} = A_{0, m} + A_{1, m} x_t + A_{2, m} \sigma_t^2\). Its variance is \(\Var(z_{m, t}) = A_{1, m}^2 \Var(x_t) + A_{2, m}^2 \Var(\sigma_t^2)\). This two-factor structure allows valuations to fluctuate even when expected growth \(x_t\) is zero if economic uncertainty \(\sigma_t^2\) shifts.

Empirical

Problem 7.7 · Greenwood-Shleifer Procyclical Expectations

Greenwood & Shleifer (2014) analyzed investor survey expectations of stock market returns (e.g. Gallup, Graham-Harvey CFO survey). How do survey expectations correlate with historical price-to-earnings ratios, and how does this challenge rational risk models?

Solution

Greenwood & Shleifer found that investor return expectations are strongly positively correlated with current valuation ratios (\(\text{Corr}(\E_t^{survey}[R_{t+1}], P_t/E_t) > 0.50\)). At market peaks (high P/E), investors expect the highest future returns. Conversely, rational risk models (Campbell-Cochrane, Bansal-Yaron) require that expected returns are countercyclical: investors should demand the highest returns at market troughs when prices are low and risk aversion or uncertainty is high. This procyclical expectations puzzle poses a fundamental challenge to the rational expectations foundation of macro-finance.

Problem 7.8 · Cross-Asset Replication of Risk Premia

How does the empirical performance of factor premia across foreign exchange, commodities, and government bonds help discriminate between macro-risk and behavioral theories?

Solution

Asness, Moskowitz, and Pedersen (2013) demonstrated that value and momentum strategies produce positive excess returns across global equities, government bonds, currencies, and commodity futures. Because institutional structures, transaction costs, and participant demographics differ vastly across these markets, proponents of rational risk argue that a common macroeconomic state variable must drive these premia, as pure behavioral retail errors would not survive in wholesale FX or sovereign debt markets.

Problem 7.9 · Realized Volatility vs Implied Volatility Smirks

What does the steepness of the S&P 500 index option implied volatility smirk imply about the relative importance of Gaussian diffusion vs jump/disaster risk in equity pricing?

Solution

Under pure Black-Scholes Gaussian diffusion, implied volatility is flat across strike prices. Empirically, deep out-of-the-money put options trade at implied volatilities 10 to 15 percentage points higher than at-the-money options. This persistent volatility smirk confirms that market participants assign substantial positive risk prices to catastrophic downward jumps, supporting the rare-disaster framework over pure Gaussian habit models.

Research

Problem 7.10 · Intermediary Asset Pricing vs Household Consumption

He & Krishnamurthy (2013) argue that asset prices are determined by the marginal utility of financial intermediaries (equity capital of broker-dealers) rather than household consumption. How does this resolve the equity premium puzzle without extreme household risk aversion?

Solution

In intermediary asset pricing, households delegate portfolio management to leveraged financial intermediaries. During financial panics, intermediary equity capital is depleted by asset writedowns, causing their regulatory balance-sheet constraints to bind. Intermediaries become intensely risk-averse in a shadow sense, demanding astronomical risk premia to hold risky assets. Because intermediary capital is 10 times more volatile and procyclical than aggregate household consumption, the model generates realistic equity premia and credit spreads with modest fundamental risk aversion.

Problem 7.11 · Structural Identification via Subjective Expectation Surveys

How can modern researchers incorporate subjective survey data directly into structural asset pricing estimation to discipline expectations without abandoning optimization (Adam, Marcet, and Beutel 2017)?

Solution

Internal rationality models (Adam et al. 2017) preserve utility maximization and forward-looking optimization, but relax the rational expectations assumption that agents know the exact true probability law of asset prices. Instead, agents form subjective beliefs about future stock price growth using Bayesian learning algorithms. Incorporating empirical survey expectations directly into the model's Euler equations matches price volatility, boom-bust valuation cycles, and return predictability with low risk aversion without requiring unobservable habit or growth states.

Problem 7.12 · Machine Learning Synthesis of Canonical Models

How might neural networks and generalized deep learning architectures estimate an unconstrained macroeconomic pricing kernel that nests habits, long-run risks, and disaster jumps?

Solution

Deep neural network SDF estimators (Chen, Pelger, and Zhu 2024) use continuous macroeconomic and financial data to non-parametrically approximate the pricing kernel \(M_{t+1} = f(X_t, X_{t+1}; \theta)\). By conditioning on consumption histories, market volatility, and tail proxies simultaneously, neural SDFs can reveal whether nonlinear habit functions, persistent growth shocks, or tail disaster jumps receive the largest empirical weights, bypassing the rigid functional form restrictions of analytical canonical models.
