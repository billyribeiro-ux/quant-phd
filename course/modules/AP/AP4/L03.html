Recursive Preferences & Long-Run Risks: The Bansal–Yaron Model

Standard consumption models assume shocks to consumption are independently and identically distributed. Bansal & Yaron (2004) proved that combining Epstein–Zin recursive utility with small, highly persistent predictable growth components and time-varying economic uncertainty can explain the equity premium, risk-free rate, and dividend yield volatility with moderate risk aversion, provided agents prefer early resolution of uncertainty and the intertemporal elasticity of substitution exceeds one.

1Epstein–Zin Preferences & Temporal Risk Separation

Standard expected utility inextricably links relative risk aversion \(\gamma\) to the inverse of the elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(\psi\): \(\gamma = 1/\psi\). Epstein & Zin (1989) and Weil (1989) decoupled these two distinct economic concepts using recursive preference aggregators.

Definition 3.1 (Epstein–Zin Recursive Preferences)

Let \(C_t\) denote real consumption and \(\beta \in (0, 1)\) the subjective time discount factor. The recursive utility index \(V_t\) is defined by

\[ \begin{aligned} V_t &= \left[ (1 - \beta) C_t^{1 - 1/\psi} + \beta \left( \E_t \left[ V_{t+1}^{1 - \gamma} \right] \right)^{\frac{1 - 1/\psi}{1 - \gamma}} \right]^{\frac{1}{1 - 1/\psi}} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\gamma \ge 0\) governs relative risk aversion across states, and \(\psi \ge 0\) measures the elasticity of intertemporal substitution (EIS) across time periods. Define the composite parameter \(\theta\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta &\equiv \frac{1 - \gamma}{1 - \frac{1}{\psi}} \end{aligned} \]

Three parameter regimes determine attitudes toward the timing of uncertainty:

  • Preference for early resolution of uncertainty: \(\gamma > 1/\psi \implies \theta < 1\) (when \(\psi > 1\)). The agent prefers learning about future consumption streams earlier rather than later.
  • Indifference to timing: \(\gamma = 1/\psi \implies \theta = 1\). This collapses exactly to standard time-separable power utility.
  • Preference for late resolution of uncertainty: \(\gamma < 1/\psi \implies \theta > 1\). The agent prefers delay in information revelation.

2Macroeconomic Environment: Growth & Volatility Dynamics

In the canonical framework of Bansal & Yaron (2004), the macroeconomy is characterized by two distinct stochastic shocks: persistent expected growth and time-varying volatility.

Definition 3.2 (Long-Run Risk Model)

In the long-run risk model, log consumption growth \(\Delta c_{t+1}\), the unobserved predictable growth component \(x_t\), and macroeconomic volatility \(\sigma_t^2\) follow the joint state space:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta c_{t+1} &= \mu + x_t + \sigma_t \eta_{t+1} \\ x_{t+1} &= \rho x_t + \phi_e \sigma_t e_{t+1} \\ \sigma_{t+1}^2 &= \bar{\sigma}^2 + \nu (\sigma_t^2 - \bar{\sigma}^2) + \sigma_w w_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\eta_{t+1}, e_{t+1}, w_{t+1} \sim \text{i.i.d.} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) are mutually independent innovations. Dividend growth \(\Delta d_{t+1}\) is leveraged on expected consumption growth:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta d_{t+1} &= \mu_d + \phi_d x_t + \varphi_d \sigma_t u_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\phi_d > 1\) denotes corporate dividend leverage and \(u_{t+1}\) is an idiosyncratic dividend shock.

Although the variance of the persistent shock \(e_{t+1}\) is small (\(\phi_e \approx 0.044\)), its persistence is near unity (\(\rho \approx 0.979\) at annual frequencies). Thus, small revisions in expected growth persist for decades, compounding into large fluctuations in expected lifetime wealth.

3Campbell–Shiller Log-Linearization & Asset Prices

To solve for equilibrium asset prices analytically, Bansal and Yaron apply the Campbell & Shiller (1988) log-linear approximation to the return on the consumption wealth portfolio \(r_{a, t+1}\) and equity dividend portfolio \(r_{m, t+1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} r_{a, t+1} &\approx k_0 + k_1 z_{c, t+1} - z_{c, t} + \Delta c_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

where \(z_{c, t} \equiv \ln(P_t^c / C_t)\) is the log price-consumption ratio, and \(k_1 = \exp(\bar{z}_c) / (1 + \exp(\bar{z}_c)) \in (0, 1)\).

Theorem 3.1 (Consumption & Equity Valuation Coefficients)

The log price-consumption ratio is linear in state variables: \(z_{c, t} = A_{0, c} + A_{1, c} x_t + A_{2, c} \sigma_t^2\), where

\[ \begin{aligned} A_{1, c} &= \frac{1 - \frac{1}{\psi}}{1 - k_1 \rho}, \quad A_{2, c} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\left( 1 - \gamma \right) \left( 1 - \frac{1}{\psi} \right) + \theta \left( k_1 A_{1, c} \phi_e \right)^2}{\theta (1 - k_1 \nu)} \end{aligned} \]

For the equity claim on dividends \(z_{m, t} = A_{0, m} + A_{1, m} x_t + A_{2, m} \sigma_t^2\) with Campbell-Shiller parameter \(\kappa_1 \approx 0.96\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A_{1, m} &= \frac{\phi_d - \frac{1}{\psi}}{1 - \kappa_1 \rho} \end{aligned} \]
Proof sketch

From the Euler equation for the wealth portfolio, \(\E_t \left[ \exp \left( \theta \ln \beta - \frac{\theta}{\psi} \Delta c_{t+1} + \theta r_{a, t+1} \right) \right] = 1\). Substituting the log-linearized return \(r_{a, t+1} \approx k_0 + k_1 z_{c, t+1} - z_{c, t} + \Delta c_{t+1}\) into the exponent and equating coefficients on the state variable \(x_t\) gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} -\theta z_{c, t} + \theta k_1 \E_t [z_{c, t+1}] + \theta \left(1 - \frac{1}{\psi}\right) \E_t [\Delta c_{t+1}] &= 0 \\ -\theta A_{1, c} x_t + \theta k_1 A_{1, c} \rho x_t + \theta \left(1 - \frac{1}{\psi}\right) x_t &= 0 \\ A_{1, c} (1 - k_1 \rho) &= 1 - \frac{1}{\psi} \implies A_{1, c} = \frac{1 - 1/\psi}{1 - k_1 \rho} \end{aligned} \]

The dividend claim follows identically using the dividend Euler equation with leverage \(\phi_d\).

Economic Role of EIS (\(\psi > 1\))

When expected growth \(x_t\) rises, there are two competing effects on the price-consumption ratio: the substitution effect (higher expected return encourages saving, raising asset prices) and the wealth effect (higher future income encourages current consumption, lowering asset prices). If \(\psi > 1\), the substitution effect dominates: \(A_{1, c} > 0\) and \(A_{1, m} > 0\), meaning asset prices rise during good economic news. If \(\psi < 1\), \(A_{1, c} < 0\), meaning stock prices would paradoxically fall in response to positive growth expectations.

4Stochastic Discount Factor & Risk Price Decomposition

Under Epstein–Zin preferences, the log stochastic discount factor \(m_{t+1}\) takes the operational form:

\[ \begin{aligned} m_{t+1} &= \theta \ln \beta - \frac{\theta}{\psi} \Delta c_{t+1} + (\theta - 1) r_{a, t+1} \end{aligned} \]

Decomposing the SDF into its innovations reveals three distinct priced economic risks:

\[ \begin{aligned} m_{t+1} - \E_t [m_{t+1}] &= -\lambda_\eta \sigma_t \eta_{t+1} - \lambda_e \sigma_t e_{t+1} - \lambda_w \sigma_w w_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]

where the market prices of risk are given analytically by:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_\eta &= \gamma \\ \lambda_e &= \left( \gamma - \frac{1}{\psi} \right) \frac{k_1 \phi_e}{1 - k_1 \rho} \\ \lambda_w &= (1 - \theta) k_1 A_{2, c} \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 3.2 (Long-Run Risk Premium)

The equilibrium expected excess return on equity consists of compensation for short-run consumption risk, long-run growth risk, and volatility risk:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_t [r_{m, t+1} - r_{f, t}] + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_{m, t}^2 &= \beta_{\eta, m} \lambda_\eta \sigma_t^2 + \beta_{e, m} \lambda_e \sigma_t^2 + \beta_{w, m} \lambda_w \sigma_w^2 \end{aligned} \]

where \(\beta_{e, m} = \kappa_1 A_{1, m} \phi_e\). When \(\gamma > 1/\psi\) and \(\rho \to 1\), the long-run risk multiplier \(\frac{k_1 \phi_e}{1 - k_1 \rho}\) magnifies small growth shocks into the dominant component of the equity premium.

Where the canon breaks

While Bansal & Yaron (2004) successfully resolves the equity premium, risk-free rate, and dividend yield volatility with moderate risk aversion, the model faces three significant empirical challenges:

  • Predictability of consumption growth: The BY calibration implies that the price-dividend ratio predicts consumption growth at 3- to 5-year horizons with \(R^2 > 30\%\). In empirical US data (1929–2008), the predictive \(R^2\) is virtually zero or negative (Beeler & Campbell 2012).
  • EIS magnitude controversy: The model strictly requires \(\psi > 1\). Microeconometric estimates of \(\psi\) using household cohort consumption surveys typically estimate \(\psi \in [0.1, 0.5]\), which would invert the valuation response to growth shocks.
  • Unobservable persistent state: Because \(\phi_e \sigma \ll \sigma\), the persistent expected growth state \(x_t\) is statistically indistinguishable from white noise in finite macro time series, forcing the model to rely on forward-looking asset prices for identification.

Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Epstein-Zin preferences imply that risk aversion and the elasticity of intertemporal substitution are completely independent concepts that can be chosen without any interaction.”

    While Epstein-Zin preferences decouple \(\gamma\) and \(\psi\) in utility specification, their relative magnitude \(\gamma \gtrless 1/\psi\) fundamentally governs the sign of risk prices: if \(\gamma < 1/\psi\), agents prefer late resolution of uncertainty, which would make the price of long-run growth risk negative.

  2. “Higher expected growth always increases stock prices regardless of investor preferences.”

    Higher expected growth has two offsetting effects: an income effect (wealthier future implies less need to save today, depressing current valuations) and a substitution effect (higher expected returns induce saving, raising valuations). Stock prices only rise on positive growth news if the substitution effect dominates, requiring \(\psi > 1\).

  3. “In the Bansal-Yaron model, large equity returns reflect large contemporaneous consumption surprises.”

    Contemporaneous consumption shocks explain almost none of equity return volatility in the BY model. Equity volatility is driven by news about persistent future growth (\(e_{t+1}\)) and news about future economic uncertainty (\(w_{t+1}\)).

Mechanics

Problem 3.1 · Epstein-Zin Composite Parameter

Compute the Epstein-Zin composite parameter \(\theta \equiv \frac{1 - \gamma}{1 - 1/\psi}\) for \(\gamma = 7.5\) and \(\psi = 1.25\). Does this agent prefer early or late resolution of uncertainty?

Solution

\(\theta = \frac{1 - 7.5}{1 - 1 / 1.25} = \frac{-6.5}{1 - 0.8} = \frac{-6.5}{0.2} = -32.5\). Because \(\gamma = 7.5 > 1 / 1.25 = 0.8\), \(\gamma > 1/\psi\), which implies a strict preference for early resolution of uncertainty.

Problem 3.2 · Price-Dividend Coefficient \(A_{1, m}\)

Given dividend leverage \(\phi_d = 2.5\), \(\psi = 1.5\), \(\kappa_1 = 0.95\), and persistence \(\rho = 0.98\), compute the analytical sensitivity coefficient \(A_{1, m}\) to two decimal places.

Solution

\(A_{1, m} = \frac{\phi_d - 1/\psi}{1 - \kappa_1 \rho} = \frac{2.5 - 1/1.5}{1 - 0.95 \times 0.98} = \frac{2.5 - 0.6667}{1 - 0.931} = \frac{1.8333}{0.069} \approx 26.57\).

Problem 3.3 · Long-Run Risk Price \(\lambda_e\)

Using parameters \(\gamma = 10\), \(\psi = 1.5\), \(k_1 = 0.96\), \(\phi_e = 0.044\), and \(\rho = 0.979\), calculate the market price of long-run growth risk \(\lambda_e\).

Solution

\(\lambda_e = (\gamma - 1/\psi) \frac{k_1 \phi_e}{1 - k_1 \rho} = (10 - 0.6667) \frac{0.96 \times 0.044}{1 - 0.96 \times 0.979} = 9.3333 \times \frac{0.04224}{1 - 0.93984} = 9.3333 \times \frac{0.04224}{0.06016} = 9.3333 \times 0.70213 \approx 6.55\).

Proof

Problem 3.4 · Derivation of Consumption Euler Equation

Prove that when \(\psi = 1/\gamma\), the Epstein-Zin composite parameter \(\theta = 1\), and the recursive pricing kernel \(m_{t+1} = \theta \ln \beta - \frac{\theta}{\psi} \Delta c_{t+1} + (\theta - 1) r_{a, t+1}\) reduces to the standard power utility stochastic discount factor.

Solution

When \(\psi = 1/\gamma\), \(1 - 1/\psi = 1 - \gamma\), so \(\theta = \frac{1 - \gamma}{1 - 1/\psi} = 1\). Substituting \(\theta = 1\) into the log SDF expression: \(m_{t+1} = 1 \cdot \ln \beta - \frac{1}{1/\gamma} \Delta c_{t+1} + (1 - 1) r_{a, t+1} = \ln \beta - \gamma \Delta c_{t+1}\). Exponentiating yields \(M_{t+1} = \beta (C_{t+1}/C_t)^{-\gamma}\), which is exactly the standard time-separable CRRA stochastic discount factor.

Problem 3.5 · Sign of Valuation Sensitivity to Growth

Prove that \(\frac{\partial z_{c, t}}{\partial x_t} > 0\) if and only if \(\psi > 1\), assuming \(k_1 \in (0, 1)\) and \(|\rho| < 1\).

Solution

From Theorem 3.1, \(\frac{\partial z_{c, t}}{\partial x_t} = A_{1, c} = \frac{1 - 1/\psi}{1 - k_1 \rho}\). Since \(k_1 \in (0, 1)\) and \(|\rho| < 1\), the denominator \(1 - k_1 \rho > 0\). Therefore, the sign of \(A_{1, c}\) is determined entirely by the numerator: \(1 - 1/\psi > 0 \iff 1/\psi < 1 \iff \psi > 1\). Hence, an increase in expected growth raises wealth valuations if and only if the elasticity of intertemporal substitution strictly exceeds one.

Problem 3.6 · Long-Run Risk Invariance under Late Resolution

Show that if households are indifferent to the timing of uncertainty (\(\theta = 1\)), the market price of long-run growth risk \(\lambda_e\) collapses to zero in the absence of contemporaneous consumption covariance.

Solution

From \(\lambda_e = (\gamma - 1/\psi) \frac{k_1 \phi_e}{1 - k_1 \rho}\), when \(\theta = 1\), we have \(\gamma = 1/\psi\). Thus \(\gamma - 1/\psi = 0\), which implies \(\lambda_e = 0\). Intuitively, with time-separable expected utility, agents do not care about news regarding future consumption growth unless that news directly impacts current consumption or future wealth in a non-linear way not captured by current consumption.

Empirical

Problem 3.7 · Beeler-Campbell Growth Predictability Test

In US historical data (1930–2008), a predictive regression of cumulative 5-year consumption growth \(\Delta c_{t, t+5}\) on the dividend-price ratio \(dp_t\) yields a slope coefficient of \(-0.04\) with an \(R^2\) of \(0.02\). Compare this with the Bansal-Yaron model implied regression slope and explain the discrepancy.

Solution

The BY model implies that low dividend-price ratios strongly predict higher future consumption growth over 3 to 5 years, with population regression slopes \(\beta_{dp} < -0.30\) and \(R^2 > 25\%\). In empirical data, price-dividend ratios predict future excess stock returns, not future consumption growth. This is the central empirical challenge highlighted by Beeler & Campbell (2012): the predictability channel that fuels the model's equity premium is virtually absent in macroeconomic consumption data.

Problem 3.8 · Macroeconomic Volatility Regressions

Describe how Bansal & Yaron identify the time-varying volatility process \(\sigma_t^2\). What observable proxies are used in empirical tests, and what is the estimated autoregressive persistence \(\nu\)?

Solution

BY identify \(\sigma_t^2\) by fitting an AR(1) to the absolute residuals or squared residuals of consumption and dividend growth regressions. Empirical estimates typically find \(\nu \approx 0.987\) at monthly frequencies (or \(\approx 0.85\) annually), reflecting prolonged periods of low volatility (such as the Great Moderation) interspersed with spikes during financial crises and severe recessions.

Problem 3.9 · Term Structure of Equity Returns

Does the Bansal-Yaron model predict an upward-sloping or downward-sloping term structure of equity risk premia (dividend strips)? How does this compare with recent empirical findings (van Binsbergen et al. 2012)?

Solution

Because long-run growth risk is persistent, distant cash flows have higher exposure to \(x_t\) shocks than near-term cash flows. Consequently, the long-run risks model predicts an upward-sloping term structure of equity risk premia (longer-duration dividend strips command higher risk premia). Empirically, van Binsbergen, Brandt, and Koijen (2012) found a downward-sloping equity term structure, presenting an empirical challenge to pure duration-based long-run risk mechanisms.

Research

Problem 3.10 · Micro vs Macro Estimates of the EIS

Synthesize the debate over whether the EIS \(\psi\) is greater or less than 1. Why is this parameter magnitude crucial for asset pricing models with Epstein-Zin preferences?

Solution

Macroeconomic asset pricing models (Bansal-Yaron) require \(\psi > 1\) so that the substitution effect dominates the wealth effect, ensuring that asset prices rise when expected growth improves and that stock volatility is high. Conversely, microeconometric studies (Hall 1988; Campbell 1999) using household-level Euler regressions often find \(\psi < 0.5\). Proponents of \(\psi > 1\) argue that micro studies suffer from measurement error, liquidity constraints, and aggregation bias, and that stockholders (who determine asset prices) have substantially higher EIS than non-stockholders.

Problem 3.11 · Structural Filtering of the Unobserved State \(x_t\)

Because the expected consumption growth state \(x_t\) has very low volatility compared to contemporaneous shocks (\(\phi_e \sigma \ll \sigma\)), it cannot be easily distinguished from white noise in finite samples. What filtering problems do econometricians face when testing the long-run risk hypothesis?

Solution

In standard ARMA representations, a persistent AR(1) process contaminated with large measurement noise is statistically indistinguishable from an i.i.d. process without century-long sample spans. Consequently, standard univariate statistical tests fail to reject the null hypothesis that consumption growth is a pure random walk. To identify \(x_t\), econometricians must rely on joint estimation with forward-looking asset prices (e.g. price-dividend ratios), creating a circularity where asset prices are used to confirm the existence of the risk that is meant to explain those same asset prices.

Problem 3.12 · Volatility Shocks vs Growth Shocks in the SDF

Evaluate the relative quantitative importance of expected growth shocks \(e_{t+1}\) versus economic uncertainty shocks \(w_{t+1}\) in explaining equity risk premia and return volatility in extended long-run risk specifications.

Solution

In the original Bansal-Yaron (2004) baseline, expected growth risk accounts for over 70% of the equity premium, while volatility risk accounts for approximately 25-30%. However, in extended specifications (e.g. Bansal, Kiku, and Yaron 2012; Campbell et al. 2018), time-varying volatility risk plays a critical role in generating large countercyclical Sharpe ratios, option implied volatility smirks, and credit spread dynamics that growth risk alone cannot reproduce.
