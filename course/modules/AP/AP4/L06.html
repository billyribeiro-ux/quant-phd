Modern asset pricing models boast dozens of parameters capable of matching headline equity premia and risk-free rates. Rigorous econometric evaluation demands assessing models across comprehensive scorecards of joint macroeconomic and financial moments. As Beeler & Campbell (2012) proved, models can successfully calibrate financial returns while relying on counterfactual macroeconomic mechanisms—such as unobserved multi-year growth predictability—demonstrating that matching unconditional returns alone is insufficient to validate a model.

1Econometric Methodology: Calibration vs. Moment Matching

In structural macro-finance, researchers evaluate candidate models using either point calibration or formal statistical estimation. In the Method of Simulated Moments (MSM), structural parameters \(\theta\) are chosen to minimize the weighted distance between sample moments \(\hat{m}_T\) and model-simulated moments \(m(\theta)\).

Definition 6.1 (Moment Matching Model Evaluation)

Moment-matching model evaluation scores a dynamic structural asset pricing model against a vector of \(K\) empirical target moments \(\hat{m} \in \R^K\) using an objective loss metric:

\[ \begin{aligned} J(\theta; W) &= \left( \hat{m} - m(\theta) \right)' W \left( \hat{m} - m(\theta) \right) \end{aligned} \]

where \(W\) is a positive-definite weighting matrix. When \(W = \hat{\Omega}^{-1}\) (the inverse covariance matrix of empirical moment errors), \(T \cdot J(\hat{\theta})\) asymptotically follows a \(\chi^2(K - p)\) distribution under the null hypothesis of correct model specification.

When computing summary scorecards without full sample covariances, empirical finance commonly utilizes normalized loss metrics, such as the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) or weighted sum of squared relative deviations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{MAPE} &= \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{|m_k(\theta) - \hat{m}_k|}{|\hat{m}_k|} \end{aligned} \]

3The Beeler & Campbell (2012) Diagnostic

The core contribution of Beeler & Campbell (2012) was to demonstrate that evaluating models solely on asset market moments produces severe model misspecification. They applied diagnostic tests to the Bansal & Yaron (2004) model and discovered that its apparent success hinged on counterfactual implications elsewhere in the macroeconomy:

  • Predictability of Real Consumption Growth: In the Bansal-Yaron calibration, the price-dividend ratio strongly forecasts real consumption growth at 3- to 5-year horizons with \(R^2 > 30\%\). In empirical US data, regressions of multi-year consumption growth on the dividend-price ratio yield \(R^2 \approx 0\) or slight negative coefficients.
  • Excessive Interest Rate Predictability: The BY model implies that the price-dividend ratio strongly predicts real interest rates, whereas in US data, real interest rates are largely unforecastable by dividend yields.
  • Downward-Biased Volatility Persistence: Generating sufficient equity volatility requires high dividend leverage (\(\phi_d = 3\)), which causes dividend growth volatility to exceed historical empirical variance.

4Model Scorecard Comparison: CC, BY, and Barro–Rietz

When evaluated across the 12 canonical moments under identical historical sample lengths, each canonical model displays distinct trade-offs:

  • Campbell–Cochrane (1999): Perfectly matches the equity premium, equity volatility, and valuation persistence. However, it forces risk-free rate volatility to zero by construction (\(\sigma(R_f) = 0\), failing empirical \(\sigma(R_f) = 5.4\%\)) and requires local risk aversion to exceed 100 in downturns.
  • Bansal–Yaron (2004): Accurately matches the Sharpe ratio and valuation persistence with low risk aversion (\(\gamma = 10\)). However, it generates counterfactual multi-year consumption growth predictability and requires \(\psi > 1\).
  • Barro (2006) / Rietz (1988): Matches the calm-sample equity premium with low risk aversion (\(\gamma = 4\)) and generates strong precautionary savings. However, in its constant-probability baseline, the price-dividend ratio is constant, completely failing to generate excess return volatility (\(\sigma(p-d) = 0\)) or return predictability (\(\beta_{dp} = 0\)).

Where the canon breaks

Moment matching and scorecard evaluations reveal deep limitations in the consensus methodology of macroeconomic finance:

  • Over-Fitting via Unconstrained Calibration: Structural models often feature 10 to 15 unobservable parameters (e.g. latent persistence \(\rho\), subjective discount factor \(\beta\), shock exposure \(\phi_e\), disaster intensity \(\lambda\)). With sufficient degrees of freedom, models can fit any designated set of moments while failing completely out of sample.
  • Finite Sample Monte Carlo Bias: Model moments are often reported as asymptotic population expectations, whereas empirical targets are estimated from noisy 80-year samples. Small-sample Jensen's inequality and Hurwicz bias can distort moment comparisons by 20% to 50%.
  • Arbitrary Moment Weighting: Standard practice often applies equal weighting or ad-hoc weights to moments rather than the efficient GMM weighting matrix \(\Omega^{-1}\), allowing researchers to mask failures on moments their model cannot match.

Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “If a structural model matches the historical equity premium and equity volatility, it has successfully resolved the equity premium puzzle.”

    Matching asset returns is easy by tuning risk aversion or shock variance. A true resolution requires matching asset returns without violating underlying macroeconomic data, such as consumption predictability, labor risk, or the term structure of interest rates.

  2. “In GMM estimation, the identity weighting matrix \(W = I\) is asymptotically equivalent to the optimal weighting matrix \(W = \Omega^{-1}\).”

    While parameter estimators under \(W=I\) remain consistent, they are statistically inefficient. Moreover, the test of overidentifying restrictions \(J_T\) requires the optimal weighting matrix to follow a standard asymptotic \(\chi^2\) distribution.

  3. “The Beeler-Campbell critique showed that recursive utility models are mathematically invalid.”

    Beeler & Campbell did not critique recursive utility itself; they demonstrated that the specific Bansal-Yaron calibration achieves its asset pricing success via an unobserved growth predictability mechanism that is refuted by historical US consumption data.

Mechanics

Problem 6.1 · Relative Error Calculation

A candidate model produces a simulated equity premium of \(4.9\%\), while the historical empirical target is \(6.2\%\). Calculate the absolute relative error percentage to two decimal places.

Solution

The relative error formula divides the absolute difference between model simulation and historical empirical target by the target value: \(\text{Rel Error} = \frac{|0.049 - 0.062|}{0.062} = \frac{0.013}{0.062} \approx 0.2097\), corresponding to an absolute relative error of 20.97%.

Problem 6.2 · Sharpe Ratio Calculation from Primitive Moments

If a simulated model generates a mean excess return of \(\E[R_m - R_f] = 0.055\) and return volatility \(\sigma(R_m) = 0.175\), compute the model Sharpe ratio to four decimal places.

Solution

The annualized Sharpe ratio is defined as the mean equity premium divided by equity return volatility. Substituting the primitive model moments yields \(\text{SR} = \frac{\E[R_m - R_f]}{\sigma(R_m)} = \frac{0.055}{0.175} \approx 0.3143\), matching historical US equity performance.

Problem 6.3 · Weighted Distance Score

Consider two moments with errors \(e_1 = 0.10\) and \(e_2 = 0.25\). If weights are \(w_1 = 2.0\) and \(w_2 = 1.0\), calculate the weighted squared distance \(J = w_1 e_1^2 + w_2 e_2^2\).

Solution

\(J = 2.0 \times (0.10)^2 + 1.0 \times (0.25)^2 = 2.0 \times 0.010 + 0.0625 = 0.020 + 0.0625 = 0.0825\).

Proof

Problem 6.4 · Asymptotic Distribution of GMM Criterion

Prove that under the null hypothesis of correct specification, the GMM criterion function \(J_T(\hat{\theta}) = T \cdot g_T(\hat{\theta})' \hat{\Omega}^{-1} g_T(\hat{\theta})\) asymptotically converges in distribution to a \(\chi^2\) with \(K - p\) degrees of freedom, where \(K\) is the number of moments and \(p\) is the number of estimated parameters.

Solution

Under standard regularity conditions, \(\sqrt{T} g_T(\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Omega)\). Expanding \(\sqrt{T} g_T(\hat{\theta})\) around \(\theta_0\) gives \(\sqrt{T} g_T(\hat{\theta}) = [I - D (D' \Omega^{-1} D)^{-1} D' \Omega^{-1}] \sqrt{T} g_T(\theta_0) + o_p(1)\), where \(D = \E[\partial g / \partial \theta']\). The projection matrix \(P = \Omega^{-1/2} [I - D (D' \Omega^{-1} D)^{-1} D' \Omega^{-1}] \Omega^{1/2}\) is idempotent with rank \(K - p\). Therefore, the quadratic form \(T g_T(\hat{\theta})' \Omega^{-1} g_T(\hat{\theta})\) represents the sum of squares of \(K - p\) independent standard normal variables, which is distributed as \(\chi^2(K - p)\).

Problem 6.5 · Hurwicz Finite-Sample Autoregressive Bias

Show that the OLS estimator of an AR(1) persistence coefficient \(\rho\) in a sample of size \(T\) is negatively biased, satisfying \(\E[\hat{\rho}] \approx \rho - \frac{1 + 3\rho}{T}\), and explain how this impacts empirical moment matching.

Solution

Following Hurwicz (1950) and Kendall (1954), the OLS error \(\hat{\rho} - \rho = \frac{\sum y_{t-1} \epsilon_t}{\sum y_{t-1}^2}\). Because \(y_{t-1}\) contains lagged innovations that correlate negatively with sample demeaned residuals, the expectation of the ratio yields \(\E[\hat{\rho} - \rho] = -\frac{1 + 3\rho}{T} + O(T^{-2})\). When comparing simulated models to empirical 80-year samples (\(T=80\)), a true persistence of \(\rho = 0.87\) will be estimated in-sample as \(\hat{\rho} \approx 0.87 - \frac{1 + 3(0.87)}{80} \approx 0.87 - 0.045 = 0.825\). Failing to account for this bias leads researchers to incorrectly reject properly calibrated models.

Problem 6.6 · Campbell-Shiller Decomposition of Predictability Slope

Prove using the Campbell-Shiller identity that the predictive regression slope of excess returns on the dividend yield \(\beta_{dp}\) reflects either cash flow predictability or discount rate predictability.

Solution

From the Campbell-Shiller approximation, \(d_t - p_t = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} r_{t+j} - \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \Delta d_{t+j} + C\). Taking covariance with \(d_t - p_t\) on both sides: \(\Var(d_t - p_t) = \Cov(d_t - p_t, \sum \rho^{j-1} r_{t+j}) - \Cov(d_t - p_t, \sum \rho^{j-1} \Delta d_{t+j})\). Dividing by \(\Var(d_t - p_t)\) gives \(1 = \beta_{r}^{long} - \beta_{d}^{long}\). For 1-year returns, \(\beta_{dp} = \frac{\Cov(r_{t+1}, d_t - p_t)}{\Var(d_t - p_t)}\). A non-zero slope \(\beta_{dp}\) mathematically requires that either returns or dividend growth are predictable from current valuation multiples.

Empirical

Problem 6.7 · Multi-Horizon Predictive Regressions in US Data

Describe the empirical behavior of predictive regressions of stock returns on the dividend yield as the forecasting horizon increases from 1 year to 5 years (Cochrane 2011). How do \(R^2\) values behave?

Solution

In US empirical data (Cochrane 2011), regressing cumulative excess stock returns \(r_{t \to t+k}\) on the dividend-price ratio \(dp_t\) produces predictive slopes that scale approximately with horizon \(k\): the 1-year regression slope is \(\approx 0.15\) with \(R^2 \approx 8\%\), whereas the 5-year slope rises to \(\approx 0.60\) with \(R^2 > 25-30\%\). High current dividend yields reliably predict high multi-year returns, confirming that stock market valuation fluctuations are driven primarily by time-varying discount rates.

Problem 6.8 · Long-Horizon Consumption Predictability Test

How did Beeler & Campbell (2012) test the consumption predictability implication of the Bansal-Yaron model? What empirical regression specification was used, and what were the historical point estimates?

Solution

Beeler & Campbell ran regressions of cumulative real consumption growth \(\sum_{j=1}^k \Delta c_{t+j}\) on the log price-dividend ratio \(p_t - d_t\) for horizons \(k \in \{1, 3, 5\}\) years. The BY model predicts positive slopes with \(R^2\) exceeding 20-30%. In US annual data (1930–2008), the empirical slope coefficients were statistically indistinguishable from zero or negative, with \(R^2\) values below 2%, indicating that price-dividend ratios do not forecast future macroeconomic consumption growth.

Problem 6.9 · Risk-Free Rate Volatility Puzzle

Why do models that match the equity premium typically struggle to match real risk-free rate volatility? Compare the Campbell-Cochrane model (\(\sigma(r_f) = 0\)) with empirical US Treasury inflation-protected securities (TIPS) volatility.

Solution

To prevent high risk aversion from generating wildly volatile real interest rates through the intertemporal substitution channel, models either assume high EIS (Bansal-Yaron) or engineer sensitivity functions that force \(\sigma(r_f) = 0\) (Campbell-Cochrane). However, empirical real interest rates measured via historical inflation-adjusted bills or TIPS exhibit standard deviations of 2% to 5% per annum. Matching both a stable yet realistically volatile real interest rate remains an econometric challenge.

Research

Problem 6.10 · Hansen-Jagannathan Distance vs Moment Scorecards

Compare the econometric properties of the Hansen-Jagannathan (1997) distance metric with discrete moment-matching scorecards. What advantages does the HJ-distance offer when comparing non-nested models?

Solution

The HJ-distance measures the least-squares distance between a candidate pricing kernel \(M(\theta)\) and the set of admissible SDFs that correctly price a reference set of test assets. Unlike GMM with model-specific weighting matrices, the HJ-distance uses a common weighting matrix (the second moment matrix of asset payoffs \(E[X X']^{-1}\)), providing a uniform metric to rank non-nested models (e.g., habit vs. long-run risks vs. disaster models) without favoring models with more volatile pricing kernels.

Problem 6.11 · Structural Identification from Macro vs Asset Prices

Discuss the epistemological dilemma in structural asset pricing where latent macroeconomic states (such as \(x_t\) in BY or \(\lambda_t\) in Wachter) cannot be identified from macroeconomic data alone, but must be inferred from asset prices.

Solution

If a latent state is identified solely by inverting asset prices (e.g. treating \(p_t - d_t\) as an exact observation of \(x_t\)), the model's ability to fit asset prices becomes tautological: the asset price is used to create the state variable that is subsequently said to explain the asset price. Econometric identification is only scientifically compelling when the latent state can be independently verified using external macroeconomic data, survey expectations, or real economic outcomes.

Problem 6.12 · Machine Learning and High-Dimensional Moment Selection

How can machine learning methods (such as LASSO or simulated generalized empirical likelihood) improve moment selection when conducting model horse races across dozens of competing macro-finance moments?

Solution

In high-dimensional moment matching, arbitrary selection of test moments can bias model evaluations toward specific preferred features. Regularized GMM and empirical likelihood algorithms automatically penalize redundant or uninformative moments, selecting the subset of moments that provide the highest statistical power against structural misspecification while controlling false discovery rates.
