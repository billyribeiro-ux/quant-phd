The Hansen-Jagannathan Volatility Bound

  • Uses: stochastic discount factor, law of one price, minimum-variance SDF
  • Introduces: Hansen–Jagannathan bound
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Before Hansen & Jagannathan (1991), evaluating whether an asset pricing model succeeded required testing its full structural specification. Hansen & Jagannathan established a model-free benchmark: any valid stochastic discount factor must have a standard deviation at least as large as a quadratic function of asset returns. This geometric duality with the mean-variance efficient frontier provides the universal diagnostic yardstick for financial economics.

1Geometric Duality and the Payoff Space Projection

Consider an economy with \(N\) traded assets having gross payoffs \(x \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) and prices \(p \in \R^N\). By the Law of One Price and the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a stochastic discount factor \(M\) such that \(\E[M x] = p\).

Because financial markets are typically incomplete, \(M\) is not unique. Let \(\mathcal{X} = \{ c^\top x : c \in \R^N \}\) denote the linear subspace of payoffs spanned by traded assets. Any valid SDF \(M\) can be decomposed via the projection theorem into:

\[ \begin{aligned} M &= M_p + \epsilon \end{aligned} \]

where \(M_p \in \mathcal{X}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(M\) onto the payoff space, and \(\epsilon \in \mathcal{X}^\perp\) is an unspanned residual orthogonal to every traded asset payoff:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[\epsilon x] &= \mathbf{0} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(M_p\) is a linear combination of asset payoffs \(M_p = x^\top \alpha\) for some vector \(\alpha \in \R^N\), pricing requires:

\[ \begin{aligned} p &= \E[M x] \\ &= \E[(M_p + \epsilon) x] \\ &= \E[M_p x] + \mathbf{0} \\ &= \E[x x^\top] \alpha \end{aligned} \]

Assuming the second-moment matrix of payoffs \(\E[x x^\top]\) is non-singular, the unique projection is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha &= (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} p \\ M_p &= p^\top (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} x \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\Cov(M_p, \epsilon) = 0\), the variance of any valid SDF satisfies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Var(M) &= \Var(M_p) + \Var(\epsilon) \\ &\ge \Var(M_p) \end{aligned} \]

This establishes that \(M_p\) is the minimum-variance stochastic discount factor among all discount factors pricing the traded assets.

2The Hansen-Jagannathan Volatility Cup

In practice, asset pricing models specify candidate SDFs that imply a particular expected value \(\E[M] = \nu\). To test whether a model with mean \(\nu\) is feasible, Hansen & Jagannathan (1991) augment the payoff space with a synthetic risk-free unit payoff paying 1 with price \(\nu\).

Definition 1.6 Hansen–Jagannathan bound

The Hansen–Jagannathan bound is the minimum volatility required of any valid stochastic discount factor having expected value \(\nu = \E[M]\) to correctly price a set of gross returns \(R \in \R^N\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma(M) &\ge \sqrt{ (\mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R])^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} (\mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R]) } \end{aligned} \]

where \(\Sigma_R = \Cov(R, R^\top)\) is the covariance matrix of asset returns, assumed positive definite.

Theorem 1.1 T Hansen-Jagannathan Frontier Duality

For any fixed mean \(\E[M] = \nu\), the lower bound on SDF variance is achieved by the unique affine projection \(M^*(\nu) = \nu + (R - \E[R])^\top \beta(\nu)\), yielding:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Var(M) &\ge (\mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R])^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} (\mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R]) \end{aligned} \]

In the plane \((\nu, \sigma(M))\), the set of feasible discount factors lies on or above a convex, cup-shaped hyperbolic region whose minimum occurs at \(\nu^* = \frac{\mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R]}{\E[R]^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R]}\).

Proof

Consider the optimization problem: minimize \(\Var(M)\) subject to \(\E[M] = \nu\) and \(\E[M R] = \mathbf{1}\). Express the pricing condition in centered moments:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[M R] &= \E[M]\E[R] + \Cov(M, R) \\ &= \nu \E[R] + \Cov(M, R) = \mathbf{1} \end{aligned} \]

Rearranging yields the linear constraint on covariances:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Cov(M, R) &= \mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R] \end{aligned} \]

Project \(M - \nu\) onto the centered returns \(R - \E[R]\): \(M - \nu = (R - \E[R])^\top b + e\), where \(\Cov(e, R) = \mathbf{0}\). Taking covariance with \(R\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Cov(M, R) &= \Sigma_R b \\ \implies b &= \Sigma_R^{-1} (\mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R]) \end{aligned} \]

The variance of \(M\) is then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Var(M) &= b^\top \Sigma_R b + \Var(e) \\ &\ge b^\top \Sigma_R b \\ &= (\mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R])^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} (\mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R]) \end{aligned} \]

Taking the square root yields the standard deviation bound.

Duality with the Sharpe Ratio Frontier

When a risk-free asset is traded, its gross return pins \(\E[M] = 1/R_f\). Substituting \(\nu = 1/R_f\) into the Hansen-Jagannathan bound:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{1} - \frac{\E[R]}{R_f} &= - \frac{\E[R] - R_f \mathbf{1}}{R_f} \end{aligned} \]

Substituting into the quadratic form:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Var(M) &\ge \frac{1}{R_f^2} (\E[R] - R_f \mathbf{1})^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} (\E[R] - R_f \mathbf{1}) \end{aligned} \]

Recognizing that \((\E[R] - R_f \mathbf{1})^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} (\E[R] - R_f \mathbf{1}) = (\text{Sharpe}^*)^2\), where \(\text{Sharpe}^*\) is the maximum attainable Sharpe ratio on the tangency portfolio:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma(M) &\ge \frac{\text{Sharpe}^*}{R_f} = \E[M] \times \text{Sharpe}^* \\ \implies \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} &\ge \text{Sharpe}^* \end{aligned} \]

Lab 1 · Hansen-Jagannathan Bound vs. Consumption SDFs

Calculate the Hansen-Jagannathan volatility cup for a 3-asset financial market and test whether CRRA consumption-based SDFs satisfy the bound across a range of risk aversion values.

import numpy as np

# Hansen & Jagannathan (1991) Bound vs. Consumption-Implied SDF Volatility
rng = np.random.default_rng(777)
t_periods = 200

# 3 simulated test asset returns: short-term bill, equity, intermediate bond
returns = np.column_stack(
    [
        1.01 + rng.normal(0.0, 0.01, size=t_periods),
        1.07 + rng.normal(0.0, 0.16, size=t_periods),
        1.03 + rng.normal(0.0, 0.06, size=t_periods),
    ]
)

mu_r = np.mean(returns, axis=0)
sigma_r = np.cov(returns, rowvar=False)
sigma_inv = np.linalg.pinv(sigma_r)
ones = np.ones(3)


def hj_bound(nu: float) -> float:
    diff = ones - nu * mu_r
    variance = float(diff.T @ sigma_inv @ diff)
    return float(np.sqrt(max(0.0, variance)))


# Consumption growth series
gc = 1.0 + rng.normal(0.018, 0.020, size=t_periods)
beta = 0.99
gammas = [1.0, 5.0, 10.0, 25.0, 35.0]

print(f"{'gamma':>5} | {'E[M]':>6} | {'sigma(M)':>8} | {'HJ Bound':>9} | {'Verdict':>8}")
print("-" * 46)
for g in gammas:
    m = beta * (gc ** (-g))
    e_m = float(np.mean(m))
    std_m = float(np.std(m))
    bound = hj_bound(e_m)
    verdict = "FEASIBLE" if std_m >= bound else "REJECTED"
    print(f"{g:5.1f} | {e_m:6.4f} | {std_m:8.4f} | {bound:9.4f} | {verdict:>8}")
gamma |   E[M] | sigma(M) |  HJ Bound |  Verdict
----------------------------------------------
  1.0 | 0.9727 |   0.0199 |    1.9152 | REJECTED
  5.0 | 0.9102 |   0.0936 |    8.1263 | REJECTED
 10.0 | 0.8458 |   0.1753 |   14.5890 | REJECTED
 25.0 | 0.7226 |   0.3929 |   26.9421 | REJECTED
 35.0 | 0.6854 |   0.5471 |   30.6687 | REJECTED

For every risk aversion level tested (\(\gamma = 1\) through \(35\)), the standard deviation of the consumption SDF \(\sigma(M)\) remains far below the Hansen-Jagannathan lower bound. Even at \(\gamma = 35\), \(\sigma(M) = 0.5471\), whereas the required bound is \(30.67\). This visual and numerical test decisively rejects the standard power utility consumption model.

Where the canon breaks

While the Hansen-Jagannathan bound is a model-free diagnostic, empirical implementations face subtle limitations:

  • Finite Sample Estimation Error: The true bound involves population moments \(\E[R]\) and \(\Sigma_R\). In small samples, sample Sharpe ratios are upward-biased, which mechanically inflates the estimated Hansen-Jagannathan cup and leads to spuriously severe rejections of structural models.
  • Non-Negativity (Positivity) Constraints: The basic Hansen-Jagannathan bound only enforces the Law of One Price, allowing candidate SDFs to take negative values (\(M < 0\)). Enforcing strict absence of arbitrage requires \(M > 0\) almost surely, yielding the tighter Hansen-Jagannathan (1991) non-negative bound, which requires numerical duality solving.
  • Short-Sale and Margin Constraints: The bound assumes that all combinations on the mean-variance efficient frontier can be traded without borrowing limits, short-sale frictions, or margin requirements.

Misconceptions

  • The Hansen-Jagannathan bound assumes that asset returns are normally distributed.

    Why it is wrong: The Hansen-Jagannathan bound makes zero distributional assumptions. It relies solely on the projection theorem in \(L^2\) Hilbert space and the definition of covariance, holding equally for fat-tailed, skewed, or jump-diffusion payoffs.

  • A candidate SDF that lies inside the Hansen-Jagannathan cup is confirmed to be the true economic SDF.

    Why it is wrong: The bound provides a necessary condition for validity, never a sufficient condition. An SDF can have ample volatility and enter the feasible region while still mispricing specific cross-sections or predicting incorrect dynamics.

  • The Hansen-Jagannathan bound requires identifying and observing a risk-free rate.

    Why it is wrong: The bound is defined across a continuum of hypothetical means \(\nu = \E[M]\). It maps out an entire frontier in \((\E[M], \sigma(M))\) space without requiring an actual risk-free asset to exist or be traded.

Problem set

Mechanics

A market has a single risky asset with expected gross return \(\E[R] = 1.08\) and return variance \(\sigma_R^2 = 0.04\). Compute the Hansen-Jagannathan lower bound on \(\sigma(M)\) for an SDF with expected value \(\E[M] = 0.95\).

Solution

The scalar formula is \(\sigma(M) \ge \frac{|1 - \nu \E[R]|}{\sigma_R}\). Here \(\nu = 0.95\), \(\E[R] = 1.08\), and \(\sigma_R = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.20\). Compute \(1 - 0.95 \times 1.08 = 1 - 1.026 = -0.026\). The bound is \(\sigma(M) \ge \frac{0.026}{0.20} = 0.13\).

If the risk-free return is \(R_f = 1.02\) and the maximum attainable Sharpe ratio on a set of 5 assets is \(\text{Sharpe}^* = 0.40\), what is the minimum volatility \(\sigma(M)\) required of any valid SDF?

Solution

When a risk-free asset exists, \(\E[M] = 1/R_f = 1/1.02 \approx 0.9804\). The bound states \(\frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} \ge \text{Sharpe}^*\). Therefore, \(\sigma(M) \ge \E[M] \times \text{Sharpe}^* = 0.9804 \times 0.40 = 0.3922\).

A consumption-based SDF \(M = \beta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma}\) has mean \(\E[M] = 0.98\) and volatility \(\sigma(M) = 0.15\). If the HJ bound at \(\nu = 0.98\) is 0.35, is this model rejected by the data?

Solution

Yes. The Hansen-Jagannathan bound establishes a necessary lower bound on volatility for any valid SDF. Because \(\sigma(M) = 0.15 < 0.35\), the consumption SDF lacks sufficient volatility to price the asset returns and is rejected.

Proof

Prove that the minimum of the Hansen-Jagannathan parabola \(\Var(M^*(\nu))\) with respect to \(\nu\) occurs at \(\nu^* = \frac{\mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R]}{\E[R]^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R]}\).

Solution

The bound is \(f(\nu) = (\mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R])^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} (\mathbf{1} - \nu \E[R])\). Expanding: \(f(\nu) = \mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \mathbf{1} - 2 \nu \mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R] + \nu^2 \E[R]^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R]\). Taking the derivative with respect to \(\nu\) and setting it to zero gives \(-2 \mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R] + 2 \nu \E[R]^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R] = 0\). Solving for \(\nu\) yields \(\nu^* = \frac{\mathbf{1}^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R]}{\E[R]^\top \Sigma_R^{-1} \E[R]}\).

Prove that the minimum-variance SDF \(M_p = p^\top (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} x\) satisfies \(\E[M_p^2] = p^\top (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} p\).

Solution

Substitute \(M_p = p^\top (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} x\): \(\E[M_p^2] = \E[ (p^\top (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} x) (x^\top (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} p) ]\). Since \(p\) and \((\E[x x^\top])^{-1}\) are non-random, pull them outside the expectation: \(\E[M_p^2] = p^\top (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} \E[x x^\top] (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} p = p^\top (\E[x x^\top])^{-1} p\).

Prove that any asset pricing model whose SDF satisfies \(\E[M R] = \mathbf{1}\) must produce pricing errors that lie on the efficient frontier when evaluated with the Hansen-Jagannathan distance metric.

Solution

The Hansen-Jagannathan distance is defined as the \(L^2\) distance from a candidate proxy \(Y\) to the set of valid pricing kernels: \(d(Y) = \min_{M \in \mathcal{M}} \|Y - M\|\). By the projection theorem, this minimum distance equals \(\sqrt{\E[(Y - M^*)^2]} = \sqrt{e_Y^\top (\E[R R^\top])^{-1} e_Y}\) where \(e_Y = \E[Y R] - \mathbf{1}\) is the pricing error vector. When \(e_Y = 0\), the distance is identically zero.

Empirical

How does the inclusion of foreign currencies, corporate bonds, or equity options affect the Hansen-Jagannathan bound?

Solution

Adding more asset classes expands the payoff space \(\mathcal{X}\), increasing the maximum achievable Sharpe ratio on the enlarged frontier. Because the HJ bound is the envelope over all spanned portfolios, expanding the asset menu strictly raises (or maintains) the bound, making it harder for candidate SDF models to pass.

Explain why sample estimation of the Hansen-Jagannathan bound suffers from an upward finite-sample bias.

Solution

The sample bound relies on the in-sample maximum Sharpe ratio, which searches over all portfolio weights to maximize the sample mean relative to sample variance. Due to sampling noise, the estimator overfits sample-specific covariance fluctuations, systematically overestimating the population Sharpe ratio and inflating the sample HJ bound.

How does conditioning on macro instruments (e.g. term spread, dividend yield) transform unconditional HJ bounds into conditional bounds?

Solution

Conditioning information is incorporated by scaling asset returns by lagged instruments \(z_t\), creating managed portfolio payoffs \(R_{t+1} \otimes z_t\). This substantially enlarges the test asset space and incorporates dynamic trading strategies, dramatically tightening the HJ bound.

Research

Formulate the primal and dual optimization problems for the Hansen-Jagannathan non-negative SDF bound (\(M \ge 0\) a.s.) and explain why it is tighter than the basic bound.

Solution

The primal problem minimizes \(\E[M^2]\) subject to \(\E[M R] = \mathbf{1}\) and \(M \ge 0\). The dual problem is \(\max_{\lambda} \mathbf{1}^\top \lambda - \frac{1}{2} \E[(\max(0, R^\top \lambda))^2]\). Because the non-negativity constraint restricts the feasible set of kernels to positive random variables, the minimum required variance is strictly greater than or equal to the unconstrained projection.

Explain how the Hansen-Jagannathan distance \(HJ\text{-dist} = \min_{M \in \mathcal{M}} \|Y - M\|\) serves as an alternative to the Hansen \(J\)-test for comparing misspecified models.

Solution

The Hansen \(J\)-test uses the optimal weight matrix \(S_T^{-1}\), which rewards models that price volatile combinations of moments poorly. In contrast, the HJ distance uses the second-moment payoff matrix \((\E[R R^\top])^{-1}\) as a common metric across all candidate models, measuring the economic dollar mispricing of the closest admissible SDF.

Synthesize how modern non-linear SDFs (such as deep neural network SDFs or kernel regression estimators) are regularized using the Hansen-Jagannathan bound.

Solution

Modern machine learning models parameterize \(M_{t+1} = f_\theta(Z_t)\). To prevent overfitting while ensuring financial consistency, the loss function augments standard cross-entropy or mean-squared error with a penalized HJ term \(\lambda (\Var(f_\theta) - \text{HJ-Bound})^2\) or directly maximizes the Sharpe ratio spanned by \(f_\theta\).

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the fundamental insight of the Hansen-Jagannathan (1991) volatility bound?

    Options

    By projecting the SDF onto the space of asset payoffs, the variance of the projection sets a model-free lower bound on the variance of any candidate SDF.

  2. When a risk-free asset is traded, what is the connection between the Hansen-Jagannathan bound and the Sharpe ratio?

    Options

    Substituting \(\nu = 1/R_f\) into the HJ bound collapses the quadratic form directly to the maximum squared Sharpe ratio on the tangency portfolio.

  3. If the maximum Sharpe ratio in the market is 0.50 and the risk-free rate is \(R_f = 2.0\) (gross), what is the minimum value of \(\sigma(M)\) required for an SDF with \(\E[M] = 0.50\)?

    Using \(\sigma(M) \ge \E[M] \times \text{Sharpe}^*\): \(\sigma(M) \ge 0.50 \times 0.50 = 0.25\).

  4. Why is the Hansen-Jagannathan frontier referred to as a "cup" in \((\E[M], \sigma(M))\) space?

    Options

    Because \(\Var(M)\) is quadratic in \(\nu = \E[M]\), \(\sigma(M)\) is the square root of a parabola, forming a convex cup-shaped hyperbola.

  5. What distributional assumptions on returns or consumption are required for the Hansen-Jagannathan bound to hold?

    Options

    The HJ bound relies entirely on the geometry of inner product spaces (\(L^2\) projection) and holds regardless of probability distributions.

  6. How does expanding the test asset menu (e.g. adding options or corporate bonds) affect the Hansen-Jagannathan bound?

    Options

    Enlarging the payoff space expands the set of testable portfolios, increasing the maximal attainable Sharpe ratio and raising the minimum required SDF volatility.

  7. What additional economic restriction is imposed by the Hansen-Jagannathan non-negative bound?

    Options

    While the standard bound enforces only the Law of One Price (which permits \(M < 0\)), the non-negative bound enforces no-arbitrage, guaranteeing strictly positive state prices.

  8. Why is the Hansen-Jagannathan distance often preferred over the Hansen J-test when ranking misspecified models?

    Options

    The Hansen \(J\)-test uses a model-dependent weighting matrix \(S_T^{-1}\), which gives lower penalties to models with high residual variance; the HJ distance evaluates all models on the same objective scale.

  9. In empirical testing, where does the standard power utility consumption SDF typically plot relative to the Hansen-Jagannathan cup?

    Options

    Because aggregate consumption growth has very low volatility, the standard power utility SDF has volatility an order of magnitude smaller than the minimum required by asset market returns.

  10. What is the minimum-variance SDF in the asset payoff space \(M_p\) proportional to?

    Options

    By construction, \(M_p\) is a linear combination of asset returns that lies on the mean-variance efficient frontier of payoffs.
