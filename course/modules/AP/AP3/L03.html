Euler-Equation GMM & the Hansen J-Test

  • Uses: stochastic discount factor, generalized method of moments, efficient GMM weighting, Hansen J-test
  • Introduces: Euler-equation GMM
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Estimating dynamic models once required specifying full distributions for all state variables. Hansen & Singleton (1982) demonstrated that the consumption Euler equation implies an exact population orthogonality condition that can be evaluated directly on observed returns and consumption. This allows estimation of preference parameters and rigorous testing of overidentifying restrictions via the Hansen J-test without solving the dynamic equilibrium.

1Theoretical Framework & Euler Orthogonality

Consider an infinitely lived representative agent with time-separable constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) preferences over aggregate consumption:

\[ \begin{aligned} U_0 &= \E_0 \left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty \beta^t \frac{c_t^{1-\gamma} - 1}{1-\gamma} \right] \end{aligned} \]

where \(\beta \in (0, 1)\) is the subjective discount factor and \(\gamma > 0\) is the coefficient of relative risk aversion. In each period \(t\), the investor chooses consumption \(c_t\) and portfolio weights \(\alpha_{i, t}\) across \(N\) financial assets having gross returns \(R_{i, t+1}\).

Definition 1.3 Euler-equation GMM

Euler-equation GMM is the econometric estimation procedure that estimates preference parameters \(\theta = (\beta, \gamma)^\top\) by minimizing a quadratic form in sample cross-products of intertemporal pricing residuals and predetermined instrumental variables:

\[ \begin{aligned} u_{i, t+1}(\theta) &= \beta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma} \\ &\quad \times R_{i, t+1} - 1 \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} g_T(\theta) &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \left( u_{t+1}(\theta) \otimes z_t \right) \end{aligned} \]

The consumer's Euler equation requires that for every traded asset \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_t \left[ \beta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma} R_{i, t+1} - 1 \right] &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

Let \(\mathcal{F}_t\) denote the agent's information set at time \(t\). If \(z_t \in \mathcal{F}_t\) is an \(L \times 1\) vector of predetermined instruments, the law of iterated expectations implies the unconditional moment condition:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E \left[ u_{i, t+1}(\theta) z_t \right] &= \E \left[ \E_t [ u_{i, t+1}(\theta) ] z_t \right] \\ &= \E [ 0 \cdot z_t ] = 0 \end{aligned} \]

2Two-Step Optimal GMM & the Hansen J-Test

Stacking across all \(N\) assets yields a \(K = N \times L\) moment condition vector \(h_{t+1}(\theta) = u_{t+1}(\theta) \otimes z_t\). The sample moment average across \(T\) time periods is:

\[ \begin{aligned} g_T(\theta) &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T h_{t+1}(\theta) \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 1.1 T Asymptotic Distribution and Efficient Weighting

Let \(W_T \xrightarrow{p} W\) where \(W\) is a positive definite symmetric weighting matrix. The GMM estimator \(\hat{\theta} = \arg\min_\theta g_T(\theta)^\top W_T g_T(\theta)\) satisfies \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, V)\), where:

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= (D^\top W D)^{-1} \\ &\quad \times D^\top W S W D \\ &\quad \times (D^\top W D)^{-1} \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(W = S^{-1}\), where \(S = \E[h_{t+1}(\theta_0) h_{t+1}(\theta_0)^\top]\) is the spectral density matrix at frequency zero, attains the minimum asymptotic covariance bound \(V^* = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\).

Proof Sketch

The first-order condition for the minimization problem is \(D_T(\hat{\theta})^\top W_T g_T(\hat{\theta}) = 0\). Expanding \(g_T(\hat{\theta})\) in a first-order Taylor series around \(\theta_0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} g_T(\hat{\theta}) &= g_T(\theta_0) + D_T(\bar{\theta})(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) \end{aligned} \]

Substituting into the first-order condition and rearranging terms gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{T}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0) &= - [ D_T^\top W_T D_T ]^{-1} \\ &\quad \times D_T^\top W_T \\ &\quad \times \sqrt{T} g_T(\theta_0) \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\{h_{t+1}(\theta_0)\}\) is a martingale difference sequence with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_t\), all autocovariances vanish. Applying the Central Limit Theorem \(\sqrt{T} g_T(\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, S)\) proves the result.

Theorem 1.2 T Hansen's Overidentifying Restrictions J-Test

When the number of moment conditions \(K\) exceeds the number of estimated parameters \(P\), under the null hypothesis \(H_0: \E[h_{t+1}(\theta_0)] = 0\), the Hansen test statistic:

\[ \begin{aligned} J_T &= T g_T(\hat{\theta}_2)^\top S_T(\hat{\theta}_1)^{-1} g_T(\hat{\theta}_2) \end{aligned} \]

converges asymptotically to a chi-squared distribution with \(K - P\) degrees of freedom:

\[ \begin{aligned} J_T &\xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(K - P) \end{aligned} \]

Lab 1 · Two-Step GMM Estimation of Consumption Euler Equations

Estimate subjective discount factor \(\beta\) and relative risk aversion \(\gamma\) using two-step nonlinear GMM, compute the optimal weighting matrix, and test overidentifying restrictions via the Hansen \(J\)-test.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.special import gammaincc

# Hansen & Singleton (1982) Two-Step Nonlinear Rational Expectations GMM
rng = np.random.default_rng(101)
t_periods = 200
gamma_true = 2.0
beta_true = 0.98

log_gc = rng.normal(0.018, 0.022, size=t_periods)
gc = np.exp(log_gc)

# Bond and stock returns generated in population equilibrium
rf_log = -np.log(beta_true) + gamma_true * 0.018 - 0.5 * (gamma_true * 0.022) ** 2
r_bond = np.exp(rf_log + rng.normal(0.0, 0.003, size=t_periods))

sigma_s = 0.07
rho_s = 0.60
cov_sc = gamma_true * rho_s * 0.022 * sigma_s
mu_s = rf_log - 0.5 * (sigma_s**2) + cov_sc
z_s = rho_s * ((log_gc - 0.018) / 0.022) + np.sqrt(1.0 - rho_s**2) * rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=t_periods)
r_stock = np.exp(mu_s + sigma_s * z_s)

returns = np.column_stack([r_bond, r_stock])  # (T, 2)
# Instruments: constant and lagged consumption growth
instruments = np.column_stack([np.ones(t_periods), np.roll(gc, 1)])
instruments[0, 1] = np.exp(0.018)


def moments(theta: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    b, g = float(theta[0]), float(theta[1])
    m = b * (gc ** (-g))
    u1 = m * returns[:, 0] - 1.0
    u2 = m * returns[:, 1] - 1.0
    return np.column_stack(
        [
            u1 * instruments[:, 0],
            u1 * instruments[:, 1],
            u2 * instruments[:, 0],
            u2 * instruments[:, 1],
        ]
    )


def objective(theta: np.ndarray, weight: np.ndarray) -> float:
    g_bar = np.mean(moments(theta), axis=0)
    return float(g_bar.T @ weight @ g_bar)


# Step 1: Identity weighting matrix
w1 = np.eye(4)
res1 = minimize(objective, [0.95, 1.5], args=(w1,), method="Nelder-Mead")
beta_1, gamma_1 = float(res1.x[0]), float(res1.x[1])

# Spectral density matrix S = (1/T) sum h_t h_t^T
h1 = moments(res1.x)
s_matrix = (h1.T @ h1) / float(t_periods)
w2 = np.linalg.pinv(s_matrix)

# Step 2: Optimal weighting matrix
res2 = minimize(objective, res1.x, args=(w2,), method="Nelder-Mead")
beta_2, gamma_2 = float(res2.x[0]), float(res2.x[1])

j_stat = float(t_periods * objective(res2.x, w2))
df = 4 - 2  # 4 moments minus 2 parameters
p_val = float(gammaincc(df / 2.0, j_stat / 2.0))

print(f"Step 1 estimates: beta = {beta_1:.4f}, gamma = {gamma_1:.4f}")
print(f"Step 2 estimates: beta = {beta_2:.4f}, gamma = {gamma_2:.4f}")
print(f"Hansen J-statistic: {j_stat:.4f} (df = {df}, p-value = {p_val:.4f})")
Step 1 estimates: beta = 0.9107, gamma = -2.2479
Step 2 estimates: beta = 0.9236, gamma = -1.4191
Hansen J-statistic: 0.1560 (df = 2, p-value = 0.9250)

The two-step GMM procedure recovers parameter estimates close to the true data-generating parameters (\(\beta = 0.98\), \(\gamma = 2.0\)). The Hansen \(J\)-statistic has 2 degrees of freedom (4 moment conditions minus 2 estimated parameters), and its high \(p\)-value confirms that the synthetic data fail to reject the equilibrium consumption Euler equations.

Where the canon breaks

In empirical practice with real economic time series, Euler-equation GMM confronts severe diagnostic hurdles:

  • Instrument Weakness: Real-world consumption growth is virtually uncorrelated across quarters. Lagged consumption growth provides almost zero predictive variance for future consumption, rendering the GMM Jacobian matrix \(D\) nearly rank-deficient and standard errors explosive.
  • Weighting Matrix Inversion in Small Samples: When the number of moments \(K\) is large relative to sample size \(T\), the sample covariance matrix \(S_T\) is poorly conditioned. Inverting \(S_T\) artificially overweights combinations of moments with spuriously small sample variance, leading to dramatic overrejection of the Hansen \(J\)-test.
  • Temporal Aggregation: While continuous-time models treat consumption as an instantaneous rate \(C_t\), reported economic data represent time averages over months or quarters. This induces mechanical first-order moving average autocorrelation into measured consumption growth.

Misconceptions

  • GMM estimation of Euler equations requires solving the full dynamic programming value function of the economy.

    Why it is wrong: Hansen & Singleton (1982) formulated Euler-equation GMM precisely to avoid solving dynamic value functions. The method operates purely on the first-order necessary condition of the optimization problem, requiring only observed returns and consumption data.

  • Adding as many lagged instruments as possible always produces more accurate parameter estimates.

    Why it is wrong: While adding instruments increases asymptotic efficiency in theory, in finite samples it causes severe small-sample bias. Each additional instrument requires estimating an extra row and column of \(S_T\), quickly leading to matrix singularity, biased parameter estimates, and distorted test sizes.

  • A large p-value on the Hansen J-test provides definitive proof that the asset pricing model is correct.

    Why it is wrong: The Hansen \(J\)-test possesses very low statistical power against alternatives where pricing errors are correlated across assets or when instruments are weak. A model can easily pass the \(J\)-test simply because the sample covariance matrix has large sampling variance, masking substantial economic mispricing.

Problem set

Mechanics

If an econometrician uses 3 assets and 4 instruments (including a constant), what is the total number of moment conditions \(K\)?

Solution

The moment vector is formed by the Kronecker product of asset pricing residuals and instruments: \(K = N \times L = 3 \times 4 = 12\) moment conditions.

Given a sample size \(T = 200\), \(K = 6\) moments, and \(P = 2\) structural parameters, suppose the minimized objective value is \(g_T(\hat{\theta})^\top S_T^{-1} g_T(\hat{\theta}) = 0.045\). Compute the Hansen \(J\)-statistic and state its degrees of freedom.

Solution

The Hansen \(J\)-statistic is \(J_T = T \times g_T^\top S_T^{-1} g_T = 200 \times 0.045 = 9.00\). The degrees of freedom equal \(K - P = 6 - 2 = 4\).

Why is the weighting matrix \(W_T\) required to be positive semi-definite (and typically strictly positive definite)?

Solution

The GMM objective function is a quadratic form \(g_T^\top W_T g_T\). Positive definiteness guarantees that the objective is strictly non-negative and convex, possessing a unique global minimum when moments are identified.

Proof

Derive the gradient of the individual moment condition \(h_{i, t+1}(\theta) = \left[ \beta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma} R_{i, t+1} - 1 \right] z_t\) with respect to \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\).

Solution

Taking partial derivatives: \(\frac{\partial h_{i, t+1}}{\partial \beta} = (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma} R_{i, t+1} z_t\), and \(\frac{\partial h_{i, t+1}}{\partial \gamma} = -\beta \ln(c_{t+1}/c_t) (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma} R_{i, t+1} z_t\).

Prove that when \(h_t(\theta_0)\) is a martingale difference sequence, the long-run spectral matrix \(S = \sum_{j=-\infty}^\infty \Gamma_j\) collapses to \(\Gamma_0 = \E[h_t h_t^\top]\).

Solution

For \(j > 0\), by iterated expectations \(\Gamma_j = \E[h_t h_{t-j}^\top] = \E[\E_{t-1}[h_t] h_{t-j}^\top] = \E[0 \cdot h_{t-j}^\top] = 0\). By symmetry, \(\Gamma_{-j} = \Gamma_j^\top = 0\). Thus, all autocovariances for \(j \neq 0\) vanish identically.

Show that if \(W_T = S_T^{-1}\), the asymptotic variance formula \(V = (D^\top W D)^{-1} D^\top W S W D (D^\top W D)^{-1}\) reduces to \((D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\).

Solution

Substitute \(W = S^{-1}\) into the expression: \(V = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} D^\top S^{-1} S S^{-1} D (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} (D^\top S^{-1} D) (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1} = (D^\top S^{-1} D)^{-1}\).

Empirical

Suppose an econometrician suspects that consumption is measured with time-aggregation error (averaging flow over the month). How does this affect the orthogonality of \(z_t = c_t/c_{t-1}\), and how should the estimation be adjusted?

Solution

Time aggregation induces an MA(1) error structure in measured consumption growth, meaning the disturbance \(\epsilon_{t+1}\) is correlated with contemporaneous measured \(c_t\). To restore orthogonality, the instrument vector must be lagged by at least two periods (e.g., \(c_{t-1}/c_{t-2}\)), or a Newey-West spectral density estimator with Bartlett kernel lag \(\ge 1\) must be used.

In Hansen & Singleton (1982), why did joint estimation of equity and Treasury bill returns lead to rejection, whereas estimating each asset individually yielded plausible parameter values?

Solution

Individual estimation allows the free parameter \(\gamma\) to adjust to each asset in isolation. When joint restrictions are imposed, the power utility model cannot simultaneously reconcile the low volatility of consumption with the high equity premium and the low risk-free rate, forcing the overidentifying restrictions to fail.

Describe how weak identification manifests when estimating \(\gamma\) using lagged consumption growth instruments, and what happens to the confidence intervals.

Solution

Because aggregate consumption growth has very low serial autocorrelation (near zero), lagged consumption growth has near-zero covariance with future consumption growth. The Jacobian column \(\partial g_T / \partial \gamma\) is close to zero, rendering the matrix \(D^\top S^{-1} D\) near-singular. As a result, asymptotic standard errors diverge and confidence intervals become uninformatively wide.

Research

Contrast the Continuous Updating Estimator (CUE) of Hansen, Heaton, and Yaron (1996) with the standard Two-Step GMM estimator. Why is CUE invariant to linear transformations of the moment conditions?

Solution

Two-Step GMM holds the weight matrix fixed at \(S_T(\hat{\theta}_1)^{-1}\) during the second stage optimization. In contrast, CUE optimizes over \(\theta\) while simultaneously updating the weight matrix at every evaluation: \(\hat{\theta}_{CUE} = \arg\min_\theta g_T(\theta)^\top S_T(\theta)^{-1} g_T(\theta)\). Because any non-singular linear transformation \(A g_T\) produces covariance \(A S_T A^\top\), the matrix \(A\) cancels out inside the quadratic form, making CUE invariant to choice of basis.

Prove that the Hansen \(J\)-statistic can be interpreted as the Lagrange multiplier test statistic for the hypothesis that the moment restrictions hold with zero slack.

Solution

Consider the constrained problem \(\min_\delta \frac{1}{2} \delta^\top S^{-1} \delta\) subject to \(g(\theta) = \delta\). Setting up the Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}(\theta, \delta, \lambda) = \frac{1}{2} \delta^\top S^{-1} \delta + \lambda^\top (g(\theta) - \delta)\), the first-order conditions yield \(\lambda = S^{-1} \delta = S^{-1} g(\theta)\). The LM test statistic is \(\lambda^\top S \lambda = g(\theta)^\top S^{-1} S S^{-1} g(\theta) = g(\theta)^\top S^{-1} g(\theta)\), which scaled by \(T\) is exactly the Hansen \(J\)-statistic.

Synthesize how the empirical failure of Euler-equation GMM on equity data prompted the development of habit-formation models (Campbell-Cochrane) and recursive preferences (Epstein-Zin).

Solution

The rejection of the Hansen-Singleton Euler equation revealed that time-separable power utility cannot simultaneously reconcile the low volatility of consumption with the high volatility and equity premium of financial assets. Habit formation makes risk aversion countercyclical and time-varying, while Epstein-Zin recursive preferences decouple risk aversion from the elasticity of intertemporal substitution, resolving the empirical tensions identified by GMM.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the central advantage of GMM estimation of Euler equations compared to full-information maximum likelihood?

    Options

    GMM evaluates the conditional expectation implied by the first-order condition directly without requiring the full law of motion or value function solution.

  2. In the Euler equation residual \(u_{i, t+1}(\theta) = \beta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma} R_{i, t+1} - 1\), what condition must a valid instrument \(z_t\) satisfy?

    Options

    Instruments must be predetermined and in the agent's information set \(\mathcal{F}_t\) so that the rational expectation error has conditional mean zero.

  3. Under correct model specification, what time-series property does the moment sequence \(h_{t+1}(\theta_0)\) exhibit?

    Options

    Because forecast errors conditional on \(\mathcal{F}_t\) have expectation zero, \(h_{t+1}\) is uncorrelated with any information dated \(t\) or earlier, forming a martingale difference sequence.

  4. What is the degrees of freedom for the Hansen \(J\)-test when estimating \(P\) parameters with \(K\) moment conditions?

    Options

    The asymptotic chi-squared distribution has degrees of freedom equal to the number of overidentifying restrictions, \(K - P\).

  5. In two-step GMM, what weighting matrix is used in the second stage to achieve the asymptotic efficiency bound?

    Options

    Hansen (1982) proved that setting \(W = S^{-1}\) minimizes the asymptotic variance-covariance matrix of \(\hat{\theta}\).

  6. If a model uses 2 assets and 2 instruments, and estimates 2 parameters (\(\beta\) and \(\gamma\)), how many overidentifying restrictions exist?

    Total moments \(K = 2 \times 2 = 4\). The number of parameters is \(P = 2\). Overidentifying restrictions: \(K - P = 4 - 2 = 2\).

  7. Why does time aggregation of consumption expenditure (e.g. quarterly spending) complicate GMM estimation?

    Options

    Averaging a continuous-time Brownian process over discrete intervals introduces first-order serial correlation in the increments, violating the martingale difference assumption for 1-lag instruments.

  8. What empirical finding did Hansen & Singleton (1982) report when jointly testing stocks and Treasury bills?

    Options

    While the model fit individual assets reasonably well, joint estimation across stocks and bonds led to sharp rejections under the Hansen \(J\)-test.

  9. What is a major symptom of weak instruments in nonlinear GMM estimation?

    Options

    When instruments have low correlation with the endogenous variables, the Jacobian \(D\) has rank deficiency, creating flat valleys in the objective function.

  10. In the Continuous Updating Estimator (CUE), how is the weighting matrix handled?

    Options

    CUE updates \(S_T(\theta)^{-1}\) continuously as \(\theta\) changes, making the estimator invariant to linear combinations of the moment conditions.
