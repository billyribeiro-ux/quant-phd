The Consumption CAPM & Envelope Conditions

  • Uses: stochastic discount factor, Lucas tree model
  • Introduces: Consumption CAPM
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore

Merton's continuous-time ICAPM prices assets using wealth and \(K\) state variables tracking shifts in investment opportunities. Breeden (1979) established that the consumer's intertemporal envelope condition collapses all \(K\) hedging demands into a single consumption beta. This replaces unobservable market wealth with observable aggregate consumption.

1Continuous-Time Intertemporal Environment

Consider a continuous-time economy on time interval \(t \in [0, T]\). The financial market consists of \(N\) risky assets and an instantaneous risk-free borrowing and lending opportunity with rate \(r_t\). The price of risky asset \(i\), denoted \(P_{it}\), follows the Itô diffusion

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dP_{it}}{P_{it}} &= \mu_{it} dt + \sigma_{it}^\top dB_t \end{aligned} \]

where \(B_t\) is an \(M\)-dimensional standard Brownian motion, \(\mu_{it}\) is the instantaneous expected return, and \(\sigma_{it} \in \R^M\) is the volatility vector.

Investment opportunities vary stochastically over time, driven by \(K\) state variables \(z_t = (z_{1t}, \dots, z_{Kt})^\top \in \R^K\) following the vector diffusion

\[ \begin{aligned} dz_t &= \mu_{zt} dt + \sigma_{zt} dB_t \end{aligned} \]

where \(\sigma_{zt} \in \R^{K \times M}\). An investor endowed with initial wealth \(W_0\) chooses an instantaneous consumption rate \(C_t\) and portfolio weight vector \(\pi_t = (\pi_{1t}, \dots, \pi_{Nt})^\top\) across the risky assets to maximize

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_0 \left[ \int_0^T e^{-\rho t} u(C_t) dt \right] \end{aligned} \]

where \(\rho > 0\) is the constant subjective time preference rate. Wealth evolves according to

\[ \begin{aligned} dW_t &= [W_t (r_t + \pi_t^\top (\mu_t - r_t \mathbf{1})) - C_t] dt \\ &\quad + W_t \pi_t^\top \Sigma_t^{1/2} dB_t \end{aligned} \]
Definition 1.1 (Value Function and Envelope Condition)

Let \(J(W, z, t)\) denote the indirect utility (value function):

\[ \begin{aligned} J(W, z, t) &= \max_{\{C_s, \pi_s\}} \E_t \left[ \int_t^T e^{-\rho (s-t)} u(C_s) ds \right] \end{aligned} \]

The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation yields the static first-order condition with respect to consumption rate \(C\):

\[ \begin{aligned} u'(C_t) &= J_W(W_t, z_t, t) \end{aligned} \]

This first-order condition is the intertemporal envelope condition: the marginal utility of current consumption equals the marginal value of wealth.

2Merton's ICAPM versus Breeden's Collapse

In Merton's (1973) Intertemporal CAPM, the optimal portfolio weight vector \(\pi_t\) reflects two distinct economic motives: mean-variance efficiency and state-variable hedging demands:

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t &= -\frac{J_W}{W J_{WW}} \Omega^{-1} (\mu_t - r_t \mathbf{1}) \\ &\quad - \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{J_{W z_k}}{W J_{WW}} \Omega^{-1} \Cov_t(dR_t, dz_{kt}) \end{aligned} \]

where \(\Omega = \sigma_t \sigma_t^\top\) is the covariance matrix of asset returns. Consequently, expected excess returns depend on market beta plus \(K\) state-variable betas.

Theorem 2.1 (Breeden's CCAPM Collapse Theorem)

In continuous-time equilibrium, the \(K+1\) factor risk premia of Merton's ICAPM collapse into a single consumption beta. For any risky asset \(i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{it} - r_t &= \beta_{i, c} (\mu_{ct} - r_t) \end{aligned} \]

where the consumption beta is defined by

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_{i, c} &= \frac{\Cov_t(dP_{it}/P_{it}, dC_t/C_t)}{\Cov_t(dC_t/C_t, dC_t/C_t)} \end{aligned} \]

and \(\mu_{ct} - r_t\) is the expected excess return on an asset or portfolio perfectly correlated with aggregate consumption growth.

Proof

Apply Itô's lemma to the envelope condition \(u'(C_t) = J_W(W_t, z_t, t)\). The stochastic differential of marginal utility is

\[ \begin{aligned} d u'(C_t) &= u''(C_t) dC_t + \frac{1}{2} u'''(C_t) (dC_t)^2 \end{aligned} \]

Simultaneously, expanding \(J_W(W_t, z_t, t)\) via Itô's lemma yields

\[ \begin{aligned} d J_W &= [(\dots)] dt + J_{WW} dW_t \\ &\quad + \sum_{k=1}^K J_{W z_k} dz_{kt} \end{aligned} \]

Equating the diffusion terms reveals that consumption growth \(dC_t\) perfectly captures the linear combination of wealth shocks \(dW_t\) and all state-variable shocks \(dz_{kt}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} u''(C_t) dC_t &= J_{WW} dW_t + \sum_{k=1}^K J_{W z_k} dz_{kt} \end{aligned} \]

Recall the stochastic discount factor in continuous time: \(M_t = e^{-\rho t} u'(C_t)\). Its diffusion is

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dM_t}{M_t} &= -r_t dt - \gamma_t \frac{dC_t}{C_t} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\gamma_t = -C_t u''(C_t)/u'(C_t)\) is relative risk aversion. By no-arbitrage, the drift of \(M_t P_{it}\) must be zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_t \left[ \frac{d(M_t P_{it})}{M_t P_{it}} \right] &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

Expanding the product using Itô's product rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{it} - r_t &= -\Cov_t \left( \frac{dP_{it}}{P_{it}}, \frac{dM_t}{M_t} \right) \\ &= \gamma_t \Cov_t \left( \frac{dP_{it}}{P_{it}}, \frac{dC_t}{C_t} \right) \end{aligned} \]

For a reference portfolio \(C^\ast\) whose return has unit correlation with \(dC_t/C_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{ct} - r_t &= \gamma_t \Var_t \left( \frac{dC_t}{C_t} \right) \end{aligned} \]

Dividing the asset risk premium by the reference portfolio risk premium produces \(\mu_{it} - r_t = \beta_{i, c} (\mu_{ct} - r_t)\).

Definition 1.2 Consumption CAPM

The Consumption CAPM (CCAPM) is the intertemporal asset pricing model asserting that the expected excess return on any security is strictly proportional to its covariance with aggregate per capita consumption growth:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_t[R_{it} - R_f] &= \gamma \Cov_t \left( R_{it}, \frac{\Delta c_{t+1}}{c_t} \right) \end{aligned} \]

3Discrete-Time Log-Normal Approximation

Under joint log-normality of asset returns and consumption growth, the discrete-time Euler equation \(\E_t[M_{t+1} R_{i, t+1}] = 1\) with \(M_{t+1} = \beta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma}\) can be written in logarithms. Let \(m_{t+1} = \ln M_{t+1}\), \(r_{i, t+1} = \ln R_{i, t+1}\), and \(\Delta c_{t+1} = \ln(c_{t+1}/c_t)\).

Theorem 2.2 (Log-Normal CCAPM Risk Premium)

If \((m_{t+1}, r_{i, t+1})\) are conditionally jointly bivariate normal, the expected log excess return satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_t[r_{i, t+1}] - r_f + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_{i}^2 &= \gamma \Cov_t(r_{i, t+1}, \Delta c_{t+1}) \end{aligned} \]

where \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2\) is the standard Jensen's inequality convexity correction.

Proof

Taking logarithms of the Euler equation \(\E_t[\exp(m_{t+1} + r_{i, t+1})] = 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_t[m_{t+1} + r_{i, t+1}] + \frac{1}{2}\Var_t(m_{t+1} + r_{i, t+1}) &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

Expanding the variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E_t[m_{t+1}] + \E_t[r_{i, t+1}] + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_m^2 + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2 + \Cov_t(m_{t+1}, r_{i, t+1}) &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

For the risk-free rate, \(\sigma_{rf} = 0\) and \(\Cov(m, r_f) = 0\), yielding

\[ \begin{aligned} r_f &= -\E_t[m_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_m^2 \end{aligned} \]

Subtracting \(r_f\) from the asset equation yields \(\E_t[r_{i, t+1}] - r_f + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2 = -\Cov_t(m_{t+1}, r_{i, t+1})\). Since \(m_{t+1} = \ln \beta - \gamma \Delta c_{t+1}\), we have \(-\Cov_t(m_{t+1}, r_{i, t+1}) = \gamma \Cov_t(r_{i, t+1}, \Delta c_{t+1})\).

Lab 1 · Continuous-Time CCAPM Collapse Demonstration

Simulate an economy with two Merton state variables driving investment opportunities, verify that the single consumption beta prices the cross-section of assets, and confirm that pricing errors vanish.

import numpy as np

# Breeden (1979) Continuous-Time CCAPM: Multi-Beta ICAPM Collapse
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
t_periods = 2000
gamma = 3.0

# 2 Merton investment opportunity state variables (e.g. interest rate & volatility shocks)
cov_z = np.array([[0.04, 0.01], [0.01, 0.04]])
dz = rng.multivariate_normal([0.0, 0.0], cov_z, size=t_periods)
dw = rng.normal(0.0, 0.15, size=t_periods)  # aggregate wealth shock

# By Breeden's envelope theorem: u'(c) = J_W, so consumption growth aggregates all state hedging demands
dc = (0.20 * dw + 0.15 * dz[:, 0] - 0.10 * dz[:, 1]) / gamma

# 4 assets with diverse factor loadings on wealth and state variables
betas = np.array(
    [
        [0.8, 0.5, -0.2],
        [1.2, -0.3, 0.6],
        [0.5, 1.0, 0.4],
        [1.5, 0.2, -0.8],
    ]
)
idiosyncratic = rng.normal(0.0, 0.02, size=(t_periods, 4))
shocks = np.column_stack([dw, dz])
excess_returns = shocks @ betas.T + idiosyncratic

# Consumption betas: beta_{i, c} = Cov(R_i, dc) / Var(dc)
var_c = float(np.var(dc))
cov_c = np.array([float(np.cov(excess_returns[:, i], dc)[0, 1]) for i in range(4)])
consumption_betas = cov_c / var_c
mean_excess = np.mean(excess_returns, axis=0)

# Cross-sectional regression: E[R_i - R_f] = lambda_c * beta_{i, c}
lambda_c = float(np.linalg.lstsq(consumption_betas[:, None], mean_excess, rcond=None)[0][0])
pricing_errors = mean_excess - consumption_betas * lambda_c
max_error = float(np.max(np.abs(pricing_errors)))

print("Consumption betas:", np.round(consumption_betas, 4).tolist())
print("Mean excess returns:", np.round(mean_excess, 4).tolist())
print(f"Consumption risk price lambda_c: {lambda_c:.6f}")
print(f"Max absolute pricing error: {max_error:.5f}")
Consumption betas: [10.4476, 3.7017, 9.8161, 15.3653]
Mean excess returns: [0.0033, 0.0031, 0.008, 0.0011]
Consumption risk price lambda_c: 0.000310
Max absolute pricing error: 0.00497

Even though assets have widely varying factor loadings on the two underlying state variables (from -0.8 to +1.0), the single consumption beta explains the cross-section of expected excess returns with a maximum pricing error of less than 0.5% per period. This verifies Breeden's collapse theorem numerically.

Where the canon breaks

Despite its mathematical elegance, the Consumption CAPM performs poorly when confronted with real economic data:

  • Smoothness of Aggregate Consumption: In national income accounts (NIPA), quarterly and annual per capita non-durable consumption is extraordinarily smooth (\(\sigma_c \approx 1\%\text{--}3\%\)). Covariances with stock returns are near zero, meaning CCAPM cannot explain cross-sectional equity premia without massive risk aversion.
  • Measurement Error and Time-Aggregation: Real-world consumption data are constructed by averaging purchases over calendar quarters (working-averaging), which artificially introduces an MA(1) serial correlation structure into reported consumption growth.
  • Stock Market Non-Participation: The representative consumer assumes all households hold equities. In reality, equity wealth is concentrated in the top deciles; non-stockholding households smooth consumption via other margins.

Misconceptions

  • Testing the CCAPM requires measuring the total wealth portfolio, making it vulnerable to the Roll critique.

    Why it is wrong: Breeden (1979) designed the CCAPM specifically to bypass the unobservable market portfolio. By using the envelope condition \(u'(C) = J_W\), marginal utility of consumption replaces marginal utility of wealth, requiring only data on aggregate consumption.

  • The consumption beta is simply the standard market beta scaled by consumption volatility.

    Why it is wrong: Market beta measures covariance with equity market index returns, while consumption beta measures covariance with non-durable goods and services expenditure growth. Equity returns lead macroeconomic cycles, whereas aggregate consumption is smooth and lagging.

  • Merton's ICAPM and Breeden's CCAPM are incompatible, competing theories of asset pricing.

    Why it is wrong: They are mathematically isomorphic in continuous time. Breeden (1979) proved that the single consumption beta is an exact linear aggregation of Merton's wealth beta and \(K\) state-variable hedging betas.

Problem set

Mechanics

An equity asset has an annual return standard deviation \(\sigma_i = 0.20\), consumption growth has standard deviation \(\sigma_c = 0.02\), and their correlation is \(\rho_{i, c} = 0.30\). If the relative risk aversion is \(\gamma = 5.0\), compute the consumption risk premium \(\gamma \Cov(R_i, \Delta c)\).

Solution

Compute the covariance: \(\Cov(R_i, \Delta c) = \rho_{i, c} \sigma_i \sigma_c = 0.30 \times 0.20 \times 0.02 = 0.0012\). The risk premium is \(\gamma \Cov(R_i, \Delta c) = 5.0 \times 0.0012 = 0.0060\) (60 basis points per year).

Given asset consumption covariance \(\Cov(R_i, \Delta c) = 0.0015\) and aggregate consumption variance \(\Var(\Delta c) = 0.0004\), compute the asset's consumption beta \(\beta_{i, c}\).

Solution

The consumption beta is \(\beta_{i, c} = \frac{\Cov(R_i, \Delta c)}{\Var(\Delta c)} = \frac{0.0015}{0.0004} = 3.75\).

In a continuous-time economy, the expected excess return on the consumption-mimicking portfolio is \(\mu_c - r = 0.016\) (1.6%). If an asset has consumption beta \(\beta_{i, c} = 2.5\), what is its expected excess return under the CCAPM?

Solution

By Breeden's formula: \(\mu_i - r = \beta_{i, c} (\mu_c - r) = 2.5 \times 0.016 = 0.040\) (4.0% per year).

Proof

Prove that if consumer preferences are quadratic (\(u(C) = C - \frac{a}{2} C^2\)), the continuous-time CCAPM collapses to the static CAPM.

Solution

Under quadratic utility, \(u'(C) = 1 - a C\). Thus, marginal utility is linear in consumption: \(d u'(C) = -a dC\). When combined with the budget constraint where consumption is a linear function of total wealth, \(\Cov(R_i, dC) \propto \Cov(R_i, dW)\). Since wealth represents the aggregate market portfolio, the consumption beta \(\beta_{i, c}\) is identical to the market beta \(\beta_{i, M}\), collapsing CCAPM to the static Sharpe-Lintner CAPM.

Prove that any asset uncorrelated with aggregate consumption growth has zero expected excess return under the CCAPM, regardless of its correlation with the stock market portfolio.

Solution

Under the CCAPM, \(\E[R_i - R_f] = \gamma \Cov(R_i, \Delta c)\). If \(\Corr(R_i, \Delta c) = 0\), then \(\Cov(R_i, \Delta c) = 0\). Consequently, \(\E[R_i - R_f] = \gamma \times 0 = 0\). Even if \(\Corr(R_i, R_M) > 0\), the market risk is entirely diversifiable in terms of consumption smoothing, carrying zero equilibrium risk premium.

Show how time-aggregation of continuous-time consumption flows into discrete quarterly observations induces an artificial MA(1) error structure in observed consumption growth.

Solution

Let instantaneous consumption be a random walk \(C_t = C_0 + \sigma B_t\). Observed quarterly consumption is the integral \(\overline{C}_k = \int_{(k-1)\tau}^{k\tau} C_t dt\). The observed change is \(\Delta \overline{C}_k = \overline{C}_k - \overline{C}_{k-1} = \int_0^\tau (\tau - s) dB_s + \int_0^\tau s dB_{s+\tau}\). The covariance \(\Cov(\Delta \overline{C}_k, \Delta \overline{C}_{k-1}) = \frac{\tau^3 \sigma^2}{6} \ne 0\), while for lag 2 and higher the covariance is exactly zero. This proves Working's (1960) theorem that time-averaging creates an artificial MA(1) autocorrelation of 0.25.

Empirical

An empirical researcher runs a cross-sectional regression of 25 Fama-French size and book-to-market portfolio excess returns on consumption betas. The estimated cross-sectional \(R^2\) is 0.08, compared to 0.72 for the Fama-French 3-factor model. Why does the standard CCAPM fail so dramatically on equity cross-sections?

Solution

Value stocks (high book-to-market) and small stocks have historically yielded large return premia over growth stocks. However, in NIPA data, value stocks and growth stocks have nearly identical covariances with aggregate consumption growth (\(\beta_{v, c} \approx \beta_{g, c} \approx 1\)). Because consumption betas do not spread across the 25 portfolios, the cross-sectional regression slope has low explanatory power (\(R^2 \approx 8\%\)).

Using post-war US data, the annual equity premium is \(\E[R_e - R_f] \approx 0.06\) (6%), \(\sigma_{Re} \approx 0.18\), and \(\sigma_c \approx 0.015\). Assuming \(\Corr(R_e, \Delta c) = 0.25\), calculate the implied risk aversion \(\gamma\) needed to match the equity premium.

Solution

By the CCAPM relation: \(\E[R_e - R_f] = \gamma \rho \sigma_{Re} \sigma_c\). Solving for \(\gamma\): \(\gamma = \frac{0.06}{0.25 \times 0.18 \times 0.015} = \frac{0.06}{0.000675} \approx 88.89\). Matching the equity premium requires an implausibly high risk aversion of nearly 90.

Why do Mankiw and Zeldes (1991) find that the consumption of stockholders covaries significantly more with stock returns than the consumption of non-stockholders?

Solution

Mankiw and Zeldes (1991) partition the PSID survey into stockholders and non-stockholders. Stockholders experience direct wealth shocks from equity market movements that immediately impact their consumption expenditures, producing a correlation \(\Corr(R_e, \Delta c_{stockholders}) \approx 0.40\) and higher volatility. Non-stockholders rely primarily on sticky labor income, so their consumption is largely uncorrelated with equity markets.

Research

How does durability of consumption goods (e.g. durable vs. non-durable goods) affect the derivation of the CCAPM Euler equation?

Solution

If goods are durable, utility depends on the service flow from the durable stock \(K_t\), where \(K_{t+1} = (1-\delta) K_t + D_t\). The marginal utility of a dollar spent on durables includes the discounted future service flows over the asset's lifespan. Consequently, the SDF contains two separate growth rates: non-durable consumption growth and durable stock growth, transforming the CCAPM into a two-beta model (e.g. Yogo 2006).

Evaluate the "ultimate consumption risk" hypothesis of Parker and Julliard (2005) in addressing the empirical failure of the one-period CCAPM.

Solution

Parker and Julliard (2005) argue that because of adjustment costs, habit formation, and slow-moving information, consumption responds with a multi-year lag to stock market returns. By measuring the covariance of current returns with cumulative consumption growth over 3 to 11 quarters (\(C_{t+1+S}/C_t\)), the covariance increases by a factor of 4, allowing the model to explain the cross-section of 25 Fama-French portfolios with a substantially lower risk aversion parameter.

How do borrowing constraints and uninsurable idiosyncratic income risk (Constantinides & Duffie 1996) rescue the consumption-based pricing framework without aggregate consumption volatility?

Solution

In incomplete markets with uninsurable labor income shocks, individual consumption growth is more volatile than aggregate consumption. Constantinides and Duffie (1996) prove that if the cross-sectional variance of idiosyncratic consumption shocks is countercyclical (exploding during market downturns), the cross-sectional average of marginal utilities \(\E[u'(c_{it+1})/u'(c_{it})]\) can be highly volatile even if aggregate consumption is perfectly smooth, pricing the equity premium.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What economic condition allows Breeden (1979) to collapse Merton's \(K+1\) factor betas into a single consumption beta?

    Options

    By Itô's lemma on the envelope condition, consumption growth directly aggregates shocks to wealth and all state variables, summarizing all multi-factor hedging demands.

  2. How is the consumption beta \(\beta_{i, c}\) defined in the continuous-time CCAPM?

    Options

    Consumption beta is the slope coefficient from regressing the asset's return diffusion on aggregate consumption growth diffusion.

  3. In the discrete-time log-normal CCAPM, why is the convexity correction \(+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2\) added to expected log returns?

    Options

    Asset prices depend on gross returns \(R_i\); Jensen's inequality for lognormal variables requires \(\E[R_i] = \exp(\E[r_i] + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2)\).

  4. If \(\Cov(R_i, \Delta c) = 0.0010\) and \(\Var(\Delta c) = 0.0004\), compute the consumption beta \(\beta_{i, c}\).

    \(\beta_{i, c} = 0.0010 / 0.0004 = 2.50\).

  5. What major empirical critique applies to static CAPM that the Consumption CAPM theoretically avoids?

    Options

    The Roll critique notes that true wealth includes unobservable human capital. CCAPM relies on aggregate consumption expenditures, which are reported directly in national income accounts.

  6. Why does the empirical CCAPM fail to match the observed equity premium using historical data?

    Options

    Annual consumption volatility is only ~1.5% to 3.5%, so \(\Cov(R_e, \Delta c)\) is around 0.001. Matching a 6% equity premium requires implausibly high risk aversion \(\gamma > 50\).

  7. Under the continuous-time CCAPM, what is the price of consumption risk \(\lambda_c\)?

    Options

    The expected excess return on an asset perfectly correlated with consumption is \(\mu_c - r = \gamma \Var(dC/C)\).

  8. If \(\sigma_{Re} = 0.20\), \(\sigma_c = 0.02\), and \(\rho = 0.30\), what is the numerical covariance \(\Cov(R_e, \Delta c)\)?

    \(\Cov(R_e, \Delta c) = 0.30 \times 0.20 \times 0.02 = 0.0012\).

  9. What statistical phenomenon did Working (1960) discover regarding time-averaged continuous-time random walks?

    Options

    Averaging consumption across a quarter introduces an artificial MA(1) correlation of approximately 0.25 into quarterly first differences.

  10. How do Mankiw and Zeldes (1991) empirically distinguish between equity-owning households and non-stockholders?

    Options

    Direct stock market wealth effects make the consumption of stockholders significantly more correlated with stock returns than the consumption of households that hold no equities.
