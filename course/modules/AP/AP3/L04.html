The Equity-Premium Puzzle

  • Uses: stochastic discount factor, Lucas tree model, consumption CAPM

Over the century spanning 1889 to 1978, US equity returns exceeded short-term Treasury debt by more than six percentage points annually. Mehra & Prescott (1985) proved that a standard representative-agent exchange economy with power utility cannot generate an equity premium larger than 0.35% when parameterized with plausible degrees of risk aversion. This quantitative failure sparked four decades of modern asset pricing research.

1The Empirical Facts and Quantitative Discrepancy

In their seminal contribution, Mehra & Prescott (1985) analyzed annual US time series from 1889 to 1978. Over this ninety-year period, the empirical moments were:

  • Average real return on equity (S&P 500): \(\E[R^e] = 6.98\%\) per annum.
  • Average real return on risk-free debt (short-term bills): \(\E[R^f] = 0.80\%\) per annum.
  • Annual equity risk premium: \(\E[R^e - R^f] = 6.18\%\).
  • Annual standard deviation of equity returns: \(\sigma(R^e) = 16.54\%\).
  • Mean real per capita consumption growth: \(\mu_c = 1.83\%\).
  • Annual standard deviation of consumption growth: \(\sigma_c = 3.57\%\).
  • First-order serial correlation of consumption growth: \(\rho_c = -0.14\).
Definition 1.4 Equity-premium puzzle

The equity-premium puzzle is the quantitative failure of the standard representative-agent consumption-based asset pricing model under power utility to generate the observed historical spread between equity and debt returns without resorting to economically implausible levels of relative risk aversion (\(\gamma > 30\)).

2Analytical Derivation of the Premium Bound

The fundamental Euler equation holds for both equity and the risk-free asset:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E [ M_{t+1} R^e_{t+1} ] &= 1 \\ \E [ M_{t+1} R^f_{t+1} ] &= 1 \end{aligned} \]

Subtracting the two equations yields the exact pricing condition for excess return \(R^e_{t+1} - R^f_{t+1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \E [ M_{t+1} (R^e_{t+1} - R^f_{t+1}) ] &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

Expanding the expectation using the definition of covariance \(\E[X Y] = \E[X]\E[Y] + \Cov(X, Y)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[M_{t+1}] \E[R^e_{t+1} - R^f_{t+1}] &= - \Cov(M_{t+1}, R^e_{t+1}) \end{aligned} \]

Since \(R^f_{t+1} = 1 / \E[M_{t+1}]\), dividing by \(\E[M_{t+1}]\) yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E[R^e_{t+1} - R^f_{t+1}] &= - R^f_{t+1} \Cov(M_{t+1}, R^e_{t+1}) \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 1.1 T The Sharpe Ratio and Risk Aversion Upper Bound

In any arbitrage-free economy with stochastic discount factor \(M\), the maximum Sharpe ratio attainable by any asset or portfolio is bounded by the coefficient of variation of the SDF:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\E[R^e] - R^f}{\sigma(R^e)} &\le \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} \end{aligned} \]

Under CRRA preferences \(u(c) = \frac{c^{1-\gamma}-1}{1-\gamma}\) with lognormally distributed consumption growth, this implies the approximate bound:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\E[R^e] - R^f}{\sigma(R^e)} &\le \gamma \sigma_c \end{aligned} \]
Proof

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, \(|\Cov(M, R^e)| \le \sigma(M) \sigma(R^e)\). Substituting this into the excess return formula:

\[ \begin{aligned} |\E[R^e - R^f]| &= R^f |\Cov(M, R^e)| \\ &\le R^f \sigma(M) \sigma(R^e) \\ &= \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} \sigma(R^e) \end{aligned} \]

Dividing by \(\sigma(R^e)\) gives the Sharpe ratio bound. Under lognormality, \(M = \beta \exp(-\gamma \Delta \ln c)\). The variance of \(M\) is \(\Var(M) = \E[M]^2 (\exp(\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2) - 1)\). Using the first-order approximation \(\exp(x) - 1 \approx x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]} &= \sqrt{ \exp(\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2) - 1 } \\ &\approx \gamma \sigma_c \end{aligned} \]

Since the correlation \(\rho(M, R^e) \ge -1\), the historical Sharpe ratio must be less than or equal to \(\gamma \sigma_c\).

Now evaluate this bound with the empirical numbers of Mehra & Prescott:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Historical Sharpe ratio} &= \frac{0.0618}{0.1654} \approx 0.3736 \end{aligned} \]

With annual consumption growth volatility \(\sigma_c = 0.0357\), the inequality requires:

\[ \begin{aligned} 0.3736 &\le \gamma \times 0.0357 \\ \implies \gamma &\ge \frac{0.3736}{0.0357} \\ &\approx 10.46 \end{aligned} \]

If correlation \(\rho(c, R^e) \approx 0.20\) is accounted for rather than assuming perfect negative correlation \(-1\), the required risk aversion becomes:

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma &= \frac{\text{Sharpe}}{ \rho(c, R^e) \sigma_c } \\ &\approx \frac{0.3736}{0.20 \times 0.0357} \\ &\approx 52.3 \end{aligned} \]

Microeconomic evidence from household choice surveys, experimental games, and labor supply decisions universally suggests that \(\gamma \in [1, 5]\), with \(\gamma > 10\) viewed as implausibly risk-averse.

Lab 1 · Mehra & Prescott (1985) Markov Chain Economy

Calibrate a 2-state Markov growth economy matching US historical consumption moments, solve for equilibrium equity and bond returns across a grid of \(\beta\) and \(\gamma \le 10\), and demonstrate that the maximum model equity premium falls far short of 6.18%.

import numpy as np

# Mehra & Prescott (1985) Calibration of the Equity Premium Puzzle
mu_c = 0.0183
sigma_c = 0.0357
rho_c = -0.14

delta = sigma_c
phi = (1.0 + rho_c) / 2.0  # 0.43

lambda_states = np.array([1.0 + mu_c + delta, 1.0 + mu_c - delta])
pi_matrix = np.array([[phi, 1.0 - phi], [1.0 - phi, phi]])
stat_dist = np.array([0.5, 0.5])


def solve_economy(beta: float, gamma: float) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    a_matrix = beta * pi_matrix * (lambda_states ** (1.0 - gamma))
    if np.max(np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(a_matrix))) >= 1.0:
        return np.nan, np.nan, np.nan
    w = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(2) - a_matrix, a_matrix @ np.ones(2))
    if np.any(w <= 0.0):
        return np.nan, np.nan, np.nan

    # Expected equity return: E[R^e | i] = sum_j Pi_ij * (w_j + 1) * lambda_j / w_i
    exp_re_states = np.array([float(np.sum(pi_matrix[i] * (w + 1.0) * lambda_states) / w[i]) for i in range(2)])
    exp_re = float(np.sum(stat_dist * exp_re_states))

    # Risk-free bond price: q_i = beta * sum_j Pi_ij * lambda_j^(-gamma)
    q = beta * (pi_matrix @ (lambda_states ** (-gamma)))
    rf_states = 1.0 / q
    exp_rf = float(np.sum(stat_dist * rf_states))

    return exp_re - 1.0, exp_rf - 1.0, exp_re - exp_rf


gammas = [1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0]
betas = [0.95, 0.98, 0.99]
max_premium = 0.0

print(f"{'gamma':>5} | {'beta':>5} | {'E[Re]-1':>8} | {'Rf-1':>7} | {'Premium':>8}")
print("-" * 43)
for g in gammas:
    for b in betas:
        re, rf, prem = solve_economy(b, g)
        if not np.isnan(prem):
            if prem > max_premium:
                max_premium = prem
            print(f"{g:5.1f} | {b:5.2f} | {re * 100:7.2f}% | {rf * 100:6.2f}% | {prem * 100:7.3f}%")

print("-" * 43)
print(f"Max model premium (gamma <= 10): {max_premium * 100:.3f}%")
print("Historical US equity premium (1889-1978): 6.180%")

Even at the upper bound of plausible risk aversion (\(\gamma = 10\)), the calibrated Markov chain economy produces an equity premium of at most 2.71% per year—less than half of the historical 6.18%. For standard parameters (\(\gamma = 2.0\)), the model premium is under 0.30%, illustrating the quantitative magnitude of the puzzle.

Where the canon breaks

The failure of the Mehra-Prescott benchmark model highlights fundamental limitations of the neoclassical representative-agent paradigm:

  • Excess Smoothness of Aggregate Consumption: In macroeconomic national accounts, aggregate consumption is extraordinarily smooth. Stocks are volatile, but because consumption is smooth, stocks do not covary strongly with consumption, making them look remarkably safe to a standard consumer.
  • Equating Risk Aversion to Inverse EIS: Under CRRA power utility, the relative risk aversion coefficient \(\gamma\) is mathematically forced to be the reciprocal of the elasticity of intertemporal substitution (\(\psi = 1/\gamma\)). Raising \(\gamma\) to match the equity premium inadvertently crushes \(\psi\), generating the risk-free rate puzzle.
  • Frictionless Exchange: The model assumes complete markets, zero transaction costs, frictionless borrowing, and zero margin constraints. In reality, market frictions prevent marginal investors from fully smoothing consumption.

Misconceptions

  • The equity premium puzzle is that equities have higher expected returns than risk-free bonds.

    Why it is wrong: Economic theory naturally predicts that risky assets must offer a risk premium. The puzzle is strictly quantitative: the *magnitude* of the historical premium (over 6%) is sixty times larger than what standard consumption covariance can justify under plausible risk aversion.

  • Simply assuming a very high risk aversion like \(\gamma = 50\) cleanly solves the puzzle.

    Why it is wrong: Setting \(\gamma = 50\) creates a catastrophic secondary failure: under power utility, it implies an elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(\psi = 0.02\), predicting counterfactually high risk-free interest rates in excess of 20% per year (the risk-free rate puzzle).

  • The equity premium puzzle is unique to the United States post-WWII period.

    Why it is wrong: Subsequent international studies across the UK, Germany, France, Japan, and other developed economies have replicated the puzzle across both pre-war and post-war samples. In almost all developed capital markets, equities deliver a Sharpe ratio that far exceeds the volatility of aggregate consumption growth.

Problem set

Mechanics

Given \(\E[R^e] = 0.08\), \(R^f = 0.02\), and \(\sigma(R^e) = 0.20\), compute the empirical Sharpe ratio of equity.

Solution

The Sharpe ratio is defined as \(S = \frac{\E[R^e] - R^f}{\sigma(R^e)} = \frac{0.08 - 0.02}{0.20} = \frac{0.06}{0.20} = 0.30\).

If annual consumption growth volatility is \(\sigma_c = 0.02\) and the correlation between equity returns and consumption growth is \(\rho = 0.25\), compute the required coefficient of relative risk aversion \(\gamma\) to match a Sharpe ratio of 0.35.

Solution

Using the relation \(S \approx \gamma \rho \sigma_c\), we solve for \(\gamma\): \(\gamma = \frac{S}{\rho \sigma_c} = \frac{0.35}{0.25 \times 0.02} = \frac{0.35}{0.005} = 70.0\).

In a 2-state symmetric Markov chain with states \(\lambda_1 = 1.05\) and \(\lambda_2 = 0.97\), what is the mean and variance of consumption growth if the transition probability is \(\phi = 0.50\)?

Solution

With \(\phi = 0.50\), states are i.i.d. with equal stationary probability 0.5. The mean growth is \(\mu = 0.5(1.05) + 0.5(0.97) = 1.01\). The variance is \(\sigma^2 = 0.5(1.05 - 1.01)^2 + 0.5(0.97 - 1.01)^2 = 0.5(0.04^2 + (-0.04)^2) = 0.0016\), giving standard deviation \(\sigma = 0.04\) (4.0%).

Proof

Prove that if \(\ln M\) and \(\ln R^e\) are bivariate normal, the exact equity premium equation in discrete time is \(\ln \E[R^e] - \ln R^f = -\Cov(\ln M, \ln R^e)\).

Solution

By lognormality, \(\E[M R^e] = \exp(\E[\ln M] + \E[\ln R^e] + \frac{1}{2}\Var(\ln M) + \frac{1}{2}\Var(\ln R^e) + \Cov(\ln M, \ln R^e)) = 1\). Also, \(R^f = 1/\E[M] = \exp(-\E[\ln M] - \frac{1}{2}\Var(\ln M))\), and \(\E[R^e] = \exp(\E[\ln R^e] + \frac{1}{2}\Var(\ln R^e))\). Multiplying \(\E[R^e]\) by \(\E[M]\) yields \(\E[R^e] \E[M] = \exp(-\Cov(\ln M, \ln R^e))\). Dividing by \(\E[M] = 1/R^f\) yields \(\E[R^e]/R^f = \exp(-\Cov(\ln M, \ln R^e))\), so taking logs gives \(\ln \E[R^e] - \ln R^f = -\Cov(\ln M, \ln R^e)\).

Prove that under CRRA preferences, the equity risk premium with lognormal consumption and dividend growth satisfies \(\ln \E[R^e] - \ln R^f = \gamma \Cov(\ln(c_{t+1}/c_t), \ln R^e_{t+1})\).

Solution

Under CRRA preferences, \(M_{t+1} = \beta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma}\), so \(\ln M_{t+1} = \ln \beta - \gamma \ln(c_{t+1}/c_t)\). Applying the covariance property \(\Cov(a + bX, Y) = b \Cov(X, Y)\), we have \(-\Cov(\ln M_{t+1}, \ln R^e_{t+1}) = -\Cov(\ln \beta - \gamma \ln(c_{t+1}/c_t), \ln R^e_{t+1}) = \gamma \Cov(\ln(c_{t+1}/c_t), \ln R^e_{t+1})\). Substituting into the result of problem P4.4 completes the proof.

Show that the maximum Sharpe ratio bound \(\frac{\E[R^e] - R^f}{\sigma(R^e)} \le \frac{\sigma(M)}{\E[M]}\) is attained if and only if \(R^e\) is a perfect negative linear function of \(M\).

Solution

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, \(|\Cov(M, R^e)| \le \sigma(M) \sigma(R^e)\) with equality if and only if \(R^e - \E[R^e] = k(M - \E[M])\) almost surely for some constant \(k\). To have positive excess returns \(\E[R^e - R^f] > 0\), the covariance \(\Cov(M, R^e)\) must be negative, requiring \(k < 0\). Thus the bound holds with strict equality if and only if \(R^e\) is an exact negative affine transformation of the stochastic discount factor \(M\).

Empirical

Explain how survivorship bias in US equity indices (Brown, Goetzmann, & Ross 1995) could partially account for the observed equity premium.

Solution

The US equity market was the most successful capital market of the 20th century. Markets in Russia, Austria, China, and Germany experienced complete closures, hyperinflation, or expropriation. Evaluating models on an ex-post winning market overstates the ex-ante expected equity premium by approximately 1% to 2% per year.

How does Mankiw & Zeldes (1991) stockholder participation explain the gap between aggregate consumption volatility and asset pricing requirements?

Solution

Mankiw & Zeldes (1991) demonstrated that only a fraction of US households held equity. The consumption of stockholders is substantially more volatile (\(\sigma \approx 6\%\)) and covaries much more strongly with stock returns than that of non-stockholders. Pricing assets using aggregate per capita consumption artificially averages in non-participating households, diluting the effective risk exposure.

What role do rare macroeconomic disasters (Barro 2006, Rietz 1988) play in resolving the equity premium puzzle?

Solution

If agents anticipate a small probability (e.g. 1.5% to 2% per year) of a catastrophic drop in consumption (e.g. 20% to 50% decline, as seen during wars, depressions, and pandemics), equities demand a substantial risk premium. Because rare disasters are seldom observed in short historical samples, the realized sample equity premium looks deceptively high relative to observed sample consumption volatility.

Research

In the external habit model of Campbell & Cochrane (1999), explain how time-varying surplus consumption generates high and countercyclical equity risk premia with constant risk-free rates.

Solution

Campbell & Cochrane introduce external habit \(X_t\), defining surplus consumption ratio \(S_t = (C_t - X_t)/C_t\). Effective risk aversion is \(\gamma / S_t\). In recessions, consumption falls towards the habit level, so \(S_t\) shrinks, driving effective risk aversion and Sharpe ratios dramatically higher. Intertemporal substitution and precautionary savings are engineered to offset each other, keeping the risk-free rate constant.

Explain how the long-run risk model of Bansal & Yaron (2004) uses Epstein-Zin preferences to generate a large equity premium from small consumption growth innovations.

Solution

Bansal & Yaron postulate a small, highly persistent predictable component in consumption growth alongside stochastic volatility. With Epstein-Zin preferences where agents prefer early resolution of uncertainty (\(\gamma > 1/\psi\)), investors fear long-run growth slumps and economic uncertainty shocks far more than transitory innovations, demanding a large equity premium.

Contrast the behavioral prospect theory explanation of Benartzi & Thaler (1995) (myopic loss aversion) with consumption-based rational pricing frameworks.

Solution

Benartzi & Thaler abandon expected utility over consumption, proposing that investors evaluate financial wealth directly, have asymmetric loss aversion (losses hurt 2.25 times more than equal gains), and monitor portfolios frequently (e.g., annually). The pain of frequent paper losses requires a 6% equity premium to induce investment in stocks, independent of aggregate consumption covariance.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What was the historical annual US equity risk premium documented by Mehra & Prescott (1985) over 1889–1978?

    Options

    Mehra & Prescott found an average equity return of 6.98% and a real risk-free rate of 0.80%, yielding an equity premium of 6.18%.

  2. Why cannot the standard CRRA representative agent model generate an equity premium larger than ~0.35% for \(\gamma \le 10\)?

    Options

    Because aggregate consumption growth has very low volatility, the covariance between consumption and returns is tiny, producing an SDF that varies far too little.

  3. Under lognormality, what is the approximate upper bound on the equity Sharpe ratio in terms of risk aversion \(\gamma\) and consumption volatility \(\sigma_c\)?

    Options

    By Cauchy-Schwarz and the lognormal SDF variance approximation, \(\sigma(M)/\E[M] \approx \gamma \sigma_c\), which bounds the maximal Sharpe ratio.

  4. If the historical equity Sharpe ratio is 0.374 and consumption volatility is \(\sigma_c = 0.0357\), what is the minimum theoretical risk aversion \(\gamma\) under perfect negative correlation (\(\rho = -1\))?

    Setting \(0.374 = \gamma \times 0.0357\) yields \(\gamma = 0.374 / 0.0357 \approx 10.48\).

  5. What level of risk aversion is typically accepted in microeconomic studies of household behavior and game experiments?

    Options

    Micro evidence from insurance choices, portfolio allocation, and experimental gambles places relative risk aversion between 1 and 5.

  6. How does Mankiw & Zeldes' (1991) empirical evidence on stockholder participation address the equity premium puzzle?

    Options

    Averaging non-stockholders into aggregate consumption severely understates the actual consumption volatility of the households who actually clear equity markets.

  7. In Barro's (2006) rare disasters framework, what resolves the equity premium puzzle?

    Options

    The possibility of rare 20–50% consumption declines makes equity payoffs in disaster states deeply painful, requiring a large ex-ante equity premium.

  8. What happens if an econometrician forces \(\gamma = 50\) to fit the equity premium in a power utility model?

    Options

    Under CRRA, \(\psi = 1/\gamma\); high \(\gamma\) implies near-zero EIS, so consumers demand huge interest rates to defer consumption in a growing economy.

  9. In Campbell & Cochrane's (1999) habit formation model, why does the equity premium fluctuate countercyclically?

    Options

    When consumption approaches the habit level \(X_t\), surplus consumption \(S_t \to 0\), causing risk aversion \(\gamma/S_t\) to surge and driving up risk premia.

  10. What role does the correlation between equity returns and consumption growth play in the equity premium calculation?

    Options

    Because stocks and consumption are imperfectly correlated (\(\rho \approx 0.2\)), the required \(\gamma\) is inflated by a factor of \(1/\rho \approx 5\).
