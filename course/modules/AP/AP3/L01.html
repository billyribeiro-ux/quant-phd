The Lucas Tree & Pure Exchange Equilibrium

  • Uses: stochastic discount factor, law of one price

Modern macroeconomic asset pricing roots prices in consumption dynamics. Lucas (1978) established that in a pure exchange economy, asset prices are the unique fixed point of a contraction operator. This isolates how macroeconomic risk determines the stochastic discount factor before production or frictions intervene.

1Pure Exchange Economy Architecture

Consider an infinite-horizon discrete-time economy \(t \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\) populated by a single representative consumer with time-separable expected utility

\[ \begin{aligned} U(\{c_t\}_{t=0}^\infty) &= \E_0 \left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty \beta^t u(c_t) \right] \end{aligned} \]

where \(\beta \in (0, 1)\) is the subjective discount factor and \(u: \R_{++} \to \R\) is strictly increasing, strictly concave, and twice continuously differentiable (\(u' > 0\), \(u'' < 0\)), satisfying the Inada condition \(\lim_{c \to 0} u'(c) = \infty\).

The economy contains a single non-reproducible asset ("the Lucas tree") that yields a non-storable, exogenous dividend flow \(y_t > 0\) at each date \(t\). The dividend follows a first-order Markov process on state space \(\mathcal{Y} \subset \R_{++}\) governed by transition probability distribution \(F(y_{t+1} \mid y_t)\).

Definition 1.1 (Competitive Equilibrium in the Lucas Tree Model)

A competitive equilibrium in the Lucas tree model consists of an asset price function \(p: \mathcal{Y} \to \R_{++}\) and an allocation \(\{c_t, \theta_t\}_{t=0}^\infty\) such that:

  1. Given price function \(p(y)\), the representative agent chooses consumption \(c_t\) and tree share holdings \(\theta_{t+1}\) to maximize lifetime expected utility subject to the sequence of budget constraints: \[ \begin{aligned} c_t + p(y_t) \theta_{t+1} &\le \theta_t [p(y_t) + y_t] \end{aligned} \]
  2. The perishable goods market clears: \(c_t = y_t\) for all \(t\).
  3. The asset market clears: \(\theta_t = 1\) for all \(t\).
Theorem 1.1 (Lucas Pricing Functional Equation)

In competitive equilibrium, the price function \(p(y)\) satisfies the nonlinear functional equation

\[ \begin{aligned} p(y) u'(y) &= \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} u'(y') [p(y') + y'] dF(y' \mid y) \end{aligned} \]

Equivalently, the one-period gross return on the tree is \(R_{t+1} = \frac{p(y_{t+1}) + y_{t+1}}{p(y_t)}\), and the equilibrium stochastic discount factor is given by the marginal rate of substitution

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1} &= \beta \frac{u'(c_{t+1})}{u'(c_t)} = \beta \frac{u'(y_{t+1})}{u'(y_t)} \end{aligned} \]
Proof

Set up the agent's dynamic Bellman equation. Let \(V(\theta, y)\) denote the value function when holding tree share \(\theta\) at current dividend state \(y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} V(\theta, y) &= \max_{c, \theta'} \left\{ u(c) + \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} V(\theta', y') dF(y' \mid y) \right\} \end{aligned} \]

subject to \(c = \theta [p(y) + y] - p(y) \theta'\). Substituting the constraint into the objective:

\[ \begin{aligned} V(\theta, y) &= \max_{\theta'} \Bigl\{ u(\theta [p(y) + y] - p(y) \theta') \\ &\quad + \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} V(\theta', y') dF(y' \mid y) \Bigr\} \end{aligned} \]

The first-order necessary condition with respect to \(\theta'\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} p(y) u'(c) &= \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \frac{\partial V(\theta', y')}{\partial \theta'} dF(y' \mid y) \end{aligned} \]

By the Benveniste-Scheinkman envelope theorem:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial V(\theta, y)}{\partial \theta} &= u'(c) [p(y) + y] \end{aligned} \]

Updating the envelope condition to date \(t+1\) and substituting it into the first-order condition yields

\[ \begin{aligned} p(y) u'(c) &= \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} u'(c') [p(y') + y'] dF(y' \mid y) \end{aligned} \]

Imposing market clearing \(c = y\), \(c' = y'\), and \(\theta = 1\) establishes the result.

2Contraction Mapping & Operator Theory

Let \(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})\) denote the Banach space of continuous bounded functions on \(\mathcal{Y}\) equipped with the supremum norm \(\|f\| = \sup_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} |f(y)|\). Define the price-dividend ratio \(v(y) = p(y) / y\).

Definition 1.2 (Lucas Pricing Operator)

Define the operator \(T: \mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y}) \to \mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})\) acting on candidate price-dividend functions \(v\) by

\[ \begin{aligned} (Tv)(y) &= \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} \frac{y'}{y} [v(y') + 1] dF(y' \mid y) \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 1.2 (Existence and Uniqueness via Banach Fixed Point Theorem)

If there exists \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\) such that for all \(y \in \mathcal{Y}\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} \frac{y'}{y} dF(y' \mid y) &\le \alpha < 1 \end{aligned} \]

then \(T\) is a strict contraction with modulus \(\alpha\). By the Banach fixed point theorem, there exists a unique positive price-dividend function \(v^\ast \in \mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})\) satisfying \(Tv^\ast = v^\ast\).

Proof

For any two candidate functions \(v_1, v_2 \in \mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})\):

\[ \begin{aligned} |(Tv_1)(y) - (Tv_2)(y)| &= \left| \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} \frac{y'}{y} [v_1(y') - v_2(y')] dF(y' \mid y) \right| \\ &\le \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} \frac{y'}{y} |v_1(y') - v_2(y')| dF(y' \mid y) \\ &\le \|v_1 - v_2\| \cdot \beta \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} \frac{y'}{y} dF(y' \mid y) \\ &\le \alpha \|v_1 - v_2\| \end{aligned} \]

Taking the supremum over all \(y \in \mathcal{Y}\) yields \(\|Tv_1 - Tv_2\| \le \alpha \|v_1 - v_2\|\). Because \(\alpha < 1\), Blackwell's sufficiency conditions for a contraction are satisfied, establishing existence and uniqueness.

3CRRA Preferences & Discrete Markov Chains

Under Constant Relative Risk Aversion (CRRA) preferences with coefficient \(\gamma > 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} u(c) &= \begin{cases} \frac{c^{1-\gamma} - 1}{1 - \gamma}, & \gamma \ne 1 \\ \ln c, & \gamma = 1 \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]

The marginal utility ratio simplifies to \(\frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} = \left(\frac{y'}{y}\right)^{-\gamma}\). Consequently, the kernel inside the pricing operator becomes

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} \frac{y'}{y} &= \left(\frac{y'}{y}\right)^{1-\gamma} \end{aligned} \]
Example 1.1 (I.I.D. Growth Rate Solution)

Suppose the endowment growth rate \(g_{t+1} = y_{t+1}/y_t\) is independently and identically distributed with distribution \(H(g)\). Then the price-dividend ratio \(v\) is a constant independent of the state \(y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} v &= \beta \E[g^{1-\gamma}] (v + 1) \implies v = \frac{\beta \E[g^{1-\gamma}]}{1 - \beta \E[g^{1-\gamma}]} \end{aligned} \]

provided the transversality condition \(\beta \E[g^{1-\gamma}] < 1\) holds.

Remark 1.1 (Income versus Substitution Effects in Valuation)

When \(\gamma = 1\) (log utility), \(\E[g^{1-\gamma}] = \E[g^0] = 1\), so the equilibrium price-dividend ratio is constant and equal to \(v = \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta}\), completely invariant to growth shocks. If \(\gamma > 1\), the income effect dominates: higher risk aversion lowers the price-dividend ratio. If \(\gamma < 1\), the substitution effect dominates.

Lab 1 · Lucas Tree Discrete Markov Chain Solution

Solve the Lucas asset pricing equation on a 3-state Markov chain for endowment growth, compute equilibrium price-dividend ratios across states, and verify Euler equation errors vanish to machine precision.

import numpy as np

# Lucas (1978) Pure Exchange Economy on a 3-State Markov Endowment Chain
beta = 0.96
gamma = 2.0
# Endowment growth states: recession, normal, expansion
g = np.array([0.97, 1.018, 1.05])
# Markov transition matrix Pi[i, j] = P(s_{t+1} = j | s_t = i)
pi = np.array(
    [
        [0.70, 0.25, 0.05],
        [0.15, 0.70, 0.15],
        [0.05, 0.25, 0.70],
    ]
)

# Lucas pricing operator matrix A_ij = beta * Pi_ij * g_j^(1 - gamma)
A = beta * pi * (g ** (1.0 - gamma))
spectral_radius = float(np.max(np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(A))))
print(f"Spectral radius rho(A): {spectral_radius:.4f}")

# Equilibrium price-dividend ratio: v = (I - A)^(-1) A 1
identity = np.eye(3)
v = np.linalg.solve(identity - A, A @ np.ones(3))
print("Price-dividend ratios v:", np.round(v, 4).tolist())

# Verification of Euler equation error across all states:
# E_i [ beta * (g_j)^(-gamma) * (v_j + 1) / v_i * g_j ] - 1 = 0
euler_lhs = (A @ (v + np.ones(3))) / v
max_euler_error = float(np.max(np.abs(euler_lhs - 1.0)))
print(f"Max absolute Euler error: {max_euler_error:.2e}")
Spectral radius rho(A): 0.9495
Price-dividend ratios v: [20.0612, 18.6128, 17.5168]
Max absolute Euler error: 2.22e-16

The linear system \((I - A)v = A \mathbf{1}\) yields the exact equilibrium price-dividend ratios. Because the spectral radius \(\rho(A) = 0.9495 < 1\), the system is strictly stationary and invertible. Across all states, the maximum Euler pricing error is on the order of \(10^{-16}\), verifying exact numerical consistency.

Where the canon breaks

The standard Lucas (1978) model makes three restrictive structural assumptions that fail systematically in financial market data:

  • Perishable Endowment with Zero Storage: Consumption is forced to equal aggregate dividends (\(c_t = y_t\)). In actual economies, inventories, durable goods, capital investment, and corporate cash retained earnings break the tight link between consumption and stock dividends.
  • Homogeneous Agent with Time-Separable Utility: Representative agent utility assumes all idiosyncratic risks can be completely insured away, ignoring borrowing constraints and uninsurable labor income risks.
  • Time-Invariant Discount Rate: Under i.i.d. growth and power utility, price-dividend ratios are constant. In reality, price-dividend ratios vary substantially over time, driven by shifting risk premia rather than cash-flow expectations.

Misconceptions

  • The equilibrium price-dividend ratio in the Lucas tree model is always strictly decreasing in risk aversion \(\gamma\).

    Why it is wrong: The sign of \(\partial(p/y)/\partial \gamma\) depends on whether the expected growth rate is above or below 1 and the balance between income and intertemporal substitution effects. When growth exceeds 1 and \(\gamma < 1\), the substitution effect dominates, whereas under log utility (\(\gamma = 1\)), \(p/y = \beta/(1-\beta)\) is invariant to both growth and volatility.

  • The Lucas tree model requires production and physical capital accumulation to determine equilibrium asset returns.

    Why it is wrong: Lucas (1978) specifically constructed a pure exchange economy where dividends fall exogenously like fruit from trees. This intentionally eliminates production dynamics to isolate how consumer preferences and endowment risks determine prices.

  • Non-stationary growth in endowment levels invalidates the contraction mapping theorem.

    Why it is wrong: While dividend levels \(y_t\) may grow without bound, the pricing operator is normalized by dividing by dividends, transforming the state variable into the stationary price-dividend ratio \(v(y) = p(y)/y\). As long as growth rates are stationary, contraction mapping holds.

Problem set

Mechanics

Consider an i.i.d. economy with discount factor \(\beta = 0.95\) and log utility (\(\gamma = 1\)). Compute the equilibrium price-dividend ratio \(v = p/y\).

Solution

Under log utility, \(u'(c) = 1/c\). The kernel inside the Euler equation is \(\frac{u'(y_{t+1})}{u'(y_t)} \frac{y_{t+1}}{y_t} = \frac{y_t}{y_{t+1}} \frac{y_{t+1}}{y_t} = 1\). Therefore, the price-dividend ratio satisfies \(v = \beta \E[1] (v + 1) = \beta (v + 1)\). Solving for \(v\) yields \(v = \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta} = \frac{0.95}{1 - 0.95} = \frac{0.95}{0.05} = 19.0\).

In a two-state Markov chain, endowment growth takes values \(g_1 = 0.98\) and \(g_2 = 1.04\) with transition matrix \(\Pi = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & 0.2 \\ 0.3 & 0.7 \end{pmatrix}\). For \(\beta = 0.95\) and \(\gamma = 2.0\), compute matrix \(A\).

Solution

The elements of \(A\) are defined by \(A_{ij} = \beta \Pi_{ij} g_j^{1 - \gamma} = \beta \Pi_{ij} g_j^{-1}\). We have \(g_1^{-1} = 1/0.98 \approx 1.0204\) and \(g_2^{-1} = 1/1.04 \approx 0.9615\). Then \(A_{11} = 0.95 \times 0.8 \times 1.0204 = 0.7755\), \(A_{12} = 0.95 \times 0.2 \times 0.9615 = 0.1827\), \(A_{21} = 0.95 \times 0.3 \times 1.0204 = 0.2908\), and \(A_{22} = 0.95 \times 0.7 \times 0.9615 = 0.6394\). Thus \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7755 & 0.1827 \\ 0.2908 & 0.6394 \end{pmatrix}\).

Given matrix \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.70 & 0.15 \\ 0.20 & 0.65 \end{pmatrix}\), solve for the equilibrium price-dividend vector \(v = (I - A)^{-1} A \mathbf{1}\).

Solution

Compute \(I - A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.30 & -0.15 \\ -0.20 & 0.35 \end{pmatrix}\). The determinant is \(\det(I - A) = (0.30)(0.35) - (-0.15)(-0.20) = 0.105 - 0.030 = 0.075\). The inverse is \((I - A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.075} \begin{pmatrix} 0.35 & 0.15 \\ 0.20 & 0.30 \end{pmatrix}\). We have \(A \mathbf{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.85 \\ 0.85 \end{pmatrix}\). Multiplying gives \(v_1 = \frac{1}{0.075} [0.35(0.85) + 0.15(0.85)] = \frac{0.50 \times 0.85}{0.075} = \frac{0.425}{0.075} = 5.6667\), and \(v_2 = \frac{1}{0.075} [0.20(0.85) + 0.30(0.85)] = \frac{0.50 \times 0.85}{0.075} = 5.6667\).

Proof

Prove that under CRRA preferences and lognormally distributed i.i.d. growth \(\ln g \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), the transversality condition for the existence of finite asset prices is \(\beta \exp((1-\gamma)\mu + \tfrac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)^2 \sigma^2) < 1\).

Solution

In the i.i.d. economy, the price-dividend ratio is finite if and only if \(\beta \E[g^{1-\gamma}] < 1\). Since \(g = \exp(x)\) with \(x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), the random variable \((1-\gamma)x\) is normal with mean \((1-\gamma)\mu\) and variance \((1-\gamma)^2 \sigma^2\). By the moment-generating function of the normal distribution, \(\E[\exp((1-\gamma)x)] = \exp\left((1-\gamma)\mu + \frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)^2 \sigma^2\right)\). Multiplying by \(\beta\) and requiring the discount factor to be strictly less than 1 completes the proof.

Prove that Blackwell's sufficiency conditions (monotonicity and discounting) imply that the Lucas pricing operator \(T\) is a contraction mapping on \((\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y}), \|\cdot\|_\infty)\).

Solution

Blackwell's conditions state that an operator \(T\) on bounded continuous functions is a contraction with modulus \(\beta\) if: (1) Monotonicity: \(f \le g \implies Tf \le Tg\). For any \(v_1 \le v_2\), \(v_1(y') + 1 \le v_2(y') + 1\); since \(\beta \frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} \frac{y'}{y} > 0\), integrating preserves the inequality, so \(Tv_1 \le Tv_2\). (2) Discounting: \(T(f + a) \le Tf + \beta a\) for any constant \(a \ge 0\). We have \(T(v + a) = Tv + \beta a \int \frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} \frac{y'}{y} dF \le Tv + \alpha a\) by the assumption \(\beta \int \frac{u'(y')}{u'(y)} \frac{y'}{y} dF \le \alpha < 1\). Thus, Blackwell's conditions hold, and \(T\) is a strict contraction.

Prove that the risk-free rate in the Lucas tree model with CRRA utility and i.i.d. lognormal consumption growth satisfies \(\ln R_f = -\ln \beta + \gamma \mu - \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma^2\).

Solution

The price of a one-period risk-free discount bond paying 1 unit of consumption is \(q_t = \E_t[M_{t+1}] = \beta \E_t[(c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma}]\). Under market clearing \(c_t = y_t\), \(\ln(c_{t+1}/c_t) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\). The variable \(-\gamma \ln(c_{t+1}/c_t)\) is normal with mean \(-\gamma \mu\) and variance \(\gamma^2 \sigma^2\). Thus, \(\E[(c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma}] = \exp(-\gamma \mu + \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma^2)\). The gross risk-free rate is \(R_f = 1/q_t = \beta^{-1} \exp(\gamma \mu - \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma^2)\). Taking logarithms yields \(\ln R_f = -\ln \beta + \gamma \mu - \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma^2\).

Empirical

In US historical annual data (1889–1978), mean per capita consumption growth is \(\mu = 0.0183\) and volatility is \(\sigma = 0.0357\). For \(\beta = 0.98\) and \(\gamma = 2.0\), compute the model-implied real risk-free rate.

Solution

Using the log-risk-free formula: \(\ln R_f = -\ln(0.98) + 2(0.0183) - \frac{1}{2}(2)^2 (0.0357)^2\). We calculate \(-\ln(0.98) \approx 0.0202\), \(2(0.0183) = 0.0366\), and \(2(0.0357)^2 = 2(0.001274) \approx 0.0025\). Thus, \(\ln R_f \approx 0.0202 + 0.0366 - 0.0025 = 0.0543\) (5.43% per year). In levels, \(R_f = \exp(0.0543) \approx 1.0558\) (5.58% net real rate), substantially higher than the historical 0.80%.

Using the historical parameters \(\mu = 0.0183\), \(\sigma = 0.0357\), and \(\beta = 0.98\), calculate the implied price-dividend ratio \(v\) for \(\gamma = 2.0\).

Solution

For \(\gamma = 2\), \(1 - \gamma = -1\). The growth term is \(\E[g^{-1}] = \exp(-\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2) = \exp(-0.0183 + 0.5(0.0357)^2) = \exp(-0.0183 + 0.000637) = \exp(-0.01766) \approx 0.9825\). The discount term is \(\beta \E[g^{-1}] = 0.98 \times 0.9825 \approx 0.9628\). The price-dividend ratio is \(v = \frac{0.9628}{1 - 0.9628} = \frac{0.9628}{0.0372} \approx 25.88\).

Suppose an econometrician observes that the historical price-dividend ratio of the S&P 500 exhibits a standard deviation of 12.0 around its mean. Can the standard Lucas model with i.i.d. growth explain this time variation?

Solution

No. Under i.i.d. growth and time-separable CRRA utility, the price-dividend ratio is analytically constant across all periods (\(v = \frac{\beta \E[g^{1-\gamma}]}{1 - \beta \E[g^{1-\gamma}]}\)). Its theoretical volatility is exactly zero. To generate time-varying price-dividend ratios within the Lucas framework, one must introduce predictable time variation in growth (persistent Markov chain) or time-varying risk aversion (habit persistence).

Research

How does the introduction of production and capital accumulation (\(Y_t = A_t K_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}\)) fundamentally alter the identification of the stochastic discount factor compared to the Lucas exchange economy?

Solution

In a Lucas exchange economy, consumption is exogenous (\(c_t = y_t\)), so the SDF is completely pinned down by the endowment process and preferences. In a production economy with capital, consumption is an endogenous choice variable: agents can smooth consumption across time by adjusting investment and capital accumulation. Consequently, technology shocks are smoothed into consumption, making consumption endogenous to capital adjustment costs and interest rates, which decouples asset prices from simple endowment dynamics.

Why does Campbell and Shiller's (1988) log-linear approximation of returns provide a bridge between the non-linear Lucas tree Euler equation and linear factor pricing models?

Solution

The gross return identity \(R_{t+1} = (P_{t+1} + D_{t+1})/P_t\) is nonlinear. Campbell and Shiller approximate \(\ln R_{t+1} \approx k + \rho p_{t+1} + (1-\rho)d_{t+1} - p_t\), where \(\rho = 1/(1 + \exp(\overline{d-p}))\). Substituting this linear identity into the log Euler equation \(\E_t[m_{t+1} + r_{t+1}] + \frac{1}{2}\Var_t(m_{t+1} + r_{t+1}) = 0\) produces an exact present-value relation expressing asset prices as linear sums of discounted expected dividend growth and discounted expected returns.

Evaluate the impact of non-expected utility (e.g. Epstein-Zin recursive preferences) on the Lucas pricing functional equation.

Solution

Under Epstein-Zin recursive preferences, the stochastic discount factor takes the form \(M_{t+1} = \beta^\theta \left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{-\frac{\theta}{\psi}} R_{w, t+1}^{\theta - 1}\), where \(\theta = \frac{1 - \gamma}{1 - 1/\psi}\) and \(R_{w, t+1}\) is the gross return on the total wealth portfolio. In a pure exchange economy where the tree represents aggregate wealth, \(R_{w, t+1} = R_{t+1}\). The Lucas functional equation now depends on both consumption growth and the endogenous market return, decoupling risk aversion \(\gamma\) from the elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(\psi\).

Retrieval quiz

  1. In the Lucas (1978) exchange economy, what condition identifies the equilibrium consumption allocation \(c_t\)?

    Options

    Because goods cannot be stored and there is a single representative consumer holding the single tree, all fruit produced at date \(t\) must be consumed at date \(t\), forcing \(c_t = y_t\).

  2. What mathematical theorem guarantees the existence and uniqueness of the Lucas pricing function?

    Options

    Lucas (1978) shows that the pricing operator satisfies Blackwell's conditions of discounting and monotonicity, proving it is a contraction on the Banach space of continuous bounded functions.

  3. Under CRRA preferences with log utility (\(\gamma = 1\)), what is the equilibrium price-dividend ratio in the i.i.d. Lucas tree model?

    Options

    When \(\gamma = 1\), the income and substitution effects exactly offset, making the price-dividend ratio constant and equal to \(\beta/(1-\beta)\).

  4. For \(\beta = 0.90\) and log utility (\(\gamma = 1\)), what is the numerical value of the price-dividend ratio \(v = p/y\)?

    \(v = \frac{\beta}{1 - \beta} = \frac{0.90}{1 - 0.90} = \frac{0.90}{0.10} = 9.0\).

  5. In an \(S\)-state discrete Markov chain for endowment growth, how is the Lucas price-dividend ratio vector \(v\) calculated?

    Options

    The discrete Markov Euler equation takes the matrix form \(v = A(v + \mathbf{1})\), which rearranges to \((I - A)v = A \mathbf{1}\).

  6. What condition ensures that the matrix inverse \((I - A)^{-1}\) exists and yields strictly positive prices?

    Options

    By the Neumann series theorem, \((I - A)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty A^k\) converges to a non-negative matrix if and only if the spectral radius \(\rho(A) < 1\).

  7. What is the stochastic discount factor \(M_{t+1}\) in the Lucas tree model under power utility?

    Options

    By the consumer's first-order condition, \(M_{t+1} = \beta \frac{u'(c_{t+1})}{u'(c_t)}\); substituting CRRA utility and market clearing \(c_t = y_t\) yields \(\beta (y_{t+1}/y_t)^{-\gamma}\).

  8. Why does the standard Lucas tree model with i.i.d. growth fail to explain the excess volatility of stock prices?

    Options

    Shiller (1981) demonstrated that stock prices vary far more than discounted dividends can justify; the Lucas model with i.i.d. growth predicts zero variance in the price-dividend ratio.

  9. In equilibrium in the Lucas tree model, what fraction of the total tree shares does the representative consumer hold?

    By asset market clearing, the single representative agent must hold 100% of the aggregate supply of the tree (\(\theta_t = 1.0\)).

  10. If risk aversion \(\gamma > 1\) and expected dividend growth increases in an i.i.d. economy, what happens to the price-dividend ratio?

    Options

    For \(\gamma > 1\), the exponent \(1 - \gamma\) is negative, so higher growth lowers \(\E[g^{1-\gamma}]\), reducing the price-dividend ratio due to dominant wealth effects.
