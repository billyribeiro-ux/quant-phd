The Risk-Free Rate Puzzle & Preferences

Resolving the equity premium puzzle by increasing risk aversion creates an immediate crisis: under standard power utility, high risk aversion forces the elasticity of intertemporal substitution toward zero. Weil (1989) proved that consumers with near-zero willingness to substitute across time demand astronomical interest rates to defer consumption in a growing economy. Understanding this puzzle led to Epstein-Zin recursive preferences that untangle risk aversion from intertemporal substitution.

1Analytical Origin of the Risk-Free Rate Puzzle

Under power utility, a single parameter \(\gamma\) governs two conceptually distinct economic traits: attitudes toward risk across states of nature (relative risk aversion, \(RRA = \gamma\)), and willingness to smooth consumption across calendar dates (elasticity of intertemporal substitution, \(EIS = \psi = 1/\gamma\)).

Definition 1.5 Risk-free-rate puzzle

The risk-free-rate puzzle is the phenomenon identified by Weil (1989) where calibrating risk aversion high enough (\(\gamma \ge 20\)) to resolve the equity premium puzzle in a power-utility economy forces the elasticity of intertemporal substitution to near zero (\(\psi \le 0.05\)), driving model risk-free interest rates to counterfactually high levels (15% to 50% per year) compared to the historical US real rate of approximately 0.8%.

To see the mathematical tension, consider the price of a one-period real risk-free discount bond paying 1 unit of consumption with certainty at \(t+1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P^f_t &= \E_t [ M_{t+1} ] \\ &= \beta \E_t \left[ (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma} \right] \end{aligned} \]

Assuming consumption growth is conditionally lognormal, \(\ln(c_{t+1}/c_t) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_c, \sigma_c^2)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln \E_t \left[ (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma} \right] &= -\gamma \mu_c + \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_c^2 \end{aligned} \]

The continuously compounded risk-free rate \(r^f = -\ln P^f = -\ln \E[M]\) is therefore:

\[ \begin{aligned} r^f &= -\ln \beta + \gamma \mu_c - \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_c^2 \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 1.1 T Decomposition of the Equilibrium Risk-Free Rate

In a lognormal exchange economy with CRRA utility, the equilibrium real risk-free rate decomposes into three distinct structural components:

\[ \begin{aligned} r^f &= -\ln \beta + \frac{1}{\psi} \mu_c - \frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_c^2 \end{aligned} \]

where \(-\ln \beta\) captures impatience, \(\frac{1}{\psi} \mu_c\) represents intertemporal consumption growth, \(-\frac{1}{2} \gamma^2 \sigma_c^2\) is the precautionary savings motive, and \(\psi = 1/\gamma\) is the elasticity of intertemporal substitution.

Evaluate this decomposition using US historical averages (\(\mu_c = 1.8\%\), \(\sigma_c = 1.5\%\), and \(\beta = 0.99\)):

  • Case \(\gamma = 1.0\) (\(\psi = 1.0\)): \[ \begin{aligned} r^f &= 0.0101 + 1.0(0.018) \\ &\quad - 0.5(1)^2(0.015)^2 \\ &= 1.01\% + 1.80\% - 0.01\% \\ &= 2.80\% \end{aligned} \] Plausible interest rate, but equity premium is only \(\approx 0.13\%\).
  • Case \(\gamma = 30.0\) (\(\psi = 0.0333\)): \[ \begin{aligned} r^f &= 0.0101 + 30(0.018) \\ &\quad - 0.5(30)^2(0.015)^2 \\ &= 1.01\% + 54.00\% - 10.12\% \\ &= 44.89\% \end{aligned} \] Risk-free rate explodes to nearly 45% per annum!

2Epstein-Zin-Weil Recursive Preferences

To sever the rigid link \(\psi = 1/\gamma\), Epstein & Zin (1989) and Weil (1989) developed recursive preferences based on Kreps-Porteus (1978) non-expected utility.

Definition 1.6 (Epstein-Zin Recursive Utility)

The continuation utility \(U_t\) is defined recursively over current consumption \(c_t\) and the certainty equivalent of future utility \(\mathcal{R}_t(U_{t+1})\):

\[ \begin{aligned} U_t &= \Big[ (1-\beta) c_t^{1 - 1/\psi} \\ &\quad + \beta (\mathcal{R}_t(U_{t+1}))^{1 - 1/\psi} \Big]^{\frac{1}{1 - 1/\psi}} \end{aligned} \]

where the certainty equivalent operator is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{R}_t(U_{t+1}) &= \left( \E_t \left[ U_{t+1}^{1-\gamma} \right] \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}} \end{aligned} \]

Here, \(\gamma\) controls relative risk aversion over atemporal lotteries, while \(\psi\) independently controls the elasticity of intertemporal substitution across dates. Defining the auxiliary parameter \(\theta = \frac{1-\gamma}{1-1/\psi}\), the stochastic discount factor becomes:

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1} &= \beta^\theta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\frac{\theta}{\psi}} \\ &\quad \times (R_{w, t+1})^{\theta - 1} \end{aligned} \]

where \(R_{w, t+1}\) is the gross return on the overall wealth portfolio. When \(\gamma = 1/\psi\), \(\theta = 1\), collapsing back to standard time-separable CRRA utility.

Lab 1 · Weil (1989) Risk-Free Rate Decomposition

Simulate the decomposition of the equilibrium real risk-free rate under power utility across a grid of risk aversion \(\gamma \in [1, 40]\), showing the explosion of the intertemporal growth term.

import numpy as np

# Weil (1989) Risk-Free Rate Puzzle: CRRA Coupling of RRA and EIS
mu_c = 0.018  # annual consumption growth (1.8%)
sigma_c = 0.015  # post-war consumption volatility (1.5%)
beta = 0.99  # subjective discount factor

gamma_grid = [1.0, 5.0, 10.0, 20.0, 30.0, 40.0]

print(f"{'gamma':>5} | {'EIS':>7} | {'Growth Term':>12} | {'Precautionary':>14} | {'Net rf':>8}")
print("-" * 56)
for g in gamma_grid:
    eis = 1.0 / g
    growth_term = g * mu_c
    prec_term = -0.5 * (g**2) * (sigma_c**2)
    rf_net = -np.log(beta) + growth_term + prec_term
    print(f"{g:5.1f} | {eis:7.4f} | {growth_term * 100:11.2f}% | {prec_term * 100:13.2f}% | {rf_net * 100:7.2f}%")

print("-" * 56)
print("Historical US real risk-free rate (1889-1978): ~0.80%")

As risk aversion rises to \(\gamma = 30\), the intertemporal substitution term \(\gamma \mu_c\) surges to 54.0%, dwarfing the precautionary savings term (-10.12%) and driving the net risk-free rate to 44.88%. This quantitatively confirms Weil's proof that high risk aversion generates a counterfactually massive risk-free rate.

Where the canon breaks

While Epstein-Zin recursive preferences resolve the mathematical coupling between risk aversion and the elasticity of intertemporal substitution, several critical theoretical and empirical tensions persist:

  • Measuring the Wealth Portfolio Return: The Epstein-Zin SDF requires the return on total wealth \(R_w\), which includes non-traded human capital and private business equity, re-introducing Roll's critique.
  • Timing of Uncertainty Resolution: When \(\gamma > 1/\psi\) (\(\theta < 1\)), agents prefer early resolution of temporal risk. While psychologically plausible, it creates dynamic consistency challenges in multi-agent bargaining contexts.
  • Empirical Estimates of EIS: Microeconomic studies estimating \(\psi\) from interest rate variations (Hall 1988) find \(\psi < 0.2\), while macroeconomic long-run risk models (Bansal & Yaron 2004) require \(\psi > 1.5\) to ensure positive substitution responses.

Misconceptions

  • The risk-free rate puzzle can be cured by assuming agents are extremely impatient (e.g. \(\beta < 0.50\)).

    Why it is wrong: Assuming low \(\beta\) makes the impatience term \(-\ln \beta\) even larger and more positive, driving the model risk-free rate even higher! To lower the risk-free rate in a power utility model, one would have to assume negative time preference (\(\beta > 1\)), which is economically bizarre.

  • Precautionary savings always lowers the risk-free rate below zero when risk aversion is high.

    Why it is wrong: The precautionary savings term is \(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2\), which scales with \(\gamma^2\). However, in post-war data, consumption volatility is tiny (\(\sigma_c \approx 0.015\)). For moderate risk aversion (\(\gamma \le 20\)), \(\frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2 \approx 0.045\) (4.5%), while the growth term \(\gamma \mu_c = 20 \times 0.018 = 0.36\) (36%), leaving the net rate vastly positive.

  • Epstein-Zin preferences violate expected utility axioms across atemporal lotteries.

    Why it is wrong: For static, single-period gambles, Epstein-Zin preferences satisfy the standard von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms. The departure occurs across temporal compound lotteries, relaxing the axiom of reduction of compound lotteries to allow agents to care about the timing of resolution of risk.

Problem set

Mechanics

In a power-utility economy with \(\beta = 0.98\), \(\gamma = 2.0\), \(\mu_c = 0.02\), and \(\sigma_c = 0.02\), compute the three components of the continuously compounded risk-free rate: impatience, growth, and precautionary savings.

Solution

Impatience: \(-\ln(0.98) \approx 0.0202\) (2.02%). Growth term: \(\gamma \mu_c = 2.0 \times 0.02 = 0.0400\) (4.00%). Precautionary savings: \(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2 = -0.5 \times 4 \times 0.0004 = -0.0008\) (-0.08%). Net risk-free rate: \(r^f = 0.0202 + 0.0400 - 0.0008 = 0.0594\) (5.94%).

Compute the value of the parameter \(\theta = \frac{1-\gamma}{1 - 1/\psi}\) for an Epstein-Zin investor with risk aversion \(\gamma = 10\) and intertemporal elasticity \(\psi = 1.5\).

Solution

The numerator is \(1 - \gamma = 1 - 10 = -9\). The denominator is \(1 - 1/\psi = 1 - 1/1.5 = 1 - 2/3 = 1/3\). Thus \(\theta = \frac{-9}{1/3} = -27\).

What subjective discount factor \(\beta\) would be required to yield a net risk-free rate of \(r^f = 0.01\) when \(\gamma = 25\), \(\mu_c = 0.018\), and \(\sigma_c = 0.02\)?

Solution

The formula is \(r^f = -\ln \beta + \gamma \mu_c - \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2\). Growth term: \(25 \times 0.018 = 0.450\). Precautionary term: \(-0.5 \times 625 \times 0.0004 = -0.125\). Net terms excluding impatience: \(0.450 - 0.125 = 0.325\). Setting \(r^f = 0.01\) gives \(-\ln \beta + 0.325 = 0.01 \implies -\ln \beta = -0.315 \implies \beta = \exp(0.315) \approx 1.37\). The agent must have a discount factor far above 1 (negative time preference).

Proof

Prove that as \(\gamma \to \infty\), the precautionary savings term in the power-utility risk-free rate dominates the growth term, driving the risk-free rate to negative infinity.

Solution

The expression for the risk-free rate is \(r^f(\gamma) = -\ln \beta + \gamma \mu_c - \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2\). This is a downward-opening quadratic polynomial in \(\gamma\). Because the leading coefficient \(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_c^2 < 0\), the quadratic term dominates the linear term \(\gamma \mu_c\) for all \(\gamma > \frac{2\mu_c}{\sigma_c^2}\). As \(\gamma \to \infty\), \(r^f(\gamma) \to -\infty\).

Prove that under Epstein-Zin preferences, if \(\gamma = 1/\psi\), the SDF collapses to the standard CRRA stochastic discount factor.

Solution

When \(\gamma = 1/\psi\), the parameter \(\theta = \frac{1-\gamma}{1-1/\psi} = \frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma} = 1\). The Epstein-Zin SDF is \(M_{t+1} = \beta^\theta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\theta/\psi} (R_{w, t+1})^{\theta - 1}\). Substituting \(\theta = 1\) gives \(M_{t+1} = \beta^1 (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-1/\psi} (R_{w, t+1})^0 = \beta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma}\), which is identically the power utility SDF.

Show that if an agent has recursive preferences and prefers early resolution of uncertainty (\(\gamma > 1/\psi\)), the certainty equivalent \(\mathcal{R}_t\) is more risk-averse than the intertemporal aggregator.

Solution

The intertemporal substitution curvature is governed by \(\rho = 1 - 1/\psi\), while the certainty equivalent curvature is \(\alpha = 1 - \gamma\). For \(\gamma > 1/\psi\), we have \(1 - \gamma < 1 - 1/\psi\), meaning \(\alpha < \rho\). A more concave utility transformation implies higher risk aversion, so the agent displays higher risk aversion over temporal lotteries than would be implied by their willingness to substitute across deterministic consumption streams.

Empirical

Discuss the empirical discrepancy between Hall's (1988) instrumental variable estimates of the EIS (\(\psi \approx 0.1\)) and the requirements of long-run risk models (\(\psi > 1\)).

Solution

Hall (1988) regressed consumption growth on real interest rates using lagged financial instruments, finding near-zero coefficients, implying \(\psi < 0.2\). However, long-run risk models require \(\psi > 1\) so that higher expected growth raises wealth-consumption ratios and lowers cost of capital. Bansal & Yaron argue that Hall's regression suffered from severe weak instruments and time-aggregation bias.

Why does Weil (1989) refer to the equity premium and risk-free rate issues as two distinct puzzles rather than a single problem?

Solution

The equity premium puzzle is that stocks are too risky or equity premia are too high relative to consumption risk. The risk-free rate puzzle is that risk-free bonds have returns that are too low relative to positive expected consumption growth and consumer impatience. Even if recursive preferences allow \(\gamma = 20\) to match the equity premium, one still needs a high \(\psi\) to prevent the risk-free rate from exploding.

Explain how demographic shifts and population aging affect the equilibrium real risk-free rate through the life-cycle precautionary savings channel.

Solution

As life expectancy rises and retirement horizons lengthen, middle-aged workers accumulate massive precautionary savings to finance old-age consumption. This global savings glut increases the supply of loanable funds, pushing the equilibrium real risk-free rate downward, consistent with secular stagnation and historically depressed real interest rates.

Research

In Bansal & Yaron's (2004) long-run risk model, explain why \(\psi > 1\) is necessary for economic uncertainty (volatility shocks) to carry a negative market price of risk.

Solution

When \(\psi > 1\), the substitution effect dominates the income effect, so higher economic volatility reduces asset valuations and depresses the wealth-consumption ratio. Investors with \(\gamma > 1/\psi\) dislike volatility shocks, demanding an additional risk premium for holding assets whose payoffs fall when macro volatility rises.

Derive the log-linearized Epstein-Zin stochastic discount factor following Campbell (1993) and explain why the market portfolio return appears alongside consumption growth.

Solution

Log-linearizing the budget constraint around the mean consumption-wealth ratio allows expressing the unobservable continuation utility \(U_{t+1}\) as a function of the market return \(R_{w, t+1}\). Taking logs of \(M_{t+1}\) yields \(m_{t+1} = \theta \ln \beta - \frac{\theta}{\psi} \Delta c_{t+1} + (\theta - 1) r_{w, t+1}\). The market return enters because it proxies for innovations to future consumption paths that affect continuation utility.

Evaluate how habit formation (Campbell & Cochrane 1999) achieves a low, constant risk-free rate without using Epstein-Zin recursive utility.

Solution

Campbell & Cochrane specify the sensitivity function \(\lambda(s_t)\) of the surplus consumption ratio such that the intertemporal growth motive and the precautionary savings motive exactly offset each other across all states of nature: \(\gamma \mu_c - \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2 (1 + \lambda(s_t))^2 = \text{constant}\). This holds the equilibrium risk-free rate perfectly constant over the business cycle.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What economic phenomenon constitutes the risk-free rate puzzle documented by Weil (1989)?

    Options

    Because CRRA preferences force \(EIS = 1/\gamma\), setting \(\gamma = 30\) forces \(EIS = 0.033\), demanding huge interest rates to defer consumption in a growing economy.

  2. In the lognormal risk-free rate equation \(r^f = -\ln \beta + \frac{1}{\psi}\mu_c - \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2\), what role does the second term play?

    Options

    When consumption is expected to grow, consumers want to borrow from the richer future to consume today; interest rates must rise to induce them to save.

  3. Why cannot the precautionary savings term \(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \sigma_c^2\) offset the growth term in post-war US data for \(\gamma \le 20\)?

    Options

    With \(\sigma_c = 0.015\), \(\sigma_c^2 = 0.000225\). Even for \(\gamma = 20\), \(0.5 \times 400 \times 0.000225 = 0.045\) (4.5%), while \(\gamma \mu_c = 20 \times 0.018 = 0.36\) (36%).

  4. Under CRRA power utility, if relative risk aversion is \(\gamma = 1.0\) (log utility), what is the elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(\psi\)?

    Under CRRA preferences, \(\psi = 1/\gamma = 1/1.0 = 1.0\).

  5. What major flexibility do Epstein-Zin-Weil recursive preferences introduce over power utility?

    Options

    Epstein-Zin preferences parameterize risk aversion across states and intertemporal substitution across dates with two separate parameters.

  6. In the Epstein-Zin stochastic discount factor \(M_{t+1} = \beta^\theta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\theta/\psi} (R_{w, t+1})^{\theta - 1}\), what condition yields standard CRRA utility?

    Options

    When \(\theta = \frac{1-\gamma}{1-1/\psi} = 1\), the market return term drops out (\((R_w)^0 = 1\)) and the exponent on consumption growth becomes \(-\gamma\).

  7. When does an Epstein-Zin agent prefer early resolution of uncertainty over late resolution?

    Options

    Early resolution preference occurs when risk aversion is strictly greater than the inverse elasticity of intertemporal substitution.

  8. Why is the appearance of the wealth portfolio return \(R_{w, t+1}\) in the Epstein-Zin SDF an empirical challenge?

    Options

    Just as in the static CAPM, the return on the entire aggregate wealth portfolio cannot be measured directly because human wealth is non-marketable.

  9. In Campbell & Cochrane's (1999) habit model, how is the risk-free rate kept constant despite fluctuating risk premia?

    Options

    Campbell & Cochrane reverse-engineer the non-linear sensitivity function \(\lambda(s_t)\) specifically to ensure the precautionary savings effect cancels the intertemporal substitution effect.

  10. What was the average historical real risk-free rate documented by Mehra & Prescott (1985) over 1889–1978?

    Options

    Over the ninety-year sample, the real yield on short-term US Treasury bills averaged only 0.80% per year.
