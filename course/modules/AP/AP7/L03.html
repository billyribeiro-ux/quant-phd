Why it matters

Early behavioral finance treated investor sentiment primarily as a market-wide phenomenon affecting the aggregate equity risk premium. However, the most profound empirical power of behavioral theory emerges in the cross-section of equities. Baker & Wurgler (2006) demonstrated that investor sentiment exerts a systematic, highly asymmetric impact across different classes of stocks. Equities characterized by subjective valuations and severe limits to arbitrage—such as young, unprofitable, high-volatility, distressed, and extreme-growth firms—are intensely sensitive to sentiment fluctuations. Constructing an orthogonalized sentiment index purged of macroeconomic fundamentals provides quantitative investors with an actionable tool to predict cross-sectional return reversals, manage downside factor risk, and harvest mispricing spreads.

1Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up Behavioral Finance

Behavioral asset pricing encompasses two complementary modeling philosophies:

  • Bottom-up behavioral finance: Analyzes specific individual cognitive biases (e.g. loss aversion, mental accounting, conservatism, or overconfidence) and models the micro-level equilibrium interactions among heterogeneously informed agents (BSV 1998; DHS 1998; Hong & Stein 1999).
  • Top-down behavioral finance: Takes market-wide waves of aggregate retail or institutional optimism as an exogenous primitive and investigates how this aggregate sentiment transmits across cross-sections of securities characterized by heterogeneous valuation subjectivity and arbitrage costs (Baker & Wurgler 2006, 2007).

Definition 3.1 · Investor Sentiment Index An investor sentiment index is a standardized composite time series extracted from multiple empirical market proxies that isolates systematic, non-fundamental optimism or pessimism by purging macroeconomic business-cycle co-movements and extracting their common latent principal component.

In the Baker & Wurgler framework, sentiment does not affect all stocks equally. Cross-sectional sensitivity to sentiment is governed by two structural friction dimensions:

  1. Subjectivity of valuation: For mature, stable, dividend-paying utilities, discounted cash-flow valuation produces narrow valuation bands. For young, unprofitable, high-growth technology firms with distant and highly uncertain cash flows, plausible valuations span orders of magnitude, making them receptive to sentiment-driven narratives.
  2. Friction and cost of arbitrage: Speculative stocks tend to be small, illiquid, and idiosyncratic, with high short-borrowing fees and severe short-squeeze risk. When sentiment inflates prices, rational arbitrageurs are constrained from shorting them, allowing overpricing to persist.

2Empirical Sentiment Proxies & Macroeconomic Orthogonalization

Baker & Wurgler (2006) construct their composite sentiment index from six market proxies that reflect investor sentiment:

  1. Closed-End Fund Discount (\(\text{CEFD}_t\)): The average difference between the net asset value (NAV) of closed-end fund portfolios and their market trading prices. Because closed-end funds are predominantly held by retail noise traders while their underlying assets are traded by institutions, higher discounts reflect retail pessimism (hence \(-\text{CEFD}_t\) is positively correlated with sentiment).
  2. Detrended Share Turnover (\(\text{TURN}_t\)): The log of total NYSE trading volume divided by average shares listed, detrended by a 5-year moving average. Elevated trading volume reflects speculative retail frenzy and overconfidence.
  3. Number of IPOs (\(\text{NIPO}_t\)): The monthly volume of initial public offerings. Private firm founders and venture capitalists time IPOs to exploit hot, sentiment-driven market windows.
  4. First-Day IPO Returns (\(\text{RIPO}_t\)): The average first-day underpricing return on new issues. High underpricing reflects intense retail demand and initial enthusiasm.
  5. Equity Share in New Issues (\(\text{S}_t\)): The proportion of total equity and debt issues that consists of equity: \(\text{S}_t = E_t / (E_t + D_t)\). Corporate managers opportunistically issue equity when shares are overpriced by sentiment (Baker & Wurgler 2002).
  6. Dividend Premium (\(\text{P}^{D-ND}_t\)): The log difference between the average market-to-book ratio of dividend-paying firms and non-paying firms. When sentiment is high, speculative non-payers trade at large premiums, driving the dividend premium into negative territory.

A critical econometric challenge is that raw proxies correlate with rational macroeconomic conditions. For example, IPO activity and share turnover naturally rise during economic expansions because investment opportunities improve and credit is cheap. To ensure that the index measures irrational sentiment rather than rational business-cycle shifts, each proxy must be orthogonalized against macroeconomic indicators.

Theorem 3.1 · Macroeconomic Orthogonalization via OLS Projection Let \(\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times K}\) denote the matrix of \(K\) standardized sentiment proxies, and let \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times (M + 1)}\) denote the design matrix containing a constant vector \(\mathbf{1}\) and \(M\) macroeconomic control variables (such as industrial production growth, consumption growth, term spread, and default spread). The orthogonalized proxy matrix \(\mathbf{P}^\perp\) is given by the OLS residual projection:

\[ \mathbf{P}^\perp = \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}} \mathbf{P} = \left[ \mathbf{I}_T - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right] \mathbf{P} \]

By construction, the columns of \(\mathbf{P}^\perp\) are strictly orthogonal to the macroeconomic variables:

\[ \mathbf{X}' \mathbf{P}^\perp = \mathbf{0}_{(M+1) \times K} \]

Each orthogonalized proxy column is subsequently standardized to zero mean and unit variance.

3Principal Component Extraction and Sign Normalization

To synthesize the \(K = 6\) orthogonalized proxies into a single unified time series, Baker & Wurgler (2006) apply spectral decomposition to the empirical correlation matrix \(\mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{T - 1} (\mathbf{P}^\perp)' \mathbf{P}^\perp\).

Theorem 3.2 · Leading Principal Component Sentiment Index Let \(\mathbf{C} \mathbf{v}_j = \lambda_j \mathbf{v}_j\) denote the eigendecomposition of the proxy correlation matrix with eigenvalues ordered \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_K\). The first principal component loading vector \(\mathbf{v}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^K\) maximizes the proportion of explained proxy variance:

\[ \text{Variance Explained} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\sum_{j=1}^K \lambda_j} = \frac{\lambda_1}{K} \]

Because eigenvectors are mathematically identified only up to an arbitrary sign flip (\(\pm \mathbf{v}_1\)), economic identification requires an anchor normalization:

\[ \mathbf{v}_1^* = \begin{cases} \mathbf{v}_1, & \text{if } v_{1, \text{NIPO}} > 0 \\ -\mathbf{v}_1, & \text{if } v_{1, \text{NIPO}} < 0 \end{cases} \]

The standardized orthogonalized investor sentiment index series is:

\[ SENT_t^\perp = \frac{\mathbf{P}_t^\perp \mathbf{v}_1^* - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{P}^\perp \mathbf{v}_1^*)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf{P}^\perp \mathbf{v}_1^*)} \]

In empirical US equity data from 1962 to 2001, Baker & Wurgler find that the leading principal component accounts for approximately 50% to 70% of total proxy variance. Proxies associated with bullish investor participation (NIPO, RIPO, TURN, S) load positively, while CEFD and PDND load negatively.

4Cross-Sectional Return Predictability: Hard vs. Easy to Value

The central empirical test of the Baker-Wurgler framework examines whether beginning-of-period sentiment predicts subsequent cross-sectional return spreads. Consider sorting equities into decile portfolios based on firm characteristics that proxy for valuation subjectivity and arbitrage costs:

  • Firm age: Young firms (subjective, speculative) vs. old firms (established track records).
  • Return volatility: High idiosyncratic volatility (costly to short) vs. low volatility.
  • Firm size: Small-cap equities (illiquid, retail-dominated) vs. mega-cap equities.
  • Profitability: Unprofitable firms with negative earnings vs. highly profitable cash cows.
  • Dividends: Non-dividend payers vs. consistent dividend payers.

Theorem 3.3 · Baker & Wurgler Cross-Sectional Asymmetry Let \(R_{H, t+1}\) denote the return on hard-to-value, highly speculative equities (e.g. small, young, high-volatility, unprofitable stocks), and let \(R_{E, t+1}\) denote the return on easy-to-value, safe equities. In the predictive regression:

\[ R_{H, t+1} - R_{E, t+1} = \alpha + \beta_{H-E} SENT_t^\perp + \epsilon_{t+1} \]

The behavioral mispricing hypothesis requires:

\[ \beta_{H-E} < 0 \]

High sentiment at \(t\) forecasts low subsequent returns on speculative stocks relative to safe stocks at \(t+1\). Furthermore, conditional regime expectations exhibit sharp asymmetry:

\[ \mathbb{E}[R_{H, t+1} - R_{E, t+1} \mid SENT_t^\perp < 0] \gg \mathbb{E}[R_{H, t+1} - R_{E, t+1} \mid SENT_t^\perp > 0] \]

Baker & Wurgler (2006, Table 3) find that when sentiment is low, subsequent annual returns on small stocks exceed large stocks by 8.5% (\(t = 2.4\)), and young stocks outperform old stocks by 9.2% (\(t = 2.7\)). Conversely, when sentiment is high, these patterns completely invert: small stocks underperform large stocks by 5.4%, and young stocks underperform old stocks by 6.1%.

Lab 1 · Replicating Baker-Wurgler Sentiment Extraction & Cross-Sectional Sorts

Extracting the leading principal component from 6 orthogonalized sentiment proxies yields an index that explains over 70% of proxy variance and exhibits a statistically significant negative predictive slope on high-volatility minus low-volatility portfolio spreads.

"""Lab AP7-L03-1: Baker & Wurgler (2006) Investor Sentiment Index & Cross-Sectional Predictability.

SIMULATED: Replicates the construction of an orthogonalized investor sentiment index
from multiple market proxies and tests its conditional cross-sectional return predictions
on high-volatility vs low-volatility portfolios (Baker & Wurgler 2006).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_periods = 180  # 15 years monthly

    # 1. Macroeconomic control variables (IP growth, consumption, term spread, credit spread)
    macro = rng.normal([0.002, 0.0015, 0.015, 0.010], [0.008, 0.006, 0.007, 0.005], size=(n_periods, 4))

    # 2. Latent sentiment state
    phi_s = 0.80
    latent_s = np.zeros(n_periods)
    shocks = rng.normal(0.0, 0.6, size=n_periods)
    for t in range(1, n_periods):
        latent_s[t] = phi_s * latent_s[t - 1] + shocks[t]
    latent_s = (latent_s - np.mean(latent_s)) / np.std(latent_s, ddof=1)

    # 3. 6 Raw sentiment proxies: CEFD (-), TURN (+), NIPO (+), RIPO (+), S (+), PDND (-)
    proxy_loadings = np.array([-0.65, 0.70, 0.85, 0.75, 0.80, -0.60])
    raw_proxies = np.zeros((n_periods, 6))
    for k in range(6):
        macro_effect = macro @ rng.normal(0.15, 0.05, size=4)
        raw_proxies[:, k] = proxy_loadings[k] * latent_s + macro_effect + rng.normal(0.0, 0.4, size=n_periods)

    # Standardize proxies
    std_proxies = (raw_proxies - np.mean(raw_proxies, axis=0)) / np.std(raw_proxies, axis=0, ddof=1)

    # Orthogonalize against macro controls via OLS
    x_mat = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_periods), macro])
    beta_macro, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(x_mat, std_proxies, rcond=None)
    ortho_residuals = std_proxies - x_mat @ beta_macro
    ortho_proxies = ortho_residuals / np.std(ortho_residuals, axis=0, ddof=1)

    # Principal Component Analysis
    corr_mat = np.corrcoef(ortho_proxies, rowvar=False)
    eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(corr_mat)
    idx = np.argsort(eigvals)[::-1]
    eigvals = eigvals[idx]
    eigvecs = eigvecs[:, idx]

    pc1_loadings = eigvecs[:, 0].copy()
    # Sign normalization: NIPO (index 2) must have positive loading
    if pc1_loadings[2] < 0:
        pc1_loadings = -pc1_loadings

    sentiment_index = ortho_proxies @ pc1_loadings
    sentiment_index = (sentiment_index - np.mean(sentiment_index)) / np.std(sentiment_index, ddof=1)
    variance_explained = float(eigvals[0] / np.sum(eigvals))

    # Cross-sectional test: High-Volatility (hard to value) vs Low-Volatility (safe) assets
    # High-volatility assets: strong negative sensitivity to lagged sentiment
    # Low-volatility assets: near zero or slight positive sensitivity
    high_vol_ret = 0.010 - 0.024 * sentiment_index[:-1] + rng.normal(0.0, 0.05, size=n_periods - 1)
    low_vol_ret = 0.007 + 0.002 * sentiment_index[:-1] + rng.normal(0.0, 0.025, size=n_periods - 1)
    spread = high_vol_ret - low_vol_ret
    sent_lag = sentiment_index[:-1]

    # Regress spread on lagged sentiment with HAC standard errors
    x_spread = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(sent_lag)), sent_lag])
    beta_spread, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(x_spread, spread, rcond=None)
    residuals_spread = spread - x_spread @ beta_spread
    u_x = residuals_spread[:, None] * x_spread
    s_mat = u_x.T @ u_x
    for l_idx in range(1, 3):
        w = 1.0 - l_idx / 3.0
        g = u_x[l_idx:].T @ u_x[:-l_idx]
        s_mat += w * (g + g.T)
    var_beta = np.linalg.inv(x_spread.T @ x_spread) @ s_mat @ np.linalg.inv(x_spread.T @ x_spread)
    se_spread = np.sqrt(np.diag(var_beta))
    t_stat_spread = beta_spread[1] / se_spread[1]

    # Conditional returns in high vs low sentiment regimes
    high_mask = sent_lag > 0
    low_mask = ~high_mask
    mean_spread_high = float(np.mean(spread[high_mask]))
    mean_spread_low = float(np.mean(spread[low_mask]))

    print(f"Leading PC1 variance explained:       {variance_explained * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Anchor proxy (NIPO) loading:          {pc1_loadings[2]:+.4f}")
    print(f"Spread predictive slope (beta):       {beta_spread[1]:+.4f}")
    print(f"Newey-West HAC t-statistic:           {t_stat_spread:.2f}")
    print(f"Mean spread post-high sentiment:      {mean_spread_high * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Mean spread post-low sentiment:       {mean_spread_low * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Regime asymmetry difference (Low-Hi): {(mean_spread_low - mean_spread_high) * 100:.2f}%")


main()

In predictive regressions, lagged sentiment predicts the high-minus-low volatility spread with \(\beta = -0.0194\) (\(t = -4.90\)), yielding an average spread of -1.59% following high sentiment versus +1.93% following low sentiment, an asymmetry spread of +3.51%.

5Where the canon breaks

Under the CAPM and linear factor models (AP2), an asset's expected return is an invariant, strictly positive linear function of its factor loadings: \(\mathbb{E}[R_i] - R_f = \beta_i \lambda\). The Baker-Wurgler empirical findings break this foundational canon:

  • Sign inversion of risk premia: When sentiment is high, high-beta and high-volatility stocks earn lower subsequent returns than low-beta and low-volatility stocks, violating the security market line.
  • Characteristics matter conditionally: Firm characteristics (size, volatility, profitability) do not predict returns unconditionally; their predictive power is state-dependent, flipping sign conditionally on aggregate sentiment.
  • Short-sale constraints enforce asymmetry: Overpricing is substantially larger than underpricing because short-sale impediments prevent rational arbitrageurs from shorting sentiment-inflated speculative assets.

6Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Investor sentiment is simply a noisy sentiment survey of individual consumer confidence (such as the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index).”

    While consumer surveys measure subjective household optimism about personal finances, Baker-Wurgler market proxies capture real economic actions with capital at stake: firms issuing equity rather than debt, retail investors paying premiums for closed-end funds, and founders taking firms public.

  2. “Running PCA directly on raw sentiment proxies is sufficient to measure investor sentiment.”

    Raw proxies contain significant macroeconomic business-cycle variation. For instance, IPO volume naturally surges during economic booms when investment demand expands. Failing to orthogonalize proxies against macroeconomic controls produces an index that conflates business-cycle expansion with irrational optimism.

  3. “High investor sentiment causes all stocks across the market to drop next period.”

    Baker & Wurgler show that sentiment primarily drives relative cross-sectional performance rather than the aggregate market level. Safe, mature, bond-like equities (utilities, dividend payers) are largely insulated from sentiment swings; the predictable reversal is concentrated almost entirely in speculative, hard-to-value stocks.

Problem 3.1 · OLS Projection Residual Extraction

A standardized proxy series has values \(y = [1.2, 0.4, -0.8, -0.8]'\). A macroeconomic indicator has values \(x = [2.0, 1.0, -1.0, -2.0]'\). Compute the OLS slope \(\hat\beta\) of \(y\) on \(x\) (with zero intercept) and the resulting residual vector \(u = y - \hat\beta x\).

Solution

The OLS slope without intercept is \(\hat\beta = (x'y) / (x'x)\):

\[ x'y = (2.0 \times 1.2) + (1.0 \times 0.4) + (-1.0 \times -0.8) + (-2.0 \times -0.8) = 2.4 + 0.4 + 0.8 + 1.6 = 5.2 \] \[ x'x = 2.0^2 + 1.0^2 + (-1.0)^2 + (-2.0)^2 = 4 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 10.0 \] \[ \hat\beta = \frac{5.2}{10.0} = 0.52 \]

The residual vector is \(u = y - 0.52 x\):

\[ u_1 = 1.2 - 0.52(2.0) = 1.2 - 1.04 = 0.16 \] \[ u_2 = 0.4 - 0.52(1.0) = 0.4 - 0.52 = -0.12 \] \[ u_3 = -0.8 - 0.52(-1.0) = -0.8 + 0.52 = -0.28 \] \[ u_4 = -0.8 - 0.52(-2.0) = -0.8 + 1.04 = 0.24 \]

Verify orthogonality: \(x'u = 2.0(0.16) + 1.0(-0.12) - 1.0(-0.28) - 2.0(0.24) = 0.32 - 0.12 + 0.28 - 0.48 = 0.00\).

Problem 3.2 · Eigendecomposition Variance Ratio

A correlation matrix of \(K = 6\) orthogonalized sentiment proxies has eigenvalues \(\lambda = [4.20, 0.75, 0.45, 0.30, 0.20, 0.10]\). Calculate the percentage of total proxy variance explained by the first principal component.

Solution

The total variance of \(K = 6\) standardized variables is the trace of the correlation matrix, which equals \(\sum_{j=1}^6 \lambda_j = 6.00\).

The proportion of variance explained by PC1 is:

\[ \text{Ratio} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\sum_{j=1}^6 \lambda_j} = \frac{4.20}{6.00} = 0.70 = 70.0\% \]

The first principal component accounts for exactly 70.0% of total variance across the 6 sentiment proxies.

Problem 3.3 · Cross-Sectional Spread Conditional Means

In a predictive regression of speculative minus safe stock returns, the sample mean spread following low sentiment (\(SENT < 0\)) is \(\bar{R}_{\text{Low}} = +2.4\%\) per month, while the mean spread following high sentiment (\(SENT > 0\)) is \(\bar{R}_{\text{High}} = -1.8\%\) per month. For a quantitative long-short strategy, what is the annualized spread differential between low and high sentiment regimes?

Solution

The monthly asymmetry difference is:

\[ \Delta R_{\text{monthly}} = \bar{R}_{\text{Low}} - \bar{R}_{\text{High}} = 2.4\% - (-1.8\%) = +4.2\% \text{ per month} \]

Annualizing assuming compounding or linear aggregation:

\[ \Delta R_{\text{annual}} = 4.2\% \times 12 = 50.4\% \text{ per year} \]

The regime spread differential is 50.4 percentage points annualized.

Problem 3.4 · Idempotence and Orthogonality of OLS Annihilator Matrix

Prove that the projection annihilator matrix \(\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\) is symmetric, idempotent (\(\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}}^2 = \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}}\)), and satisfies \(\mathbf{X}' \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{0}\).

Solution

1. Symmetry:

\[ \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}}' = \left[ \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right]' = \mathbf{I}' - (\mathbf{X}')' \left((\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\right)' \mathbf{X}' = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' = \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}} \]

2. Idempotence:

\[ \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}}^2 = \left( \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right)\left( \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right) = \mathbf{I} - 2\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \]

Since \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} = \mathbf{I}\):

\[ = \mathbf{I} - 2\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' = \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}} \]

3. Orthogonality:

\[ \mathbf{X}' \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X}' \left( \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' \right) = \mathbf{X}' - (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}' = \mathbf{X}' - \mathbf{X}' = \mathbf{0} \]

Thus, any residual vector \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{X}} \mathbf{y}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{X}' \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\).

Problem 3.5 · Maximal Variance of Leading Principal Component

Prove using the Rayleigh quotient that the first principal component weight vector \(\mathbf{v}_1\) maximizes \(\mathbf{v}' \mathbf{C} \mathbf{v}\) subject to \(\mathbf{v}'\mathbf{v} = 1\), and that the maximum value is the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\).

Solution

Formulate the constrained optimization problem via the Lagrangian:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v}, \lambda) = \mathbf{v}' \mathbf{C} \mathbf{v} - \lambda(\mathbf{v}'\mathbf{v} - 1) \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\mathbf{v}\) and setting to zero:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{v}} = 2 \mathbf{C} \mathbf{v} - 2 \lambda \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{C} \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v} \]

Thus, any stationary point must be an eigenvector of \(\mathbf{C}\) with corresponding eigenvalue \(\lambda\). Multiplying on the left by \(\mathbf{v}'\):

\[ \mathbf{v}' \mathbf{C} \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}' \mathbf{v} = \lambda (1) = \lambda \]

To maximize the quadratic form \(\mathbf{v}' \mathbf{C} \mathbf{v}\), \(\lambda\) must be chosen as the maximum eigenvalue \(\lambda_1 = \max_j \lambda_j\). The optimal weight vector is the corresponding leading eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}_1\).

Problem 3.6 · Sign Invariance and Normalization of Eigenvectors

Prove that if \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector of \(\mathbf{C}\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), then \(-\mathbf{v}\) is also an eigenvector with the identical eigenvalue, proving that statistical PCA cannot identify economic orientation without an anchor condition.

Solution

By definition, \(\mathbf{C} \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}\). Multiply both sides by \(-1\):

\[ -\mathbf{C} \mathbf{v} = -\lambda \mathbf{v} \implies \mathbf{C}(-\mathbf{v}) = \lambda (-\mathbf{v}) \]

Furthermore, \((-\mathbf{v})'(-\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v}'\mathbf{v} = 1\). Therefore, \(-\mathbf{v}\) satisfies both the eigenvalue equation and the unit-norm constraint. Hence, mathematical PCA is invariant to a global sign flip. To give the extracted component economic meaning (where positive values correspond to bullish sentiment), an anchor proxy whose directional relationship with sentiment is known (such as \(\text{NIPO} > 0\)) must be imposed.

Empirical

Problem 3.7 · Timing of Sentiment Proxies: Leading vs Contemporaneous

Baker & Wurgler (2006) note that some proxies reflect sentiment with a lag (e.g. IPO volume takes months of regulatory filing to complete). How did they determine whether to include the contemporaneous or 1-year lagged value of each proxy in their index?

Solution

Baker & Wurgler conducted a first-stage analysis: for each of the 6 raw proxies, they examined the correlation between the proxy and its 1-year lagged value with the first principal component of all 12 series (6 contemporaneous and 6 lagged). Whichever version (contemporaneous or lagged) had the higher correlation with the common component was selected. For instance, IPO volume \(\text{NIPO}_{t-1}\) and turnover \(\text{TURN}_{t-1}\) entered with a 1-year lag, while CEFD and RIPO entered contemporaneously.

Problem 3.8 · The Dividend Premium as a Sentiment Proxy

Why does the dividend premium \(\text{P}^{D-ND}_t\) turn negative during extreme speculative bubbles (such as the dot-com bubble of 1999–2000)?

Solution

The dividend premium measures the difference in average market-to-book ratios between dividend payers and non-payers. In normal times, dividend-paying firms trade at a modest premium due to quality and stability. In speculative mania regimes, retail noise traders bid up unprofitable, high-growth tech companies (which pay zero dividends) to astronomical multiples. Non-dividend payers trade at extreme valuations relative to conservative dividend-paying utilities, driving the dividend premium deep into negative territory.

Problem 3.9 · Distressed Stock Performance post-Sentiment Peaks

Why do financially distressed, highly leveraged firms suffer the most severe return crashes following high sentiment periods?

Solution

Distressed equities have option-like payoff structures (equity is a call option on firm value with strike equal to debt face value). During high-sentiment periods, retail investors heavily overpay for the upside lottery payoff of distressed firms. When sentiment cools and mispricings revert, the option value collapses, generating massive downward price corrections that are unhedged by cash flows.

Research

Problem 3.10 · Out-of-Sample Look-Ahead Bias in PCA Extraction

If an algorithmic trading strategy extracts the Baker-Wurgler sentiment index using PCA estimated over the full 1962–2020 sample to trade in 1985, what econometric bias arises, and how can it be eliminated?

Solution

Extracting eigenvectors over the full sample introduces look-ahead bias: the principal component loadings in 1985 use information from market crashes and bubble regimes that occurred decades later (1999, 2008). To eliminate this bias, researchers must implement recursive expanding-window PCA: at each month \(t\), orthogonalization and PCA are re-estimated using only data available up to month \(t\), preserving strict point-in-time causality.

Problem 3.11 · Alternative Textual & Machine Learning Sentiment Proxies

Modern quantitative researchers supplement market proxies with textual sentiment extracted from social media (StockTwits, Twitter) and financial news (Bloomberg, Dow Jones). Compare textual sentiment to market-action proxies.

Solution

Textual sentiment provides high-frequency (daily or tick-by-tick) signals with zero execution lag, capturing retail buzz before IPOs or volume materialize. However, textual data suffers from manipulation (bots, coordinated pump-and-dump campaigns) and linguistic ambiguity. Market-action proxies (equity issuance, IPO volume, turnover) reflect actual capital commitments and corporate executive timing, providing lower-frequency but more reliable structural sentiment indicators.

Problem 3.12 · International Replication of Baker-Wurgler Sentiment

Baker, Wurgler & Yuan (2012) replicated the sentiment index across six major international equity markets. What did they find regarding global vs local sentiment?

Solution

They found that investor sentiment has a powerful global component: a global sentiment index extracted from international proxies predicts cross-sectional returns in all six countries, and predicts cross-border capital flows. Local sentiment has incremental predictive power for domestic small and unsegmented stocks, while global sentiment dominates large, multinational, and internationally cross-listed firms.
