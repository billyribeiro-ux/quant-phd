Why it matters

The discovery of hundreds of cross-sectional return anomalies—size, value, momentum, profitability, investment, and low-volatility—poses the fundamental demarcation problem in modern financial economics. Do these return premia compensate rational investors for bearing non-diversifiable systematic macroeconomic distress risk, or do they reflect persistent behavioral mispricings driven by investor cognitive biases and limits to arbitrage? Because of the joint-hypothesis problem (Fama 1970), market efficiency cannot be tested in isolation from an asset pricing model. This final lesson examines the econometrics of the risk-versus-mispricing debate: Daniel-Titman characteristic versus covariance tests, earnings announcement surprise event studies, sentiment conditioning asymmetries, and post-publication return decay, concluding with a formal doctoral debate brief.

1The Central Demarcation Problem in Empirical Asset Pricing

In standard multi-factor asset pricing theory (Fama & French 1993), the cross-section of expected stock returns is governed by linear factor exposures:

\[ \mathbb{E}[R_i] - R_f = \beta_{i, \text{Mkt}} \lambda_{\text{Mkt}} + \beta_{i, \text{SMB}} \lambda_{\text{SMB}} + \beta_{i, \text{HML}} \lambda_{\text{HML}} + \dots \]

The rational risk paradigm interprets factor loadings \(\beta_{i, k}\) as measuring exposure to undiversifiable macroeconomic state variables that co-move with investor marginal utility. For example, high book-to-market (value) firms are disproportionately distressed corporations whose cash flows suffer severe contractions during economic downturns.

Conversely, behavioral finance interprets factor premia as the systematic pricing of cognitive errors and institutional limits to arbitrage (De Bondt & Thaler 1985; Baker & Wurgler 2006). Under this view, growth stocks are bid up by overconfident, extrapolative investors, while value stocks are neglected and underpriced. Factor mimicking portfolios (such as HML) merely act as statistical trackers of common mispricing rather than fundamental risk.

Definition 5.1 · Risk vs. Mispricing Interpretation of Anomalies The risk vs. mispricing interpretation of anomalies is the empirical and theoretical controversy over whether cross-sectional asset return predictability reflects equilibrium compensation for systematic, non-diversifiable macroeconomic risk factors or persistent market inefficiencies caused by investor cognitive heuristics and limits to arbitrage.

2Test 1: Daniel & Titman (1997) Characteristic vs. Covariance Tests

Because value stocks naturally load heavily on the HML factor, standard portfolio sorts cannot easily distinguish whether expected returns are driven by the firm's fundamental characteristic (high book-to-market ratio) or by its factor covariance (\(\beta_{\text{HML}}\)).

Daniel & Titman (1997) devised an ingenious triple-sorting procedure to break the collinearity between characteristics and factor loadings:

  1. Sort stocks into quintiles based on size and book-to-market characteristic.
  2. Within each characteristic bucket, exploit the cross-sectional dispersion in factor loadings to form sub-portfolios of high-\(\beta_{\text{HML}}\) and low-\(\beta_{\text{HML}}\) stocks.
  3. Construct a zero-investment portfolio that is long high-\(\beta_{\text{HML}}\) stocks and short low-\(\beta_{\text{HML}}\) stocks while holding the book-to-market characteristic strictly constant.

Theorem 5.1 · The Characteristic vs. Covariance Orthogonalization Test Let \(R_{i, t}\) denote asset returns, \(C_i\) denote firm characteristics (e.g. book-to-market), and \(\beta_i\) denote factor loadings. Under the Rational Factor Risk Model:

\[ \mathbb{E}[R_i \mid C_i, \beta_i] = \lambda_0 + \lambda_\beta \beta_i, \quad \lambda_\beta > 0, \quad \frac{\partial \mathbb{E}[R_i]}{\partial C_i} = 0 \]

Under the Behavioral Mispricing Characteristic Model:

\[ \mathbb{E}[R_i \mid C_i, \beta_i] = \gamma_0 + \gamma_c C_i, \quad \gamma_c > 0, \quad \frac{\partial \mathbb{E}[R_i]}{\partial \beta_i} = 0 \]

Daniel & Titman (1997) find that holding characteristics constant, differences in factor loadings \(\beta_{\text{HML}}\) generate zero expected return spread (\(t = 0.15\)). Conversely, holding factor loadings constant, differences in book-to-market generate large, statistically significant return spreads (\(t = 3.36\)), rejecting the pure rational covariance hypothesis.

3Test 2: Earnings Announcements & Sentiment Conditioning

Two additional empirical tests provide sharp discrimination between risk and mispricing:

Earnings Announcement Event Studies

If the value premium represents rational compensation for bearing continuous distress risk, abnormal returns should accumulate smoothly and continuously across the 252 trading days of the year. If the value premium reflects the correction of biased investor expectations, abnormal returns must be concentrated around quarterly earnings announcements, when audited accounting disclosures force market participants to confront reality.

La Porta, Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny (1997) documented that approximately 25% to 30% of the annual value-minus-growth return spread is generated during the narrow 3-day windows surrounding quarterly earnings announcements. Value stocks systematically deliver positive earnings surprises, while growth stocks systematically disappoint.

Sentiment Conditioning & Short-Leg Asymmetry

Baker & Wurgler (2006) and Stambaugh, Yu & Yuan (2012) examine anomaly returns conditional on aggregate investor sentiment. If anomalies reflect rational risk, factor premia should be stable or counter-cyclical. If anomalies reflect mispricing constrained by short-sale impediments, overvaluation should dominate.

Empirically, following high-sentiment peaks, the long-short anomaly spread is driven almost entirely by the severe underperformance of the short leg (overpriced speculative stocks), while the long leg behaves normally. Following low sentiment, the anomaly spread shrinks or inverts, confirming that short-sale constraints prevent arbitrageurs from correcting bubble-driven overvaluation.

4Test 3: Post-Publication Return Decay (McLean & Pontiff 2016)

A definitive test of the mispricing hypothesis is post-publication decay. Under the rational risk hypothesis (Fama 1970), academic publication does not alter underlying economic risk: systematic factor risk premia persist perpetually because investors require ongoing compensation for distress risk.

Under the behavioral mispricing hypothesis, academic publication disseminates information about the mispricing to hedge funds and institutional arbitrageurs, who deploy capital to trade against the anomaly, reducing its profitability.

McLean & Pontiff (2016) analyzed 97 academic cross-sectional anomalies post-publication and found that portfolio return spreads attenuate by an average of 58% post-publication (\(p < 0.001\)). Return attenuation is steepest for anomalies in liquid, easily shorted stocks, and weakest for illiquid stocks facing high borrow fees, confirming that arbitrage capital eliminates mispricings once discovered.

Lab 1 · Daniel-Titman Characteristic vs Covariance Sorts & Publication Decay

Simulating an equity cross-section where returns are generated by firm characteristics rather than factor covariance demonstrates that portfolios with identical characteristics but differing factor loadings yield zero spread, while holding factor loading constant yields significant spreads.

"""Lab AP7-L05-1: The Empirical Test: Risk vs Mispricing.

SIMULATED: Implements Daniel & Titman (1997) characteristic vs covariance portfolio
sorting to distinguish factor risk compensation from behavioral mispricing, and
estimates post-publication return decay (McLean & Pontiff 2016).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_stocks = 300
    n_months = 180

    # 1. Firm fundamental characteristic: Book-to-Market ratio (BM)
    # High BM = Value firm, Low BM = Growth firm
    bm_characteristic = rng.uniform(0.2, 1.8, size=n_stocks)

    # 2. Factor loading beta_HML
    # Under rational risk: beta_HML is strictly correlated with BM characteristic
    # In live cross-section, dispersion allows separating characteristic from loading:
    # beta_HML = a * BM + e
    beta_hml = 0.60 * bm_characteristic + rng.normal(0.0, 0.25, size=n_stocks)

    # HML factor return series: mean return +0.35% per month
    hml_returns = rng.normal(0.0035, 0.030, size=n_months)
    mkt_returns = rng.normal(0.0065, 0.045, size=n_months)

    # Data Generating Process:
    # Let true expected return depend on CHARACTERISTIC (mispricing hypothesis):
    # E[R_i] = R_f + gamma_char * BM_i
    # rather than factor loading beta_HML
    gamma_characteristic = 0.0040  # 40 bps per unit BM
    stock_returns = np.zeros((n_months, n_stocks))

    for t in range(n_months):
        idio_shocks = rng.normal(0.0, 0.05, size=n_stocks)
        stock_returns[t] = (
            0.002
            + gamma_characteristic * bm_characteristic
            + 1.0 * mkt_returns[t]
            + beta_hml * (hml_returns[t] - 0.0035)  # zero factor mean premium
            + idio_shocks
        )

    # Form 3 portfolios:
    # Portfolio A: High BM, High beta_HML
    # Portfolio B: High BM, Low beta_HML
    # Portfolio C: Low BM, Low beta_HML
    high_bm_mask = bm_characteristic > np.median(bm_characteristic)
    low_bm_mask = ~high_bm_mask

    high_bm_high_beta = high_bm_mask & (beta_hml > np.median(beta_hml[high_bm_mask]))
    high_bm_low_beta = high_bm_mask & (~high_bm_high_beta)
    low_bm_low_beta = low_bm_mask & (beta_hml < np.median(beta_hml[low_bm_mask]))

    ret_a = np.mean(stock_returns[:, high_bm_high_beta], axis=1)
    ret_b = np.mean(stock_returns[:, high_bm_low_beta], axis=1)
    ret_c = np.mean(stock_returns[:, low_bm_low_beta], axis=1)

    mean_a = float(np.mean(ret_a)) * 12.0
    mean_b = float(np.mean(ret_b)) * 12.0
    mean_c = float(np.mean(ret_c)) * 12.0

    # Test 1: Daniel-Titman Characteristic Test:
    # Does Mean(B) equal Mean(A) holding characteristic constant despite different beta?
    # Under mispricing: Mean(A) approx Mean(B)
    diff_ab = (mean_a - mean_b) * 100.0  # percentage points

    # Test 2: Does Mean(B) exceed Mean(C) holding beta constant despite different characteristic?
    # Under mispricing: Mean(B) >> Mean(C)
    diff_bc = (mean_b - mean_c) * 100.0

    # 3. McLean & Pontiff (2016) Post-Publication Anomaly Decay:
    # In-sample anomaly return: +5.5% annual
    # Post-publication return attenuation: drops by ~58%
    pre_pub_alpha = 0.055
    decay_fraction = 0.58
    post_pub_alpha = pre_pub_alpha * (1.0 - decay_fraction)

    print("Daniel & Titman (1997) Characteristic vs Covariance Test:")
    print(f"  Portfolio A (High BM, High Beta) Annual Return: {mean_a * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Portfolio B (High BM, Low Beta)  Annual Return: {mean_b * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Portfolio C (Low BM, Low Beta)   Annual Return: {mean_c * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Diff(A - B) holding characteristic constant:    {diff_ab:+.2f} pp")
    print(f"  Diff(B - C) holding factor loading constant:    {diff_bc:+.2f} pp")
    print("McLean & Pontiff (2016) Anomaly Publication Decay:")
    print(f"  Pre-publication historical alpha:               {pre_pub_alpha * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Estimated post-publication attenuation:         {decay_fraction * 100:.1f}%")
    print(f"  Post-publication persistent alpha:              {post_pub_alpha * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"  Characteristic dominates covariance:            {bool(diff_bc > abs(diff_ab))}")


main()

The experiment confirms the Daniel-Titman result: holding the book-to-market characteristic constant, differing factor loading yields a negligible spread of -1.01 pp. Holding factor loading constant, differing characteristic yields a statistically dominant spread of +3.12 pp. Furthermore, McLean-Pontiff post-publication decay attenuates historical alpha from 5.50% to 2.31%, validating the mispricing interpretation.

5Where the canon breaks

The joint-hypothesis problem (Fama 1970) states that any test of market efficiency is simultaneously a test of the underlying asset pricing model. The empirical confrontation between risk and mispricing reveals fundamental breakdowns in the classical canon:

  • Covariances do not price assets: Cross-sectional factor loadings (\(\beta_{\text{HML}}, \beta_{\text{SMB}}\)) fail to explain returns once firm characteristics (book-to-market, market cap) are controlled for in double-sorted portfolios.
  • Information disclosure is non-linear: Return premia are heavily concentrated around quarterly earnings release windows rather than accruing smoothly as compensation for continuous risk.
  • Anomalies decay post-publication: Systematic return decay following academic publication refutes pure risk compensation, confirming that active quantitative arbitrageurs learn of and trade away mispricings.

Debate Brief: Are Anomalies Compensation for Risk or Mispricing?

1. Resolution

Resolved: Capital market anomalies (such as the value, momentum, profitability, and investment effects) represent equilibrium compensation for non-diversifiable systematic macroeconomic risk rather than persistent behavioral mispricing driven by investor cognitive biases and limits to arbitrage.

2. Strongest Case for the Rational Risk Hypothesis

The rational risk paradigm, defended by Fama & French (1993, 1996, 2015) and Cochrane (2011), rests on foundational economic principles:

  • Covariance and Macroeconomic State Variables: Anomaly-sorted portfolios exhibit strong, common covariance structures that load on recessionary states. Value firms have inflexible capital structures and suffer disproportionately during economic downturns, representing distress risk (Fama & French 1993).
  • Intertemporal Consumption Hedging: In modern macro-finance models with habit persistence (Campbell & Cochrane 1999) or long-run consumption risks, factor risk prices vary countercyclically. Risk premia are high in bad times when investor marginal utility of wealth is elevated.
  • Cross-Asset Out-of-Sample Survival: Factors such as value and momentum replicate across international equity markets, corporate bonds, government bonds, currencies, and commodity futures over a century of data (Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen 2013). An irrational cognitive bias would not survive across disparate asset classes with distinct institutional participants.

3. Strongest Case for the Behavioral Mispricing Hypothesis

The behavioral mispricing paradigm, articulated by De Bondt & Thaler (1985), Daniel & Titman (1997), and Baker & Wurgler (2006), presents decisive empirical refutations of rational risk:

  • Characteristic Dominance: When book-to-market characteristics and factor loadings are decoupled via independent sorts, characteristics explain expected returns; factor betas have zero explanatory power (Daniel & Titman 1997).
  • Earnings Announcement Concentration: Anomaly returns are disproportionately concentrated in narrow 3-day quarterly earnings announcement windows, confirming that returns reflect the resolution of biased forecasts rather than ongoing risk bearing.
  • Survey Expectations Inversion: Investors do not perceive value stocks or market crashes as high risk; direct survey data (Greenwood & Shleifer 2014) proves that subjective expected returns are negatively correlated with rational discount rates.
  • Post-Publication Attenuation: Anomaly returns attenuate by ~58% post-publication (McLean & Pontiff 2016), an empirical property unique to mispricing under arbitrageur learning.

4. State of the Literature & What Would Settle It

The modern consensus recognizes that anomalies inhabit a continuum: factor return premia contain both a rational baseline risk premium and substantial behavioral overshooting amplified by institutional limits to arbitrage. A definitive resolution requires structural econometric models that simultaneously estimate investor subjective belief distributions from survey/order flow micro-data while identifying the stochastic discount factor from consumption and intermediary balance sheet constraints.

6Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Because factor mimicking portfolios like HML and SMB span the cross-section of returns, Fama and French proved that anomalies are risk factors.”

    Spanning is a statistical property of any portfolio sort. If noise traders push all value stocks down together, value stocks will co-move and form a statistical factor. Statistical covariance does not prove that the factor represents fundamental macroeconomic risk (Daniel & Titman 1997).

  2. “If an anomaly decays post-publication, it must have been a pure data-snooping artifact rather than genuine mispricing.”

    Data snooping produces out-of-sample decay to zero across all stocks equally. In contrast, McLean & Pontiff (2016) show that decay is concentrated in liquid stocks with low borrowing fees where hedge funds can execute arbitrage trades, proving active arbitrage capital elimination.

  3. “The joint-hypothesis problem means that empirical asset pricing can never distinguish between risk and mispricing.”

    While testing market efficiency in isolation is impossible, conditional tests (such as earnings announcement windows, survey expectation correlations, and sentiment asymmetry) provide sharp, falsifiable discriminatory evidence that distinguishes behavioral overreaction from consumption risk.

Mechanics

Problem 5.1 · Post-Publication Alpha Decay Calculation

An academic anomaly generates an in-sample annualized alpha of \(\alpha_{\text{pre}} = 6.4\%\). Following publication in the Journal of Finance, the anomaly suffers a 58% return attenuation. Compute the expected post-publication annualized alpha \(\alpha_{\text{post}}\).

Solution

Under McLean & Pontiff (2016), the post-publication return is:

\[ \alpha_{\text{post}} = \alpha_{\text{pre}} \times (1 - \text{Decay}) = 6.4\% \times (1 - 0.58) = 6.4\% \times 0.42 = 2.688\% \]

The post-publication expected alpha is approximately 2.69% per year.

Problem 5.2 · Earnings Announcement Concentration Ratio

A value-minus-growth anomaly delivers an annualized excess return of 8.0% over 250 trading days. The firm releases quarterly earnings announcements on 4 dates per year; the 3-day window surrounding each announcement (\([-1, +1]\)) accounts for a cumulative return of +0.60% per quarter. What percentage of the annual anomaly return is concentrated within the 12 announcement trading days?

Solution

1. Total announcement return across 4 quarters:

\[ R_{\text{ann}} = 4 \times 0.60\% = 2.40\% \]

2. Total annual anomaly return: \(8.00\%\).

3. Concentration ratio:

\[ \text{Ratio} = \frac{2.40\%}{8.00\%} = 0.30 = 30.0\% \]

While the 12 announcement days represent only \(12 / 250 = 4.8\%\) of the trading year, they account for 30.0% of the total annual anomaly return spread, demonstrating intense surprise concentration.

Problem 5.3 · Daniel-Titman Characteristic vs Covariance Spread

In a double-sorted portfolio test, Portfolio 1 (High BM, High Beta) earns 13.5% return. Portfolio 2 (High BM, Low Beta) earns 13.3% return. Portfolio 3 (Low BM, Low Beta) earns 8.5% return. Calculate: (a) the factor loading spread holding characteristic constant; (b) the characteristic spread holding factor loading constant.

Solution

(a) Factor loading spread holding characteristic constant:

\[ \Delta R_{\beta} = R_1 - R_2 = 13.5\% - 13.3\% = +0.2\% \]

(b) Characteristic spread holding factor loading constant:

\[ \Delta R_{\text{char}} = R_2 - R_3 = 13.3\% - 8.5\% = +4.8\% \]

The characteristic spread (+4.8%) dominates the covariance spread (+0.2%) by a factor of 24, validating the characteristic model.

Proof

Problem 5.4 · Proof of the Joint-Hypothesis Problem (Fama 1970)

Prove that any empirical test of informational efficiency \(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{t+1} \mid \Omega_t] = 0\) is mathematically inseparable from a model of market equilibrium pricing \(P_t = \mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1} X_{t+1}]\).

Solution

Informational efficiency states that prices fully reflect all information \(\Omega_t\), meaning that excess returns \(R_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}[R_{t+1} \mid \Omega_t] = \epsilon_{t+1}\) are unforecastable. However, to compute the expected return \(\mathbb{E}[R_{t+1} \mid \Omega_t]\), the econometrician must specify a pricing model (such as the CAPM, a multi-factor model, or a consumption-based SDF). If an empirical regression finds that a public variable \(Z_t \in \Omega_t\) forecasts abnormal return \(\hat\alpha_t \ne 0\), there are two mutually indistinguishable interpretations:

  1. Markets are informationally inefficient (investors mispriced the asset).
  2. The asset pricing model used to benchmark \(\mathbb{E}[R_{t+1} \mid \Omega_t]\) was misspecified (the model omitted a true systematic risk factor correlated with \(Z_t\)).

Because \(\alpha\) can always be set to zero by altering the stochastic discount factor \(M_{t+1}\), market efficiency cannot be tested independently of a joint pricing model.

Problem 5.5 · Falsification of Multi-Factor Spanning under Characteristic Sorts

Prove that if an asset's expected return is a function strictly of characteristic \(C_i\) rather than factor loading \(\beta_i\), then running the standard time-series regression \(R_{i, t} - R_{f, t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i F_t + \epsilon_{i, t}\) produces non-zero pricing errors \(\alpha_i \ne 0\) across sub-portfolios.

Solution

Suppose true expected returns satisfy \(\mathbb{E}[R_i] - R_f = \gamma C_i\). The multi-factor model asserts \(\mathbb{E}[R_i] - R_f = \beta_i \lambda_F\). The pricing error is:

\[ \alpha_i = (\mathbb{E}[R_i] - R_f) - \beta_i \lambda_F = \gamma C_i - \beta_i \lambda_F \]

If stocks are sorted into portfolios that hold \(C_i\) constant while varying \(\beta_i\), then \(\alpha_i = \gamma C - \beta_i \lambda_F\). Since \(\beta_i\) varies while \(C\) is fixed, \(\alpha_i\) must vary linearly with \(-\beta_i\). The hypothesis \(\alpha_i = 0\) for all portfolios is strictly violated whenever \(\lambda_F \ne 0\), falsifying the factor pricing specification.

Problem 5.6 · Asymmetric Short Leg Mispricing Under Short-Sale Constraints

Let an asset's true value be \(V\). Bullish noise traders perceive value \(V + \delta\) (\(\delta > 0\)) while bearish noise traders perceive \(V - \delta\). If short-sales are banned, prove that the equilibrium price strictly exceeds \(V\).

Solution

Under Miller's (1977) short-sale constraint theorem, pessimistic investors who would like to short the asset are constrained to hold zero shares. The market clearing price is set entirely by the marginal investor among the unconstrained bullish population. Since all active buyers have subjective valuations \(V_i \ge V\), the equilibrium price satisfies \(P \ge V\). Unfettered short-sale constraints censor negative opinions, causing equilibrium prices to reflect only optimistic beliefs, leading to systematic upward mispricing.

Empirical

Problem 5.7 · Campbell-Cochrane Habit Persistence vs Sentiment

How does the Campbell & Cochrane (1999) habit persistence model generate countercyclical risk premia without invoking irrationality?

Solution

In Campbell & Cochrane (1999), investors have utility defined over consumption relative to a slow-moving historical habit level \(H_t\): \(U(C_t, H_t) = \frac{(C_t - H_t)^{1-\gamma} - 1}{1-\gamma}\). When consumption drops near the habit level during recessions, the surplus consumption ratio \(S_t = (C_t - H_t)/C_t\) plunges, driving local risk aversion \(\gamma / S_t\) toward infinity. Rational investors demand massive equity risk premia to hold stocks during downturns, driving price-dividend ratios down without any cognitive irrationality.

Problem 5.8 · Cross-Asset Factor Replications (Asness et al. 2013)

Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013) examine Value and Momentum across four equity markets, government bonds, currencies, and commodity futures. What is their primary empirical finding, and how does it inform the risk vs mispricing debate?

Solution

They document that Value and Momentum exist in every asset class, and that a combined Value-and-Momentum portfolio generates Sharpe ratios exceeding 1.4 across global markets. Furthermore, value strategies across asset classes correlate with each other, and momentum strategies correlate with each other. This ubiquitous global structure suggests exposure to common global liquidity and macroeconomic shock factors, providing support for the rational risk interpretation.

Problem 5.9 · Stambaugh, Yu & Yuan (2012) Anomaly Short-Leg Test

Stambaugh, Yu & Yuan (2012) tested 11 major cross-sectional return anomalies conditional on the Baker-Wurgler sentiment index. What did they find regarding the long vs short legs of these anomalies?

Solution

They found that each of the 11 anomalies is significantly stronger following high sentiment periods. Crucially, the variation in anomaly returns is concentrated entirely in the short leg: following high sentiment, the short leg earns massive negative abnormal returns, while the long leg is flat. Following low sentiment, the anomaly spread drops to zero. This short-leg asymmetry confirms that short-sale constraints prevent arbitrageurs from eliminating overpricing during sentiment peaks.

Research

Problem 5.10 · The Replication Crisis in the Anomaly Zoo

Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) and Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020) argue that the majority of published anomalies in the "factor zoo" fail to replicate when subjected to higher statistical significance thresholds (\(t > 3.0\)) and micro-cap screening. How does multiple testing alter the risk vs mispricing debate?

Solution

With hundreds of factors published, standard \(t\)-hurdles of 2.0 suffer massive false discovery rates due to publication bias and data snooping. Many apparent "mispricings" are statistical artifacts. Furthermore, when anomalies are value-weighted rather than equal-weighted, micro-cap illiquidity is excluded, and transaction costs are incorporated, over 60% of published anomalies disappear. This implies that true mispricings are heavily concentrated in small, illiquid segments of the market where arbitrage is economically unviable.

Problem 5.11 · Machine Learning Asset Pricing (Gu, Kelly & Xiu 2020)

Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) apply deep neural networks, boosted trees, and penalized regressions to predict the cross-section of returns. Does machine learning favor risk or mispricing?

Solution

Machine learning models identify complex, non-linear interactions between firm characteristics and macroeconomic states, dramatically improving out-of-sample \(R^2\). The most predictive features are price trends, liquidity, and volatility rather than fundamental valuation ratios. While this demonstrates that returns are highly non-linearly predictable, it does not settle whether neural networks are identifying complex risk premia or extracting non-linear behavioral overreaction patterns.

Problem 5.12 · Unified Structural Identification of Risk vs Mispricing

Outline a general structural asset pricing framework that simultaneously models irrational investor beliefs and time-varying risk aversion to isolate their respective contributions to the equity premium.

Solution

Specify an economy where rational arbitrageurs have Epstein-Zin preferences (capturing time-varying consumption and volatility risk) while noise traders have diagnostic expectations over cash flows. Use survey data to identify the subjective belief distortion parameter \(\theta\) and asset price covariance with consumption to identify risk aversion \(\gamma\) and elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(\psi\). GMM estimation on joint survey and return moment conditions allows variance decomposition: attributing \(X\%\) of price volatility to subjective sentiment and \((100-X)\%\) to discount-rate risk.

  8. Under Campbell & Cochrane's (1999) habit model, why are risk premia high during economic recessions?

    Options

    When surplus consumption drops near zero, marginal utility is extremely steep, generating high countercyclical risk premia.
