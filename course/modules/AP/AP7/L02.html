Why it matters

Financial asset prices exhibit two pervasive empirical regularities across global equity markets: positive return autocorrelation over 3- to 12-month horizons (cross-sectional momentum) followed by negative return autocorrelation over 3- to 5-year horizons (long-term contrarian reversal). Pure rational expectation models cannot simultaneously explain both phenomena without postulating implausibly volatile, regime-shifting macroeconomic risk premia. Behavioral asset pricing resolves this puzzle through micro-founded structural models grounded in experimental cognitive psychology. This lesson examines how conservatism, representativeness, investor overconfidence, biased self-attribution, and gradual information diffusion generate endogenous price underreaction in the short run and price overshooting in the long run.

1Empirical Anomalies: Short-Run Momentum and Long-Run Reversal

In efficient capital markets, asset prices adjust instantaneously and completely to newly arrived fundamental information. In historical cross-sections, however, stock returns violate this condition across distinct time horizons:

  1. Long-horizon contrarian reversal: De Bondt & Thaler (1985) documented that portfolios formed from past long-term losers (stocks in the lowest performance decile over the prior 3 to 5 years) outperform past long-term winners by an average of 25% to 30% cumulative excess return over the subsequent 3 to 5 years.
  2. Intermediate-horizon momentum: Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) showed that strategies selecting stocks based on performance over the past 3 to 12 months generate significant abnormal returns of approximately 1% per month over holding periods of 3 to 12 months.

Definition 2.1 · Over- and Under-Reaction Models Over- and under-reaction models are structural asset pricing models in which boundedly rational investors subject to cognitive heuristics under-incorporate fundamental news innovations in the short run (causing return continuation/momentum) and over-extrapolate past trends in the long run (causing price overshooting and subsequent contrarian reversals).

2The Barberis, Shleifer & Vishny (BSV 1998) Regime-Switching Model

Barberis, Shleifer & Vishny (1998) construct a parsimonious model linking underreaction and overreaction to two well-documented psychological heuristics:

  • Conservatism heuristic (Edwards 1968): Individuals update their prior beliefs insufficiently when presented with new evidence, clinging to their prior anchors.
  • Representativeness heuristic (Tversky & Kahneman 1974): Individuals view a brief sample of recent realizations as overly representative of the underlying data-generating process, seeing patterns and trends where none exist.

In the BSV model, corporate earnings \(N_t\) follow a true random walk:

\[ N_{t+1} = N_t + \epsilon_{t+1}, \quad \epsilon_{t+1} \in \{+y, -y\} \quad \text{with equal probability } \frac{1}{2} \]

However, the representative investor mistakenly believes earnings shocks are generated by one of two unobservable Markov regimes:

  • Regime 1 (Mean-Reverting / Conservatism): If earnings increased today (\(\epsilon_t = +y\)), investors believe the probability of another increase tomorrow is low: \(\mathbb{P}(\epsilon_{t+1} = +y \mid \epsilon_t = +y) = \pi_L < \frac{1}{2}\). Investors treat earnings innovations as temporary noise, generating underreaction.
  • Regime 2 (Trending / Representativeness): If earnings increased today, investors believe the probability of another increase tomorrow is high: \(\mathbb{P}(\epsilon_{t+1} = +y \mid \epsilon_t = +y) = \pi_H > \frac{1}{2}\). Investors believe the firm is on a secular growth trend, generating overreaction.

Regime transitions follow a Markov chain with switching probabilities \(\lambda_1 = \mathbb{P}(s_{t+1} = 2 \mid s_t = 1)\) and \(\lambda_2 = \mathbb{P}(s_{t+1} = 1 \mid s_t = 2)\). The investor observes earnings changes \(\{\epsilon_t\}\) and updates the subjective posterior probability \(q_t = \mathbb{P}(s_t = 1 \mid \epsilon_t, \dots, \epsilon_1)\) via Bayes' rule.

Theorem 2.1 · BSV Asset Price Solution In the BSV (1998) economy with risk-neutral discounting at rate \(r\), the equilibrium stock price \(P_t\) is given by:

\[ P_t = \frac{N_t}{r} + y \left( p_1 - p_2 q_t \right) \frac{\epsilon_t}{y} \]

where \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) are positive constants determined by \((r, \pi_L, \pi_H, \lambda_1, \lambda_2)\). When a firm reports a single earnings surprise, \(q_t\) remains near 1 (Regime 1), leading the price to underreact to \(\epsilon_t\). When a firm reports a long sequence of identical earnings surprises, \(q_t \to 0\) (Regime 2), causing the price to overreact and over-extrapolate future growth, setting up subsequent predictable return reversals.

3The Daniel, Hirshleifer & Subrahmanyam (DHS 1998) Model

Daniel, Hirshleifer & Subrahmanyam (1998) propose an alternative cognitive foundation based on:

  1. Investor overconfidence: Informed traders overestimate the precision of their private fundamental signals while properly discounting publicly available signals.
  2. Biased self-attribution: When subsequent public news confirms an investor's private signal, the investor attributes the success to superior skill, increasing confidence. When public news contradicts the private signal, the investor attributes the error to bad luck, failing to revise confidence downward.

Consider an informed investor who receives private signal \(s = \theta + \epsilon\), where true asset payoff is \(\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\theta^2)\) and noise is \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2)\). While the objective noise variance is \(\sigma_\epsilon^2\), the overconfident investor believes the variance is \(\sigma_c^2 < \sigma_\epsilon^2\).

Theorem 2.2 · DHS Price Trajectory and Self-Attribution Amplification Let \(P_1\) denote the price after private signal arrival, \(P_2\) the price after public signal arrival, and \(P_3\) the terminal fundamental payoff. Under biased self-attribution:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(P_2 - P_1, P_1 - P_0) > 0 \quad \text{(Short-Run Momentum)} \] \[ \operatorname{Cov}(P_3 - P_2, P_1 - P_0) < 0 \quad \text{(Long-Run Reversal)} \]

Overconfidence creates an initial overreaction at \(t = 1\). At \(t = 2\), confirming public signals trigger asymmetric confidence updates, driving prices further away from fundamental value before public disclosures eventually enforce convergence at \(t = 3\).

4The Hong & Stein (1999) Unified Heterogeneous Agent Model

Hong & Stein (1999) dispense with regime switching and biased attribution, deriving underreaction and overreaction from the interaction of two boundedly rational groups of traders:

  1. News Watchers: Make forecasts based on private fundamental information \(D_t\). However, fundamental news diffuses gradually across the population of news watchers across \(z\) sub-periods. Because news watchers condition only on fundamentals and ignore market prices, price adjustment is sluggish, producing initial price underreaction.
  2. Momentum Traders: Condition exclusively on past price changes \(\Delta P_{t-k}\) over a lookback horizon \(j\). They possess no fundamental information and attempt to profit from the trend initiated by gradual information diffusion.

Theorem 2.3 · Hong-Stein Autocorrelation Structure In the Hong & Stein (1999) interaction equilibrium:

  1. Information diffusion speed \(z\): Slower information diffusion (higher \(z\)) increases short-run price underreaction, generating higher profitability for trend-chasing momentum strategies.
  2. Endogenous overshooting: Momentum traders accelerate the price response toward fundamentals, but their collective buying pushes prices beyond fundamental value:
    3. \[ \lim_{t \to \infty} P_t = D_\infty + \text{Overshoot} \]
  3. Term structure of autocorrelations: Price changes exhibit strictly positive autocorrelation at short lags (\(\operatorname{Corr}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) > 0\)) and strictly negative autocorrelation at long lags (\(\operatorname{Corr}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-k}) < 0\) for \(k \gg j\)).

Lab 1 · Simulating the Hong-Stein Unified Under- and Over-Reaction Dynamics

Simulating the interaction of boundedly rational news-watchers with slow information diffusion and trend-chasing momentum traders generates positive short-term autocorrelation (lags 1–3) and negative long-term autocorrelation (lags 6–12).

"""Lab AP7-L02-1: Hong & Stein (1999) Underreaction and Overreaction Dynamics.

SIMULATED: Simulates boundedly rational news-watchers (gradual information diffusion)
and momentum traders (trend chasing), reproducing short-term momentum and long-term
reversals in asset price changes (Hong & Stein 1999).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_periods = 1200

    # Model parameters:
    # Information diffusion speed: news epsilon_t takes z periods to diffuse fully
    z_diffusion = 4  # news spreads across z equal cohorts of news watchers
    j_momentum = 3  # momentum traders look back j periods of past price changes
    phi_momentum = 0.65  # aggressiveness of momentum trend chasing

    # Fundamental dividend innovations: epsilon_t ~ N(0, sigma^2)
    innovations = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n_periods)

    # True fundamental value: random walk D_t = D_{t-1} + epsilon_t
    fundamentals = np.cumsum(innovations)

    # Price dynamics:
    # News-watchers' price adjustment without momentum traders:
    # P_t^NW = D_{t-z} + (1/z) * epsilon_{t-z+1} + ... + (z/z) * epsilon_t
    # Gradual diffusion causes underreaction to current news
    price_changes = np.zeros(n_periods)
    prices = np.zeros(n_periods)

    for t in range(z_diffusion, n_periods):
        # Gradual diffusion fundamental component
        diffusion_term = np.mean(innovations[t - z_diffusion + 1 : t + 1])

        # Momentum trader demand depends on recent price changes: sum_{k=1}^j Delta P_{t-k}
        recent_momentum = np.sum(price_changes[t - j_momentum : t]) if t >= j_momentum else 0.0
        momentum_term = phi_momentum * (recent_momentum / j_momentum)

        # Price change at t combines gradual news absorption + momentum feedback
        price_changes[t] = diffusion_term + momentum_term - 0.20 * (prices[t - 1] - fundamentals[t - 1])
        prices[t] = prices[t - 1] + price_changes[t]

    # Analyze return autocorrelations across lags 1 to 12
    valid_changes = price_changes[z_diffusion + 10 :]
    lags = [1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12]
    autocorrs = {}

    for lag in lags:
        c_val = np.corrcoef(valid_changes[lag:], valid_changes[:-lag])[0, 1]
        autocorrs[lag] = float(c_val)

    # Peak momentum lag and peak reversal lag
    max_autocorr_lag = max(autocorrs, key=lambda k: autocorrs[k])
    min_autocorr_lag = min(autocorrs, key=lambda k: autocorrs[k])

    print("Hong-Stein Return Autocorrelation Structure:")
    for lag in lags:
        print(f"  Lag {lag:2d} Autocorrelation: {autocorrs[lag]:+.4f}")
    print(f"Peak positive autocorrelation (momentum): lag {max_autocorr_lag} ({autocorrs[max_autocorr_lag]:+.4f})")
    print(f"Trough negative autocorrelation (reversal): lag {min_autocorr_lag} ({autocorrs[min_autocorr_lag]:+.4f})")
    print(f"Short-run momentum (lag 1 > 0):            {bool(autocorrs[1] > 0)}")
    print(f"Long-run reversal (lag 12 < 0):            {bool(autocorrs[12] < 0)}")


main()

5Where the canon breaks

Classical asset pricing insists that market prices must follow martingales with respect to public information. The cognitive heuristic models break this canon:

  • Autocorrelation without risk premia: Predictable return patterns (momentum and reversal) emerge from bounded rationality and cognitive heuristics rather than time-varying consumption risk premia.
  • Trading volume is informative and excessive: While rational expectations models yield no-trade theorems, overconfidence in DHS (1998) generates massive equilibrium trading volume and excessive price volatility.
  • Information diffusion is sluggish: The Hong-Stein model establishes that even when information is public or quasi-public, market prices underreact because market participants face cognitive processing constraints.

6Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Momentum and reversal are mutually contradictory anomalies that cancel each other out.”

    Momentum and reversal operate over entirely different time horizons. As shown by Hong & Stein (1999) and BSV (1998), intermediate-horizon momentum (3 to 12 months) reflects sluggish initial news absorption, while long-horizon reversal (3 to 5 years) reflects the eventual unwinding of accumulated overshooting.

  2. “Rational Bayesian updating ensures that investors quickly learn true structural parameters and eliminate cognitive biases.”

    Biased self-attribution (Daniel et al. 1998) prevents Bayesian convergence. When successful trades are attributed to skill and failed trades are dismissed as unforecastable bad luck, market experience actually reinforces and amplifies overconfidence over time.

  3. “Trend-following momentum traders stabilize asset prices by accelerating price discovery toward fundamentals.”

    While momentum trading speeds up initial price movement toward fundamentals, momentum traders condition only on past price changes and cannot observe fundamental value. Consequently, they continue buying after prices reach intrinsic value, endogenously creating bubbles and overreaction.

Mechanics

Problem 2.1 · DHS Overconfidence Price Markup

An asset has fundamental payoff \(\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1.0)\). An informed investor observes private signal \(s = \theta + \epsilon\) where true noise variance is \(\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 1.0\). Due to overconfidence, the investor believes the noise variance is \(\sigma_c^2 = 0.25\). For an observed signal \(s = 1.50\), compute the overconfident investor's posterior expectation \(\mathbb{E}_c[\theta \mid s]\) and compare it to the rational Bayesian expectation \(\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid s]\).

Solution

Under normal updating with prior mean 0, the posterior expectation is \(\frac{\sigma_\theta^2}{\sigma_\theta^2 + \sigma^2} s\).

1. Rational expectation:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid s] = \frac{1.0}{1.0 + 1.0} \times 1.50 = 0.50 \times 1.50 = 0.75 \]

2. Overconfident expectation:

\[ \mathbb{E}_c[\theta \mid s] = \frac{1.0}{1.0 + 0.25} \times 1.50 = \frac{1.0}{1.25} \times 1.50 = 0.80 \times 1.50 = 1.20 \]

The overconfident investor forms an expectation of 1.20, overreacting to the private signal by \(1.20 - 0.75 = 0.45\) (a 60% markup over the rational expectation).

Problem 2.2 · Bayesian Regime Updating in BSV (1998)

In the BSV model, let the prior probability of Regime 1 be \(q_0 = 0.80\). In Regime 1, \(\mathbb{P}(\epsilon_{t+1} = +y \mid \epsilon_t = +y) = \pi_L = 0.25\). In Regime 2, \(\mathbb{P}(\epsilon_{t+1} = +y \mid \epsilon_t = +y) = \pi_H = 0.75\). A firm reports two consecutive positive earnings surprises (\(\epsilon_1 = +y, \epsilon_2 = +y\)). Ignoring regime transitions for one period, compute the updated posterior probability \(q_2 = \mathbb{P}(s = 1 \mid \epsilon_1, \epsilon_2)\).

Solution

Using Bayes' rule:

\[ q_2 = \frac{q_0 \times \pi_L}{q_0 \times \pi_L + (1 - q_0) \times \pi_H} \] \[ \text{Numerator} = 0.80 \times 0.25 = 0.20 \] \[ \text{Denominator} = 0.20 + (0.20 \times 0.75) = 0.20 + 0.15 = 0.35 \] \[ q_2 = \frac{0.20}{0.35} = \frac{4}{7} \approx 0.5714 \]

The second positive earnings shock drops the probability of the mean-reverting regime from 0.80 to 0.5714, shifting investor weight toward the trending regime.

Problem 2.3 · Hong-Stein Information Diffusion Price Path

Fundamental news \(\Delta D = \$12.00\) arrives at \(t = 0\) and diffuses equally across \(z = 4\) cohorts of news watchers over periods \(t \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\). Assuming no momentum traders and zero discount rate, calculate the cumulative price change \(\Delta P_t\) in each period \(t = 1, 2, 3, 4\) and the lag-1 return autocorrelation.

Solution

Because news diffuses equally across 4 cohorts, each cohort incorporates \(\$12.00 / 4 = \$3.00\) into the price per period:

\[ \Delta P_1 = \$3.00, \quad \Delta P_2 = \$3.00, \quad \Delta P_3 = \$3.00, \quad \Delta P_4 = \$3.00 \]

Prices adjust gradually: \(P_1 = 3, P_2 = 6, P_3 = 9, P_4 = 12\). During the diffusion window, \(\Delta P_t = \Delta P_{t-1} = \$3.00\), generating perfect positive short-run return autocorrelation (\(\operatorname{Corr}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = +1.0\)).

Problem 2.4 · Proof of Momentum Profitability under Sluggish Diffusion

Prove that if asset price changes satisfy \(\Delta P_t = \sum_{k=0}^{z-1} \frac{1}{z} \epsilon_{t-k}\) where \(\epsilon_t \sim \operatorname{i.i.d.} (0, \sigma^2)\), then a simple momentum strategy buying when \(\Delta P_{t-1} > 0\) earns strictly positive expected return for any \(z > 1\).

Solution

The autocovariance of price changes at lag 1 is:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = \operatorname{Cov}\left( \frac{1}{z}\sum_{k=0}^{z-1} \epsilon_{t-k}, \frac{1}{z}\sum_{j=0}^{z-1} \epsilon_{t-1-j} \right) \]

Because \(\epsilon_t\) is i.i.d. with variance \(\sigma^2\), the terms overlap for indices where \(t - k = t - 1 - j\), which occurs for \(k \in \{1, \dots, z-1\}\) (a total of \(z - 1\) overlapping terms):

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\Delta P_t, \Delta P_{t-1}) = \frac{z - 1}{z^2} \sigma^2 \]

For any finite \(z > 1\), \(\frac{z - 1}{z^2} \sigma^2 > 0\). The regression slope of \(\Delta P_t\) on \(\Delta P_{t-1}\) is \(\beta = \frac{z - 1}{z} > 0\). Therefore, a strategy conditioning on \(\Delta P_{t-1} > 0\) yields strictly positive expected profits.

Problem 2.5 · Proof of Contrarian Reversal after Trend Extrapolation

Prove that if prices overreact such that \(P_T = D_T + \kappa \sum_{k=0}^{H} \epsilon_{T-k}\) with \(\kappa > 0\), subsequent multi-period returns \(R_{T+H+1} - R_T\) are negatively correlated with past returns.

Solution

Let terminal fundamental value converge to \(D_\infty = D_T + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \epsilon_{T+j}\). The subsequent long-run price change is:

\[ P_\infty - P_T = (D_\infty) - \left( D_T + \kappa \sum_{k=0}^H \epsilon_{T-k} \right) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \epsilon_{T+j} - \kappa \sum_{k=0}^H \epsilon_{T-k} \]

The past cumulative return is \(\Delta P_{\text{past}} = P_T - P_{T-H} = (1 + \kappa) \sum_{k=0}^H \epsilon_{T-k}\). The covariance between subsequent returns and past returns is:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}\left( P_\infty - P_T, P_T - P_{T-H} \right) = -\kappa (1 + \kappa) \sum_{k=0}^H \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_{T-k}) = -\kappa (1 + \kappa)(H + 1)\sigma^2 < 0 \]

Since \(\kappa > 0\), the covariance is strictly negative, proving long-run contrarian reversal.

Problem 2.6 · Asymmetric Bayesian Updating under Self-Attribution

Formalize the biased self-attribution learning rule and prove that public disclosure does not asymptotically eliminate investor overconfidence.

Solution

Let investor confidence be parameterized by subjective precision \(\tau_t = 1 / \sigma_{c, t}^2\). Let private signal be \(s_1 = \theta + \epsilon\) and subsequent public news be \(s_2 = \theta + \eta\). The self-attribution update rule is:

\[ \tau_{t+1} = \begin{cases} \tau_t + \delta_H |s_2|, & \text{if } \operatorname{sign}(s_2) = \operatorname{sign}(s_1) \quad (\text{confirmation}) \\ \tau_t - \delta_L |s_2|, & \text{if } \operatorname{sign}(s_2) \ne \operatorname{sign}(s_1) \quad (\text{disconfirmation}) \end{cases} \]

where \(\delta_H > \delta_L \ge 0\). Taking the expectation over public news states:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta \tau_t] = \mathbb{P}(\text{Confirm}) \delta_H \mathbb{E}[|s_2| \mid \text{Confirm}] - \mathbb{P}(\text{Disconfirm}) \delta_L \mathbb{E}[|s_2| \mid \text{Disconfirm}] \]

Because \(\delta_H > \delta_L\) and \(\mathbb{P}(\text{Confirm}) > \frac{1}{2}\) when signals are informative, \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta \tau_t] > 0\). Subjective precision diverges toward infinity (\(\tau_t \to \infty\)) rather than converging to true objective precision, proving that public signals fail to eliminate overconfidence.

Empirical

Problem 2.7 · Post-Earnings Announcement Drift (PEAD)

Bernard and Thomas (1989, 1990) documented that stocks in the highest standardized unexpected earnings (SUE) decile continue to drift upward for 60 trading days following an earnings announcement. Relate this phenomenon to the BSV conservatism heuristic.

Solution

In BSV (1998), investors anchored on prior earnings beliefs update insufficiently upon receiving an earnings surprise (conservatism). Consequently, the announcement date price jump captures only a fraction of the full fundamental surprise. As subsequent corporate filings, quarterly dividends, and revisions arrive, prices slowly drift toward the true fundamental value over the next 60 trading days, generating PEAD.

Problem 2.8 · Jegadeesh-Titman 6-Month Cross-Sectional Momentum

In a standard 6-month / 6-month Jegadeesh-Titman momentum sort, the long-short winner-minus-loser portfolio historically yielded approximately 12% annualized return. Why does momentum crash during market turnarounds (Daniel & Moskowitz 2016)?

Solution

Past losers in a bear market consist disproportionately of high-beta, highly leveraged, distressed firms. When the aggregate market rebounds sharply, these distressed loser stocks experience explosive upward returns (high option delta on enterprise value). The momentum portfolio—which is short these high-beta losers and long low-beta winners—suffers catastrophic negative returns (momentum crashes), confirming non-linear risk exposure.

Problem 2.9 · Hong, Lim & Stein (2000) Analyst Coverage Test

Hong, Lim & Stein (2000) tested the Hong-Stein hypothesis that slower information diffusion increases momentum profitability. How did they measure information diffusion speed, and what did they find?

Solution

They proxied for information diffusion speed using residual analyst coverage (number of sell-side equity research analysts covering a firm, orthogonalized with respect to market capitalization). They found that momentum profitability is significantly higher among stocks with low analyst coverage. For small, uncovered stocks, news diffuses slowly, generating massive momentum continuation; for stocks with high coverage, information is incorporated quickly, attenuating momentum.

Research

Problem 2.10 · Distinguishing BSV vs DHS vs Hong-Stein Empirically

Outline an empirical test to distinguish whether cross-sectional momentum is driven primarily by gradual information diffusion (Hong-Stein) or biased self-attribution (DHS).

Solution

Examine trading volume and price revisions following private vs public announcements. Hong-Stein predicts that momentum is driven by news watchers; momentum strategies should be more profitable in stocks with low institutional attention and low trading volume during initial news arrival. DHS predicts that momentum is amplified by confirming public news; momentum should be strongest among stocks where public news releases confirm prior analyst recommendations, accompanied by surging retail trading volume.

Problem 2.11 · Machine Learning Extraction of Extrapolative Regimes

Propose a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) architecture to estimate the BSV regime probabilities \(q_t\) directly from high-frequency order flow and corporate news sentiment.

Solution

Train a 2-state Gaussian HMM where the hidden states represent Mean-Reverting (Regime 1) and Trending (Regime 2) beliefs. The observed emission variables include abnormal retail order imbalance, news sentiment polarity, and cumulative price run-ups. By calculating the Viterbi smoothed state probabilities, the model estimates \(q_t\). Conditional factor strategies that underweight stocks when \(q_t\) indicates extreme overreaction test the empirical validity of structural regime switching.

Problem 2.12 · Limits to Arbitrage in Correcting Momentum

Why do hedge funds not aggressively short past losers and buy past winners to eliminate momentum profits?

Solution

Momentum requires frequent monthly rebalancing, incurring substantial transaction costs and bid-ask spreads. Furthermore, the short leg of momentum consists of illiquid, hard-to-borrow micro-cap stocks with elevated borrow fees and severe short-squeeze risk. Finally, momentum crashes create extreme left-tail downside risk, deterring risk-averse arbitrageurs operating under value-at-risk (VaR) constraints.
