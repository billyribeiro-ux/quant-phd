Why it matters

Modern rational asset pricing rests on the foundation of time-varying discount rates: when market prices are elevated relative to dividends (low dividend yields), rational investors must anticipate low future expected returns to clear the market. In striking empirical contrast, direct survey measurements of investor expectations reveal the exact opposite: when stock prices and valuations are high, both individual and institutional investors expect subsequent returns to be exceptionally high. This stark divergence between model-implied rational discount rates and empirical survey expectations represents one of the most consequential battlegrounds in modern macro-finance. This lesson formalizes extrapolative expectations and analyzes Bordalo, Gennaioli, La Porta & Shleifer's (2019) diagnostic expectations framework, providing a unified, psychologically founded theory of expectation formation across financial markets.

1The Survey Expectations Puzzle: Greenwood & Shleifer (2014)

In standard consumption-based and present-value asset pricing models (AP5), the price-dividend ratio reflects time-varying discount rates under rational expectations: \(\frac{P_t}{D_t} \approx \frac{1}{r_t - g}\). When stock prices boom, expected future returns \(\mathbb{E}_t[R_{t+1}]\) must decline to maintain equilibrium.

Greenwood & Shleifer (2014) investigated whether actual human investors hold beliefs consistent with this rational discount-rate mechanism by assembling six major independent survey datasets spanning retail investors, chief financial officers, and institutional professionals:

  1. Gallup investor survey: Monthly survey of individual retail investors with at least $10,000 in investable assets (1996–2012).
  2. Duke/CFO magazine global business outlook: Quarterly survey of chief financial officers of US corporations forecasting expected 1-year and 10-year S&P 500 returns.
  3. American Association of Individual Investors (AAII): Weekly sentiment survey classifying retail investors as bullish, neutral, or bearish.
  4. Investors Intelligence (II): Weekly survey tracking the published market outlook of independent professional financial newsletter writers.
  5. Robert Shiller investor survey: Ongoing survey tracking individual and institutional investor confidence and crash probability assessments.
  6. University of Michigan consumer sentiment: Monthly survey asking households whether they expect stock prices to increase over the next year.

Definition 4.1 · Survey Expectations of Returns Survey expectations of returns are direct, empirical elicitations of subjective probability beliefs \(\mathbb{E}_t^S[R_{t+1}]\) regarding future financial asset returns reported by market participants (retail households, corporate managers, or professional forecasters) across periodic survey instruments.

Empirical Finding 4.1 · The Greenwood-Shleifer Empirical Clash Across all six independent survey series examined by Greenwood & Shleifer (2014):

  1. Mutual correlation: All survey expectations measures are strongly positively correlated with one another (pairwise correlations between +0.43 and +0.77).
  2. Extrapolation from past returns: Survey expectations are strongly positively correlated with recent past realized stock market returns (\(\operatorname{Corr}(\mathbb{E}_t^S[R_{t+1}], R_{t-12:t}) > 0.50\)) and with current valuation ratios (\(\operatorname{Corr}(\mathbb{E}_t^S[R_{t+1}], P_t/D_t) > 0.60\)).
  3. Negative correlation with rational returns: Survey expected returns are negatively correlated with subsequent realized market excess returns (\(\operatorname{Corr}(\mathbb{E}_t^S[R_{t+1}], R_{t+1}) < 0\)) and negatively correlated with model-implied rational expected returns \(\mathbb{E}_t[R_{t+1}]\):
    4. \[ \operatorname{Corr}\left( \mathbb{E}_t^S[R_{t+1}], \mathbb{E}_t^{\text{Rational}}[R_{t+1}] \right) < 0 \]

This empirical clash poses a fundamental challenge to rational expectations macro-finance. In rational models (such as Campbell & Cochrane 1999 habit formation or Bansal & Yaron 2004 long-run risks), price booms occur because risk aversion or economic risk falls, driving down the required discount rate. If actual investors were rational, they would report low expected returns during market peaks. Instead, they report peak optimism precisely at market tops, and peak pessimism at market bottoms.

2Mathematical Formalization of Extrapolative Expectations

To reconcile the Greenwood-Shleifer findings, behavioral theorists formalize subjective belief updating through extrapolative expectations.

Definition 4.2 · Extrapolative Expectations Extrapolative expectations specify that subjective expectations of future returns or growth are formed as a positive function of recent past realizations, typically modeled as an exponentially weighted moving average:

\[ S_t = (1 - \theta) \sum_{k=0}^\infty \theta^k R_{t-k}, \quad \theta \in (0, 1) \] \[ \mathbb{E}_t^e[R_{t+1}] = \mu_0 + \beta_e S_t, \quad \beta_e > 0 \]

where \(S_t = (1 - \theta)R_t + \theta S_{t-1}\) denotes the perceived sentiment/trend state variable and \(\theta\) governs investor memory decay.

In an equilibrium setting with extrapolators (Barberis et al. 1998), extrapolative expectations generate endogenous boom-bust price cycles. A positive fundamental cash-flow shock increases realized return \(R_t\). Extrapolators update \(S_t\) upward, raising their expected return \(\mathbb{E}_t^e[R_{t+1}]\). This higher demand bids up asset prices further at \(t+1\), generating another positive return. The feedback loop continues until valuations become detached from fundamentals, at which point cash-flow reality forces a crash, reversing the extrapolation process.

3Diagnostic Expectations: Bordalo, Gennaioli, La Porta & Shleifer (2019)

While extrapolative rules capture past return continuation, they are often criticized as mechanical and non-structural. Bordalo, Gennaioli, La Porta & Shleifer (2019) provide a micro-founded structural theory of expectations grounded in Kahneman and Tversky's (1974) representativeness heuristic.

Definition 4.3 · Diagnostic Expectations Let \(\Omega_t\) denote the information set available at time \(t\), and let \(f(x \mid \Omega_t)\) denote the objective rational conditional probability density of future state variable \(x\). Diagnostic expectations distort the objective distribution by overweighting states whose likelihood has increased the most relative to the prior reference state \(\Omega_{t-1}\):

\[ f_t^\theta(x) = f(x \mid \Omega_t) \left[ \frac{f(x \mid \Omega_t)}{f(x \mid \Omega_{t-1})} \right]^\theta \frac{1}{Z_t} \]

where \(\theta \ge 0\) is the diagnostic parameter measuring representativeness distortion, and \(Z_t = \int f(x \mid \Omega_t) \left[ \frac{f(x \mid \Omega_t)}{f(x \mid \Omega_{t-1})} \right]^\theta \mathrm{d}x\) is the normalization constant ensuring the distorted density integrates to 1.

When \(\theta = 0\), the diagnostic density collapses to the rational conditional density \(f(x \mid \Omega_t)\). When \(\theta > 0\), states that have become more objectively likely in light of recent news become disproportionately salient in the decision-maker's mind, leading to cognitive overreaction.

Theorem 4.2 · Linear-Gaussian Diagnostic Expectation Updating In a linear-Gaussian environment where the underlying variable \(x_{t+1}\) conditional on information \(\Omega_t\) is normally distributed with conditional variance \(\sigma^2\), the diagnostic expectation satisfies the elegant additive updating formula:

\[ \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[x_{t+1}] = \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] + \theta \left( \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[x_{t+1}] \right) \]

The diagnostic expectation equals the rational expectation plus a distortion term proportional to the newly arrived news innovation (the forecast revision \(\mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[x_{t+1}]\)).

Proof of Theorem 4.2 Let \(f(x \mid \Omega_t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu_t)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)\) where \(\mu_t = \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}]\), and let \(f(x \mid \Omega_{t-1}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu_{t-1})^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)\) where \(\mu_{t-1} = \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[x_{t+1}]\). The likelihood ratio is:

\[ \frac{f(x \mid \Omega_t)}{f(x \mid \Omega_{t-1})} = \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu_t)^2 - (x - \mu_{t-1})^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) = \exp\left( \frac{2x(\mu_t - \mu_{t-1}) - (\mu_t^2 - \mu_{t-1}^2)}{2\sigma^2} \right) \]

Raising this ratio to the power \(\theta\) and multiplying by \(f(x \mid \Omega_t)\):

\[ f_t^\theta(x) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu_t)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \theta \frac{x(\mu_t - \mu_{t-1})}{\sigma^2} \right) = \exp\left( -\frac{x^2 - 2x\mu_t + \mu_t^2 - 2\theta x(\mu_t - \mu_{t-1})}{2\sigma^2} \right) \] \[ \propto \exp\left( -\frac{x^2 - 2x\left[ \mu_t + \theta (\mu_t - \mu_{t-1}) \right]}{2\sigma^2} \right) \]

Completing the square reveals that \(f_t^\theta(x)\) is a Gaussian density with identical variance \(\sigma^2\) and distorted mean:

\[ \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[x_{t+1}] = \mu_t + \theta(\mu_t - \mu_{t-1}) = \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] + \theta \left( \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[x_{t+1}] \right) \]

Bordalo et al. (2019) test this formulation on consensus professional analyst forecasts of corporate earnings growth. They establish a key empirical signature of diagnostic expectations:

\[ \text{Forecast Error}_{t+1} = \text{Actual}_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[\text{Actual}_{t+1}] = -\theta \left( \mathbb{E}_t[\text{Actual}_{t+1}] - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\text{Actual}_{t+1}] \right) + \eta_{t+1} \]

Under rational expectations (\(\theta = 0\)), forecast errors are unforecastable orthogonal noise (\(\operatorname{Cov}(\text{Error}_{t+1}, \text{Revision}_t) = 0\)). Under diagnostic expectations (\(\theta > 0\)), forecast errors are systematically and negatively predicted by recent revisions. Positive earnings surprises cause analysts to extrapolate excessively, leading to predictable negative forecast errors and return reversals over the subsequent 12 to 24 months.

Lab 1 · Survey Expectations vs. Rational Forecasts & Diagnostic Updating

Simulating rational valuation dynamics alongside extrapolative survey beliefs and diagnostic earnings expectations verifies that survey expectations negatively correlate with rational discount rates, while diagnostic forecast errors are negatively predicted by recent news surprises.

"""Lab AP7-L04-1: Survey Expectations vs Rational Forecasts & Diagnostic Expectations.

SIMULATED: Compares rational model-implied return forecasts with empirical survey
expectations (extrapolative), and tests the diagnostic expectations belief-distortion
equation on corporate earnings forecasts (Greenwood & Shleifer 2014; Bordalo et al. 2019).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_periods = 200

    # 1. State variable: persistent dividend-price ratio / valuation state x_t
    # In rational Campbell-Cochrane / Campbell-Shiller models, high dividend-price ratio
    # implies HIGH rational expected returns (discount-rate variation).
    phi_x = 0.88
    x_val = np.zeros(n_periods)
    shocks = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=n_periods)
    for t in range(1, n_periods):
        x_val[t] = phi_x * x_val[t - 1] + shocks[t]

    # Realized returns: R_{t+1} = mu + beta * x_t + u_{t+1}
    # where u_{t+1} negatively correlates with shock_{t+1} (price dividend ratio drops when returns are bad)
    return_innovations = -0.75 * shocks + rng.normal(0.0, 0.65, size=n_periods)
    returns = 0.008 + 0.015 * x_val + return_innovations

    # Rational return expectation: E_t[R_{t+1}] = mu + beta * x_t
    rational_expectations = 0.008 + 0.015 * x_val

    # 2. Survey return expectations: Greenwood & Shleifer (2014) finding
    # Investors extrapolate recent past returns:
    # E_t^S[R_{t+1}] = c_0 + c_1 * Past_Return_t
    # Because past high returns correspond to low dividend-price ratios (negative shock),
    # survey expectations move in the OPPOSITE direction of rational discount rates!
    past_returns_ewma = np.zeros(n_periods)
    theta_extrap = 0.70
    for t in range(1, n_periods):
        past_returns_ewma[t] = theta_extrap * past_returns_ewma[t - 1] + (1.0 - theta_extrap) * returns[t - 1]

    survey_expectations = 0.008 + 0.85 * past_returns_ewma

    # Correlation between survey expectations and rational expectations
    corr_survey_rational = float(np.corrcoef(survey_expectations[20:], rational_expectations[20:])[0, 1])

    # Predictive regression: Realized returns on Survey Expectations vs Rational Expectations
    y_ret = returns[21:]
    x_survey = survey_expectations[20:-1]
    x_rational = rational_expectations[20:-1]

    slope_survey = float(np.polyfit(x_survey, y_ret, 1)[0])
    slope_rational = float(np.polyfit(x_rational, y_ret, 1)[0])

    # 3. Diagnostic Expectations updating (Bordalo et al. 2019):
    # E_t^theta[g_{t+1}] = E_t[g_{t+1}] + theta * (E_t[g_{t+1}] - E_{t-1}[g_{t+1}])
    theta_diagnostic = 0.50
    earnings_growth_shock = rng.normal(0.0, 0.05, size=n_periods)
    earnings_growth = 0.02 + earnings_growth_shock

    rational_earnings_exp = np.full(n_periods, 0.02)
    # News revision: current shock
    news_innovation = earnings_growth_shock
    diagnostic_earnings_exp = rational_earnings_exp + theta_diagnostic * news_innovation

    # Diagnostic forecast errors: Realized - Expected
    forecast_errors = earnings_growth - diagnostic_earnings_exp
    # Predictability of forecast errors by recent surprise:
    # Under rational expectations, Cov(error_{t+1}, news_t) = 0
    # Under diagnostic expectations, forecast error is negatively predicted by news_t (overreaction reversal)
    corr_error_news = float(np.corrcoef(forecast_errors[1:], news_innovation[:-1])[0, 1])

    print("Greenwood-Shleifer Survey Expectations Analysis:")
    print(f"  Corr(Survey Expectation, Rational Expectation): {corr_survey_rational:+.4f}")
    print(f"  Predictive slope of returns on Survey Expectation: {slope_survey:+.4f}")
    print(f"  Predictive slope of returns on Rational Expectation: {slope_rational:+.4f}")
    print("Bordalo et al. Diagnostic Expectations Analysis:")
    print(f"  Diagnostic distortion coefficient theta:          {theta_diagnostic:.2f}")
    print(f"  Diagnostic forecast error standard deviation:     {float(np.std(forecast_errors)):.4f}")
    print(f"  Corr(Forecast Error t+1, News Innovation t):       {corr_error_news:+.4f}")
    print(f"  Surveys negatively correlate with rational:       {bool(corr_survey_rational < 0)}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The rational expectations hypothesis asserts that economic agents know the true data-generating process and form mathematical conditional expectations without systematic bias (\(\mathbb{E}^S[X] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \Omega]\)). Survey and diagnostic research overturns this core axiom:

  • Direct belief data refutes the rational expectations proxy: Economists traditionally treated realized returns as a noisy proxy for expected returns. Direct survey measurement proves that subjective expectations systematically diverge from realized returns and rational discount rates.
  • Forecast errors are predictable: Across corporate CFOs, equity analysts, and macroeconomic forecasters, forecast errors correlate with past forecast revisions, refuting full informational efficiency.
  • Representativeness provides a micro-foundation for extrapolation: Rather than assuming ad hoc rule-of-thumb extrapolation, diagnostic expectations derive extrapolative overreaction from a single cognitive primitive (Tversky & Kahneman 1974 representativeness).

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Survey expectations are merely cheap talk without financial stakes and can be safely ignored in asset pricing models.”

    Greenwood & Shleifer (2014) demonstrate that survey expectations predict actual capital allocation decisions: retail investor equity mutual fund inflows, CFO corporate equity issuance, and aggregate hedge fund beta exposure are all strongly positively correlated with survey expected returns.

  2. “Diagnostic expectations imply that investors are completely irrational and ignore Bayesian updating.”

    Diagnostic expectations are anchored directly on the rational conditional distribution \(f(x \mid \Omega_t)\). Diagnostic agents incorporate all available information in the rational information set; they merely tilt the objective probabilities by overweighting salient, representative states where the likelihood ratio has spiked.

  3. “If dividend yields are low, rational investors expect low returns, so survey respondents who say they expect high returns must be answering a different question.”

    The six surveys ask precise, quantitative questions (e.g. Duke CFOs provide numerical percentage return forecasts over 1-year and 10-year horizons). The divergence reflects a genuine cognitive rift: human investors equate past price appreciation with future profitability, violating present-value identities.

Mechanics

Problem 4.1 · Diagnostic Mean Updating Calculation

Prior rational earnings growth forecast at \(t-1\) was \(\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[g_{t+1}] = 4.0\%\). At \(t\), positive earnings surprise arrives, updating the rational forecast to \(\mathbb{E}_t[g_{t+1}] = 6.5\%\). If the diagnostic parameter is \(\theta = 0.60\), compute the diagnostic expectation \(\mathbb{E}_t^\theta[g_{t+1}]\).

Solution

Under Theorem 4.2:

\[ \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[g_{t+1}] = \mathbb{E}_t[g_{t+1}] + \theta \left( \mathbb{E}_t[g_{t+1}] - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[g_{t+1}] \right) \]

Substitute the parameters:

\[ \text{Revision} = 6.5\% - 4.0\% = 2.5\% \] \[ \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[g_{t+1}] = 6.5\% + 0.60 \times (2.5\%) = 6.5\% + 1.5\% = 8.0\% \]

The diagnostic expectation is 8.0%, overshooting the rational forecast by 1.5 percentage points.

Problem 4.2 · Extrapolative EWMA Trend Calculation

An extrapolator with memory parameter \(\theta = 0.80\) has prior sentiment state \(S_{t-1} = 5.0\%\). In period \(t\), the realized market return is \(R_t = 15.0\%\). Compute the updated sentiment state \(S_t = (1 - \theta) R_t + \theta S_{t-1}\).

Solution

Using the recursive EWMA definition:

\[ S_t = (1 - 0.80) \times 15.0\% + 0.80 \times 5.0\% \] \[ S_t = 0.20 \times 15.0\% + 0.80 \times 5.0\% = 3.0\% + 4.0\% = 7.0\% \]

The updated extrapolative sentiment state is 7.0%.

Problem 4.3 · Predictable Forecast Error Calculation

An analyst forms diagnostic expectation \(\mathbb{E}_t^\theta[g_{t+1}] = 8.0\%\) based on revision \(\Delta = 2.5\%\) with \(\theta = 0.60\). The subsequent realized earnings growth turns out to equal the rational expectation \(\mathbb{E}_t[g_{t+1}] = 6.5\%\). Calculate the analyst's forecast error \(\text{Actual} - \text{Forecast}\).

Solution

The forecast error is defined as realized value minus forecast:

\[ \text{Error} = g_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[g_{t+1}] = 6.5\% - 8.0\% = -1.5\% \]

Notice that \(\text{Error} = -\theta \Delta = -0.60 \times 2.5\% = -1.5\%\). The forecast error is strictly negative and predicted exactly by the prior positive news innovation.

Problem 4.4 · Proof of Negative Correlation Between Diagnostic Forecast Errors and Revisions

Prove that under diagnostic expectations with parameter \(\theta > 0\), the covariance between the forecast error \(\text{FE}_{t+1} = x_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[x_{t+1}]\) and the forecast revision \(\operatorname{Rev}_t = \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[x_{t+1}]\) is strictly negative.

Solution

Let \(x_{t+1} = \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] + \eta_{t+1}\), where \(\eta_{t+1}\) is rational forecast error satisfying \(\mathbb{E}[\eta_{t+1} \mid \Omega_t] = 0\). The diagnostic forecast is \(\mathbb{E}_t^\theta[x_{t+1}] = \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] + \theta \operatorname{Rev}_t\). The diagnostic forecast error is:

\[ \text{FE}_{t+1} = x_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[x_{t+1}] = \left( \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] + \eta_{t+1} \right) - \left( \mathbb{E}_t[x_{t+1}] + \theta \operatorname{Rev}_t \right) = \eta_{t+1} - \theta \operatorname{Rev}_t \]

Compute the covariance with \(\operatorname{Rev}_t\):

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\text{FE}_{t+1}, \operatorname{Rev}_t) = \operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{t+1} - \theta \operatorname{Rev}_t, \operatorname{Rev}_t) = \operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{t+1}, \operatorname{Rev}_t) - \theta \operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{Rev}_t) \]

Since \(\eta_{t+1}\) is orthogonal to all information at \(t\) (including \(\operatorname{Rev}_t\)), \(\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{t+1}, \operatorname{Rev}_t) = 0\). Therefore:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\text{FE}_{t+1}, \operatorname{Rev}_t) = -\theta \operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{Rev}_t) \]

Whenever revisions have non-zero variance and \(\theta > 0\), this covariance is strictly negative: \(-\theta \operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{Rev}_t) < 0\).

Problem 4.5 · Campbell-Shiller Identity with Extrapolative Expectations

Prove that if prices are set by extrapolators whose subjective expected return satisfies \(\mathbb{E}_t^e[r_{t+1}] = \mu + \beta_e \Delta p_t\) with \(\beta_e > 0\), the dividend-price ratio \(dp_t\) is negatively correlated with subjective future returns.

Solution

Under the Campbell-Shiller approximation, \(dp_t \approx \frac{k}{1-\rho} + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+j}] - \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho^{j-1} \mathbb{E}_t[\Delta d_{t+j}]\). A high realized return \(\Delta p_t\) causes prices to rise relative to dividends, driving \(dp_t\) downward (\(\operatorname{Cov}(dp_t, \Delta p_t) < 0\)). Since extrapolator expected returns satisfy \(\mathbb{E}_t^e[r_{t+1}] = \mu + \beta_e \Delta p_t\):

\[ \operatorname{Cov}\left( dp_t, \mathbb{E}_t^e[r_{t+1}] \right) = \beta_e \operatorname{Cov}(dp_t, \Delta p_t) < 0 \]

Thus, low dividend-price ratios (high valuations) coincide with high subjective expected returns, proving the Greenwood-Shleifer clash.

Problem 4.6 · Normalization Factor in Diagnostic Density

Prove that for the Gaussian likelihood ratio distorted by parameter \(\theta\), the normalization factor \(Z_t = \int f(x \mid \Omega_t)^{1+\theta} f(x \mid \Omega_{t-1})^{-\theta} \mathrm{d}x\) is strictly finite and equal to \(\exp\left( \frac{\theta(1+\theta)(\mu_t - \mu_{t-1})^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)\).

Solution

Evaluate the unnormalized Gaussian product:

\[ f_t(x)^{1+\theta} f_{t-1}(x)^{-\theta} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} \exp\left( -\frac{(1+\theta)(x - \mu_t)^2 - \theta(x - \mu_{t-1})^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) \]

Expand the quadratic terms:

\[ (1+\theta)(x^2 - 2x\mu_t + \mu_t^2) - \theta(x^2 - 2x\mu_{t-1} + \mu_{t-1}^2) = x^2 - 2x\left[\mu_t + \theta(\mu_t - \mu_{t-1})\right] + (1+\theta)\mu_t^2 - \theta \mu_{t-1}^2 \]

Let \(\mu_\theta = \mu_t + \theta(\mu_t - \mu_{t-1})\). Completing the square:

\[ (x - \mu_\theta)^2 - \mu_\theta^2 + (1+\theta)\mu_t^2 - \theta \mu_{t-1}^2 = (x - \mu_\theta)^2 - \theta(1+\theta)(\mu_t - \mu_{t-1})^2 \]

Integrating \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu_\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) \mathrm{d}x = 1\), the constant factor remaining is:

\[ Z_t = \exp\left( \frac{\theta(1+\theta)(\mu_t - \mu_{t-1})^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) \]

Since \(\theta > 0\), \(Z_t \ge 1\) is strictly finite and positive.

Empirical

Problem 4.7 · Investor Mutual Fund Inflows and Survey Expectations

Greenwood & Shleifer (2014) regress aggregate equity mutual fund net inflows on survey expectations versus Campbell-Cochrane model expected returns. What do they find?

Solution

They find that net equity mutual fund inflows are strongly positively correlated with survey expected returns (\(t > 4.0\)), but negatively correlated with rational consumption-based model expected returns. Investors pour fresh capital into equity funds when they report high survey optimism (at market peaks), proving that survey responses reflect genuine, economically meaningful investor demand rather than non-binding survey noise.

Problem 4.8 · Analyst Long-Term Growth (LTG) Overreaction

Bordalo et al. (2019) examine I/B/E/S consensus analyst Long-Term Growth (LTG) forecasts. What is the empirical relationship between past firm earnings growth, LTG forecasts, and subsequent 3-year stock returns?

Solution

Firms that experienced high earnings growth over the prior 3 years receive highly elevated analyst LTG forecasts (diagnostic overreaction). However, subsequent realized earnings growth consistently disappoints these optimistic forecasts, and high-LTG stocks earn significantly lower subsequent 3-year stock returns (-5.2% annualized spread) than low-LTG stocks, confirming diagnostic belief reversal.

Problem 4.9 · Credit Spreads and Diagnostic Beliefs

Bordalo, Gennaioli & Shleifer (2018) apply diagnostic expectations to corporate credit markets. How does diagnostic overreaction explain the credit cycle and narrow high-yield bond spreads prior to recessions?

Solution

During economic expansions, low default rates are interpreted by diagnostic credit investors as representative of a permanent benign credit regime. Credit spreads compress to record lows and corporate debt issuance surges. When macroeconomic conditions experience even modest negative shocks, defaults spike above the diagnostics' suppressed forecasts, causing violent credit spread blowouts and credit market freezes.

Research

Problem 4.10 · Testing Rational inattention vs Diagnostic Expectations

Compare the empirical predictions of rational inattention (Sims 2003, Coibion & Gorodnichenko 2015) with diagnostic expectations (Bordalo et al. 2019) regarding forecast error predictability.

Solution

Under rational inattention, agents face information-processing friction; their forecast errors are POSITIVELY correlated with past revisions (\(\operatorname{Cov}(\text{Error}_{t+1}, \operatorname{Rev}_t) > 0\)) because agents underreact and update sluggishly. Under diagnostic expectations, agents overreact to news; forecast errors are NEGATIVELY correlated with past revisions (\(\operatorname{Cov}(\text{Error}_{t+1}, \operatorname{Rev}_t) < 0\)). Empirical data on consensus macroeconomic and earnings forecasts reveals that individual forecasters overreact (negative covariance), while consensus forecasts can exhibit underreaction due to aggregation of staggered information.

Problem 4.11 · Structural Estimation of the Diagnostic Parameter Theta

Propose a generalized method of moments (GMM) estimator to identify the structural parameter \(\theta\) from a panel of consensus quarterly analyst earnings forecasts.

Solution

Use the diagnostic forecast error moment condition: \(\mathbb{E}\left[ \left( g_{i, t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[g_{i, t+1}] + \theta(\mathbb{E}_t[g_{i, t+1}] - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[g_{i, t+1}]) \right) \mathbf{Z}_{i, t} \right] = \mathbf{0}\), where \(\mathbf{Z}_{i, t}\) includes lagged revisions and firm characteristics. Estimating this moment condition across Compustat/IBES panels yields consistent point estimates of \(\hat\theta \approx 0.40 - 0.70\), rejecting rational expectations (\(\theta = 0\)) with \(p < 0.001\).

Problem 4.12 · General Equilibrium Asset Pricing with Diagnostic Agents

Discuss the challenges of solving a dynamic general equilibrium asset pricing model when agents have diagnostic expectations over consumption growth.

Solution

Because diagnostic expectations depend on the forecast revision \(\mathbb{E}_t[c_{t+1}] - \mathbb{E}_{t-1}[c_{t+1}]\), the subjective probability distribution is history-dependent and non-time-consistent. The Law of Iterated Expectations fails: \(\mathbb{E}_t^\theta[\mathbb{E}_{t+1}^\theta[c_{t+2}]] \ne \mathbb{E}_t^\theta[c_{t+2}]\). Standard dynamic programming and Bellman equations cannot be applied without augmenting the state space to include prior subjective belief distributions.
