Why it matters

Textbook financial theory rests on the premise that rational arbitrageurs aggressively eliminate any mispricing between an asset's market price and fundamental present value. If irrational noise traders push prices away from intrinsic value, riskless arbitrage trades are assumed to restore market efficiency instantaneously, while unprofitable noise traders are bankrupted and extinguished. This lesson analyzes why this classical Friedman premise breaks down. When rational arbitrageurs face finite investment horizons, risk aversion, and delegated capital subject to interim client redemptions, noise-trader sentiment introduces non-diversifiable resale-price risk that limits arbitrage positions, permits persistent price dislocations, and enables irrational traders to survive and even earn higher expected returns in equilibrium.

1The Classical Paradigm and the Friedman Market Selection Hypothesis

The classical efficient markets hypothesis formalized by Fama (1970) relies critically on Milton Friedman's (1953) market selection hypothesis. Friedman argued that trading against fundamental value is an actuarially losing strategy: traders with biased beliefs buy high and sell low, transferring their wealth to rational, profit-maximizing arbitrageurs until their market impact vanishes asymptotically.

Under this classical view, two conditions guarantee informational efficiency:

  1. Costless and infinite arbitrage: Unconstrained arbitrageurs identify substitute securities under the law of one price and take offsetting long and short positions that earn riskless profits without absorbing capital.
  2. Market selection against noise: Investors who misestimate cash flows or discount rates systematically suffer negative abnormal returns, causing their wealth share to converge to zero almost surely.

Modern behavioral finance, pioneered by De Long, Shleifer, Summers & Waldmann (1990) and Shleifer & Vishny (1997), demonstrates that both conditions fail in actual financial markets. Arbitrage is neither costless nor unconstrained; instead, real-world arbitrage is conducted by specialized professional fund managers operating under contractual, financial, and cognitive frictions.

2The De Long, Shleifer, Summers & Waldmann (DSSW 1990) Model

To formalize the limits imposed by irrational investor sentiment on rational traders, De Long et al. (1990) developed an overlapping generations (OLG) model of asset price determination with two types of investors.

Definition 1.1 · DSSW Overlapping Generations Economy Consider an economy with overlapping generations of two-period lived agents. In each period \(t\), a continuum of generation-\(t\) investors are born with measure 1. Investors work when young, invest their labor endowment, consume all their accumulated terminal wealth \(W_{t+1}\) when old, and die.

Two assets exist in fixed supply:

  1. Risk-free safe asset: In perfectly elastic supply, paying a constant exogenous gross return \(1 + r\) per period (where \(r > 0\)).
  2. Risky asset: In fixed unit supply \(Q = 1\), paying a constant real dividend \(d\) in every period, trading at equilibrium price \(P_t\). Fundamental present value under the risk-free rate is \(V = d / r\).

The population consists of two types of agents:

  • Rational arbitrageurs (measure \(1 - \mu\)): Form rational Bayesian expectations of future asset prices \(\mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}]\).
  • Noise traders (measure \(\mu \in [0, 1]\)): Misperceive the expected resale price of the risky asset by a stochastic sentiment shock \(\rho_t \sim \operatorname{i.i.d.} \mathcal{N}(\mu_\rho, \sigma_\rho^2)\).

Definition 1.2 · Noise-Trader Risk Noise-trader risk is the systematic risk that unpredictable future shifts in irrational investor sentiment widen asset price dislocations away from fundamental value before fundamentals can reassert themselves, exposing rational investors with finite horizons to capital losses upon liquidation.

Both types of young investors possess Constant Absolute Risk Aversion (CARA) utility over terminal old-age wealth:

\[ U(W_{t+1}) = -e^{-2\gamma W_{t+1}}, \quad \gamma > 0 \]

Under normally distributed returns, maximizing expected CARA utility is equivalent to maximizing the mean-variance objective \(\mathbb{E}_t[W_{t+1}] - \gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(W_{t+1})\).

Let \(w_t\) denote the young agent's initial labor wealth endowment, and let \(\lambda_{a, t}\) and \(\lambda_{n, t}\) denote the number of risky asset shares demanded by rational arbitrageurs and noise traders, respectively. Terminal wealth is given by:

\[ W_{t+1} = (1 + r)(w_t - \lambda_t P_t) + \lambda_t (P_{t+1} + d) = (1 + r)w_t + \lambda_t \left[ P_{t+1} + d - (1 + r)P_t \right] \]

Theorem 1.1 · DSSW Equilibrium Asset Price Decomposition In the stationary linear equilibrium of the DSSW (1990) economy, the equilibrium asset price \(P_t\) is uniquely determined by:

\[ P_t = \frac{d}{r} + \frac{\mu \mu_\rho}{r} - \frac{2\gamma \mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2}{r (1 + r)^2} + \frac{\mu (\rho_t - \mu_\rho)}{1 + r} \]

The price decomposes into four economically distinct components:

  1. Fundamental value: \(V = d / r\), the discounted present value of dividends.
  2. Average sentiment markup: \(\frac{\mu \mu_\rho}{r}\), the persistent price distortion caused by mean noise-trader bullishness (\(\mu_\rho > 0\)).
  3. Noise-trader risk discount: \(-\frac{2\gamma \mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2}{r (1 + r)^2}\), the compensation demanded by risk-averse investors for bearing the resale-price variance created by future noise-trader sentiment fluctuations.
  4. Transitory sentiment innovation: \(\frac{\mu (\rho_t - \mu_\rho)}{1 + r}\), the immediate pass-through of current sentiment shocks.

Proof of Theorem 1.1 Each young rational investor chooses share demand \(\lambda_{a, t}\) to maximize:

\[ \max_{\lambda_{a, t}} \left\{ (1 + r)w_t + \lambda_{a, t} \left( \mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}] + d - (1 + r)P_t \right) - \gamma \lambda_{a, t}^2 \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1}) \right\} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\lambda_{a, t}\) and setting to zero yields the rational demand function:

\[ \lambda_{a, t} = \frac{\mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}] + d - (1 + r)P_t}{2\gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1})} \]

Noise traders misperceive the expected price by \(\rho_t\), so their perceived expectation is \(\mathbb{E}_t^n[P_{t+1}] = \mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}] + \rho_t\). Their demand function is therefore:

\[ \lambda_{n, t} = \frac{\mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}] + \rho_t + d - (1 + r)P_t}{2\gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1})} = \lambda_{a, t} + \frac{\rho_t}{2\gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1})} \]

Asset market clearing requires aggregate demand to equal the fixed supply of \(Q = 1\) shares:

\[ (1 - \mu)\lambda_{a, t} + \mu \lambda_{n, t} = 1 \implies \lambda_{a, t} + \frac{\mu \rho_t}{2\gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1})} = 1 \]

Substituting \(\lambda_{a, t}\) into the market clearing equation yields:

\[ \frac{\mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}] + d - (1 + r)P_t}{2\gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1})} + \frac{\mu \rho_t}{2\gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1})} = 1 \] \[ (1 + r)P_t = d + \mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}] + \mu \rho_t - 2\gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1}) \]

We conjecture a stationary linear price function of the form \(P_t = \Pi_0 + \Pi_1 \rho_t\). Because \(\rho_{t+1}\) is i.i.d. with mean \(\mu_\rho\) and variance \(\sigma_\rho^2\), the conditional moments at time \(t\) are:

\[ \mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}] = \Pi_0 + \Pi_1 \mu_\rho, \quad \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1}) = \Pi_1^2 \sigma_\rho^2 \]

Equating coefficients across \(\rho_t\) and the constant term:

\[ (1 + r)(\Pi_0 + \Pi_1 \rho_t) = d + (\Pi_0 + \Pi_1 \mu_\rho) + \mu \rho_t - 2\gamma \Pi_1^2 \sigma_\rho^2 \]

Matching the coefficients on \(\rho_t\):

\[ (1 + r)\Pi_1 = \mu \implies \Pi_1 = \frac{\mu}{1 + r} \]

Matching the constant terms:

\[ (1 + r)\Pi_0 = d + \Pi_0 + \Pi_1 \mu_\rho - 2\gamma \Pi_1^2 \sigma_\rho^2 \] \[ r \Pi_0 = d + \frac{\mu \mu_\rho}{1 + r} - 2\gamma \left( \frac{\mu}{1 + r} \right)^2 \sigma_\rho^2 \] \[ \Pi_0 = \frac{d}{r} + \frac{\mu \mu_\rho}{r (1 + r)} - \frac{2\gamma \mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2}{r (1 + r)^2} \]

Combining \(\Pi_0\) and \(\Pi_1 \rho_t\) and rearranging \(\frac{\mu \mu_\rho}{r(1+r)} + \frac{\mu \rho_t}{1+r} = \frac{\mu \mu_\rho}{r} + \frac{\mu(\rho_t - \mu_\rho)}{1+r}\) produces the stated expression.

Theorem 1.2 · Overturning the Friedman Critique ("Create Their Own Space") Let \(\Delta R = R_n - R_a\) denote the differential between the expected return earned by noise traders and the expected return earned by rational arbitrageurs. In the DSSW economy, the expected return differential is given by:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta R] = \mu_\rho - \frac{(1 + r)^2 \mu_\rho^2 + \sigma_\rho^2}{2\gamma \mu \sigma_\rho^2} \]

Whenever average noise-trader optimism \(\mu_\rho > 0\) is moderately positive and risk aversion \(\gamma\) is sufficiently large, noise traders earn strictly higher expected returns than rational arbitrageurs: \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta R] > 0\).

Proof of Theorem 1.2 The difference in portfolio returns between noise traders and arbitrageurs stems entirely from their differential asset holdings:

\[ R_{n, t+1} - R_{a, t+1} = (\lambda_{n, t} - \lambda_{a, t})\left[ \frac{P_{t+1} + d - (1 + r)P_t}{w_t} \right] \]

Recall from the demand equations that \(\lambda_{n, t} - \lambda_{a, t} = \frac{\rho_t}{2\gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1})} = \frac{(1 + r)^2 \rho_t}{2\gamma \mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2}\). From the equilibrium pricing formula, the realized excess dollar return per share is:

\[ P_{t+1} + d - (1 + r)P_t = 2\gamma \left( \frac{\mu}{1 + r} \right)^2 \sigma_\rho^2 - \mu \rho_t + \frac{\mu (\rho_{t+1} - \mu_\rho)}{1 + r} \]

Multiplying the share holding differential by the return per share and taking unconditional expectations:

\[ \mathbb{E}[(\lambda_{n, t} - \lambda_{a, t})(P_{t+1} + d - (1 + r)P_t)] = \frac{(1 + r)^2}{2\gamma \mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2} \mathbb{E}\left[ \rho_t \left( 2\gamma \left( \frac{\mu}{1 + r} \right)^2 \sigma_\rho^2 - \mu \rho_t + \frac{\mu(\rho_{t+1} - \mu_\rho)}{1 + r} \right) \right] \]

Since \(\mathbb{E}[\rho_t] = \mu_\rho\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\rho_t^2] = \mu_\rho^2 + \sigma_\rho^2\), and \(\rho_{t+1}\) is independent of \(\rho_t\):

\[ = \frac{(1 + r)^2}{2\gamma \mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2} \left[ 2\gamma \left( \frac{\mu}{1 + r} \right)^2 \sigma_\rho^2 \mu_\rho - \mu (\mu_\rho^2 + \sigma_\rho^2) \right] = \mu_\rho - \frac{(1 + r)^2 (\mu_\rho^2 + \sigma_\rho^2)}{2\gamma \mu \sigma_\rho^2} \]

Setting \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta R] > 0\) requires \(\mu_\rho > \frac{(1 + r)^2 (\mu_\rho^2 + \sigma_\rho^2)}{2\gamma \mu \sigma_\rho^2}\), which holds whenever risk aversion \(\gamma\) is sufficiently large and \(\sigma_\rho^2\) is bounded.

Remark 1.1 · The Four DSSW Effects The expected return differential reflects four distinct economic forces:

  1. The "Hold More Space" Effect: Noise traders are bullish (\(\mu_\rho > 0\)) and hold more of the risky asset than arbitrageurs. Because the risky asset pays an equity premium to compensate for fundamental and sentiment risk, noise traders capture more of this premium.
  2. The "Friedman" Market Timing Penalty: Because sentiment fluctuates, noise traders buy most aggressively when \(\rho_t\) is high (prices are elevated) and sell or hold less when \(\rho_t\) is low (prices are depressed). This poor market timing penalizes noise-trader returns by \(-\frac{(1 + r)^2 \sigma_\rho^2}{2\gamma \mu \sigma_\rho^2}\).
  3. The "Create Space" Effect: As noise-trader sentiment variance \(\sigma_\rho^2\) increases, price volatility increases. Risk-averse arbitrageurs retreat from the risky asset, lowering the equilibrium price and raising the expected return on the risky asset. Noise traders, being less sensitive to risk, benefit disproportionately from this elevated return.
  4. The Overconfidence Penalty: Excessive optimism leads to over-allocation that generates second-order quadratic utility losses.

3Performance-Based Arbitrage and Delegated Capital Frictions

While the DSSW model assumes risk-averse agents with fixed wealth, professional arbitrageurs in hedge funds and investment banks manage delegated outside capital. Shleifer & Vishny (1997) formalized the consequences of this separation between capital ownership and arbitrage execution.

In the Shleifer-Vishny model, outside investors cannot observe whether an arbitrageur's poor performance is caused by an expanded mispricing (which makes the trade even more attractive) or by manager incompetence. Rational Bayesian investors update their assessment of manager skill based on recent performance: funds suffering negative returns face immediate capital withdrawals.

When an irrational sentiment shock pushes asset prices further below fundamental value, fund managers suffer mark-to-market losses. Instead of receiving capital injections to double down on an extraordinarily attractive dislocation, the arbitrageur experiences client redemptions and margin calls. The arbitrageur is forced to liquidate positions into an illiquid market, depressing prices further and exacerbating the mispricing precisely when fundamental value is furthest from the market price.

Lab 1 · Simulating DSSW Noise-Trader Risk and Wealth Dynamics

Simulating the DSSW overlapping generations economy under persistent noise-trader shocks verifies that resale-price risk creates a measurable discount below fundamental value and drives equilibrium price volatility above fundamental volatility.

"""Lab AP7-L01-1: DSSW (1990) Noise Trader Risk and Limits to Arbitrage.

SIMULATED: Simulates an overlapping generations economy with rational arbitrageurs
and noise traders with stochastic sentiment shocks, evaluating price volatility,
arbitrageur demand, and expected returns (De Long, Shleifer, Summers & Waldmann 1990).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_periods = 1000

    # Model parameters
    risk_free_rate = 0.05  # r = 5% per period
    risk_aversion = 1.0  # gamma = 1.0 (CARA utility)
    dividend = 1.0  # constant dividend d = 1.0
    mu_sentiment = 0.10  # average noise-trader bullish sentiment
    sigma_sentiment = 0.25  # volatility of sentiment shocks
    noise_trader_share = 0.30  # fraction mu of noise traders in population

    # Fundamental value: V = d / r = 1.0 / 0.05 = 20.0
    fundamental_value = dividend / risk_free_rate

    # Generate i.i.d. sentiment shocks rho_t ~ N(mu_sentiment, sigma_sentiment^2)
    sentiment_shocks = rng.normal(mu_sentiment, sigma_sentiment, size=n_periods)

    # Analytical DSSW equilibrium price formula:
    # P_t = 1 + (d - r)/r + (mu*(rho_t - mu_s))/(1+r) + (mu*mu_s)/r - (2 * gamma * mu^2 * sigma_s^2) / (r * (1+r)^2)
    # For normalized asset with dividend d and risk-free return r:
    # Fundamental P* = d / r
    # Mispricing components:
    # 1. Average sentiment shift: (noise_trader_share * mu_sentiment) / risk_free_rate
    # 2. Resale price risk discount:
    #    -(2 * gamma * mu^2 * sigma^2) / (r * (1 + r)^2)
    # 3. Fluctuating sentiment shock: (noise_trader_share * (sentiment_shocks - mu_sentiment)) / (1 + risk_free_rate)

    avg_sentiment_shift = (noise_trader_share * mu_sentiment) / risk_free_rate
    risk_discount = (2.0 * risk_aversion * (noise_trader_share**2) * (sigma_sentiment**2)) / (
        risk_free_rate * ((1.0 + risk_free_rate) ** 2)
    )
    fluctuation = (noise_trader_share * (sentiment_shocks - mu_sentiment)) / (1.0 + risk_free_rate)

    prices = fundamental_value + avg_sentiment_shift - risk_discount + fluctuation

    # Arbitrageur and noise trader portfolio holdings
    # Arbitrageur demand: q_a,t = (d + E_t[P_{t+1}] - (1+r)P_t) / (2 * gamma * Var_t(P_{t+1}))
    # Var_t(P_{t+1}) = (noise_trader_share^2 * sigma_sentiment^2) / (1 + r)^2
    var_future_price = ((noise_trader_share**2) * (sigma_sentiment**2)) / ((1.0 + risk_free_rate) ** 2)
    expected_future_price = fundamental_value + avg_sentiment_shift - risk_discount

    arb_demand = (dividend + expected_future_price - (1.0 + risk_free_rate) * prices) / (
        2.0 * risk_aversion * var_future_price
    )
    noise_demand = arb_demand + sentiment_shocks / (2.0 * risk_aversion * var_future_price)

    # Calculate returns and performance metrics
    total_returns = (dividend + prices[1:]) / prices[:-1] - 1.0
    excess_returns = total_returns - risk_free_rate

    mean_price = float(np.mean(prices))
    price_volatility = float(np.std(prices, ddof=1))
    mean_excess_return = float(np.mean(excess_returns))
    mean_arb_demand = float(np.mean(arb_demand))
    mean_noise_demand = float(np.mean(noise_demand))

    print(f"Fundamental value V:               ${fundamental_value:.2f}")
    print(f"Mean equilibrium price:            ${mean_price:.2f}")
    print(f"Average sentiment price markup:    ${avg_sentiment_shift:.2f}")
    print(f"Noise trader risk price discount:  ${risk_discount:.4f}")
    print(f"Equilibrium price volatility:      ${price_volatility:.4f}")
    print(f"Mean asset excess return:          {mean_excess_return * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"Mean arbitrageur share demand:     {mean_arb_demand:.4f}")
    print(f"Mean noise trader share demand:    {mean_noise_demand:.4f}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The classical efficient markets framework (AP1, AP2) presumes that arbitrage is limited only by transaction costs and information arrival. The noise-trader and limits-to-arbitrage literature overturns this foundation:

  • Absence of fundamental risk does not imply absence of trading risk: Even when fundamental cash flows are guaranteed and identical across twin securities (e.g. Royal Dutch / Shell parity or closed-end fund discounts), noise-trader risk creates severe mark-to-market risk that prevents arbitrageurs from eliminating the spread.
  • Delegated capital introduces pro-cyclical liquidation: Unlike hypothetical textbook arbitrageurs with infinite horizons, real-world arbitrageurs face capital withdrawals when asset prices dislocate, turning rational arbitrageurs into forced sellers during market crises.
  • Noise traders can permanently survive in equilibrium: Milton Friedman's market selection theorem fails because noise traders bear the resale price risk they generate, enabling them to earn higher expected returns than rational investors despite inferior information.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “If two identical cash-flow streams trade at different prices, an arbitrageur can execute a pure riskless arbitrage with zero capital risk.”

    This ignores interim mark-to-market and margin risk. As demonstrated by the Royal Dutch and Shell dislocation (Shleifer & Vishny 1997), mispricings can widen for years before converging. Arbitrageurs funded by leverage or client capital face margin calls and forced liquidation before the horizon converges.

  2. “Irrational traders who trade on noise must lose money on average and be eliminated by Darwinian market selection.”

    The DSSW (1990) model disproves this premise. If noise traders are bullish on average, they hold a larger share of the risky market portfolio than rational investors. By holding more risky assets, they earn the equity risk premium and can accumulate wealth faster in expectation than cautious arbitrageurs.

  3. “Arbitrage capital always flows to the highest expected return opportunities, stabilizing prices during market panics.”

    Under performance-based arbitrage, institutional capital flows are governed by asymmetric information between fund investors and managers. Losses generated by market-wide dislocations trigger redemptions, forcing arbitrageurs to liquidate assets precisely when expected returns are highest.

Mechanics

Problem 1.1 · DSSW Price Discount Calculation

Consider a DSSW economy where the risk-free rate is \(r = 0.04\), risk aversion is \(\gamma = 1.5\), the noise trader population fraction is \(\mu = 0.25\), and the volatility of sentiment shocks is \(\sigma_\rho = 0.20\). Compute the noise-trader risk price discount \(\frac{2\gamma \mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2}{r (1 + r)^2}\).

Solution

We evaluate the analytical expression:

\[ \text{Discount} = \frac{2 \times \gamma \times \mu^2 \times \sigma_\rho^2}{r (1 + r)^2} \]

Substitute the parameter values:

\[ \mu^2 = (0.25)^2 = 0.0625, \quad \sigma_\rho^2 = (0.20)^2 = 0.04 \] \[ 2 \times 1.5 \times 0.0625 \times 0.04 = 0.0075 \] \[ r (1 + r)^2 = 0.04 \times (1.04)^2 = 0.04 \times 1.0816 = 0.043264 \] \[ \text{Discount} = \frac{0.0075}{0.043264} \approx 0.17335 \]

The noise-trader risk discount depresses the equilibrium asset price by approximately $0.1734 below fundamental present value.

Problem 1.2 · Arbitrageur Share Demand Under Sentiment Shock

In a DSSW economy with \(d = 1.0\), \(r = 0.05\), \(\gamma = 1.0\), and price variance \(\operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1}) = 0.04\), the current market price is \(P_t = 22.00\) and expected future price is \(\mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}] = 21.00\). Calculate the number of shares demanded by a rational arbitrageur \(\lambda_{a, t}\).

Solution

Rational arbitrageur demand is given by:

\[ \lambda_{a, t} = \frac{\mathbb{E}_t[P_{t+1}] + d - (1 + r)P_t}{2\gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(P_{t+1})} \]

Substitute the given parameters:

\[ \text{Numerator} = 21.00 + 1.00 - (1 + 0.05) \times 22.00 = 22.00 - 23.10 = -1.10 \] \[ \text{Denominator} = 2 \times 1.0 \times 0.04 = 0.08 \] \[ \lambda_{a, t} = \frac{-1.10}{0.08} = -13.75 \]

The rational arbitrageur takes a short position of 13.75 shares because the asset is substantially overpriced relative to expected payoff.

Problem 1.3 · Total Equilibrium Price Volatility

In the DSSW economy, what is the unconditional variance of the asset price \(\operatorname{Var}(P_t)\) in terms of \(\mu\), \(\sigma_\rho\), and \(r\)? For \(\mu = 0.40\), \(\sigma_\rho = 0.30\), and \(r = 0.05\), compute the standard deviation of prices \(\sigma(P_t)\).

Solution

From Theorem 1.1, the only stochastic component of \(P_t\) is \(\frac{\mu (\rho_t - \mu_\rho)}{1 + r}\). Therefore:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(P_t) = \left( \frac{\mu}{1 + r} \right)^2 \operatorname{Var}(\rho_t) = \frac{\mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2}{(1 + r)^2} \]

The standard deviation of prices is:

\[ \sigma(P_t) = \frac{\mu \sigma_\rho}{1 + r} = \frac{0.40 \times 0.30}{1.05} = \frac{0.120}{1.05} \approx 0.1143 \]

The standard deviation of prices is approximately $0.1143 per share.

Problem 1.4 · Derivation of Rational Euler Demand under CARA Utility

Prove that for a CARA utility investor with coefficient \(2\gamma\) facing normally distributed wealth \(W_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbb{E}_t[W_{t+1}], \operatorname{Var}_t(W_{t+1}))\), the optimal demand for the risky asset \(\lambda_t\) maximizes \(\mathbb{E}_t[W_{t+1}] - \gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(W_{t+1})\).

Solution

The investor maximizes expected utility \(\mathbb{E}_t[-e^{-2\gamma W_{t+1}}]\). Using the moment generating function of a normal random variable \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Z, \sigma_Z^2)\), \(\mathbb{E}[e^{t Z}] = e^{t \mu_Z + \frac{1}{2} t^2 \sigma_Z^2}\). Setting \(t = -2\gamma\) and \(Z = W_{t+1}\):

\[ \mathbb{E}_t[-e^{-2\gamma W_{t+1}}] = -\exp\left( -2\gamma \mathbb{E}_t[W_{t+1}] + \frac{1}{2}(-2\gamma)^2 \operatorname{Var}_t(W_{t+1}) \right) = -\exp\left( -2\gamma \left[ \mathbb{E}_t[W_{t+1}] - \gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(W_{t+1}) \right] \right) \]

Because \(-\exp(-2\gamma x)\) is a strictly monotonically increasing function of \(x\), maximizing expected utility is mathematically equivalent to maximizing the certainty equivalent objective:

\[ \max_{\lambda_t} \left\{ \mathbb{E}_t[W_{t+1}] - \gamma \operatorname{Var}_t(W_{t+1}) \right\} \]

Substituting \(W_{t+1} = (1 + r)w_t + \lambda_t [P_{t+1} + d - (1 + r)P_t]\) yields the mean-variance objective function.

Problem 1.5 · Proof of Price Volatility Excess over Fundamentals

Prove that whenever \(\mu > 0\) and \(\sigma_\rho > 0\), the variance of asset prices strictly exceeds the variance of fundamental values in the DSSW model, thereby proving excess volatility without cash flow variance.

Solution

In the DSSW economy, dividends are constant at \(d\), so fundamental value \(V = d / r\) is constant with variance \(\operatorname{Var}(V) = 0\).

From Theorem 1.1, the equilibrium market price is \(P_t = \Pi_0 + \frac{\mu}{1 + r}\rho_t\). The unconditional price variance is:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(P_t) = \frac{\mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2}{(1 + r)^2} \]

Since \(\mu > 0\) and \(\sigma_\rho > 0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(P_t) > 0 = \operatorname{Var}(V)\). Hence, market price variance is strictly greater than fundamental present-value variance. All price volatility in this economy is generated entirely by noise-trader sentiment fluctuations rather than cash-flow news.

Problem 1.6 · Bounded Horizon Arbitrage Breakdown

Prove that as the rational arbitrageur's horizon \(T \to \infty\) with zero interim redemption risk, the noise-trader risk discount converges to zero and \(P_t \to V\).

Solution

Let an arbitrageur hold the asset until terminal liquidation at horizon \(T\). In a multi-period setting where the asset liquidates at fundamental value \(V_T\) with certainty at \(T\), the terminal resale price is known: \(\operatorname{Var}_T(V_T) = 0\). Backwards induction from \(T\) yields that no resale price risk exists at \(T-1\). By iteration, if an arbitrageur is guaranteed to hold until terminal fundamental convergence without margin calls or liquidation risk, \(\sigma_\rho^2\) does not enter the terminal valuation, and the resale-price variance vanishes. It is precisely the finite horizon and interim trading necessity that generates noise-trader risk.

Empirical

Problem 1.7 · Royal Dutch / Shell Twin Share Arbitrage Breakdown

In the historical Royal Dutch and Shell merger agreement of 1907, cash flows were divided strictly 60:40 between Royal Dutch and Shell. In 1998, Royal Dutch traded at a 35% discount relative to its parity value. Explain why this persistent dislocation represents limits to arbitrage rather than rational market efficiency.

Solution

Royal Dutch and Shell were claims on the identical corporate cash flows in exact 60:40 proportions. Under the law of one price, the price ratio must equal 1.50. In 1998, the ratio deviated by more than 35%. A classical arbitrageur would short the expensive share and buy the cheap share. However, because no legal mechanism forced parity convergence before an indefinite merger date, arbitrageurs faced noise-trader risk. In 1998, Long-Term Capital Management held an enormous long-short position in Royal Dutch/Shell; when the mispricing widened during the Russian default crisis, margin calls forced LTCM to liquidate at the trough, crystallizing severe losses.

Problem 1.8 · Closed-End Fund Discount Puzzles

Closed-end mutual funds hold publicly traded portfolios of securities whose Net Asset Value (NAV) is observable daily. Yet closed-end funds trade at persistent discounts of 10% to 15% below NAV upon IPO. How does noise-trader risk explain both the average discount and the correlation of discounts across funds?

Solution

De Long et al. (1990) show that closed-end funds are predominantly held by retail noise traders, whereas the underlying assets are held by institutional investors. Buying a closed-end fund subjects the investor to two risks: fundamental asset risk plus sentiment risk of the fund's retail investor base. Rational investors demand a discount (the noise-trader risk discount \(\frac{2\gamma \mu^2 \sigma_\rho^2}{r(1+r)^2}\)) to hold the fund. Furthermore, because retail sentiment is a systematic market-wide factor, discounts across unrelated funds move together.

Problem 1.9 · Hedge Fund Alpha Attenuation Under Delegated Capital

Evaluate why hedge fund equity capital contracts during systemic crises using the Shleifer & Vishny (1997) performance-based arbitrage framework.

Solution

Under delegated portfolio management, fund investors cannot observe manager skill directly. Following negative quarterly returns caused by widened mispricings, Bayesian investors update downward on manager skill and submit redemption notices. The fund manager must liquidate positions to return cash, triggering fire sales that further depress prices. Consequently, institutional arbitrage capital is pro-cyclical: abundant when mispricings are narrow and scarce when dislocations are widest.

Research

Problem 1.10 · Synchronization Risk vs Noise-Trader Risk

Contrast Abreu and Brunnermeier's (2002) synchronization risk with DSSW (1990) noise-trader risk in explaining why rational arbitrageurs ride asset price bubbles.

Solution

DSSW noise-trader risk assumes rational arbitrageurs know prices will revert but fear that sentiment will worsen at their liquidation horizon. Abreu and Brunnermeier's synchronization risk introduces dispersion in the timing of when arbitrageurs become aware of an overpricing. Even when each arbitrageur knows the asset is overvalued, no single trader has sufficient capital to burst the bubble alone. Arbitrageurs face a coordination problem: they ride the bubble to harvest positive returns, attempting to exit just prior to the coordinated crash.

Problem 1.11 · Design of an Empirical Test for Performance-Based Arbitrage

Propose an empirical research design using Form 13F institutional holdings to test whether hedge fund liquidations during market drawdowns amplify price dislocations.

Solution

Identify hedge funds facing severe quarterly outflows using Morningstar/13F institutional ownership panels. Construct a measure of 'crowded fire-sale exposure' for each stock: the aggregate fraction of shares held by distressed, outflow-facing funds. Regress subsequent abnormal stock returns around crisis quarters on crowded fire-sale exposure. A finding that high-exposure stocks suffer steep transitory price drops followed by predictable 12-month reversals validates performance-based limits to arbitrage.

Problem 1.12 · Survival of Noise Traders Under Wealth Accumulation

Discuss whether noise traders can dominate the market in the infinite-horizon limit if their relative risk aversion is logarithmic.

Solution

In the long run, wealth dynamics depend on the log growth rate of wealth \(\mathbb{E}[\ln(W_{t+1}/W_t)]\). Because the logarithm is strictly concave, excessive portfolio volatility severely penalizes the long-run compound geometric growth rate of wealth. Even if noise traders earn higher arithmetic mean returns (\(\mathbb{E}[R_n] > \mathbb{E}[R_a]\)), excessive variance can drive their share of total market wealth to zero almost surely as \(T \to \infty\), re-establishing a modified form of market selection.

  1. What is the central mechanism of noise-trader risk in the DSSW (1990) model?
