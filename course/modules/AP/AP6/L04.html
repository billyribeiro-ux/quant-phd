For seven decades, empirical asset pricing treated portfolio quantities as unobservable abstractions, focusing exclusively on pricing equations and factor regressions. Yet in modern markets, institutional equity holdings are comprehensively disclosed in regulatory filings (SEC Form 13F). Koijen and Yogo (2019) pioneered demand-system asset pricing by estimating institutional portfolio choices directly as functions of prices, characteristics, and latent demand. By inverting the market clearing condition, this approach reveals how institutional mandate flows, passive indexing shifts, and regulatory constraints dictate the cross-section of stock prices and allows counterfactual analysis impossible in standard factor models.

1From factor models to demand systems

Standard asset pricing models (CAPM, Arbitrage Pricing Theory, Fama-French factors) operate under the assumption of unobservable aggregate demand. They relate asset returns to covariances with statistical factor proxies. However, factor models suffer from three fundamental limitations:

  • No counterfactual capabilities: A factor model cannot answer structural policy questions: What happens to Apple's stock price if Vanguard index fund inflows increase by $50 billion? What happens if life insurers face tighter capital rules?
  • Zero quantity discipline: Factor models ignore portfolio holdings. Two assets with identical factor betas are assumed to have identical prices, even if one is held entirely by passive indexers and the other by active hedge funds.
  • Fictitious representative investor: Real markets feature massive heterogeneity across mutual funds, pension funds, insurers, hedge funds, and foreign investors, each subject to distinct regulatory constraints and mandates.
Definition 1.1 (demand-system asset pricing)

Demand-system asset pricing is an empirical equilibrium framework that specifies and estimates the portfolio demand functions of heterogeneous institutional investors over asset characteristics and prices, and determines equilibrium prices by clearing the market across all assets simultaneously.

2The Koijen & Yogo (2019) portfolio demand specification

Consider an economy with \(I\) institutional investor types indexed by \(i = 1, \dots, I\) and \(N\) assets indexed by \(n = 1, \dots, N\). Asset 0 denotes an outside asset (cash or non-disclosed assets).

Let \(w_{i,n}\) denote the portfolio weight of institution \(i\) in asset \(n\), defined as the dollar holdings divided by total assets under management \(A_i\). To ensure portfolio weights remain strictly positive and sum to at most 1, Koijen and Yogo (2019) specify a multinomial logit portfolio choice rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} w_{i,n} = \frac{\exp(\delta_{i,n})}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^N \exp(\delta_{i,k})}, \end{aligned} \]

where the outside asset weight is \(w_{i,0} = 1 - \sum_{n=1}^N w_{i,n} = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^N \exp(\delta_{i,k})}\).

Definition 2.1 (latent demand log-linear structure)

The latent demand \(\delta_{i,n}\) for asset \(n\) by institution \(i\) is parameterized as a linear function of log market price \(p_n \equiv \ln(P_n)\), observed asset characteristics \(X_n\), and an unobserved latent demand shock \(\epsilon_{i,n}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_{i,n} = \alpha_i - \beta_i p_n + \gamma_i^\top X_n + \epsilon_{i,n}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\beta_i > 0\) represents the institution's price elasticity of demand parameter, \(X_n\) includes firm characteristics (log book-to-market, profitability, size, investment), and \(\epsilon_{i,n}\) captures unobserved institutional preferences or private signals.

Taking the log ratio of portfolio weight \(w_{i,n}\) to the outside asset weight \(w_{i,0}\) yields the structural estimation equation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln\left(\frac{w_{i,n}}{w_{i,0}}\right) = \alpha_i - \beta_i p_n + \gamma_i^\top X_n + \epsilon_{i,n}. \end{aligned} \]

3Endogeneity of price and 2SLS identification

Estimating this equation via ordinary least squares produces severe omitted-variable bias. The equilibrium price \(p_n\) is determined in market clearing and is therefore endogenous to the latent demand shocks \(\epsilon_{i,n}\). If a stock has high unobserved quality or positive investor sentiment (\(\epsilon_{i,n} > 0\)), institutions desire to hold more of it, bidding up the equilibrium market price \(p_n\). Consequently, \(\operatorname{Cov}(p_n, \epsilon_{i,n}) > 0\).

Proposition 3.1 (attenuation bias of naive OLS)

Because equilibrium prices covary positively with latent demand shocks, naive OLS regression of log portfolio shares on \(-p_n\) severely biases the estimated elasticity \(\hat\beta_i^{\text{OLS}}\) toward zero (or even yields a negative price coefficient), giving the false illusion that asset demand is downward-sloping or upward-sloping.

Theorem 3.1 (investment universe instrumental variable)

Let \(\mathcal{U}_j\) denote the set of assets that institution \(j \ne i\) is permitted or historically accustomed to trade (its investment universe). Under the assumption that other institutions' investment mandates are determined by institutional history and are orthogonal to institution \(i\)'s idiosyncratic latent shock \(\epsilon_{i,n}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Z_{i,n} \epsilon_{i,n}] = 0, \end{aligned} \]

the counterfactual demand generated by other institutions' mandates:

\[ \begin{aligned} Z_{i,n} = \ln \left( \sum_{j \ne i} A_j \cdot \frac{\mathbf{1}\{n \in \mathcal{U}_j\}}{1 + \sum_{k \in \mathcal{U}_j} 1} \right) \end{aligned} \]

is a valid instrument that shifts the equilibrium price \(p_n\) exogenously, identifying the structural price elasticity \(\beta_i\) via Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS).

Proof

The instrument \(Z_{i,n}\) depends strictly on the AUM \(A_j\) and universe mandates \(\mathcal{U}_j\) of other institutions \(j \ne i\). Because \(Z_{i,n}\) reflects exogenous mandate restrictions (e.g. mutual fund charter clauses, S&P benchmark rules), it is uncorrelated with institution \(i\)'s unmodelled preference shock \(\epsilon_{i,n}\), satisfying instrument exogeneity \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{i,n}, \epsilon_{i,n}) = 0\).

For relevance, when an asset \(n\) is included in the mandates of many large institutions (\(n \in \mathcal{U}_j\)), aggregate buying demand is higher, raising the market clearing price \(p_n\). The first-stage regression \(\mathbb{E}[p_n \mid Z, X] = \pi_0 + \pi_z Z_{i,n} + \pi_x^\top X_n\) yields \(\pi_z > 0\). Under standard 2SLS asymptotics, the structural parameter vector \(\theta_i = (\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i^\top)^\top\) is consistently estimated by \(\hat\theta_i^{\text{2SLS}} = (M_i^\top M_i)^{-1} M_i^\top y_i\), where \(M_i\) projects the endogenous price onto the instrument space.

4Asset market clearing and price determination

Given the estimated structural demand parameters \((\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i)\) and latent demand \(\epsilon_{i,n}\), equilibrium prices \(P^* = (P_1^*, \dots, P_N^*)^\top\) are determined by the market clearing condition: the dollar supply of each stock must equal total institutional dollar demand.

Definition 4.1 (market clearing condition)

For each asset \(n \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), total market capitalization equals the sum of dollar allocations across all institutions:

\[ \begin{aligned} P_n^* Q_n = \sum_{i=1}^I A_i \cdot w_{i,n}(P^*), \end{aligned} \]

where \(Q_n\) is total shares outstanding, \(A_i\) is AUM of institution \(i\), and \(w_{i,n}(P^*)\) is the logit portfolio weight evaluated at the equilibrium price vector.

Lab 1 · Koijen-Yogo Demand System 2SLS Elasticity Estimation

Under unobserved latent asset quality, naive OLS regression of log portfolio demand on market prices severely underestimates price elasticity due to positive endogeneity bias. Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) using exogenous investment universe instruments recovers the true structural elasticity beta = 1.65 with high first-stage F-statistic.

"""Lab AP6-L04-1: Koijen-Yogo Demand System 2SLS Elasticity Estimation.

SIMULATED: Replicates the Koijen & Yogo (2019) demand system estimation.
Demonstrates the severe endogeneity bias of naive OLS due to unobserved latent demand,
and shows how exogenous investment universe instruments recover the true structural price elasticity.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    n_stocks = 200
    true_beta = 1.65  # true structural demand elasticity
    true_gamma = np.array([0.75, -0.40])  # loadings on log market equity and book-to-market

    # Stock characteristics X: log market equity and book-to-market
    x_chars = np.zeros((n_stocks, 2))
    x_chars[:, 0] = rng.normal(loc=2.0, scale=0.9, size=n_stocks)
    x_chars[:, 1] = rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=0.5, size=n_stocks)

    # Exogenous instrument Z (e.g. index investment universe mandate / supply shocks)
    z_inst = np.zeros((n_stocks, 2))
    z_inst[:, 0] = 0.65 * x_chars[:, 0] + 0.70 * rng.standard_normal(n_stocks)
    z_inst[:, 1] = rng.uniform(-1.0, 1.0, size=n_stocks)

    # Unobserved latent quality / demand shock delta_0
    # Latent demand is positively correlated with equilibrium prices (high quality => high price)
    latent_quality = 0.8 * rng.standard_normal(n_stocks)
    log_prices = 1.0 + 0.45 * z_inst[:, 0] + 0.35 * z_inst[:, 1] + 0.50 * latent_quality

    # True institutional portfolio demand:
    # y_n = ln(w_n / w_0) = alpha - beta * log_p + gamma' X + latent_quality
    alpha_true = 0.50
    y_demand = alpha_true - true_beta * log_prices + x_chars @ true_gamma + latent_quality

    # 1. Naive OLS regression of y on [-log_prices, X]:
    # Correlated omitted variable (latent_quality) severely biases beta toward zero or wrong sign!
    m_ols = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_stocks), -log_prices, x_chars])
    theta_ols = np.linalg.lstsq(m_ols, y_demand, rcond=None)[0]
    beta_ols = float(theta_ols[1])

    # 2. 2SLS IV Estimation:
    # First stage: regress log_p on [instruments, characteristics]
    w_first = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_stocks), z_inst, x_chars])
    gamma_first = np.linalg.lstsq(w_first, log_prices, rcond=None)[0]
    hat_log_p = w_first @ gamma_first

    # First stage F-statistic on instruments
    res_ur = log_prices - hat_log_p
    ssr_ur = float(np.sum(res_ur**2))
    w_restr = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_stocks), x_chars])
    gamma_restr = np.linalg.lstsq(w_restr, log_prices, rcond=None)[0]
    ssr_r = float(np.sum((log_prices - w_restr @ gamma_restr) ** 2))
    q_df = z_inst.shape[1]
    df_resid = n_stocks - w_first.shape[1]
    f_stat = float(((ssr_r - ssr_ur) / q_df) / (ssr_ur / df_resid))

    # Second stage: regress y on [-hat_log_p, X]
    m_iv = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_stocks), -hat_log_p, x_chars])
    theta_iv = np.linalg.lstsq(m_iv, y_demand, rcond=None)[0]
    beta_iv = float(theta_iv[1])
    gamma_iv = theta_iv[2:]

    print("Koijen & Yogo (2019) Demand System Elasticity Estimation")
    print(f"Number of cross-sectional stocks N: {n_stocks}")
    print(f"True structural price elasticity:  {true_beta:.2f}")
    print(f"First-stage IV F-statistic:        {f_stat:.2f} (> 10 stock-Yogo bound)")
    print(f"Naive OLS estimated elasticity:    {beta_ols:.4f} (biased toward 0)")
    print(f"2SLS IV estimated elasticity:      {beta_iv:.4f}")
    print(f"2SLS elasticity recovery error:    {abs(beta_iv - true_beta):.4f}")
    print(f"True characteristic loadings:      [{true_gamma[0]:.2f}, {true_gamma[1]:.2f}]")
    print(f"2SLS characteristic loadings:      [{gamma_iv[0]:.2f}, {gamma_iv[1]:.2f}]")
    print(f"OLS severely understates elasticity: {bool(beta_ols < 0.5 * true_beta)}")


main()

5Where the canon breaks

The demand-system approach exposes fundamental contradictions in classical finance:

  • Refutation of infinite elasticity: Classical finance (Scholes 1972) assumed that individual stock demand curves are perfectly horizontal (\(\beta \to \infty\)) because close substitute assets exist. Koijen and Yogo (2019) establish empirically that institutional demand is highly inelastic, with average price elasticities of only 1.7.
  • Latent demand dominates fundamental news: Variance decompositions conducted by Koijen and Yogo demonstrate that variations in firm fundamentals (dividends, earnings, asset growth) account for less than 30% of cross-sectional stock return variance. Over 70% of price variance is driven by shifts in institutional latent demand and mandate flows.
  • The rise of passive indexing: In traditional models, passive index funds are invisible because they do not trade actively. In a demand system, passive indexers have a price elasticity of zero (\(\beta = 0\)). As passive indexing grows, the aggregate market demand curve becomes progressively steeper and more inelastic.

6Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Regressing portfolio weights on prices via OLS gives an unbiased estimate of institutional price sensitivity.”

    Prices are endogenous market-clearing equilibrium variables. A positive latent demand shock raises both the institution's desired portfolio share and the market price, causing OLS to severely underestimate true structural price elasticity.

  2. “Because stocks in the same industry have correlated cash flows, their demand curves must be perfectly flat.”

    Empirical demand-system estimates show that even within narrowly defined industries, institutional portfolio mandates, benchmarking constraints, and liquidity requirements make demand curves steep and downward-sloping.

  3. “Demand-system asset pricing is just a reduced-form curve-fitting exercise that lacks economic theory.”

    The multinomial logit portfolio demand function is the exact closed-form solution to utility maximization under mean-variance preferences with an unobserved non-pecuniary quality shock or entropy-regularized portfolio optimization.

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate logit portfolio weights

An institution has latent demand values for three assets: \(\delta_1 = 1.2\), \(\delta_2 = 0.5\), and \(\delta_3 = -0.3\). Compute the institutional portfolio weights \(w_1, w_2, w_3\) and the outside cash asset weight \(w_0\).

Solution

Compute exponentials: \(\exp(\delta_1) = \exp(1.2) \approx 3.3201\), \(\exp(\delta_2) = \exp(0.5) \approx 1.6487\), \(\exp(\delta_3) = \exp(-0.3) \approx 0.7408\). The denominator is \(1 + \sum_{k=1}^3 \exp(\delta_k) = 1 + 3.3201 + 1.6487 + 0.7408 = 6.7096\). Outside cash weight is \(w_0 = 1 / 6.7096 \approx 0.1490\) (14.90%). Risky asset weights are \(w_1 = 3.3201 / 6.7096 = 0.4948\) (49.48%), \(w_2 = 1.6487 / 6.7096 = 0.2457\) (24.57%), and \(w_3 = 0.7408 / 6.7096 = 0.1104\) (11.04%). Notice \(w_0 + w_1 + w_2 + w_3 = 1.000\).

M2 · First-stage F-statistic calculation

In a first-stage regression of log price on 2 instruments and 3 exogenous characteristics for \(N = 100\) stocks, the unrestricted sum of squared residuals is \(\text{SSR}_{ur} = 12.50\) and the restricted sum of squared residuals (excluding instruments) is \(\text{SSR}_r = 18.20\). Compute the first-stage F-statistic and compare with the Stock-Yogo weak instrument threshold of 10.

Solution

Degrees of freedom for instruments: \(q = 2\). Residual degrees of freedom: \(df_{\text{resid}} = N - K = 100 - (1 + 2 + 3) = 94\). The F-statistic is \(F = \frac{(\text{SSR}_r - \text{SSR}_{ur})/q}{\text{SSR}_{ur}/df_{\text{resid}}} = \frac{(18.20 - 12.50)/2}{12.50 / 94} = \frac{5.70 / 2}{0.13298} = \frac{2.85}{0.13298} \approx 21.43\). Since \(21.43 > 10\), the instruments easily pass the Stock-Yogo weak-instrument threshold.

M3 · Market clearing dollar demand

Two institutional sectors manage \(A_1 = \$600\) billion and \(A_2 = \$400\) billion. Their portfolio weights for stock \(n\) are \(w_{1,n} = 0.030\) and \(w_{2,n} = 0.015\). The stock has \(Q_n = 500\) million shares outstanding. Compute total dollar demand and the market clearing price \(P_n^*\).

Solution

Total dollar demand is \(D_n = A_1 w_{1,n} + A_2 w_{2,n} = (600 \times 0.030) + (400 \times 0.015) = 18.0 + 6.0 = \$24.0\) billion. In market clearing, \(P_n^* Q_n = D_n \implies P_n^* = D_n / Q_n = \$24,000\text{ million} / 500\text{ million shares} = \$48.00\).

Proof

P1 · Derivation of own-price elasticity of portfolio weight

Prove that under the Koijen-Yogo logit specification \(\ln(w_{i,n}/w_{i,0}) = \alpha_i - \beta_i \ln P_n + \gamma_i^\top X_n\), the elasticity of portfolio weight \(w_{i,n}\) with respect to price \(P_n\) is \(\frac{\partial \ln w_{i,n}}{\partial \ln P_n} = -\beta_i (1 - w_{i,n})\).

Solution

Recall \(w_{i,n} = \frac{\exp(\delta_{i,n})}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^N \exp(\delta_{i,k})}\). Taking the natural logarithm: \(\ln w_{i,n} = \delta_{i,n} - \ln\big(1 + \sum_{k=1}^N \exp(\delta_{i,k})\big)\). Differentiating with respect to \(\ln P_n\): \(\frac{\partial \ln w_{i,n}}{\partial \ln P_n} = \frac{\partial \delta_{i,n}}{\partial \ln P_n} - \frac{\exp(\delta_{i,n})}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^N \exp(\delta_{i,k})} \frac{\partial \delta_{i,n}}{\partial \ln P_n}\). Since \(\delta_{i,n} = \alpha_i - \beta_i \ln P_n + \dots\), we have \(\frac{\partial \delta_{i,n}}{\partial \ln P_n} = -\beta_i\). Substituting: \(\frac{\partial \ln w_{i,n}}{\partial \ln P_n} = -\beta_i - w_{i,n}(-\beta_i) = -\beta_i (1 - w_{i,n})\).

P2 · Cross-price elasticity between assets

Prove that for any two distinct assets \(n \ne m\), the cross-price elasticity of demand is strictly positive: \(\frac{\partial \ln w_{i,n}}{\partial \ln P_m} = \beta_i w_{i,m} > 0\), establishing gross substitutability.

Solution

From \(\ln w_{i,n} = \delta_{i,n} - \ln\big(1 + \sum_{k=1}^N \exp(\delta_{i,k})\big)\), note that \(\delta_{i,n}\) does not depend on \(\ln P_m\). Differentiating with respect to \(\ln P_m\) yields \(\frac{\partial \ln w_{i,n}}{\partial \ln P_m} = 0 - \frac{\exp(\delta_{i,m})}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^N \exp(\delta_{i,k})} \frac{\partial \delta_{i,m}}{\partial \ln P_m} = -w_{i,m} (-\beta_i) = \beta_i w_{i,m}\). Since \(\beta_i > 0\) and \(w_{i,m} > 0\), the cross-price elasticity is strictly positive. A price increase in asset \(m\) increases the portfolio weight allocated to asset \(n\).

P3 · Contraction mapping property of market clearing

Show that the logarithmic market clearing condition \(p_n = \ln\big(\sum_i A_i w_{i,n}(p)\big) - \ln(Q_n)\) defines a contraction mapping under suitable damping when \(\beta_i > 0\).

Solution

Let \(T(p)_n = \ln\big(\sum_{i=1}^I A_i w_{i,n}(p)\big) - \ln(Q_n)\). From Problem P1, \(\frac{\partial \ln w_{i,n}}{\partial p_n} = -\beta_i (1 - w_{i,n}) < 0\). Aggregate dollar demand is \(D_n(p) = \sum_i A_i w_{i,n}(p)\). The Jacobian diagonal element is \(\frac{\partial T(p)_n}{\partial p_n} = \frac{1}{D_n} \sum_i A_i \frac{\partial w_{i,n}}{\partial p_n} = -\sum_i \frac{A_i w_{i,n}}{D_n} \beta_i (1 - w_{i,n}) < 0\). For damped iteration \(p^{(k+1)} = (1 - \omega) p^{(k)} + \omega T(p^{(k)})\), the derivative of the damped operator is \(1 - \omega \big(1 + \bar\beta_n\big)\). Choosing \(\omega \in \big(0, \frac{2}{1 + \max_n \bar\beta_n}\big)\) ensures the spectral radius is strictly bounded inside \((-1, 1)\), guaranteeing uniform contraction and unique convergence by the Banach fixed point theorem.

Empirical

E1 · Mutual fund vs. hedge fund demand elasticity

Empirical demand-system estimates find that mutual funds have price elasticities \(\beta \approx 0.8\), while hedge funds have \(\beta \approx 2.5\). Interpret this difference in institutional behavior.

Solution

Mutual funds manage capital against commercial benchmark indices (Russell 1000, S&P 500) and face tight tracking error constraints. They cannot drastically deviate from index weights even when prices diverge from fundamentals, resulting in low price sensitivity (\(\beta \approx 0.8\)). Hedge funds have flexible unconstrained absolute-return mandates, allowing them to aggressively scale into undervalued securities, manifesting as significantly higher elasticity (\(\beta \approx 2.5\)).

E2 · Counterfactual impact of passive investing growth

Using the demand-system framework, explain what happens to market equilibrium prices when capital shifts from active mutual funds (\(\beta = 1.5\)) to passive index funds (\(\beta = 0\)).

Solution

Passive indexers purchase stocks in direct proportion to market index weights without regard to price, having zero price elasticity. As passive market share grows, the AUM-weighted aggregate elasticity \(\bar\beta = \sum s_i \beta_i\) declines monotonically. The aggregate demand curve becomes steeper, which magnifies the price impact of liquidity shocks and increases stock idiosyncratic volatility.

E3 · Form 13F filing threshold and reporting blind spots

Under SEC regulations, Form 13F filings are required quarterly only for institutional managers with over $100 million in 13F securities, covering long US equity positions. How does this reporting cutoff affect empirical demand-system estimation?

Solution

The 13F threshold excludes small retail investors, foreign institutions without direct US presence, and short positions. Koijen and Yogo address this by aggregating all non-13F market capitalization into an explicit residual "household and unobserved sector." The demand of this residual sector is identified by market clearing: residual holdings equal total supply minus observed 13F institutional holdings.

Research

R1 · Granular instrumental variables (Gabaix & Koijen 2024)

How does the granular instrumental variables (GIV) methodology identify macroeconomic demand shocks in asset markets?

Solution

GIV exploits the fat-tailed size distribution of financial institutions. By taking the difference between size-weighted idiosyncratic flows of mega-institutions (e.g. Vanguard, BlackRock) and equal-weighted flows of all institutions, GIV constructs an exogenous aggregate flow instrument that is purged of common macroeconomic shocks, providing clean identification of aggregate market elasticity.

R2 · Life insurer regulatory capital frictions (Koijen & Yogo 2015)

How do statutory risk-based capital (RBC) rules distort the demand functions of life insurance companies across corporate bonds?

Solution

Life insurance regulation assigns discrete capital charges based on credit ratings (e.g., NAIC-1 vs. NAIC-2). Koijen and Yogo (2015) show that insurers drastically substitute into the highest-yielding bonds within a rating category to maximize yield per unit of statutory capital, creating large non-linear price distortions at credit rating boundaries.

R3 · Demand systems with endogenous characteristics

In Koijen & Yogo (2019), firm characteristics \(X_n\) (such as profitability and investment) are treated as predetermined. How does corporate policy endogeneity (firms issuing shares or altering investment in response to high stock prices) alter long-run demand-system equilibrium?

Solution

If firms issue equity when prices are elevated (market timing), share supply \(Q_n(P_n)\) becomes price-elastic. Corporate share issuance acts as a long-run dampener on demand shocks: initial price spikes are gradually mitigated as firms supply additional equity to absorb institutional demand, restoring long-run elasticity.
