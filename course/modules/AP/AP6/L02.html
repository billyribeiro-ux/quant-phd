Intermediary Asset Pricing & Balance Sheet Constraints

Standard consumption-based asset pricing models assume that the representative household directly trades all assets and prices risk via its marginal utility of consumption. In modern financial markets, however, households delegate investments to institutions, while sophisticated securities (swaps, options, credit default swaps, foreign exchange) are intermediated exclusively by primary dealer banks. When adverse shocks deplete dealer equity capital, their marginal value of wealth surges, balance sheet constraints bind, and risk premia spike across virtually all asset classes simultaneously. Intermediary asset pricing replaces household consumption with intermediary balance sheet health as the true empirical driver of the economy-wide stochastic discount factor.

1The intermediary as the marginal investor

In standard macroeconomic models, the marginal investor is an unconstrained representative consumer whose consumption Euler equation prices all payoffs. As shown in Lesson 1, limits to arbitrage prevent households from directly exploiting complex relative-value mispricings. Instead, specialized financial intermediaries—specifically primary dealer banks and leveraged funds—clear the market.

Definition 1.1 (intermediary asset pricing)

Intermediary asset pricing is the theoretical and empirical framework in which asset prices and risk premia are determined by the marginal value of wealth and balance sheet constraints of specialist financial intermediaries rather than the consumption of ultimate households.

Because intermediaries face capital regulations, collateral constraints, and financing frictions, the shadow price of their balance sheet capacity acts as a state variable for the stochastic discount factor (SDF). When intermediary capital is scarce, the effective risk aversion of the market rises, widening credit spreads, steepening option implied volatility smiles, and driving up expected returns across all risky assets.

2The He & Krishnamurthy (2013) dynamic equilibrium model

He and Krishnamurthy (2013) formulate a continuous-time equilibrium economy with two classes of agents: households and specialized intermediaries. Households cannot invest directly in risky capital due to expertise constraints and instead invest in intermediaries via equity and debt.

Definition 2.1 (intermediary equity capital ratio)

Let \(E_t\) denote the aggregate equity capitalization of specialized financial intermediaries, and let \(W_t = E_t + D_t\) denote total intermediary assets financed by equity and debt. The intermediary equity capital ratio is defined as:

\[ \begin{aligned} \eta_t = \frac{E_t}{E_t + D_t} = \frac{E_t}{W_t} \in (0, 1). \end{aligned} \]

Intermediaries raise equity from households subject to an agency moral-hazard constraint: intermediary managers must retain a fraction \(\alpha > 0\) of total equity on their personal account to prevent diversion. This contractual friction limits the inflow of outside equity during crises.

Theorem 2.1 (He-Krishnamurthy crisis non-linearity)

In the He-Krishnamurthy (2013) economy, the equilibrium risk premium on the aggregate risky asset is a decreasing, strictly convex function of the intermediary capital ratio \(\eta_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_t - r_t = \frac{\gamma}{\eta_t} \sigma^2, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\gamma\) is the manager's coefficient of relative risk aversion and \(\sigma\) is asset return volatility. When \(\eta_t\) falls below a critical threshold \(\eta^*\), the equity constraint binds, creating an abrupt crisis regime characterized by surging risk premia, elevated return volatility, and sharp balance sheet contraction.

Proof sketch

Intermediaries manage total wealth \(W_t\), of which their equity is \(E_t = \eta_t W_t\). The manager's personal wealth is \(\alpha E_t\). The manager invests all assets \(W_t\) in the risky technology with expected excess return \(\mu_t - r_t\) and volatility \(\sigma\). The manager's portfolio choice problem maximizes expected utility over personal wealth:

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{\theta_t} \left\{ \theta_t (\mu_t - r_t) - \frac{\gamma}{2} \theta_t^2 \sigma^2 \right\}, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\theta_t\) is the portfolio weight on risky assets relative to personal equity. By the first-order condition, \(\theta_t = \frac{\mu_t - r_t}{\gamma \sigma^2}\). In market clearing, the intermediary must absorb the entire asset stock: \(\theta_t \alpha E_t = \alpha W_t \implies \theta_t = \frac{W_t}{E_t} = \frac{1}{\eta_t}\). Equating the first-order condition to market clearing yields \(\frac{1}{\eta_t} = \frac{\mu_t - r_t}{\gamma \sigma^2}\), which rearranges to \(\mu_t - r_t = \frac{\gamma}{\eta_t} \sigma^2\). The second derivative with respect to \(\eta_t\) is \(\frac{2\gamma \sigma^2}{\eta_t^3} > 0\), establishing strict convexity.

3Broker-dealer leverage: Adrian, Etula & Muir (2014)

Adrian, Etula, and Muir (2014) present an empirical implementation of intermediary asset pricing focusing on Security Broker-Dealers. Using Federal Reserve Flow of Funds aggregate data, they define broker-dealer leverage as:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Lev}_t = \frac{\text{Total Financial Assets}_t}{\text{Total Equity}_t}. \end{aligned} \]
Proposition 3.1 (Adrian-Etula-Muir leverage SDF)

Broker-dealer leverage shocks act as a single-factor linear SDF for the cross-section of equities and corporate bonds:

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1} = 1 - b_{\text{Lev}} \Delta \text{Lev}_{t+1}, \end{aligned} \]

where broker-dealer leverage expands procyclically during booms and contracts during crises. Innovations to leverage carry a positive price of risk, explaining cross-sectional return variation in size, book-to-market, and credit risk portfolios.

4Primary dealer equity capital ratio: He, Kelly & Manela (2017)

While Adrian et al. (2014) focus on regulatory book leverage of broker-dealer subsidiaries, He, Kelly, and Manela (2017) argue that the true economic constraint operates at the bank holding company level, and that market values rather than book values govern marginal risk bearing. They identify the ultimate market makers as the Federal Reserve Bank of New York Primary Dealers.

Definition 4.1 (HKM primary dealer capital ratio)

The HKM dealer capital ratio \(\eta_t\) is the market value of common equity divided by the market value of total assets (market equity plus book debt) aggregated across all NY Fed primary dealer holding companies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \eta_t = \frac{\sum_{i \in \text{Dealers}} \text{Market Equity}_{i,t}}{\sum_{i \in \text{Dealers}} (\text{Market Equity}_{i,t} + \text{Book Debt}_{i,t})}. \end{aligned} \]

Because market equity fluctuates immediately with asset values, \(\eta_t\) falls during market downturns. The empirical intermediary factor is the innovation to this ratio extracted from an AR(1) process:

\[ \begin{aligned} \eta_t = c + \phi \eta_{t-1} + u_t, \quad \Delta \eta_t \equiv u_t. \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 4.1 (intermediary capital asset pricing relation)

In a multi-asset economy where primary dealers are the marginal price-setters, the expected excess return on any test asset \(j\) satisfies the linear factor pricing relation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[R_{j,t+1}^e] = \beta_j^\eta \lambda_\eta, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\beta_j^\eta = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_{j,t+1}^e, u_{t+1})}{\operatorname{Var}(u_{t+1})}\) is the asset's dealer capital beta, and the risk price \(\lambda_\eta > 0\) is strictly positive and equal across all asset classes.

Proof

Under the no-arbitrage condition, there exists an SDF \(M_{t+1}\) such that \(\mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1} R_{j,t+1}^e] = 0\) for all excess returns \(R_j^e\). First-order Taylor linearization of the intermediary marginal utility of wealth with respect to capital ratio shock \(u_{t+1}\) gives the linear representation:

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1} = 1 - b_\eta (u_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}[u_{t+1}]). \end{aligned} \]

Substituting this SDF into the no-arbitrage equation yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[R_{j,t+1}^e] = \operatorname{Cov}(M_{t+1}, R_{j,t+1}^e) \cdot (-R_f) = b_\eta R_f \operatorname{Cov}(u_{t+1}, R_{j,t+1}^e). \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying and dividing by \(\operatorname{Var}(u_{t+1})\) gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[R_{j,t+1}^e] = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_{j,t+1}^e, u_{t+1})}{\operatorname{Var}(u_{t+1})} \cdot \big[ b_\eta R_f \operatorname{Var}(u_{t+1}) \big] = \beta_j^\eta \lambda_\eta, \end{aligned} \]

where \(\lambda_\eta \equiv b_\eta R_f \operatorname{Var}(u_{t+1}) > 0\). This holds identically for every traded asset class.

Lab 1 · Primary Dealer Intermediary Capital Ratio SDF Estimation

Under the He-Kelly-Manela framework, extracting AR(1) innovations from the aggregate primary dealer equity capital ratio produces a systematic risk factor that prices multiple asset classes. High-beta assets (options, equities, high-yield credit) demand higher expected returns, yielding a statistically positive risk price lambda_eta and high cross-sectional R-squared.

"""Lab AP6-L02-1: Primary Dealer Intermediary Capital Ratio SDF Estimation.

SIMULATED: Estimates the He, Kelly & Manela (2017) intermediary asset pricing model.
Constructs aggregate dealer equity capital ratio eta_t, extracts AR(1) innovations u_t,
computes multi-asset factor betas beta_i^eta, and evaluates cross-sectional risk price lambda_eta.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    n_quarters = 160  # 40 years of quarterly data
    n_assets = 8  # 8 test asset classes: US equities, Corp bonds, MBS, FX, Options, CDS, Commodities, Treasuries

    # Simulate primary dealer equity capital ratio eta_t = Equity / (Equity + Debt)
    # Calibrated to HKM (2017) historical dealer equity capital ratio: mean ~8%, AR(1) persistence ~0.90
    phi_eta = 0.90
    mu_eta = 0.08 * (1.0 - phi_eta)
    sigma_eta = 0.012

    eta = np.zeros(n_quarters)
    eta[0] = 0.08
    for t in range(1, n_quarters):
        eta[t] = mu_eta + phi_eta * eta[t - 1] + sigma_eta * rng.standard_normal()

    # AR(1) shocks: u_t = eta_t - (mu + phi * eta_{t-1})
    x_ar = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_quarters - 1), eta[:-1]])
    ar_coefs = np.linalg.lstsq(x_ar, eta[1:], rcond=None)[0]
    estimated_phi = float(ar_coefs[1])
    dealer_shocks = eta[1:] - x_ar @ ar_coefs
    var_u = float(np.var(dealer_shocks, ddof=1))

    # True cross-sectional risk price for dealer capital: lambda_eta = 0.08 (8% annualized per unit beta)
    lambda_eta_true = 0.08 / 4.0  # quarterly: 2% per quarter
    true_betas = np.array([2.5, 1.8, 1.4, 0.9, 3.2, 1.1, 0.7, 0.3])

    # Simulate test asset excess returns R_{i,t}^e = beta_i * lambda + beta_i * u_t + e_{i,t}
    returns_excess = np.zeros((n_quarters - 1, n_assets))
    for j in range(n_assets):
        idio_vol = 0.03 + 0.005 * j
        returns_excess[:, j] = (
            true_betas[j] * lambda_eta_true
            + true_betas[j] * dealer_shocks
            + idio_vol * rng.standard_normal(n_quarters - 1)
        )

    # 1. Estimate time-series betas: beta_j = Cov(R_j^e, u) / Var(u)
    betas_hat = np.zeros(n_assets)
    for j in range(n_assets):
        cov_ju = float(np.cov(returns_excess[:, j], dealer_shocks)[0, 1])
        betas_hat[j] = cov_ju / var_u

    # 2. Cross-sectional regression: E[R_j^e] = lambda_eta * beta_j
    mean_excess_returns = np.mean(returns_excess, axis=0)
    risk_price_hat = float((betas_hat @ mean_excess_returns) / (betas_hat @ betas_hat))
    pricing_errors = mean_excess_returns - risk_price_hat * betas_hat
    cross_r2 = float(
        1.0 - np.sum(pricing_errors**2) / np.sum((mean_excess_returns - np.mean(mean_excess_returns)) ** 2)
    )

    # Annualize risk price and compute SDF factor loading b_eta
    annual_risk_price = risk_price_hat * 4.0
    b_eta = risk_price_hat / var_u

    print("He, Kelly & Manela (2017) Intermediary Asset Pricing Engine")
    print(f"Sample length T:                 {n_quarters} quarters")
    print(f"Estimated capital AR(1) phi:      {estimated_phi:.4f}")
    print(f"Dealer capital shock vol sigma_u: {np.sqrt(var_u):.4f}")
    print(f"Estimated dealer risk price:     {annual_risk_price * 100:.2f}% per year")
    print(f"Cross-sectional R^2:             {cross_r2 * 100:.2f}%")
    print(f"SDF capital factor loading b:    {b_eta:.2f}")
    print(f"Beta range across asset classes: [{np.min(betas_hat):.2f}, {np.max(betas_hat):.2f}]")
    print(f"Max pricing error (quarterly):   {np.max(np.abs(pricing_errors)):.4f}")


main()

5Where the canon breaks

The intermediary asset pricing revolution fundamentally overturns the consumption-CAPM paradigm:

  • Household consumption beta is uninformative: Decades of testing the consumption-CAPM established that household consumption growth is too smooth to explain the equity risk premium (the equity premium puzzle) and cannot price the cross-section of assets. Intermediary capital shocks, by contrast, exhibit massive cyclical volatility and accurately price the cross-section (He, Kelly & Manela 2017).
  • The cross-asset pricing divergence: Traditional factor models (Fama-French three-factor, five-factor) are equity-specific and completely fail when applied to corporate bonds, currencies, or option volatility surfaces. Intermediary capital functions as a universal pricing kernel spanning equity, credit, derivative, and currency markets under a single factor.
  • State-dependent pricing: In normal times, when dealer balance sheets are unconstrained, traditional factor betas may appear to dominate. In crisis regimes, intermediary capital constraints bind non-linearly, rendering classical linear models invalid (He & Krishnamurthy 2013).

6Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Because households hold the vast majority of total wealth in the economy, their preferences must determine asset prices.”

    Households hold wealth through passive intermediaries (mutual funds, pensions) that cannot participate in OTC derivative, repo, or currency markets. The marginal price-setting transactions that equate supply and demand in these complex markets are executed by primary dealer trading desks.

  2. “Broker-dealer leverage and dealer equity capital ratios measure the exact same economic phenomenon.”

    Adrian-Etula-Muir broker-dealer leverage is measured from regulatory book values of domestic dealer subsidiaries and moves procyclically. He-Kelly-Manela dealer capital ratio is measured using holding company market equity and moves countercyclically, reflecting market-wide valuation of financial balance sheet risk.

  3. “Intermediary capital risk is only relevant for distressed bank stocks, not for Treasuries or corporate bonds.”

    Dealers provide market liquidity and repo financing for Treasuries and corporate bonds. When dealer capital is depleted, the cost of financing inventories increases, widening credit spreads and Treasury basis spreads even on default-free debt.

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate primary dealer equity capital ratio

A primary dealer banking group has market capitalization of equity \(E = \$120\) billion and total book liabilities \(D = \$1,380\) billion. Calculate the intermediary capital ratio \(\eta\). If market equity drops by 25% while liabilities remain constant, what is the new capital ratio \(\eta'\)?

Solution

Initial capital ratio is \(\eta = 120 / (120 + 1380) = 120 / 1500 = 0.080\) (8.00%). When equity drops by 25%, new equity is \(E' = 120 \times 0.75 = \$90\) billion. The new capital ratio is \(\eta' = 90 / (90 + 1380) = 90 / 1470 \approx 0.0612\) (6.12%). The capital ratio drops by 1.88 percentage points (a 23.5% relative decline).

M2 · Factor beta and expected excess return calculation

An equity option portfolio has covariance with dealer capital ratio innovations of \(\operatorname{Cov}(R_i^e, u) = 0.00045\). Dealer capital innovation variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(u) = 0.00015\). The annualized dealer risk price is \(\lambda_\eta = 0.08\) (8.0%). Compute the asset's dealer beta \(\beta_i^\eta\) and its predicted annual excess return.

Solution

The dealer factor beta is \(\beta_i^\eta = \operatorname{Cov}(R_i^e, u) / \operatorname{Var}(u) = 0.00045 / 0.00015 = 3.00\). The predicted annual excess return is \(\mathbb{E}[R_i^e] = \beta_i^\eta \lambda_\eta = 3.00 \times 0.08 = 0.240\) (24.0% per year).

M3 · Non-linear He-Krishnamurthy risk premium

Under the He-Krishnamurthy formula \(\mu - r = \frac{\gamma}{\eta} \sigma^2\), suppose manager risk aversion is \(\gamma = 2.0\) and asset volatility is \(\sigma = 0.20\). Compute the equilibrium risk premium when capital is abundant at \(\eta = 0.10\) versus when capital is distressed at \(\eta = 0.04\).

Solution

When \(\eta = 0.10\): \(\mu - r = \frac{2.0}{0.10} \times (0.20)^2 = 20 \times 0.04 = 0.80\) (or 8.0% annualized if scaled). When \(\eta = 0.04\): \(\mu - r = \frac{2.0}{0.04} \times (0.20)^2 = 50 \times 0.04 = 2.00\) (or 20.0%). Depleting capital from 10% to 4% causes the equilibrium risk premium to surge by 2.5 times due to the \(1/\eta\) non-linearity.

P1 · Derivation of SDF factor loading

Prove that in a single-factor linear SDF model \(M = 1 - b_\eta (u - \bar{u})\), the factor loading \(b_\eta\) is related to the cross-sectional risk price \(\lambda_\eta\) by \(b_\eta = \frac{\lambda_\eta}{R_f \operatorname{Var}(u)}\).

Solution

For any excess return \(R_i^e\), the pricing identity is \(\mathbb{E}[M R_i^e] = 0\). Expanding the expectation yields \(\mathbb{E}[M]\mathbb{E}[R_i^e] + \operatorname{Cov}(M, R_i^e) = 0\). Since \(\mathbb{E}[M] = 1/R_f\), this gives \(\frac{1}{R_f}\mathbb{E}[R_i^e] = -\operatorname{Cov}(1 - b_\eta u, R_i^e) = b_\eta \operatorname{Cov}(u, R_i^e)\). Dividing by \(\operatorname{Var}(u)\), we obtain \(\mathbb{E}[R_i^e] = R_f b_\eta \operatorname{Var}(u) \beta_i^\eta\). Comparing this to the factor risk premium equation \(\mathbb{E}[R_i^e] = \lambda_\eta \beta_i^\eta\), we identify \(\lambda_\eta = R_f b_\eta \operatorname{Var}(u)\). Solving for \(b_\eta\) yields \(b_\eta = \frac{\lambda_\eta}{R_f \operatorname{Var}(u)}\).

P2 · Cross-sectional orthogonal pricing error property

Prove that when the risk price \(\lambda_\eta\) is estimated via cross-sectional ordinary least squares regression without intercept (\(\min_\lambda \sum_j (\bar{R}_j^e - \lambda \beta_j)^2\)), the vector of pricing errors \(\alpha\) is strictly orthogonal to the vector of factor betas: \(\beta^\top \alpha = 0\).

Solution

The OLS objective function is \(S(\lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^N (\bar{R}_j^e - \lambda \beta_j)^2 = (\bar{R}^e - \lambda \beta)^\top (\bar{R}^e - \lambda \beta)\). The first-order condition with respect to \(\lambda\) is \(\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = -2 \beta^\top (\bar{R}^e - \lambda \beta) = 0\). Recognizing that the pricing error vector is \(\alpha = \bar{R}^e - \lambda \beta\), the first-order condition immediately simplifies to \(-2 \beta^\top \alpha = 0 \implies \beta^\top \alpha = 0\). The pricing errors are orthogonal to the factor betas by construction.

P3 · Moral hazard constraint and equity capital rationing

Consider an intermediary manager who can secretly divert a fraction \(d \in [0, 1]\) of outside equity funds for personal benefit with deadweight cost \(\frac{c}{2} d^2\). Prove that incentive compatibility requires the manager to hold a strictly positive minimum share of inside equity \(\alpha^* > 0\).

Solution

Let total assets be \(W\) and outside equity be \(E_o = (1 - \alpha) W\). If the manager diverts fraction \(d\), they gain \(d E_o - \frac{c}{2} d^2 W\) and forfeit their legitimate share of final return \(\alpha R W\). The manager's diversion problem is \(\max_d \{ d (1 - \alpha) W - \frac{c}{2} d^2 W \}\). The first-order condition gives \(d^* = \frac{1 - \alpha}{c}\). To ensure zero diversion (\(d^* = 0\)) or enforce that diverting is dominated by honest investment, the manager's personal wealth skin-in-the-game must satisfy \(\alpha \ge 1 - c d_{\text{tol}}\). When outside monitoring is imperfect (\(c < \infty\)), the intermediary cannot raise unlimited outside equity without committing substantial inside capital \(\alpha^* > 0\), creating an endogenous equity constraint.

Empirical

E1 · The March 2020 Treasury market breakdown

During the COVID-19 panic in March 2020, even ultra-liquid off-the-run US Treasuries experienced unprecedented price discounts and illiquidity. How does intermediary asset pricing explain this phenomenon?

Solution

Foreign central banks and hedge funds engaged in massive cash-raising sales of US Treasuries. Because primary dealer balance sheets were already constrained by regulatory leverage caps (SLR) and internal Value-at-Risk limits, dealers could not absorb the hundreds of billions in Treasury supply without breaching capital constraints. The marginal cost of dealer balance sheet capacity spiked, driving cash-futures basis spreads to extreme levels until the Federal Reserve intervened with unprecedented daily asset purchases and temporary SLR exemptions.

E2 · FX carry trade crash risk and dealer capital

Why do high-interest-rate carry trade currencies experience catastrophic crashes during global financial distress? Relate this to dealer capital betas.

Solution

Carry trade strategies borrow in low-yielding funding currencies (JPY, CHF) and invest in high-yielding currencies (AUD, BRL). High-yield currencies have high positive betas with respect to global dealer capital: when global dealers suffer capital losses, they cut leverage and unwind currency carry trades simultaneously. Carry trade returns plunge precisely when the intermediary SDF is high, demonstrating that carry trade returns represent compensation for intermediary liquidity risk.

E3 · Equity option index volatility smirk

Why is the implied volatility of out-of-the-money S&P 500 put options consistently much higher than call options? How does intermediary asset pricing account for the expensive volatility surface?

Solution

Institutional end-users have structural demand to purchase index put options for crash protection. Primary dealers are the natural counterparties, taking the short side of index put options and hedging dynamically. Holding net short crash options exposes dealers to severe downside jump risk and requires massive margin reserves. Dealers charge a large liquidity and capital premium for warehousing crash risk, manifesting as a persistent implied volatility smirk that reflects dealer shadow costs.

Research

R1 · Market equity versus book equity in intermediary SDF

Compare the advantages and econometric vulnerabilities of using market equity capital ratios (He, Kelly & Manela) versus book equity leverage (Adrian, Etula & Muir).

Solution

Market equity reflects forward-looking solvency and incorporates immediate asset write-downs in real time, avoiding the multi-quarter reporting lag of accounting book equity. However, market equity contains the stock prices of the dealers themselves, which could induce mechanical correlation with the equity market factor. He, Kelly & Manela address this by showing that dealer capital ratio innovations price nine non-equity asset classes where mechanical correlation cannot explain cross-sectional explanatory power.

R2 · Primary dealer concentration post-2008

How has the consolidation of the NY Fed primary dealer network (from over 40 dealers in the 1990s to approximately 24 today) affected systemic asset pricing fragility?

Solution

Consolidation concentrates market-making capacity in a handful of global mega-banks (JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Citigroup, Morgan Stanley). While these institutions have higher regulatory capital ratios, the idiosyncratic distress of any single dealer now removes a substantial fraction of total market-making capacity. Systemic fragility becomes more concentrated, increasing the sensitivity of market-wide risk premia to individual dealer balance sheet shocks.

R3 · Non-bank financial intermediaries (Private credit & hedge funds)

As private credit and hedge funds increasingly intermediate debt and equity markets, how does the definition of the marginal intermediary evolve?

Solution

Traditional intermediary asset pricing models focus on bank holding companies regulated by the Fed. As market share shifts to non-bank financial intermediaries (shadow banks), the relevant state variable expands to include hedge fund equity capital, private credit dry powder, and repo haircut terms. However, because shadow banks rely on regulated primary dealers for prime brokerage, leverage, and repo financing, primary dealer balance sheets remain the ultimate bottleneck for market liquidity.
