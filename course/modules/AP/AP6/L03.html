Slow-Moving Capital & Dynamic Search Frictions

  • Uses: present value, simple returns, stochastic discount factor, limits to arbitrage, intermediary asset pricing
  • Introduces: slow-moving capital

  • Bpublished, not settled

Standard competitive market models assume that capital is perfectly fluid, flowing instantaneously to eliminate mispricings created by localized supply or liquidity shocks. In reality, large pools of investment capital are sluggish: institutional mandates, investment committee governance lags, search frictions, and contractual capital calls delay the arrival of outside capital by weeks, months, or quarters. When sudden asset supply surges—such as corporate bond issuance, index rebalancing, or forced liquidations—local dealer balance sheets become overloaded. The result is severe price undershooting followed by prolonged post-event recovery, generating predictable return dynamics driven entirely by slow-moving capital.

1The phenomenon of slow-moving capital

Modern telecommunications transmit trading signals in nanoseconds, yet the reallocation of real financial capital across asset classes operates with substantial inertia. As documented by Darrell Duffie (2010) in his American Finance Association Presidential Address, empirical asset markets display persistent supply-driven price dislocations across catastrophic reinsurance, convertible bonds, sovereign debt auctions, and mortgage-backed securities.

Definition 1.1 (slow-moving capital)

Slow-moving capital is the macroeconomic and market-microstructure friction wherein capital does not adjust instantaneously to asset price dislocations due to contractual commitments, search-and-matching delays, institutional governance protocols, and localized market segmentation.

The core structural consequence of slow-moving capital is a sharp bifurcation between short-run asset demand elasticity and long-run asset demand elasticity. In the immediate aftermath of a shock, asset demand is determined strictly by local dealers whose capacity is tightly bounded; over time, outside investors arrive, expanding effective elasticity.

2The Duffie (2010) dynamic search framework

Consider an infinite-horizon continuous-time economy with risk-free discount rate \(r\). An asset yields a constant dividend stream \(d > 0\) per unit of time, so its fundamental unconstrained price is:

\[ \begin{aligned} P^* = \frac{d}{r}. \end{aligned} \]

The market is populated by two groups of market participants:

  • Continuous market makers (dealers): Always present in the trading venue, with quadratic holding/inventory cost parameter \(\gamma_d > 0\).
  • Outside investors (slow capital): Initially inactive or searching. Each outside investor makes contact with the market at the jump times of an independent Poisson process with arrival intensity \(\lambda > 0\). Once in the market, outside investors have aggregate holding cost parameter \(\gamma_o \ll \gamma_d\).
Theorem 2.1 (dynamic supply shock absorption path)

At time \(t=0\), an unexpected supply shock dumps \(\Delta S\) units of the asset onto the market. If outside capital arrives with Poisson intensity \(\lambda\), the dealer inventory \(I(t)\) decays exponentially:

\[ \begin{aligned} I(t) = \Delta S \cdot e^{-\lambda t}. \end{aligned} \]

Assuming pure transitory dislocation (\(\gamma_o \to 0\)), the equilibrium market price path \(P(t)\) exhibits an initial discontinuous plunge followed by an exponential price rebound:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(t) = P^* - \frac{\gamma_d \Delta S}{r + \lambda} \cdot e^{-\lambda t}, \quad \forall t \ge 0. \end{aligned} \]
Proof

Because outside investors contact the market at Poisson rate \(\lambda\), the cumulative fraction of outside capital mobilized by time \(t\) is \(1 - e^{-\lambda t}\). Therefore, the residual excess inventory that must be warehoused on dealer balance sheets at time \(t\) is \(I(t) = \Delta S e^{-\lambda t}\).

The marginal holding cost incurred by dealers at date \(t\) is \(\gamma_d I(t) = \gamma_d \Delta S e^{-\lambda t}\). In competitive equilibrium, the asset price equals the present value of future dividends minus the present value of future marginal holding costs discounted at the risk-free rate \(r\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P(t) = \int_t^\infty e^{-r(s-t)} \big[ d - \gamma_d I(s) \big] \, \mathrm{d}s. \end{aligned} \]

Splitting the integral into the fundamental present value and the holding cost component:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(t) = \frac{d}{r} - \gamma_d \Delta S \int_t^\infty e^{-r(s-t)} e^{-\lambda s} \, \mathrm{d}s. \end{aligned} \]

Substituting \(u = s - t\), so that \(s = t + u\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_t^\infty e^{-r(s-t)} e^{-\lambda s} \, \mathrm{d}s &= e^{-\lambda t} \int_0^\infty e^{-(r + \lambda) u} \, \mathrm{d}u \\ &= \frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{r + \lambda}. \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying by \(\gamma_d \Delta S\) establishes the result:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(t) = P^* - \frac{\gamma_d \Delta S}{r + \lambda} e^{-\lambda t}. \end{aligned} \]
Definition 2.2 (dislocation half-life)

The half-life \(t_{1/2}\) of a supply-shock price dislocation is the elapsed time required for the initial price discount to decay by 50%:

\[ \begin{aligned} t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda}. \end{aligned} \]

3Institutional sources of capital sluggishness

Why cannot capital reallocate instantly in modern electronic markets? Empirical research identifies four structural drivers:

  • Mandates and organizational boundaries: Asset managers are bound by strict investment policy statements (IPS). An equity fund cannot purchase dislocated corporate bonds, even if the risk-adjusted return is extraordinary. Reallocating capital across asset classes requires board or committee approval.
  • Capital commitments and drawdowns: Private equity, private credit, and distressed debt funds operate via uncalled capital commitments. Calling committed capital takes 10 to 15 business days for capital call notices, followed by wire settlement.
  • Search and bilateral negotiation in OTC markets: Over-the-counter securities (syndicated loans, municipal bonds, bespoke derivatives) lack centralized order books. Sourcing matching counterparties requires bilateral dealer negotiation and ISDA master agreement onboarding.
  • Attention and cognitive bottlenecks: Portfolio managers do not monitor all markets continuously. Extreme market events (e.g. Hurricane Katrina catastrophe bond losses, or the 2007 quant meltdown) require forensic risk assessment before capital is deployed.

4Margin cascades and delayed supply absorption

The arrival delay of outside capital interacts catastrophically with leverage and margin requirements. When a supply shock depresses prices at \(t=0\), existing leveraged holders suffer immediate mark-to-market losses. Instead of buying the dip, prime brokers issue margin calls, forcing secondary liquidations that dump further supply onto already-congested dealer balance sheets before \(\lambda\)-arriving outside capital can absorb the initial dislocation.

Lab 1 · Slow-Moving Capital and Dynamic Supply Shock Absorption

Under the Duffie (2010) search framework, an unexpected supply shock dumps 20 shares onto dealer balance sheets at t=0. The initial price drops sharply due to dealer inventory costs, then recovers exponentially as outside capital arrives with search intensity lambda = 0.80 per year, yielding an analytical half-life of 0.87 years (10.4 months).

"""Lab AP6-L03-1: Slow-Moving Capital and Dynamic Supply Shock Absorption.

SIMULATED: Calibrates the Duffie (2010) search-and-matching framework where outside
investor capital arrives with search friction intensity lambda. Demonstrates the
immediate price drop (liquidity discount) and subsequent exponential price recovery.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def main() -> None:
    # Model parameters calibrated to Duffie (2010) Presidential Address
    # Asset pays steady dividend flow d = 5.0 per year. Risk-free rate r = 0.05
    r = 0.05
    d = 5.0
    p_fundamental = d / r  # $100.00 fundamental price

    # Sudden supply shock at t = 0: Delta S = 20 units dumped on the dealer market
    # Dealer inventory capacity parameter gamma_d (holding cost / risk aversion)
    # Outside capital arrival rate: lambda = 0.8 per year (mean search time 1.25 years)
    delta_s = 20.0
    gamma_d = 0.15  # dealer marginal holding cost per unit of inventory
    lambda_arrival = 0.80  # search arrival intensity of long-term investors

    # Time grid: t in [0, 5] years
    n_points = 21
    time_grid = np.linspace(0.0, 5.0, n_points)

    # Dealer inventory path: I(t) = Delta S * exp(-lambda * t)
    # For a pure transitory dislocation with gamma_outside -> 0:
    # Discount Delta P(t) = - gamma_d / (r + lambda) * Delta S * exp(-lambda * t)
    discount_initial = (gamma_d / (r + lambda_arrival)) * delta_s
    price_path = np.zeros(n_points)
    inventory_path = np.zeros(n_points)

    for i, t in enumerate(time_grid):
        inventory_path[i] = delta_s * np.exp(-lambda_arrival * t)
        dislocation_t = discount_initial * np.exp(-lambda_arrival * t)
        price_path[i] = p_fundamental - dislocation_t

    half_life = np.log(2.0) / lambda_arrival

    print("Duffie (2010) Slow-Moving Capital Supply Shock Dynamics")
    print(f"Fundamental asset price P*:       ${p_fundamental:.2f}")
    print(f"Supply shock magnitude Delta S:   {delta_s:.1f} shares")
    print(f"Outside capital search rate lambda: {lambda_arrival:.2f} / year")
    print(f"Dislocation half-life:            {half_life:.2f} years ({half_life * 12:.1f} months)")
    print(f"Post-shock price at t=0:          ${price_path[0]:.2f}")
    print(f"Initial liquidity discount:       ${discount_initial:.2f} ({discount_initial / p_fundamental * 100:.2f}%)")
    print(f"Recovered price at t=1 year:      ${price_path[4]:.2f}")
    print(f"Recovered price at t=5 years:     ${price_path[-1]:.2f}")
    print(f"Rebound share after 1 year:       {(price_path[4] - price_path[0]) / discount_initial * 100:.2f}%")


main()

5Where the canon breaks

The slow-moving capital framework refutes several core tenets of frictionless market efficiency:

  • Law of one price breakdowns without arbitrage: Identical cash flows trade at persistent price differentials across segmented markets because capital cannot flow across institutional silos without delay (Duffie 2010).
  • Predictable price reversals without fundamental news: Textbook random walk models require price changes to be unforecastable martingale differences. Under slow-moving capital, supply shocks produce large, predictable positive excess returns during the recovery phase that reflect the compensation for liquidity provision.
  • Inefficacy of instant price discovery: Perfect informational transparency does not guarantee rapid price correction. Market participants may know with certainty that an asset is underpriced, yet lack the balance sheet capacity to purchase it until contractual capital arrives.

6Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Because algorithmic trading executes in microseconds, capital moves instantaneously in modern markets.”

    Execution speed is not capital speed. High-frequency market makers operate with tiny balance sheets ($10M–$50M) and zero overnight inventory. Absorbing a $10B corporate bond or Treasury supply shock requires institutional balance sheet capital, which moves at the speed of monthly investment committees and contractual wire transfers.

  2. “Slow price recovery proves that market participants are irrational and slow to understand news.”

    Duffie's model features fully rational agents with perfect information. The price discount is not a cognitive error; it is the competitive market-clearing equilibrium compensation required by risk-averse dealers to warehouse massive inventory until outside buyers arrive.

  3. “Supply shocks in financial assets only affect the issuing firm, leaving secondary market pricing unaffected.”

    Because dealer balance sheets are shared across multiple asset classes, a supply shock in one market (e.g. mega-cap Treasury issuance or catastrophic insurance claims) absorbs dealer capacity, causing price dislocations to spill over into unrelated corporate bond and equity derivative markets.

7Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Calculate dislocation half-life

Suppose outside capital arrives in a distressed corporate debt market with Poisson search intensity \(\lambda = 1.386\) per year. Compute the half-life of price dislocations in years and months.

Solution

The half-life formula is \(t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693147}{1.386} \approx 0.500\) years (exactly 6.0 months).

M2 · Initial liquidity discount calculation

An asset pays annual perpetual dividend \(d = \$6.00\) and the risk-free rate is \(r = 0.04\). A supply shock of \(\Delta S = 50\) units hits the dealer market. Dealer holding cost is \(\gamma_d = 0.20\) and outside search arrival is \(\lambda = 0.96\). Compute the fundamental price \(P^*\), the initial discount \(\Delta P(0)\), and the post-shock price \(P(0)\).

Solution

The fundamental price is \(P^* = d / r = 6.00 / 0.04 = \$150.00\). The initial liquidity discount is \(\Delta P(0) = \frac{\gamma_d \Delta S}{r + \lambda} = \frac{0.20 \times 50}{0.04 + 0.96} = \frac{10.0}{1.00} = \$10.00\). The post-shock market price is \(P(0) = P^* - \Delta P(0) = 150.00 - 10.00 = \$140.00\) (a 6.67% discount).

M3 · Dynamic price recovery calculation

Using the parameters from M2, compute the expected price of the asset at \(t = 2.0\) years post-shock.

Solution

The price path is \(P(t) = P^* - \Delta P(0) e^{-\lambda t}\). For \(t = 2.0\), \(e^{-\lambda t} = e^{-0.96 \times 2.0} = e^{-1.92} \approx 0.1466\). The remaining discount is \(10.00 \times 0.1466 = \$1.47\). The price at \(t=2\) is \(P(2) = 150.00 - 1.47 = \$148.53\), recovering 85.3% of the initial price dislocation.

Proof

P1 · Expected return during recovery phase

Prove that under the Duffie (2010) price path \(P(t) = P^* - \Delta P(0) e^{-\lambda t}\), the expected instantaneous return on the asset strictly exceeds the risk-free rate \(r\) for all finite \(t\), and converges to \(r\) as \(t \to \infty\).

Solution

The instantaneous total return is the dividend yield plus the capital gains rate: \(\mu(t) = \frac{d + P'(t)}{P(t)}\). Since \(P'(t) = \lambda \Delta P(0) e^{-\lambda t} > 0\), capital gains are strictly positive. Furthermore, \(d = r P^*\). Substituting: \(\mu(t) = \frac{r P^* + \lambda \Delta P(0) e^{-\lambda t}}{P^* - \Delta P(0) e^{-\lambda t}} = \frac{r (P^* - \Delta P(0) e^{-\lambda t}) + (r + \lambda) \Delta P(0) e^{-\lambda t}}{P^* - \Delta P(0) e^{-\lambda t}} = r + \frac{(r + \lambda) \Delta P(0) e^{-\lambda t}}{P(t)}\). Because \(\Delta P(0) > 0\) and \(r, \lambda > 0\), the second term is strictly positive for all finite \(t\), proving \(\mu(t) > r\). As \(t \to \infty\), \(e^{-\lambda t} \to 0\), so \(\mu(t) \to r\).

P2 · Sensitivity of initial discount to search frictions

Prove that the initial price discount \(\Delta P(0) = \frac{\gamma_d \Delta S}{r + \lambda}\) is strictly decreasing and convex in the arrival rate of outside capital \(\lambda\).

Solution

The initial discount is \(f(\lambda) = \frac{K}{r + \lambda}\), where \(K = \gamma_d \Delta S > 0\). The first derivative is \(f'(\lambda) = -\frac{K}{(r + \lambda)^2} < 0\), establishing that the discount is strictly decreasing in \(\lambda\) (faster capital arrival reduces the dislocation). The second derivative is \(f''(\lambda) = \frac{2K}{(r + \lambda)^3} > 0\) for all \(r, \lambda > 0\), proving strict convexity. Marginal reductions in search friction provide the largest liquidity benefits when capital is most sluggish (low \(\lambda\)).

P3 · Aggregate dealer holding cost minimization

Show that the total cumulative holding cost incurred by the dealer sector over the entire recovery horizon is \(\int_0^\infty \gamma_d I(t) \, \mathrm{d}t = \frac{\gamma_d \Delta S}{\lambda}\).

Solution

Dealer inventory is \(I(t) = \Delta S e^{-\lambda t}\). Integrating cumulative inventory costs over \([0, \infty)\): \(\int_0^\infty \gamma_d I(t) \, \mathrm{d}t = \gamma_d \Delta S \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t} \, \mathrm{d}t = \gamma_d \Delta S \left[ -\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda} \right]_0^\infty = \gamma_d \Delta S \left( 0 - \left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right) \right) = \frac{\gamma_d \Delta S}{\lambda}\). Total inventory burden is inversely proportional to search intensity \(\lambda\).

Empirical

E1 · Catastrophe bond pricing post-Katrina (2005)

Following Hurricane Katrina in August 2005, prices of catastrophe bonds not exposed to hurricane risk fell dramatically, while secondary cat bond yields spiked. How does slow-moving capital explain this contagion?

Solution

Insurers faced unprecedented claim payouts and dumped non-hurricane cat bonds to raise immediate liquidity. The specialized dedicated cat-bond investment funds had exhausted their cash reserves. Generalist fixed-income investors were not mandate-approved to hold reinsurance risk. It took four to six months for institutional investors to amend mandates, perform actuarial risk modeling, and allocate new capital, during which unexposed cat bonds offered massive excess returns.

E2 · Index addition announcement versus effective date

When S&P announces that a stock will be added to the S&P 500 index in five trading days, stock prices jump on the announcement date, rise further until the effective date, and often partially reverse thereafter. Relate this pattern to slow-moving capital.

Solution

Passive index tracking funds are legally mandated to execute trades exactly at the market close on the effective date to minimize tracking error. Arbitrageurs step in on the announcement date to warehouse shares in anticipation. Because arbitrage capital is limited, prices rise until the effective date to clear the forced institutional inflow, followed by post-event mean reversion as transitory demand pressure dissipates.

E3 · Convertible bond arbitrage during the 2008 Lehman collapse

In October 2008, convertible bonds traded at unprecedented discounts to their underlying stock and option replication values (the convertible bond basis widened to over 1,500 bps). How did slow-moving capital generate this dislocation?

Solution

Convertible bond hedge funds were heavily financed by Lehman Brothers and other prime brokers. Following Lehman's failure, prime brokers withdrew credit lines and raised short-rebate fees, triggering massive forced liquidations of convertible bonds. Long-only institutional investors were prevented by investment mandates from holding unrated or hybrid securities. The basis persisted for over six months until private equity and unconstrained capital vehicles raised dedicated distressed funds.

Research

R1 · Endogenous search intensity and price dislocations

In Duffie (2010), search intensity \(\lambda\) is treated as exogenous. How does endogenizing search effort (where outside investors invest resources to monitor the market faster when mispricings are large) alter price dynamics?

Solution

Endogenizing search makes \(\lambda(P^* - P)\) an increasing function of the dislocation. For mild supply shocks, search is sluggish because the return does not justify monitoring costs. For massive dislocations, high expected returns induce rapid capital mobilization, truncating the half-life of extreme dislocations and generating non-linear recovery speeds.

R2 · Private debt lock-ups and secondary liquidity

Private credit funds typically impose 5-to-10 year lock-ups on investor capital. How does this structural lock-up affect the vulnerability of private debt markets to slow-moving capital shocks?

Solution

Structural lock-ups eliminate performance-based investor redemptions, preventing the forced fire sales seen in open-end mutual funds. However, lock-ups also trap capital within specific vintages. When high-yielding distressed opportunities arise in secondary markets, existing private debt capital cannot be quickly redeployed without raising new fund vehicles, prolonging secondary price dislocations.

R3 · Central bank standing repo facility as a friction dampener

How does the Federal Reserve's Standing Repo Facility (SRF) alter the effective \(\lambda\) of outside capital during Treasury supply surges?

Solution

The SRF allows primary dealers and eligible counterparties to exchange Treasuries for cash overnight at a fixed spread against the target rate. By providing an elastic, standing balance sheet backstop, the central bank effectively acts as an instantaneous outside investor with \(\lambda \to \infty\), capping inventory holding costs and eliminating supply-driven liquidity discounts.
