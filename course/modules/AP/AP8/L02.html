Cross-Sectional Bootstrap & Fund Skill

In a cross-section of thousands of active mutual funds, random chance guarantees that some managers generate stellar track records and high t-statistics of alpha purely through luck. Because fund returns feature heavy tails and pervasive contemporaneous cross-correlation from overlapping holdings, standard parametric t-tests severely overstate skill. The cross-sectional bootstrap solves this by simulating the entire cross-section under the null hypothesis of zero true skill while preserving non-normality and cross-correlation, revealing whether real-world managers beat costs.

1The Cross-Section of Mutual Fund Alpha and the Luck Illusion

Evaluating whether active investment managers possess genuine stock-picking skill is a foundational empirical challenge in asset pricing. Since Jensen (1968) introduced Jensen's alpha, performance evaluation has centered on estimating factor model intercepts. For fund \( i \) over time \( t = 1, \dots, T_i \), excess return over the risk-free rate is modeled against multi-factor benchmarks:

\[ R_{it} - R_{ft} = \alpha_i + \boldsymbol{\beta}_i^\top \mathbf{F}_t + e_{it} \]

where \( \mathbf{F}_t \) contains the market factor, size (SMB), value (HML) (Fama & French 1993), and momentum (UMD) (Carhart 1997). The estimate \( \hat{\alpha}_i \) and its associated t-statistic \( t(\hat{\alpha}_i) = \hat{\alpha}_i / \se(\hat{\alpha}_i) \) measure performance relative to passive factor exposures.

In a large fund universe (\( N > 3,000 \)), testing luck vs. skill encounters an extreme multiple-testing problem. Even if every fund manager has zero true gross skill (\( \alpha_i = 0 \)), independent Student-t distributions predict that approximately 2.5% of funds will achieve \( t(\hat{\alpha}_i) > 1.96 \) and 0.13% will achieve \( t(\hat{\alpha}_i) > 3.0 \) purely by sampling variation. In a sample of 3,000 funds, roughly 75 funds will appear statistically skilled at the 5% level by pure chance.

Carhart (1997) demonstrated that apparent multi-year persistence in mutual fund outperformance documented by earlier researchers disappeared once controlling for 1-year cross-sectional momentum. Winning managers were not uncovering mispriced securities; they were holding recent winners, absorbing systematic factor risk rather than delivering persistent managerial alpha.

2Non-Normality and Cross-Sectional Correlation in Fund Residuals

Parametric inference on the cross-section of fund alphas breaks down due to two structural features of mutual fund data documented by Fama & French (2010):

  1. Non-Normal Idiosyncratic Residuals: Fund return residuals \( e_{it} \) exhibit substantial excess kurtosis and negative skewness, driven by concentrated stock bets, earnings surprise jumps, and liquidity shocks. As a result, standard normal and Student-t critical values fail to represent the true sampling variability of \( t(\hat{\alpha}) \).
  2. Contemporaneous Cross-Sectional Correlation: Active mutual funds do not hold independent portfolios. Funds in the same style category (e.g. large-cap growth or small-cap value) hold overlapping stock positions and shared unmodeled industry exposures. Consequently, the contemporaneous covariance matrix \( \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_e = \E[\mathbf{e}_t \mathbf{e}_t^\top] \) has substantial off-diagonal elements: \[ \Cov(e_{it}, e_{jt}) = \sigma_{ij} \neq 0 \quad \text{for } i \neq j \]

If an econometrician bootstraps each fund independently, the cross-sectional correlation structure is destroyed. In a market month where an unmodeled sector shock (e.g. semiconductor surge) lifts all tech-focused funds, independent resampling treats their simultaneous high returns as multiple independent confirmation events, drastically understating the dispersion of luck and generating false discoveries.

3The Cross-Sectional Bootstrap Methodology

To overcome non-normality and cross-sectional correlation, Fama & French (2010) developed the cross-sectional bootstrap test of fund skill.

Definition 2.1 (Cross-Sectional Bootstrap Test of Fund Skill)

A cross-sectional bootstrap test of fund skill is a non-parametric resampling procedure that generates the empirical distribution of cross-sectional alpha estimates and t-statistics under the null hypothesis of zero true skill (\( \alpha_i = 0 \)), by centering each fund's return series around its factor exposures and jointly resampling calendar time periods with replacement across all funds simultaneously.

Theorem 2.1 (Preservation of Contemporaneous Correlation and Factor Structure)

Let \( \mathbf{R}_t = [R_{1t}, \dots, R_{Nt}]^\top \) denote the \( N \times 1 \) vector of fund returns at calendar month \( t \), and let \( \mathbf{F}_t \) denote the \( K \times 1 \) vector of benchmark factor returns. Suppose time periods \( \tau(t) \in \{1, \dots, T\} \) are drawn independently and uniformly with replacement, applying the identical time draw \( \tau(t) \) to all funds \( i = 1, \dots, N \) and factors simultaneously.

Then the bootstrapped panel preserves the exact empirical contemporaneous covariance structure \( \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_e = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \hat{\mathbf{e}}_t \hat{\mathbf{e}}_t^\top \) and the exact contemporaneous cross-factor covariance \( \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_F = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbf{F}_t \mathbf{F}_t^\top \).

Proof

For any bootstrap replication \( b \), the sampled time series is \( \{ \mathbf{R}_{\tau_b(t)}, \mathbf{F}_{\tau_b(t)} \}_{t=1}^T \), where \( \tau_b(t) \iid \operatorname{Uniform}(\{1, \dots, T\}) \). For any pair of funds \( i \) and \( j \), the bootstrapped residuals evaluated at the original OLS factor coefficients are \( e_{i, \tau_b(t)} \) and \( e_{j, \tau_b(t)} \).

The sample covariance across the bootstrap replication is:

\[ \widehat{\Cov}_b(e_i, e_j) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T e_{i, \tau_b(t)} e_{j, \tau_b(t)} - \left( \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T e_{i, \tau_b(t)} \right) \left( \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T e_{j, \tau_b(t)} \right) \]

Taking the expectation over the bootstrap resampling measure conditional on the original sample:

\[ \E_* \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T e_{i, \tau_b(t)} e_{j, \tau_b(t)} \right] = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \E_* [e_{i, \tau_b(t)} e_{j, \tau_b(t)}] = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \left( \frac{1}{T} \sum_{s=1}^T e_{is} e_{js} \right) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{s=1}^T e_{is} e_{js} \]

Because the time draw \( \tau_b(t) \) is identical across all funds at time \( t \), any contemporaneous co-movement present in the historical month \( s \) is preserved as a joint co-movement in the resampled period. Cross-sectional independence is never assumed.

The operational Fama-French bootstrap algorithm proceeds in four steps:

  1. In-Sample Factor Regressions: For each fund \( i = 1, \dots, N \), run the multi-factor regression to obtain OLS estimates \( \hat{\alpha}_i \), \( \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i \), \( \se(\hat{\alpha}_i) \), \( t(\hat{\alpha}_i) \), and time-series residuals \( \hat{e}_{it} \).
  2. Construct Zero-Alpha Null Series: Center returns under the null hypothesis \( H_0: \alpha_i = 0 \): \[ \widetilde{R}_{it} = R_{it} - \hat{\alpha}_i = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i^\top \mathbf{F}_t + \hat{e}_{it} \]
  3. Joint Time-Resampling: For bootstrap iteration \( b = 1, \dots, B \) (typically \( B \ge 1,000 \)):
    • Draw calendar months \( \tau(1), \dots, \tau(T) \) with replacement from \( \{1, \dots, T\} \).
    • Form bootstrap returns \( \widetilde{R}_{i, \tau(t)}^{(b)} \) and factor returns \( \mathbf{F}_{\tau(t)}^{(b)} \) for all funds simultaneously.
    • Re-estimate factor regressions on the bootstrapped panel to obtain \( t(\hat{\alpha}_i^{(b)}) \).
    • Sort \( \{ t(\hat{\alpha}_i^{(b)}) \}_{i=1}^N \) from lowest to highest to obtain cross-sectional percentiles (e.g. 1st, 5th, 10th, through to 90th, 95th, 99th).
  4. Statistical Inference on Cross-Sectional Tails: Compare the actual cross-sectional percentiles of \( t(\hat{\alpha}_i) \) to the distribution of bootstrap percentiles. The empirical p-value for the \( k \)-th percentile is the proportion of bootstrap iterations where the simulated percentile is more extreme than the actual percentile.

4Empirical Tail Evidence: Gross vs. Net Returns

Fama & French (2010) applied this methodology to 3,156 U.S. open-end equity mutual funds from 1984 to 2006. Their findings establish a stark contrast between net returns (what investors receive after fees) and gross returns (before fees and expenses):

Net Return Results (After Fees):

  • Left Tail (Unskilled Managers): In the bottom 10 percentiles (1st through 10th), actual \( t(\hat{\alpha}) \) values are substantially more negative than the bootstrap null. For example, at the 1st percentile, the actual \( t(\hat{\alpha}) = -3.55 \) compared to a bootstrap null of \( -2.71 \) (\( p < 0.001 \)). This demonstrates that poor fund performance is not bad luck; it reflects genuinely unskilled management that destroys value even beyond fee drag.
  • Right Tail (Skilled Managers): In the top percentiles (90th, 95th, 99th), actual \( t(\hat{\alpha}) \) values are indistinguishable from or lower than the bootstrap null. At the 95th percentile, actual \( t(\hat{\alpha}) = 1.64 \) while the null average is \( 1.70 \). Virtually no active funds achieve net returns that beat passive benchmarks by more than what pure sampling variation predicts.

Gross Return Results (Before Fees):

When fees and expenses (averaging ~1.2% per year) are added back to fund returns, the distribution shifts rightward. In gross returns, the left tail matches the zero-skill null, while the extreme right tail (98th and 99th percentiles) exhibits \( t(\hat{\alpha}) \) values that exceed the bootstrap null (\( p = 0.03 \)). A small cohort of elite managers possesses genuine stock-picking skill, but their extracted management fees and trading costs fully absorb these rents, leaving investors with zero net alpha in efficiently inefficient markets.

Lab 1 · Fama-French Cross-Sectional Bootstrap Fund Skill Test

Simulating a panel of mutual funds with fat-tailed residuals and contemporaneous cross-correlation under zero gross skill verifies that fee drag shifts the actual t(alpha) distribution left of the bootstrap null, creating an apparent excess of underperformers while right-tail winners remain indistinguishable from luck.

"""Lab AP8-L02-1: Fama-French (2010) Cross-Sectional Bootstrap Fund Skill Test.

SIMULATED: Implements the cross-sectional bootstrap test of mutual fund skill,
evaluating the empirical distribution of fund alpha t-statistics against the simulated
null distribution under zero true skill while preserving cross-sectional residual correlation.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def run_fama_french_bootstrap(
    n_funds: int = 250,
    n_months: int = 120,
    n_boot: int = 500,
    seed: int = 42,
) -> dict[str, list[float] | np.ndarray]:
    """Execute the Fama & French (2010) cross-sectional bootstrap test.

    Returns actual cross-sectional percentiles of t(alpha), mean bootstrapped null percentiles,
    and empirical one-sided p-values.
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    # 1. Simulate market excess return
    r_m = rng.normal(0.006, 0.045, size=n_months)  # ~7.2% annual, 15.6% vol

    # 2. Simulate fund characteristics
    betas = rng.normal(1.0, 0.12, size=n_funds)

    # True monthly net alphas: 80% zero-skill (-10 bps fee), 15% negative, 5% positive
    true_alpha = np.zeros(n_funds)
    n_unskilled = int(0.15 * n_funds)
    n_skilled = int(0.05 * n_funds)
    n_zero = n_funds - n_unskilled - n_skilled

    true_alpha[:n_unskilled] = -0.0025  # -25 bps/mo (-3.0%/yr)
    true_alpha[n_unskilled : n_unskilled + n_zero] = -0.0010  # -10 bps/mo (fee drag)
    true_alpha[n_unskilled + n_zero :] = 0.0015  # +15 bps/mo (+1.8%/yr net skill)

    # Common unobserved factor driving cross-sectional correlation
    common_factor = rng.standard_t(df=5, size=n_months) * 0.015
    loadings = rng.uniform(0.3, 1.2, size=(n_funds, 1))

    # Idiosyncratic shocks with fat tails (Student-t df=5)
    idio = rng.standard_t(df=5, size=(n_funds, n_months)) * 0.025
    residuals = loadings * common_factor + idio

    # Fund returns: R_it = alpha_i + beta_i * R_mt + e_it
    r_funds = true_alpha[:, np.newaxis] + betas[:, np.newaxis] * r_m + residuals

    # 3. Estimate actual OLS regressions: R_it = alpha_i + beta_i * R_mt + e_it
    var_m = np.var(r_m, ddof=1)
    mean_m = np.mean(r_m)
    cov_im = np.mean((r_funds - np.mean(r_funds, axis=1, keepdims=True)) * (r_m - mean_m), axis=1) * (
        n_months / (n_months - 1)
    )

    b_hat = cov_im / var_m
    a_hat = np.mean(r_funds, axis=1) - b_hat * mean_m
    e_hat = r_funds - (a_hat[:, np.newaxis] + b_hat[:, np.newaxis] * r_m)

    s2_e = np.sum(e_hat**2, axis=1) / (n_months - 2)
    se_a = np.sqrt(s2_e * (1.0 / n_months + (mean_m**2) / ((n_months - 1) * var_m)))
    t_hat = a_hat / se_a

    # 4. Construct zero-alpha returns: R_tilde_it = b_hat_i * R_mt + e_hat_it
    r_null = b_hat[:, np.newaxis] * r_m + e_hat

    # 5. Bootstrap: resample time periods with replacement across all funds
    percentile_ranks = [1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 75, 90, 95, 99]
    boot_t_percentiles = np.zeros((n_boot, len(percentile_ranks)))

    for b in range(n_boot):
        time_idx = rng.choice(n_months, size=n_months, replace=True)
        r_m_boot = r_m[time_idx]
        r_null_boot = r_null[:, time_idx]

        var_mb = np.var(r_m_boot, ddof=1)
        mean_mb = np.mean(r_m_boot)
        cov_diff = (r_null_boot - np.mean(r_null_boot, axis=1, keepdims=True)) * (r_m_boot - mean_mb)
        cov_imb = np.mean(cov_diff, axis=1) * (n_months / (n_months - 1))

        b_boot = cov_imb / var_mb
        a_boot = np.mean(r_null_boot, axis=1) - b_boot * mean_mb
        e_boot = r_null_boot - (a_boot[:, np.newaxis] + b_boot[:, np.newaxis] * r_m_boot)

        s2_eb = np.sum(e_boot**2, axis=1) / (n_months - 2)
        se_ab = np.sqrt(s2_eb * (1.0 / n_months + (mean_mb**2) / ((n_months - 1) * var_mb)))
        t_boot = a_boot / se_ab

        t_boot_sorted = np.sort(t_boot)
        boot_t_percentiles[b, :] = np.percentile(t_boot_sorted, percentile_ranks)

    actual_percentiles = np.percentile(np.sort(t_hat), percentile_ranks)
    null_mean_percentiles = np.mean(boot_t_percentiles, axis=0)

    # One-sided p-values:
    # Left tail (p <= 50): Prob(t_boot <= t_actual)
    # Right tail (p > 50): Prob(t_boot >= t_actual)
    p_values = []
    for j, p_rank in enumerate(percentile_ranks):
        if p_rank <= 50:
            pval = float(np.mean(boot_t_percentiles[:, j] <= actual_percentiles[j]))
        else:
            pval = float(np.mean(boot_t_percentiles[:, j] >= actual_percentiles[j]))
        p_values.append(pval)

    return {
        "percentiles": [float(p) for p in percentile_ranks],
        "actual_t": [float(x) for x in actual_percentiles],
        "null_t": [float(x) for x in null_mean_percentiles],
        "p_values": p_values,
    }


def main() -> None:
    res = run_fama_french_bootstrap(n_funds=250, n_months=120, n_boot=500, seed=42)

    print("================================================================================")
    print("FAMA & FRENCH (2010) CROSS-SECTIONAL BOOTSTRAP TEST OF MUTUAL FUND SKILL")
    print("================================================================================")
    hdr = f"{'Percentile':>10} | {'Actual t(α)':>13} | {'Null t(α)':>11} | {'Diff':>8} | {'p-val':>7} | {'Result':>16}"
    print(hdr)
    print("-" * 80)

    for p, act, null, pval in zip(res["percentiles"], res["actual_t"], res["null_t"], res["p_values"], strict=True):
        diff = act - null
        if p <= 10:
            interp = "Unskilled (p<0.05)" if pval < 0.05 else "Chance"
        elif p >= 95:
            interp = "Skilled (p<0.05)" if pval < 0.05 else "Chance luck"
        else:
            interp = "Fee drag / neutral"
        print(f"{p:9.0f}% | {act:13.3f} | {null:11.3f} | {diff:8.3f} | {pval:7.3f} | {interp:>16}")

    print("-" * 80)
    print("Null Hypothesis: All fund managers possess zero true gross skill (alpha = 0).")
    print("Conclusion: Negative alphas in left tail exceed sampling luck (true underperformance);")
    print("extreme right tail shows a small fraction of skilled managers covering fees.")

The cross-sectional bootstrap confirms the Fama & French (2010) empirical reality: across percentiles, actual net alphas sit consistently below the zero-skill null due to fee drag. 5Where the canon breaks

The cross-sectional bootstrap method, while vastly superior to parametric tests, confronts methodological boundaries in empirical implementation:

  • Survivorship & Incubation Bias: Commercial databases omit liquidated or merged funds unless survivorship-bias-free CRSP files are used. Furthermore, fund families incubate multiple private strategies and only open winning funds to the public, creating an upward-biased track record at inception.
  • Factor Benchmark Ambiguity: Alphas depend on the benchmark factors included in \( \mathbf{F}_t \). Adding quality (QMJ), profitability (RMW), or investment (CMA) factors (Fama & French 2015) absorbs residual variance and redefines managerial skill as factor exposure.
  • Multiple Testing Across Academic Literature: While the bootstrap controls for cross-sectional correlation across funds within a sample, it does not control for the hundreds of factor models tested across the academic literature (Harvey, Liu & Zhu 2016).

6Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “If a mutual fund achieves a 10-year track record with an alpha t-statistic of 2.20 (p = 0.028), we can conclude with 95% confidence that the manager is skilled.”

    In isolation, a t-statistic of 2.20 has a p-value of 0.028. But in a universe of 3,000 competing funds, roughly 84 funds will achieve \( t > 2.20 \) purely by sampling luck under the null of zero skill (Fama & French 2010). Individual p-values without cross-sectional null benchmarking are meaningless.

  2. “Bootstrapping fund skill can be performed by randomly scrambling the monthly return time series of each fund independently.”

    Independent time-scrambling destroys the contemporaneous cross-sectional correlation of fund residuals caused by common industry and style holdings. This compresses the variance of bootstrapped cross-sectional percentiles and causes massive false-discovery over-rejections.

  3. “Because top-performing mutual funds do not outperform the bootstrap null net of fees, active portfolio managers have zero investment talent.”

    Fama & French (2010) show that in gross returns before expenses, the top 2% of managers possess genuine skill. As modeled by Berk & Green (2004), managers capture their skill through fund scale and fees rather than passing net alpha to investors.

7Problem set

Problem 2.1 (Expected False Discoveries in Fund Panels). A quantitative allocator screens a universe of \( N = 4,000 \) mutual funds, evaluating 5-year monthly track records (\( T = 60 \)) against the CAPM. Assuming fund residuals are independent and all managers have zero skill (\( \alpha_i = 0 \)), calculate the expected number of funds that will achieve a two-sided t-statistic \( |t(\hat{\alpha}_i)| > 2.50 \).

Solution

Under the null hypothesis of zero skill and independent normal residuals, the t-statistic follows a Student-t distribution with \( T - 2 = 58 \) degrees of freedom. For \( t = 2.50 \) with \( \nu = 58 \), the two-sided p-value is approximately \( p = 2 \cdot (1 - F_{t, 58}(2.50)) \approx 0.0152 \).

The expected number of false discoveries purely from sampling noise is:

\[ \E[N_{discoveries}] = N \cdot p = 4,000 \cdot 0.0152 \approx 60.8 \text{ funds} \]

Approximately 61 funds will exhibit statistically significant alpha at the 1.5% level purely through luck.

Problem 2.2 (Fee Drag and Alpha Distribution Shift). A fund universe has zero true gross skill, so gross alphas satisfy \( \alpha_i^g = 0 \). All funds charge an annual expense fee of \( f = 1.20\% \) (10 basis points per month). In monthly factor regressions over \( T = 120 \) months with residual standard error \( \se(\hat{\alpha}_i) = 0.0018 \) (18 bps/mo), compute the theoretical mean t-statistic of net alpha across the cross-section.

Solution

Net alpha is \( \alpha_i^n = \alpha_i^g - f = 0 - 0.0010 = -0.0010 \) per month. The expected t-statistic for each fund is:

\[ \E[t(\hat{\alpha}_i^n)] \approx \frac{\E[\hat{\alpha}_i^n]}{\se(\hat{\alpha}_i)} = \frac{-0.0010}{0.0018} \approx -0.556 \]

The entire cross-sectional distribution of t-statistics is shifted leftward by 0.556 standard deviations, depressing the median and causing the left tail of underperformers to expand dramatically.

Problem 2.3 (Centering Returns Under the Null). A mutual fund has estimated annualized CAPM alpha \( \hat{\alpha}_i = +3.60\% \) (+30 bps/month) and market beta \( \hat{\beta}_i = 1.10 \). In month \( t \), market excess return is \( R_{mt} = +2.00\% \) and the fund's actual excess return is \( R_{it} = +2.80\% \). Calculate the fund's zero-skill null return \( \widetilde{R}_{it} \) for this month.

Solution

The zero-skill null return subtracts the fund's estimated alpha from its actual return:

\[ \widetilde{R}_{it} = R_{it} - \hat{\alpha}_i = 2.80\% - 0.30\% = +2.50\% \]

Alternatively, verifying via factor components: the OLS residual is \( \hat{e}_{it} = R_{it} - (\hat{\alpha}_i + \hat{\beta}_i R_{mt}) = 2.80\% - (0.30\% + 1.10 \cdot 2.00\%) = 2.80\% - 2.50\% = +0.30\% \). Then \( \widetilde{R}_{it} = \hat{\beta}_i R_{mt} + \hat{e}_{it} = 1.10 \cdot 2.00\% + 0.30\% = 2.20\% + 0.30\% = +2.50\% \).

Problem 2.4 (Expectation of the Centered Bootstrap Alpha). Prove that when OLS regressions are run on the centered bootstrap series \( \widetilde{R}_{it} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i^\top \mathbf{F}_t + \hat{e}_{it} \), the expected OLS alpha estimate across bootstrap replications conditional on the original sample is identically zero.

Solution

For any fund \( i \), the centered return at month \( s \) is \( \widetilde{R}_{is} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i^\top \mathbf{F}_s + \hat{e}_{is} \). In bootstrap replication \( b \), month indices \( \tau(t) \) are sampled with replacement. The bootstrap regression model is \( \widetilde{R}_{i, \tau(t)} = \alpha_i^{(b)} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_i^{(b)\top} \mathbf{F}_{\tau(t)} + u_{it} \).

By the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, the OLS intercept estimate satisfies \( \hat{\alpha}_i^{(b)} = \bar{\widetilde{R}}_{i}^{(b)} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i^{(b)\top} \bar{\mathbf{F}}^{(b)} \). Taking the expectation under the bootstrap distribution \( \E_* \) conditional on the sample:

\[ \E_*[\widetilde{R}_{i, \tau(t)}] = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{s=1}^T \widetilde{R}_{is} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{s=1}^T (\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i^\top \mathbf{F}_s + \hat{e}_{is}) = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i^\top \bar{\mathbf{F}} + \frac{1}{T} \sum_{s=1}^T \hat{e}_{is} \]

Because OLS residuals sum to zero (\( \sum_{s=1}^T \hat{e}_{is} = 0 \)), \( \E_*[\widetilde{R}_{i, \tau(t)}] = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i^\top \bar{\mathbf{F}} \). Since \( \E_*[\mathbf{F}_{\tau(t)}] = \bar{\mathbf{F}} \), it follows that \( \E_*[\hat{\alpha}_i^{(b)}] = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i^\top \bar{\mathbf{F}} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_i^\top \bar{\mathbf{F}} = 0 \). The bootstrap null exactly imposes zero true skill.

Problem 2.5 (Impact of Common Residual Factors on Independent Bootstrap Variance). Suppose fund residuals follow a one-factor structure \( e_{it} = \gamma_i f_t + \epsilon_{it} \), with \( f_t \iid \Normal(0, \sigma_f^2) \) and \( \epsilon_{it} \iid \Normal(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2) \). Prove that an independent bootstrap (resampling time separately for each fund) underestimates the cross-sectional variance of average fund performance \( \bar{e} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \bar{e}_i \) relative to the joint bootstrap.

Solution

Under the joint bootstrap, the resampled month \( \tau(t) \) is shared across all funds. The cross-sectional average residual in month \( t \) is \( \bar{e}_t = \bar{\gamma} f_t + \bar{\epsilon}_t \). Over \( T \) months, the variance of the grand mean \( \bar{e} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \bar{e}_t \) is:

\[ \Var_{joint}(\bar{e}) = \frac{1}{T} \left( \bar{\gamma}^2 \sigma_f^2 + \frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{N} \right) \]

Under an independent bootstrap, each fund \( i \) draws an independent time index \( \tau_i(t) \). The cross-sectional average residual is \( \bar{e}_t^{indep} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (\gamma_i f_{\tau_i(t)} + \epsilon_{i, \tau_i(t)}) \). Because the draws are independent across funds, \( \Cov(f_{\tau_i(t)}, f_{\tau_j(t)}) = 0 \) for \( i \neq j \). The variance becomes:

\[ \Var_{indep}(\bar{e}) = \frac{1}{T} \left( \frac{\overline{\gamma^2} \sigma_f^2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2}{N} \right) \]

As \( N \to \infty \), \( \Var_{indep}(\bar{e}) \to 0 \), whereas \( \Var_{joint}(\bar{e}) \to \frac{\bar{\gamma}^2 \sigma_f^2}{T} > 0 \). Independent resampling completely eliminates the aggregate systematic variation from unobserved common factors, drastically understating the sampling variance of market-wide lucky streaks.

Problem 2.6 (Consistency of the Cross-Sectional Bootstrap Percentile Estimator). State the regularity conditions under which the bootstrap distribution of the \( \alpha \)-percentile \( q_\alpha(t(\hat{\alpha})) \) converges in probability to the true null asymptotic distribution as \( T, N \to \infty \).

Solution

Let \( F_T(x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \indic\{ t(\hat{\alpha}_i) \le x \} \) denote the empirical distribution of fund t-statistics. Convergence of the bootstrap percentile estimator requires three conditions:

  1. Stationarity and Ergodicity: The joint vector process \( (\mathbf{R}_t, \mathbf{F}_t) \) is strictly stationary and alpha-mixing with mixing coefficients \( \sum_{k=1}^\infty \alpha(k)^{1 - 2/\delta} < \infty \) for some \( \delta > 2 \), ensuring the Law of Large Numbers applies to bootstrapped moments.
  2. Finite Moments: Payoffs and factors possess finite moments of order \( 4 + \delta \), ensuring that sample variances and OLS standard error denominators converge uniformly away from zero: \( \inf_i \se(\hat{\alpha}_i) > c > 0 \).
  3. Smoothness of the Asymptotic Distribution: The limiting cumulative distribution function \( F_\infty(x) \) is strictly increasing and continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of the quantile \( q_\alpha \), so that \( F_\infty'(q_\alpha) > 0 \). Under these conditions, the bootstrap empirical quantile \( \hat{q}_\alpha^{(b)} \) converges in probability to \( q_\alpha \) as \( T, B \to \infty \).

Problem 2.7 (Analyzing Fama-French Net vs. Gross Table 2). In Table 2 of Fama & French (2010), the 3-factor net alpha t-statistic at the 5th percentile is \( -2.48 \) (actual) versus \( -1.82 \) (bootstrap null), with \( p < 0.001 \). At the 95th percentile, the net alpha t-statistic is \( +1.64 \) (actual) versus \( +1.70 \) (bootstrap null), with \( p = 0.58 \). Interpret these two numbers in terms of the economic prevalence of skill.

Solution

The asymmetric comparison demonstrates two fundamental economic conclusions:

  1. Left Tail Underperformance is Real: At the 5th percentile, the actual t-statistic (\( -2.48 \)) is substantially more negative than the bootstrap null (\( -1.82 \)). The empirical p-value of \( p < 0.001 \) means that in less than 1 out of 1,000 simulations could luck produce underperformance this severe. A substantial group of active mutual fund managers has negative true skill (destroying value through excessive turnover, transaction costs, and poor security selection beyond fees).
  2. Right Tail Outperformance is Pure Luck: At the 95th percentile, the actual t-statistic (\( +1.64 \)) is actually lower than the bootstrap null average (\( +1.70 \)), and 58% of zero-skill simulations generate a 95th percentile greater than \( +1.64 \). Top-performing active funds net of fees are entirely consistent with pure sampling chance. There is no evidence of net skill covering fees for investors.

Problem 2.8 (Impact of Fund Incubation on Track Records). An asset management firm creates 20 incubator algorithmic funds in private accounts with internal seed capital. After 3 years, only the top 3 funds (which achieved Sharpe ratios > 1.8) are launched to public investors, while the remaining 17 funds are silently liquidated. Explain how this incubation strategy distorts cross-sectional bootstrap inference if unadjusted CRSP data is used.

Solution

Incubation creates severe selection bias and survivorship bias. By selecting only the top 3 funds from 20 random draws, the opening public track record represents the maximum of 20 draws: \( \max_{1 \le j \le 20} \{ \hat{\alpha}_j \} \).

If these historical incubated returns are included in the public database, the econometrician observes only the upper extreme of the firm's private draws while the 17 failed realizations are discarded. In the cross-sectional bootstrap, treating these incubated track records as unconditioned realizations from the fund population artificially inflates observed alphas and biases the right tail, generating spurious evidence of managerial skill.

Problem 2.9 (Comparing CAPM vs. 4-Factor Alphas in the Bootstrap). When evaluating a large growth equity fund, its CAPM alpha is \( \hat{\alpha}_{CAPM} = +2.4\% / \text{yr} \) with \( t = 2.45 \). When evaluated against the Carhart 4-factor model, its alpha becomes \( \hat{\alpha}_{4F} = -0.3\% / \text{yr} \) with \( t = -0.35 \). The fund's loadings are \( \beta_{MKT} = 1.05 \), \( \beta_{SMB} = -0.10 \), \( \beta_{HML} = -0.45 \), and \( \beta_{UMD} = +0.38 \). Deconstruct what generated the apparent CAPM alpha.

Solution

The apparent CAPM alpha was entirely generated by systematic factor tilts rather than idiosyncratic stock picking:

  1. Growth Tilt (\( \beta_{HML} = -0.45 \)): Negative loading on value means positive loading on growth. Over periods where growth outperforms value, this generates positive returns.
  2. Momentum Loading (\( \beta_{UMD} = +0.38 \)): A substantial loading on momentum captures the historical momentum premium (typically ~6-8% annualized). The momentum factor contribution alone is \( 0.38 \times 7.0\% \approx +2.66\% \) per year.

Once controlling for these factor exposures, the fund's true stock-picking alpha is slightly negative (\( -0.3\% \)), reflecting management fee drag. The CAPM alpha was an illusion of factor exposure.

Problem 2.10 (Dynamic Factor Betas and Conditional Bootstrap). Standard cross-sectional bootstrap tests assume constant factor betas \( \boldsymbol{\beta}_i \) over a fund's life. Formulate a conditional bootstrap methodology that accounts for dynamic market timing or factor tilting: \( \boldsymbol{\beta}_{it} = \boldsymbol{\beta}_{i0} + \mathbf{b}_i z_{t-1} \), where \( z_{t-1} \) is a macroeconomic state variable.

Solution

A conditional cross-sectional bootstrap requires the following formulation:

  1. Conditional Factor Model Estimation: For each fund \( i \), specify \( R_{it} - R_{ft} = \alpha_i + \boldsymbol{\beta}_{i0}^\top \mathbf{F}_t + \mathbf{b}_i^\top (z_{t-1} \mathbf{F}_t) + e_{it} \). Estimate \( \hat{\alpha}_i \), \( \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i0} \), and interaction coefficients \( \hat{\mathbf{b}}_i \) via OLS.
  2. State-Dependent Null Return Construction: Subtract \( \hat{\alpha}_i \) to form the conditional null return: \[ \widetilde{R}_{it} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i0}^\top \mathbf{F}_t + \hat{\mathbf{b}}_i^\top (z_{t-1} \mathbf{F}_t) + \hat{e}_{it} \]
  3. Block Bootstrap with State Alignment: Because macro state variable \( z_t \) is autocorrelated, simple i.i.d. resampling of months would destroy the temporal dependence of \( z_{t-1} \). Instead, use a stationary block bootstrap (Politis & Romano 1994) to resample contiguous blocks of calendar months \( (\mathbf{F}_t, z_{t-1}, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_t) \) jointly across all funds.
  4. Inference: Re-estimate the conditional model on each bootstrap draw to evaluate whether managers possess genuine timing ability beyond public macroeconomic predictors.

Problem 2.11 (Machine Learning Factor Models and the Factor Zoo). If the true benchmark contains 50 latent factors discovered via principal component analysis or autoencoders, evaluate the statistical power and false discovery rate of the Fama-French bootstrap compared to a 3-factor benchmark.

Solution

Expanding the factor benchmark to 50 latent factors has two countervailing econometric consequences:

  1. False Discovery Reduction: By spanning complex unmodeled covariance across assets, latent factors soak up residual correlation that a 3-factor model misses. Alphas that were previously confounded by unmodeled factor risks are correctly attributed to factor loadings, reducing the false discovery rate in right-tail tests.
  2. In-Sample Overfitting and Power Loss: Estimating 50 factor loadings on short fund track records (\( T = 60 \) to \( 120 \)) consumes substantial degrees of freedom, inflating the standard error of alpha \( \se(\hat{\alpha}_i) \). Furthermore, latent factors may inadvertently absorb idiosyncratic trading signals that represent genuine manager skill, drastically reducing the statistical power to detect truly skilled managers.

Problem 2.12 (Cross-Sectional Bootstrap Under Asymmetric Fund Inception/Death). In real-world data, the fund panel is unbalanced: funds enter and exit at different dates. Describe how Fama & French (2010) handle missing months in the joint bootstrap, and evaluate whether this creates potential sample-selection bias.

Solution

In an unbalanced panel, when calendar month \( \tau(t) \) is sampled, fund \( i \) only has a bootstrapped return if fund \( i \) was active during the historical month \( \tau(t) \). If fund \( i \) did not exist in month \( \tau(t) \), its return for that bootstrap month is treated as missing.

To avoid survivorship bias, Fama & French require a fund to have at least a minimum number of valid observations (e.g. \( T_i \ge 60 \) months) in the bootstrap draw to estimate its regression. However, if fund death is endogenous to recent performance (poor funds liquidate, surviving funds persist), joint resampling over-samples months where deceased funds were still alive and under-represents post-liquidation periods. This can slightly understate the persistence of poor performance unless survivorship adjustments are modeled explicitly.

9Retrieval quiz

  1. Why cannot standard parametric Student-t distributions be used to evaluate extreme alpha t-statistics in a cross-section of mutual funds?

    Options

    Non-normal fat tails and cross-sectional correlation violate the i.i.d. normality assumptions of standard t-tests, severely distorting theoretical critical values.

  2. In the Fama & French (2010) bootstrap, how is the return series constructed under the null hypothesis of zero skill?

    Options

    Subtracting estimated alpha forces the sample alpha to exactly zero while preserving the fund's factor exposures and historical residual properties.

  3. Why must time periods be resampled jointly across all funds simultaneously in the bootstrap?

    Options

    Joint resampling ensures that unmodeled industry or macro shocks affecting multiple funds simultaneously in month t are preserved in the resampled draw.

  4. What did Fama & French (2010) conclude regarding the performance of U.S. mutual funds net of fees?

    Options

    Net of fees, winning funds are indistinguishable from lucky chance, whereas underperforming funds show statistically significant negative skill beyond fees.

  5. In a simulation of 1,000 bootstrap iterations under zero skill, exactly 50 iterations produce a 95th-percentile t-statistic greater than the actual observed 95th-percentile t-statistic. What is the one-sided empirical p-value (in percent)?

    The empirical p-value is \( (50 / 1,000) \times 100\% = 5.0\% \).

  6. What factor did Carhart (1997) demonstrate was responsible for apparent multi-year performance persistence in winning mutual funds?

    Options

    Carhart (1997) showed that persistence in 1-year fund winners was explained by unintentional or intentional momentum factor exposure rather than persistent stock picking.

  7. What do Fama & French (2010) find when evaluating mutual fund gross returns (before fees)?

    Options

    In gross returns, the extreme right tail exceeds the zero-skill null, proving that true managerial talent exists but is captured by the fund family through fees.

  8. In a panel of 3,000 mutual funds with independent normal residuals under zero skill, how many funds are expected to achieve a t-statistic of alpha exceeding 1.96 purely by chance (at the 2.5% one-sided level)?

    Expected false discoveries: \( 3,000 \times 0.025 = 75 \) funds.

  9. How does survivorship bias distort empirical estimates of mutual fund skill?

    Options

    Funds that perform poorly are liquidated or merged; excluding their dismal pre-termination returns creates an upward bias in historical performance.

  10. Why does subtracting estimated alpha hat_alpha_i from returns preserve factor exposures?

    Options

    Subtracting a scalar constant shifts the mean of the dependent variable but leaves its covariance with independent factor regressors completely unchanged.
