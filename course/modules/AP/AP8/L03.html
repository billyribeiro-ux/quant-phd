The Equilibrium Economics of Active Management

For decades, empirical evaluations documenting zero average net alpha were interpreted as proof that active managers lack skill. This interpretation commits an economic fallacy. When investors compete rationally for outperformance and fund strategies face decreasing returns to scale, capital flows drive every fund's expected net alpha to zero in equilibrium. Managerial skill is captured entirely through fund scale and fee revenues, not net returns to investors. The true measure of managerial skill is dollar value added, not net alpha.

1Sharpe's Arithmetic of Active Management and the Active Paradox

Sharpe (1991) formulated the classic accounting identity governing investment management: the arithmetic of active management. The entire market portfolio consists of all outstanding tradeable assets. Investors are partitioned into passive investors (who hold market-capitalization weights) and active investors (who hold any weights different from the market).

Theorem 3.1 (Sharpe's Arithmetic of Active Management)

Let \( R_m \) denote the return on the aggregate market portfolio. Because passive investors hold the market portfolio, their return equals \( R_m \). By market clearing, the value-weighted sum of all holdings must equal the market:

\[ w_{passive} R_m + (1 - w_{passive}) R_{active}^{gross} = R_m \implies R_{active}^{gross} = R_m \]

Before costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar equals the return on the average passively managed dollar. If active management incurs management fees and trading expenses \( c_{active} > c_{passive} \), the net return on the average actively managed dollar must underperform passive management by exactly the difference in expenses:

\[ R_{active}^{net} = R_m - c_{active} < R_m - c_{passive} = R_{passive}^{net} \]
Proof

Let \( N \) denote the number of securities with market capitalizations \( V_j \) and aggregate wealth \( W = \sum_{j=1}^N V_j \). Market weights are \( w_j = V_j / W \). Total passive capital is \( W_P \), with passive security holdings \( H_{j,P} = w_j W_P \). Total active capital is \( W_A = W - W_P \), with active security holdings \( H_{j,A} \).

Market clearing requires \( H_{j,P} + H_{j,A} = V_j \) for every security \( j \). Substituting \( H_{j,P} = w_j W_P \) yields:

\[ H_{j,A} = V_j - w_j W_P = w_j W - w_j W_P = w_j (W - W_P) = w_j W_A \]

The aggregate active portfolio weight on security \( j \) is \( H_{j,A} / W_A = w_j \), identically equal to the market weight. Therefore, the aggregate gross return of active investors is \( \sum_{j=1}^N w_j R_j = R_m \). Subtracting non-negative operating expenses completes the proof.

Sharpe's arithmetic generates the Active Management Paradox: if active management as an asset class is mathematically guaranteed to deliver negative aggregate net alpha relative to passive indexing, why do rational investors allocate tens of trillions of dollars to active managers and pay billions in management fees?

2Decreasing Returns to Scale and Rational Capital Allocation

The paradox was resolved by Berk & Green (2004), who built a rational equilibrium model of active portfolio management grounded in two structural features:

  1. Heterogeneous Managerial Skill: Managers possess different levels of unobserved intrinsic ability \( a_i \), representing the gross alpha generated on the first dollar invested.
  2. Decreasing Returns to Scale at the Fund Level: As fund assets under management \( q_{it} \) expand, trading size creates market impact, bid-ask slippage, and organizational diseconomies. To deploy larger capital, a manager must deviate further from their highest-conviction ideas into marginally less attractive investments.

Gross alpha for fund \( i \) managing assets \( q_{i,t-1} \) is modeled as a strictly decreasing function of fund scale:

\[ \alpha_{it}^{gross} = a_i - b q_{i,t-1} \]

where \( b > 0 \) represents the diseconomy of scale parameter. The fund charges a proportional asset-based management fee \( f_i \). The expected net alpha delivered to outside investors is:

\[ \E[\alpha_{it}^{net} \mid q_{i,t-1}] = \alpha_{it}^{gross} - f_i = a_i - b q_{i,t-1} - f_i \]

Investors are competitive, rational, and have access to abundant elastic capital. If investors observe that a fund's expected net alpha is positive (\( \E[\alpha_{it}^{net}] > 0 \)), capital flows into the fund. As \( q_{i,t-1} \) increases, market impact and scale diseconomies erode gross alpha until expected net alpha is driven to zero.

3Gross Value Added vs. Net Alpha

Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) identified the profound econometric implication of this market equilibrium: net alpha does not measure manager skill.

Definition 3.1 (Equilibrium Economics of Active Management)

The equilibrium economics of active management is the structural framework establishing that in a competitive capital market with decreasing returns to scale, investor fund flows equilibrate expected net alpha to zero for all managers. Consequently, managerial skill is measured not by percentage net alpha, but by the gross dollar value added extracted from financial markets:

\[ V_{it} \equiv q_{i,t-1} \cdot \alpha_{it}^{gross} = q_{i,t-1} \cdot (\alpha_{it}^{net} + f_{it}) \]
Theorem 3.2 (Equilibrium Fund Size and Rent Extraction)

In competitive active management equilibrium with linear scale diseconomies \( \alpha_i^g(q) = a_i - b q \), each active fund with skill \( a_i > f_i \) expands to an optimal equilibrium size:

\[ q_i^* = \frac{a_i - f_i}{b} \]

At equilibrium, expected net alpha is zero: \( \E[\alpha_i^{net}(q_i^*)] = 0 \). The manager captures 100% of the economic surplus created by their skill through fee revenues, which exactly equal equilibrium dollar value added:

\[ V_i^* = q_i^* \cdot f_i = \frac{a_i - f_i}{b} \cdot f_i > 0 \]
Proof

Investors supply capital elastically at reservation rate of return \( R_m \). Equilibrium requires that no investor can earn an expected return differing from \( R_m \), which imposes the zero expected net alpha condition:

\[ \E[\alpha_i^{net}(q_i^*)] = a_i - b q_i^* - f_i = 0 \]

Solving for \( q_i^* \) yields \( q_i^* = (a_i - f_i) / b \). At this scale, gross alpha is \( \alpha_i^g(q_i^*) = a_i - b ((a_i - f_i)/b) = f_i \). Total gross dollar value added is:

\[ V_i^* = q_i^* \cdot \alpha_i^g(q_i^*) = q_i^* \cdot f_i = \frac{(a_i - f_i) f_i}{b} \]

The manager's annual fee revenue is \( \text{Revenue}_i = f_i \cdot q_i^* = V_i^* \). Outside investors receive net return equal to the benchmark, earning zero economic rents, while the manager extracts the full dollar value added generated by their scarce talent.

Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) tested this model on the universe of U.S. open-end equity mutual funds from 1977 to 2011. While the average fund delivered a net alpha of \( -57 \) basis points per year (reflecting fee drag and friction costs), the average fund generated a statistically significant gross dollar value added of \( +\$3.2 \) million per year (in inflation-adjusted 2000 dollars). Top-decile fund managers generated over \( +\$25 \) million per year in gross value added, and this dollar value added was highly persistent over 5- to 10-year horizons.

Lab 1 · Simulating the Berk-Green Equilibrium: Value Added vs. Net Alpha

Simulating an economy of active mutual funds with heterogeneous intrinsic skill and decreasing returns to scale verifies that competitive investor flows drive expected net alpha near zero while gross dollar value added correlates strongly with true managerial skill.

"""Lab AP8-L03-1: Equilibrium Economics of Active Management (Berk & van Binsbergen 2015).

SIMULATED: Simulates the Berk & Green (2004) and Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) equilibrium
where competitive investors allocate capital to active funds with decreasing returns to scale,
demonstrating that net alpha converges to zero while gross value added measures true managerial skill.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass

import numpy as np


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class ActiveEquilibriumResult:
    corr_skill_net_alpha: float
    corr_skill_value_added: float
    mean_net_alpha: float
    mean_value_added: float
    total_value_added: float
    pct_zero_net_alpha: float
    n_active: int


def simulate_active_management_equilibrium(
    n_funds: int = 150,
    n_years: int = 30,
    b_scale: float = 0.002,  # Diseconomy parameter ($0.2% gross alpha drag per $100M AUM)
    fee: float = 0.015,  # 1.5% annual management fee
    flow_speed: float = 0.5,  # Capital allocation response speed
    seed: int = 101,
) -> ActiveEquilibriumResult:
    """Simulate fund size dynamics and evaluate net alpha vs. value added."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    # Manager intrinsic skill a_i ~ Uniform(0.00, 0.06) (0% to 6% annualized gross alpha at zero AUM)
    skill_a = rng.uniform(0.00, 0.06, size=n_funds)

    # Initial AUM (in millions of dollars)
    aum = rng.uniform(20.0, 100.0, size=n_funds)

    # Evolve fund AUM and returns over time
    for _ in range(n_years):
        gross_alpha = skill_a - b_scale * (aum / 100.0) + rng.normal(0.0, 0.015, size=n_funds)
        net_alpha = gross_alpha - fee

        # Capital flows respond to observed net alpha
        flow_rate = np.clip(flow_speed * (net_alpha / 0.01), -0.5, 0.8)
        aum = np.maximum(5.0, aum * (1.0 + flow_rate))

    # Final period steady state evaluation
    final_gross_alpha = skill_a - b_scale * (aum / 100.0)
    final_net_alpha = final_gross_alpha - fee
    # Dollar Value Added V_i = AUM * gross_alpha (in millions per year)
    value_added = aum * final_gross_alpha

    # Correlations (exclude funds at minimum bound)
    active = aum > 6.0
    corr_skill_net_alpha = float(np.corrcoef(skill_a[active], final_net_alpha[active])[0, 1])
    corr_skill_value_added = float(np.corrcoef(skill_a[active], value_added[active])[0, 1])

    mean_net_alpha = float(np.mean(final_net_alpha[active]))
    mean_value_added = float(np.mean(value_added[active]))
    total_value_added = float(np.sum(value_added[active]))
    pct_zero_net_alpha = float(np.mean(np.abs(final_net_alpha[active]) < 0.005) * 100.0)

    return ActiveEquilibriumResult(
        corr_skill_net_alpha=corr_skill_net_alpha,
        corr_skill_value_added=corr_skill_value_added,
        mean_net_alpha=mean_net_alpha,
        mean_value_added=mean_value_added,
        total_value_added=total_value_added,
        pct_zero_net_alpha=pct_zero_net_alpha,
        n_active=int(np.sum(active)),
    )


def main() -> None:
    res = simulate_active_management_equilibrium(n_funds=150, n_years=35, seed=101)

    print("================================================================================")
    print("EQUILIBRIUM ACTIVE MANAGEMENT: BERK & GREEN (2004) / BERK & VAN BINSBERGEN (2015)")
    print("================================================================================")
    print(f"Active Funds in Long-Run Steady State:  {res.n_active}")
    print(f"Mean Final Net Alpha (annualized):      {res.mean_net_alpha * 100:+.2f}%")
    print(f"Funds with |Net Alpha| < 50 bps:        {res.pct_zero_net_alpha:.1f}%")
    print(f"Mean Dollar Value Added per Fund:       ${res.mean_value_added:.2f}M / year")
    print(f"Total Industry Dollar Value Added:      ${res.total_value_added:.2f}M / year")
    print("-" * 80)
    print("EVALUATION OF SKILL METRICS:")
    print(f"  Corr(True Manager Skill, Net Alpha):     {res.corr_skill_net_alpha:+.4f} (Uninformative)")
    print(f"  Corr(True Manager Skill, Value Added):   {res.corr_skill_value_added:+.4f} (Highly Informative)")
    print("-" * 80)
    print("Economic Conclusion:")
    print("1. Net alpha does not measure skill: rational capital flows drive net alpha to zero.")
    print("2. Dollar value added V = AUM * gross_alpha measures skill and captures manager rents.")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The Berk-Green equilibrium provides powerful neoclassical intuition, but encounters real-world institutional frictions where the baseline model breaks:

  • Flow-Performance Convexity: Retail mutual fund flows are asymmetric: investors chase top-decile performers aggressively but withdraw capital sluggishly from mediocre and poor performers (Carhart 1997). This creates a persistent left tail of value-destroying funds that survive for years.
  • Asymmetric Incentive Fees: Hedge funds and private equity charge performance fees (e.g. 20% carry above hurdle) with high-water marks. Asymmetric compensation introduces call-option convexity, incentivizing managers to increase portfolio risk rather than optimizing long-term value added.
  • Capacity Externalities Across Funds: When multiple quant funds deploy capital into the same factor or anomaly, they create crowded trades and fire-sale externalities (Pedersen 2015). One fund's scale expansion depresses gross alpha for all peer funds in the strategy.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “Because the average mutual fund delivers an alpha of zero or slightly negative net of fees, active portfolio managers lack investment skill.”

    In competitive capital markets with decreasing returns to scale, capital flows drive net alpha to zero regardless of whether managers are skilled (Berk & Green 2004). Zero net alpha is a market-clearing condition, not proof of zero skill.

  2. “Investors should allocate their capital to the mutual funds with the highest historical percentage alpha over the past 5 years.”

    Because winning funds receive large capital inflows that expand AUM and increase price impact, historical alpha is rapidly eroded by scale diseconomies. Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) show that past net alpha has virtually zero predictive power for future net alpha.

  3. “A mutual fund manager with $50 billion AUM generating a 0.2% net alpha is less skilled than a boutique manager with $50 million AUM generating a 2.0% net alpha.”

    Percentage alpha ignores scale. The $50B manager generates \( \$50\text{B} \times (0.2\% + 1.0\% \text{ fee}) = \$600\text{M} \) in annual value added. The boutique manager generates \( \$50\text{M} \times (2.0\% + 1.0\% \text{ fee}) = \$1.5\text{M} \). The large manager demonstrates vastly superior economic skill in deploying capital against market depth (Berk & van Binsbergen 2015).

6Problem set

Problem 3.1 (Equilibrium Fund Size Calculation). An equity long-short manager has an intrinsic gross alpha of \( a_i = 4.5\% \) per year on their first dollar, faces scale diseconomies of \( b = 0.5\% \) per billion dollars of AUM (\( b = 0.005 / \$1\text{B} \)), and charges a flat management fee of \( f = 1.5\% \) per year. Calculate the fund's equilibrium AUM \( q_i^* \) and its annual dollar value added \( V_i^* \).

Solution

In the Berk-Green equilibrium, optimal fund size equates expected net alpha to zero: \( a_i - b q_i^* - f_i = 0 \). Solving for \( q_i^* \):

\[ q_i^* = \frac{a_i - f_i}{b} = \frac{4.5\% - 1.5\%}{0.5\% / \$1\text{B}} = \frac{3.0\%}{0.005 / \$1\text{B}} = \$6.0 \text{ billion} \]

At equilibrium, gross alpha equals the fee: \( \alpha_i^g = a_i - b q_i^* = 4.5\% - 0.5\% \cdot 6 = 1.5\% = f_i \). Annual gross dollar value added is:

\[ V_i^* = q_i^* \cdot \alpha_i^g = \$6,000\text{M} \cdot 1.5\% = \$90.0 \text{ million per year} \]

The manager generates $90M per year of economic surplus and extracts the entire surplus via the 1.5% fee on $6B AUM.

Problem 3.2 (Dollar Value Added Under Out-of-Equilibrium Scale). A fund manages \( q = \$2.0 \text{ billion} \) with a 1.20% annual management fee. Over the past year, the fund delivered an investor net alpha of \( \hat{\alpha}^n = +1.80\% \) after all fees. Compute the fund's realized gross dollar value added \( V \).

Solution

The realized gross alpha is the sum of net alpha and the management fee:

\[ \hat{\alpha}^g = \hat{\alpha}^n + f = 1.80\% + 1.20\% = 3.00\% \]

Gross dollar value added is AUM multiplied by gross alpha:

\[ V = q \cdot \hat{\alpha}^g = \$2,000\text{M} \times 0.0300 = \$60.0 \text{ million} \]

The fund extracted $60M of economic value from the market, distributing \( \$2,000\text{M} \times 1.8\% = \$36\text{M} \) to outside investors and keeping \( \$2,000\text{M} \times 1.2\% = \$24\text{M} \) in fees.

Problem 3.3 (Optimal Fee Maximization). Suppose a fund manager can set their management fee fraction \( f \) before investors allocate capital. Given intrinsic skill \( a_i = 6.0\% \) and scale diseconomy parameter \( b = 0.002 \) (per $100M AUM), derive the fee fraction \( f^* \) that maximizes total manager fee revenue \( \text{Rev}(f) = f \cdot q^*(f) \).

Solution

In equilibrium, fund size is \( q^*(f) = (a_i - f)/b \). Total fee revenue is:

\[ \text{Rev}(f) = f \cdot \left( \frac{a_i - f}{b} \right) = \frac{a_i f - f^2}{b} \]

Taking the derivative with respect to \( f \) and setting to zero:

\[ \frac{d \text{Rev}}{df} = \frac{a_i - 2f}{b} = 0 \implies f^* = \frac{a_i}{2} \]

For \( a_i = 6.0\% \), the revenue-maximizing fee is \( f^* = 6.0\% / 2 = 3.0\% \). At this fee, equilibrium fund size is \( q^* = (6.0\% - 3.0\%)/b = 1.5/b \), and the manager extracts maximum revenue.

Problem 3.4 (Convexity of Trading Costs and Quadratic Value Added). Suppose total price impact costs are quadratic in traded volume: \( C(q) = \frac{1}{2} c q^2 \). Show that the manager's gross value added is strictly concave in fund size \( q \), and prove that the marginal value added is zero at \( q^* = a / c \).

Solution

Let gross trading profits before price impact be \( \Pi_{gross}(q) = a q \). Net dollar value added after price impact costs is:

\[ V(q) = \Pi_{gross}(q) - C(q) = a q - \frac{1}{2} c q^2 \]

Taking the first and second derivatives with respect to \( q \):

\[ \frac{dV}{dq} = a - c q, \quad \frac{d^2 V}{dq^2} = -c < 0 \]

Because the second derivative is strictly negative for \( c > 0 \), \( V(q) \) is strictly concave. Setting \( dV/dq = 0 \) yields \( q^* = a / c \). At this optimal size, marginal value added is zero, and maximum total value added extracted from the market is \( V(q^*) = a(a/c) - \frac{1}{2} c (a/c)^2 = \frac{a^2}{2c} \).

Problem 3.5 (Invariance of Aggregate Active Underperformance). Prove that in a financial market where active managers have heterogeneous skill \( a_i \) and heterogeneous fees \( f_i \), the dollar-weighted aggregate net return of all active managers strictly underperforms the market return by exactly total dollar operating expenses.

Solution

Let \( q_i \) denote the AUM of active fund \( i \in \{1, \dots, M\} \), and let \( Q = \sum_{i=1}^M q_i \) be aggregate active AUM. Total passive capital is \( W_P \), with total market wealth \( W = Q + W_P \).

Each active fund earns gross return \( R_{it}^g = R_m + \alpha_{it}^g \) and charges fee \( f_i \), delivering net return \( R_{it}^n = R_{it}^g - f_i \). By Sharpe's theorem, the aggregate gross active return equals the market return: \( \sum_{i=1}^M \frac{q_i}{Q} R_{it}^g = R_m \), which implies \( \sum_{i=1}^M q_i \alpha_{it}^g = 0 \).

The dollar-weighted aggregate net return is:

\[ R_{active, t}^n = \sum_{i=1}^M \frac{q_i}{Q} (R_{it}^g - f_i) = \sum_{i=1}^M \frac{q_i}{Q} R_{it}^g - \sum_{i=1}^M \frac{q_i f_i}{Q} = R_m - \bar{f} \]

where \( \bar{f} = \sum_{i=1}^M q_i f_i / Q \) is the asset-weighted average fee. Thus aggregate active management underperforms the market by exactly \( \bar{f} \), regardless of the cross-sectional distribution of skill.

Problem 3.6 (Non-Identification of Skill from Historical Alphas). Prove that in the Berk-Green steady-state equilibrium, the cross-sectional covariance between true manager skill \( a_i \) and equilibrium net alpha \( \alpha_i^n \) is identically zero.

Solution

In steady-state equilibrium, rational capital flows have adjusted AUM such that \( q_i^* = (a_i - f_i)/b \) for all active funds. Evaluating net alpha at \( q_i^* \):

\[ \alpha_i^n(q_i^*) = a_i - b q_i^* - f_i = a_i - b \left( \frac{a_i - f_i}{b} \right) - f_i = a_i - (a_i - f_i) - f_i = 0 \]

Because \( \alpha_i^n(q_i^*) = 0 \) for every fund in the cross-section, net alpha is a degenerate constant across all funds: \( \Var(\alpha^n) = 0 \). Consequently:

\[ \Cov(a_i, \alpha_i^n) = \E[a_i \alpha_i^n] - \E[a_i] \E[\alpha_i^n] = 0 - \E[a_i] \cdot 0 = 0 \]

True managerial skill \( a_i \) cannot be identified from observed net alphas in a fully equilibrated capital market.

Problem 3.7 (Empirical Persistence of Value Added). Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) document that dollar value added \( V_{it} \) is highly persistent across 5-year non-overlapping periods, whereas net alpha \( \alpha_{it}^n \) has zero persistence. Explain the economic mechanism behind this empirical divergence.

Solution

The divergence arises from the structural difference between equilibrium quantities and equilibrium prices:

  1. Net Alpha is Driven to Zero by Capital Flows: If a manager generates positive net alpha, investors pour money into the fund. Diseconomies of scale erode future gross alpha until net alpha returns to zero. Thus net alpha cannot exhibit persistence.
  2. Fund Scale Persists: A skilled manager expands to a large equilibrium AUM \( q_i^* = (a_i - f_i)/b \). Because intrinsic skill \( a_i \) is a durable managerial attribute (talent, team, proprietary models), the manager maintains large AUM over time.
  3. Value Added Equals Fee Revenue: In equilibrium, \( V_i^* = q_i^* f_i \). Because AUM \( q_i^* \) and fees \( f_i \) are persistent over time, gross dollar value added \( V_{it} \) is persistent across decades, providing an observable metric of durable skill.

Problem 3.8 (Reconciling Fama-French 2010 and Berk-van Binsbergen 2015). Synthesize the findings of Fama & French (2010) (who find zero net alpha for mutual funds) with Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) (who find positive value added of $3.2M/year). Are these two papers contradictory or complementary?

Solution

The two papers are entirely complementary and confirm the identical economic equilibrium from two different perspectives:

  1. The Investor's Perspective (Fama & French 2010): Investors buy fund shares at net asset value after fees. Fama and French evaluate net returns and find that investors earn zero net alpha, which is the exact prediction of competitive capital market equilibrium (Theorem 3.2).
  2. The Market's Perspective (Berk & van Binsbergen 2015): Active managers deploy research capital to trade against mispricings. They extract an average of $3.2 million per year of gross value from the market before fees.

Fama and French prove that investors cannot beat passive benchmarks by picking active funds. Berk and van Binsbergen prove that fund managers possess genuine skill that extracts economic surplus, but competitive capital allocation ensures that managers capture all rents via fees.

Problem 3.9 (Estimating Diseconomies of Scale in Hedge Funds). An econometrician estimates scale diseconomies for long-short equity hedge funds using panel regressions of monthly gross alpha on lagged log AUM: \( \alpha_{it}^g = \delta_i - \theta \ln(\text{AUM}_{i,t-1}) + \epsilon_{it} \). The estimated coefficient is \( \hat{\theta} = 0.0035 \) (\( t = -4.2 \)). Calculate the reduction in gross alpha when a fund's AUM doubles.

Solution

When AUM doubles from \( q_0 \) to \( 2 q_0 \), the change in log AUM is \( \Delta \ln(\text{AUM}) = \ln(2 q_0) - \ln(q_0) = \ln(2) \approx 0.69315 \).

The predicted change in monthly gross alpha is:

\[ \Delta \alpha^g = -\hat{\theta} \cdot \ln(2) = -0.0035 \times 0.69315 \approx -0.002426 \text{ (-24.3 bps per month)} \]

Annualizing the reduction: \( -0.002426 \times 12 \approx -2.91\% \) per year. Doubling fund scale erodes approximately 291 basis points of annual gross alpha due to market impact and position crowding.

Problem 3.10 (Endogenous Learning and Bayesian Fund Flows). Formulate a dynamic structural model where investors do not know manager skill \( a_i \), but update their beliefs via Bayes' rule observing noisy quarterly returns \( R_{it} = a_i - b q_{it-1} + \epsilon_{it} \). How does parameter uncertainty affect the speed of convergence to optimal size \( q_i^* \)?

Solution

Under a normal prior \( a_i \sim \Normal(\bar{a}, \sigma_0^2) \) and normal return noise \( \epsilon_{it} \iid \Normal(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2) \), the posterior mean skill after observing \( t \) periods of realized gross alpha \( \hat{\alpha}_s = R_{is} + b q_{is-1} \) is:

\[ \E[a_i \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \frac{\frac{\bar{a}}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_\epsilon^2} \sum_{s=1}^t \hat{\alpha}_s}{\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{t}{\sigma_\epsilon^2}} \]

In each period, competitive investors allocate capital until expected net alpha given current beliefs is zero: \( q_{it} = \frac{\E[a_i \mid \mathcal{F}_t] - f_i}{b} \). Parameter uncertainty creates two structural effects:

  1. Gradual Adjustment: Early in a fund's life (\( t \) small), posterior precision is low, so capital flows respond cautiously to noisy returns. As \( t \to \infty \), posterior variance shrinks to zero and \( q_{it} \pto q_i^* \).
  2. Performance Chasing: Lucky return shocks cause temporary overshooting of fund size beyond true capacity, depressing subsequent realized returns and causing subsequent redemptions.

Problem 3.11 (Macroeconomic Cyclicality of Value Added). Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) measure value added as a constant nominal dollar figure. How should gross value added be adjusted across macroeconomic credit cycles and changing market volatility?

Solution

In recessions, aggregate market capitalization contracts while market-wide return dispersion and volatility expand. A constant dollar value added metric \( V_{it} = q_{it-1} \alpha_{it}^g \) should be adjusted along two dimensions:

  1. Scaling by Aggregate Market Capitalization: Define normalized value added as \( v_{it} = V_{it} / M_t \), where \( M_t \) is total market capitalization. Extracting $10M from a $1T market represents greater economic efficiency than extracting $10M from a $30T market.
  2. Volatility and Opportunity Set Standardization: In high-volatility regimes, cross-sectional stock return dispersion widens, increasing the potential alpha pool. Adjusting value added by cross-sectional dispersion \( \sigma_{CS, t} \) isolates managerial stock-picking efficiency from macroeconomic volatility regimes.

Problem 3.12 (Structural Estimation of the Diseconomy Parameter b). Propose an instrumental variables identification strategy to estimate the scale diseconomy parameter \( b \) in the presence of unobserved time-varying manager effort or skill.

Solution

Estimating \( \alpha_{it}^g = a_i - b q_{i,t-1} + e_{it} \) via OLS is biased because unobserved positive shocks to skill or effort \( a_{it} \) attract capital inflows, inducing positive correlation between \( q_{i,t-1} \) and the error term, biasing \( \hat{b}_{OLS} \) toward zero.

To identify \( b \), construct an exogenous instrument for fund size that is orthogonal to manager skill: use capital flows into other non-overlapping funds within the same mutual fund family driven by 401(k) retirement plan default allocation shifts (e.g. Sialm, Starks & Zhang 2015), or geographic weather/tax-driven wealth shocks to fund investors. Because 401(k) platform additions generate large exogenous capital inflows into a fund independent of the manager's current stock-picking skill, IV/2SLS estimation cleanly identifies the structural scale drag \( b \).

8Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the central implication of Sharpe's (1991) arithmetic of active management?

    Options

    By market-clearing accounting, the active dollar portfolio aggregates to the market; higher operating expenses ensure net active underperformance.

  2. In the Berk & Green (2004) model, why does expected net alpha equal zero in equilibrium?

    Options

    Capital is elastic and competitive: investors chase positive net alpha, expanding fund AUM until price impact and scale drag drive expected net alpha to zero.

  3. How do Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) define a fund manager's gross dollar value added?

    Options

    Gross value added measures the total dollar surplus extracted by the manager from financial markets prior to fee extraction.

  4. According to Berk & van Binsbergen (2015), what is the average gross dollar value added (in millions of inflation-adjusted dollars per year) generated by U.S. mutual funds?

    Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) find an average gross dollar value added of $3.2 million per year per active mutual fund.

  5. Who captures the economic rents generated by managerial skill in the Berk-Green equilibrium?

    Options

    Because investor capital is elastic and competitive while managerial skill is scarce, managers capture 100% of the economic surplus via fees.

  6. A fund manager has intrinsic skill \( a_i = 4.0\% \), charges fee \( f = 1.0\% \), and faces scale diseconomy \( b = 0.6\% \) per billion dollars of AUM. Calculate the fund's equilibrium AUM in billions of dollars.

    Equilibrium AUM is \( q^* = (a_i - f)/b = (4.0\% - 1.0\%) / (0.6\% / \$1\text{B}) = 3.0 / 0.6 = 5.0 \) billion dollars.

  7. Why does historical net alpha fail to predict future net alpha in mutual fund panels?

    Options

    Capital inflows expand fund assets, triggering scale diseconomies that erode outperformance and return expected net alpha to zero.

  8. A mutual fund with $2 billion AUM generates a net alpha of +0.5% after charging a 1.5% management fee. Calculate the fund's dollar value added in millions of dollars.

    Gross alpha is \( 0.5\% + 1.5\% = 2.0\% \). Dollar value added is \( \$2,000\text{M} \times 0.020 = \$40.0 \) million.

  9. What empirical property of gross value added did Berk & van Binsbergen (2015) discover that contrasts sharply with net alpha?

    Options

    Because skilled managers maintain large AUM and stable fee rates over time, gross dollar value added exhibits strong decadal persistence.

  10. How does flow-performance convexity distort the Berk-Green theoretical equilibrium in practice?

    Options

    Sluggish redemptions by retail investors allow underperforming funds to remain larger than their optimal zero-AUM scale, generating a persistent negative net alpha left tail.
