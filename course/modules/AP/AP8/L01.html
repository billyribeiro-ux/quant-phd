The Joint-Hypothesis Problem & Efficiently Inefficient Markets

Informational market efficiency cannot be tested in a vacuum. Because fundamental asset values are unobservable, every empirical test of pricing efficiency must simultaneously evaluate an equilibrium model of expected returns. When information acquisition requires costly resources, perfectly revealing prices destroy search incentives. In equilibrium, markets must be efficiently inefficient: prices deviate from fundamental value by just enough to reward active searchers and arbitrageurs for their research expenses, margin frictions, and execution risks.

1The Joint-Hypothesis Problem in Empirical Finance

In his seminal reviews, Fama (1970) defined an informationally efficient financial market as one in which prices always fully reflect all available information. Formally, let \( \Phi_t \) denote the information set available to market participants at time \( t \), and let \( P_{j,t} \) denote the market price of asset \( j \). The conditional probability distribution of future prices perceived by the market is \( f_m(P_{j,t+1} \mid \Phi_t) \), and the true objective data-generating distribution is \( f(P_{j,t+1} \mid \Phi_t) \). Under the efficient-markets hypothesis, \( f_m = f \).

Definition 1.1 (The Joint-Hypothesis Problem)

Any statistical test of market efficiency must evaluate whether observed price deviations from an expected benchmark represent abnormal predictability. Expected returns conditional on current information satisfy:

\[ \E[R_{j,t+1} \mid \Phi_t] = f(\Phi_t) \]

where \( f(\cdot) \) is determined by a maintained equilibrium asset pricing model. Consequently, an empirical rejection of the null hypothesis \( H_0: \E[R_{j,t+1} - f(\Phi_t) \mid \Phi_t] = 0 \) is inherently ambiguous: it demonstrates either that markets are informationally inefficient, or that the maintained pricing model \( f(\Phi_t) \) is misspecified, or both.

Fama (1991) emphasized that the joint-hypothesis problem cannot be circumvented. Because economists cannot directly observe the marginal investor's true stochastic discount factor \( M_{t+1} \), any test of the Euler equation \( \E_t[M_{t+1} R_{j,t+1}] = 1 \) requires parameterizing \( M_{t+1} \) via the CAPM, multi-factor ICAPM, or consumption models. What one researcher labels an anomalous mispricing, another interprets as an omitted macroeconomic risk factor.

In his 1991 review, Fama replaced the traditional tripartite taxonomy of EMH forms (weak, semi-strong, strong) with three operational testing genres:

  1. Tests for return predictability: Evaluating whether lagged variables (such as dividend-price ratios, default spreads, and short rates) forecast multi-period returns, acknowledging that predictability reflects time-varying discount rates rather than inefficiency.
  2. Event studies: Analyzing narrow event windows around corporate announcements to evaluate the speed of public information incorporation, where model misspecification has negligible impact on daily expected returns.
  3. Tests for private information: Examining whether corporate insiders or active professional managers possess proprietary signals that yield persistent abnormal performance.

2The Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets

The neoclassical ideal of costless, instantaneous price discovery faces a logical contradiction when information acquisition requires costly effort. The Grossman-Stiglitz paradox demonstrates that informational efficiency cannot exist in competitive equilibrium if gathering information is costly.

Theorem 1.1 (Impossibility of Costless Informationally Efficient Markets)

Consider a competitive financial market where acquiring a private informative signal requires paying a positive research cost \( c > 0 \). If market prices fully and costlessly reveal all private information to uninformed market participants, then no competitive equilibrium exists in which private information is gathered.

Proof

Assume by contradiction that there exists a competitive equilibrium in which a strictly positive fraction of investors \( \lambda > 0 \) pays cost \( c > 0 \) to observe the private signal \( s = v + \epsilon \). Let the equilibrium market-clearing price be \( P \).

If the market is fully informationally efficient, the price function \( P(s) \) is invertible with respect to the signal \( s \). Uninformed investors observing \( P \) can invert the price to deduce \( s \) exactly without paying research cost \( c \).

Let \( U_I \) and \( U_U \) denote the expected utilities of informed and uninformed traders, respectively. Since uninformed traders obtain the identical posterior distribution of the asset payoff \( v \) from the revealed price without paying \( c \), their net payoff satisfies:

\[ W_U = W_I + c \]

Because utility is strictly monotonically increasing in final wealth, \( \E[U(W_U)] > \E[U(W_I)] \) for any \( c > 0 \). Informed traders earn a strictly lower expected utility than uninformed free-riders. Consequently, no rational agent will pay \( c \), implying \( \lambda = 0 \).

However, if \( \lambda = 0 \), no agent gathers information, and the market price \( P \) reveals zero private information. But if \( P \) reveals no information, any individual agent who pays cost \( c \) can trade against completely uninformed counterparties and earn an informational rent exceeding \( c \). Thus \( \lambda = 0 \) cannot be an equilibrium either. Hence, no competitive equilibrium exists with fully revealing prices.

To establish an equilibrium, financial markets must contain noise: shocks to aggregate asset supply or liquidity trading that prevent uninformed traders from perfectly inferring private signals from prices. Price informativeness is determined endogenously by the balance between informed search capital and noise trading.

3The Efficiently Inefficient Market Equilibrium

Modern asset pricing resolves the information paradox by recognizing that financial markets are neither perfectly efficient nor hopelessly broken. Pedersen (2015) formalized this balance as the theory of efficiently inefficient markets.

Definition 1.2 (Efficiently Inefficient Markets)

An efficiently inefficient market is an equilibrium market state in which security prices deviate from fundamental values by an amount that is just sufficient to compensate active professional searchers and arbitrageurs for their research costs, financing margins, liquidity constraints, and execution risks, yielding zero expected net alpha to marginal outside investors.

Consider an asset with liquidation payoff \( v \sim \Normal(\mu_v, \sigma_v^2) \) and unobserved supply shock \( x \sim \Normal(0, \sigma_x^2) \). Informed investors pay cost \( c \) to observe signal \( s = v + \epsilon \), with \( \epsilon \sim \Normal(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2) \). Investors have constant absolute risk aversion (CARA) utility with risk aversion parameter \( \gamma \). In a rational expectations equilibrium with informed fraction \( \lambda \), the price satisfies:

\[ P(\lambda) = \mu_v + \frac{\lambda \sigma_v^2}{\lambda \sigma_v^2 + \gamma \sigma_\epsilon^2 \sigma_x^2} (v - \mu_v) - \gamma \sigma_{agg}^2 x \]

The conditional variance of the asset payoff for informed traders is \( \Var(v \mid s) = (1/\sigma_v^2 + 1/\sigma_\epsilon^2)^{-1} \). For uninformed traders observing price \( P \), the precision of the price signal is:

\[ h_P(\lambda) = \frac{1}{\sigma_x^2} \left( \frac{\lambda}{\gamma \sigma_\epsilon^2} \right)^2, \quad \Var(v \mid P) = \left( \frac{1}{\sigma_v^2} + h_P(\lambda) \right)^{-1} \]
Theorem 1.2 (Equilibrium Fraction of Informed Traders)

In the CARA-normal Grossman-Stiglitz / Pedersen economy, the unique equilibrium fraction of informed traders \( \lambda^* \in [0, 1] \) satisfies the indifference condition:

\[ \frac{\Var(v \mid P(\lambda^*))}{\Var(v \mid s)} = \exp(2 \gamma c) \]

provided \( 1 < \exp(2 \gamma c) \le \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\Var(v \mid s)} \). The equilibrium degree of pricing inefficiency, measured by the mispricing variance \( \Var(v \mid P(\lambda^*)) \), is strictly increasing in information cost \( c \) and risk aversion \( \gamma \).

Proof

With CARA utility and normal payoffs, an investor's expected utility is \( \E[U(W)] = -\E[\exp(-\gamma W)] \). By standard properties of the lognormal distribution, maximizing expected utility is equivalent to maximizing the certainty equivalent \( \E[W] - \frac{\gamma}{2}\Var(W) \).

Taking the ratio of unconditional expected utilities for informed and uninformed agents yields the utility ratio \( \E[U_I] / \E[U_U] = \sqrt{\Var(v \mid s) / \Var(v \mid P)} \exp(\gamma c) \). Setting this ratio to 1 for market-clearing indifference produces:

\[ \frac{\Var(v \mid P(\lambda))}{\Var(v \mid s)} = \exp(2 \gamma c) \]

The function \( g(\lambda) \equiv \Var(v \mid P(\lambda)) \) is strictly decreasing in \( \lambda \) because price precision \( h_P(\lambda) \) is strictly increasing in \( \lambda \). Therefore, the left-hand side is strictly decreasing on \( [0, 1] \), taking maximum value \( \sigma_v^2 / \Var(v \mid s) \) at \( \lambda = 0 \). By the intermediate value theorem, a unique root \( \lambda^* \) exists on \( (0, 1) \). Because the target ratio \( \exp(2 \gamma c) \) is strictly increasing in \( c \), implicit differentiation shows that \( \partial \lambda^* / \partial c < 0 \) and \( \partial \Var(v \mid P(\lambda^*)) / \partial c > 0 \).

Example 1.1 (Arbitrage Capital and Inefficiency Spread)

Suppose active quant hedge funds incur annual management research costs \( c = 1.5\% \) of AUM and face prime brokerage borrowing fees and balance-sheet haircuts requiring an expected hurdle of \( m = 2.0\% \). In the Pedersen framework, the equilibrium gross alpha of the marginal mispriced security must equal \( \alpha^* = c + m = 3.5\% \). Outside investors who pay these fees earn net alpha \( \alpha^{net} = 3.5\% - 1.5\% - 2.0\% = 0.0\% \). Prices remain inefficient by 350 basis points in equilibrium.

Lab 1 · Solving the Efficiently Inefficient Market Equilibrium

Solving the competitive rational expectations equilibrium across a spectrum of information acquisition costs verifies that the equilibrium fraction of informed traders decreases monotonically with cost, reducing price informativeness and widening the equilibrium mispricing variance.

"""Lab AP8-L01-1: Equilibrium Degree of Inefficiency in the Grossman-Stiglitz / Pedersen Framework.

SIMULATED: Solves the competitive rational expectations equilibrium with costly information acquisition,
computing the equilibrium fraction of informed searchers lambda*(c), price informativeness,
and the resulting equilibrium degree of mispricing across information cost levels.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def solve_gs_equilibrium(
    c: float,
    gamma: float = 1.0,
    sigma_v2: float = 4.0,
    sigma_eps2: float = 1.0,
    sigma_x2: float = 1.0,
    tol: float = 1e-8,
    max_iter: int = 100,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Solve for equilibrium informed fraction lambda in [0, 1].

    In the classic CARA-normal setup (Grossman & Stiglitz 1980, Pedersen 2015):
    - Payoff v ~ N(mu_v, sigma_v^2)
    - Signal s = v + epsilon, epsilon ~ N(0, sigma_eps^2)
    - Noise trader supply shock x ~ N(0, sigma_x^2)
    - Research cost: c > 0
    - Risk aversion: gamma

    Indifference between informed and uninformed expected utility requires:
        Var(v | P(lambda)) / Var(v | s) = exp(2 * gamma * c)
    where:
        Var(v | s) = 1 / (1/sigma_v^2 + 1/sigma_eps^2)
        Var(v | P(lambda)) = 1 / (1/sigma_v^2 + h_P(lambda))
        h_P(lambda) = (lambda / (gamma * sigma_eps^2))^2 / sigma_x^2
    """
    prec_v = 1.0 / sigma_v2
    prec_eps = 1.0 / sigma_eps2
    prec_inf = prec_v + prec_eps
    var_inf = 1.0 / prec_inf

    target_ratio = math.exp(2.0 * gamma * c)

    def var_uninformed(lam: float) -> float:
        if lam <= 1e-12:
            return sigma_v2
        h_p = ((lam / (gamma * sigma_eps2)) ** 2) / sigma_x2
        return 1.0 / (prec_v + h_p)

    ratio_0 = var_uninformed(0.0) / var_inf
    ratio_1 = var_uninformed(1.0) / var_inf

    if ratio_0 <= target_ratio:
        lam_star = 0.0
    elif ratio_1 >= target_ratio:
        lam_star = 1.0
    else:
        lo = 0.0
        hi = 1.0
        for _ in range(max_iter):
            mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
            cur_ratio = var_uninformed(mid) / var_inf
            if abs(cur_ratio - target_ratio) < tol:
                break
            if cur_ratio > target_ratio:
                lo = mid
            else:
                hi = mid
        lam_star = 0.5 * (lo + hi)

    var_uninf = var_uninformed(lam_star)
    # Informativeness: percentage reduction in payoff variance from market price
    informativeness = 1.0 - (var_uninf / sigma_v2)
    # Mispricing standard deviation
    mispricing_std = math.sqrt(var_uninf)

    return {
        "cost": c,
        "lambda_star": lam_star,
        "var_uninformed": var_uninf,
        "informativeness": informativeness,
        "mispricing_std": mispricing_std,
    }


def main() -> None:
    costs = [0.05, 0.15, 0.30, 0.50, 0.70]
    print("================================================================================")
    print("GROSSMAN-STIGLITZ & PEDERSEN EQUILIBRIUM: COSTLY INFORMATION & MARKET EFFICIENCY")
    print("================================================================================")
    print(f"{'Cost (c)':>10} | {'Informed (λ*)':>14} | {'Price Info':>12} | {'Mispricing Std':>16}")
    print("-" * 62)

    for c in costs:
        res = solve_gs_equilibrium(c=c)
        print(
            f"{res['cost']:10.2f} | "
            f"{res['lambda_star']:14.4f} | "
            f"{res['informativeness'] * 100:11.2f}% | "
            f"{res['mispricing_std']:16.4f}"
        )

    print("-" * 62)
    print("Equilibrium Condition: Expected Utility(Informed) == Expected Utility(Uninformed)")
    print("Key Finding: As information cost c rises, equilibrium informed capital λ* falls,")
    print("price informativeness deteriorates, and the equilibrium degree of inefficiency rises.")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The classical efficient markets paradigm breaks along three distinct dimensions when confronted with realistic market frictions:

  • Costless Arbitrage Illusion: The textbook hypothesis assumes that arbitrage is costless, instantaneous, and unlimited. In practice, arbitrageurs face explicit search costs, short-sale borrowing fees, margin requirements, and finite capital horizons (Pedersen 2015).
  • Noise-Trader Risk & Resale Uncertainty: Even when fundamental value is known with certainty, arbitrageurs face the risk that irrational noise-trader sentiment worsens over their liquidation horizon (De Long et al. 1990). Resale-price risk imposes an endogenous limit on arbitrage capacity.
  • The Fallacy of the Zero-Cost Price Signal: Neoclassical models treat market prices as public goods that transmit information without consuming economic resources. As proven by Grossman and Stiglitz, informational efficiency cannot be an equilibrium without compensation for the resources spent discovering prices.

5Misconceptions & pitfalls

  1. “If an empirical study documents a statistically significant return anomaly, it proves that the market is inefficient.”

    Due to the joint-hypothesis problem (Fama 1970), any empirical test is simultaneously a test of market efficiency and the underlying equilibrium model of expected returns. An apparent anomaly may simply indicate that the maintained model omits an important systematic risk premium.

  2. “Market efficiency implies that prices are always equal to intrinsic fundamental value.”

    In the modern efficiently-inefficient equilibrium (Pedersen 2015), prices intentionally deviate from intrinsic value by an amount sufficient to cover the research costs, financing margins, and execution risks of professional arbitrageurs. True fundamental equality is economically impossible.

  3. “If active managers as a group cannot beat passive index funds net of fees, financial markets must be perfectly efficient.”

    Zero net alpha across active managers is an equilibrium condition of competitive capital allocation, not proof of price perfection. Active managers generate substantial gross value added to cover their research expenses and keep prices near efficiency (Berk & Green 2004).

6Problem set

Problem 1.1 (Precision and Indifference). In a CARA-normal economy, prior payoff variance is \( \sigma_v^2 = 9.0 \), informed signal noise variance is \( \sigma_\epsilon^2 = 1.0 \), and investor risk aversion is \( \gamma = 0.5 \). Compute the informed conditional variance \( \Var(v \mid s) \) and determine the maximum research cost \( c_{max} \) for which any informed trading can occur in equilibrium.

Solution

The precision of the prior is \( h_v = 1/\sigma_v^2 = 1/9 \). The precision of the signal is \( h_\epsilon = 1/\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 1 \). The informed conditional precision is \( h_{inf} = h_v + h_\epsilon = 1/9 + 1 = 10/9 \), giving conditional variance:

\[ \Var(v \mid s) = \frac{1}{10/9} = 0.90 \]

For informed trading to occur in equilibrium, the indifference ratio at \( \lambda = 0 \) must satisfy \( \Var(v \mid P(0)) / \Var(v \mid s) \ge \exp(2 \gamma c) \). At \( \lambda = 0 \), prices reveal no information, so \( \Var(v \mid P(0)) = \sigma_v^2 = 9.0 \). Setting \( 9.0 / 0.90 = 10.0 = \exp(2 \cdot 0.5 \cdot c_{max}) = \exp(c_{max}) \) yields \( c_{max} = \ln(10) \approx 2.3026 \). If research cost exceeds 2.3026, no investor acquires information.

Problem 1.2 (Price Precision Under Partial Information). In the Grossman-Stiglitz setup with \( \gamma = 1.0 \), \( \sigma_\epsilon^2 = 1.0 \), and \( \sigma_x^2 = 4.0 \), calculate the price precision \( h_P(\lambda) \) when the informed fraction is \( \lambda = 0.40 \).

Solution

The price signal precision formula is \( h_P(\lambda) = \frac{1}{\sigma_x^2} \left( \frac{\lambda}{\gamma \sigma_\epsilon^2} \right)^2 \). Substituting the given parameters:

\[ h_P(0.40) = \frac{1}{4.0} \left( \frac{0.40}{1.0 \cdot 1.0} \right)^2 = \frac{1}{4.0} \cdot 0.16 = 0.04 \]

The information transmitted through the equilibrium price adds 0.04 to the precision of uninformed investors, reducing their uncertainty below the prior variance.

Problem 1.3 (Pedersen Hurdle Calculation). A long-short equity arbitrage fund expends 120 basis points per year on data, alternative feeds, and analyst compensation. Financing costs require a 50 basis point margin haircut drag and 80 basis points in borrow fees. What is the minimum gross alpha hurdle required for the fund to break even net of frictions?

Solution

In the Pedersen efficiently-inefficient framework, the equilibrium gross alpha hurdle \( \alpha^* \) must cover all operating research expenses, financing haircuts, and borrow fees:

\[ \alpha^* = c_{research} + c_{haircut} + c_{borrow} = 1.20\% + 0.50\% + 0.80\% = 2.50\% \]

The fund requires a gross alpha of at least 250 basis points (2.50% annualized) to deliver non-negative net alpha to outside investors in equilibrium.

Problem 1.4 (Monotonicity of Price Informativeness). Prove that in the CARA-normal model, the variance of the payoff conditional on price, \( \Var(v \mid P(\lambda)) \), is strictly decreasing in the informed trader fraction \( \lambda \in [0, 1] \).

Solution

The conditional variance of payoff \( v \) given price \( P \) is:

\[ \Var(v \mid P(\lambda)) = \left( \frac{1}{\sigma_v^2} + h_P(\lambda) \right)^{-1} \]

where \( h_P(\lambda) = \frac{1}{\sigma_x^2} \left( \frac{\lambda}{\gamma \sigma_\epsilon^2} \right)^2 = \frac{\lambda^2}{\gamma^2 \sigma_\epsilon^4 \sigma_x^2} \). Taking the derivative with respect to \( \lambda \) for \( \lambda > 0 \):

\[ \frac{d h_P(\lambda)}{d\lambda} = \frac{2 \lambda}{\gamma^2 \sigma_\epsilon^4 \sigma_x^2} > 0 \]

By the chain rule, \( \frac{d \Var(v \mid P(\lambda))}{d\lambda} = - \left( \frac{1}{\sigma_v^2} + h_P(\lambda) \right)^{-2} \frac{d h_P(\lambda)}{d\lambda} < 0 \). Hence, conditional variance is strictly decreasing in \( \lambda \), meaning prices become strictly more informative as informed capital expands.

Problem 1.5 (Comparative Statics of Information Cost). Prove that the equilibrium fraction of informed traders \( \lambda^*(c) \) satisfies \( \frac{d \lambda^*}{dc} < 0 \).

Solution

The equilibrium indifference condition is \( F(\lambda, c) \equiv \frac{\Var(v \mid P(\lambda))}{\Var(v \mid s)} - \exp(2 \gamma c) = 0 \). By the implicit function theorem, \( \frac{d \lambda^*}{dc} = - \frac{\partial F / \partial c}{\partial F / \partial \lambda} \).

Evaluating the partial derivatives: \( \frac{\partial F}{\partial c} = -2 \gamma \exp(2 \gamma c) < 0 \), and \( \frac{\partial F}{\partial \lambda} = \frac{1}{\Var(v \mid s)} \frac{d \Var(v \mid P(\lambda))}{d\lambda} < 0 \) (from Problem 1.4). Therefore:

\[ \frac{d \lambda^*}{dc} = - \frac{-2 \gamma \exp(2 \gamma c)}{\frac{1}{\Var(v \mid s)} \frac{d \Var(v \mid P)}{d\lambda}} < 0 \]

The numerator is negative and the denominator is negative, so with the leading negative sign, the total derivative is strictly negative. An increase in information acquisition cost strictly reduces the equilibrium informed capital.

Problem 1.6 (Equilibrium Existence and Uniqueness). Prove that under the condition \( 1 < \exp(2 \gamma c) < \sigma_v^2 / \Var(v \mid s) \), there exists a unique interior equilibrium \( \lambda^* \in (0, 1) \).

Solution

Define the function \( \Phi(\lambda) = \frac{\Var(v \mid P(\lambda))}{\Var(v \mid s)} \) on the compact domain \( [0, 1] \). At \( \lambda = 0 \), \( h_P(0) = 0 \), so \( \Phi(0) = \sigma_v^2 / \Var(v \mid s) \). At \( \lambda = 1 \), \( \Phi(1) = \Var(v \mid P(1)) / \Var(v \mid s) \ge 1 \).

From Problem 1.4, \( \Phi(\lambda) \) is continuous and strictly monotonically decreasing on \( [0, 1] \). The target value \( K \equiv \exp(2 \gamma c) \) satisfies \( \Phi(1) < K < \Phi(0) \) under the stated hypothesis. By the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists at least one root \( \lambda^* \in (0, 1) \) such that \( \Phi(\lambda^*) = K \). Because \( \Phi(\lambda) \) is strictly monotonic, this root is strictly unique.

Problem 1.7 (Event Study Horizon and Joint-Hypothesis Vulnerability). Contrast the joint-hypothesis vulnerability of a 3-day corporate earnings event study with a 5-year post-IPO long-run performance study. Quantify the relative impact of model misspecification on abnormal return inference.

Solution

In an event study with horizon \( \tau \) days, cumulative abnormal return is \( \operatorname{CAR}_{i} = \sum_{t=1}^\tau (R_{it} - \E_t[R_{it}]) \). Daily expected returns are roughly \( \E[R_{it}] \approx 10\% / 252 \approx 0.04\% \) per day.

Over a 3-day window (\( \tau = 3 \)), expected return is \( 3 \times 0.04\% = 0.12\% \). A 50% model misspecification error biases cumulative expected return by only 6 basis points (0.06%), which is negligible relative to a typical 300 to 500 basis point earnings announcement surprise.

Over a 5-year horizon (\( \tau = 1,260 \) days), expected return is \( 1,260 \times 0.04\% \approx 50.4\% \). A 50% misspecification generates a cumulative bias of 25.2 percentage points (2,520 basis points), completely overwhelming observed long-run abnormal returns. Hence, long-horizon tests are severely compromised by the joint-hypothesis problem, whereas short-horizon event studies are robust.

Problem 1.8 (Empirical Estimation of Price Informativeness). An empirical researcher measures the variance of 1-year corporate earnings surprises across two market sectors. In Sector A, stock price revisions explain 65% of future earnings variance. In Sector B, price revisions explain 25%. Using Grossman-Stiglitz reasoning, explain what economic structural parameters account for this cross-sector divergence.

Solution

Price informativeness \( 1 - \Var(v \mid P)/\sigma_v^2 \) depends on the signal precision \( h_P(\lambda) = \frac{\lambda^2}{\gamma^2 \sigma_\epsilon^4 \sigma_x^2} \). Sector A exhibits higher price informativeness (65% vs 25%) due to three structural factors:

  1. Lower Information Acquisition Costs (\( c \)): Sector A firms have standardized reporting and extensive sell-side coverage, lowering \( c \) and expanding informed capital fraction \( \lambda^* \).
  2. Lower Noise Trader Volatility (\( \sigma_x^2 \)): Sector A is dominated by institutional indexers and quants, whereas Sector B experiences heavy retail speculative flow, increasing noise shock variance \( \sigma_x^2 \) and obscuring fundamental price signals.
  3. Higher Fundamental Signal Quality (\( 1/\sigma_\epsilon^2 \)): Sector A cash flows have lower idiosyncratic noise, making research signals more precise.

Problem 1.9 (Testing Time-Varying Expected Returns). A predictive regression of annual stock market excess returns on the dividend-price ratio yields \( R_{t+1} - R_{f,t} = 0.08 + 0.32 \cdot (D_t/P_t) + e_{t+1} \) with an OLS \( R^2 = 0.09 \). Why does this predictability support modern rational market models rather than refuting market efficiency?

Solution

Under modern asset pricing (AP5, Fama 1991), expected returns are not constant over time: \( \E_t[R_{t+1}] = R_{f,t} + \operatorname{Cov}_t(R_{t+1}, -M_{t+1})/\E_t[M_{t+1}] \). In recessions, aggregate investor wealth declines, risk aversion rises, and consumption volatility expands, driving up the required equity risk premium.

Because stock prices fall in recessions, the dividend yield \( D_t/P_t \) spikes during cyclical downturns precisely when rational discount rates are elevated. The positive regression coefficient \( \beta = 0.32 \) reflects countercyclical risk compensation. In the joint-hypothesis framework, predictability is consistent with rational discount-rate variation rather than market inefficiency.

Problem 1.10 (Endogenous Noise Trading and Market Stability). In the Grossman-Stiglitz framework, noise trading \( x \) is assumed exogenous. Formulate the consequences if noise trader participation is made endogenous to price volatility \( \Var(P) \). Could a self-reinforcing informational collapse occur?

Solution

Suppose noise trader volume is an increasing function of perceived market stability: \( \sigma_x^2 = g(\Var(P)) \) with \( g' < 0 \). When market volatility increases, noise traders exit, reducing liquidity supply \( \sigma_x^2 \).

As \( \sigma_x^2 \to 0 \), prices become increasingly revealing of informed signals, reducing informed profits. Informed traders exit (\( \lambda \to 0 \)), which reduces price informativeness and increases fundamental uncertainty \( \Var(v \mid P) \). This feedback loop generates multiple equilibria: a liquid, informative equilibrium with active noise trading and high informed participation, and an illiquid market freeze where volatility spikes, noise traders withdraw, informed research collapses, and price discovery ceases.

Problem 1.11 (Machine Learning, Alternative Data, and Equilibrium Search Costs). The proliferation of alternative data (satellite imagery, credit card transactions, web scraping) has lowered data generation costs while increasing data processing infrastructure costs. Evaluate how this technological shift alters the equilibrium degree of inefficiency \( \alpha^* \) in the Pedersen framework.

Solution

In Pedersen's formulation, equilibrium inefficiency satisfies \( \alpha^* = c + m \), where \( c \) is search cost per unit capital and \( m \) is balance sheet financing cost. The technological shift has two offsetting effects:

  1. Fixed Infrastructure vs. Variable Costs: Alternative data requires high fixed investments in infrastructure and quantitative engineering. For large quantitative managers, variable search cost per dollar managed decreases, compressing gross alpha in easily automated, high-frequency signals.
  2. Fast Data Decay & Capacity Constraints: Alternative data signals decay rapidly as multiple quants subscribe to the same vendors, reducing capacity. Quants must continuously discover novel uncrowded data sources to sustain alpha, maintaining positive equilibrium search costs \( c > 0 \).

The net result is that gross alpha compresses in standardized liquid markets while widening in illiquid, complex assets where alternative data cannot be easily structured.

Problem 1.12 (Designing Falsifiable Tests Under the Joint-Hypothesis). Propose an empirical research design that cleanly separates rational multi-factor risk from behavioral mispricing for the cross-sectional momentum anomaly, explicitly addressing the joint-hypothesis challenge.

Solution

A rigorous research design must identify orthogonal predictions where rational risk models and behavioral models make opposite signs:

  1. Quarterly Earnings Announcement Windows: Rational risk models predict that momentum returns accrue continuously as compensation for holding distress risk over time. Behavioral models (e.g. Hong-Stein underreaction) predict that momentum returns concentrate disproportionately during the 3-day quarterly earnings announcements of winner and loser firms as delayed information is officially confirmed.
  2. Survey Expectation Correlations: Estimate the correlation between subjective investor expectations of winner firms and consumption marginal utility. Rational models require that investors demand higher expected returns on winners because winners pay off poorly in bad times; behavioral models predict investors extrapolate past price trends and perceive winners as safe.
  3. Post-Publication Attenuation: Analyze whether momentum returns attenuate specifically in stocks where hedge fund short-selling borrow fees are low, confirming active arbitrage capital elimination rather than static factor covariance.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. Why does Fama (1970) refer to tests of market efficiency as 'joint-hypothesis tests'?

    Options

    Without a model defining expected returns, abnormal return deviations cannot be identified; any rejection of zero abnormal returns may reflect an omitted risk factor rather than mispricing.

  2. In the Grossman-Stiglitz framework, what happens if market prices perfectly reveal all private signals?

    Options

    Uninformed investors free-ride on prices without paying research costs; informed traders earn strictly lower net utility, destroying the incentive to acquire information.

  3. How does Pedersen (2015) define an 'efficiently inefficient' market?

    Options

    Pedersen's equilibrium requires that asset prices deviate from fundamental value by just enough to reward active searchers for their real-world economic frictions.

  4. Why are short-horizon event studies (1 to 3 days) less vulnerable to the joint-hypothesis problem than multi-year studies?

    Options

    Because daily normal expected returns are only a few basis points, even severe asset pricing model errors produce negligible cumulative return distortions over 3-day event windows.

  5. In a Grossman-Stiglitz CARA-normal economy with risk aversion \( \gamma = 0.5 \), prior variance \( \sigma_v^2 = 10.0 \), and informed variance \( \Var(v \mid s) = 1.0 \), compute the maximum cost \( c \) (in natural units) at which informed traders can exist in equilibrium.

    Indifference requires \( \sigma_v^2 / \Var(v \mid s) \ge \exp(2 \gamma c) \implies 10.0 / 1.0 = 10.0 \ge \exp(1.0 \cdot c) \implies c \le \ln(10) \approx 2.3026 \).

  6. How did Fama (1991) reclassify the classic 1970 weak-form tests of market efficiency?

    Options

    Fama (1991) renamed weak-form tests to 'tests for return predictability', reflecting that expected returns vary predictably with business cycle macroeconomic state variables.

  7. In the De Long et al. (1990) DSSW model, what risk prevents rational arbitrageurs from eliminating mispricings?

    Options

    Arbitrageurs with finite horizons face the risk that irrational sentiment becomes even more extreme, forcing liquidation at depressed prices before fundamentals reassert themselves.

  8. A hedge fund faces research expenses of 1.4% of AUM, financing haircut borrowing costs of 0.8%, and execution liquidity slippage of 1.0%. In the Pedersen framework, what is the required equilibrium gross alpha (in percent per year) for the fund to break even net of frictions?

    The equilibrium gross alpha hurdle is \( \alpha^* = 1.4\% + 0.8\% + 1.0\% = 3.2\% \) per year.

  9. What role do noise traders play in generating an equilibrium in the Grossman-Stiglitz economy?

    Options

    Noise trader supply shocks obscure the connection between prices and private signals, creating an imperfectly revealing price that allows informed traders to earn informational rents.

  10. Why does the existence of return predictability over multi-year horizons fail to disprove market efficiency?

    Options

    Modern asset pricing establishes that expected returns should vary over time: risk aversion and consumption risk are high in recessions, requiring higher expected returns.
