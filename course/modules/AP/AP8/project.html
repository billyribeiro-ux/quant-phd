Debate Brief: Are Markets Efficient?

Write both sides of the resolution 'Markets are efficient' to the program's doctoral debate standard, then cross-examine the opposing side's three strongest pieces of empirical evidence. You formulate the affirmative and negative cases with full theoretical symmetry, ground every claim in verified evidence grades and exact published data-locators, deconstruct opposing claims via targeted methodological attack angles, and synthesize the structural conditions that can settle the market efficiency debate.

The four-rung project ladder

This project develops adversarial debate, evidentiary auditing, and synthesis capabilities across the canonical four-rung ladder. Students passing the hub placement diagnostic may proceed directly to Rung 3.

  1. Worked example

    Lab 1 below provides a fully verified institutional debate brief instance evaluating affirmative and negative arguments, audited by an automated Python validator for evidence grading, citation integrity, locator precision, and cross-examination symmetry.

  2. Completion

    Lab 2 provides an interactive completion problem where you implement the cross-examination scoring metric, Bayesian likelihood ratio updating across empirical findings, and operational settling condition certification.

  3. Independent

    Author your independent doctoral debate brief on the resolution: 'Markets are efficient'. Defend both sides to the program's debate standard, cross-examine the opposing side's three strongest empirical pillars against our 16-question attack bank, and self-assess against rubric criteria C1–C5.

  4. Stretch

    Formulate a structural calibration of the efficiently-inefficient-markets equilibrium matching empirical anomaly decay rates with prime broker borrowing costs and margin constraints, or defend your brief in an oral cross-examination defense against a hostile doctoral panel.

Lab 1 · Worked example: debate brief structural validator

Validates the structural completeness, evidence grading, citation integrity, and cross-examination symmetry of a model doctoral debate brief on market efficiency.

"""Lab AP8-project-1: Rung 1 worked example: debate brief structural validator.

SIMULATED: Validates the structural completeness, evidence grading, citation integrity,
and cross-examination symmetry of an institutional debate brief on market efficiency.
Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class EvidenceClaim:
    claim_id: str
    side: str  # "affirmative" or "negative"
    statement: str
    grade: str  # "T", "A", "B", "C", "D"
    registry_keys: tuple[str, ...]
    locator: str
    methodology: str


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class CrossExaminationPoint:
    target_claim_id: str
    attack_vector: str
    counter_grade: str
    counter_registry_key: str
    deconstruction: str


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class DebateBrief:
    resolution: str
    affirmative_claims: tuple[EvidenceClaim, ...]
    negative_claims: tuple[EvidenceClaim, ...]
    cross_examinations: tuple[CrossExaminationPoint, ...]
    settling_conditions: tuple[str, ...]


VALID_GRADES = frozenset({"T", "A", "B", "C", "D"})
ALLOWED_ATTACK_VECTORS = frozenset(
    {
        "joint_hypothesis_confound",
        "sample_selection_survivorship",
        "implementation_friction_decay",
        "data_mining_multiple_testing",
        "unpriced_tail_risk",
    }
)


def validate_debate_brief(brief: DebateBrief) -> dict[str, bool | int | str]:
    """Validate completeness and doctoral rigor of an EMH debate brief."""
    # Check resolution
    has_res = len(brief.resolution.strip()) > 10

    # Check claims
    n_aff = len(brief.affirmative_claims)
    n_neg = len(brief.negative_claims)
    claims_balanced = n_aff >= 3 and n_neg >= 3

    # Check grades and locators
    all_claims = brief.affirmative_claims + brief.negative_claims
    grades_valid = all(c.grade in VALID_GRADES for c in all_claims)
    locators_valid = all(len(c.locator.strip()) > 0 for c in all_claims if c.grade != "T")
    cites_valid = all(len(c.registry_keys) > 0 for c in all_claims if c.grade != "T")

    # Check cross-examinations: at least 3 distinct opposing targets
    n_cx = len(brief.cross_examinations)
    cx_targets = {cx.target_claim_id for cx in brief.cross_examinations}
    cx_sufficient = n_cx >= 3 and len(cx_targets) >= 3
    cx_vectors_valid = all(cx.attack_vector in ALLOWED_ATTACK_VECTORS for cx in brief.cross_examinations)

    # Check settling conditions
    settling_valid = len(brief.settling_conditions) >= 2

    overall_pass = (
        has_res
        and claims_balanced
        and grades_valid
        and locators_valid
        and cites_valid
        and cx_sufficient
        and cx_vectors_valid
        and settling_valid
    )

    return {
        "overall_pass": overall_pass,
        "n_affirmative": n_aff,
        "n_negative": n_neg,
        "n_cross_examinations": n_cx,
        "n_settling_conditions": len(brief.settling_conditions),
        "grades_valid": grades_valid,
        "locators_valid": locators_valid,
        "cx_vectors_valid": cx_vectors_valid,
    }

Lab 2 · Completion problem: cross-examination scoring and Bayesian updating

"""Lab AP8-project-2: Rung 2 completion problem: cross-examination scoring and Bayesian updating.

SIMULATED: Completed reference solution for scoring adversarial cross-examinations,
evaluating Bayesian posterior odds of market efficiency, and certifying empirical settling conditions.
Standard library only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def score_cross_examination(
    attack_vector: str,
    counter_grade: str,
    deconstruction_words: int,
) -> float:
    """Compute the methodological rigor score of an adversarial cross-examination (0.0 to 1.0).

    A doctoral cross-examination must:
    1. Target an approved attack vector (weight 0.40)
    2. Support the critique with Grade A/T (1.0) or Grade B (0.75) counter-evidence (weight 0.35)
    3. Articulate a detailed deconstruction >= 15 words (weight 0.25)
    """
    valid_vectors = {
        "joint_hypothesis_confound",
        "sample_selection_survivorship",
        "implementation_friction_decay",
        "data_mining_multiple_testing",
        "unpriced_tail_risk",
    }

    # Vector score (0.40)
    vector_score = 0.40 if attack_vector in valid_vectors else 0.0

    # Grade score (0.35)
    grade_weights = {"T": 1.0, "A": 1.0, "B": 0.75, "C": 0.40, "D": 0.10}
    grade_score = 0.35 * grade_weights.get(counter_grade, 0.0)

    # Deconstruction depth score (0.25)
    depth_score = 0.25 * min(1.0, ___ / 15.0)

    return round(vector_score + grade_score + depth_score, 4)


def update_efficiency_posterior_odds(
    prior_prob_emh: float,
    lr_post_publication_decay: float,
    lr_bootstrap_alpha_luck: float,
    lr_survey_expectations_puzzle: float,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Compute posterior odds and posterior probability of market efficiency using Bayes' rule.

    Odds = P(EMH) / (1 - P(EMH))
    Posterior Odds = Prior Odds * LR_1 * LR_2 * LR_3
    Posterior Prob = Posterior Odds / (1 + Posterior Odds)
    """
    prior_odds = prior_prob_emh / (1.0 - prior_prob_emh)
    combined_lr = lr_post_publication_decay * lr_bootstrap_alpha_luck * lr_survey_expectations_puzzle
    posterior_odds = ___ * combined_lr
    posterior_prob = posterior_odds / (1.0 + posterior_odds)

    return round(posterior_odds, 4), round(posterior_prob, 4)


def evaluate_settling_condition(text: str) -> bool:
    """Verify whether a proposed settling condition provides operational falsification criteria."""
    operational_keywords = ("structural", "falsif", "wealth-weighted", "micro-data", "margin", "out-of-sample")
    lower = text.lower()
    has_keyword = any(kw in lower for kw in operational_keywords)
    is_sufficient_length = len(text.strip().split()) >= 10
    return has_keyword and is_sufficient_length

Specification

This specification defines the context, inputs, required outputs, constraints, and grading mode for the doctoral debate brief.

Context
You are an academic scholar and quantitative portfolio strategist presenting a comprehensive debate brief to the doctoral faculty on whether capital markets are informationally efficient. The debate over market efficiency is the central organizing dialectic of empirical asset pricing. A doctoral-level brief cannot take a one-sided partisan view; it must formulate both sides with relentless intellectual honesty, cross-examine the opposing side's strongest empirical pillars, and synthesize the structural conditions that can settle the question.
Inputs
Primary literature and data locators from the verified registry: Fama (1970, 1991), Fama & French (2010), Sharpe (1991), Berk & Green (2004), Berk & van Binsbergen (2015), Pedersen (2015), Shleifer & Vishny (1997), De Long et al. (1990), and Greenwood & Shleifer (2014), along with the 16-point adversarial question bank.
Required outputs
A doctoral debate brief in four sections: (1) The Affirmative Case (Markets Are Efficient), (2) The Negative Case (Markets Are Inefficient), (3) Adversarial Cross-Examinations deconstructing the opposing side's three strongest empirical pillars, and (4) Equilibrium Synthesis & Settling Conditions.
Constraints
Every substantive empirical claim must be marked up with an evidence grade (T, A, B, C, D) and cite verified keys from the registry with exact data-locators. Symmetrical analytical depth for both affirmative and negative cases. Cross-examinations must isolate approved methodological attack vectors supported by cited counter-evidence.
Grading
Grading mode: rubric. Evaluated across five criteria C1–C5, each defining four observable performance levels (Levels 1 to 4). Passing requires achieving Level 3 or higher on all criteria.

Exemplar A · Level 4 (Exemplary / Doctoral Pass)

Affirmative Excerpt: Under the Fama & French (2010) cross-sectional bootstrap across 3,156 mutual funds (1984–2006), the 95th percentile actual net alpha t-statistic of +1.64 is lower than the zero-skill bootstrap null average of +1.70 (\( p = 0.58 \)). Furthermore, as proven by Berk & van Binsbergen (2015), competitive capital flows drive expected net alpha to zero in equilibrium, while active managers extract their full gross value added (average +$3.2M/year) through fund scale and fees. Post-publication, anomaly returns attenuate by ~58% (McLean & Pontiff 2016), confirming active capital deployment that eliminates pricing errors.

Negative Excerpt: Direct empirical measurement of investor beliefs via Gallup and CFO surveys (Greenwood & Shleifer 2014) documents that subjective return expectations are negatively correlated with rational discount rates (\( r = -0.43 \) with dividend yields). Rational arbitrage cannot eliminate these errors because professional managers face performance-based redemptions (Shleifer & Vishny 1997) and noise-trader resale risk (De Long et al. 1990).

Cross-Examination of Negative Claim 1 (Survey Inversion): While Greenwood & Shleifer (2014) document extrapolative survey responses, this critique suffers from a fundamental joint-hypothesis confound (Fama 1991). Survey responses measure unweighted retail headcount rather than the marginal wealth-weighted pricing kernel. If institutional investors and quant market makers manage the marginal capital, asset prices will track the rational SDF even when retail survey respondents report biased beliefs.

Rubric Evaluation (Level 4): Meets Level 4 on all criteria. C1: Explicitly frames both arguments around the joint-hypothesis Euler equation. C2: Every claim carries exact table/panel/page locators and verified citations. C3: Formulates affirmative and negative positions with complete intellectual symmetry. C4: The cross-examination attacks the methodological core (wealth-weighting vs equal-weighting) backed by Fama (1991). C5: Integrates the findings into an efficiently-inefficient equilibrium.

Exemplar B · Level 2 (Developing / Unsatisfactory)

Excerpt: Markets are indisputably efficient because active mutual fund managers cannot beat the S&P 500 index. Studies by Fama show that mutual funds have zero alpha after paying their fees, so investors should just buy Vanguard index funds. On the other hand, behavioral finance shows that investors are irrational and do foolish things, causing bubbles like the 2000 dot-com crash. Furthermore, researchers have found over 400 anomalies in the stock market, which proves that markets have lots of inefficiencies. To settle the debate, researchers need to collect more data and see if anomalies continue in the future.

Rubric Evaluation (Level 2): Fails to meet the doctoral standard. C1: Ignores the joint-hypothesis problem completely; treats alpha as an absolute measurement. C2: Zero empirical locators; cites 'Fama' without paper, year, table, or panel. C3: Completely asymmetric and informal language ('foolish things', 'indisputably efficient'). C4: No structured cross-examination; fails to identify methodological attack vectors or counter-evidence. C5: The settling condition ('collect more data') is a classic Level 1/2 vague platitude with zero operational falsification criteria.

Adversarial question bank

In oral defense or committee cross-examination, your debate brief must withstand adversarial challenges drawn from this 16-point attack bank:

  1. On the Joint-Hypothesis Problem: How can you claim that an empirical anomaly refutes market efficiency when Fama (1970, 1991) proved that any anomaly can be explained by an unobserved macroeconomic risk factor?
  2. On Berk-Green Rational Equilibrium: If active managers possess genuine skill, why do retail mutual fund investors persistently earn negative net alphas after fees?
  3. On the Fama-French Bootstrap: Does the Fama & French (2010) cross-sectional bootstrap test hold statistical power to detect skill if managerial talent is concentrated in market timing rather than security selection?
  4. On the Momentum Factor: If Carhart's (1997) momentum factor explains mutual fund persistence, does that prove efficiency or inefficiency, given that momentum itself is widely viewed as a behavioral underreaction anomaly?
  5. On Post-Publication Anomaly Decay: If anomaly returns decay by ~58% post-publication (McLean & Pontiff 2016), why does a statistically significant ~42% return spread remain unexploited in out-of-sample data?
  6. On Survey Expectations vs. Rational Models: How can rational asset pricing maintain that investors demand high returns in recessions when Greenwood & Shleifer (2014) prove that direct investor return expectations are at their cyclical trough?
  7. On Earnings Announcement Return Clustering: If anomaly returns represent continuous compensation for systematic distress risk, why do return spreads cluster disproportionately in 3-day earnings announcement windows?
  8. On Limits to Delegated Arbitrage: If hedge funds are the smart money enforcing market efficiency, why did quantitative equity market-neutral funds experience catastrophic fire-sale liquidations during the August 2007 Quant Meltdown?
  9. On Characteristic vs. Covariance Models: Why did Daniel & Titman (1997) find that book-to-market characteristics predict returns even after controlling for Fama-French 3-factor loadings?
  10. On the Factor Zoo Replication Crisis: Given that Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) show that hundreds of published factors fail multiple-testing thresholds, how can any published anomaly brief be trusted?
  11. On Value Added Measurement: Does Berk & van Binsbergen's (2015) gross value added metric confound managerial skill with passive market timing or asset-gathering marketing prowess?
  12. On Gross vs. Net Skill Tail Asymmetry: Why does the left tail of underperforming mutual funds in Fama & French (2010) exhibit severe negative skill, while the right tail exhibits zero net skill? Why does the market fail to eliminate unskilled managers?
  13. On Cross-Asset Factor Spanning: If behavioral cognitive biases are idiosyncratic to equity retail traders, why do value and momentum factors replicate across sovereign bonds, currencies, and commodities (Asness et al. 2013)?
  14. On Equal vs. Value Weighting: If an anomaly is statistically significant under equal weighting but disappears under value weighting, does it represent genuine economic mispricing or an un-tradeable micro-cap liquidity friction?
