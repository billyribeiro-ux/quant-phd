State prices are a list of numbers, one per state. Divide each by the probability of its state and the list becomes a random variable, the stochastic discount factor, and every price becomes an expectation, \(p = \E[mx]\). That one equation is the common language of asset pricing: CAPM, consumption models, factor models and option pricing are all claims about what \(m\) is. This lesson proves that some \(m\) exists whenever the law of one price holds, that exactly one lies in the space of traded payoffs, and that this one, \(x^\ast\), is the projection of every other. The project recovers \(x^\ast\) from data.

1Payoffs as random variables

Keep the one-period market \((X, p)\) with \(S\) states and the probabilities \(\pi_s > 0\) of Lesson 1. A payoff \(z \in \R^S\) is a random variable on \(\Omega = \{1, \dots, S\}\), and its expectation is \(\E[z] = \sum_s \pi_s z_s\). Write \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_N)^\top\) for the random vector of the \(N\) asset payoffs, so that \(x(s)\) is the \(s\)-th row of \(X\). Its second-moment matrix is \[ \E[xx^\top] = \sum_s \pi_s\, x(s)x(s)^\top = X^\top \Pi X, \qquad \Pi = \diag(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_S). \]

Definition 1.1 (stochastic discount factor)

A stochastic discount factor (SDF) for the market \((X, p)\) is a random variable \(m\) on \(\Omega\) such that \[ p = \E[m\,x], \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad p_n = \sum_{s=1}^S \pi_s\, m_s\, X_{sn} \quad (n = 1, \dots, N). \]

By linearity of expectation, an SDF prices every portfolio: \(\E[m\,(X\theta)] = \E[m x^\top]\theta = p^\top\theta\). The name comes from the riskless payoff. If \(\mathbf 1 \in M\), then \(\E[m] = q(\mathbf 1) = 1/R_f\), and for a payoff whose value does not depend on the state, \(m\) discounts it at the riskless rate. For a risky payoff, \(m\) discounts more heavily in some states than in others.

2SDFs are state prices divided by probabilities

Proposition 2.1 (SDFs and state prices correspond one to one)

A vector \(\psi \in \R^S\) is a state-price vector if and only if \(m = \psi/\pi\) (entrywise, \(m_s = \psi_s/\pi_s\)) is an SDF. The map \(\psi \mapsto \psi/\pi\) is a bijection from state-price vectors to SDFs, and it preserves signs: \(\psi \gg 0\) if and only if \(m \gg 0\).

Proof

\(\E[m\,x_n] = \sum_s \pi_s m_s X_{sn} = \sum_s \psi_s X_{sn} = (X^\top\psi)_n\) when \(m_s = \psi_s/\pi_s\). So \(m\) is an SDF iff \(X^\top\psi = p\). The inverse map is \(\psi_s = \pi_s m_s\), and since every \(\pi_s > 0\), \(\psi_s\) and \(m_s\) have the same sign.

Theorem 2.2 (existence of an SDF)

For a market \((X, p)\) on a finite probability space with \(\pi \gg 0\):

  1. an SDF exists if and only if the law of one price holds;
  2. a strictly positive SDF exists if and only if the market has no arbitrage.
Proof

(i) is Lesson 1's Theorem 2.4 (law of one price ⇔ a state-price vector exists) combined with Proposition 2.1. (ii) is the fundamental theorem of Lesson 2 (no arbitrage ⇔ a strictly positive state-price vector exists) combined with the sign preservation of Proposition 2.1.

Nothing new has been proved: Theorem 2.2 restates Lessons 1 and 2 in the language of expectations. What the restatement buys is a geometry. With the inner product \(\langle y, z\rangle = \E[yz]\), the condition \(p_n = \E[m\,x_n]\) says that the price of a payoff is its inner product with \(m\). Pricing becomes projection.

3The SDF in the payoff space

Equip \(\R^S\) with \(\langle y, z\rangle_\pi = \E[yz] = \sum_s \pi_s y_s z_s\). It is an inner product: bilinear and symmetric, and positive definite because \(\E[z^2] = \sum_s \pi_s z_s^2 > 0\) for \(z \ne 0\) when every \(\pi_s > 0\). Its norm is \(\|z\| = \sqrt{\E[z^2]}\). The payoff space \(M = \operatorname{range}(X)\) is a subspace, and orthogonal projection onto it is defined with respect to this inner product. M6 constructs conditional expectation as exactly such a projection; here the subspace is the span of the payoffs rather than the functions of a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra.

Theorem 3.1 (Riesz representation on a finite-dimensional inner-product space)

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional real inner-product space and \(f : V \to \R\) linear. There is exactly one \(v^\ast \in V\) with \(f(v) = \langle v^\ast, v\rangle\) for every \(v \in V\).

Proof

Existence. Let \(e_1, \dots, e_k\) be an orthonormal basis of \(V\) (Gram–Schmidt applied to any basis). Set \(v^\ast = \sum_{j} f(e_j)\,e_j\). For \(v = \sum_j c_j e_j\), linearity of \(f\) and orthonormality give \(f(v) = \sum_j c_j f(e_j) = \langle \sum_j f(e_j) e_j, \sum_i c_i e_i \rangle = \langle v^\ast, v\rangle\). Uniqueness. If \(\langle v^\ast, v\rangle = \langle w^\ast, v\rangle\) for every \(v \in V\), take \(v = v^\ast - w^\ast\): \(\|v^\ast - w^\ast\|^2 = 0\), so \(v^\ast = w^\ast\).

Theorem 3.2 (existence and uniqueness of the SDF in the payoff space)

Let the market \((X, p)\) on a finite probability space with \(\pi \gg 0\) satisfy the law of one price, with pricing functional \(q\) on \(M\). Then:

  1. there is exactly one SDF in the payoff space, \(x^\ast \in M\) with \(p = \E[x^\ast x]\);
  2. if the payoffs are linearly independent (\(X\) has full column rank), \(\E[xx^\top]\) is invertible and \[ x^\ast = x^\top b, \qquad b = \E[xx^\top]^{-1}\, p. \]

This is the sense in which the SDF exists and is unique, the result this module calls the existence and uniqueness of the SDF: an SDF exists exactly under the law of one price; SDFs are unique only in a complete market; and in every market satisfying the law of one price, exactly one SDF is itself a traded payoff.

Proof

(i) \(M\) with \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_\pi\) is a finite-dimensional inner-product space and \(q : M \to \R\) is linear (Lesson 1, Theorem 2.4). By Theorem 3.1 there is exactly one \(x^\ast \in M\) with \(q(z) = \E[x^\ast z]\) for every \(z \in M\). Applied to the columns \(z = x_n \in M\), \(\E[x^\ast x_n] = q(x_n) = p_n\), so \(x^\ast\) is an SDF. Conversely, any SDF \(m \in M\) satisfies \(\E[m\,z] = q(z)\) for every \(z = X\theta \in M\), because \(\E[m\,X\theta] = p^\top\theta = q(X\theta)\); by the uniqueness in Theorem 3.1, \(m = x^\ast\).

(ii) For \(c \in \R^N\), \(c^\top \E[xx^\top] c = \E[(x^\top c)^2] = \sum_s \pi_s (Xc)_s^2\), which is positive for \(c \ne 0\) because \(Xc \ne 0\) by full column rank and \(\pi \gg 0\). So \(\E[xx^\top]\) is positive definite, hence invertible. Every element of \(M\) is \(x^\top b\) for some \(b\), and \(x^\top b\) is an SDF iff \(\E[x\,x^\top b] = \E[xx^\top] b = p\), iff \(b = \E[xx^\top]^{-1}p\).

For a textbook treatment, Cochrane's Section 4.1, "Law of One Price and Existence of a Discount Factor", treats the existence of a discount factor in the payoff space.

Corollary 3.3 (uniqueness of the SDF)

Under the law of one price, the SDF is unique if and only if \(M = \R^S\), that is, the market is complete, and then the unique SDF is \(x^\ast\).

Proof

By Proposition 2.1, SDFs are in bijection with state-price vectors, which are unique iff \(M = \R^S\) (Lesson 1, Proposition 2.6). If \(M = \R^S\), every SDF lies in \(M\), so by Theorem 3.2 it is \(x^\ast\).

4Every SDF projects onto \(x^\ast\)

Theorem 4.1 (the structure of the set of SDFs)

Under the law of one price, a random variable \(m\) is an SDF if and only if \(m = x^\ast + \varepsilon\) with \(\E[\varepsilon\, x_n] = 0\) for every \(n\), that is, \(\varepsilon \perp M\). Consequently \(x^\ast\) is the orthogonal projection of every SDF onto \(M\), and \[ \E[m^2] = \E[x^{\ast 2}] + \E[\varepsilon^2] \ge \E[x^{\ast 2}], \] with equality only for \(m = x^\ast\): \(x^\ast\) is the SDF with the smallest second moment.

Proof

If \(m\) is an SDF, then \(\E[(m - x^\ast)x_n] = p_n - p_n = 0\) for every \(n\), so \(\varepsilon = m - x^\ast \perp M\). Conversely, if \(\varepsilon \perp M\), then \(\E[(x^\ast + \varepsilon)x_n] = p_n + 0\). Since \(x^\ast \in M\) and \(m - x^\ast \perp M\), \(x^\ast\) is the orthogonal projection of \(m\) onto \(M\) (the projection is the unique point of \(M\) whose difference from \(m\) is orthogonal to \(M\)). Pythagoras gives \(\E[m^2] = \E[x^{\ast2}] + 2\E[x^\ast\varepsilon] + \E[\varepsilon^2] = \E[x^{\ast2}] + \E[\varepsilon^2]\), because \(x^\ast \in M\) and \(\varepsilon \perp M\). The inequality is an equality iff \(\E[\varepsilon^2] = 0\) iff \(\varepsilon = 0\), using \(\pi \gg 0\).

Example 4.2 (the bond-and-stock market with probabilities)

Lesson 1's three states with a bond \((1, 1, 1)\) at 0.96 and a stock \((1.4, 1.1, 0.6)\) at 0.976, and probabilities \(\pi = (0.25, 0.5, 0.25)\). The nonnegative state-price vectors form the segment from \((0.5, 0, 0.46)\) to \((0, 0.8, 0.16)\) (Lesson 3, Example 4.3), so the nonnegative SDFs \(\psi/\pi\) form the segment from \((2, 0, 1.84)\) to \((0, 1.6, 0.64)\). Exactly one of them is in \(M\): \(x^\ast\), at \(s = 0.587879\) along the segment (Lab 1). Theorem 3.2 gives \[ \E[xx^\top] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1.05 \\ 1.05 & 1.1850 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad b = \E[xx^\top]^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0.96\\0.976\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1.367273\\-0.387879\end{pmatrix}, \] so \(x^\ast = 1.367273 - 0.387879\,x_{\text{stock}} = (0.824242, 0.940606, 1.134545)\). Check: \(\E[x^\ast] = 0.25 \times 0.824242 + 0.5 \times 0.940606 + 0.25 \times 1.134545 = 0.96\), the bond's price. Here \(\E[x_{\text{stock}}] = 0.25 \times 1.4 + 0.5 \times 1.1 + 0.25 \times 0.6 = 1.05\) and \(\E[x_{\text{stock}}^2] = 0.25 \times 1.96 + 0.5 \times 1.21 + 0.25 \times 0.36 = 1.185\).

Lab 1 · The line of SDFs and its shortest point

Along the segment of nonnegative state prices in Example 4.2, the SDFs \(m = \psi/\pi\) should all price both assets, none of the five sampled points should lie in the payoff space (\(x^\ast\) lies between the \(s = 0.50\) and \(s = 0.75\) points), and their second moments should all exceed \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = 0.934012\). The lab also checks that \(x^\ast\) is strictly positive in this market, so that its state prices \(\pi x^\ast\) are a strictly positive state-price vector.

Lab 2 · Projecting a known SDF

A SIMULATED market of six states and three payoffs is priced by a lognormal SDF \(m\) the analyst does not see. Computing \(x^\ast = x^\top \E[xx^\top]^{-1}p\) from the prices alone should give an SDF that prices all three payoffs exactly, the residual \(m - x^\ast\) should be orthogonal to every payoff, the second moments should add up as in Theorem 4.1, and a second SDF built from \(x^\ast\) plus an orthogonal noise should price the payoffs too.

import numpy as np

# SIMULATED one-period market: 6 states, 3 traded payoffs, priced by a strictly positive SDF m.
rng = np.random.default_rng(505)
probs = rng.dirichlet(np.full(6, 4.0))
m = np.exp(rng.normal(-0.03, 0.25, 6))  # the SDF that generated the prices (unknown to the analyst)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(6), 1.0 + 0.2 * rng.standard_normal(6), 1.0 + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal(6)])
p = X.T @ (probs * m)  # p = E[m x], one price per payoff
print("state probabilities:", np.round(probs, 4))
print("prices p = E[m x]:  ", np.round(p, 6))

# x* = x' b with E[x x'] b = p: the unique SDF in the payoff space (Theorem 3.2).
second = X.T @ (probs[:, None] * X)  # E[x x'] under the state probabilities
b = np.linalg.solve(second, p)
x_star = X @ b
print("x* by state:        ", np.round(x_star, 6))
print("E[x* x] - p:        ", np.round(X.T @ (probs * x_star) - p, 12) + 0.0)

# m - x* is orthogonal to every payoff, so x* is the projection of m on the payoff space (Theorem 4.1).
eps = m - x_star
print("E[(m - x*) x]:      ", np.round(X.T @ (probs * eps), 12) + 0.0)
print(f"E[m^2] = {probs @ m**2:.6f} = E[x*^2] + E[eps^2] = {probs @ x_star**2:.6f} + {probs @ eps**2:.6f}")

# Any other SDF is x* plus a noise orthogonal to the payoffs; build one from a random vector.
z = rng.standard_normal(6)
coef = np.linalg.solve(second, X.T @ (probs * z))
other = x_star + (z - X @ coef)
print(
    "another SDF prices the payoffs:",
    bool(np.allclose(X.T @ (probs * other), p)),
    "| min value:",
    round(float(other.min()), 4),
)

The prices pin down \(x^\ast\) and nothing else: \(\E[m^2] = 1.133251\) splits into \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = 1.004012\), which the prices reveal, and \(\E[\varepsilon^2] = 0.129239\), which they cannot. Recovering \(x^\ast\) is therefore all that price data can do without a model: any claim about \(m\) beyond its projection is a claim about the unpriced component \(\varepsilon\).

5Positivity: \(x^\ast\) need not be an arbitrage-free pricer

Theorem 2.2 says a strictly positive SDF exists under no arbitrage. It does not say \(x^\ast\) is one. Projection onto \(M\) does not preserve positivity, and \(x^\ast\) can be negative in some state of a market that has no arbitrage.

Example 5.1 (a negative \(x^\ast\) in an arbitrage-free market)

Three states with \(\pi = (0.25, 0.5, 0.25)\), and one traded payoff \(z = (3, 1, -1)\), priced by the strictly positive SDF \(m = (1.2, 0.9, 0.8)\) at \(\E[mz] = 0.25 \times 3.6 + 0.5 \times 0.9 - 0.25 \times 0.8 = 1.15\). The market has no arbitrage (Theorem 2.2), because \(m \gg 0\) prices it. Its payoff space is the line through \(z\), and \(x^\ast = cz\) with \(\E[cz\cdot z] = 1.15\): \(c = 1.15/\E[z^2] = 1.15/3 = 0.383333\), so \[ x^\ast = (1.15,\; 0.383333,\; -0.383333). \] \(x^\ast\) is negative in state 3. As a pricing rule it would assign the payoff \(e_3\), which is not traded, the negative value \(\E[x^\ast e_3] = 0.25 \times (-0.383333) = -0.095833\). That is an arbitrage price for a payoff the market does not trade, so \(x^\ast\) cannot be used to extend prices to new payoffs.

Proposition 5.2 (when \(x^\ast\) is positive)

If the market has no arbitrage and is complete (\(M = \R^S\)), then \(x^\ast \gg 0\). In an incomplete market without arbitrage, \(x^\ast \gg 0\) holds if and only if the strictly positive SDFs include the one in \(M\), and this can fail (Example 5.1).

Proof

In a complete market the SDF is unique and equals \(x^\ast\) (Corollary 3.3); under no arbitrage some SDF is strictly positive (Theorem 2.2), so \(x^\ast\) is. The second statement restates the definitions: \(x^\ast\) is the unique SDF in \(M\), so it is strictly positive iff some strictly positive SDF lies in \(M\).

Lab 3 · Positive SDFs with the smallest second moment

In Example 5.1, minimizing \(\E[m^2]\) over SDFs with \(m \ge \text{floor}\) gives \(m = \max(\lambda z, \text{floor})\) for the \(\lambda\) at which \(\E[mz] = 1.15\): minimizing \(\E[m^2] - 2\lambda(\E[mz] - 1.15)\) state by state over \(m_s \ge \text{floor}\) gives \(m_s = \max(\lambda z_s, \text{floor})\). The lab finds \(\lambda\) by bisection. With a floor of zero the minimizer should set state 3 to zero and have a second moment above \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = 0.440833\); raising the floor should raise it further.

import numpy as np

# Example 5.1: three states, one traded payoff z that is negative in state 3.
probs = np.array([0.25, 0.5, 0.25])
m_true = np.array([1.2, 0.9, 0.8])  # a strictly positive SDF: the market is free of arbitrage
z = np.array([3.0, 1.0, -1.0])
price = float(probs @ (m_true * z))
x_star = z * price / float(probs @ z**2)  # projection of m_true on span{z}
print(f"price of z: {price:.4f}")
print("x* by state:", np.round(x_star, 6), "| x* prices z:", round(float(probs @ (x_star * z)), 6))
print(f"E[x*^2] = {probs @ x_star**2:.6f} <= E[m^2] = {probs @ m_true**2:.6f}")

# The SDF with the least second moment among those with m >= floor that price z is m = max(lam * z, floor),
# with lam set so that E[m z] = price (pointwise minimization of m^2 - 2 lam m z over m >= floor).
for floor in (0.0, 0.1, 0.5):
    lam_lo, lam_hi = 0.0, 10.0
    for _ in range(200):
        lam = 0.5 * (lam_lo + lam_hi)
        if probs @ (np.maximum(lam * z, floor) * z) < price:
            lam_lo = lam
        else:
            lam_hi = lam
    m_pos = np.maximum(lam * z, floor)
    print(
        f"floor {floor:.1f}: m = {np.round(m_pos, 4)}, prices z: {round(float(probs @ (m_pos * z)), 6)}, "
        f"E[m^2] = {probs @ m_pos**2:.4f}"
    )

Requiring the SDF to be nonnegative costs second moment: 0.4809 against 0.440833 with a floor of zero, and more with higher floors. The nonnegative minimizer is not in the payoff space, so data on \(z\) alone cannot recover it by projection. Lesson 5's variance bound inherits this: the bound computed from \(x^\ast\) ignores positivity and is weaker than the bound for positive SDFs, which is a convex program rather than a projection. The lab computes the minimizer for one market; it does not show that the floor-zero minimizer is the economically relevant SDF, which needs a model of who holds the payoffs.

6The sample \(x^\ast\)

With data, each of \(T\) observed periods is treated as one state of equal probability \(1/T\). The sample second-moment matrix is \(\E_T[xx^\top] = \tfrac1T\sum_t x_t x_t^\top\), and the sample \(x^\ast\) has weights \(b_T = \E_T[xx^\top]^{-1}p\). By Theorem 3.2, applied to the sample market, \(x^\ast_t = x_t^\top b_T\) prices every payoff exactly in sample: \(\E_T[x^\ast x] = p\). By Problem P3, \(b_T\) is the coefficient vector of the no-intercept least-squares regression of any sample SDF on the payoffs, because the normal equations \(\E_T[xx^\top]b = \E_T[x\,m]\) need only the cross-moments, which are the prices. Lesson 7 asks how well the sample \(x^\ast\) prices payoffs in periods it was not fitted to.

Lab 4 · The sample \(x^\ast\) of nine currencies

The payoffs are Lesson 1's one-month currency payoffs without DKK: nine payoffs, each costing one dollar, so \(p = \mathbf 1\). The data are month-end spot rates from the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, G.5/H.10 Foreign Exchange Rates (noon buying rates in New York), retrieved 2026-10-19. Treating each month as an equally likely state, the sample \(x^\ast\) should price all nine payoffs exactly in sample, to rounding error, and its weights should be large and of both signs, because the payoffs are strongly correlated.

# The sample x* of nine one-month currency payoffs, each costing one dollar (DKK left out, Lesson 1).
with (Path("data") / "fx_monthly.csv").open(newline="", encoding="utf-8") as handle:
    rows = list(csv.reader(handle))
currencies = rows[0][1:]
prices = np.array([[float(v) if v else math.nan for v in row[1:]] for row in rows[1:]])
start = int(np.argmax(~np.isnan(prices).any(axis=1)))
keep = [i for i, c in enumerate(currencies) if c != "DKK"]
x = prices[start + 1 :, keep] / prices[start:-1, keep]  # (T, 9) payoffs, each with price 1
n_obs = x.shape[0]
b = np.linalg.solve(x.T @ x / n_obs, np.ones(x.shape[1]))
m = x @ b
print(f"T = {n_obs}; E_T[x* x] - 1 for every currency: max abs {np.abs(x.T @ m / n_obs - 1).max():.1e}")
print(f"x*: mean {m.mean():.4f}, s.d. {m.std():.4f}, min {m.min():.4f}, max {m.max():.4f}")
print("weights b:", np.round(b, 2))

The pricing errors are of the order of \(10^{-14}\): Theorem 3.2 holds in the sample market by construction, so this is a check of the arithmetic, not evidence about the economy. The sample \(x^\ast\) ranges from 0.2078 to 1.5187, positive in every month of this sample, and its mean, 0.9996, is close to one (Problem E2). Weights of \(-7.75\) on CHF and over 2 on several others show what inverting a nearly singular \(\E_T[xx^\top]\) does. None of these payoffs includes the interest a currency earns on deposit, so this \(x^\ast\) prices currency held as cash. Whether it prices the same payoffs in other months is Lesson 7's question.

7Where the canon breaks

  • Infinitely many states. Theorem 3.1 uses a finite-dimensional space. With a general probability space the payoff space sits in \(L^2\), and the Riesz theorem there needs the pricing functional to be continuous in the \(L^2\) norm. With finitely many traded payoffs \(M\) is finite-dimensional and every linear functional on it is continuous, so Theorem 3.2 survives; with infinitely many payoffs (options at every strike, dynamic trading), continuity is an assumption that has to be made, and the SDF can fail to exist in \(L^2\) even when prices satisfy the law of one price.
  • \(x^\ast\) is a statistical object, not marginal utility. Equilibrium models say that the SDF is a marginal rate of substitution. Theorem 4.1 says that prices identify only its projection onto traded payoffs. A consumption-based SDF can price every traded asset and still differ from \(x^\ast\) by a large orthogonal component, and AP3 tests such models through what they imply for \(x^\ast\)'s moments, not by comparing them with \(x^\ast\) directly.
  • Negative \(x^\ast\). Example 5.1 shows that the projection can be negative. Using \(x^\ast\) to price a new, unspanned payoff (an option, say) can then produce an arbitrage price. Pricing new payoffs needs a positive SDF, and Lesson 3's bounds are the honest answer when the market does not select one.
  • Conditioning information. Everything here is one period with no information at date 0. With information, \(p_t = \E_t[m_{t+1}x_{t+1}]\) holds conditionally, and the unconditional projection of this lesson is one of many projections, one for each set of managed portfolios formed from the information. Lesson 6 takes the multiperiod setting; AP3 and S5 take the conditional estimation.

8Misconceptions

  1. “The stochastic discount factor is unique.”

    It is unique only in a complete market (Corollary 3.3). In general the SDFs form \(x^\ast + M^\perp\), and the prices identify only \(x^\ast\) (Theorem 4.1; Lab 2's second SDF prices everything the true one does).

  2. “\(x^\ast\) is positive whenever the market has no arbitrage.”

    No arbitrage guarantees some strictly positive SDF, not that the one in the payoff space is positive. Example 5.1 has no arbitrage and \(x^\ast = (1.15, 0.383333, -0.383333)\).

  3. “An SDF needs a model of preferences.”

    Theorem 2.2 derives its existence from the law of one price alone, and \(x^\ast\) is computed from prices and payoffs by \(b = \E[xx^\top]^{-1}p\). Preferences are needed to say which SDF is the marginal rate of substitution, not that an SDF exists.

  4. “The SDF's mean is one.”

    When a riskless payoff is traded, \(\E[m] = 1/R_f\) (Section 1), which is 0.96 in Example 4.2. The mean equals one only when the riskless rate is zero.

9Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · An SDF from state prices

Two states with probabilities \((0.6, 0.4)\) and state prices \((0.448, 0.522)\) (Lesson 1, Problem M2). Find the SDF, its mean and the riskless rate, and check that it prices the stock \((1.3, 0.8)\) at 1.00.

Solution

\(m = (0.448/0.6, 0.522/0.4) = (0.746667, 1.305)\). \(\E[m] = 0.6 \times 0.746667 + 0.4 \times 1.305 = 0.448 + 0.522 = 0.97 = 1/R_f\), so \(R_f = 1.030928\). \(\E[m\,x] = 0.6 \times 0.746667 \times 1.3 + 0.4 \times 1.305 \times 0.8 = 0.5824 + 0.4176 = 1.00\). The market is complete (two states, two independent payoffs), so this is the only SDF and it equals \(x^\ast\).

M2 · \(x^\ast\) for one payoff

Three equally likely states and one traded payoff \(z = (2, 1, 0)\) at price 0.9. Find \(x^\ast\) and \(\E[x^{\ast2}]\).

Solution

\(x^\ast = cz\) with \(c\,\E[z^2] = 0.9\). \(\E[z^2] = (4 + 1 + 0)/3 = 5/3\), so \(c = 0.54\) and \(x^\ast = (1.08, 0.54, 0)\). \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = c^2\E[z^2] = 0.2916 \times 5/3 = 0.486\). Equivalently \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = \E[x^\ast z]\,c = 0.9 \times 0.54 = 0.486\). The SDF is zero in state 3, where \(z\) pays nothing: the projection puts no weight on a state the payoff space cannot see.

M3 · Verify Example 4.2

Verify that \(x^\ast = (0.824242, 0.940606, 1.134545)\) of Example 4.2 prices the stock at 0.976, and compute \(\E[x^{\ast 2}]\).

Solution

\(\E[x^\ast x_{\text{stock}}] = 0.25 \times 0.824242 \times 1.4 + 0.5 \times 0.940606 \times 1.1 + 0.25 \times 1.134545 \times 0.6 = 0.288485 + 0.517333 + 0.170182 = 0.976\). \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = 0.25 \times 0.679375 + 0.5 \times 0.884740 + 0.25 \times 1.287193 = 0.169844 + 0.442370 + 0.321798 = 0.934012\), as Lab 1 prints. Also \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = b^\top p = 1.367273 \times 0.96 - 0.387879 \times 0.976 = 1.312582 - 0.378570 = 0.934012\), because \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = \E[x^\ast x^\top] b = p^\top b\).

Proof

P1 · \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = p^\top \E[xx^\top]^{-1} p\)

With linearly independent payoffs, prove \(\E[x^{\ast 2}] = p^\top\E[xx^\top]^{-1}p\), and show that this is the price of \(x^\ast\).

Solution

\(x^\ast = x^\top b\) with \(b = \E[xx^\top]^{-1}p\), so \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = b^\top \E[xx^\top] b = p^\top \E[xx^\top]^{-1}\E[xx^\top]\E[xx^\top]^{-1}p = p^\top\E[xx^\top]^{-1}p\), using symmetry of \(\E[xx^\top]\). The price of the payoff \(x^\ast \in M\) is \(q(x^\ast) = \E[x^\ast x^\ast]\), by the pricing property of \(x^\ast\) applied to itself. So \(x^\ast\)'s price equals its second moment, which is positive unless all prices are zero. Lesson 5 normalizes \(x^\ast\) by this price to get the return \(R^\ast\).

P2 · \(x^\ast\) does not depend on how the payoffs are packaged

Let \(A\) be an invertible \(N \times N\) matrix, and replace the assets by the portfolios \(\tilde x = A^\top x\) with prices \(\tilde p = A^\top p\). Prove that \(x^\ast\) is unchanged.

Solution

The payoff space is unchanged: \(\tilde x^\top \tilde\theta = x^\top A\tilde\theta\), and \(\tilde\theta \mapsto A\tilde\theta\) is a bijection of \(\R^N\). The prices of portfolios are unchanged too: \(\tilde p^\top\tilde\theta = p^\top A\tilde\theta\). So the pricing functional \(q\) on \(M\) is the same, and \(x^\ast\), its unique representative in \(M\) (Theorem 3.2), is the same. By formula: \(\tilde b = \E[\tilde x\tilde x^\top]^{-1}\tilde p = (A^\top\E[xx^\top]A)^{-1}A^\top p = A^{-1}\E[xx^\top]^{-1}p = A^{-1}b\), and \(\tilde x^\top\tilde b = x^\top A A^{-1}b = x^\top b\). The project's autograder checks exactly this.

P3 · \(x^\ast\) as a regression

Suppose \(m\) is any SDF. Show that \(b = \E[xx^\top]^{-1}p\) is the coefficient vector of the no-intercept least-squares regression of \(m\) on \(x\), and interpret.

Solution

The no-intercept regression of \(m\) on \(x\) minimizes \(\E[(m - x^\top \beta)^2]\); its normal equations are \(\E[x x^\top]\beta = \E[x\,m]\). Since \(m\) is an SDF, \(\E[xm] = p\), so \(\beta = \E[xx^\top]^{-1}p = b\). The fitted value \(x^\top b = x^\ast\) is the projection of \(m\) on \(M\), as Theorem 4.1 says. The interpretation: you can regress an SDF you do not observe on the payoffs, because the regression needs only the cross-moments \(\E[xm]\), and those are the prices.

Empirical

E1 · How far is the true SDF from \(x^\ast\)?

In Lab 2, what fraction of the true SDF's second moment do the prices reveal? Change the SDF's volatility from 0.25 to 0.5 and predict the effect.

Solution

\(\E[x^{\ast2}]/\E[m^2] = 1.004012/1.133251 = 0.886\). The rest, 11.4%, is \(\E[\varepsilon^2]\), orthogonal to the three payoffs. With six states and three payoffs, \(M^\perp\) has dimension 3. Doubling the log-volatility of \(m\) increases its variation in every direction, including the three unpriced ones, so \(\E[\varepsilon^2]\) grows; \(\E[x^{\ast2}]\) grows too, by the part of the extra variation that lies in \(M\). With payoffs that load weakly on the SDF, as here, the revealed fraction falls. Change rng.normal(-0.03, 0.25, 6) to rng.normal(-0.03, 0.5, 6) to see the size for this draw.

E2 · Reading the currency \(x^\ast\)

Lab 4's sample \(x^\ast\) has mean 0.9996. Explain why the mean is close to one, and say what it would be if a one-month deposit at a riskless dollar rate of 0.3% for the month had been one of the payoffs.

Solution

If the constant payoff \(\mathbf 1\) were in the sample payoff space, \(\E_T[x^\ast] = q(\mathbf 1)\), the price of a sure dollar. It is not, but each currency payoff has a sample mean close to one and price one, so the constant is nearly spanned: a portfolio of the currencies has a payoff close to one in every month, and pricing it gives a mean of \(x^\ast\) close to its price, about one. With a riskless deposit paying \(1.003\) for a price of one, the constant is spanned exactly, and every SDF that prices the deposit, \(x^\ast\) included, has \(\E_T[m] \times 1.003 = 1\), so \(\E_T[x^\ast] = 1/1.003 = 0.997009\). Adding a payoff can only enlarge \(M\) and so can change \(x^\ast\); here it pins its mean.

E3 · When does \(x^\ast\) turn negative?

In Example 5.1, replace \(z\) by \(z_k = (3, 1, -k)\) for \(k \ge 0\), with the price still given by \(m = (1.2, 0.9, 0.8)\). For which \(k\) is \(x^\ast\) negative in some state?

Solution

\(x^\ast = c\,z_k\) with \(c = \E[m z_k]/\E[z_k^2]\). The price is \(\E[mz_k] = 0.25 \times 3.6 + 0.5 \times 0.9 - 0.25 \times 0.8k = 1.35 - 0.2k\), and \(\E[z_k^2] = 0.25 \times 9 + 0.5 + 0.25k^2 > 0\). If \(0 < k < 6.75\), the price is positive, \(c > 0\), and \(x^\ast\) has the sign of \(z_k\): negative in state 3. At \(k = 0\), \(z_0 \ge 0\) and \(x^\ast \ge 0\), zero in state 3. At \(k = 6.75\) the price is zero and \(x^\ast = 0\). For \(k > 6.75\), \(c < 0\) and \(x^\ast\) is negative in states 1 and 2. So \(x^\ast\) has a negative entry for every \(k > 0\) except \(k = 6.75\): a single payoff of mixed sign produces a projection of mixed sign unless its price is zero.

Research

R1 · What would identify the SDF?

An economist claims to have estimated "the" SDF from the prices of 25 portfolios over 600 months. What has been estimated, and what additional assumption would make it the SDF of an economic model?

Solution

From prices alone, at most \(x^\ast\) for the 25 payoffs, the projection of every SDF onto their span (Theorem 4.1), estimated with sampling error. Calling it "the" SDF requires either completeness (the 25 payoffs span every relevant state, Corollary 3.3), which no finite set of portfolios does for a real economy, or a model that restricts the SDF to a known space, for example \(m = a + h^\top f\) for some observed factors \(f\). The second turns the economist's estimate into a test: does the model's SDF, projected onto the payoffs, equal \(x^\ast\)? S5 and AP3 build those tests.

R2 · A positive SDF for pricing a new payoff

You must quote a payoff not spanned by traded assets in a market where \(x^\ast\) has negative values. Propose a rule that picks one SDF, guarantees the quote is arbitrage-free, and is computable by convex optimization.

Solution

Choose \(m\) to minimize a strictly convex criterion over strictly positive SDFs, for example \(\E[m^2]\) subject to \(\E[mx] = p\) and \(m \ge \delta\) for a small \(\delta > 0\) (Lab 3's problem), or the relative entropy \(\E[m\log m]\) subject to \(\E[mx] = p\), whose solution is automatically positive. Any SDF with \(m \gg 0\) gives a quote \(\E[mz]\) strictly inside Lesson 3's arbitrage interval (Lesson 2, Proposition 4.1). The choice of criterion is a modelling decision with consequences, so a quote should state the criterion and report the arbitrage interval next to it, which shows how much of the quote is model.

R3 · Continuity in infinite dimensions

Give an example, on a countable probability space, of a payoff space and a linear pricing functional for which no SDF exists in \(L^2\), and say which hypothesis of Theorem 3.1 fails.

Solution

Let \(\Omega = \{1, 2, \dots\}\) with \(\pi_s = 2^{-s}\). Let \(M\) be the payoffs that are nonzero in only finitely many states (an infinite-dimensional space, since one Arrow payoff \(e_s\) per state is traded), and let each \(e_s\) have price 1, so \(q(z) = \sum_s z_s\). An SDF would need \(\E[m e_s] = \pi_s m_s = 1\), so \(m_s = 2^s\), and \(\E[m^2] = \sum_s 2^{-s}4^s = \sum_s 2^s = \infty\): no SDF in \(L^2\). The functional \(q\) is linear but not continuous in the \(L^2\) norm: the payoffs \(e_s/\sqrt{\pi_s}\) have norm 1 and price \(2^{s/2} \to \infty\). Theorem 3.1 needs finite dimension, or, in a Hilbert space, continuity of the functional and a closed subspace; both fail here.
