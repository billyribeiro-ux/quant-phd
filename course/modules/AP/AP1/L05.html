The Minimum-Variance SDF and the Mean-Variance Frontier

AP2 built the mean-variance frontier from portfolio weights and a covariance matrix. This lesson builds it again from the stochastic discount factor and finds that the two are the same object seen from two sides. The SDF with the least volatility for a given mean is a portfolio on the frontier, and its volatility is the largest Sharpe ratio any portfolio can reach. So any model of the SDF, consumption-based or factor-based, must be at least as volatile as the best Sharpe ratio in the market. AP3 uses this bound to judge consumption models. The project estimates it from data.

1Returns, \(R^\ast\) and the riskless rate

Keep the market of Lesson 4: finitely many states with probabilities \(\pi \gg 0\), payoffs \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_N)\) with prices \(p\), the law of one price, and \(x^\ast \in M\) the unique SDF in the payoff space, with inner product \(\E[yz]\). A return is a payoff with price 1, and an excess return is a payoff with price 0. If \(R\) and \(R'\) are returns, \(R - R'\) is an excess return.

Definition 1.1 (\(R^\ast\))

If \(x^\ast \ne 0\), let \(R^\ast = x^\ast / q(x^\ast) = x^\ast / \E[x^{\ast 2}]\), the return on the payoff \(x^\ast\). (The price of \(x^\ast\) is \(\E[x^\ast x^\ast]\), by its own pricing property.)

Proposition 1.2 (the riskless rate)

If the riskless payoff \(\mathbf 1\) is in \(M\) with price \(q(\mathbf 1) > 0\), then every SDF has \(\E[m] = 1/R_f\) with \(R_f = 1/q(\mathbf 1)\), and in particular \(\E[x^\ast] = 1/R_f\).

Proof

Every SDF prices the payoff \(\mathbf 1 \in M\): \(\E[m \cdot 1] = q(\mathbf 1) = 1/R_f\).

Theorem 1.3 (\(R^\ast\) has the smallest second moment of all returns)

If \(x^\ast \ne 0\), then for every return \(R \in M\), \(\E[R^2] \ge \E[R^{\ast2}] = 1/\E[x^{\ast2}]\), with equality only for \(R = R^\ast\). Moreover \(\E[R^\ast R] = \E[R^{\ast 2}]\) for every return \(R \in M\).

Proof

For a return \(R \in M\), \(\E[x^\ast R] = q(R) = 1\), so \(\E[R^\ast R] = \E[x^\ast R]/\E[x^{\ast2}] = 1/\E[x^{\ast2}]\), the same for every return, including \(R^\ast\) itself: \(\E[R^{\ast2}] = 1/\E[x^{\ast2}]\). Then \(\E[(R - R^\ast)R^\ast] = 0\), and Pythagoras gives \(\E[R^2] = \E[R^{\ast2}] + \E[(R - R^\ast)^2] \ge \E[R^{\ast2}]\), with equality iff \(R = R^\ast\).

2The minimum-variance SDF with a given mean

Lesson 4 showed that \(x^\ast\) minimizes \(\E[m^2]\). Asking instead for the least variance among SDFs with a given mean \(v\) brings the constant into play: the candidates are SDFs in \(\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf 1, x\}\), whether or not \(\mathbf 1\) is traded. Write \(\mu = \E[x]\) and \(\Sigma = \Cov(x)\), the covariance matrix of the payoffs.

Theorem 2.1 (the minimum-variance SDF with mean \(v\))

Assume \(\Sigma\) is invertible (no nonzero portfolio has a constant payoff; in particular no riskless payoff is traded). For each \(v \in \R\), among all random variables \(m\) with \(\E[m] = v\) and \(\E[mx] = p\), exactly one minimizes \(\Var(m)\), namely \[ m_v = v + (x - \mu)^\top b_v, \qquad b_v = \Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu), \] and its variance is \(\sigma^2_{\min}(v) = (p - v\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)\).

Proof

\(m_v\) is feasible. \(\E[m_v] = v + 0 = v\), and \(\E[m_v x] = v\mu + \E[x(x - \mu)^\top]b_v = v\mu + \Sigma b_v = v\mu + p - v\mu = p\), using \(\E[x(x-\mu)^\top] = \Cov(x) = \Sigma\).

It has the least variance. Let \(m\) be feasible and \(\varepsilon = m - m_v\). Then \(\E[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\E[\varepsilon x] = 0\), so \(\Cov(\varepsilon, x_n) = \E[\varepsilon x_n] - \E[\varepsilon]\E[x_n] = 0\) for every \(n\). \(m_v - v\) is a linear combination of the \(x_n - \mu_n\), so \(\Cov(\varepsilon, m_v) = 0\), and \(\Var(m) = \Var(m_v) + \Var(\varepsilon) \ge \Var(m_v)\). Equality forces \(\Var(\varepsilon) = 0\), so \(\varepsilon\) is a constant, and that constant is \(\E[\varepsilon] = 0\).

Its variance. \(\Var(m_v) = b_v^\top\Sigma b_v = (p - v\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)\).

The SDF \(m_v\) is the minimum-variance SDF with mean \(v\): an affine function of the payoffs, so it lives in \(\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf 1, x\}\), the payoff space enlarged by the constant. When a riskless payoff is traded, the constant is already in \(M\), the mean is pinned at \(1/R_f\) (Proposition 1.2), and the minimum-variance SDF is \(x^\ast\) itself.

Proposition 2.2 (with a riskless payoff, \(x^\ast\) is the minimum-variance SDF)

If \(\mathbf 1 \in M\), then every SDF has mean \(1/R_f\), and \(x^\ast\) has the least variance among all SDFs.

Proof

All SDFs have the same mean (Proposition 1.2), so minimizing \(\Var(m) = \E[m^2] - 1/R_f^2\) is minimizing \(\E[m^2]\), and Lesson 4's Theorem 4.1 says \(x^\ast\) does that.

Proposition 2.3 (\(x^\ast\) is a minimum-variance SDF)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 2.1, \(x^\ast = m_{v^\ast}\) with \(v^\ast = \E[x^\ast]\): \(x^\ast\) is the minimum-variance SDF at its own mean.

Proof

\(x^\ast\) is feasible for \(v = v^\ast\). Every feasible \(m\) with mean \(v^\ast\) is an SDF, so \(\E[m^2] \ge \E[x^{\ast2}]\) by Lesson 4's Theorem 4.1, and subtracting \(v^{\ast2}\) from both sides gives \(\Var(m) \ge \Var(x^\ast)\). So \(x^\ast\) minimizes variance at mean \(v^\ast\), and by the uniqueness in Theorem 2.1, \(x^\ast = m_{v^\ast}\).

Theorem 2.4 (the variance bound)

Let \(m\) be any SDF with \(\E[m] = v \ne 0\), and let \(R^e\) be any excess return in \(M\) with \(\Var(R^e) > 0\). Then \[ \frac{\sigma(m)}{|\E[m]|} \;\ge\; \frac{|\E[R^e]|}{\sigma(R^e)}. \] If a riskless payoff is traded, \(v = 1/R_f\), and the right-hand side is the absolute Sharpe ratio of any return \(R\) in excess of \(R_f\): \(\sigma(m)/\E[m] \ge |\E[R] - R_f|/\sigma(R)\).

Proof

\(R^e\) has price 0, so \(0 = \E[mR^e] = \E[m]\E[R^e] + \Cov(m, R^e)\). Hence \(|\E[m]|\,|\E[R^e]| = |\Cov(m, R^e)| \le \sigma(m)\sigma(R^e)\) by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for covariances. Divide by \(|\E[m]|\,\sigma(R^e) > 0\). With a riskless payoff, \(R^e = R - R_f\) has price \(1 - R_f\E[m] = 0\), and \(\sigma(R^e) = \sigma(R)\).

Corollary 2.5 (the bound is attained)

With a riskless payoff and \(\Var(x^\ast) > 0\), the largest Sharpe ratio of any return in \(M\) equals \(\sigma(x^\ast)/\E[x^\ast]\), and every SDF has \(\sigma(m)/\E[m] \ge \sigma(x^\ast)/\E[x^\ast]\).

Proof

The second claim is Proposition 2.2, since all SDFs share the mean \(1/R_f\). For the first, Theorem 2.4 with \(m = x^\ast\) bounds every Sharpe ratio by \(\sigma(x^\ast)/\E[x^\ast]\), and the Cauchy–Schwarz step in its proof is an equality when \(R^e\) is an affine function of \(x^\ast\) with nonzero slope. Such an excess return exists: \(x^\ast \in M\) has price \(\E[x^{\ast2}]\), the riskless payoff \(\E[x^{\ast2}]R_f\,\mathbf 1 \in M\) has the same price, so \(R^e = x^\ast - \E[x^{\ast2}]R_f\) has price zero. Its mean is \(\E[x^\ast] - \E[x^{\ast2}]R_f = R_f(\E[x^\ast]^2 - \E[x^{\ast2}]) = -R_f\Var(x^\ast)\), using \(\E[x^\ast] = 1/R_f\), and its standard deviation is \(\sigma(x^\ast)\). So \(|\E[R^e]|/\sigma(R^e) = R_f\,\sigma(x^\ast) = \sigma(x^\ast)/\E[x^\ast]\).

Theorem 2.1 and Theorem 2.4, both proved above, are the mean–standard deviation duality between SDFs and returns that characterize; their paper also imposes that marginal rates of substitution are positive, which this lesson does not. Theorem 2.1 and Theorem 2.4 are that duality without the positivity restriction: \(\sigma_{\min}(v)\) traces the lowest volatility an SDF with mean \(v\) can have, and it equals \(|v|\) times the largest Sharpe ratio of excess returns in \(M\) when the riskless rate is \(1/v\) (Problem P2). AP3 names this the Hansen–Jagannathan bound and confronts consumption-based SDFs with it; the version that imposes \(m \ge 0\) is a convex program, not a projection (Lesson 4, Lab 3).

Lab 1 · The SDF frontier and the Sharpe ratio

A SIMULATED market of ten states and four risky payoffs, with no riskless payoff, is priced by a lognormal SDF with mean 0.97. For each candidate mean \(v\), \(\sigma_{\min}(v)/v\) from Theorem 2.1 should equal the largest Sharpe ratio of a zero-price payoff formed with a hypothetical riskless asset priced at \(v\), found here by checking the minimum-variance direction against 2,000 random portfolios. The true SDF's volatility should exceed \(\sigma_{\min}\) at its mean.

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED market without a riskless payoff: 10 states, 4 risky payoffs priced by a strictly positive SDF.
rng = np.random.default_rng(653)
probs = rng.dirichlet(np.full(10, 5.0))
m_true = np.exp(rng.normal(0.0, 0.35, 10))
m_true *= 0.97 / float(probs @ m_true)
X = 1.0 + 0.3 * rng.standard_normal((10, 4))
p = X.T @ (probs * m_true)
mu = X.T @ probs  # E[x]
cov = X.T @ (probs[:, None] * X) - np.outer(mu, mu)  # Cov(x)


def sigma_min(v: float) -> float:
    """Smallest standard deviation of an SDF with mean v that prices x (Theorem 2.1)."""
    g = p - v * mu
    return float(np.sqrt(g @ np.linalg.solve(cov, g)))


def max_sharpe(v: float) -> float:
    """Largest Sharpe ratio of a zero-price payoff x'theta - (p'theta / v) * 1, if a riskless asset cost v."""
    best = 0.0
    for theta in np.vstack([np.linalg.solve(cov, p - v * mu), rng.standard_normal((2000, 4))]):
        y = X @ theta - (p @ theta) / v
        sd = float(np.sqrt(probs @ (y - probs @ y) ** 2))
        best = max(best, abs(float(probs @ y)) / sd)
    return best


def sd(z: NDArray[np.float64]) -> float:
    return float(np.sqrt(probs @ (z - probs @ z) ** 2))


print("  v      sigma_min(v)  sigma_min(v)/v  max Sharpe at R_f = 1/v")
for v in (0.90, 0.95, 0.97, 1.00, 1.05):
    print(f"{v:5.2f}   {sigma_min(v):.6f}      {sigma_min(v) / v:.6f}        {max_sharpe(v):.6f}")
print(f"true SDF: mean {probs @ m_true:.6f}, s.d. {sd(m_true):.6f} >= sigma_min at its mean {sigma_min(0.97):.6f}")
v_star = float(mu @ np.linalg.solve(cov, p) / (mu @ np.linalg.solve(cov, mu)))
print(f"the mean with the smallest bound: v = {v_star:.6f}, sigma_min = {sigma_min(v_star):.6f}")
  v      sigma_min(v)  sigma_min(v)/v  max Sharpe at R_f = 1/v

 0.95   0.164195      0.172837        0.172837
 0.97   0.174605      0.180006        0.180006
 1.00   0.411455      0.411455        0.411455
 1.05   0.869880      0.828457        0.828457

The two columns agree at every \(v\): none of the 2,000 random portfolios beats the Sharpe ratio of the portfolio along \(\Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)\), and that ratio is \(\sigma_{\min}(v)/v\). The bound is a curve in \(v\), not a number: without a riskless payoff the data do not pin the SDF's mean, and \(\sigma_{\min}\) is smallest, 0.140481, at \(v = 0.959009\). The true SDF has a volatility of 0.422254 at mean 0.97, more than twice the bound there: it prices the four payoffs correctly but is far from the least volatile SDF that does. A model SDF below the curve is rejected by these prices; one above it is not thereby confirmed.

3The frontier in SDF terms

AP2 characterized the mean-variance frontier by optimizing over portfolio weights. In SDF terms it has a two-line description. For the rest of this section assume a riskless payoff \(\mathbf 1 \in M\) with \(R_f > 0\) and \(\Var(x^\ast) > 0\).

Definition 3.1 (\(R^{e\ast}\))

Let \(E\) be the space of excess returns in \(M\) (the zero-price payoffs). \(R^{e\ast}\) is the orthogonal projection of the constant \(\mathbf 1\) onto \(E\): the unique \(R^{e\ast} \in E\) with \(\E[R^{e\ast}R^e] = \E[R^e]\) for every \(R^e \in E\).

Theorem 3.2 (the frontier is \(R^\ast + w\,R^{e\ast}\))

Every return \(R \in M\) decomposes orthogonally as \[ R = R^\ast + w\,R^{e\ast} + \eta, \qquad w \in \R, \quad \E[\eta] = 0, \quad \E[\eta R^\ast] = \E[\eta R^{e\ast}] = 0. \] A return has the least variance among returns with its mean if and only if \(\eta = 0\). The minimum-variance returns are exactly \(\{R^\ast + w\,R^{e\ast} : w \in \R\}\).

Proof

Decomposition. \(R - R^\ast\) has price 0, so it is in \(E\). Project it onto \(R^{e\ast}\) within \(E\): \(R - R^\ast = wR^{e\ast} + \eta\) with \(\eta \in E\) and \(\E[\eta R^{e\ast}] = 0\) (if \(R^{e\ast} = 0\), set \(w = 0\)). Then \(\E[\eta] = \E[\eta \cdot \mathbf 1] = \E[\eta R^{e\ast}] = 0\), because \(R^{e\ast}\) is the projection of \(\mathbf 1\) onto \(E\) and \(\eta \in E\). And \(\E[\eta R^\ast] = \E[\eta x^\ast]/\E[x^{\ast2}] = q(\eta)/\E[x^{\ast2}] = 0\), because \(\eta\) has price 0. Also \(\E[R^{e\ast}R^\ast] = q(R^{e\ast})/\E[x^{\ast2}] = 0\).

Minimum variance. Since \(\E[\eta] = 0\), \(\E[R] = \E[R^\ast] + w\,\E[R^{e\ast}]\). Applying the defining property of \(R^{e\ast}\) to \(R^e = R^{e\ast}\) gives \(\E[R^{e\ast}] = \E[R^{e\ast2}] \ge 0\), and it is positive: by Problem P3, \(R^{e\ast} = (R_f - R^\ast)/R_f \ne 0\). So \(w = (\E[R] - \E[R^\ast])/\E[R^{e\ast}]\) is fixed by the mean of \(R\). By the three orthogonality relations, \(\E[R^2] = \E[(R^\ast + wR^{e\ast})^2] + \E[\eta^2]\). Among returns with a given mean, \(w\) and \(\E[R]^2\) are fixed, so \(\Var(R) = \E[R^2] - \E[R]^2\) is smallest exactly when \(\E[\eta^2] = 0\), that is \(\eta = 0\) (using \(\pi \gg 0\)). Every \(R^\ast + wR^{e\ast}\) is a return in \(M\), since its price is \(1 + w \cdot 0\), so every mean is attained on the line.

This characterization is the mean-variance frontier in SDF terms: the frontier is a line through \(R^\ast\), the return proportional to the minimum-second-moment SDF, in the direction \(R^{e\ast}\), the excess return that best mimics a sure payment. With a riskless payoff, Problem P3 shows \(R^{e\ast} = (R_f - R^\ast)/R_f\), so every frontier return is a combination of \(R^\ast\) and the riskless return. That is the SDF version of two-fund separation.

Example 3.3 (the bond-and-stock market)

In Lesson 4's Example 4.2, \(x^\ast = (0.824242, 0.940606, 1.134545)\) with \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = 0.934012\), so \(R^\ast = x^\ast/0.934012 = (0.882475, 1.007060, 1.214701)\), with mean \(1.027824\), below \(R_f = 1/0.96 = 1.041667\). The SDF's volatility is \(\sigma(x^\ast) = 0.111410\), so the variance bound is \(\sigma(x^\ast)/\E[x^\ast] = 0.111410/0.96 = 0.116052\). The stock's return \(x_{\text{stock}}/0.976\) has mean \(1.05/0.976 = 1.075820\) and Sharpe ratio 0.116052: it attains the bound. That is forced here: \(M\) has dimension 2 and contains the independent payoffs \(\mathbf 1\) and \(R^\ast\), so \(M = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf 1, R^\ast\}\), every return in \(M\) is a combination of \(R_f\) and \(R^\ast\), and every return is on the frontier.

Lab 2 · \(R^\ast\), \(R^{e\ast}\) and the frontier

A SIMULATED market of eight states has a riskless payoff and three risky payoffs, priced by a lognormal SDF scaled to mean 0.98. The lab should find \(\E[x^\ast] = 1/R_f\), \(\E[R^{\ast2}] = 1/\E[x^{\ast2}]\) with every other return having a larger second moment, \(\E[R^{e\ast}] = \E[R^{e\ast2}]\), every return \(R^\ast + wR^{e\ast}\) having the same absolute Sharpe ratio equal to the market's maximum, and that maximum equal to \(\sigma(x^\ast)/\E[x^\ast]\), below the true SDF's \(\sigma/\E\).

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# SIMULATED market: 8 states, a riskless payoff and 3 risky payoffs, priced by a strictly positive SDF m.
rng = np.random.default_rng(606)
probs = rng.dirichlet(np.full(8, 5.0))
m = np.exp(rng.normal(0.0, 0.3, 8))
m *= 0.98 / float(probs @ m)  # scale so that E[m] = 0.98: a riskless rate of about 2% for the period
risky = 1.0 + 0.25 * rng.standard_normal((8, 3))
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(8), risky])
p = X.T @ (probs * m)


def E(v: NDArray[np.float64]) -> float:  # noqa: N802  expectation under the state probabilities
    return float(probs @ v)

r_f = 1.0 / p[0]
R = risky / p[1:]  # gross returns of the risky assets
Re = R - r_f  # excess returns
print(f"R_f = {r_f:.6f}, E[x*] = {E(x_star):.6f} = 1/R_f: {np.isclose(E(x_star), 1 / r_f)}")

r_star = x_star / E(x_star**2)

for w in rng.dirichlet(np.ones(3), size=3):

print(f"maximum Sharpe ratio of the excess returns: {max_sr:.6f}")

4Beta pricing from the SDF

Theorem 4.1 (expected returns and covariance with the SDF)

With a riskless payoff, every return \(R \in M\) and every SDF \(m\) satisfy \[ \E[R] - R_f = -R_f\,\Cov(m, R). \] If \(\Var(R^\ast) > 0\), then also \(\E[R] - R_f = \beta_{R,R^\ast}\,(\E[R^\ast] - R_f)\) with \(\beta_{R,R^\ast} = \Cov(R, R^\ast)/\Var(R^\ast)\).

Proof

\(1 = \E[mR] = \E[m]\E[R] + \Cov(m, R) = \E[R]/R_f + \Cov(m, R)\); multiply by \(R_f\). For the beta form, apply the first identity with \(m = x^\ast = \E[x^{\ast2}]R^\ast\): \(\E[R] - R_f = -R_f\E[x^{\ast2}]\Cov(R^\ast, R)\). Applied to \(R = R^\ast\) it gives \(\E[R^\ast] - R_f = -R_f\E[x^{\ast2}]\Var(R^\ast)\). Dividing, \((\E[R] - R_f)/(\E[R^\ast] - R_f) = \Cov(R^\ast, R)/\Var(R^\ast)\).

A single-factor beta model with \(R^\ast\) as the factor therefore prices every return exactly, by construction. AP2's tangency portfolio plays the same role: any frontier return other than the riskless one works as a pricing factor (Problem P1). This is why a beta model is only as informative as the claim that its factor is on the frontier, which is the substance of the Roll critique AP2 discusses.

Lab 3 · Every SDF gives the same expected returns

In Lab 2's market, \(-R_f\Cov(m, R)\) should equal \(\E[R] - R_f\) for each risky return, both with the true SDF and with \(x^\ast\), although the two SDFs differ; and \(\beta_{R,R^\ast}(\E[R^\ast] - R_f)\) should give the same numbers.

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray

# Lab 2's SIMULATED market, rebuilt: 8 states, a riskless payoff and 3 risky payoffs priced by a positive SDF.
rng = np.random.default_rng(606)
probs = rng.dirichlet(np.full(8, 5.0))
m = np.exp(rng.normal(0.0, 0.3, 8))
m *= 0.98 / float(probs @ m)
risky = 1.0 + 0.25 * rng.standard_normal((8, 3))
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(8), risky])
p = X.T @ (probs * m)


def E(v: NDArray[np.float64]) -> float:  # noqa: N802  expectation under the state probabilities
    return float(probs @ v)


def cov(a: NDArray[np.float64], b: NDArray[np.float64]) -> float:
    return E(a * b) - E(a) * E(b)


x_star = X @ np.linalg.solve(X.T @ (probs[:, None] * X), p)
r_f = 1.0 / p[0]
R = risky / p[1:]
r_star = x_star / E(x_star**2)

The four columns agree to six decimals. Covariance with the SDF is the only thing that determines a return's risk premium, and every SDF has the same covariance with every traded return: two SDFs differ by an \(\varepsilon \perp M\), and since the constant is in \(M\), \(\E[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\Cov(\varepsilon, R) = \E[\varepsilon R] = 0\). All three risky assets have negative premia in this draw: their payoffs covary positively with the SDF, so they pay off in states the SDF values highly. The lab is a check of an identity; it says nothing about which SDF the economy uses.

5Where the canon breaks

  • Positivity is ignored. \(\sigma_{\min}(v)\) is the least volatility of any SDF with mean \(v\), including SDFs that are negative in some states. Imposing \(m \ge 0\) shrinks the feasible set, so it can only raise the bound; the positivity restriction is part of Hansen and Jagannathan's original frontier (). The positive version is not a projection; Lesson 4's Lab 3 computes a case of it.
  • The bound is estimated. In data \(\mu\), \(\Sigma\) and \(p\) are sample quantities, and \(\sigma_{\min}(v)\) is a function of the sample maximum Sharpe ratio, which is biased upward: the in-sample tangency portfolio is chosen to look best in sample. With many assets and few periods the estimated bound can be far above the population one. Lesson 7 measures this directly; S7 in this program treats the bias of a maximized Sharpe ratio.
  • The frontier depends on the payoffs included. \(R^\ast\), \(R^{e\ast}\) and the bound are defined for a given \(M\). Adding an asset can only enlarge \(M\), raise the maximum Sharpe ratio and tighten the bound. A model SDF that passes the bound for 25 portfolios can fail it for 26; a bound is a statement about the assets used to compute it.
  • Unconditional moments. Everything here uses unconditional means and covariances. If the SDF and returns vary with information available at date 0, conditional bounds can be tighter, and managed portfolios (payoffs scaled by date-0 signals) enter \(M\). Lesson 6 introduces the conditional structure.

6Misconceptions

  1. “\(R^\ast\) is the tangency portfolio.”

    \(R^\ast\) is the minimum-second-moment return, and its mean is below \(R_f\) whenever \(x^\ast\) is risky (Lab 2: 0.994190 against 1.020408). It lies on the lower branch of the frontier. It prices returns as a beta factor (Theorem 4.1), but nobody maximizing a Sharpe ratio would hold it long.

  2. “An SDF that satisfies the variance bound is a good model.”

    Theorem 2.4 is a necessary condition. Lab 1's true SDF has about 2.4 times the bound's volatility (0.422254 against 0.174605) and prices everything; any SDF above the bound can still misprice the assets. Passing the bound rules nothing in.

  3. “Without a riskless asset the variance bound is undefined.”

    It is a function of the SDF's mean, \(\sigma_{\min}(v)\) (Theorem 2.1). The data do not pin the mean, so the bound is a curve, smallest at one value of \(v\) (Lab 1: 0.140481 at \(v = 0.959009\)).

  4. “A single-factor beta model that prices all returns proves the factor is a risk factor.”

    Theorem 4.1 shows that \(R^\ast\), or any frontier return, prices every return exactly by construction. Exact pricing with a factor follows whenever the factor is on the frontier, a statement about the assets used, not about economic risk.

7Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · A lower bound from one Sharpe ratio

The riskless rate is 1% for the period and a portfolio has an expected excess return of 0.5% and a volatility of 4% for the period. What is the least volatility any SDF can have?

Solution

Theorem 2.4: \(\sigma(m) \ge \E[m] \times SR = (1/1.01) \times (0.005/0.04) = 0.990099 \times 0.125 = 0.123762\). Any SDF that prices this portfolio and the riskless asset has a standard deviation of at least 0.1238 for the period, about 12.5% of its mean.

M2 · \(\sigma_{\min}(v)\) with one payoff

One risky payoff with mean 1.05, standard deviation 0.2 and price 1, and no riskless payoff. Compute \(\sigma_{\min}(v)\) for \(v = 0.95\) and find the \(v\) that minimizes it.

Solution

\(\sigma_{\min}(v) = |p - v\mu|/\sigma = |1 - 1.05v|/0.2\). At \(v = 0.95\): \(|1 - 0.9975|/0.2 = 0.0125\). It is zero at \(v = 1/1.05 = 0.952381\): with one payoff, the constant SDF \(m = 1/1.05\) prices it, and that is the riskless rate equal to the payoff's expected return. With one risky payoff there is no excess return that the data identify, so the bound can be zero.

M3 · Beta with respect to \(R^\ast\)

In Example 3.3 the stock's return has mean 1.075820, \(R_f = 1.041667\) and \(\E[R^\ast] = 1.027824\). What is the stock's beta on \(R^\ast\)?

Solution

Theorem 4.1: \(\beta = (\E[R] - R_f)/(\E[R^\ast] - R_f) = (1.075820 - 1.041667)/(1.027824 - 1.041667) = 0.034153/(-0.013843) = -2.4672\). The stock has a positive premium and a negative beta on \(R^\ast\): it pays off when \(R^\ast\), and so the SDF, is low.

Proof

P1 · Any frontier return prices by beta

With a riskless payoff, let \(R^{mv} = R^\ast + wR^{e\ast}\) be a frontier return with \(\E[R^{mv}] \ne R_f\). Prove that \(\E[R] - R_f = \beta_{R,R^{mv}}(\E[R^{mv}] - R_f)\) for every return \(R \in M\).

Solution

\(R^{mv} = R^\ast + wR^{e\ast}\) lies in \(\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf 1, R^\ast\}\), because \(R^{e\ast} = (R_f - R^\ast)/R_f\) (Problem P3); write \(R^{mv} = \alpha + \gamma R^\ast\). If \(\gamma = 0\), \(R^{mv}\) is a constant return, which must equal \(R_f\) since its price is 1, contradicting \(\E[R^{mv}] \ne R_f\). So \(\gamma \ne 0\), and \(x^\ast = \E[x^{\ast2}]R^\ast = a + cR^{mv}\) with \(c = \E[x^{\ast2}]/\gamma \ne 0\). Theorem 4.1 with \(m = x^\ast\) gives \(\E[R] - R_f = -R_fc\,\Cov(R^{mv}, R)\) for every return \(R\), and for \(R = R^{mv}\), \(\E[R^{mv}] - R_f = -R_fc\Var(R^{mv})\), with \(\Var(R^{mv}) = \gamma^2\Var(R^\ast) > 0\). Dividing gives \(\E[R] - R_f = \beta_{R,R^{mv}}(\E[R^{mv}] - R_f)\). Any frontier return other than \(R_f\) is a valid single pricing factor.

P2 · \(\sigma_{\min}(v) = |v| \times\) maximum Sharpe ratio

Under the assumptions of Theorem 2.1, suppose a riskless payoff with price \(v > 0\) were added. Show that the maximum Sharpe ratio of the enlarged market's excess returns is \(\sigma_{\min}(v)/v\).

Solution

Excess returns of the enlarged market are zero-price payoffs \(y = x^\top\theta - (p^\top\theta/v)\mathbf 1\). Then \(\E[y] = \mu^\top\theta - p^\top\theta/v = -(p - v\mu)^\top\theta/v\) and \(\sigma(y) = \sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma\theta}\). By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(|(p - v\mu)^\top\theta| = |(\Sigma^{-1/2}(p - v\mu))^\top(\Sigma^{1/2}\theta)| \le \sqrt{(p - v\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)}\sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma\theta}\), with equality at \(\theta \propto \Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)\). So \(\max |\E[y]|/\sigma(y) = \sigma_{\min}(v)/v\). The enlarged market's minimum-variance SDF has mean \(v\) (Proposition 2.2) and is the same \(m_v\), since it must price both \(x\) and the constant at \(v\); Corollary 2.5 then confirms the same number. Lab 1 checks it numerically.

P3 · With a riskless payoff, \(R^{e\ast} = (R_f - R^\ast)/R_f\)

Assume \(\mathbf 1 \in M\), \(R_f > 0\) and \(\Var(x^\ast) > 0\). Prove that \(\E[R^\ast] < R_f\) and \(R^{e\ast} = (R_f - R^\ast)/R_f\), and deduce that the frontier is the set of combinations of \(R^\ast\) and \(R_f\).

Solution

\(\E[R^\ast] < R_f\). \(R_f\) is a return, so \(\E[R^\ast R_f] = \E[R^{\ast2}]\) (Theorem 1.3), that is \(\E[R^\ast] = \E[R^{\ast2}]/R_f\). And \(\E[R^{\ast2}] = 1/\E[x^{\ast2}] < 1/\E[x^\ast]^2 = R_f^2\), strictly because \(\Var(x^\ast) > 0\). So \(\E[R^\ast] < R_f\).

\(R^{e\ast}\) is a multiple of \(R_f - R^\ast\). Every excess return \(R^e \in E\) satisfies \(\E[x^\ast R^e] = 0\), i.e. \(\E[R^\ast R^e] = 0\). The constant decomposes as \(\mathbf 1 = \tfrac{1}{R_f}R_f\), and \(R_f = R^\ast + (R_f - R^\ast)\) with \(R_f - R^\ast \in E\). For any \(R^e \in E\), \(\E[\mathbf 1\cdot R^e] = \tfrac1{R_f}\E[(R^\ast + (R_f - R^\ast))R^e] = \tfrac1{R_f}\E[(R_f - R^\ast)R^e]\). So \(R^{e\ast} = (R_f - R^\ast)/R_f\) satisfies the defining property \(\E[R^{e\ast}R^e] = \E[R^e]\) for all \(R^e \in E\), and it lies in \(E\). By uniqueness of the projection, \(R^{e\ast} = (R_f - R^\ast)/R_f\). It is nonzero because \(\E[R^\ast] < R_f\).

The frontier. \(R^\ast + wR^{e\ast} = (1 - w/R_f)R^\ast + (w/R_f)R_f\): the frontier is the set of combinations of \(R^\ast\) and the riskless return, a line through both.

Empirical

E1 · The currency \(x^\ast\) as a variance bound

Lesson 4's Lab 4 prints the sample \(x^\ast\) of nine currency payoffs: mean 0.9996, standard deviation 0.1410. Without a riskless payoff, is \(0.1410/0.9996\) a variance bound for SDFs with mean 0.9996? What Sharpe ratio, in monthly and annualized terms, does it correspond to?

Solution

Yes: by Proposition 2.3, \(x^\ast\) is the minimum-variance SDF at its own mean, so \(\sigma_{\min}(0.9996) = 0.1410\) for the sample market, and every SDF with that mean that prices the nine payoffs in sample has a standard deviation of at least 0.1410. By Problem P2 it corresponds to a maximum monthly Sharpe ratio of \(0.1410/0.9996 = 0.1411\) for excess returns over a hypothetical riskless rate of \(1/0.9996\), about \(0.1411 \times \sqrt{12} = 0.4888\) annualized. It is an in-sample maximum over nine payoffs, so it overstates what an investor could have achieved (Section 5), and it does not include deposit interest.

E2 · Adding an asset tightens the bound

In Lab 1, drop one of the four payoffs. Predict, before running, how \(\sigma_{\min}(v)\) changes at every \(v\).

Solution

It can only fall or stay equal at every \(v\). The feasible set \(\{m : \E[m] = v,\ \E[mx] = p\}\) gains a constraint when an asset is added, so the minimum variance over it rises; dropping an asset removes a constraint, and the minimum falls. In Sharpe-ratio terms (Problem P2), the best portfolio of three payoffs cannot beat the best of four. To check, change the payoff matrix to three columns with X = X[:, :3] after the prices are computed and slice p, mu and cov accordingly.

E3 · How close is \(x^\ast\) to the true SDF in Lab 2?

Using Lab 2's output, compute the share of the true SDF's variance that \(x^\ast\) captures, and interpret it.

Solution

Both SDFs have mean 0.98. Their \(\sigma/\E\) ratios are 0.208325 and 0.162392, so \(\Var(x^\ast)/\Var(m) = (0.162392/0.208325)^2 = 0.6076\). About 61% of the true SDF's variance lies in the payoff space and is priced; the remaining 39% is orthogonal to every payoff, and changing it would not change any price. A model that matches 61% of the true SDF's variance with the right projection prices these assets perfectly.

Research

R1 · Testing a model SDF against the bound

A consumption model implies an SDF series with mean 0.99 and monthly standard deviation 0.02. The test assets' estimated \(\sigma_{\min}(0.99)\) is 0.15. What can and cannot be concluded?

Solution

If the estimates were exact, the model fails: it cannot price the test assets, because every SDF with mean 0.99 that prices them has \(\sigma \ge 0.15\) (Theorem 2.1). That conclusion does not depend on which prices it gets wrong. With estimation, the bound is biased upward (Section 5), so a fair test needs its sampling distribution, for example by bootstrap or by the asymptotic distribution of the estimated maximum Sharpe ratio. A factor of 7.5 between the two numbers is unlikely to be closed by sampling error alone, but that is a claim to be checked, not assumed. A model that passed would only have passed a necessary condition.

R2 · Which assets should define the bound?

You are asked to compute a variance bound to evaluate SDF models. Discuss the choice between 6 broad portfolios and 100 narrow ones.

Solution

More assets give a tighter population bound (Problem E2) and so a stronger test. But the sample maximum Sharpe ratio of 100 assets over a few hundred periods is heavily biased upward, and its sampling variance is large, so the estimated bound may reject true models. Six broad portfolios give a weaker but better-estimated bound. A defensible design fixes the asset set before looking at the results, reports the bound with its sampling uncertainty, and checks the conclusion against an out-of-sample bound (Lesson 7's approach), which removes the in-sample maximization bias at the cost of power.

R3 · The bound with positivity

Formulate the minimum-variance SDF with mean \(v\) subject to \(m \ge 0\) as a convex optimization problem, and explain why it can exceed \(\sigma_{\min}(v)\).

Solution

Minimize \(\E[m^2]\) over random variables \(m\) subject to \(\E[m] = v\), \(\E[mx] = p\), \(m \ge 0\). With finitely many states it is a quadratic program in \((m_1, \dots, m_S)\) with linear constraints; with data, each period is a state. Its feasible set is the unconstrained feasible set intersected with the orthant, so its optimum is at least the unconstrained one, \(v^2 + \sigma_{\min}(v)^2\), with equality iff \(m_v \ge 0\). The constrained minimizer has the form \(\max(a + x^\top b, 0)\) for some \(a, b\), by the pointwise argument of Lesson 4's Lab 3 with multipliers on all \(N + 1\) constraints. It is not linear in the payoffs, which is why it is not a projection.
