Payoffs, Prices and the Law of One Price

  • Ttheorem

Every pricing model in this program, from CAPM to the Black–Scholes formula, assigns a price to a payoff. The weakest restriction any such model can impose is that two portfolios with the same payoff cost the same. This lesson shows that this one restriction already forces prices to be linear in payoffs, and that linear pricing is the same thing as a vector of state prices. Everything later in the module adds conditions to this vector: positivity in Lesson 2, an expectation form in Lesson 4. The lesson ends on real exchange-rate data, where two currencies' payoffs nearly coincide and the linear algebra of this lesson becomes a numerical problem.

1The one-period market

Fix a finite probability space \((\Omega, 2^\Omega, \Prob)\) with \(\Omega = \{1, \dots, S\}\) and \(\pi_s = \Prob(\{s\}) > 0\) for every state \(s\). Trade happens at date 0; at date 1 one state \(s\) is revealed and every asset pays off. All payoffs and prices are in one currency unit, and no asset pays anything at date 0.

Definition 1.1 (market, portfolio, payoff space)

A market is a pair \((X, p)\): a payoff matrix \(X \in \R^{S \times N}\) whose entry \(X_{sn}\) is what one unit of asset \(n\) pays in state \(s\), and a price vector \(p \in \R^N\) whose entry \(p_n\) is what one unit of asset \(n\) costs at date 0. A portfolio is a vector \(\theta \in \R^N\) of holdings, negative entries being short positions. It costs \(p^\top \theta\) and pays \(X\theta \in \R^S\). The payoff space is the set of payoffs that portfolios can produce, \[ M = \{ X\theta : \theta \in \R^N \} = \operatorname{range}(X) \subseteq \R^S. \]

\(M\) is a linear subspace of the vector space \(\R^S\), because \(X\) is a linear map. Two different portfolios can produce the same payoff: \(X\theta = X\theta'\) exactly when \(\theta - \theta' \in \ker X\). A nonzero vector in \(\ker X\) is a portfolio that pays nothing in any state. Its existence means that some asset is redundant: its payoff is a linear combination of the others'.

Remark 1.2 (what the model leaves out)

The definition assumes that any real quantity of any asset can be bought or sold at the single price \(p_n\): no bid–ask spread, no short-sale constraint, no position limit and no default by the counterparty. Section 6 takes up the spread and the limits on trading in size; Lesson 2 (Section 5 and Problem R2) takes up short-sale constraints; counterparty default is outside this module. The probabilities \(\pi_s\) play no role until Lesson 4; this lesson's results hold for any strictly positive \(\pi\), because they depend only on which states are possible.

2The law of one price

Definition 2.1 (law of one price)

The market \((X, p)\) satisfies the law of one price if any two portfolios with the same payoff have the same cost: for all \(\theta, \theta' \in \R^N\), \[ X\theta = X\theta' \;\Longrightarrow\; p^\top \theta = p^\top \theta'. \]

Proposition 2.2 (the law of one price is a kernel condition)

The market satisfies the law of one price if and only if \(p^\top \theta = 0\) for every \(\theta \in \ker X\), that is, if and only if \(p \perp \ker X\).

Proof

(⇒) If \(X\theta = 0 = X0\), the law of one price gives \(p^\top\theta = p^\top 0 = 0\). (⇐) If \(X\theta = X\theta'\), then \(\theta - \theta' \in \ker X\), so \(p^\top(\theta - \theta') = 0\).

A violation is therefore a portfolio \(\theta\) with \(X\theta = 0\) and \(p^\top \theta \ne 0\). If \(p^\top\theta < 0\), it pays you \(-p^\top\theta\) today and owes nothing in any state; if \(p^\top\theta > 0\), the portfolio \(-\theta\) does. If \(X\) has full column rank, \(\ker X = \{0\}\) and the law of one price holds whatever the prices are.

The main result of the lesson says that the law of one price has two other faces: a linear function on the payoff space, and a vector of prices for the \(S\) states.

Definition 2.3 (linear pricing functional, state-price vector)

A linear pricing functional for \((X, p)\) is a linear map \(q : M \to \R\) with \(q(X\theta) = p^\top \theta\) for every portfolio \(\theta\). A state-price vector is a vector \(\psi \in \R^S\) with \[ X^\top \psi = p, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad p_n = \sum_{s=1}^{S} \psi_s X_{sn} \quad (n = 1, \dots, N). \] Its entry \(\psi_s\) is the state price of state \(s\): the value the vector assigns to the payoff \(e_s\) that pays one unit in state \(s\) and nothing in any other state.

A state-price vector need not be positive and need not be unique. Positivity is the subject of Lesson 2. Uniqueness is settled by Proposition 2.6 below.

Theorem 2.4 (three faces of the law of one price)

For a market \((X, p)\) with \(X \in \R^{S \times N}\) and \(p \in \R^N\), the following are equivalent:

  1. the law of one price holds;
  2. there is a linear pricing functional \(q : M \to \R\), and it is unique;
  3. there is a state-price vector \(\psi \in \R^S\) with \(X^\top \psi = p\).
Proof

(i) ⇒ (ii). For \(z \in M\) choose any \(\theta\) with \(X\theta = z\) and set \(q(z) = p^\top\theta\). By the law of one price this does not depend on which \(\theta\) is chosen, so \(q\) is a well-defined function on \(M\). It is linear: if \(z = X\theta\) and \(z' = X\theta'\), then \(az + bz' = X(a\theta + b\theta')\), so \(q(az + bz') = p^\top(a\theta + b\theta') = a\,q(z) + b\,q(z')\). Any linear pricing functional \(q'\) satisfies \(q'(X\theta) = p^\top\theta = q(X\theta)\) for every \(\theta\), so \(q' = q\) on \(M\).

(ii) ⇒ (iii). Let \(P\) be the orthogonal projection of \(\R^S\) onto the subspace \(M\), and define \(\tilde q = q \circ P : \R^S \to \R\). It is linear, as a composition of linear maps, and it agrees with \(q\) on \(M\) because \(Pz = z\) for \(z \in M\). Set \(\psi_s = \tilde q(e_s)\). Every \(z \in \R^S\) is \(\sum_s z_s e_s\), so \(\tilde q(z) = \sum_s z_s \psi_s = \psi^\top z\). The \(n\)-th column of \(X\) is the payoff \(Xe_n \in M\) of the portfolio holding one unit of asset \(n\), so \[ (X^\top\psi)_n = \psi^\top (X e_n) = \tilde q(X e_n) = q(Xe_n) = p^\top e_n = p_n. \]

(iii) ⇒ (i). If \(X\theta = 0\), then \(p^\top\theta = (X^\top\psi)^\top\theta = \psi^\top X\theta = 0\). By Proposition 2.2 the law of one price holds.

The proof of (ii) ⇒ (iii) extends \(q\) from \(M\) to all of \(\R^S\) by sending the orthogonal complement \(M^\perp\) to zero. Any other extension works too. The extensions differ exactly on \(M^\perp\), and that freedom is the non-uniqueness of state prices.

Corollary 2.5 (the law of one price as a range condition)

The law of one price holds if and only if \(p \in \operatorname{range}(X^\top)\).

Proof

Statement (iii) of Theorem 2.4 says exactly that \(p\) is in the range of \(X^\top\). Together with Proposition 2.2, this proves the identity \(\operatorname{range}(X^\top) = (\ker X)^\perp\) for any real matrix: the law of one price holds iff \(p \perp \ker X\) iff \(p \in \operatorname{range}(X^\top)\).

Proposition 2.6 (how many state-price vectors)

If \(\psi_0\) is a state-price vector, the set of all state-price vectors is the affine subspace \(\psi_0 + \ker X^\top\), of dimension \(S - \operatorname{rank} X\). State prices are unique if and only if \(\operatorname{rank} X = S\), that is, if and only if \(M = \R^S\): every payoff can be produced by some portfolio.

Proof

\(X^\top\psi = p\) iff \(X^\top(\psi - \psi_0) = 0\) iff \(\psi - \psi_0 \in \ker X^\top\). By rank–nullity applied to \(X^\top : \R^S \to \R^N\), \(\dim \ker X^\top = S - \operatorname{rank} X^\top = S - \operatorname{rank} X\). This is zero iff \(\operatorname{rank} X = S\), which, since \(M = \operatorname{range}(X) \subseteq \R^S\) has dimension \(\operatorname{rank} X\), holds iff \(M = \R^S\).

Example 2.7 (a mispriced fund)

Three states and three assets: a bond paying 1 in every state, a stock paying \(1.4, 1.1, 0.6\), and a fund that holds one bond and one stock, paying \(2.4, 2.1, 1.6\). The quoted prices are \(p = (0.96, 0.976, 1.95)\). The fund's payoff equals the bond's plus the stock's, so \(\theta = (1, 1, -1)\) has \(X\theta = 0\), and its cost is \(0.96 + 0.976 - 1.95 = -0.014\). Buying the bond and the stock and selling the fund pays 0.014 today and nets to zero in every state: the law of one price fails. Scaled by \(1/0.014 = 71.4286\), the portfolio pays exactly 1 today. Repricing the fund at \(0.96 + 0.976 = 1.936\) restores the law of one price. With the repriced fund, \(\operatorname{rank} X = 2 < 3 = S\), so by Proposition 2.6 the state-price vectors form a line, \(\psi_0 + t\,v\) with \(v = (-0.5, 0.8, -0.3)\), the cross product of the bond's and the stock's payoff columns.

Lab 1 · The law of one price by linear algebra

The lab finds the redundancy in Example 2.7 by least squares, confirms that the kernel portfolio pays zero in every state and costs −0.014, scales it to cost −1, repairs the fund's price, and walks along the line of state-price vectors. Every point on the line should price all three assets exactly, and some points should have a negative entry: state prices exist under the law of one price but need not be positive. Its last lines check Example 3.2 on illustrative numbers: one foreign bond minus \(F\) dollar bonds should pay exactly the forward's payoff, and cost zero only at \(F = \mathcal S_0(1 + r)/(1 + \tilde r)\).

import numpy as np

# An illustrative one-period market on three states. Columns of X are assets, rows are states.
X = np.array(

        [1.0, 0.6, 1.6],  # state 3

c, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X[:, :2], X[:, 2], rcond=None)
print("fund = bond x", round(float(c[0]), 6), "+ stock x", round(float(c[1]), 6))
print("rank of X:", int(np.linalg.matrix_rank(X)))

# Kernel vector theta = (c, -1): its payoff is zero in every state (Proposition 2.2 needs p'theta = 0).
theta = np.append(c, -1.0)
print("payoff of theta:", np.round(X @ theta, 12) + 0.0, "| cost p'theta:", round(float(p @ theta), 6))

# A LOOP violation: scale theta so that it costs -1, i.e. you are paid 1 today and owe nothing tomorrow.
scaled = theta / -float(p @ theta)
print("scaled portfolio:", np.round(scaled, 4), "| cost:", round(float(p @ scaled), 6))

# Repricing the fund at the cost of its replicating portfolio restores LOOP.

direction = np.cross(X[:, 0], X[:, 1])  # spans ker X' (orthogonal to both independent columns)
psi0, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X.T, p_fixed, rcond=None)  # the minimum-norm solution
print("direction of ker X':", np.round(direction, 6))

    psi = psi0 + t * direction
    print(f"t = {t:+.2f}: psi = {np.round(psi, 4)}, prices all three: {bool(np.allclose(X.T @ psi, p_fixed))}")

The kernel of \(X\) is one-dimensional, so there is one direction of zero-payoff portfolios, and the quoted fund price gives it a nonzero cost. 3Pricing redundant payoffs

The law of one price prices everything in \(M\) and nothing outside it. Inside \(M\), every payoff has a unique price; outside \(M\), the market says nothing yet.

Proposition 3.1 (the price of a redundant asset)

Suppose the market \((X, p)\) satisfies the law of one price, and add an asset with payoff \(z \in \R^S\) and price \(c\). The enlarged market satisfies the law of one price if and only if either \(z \notin M\), or \(z \in M\) and \(c = q(z) = p^\top\theta_z\) for any \(\theta_z\) with \(X\theta_z = z\).

Proof

A portfolio of the enlarged market is \((\theta, a)\) with payoff \(X\theta + az\) and cost \(p^\top\theta + ac\). If \(z \notin M\), then \(X\theta + az = 0\) forces \(a = 0\) (otherwise \(z = -X\theta/a \in M\)) and hence \(X\theta = 0\) and \(p^\top\theta = 0\); the kernel condition holds. If \(z \in M\), the portfolio \((\theta_z, -1)\) pays zero, so the law of one price requires \(p^\top\theta_z - c = 0\). Conversely, if \(c = p^\top\theta_z\) and \(X\theta + az = 0\), then \(X(\theta + a\theta_z) = 0\), so \(p^\top(\theta + a\theta_z) = 0\), that is, \(p^\top\theta + ac = 0\).

This is the logic of every replication argument: a payoff in \(M\) has the price of the portfolio that produces it. A payoff outside \(M\) can take any price without violating the law of one price. Lesson 2 shows that it cannot take any price without creating an arbitrage opportunity, and Lesson 3 computes the range it can take.

Example 3.2 (the forward price of a currency as a redundant payoff)

Let \(\mathcal S_1\) be the dollar price of one unit of a foreign currency at date 1, a random variable. Three assets are traded: a dollar bond paying 1 dollar at date 1 for \(1/(1 + r)\) dollars today; a foreign bond paying 1 unit of currency at date 1, which costs \(1/(1 + \tilde r)\) units today, that is \(\mathcal S_0/(1 + \tilde r)\) dollars, with \(\mathcal S_0\) today's spot rate; and a forward contract to buy one unit at date 1 for \(F\) dollars, which costs nothing today and pays \(\mathcal S_1 - F\). The forward's payoff is in \(M\): it equals one foreign bond minus \(F\) dollar bonds, in every state. By Proposition 3.1 its price must be that portfolio's cost, \(\mathcal S_0/(1 + \tilde r) - F/(1 + r)\). Setting this equal to zero gives the only forward price consistent with the law of one price, \[ F = \mathcal S_0\,\frac{1 + r}{1 + \tilde r}. \] Nothing about the distribution of \(\mathcal S_1\) enters: the relation holds state by state. A quoted \(F\) that differs from it, with all three assets tradable at one price each, is a violation of the law of one price.

4Near-redundancy in data

Exact redundancy is a property of a model. In data, payoffs are measured with error and markets change, so the question becomes how close to redundant two payoffs are, and what that does to the linear algebra. A clean way to build traded payoffs from public data is to hold currency. One US dollar spent on currency \(n\) at month-end \(t\) buys \(1/\mathcal S_{n,t}\) units, where \(\mathcal S_{n,t}\) is the dollar price of one unit; held for a month, those units are worth \(\mathcal S_{n,t+1}/\mathcal S_{n,t}\) dollars. That is a payoff with price 1, the gross simple return on the currency's spot rate. It ignores the interest the currency would earn on deposit, so it is the payoff of holding the currency itself, not of a deposit.

Each month is one draw of the state. The sample analogue of \(X^\top X\), the matrix whose rank decided everything above, is the second-moment matrix \(\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t x_t x_t^\top\) of the \(T\) monthly payoff vectors \(x_t \in \R^N\). An exactly redundant payoff makes it singular. A nearly redundant one makes its smallest eigenvalue tiny.

Lab 2 · Two currencies that nearly coincide

The data are month-end spot rates for ten currencies, from the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, G.5/H.10 Foreign Exchange Rates (noon buying rates in New York), retrieved 2026-10-19 (fx_monthly.csv). The lab builds the ten one-month currency payoffs from the first month in which every currency has a rate, and computes the eigenvalues of their second-moment matrix. If two of the payoffs nearly coincide, the smallest eigenvalue should be orders of magnitude below the next one, a regression of one payoff on the other should have an \(R^2\) near one, and dropping one of the pair should restore a reasonable condition number.

import csv
import math
from pathlib import Path

import numpy as np

# Month-end spot rates, USD per unit of foreign currency (Federal Reserve H.10 noon buying rates in New York).
with (Path("data") / "fx_monthly.csv").open(newline="", encoding="utf-8") as handle:
    rows = list(csv.reader(handle))
currencies, months = rows[0][1:], [row[0] for row in rows[1:]]
prices = np.array([[float(v) if v else math.nan for v in row[1:]] for row in rows[1:]])
start = int(np.argmax(~np.isnan(prices).any(axis=1)))  # first month with a rate for every currency

# Asset n: one dollar of currency n held as cash for a month. Price 1 dollar; payoff S[t+1]/S[t] dollars.
gross = prices[start + 1 :] / prices[start:-1]
n_obs, n_assets = gross.shape
print(f"{n_obs} monthly payoffs, {months[start + 1]} to {months[-1]}, {n_assets} currencies")

second = gross.T @ gross / n_obs  # sample second-moment matrix E_T[x x']
eigvals = np.linalg.eigvalsh(second)
print(f"eigenvalues of E_T[x x']: smallest {eigvals[0]:.3e}, next {eigvals[1]:.3e}, largest {eigvals[-1]:.4f}")
print(f"condition number: {eigvals[-1] / eigvals[0]:.4g}")

# Which payoffs nearly coincide? Regress the DKK payoff on the EUR payoff and a constant.
dkk, eur = gross[:, currencies.index("DKK")], gross[:, currencies.index("EUR")]
design = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_obs), eur])
coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(design, dkk, rcond=None)
resid = dkk - design @ coef
print(f"DKK on EUR: slope {coef[1]:.4f}, R^2 {1 - resid.var() / dkk.var():.5f}")
print(f"monthly s.d. of the DKK payoff {1e4 * dkk.std():.1f} bp; of the residual {1e4 * resid.std():.2f} bp")
print(f"E_T[(DKK - EUR)^2] / 2 = {np.mean((dkk - eur) ** 2) / 2:.3e}  (Rayleigh quotient of u = (1, -1) / sqrt 2)")

# Without DKK the second-moment matrix is far better conditioned.
keep = [i for i, c in enumerate(currencies) if c != "DKK"]
eig9 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(second[np.ix_(keep, keep)])
print(f"without DKK: smallest eigenvalue {eig9[0]:.3e}, condition number {eig9[-1] / eig9[0]:.4g}")

# The near-redundancy is stable: the same regression before and after 2009-01.
ends = months[start + 1 :]  # month in which each payoff is received
for label, mask in (
    ("1999-02 to 2008-12", np.array([m < "2009-01" for m in ends])),
    ("2009-01 to 2026-09", np.array([m >= "2009-01" for m in ends])),
):
    y, x = dkk[mask], eur[mask]
    sub = np.column_stack([np.ones(y.size), x])
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(sub, y, rcond=None)
    e = y - sub @ b
    r2 = 1 - e.var() / y.var()
    print(f"{label}: {y.size} months, slope {b[1]:.4f}, R^2 {r2:.5f}, residual s.d. {1e4 * e.std():.2f} bp")

The smallest eigenvalue is about \(3.6 \times 10^{-7}\), more than 300 times smaller than the next, and the DKK and EUR payoffs move together with an \(R^2\) of 0.99891 and a residual standard deviation under 9 basis points a month, against 262 for the payoff itself. The fit is about as tight in the two halves of the sample. The second-moment matrix is invertible, so in the sample the DKK payoff is not exactly redundant and the law of one price does not pin its price to the EUR payoff's. But any computation that inverts this matrix, which Lessons 4, 5 and 7 do, divides by its smallest eigenvalue: tiny measurement differences between the two series become large portfolio weights. The lab does not say why the two currencies move together, and nothing in this module depends on the reason.

Example 4.1 (what the condition number means)

Lab 2 prints a condition number of \(2.776 \times 10^{7}\) with all ten currencies and \(1.008 \times 10^{5}\) without DKK. The condition number of a symmetric positive-definite matrix is the ratio of its largest to its smallest eigenvalue, and it bounds how much a relative error in the right-hand side of a linear system can be amplified in the solution. Solving with the ten-currency matrix can amplify relative errors in the prices by a factor of up to about 28 million, against about 100 thousand without DKK: dropping one of two nearly identical payoffs costs almost nothing in spanning and gains a factor of \(2.776 \times 10^{7} / 1.008 \times 10^{5} = 275\) in numerical stability.

5Linear pricing and returns

A state-price vector turns every payoff in \(M\) into a price, and so it also turns payoffs into returns. If \(z \in M\) has price \(q(z) \ne 0\), its gross return is \(R = z/q(z)\), and the linear pricing functional prices every return at one: \(q(R) = 1\). If a portfolio pays 1 in every state, the riskless payoff \(\mathbf{1} \in M\), its price is \(q(\mathbf{1}) = \sum_s \psi_s\), and its gross return \(R_f = 1/\sum_s \psi_s\) is the riskless rate, provided the sum is nonzero. Lesson 4 rewrites these identities as expectations, and Lesson 5 builds the mean-variance frontier from them.

Example 5.1 (a riskless rate from the bond)

In Example 2.7 the bond pays 1 in every state and costs 0.96. Every state-price vector therefore has \(\psi_1 + \psi_2 + \psi_3 = 0.96\), whatever its position on the line, and \(R_f = 1/0.96 = 1.041667\): a riskless rate of 4.1667% for the period. The line's direction \(v = (-0.5, 0.8, -0.3)\) sums to zero, as it must: moving along the line cannot change the price of a payoff in \(M\).

6Where the canon breaks

  • Two prices, not one. With a bid–ask spread an asset is bought at \(p^a_n\) and sold at \(p^b_n < p^a_n\), and the cost of a portfolio is no longer linear in \(\theta\): it is \(\kappa(\theta) = \sum_n (p^a_n \theta_n^+ - p^b_n \theta_n^-)\), convex and positively homogeneous. Two portfolios with the same payoff can then have different costs without anyone being able to profit, and the law of one price becomes the existence of some price between bid and ask that satisfies Proposition 2.2. Problem R1 proves this.
  • Constrained arbitrageurs. The argument that a violation must disappear assumes someone can trade the zero-payoff portfolio in size, and keep trading it if the mispricing first widens. For a model of this, in which specialized arbitrageurs invest other people's money and, when funds respond strongly enough to past performance, investors withdraw funds after poor performance, see . The model's conclusions about when arbitrage is cut back are results inside that model; whether it describes a given market is an empirical question the model does not settle. Example 3.2's forward–spot relation, with deposits at the two currencies' interest rates, is a law-of-one-price relation whose empirical deviations, and the costs of trading against them, AP6 and E8 take up.
  • Finitely many states. Everything here is finite-dimensional linear algebra. With infinitely many states, the payoff space can be infinite-dimensional and a linear pricing functional need not be continuous; Lesson 4 works with a finite number of traded payoffs, which avoids the issue, and Lesson 6 says what changes in general.
  • Redundancy is never exact in data. Lab 2 shows a pair of payoffs that are redundant to within 9 basis points a month but not exactly. A test of the law of one price on such data is a test of whether a residual is zero, and it inherits every problem of measuring the residual: stale or asynchronous quotes, rounding and data errors. A violation of a few basis points in recorded prices is weak evidence of a tradable violation.

7Misconceptions

  1. “The law of one price says that every asset has a price.”

    It says that payoffs, not assets, have prices: two portfolios with the same payoff cost the same. When no asset is redundant (\(\ker X = \{0\}\)) it holds for every price vector, so it restricts nothing (Proposition 2.2).

  2. “State prices are probabilities, or at least positive.”

    Under the law of one price alone a state-price vector can have negative entries (Lab 1, \(t = 0.7\)). Its entries need not sum to one either: they sum to the price of the riskless payoff, \(1/R_f\) (Example 5.1). Positivity requires the stronger condition of Lesson 2.

  3. “If the market has state prices, they are unique.”

    They are unique only if every payoff in \(\R^S\) can be produced, \(\operatorname{rank} X = S\) (Proposition 2.6). With fewer independent payoffs than states the state prices form an affine subspace of dimension \(S - \operatorname{rank} X\), and the market prices only the payoffs in \(M\).

  4. “Two payoffs with a correlation of 0.999 are redundant, so the law of one price fixes the relative price.”

    Redundancy is an exact linear relation in every state. Lab 2's DKK and EUR payoffs differ by a residual with a standard deviation of 8.65 basis points a month; in the model of Section 1 that residual is a payoff outside the span of the other, which the law of one price does not price.

8Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · Check the law of one price

Two states, three assets with payoffs \(x_1 = (1, 1)\), \(x_2 = (2, 0)\), \(x_3 = (0, 2)\) and prices \(p = (0.95, 1.00, 0.92)\). Does the law of one price hold? If not, give a zero-payoff portfolio that pays you 1 today.

Solution

\(x_2 + x_3 = (2, 2) = 2x_1\), so \(\theta = (2, -1, -1)\) has \(X\theta = 0\). Its cost is \(2 \times 0.95 - 1.00 - 0.92 = -0.02\). The law of one price fails. Scaling by \(1/0.02 = 50\), the portfolio \((100, -50, -50)\) costs \(-1\): it pays you 1 today and nets to zero in both states. The kernel of \(X\) is one-dimensional (rank 2 with three assets), so every violation is a multiple of this one.

M2 · State prices by hand

Two states, a bond paying \((1, 1)\) at price 0.97 and a stock paying \((1.3, 0.8)\) at price 1.00. Find the state prices and the riskless rate.

Solution

Solve \(\psi_1 + \psi_2 = 0.97\) and \(1.3\psi_1 + 0.8\psi_2 = 1.00\). Subtracting \(0.8\) times the first equation from the second gives \(0.5\psi_1 = 1.00 - 0.776 = 0.224\), so \(\psi_1 = 0.448\) and \(\psi_2 = 0.522\). The matrix has rank 2 = S, so these are the only state prices (Proposition 2.6). The riskless rate is \(R_f = 1/0.97 = 1.030928\).

M3 · Price a redundant payoff

In the market of M2, what must a payoff of \((2.1, 1.1)\) cost for the law of one price to hold?

Solution

The payoff is in \(M = \R^2\). By Theorem 2.4 its price is \(\psi^\top z = 0.448 \times 2.1 + 0.522 \times 1.1 = 0.9408 + 0.5742 = 1.515\). Check by replication: \(a(1,1) + b(1.3, 0.8) = (2.1, 1.1)\) gives \(0.5b = 1.0\), \(b = 2\), \(a = -0.5\); the cost is \(-0.5 \times 0.97 + 2 \times 1.00 = 1.515\).

Proof

P1 · The range–kernel identity directly

Prove, without Theorem 2.4, that \(\operatorname{range}(A^\top) = (\ker A)^\perp\) for any real \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\).

Solution

(⊆) If \(y = A^\top u\) and \(x \in \ker A\), then \(y^\top x = u^\top A x = 0\). (⊇) Both sides are subspaces of \(\R^n\). Their dimensions are \(\operatorname{rank} A^\top = \operatorname{rank} A\) and \(n - \dim\ker A = n - (n - \operatorname{rank} A) = \operatorname{rank} A\) by rank–nullity. A subspace contained in another of the same finite dimension equals it.

P2 · Every extension is a state-price vector

Let \(q\) be the linear pricing functional of a market satisfying the law of one price. Show that the state-price vectors are exactly the vectors \(\psi\) for which \(z \mapsto \psi^\top z\) agrees with \(q\) on \(M\), and deduce Proposition 2.6 again.

Solution

If \(X^\top\psi = p\), then for \(z = X\theta \in M\), \(\psi^\top z = \psi^\top X\theta = p^\top\theta = q(z)\). Conversely, if \(\psi^\top z = q(z)\) for all \(z \in M\), apply it to the columns \(Xe_n\): \(\psi^\top X e_n = q(Xe_n) = p_n\), so \(X^\top\psi = p\). Two such vectors \(\psi, \psi'\) agree on \(M\), so \(\psi - \psi' \in M^\perp = (\operatorname{range} X)^\perp = \ker X^\top\) (P1 applied to \(X^\top\)); and any \(\psi + v\) with \(v \in M^\perp\) agrees with \(q\) on \(M\). So the set is \(\psi + M^\perp\), of dimension \(S - \dim M = S - \operatorname{rank} X\).

P3 · The minimum-norm state-price vector lies in \(M\)

Under the law of one price, show that there is exactly one state-price vector in \(M\), and that it is the state-price vector with the smallest Euclidean norm.

Solution

Take any state-price vector \(\psi\) and write \(\psi = P\psi + (I - P)\psi\) with \(P\) the projection onto \(M\). Since \((I - P)\psi \in M^\perp = \ker X^\top\), the vector \(\psi_M = P\psi\) also satisfies \(X^\top\psi_M = X^\top\psi = p\), and it lies in \(M\). If \(\psi_M, \psi'_M \in M\) are both state-price vectors, their difference lies in \(M \cap M^\perp = \{0\}\). For any state-price vector \(\psi = \psi_M + v\) with \(v \in M^\perp\), Pythagoras gives \(\|\psi\|^2 = \|\psi_M\|^2 + \|v\|^2 \ge \|\psi_M\|^2\), with equality iff \(v = 0\). This is the vector np.linalg.lstsq returns in Lab 1, and Lesson 4 meets it again, weighted by probabilities, as \(x^\ast\).

Empirical

E1 · Reading Lab 2's eigenvalues

Lab 2 prints the smallest eigenvalue \(3.607 \times 10^{-7}\) of the ten-currency second-moment matrix and the quantity \(\tfrac12 \E_T[(x_{DKK} - x_{EUR})^2]\). Explain why the second is an upper bound for the first, and why the two are close.

Solution

For a symmetric matrix \(A\), the smallest eigenvalue is \(\lambda_{\min} = \min_{\|u\| = 1} u^\top A u\) (the Rayleigh quotient, by the spectral theorem). With \(A = \tfrac1T\sum_t x_t x_t^\top\), \(u^\top A u = \tfrac1T \sum_t (u^\top x_t)^2\) is the mean squared payoff of the portfolio \(u\). Take \(u\) with \(1/\sqrt2\) on DKK, \(-1/\sqrt2\) on EUR and zero elsewhere: its mean squared payoff is \(\tfrac12\E_T[(x_{DKK} - x_{EUR})^2] = 3.807 \times 10^{-7}\), which Lab 2 prints, so \(\lambda_{\min} \le 3.807 \times 10^{-7}\). The printed \(\lambda_{\min} = 3.607 \times 10^{-7}\) is 5% lower: the best unit-norm portfolio adjusts the weights slightly (the regression slope is 0.9956, not 1) and uses the other currencies a little, but the DKK-minus-EUR position is almost the whole story.

E2 · Spanning versus redundancy

Lab 2 has \(N = 10\) payoffs and \(T = 332\) months. Viewing each month as a state, is the sample market able to produce every payoff over the 332 states? What does Proposition 2.6 then say about the sample state prices?

Solution

No. The payoff matrix of the sample market is \(332 \times 10\), of rank at most 10, so \(M\) is a subspace of dimension at most 10 in \(\R^{332}\). The second-moment matrix is invertible, so the rank is exactly 10 and \(\ker X = \{0\}\): the law of one price holds for any prices. By Proposition 2.6 the state-price vectors form an affine subspace of dimension \(332 - 10 = 322\). The data cannot identify state prices; they can identify at most the price functional on a 10-dimensional space of payoffs. Lesson 4 picks one natural representative.

E3 · An exactly redundant pair

Suppose the DKK payoff were exactly \(0.9956\) times the EUR payoff in every month, with both still costing one dollar. Does the law of one price hold? If not, give the violating portfolio and its cost per dollar of EUR sold.

Solution

The portfolio \(\theta\) long one dollar of DKK and short 0.9956 dollars of EUR pays \(x_{DKK} - 0.9956\,x_{EUR} = 0\) every month, so \(\theta \in \ker X\). It costs \(1 - 0.9956 = 0.0044\) dollars, which is not zero, so the law of one price fails (Proposition 2.2). The opposite portfolio, short one dollar of DKK and long 0.9956 of EUR, pays you 0.0044 today, or \(0.0044/0.9956 = 0.00442\) per dollar of EUR bought, and owes nothing. The second-moment matrix of that market would be singular. The real data escape the violation only because the relation is not exact: the residual, with a standard deviation of 8.65 basis points a month, is a payoff outside the span of the EUR payoff.

Research

R1 · The law of one price with a bid–ask spread

Each asset trades at an ask \(p^a_n\) and a bid \(p^b_n \le p^a_n\), and a portfolio costs \(\kappa(\theta) = \sum_n (p^a_n\theta_n^+ - p^b_n\theta_n^-)\). Prove the analogue of Proposition 2.2: no zero-payoff portfolio has a negative cost, \(\kappa(\theta) \ge 0\) for every \(\theta \in \ker X\), if and only if some price vector \(\tilde p\) with \(p^b \le \tilde p \le p^a\) satisfies \(\tilde p \perp \ker X\).

Solution

Structure of \(\kappa\). Each term \(p^a_n t^+ - p^b_n t^- = p^b_n t + (p^a_n - p^b_n)t^+\) is a convex function of \(t\) (a linear function plus a nonnegative multiple of the convex \(t^+\)), so \(\kappa\) is convex; and \(\kappa(\lambda\theta) = \lambda\kappa(\theta)\) for \(\lambda \ge 0\). A convex positively homogeneous function is subadditive: \(\kappa(\theta + \theta') = 2\kappa(\tfrac12\theta + \tfrac12\theta') \le \kappa(\theta) + \kappa(\theta')\).

(⇐) For \(p^b_n \le \tilde p_n \le p^a_n\) and any real \(t\), \(p^a_n t^+ - p^b_n t^- \ge \tilde p_n t^+ - \tilde p_n t^- = \tilde p_n t\). Summing, \(\kappa(\theta) \ge \tilde p^\top\theta\), and for \(\theta \in \ker X\) the right side is 0.

(⇒) Choose a basis of \(\ker X\) and write its elements as \(\theta = Kz\). Consider the linear program \(\min\, \sum_n (p^a_n u_n - p^b_n w_n)\) over \(u, w \ge 0\) and \(z\), subject to \(u - w = Kz\). Its value is \(\min_{\theta \in \ker X}\kappa(\theta)\), because for a given \(\theta\) the cheapest split is \(u = \theta^+\), \(w = \theta^-\) (raising both by the same amount adds \(p^a_n - p^b_n \ge 0\)). By hypothesis \(\kappa \ge 0\) on \(\ker X\), and \(\kappa(0) = 0\), so the value is 0 and the program is bounded. By linear-programming duality (M7's Lagrangian duality for linear programs, or Lesson 3's Theorem 4.1 argument), the dual is feasible: there is a multiplier \(\tilde p\) on \(u - w - Kz = 0\) with \(p^a - \tilde p \ge 0\) (from \(u \ge 0\)), \(\tilde p - p^b \ge 0\) (from \(w \ge 0\)) and \(K^\top\tilde p = 0\) (from \(z\) free). The last condition is \(\tilde p \perp \ker X\).

Reading. With \(p^a = p^b\), \(\tilde p = p\) is forced and this is Proposition 2.2. With a spread, the law of one price becomes the existence of some price between bid and ask that satisfies it, and two zero-payoff portfolios \(\theta\) and \(-\theta\) can both cost a positive amount: \(\kappa(\theta) + \kappa(-\theta) = \sum_n (p^a_n - p^b_n)|\theta_n|\), the round-trip spread.

R2 · Designing a law-of-one-price test

You want to test whether a fund that holds a published basket of currencies is priced at the value of its holdings, using daily data. List the measurement problems that could produce an apparent violation, and say how you would separate them from a tradable one.

Solution
  • Asynchronous prices. The fund's price and the currencies' rates may be recorded at different times; the H.10 rates of Lab 2 are noon buying rates in New York. Use quotes stamped within the same second, or bound the effect by the price movement over the gap.
  • Holdings not known exactly. The basket, its weights and any cash or accrued interest must be the ones in force on the day; a stale basket creates a payoff outside \(M\), which the law of one price does not price.
  • Spreads and fees. Compare the fund's bid with the basket's ask, and the fund's ask with the basket's bid (R1). Only a gap outside both is a violation at quoted prices.
  • Execution. Even then, a violation is tradable only for an investor who can create or redeem fund units or carry the basket, and only after the funding and capital costs of holding the position (AP6 takes these up).

The test statistic is the gap after all four adjustments, and the claim is credible only if the gap exceeds the size the measurement problems can produce.

R3 · How many states does a market need?

A practitioner says: “We trade 40 instruments, so a 40-state model is enough to price everything we trade.” Evaluate the claim using Proposition 2.6.

Solution

The number of states is a property of the uncertainty, not of the instruments. With \(S\) states and 40 instruments whose payoff matrix has rank \(k = \operatorname{rank} X \le \min(S, 40)\), the 40 prices determine the pricing functional on a \(k\)-dimensional payoff space. If the true uncertainty has more than \(k\) relevant states, there is a family of state-price vectors of dimension \(S - k\), and a new payoff outside \(M\), such as an option on one of the instruments, is not priced by the law of one price. Choosing \(S = 40\) does not remove that freedom; it hides it by assuming that the 40 payoffs span all uncertainty. The claim is correct only for payoffs that are linear combinations of the 40 instruments' payoffs.
