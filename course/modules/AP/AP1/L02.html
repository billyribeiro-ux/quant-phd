No Arbitrage and the Fundamental Theorem

  • Uses: the law of one price, state prices, convex sets, compactness, orthogonal projection
  • Introduces: the fundamental theorem of asset pricing
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study

The law of one price rules out getting money for nothing from two identical payoffs. It does not rule out getting money for nothing from a payoff that is never negative and sometimes positive. Ruling that out is the condition of no arbitrage, and the fundamental theorem of this lesson says it is equivalent to the existence of strictly positive state prices. Every risk-neutral valuation you will meet, from a binomial tree to a volatility surface, is an application of this theorem. The proof here is complete and uses nothing beyond projections in \(\R^n\) and compactness, and a lab runs it.

1Arbitrage

Keep the one-period market \((X, p)\) of Lesson 1: \(S\) states, \(N\) assets, payoff matrix \(X \in \R^{S \times N}\), price vector \(p \in \R^N\), portfolio \(\theta\) costing \(p^\top\theta\) and paying \(X\theta\). For vectors, \(u \ge 0\) means every entry is nonnegative and \(u \gg 0\) means every entry is strictly positive.

Definition 1.1 (arbitrage, no arbitrage)

An arbitrage is a portfolio \(\theta\) with \[ X\theta \ge 0, \qquad p^\top\theta \le 0, \qquad (X\theta, -p^\top\theta) \ne 0. \] It never pays a negative amount, it costs nothing or pays you today, and it does something: either it pays a positive amount in some state, or it pays you today, or both. The market satisfies no arbitrage if no such portfolio exists.

Stack the date-0 cash flow on top of the date-1 payoff. A portfolio's cash flows form the vector \((-p^\top\theta, X\theta) \in \R^{1 + S}\), and an arbitrage is a portfolio whose cash-flow vector is nonnegative and nonzero. The set of all cash-flow vectors, \[ L = \{(-p^\top\theta,\; X\theta) : \theta \in \R^N\} \subseteq \R^{1+S}, \] is a linear subspace, the range of the matrix \(G = \begin{pmatrix} -p^\top \\ X \end{pmatrix}\). No arbitrage says exactly that \(L \cap \R^{1+S}_{+} = \{0\}\): the subspace meets the nonnegative orthant only at the origin.

Proposition 1.2 (no arbitrage implies the law of one price)

If the market has no arbitrage, it satisfies the law of one price. The converse is false.

Proof

If \(X\theta = 0\) and \(p^\top\theta \ne 0\), then whichever of \(\theta\) and \(-\theta\) has negative cost is an arbitrage: its payoff is zero and its cash-flow vector \((-p^\top\theta, 0)\) is nonnegative and nonzero. For the converse, take one state and one asset paying 1 at price \(-1\). No asset is redundant, so the law of one price holds (Lesson 1, Proposition 2.2), but buying the asset pays you 1 today and 1 tomorrow: an arbitrage.

2Separating a subspace from a simplex

The fundamental theorem rests on one geometric fact: a subspace that misses a compact convex set can be separated from it by a hyperplane through the origin. Here is a complete proof for the case needed.

Lemma 2.1 (separating a subspace from a compact convex set)

Let \(L \subseteq \R^n\) be a linear subspace and \(K \subseteq \R^n\) a nonempty compact convex set with \(L \cap K = \emptyset\). Then there is \(w \in \R^n\) with \(w^\top l = 0\) for every \(l \in L\) and \(w^\top k \ge \|w\|^2 > 0\) for every \(k \in K\).

Proof

Let \(P\) be the orthogonal projection onto \(L\) and \(Q = I - P\) the projection onto \(L^\perp\). The distance from \(k\) to \(L\) is \(\|Qk\|\), a continuous function of \(k\). By compactness of \(K\), it attains its minimum at some \(k^\ast \in K\). Set \(w = Qk^\ast\). Then \(w \in L^\perp\), which is the first claim, and \(w \ne 0\), because \(w = 0\) would put \(k^\ast = Pk^\ast\) in \(L \cap K\).

Take any \(k \in K\). For \(t \in [0, 1]\), \(k_t = k^\ast + t(k - k^\ast) \in K\) by convexity, so \(f(t) = \|Qk_t\|^2 = \|w + tQ(k - k^\ast)\|^2\) is minimized over \([0,1]\) at \(t = 0\). \(f\) is a quadratic with \(f'(0) = 2\,w^\top Q(k - k^\ast)\), and a minimum at the left end of the interval requires \(f'(0) \ge 0\). Since \(Q\) is symmetric and \(Qw = w\), \(w^\top Q v = (Qw)^\top v = w^\top v\) for every \(v\), so \(w^\top(k - k^\ast) \ge 0\). Finally \(w^\top k^\ast = w^\top(Pk^\ast + Qk^\ast) = 0 + \|w\|^2\), because \(Pk^\ast \in L\) and \(w \perp L\). Hence \(w^\top k \ge w^\top k^\ast = \|w\|^2 > 0\).

3The fundamental theorem for finitely many states

Theorem 3.1 (fundamental theorem of asset pricing, one period, finitely many states)

Let \((X, p)\) be a one-period market with \(S\) states, \(X \in \R^{S \times N}\) and \(p \in \R^N\), in which every portfolio \(\theta \in \R^N\) can be traded at cost \(p^\top\theta\). The market has no arbitrage if and only if there is a strictly positive state-price vector: some \(\psi \in \R^S\) with \[ \psi \gg 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad X^\top\psi = p. \]

This equivalence, between no arbitrage and strictly positive state prices, is the fundamental theorem of asset pricing in its simplest form. Lesson 6 restates it with probabilities and many periods, and records what changes with infinitely many states or continuous trading.

Proof

(⇐) Let \(\psi \gg 0\) with \(X^\top\psi = p\), and suppose \(X\theta \ge 0\) and \(p^\top\theta \le 0\). Then \(0 \ge p^\top\theta = \psi^\top X\theta = \sum_s \psi_s (X\theta)_s \ge 0\), so \(p^\top\theta = 0\) and \(\sum_s \psi_s (X\theta)_s = 0\). Every term of the sum is nonnegative and every \(\psi_s > 0\), so every \((X\theta)_s = 0\). The cash-flow vector \((-p^\top\theta, X\theta)\) is zero, and \(\theta\) is not an arbitrage.

(⇒) Let \(L = \operatorname{range}(G) \subseteq \R^{1+S}\) be the subspace of cash-flow vectors of Section 1, and \(K = \{k \in \R^{1+S} : k \ge 0,\ \sum_j k_j = 1\}\) the standard simplex. \(K\) is nonempty, convex, closed and bounded, hence compact. Every nonzero nonnegative vector is a positive multiple of a point of \(K\), and \(L\) is closed under positive multiples, so no arbitrage (\(L \cap \R^{1+S}_+ = \{0\}\)) gives \(L \cap K = \emptyset\). Lemma 2.1 yields \(w = (w_0, w_1, \dots, w_S)\) with \(w \perp L\) and \(w^\top k > 0\) for every \(k \in K\). Applied to the unit vectors \(e_0, \dots, e_S \in K\), the second property gives \(w_j > 0\) for every \(j\): \(w \gg 0\). The first property says that for every \(\theta\), \[ 0 = w^\top G\theta = -w_0\, p^\top\theta + \sum_{s=1}^S w_s (X\theta)_s = \big(-w_0\, p + X^\top w_{1:S}\big)^\top \theta, \] so \(X^\top w_{1:S} = w_0\, p\). Set \(\psi = w_{1:S}/w_0\). Then \(\psi \gg 0\) and \(X^\top\psi = p\).

Remark 3.2 (what the proof used)

The proof used three facts: \(L\) is a subspace (portfolios can be scaled by any real number and added, at linear cost), the simplex is compact, and \(\R^{1+S}\) is finite-dimensional, so the projection onto \(L\) exists and the distance is attained. With short-sale constraints \(L\) becomes a cone and the first fact fails (Problem R2). With infinitely many states the second and third need replacing, and Lesson 6 records what replaces them. The weight \(w_0\) on today's cash flow is the reason state prices are measured relative to money today: the separating hyperplane prices one unit of date-0 cash at \(w_0\), and normalizing it to 1 gives \(\psi\).

Corollary 3.3 (positive pricing of payoffs)

Under no arbitrage, the linear pricing functional \(q\) of Lesson 1 is strictly positive on the payoff space: if \(z \in M\), \(z \ge 0\) and \(z \ne 0\), then \(q(z) > 0\). The riskless payoff, if traded, has a positive price, so \(R_f = 1/\sum_s \psi_s > 0\).

Proof

\(q(z) = \psi^\top z\) for the strictly positive \(\psi\) of Theorem 3.1 (Lesson 1, Problem P2), and \(\psi^\top z = \sum_s \psi_s z_s > 0\) when \(z \ge 0\) has a positive entry. The riskless payoff \(\mathbf 1\) is such a \(z\).

Example 3.4 (the interval of arbitrage-free stock prices)

Lesson 1's three-state market without the fund: a bond paying \((1, 1, 1)\) at 0.96 and a stock paying \((1.4, 1.1, 0.6)\) at price \(c\). Strictly positive state prices must satisfy \(\psi_1 + \psi_2 + \psi_3 = 0.96\) and \(1.4\psi_1 + 1.1\psi_2 + 0.6\psi_3 = c\). The second sum is a weighted average of the payoffs \(1.4, 1.1, 0.6\) with strictly positive weights \(\psi_s/0.96\), multiplied by 0.96. As the weights range over the open simplex, the average ranges over the open interval \((0.6, 1.4)\), so state prices exist with \(\psi \gg 0\) exactly when \(0.96 \times 0.6 < c < 0.96 \times 1.4\), that is, \(0.576 < c < 1.344\). At \(c = 0.576\), buying one stock and selling 0.6 bonds costs nothing and pays \((0.8, 0.5, 0)\): an arbitrage. At \(c = 1.344\), selling one stock and buying 1.4 bonds costs nothing and pays \((0, 0.3, 0.8)\).

Lab 1 · Searching for strictly positive state prices

The lab solves the linear program \(\max\, t\) subject to \(X^\top\psi = p\), \(\psi \ge t\,\mathbf 1\) and \(t \le 1\), whose optimum is positive exactly when a strictly positive state-price vector exists. For the market of Example 3.4, the optimum should be positive for stock prices strictly inside \((0.576, 1.344)\), zero at the two ends, where state prices exist but one of them must be zero, and negative outside, where no nonnegative state-price vector exists.

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray
from scipy.optimize import linprog  # type: ignore[import-untyped]

# The three-state market of Lesson 1: a bond paying 1 and a stock. Illustrative numbers.
X = np.array([[1.0, 1.4], [1.0, 1.1], [1.0, 0.6]])
bond_price = 0.96


def strictly_positive_state_prices(prices: NDArray[np.float64]) -> tuple[float, NDArray[np.float64]]:
    """Largest t such that some psi >= t (componentwise, t <= 1) solves X' psi = prices; returns (t, psi).

    Theorem 3.1: no arbitrage holds exactly when the optimum t is strictly positive.
    """
    n_states = X.shape[0]
    c = np.zeros(n_states + 1)
    c[-1] = -1.0  # maximize t
    a_eq = np.hstack([X.T, np.zeros((X.shape[1], 1))])
    a_ub = np.hstack([-np.eye(n_states), np.ones((n_states, 1))])  # t - psi_s <= 0
    bounds = [(None, None)] * n_states + [(None, 1.0)]
    res = linprog(c, A_ub=a_ub, b_ub=np.zeros(n_states), A_eq=a_eq, b_eq=prices, bounds=bounds, method="highs")
    return float(res.x[-1]), np.asarray(res.x[:-1])


print("stock price   max min-state-price   no arbitrage?")
for stock_price in (0.50, 0.576, 0.976, 1.344, 1.40):
    t, psi = strictly_positive_state_prices(np.array([bond_price, stock_price]))
    print(f"{stock_price:11.3f}   {t:+.6f}            {t > 1e-12}")
print(f"open interval of arbitrage-free stock prices: ({0.6 * bond_price:.3f}, {1.4 * bond_price:.3f})")
stock price   max min-state-price   no arbitrage?
      0.500   -0.058462            False
      0.576   -0.000000            False
      0.976   +0.307692            True
      1.344   -0.000000            False
      1.400   -0.050909            False
open interval of arbitrage-free stock prices: (0.576, 1.344)

The sign of the optimum reproduces the interval of Example 3.4. At \(c = 0.976\) the optimum is \(0.307692 = 4/13\), attained by \(\psi = (4/13, 4/13, 0.96 - 8/13)\): set \(\psi_1 = \psi_2 = t\) and \(\psi_3 = 0.96 - 2t\) in the stock's equation, \(2.5t + 0.6(0.96 - 2t) = 0.976\), so \(1.3t = 0.4\). At the ends of the interval the optimum is zero, so the best the market can do is a state price of exactly zero, and Theorem 3.1 says there is an arbitrage. The lab tests the condition of Theorem 3.1 numerically; it does not prove it, and a solver's tolerance decides how close to zero counts as zero. Lesson 3 turns the same idea into an exact test.

The proof of Theorem 3.1 is constructive enough to run. The next lab minimizes the distance from the simplex to the cash-flow subspace and reads the state prices off the separating vector, exactly as the proof does.

Lab 2 · Running the separating-hyperplane proof

With the stock at 0.976, the market has no arbitrage, so the cash-flow subspace \(L \subseteq \R^4\) misses the simplex. Minimizing \(\|Qk\|\) over the simplex by projected gradient descent should give a vector \(w = Qk^\ast\) that is orthogonal to \(L\) and strictly positive, and \(\psi = w_{1:3}/w_0\) should price the bond and the stock exactly.

import numpy as np

# The separating-hyperplane proof of Theorem 3.1, run on the three-state market with the stock at 0.976.
X = np.array([[1.0, 1.4], [1.0, 1.1], [1.0, 0.6]])
p = np.array([0.96, 0.976])
n_states = X.shape[0]

# L = {(-p'theta, X theta)}: a 2-dimensional subspace of R^4, spanned by the columns of G.
G = np.vstack([-p, X])
P = G @ np.linalg.solve(G.T @ G, G.T)  # orthogonal projection onto L
resid = np.eye(n_states + 1) - P  # projection onto the orthogonal complement of L

# Minimize ||(I - P) k|| over the simplex K = {k >= 0, sum k = 1} by projected gradient descent.
k = np.full(n_states + 1, 1.0 / (n_states + 1))
for _ in range(20000):
    k = k - 0.5 * resid @ k
    # Euclidean projection onto the simplex (sort-and-threshold).
    u = np.sort(k)[::-1]
    css = np.cumsum(u)
    rho = int(np.nonzero(u * np.arange(1, k.size + 1) > css - 1.0)[0][-1])
    k = np.maximum(k - (css[rho] - 1.0) / (rho + 1), 0.0)
w = resid @ k  # the separating vector of the proof
print("separating vector w:", np.round(w, 6))
print(
    "w is orthogonal to L:", bool(np.allclose(G.T @ w, 0.0, atol=1e-10)), "| w strictly positive:", bool(np.all(w > 0))
)
psi = w[1:] / w[0]
print("state prices psi = w[1:] / w[0]:", np.round(psi, 6))
print("X' psi:", np.round(X.T @ psi, 6), "| quoted prices:", p)
separating vector w: [0.235219 0.072375 0.072375 0.08106 ]
w is orthogonal to L: True | w strictly positive: True
state prices psi = w[1:] / w[0]: [0.307692 0.307692 0.344615]
X' psi: [0.96  0.976] | quoted prices: [0.96  0.976]

The separating vector is strictly positive, as the proof requires, and dividing by its first entry gives state prices \((0.307692, 0.307692, 0.344615)\) that reproduce both prices. These are one of infinitely many strictly positive state-price vectors for this market (Lesson 1, Proposition 2.6): the proof picks the one that comes from the point of the simplex closest to \(L\). The lab shows that the existence proof computes something; it does not make projected gradient descent a good algorithm for the problem, which a linear program solves exactly.

4Prices of new payoffs

Lesson 1 left a payoff outside the payoff space \(M\) unpriced by the law of one price. No arbitrage does restrict it.

Proposition 4.1 (an arbitrage-free price for a new payoff)

Let \((X, p)\) have no arbitrage, and let \(z \in \R^S\) be any payoff. Adding \(z\) at price \(c\) keeps the market free of arbitrage if \(c = \psi^\top z\) for some strictly positive state-price vector \(\psi\) of \((X, p)\). Conversely, if the enlarged market has no arbitrage, then \(c = \psi^\top z\) for some such \(\psi\).

Proof

Apply Theorem 3.1 to the enlarged market with payoff matrix \((X \;\; z)\) and prices \((p, c)\). It has no arbitrage iff some \(\psi \gg 0\) satisfies \(X^\top\psi = p\) and \(z^\top\psi = c\), that is, iff \(c = \psi^\top z\) for a strictly positive state-price vector of the original market.

The set \(\{\psi^\top z : \psi \gg 0,\ X^\top\psi = p\}\) is the image of a convex set under a linear map, so it is an interval. If \(z \in M\) it is the single point \(q(z)\); otherwise it can be a nondegenerate interval. Lesson 3 computes its end points by linear programming and shows they are the costs of the cheapest super-replicating and dearest sub-replicating portfolios.

5Where the canon breaks

  • Frictions turn the subspace into a cone. The proof needs the set of attainable cash flows to be a subspace. A short-sale ban on asset \(n\) restricts \(\theta_n \ge 0\); a bid–ask spread makes the cost convex rather than linear. The set of attainable cash flows becomes a convex cone, and the separation argument then gives state prices that satisfy inequalities rather than equations: the price of a shortable-only-at-a-cost asset need not equal \(\psi^\top x_n\), only lie between bid and ask bounds. Problem R2 works the short-sale case.
  • No arbitrage is a statement about prices, not about expected returns. Theorem 3.1 holds for any strictly positive probabilities \(\pi\); the probabilities never appear. An investor who believes a stock has a high expected return is not facing an arbitrage, however attractive the bet. What practitioners call arbitrage is often a convergence trade that can lose money before it converges. Shleifer and Vishny model arbitrage as conducted by a relatively small number of highly specialized investors who take large positions using other people's money, and argue that it then becomes ineffective in extreme circumstances, when prices diverge far from fundamental values. That is a statement about trades that bear risk, which Definition 1.1 excludes.
  • The theorem is about one period and finitely many states. With many trading dates the portfolio can change along the way; Lesson 6 shows that, with finitely many states and dates, the theorem survives node by node. With infinitely many states or continuous trading it needs a different topology and a different notion of arbitrage, and Lesson 6 records the precise published statements.
  • Data rarely show an arbitrage at the quoted prices. An apparent violation of Theorem 3.1 in recorded prices can come from stale quotes, quotes that were not simultaneously executable, or fees. A strictly positive state-price vector fitted to noisy prices is evidence that the prices are consistent, not that each quote was tradable.

6Misconceptions

  1. “No arbitrage and the law of one price are the same condition.”

    No arbitrage implies the law of one price, but not conversely (Proposition 1.2): a market with no redundant asset satisfies the law of one price at any prices, including a negative price for a positive payoff, which is an arbitrage.

  2. “No arbitrage means the state prices are unique and positive.”

    It means some state-price vector is strictly positive. Uniqueness is a separate condition, \(\operatorname{rank} X = S\) (Lesson 1, Proposition 2.6). In Lab 2 the proof picks one positive vector among infinitely many.

  3. “An asset with a high expected return relative to its risk is an arbitrage opportunity.”

    An arbitrage never loses in any state (Definition 1.1). Expected returns depend on the probabilities, which Theorem 3.1 never uses; a strategy that can lose money in some state is a bet, however favourable.

  4. “If a state price is zero, the market still has no arbitrage, because zero is not negative.”

    Theorem 3.1 requires strictly positive state prices. Example 3.4 at \(c = 0.576\) has the nonnegative state-price vector \((0, 0, 0.96)\), and buying the stock against 0.6 bonds is an arbitrage that pays in states 1 and 2.

7Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Find the arbitrage

Two states. A bond pays \((1, 1)\) at 0.95 and a stock pays \((1.2, 0.9)\) at 1.16. Is there an arbitrage? If so, give one.

Solution

The state prices solve \(\psi_1 + \psi_2 = 0.95\) and \(1.2\psi_1 + 0.9\psi_2 = 1.16\). Subtracting \(0.9\) times the first from the second, \(0.3\psi_1 = 1.16 - 0.855 = 0.305\), so \(\psi_1 = 1.01667\) and \(\psi_2 = -0.06667 < 0\). The state prices are unique (rank 2 = S) and not strictly positive, so by Theorem 3.1 there is an arbitrage. The stock is too expensive relative to the bond: the largest arbitrage-free stock price is \(0.95 \times 1.2 = 1.14\). Sell one stock and buy 1.2 bonds: cost \(-1.16 + 1.14 = -0.02\), payoff \((-1.2 + 1.2, -0.9 + 1.2) = (0, 0.3) \ge 0\).

M2 · The arbitrage-free interval

A bond pays \((1, 1, 1, 1)\) at 0.98. A stock pays \((0.7, 0.9, 1.1, 1.5)\). For which stock prices is the market free of arbitrage?

Solution

As in Example 3.4, \(c = 0.98 \sum_s w_s x_s\) with strictly positive weights \(w_s = \psi_s/0.98\) summing to one, so \(c\) ranges over the open interval \((0.98 \times 0.7, 0.98 \times 1.5) = (0.686, 1.47)\). The two middle states do not affect the interval; they only add freedom in the state prices.

M3 · Check a state-price vector

In M2 with the stock at 1.078, verify that \(\psi = (0.14, 0.2, 0.4, 0.24)\) is a strictly positive state-price vector, and find a second one.

Solution

\(0.14 + 0.2 + 0.4 + 0.24 = 0.98\), the bond's price, and \(0.7 \times 0.14 + 0.9 \times 0.2 + 1.1 \times 0.4 + 1.5 \times 0.24 = 0.098 + 0.18 + 0.44 + 0.36 = 1.078\), the stock's. All entries are positive. Any direction \(d\) with \(\sum_s d_s = 0\) and \(\sum_s x_s d_s = 0\) preserves both prices; \(d = (1, -2, 1, 0)\) works, because \(0.7 - 1.8 + 1.1 = 0\). Then \(\psi + 0.05\,d = (0.19, 0.1, 0.45, 0.24)\) is strictly positive and prices both assets: its sum is 0.98 and \(0.133 + 0.09 + 0.495 + 0.36 = 1.078\). The rank is 2 with 4 states, so the state-price vectors form a plane (Lesson 1, Proposition 2.6), and its strictly positive part is an open polygon.

P1 · Stiemke's alternative from Theorem 3.1

Let \(A \in \R^{m \times n}\). Prove that exactly one of the following holds: (a) there is \(y \in \R^m\) with \(y \gg 0\) and \(A^\top y = 0\); (b) there is \(x \in \R^n\) with \(Ax \ge 0\) and \(Ax \ne 0\).

Solution

Consider the market with \(S = m\) states, \(N = n\) assets, payoff matrix \(X = A\) and every price zero, \(p = 0\). A portfolio \(\theta\) costs \(p^\top\theta = 0\), so its cash-flow vector is \((0, A\theta)\), and it is an arbitrage exactly when \(A\theta \ge 0\) and \(A\theta \ne 0\). So the market has no arbitrage iff (b) fails. By Theorem 3.1 it has no arbitrage iff some \(\psi \gg 0\) has \(A^\top\psi = p = 0\), which is (a). Hence (a) holds iff (b) fails: exactly one holds. Statements of this kind, a linear system against a sign condition, are called theorems of the alternative; Farkas' lemma, used in Lesson 3, is another.

P2 · Positive prices and no arbitrage

Prove that the market has no arbitrage if and only if (a) the law of one price holds and (b) its linear pricing functional \(q\) on \(M\) satisfies \(q(z) > 0\) for every \(z \in M\) with \(z \ge 0\), \(z \ne 0\).

Solution

(⇒) Proposition 1.2 gives (a), and Corollary 3.3 gives (b). (⇐) Suppose (a) and (b), and let \(X\theta \ge 0\), \(p^\top\theta \le 0\). Write \(z = X\theta \in M\); then \(q(z) = p^\top\theta \le 0\). If \(z \ne 0\), (b) gives \(q(z) > 0\), a contradiction; so \(z = 0\), and then (a) gives \(p^\top\theta = q(0) = 0\). The cash-flow vector is zero, so \(\theta\) is not an arbitrage. This is the form in which the theorem extends: a strictly positive linear functional on \(M\) that extends to a strictly positive linear functional on all payoffs.

P3 · Where the simplex matters

In the proof of Theorem 3.1 (⇒), why does Lemma 2.1 need the simplex \(K\) and not the whole orthant \(\R^{1+S}_+ \setminus \{0\}\)? Give the step that fails.

Solution

Lemma 2.1 needs \(K\) compact, so that the distance to \(L\) is attained at some \(k^\ast\) and \(w = Qk^\ast \ne 0\). The set \(\R^{1+S}_+ \setminus \{0\}\) is neither bounded nor closed. Its distance to \(L\) is zero (scale any of its points towards the origin: \(\|Q(tk)\| = t\|Qk\| \to 0\)), so the infimum is 0 and is not attained, and the construction yields \(w = 0\), which separates nothing. The simplex removes the scaling freedom: it contains one point on each ray of the orthant, and \(L\), a subspace, is invariant under scaling, so missing the simplex is equivalent to missing the orthant minus the origin.

Empirical

E1 · Lab 1 at more prices

Extend Lab 1 to a grid of stock prices from 0.5 to 1.4 in steps of 0.01. Predict the shape of the optimum \(t\) as a function of \(c\) before running.

Solution

The optimum is the largest attainable smallest entry of a state-price vector. Inside \((0.576, 1.344)\) it is positive, and as a function of \(c\) it is concave, because the optimal value of a maximization linear program is concave in its right-hand side. It rises from 0 at \(c = 0.576\) to a peak and falls back to 0 at \(c = 1.344\). The peak is where all three state prices can be equal, \(\psi = (0.32, 0.32, 0.32)\), at \(c = 0.32 \times (1.4 + 1.1 + 0.6) = 0.992\), with optimum 0.32; no state-price vector can have a smallest entry above \(0.96/3 = 0.32\). Outside the interval the optimum is negative. Change the loop to for stock_price in np.arange(0.5, 1.4001, 0.01): to check.

E2 · How tight is the interval?

In Example 3.4, how wide is the arbitrage-free interval relative to the bond price, and what determines it? What happens to it if a fourth state with stock payoff 2.0 is added?

Solution

The interval is \(0.96 \times (\min_s x_s, \max_s x_s)\): its width is the bond price times the range of the stock's payoff, \(0.96 \times 0.8 = 0.768\). It depends only on the extreme payoffs, never on the probabilities. A fourth state with payoff 2.0 raises the upper end to \(0.96 \times 2.0 = 1.92\), whatever the probability of that state, as long as it is positive. This is why no-arbitrage bounds are wide: a payoff that is possible at all, however unlikely, moves them.

E3 · Lab 2 with an arbitrage

Rerun Lab 2 with the stock at 1.40. What should the projected-gradient loop produce, and why?

Solution

At 1.40 the market has an arbitrage (Example 3.4), so \(L\) meets the simplex and the minimum distance is zero. The loop drives \(\|Qk\|\) towards zero and \(w = Qk\) towards the zero vector, so the positivity check holds at best by rounding, and \(\psi = w_{1:3}/w_0\) is a ratio of numbers near zero, with no meaning. The point \(k\) the loop approaches lies in \(L \cap K\): it is the normalized cash-flow vector of an arbitrage. One such vector: selling one stock and buying 1.4 bonds costs \(-1.40 + 1.344 = -0.056\) and pays \((0, 0.3, 0.8)\), so its cash-flow vector is \((0.056, 0, 0.3, 0.8)\), and divided by its sum, 1.156, it lies in \(L \cap K\).

Research

R1 · An arbitrage in a published price

A colleague finds that a fitted strictly positive state-price vector fails to exist for the closing prices of a set of options on one day. List the explanations you would check, in order, before calling it an arbitrage.

Solution
  1. Data. Were all prices recorded at the same moment? Closing prices of options and the underlying often are not.
  2. Bid or ask. A state-price vector must price each option between its bid and ask, not at a single mid quote (Section 5).
  3. Payoffs. Are the payoffs specified right: exercise style, dividends, settlement, contract multiplier? A mis-specified payoff column creates a spurious violation.
  4. Executability. Could the arbitrage portfolio have been traded in the size needed, with its margin and financing?

Only a violation that survives all four, with bids and asks, is an arbitrage in the sense of Definition 1.1.

R2 · No arbitrage under a short-sale ban

Suppose asset 2 cannot be sold short: portfolios must have \(\theta_2 \ge 0\). State the version of Theorem 3.1 for this market, and prove the direction "state prices exist ⇒ no arbitrage".

Solution

Statement. There is no \(\theta\) with \(\theta_2 \ge 0\) that is an arbitrage if and only if there is \(\psi \gg 0\) with \(\psi^\top x_n = p_n\) for every \(n \ne 2\) and \(\psi^\top x_2 \le p_2\).

⇐. Let \(\theta\) have \(\theta_2 \ge 0\), \(X\theta \ge 0\), \(p^\top\theta \le 0\). Then \(0 \ge p^\top\theta = \sum_{n\ne2} \theta_n \psi^\top x_n + \theta_2 p_2 \ge \sum_{n} \theta_n\psi^\top x_n = \psi^\top X\theta \ge 0\), using \(\theta_2 p_2 \ge \theta_2\psi^\top x_2\). So \(\psi^\top X\theta = 0\), and as in Theorem 3.1, \(X\theta = 0\); then \(p^\top\theta \ge \psi^\top X\theta = 0\) gives \(p^\top\theta = 0\). No arbitrage.

The asset that cannot be shorted may be overpriced relative to the state prices, because the trade that would exploit the overpricing, selling it, is not allowed. The converse direction needs separation of a cone (not a subspace) from the simplex; the argument of Lemma 2.1 goes through with \(L\) replaced by the closed convex cone of attainable cash flows, but the first-order condition then gives inequalities.

R3 · Is the theorem testable?

Theorem 3.1 is a mathematical equivalence. In what sense, if any, can it be tested on data?

Solution

The theorem itself cannot fail: it is proved. What can be tested is whether a given data set, with a given specification of states and payoffs, admits strictly positive state prices. A failure rejects the joint hypothesis of the specification (the states, the payoffs, the simultaneity of the prices) and of no arbitrage at those prices. A success shows only consistency. The test is sharpest when the payoffs are contractual (options at the same expiry on one underlying), so that the specification is not in doubt, and weakest when the states have to be modelled.
