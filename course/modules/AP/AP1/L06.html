Martingale Measures and the General Theorem

  • Uses: the fundamental theorem of asset pricing, the stochastic discount factor, state prices, arbitrage opportunities, market completeness, risk-neutral probabilities, the Radon–Nikodym theorem, filtrations, martingales, conditional expectation, doubling strategies, stopping times
  • Introduces: the equivalent martingale measure
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Option desks price by taking expectations under a risk-neutral measure and discounting at the riskless rate. That measure is the stochastic discount factor of Lesson 4 in disguise: a change of probability whose density is the SDF scaled by the riskless rate. This lesson makes the disguise exact, extends the fundamental theorem to many trading dates on a finite tree, and shows why the doubling strategy of M6, which wins with probability one, is not an arbitrage under the rules that make the theorem true. It ends with the precise statements of the general theorem for infinite state spaces and continuous trading, cited to the papers that prove them.

1Risk-neutral probabilities in one period

Take the one-period market \((X, p)\) with \(K\) assets \(x_k\) (columns of \(X\), \(X_{sk}\) the payoff of asset \(k\) in state \(s\)) on a finite \(\Omega\) with \(\pi \gg 0\), and suppose a riskless payoff \(\mathbf 1 \in M\) with price \(1/R_f > 0\). Write \(\Prob\) for the probability \(\pi\).

Definition 1.1 (equivalent martingale measure, one period)

A probability \(\mathbb Q\) on \(\Omega\) is equivalent to \(\Prob\) if both give positive probability to the same events; on a finite \(\Omega\) with \(\pi \gg 0\), this means \(\mathbb Q(\{s\}) > 0\) for every \(s\). An equivalent martingale measure (EMM) is a probability \(\mathbb Q\) equivalent to \(\Prob\) under which every traded payoff, discounted at the riskless rate, has expectation equal to its price: \[ p_k = \frac{1}{R_f}\,\E^{\mathbb Q}[x_k] \qquad (k = 1, \dots, K). \] In the multiperiod setting of Section 3, the condition becomes that discounted prices are \(\mathbb Q\)-martingales, which is where the name comes from.

Theorem 1.2 (EMMs are normalized strictly positive SDFs)

With a riskless payoff traded at price \(1/R_f > 0\), the map \(m \mapsto \mathbb Q_m\), \(\mathbb Q_m(\{s\}) = R_f\,\pi_s m_s\), is a bijection from strictly positive SDFs onto EMMs. Its inverse is \(m = \frac{1}{R_f}\frac{d\mathbb Q}{d\Prob}\), with \(\frac{d\mathbb Q}{d\Prob}(s) = \mathbb Q(\{s\})/\pi_s\). Consequently the market has no arbitrage if and only if an EMM exists, and, under no arbitrage, the EMM is unique if and only if the market is complete.

Proof

Let \(m \gg 0\) be an SDF. \(\mathbb Q_m(\{s\}) = R_f\pi_s m_s > 0\), and \(\sum_s \mathbb Q_m(\{s\}) = R_f\E[m] = R_f \cdot \tfrac{1}{R_f} = 1\), because \(m\) prices the riskless payoff. So \(\mathbb Q_m\) is a probability equivalent to \(\Prob\). For each asset, \(\tfrac{1}{R_f}\E^{\mathbb Q_m}[x_k] = \tfrac1{R_f}\sum_s R_f\pi_s m_s X_{sk} = \E[m x_k] = p_k\). So \(\mathbb Q_m\) is an EMM. Conversely, if \(\mathbb Q\) is an EMM, \(m_s = \mathbb Q(\{s\})/(R_f\pi_s)\) is strictly positive and \(\E[m x_k] = \tfrac1{R_f}\E^{\mathbb Q}[x_k] = p_k\), so \(m\) is a strictly positive SDF, and the two maps are inverse to each other. The density \(\mathbb Q(\{s\})/\pi_s\) is the Radon–Nikodym derivative of \(\mathbb Q\) with respect to \(\Prob\) on a finite space. The existence claim follows from Lesson 4's Theorem 2.2 (no arbitrage ⇔ a strictly positive SDF exists) through the bijection. For uniqueness, assume no arbitrage, so a strictly positive SDF \(m\) exists. If the market is complete, Lesson 4's Corollary 3.3 says \(m\) is the only SDF, so there is one EMM. If it is not, \(M \ne \R^\Omega\) and there is a nonzero \(\varepsilon \in M^\perp\); every \(m + t\varepsilon\) is an SDF, and for \(|t|\) small enough it is still strictly positive, because \(\Omega\) is finite. Two distinct strictly positive SDFs give two distinct EMMs through the bijection.

In the notation of state prices, \(\mathbb Q(\{s\}) = R_f\psi_s\): risk-neutral probabilities are state prices scaled to sum to one. The scaling factor is the price of the riskless payoff, \(\sum_s \psi_s = 1/R_f\). For a textbook treatment, Cochrane's Section 3.2, "Risk-Neutral Probabilities", treats risk-neutral probabilities for contingent claims.

Example 1.3 (the risk-neutral measure of \(x^\ast\))

In Lesson 4's bond-and-stock market, \(\pi = (0.25, 0.5, 0.25)\), \(R_f = 1/0.96\), and \(x^\ast = (0.824242, 0.940606, 1.134545)\) is strictly positive. Its EMM is \(\mathbb Q(\{s\}) = R_f\pi_s x^\ast_s = (0.214646, 0.489899, 0.295455)\), which sums to one, with density \(d\mathbb Q/d\Prob = R_f x^\ast = (0.858586, 0.979798, 1.181818)\). Check the stock: \(\E^{\mathbb Q}[x_{\text{stock}}]/R_f = 0.96 \times (0.214646 \times 1.4 + 0.489899 \times 1.1 + 0.295455 \times 0.6) = 0.96 \times 1.016667 = 0.976\). The ends of the segment of nonnegative state prices, \((0.5, 0, 0.46)\) and \((0, 0.8, 0.16)\), give \(\mathbb Q = (0.520833, 0, 0.479167)\) and \((0, 0.833333, 0.166667)\): they price both assets, but they put zero probability on a state, so they are not equivalent to \(\Prob\) and are not EMMs.

2A multiperiod market on a finite tree

Now let trade happen at dates \(t = 0, 1, \dots, n\), for a horizon of \(n\) periods. Uncertainty is a finite \(\Omega\) with \(\Prob(\{\omega\}) > 0\) for every \(\omega\), and information is a filtration \(\mathcal F_0 \subseteq \mathcal F_1 \subseteq \dots \subseteq \mathcal F_n\), with \(\mathcal F_0 = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}\). On a finite space each \(\mathcal F_t\) is generated by a partition of \(\Omega\); a cell of that partition is a node at date \(t\), and the cells of \(\mathcal F_{t+1}\) inside a node are its successors. There are \(K\) risky assets with prices \(S_t = (S^1_t, \dots, S^K_t)\), \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable (constant on each date-\(t\) node), and a bond with price \(B_t > 0\), \(\mathcal F_{t-1}\)-measurable for \(t \ge 1\) (known one period ahead) and \(B_0 = 1\). No asset pays dividends before \(n\). Discounted prices are \(\tilde S_t = S_t/B_t\).

Definition 2.1 (trading strategy, gains, arbitrage)

A trading strategy is a predictable process \(\phi = (\phi_t)_{t=1}^n\), \(\phi_t \in \R^K\) being \(\mathcal F_{t-1}\)-measurable: the holdings of the risky assets over \((t-1, t]\), chosen with the information at \(t-1\). Financed by borrowing or lending in the bond, it is self-financing, and its discounted gain is the martingale transform \[ G_n(\phi) = \sum_{t=1}^n \phi_t^\top (\tilde S_t - \tilde S_{t-1}). \] An arbitrage is a strategy with \(G_n(\phi) \ge 0\) in every state and \(G_n(\phi) > 0\) in some state: it starts from zero wealth and ends with a nonnegative, sometimes positive, discounted wealth.

The strategy starts from zero: any initial purchase is financed by the bond, and every later rebalancing is financed the same way, so the bond holding is whatever keeps the portfolio's value equal to the accumulated gains. Measuring in units of the bond (discounting by \(B_t\)) makes the bond's own price constant, so only the risky assets contribute to the discounted gain.

3The fundamental theorem on a finite tree

Definition 3.1 (equivalent martingale measure, many periods)

An equivalent martingale measure is a probability \(\mathbb Q\) on \(\Omega\) with \(\mathbb Q(\{\omega\}) > 0\) for every \(\omega\), under which each discounted price \(\tilde S^k\) is a martingale with respect to \((\mathcal F_t)\): \(\E^{\mathbb Q}[\tilde S^k_{t+1} \mid \mathcal F_t] = \tilde S^k_t\) for \(t = 0, \dots, n-1\).

Theorem 3.2 (fundamental theorem of asset pricing, finite \(\Omega\), finitely many dates)

Under the assumptions of Section 2 (finite \(\Omega\) with \(\Prob(\{\omega\}) > 0\), a filtration generated by partitions, \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable prices, a predictable positive bond, no dividends before \(n\), and any predictable \(\R^K\)-valued strategy allowed), the market has no arbitrage if and only if an equivalent martingale measure exists.

Proof

(⇐) Let \(\mathbb Q\) be an EMM and \(\phi\) a strategy. Each \(\phi_t\) is \(\mathcal F_{t-1}\)-measurable and bounded (a finite space), so by the tower property \[ \E^{\mathbb Q}\big[\phi_t^\top(\tilde S_t - \tilde S_{t-1})\big] = \E^{\mathbb Q}\big[\phi_t^\top\,\E^{\mathbb Q}[\tilde S_t - \tilde S_{t-1} \mid \mathcal F_{t-1}]\big] = 0, \] and \(\E^{\mathbb Q}[G_n(\phi)] = 0\). If \(G_n(\phi) \ge 0\) everywhere, it is a nonnegative variable with \(\mathbb Q\)-mean zero, and since \(\mathbb Q(\{\omega\}) > 0\) for every \(\omega\), \(G_n(\phi) = 0\) everywhere. So \(\phi\) is not an arbitrage.

(⇒), step 1: no arbitrage in each one-period submarket. Fix a date \(t < n\) and a node \(A\) of \(\mathcal F_t\), with successors \(A_1, \dots, A_J\). The discounted price increments \(\Delta_j = \tilde S_{t+1}(A_j) - \tilde S_t(A)\in \R^K\) define a one-period market on the \(J\) successor states: \(K\) assets with payoffs \(\Delta_j\) and price 0. Suppose some \(\eta \in \R^K\) had \(\eta^\top\Delta_j \ge 0\) for all \(j\) and \(> 0\) for some \(j\). The strategy \(\phi_{t+1} = \eta\,\indic_A\), zero at every other date, is predictable (\(A \in \mathcal F_t\)), and \(G_n(\phi) = \indic_A\,\eta^\top(\tilde S_{t+1} - \tilde S_t)\) is nonnegative and positive on some \(A_j\), which has positive probability: an arbitrage. So, under no arbitrage, each node's one-period market has none.

Step 2: one-period state prices at each node. Apply Lesson 2's Theorem 3.1 to the node's market with payoff matrix \(D \in \R^{J \times (K+1)}\) whose columns are the \(K\) increments and the constant 1, with prices \((0, \dots, 0, 1)\): the constant asset pays 1 for a price of 1, which creates no arbitrage together with the zero-price increments because the increments' market has none and the constant's cash flows cancel (Problem P1 checks this). It gives strictly positive \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_J\) with \(\sum_j \psi_j \Delta_j = 0\) and \(\sum_j \psi_j = 1\). Set the transition probability \(q(A_j \mid A) = \psi_j > 0\); then \(\sum_j q(A_j \mid A)\,\tilde S_{t+1}(A_j) = \tilde S_t(A)\).

Step 3: assemble \(\mathbb Q\). Every \(\omega\) lies in exactly one node at each date, \(A_0(\omega) = \Omega \supseteq A_1(\omega) \supseteq \dots \supseteq A_n(\omega)\). Define \(\mathbb Q(\{\omega\}) = \prod_{t=0}^{n-1} q(A_{t+1}(\omega) \mid A_t(\omega))\) if the date-\(n\) nodes are singletons; in general, divide \(\mathbb Q\) of a date-\(n\) node among its points in proportion to \(\Prob\). Each factor is positive, so \(\mathbb Q(\{\omega\}) > 0\). Summing over successors node by node, from date \(n\) back to 0, the transition probabilities out of each node sum to one, so \(\mathbb Q(\Omega) = 1\), and \(\mathbb Q(A_j \mid A) = q(A_j \mid A)\). Then \(\E^{\mathbb Q}[\tilde S_{t+1} \mid \mathcal F_t]\) on \(A\) is \(\sum_j q(A_j \mid A)\tilde S_{t+1}(A_j) = \tilde S_t(A)\) by Step 2: the discounted prices are \(\mathbb Q\)-martingales.

Harrison and Pliska prove, for a securities market on a finite, filtered probability space, that the model contains no arbitrage opportunities if and only if there is an equivalent martingale measure. Theorem 3.2 is that result for the market of Section 2, and the proof above is the standard node-by-node reduction to the one-period theorem.

Corollary 3.3 (risk-neutral valuation)

Under an EMM \(\mathbb Q\), any payoff \(H\) at date \(n\) that is attained by a strategy, \(H/B_n = c + G_n(\phi)\), has the unique arbitrage-free date-0 price \(c = \E^{\mathbb Q}[H/B_n]\), the same for every EMM.

Proof

\(\E^{\mathbb Q}[H/B_n] = c + \E^{\mathbb Q}[G_n(\phi)] = c\), by the computation in (⇐). The replicating strategy costs \(c\), so any other price creates an arbitrage by trading \(H\) against it, and \(c\) does not depend on which EMM was used, since the left side equals \(c\) for all of them.

Lab 1 · A two-period tree and its martingale measure

A SIMULATED binomial tree: the stock moves by 1.2 or 0.9 each period, the bond earns 2% a period, and \(\Prob(\text{up}) = 0.6\). Step 2 of the proof should give the one-step risk-neutral probability \(q = (1.02 - 0.9)/(1.2 - 0.9) = 0.4\) at every node, the discounted price should be a \(\mathbb Q\)-martingale at each of the three non-terminal nodes, the density \(d\mathbb Q/d\Prob\) should have \(\Prob\)-mean one, and the call struck at 100 should have one value. With a bond rate of 25%, above the stock's best return, \(q\) should exceed one and a short-stock, long-bond strategy should gain on every path.

import itertools
import math

# SIMULATED two-period tree: the stock moves up (x1.2) or down (x0.9) each period; the bond earns 2% a period.
# Discounted prices S_t / B_t must be a Q-martingale node by node (Theorem 3.2).
up, down, rate, s0, p_up = 1.2, 0.9, 0.02, 100.0, 0.6
q = (1 + rate - down) / (up - down)  # one-step risk-neutral probability of "up"
print(f"one-step q(up) = {q:.6f}  (no arbitrage needs 0 < q < 1: down < 1 + r < up)")

paths = list(itertools.product((1, 0), repeat=2))  # 1 = up
q_path = {w: math.prod(q if u else 1 - q for u in w) for w in paths}
p_path = {w: math.prod(p_up if u else 1 - p_up for u in w) for w in paths}  # the physical measure P
density = {w: q_path[w] / p_path[w] for w in paths}  # Radon-Nikodym derivative dQ/dP on the four paths
print("paths (1 = up):", paths)
print("Q of each path:", [round(q_path[w], 6) for w in paths])
print("dQ/dP on paths:", [round(density[w], 6) for w in paths])
print("E_P[dQ/dP] =", round(sum(p_path[w] * density[w] for w in paths), 12))


def stock(node: tuple[int, ...]) -> float:
    """Stock price after the moves in `node` (1 = up, 0 = down)."""
    return s0 * math.prod(up if u else down for u in node)


for node in [(), (1,), (0,)]:
    t = len(node)
    nxt = [stock((*node, u)) / (1 + rate) ** (t + 1) for u in (1, 0)]
    mart = q * nxt[0] + (1 - q) * nxt[1]
    print(f"node {node}: discounted price {stock(node) / (1 + rate) ** t:.6f}, E_Q[next discounted] {mart:.6f}")

# A call struck at 100 paying at date 2, valued as E_Q[payoff / B_2].
value = sum(q_path[w] * max(stock(w) - 100.0, 0.0) for w in paths) / (1 + rate) ** 2
print(f"call value E_Q[(S_2 - 100)^+ / B_2] = {value:.6f}")

# If the bond paid 25% a period, 1 + r > up: shorting the stock and buying bonds is an arbitrage.
bad_rate = 0.25
bad_q = (1 + bad_rate - down) / (up - down)
gain = [round(s0 * (1 + bad_rate) ** 2 - stock(w), 2) for w in paths]
print(f"rate {bad_rate:.2f}: q(up) = {bad_q:.4f}; short 1 share into bonds, date-2 gain by path: {gain}")

The martingale condition holds node by node, and the density \(d\mathbb Q/d\Prob\) takes the values \(0.444444, 1, 1, 2.25\): the measure shifts weight from the up-up path to the down-down path, which is the SDF of Theorem 1.2 at work over two periods. The call's value 10.457516 is the price of the replicating strategy; it does not use \(\Prob(\text{up}) = 0.6\) at all. At a 25% rate the "risk-neutral probability" 1.1667 is not a probability, and the arbitrage gains of 12.25 to 75.25 are the certificate of Lesson 3 in this market.

4The SDF over many periods

Theorem 1.2's correspondence extends to the tree. Given an EMM \(\mathbb Q\), let \(Z_n = d\mathbb Q/d\Prob\) and \(Z_t = \E[Z_n \mid \mathcal F_t]\), the density process. The state-price deflator \(\xi_t = Z_t/B_t\) prices the traded assets: for \(u \le t\), \(\xi_u S_u = \E[\xi_t S_t \mid \mathcal F_u]\), and \(\xi_t B_t = Z_t\) is a \(\Prob\)-martingale, so the one-period SDF from \(t\) to \(t+1\) is \(m_{t+1} = \xi_{t+1}/\xi_t\) and \(p_t = \E_t[m_{t+1}x_{t+1}]\) holds node by node for the bond and the risky assets. Problem P2 proves the identity. In a complete tree (each node's market complete) the EMM and the deflator are unique; in Lab 1 every node has two successors and two assets, so it is.

5Why the doubling strategy is not an arbitrage

In M6 the doubling strategy bets on a fair coin, doubles the stake after every loss and stops at the first win. With unlimited time it wins one unit with probability one. A fair coin is a martingale, so an investor trading a discounted price that moves like the coin, under a measure that is already risk-neutral, faces an EMM; Theorem 3.2's (⇐) says no arbitrage exists. The catch is the horizon.

Proposition 5.1 (doubling on a finite horizon)

Let the discounted price move by \(\pm 1\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each period, and play doubling with stakes \(1, 2, 4, \dots\) until the first win or until date \(n\). Then \(\Prob(\text{win by } n) = 1 - 2^{-n}\), the gain is \(+1\) on winning and \(-(2^n - 1)\) otherwise, \(\E[G_n] = 0\), and the expected deepest cumulative loss along the way is \(n/2\).

Proof

The first win occurs at flip \(k + 1\) after \(k\) losses with probability \(2^{-(k+1)}\), for \(k = 0, \dots, n-1\); no win occurs with probability \(2^{-n}\). After \(k\) losses the cumulative loss is \(1 + 2 + \dots + 2^{k-1} = 2^k - 1\), and the win at stake \(2^k\) nets \(+1\). So \(\E[G_n] = (1 - 2^{-n}) \cdot 1 - 2^{-n}(2^n - 1) = 0\). The deepest point of a path is \(2^k - 1\) when the first win follows \(k\) losses, and \(2^n - 1\) without a win: \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}2^{-(k+1)}(2^k - 1) + 2^{-n}(2^n - 1) = \tfrac n2 - (1 - 2^{-n}) + (1 - 2^{-n}) = \tfrac n2\).

On any finite horizon the strategy is a fair bet with a huge loss in a small-probability state: not an arbitrage, exactly as Theorem 3.2 says. With an infinite horizon the strategy is admissible only if its wealth is allowed to go unboundedly negative before the win. The general theorems below restrict strategies to those whose wealth is bounded below, an admissibility condition, precisely to exclude it. M6's optional stopping theorem is the probabilistic face of the same fact: the win time is a stopping time that is not bounded and at which the stopped martingale is not uniformly integrable.

Lab 2 · Doubling against a fair coin

Over 400,000 SIMULATED paths and horizons of 4, 8 and 12 flips, the win frequency should match \(1 - 2^{-n}\), the mean gain should be near zero, and the mean deepest loss should be near \(n/2\), as Proposition 5.1 says.

import numpy as np

# SIMULATED doubling strategy on a fair coin (Section 5): stake 1, double after every loss, stop at
# the first win or at the horizon n. Exactly: P(win by n) = 1 - 2^-n; gain +1 on a win, -(2^n - 1) otherwise.
rng = np.random.default_rng(404)
n_paths = 400_000
print("horizon  P(win) sim  P(win) exact  mean gain sim  exact mean gain  E[worst loss] sim  exact")
for horizon in (4, 8, 12):
    flips = rng.random((n_paths, horizon)) < 0.5  # True = win
    first_win = np.where(flips.any(axis=1), flips.argmax(axis=1), horizon)  # losses before the first win
    won = first_win < horizon
    gain = np.where(won, 1.0, -(2.0**horizon - 1.0))
    worst = 2.0**first_win - 1.0  # deepest cumulative loss reached on the path
    exact_worst = sum(0.5 ** (k + 1) * (2.0**k - 1.0) for k in range(horizon)) + 0.5**horizon * (2.0**horizon - 1.0)
    print(
        f"{horizon:7d}  {won.mean():.6f}    {1 - 0.5**horizon:.6f}      {gain.mean():+.4f}        +0.0000"
        f"           {worst.mean():8.4f}          {exact_worst:.4f}"
    )

The simulated win rates and deepest losses match the exact values to Monte Carlo error. The mean gain wanders around zero by a few hundredths in both directions: with a loss of \(2^{12} - 1 = 4{,}095\) in about one path in 4,096, the sample mean is noisy, and its sign in a given run says nothing. The expected deepest loss grows without bound with the horizon, so the "sure win" needs unbounded credit. The lab illustrates Proposition 5.1; the exclusion of the infinite-horizon strategy is a modelling choice of the general theorems, not something a simulation can show.

6The general theorem

With infinitely many states or continuous trading, two things change. The set of attainable gains is no longer finite-dimensional, so the separating-hyperplane argument of Lesson 2 needs a topology in which that set is closed; and strategies must be restricted, as Section 5 showed, or no EMM can rule out arbitrage. The published theorems resolve both, under different conditions.

  • Harrison and Kreps's Theorem 2: if the price model admits no simple free lunches, equivalent martingale measures correspond one to one to the continuous, strictly positive linear extensions of the price functional; its proof shows that a measure gives every simple self-financing strategy zero expected discounted gain exactly when discounted prices are martingales under it. The (⇐) half of Theorem 3.2 above is that computation, on a finite tree.
  • Harrison and Pliska prove the finite version: on a finite, filtered probability space, the market contains no arbitrage possibilities if and only if there is an equivalent martingale measure for the discounted price process. This is Theorem 3.2 above.
  • Delbaen and Schachermayer prove that for a locally bounded real-valued semimartingale \(S\), there is a probability measure equivalent to \(\Prob\) under which \(S\) is a local martingale if and only if \(S\) satisfies the condition of no free lunch with vanishing risk. No free lunch with vanishing risk strengthens no arbitrage: it also rules out sequences of admissible strategies whose losses vanish uniformly while their gains converge to a nonnegative, sometimes positive, limit. The local-boundedness hypothesis is part of the statement; this module does not cover the case without it.

The theorem numbers for Harrison–Pliska and Delbaen–Schachermayer were checked against Schachermayer's survey of the fundamental theorem, which quotes both statements with these numbers, and not against the original articles. These proofs need functional analysis that this module does not develop: closedness of the set of attainable gains in a suitable topology and a separation theorem in infinite dimensions. They are cited, not re-proved; SC1–SC3 develop the stochastic calculus they rest on.

7Where the canon breaks

  • \(\mathbb Q\) is not a forecast. Risk-neutral probabilities are state prices times \(R_f\) (Theorem 1.2). They overweight the states the SDF values highly, which are bad states for investors. Reading \(\mathbb Q\) as a forecast of what will happen confuses price with probability; Lab 1's \(\mathbb Q\) puts 0.36 on the down-down path whose physical probability is 0.16.
  • Incompleteness. In an incomplete market there are many EMMs and Corollary 3.3 prices only attained payoffs. A payoff that cannot be replicated has an interval of arbitrage-free prices, the multiperiod version of Lesson 3's bounds, and choosing one EMM is a modelling assumption, not a consequence of no arbitrage.
  • Admissibility is a modelling choice. The general theorems allow only strategies with wealth bounded below. That rules out doubling, but it also makes the conclusion depend on a credit constraint written into the model. With different constraints (short-sale bans, margin requirements), the set of attainable gains changes, and Theorem 3.2 no longer applies as stated; the module does not develop the constrained theorems.
  • Frictions in continuous time. The continuous-time theorem assumes frictionless trading at every instant. With proportional costs, continuous rebalancing is infinitely expensive, so the frictionless theorem does not describe such a market; this module does not cover the theory that replaces it. DV1 and QR5 meet the consequences for hedging.

8Misconceptions

  1. “The risk-neutral measure assumes investors are risk neutral.”

    It assumes nothing about preferences. It is a rescaling of a strictly positive SDF (Theorem 1.2), and the SDF can reflect any degree of risk aversion. Under \(\mathbb Q\) every asset earns \(R_f\) in expectation because \(\mathbb Q\) has absorbed the risk premia into the probabilities.

  2. “A strategy that wins with probability one is an arbitrage.”

    Doubling wins with probability one on an infinite horizon, but it needs unbounded credit, and on every finite horizon it has mean gain zero and a loss of \(2^n - 1\) with probability \(2^{-n}\) (Proposition 5.1). Arbitrage requires a nonnegative gain in every state with admissible strategies.

  3. “Any probability that prices the assets is an equivalent martingale measure.”

    It must also be equivalent to \(\Prob\): positive on every state that \(\Prob\) makes possible. Example 1.3's measures \((0.520833, 0, 0.479167)\) price both assets but give a possible state zero probability, and correspond to an arbitrage-boundary price, not to no arbitrage.

  4. “The fundamental theorem in continuous time says: no arbitrage if and only if there is an EMM.”

    The Delbaen–Schachermayer theorem needs the stronger condition of no free lunch with vanishing risk, admissible (wealth bounded below) strategies, and for locally bounded prices gives a local martingale measure, not a martingale measure. Plain no arbitrage is not enough in general.

Mechanics

M1 · Risk-neutral probabilities from state prices

Lesson 1's two-state market has state prices \((0.448, 0.522)\). Find the risk-neutral probabilities and the density \(d\mathbb Q/d\Prob\) when \(\Prob = (0.6, 0.4)\).

Solution

\(R_f = 1/(0.448 + 0.522) = 1/0.97\). \(\mathbb Q = R_f\psi = (0.448/0.97, 0.522/0.97) = (0.461856, 0.538144)\). Density \(\mathbb Q/\Prob = (0.769759, 1.345361)\), with \(\Prob\)-mean \(0.6 \times 0.769759 + 0.4 \times 1.345361 = 0.461856 + 0.538144 = 1\). It equals \(R_f\,m\), with \(m = (0.746667, 1.305)\) from Lesson 4's Problem M1.

M2 · A put on a two-period tree

A stock at 100 moves by 1.1 or 0.95 each period; the bond earns 1% a period. Price a European put struck at 100 maturing at date 2.

Solution

\(q = (1.01 - 0.95)/(1.1 - 0.95) = 0.4\). Terminal prices: 121, 104.5, 90.25; put payoffs: 0, 0, 9.75. Only the down-down path pays, with \(\mathbb Q\)-probability \(0.6^2 = 0.36\). Value \(= 0.36 \times 9.75/1.01^2 = 3.51/1.0201 = 3.440839\).

M3 · Which measures are EMMs?

Three states with \(\Prob = (0.25, 0.5, 0.25)\), a bond with \(R_f = 1/0.96\), and the stock \((1.4, 1.1, 0.6)\) at 0.976. Is \(\mathbb Q = (0.25, 0.4, 0.35)\) an EMM? Is \((0.2, 0.5, 0.3)\)?

Solution

An EMM needs \(\E^{\mathbb Q}[x_{\text{stock}}] = 0.976 \times R_f = 0.976/0.96 = 1.016667\). For \((0.25, 0.4, 0.35)\): \(0.35 + 0.44 + 0.21 = 1.0\): no. For \((0.2, 0.5, 0.3)\): \(0.28 + 0.55 + 0.18 = 1.01\): no. The EMMs form the open segment of \(\mathbb Q = R_f\psi\) with \(\psi\) strictly inside the segment of Example 1.3, for example \(\mathbb Q = (0.260417, 0.416667, 0.322917)\): \(0.364583 + 0.458333 + 0.19375 = 1.016667\) ✓.

Proof

P1 · The node market of Step 2

In Step 2 of the proof of Theorem 3.2, show that the node's market with assets \(\Delta\) (price 0) and the constant (price 1) has no arbitrage, so that Lesson 2's Theorem 3.1 applies, and that its state prices sum to one.

Solution

A portfolio \((\eta, a)\) costs \(a\) and pays \(\eta^\top\Delta_j + a\) in successor \(j\). If it were an arbitrage, \(-a \ge 0\) and \(\eta^\top\Delta_j + a \ge 0\) for all \(j\), with a nonzero cash flow. Then \(\eta^\top\Delta_j \ge -a \ge 0\) for all \(j\). By Step 1, \(\eta^\top\Delta_j = 0\) for all \(j\). Then \(a \ge 0\) and \(-a \ge 0\) give \(a = 0\), and all cash flows are zero: no arbitrage. Theorem 3.1 gives \(\psi \gg 0\) with \(\sum_j\psi_j\Delta_j = 0\) (the price of each increment) and \(\sum_j\psi_j = 1\) (the price of the constant).

P2 · The state-price deflator

On the tree of Section 2 with an EMM \(\mathbb Q\), let \(Z_t = \E[d\mathbb Q/d\Prob \mid \mathcal F_t]\) and \(\xi_t = Z_t/B_t\). Prove that \(\xi_t\tilde S_t B_t = \xi_t S_t\) is a \(\Prob\)-martingale, i.e. \(\E[\xi_{t+1}S_{t+1} \mid \mathcal F_t] = \xi_t S_t\).

Solution

The Bayes rule for conditional expectations under a change of measure: for an \(\mathcal F_{t+1}\)-measurable \(Y\), \(\E^{\mathbb Q}[Y \mid \mathcal F_t] = \E[Z_{t+1}Y \mid \mathcal F_t]/Z_t\). On a finite space this is a direct computation on each node \(A\): \(\E^{\mathbb Q}[Y \mid A] = \sum_{\omega \in A}\mathbb Q(\omega)Y(\omega)/\mathbb Q(A)\), and \(\mathbb Q(\omega) = Z_n(\omega)\Prob(\omega)\), \(\mathbb Q(A) = Z_t(A)\Prob(A)\), \(\sum_{\omega \in A}Z_n(\omega)\Prob(\omega)Y(\omega) = \Prob(A)\,\E[Z_{t+1}Y \mid A]\) by the tower property, since \(Y\) is \(\mathcal F_{t+1}\)-measurable. Now take \(Y = \tilde S_{t+1}\): \(\tilde S_t = \E^{\mathbb Q}[\tilde S_{t+1} \mid \mathcal F_t] = \E[Z_{t+1}S_{t+1}/B_{t+1} \mid \mathcal F_t]/Z_t\). Multiplying by \(Z_t\), \(\xi_tS_t = Z_t\tilde S_t = \E[Z_{t+1}S_{t+1}/B_{t+1} \mid \mathcal F_t] = \E[\xi_{t+1}S_{t+1} \mid \mathcal F_t]\).

P3 · Uniqueness on a complete tree

Suppose every node of the tree has exactly \(K + 1\) successors and the node's \((K+1) \times (K+1)\) matrix of \((\Delta_j^\top, 1)\) is invertible. Prove that the EMM is unique.

Solution

Any EMM \(\mathbb Q\) has transition probabilities \(\mathbb Q(A_j \mid A)\) that satisfy \(\sum_j \mathbb Q(A_j \mid A)\Delta_j = 0\) (the martingale condition at \(A\)) and \(\sum_j\mathbb Q(A_j \mid A) = 1\): \(K + 1\) linear equations in \(K + 1\) unknowns with an invertible matrix. So the transition probabilities are unique at every node, and \(\mathbb Q\) of each date-\(n\) node is their product along its path, unique too. (Within a date-\(n\) node, \(\mathbb Q\) is determined only up to how it divides among points, and the prices cannot distinguish them; on the filtration's \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal F_n\), \(\mathbb Q\) is unique.)

Empirical

E1 · Physical versus risk-neutral

In Lab 1, compute the expected stock return over two periods under \(\Prob\) and under \(\mathbb Q\). Which one is the bond's return, and why?

Solution

Under \(\mathbb Q\), the discounted price is a martingale, so \(\E^{\mathbb Q}[S_2] = S_0B_2 = 100 \times 1.02^2 = 104.04\): a two-period return of 4.04%, the bond's. Under \(\Prob\), each period's expected gross return is \(0.6 \times 1.2 + 0.4 \times 0.9 = 1.08\), so \(\E[S_2] = 100 \times 1.08^2 = 116.64\), a 16.64% return. The difference is the risk premium; \(\mathbb Q\) removes it by tilting probability towards the down states.

E2 · How noisy is Lab 2's mean gain?

At a horizon of 12, what is the standard deviation of the gain, and what standard error should Lab 2's mean gain have over 400,000 paths? Are the printed means consistent with zero?

Solution

\(\E[G^2] = (1 - 2^{-12}) \cdot 1 + 2^{-12}(4095)^2 = 0.999756 + 4094.000244 = 4095\), so \(\sigma(G) = \sqrt{4095} = 63.99\). The standard error is \(63.99/\sqrt{400{,}000} = 0.1012\). The printed mean +0.0477 is half a standard error from zero. At horizon 8, \(\sigma = \sqrt{255} = 15.97\), standard error 0.0252, and \(-0.0470\) is 1.9 standard errors away: borderline: a deviation this large occurs in about one run in sixteen (two-sided \(p = 0.063\)). At horizon 4, \(\sigma = \sqrt{15}\), standard error 0.0061, and +0.0099 is 1.6 standard errors away. All three are consistent with zero.

E3 · Reading \(\mathbb Q\) off option prices

On one expiry, butterfly spreads at every strike are traded. Explain how their prices give a discrete risk-neutral distribution, and what no arbitrage requires of them.

Solution

A butterfly long one call at \(K - h\), short two at \(K\), long one at \(K + h\) pays a tent of height \(h\) centred at \(K\). With strikes on a grid of spacing \(h\), the butterflies divided by \(h\) approximate the digital payoffs of the grid's states, so their prices divided by \(h\) are approximately the state prices \(\psi_K\), and \(R_f\psi_K\) is a risk-neutral probability. No arbitrage requires every butterfly price to be strictly positive (a nonnegative, nonzero payoff has a positive price, Lesson 2's Corollary 3.3), which is convexity of call prices in the strike. DV2 develops the continuous version.

Research

R1 · Choosing among many EMMs

In an incomplete tree with many EMMs, list three principled ways to choose one for pricing an unattained payoff, and what each assumes.

Solution
  • Variance-optimal martingale measure. Choose the signed martingale measure whose density is closest to one in \(L^2\), the dynamic analogue of \(x^\ast\). It assumes the unhedgeable risk earns no premium, and its density can be negative (Lesson 4, Section 5), in which case it is not an EMM. It is not in general the same as the measure built by applying the one-period \(L^2\) projection node by node; the two can differ when the one-period mean–variance tradeoff varies across nodes.
  • Minimal relative entropy. Minimize \(\E^{\mathbb Q}[\log d\mathbb Q/d\Prob]\) over EMMs. The objective is finite only for measures with a density, and it is a different notion of "closest to \(\Prob\)" from the \(L^2\) one; this module does not develop its properties.
  • Equilibrium. Take the SDF of a calibrated equilibrium model (AP3). It assumes the model is right about the unhedgeable risk.

Each gives a price inside the arbitrage interval; reporting the interval next to the chosen price shows how much of it is the choice.

R2 · An infinite-horizon arbitrage-like strategy

Construct, on a countable probability space, a strategy whose gain is \(+1\) almost surely but whose wealth is not bounded below, and explain which hypothesis of the Delbaen–Schachermayer theorem it violates.

Solution

The doubling strategy on an infinite sequence of fair coin flips: stop at the first win, which happens almost surely, with gain \(+1\). Before stopping, the wealth after \(k\) losses is \(-(2^k - 1)\), which is not bounded below over paths. The theorem applies to admissible strategies, whose wealth process is bounded below by a constant; doubling is not admissible. Truncated at any finite horizon it is admissible but is no longer a sure win (Proposition 5.1).

R3 · Is no arbitrage enough in continuous time?

Explain, without proofs, why the continuous-time theorem replaces no arbitrage with no free lunch with vanishing risk.

Solution

In infinite dimensions the set of attainable gains need not be closed, and the separation argument of Lesson 2 needs a closed convex set. A market can then have no arbitrage while a sequence of admissible strategies, whose worst losses shrink to zero uniformly, has gains converging to a nonnegative, sometimes positive, limit: an arbitrage "in the limit". No free lunch with vanishing risk rules out such sequences. Delbaen and Schachermayer show that under it the relevant set of attainable gains is closed in the needed sense, which is what makes the separation, and so the equivalence with a (local) martingale measure, go through.
