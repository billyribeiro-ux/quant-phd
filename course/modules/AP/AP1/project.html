SDF Recovery

A research group wants to know whether a stochastic discount factor recovered from one decade of prices tells it anything about the next. You will write the code that recovers the minimum-variance SDF from a set of test assets, prove the results it rests on, and run it on fourteen years of currency payoffs to measure how well it prices the following fourteen.

Rungs 1 and 2

Lab 1 · Worked example: \(x^\ast\) in population and in a sample

In Lesson 4's market, \(x^\ast = (0.824242, 0.940606, 1.134545)\) should price both assets exactly. A SIMULATED sample of eight months drawn from the same states should give a sample \(x^\ast\) that prices its own eight months exactly and a fresh eight months with nonzero errors.

import numpy as np

# Worked example: x* for Lesson 4's bond-and-stock market, then for a sample drawn from it.
probs = np.array([0.25, 0.5, 0.25])  # state probabilities: E[f] = probs @ f
X = np.array([[1.0, 1.4], [1.0, 1.1], [1.0, 0.6]])  # rows are states, columns are the bond and the stock
p = np.array([0.96, 0.976])  # prices of one unit of each asset

# Step 1. Second-moment matrix E[x x'] = X' diag(probs) X. It must be invertible: no redundant payoff.
second = X.T @ (probs[:, None] * X)
print("E[x x'] =", np.round(second, 6).tolist(), "| determinant:", round(float(np.linalg.det(second)), 6))

# Step 2. Weights b solve E[x x'] b = p; x* = x'b is the unique SDF in the payoff space (L04, Theorem 3.2).
b = np.linalg.solve(second, p)  # solve, never invert explicitly: same answer, better conditioned
x_star = X @ b
print("b =", np.round(b, 6), "| x* by state =", np.round(x_star, 6))

# Step 3. Verify that it prices both assets. This is the check the autograder runs, to rounding.
print("E[x* x] - p =", np.round(X.T @ (probs * x_star) - p, 12) + 0.0)

# Step 4. A SIMULATED sample of 8 months from the states: the sample x* uses equal weights 1/T (L07, Definition 1.1).
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
train = X[rng.choice(3, size=8, p=probs)]
test = X[rng.choice(3, size=8, p=probs)]
b_hat = np.linalg.solve(train.T @ train / len(train), p)
in_err = train.T @ (train @ b_hat) / len(train) - p  # zero by construction (L07, Proposition 2.1)
out_err = test.T @ (test @ b_hat) / len(test) - p  # the only informative number
print("sample b =", np.round(b_hat, 6), "| in-sample errors =", np.round(in_err, 12) + 0.0)
print("out-of-sample errors =", np.round(out_err, 6), "| rms =", round(float(np.sqrt(np.mean(out_err**2))), 6))
E[x x'] = [[1.0, 1.05], [1.05, 1.185]] | determinant: 0.0825
b = [ 1.367273 -0.387879] | x* by state = [0.824242 0.940606 1.134545]
E[x* x] - p = [0. 0.]
sample b = [0.22303  0.775758] | in-sample errors = [0. 0.]
out-of-sample errors = [0.048485 0.096364] | rms = 0.076278

The comments carry the reasons. The determinant check is where redundancy would show up: a zero determinant means no unique \(x^\ast\) weights (Lesson 4, Theorem 3.2 needs linearly independent payoffs). The sample weights \((0.22303, 0.775758)\) are far from the population's \((1.367273, -0.387879)\): eight draws from three states identify the payoff space but estimate its second-moment matrix badly, and the out-of-sample errors of about 5 and 10 cents per dollar of price show it. In-sample errors are zero in both cases and prove nothing about the estimate (Lesson 7, Proposition 2.1).

The first blank is \(\tfrac1T\sum_t x_tx_t^\top\), train.T @ train / len(train); dividing by \(T - 1\) instead scales the weights by \((T-1)/T\) and breaks the in-sample identity. The second solves the normal equations, np.linalg.solve(second, prices). The weight on the riskless payoff, 1.9386, is nearly twice the SDF's mean: the risky weights carry the rest. The risky payoffs' prices are their full-sample \(\E[mx]\), so the out-of-sample errors here measure estimation error alone, without any change in the economy between the halves.

Rung 3 · Specification

Context
A research group prices a set of test assets with the SDF it recovers from their own history, and wants to know whether that SDF is evidence about anything beyond the sample it was fitted to. You write the recovery code to the interface below, prove the four results the code and its interpretation rest on, and run it on nine currency payoffs: fit on the first half of the months, price the second half, and report what the errors say and do not say.
Inputs
The autograder's instance comes from grader/instance.py, seeded by your workspace ID. It holds a finite market (state probabilities, a payoff matrix, prices generated by a strictly positive SDF, with or without a riskless payoff) and a training and a test sample of states drawn from it. All of it is SIMULATED. The report uses market data: the embedded month-end spot rates fx_monthly.csv (MANIFEST id fx_monthly; Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, G.5/H.10 Foreign Exchange Rates (noon buying rates in New York), in the public domain, retrieved 2026-10-19; download the file), turned into the nine payoffs and the split by Lab 3.
Required outputs
Everything lives in a repository of your own: sdf_recovery.py at its root and the report/ folder beside it. The module sdf_recovery.py has exactly these functions, in the conventions of the next section: second_moment_matrix(payoffs), x_star_weights(payoffs, prices), sdf_values(payoffs, weights), pricing_errors(payoffs, prices, sdf), population_x_star(probs, payoffs, prices), min_variance_sdf(payoffs, prices, mean) returning (a, b), min_sdf_std(payoffs, prices, mean), and recover_sdf(train, test, prices) returning the frozen dataclass SDFRecovery. Start from the starter file, whose docstrings and dataclass are complete. For the report, commit three more files: report/run_report.py, which builds the payoffs exactly as Lab 3 does, calls your recover_sdf, and prints every number the report quotes, rounded as quoted; report/sdf_report.md (or a PDF), the report of §7 with its proof appendix; and report/self_assessment.md, the summary the self-assessment form produces once you reveal the exemplar scoring.
Constraints
NumPy and the Python standard library only. Functions are pure: they validate their inputs, raise ValueError naming the violated condition, and never print, warn or change their arguments. Every array argument (payoffs, prices, probabilities, SDF values, weights) and the mean must be finite; a NaN or an infinite value raises ValueError. A payoff sample is a two-dimensional array of observations by assets; sample moments divide by \(T\), never \(T - 1\); a pricing error is \(\E[m\,x] - p\). x_star_weights raises on redundant payoffs (linearly dependent columns) and on fewer observations than assets. min_variance_sdf and min_sdf_std raise when the payoff covariance matrix is singular, for example when a riskless payoff is among the assets: then the SDF's mean is pinned, and x_star_weights is the right function (Lesson 5, Proposition 2.2). population_x_star requires strictly positive probabilities that sum to one. The report reviews the split as specified: no other split, no other payoffs, and checks may vary only the ridge penalty of Lesson 7, labelled as a check. The whole suite must finish within 60 seconds in your browser.
Grading mode
Both modes apply (BLUEPRINT §5.2). The code is autograded by grader/test_project.py, 46 checks run with warnings as errors: the definitions, exact in-sample pricing, invariance to units and to repackaging the assets into portfolios, the projection and minimum-second-moment properties of \(x^\ast\), the minimum-variance SDF and the variance bound, the out-of-sample errors with training-sample weights, failure behaviour on invalid input, purity, and imports. Conventions, in brief

  • A sample of \(T\) observations of \(N\) payoffs is a \((T, N)\) array; row \(t\) is the payoff vector in observed state \(t\). second_moment_matrix is \(X^\top X/T\).
  • x_star_weights returns \(b = \E_T[xx^\top]^{-1}p\); sdf_values returns \(Xb\), one value per observation.
  • pricing_errors returns \(\E_T[m\,x] - p\) for given SDF values \(m\), one per asset.
  • population_x_star returns the values of \(x^\ast\) in each of \(S\) states with probabilities \(\pi\): \(X b\) with \(X^\top\diag(\pi)X\,b = p\).
  • min_variance_sdf returns \((a, b)\) with \(m = a + x^\top b\), \(b = \Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)\) and \(a = v - \mu^\top b\), where \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) are the sample mean and covariance (dividing by \(T\)); min_sdf_std returns \(\sqrt{(p - v\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)}\) (Lesson 5, Theorem 2.1).
  • recover_sdf estimates \(b\) on train only, and reports the in-sample and out-of-sample errors, their root mean square out of sample, the training mean and standard deviation of \(x^\ast\) (dividing by \(T\)), and the share of test observations with \(x^\ast < 0\).

Appendix A · Proofs

A1. The SDF must satisfy \(\E[mx] = p\). Try \(m = x^\top b\). Then \(\E[xx^\top]b = p\), so \(b = \E[xx^\top]^{-1}p\). This SDF is in the payoff space, and it is unique because the solution of a linear system is unique.

A2. Any SDF can be written \(m = x^\ast + \varepsilon\). Then \(\E[m^2] = \E[x^{\ast2}] + \E[\varepsilon^2] \ge \E[x^{\ast2}]\), so \(x^\ast\) has the smallest second moment.

A4. The minimum-variance SDF with mean \(v\) is \(v + (x - \mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)\), which is what min_variance_sdf computes, and it is the regression of any SDF on the payoffs, so it has the least variance.

  • Meets C1 level 2: the formula and an existence argument.
  • Misses C1 level 3: no assumptions stated; inverts \(\E[xx^\top]\) without showing it is invertible; "the solution of a linear system is unique" proves uniqueness among SDFs of the form \(x^\top b\) only if the system is nonsingular, which is not shown.
  • Meets C2 level 2: the Pythagorean identity and the inequality.
  • Misses C2 level 3: never shows \(\E[\varepsilon x] = 0\), which is what makes the cross term vanish, nor that every \(x^\ast + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \perp M\) is an SDF.
  • Meets C3 at level 1 only: A3 is missing.
  • Misses C4 level 3 in A4 as well: asserts minimality by analogy with regression, without the feasibility check or the variance decomposition.

Exemplar B

Official scoring: C1 4 · C2 4 · C3 4 · C4 4 · C5 3 · C6 4. Complete proofs with their assumptions, every required number with its unit and source, and a careful reading of what one split shows. It falls short of level 4 once: it notices that every test error is positive but does not propose a check that would separate a change in the payoffs' means from estimation noise.

Summary

Does the minimum-second-moment SDF recovered from 166 months of nine currency payoffs price the next 166 months? No. It prices its own months exactly, as it must, and the next 166 with a root-mean-square error of 270.0 basis points per dollar of price, against 20.1 for the constant SDF \(m = 1\). The estimate adds error rather than information.

  • Meets C6 level 4 in part: the question, a one-sentence answer and the two carrying numbers with units, in 59 words.
Data and method

The payoffs are one dollar held for one month in each of AUD, CAD, CHF, EUR, GBP, JPY, NOK, NZD and SEK, converted back at the next month-end spot rate: payoff \(S_{t+1}/S_t\), price one dollar. The rates are the Federal Reserve's G.5/H.10 noon buying rates in New York, month-end values, retrieved 2026-10-19. The payoffs omit the interest a deposit in each currency would earn, so they price currency held as cash. DKK is left out because its payoff is nearly a multiple of EUR's (Lesson 1, Lab 2: a regression \(R^2\) of 0.99891), which makes the second-moment matrix nearly singular. The sample runs from 1999-02 to 2026-09; the first 166 months (1999-02 to 2012-11) train and the last 166 (2012-12 to 2026-09) test. On the training months, \(\hat b = \hat S^{-1}p\) with \(\hat S = \tfrac1T\sum_t x_tx_t^\top\), dividing by \(T = 166\), and \(\hat x^\ast_t = x_t^\top\hat b\). The script prints the checksums of Lab 3 (sum 2989.0264134608, sum of squares 2992.6599604987), so the payoffs are the reference payoffs.

In-sample verification

The largest absolute training-sample pricing error is \(1.1 \times 10^{-14}\), rounding error. This verifies the code, not the economics: by Proposition 2.1 of Lesson 7, proved as A3 below, the sample \(x^\ast\) prices its own sample exactly for any data with an invertible \(\hat S\). Payoffs drawn at random would pass the same check.

  • Meets C4 level 4 in part: payoffs, prices, omissions, the DKK exclusion with its lesson figure, the split dates and the estimator with its moment convention.
  • Meets C5 level 3 in part: the zero in-sample errors are read as a code check, with the result that makes them uninformative.
Out-of-sample pricing

With the training weights, the test-half pricing errors, \(\E_{\text{test}}[\hat x^\ast x] - 1\), are, in basis points of the one-dollar price: AUD 249.4, CAD 266.3, CHF 289.2, EUR 295.1, GBP 301.4, JPY 243.7, NOK 248.1, NZD 259.0, SEK 271.2. Their root mean square is 270.0. The constant SDF \(m = 1\), which prices each payoff at its test-half mean, errs by 20.1. Every error is positive: the recovered SDF values every currency above its price in the test half. On the training months \(\hat x^\ast\) has mean 0.9981 and standard deviation 0.1824 per month; on the test months it stays positive, with a smallest value of 0.0401. By Lesson 5's Proposition 2.3 the training \(\hat x^\ast\) is the minimum-variance SDF at its own mean for the training sample, so 0.1824 is the sample variance bound there. By Theorem 3.3 of Lesson 7 the sample second moment of \(\hat x^\ast\), \(0.9981^2 + 0.1824^2\), overstates the population second moment in expectation; with no riskless payoff the theorem says nothing directly about the standard deviation.

Limitations

One split is one draw: a different split date or the reversed split would give a different number. The errors mix two things the design cannot separate: estimation error in \(\hat S^{-1}\), which is large because the payoffs are strongly correlated, and a change in the payoffs' distribution between 1999–2012 and 2012–2026. The estimate is unconditional; an SDF that varies with information known at each month-end would need managed payoffs. The payoffs omit deposit interest, so the result is about currency held as cash; with interest, the prices stay at one dollar but the payoffs change. Shrinkage helps but does not reverse the conclusion: Lesson 7's Lab 2 reports 80.2 basis points for ridge with a penalty chosen inside the training half. Nothing here is evidence of an arbitrage, which is a statement about every state, not about one estimate's fit.

  • Meets C4 level 4: every per-currency error, the root mean square and the benchmark with units; the shape of \(x^\ast\) on both halves; and the link to the variance bound, with the bias result stated for the second moment, which is what Theorem 3.3 covers.
  • Meets C5 level 3: one split, four limitations, the ridge figure labelled as a lesson figure, and the arbitrage reading ruled out.
  • Misses C5 level 4: notes that every error is positive but does not propose a check that would separate a shift in the payoffs' means from estimation noise, for example pricing the test half with the test half's own means and the training half's \(\hat S\).
Appendix A · Proofs

Setting. Finitely many states \(s = 1, \dots, S\) with probabilities \(\pi_s > 0\); payoffs \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_N)\) with \(N\) linearly independent columns of the payoff matrix \(X\); prices \(p\) given by some SDF, \(p = \E[mx]\) (equivalently, the law of one price holds). Inner product \(\E[yz] = \sum_s\pi_sy_sz_s\), positive definite because \(\pi \gg 0\). The sample results treat \(T\) observations as \(T\) equally likely states.

A1 (existence and uniqueness of \(x^\ast\)). For \(c \ne 0\), \(c^\top\E[xx^\top]c = \E[(x^\top c)^2] = \sum_s\pi_s(Xc)_s^2 > 0\), because \(Xc \ne 0\) by linear independence and every \(\pi_s > 0\). So \(\E[xx^\top]\) is positive definite and invertible. Let \(b = \E[xx^\top]^{-1}p\) and \(x^\ast = x^\top b \in M\). Then \(\E[x^\ast x] = \E[xx^\top]b = p\): \(x^\ast\) is an SDF in \(M\). If \(y = x^\top c \in M\) is also an SDF, then \(\E[xx^\top](b - c) = p - p = 0\), so \(b = c\) and \(y = x^\ast\). With redundant payoffs, \(\E[xx^\top]\) is singular, \(b\) is not unique, but \(x^\top b\) still is, by the same argument applied to a maximal independent subset.

A2 (projection). If \(m\) is an SDF, \(\E[(m - x^\ast)x_n] = p_n - p_n = 0\) for every \(n\), so \(\varepsilon = m - x^\ast\) is orthogonal to every element of \(M\). Conversely, if \(\varepsilon \perp M\), \(\E[(x^\ast + \varepsilon)x_n] = p_n\). Since \(x^\ast \in M\), \(\E[x^\ast\varepsilon] = 0\), and \(\E[m^2] = \E[x^{\ast2}] + 2\E[x^\ast\varepsilon] + \E[\varepsilon^2] = \E[x^{\ast2}] + \E[\varepsilon^2] \ge \E[x^{\ast2}]\), with equality iff \(\E[\varepsilon^2] = 0\) iff \(\varepsilon = 0\), using \(\pi \gg 0\). \(x^\ast\) need not be positive: Lesson 4's Example 5.1 has \(x^\ast = (1.15, 0.383333, -0.383333)\) in a market with no arbitrage. So \(x^\ast\) cannot be used to price a payoff outside \(M\) without risking an arbitrage price.

A3 (sample identity and bias). With \(\hat S = \tfrac1T\sum_tx_tx_t^\top\) invertible, \(\E_T[\hat x^\ast x] = \hat S\hat S^{-1}p = p\), for any data. For the bias, let \(f(A) = p^\top A^{-1}p\) on positive-definite \(A\). For fixed \(c\), \(g_A(c) = 2c^\top p - c^\top Ac\) is concave in \(c\) with maximum at \(c = A^{-1}p\), equal to \(f(A)\); so \(f(A) = \sup_c g_A(c)\), a supremum of functions affine in \(A\), hence convex. Assume \(\hat S\) is positive definite almost surely, \(\E[\hat S] = S\) and \(\E[f(\hat S)] < \infty\). Then \(f(S) = \sup_c\E[g_{\hat S}(c)] \le \E[\sup_cg_{\hat S}(c)] = \E[f(\hat S)]\). Since \(\E_T[\hat x^{\ast2}] = \hat b^\top\hat S\hat b = f(\hat S)\) and \(\E[x^{\ast2}] = f(S)\), the sample second moment is biased upward. It is unbiased if \(\hat S = S\) almost surely, and strictly biased for \(N = 1\) when \(\hat S\) is not constant, because \(s \mapsto p^2/s\) is strictly convex. With a riskless payoff the mean is pinned at \(1/R_f\) in every sample, so the bias is in the variance, which is the in-sample variance bound of Lesson 5.

A4 (minimum-variance SDF with mean \(v\)). Assume \(\Sigma = \Cov(x)\) is invertible and let \(\mu = \E[x]\). Let \(m_v = v + (x - \mu)^\top\beta\), \(\beta = \Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)\). Feasibility: \(\E[m_v] = v\), and \(\E[m_vx] = v\mu + \E[x(x - \mu)^\top]\beta = v\mu + \Sigma\beta = p\). Minimality: if \(m\) is feasible, \(\varepsilon = m - m_v\) has \(\E[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\E[\varepsilon x] = 0\), so \(\Cov(\varepsilon, x_n) = 0\) and \(\Cov(\varepsilon, m_v) = 0\); hence \(\Var(m) = \Var(m_v) + \Var(\varepsilon)\), with equality iff \(\varepsilon\) is constant, and that constant is 0. Its variance is \(\beta^\top\Sigma\beta = (p - v\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(p - v\mu)\). With a riskless payoff \(\Sigma\) is singular and the mean is pinned; then A2's \(x^\ast\) is the minimum-variance SDF.

  • Meets C1 level 4: the assumptions, positive definiteness from independence and \(\pi \gg 0\), existence by construction, uniqueness by invertibility, and what happens with redundant payoffs.
  • Meets C2 level 4: orthogonality shown in both directions, the identity with its justification, the equality case, and the positivity caveat with an example.
  • Meets C3 level 4: the identity, convexity as a supremum of affine functions, Jensen with its assumptions, the equality and strict cases, and the link to the variance bound.
  • Meets C4 level 4 for A4: feasibility, minimality with the equality case, the variance, and the riskless case.
  • Meets C6 level 4: the five sections in order, A1–A4, a body of 596 words with a 59-word Summary, and every number printed by the script or labelled as a lesson figure.

Rung 4 · Stretch

  • Separate the two sources of error. Price the test half with the training weights but the test half's own second-moment matrix, and with the test half's weights but the training half's moments, and say which source dominates the 270 basis points.
  • Rolling recovery. Re-estimate \(x^\ast\) every month on a trailing window and price the next month; report the distribution of errors and its dependence on the window length.
