The Empirically Flat Security Market Line

The single most celebrated empirical prediction of the CAPM—that average stock returns increase linearly with market beta—is persistently contradicted by financial data. Decades of cross-sectional tests reveal an empirically flat security market line: low-beta stocks earn higher returns than the model predicts, while high-beta stocks earn lower returns, challenging single-factor equilibrium pricing.

1The Flat SML Phenomenon and Cross-Sectional Evidence

Under the classical CAPM derived in Lesson 4 and Lesson 5, the cross-sectional relationship between expected excess return and market beta is \(E[r_i] - r_f = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \beta_i\), with theoretical restrictions \(\gamma_0 = 0\) and \(\gamma_1 = E[r_m] - r_f > 0\).

Definition 1.1 (Empirically Flat Security Market Line)

The flat security market line refers to the empirical finding that across equity cross-sections, the estimated slope \(\hat{\gamma}_1\) of average return on beta is substantially smaller than the realized market equity risk premium, while the estimated intercept \(\hat{\gamma}_0\) is significantly strictly positive:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\gamma}_0 > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad 0 \le \hat{\gamma}_1 \ll E[r_m] - r_f \end{aligned} \]

Consequently, low-beta portfolios systematically achieve positive CAPM alphas, while high-beta portfolios systematically achieve negative CAPM alphas.

Evidence 1.1 (Cross-Sectional Evidence and the Beta-Return Debate)

In their seminal empirical evaluation of the cross-section of expected stock returns, Fama and French (1992) examined NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ stocks. They documented that when portfolios are sorted to isolate market beta from firm size, the cross-sectional relationship between average return and market beta is virtually flat. Conversely, characteristics such as firm size and book-to-market equity ratio exhibited robust cross-sectional explanatory power that market beta failed to capture.

Counter-evidence: In earlier landmark work, Fama and MacBeth (1973) found a positive, statistically significant trade-off between beta and expected return using monthly data grouped into 20 beta-sorted portfolios. Furthermore, continuous-time and conditional CAPM literature argues that when conditioning variables and time-varying covariances are modeled, the conditional SML exhibits a positive slope.

2Econometric Explanation: Errors-in-Variables Attenuation Bias

A primary econometric mechanism contributing to an empirically flat SML is measurement error in estimated betas.

Theorem 2.1 (Attenuation Bias in Cross-Sectional Regressions)

Suppose the true data-generating process satisfies the exact CAPM: \(\bar{r}_i = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \beta_i + u_i\), with \(\gamma_1 > 0\). If the true betas are unobserved and replaced by first-pass time-series estimates \(\hat{\beta}_i = \beta_i + \nu_i\), where measurement error \(\nu_i\) is classical with mean zero, variance \(\sigma_\nu^2 > 0\), and uncorrelated with \(\beta_i\) and \(u_i\), the OLS estimator of the cross-sectional slope converges in probability to

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{plim}_{n \to \infty} \hat{\gamma}_1 = \gamma_1 \left[\frac{\sigma_\beta^2}{\sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2}\right] < \gamma_1 \end{aligned} \]

The attenuation factor \(\kappa = \frac{\sigma_\beta^2}{\sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2} \in (0, 1)\) strictly attenuates the estimated slope toward zero, while inflating the estimated intercept: \(\text{plim } \hat{\gamma}_0 = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 (1 - \kappa) \bar{\beta} > \gamma_0\).

Proof

The cross-sectional OLS slope of average return \(\bar{r}_i\) on estimated beta \(\hat{\beta}_i\) across \(n\) assets is

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\gamma}_1 = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{\beta}_i - \bar{\hat{\beta}})(\bar{r}_i - \bar{\bar{r}})}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{\beta}_i - \bar{\hat{\beta}})^2} \end{aligned} \]

Substituting \(\hat{\beta}_i = \beta_i + \nu_i\) and \(\bar{r}_i = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \beta_i + u_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{plim}_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{\beta}_i - \bar{\hat{\beta}})^2 &= \Var(\beta_i + \nu_i) = \sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2 \\ \text{plim}_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{\beta}_i - \bar{\hat{\beta}})(\bar{r}_i - \bar{\bar{r}}) &= \Cov(\beta_i + \nu_i, \gamma_1 \beta_i + u_i) = \gamma_1 \sigma_\beta^2 \end{aligned} \]

Dividing the covariance limit by the variance limit yields \(\text{plim } \hat{\gamma}_1 = \gamma_1 \frac{\sigma_\beta^2}{\sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2}\). For the intercept, \(\hat{\gamma}_0 = \bar{\bar{r}} - \hat{\gamma}_1 \bar{\hat{\beta}}\). Since \(\text{plim } \bar{\bar{r}} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \bar{\beta}\) and \(\text{plim } \bar{\hat{\beta}} = \bar{\beta}\), taking limits gives \(\text{plim } \hat{\gamma}_0 = (\gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \bar{\beta}) - \gamma_1 \kappa \bar{\beta} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 (1 - \kappa) \bar{\beta}\). Because \(\gamma_1 > 0, \bar{\beta} \approx 1\), and \(\kappa < 1\), the intercept is strictly positive.

3Economic Explanations: Leverage Constraints and Margin Frictions

Beyond econometric measurement error, structural economic frictions offer compelling explanations for the flat SML:

  • Fischer Black's Zero-Beta Model (1972): When borrowing at the riskless rate is prohibited, investors cannot leverage the tangency portfolio to achieve high target returns. Investors desiring higher returns tilt toward high-beta risky assets, bidding up their prices and lowering their expected returns. The resulting equilibrium SML slope is \(E[r_m] - E[r_z] < E[r_m] - r_f\), where \(E[r_z] > r_f\) is the expected return on the zero-beta portfolio.
  • Leverage-Constrained Investors and Betting Against Beta: Many institutional market participants (e.g., mutual funds, pension funds, endowment trusts) are legally restricted from using leverage. To beat their benchmark, they overweight high-beta stocks. Unconstrained arbitrageurs (hedge funds) face borrowing haircuts and financing risk. Frazzini and Pedersen (2014) showed that this creates persistent demand for high-beta stocks, flattening the equilibrium SML and generating positive alphas for low-beta assets.

Lab 1 · The empirically flat security market line and beta attenuation

Demonstrates that finite-sample estimation noise in first-pass betas creates errors-in-variables attenuation bias that flattens the second-pass cross-sectional regression slope toward zero across SIMULATED cross-sections.

"""Lab AP2-L08-1: The empirically flat security market line and beta attenuation.

SIMULATED cross-sections (seeds 1 to 5).
Demonstrates the econometric mechanism by which sampling noise and errors-in-variables
in estimated betas bias the second-pass cross-sectional regression slope toward zero,
producing a flat-looking security market line even when the true risk premium is positive.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    n_assets = 15
    t_obs = 60
    true_gamma_0 = 0.002  # monthly zero-beta rate ~ 20 bps
    true_gamma_1 = 0.006  # monthly market risk premium ~ 60 bps (7.2% annualized)

    print("--- SIMULATED Cross-Sectional Regressions: Flat SML Demonstration ---")
    print("Seed | True Slope | Estimated Slope | Estimated Intercept | t-stat (Slope)")
    print("------------------------------------------------------------------------")

    for seed in range(1, 6):
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        # Generate true betas in [0.6, 1.4]
        true_betas = [0.6 + 0.8 * (i / (n_assets - 1)) for i in range(n_assets)]

        # Generate market excess returns
        mkt = [rng.gauss(true_gamma_1, 0.045) for _ in range(t_obs)]
        mean_m = sum(mkt) / float(t_obs)
        s_mm = sum((m - mean_m) ** 2 for m in mkt)

        # Generate asset returns and estimate first-pass betas
        beta_hats: list[float] = []
        mean_returns: list[float] = []

        for i in range(n_assets):
            b_true = true_betas[i]
            # True expected excess return
            e_r = true_gamma_0 + true_gamma_1 * b_true
            series = [e_r + b_true * (mkt[t] - true_gamma_1) + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.035) for t in range(t_obs)]
            mean_y = sum(series) / float(t_obs)
            s_ym = sum((series[t] - mean_y) * (mkt[t] - mean_m) for t in range(t_obs))
            b_hat = s_ym / s_mm
            beta_hats.append(b_hat)
            mean_returns.append(mean_y)

        # Second-pass cross-sectional regression: mean_returns = gamma_0 + gamma_1 * beta_hats
        mean_bh = sum(beta_hats) / float(n_assets)
        mean_mr = sum(mean_returns) / float(n_assets)
        s_bb = sum((b - mean_bh) ** 2 for b in beta_hats)
        s_by = sum((beta_hats[i] - mean_bh) * (mean_returns[i] - mean_mr) for i in range(n_assets))

        gamma_1_hat = s_by / s_bb
        gamma_0_hat = mean_mr - gamma_1_hat * mean_bh

        residuals = [mean_returns[i] - (gamma_0_hat + gamma_1_hat * beta_hats[i]) for i in range(n_assets)]
        s2 = sum(e * e for e in residuals) / float(n_assets - 2)
        se_g1 = math.sqrt(s2 / s_bb)
        t_stat = gamma_1_hat / se_g1

        print(
            f"{seed:4d} |   {true_gamma_1:8.4f} |        {gamma_1_hat:8.4f} |"
            f"            {gamma_0_hat:8.4f} |       {t_stat:6.2f}"
        )


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The failure of the empirical SML has profound ramifications for financial practice and asset pricing:

  • Corporate Cost of Capital: Corporations routinely use the CAPM formula \(r_f + \beta (E[r_m] - r_f)\) to compute their discount rate (WACC) for capital budgeting. If the true empirical SML is flat, the CAPM overstates the hurdle rate for high-beta investment projects (causing underinvestment) and understates the hurdle rate for low-beta projects (causing overinvestment).
  • Alpha of Low-Risk Strategies: A passive low-volatility or minimum-variance equity portfolio systematically outperforms its CAPM benchmark, generating anomalous positive alpha that defies traditional single-factor risk theory.
  • Multi-Factor Replacement: The empirical flatness of the SML prompted the development of multi-factor linear pricing models (Fama-French 1993, Carhart 1997) that replace the single market beta with multi-dimensional risk factors.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A flat estimated SML proves that market risk is uncompensated.”

    False. As proved in Theorem 2.1 and demonstrated in Lab 1, first-pass estimation error in beta mechanically biases the cross-sectional slope downward (attenuation bias). A flat OLS estimate can occur even in an economy where market risk is heavily compensated, purely as an artifact of measurement noise.

  2. “High-beta stocks always produce higher realized returns than low-beta stocks in bull markets.”

    False. Beta measures systematic covariance over time, but individual stocks carry substantial idiosyncratic risk. In any given bull market, a significant fraction of high-beta stocks underperform due to negative firm-specific shocks.

  3. “Fama and French (1992) proved that beta has zero correlation with stock returns.”

    False. In univariate sorts on beta, average returns and betas are positively correlated because firm size and beta correlate. Fama and French's key finding was that after controlling for market capitalization, the partial relationship between beta and return became statistically insignificant.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Attenuation bias factor calculation

In a cross-section of stocks, the true variance of betas is \(\sigma_\beta^2 = 0.08\). Betas are estimated from 36 monthly observations, resulting in an average estimation error variance of \(\sigma_\nu^2 = 0.04\). The true market risk premium is \(\gamma_1 = 0.06\) (6.0%). Calculate the attenuation factor \(\kappa\) and the asymptotic probability limit of the estimated cross-sectional slope \(\hat{\gamma}_1\).

Solution

The attenuation factor is \(\kappa = \frac{\sigma_\beta^2}{\sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2} = \frac{0.08}{0.08 + 0.04} = \frac{0.08}{0.12} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.6667\). The probability limit of the estimated slope is \(\text{plim } \hat{\gamma}_1 = \kappa \gamma_1 = \frac{2}{3} \times 0.06 = 0.040\) (4.0%). Measurement error alone flattens the estimated slope by 33.3%, reducing the apparent risk premium from 6.0% to 4.0%.

M2 · Hurdle rate distortion under flat SML

The risk-free rate is \(r_f = 0.03\) and the historical market premium is \(E[r_m] - r_f = 0.06\). A mining firm with \(\beta = 1.50\) calculates its cost of capital under the theoretical CAPM. An empirical study finds the empirical SML has \(\hat{\gamma}_0 = 0.045\) and \(\hat{\gamma}_1 = 0.035\). Calculate the hurdle rate under both models and find the difference.

Solution

Under the theoretical CAPM: \(k_{\text{CAPM}} = r_f + \beta (E[r_m] - r_f) = 0.03 + 1.50(0.06) = 0.03 + 0.090 = 0.120\) (12.0%). Under the empirical flat SML: \(k_{\text{emp}} = \hat{\gamma}_0 + \hat{\gamma}_1 \beta = 0.045 + 0.035(1.50) = 0.045 + 0.0525 = 0.0975\) (9.75%). The theoretical CAPM overstates the project hurdle rate by \(0.120 - 0.0975 = 0.0225\) (2.25% or 225 basis points), potentially causing the firm to reject profitable positive-NPV projects.

M3 · Inflated empirical intercept calculation

Using the parameters from M1 (\(\sigma_\beta^2 = 0.08, \sigma_\nu^2 = 0.04, \gamma_1 = 0.06, \kappa = 2/3\)), suppose the true intercept is \(\gamma_0 = 0.02\) and the cross-sectional mean beta is \(\bar{\beta} = 1.0\). Calculate the asymptotic probability limit of the estimated intercept \(\hat{\gamma}_0\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.1, \(\text{plim } \hat{\gamma}_0 = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 (1 - \kappa) \bar{\beta}\). Substituting the given values: \(\text{plim } \hat{\gamma}_0 = 0.02 + 0.06 \left(1 - \frac{2}{3}\right) (1.0) = 0.02 + 0.06 \left(\frac{1}{3}\right) = 0.02 + 0.02 = 0.04\) (4.0%). The estimated intercept is inflated from 2.0% to 4.0% purely as a consequence of attenuation bias.

Proof

P1 · Portfolio grouping reduces measurement variance

Prove that grouping \(N\) individual assets with uncorrelated beta estimation errors \(\nu_i \sim \text{i.i.d.}(0, \sigma_\nu^2)\) into an equal-weighted portfolio reduces the estimation error variance of the portfolio beta to \(\sigma_\nu^2 / N\).

Solution

The portfolio beta estimator is \(\hat{\beta}_p = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \hat{\beta}_i = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (\beta_i + \nu_i) = \beta_p + \nu_p\), where \(\nu_p = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_i\). Since the individual errors \(\nu_i\) are pairwise uncorrelated with common variance \(\sigma_\nu^2\), the variance of the sample average is \(\Var(\nu_p) = \Var\left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \nu_i\right) = \frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{i=1}^N \Var(\nu_i) = \frac{1}{N^2} (N \sigma_\nu^2) = \frac{\sigma_\nu^2}{N}\). As \(N\) grows, \(\Var(\nu_p) \to 0\), eliminating attenuation bias.

P2 · Zero-beta expected return exceeds riskless rate

In Fischer Black's (1972) zero-beta CAPM, prove that if borrowing at the riskless rate is prohibited but riskless lending is permitted, the expected return on the zero-beta portfolio \(E[r_z]\) must satisfy \(E[r_z] \ge r_f\).

Solution

If riskless lending is available at \(r_f\), any investor can hold a portfolio combining \(r_f\) and risky assets. If \(E[r_z] < r_f\), an investor could construct an arbitrage: long the riskless asset at \(r_f\) and short the zero-beta portfolio \(z\) at \(E[r_z]\), earning a positive expected spread \(r_f - E[r_z] > 0\) with zero market risk (\(\Cov(r_f - r_z, r_m) = 0\)). In competitive equilibrium, absence of arbitrage requires \(E[r_z] \ge r_f\). When borrowing constraints bind, the shadow price of borrowing is strictly positive, ensuring \(E[r_z] > r_f\).

P3 · Attenuation bias strictly increases with noise

Prove that the attenuation factor \(\kappa(\sigma_\nu^2) = \frac{\sigma_\beta^2}{\sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2}\) is a strictly decreasing function of the error variance \(\sigma_\nu^2\) for all \(\sigma_\beta^2 > 0\).

Solution

Differentiating \(\kappa\) with respect to \(\sigma_\nu^2\): \(\frac{d\kappa}{d\sigma_\nu^2} = \frac{d}{d\sigma_\nu^2} \left[\sigma_\beta^2 (\sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2)^{-1}\right] = -\sigma_\beta^2 (\sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2)^{-2} = -\frac{\sigma_\beta^2}{(\sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2)^2}\). Since \(\sigma_\beta^2 > 0\) and the denominator is strictly positive, \(\frac{d\kappa}{d\sigma_\nu^2} < 0\) strictly for all \(\sigma_\nu^2 \ge 0\). Thus, any increase in beta measurement noise strictly flattens the estimated cross-sectional slope.

Empirical

E1 · Fama and French (1992) size and beta sort methodology

Explain how Fama and French (1992) constructed 100 size-beta portfolios to disentangle the cross-sectional correlation between market capitalization and market beta.

Solution

Because market beta and market capitalization correlate negatively (small firms tend to have high betas), running a univariate sort confounds size and beta effects. Fama and French (1992) implemented a two-dimensional sort: (1) First, all NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ stocks were sorted into 10 size deciles based on market equity. (2) Within each size decile, stocks were sorted into 10 beta deciles based on pre-ranking betas estimated from time-series regressions. This generated a \(10 \times 10\) matrix of 100 portfolios where beta varied substantially while size was held fixed, allowing researchers to evaluate the pure partial effect of beta on average return.

E2 · The Betting Against Beta (BAB) factor construction

Describe the mechanical long/short portfolio construction of Frazzini and Pedersen's (2014) Betting Against Beta (BAB) factor and state why it has beta zero.

Solution

Frazzini and Pedersen (2014) construct the BAB factor by: (1) Sorting all assets into a low-beta portfolio \(L\) (average beta \(\beta_L < 1\)) and a high-beta portfolio \(H\) (average beta \(\beta_H > 1\)). (2) Leveraging the low-beta portfolio to beta 1 by investing \(\frac{1}{\beta_L}\) financed by cash. (3) Deleveraging the high-beta portfolio to beta 1 by investing \(\frac{1}{\beta_H}\). (4) The BAB long/short spread is \(r_{\text{BAB}} = \frac{1}{\beta_L}(r_L - r_f) - \frac{1}{\beta_H}(r_H - r_f)\). The market beta of the strategy is \(\beta_{\text{BAB}} = \frac{1}{\beta_L} \beta_L - \frac{1}{\beta_H} \beta_H = 1 - 1 = 0\). Because low-beta stocks have positive alpha and high-beta stocks have negative alpha, BAB generates large, statistically significant positive alpha with zero market risk.

E3 · Shanken (1992) correction for two-pass standard errors

Why do standard OLS standard errors in second-pass cross-sectional regressions overstate the statistical significance of factor risk premia, and how does Shanken's (1992) correction adjust them?

Solution

In the second-pass cross-sectional regression, estimated betas \(\hat{\beta}_i\) are treated as fixed explanatory variables, ignoring the fact that they are estimated with sampling error in the first pass. This generated-regressor problem introduces additional variance into the second-pass error term. Shanken (1992) showed that the true asymptotic variance of the risk premium estimator is inflated by a factor of \(1 + \hat{\gamma}_1' \hat{\Sigma}_f^{-1} \hat{\gamma}_1\), where \(\hat{\Sigma}_f\) is the factor covariance matrix. Ignoring this correction leads to understated standard errors and spurious rejections of the null hypothesis.

Research

R1 · Benchmark-constrained institutional mandates

How do performance benchmarks (e.g., tracking error constraints against the S&P 500) prevent institutional asset managers from exploiting the flat SML?

Solution

Institutional investment managers are evaluated on information ratios and tracking error volatility relative to an equity benchmark. Holding a leveraged low-beta portfolio produces high tracking error against the benchmark, risking client termination during periods when high-beta stocks rally. Brennan (1993) and Baker et al. (2011) showed that tracking-error constraints create an institutional wedge: managers refuse to hold leveraged low-beta assets, leaving the anomaly uncorrected and perpetuating the flat SML in equilibrium.

R2 · Lottery preferences and retail investor demand for high-beta stocks

Explain how cumulative prospect theory and retail investor demand for positive skewness contribute to the low-beta anomaly (Kumar 2009; Bali et al. 2011).

Solution

Retail investors exhibit psychological preferences for lottery-like payoffs: small probability of massive gains combined with high idiosyncratic skewness. High-beta stocks, small-cap growth stocks, and distressed equities have high positive skewness. Retail investors overpay for these securities, driving their prices above fundamental values and driving expected forward returns below the SML required return, contributing to the poor performance of high-beta assets.

R3 · Conditional CAPM with macroeconomic state variables

Summarize the argument of Lewellen and Nagel (2006) regarding whether time-varying conditional betas can explain the empirical failure of the unconditional CAPM.

Solution

Lewellen and Nagel (2006) investigated whether the conditional CAPM—allowing beta and the risk premium to vary over time—can explain the cross-sectional failures documented by Fama and French. They derived an exact expression for the unconditional alpha: \(\alpha_{\text{uncond}} = E[\alpha_t] + \Cov(\beta_t, \gamma_{1t})\). For covariance between beta and the market premium to explain the empirical value and size alphas, the true conditional betas would need to fluctuate by impossible magnitudes across the business cycle. They concluded that conditional variation in beta is far too small to rescue the CAPM.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. What does the term "empirically flat Security Market Line" describe?

    Options

    An empirically flat SML means low-beta stocks earn higher returns than the CAPM predicts, and high-beta stocks earn lower returns, flattening the regression slope.

  2. What econometric effect arises when estimated betas with measurement error are used in second-pass cross-sectional regressions?

    Options

    Measurement error in explanatory variables attenuates the OLS slope coefficient by \(\sigma_\beta^2 / (\sigma_\beta^2 + \sigma_\nu^2)\), biasing it toward zero.

  3. If the true variance of asset betas is \(\sigma_\beta^2 = 0.16\) and the measurement error variance is \(\sigma_\nu^2 = 0.04\), what is the attenuation factor \(\kappa\)?

    \(\kappa = \frac{0.16}{0.16 + 0.04} = \frac{0.16}{0.20} = 0.80\).

  4. How did Fischer Black's (1972) zero-beta CAPM explain a flat SML with a positive intercept?

    Options

    When borrowing constraints bind, the shadow rate of riskless borrowing is higher than \(r_f\), replacing \(r_f\) with \(E[r_z] > r_f\) in the SML pricing equation.

  5. What did Fama and French (1992) find regarding the role of market beta after controlling for firm size and book-to-market?

    Options

    Fama and French (1992) found that market beta had little to no explanatory power for cross-sectional stock returns once size and book-to-market were controlled.

  6. What is the portfolio construction of Frazzini and Pedersen's (2014) Betting Against Beta (BAB) factor?

    Options

    The BAB factor leverages low-beta stocks to beta 1 and deleverages high-beta stocks to beta 1, creating a market-neutral strategy that captures low-beta alpha.

  7. Why does portfolio grouping (e.g., Fama-MacBeth 20 portfolios) mitigate attenuation bias in empirical tests?

    Options

    Averaging returns across \(N\) stocks in a portfolio reduces the variance of beta estimation error to \(\sigma_\nu^2 / N\), restoring the attenuation factor \(\kappa \to 1\).

  8. What corporate capital budgeting consequence occurs if a firm uses the theoretical CAPM in a world where the SML is flat?

    Options

    The theoretical CAPM produces steeper required returns than the empirical line, penalizing high-beta projects with artificially high hurdle rates.
