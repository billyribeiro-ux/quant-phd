Modern quantitative finance begins with Markowitz's realization that investors care about portfolio risk, not individual asset risk. Because assets covary, portfolio variance is a non-linear quadratic form. Solving the constrained optimization yields an analytical hyperbola in mean-standard-deviation space that governs all rational allocation.

1Foundations of Mean-Variance Analysis

Consider an investment universe of \(n \ge 2\) risky assets over a single evaluation period. Let \(r = (r_1, \dots, r_n)^\top\) denote the vector of simple returns with expected return vector \(\mu = \E[r] \in \R^n\) and covariance matrix \(\Sigma = \Cov(r) \in \R^{n \times n}\). Throughout this unit, returns are net simple returns per stated period.

A portfolio is defined by a vector of weights \(w = (w_1, \dots, w_n)^\top \in \R^n\), where \(w_i\) represents the fraction of wealth allocated to asset \(i\). The full-investment budget constraint requires \(\iota^\top w = 1\), where \(\iota = (1, \dots, 1)^\top \in \R^n\) is the vector of ones. The portfolio return is the scalar \(r_p = w^\top r\), with portfolio expected return \(\mu_p = \E[r_p] = w^\top \mu\) and portfolio variance \(\sigma_p^2 = \Var(r_p) = w^\top \Sigma w\).

Definition 1.1 (Model Assumptions A1–A2)

The baseline mean-variance universe satisfies two standing regularity conditions:

  1. Assumption A1 (Nonsingular Covariance): The covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) is symmetric and strictly positive definite, so \(\Sigma^{-1}\) exists and \(w^\top \Sigma w > 0\) for all \(w \neq 0\).
  2. Assumption A2 (Non-Degenerate Expected Returns): The expected return vector \(\mu\) is not proportional to the vector of ones \(\iota\) (that is, \(\mu \neq c \iota\) for any scalar \(c \in \R\)), ensuring that assets do not all share identical expected returns.
Theorem 1.1 (Global Minimum-Variance Portfolio)

Under Assumption A1, the optimization problem

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{w \in \R^n} \frac{1}{2} w^\top \Sigma w \quad \text{subject to} \quad \iota^\top w = 1 \end{aligned} \] has a unique global solution given by

\[ \begin{aligned} w_{\text{GMV}} = \frac{\Sigma^{-1} \iota}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota} \end{aligned} \]

The resulting minimum portfolio variance is \(\sigma_{\text{GMV}}^2 = \frac{1}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota}\), and the expected return is \(\mu_{\text{GMV}} = \frac{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota}\).

Proof

Formulate the Lagrangian using a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda \in \R\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = \frac{1}{2} w^\top \Sigma w - \lambda (\iota^\top w - 1) \end{aligned} \]

By the KKT conditions for convex quadratic programming (M7), the first-order condition with respect to \(w\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_w \mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = \Sigma w - \lambda \iota = 0 \implies w = \lambda \Sigma^{-1} \iota \end{aligned} \]

Premultiplying by \(\iota^\top\) and applying the budget constraint \(\iota^\top w = 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 = \iota^\top w = \lambda (\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota) \implies \lambda = \frac{1}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota} \end{aligned} \]

Substituting \(\lambda\) back into the first-order condition yields the unique solution \(w_{\text{GMV}}\). The variance is

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_{\text{GMV}}^2 &= w_{\text{GMV}}^\top \Sigma w_{\text{GMV}} \\ &= \left(\frac{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota}\right) \Sigma \left(\frac{\Sigma^{-1} \iota}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota}\right) \\ &= \frac{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota}{(\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota)^2} = \frac{1}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota} \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\Sigma\) is positive definite, the Hessian \(\nabla_{ww}^2 \mathcal{L} = \Sigma\) is positive definite, establishing global uniqueness.

2The Mean-Variance Efficient Frontier

The investor seeking optimal portfolio selection solves the parametric mean-variance optimization problem originally formulated by Markowitz (1952). For a prescribed target expected return \(\mu_p \in \R\), the investor minimizes variance subject to the budget constraint and the target return constraint.

Theorem 1.2 (Equation of the Mean-Variance Frontier)

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, define the four scalar constants:

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= \iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota, \quad B = \iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu \\ C &= \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu, \quad D = AC - B^2 \end{aligned} \]

Then \(D > 0\), and the minimum-variance portfolio for target expected return \(\mu_p\) has weights

\[ \begin{aligned} w(\mu_p) = \frac{C - B \mu_p}{D} \Sigma^{-1} \iota + \frac{A \mu_p - B}{D} \Sigma^{-1} \mu \end{aligned} \]

The locus of all such portfolios forms a hyperbola in \((\sigma_p, \mu_p)\) space:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_p^2 &= \frac{A \mu_p^2 - 2B \mu_p + C}{D} \\ &= \frac{1}{A} + \frac{A}{D}\left(\mu_p - \frac{B}{A}\right)^2 \end{aligned} \]

The upper branch (\(\mu_p \ge B/A\)) constitutes the mean-variance efficient frontier.

Proof

First, we show \(D > 0\). The inverse covariance matrix defines an inner product on \(\R^n\): \(\langle x, y \rangle_\Sigma = x^\top \Sigma^{-1} y\). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \(\langle \iota, \mu \rangle_\Sigma^2 \le \langle \iota, \iota \rangle_\Sigma \langle \mu, \mu \rangle_\Sigma\), with equality if and only if \(\mu\) is collinear with \(\iota\). Under Assumption A2, \(\mu\) and \(\iota\) are linearly independent, so \(B^2 < A C\), which implies \(D = AC - B^2 > 0\).

Now formulate the dual-constrained Lagrangian with multipliers \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \in \R\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(w, \lambda_1, \lambda_2) &= \frac{1}{2} w^\top \Sigma w - \lambda_1 (\iota^\top w - 1) \\ &\quad - \lambda_2 (\mu^\top w - \mu_p) \end{aligned} \]

The first-order stationarity condition is

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_w \mathcal{L} &= \Sigma w - \lambda_1 \iota - \lambda_2 \mu = 0 \\ &\implies w = \lambda_1 \Sigma^{-1} \iota + \lambda_2 \Sigma^{-1} \mu \end{aligned} \]

Applying the constraints \(\iota^\top w = 1\) and \(\mu^\top w = \mu_p\) gives the system

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_1 A + \lambda_2 B &= 1 \\ \lambda_1 B + \lambda_2 C &= \mu_p \end{aligned} \]

Using Cramer's rule on the system matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ B & C \end{pmatrix}\) with determinant \(D = AC - B^2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_1 = \frac{C - B \mu_p}{D}, \quad \lambda_2 = \frac{A \mu_p - B}{D} \end{aligned} \]

Substituting \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) into \(w = \lambda_1 \Sigma^{-1} \iota + \lambda_2 \Sigma^{-1} \mu\) produces the weight formula. Finally, the portfolio variance is

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_p^2 &= w^\top \Sigma w = w^\top (\lambda_1 \iota + \lambda_2 \mu) \\ &= \lambda_1 (\iota^\top w) + \lambda_2 (\mu^\top w) = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \mu_p \\ &= \frac{C - B \mu_p}{D} + \frac{A \mu_p - B}{D} \mu_p \\ &= \frac{A \mu_p^2 - 2B \mu_p + C}{D} \end{aligned} \]

Completing the square with respect to \(\mu_p\) yields the vertex form.

Lab 1 · Global minimum-variance weights and exact frontier

Verifies in exact Fraction arithmetic that the GMV weights sum to 1, achieve variance 1/A, and that frontier portfolios match both the target return and the theoretical quadratic variance formula.

"""Lab AP2-L01-1: Exact Global Minimum-Variance portfolio and efficient frontier.

Hypothetical parameters (3 assets), not real data.
Computes w_GMV and two frontier portfolios in exact Fraction arithmetic,
verifying budget sum(w) = 1, target return matching, and variance formulas.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def invert_3x3(mat: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[list[Fraction]]:
    """Invert 3x3 matrix using exact row elimination."""
    n = 3
    aug = [[mat[i][j] for j in range(n)] + [Fraction(1 if i == k else 0) for k in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        p = aug[i][i]
        for j in range(2 * n):
            aug[i][j] /= p
        for r in range(n):
            if r != i:
                f = aug[r][i]
                for j in range(2 * n):
                    aug[r][j] -= f * aug[i][j]
    return [[aug[i][n + j] for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def main() -> None:
    # Hypothetical covariance matrix and expected return vector
    cov = [
        [Fraction(4, 100), Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(1, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(5, 100), Fraction(2, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(2, 100), Fraction(6, 100)],
    ]
    mu = [Fraction(6, 100), Fraction(10, 100), Fraction(12, 100)]
    iota = [Fraction(1, 1), Fraction(1, 1), Fraction(1, 1)]

    inv = invert_3x3(cov)
    inv_iota = [sum((inv[i][j] * iota[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    inv_mu = [sum((inv[i][j] * mu[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]

    scalar_a = sum((iota[i] * inv_iota[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
    scalar_b = sum((iota[i] * inv_mu[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
    scalar_c = sum((mu[i] * inv_mu[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
    scalar_d = scalar_a * scalar_c - scalar_b * scalar_b

    print(f"Scalar A: {scalar_a}")
    print(f"Scalar B: {scalar_b}")
    print(f"Scalar C: {scalar_c}")
    print(f"Scalar D: {scalar_d}")

    # GMV portfolio: w_GMV = Sigma^-1 iota / A
    w_gmv = [inv_iota[i] / scalar_a for i in range(3)]
    mu_gmv = sum((w_gmv[i] * mu[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
    var_gmv = sum((w_gmv[i] * sum(cov[i][j] * w_gmv[j] for j in range(3)) for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))

    print(f"w_GMV: [{w_gmv[0]}, {w_gmv[1]}, {w_gmv[2]}]")
    print(f"sum(w_GMV): {sum(w_gmv, Fraction(0, 1))}")
    print(f"mu_GMV: {mu_gmv}")
    print(f"var_GMV direct: {var_gmv}")
    print(f"var_GMV 1/A: {Fraction(1, 1) / scalar_a}")

    # Frontier portfolios for target returns 8% and 10%
    for target in (Fraction(8, 100), Fraction(10, 100)):
        l1 = (scalar_c - scalar_b * target) / scalar_d
        l2 = (scalar_a * target - scalar_b) / scalar_d
        w_p = [l1 * inv_iota[i] + l2 * inv_mu[i] for i in range(3)]
        achieved_mu = sum((w_p[i] * mu[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
        var_direct = sum((w_p[i] * sum(cov[i][j] * w_p[j] for j in range(3)) for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
        var_formula = (scalar_a * target * target - 2 * scalar_b * target + scalar_c) / scalar_d

        print(f"Target {target}: w=[{w_p[0]}, {w_p[1]}, {w_p[2]}], sum={sum(w_p, Fraction(0, 1))}")
        print(f"Target {target}: achieved_mu={achieved_mu}, var_direct={var_direct}, var_formula={var_formula}")

3Where the canon breaks

Standard textbook presentations assume \(\Sigma\) and \(\mu\) are fixed, known parameters. In real-world asset management, true parameters are unobservable and must be estimated from finite sample data. As Michaud (1989) proved, standard mean-variance optimizers act as "error maximizers": they place the largest positive weights on assets whose expected returns are estimated with positive error and whose variances or covariances are underestimated. Furthermore, when the number of assets \(n\) approaches or exceeds the sample size \(T\), the sample covariance matrix becomes ill-conditioned or singular, requiring shrinkage techniques (such as Ledoit-Wolf) that deviate from the classical unconstrained Markowitz solution.

4Misconceptions

  1. “The global minimum-variance portfolio always holds non-negative weights in all assets.”

    False. If some assets are strongly positively correlated with disparate variances, the GMV optimizer exploits negative weights (short sales) to cancel variance. Only under special conditions (such as uncorrelated assets with identical variances) are all GMV weights guaranteed to be strictly positive.

  2. “All points on the mean-variance frontier are efficient.”

    False. The full frontier is a hyperbola in \((\sigma, \mu)\) space. The lower branch, where \(\mu_p < \mu_{\text{GMV}} = B/A\), is strictly inefficient because every portfolio on the lower branch is dominated by a portfolio on the upper branch with the exact same variance but higher expected return.

  3. “Increasing the number of assets always strictly reduces the minimum achievable portfolio variance.”

    False. Adding a new asset will leave the minimum variance unchanged if the new asset's return can be replicated as a linear combination of existing assets, or if the asset has zero correlation and infinite variance. More generally, in finite samples, adding assets increases estimation error and can worsen out-of-sample portfolio variance.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · GMV weights for two uncorrelated assets

Consider two assets with expected returns \(\mu_1 = 0.08\), \(\mu_2 = 0.12\) and variances \(\sigma_1^2 = 0.04\), \(\sigma_2^2 = 0.09\), with \(\Cov(r_1, r_2) = 0\). Compute the GMV weight \(w_1\) and the resulting portfolio standard deviation.

Solution

For two uncorrelated assets, portfolio variance is \(w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + (1-w_1)^2 \sigma_2^2\). Differentiating with respect to \(w_1\) and setting to zero yields \(2 w_1 \sigma_1^2 - 2(1-w_1)\sigma_2^2 = 0\), which gives \(w_1 = \frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2} = \frac{0.09}{0.13} = \frac{9}{13} \approx 0.6923\). The GMV variance is \(\sigma_{\text{GMV}}^2 = (9/13)^2 (0.04) + (4/13)^2 (0.09) = \frac{4.68}{169} \approx 0.02769\). The portfolio standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.02769} \approx 0.1664\) (16.64%).

M2 · Scalar constants A, B, C, D

Let \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mu = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Compute the four Markowitz scalars \(A, B, C, D\).

Solution

The inverse matrix is \(\Sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/4 \end{pmatrix}\). The scalar \(A = \iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota = 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4\). The scalar \(B = \iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu = (1)(1/2)(1) + (1)(1/4)(2) = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1\). The scalar \(C = \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu = (1)(1/2)(1) + (2)(1/4)(2) = 1/2 + 1 = 3/2\). Finally, \(D = AC - B^2 = (3/4)(3/2) - (1)^2 = 9/8 - 1 = 1/8\). Note that \(D = 1/8 > 0\), as guaranteed by linear independence.

M3 · Minimum variance for target return

Using the scalar values from M2 (\(A = 3/4, B = 1, C = 3/2, D = 1/8\)), compute the minimum portfolio variance for a target expected return \(\mu_p = 1.5\).

Solution

Apply the frontier variance formula \(\sigma_p^2 = \frac{A \mu_p^2 - 2B \mu_p + C}{D}\). For \(\mu_p = 1.5 = 3/2\): the numerator is \((3/4)(9/4) - 2(1)(3/2) + 3/2 = 27/16 - 3 + 3/2 = 27/16 - 24/16 = 3/16\). Dividing by \(D = 1/8\) gives \(\sigma_p^2 = \frac{3/16}{1/8} = \frac{3}{2} = 1.5\). The required portfolio standard deviation is \(\sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.2247\).

Proof

P1 · Uniqueness of the GMV portfolio

Prove that if \(\Sigma\) is symmetric and strictly positive definite, the GMV portfolio is unique.

Solution

Suppose there exist two distinct portfolios \(w_1 \neq w_2\) that both solve the GMV problem with minimum variance \(\sigma_{\text{min}}^2\). Both satisfy \(\iota^\top w_1 = 1\) and \(\iota^\top w_2 = 1\). Consider the midpoint portfolio \(\bar{w} = \frac{1}{2} w_1 + \frac{1}{2} w_2\). The budget constraint is satisfied: \(\iota^\top \bar{w} = \frac{1}{2}(1) + \frac{1}{2}(1) = 1\). Now evaluate variance: \(\bar{w}^\top \Sigma \bar{w} = \frac{1}{4} w_1^\top \Sigma w_1 + \frac{1}{4} w_2^\top \Sigma w_2 + \frac{1}{2} w_1^\top \Sigma w_2\), which equals \(\frac{1}{2} \sigma_{\text{min}}^2 + \frac{1}{2} w_1^\top \Sigma w_2\). Since \(\Sigma\) is strictly positive definite, \((w_1 - w_2)^\top \Sigma (w_1 - w_2) > 0\), which expands to \(w_1^\top \Sigma w_1 + w_2^\top \Sigma w_2 - 2 w_1^\top \Sigma w_2 > 0 \implies w_1^\top \Sigma w_2 < \sigma_{\text{min}}^2\). Therefore, \(\bar{w}^\top \Sigma \bar{w} < \sigma_{\text{min}}^2\). This contradicts the minimality of \(\sigma_{\text{min}}^2\). Hence, \(w_1 = w_2\), establishing uniqueness.

P2 · Covariance between GMV and any other portfolio

Prove that for any portfolio \(w\) satisfying \(\iota^\top w = 1\), the covariance \(\Cov(r_{\text{GMV}}, r_w)\) is constant and equal to \(\sigma_{\text{GMV}}^2\).

Solution

Recall that \(w_{\text{GMV}} = \frac{\Sigma^{-1} \iota}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota}\). The covariance between \(r_{\text{GMV}} = w_{\text{GMV}}^\top r\) and \(r_w = w^\top r\) is \(\Cov(r_{\text{GMV}}, r_w) = w_{\text{GMV}}^\top \Sigma w = \left(\frac{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota}\right) \Sigma w\). Using \(\Sigma^{-1} \Sigma = I\), this simplifies to \(\frac{\iota^\top w}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota}\). Because \(w\) satisfies \(\iota^\top w = 1\), this equals \(\frac{1}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota} = \sigma_{\text{GMV}}^2\). Thus, the GMV portfolio has identical covariance with every feasible portfolio, regardless of that portfolio's composition or expected return.

P3 · Strict positivity of determinant D

Prove directly from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that \(D = AC - B^2 > 0\) under Assumptions A1 and A2.

Solution

Under Assumption A1, \(\Sigma\) is symmetric positive definite. Since \(\Sigma^{-1}\) is also positive definite, it possesses a unique symmetric positive definite square root \(\Sigma^{-1/2}\). Define vectors \(u = \Sigma^{-1/2} \iota\) and \(v = \Sigma^{-1/2} \mu\). Then \(\|u\|^2 = u^\top u = \iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota = A\), \(\|v\|^2 = v^\top v = \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu = C\), and the standard Euclidean inner product is \(u^\top v = \iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu = B\). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in \(\R^n\), \((u^\top v)^2 \le \|u\|^2 \|v\|^2\), with equality if and only if \(u\) and \(v\) are linearly dependent. If \(u\) and \(v\) were linearly dependent, there would exist \(k \in \R\) such that \(v = k u \implies \Sigma^{-1/2} \mu = k \Sigma^{-1/2} \iota \implies \mu = k \iota\), which directly violates Assumption A2. Therefore, \(u\) and \(v\) are linearly independent, ensuring the strict inequality \(B^2 < AC \iff D = AC - B^2 > 0\).

Empirical

E1 · Sensitivity of weights to expected return perturbations

Explain why small estimation errors in \(\mu\) cause large shifts in frontier portfolio weights \(w(\mu_p)\), while GMV weights are completely unaffected.

Solution

The GMV formula \(w_{\text{GMV}} = \Sigma^{-1} \iota / (\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota)\) depends solely on the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) and the budget vector \(\iota\); it does not contain the expected return vector \(\mu\) at all. In contrast, the frontier portfolio weights \(w(\mu_p) = \lambda_1 \Sigma^{-1} \iota + \lambda_2 \Sigma^{-1} \mu\) depend directly on \(\mu\) through \(\Sigma^{-1} \mu\) and through the scalar coefficients \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\) which involve \(B = \iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu\) and \(C = \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu\). When assets are correlated, \(\Sigma\) has small eigenvalues, causing \(\Sigma^{-1}\) to act as an amplifier for any perturbation in \(\mu\). Consequently, a 1% shift in an estimated mean can flip optimal portfolio weights from +50% to -50%, while GMV weights remain perfectly stable.

E2 · GMV out-of-sample performance

Why do empirical studies often find that the sample GMV portfolio achieves a higher realized Sharpe ratio out-of-sample than mean-variance portfolios optimized for higher target returns?

Solution

Expected stock returns \(\mu\) are notoriously difficult to estimate: the standard error of a sample mean scales as \(\sigma / \sqrt{T}\), meaning that decades of return data are required to pin down annual expected returns within 1–2%. Covariance matrices, however, can be estimated much more precisely using higher-frequency data. Because frontier portfolios that target high returns rely heavily on estimated expected returns, their out-of-sample performance degrades severely due to parameter estimation error. The GMV portfolio completely sidesteps the estimation of \(\mu\), avoiding the largest source of parameter uncertainty and often delivering superior out-of-sample risk-adjusted returns.

E3 · Impact of short-sale constraints

How does the addition of non-negativity constraints \(w_i \ge 0\) alter the geometry of the Markowitz efficient frontier?

Solution

Without short-sale constraints, the frontier is a smooth, continuous hyperbola formed by linear combinations of two mutual funds. When non-negativity constraints \(w_i \ge 0\) are imposed, the feasible set is restricted to the simplex \(\Delta = \{w \in \R^n : \iota^\top w = 1, w \ge 0\}\). By the KKT conditions with inequality constraints, different subsets of constraints become active as the target return varies. The resulting constrained frontier is piecewise hyperbolic: it is composed of parabolic segments in variance space, each corresponding to a specific corner portfolio where assets enter or exit the active investment set.

Research

R1 · High-dimensional covariance matrix singularity

Analyze what happens to the mean-variance frontier when the number of assets \(n\) exceeds the sample length \(T\), and describe the standard regularization remedy.

Solution

When \(n > T\), the sample covariance matrix \(\hat{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{T-1} \sum_{t=1}^T (r_t - \bar{r})(r_t - \bar{r})^\top\) has rank at most \(T - 1 < n\). Consequently, \(\hat{\Sigma}\) has at least \(n - T + 1\) zero eigenvalues and is singular, violating Assumption A1. The inverse \(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\) does not exist, and the unregularized mean-variance problem has unbounded solutions: an investor can achieve apparent zero variance by taking portfolios in the null space of \(\hat{\Sigma}\). The standard remedy is shrinkage estimation (Ledoit and Wolf, 2004), which forms a convex combination \(\Sigma_{\text{shrunk}} = \delta F + (1 - \delta) \hat{\Sigma}\), where \(F\) is a well-conditioned target (such as the identity matrix or a single-index model) and \(\delta \in (0, 1)\) is chosen to minimize expected quadratic loss, restoring strict positive definiteness.

R2 · Robust portfolio optimization

Formulate the robust counterpart to the GMV problem where the covariance matrix is known only to lie in an uncertainty set \(\mathcal{U}_\Sigma\).

Solution

In robust optimization, the investor minimizes worst-case variance over the uncertainty set: \(\min_{w: \iota^\top w = 1} \max_{\Sigma \in \mathcal{U}_\Sigma} w^\top \Sigma w\). A standard elliptical uncertainty set around a nominal estimate \(\Sigma_0\) is \(\mathcal{U}_\Sigma = \{\Sigma : \Sigma \succeq 0, \|\Sigma - \Sigma_0\|_F \le \rho\}\), where \(\|\cdot\|_F\) is the Frobenius norm and \(\rho > 0\) is the uncertainty radius. For this set, the worst-case variance is \(\max_{\Sigma} w^\top \Sigma w = w^\top \Sigma_0 w + \rho \|w\|_2^2\). The robust problem is \(\min_{w} \frac{1}{2} w^\top \Sigma_0 w + \frac{\rho}{2} w^\top w\), equivalent to \(\min_{w} \frac{1}{2} w^\top (\Sigma_0 + \rho I) w\) subject to \(\iota^\top w = 1\). This proves that robust optimization under Frobenius uncertainty is mathematically equivalent to Tikhonov (ridge) regularization of the covariance matrix.

R3 · Resampled efficient frontiers

Describe Michaud's (1998) resampled efficiency methodology and evaluate whether it produces genuinely superior portfolio allocations.

Solution

Michaud's resampling procedure draws \(M\) synthetic samples of size \(T\) from the estimated distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}, \hat{\Sigma})\), computes the classical mean-variance frontier for each simulated draw, and averages the resulting portfolio weights across simulations for corresponding rank-ordered points. While this heuristic smooths out extreme corner solutions and prevents concentrated allocations, academic research (e.g., Scherer, 2002) demonstrates that resampling is fundamentally an ad-hoc shrinkage estimator toward the equal-weighted portfolio. It lacks an axiomatic decision-theoretic foundation and can be dominated by Bayesian shrinkage estimators (such as Black-Litterman or Jorion's Bayes-Stein shrinkage) that explicitly account for parameter prior distributions.

7Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the algebraic expression for the global minimum-variance portfolio weight vector \(w_{\text{GMV}}\)?

    Options

    Minimizing \(\frac{1}{2} w^\top \Sigma w\) subject to \(\iota^\top w = 1\) yields the stationarity condition \(\Sigma w = \lambda \iota\), which gives \(w = \lambda \Sigma^{-1} \iota\). Normalizing by \(\iota^\top w = 1\) produces \(w_{\text{GMV}} = \frac{\Sigma^{-1} \iota}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota}\).

  2. Why is the determinant constant \(D = AC - B^2\) strictly positive in the Markowitz framework?

    Options

    \(D = \langle \iota, \iota \rangle_{\Sigma^{-1}} \langle \mu, \mu \rangle_{\Sigma^{-1}} - \langle \iota, \mu \rangle_{\Sigma^{-1}}^2 > 0\) by Cauchy–Schwarz because Assumption A2 guarantees that \(\mu\) and \(\iota\) are linearly independent.

  3. If the scalar \(A = \iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota = 20\), what is the portfolio variance \(\sigma_{\text{GMV}}^2\) of the global minimum-variance portfolio?

    \(\sigma_{\text{GMV}}^2 = \frac{1}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \iota} = \frac{1}{A} = \frac{1}{20} = 0.05\).

  4. What geometric curve does the minimum-variance frontier trace out in \((\sigma_p, \mu_p)\) coordinates?

    Options

    In \((\sigma_p^2, \mu_p)\) variance space, the locus is a parabola \(\sigma_p^2 = \frac{1}{A} + \frac{A}{D}(\mu_p - B/A)^2\). Taking the square root \(\sigma_p\) produces a hyperbola in standard-deviation space.

  5. The covariance between the return of the GMV portfolio and the return of any other valid portfolio \(w\) is…

    Options

    \(\Cov(r_{\text{GMV}}, r_w) = \frac{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} \Sigma w}{A} = \frac{1}{A} = \sigma_{\text{GMV}}^2\).

  6. Which branch of the minimum-variance hyperbola constitutes the efficient frontier?

    Options

    Efficiency requires maximizing expected return for a given level of risk. Portfolios on the lower branch are strictly dominated by upper-branch portfolios with identical variance.

  7. If the scalar constants are \(A = 50\) and \(B = 5\), what is the expected return \(\mu_{\text{GMV}}\) of the global minimum-variance portfolio?

    \(\mu_{\text{GMV}} = \frac{B}{A} = \frac{5}{50} = 0.10\) (10%).

  8. Why do mean-variance optimizers frequently produce extreme, concentrated allocations in practical applications?

    Options

    Michaud (1989) showed that mean-variance optimizers amplify estimation errors, placing the largest bets on assets whose statistical estimation errors are most favorable.
