The Tangency Portfolio and Two-Fund Separation

  • Areplicated

Introducing a riskless asset collapses an entire continuum of risky portfolios into a single optimal risky fund. Tobin's two-fund separation proves that all mean-variance investors, regardless of their individual degree of risk aversion, allocate between the riskless asset and this unique tangency portfolio, laying the operational foundation for passive indexing.

1The Riskless Asset and Sharpe Ratio Maximization

In Lesson 1, we analyzed portfolio selection across \(n\) risky assets. Now introduce a riskless asset earning a certain net per-period return \(r_f \in \R\). An investor can lend (invest at \(r_f\)) or borrow (issue riskless debt at \(r_f\)) without restriction.

Definition 1.1 (Model Assumptions A3–A4)

In addition to Assumptions A1 (\(\Sigma \succ 0\)) and A2 (\(\mu \neq c \iota\)), the model assumes:

  1. Assumption A3 (Riskless Asset): There exists a riskless asset offering net simple return \(r_f\) per period with zero variance (\(\Var(r_f) = 0\)) and zero covariance with all risky assets (\(\Cov(r_i, r_f) = 0\) for all \(i\)).
  2. Assumption A4 (Identified Tangency Denominator): The expected return vector satisfies \(\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota) \neq 0\), ensuring that the tangency portfolio weights are well-defined.

For any risky portfolio \(w\) satisfying \(\iota^\top w = 1\) with \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w} > 0\), its excess return over the riskless rate is \(\mu_p - r_f = w^\top (\mu - r_f \iota)\). The population Sharpe ratio is defined as

\[ \SR(w) = \frac{\mu_p - r_f}{\sigma_p} = \frac{w^\top (\mu - r_f \iota)}{\sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}} \]
Theorem 1.1 (The Tangency Portfolio)

Under Assumptions A1, A2, A3, and A4, the portfolio of risky assets that maximizes the Sharpe ratio \(\SR(w)\) subject to the budget constraint \(\iota^\top w = 1\) is uniquely given by

\[ w_T = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)} \]

Furthermore, the vector of covariances between all individual risky assets and the tangency portfolio is strictly proportional to their excess expected returns:

\[ \Sigma w_T = c (\mu - r_f \iota), \quad \text{where } c = \frac{1}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)} \]
Proof

Notice that \(\SR(w)\) is homogeneous of degree zero in \(w\): for any scalar \(k > 0\), \(\SR(k w) = \SR(w)\). Therefore, maximizing \(\SR(w)\) subject to \(\iota^\top w = 1\) is equivalent to solving the unconstrained ray problem, which can be reparametrized by setting the denominator to an arbitrary constant or fixing the numerator. Equivalently, consider the quadratic problem with normalized excess return:

\[ \min_{v \in \R^n} \frac{1}{2} v^\top \Sigma v \quad \text{subject to} \quad (\mu - r_f \iota)^\top v = 1 \]

The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal{L}(v, \lambda) = \frac{1}{2} v^\top \Sigma v - \lambda [(\mu - r_f \iota)^\top v - 1]\). The first-order condition is

\[ \Sigma v - \lambda (\mu - r_f \iota) = 0 \implies v = \lambda \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota) \]

Substituting into the normalization condition \((\mu - r_f \iota)^\top v = 1\) gives

\[ \lambda (\mu - r_f \iota)^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota) = 1 \implies \lambda = \frac{1}{(\mu - r_f \iota)^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)} \]

To recover portfolio weights summing to one, scale \(v\) by its sum: \(w_T = \frac{v}{\iota^\top v}\). Since \(\lambda\) appears in both numerator and denominator, it cancels out:

\[ w_T = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)} \]

To establish the proportionality condition, multiply both sides by \(\Sigma\):

\[ \Sigma w_T = \Sigma \left(\frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}\right) = \frac{\mu - r_f \iota}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)} = c (\mu - r_f \iota) \]

where \(c = \frac{1}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}\) is a non-zero scalar under Assumption A4.

2Tobin's Two-Fund Separation Theorem

The existence of the tangency portfolio leads directly to the monumental separation theorem established by Tobin (1958).

Theorem 1.2 (Two-Fund Separation Theorem)

Under Assumptions A1, A2, A3, and A4:

  1. Risky Two-Fund Separation: Every portfolio \(w(\mu_p)\) on the risky mean-variance efficient frontier can be represented as a linear combination of any two distinct frontier portfolios. In particular, choosing the global minimum-variance portfolio \(w_{\text{GMV}}\) and the tangency portfolio \(w_T\):
    2. \[ w(\mu_p) = (1 - \alpha) w_{\text{GMV}} + \alpha w_T, \quad \text{where } \alpha = \frac{\mu_p - \mu_{\text{GMV}}}{\mu_T - \mu_{\text{GMV}}} \]
  2. Monetary Two-Fund Separation (Tobin): With riskless lending and borrowing, every mean-variance efficient portfolio of all available assets consists of an allocation between the riskless asset and the unique tangency portfolio \(w_T\).
Proof

For Part 1, recall from Theorem 1.2 of Lesson 1 that any frontier portfolio satisfies

\[ w(\mu_p) = \lambda_1(\mu_p) \Sigma^{-1} \iota + \lambda_2(\mu_p) \Sigma^{-1} \mu \]

Notice that \(w(\mu_p)\) lies in the two-dimensional subspace spanned by the vectors \(\Sigma^{-1} \iota\) and \(\Sigma^{-1} \mu\). The GMV portfolio corresponds to \(\lambda_2 = 0\), which is a multiple of \(\Sigma^{-1} \iota\). The tangency portfolio has \(\mu - r_f \iota\), so it is a non-trivial linear combination of \(\Sigma^{-1} \mu\) and \(\Sigma^{-1} \iota\). Because \(\mu\) is not collinear with \(\iota\) (Assumption A2), \(w_{\text{GMV}}\) and \(w_T\) are linearly independent vectors in this 2D subspace. Hence, \(\{w_{\text{GMV}}, w_T\}\) forms a basis for the frontier subspace.

For any target mean \(\mu_p\), taking expectations on both sides of \(w = (1-\alpha) w_{\text{GMV}} + \alpha w_T\):

\[ \mu_p = (1 - \alpha) \mu_{\text{GMV}} + \alpha \mu_T \implies \mu_p - \mu_{\text{GMV}} = \alpha (\mu_T - \mu_{\text{GMV}}) \implies \alpha = \frac{\mu_p - \mu_{\text{GMV}}}{\mu_T - \mu_{\text{GMV}}} \]

Since \((1 - \alpha) \iota^\top w_{\text{GMV}} + \alpha \iota^\top w_T = (1 - \alpha)(1) + \alpha(1) = 1\), the resulting weights satisfy the budget constraint and match \(\mu_p\). By uniqueness of the minimum-variance portfolio for target \(\mu_p\), this linear combination is identical to \(w(\mu_p)\).

For Part 2, any combined portfolio of the riskless asset and a risky portfolio \(w\) has expected return \(E[r_c] = (1 - a) r_f + a w^\top \mu = r_f + a (w^\top \mu - r_f)\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_c = a \sigma_p\) for \(a \ge 0\). The Sharpe ratio of this combination is \(\frac{E[r_c] - r_f}{\sigma_c} = \frac{w^\top \mu - r_f}{\sigma_p} = \SR(w)\). To maximize expected return for any chosen volatility \(\sigma_c\), the investor must maximize the slope \(\SR(w)\). By Theorem 1.1, \(\SR(w)\) is uniquely maximized at \(w = w_T\). Therefore, all optimal allocations place capital into the riskless asset and \(w_T\).

Lab 1 · Tangency portfolio weights and two-fund separation

Verifies in exact Fraction arithmetic that Sigma @ w_T is strictly proportional to mu - r_f * iota, and proves that a random frontier portfolio decomposes exactly into a linear combination of w_GMV and w_T.

"""Lab AP2-L02-1: Tangency portfolio and two-fund separation.

Hypothetical parameters (3 assets), not real data.
Computes the tangency portfolio weights w_T in exact Fraction arithmetic,
verifies proportionality Sigma @ w_T proportional to mu - r_f * iota,
and checks two-fund separation against the GMV portfolio.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from fractions import Fraction


def invert_3x3(mat: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[list[Fraction]]:
    """Invert 3x3 matrix using exact row elimination."""
    n = 3
    aug = [[mat[i][j] for j in range(n)] + [Fraction(1 if i == k else 0) for k in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        p = aug[i][i]
        for j in range(2 * n):
            aug[i][j] /= p
        for r in range(n):
            if r != i:
                f = aug[r][i]
                for j in range(2 * n):
                    aug[r][j] -= f * aug[i][j]
    return [[aug[i][n + j] for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def main() -> None:
    cov = [
        [Fraction(4, 100), Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(1, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(5, 100), Fraction(2, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(2, 100), Fraction(6, 100)],
    ]
    mu = [Fraction(6, 100), Fraction(10, 100), Fraction(12, 100)]
    r_f = Fraction(2, 100)
    iota = [Fraction(1, 1), Fraction(1, 1), Fraction(1, 1)]

    inv = invert_3x3(cov)
    excess = [mu[i] - r_f for i in range(3)]
    inv_excess = [sum((inv[i][j] * excess[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    denom_t = sum(inv_excess, Fraction(0, 1))
    w_t = [inv_excess[i] / denom_t for i in range(3)]

    print(f"Excess returns: [{excess[0]}, {excess[1]}, {excess[2]}]")
    print(f"w_T: [{w_t[0]}, {w_t[1]}, {w_t[2]}]")
    print(f"sum(w_T): {sum(w_t, Fraction(0, 1))}")

    # Proportionality check: Sigma @ w_T = c * excess
    sig_wt = [sum((cov[i][j] * w_t[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    props = [sig_wt[i] / excess[i] for i in range(3)]
    print(f"Proportionality constants: [{props[0]}, {props[1]}, {props[2]}]")
    print(f"Proportionality holds: {props[0] == props[1] == props[2]}")

    # GMV portfolio
    inv_iota = [sum((inv[i][j] * iota[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    denom_gmv = sum(inv_iota, Fraction(0, 1))
    w_gmv = [inv_iota[i] / denom_gmv for i in range(3)]
    mu_gmv = sum((w_gmv[i] * mu[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
    mu_t = sum((w_t[i] * mu[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))

    # Two-fund separation check on a seeded random target return
    rng = random.Random(42)
    alpha = Fraction(rng.randint(2, 8), 10)  # mix 20% to 80%
    target_mu = (Fraction(1, 1) - alpha) * mu_gmv + alpha * mu_t
    w_separated = [(Fraction(1, 1) - alpha) * w_gmv[i] + alpha * w_t[i] for i in range(3)]

    # Compute frontier weights directly for target_mu
    scalar_a = denom_gmv
    scalar_b = sum((iota[i] * inv_excess[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) + r_f * scalar_a
    inv_mu_term = [sum((inv[i][j] * mu[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    scalar_c = sum((mu[i] * inv_mu_term[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
    scalar_d = scalar_a * scalar_c - scalar_b * scalar_b
    l1 = (scalar_c - scalar_b * target_mu) / scalar_d
    l2 = (scalar_a * target_mu - scalar_b) / scalar_d
    inv_mu = [sum((inv[i][j] * mu[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    w_frontier = [l1 * inv_iota[i] + l2 * inv_mu[i] for i in range(3)]

    print(f"Alpha: {alpha}")
    print(f"Target mu: {target_mu}")
    print(f"w_separated: [{w_separated[0]}, {w_separated[1]}, {w_separated[2]}]")
    print(f"w_frontier:  [{w_frontier[0]}, {w_frontier[1]}, {w_frontier[2]}]")
    print(f"Separation exact match: {w_separated == w_frontier}")

3Where the canon breaks

The classical two-fund separation theorem breaks down whenever borrowing and lending rates diverge. In practice, institutional and retail investors face borrowing rates \(r_{f,\text{borrow}} > r_{f,\text{lend}}\). With unequal borrowing and lending rates, the efficient set ceases to be a single straight ray; instead, it consists of a lending ray tangent to the frontier at portfolio \(T_1\), a curved segment along the risky frontier between \(T_1\) and a second tangency portfolio \(T_2\), and a steeper borrowing ray tangent at \(T_2\). When leverage constraints or margin requirements are binding, investors tilt toward high-beta assets rather than leveraging the tangency portfolio, generating the "betting-against-beta" anomaly.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Different investors with different risk tolerances choose different portfolios of risky assets.”

    False. Under Tobin's two-fund separation, every investor with mean-variance preferences faces the exact same tangency portfolio \(w_T\). An investor's risk aversion determines only the allocation fraction \(a\) between the riskless asset and \(w_T\), never the relative proportions of risky assets within \(w_T\).

  2. “The tangency portfolio always has a higher expected return than the global minimum-variance portfolio.”

    False. If \(r_f > \mu_{\text{GMV}}\), the tangency line from \((0, r_f)\) touches the lower branch of the risky hyperbola, resulting in \(\mu_T < \mu_{\text{GMV}}\). In realistic market equilibria, \(r_f < \mu_{\text{GMV}}\) ensures that the tangency portfolio sits on the upper, efficient branch.

  3. “The two funds in two-fund separation must be the riskless asset and the tangency portfolio.”

    False. In the absence of a riskless asset (risky two-fund separation), any two distinct portfolios on the risky efficient frontier (such as the GMV portfolio and any other frontier point) span the entire frontier. Tobin's monetary separation is a special case involving the riskless asset.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Tangency weights for two uncorrelated assets

Consider two assets with \(\mu_1 = 0.08, \mu_2 = 0.14\), \(\sigma_1^2 = 0.04, \sigma_2^2 = 0.09\), and \(\Cov(r_1, r_2) = 0\). Let \(r_f = 0.02\). Compute the tangency portfolio weight \(w_{T,1}\) and its Sharpe ratio.

Solution

Excess returns are \(\mu_1 - r_f = 0.08 - 0.02 = 0.06\) and \(\mu_2 - r_f = 0.14 - 0.02 = 0.12\). The inverse covariance matrix is diagonal with entries \(1/0.04 = 25\) and \(1/0.09 = 100/9\). Unscaled weights: \(v_1 = 25 \times 0.06 = 1.5\); \(v_2 = (100/9) \times 0.12 = 12/9 = 4/3 \approx 1.3333\). Sum of unscaled weights: \(1.5 + 4/3 = 3/2 + 4/3 = 17/6 \approx 2.8333\). Tangency weights: \(w_{T,1} = \frac{3/2}{17/6} = \frac{9}{17} \approx 0.5294\); \(w_{T,2} = \frac{4/3}{17/6} = \frac{8}{17} \approx 0.4706\). Expected return \(\mu_T = \frac{9}{17}(0.08) + \frac{8}{17}(0.14) = \frac{0.72 + 1.12}{17} = \frac{1.84}{17} \approx 0.1082\). Variance \(\sigma_T^2 = (9/17)^2 (0.04) + (8/17)^2 (0.09) = \frac{81 \times 0.04 + 64 \times 0.09}{289} = \frac{3.24 + 5.76}{289} = \frac{9.00}{289}\), so \(\sigma_T = 3/17 \approx 0.1765\). Sharpe ratio: \(\SR_T = \frac{1.84/17 - 0.34/17}{3/17} = \frac{1.50}{3} = 0.50\).

M2 · Two-fund separation decomposition

A frontier portfolio has expected return \(\mu_p = 0.12\). The GMV portfolio has \(\mu_{\text{GMV}} = 0.08\) and the tangency portfolio has \(\mu_T = 0.10\). Find the decomposition weight \(\alpha\) such that \(w_p = (1 - \alpha) w_{\text{GMV}} + \alpha w_T\).

Solution

Taking expectations: \(\mu_p = (1 - \alpha) \mu_{\text{GMV}} + \alpha \mu_T \implies \mu_p - \mu_{\text{GMV}} = \alpha (\mu_T - \mu_{\text{GMV}})\). Substituting the given values: \(0.12 - 0.08 = \alpha (0.10 - 0.08) \implies 0.04 = \alpha (0.02) \implies \alpha = 2.0\). The portfolio requires \(1 - \alpha = -1.0\) (shorting the GMV fund) and \(\alpha = 2.0\) (leveraging the tangency portfolio).

M3 · Optimal allocation with risk aversion

An investor has mean-variance utility \(U(w) = E[r_c] - \frac{\gamma}{2} \Var(r_c)\) with risk aversion \(\gamma = 4\). The tangency portfolio has \(\mu_T = 0.10\) and \(\sigma_T^2 = 0.04\), with \(r_f = 0.02\). What fraction \(a\) of wealth should the investor allocate to the tangency portfolio?

Solution

For allocation \(a\) in the tangency portfolio and \(1-a\) in the riskless asset, \(E[r_c] = r_f + a (\mu_T - r_f) = 0.02 + a (0.08)\) and \(\Var(r_c) = a^2 \sigma_T^2 = 0.04 a^2\). Utility is \(U(a) = 0.02 + 0.08 a - \frac{4}{2} (0.04 a^2) = 0.02 + 0.08 a - 0.08 a^2\). Differentiating with respect to \(a\) and setting to zero: \(0.08 - 0.16 a = 0 \implies a = \frac{0.08}{0.16} = 0.50\). The investor allocates 50% to the tangency portfolio and 50% to the riskless asset.

Proof

P1 · Geometric tangency condition

Prove that the tangency portfolio geometrically represents the point where a straight line passing through \((0, r_f)\) in \((\sigma, \mu)\) space is tangent to the risky efficient frontier.

Solution

Recall that any frontier portfolio satisfies \(\sigma_p^2 = \frac{A \mu_p^2 - 2B \mu_p + C}{D}\). Differentiating implicitly with respect to \(\sigma_p\): \(2 \sigma_p = \frac{2 A \mu_p - 2 B}{D} \frac{d\mu_p}{d\sigma_p} \implies \frac{d\mu_p}{d\sigma_p} = \frac{D \sigma_p}{A \mu_p - B}\). A line from \((0, r_f)\) to \((\sigma_p, \mu_p)\) has slope \(\frac{\mu_p - r_f}{\sigma_p}\). Tangency requires the line's slope to equal the frontier's derivative: \(\frac{\mu_p - r_f}{\sigma_p} = \frac{D \sigma_p}{A \mu_p - B} \iff (\mu_p - r_f)(A \mu_p - B) = D \sigma_p^2 = A \mu_p^2 - 2B \mu_p + C\). Expanding the left side: \(A \mu_p^2 - (B + A r_f)\mu_p + B r_f = A \mu_p^2 - 2B \mu_p + C\). Cancelling \(A \mu_p^2\): \((B - A r_f)\mu_p = C - B r_f \implies \mu_p = \frac{C - B r_f}{B - A r_f}\). This is precisely the expected return \(\mu_T = w_T^\top \mu\) of the tangency portfolio \(w_T = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}{B - A r_f}\), proving exact geometric tangency.

P2 · Maximizing Sharpe ratio is scale-invariant

Prove that the unconstrained problem \(\max_{v \in \R^n} \frac{v^\top (\mu - r_f \iota)}{\sqrt{v^\top \Sigma v}}\) has solution rays \(v = k \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)\) for any \(k > 0\).

Solution

Let \(f(v) = \frac{v^\top z}{(v^\top \Sigma v)^{1/2}}\), where \(z = \mu - r_f \iota\). Take the gradient with respect to \(v\): \(\nabla f(v) = \frac{z (v^\top \Sigma v)^{1/2} - (v^\top z) \frac{\Sigma v}{(v^\top \Sigma v)^{1/2}}}{v^\top \Sigma v} = \frac{1}{(v^\top \Sigma v)^{1/2}} \left[ z - \frac{v^\top z}{v^\top \Sigma v} \Sigma v \right]\). Setting \(\nabla f(v) = 0\) requires \(z = \left(\frac{v^\top z}{v^\top \Sigma v}\right) \Sigma v\). Let the scalar \(c = \frac{v^\top z}{v^\top \Sigma v}\). Then \(z = c \Sigma v \implies v = \frac{1}{c} \Sigma^{-1} z\). For \(f(v) > 0\), the scalar \(c\) must be strictly positive, meaning \(v\) must be a positive scalar multiple of \(\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)\). Normalizing by \(\iota^\top v = 1\) uniquely identifies the tangency portfolio \(w_T\).

P3 · Covariance of any asset with the tangency portfolio

Prove that for every asset \(i\), \(\Cov(r_i, r_T) = \frac{\mu_i - r_f}{\mu_T - r_f} \sigma_T^2\).

Solution

From Theorem 1.1, \(\Sigma w_T = c (\mu - r_f \iota)\), where \(c = \frac{1}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}\). The \(i\)-th coordinate of \(\Sigma w_T\) is \(\Cov(r_i, r_T) = c (\mu_i - r_f)\). Now compute the variance of the tangency portfolio: \(\sigma_T^2 = w_T^\top \Sigma w_T = w_T^\top [c (\mu - r_f \iota)] = c (w_T^\top \mu - r_f) = c (\mu_T - r_f)\). Solving for \(c\) yields \(c = \frac{\sigma_T^2}{\mu_T - r_f}\). Substituting this expression into \(\Cov(r_i, r_T) = c (\mu_i - r_f)\) gives \(\Cov(r_i, r_T) = \left(\frac{\sigma_T^2}{\mu_T - r_f}\right)(\mu_i - r_f) = \frac{\mu_i - r_f}{\mu_T - r_f} \sigma_T^2\). This fundamental identity establishes that an asset's covariance with the tangency portfolio is proportional to its excess expected return.

Empirical

E1 · Extreme short positions in sample tangency weights

Empirical calculations of \(w_T = \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota) / \dots\) on historical asset data frequently produce extreme long/short positions (e.g., +400% in one sector, -350% in another). Explain why.

Solution

The vector of excess returns \(\mu - r_f \iota\) enters the tangency formula directly through the matrix-vector product \(\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)\). In equity markets, assets share large common factor exposures (such as market, industry, or macro factors), causing the correlation matrix to have several eigenvalues that are very close to zero. The inverse matrix \(\Sigma^{-1}\) consequently has large eigenvalues corresponding to near-collinear eigenvectors. When noisy historical average returns are projected onto these near-collinear eigenvectors, \(\Sigma^{-1}\) severely amplifies the estimation noise, commanding massive opposing long and short positions to exploit minuscule differences in sample returns that do not persist out-of-sample.

E2 · Black-Litterman shrinkage

How does the Black-Litterman model resolve the instability of tangency portfolio weights?

Solution

The Black-Litterman (1992) approach replaces raw sample historical returns with an equilibrium prior \(\Pi = \gamma \Sigma w_m\), derived by reverse-optimizing the market portfolio \(w_m\). Because the market portfolio is well-diversified with non-negative weights by definition, the implied equilibrium excess returns \(\Pi\) are consistent and stable. The investor then combines this prior with subjective views using Bayesian updating: \(\E[r \mid \text{views}] = [(\tau \Sigma)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P]^{-1} [(\tau \Sigma)^{-1} \Pi + P^\top \Omega^{-1} Q]\). Because views are shrunk toward the market equilibrium, the resulting optimal weights are well-behaved and avoid the extreme long/short positions of unconstrained Markowitz optimization.

E3 · Effect of borrowing constraints on market risk premia

When investors cannot borrow at the riskless rate \(r_f\) (margin constraints), how does their demand for high-beta assets distort equilibrium expected returns?

Solution

Under unconstrained two-fund separation, an investor seeking higher expected return than \(\mu_T\) borrows at \(r_f\) to leverage the tangency portfolio. When borrowing is restricted or carries a prohibitive margin premium, leverage-constrained investors (such as mutual funds, pension funds, and retail traders) can achieve higher expected returns only by overweighting high-beta and high-volatility risky assets. This excess demand bids up the prices of high-beta assets, reducing their future expected returns, and depresses the prices of low-beta assets. Consequently, low-beta assets deliver higher risk-adjusted returns than predicted by the CAPM, creating a flatter empirical security market line.

Research

R1 · Generalization of two-fund separation to non-normal distributions

State the necessary and sufficient conditions on asset return distributions under which two-fund separation holds for all risk-averse expected-utility maximizers.

Solution

Two-fund separation holds for all risk-averse expected-utility investors if and only if the joint distribution of asset returns belongs to the elliptical family (Chamberlain, 1983; Owen and Rabinovitch, 1983), or if all utility functions are quadratic. For elliptical distributions (which include the multivariate normal, Student-t, and logistic distributions), any linear combination of asset returns is completely characterized by its mean and variance (up to a common scale parameter), so expected utility \(\E[u(w^\top r)]\) can be written as a function of \(\mu_p\) and \(\sigma_p\) alone. When return distributions exhibit asymmetric skewness or tail co-dependence (copulas outside the elliptical class), third and higher co-moments matter, and two-fund separation fails.

R2 · K-fund separation in multi-period settings

Explain why continuous-time dynamic portfolio choice in Merton's (1973) framework requires \(K + 2\) funds rather than two funds.

Solution

In a dynamic multi-period or continuous-time setting, investment opportunities (the riskless rate, asset volatilities, and risk premia) change stochastically over time. Rational intertemporal investors maximize the expected lifetime utility of consumption, so their value function \(J(W, x, t)\) depends on wealth \(W\) and on \(K\) state variables \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_K)^\top\) that describe the future investment opportunity set. By Ito's lemma and dynamic programming, the optimal portfolio demand contains the traditional myopic mean-variance component plus \(K\) intertemporal hedging demands that hedge against adverse shifts in each state variable. Consequently, investors require \(K + 2\) mutual funds to span their optimal portfolios: the riskless asset, the market (tangency) portfolio, and \(K\) state-variable hedging portfolios.

R3 · Unidentified tangency portfolio

Analyze the singularity condition where \(\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota) = 0\) (violating Assumption A4), and explain the financial meaning of this degenerate regime.

Solution

When \(\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota) = 0\), the scalar \(B - A r_f = 0 \implies r_f = B/A = \mu_{\text{GMV}}\). In this case, the riskless rate equals the expected return of the global minimum-variance portfolio. Geometrically, the horizontal line through \((0, r_f)\) in \((\sigma, \mu)\) space intersects the vertex of the risky hyperbola horizontally; it is an asymptote to the hyperbola, so no unique tangent line with finite non-zero slope exists. Financially, excess expected returns cannot be normalized to sum to one because the excess return vector \(\mu - r_f \iota\) lies entirely in the subspace orthogonal to \(\Sigma^{-1} \iota\). In this degenerate regime, the Sharpe ratio is bounded and the tangency portfolio escapes to infinity.

7Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the algebraic expression for the tangency portfolio weights \(w_T\)?

    Options

    Maximizing the Sharpe ratio \(\frac{w^\top (\mu - r_f \iota)}{\sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}}\) subject to \(\iota^\top w = 1\) yields \(w_T = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}{\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}\).

  2. According to Tobin's two-fund separation theorem, how do two mean-variance investors with different risk tolerances differ in their asset allocations?

    Options

    Two-fund separation states that the optimal risky portfolio is independent of risk preferences. Risk aversion governs only the allocation between the riskless asset and the tangency portfolio.

  3. What is the covariance between an individual asset \(r_i\) and the tangency portfolio \(r_T\)?

    Options

    Because \(\Sigma w_T = c (\mu - r_f \iota)\), the \(i\)-th element is \(\Cov(r_i, r_T) = c (\mu_i - r_f)\), which is proportional to the excess expected return.

  4. Under what condition on the riskless rate \(r_f\) is the tangency portfolio undefined (Assumption A4 violated)?

    Options

    \(\iota^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota) = B - A r_f = 0 \iff r_f = B/A = \mu_{\text{GMV}}\). In this degenerate case, the tangency ray is horizontal and touches the vertex at infinity.

  5. If the tangency portfolio has expected return \(\mu_T = 0.11\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_T = 0.06\), with \(r_f = 0.02\), what is the tangency Sharpe ratio \(\SR_T\)?

    \(\SR_T = \frac{\mu_T - r_f}{\sigma_T} = \frac{0.11 - 0.02}{0.06} = \frac{0.09}{0.06} = 1.50\).

  6. In the absence of a riskless asset, what does risky two-fund separation assert?

    Options

    Because the vector of frontier weights \(w(\mu_p)\) lies in a two-dimensional subspace spanned by \(\Sigma^{-1}\iota\) and \(\Sigma^{-1}\mu\), any two distinct frontier portfolios form a basis for that subspace.

  7. How does the presence of borrowing constraints (\(r_{f,\text{borrow}} > r_{f,\text{lend}}\)) affect the shape of the efficient set?

    Options

    Different lending and borrowing rates create two distinct tangency rays from \((0, r_{f,\text{lend}})\) and \((0, r_{f,\text{borrow}})\), connected by the intervening segment of the risky frontier.

  8. A target frontier portfolio has \(\mu_p = 0.096\). If \(\mu_{\text{GMV}} = 0.08\) and \(\mu_T = 0.10\), what is the weight \(\alpha\) placed on the tangency portfolio in the decomposition \(w_p = (1-\alpha)w_{\text{GMV}} + \alpha w_T\)?

    \(\alpha = \frac{\mu_p - \mu_{\text{GMV}}}{\mu_T - \mu_{\text{GMV}}} = \frac{0.096 - 0.08}{0.10 - 0.08} = \frac{0.016}{0.020} = 0.80\).
