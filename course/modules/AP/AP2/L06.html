The equity risk premium and Jensen's alpha form the quantitative cornerstone of performance attribution. By framing equilibrium asset pricing as a time-series regression, we disentangle passive market exposure from abnormal managerial skill, establishing formal hypothesis tests for whether observed alpha reflects genuine alpha or statistical noise.

1The Equity Risk Premium and Time-Series Regression

In equilibrium, investors demand compensation for bearing systematic macroeconomic risk that cannot be eliminated through diversification.

Definition 1.1 (Equity Risk Premium)

The equity risk premium is the population expected excess return of the broad market portfolio over the riskless rate of return per period:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{ERP} = E[r_m] - r_f \end{aligned} \]

Under competitive equilibrium with risk-averse investors, the equity risk premium is strictly positive (\(E[r_m] - r_f > 0\)), compensating marginal wealth holders for bearing market-wide covariance.

Definition 1.2 (Jensen's Alpha and Time-Series Specification)

Given a time series of \(T\) periodic simple excess returns on asset \(i\) and the market portfolio \(m\), the empirical time-series specification of the CAPM is the linear regression

\[ \begin{aligned} r_{it} - r_{ft} = \alpha_i + \beta_i (r_{mt} - r_{ft}) + \varepsilon_{it}, \quad t = 1, \dots, T \end{aligned} \]

The regression intercept \(\alpha_i\) is jensen's alpha. Under the null hypothesis that the CAPM correctly prices asset \(i\), \(\alpha_i = 0\). An intercept \(\alpha_i \ne 0\) measures abnormal average performance beyond exposure to the market factor.

2Ordinary Least Squares Derivation and Geometry

Let \(y \in \R^T\) be the vector of asset excess returns \(y_t = r_{it} - r_{ft}\), and let \(x \in \R^T\) be the vector of market excess returns \(x_t = r_{mt} - r_{ft}\). The design matrix is \(X = [\iota, x] \in \R^{T \times 2}\), where \(\iota\) is the vector of ones.

Theorem 2.1 (OLS Estimator Closed Forms)

By the least-squares projection theorem (M3), the parameter vector \(\theta = [\alpha_i, \beta_i]'\) minimizing the sum of squared residuals \(\sum_{t=1}^T \varepsilon_{it}^2\) is the unique solution to the normal equations \(X'X \hat{\theta} = X'y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\beta}_i &= \frac{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar{x})(y_t - \bar{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\widehat{\Cov}(y, x)}{\widehat{\Var}(x)} \\ \hat{\alpha}_i &= \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_i \bar{x} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\bar{x} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T x_t\) and \(\bar{y} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T y_t\) are sample means.

Proof

The sum of squared residuals is \(S(\alpha, \beta) = \sum_{t=1}^T (y_t - \alpha - \beta x_t)^2\). Differentiating with respect to \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial S}{\partial \alpha} &= -2 \sum_{t=1}^T (y_t - \alpha - \beta x_t) = 0 \implies \bar{y} - \alpha - \beta \bar{x} = 0 \implies \hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta} \bar{x} \\ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \beta} &= -2 \sum_{t=1}^T x_t (y_t - \alpha - \beta x_t) = 0 \end{aligned} \]

Substituting \(\alpha = \bar{y} - \beta \bar{x}\) into the second condition gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{t=1}^T x_t \left[(y_t - \bar{y}) - \beta (x_t - \bar{x})\right] = 0 \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\sum_{t=1}^T \bar{x}(y_t - \bar{y}) = 0\), we can center the first term: \(\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar{x})(y_t - \bar{y}) - \beta \sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar{x})^2 = 0\). Solving for \(\beta\) yields the closed-form slope \(\hat{\beta}_i = \frac{\sum (x_t - \bar{x})(y_t - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2}\).

3Statistical Inference on Jensen's Alpha

To evaluate whether an estimated alpha \(\hat{\alpha}_i\) is statistically distinct from zero, we impose the classical regression assumption:

  • Assumption A6 (Independent and Identically Distributed Normal Errors): The disturbances satisfy \(\varepsilon_{it} \sim \text{i.i.d.} \Normal(0, \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\) conditional on the market history \(X\).
Theorem 3.1 (Sampling Distribution and Standard Error of Alpha)

Under Assumption A6, conditional on \(x\):

  1. The unbiased estimator of the residual variance with \(T - 2\) degrees of freedom is \[ \begin{aligned} s^2 = \frac{1}{T - 2} \sum_{t=1}^T \hat{\varepsilon}_{it}^2 = \frac{1}{T - 2} \sum_{t=1}^T (y_t - \hat{\alpha}_i - \hat{\beta}_i x_t)^2 \end{aligned} \]
  2. The sampling variance of \(\hat{\alpha}_i\) is \[ \begin{aligned} \Var(\hat{\alpha}_i \mid X) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \left[\frac{1}{T} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar{x})^2}\right] \end{aligned} \] with standard error estimated by \(\se(\hat{\alpha}_i) = s \sqrt{\frac{1}{T} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar{x})^2}}\).
  3. Under the null hypothesis \(H_0: \alpha_i = 0\), the test statistic \[ \begin{aligned} t_{\alpha} = \frac{\hat{\alpha}_i}{\se(\hat{\alpha}_i)} \end{aligned} \] follows an exact Student's \(t\)-distribution with \(T - 2\) degrees of freedom.
Proof

The covariance matrix of the OLS estimator is \(\Var(\hat{\theta} \mid X) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 (X'X)^{-1}\). The matrix \(X'X\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} X'X = \begin{bmatrix} T & \sum x_t \\ \sum x_t & \sum x_t^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} T & T \bar{x} \\ T \bar{x} & \sum x_t^2 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

Its determinant is \(\det(X'X) = T \sum x_t^2 - (T\bar{x})^2 = T \sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2\). Inverting the \(2 \times 2\) matrix:

\[ \begin{aligned} (X'X)^{-1} = \frac{1}{T \sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2} \begin{bmatrix} \sum x_t^2 & -T\bar{x} \\ -T\bar{x} & T \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

The top-left element corresponds to \(\Var(\hat{\alpha}_i \mid X)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\sum x_t^2}{T \sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2 + T \bar{x}^2}{T \sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{1}{T} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{\sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2} \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying by \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) yields the variance. Substituting the unbiased estimator \(s^2 \sim \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \chi^2(T-2)}{T-2}\) into the denominator of \(\hat{\alpha}_i \sim \Normal(\alpha_i, \Var(\hat{\alpha}_i))\) completes the Student's \(t\) construction.

Lab 1 · Jensen's alpha, beta, and time-series inference

Verifies that the closed-form formulas for alpha, beta, residual variance with T-2 degrees of freedom, and se(alpha) match the normal equations solve on a SIMULATED panel of T = 120 observations.

"""Lab AP2-L06-1: Jensen's alpha, beta, and time-series inference.

SIMULATED panel of T = 120 monthly observations (seed 42).
Computes OLS estimates for alpha and beta, residual variance with T-2 degrees of freedom,
standard error of alpha, and the t-statistic testing the null hypothesis alpha = 0.
Compares closed forms against normal equations.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    t_obs = 120

    # True data-generating process: alpha = 0.001 (10 bps/month), beta = 1.15
    true_alpha = 0.001
    true_beta = 1.15
    true_sigma_e = 0.025  # 2.5% monthly idiosyncratic volatility

    # Market excess return: mean 0.0065, std 0.045
    r_m = [rng.gauss(0.0065, 0.045) for _ in range(t_obs)]
    r_i = [true_alpha + true_beta * r_m[t] + rng.gauss(0.0, true_sigma_e) for t in range(t_obs)]

    # Closed-form OLS formulas
    mean_m = sum(r_m) / float(t_obs)
    mean_i = sum(r_i) / float(t_obs)
    s_mm = sum((m - mean_m) ** 2 for m in r_m)
    s_im = sum((r_m[t] - mean_m) * (r_i[t] - mean_i) for t in range(t_obs))

    beta_hat = s_im / s_mm
    alpha_hat = mean_i - beta_hat * mean_m

    residuals = [r_i[t] - (alpha_hat + beta_hat * r_m[t]) for t in range(t_obs)]
    sse = sum(e * e for e in residuals)
    df = t_obs - 2
    s2 = sse / float(df)
    var_alpha = s2 * (1.0 / float(t_obs) + (mean_m * mean_m) / s_mm)
    se_alpha = math.sqrt(var_alpha)
    t_stat = alpha_hat / se_alpha

    # Normal equations check:
    # [ T       sum(r_m)   ] [ alpha ] = [ sum(r_i)     ]
    # [ sum(r_m) sum(r_m^2) ] [ beta  ]   [ sum(r_i*r_m) ]
    sum_m = sum(r_m)
    sum_m2 = sum(m * m for m in r_m)
    sum_i = sum(r_i)
    sum_im = sum(r_i[t] * r_m[t] for t in range(t_obs))
    det = float(t_obs) * sum_m2 - sum_m * sum_m
    norm_alpha = (sum_m2 * sum_i - sum_m * sum_im) / det
    norm_beta = (float(t_obs) * sum_im - sum_m * sum_i) / det

    print("--- SIMULATED Jensen Regression (T = 120) ---")
    print(f"Sample length T:             {t_obs}")
    print(f"Sample mean excess market:   {mean_m:.6f}")
    print(f"Sample mean excess asset:    {mean_i:.6f}")
    print(f"Estimated beta (closed):     {beta_hat:.6f}")
    print(f"Estimated beta (normal eq):  {norm_beta:.6f}")
    print(f"Estimated alpha (closed):    {alpha_hat:.6f}")
    print(f"Estimated alpha (normal eq): {norm_alpha:.6f}")
    print(f"Residual variance s^2:       {s2:.8f}")
    print(f"Residual std s:              {math.sqrt(s2):.6f}")
    print(f"Standard error se(alpha):    {se_alpha:.6f}")
    print(f"t-statistic (H0: alpha = 0): {t_stat:.4f}")
    print(f"Agreement within 1e-12:      {abs(alpha_hat - norm_alpha) < 1e-12 and abs(beta_hat - norm_beta) < 1e-12}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

Time-series regression tests of Jensen's alpha encounter several structural breakdowns in financial econometrics:

  • Fat Tails and Heteroskedasticity: Financial return innovations exhibit heavy tails, skewness, and volatility clustering (GARCH effects), violating Assumption A6. Standard errors require White (1980) or Newey-West (1987) adjustments to maintain asymptotic validity.
  • Time-Varying Beta (Market Timing): If a manager actively shifts portfolio cash allocations in response to macroeconomic forecasts, beta fluctuates over time (\(\beta_t\)). Running a static OLS regression creates non-linear curvature in the scatter plot, generating an artificial negative alpha (Treynor and Mazuy, 1966).
  • Benchmark Inefficiency: As established by Roll (1977), if the market proxy used in the regression is not mean-variance efficient, estimated alphas reflect benchmark misspecification rather than manager skill.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A positive estimated alpha \(\hat{\alpha} > 0\) proves superior investment skill.”

    False. In finite samples, sampling noise generates non-zero alpha estimates even when true skill is zero (\(\alpha = 0\)). Managerial skill is only supported if \(\hat{\alpha}\) is statistically distinguishable from zero after formal hypothesis testing (e.g., \(|t_\alpha| \ge 1.96\)), and survives adjustments for multiple hypothesis testing across fund universes.

  2. “The standard error of alpha depends only on the number of observations \(T\).”

    False. As proven in Theorem 3.1, \(\Var(\hat{\alpha}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 [\frac{1}{T} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{\sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2}]\). High idiosyncratic volatility \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) directly inflates \(\se(\hat{\alpha})\), requiring decades of data to achieve statistical significance for volatile hedge fund strategies.

  3. “Subtracting the riskless rate does not affect the estimated beta.”

    False. If the riskless rate \(r_f\) is constant over the sample period, subtracting it does not change the sample covariance or variance, leaving \(\hat{\beta}\) unchanged. However, when \(r_{ft}\) fluctuates over time, regressions on raw returns \(r_{it} = a_i + b_i r_{mt} + e_{it}\) yield biased slope estimates that confound interest rate exposure with equity market exposure.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Time-series regression estimates calculation

Over \(T = 3\) periods, an asset's excess returns are \(y = [0.04, 0.08, 0.12]\) and the market excess returns are \(x = [0.02, 0.04, 0.06]\). Compute the sample means \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\), the OLS beta \(\hat{\beta}\), and Jensen's alpha \(\hat{\alpha}\).

Solution

Compute sample means: \(\bar{x} = \frac{0.02 + 0.04 + 0.06}{3} = 0.04\); \(\bar{y} = \frac{0.04 + 0.08 + 0.12}{3} = 0.08\). Deviations: \(x - \bar{x} = [-0.02, 0.00, 0.02]\) and \(y - \bar{y} = [-0.04, 0.00, 0.04]\). Covariance sum: \(\sum (x_t - \bar{x})(y_t - \bar{y}) = (-0.02)(-0.04) + 0 + (0.02)(0.04) = 0.0008 + 0.0008 = 0.0016\). Variance sum: \(\sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2 = (-0.02)^2 + 0^2 + (0.02)^2 = 0.0004 + 0.0004 = 0.0008\). Slope: \(\hat{\beta} = \frac{0.0016}{0.0008} = 2.00\). Intercept: \(\hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta} \bar{x} = 0.08 - 2.00(0.04) = 0.08 - 0.08 = 0.00\).

M2 · Standard error and t-statistic calculation

In a time-series regression with \(T = 62\) observations, the estimated alpha is \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.0040\) (40 bps monthly), the residual variance is \(s^2 = 0.0004\), the sample mean market excess return is \(\bar{x} = 0.006\), and \(\sum_{t=1}^T (x_t - \bar{x})^2 = 0.10\). Compute \(\se(\hat{\alpha})\) and the \(t\)-statistic testing \(H_0: \alpha = 0\).

Solution

The variance factor is \(\frac{1}{T} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{\sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{1}{62} + \frac{0.006^2}{0.10} = 0.016129 + \frac{0.000036}{0.10} = 0.016129 + 0.000360 = 0.016489\). The variance of alpha is \(\Var(\hat{\alpha}) = s^2 \times 0.016489 = 0.0004 \times 0.016489 \approx 6.5956 \times 10^{-6}\). The standard error is \(\se(\hat{\alpha}) = \sqrt{6.5956 \times 10^{-6}} \approx 0.002568\) (25.7 bps). The \(t\)-statistic is \(t_\alpha = \frac{0.0040}{0.002568} \approx 1.5576\). Since \(1.56 < 2.00\) (critical value for \(df = 60\) at 5%), the alpha is not statistically significant.

M3 · Annualized alpha and information ratio

A mutual fund delivers a monthly alpha of \(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{mo}} = 0.0025\) (25 bps/month) with monthly idiosyncratic volatility \(s_\varepsilon = 0.015\) (1.5%/month). Calculate the annualized alpha and the fund's Information Ratio.

Solution

Annualized alpha is \(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{ann}} = 12 \times \hat{\alpha}_{\text{mo}} = 12 \times 0.0025 = 0.030\) (3.0% per year). Annualized tracking error (idiosyncratic volatility) is \(s_{\varepsilon,\text{ann}} = \sqrt{12} \times 0.015 \approx 3.4641 \times 0.015 \approx 0.05196\) (5.20% per year). The Information Ratio is defined as the ratio of alpha to tracking error: \(\text{IR} = \frac{\hat{\alpha}_{\text{ann}}}{s_{\varepsilon,\text{ann}}} = \frac{0.030}{0.05196} \approx 0.577\). Equivalently, in monthly terms: \(\text{IR}_{\text{mo}} = \frac{0.0025}{0.015} = 0.1667\), and \(\text{IR}_{\text{ann}} = \sqrt{12} \times 0.1667 \approx 0.577\).

P1 · Unbiasedness of the OLS alpha estimator

Prove that under the classical linear regression model with \(E[\varepsilon_t \mid X] = 0\), the OLS estimator \(\hat{\alpha}\) is an unbiased estimator of true alpha: \(E[\hat{\alpha} \mid X] = \alpha\).

Solution

Recall that \(\hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta} \bar{x}\). Under the data generating process, \(y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + \varepsilon_t\), so \(\bar{y} = \alpha + \beta \bar{x} + \bar{\varepsilon}\). Substituting into the alpha formula: \(\hat{\alpha} = (\alpha + \beta \bar{x} + \bar{\varepsilon}) - \hat{\beta} \bar{x} = \alpha - (\hat{\beta} - \beta)\bar{x} + \bar{\varepsilon}\). Taking conditional expectations: \(E[\hat{\alpha} \mid X] = \alpha - E[\hat{\beta} - \beta \mid X]\bar{x} + E[\bar{\varepsilon} \mid X]\). Since \(\hat{\beta}\) is unbiased (\(E[\hat{\beta} \mid X] = \beta\)) and \(E[\bar{\varepsilon} \mid X] = 0\), the last two terms vanish identically. Thus \(E[\hat{\alpha} \mid X] = \alpha\).

P2 · Derivation of the degrees of freedom for residual variance

Prove that under Assumption A6, the sum of squared residuals \(\sum_{t=1}^T \hat{\varepsilon}_t^2\) has \(T - 2\) degrees of freedom.

Solution

In vector notation, the residual vector is \(\hat{\varepsilon} = (I - P_X) y = (I - P_X) \varepsilon\), where \(P_X = X(X'X)^{-1}X'\) is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the 2-dimensional column space of \(X\). The residual projection matrix \(M_X = I - P_X\) is symmetric and idempotent (\(M_X' = M_X\) and \(M_X^2 = M_X\)). By the properties of idempotent matrices, the rank and trace of \(M_X\) are equal: \(\text{tr}(M_X) = \text{tr}(I_T) - \text{tr}(P_X) = T - \text{tr}((X'X)^{-1}X'X) = T - \text{tr}(I_2) = T - 2\). Since \(\varepsilon \sim \Normal(0, \sigma^2 I_T)\), the quadratic form \(\hat{\varepsilon}'\hat{\varepsilon} = \varepsilon' M_X \varepsilon\) distributed as \(\sigma^2 \chi^2(T-2)\) by Cochran's theorem.

P3 · Invariance of beta to constant riskless rate

Prove that when the riskless rate is a constant scalar \(r_f\) across all periods, the OLS slope coefficient of raw returns on raw market returns equals the OLS slope coefficient of excess returns on market excess returns.

Solution

Let raw returns be \(R_{it}\) and \(R_{mt}\), so excess returns are \(y_t = R_{it} - r_f\) and \(x_t = R_{mt} - r_f\). Sample means satisfy \(\bar{y} = \bar{R}_i - r_f\) and \(\bar{x} = \bar{R}_m - r_f\). Deviations from means are \(y_t - \bar{y} = (R_{it} - r_f) - (\bar{R}_i - r_f) = R_{it} - \bar{R}_i\) and \(x_t - \bar{x} = (R_{mt} - r_f) - (\bar{R}_m - r_f) = R_{mt} - \bar{R}_m\). Because deviations from sample means are identical whether returns are measured in raw or excess form, the sample covariance \(\sum (x_t - \bar{x})(y_t - \bar{y})\) and sample variance \(\sum (x_t - \bar{x})^2\) are identical. Thus \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{excess}} = \hat{\beta}_{\text{raw}}\).

Empirical

E1 · Minimum sample length for alpha detection

A hedge fund claims a true alpha of \(\alpha = 0.02\) (2.0% annually) with annual idiosyncratic volatility \(\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.08\) (8.0%). How many years of historical annual data \(T\) are required to reject \(H_0: \alpha = 0\) at the 5% two-sided significance level (\(t \ge 1.96\)), assuming \(\bar{x} \approx 0\)?

Solution

When \(\bar{x} \approx 0\), the standard error simplifies to \(\se(\hat{\alpha}) \approx \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon}{\sqrt{T}}\). To achieve statistical significance at the 5% level: \(\frac{\alpha}{\sigma_\varepsilon / \sqrt{T}} \ge 1.96 \implies \sqrt{T} \ge 1.96 \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon}{\alpha}\). Substituting values: \(\sqrt{T} \ge 1.96 \times \frac{0.08}{0.02} = 1.96 \times 4 = 7.84\). Squaring both sides: \(T \ge 7.84^2 \approx 61.47\) years. This fundamental calculation shows why detecting modest annual alphas in equity strategies requires decades of track record data.

E2 · Market timing and non-linear alpha bias

Explain the Treynor-Mazuy (1966) quadratic model of market timing and show how successful market timing distorts the standard CAPM Jensen regression.

Solution

Treynor and Mazuy (1966) proposed the regression model \(r_{it} - r_{ft} = \alpha_i + \beta_i (r_{mt} - r_{ft}) + \gamma_i (r_{mt} - r_{ft})^2 + \varepsilon_{it}\). A successful market timer increases market beta prior to bull markets and decreases beta prior to bear markets, corresponding to \(\gamma_i > 0\). If an evaluator fits a linear CAPM model omitting the quadratic term \((r_m - r_f)^2\), the omitted positive convex relationship pulls down the estimated intercept \(\hat{\alpha}_i\), falsely penalizing an active manager with an artificially depressed or negative Jensen's alpha.

E3 · Heteroskedasticity and White robust standard errors

Why do standard OLS standard errors \(\se(\hat{\alpha})\) typically understate true sampling uncertainty during crisis periods, and how does White's (1980) heteroskedasticity-consistent estimator correct for this?

Solution

During financial crises, idiosyncratic return volatility \(\Var(\varepsilon_t \mid x_t)\) spikes precisely when market returns \(x_t\) experience extreme volatility. Standard OLS assumes constant variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), averaging turbulent and tranquil periods. White's (1980) heteroskedasticity-consistent covariance estimator replaces \(\sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1}\) with \((X'X)^{-1} \left(\sum_{t=1}^T \hat{\varepsilon}_t^2 x_t x_t'\right) (X'X)^{-1}\), weighting each cross-product by the squared residual at that specific time step, preventing inflated \(t\)-statistics during volatile regimes.

Research

R1 · Multiple testing and the false discovery rate in alpha screening

Explain how Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016) apply multiple testing corrections to asset pricing anomalies and factor alphas, and state the recommended \(t\)-statistic hurdle.

Solution

Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016) demonstrated that testing hundreds of proposed factors or mutual fund alphas creates massive data snooping and false discoveries under the standard \(t \ge 2.0\) threshold. When 1,000 uninformative strategies are tested at \(\alpha = 0.05\), roughly 50 will show statistically significant alphas purely by luck. Controlling for family-wise error rate or False Discovery Rate (FDR) requires raising the critical \(t\)-statistic hurdle to at least \(t \ge 3.0\) (corresponding to \(p < 0.0027\)) to declare an anomaly or alpha genuinely significant.

R2 · Berk and Green (2004) equilibrium of active management

Summarize the rational equilibrium model of Berk and Green (2004) explaining why skilled mutual fund managers exhibit zero net-of-fee alpha over time.

Solution

Berk and Green (2004) model an economy where managers have heterogeneous investment skill but face decreasing returns to scale: larger assets under management (AUM) increase market impact and dilute trade profitability. Rational investors observe positive historical performance and direct capital into skilled funds. Fund inflows expand AUM until the fund's gross alpha is entirely consumed by bureaucratic overhead and scale diseconomies, driving net alpha to zero in competitive equilibrium while the manager captures the economic rent via percentage fees.

R3 · Bootstrap inference for mutual fund alphas

Describe the non-parametric bootstrap methodology of Kosowski, Timmermann, Wermers, and White (2006) and Fama and French (2010) for evaluating cross-sectional fund alphas.

Solution

To eliminate parametric distributional assumptions and account for cross-fund residual correlation, Kosowski et al. (2006) and Fama and French (2010) resample time vectors of fund residuals with replacement under the imposed null hypothesis of zero true alpha (\(\alpha_i = 0\)). By generating thousands of simulated luck-only fund return histories and computing the resulting cross-sectional distribution of \(t(\hat{\alpha})\), they compare the actual empirical distribution of \(t\)-statistics against the simulated null distribution, proving that fewer than 2% of US mutual funds possess genuine managerial skill beyond luck.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the economic definition of the equity risk premium?

    Options

    The equity risk premium represents the expected compensation required by investors to hold the risky market portfolio instead of the risk-free asset.

  2. In the time-series regression \(r_{it} - r_{ft} = \alpha_i + \beta_i (r_{mt} - r_{ft}) + \varepsilon_{it}\), what does the intercept \(\alpha_i\) measure?

    Options

    Jensen's alpha is the intercept of the excess return time-series regression; under the CAPM null hypothesis, it equals zero.

  3. In an OLS regression of monthly excess returns on market excess returns over a sample of \(T = 60\) observations, how many degrees of freedom does the residual variance \(s^2\) possess?

    Degrees of freedom is \(T - 2 = 60 - 2 = 58\), accounting for estimating two parameters (alpha and beta).

  4. What is the null hypothesis when evaluating whether an investment manager generates genuine abnormal performance?

    Options

    The null hypothesis states that the fund's excess returns are fully explained by its exposure to the market factor, so alpha is zero.

  5. If an estimated alpha is \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.0050\) (50 bps) and its standard error is \(\se(\hat{\alpha}) = 0.0020\) (20 bps), what is the \(t\)-statistic?

    \(t_\alpha = \frac{\hat{\alpha}}{\se(\hat{\alpha})} = \frac{0.0050}{0.0020} = 2.5\).

  6. How does high idiosyncratic volatility \(\sigma_\varepsilon\) impact the standard error of Jensen's alpha?

    Options

    Standard error is directly proportional to residual standard deviation \(s_\varepsilon\). Greater idiosyncratic noise obscures the true signal, inflating standard errors.

  7. Under what assumption does the \(t\)-statistic for Jensen's alpha follow an exact Student's \(t\)-distribution in finite samples?

    Options

    Exact finite-sample Student's \(t\) inference requires normal i.i.d. regression disturbances; otherwise, inference relies on asymptotic normality.

  8. What phenomenon occurs if an evaluator fits a linear CAPM model to a fund manager who successfully times the market?

    Options

    As shown by Treynor and Mazuy (1966), successful market timing generates convex returns, which distorts a linear fit and biases linear alpha downward.
