The Capital Asset Pricing Model bridges normative portfolio optimization and equilibrium asset pricing. By aggregating individual mean-variance investors under competitive market clearing, the model reveals that an asset's expected return is governed not by its total risk, but solely by its covariance with aggregate wealth—its market beta.

1Equilibrium Assumptions and Market Clearing

The classical Capital Asset Pricing Model developed by Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965) posits a competitive financial economy operating under five structural assumptions:

  1. Assumption A1 (Positive Definite Covariance): The covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) of risky asset returns is symmetric and positive definite.
  2. Assumption A2 (Non-proportional Means): The vector of ones \(\iota\) and expected returns \(\mu\) are linearly independent, ensuring the efficient frontier is non-degenerate.
  3. Assumption A3 (Riskless Borrowing and Lending): Investors can borrow and lend unlimited quantities at a common net riskless rate \(r_f\) per period.
  4. Assumption A4 (Identified Tangency): The excess return vector satisfies \(\iota' \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota) \ne 0\), ensuring the tangency portfolio denominator is non-zero.
  5. Assumption A5 (Competitive Market Clearing and Homogeneous Beliefs): All investors share identical subjective probability assessments of \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) over a single common investment horizon, act as price-takers to maximize mean-variance utility, and asset prices adjust such that the aggregate supply of each risky asset equals aggregate investor demand.
Theorem 1.1 (Market Portfolio is the Tangency Portfolio in Equilibrium)

Under Assumptions A1–A5, the value-weighted market portfolio \(w_m\) is identical to the unique tangency portfolio \(w_T\):

\[ \begin{aligned} w_m = w_T = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}{\iota' \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota)} \end{aligned} \]
Proof

By the two-fund separation theorem (Lesson 2), every mean-variance investor \(k \in \{1, \dots, K\}\) chooses a risky portfolio consisting entirely of the tangency portfolio \(w_T\). Let \(W_k\) denote the total wealth of investor \(k\), and let \(a_k\) denote the fraction of wealth allocated to risky assets. Investor \(k\)'s dollar holding in risky asset \(i\) is \(D_{k,i} = W_k a_k w_{T,i}\).

Aggregating across all \(K\) investors in the economy, total market demand for risky asset \(i\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} D_i = \sum_{k=1}^K D_{k,i} = \left(\sum_{k=1}^K W_k a_k\right) w_{T,i} \end{aligned} \]

In competitive equilibrium under Assumption A5, markets clear: total demand \(D_i\) equals total market capitalization \(M_i\) for every asset \(i\). Total market value of all risky assets is \(M = \sum_{i=1}^n M_i = \sum_{k=1}^K W_k a_k\). The market portfolio weight of asset \(i\) is by definition \(w_{m,i} = M_i / M\). Substituting aggregate demand yields

\[ \begin{aligned} w_{m,i} = \frac{\left(\sum_{k=1}^K W_k a_k\right) w_{T,i}}{\sum_{k=1}^K W_k a_k} = w_{T,i} \end{aligned} \]

Hence, \(w_m = w_T\). In equilibrium, the market portfolio must lie on the efficient frontier and coincide exactly with the tangency portfolio.

2Derivation of the CAPM Pricing Equation and Beta

Because the market portfolio is the tangency portfolio in equilibrium, its portfolio weights satisfy the first-order condition derived in Lesson 2:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Sigma w_m = \frac{1}{\lambda} (\mu - r_f \iota) \end{aligned} \]

where \(\lambda = \iota' \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota)\). Multiplying both sides by \(w_m'\) yields the market portfolio variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_m^2 = w_m' \Sigma w_m = \frac{1}{\lambda} w_m' (\mu - r_f \iota) = \frac{\mu_m - r_f}{\lambda} \end{aligned} \]

where \(\mu_m = w_m' \mu\) is the expected return of the market portfolio. Solving for the multiplier gives \(\lambda = (\mu_m - r_f) / \sigma_m^2\). Substituting this expression for \(\lambda\) back into the first-order condition vector yields

\[ \begin{aligned} \Sigma w_m = \left(\frac{\sigma_m^2}{\mu_m - r_f}\right)^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota) = \left(\frac{\mu_m - r_f}{\sigma_m^2}\right) (\mu - r_f \iota) \end{aligned} \]

Examining the \(i\)-th row of this vector equation, the \(i\)-th element of \(\Sigma w_m\) is the covariance between asset \(i\) and the market portfolio: \((\Sigma w_m)_i = \Cov(r_i, r_m)\). Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \Cov(r_i, r_m) = \frac{\mu_m - r_f}{\sigma_m^2} (\mu_i - r_f) \implies \mu_i - r_f = \frac{\Cov(r_i, r_m)}{\sigma_m^2} (\mu_m - r_f) \end{aligned} \]
Definition 2.1 (Beta)

The systematic risk exposure of an asset or portfolio \(i\) with respect to the market portfolio \(m\) is the dimensionless ratio

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_i = \frac{\Cov(r_i, r_m)}{\Var(r_m)} = \frac{\sigma_{im}}{\sigma_m^2} = \Corr(r_i, r_m) \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m} \end{aligned} \]
Definition 2.2 (Capital Asset Pricing Model)

The Capital Asset Pricing Model states that in competitive equilibrium under Assumptions A1–A5, the expected excess return on any asset or portfolio \(i\) is strictly proportional to its market beta:

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r_i] - r_f = \beta_i (E[r_m] - r_f) \end{aligned} \]

Equivalently, expressing the relation in gross expected returns:

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r_i] = r_f + \beta_i (E[r_m] - r_f) \end{aligned} \]
Theorem 2.1 (Linearity and Aggregation of Beta)

Beta is linear with respect to portfolio weights. For any portfolio \(p = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i r_i\) with \(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1\), the portfolio beta is the weighted sum of individual asset betas:

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_p = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \beta_i \end{aligned} \]

In particular, the beta of the market portfolio itself is identically 1 (\(\beta_m = \Var(r_m)/\Var(r_m) = 1\)), and the beta of the riskless asset is identically 0 (\(\beta_f = \Cov(r_f, r_m)/\Var(r_m) = 0\)).

3Decomposition of Risk: Systematic versus Idiosyncratic

Consider the orthogonal decomposition of asset \(i\)'s excess return on the market excess return:

\[ \begin{aligned} r_i - r_f = \beta_i (r_m - r_f) + \varepsilon_i \end{aligned} \]

By construction of \(\beta_i = \Cov(r_i, r_m)/\Var(r_m)\), the residual disturbance \(\varepsilon_i\) has zero mean and is uncorrelated with the market return:

\[ \begin{aligned} E[\varepsilon_i] &= E[r_i - r_f] - \beta_i E[r_m - r_f] = 0 \\ \Cov(\varepsilon_i, r_m - r_f) &= \Cov(r_i - r_f, r_m - r_f) - \beta_i \Var(r_m - r_f) = \sigma_{im} - \frac{\sigma_{im}}{\sigma_m^2} \sigma_m^2 = 0 \end{aligned} \]

Taking the variance of both sides gives the fundamental variance decomposition:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Var(r_i) = \sigma_i^2 = \beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon_i}^2 \end{aligned} \]
  • Systematic Risk (\(\beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2\)): The portion of total volatility driven by exposure to common macroeconomic fluctuations. This risk cannot be eliminated through diversification and earns an equilibrium risk premium \(\beta_i (E[r_m] - r_f)\).
  • Idiosyncratic Risk (\(\sigma_{\varepsilon_i}^2\)): The firm-specific portion of volatility. Because idiosyncratic shocks average to zero in a well-diversified portfolio, competitive financial markets attach zero equilibrium compensation to \(\sigma_{\varepsilon_i}^2\).

Lab 1 · Equilibrium CAPM and beta in exact arithmetic

Verifies that the market portfolio in competitive equilibrium equals the tangency portfolio, and that asset expected returns satisfy the CAPM pricing equation exactly in Fraction arithmetic.

"""Lab AP2-L04-1: The CAPM pricing equation and beta in market equilibrium.

Hypothetical parameters (3 assets), not real data.
In competitive equilibrium with identical beliefs and mean-variance utility,
the market portfolio clears as the tangency portfolio.
Verifies that beta from covariances satisfies the CAPM pricing equation exactly.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def invert_3x3(mat: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[list[Fraction]]:
    """Invert 3x3 matrix using exact row elimination."""
    n = 3
    aug = [[mat[i][j] for j in range(n)] + [Fraction(1 if i == k else 0) for k in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        p = aug[i][i]
        for j in range(2 * n):
            aug[i][j] /= p
        for r in range(n):
            if r != i:
                f = aug[r][i]
                for j in range(2 * n):
                    aug[r][j] -= f * aug[i][j]
    return [[aug[i][n + j] for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def main() -> None:
    cov = [
        [Fraction(4, 100), Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(1, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(5, 100), Fraction(2, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(2, 100), Fraction(6, 100)],
    ]
    mu = [Fraction(6, 100), Fraction(10, 100), Fraction(12, 100)]
    r_f = Fraction(2, 100)

    # In equilibrium, market portfolio equals tangency portfolio
    inv = invert_3x3(cov)
    excess = [mu[i] - r_f for i in range(3)]
    inv_excess = [sum((inv[i][j] * excess[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    denom_t = sum(inv_excess, Fraction(0, 1))
    w_m = [inv_excess[i] / denom_t for i in range(3)]

    # Market expected return and variance
    mu_m = sum((w_m[i] * mu[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
    cov_i_m = [sum((cov[i][j] * w_m[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    var_m = sum((w_m[i] * cov_i_m[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))

    # Market beta: beta_i = Cov(r_i, r_m) / Var(r_m)
    betas = [cov_i_m[i] / var_m for i in range(3)]

    # CAPM pricing equation: E[r_i] - r_f = beta_i * (E[r_m] - r_f)
    equity_risk_premium = mu_m - r_f
    capm_excess = [betas[i] * equity_risk_premium for i in range(3)]
    capm_expected = [r_f + capm_excess[i] for i in range(3)]

    print(f"Market weights: [{w_m[0]}, {w_m[1]}, {w_m[2]}]")
    print(f"Market expected return E[r_m]: {mu_m}")
    print(f"Market variance Var(r_m): {var_m}")
    print(f"Equity risk premium E[r_m] - r_f: {equity_risk_premium}")
    print(f"Asset betas: [{betas[0]}, {betas[1]}, {betas[2]}]")
    print(f"CAPM expected returns: [{capm_expected[0]}, {capm_expected[1]}, {capm_expected[2]}]")
    print(f"Actual expected returns: [{mu[0]}, {mu[1]}, {mu[2]}]")
    print(f"CAPM identity holds exactly: {capm_expected == mu}")

4Where the canon breaks

The CAPM is an elegant benchmark whose real-world application faces severe foundational frictions:

  • Homogeneous Beliefs: In reality, investors hold divergent expectations regarding future cash flows, leading to trade even without liquidity shocks and creating dispersed subjective frontiers.
  • Frictionless Borrowing at \(r_f\): Borrowing rates exceed lending rates due to default risk and intermediary margins, creating leverage constraints that tilt demand toward high-beta assets and flatten the risk-return line.
  • Single-Period Horizon: Real investors care about multi-period consumption, stochastic interest rates, and changing investment opportunity sets, necessitating intertemporal hedging terms (addressed in Lesson 9).
  • Market Portfolio Unobservability: As demonstrated by Roll (1977) and explored in Lesson 7, the true market portfolio contains all wealth (human capital, private businesses, real estate), rendering empirical tests joint tests of the model and the chosen proxy.

5Misconceptions

  1. “High total volatility implies high expected return under the CAPM.”

    False. The CAPM dictates that expected return is proportional only to systematic risk \(\beta_i\), not total variance \(\sigma_i^2\). A biotech stock with 90% idiosyncratic volatility and zero covariance with the market has \(\beta_i = 0\) and earns an expected return equal to the riskless rate \(r_f\). Idiosyncratic risk is uncompensated because costless diversification eliminates it.

  2. “Beta must always be positive.”

    False. If an asset has negative covariance with the market (\(\Cov(r_i, r_m) < 0\)), its beta is negative (\(\beta_i < 0\)). Such an asset acts as insurance against market crashes. Consequently, investors accept an expected return strictly below the riskless rate (\(E[r_i] < r_f\)) in exchange for positive payoffs during market downturns.

  3. “An asset with \(\beta = 1\) has the same total volatility as the market portfolio.”

    False. Total variance is \(\sigma_i^2 = \beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon_i}^2\). If \(\beta_i = 1\), total variance is \(\sigma_i^2 = \sigma_m^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon_i}^2 \ge \sigma_m^2\). The asset has the same volatility as the market if and only if its idiosyncratic variance is zero (\(\sigma_{\varepsilon_i}^2 = 0\)).

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · CAPM expected return calculation

The riskless rate is \(r_f = 0.035\) and the expected return on the market portfolio is \(E[r_m] = 0.095\). Asset A has \(\beta_A = 0.8\) and Asset B has \(\beta_B = 1.4\). Compute the expected return for each asset and for a portfolio consisting of 60% Asset A and 40% Asset B.

Solution

The equity risk premium is \(E[r_m] - r_f = 0.095 - 0.035 = 0.060\) (6.0%). For Asset A: \(E[r_A] = r_f + \beta_A(E[r_m] - r_f) = 0.035 + 0.8(0.060) = 0.035 + 0.048 = 0.083\) (8.3%). For Asset B: \(E[r_B] = 0.035 + 1.4(0.060) = 0.035 + 0.084 = 0.119\) (11.9%). For the portfolio: \(\beta_p = 0.6(0.8) + 0.4(1.4) = 0.48 + 0.56 = 1.04\). The portfolio expected return is \(E[r_p] = 0.035 + 1.04(0.060) = 0.035 + 0.0624 = 0.0974\) (9.74%), which matches \(0.6(0.083) + 0.4(0.119) = 0.0498 + 0.0476 = 0.0974\).

M2 · Beta calculation from covariance and correlation

An asset has standard deviation \(\sigma_i = 0.25\). The market portfolio has volatility \(\sigma_m = 0.15\). The correlation between the asset and the market is \(\Corr(r_i, r_m) = 0.60\). Compute the asset's beta and decompose its total variance into systematic and idiosyncratic components.

Solution

Covariance is \(\Cov(r_i, r_m) = \Corr(r_i, r_m) \sigma_i \sigma_m = 0.60 \times 0.25 \times 0.15 = 0.0225\). Beta is \(\beta_i = \frac{\Cov(r_i, r_m)}{\Var(r_m)} = \frac{0.0225}{0.15^2} = \frac{0.0225}{0.0225} = 1.00\). Equivalently, \(\beta_i = \Corr(r_i, r_m) \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m} = 0.60 \times \frac{0.25}{0.15} = 1.00\). Total variance is \(\sigma_i^2 = 0.25^2 = 0.0625\). Systematic variance is \(\beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 = 1.00^2 \times 0.15^2 = 0.0225\) (36.0% of total variance). Idiosyncratic variance is \(\sigma_{\varepsilon_i}^2 = \sigma_i^2 - \beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 = 0.0625 - 0.0225 = 0.0400\) (64.0% of total variance).

M3 · Leverage and portfolio beta

An investor holds a portfolio financed with $100,000 equity and $40,000 borrowed at \(r_f = 0.03\). The $140,000 is allocated to a stock index with \(\beta = 1.10\) and \(E[r] = 0.09\). Compute the beta of the leveraged equity position and its expected return.

Solution

The portfolio allocation weights relative to equity wealth are \(w_{\text{risky}} = \frac{140,000}{100,000} = 1.40\) and \(w_f = -\frac{40,000}{100,000} = -0.40\). By linearity of beta, \(\beta_E = w_{\text{risky}} \beta_{\text{risky}} + w_f \beta_f = 1.40(1.10) + (-0.40)(0) = 1.54\). The equity expected return is \(E[r_E] = r_f + \beta_E(E[r_m] - r_f)\). Since the index has \(\beta = 1.10\) and \(E[r] = 0.09 = 0.03 + 1.10(E[r_m] - 0.03)\), we find \(E[r_m] - 0.03 = \frac{0.06}{1.10} \approx 0.054545\). Then \(E[r_E] = 0.03 + 1.54(0.054545) = 0.03 + 0.084 = 0.114\) (11.4%). Equivalently, \(1.4(0.09) - 0.4(0.03) = 0.126 - 0.012 = 0.114\).

P1 · Market beta equals one

Prove from the definition of covariance that the market portfolio has a beta of exactly 1, and that the riskless asset has a beta of exactly 0.

Solution

For the market portfolio, setting \(i = m\) in Definition 2.1 gives \(\beta_m = \frac{\Cov(r_m, r_m)}{\Var(r_m)} = \frac{\Var(r_m)}{\Var(r_m)} = 1\), since \(\Var(r_m) > 0\) under Assumption A1. For the riskless asset, \(r_f\) is deterministic with zero variance: \(\Cov(r_f, r_m) = E[(r_f - E[r_f])(r_m - E[r_m])] = E[0 \cdot (r_m - E[r_m])] = 0\). Thus, \(\beta_f = \frac{0}{\Var(r_m)} = 0\).

P2 · Portfolio beta is a linear operator

Prove that for any portfolio of \(n\) risky assets with weights \(w \in \R^n\) satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1\), the portfolio beta satisfies \(\beta_p = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \beta_i\).

Solution

The return of portfolio \(p\) is \(r_p = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i r_i\). By the bilinearity of covariance, \(\Cov(r_p, r_m) = \Cov\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i r_i, r_m\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \Cov(r_i, r_m)\). Dividing both sides by \(\Var(r_m)\) yields \(\beta_p = \frac{\Cov(r_p, r_m)}{\Var(r_m)} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \frac{\Cov(r_i, r_m)}{\Var(r_m)} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \beta_i\). Thus, beta is a linear combination of asset betas.

P3 · Orthogonality of idiosyncratic residuals

Prove that in the decomposition \(r_i - r_f = \beta_i (r_m - r_f) + \varepsilon_i\), the idiosyncratic residual \(\varepsilon_i\) has zero expected value and zero covariance with the market return.

Solution

Taking expectations of both sides: \(E[\varepsilon_i] = E[r_i - r_f] - \beta_i E[r_m - r_f] = (\mu_i - r_f) - \beta_i (\mu_m - r_f) = 0\), which vanishes identically under the CAPM pricing equation. For covariance with the market excess return: \(\Cov(\varepsilon_i, r_m - r_f) = \Cov(r_i - r_f - \beta_i (r_m - r_f), r_m - r_f) = \Cov(r_i, r_m) - \beta_i \Var(r_m) = \sigma_{im} - \frac{\sigma_{im}}{\sigma_m^2} \sigma_m^2 = \sigma_{im} - \sigma_{im} = 0\). Thus \(\varepsilon_i\) is strictly orthogonal to the market factor.

Empirical

E1 · Insurance asset with negative beta

An agricultural derivative has a beta of \(\beta_d = -0.50\). If \(r_f = 0.04\) and \(E[r_m] = 0.10\), what is the equilibrium expected return of this derivative? Explain why investors willingly hold an asset that earns less than the risk-free rate.

Solution

Under the CAPM, \(E[r_d] = r_f + \beta_d(E[r_m] - r_f) = 0.04 + (-0.50)(0.10 - 0.04) = 0.04 - 0.03 = 0.01\) (1.0%). Investors willingly hold this asset at an expected return of 1.0% (well below the 4.0% risk-free rate) because its negative covariance with the market reduces overall portfolio variance. The asset serves as an insurance hedge: when aggregate market wealth declines, the derivative yields positive returns, smoothing consumption across bad states of nature.

E2 · Beta estimation standard error and sample length

In a time-series regression of monthly excess stock returns on market excess returns over \(T = 60\) months, the residual standard deviation is \(s_\varepsilon = 0.05\) (5% monthly) and the market sample variance is \(s_m^2 = 0.002025\) (volatility 4.5%). Calculate the standard error of the estimated beta.

Solution

The OLS standard error of the slope coefficient \(\hat{\beta}\) is \(\se(\hat{\beta}) = \sqrt{\frac{s_\varepsilon^2}{\sum_{t=1}^T (r_{mt} - \bar{r}_m)^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{s_\varepsilon^2}{(T - 1) s_m^2}}\). Substituting the given values: \(\se(\hat{\beta}) = \sqrt{\frac{0.05^2}{59 \times 0.002025}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.0025}{0.119475}} \approx \sqrt{0.020925} \approx 0.1447\). A 95% confidence interval for beta has approximate width \(\pm 2 \times 0.145 \approx \pm 0.29\), illustrating that 5 years of monthly data produces considerable estimation uncertainty.

E3 · Blume beta adjustment for mean reversion

Marshall Blume (1975) observed that historical sample betas exhibit empirical mean reversion toward 1.0. Given the empirical adjustment formula \(\beta_{\text{adj}} = 0.33 + 0.67 \beta_{\text{hist}}\), calculate the adjusted betas for a utility stock with \(\beta_{\text{hist}} = 0.60\) and a technology stock with \(\beta_{\text{hist}} = 1.60\).

Solution

For the utility stock: \(\beta_{\text{adj}} = 0.33 + 0.67(0.60) = 0.33 + 0.402 = 0.732\). The adjustment shrinks the low beta upward toward 1.0. For the technology stock: \(\beta_{\text{adj}} = 0.33 + 0.67(1.60) = 0.33 + 1.072 = 1.402\). The adjustment shrinks the high beta downward toward 1.0. This shrinkage corrects for measurement error (errors-in-variables) and economic changes in operating leverage over time.

Research

R1 · Black CAPM without riskless lending or borrowing

Summarize the zero-beta CAPM of Fischer Black (1972) and explain how the pricing equation changes when no riskless borrowing or lending is available.

Solution

When investors cannot borrow or lend at a riskless rate, the market portfolio is still mean-variance efficient along the risky-asset hyperbola. Fischer Black (1972) proved that any efficient portfolio \(m\) possesses an associated "zero-beta portfolio" \(z\) on the inefficient frontier with \(\Cov(r_z, r_m) = 0\). The pricing equation becomes \(E[r_i] - E[r_z] = \beta_i (E[r_m] - E[r_z])\). The expected return on the zero-beta portfolio \(E[r_z]\) takes the place of \(r_f\), providing a theoretical explanation for an empirical intercept that exceeds the prevailing Treasury bill rate.

R2 · Heterogeneous beliefs and speculative trading

How do heterogeneous beliefs regarding expected returns affect the composition of the market portfolio and the validity of the CAPM pricing equation?

Solution

When investors hold divergent beliefs \(\mu_k \ne \mu_j\), each investor chooses a different subjective tangency portfolio \(w_{T,k}\). The market portfolio becomes a wealth-weighted average of these subjective tangencies: \(w_m = \sum_k (W_k / W) w_{T,k}\). In general, a weighted sum of frontier portfolios from different opportunity sets does not lie on any single investor's frontier. Consequently, the market portfolio is not mean-variance efficient for any individual investor, and the exact CAPM linear relation fails, producing nonzero alphas driven by speculative disagreement and short-sale constraints.

R3 · Non-traded human capital and the Mayers extension

Explain David Mayers' (1972) extension of the CAPM to include non-marketable human capital, and write the resulting asset pricing formula.

Solution

Mayers (1972) modeled an economy where total wealth comprises marketable financial assets \(V_m\) and non-marketable human capital \(V_h\) with aggregate wage return \(r_h\). Equilibrium pricing reflects covariance with total wealth \(W = V_m + V_h\). The pricing formula becomes \(E[r_i] - r_f = \beta_i^m (E[r_m] - r_f) + \beta_i^h (E[r_h] - r_f)\), where \(\beta_i^h\) reflects covariance with aggregate labor income. Stocks whose returns correlate negatively with labor income hedge career risk and command lower expected returns.

Beta is linear across portfolios: \(\beta_p = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \beta_i\), with \(\beta_m = 1\) and \(\beta_f = 0\) T Theorem 2.1 (covariance bilinearity)

8Retrieval quiz

  1. What economic condition guarantees that the market portfolio coincides with the tangency portfolio in equilibrium?

    Options

    By two-fund separation, all mean-variance investors allocate their risky wealth to the tangency portfolio. When markets clear, aggregate demand equals aggregate supply, forcing the value-weighted market portfolio to match the tangency portfolio.

  2. What is the mathematical definition of asset \(i\)'s CAPM beta \(\beta_i\)?

    Options

    Beta is the regression slope of asset return on the market return, defined as covariance with the market divided by market variance.

  3. An asset has \(\sigma_i = 0.20\), the market has \(\sigma_m = 0.12\), and their correlation is \(\Corr(r_i, r_m) = 0.75\). What is the asset's beta \(\beta_i\)?

    \(\beta_i = \Corr(r_i, r_m) \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m} = 0.75 \times \frac{0.20}{0.12} = 0.75 \times 1.6667 = 1.25\).

  4. Why does idiosyncratic risk carry zero expected return premium in the CAPM?

    Options

    In competitive equilibrium, markets only pay for risks that cannot be diversified away. Since idiosyncratic risk washes out in aggregate wealth, its equilibrium shadow price is zero.

  5. If \(r_f = 0.04\), \(E[r_m] = 0.10\), and an asset has \(\beta = 0.80\), what is its CAPM equilibrium expected return?

    \(E[r_i] = r_f + \beta_i(E[r_m] - r_f) = 0.04 + 0.80(0.10 - 0.04) = 0.04 + 0.048 = 0.088\) (8.8%).

  6. What is the beta of a portfolio with 50% in the riskless asset and 50% in the market portfolio?

    Options

    By linearity of beta, \(\beta_p = 0.5 \beta_f + 0.5 \beta_m = 0.5(0) + 0.5(1) = 0.50\).

  7. What does a negative asset beta imply about the asset's equilibrium expected return under the CAPM?

    Options

    When \(\beta_i < 0\), \(\beta_i (E[r_m] - r_f) < 0\), so \(E[r_i] = r_f + \beta_i(E[r_m] - r_f) < r_f\). The asset provides valuable portfolio insurance.

  8. Which two foundational papers introduced the Capital Asset Pricing Model?

    Options

    Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965) independently formulated the mean-variance equilibrium model that prices assets according to systematic market covariance.
