The Capital Market Line

  • Uses: tangency portfolio, two-fund separation, Sharpe ratio
  • Introduces: capital market line
  • Ttheorem

  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore

The Capital Market Line transforms the curved Markowitz boundary into a linear pricing frontier. When investors can freely borrow and lend at a riskless rate, the trade-off between total risk and expected return becomes strictly linear, with every efficient portfolio priced by a single economic quantity: the tangency Sharpe ratio.

1Equation and Geometry of the Capital Market Line

Building on the two-fund separation result of Lesson 2, consider an investor allocating a fraction \(a \in \R\) of wealth to the unique tangency portfolio \(w_T\) and the remaining fraction \(1 - a\) to the riskless asset with per-period return \(r_f\).

The return of the combined portfolio is \(r_c = (1 - a) r_f + a r_T = r_f + a (r_T - r_f)\). The expected return and variance of this combined portfolio are

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r_c] &= r_f + a (\mu_T - r_f) \\ \sigma_c^2 &= \Var(r_c) = a^2 \Var(r_T) = a^2 \sigma_T^2 \implies \sigma_c = |a| \sigma_T \end{aligned} \]
Definition 1.1 (Capital Market Line)

For non-negative allocations to the tangency portfolio (\(a \ge 0\)), substituting \(a = \sigma_c / \sigma_T\) expresses the expected return of the combined portfolio directly as an affine function of its total standard deviation \(\sigma_c\):

\[ E[r_c] = r_f + \left(\frac{\mu_T - r_f}{\sigma_T}\right) \sigma_c = r_f + \sigma_c \cdot \SR_T \]

This linear locus in \((\sigma, E[r])\) space is the capital market line. Its vertical intercept is the riskless rate \(r_f\), and its constant slope is the population Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio, \(\SR_T\).

Theorem 1.1 (Optimality and Domination along the CML)

Under Assumptions A1–A4, the Capital Market Line dominates all individual risky assets and all non-tangency portfolios in mean-standard-deviation space:

  1. For any individual asset \(i\) or any portfolio \(p\), its Sharpe ratio satisfies \(\SR_p \le \SR_T\).
  2. For any given level of volatility \(\sigma\), the expected return on the CML strictly exceeds the expected return of any inefficient portfolio with identical volatility: \(E[r_{\text{CML}}(\sigma)] \ge E[r_p(\sigma)]\), with equality if and only if \(p = w_T\).
Proof

Recall from Lesson 2 that for any asset \(i\), its covariance with the tangency portfolio satisfies \(\Cov(r_i, r_T) = \frac{\mu_i - r_f}{\mu_T - r_f} \sigma_T^2\). By the definition of the correlation coefficient,

\[ \Corr(r_i, r_T) = \frac{\Cov(r_i, r_T)}{\sigma_i \sigma_T} = \frac{\mu_i - r_f}{\mu_T - r_f} \frac{\sigma_T^2}{\sigma_i \sigma_T} = \left(\frac{\mu_i - r_f}{\sigma_i}\right) \left(\frac{\sigma_T}{\mu_T - r_f}\right) = \frac{\SR_i}{\SR_T} \]

Because correlation is bounded by \(-1 \le \Corr(r_i, r_T) \le 1\), it follows immediately that

\[ \frac{\SR_i}{\SR_T} \le 1 \implies \SR_i \le \SR_T \]

Equality holds if and only if \(\Corr(r_i, r_T) = 1\), which occurs if and only if asset \(i\) is a positive linear transformation of the tangency portfolio return. For any target standard deviation \(\sigma > 0\), the CML offers expected return \(r_f + \sigma \SR_T\), whereas a portfolio \(p\) with standard deviation \(\sigma\) offers \(r_f + \sigma \SR_p\). Since \(\SR_p \le \SR_T\), \(E[r_{\text{CML}}] \ge E[r_p]\) strictly whenever \(p\) is not the tangency portfolio.

2Lending and Borrowing Regions

The parameter \(a\) defines two distinct financial operating regions along the Capital Market Line:

  • Lending Region (\(0 \le a < 1\)): The investor places a positive fraction \(1 - a > 0\) of wealth in the riskless asset and the remaining fraction \(a\) in the tangency portfolio. Portfolio volatility satisfies \(\sigma_c < \sigma_T\), and expected return satisfies \(r_f \le E[r_c] < \mu_T\). This region accommodates highly risk-averse investors who require lower risk than the pure tangency portfolio.
  • Pure Tangency (\(a = 1\)): Wealth is 100% invested in the tangency portfolio, with \(\sigma_c = \sigma_T\) and \(E[r_c] = \mu_T\).
  • Borrowing / Leveraged Region (\(a > 1\)): The investor borrows cash at the riskless rate \(r_f\) by selling the riskless asset short (\(1 - a < 0\)) and invests more than 100% of wealth into the tangency portfolio. Here \(\sigma_c > \sigma_T\) and \(E[r_c] > \mu_T\). This region accommodates aggressively risk-tolerant investors who leverage systematic risk.

Lab 1 · Capital Market Line allocations and Sharpe slope verification

Verifies that a grid of allocations a in [0.0, 2.0] traces out a straight line whose points lie exactly on the CML, with slope equal to the tangency Sharpe ratio to machine precision.

"""Lab AP2-L03-1: The Capital Market Line and Sharpe ratio.

Hypothetical parameters (3 assets), not real data.
Constructs the Capital Market Line combining the riskless asset and tangency portfolio,
evaluates a grid of allocations, and verifies that the slope equals the tangency Sharpe ratio.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction


def invert_3x3(mat: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[list[Fraction]]:
    """Invert 3x3 matrix using exact row elimination."""
    n = 3
    aug = [[mat[i][j] for j in range(n)] + [Fraction(1 if i == k else 0) for k in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        p = aug[i][i]
        for j in range(2 * n):
            aug[i][j] /= p
        for r in range(n):
            if r != i:
                f = aug[r][i]
                for j in range(2 * n):
                    aug[r][j] -= f * aug[i][j]
    return [[aug[i][n + j] for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]


def main() -> None:
    cov = [
        [Fraction(4, 100), Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(1, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(5, 100), Fraction(2, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(2, 100), Fraction(6, 100)],
    ]
    mu = [Fraction(6, 100), Fraction(10, 100), Fraction(12, 100)]
    r_f = Fraction(2, 100)

    inv = invert_3x3(cov)
    excess = [mu[i] - r_f for i in range(3)]
    inv_excess = [sum((inv[i][j] * excess[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    denom_t = sum(inv_excess, Fraction(0, 1))
    w_t = [inv_excess[i] / denom_t for i in range(3)]

    mu_t_frac = sum((w_t[i] * mu[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
    sig_w_term = [sum((cov[i][j] * w_t[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
    var_t_frac = sum((w_t[i] * sig_w_term[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))

    mu_t = float(mu_t_frac)
    sigma_t = math.sqrt(float(var_t_frac))
    r_f_float = float(r_f)
    sr_t = (mu_t - r_f_float) / sigma_t

    print(f"Tangency expected return: {mu_t:.6f}")
    print(f"Tangency volatility: {sigma_t:.6f}")
    print(f"Tangency Sharpe ratio (CML slope): {sr_t:.6f}")

    # Grid of tangency allocations w_T
    grid_w = [0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 2.0]
    for w in grid_w:
        e_p = (1.0 - w) * r_f_float + w * mu_t
        sigma_p = w * sigma_t
        cml_predicted = r_f_float + sr_t * sigma_p
        diff = abs(e_p - cml_predicted)
        print(f"w_T={w:.2f}: sigma_p={sigma_p:.6f}, E[r_p]={e_p:.6f}, CML={cml_predicted:.6f}, diff={diff:.2e}")

3Where the canon breaks

The classical Capital Market Line assumes unlimited borrowing at the riskless rate \(r_f\). In actual financial markets, margin requirements under Regulation T restrict leverage for retail investors, while prime brokers impose haircuts and variable borrowing rates for hedge funds. When borrowing is prohibited or subject to a spread \(r_{f,\text{borrow}} > r_f\), the CML is kinked at \(a = 1\). Beyond the tangency portfolio, investors cannot trace the theoretical CML ray; instead, they must either move along the risky Markowitz frontier toward higher-volatility individual assets or pay higher debit interest, which flattens the effective risk-return trade-off in the leveraged region.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Individual stocks lie on the Capital Market Line.”

    False. The Capital Market Line prices only efficient portfolios that combine the riskless asset with the tangency portfolio. Because individual stocks contain idiosyncratic (diversifiable) risk, their correlation with the tangency portfolio is strictly less than 1, placing them strictly below the CML in \((\sigma, E[r])\) space. Individual stocks are priced along the Security Market Line (SML), not the CML.

  2. “The CML slope depends on the investor's risk aversion.”

    False. The slope of the CML is the population Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio \(\SR_T = (\mu_T - r_f)/\sigma_T\), which depends purely on the objective investment opportunity set (\(\mu, \Sigma, r_f\)). An investor's risk aversion determines where along the CML they choose to locate, but does not alter the slope or position of the line itself.

  3. “Shorting the tangency portfolio (\(a < 0\)) traces the CML downward to the left.”

    False. Standard deviation is strictly non-negative: \(\sigma_c = |a| \sigma_T \ge 0\). If an investor shorts the tangency portfolio (\(a < 0\)), portfolio expected return drops below \(r_f\) while volatility increases: \(E[r_c] = r_f - |a|(\mu_T - r_f) = r_f - \sigma_c \SR_T\). This traces an inefficient ray sloping downward to the right in the lower half-plane, which is strictly dominated by holding the riskless asset.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · CML expected return calculation

The riskless rate is \(r_f = 0.03\). The tangency portfolio has expected return \(\mu_T = 0.11\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_T = 0.16\). An efficient portfolio on the CML has standard deviation \(\sigma_p = 0.24\). What is its expected return?

Solution

Compute the tangency Sharpe ratio: \(\SR_T = \frac{\mu_T - r_f}{\sigma_T} = \frac{0.11 - 0.03}{0.16} = \frac{0.08}{0.16} = 0.50\). Using the CML equation: \(E[r_p] = r_f + \sigma_p \cdot \SR_T = 0.03 + (0.24)(0.50) = 0.03 + 0.12 = 0.15\) (15.0%). The allocation to the tangency portfolio is \(a = \sigma_p / \sigma_T = 0.24 / 0.16 = 1.50\), which requires 50% leverage.

M2 · Required volatility for target return

Using the parameters from M1 (\(r_f = 0.03, \SR_T = 0.50\)), what volatility \(\sigma_p\) must an investor accept along the CML to achieve an expected return of \(E[r_p] = 0.09\)?

Solution

Invert the CML equation: \(E[r_p] = r_f + \sigma_p \SR_T \implies \sigma_p = \frac{E[r_p] - r_f}{\SR_T}\). Substituting values: \(\sigma_p = \frac{0.09 - 0.03}{0.50} = \frac{0.06}{0.50} = 0.12\) (12.0%). The investor achieves this with weight \(a = \sigma_p / \sigma_T = 0.12 / 0.16 = 0.75\) in the tangency portfolio and \(0.25\) in the riskless asset.

M3 · Leverage and borrowing cost

An investor with $100,000 equity borrows $50,000 at \(r_f = 0.04\) to invest a total of $150,000 in the tangency portfolio (\(\mu_T = 0.12, \sigma_T = 0.18\)). Calculate the expected dollar gain, expected return on equity, and volatility of the equity position.

Solution

The investment in the tangency portfolio is $150,000, so the weight is \(a = 150,000 / 100,000 = 1.50\). Expected gross return on the risky asset is \(150,000 \times 0.12 = \$18,000\). Interest paid on the borrowed $50,000 is \(50,000 \times 0.04 = \$2,000\). Expected net dollar gain on equity is \(18,000 - 2,000 = \$16,000\). Expected return on equity is \(\frac{16,000}{100,000} = 0.16\) (16.0%). By the CML, \(E[r_c] = r_f + a(\mu_T - r_f) = 0.04 + 1.5(0.08) = 0.16\). The standard deviation of equity return is \(\sigma_c = a \sigma_T = 1.5 \times 0.18 = 0.27\) (27.0%).

Proof

P1 · Proof that inefficient assets lie strictly below the CML

Prove that if an asset or portfolio \(p\) has \(\Corr(r_p, r_T) < 1\), it lies strictly below the Capital Market Line in \((\sigma, E[r])\) space.

Solution

From Theorem 1.1, the Sharpe ratio of any asset or portfolio satisfies \(\SR_p = \Corr(r_p, r_T) \SR_T\). The expected excess return of portfolio \(p\) is \(\mu_p - r_f = \sigma_p \SR_p = \sigma_p \Corr(r_p, r_T) \SR_T\). A point on the CML with identical volatility \(\sigma_p\) has expected excess return \(\mu_{\text{CML}}(\sigma_p) - r_f = \sigma_p \SR_T\). The difference between the CML return and portfolio \(p\)'s return is \(\mu_{\text{CML}}(\sigma_p) - \mu_p = \sigma_p \SR_T [1 - \Corr(r_p, r_T)]\). Since \(\sigma_p > 0\) and \(\SR_T > 0\), the assumption \(\Corr(r_p, r_T) < 1\) implies \(1 - \Corr(r_p, r_T) > 0\), so \(\mu_{\text{CML}}(\sigma_p) - \mu_p > 0\). Hence, portfolio \(p\) lies strictly below the CML.

P2 · CML as envelope of risky portfolios

Prove that the Capital Market Line is the upper envelope of all possible rays emanating from \((0, r_f)\) that pass through feasible risky portfolios.

Solution

Any ray from \((0, r_f)\) passing through an arbitrary feasible risky portfolio \(w\) with \(\iota^\top w = 1\) has equation \(E[r] = r_f + \sigma \left(\frac{w^\top \mu - r_f}{\sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}}\right) = r_f + \sigma \SR(w)\). By definition, the upper envelope of a family of linear rays emanating from a single point \((0, r_f)\) is the ray with the maximum slope: \(\max_{w: \iota^\top w = 1} \SR(w)\). In Theorem 1.1 of Lesson 2, we proved that \(\SR(w)\) achieves its unique global maximum over the feasible set \(\{w : \iota^\top w = 1\}\) at \(w = w_T\). Therefore, the ray with slope \(\SR(w_T) = \SR_T\) forms the unique upper envelope for all \(\sigma \ge 0\).

P3 · Distance between inefficient asset and CML

Express the vertical distance between the CML and an individual asset \(i\) in terms of the asset's idiosyncratic volatility.

Solution

Let asset \(i\) have return \(r_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i r_T + \varepsilon_i\), with \(\Cov(r_T, \varepsilon_i) = 0\). Total variance is \(\sigma_i^2 = \beta_i^2 \sigma_T^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^2\). The vertical distance at volatility \(\sigma_i\) is \(\Delta = [r_f + \sigma_i \SR_T] - \mu_i = \sigma_i \SR_T - (\mu_i - r_f)\). Since \(\mu_i - r_f = \beta_i (\mu_T - r_f)\) under tangency efficiency, we have \(\Delta = \sigma_i \SR_T - \beta_i \sigma_T \SR_T = (\sigma_i - \beta_i \sigma_T) \SR_T\). Squaring the relationship: \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\beta_i^2 \sigma_T^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^2}\). Thus, \(\Delta = \left(\sqrt{\beta_i^2 \sigma_T^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^2} - \beta_i \sigma_T\right)\SR_T\). Rationalizing the numerator yields \(\Delta = \frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^2}{\sqrt{\beta_i^2 \sigma_T^2 + \sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^2} + \beta_i \sigma_T} \SR_T = \frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^2}{\sigma_i + \beta_i \sigma_T} \SR_T\). This proves that the vertical shortfall below the CML is strictly positive and directly proportional to the asset's idiosyncratic variance \(\sigma_{\varepsilon,i}^2\).

Empirical

E1 · Borrowing rate spread and the kinked CML

Suppose an investor can lend at \(r_{f,\text{lend}} = 0.02\) but must borrow at \(r_{f,\text{borrow}} = 0.05\). The tangency portfolio for lending has \(\mu_{T1} = 0.10, \sigma_{T1} = 0.15\), and the tangency portfolio for borrowing has \(\mu_{T2} = 0.12, \sigma_{T2} = 0.20\). Graphically and analytically describe the investor's efficient frontier.

Solution

The efficient set is divided into three distinct regions: (1) For \(\sigma \le 0.15\), the investor lends: the frontier is a straight ray \(E[r] = 0.02 + \sigma \left(\frac{0.10 - 0.02}{0.15}\right) = 0.02 + 0.5333 \sigma\). (2) For \(0.15 \le \sigma \le 0.20\), neither lending nor borrowing is optimal: the investor moves along the risky Markowitz frontier between \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), tracing the curved hyperbola segment. (3) For \(\sigma \ge 0.20\), the investor borrows at 5%: the frontier becomes a second straight ray \(E[r] = 0.05 + \sigma \left(\frac{0.12 - 0.05}{0.20}\right) = 0.05 + 0.3500 \sigma\). The slope flattens from \(0.5333\) to \(0.3500\) across the kink, representing the penalty of the borrowing spread.

E2 · Tracking error and active management

Explain why active mutual fund managers who promise "alpha" frequently construct portfolios that lie well below the Capital Market Line.

Solution

Active fund managers take concentrated positions in stocks they believe are undervalued, deliberately incurring idiosyncratic risk \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 > 0\). As proved in problem P3, an asset or portfolio's vertical distance below the CML is proportional to \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Unless the manager's alpha \(\alpha\) is extraordinarily large relative to tracking error variance (\(\alpha > \Delta\)), the uncompensated idiosyncratic volatility degrades the portfolio's Sharpe ratio below that of the passive tangency portfolio. Empirical studies (e.g., Fama and French, 2010) show that net of fees, fewer than 2% of active funds achieve a Sharpe ratio that exceeds the CML benchmark.

E3 · Historical equity Sharpe ratio benchmark

Using long-term US historical data (approximate annual equity market excess return of 6.0% and volatility of 18.0%), calculate the historical CML slope and discuss its economic magnitude.

Solution

The historical annualized market Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_m = \frac{0.06}{0.18} \approx 0.3333\). Under the CML, an investor taking a 10% annual volatility expects an excess return of \(0.10 \times 0.3333 = 3.33\%\). To achieve an expected excess return of 10%, an investor must bear a standard deviation of \(\frac{0.10}{0.3333} = 30\%\). In monthly terms, this corresponds to a Sharpe ratio of \(\frac{0.3333}{\sqrt{12}} \approx 0.0962\) per month. This annualized figure of 0.30–0.40 represents the standard empirical hurdle for market-wide risk-adjusted return across developed equity markets.

Research

R1 · Leverage constraints and the Betting Against Beta factor

Summarize the theoretical model of Frazzini and Pedersen (2014) explaining how leverage constraints distort the slope of the empirical CML into the "Betting Against Beta" anomaly.

Solution

Frazzini and Pedersen (2014) develop an equilibrium model where a subset of investors (such as individuals, mutual funds, and pension funds) face borrowing constraints \(a \le 1\). To achieve high expected returns without borrowing, constrained investors overweight high-beta assets. Unconstrained investors (such as hedge funds) can leverage, but require an extra return premium to bear margin risk. In equilibrium, high-beta assets become overpriced and offer low risk-adjusted returns (\(\SR_{\text{high}} < \SR_m\)), while low-beta assets are underpriced and offer high risk-adjusted returns (\(\SR_{\text{low}} > \SR_m\)). The resulting Betting Against Beta (BAB) strategy—long leveraged low-beta assets, short deleveraged high-beta assets—yields significant positive alpha, demonstrating a structural departure from the single unconstrained CML.

R2 · Volatility timing and dynamic CML shifting

How does time-varying market volatility (e.g., GARCH clustering) invalidate the static CML, and what dynamic strategy restores conditional efficiency?

Solution

In a dynamic market, conditional variance \(\sigma_{t}^2\) fluctuates significantly while the expected equity risk premium \(\mu_t - r_f\) changes more slowly. Consequently, the conditional CML slope \(\SR_t = (\mu_t - r_f)/\sigma_t\) fluctuates over time. A static investor holding fixed weights in the tangency portfolio experiences fluctuating portfolio risk and suboptimal multi-period compounding. Moreira and Muir (2017) show that a "volatility timing" strategy—scaling allocation inversely with conditional variance, \(a_t \propto 1/\sigma_t^2\)—reduces exposure during volatile market regimes and increases exposure during tranquil regimes, generating significant alphas and proving that conditional CML efficiency requires dynamic leverage scaling.

R3 · Inflation risk and real versus nominal CML

Under stochastic inflation, is a Treasury bill truly riskless? Derive the real CML when the nominal riskless asset has uncertain purchasing power.

Solution

Let nominal return on the riskless bond be \(R_f\), and let inflation be \(\pi\) with \(\Var(\pi) = \sigma_\pi^2 > 0\). The real return on the "riskless" asset is approximately \(r_f^{\text{real}} \approx R_f - \pi\), which has variance \(\Var(r_f^{\text{real}}) = \sigma_\pi^2 > 0\) and covariance with risky assets \(\Cov(r_i, r_f^{\text{real}}) = -\Cov(r_i, \pi)\). Consequently, in real terms, no truly riskless asset exists (unless inflation-indexed bonds like TIPS are traded without default or liquidity risk). The real efficient frontier reverts to the Markowitz hyperbola without a riskless asset, and the straight CML is replaced by a curved boundary where the vertex represents the minimum-variance real asset (which typically holds a mix of cash, TIPS, and inflation-hedging commodities).

7Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the mathematical equation of the Capital Market Line?

    Options

    The CML relates total portfolio risk \(\sigma_p\) to expected return \(E[r_p]\) linearly, with intercept \(r_f\) and slope equal to the tangency Sharpe ratio \(\SR_T = (\mu_T - r_f)/\sigma_T\).

  2. What economic quantity represents the slope of the Capital Market Line?

    Options

    Differentiating \(E[r_p]\) with respect to \(\sigma_p\) along the CML yields \(\frac{d E[r_p]}{d \sigma_p} = \frac{\mu_T - r_f}{\sigma_T} = \SR_T\).

  3. Why do individual risky stocks lie strictly below the Capital Market Line?

    Options

    By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(\SR_i = \Corr(r_i, r_T) \SR_T\). Because individual stocks carry uncompensated idiosyncratic risk, \(\Corr(r_i, r_T) < 1\), placing them below the CML.

  4. If the riskless rate is \(r_f = 0.02\), the tangency Sharpe ratio is \(\SR_T = 0.50\), and an efficient portfolio has standard deviation \(\sigma_p = 0.20\), what is its expected return?

    \(E[r_p] = r_f + \sigma_p \SR_T = 0.02 + 0.20 \times 0.50 = 0.02 + 0.10 = 0.12\) (12%).

  5. What allocation corresponds to the "borrowing region" of the Capital Market Line?

    Options

    When \(a > 1\), the investor borrows cash at \(r_f\) to invest more than 100% of wealth into the tangency portfolio, achieving higher expected return and volatility.

  6. If the tangency portfolio has volatility \(\sigma_T = 0.16\), what weight \(a\) must an investor place on the tangency portfolio to achieve a portfolio standard deviation \(\sigma_c = 0.20\)?

    \(a = \frac{\sigma_c}{\sigma_T} = \frac{0.20}{0.16} = 1.25\).

  7. How does an increase in the riskless borrowing rate \(r_{f,\text{borrow}} > r_{f,\text{lend}}\) affect the Capital Market Line?

    Options

    A higher borrowing rate reduces the Sharpe ratio of leveraged portfolios, creating a kinked frontier with lower slope for \(\sigma > \sigma_T\).

  8. What is the difference between the Capital Market Line (CML) and the Security Market Line (SML)?

    Options

    The CML uses total volatility \(\sigma\) and prices only efficient combinations; the SML uses systematic beta \(\beta\) and prices every individual asset and portfolio in equilibrium.
