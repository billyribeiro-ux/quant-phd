Richard Roll's 1977 critique revealed a profound mathematical identity at the heart of empirical asset pricing: testing the CAPM is mathematically equivalent to testing whether the chosen market index is mean-variance efficient. Because the true market portfolio contains all marketable and non-marketable wealth, empirical tests of the CAPM are joint tests of the model and the index proxy.

1Equivalence of the Exact SML and Benchmark Efficiency

In empirical practice, researchers test the security market line using an observable equity market index \(p\) (such as the S&P 500 or the CRSP value-weighted index) as a proxy for the unobservable true market portfolio. Roll (1977) proved that the existence of an exact linear SML with respect to an arbitrary portfolio \(p\) is an algebraic identity that holds if and only if portfolio \(p\) is mean-variance efficient.

Consider the standard investment universe satisfying Assumptions A1–A3, augmented by:

  • Assumption A9 (Non-Riskless Benchmark Mean): The candidate benchmark portfolio \(p\) has an expected return distinct from the riskless rate: \(E[r_p] \ne r_f\).
Theorem 1.1 (Roll's Equivalence Theorem)

Under Assumptions A1–A3 and A9, an exact Security Market Line holds for all \(n\) individual assets with respect to a candidate portfolio \(p\) with weights \(w \in \R^n\) (\(\iota' w = 1\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_i - r_f = \beta_{i,p} (\mu_p - r_f) \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, n \end{aligned} \]

if and only if portfolio \(p\) is the unique tangency portfolio \(w_T\).

Proof

We prove both directions of the equivalence.

(\(\Leftarrow\)) Sufficiency: Suppose \(w = w_T\). By the first-order condition derived in Lesson 2, the tangency portfolio weights satisfy \(\Sigma w_T = c (\mu - r_f \iota)\), where \(c = \frac{1}{\iota' \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota)}\). Multiplying by \(w_T'\) yields \(\sigma_T^2 = w_T' \Sigma w_T = c (\mu_T - r_f)\), which implies \(c = \frac{\sigma_T^2}{\mu_T - r_f}\). The \(i\)-th element of \(\Sigma w_T\) is the covariance \(\Cov(r_i, r_T)\). Thus,

\[ \begin{aligned} \Cov(r_i, r_T) = \frac{\sigma_T^2}{\mu_T - r_f} (\mu_i - r_f) \implies \mu_i - r_f = \frac{\Cov(r_i, r_T)}{\sigma_T^2} (\mu_T - r_f) = \beta_{i,T} (\mu_T - r_f) \end{aligned} \]

This holds for every asset \(i = 1, \dots, n\). Hence, tangency efficiency implies an exact SML.

(\(\Rightarrow\)) Necessity: Suppose an exact SML holds for all \(n\) assets with respect to portfolio \(p\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_i - r_f = \frac{\Cov(r_i, r_p)}{\sigma_p^2} (\mu_p - r_f) \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, n \end{aligned} \]

Writing this in vector notation across all \(n\) assets: \(\mu - r_f \iota = \frac{\mu_p - r_f}{\sigma_p^2} \Sigma w\). Because \(\mu_p - r_f \ne 0\) by Assumption A9, we can solve for \(\Sigma w\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Sigma w = \left(\frac{\sigma_p^2}{\mu_p - r_f}\right) (\mu - r_f \iota) \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\Sigma\) is symmetric and positive definite (Assumption A1), its inverse \(\Sigma^{-1}\) exists. Multiplying by \(\Sigma^{-1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} w = \left(\frac{\sigma_p^2}{\mu_p - r_f}\right) \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota) \end{aligned} \]

Since portfolio weights must sum to 1 (\(\iota' w = 1\)), we take the inner product with \(\iota'\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 = \iota' w = \left(\frac{\sigma_p^2}{\mu_p - r_f}\right) \iota' \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota) \implies \left(\frac{\sigma_p^2}{\mu_p - r_f}\right) = \frac{1}{\iota' \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota)} \end{aligned} \]

Substituting this scalar back into the expression for \(w\) yields

\[ \begin{aligned} w = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)}{\iota' \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r_f \iota)} = w_T \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, portfolio \(p\) must be identical to the tangency portfolio \(w_T\). An exact linear SML cannot hold with respect to any portfolio that is not mean-variance efficient.

2The Roll Critique and Empirical Testability

The mathematical identity established in Theorem 1.1 leads directly to the foundational methodological critique articulated by Richard Roll.

Definition 2.1 (The Roll Critique)

The roll critique asserts two interrelated propositions regarding the empirical testability of the CAPM:

  1. The Only Testable Implication: The only economic hypothesis of the CAPM is that the true aggregate wealth portfolio of the economy is mean-variance efficient. The linearity of the Security Market Line is an exact mathematical tautology of efficiency, not an independent economic hypothesis.
  2. Unobservability and Joint Hypothesis Problem: The true market portfolio contains all invested wealth in the global economy—including non-public equities, debt securities, real estate, precious commodities, and non-marketable human capital. Because aggregate wealth is unobservable, any empirical test must substitute a proxy index (such as the S&P 500). Consequently, any test of the CAPM is an inseparable joint test of the underlying model and the mean-variance efficiency of the chosen proxy index.
Evaluation 2.1 (Empirical Consequences and Proxy Sensitivity)

As documented by Roll (1977), substituting an inefficient market proxy produces severe distortions in empirical tests:

  • False Rejections: The true aggregate market portfolio could be perfectly mean-variance efficient, yet if the chosen equity proxy lies slightly inside the efficient frontier, empirical tests will reject the CAPM and detect spurious pricing errors (\(\alpha \ne 0\)).
  • False Acceptances: The true market portfolio could be inefficient, yet an arbitrarily chosen proxy that happens to lie close to the ex-post sample efficient frontier will generate a linear SML and accept the CAPM.
  • Beta Instability: Estimated betas are highly sensitive to the composition of the proxy. Shifting the benchmark from an equity-only index to an index including corporate bonds or real estate reshuffles the cross-sectional ranking of asset betas.

Counter-evidence: Stambaugh (1982) tested the sensitivity of CAPM tests to broader market proxies including bonds, real estate, and consumer durables. He found that inferences regarding the CAPM were largely insensitive to the inclusion of non-stock assets because equity returns dominate the volatility of broader wealth indices. Nonetheless, the unobservability of human capital remains an unresolved theoretical barrier.

3Computational Demonstration

We demonstrate Roll's equivalence theorem in exact rational arithmetic on the 3-asset economy from Lesson 2. We evaluate the SML pricing equation against: (1) the true tangency portfolio \(w_T = [21/131, 49/131, 61/131]\), and (2) an inefficient equal-weighted proxy \(w_{\text{proxy}} = [1/3, 1/3, 1/3]\).

Lab 1 · The Roll Critique: proxy efficiency and the exact Security Market Line

Verifies that the SML pricing errors (alphas) are identically zero when evaluated against the true tangency portfolio, and strictly non-zero when evaluated against an equal-weighted inefficient proxy.

"""Lab AP2-L07-1: The Roll Critique: proxy efficiency and the exact Security Market Line.

Hypothetical parameters (3 assets), not real data.
Evaluates the SML pricing equation against:
(1) the true tangency portfolio w_T, and
(2) an inefficient market proxy w_proxy (equal weights [1/3, 1/3, 1/3]).
Demonstrates that the SML holds if and only if the proxy is mean-variance efficient.
All calculations performed in exact Fraction arithmetic.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    cov = [
        [Fraction(4, 100), Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(1, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(5, 100), Fraction(2, 100)],
        [Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(2, 100), Fraction(6, 100)],
    ]
    mu = [Fraction(6, 100), Fraction(10, 100), Fraction(12, 100)]
    r_f = Fraction(2, 100)

    # Tangency portfolio from L02
    w_t = [Fraction(21, 131), Fraction(49, 131), Fraction(61, 131)]

    # Non-tangency proxy: equal weights
    w_proxy = [Fraction(1, 3), Fraction(1, 3), Fraction(1, 3)]

    # Helper to evaluate SML for any chosen index/proxy portfolio w
    def evaluate_sml(w: list[Fraction], name: str) -> tuple[list[Fraction], list[Fraction], Fraction]:
        mu_p = sum((w[i] * mu[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
        cov_i_p = [sum((cov[i][j] * w[j] for j in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1)) for i in range(3)]
        var_p = sum((w[i] * cov_i_p[i] for i in range(3)), Fraction(0, 1))
        betas = [cov_i_p[i] / var_p for i in range(3)]
        erp_p = mu_p - r_f
        alphas = [(mu[i] - r_f) - betas[i] * erp_p for i in range(3)]

        print(f"--- Proxy: {name} ---")
        print(f"Weights:               [{w[0]}, {w[1]}, {w[2]}]")
        print(f"Expected return:       {mu_p}")
        print(f"Variance:              {var_p}")
        print(f"Betas:                 [{betas[0]}, {betas[1]}, {betas[2]}]")
        print(f"Pricing errors (alpha):[{alphas[0]}, {alphas[1]}, {alphas[2]}]")
        return betas, alphas, erp_p

    _, alphas_t, _ = evaluate_sml(w_t, "Tangency Portfolio (Efficient)")
    print(f"SML holds exactly on Tangency: {all(a == Fraction(0, 1) for a in alphas_t)}")

    _, alphas_proxy, _ = evaluate_sml(w_proxy, "Equal-Weighted Proxy (Inefficient)")
    print(f"SML holds on Inefficient Proxy: {all(a == Fraction(0, 1) for a in alphas_proxy)}")
    eff_ok = all(a == Fraction(0, 1) for a in alphas_t)
    ineff_fail = not all(a == Fraction(0, 1) for a in alphas_proxy)
    print(f"Roll conclusion: SML residuals are zero iff proxy is tangency: {eff_ok and ineff_fail}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The Roll critique reveals that conventional empirical methodologies cannot definitively validate or refute the CAPM:

  • Tautology versus Science: If a researcher searches over candidate indices until finding one that satisfies the SML, they have merely computed a sample efficient portfolio via quadratic programming, not tested an economic theory of pricing.
  • Human Capital Unobservability: According to human capital estimates (e.g., Mayers 1972, Campbell 1996), capitalized future labor income accounts for more than 70% of total aggregate wealth in modern economies. Because human capital is non-traded and cannot be marked to market, the true market portfolio will never be observed.
  • Cross-Sectional Rank Inversion: Roll and Ross (1994) showed that a proxy can be arbitrarily close to the efficient frontier in terms of Sharpe ratio (e.g., 99% of the maximum Sharpe ratio), yet the cross-sectional relationship between beta and expected return against that proxy can have an arbitrary slope—even zero or negative.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The Roll critique proved that the CAPM is wrong.”

    False. Roll did not prove that the CAPM is mathematically or economically invalid. Rather, he proved that the CAPM has never been tested and may be impossible to test, because tests using observable stock market proxies are joint tests of the model and the proxy's efficiency.

  2. “If a market proxy has a 95% correlation with the true market, it will yield approximately the correct SML.”

    False. Roll and Ross (1994) proved mathematically that a market proxy with a correlation exceeding 0.95 with the true market portfolio can produce an estimated SML slope that is zero, negative, or completely flat. Proximity in return space does not preserve cross-sectional beta rankings.

  3. “The S&P 500 is the market portfolio.”

    False. The S&P 500 represents only large-cap US publicly traded equities. It completely omits US small-cap stocks, international equities, sovereign and corporate bonds, commercial real estate, private equity, venture capital, and human capital.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Pricing error on an inefficient proxy

In a 2-asset universe with \(r_f = 0.02\), the true tangency portfolio has \(\mu_T = 0.10, \sigma_T^2 = 0.04\). An inefficient proxy \(p\) has expected return \(\mu_p = 0.08\) and variance \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.05\). Asset 1 has \(\mu_1 = 0.06\) and covariance with the proxy \(\Cov(r_1, r_p) = 0.025\). Compute Asset 1's beta with respect to proxy \(p\), the proxy's expected risk premium, and the resulting pricing error (alpha).

Solution

Beta with respect to proxy \(p\) is \(\beta_{1,p} = \frac{\Cov(r_1, r_p)}{\sigma_p^2} = \frac{0.025}{0.05} = 0.50\). The proxy risk premium is \(\mu_p - r_f = 0.08 - 0.02 = 0.06\) (6.0%). The SML predicted return is \(r_f + \beta_{1,p}(\mu_p - r_f) = 0.02 + 0.50(0.06) = 0.050\) (5.0%). The actual excess return is \(\mu_1 - r_f = 0.06 - 0.02 = 0.040\) (4.0%). The pricing error is \(\alpha_1 = 0.06 - 0.05 = +0.010\) (+1.0% or 100 bps). Because proxy \(p\) is inefficient, the SML fails and exhibits non-zero alpha.

M2 · Required covariance for zero alpha

Using the parameters from M1 (\(r_f = 0.02, \mu_p = 0.08, \sigma_p^2 = 0.05, \mu_1 = 0.06\)), what numerical value of covariance \(\Cov(r_1, r_p)\) would be required for Asset 1 to exhibit zero pricing error (\(\alpha_1 = 0\)) with respect to proxy \(p\)?

Solution

For zero pricing error, we require \(\mu_1 - r_f = \beta_{1,p}(\mu_p - r_f)\). Substituting known values: \(0.06 - 0.02 = \beta_{1,p}(0.08 - 0.02) \implies 0.04 = 0.06 \beta_{1,p} \implies \beta_{1,p} = \frac{0.04}{0.06} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.6667\). Since \(\beta_{1,p} = \frac{\Cov(r_1, r_p)}{\sigma_p^2}\), we solve for covariance: \(\Cov(r_1, r_p) = \beta_{1,p} \sigma_p^2 = \frac{2}{3} \times 0.05 = \frac{0.10}{3} \approx 0.033333\).

M3 · Roll beta sensitivity between two indices

Asset 1 has covariance 0.04 with Stock Index A (\(\sigma_A^2 = 0.04\)) and covariance 0.03 with Broad Wealth Index B (\(\sigma_B^2 = 0.025\)). Compute the asset's beta with respect to Index A and Index B.

Solution

Beta with respect to Index A: \(\beta_{1,A} = \frac{\Cov(r_1, r_A)}{\sigma_A^2} = \frac{0.04}{0.04} = 1.00\). Beta with respect to Index B: \(\beta_{1,B} = \frac{\Cov(r_1, r_B)}{\sigma_B^2} = \frac{0.03}{0.025} = 1.20\). The asset's systematic risk profile increases by 20% merely by switching the proxy from Index A to Index B, illustrating proxy sensitivity.

Proof

P1 · Zero-cost portfolio orthogonality

Prove that if an exact SML holds with respect to portfolio \(p\), every zero-cost portfolio with zero exposure to \(p\) has zero expected return.

Solution

Let \(w \in \R^n\) represent a zero-cost portfolio, so \(\iota' w = 0\). The portfolio return is \(r_w = \sum w_i r_i\). Its beta with respect to \(p\) is \(\beta_{w,p} = \sum w_i \beta_{i,p} = 0\). By linearity of expectation, the expected return is \(E[r_w] = \sum w_i \mu_i\). Since \(\iota' w = 0\), we can express this using excess returns: \(E[r_w] = \sum w_i (\mu_i - r_f)\). Under the exact SML, \(\mu_i - r_f = \beta_{i,p}(\mu_p - r_f)\). Substituting into the sum: \(E[r_w] = \sum w_i \beta_{i,p} (\mu_p - r_f) = (\mu_p - r_f) \sum w_i \beta_{i,p} = (\mu_p - r_f) \beta_{w,p} = (\mu_p - r_f) \times 0 = 0\). Thus, any zero-beta, zero-cost portfolio has exactly zero expected return.

P2 · Necessity of non-zero excess return in Roll's theorem

Show why Assumption A9 (\(\mu_p \ne r_f\)) is necessary for Roll's equivalence theorem.

Solution

Suppose \(\mu_p = r_f\). Then the SML relation \(\mu_i - r_f = \beta_{i,p} (\mu_p - r_f)\) degenerates to \(\mu_i - r_f = \beta_{i,p}(0) = 0\) for all \(i\), which would require all assets to have expected returns equal to the riskless rate (\(\mu = r_f \iota\)). This violates Assumption A2 (non-proportional means), which guarantees that risky assets have non-identical expected returns. If \(\mu_p = r_f\), the scalar \(\frac{\sigma_p^2}{\mu_p - r_f}\) diverges to infinity and the portfolio weights cannot be normalized. Therefore, \(\mu_p \ne r_f\) is strictly necessary for identification.

P3 · Proxy efficiency and the maximum Sharpe ratio

Prove that if an SML holds with respect to portfolio \(p\), the Sharpe ratio of \(p\) equals the maximum achievable Sharpe ratio in the economy.

Solution

By Theorem 1.1, the condition that an exact SML holds for all \(n\) assets implies that \(w_p = w_T\) identically. In Theorem 1.1 of Lesson 2, we proved that the tangency portfolio \(w_T\) uniquely maximizes the population Sharpe ratio \(\SR(w) = \frac{w'\mu - r_f}{\sqrt{w'\Sigma w}}\) over all feasible portfolios \(\iota' w = 1\). Therefore, \(\SR_p = \SR(w_T) = \max_w \SR(w) = \SR_T\). Any portfolio for which the SML holds must attain the global maximum Sharpe ratio.

Empirical

E1 · Stambaugh (1982) asset composition test

Summarize the empirical methodology and findings of Robert Stambaugh (1982) regarding the inclusion of bonds, real estate, and consumer durables in CAPM market proxies.

Solution

Stambaugh (1982) constructed expanded market proxies combining common stocks with US government and corporate bonds, residential real estate, and consumer durables. Using both time-series and cross-sectional tests, he found that: (1) The correlations between stock-only indices and broader wealth indices were extremely high (exceeding 0.98 in monthly returns). (2) Inferences regarding whether the CAPM held or failed were virtually identical across proxies. Because equity volatility (\(\approx 16\text{--}20\%\)) vastly exceeds the volatility of cash and bonds, stock market fluctuations dominate broader portfolio returns, suggesting that proxy omission of fixed income does not drive empirical CAPM rejections.

E2 · Inefficiency bounds and Shanken's (1987) proxy correlation

Jay Shanken (1987) derived a bound linking the correlation of a proxy with the true market \(\rho = \Corr(r_p, r_m)\) to the power of CAPM rejection tests. Summarize the economic intuition of Shanken's bound.

Solution

Shanken (1987) showed that if the proxy's correlation with the true market exceeds \(\rho_{\text{min}}\), the distance between the proxy's Sharpe ratio and the true maximum Sharpe ratio is tightly bounded. Specifically, \(\SR_p \ge \rho \SR_m - \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \sqrt{\SR_m^2 - \SR_p^2}\). If an equity proxy has \(\rho \ge 0.90\), rejecting the proxy's efficiency implies that the true market portfolio can be efficient only if the true market Sharpe ratio is implausibly large (e.g., exceeding 1.5). Thus, testing a well-chosen proxy can provide informative upper bounds on true market efficiency.

E3 · Roll and Ross (1994) flat SML demonstration

Explain the geometric finding of Roll and Ross (1994) showing how a market proxy located just inside the efficient frontier can produce an estimated cross-sectional SML slope of zero.

Solution

Roll and Ross (1994) analyzed the geometry of the mean-variance frontier and showed that the set of portfolios that generate an exact zero cross-sectional slope with expected returns is an entire hyperbolic boundary situated arbitrarily close to the true efficient frontier. By perturbing the tangency portfolio weights by an infinitesimal amount that reduces the Sharpe ratio by less than 1%, one can construct a proxy whose cross-sectional regression of asset returns on proxy betas yields a slope of exactly zero or even negative, demonstrating that beta rankings are hypersensitive to benchmark selection.

Research

R1 · Gibbons-Ross-Shanken test as a direct efficiency test

How does the Gibbons, Ross, and Shanken (GRS, 1989) \(F\)-test operationalize Roll's insight by evaluating whether a market proxy lies on the ex-post mean-variance frontier?

Solution

The GRS (1989) test evaluates the null hypothesis that all \(N\) time-series alphas are zero against a given benchmark \(p\). Roll proved that \(\alpha_i = 0\) for all \(i\) if and only if \(p\) is mean-variance efficient. Geometrically, the GRS statistic measures the difference between the squared Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio formed from all \(N+1\) assets (\(\SR_{N+1}^2\)) and the squared Sharpe ratio of the benchmark proxy alone (\(\SR_p^2\)): \(\hat{\alpha}' \hat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha} = \SR_{N+1}^2 - \SR_p^2\). If this gap is statistically significant, the proxy is definitively not mean-variance efficient, validating Roll's thesis.

R2 · Jagannathan and Wang (1996) conditional CAPM with human capital

Summarize the model of Jagannathan and Wang (1996) and explain how incorporating human capital and time-varying conditioning variables rescues the empirical performance of the CAPM.

Solution

Jagannathan and Wang (1996) addressed the Roll critique by constructing a conditional CAPM where: (1) Total wealth includes human capital, proxied by the growth rate of per capita labor income \(R_{\text{labor}}\). (2) Expected returns and betas are allowed to vary over time with macroeconomic conditions, proxied by the default yield spread. They showed that when beta varies over the business cycle and labor income is included in aggregate wealth, the model explains the cross-section of 25 Fama-French size and book-to-market portfolios as successfully as multi-factor models, providing empirical support for Roll's critique of equity-only static proxies.

R3 · Unobservable market portfolio and Hansen-Jagannathan bounds

How do Hansen-Jagannathan (1991) volatility bounds provide a test of asset pricing models that avoids specifying the exact weights of the market portfolio?

Solution

Hansen and Jagannathan (1991) derived non-parametric bounds on the stochastic discount factor (SDF) \(m\) using asset payoff and price data alone: \(\frac{\sigma(m)}{E[m]} \ge \max_p \SR_p\). Any valid asset pricing model must imply an SDF whose volatility satisfies this lower bound. In the CAPM, the SDF is affine in the market return: \(m = a - b r_m\), which requires \(\frac{\sigma(r_m)}{E[r_m] - r_f} = \frac{1}{\SR_m}\). The HJ bound establishes that if any traded portfolio achieves a Sharpe ratio higher than the market proxy, the proxy cannot be the true market portfolio, bypassing the need to observe individual unobservable assets.
