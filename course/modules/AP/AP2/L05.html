The Security Market Line translates the equilibrium CAPM into an operational cross-sectional pricing benchmark. Whereas the Capital Market Line evaluates only efficient portfolios against total risk, the Security Market Line prices every security—efficient or inefficient—against its systematic beta, defining mispricing as alpha deviations from the line.

1Definition and Geometry of the Security Market Line

In Lesson 4, we derived the equilibrium capital asset pricing model: under competitive market clearing, every asset \(i\) satisfies \(E[r_i] - r_f = \beta_i (E[r_m] - r_f)\). The geometric representation of this pricing relation across all assets defines the central benchmark of modern asset pricing.

Definition 1.1 (Security Market Line)

The security market line is the affine locus in \((\beta, E[r])\) space representing expected return as a linear function of systematic risk beta:

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r] = r_f + \beta (E[r_m] - r_f) \end{aligned} \]

The vertical intercept at \(\beta = 0\) is the per-period riskless rate \(r_f\), and the constant slope is the expected equity risk premium \(E[r_m] - r_f\). Every correctly priced asset and portfolio lies exactly on the line.

Definition 1.2 (Mispricing and Alpha)

For any individual asset or portfolio \(i\), its expected mispricing or alpha relative to the Security Market Line is defined as

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_i = E[r_i] - \left[r_f + \beta_i (E[r_m] - r_f)\right] = (E[r_i] - r_f) - \beta_i (E[r_m] - r_f) \end{aligned} \]

Under the null hypothesis of the CAPM in competitive equilibrium, \(\alpha_i = 0\) identically for every asset \(i\in \{1, \dots, n\}\). A security with \(\alpha_i > 0\) lies strictly above the SML (underpriced relative to its systematic risk), while a security with \(\alpha_i < 0\) lies strictly below the SML (overpriced).

2Comparison: Capital Market Line versus Security Market Line

The distinction between the Capital Market Line (CML) and the Security Market Line (SML) is a foundational conceptual boundary in asset pricing theory:

Dimension Capital Market Line (CML) Security Market Line (SML)
Space \((\sigma, E[r])\) total volatility space \((\beta, E[r])\) systematic risk space
Risk Measure Total standard deviation \(\sigma_p\) Market beta \(\beta_i = \Cov(r_i, r_m)/\Var(r_m)\)
Slope Tangency Sharpe ratio \(\SR_T = \frac{E[r_m] - r_f}{\sigma_m}\) Equity risk premium \(E[r_m] - r_f\)
Applicability Efficient portfolios only (combinations of \(r_f\) and \(w_m\)) All individual assets, inefficient portfolios, and efficient portfolios
Individual Assets Lie strictly below the CML (\(\Corr(r_i, r_m) < 1\)) Lie directly on the SML in equilibrium (\(\alpha_i = 0\))
Theorem 2.1 (Consistency of CML and SML on Efficient Portfolios)

For any efficient portfolio \(c = (1 - a) r_f + a r_m\) on the Capital Market Line with \(a \ge 0\), its SML pricing coincides identically with its CML pricing.

Proof

For an efficient portfolio \(c\), its volatility is \(\sigma_c = a \sigma_m\) and its beta is \(\beta_c = a \beta_m = a(1) = a\). Along the CML, its expected return is

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r_c] = r_f + \sigma_c \SR_m = r_f + (a \sigma_m) \left(\frac{E[r_m] - r_f}{\sigma_m}\right) = r_f + a (E[r_m] - r_f) \end{aligned} \]

Along the SML, its expected return is

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r_c] = r_f + \beta_c (E[r_m] - r_f) = r_f + a (E[r_m] - r_f) \end{aligned} \]

Both expressions are identical. Thus, the SML and CML are perfectly consistent for all efficient portfolios.

3Cross-Sectional Regression Formulation

The empirical test of the Security Market Line across a cross-section of \(n\) assets expresses expected excess returns as a linear regression on market betas:

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r_i] - r_f = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \beta_i + \eta_i, \quad i = 1, \dots, n \end{aligned} \]

The CAPM imposes two testable parametric restrictions on this cross-sectional relationship:

  1. Zero Intercept: \(\gamma_0 = 0\). Assets with zero systematic risk (\(\beta = 0\)) earn exactly the riskless rate \(r_f\).
  2. Slope Equals Risk Premium: \(\gamma_1 = E[r_m] - r_f\). The compensation per unit of systematic risk equals the excess return of the market portfolio.

Lab 1 · The Security Market Line on exact and SIMULATED cross-sections

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    # 1. Exact SML check on L04 economy
    betas_exact = [Fraction(262, 543), Fraction(524, 543), Fraction(655, 543)]
    mu_excess_exact = [Fraction(4, 100), Fraction(8, 100), Fraction(10, 100)]
    erp_exact = Fraction(543, 6550)

    # Cross-sectional regression: mu_excess = gamma_0 + gamma_1 * beta
    n_exact = len(betas_exact)
    mean_b = sum(betas_exact, Fraction(0, 1)) / Fraction(n_exact, 1)
    mean_y = sum(mu_excess_exact, Fraction(0, 1)) / Fraction(n_exact, 1)
    s_bb = sum(((b - mean_b) ** 2 for b in betas_exact), Fraction(0, 1))
    s_by = sum(((betas_exact[i] - mean_b) * (mu_excess_exact[i] - mean_y) for i in range(n_exact)), Fraction(0, 1))
    gamma_1_exact = s_by / s_bb
    gamma_0_exact = mean_y - gamma_1_exact * mean_b

    print("--- Part 1: Exact Equilibrium Economy (L04) ---")
    print(f"SML Intercept gamma_0: {gamma_0_exact}")
    print(f"SML Slope gamma_1:     {gamma_1_exact}")
    print(f"Theoretical ERP:       {erp_exact}")
    print(f"Exact match with ERP:  {gamma_1_exact == erp_exact}")

    alphas_exact = [mu_excess_exact[i] - betas_exact[i] * erp_exact for i in range(n_exact)]
    print(f"Pricing errors (alphas): [{alphas_exact[0]}, {alphas_exact[1]}, {alphas_exact[2]}]")

    # 2. Seeded 10-asset SIMULATED cross-section
    print("\n--- Part 2: 10-Asset SIMULATED Cross-Section ---")
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n_sim = 10
    sim_betas: list[float] = []
    sim_excess: list[float] = []
    true_erp = 0.06  # 6% annual / periodic risk premium

    for i in range(n_sim):
        b = round(0.5 + 0.1 * i + rng.uniform(-0.05, 0.05), 4)
        alpha_err = round(rng.gauss(0.0, 0.005), 4)  # idiosyncratic deviation
        ret = round(b * true_erp + alpha_err, 4)
        sim_betas.append(b)
        sim_excess.append(ret)

    # OLS fit on simulated sample
    mean_sb = sum(sim_betas) / float(n_sim)
    mean_sy = sum(sim_excess) / float(n_sim)
    s_sbb = sum((b - mean_sb) ** 2 for b in sim_betas)
    s_sby = sum((sim_betas[i] - mean_sb) * (sim_excess[i] - mean_sy) for i in range(n_sim))
    gamma_1_sim = s_sby / s_sbb
    gamma_0_sim = mean_sy - gamma_1_sim * mean_sb

    print(f"SIMULATED SML Intercept gamma_0: {gamma_0_sim:.6f}")
    print(f"SIMULATED SML Slope gamma_1:     {gamma_1_sim:.6f}")
    print("Asset | Beta   | Mean Excess | SML Fitted | Mispricing (alpha)")
    print("---------------------------------------------------------------")
    for i in range(n_sim):
        fitted = gamma_0_sim + gamma_1_sim * sim_betas[i]
        alpha_i = sim_excess[i] - fitted
        print(f"{i + 1:5d} | {sim_betas[i]:6.4f} | {sim_excess[i]:11.4f} | {fitted:10.4f} | {alpha_i:10.4f}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

The empirical Security Market Line routinely breaks down when tested against historical equity cross-sections:

  • Empirically Flat Slope: As documented by Black, Jensen, and Scholes (1972) and Fama and French (1992), the estimated slope \(\hat{\gamma}_1\) is substantially flatter than the realized equity risk premium, while the intercept \(\hat{\gamma}_0\) is significantly positive. Low-beta assets systematically earn higher returns than the SML predicts, while high-beta assets earn lower returns.
  • Omitted Factor Dimensions: Cross-sectional stock returns vary systematically with firm characteristics such as market capitalization (size) and book-to-market equity (value) that are uncorrelated with market beta.
  • Errors-in-Variables Attenuation: True asset betas are unobservable and must be estimated from time series. Sampling error in \(\hat{\beta}_i\) biases the second-pass cross-sectional regression slope toward zero (attenuation bias), flattening the apparent empirical SML.

5Misconceptions

  1. “An asset lying above the Security Market Line is overpriced.”

    False. An asset lying above the SML has an expected return exceeding the equilibrium required return for its level of systematic risk (\(E[r_i] > r_f + \beta_i(E[r_m] - r_f)\)), meaning it has positive alpha (\(\alpha_i > 0\)). Because it offers higher expected return than warranted by its risk, its current price is depressed; it is underpriced (a buying opportunity). Conversely, an asset lying below the SML has negative alpha and is overpriced.

  2. “The CML and SML have the same slope.”

    False. The slope of the CML is the Sharpe ratio \(\SR_m = (E[r_m] - r_f)/\sigma_m\) (excess return per unit of total volatility). The slope of the SML is the equity risk premium \(E[r_m] - r_f\) (excess return per unit of dimensionless beta). These two quantities have different units and different numerical magnitudes.

  3. “A stock with a beta of 0.5 must have half the total volatility of the market.”

    False. Beta reflects covariance, not total volatility: \(\beta_i = \Corr(r_i, r_m) \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m}\). If correlation is low (e.g., \(\Corr = 0.25\)), a stock with \(\beta = 0.5\) will have \(\sigma_i = 0.5 \times \sigma_m / 0.25 = 2 \sigma_m\)—twice the volatility of the market, dominated by idiosyncratic variance.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · SML expected return and alpha evaluation

The riskless rate is \(r_f = 0.03\) and the market expected return is \(E[r_m] = 0.08\). A portfolio manager forecasts that Stock X (\(\beta_X = 1.25\)) will earn an expected return of \(E[r_X] = 0.105\). Calculate the required return on the SML and the stock's alpha. Is the stock underpriced or overpriced?

Solution

The market risk premium is \(E[r_m] - r_f = 0.08 - 0.03 = 0.05\) (5.0%). The SML required return is \(E[r_X]_{\text{SML}} = r_f + \beta_X(E[r_m] - r_f) = 0.03 + 1.25(0.05) = 0.03 + 0.0625 = 0.0925\) (9.25%). Stock X's alpha is \(\alpha_X = E[r_X] - E[r_X]_{\text{SML}} = 0.1050 - 0.0925 = +0.0125\) (+1.25% or +125 basis points). Because \(\alpha_X > 0\), the stock lies strictly above the SML and offers higher expected return than required for its systematic risk. Therefore, Stock X is underpriced.

M2 · SML equation parameter determination

In an economy with an exact SML, Asset 1 has \(\beta_1 = 0.60\) and \(E[r_1] = 0.062\), while Asset 2 has \(\beta_2 = 1.40\) and \(E[r_2] = 0.110\). Solve for the riskless rate \(r_f\) and the market expected return \(E[r_m]\).

Solution

Set up the system of two equations: (1) \(0.062 = r_f + 0.60(E[r_m] - r_f)\) and (2) \(0.110 = r_f + 1.40(E[r_m] - r_f)\). Subtracting equation (1) from (2) gives \(0.110 - 0.062 = (1.40 - 0.60)(E[r_m] - r_f) \implies 0.048 = 0.80(E[r_m] - r_f)\). Thus the equity risk premium is \(E[r_m] - r_f = \frac{0.048}{0.80} = 0.060\) (6.0%). Substituting back into (1): \(0.062 = r_f + 0.60(0.060) = r_f + 0.036 \implies r_f = 0.062 - 0.036 = 0.026\) (2.6%). Finally, \(E[r_m] = r_f + 0.060 = 0.026 + 0.060 = 0.086\) (8.6%).

M3 · SML graphical placement

Given \(r_f = 0.04\) and \(E[r_m] = 0.10\), evaluate the SML placement for three assets: Asset A (\(\beta = 0.5, E[r] = 0.07\)), Asset B (\(\beta = 1.0, E[r] = 0.10\)), and Asset C (\(\beta = 1.5, E[r] = 0.11\)). State whether each asset lies on, above, or below the SML.

Solution

The market risk premium is \(0.10 - 0.04 = 0.06\). For Asset A: \(E[r_A]_{\text{SML}} = 0.04 + 0.5(0.06) = 0.070\). Since \(E[r_A] = 0.070\), \(\alpha_A = 0.00\); Asset A lies directly on the SML. For Asset B: \(E[r_B]_{\text{SML}} = 0.04 + 1.0(0.06) = 0.100\). Since \(E[r_B] = 0.100\), \(\alpha_B = 0.00\); Asset B lies directly on the SML (it is the market portfolio). For Asset C: \(E[r_C]_{\text{SML}} = 0.04 + 1.5(0.06) = 0.130\). Since \(E[r_C] = 0.110 < 0.130\), \(\alpha_C = 0.110 - 0.130 = -0.020\) (-2.0%); Asset C lies strictly below the SML and is overpriced.

P1 · SML holds for any efficient reference portfolio

Prove that if a reference portfolio \(p\) is mean-variance efficient along the Capital Market Line, an exact SML holds with respect to \(p\) for every risky asset in the economy.

Solution

Let \(p\) be an efficient portfolio on the CML, so \(r_p = (1 - a) r_f + a r_m\) for some \(a > 0\). The covariance of any asset \(i\) with portfolio \(p\) is \(\Cov(r_i, r_p) = \Cov(r_i, r_f + a(r_m - r_f)) = a \Cov(r_i, r_m)\). The variance of portfolio \(p\) is \(\Var(r_p) = a^2 \Var(r_m)\). The beta of asset \(i\) with respect to portfolio \(p\) is \(\beta_{i,p} = \frac{\Cov(r_i, r_p)}{\Var(r_p)} = \frac{a \Cov(r_i, r_m)}{a^2 \Var(r_m)} = \frac{1}{a} \beta_i\). The expected excess return of portfolio \(p\) is \(E[r_p] - r_f = a(E[r_m] - r_f)\). Multiplying beta with the excess return of \(p\) gives \(\beta_{i,p} (E[r_p] - r_f) = \left(\frac{1}{a} \beta_i\right) [a (E[r_m] - r_f)] = \beta_i (E[r_m] - r_f) = E[r_i] - r_f\). Thus, an exact linear SML holds with respect to any efficient benchmark \(p\).

P2 · Zero-beta portfolio SML formulation

Prove that if an asset or portfolio \(z\) has \(\beta_z = 0\) with respect to the market, its expected return under the SML equals the riskless rate: \(E[r_z] = r_f\).

Solution

Under the definition of the SML (Definition 1.1), for any asset \(z\), \(E[r_z] = r_f + \beta_z (E[r_m] - r_f)\). If \(\beta_z = 0\), the systematic risk premium term vanishes identically: \(\beta_z (E[r_m] - r_f) = 0 \times (E[r_m] - r_f) = 0\). Therefore, \(E[r_z] = r_f + 0 = r_f\). In competitive equilibrium, any asset with zero systematic covariance with aggregate market wealth must offer an expected return exactly equal to the riskless borrowing and lending rate.

P3 · Aggregate alpha sums to zero

Prove that the value-weighted sum of alphas across all assets in the market economy is identically zero.

Solution

Let \(w_m \in \R^n\) be the value weights of the market portfolio, with \(\sum_{i=1}^n w_{m,i} = 1\). By Definition 1.2, \(\alpha_i = (E[r_i] - r_f) - \beta_i (E[r_m] - r_f)\). The value-weighted sum of alphas is \(\sum_{i=1}^n w_{m,i} \alpha_i = \sum_{i=1}^n w_{m,i} (E[r_i] - r_f) - (E[r_m] - r_f) \sum_{i=1}^n w_{m,i} \beta_i\). Since \(\sum_{i=1}^n w_{m,i} (E[r_i] - r_f) = E[r_m] - r_f\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^n w_{m,i} \beta_i = \beta_m = 1\) by Theorem 2.1, the expression simplifies to \((E[r_m] - r_f) - (E[r_m] - r_f)(1) = 0\). Hence, positive alphas must be exactly offset by negative alphas in market equilibrium.

Empirical

E1 · Fama-MacBeth two-pass regression design

Describe the two-pass regression methodology of Fama and MacBeth (1973) used to test whether the Security Market Line is empirically linear with a zero intercept.

Solution

The Fama-MacBeth (1973) procedure consists of: (1) First pass: For each test asset or portfolio \(i\), estimate beta \(\hat{\beta}_i\) using time-series regressions of excess returns on market excess returns over an estimation window. (2) Second pass: For each time period \(t\), run a cross-sectional OLS regression across assets of realized returns on estimated betas: \(R_{it} = \hat{\gamma}_{0t} + \hat{\gamma}_{1t} \hat{\beta}_i + e_{it}\). (3) Inference: Compute the time-series averages of \(\hat{\gamma}_{0t}\) and \(\hat{\gamma}_{1t}\) over \(T\) periods, and test \(H_0: \bar{\gamma}_0 = 0\) and \(H_0: \bar{\gamma}_1 = \bar{R}_m - R_f\) using standard errors derived from the time-series volatility of the monthly coefficient estimates. This accounts for cross-sectional residual correlation.

E2 · Errors-in-variables and portfolio grouping

Explain why early empirical researchers (Blunt, Jensen, Scholes 1972; Fama and MacBeth 1973) grouped individual stocks into portfolios based on historical beta before running cross-sectional SML regressions.

Solution

Individual stock return series contain substantial idiosyncratic volatility \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), causing individual beta estimates \(\hat{\beta}_i = \beta_i + u_i\) to suffer from large measurement errors \(u_i\). In a second-pass cross-sectional regression, classical errors-in-variables creates attenuation bias: the estimated slope is biased toward zero by a factor of \(\frac{\Var(\beta)}{\Var(\beta) + \Var(u)}\). By grouping stocks into diversified portfolios of 20 to 50 stocks sorted by ranked beta, portfolio idiosyncratic errors average to zero (\(\Var(u_p) \approx \frac{1}{N} \Var(u_i)\)), drastically reducing measurement error while preserving cross-sectional dispersion in beta.

E3 · Alpha decay and market efficiency

If an algorithmic hedge fund identifies a stock trading at \(\alpha = +0.03\) (300 bps annualized above the SML), explain the market mechanism that causes \(\alpha\) to decay toward zero over time.

Solution

When multiple quantitative hedge funds detect \(\alpha > 0\), they initiate buying orders to capture the risk-adjusted excess return. This collective demand increases the current equilibrium price \(P_0\) of the asset. Because the expected terminal cash flow \(\E[P_1 + D_1]\) is fixed by fundamentals, the higher base price reduces the expected forward return: \(E[r] = \frac{\E[P_1 + D_1] - P_0}{P_0}\). Capital continues to flow into the asset until the forward expected return drops to the SML required return \(r_f + \beta_i(E[r_m] - r_f)\), driving \(\alpha\) to zero in accordance with competitive market equilibrium.

Research

R1 · The low-volatility anomaly and SML failure

Summarize the empirical findings of Baker, Bradley, and Wurgler (2011) regarding the benchmark performance of low-beta portfolios relative to high-beta portfolios over long historical horizons.

Solution

Baker, Bradley, and Wurgler (2011) examined US equity returns from 1968 to 2008 and found that a dollar invested in the lowest-risk quintile of stocks accumulated substantial cumulative outperformance over a dollar invested in the highest-risk quintile, directly contradicting the SML. They attribute this low-volatility anomaly to institutional frictions: benchmark-constrained institutional managers seek to maximize alpha against an equity index, discouraging them from leveraging low-beta stocks, while retail investors exhibit lottery-like preferences for volatile high-beta stocks, leading to persistent high-beta overpricing.

R2 · Gibbons-Ross-Shanken (GRS) multivariate test

State the null hypothesis and intuition of the Gibbons, Ross, and Shanken (1989) test of the joint efficiency of the Security Market Line across \(N\) test assets.

Solution

The GRS (1989) statistic tests the joint null hypothesis that all \(N\) pricing errors are simultaneously zero: \(H_0: \alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \dots = \alpha_N = 0\). The test statistic is \(F = \frac{T - N - 1}{N} \frac{\hat{\alpha}' \hat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha}}{1 + (\hat{\mu}_m - r_f)^2 / \hat{\sigma}_m^2}\), which follows an exact central \(F_{N, T-N-1}\) distribution under normal homoskedastic errors. Geometrically, the quadratic form \(\hat{\alpha}' \hat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \hat{\alpha}\) measures the increase in the squared Sharpe ratio achievable by expanding the investment universe from the market proxy alone to the full set of \(N\) assets plus the market.

R3 · Size and value premia as SML violations

How did the empirical evidence in Fama and French (1992) refute the hypothesis that market beta suffices to describe the cross-section of average stock returns?

Solution

Fama and French (1992) evaluated NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ stocks over the period 1963–1990. When portfolios were formed on size and beta, they found that the relation between market beta and average return was virtually flat, even when beta was estimated without size contamination. Conversely, market capitalization (size) and book-to-market equity ratio captured strong cross-sectional return variations that market beta could not explain, demonstrating that the single-factor SML fails empirically as an operational model of required returns.

  1. What is the graphical representation of the CAPM in expected return versus systematic risk space called?

    Options

    The Security Market Line plots expected return against beta \(\beta\) for all individual assets and portfolios.

  2. What is the economic interpretation of an asset that plots strictly above the Security Market Line?

    Options

    An asset above the SML delivers more expected return than required for its systematic risk, indicating an underpriced security with positive alpha.

  3. If \(r_f = 0.03\), \(E[r_m] = 0.09\), and a fund with \(\beta = 1.20\) realizes an expected return of \(0.117\), what is the fund's alpha \(\alpha\)?

    \(E[r]_{\text{SML}} = 0.03 + 1.20(0.09 - 0.03) = 0.03 + 0.072 = 0.102\). \(\alpha = 0.117 - 0.102 = 0.015\) (1.5% or 150 bps).

  4. What is the key structural difference between the CML and the SML?

    Options

    The CML measures total risk \(\sigma\) and prices only efficient combinations of the riskless asset and market portfolio. The SML measures systematic risk \(\beta\) and prices all assets.

  5. If the SML has intercept \(r_f = 0.02\) and passes through the point \((\beta = 1.0, E[r] = 0.08)\), what is the slope of the SML?

    The slope of the SML is the equity risk premium: \(\text{Slope} = E[r_m] - r_f = 0.08 - 0.02 = 0.06\) (6.0%).

  6. What is the theoretical value of the aggregate value-weighted alpha across all securities in the market portfolio?

    Options

    Because the market portfolio has beta 1 and expected return \(E[r_m]\), the market alpha is \((E[r_m] - r_f) - 1(E[r_m] - r_f) = 0\). The value-weighted sum of individual alphas must sum to zero.

  7. Why do empirical tests of the SML often group individual stocks into portfolios?

    Options

    Portfolio grouping averages out individual estimation errors in \(\hat{\beta}_i\), mitigating the classical attenuation bias that flattens cross-sectional regressions.

  8. An asset has beta \(\beta = 0.0\). If \(r_f = 0.04\) and \(E[r_m] = 0.11\), what is its equilibrium expected return on the SML?

    \(E[r] = r_f + 0 \cdot (E[r_m] - r_f) = r_f = 0.04\) (4.0%).
