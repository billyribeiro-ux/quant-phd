Real-world investors do not live in a single-period vacuum; they face multi-period horizons where future investment opportunities shift unpredictably. Robert Merton's Intertemporal CAPM demonstrates that when risk-averse investors demand hedging portfolios against adverse macroeconomic state variable shifts, equilibrium expected returns reflect multiple risk premia beyond market beta.

1From Static Wealth to Dynamic Investment Opportunity Sets

The classical CAPM derived in Lesson 4 restricts attention to a single investment period where the riskless rate and return distribution are static. In dynamic financial markets, however, the investment opportunity set changes stochastically over time:

  • Real riskless interest rates fluctuate in response to central bank policy and macroeconomic growth.
  • Market volatility clusters and jumps across turbulent and tranquil regimes.
  • Expected future equity risk premia vary across the business cycle.

An investor planning lifetime consumption cares not only about terminal wealth today, but also about the purchasing power and reinvestment returns of that wealth tomorrow. An adverse shock to future investment opportunities (such as a drop in future interest rates or a collapse in Sharpe ratios) lowers future consumption even if current financial wealth remains unchanged. Consequently, risk-averse investors demand assets that hedge against unfavorable shifts in state variables.

2The ICAPM Pricing Equation

In his seminal paper, Merton (1973) formulated dynamic portfolio choice and general equilibrium in continuous time using stochastic calculus. The continuous-time derivation from first principles is omitted here; instead, we state the resulting discrete-factor equilibrium pricing relationship in an economy satisfying explicit structural assumptions:

  • Assumption A1–A3: Risky asset covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) is symmetric and positive definite, expected returns are non-proportional, and a per-period riskless rate \(r_f\) exists.
  • Assumption A7 (State-Variable Pricing): Multi-period investors maximize expected lifetime utility of consumption under competitive market clearing. Systematic risk is spanned by aggregate wealth (market factor \(m\)) and \(K\) state variables \(h_1, \dots, h_K\) that describe changes in the investment opportunity set.
Definition 2.1 (Intertemporal CAPM)

The intertemporal capm states that under Assumption A7, the expected excess return on any asset or portfolio \(i\) satisfies the multi-factor linear pricing equation

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r_i] - r_f = \beta_{i,m} \lambda_m + \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_{i,h_k} \lambda_{h_k} \end{aligned} \]

where:

  • \(\beta_{i,m}\) is the multivariate market beta measuring covariance with aggregate market wealth;
  • \(\lambda_m = E[r_m] - r_f\) is the market risk premium;
  • \(\beta_{i,h_k}\) is the factor exposure (beta) of asset \(i\) with respect to state variable \(h_k\);
  • \(\lambda_{h_k}\) is the equilibrium risk premium associated with state variable \(h_k\).
Theorem 2.1 (CAPM as a Special Case)

Under Assumption A7, if the investment opportunity set is constant over time (all state variable volatilities are zero) or if investors have logarithmic utility of wealth, all hedging premia vanish identically (\(\lambda_{h_k} = 0\) for all \(k\)), and the ICAPM collapses strictly to the single-factor CAPM:

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r_i] - r_f = \beta_{i,m} (E[r_m] - r_f) \end{aligned} \]
Proof

When the investment opportunity set is constant, state variables do not fluctuate: \(\Var(h_k) = 0\) for all \(k \in \{1, \dots, K\}\). Consequently, \(\beta_{i,h_k} = 0\) for all assets, and hedging demand is zero. Alternatively, for an investor with log utility \(u(W) = \ln W\), the Arrow-Pratt relative risk aversion is \(-W u''(W)/u'(W) = 1\). The indirect utility of wealth separates additively: \(J(W, S, t) = \frac{1}{\rho} \ln W + g(S, t)\), where \(S\) is the vector of state variables. The cross-derivative \(\frac{\partial^2 J}{\partial W \partial S_k} = 0\) vanishes identically. Because hedging demand is proportional to \(\frac{\partial^2 J}{\partial W \partial S_k}\), log-utility investors hold only the myopic tangency portfolio regardless of state-variable shocks. In either case, aggregate demand for hedging vanishes, yielding \(\lambda_{h_k} = 0\) and recovering the single-factor CAPM.

3Decomposition of CAPM Alpha into Hedging Premia

Consider an evaluator who erroneously tests the single-factor CAPM on an economy governed by a two-factor ICAPM (market factor \(m\) and one state variable hedging factor \(h\), orthogonal to the market with \(\Cov(m, h) = 0\)). The true return-generating model is

\[ \begin{aligned} E[r_i] - r_f = \beta_{i,m} \lambda_m + \beta_{i,h} \lambda_h \end{aligned} \]

If the evaluator estimates the single-factor CAPM expected excess return \(\beta_{i,m} \lambda_m\), the asset's apparent CAPM alpha is

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_i^{\text{CAPM}} = (E[r_i] - r_f) - \beta_{i,m} \lambda_m = \beta_{i,h} \lambda_h \end{aligned} \]

This reveals a profound economic insight: assets that appear to generate positive or negative "alpha" in a single-factor CAPM are not necessarily mispriced. Instead, their non-zero alphas represent equilibrium compensation for hedging intertemporal state-variable risk.

Lab 1 · The Intertemporal CAPM and state-variable hedging premia

Verifies in exact Fraction arithmetic that for three assets in a two-factor ICAPM economy with orthogonal market and state-variable factors, each asset's CAPM alpha equals its intertemporal hedging premium beta_{i,h} * lambda_h identically.

"""Lab AP2-L09-1: The Intertemporal CAPM (ICAPM) and state-variable hedging premia.

Finite-state economy in exact Fraction arithmetic.
Two factors: market factor m and state-variable hedging factor h (with Cov(m, h) = 0).
Demonstrates that each asset's CAPM alpha equals its intertemporal hedging premium:
alpha_i^CAPM = beta_{i,h} * lambda_h.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def main() -> None:
    # Factor risk premia
    lambda_m = Fraction(6, 100)  # 6% market risk premium
    lambda_h = Fraction(3, 100)  # 3% state-variable hedging premium
    r_f = Fraction(2, 100)  # 2% riskless rate

    # Three assets with different factor exposures (betas)
    # Asset 1: low market beta, positive hedging beta (hedges adverse shifts)
    # Asset 2: market-matching beta, zero hedging beta
    # Asset 3: high market beta, negative hedging beta
    betas_m = [Fraction(7, 10), Fraction(1, 1), Fraction(13, 10)]
    betas_h = [Fraction(5, 10), Fraction(0, 1), Fraction(-4, 10)]

    # Expected excess returns under the 2-factor ICAPM pricing equation:
    # E[r_i] - r_f = beta_{i,m} * lambda_m + beta_{i,h} * lambda_h
    excess_returns = [betas_m[i] * lambda_m + betas_h[i] * lambda_h for i in range(3)]
    expected_returns = [r_f + excess_returns[i] for i in range(3)]

    # CAPM single-factor benchmark:
    # Market excess return is lambda_m
    # CAPM predicted excess return = beta_{i,m} * lambda_m
    capm_excess = [betas_m[i] * lambda_m for i in range(3)]

    # CAPM pricing error (alpha)
    capm_alphas = [excess_returns[i] - capm_excess[i] for i in range(3)]

    # Theoretical hedging premia
    hedging_premia = [betas_h[i] * lambda_h for i in range(3)]

    print("--- ICAPM Two-Factor Economy (Exact Fraction) ---")
    print(f"Riskless rate r_f:           {r_f}")
    print(f"Market risk premium lambda_m:{lambda_m}")
    print(f"Hedging premium lambda_h:    {lambda_h}")

    for i in range(3):
        print(f"\nAsset {i + 1}:")
        print(f"  beta_m: {betas_m[i]}, beta_h: {betas_h[i]}")
        print(f"  Total expected return:      {expected_returns[i]}")
        print(f"  Total excess return:        {excess_returns[i]}")
        print(f"  CAPM predicted excess:      {capm_excess[i]}")
        print(f"  CAPM alpha:                 {capm_alphas[i]}")
        print(f"  Hedging premium (beta_h*lh):{hedging_premia[i]}")
        print(f"  Alpha equals hedging premium: {capm_alphas[i] == hedging_premia[i]}")

    all_match = all(capm_alphas[i] == hedging_premia[i] for i in range(3))
    print(f"\nExact identity holds for all assets: {all_match}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

While the ICAPM provides an elegant economic foundation for multi-factor pricing, its empirical execution faces severe theoretical limits:

  • Unspecified State Variables: Merton's theory dictates that state variables must describe changes in the investment opportunity set, but does not name which macroeconomic variables matter. Researchers have tested term structure slopes, credit spreads, inflation, and aggregate volatility, leading to factor identification debates.
  • Negative Hedging Premia: Assets that pay off when investment opportunities deteriorate (e.g., long-term Treasury bonds during flight-to-safety episodes) have positive hedging betas (\(\beta_{i,h} > 0\)). If investors are willing to accept lower expected returns for this insurance, the factor risk premium \(\lambda_h\) can be negative, confounding standard intuition.
  • Continuous-Time Frictions: The continuous-time derivation relies on continuous trading and Brownian motion diffusion processes without jumps. In actual markets, discrete jump risks, transaction costs, and liquidity illiquidity create portfolio rebalancing frictions that break continuous hedging.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The ICAPM is an empirical ad-hoc factor model like the Fama-French 3-factor model.”

    False. The ICAPM is a rigorous general equilibrium theory derived from utility maximization across intertemporal horizons. Unlike empirical factor models that add factors based on statistical anomaly screens, ICAPM factors must strictly represent state variables that predict changes in the investment opportunity set.

  2. “All risk premia in the ICAPM must be positive.”

    False. The market risk premium \(\lambda_m\) is positive because investors dislike wealth variance. However, a state variable risk premium \(\lambda_h\) can be negative if the state variable correlates with a deterioration in investment opportunities for which investors gladly pay an insurance premium.

  3. “A positive CAPM alpha implies abnormal excess returns above risk compensation.”

    False. If the economy is governed by the ICAPM, an asset with \(\alpha^{\text{CAPM}} > 0\) may simply possess negative covariance with an unfavorable state variable (providing negative hedging benefits), requiring a higher expected return to induce investors to hold it.

Mechanics

M1 · ICAPM expected return and hedging calculation

The riskless rate is \(r_f = 0.02\). The market factor risk premium is \(\lambda_m = 0.05\). The interest rate state-variable factor premium is \(\lambda_h = -0.015\). Asset A has \(\beta_{A,m} = 1.20\) and \(\beta_{A,h} = 0.80\). Compute Asset A's expected return and its single-factor CAPM alpha.

Solution

Under the two-factor ICAPM: \(E[r_A] = r_f + \beta_{A,m} \lambda_m + \beta_{A,h} \lambda_h = 0.02 + 1.20(0.05) + 0.80(-0.015) = 0.02 + 0.060 - 0.012 = 0.068\) (6.8%). Under the single-factor CAPM benchmark: \(E[r_A]_{\text{CAPM}} = r_f + \beta_{A,m} \lambda_m = 0.02 + 1.20(0.05) = 0.080\) (8.0%). The apparent CAPM alpha is \(\alpha_A^{\text{CAPM}} = E[r_A] - E[r_A]_{\text{CAPM}} = 0.068 - 0.080 = -0.012\) (-1.2% or -120 bps), which matches the hedging premium \(\beta_{A,h} \lambda_h = 0.80(-0.015) = -0.012\) identically.

M2 · Solving factor premia from basis assets

Two basis assets have risk-free rate \(r_f = 0.03\). Asset 1 has \(\beta_{1,m} = 1.0, \beta_{1,h} = 0.5\) and expected return \(E[r_1] = 0.08\). Asset 2 has \(\beta_{2,m} = 0.5, \beta_{2,h} = 1.0\) and expected return \(E[r_2] = 0.065\). Solve for the factor risk premia \(\lambda_m\) and \(\lambda_h\).

Solution

Set up the excess return equations: (1) \(1.0 \lambda_m + 0.5 \lambda_h = 0.08 - 0.03 = 0.05\); (2) \(0.5 \lambda_m + 1.0 \lambda_h = 0.065 - 0.03 = 0.035\). Multiply (2) by 2: \(1.0 \lambda_m + 2.0 \lambda_h = 0.070\). Subtract (1) from this: \(1.5 \lambda_h = 0.070 - 0.050 = 0.020 \implies \lambda_h = \frac{0.020}{1.5} = \frac{2}{150} \approx 0.013333\) (1.33%). Substitute into (1): \(1.0 \lambda_m + 0.5\left(\frac{2}{150}\right) = 0.05 \implies \lambda_m = 0.05 - \frac{1}{150} = \frac{7.5 - 1}{150} = \frac{6.5}{150} \approx 0.043333\) (4.33%).

M3 · Portfolio hedging beta aggregation

An investor holds 70% in Stock Fund S (\(\beta_{S,m} = 1.10, \beta_{S,h} = -0.40\)) and 30% in Treasury Bond Fund B (\(\beta_{B,m} = 0.10, \beta_{B,h} = 1.50\)). Compute the portfolio's market beta and state-variable hedging beta.

Solution

By linearity of beta: \(\beta_{p,m} = 0.70(1.10) + 0.30(0.10) = 0.77 + 0.03 = 0.80\). The portfolio's state-variable hedging beta is \(\beta_{p,h} = 0.70(-0.40) + 0.30(1.50) = -0.28 + 0.45 = +0.17\). The 30% allocation to Treasury bonds transforms the net hedging exposure of the portfolio from negative (-0.40) to positive (+0.17), providing net insurance against adverse state-variable shifts.

P1 · Logarithmic utility eliminates intertemporal hedging demands

Prove that an investor with logarithmic utility of terminal wealth \(u(W) = \ln W\) has zero hedging demand for state-variable shifts.

Solution

Let the indirect utility function (value function) of the dynamic program be \(J(W, S, t) = \max \E_t \int_t^T e^{-\rho (s-t)} \ln(C_s) ds\). Under logarithmic utility, the value function is strictly additively separable in wealth and state variables: \(J(W, S, t) = \frac{1}{\rho}(1 - e^{-\rho(T-t)}) \ln W + g(S, t)\). Merton's dynamic portfolio demand vector is given by \(w^* = -\frac{J_W}{W J_{WW}} \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota) - \sum_{k} \frac{J_{W S_k}}{W J_{WW}} \Sigma^{-1} \sigma_{S_k}\). Here \(J_W = \frac{A(t)}{W}\) and \(J_{WW} = -\frac{A(t)}{W^2}\), giving relative risk aversion \(-\frac{W J_{WW}}{J_W} = 1\). Crucially, the mixed partial derivative with respect to wealth and any state variable is \(J_{W S_k} = \frac{\partial}{\partial S_k}\left(\frac{A(t)}{W}\right) = 0\). Because \(J_{W S_k} = 0\), the hedging demand term vanishes identically for every state variable \(k\).

P2 · Three-fund separation theorem in the ICAPM

State and prove the three-fund separation theorem when the investment opportunity set is driven by a single state variable \(h\).

Solution

In an economy with one state variable \(h\), Merton's first-order condition shows that every investor \(k\)'s optimal asset demand vector satisfies \(w_k^* = \theta_{1,k} \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota) + \theta_{2,k} \Sigma^{-1} \sigma_h\), where \(\theta_{1,k} = -\frac{J_W^k}{W_k J_{WW}^k}\) is risk tolerance and \(\theta_{2,k} = -\frac{J_{Wh}^k}{W_k J_{WW}^k}\) is hedging propensity. The two risky vector directions \(\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f \iota)\) and \(\Sigma^{-1} \sigma_h\) can each be normalized to form mutual funds \(F_1\) (the tangency portfolio) and \(F_2\) (the state-variable hedging portfolio). Any investor's total wealth is allocated across: (1) the riskless asset, (2) the market/tangency fund \(F_1\), and (3) the hedge fund \(F_2\). Thus, three mutual funds suffice to span the optimal portfolio choice of all investors in the economy.

P3 · Orthogonalization of state variable factor

Prove that if an initial state variable factor \(f\) has non-zero covariance with the market return, defining the orthogonalized factor \(h = f - \beta_{f,m} r_m\) preserves the linear ICAPM pricing equation.

Solution

Let the initial pricing equation be \(E[r_i] - r_f = b_{i,m} \lambda_m^* + b_{i,f} \lambda_f\). Decompose factor \(f\) on the market: \(f = \alpha_f + \beta_{f,m} r_m + h\), where by construction \(\Cov(h, r_m) = 0\). The regression of asset return on the orthogonalized factors yields exposures \(\beta_{i,m} = b_{i,m} + b_{i,f} \beta_{f,m}\) and \(\beta_{i,h} = b_{i,f}\). Substituting these into the original equation: \(E[r_i] - r_f = (\beta_{i,m} - \beta_{i,h} \beta_{f,m}) \lambda_m^* + \beta_{i,h} \lambda_f = \beta_{i,m} \lambda_m^* + \beta_{i,h} (\lambda_f - \beta_{f,m} \lambda_m^*)\). Setting \(\lambda_m = \lambda_m^*\) and \(\lambda_h = \lambda_f - \beta_{f,m} \lambda_m^*\) yields \(E[r_i] - r_f = \beta_{i,m} \lambda_m + \beta_{i,h} \lambda_h\). Thus, orthogonalization preserves exact linear factor pricing.

Empirical

E1 · Campbell (1993) discrete-time ICAPM

Summarize how John Campbell (1993, 1996) implemented the ICAPM in discrete time using a vector autoregression (VAR) without observing aggregate consumption.

Solution

Campbell (1993, 1996) combined a log-linear approximation of the intertemporal budget constraint with Euler equations to substitute out consumption. In his model, innovations to wealth returns decompose into: (1) revisions in expectations of future dividend cash flows (cash-flow news), and (2) revisions in expectations of future discount rates (discount-rate news). Using a first-order VAR with state variables (dividend yield, Treasury yield spread, short rate), Campbell showed that risk premia reflect covariance with cash-flow news (with coefficient of relative risk aversion \(\gamma\)) and covariance with discount-rate news (with coefficient \(\gamma - 1\)), operationalizing the ICAPM empirically.

E2 · Value stocks as hedges against discount rate shocks

Explain the Campbell and Vuolteenaho (2004) "Bad Beta, Good Beta" ICAPM decomposition and state why value stocks command a higher risk premium than growth stocks.

Solution

Campbell and Vuolteenaho (2004) decomposed market beta into two components: cash-flow beta \(\beta_{\text{CF}}\) ("bad beta") and discount-rate beta \(\beta_{\text{DR}}\) ("good beta"). An investor dislikes cash-flow shocks because they permanently decrease wealth without improving future investment opportunities. In contrast, negative discount-rate shocks decrease current wealth but increase future expected returns, providing an offsetting reinvestment hedge. They showed empirically that value stocks have high bad cash-flow betas, while growth stocks have high good discount-rate betas. Consequently, value stocks earn higher average returns as compensation for bearing unhedged cash-flow risk.

E3 · Aggregate market volatility as an ICAPM state variable

Why does market volatility (VIX) qualify as an ICAPM state variable, and what sign is predicted for its factor risk premium \(\lambda_{\text{VIX}}\)?

Solution

An increase in aggregate market volatility deteriorates the investment opportunity set by reducing the market Sharpe ratio for a given level of risk. Risk-averse investors demand assets that yield positive returns when market volatility spikes (such as VIX call options or long put options). Because investors willingly accept negative expected returns on volatility hedges in exchange for crash protection, the equilibrium risk premium for bearing volatility exposure is strictly negative: \(\lambda_{\text{VIX}} < 0\). Assets with positive exposure to volatility shocks (\(\beta_{i,\text{VIX}} > 0\)) earn lower expected returns, while assets vulnerable to volatility spikes earn high expected returns.

Research

R1 · Fama-French factors as empirical proxies for ICAPM state variables

Summarize the debate regarding whether the Fama-French SMB and HML factors are empirical proxies for unobserved ICAPM state variables or behavioral mispricing artifacts.

Solution

Fama and French (1993, 1996) argued that their SMB (size) and HML (value) factors proxy for undiversifiable state-variable risks in the spirit of Merton's ICAPM, specifically distress risk and business cycle vulnerability. In contrast, Lakonishok, Shleifer, and Vishny (1994) argued that the value premium reflects cognitive biases (extrapolation of past growth) and agency frictions rather than macroeconomic hedging demands. While Campbell et al. (2004) provided support for the ICAPM cash-flow risk interpretation, proving that a statistical factor strictly satisfies ICAPM state-variable conditions remains an active challenge.

R2 · Macroeconomic uncertainty and the Petkova (2006) test

Describe the empirical methodology of Petkova (2006) testing whether the Fama-French factors lose explanatory power after controlling for direct macroeconomic ICAPM state variables.

Solution

Petkova (2006) estimated an empirical ICAPM where state variables included the default spread, term spread, short-term Treasury bill rate, and dividend yield. In cross-sectional regressions on 25 size and book-to-market portfolios, she found that: (1) Innovations in these four macroeconomic state variables explained the cross-section of returns with statistically significant risk premia. (2) When macroeconomic state variables were included alongside HML and SMB, the explanatory power of the Fama-French factors was substantially weakened, concluding that HML and SMB derive their empirical success from proxying for underlying ICAPM state variables.

R3 · Long-run risks and the Bansal-Yaron (2004) consumption model

How does the Bansal and Yaron (2004) Long-Run Risks model connect Merton's intertemporal hedging intuition with Epstein-Zin recursive preferences?

Solution

Bansal and Yaron (2004) modeled an economy where consumption growth contains a small, persistent predictable component (long-run growth risk) and consumption volatility fluctuates over time (economic uncertainty risk). By utilizing Epstein-Zin recursive utility, they decoupled relative risk aversion from the elasticity of intertemporal substitution (EIS). When investors prefer early resolution of uncertainty (\(\gamma > 1/\psi\)), they demand large risk premia for assets exposed to long-run growth shocks and economic uncertainty shocks, providing a structural general equilibrium microfoundation for Merton's intertemporal state-variable hedging premia.

8Retrieval quiz

  1. What economic mechanism distinguishes Merton's ICAPM from the static single-period CAPM?

    Options

    In the ICAPM, investors care about future reinvestment rates and consumption purchasing power, demanding hedging portfolios against state-variable shifts.

  2. Under what preference condition does an investor have zero intertemporal hedging demand even when investment opportunities fluctuate?

    Options

    For log-utility investors, the cross-derivative \(J_{WS} = 0\) vanishes, leading to purely myopic single-period portfolio demands.

  3. If \(r_f = 0.03\), \(\lambda_m = 0.06\), \(\lambda_h = 0.02\), and an asset has \(\beta_m = 0.70\) and \(\beta_h = 0.50\), what is its ICAPM equilibrium expected return?

    \(E[r] = 0.03 + 0.70(0.06) + 0.50(0.02) = 0.03 + 0.042 + 0.010 = 0.082\) (8.2%).

  4. If an economy satisfies a 2-factor ICAPM, how does a single-factor CAPM test misinterpret an asset with positive hedging exposure (\(\beta_h > 0, \lambda_h > 0\))?

    Options

    Omitting the state-variable hedging factor absorbs the unmodeled risk premium into the intercept, creating an apparent CAPM alpha.

  5. Can an ICAPM factor risk premium \(\lambda_h\) be negative?

    Options

    Assets providing insurance against unfavorable states are in high demand, pushing their prices up and expected returns down, resulting in negative factor premia.

  6. In an ICAPM economy with one state variable, how many distinct mutual funds are required to achieve full portfolio separation across all investors?

    Three funds: the riskless asset, the market (tangency) portfolio, and the state-variable hedging portfolio (three-fund separation).

  7. Which seminal paper introduced the continuous-time Intertemporal CAPM?

    Options

    Robert C. Merton formulated the continuous-time ICAPM in Econometrica (1973).

  8. In Campbell and Vuolteenaho (2004), why do value stocks command higher required returns than growth stocks?

    Options

    Campbell and Vuolteenaho showed that value stocks carry high cash-flow beta which cannot be hedged, commanding a large risk premium.
