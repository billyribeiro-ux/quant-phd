Insider trading and tipping

  • Uses: Securities Exchange Act of 1934
  • Introduces: insider-trading doctrine (classical theory); tipper–tippee liability; misappropriation theory
  • Ttheorem, proved on the page
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle source or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Trading on information that other market participants lack is not, under the two opinions read here, a violation in itself. Liability follows from a duty, and the three tests in this lesson locate the duty in one of three sources: a fiduciary relationship with the company's shareholders, a breach by the insider who gave the information, or a deception practised on the source of the information. This lesson states each test with its printed page, applies the three tests to eight authored patterns, and shows which element decides each pattern. The lab does not say how a court would rule.

This lesson is an educational reading of the cited opinions. It is not legal advice. It relies on two admitted opinions and on the statute text that one of them quotes. Each quoted passage, and each holding stated in a claim span, carries its registry key and the printed page of the opinion it comes from.

1The provision and the question it asks

Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 is the provision that the two opinions apply, together with Rule 10b–5, which this lesson uses by name and does not read. Dirks v. SEC quotes its operative text in a footnote:

“To use or employ, in connection with the purchase or sale of any security registered on a national securities exchange or any security not so registered, any manipulative or deceptive device or contrivance in contravention of such rules and regulations as the Commission may prescribe as necessary or appropriate in the public interest or for the protection of investors.”

The later opinion in United States v. O'Hagan states the limit that matters here: liability under Rule 10b–5 “does not extend beyond conduct encompassed by § 10(b)'s prohibition.” The question for each doctrine below is therefore whether a trade involves a deceptive device, and the answer depends on whose duty the trader breached.

The sources use the phrase material nonpublic information without defining “material” in the passages read for this lesson. This lesson does not define it. Every pattern in the lab states materiality as a fact.

2The classical theory and the insider

Definition 2.1 (insider-trading doctrine, classical theory)

The insider-trading doctrine in its classical form is the rule that a person with a fiduciary relationship to the corporation violates § 10(b) and Rule 10b–5 by trading in the corporation's securities on information that is material and nonpublic, without disclosing it first. O'Hagan states the classical theory as follows:

“Under the ‘traditional’ or ‘classical theory’ of insider trading liability, § 10(b) and Rule 10b–5 are violated when a corporate insider trades in the securities of his corporation on the basis of material, nonpublic information.”

“The classical theory applies not only to officers, directors, and other permanent insiders of a corporation, but also to attorneys, accountants, consultants, and others who temporarily become fiduciaries of a corporation.” The lab therefore reads the role issuer_insider to include a temporary fiduciary, and the fact that the person is one must be stated in the pattern.

“[A]n insider will be liable under Rule 10b-5 for inside trading only where he fails to disclose material nonpublic information before trading on it and thus makes ‘secret profits.’” The same page states the source of the duty. The opinion says that “a duty to disclose under § 10(b) does not arise from the mere possession of nonpublic market information,” and that “[s]uch a duty arises rather from the existence of a fiduciary relationship.” Possession of the information is therefore not the source of the duty; the fiduciary relationship is.

Example 2.2 (pattern P1, HYPOTHETICAL)

A director of the issuer buys shares after learning of an unannounced acquisition agreement. The pattern's flags state that the information is material and nonpublic, and that nothing was public before the trade. The pattern does not say that the shares are the issuer's, and the classifier does not test that element. Each of the four checks passes, so Lab 1 fires the classical branch on P1 (every element of the branch is stated), with the key gpo1997united at pp. 651-652.

3Tipping and the tippee's duty

A tippee is a person who receives material nonpublic information from an insider. Dirks states the difference between an insider and a tippee: “Unlike insiders who have independent fiduciary duties to both the corporation and its shareholders, the typical tippee has no such relationships.”

Definition 3.1 (tipper–tippee liability)

Tipper–tippee liability is the liability of a tippee for trading on material nonpublic information received from an insider. In Dirks it is derivative: the tippee's duty comes from the insider's breach, and it arises only when the breach is established. The opinion states the test in one sentence:

“Thus, a tippee assumes a fiduciary duty to the shareholders of a corporation not to trade on material nonpublic information only when the insider has breached his fiduciary duty to the shareholders by disclosing the information to the tippee and the tippee knows or should know that there has been a breach.”

“Thus, the tippee's duty to disclose or abstain is derivative from that of the insider's duty.” The opinion says that the SEC's theory of tippee liability “appears rooted in the idea that the antifraud provisions require equal information among all traders,” and that this idea conflicts with the principle that only some persons, under some circumstances, are barred from trading while in possession of material nonpublic information. “[T]he conclusion that recipients of inside information do not invariably acquire a duty to disclose or abstain does not mean that such tippees always are free to trade on the information.”

The breach itself turns on purpose. “Whether disclosure is a breach of duty therefore depends in large part on the purpose of the disclosure.” The test is stated next: “Thus, the test is whether the insider personally will benefit, directly or indirectly, from his disclosure. Absent some personal gain, there has been no breach of duty to stockholders. And absent a breach by the insider, there is no derivative breach.” The personal benefit that counts may be financial or not: “a direct or indirect personal benefit from the disclosure, such as a pecuniary gain or a reputational benefit that will translate into future earnings.”

“[T]here may be a relationship between the insider and the recipient that suggests a quid pro quo from the latter, or an intention to benefit the particular recipient. The elements of fiduciary duty and exploitation of nonpublic information also exist when an insider makes a gift of confidential information to a trading relative or friend.” The same page adds that the benefit question is hard to answer: “Determining whether an insider personally benefits from a particular disclosure, a question of fact, will not always be easy for courts.”

Example 3.2 (patterns P2 to P5, HYPOTHETICAL)

P2 is a tip to a friend with no payment and no gift. The tipper's benefit is none, so Lab 1 fails the benefit element at p. 662 and does not fire the tippee branch. P3 is a tip for a cash payment, and P4 is a gift to a brother-in-law who trades, with no payment. Both state a personal benefit, and Lab 1 fires the tippee branch on both, with the key gpo1983dirks at p. 660. P5 is a tip for a cash payment to a manager with no reason to know of the payment. The benefit element passes, and the knowledge element fails at p. 660, so the tippee branch does not fire.

Remark 3.3

The benefit test decides whether a tip is a breach. It does not decide whether the tippee knew or should have known of the breach, which is a separate element. A pattern can state a benefit and still fail on knowledge, as P5 does.

4The misappropriation theory

Definition 4.1 (misappropriation theory)

The misappropriation theory holds that a person commits fraud in connection with a securities transaction when the person misappropriates confidential information for trading and breaches a duty owed to the source of that information. O'Hagan states it as follows:

“The ‘misappropriation theory’ holds that a person commits fraud ‘in connection with’ a securities transaction, and thereby violates § 10(b) and Rule 10b–5, when he misappropriates confidential information for securities trading purposes, in breach of a duty owed to the source of the information.”

“In lieu of premising liability on a fiduciary relationship between company insider and purchaser or seller of the company's stock, the misappropriation theory premises liability on a fiduciary-turned-trader's deception of those who entrusted him with access to confidential information.” The trader need not be an insider of the company whose stock he trades. The opinion's footnote 5 says that the trader was not an insider of that company, and that “[u]nder the misappropriation theory, [his] securities trading does not escape Exchange Act sanction.”

The opinion locates the deception in nondisclosure. “Deception through nondisclosure is central to the theory of liability for which the Government seeks recognition.” The opinion then states the limit that the lab's last check uses: “if the fiduciary discloses to the source that he plans to trade on the nonpublic information, there is no ‘deceptive device’ and thus no § 10(b) violation—although the fiduciary-turned-trader may remain liable under state law for breach of a duty of loyalty.”

The fraud is consummated when, without disclosure to his principal, the fiduciary “uses the information to purchase or sell securities.” The opinion finds the “in connection with” element satisfied because “[t]he securities transaction and the breach of duty thus coincide.” The opinion also limits the theory: “The theory does not catch all conceivable forms of fraud involving confidential information; rather, it catches fraudulent means of capitalizing on such information through securities transactions.”

Example 4.2 (patterns P6 and P7, HYPOTHETICAL)

P6 is an associate at an accounting firm who reads a client's confidential file on a planned acquisition, buys shares of the target, and does not tell the client. Lab 1 fires the misappropriation branch on P6, with the key gpo1997united at p. 652. P7 is the same associate, who tells the client in writing that he plans to trade, and then trades. The disclosure element fails at p. 655, so the branch does not fire on P7.

Remark 4.3

The opinion's footnote 7 covers a trader who owes duties to two entities and discloses to only one: “the trader may still be liable under the misappropriation theory.” Lab 1 does not model that case. Its misappropriation branch has one source of duty.

Lab 1 · Insider-trading fact-pattern classifier (HYPOTHETICAL patterns)

Stated before running. Under the rules as coded, four of the eight HYPOTHETICAL patterns state every element of a branch: P1, P3, P4 and P6. def branch_checks(p: Pattern) -> tuple[str, tuple[Check, ...]]:
    """The branch a pattern is tested against, and its elements in the order the classifier checks them."""
    if p.role == "issuer_insider":
        return "classical", (
            Check("material information", p.material, KEY_O_HAGAN, "pp. 651-652"),
            Check("nonpublic information", p.nonpublic, KEY_O_HAGAN, "pp. 651-652"),
            Check("a trade", p.traded, KEY_O_HAGAN, "pp. 651-652"),
            Check("no public disclosure before the trade", not p.public_before_trade, KEY_DIRKS, "p. 654"),
        )
    if p.role == "tippee":
        benefit_ok = p.tipper_benefit in PERSONAL_BENEFITS
        benefit_locator = PERSONAL_BENEFITS[p.tipper_benefit] if benefit_ok else NO_BENEFIT_LOCATOR
        return "tippee", (
            Check("material nonpublic information from an insider", p.material and p.nonpublic, KEY_DIRKS, "p. 654"),
            Check(f"tipper's personal benefit ({p.tipper_benefit})", benefit_ok, KEY_DIRKS, benefit_locator),
            Check("tippee traded", p.traded, KEY_DIRKS, "p. 660"),
            Check("tippee knew or should have known of the breach", p.knew_or_should_have_known, KEY_DIRKS, "p. 660"),
        )
    if p.role == "outsider":
        return "misappropriation", (
            Check("confidential information", p.confidential, KEY_O_HAGAN, "p. 652"),
            Check("duty owed to the source of the information", p.duty_to_source, KEY_O_HAGAN, "p. 652"),
            Check("information used to trade", p.traded, KEY_O_HAGAN, "p. 656"),
            Check("source not told of the trade before it was made", not p.source_told_of_trade, KEY_O_HAGAN, "p. 655"),
        )
    raise ValueError(f"{p.label}: unknown role {p.role!r}")


def classify(p: Pattern) -> Decision:
    """The first element the pattern does not state decides the outcome; if none fails, the branch fires."""
    branch, checks = branch_checks(p)
    for check in checks:
        if not check.passed:
            return Decision(p.label, branch, False, f"fails: {check.element}", check.key, check.locator)
    key, locator = BRANCH_RULE[branch]
    return Decision(p.label, branch, True, "all elements stated", key, locator)


def main() -> None:
    """Print each pattern's facts, then the decision for each one, then the two lists of labels."""
    print("LX2 lab L02: insider-trading fact-pattern classifier. HYPOTHETICAL patterns; rules as coded in this lab.")
    print()
    for p in PATTERNS:
        print(f"{p.label}: {p.facts}")
    decisions = [classify(p) for p in PATTERNS]
    print()
    print(f"{'pattern':<9}{'branch':<19}{'result':<11}{'deciding element':<60}{'key':<15}locator")
    for d in decisions:
        result = "fired" if d.fired else "not fired"
        print(f"{d.label:<9}{d.branch:<19}{result:<11}{d.element:<60}{d.key:<15}{d.locator}")
    fired = [d.label for d in decisions if d.fired]
    not_fired = [d.label for d in decisions if not d.fired]
    print()
    print(f"fired: {', '.join(fired)} ({len(fired)} of {len(decisions)})")
    print(f"not fired: {', '.join(not_fired)} ({len(not_fired)} of {len(decisions)})")
    print("Read as stated, not assessed: materiality, confidentiality, benefit, knowledge and disclosure.")
    print("Scienter is not checked.")

5Where the canon breaks

The three tests are the lesson's reading of the two opinions, and the reading has five limits.

  • Scienter is outside the lab. Scienter, “a mental state embracing intent to deceive, manipulate, or defraud,” is “an independent element of a Rule 10b-5 violation.” Without the lab's check, a pattern that states every element of a branch can still fail on intent, so a fired branch is not a finding of liability.

  • Materiality is a stated fact. “[I]t may not be clear—either to the corporate insider or to the recipient analyst—whether the information will be viewed as material nonpublic information.” The lab accepts the pattern's statement. Each pattern's materiality flag is either true or false, so the lab cannot represent an uncertain materiality.

  • Benefit is one label. The lab records one value for the tipper's benefit. Dirks lists objective facts that support an inference of benefit, including a relationship suggesting a quid pro quo and an intention to benefit the recipient (p. 664). The lab has no field for either fact, so a pattern that differs from P3 only in the relationship or the intention would print the same row.

  • The misappropriation check reads one source of duty. Footnote 7 of O'Hagan (p. 655 n. 7) covers a trader with duties to two entities, which Lab 1 does not model. Remark 4.3 gives the limit.

  • One trade per pattern. Each pattern has one trade and one trader. The lab does not model a sequence of trades, a market, or price effects, so none of its results speak to those.

6Misconceptions

  1. “Holding material nonpublic information is itself the offence.”

    Under the test at p. 654, a duty to disclose or abstain does not arise from mere possession of nonpublic information. It arises from a fiduciary relationship. Pattern P8 in Lab 1 overhears confidential information, has no duty to its source, and is not fired.

  2. “A tippee always inherits the insider's duty.”

    The tippee assumes the duty only when the insider breached it and the tippee knows or should know of the breach (p. 660). Pattern P5 states a personal benefit and fails the knowledge element, so Lab 1 does not fire the tippee branch on it.

  3. “Any tip to a friend is a breach.”

    The test is personal benefit: “Absent some personal gain, there has been no breach of duty to stockholders” (p. 662). Pattern P2 is a tip to a friend with no benefit, and it is not fired. A gift of confidential information to a trading relative or friend is a different case. P4 is fired on it: the gift benefit element cites p. 664, and the decision row prints p. 660.

  4. “Misappropriation requires a duty to the issuer of the stock traded.”

    The misappropriation theory requires a duty owed to the source of the information (p. 652). O'Hagan was an outsider to the company whose stock he traded (p. 653 n. 5). Pattern P6 is an outsider case, and Lab 1 fires it.

Mechanics

M1 · Trace P1 through the classical branch

List the four checks the classical branch runs on pattern P1, in order, with the result and the locator of each.

Solution

The checks are material information (passes; gpo1997united, pp. 651-652), nonpublic information (passes; gpo1997united, pp. 651-652), a trade (passes; gpo1997united, pp. 651-652; the classifier does not test that the security is the issuer's), and no public disclosure before the trade (passes; gpo1983dirks, p. 654). The last check tests the negation of the pattern's public-before-trade flag, which is false for P1, so it passes. All four pass, so the branch fires. The printed row reads fired and “all elements stated,” with the branch's locator pp. 651-652.

M2 · Find the one fact that separates P6 from P7

Patterns P6 and P7 differ in one stated fact. Name the fact, the check that decides P7, and its locator.

Solution

The differing fact is whether the associate told the client of the trade: P7 states that he did, and P6 does not. The lab records this fact as the source-told flag. The check “source not told of the trade before it was made” therefore fails on P7. The decision is not fired, the key is gpo1997united, and the locator is p. 655, the passage on disclosure to the source.

M3 · Change P3's benefit to none

Pattern P3 has a pecuniary benefit and fires the tippee branch. Suppose its benefit is set to none and nothing else changes. Which check decides the outcome, and at what locator?

Solution

The first tippee check, material nonpublic information from an insider, still passes. The second check, the tipper's personal benefit, fails, because none is not one of the personal benefits the classifier accepts. The decision is not fired, with the element “fails: tipper's personal benefit (none)”, the key gpo1983dirks, and the locator p. 662, which is the passage that states there is no breach without personal gain. The tippee's own knowledge and trading are not reached.

Proof

Pr1 · A tippee whose tipper received no benefit has no tippee duty

Assumption A1 of this lesson is a reading, not a sentence of the opinion: the classifier's value none for the tipper's benefit means that the insider received no direct or indirect personal benefit. Assuming A1, and using only the quoted text of Dirks, prove that a tippee whose tipper received no personal benefit has no duty to disclose or abstain under the tippee test of Definition 3.1. State every hypothesis you use.

Solution

Hypothesis H1 (p. 660): a tippee assumes the duty only when the insider has breached the duty by disclosing the information, and the tippee knows or should know of the breach. Hypothesis H2 (p. 662): absent some personal gain there has been no breach of duty to stockholders. Hypothesis H3 (p. 662): absent a breach by the insider there is no derivative breach. Assumption A1 of this lesson, as stated in the problem, applies. Proof: suppose the tipper received no personal benefit. By A1 and H2, the tipper did not breach the duty to stockholders by disclosing. By H3, there is no derivative breach. H1 says the tippee assumes the duty only when the insider has breached, and that condition fails, so the tippee does not assume the duty. Only the necessity direction of H1 is used. The converse, that a benefit creates the duty, is not claimed. Grade C, with the keys and pages above.

Pr2 · The classifier fires a branch if and only if every check passes

Prove that, in the code of Lab 1, a branch fires for a pattern if and only if every check of that branch passes for it. Say which lines of the function classify the proof uses.

Solution

The loop in classify returns a not-fired decision at the first check that does not pass. If the loop finishes without returning, every check has passed, and the function returns the fired decision. So a not-fired result implies some check failed, and a fired result implies every check passed. Conversely, if every check passes, the loop never returns early, and the fired decision is returned. The proof uses the loop's early return and the final return after the loop. It concerns the code only. It shows nothing about whether a court applies the same test. Grade C, with the basis named as the classify function of the lab twin.

Pr3 · No trade, no tippee branch

Show that the tippee branch cannot fire for a pattern in which the tippee did not trade, whatever the other facts. Name the check that does the work and its locator.

Solution

By problem Pr2, the tippee branch fires only if its check “tippee traded” passes. If the pattern states that the tippee did not trade, that check fails, whatever the other checks show. The check cites gpo1983dirks at p. 660, the passage in which the tippee's duty is a duty not to trade on material nonpublic information. The tippee branch therefore cannot fire for a non-trading tippee.

Research

R1 · Facts behind the benefit label

The lab records one label for the tipper's benefit. List the objective facts that Dirks gives for inferring a personal benefit, with a page for each, and say which of them the lab cannot record.

Solution

At p. 663, the opinion gives a direct or indirect personal benefit, such as a pecuniary gain or a reputational benefit that will translate into future earnings. At p. 664, it gives a relationship between the insider and the recipient that suggests a quid pro quo, an intention to benefit the particular recipient, and a gift of confidential information to a trading relative or friend. The lab records the benefit as one label. It records a gift as a value of the label, but it has no field for the relationship or for the intention, so it cannot test either fact.

R2 · Elements a court must decide

The lab reads materiality, benefit and knowledge as stated, and it does not check scienter. For materiality, benefit and scienter, name the page on which Dirks addresses the question, and quote the words that do so.

Solution

Materiality: at p. 662 the opinion says it “may not be clear” whether information “will be viewed as material nonpublic information.” Benefit: at p. 664 it calls the question “a question of fact.” Scienter: at p. 663 n. 23 it calls scienter “an independent element of a Rule 10b-5 violation.” The lab records no evidence on any of the three, so it cannot decide them.

R3 · Confidential information used outside a trade

The pattern states confidential information, a duty to the client, and no securities trade. The “information used to trade” check fails at p. 656. 9Retrieval quiz

  1. Under the classical theory as O'Hagan states it, which trader violates § 10(b) and Rule 10b–5 by trading on material nonpublic information?

    Options

    O'Hagan, pp. 651-652, states the classical theory for a corporate insider, and p. 652 extends it to temporary fiduciaries. Dirks, p. 654, says that mere possession does not create the duty.

  2. Under Dirks at p. 660, when does a tippee assume the insider's duty?

    Options

    The sentence at p. 660 uses “only when” and states both conditions. Payment is one form of personal benefit, and a reputational benefit also counts (p. 663), so payment is not a condition of the tippee's duty.

  3. A tipper gives a friend a tip and receives nothing. What does Dirks at p. 662 say about the tippee's duty?

    Options

    Dirks, p. 662: “Absent some personal gain, there has been no breach of duty to stockholders. And absent a breach by the insider, there is no derivative breach.” The friendship is not the test. The benefit is.

  4. Under O'Hagan at p. 655, what follows if the fiduciary discloses to the source that he plans to trade?

    Options

    O'Hagan, p. 655: “if the fiduciary discloses to the source that he plans to trade on the nonpublic information, there is no ‘deceptive device’ and thus no § 10(b) violation—although the fiduciary-turned-trader may remain liable under state law for breach of a duty of loyalty.”

  5. What does Dirks, p. 663 n. 23, say about scienter in a Rule 10b-5 case?

    Options

    Footnote 23 calls scienter “an independent element of a Rule 10b-5 violation.” The text at p. 663, to which footnote 23 is attached, says scienter is relevant in some cases to whether the tipper has violated his duty, so the third option is wrong.

  6. Which pattern in Lab 1 has a gift to a trading relative as the tipper's benefit, and fires the tippee branch?

    Options

    P4 is the gift to a brother-in-law, and Lab 1 fires it. P2 has no benefit and fails at p. 662. P7 is a misappropriation pattern that fails the disclosure check at p. 655.

  7. How many of the eight patterns in Lab 1 fire a branch?

    The summary line of the printed output reads “fired: P1, P3, P4, P6 (4 of 8).”

    Only P5 fails the knowledge element, at p. 660. P2 fails at the benefit element first, so its knowledge is not reached.
