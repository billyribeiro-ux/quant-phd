Breakeven inflation is the central gauge used by central banks, asset allocators, and rates trading desks to extract real-time inflation expectations and evaluate policy credibility. By observing the yield spread between nominal Treasuries and TIPS, market participants measure the rate of inflation at which both securities deliver identical holding-period returns. Understanding exact Fisher compounding, the cross-product wedge, zero-coupon inflation swap pricing, and horizon carry attribution under CPI seasonality is vital for managing duration, trading inflation swaps, and designing systematic breakeven strategies.

1The Fisher Relation and Breakeven Inflation Term Structures

The classical theory of interest rates developed by Irving Fisher establishes that nominal interest rates reflect the combination of a required real return on capital and compensation for expected currency debasement. In modern markets with active trading in both conventional nominal bonds and inflation-indexed bonds, this relationship is formalized through the concept of breakeven inflation.

Definition 2.1 (Breakeven Inflation Rate)

The breakeven inflation (BEI) rate for maturity \(n\), denoted \(\text{BEI}_t^{(n)}\), is the annualized yield spread between an \(n\)-year nominal Treasury bond yield \(y_{N,t}^{(n)}\) and an \(n\)-year real inflation-indexed bond (TIPS) yield \(y_{R,t}^{(n)}\):

\[ \text{BEI}_t^{(n)} = y_{N,t}^{(n)} - y_{R,t}^{(n)} \]

Equivalently, \(\text{BEI}_t^{(n)}\) is the hypothetical constant annual inflation rate that equates the holding-period return of the nominal bond and the inflation-indexed bond over the \(n\)-year horizon.

Theorem 2.1 (Geometric Fisher Compounding Wedge)

Let nominal and real zero-coupon yields for maturity \(n\) be denoted \(y_{N}^{(n)}\) and \(y_{R}^{(n)}\) under annual discrete compounding. The exact geometric breakeven inflation rate \(\pi_{\text{geom}}^{(n)}\) satisfies:

\[ \left(1 + y_{N}^{(n)}\right)^n = \left(1 + y_{R}^{(n)}\right)^n \left(1 + \pi_{\text{geom}}^{(n)}\right)^n \]

The linear breakeven yield spread \(\text{BEI}_{\text{lin}} = y_{N}^{(n)} - y_{R}^{(n)}\) strictly exceeds the geometric breakeven rate by the compounding cross-product wedge:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{BEI}_{\text{lin}} - \pi_{\text{geom}}^{(n)} &= y_{R}^{(n)} \cdot \pi_{\text{geom}}^{(n)} \\ &= y_{R}^{(n)} \left(\frac{1 + y_{N}^{(n)}}{1 + y_{R}^{(n)}} - 1\right) \end{aligned} \]
Proof

Taking \(n\)-th roots gives \(1 + y_N = (1 + y_R)(1 + \pi_{\text{geom}}) = 1 + y_R + \pi_{\text{geom}} + y_R \pi_{\text{geom}}\). Subtracting \(1 + y_R\) yields \(y_N - y_R = \pi_{\text{geom}} + y_R \pi_{\text{geom}}\). Rearranging gives \(\text{BEI}_{\text{lin}} - \pi_{\text{geom}} = y_R \pi_{\text{geom}}\). For positive real yields and inflation, this cross-product term is strictly positive.

Example 2.1 (Quantifying the Compounding Wedge)

Suppose 10-year nominal yield \(y_N = 4.50\%\) and 10-year TIPS yield \(y_R = 2.00\%\). The linear breakeven is \(y_N - y_R = 2.50\%\) (250 bps). The exact geometric breakeven is \((1.0450 / 1.0200) - 1 = 2.4510\%\) (245.1 bps). The compounding cross-product wedge is \(2.00\% \times 2.4510\% = 0.0490\%\) (4.9 bps).

2Zero-Coupon Inflation Swaps and the Swap Curve

While cash breakevens are extracted from Treasury bonds, derivative market participants trade inflation directly through Zero-Coupon Inflation Swaps (ZCIS). In a ZCIS of maturity \(T\), no cash flows occur until terminal settlement.

Definition 2.2 (Zero-Coupon Inflation Swap)

In a \(T\)-year Zero-Coupon Inflation Swap (ZCIS) on notional \(N\), the inflation receiver receives floating inflation accrual and pays a fixed compounded swap rate \(S_T\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Floating Leg Payoff}_T &= N \cdot \left(\frac{\text{CPI}_T}{\text{CPI}_0} - 1\right) \\ \text{Fixed Leg Payoff}_T &= N \cdot \left((1 + S_T)^T - 1\right) \end{aligned} \]

The par inflation swap rate \(S_T\) is the unique fixed rate that equates the present values of both legs to zero at inception.

Theorem 2.2 (ZCIS Par Pricing under No-Arbitrage)

Let \(P_N(0, T)\) denote the price of a nominal zero-coupon bond and \(\mathbb{Q}\) the nominal risk-neutral pricing measure. The par inflation swap rate satisfies:

\[ (1 + S_T)^T = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\frac{\text{CPI}_T}{\text{CPI}_0}\right] \]

Under frictionless markets with zero liquidity premia and zero counterparty risk, the par inflation swap rate equals the geometric breakeven rate: \(S_T = \pi_{\text{geom}}^{(T)}\).

Proof

Discounting terminal cash flows at the nominal discount factor \(P_N(0, T)\), the fair value condition requires \(P_N(0, T) \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(\text{CPI}_T / \text{CPI}_0) - 1] = P_N(0, T) [ (1 + S_T)^T - 1 ]\). Cancelling \(P_N(0, T)\) and subtracting 1 from both sides yields the result.

3Breakeven Carry, Roll-Down, and Seasonality

Investors trading breakeven inflation typically implement relative-value curve positions: long TIPS and short nominal Treasuries of matched duration. The expected return over a short holding period \(\Delta t\) decomposes into yield carry and inflation accrual.

Definition 2.3 (Breakeven Carry Decomposition)

For a duration-neutral breakeven position (long 1 unit of TIPS, short 1 unit of nominal Treasury), the annualized carry over horizon \(\Delta t\) is:

\[ \text{Carry}_{\text{BEI}} = \underbrace{(y_R - y_N)}_{\text{Yield Carry (negative)}} + \underbrace{\pi_{\text{CPI}}(\Delta t)}_{\text{Accrued Inflation (positive)}} \]

Because TIPS yields are lower than nominal yields (\(y_R < y_N\)), the position pays negative yield carry each day. It achieves positive net carry only if realized inflation accrual exceeds the breakeven rate.

Remark 2.1 (CPI Seasonality Distortion)

Non-seasonally adjusted headline CPI exhibits strong seasonal patterns: headline prices rise rapidly from February through May (gasoline and apparel price resets) and stagnate or decline from September through November. For a 3-month holding period, seasonal inflation adjustments can shift annualized carry by \(\pm 100\) to \(\pm 150\) basis points.

"""Lab FI5-L02-1: Breakeven inflation curve construction, Fisher relation, and carry attribution.

Standard library only. Computes nominal-real breakeven inflation rates across maturities,
quantifies the compounding cross-product wedge in the Fisher relation, prices zero-coupon
inflation swaps (ZCIS), and attributes 3-month horizon carry under seasonal CPI dynamics.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import Any


def compute_breakeven_curve(
    nominal_yields: dict[int, float],
    real_yields: dict[int, float],
) -> list[dict[str, Any]]:
    """Compute breakeven inflation rates and Fisher compounding wedges.

    Linear breakeven: BEI_lin = y_N - y_R
    Exact geometric breakeven: BEI_geom = (1 + y_N) / (1 + y_R) - 1
    Compounding wedge: BEI_lin - BEI_geom = y_R * BEI_geom
    """
    maturities = sorted(set(nominal_yields.keys()) & set(real_yields.keys()))
    curve = []

    for mat in maturities:
        y_n = nominal_yields[mat]
        y_r = real_yields[mat]
        bei_linear = y_n - y_r
        bei_geometric = (1.0 + y_n) / (1.0 + y_r) - 1.0
        wedge_bps = (bei_linear - bei_geometric) * 10_000.0

        curve.append(
            {
                "maturity": mat,
                "nominal_yield": y_n,
                "real_yield": y_r,
                "bei_linear": bei_linear,
                "bei_geometric": bei_geometric,
                "wedge_bps": wedge_bps,
            }
        )

    return curve


def price_zero_coupon_inflation_swap(
    maturity_years: int,
    nominal_discount_factor: float,
    expected_inflation_rate: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Price a Zero-Coupon Inflation Swap (ZCIS) under no-arbitrage.

    At maturity T, floating leg pays: [CPI_T / CPI_0 - 1] * Notional
    Fixed leg pays: [(1 + S)^T - 1] * Notional
    Par swap rate S satisfies: (1 + S)^T = E[CPI_T / CPI_0]
    """
    compounded_factor = (1.0 + expected_inflation_rate) ** maturity_years
    par_swap_rate = compounded_factor ** (1.0 / maturity_years) - 1.0

    pv_floating = nominal_discount_factor * (compounded_factor - 1.0)
    pv_fixed_at_par = nominal_discount_factor * ((1.0 + par_swap_rate) ** maturity_years - 1.0)

    return {
        "maturity": float(maturity_years),
        "par_swap_rate": par_swap_rate,
        "pv_floating": pv_floating,
        "pv_fixed_at_par": pv_fixed_at_par,
        "net_pv_par": pv_floating - pv_fixed_at_par,
    }


def attribute_breakeven_carry(
    nominal_yield: float,
    real_yield: float,
    horizon_years: float,
    expected_annual_cpi_growth: float,
    seasonal_adjustment_annualized: float,
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Attribute horizon carry on a long TIPS / short Treasury breakeven position.

    Carry consists of:
    1. Yield carry: Real coupon accrual minus Nominal coupon accrual: (y_R - y_N) * dt
    2. Inflation accrual carry: Actual CPI index growth: (pi + s) * dt
    3. Net carry: Yield carry + Inflation accrual carry
    """
    dt = horizon_years
    yield_carry_pct = (real_yield - nominal_yield) * dt * 100.0
    total_cpi_rate = expected_annual_cpi_growth + seasonal_adjustment_annualized
    inflation_accrual_pct = total_cpi_rate * dt * 100.0
    net_carry_pct = yield_carry_pct + inflation_accrual_pct
    net_carry_bps = net_carry_pct * 100.0

    return {
        "horizon_months": dt * 12.0,
        "yield_carry_bps": yield_carry_pct * 100.0,
        "inflation_accrual_bps": inflation_accrual_pct * 100.0,
        "net_carry_bps": net_carry_bps,
    }

Where the canon breaks

The standard model equates breakeven inflation to expected inflation: \(\text{BEI} \equiv \mathbb{E}[\pi]\). This identity fails empirically due to three structural market realities:

  1. Breakeven is not pure expectation: As demonstrated by D'Amico, Kim, and Wei (2018) (damico2018tips), market breakevens systematically diverge from survey inflation forecasts because they bundle an unobserved inflation risk premium and an unobserved TIPS illiquidity discount.
  2. Seasonality traps in short tenors: 1-year and 2-year breakeven inflation rates are heavily distorted by predictable calendar cycles in non-seasonally adjusted CPI-U. Quoting 1-year breakevens without seasonal adjustment produces illusory macro forecast revisions.
  3. Tax asymmetry: Nominal Treasury coupon payments are taxed on full cash yield, whereas TIPS coupons are taxed on real cash yield while phantom principal increases are taxed before receipt. Different marginal tax rates between retail and institutional holders prevent clean no-arbitrage parity.

Misconceptions

  • A decline in 10-year breakeven inflation proves that market inflation expectations have fallen.

    Breakeven inflation is contaminated by the TIPS liquidity premium. During severe liquidity shocks (such as October 2008), the TIPS liquidity premium spikes violently, driving real yields higher and depressing breakevens even when survey-expected inflation remains unchanged.

  • The simple difference between nominal yield and real yield (\(y_N - y_R\)) is the mathematically exact breakeven inflation rate.

    The simple difference is a linear approximation that ignores compound interest. The exact geometric breakeven is \((1 + y_N) / (1 + y_R) - 1\). The difference between linear and geometric breakeven equals the cross-product \(y_R \cdot \pi_{\text{geom}}\), which exceeds 4 to 8 basis points at normal interest rate levels.

  • Holding a duration-matched long TIPS / short Treasury position is costless if inflation exactly equals breakeven.

    The position carries negative daily yield carry equal to \(y_R - y_N\), while inflation accrual occurs in discrete lumps when monthly CPI prints are published. Furthermore, calendar seasonality creates substantial quarterly carry imbalances.

Problem set

Problem M.1: The 5-year nominal Treasury yield is 4.20% and the 5-year TIPS yield is 1.80% (annual compounding). Compute the linear breakeven rate and the exact geometric breakeven rate.

Solution

Linear breakeven is \(\text{BEI}_{\text{lin}} = 4.20\% - 1.80\% = 2.40\%\) (240 bps). Geometric breakeven is \(\pi_{\text{geom}} = (1.0420 / 1.0180) - 1 = 0.0235756 \approx 2.358\%\) (235.8 bps). The compounding cross-product wedge is \(240 - 235.8 = 4.2\) bps.

Problem M.2: In a 5-year Zero-Coupon Inflation Swap, the initial reference CPI is 300.00. At maturity 5 years later, the terminal CPI is 340.50. Calculate the percentage floating leg payoff received by the inflation receiver.

Solution

The floating leg payoff percentage is \((\text{CPI}_T / \text{CPI}_0) - 1 = (340.50 / 300.00) - 1 = 1.1350 - 1 = 13.50\%\). On a $10,000,000 notional swap, the payoff is $1,350,000.

Problem M.3: A trader holds a 10-year breakeven position with nominal yield 4.00% and TIPS yield 1.70%. Over a 6-month horizon (\(\Delta t = 0.5\) yr), annualized headline inflation accrues at 2.80%. Calculate the net horizon carry in basis points.

Solution

Yield carry is \((y_R - y_N) \Delta t = (1.70\% - 4.00\%) \times 0.5 = -2.30\% \times 0.5 = -1.15\%\) (-115 bps). Inflation accrual carry is \(2.80\% \times 0.5 = +1.40\%\) (+140 bps). Net horizon carry is \(-115 + 140 = +25.0\) bps.

Problem P.1: Prove that under continuous compounding, the linear breakeven inflation rate equals the exact geometric breakeven rate with zero cross-product wedge.

Solution

Let nominal price be \(P_N = e^{-n y_N}\) and real price be \(P_R = e^{-n y_R}\). Exact no-arbitrage breakeven inflation \(\pi_{\text{cont}}\) satisfies \(e^{-n y_N} = e^{-n y_R} e^{-n \pi_{\text{cont}}}\). Taking natural logarithms yields \(-n y_N = -n y_R - n \pi_{\text{cont}}\). Dividing by \(-n\) gives \(y_N = y_R + \pi_{\text{cont}}\), or \(\pi_{\text{cont}} = y_N - y_R\). Under continuous compounding, the linear yield spread is identically equal to the exact inflation rate with zero cross-product error.

Problem P.2: Prove that an increase in inflation volatility strictly increases the breakeven inflation rate on coupon-bearing bonds via Jensen's convexity inequality.

Solution

Bond price is a strictly convex function of yields: \(\partial^2 P / \partial y^2 > 0\). For TIPS, real cash flows are discounted at real yields. Under inflation uncertainty, Jensen's inequality implies \(\mathbb{E}[P(\pi)] > P(\mathbb{E}[\pi])\). Because the terminal price payoff of TIPS incorporates the convex deflation put option \(\max(1.0, I_T)\), higher inflation volatility increases the option value of the floor, depressing the required TIPS yield \(y_R\). Since \(\text{BEI} = y_N - y_R\), a lower \(y_R\) increases breakeven inflation.

Problem P.3: Prove that a Zero-Coupon Inflation Swap is equivalent to a portfolio of a long real zero-coupon bond, a short nominal zero-coupon bond, and a cash loan under frictionless markets.

Solution

At terminal date \(T\), the ZCIS pays \((\text{CPI}_T / \text{CPI}_0) - (1+S)^T\). A long real zero-coupon bond pays \(\text{CPI}_T / \text{CPI}_0\). A short nominal zero-coupon bond pays \(-(1+S)^T\). The net terminal cash flow of the bond package is identical to the ZCIS terminal payoff in every state of nature. Since both portfolios deliver identical cash flows at \(T\) with zero intermediate flows, no-arbitrage guarantees identical market valuations.

Problem E.1: During the COVID-19 shock in March 2020, 10-year US breakeven inflation dropped from 1.65% to 0.55% in two weeks. Analyze whether this movement reflected sudden deflation expectations or market microstructure stress.

Solution

Empirical analysis confirms this collapse was driven by market microstructure dislocation rather than a true drop in long-term inflation expectations. Survey inflation expectations (e.g. University of Michigan, SPF) remained stable near 2.2%. In March 2020, primary dealers faced severe balance-sheet exhaustion under the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR), and hedge funds were forced to liquidate leveraged TIPS positions, causing TIPS yields to spike and compressing breakevens.

Problem E.2: How does the empirical term structure of breakeven inflation typically slope during economic expansions versus late-cycle monetary tightening?

Solution

During normal expansions, the breakeven curve is upward-sloping, reflecting an increasing term premium and inflation risk premium for long horizons. During late-cycle aggressive monetary tightening (such as 2022–2023), short-term breakevens invert sharply above long-term breakevens, as near-term commodity shocks elevate 1- to 2-year inflation while long-term credibility anchors 10- and 30-year expectations near 2.2%.

Problem E.3: Why do market quotes for 1-year breakeven inflation display predictable oscillations of 80 to 120 basis points between spring and autumn?

Solution

Non-seasonally adjusted CPI-U rises sharply between January and May due to winter-to-spring seasonal price increases, then decelerates or declines between August and November. Because 1-year TIPS pay based on unadjusted CPI-U, the unhedged seasonal swing accounts for a large fraction of the annual price path, generating predictable seasonal variation in quoted short breakevens.

Problem R.1: Design a seasonally adjusted breakeven inflation estimator that purges monthly seasonal patterns from cash TIPS breakevens.

Solution

Let \(\gamma_m\) denote the historical monthly seasonal log-factor of headline CPI-U relative to trend (\(\sum_{m=1}^{12} \gamma_m = 0\)). For a TIPS bond maturing in month \(m_T\) evaluated in month \(m_t\), the seasonal carry adjustment is \(\Delta \gamma = (\gamma_{m_T} - \gamma_{m_t}) / \tau\). The seasonally adjusted breakeven is \(\text{BEI}^* = \text{BEI} - \Delta \gamma\). This estimator isolates underlying trend inflation expectations by subtracting deterministic calendar seasonality.

Problem R.2: Evaluate the impact of indexation lags on the pricing of inflation caps and floors in the inter-dealer derivatives market.

Solution

In standard ZCIS and inflation swaptions, payoffs reference \(\text{CPI}_{T-3}\) rather than contemporaneous \(\text{CPI}_T\). Because historical CPI data is published well before expiration, the final 2.5 months of the contract carry zero price uncertainty. Derivatives pricing models (e.g. Jarrow-Yildirim or market models) must split the index distribution into realized historical prints and stochastic forward diffusion, reducing effective implied volatility for short-tenor options.

Problem R.3: Why do corporate issuers rarely issue inflation-indexed debt compared to sovereign governments?

Solution

Sovereign governments collect tax revenues that scale directly with nominal GDP and aggregate price levels, making inflation-linked liabilities a natural balance-sheet hedge. In contrast, corporate firms face firm-specific revenue dynamics that may not correlate positively with headline CPI-U (especially under stagflationary cost shocks). Issuing TIPS exposes corporates to severe basis risk between general consumer inflation and firm revenues.

    In continuous time, \(e^{-n y_N} = e^{-n (y_R + \pi)} \implies y_N = y_R + \pi\).

  10. What is a key difference between Zero-Coupon Inflation Swaps and cash TIPS breakevens?

    Options

    Inflation swaps are unfunded derivatives and do not carry the balance-sheet warehousing frictions of cash TIPS.
