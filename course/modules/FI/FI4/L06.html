The Treasury Cash-Futures Basis Trade and the Dash for Cash

The Treasury cash-futures basis trade represents one of the largest leveraged quantitative arbitrage strategies in modern finance. By harvesting small pricing discrepancies between physical Treasury bonds and Treasury futures contracts through 20x to 50x repo leverage, multi-strategy hedge funds provide essential liquidity to government debt auctions. However, when market stress strikes, this hyper-leveraged structure transforms into a systemic vulnerability. In March 2020, margin calls and dealer balance-sheet constraints forced rapid unwinds, turning the safest bond market in the world into a chaotic dash for cash.

1The Cash-Futures Basis and Implied Repo Rate

Treasury futures contracts traded on the Chicago Board of Trade (CME Group) allow the short position to deliver any eligible Treasury bond from a defined basket at contract expiration. To standardize delivery pricing across bonds with different coupons and maturities, the exchange assigns a fixed conversion factor \(CF\) to each deliverable security.

Definition 6.1 (Treasury Cash-Futures Basis Trade)

The Treasury cash–futures basis trade is a relative-value arbitrage strategy in which a market participant buys a cash Treasury bond (dirty price \(P_{\text{cash}}\)), sells an equivalent number of Treasury futures contracts (quoted price \(F\)), and finances the cash bond purchase in the overnight or term repo market at rate \(r_{\text{repo}}\).

The gross basis per 100 face value is defined as:

\[ \text{Basis} = P_{\text{cash}} - (F \times CF) \]

where \(CF\) is the conversion factor of the cheapest-to-deliver (CTD) bond.

Definition 6.2 (Implied Repo Rate)

The implied repo rate (IRR) is the annualized rate of return earned by buying the cash Treasury bond today at dirty price \(P_{\text{cash}}\) and delivering it into the futures contract at delivery date \(t + d\) at invoice price \(F \times CF + \text{AI}_d\) (where \(\text{AI}_d\) is accrued interest at delivery):

\[ r_{\text{implied}} = \left(\frac{F \times CF + \text{AI}_d}{P_{\text{cash}}} - 1\right) \times \frac{360}{d} \]

The net basis carry (or net implied repo rate) is the spread \(s_{\text{net}} = r_{\text{implied}} - r_{\text{repo}}\). When \(s_{\text{net}} > 0\), the basis trade earns positive net carry above repo borrowing costs.

Because the net carry on a basis trade is typically thin (often only 10 to 30 basis points annualized), hedge funds achieve commercial equity returns by levering the position up to 50:1 using bilateral repo financing with haircuts of 1% to 2%.

2Leverage, Margin Calls, and Solvency Mechanics

A leveraged basis trade sits between two distinct margin regimes: bilateral repo financing and exchange-cleared futures margin.

Theorem 6.1 (Leveraged Basis Trade Equity Requirement and Return)

Consider a basis trade on notional \(N > 0\) of cash Treasuries at price \(P_{\text{cash}}\), hedged with \(CF\) futures equivalent contracts at price \(F\). Let the position be governed by:

  1. Repo haircut (\(h \in (0, 1)\)): Cash borrowed via repo is \(B_0 = N (P_{\text{cash}} / 100) (1 - h)\); cash equity posted is \(E_{\text{cash}} = N (P_{\text{cash}} / 100) h\).
  2. Futures initial margin (\(m \in (0, 1)\)): Futures equity posted is \(E_{\text{fut}} = N (F / 100) CF \cdot m\).
  3. Holding period (\(d\) days): Repo borrowing rate is \(r_{\text{repo}}\) under ACT/360.

Then total initial equity required is \(E_0 = E_{\text{cash}} + E_{\text{fut}}\), and effective leverage is \(\mathcal{L} = \frac{N (P_{\text{cash}} / 100)}{E_0}\). Under price changes \(\Delta P_{\text{cash}}\) and \(\Delta F\), total trade P&L is:

\[ \Pi = N \left[\frac{\Delta P_{\text{cash}}}{100} - \frac{\Delta F \times CF}{100}\right] - B_0 \cdot r_{\text{repo}} \frac{d}{360} \]

and realized return on equity is \(\text{ROE} = \frac{\Pi}{E_0}\).

Proof

The cash purchase requires total outlay \(N (P_{\text{cash}} / 100)\). Borrowing \(B_0 = N (P_{\text{cash}} / 100) (1 - h)\) leaves an equity requirement of \(E_{\text{cash}} = N (P_{\text{cash}} / 100) h\). The CME clearinghouse requires posting initial margin \(E_{\text{fut}} = N (F / 100) CF \cdot m\). Total initial capital is \(E_0 = E_{\text{cash}} + E_{\text{fut}}\). Over \(d\) days, the long bond position gains \(N \frac{\Delta P_{\text{cash}}}{100}\), the short futures position gains \(-N \frac{\Delta F \times CF}{100}\), and repo financing incurs interest \(B_0 \cdot r_{\text{repo}} \frac{d}{360}\). Summing these components yields \(\Pi\). Dividing by initial equity \(E_0\) gives \(\text{ROE}\).

Theorem 6.2 (Dual Margin Call Solvency Condition)

Let an adverse market dislocation occur where cash Treasury prices fall (\(\Delta P_{\text{cash}} < 0\)) and futures prices do not fall by the full hedge ratio (\(\Delta P_{\text{cash}} < \Delta F \times CF\)). Then:

  1. The repo lender issues a variation margin call: \(M^{\text{call}}_{\text{repo}} = N \frac{|\Delta P_{\text{cash}}|}{100} (1 - h)\).
  2. The futures exchange issues a variation margin call if futures losses breach maintenance margin.

The trade faces immediate forced liquidation if available liquid cash reserves \(C_{\text{liq}} < M^{\text{call}}_{\text{repo}} + M^{\text{call}}_{\text{fut}}\).

Proof

At the new price \(P' = P_{\text{cash}} - |\Delta P_{\text{cash}}|\), the maximum permissible repo loan is \(B_{\text{max}} = N (P' / 100) (1 - h)\). Because the initial loan \(B_0 = N (P_{\text{cash}} / 100) (1 - h)\) exceeds \(B_{\text{max}}\), the lender marks collateral to market and demands immediate repayment of the difference: \(B_0 - B_{\text{max}} = N \frac{|\Delta P_{\text{cash}}|}{100} (1 - h)\). Concurrently, any mark-to-market loss on the short futures position (\(\Delta F > 0\) or insufficient decline) debits the futures margin account. If total margin calls exceed unencumbered liquidity \(C_{\text{liq}}\), the fund cannot meet its contractual margin calls and must liquidate underlying collateral, proving the solvency condition.

3The March 2020 Market Dislocation ("Dash for Cash")

In March 2020, the onset of the global COVID-19 pandemic triggered an acute liquidity shock that exposed the fragility of the leveraged basis trade.

Definition 6.3 (The March 2020 Dash for Cash)

The March 2020 dash for cash was an unprecedented systemic dislocation in global money and sovereign bond markets between March 9 and March 23, 2020. Facing extreme pandemic uncertainty, foreign central banks, sovereign wealth funds, and corporate treasuries liquidated hundreds of billions of dollars of US Treasury securities to obtain US dollar cash balances.

He, Nagel, and Song (2022) document that during the March 2020 crisis, Treasury yields spiked rather than fell, cash-futures bases blew out to historical extremes, and Treasuries traded at an "inconvenience yield" relative to overnight index swaps (OIS). Primary dealers, already constrained by the Supplementary Leverage Ratio, could not expand their balance sheets to absorb the tidal wave of selling. As cash Treasury prices plunged relative to futures, basis-trading hedge funds faced catastrophic margin calls from repo lenders and futures exchanges.

As modeled by Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2008) and documented in the historical collapse of Long-Term Capital Management (1999), margin calls forced funds to liquidate cash Treasuries into a market devoid of dealer bids. This deleveraging spiral severely exacerbated the cash crash until the Federal Reserve intervened with open-ended Quantitative Easing purchases of over $1 trillion in Treasuries and emergency temporary SLR regulatory relief (FRB 2020).

repo leverage, margin calls, haircut sensitivity, and unwinding dynamics during
the March 2020 dash-for-cash market dislocation.

def main() -> None:
    # Portfolio setup: $100,000,000 face value cash-futures basis trade (SIMULATED)
    notional_usd = 100_000_000
    conversion_factor = 0.80
    haircut = 0.02  # 2.0% bilateral repo haircut
    initial_margin_rate = 0.03  # 3.0% CME futures initial margin

    # Capital requirements at trade inception (Day 0)
    p0_cash = 100.00
    f0_fut = 125.00  # Parity: 125.00 * 0.80 = 100.00

    repo_borrowed_usd = notional_usd * (p0_cash / 100.0) * (1.0 - haircut)  # $98,000,000
    repo_equity_usd = notional_usd * (p0_cash / 100.0) * haircut  # $2,000,000
    futures_equity_usd = notional_usd * (f0_fut / 100.0) * conversion_factor * initial_margin_rate  # $3,000,000
    total_equity_usd = repo_equity_usd + futures_equity_usd  # $5,000,000

    leverage = notional_usd / total_equity_usd  # 20.0x leverage

    print("Treasury Basis Trade Structure (SIMULATED, Day 0):")
    print(f"  Trade Notional:            ${notional_usd:,.0f}")
    print(f"  Cash Treasury Price:       {p0_cash:.2f}")
    print(f"  Futures Quoted Price:      {f0_fut:.2f} (CF = {conversion_factor:.2f})")
    print(f"  Repo Borrowing:            ${repo_borrowed_usd:,.0f} (Haircut = {haircut:.1%})")
    print(f"  Repo Haircut Equity:       ${repo_equity_usd:,.0f}")
    print(f"  Futures Margin Equity:     ${futures_equity_usd:,.0f} (Margin = {initial_margin_rate:.1%})")
    print(f"  Total Fund Equity Posted:  ${total_equity_usd:,.0f}")
    print(f"  Effective Balance Leverage:{leverage:.1f}x")

    # Three-day March 2020 stress simulation path
    # Day 0: Normal equilibrium
    # Day 1: Emerging dislocation (foreign central banks sell Treasuries)
    # Day 2: Dash for cash dislocation (severe basis blowout and haircut hike)
    market_path = [

        day = step["day"]
        p_c = float(str(step["p_cash"]))
        p_f = float(str(step["f_fut"]))
        h = float(str(step["haircut"]))
        m = float(str(step["fut_margin"]))

        # Basis = Implied futures delivery value minus cash price

        net_market_pnl = cash_pnl + fut_pnl

    p_f2 = float(str(d2["f_fut"]))

    print("           triggering a fire-sale spiral until Federal Reserve intervened with massive QE.")

4Where the canon breaks

Textbook market theory assumes that relative-value arbitrageurs stabilize asset prices by leaning against irrational market noise. In the Treasury basis trade, extreme leverage causes arbitrageurs to become net destabilizers during crises. When negative shocks hit, the mechanical interaction of binding repo haircuts, exchange variation margin calls, and dealer balance-sheet constraints forces arbitrageurs to dump the very assets they are trying to arbitrage, magnifying the dislocation rather than dampening it.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Because the cash-futures basis trade is fully hedged in DV01, it carries zero market risk and cannot lose money.”

    The basis trade hedges parallel interest rate risk, but is 100% exposed to basis risk (the spread between cash and futures prices). If the cash price falls faster than the futures price, the basis trade incurs massive losses.

  2. “During the March 2020 crisis, investors rushed to buy US Treasuries as a traditional safe-haven asset.”

    In mid-March 2020, Treasury yields spiked violently as market participants sold Treasuries to raise US dollar cash, causing physical sovereign debt to trade like an illiquid risk asset.

  3. “Hedge funds unwound the basis trade in March 2020 because they lost faith in the solvency of the US government.”

    The unwinds were driven entirely by liquidity mechanics: margin calls from repo lenders and CME clearinghouses exceeded fund cash reserves, forcing mechanical liquidations.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Gross basis and implied repo rate

A 10-year Treasury bond with conversion factor 0.8250 has a clean cash price of 99.50. The 10-year Treasury futures contract trades at 121.00. Compute the gross basis per 100 face value. If days to delivery are 60 and accrued interest is zero, compute the gross implied repo rate under ACT/360.

Solution

The implied futures price is \(F \times CF = 121.00 \times 0.8250 = 99.825\). Gross basis is \(\text{Basis} = P_{\text{cash}} - (F \times CF) = 99.50 - 99.825 = -0.325\) points (-32.5 cents). The gross implied repo rate is \(r_{\text{implied}} = (\frac{99.825}{99.50} - 1) \times \frac{360}{60} = (1.0032663 - 1) \times 6 = 0.019598 = 1.96\%\).

M2 · Net basis carry calculation

Using the bond in M1 with gross implied repo rate of 1.96%, if the fund's repo borrowing rate is 1.60%, compute the net basis carry in basis points. If the fund operates at 25x leverage, compute the annualized return on equity from carry.

Solution

Net carry is \(s_{\text{net}} = r_{\text{implied}} - r_{\text{repo}} = 1.96\% - 1.60\% = 0.36\% = 36.0\) basis points. At \(25\times\) leverage, annualized return on equity from carry is \(\text{ROE} = 25 \times 0.36\% = 9.00\%\).

M3 · Repo margin call calculation

A hedge fund borrows $98,000,000 in repo against $100,000,000 market value of Treasuries at a 2.0% haircut. If Treasury prices drop by 2.50% overnight, calculate the cash margin call demanded by the repo lender.

Solution

The new collateral value is \(\$100{,}000{,}000 \times (1 - 0.025) = \$97{,}500{,}000\). The maximum permissible repo loan is \(97{,}500{,}000 \times 0.98 = \$95{,}550{,}000\). The margin call is \(\$98{,}000{,}000 - \$95{,}550{,}000 = \$2{,}450{,}000\).

Proof

P1 · Basis convergence at delivery

Prove that at the exact delivery timestamp \(T\) of the futures contract, the net basis of the cheapest-to-deliver bond must converge to zero under the absence of delivery-option frictions.

Solution

At delivery date \(T\), a trader holding the cheapest-to-deliver cash bond can immediately deliver it to settle a short futures contract. The contractual invoice cash received is \(F_T \times CF + \text{AI}_T\). If the market spot price \(P_T < F_T \times CF + \text{AI}_T\), an arbitrageur buys cash bonds and delivers into futures for an instantaneous riskless profit. If \(P_T > F_T \times CF + \text{AI}_T\), the short futures position would deliver an alternative bond or buy the futures back. In competitive equilibrium without delivery frictions, no-arbitrage mandates \(P_T = F_T \times CF + \text{AI}_T\), forcing \(\text{Basis}_T \to 0\).

P2 · Non-linear margin spiral under haircut expansion

Prove that if a fund's equity is \(E\) and collateral value drops by fraction \(x\), while the repo haircut expands from \(h_0\) to \(h_1 > h_0\), the required asset liquidation fraction \(L\) is strictly convex in the haircut increase \(\Delta h = h_1 - h_0\).

Solution

Permissible position size is \(V(h) = \frac{E}{h}\). The liquidation required to restore compliance is \(V(h_0) - V(h_1) = E (\frac{1}{h_0} - \frac{1}{h_1}) = E \frac{h_1 - h_0}{h_0 h_1}\). Differentiating with respect to \(h_1\) yields \(\frac{dV}{dh_1} = -\frac{E}{h_1^2} < 0\) and \(\frac{d^2V}{dh_1^2} = \frac{2E}{h_1^3} > 0\). The strictly positive second derivative establishes strict convexity, proving that incremental haircut increases force exponentially escalating liquidations.

P3 · Invariance of basis trade to parallel yield shifts

Prove that when hedged using the exact conversion factor \(CF\) on the CTD bond, the first-order sensitivity of the gross basis trade with respect to a parallel yield shift \(\Delta y\) is identically zero.

Solution

The value of the basis trade per unit face value is \(V(y) = P(y) - F(y) \times CF\). By Definition 6.1, for the cheapest-to-deliver bond, futures price tracks cash price via \(F(y) \approx \frac{P(y)}{CF}\). Differentiating yields \(\frac{dV}{dy} = \frac{dP}{dy} - \frac{dF}{dy} \times CF = \frac{dP}{dy} - \frac{d}{dy}\left[\frac{P(y)}{CF}\right] \times CF = \frac{dP}{dy} - \frac{dP}{dy} = 0\). The trade is first-order immunized against parallel yield shifts.

Empirical

E1 · Foreign official sector selling in March 2020

Document the magnitude of US Treasury sales by foreign central banks during the two weeks of mid-March 2020 and evaluate why official accounts sold long-dated Treasuries rather than short-term T-bills.

Solution

Foreign official institutions sold approximately $250 billion in US Treasuries during March 2020. Facing domestic currency depreciation and sudden dollar shortages, central banks needed immediately deliverable dollar cash to intervene in foreign exchange markets. Because foreign reserves were predominantly held in long-term Treasury notes rather than bills, foreign central banks dumped 10-year and 30-year notes, overwhelming primary dealer warehousing capacity.

E2 · CME futures initial margin hikes

During the March 2020 dislocation, CME Group raised initial margin requirements on 10-year Treasury futures from 2.5% to over 4.5%. Analyze how this exchange policy exacerbated the funding squeeze on basis-trading funds.

Solution

A fund short 10-year futures saw initial margin requirements nearly double from $2,500 to $4,500 per contract. For a fund holding 20,000 contracts, this required posting an immediate $40 million in additional cash margin to the exchange, draining liquid cash reserves at the exact moment repo lenders were simultaneously issuing multi-million dollar haircut margin calls.

E3 · Federal Reserve emergency asset purchase scale

Examine the intervention of the Federal Open Market Committee between March 15 and March 31, 2020, documenting the daily purchase rate of Treasuries and Agency MBS.

Solution

On March 15, the Fed announced $500B in Treasury purchases. Finding markets still paralyzed, on March 23 the Fed announced open-ended ("unlimited") asset purchases. At its peak in late March, the New York Fed was purchasing up to $75 billion of Treasuries and $30 billion of Agency MBS per day—an unprecedented pace that absorbed dealer inventories, restored market depth, and compressed the basis back toward parity.

Research

R1 · Treasury inconvenience yields during COVID-19

Using the empirical findings of He, Nagel, and Song (2022), formulate how the breakdown of primary dealer intermediation inverted the sign of the Treasury convenience yield.

Solution

He, Nagel, and Song (2022) decompose Treasury yields into \(y_t = y^{\text{OIS}}_t - c_t\), where \(c_t\) is the convenience yield. Historically, \(c_t > 0\) (Treasuries yield less than OIS). In March 2020, \(c_t\) flipped to \(-40\) to \(-60\) bps. The authors demonstrate that holding Treasuries consumed dealer SLR equity, whereas unfunded OIS swaps did not. The convenience yield became an "inconvenience yield" reflecting the shadow price of dealer balance-sheet constraints.

R2 · Systemic risk of multi-strategy basis trade concentration

In 2023–2024, the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) warned of renewed hedge fund concentration in the Treasury basis trade. Formulate a macroprudential stress-test model evaluating the fire-sale impact of a 50 bps basis widening.

Solution

Let aggregate basis-trade notional be \(N_{\text{agg}} = \$800\text{B}\) levered \(30\times\) ($27B equity). A 50 bps basis widening inflicts a mark-to-market loss of \(\$800\text{B} \times 0.0050 \times D \approx \$28\text{B}\) (assuming 7Y CTD duration), wiping out aggregate equity. Repo lenders issue margin calls of \(\$800\text{B} \times 0.0050 \approx \$4\text{B}\). Forced liquidations of 50% of the book ($400B Treasuries) into primary dealers with limited SLR capacity would spike yields by an estimated 25–40 bps, requiring central-bank backstop intervention.

R3 · Comparison with the 1998 LTCM crisis

Compare the mechanisms of the March 2020 cash-futures basis trade unwind with the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) in on-the-run vs. off-the-run Treasury spread trades.

Solution

Both episodes involved highly levered relative-value arbitrageurs exploiting tiny pricing spreads (LTCM in on/off-the-run swap spreads; March 2020 in cash-futures basis). In both cases, an exogenous macro shock (1998 Russian default; 2020 COVID) triggered flight-to-cash. The divergence in collateral prices triggered escalating margin calls, while lenders raised haircuts, causing procyclical deleveraging that paralyzed market liquidity until coordinated intervention halted the spiral.
