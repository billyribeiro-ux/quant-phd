Swap Spreads and Negative Spreads

For decades, standard fixed-income theory asserted that interest rate swap rates must trade strictly higher than sovereign government bond yields of matching maturity, reflecting commercial bank credit and liquidity premia. In late 2008 and continuously since 2015, this core canon shattered: US 30-year swap spreads inverted deeply into negative territory, followed by 10-year and 5-year spreads. Negative swap spreads represent one of the most prominent persistent anomalies in modern capital markets, exposing how dealer balance-sheet constraints sever classical no-arbitrage bounds.

1Interest Rate Swaps and Swap Spread Definition

A plain-vanilla fixed-for-floating interest rate swap is an over-the-counter contract in which Counterparty A pays a constant fixed coupon rate \(S_T\) on notional \(N\) over maturity \(T\), while receiving periodic floating rate payments indexed to an overnight reference rate such as SOFR.

Definition 5.1 (Swap Spread)

Let \(S_T\) be the fixed par rate on a standardized fixed-for-floating interest rate swap of maturity \(T\), and let \(Y_T\) be the par yield to maturity on an on-the-run benchmark US Treasury bond of identical maturity \(T\). The swap spread \(s^{\text{sw}}_T\) is the arithmetic yield difference:

\[ s^{\text{sw}}_T = S_T - Y_T \]

Expressed in basis points, \(s^{\text{sw}}_T = (S_T - Y_T) \times 10{,}000\).

Historically, when floating legs were indexed to 3-month unsecured LIBOR, swap spreads were invariably positive (\(S_T > Y_T\)). Investors reasoned that commercial banks (the swap counterparties) carried default risk, whereas the US Treasury was default-free, requiring \(S_T\) to trade at a positive credit spread above \(Y_T\).

2The Negative Swap Spread Phenomenon

In November 2008, 30-year US swap spreads dropped below zero for the first time. Following the implementation of post-crisis capital regulations in 2015, negative swap spreads became a permanent, structural feature of the dollar yield curve at the 10-year and 30-year tenors.

Theorem 5.1 (Textbook Arbitrage of Negative Swap Spreads)

Suppose the swap spread is strictly negative: \(s^{\text{sw}}_T = S_T - Y_T < 0 \iff Y_T > S_T\). Consider the following portfolio established at time \(t=0\) on notional \(N > 0\):

  1. Leg 1: Buy a par Treasury bond of maturity \(T\) paying fixed coupon \(Y_T\).
  2. Leg 2: Enter a pay-fixed interest rate swap of maturity \(T\), paying fixed rate \(S_T\) and receiving floating rate \(r^{\text{float}}_t\).
  3. Leg 3: Finance the Treasury purchase in the overnight repo market, paying repo rate \(r^{\text{repo}}_t\).

If \(r^{\text{repo}}_t \equiv r^{\text{float}}_t\) for all \(t \in [0, T]\) and financing involves zero balance-sheet cost or haircuts, the net cash flow per period is strictly positive and riskless:

\[ \Pi_t = N \left[Y_T - S_T + (r^{\text{float}}_t - r^{\text{repo}}_t)\right] = N (Y_T - S_T) > 0 \]
Proof

At inception \(t=0\), buying the par Treasury costs \(N\), which is fully funded by borrowing \(N\) in the repo market, producing zero initial net cash outlay. Over each period \(t\), the cash inflows are the Treasury coupon \(N Y_T\) and the floating swap payment \(N r^{\text{float}}_t\). Cash outflows are the fixed swap payment \(N S_T\) and the repo interest payment \(N r^{\text{repo}}_t\). Summing cash flows yields \(\Pi_t = N [Y_T - S_T + (r^{\text{float}}_t - r^{\text{repo}}_t)]\). Under the assumption that \(r^{\text{repo}}_t = r^{\text{float}}_t\), the floating terms cancel identically, leaving \(\Pi_t = N (Y_T - S_T)\). Because \(Y_T > S_T\), \(\Pi_t > 0\) in every period, proving the textbook arbitrage.

3Why Arbitrage Fails: Regulatory Capital and Balance-Sheet Frictions

If Theorem 5.1 represents a riskless cash-generating machine, why do relative-value hedge funds and primary dealers not trade it in infinite size until the spread closes to zero?

The answer lies in dealer balance-sheet constraints and the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR). As formulated by Duffie (2010), when regulatory capital and balance-sheet capacity are slow-moving or constrained, arbitrageurs require substantial risk premia to warehouse assets, allowing large pricing anomalies to persist indefinitely.

Theorem 5.2 (Balance-Sheet Cost Barrier to Swap Spread Arbitrage)

Let an arbitrageur executing Theorem 5.1 face the following institutional frictions:

  1. Repo spread friction: Overnight repo borrowing costs exceed floating swap receipts by \(\delta = r^{\text{repo}} - r^{\text{float}} \ge 0\).
  2. Leverage capital charge: Holding the cash Treasury on-balance-sheet incurs a binding capital allocation \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) under the Supplementary Leverage Ratio.
  3. Hurdle rate of capital: The firm requires an equity return hurdle of \(\rho > 0\).

Then the net economic profit per dollar of notional is:

\[ \pi = (Y_T - S_T) - \delta - \lambda \rho \]

Consequently, the negative swap spread cannot be eliminated by arbitrage capital unless the yield spread exceeds the capital friction barrier:

\[ Y_T - S_T > \delta + \lambda \rho \]
Proof

Per dollar of notional (\(N=1\)), the gross interest cash flow is \(Y_T - S_T - (r^{\text{repo}} - r^{\text{float}}) = (Y_T - S_T) - \delta\). Holding the $1 cash Treasury asset increases Total Leverage Exposure by $1 under the SLR. Entering the interest rate swap requires only derivative margin (which nets off-balance-sheet). Allocating \(\lambda\) of equity capital to the Treasury asset incurs an annual capital opportunity cost of \(\lambda \rho\). Subtracting this capital cost from gross cash flow yields net economic profit \(\pi = (Y_T - S_T) - \delta - \lambda \rho\). Arbitrage capital enters if and only if economic profit \(\pi > 0\), which requires \(Y_T - S_T > \delta + \lambda \rho\).

Remark 5.1 (Structural Supply and Demand Imbalances)

Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen (2012) show that the aggregate supply of US Treasury debt fundamentally drives relative Treasury yields. In long-dated tenors (30 years), two massive structural forces dictate pricing: (1) enormous Treasury issuance to fund sovereign deficits, flooding the cash market; and (2) massive demand from corporate pension funds and life insurers to receive fixed in 30-year swaps to hedge long-duration liabilities. Because pension funds prefer cleared swaps over physical Treasuries to minimize balance-sheet usage, swap rates are driven down while Treasury yields are driven up, locking the 30-year swap spread deeply negative.

Lab 1 · Swap Spread Valuation and Capital-Adjusted Arbitrage Limits

Given simulated 10Y and 30Y Treasury yields of 4.25% and 4.55% against SOFR swap rates of 4.02% and 4.10%, the 10Y and 30Y swap spreads are -23.0 bps and -45.0 bps. An arbitrageur receiving the 45 bps Treasury yield spread over swaps earns $350,000 gross annual carry on $100M notional, but faces a $600,000 SLR capital hurdle (5% equity at 12% ROE), generating a net economic loss of -$250,000.

"""Lab FI4-L05-1: Swap spread mechanics and balance-sheet funded arbitrage limits.

Calculates term swap spreads and models why negative swap spreads persist when
holding cash Treasuries incurs binding Supplementary Leverage Ratio equity costs.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import NamedTuple


class TermPoint(NamedTuple):
    tenor_years: int
    treasury_yield_pct: float
    swap_rate_pct: float

    @property
    def swap_spread_bps(self) -> float:
        """Swap spread in basis points: (Swap Rate - Treasury Yield) * 10,000."""
        return (self.swap_rate_pct - self.treasury_yield_pct) * 100.0


def main() -> None:
    # SIMULATED term structure of Treasury par yields and SOFR fixed swap rates
    curve = [
        TermPoint(tenor_years=2, treasury_yield_pct=4.60, swap_rate_pct=4.52),
        TermPoint(tenor_years=5, treasury_yield_pct=4.30, swap_rate_pct=4.15),
        TermPoint(tenor_years=10, treasury_yield_pct=4.25, swap_rate_pct=4.02),
        TermPoint(tenor_years=30, treasury_yield_pct=4.55, swap_rate_pct=4.10),
    ]

    print("Term Structure of Swap Spreads (SIMULATED):")
    print(f"  {'Tenor':<8s} {'Treasury Yield':<16s} {'SOFR Swap Rate':<16s} {'Swap Spread':<15s}")
    for pt in curve:
        print(
            f"  {pt.tenor_years:>2d}Y     {pt.treasury_yield_pct:>6.2f}%          "
            f"{pt.swap_rate_pct:>6.2f}%          {pt.swap_spread_bps:>+6.1f} bps"
        )

    # Focus on 30-Year Negative Swap Spread: -45.0 bps
    pt30 = curve[3]
    pt30.treasury_yield_pct - pt30.swap_rate_pct  # +0.45% = +45 bps
    trade_notional_usd = 100_000_000  # $100M notional

    print("\nClassical 'Arbitrage' Trade on 30Y Spread (Receive Treasury, Pay Swap):")
    print(f"  Long $100M 30Y Treasury:   Receive {pt30.treasury_yield_pct:.2f}% fixed")
    print(f"  Pay Fixed 30Y SOFR Swap:   Pay     {pt30.swap_rate_pct:.2f}% fixed, Receive SOFR")
    print(f"  Finance Treasury via Repo: Pay     SOFR + 10 bps ({0.10:.2f}%) overnight repo")
    net_carry_bps = (pt30.treasury_yield_pct - pt30.swap_rate_pct) * 100.0 - 10.0
    gross_annual_pnl = trade_notional_usd * (net_carry_bps / 10_000.0)
    print(f"  Net Annual Carry Spread:   {net_carry_bps:+.1f} bps")
    print(f"  Gross Annual Cash PnL:     ${gross_annual_pnl:,.2f}")

    # Balance Sheet Capital Cost under Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR)
    slr_equity_weight = 0.05  # 5% Tier 1 capital allocated against $100M Treasury asset
    allocated_equity = trade_notional_usd * slr_equity_weight  # $5,000,000 equity
    cost_of_equity = 0.12  # 12.0% required return on bank capital
    annual_capital_hurdle = allocated_equity * cost_of_equity

    economic_profit = gross_annual_pnl - annual_capital_hurdle
    roe = gross_annual_pnl / allocated_equity

    print("\nRegulatory Capital Friction (SLR Impact):")
    print(f"  Treasury On-Balance-Sheet Asset: ${trade_notional_usd:,.0f}")
    print(f"  Tier 1 Equity Allocated (5% SLR): ${allocated_equity:,.0f}")
    print(f"  Annual Cost of Capital (12% ROE): ${annual_capital_hurdle:,.2f}")
    print(f"  Return on Allocated Equity (ROE): {roe:.2%}")
    print(f"  Net Economic Profit (EVA):        ${economic_profit:,.2f}")
    viability_str = (
        "Profitable" if economic_profit > 0 else "Unviable (Negative spread persists due to SLR balance sheet cost)"
    )
    print(f"  Arbitrage Viability:              {viability_str}")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

Textbook derivative pricing asserts the Law of One Price: two instruments that deliver identical cash flows must trade at the identical yield under no-arbitrage. In the interest rate swap market, this canon breaks down completely. A 30-year fixed US Treasury bond and a 30-year fixed SOFR swap have virtually identical interest rate risk (duration and dv01), yet Treasuries trade at a persistent yield premium of 30 to 50 basis points over swaps. The Law of One Price fails because holding physical bonds requires unweighted balance-sheet space under the SLR, whereas entering an unfunded derivative swap does not.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A negative swap spread means that the market perceives the US government as having higher default risk than commercial banks.”

    Negative swap spreads reflect balance-sheet capacity constraints and pension liability hedging flows, not sovereign default risk. Centrally cleared swaps are collateralized daily with variation margin, eliminating counterparty credit risk.

  2. “If a swap spread is negative, buying the Treasury and paying fixed on the swap is a free lunch with zero capital requirement.”

    Buying the Treasury bond requires on-balance-sheet financing, which consumes scarce Tier 1 equity capital under the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR), creating a substantial capital cost.

  3. “Swap spreads must eventually revert to positive values over time as markets normalize.”

    US 30-year swap spreads have remained persistently negative since 2015. Under structural Treasury deficit issuance and binding bank capital constraints, negative swap spreads represent a stable equilibrium.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Direct swap spread computation

If the benchmark 10-year Treasury note yields 4.18% and the 10-year SOFR fixed swap rate is quoted at 3.94%, compute the 10-year swap spread in basis points.

Solution

Applying Definition 5.1: \(s^{\text{sw}}_{10} = S_{10} - Y_{10} = 3.94\% - 4.18\% = -0.24\% = -24.0\) basis points.

M2 · Gross annual carry on negative swap spread trade

A trader establishes a $100,000,000 notional trade on a 30-year swap spread of -40 bps. The Treasury yields 4.60%, the swap rate is 4.20%, and the overnight repo borrowing rate matches floating swap receipts. Compute the gross annual cash flow in dollars.

Solution

Gross annual cash flow is \(\Pi = N (Y - S) = 100{,}000{,}000 \times (0.0460 - 0.0420) = 100{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0040 = \$400{,}000\) per year.

M3 · Capital hurdle barrier calculation

Suppose a primary dealer is subject to a 5.0% SLR requirement (\(\lambda = 0.05\)) and has an equity hurdle rate of 10.0% (\(\rho = 0.10\)). Repo borrowing costs exceed floating swap receipts by 8 basis points (\(\delta = 0.0008\)). What minimum Treasury yield premium \(Y_T - S_T\) in basis points is required to break even?

Solution

By Theorem 5.2, break-even requires \(Y_T - S_T = \delta + \lambda \rho = 0.0008 + (0.05 \times 0.10) = 0.0008 + 0.0050 = 0.0058 = 58.0\) basis points. The negative spread must be at least -58 basis points for the trade to be viable.

Proof

P1 · Duration matching in swap spread arbitrage

Prove that if an arbitrageur matches DV01 exactly between a par Treasury bond with modified duration \(D^{\text{mod}}_T\) and a par swap with annuity factor \(A_T = \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{1}{(1+S_T)^t}\), the portfolio is immune to first-order parallel shifts in interest rates.

Solution

The Treasury price sensitivity per 1 bp shift is \(\Delta P_{\text{bond}} \approx -D^{\text{mod}}_T \cdot P_{\text{bond}} \cdot \Delta y\). For a par bond, \(P_{\text{bond}} = N_{\text{bond}}\). For an interest rate swap, the present value sensitivity per 1 bp shift is \(\Delta V_{\text{swap}} \approx N_{\text{swap}} \cdot A_T \cdot \Delta y\). For a pay-fixed swap, value increases when rates rise. Matching DV01 requires \(N_{\text{bond}} D^{\text{mod}}_T = N_{\text{swap}} A_T\). Differentiating the combined value \(V = P_{\text{bond}} + V_{\text{swap}}\) with respect to a parallel rate shift \(\Delta y\) gives \(\frac{dV}{dy} = -N_{\text{bond}} D^{\text{mod}}_T + N_{\text{swap}} A_T = 0\), establishing first-order duration immunity.

P2 · Non-negativity of swap spreads under frictionless repo

Prove that in an idealized economy with zero repo haircuts, zero balance-sheet costs, and unconstrained short-selling of Treasuries, the swap spread must satisfy \(s^{\text{sw}}_T \ge 0\).

Solution

Suppose \(s^{\text{sw}}_T < 0 \implies Y_T > S_T\). By Theorem 5.1, an arbitrageur can borrow cash at \(r^{\text{repo}}\), purchase the Treasury at \(Y_T\), and enter a pay-fixed swap at \(S_T\). Under zero haircuts and frictionless repo, initial investment is 0. Cash flows are strictly positive in every period: \(\Pi_t = N (Y_T - S_T) > 0\). This constitutes a pure arbitrage (positive payoff with zero investment and zero risk). Under the no-arbitrage axiom of competitive equilibrium, such opportunities cannot exist. Hence \(s^{\text{sw}}_T \ge 0\).

P3 · Bounds on swap spread under asymmetric funding rates

Prove that if an investor's repo borrowing rate is \(r^{\text{borrow}} = \text{SOFR} + \delta_{\text{borrow}}\) and reverse-repo lending rate is \(r^{\text{lend}} = \text{SOFR} - \delta_{\text{lend}}\), the no-arbitrage bounds for the swap spread widen to an interval \([-\delta_{\text{borrow}}, +\delta_{\text{lend}}]\) in the absence of balance-sheet capital charges.

Solution

To exploit a negative spread (\(Y_T > S_T\)), the investor borrows at \(r^{\text{borrow}}\), earning net spread \(Y_T - S_T - \delta_{\text{borrow}}\). Arbitrage enters only if \(Y_T - S_T > \delta_{\text{borrow}} \iff s^{\text{sw}}_T < -\delta_{\text{borrow}}\). To exploit a positive spread (\(S_T > Y_T\)), the investor shorts the Treasury (lending cash at \(r^{\text{lend}}\)) and receives fixed on the swap, earning \(S_T - Y_T - \delta_{\text{lend}}\). Arbitrage enters only if \(S_T - Y_T > \delta_{\text{lend}} \iff s^{\text{sw}}_T > +\delta_{\text{lend}}\). Thus the no-arbitrage band is \([-\delta_{\text{borrow}}, +\delta_{\text{lend}}]\).

Empirical

E1 · The 30-year pension liability hedging flow

Analyze how demographic aging and pension accounting standards (such as ERISA and PBO discounting) create structural demand for long-duration swaps that persistently depresses 30-year swap rates.

Solution

Corporate defined-benefit pension funds must discount long-dated future retirement liabilities using high-quality corporate yield curves. To eliminate interest rate risk (liability-driven investing, LDI), pension funds must acquire long duration. Rather than purchasing physical 30-year Treasuries (which requires substantial cash capital), pension funds enter receive-fixed 30-year interest rate swaps. This creates massive, price-inelastic demand to receive fixed, driving 30-year swap rates down relative to physical Treasury bond yields.

E2 · Post-2008 swap spread inversion timeline

Document the structural shift in US 30-year swap spreads from pre-2008 positive levels (+30 to +60 bps) to negative levels during the Global Financial Crisis and post-2015.

Solution

Prior to 2008, 30-year swap spreads averaged +30 to +50 bps, reflecting LIBOR interbank credit risk. In October–November 2008, massive deleveraging and Treasury issuance inverted the 30-year spread below zero for the first time. While spreads briefly recovered, the phased implementation of the Basel III Supplementary Leverage Ratio in 2015 permanently locked 30-year swap spreads between -30 bps and -60 bps, where they have remained continuously.

E3 · Swap spreads during the UK mini-budget LDI crisis

In September 2022, the UK "mini-budget" triggered an acute LDI crisis in British Gilts. Analyze why rapid yield spikes forced pension funds to unwind receive-fixed swaps and how this affected swap spreads.

Solution

As 30-year Gilt yields surged over 100 bps in days, the market value of pension funds' receive-fixed swaps collapsed. Clearinghouses issued massive variation margin calls. Lacking sufficient cash, pension funds were forced to liquidate both physical Gilts and swap positions simultaneously. The resulting fire sale widened Gilt yields even faster than swap rates, demonstrating how margin spirals in derivative overlays propagate into underlying sovereign cash debt.

Research

R1 · Slow-moving capital and limits to arbitrage

Using Duffie's (2010) presidential address framework, formulate why search frictions, specialized institutional mandates, and capital immobility prevent asset managers from closing swap spread dislocations.

Solution

In Duffie's model, capital cannot reallocate instantaneously across segmented markets. Asset managers specialize: equity funds cannot trade repo, and sovereign wealth funds avoid derivative swaps. When an exogenous shock (e.g. Treasury debt deluge) hits, the capital available to absorb the dislocation is limited to a small group of specialized relative-value funds. Because these funds face convex balance-sheet costs and finite risk capital, asset prices must dislocate substantially to induce slow-moving capital to enter.

R2 · Sovereign debt supply and the convenience yield

Using the empirical framework of Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen (2012), formulate the regression model relating the aggregate US debt-to-GDP ratio to the convenience yield wedge between Treasuries and private benchmarks.

Solution

The authors specify \(\text{Spread}_t = \alpha + \beta \ln(\text{Debt}_t / \text{GDP}_t) + \gamma X_t + \epsilon_t\), where \(\text{Spread}_t\) is the yield gap between AAA corporate debt/swaps and Treasuries. They estimate \(\beta < 0\), establishing that as sovereign debt supply expands, the marginal safety and liquidity utility ("convenience yield") of Treasuries diminishes. When debt supply reaches extreme levels, the convenience yield vanishes entirely and balance-sheet absorption costs push Treasury yields above swap rates.

R3 · SOFR discounting vs. Libor discounting transition

Analyze the mathematical impact on historical swap spread time series when clearinghouses switched discounting regimes from Fed Funds/Libor to SOFR discounting in October 2020.

Solution

Under Libor discounting, the floating leg contained an embedded bank credit risk premium, which naturally elevated swap rates. When clearinghouses (CME and LCH) executed the big-bang transition to SOFR discounting in October 2020, floating legs transitioned to a secured rate. Because SOFR carries zero bank credit risk, the baseline swap rate curve shifted downward, structurally widening the negative spread between SOFR swap rates and physical Treasury yields.
