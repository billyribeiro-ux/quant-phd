Term Premium: Definition, Measurement & Macroeconomic Drivers

Long-term Treasury yields reflect two distinct economic forces: expected future short-term policy rates and an additional compensation demanded by investors for holding duration risk, known as the term premium. Disentangling these two components is essential for monetary policymakers evaluating financial conditions and for quantitative fixed-income managers allocating curve risk. When a 10-year yield shifts by 50 basis points, trading strategies differ drastically depending on whether expected policy rates shifted or the term premium expanded.

1Theoretical Definition and Yield Decomposition

The yield on an \(n\)-period zero-coupon bond \(y_t^{(n)}\) can be priced either under the risk-neutral pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) or decomposed into physical expectations under \(\mathbb{P}\) plus a residual risk premium.

Definition 3.1 (Term Premium)

The term premium \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}\) on an \(n\)-period zero-coupon bond is defined as the difference between the observed zero-coupon yield \(y_t^{(n)}\) and the average expected short rate over the bond's remaining life under the physical probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\):

\[ \mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)} \equiv y_t^{(n)} - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}\left[r_{t+i}\right] \]

where \(r_{t+i} \equiv y_{t+i}^{(1)}\) is the 1-period risk-free rate at time \(t+i\).

Theorem 3.1 (Additive Yield Decomposition Identity)

Every zero-coupon bond yield identically decomposes into an expected short rate component and the term premium:

\[ y_t^{(n)} = \bar{r}_t^{(n)} + \mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)} \]

where \(\bar{r}_t^{(n)} \equiv \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[r_{t+i}]\). Furthermore, the term premium identically equals the average expected excess holding return over the bond's life:

\[ \mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}\left[rx_{t+j}^{(n-j+1)}\right] \]
Proof

By definition, 1-period excess returns satisfy \(rx_{t+j}^{(k)} = p_{t+j}^{(k-1)} - p_{t+j-1}^{(k)} - r_{t+j-1}\). Summing from \(j=1\) to \(n-1\) with \(k = n-j+1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} rx_{t+j}^{(n-j+1)} &= \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \left(p_{t+j}^{(n-j)} - p_{t+j-1}^{(n-j+1)}\right) - \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} r_{t+j-1} \\ &= -r_{t+n-1} - p_t^{(n)} - \sum_{i=0}^{n-2} r_{t+i} = n y_t^{(n)} - \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} r_{t+i} \end{aligned} \]

Taking conditional expectations under \(\mathbb{P}\) and dividing by \(n\) yields \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Physical Measure vs. Risk-Neutral Measure

Under no-arbitrage pricing with stochastic discount factor \(M_{t+1}\), bond prices satisfy \(P_t^{(n)} = \mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1} P_{t+1}^{(n-1)}] = \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}}[\exp(-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} r_{t+i})]\). In a log-normal Gaussian term structure model:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t^{(n)} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[r_{t+i}\right] - \frac{1}{2n}\operatorname{Var}_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\left(p_{t+n}\right) \\ &= \bar{r}_t^{(n)} + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}}[r_{t+i}] - \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[r_{t+i}]\right) - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_n^2 \end{aligned} \]

The term premium is driven by the divergence between risk-neutral \(\mathbb{Q}\) and physical \(\mathbb{P}\) expectations, which reflects time-varying market prices of risk \(\lambda_t\).

3Macroeconomic Drivers and Empirical Properties

Because physical short-rate expectations are unobservable, economists rely on dynamic affine term structure models (DTSMs), such as Adrian, Crump, and Moench (2013) (ACM), to estimate \(\bar{r}_t^{(n)}\) and \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}\) in real time.

Adrian, Crump, and Moench (2013) demonstrate that the 10-year Treasury term premium is strongly countercyclical: it surges during recessions when investor risk aversion and macroeconomic uncertainty peak, and compresses during sustained economic expansions.

Lab 1 · Term premium extraction and yield curve decomposition

Under the expectations hypothesis identity, every observed yield decomposes into the average physical expected short rate plus the term premium: \(y_t^{(n)} = \bar{r}_t^{(n)} + \mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}\). When the short rate follows a persistent AR(1) process, the expected rate path reverts toward the long-run neutral rate, and the term premium accounts for a substantial fraction of the observed long-term yield.

"""Lab FI3-L03-1: Term premium extraction and yield curve decomposition.

SIMULATED: generates synthetic yield paths to decompose the 10-year yield into
the physical expected short-rate path and the term premium.
Standard library and NumPy only; no network.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def decompose_yield(
    r_t: float,
    mu_r: float,
    phi_r: float,
    maturity: int,
    y_observed: float,
) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Decompose observed yield into average expected short rate and term premium:

    y_t^{(n)} = (1/n) * sum_{i=0}^{n-1} E_t[r_{t+i}] + TP_t^{(n)}
    under AR(1) short-rate dynamics: r_{t+1} - mu_r = phi_r * (r_t - mu_r) + eps.
    """
    expected_sum = 0.0
    for i in range(maturity):
        # E_t[r_{t+i}] = mu_r + phi_r^i * (r_t - mu_r)
        e_r_i = mu_r + (phi_r**i) * (r_t - mu_r)
        expected_sum += e_r_i

    avg_expected_r = expected_sum / float(maturity)
    term_premium = y_observed - avg_expected_r
    pct_tp = (term_premium / y_observed) * 100.0 if y_observed != 0 else 0.0

    return avg_expected_r, term_premium, pct_tp


def main() -> None:
    # Stylized parameters for monetary policy regime:
    # Long-run neutral short rate mu_r = 3.5%, persistence phi_r = 0.85 per annum
    mu_r = 0.035
    phi_r = 0.85
    maturity = 10

    scenarios = [
        ("Expansion (r_t = 5.0%, y_10 = 5.5%)", 0.050, 0.055),
        ("Neutral (r_t = 3.5%, y_10 = 4.8%)", 0.035, 0.048),
        ("Recession / Easing (r_t = 1.5%, y_10 = 3.8%)", 0.015, 0.038),
    ]

    print("10-Year Yield Decomposition: y_t^(10) = Expected Short Rate + Term Premium:")
    for name, r_t, y_10 in scenarios:
        avg_r, tp, pct_tp = decompose_yield(r_t, mu_r, phi_r, maturity, y_10)
        print(f"\nScenario: {name}")
        print(f"  Short rate r_t                   = {r_t:.2%}")
        print(f"  Observed 10y yield               = {y_10:.2%}")
        print(f"  10y Average Expected Short Rate  = {avg_r:.4%}")
        print(f"  10y Term Premium (TP)            = {tp:.4%} ({tp * 10000:.1f} bps)")
        print(f"  Term Premium share of 10y yield  = {pct_tp:.1f}%")

    # Invariant check: Expected short rate + Term premium == Observed yield
    for _, r_t, y_10 in scenarios:
        avg_r, tp, _ = decompose_yield(r_t, mu_r, phi_r, maturity, y_10)
        assert abs((avg_r + tp) - y_10) < 1e-12, "Decomposition must articulate exactly"
    print("\nArticulation check: avg_expected_r + term_premium == observed_yield verified for all cases.")


main()

Where the canon breaks

Textbook fixed income often asserts that the term premium is always positive and upward-sloping across maturities as a reward for illiquidity and duration risk. Empirically, the canon breaks decisively:

  • Persistent Negative Term Premia: Between 2014 and 2021, 10-year US and European term premia plummeted below zero, driven by central bank balance-sheet expansions and institutional hedging demand from pension funds.
  • Small-Sample Econometric Bias: As shown by Bauer and Rudebusch (2017), persistent short rates cause OLS estimators in affine models to underestimate mean-reversion speed, artificially inflating estimated term premium volatility by 30% to 50%.

Misconceptions

  1. A steep yield curve proves that the market expects rapid interest rate hikes.

    Curve steepness reflects the sum of expected short-rate changes and the slope of the term premium. An upward-sloping yield curve can occur even when the market expects rate cuts if the term premium is sufficiently positive.

  2. The term premium is an unobservable academic residual with no direct economic impact.

    The term premium directly dictates real corporate financing costs, mortgage benchmarks, and sovereign debt issuance yields. Central bank asset purchase programs operate primarily by compressing the term premium.

  3. The term premium must strictly be positive because long bonds carry more duration risk than short bills.

    When Treasury returns are negatively correlated with equity market returns, bonds act as a valuable deflation hedge, lowering the required risk premium to negative values under consumption-based CAPM.

Problem set

Mechanics

Suppose the 5-year zero-coupon yield is \(y_t^{(5)} = 4.25\%\). Surveys of professional forecasters indicate expected 1-year short rates over the next 5 years: \(r_t = 3.00\%\), \(\mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+1}] = 3.50\%\), \(\mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+2}] = 3.75\%\), \(\mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+3}] = 4.00\%\), \(\mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+4}] = 4.00\%\). Compute the 5-year term premium in basis points.

Solution

The average expected short rate is \(\bar{r}_t^{(5)} = (3.00\% + 3.50\% + 3.75\% + 4.00\% + 4.00\%) / 5 = 18.25\% / 5 = 3.65\%\). The 5-year term premium is \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(5)} = y_t^{(5)} - \bar{r}_t^{(5)} = 4.25\% - 3.65\% = 0.60\% = 60\text{ bps}\).

The short rate follows an AR(1) process under \(\mathbb{P}\): \(r_{t+1} - \mu = \phi(r_t - \mu) + \epsilon_{t+1}\) with \(\mu = 3.0\%\), \(\phi = 0.8\), and current short rate \(r_t = 5.0\%\). Calculate the expected short rate \(\mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+3}]\).

Solution

\(\mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+3}] = \mu + \phi^3(r_t - \mu) = 0.03 + 0.8^3(0.05 - 0.03) = 0.03 + 0.512(0.02) = 0.03 + 0.01024 = 4.024\%\).

Given 2-year yield \(y_t^{(2)} = 4.00\%\) with \(\bar{r}_t^{(2)} = 3.80\%\), and 10-year yield \(y_t^{(10)} = 4.75\%\) with \(\bar{r}_t^{(10)} = 3.90\%\). Decompose the 10-minus-2 yield spread into the expected rate spread and the term premium slope.

Solution

\(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(2)} = 4.00\% - 3.80\% = 20\text{ bps}\), \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(10)} = 4.75\% - 3.90\% = 85\text{ bps}\). Yield spread is \(75\text{ bps}\). Expected rate spread is \(3.90\% - 3.80\% = 10\text{ bps}\). Term premium slope is \(85 - 20 = 65\text{ bps}\) (accounting for \(86.7\%\) of the curve slope).

Proof

Prove that \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[rx_{t+j}^{(n-j+1)}]\).

Solution

Recall \(rx_{t+j}^{(k)} = p_{t+j}^{(k-1)} - p_{t+j-1}^{(k)} - r_{t+j-1}\). Summing over \(j=1,\dots,n-1\) with \(k=n-j+1\) produces a telescoping sum of log bond prices: \(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(p_{t+j}^{(n-j)} - p_{t+j-1}^{(n-j+1)}) = p_{t+n-1}^{(1)} - p_t^{(n)} = -r_{t+n-1} - p_t^{(n)}\). Subtracting the sum of short rates gives \(-p_t^{(n)} - \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} r_{t+i} = n y_t^{(n)} - \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} r_{t+i}\). Taking conditional expectations and dividing by \(n\) delivers the result.

Prove that the yield term premium is the arithmetic average of forward term premia across maturities: \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{FTP}_t^{(m, m+1)}\), where \(\mathrm{FTP}_t^{(m, m+1)} \equiv f_t^{(m, m+1)} - \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}[r_{t+m}]\).

Solution

From the definition of forward rates, \(n y_t^{(n)} = r_t + \sum_{m=1}^{n-1} f_t^{(m, m+1)}\). Similarly, \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{P}[r_{t+i}] = r_t + \sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{P}[r_{t+m}]\). Subtracting the two identities gives \(n \mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)} = \sum_{m=1}^{n-1}(f_t^{(m, m+1)} - \mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{P}[r_{t+m}]) = \sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{FTP}_t^{(m, m+1)}\). Dividing by \(n\) proves the proposition.

Under the continuous-time Vasicek model with constant market price of risk \(\lambda\), prove that the asymptotic forward term premium \(\lim_{\tau \to \infty} (f_t(\tau) - \mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{P}[r_{t+\tau}])\) is constant.

Solution

Under \(\mathbb{P}\), \(\lim_{\tau \to \infty}\mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{P}[r_{t+\tau}] = \theta\). Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the long-run mean is \(\theta^* = \theta - \frac{\lambda \sigma}{\kappa}\). The asymptotic forward rate is \(\lim_{\tau \to \infty} f_t(\tau) = \theta^* - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2} = \theta - \frac{\lambda \sigma}{\kappa} - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\). Thus the difference is \(-\frac{\lambda \sigma}{\kappa} - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\), which is strictly constant.

Empirical

Explain how finite-sample Hurwicz bias in estimated autoregressive parameters \(\hat{\phi} < \phi\) distorts the estimated term premium in historical yield data.

Solution

When the true short rate persistence \(\phi\) is close to 1, OLS underestimates persistence: \(\hat{\phi}_{\text{OLS}} \approx \phi - (1 + 3\phi)/T\). Under attenuated persistence \(\hat{\phi}\), projected short rates mean-revert to \(\mu\) excessively fast, rendering modeled average expected short rates \(\bar{r}_t^{(n)}\) unrealistically smooth. Because \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)} = y_t^{(n)} - \bar{r}_t^{(n)}\), observed cyclical yield variations are wrongly attributed to the term premium, overstating term premium volatility (Bauer and Rudebusch, 2017).

During the 2008 global financial crisis, the Federal Reserve reduced the federal funds target to 0–25 bps, while 10-year Treasury yields remained near 3.50%. According to ACM model estimates, what drove the yield spread: expected rate increases or term premium?

Solution

Model estimates indicated that forward expectations of short rates remained near the zero lower bound for years (\(\bar{r}_t^{(10)} \approx 1.5\%\)). The elevated 10-year yield of 3.50% was primarily driven by a surge in the 10-year term premium to \(\approx 200\text{ bps}\), reflecting high macroeconomic volatility, flight from private credit risk, and deep uncertainty about Treasury supply.

In November 2010, the Fed announced QE2 (purchasing $600B of Treasuries). Empirical event evidence shows 10-year yields fell ~18 bps on announcement, while fed funds futures for 2 years forward barely moved. Analyze the transmission channel.

Solution

Because short rate expectations did not adjust, the signaling channel of monetary policy was minimal. The ~18 bps reduction in long yields operated almost entirely through the portfolio balance channel, compressing the 10-year term premium by removing duration risk from private balance sheets.

Research

Prior to 1998, stock and Treasury returns were positively correlated in the US (\(\operatorname{Cov}(r_s, r_b) > 0\)), whereas from 1998 to 2020, the correlation was predominantly negative (\(\operatorname{Cov}(r_s, r_b) < 0\)). Explain why this structural shift creates downward pressure on the equilibrium term premium in an asset pricing framework.

Solution

Under consumption CAPM, \(\mathbb{E}[R_i - R_f] \propto \operatorname{Cov}(R_m, R_i)\). When bonds covary positively with equities (supply-shock regimes), bonds lose value during recessions, demanding a positive term premium. When inflation is anchored and demand shocks dominate, equity downturns trigger flight-to-safety flows into Treasuries, making bonds a deflation hedge (\(\operatorname{Cov}(r_s, r_b) < 0\)). As a hedging asset, bonds command a negative equilibrium risk premium.

Describe how high-frequency event studies around FOMC policy announcements use OIS swaps and zero-coupon Treasury curves to separate policy rate surprises from term premium revisions.

Solution

Researchers measure yield changes in a tight 30-minute window around FOMC statements. The shift in short-dated OIS (1-month to 1-year) captures the pure target and forward guidance surprise \(\Delta \mathbb{E}_t[r]\). The difference between the 10-year Treasury yield change and the 10-year OIS rate change (or DTSM expectation change) isolates the immediate term premium response.

Critique the practice of using unrestricted OLS estimates for VAR state dynamics in affine models as implemented in Adrian, Crump, and Moench (2013) versus the bias-corrected approach of Bauer and Rudebusch (2017).

Solution

ACM estimate state dynamics using unrestricted OLS for computational speed. However, because yields are highly persistent, OLS systematically underestimates the spectral radius of the state transition matrix. Bauer and Rudebusch (2017) demonstrate that bias-correcting pushes persistence closer to 1, causing expectations to vary more over business cycles and reducing the model-implied volatility of the term premium by up to 50%.

Retrieval quiz

  1. By definition, what is the term premium on an \(n\)-period zero-coupon bond?

    Options

    The term premium is defined as \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)} \equiv y_t^{(n)} - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{P}[r_{t+i}]\).

  2. If the pure expectations hypothesis holds under \(\mathbb{P}\), what is the term premium?

    Options

    Under the PEH, expected excess returns are zero, so the term premium vanishes.

  3. If the 10-year yield is 4.50% and the average expected short rate over the next 10 years is 3.75%, what is the 10-year term premium in basis points?

    \(4.50\% - 3.75\% = 0.75\% = 75\text{ bps}\).

  4. How does OLS persistence bias in estimated autoregressive parameters affect model-based term premium estimates?

    Options

    Bauer and Rudebusch (2017) demonstrate that downward Hurwicz bias compresses expected rate variability, forcing the term premium to absorb excess yield variance.

  5. Over the macroeconomic business cycle in the United States, how does the term premium typically behave?

    Options

    Risk premia spike in recessions when risk aversion and uncertainty are highest.

  6. Can the term premium be negative in real-world debt markets?

    Options

    Negative stock-bond covariance and central bank QE pushed 10-year term premia below zero during 2014-2021.

  7. If a 5-year bond has yield 4.20% and expected 1-year rates for years 1 through 5 are 3.0%, 3.5%, 3.6%, 4.0%, and 3.9%, what is the term premium in basis points?

    Average expected rate is \((3.0+3.5+3.6+4.0+3.9)/5 = 18.0 / 5 = 3.60\%\). The term premium is \(4.20\% - 3.60\% = 0.60\% = 60\text{ bps}\).

  8. How does the yield term premium relate to forward term premia across intervening maturities?

    Options

    \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{FTP}_t^{(m, m+1)}\).

  9. In the ACM dynamic term structure model, what factors drive variation in the term premium?

    Options
