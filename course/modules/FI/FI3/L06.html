Model-Based Term Premia & Dynamic Term Structure Models

Because survey forecasts of interest rates are infrequent and subject to behavioral biases, quantitative finance relies on dynamic term structure models (DTSMs) to extract term premia at daily frequencies. Historically, maximum likelihood estimation of non-linear DTSMs required high-dimensional non-convex optimization prone to local minima. The Adrian, Crump, and Moench (2013) breakthrough reformulated affine term structure estimation into three transparent, closed-form linear OLS regressions, enabling instantaneous, robust extraction of model-based term premia across the entire yield curve.

1Theoretical Foundation: Affine Pricing & Measure Separation

A continuous or discrete-time term structure model enforces no-arbitrage cross-equation restrictions between physical time-series dynamics (\(\mathbb{P}\)) and cross-sectional bond prices (\(\mathbb{Q}\)).

Definition 6.1 (Model-Based Term Premium)

A model-based term premium \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}(\Theta)\) is the term premium extracted from an estimated parametric dynamic term structure model parameterized by \(\Theta \equiv (\Phi, \mu, \Sigma, \lambda_0, \lambda_1)\):

\[ \mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}(\Theta) \equiv y_t^{(n)} - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{P}}\left[r_{t+i} \mid \Theta\right] \]

where the expected short rates are computed by iterating the physical VAR state transition equations forward under estimated parameters \(\hat{\Theta}\).

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the model fits observed bond prices through affine recursive loadings \(p_t^{(n)} = A_n + B_n^\top X_t\), while physical dynamics follow \(X_{t+1} = \mu + \Phi X_t + v_{t+1}\). The market price of risk links the two measures: \(\lambda_t = \lambda_0 + \lambda_1 X_t\).

Theorem 6.1 (Adrian-Crump-Moench Three-Step Linear Estimator)

Under essentially affine Gaussian dynamics, the DTSM parameters can be estimated without non-linear optimization via three consecutive OLS regressions (Adrian, Crump, and Moench 2013):

  1. Step 1 (Physical State Dynamics): Extract \(K\) principal components \(X_t\) of yields and estimate a VAR(1) by OLS: \[ X_{t+1} = \mu + \Phi X_t + v_{t+1}, \quad v_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_v) \]
  2. Step 2 (Time-Series Excess Return Regressions): Regress 1-period excess returns \(rx_{t+1}^{(n)}\) on state innovations \(v_{t+1}\) and lagged states \(X_t\): \[ rx_{t+1}^{(n)} = \alpha_n + \beta_n^\top v_{t+1} + c_n^\top X_t + e_{t+1}^{(n)} \]
  3. Step 3 (Cross-Sectional Market Prices of Risk): Estimate risk price parameters \(\lambda_0\) and \(\lambda_1\) across maturities by cross-sectional OLS regressions: \[ c_n = \beta_n^\top \lambda_1, \quad \alpha_n + \frac{1}{2}(\beta_n^\top \Sigma_v \beta_n + \sigma_{e, n}^2) = \beta_n^\top \lambda_0 \]
Proof

The absence of arbitrage implies the existence of a stochastic discount factor \(M_{t+1} = \exp(-r_t - \frac{1}{2}\lambda_t^\top \lambda_t - \lambda_t^\top \Sigma_v^{-1/2} v_{t+1})\). The pricing equation \(\mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1} \exp(rx_{t+1}^{(n)} + r_t)] = 1\) with log-normal innovations produces the conditional moment condition \(\mathbb{E}_t[rx_{t+1}^{(n)}] + \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Var}_t(rx_{t+1}^{(n)}) = \operatorname{Cov}_t(rx_{t+1}^{(n)}, -m_{t+1}) = \beta_n^\top (\lambda_0 + \lambda_1 X_t)\). Equating coefficients on \(X_t\) and constants yields the linear Step 3 system. \(\blacksquare\)

2Comparison with Kalman-Filter DTSMs

Before ACM, the standard approaches to DTSMs (e.g., Dai & Singleton 2000; Kim & Wright 2005) used state-space Kalman filtering with Quasi-Maximum Likelihood (QMLE). While QMLE handles unobservable latent states and missing data gracefully, the likelihood surface is notorious for multiple local maxima, flat ridges in the likelihood function, and severe sensitivity to starting values. The ACM estimator executes in milliseconds, converges deterministically, and easily scales to 5 or more state factors.

3Persistence Bias & Robustness

Despite its computational elegance, Bauer and Rudebusch (2017) demonstrate that Step 1 OLS estimation of \(\Phi\) suffers from downward Hurwicz persistence bias. When \(\Phi\) is underestimated, physical rate forecasts \(\mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{P}[r_{t+i}]\) revert to the mean too quickly, causing the model-based term premium \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}\) to absorb excessive business-cycle variation.

Lab 1 · Adrian-Crump-Moench (ACM) linear regression term premium model

Under the ACM (2013) 3-step linear regression framework, observed bond yields decompose additively into the model-implied average expected short rate and the model-based term premium, satisfying the exact identity \(y_t^{(n)} = \bar{r}_t^{(n)} + \mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}\) for all maturities.

"""Lab FI3-L06-1: Adrian-Crump-Moench (ACM) linear regression term premium model.

SIMULATED: estimates the Adrian, Crump & Moench (2013) 3-step linear regression
affine term structure model and extracts model-based term premia.
Standard library and NumPy only; no network.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import Any

import numpy as np


def estimate_acm(yields: np.ndarray, maturities: np.ndarray, num_factors: int = 3) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """Estimate ACM (2013) affine model via 3 OLS steps."""
    t_obs, k_mat = yields.shape

    # Step 1: PCA factors & VAR(1)
    y_mean = np.mean(yields, axis=0)
    y_c = yields - y_mean
    _, _, vt = np.linalg.svd(y_c, full_matrices=False)
    factors = y_c @ vt[:num_factors, :].T

    x_lag = np.column_stack([np.ones(t_obs - 1), factors[:-1]])
    x_lead = factors[1:]
    b_var, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(x_lag, x_lead, rcond=None)
    mu = b_var[0, :]
    phi = b_var[1:, :].T
    v_res = x_lead - x_lag @ b_var
    sigma_v = (v_res.T @ v_res) / float(len(v_res))

    # Step 2: Excess return regressions
    rx_mat = np.empty((t_obs - 1, k_mat - 1), dtype=np.float64)
    y1 = yields[:-1, 0]
    for k in range(1, k_mat):
        n = maturities[k]
        rx_mat[:, k - 1] = -(n - 1.0) * yields[1:, k - 1] + n * yields[:-1, k] - y1

    z_reg = np.column_stack([np.ones(t_obs - 1), v_res, factors[:-1]])
    coeffs, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(z_reg, rx_mat, rcond=None)
    alpha = coeffs[0, :]
    beta = coeffs[1 : 1 + num_factors, :].T
    c_mat = coeffs[1 + num_factors :, :].T
    res_e = rx_mat - z_reg @ coeffs
    sigma_e_sq = np.var(res_e, axis=0)

    # Step 3: Cross-sectional risk prices
    lambda1, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(beta, c_mat, rcond=None)
    alpha_adj = alpha + 0.5 * (np.sum((beta @ sigma_v) * beta, axis=1) + sigma_e_sq)
    lambda0, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(beta, alpha_adj, rcond=None)

    # Step 4: Expected short rates and term premium
    short_rate = yields[:, 0]
    w_sr = np.column_stack([np.ones(t_obs), factors])
    b_sr, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(w_sr, short_rate, rcond=None)
    delta0 = b_sr[0]
    delta1 = b_sr[1:]

    expected_short_rate = np.empty((t_obs, k_mat), dtype=np.float64)
    term_premium = np.empty((t_obs, k_mat), dtype=np.float64)

    for k in range(k_mat):
        n_steps = max(1, round(float(maturities[k])))
        e_factors = factors.copy()
        sum_r = delta0 + e_factors @ delta1
        for _ in range(1, n_steps):
            e_factors = mu + e_factors @ phi.T
            sum_r += delta0 + e_factors @ delta1
        avg_r = sum_r / float(n_steps)
        expected_short_rate[:, k] = avg_r
        term_premium[:, k] = yields[:, k] - avg_r

    return {
        "term_premium": term_premium,
        "expected_short_rate": expected_short_rate,
        "fitted_yields": yields.copy(),
        "phi": phi,
        "lambda1": lambda1,
        "lambda0": lambda0,
    }


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2013)
    t_obs = 100
    maturities = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 10.0], dtype=np.float64)

    # Persistent 3-factor yield dynamics (Level, Slope, Curvature)
    phi_diag = np.array([0.96, 0.84, 0.72])
    f = np.zeros((t_obs, 3), dtype=np.float64)
    f[0] = [0.045, -0.015, 0.005]
    for t in range(t_obs - 1):
        f[t + 1] = f[0] * (1.0 - phi_diag) + phi_diag * f[t] + rng.normal(0.0, [0.004, 0.005, 0.004])

    yields = np.zeros((t_obs, 5), dtype=np.float64)
    for i, m in enumerate(maturities):
        b = np.array([1.0, np.exp(-m / 3.0), (m / 3.0) * np.exp(-m / 3.0)])
        yields[:, i] = 0.015 + 0.004 * np.log(m) + f @ b

    acm = estimate_acm(yields, maturities, num_factors=3)
    tp = acm["term_premium"]
    esr = acm["expected_short_rate"]
    phi = acm["phi"]

    print("ACM (2013) Term-Structure Model Estimates:")
    print(f"  VAR(1) Phi matrix eigenvalues: {np.linalg.eigvals(phi).round(4)}")

    print("\nTerm Premium Decomposition at Final Observation (t = T):")
    for i, m in enumerate(maturities):
        print(
            f"  Maturity {m:4.1f}y: Yield = {yields[-1, i]:.4%}, "
            f"ExpShortRate = {esr[-1, i]:.4%}, "
            f"TermPremium = {tp[-1, i]:.4%} ({tp[-1, i] * 10000:6.1f} bps)"
        )

    # Verify articulation: term_premium + expected_short_rate == yields
    max_err = float(np.max(np.abs((tp + esr) - yields)))
    print(f"\nMaximum decomposition error |TP + ESR - Yield|: {max_err:.2e}")
    print(f"Exact articulation holds (error < 1e-12): {max_err < 1e-12}")

Where the canon breaks

Textbook affine models frequently presume that estimating models via OLS linear regressions produces unbiased physical term premia. In practice, the canon breaks:

  • Eigenvalue Attenuation Bias: Downward Hurwicz bias in VAR transition matrix \(\Phi\) dampens the persistence of state variables, causing expectations \(\mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+i}]\) to mean-revert too rapidly.
  • Zero Lower Bound Violations: Standard Gaussian affine models allow positive probability of nominal yields plunging deeply negative, requiring shadow-rate extensions (e.g., Black 1995; Krippner 2013) during near-zero policy rate regimes.

Misconceptions

  1. Dynamic term structure models require non-linear numerical optimization to enforce no-arbitrage conditions.

    The Adrian, Crump, and Moench (2013) framework proves that no-arbitrage restrictions can be mapped analytically into linear cross-sectional regressions, eliminating non-linear optimization entirely.

  2. Model-based term premia and survey-based term premia always move in lockstep.

    Surveys reflect professional consensus forecasts subject to information stickiness and cognitive heuristics, whereas model-based DTSMs filter expectations through no-arbitrage pricing kernels and historical VAR dynamics.

  3. The 1-period excess return regressions in ACM (Step 2) violate no-arbitrage because they are unconstrained.

    Step 2 runs unconstrained regressions to estimate factor exposures and conditional expected returns; no-arbitrage restrictions are then formally imposed in Step 3 via the cross-sectional market price of risk regressions.

Problem set

Mechanics

In ACM Step 1, the estimated VAR(1) persistence matrix for a 2-factor model is \(\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} 0.95 & 0.02 \\ -0.01 & 0.88 \end{pmatrix}\) with zero mean. If current state is \(X_t = [1.5, -0.5]^\top\), compute the forecasted state vector \(\mathbb{E}_t[X_{t+2}]\).

Solution

First compute \(\mathbb{E}_t[X_{t+1}] = \Phi X_t = \begin{pmatrix} 0.95(1.5) + 0.02(-0.5) \\ -0.01(1.5) + 0.88(-0.5) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.425 - 0.010 \\ -0.015 - 0.440 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.415 \\ -0.455 \end{pmatrix}\). Next compute \(\mathbb{E}_t[X_{t+2}] = \Phi \mathbb{E}_t[X_{t+1}] = \begin{pmatrix} 0.95(1.415) + 0.02(-0.455) \\ -0.01(1.415) + 0.88(-0.455) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.34425 - 0.0091 \\ -0.01415 - 0.4004 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.33515 \\ -0.41455 \end{pmatrix}\). This provides the two-step ahead conditional expectation.

Suppose the 10-year observed yield is \(y_t^{(10)} = 4.85\%\) and the ACM model produces an average expected short rate over the 10-year horizon of \(\bar{r}_t^{(10)} = 3.65\%\). Calculate the model-based 10-year term premium in basis points.

Solution

Under the additive yield decomposition identity, the model-based term premium is simply the difference: \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(10)} = y_t^{(10)} - \bar{r}_t^{(10)} = 4.85\% - 3.65\% = 1.20\% = 120\text{ bps}\). This positive premium compensates investors for duration risk.

In ACM Step 3, the cross-sectional equation is \(c_n = \beta_n^\top \lambda_1\). If for maturity \(n\), factor loadings are \(\beta_n = [1.2, -0.4]^\top\) and the risk price matrix is \(\lambda_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.05 & -0.02 \\ 0.01 & 0.04 \end{pmatrix}\), compute the vector \(c_n^\top\).

Solution

Compute \(c_n^\top = \beta_n^\top \lambda_1 = [1.2, -0.4] \begin{pmatrix} 0.05 & -0.02 \\ 0.01 & 0.04 \end{pmatrix} = [1.2(0.05) + (-0.4)(0.01), 1.2(-0.02) + (-0.4)(0.04)] = [0.06 - 0.004, -0.024 - 0.016] = [0.056, -0.040]\). This determines how expected excess returns load on the state vector.

Proof

Prove that under the ACM affine specification, the expected short rate \(\bar{r}_t^{(n)}\) is an affine function of the current state vector: \(\bar{r}_t^{(n)} = \bar{\delta}_{0, n} + \bar{\delta}_{1, n}^\top X_t\).

Solution

The short rate is \(r_t = \delta_0 + \delta_1^\top X_t\). Under the physical VAR(1), \(\mathbb{E}_t[X_{t+i}] = \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} \Phi^j \mu + \Phi^i X_t\). Substituting this into the short-rate formula gives \(\mathbb{E}_t[r_{t+i}] = \delta_0 + \delta_1^\top (\sum_{j=0}^{i-1} \Phi^j \mu) + (\delta_1^\top \Phi^i) X_t\). Summing from \(i=0\) to \(n-1\) and dividing by \(n\): \(\bar{r}_t^{(n)} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} [\delta_0 + \delta_1^\top (\sum_{j=0}^{i-1} \Phi^j \mu)] + \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \delta_1^\top \Phi^i\right) X_t\). Defining \(\bar{\delta}_{0, n}\) as the constant term and \(\bar{\delta}_{1, n}^\top \equiv \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \delta_1^\top \Phi^i\) establishes the affine relationship. \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that the risk-neutral state transition matrix \(\Phi^\mathbb{Q}\) in the ACM model is given by \(\Phi^\mathbb{Q} = \Phi - \Sigma_v \lambda_1\).

Solution

By Girsanov's theorem for discrete Gaussian systems, the risk-neutral shock innovation satisfies \(v_{t+1}^\mathbb{Q} = v_{t+1} + \Sigma_v \lambda_t\). Substituting \(v_{t+1} = X_{t+1} - \mu - \Phi X_t\) gives \(X_{t+1} = \mu + \Phi X_t - \Sigma_v (\lambda_0 + \lambda_1 X_t) + v_{t+1}^\mathbb{Q} = (\mu - \Sigma_v \lambda_0) + (\Phi - \Sigma_v \lambda_1) X_t + v_{t+1}^\mathbb{Q}\). Therefore, the risk-neutral autoregressive slope matrix is \(\Phi^\mathbb{Q} \equiv \Phi - \Sigma_v \lambda_1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Prove that if \(\lambda_1 = 0\), the term premium \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}\) is constant over time.

Solution

When \(\lambda_1 = 0\), the market price of risk \(\lambda_t = \lambda_0\) is constant. Consequently, the difference between \(\Phi^\mathbb{Q}\) and \(\Phi^\mathbb{P}\) vanishes: \(\Phi^\mathbb{Q} = \Phi\). Thus, expected rate changes under \(\mathbb{Q}\) and \(\mathbb{P}\) are identical across states, causing \(\mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{Q}[r_{t+i}] - \mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{P}[r_{t+i}]\) to depend only on constant risk prices \(\lambda_0\) and volatility adjustments. Hence \(\mathrm{TP}_t^{(n)}\) is completely independent of \(X_t\), rendering it constant over time. \(\blacksquare\)

Empirical

Why do Adrian, Crump, and Moench (2013) choose 5 principal components of yields rather than 3 in their benchmark empirical implementation?

Solution

While 3 principal components (level, slope, curvature) capture 99% of the cross-sectional variance of yields, Cochrane and Piazzesi (2005) demonstrated that the 4th and 5th principal components contain critical information for forecasting bond risk premia. Retaining 5 factors allows the model to capture high-order curvature and tent-shaped predictability without sacrificing computational tractability.

How did the 10-year ACM term premium evolve during the Federal Reserve's Quantitative Easing (QE) programs between 2008 and 2014?

Solution

Federal Reserve ACM estimates indicate that the 10-year term premium declined by approximately 100 to 150 basis points over the successive QE programs (QE1, QE2, QE3). By mid-2014, the 10-year term premium entered negative territory for the first time in modern records, as massive central bank asset purchases absorbed duration risk from the private market.

According to Bauer and Rudebusch (2017), what is the quantitative impact of bias correction on the estimated persistence of the short rate in US Treasury data?

Solution

Bauer and Rudebusch show that OLS estimates the largest eigenvalue of \(\Phi\) at approximately 0.95 to 0.97 in monthly data, whereas bias-corrected estimators (such as median-unbiased and indirect inference) place the largest eigenvalue at 0.99 or higher. This shift substantially increases the multi-year persistence of interest rate forecasts.

Research

Evaluate the trade-offs between linear regression DTSMs (ACM) and shadow-rate Gaussian DTSMs (e.g., Krippner 2013) near the zero lower bound.

Solution

Linear ACM models have closed-form estimators and instant convergence, but their Gaussian state variables assign positive probability to negative nominal short rates, generating distortions when policy rates are constrained at zero. Shadow-rate models enforce the call option nature of currency \(r_t = \max(s_t, 0)\), preserving the zero lower bound at the cost of non-linear numerical filtering and loss of closed-form regression estimators.

How does incorporating survey expectations directly into the estimation of DTSMs (e.g., Kim and Orphanides 2012) resolve the persistence bias identified by Bauer and Rudebusch (2017)?

Solution

Survey forecasts of short rates provide direct, model-free observations of \(\mathbb{E}_t^\mathbb{P}[r_{t+i}]\). Including survey forecasts in the state-space observation vector anchors the long-horizon mean-reversion level and prevents the econometric filter from relying exclusively on noisy, downward-biased OLS time-series regressions to identify physical persistence.

Formulate an arbitrage-free curve-trading strategy that exploits cross-sectional pricing residuals in the ACM Step 3 fit.

Solution

Compute daily fitting residuals \(e_t^{(n)} = y_t^{(n)} - \hat{y}_t^{(n)}(\Theta)\) across 2-, 3-, 5-, 7-, and 10-year tenors. When a bond's yield is anomalously cheap relative to the model-implied no-arbitrage curve (\(e_t^{(n)} > 2\sigma_e\)), buy the undervalued bond and sell duration-matched surrounding liquid benchmark bonds. As fitting errors mean-revert to zero, the trade captures mispricing alpha isolated from macro factor risk.
