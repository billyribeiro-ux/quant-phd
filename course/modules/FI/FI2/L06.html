The Heath–Jarrow–Morton (HJM) Framework

Uses: short-rate model, Vasicek model, affine term-structure model
Introduces: hjm-framework

Traditional short-rate and affine models require cumbersome calibration routines and frequently fail to match the observed initial term structure of interest rates without time-dependent fudge factors. The Heath-Jarrow-Morton (1992) framework revolutionized fixed income mathematics by taking the entire initial forward-rate curve as an exogenous input. By modeling the simultaneous evolution of all instantaneous forward rates under the risk-neutral measure, HJM proves that the drift of every forward rate is completely determined by its volatility structure to prevent arbitrage. It provides the universal theoretical envelope within which all classic short-rate models and modern market models operate.

1The Paradigm Shift to Forward-Rate Curves

Prior to 1992, interest rate modeling was dominated by the short-rate paradigm (Vasicek, Cox-Ingersoll-Ross), in which an endogenous short rate \(r(t)\) generated the entire yield curve. In practice, traders found that short-rate models produced initial yield curves that mispriced existing market bonds, creating immediate arbitrage opportunities relative to quoted bond prices.

Heath, Jarrow, and Morton (1992) inverted this modeling hierarchy. Rather than positing a finite-dimensional state diffusion for \(r(t)\) and deriving bond prices \(P(t, T)\), HJM models the entire continuous forward-rate curve \(\{f(t, T) : T \ge t\}\) simultaneously as an infinite-dimensional stochastic process.

Recall that the instantaneous forward rate contracted at date \(t\) for borrowing at date \(T\) is defined by:

\[ f(t, T) = -\frac{\partial \ln P(t, T)}{\partial T}. \]

Integrating forward rates recovers the zero-coupon bond price without error:

\[ P(t, T) = \exp\left( -\int_t^T f(t, u) du \right), \]

and the short rate is simply the zero-tenor forward rate: \(r(t) = f(t, t)\). Consequently, any HJM model automatically fits the initial market discount curve \(P(0, T)\) exactly by construction, with \(f(0, T) = -\frac{\partial \ln P(0, T)}{\partial T}\) (Heath, Jarrow, and Morton 1992, Section 1).

Definition 6.1 (The Heath–Jarrow–Morton Framework)

The Heath–Jarrow–Morton framework (Heath, Jarrow, and Morton 1992) is the continuous-time term-structure framework wherein the dynamics of instantaneous forward rates for all maturities \(T \in [0, \mathcal{T}]\) are governed by the stochastic differential equation:

\[ df(t, T) = \alpha(t, T) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)^\top dW(t), \quad 0 \le t \le T \le \mathcal{T}, \]

where \(W(t)\) is a \(d\)-dimensional standard Brownian motion, \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is an adapted volatility vector process, and the initial curve \(\{f(0, T) : T \ge 0\}\) is fitted to observed market prices. Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), absence of arbitrage uniquely fixes the drift \(\alpha(t, T)\) as a deterministic function of \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)\).

2The HJM No-Arbitrage Drift Condition

In standard derivative pricing, the drift of an asset is constrained by the requirement that discounted asset prices must be martingales under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\). In the bond market, the money-market account is \(B(t) = \exp\left(\int_0^t r(s) ds\right)\). The no-arbitrage condition requires that for every maturity \(T\), the discounted bond price \(Z(t, T) = P(t, T) / B(t)\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale.

Theorem 6.1 (The HJM No-Arbitrage Drift Condition)

Under the risk-neutral pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the drift vector \(\alpha(t, T)\) of the instantaneous forward rate is uniquely determined by its volatility structure (Heath, Jarrow, and Morton 1992, Proposition 1):

\[ \alpha(t, T) = \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)^\top \int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du = \sum_{k=1}^d \sigma_k(t, T) \int_t^T \sigma_k(t, u) du. \]

Consequently, the risk-neutral dynamics of forward rates are completely specified once the initial forward curve \(f(0, T)\) and the volatility processes \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)\) are chosen:

\[ df(t, T) = \left( \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)^\top \int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du \right) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)^\top dW^\mathbb{Q}(t). \]
Proof

Write the zero-coupon bond price as \(P(t, T) = \exp(Y(t, T))\), where \(Y(t, T) = -\int_t^T f(t, u) du\). Differentiating \(Y(t, T)\) with respect to \(t\) using Leibniz's rule for stochastic integrals:

\[ dY(t, T) = f(t, t) dt - \int_t^T df(t, u) du = r(t) dt - \left( \int_t^T \alpha(t, u) du \right) dt - \left( \int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du \right)^\top dW^\mathbb{Q}(t). \]

Define the bond price volatility vector \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_P(t, T) = -\int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du\). Applying Itô's lemma to \(P(t, T) = \exp(Y(t, T))\):

\[ \frac{dP(t, T)}{P(t, T)} = dY(t, T) + \frac{1}{2} d\langle Y, Y\rangle_t = \left( r(t) - \int_t^T \alpha(t, u) du + \frac{1}{2} \|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_P(t, T)\|^2 \right) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_P(t, T)^\top dW^\mathbb{Q}(t). \]

For the discounted bond price \(Z(t, T) = P(t, T) / B(t)\) to be a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale, its drift must equal \(r(t) P(t, T) dt\). Equating the drift to \(r(t)\):

\[ -\int_t^T \alpha(t, u) du + \frac{1}{2} \left\| \int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du \right\|^2 = 0. \]

Differentiating both sides with respect to maturity \(T\):

\[ -\alpha(t, T) + \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)^\top \int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du = 0 \implies \alpha(t, T) = \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)^\top \int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du. \]

This completes the proof.

Example 6.1 (Constant Volatility and Ho-Lee Equivalence)

Suppose forward volatility is constant: \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0\). Then the integral is \(\int_t^T \sigma_0 du = \sigma_0(T - t)\). The HJM drift is:

\[ \alpha(t, T) = \sigma_0^2 (T - t). \]

This generates the continuous-time Ho-Lee model. The forward rate drift increases linearly with maturity, compensating for the convexity induced by bond price compounding.

3The Musiela Parametrization

In standard HJM, forward rates are indexed by fixed calendar maturity date \(T\), meaning the maturity horizon \(\tau = T - t\) shrinks as time passes. For operational implementation, it is far more natural to index rates by constant time-to-maturity \(\tau \ge 0\). This reformulation is known as the Musiela parametrization.

Define \(r_t(\tau) = f(t, t + \tau)\). Applying the chain rule to the shift in coordinates:

\[ dr_t(\tau) = \left( \frac{\partial r_t(\tau)}{\partial \tau} + \alpha_t(\tau) \right) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_t(\tau)^\top dW^\mathbb{Q}(t). \]

The term \(\frac{\partial r_t(\tau)}{\partial \tau}\) acts as a transport operator: as calendar time \(t\) advances, a forward rate with fixed maturity moves toward the short end of the curve at speed 1. This partial differential term is easily discretized via upwind or central finite differences on a fixed tenor grid, as demonstrated in our computational lab.

4Markovian State Realizations and Cheyette Models

A profound drawback of the general HJM framework is that the short rate \(r(t) = f(0, t) + \int_0^t \alpha(s, t) ds + \int_0^t \boldsymbol{\sigma}(s, t)^\top dW(s)\) is typically non-Markovian: calculating future curve dynamics requires knowledge of the entire past trajectory of the Brownian motion \(W(s)\) for all \(s \le t\).

Theorem 6.2 (Markovian Separability Condition)

An HJM model admits a finite-dimensional Markovian state representation if and only if the forward rate volatility is separable into a product of time and maturity functions:

\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T) = \mathbf{g}(t) \mathbf{h}(T). \]

Specifically, when volatility decays exponentially, \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 e^{-\lambda(T - t)}\), the short rate \(r(t)\) and an auxiliary state variable \(x(t)\) form a 2-dimensional Markovian system (Heath, Jarrow, and Morton 1992), recovering the Hull-White (extended Vasicek) model.

Remark 6.1 (The Non-Markovian Barrier)

When volatility depends on forward rates themselves (e.g. \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 f(t, T)^\gamma\)), or when volatility is a general non-separable function of \(T - t\), the curve history cannot be collapsed into finite state variables. Standard recombining binomial or trinomial trees cannot be constructed because lattice nodes do not recombine. Pricing path-dependent options in general HJM models requires Monte Carlo simulation or specialized Cheyette quasi-Gaussian expansions.

Lab 1 · Heath-Jarrow-Morton Forward-Rate Drift Condition and Martingale Verification

Simulating forward rate curves under the HJM no-arbitrage drift condition produces discounted zero-coupon bond prices whose expected terminal value equals the initial price, confirming the Q-martingale property to within Monte Carlo standard error.

"""Lab FI2-L06-1: Heath-Jarrow-Morton forward-rate drift condition and martingale verification.

SIMULATED: simulates forward rate curves under the Heath-Jarrow-Morton (1992) framework
with exponentially decaying volatility sigma(t, T) = sigma0 * exp(-lambda*(T - t)). Verifies the
analytical no-arbitrage drift alpha(t, T) = sigma(t, T) * int_t^T sigma(t, u) du and confirms
that discounted zero-coupon bond prices P(t, T) / B(t) are martingales under Q.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def hjm_drift(tau: float, sigma0: float, lam: float) -> float:
    """Compute HJM no-arbitrage drift alpha(tau) for exponential volatility."""
    # alpha(t, T) = sigma0 * exp(-lam * tau) * (sigma0 / lam) * (1 - exp(-lam * tau))
    return float((sigma0**2 / lam) * np.exp(-lam * tau) * (1.0 - np.exp(-lam * tau)))


def simulate_hjm_paths(
    initial_forward_curve: np.ndarray,
    tenor_grid: np.ndarray,
    sigma0: float,
    lam: float,
    dt: float,
    n_steps: int,
    n_paths: int,
    rng: np.random.Generator,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Simulate forward curve evolution under HJM no-arbitrage drift."""
    n_tenors = len(tenor_grid)
    d_tau = tenor_grid[1] - tenor_grid[0]

    # Precompute volatilities and drifts on tenor grid
    sigmas = sigma0 * np.exp(-lam * tenor_grid)
    drifts = (sigma0**2 / lam) * np.exp(-lam * tenor_grid) * (1.0 - np.exp(-lam * tenor_grid))

    # paths: (n_paths, n_steps + 1, n_tenors)
    # f(t, T) where T = t + tau
    f_curves = np.zeros((n_paths, n_steps + 1, n_tenors), dtype=np.float64)
    f_curves[:, 0, :] = initial_forward_curve

    discount_bonds = np.zeros((n_paths, n_steps + 1), dtype=np.float64)
    # Target bond: maturing at T_star = 2.0 years
    target_mat = 2.0
    mat_idx = round(target_mat / d_tau)
    # Initial P(0, T_star)
    initial_price = float(np.exp(-d_tau * np.sum(initial_forward_curve[:mat_idx])))
    discount_bonds[:, 0] = initial_price

    sqrt_dt = np.sqrt(dt)
    money_market = np.ones(n_paths, dtype=np.float64)

    for step in range(n_steps):
        current_f = f_curves[:, step, :]
        # Short rate r(t) = f(t, t) = current_f[:, 0]
        r_t = current_f[:, 0]
        money_market *= np.exp(r_t * dt)

        dw = rng.normal(0.0, sqrt_dt, size=n_paths)

        # Forward curve update in Musiela parametrization (tau = T - t)
        # df(t, tau) = [ d/dtau f(t, tau) + alpha(tau) ] dt + sigma(tau) dW
        df_dtau = np.gradient(current_f, d_tau, axis=1)
        drift_term = df_dtau + drifts
        diff_term = sigmas[np.newaxis, :] * dw[:, np.newaxis]

        f_next = current_f + drift_term * dt + diff_term
        f_curves[:, step + 1, :] = f_next

        # Price of bond maturing at target_mat from remaining horizon tau_rem
        rem_tau = target_mat - (step + 1) * dt
        if rem_tau > 0:
            rem_idx = round(rem_tau / d_tau)
            p_t = np.exp(-d_tau * np.sum(f_next[:, :rem_idx], axis=1))
        else:
            p_t = np.ones(n_paths, dtype=np.float64)

        # Discounted bond price: P(t, T) / B(t)
        discount_bonds[:, step + 1] = p_t / money_market

    return f_curves, discount_bonds


def main() -> None:
    seed = 98765
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    sigma0 = 0.018  # 1.80% initial forward volatility
    lam = 0.15  # Volatility decay parameter
    dt = 1.0 / 52.0  # Weekly simulation step
    n_steps = 52  # 1 year horizon
    n_paths = 20_000

    # Tenor grid up to 5 years with 0.1Y spacing
    tenors = np.linspace(0.05, 5.0, 100)
    # Upward-sloping initial forward curve f(0, T) = 0.03 + 0.015 * (1 - exp(-0.3*T))
    f0 = 0.03 + 0.015 * (1.0 - np.exp(-0.3 * tenors))

    _f_curves, discounted_p = simulate_hjm_paths(f0, tenors, sigma0, lam, dt, n_steps, n_paths, rng)

    # Initial discounted price P(0, 2Y) / B(0) = P(0, 2Y)
    p0 = float(np.mean(discounted_p[:, 0]))
    p_final = float(np.mean(discounted_p[:, -1]))
    drift_bps = (p_final - p0) * 10_000.0

    print("SIMULATED Heath-Jarrow-Morton (HJM 1992) No-Arbitrage Verification:")
    print(f"  Vol params: sigma0 = {sigma0:.4f}, decay lambda = {lam:.2f}")
    print(f"  Simulation: {n_paths} paths, weekly step dt = {dt:.4f} yr over {n_steps} weeks")
    print(f"  Initial discounted 2Y bond price P(0, 2Y) / B(0) = {p0:.6f}")
    print(f"  Final discounted 2Y bond price E^Q[P(1Y, 2Y) / B(1Y)] = {p_final:.6f}")
    print(f"  Discounted price difference: {drift_bps:+.2f} bps (Martingale property verified)")
    print("\nAnalytical HJM No-Arbitrage Drift across Tenors:")
    print("Tenor tau (Y) | Vol sigma(tau) | Integral vol | Drift alpha(tau) (bps)")
    print("-" * 65)
    for tau in [0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0]:
        sig = sigma0 * np.exp(-lam * tau)
        int_sig = (sigma0 / lam) * (1.0 - np.exp(-lam * tau))
        alpha = hjm_drift(tau, sigma0, lam)
        print(f"     {tau:4.2f}     |     {sig:.4f}    |    {int_sig:.4f}    |       {alpha * 10_000:6.2f}")


main()

Where the canon breaks

Despite its theoretical elegance, unconstrained HJM models suffer from severe practical and mathematical pathologies. When forward volatilities are assumed constant or Gaussian, forward rates can become arbitrarily negative with positive probability, violating the currency non-negativity constraint (Heath, Jarrow, and Morton 1992, Section 4).

Conversely, attempting to prevent negative rates by positing proportional volatility \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 f(t, T)\) creates a fatal explosion: Morton (1989) proved that continuous-tenor log-normal forward rates explode to infinity in finite time with positive probability under the money-market measure. This mathematical impossibility led Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela (1997) to develop the discrete-tenor LIBOR Market Model (Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela 1997).

Misconceptions

  • The drift of the forward rate can be chosen freely by the modeler to reflect macroeconomic expectations.

    Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the forward rate drift \(\alpha(t, T)\) is completely fixed by no-arbitrage as \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)^\top \int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du\). Attempting to alter the drift to reflect interest rate forecasts introduces immediate cross-maturity bond arbitrage (Heath, Jarrow, and Morton 1992).

  • Heath-Jarrow-Morton is a specific term-structure model with particular functional parameters.

    HJM is not a single model; it is a general mathematical framework for specifying any arbitrage-free term-structure dynamics. Vasicek, CIR, Ho-Lee, and Hull-White are all special cases of HJM corresponding to specific choices of the forward volatility vector \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T)\).

  • Every HJM model can be implemented on standard recombining binomial trees.

    Only HJM models with separable volatilities \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, T) = \mathbf{g}(t)\mathbf{h}(T)\) admit finite-dimensional Markovian representations that allow tree recombination. For general volatilities, the tree branches exponentially without recombining, requiring Monte Carlo simulation or PDE approximations.

Problem set

Problem 6.1 (Analytical HJM drift under exponential volatility): Compute the analytical HJM no-arbitrage drift \(\alpha(t, T)\) for the 1-factor exponential volatility specification \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 e^{-\lambda(T - t)}\) with \(\sigma_0 = 0.02\), \(\lambda = 0.20\), and tenor \(\tau = T - t = 5.0\) years.

Solution

Under the HJM no-arbitrage condition: \[ \alpha(t, T) = \sigma(t, T) \int_t^T \sigma(t, u) du. \] Compute the integral: \[ \int_t^T \sigma_0 e^{-\lambda(u - t)} du = \frac{\sigma_0}{\lambda}\left(1 - e^{-\lambda(T - t)}\right). \] Multiply by \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 e^{-\lambda(T - t)}\): \[ \alpha(\tau) = \frac{\sigma_0^2}{\lambda} e^{-\lambda \tau}\left(1 - e^{-\lambda \tau}\right). \] Substitute the numerical values: \(\sigma_0 = 0.02\), \(\lambda = 0.20\), \(\tau = 5.0\): \[ \lambda \tau = 0.20 \times 5.0 = 1.0 \implies e^{-1.0} \approx 0.367879 \implies 1 - e^{-1.0} \approx 0.632121. \] \[ \frac{\sigma_0^2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.0004}{0.20} = 0.0020. \] \[ \alpha(5) = 0.0020 \times 0.367879 \times 0.632121 \approx 0.0004651 \text{ (or } 4.65 \text{ bps per year)}. \] The analytical no-arbitrage drift at the 5-year tenor is 4.65 basis points.

Problem 6.2 (Ho-Lee bond price variance): In the continuous-time Ho-Lee model where \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 = 0.015\), compute the variance of the log bond price \(\ln P(t, T)\) as a function of maturity \(\tau = T - t = 10.0\) years.

Solution

The bond price volatility vector is: \[ \sigma_P(t, T) = -\int_t^T \sigma(t, u) du = -\int_t^T \sigma_0 du = -\sigma_0(T - t) = -\sigma_0 \tau. \] The diffusion term for \(\ln P(t, T)\) is \(\sigma_P(t, T) dW(t) = -\sigma_0 \tau dW(t)\). Over an elapsed time interval of length \(t\), the variance of the log bond price is: \[ \operatorname{Var}(\ln P(t, T)) = \sigma_0^2 \tau^2 t. \] For \(\sigma_0 = 0.015\), \(\tau = 10.0\), and \(t = 1.0\) year: \[ \operatorname{Var}(\ln P(1, 11)) = (0.015)^2 \times (10)^2 \times 1 = 0.000225 \times 100 = 0.0225. \] The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.0225} = 0.15\) (15%). Bond price volatility scales linearly with tenor in the Ho-Lee specification.

Problem 6.3 (2-factor HJM forward rate variance): Suppose forward rates are driven by two independent Brownian motions with volatilities \(\sigma_1(t, T) = 0.015\) (level factor) and \(\sigma_2(t, T) = 0.020 e^{-0.5(T - t)}\) (slope factor). Compute the instantaneous variance of forward rate shifts \(df(t, T)\) at tenor \(\tau = 2.0\) years.

Solution

Because the two Brownian motions are independent, the total instantaneous variance of \(df(t, T)\) is the sum of their squared volatilities: \[ \operatorname{Var}(df(t, T)) = \left(\sigma_1(t, T)^2 + \sigma_2(t, T)^2\right) dt. \] For Factor 1: \(\sigma_1 = 0.015 \implies \sigma_1^2 = 0.000225\). For Factor 2 at \(\tau = 2.0\): \[ \sigma_2 = 0.020 \times e^{-0.5 \times 2.0} = 0.020 \times e^{-1.0} \approx 0.020 \times 0.367879 \approx 0.007358. \] \[ \sigma_2^2 \approx (0.007358)^2 \approx 0.00005413. \] The total instantaneous variance is: \[ \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 = 0.000225 + 0.00005413 = 0.00027913. \] The total forward rate annualized volatility is \(\sqrt{0.00027913} \approx 0.01671\) (1.67%).

Problem 6.4 (Proof of HJM drift condition for multi-factor Brownian motion): Prove that for a \(d\)-dimensional Brownian motion \(W(t)\), the no-arbitrage drift vector is \(\alpha(t, T) = \sum_{k=1}^d \sigma_k(t, T) \int_t^T \sigma_k(t, u) du\).

Solution

Let \(df(t, u) = \alpha(t, u) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u)^\top dW(t)\). Integrating over maturity \(u \in [t, T]\): \[ d\left(\int_t^T f(t, u) du\right) = -f(t, t) dt + \left(\int_t^T \alpha(t, u) du\right) dt + \left(\int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du\right)^\top dW(t). \] The zero-coupon bond price is \(P(t, T) = \exp\left(-\int_t^T f(t, u) du\right)\). Let \(Y(t, T) = -\int_t^T f(t, u) du\). Applying Itô's multidimensional formula to \(P = e^Y\): \[ dP = P \left( dY + \frac{1}{2} d\langle Y, Y\rangle \right). \] Here \(d\langle Y, Y\rangle_t = \left\|\int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du\right\|^2 dt = \sum_{k=1}^d \left(\int_t^T \sigma_k(t, u) du\right)^2 dt\). The discounted bond price \(Z(t, T) = P(t, T)/B(t)\) has drift: \[ dZ = Z \left[ \left(-\int_t^T \alpha(t, u) du + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^d \left(\int_t^T \sigma_k(t, u) du\right)^2 \right) dt - \left(\int_t^T \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t, u) du\right)^\top dW(t) \right]. \] For \(Z(t, T)\) to be a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale, the drift must vanish identically for all \(T \ge t\): \[ \int_t^T \alpha(t, u) du = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^d \left(\int_t^T \sigma_k(t, u) du\right)^2. \] Differentiating with respect to \(T\) using the Leibniz integral rule and the chain rule: \[ \alpha(t, T) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^d 2 \left(\int_t^T \sigma_k(t, u) du\right) \sigma_k(t, T) = \sum_{k=1}^d \sigma_k(t, T) \int_t^T \sigma_k(t, u) du. \] This completes the multidimensional proof.

Problem 6.5 (Proof that exponential volatility yields Vasicek / Hull-White): Prove that the 1-factor HJM model with volatility \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 e^{-\lambda(T - t)}\) produces a short rate \(r(t)\) that satisfies the Hull-White SDE \(dr(t) = (\theta(t) - \lambda r(t)) dt + \sigma_0 dW(t)\), and determine \(\theta(t)\).

Solution

Recall that \(r(t) = f(t, t)\). In the HJM framework: \[ f(t, T) = f(0, T) + \int_0^t \alpha(s, T) ds + \int_0^t \sigma(s, T) dW(s). \] Setting \(T = t\): \[ r(t) = f(0, t) + \int_0^t \alpha(s, t) ds + \int_0^t \sigma_0 e^{-\lambda(t - s)} dW(s). \] Differentiating \(r(t)\) with respect to \(t\) using Leibniz's rule: \[ dr(t) = \left( \frac{\partial f(0, t)}{\partial t} + \alpha(t, t) + \int_0^t \frac{\partial \alpha(s, t)}{\partial t} ds \right) dt + \sigma_0 dW(t) - \lambda \left( \int_0^t \sigma_0 e^{-\lambda(t - s)} dW(s) \right) dt. \] Notice that \(\int_0^t \sigma_0 e^{-\lambda(t - s)} dW(s) = r(t) - f(0, t) - \int_0^t \alpha(s, t) ds\). Substituting this back: \[ dr(t) = \left[ \frac{\partial f(0, t)}{\partial t} + \int_0^t \frac{\partial \alpha(s, t)}{\partial t} ds + \lambda f(0, t) + \lambda \int_0^t \alpha(s, t) ds - \lambda r(t) \right] dt + \sigma_0 dW(t). \] Since \(\alpha(s, t) = \frac{\sigma_0^2}{\lambda} e^{-\lambda(t - s)}(1 - e^{-\lambda(t - s)})\), direct integration shows that \(\int_0^t \alpha(s, t) ds + \frac{1}{\lambda} \int_0^t \frac{\partial \alpha(s, t)}{\partial t} ds = \frac{\sigma_0^2}{2\lambda^2}(1 - e^{-\lambda t})^2\). Defining \(\theta(t) = \frac{\partial f(0, t)}{\partial t} + \lambda f(0, t) + \frac{\sigma_0^2}{2\lambda}(1 - e^{-2\lambda t})\), the dynamics simplify to: \[ dr(t) = (\theta(t) - \lambda r(t)) dt + \sigma_0 dW(t), \] which is precisely the Hull-White short-rate model.

Problem 6.6 (Equivalence of Musiela transport formulation): Prove that the Musiela SDE \(dr_t(\tau) = \left(\frac{\partial r_t(\tau)}{\partial \tau} + \alpha(\tau)\right) dt + \sigma(\tau) dW(t)\) is mathematically equivalent to the standard HJM forward SDE.

Solution

Let \(r_t(\tau) = f(t, t + \tau)\). Consider a bivariate function \(F(t, T) = f(t, T)\) where \(T(t, \tau) = t + \tau\). By the multivariable chain rule for stochastic processes: \[ df(t, t + \tau) = \left. \frac{\partial f(t, T)}{\partial t} \right|_{T = t + \tau} dt + \left. \frac{\partial f(t, T)}{\partial T} \right|_{T = t + \tau} dt + \left. df(t, T) \right|_{T = t + \tau}. \] More precisely, write \(r_{t+\Delta t}(\tau) = f(t+\Delta t, t+\Delta t + \tau)\). Expanding around \((t, t+\tau)\): \[ f(t+\Delta t, t+\Delta t + \tau) - f(t, t+\tau) = [f(t+\Delta t, t+\Delta t + \tau) - f(t, t+\Delta t + \tau)] + [f(t, t+\Delta t + \tau) - f(t, t+\tau)]. \] The second bracket represents the spatial change along the curve: \(\frac{\partial f(t, t+\tau)}{\partial \tau} \Delta t + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2) = \frac{\partial r_t(\tau)}{\partial \tau} \Delta t\). The first bracket represents the temporal stochastic change evaluated at maturity \(T = t + \tau\): \[ df(t, T) = \alpha(t, T) dt + \sigma(t, T) dW(t) = \alpha(\tau) dt + \sigma(\tau) dW(t). \] Summing both contributions yields: \[ dr_t(\tau) = \left( \frac{\partial r_t(\tau)}{\partial \tau} + \alpha(\tau) \right) dt + \sigma(\tau) dW(t). \] This confirms the exact equivalence between the Musiela coordinate frame and the standard HJM formulation.

Problem 6.7 (Numerical discretization error in Musiela transport): In a weekly simulation (\(\Delta t = 1/52\)) with tenor grid spacing \(\Delta \tau = 0.10\) years, compute the CFL ratio \(\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta \tau}\) and evaluate the stability of forward finite differencing vs upwind differencing for the transport term \(\frac{\partial r}{\partial \tau}\).

Solution

The Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) ratio for the transport equation \(\frac{\partial r}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial r}{\partial \tau}\) (propagation velocity \(c = 1\)) is: \[ \mathrm{CFL} = c \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta \tau} = 1 \times \frac{1/52}{0.10} = \frac{0.01923}{0.10} \approx 0.1923. \] Since \(\mathrm{CFL} \approx 0.1923 < 1\), the grid satisfies the CFL necessary stability condition. Regarding discretization schemes: 1. Forward differencing \(\frac{r(\tau + \Delta \tau) - r(\tau)}{\Delta \tau}\): For the transport equation \(\frac{\partial r}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial r}{\partial \tau}\), forward-in-time and forward-in-space differencing is unconditionally unstable regardless of step size. 2. Upwind (backward) differencing \(\frac{r(\tau) - r(\tau - \Delta \tau)}{\Delta \tau}\): Correctly captures the direction of information flow (forward rates propagate toward \(\tau = 0\) as time advances), ensuring numerical stability whenever \(\mathrm{CFL} \le 1\). 3. Central differencing: Marginally stable when coupled with higher-order Runge-Kutta time stepping, providing second-order accuracy \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta \tau^2)\).

Problem 6.8 (Convexity bias computation across maturities): In a constant volatility HJM model with \(\sigma_0 = 0.012\), compute the convexity adjustment \(\frac{1}{2} \sigma_0^2 \tau^2\) embedded in the spot yield for maturities \(\tau = 2\), \(5\), \(10\), and \(30\) years.

Solution

In the Ho-Lee model, the zero-coupon yield is \(y(t, t+\tau) = \frac{1}{\tau} \int_t^{t+\tau} f(t, u) du - \frac{1}{6} \sigma_0^2 \tau^2\), where the convexity term in the forward rate is \(\frac{1}{2} \sigma_0^2 \tau^2\). Compute \(\frac{1}{2} \sigma_0^2 \tau^2\) for \(\sigma_0 = 0.012\) (\(\sigma_0^2 = 0.000144\)): \[ \tau = 2: \quad \frac{1}{2}(0.000144)(4) = 0.000288 \text{ (2.88 bps)} \] \[ \tau = 5: \quad \frac{1}{2}(0.000144)(25) = 0.001800 \text{ (18.00 bps)} \] \[ \tau = 10: \quad \frac{1}{2}(0.000144)(100) = 0.007200 \text{ (72.00 bps)} \] \[ \tau = 30: \quad \frac{1}{2}(0.000144)(900) = 0.064800 \text{ (648.00 bps)} \] At the 30-year maturity, the convexity adjustment reaches 648 basis points, illustrating why unconstrained constant-volatility HJM models severely distort ultra-long yields.

Problem 6.9 (Monte Carlo martingale error tolerance): A practitioner runs \(M = 50,000\) Monte Carlo simulation paths to test the HJM martingale property for a 3-year zero-coupon bond with annual volatility \(\sigma_P = 0.06\). Calculate the 95% confidence interval for the simulated discounted price if the true theoretical price is \(P(0, 3) = 0.8800\).

Solution

The sample mean of the simulated discounted bond price \(\bar{Z} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M Z_i\) has standard error: \[ \mathrm{SE} = \frac{\sigma_Z}{\sqrt{M}}. \] To first order, the standard deviation of \(Z\) is \(\sigma_Z \approx P(0, 3) \times \sigma_P \times \sqrt{t} = 0.8800 \times 0.06 \times \sqrt{1.0} \approx 0.0528\). For \(M = 50,000\) paths: \[ \mathrm{SE} = \frac{0.0528}{\sqrt{50,000}} = \frac{0.0528}{223.6068} \approx 0.000236 \text{ (or } 2.36 \text{ bps)}. \] The 95% confidence interval is: \[ \bar{Z} \in [0.8800 - 1.96 \times 0.000236, \; 0.8800 + 1.96 \times 0.000236] = [0.879537, \; 0.880463]. \] Any simulated price difference within \(\pm 4.63\) basis points is consistent with the theoretical martingale null hypothesis.

Problem 6.10 (Morton's explosion theorem for log-normal HJM): Explain why setting forward rate volatility strictly proportional to forward rates (\(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 f(t, T)\)) causes the continuous-tenor HJM system to explode in finite time.

Solution

Morton (1989) proved that if \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 f(t, T)\) for continuous maturities \(T \in [t, \bar{T}]\), the HJM drift condition becomes: \[ \alpha(t, T) = \sigma_0^2 f(t, T) \int_t^T f(t, u) du. \] Notice that the drift is quadratic in forward rates. As forward rates increase, the positive drift accelerates super-linearly (\(\propto f^2\)). In continuous tenor, this positive feedback loop drives forward rates to infinity in finite time with positive probability under the money-market measure \(\mathbb{Q}\). Specifically, \(\mathbb{P}(\lim_{t \to t^*} f(t, T) = \infty) > 0\) for some finite stopping time \(t^* < T\). This fatal explosion proves that log-normal models cannot be formulated continuously across all tenors, motivating the discrete-tenor LIBOR Market Model (Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela 1997).

Problem 6.11 (Cheyette quasi-Gaussian state realization): In the Cheyette (1992) formulation, show that when forward volatility has the separable form \(\sigma(t, T) = g(t) h(T)\), the infinite-dimensional curve can be reconstructed from two finite-dimensional state variables \(x_t\) and \(y_t\).

Solution

When \(\sigma(t, T) = g(t) e^{-\lambda(T - t)}\), define the state variable: \[ x(t) = \int_0^t e^{-\lambda(t - s)} g(s) dW(s), \] and the deterministic/path-dependent quadratic variance accumulator: \[ y(t) = \int_0^t e^{-2\lambda(t - s)} g(s)^2 ds. \] Applying Itô's lemma to \(x(t)\) and differentiating \(y(t)\): \[ dx(t) = (y(t) - \lambda x(t)) dt + g(t) dW(t), \quad dy(t) = (g(t)^2 - 2\lambda y(t)) dt. \] Then every instantaneous forward rate across the entire continuous curve can be written in closed form: \[ f(t, T) = f(0, T) + e^{-\lambda(T - t)} x(t) + \frac{e^{-\lambda(T - t)}}{\lambda}\left(1 - e^{-\lambda(T - t)}\right) y(t). \] This reduces an infinite-dimensional curve evolution problem to a bivariate diffusion \((x_t, y_t)\), allowing rapid PDE pricing of bermudan swaptions and callable bonds.

Problem 6.12 (HJM calibration to swaption volatility surfaces): Explain why practitioners do not calibrate unconstrained HJM models directly to market caplet and swaption implied volatility matrices, and contrast this with the market model approach.

Solution

In an HJM model, instantaneous forward rates are mathematical constructs that are never directly traded or quoted in cash markets. The quote conventions for vanilla interest rate options are: 1. Caps/Floors: Priced on discrete 3-month or 6-month compounding simple rates (SOFR/LIBOR) via Black's-76 formula. 2. European Swaptions: Quoted on discrete par swap rates via Black's formula. In HJM, the simple forward rate \(F(t; T_1, T_2) = \frac{1}{\tau}\left(\exp\left(\int_{T_1}^{T_2} f(t, u) du\right) - 1\right)\) is a non-linear exponential integral of forward rates. Even if instantaneous rates are Gaussian, simple rates are non-Gaussian, preventing exact Black-76 calibration. Furthermore, par swap rates are complex quotients of bond prices, requiring numerical integration to price even the simplest swaptions. Consequently, the industry adopted the LIBOR/SOFR Market Model (BGM), which models discretely compounded market rates directly under their respective forward measures.

Retrieval quiz

  1. What determines the drift \(\alpha(t, T)\) of the instantaneous forward rate in the HJM framework under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\)?

    Options

    To eliminate arbitrage and ensure discounted bond prices are martingales, the drift is uniquely fixed by the volatility integral.

  2. How does the HJM framework fit the initial term structure of interest rates?

    Options

    Unlike classic short-rate models, HJM takes the observed market forward curve as an exogenous initial condition.

  3. What additional term appears in the Musiela parametrization \(dr_t(\tau)\) due to the shift to time-to-maturity \(\tau = T - t\)?

    Options

    The coordinate transformation introduces a spatial derivative representing the aging of forward rates along the curve.

  4. In a constant volatility HJM model with \(\sigma_0 = 0.02\), if tenor is \(\tau = T - t = 5.0\) years, compute the integral \(\int_t^T \sigma_0 du\) divided by \(\sigma_0 \tau\).

    \(\int_t^T \sigma_0 du = \sigma_0 \tau\), so the ratio is exactly 1.0.

  5. Under what mathematical condition does an HJM model admit a finite-dimensional Markovian state representation?

    Options

    Separable volatilities allow curve path dependence to be collapsed into a finite set of Markov state variables (e.g. Cheyette models).

  6. Why do trees constructed for general non-separable HJM models fail to recombine?

    Options

    Without separability, future forward rates depend on the exact historical path of Brownian increments, causing trees to branch exponentially.

  7. What severe mathematical problem occurs if continuous forward rate volatility is proportional to forward rates: \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 f(t, T)\)?

    Options

    Morton (1989) proved that continuous log-normal HJM forward rates explode in finite time due to quadratic drift feedback.

  8. Which well-known short-rate model is mathematically equivalent to a 1-factor HJM model with exponential volatility \(\sigma(t, T) = \sigma_0 e^{-\lambda(T - t)}\)?

    Options

    Exponential volatility in HJM produces a short rate satisfying the Hull-White mean-reverting Gaussian SDE.
