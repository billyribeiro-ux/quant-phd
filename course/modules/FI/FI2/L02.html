The Vasicek Model: Gaussian Dynamics & Analytical Yield Curves

Before Oldrich Vasicek published his 1977 seminal paper, term-structure models lacked an endogenous no-arbitrage foundation connecting short-rate dynamics to bond prices. Vasicek introduced mean-reverting Gaussian diffusion to interest rates, delivering the first fully analytical closed-form zero-coupon bond pricing formula and explaining how curve shapes (upward-sloping, inverted, and humped) emerge from market price of risk and duration convexity. It remains the pedagogical cornerstone of quantitative fixed income.

1The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Short-Rate Process

Vasicek modeled the short rate as a continuous-time Gaussian mean-reverting diffusion, known in physics as the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.

Definition 2.1 (Vasicek Short-Rate Model)

In the Vasicek model (Vasicek 1977, Section 3), the instantaneous short rate \(r(t)\) evolves under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\) according to the stochastic differential equation:

\[ dr(t) = \kappa (\theta^\mathbb{P} - r(t)) dt + \sigma dW^\mathbb{P}(t) \]

where:

  • \(\kappa > 0\) is the speed of mean reversion, dictating how rapidly the short rate pulls back toward equilibrium.
  • \(\theta^\mathbb{P}\) is the long-term physical mean of the short rate.
  • \(\sigma > 0\) is the instantaneous short-rate volatility.
  • \(W^\mathbb{P}(t)\) is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion under \(\mathbb{P}\).

Assuming a constant market price of risk \(\lambda\), the dynamics under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) remain an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process:

\[ dr(t) = \kappa (\theta - r(t)) dt + \sigma dW^\mathbb{Q}(t) \]

where \(\theta = \theta^\mathbb{P} - \frac{\lambda \sigma}{\kappa}\) is the risk-adjusted long-term mean under \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Theorem 2.1 (Conditional Distribution of the Vasicek Short Rate)

Given the initial rate \(r(s)\) at time \(s \le t\), the conditional distribution of \(r(t)\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\) is strictly Gaussian:

\[ r(t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \theta + (r(s) - \theta) e^{-\kappa (t - s)}, \; \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} \left(1 - e^{-2\kappa (t - s)}\right) \right) \]
Proof

Consider the integrating factor \(e^{\kappa t}\). Applying Itô's lemma to the product \(Y(t) = e^{\kappa t} r(t)\):

\[ d(e^{\kappa t} r(t)) = \kappa e^{\kappa t} r(t) dt + e^{\kappa t} dr(t) = \kappa e^{\kappa t} r(t) dt + e^{\kappa t} [\kappa(\theta - r(t)) dt + \sigma dW^\mathbb{Q}(t)] = \kappa \theta e^{\kappa t} dt + \sigma e^{\kappa t} dW^\mathbb{Q}(t) \]

Integrating both sides from \(s\) to \(t\):

\[ e^{\kappa t} r(t) - e^{\kappa s} r(s) = \kappa \theta \int_s^t e^{\kappa u} du + \sigma \int_s^t e^{\kappa u} dW^\mathbb{Q}(u) = \theta \left(e^{\kappa t} - e^{\kappa s}\right) + \sigma \int_s^t e^{\kappa u} dW^\mathbb{Q}(u) \]

Multiplying across by \(e^{-\kappa t}\) isolates \(r(t)\):

\[ r(t) = \theta + (r(s) - \theta) e^{-\kappa(t - s)} + \sigma \int_s^t e^{-\kappa(t - u)} dW^\mathbb{Q}(u) \]

Because the Itô integral of a deterministic integrand with respect to Brownian motion is Gaussian with mean zero, \(r(t)\) is normally distributed. Its conditional mean is \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[r(t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = \theta + (r(s) - \theta) e^{-\kappa(t - s)}\). By Itô's isometry, the conditional variance is:

\[ \operatorname{Var}^\mathbb{Q}(r(t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s) = \sigma^2 \int_s^t e^{-2\kappa(t - u)} du = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} \left[ 1 - e^{-2\kappa(t - s)} \right] \]

This completes the proof.

2Zero-Coupon Bond Pricing & Analytical Affine Solution

Because the integral \(\int_t^T r(s) ds\) is a linear transformation of Gaussian Brownian increments, zero-coupon bond prices admit an exact exponential-affine closed form.

Theorem 2.2 (Vasicek Bond Pricing Formula)

In the Vasicek model, the price at time \(t\) of a zero-coupon bond maturing at \(T\) with horizon \(\tau = T - t\) is given by:

\[ P(t, T) = \exp(A(\tau) - B(\tau) r(t)) \]

where the affine coefficients \(A(\tau)\) and \(B(\tau)\) are:

\[ B(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa \tau}}{\kappa} \] \[ A(\tau) = \left(\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\right) (B(\tau) - \tau) - \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa} B(\tau)^2 \]

The continuously compounded spot yield to maturity \(y(t, T) = -\frac{\ln P(t, T)}{\tau}\) is:

\[ y(t, T) = -\frac{A(\tau)}{\tau} + \frac{B(\tau)}{\tau} r(t) \]
Proof

By Theorem 1.1, the bond price \(P(t, T) = F(t, r; T)\) satisfies the term-structure PDE under \(\mathbb{Q}\):

\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial t} + \kappa(\theta - r) \frac{\partial F}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial r^2} - r F = 0, \quad F(T, r; T) = 1 \]

Substitute the exponentially affine ansatz \(F(t, r; T) = \exp(A(\tau) - B(\tau) r)\) with \(\tau = T - t\). The partial derivatives are:

\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial t} = -P \left(A'(\tau) - B'(\tau) r\right), \quad \frac{\partial F}{\partial r} = -B(\tau) P, \quad \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial r^2} = B(\tau)^2 P \]

Dividing by \(P > 0\) and rearranging terms by powers of \(r\):

\[ r \left[ B'(\tau) + \kappa B(\tau) - 1 \right] + \left[ -A'(\tau) - \kappa \theta B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 B(\tau)^2 \right] = 0 \]

Because this equation must hold for all \(r \in \mathbb{R}\), both bracketed expressions must vanish independently:

  1. \(\frac{dB}{d\tau} + \kappa B(\tau) = 1\) with initial condition \(B(0) = 0\). The integrating factor is \(e^{\kappa \tau}\), yielding \(\frac{d}{d\tau}(e^{\kappa \tau} B) = e^{\kappa \tau}\). Integrating gives \(e^{\kappa \tau} B(\tau) = \frac{e^{\kappa \tau} - 1}{\kappa}\), hence \(B(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa \tau}}{\kappa}\).
  2. \(\frac{dA}{d\tau} = -\kappa \theta B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 B(\tau)^2\) with initial condition \(A(0) = 0\). Using the identity \(\frac{d}{d\tau}(B(\tau) - \tau) = B'(\tau) - 1 = -\kappa B(\tau)\), direct integration verifies that \(A(\tau) = (\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2})(B(\tau) - \tau) - \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa} B(\tau)^2\).

At \(\tau = 0\), \(B(0) = 0\) and \(A(0) = 0\), satisfying the terminal payoff \(P(T, T) = \exp(0) = 1\).

3Yield Curve Shapes and the Asymptotic Long Rate

The Vasicek model produces three distinct term-structure profiles depending on the current short rate \(r(t)\) relative to the long-term risk-neutral anchor \(\theta\).

Theorem 2.3 (Asymptotic Long-Term Yield)

As maturity \(\tau \to \infty\), the zero-coupon yield converges to a finite asymptotic long rate \(y_\infty\):

\[ y_\infty = \lim_{\tau \to \infty} y(t, t + \tau) = \theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2} \]
Proof

Examine the limits of \(B(\tau)/\tau\) and \(-A(\tau)/\tau\) as \(\tau \to \infty\):

\[ \lim_{\tau \to \infty} \frac{B(\tau)}{\tau} = \lim_{\tau \to \infty} \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa \tau}}{\kappa \tau} = 0 \] \[ \lim_{\tau \to \infty} -\frac{A(\tau)}{\tau} = \lim_{\tau \to \infty} \left[ -\left(\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\right) \frac{B(\tau) - \tau}{\tau} + \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa} \frac{B(\tau)^2}{\tau} \right] = \theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2} \]

Thus \(y(t, \tau) \to y_\infty = \theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\). The asymptotic yield is strictly lower than the long-term mean \(\theta\) due to the negative Jensen convexity term \(-\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\).

Remark 2.1 (Three Canonical Curve Regimes)

Depending on the initial condition \(r(t)\):

  • Monotonically Increasing: If \(r(t) \le y_\infty - \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa^2}\), the yield curve is upward-sloping across all maturities.
  • Monotonically Decreasing (Inverted): If \(r(t) \ge \theta\), the yield curve is downward-sloping across all maturities.
  • Humped: If \(y_\infty - \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa^2} < r(t) < \theta\), the curve initially rises, peaks at an intermediate maturity, and then slopes downward toward \(y_\infty\).

Lab 1 · Vasicek Closed Form vs. Monte Carlo

Simulating 25,000 Euler-discretized Vasicek paths with daily increments (dt = 1/252) produces empirical zero-coupon bond prices that match analytical closed-form prices within Monte Carlo standard error (< 10 bps difference across tenors 0.5Y to 10Y).

"""Lab FI2-L02-1: Vasicek closed form vs. Monte Carlo.

SIMULATED: simulates the 1-factor Gaussian Vasicek short-rate process under Q,
comparing analytical zero-coupon bond prices with Euler-discretized Monte Carlo
expected discount payoffs across multiple tenors [0.5Y, 1.0Y, 2.0Y, 5.0Y, 10.0Y].
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def vasicek_closed_form(
    r0: float,
    kappa: float,
    theta: float,
    sigma: float,
    tau: float,
) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Compute analytical zero-coupon bond price and yield under the Vasicek model."""
    if tau == 0.0:
        return 1.0, r0
    b_tau = (1.0 - np.exp(-kappa * tau)) / kappa
    a_tau = (theta - (sigma**2) / (2.0 * kappa**2)) * (b_tau - tau) - (sigma**2 / (4.0 * kappa)) * (b_tau**2)
    log_price = a_tau - b_tau * r0
    price = float(np.exp(log_price))
    spot_yield = -log_price / tau
    return price, float(spot_yield)


def vasicek_monte_carlo(
    r0: float,
    kappa: float,
    theta: float,
    sigma: float,
    tenors: list[float],
    dt: float,
    n_paths: int,
    seed: int,
) -> tuple[dict[float, float], dict[float, float], dict[float, float]]:
    """Simulate Vasicek paths and compute expected discount factors and standard errors."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    max_tenor = max(tenors)
    n_steps = round(max_tenor / dt)
    sqrt_dt = np.sqrt(dt)

    paths = np.zeros((n_paths, n_steps + 1), dtype=np.float64)
    paths[:, 0] = r0

    for step in range(n_steps):
        r_t = paths[:, step]
        dw = rng.normal(0.0, sqrt_dt, size=n_paths)
        paths[:, step + 1] = r_t + kappa * (theta - r_t) * dt + sigma * dw

    mc_prices: dict[float, float] = {}
    mc_yields: dict[float, float] = {}
    mc_stderrs: dict[float, float] = {}

    for tenor in tenors:
        step_idx = round(tenor / dt)
        integral_r = dt * (0.5 * paths[:, 0] + np.sum(paths[:, 1:step_idx], axis=1) + 0.5 * paths[:, step_idx])
        discount_factors = np.exp(-integral_r)
        price_mean = float(np.mean(discount_factors))
        price_se = float(np.std(discount_factors, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_paths))
        mc_prices[tenor] = price_mean
        mc_stderrs[tenor] = price_se
        mc_yields[tenor] = -np.log(price_mean) / tenor

    return mc_prices, mc_yields, mc_stderrs


def main() -> None:
    # Model parameters
    r0 = 0.030  # 3.00% initial short rate
    kappa = 0.20  # Mean reversion speed
    theta = 0.050  # Long-term mean 5.00%
    sigma = 0.015  # Instantaneous volatility 1.50%

    tenors = [0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0]
    dt = 1.0 / 252.0
    n_paths = 25_000
    seed = 12345

    mc_prices, mc_yields, mc_se = vasicek_monte_carlo(r0, kappa, theta, sigma, tenors, dt, n_paths, seed)

    print("SIMULATED Vasicek: Analytical Closed Form vs. Euler Monte Carlo:")
    print(f"  Params: r0 = {r0:.4f}, kappa = {kappa:.2f}, theta = {theta:.4f}, sigma = {sigma:.4f}")
    print(f"  MC: {n_paths} paths, dt = 1/252 yr, seed = {seed}")
    print("Tenor | Analytical P |    MC P     |  MC SE  | Diff (bps) | Analytical y |    MC y")
    print("-" * 75)

    for tenor in tenors:
        p_exact, y_exact = vasicek_closed_form(r0, kappa, theta, sigma, tenor)
        p_mc = mc_prices[tenor]
        y_mc = mc_yields[tenor]
        se = mc_se[tenor]
        diff_bps = (p_mc - p_exact) * 10_000.0

        print(
            f" {tenor:4.1f} |   {p_exact:.6f}   |  {p_mc:.6f}  | {se:.5f} |   {diff_bps:+6.2f}   |    "
            f"{y_exact * 100:.3f}%   |   {y_mc * 100:.3f}%"
        )

Where the canon breaks

The fatal structural flaw of the Vasicek model is that the Gaussian distribution has support on the entire real line: \(r(t) \in (-\infty, \infty)\). In the Vasicek model, interest rates have a strictly positive probability of becoming negative:

\[ \mathbb{P}(r(t) < 0) = \Phi\left( -\frac{\mathbb{E}[r(t)]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(r(t))}} \right) > 0 \]

When Vasicek wrote in 1977, nominal interest rates were high, and negative rates were viewed as an unphysical mathematical anomaly. However, under low-rate regimes, the model routinely predicts large probabilities of deeply negative yields. Furthermore, because bond prices \(\lim_{r \to -\infty} P(t, T) = \infty\) explode exponentially as rates become negative, valuing long-dated interest rate options under Vasicek leads to severe mispricing and negative forward rate arbitrage.

Misconceptions

  • The long-term yield in the Vasicek model equals the risk-neutral mean reversion level: \(y_\infty = \theta\).

    This ignores the convexity effect. Because zero-coupon bond prices are convex functions of the short rate (\(\mathbb{E}[e^{-X}] > e^{-\mathbb{E}[X]}\)), volatility depresses long-term yields. The exact asymptotic yield is \(y_\infty = \theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\), which is strictly strictly less than \(\theta\).

  • Mean reversion speed \(\kappa\) can be estimated purely from cross-sectional yield curves at a single date.

    Cross-sectional yield curve calibration identifies the risk-neutral parameter \(\kappa\) only up to rotation with factor volatilities. Crucially, time-series mean reversion under \(\mathbb{P}\) requires historical time-series data; cross-sectional calibration cannot distinguish between a high-persistence physical factor with a large risk premium and a low-persistence factor (Dai and Singleton 2000).

  • Modified duration of a zero-coupon bond in the Vasicek model is equal to its time to maturity \(\tau\).

    While the duration of a zero-coupon bond with respect to its own yield-to-maturity is \(\tau\), the sensitivity of the bond price to the instantaneous short rate \(r(t)\) is governed by the factor loading \(B(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa \tau}}{\kappa}\). Because \(B(\tau) < \tau\) for all \(\tau > 0\), mean reversion dampens the impact of short-rate shocks on long-term bond prices.

Problem set

Problem 2.1 (Vasicek B coefficient calculation): In a Vasicek model with mean reversion \(\kappa = 0.25\), calculate the analytical factor loading \(B(\tau)\) for maturities \(\tau = 1.0\), \(\tau = 5.0\), and \(\tau = 10.0\) years, and find the limiting value \(B(\infty)\).

Solution

The Vasicek loading is \(B(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa \tau}}{\kappa}\). With \(\kappa = 0.25\): \[ B(1) = \frac{1 - e^{-0.25}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - 0.77880}{0.25} \approx 0.88480 \] \[ B(5) = \frac{1 - e^{-1.25}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - 0.28650}{0.25} \approx 2.85398 \] \[ B(10) = \frac{1 - e^{-2.50}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - 0.08208}{0.25} \approx 3.67166 \] As \(\tau \to \infty\), \(e^{-\kappa \tau} \to 0\), so \(B(\infty) = \frac{1}{\kappa} = \frac{1}{0.25} = 4.0000\). The factor loading saturates at 4.0 years.

Problem 2.2 (Asymptotic yield and convexity drag): Given Vasicek parameters \(\theta = 0.055\), \(\kappa = 0.30\), and \(\sigma = 0.018\), compute the convexity drag \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\) in basis points and determine the asymptotic long-term yield \(y_\infty\).

Solution

The convexity drag is: \[ \text{Drag} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2} = \frac{(0.018)^2}{2(0.30)^2} = \frac{0.000324}{2 \times 0.090} = \frac{0.000324}{0.180} = 0.00180 = 18.0 \text{ bps} \] The asymptotic yield is: \[ y_\infty = \theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2} = 0.0550 - 0.0018 = 0.05320 = 5.320\% \] Convexity depresses the 30Y+ curve anchor by exactly 18 basis points below the long-term short-rate mean.

Problem 2.3 (Half-life of rate deviations): In an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with \(\kappa = 0.35\), calculate the half-life of interest rate deviations from the long-term mean in years.

Solution

The expected deviation decays exponentially as \(\mathbb{E}[r(t) - \theta] = (r(0) - \theta) e^{-\kappa t}\). The half-life \(t_{1/2}\) satisfies \(e^{-\kappa t_{1/2}} = 0.5\): \[ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\kappa} = \frac{0.693147}{0.35} \approx 1.9804 \text{ years} \] It takes approximately 1.98 years (around 24 months) for half of any shock to the short rate to dissipate.

Problem 2.4 (Unconditional stationary variance): Prove that the unconditional stationary variance of the Vasicek short rate is \(\operatorname{Var}(r(\infty)) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\).

Solution

From Theorem 2.1, the conditional variance given \(r(0)\) is \(\operatorname{Var}(r(t) \mid r(0)) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t})\). Taking the continuous limit as \(t \to \infty\): \[ \lim_{t \to \infty} \operatorname{Var}(r(t) \mid r(0)) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} \lim_{t \to \infty} (1 - e^{-2\kappa t}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} (1 - 0) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} \] Alternatively, by Itô's lemma on \(r(t)^2\): \(d(r^2) = 2r dr + (dr)^2 = 2r[\kappa(\theta - r)dt + \sigma dW] + \sigma^2 dt\). Taking expectations in the stationary state where \(\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[r^2] = 0\): \[ 2\kappa \theta \mathbb{E}[r] - 2\kappa \mathbb{E}[r^2] + \sigma^2 = 0 \implies \mathbb{E}[r^2] = \theta^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} \] Then \(\operatorname{Var}(r) = \mathbb{E}[r^2] - (\mathbb{E}[r])^2 = \theta^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} - \theta^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\).

Problem 2.5 (Analytical derivative of Vasicek A coefficient): Prove that the derivative \(\frac{dA}{d\tau}\) evaluates to \(-\kappa \theta B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 B(\tau)^2\) for all \(\tau \ge 0\).

Solution

The analytical expression is \(A(\tau) = (\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2})(B(\tau) - \tau) - \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa} B(\tau)^2\). We differentiate with respect to \(\tau\). Note that \(B'(\tau) = \frac{d}{d\tau}(\frac{1 - e^{-\kappa \tau}}{\kappa}) = e^{-\kappa \tau} = 1 - \kappa B(\tau)\). Thus \(\frac{d}{d\tau}(B(\tau) - \tau) = B'(\tau) - 1 = -\kappa B(\tau)\). Differentiating the first term of \(A(\tau)\): \[ \frac{d}{d\tau}\left[\left(\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\right)(B(\tau) - \tau)\right] = \left(\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\right)(-\kappa B(\tau)) = -\kappa \theta B(\tau) + \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} B(\tau) \] Differentiating the second term: \[ \frac{d}{d\tau}\left[-\frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa} B(\tau)^2\right] = -\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} B(\tau) B'(\tau) = -\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} B(\tau) (1 - \kappa B(\tau)) = -\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 B(\tau)^2 \] Summing both derivative terms: \[ \frac{dA}{d\tau} = -\kappa \theta B(\tau) + \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} B(\tau) - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa} B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 B(\tau)^2 = -\kappa \theta B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 B(\tau)^2 \] The intermediate terms cancel identically, proving the ODE is satisfied.

Problem 2.6 (Zero-rate derivative condition): Prove that the instantaneous forward curve in the Vasicek model satisfies \(f(t, t) = r(t)\) and \(\left.\frac{\partial f(t, T)}{\partial T}\right|_{T=t} = \kappa(\theta - r(t))\).

Solution

The forward rate is \(f(t, T) = -\frac{\partial \ln P(t, T)}{\partial T} = -A'(\tau) + B'(\tau) r(t)\) where \(\tau = T - t\). At \(\tau = 0\), \(B'(0) = 1\) and \(A'(0) = 0\), so \(f(t, t) = 0 + 1 \cdot r(t) = r(t)\). Next, differentiate with respect to \(T\) (which equals differentiation with respect to \(\tau\)): \[ \frac{\partial f(t, T)}{\partial T} = -A''(\tau) + B''(\tau) r(t) \] From Problem 2.5, \(A'(\tau) = -\kappa \theta B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 B(\tau)^2\). Differentiating: \(A''(\tau) = -\kappa \theta B'(\tau) + \sigma^2 B(\tau) B'(\tau)\). At \(\tau = 0\), since \(B(0) = 0\) and \(B'(0) = 1\), we have \(A''(0) = -\kappa \theta (1) + 0 = -\kappa \theta\). For \(B\), \(B'(\tau) = e^{-\kappa \tau} \implies B''(\tau) = -\kappa e^{-\kappa \tau} \implies B''(0) = -\kappa\). Evaluating the derivative at \(\tau = 0\): \[ \left.\frac{\partial f(t, T)}{\partial T}\right|_{T=t} = -(-\kappa \theta) + (-\kappa) r(t) = \kappa \theta - \kappa r(t) = \kappa(\theta - r(t)) \] The initial slope of the forward curve is precisely equal to the instantaneous risk-neutral drift of the short rate.

Problem 2.7 (Empirical negative rate probability): Calibrate a Vasicek model to a low-rate environment with \(\theta = 0.015\) (1.50%), \(\kappa = 0.20\), and \(\sigma = 0.010\). If the current rate is \(r(0) = 0.005\) (0.50%), compute the exact probability that the short rate is negative in 2 years: \(\mathbb{P}(r(2) < 0)\).

Solution

We compute the conditional mean and standard deviation at \(t = 2\): \[ \mathbb{E}[r(2)] = \theta + (r(0) - \theta) e^{-\kappa t} = 0.015 + (0.005 - 0.015) e^{-0.20 \times 2} = 0.015 - 0.010 e^{-0.40} \approx 0.015 - 0.006703 = 0.008297 \] \[ \operatorname{Var}(r(2)) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t}) = \frac{(0.010)^2}{2(0.20)}(1 - e^{-0.80}) = \frac{0.00010}{0.40}(1 - 0.449329) = 0.00025 \times 0.550671 \approx 0.00013767 \] \[ \text{SD}(r(2)) = \sqrt{0.00013767} \approx 0.011733 = 1.173\% \] Standardizing the zero threshold: \[ Z = \frac{0 - 0.008297}{0.011733} \approx -0.7071 \] The probability of negative interest rates is \(\Phi(-0.7071) \approx 0.2398 = 23.98\%\). In this low-rate setting, there is nearly a 24% chance of negative short rates within 2 years.

Problem 2.8 (Pricing a 10Y zero coupon bond): Using the parameters \(r(0) = 0.035\), \(\theta = 0.045\), \(\kappa = 0.25\), and \(\sigma = 0.015\), compute the exact analytical price of a 10-year zero-coupon bond \(P(0, 10)\) and its yield \(y(0, 10)\).

Solution

For \(\tau = 10\): \[ B(10) = \frac{1 - e^{-0.25 \times 10}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - e^{-2.50}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - 0.082085}{0.25} \approx 3.67166 \] \[ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2} = \frac{0.000225}{2(0.0625)} = \frac{0.000225}{0.125} = 0.00180 \] \[ \theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2} = 0.045 - 0.0018 = 0.04320 \] \[ B(10) - \tau = 3.67166 - 10 = -6.32834 \] \[ \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa} B(10)^2 = \frac{0.000225}{1.00} \times (3.67166)^2 = 0.000225 \times 13.4811 = 0.003033 \] \[ A(10) = 0.04320 \times (-6.32834) - 0.003033 = -0.273384 - 0.003033 = -0.276417 \] The log price is: \[ \ln P(0, 10) = A(10) - B(10) r(0) = -0.276417 - (3.67166 \times 0.035) = -0.276417 - 0.128508 = -0.404925 \] Price: \(P(0, 10) = \exp(-0.404925) \approx 0.66703\). Yield: \(y(0, 10) = -(-0.404925) / 10 = 0.04049 = 4.049\%\).

Problem 2.9 (Yield curve inversion condition): Determine whether the yield curve in Problem 2.8 is upward sloping, downward sloping, or humped. Find the maximum yield and the maturity at which it occurs if humped.

Solution

Check the classification thresholds from Remark 2.1: \[ y_\infty = \theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2} = 0.04320 \] \[ \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa^2} = \frac{0.00180}{2} = 0.00090 \] \[ \text{Threshold} = y_\infty - \frac{\sigma^2}{4\kappa^2} = 0.04320 - 0.00090 = 0.04230 \] The current rate is \(r(0) = 0.0350\). Because \(r(0) = 0.0350 < 0.04230\), the yield curve is strictly monotonically increasing across all maturities. The yield starts at \(r(0) = 3.50\%\) at \(\tau = 0\) and increases monotonically toward \(y_\infty = 4.32\%\) as \(\tau \to \infty\).

Problem 2.10 (Black's shadow rate model vs. Vasicek): Fischer Black (1995) proposed that the observed short rate is \(R(t) = \max(r(t), 0)\), where \(r(t)\) is an unconstrained shadow rate that can be negative. Contrast Black's shadow rate model with the Vasicek model in terms of European call option pricing on zero-coupon bonds.

Solution

In the standard Vasicek model, bond prices are log-normally distributed conditional on \(r(t)\), and European call options on zero-coupon bonds can be evaluated using Jamshidian's (1989) decomposition of Black-Scholes formulas. However, when rates approach zero, Vasicek permits nominal rates to go negative without bound, causing call prices on discount bonds to exceed par value \(P(t, T) > 1\). Under Black's (1995) shadow-rate formulation, physical currency offers an embedded zero-yield option: currency truncation \(R(t) = \max(r(t), 0)\) enforces \(P(t, T) \le 1\) strictly. However, this max operator destroys the analytic affine property: the term-structure PDE becomes non-linear with a state-dependent free boundary, requiring numerical PDE methods or approximation expansions. European bond call options under the shadow-rate model exhibit suppressed volatility near the strike, generating a steep volatility skew that Vasicek fails to capture.

Problem 2.11 (Time-varying parameters and Hull-White extension): Explain how Hull and White (1990) extended the Vasicek model to \(dr = (\theta(t) - \kappa r) dt + \sigma dW\) to achieve an exact fit to an arbitrary initial yield curve, and describe the trade-off regarding parameter stability.

Solution

In the homogeneous Vasicek model, \(\theta\) is constant, so the model only has 3 parameters \((\kappa, \theta, \sigma)\) to fit the entire continuous initial term structure \(P(0, T)\). As a result, Vasicek generates pricing errors against the observed market curve. Hull and White (1990) replaced constant \(\theta\) with a time-dependent function \(\theta(t)\). By differentiating the initial forward curve, \(\theta(t)\) is analytically calibrated: \(\theta(t) = f(0, t) + \frac{1}{\kappa} \frac{\partial f(0, t)}{\partial t} + \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t})\). While this achieves zero in-sample pricing error on zero-coupon bonds, the trade-off is parameter instability: all market noise, illiquidity premiums, and bootstrapping kinks from the current curve are embedded into future expected drift \(\theta(t)\). On subsequent days, recalibrating \(\theta(t)\) produces intertemporal parameter instability, violating temporal consistency.

Problem 2.12 (Yield spread regressions and failure of expectations hypothesis): Vasicek models impose the expectations hypothesis under \(\mathbb{Q}\), but Campbell and Shiller (1991) find empirical slope coefficients \(\beta_\tau < 1\) in regressions of yield changes on term spreads. How does the constant market price of risk in Vasicek explain or fail to explain this empirical finding?

Solution

Under the Campbell-Shiller regression, \(y(t+1, T-1) - y(t, T) = \alpha_\tau + \beta_\tau \frac{y(t, T) - r(t)}{\tau - 1} + \epsilon_{t+1}\). Under the pure expectations hypothesis, \(\beta_\tau = 1.0\). In the Vasicek model with constant market price of risk \(\lambda\), the term premium is deterministic and constant over time: \(\text{TP}(\tau) = y(t, \tau) - \frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{P}[\int_t^{t+\tau} r_s ds] = \text{const}(\tau)\). Because the risk premium is constant, the time-variation in the term spread is driven entirely by expected changes in the short rate. Consequently, Vasicek predicts \(\beta_\tau = 1.0\) for all maturities. Empirically, Campbell and Shiller (1991) find that \(\beta_\tau\) is negative and increasingly negative with maturity (e.g. \(\beta_{10} \approx -3.5\)). This empirical failure proves that the term premium in financial markets is strongly time-varying and countercyclical, ruling out constant-risk-premium models like Vasicek and requiring affine models with state-dependent risk prices \(\lambda(t) = \lambda_0 + \lambda_1 X_t\) (Dai and Singleton 2000).

    \(B(\infty) = 1 / 0.50 = 2.0\) years.
