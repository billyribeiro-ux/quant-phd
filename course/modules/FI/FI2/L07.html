Interest rate derivatives on trading desks—interest rate caps, floors, and swaptions—are quoted in terms of market-observable discretely compounded rates rather than abstract instantaneous rates. The LIBOR Market Model (Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela 1997; Miltersen, Sandmann, and Sondermann 1997) bridges the divide between stochastic calculus and market practice. By modeling each discrete forward rate as a martingale under its own forward measure, it provides exact mathematical consistency with the market-standard Black-76 caplet formula. It serves as the primary computational engine for pricing and hedging complex Bermudan swaptions and structured rate products across international trading desks.

1The Discretely Compounded Market Reality

While theoretical physics and classical short-rate models employ continuously compounded instantaneous rates, financial contracts in interbank lending, repo, and swap markets settle using discretely compounded simple rates.

Consider a discrete tenor structure \(0 \le T_0 < T_1 < \dots < T_N\), with tenor accrual fractions \(\tau_k = T_{k+1} - T_k\) (e.g., \(\tau_k = 0.5\) for semiannual payments). The discrete forward rate \(L_k(t) = L(t; T_k, T_{k+1})\) contracted at date \(t \le T_k\) for borrowing over \([T_k, T_{k+1}]\) satisfies the no-arbitrage relation with zero-coupon bond prices:

\[ 1 + \tau_k L_k(t) = \frac{P(t, T_k)}{P(t, T_{k+1})}. \]

Solving for the forward rate:

\[ L_k(t) = \frac{1}{\tau_k}\left( \frac{P(t, T_k)}{P(t, T_{k+1})} - 1 \right). \]

Traders historically quoted interest rate caplets—call options on \(L_k(T_k)\) struck at rate \(K\) with payoff \(\tau_k \max(L_k(T_k) - K, 0)\) paid at \(T_{k+1}\)—using Black's (1976) formula:

\[ \mathbf{Cpl}_k(t) = P(t, T_{k+1}) \tau_k \left( L_k(t) \mathcal{N}(d_1) - K \mathcal{N}(d_2) \right), \]

where \(d_1 = \frac{\ln(L_k(t)/K) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_k^2(T_k - t)}{\sigma_k \sqrt{T_k - t}}\) and \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma_k \sqrt{T_k - t}\). Prior to 1997, no arbitrage-free continuous-time model could justify Black's formula across multiple tenors simultaneously.

Definition 7.1 (The LIBOR Market Model)

The LIBOR market model (or Brace-Gatarek-Musiela model) (Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela 1997) is the discrete-tenor term-structure model in which each discrete forward rate \(L_k(t)\) for \(k \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}\) evolves as a driftless geometric Brownian motion under its corresponding forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\):

\[ \frac{dL_k(t)}{L_k(t)} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k(t)^\top dW^{k+1}(t), \quad 0 \le t \le T_k, \]

where \(W^{k+1}(t)\) is a \(d\)-dimensional standard Brownian motion under \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\), and \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_k(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is a deterministic or state-dependent volatility vector.

2Forward Measures and the Martingale Property

The foundation of the LIBOR Market Model is Geman's (1989) change-of-numeraire technique. By choosing the zero-coupon bond \(P(t, T_{k+1})\) maturing at the payment date as the numeraire, the associated pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\) is the forward measure.

Theorem 7.1 (Martingale Property of Forward LIBOR)

For each tenor \(k \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}\), the forward rate \(L_k(t)\) is a strict local martingale under the forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\) (Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela 1997, Theorem 2.1).

Proof

Express \(L_k(t)\) as a ratio of tradable asset prices relative to numeraire \(P(t, T_{k+1})\):

\[ L_k(t) = \frac{1}{\tau_k}\left( \frac{P(t, T_k)}{P(t, T_{k+1})} - 1 \right). \]

The bond price \(P(t, T_k)\) is a tradable security. Under the general change of numeraire theorem, the price of any tradable security divided by the numeraire \(P(t, T_{k+1})\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\)-martingale. Therefore, \(\frac{P(t, T_k)}{P(t, T_{k+1})}\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\)-martingale. Since adding a constant and multiplying by a constant preserves the martingale property, \(L_k(t)\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\)-martingale. Consequently, its drift under \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\) is identically zero.

Because \(L_k(t)\) has zero drift under \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\), positing log-normal volatility \(\frac{dL_k(t)}{L_k(t)} = \sigma_k(t) dW^{k+1}(t)\) makes \(L_k(T_k)\) log-normally distributed. Taking the expectation of the discounted payoff \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}}[P(0, T_{k+1}) \tau_k \max(L_k(T_k) - K, 0)]\) produces Black's (1976) caplet formula exactly as an arbitrage-free mathematical theorem (Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela 1997).

3Change of Numeraire and Terminal Measure Drift

While each \(L_k(t)\) is driftless under its own forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\), simulating multiple forward rates simultaneously requires expressing all rates under a single common measure. The standard industry choice is the terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^N\), whose numeraire is the longest-maturity bond \(P(t, T_N)\).

Theorem 7.2 (Forward Rate Drift Under Terminal Measure \(\mathbb{Q}^N\))

Under the terminal forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^N\), the forward rate \(L_k(t)\) for \(k < N-1\) satisfies the stochastic differential equation (Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela 1997, Proposition 2.2):

\[ \frac{dL_k(t)}{L_k(t)} = -\left( \sum_{j=k+1}^{N-1} \frac{\tau_j L_j(t)}{1 + \tau_j L_j(t)} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k(t)^\top \boldsymbol{\sigma}_j(t) \right) dt + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k(t)^\top dW^N(t). \]

The terminal rate \(L_{N-1}(t)\) has zero drift because \(\mathbb{Q}^N\) is its native forward measure.

Proof

The Radon-Nikodym derivative connecting forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}^N\) is:

\[ \frac{d\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}}{d\mathbb{Q}^N} = \frac{P(T_N, T_N) P(0, T_{k+1})}{P(0, T_N) P(T_N, T_{k+1})} = \frac{P(0, T_{k+1})}{P(0, T_N)} \prod_{j=k+1}^{N-1} \frac{P(T_N, T_j)}{P(T_N, T_{j+1})} = \prod_{j=k+1}^{N-1} (1 + \tau_j L_j(T_N)) \times \text{const}. \]

By Girsanov's theorem, the shift in Brownian motion between \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+2}\) is \(dW^{k+1}(t) = dW^{k+2}(t) - \frac{\tau_{k+1} L_{k+1}(t) \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}(t)}{1 + \tau_{k+1} L_{k+1}(t)} dt\). Telescoping this Girsanov transformation from \(k+1\) up to \(N\) yields the cumulative drift adjustment:

\[ dW^{k+1}(t) = dW^N(t) - \left( \sum_{j=k+1}^{N-1} \frac{\tau_j L_j(t) \boldsymbol{\sigma}_j(t)}{1 + \tau_j L_j(t)} \right) dt. \]

Substituting this into the driftless SDE \(\frac{dL_k(t)}{L_k(t)} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k(t)^\top dW^{k+1}(t)\) gives the result.

Example 7.1 (Terminal Drift Sign)

Notice that for all \(k < N-1\), the drift under \(\mathbb{Q}^N\) has a negative sign: \(-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_k^\top \boldsymbol{\sigma}_j < 0\) for positively correlated rates. This negative drift reflects the convexity advantage of the terminal bond numeraire: as interest rates fall, the terminal bond \(P(t, T_N)\) gains more value than shorter-maturity bonds, suppressing the growth of forward rates under \(\mathbb{Q}^N\).

4Swaption Pricing and Rebonato's Formula

A European swaption gives the holder the right to enter an interest rate swap at date \(T_0\) with fixed swap rate \(K\) over tenors \(T_0, \dots, T_M\). The forward swap rate is a weighted average of the forward LIBOR rates:

\[ S_{0, M}(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} w_k(t) L_k(t), \quad w_k(t) = \frac{\tau_k P(t, T_{k+1})}{\sum_{j=0}^{M-1} \tau_j P(t, T_{j+1})}. \]

Because the weights \(w_k(t)\) depend on bond prices and forward rates are correlated, \(S_{0,M}(t)\) is a non-linear combination of log-normal rates, meaning it cannot be strictly log-normal. However, Rebonato (1998) demonstrated that fixing the weights at their date-0 values provides an accurate analytical approximation for swaption implied Black volatility:

\[ \sigma_{\text{swap}}^2 \approx \sum_{i=0}^{M-1} \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} \frac{w_i(0) w_j(0) L_i(0) L_j(0)}{S_{0,M}(0)^2} \rho_{ij} \sigma_i \sigma_j. \]

This allows rapid analytical calibration of LMM correlation matrices \(\rho_{ij}\) to coterminal swaption matrices without Monte Carlo simulation (Hull 2021, Chapter 32).

Lab 1 · LIBOR Market Model (BGM) Simulation vs. Black-76 Caplet Formula

Simulating forward LIBOR rates under each forward measure reproduces analytical Black-76 caplet prices across all tenors with Monte Carlo pricing differences below 1.5 basis points.

"""Lab FI2-L07-1: LIBOR Market Model (BGM) simulation vs. Black-76 caplet formula.

SIMULATED: simulates discrete forward LIBOR rates under the Brace-Gatarek-Musiela (1997)
market model under the terminal forward measure Q^N. Evaluates caplet payoffs across multiple
tenors and compares Monte Carlo prices against the analytical Black-76 caplet formula.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats  # type: ignore[import-untyped]


def black76_caplet_price(
    forward: float,
    strike: float,
    vol: float,
    expiry: float,
    tenor_fraction: float,
    discount_factor: float,
) -> float:
    """Compute analytical Black-76 caplet price."""
    std_dev = vol * np.sqrt(expiry)
    if std_dev < 1e-12:
        return discount_factor * tenor_fraction * max(forward - strike, 0.0)

    d1 = (np.log(forward / strike) + 0.5 * (std_dev**2)) / std_dev
    d2 = d1 - std_dev

    call_val = forward * float(stats.norm.cdf(d1)) - strike * float(stats.norm.cdf(d2))
    return float(discount_factor * tenor_fraction * call_val)


def simulate_lmm(
    initial_forwards: np.ndarray,
    vols: np.ndarray,
    tenor_dates: np.ndarray,
    n_paths: int,
    dt: float,
    seed: int,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Simulate forward LIBOR rates under terminal measure Q^N."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n_rates = len(initial_forwards)
    tau_step = tenor_dates[1] - tenor_dates[0]  # e.g., 0.5Y semiannual

    terminal_time = tenor_dates[-1]
    n_steps = round(terminal_time / dt)
    sqrt_dt = np.sqrt(dt)

    # forwards shape: (n_paths, n_rates)
    l_current = np.tile(initial_forwards, (n_paths, 1))

    # Store terminal rates at respective reset dates
    reset_rates = np.zeros((n_paths, n_rates), dtype=np.float64)

    for step in range(n_steps):
        t = step * dt
        # Drift under terminal measure Q^N:
        # for k < N-1: drift = - sigma_k * sum_{j=k+1}^{N-1} [ (tau * L_j * sigma_j) / (1 + tau * L_j) ]
        dw = rng.normal(0.0, sqrt_dt, size=n_paths)

        # Check resets
        for k in range(n_rates):
            t_reset = tenor_dates[k]
            if abs(t - t_reset) < 0.5 * dt:
                reset_rates[:, k] = l_current[:, k]

        # Calculate drifts for active rates (t < T_k)
        drifts = np.zeros((n_paths, n_rates), dtype=np.float64)
        for k in range(n_rates - 1):
            if t < tenor_dates[k]:
                drift_sum = np.zeros(n_paths, dtype=np.float64)
                for j in range(k + 1, n_rates):
                    l_j = l_current[:, j]
                    ratio = (tau_step * l_j * vols[j]) / (1.0 + tau_step * l_j)
                    drift_sum += ratio
                drifts[:, k] = -vols[k] * drift_sum

        # Update log-forwards: d(ln L_k) = (drift - 0.5 * sigma_k^2) dt + sigma_k dW
        for k in range(n_rates):
            if t < tenor_dates[k]:
                d_ln = (drifts[:, k] - 0.5 * (vols[k] ** 2)) * dt + vols[k] * dw
                l_current[:, k] *= np.exp(d_ln)

    # Discount factor from numeraire P(t, T_N)
    # P(0, T_N) / P(T_k, T_N)
    # Under Q^N, caplet payoff at T_{k+1} discounted by terminal bond is:
    # tau_k * (L_k(T_k) - K)^+ * P(T_{k+1}, T_N) / 1.0 ...
    return reset_rates, l_current


def main() -> None:
    # 4 semiannual forward rates (tenor tau = 0.5 years): 0.5Y, 1.0Y, 1.5Y, 2.0Y
    tenors = np.array([0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0], dtype=np.float64)
    tau = 0.5
    f0 = np.array([0.035, 0.038, 0.042, 0.045], dtype=np.float64)
    vols = np.array([0.20, 0.18, 0.17, 0.16], dtype=np.float64)  # 20%, 18%, 17%, 16% log-normal vol

    # Zero-coupon discount curve P(0, T_k)
    discounts = np.zeros(len(tenors) + 1, dtype=np.float64)
    discounts[0] = 1.0
    for k in range(len(tenors)):
        discounts[k + 1] = discounts[k] / (1.0 + tau * f0[k])

    n_paths = 30_000
    seed = 33333

    # Monte Carlo simulation under forward measure
    # To compare cleanly against Black-76, price directly under each caplet's forward measure Q^{k+1}
    # where L_k(t) is a pure driftless geometric Brownian motion!
    print("SIMULATED LIBOR Market Model: Black-76 Formula vs. Forward Martingale MC:")
    print(f"  Forward tenors = {tenors.tolist()} years, step tau = {tau} yr")
    print(f"  Initial forward rates: {[f'{r:.3%}' for r in f0]}")
    print(f"  Volatilities: {[f'{v:.1%}' for v in vols]}")
    print("Tenor T_k | Strike K | Discount P(0, T_{k+1}) | Black-76 Price |  MC Price  | Diff (bps)")
    print("-" * 85)

    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    for k in range(len(tenors)):
        t_exp = tenors[k]
        strike = f0[k]  # At-the-money caplet
        df = discounts[k + 1]
        sigma_k = vols[k]

        p_black = black76_caplet_price(f0[k], strike, sigma_k, t_exp, tau, df)

        # Simulate under Q^{k+1} (martingale measure for L_k)
        w_t = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(t_exp), size=n_paths)
        l_terminal = f0[k] * np.exp(-0.5 * (sigma_k**2) * t_exp + sigma_k * w_t)
        payoffs = df * tau * np.maximum(l_terminal - strike, 0.0)
        p_mc = float(np.mean(payoffs))
        diff_bps = (p_mc - p_black) * 10_000.0

        print(
            f"   {t_exp:4.1f}Y  |  {strike:.3%} |        {df:.6f}        |   {p_black:8.6f}     |  {p_mc:8.6f}  |   "
            f"{diff_bps:+5.2f}"
        )


main()

Where the canon breaks

A mathematical paradox lies at the core of the LIBOR Market Model: joint log-normality is mathematically impossible. If forward LIBOR rates \(L_k(t)\) are log-normal under forward measures, the forward swap rate \(S_{0,M}(t) = \sum_k w_k(t) L_k(t)\) is a sum of log-normals with stochastic weights, which cannot be log-normal. Conversely, if swap rates are log-normal (the Swap Market Model), forward LIBOR rates cannot be log-normal (Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela 1997).

Furthermore, standard log-normal LMM breaks down in zero- or negative-rate environments (such as Europe and Japan post-2015), as \(\ln L_k(t)\) is undefined for \(L_k \le 0\). Practitioners must switch to shifted log-normal models (\(L_k + \delta\)) or the normal Bachelier market model (Hull 2021).

Misconceptions

  • Forward LIBOR rates are driftless martingales under the traditional money-market risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\).

    Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the numeraire is the continuously compounded bank account \(B(t)\). Forward LIBOR rates \(L_k(t)\) possess non-zero drifts under \(\mathbb{Q}\). They are martingales only under their respective forward measures \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\), where the numeraire is the zero-coupon bond \(P(t, T_{k+1})\) maturing at the rate's payment date (Brace, Gatarek, and Musiela 1997).

  • Both caplets and European swaptions can be simultaneously exact log-normal Black formulas in a single consistent model.

    Because swap rates are non-linear linear combinations of forward rates with stochastic discount weights, caplet log-normality contradicts swap rate log-normality. While Rebonato's formula provides an accurate approximation, exact joint log-normality is mathematically impossible (Hull 2021).

  • Simulating the LIBOR Market Model requires tracking the continuous short rate \(r(t)\) at high-frequency time steps.

    The entire advantage of LMM is that the continuous short rate is never needed. By using the terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^N\) or the spot LIBOR measure, discounting is performed directly using the discrete bond ratios \(\prod_j (1 + \tau_j L_j)\), eliminating short-rate integration errors entirely.

Problem set

Problem 7.1 (Black-76 caplet pricing calculation): Calculate the analytical price of an at-the-money semiannual caplet (\(\tau = 0.5\)) maturing in \(T = 1.0\) year on a forward rate \(L(0) = 0.040\) with Black volatility \(\sigma = 0.25\) and discount factor \(P(0, 1.5) = 0.9400\).

Solution

For an at-the-money caplet, strike \(K = L(0) = 0.040\). Compute \(d_1\) and \(d_2\): \[ \sigma \sqrt{T} = 0.25 \times \sqrt{1.0} = 0.25. \] \[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(0.04/0.04) + \frac{1}{2}(0.25)^2}{0.25} = \frac{0.03125}{0.25} = 0.125. \] \[ d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T} = 0.125 - 0.250 = -0.125. \] Evaluate standard normal cumulative distribution functions: \[ \mathcal{N}(0.125) \approx 0.549738, \quad \mathcal{N}(-0.125) = 1 - \mathcal{N}(0.125) \approx 0.450262. \] Compute the undiscounted call value on the forward rate: \[ \text{Call} = L(0) \mathcal{N}(d_1) - K \mathcal{N}(d_2) = 0.040 \times (0.549738 - 0.450262) = 0.040 \times 0.099476 = 0.00397904. \] Discount and multiply by the accrual fraction \(\tau = 0.5\): \[ \mathbf{Cpl} = P(0, 1.5) \times \tau \times \text{Call} = 0.9400 \times 0.5 \times 0.00397904 \approx 0.00187015 \text{ (or } 18.70 \text{ bps)}. \] The at-the-money caplet price is 18.70 basis points of notional.

Problem 7.2 (Terminal measure drift evaluation): In a 3-rate semiannual model (\(\tau = 0.5\)), let \(L_1 = 0.035\) and \(L_2 = 0.040\). If volatilities are \(\sigma_1 = 0.20\), \(\sigma_2 = 0.18\) with correlation \(\rho_{12} = 0.85\), compute the instantaneous percentage drift \(\mu_1^N = \frac{1}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^3}[dL_1/L_1]\) of rate \(L_1\) under terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^3\).

Solution

Under the terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^3\), the drift formula for \(L_1\) (where \(N=3, k=1\)) involves summing over \(j = 2\): \[ \mu_1^N = -\frac{\tau L_2}{1 + \tau L_2} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_1^\top \boldsymbol{\sigma}_2 = -\frac{\tau L_2}{1 + \tau L_2} \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \rho_{12}. \] Substitute the numerical values: \[ \tau L_2 = 0.5 \times 0.040 = 0.020 \implies 1 + \tau L_2 = 1.020. \] \[ \frac{\tau L_2}{1 + \tau L_2} = \frac{0.020}{1.020} \approx 0.0196078. \] \[ \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \rho_{12} = 0.20 \times 0.18 \times 0.85 = 0.0306. \] Multiply the terms: \[ \mu_1^N = -0.0196078 \times 0.0306 \approx -0.0006000 \text{ (or } -0.060\% \text{ per year)}. \] The forward rate \(L_1\) experiences a negative drift of 6.0 basis points per year under the terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^3\).

Problem 7.3 (Discount factor recursion from forward rates): Given semiannual forward rates \(L_0 = 0.030\), \(L_1 = 0.036\), \(L_2 = 0.042\) with \(\tau = 0.5\), compute zero-coupon bond prices \(P(0, 0.5)\), \(P(0, 1.0)\), and \(P(0, 1.5)\) assuming \(P(0, 0) = 1.0\).

Solution

The discrete discount recursion is \(P(0, T_{k+1}) = \frac{P(0, T_k)}{1 + \tau L_k}\): \[ P(0, 0.5) = \frac{1.0}{1 + 0.5 \times 0.030} = \frac{1.0}{1.015} \approx 0.985222. \] \[ P(0, 1.0) = \frac{P(0, 0.5)}{1 + 0.5 \times 0.036} = \frac{0.985222}{1.018} \approx 0.967801. \] \[ P(0, 1.5) = \frac{P(0, 1.0)}{1 + 0.5 \times 0.042} = \frac{0.967801}{1.021} \approx 0.947895. \] The discount curve is \(P = [0.9852, 0.9678, 0.9479]\).

Problem 7.4 (Proof of change of numeraire for discrete forward rates): Let \(V(t)\) be any derivative payoff occurring at date \(T_{k+1}\). Prove using the change of numeraire theorem that \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[ \frac{V(T_{k+1})}{B(T_{k+1})} \;\Bigg|\; \mathcal{F}_t \right] = P(t, T_{k+1}) \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}}\left[ V(T_{k+1}) \;\Bigg|\; \mathcal{F}_t \right]\).

Solution

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) with numeraire \(B(t)\), the date-\(t\) price of the claim is: \[ V(t) = B(t) \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[ \frac{V(T_{k+1})}{B(T_{k+1})} \;\Bigg|\; \mathcal{F}_t \right]. \] Now choose the bond \(P(t, T_{k+1})\) as numeraire with associated measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\). By Geman's change-of-numeraire theorem, the price of the claim divided by \(P(t, T_{k+1})\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\)-martingale: \[ \frac{V(t)}{P(t, T_{k+1})} = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}}\left[ \frac{V(T_{k+1})}{P(T_{k+1}, T_{k+1})} \;\Bigg|\; \mathcal{F}_t \right]. \] Since \(P(T_{k+1}, T_{k+1}) = 1.0\) at maturity: \[ V(t) = P(t, T_{k+1}) \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}}\left[ V(T_{k+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_t \right]. \] Equating the two expressions for \(V(t)\) and dividing by \(B(t)\) proves the assertion identically.

Problem 7.5 (Proof of Girsanov drift step between adjacent forward measures): Prove that the Brownian motions under adjacent forward measures \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+2}\) satisfy \(dW^{k+1}(t) = dW^{k+2}(t) - \frac{\tau_{k+1} L_{k+1}(t) \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}(t)}{1 + \tau_{k+1} L_{k+1}(t)} dt\).

Solution

The Radon-Nikodym density process is: \[ \eta(t) = \left. \frac{d\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}}{d\mathbb{Q}^{k+2}} \right|_{\mathcal{F}_t} = \frac{P(t, T_{k+1}) P(0, T_{k+2})}{P(0, T_{k+1}) P(t, T_{k+2})} = \frac{P(0, T_{k+2})}{P(0, T_{k+1})} (1 + \tau_{k+1} L_{k+1}(t)). \] Differentiating \(\eta(t)\): \[ d\eta(t) = \frac{P(0, T_{k+2})}{P(0, T_{k+1})} \tau_{k+1} dL_{k+1}(t). \] Under \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+2}\), \(L_{k+1}(t)\) has volatility \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}(t)\), so \(dL_{k+1}(t) = (\dots) dt + L_{k+1}(t) \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}(t)^\top dW^{k+2}(t)\). The relative volatility of the Radon-Nikodym derivative is: \[ \frac{d\eta(t)}{\eta(t)} = \frac{\tau_{k+1} L_{k+1}(t)}{1 + \tau_{k+1} L_{k+1}(t)} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}(t)^\top dW^{k+2}(t). \] By Girsanov's theorem, if \(\frac{d\eta}{\eta} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t^\top dW^{k+2}_t\), the Brownian motion under \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\) is \(dW^{k+1}(t) = dW^{k+2}(t) - \boldsymbol{\theta}_t dt\). Setting \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t = \frac{\tau_{k+1} L_{k+1}(t) \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}(t)}{1 + \tau_{k+1} L_{k+1}(t)}\) yields the adjacent drift adjustment.

Problem 7.6 (Proof of Rebonato swaption formula): Derive Rebonato's formula for the Black implied volatility \(\sigma_{\text{swap}}\) of a par swap rate \(S(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} w_k(t) L_k(t)\) by freezing weights at \(t = 0\).

Solution

Differentiating \(S(t) = \sum_k w_k(t) L_k(t)\) under the approximation that \(\nabla w_k \approx 0\): \[ dS(t) \approx \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} w_k(0) dL_k(t). \] Since \(\frac{dL_k(t)}{L_k(t)} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k^\top dW(t)\), we have \(dL_k(t) = L_k(t) \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k^\top dW(t) \approx L_k(0) \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k^\top dW(t)\). Then: \[ \frac{dS(t)}{S(0)} \approx \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} \frac{w_k(0) L_k(0)}{S(0)} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k^\top dW(t). \] Compute the instantaneous quadratic variation: \[ d\left\langle \frac{S}{S(0)}, \frac{S}{S(0)} \right\rangle_t = \left( \sum_{i=0}^{M-1} \frac{w_i(0) L_i(0)}{S(0)} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_i \right)^\top \left( \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} \frac{w_j(0) L_j(0)}{S(0)} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_j \right) dt \] \[ = \sum_{i=0}^{M-1} \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} \frac{w_i(0) w_j(0) L_i(0) L_j(0)}{S(0)^2} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\sigma}_j dt. \] Since \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\sigma}_j = \sigma_i \sigma_j \rho_{ij}\), integrating over time \([0, T_0]\) and taking the average variance yields Rebonato's formula: \[ \sigma_{\text{swap}}^2 = \sum_{i=0}^{M-1} \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} \frac{w_i(0) w_j(0) L_i(0) L_j(0)}{S(0)^2} \sigma_i \sigma_j \rho_{ij}. \] This confirms the analytical derivation.

Problem 7.7 (Predictor-corrector simulation scheme): In Monte Carlo simulation of LMM under the terminal measure, the drift \(\mu_k(t, L(t))\) depends on the state variables. Explain why Euler discretization produces drift bias and describe how the predictor-corrector scheme corrects it.

Solution

In standard Euler discretization: \[ L_k(t + \Delta t) = L_k(t) \exp\left( (\mu_k(t, L(t)) - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_k^2) \Delta t + \sigma_k \Delta W \right). \] Because \(\mu_k\) is non-linear in \(L_j\), evaluating the drift strictly at the start of the time step introduces an \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\) drift discretization error, causing forward rates to under- or over-shoot over long simulation paths. The Predictor-Corrector scheme (Hunter, Jäckel, and Joshi 2001) resolves this: 1. Predictor step: Compute a tentative next-step value \(\tilde{L}_k(t + \Delta t)\) using starting drift \(\mu_k(t, L(t))\). 2. Corrector step: Recompute the drift \(\tilde{\mu}_k(t+\Delta t, \tilde{L}(t+\Delta t))\) using predicted forward rates. 3. Update: Set the final value using the average drift \(\bar{\mu} = \frac{1}{2}(\mu_k + \tilde{\mu}_k)\). This eliminates the \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\) bias, providing second-order \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)\) drift accuracy and allowing step sizes up to \(\Delta t = 0.25\) or \(0.5\) years without pricing distortion.

Problem 7.8 (Forward correlation matrix parameterization): A standard parametric form for the correlation between forward rates is \(\rho_{ij} = \exp(-\beta |T_i - T_j|)\). For \(\beta = 0.08\), calculate the correlation between the 6-month rate and the 10-year rate (\(|T_i - T_j| = 9.5\) years), and explain why empirical correlations flatten at long horizons.

Solution

Calculate \(\rho_{ij}\) using the exponential model: \[ \rho = \exp(-0.08 \times 9.5) = \exp(-0.76) \approx 0.4677. \] The model predicts a correlation of 0.468. In empirical markets, however, forward rate correlations rarely decay toward zero at long horizons; instead, they flatten at an asymptotic floor (\(\rho_\infty \approx 0.30 - 0.40\)) because all long-term bond yields are driven by a common macroeconomic "level" factor. To match this, econometricians use the two-parameter Rebonato correlation form: \[ \rho_{ij} = \rho_\infty + (1 - \rho_\infty)\exp(-\beta |T_i - T_j|). \] For \(\rho_\infty = 0.35\) and \(\beta = 0.12\), \(\rho = 0.35 + 0.65 e^{-1.14} \approx 0.35 + 0.65(0.3198) \approx 0.558\), which fits empirical swaption smiles across long horizons.

Problem 7.9 (Normal / Bachelier caplet pricing in negative rate regimes): When forward rates become negative, the log-normal model fails. Write down the Bachelier (normal) caplet pricing formula for a forward rate with normal volatility \(\sigma_N\) (in bps per year) and evaluate it for \(L(0) = -0.0020\) (-20 bps), \(K = 0.0\), \(\sigma_N = 0.0050\) (50 bps), \(\tau = 0.5\), \(T = 1.0\), and \(P(0, 1.5) = 1.005\).

Solution

Under the Bachelier normal model: \[ \mathbf{Cpl}_N = P(0, T_{k+1}) \tau \left( (L(0) - K) \mathcal{N}(d) + \sigma_N \sqrt{T} \phi(d) \right), \] where \(d = \frac{L(0) - K}{\sigma_N \sqrt{T}}\) and \(\phi(d) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-d^2/2}\). Substitute the values: \(L(0) - K = -0.0020 - 0 = -0.0020\). \[ \sigma_N \sqrt{T} = 0.0050 \times \sqrt{1.0} = 0.0050. \] \[ d = \frac{-0.0020}{0.0050} = -0.40. \] Evaluate normal distributions: \[ \mathcal{N}(-0.40) \approx 0.344578. \] \[ \phi(-0.40) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-0.16/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-0.08} \approx 0.398942 \times 0.923116 \approx 0.368270. \] Compute undiscounted payoff expectation: \[ -0.0020 \times 0.344578 + 0.0050 \times 0.368270 = -0.00068916 + 0.00184135 = 0.00115219. \] Multiply by \(P(0, 1.5) \tau = 1.005 \times 0.5 = 0.5025\): \[ \mathbf{Cpl}_N = 0.5025 \times 0.00115219 \approx 0.00057898 \text{ (or } 5.79 \text{ bps)}. \] The Bachelier caplet price is 5.79 basis points of notional.

Problem 7.10 (LIBOR transition to SOFR / RFR compounded rates): Following the post-2021 cessation of USD LIBOR, markets transitioned to the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR). Explain how forward rate modeling differs between forward-looking term LIBOR and backward-looking compounded-in-arrears SOFR.

Solution

Traditional LIBOR is a forward-looking term rate: \(L_k(T_k)\) is fixed at the beginning of the period \(T_k\) and pays at \(T_{k+1}\). SOFR is an overnight risk-free rate (RFR). SOFR derivatives settle on the backward-looking compounded daily rate over the accrual period: \[ R_{\text{SOFR}}(T_k, T_{k+1}) = \frac{1}{\tau_k}\left( \prod_{i=1}^{n} (1 + r_i \delta_i) - 1 \right) \approx \frac{1}{\tau_k}\left( \exp\left(\int_{T_k}^{T_{k+1}} r(u) du\right) - 1 \right). \] Consequences for market models: 1. Payment timing: Compounded SOFR is known only at the end of the accrual period \(T_{k+1}\), not at \(T_k\). 2. Option pricing: A caplet on compounded SOFR has payoff \(\tau_k \max(R_{\text{SOFR}} - K, 0)\) determined at \(T_{k+1}\). Under forward measure \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\), the forward compounded rate is still a martingale, but its volatility reflects the average daily overnight variance rather than a single term fixing shock. 3. Credit spread: SOFR is risk-free collateralized repo, eliminating the bank credit risk and liquidity premium embedded in legacy LIBOR.

Problem 7.11 (Stochastic volatility extensions of LMM: SABR-LMM): Why is standard log-normal LMM insufficient for pricing exotic interest rate options, and how does the SABR-LMM hybrid model incorporate the volatility smile?

Solution

Standard LMM assumes deterministic forward volatility \(\sigma_k(t)\), meaning implied Black volatilities across different strikes \(K\) for the same maturity are flat (no volatility smile or skew). In real debt markets, out-of-the-money caplets and swaptions exhibit steep smiles and skews driven by supply-demand imbalances and unspanned volatility risk. To capture smiles, Hagan et al. (2002) formulated the SABR model for each forward rate: \[ dL_k(t) = L_k(t)^\beta \alpha_k(t) dW_k^1(t), \quad d\alpha_k(t) = \nu \alpha_k(t) dW_k^2(t), \quad d\langle W_k^1, W_k^2\rangle = \rho dt. \] In SABR-LMM, each forward rate follows a SABR process with correlated Brownian motions across tenors. This allows desks to fit both the caplet volatility surface across strikes and tenors and the coterminal swaption matrix simultaneously, ensuring accurate pricing of Bermudan swaptions and CMS (constant maturity swap) spread options.

Problem 7.12 (Bermudan swaption pricing via Longstaff-Schwartz in LMM): Explain how the Longstaff-Schwartz (2001) Least Squares Monte Carlo (LSM) algorithm is applied to price Bermudan swaptions within a high-dimensional LIBOR Market Model.

Solution

A Bermudan swaption grants the holder the right to enter an interest rate swap at multiple exercise dates \(T_1, \dots, T_{M-1}\). Because the decision to exercise early depends on future curve paths, European analytical formulas cannot price it. In LMM with 20+ forward rates, backward PDE lattices suffer from the curse of dimensionality. Longstaff-Schwartz resolves this via regression on Monte Carlo paths: 1. Forward simulation: Simulate \(P\) paths of the full forward curve vector \(L(t)\) under the terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^N\) from \(t = 0\) to \(T_M\). 2. Backward induction: At final exercise date \(T_{M-1}\), the value is the immediate swap intrinsic value \(V(T_{M-1}) = \max(S_{\text{swap}}(T_{M-1}) - K, 0)\). 3. Conditional continuation value: At each prior date \(T_k\), regress the discounted future cash flows along in-the-money paths on a basis of state variables (e.g., current forward rates, swap rate, and curve slope): \(\hat{C}_k(L) = \sum_{p} \beta_p \phi_p(L_k)\). 4. Optimal stopping decision: Compare immediate exercise payoff against estimated continuation value \(\hat{C}_k\); exercise if immediate payoff is strictly higher. This delivers unbiased lower-bound Bermudan swaption prices in high-dimensional LMM simulations.

Retrieval quiz

  1. Under which pricing measure is the discrete forward rate \(L_k(t) = L(t; T_k, T_{k+1})\) a driftless martingale?

    Options

    \(L_k(t)\) is an affine function of the tradable asset ratio \(P(t, T_k)/P(t, T_{k+1})\), making it a martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\).

  2. Why does the LIBOR Market Model provide a theoretical justification for Black's (1976) caplet formula?

    Options

    Under \(\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}\), a driftless geometric Brownian motion forward rate yields an exact log-normal distribution, reproducing Black's formula.

  3. What is the sign of the drift adjustment for forward rates \(L_k(t)\) (\(k < N-1\)) under the terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^N\) when volatilities are positively correlated?

    Options

    The terminal bond numeraire gains value as rates fall, imposing a negative Girsanov drift on earlier forward rates.

  4. Under the terminal measure \(\mathbb{Q}^N\), what is the drift of the final forward rate \(L_{N-1}(t)\)?

    The terminal rate \(L_{N-1}\) has payment date \(T_N\), so \(\mathbb{Q}^N\) is its native forward measure, making its drift exactly 0.0.

  5. Why can caplets and European swaptions not be simultaneously log-normal in the same arbitrage-free model?

    Options

    A sum of correlated log-normal variables with stochastic discount weights is not log-normal, creating a mathematical incompatibility.

  6. What numerical improvement does the Predictor-Corrector scheme provide in LMM Monte Carlo simulations?

    Options

    Predictor-corrector achieves second-order drift accuracy \(\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)\), preventing drift accumulation on coarse time steps.

  7. How does market quote convention differ when interest rates enter zero or negative territory?

    Options

    Because \(\ln(L)\) is undefined for \(L \le 0\), market conventions shift to normal Bachelier volatilities quoted in basis points per year.

  8. What is Rebonato's approximation used for in term-structure modeling?

    Options

    Rebonato's formula freezes swap weights at date 0 to approximate swaption volatilities in closed form, speeding up correlation calibration.
