Latent state vectors governing interest rate dynamics cannot be observed directly in financial markets. Attempting to invert cross-sectional yields by assuming arbitrary maturities are priced without error introduces severe sample bias and discards curve information. State-space modeling coupled with Kalman filtering solves this fundamental dilemma: it separates physical time-series drift from risk-neutral cross-sectional pricing, treats all observed yields symmetrically with idiosyncratic measurement errors, and evaluates the exact prediction-error log-likelihood under Gaussian dynamics. This architecture is the computational backbone for central bank yield curve decomposition, real-time risk premia extraction, and macro-finance term-structure estimation.

1The Latent State Identification Dilemma

In a multi-factor affine term-structure model, the state vector \(X(t) \in \mathbb{R}^N\) evolves under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\) as a continuous-time diffusion:

\[ dX(t) = K^\mathbb{P} (\theta^\mathbb{P} - X(t)) dt + \Sigma dW^\mathbb{P}(t), \]

while cross-sectional zero-coupon bond prices \(P(t, t+\tau)\) and yields \(y(t, \tau)\) are determined under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) via the affine Riccati ODE solutions:

\[ y(t, \tau) = -\frac{1}{\tau} \ln P(t, t+\tau) = A(\tau) + B(\tau)^\top X(t), \]

where \(A(\tau) = -a(\tau)/\tau\) and \(B(\tau) = -b(\tau)/\tau\).

An econometrician observing \(M\) bond yields at each observation date \(t\) (\(M > N\), typically \(M \in \{6, \dots, 20\}\) tenors such as 3M, 6M, 1Y, 2Y, 5Y, 10Y, 30Y) faces an overdetermined system. If the model were exact, any \(N\) yields could be inverted to reveal \(X(t)\). However, real market yields are contaminated by microstructure noise, bid-ask spreads, quoting conventions, and idiosyncratic liquidity premia.

The Chen and Scott (1993) approach arbitrarily selects \(N\) tenors assumed to be measured with zero error, inverting \(X(t) = [B(\tau_{1:N})]^\top (Y_{1:N,t} - A(\tau_{1:N}))\), and treats the remaining \(M - N\) tenors as subject to error. This ad hoc selection biases parameter estimates, as different choices of "exact" maturities yield divergent physical persistence estimates and risk premia.

The state-space framework coupled with maximum likelihood estimation eliminates this arbitrary asymmetry by treating the entire state vector \(X(t)\) as unobserved (latent) and all \(M\) observed yields as noisy linear signals (Hamilton 1994, Chapter 13).

Definition 5.1 (Kalman-Filter MLE of Affine Models)

Kalman-filter maximum-likelihood estimation of affine models (Hamilton 1994, Chapter 13; Dai and Singleton 2000) is the statistical estimation methodology wherein an affine term-structure model is formulated as a discrete-time linear state-space system:

\[ \begin{aligned} X_{t+1} &= c + \Phi X_t + \eta_{t+1}, \quad &\eta_{t+1} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q), \\ Y_t &= A(\Theta^\mathbb{Q}) + H(\Theta^\mathbb{Q}) X_t + \varepsilon_t, \quad &\varepsilon_t &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, R), \end{aligned} \]

where the unobserved state vector \(X_t \in \mathbb{R}^N\) evolves under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\) according to a discrete VAR(1) transition, while the observed yield vector \(Y_t \in \mathbb{R}^M\) satisfies affine pricing restrictions under \(\mathbb{Q}\) contaminated by independent Gaussian measurement error \(\varepsilon_t\). The parameter vector \(\Theta = (\Theta^\mathbb{P}, \Theta^\mathbb{Q}, R)\) is estimated by maximizing the exact prediction-error decomposition log-likelihood computed via the linear Kalman filter recursion.

2Discrete-Time Transition and Measurement Equations

Let the observation frequency be \(\Delta t\) (e.g., \(\Delta t = 1/12\) for monthly sampling). Integrating the physical SDE \(dX(t) = K^\mathbb{P}(\theta^\mathbb{P} - X(t)) dt + \Sigma dW^\mathbb{P}(t)\) over the discrete interval \([t, t+\Delta t]\) yields an exact vector autoregression:

\[ X_{t+1} = c + \Phi X_t + \eta_{t+1}, \]

where the discrete-time transition parameters are defined analytically by matrix exponentials:

\[ \Phi = \exp(-K^\mathbb{P} \Delta t), \quad c = (I_N - \Phi)\theta^\mathbb{P}. \]

The discrete-time transition shock \(\eta_{t+1} = \int_t^{t+\Delta t} \exp(-K^\mathbb{P}(t+\Delta t - s)) \Sigma dW^\mathbb{P}(s)\) is mean-zero Gaussian with covariance matrix \(Q\) satisfying the continuous Lyapunov integral:

\[ Q = \int_0^{\Delta t} \exp(-K^\mathbb{P} s) \Sigma \Sigma^\top \exp(-(K^\mathbb{P})^\top s) ds. \]

When \(K^\mathbb{P} = \operatorname{diag}(\kappa_1^\mathbb{P}, \dots, \kappa_N^\mathbb{P})\) and \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_N)\) are diagonal, the \(i\)-th diagonal element of \(Q\) simplifies to:

\[ Q_{ii} = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{2\kappa_i^\mathbb{P}}\left(1 - \exp(-2\kappa_i^\mathbb{P}\Delta t)\right) \approx \sigma_i^2 \Delta t. \]

The measurement equation maps the latent state vector to the \(M\)-dimensional vector of observed yields \(Y_t = [y_t(\tau_1), \dots, y_t(\tau_M)]^\top\):

\[ Y_t = A + H X_t + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, R), \]

where \(A \in \mathbb{R}^M\) has rows \(A_m = A(\tau_m)\), \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}\) has rows \(H_{m,:} = B(\tau_m)^\top\), and \(R = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 I_M\) (or \(\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{\varepsilon,1}^2, \dots, \sigma_{\varepsilon,M}^2)\)) represents the measurement error covariance matrix (Dai and Singleton 2000).

Example 5.1 (Dimensions in a 3-Factor Model)

In a standard 3-factor Gaussian ATSM calibrated to monthly data across 6 maturities (\(\tau \in \{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10\}\) years):

  • State dimension \(N = 3\), measurement dimension \(M = 6\), time horizon \(T = 60\) months.
  • State transition: \(X_{t+1} \in \mathbb{R}^3\), \(\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\), \(c \in \mathbb{R}^3\), \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\).
  • Measurement: \(Y_t \in \mathbb{R}^6\), \(A \in \mathbb{R}^6\), \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 3}\), \(R \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 6}\).
  • At each date, 6 market yields provide signals to infer 3 latent state variables.

3The Linear Kalman Filter Recursion

The Kalman filter recursively computes optimal minimum mean squared error (MMSE) linear estimators of the unobserved state \(X_t\) conditional on information available up to date \(t\).

Theorem 5.1 (Forward Kalman Filter Recursion)

Let \(\hat{X}_{t|t-1} = \mathbb{E}[X_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\) and \(P_{t|t-1} = \operatorname{Var}(X_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})\) denote the one-step-ahead state prediction and its error covariance. Given the linear-Gaussian state-space system, the forward recursion proceeds as follows (Hamilton 1994, Chapter 13):

  1. State Prediction: \[ \hat{X}_{t|t-1} = c + \Phi \hat{X}_{t-1|t-1}, \quad P_{t|t-1} = \Phi P_{t-1|t-1} \Phi^\top + Q. \]
  2. Measurement Prediction & Innovation: \[ \hat{Y}_{t|t-1} = A + H \hat{X}_{t|t-1}, \quad v_t = Y_t - \hat{Y}_{t|t-1}. \]
  3. Innovation Covariance: \[ F_t = H P_{t|t-1} H^\top + R. \]
  4. Kalman Gain Matrix: \[ K_t = P_{t|t-1} H^\top F_t^{-1}. \]
  5. State Update & Updated Covariance: \[ \hat{X}_{t|t} = \hat{X}_{t|t-1} + K_t v_t, \quad P_{t|t} = (I_N - K_t H) P_{t|t-1}. \]
Proof

By the properties of joint Gaussian distributions, the conditional distribution of \(X_t\) given \(Y_t\) and the past information \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) is Gaussian. Since the joint distribution of \((X_t, Y_t)\) conditional on \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) is:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} X_t \\ Y_t \end{pmatrix} \;\Bigg|\; \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \begin{pmatrix} \hat{X}_{t|t-1} \\ A + H \hat{X}_{t|t-1} \end{pmatrix}, \; \begin{pmatrix} P_{t|t-1} & P_{t|t-1} H^\top \\ H P_{t|t-1} & H P_{t|t-1} H^\top + R \end{pmatrix} \right), \]

standard Gaussian conditioning gives \(\mathbb{E}[X_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \hat{X}_{t|t-1} + \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, Y_t) \operatorname{Var}(Y_t)^{-1} (Y_t - \hat{Y}_{t|t-1}) = \hat{X}_{t|t-1} + P_{t|t-1} H^\top F_t^{-1} v_t\). The conditional covariance is \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t) = P_{t|t-1} - P_{t|t-1} H^\top F_t^{-1} H P_{t|t-1} = (I_N - K_t H) P_{t|t-1}\).

Remark 5.1 (Joseph Form for Numerical Stability)

In finite-precision floating-point arithmetic, the standard covariance update \(P_{t|t} = (I_N - K_t H) P_{t|t-1}\) can lose symmetry and positive semi-definiteness. Practitioners enforce numerical stability either by symmetrizing \(P_{t|t} = \frac{1}{2}(P_{t|t} + P_{t|t}^\top)\) at each step or by employing the algebraically identical Joseph form: \[ P_{t|t} = (I_N - K_t H) P_{t|t-1} (I_N - K_t H)^\top + K_t R K_t^\top, \] which is guaranteed to remain positive semi-definite for any gain matrix \(K_t\).

4The Prediction-Error Log-Likelihood & Stationary Initialization

Because conditioning on the entire past history \(Y_{1:t-1}\) orthogonalizes successive observations via the innovations \(v_t\), the joint density of the observed yield sample \(\{Y_1, \dots, Y_T\}\) factors into a product of conditional densities:

\[ p(Y_1, \dots, Y_T; \Theta) = \prod_{t=1}^T p(Y_t \mid Y_{1:t-1}; \Theta). \]

Since each conditional distribution \(Y_t \mid Y_{1:t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(A + H \hat{X}_{t|t-1}, F_t)\) is Gaussian, the exact sample log-likelihood is given by (Hamilton 1994):

\[ \ln L(\Theta; Y) = -\frac{TM}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^T \ln \det(F_t) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^T v_t^\top F_t^{-1} v_t. \]

To initialize the recursion at \(t = 1\), the unconditional mean \(\hat{X}_{1|0}\) and unconditional covariance \(P_{1|0}\) of the stationary VAR(1) process are required:

\[ \hat{X}_{1|0} = (I_N - \Phi)^{-1} c = \theta^\mathbb{P}. \]

The unconditional covariance matrix \(P_{1|0}\) satisfies the discrete Lyapunov (Sylvester) equation:

\[ P_{1|0} = \Phi P_{1|0} \Phi^\top + Q. \]

Vectorizing both sides using the identity \(\operatorname{vec}(A B C) = (C^\top \otimes A)\operatorname{vec}(B)\) yields:

\[ \operatorname{vec}(P_{1|0}) = \left(I_{N^2} - \Phi \otimes \Phi\right)^{-1} \operatorname{vec}(Q). \]

This stationary initialization ensures that the initial observation \(Y_1\) is properly weighted in the likelihood function rather than treated as deterministic.

Lab 1 · State-Space Kalman Filter Log-Likelihood on Simulated Curve

Kalman filter recursive estimation on a simulated 3-factor Gaussian ATSM recovers latent state dynamics with cross-sectional pricing RMSE below 10 bps and innovation sequences exhibiting white-noise properties.

"""Lab FI2-L05-1: State-space Kalman filter log-likelihood on simulated curve.

SIMULATED: formulates a 3-factor Gaussian ATSM in state-space form, runs the forward Kalman
filter on simulated monthly yield observations over 60 periods, computes prediction innovations,
evaluates the Gaussian log-likelihood, and verifies innovation whiteness.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def solve_riccati(
    tau_grid: np.ndarray,
    delta0: float,
    kappa: np.ndarray,
    sigma: np.ndarray,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Analytical yield loadings for Gaussian ATSM."""
    n_tenors = len(tau_grid)
    a_vec = np.zeros(n_tenors, dtype=np.float64)
    b_mat = np.zeros((n_tenors, 3), dtype=np.float64)

    for m, tm in enumerate(tau_grid):
        for i in range(3):
            ki = kappa[i]
            si = sigma[i]
            b_mat[m, i] = -(1.0 - np.exp(-ki * tm)) / ki
            int_b2 = (1.0 / (ki**2)) * (
                tm - 2.0 * (1.0 - np.exp(-ki * tm)) / ki + (1.0 - np.exp(-2.0 * ki * tm)) / (2.0 * ki)
            )
            a_vec[m] += 0.5 * (si**2) * int_b2
        a_vec[m] -= delta0 * tm

    a_yield = -a_vec / tau_grid
    h_yield = -b_mat / tau_grid[:, np.newaxis]
    return a_yield, h_yield


def run_kalman(
    yields: np.ndarray,
    a_yield: np.ndarray,
    h_yield: np.ndarray,
    c_vec: np.ndarray,
    phi_mat: np.ndarray,
    q_cov: np.ndarray,
    r_cov: np.ndarray,
) -> tuple[float, np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Execute Kalman filter recursion and return log-likelihood and diagnostics."""
    n_periods, n_maturities = yields.shape
    n_states = 3

    # Stationary initialization
    i_kron = np.eye(n_states * n_states) - np.kron(phi_mat, phi_mat)
    cov_x0 = np.linalg.solve(i_kron, q_cov.flatten()).reshape((n_states, n_states))
    cov_x0 = 0.5 * (cov_x0 + cov_x0.T)
    mean_x0 = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(n_states) - phi_mat, c_vec)

    x_curr = mean_x0.copy()
    p_curr = cov_x0.copy()

    log_likelihood = 0.0
    filtered_states = np.zeros((n_periods, n_states), dtype=np.float64)
    innovations = np.zeros((n_periods, n_maturities), dtype=np.float64)
    pricing_errors = np.zeros((n_periods, n_maturities), dtype=np.float64)

    two_pi_m = n_maturities * np.log(2.0 * np.pi)

    for t in range(n_periods):
        # 1. State prediction
        x_pred = c_vec + phi_mat @ x_curr
        p_pred = phi_mat @ p_curr @ phi_mat.T + q_cov

        # 2. Measurement prediction
        y_pred = a_yield + h_yield @ x_pred
        v_t = yields[t] - y_pred
        innovations[t] = v_t

        # 3. Innovation covariance & log-likelihood
        f_cov = h_yield @ p_pred @ h_yield.T + r_cov
        f_inv_v = np.linalg.solve(f_cov, v_t)
        _sign, logdet = np.linalg.slogdet(f_cov)

        step_ll = -0.5 * (two_pi_m + logdet + float(v_t @ f_inv_v))
        log_likelihood += step_ll

        # 4. State update
        k_gain = (np.linalg.solve(f_cov, h_yield @ p_pred.T)).T
        x_curr = x_pred + k_gain @ v_t
        p_curr = (np.eye(n_states) - k_gain @ h_yield) @ p_pred
        p_curr = 0.5 * (p_curr + p_curr.T)

        filtered_states[t] = x_curr
        pricing_errors[t] = yields[t] - (a_yield + h_yield @ x_curr)

    return log_likelihood, filtered_states, innovations, pricing_errors


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
    n_periods = 60
    tenors = np.array([0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0], dtype=np.float64)
    n_maturities = len(tenors)

    delta0 = 0.025
    kappa = np.array([0.06, 0.28, 0.85], dtype=np.float64)
    sigma = np.array([0.010, 0.014, 0.018], dtype=np.float64)

    a_yield, h_yield = solve_riccati(tenors, delta0, kappa, sigma)

    c_vec = np.zeros(3, dtype=np.float64)
    phi_mat = np.diag([0.98, 0.92, 0.80])
    dt = 1.0 / 12.0
    q_cov = np.diag(sigma**2 * dt)
    meas_std = 0.0005  # 5 bps
    r_cov = np.eye(n_maturities) * (meas_std**2)

    # Simulate true state trajectory and yields
    x_true = np.zeros((n_periods, 3), dtype=np.float64)
    yields = np.zeros((n_periods, n_maturities), dtype=np.float64)
    for t in range(1, n_periods):
        eta = rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(3), q_cov)
        x_true[t] = phi_mat @ x_true[t - 1] + eta
    for t in range(n_periods):
        noise = rng.normal(0.0, meas_std, size=n_maturities)
        yields[t] = a_yield + h_yield @ x_true[t] + noise

    ll, x_filt, innov, errors = run_kalman(yields, a_yield, h_yield, c_vec, phi_mat, q_cov, r_cov)

    rmse_bps = float(np.sqrt(np.mean(errors**2)) * 10_000.0)
    corr_factors = [float(np.corrcoef(x_filt[:, i], x_true[:, i])[0, 1]) for i in range(3)]

    print("SIMULATED 3-Factor ATSM Kalman Filter Estimation Diagnostics:")
    print(f"  Sample length T = {n_periods} months, Maturities M = {n_maturities} tenors")
    print(f"  Total log-likelihood: {ll:.2f}")
    print(f"  Yield pricing RMSE: {rmse_bps:.2f} bps (injected noise: {meas_std * 10_000:.1f} bps)")
    print(f"  Latent factor correlations (filtered vs. true): {corr_factors}")
    print("Tenor | Mean Innov (bps) | Innov Std (bps) | Lag-1 Autocorr")
    print("-" * 55)
    for m, t in enumerate(tenors):
        v = innov[:, m]
        mean_bp = float(np.mean(v) * 10_000.0)
        std_bp = float(np.std(v, ddof=1) * 10_000.0)
        v_dm = v - np.mean(v)
        rho1 = float(np.sum(v_dm[:-1] * v_dm[1:]) / ((len(v) - 1) * np.var(v)))
        print(f" {t:5.2f}|     {mean_bp:+6.2f}     |     {std_bp:6.2f}    |     {rho1:+6.3f}")


main()

Where the canon breaks

In unconstrained ATSM estimation, the Kalman prediction-error log-likelihood surface is notoriously ill-behaved. When physical drift \(K^\mathbb{P}\) and risk-neutral drift \(K^\mathbb{Q}\) are estimated jointly with unrestricted market prices of risk \(\Lambda_1\), the information matrix is frequently singular or ill-conditioned (Dai and Singleton 2000, Section 4).

Furthermore, because interest rates are persistent (\(\Phi \approx 0.98\) monthly), finite samples suffer from severe Hurwicz-Kendall downward bias in persistence estimates. Uncorrected MLE yields biased physical dynamics, dramatically underestimating the true persistence of yield shifts and misrepresenting long-horizon term premia (Cochrane and Piazzesi 2005).

Misconceptions

  • Cross-sectional yield curve fitting identifies expected future interest rates and term premia.

    Fitting a term-structure model to a single cross-section of yields depends exclusively on risk-neutral dynamics (\(\mathbb{Q}\)). Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), expected returns on all bonds equal the risk-free rate, which is an artificial pricing artifact. To measure actual expected returns, term premia, or physical rate forecasts, one must identify the physical drift (\(\mathbb{P}\)) from historical time-series data (Cochrane and Piazzesi 2005).

  • Assuming \(N\) yields are priced without error in Chen-Scott inversion is an innocent computational convenience.

    Parameter estimates and risk premia are sensitive to which maturities are designated as "error-free." If the 3-month yield is chosen, money-market microstructure noise is forced into the latent state \(X_t\). If the 10-year yield is chosen, duration effects amplify measurement noise. The Kalman filter treats all \(M\) maturities symmetrically with explicit error variances \(\sigma_{\varepsilon, m}^2\), eliminating benchmark selection bias (Hamilton 1994).

  • The linear Kalman filter cannot be applied to models with square-root diffusions like CIR or \(A_m(N)\).

    While the standard Kalman filter is exact for Gaussian models (\(A_0(N)\)), it serves as a Quasi-Maximum Likelihood Estimator (QMLE) for affine models with square-root components. In the measurement equation \(Y_t = A + H X_t + \varepsilon_t\), the mapping remains strictly affine. The linear Kalman filter remains the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE), and the resulting quasi-log-likelihood yields consistent parameter estimates (Duffie and Kan 1996).

Problem set

Problem 5.1 (Exact discrete-time covariance integral): Let \(dX_t = -\kappa X_t dt + \sigma dW_t\) be a scalar Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Compute the exact discrete-time transition error variance \(Q = \operatorname{Var}\left(\int_t^{t+\Delta t} e^{-\kappa(t+\Delta t - s)}\sigma dW_s\right)\) for \(\kappa = 0.50\), \(\sigma = 0.02\), and \(\Delta t = 1/12\).

Solution

By Itô's isometry: \[ Q = \sigma^2 \int_0^{\Delta t} e^{-2\kappa u} du = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\left(1 - e^{-2\kappa \Delta t}\right). \] Substitute the parameter values: \(\kappa = 0.50\), \(\sigma = 0.02\), \(\Delta t = 1/12 \approx 0.083333\): \[ 2\kappa \Delta t = 2 \times 0.50 \times \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{12} \approx 0.083333. \] \[ e^{-2\kappa \Delta t} = e^{-1/12} \approx 0.920044 \implies 1 - e^{-2\kappa \Delta t} \approx 0.079956. \] \[ Q = \frac{(0.02)^2}{2(0.50)} \times 0.079956 = 0.0004 \times 0.079956 = 3.1982 \times 10^{-5}. \] The discrete variance is \(3.198 \times 10^{-5}\), slightly below the continuous approximation \(\sigma^2 \Delta t = 0.0004 / 12 \approx 3.333 \times 10^{-5}\).

Problem 5.2 (Woodbury matrix identity for Kalman innovation inversion): When \(M = 8\) yields are observed and \(N = 2\) latent factors are used, compute the size of the matrix inversion required under the standard formula vs the Woodbury identity.

Solution

Under the standard formula, \(F_t = H P_{t|t-1} H^\top + R\) requires inverting an \(M \times M = 8 \times 8\) matrix. Using the Woodbury identity with \(R = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 I_M\): \[ F_t^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2} I_M - \frac{1}{\sigma_\varepsilon^4} H \left( P_{t|t-1}^{-1} + \frac{1}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2} H^\top H \right)^{-1} H^\top, \] the matrix being inverted inside parentheses is \(N \times N = 2 \times 2\). Inverting a \(2 \times 2\) matrix requires only \(2^3 = 8\) operations compared to \(8^3 = 512\) operations for an \(8 \times 8\) matrix, providing a 64-fold computational speedup during MLE optimization.

Problem 5.3 (Innovation quadratic form expectation): For an \(M\)-dimensional observation vector \(Y_t\) with true parameter vector \(\Theta_0\), compute \(\mathbb{E}[v_t^\top F_t^{-1} v_t]\) and its variance across independent time steps.

Solution

Since \(v_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, F_t)\), we can define the standardized innovation vector \(u_t = F_t^{-1/2} v_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_M)\). The quadratic form is: \[ v_t^\top F_t^{-1} v_t = u_t^\top u_t = \sum_{m=1}^M u_{m,t}^2. \] Since \(u_{m,t} \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), the sum of their squares is distributed as a chi-squared random variable with \(M\) degrees of freedom: \[ v_t^\top F_t^{-1} v_t \sim \chi^2(M). \] Therefore, its expected value is \(\mathbb{E}[v_t^\top F_t^{-1} v_t] = M\) and its variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(v_t^\top F_t^{-1} v_t) = 2M\).

Problem 5.4 (Discrete Lyapunov equation uniqueness): Prove that the discrete Lyapunov equation \(P = \Phi P \Phi^\top + Q\) has a unique symmetric positive semi-definite solution when all eigenvalues of \(\Phi\) satisfy \(|\lambda_i(\Phi)| < 1\) and \(Q \ge 0\).

Solution

Vectorizing both sides: \(\operatorname{vec}(P) = \operatorname{vec}(\Phi P \Phi^\top) + \operatorname{vec}(Q) = (\Phi \otimes \Phi) \operatorname{vec}(P) + \operatorname{vec}(Q)\). Rearranging: \[ \left(I_{N^2} - \Phi \otimes \Phi\right) \operatorname{vec}(P) = \operatorname{vec}(Q). \] The eigenvalues of the Kronecker product \(\Phi \otimes \Phi\) are the pairwise products \(\lambda_i(\Phi)\lambda_j(\Phi)\) for \(i, j \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). Since \(|\lambda_i(\Phi)| < 1\) for all \(i\), we have \(|\lambda_i \lambda_j| < 1\). Thus, no eigenvalue of \(\Phi \otimes \Phi\) equals 1, making \(I_{N^2} - \Phi \otimes \Phi\) strictly invertible. Repeated forward substitution yields the Neumann series: \[ P = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \Phi^k Q (\Phi^\top)^k. \] Because \(\rho(\Phi) < 1\), \(\|\Phi^k\| \le C \rho^k \to 0\) geometrically, ensuring absolute convergence. Since each term \(\Phi^k Q (\Phi^\top)^k\) is symmetric and positive semi-definite, their sum \(P\) is uniquely defined, symmetric, and positive semi-definite.

Problem 5.5 (Proof of innovation whiteness): Prove that the innovation sequence \(v_t = Y_t - \mathbb{E}[Y_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\) satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[v_t v_{t-k}^\top] = 0\) for all \(k \ge 1\).

Solution

Consider the cross-covariance \(\mathbb{E}[v_t v_{t-k}^\top]\) for \(k \ge 1\). By the law of iterated expectations: \[ \mathbb{E}[v_t v_{t-k}^\top] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbb{E}[v_t v_{t-k}^\top \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] \right]. \] Because \(v_{t-k}\) is measurable with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_{t-k} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\), it can be factored out of the conditional expectation: \[ \mathbb{E}[v_t v_{t-k}^\top \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \mathbb{E}[v_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] v_{t-k}^\top. \] By definition, \(v_t = Y_t - \hat{Y}_{t|t-1}\) where \(\hat{Y}_{t|t-1} = \mathbb{E}[Y_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\). Therefore, \(\mathbb{E}[v_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\), which implies: \[ \mathbb{E}[v_t v_{t-k}^\top] = \mathbb{E}[0 \cdot v_{t-k}^\top] = 0. \] Hence, under the true model, the Kalman innovations are serially orthogonal white noise.

Problem 5.6 (Proof of Joseph form covariance equivalence): Prove that the Joseph form covariance update \(P_{t|t} = (I - K_t H) P_{t|t-1} (I - K_t H)^\top + K_t R K_t^\top\) reduces algebraically to \(P_{t|t} = (I - K_t H) P_{t|t-1}\) when \(K_t = P_{t|t-1} H^\top F_t^{-1}\).

Solution

Expand the product in the Joseph form: \[ P_{t|t} = (P_{t|t-1} - K_t H P_{t|t-1})(I - H^\top K_t^\top) + K_t R K_t^\top \] \[ = P_{t|t-1} - K_t H P_{t|t-1} - P_{t|t-1} H^\top K_t^\top + K_t H P_{t|t-1} H^\top K_t^\top + K_t R K_t^\top. \] Factor \(K_t\) and \(K_t^\top\) in the last two terms: \[ K_t (H P_{t|t-1} H^\top + R) K_t^\top = K_t F_t K_t^\top. \] Now substitute the optimal Kalman gain definition \(K_t = P_{t|t-1} H^\top F_t^{-1}\), which implies \(K_t F_t = P_{t|t-1} H^\top\): \[ K_t F_t K_t^\top = (P_{t|t-1} H^\top) K_t^\top. \] This exactly cancels the third term \(-P_{t|t-1} H^\top K_t^\top\): \[ P_{t|t} = P_{t|t-1} - K_t H P_{t|t-1} - P_{t|t-1} H^\top K_t^\top + P_{t|t-1} H^\top K_t^\top = (I - K_t H) P_{t|t-1}. \] This confirms exact algebraic equivalence.

Problem 5.7 (Scalar steady-state Kalman gain): In a 1-factor model with \(\Phi = 0.90\), \(Q = 0.0001\), \(H = 1.0\), and \(R = 0.00005\), solve the scalar Riccati equation for the steady-state prior variance \(P_\infty\) and find the steady-state Kalman gain \(K_\infty\).

Solution

The steady-state Riccati equation is: \[ P_\infty = \Phi^2 \frac{P_\infty R}{P_\infty + R} + Q. \] Substitute \(\Phi^2 = 0.81\), \(Q = 10^{-4}\), \(R = 5 \times 10^{-5}\): \[ P_\infty (P_\infty + 5 \times 10^{-5}) = 0.81 P_\infty (5 \times 10^{-5}) + 10^{-4} (P_\infty + 5 \times 10^{-5}). \] \[ P_\infty^2 + 5 \times 10^{-5} P_\infty = 4.05 \times 10^{-5} P_\infty + 10^{-4} P_\infty + 5 \times 10^{-9}. \] \[ P_\infty^2 - 9.05 \times 10^{-5} P_\infty - 5 \times 10^{-9} = 0. \] Solving the quadratic equation: \[ P_\infty = \frac{9.05 \times 10^{-5} + \sqrt{(9.05 \times 10^{-5})^2 + 4(5 \times 10^{-9})}}{2} = \frac{9.05 \times 10^{-5} + \sqrt{8.19 \times 10^{-9} + 2.0 \times 10^{-8}}}{2} \] \[ \sqrt{2.819 \times 10^{-8}} \approx 1.679 \times 10^{-4}. \] \[ P_\infty = \frac{0.0000905 + 0.0001679}{2} \approx 0.0001292. \] The steady-state innovation variance is \(F_\infty = P_\infty + R = 0.0001292 + 0.00005 = 0.0001792\). The steady-state Kalman gain is: \[ K_\infty = \frac{P_\infty}{F_\infty} = \frac{0.0001292}{0.0001792} \approx 0.721. \] The filter places 72.1% weight on the new yield innovation and 27.9% weight on the prior state prediction.

Problem 5.8 (Hurwicz bias in monthly yield curve data): In a monthly sample of \(T = 120\) observations, the true AR(1) persistence is \(\Phi = 0.98\). Using Kendall's approximation \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\Phi} - \Phi] \approx -\frac{1 + 3\Phi}{T}\), compute the biased estimate \(\hat{\Phi}\) and its implied annualized mean-reversion speed \(\kappa = -12 \ln \Phi\).

Solution

Kendall's bias approximation is: \[ \operatorname{Bias} \approx -\frac{1 + 3(0.98)}{120} = -\frac{3.94}{120} \approx -0.03283. \] The expected OLS estimate is: \[ \mathbb{E}[\hat{\Phi}] \approx 0.98 - 0.03283 = 0.94717. \] The true annualized mean-reversion rate is: \[ kappa_{\text{true}} = -12 \ln(0.98) \approx -12 \times (-0.02020) = 0.2424 \text{ year}^{-1}, \] with a half-life of \(\frac{\ln 2}{0.2424} \approx 2.86\) years. The estimated annualized mean-reversion rate is: \[ \kappa_{\text{est}} = -12 \ln(0.94717) \approx -12 \times (-0.05427) = 0.6512 \text{ year}^{-1}, \] with a half-life of \(\frac{\ln 2}{0.6512} \approx 1.06\) years. The finite-sample downward bias in \(\Phi\) causes an almost 3-fold overestimation of the speed of mean reversion.

Problem 5.9 (Ljung-Box test on Kalman innovations): Over \(T = 240\) months, the sample autocorrelation of the 5-year yield innovation has \(\hat{\rho}_1 = 0.28\), \(\hat{\rho}_2 = 0.15\), and \(\hat{\rho}_3 = 0.10\). Compute the Ljung-Box test statistic \(Q_{LB}(3) = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^3 \frac{\hat{\rho}_k^2}{T-k}\) and test at the \(\alpha = 0.01\) level (\(\chi^2_{0.01}(3) = 11.345\)).

Solution

Compute each term in the sum: \[ \frac{\hat{\rho}_1^2}{T-1} = \frac{0.28^2}{239} = \frac{0.0784}{239} \approx 0.00032803 \] \[ \frac{\hat{\rho}_2^2}{T-2} = \frac{0.15^2}{238} = \frac{0.0225}{238} \approx 0.00009454 \] \[ \frac{\hat{\rho}_3^2}{T-3} = \frac{0.10^2}{237} = \frac{0.0100}{237} \approx 0.00004219 \] Sum of terms: \(0.00032803 + 0.00009454 + 0.00004219 = 0.00046476\). Multiply by \(T(T+2) = 240 \times 242 = 58,080\): \[ Q_{LB}(3) = 58,080 \times 0.00046476 \approx 26.99. \] Since \(Q_{LB}(3) = 26.99 > 11.345\), we reject the null hypothesis of white noise innovations at the 1% significance level. The 5-year yield innovations contain persistent predictable dynamics not captured by the current model specification.

Problem 5.10 (Cross-sectional RMSE vs model dimensionality): In empirical term-structure studies (e.g. Dai and Singleton 2000), typical pricing RMSE values are: 1-factor model \(\approx 35\) bps, 2-factor model \(\approx 15\) bps, 3-factor model \(\approx 5\) bps, 4-factor model \(\approx 4.5\) bps. Explain why the marginal benefit of adding a fourth factor is minimal for sovereign bonds.

Solution

Principal component analysis (Litterman and Scheinkman 1991) establishes that three factors—level, slope, and curvature—explain over 99.5% of the total variance of sovereign bond yields. Moving from 1 to 2 factors captures the curve slope, cutting RMSE from 35 bps to 15 bps. Moving from 2 to 3 factors captures the hump (curvature), reducing RMSE to 5 bps, which is comparable to the typical bid-ask spread and microstructure noise in Treasury markets. A fourth factor captures less than 0.3% of curve variance, often picking up localized ten-year auction cycle effects or butterfly distortions rather than systematic macroeconomic risk. Because the noise floor of the yield curve is approximately 3–5 bps, adding a fourth factor risks overfitting in-sample while failing to improve out-of-sample forecast accuracy.

Problem 5.11 (Adrian-Crump-Moench linear regression vs Kalman MLE): Adrian, Crump, and Moench (2013) introduced an alternative estimation methodology based on 5 linear OLS regressions using principal components. Contrast the numerical stability and factor interpretation between ACM and Kalman filter MLE.

Solution

ACM avoids non-linear numerical optimization entirely by taking the first \(N\) principal components of yields as observable state variables. Advantages of ACM: 1. Computational speed: Runs in milliseconds via closed-form OLS regressions without gradient-based search or local minima. 2. Robustness: Never suffers from convergence failure, making it ideal for daily real-time central bank monitoring. Trade-offs: 1. Factor noise: By assuming PCA factors are exact linear combinations of yields, ACM assumes they are measured without error. If yields contain measurement error, PCA factors inherit that noise, creating errors-in-variables bias in the regression coefficients. 2. Kalman filter MLE: Optimally filters out idiosyncratic noise across all maturities simultaneously and handles missing data or irregular tenors, though it requires non-convex numerical optimization.

Problem 5.12 (Zero lower bound and shadow-rate filtering): At the zero lower bound, market yields are non-linear in state variables due to currency optionality: \(r_t = \max(s_t, 0)\). Explain why the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) struggles when the shadow rate \(s_t\) is deeply negative.

Solution

In the Black (1995) shadow-rate model, the short rate is \(r_t = \max(s_t, 0)\), which acts as a call option on the shadow rate \(s_t\). Zero-coupon yields become non-linear functions \(y_t(\tau) = g(X_t; \tau)\). The Extended Kalman Filter linearizes \(g(X_t)\) around the prior estimate \(\hat{X}_{t|t-1}\) using the Jacobian \(H_t = \left.\frac{\partial g}{\partial X}\right|_{\hat{X}_{t|t-1}}\). When the shadow rate is deeply negative (\(s_t \ll 0\)), the call option is deeply out-of-the-money, so \(\frac{\partial g}{\partial X} \approx 0\) for short maturities. Consequently, the Kalman gain for short maturities collapses to zero (\(K_t \approx 0\)), leaving the filter unable to update the latent shadow state from short-end yields. To resolve this, econometricians use the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) or particle filters, which evaluate the full non-linear distribution without relying on local derivatives.
