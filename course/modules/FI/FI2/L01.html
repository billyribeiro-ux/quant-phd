Every fixed-income security—from plain-vanilla Treasury bills to exotic Bermudan swaptions—derives its fundamental economic value from the trajectory of instantaneous borrowing rates. In continuous time, pricing the entire infinite-dimensional term structure requires specifying a unified no-arbitrage law of motion. A short-rate model provides the foundational engine: by anchoring pricing to an instantaneous stochastic rate under an equivalent martingale measure, it bridges microscopic interest rate uncertainty with cross-sectional curve geometry.

1The Instantaneous Short Rate and the Money-Market Numeraire

In discrete time, interest is compounded across discrete intervals \(\Delta t\). In continuous time, the core state variable is the instantaneous borrowing rate.

Definition 1.1 (Instantaneous Short Rate and Money-Market Account)

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}, \mathbb{P})\) be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions. The instantaneous short rate \(r(t)\) is the continuous limit of the instantaneous forward rate for borrowing at time \(t\) over an infinitesimal increment \(dt\):

\[ \begin{aligned} r(t) &= \lim_{T \downarrow t} f(t, T) \\ &= -\left.\frac{\partial \ln P(t, T)}{\partial T}\right|_{T=t} \end{aligned} \]

where \(P(t, T)\) is the price at time \(t\) of a zero-coupon bond maturing at \(T\). The continuous-time money-market account (bank account numeraire) \(B(t)\) evolves according to the ordinary differential equation:

\[ dB(t) = r(t) B(t) dt, \quad B(0) = 1 \implies B(t) = \exp\left(\int_0^t r(s) ds\right) \]

A short-rate model specifies a stochastic differential equation (SDE) for \(r(t)\) under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\) or the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), determining the prices of all zero-coupon bonds as conditional expectations of the stochastic discount factor.

Remark 1.1 (Idealization vs. Market Reality)

No tradeable instrument pays the literal instantaneous rate \(r(t)\). In financial markets, the closest empirical proxies are overnight collateralized rates such as SOFR or the effective federal funds rate (Hull 2021, Chapter 4). The short rate is an analytical construct that acts as the operational state variable driving continuous discounting.

2No-Arbitrage Pricing and the Term-Structure PDE

To value bonds across the entire maturity spectrum, we construct a dynamic hedging portfolio between bonds of different maturities, eliminating instantaneous Brownian risk.

Theorem 1.1 (Term-Structure Black-Scholes PDE)

Assume the short rate \(r(t)\) follows the continuous Markov diffusion under physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\):

\[ dr(t) = \mu(t, r) dt + \sigma(t, r) dW(t)^\mathbb{P} \]

where \(\mu(t, r)\) and \(\sigma(t, r)\) satisfy Lipschitz continuity and linear growth conditions. Let \(P(t, T) = F(t, r; T)\) denote the price of a default-free zero-coupon bond maturing at \(T\) with payoff \(F(T, r; T) = 1\). If the market admits no arbitrage, there exists a market price of risk \(\lambda(t, r)\) such that \(F(t, r; T)\) satisfies the partial differential equation:

\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial t} + \left[\mu(t, r) - \lambda(t, r)\sigma(t, r)\right] \frac{\partial F}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma(t, r)^2 \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial r^2} - r F = 0 \]

subject to terminal boundary condition \(F(T, r; T) = 1\).

Proof

By Itô's lemma, the bond price \(P_1 = F(t, r; T_1)\) follows:

\[ dP_1 = \mu_1 P_1 dt + \sigma_1 P_1 dW^\mathbb{P}, \quad \text{where } \mu_1 = \frac{F_t + \mu F_r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 F_{rr}}{F}, \quad \sigma_1 = \frac{\sigma F_r}{F} \]

Construct a portfolio consisting of one unit of bond \(T_1\) and \(-\Delta\) units of bond \(T_2\). Its value is \(\Pi = P_1 - \Delta P_2\). Setting \(\Delta = \frac{\sigma_1 P_1}{\sigma_2 P_2} = \frac{F_r(t, r; T_1)}{F_r(t, r; T_2)}\) eliminates the \(dW^\mathbb{P}\) term entirely. In the absence of arbitrage, this riskless portfolio must earn the instantaneous risk-free rate:

\[ d\Pi = (\mu_1 P_1 - \Delta \mu_2 P_2) dt = r \Pi dt = r (P_1 - \Delta P_2) dt \]

Rearranging yields:

\[ \frac{\mu_1 - r}{\sigma_1} = \frac{\mu_2 - r}{\sigma_2} = \lambda(t, r) \]

Because \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) are arbitrary, this Sharpe ratio \(\lambda(t, r)\) must be independent of maturity \(T\). Substituting \(\mu_1\) and \(\sigma_1\) into \(\frac{\mu_1 - r}{\sigma_1} = \lambda(t, r)\) yields the term-structure PDE.

Theorem 1.2 (Feynman-Kac Risk-Neutral Representation)

Define the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) via the Radon-Nikodym derivative \(d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P} = \exp(-\int_0^T \lambda(s, r) dW(s)^\mathbb{P} - \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \lambda(s, r)^2 ds)\). Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), Girsanov's theorem establishes that \(W^\mathbb{Q}(t) = W^\mathbb{P}(t) + \int_0^t \lambda(s, r) ds\) is a standard Brownian motion, and the short-rate drift becomes \(\mu^\mathbb{Q}(t, r) = \mu(t, r) - \lambda(t, r)\sigma(t, r)\):

\[ dr(t) = \mu^\mathbb{Q}(t, r) dt + \sigma(t, r) dW^\mathbb{Q}(t) \]

By the Feynman-Kac theorem, the solution to the term-structure PDE is the conditional expectation:

\[ P(t, T) = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[ \exp\left(-\int_t^T r(s) ds\right) \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_t \right] \]
Proof

Consider the discounted bond process \(Z(s) = \exp(-\int_t^s r(u) du) F(s, r(s); T)\) for \(s \in [t, T]\). Applying Itô's lemma to \(Z(s)\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\) reveals that its drift is proportional to \(F_s + \mu^\mathbb{Q} F_r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 F_{rr} - r F\), which vanishes by the term-structure PDE. Thus \(Z(s)\) is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-martingale. Equating \(Z(t) = F(t, r; T) = P(t, T)\) with \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[Z(T) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\exp(-\int_t^T r(s) ds) \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) concludes the proof.

Lab 1 · Short-Rate Euler-Maruyama Simulation and Yield Curve Extraction

Simulating a mean-reverting short-rate process under Q with r0 = 0.035, kappa = 0.25, theta = 0.045, and sigma = 0.015 produces zero-coupon bond prices that decrease monotonically with maturity while spot yields increase toward theta.

"""Lab FI2-L01-1: Short-rate Euler-Maruyama simulation and yield curve extraction.

SIMULATED: simulates a 1-factor risk-neutral short-rate trajectory under Q, computes
pathwise money-market accumulation factors, and extracts zero-coupon bond prices
and annualized spot rates across tenors [0.5Y, 1.0Y, 2.0Y, 5.0Y].
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def simulate_short_rate_paths(
    r0: float,
    kappa: float,
    theta: float,
    sigma: float,
    n_paths: int,
    n_steps: int,
    dt: float,
    rng: np.random.Generator,
) -> np.ndarray:
    """Simulate short-rate paths via Euler-Maruyama scheme under Q."""
    paths = np.zeros((n_paths, n_steps + 1), dtype=np.float64)
    paths[:, 0] = r0
    sqrt_dt = np.sqrt(dt)

    for step in range(n_steps):
        r_t = paths[:, step]
        dw = rng.normal(0.0, sqrt_dt, size=n_paths)
        dr = kappa * (theta - r_t) * dt + sigma * dw
        paths[:, step + 1] = r_t + dr

    return paths


def compute_bond_prices(
    paths: np.ndarray,
    tenors: list[float],
    dt: float,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Compute Monte Carlo zero-coupon bond prices and spot yields."""
    paths.shape[0]
    prices = []
    yields = []

    for tenor in tenors:
        step_idx = round(tenor / dt)
        # Trapezoidal rule for integral of r_s from 0 to tenor
        integral_r = dt * (0.5 * paths[:, 0] + np.sum(paths[:, 1:step_idx], axis=1) + 0.5 * paths[:, step_idx])
        discount_factors = np.exp(-integral_r)
        price = float(np.mean(discount_factors))
        spot_yield = -np.log(price) / tenor
        prices.append(price)
        yields.append(spot_yield)

    return np.array(prices, dtype=np.float64), np.array(yields, dtype=np.float64)


def main() -> None:
    seed = 42
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    # Parameters for short-rate process under Q
    r0 = 0.035  # Initial short rate: 3.50%
    kappa = 0.25  # Mean-reversion speed
    theta = 0.045  # Long-term mean under Q: 4.50%
    sigma = 0.015  # Instantaneous volatility: 1.50%

    tenors = [0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0]
    max_tenor = max(tenors)
    dt = 1.0 / 252.0  # Daily time step (252 days per year)
    n_steps = round(max_tenor / dt)
    n_paths = 20_000

    paths = simulate_short_rate_paths(r0, kappa, theta, sigma, n_paths, n_steps, dt, rng)
    prices, spot_yields = compute_bond_prices(paths, tenors, dt)

    print("SIMULATED Short-Rate Term Structure (Euler-Maruyama Monte Carlo):")
    print(f"  Initial rate r(0) = {r0:.4f}, kappa = {kappa:.2f}, theta = {theta:.4f}, sigma = {sigma:.4f}")
    print(f"  Simulated paths = {n_paths}, step size dt = {dt:.6f} years")
    print("Tenor (Y) | Bond Price P(0, T) | Spot Yield y(0, T)")
    print("-" * 50)
    for tenor, p, y in zip(tenors, prices, spot_yields, strict=True):
        print(f"  {tenor:5.1f}   |      {p:8.6f}      |      {y * 100:6.3f}%")


main()

Where the canon breaks

One-factor short-rate models suffer from an insurmountable structural limitation: they impose perfect cross-sectional correlation across all bond yields. In any one-factor Markov model where \(P(t, T) = F(t, r(t); T)\), the instantaneous return of every bond is driven by the exact same Brownian shock \(dW^\mathbb{Q}(t)\):

\[ \frac{dP(t, T)}{P(t, T)} = r(t) dt + \sigma(t, r) \frac{\partial \ln F(t, r; T)}{\partial r} dW^\mathbb{Q}(t) \]

Empirical principal component analysis of Treasury yields demonstrates that one factor explains approximately 85% to 90% of yield curve variance, but fails completely to capture slope rotations and curvature shifts (Dai and Singleton 2000, Section 1). Hedging a 10-year Treasury bond with a 2-year note under a one-factor model leaves substantial residual variance exposed to non-parallel curve twists.

Misconceptions

  • Zero-coupon bond prices can be computed by discounting at the expected physical short rate: \(P(t, T) = \exp(-\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{P}[\int_t^T r(s) ds])\).

    This is doubly false. First, Jensen's inequality implies \(\mathbb{E}[\exp(-X)] > \exp(-\mathbb{E}[X])\). Second, physical expectation \(\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{P}\) ignores the market price of interest rate risk. Pricing must take expectations under the equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), accounting for covariance between interest rates and the pricing kernel.

  • The market price of risk \(\lambda(t, r)\) depends on the maturity \(T\) of the bond being priced.

    By no-arbitrage, the market price of risk \(\lambda(t, r) = \frac{\mu(t, T) - r}{\sigma(t, T)}\) must be strictly identical across all maturities \(T\). If two bonds had different Sharpe ratios with respect to the same short-rate shock, an investor could construct an arbitrage portfolio by longing the higher-Sharpe bond and shorting the lower-Sharpe bond with zero net risk.

  • Because the short rate is riskless over an infinitesimal interval \(dt\), long-term bond returns must equal the risk-free rate over long holding periods.

    While the instantaneous return on any bond under \(\mathbb{Q}\) is \(r(t) dt\), long-term holding period returns under \(\mathbb{P}\) carry substantial term premia. Investors bear duration risk, so expected returns over finite horizons exceed the compounded cash rate when the term premium is positive (Cochrane and Piazzesi 2005).

Problem set

Problem 1.1 (Money-market accumulation): Suppose the short rate is constant at \(r(t) = 0.045\) for \(t \in [0, 2]\) and then steps up to \(r(t) = 0.055\) for \(t \in (2, 5]\). Calculate the exact value of the money market account \(B(5)\) at time \(t = 5\) assuming \(B(0) = 1\).

Solution

The money-market account evolves as \(B(t) = \exp(\int_0^t r(s) ds)\). We split the integral into two piecewise intervals: \[ \int_0^5 r(s) ds = \int_0^2 0.045 \, ds + \int_2^5 0.055 \, ds = (0.045 \times 2) + (0.055 \times 3) = 0.090 + 0.165 = 0.255 \] Then \(B(5) = \exp(0.255) \approx 1.29046\). The money market account accumulates to approximately $1.29046 per dollar invested.

Problem 1.2 (Spot yield extraction): A zero-coupon bond maturing in \(\tau = 4\) years trades at a clean price of \(P(0, 4) = 0.8350\). Calculate the continuously compounded spot rate \(y(0, 4)\) and the effective annual yield \(y_{\text{ann}}\).

Solution

The continuously compounded spot rate satisfies \(P(0, \tau) = \exp(-y(0, \tau) \tau)\): \[ y(0, 4) = -\frac{\ln P(0, 4)}{4} = -\frac{\ln(0.8350)}{4} = -\frac{-0.18032}{4} \approx 0.04508 = 4.508\% \] The annual compounding yield satisfies \(P(0, 4) = (1 + y_{\text{ann}})^{-4}\): \[ y_{\text{ann}} = (0.8350)^{-1/4} - 1 = (1.19760)^{0.25} - 1 \approx 1.04611 - 1 = 4.611\% \] The continuous rate is 4.508% and annual rate is 4.611%.

Problem 1.3 (Forward rate relation): Zero-coupon bond prices for 1-year and 3-year maturities are \(P(0, 1) = 0.9650\) and \(P(0, 3) = 0.8850\). Calculate the continuously compounded forward rate \(f(0; 1, 3)\) for borrowing from year 1 to year 3.

Solution

By no-arbitrage, \(P(0, 3) = P(0, 1) \exp(-f(0; 1, 3) \times (3 - 1))\). Solving for the forward rate: \[ f(0; 1, 3) = \frac{\ln P(0, 1) - \ln P(0, 3)}{3 - 1} = \frac{\ln(0.9650 / 0.8850)}{2} = \frac{\ln(1.09040)}{2} \approx \frac{0.08654}{2} = 0.04327 = 4.327\% \] The implied forward borrowing rate from \(T_1 = 1\) to \(T_2 = 3\) is 4.327% per annum.

Problem 1.4 (Uniqueness of market price of risk): Prove that in a one-factor continuous-time economy with two non-redundant zero-coupon bonds, the presence of distinct Sharpe ratios \(\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2\) permits the construction of a zero-net-investment arbitrage portfolio.

Solution

Let bond 1 and bond 2 have dynamics \(dP_i / P_i = \mu_i dt + \sigma_i dW^\mathbb{P}\) with \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2 > 0\). Suppose \(\frac{\mu_1 - r}{\sigma_1} > \frac{\mu_2 - r}{\sigma_2}\). Form a portfolio with \(\omega_1 = \frac{1}{\sigma_1}\) dollars in bond 1, \(\omega_2 = -\frac{1}{\sigma_2}\) dollars in bond 2, and finance the net difference \(\omega_B = -(\omega_1 + \omega_2) = \frac{1}{\sigma_2} - \frac{1}{\sigma_1}\) via the money-market account. The net portfolio value is \(\Pi = \omega_1 + \omega_2 + \omega_B = 0\) (zero initial capital). The instantaneous dollar payoff is: \[ d\Pi = \omega_1 dP_1/P_1 + \omega_2 dP_2/P_2 + \omega_B r dt = \left( \frac{\mu_1 - r}{\sigma_1} - \frac{\mu_2 - r}{\sigma_2} \right) dt + \left( \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_1} - \frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_2} \right) dW^\mathbb{P} \] The Brownian shock cancels completely: \((1 - 1) dW^\mathbb{P} = 0\). The portfolio produces a strictly positive, riskless deterministic return: \[ d\Pi = \left( \lambda_1 - \lambda_2 \right) dt > 0 \] This is a textbook arbitrage: positive return with zero capital and zero downside risk. Hence, no-arbitrage requires \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda\).

Problem 1.5 (Local martingale property of discounted bonds): Prove that under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the discounted zero-coupon bond process \(\tilde{P}(t, T) = P(t, T) / B(t)\) is a local martingale.

Solution

Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the short-rate SDE is \(dr = \mu^\mathbb{Q} dt + \sigma dW^\mathbb{Q}\). Let \(P(t, T) = F(t, r; T)\). Applying Itô's lemma to \(F(t, r; T)\): \[ dF = \left[ F_t + \mu^\mathbb{Q} F_r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 F_{rr} \right] dt + \sigma F_r dW^\mathbb{Q} \] By the term-structure PDE, the bracketed drift term equals \(r F\). Thus \(dF = r F dt + \sigma F_r dW^\mathbb{Q}\). Next, consider \(\tilde{F} = F / B = F \exp(-\int_0^t r_s ds)\). Applying integration by parts (or Itô's product rule) to \(Z(t) = B(t)^{-1} F(t)\): \[ d\tilde{F} = d(B^{-1}) F + B^{-1} dF + d[B^{-1}, F] = (-r B^{-1} dt) F + B^{-1} (r F dt + \sigma F_r dW^\mathbb{Q}) + 0 \] The \(dt\) terms cancel identically: \(-r B^{-1} F dt + r B^{-1} F dt = 0\). We obtain: \[ d\tilde{F} = \frac{\sigma F_r}{B} dW^\mathbb{Q} \] Because \(d\tilde{F}\) has zero drift, \(\tilde{P}(t, T)\) is a continuous local martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Problem 1.6 (Bond price derivative at maturity): Prove that for any smooth term-structure model, the zero-coupon bond price satisfies the initial derivative condition \(\lim_{T \downarrow t} \frac{\partial P(t, T)}{\partial T} = -r(t)\).

Solution

From the risk-neutral pricing representation, \(P(t, T) = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[\exp(-\int_t^T r(s) ds) \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\). By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, for small \(\tau = T - t\), \(\int_t^T r(s) ds = r(t) \tau + o(\tau)\). Taylor expanding the exponential: \[ \exp\left(-\int_t^T r(s) ds\right) = 1 - \int_t^T r(s) ds + \frac{1}{2}\left(\int_t^T r(s) ds\right)^2 - \cdots \] Taking conditional expectation: \[ P(t, T) = 1 - \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}\left[\int_t^T r(s) ds \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_t\right] + O(\tau^2) = 1 - r(t)(T - t) + O((T - t)^2) \] Differentiating with respect to \(T\) and taking the limit as \(T \downarrow t\): \[ \left.\frac{\partial P(t, T)}{\partial T}\right|_{T=t} = \lim_{T \downarrow t} \frac{P(t, T) - P(t, t)}{T - t} = \lim_{T \downarrow t} \frac{1 - r(t)(T - t) - 1}{T - t} = -r(t) \] This confirms that the instantaneous yield at zero maturity equals the short rate: \(y(t, t) = -\lim_{T \downarrow t} \frac{\ln P(t, T)}{T - t} = r(t)\).

Problem 1.7 (Daily SOFR simulation): Suppose the daily short rate evolves as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with \(\kappa = 0.50\), \(\theta = 0.040\), and \(\sigma = 0.012\). If yesterday's rate was \(r_0 = 0.052\) and today's standardized Gaussian shock is \(Z = -1.80\), compute today's short rate using an Euler step with \(dt = 1/252\).

Solution

Under the Euler-Maruyama discretization, \(\Delta r = \kappa(\theta - r_0) \Delta t + \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} Z\). With \(\Delta t = 1/252 \approx 0.0039683\) and \(\sqrt{\Delta t} \approx 0.062994\): \[ \Delta r = 0.50 \times (0.040 - 0.052) \times 0.0039683 + 0.012 \times 0.062994 \times (-1.80) \] Calculating the drift and diffusion contributions: \[ \text{Drift} = 0.50 \times (-0.012) \times 0.0039683 = -0.00002381 \quad (-0.24 \text{ bps}) \] \[ \text{Diffusion} = 0.012 \times 0.062994 \times (-1.80) = -0.00136067 \quad (-13.61 \text{ bps}) \] Total change: \(\Delta r = -0.00002381 - 0.00136067 = -0.00138448\). Today's rate is: \[ r_1 = r_0 + \Delta r = 0.052 - 0.0013845 = 0.0506155 \approx 5.062\% \] The short rate drops by 13.85 bps to 5.062%.

Problem 1.8 (Convexity bias quantification): In a simple Gaussian short-rate framework, the forward rate \(f(t, T)\) differs from expected future spot rates by a convexity adjustment: \(f(t, T) = \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{Q}[r(T) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] - \frac{1}{2} \sigma_P(t, T)^2\). If a 10-year zero-coupon bond has volatility \(\sigma_P = 0.15\) (15% price volatility), compute the convexity adjustment in basis points.

Solution

The convexity adjustment arises from Jensen's inequality when converting between prices and yields. The convexity term is: \[ \text{Adjustment} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma_P^2 = \frac{1}{2} (0.15)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.0225 = 0.01125 = 112.5 \text{ bps} \] This means the forward rate is depressed by 112.5 basis points relative to the expected future short rate due to bond price convexity. Higher volatility strictly lowers long-term forward rates.

Problem 1.9 (Negative rate probability in Gaussian model): If the short rate follows a Gaussian distribution with mean \(\mathbb{E}[r(t)] = 0.025\) and standard deviation \(\text{SD}(r(t)) = 0.015\), compute the exact probability that the short rate is negative: \(\mathbb{P}(r(t) < 0)\).

Solution

Because \(r(t)\) is Gaussian, we standardize the variable: \[ Z = \frac{0 - \mathbb{E}[r(t)]}{\text{SD}(r(t))} = \frac{0 - 0.025}{0.015} = -\frac{5}{3} \approx -1.6667 \] Using the standard normal cumulative distribution function \(\Phi(-1.6667)\): \[ \mathbb{P}(r(t) < 0) = \Phi(-1.6667) \approx 0.04779 = 4.78\% \] There is an approximate 4.78% probability of observing negative interest rates under these Gaussian parameters.

Problem 1.10 (Affine term structure admissibility): Consider a general diffusion \(dr = \mu(r) dt + \sigma(r) dW\). Explain why the requirement that bond prices be exponentially affine in the state variable, \(P(t, T) = \exp(A(T-t) - B(T-t) r)\), restricts \(\mu(r)\) and \(\sigma(r)^2\) to be strictly linear in \(r\).

Solution

Substitute the exponentially affine ansatz \(P(t, T) = \exp(A(\tau) - B(\tau) r)\) into the term-structure PDE. The derivatives are \(P_t = -P (A' - B' r)\), \(P_r = -B P\), and \(P_{rr} = B^2 P\). Dividing by \(P\) gives: \[ -A'(\tau) + B'(\tau) r - B(\tau) \mu(r) + \frac{1}{2} B(\tau)^2 \sigma(r)^2 - r = 0 \] This equation must hold identically for all values of \(r\) and all horizons \(\tau\). Collecting terms: \[ [-A'(\tau)] + [B'(\tau) - 1] r - B(\tau) \mu(r) + \frac{1}{2} B(\tau)^2 \sigma(r)^2 = 0 \] For this equality to separate into a system of ODEs for \(A(\tau)\) and \(B(\tau)\) independent of \(r\), the functions \(\mu(r)\) and \(\sigma(r)^2\) must themselves be linear affine functions of \(r\): \[ \mu(r) = k_0 + k_1 r, \quad \sigma(r)^2 = s_0 + s_1 r \] If either \(\mu(r)\) or \(\sigma(r)^2\) contained non-linear terms (e.g. \(r^2\) or \(\ln r\)), the PDE would retain residual non-linear dependencies in \(r\) that cannot be satisfied by scalar functions \(A(\tau)\) and \(B(\tau)\). This establishes the foundational necessity of the affine class (Duffie and Kan 1996).

Problem 1.11 (Economic foundation of market price of risk): In a Lucas exchange economy with representative agent utility \(U(C_t) = e^{-\delta t} \frac{C_t^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\), derive how the market price of interest rate risk \(\lambda(t)\) is determined by consumption growth volatility and risk aversion \(\gamma\).

Solution

In equilibrium asset pricing, the stochastic discount factor (pricing kernel) is the marginal utility of consumption: \(M_t = e^{-\delta t} C_t^{-\gamma}\). Applying Itô's lemma to \(M_t\) where aggregate consumption follows \(dC_t / C_t = \mu_c dt + \sigma_c dW_t^\mathbb{P}\): \[ dM_t = M_t \left[ \left(-\delta - \gamma \mu_c + \frac{1}{2}\gamma(\gamma+1)\sigma_c^2\right) dt - \gamma \sigma_c dW_t^\mathbb{P} \right] \] The instantaneous risk-free rate is \(r_t = -\frac{1}{dt} \mathbb{E}^\mathbb{P}[dM_t/M_t] = \delta + \gamma \mu_c - \frac{1}{2}\gamma(\gamma+1)\sigma_c^2\). The diffusion volatility of the pricing kernel is \(\sigma_M = -\gamma \sigma_c\). By definition, the market price of risk in the economy is the volatility of the stochastic discount factor: \[ \lambda(t) = -\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(dM/M, dW^\mathbb{P})}{dt} = \gamma \sigma_c \] Thus, the market price of risk is fundamentally determined by the product of relative risk aversion \(\gamma\) and macro consumption volatility \(\sigma_c\) (Cox, Ingersoll, and Ross 1985).

Problem 1.12 (Incomplete markets and non-uniqueness of Q): Suppose interest rates are driven by an unobservable latent macroeconomic state variable that cannot be perfectly replicated by traded bonds. Explain why the equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is no longer unique, and assess the empirical implications for term-structure calibration.

Solution

By the Second Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing, an equivalent martingale measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is unique if and only if the financial market is complete. If the Brownian filtration has dimension \(d > 1\) (e.g. monetary policy shocks, inflation shocks, liquidity shocks) but only \(k < d\) non-collinear bonds are traded, the orthogonal projection of the pricing kernel onto the traded asset subspace leaves \(d - k\) unpriced orthogonal risk dimensions. Any market price of risk vector satisfying \(\sigma_{\text{traded}} \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{traded}} - r \mathbf{1}\) generates a valid no-arbitrage measure \(\mathbb{Q}\). Empirically, this implies that cross-sectional bond prices alone cannot uniquely identify the physical factor dynamics \(\mathbb{P}\). Estimating the model requires simultaneous estimation of time-series transitions under \(\mathbb{P}\) and cross-sectional restrictions under \(\mathbb{Q}\) via state-space Kalman filtering (Dai and Singleton 2000).

Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the continuous-time definition of the money-market account \(B(t)\)?

    Options

    \(B(t)\) satisfies \(dB(t) = r(t)B(t)dt\) with \(B(0)=1\), integrating to \(\exp(\int_0^t r(s) ds)\).

  2. Under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\), what does the term \(\lambda(t, r) = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma}\) represent?

    Options

    \(\lambda\) measures excess expected return per unit of short-rate volatility risk.

  3. If a 1-year zero-coupon bond has price \(P(0, 1) = 0.9608\), calculate the continuously compounded 1-year spot rate (in decimal format).

    \(y(0, 1) = -\ln(0.9608)/1 \approx 0.0400\) (4.00%).

  4. How does Girsanov's theorem modify the drift of the short rate when moving from \(\mathbb{P}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\)?

    Options

    The Girsanov change of measure subtracts the risk premium drift: \(\mu^\mathbb{Q} = \mu^\mathbb{P} - \lambda \sigma\).

  5. Why does a one-factor short-rate model fail to provide adequate hedging for fixed-income portfolios?

    Options

    All yields in a one-factor model are driven by a single Brownian motion, resulting in 100% correlation.

  6. If the physical expected return on a bond is 5.5% (\(\mu = 0.055\)), the risk-free rate is \(r = 0.025\), and the bond volatility is \(\sigma = 0.020\), what is the market price of risk \(\lambda\)?

    \(\lambda = (\mu - r) / \sigma = (0.055 - 0.025) / 0.020 = 0.030 / 0.020 = 1.50\).

  7. What terminal boundary condition must the solution \(F(t, r; T)\) of the term-structure PDE satisfy at maturity \(t = T\)?

    Options

    A zero-coupon bond pays exactly 1 dollar of principal at its maturity date \(T\).

  8. What mathematical property does the discounted bond price \(\tilde{P}(t, T) = P(t, T)/B(t)\) possess under \(\mathbb{Q}\)?

    Options

    Deflating by the money market account converts tradeable asset prices into martingales under \(\mathbb{Q}\).
