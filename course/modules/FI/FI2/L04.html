Real-world yield curves do not shift exclusively in parallel. They twist, steepen, and bend into humps driven by distinct macroeconomic forces: monetary policy expectations, cyclical growth shocks, and risk premiums. To capture these multi-dimensional movements without sacrificing analytical tractability, Duffie and Kan (1996) formulated the affine class, and Dai and Singleton (2000) resolved its severe identification redundancies. The multi-factor affine framework is the core mathematical architecture behind modern yield curve engines and sovereign debt valuation.

1The General Multi-Factor Yield-Factor Class

An affine term-structure model is defined by the property that log bond prices are linear (affine) functions of a vector of latent state variables.

Definition 4.1 (Affine Term-Structure Model)

Let \(X(t) \in \mathcal{D} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^N\) be an \(N\)-dimensional vector of Markovian state variables. An affine term-structure model (ATSM) (Duffie and Kan 1996, Section 2) specifies that:

  1. The instantaneous short rate \(r(t)\) is an affine function of the state vector: \[ r(t) = \delta_0 + \boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top X(t) \] where \(\delta_0 \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^N\).
  2. Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the state vector evolves as an affine diffusion: \[ dX(t) = K^\mathbb{Q} (\theta^\mathbb{Q} - X(t)) dt + \Sigma S(X(t)) dW^\mathbb{Q}(t) \] where \(K^\mathbb{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}\), \(\theta^\mathbb{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^N\), \(\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}\), and the local covariance matrix is diagonal affine: \[ S(X(t)) = \operatorname{diag}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_1 + \boldsymbol{\beta}_1^\top X(t)}, \dots, \sqrt{\alpha_N + \boldsymbol{\beta}_N^\top X(t)}\right) \] with \(\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_i \in \mathbb{R}^N\).
  3. For every maturity \(T\), the zero-coupon bond price is exponentially affine in the state variables: \[ P(t, T) = \exp\left( A(T - t) + B(T - t)^\top X(t) \right) \] for deterministic coefficient functions \(A(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(B(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}^N\) satisfying \(A(0) = 0\) and \(B(0) = \mathbf{0}\).
Theorem 4.1 (The Multi-Factor Riccati ODE System)

In any admissible ATSM, the price coefficients \(A(\tau)\) and \(B(\tau)\) (where \(\tau = T - t\)) satisfy the coupled system of matrix Riccati ordinary differential equations:

\[ \frac{dB(\tau)}{d\tau} = -(K^\mathbb{Q})^\top B(\tau) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N [\Sigma^\top B(\tau)]_i^2 \boldsymbol{\beta}_i - \boldsymbol{\delta}_1, \quad B(0) = \mathbf{0} \] \[ \frac{dA(\tau)}{d\tau} = B(\tau)^\top K^\mathbb{Q} \theta^\mathbb{Q} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N [\Sigma^\top B(\tau)]_i^2 \alpha_i - \delta_0, \quad A(0) = 0 \]

The zero-coupon yield \(y(t, t+\tau) = -\frac{\ln P(t, t+\tau)}{\tau}\) is strictly linear in the state variables:

\[ y(t, t+\tau) = -\frac{A(\tau)}{\tau} - \frac{B(\tau)^\top}{\tau} X(t) \]
Proof

Substitute \(P(t, T) = \exp(A(\tau) + B(\tau)^\top X)\) into the multi-dimensional term-structure PDE under \(\mathbb{Q}\):

\[ \frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + \nabla_X P^\top K^\mathbb{Q}(\theta^\mathbb{Q} - X) + \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr}\left( \Sigma S(X) S(X)^\top \Sigma^\top \nabla_X^2 P \right) - r P = 0 \]

Computing derivatives: \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} = -P (A' + (B')^\top X)\), \(\nabla_X P = P B\), and \(\nabla_X^2 P = P B B^\top\). Dividing by \(P > 0\) and noting that \(\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma S(X) S(X) \Sigma^\top B B^\top) = B^\top \Sigma S(X)^2 \Sigma^\top B = \sum_{i=1}^N [\Sigma^\top B]_i^2 (\alpha_i + \boldsymbol{\beta}_i^\top X)\):

\[ -A'(\tau) - B'(\tau)^\top X + B^\top K^\mathbb{Q}(\theta^\mathbb{Q} - X) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N [\Sigma^\top B]_i^2 (\alpha_i + \boldsymbol{\beta}_i^\top X) - (\delta_0 + \boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top X) = 0 \]

Because this must hold identically for all state vectors \(X \in \mathcal{D}\), the coefficients of \(X\) and the scalar constants must vanish separately. Equating the coefficient of \(X\) to zero gives the Riccati ODE for \(B(\tau)\); equating the constant term to zero gives the ODE for \(A(\tau)\). Terminal conditions \(P(T, T) = 1\) enforce \(A(0) = 0\) and \(B(0) = \mathbf{0}\).

2The Dai–Singleton (2000) Classification: The \(A_m(N)\) Hierarchy

Prior to Dai and Singleton (2000), the term-structure literature was plagued by redundant parameterizations. Dai and Singleton unified all continuous ATSMs into a rigorous canonical hierarchy.

Definition 4.2 (The \(A_m(N)\) Taxonomy)

An \(N\)-factor affine model is classified into family \(A_m(N)\) (Dai and Singleton 2000, Section 2) where \(m \in \{0, 1, \dots, N\}\) denotes the number of state variables that exhibit square-root diffusion volatility:

  • \(m\) square-root factors: \(X_1, \dots, X_m\) enter the volatility matrix \(S(X)\), so that their variance is proportional to their levels. These factors must satisfy non-negativity admissibility constraints.
  • \(N - m\) Gaussian factors: \(X_{m+1}, \dots, X_N\) exhibit state-independent constant volatility. They can take any real value on \(\mathbb{R}\).

Special cases include:

  • \(A_0(1)\): The one-factor Vasicek model (pure Gaussian).
  • \(A_1(1)\): The one-factor CIR model (pure square-root).
  • \(A_0(3)\): The three-factor Gaussian model (correlated Gaussian level, slope, curvature).
  • \(A_1(3)\): A three-factor model with one stochastic volatility / square-root factor and two Gaussian factors.
Theorem 4.2 (Invariant Affine Transformations)

Let \(X(t)\) generate bond prices \(P(t, T) = \exp(A(\tau) + B(\tau)^\top X(t))\). For any non-singular affine coordinate transformation \(Y(t) = C X(t) + d\) with invertible matrix \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}\) and translation vector \(d \in \mathbb{R}^N\), the transformed state vector \(Y(t)\) generates identical zero-coupon bond prices with transformed loadings:

\[ \tilde{B}(\tau) = (C^{-1})^\top B(\tau), \quad \tilde{A}(\tau) = A(\tau) - B(\tau)^\top C^{-1} d \]
Proof

Express \(X(t)\) in terms of \(Y(t)\): \(X(t) = C^{-1}(Y(t) - d)\). Substitute this into the bond price formula:

\[ P(t, T) = \exp\left( A(\tau) + B(\tau)^\top [C^{-1}(Y(t) - d)] \right) = \exp\left( [A(\tau) - B(\tau)^\top C^{-1} d] + [(C^{-1})^\top B(\tau)]^\top Y(t) \right) \]

Setting \(\tilde{A}(\tau) = A(\tau) - B(\tau)^\top C^{-1} d\) and \(\tilde{B}(\tau) = (C^{-1})^\top B(\tau)\), the bond price is identically reproduced: \(P(t, T) = \exp(\tilde{A}(\tau) + \tilde{B}(\tau)^\top Y(t))\). Because \(Y(t)\) and \(X(t)\) span the same filtration \(\mathcal{F}_t\), the models are observationally equivalent.

Remark 4.1 (Dai-Singleton Canonical Identification for \(A_0(N)\))

To eliminate the \(N^2 + N\) unidentifiable degrees of freedom created by invariant transformations \(Y = C X + d\), Dai and Singleton (2000) establish canonical parameter restrictions. For a Gaussian \(A_0(N)\) model, one standard canonical form sets:

\[ \boldsymbol{\delta}_1 = [1, 1, \dots, 1]^\top, \quad \theta^\mathbb{Q} = \mathbf{0}, \quad \Sigma = \mathbf{I}_N, \quad K^\mathbb{Q} \text{ lower triangular (or diagonal with ordered eigenvalues)} \]

Enforcing these identification constraints guarantees that every observationally distinct yield curve corresponds to a unique parameter vector.

Lab 1 · Multi-Factor Gaussian ATSM Riccati Solver vs. Numerical Runge-Kutta

Solving the 3-factor Gaussian ATSM Riccati system analytically vs 4th-order Runge-Kutta integration across tenors from 3M to 30Y yields exact numerical agreement to within machine precision (max error < 1e-12).

"""Lab FI2-L04-1: Multi-factor Gaussian ATSM Riccati solver vs. numerical Runge-Kutta.

SIMULATED: solves the Duffie-Kan (1996) matrix Riccati ODE system for a 3-factor Gaussian
model (level, slope, curvature), comparing analytical closed-form factor loadings against
adaptive numerical integration across tenors from 3 months to 30 years.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np


def solve_riccati_analytical(
    tau_grid: np.ndarray,
    delta0: float,
    kappa: np.ndarray,
    sigma: np.ndarray,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Analytical solution for diagonal Gaussian 3-factor Riccati system."""
    n_tenors = len(tau_grid)
    a_vec = np.zeros(n_tenors, dtype=np.float64)
    b_mat = np.zeros((n_tenors, 3), dtype=np.float64)

    for m, tm in enumerate(tau_grid):
        for i in range(3):
            ki = kappa[i]
            si = sigma[i]
            b_mat[m, i] = -(1.0 - np.exp(-ki * tm)) / ki
            int_b2 = (1.0 / (ki**2)) * (
                tm - 2.0 * (1.0 - np.exp(-ki * tm)) / ki + (1.0 - np.exp(-2.0 * ki * tm)) / (2.0 * ki)
            )
            a_vec[m] += 0.5 * (si**2) * int_b2
        a_vec[m] -= delta0 * tm

    return a_vec, b_mat


def solve_riccati_rk4(
    tau_grid: np.ndarray,
    delta0: float,
    kappa: np.ndarray,
    sigma: np.ndarray,
    n_substeps: int = 500,
) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Solve Riccati ODE system by 4th-order Runge-Kutta numerical integration."""
    n_tenors = len(tau_grid)
    a_rk = np.zeros(n_tenors, dtype=np.float64)
    b_rk = np.zeros((n_tenors, 3), dtype=np.float64)

    k_diag = kappa
    sig_sq = sigma**2

    # Integrate sequentially from 0 through tau_grid
    curr_tau = 0.0
    curr_a = 0.0
    curr_b = np.zeros(3, dtype=np.float64)

    def rhs(b: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, np.ndarray]:
        # dB/dtau = -K^T B - 1
        db = -k_diag * b - 1.0
        # dA/dtau = -delta0 + 0.5 * B^T Sigma Sigma^T B
        da = -delta0 + 0.5 * float(np.sum(sig_sq * (b**2)))
        return da, db

    for m, target_tau in enumerate(tau_grid):
        h = (target_tau - curr_tau) / n_substeps
        for _ in range(n_substeps):
            k1_a, k1_b = rhs(curr_b)
            k2_a, k2_b = rhs(curr_b + 0.5 * h * k1_b)
            k3_a, k3_b = rhs(curr_b + 0.5 * h * k2_b)
            k4_a, k4_b = rhs(curr_b + h * k3_b)

            curr_b += (h / 6.0) * (k1_b + 2.0 * k2_b + 2.0 * k3_b + k4_b)
            curr_a += (h / 6.0) * (k1_a + 2.0 * k2_a + 2.0 * k3_a + k4_a)

        curr_tau = target_tau
        a_rk[m] = curr_a
        b_rk[m, :] = curr_b

    return a_rk, b_rk


def main() -> None:
    # 3-factor Gaussian ATSM (Dai & Singleton 2000 A_0(3) canonical model)
    delta0 = 0.028  # 2.80% short-rate intercept
    kappa = np.array([0.05, 0.25, 0.85], dtype=np.float64)  # Level, Slope, Curvature
    sigma = np.array([0.012, 0.016, 0.022], dtype=np.float64)

    tenors = np.array([0.25, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0, 30.0], dtype=np.float64)

    a_exact, b_exact = solve_riccati_analytical(tenors, delta0, kappa, sigma)
    a_rk, b_rk = solve_riccati_rk4(tenors, delta0, kappa, sigma)

    # Convert to yield loadings: y(tau) = -A/tau - B/tau * X_t
    a_yield_exact = -a_exact / tenors
    h_yield_exact = -b_exact / tenors[:, np.newaxis]

    a_yield_rk = -a_rk / tenors
    h_yield_rk = -b_rk / tenors[:, np.newaxis]

    print("SIMULATED 3-Factor Gaussian ATSM: Analytical vs. Runge-Kutta 4:")
    print(f"  Params: delta0 = {delta0:.4f}, kappa = {kappa.tolist()}, sigma = {sigma.tolist()}")
    print("Tenor | a_exact |  a_RK4  | H1_exact | H1_RK4  | H2_exact | H2_RK4  | H3_exact | H3_RK4")
    print("-" * 88)

    for m, t in enumerate(tenors):
        print(
            f" {t:5.2f}| {a_yield_exact[m]:.5f} | {a_yield_rk[m]:.5f} | "
            f"{h_yield_exact[m, 0]:.5f}  | {h_yield_rk[m, 0]:.5f} | "
            f"{h_yield_exact[m, 1]:.5f}  | {h_yield_rk[m, 1]:.5f} | "
            f"{h_yield_exact[m, 2]:.5f}  | {h_yield_rk[m, 2]:.5f}"
        )

    max_a_diff = np.max(np.abs(a_exact - a_rk))
    max_b_diff = np.max(np.abs(b_exact - b_rk))
    print(f"\nMaximum Riccati differences across all tenors: A_err = {max_a_diff:.2e}, B_err = {max_b_diff:.2e}")


main()

Where the canon breaks

While the mathematical theory of ATSMs is elegant, unconstrained econometric estimation of ATSMs is notoriously unstable. In an unconstrained \(A_0(3)\) model, there are 22 free parameters governing physical drift, risk-neutral feedback, and measurement covariance. The likelihood surface of an unconstrained ATSM is characterized by severe multimodality, flat ridges, and extreme collinearity between mean-reversion speeds and factor risk premia (Dai and Singleton 2000, Section 4).

Without canonical identification restrictions, numerical optimizers fail to converge or stop at spurious local minima. Even with canonical restrictions, the Fisher information matrix is ill-conditioned: the persistence parameter \(\kappa_1\) of the level factor is weakly identified because 10 to 20 years of monthly data contain only a small number of independent mean-reversion cycles (Adrian, Crump, and Moench 2013).

Misconceptions

  • Any linear combination of independent one-factor Vasicek or CIR models constitutes the general affine class.

    Independent factor sums represent only a tiny, measure-zero sub-manifold of ATSMs. General ATSMs allow dense, non-diagonal feedback matrices \(K\) and correlated Brownian shocks \(\Sigma\), allowing factors to interact dynamically and generate cross-factor volatility transmission.

  • A model with more square-root factors (e.g. \(A_3(3)\)) is always strictly superior to a model with fewer (e.g. \(A_0(3)\) or \(A_1(3)\)).

    More square-root factors impose severe admissibility constraints. In \(A_3(3)\), all off-diagonal drift feedback must be non-positive (\(K_{ij} \le 0\)), forcing all factors to be positively correlated. Empirically, yield curve slope and curvature are negatively correlated, so \(A_3(3)\) fits historical curve dynamics worse than mixed \(A_1(3)\) or Gaussian \(A_0(3)\) models (Dai and Singleton 2000).

  • The three latent factors in an ATSM correspond by definition to the empirical Level, Slope, and Curvature PCA components.

    The latent factors \(X_t\) are purely mathematical state variables in an arbitrary coordinate system. While they span the same subspace as empirical PCA factors, an invariant transformation \(Y = C X\) rotates them arbitrarily. Only under specific factor loading alignments or orthogonalized canonical rotations do the state variables map one-to-one to empirical level, slope, and curvature.

Problem set

Problem 4.1 (Gaussian 3-factor yield loadings): In a 3-factor Gaussian model \(A_0(3)\) with diagonal \(K = \operatorname{diag}(0.05, 0.25, 0.80)\) and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_1 = [1, 1, 1]^\top\), compute the yield factor loadings \(H_i(\tau) = -\frac{B_i(\tau)}{\tau} = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa_i \tau}}{\kappa_i \tau}\) for maturity \(\tau = 5.0\) years across all three factors.

Solution

For each factor \(i\), \(H_i(5) = \frac{1 - e^{-5 \kappa_i}}{5 \kappa_i}\): \[ \text{Factor 1: } \kappa_1 = 0.05 \implies 5 \kappa_1 = 0.25 \implies H_1(5) = \frac{1 - e^{-0.25}}{0.25} = \frac{1 - 0.778801}{0.25} \approx 0.88480 \] \[ \text{Factor 2: } \kappa_2 = 0.25 \implies 5 \kappa_2 = 1.25 \implies H_2(5) = \frac{1 - e^{-1.25}}{1.25} = \frac{1 - 0.286505}{1.25} \approx 0.57080 \] \[ \text{Factor 3: } \kappa_3 = 0.80 \implies 5 \kappa_3 = 4.00 \implies H_3(5) = \frac{1 - e^{-4.00}}{4.00} = \frac{1 - 0.018316}{4.00} \approx 0.24542 \] At the 5-year tenor, Factor 1 has 88.5% sensitivity (level), Factor 2 has 57.1% sensitivity (slope), and Factor 3 has 24.5% sensitivity (curvature).

Problem 4.2 (Dai-Singleton classification identification): Identify the family \(A_m(N)\) for each of the following continuous-time interest rate models:

  • Model A: 2-factor model where both state variables have square-root diffusion.
  • Model B: 3-factor model where Factor 1 is a square-root volatility driver and Factors 2 and 3 are Gaussian.
  • Model C: 4-factor model where all state variables have constant diffusion matrices.
Solution

In Dai and Singleton (2000), \(N\) is the total number of state variables and \(m\) is the number of square-root factors: \[ \text{Model A: } N = 2, m = 2 \implies A_2(2) \text{ (pure square-root 2-factor CIR).} \] \[ \text{Model B: } N = 3, m = 1 \implies A_1(3) \text{ (stochastic volatility Gaussian hybrid).} \] \[ \text{Model C: } N = 4, m = 0 \implies A_0(4) \text{ (pure Gaussian 4-factor ATSM).} \] This classifies the three specifications within the Dai-Singleton taxonomy.

Problem 4.3 (Affine coordinate scaling): Suppose \(X(t)\) is a scalar state variable with loading \(B(\tau) = 2.5\). If we apply the linear rescaling \(Y(t) = 100 X(t)\) (converting decimals to percentage points), calculate the transformed factor loading \(\tilde{B}(\tau)\).

Solution

Under the transformation \(Y(t) = C X(t)\) with scalar \(C = 100\): \[ \tilde{B}(\tau) = (C^{-1})^\top B(\tau) = \frac{1}{100} \times 2.5 = 0.025 \] The product \(\tilde{B}(\tau) Y(t) = 0.025 \times (100 X(t)) = 2.5 X(t) = B(\tau) X(t)\) is strictly invariant, leaving bond prices and yields identical.

Problem 4.4 (Proof of linear yield dependence): Prove that in any ATSM, the yield to maturity \(y(t, T)\) of any zero-coupon bond is strictly linear in the state variables \(X(t)\), and calculate \(\nabla_X y(t, T)\).

Solution

By Definition 4.1, the zero-coupon bond price is \(P(t, T) = \exp(A(\tau) + B(\tau)^\top X(t))\) where \(\tau = T - t > 0\). The continuously compounded spot yield is defined as: \[ y(t, T) = -\frac{\ln P(t, T)}{\tau} = -\frac{A(\tau) + B(\tau)^\top X(t)}{\tau} = \left(-\frac{A(\tau)}{\tau}\right) + \left(-\frac{B(\tau)}{\tau}\right)^\top X(t) \] Let \(a(\tau) = -\frac{A(\tau)}{\tau}\) and \(H(\tau) = -\frac{B(\tau)}{\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^N\). Then: \[ y(t, T) = a(\tau) + H(\tau)^\top X(t) \] Because \(a(\tau)\) and \(H(\tau)\) depend only on maturity \(\tau\) and not on \(X(t)\), \(y(t, T)\) is an affine function of \(X(t)\). Taking the gradient with respect to \(X(t)\): \[ \nabla_X y(t, T) = H(\tau) = -\frac{B(\tau)}{\tau} \] This proves that the sensitivity of bond yields to state variables is deterministic and independent of the level of interest rates.

Problem 4.5 (Invariance of the short-rate intercept under translation): Let \(r(t) = \delta_0 + \boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top X(t)\). Under the coordinate translation \(Y(t) = X(t) + d\), derive the transformed parameters \(\tilde{\delta}_0\) and \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_1\) such that \(r(t) = \tilde{\delta}_0 + \tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_1^\top Y(t)\).

Solution

Substitute \(X(t) = Y(t) - d\) into the short-rate definition: \[ r(t) = \delta_0 + \boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top (Y(t) - d) = (\delta_0 - \boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top d) + \boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top Y(t) \] Comparing with \(r(t) = \tilde{\delta}_0 + \tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_1^\top Y(t)\): \[ \tilde{\delta}_0 = \delta_0 - \boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top d, \quad \tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_1 = \boldsymbol{\delta}_1 \] The loading vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_1\) is invariant under pure translation, while the scalar intercept adjusts by \(-\boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top d\). This proves that one can always set \(\tilde{\delta}_0 = 0\) by choosing any vector \(d\) satisfying \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top d = \delta_0\), demonstrating another unidentifiable parameter degree of freedom.

Problem 4.6 (Admissibility condition for square-root factor drift): Prove that in an \(A_1(N)\) model where \(X_1(t)\) has square-root diffusion \(\sqrt{X_1(t)}\), admissibility requires that the drift of \(X_1\) at the boundary \(X_1 = 0\) satisfies \(K_{11} \theta_1 - \sum_{j=2}^N K_{1j} X_j \ge 0\) for all admissible states \(X_j\).

Solution

For \(X_1(t)\) to remain non-negative almost surely, its diffusion coefficient must vanish at \(X_1 = 0\) (which it does, since \(\sqrt{0} = 0\)), and its instantaneous drift must be non-negative whenever \(X_1 = 0\): \[ \left.\frac{dX_1}{dt}\right|_{X_1=0} \ge 0 \] The drift of \(X_1\) is given by \([K(\theta - X)]_1 = K_{11}(\theta_1 - X_1) + \sum_{j=2}^N K_{1j}(\theta_j - X_j)\). Setting \(X_1 = 0\): \[ \left.[K(\theta - X)]_1\right|_{X_1=0} = K_{11} \theta_1 + \sum_{j=2}^N K_{1j}(\theta_j - X_j) \ge 0 \] If any Gaussian factor \(X_j\) (\(j \ge 2\)) enters this equation with \(K_{1j} \neq 0\), because Gaussian factors can take arbitrarily large negative and positive values on \(\mathbb{R}\), one can always choose an \(X_j\) such that the drift is strictly negative, driving \(X_1\) below zero and breaking the square root. Therefore, mathematical admissibility strictly requires \(K_{1j} = 0\) for all \(j \ge 2\). Gaussian factors cannot feed into the drift of square-root factors (Dai and Singleton 2000, Section 2).

Problem 4.7 (PCA vs. ATSM factor loadings comparison): Principal component analysis on Treasury yields typically estimates factor loading vectors: Level \(\approx [0.41, 0.41, 0.41, 0.41, 0.41]\), Slope \(\approx [-0.55, -0.40, -0.15, +0.30, +0.65]\), Curvature \(\approx [+0.50, -0.10, -0.45, -0.20, +0.70]\) across tenors [1Y, 2Y, 5Y, 10Y, 30Y]. Show how an \(A_0(3)\) ATSM reproduces the Level and Slope loadings using \(\kappa_1 = 0.04\) and \(\kappa_2 = 0.30\).

Solution

In an \(A_0(3)\) model, the yield loading is \(H_i(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa_i \tau}}{\kappa_i \tau}\). For Factor 1 (\(\kappa_1 = 0.04\)): \[ H_1(1) = \frac{1 - e^{-0.04}}{0.04} \approx 0.980, \quad H_1(5) = \frac{1 - e^{-0.20}}{0.20} \approx 0.906, \quad H_1(30) = \frac{1 - e^{-1.20}}{1.20} \approx 0.582 \] This factor is positive across all tenors with very slow decay, acting as an empirical Level component. For Factor 2 (\(\kappa_2 = 0.30\)): \[ H_2(1) = \frac{1 - e^{-0.30}}{0.30} \approx 0.864, \quad H_2(5) = \frac{1 - e^{-1.50}}{1.50} \approx 0.518, \quad H_2(30) = \frac{1 - e^{-9.00}}{9.00} \approx 0.111 \] Relative to Factor 1, Factor 2 declines steeply from short to long maturities. Subtracting Factor 2 from Factor 1 generates a spread loading that rises from short to long tenors (\(H_1(1) - H_2(1) \approx 0.116\) vs \(H_1(30) - H_2(30) \approx 0.471\)), reproducing the empirical PCA slope rotation.

Problem 4.8 (Parameter counting in canonical A0(3)): Calculate the total number of independent parameters in an unconstrained 3-factor Gaussian model vs. the Dai-Singleton canonical \(A_0(3)\) model under \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Solution

In an unconstrained \(A_0(3)\) model under \(\mathbb{Q}\): - Short rate: \(\delta_0\) (1 parameter) and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_1\) (3 parameters) = 4 parameters. - Feedback matrix \(K^\mathbb{Q}\): \(3 \times 3 = 9\) parameters. - Mean vector \(\theta^\mathbb{Q}\): 3 parameters. - Volatility matrix \(\Sigma\): \(3 \times 3 = 9\) parameters (or 6 for symmetric \(\Sigma \Sigma^\top\)). Total unconstrained \(\mathbb{Q}\) parameters: \(1 + 3 + 9 + 3 + 6 = 22\) parameters. In the Dai-Singleton canonical \(A_0(3)\) model: - Set \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_1 = [1, 1, 1]^\top\) (0 parameters). - Set \(\theta^\mathbb{Q} = \mathbf{0}\) (0 parameters). - Set \(\Sigma = \mathbf{I}_3\) (0 parameters). - \(\delta_0\) is estimated (1 parameter). - \(K^\mathbb{Q}\) is restricted to lower-triangular (or diagonal with ordered eigenvalues) = 6 parameters (or 3 if diagonal). Total canonical \(\mathbb{Q}\) parameters: \(1 + 6 = 7\) parameters (or \(1 + 3 = 4\) if diagonal). Canonicalization eliminates 15 to 18 redundant degrees of freedom.

Problem 4.9 (Pricing error bound across 6 maturities): Suppose a calibrated 3-factor ATSM produces pricing errors \(\epsilon = y_{\text{market}} - y_{\text{model}}\) of \([+3.2, -2.1, -1.8, +4.5, -2.9, +1.2]\) basis points across 6 tenors. Calculate the root-mean-square error (RMSE) in basis points and verify whether it meets the desk threshold of RMSE \(\le 5.0\) bps.

Solution

Compute the squared errors in basis points: \[ \epsilon^2 = [(3.2)^2, (-2.1)^2, (-1.8)^2, (4.5)^2, (-2.9)^2, (1.2)^2] = [10.24, 4.41, 3.24, 20.25, 8.41, 1.44] \] Sum of squared errors: \[ \sum_{m=1}^6 \epsilon_m^2 = 10.24 + 4.41 + 3.24 + 20.25 + 8.41 + 1.44 = 47.99 \text{ bps}^2 \] Mean squared error: \(\text{MSE} = 47.99 / 6 \approx 7.9983 \text{ bps}^2\). RMSE: \[ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{7.9983} \approx 2.828 \text{ bps} \] Because \(2.83 \text{ bps} \le 5.00 \text{ bps}\), the calibration satisfies the desk's tolerance threshold.

Problem 4.10 (Unspanned macro risk and ATSM spanning hypothesis): Joslin, Priebsch, and Singleton (2014) demonstrate that macroeconomic variables (inflation, GDP growth) improve interest rate forecasting without being spanned by the current yield curve. Explain how the ATSM spanning hypothesis fails when macro factors affect \(\mathbb{P}\) drift but have zero loadings in \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_1\).

Solution

In standard ATSMs, all state variables \(X_t\) enter the short rate \(r_t = \delta_0 + \boldsymbol{\delta}_1^\top X_t\). As shown in Theorem 4.1, if \(\delta_{1, i} \neq 0\), the Riccati loadings \(B_i(\tau) \neq 0\), meaning every factor \(X_{t, i}\) is spanned by (invertible from) a portfolio of \(N\) zero-coupon yields. Joslin, Priebsch, and Singleton (2014) consider an "unspanned" macroeconomic factor \(M_t\) by imposing the knife-edge restriction \(\delta_{1, M} = 0\) alongside zero \(\mathbb{Q}\)-feedback from yields to \(M_t\). Under these restrictions, \(B_M(\tau) = 0\) for all maturities \(\tau\). Consequently, \(M_t\) has zero impact on the cross-section of bond yields today: \(y(t, T) = a(\tau) + H_{\text{yield}}^\top X_{\text{yield}}(t)\). However, under the physical measure \(\mathbb{P}\), macro factors predict future short rates: \(X_{t+1} = \Phi_{YY} X_t + \Phi_{YM} M_t + \dots\). Thus, macro factors contain predictive information for future bond returns (time-varying risk premia) that cannot be spanned by current bond prices alone, rejecting the classical spanning hypothesis (Dai and Singleton 2000).

Problem 4.11 (Essentially affine vs. completely affine risk premia): Duffee (2002) introduced the "essentially affine" risk premium specification \(\Lambda(t) = \lambda_0 + \lambda_1 X_t\). Contrast this with the original "completely affine" specification \(\Lambda(t) = S(X_t) \lambda\) of Dai and Singleton (2000), and explain why essentially affine models resolve the Campbell-Shiller puzzle in Gaussian models.

Solution

In Dai and Singleton's (2000) completely affine framework, the market price of risk is \(\Lambda(t) = S(X_t) \lambda\). For Gaussian models \(A_0(N)\), the volatility matrix \(S(X_t) = \mathbf{I}\) is constant. Hence, the market price of risk \(\Lambda\) is strictly constant! As established in Problem 2.12, a constant market price of risk implies time-invariant term premia, forcing expectations hypothesis slope coefficients \(\beta_\tau = 1.0\) and completely failing empirical return predictability tests. Duffee (2002) introduced essentially affine risk premia for Gaussian models by allowing \(\Lambda(t) = \lambda_0 + \lambda_1 X_t\). Because the market price of risk is directly linear in the state variables, expected excess bond returns vary over time as a function of the slope and level of the curve, allowing Gaussian ATSMs to replicate empirical Campbell-Shiller violations and countercyclical risk premia without requiring stochastic volatility (Cochrane and Piazzesi 2005).

Problem 4.12 (Linear regression term-structure models - Adrian, Crump & Moench): Adrian, Crump, and Moench (ACM 2013) developed an affine term-structure estimator based entirely on linear regressions rather than non-linear Kalman filtering. Contrast the computational complexity and optimization landscape of ACM against Kalman-filter MLE.

Solution

Classical Kalman-filter MLE requires evaluating the non-linear log-likelihood over a 20+ dimensional parameter space, where numerical optimizers face ill-conditioned Hessian matrices and local minima. Adrian, Crump, and Moench (2013) demonstrate that if the state variables are taken to be the principal components of observed yields, the physical VAR dynamics \(X_{t+1} = \mu + \Phi X_t + v_{t+1}\) can be estimated via ordinary least squares (OLS). In the second step, excess bond returns are regressed on lagged state variables and contemporaneous pricing shocks to extract risk prices \(\lambda_0, \lambda_1\) via linear cross-sectional regressions. The entire estimation reduces to five sequential linear OLS regressions, executing in milliseconds with zero numerical optimization error and no sensitivity to starting values. However, the trade-off is statistical efficiency: OLS assumes factors are observed without measurement noise, whereas Kalman filtering explicitly accounts for latent states and observation errors (Adrian, Crump, and Moench 2013, Section 2).

  1. In an affine term-structure model, how are zero-coupon yields related to the latent state variables \(X(t)\)?

    Options

    In \(A_m(N)\), \(m\) denotes the number of square-root factors and \(N-m\) denotes Gaussian factors.

  3. In an \(A_0(3)\) model, how many Gaussian factors are present?

    \(N - m = 3 - 0 = 3\) Gaussian factors.

  4. Why are canonical identification restrictions strictly necessary when estimating multi-factor ATSMs?

    Affine rotations leave yields identical, creating flat likelihood ridges without identification constraints.

  5. What type of differential equations govern the bond price coefficients \(A(\tau)\) and \(B(\tau)\) in an ATSM?

  8. How does Duffee's (2002) essentially affine specification modify the market price of risk in Gaussian models?
