Par Yields, Curve Shapes, and Term Spreads

Uses: bond price–yield relation, yield to maturity, spot (zero-coupon) rate, forward rate, discount factor, annuity

Par yields translate unobservable zero-coupon discount factors into the coupon rates newly issued bonds must carry to price at face value. Primary debt issuance, constant-maturity benchmarks, and interest-rate swap fixed legs are quoted directly on the par curve. In turn, the term spread between long-term and short-term par yields is one of the most reliable leading indicators of economic contractions and central bank monetary policy shifts.

1Par Yields and Repricing

A par bond is a coupon-bearing fixed-income security whose dirty market price equals its nominal face value. Because existing bonds trade away from par as yields fluctuate, debt issuers and swap desks need an analytical operator that converts discount factors into the exact coupon rate required to issue at 100.

Definition 4.1 (Par Yield)

The par yield \(c^*(\tau)\) for maturity \(\tau\) under annual compounding is the coupon rate per dollar of face value that equates the bond's present value under the zero curve to its face value (\(P = 1\)):

\[ c^*(\tau) \sum_{k=1}^\tau Z(k) + Z(\tau) = 1 \iff c^*(\tau) = \frac{1 - Z(\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^\tau Z(k)} = \frac{1 - Z(\tau)}{A(\tau)} \]

where \(A(\tau) = \sum_{k=1}^\tau Z(k)\) denotes the annuity factor. For frequency \(m\) coupon payments per year across \(N = m\tau\) periods, the annualized bond-equivalent par yield is:

\[ c_m^*(\tau) = m \times \frac{1 - Z(t_N)}{\sum_{k=1}^N Z(t_k)} \]
Theorem 4.1 (Par Bond Repricing Theorem)

Assume the following hypotheses:

  1. Assumption 1: The discount factors satisfy \(1 > Z(1) > Z(2) > \dots > Z(\tau) > 0\).
  2. Assumption 2: A bond pays coupon cash flow \(c \times F\) at integer dates \(k \in \{1, \dots, \tau\}\) and principal \(F > 0\) at date \(\tau\).
  3. Assumption 3: Valuation is governed by linear no-arbitrage pricing: \(P = \sum_{k=1}^\tau c F Z(k) + F Z(\tau)\).

Then the bond dirty price \(P\) equals face value \(F\) if and only if \(c = c^*(\tau)\). Furthermore, the unique yield to maturity \(y\) of this bond equals \(c^*(\tau)\).

Proof

Set \(P = F\). Factoring out \(F\) yields \(F = F [c \sum_{k=1}^\tau Z(k) + Z(\tau)]\). Since \(F > 0\), dividing by \(F\) gives \(1 = c A(\tau) + Z(\tau)\). Solving for \(c\) uniquely yields \(c = \frac{1 - Z(\tau)}{A(\tau)} = c^*(\tau)\). Substituting \(y = c^*(\tau)\) into the yield to maturity equation shows that \(c^*(\tau) \sum_{k=1}^\tau (1+c^*(\tau))^{-k} + (1+c^*(\tau))^{-\tau} = c^*(\tau) \frac{1 - (1+c^*(\tau))^{-\tau}}{c^*(\tau)} + (1+c^*(\tau))^{-\tau} = 1\), satisfying the definition of YTM. Monotonicity (Theorem 1.1) guarantees uniqueness.

2Curve Ordering and Term Spreads

Theorem 4.2 (Curve Ordering Theorem)

Let \(s(\tau)\) be the spot rate, \(f(\tau-1, \tau)\) the 1-period forward rate, and \(c^*(\tau)\) the par yield for \(\tau \ge 2\). Then:

  1. If the spot curve is strictly increasing (\(s(\tau) > s(\tau-1)\)), then \(f(\tau-1, \tau) > s(\tau) > c^*(\tau)\).
  2. If the spot curve is strictly decreasing (\(s(\tau) < s(\tau-1)\)), then \(f(\tau-1, \tau) < s(\tau) < c^*(\tau)\).
  3. If the spot curve is flat (\(s(\tau) = s_0\)), then \(f(\tau-1, \tau) = s(\tau) = c^*(\tau) = s_0\).
Proof

In an increasing spot curve, \(1 + f(\tau-1, \tau) = (1+s(\tau)) [ \frac{1+s(\tau)}{1+s(\tau-1)} ]^{\tau-1} > 1+s(\tau)\), so \(f(\tau-1, \tau) > s(\tau)\). Since the par bond pays cash flows at dates \(k < \tau\) discounted at rates \(s(k) < s(\tau)\), its value discounted at flat rate \(s(\tau)\) would be below par. To reach par, its yield to maturity \(c^*(\tau)\) must satisfy \(c^*(\tau) < s(\tau)\). Combining the bounds gives \(f(\tau-1, \tau) > s(\tau) > c^*(\tau)\). The reverse inequalities follow similarly for inverted curves.

Definition 4.2 (Term Spread)

The term spread \(S(\tau_1, \tau_2)\) between maturities \(\tau_2 > \tau_1\) is the arithmetic difference between their benchmark yields:

\[ S(\tau_1, \tau_2) = y(\tau_2) - y(\tau_1) \]

Standard empirical benchmarks include the 10-Year minus 2-Year Treasury spread \(S(2, 10) = y(10) - y(2)\) and the 10-Year minus 3-Month spread \(S(0.25, 10)\), typically measured in basis points (\(1\text{ bps} = 0.01\%\)).

Example 4.1 (Analytical Par Yield Computation)

Given spot rates \(s(1) = 3.0\%\), \(s(2) = 4.0\%\), and \(s(3) = 5.0\%\) under annual compounding:

\[ Z(1) = \frac{1}{1.03} \approx 0.970874, \quad Z(2) = \frac{1}{1.04^2} \approx 0.924556, \quad Z(3) = \frac{1}{1.05^3} \approx 0.863838 \]

The annuity factors are \(A(1) \approx 0.970874\), \(A(2) \approx 1.895430\), and \(A(3) \approx 2.759268\). The par yields are:

\[ c^*(1) = \frac{1 - 0.970874}{0.970874} = 3.0000\%, \quad c^*(2) = \frac{1 - 0.924556}{1.895430} \approx 3.9803\%, \quad c^*(3) = \frac{1 - 0.863838}{2.759268} \approx 4.9347\% \]

Notice that for year 3, \(c^*(3) \approx 4.9347\% < s(3) = 5.0000\% < f(2, 3) = \frac{Z(2)}{Z(3)} - 1 \approx 7.0288\%\), confirming Theorem 4.2.

Remark 4.1 (Par Curve Smoothing)

Because the par yield is an annuity-weighted harmonic average of spot rates, the par curve is always smoother and less volatile than the underlying spot curve and forward curve. A sharp kink in the forward curve appears as a mild slope change in the par curve.

Lab 1 · Par Yield Computation and Repricing Verification

SIMULATED: Evaluates a 10-year ascending spot curve, derives annual par yields, confirms the curve ordering theorem f > s > c*, and verifies exact par bond repricing to $100.00 face.

"""Lab FI1-L04-1: Par yield computation, repricing check, and term spread analysis.

SIMULATED: annual zero curve with derived par yields and term spreads.
Calculations use exact rational arithmetic and high-precision floats.
Standard library only; no network.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def compute_par_yield(discount_factors: list[Fraction]) -> Fraction:
    """Compute annual par yield from a list of annual discount factors Z(1), ..., Z(n)."""
    z_n = discount_factors[-1]
    annuity = sum(discount_factors)
    return (Fraction(1, 1) - z_n) / annuity


def main() -> None:
    # 1. Given zero-coupon spot rates for maturities 1 through 10 years
    # Ascending curve: s(tau) = 0.03 + 0.003 * tau
    maturities = list(range(1, 11))
    spot_rates = {tau: Fraction(30 + 3 * tau, 1000) for tau in maturities}

    discount_factors = [Fraction(1, 1) / ((1 + spot_rates[tau]) ** tau) for tau in maturities]

    # 2. Compute par yields
    par_yields: dict[int, Fraction] = {}
    print("Maturity | Spot Rate s(tau) | Par Yield c*(tau) | 1y Forward f(tau-1, tau)")
    print("-" * 65)

    for tau in maturities:
        c_star = compute_par_yield(discount_factors[:tau])
        par_yields[tau] = c_star
        s_val = spot_rates[tau]
        f_val = s_val if tau == 1 else (discount_factors[tau - 2] / discount_factors[tau - 1]) - 1

        print(f"{tau:8d} | {float(s_val):16.4%} | {float(c_star):17.4%} | {float(f_val):24.4%}")

        # In an upward-sloping spot curve, f(tau) > s(tau) > c*(tau) for tau > 1
        if tau > 1:
            assert f_val > s_val > c_star

    print("-" * 65)
    print("Ordering check verified: forward rate > spot rate > par yield for all tau > 1.")

    # 3. Repricing check: a bond with coupon c*(tau) must price exactly to 100 per 100 face
    print("\nPar Bond Repricing Verification (Face = 100.00):")
    for tau in [2, 5, 10]:
        c_star = par_yields[tau]
        dfs = discount_factors[:tau]
        pv = 100 * (c_star * sum(dfs) + dfs[-1])
        err = abs(float(pv) - 100.0)
        print(f"  tau = {tau:2d} yr: Price = {float(pv):.10f}, |Error from 100.00| = {err:.2e}")
        assert err < 1e-12

    # 4. Term spreads: 10y - 2y spread for spot and par curves
    spot_10_2 = spot_rates[10] - spot_rates[2]
    par_10_2 = par_yields[10] - par_yields[2]
    print("\nTerm Spreads (10-Year minus 2-Year):")
    print(f"  Spot Curve 10y - 2y Spread = {float(spot_10_2):.4%} ({float(spot_10_2) * 10000:.2f} bps)")
    print(f"  Par Curve 10y - 2y Spread  = {float(par_10_2):.4%} ({float(par_10_2) * 10000:.2f} bps)")

3Where the canon breaks

Financial textbooks often assume that benchmark Treasury yields quoted on financial media are pure spot rates. In reality, published Constant Maturity Treasury (CMT) rates represent par yields estimated from on-the-run benchmark issues. Because on-the-run issues enjoy superior market liquidity and special repo financing advantages, they trade at an on-the-run liquidity premium, depressing measured par yields relative to off-the-run bonds. Discounting off-the-run or corporate cash flows using unadjusted CMT par yields introduces systematic pricing errors.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A 10-year Treasury par yield equals the 10-year zero-coupon spot rate.”

    A 10-year par yield is the coupon rate on a bond paying 20 semiannual cash flows. It is an annuity-weighted average of all 20 spot rates between 6 months and 10 years. Only a zero-coupon bond has a yield equal to the spot rate.

  2. “When the yield curve inverts, par yields fall below spot rates.”

    In an inverted term structure, early spot rates exceed later spot rates. Because the par bond's early coupon cash flows are discounted at higher rates, the par coupon rate must exceed the terminal spot rate to achieve par pricing: \(c^*(\tau) > s(\tau) > f(\tau-1, \tau)\).

  3. “A negative 10-2 term spread directly causes an economic recession.”

    The term spread is a market pricing signal reflecting policy stance, expected growth, and inflation premia. Inversion reflects aggressive central bank tightening and market anticipation of future economic slowdown; it is an indicator, not the causal engine.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Par yield from discount factors

Given annual discount factors \(Z(1) = 0.960000\) and \(Z(2) = 0.910000\), compute the 2-year annual par yield \(c^*(2)\) as a percentage.

Solution

Apply Definition 4.1: \(c^*(2) = \frac{1 - Z(2)}{Z(1) + Z(2)} = \frac{1 - 0.910000}{0.960000 + 0.910000} = \frac{0.090000}{1.870000} \approx 0.048128\) or 4.813%.

M2 · Term spread calculation

If the 2-year spot rate is 4.00% and the 10-year spot rate is 4.75%, calculate the 10-2 term spread \(S(2, 10)\) in basis points.

Solution

By Definition 4.2, \(S(2, 10) = s(10) - s(2) = 0.0475 - 0.0400 = 0.0075\). In basis points: \(0.0075 \times 10{,}000 = 75\text{ bps}\).

M3 · Semiannual par yield conversion

Given semiannual discount factors \(Z(0.5) = 0.980000\) and \(Z(1.0) = 0.955000\), compute the 1-year semiannual bond-equivalent par yield \(c_2^*(1.0)\).

Solution

Using the semiannual par yield formula: \(c_2^*(1.0) = 2 \times \frac{1 - Z(1.0)}{Z(0.5) + Z(1.0)} = 2 \times \frac{0.045000}{1.935000} \approx 2 \times 0.0232558 = 0.046512\) or 4.651%.

Proof and Mathematical Rigor

P1 · Par yield on a flat term structure

Prove that under a flat term structure where \(Z(k) = (1+s)^{-k}\) for constant \(s > 0\), the annual par yield \(c^*(\tau)\) identically equals \(s\) for all \(\tau \ge 1\).

Solution

The annuity factor is \(A(\tau) = \sum_{k=1}^\tau (1+s)^{-k} = \frac{1 - (1+s)^{-\tau}}{s} = \frac{1 - Z(\tau)}{s}\). By Definition 4.1, \(c^*(\tau) = \frac{1 - Z(\tau)}{A(\tau)} = \frac{1 - Z(\tau)}{[1 - Z(\tau)] / s} = s\). Thus the par yield identically equals the spot rate across all maturities.

P2 · Par yield as a weighted average of forward rates

Prove that the annual par yield \(c^*(\tau)\) can be represented as a convex combination of forward rates: \(c^*(\tau) = \sum_{k=1}^\tau w_k f(k-1, k)\) with \(\sum_{k=1}^\tau w_k = 1\) and \(w_k \ge 0\).

Solution

Observe telescoping sum \(1 - Z(\tau) = \sum_{k=1}^\tau [Z(k-1) - Z(k)]\) with \(Z(0) = 1\). Since \(Z(k-1) - Z(k) = Z(k) f(k-1, k)\), we have \(1 - Z(\tau) = \sum_{k=1}^\tau Z(k) f(k-1, k)\). Dividing by \(A(\tau) = \sum_{j=1}^\tau Z(j)\) yields weights \(w_k = \frac{Z(k)}{A(\tau)} > 0\) that sum to 1.

P3 · Monotonicity of par curves

Prove that if the spot curve \(s(\tau)\) is strictly increasing in \(\tau\), then the par curve \(c^*(\tau)\) is also strictly increasing in \(\tau\).

Solution

From Theorem 4.2, when \(s(\tau)\) is strictly increasing, each incremental forward rate satisfies \(f(\tau, \tau+1) > s(\tau+1) > c^*(\tau)\). Because the par yield is an annuity-weighted average of forward rates, appending a period with a forward rate strictly above the prior average strictly increases the new average: \(c^*(\tau+1) > c^*(\tau)\).

Empirical and Replicated Properties

E1 · Historical recession lead times

In historical US Treasury records from 1960 to 2020, what is the empirical lead time between 10-2 curve inversion and economic recession?

Solution

Replicated empirical evidence establishes that yield curve inversions preceded every NBER-dated US recession since 1960 with only one false signal in 1966. The average lead time from initial inversion to cycle peak is approximately 14 months, with an empirical range of 8 to 22 months.

E2 · Term premium compression under QE

How did central bank large-scale asset purchase programs (quantitative easing) impact the 10-2 term spread during the 2010–2020 period?

Solution

Published central bank empirical research establishes that quantitative easing removed substantial duration risk from private balance sheets. This supply absorption compressed the 10-year term premium by an estimated 50 to 100 basis points, flattening the 10-2 term spread even while policy rates were held near zero.

E3 · Forecasting power: 10y-3m vs 10y-2y

Compare the empirical forecasting accuracy of the 10-Year minus 3-Month spread against the 10-Year minus 2-Year spread in predicting economic downturns.

Solution

Empirical analyses by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York demonstrate that the 10-Year minus 3-Month spread achieves higher statistical pseudo-R-squared values in probit recession models over 2-to-6 quarter horizons, because the 3-month rate cleanly reflects current policy stance without anticipatory risk premia.

Research Formulation

R1 · Probit recession forecasting model

Formulate an econometric probit model using the 10-2 term spread to predict the 12-month forward probability of an economic recession.

Solution

Define indicator \(Y_{t+12} = 1\) if month \(t+12\) is an NBER recession month, and 0 otherwise. Formulate the probit specification \(P(Y_{t+12} = 1 \mid S_t) = \Phi(\beta_0 + \beta_1 S_t)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and \(S_t = y_{10,t} - y_{2,t}\). Maximum likelihood estimation yields \(\beta_1 < 0\), establishing that a narrower or inverted spread increases recession probability.

R2 · Emerging market sovereign par curve inversion

Explain why an emerging market sovereign experiencing severe fiscal distress may exhibit an inverted par curve even under double-digit inflation expectations.

Solution

In distressed sovereign debt markets, curve shape is dominated by credit default hazard rather than monetary policy or inflation expectations. When refinancing risks are acute over the immediate 1-to-2 year horizon, short-dated credit default spreads widen dramatically, driving front-end yields above long-dated yields and causing par curve inversion.

R3 · Unanticipated policy rate shock transmission

Explain why an unanticipated 50 bps monetary tightening shock causes a larger upward shift in 2-year par yields than in 10-year par yields.

Solution

Short-term spot rates respond almost 1-to-1 to unanticipated policy rate adjustments. However, monetary policy shocks exhibit mean reversion over multi-year horizons. Since a 10-year par yield averages spot rates across a 10-year window, the temporary rate increase is diluted, producing an upward shift of only 10 to 15 bps and flattening the curve.

7Active retrieval quiz

  1. What is the annual par yield for a 1-year horizon with discount factor \(Z(1)\)?

    Options

    For \(\tau = 1\), \(c^*(1) = \frac{1 - Z(1)}{Z(1)} = \frac{1}{Z(1)} - 1 = s(1)\) (Definition 4.1).

  2. Under an upward-sloping spot curve, which relationship holds between spot rates \(s(\tau)\), forward rates \(f(\tau-1, \tau)\), and par yields \(c^*(\tau)\) for \(\tau \ge 2\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 4.2, forward rates lie above spot rates, which in turn lie above par yields in an upward-sloping term structure.

  3. If a bond's coupon rate is strictly higher than the par yield for its maturity, the bond must trade at:

    Options

    Coupons greater than the par yield increase total cash flows above the par-pricing level, producing a premium price.

  4. What does a negative 10-Year minus 2-Year Treasury spread indicate?

    Options

    When 2-year yields exceed 10-year yields, the spread is negative, defining an inverted curve.

  5. If \(Z(1) = 0.952381\) (corresponding to \(s(1) = 5.0\%\)), what is the 1-year annual par yield \(c^*(1)\) in percent?

    \(c^*(1) = (1 - 0.952381) / 0.952381 \approx 0.0500\) or 5.0%.

  6. If the 10-year Treasury yield is 4.50% and the 2-year Treasury yield is 3.75%, what is the 10-2 term spread in basis points?

    \((0.0450 - 0.0375) \times 10{,}000 = 75\text{ bps}\).

  7. Why does a par bond's yield to maturity exactly equal its coupon rate?

    Options

    When a bond trades at par, purchase price equals redemption face value, so the IRR equals the coupon rate.

  8. Can the 10-year par yield be directly observed without constructing a discount curve or tracking on-the-run benchmark issues?

    Options

    Existing seasoned bonds rarely trade at exactly par, so par yields must be extracted from fitted discount curves or newly auctioned benchmarks.
