Fixed-income pricing begins with the exact mapping between observable transaction prices and annualized discount rates. Because fixed-coupon bonds promise contractual cash flows, the inverse relationship between price and yield provides the foundational metric for debt comparison across issuers and maturities. Mastering this nonlinear function and its numerical inversion via bisection is essential for building arbitrage-free curve engines and risk management systems.

1Fixed-Coupon Bond Pricing and Cash Flows

Consider a standard fixed-coupon bond with face value \(F\), nominal annual coupon rate \(c_{\text{ann}}\), coupon payment frequency \(m\) times per year, and \(n\) remaining coupon periods to maturity. Each period, the bond pays a coupon \(C = c F\), where \(c = c_{\text{ann}} / m\) is the periodic coupon rate, and at maturity period \(n\) it returns the principal face value \(F\).

Definition 1.1 (Bond Price-Yield Relation)

Let a bond have contractual cash flows \(CF_k = c F\) for \(k = 1, \dots, n-1\) and \(CF_n = c F + F\). For a constant periodic discount yield \(y > -1\), the bond price–yield relation is the function \(P: (-1, \infty) \to (0, \infty)\) given by:

\[ P(y) = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{c F}{(1 + y)^k} + \frac{F}{(1 + y)^n} \]

Using the finite geometric sum for an annuity, for \(y \neq 0\), the closed form is:

\[ P(y) = c F \left[\frac{1 - (1 + y)^{-n}}{y}\right] + F (1 + y)^{-n} \]

When \(y = 0\), \(P(0) = n c F + F\).

Theorem 1.1 (Price-Yield Strict Monotonicity and Convexity)

Let the bond parameters satisfy the following explicit conditions:

  1. Assumption 1: Face value \(F > 0\).
  2. Assumption 2: Periodic coupon rate \(c \ge 0\).
  3. Assumption 3: Total periods \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) with \(n \ge 1\).
  4. Assumption 4: Periodic yield \(y \in (-1, \infty)\).

Then the bond pricing function \(P(y)\) is strictly decreasing and strictly convex on \((-1, \infty)\). That is, \(\frac{dP}{dy} < 0\) and \(\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} > 0\) for all \(y > -1\), with asymptotic limits \(\lim_{y \to -1^+} P(y) = \infty\) and \(\lim_{y \to \infty} P(y) = 0\).

Proof

Differentiating \(P(y) = \sum_{k=1}^n CF_k (1 + y)^{-k}\) term by term with respect to \(y\):

\[ \frac{dP}{dy} = -\sum_{k=1}^n k CF_k (1 + y)^{-(k + 1)} \]

Under Assumptions 1–3, every cash flow \(CF_k \ge 0\) for \(k < n\) and \(CF_n = F(1 + c) > 0\). Since \(1 + y > 0\) by Assumption 4 and \(k \ge 1\), every term in the sum is non-negative and the final term \(n F (1 + c)(1 + y)^{-(n+1)} > 0\) is strictly positive. Therefore, \(\frac{dP}{dy} < 0\), establishing strict monotonicity.

Differentiating a second time:

\[ \frac{d^2P}{dy^2} = \sum_{k=1}^n k(k + 1) CF_k (1 + y)^{-(k + 2)} \]

Every term is strictly positive for \(y > -1\), which proves strict convexity \(\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} > 0\). Finally, as \(y \to -1^+\), \((1 + y)^{-n} \to \infty\), so \(P(y) \to \infty\). As \(y \to \infty\), each term \((1 + y)^{-k} \to 0\), so \(P(y) \to 0\).

2Yield to Maturity and Root-Finding

Definition 1.2 (Yield to Maturity)

For an observed bond market dirty price \(P_{\text{market}} > 0\), the yield to maturity (YTM) is the unique periodic discount rate \(y^* \in (-1, \infty)\) satisfying the equation:

\[ P(y^*) = P_{\text{market}} \]

The yield to maturity represents the internal rate of return of the contractual bond cash flows, setting the net present value of buying the bond to zero: \(-P_{\text{market}} + \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{CF_k}{(1 + y^*)^k} = 0\).

Theorem 1.2 (Existence and Uniqueness of Yield to Maturity)

Let Assumptions 1–3 hold. For any target dirty price \(P_{\text{market}} \in (0, \infty)\), there exists a unique periodic yield to maturity \(y^* \in (-1, \infty)\) such that \(P(y^*) = P_{\text{market}}\).

Proof

By Theorem 1.1, \(P: (-1, \infty) \to (0, \infty)\) is continuous and strictly decreasing, with image \(P((-1, \infty)) = (0, \infty)\). By the Intermediate Value Theorem, since \(P_{\text{market}} \in (0, \infty)\), there exists \(y^* \in (-1, \infty)\) such that \(P(y^*) = P_{\text{market}}\). Furthermore, strict monotonicity guarantees that \(P(y_1) \neq P(y_2)\) whenever \(y_1 \neq y_2\), establishing uniqueness.

Remark 1.1 (Numerical Solution by Bisection)

Because \(P(y) = P_{\text{market}}\) is a high-degree polynomial in \(v = (1 + y)^{-1}\), no general closed-form algebraic solution exists for \(n \ge 5\) by the Abel-Ruffini theorem. However, strict monotonicity guarantees that the bisection algorithm converges deterministically. Starting from an initial bracket \([y_{\text{low}}, y_{\text{high}}]\) with \(P(y_{\text{low}}) > P_{\text{market}} > P(y_{\text{high}})\), each iteration halves the uncertainty interval: \(|y^{(k)} - y^*| \le \frac{y_{\text{high}} - y_{\text{low}}}{2^k}\). Within 20 iterations, interval width shrinks by a factor exceeding \(10^6\).

Lab 1 · Price-Yield Grid and Bisection Yield to Maturity

SIMULATED: A 20-period bond with face $1,000 and 5% periodic coupon is evaluated across yields from 1% to 9%. The price strictly falls as yield rises. For target price $1,050.00, bisection identifies the unique yield within half a cent.

"""Lab FI1-L01-1: Bond price-yield relation and yield to maturity by bisection.

SIMULATED: fixed-coupon bond cash flows on a synthetic grid. Money is in integer cents;
yields are rational fractions or floats. Standard library only; no network.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction


def compute_unrounded_price(face_cents: int, coupon_rate: Fraction, periods: int, y: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Compute exact rational dirty price of a coupon bond on a coupon date."""
    if y == 0:
        return Fraction(face_cents) * (1 + coupon_rate * periods)
    v = Fraction(1, 1) / (1 + y)
    vn = v**periods
    annuity = (1 - vn) / y
    return Fraction(face_cents) * (coupon_rate * annuity + vn)


def solve_ytm(dirty_cents: int, face_cents: int, coupon_rate: Fraction, periods: int) -> Fraction:
    """Solve for yield to maturity per period using bisection."""
    low = Fraction(-1, 2)
    while compute_unrounded_price(face_cents, coupon_rate, periods, low) < dirty_cents:
        low = Fraction(low - 1, 2)

    high = Fraction(1, 1)
    while compute_unrounded_price(face_cents, coupon_rate, periods, high) > dirty_cents:
        high = high * 2

    tol = Fraction(1, 2)  # half cent tolerance
    target = Fraction(dirty_cents, 1)
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = (low + high) / 2
        p_mid = compute_unrounded_price(face_cents, coupon_rate, periods, mid)
        if abs(p_mid - target) < tol:
            return mid
        if p_mid > target:
            low = mid
        else:
            high = mid
    raise ValueError("Bisection did not converge")


def main() -> None:
    face_cents = 100_000  # $1,000.00 face value
    coupon_rate = Fraction(5, 100)  # 5.0% coupon per period
    periods = 20  # 20 periods

    yield_grid = [
        Fraction(1, 100),
        Fraction(3, 100),
        Fraction(5, 100),
        Fraction(7, 100),
        Fraction(9, 100),
    ]

    print("Yield Grid Pricing (face = $1000.00, coupon = 5.0%, periods = 20):")
    prices_cents: list[int] = []
    for y in yield_grid:
        unrounded = compute_unrounded_price(face_cents, coupon_rate, periods, y)
        price_cents = math.floor(unrounded + Fraction(1, 2))
        prices_cents.append(price_cents)
        print(f"  y = {float(y):.2%}: price = ${price_cents / 100:.2f} ({price_cents} cents)")

    # Verify strict monotonicity
    is_decreasing = all(prices_cents[i] > prices_cents[i + 1] for i in range(len(prices_cents) - 1))
    print(f"Monotonicity verified (price strictly falls as yield rises): {is_decreasing}")

    # Solve YTM for target price $1050.00 (105,000 cents)
    target_price = 105_000
    solved_ytm = solve_ytm(target_price, face_cents, coupon_rate, periods)
    repriced_unrounded = compute_unrounded_price(face_cents, coupon_rate, periods, solved_ytm)
    repriced_cents = math.floor(repriced_unrounded + Fraction(1, 2))
    residual_cents = repriced_cents - target_price

    print(f"YTM for target ${target_price / 100:.2f}:")
    print(f"  Solved YTM = {float(solved_ytm):.6%}")
    print(f"  Repriced price = ${repriced_cents / 100:.2f} ({repriced_cents} cents)")
    print(f"  Residual = {residual_cents} cents (|error| < 0.5 cents)")

3Where the canon breaks

Textbooks frequently describe yield to maturity as the single return earned by an investor who holds the bond to maturity. This description fails because yield to maturity implicitly assumes that all intermediate coupon cash flows are reinvested at exactly the constant rate \(y^*\) over the entire life of the bond. In real financial markets, future interest rates fluctuate stochastically, creating reinvestment risk. If future rates drop, coupons are reinvested at lower yields, resulting in a realized compound return strictly below the initial yield to maturity.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A bond's yield to maturity is the rate of return guaranteed to be realized by the investor if held to maturity.”

    The realized holding-period return equals the initial yield to maturity if and only if every coupon is reinvested at that exact rate until the final maturity date. When reinvestment rates vary, the realized holding return diverges substantially from the initial yield.

  2. “A bond priced at a premium above par is always overpriced and offers a poorer investment than a discount bond.”

    Premium bonds trade above face value because their contractual coupon rate exceeds the current market required yield. In an efficient market, both premium and discount bonds with identical credit risk and maturity offer market-clearing risk-adjusted expected returns.

  3. “Because price is an inverse function of yield, a 100 bp yield increase reduces bond price by the exact same dollar amount that a 100 bp decrease increases it.”

    Strict convexity (\(P''(y) > 0\)) dictates that the price-yield curve curves upward. Consequently, a given decrease in yield produces a strictly larger dollar price gain than the dollar price loss caused by an equal-sized increase in yield.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Closed-form bond pricing

A bond has face value $1,000, annual coupon rate 6.0% paid semiannually, and 5 years to maturity. The prevailing annual yield to maturity is 5.0% compounded semiannually. Compute the exact dirty price on a coupon payment date to the cent.

Solution

The periodic coupon rate is \(c = 0.06 / 2 = 0.03\), so each semiannual coupon is \(C = 1000 \times 0.03 = 30\) dollars. The number of periods is \(n = 5 \times 2 = 10\). The periodic yield is \(y = 0.05 / 2 = 0.025\). The discount factor over 10 periods is \(v^{10} = (1.025)^{-10} \approx 0.781198\). The annuity factor is \(\frac{1 - 0.781198}{0.025} \approx 8.752064\). Present value of coupons is \(30 \times 8.752064 = 262.5619\) dollars. Present value of principal is \(1000 \times 0.781198 = 781.1984\) dollars. Total dirty price is \(262.5619 + 781.1984 = 1043.76\) dollars (104,376 cents).

M2 · Par bond identity

Show that when the periodic yield \(y\) equals the periodic coupon rate \(c > 0\), the bond price equals face value \(F\) for any number of periods \(n \ge 1\).

Solution

Substitute \(c = y\) into the closed-form pricing formula: \(P(y) = y F \left[\frac{1 - (1 + y)^{-n}}{y}\right] + F (1 + y)^{-n}\). The periodic yield \(y\) cancels directly in the annuity term, leaving \(F [1 - (1 + y)^{-n}] + F (1 + y)^{-n} = F - F(1 + y)^{-n} + F(1 + y)^{-n} = F\). Thus, whenever coupon rate equals yield, the bond trades at par regardless of maturity.

M3 · Zero-coupon bond pricing

Compute the price of a 10-year zero-coupon Treasury bond with face value $100,000 assuming a semiannual yield of 4.0% per annum.

Solution

For a zero-coupon bond, \(c = 0\). The number of semiannual periods is \(n = 10 \times 2 = 20\). The semiannual yield is \(y = 0.04 / 2 = 0.02\). The price is given by \(P = F (1 + y)^{-n} = 100{,}000 \times (1.02)^{-20} \approx 100{,}000 \times 0.6729713 = 67{,}297.13\) dollars (6,729,713 cents).

Proof

P1 · Strict convexity proof

Prove that for any fixed-coupon bond satisfying Assumptions 1–3, the price function \(P(y)\) satisfies \(\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} > 0\) for all \(y > -1\).

Solution

The price function is \(P(y) = \sum_{k=1}^n CF_k (1 + y)^{-k}\). Since \(y > -1\), the function \((1 + y)^{-k}\) is infinitely differentiable. Taking the first derivative yields \(\frac{dP}{dy} = -\sum_{k=1}^n k CF_k (1 + y)^{-k - 1}\). Differentiating a second time yields \(\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} = \sum_{k=1}^n k(k + 1) CF_k (1 + y)^{-k - 2}\). Since \(k \ge 1\), we have \(k(k + 1) \ge 2 > 0\). Under Assumption 4, \(1 + y > 0\), so \((1 + y)^{-k-2} > 0\). Cash flows satisfy \(CF_k \ge 0\) for \(k < n\) and \(CF_n = F(1 + c) > 0\). Because every term is non-negative and the final term is strictly positive, the second derivative is strictly positive, proving strict convexity.

P2 · Bisection error bound

Prove that the bisection algorithm applied to a continuous strictly monotonic function on \([a_0, b_0]\) with \(f(a_0) f(b_0) < 0\) converges to the root \(x^*\) with absolute error bounded by \(\frac{b_0 - a_0}{2^k}\) after \(k\) steps.

Solution

At iteration 0, the root lies in the bracket \([a_0, b_0]\) of length \(L_0 = b_0 - a_0\). At step 1, midpoint \(m_0 = (a_0 + b_0)/2\) is computed. The sub-interval containing the sign change is chosen as \([a_1, b_1]\), which has length \(L_1 = L_0 / 2\). By mathematical induction, after \(k\) steps the bracket \([a_k, b_k]\) has length \(L_k = (b_0 - a_0)/2^k\). The midpoint \(m_k = (a_k + b_k)/2\) is at most distance \(L_k / 2\) from any point in the interval, so \(|m_k - x^*| \le \frac{b_0 - a_0}{2^{k+1}} < \frac{b_0 - a_0}{2^k}\). As \(k \to \infty\), \(2^{-k} \to 0\), ensuring guaranteed convergence.

P3 · Asymptotic yield limits

Prove that for a fixed-coupon bond with \(F > 0\) and \(c \ge 0\), \(\lim_{y \to -1^+} P(y) = \infty\) and \(\lim_{y \to \infty} P(y) = 0\).

Solution

Write \(P(y) = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} c F (1 + y)^{-k} + F(1 + c)(1 + y)^{-n}\). As \(y \to -1^+\), let \(u = 1 + y > 0\). As \(u \to 0^+\), \(u^{-n} \to \infty\) since \(n \ge 1\). Because \(F(1 + c) > 0\) and all coupon terms are non-negative, the sum diverges to \(+\infty\). Conversely, as \(y \to \infty\), \(u = 1 + y \to \infty\), so \(u^{-k} \to 0\) for every \(k \ge 1\). As a finite sum of terms each converging to 0, \(\lim_{y \to \infty} P(y) = 0\).

Empirical

E1 · Coupon effect on price volatility

Compare the percentage price drop for a 30-year 2.0% coupon bond versus a 30-year 8.0% coupon bond when annual semiannual yields increase from 4.0% to 5.0%.

Solution

For the 2.0% coupon bond (\(c=0.01\), \(n=60\)): at \(y=0.02\), price is \(1000 \times [0.01 \times \frac{1 - 1.02^{-60}}{0.02} + 1.02^{-60}] = 653.45\). At \(y=0.025\), price is \(1000 \times [0.01 \times \frac{1 - 1.025^{-60}}{0.025} + 1.025^{-60}] = 535.89\). Price change is \((535.89 - 653.45) / 653.45 = -17.99\%\). For the 8.0% coupon bond (\(c=0.04\)): at \(y=0.02\), price is \(1695.22\). At \(y=0.025\), price is \(1463.18\). Price change is \((1463.18 - 1695.22) / 1695.22 = -13.69\%\). The lower coupon bond experiences greater percentage price decline because a larger fraction of its present value is received in the distant principal payment.

E2 · Premium vs discount pricing

Compute prices for three 10-year semiannual bonds with coupon rates 3%, 5%, and 7% when the market yield is 5%. Verify the premium-par-discount relationship.

Solution

For \(F = 1000\), \(n = 20\), \(y = 0.025\): for \(c = 0.015\) (3% coupon), price is \(15 \times 15.58916 + 1000 \times 0.610271 = 844.11\) dollars (discount bond, \(P < 1000\)). For \(c = 0.025\) (5% coupon), price is \(25 \times 15.58916 + 1000 \times 0.610271 = 1000.00\) dollars (par bond, \(P = 1000\)). For \(c = 0.035\) (7% coupon), price is \(35 \times 15.58916 + 1000 \times 0.610271 = 1155.89\) dollars (premium bond, \(P > 1000\)).

E3 · Negative yields in European debt

During the 2019–2021 period, German Bunds traded at negative yields. Calculate the price of a 5-year annual bond with 0.0% coupon trading at a yield to maturity of -0.50% per annum.

Solution

For a zero-coupon bond with \(F = 100\), \(n = 5\), and \(y = -0.005\): \(P = 100 \times (1 + y)^{-n} = 100 \times (1 - 0.005)^{-5} = 100 \times (0.995)^{-5} \approx 102.54\) euros. The bond trades at a premium of 102.54 because holding cash incurs negative storage and deposit facility rates, making investors willing to pay a premium for guaranteed capital return.

Research

R1 · Newton-Raphson vs bisection efficiency

Formulate the Newton-Raphson update rule for solving yield to maturity. Discuss its convergence order versus bisection and identify why trading systems retain bisection fallbacks.

Solution

The target function is \(g(y) = P(y) - P_{\text{market}}\). The Newton update is \(y_{k+1} = y_k - \frac{g(y_k)}{g'(y_k)} = y_k + \frac{P(y_k) - P_{\text{market}}}{\sum_{t=1}^n t CF_t (1 + y_k)^{-t-1}}\). Near the root, Newton-Raphson achieves quadratic convergence (\(e_{k+1} \approx C e_k^2\)), doubling correct significant digits each iteration. However, if initialized far from the root or in extreme market conditions (deep discount or negative yields), Newton steps can overshoot or leave the domain \((-1, \infty)\). Bisection provides unconditional linear convergence and cannot fail, making hybrid Brent or bisection fallbacks standard in production engines.

R2 · Reinvestment risk and realized return

Derive the formula for the realized annual compound return of a coupon bond when coupons are reinvested at a constant reinvestment rate \(r_{\text{re}} \neq y^*\).

Solution

At maturity \(n\), all coupons accumulate at reinvestment rate \(r_{\text{re}}\) per period. Total accumulated terminal wealth is \(W_n = C \sum_{k=1}^n (1 + r_{\text{re}})^{n-k} + F = C \left[\frac{(1 + r_{\text{re}})^n - 1}{r_{\text{re}}}\right] + F\). The realized periodic compound return \(R\) satisfies \(P_0 (1 + R)^n = W_n\), giving \(R = \left(\frac{W_n}{P_0}\right)^{1/n} - 1\). If \(r_{\text{re}} = y^*\), then \(W_n = P_0 (1 + y^*)^n\) and \(R = y^*\). If \(r_{\text{re}} < y^*\), then \(R < y^*\), demonstrating the impact of coupon reinvestment drag.

R3 · Perpetual debt pricing

Derive the price of a console bond (perpetuity) paying coupon \(C\) each period forever. Show that its yield to maturity has an exact closed form and verify its monotonicity.

Solution

For an infinite sequence of cash flows \(C\) at periodic yield \(y > 0\), the price is the sum of an infinite geometric series: \(P = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{C}{(1 + y)^k} = \frac{C}{1 + y} \frac{1}{1 - (1+y)^{-1}} = \frac{C}{y}\). The yield to maturity can be solved analytically without numerical iteration: \(y^* = \frac{C}{P_{\text{market}}}\). Differentiating yields \(\frac{dP}{dy} = -\frac{C}{y^2} < 0\) and \(\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} = \frac{2C}{y^3} > 0\), confirming strict monotonicity and convexity analytically.

7Retrieval quiz

  1. What is the sign of the first derivative of a fixed-coupon bond's price with respect to its periodic yield for \(y > -1\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 1.1, \(\frac{dP}{dy} = -\sum k CF_k (1 + y)^{-(k+1)} < 0\) everywhere on \((-1, \infty)\).

  2. When a bond's annual coupon rate equals its annual yield to maturity, what is its dirty price on a coupon payment date?

    Options

    When \(c = y\), the annuity and principal terms combine to equal face value \(F\) exactly.

  3. A 10-year bond pays a 5.0% annual coupon semiannually. If the annual yield to maturity is 5.0% compounded semiannually, what is the price of the bond per $1,000 face value?

    Coupon rate equals yield, so the bond trades at exactly par ($1,000).

  4. Why does bisection guarantee finding the yield to maturity for any positive bond price?

    Options

    Continuity and strict monotonicity guarantee a single sign change and prevent cycling or divergence.

  5. A bond pays an annual coupon of $50 at the end of each year and matures in 1 year, paying $1,000 face value. What is the total cash flow received at maturity in dollars?

    At maturity the bondholder receives coupon plus face: \(50 + 1000 = 1{,}050\) dollars.

  6. What does strict convexity of the price-yield relation imply about price changes for symmetric yield shifts \(\pm \Delta y\)?

    Options

    Convexity curves the function upward, magnifying price gains and dampening price losses.

  7. How many semiannual coupon payments remain on a 10-year Treasury note?

    \(10 \times 2 = 20\) semiannual periods.

  8. As the periodic yield \(y \to \infty\), what is the theoretical limit of a fixed-coupon bond's price?

    Options

    Every term \((1 + y)^{-k} \to 0\) as \(y \to \infty\), driving the price to 0.

  9. What implicit assumption does yield to maturity make about coupon cash flows?

    Options

    YTM is a single internal rate of return, which mathematically assumes reinvestment at that same flat rate.

  10. A consol bond pays an annual coupon of $40 in perpetuity. If its market price is $500, what is its yield to maturity in percent?

    \(y = C / P = 40 / 500 = 0.08 = 8\%\).
