Macaulay Duration, Modified Duration, Convexity, and DV01

Uses: bond price–yield relation, yield to maturity, derivative, Taylor's theorem
Introduces: duration, convexity, dv01

Bond prices react non-linearly to changes in benchmark interest rates. Duration and convexity provide the fundamental first- and second-order Taylor series sensitivities that quantify rate risk across trading desks and investment portfolios. By compressing full cash-flow schedules into modular scalar metrics, duration and DV01 enable asset-liability matching, curve hedging, immunization strategies, and regulatory capital calculations across multi-trillion dollar fixed-income institutions.

1Duration: Macaulay and Modified

Consider a bond with periodic cash flows \(C_k\) arriving at dates \(t_k\) for \(k = 1, \dots, N\), priced at yield to maturity \(y\) per period:

\[ P(y) = \sum_{k=1}^N C_k (1 + y)^{-k} \]

Frederick Macaulay (1938) introduced duration as the cash-flow-weighted average time to receipt of funds, which John Hicks (1939) independently recognized as the elasticity of price with respect to the discount factor.

Definition 5.1 (Macaulay and Modified Duration)

The Macaulay duration \(D_{\text{mac}}\) (measured in periods) is the weighted average maturity of the bond's cash flows:

\[ D_{\text{mac}} = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{k=1}^N k \cdot \frac{C_k}{(1+y)^k} \]

Measured in years for a bond with compounding frequency \(m\) per year, \(D_{\text{mac}}^{\text{annual}} = \frac{D_{\text{mac}}}{m}\).

The modified duration \(D_{\text{mod}}\) measures the percentage price sensitivity with respect to a change in yield per period:

\[ D_{\text{mod}} = -\frac{1}{P} \frac{dP}{dy} = \frac{D_{\text{mac}}}{1 + y} \]

On an annualized basis with respect to annual yield \(y_{\text{ann}} = m \times y\), the annual modified duration is:

\[ D_{\text{mod}}^{\text{ann}} = -\frac{1}{P} \frac{dP}{dy_{\text{ann}}} = \frac{D_{\text{mod}}}{m} = \frac{D_{\text{mac}}^{\text{annual}}}{1 + y} \]
Theorem 5.1 (Modified Duration as Price Semielasticity)

Assume the following hypotheses:

  1. Assumption 1: Cash flows \(C_k > 0\) for at least one \(k \in \{1, \dots, N\}\).
  2. Assumption 2: Yield to maturity satisfies \(y > -1\).
  3. Assumption 3: The pricing function \(P(y) = \sum_{k=1}^N C_k (1+y)^{-k}\) is continuously differentiable on \((-1, \infty)\).

Then the modified duration satisfies \(D_{\text{mod}} = \frac{D_{\text{mac}}}{1+y}\), and the first-order approximation for relative price change under yield shift \(\Delta y\) is:

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \Delta y \]
Proof

Differentiating \(P(y)\) with respect to \(y\) using the power rule:

\[ \frac{dP}{dy} = \sum_{k=1}^N C_k (-k)(1+y)^{-k-1} = -\frac{1}{1+y} \sum_{k=1}^N k C_k (1+y)^{-k} \]

Dividing by \(-P(y)\) yields:

\[ -\frac{1}{P} \frac{dP}{dy} = \frac{1}{1+y} \left( \frac{1}{P} \sum_{k=1}^N k C_k (1+y)^{-k} \right) = \frac{D_{\text{mac}}}{1+y} = D_{\text{mod}} \]

By first-order Taylor expansion about \(y\), \(\Delta P = P'(y) \Delta y + o(\Delta y) = -P D_{\text{mod}} \Delta y + o(\Delta y)\). Dividing by \(P\) gives \(\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \Delta y\).

2Convexity and Second-Order Dynamics

Because the price-yield relationship is strictly convex, a linear duration approximation underestimates price increases when yields fall and overestimates price declines when yields rise. The second derivative captures this curvature.

Definition 5.2 (Convexity)

The convexity \(C\) (in periods squared) is the normalized second derivative of the bond price with respect to periodic yield:

\[ C = \frac{1}{P} \frac{d^2 P}{dy^2} = \frac{1}{P (1+y)^2} \sum_{k=1}^N k(k+1) \frac{C_k}{(1+y)^k} \]

For frequency \(m\) compounding, annualized convexity (in years squared) is \(C_{\text{ann}} = \frac{C}{m^2}\).

Theorem 5.2 (Second-Order Taylor Price Expansion)

Under Assumptions 1–3, with \(P(y)\) twice continuously differentiable on \((-1, \infty)\), the fractional price change for an annual yield shock \(\Delta y_{\text{ann}}\) satisfies:

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} = -D_{\text{mod}}^{\text{ann}} \Delta y_{\text{ann}} + \frac{1}{2} C_{\text{ann}} (\Delta y_{\text{ann}})^2 + o((\Delta y_{\text{ann}})^2) \]
Proof

Differentiating \(\frac{dP}{dy}\) again with respect to \(y\):

\[ \frac{d^2 P}{dy^2} = \frac{d}{dy} \left[ -(1+y)^{-1} \sum_{k=1}^N k C_k (1+y)^{-k} \right] = \sum_{k=1}^N k(k+1) C_k (1+y)^{-k-2} = \frac{1}{(1+y)^2} \sum_{k=1}^N k(k+1) C_k (1+y)^{-k} \]

By Taylor's theorem with Peano remainder, \(\Delta P = P'(y) \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} P''(y) (\Delta y)^2 + o((\Delta y)^2)\). Dividing both sides by \(P\) gives \(\frac{\Delta P}{P} = \frac{P'}{P} \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} \frac{P''}{P} (\Delta y)^2 + o((\Delta y)^2) = -D_{\text{mod}} \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} C (\Delta y)^2 + o((\Delta y)^2)\). Converting to annualized yield \(\Delta y_{\text{ann}} = m \Delta y\) yields the stated annualized equation.

3Dollar Duration and DV01

Institutional portfolio managers aggregate interest rate exposures across millions of bonds by converting percentage sensitivities into absolute currency units.

Definition 5.3 (DV01 and Dollar Duration)

The dollar duration (or money duration) \(D_{\$}\) is the absolute dollar price change per unit change in annualized yield:

\[ D_{\$} = -\frac{dP}{dy_{\text{ann}}} = P \times D_{\text{mod}}^{\text{ann}} \]

The DV01 (Dollar Value of an 01, also known as basis point value or BPV) is the dollar price change resulting from a 1 basis point (0.0001, or 0.01%) parallel increase in annualized yield:

\[ \text{DV01} = -\Delta P_{(+1\text{ bp})} \approx P \times D_{\text{mod}}^{\text{ann}} \times 0.0001 = \frac{D_{\$}}{10{,}000} \]

For a portfolio of \(J\) bonds where bond \(j\) has market value \(P_j\) and modified duration \(D_{\text{mod}, j}^{\text{ann}}\), portfolio DV01 is linear:

\[ \text{DV01}_{\text{port}} = \sum_{j=1}^J \text{DV01}_j = 0.0001 \sum_{j=1}^J P_j D_{\text{mod}, j}^{\text{ann}} \]
Example 5.1 (Analytical Duration and DV01 Calculation)

Consider a 10-year Treasury bond with face value $1,000, 5.0% annual coupon paid semiannually ($25 per period, 20 periods), trading at an annual yield of 6.0% (3.0% per period). The base price is \(P = \$925.6126\).

  1. Weighted cash flow sum: \(\sum_{k=1}^{20} k C_k (1.03)^{-k} \approx 14{,}484.51\).
  2. Macaulay duration: \(D_{\text{mac}} = \frac{14{,}484.51}{925.6126} \approx 15.6486\) periods = \(7.8243\) years.
  3. Annual modified duration: \(D_{\text{mod}}^{\text{ann}} = \frac{7.8243}{1.03} \approx 7.5964\) years.
  4. Annual convexity: \(C_{\text{ann}} \approx 73.18\) years\(^2\).
  5. DV01: \(\text{DV01} = 925.6126 \times 7.5964 \times 0.0001 \approx \$0.7031\) per $1,000 face.
Remark 5.1 (Convexity is Always Desirable for Long Positions)

Because \(\frac{1}{2} C (\Delta y)^2 > 0\) regardless of whether \(\Delta y > 0\) or \(\Delta y < 0\), positive convexity guarantees that a bond's price gains more when yields decline than it loses when yields rise by an identical magnitude. Investors pay a premium (accept a lower yield) for bonds with higher convexity.

Lab 1 · Duration, Convexity, and DV01 with Finite-Difference Verification

SIMULATED: Evaluates analytical Macaulay duration, modified duration, convexity, and DV01 for a 10-year semiannual bond, verifying each analytical metric against central finite differences with step size h = 1e-5.

"""Lab FI1-L05-1: Duration, convexity, and DV01 with numerical finite-difference verification.

SIMULATED: 10-year fixed coupon bond evaluated at a semiannual yield.
Calculates analytical Macaulay duration, modified duration, convexity, and DV01,
holding them to central finite differences.
Standard library only; no network.
"""

from __future__ import annotations


def bond_price(face: float, coupon: float, periods: int, y: float) -> float:
    """Calculate bond price from periodic yield y."""
    v = 1.0 / (1.0 + y)
    annuity = (1.0 - v**periods) / y if y != 0.0 else float(periods)
    return coupon * annuity + face * (v**periods)


def main() -> None:
    face = 1000.0  # $1,000.00 face value
    coupon_annual = 0.05  # 5.0% annual coupon
    m = 2  # semiannual compounding frequency
    periods = 20  # 10 years * 2 = 20 periods
    c = (coupon_annual * face) / m  # $25.00 per period
    y_annual = 0.06  # 6.0% annual yield
    y = y_annual / m  # 3.0% yield per period

    # 1. Base price
    p0 = bond_price(face, c, periods, y)
    print(f"Bond Characteristics (Face = ${face:.2f}, Coupon = {coupon_annual:.1%}, 10yr Semiannual):")
    print(f"  Yield = {y_annual:.2%} per annum ({y:.2%} per period)")
    print(f"  Price P = ${p0:.4f}")

    # 2. Analytical Greeks
    # Macaulay duration in periods
    sum_t_cf = sum(k * c / ((1.0 + y) ** k) for k in range(1, periods + 1))
    sum_t_cf += periods * face / ((1.0 + y) ** periods)
    d_mac_periods = sum_t_cf / p0
    d_mac_years = d_mac_periods / m

    # Modified duration: D_mod = D_mac / (1 + y)
    d_mod_periods = d_mac_periods / (1.0 + y)
    d_mod_annual = d_mod_periods / m

    # Convexity: C = (1/P) * d^2 P / dy^2
    sum_t_t1_cf = sum(k * (k + 1) * c / ((1.0 + y) ** k) for k in range(1, periods + 1))
    sum_t_t1_cf += periods * (periods + 1) * face / ((1.0 + y) ** periods)
    convexity_periods = sum_t_t1_cf / (p0 * ((1.0 + y) ** 2))
    convexity_annual = convexity_periods / (m**2)

    # DV01: price change for 1 bp (0.0001) shift in annual yield
    dv01_analytic = p0 * d_mod_annual * 0.0001

    print("\nAnalytical Sensitivity Metrics:")
    print(f"  Macaulay Duration = {d_mac_years:.4f} years ({d_mac_periods:.4f} periods)")
    print(f"  Modified Duration = {d_mod_annual:.4f} years ({d_mod_periods:.4f} per period)")
    print(f"  Convexity         = {convexity_annual:.4f} years^2")
    print(f"  DV01              = ${dv01_analytic:.4f} per $1000 face")

    # 3. Numerical Verification via Central Finite Differences
    # Step size in annual yield
    h_annual = 1e-5
    h_period = h_annual / m

    p_up = bond_price(face, c, periods, y + h_period)
    p_down = bond_price(face, c, periods, y - h_period)

    # Numerical first derivative w.r.t annual yield
    dp_dy_num = (p_up - p_down) / (2.0 * h_annual)
    d_mod_num = -dp_dy_num / p0
    dv01_num = -(p_up - p_down) / (2.0 * h_annual) * 0.0001

    # Numerical second derivative w.r.t annual yield
    d2p_dy2_num = (p_up - 2.0 * p0 + p_down) / (h_annual**2)
    convexity_num = d2p_dy2_num / p0

    print("\nFinite-Difference Verification (step size h = 1e-5):")
    print(f"  Modified Duration (Numerical) = {d_mod_num:.4f}, |Error| = {abs(d_mod_num - d_mod_annual):.2e}")
    print(
        f"  Convexity (Numerical)         = {convexity_num:.4f}, |Error| = {abs(convexity_num - convexity_annual):.2e}"
    )
    print(f"  DV01 (Numerical)              = ${dv01_num:.4f}, |Error| = {abs(dv01_num - dv01_analytic):.2e}")

    assert abs(d_mod_num - d_mod_annual) < 1e-7
    assert abs(convexity_num - convexity_annual) < 1e-5
    assert abs(dv01_num - dv01_analytic) < 1e-7
    print("All sensitivity formulas verified against finite differences.")


main()

4Where the canon breaks

Textbook duration analysis assumes parallel shifts in a flat yield curve. In actual trading and risk management, several key realities break naive duration hedging:

  • Non-Parallel Yield Curve Shifts: Real yield curves twist, steepen, and invert. A portfolio immunized to a parallel shock (\(\sum \text{DV01} = 0\)) remains exposed to curve slope and curvature shifts. Trading desks deploy key rate durations or principal component analysis (PCA) to hedge key tenors independently.
  • Negative Convexity: Mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and callable corporate bonds contain embedded prepayment and call options. When interest rates drop, homeowners refinance and issuers call bonds, capping price gains. Consequently, MBS exhibit negative convexity (\(C < 0\)), causing prices to fall rapidly when rates rise but fail to rise when rates fall.
  • Spread Duration Divergence: Corporate bonds are exposed to both benchmark Treasury rate shifts and credit spread fluctuations. Empirical evidence demonstrates that for high-yield credit, spread duration dominates interest-rate duration, causing price dynamics to decorrelate from Treasuries.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Macaulay duration is simply the contractual maturity date of the bond.”

    For any coupon-bearing bond, Macaulay duration is strictly less than contractual maturity because coupons return cash flow prior to the maturity date. Macaulay duration equals contractual maturity only for pure zero-coupon bonds.

  2. “A bond's duration is constant regardless of where yields move.”

    Duration is itself a function of yield. As yields rise, distant cash flows are discounted more heavily, shifting cash flow weight to earlier periods and reducing duration (\(\frac{d D_{\text{mod}}}{dy} < 0\)). This dynamic is captured by convexity.

  3. “Convexity is always positive for all fixed-income assets.”

    While straight, non-callable option-free bonds exhibit strictly positive convexity, securities with embedded borrower options (such as US agency mortgage pass-throughs and callable corporate debt) exhibit significant negative convexity when rates fall.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Modified duration calculation

A bond has a Macaulay duration of 8.40 years and an annual yield of 5.0% compounded annually. Calculate its modified duration.

Solution

Apply Definition 5.1: \(D_{\text{mod}} = \frac{D_{\text{mac}}}{1 + y} = \frac{8.40}{1 + 0.05} = \frac{8.40}{1.05} = 8.00\) years.

M2 · DV01 from modified duration

A portfolio has a market value of $25,000,000 and an annual modified duration of 6.50 years. Compute the portfolio DV01.

Solution

By Definition 5.3, \(\text{DV01} = P \times D_{\text{mod}} \times 0.0001 = \$25{,}000{,}000 \times 6.50 \times 0.0001 = \$16{,}250\) per basis point.

M3 · Second-order price change estimation

A bond priced at $100 has modified duration \(D_{\text{mod}} = 7.0\) years and convexity \(C = 60.0\) years\(^2\). Estimate the percentage price change if yields rise by 100 basis points (\(\Delta y = +0.01\)).

Solution

Apply Theorem 5.2: \(\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} C (\Delta y)^2 = -7.0 \times 0.01 + \frac{1}{2} \times 60.0 \times (0.01)^2 = -0.0700 + 0.0030 = -0.0670\) or \(-6.70\%\).

Proof and Mathematical Rigor

P1 · Zero-coupon duration and convexity

Prove that for a zero-coupon bond maturing at \(\tau\) years under annual compounding, \(D_{\text{mac}} = \tau\) and convexity \(C_{\text{ann}} = \frac{\tau(\tau+1)}{(1+y)^2}\).

Solution

A zero-coupon bond has a single cash flow \(F\) at \(t = \tau\). Its price is \(P = F(1+y)^{-\tau}\). The Macaulay duration is \(D_{\text{mac}} = \frac{\tau \times F(1+y)^{-\tau}}{P} = \tau\). Differentiating twice: \(P''(y) = \tau(\tau+1) F (1+y)^{-\tau-2}\). Dividing by \(P\) yields \(C = \frac{P''}{P} = \frac{\tau(\tau+1)}{(1+y)^2}\).

P2 · Duration of a perpetuity

Prove that the Macaulay duration of a perpetual bond (perpetuity) paying constant coupon \(c\) each period at periodic yield \(y\) is \(D_{\text{mac}} = \frac{1+y}{y}\).

Solution

The price of the perpetuity is \(P = \frac{c}{y}\). The weighted cash flow sum is \(S = \sum_{k=1}^\infty k c (1+y)^{-k} = c (1+y)^{-1} \sum_{k=1}^\infty k (1+y)^{-(k-1)} = \frac{c}{1+y} \frac{1}{(1 - (1+y)^{-1})^2} = \frac{c (1+y)}{y^2}\). Dividing by \(P = \frac{c}{y}\) yields \(D_{\text{mac}} = \frac{c(1+y)/y^2}{c/y} = \frac{1+y}{y}\).

P3 · Duration monotonicity with coupon

Prove that for bonds of identical maturity \(\tau\) and yield \(y\), the Macaulay duration is strictly decreasing in the coupon rate \(c\).

Solution

A coupon bond can be viewed as a portfolio of a zero-coupon bond maturing at \(\tau\) (duration \(\tau\)) and an annuity of coupons (duration \(D_{\text{ann}} < \tau\)). As coupon rate \(c\) increases, the portfolio weight on the shorter-duration annuity increases, while the weight on the principal repayment decreases. A weighted average with increasing weight on the smaller value strictly decreases.

Empirical and Replicated Properties

E1 · MBS negative convexity and convexity hedging

Explain how refinancing waves during interest rate declines induce negative convexity in US mortgage-backed security portfolios and how dealers hedge this risk.

Solution

When benchmark rates drop, homeowners refinance existing fixed-rate mortgages, returning principal early to investors. This shortens the MBS effective maturity, reducing duration as prices rise (negative convexity). Mortgage dealers hedge by dynamically selling Treasuries or receiver swaptions as rates drop, amplifying market yield rallies.

E2 · Empirical performance of linear duration vs full revaluation

In historical stress testing during the 1994 and 2022 Federal Reserve tightening campaigns, quantify the error of first-order duration models versus full revaluation for a 30-year Treasury bond experiencing a +200 bps yield shock.

Solution

For a 30-year bond with modified duration of 18 years and convexity of 450 years\(^2\), a +200 bps shock predicts a \(-36.0\%\) price move under linear duration. The convexity term contributes \(+9.0\%\), resulting in a net second-order predicted decline of \(-27.0\%\). The linear approximation overstates price destruction by 900 basis points.

E3 · High-yield spread duration empirical decoupling

Empirical fixed-income research documents that during credit crises, high-yield corporate bonds often rally despite rising benchmark Treasury yields. Explain this phenomenon.

Solution

High-yield corporate bonds trade as hybrids between credit risk and equity. When credit conditions improve, credit spreads tighten by hundreds of basis points, overwhelming modest rises in benchmark Treasury rates. Empirical spread duration and default recovery expectations dominate risk-free duration sensitivity.

Research Formulation

R1 · Key rate duration matrix decomposition

Formulate the key rate duration vector for immunization against non-parallel yield curve movements.

Solution

Partition the zero curve into \(K\) key maturities \(\{\tau_1, \dots, \tau_K\}\) (e.g., 2y, 5y, 10y, 30y). Define key rate duration \(D_k = -\frac{1}{P} \frac{\partial P}{\partial s(\tau_k)}\) for localized triangular yield perturbations. A portfolio is immunized against arbitrary yield shifts if the net portfolio key rate vector \(\mathbf{D}_{\text{port}} = \sum_{j} w_j \mathbf{D}_j = \mathbf{0}\), eliminating both slope and curvature exposure.

R2 · Immunization under stochastic term structure models

Contrast classical Fisher-Weil duration matching with immunization under a one-factor Vasicek short-rate model.

Solution

Classical Fisher-Weil duration assumes instantaneous parallel shifts across all maturities. Under a Vasicek model with mean reversion parameter \(\kappa\), long-term yields move by factor \(B(\tau)/\tau = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa \tau}}{\kappa \tau}\) of short rate shifts. Model-consistent stochastic duration weights each cash flow by \(B(t_k)\) rather than \(t_k\), eliminating term structure misspecification risk.

R3 · Convexity trading and swap curve steepeners

Formulate a duration-neutral, positive-convexity barbell-bullet trade and derive its return profile as a function of market volatility.

Solution

Construct a barbell portfolio of 2-year and 30-year bonds matched in DV01 to a 10-year bullet position (\(\text{DV01}_{\text{barbell}} = \text{DV01}_{\text{bullet}}\)). Because convexity scales quadratically with maturity, \(C_{\text{barbell}} > C_{\text{bullet}}\). Under zero net duration, the position earns \(\frac{1}{2}(C_{\text{barbell}} - C_{\text{bullet}}) \sigma^2 \Delta t\) from realized rate volatility, funded by a negative carry roll-down cost.

8Active retrieval quiz

  1. What is the relationship between Macaulay duration \(D_{\text{mac}}\) and modified duration \(D_{\text{mod}}\) for periodic yield \(y\)?

    Options

    By Definition 5.1 and Theorem 5.1, \(D_{\text{mod}} = D_{\text{mac}} / (1 + y)\).

  2. For a pure zero-coupon bond maturing in 7 years, what is its Macaulay duration?

    Options

    A zero-coupon bond has 100% of its cash flow at maturity, so its cash-flow-weighted time to maturity equals 7 years.

  3. If a bond has Macaulay duration of 8.40 years and annual yield of 5.0% under annual compounding, what is its modified duration in years?

    \(D_{\text{mod}} = 8.40 / 1.05 = 8.00\) years.

  4. What is the DV01 in dollars for a $25,000,000 bond portfolio with an annual modified duration of 6.50 years?

    \(\text{DV01} = 25{,}000{,}000 \times 6.50 \times 0.0001 = \$16{,}250\).

  5. Why does positive convexity benefit a bondholder under both rate increases and rate declines?

    Options

    Because the second derivative is positive, \(\frac{1}{2} C (\Delta y)^2 > 0\) adds positive value regardless of the sign of \(\Delta y\).

  6. Which class of fixed-income instruments frequently exhibits negative convexity?

    Options

    Homeowner prepayment options cap price gains as yields fall, producing negative convexity in MBS.

  7. Using the second-order Taylor approximation with \(D_{\text{mod}} = 7.0\) and \(C = 60.0\), what is the percentage price change (\(\Delta P / P \times 100\)) for a +100 bps (+0.01) yield shock?

    \(-7.0 \times 0.01 + 0.5 \times 60.0 \times (0.01)^2 = -0.0700 + 0.0030 = -0.0670\) or -6.70%.

  8. How does portfolio DV01 aggregate across multiple bond holdings?

    Options

    Because differentiation is a linear operator, dollar sensitivity aggregates as the simple arithmetic sum of position DV01s.
