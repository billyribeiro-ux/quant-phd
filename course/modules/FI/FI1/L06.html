Bootstrapping the Zero Curve

Because zero-coupon government bonds trade actively only at short maturities, the long-dated discount curve is unobservable directly. Curve bootstrapping solves this fundamental market incompleteness through triangular back-substitution, recursively extracting zero-coupon discount factors from a cascade of coupon-bearing benchmark bonds. By construction, bootstrapping guarantees zero pricing error on all calibration instruments, providing the exact arbitrage-free foundation for derivatives pricing and relative-value analytics.

1The Bootstrapping Algorithm

Suppose the market contains \(N\) benchmark coupon bonds with sequential maturities \(t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_N\), where each bond \(n\) matures at \(t_n\) and pays periodic coupon cash flows \(C_{n,k}\) at dates \(t_k\) (\(k \le n\)) and principal \(F_n\) at date \(t_n\). Let \(P_n\) denote the observed dirty market price of bond \(n\).

Definition 6.1 (Curve Bootstrapping)

Curve bootstrapping is the recursive algorithm that solves the lower-triangular linear system of cash-flow equations for discount factors \(Z(t_1), Z(t_2), \dots, Z(t_N)\):

\[ P_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k) + (C_{n,n} + F_n) Z(t_n), \quad \text{for } n = 1, 2, \dots, N \]

Given previously determined discount factors \(Z(t_1), \dots, Z(t_{n-1})\), the \(n\)-th discount factor is obtained by direct back-substitution:

\[ Z(t_n) = \frac{P_n - \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k)}{C_{n,n} + F_n} \]

For par bonds with periodic coupon rate \(c_n\) per period and \(P_n = F_n = 1\), this simplifies to:

\[ Z(t_n) = \frac{1 - c_n \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} Z(t_k)}{1 + c_n} \]
Theorem 6.1 (Bootstrapping Existence and Uniqueness)

Assume the following hypotheses:

  1. Assumption 1: The cash-flow matrix \(\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}\) is lower triangular with entries \(C_{n,k} \ge 0\) for \(k \le n\) and strictly positive diagonal entries \(C_{n,n} + F_n > 0\) for all \(n = 1, \dots, N\).
  2. Assumption 2: Market prices \(P_n > 0\) are strictly positive for all \(n = 1, \dots, N\).

Then the system \(\mathbf{C} \mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{P}\) possesses a unique solution vector \(\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^N\). Furthermore, if \(P_n > \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k)\) at each step, then all bootstrapped discount factors are strictly positive: \(Z(t_n) > 0\).

Proof

The cash-flow matrix \(\mathbf{C}\) is lower triangular with diagonal elements \(C_{n,n} + F_n\). The determinant is \(\det(\mathbf{C}) = \prod_{n=1}^N (C_{n,n} + F_n)\). By Assumption 1, every diagonal element is strictly positive, so \(\det(\mathbf{C}) > 0\). The matrix is therefore non-singular, and by the fundamental theorem of linear algebra, \(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{C}^{-1} \mathbf{P}\) exists and is unique. Positivity of \(Z(t_n)\) follows immediately from the back-substitution numerator being strictly positive by hypothesis.

2Exact Repricing and Spot Yield Derivation

Theorem 6.2 (Exact Repricing Theorem)

Let \(\{Z(t_n)\}_{n=1}^N\) be discount factors bootstrapped from benchmark bonds \(\{B_n\}_{n=1}^N\) with dirty market prices \(\{P_n\}_{n=1}^N\). Then for every calibration bond \(n \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), the theoretical model price \(\hat{P}_n = \sum_{k=1}^n C_{n,k} Z(t_k) + F_n Z(t_n)\) satisfies:

\[ \hat{P}_n - P_n = 0 \]

identically, with zero pricing error.

Proof

Substitute the explicit expression for \(Z(t_n)\) from Definition 6.1 into the theoretical price \(\hat{P}_n\):

\[ \hat{P}_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k) + (C_{n,n} + F_n) \left[ \frac{P_n - \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k)}{C_{n,n} + F_n} \right] = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k) + P_n - \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k) = P_n \]

Hence \(\hat{P}_n = P_n\) holds with mathematical identity.

Example 6.1 (Three-Period Semiannual Par Bootstrap)

Consider three sequential semiannual par bonds with par coupons \(c_1 = 3.0\%\), \(c_2 = 3.6\%\), and \(c_3 = 4.0\%\) per annum on $100 face value. Per-period coupon rates are \(c_1/2 = 0.015\), \(c_2/2 = 0.018\), and \(c_3/2 = 0.020\).

  1. Period 1 (\(t_1 = 0.5\) yr): \[Z(0.5) = \frac{100}{100 + 1.50} = \frac{100}{101.50} \approx 0.985222\]
  2. Period 2 (\(t_2 = 1.0\) yr): \[Z(1.0) = \frac{100 - 1.80 \times Z(0.5)}{100 + 1.80} = \frac{100 - 1.80 \times 0.985222}{101.80} = \frac{100 - 1.773400}{101.80} = \frac{98.226600}{101.80} \approx 0.964898\]
  3. Period 3 (\(t_3 = 1.5\) yr): \[Z(1.5) = \frac{100 - 2.00 \times [Z(0.5) + Z(1.0)]}{100 + 2.00} = \frac{100 - 2.00 \times [0.985222 + 0.964898]}{102.00} = \frac{100 - 3.900240}{102.00} \approx 0.942155\]

Annualized bond-equivalent zero rates are: \(s(0.5) = 2 \times [Z(0.5)^{-1} - 1] = 3.00\%\), \(s(1.0) = 2 \times [Z(1.0)^{-1/2} - 1] \approx 3.608\%\), and \(s(1.5) = 2 \times [Z(1.5)^{-1/3} - 1] \approx 4.020\%\).

"""Lab FI1-L06-1: Bootstrapping the zero curve from coupon bonds.

SIMULATED: synthetic coupon bonds across 10 semiannual maturities.
Honest-pending note: real H.15 constant-maturity snapshot is pending Board redistribution
verification under Phase 2 Architect Ruling 5. This lab runs on a SIMULATED curve.
Standard library only; no network.

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction


def bootstrap_zero_curve(bonds: list[dict[str, object]]) -> list[tuple[int, float, float]]:
    """Bootstrap discount factors and zero rates from a sequential set of coupon bonds.

    Returns a list of tuples: (period n, discount factor DF_n, zero rate z_n per period).
    """
    n_bonds = len(bonds)
    dfs: list[Fraction] = [Fraction(1, 1)]  # DF_0 = 1
    results: list[tuple[int, float, float]] = []

    for n in range(1, n_bonds + 1):
        b = bonds[n - 1]
        fc = b["face_cents"]
        pc = b["price_cents"]
        assert isinstance(fc, int)
        assert isinstance(pc, int)
        cr = b["coupon_rate"]
        assert isinstance(cr, Fraction)

        price_ratio = Fraction(pc, fc)
        sum_prior_df = sum(dfs[1:n])
        df_n = (price_ratio - cr * sum_prior_df) / (1 + cr)
        dfs.append(df_n)

        z_n = float(df_n) ** (-1.0 / n) - 1.0
        results.append((n, float(df_n), z_n))

    return results


def main() -> None:
    # 1. Construct a set of 10 par bonds with ascending coupon rates
    # Maturities 1 through 10 semiannual periods (0.5 to 5.0 years)
    face_cents = 100_000  # $1,000.00
    par_coupons_annual = [0.030, 0.033, 0.036, 0.038, 0.040, 0.042, 0.044, 0.045, 0.046, 0.047]

    bonds = []
    for n, c_ann in enumerate(par_coupons_annual, start=1):
        c_period = Fraction(round(c_ann / 2 * 100_000), 100_000)
        # Par bond price equals face value on coupon date
        bonds.append(
            {
                "periods": n,
                "coupon_rate": c_period,
                "face_cents": face_cents,
                "price_cents": face_cents,
            }
        )

    # 2. Run bootstrapping
    curve = bootstrap_zero_curve(bonds)

    print("Bootstrapped Zero Curve (from 10 Semiannual Par Bonds):")
    print("Period | Maturity | Par Coupon (Ann) | Discount Factor | Zero Rate (Ann)")
    print("-" * 72)
    for n, df_n, zn in curve:
        mat_years = n * 0.5
        c_ann = par_coupons_annual[n - 1]
        zn_annual = zn * 2.0  # bond equivalent annual zero rate
        print(f"{n:6d} | {mat_years:6.1f} yr | {c_ann:16.3%} | {df_n:15.6f} | {zn_annual:15.4%}")
    print("-" * 72)

    # 3. Repricing check: reprice each input bond using bootstrapped discount factors
    print("\nRepricing Verification against Input Prices ($1000.00 Par):")
    max_error = 0.0
    for b in bonds:
        period_num = b["periods"]
        assert isinstance(period_num, int)
        c = b["coupon_rate"]
        assert isinstance(c, Fraction)
        c_float = float(c)
        # Cash flows: c * face at k=1..n-1, (1+c)*face at n
        pv = 0.0
        for k in range(1, period_num):
            df_k = curve[k - 1][1]
            pv += c_float * (face_cents / 100.0) * df_k
        df_n = curve[period_num - 1][1]
        pv += (1.0 + c_float) * (face_cents / 100.0) * df_n

        err = abs(pv - 1000.0)
        if err > max_error:
            max_error = err
        print(f"  Bond {period_num:2d} (tau = {period_num * 0.5:4.1f} yr): Repriced = ${pv:.6f}, |Error| = {err:.2e}")
        assert err < 1e-9

    print(f"\nMax repricing error across all 10 bonds = {max_error:.2e}")
    print("Bootstrapping exact repricing theorem verified: error < 1e-9 on all bonds.")

3Where the canon breaks

While curve bootstrapping is mathematically exact on ideal synthetic grids, applying it to real market bonds encounters severe structural flaws:

  • Sawtooth Forward Rates from Interpolation: Bootstrapping only yields discount factors at discrete benchmark coupon dates. Interpolating zero rates linearly between maturities creates discontinuous first derivatives, which translates into wild, sawtooth-shaped forward curves that oscillate unphysically.
  • Cash-Flow Date Asynchrony: Off-the-run Treasury bonds do not share payment dates. Constructing a lower-triangular matrix requires interpolation across intermediate coupon dates, introducing circular dependence and model risk.
  • Noise Propagation and Overfitting: Because bootstrapping forces zero repricing error on every input instrument, market microstructure noise, bid-ask bounce, and liquidity premia in individual bonds directly distort the extracted discount curve, propagating forward to all subsequent maturities.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Bootstrapping involves solving a complex numerical optimization or non-linear regression.”

    Bootstrapping is a direct algebraic algorithm solving a lower-triangular linear system. No optimization, loss minimization, or iterative gradient search is required; each discount factor is computed in closed form by back-substitution.

  2. “Linear interpolation between bootstrapped zero rates preserves smooth forward curves.”

    Linear interpolation of zero rates introduces discontinuous jumps in the slope of the discount curve. Because forward rates depend on the derivative of spot rates (\(f = s + \tau s'\)), linear zero interpolation produces step-function forward rates with violent jumps at benchmark tenors.

  3. “Bootstrapping can be run on arbitrary collections of bonds without ordering.”

    Bootstrapping strictly requires bonds to be sorted by increasing maturity. Each subsequent bond must introduce at most one new cash-flow maturity date to maintain the lower-triangular structure necessary for recursive back-substitution.

5Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · One-period zero discount factor

A 6-month Treasury bill with face value $1,000 trades at a dirty price of $985.00. Calculate the 6-month discount factor \(Z(0.5)\) and annualized semiannual spot rate.

Solution

The bill has no intermediate coupon, so \(Z(0.5) = \frac{P}{F} = \frac{985.00}{1000.00} = 0.985000\). The bond-equivalent annual spot rate is \(s_2(0.5) = 2 \times [Z(0.5)^{-1} - 1] = 2 \times [\frac{1}{0.985} - 1] \approx 2 \times 0.015228 = 0.030457\) or 3.046%.

M2 · Two-period par bond bootstrap

Given \(Z(0.5) = 0.985000\), bootstrap \(Z(1.0)\) from a 1-year annual par bond paying 4.0% semiannual coupon ($2.00 per period on $100 face) trading at par ($100.00).

Solution

Apply Definition 6.1: \(100 = 2.00 \times Z(0.5) + (100 + 2.00) \times Z(1.0) \implies Z(1.0) = \frac{100 - 2.00 \times 0.985000}{102.00} = \frac{100 - 1.970000}{102.00} = \frac{98.030000}{102.00} \approx 0.961078\).

M3 · Repricing verification calculation

Using \(Z(0.5) = 0.985000\) and \(Z(1.0) = 0.961078\), verify the model price of a 1-year bond paying 6.0% semiannual coupon ($3.00 per period on $100 face).

Solution

Compute theoretical price: \(\hat{P} = 3.00 \times Z(0.5) + (100 + 3.00) \times Z(1.0) = 3.00 \times 0.985000 + 103.00 \times 0.961078 = 2.955000 + 98.991034 = 101.946034\). The bond trades at a premium of $101.95.

Proof and Mathematical Rigor

P1 · Error propagation bound in bootstrapping

Prove that an pricing error \(\epsilon_1\) in the first benchmark bond propagates to all subsequent bootstrapped discount factors \(Z(t_n)\) for \(n > 1\).

Solution

From Definition 6.1, \(Z(t_n) = \frac{P_n - \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k)}{C_{n,n} + F_n}\). Differentiating with respect to \(Z(t_1)\) yields \(\frac{\partial Z(t_n)}{\partial Z(t_1)} = -\frac{C_{n,1}}{C_{n,n} + F_n} - \sum_{k=2}^{n-1} \frac{C_{n,k}}{C_{n,n} + F_n} \frac{\partial Z(t_k)}{\partial Z(t_1)}\). By induction, because all coupon cash flows \(C_{n,k} > 0\), the derivative is strictly non-zero, proving that any error in \(Z(t_1)\) cascades through all subsequent tenors.

P2 · Non-negativity condition for bootstrapped discount factors

State and prove the necessary and sufficient condition on bond price \(P_n\) such that the bootstrapped discount factor \(Z(t_n)\) is strictly positive.

Solution

From \(Z(t_n) = \frac{P_n - \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k)}{C_{n,n} + F_n}\) with \(C_{n,n} + F_n > 0\), \(Z(t_n) > 0\) if and only if \(P_n > \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} C_{n,k} Z(t_k)\). Economically, the market price of the bond must exceed the aggregate present value of all intermediate coupon cash flows maturing prior to the final principal repayment date.

P3 · Exact equivalence between par bootstrap and par yield formula

Prove that bootstrapping discount factors from a cascade of par bonds is mathematically equivalent to inverting the par yield equation \(c^*(\tau) = \frac{1 - Z(\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^\tau Z(k)}\).

Solution

Rearranging the par yield formula yields \(c^*(\tau) \sum_{k=1}^\tau Z(k) = 1 - Z(\tau) \implies c^*(\tau) \sum_{k=1}^{\tau-1} Z(k) + (1 + c^*(\tau)) Z(\tau) = 1\). Solving for \(Z(\tau)\) yields \(Z(\tau) = \frac{1 - c^*(\tau) \sum_{k=1}^{\tau-1} Z(k)}{1 + c^*(\tau)}\). This matches the par bond recursive bootstrap formula in Definition 6.1 identically.

Empirical and Replicated Properties

E1 · Spline interpolation versus linear bootstrap

Explain why financial practitioners apply cubic spline or monotonic Hermite interpolation rather than piecewise linear interpolation to bootstrapped zero curves.

Solution

Piecewise linear interpolation of zero rates has non-differentiable corners at benchmark maturities, causing forward rates (\(f = s + \tau s'\)) to display discontinuous step-function jumps. Monotonic cubic or Hermite splines ensure continuous first and second derivatives, producing economically realistic, smooth forward curve trajectories.

E2 · On-the-run versus off-the-run curve divergence

How do yield differences between on-the-run and off-the-run US Treasuries impact bootstrapped discount factors?

Solution

On-the-run Treasuries trade at lower yields due to their liquidity premium and repo market financing advantages. Bootstrapping exclusively from on-the-run bonds yields discount factors that systematically overprice off-the-run debt by 2 to 10 basis points in yield terms, distorting relative-value arbitrage models.

E3 · Negative forward rates under microstructural distortion

Describe how a pricing outlier in a single benchmark Treasury can induce negative forward rates in a bootstrapped yield curve.

Solution

If benchmark bond \(n\) is subject to intense short squeeze pressure, its price spikes, causing its bootstrapped discount factor \(Z(t_n)\) to rise above the preceding discount factor: \(Z(t_n) > Z(t_{n-1})\). Since \(1 + f(t_{n-1}, t_n) = Z(t_{n-1}) / Z(t_n)\), this yields a forward gross return less than 1, implying a negative forward rate.

Research Formulation

R1 · Regularized bootstrapping with smoothing penalty

Formulate an objective function that combines exact repricing on benchmark bonds with a Tikhonov roughness penalty on the second derivative of the forward curve.

Solution

Formulate the constrained optimization problem: \(\min_{Z} \int_0^{T_{\max}} [f''(\tau)]^2 d\tau\) subject to \(\sum_{k=1}^n C_{n,k} Z(t_k) + F_n Z(t_n) = P_n\) for all \(n = 1, \dots, N\). This balances exact fitting of market prices against curvature minimization, penalizing spurious forward curve oscillations.

R2 · Multi-curve bootstrapping in post-2008 money markets

Explain why modern interest-rate derivatives desks bootstrap separate forwarding and discounting curves (dual-curve framework) rather than a single classical curve.

Solution

Prior to 2008, Libor served as both proxy for risk-free discounting and forward floating rates. Following the financial crisis, bank credit risk and liquidity premia caused Libor-OIS spreads to widen dramatically. Modern multi-curve frameworks decouple the discounting curve (bootstrapped from risk-free OIS/SOFR) from forwarding curves (bootstrapped from tenor-specific swap benchmarks).

R3 · Optimal benchmark instrument selection under illiquidity

Formulate an algorithmic filter to select optimal sovereign bonds for bootstrapping when market quotes contain heterogeneous bid-ask spreads and liquidity noise.

Solution

Define an integer programming objective that selects a subset of bonds minimizing cumulative bid-ask spread width and trading concessions while constraining maturity spacing to prevent excessive gaps (\(\Delta t_n \le \delta_{\max}\)). Filter out issues with high repo specials or extreme coupon rates to minimize taxation and liquidity distortions.
