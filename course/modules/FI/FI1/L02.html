Yield Quotation Conventions, Day Counts, and Clean and Dirty Price

Secondary bond trading rarely settles on exact coupon payment dates. Accrued interest arithmetic ensures fair compensation between buyers and sellers for the fraction of the coupon period during which each party held the asset. Because distinct markets institutionalized different calendar conventions and compounding definitions, quantitative engines must translate quotes without ambiguity to prevent pricing discrepancy and trade settlement failure.

1Day-Count Conventions

A bond accrues interest continuously between coupon payment dates. The accrual fraction between a starting date \(D_1\) and an ending date \(D_2\) is determined by a legally binding day-count convention.

Definition 2.1 (Day-Count Convention)

A day-count convention is a specified rule \(\alpha: \mathcal{D} \times \mathcal{D} \to [0, \infty)\) mapping a start date \(D_1\) and end date \(D_2\) with \(D_1 \le D_2\) to a year fraction \(\alpha(D_1, D_2)\). Standard market conventions include:

  1. ACT/360: Uses the actual number of calendar days elapsed divided by 360: \[ \alpha_{\text{ACT/360}}(D_1, D_2) = \frac{\text{ActualDays}(D_1, D_2)}{360} \] Standard in US money markets and commercial paper.
  2. ACT/365: Uses the actual number of calendar days elapsed divided by 365: \[ \alpha_{\text{ACT/365}}(D_1, D_2) = \frac{\text{ActualDays}(D_1, D_2)}{365} \] Standard in UK money markets and Commonwealth sovereign debt.
  3. 30/360 (Bond Basis): Models each calendar month as having exactly 30 days and each year as having 360 days. Letting \(D_1 = (Y_1, M_1, d_1)\) and \(D_2 = (Y_2, M_2, d_2)\), with adjusted days \(d_1^* = \min(d_1, 30)\) and \(d_2^* = \min(d_2, 30)\): \[ \text{Days}_{30/360} = 360(Y_2 - Y_1) + 30(M_2 - M_1) + (d_2^* - d_1^*) \] \[ \alpha_{30/360}(D_1, D_2) = \frac{\text{Days}_{30/360}}{360} \] Standard in US corporate and municipal bond markets.
Theorem 2.1 (Day-Count Ratio Invariant)

For any two distinct calendar dates \(D_1 < D_2\), the year fraction computed under ACT/360 is strictly larger than under ACT/365, satisfying the exact rational scaling identity:

\[ \frac{\alpha_{\text{ACT/360}}(D_1, D_2)}{\alpha_{\text{ACT/365}}(D_1, D_2)} = \frac{365}{360} = \frac{73}{72} \approx 1.013889 \]
Proof

Let \(\Delta D = \text{ActualDays}(D_1, D_2) > 0\). By Definition 2.1, \(\alpha_{\text{ACT/360}} = \Delta D / 360\) and \(\alpha_{\text{ACT/365}} = \Delta D / 365\). Dividing the two expressions, \(\Delta D\) cancels algebraically: \(\frac{\Delta D / 360}{\Delta D / 365} = \frac{365}{360} = \frac{73}{72}\). Because \(73/72 > 1\), \(\alpha_{\text{ACT/360}} > \alpha_{\text{ACT/365}}\) for all \(D_2 > D_1\).

2Clean and Dirty Price

Definition 2.2 (Clean and Dirty Price)

In fixed-income markets, bond prices are quoted on a clean basis but settled on a dirty basis:

  1. The clean and dirty price relation articulates market quotes and cash settlements. The dirty price (or invoice price) \(P_{\text{dirty}}\) is the total cash amount paid by the buyer to the seller per unit of face value at settlement.
  2. Accrued interest \(AI\) is the coupon interest earned by the seller from the previous coupon date \(D_{\text{last}}\) up to the settlement date \(D_{\text{settle}}\): \[ AI = \alpha(D_{\text{last}}, D_{\text{settle}}) \times C \] where \(C\) is the full coupon payment for the period and \(\alpha\) is the applicable day-count fraction.
  3. The clean price \(P_{\text{clean}}\) is the quoted price excluding accrued interest: \[ P_{\text{clean}} = P_{\text{dirty}} - AI \]
Theorem 2.2 (Price Continuity and Coupon Discontinuity)

Under constant market yield, the clean price \(P_{\text{clean}}\) is continuous with respect to settlement time, whereas the dirty price \(P_{\text{dirty}}\) exhibits a jump discontinuity of magnitude \(C\) on each coupon date.

Proof

Let \(t\) denote the fraction of the coupon period elapsed since the prior coupon date. As \(t \to 1^-\) (immediately before coupon payment), accrued interest approaches the full coupon: \(\lim_{t \to 1^-} AI(t) = C\). At the instant the coupon is paid (\(t = 1\)), the right to receive the coupon detaches, so the accrued interest resets to zero: \(AI(1^+) = 0\). Thus, \(AI\) exhibits a downward jump of \(-C\). Because the present value of the remaining cash flows drops by \(C\) upon payment of the coupon, the invoice price \(P_{\text{dirty}}\) drops by \(C\). Since \(P_{\text{clean}} = P_{\text{dirty}} - AI\), the jumps in \(P_{\text{dirty}}\) and \(AI\) offset identically, leaving \(P_{\text{clean}}\) continuous across coupon dates.

3Yield Quotation Conventions

Definition 2.3 (Yield Quotation Conventions)

Different financial sectors quote interest rates under distinct yield quotation conventions:

  1. Bond Equivalent Yield (BEY): The annualized yield for semiannual coupon bonds, computed as \(BEY = 2 \times y_{\text{semi}}\), where \(y_{\text{semi}}\) is the semiannual yield to maturity.
  2. Money Market Yield (MMY / CD Equivalent Yield): Simple interest rate based on an ACT/360 day count: \[ y_{\text{MMY}} = \frac{F - P}{P} \times \frac{360}{\Delta D} \]
  3. Discount Yield (Bank Discount Basis): Quoted per unit of face value \(F\), rather than purchase price \(P\): \[ d = \frac{F - P}{F} \times \frac{360}{\Delta D} \implies P = F \left(1 - d \frac{\Delta D}{360}\right) \]
  4. Effective Annual Yield (EAY): Accounts for compounding over the year with frequency \(m\): \[ EAY = \left(1 + \frac{y_{\text{nominal}}}{m}\right)^m - 1 \]

Lab 1 · Day Counts, Accrued Interest, and Quotation Conversions

SIMULATED: Compares ACT/360, ACT/365, and 30/360 on 2024 date pairs, calculates accrued interest and invoice price for a 6% note, and converts a 5.0% discount yield to MMY and BEY.

"""Lab FI1-L02-1: Day-count conventions, accrued interest, and clean and dirty prices.

SIMULATED: synthetic fixed-income security cash flows and date intervals.
Money is in integer cents; day count fractions are rational Fraction instances.
Standard library only; no network.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import datetime
import math
from fractions import Fraction


def day_count_fraction(start: datetime.date, end: datetime.date, convention: str) -> Fraction:
    """Compute day-count fraction for ACT/360, ACT/365, and 30/360."""
    if convention == "ACT/360":
        return Fraction((end - start).days, 360)
    if convention == "ACT/365":
        return Fraction((end - start).days, 365)
    if convention == "30/360":
        d1 = min(start.day, 30)
        d2 = min(end.day, 30)
        days = 360 * (end.year - start.year) + 30 * (end.month - start.month) + (d2 - d1)
        return Fraction(days, 360)
    raise ValueError(f"Unknown convention: {convention}")


def main() -> None:
    # 1. Day count comparison across date pairs
    d1 = datetime.date(2024, 1, 15)
    d2 = datetime.date(2024, 7, 15)
    actual_days_1 = (d2 - d1).days

    f_act360 = day_count_fraction(d1, d2, "ACT/360")
    f_act365 = day_count_fraction(d1, d2, "ACT/365")
    f_30_360 = day_count_fraction(d1, d2, "30/360")

    print(f"Date Interval 1: {d1} to {d2} (actual days = {actual_days_1}):")
    print(f"  ACT/360 fraction = {f_act360} ({float(f_act360):.6f})")
    print(f"  ACT/365 fraction = {f_act365} ({float(f_act365):.6f})")
    print(f"  30/360 fraction  = {f_30_360} ({float(f_30_360):.6f})")
    print(f"  ACT/365 to ACT/360 ratio check (exact 360/365): {f_act365 / f_act360 == Fraction(360, 365)}")

    # February in leap year 2024
    feb1 = datetime.date(2024, 2, 1)
    mar1 = datetime.date(2024, 3, 1)
    actual_days_feb = (mar1 - feb1).days
    feb_360 = day_count_fraction(feb1, mar1, "ACT/360")
    feb_30_360 = day_count_fraction(feb1, mar1, "30/360")
    print(f"\nDate Interval 2: {feb1} to {mar1} (actual leap days = {actual_days_feb}):")
    print(f"  ACT/360 fraction = {feb_360} ({float(feb_360):.6f})")
    print(f"  30/360 fraction  = {feb_30_360} ({float(feb_30_360):.6f}, exactly 1/12 month)")

    # 2. Accrued interest and dirty price computation
    face_cents = 100_000  # $1,000.00 face value
    clean_price_cents = 98_500  # $985.00 quoted clean price
    annual_coupon_rate = Fraction(6, 100)  # 6.0% annual coupon
    semiannual_coupon_cents = math.floor(Fraction(face_cents) * annual_coupon_rate / 2 + Fraction(1, 2))

    # Assume 45 days accrued into a 180-day semiannual coupon period (30/360)
    accrued_fraction = Fraction(45, 180)
    accrued_interest_cents = math.floor(Fraction(semiannual_coupon_cents) * accrued_fraction + Fraction(1, 2))
    dirty_price_cents = clean_price_cents + accrued_interest_cents

    print("\nAccrued Interest and Invoice Price:")
    print(f"  Face Value        = ${face_cents / 100:.2f}")
    print(f"  Clean Price       = ${clean_price_cents / 100:.2f} ({clean_price_cents} cents)")
    print(f"  Semiannual Coupon = ${semiannual_coupon_cents / 100:.2f} ({semiannual_coupon_cents} cents)")
    print(f"  Accrual Fraction  = {accrued_fraction} (45/180 days)")
    print(f"  Accrued Interest  = ${accrued_interest_cents / 100:.2f} ({accrued_interest_cents} cents)")
    print(f"  Dirty Price       = ${dirty_price_cents / 100:.2f} ({dirty_price_cents} cents)")

    # 3. Yield quote conversion: MMY vs BEY
    # A 180-day money-market instrument with discount yield d = 5.0%
    d_mmy = Fraction(5, 100)
    days_m = 180
    # Price per 100: P = 100 * (1 - d * days / 360)
    price_mmy = 100 * (1 - float(d_mmy) * days_m / 360)
    # MMY (money market yield, ACT/360 simple return): (100 - P)/P * (360/days)
    mmy = ((100.0 - price_mmy) / price_mmy) * (360.0 / days_m)
    # BEY (bond equivalent yield, ACT/365): (100 - P)/P * (365/days)
    bey = ((100.0 - price_mmy) / price_mmy) * (365.0 / days_m)
    print("\nYield Quotation Conversion (180-day paper, discount = 5.00%):")
    print(f"  Price = {price_mmy:.4f} per 100")
    print(f"  MMY (ACT/360) = {mmy:.4%}")
    print(f"  BEY (ACT/365) = {bey:.4%}")
    print(f"  BEY / MMY ratio = {bey / mmy:.6f} (matches 365/360: {365 / 360:.6f})")

4Where the canon breaks

Market lore assumes that a quoted clean price cleanly separates capital value from interest accrual. This separation breaks down in distressed debt markets where issuers face bankruptcy or default risk. When an issuer misses a scheduled coupon payment, trading shifts from "and interest" (clean price plus accrued interest) to trading "flat" (dirty price equals clean price with no accrued interest transferred). In flat trading, accrued coupon claims become contingent claims subject to insolvency recovery haircuts rather than predictable contractual transfers.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The clean price is the total cash transferred from the buyer to the seller at trade settlement.”

    The clean price is purely an accounting quote designed to remove the sawtooth effect of coupon accrual. Actual trade settlement requires transferring the dirty invoice price (\(P_{\text{dirty}} = P_{\text{clean}} + AI\)).

  2. “A Treasury bill quoted at a discount yield of 5.0% offers the exact same annualized yield as a Treasury note quoted at a bond-equivalent yield of 5.0%.”

    The bank discount yield divides the price discount by face value \(F\) and assumes a 360-day year. The bond-equivalent yield divides by purchase price \(P < F\) and scales by 365 days, meaning a 5.0% discount yield represents an investment yield noticeably higher than 5.0%.

  3. “Accrued interest represents an additional profit or fee pocketed by the executing broker.”

    Accrued interest is not a transaction fee. It is a contractual pro-rata transfer directly from the buyer to the seller to compensate the seller for the days the bond was held since the last coupon payment.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Accrued interest on 30/360

A corporate bond with face value $100,000 pays an 8.0% annual coupon semiannually on March 15 and September 15. The trade settles on May 15 under 30/360. Calculate the accrued interest in dollars.

Solution

Under 30/360, each month has 30 days. From March 15 to May 15 is exactly 2 full months: \(2 \times 30 = 60\) days. The semiannual coupon period has 180 days. The semiannual coupon is \(C = 100{,}000 \times 0.08 / 2 = 4{,}000\) dollars. Accrued interest is \(AI = 4{,}000 \times \frac{60}{180} = 4{,}000 \times \frac{1}{3} = 1{,}333.33\) dollars.

M2 · Discount to MMY conversion

A 90-day commercial paper is quoted at a discount yield of 4.0% per annum on a $1,000,000 face value. Calculate the purchase price and the money market yield (MMY).

Solution

Price: \(P = F [1 - d \times \frac{90}{360}] = 1{,}000{,}000 \times [1 - 0.04 \times 0.25] = 1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.99 = 990{,}000\) dollars. MMY: \(y_{\text{MMY}} = \frac{F - P}{P} \times \frac{360}{90} = \frac{10{,}000}{990{,}000} \times 4 \approx 0.040404\) or 4.0404%.

M3 · ACT/360 vs ACT/365 comparison

An institutional loan of $10,000,000 accrues interest at 6.0% simple interest over a 182-day period. Compute the total dollar interest owed under ACT/360 versus ACT/365.

Solution

Under ACT/360: \(I_{360} = 10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.06 \times \frac{182}{360} = 303{,}333.33\) dollars. Under ACT/365: \(I_{365} = 10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.06 \times \frac{182}{365} = 299{,}178.08\) dollars. ACT/360 charges \(303{,}333.33 - 299{,}178.08 = 4{,}155.25\) dollars more interest.

Proof

P1 · General discount to investment yield relationship

Prove that for any positive discount rate \(d > 0\) and days to maturity \(\Delta D \in (0, 360)\), the money market yield \(y_{\text{MMY}}\) is strictly greater than the discount yield \(d\).

Solution

By definition, \(P = F (1 - d \frac{\Delta D}{360})\). The money market yield is \(y_{\text{MMY}} = \frac{F - P}{P} \frac{360}{\Delta D} = \frac{F d (\Delta D / 360)}{F (1 - d \Delta D / 360)} \frac{360}{\Delta D} = \frac{d}{1 - d \frac{\Delta D}{360}}\). Since \(d > 0\) and \(\Delta D > 0\), the denominator satisfies \(1 - d \frac{\Delta D}{360} < 1\) (assuming positive price \(P > 0\)). Dividing a positive quantity \(d\) by a denominator strictly between 0 and 1 yields \(\frac{d}{1 - d \Delta D / 360} > d\), proving \(y_{\text{MMY}} > d\) strictly.

P2 · BEY from money market yield

Derive the conversion formula from a money market yield \(y_{\text{MMY}}\) to a bond equivalent yield \(BEY\) for short-term instruments maturing in \(\Delta D \le 182\) days.

Solution

For an instrument with maturity \(\Delta D \le 182\) days, \(BEY\) scales simple return to a 365-day year: \(BEY = \frac{F - P}{P} \frac{365}{\Delta D}\). Since \(y_{\text{MMY}} = \frac{F - P}{P} \frac{360}{\Delta D}\), we have \(\frac{F - P}{P} \frac{1}{\Delta D} = \frac{y_{\text{MMY}}}{360}\). Substituting this into the BEY expression yields \(BEY = y_{\text{MMY}} \times \frac{365}{360}\). For \(\Delta D > 182\), the bond equivalent yield compounds semiannually, requiring solving a quadratic equation to match semiannual compounding.

P3 · Invariance of clean price under instantaneous rollover

Prove that if market yield \(y\) remains unchanged, the clean price \(P_{\text{clean}}(t)\) is differentiable across coupon boundaries.

Solution

Between coupon dates, the full price evolves by compound growth: \(P_{\text{dirty}}(t) = P_{\text{dirty}}(0) (1 + y)^t\). The accrued interest is linear: \(AI(t) = t C\). The clean price is \(P_{\text{clean}}(t) = P_{\text{dirty}}(0) (1 + y)^t - t C\). At coupon boundary \(t = 1\), the new coupon cycle begins with \(P_{\text{dirty}}(1^+) = P_{\text{dirty}}(1^-) - C = P_{\text{dirty}}(0)(1 + y) - C\) and \(AI(1^+) = 0\). Thus \(P_{\text{clean}}(1^+) = P_{\text{dirty}}(0)(1+y) - C = P_{\text{clean}}(1^-)\). The left and right values agree, establishing continuity across the payment boundary.

Empirical

E1 · Leap year distortions

Evaluate the difference in accrued interest for February 2024 (29 days) under ACT/360, ACT/365, and 30/360 for an annual coupon of $10,000.

Solution

Under ACT/360: \(AI = 10{,}000 \times \frac{29}{360} = 805.56\) dollars. Under ACT/365: \(AI = 10{,}000 \times \frac{29}{365} = 794.52\) dollars. Under 30/360: February is treated as having 30 days (from Feb 1 to Mar 1 is 1 full month = 30 days), giving \(AI = 10{,}000 \times \frac{30}{360} = 833.33\) dollars. 30/360 yields the largest accrued interest because it standardizes February to a 30-day month.

E2 · Treasury invoice price calculation

A US Treasury note trades at a clean price quote of 99-16 (meaning 99 and 16/32, or 99.50% of par). Face value is $1,000,000 and accrued interest is $14,250. Calculate the total settlement wire amount.

Solution

Clean price in dollars: \(1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.9950 = 995{,}000\) dollars. Dirty invoice price: \(P_{\text{dirty}} = P_{\text{clean}} + AI = 995{,}000 + 14{,}250 = 1{,}009{,}250\) dollars (100,925,000 cents). The buyer must wire exactly $1,009,250.00 at settlement.

E3 · T-Bill vs T-Note comparison

A 6-month Treasury bill trades at a 4.80% discount yield. A 6-month Treasury note trades at a 4.95% bond-equivalent yield. Determine which security offers the higher annualized investment yield.

Solution

Assuming 182 days: T-bill price per 100 face is \(P = 100 \times (1 - 0.048 \times \frac{182}{360}) = 100 \times (1 - 0.024267) = 97.5733\). The BEY of the T-bill is \(\frac{100 - 97.5733}{97.5733} \times \frac{365}{182} = \frac{2.4267}{97.5733} \times 2.005495 \approx 0.04988\) or 4.988%. The T-bill's BEY of 4.988% exceeds the T-note's BEY of 4.950% by 3.8 basis points, offering higher yield.

Research

R1 · UK Gilt ex-dividend convention

Explain the ex-dividend trading convention historically used in the UK Gilt market. Show how accrued interest can be negative and formulate the resulting invoice price.

Solution

In the UK Gilt market, a bond traded during the ex-dividend period (typically 7 business days prior to coupon payment) settles such that the seller, not the buyer, receives the upcoming coupon payment from the Debt Management Office. To compensate the buyer who owns the bond on the coupon date without receiving the coupon, accrued interest becomes negative: \(AI = -\alpha(D_{\text{settle}}, D_{\text{next}}) \times C\). The dirty invoice price is \(P_{\text{dirty}} = P_{\text{clean}} - |AI| < P_{\text{clean}}\), so the buyer pays strictly less than the clean price.

R2 · Negative interest rate floor in money market yields

Analyze the mathematical behavior of discount yield \(d\) and money market yield \(y_{\text{MMY}}\) when price exceeds par (\(P > F\)) due to negative nominal interest rates.

Solution

When \(P > F\), both the discount yield \(d = \frac{F - P}{F} \frac{360}{\Delta D}\) and the MMY \(y_{\text{MMY}} = \frac{F - P}{P} \frac{360}{\Delta D}\) become negative. Because \(P > F\), dividing by \(P\) yields a smaller absolute value than dividing by \(F\): \(|y_{\text{MMY}}| = \frac{P - F}{P} \frac{360}{\Delta D} < \frac{P - F}{F} \frac{360}{\Delta D} = |d|\). In negative territory, a smaller absolute negative number represents a higher yield: \(y_{\text{MMY}} > d\). The ordering \(y_{\text{MMY}} > d\) remains invariant across positive and negative yield regimes.

R3 · International day-count harmonization

Discuss the market frictions created by disparate day counts across swaps (ACT/360), sovereign bonds (ACT/ACT ICMA), and corporate debt (30/360). Formulate the basis risk incurred in hedging.

Solution

When a trader hedges a fixed-rate bond (accruing on 30/360) using an interest-rate swap (accruing on ACT/360 or ACT/365), cash flow mismatches arise each period: \(\Delta CF = F \times [c_{\text{bond}} \alpha_{30/360} - c_{\text{swap}} \alpha_{\text{ACT/360}}]\). Because the number of actual days varies (28 to 31) while 30/360 remains fixed at 30, the hedge experiences non-zero cash flow tracking error ("day-count basis risk"). Desks must dynamically model day-count cash flow adjustments in curve construction engines.
