A bond's price moves when its yield (defined in Section 1) moves. This lesson uses a SIMULATED bond to show how the price depends on the yield, how its first and second derivatives follow from the cash flows, and how a Taylor expansion approximates the price for a small change in yield. At shifts of \(+0.01\) and \(-0.01\) the second-order Taylor expansion of Theorem 3.1 (Section 3) is off by 15 and 16 whole cents (Lab 1 Part C prints the differences \(-15\) and \(+16\) cents; the unrounded error at \(+0.01\) is \(-15.06\) cents, printed in Lab 1 Part B), and for positive cash flows the sign of that error is the sign of \(-\Delta\) (Proposition 3.2, Section 3).

1A bond's price as a function of its yield

A bond pays cash flows \(c_1, \dots, c_N\), in cents, at the ends of years \(1, \dots, N\). Its yield \(y\) is a decimal with \(y > -1\), and the price is the present value of the cash flows at that yield, with annual compounding. In the terms of M1 (Lesson L05, present value and discount factor) this is the sum of each cash flow times its discount factor \((1+y)^{-t}\):

Definition 1.1 (price as a function of yield)

For \(y > -1\), the price is \(P(y) = \sum_{t=1}^{N} c_t\,(1 + y)^{-t}\), an exact number of cents before rounding (Section 4 discusses the rounding). Shifts in this lesson are decimal changes in yield: a shift of \(0.01\) is 100 basis points. The face value is the principal repaid at maturity; a bond whose price equals its face value is said to be at par.

The lab uses a SIMULATED bond, a generated specification and not a quoted security: face value 100000 cents, a coupon (the periodic interest payment) of 5 percent of face paid each year (5000 cents), ten annual payments, and the face repaid with the tenth coupon. So \(c_t = 5000\) for \(t < 10\) and \(c_{10} = 105000\). The base yield is \(y_0 = 0.04\).

2The first and second derivatives

Proposition 2.1 (derivatives of the price)

\(P\) is infinitely differentiable on \((-1, \infty)\), with

\[P'(y) = -\sum_{t=1}^{N} t\,c_t\,(1+y)^{-t-1}, \qquad P''(y) = \sum_{t=1}^{N} t(t+1)\,c_t\,(1+y)^{-t-2}.\]

If every \(c_t > 0\), then \(P'(y) < 0\) and \(P''(y) > 0\) for every \(y > -1\).

Proof

\(P\) is a finite sum of functions \(y \mapsto c_t (1+y)^{-t}\), each of which is infinitely differentiable on \((-1, \infty)\). Differentiating term by term, \(\tfrac{d}{dy}(1+y)^{-t} = -t(1+y)^{-t-1}\) and \(\tfrac{d^2}{dy^2}(1+y)^{-t} = t(t+1)(1+y)^{-t-2}\), which gives the two formulas. If every \(c_t > 0\), each term of \(P'\) is negative, because \(t\,c_t\,(1+y)^{-t-1} > 0\), and each term of \(P''\) is positive, because \(t(t+1)c_t(1+y)^{-t-2} > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.2 (price at face when the yield equals the coupon rate)

Let \(N \ge 1\), a face value \(F > 0\), and a coupon rate \(r > -1\). Let the coupons be \(rF\) at each year \(t < N\), with the face \(F\) added to the last coupon. If the yield equals the coupon rate \(r\), the price equals \(F\).

Proof

Put \(v = 1/(1+r)\), so \(v\) is defined because \(r > -1\). At yield \(r\) the price is \(\sum_{t=1}^{N} rF\,v^t + F v^N\). If \(r = 0\), every coupon is \(0\) and \(v = 1\), so the price is \(F\). If \(r \ne 0\), then \(1 - v = rv\), and the geometric series of Lesson L06 of M1 gives \(\sum_{t=1}^{N} v^t = v\,(1 - v^N)/(1 - v) = (1 - v^N)/r\). Hence \(rF \sum_{t=1}^N v^t = F(1 - v^N)\), and the price is \(F(1 - v^N) + F v^N = F\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · The SIMULATED bond: derivatives, Taylor errors and the price in cents

The bond is SIMULATED: face 100000 cents, a coupon of 5000 cents a year, ten years, and the face repaid at year 10, priced at base yield \(0.04\). Predicted before running: the coupon rate is 5 percent, so at the yield \(0.05\) the price equals face, 100000 cents (Proposition 2.2), and the printed price there is 100000 cents. Propositions 2.1 and 3.2 predict \(P' < 0\), \(P'' > 0\), and remainder signs that alternate with the sign of the shift, with the quadratic term of the expansion giving the first-order error ratio near 4 and the cubic remainder of Theorem 3.1 giving the second-order error ratio near 8. The lab prints two preview numbers, \(-P'/P\) and \(P''/P\), and does not define them; they belong to unit FI1 of the curriculum (Bond Math & Curve Construction).

"""M2 lab L23: the Taylor expansion of a bond price in its yield. Duration and convexity are previewed, not defined.

SIMULATED: the bond is a generated specification, not a quoted security. Face value 100000 cents, an annual coupon of
5 percent of face (5000 cents a year), ten annual coupons, and the face repaid with the last coupon. Yield compounds
annually, so the price at yield y is P(y) = sum over t = 1..10 of c_t / (1 + y)**t, with c_t the cash flow in cents.
The price is exact rational arithmetic (Fraction) on the float yields, so no rounding enters the price before it is
reported. The two sensitivities P'(y0) and P''(y0) are floats: each is the exact rational value rounded once to the
nearest double, so its relative error is at most 2**-53. The Taylor polynomials are exact given these two float
sensitivities, which they use as exact rationals. Only the reported price and the reported expansion are
integer numbers of cents: each is the unrounded value rounded to the nearest cent with halves to even, which is
Fraction's round(). Floats also appear in the
central-difference checks and in the printed values.
Part A checks the closed-form first and second derivatives of P against central differences. Part B expands P about
the base yield 4 percent, and prints the errors of the first-order and the second-order expansions at steps of
0.01, 0.005 and 0.0025, with the ratio of successive errors. Part C prints the price in cents, exact and from the
second-order expansion. The two numbers printed as previews, -P'/P and P''/P, are only printed; this lab does not
define them.
Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections.abc import Callable
from fractions import Fraction

FACE_CENTS = 100_000
COUPON_CENTS = 5_000
YEARS = 10
BASE_YIELD = 0.04
STEPS: tuple[float, ...] = (0.01, 0.005, 0.0025)


def cash_flow_cents(t: int) -> int:
    """The cash flow in cents at year t = 1..YEARS: a coupon each year, and the face at maturity."""
    if not 1 <= t <= YEARS:
        raise ValueError(f"year must be from 1 to {YEARS}, got {t}")
    return COUPON_CENTS + (FACE_CENTS if t == YEARS else 0)


def growth_factor(y: float) -> Fraction:
    """The exact rational 1 + y for a float yield y > -1; the float is taken as the exact value it stores."""
    if y <= -1.0:
        raise ValueError(f"yield must exceed -1, got {y}")
    return 1 + Fraction(y)


def price(y: float) -> Fraction:
    """The unrounded price P(y) in cents for yield y > -1, with annual compounding, as an exact rational."""
    v = growth_factor(y)
    return sum((Fraction(cash_flow_cents(t)) / v**t for t in range(1, YEARS + 1)), Fraction(0))


def price_first_derivative(y: float) -> float:
    """P'(y) = minus the sum over t of t c_t (1 + y)**(-t - 1), in cents per unit of yield, rounded once to a float."""
    v = growth_factor(y)
    return float(-sum((Fraction(t * cash_flow_cents(t)) / v ** (t + 1) for t in range(1, YEARS + 1)), Fraction(0)))


def price_second_derivative(y: float) -> float:
    """P''(y) = the sum over t of t (t + 1) c_t (1 + y)**(-t - 2), in cents per unit of yield squared, as a float."""
    v = growth_factor(y)
    exact = sum((Fraction(t * (t + 1) * cash_flow_cents(t)) / v ** (t + 2) for t in range(1, YEARS + 1)), Fraction(0))
    return float(exact)


def price_cents(y: float) -> int:
    """The price as an integer number of cents: the unrounded price rounded half to even."""
    return round(price(y))


def taylor_first(y0: float, dy: float) -> Fraction:
    """First-order Taylor polynomial of P about y0, at y0 + dy: exact for the float shift dy and float sensitivity."""
    return price(y0) + Fraction(price_first_derivative(y0)) * Fraction(dy)


def taylor_second(y0: float, dy: float) -> Fraction:
    """Second-order Taylor polynomial of P about y0, at y0 + dy: exact for the float shift dy and sensitivities."""
    return taylor_first(y0, dy) + Fraction(1, 2) * Fraction(price_second_derivative(y0)) * Fraction(dy) ** 2


def central_difference(fun: Callable[[float], float], y: float, h: float) -> float:
    """The central difference (fun(y + h) - fun(y - h)) / (2 h) of a one-variable function; h must be positive."""
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    return (fun(y + h) - fun(y - h)) / (2.0 * h)


def main() -> None:
    y0 = BASE_YIELD
    print("SIMULATED bond: face 100000 cents, 5000 cents a year for 10 years, face repaid at year 10")
    print(f"base yield {y0:.2%}; unrounded price {float(price(y0)):.6f} cents")
    print(f"integer price in cents: {price_cents(y0)}")

    p1 = price_first_derivative(y0)
    p2 = price_second_derivative(y0)
    fd1 = central_difference(lambda y: float(price(y)), y0, 1e-5)
    fd2 = central_difference(price_first_derivative, y0, 1e-4)
    print(f"P'(y0) closed form {p1:.6f}, central difference {fd1:.6f}, difference {abs(p1 - fd1):.1e}")
    print(f"P''(y0) closed form {p2:.6f}, central difference {fd2:.6f}, difference {abs(p2 - fd2):.1e}")

    p0 = float(price(y0))
    print(f"preview only, not defined here: -P'/P = {-p1 / p0:.6f}, P''/P = {p2 / p0:.6f}")

    previous_first = previous_second = Fraction(0)
    for k, dy in enumerate(STEPS):
        exact = price(y0 + dy)
        e1 = exact - taylor_first(y0, dy)
        e2 = exact - taylor_second(y0, dy)
        line = f"shift dy = {dy:<7g} first-order error {float(e1): .6e} second-order error {float(e2): .6e}"
        if k > 0:
            line += f" ratios {float(previous_first / e1):.4f} {float(previous_second / e2):.4f}"
        print(line)
        previous_first, previous_second = e1, e2

    for dy in (0.01, -0.01):
        exact_cents = price_cents(y0 + dy)
        approx_cents = round(taylor_second(y0, dy))
        print(
            f"yield shift {dy:+.2f}: exact price {exact_cents} cents, second-order expansion {approx_cents} cents, "
            f"difference {exact_cents - approx_cents} cents"
        )

3The Taylor expansion and its remainder

Theorem 3.1 (the second-order expansion of the price)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), let \(y_0 > -1\) and \(\Delta\) be such that \(y_0 + \Delta > -1\). Put \(\mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) = P(y_0) + P'(y_0)\Delta\) and \(\mathcal{P}_2(\Delta) = \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) + \tfrac12 P''(y_0)\Delta^2\). Then \(P(y_0+\Delta) = \mathcal{P}_2(\Delta) + R(\Delta)\) with \(|R(\Delta)| \le \tfrac16 M|\Delta|^3\), where \(M\) is the maximum of \(|P'''|\) on the closed interval between \(y_0\) and \(y_0 + \Delta\).

Proof

\(P\) is infinitely differentiable on \((-1, \infty)\) by Proposition 2.1, so \(P'''\) is continuous and the maximum \(M\) exists on the compact interval. The Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder (Lesson L08), applied with three derivatives, gives \(\theta\) between \(y_0\) and \(y_0 + \Delta\) with \(R(\Delta) = \tfrac16 P'''(\theta)\Delta^3\). Then \(|R(\Delta)| \le \tfrac16 M|\Delta|^3\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.2 (the sign of the remainder)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), let \(y_0 > -1\). If every \(c_t > 0\), then \(R(\Delta)\) has the sign of \(-\Delta\) for every \(\Delta \ne 0\) with \(y_0 + \Delta > -1\), where \(R\) is the remainder of Theorem 3.1. In particular \(R(\Delta) < 0\) for \(\Delta > 0\), and \(R(\Delta) > 0\) for \(\Delta < 0\).

Proof

Differentiating \(P''(y) = \sum t(t+1)c_t(1+y)^{-t-2}\) once more gives \(P'''(y) = -\sum t(t+1)(t+2)c_t(1+y)^{-t-3}\), which is negative when every \(c_t > 0\). The remainder is \(R(\Delta) = \tfrac16 P'''(\theta)\Delta^3\) by the proof of Theorem 3.1, so \(R\) has the sign of \(P'''(\theta)\Delta^3\), which is the sign of \(-\Delta^3\), that is, of \(-\Delta\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.3 (the first-order expansion lies below the price)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), let \(y_0 > -1\). If every \(c_t > 0\), then \(P(y_0 + \Delta) - \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) \ge 0\) for every \(\Delta\) with \(y_0 + \Delta > -1\).

Proof

By the Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with two derivatives, \(P(y_0 + \Delta) - \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) = \tfrac12 P''(\theta)\Delta^2\) for some \(\theta\) between \(y_0\) and \(y_0 + \Delta\). Since \(P'' > 0\) by Proposition 2.1, this is non-negative. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.4 (what the expansion does not give)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), Theorem 3.1 bounds the error by a constant times \(|\Delta|^3\), where the constant depends on the third derivative over the whole interval between the two yields. The bound holds for a large shift too, but the interval is then wide, \(M\) is taken over it, and this page computes no value of that bound. Proposition 3.3 is an inequality for every shift, and it does not give the size of the error.

4Where the canon breaks

The expansion is a statement about one bond with fixed cash flows. It assumes the cash flows do not depend on the yield. A bond with a call or prepayment option can have cash flows that depend on the yield, and the derivatives of Proposition 2.1 assume fixed cash flows, so they do not apply to such a bond as written.

  • The price is a sum of powers of \((1+y)\), not a linear function of \(y\). Proposition 3.3 shows that the tangent line, the graph of the first-order polynomial \(\mathcal{P}_1\) of Theorem 3.1, never lies above the price for a bond with positive cash flows, so a linear approximation never overstates the price at any shift.
  • Money is rounded once, at the end. The price is an exact rational number, computed from the float yields with Fraction, so no rounding happens inside the price calculation; the Taylor polynomials are exact given the two float sensitivities. The two sensitivities P'(y0) and P''(y0) are floats, each rounded once to the nearest double, so its relative error is at most 2 to the power of minus 53; the Taylor terms are exact given these floats. The price is rounded to an integer number of cents once, at the last step, with halves to even. Lab 1 rounds only the printed price and the printed expansion to whole cents; its central differences are float checks, and they are printed only as checks.
  • The preview ratios are named, not defined. The lab prints \(-P'/P\) and \(P''/P\). This lesson does not define the terms they are named after, and no claim about their use is made here.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The second-order expansion gives the bond price exactly.”

    Lab 1 prints a difference of \(-15\) cents between the price and the second-order expansion, both in whole cents, at a shift of \(+0.01\), and Theorem 3.1 bounds the remainder by a constant times a cubic term. The expansion is an approximation, and its error is the remainder, which is not zero.

  2. “A bond whose yield equals its coupon rate has a price different from face.”

    Proposition 2.2 proves that the price equals the face value at that yield. Lab 1 prints 100000 cents at the yield 0.05, which equals the coupon rate of 5 percent.

  3. “A higher yield always raises the price of a bond with positive cash flows.”

    Proposition 2.1 gives \(P'(y) < 0\) when every cash flow is positive, so the price falls as the yield rises. The expansion in Lab 1 has \(P'(y_0) = -851466.74\) cents per unit of yield, which is negative.

6Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Par for a two-year bond

A bond of face 1000 pays a coupon of 5 percent a year, annually, for 2 years, with the face repaid at year 2. Find its price at yield 0.05.

Solution

Here \(F = 1000\), the coupon rate \(r = 0.05\), the yield \(y = 0.05\), \(N = 2\), and the coupon is \(rF = 50\). Proposition 2.2 gives a price equal to the face, 1000. Directly, \(50/1.05 + 1050/1.05^2 = 47.619 + 952.381 = 1000.000\), with the terms rounded to three decimals for display.

M2 · A single cash flow

Let a single cash flow \(c\) be paid at year 1, so \(P(y) = c(1+y)^{-1}\). Compute \(P'\) and \(P''\) from Proposition 2.1, and check them by differentiating directly.

Solution

Proposition 2.1 with \(N = 1\) gives \(P'(y) = -c(1+y)^{-2}\) and \(P''(y) = 2c(1+y)^{-3}\). Differentiating \(c(1+y)^{-1}\) directly gives \(-c(1+y)^{-2}\), and differentiating again gives \(2c(1+y)^{-3}\), which agrees.

M3 · A zero-coupon bond

A zero-coupon bond pays 100 cents at year 2 only. Write its price function and its first derivative at \(y = 0.04\).

Solution

\(P(y) = 100(1+y)^{-2}\), so \(P'(y) = -200(1+y)^{-3}\). At \(y = 0.04\), \((1.04)^{-3}\) is the factor, and \(P'(0.04) = -200/1.04^3\), a negative number, which agrees with Proposition 2.1 because the one cash flow is positive.

Proof

P1 · A decreasing price function with increasing slope

Using Proposition 2.1, show that \(P\) is strictly decreasing and that \(P'\) is strictly increasing on \((-1, \infty)\) when every cash flow is positive.

Solution

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), \(P' < 0\) gives that \(P\) is strictly decreasing, by Corollary 3.1 (ii) of Lesson L06 applied to \(-P\), and \(P'' > 0\) gives that \(P'\) is strictly increasing, by the same corollary applied to \(P'\). Both facts come from the signs of the terms of \(P'\) and \(P''\) in Proposition 2.1.

P2 · The sign of the cubic term

Show that \(P'''(y) < 0\) on \((-1, \infty)\) when every cash flow is positive, and state the sign of \(R(\Delta)\) for \(\Delta > 0\).

Solution

Differentiating \(P''\) gives \(P''' = -\sum t(t+1)(t+2)c_t(1+y)^{-t-3}\), and each term is negative. So \(P'''<0\), and \(R(\Delta) = \tfrac16 P'''(\theta)\Delta^3 < 0\) for \(\Delta > 0\), which matches Proposition 3.2.

P3 · The first-order error sign

Prove that the first-order error \(P(y_0 + \Delta) - \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta)\) is non-negative for every \(\Delta\), using only Proposition 2.1 and Taylor's theorem.

Solution

Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder of order one gives \(P(y_0+\Delta) - \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) = \tfrac12 P''(\theta)\Delta^2\) for some \(\theta\). Since \(P''(\theta) > 0\) and \(\Delta^2 \ge 0\), the error is non-negative, which is Proposition 3.3.

Empirical

E1 · The sign of the second-order errors (Lab 1)

Lab 1 reports the second-order error at a shift of \(+0.01\) as a negative number of cents, and the error at \(-0.01\) as positive. Explain the two signs from Proposition 3.2.

Solution

Proposition 3.2 gives \(R(\Delta)\) the sign of \(-\Delta\). At \(+0.01\) the remainder is negative, and the exact price is below the second-order expansion. At \(-0.01\) the remainder is positive, and the exact price is above it. The lab's printed difference of \(-15\) cents at \(+0.01\) and \(+16\) cents at \(-0.01\) matches these signs.

E2 · The error ratios (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints first-order error ratios near 4 and second-order error ratios near 8 as the shift halves. Explain each limit from the form of the remainder.

Solution

The first-order error is \(\tfrac12 P''(\theta)\Delta^2\), so halving \(\Delta\) divides it by about 4. The second-order error is \(\tfrac16 P'''(\theta)\Delta^3\), so halving divides it by about 8. The printed ratios 3.9279 and 7.8807, then 3.9634 and 7.9396, move toward these limits.

E3 · The price in cents (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints, at the yield 0.05 (the base yield plus the shift 0.01), a price of 100000 cents and the second-order expansion there rounded to 100015 cents. Explain why the expansion is rounded to an integer for the comparison, and state which quantity is money.

Solution

The price is money, and money is reported as an integer number of cents. The lab's price line is the unrounded price rounded to the cent. Proposition 2.2 gives exactly face at the yield 5 percent. The float yield the lab uses for the shift, 0.04 + 0.01, stores a value whose exact rational value differs from the decimal 0.05 (that is, 1/20) by 2.78e-18 (exact arithmetic). With \(P'(0.05) = -7.72 \times 10^5\) cents per unit of yield, the unrounded price moves by about \(-2.1 \times 10^{-12}\) cents, so the rounded price is 100000. The lab prints neither the offset of 2.78e-18 nor its effect on the unrounded price. The expansion is an approximation of that money amount, and it is rounded to the nearest cent so that the printed comparison is in whole cents. The derivatives are sensitivities in cents per unit of yield, and the lab keeps them as floats, each rounded once to the nearest double. The price itself is an exact rational, so the money amount contains no float; the expansion is built from the float sensitivities.

Research

R1 · The yield as a function of the price

Let \(P_0\) be a fixed price, and let \(G(P_0, y) = P(y) - P_0\). Use Lesson L21 to find \(dy/dP_0\), and state its sign for this bond.

Solution

\(G_{P_0} = -1\) and \(G_y = P'(y)\), so, assuming Assumption 1.2 of Lesson L21 and Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06, Theorem 2.1 of Lesson L21 gives \(dy/dP_0 = -G_{P_0}/G_y = 1/P'(y)\). Since \(P'(y) < 0\) by Proposition 2.1, \(dy/dP_0 < 0\): a higher price goes with a lower yield. This uses the implicit-function theorem of L21, under its intermediate value assumption, for the local statement, and the sign follows from Proposition 2.1.

R2 · A larger shift

Explain what Theorem 3.1 and Proposition 3.2 give, and do not give, for a shift of \(+0.5\) in yield, and state which quantity would have to be bounded to get a numerical bound for that shift.

Solution

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), Theorem 3.1 does apply to a shift of \(0.5\), because the expansion is valid for any shift with \(y_0 + \Delta > -1\). But its bound is \(\tfrac16 M|\Delta|^3\), with \(M\) the maximum of \(|P'''|\) over the wider interval between \(y_0\) and \(y_0 + 0.5\), and this page does not compute that \(M\). Proposition 3.2 gives only the sign of the remainder, not its size. So the quantity to bound is \(M\) over that interval, which can be computed from the closed form of \(P'''\) but is not computed on this page.

R3 · Which cash flows break the sign results

State which hypothesis of Propositions 2.1, 3.2 and 3.3 would fail if one cash flow were negative, and say what the sign of the price derivative would then depend on.

Solution

The hypothesis that every \(c_t > 0\) would fail. Then \(P'\) is a sum with mixed signs, and its sign at a given yield depends on the sizes of the terms, so the conclusions \(P' < 0\), \(P'' > 0\) and the signs of the remainder are not guaranteed. The proofs use the positivity of each term.

  1. When every cash flow is positive, what is the sign of \(P'(y)\)?

    Options

    Proposition 2.1: each term of \(P'\) is \(-t\,c_t(1+y)^{-t-1}\), which is negative.

  2. A bond with coupon rate 5 percent and annual coupons is priced at yield 5 percent. What is its price relative to face?

    Options

    Proposition 2.2 with the yield equal to the coupon rate \(r = 0.05\).

  3. What is the price of a bond of face 1000, annual coupon rate 5 percent, two years, at yield 0.05?

    Proposition 2.2 gives the face value, 1000 (Problem M1).

  4. What does Theorem 3.1 say about the error of the second-order expansion?

    Options

    Theorem 3.1: \(|R(\Delta)| \le \tfrac16 M|\Delta|^3\).

  5. When every cash flow is positive, the sign of the remainder \(R(\Delta)\) is:

    Options

    Proposition 3.2: \(R(\Delta) = \tfrac16 P'''(\theta)\Delta^3\) with \(P''' < 0\).

  6. For a zero-coupon bond paying 100 cents at year 2, what is \(P'(y)\) at \(y = 0\)?

    \(P(y) = 100(1+y)^{-2}\), so \(P'(y) = -200(1+y)^{-3}\), which is \(-200\) at \(y = 0\).

  7. Which statement about the tangent line of \(P\) at \(y_0\) is correct for positive cash flows?

    Options

    Proposition 3.3: the first-order error \(P(y_0+\Delta) - \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta)\) is non-negative.

  8. Which hypothesis of Proposition 2.1 guarantees the sign \(P'(y) < 0\)?

    Options

    Every cash flow positive is sufficient for \(P'(y) < 0\), not necessary. With two cash flows \(c_1 = -1\) and \(c_2 = 1000\) (\(N = 2\)) at \(y = 0.04\), the formula of Proposition 2.1 gives \(P'(y) = -1777.07\) cents per unit of yield, which is negative.
