Finite-Difference Derivative Approximation

A pricing or risk program may have a function that it can evaluate but cannot differentiate by hand, and a sensitivity is the derivative of a price with respect to an input. A difference quotient gives a number without any formula for the derivative. It has two separate errors. One comes from replacing a limit by a step of finite size. The other comes from computing with numbers that carry rounding error. This lesson proves the size of the first error for two quotients, models the second, and shows that the step size that makes the total error smallest is finite and computable under the model.

1Two difference quotients

Let \(f\) be a real function defined near a point \(x\). The derivative \(f'(x)\) is the limit of the difference quotient as the step goes to zero. In a program the step is a fixed positive number \(h\), and the quotient is used as an estimate. Two quotients are standard.

Definition 1.1 (forward and central difference quotients)

Let \(f\) be defined on \([x - h, x + h]\) with \(h > 0\). The forward difference quotient and the central difference quotient are

\[D_h f(x) = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h},\]

\[C_h f(x) = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h}.\]

Each uses function values only, so each can be computed for a function given as a program rather than a formula. The finite-difference derivative approximation of \(f'(x)\) is either quotient, with the step \(h\) chosen by the user.

The two quotients differ in how many values they use, and Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 show that the difference matters for accuracy. The forward quotient uses \(f(x)\) and \(f(x + h)\). The central quotient uses \(f(x - h)\) and \(f(x + h)\) and does not use \(f(x)\).

2Truncation error: the step's own error

Even with exact arithmetic, the quotient differs from the derivative. The difference is the truncation error. Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder, defined in Lesson L08, gives its size. Here it is used for \(f\) with a continuous second derivative on the interval, which is the hypothesis of the first theorem below.

Theorem 2.1 (error of the forward quotient)

Let \(h > 0\) and let \(f\) have a continuous second derivative on \([x, x + h]\). Then there is \(\xi\) with \(x < \xi < x + h\) such that

\[D_h f(x) - f'(x) = \frac{h}{2}\, f''(\xi).\]

Proof

Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder of order one, at the point \(x\), applies to \(f\) on \([x, x + h]\), because \(f\) is twice differentiable there and \(f'\) is continuous there. It gives \(\xi\) with \(x < \xi < x + h\) and \(f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x)\,h + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\,h^2\). Subtract \(f(x)\), divide by \(h\), and subtract \(f'(x)\). The left side is \(D_h f(x) - f'(x)\), and the right side is \(\tfrac12 f''(\xi)\,h\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 2.2 (error of the central quotient)

Let \(h > 0\) and let \(f\) have a continuous third derivative on \([x - h, x + h]\). Then there is \(\xi\) with \(x - h < \xi < x + h\) such that

\[C_h f(x) - f'(x) = \frac{h^2}{6}\, f'''(\xi).\]

Proof

Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder of order two, at \(x\), applies to \(f\) on each of the two intervals. It gives points \(\xi_+ \in (x, x + h)\) and \(\xi_- \in (x - h, x)\) with

\[f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x)\,h + \tfrac12 f''(x)\,h^2 + \tfrac16 f'''(\xi_+)\,h^3,\]

\[f(x - h) = f(x) - f'(x)\,h + \tfrac12 f''(x)\,h^2 - \tfrac16 f'''(\xi_-)\,h^3.\]

Subtract the second from the first. The terms in \(f(x)\) and \(f''(x)\) cancel, leaving \(f(x + h) - f(x - h) = 2f'(x)\,h + \tfrac16\bigl(f'''(\xi_+) + f'''(\xi_-)\bigr)h^3\). Divide by \(2h\):

\[C_h f(x) - f'(x) = \frac{h^2}{12}\bigl(f'''(\xi_+) + f'''(\xi_-)\bigr).\]

Let \(m\) be half the sum in parentheses, so that the error is \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}\,m\). The number \(m\) lies between \(f'''(\xi_-)\) and \(f'''(\xi_+)\). The function \(f'''\) is continuous between \(\xi_-\) and \(\xi_+\), so the intermediate value theorem gives \(\xi\) between \(\xi_-\) and \(\xi_+\), hence with \(x - h < \xi < x + h\), such that \(f'''(\xi) = m\). \(\blacksquare\)

Assumption 1. The derivative of \(\exp\) is \(\exp\), and the derivative of \(\sin\) is \(\cos\). This page assumes both facts and does not prove them. Each result that uses them says so, as Example 2.3, Problem M3 and Problems E1 and E3 do.

Example 2.3 (exp at 1, two steps)

Let \(f = \exp\) and \(x = 1\). Under Assumption 1 (the derivative of \(\exp\) is \(\exp\), which this page assumes and does not prove), \(f'(1) = e\). Theorem 2.1 applies with \(f''(\xi) = e^{\xi}\), and \(e < e^{\xi} < e^{1 + h}\) for \(0 < h\). At \(h = 10^{-2}\) the forward error therefore lies between \(e \cdot 0.005\) and \(e^{1.01} \cdot 0.005\), which is between \(0.0135914\) and \(0.0137280\). Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.2 applies with \(f'''(\xi) = e^{\xi}\) and \(\xi\) in \((0.99, 1.01)\). At \(h = 10^{-2}\) the central error lies between \(e^{0.99} \cdot 10^{-4}/6\) and \(e^{1.01} \cdot 10^{-4}/6\), which is between \(4.4854 \times 10^{-5}\) and \(4.5760 \times 10^{-5}\). Lab 1 prints the two errors at \(h = 10^{-2}\), and the printed values are checked against these intervals in the lab's interpretation below.

3Rounding error: a model

A program does not compute \(f\) exactly. Each stored value of \(f\) differs from the true value by an amount bounded as Definition 3.1 states, and the subtraction and division add more. This section models the first effect and leaves the others out. The model is an assumption of this page, and no source is cited for it.

Definition 3.1 (evaluation error bound)

Let \(\tilde f\) be the program's version of \(f\), and let \(\delta \ge 0\). Say that \(\tilde f\) has evaluation error at most \(\delta\) at the points \(x - h\), \(x\) and \(x + h\) when \(|\tilde f(t) - f(t)| \le \delta\) for each of these three points \(t\).

Proposition 3.2 (rounding contributes at most 2δ/h or δ/h)

Let \(h > 0\). Assume that \(\tilde f\) has evaluation error at most \(\delta\) at the points \(x - h\), \(x\) and \(x + h\), and assume that the subtraction and the division by \(h\) (or \(2h\)) are carried out exactly. Let \(\tilde D_h\) and \(\tilde C_h\) be the quotients computed from \(\tilde f\). Then

\[|\tilde D_h f(x) - D_h f(x)| \le \frac{2\delta}{h}, \qquad |\tilde C_h f(x) - C_h f(x)| \le \frac{\delta}{h}.\]

Proof

The computed numerator of \(\tilde D_h\) is \(\tilde f(x + h) - \tilde f(x)\), and the exact numerator is \(f(x + h) - f(x)\). Their difference is \([\tilde f(x + h) - f(x + h)] - [\tilde f(x) - f(x)]\), whose absolute value is at most \(\delta + \delta = 2\delta\) by the triangle inequality. Dividing by \(h\) gives the first bound. For \(\tilde C_h\) the numerator difference is at most \(2\delta\) by the same argument with \(x - h\) and \(x + h\), and dividing by \(2h\) gives \(\delta/h\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.2 has two hypotheses that the proof uses, and both are stated in it. The first is the evaluation error bound. The second is that the arithmetic after the function evaluations is exact. Section 5 names what the model leaves out.

4Balancing the two errors

Combining Theorem 2.1 with Proposition 3.2 gives a bound on the total error of the forward quotient. Let \(M = \max |f''|\) on \([x, x + h_0]\) for a fixed \(h_0 > 0\), and take \(0 < h \le h_0\).

Corollary 4.1 (the balanced step for the forward quotient)

Assume that \(f''\) is continuous on \([x, x + h_0]\), so that \(M\) is finite, and that \(\tilde f\) has evaluation error at most \(\delta\) at \(x - h\), \(x\) and \(x + h\) for each \(h\) in \((0, h_0]\), with the arithmetic exact as in Proposition 3.2. Then for each \(h\) in \((0, h_0]\),

\[|\tilde D_h f(x) - f'(x)| \le B(h) = \frac{M}{2}\,h + \frac{2\delta}{h}.\]

If \(\delta > 0\) and \(M > 0\), the function \(B\) is smallest at \(h^* = 2\sqrt{\delta/M}\), and \(B(h^*) = 2\sqrt{M\delta}\), provided \(h^* \le h_0\).

Proof

The bound follows from the triangle inequality: the truncation part is at most \(\tfrac12 M h\) by Theorem 2.1, and the rounding part is at most \(2\delta/h\) by Proposition 3.2. For the minimum, \(B'(h) = \tfrac{M}{2} - \tfrac{2\delta}{h^2}\), which is zero exactly when \(h^2 = 4\delta/M\), that is, \(h = 2\sqrt{\delta/M}\). Since \(B''(h) = 4\delta/h^3 > 0\) for \(h > 0\), this stationary point is the minimum. At it, \(\tfrac{M}{2}h^* = \sqrt{M\delta}\) and \(2\delta/h^* = \sqrt{M\delta}\), so \(B(h^*) = 2\sqrt{M\delta}\). \(\blacksquare\)

The same argument for the central quotient, with Theorem 2.2 and Proposition 3.2, gives the bound \(\tfrac{M_3}{6}h^2 + \tfrac{\delta}{h}\), where \(M_3 = \max |f'''|\) on \([x - h_0, x + h_0]\). Its smallest value is at \(h^* = (3\delta/M_3)^{1/3}\). The derivation follows the calculation of Corollary 4.1 and is worked in Problem P3.

"""M2 lab L13: forward and central difference approximations to f'(x), and the rounding turn-up.

Standard library only. The function is exp and the point is x = 1, so the exact derivative is e.
The step sizes h = 10^-k for k = 1 to 12 are evaluated in IEEE double precision. Nothing is random
and nothing is fetched, so the output is the same on every run on the same Python build. The
errors are measured against math.e, a value known in closed form, so the lab shows the errors as
they happen and does not assume a shape for them.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from collections.abc import Callable

X0 = 1.0
EXACT = math.e  # f'(1) = e, under Assumption 1 of lesson L13 (the derivative of exp is exp)


def forward(f: Callable[[float], float], x: float, h: float) -> float:
    """Forward difference (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h, an approximation to f'(x) with error of order h."""
    return (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h


def central(f: Callable[[float], float], x: float, h: float) -> float:
    """Central difference (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2h), an approximation to f'(x) with error of order h squared."""
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2.0 * h)


def sweep() -> list[tuple[int, float, float]]:
    """For k = 1 to 12, the absolute errors of both approximations at h = 10^-k, as (k, forward, central)."""
    rows: list[tuple[int, float, float]] = []
    for k in range(1, 13):
        h = 10.0 ** (-k)
        fwd = abs(forward(math.exp, X0, h) - EXACT)
        cen = abs(central(math.exp, X0, h) - EXACT)
        rows.append((k, fwd, cen))
    return rows


def main() -> None:
    rows = sweep()
    print("step h, forward error, central error")
    for k, fwd, cen in rows:
        print(f"1e-{k:02d} {fwd:.3e} {cen:.3e}")
    fwd_best = min(rows, key=lambda r: r[1])[0]
    cen_best = min(rows, key=lambda r: r[2])[0]
    print(f"forward error is smallest at h = 1e-{fwd_best:02d}")
    print(f"central error is smallest at h = 1e-{cen_best:02d}")
    f_at = {k: fwd for k, fwd, _ in rows}
    c_at = {k: cen for k, _, cen in rows}
    print(f"forward error ratio, h = 1e-2 over h = 1e-3: {f_at[2] / f_at[3]:.2f}")
    print(f"central error ratio, h = 1e-2 over h = 1e-3: {c_at[2] / c_at[3]:.2f}")

5Where the canon breaks

The theorems above fix the truncation error exactly and the rounding error under a model. Four points separate the textbook result from a computation that a student may run.

  • The exact step is not represented exactly. Proposition 3.2 assumes that \(x - h\), \(x\) and \(x + h\) are the points used. In double precision \(x + h\) is rounded to a stored number. At \(x = 1\) the rounding moves the point by at most \(2^{-53}\), which is a relative change of at most \(2^{-53}/h\) in the step. At \(h = 10^{-8}\) that is about \(1.1 \times 10^{-8}\). Its effect on the quotient of \(\exp\) at \(1\) is about \(3.0 \times 10^{-8}\) (Problem R3), which is the same order as the minimum \(2\sqrt{M\delta} \approx 4.9 \times 10^{-8}\) of Corollary 4.1 with \(M = e\) (the value used in Lab 1) and \(\delta = 2^{-52}\). The model therefore does not certify the quotient at \(h = 10^{-8}\) beyond that order. Lab 1 includes this effect, and the model does not.
  • The balanced step needs \(M\) and \(\delta\). Corollary 4.1 gives \(h^*\) in terms of the second derivative bound \(M\) and the error bound \(\delta\). In a program the user does not in general know either. The formula is a statement about the bound, not a rule that picks \(h\) for an unknown function.
  • Random or noisy evaluations are outside the model. Proposition 3.2 is a worst-case bound on a deterministic error. If each evaluation carries independent noise, the quotient is a random variable with a different size of error, and the bound describes a worst case, not a typical one.
  • A kink breaks Theorem 2.1. The theorem needs a continuous second derivative on the interval. At a point where \(f\) has a corner, such as \(f(t) = |t|\) at \(t = 0\), the forward quotient equals \(1\) for every \(h > 0\), which is the right-hand slope, and the central quotient gives \(0\), the average of the two one-sided slopes \(-1\) and \(1\). The central quotient returns a number at a point where the derivative does not exist.

6Misconceptions

  1. “A smaller step always gives a more accurate derivative.”

    Theorem 2.1 makes the truncation error shrink with \(h\), but Proposition 3.2 makes the rounding error grow as \(\delta/h\) or \(2\delta/h\). Corollary 4.1 gives a best step. Lab 1 prints the forward error at \(h = 10^{-8}\) as \(6.603 \times 10^{-9}\), and at \(h = 10^{-9}\) as \(2.154 \times 10^{-7}\), which is about 32.6 times larger (ratio of the printed values).

  2. “The central quotient is the forward quotient with a symmetric label, so both have the same accuracy.”

    The central quotient also uses function values, but its two expansions cancel the \(f(x)\) and \(\tfrac12 f''(x)h^2\) terms (proof of Theorem 2.2), so its error is of order \(h^2\), while Theorem 2.1 has order \(h\). Lab 1 prints a central error of \(4.530 \times 10^{-5}\) at \(h = 10^{-2}\) and a forward error of \(1.364 \times 10^{-2}\) at the same step, a ratio of \(301\) (printed values \(1.364 \times 10^{-2} / 4.530 \times 10^{-5} = 301.1\)).

  3. “The error of a difference quotient is only its truncation error.”

    Proposition 3.2 shows a second part that grows as the step shrinks. The total error is the sum of the two parts, bounded by \(B(h)\) in Corollary 4.1, and the lab's turn-up at \(h \le 10^{-8}\) is consistent with this second part. The lab prints total errors and does not separate the two parts.

Mechanics

M1 · The forward quotient of \(x^2\) at \(x = 1\)

Compute \(D_h f(1)\) for \(f(t) = t^2\) and \(h = 0.1\), and find the exact error.

Solution

\(D_{0.1} f(1) = \bigl((1.1)^2 - 1^2\bigr)/0.1 = (1.21 - 1)/0.1 = 2.1\). The derivative is \(f'(1) = 2\), so the exact error is \(2.1 - 2 = 0.1\). Theorem 2.1 gives this as \(\tfrac{h}{2} f''(\xi) = 0.05 \cdot 2 = 0.1\) for every \(\xi\), since \(f'' = 2\) is constant.

M2 · The central quotient of a quadratic

Show that \(C_h f(x) = f'(x)\) exactly for \(f(t) = a t^2 + b t + c\), for every \(h > 0\), and say which theorem explains this.

Solution

Compute \(f(x + h) - f(x - h) = a\bigl((x + h)^2 - (x - h)^2\bigr) + b\bigl((x + h) - (x - h)\bigr) = 4a x h + 2b h\). Dividing by \(2h\) gives \(2a x + b = f'(x)\). Theorem 2.2 explains it: \(f'''\) is zero for a quadratic, so the error term \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''(\xi)\) is zero.

M3 · A bound for the forward error of sin at zero

For \(f = \sin\) at \(x = 0\) and \(h = 0.01\), compute \(D_h f(0)\), the exact error, and the bound \(\tfrac{h}{2}\max|f''|\) on \([0, h]\).

Solution

\(D_{0.01} \sin(0) = \sin(0.01)/0.01\). Using \(\sin(0.01) = 0.009999833334\) gives \(D = 0.99998333\), so the error is \(D - 1 = -1.6667 \times 10^{-5}\). Under Assumption 1 (the derivative of \(\sin\) is \(\cos\), so \(f'' = -\sin\)), on \([0, 0.01]\) we have \(|f''| = |\sin t| \le \sin(0.01) \approx 0.0099998\), so Theorem 2.1 bounds the error by \(\tfrac{0.01}{2} \cdot \sin(0.01) \approx 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\), and the error \(-1.67 \times 10^{-5}\) lies inside that bound.

P1 · The central quotient of a cubic

For \(f(t) = t^3\) show that \(C_h f(x) - f'(x) = h^2\) for every \(x\) and \(h > 0\), and check the result against Theorem 2.2.

Solution

\((x + h)^3 - (x - h)^3 = 6x^2 h + 2h^3\). Dividing by \(2h\) gives \(3x^2 + h^2\), and \(f'(x) = 3x^2\), so the error is \(h^2\). Theorem 2.2 has \(f'''(\xi) = 6\) for every \(\xi\), so its error is \(\tfrac{h^2}{6} \cdot 6 = h^2\), which agrees.

P2 · The forward quotient cannot be exact for a non-linear function

Let \(f(t) = t^2\) and \(x = 0\). Show that \(D_h f(0) = h\) and that the error is \(h\), and state the hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 that holds here.

Solution

\(D_h f(0) = (h^2 - 0)/h = h\), and \(f'(0) = 0\), so the error is \(h\). Here \(f'' = 2\), and Theorem 2.1 gives \(\tfrac{h}{2} \cdot 2 = h\). The hypothesis, a continuous second derivative on \([0, h]\), holds because \(f'' = 2\) is constant. Because \(f''\) is constant, the point \(\xi\) in Theorem 2.1 does not affect the value.

P3 · The balanced step for the central quotient

Under the central hypotheses of Theorem 2.2 with \(M_3 = \max|f'''|\) on \([x - h_0, x + h_0]\), show that the bound \(B_c(h) = \tfrac{M_3}{6}h^2 + \tfrac{\delta}{h}\) has its smallest value at \(h^* = (3\delta/M_3)^{1/3}\), and derive the value of \(B_c(h^*)\).

Solution

\(B_c'(h) = \tfrac{M_3}{3}h - \tfrac{\delta}{h^2}\), which is zero when \(h^3 = 3\delta/M_3\). Also \(B_c''(h) = \tfrac{M_3}{3} + \tfrac{2\delta}{h^3} > 0\) for \(h > 0\), so the stationary point is the minimum. At \(h^*\), \(M_3 h^{*2} = 3\delta/h^*\), so \(\tfrac{M_3}{6}h^{*2} = \tfrac{\delta}{2h^*}\). So \(B_c(h^*) = \tfrac{\delta}{2h^*} + \tfrac{\delta}{h^*} = \tfrac{3\delta}{2h^*}\).

Empirical

E1 · The ratio of forward errors

Lab 1 prints a forward-error ratio of 10.03 for \(h = 10^{-2}\) over \(h = 10^{-3}\). Show from Theorem 2.1 that the ratio lies between 9.990 and 10.101, and explain why it is not exactly 10.

Solution

Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.1 gives forward errors \(\tfrac{h_1}{2}e^{\xi_1}\) and \(\tfrac{h_2}{2}e^{\xi_2}\), with \(\xi_1 \in (1, 1.01)\) and \(\xi_2 \in (1, 1.001)\). The ratio is \(10\, e^{\xi_1 - \xi_2}\), and \(\xi_1 - \xi_2\) lies in \((-0.001, 0.01)\). So the ratio lies in \((10e^{-0.001}, 10e^{0.01}) = (9.990, 10.101)\). It is not exactly 10 because \(\xi_1\) and \(\xi_2\) differ, and \(e^{\xi}\) changes with \(\xi\). The printed 10.03 lies inside the interval, which is the check this problem asks for.

E2 · Why the forward error grows below the balanced step (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints a forward error of \(2.154 \times 10^{-7}\) at \(h = 10^{-9}\), larger than the \(6.603 \times 10^{-9}\) at \(h = 10^{-8}\). Using Proposition 3.2, state the bound on the rounding part at each step and say which part dominates at \(10^{-9}\).

Solution

Proposition 3.2 gives a rounding part of at most \(2\delta/h\), with \(\delta = 2^{-52} \approx 2.22 \times 10^{-16}\). At \(h = 10^{-8}\) that is \(4.44 \times 10^{-8}\), and at \(h = 10^{-9}\) it is \(4.44 \times 10^{-7}\). The truncation part is \(\tfrac{h}{2}e^{\xi}\), about \(1.36 \times 10^{-8}\) at \(10^{-8}\) and \(1.36 \times 10^{-9}\) at \(10^{-9}\). At \(10^{-9}\) the rounding bound \(4.44 \times 10^{-7}\) is about 327 times the truncation part \(1.36 \times 10^{-9}\), so the rounding bound dominates. The printed 2.154 × 10⁻⁷ lies below the total bound of about 4.45 × 10⁻⁷ (rounding plus truncation), which is consistent.

E3 · The central step predicted from the model (Lab 1)

Using \(\delta = 2^{-52}\) and \(M_3 = e\) (the value of \(\max|f'''|\) for \(\exp\) as the interval shrinks to \(\{1\}\), under Assumption 1), compute the central step \((3\delta/M_3)^{1/3}\) that P3 gives. State which printed decade of Lab 1 contains it, and say whether the lab's smallest central error is at that decade.

Solution

\(3\delta/e = 3 \times 2.220446 \times 10^{-16}/2.718282 = 2.45057 \times 10^{-16}\), and its cube root is \(6.25781 \times 10^{-6}\). That lies between the printed decades \(10^{-6}\) and \(10^{-5}\), nearer \(10^{-5}\) on a logarithmic scale. Lab 1 prints its smallest central error at \(h = 10^{-5}\), so the model's step and the lab's minimum agree at the decade resolution the sweep uses. The printed value at \(10^{-7}\) ties with it at three digits. The sweep does not resolve a finer step, so this agreement does not fix the exact minimizer.

Research

R1 · A step for noisy evaluations

A simulation returns \(\tilde f\) with evaluation error at most \(\delta = 10^{-3}\) at each point, but the error is not a rounding error and changes from call to call. Using Corollary 4.1 with \(M = e\), compute the step it would suggest, and state which hypothesis of Proposition 3.2 fails when the error is random.

Solution

Corollary 4.1 gives \(h^* = 2\sqrt{\delta/M} = 2\sqrt{10^{-3}/e} \approx 0.038360\). The formula is a worst-case bound, so the number is a step under the stated bound, not an optimal step for the simulation. Proposition 3.2 treats the error at each point as a fixed function bounded by \(\delta\). A random error that changes from call to call is not such a function, so the hypothesis is not met as stated, and the step is then a choice under uncertainty rather than the minimizer of a deterministic bound. The problem does not settle which step to use, and a study of random errors is a separate question.

R2 · Why a bound needs M

A user has a function \(f\) whose second derivative bound \(M\) is unknown. Explain why Corollary 4.1 cannot produce a certified step from the evaluations alone, and say what would be needed to certify a derivative to within a stated tolerance.

Solution

Corollary 4.1 needs \(M = \max|f''|\) on an interval, and \(h^* = 2\sqrt{\delta/M}\) depends on \(M\). From evaluations at finitely many points the user cannot determine the maximum of \(|f''|\) on an interval, because values between the sample points are unknown. A certificate needs an analytic bound on \(f''\) from the formula, or a guaranteed enclosure of its values, which is a different tool from the difference quotient. The quotient alone gives an estimate with an error that is unknown up to \(M\).

R3 · The size of the omitted rounding of the point

Section 5 says that rounding of \(x + h\) changes the step by a relative amount up to \(2^{-53}/h\). Compute that relative amount at \(h = 10^{-8}\), and estimate its effect on the forward quotient of \(\exp\) at \(1\), which has derivative \(e\).

Solution

The relative change in the step is at most \(2^{-53}/10^{-8} = 1.11 \times 10^{-16}/10^{-8} = 1.11 \times 10^{-8}\). If the stored point differs from the nominal point \(x + h\) by a relative amount \(\eta\) of \(h\) while the division still uses the nominal \(h\), the numerator changes by about \(e\,\eta\,h\) under Assumption 1 (\(f' = \exp\) and \(f'(1) = e\)), so \(D_h\) changes by about \(e\,\eta\). That is about \(e \times 1.11 \times 10^{-8} \approx 3.0 \times 10^{-8}\). This is the same order as the minimum \(2\sqrt{M\delta} \approx 4.9 \times 10^{-8}\) of the bound in Corollary 4.1 with \(M = e\) and \(\delta = 2^{-52}\), so the model does not certify the quotient at \(h = 10^{-8}\) beyond that order.

Answer from memory, then check. Finishing the quiz adds this lesson's cards to your review deck.

  1. Which quotient uses two function values and has a truncation error of order \(h^2\) for a smooth \(f\)?

    Options

    Theorem 2.1 gives an error of order \(h\) for the forward quotient, and Theorem 2.2 gives order \(h^2\) for the central one.

  2. In Lab 1, where is the smallest printed forward error?

    Options

    The lab prints \(6.603 \times 10^{-9}\) at \(h = 10^{-8}\), smaller than every other printed forward error.

  3. For \(f(t) = t^2\) at \(x = 1\), what is the forward quotient at \(h = 0.5\)?

    \(f(1.5) = 2.25\) and \(f(1) = 1\), so \((2.25 - 1)/0.5 = 2.5\).

  4. Why does the central quotient have no \(f(x)\) term in its numerator?

    Options

    Definition 1.1 uses \(f(x + h) - f(x - h)\). In the proof of Theorem 2.2 the symmetric pair cancels the \(f(x)\) and \(\tfrac12 f''(x)h^2\) terms of the two expansions, so the error is of order \(h^2\).

  5. In Proposition 3.2, what does the bound \(2\delta/h\) do as \(h\) shrinks?

    Options

    The bound is proportional to \(1/h\), so halving \(h\) doubles it.

  6. How many function values does the central quotient use?

    It uses \(f(x + h)\) and \(f(x - h)\), so two values.

  7. What does Corollary 4.1 give as the balanced step for the forward quotient?

    Options

    Setting \(B'(h) = \tfrac{M}{2} - \tfrac{2\delta}{h^2}\) to zero gives \(h^2 = 4\delta/M\).

  8. For \(f(t) = t^2\) at \(x = 1\) and \(h = 0.1\), the exact error of the forward quotient is the value of Theorem 2.1's \(\tfrac{h}{2} f''\). What is that value?

    \(f'' = 2\), so \(\tfrac{0.1}{2} \cdot 2 = 0.1\), which matches Problem M1.

  9. Which statement about the central quotient for a function with a corner, such as \(|t|\) at \(0\), is correct?

    Options

    For \(|t|\) the central quotient is \((|h| - |-h|)/(2h) = 0\), and the derivative does not exist at \(0\).

  10. Which assumption in Proposition 3.2 is about the arithmetic after the function values are stored?

    Options

    The proposition states both hypotheses; the second one covers the arithmetic after the evaluations.
