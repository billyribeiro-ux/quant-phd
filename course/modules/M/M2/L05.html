Differentiation Rules and the Chain Rule

  • Uses: the derivative as a limit (Lesson 4, Definition 1.1); differentiable implies continuous (Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1); the limit laws (Lesson 2, Proposition 1.1); mathematical induction from M1
  • Introduces: no new concept. The product, quotient and chain rules are proved in this lesson.
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A derivative of a product, a quotient or a composition of functions can be reduced to the derivatives of its parts. With a constant rate \(r\), the discount factor for \(t\) periods is \(1/(1 + r)^t\), and a price can be a function of a yield through several steps. Computing each derivative from the limit in Lesson 4 repeats the same argument. The three rules in this lesson reduce each such derivative to the derivatives of its parts, and each rule is proved from the definition, so it applies exactly under its hypotheses.

1Limit laws as the step goes to zero, and the product and quotient rules

Lemma 1.1 (limit laws as the step goes to zero)

Let \(F\) and \(G\) be real functions defined for \(0 < |h| < r\), for some \(r > 0\). Suppose \(F(h) \to L\) and \(G(h) \to M\) as \(h \to 0\), in the sense of Lesson 3, Definition 1.1 with \(0\) in place of \(a\). Then: (a) \(F + G \to L + M\); (b) \(FG \to LM\); (c) if \(M \ne 0\), then \(G(h) \ne 0\) for small \(|h|\), and \(F/G \to L/M\). Hypotheses: the two limits exist and are real, and in (c) the limit \(M\) is nonzero.

Proof (the argument of Lesson 2, Proposition 1.1, with the substitutions stated)

The substitutions are: the index \(n\) is replaced by the real variable \(h\), the condition \(n \ge N\) is replaced by \(0 < |h| < \delta\), and the sequence limits are replaced by the limits as \(h \to 0\). Each step of the proof of Lesson 2, Proposition 1.1 then holds for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) for a suitable \(\delta\). Part (a) is the triangle inequality with two thresholds. Part (b) uses the bound \(|F(h)| < |L| + 1\) for small \(|h|\), together with the estimate \(|FG - LM| \le |F|\,|G - M| + |M|\,|F - L|\). For part (c), choose \(r' > 0\) with \(|G(h) - M| < |M|/2\) for \(0 < |h| < r'\). Then \(|G(h)| > |M|/2 > 0\), so \(G(h) \ne 0\) there. Now \[\frac{F}{G} - \frac{L}{M} = \frac{M(F - L) - L(G - M)}{M\,G}, \qquad \left|\frac{F}{G} - \frac{L}{M}\right| \le \frac{2}{|M|^2}\bigl(|M|\,|F - L| + |L|\,|G - M|\bigr).\] Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Choose \(\delta > 0\), no larger than \(r'\), with \(|F(h) - L| < \varepsilon|M|/4\) and \(|G(h) - M| < \varepsilon|M|^2/(4(|L| + 1))\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta\), which is possible because both limits exist. Then the bracket is less than \(\varepsilon|M|^2/4 + \varepsilon|M|^2/4 = \varepsilon|M|^2/2\), and the whole expression is less than \(\varepsilon\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.2 (product rule)

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), and let \(a \in I\). Hypotheses: \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(a\) in the sense of Lesson 4, Definition 1.1. Then \(fg\) is differentiable at \(a\), and \((fg)'(a) = f'(a)\,g(a) + f(a)\,g'(a)\).

Proof

For \(h \ne 0\) with \(a + h \in I\), write \(Q_f(h)\) and \(Q_g(h)\) for the difference quotients of \(f\) and \(g\) at \(a\). Then

\[\frac{f(a+h)g(a+h) - f(a)g(a)}{h} = f(a+h)\,\frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h} + g(a)\,\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = f(a+h)\,Q_g(h) + g(a)\,Q_f(h),\]

where the middle term \(f(a+h)g(a)\) is added and subtracted. As \(h \to 0\), \(f(a+h) \to f(a)\), by the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) form established in the proof of Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1. Also \(Q_g(h) \to g'(a)\) and \(Q_f(h) \to f'(a)\). Lemma 1.1 (a) and (b) give the limit \(f(a)g'(a) + g(a)f'(a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.3 (quotient rule)

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), let \(a \in I\), and suppose \(g(a) \ne 0\). Hypotheses: \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(a\), and \(g(a) \ne 0\). Then \(f/g\) is defined for \(x\) near \(a\), it is differentiable at \(a\), and \((f/g)'(a) = \dfrac{f'(a)\,g(a) - f(a)\,g'(a)}{g(a)^2}\).

Proof

Since \(g\) is differentiable at \(a\), it is continuous there (Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1). So there is \(r > 0\) with \(|g(x) - g(a)| < |g(a)|/2\) for \(|x - a| < r\), and then \(|g(x)| > |g(a)|/2 > 0\). Thus \(f/g\) is defined on that neighbourhood. For \(0 < |h| < r\), with \(a + h \in I\),

\[\frac{1}{h}\left(\frac{f(a+h)}{g(a+h)} - \frac{f(a)}{g(a)}\right) = \frac{g(a)\,Q_f(h) - f(a)\,Q_g(h)}{g(a)\,g(a+h)}.\]

The numerator tends to \(g(a)f'(a) - f(a)g'(a)\) and the denominator to \(g(a)^2 \ne 0\), by Lemma 1.1 (a), (b) and (c), using \(g(a+h) \to g(a)\). So the difference quotient of \(f/g\) at \(a\) has the stated limit. \(\blacksquare\)

For a function \(f\), a point \(x\) and a step \(h \ne 0\), the central difference is \((f(x + h) - f(x - h))/(2h)\). It uses values on both sides of \(x\).

Lab 1 · The three rules against central differences at x = 1.5

Predicted before running. For \(p(x) = (x^2 + 1)x^3\), the product rule gives \(p'(x) = 2x \cdot x^3 + (x^2 + 1)\cdot 3x^2\), which is \(32.0625\) at \(x = 1.5\). For \(q(x) = (x^2+1)/(x+2)\), the quotient rule gives \(q'(1.5) = 7.25/12.25 = 29/49 \approx 0.591837\). For \(c(x) = (x^2+1)^3\), the chain rule gives \(c'(x) = 6x(x^2+1)^2\), which is \(95.0625\) at \(1.5\). The central difference has error of order \(h^2\), so at \(h = 10^{-4}\) each absolute difference should be of order \(10^{-6}\) or smaller.

"""M2 lab L05: the product, quotient and chain rules at x = 1.5, against central differences.

Standard library only. No randomness and no network. The three functions are p(x) = (x^2 + 1)(x^3), the quotient
q(x) = (x^2 + 1)/(x + 2) and the composite c(x) = (x^2 + 1)^3. For each one, the script prints the derivative from the
rule of this lesson (the analytic value) and the central difference (f(x + h) - f(x - h))/(2h) with h = 10^-4, with
the absolute difference between them. The central difference has error of order h^2, and at this step
each printed absolute difference is below 1e-6 (the table gives the three values). The agreement is a numerical
check at one point and step, not a proof of the rules; the proofs are on the page.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections.abc import Callable

H = 1e-4
X = 1.5


def p(x: float) -> float:
    """The product (x^2 + 1) * x^3."""
    return (x * x + 1.0) * x**3


def p_prime(x: float) -> float:
    """The product rule u'v + uv' with u = x^2 + 1 and v = x^3, giving 2x * x^3 + (x^2 + 1) * 3x^2."""
    return 2.0 * x * x**3 + (x * x + 1.0) * 3.0 * x * x


def q(x: float) -> float:
    """The quotient (x^2 + 1)/(x + 2), defined for x != -2."""
    return (x * x + 1.0) / (x + 2.0)


def q_prime(x: float) -> float:
    """The quotient rule: (u'v - uv')/v^2 with u = x^2 + 1 and v = x + 2, simplified to (x^2 + 4x - 1)/(x + 2)^2."""
    return (x * x + 4.0 * x - 1.0) / (x + 2.0) ** 2


def c(x: float) -> float:
    """The composite (x^2 + 1)^3, the outer function u^3 at the inner function u = x^2 + 1."""
    return (x * x + 1.0) ** 3


def c_prime(x: float) -> float:
    """The chain rule: (outer') at the inner value times the inner derivative, 3(x^2 + 1)^2 * 2x."""
    return 3.0 * (x * x + 1.0) ** 2 * 2.0 * x


def central(f: Callable[[float], float], x: float, h: float) -> float:
    """The central difference (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2h)."""
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2.0 * h)


def main() -> None:
    print("function, analytic derivative, central difference, absolute difference")
    cases = (
        ("product p", p, p_prime),
        ("quotient q", q, q_prime),
        ("composite c", c, c_prime),
    )
    for name, f, df in cases:
        analytic = df(X)
        numeric = central(f, X, H)
        print(f"{name}, {analytic:.9f}, {numeric:.9f}, {abs(analytic - numeric):.3e}")


main()

product p, 32.062500000, 32.062500235, 2.350e-07
quotient q, 0.591836735, 0.591836734, 3.328e-10
composite c, 95.062500000, 95.062500855, 8.550e-07

Each analytic value is computed from the rule on the page, and each central difference is computed from the function values alone. The absolute differences are 2.350e-07, 3.328e-10 and 8.550e-07, and all three are below \(10^{-6}\) at this one point and step. That is a numerical check of three rules at \(x = 1.5\), not a proof. The proofs in §1 and §2 hold for every point where the hypotheses hold. C Basis: Lab 1.

2The chain rule

Proposition 2.1 (chain rule)

Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\) and differentiable at \(a \in I\). Let \(g\) be defined on an open interval \(J\) and differentiable at \(b = f(a)\), with \(f(I) \subset J\). Hypotheses: both derivatives exist in the sense of Lesson 4, Definition 1.1, and every value \(f(x)\) with \(x \in I\) lies in \(J\). Then \(g \circ f\) is differentiable at \(a\), and \((g \circ f)'(a) = g'(f(a))\,f'(a)\).

Proof

Define \(G\) on the set \(J - b = \{k : b + k \in J\}\) by \(G(k) = (g(b + k) - g(b))/k\) for \(k \ne 0\), and \(G(0) = g'(b)\). By the definition of \(g'(b)\) in Lesson 4, \(G(k) \to G(0)\) as \(k \to 0\), so \(G\) is continuous at 0. Let \(k(h) = f(a + h) - f(a)\). For \(h \ne 0\) with \(a + h \in I\),

\[g(f(a+h)) - g(f(a)) = G\bigl(k(h)\bigr)\,k(h).\]

If \(k(h) \ne 0\), this is the definition of \(G\) at \(k(h)\). If \(k(h) = 0\), both sides are 0. Dividing by \(h\), the difference quotient of \(g \circ f\) at \(a\) is \(G(k(h))\,Q_f(h)\). Now \(k(h) \to 0\) as \(h \to 0\), by the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) form in the proof of Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1. Let \(\varepsilon > 0\), choose \(\eta > 0\) with \(|G(k) - G(0)| < \varepsilon\) for \(|k| < \eta\), and then \(\delta > 0\) with \(|k(h)| < \eta\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta\). Then \(G(k(h)) \to G(0) = g'(b)\) as \(h \to 0\). Since \(Q_f(h) \to f'(a)\), Lemma 1.1 (b) gives \((g \circ f)'(a) = g'(b)\,f'(a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (a power of a sum of squares)

Let \(c(x) = (x^2 + 1)^3\), the composite of \(f(x) = x^2 + 1\) with \(g(u) = u^3\). Here \(f'(x) = 2x\) by Lesson 4, Example 1.2 and the sum rule of Lesson 4, Problem P2, since the constant 1 has derivative 0 (Lesson 4, Problem R3). Also \(g'(u) = 3u^2\) by the power rule of Problem P1 in this lesson. The chain rule gives \(c'(x) = 3(x^2+1)^2 \cdot 2x = 6x(x^2+1)^2\). At \(x = 1.5\) this is \(6 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 3.25^2 = 95.0625\), which is the value in Lab 1.

Example 2.2 uses \(g'(u) = 3u^2\) for the cube, which Problem P1 proves from the product rule.

3Where the canon breaks

Each rule holds under its hypotheses. The errors below come from omitting a hypothesis or evaluating a derivative at the wrong point.

  • The product of derivatives is not the derivative of the product. Proposition 1.2 has two cross terms. Misconception 1 gives the case that shows the omission.
  • The quotient rule needs a nonzero denominator at the point. Proposition 1.3 uses \(g(a) \ne 0\) and continuity of \(g\) at \(a\). Where \(g(a) = 0\) the quotient need not be defined at \(a\), and the rule does not apply there.
  • The chain rule evaluates the outer derivative at the inner value. Proposition 2.1 uses \(g'(f(a))\), not \(g'(a)\). Misconception 2 shows the error, and the condition \(f(I) \subset J\) makes the composition defined.

4Misconceptions

  1. “The derivative of a product is the product of the derivatives.”

    Take \(f(x) = x\) and \(g(x) = x\). Then \(fg(x) = x^2\), with derivative \(2x\), while \(f'g' = 1 \cdot 1 = 1\). The product rule of Proposition 1.2 gives \(x \cdot 1 + x \cdot 1 = 2x\), which is correct.

  2. “The chain rule differentiates the outer function at the point \(a\).”

    Take \(f(x) = x + 1\) and \(g(u) = u^2\), at \(a = 0\). Then \(g \circ f(x) = (x+1)^2\), whose derivative at 0 is \(2 \cdot 1 = 2\). The wrong formula \(g'(a) f'(a)\) gives \(g'(0) \cdot 1 = 0\). The correct value uses \(g'(f(0)) = g'(1) = 2\).

  3. “A central difference gives the derivative exactly for a cubic.”

    For \(x^3\), the central difference at \(x = 1\) is \(((1+h)^3 - (1-h)^3)/(2h) = 3 + h^2\), which equals 3 only when \(h = 0\). The error is exactly \(h^2\), which at \(h = 10^{-4}\) is \(10^{-8}\). Lab 1 prints central-difference errors for the other functions \(p\), \(q\) and \(c\): 2.350e-07, 3.328e-10 and 8.550e-07. None of these is \(h^2\). The leading term of the central-difference error is \(h^2 f'''(x)/6\). This page does not prove that expansion. For \(p\), \(p'''(1.5) = 141\) gives 2.35e-07, which matches the printed error, as in E1.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · A product of a linear factor and a line

Use Proposition 1.2 to find the derivative of \(x(x + 1)\) at every point, and check it by expanding.

Solution

With \(f(x) = x\) and \(g(x) = x + 1\), \(f' = 1\) and \(g' = 1\). The rule gives \((fg)'(x) = 1 \cdot (x+1) + x \cdot 1 = 2x + 1\). Expanding, \(x^2 + x\) has derivative \(2x + 1\), which agrees.

M2 · A quotient at a point where the derivative vanishes

Let \(q(x) = x/(x^2 + 1)\). Find \(q'(x)\) and \(q'(1)\).

Solution

With \(f = x\), \(g = x^2 + 1\), \(f' = 1\), and \(g' = 2x\), Proposition 1.3 gives \(q'(x) = ((x^2+1) - x \cdot 2x)/(x^2+1)^2 = (1 - x^2)/(x^2+1)^2\). At \(x = 1\) the numerator is 0, so \(q'(1) = 0\). The denominator is never zero.

M3 · A power of a linear function

Let \(h(x) = (3x - 1)^4\). Find \(h'(1)\) using Proposition 2.1.

Solution

The outer function is \(u^4\), with derivative \(4u^3\), and the inner function is \(3x - 1\), with derivative 3. The chain rule gives \(h'(x) = 4(3x - 1)^3 \cdot 3 = 12(3x-1)^3\). At \(x = 1\), \(3x - 1 = 2\), so \(h'(1) = 12 \cdot 8 = 96\).

Proof

P1 · The power rule for positive integers

Let \(n \ge 1\) be an integer and \(p_n(x) = x^n\). Prove by mathematical induction, using Proposition 1.2, that \(p_n'(x) = n x^{n-1}\) for every real \(x\).

Solution

For \(n = 1\), \(p_1(x) = x\) has derivative 1 by the difference quotient \((a + h - a)/h = 1\), which is \(1 \cdot x^0\). Assume \(p_n'(x) = n x^{n-1}\) for some \(n \ge 1\). Write \(p_{n+1} = x^n \cdot x\). Proposition 1.2 gives \(p_{n+1}'(x) = n x^{n-1} \cdot x + x^n \cdot 1 = (n+1)x^n\), which is the claim for \(n + 1\). The induction is complete.

P2 · The reciprocal rule

Let \(g\) be differentiable at \(a\) with \(g(a) \ne 0\). Prove that \(1/g\) is differentiable at \(a\) with \((1/g)'(a) = -g'(a)/g(a)^2\).

Solution

Apply Proposition 1.3 with \(f = 1\), a constant function. Its derivative is 0 by the constant case of Lesson 4, Problem R3. The rule gives \((1/g)'(a) = (0 \cdot g(a) - 1 \cdot g'(a))/g(a)^2 = -g'(a)/g(a)^2\).

P3 · The square of a differentiable function

Let \(f\) be differentiable at \(a\). Use Proposition 2.1 with \(g(u) = u^2\) to show that \(f^2\) is differentiable at \(a\) with \((f^2)'(a) = 2 f(a) f'(a)\).

Solution

Take \(J = \mathbb{R}\), so \(f(I) \subset J\) holds for any \(I\). The function \(g(u) = u^2\) has \(g'(u) = 2u\) by Lesson 4, Example 1.2. Proposition 2.1 gives \((g \circ f)'(a) = g'(f(a)) f'(a) = 2 f(a) f'(a)\). The composition \(g \circ f\) is \(f^2\), which is the claim.

Empirical

E1 · The product error (from Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the absolute difference for the product as \(2.350 \times 10^{-7}\). Compare the printed size with the leading term \(h^2 p'''(x)/6\) of the central-difference error. This page does not prove that term, so the comparison is numerical.

Solution

For \(p(x) = x^5 + x^3\), \(p'''(x) = 60x^2 + 6\), which at \(1.5\) is \(60 \cdot 2.25 + 6 = 141\). With \(h = 10^{-4}\), \(h^2 p'''/6 = 10^{-8} \cdot 141/6 = 2.35 \times 10^{-7}\), which is the printed size.

E2 · The quotient value (from Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the analytic quotient derivative at 1.5 as 0.591836735. Check it as an exact fraction.

Solution

The quotient rule gives \((x^2 + 4x - 1)/(x+2)^2\), which at \(x = 1.5\) is \((2.25 + 6 - 1)/12.25 = 7.25/12.25 = 29/49\). Its decimal value is \(0.5918367346\ldots\), and the lab prints this rounded to nine decimal places, which is 0.591836735.

E3 · The composite value (from Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the analytic chain-rule derivative at 1.5 as 95.062500000. Check it from \(6x(x^2+1)^2\).

Solution

At \(x = 1.5\), \(x^2 + 1 = 3.25\), so \(3.25^2 = 10.5625\), and \(6 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10.5625 = 9 \cdot 10.5625 = 95.0625\). The printed value agrees with this exact value.

Research

R1 · The central difference of a cubic

Let \(p(x) = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d\). Show that for every \(x\) and \(h \ne 0\), the central difference \((p(x+h) - p(x-h))/(2h)\) equals \(p'(x) + a h^2\).

Solution

For the linear term, the central difference is \(c\). For the quadratic term, \(((x+h)^2 - (x-h)^2)/(2h) = 4xh/(2h) = 2x\), so it contributes \(2bx\), which is the derivative of \(bx^2\). For the cubic term, \(((x+h)^3 - (x-h)^3)/(2h) = (6x^2 h + 2h^3)/(2h) = 3x^2 + h^2\), so it contributes \(a(3x^2 + h^2)\). Adding the parts gives \(3ax^2 + 2bx + c + a h^2 = p'(x) + a h^2\).

R2 · Why the composition must be defined near the point

Let \(f(x) = x\) on \(I = (-1, 1)\), and let \(g(u) = 1/u\) on \(J = (0, \infty)\). Show that \(g \circ f\) is not defined on \(I\), and explain what Proposition 2.1 then says.

Solution

The composition needs \(g(f(x))\) for every \(x\) in \(I\). For \(x \le 0\) in \(I\), \(f(x) = x \le 0\) is not in \(J\), so \(g(f(x))\) is not defined. The hypothesis \(f(I) \subset J\) fails, and Proposition 2.1 does not apply. Restricting \(I\) to \((0, 1)\) makes the hypothesis hold, and then the chain rule applies at each point of that interval.

R3 · The quotient from the product and the reciprocal

Using Proposition 1.2 and Problem P2, derive the quotient rule of Proposition 1.3 for \(f/g\) when \(g(a) \ne 0\).

Solution

Write \(f/g = f \cdot (1/g)\). By Proposition 1.2, \((f/g)'(a) = f'(a)(1/g)(a) + f(a)(1/g)'(a)\). Substituting \((1/g)(a) = 1/g(a)\) and Problem P2's \((1/g)'(a) = -g'(a)/g(a)^2\), the sum is \(f'(a)/g(a) - f(a)g'(a)/g(a)^2 = (f'(a)g(a) - f(a)g'(a))/g(a)^2\), which is the quotient rule.
